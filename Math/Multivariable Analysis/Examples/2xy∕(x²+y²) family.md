The function $f(x,y) = \dfrac{2xy}{x^2 + y^2}$ with $f(0,0) = 0$, and its relative $\dfrac{2xy}{x^2 + y}$. In polar coordinates $f = \sin 2\theta$: it is constant on each line through the origin, with a different constant on each line. It vanishes on both axes, so both partials exist at the origin, yet $f$ has no limit there. It is the standard example separating "partials exist" from continuity and from differentiability. Its uses in MATH 452:

- The relative $\frac{2xy}{x^2+y}$ is not continuous at the origin ([[Multivariable Analysis §3 Continuity and Limits of Functions#^ex-3-1|§3]])
- No limit at the origin: along $y = kx$ the value depends on $k$ ([[Multivariable Analysis §3 Continuity and Limits of Functions#^ex-3-2|§3]])
- Partials exist at the origin, but $f$ is not continuous there ([[Multivariable Analysis §4 Partial Derivatives#^ex-4-1|§4]])
- Why differentiability is not defined as "the partials exist" ([[Multivariable Analysis §6 Differentiability#^rem-6-1|§6]])
- Partials can exist without differentiability ([[Multivariable Analysis §6 Differentiability#^rem-6-4|§6]])
- Partials exist, but $f$ is not differentiable at the origin ([[Multivariable Analysis §6 Differentiability#^ex-6-1|§6]])
