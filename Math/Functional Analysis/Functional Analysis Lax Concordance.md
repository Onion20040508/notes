# Concordance with Lax

The course follows Lax, *Functional Analysis*, Chapters 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10 and 15, but not always by the same route. This chapter records the differences so that the notes and the book can be read side by side: notation (Table C.1), where the two routes diverge (C.2), which parts of the notes correspond to which parts of the book (C.3), and an index of every Lax result cited in the notes, ordered by Lax's chapter and section (C.4); within a section, items Lax does not number (definitions, displayed conditions) come first in the order the notes use them, followed by numbered results in Lax's numbering. The index is generated by `qa556.py` from the reference lines at the foot of the boxes, so it cannot drift from the notes.

\subsection{Criteria and the Norm of a Weak Limit}

\begin{thmbox}[{{Criterion for Weak Convergence}}]\label{thm:weak-criterion}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space, $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset X$ and $x_0 \in X$. Then $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$ in $X$ if and only if

1. $\sup_n \Vert x_n\Vert  < \infty$, and
2. $\lim_{n \to \infty} \ell(x_n) = \ell(x_0)$ for all $\ell \in D$, where $D$ is a dense subset of $X'$.

\laxref{§10.1, Thm 1 (the direction $\Leftarrow$, Exercise 3) and Thm 4$'$ (weakly convergent sequences are bounded)}
\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}
This is Banach–Steinhaus (Theorem [[§42 Uniform Boundedness and Approximation#^thm-42-7\|§42.7]]). Think of $x_n$ not as an element of $X$ but as an element of the dual of $X'$: $x_n \in (X')' = \mathcal{L}(X', \mathbb{F})$, via $x_n(\ell) = \langle x_n, \ell \rangle = \ell(x_n)$. So $\{x_n\}$ is a sequence of bounded linear maps from $X'$ to $\mathbb{F}$, and

$X'$ is a Banach space even if $X$ is not (Corollary [[§31 Dual Spaces#^cor-31-1\|§31.1]]), while $\mathbb{F}$ is a normed space. The norm of $x_n$ as an element of $(X')'$ is $\Vert x_n\Vert $ (Proposition [[§37 Separable Duals and Reflexive Spaces#^prop-37-5\|§37.5]]), and $x_0$ is the candidate limit map $A \in \mathcal{L}(X', \mathbb{F})$. Weak convergence says: for all $\ell \in X'$, $\ell(x_n) = \langle x_n, \ell \rangle \to \langle x_0, \ell \rangle$, i.e. the maps $x_n$ converge pointwise on $X'$ to $x_0$. Banach–Steinhaus says this happens if and only if (1) $\sup_n \Vert x_n\Vert  < \infty$ and (2) the convergence holds for all $\ell$ in a dense subset $D \subset X'$.
\end{proof}

\begin{thmbox}[{{The Norm of a Weak Limit}}]\label{thm:weak-lsc}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space, $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset X$, $x_0 \in X$, and $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$ in $X$. Then $\{x_n\}$ is a bounded sequence, and

$$
\|x_0\| \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \|x_n\| .
$$

\laxref{§10.1, Thms 4$'$ and 5}
\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}
*Bounded:* this is part (1) of Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-5\|§43.5]], i.e. the uniform boundedness principle applied to the $x_n \in \mathcal{L}(X', \mathbb{F})$, which are pointwise bounded because each $\{\ell(x_n)\}$ converges. Just as every convergent sequence is bounded, every weakly convergent sequence is bounded.

*The norm bound.* Since $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$, for every $\ell \in X'$ we have $\ell(x_0) = \lim_n \ell(x_n)$, hence $\vert \ell(x_0)\vert  = \lim_n \vert \ell(x_n)\vert $. Also $\vert \ell(x_n)\vert  \le \Vert \ell\Vert \, \Vert x_n\Vert $. Take the $\liminf$ on both sides: the left side has a limit, equal to $\vert \ell(x_0)\vert $; the right side may not have a limit, but the $\liminf$ always makes sense. So

$$
|\ell(x_0)| = \lim_{n \to \infty} |\ell(x_n)| = \liminf_{n \to \infty} |\ell(x_n)| \le \|\ell\| \liminf_{n \to \infty} \|x_n\| .
$$

Now take the supremum over all $\ell \in X'$ with $\Vert \ell\Vert  \le 1$: by Theorem [[§36 Duality in Normed Spaces#^thm-36-2\|§36.2]] the left side becomes $\Vert x_0\Vert $, so

$$
\|x_0\| = \sup_{\ell \in X',\ \|\ell\| \le 1} |\ell(x_0)| \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \|x_n\| .
$$

\end{proof}

In Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-1\|§43.1]], $\Vert e_n\Vert  = 1$ for all $n$ while the weak limit $0$ has norm $0$: the inequality can be strict. Norm can be lost in a weak limit, but not gained.

\begin{propbox}[{{Weak Convergence and Convergence of the Norms in a Hilbert Space}}]\label{prop:weak-norm-strong}
Let $H$ be a Hilbert space, $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset H$ and $x_0 \in H$. Then $x_n \to x_0$ strongly if and only if

$$
\|x_n\| \to \|x_0\| \qquad \text{and} \qquad x_n \rightharpoonup x_0 \quad \text{in } H .
$$

\end{propbox}

\begin{proof}
*(HW10, Problem 5. Not from lecture; filled in.)* ($\Rightarrow$) Assume $\Vert x_n - x_0\Vert  \to 0$. Then $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$ by Proposition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^prop-43-1\|§43.1]](1), and by the reverse triangle inequality $\bigl\vert  \Vert x_n\Vert  - \Vert x_0\Vert  \bigr\vert  \le \Vert x_n - x_0\Vert  \to 0$.

($\Leftarrow$) Assume $\Vert x_n\Vert  \to \Vert x_0\Vert $ and $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$. Expanding the square of the norm with the inner product,

$$
\|x_n - x_0\|^2 = (x_n - x_0, x_n - x_0) = \|x_n\|^2 - (x_n, x_0) - (x_0, x_n) + \|x_0\|^2 = \|x_n\|^2 - 2 \operatorname{Re} (x_n, x_0) + \|x_0\|^2 ,
$$

because $(x_0, x_n) = \overline{(x_n, x_0)}$. The map $\ell(x) = (x, x_0)$ is a bounded linear functional on $H$ (Theorem [[§26 Bounded Linear Functionals and the Riesz Representation Theorem#^thm-26-5\|§26.5]](1)), so weak convergence gives

$$
(x_n, x_0) = \ell(x_n) \to \ell(x_0) = (x_0, x_0) = \|x_0\|^2 .
$$

Together with $\Vert x_n\Vert ^2 \to \Vert x_0\Vert ^2$,

$$
\|x_n - x_0\|^2 \to \|x_0\|^2 - 2 \|x_0\|^2 + \|x_0\|^2 = 0 .
$$

\end{proof}

So in a Hilbert space the only thing that separates weak from strong convergence is a loss of norm in the limit: by Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-6\|§43.6]], $\Vert x_0\Vert  \le \liminf_n \Vert x_n\Vert $ always, and the convergence is strong exactly when no norm is lost, $\Vert x_n\Vert  \to \Vert x_0\Vert $. For $e_n \rightharpoonup 0$ in $\ell^2$ the norms are $1$ and the norm of the limit is $0$. The proof does not use the completeness of $H$; it holds in every inner product space.

\subsection{Mazur's Theorem}

The norm bound says how large the weak limit can be; Mazur's theorem says where it is. Its proof uses the hyperplane separation theorem of Chapter 2, in a form adapted to normed spaces, which comes first.

\begin{thmbox}[{{Hyperplane Separation in a Normed Space}}]\label{thm:separation-normed}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space over $\mathbb{R}$, $K$ a closed convex set in $X$, and $x_0 \notin K$. Then there exist $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\ell \in X'$ such that

$$
\ell(x_0) > a > \ell(x) \qquad \text{for all } x \in K .
$$

\laxref{§8.4, in the proof of Thm 17 (a ball about $x_0$ is separated from $K$ by the extended hyperplane separation theorem, Ch. 3 Thm 6)}
\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}

(The proof was left to the class and then set as HW10, Problem 4; it is filled in here.) If $K = \varnothing$, take $\ell = 0$ and $a = -1$. Otherwise let $d = \inf_{y \in K} \Vert x_0 - y\Vert $; then $d > 0$, since otherwise there are $y_k \in K$ with $y_k \to x_0$, and $K$ closed would give $x_0 \in K$. Fix $0 < \varepsilon < d$ and let

$$
K_\varepsilon = \{ x \in X : \text{there is } y \in K \text{ with } \|x - y\| < \varepsilon \} .
$$

*$K_\varepsilon$ is convex.* If $x_1, x_2 \in K_\varepsilon$ with $\Vert x_i - y_i\Vert  < \varepsilon$, $y_i \in K$, and $0 \le t \le 1$, then $y = t y_1 + (1-t) y_2 \in K$ and $\Vert t x_1 + (1-t) x_2 - y\Vert  \le t\Vert x_1 - y_1\Vert  + (1-t)\Vert x_2 - y_2\Vert  < \varepsilon$.

*Every point of $K_\varepsilon$ is interior.* If $\Vert x - y\Vert  < \varepsilon$ with $y \in K$, put $\rho = \varepsilon - \Vert x - y\Vert  > 0$; then $B(x, \rho) \subset K_\varepsilon$ by the triangle inequality, so $x$ is a metric interior point, hence an interior point (Proposition [[§11 Normed Linear Spaces#^prop-11-9\|§11.9]]).

*$x_0 \notin K_\varepsilon$,* since every point of $K_\varepsilon$ is within $\varepsilon < d$ of $K$.

By Theorem [[§8 The Hyperplane Separation Theorem#^thm-8-1\|§8.1]] there are a linear $\ell : X \to \mathbb{R}$ and $c_0$ with $\ell(x_0) = c_0$ and $\ell(x) < c_0$ for all $x \in K_\varepsilon$. Fix $y_1 \in K$. For $\Vert u\Vert  < 1$, $y_1 + \varepsilon u \in K_\varepsilon$, so $\ell(y_1) + \varepsilon\, \ell(u) < c_0$, i.e. $\ell(u) < (c_0 - \ell(y_1))/\varepsilon =: C$ for all $\Vert u\Vert  < 1$; applied to $-u$ as well, $\vert \ell(u)\vert  \le C$ on the open unit ball, so $\ell$ is bounded, $\ell \in X'$. And $\ell \neq 0$, since $\ell(x_0) = c_0 > \ell(y_1)$. Now for $y \in K$ and $\Vert u\Vert  < 1$, $y + \varepsilon u \in K_\varepsilon$, so $\ell(y) + \varepsilon\, \ell(u) < c_0$; taking the supremum over $\Vert u\Vert  < 1$, $\ell(y) + \varepsilon \Vert \ell\Vert  \le c_0$. Thus

$$
\ell(y) \le c_0 - \varepsilon \|\ell\| < c_0 - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \|\ell\| =: a < c_0 = \ell(x_0) \qquad \text{for all } y \in K .
$$

\end{proof}

It is important that $\ell$ is *bounded*, and that $K$ is *closed* and convex. The version proved in Chapter 2 (Theorem [[§8 The Hyperplane Separation Theorem#^thm-8-1\|§8.1]]) was for a linear space without a norm, for a convex set $K$ consisting of interior points (“open”) with $y_0 \notin K$; it produced a linear functional, not necessarily bounded. The idea of the proof: since $K$ is closed and $x_0 \notin K$, the distance from $x_0$ to $K$ is positive, so $K$ can be made slightly bigger, to the set $K_\varepsilon$ of the proof, without reaching $x_0$. Every point of $K_\varepsilon$ is an interior point, $K_\varepsilon$ is convex, and Theorem [[§8 The Hyperplane Separation Theorem#^thm-8-1\|§8.1]] applies; or redo its proof from scratch.

![[m556-43-3.svg]]

\begin{corbox}[Hyperplane Separation over $\mathbb{C}$]\label{cor:separation-normed-complex}
*(Not from lecture.)* Let $X$ be a normed linear space over $\mathbb{C}$, $K$ a closed convex set in $X$, and $x_0 \notin K$. Then there exist $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\ell \in X'$ such that

$$
\operatorname{Re} \ell(x_0) > a > \operatorname{Re} \ell(x) \qquad \text{for all } x \in K .
$$

\end{corbox}

\begin{proof}

Regard $X$ as a real normed space $X_{\mathbb{R}}$ (the same vectors and norm, with scalars restricted to $\mathbb{R}$). $K$ is still closed (the norm is the same) and convex (convexity only uses real coefficients $t \in [0,1]$), and $x_0 \notin K$. Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-8\|§43.8]] gives $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and a real-linear bounded $\ell_r : X \to \mathbb{R}$ with $\ell_r(x_0) > a > \ell_r(x)$ for all $x \in K$. Define, as in the complex Hahn–Banach theorem (Theorem [[§9 The Complex Hahn–Banach Theorem#^thm-9-2\|§9.2]]),

$$
\ell(x) = \ell_r(x) - i\, \ell_r(ix) .
$$

*$\ell$ is complex-linear.* It is additive and real-homogeneous because $\ell_r$ is, and $\ell(ix) = \ell_r(ix) - i\,\ell_r(-x) = \ell_r(ix) + i\,\ell_r(x) = i\bigl( \ell_r(x) - i\,\ell_r(ix) \bigr) = i\,\ell(x)$; together, $\ell((s + it)x) = s\,\ell(x) + t\,\ell(ix) = (s + it)\,\ell(x)$.

*$\operatorname{Re} \ell = \ell_r$,* by construction.

*$\ell$ is bounded.* Given $x$, write $\ell(x) = \vert \ell(x)\vert \, e^{i\varphi}$ and put $c = e^{-i\varphi}$. Then $\ell(cx) = \vert \ell(x)\vert $ is real, so $\vert \ell(x)\vert  = \operatorname{Re} \ell(cx) = \ell_r(cx) \le \Vert \ell_r\Vert \, \Vert cx\Vert  = \Vert \ell_r\Vert \, \Vert x\Vert $. Hence $\ell \in X'$.

Finally $\operatorname{Re} \ell(x_0) = \ell_r(x_0) > a > \ell_r(x) = \operatorname{Re} \ell(x)$ for $x \in K$.
\end{proof}

The proof of Mazur's theorem below goes through over $\mathbb{C}$ with $\operatorname{Re} \ell$ in place of $\ell$, using Corollary [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^cor-43-9\|§43.9]]. Lecture 20 opened with this point: Mazur's theorem holds over $\mathbb{C}$ as well, so the hyperplane separation theorem has to be adapted to complex scalars; the adaptation was left to the class, and the corollary is that adaptation.

\begin{thmbox}[Mazur's Theorem]\label{thm:mazur}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space, $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset X$, $x_0 \in X$, and $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$ in $X$. Then $x_0$ belongs to the closed convex hull of $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$. That is, for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there are finitely many terms $x_1, \ldots, x_j$ of the sequence and $\lambda_k \ge 0$, $k = 1, \ldots, j$, with $\sum_{k=1}^j \lambda_k = 1$, such that

$$
\Bigl\| x_0 - \sum_{k=1}^j \lambda_k x_k \Bigr\| < \varepsilon .
$$

\laxref{§10.1, Thm 6 (for a closed convex $K$ containing the $x_n$; proved with support functions, §8.4 Thm 17)}
\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Mazur's theorem]
Assume $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$, but $x_0$ is not in the closed convex hull of $\{x_n\}$; call the closed convex hull $K$.

($K$ is closed by definition, and convex: if $z_k \to z$ and $w_k \to w$ with $z_k, w_k$ in the convex hull, then $t z_k + (1-t) w_k$ is in the convex hull and tends to $t z + (1-t) w$.) By the hyperplane separation theorem (Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-8\|§43.8]]) there are $\ell \in X'$ and $a \in \mathbb{R}$ with

$$
\ell(x_0) > a \qquad \text{and} \qquad \ell(x_n) < a \quad \text{for all } n ,
$$

since every $x_n \in K$. But $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$ implies $\ell(x_n) \to \ell(x_0)$, and a sequence of numbers all strictly less than $a$ cannot converge to something strictly bigger than $a$: the limit would satisfy $\ell(x_0) \le a$. This is a contradiction.
\end{proof}

In other words, the weak limit can be approximated in norm by convex combinations of the terms of the sequence.

(The convex hull of $\{x_n\}$ (Definition [[§3 Linear Maps, Convexity, and Linear Functionals#^def-3-4\|§3.4]]) consists of the finite convex combinations $\sum_k \lambda_k x_{n_k}$, $\lambda_k \ge 0$, $\sum_k \lambda_k = 1$: they form a convex set containing every $x_n$, and every convex set containing the $x_n$ contains them, by induction on the number of terms. The closed convex hull is the closure of this set; $x_0$ lies in it exactly when every ball about $x_0$ contains such a combination. Taking the first $j$ terms, with some $\lambda_k = 0$ allowed, loses nothing.)

![[m556-43-4.svg]]

Mazur's theorem for $e_n \rightharpoonup 0$ in $\ell^2$ (Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-1\|§43.1]]): no $e_n$ comes near $0$, since $\Vert e_n\Vert  = 1$, but the convex combinations $\frac1N (e_1 + \cdots + e_N)$, with $N$ entries $\frac1N$, have norm $\bigl( N \cdot \frac{1}{N^2} \bigr)^{1/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt N} \to 0$. Spreading the mass over many orthogonal directions is what the weak limit sees; it also shows the strict inequality $\Vert 0\Vert  < \liminf \Vert e_n\Vert  = 1$ of Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-6\|§43.6]].

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\sin(mx)$}{sin(mx)} in \texorpdfstring{$L^p$}{Lp}}

\begin{exbox}[{{$\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^p[0, 2\pi]$ for $1 < p < \infty$}}]\label{ex:sin-weak-Lp}
We know $\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^2([0, 2\pi])$ (Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-2\|§43.2]]). Since $\vert \sin(mx)\vert  \le 1$, also $\sin(mx) \in L^p([0, 2\pi])$ for $1 \le p < \infty$. *Question:* does $\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^p([0, 2\pi])$ for all $1 < p < \infty$? Yes. (The case $p = 1$ needs a different argument and is Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-5\|§43.5]].)

Recall $(L^p)' = L^{p'}$ with $\frac1p + \frac1{p'} = 1$, so $1 < p' < \infty$ (Theorem [[§31 Dual Spaces#^thm-31-4\|§31.4]]). We check the two conditions of Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-5\|§43.5]].

1. *Bounded:* $\sup_m \Vert \sin(mx)\Vert _{L^p([0, 2\pi])} \le \bigl( \int_0^{2\pi} 1\, dx \bigr)^{1/p} = (2\pi)^{1/p} < \infty$.
2. *Convergence on a dense set:* for every $g \in L^2([0, 2\pi])$, $\int_0^{2\pi} g(x) \sin(mx)\, dx \to 0$ as $m \to \infty$. Continuous functions are in $L^2$, so for all $g \in C[0, 2\pi]$, $\int_0^{2\pi} g(x) \sin(mx)\, dx \to 0$. And $C[0, 2\pi]$ is dense in $L^{p'}([0, 2\pi])$, because $p' < \infty$: it contains $C_c^\infty((0, 2\pi))$, which is dense by Theorem [[§19 The Function Spaces Lᵖ(Ω)#^thm-19-4\|§19.4]] (the endpoints have measure zero, so $L^{p'}([0, 2\pi]) = L^{p'}((0, 2\pi))$).

By Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-5\|§43.5]], $\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^p([0, 2\pi])$.
\end{exbox}

\begin{exbox}[{{$\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^1[0, 2\pi]$}}]\label{ex:sin-weak-L1}
*(Posed in lecture as a homework question. The answer is not from lecture; filled in.)* Does $\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^1([0, 2\pi])$? Here $(L^1)' = L^\infty$, and continuous functions are not dense in $L^\infty$ (Proposition [[§19 The Function Spaces Lᵖ(Ω)#^prop-19-5\|§19.5]]), so the argument of Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-4\|§43.4]] does not apply; one has to test against $L^\infty$ functions. The answer is yes.

Let $\ell \in (L^1([0, 2\pi]))'$. By Theorem [[§31 Dual Spaces#^thm-31-4\|§31.4]] with $p = 1$ there is $g \in L^\infty([0, 2\pi])$ with $\ell(f) = \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\, g(x)\, dx$ for all $f \in L^1([0, 2\pi])$. Since $\vert g(x)\vert  \le \Vert g\Vert _{L^\infty}$ for almost every $x$ and the interval has finite length,

$$
\int_0^{2\pi} |g(x)|^2\, dx \le 2\pi\, \|g\|_{L^\infty}^2 < \infty ,
$$

so $g \in L^2([0, 2\pi])$. By Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-2\|§43.2]],

$$
\ell(\sin(mx)) = \int_0^{2\pi} \sin(mx)\, g(x)\, dx \longrightarrow 0 = \ell(0) \qquad (m \to \infty) .
$$

As $\ell \in (L^1([0, 2\pi]))'$ is arbitrary, $\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^1([0, 2\pi])$. No dense subset is needed: on a bounded interval every $L^\infty$ function already lies in $L^2$, where the convergence is known.
\end{exbox}

\begin{exbox}[Weak Limits Do Not Respect Products]\label{ex:weak-product-fails}
*(Posed in lecture as a homework question, then set as HW10, Problem 2. The example is not from lecture; filled in.)* If $\{f_n\} \subset L^{p_1}([a,b])$ and $\{g_n\} \subset L^{p_2}([a,b])$, Hölder gives $f_n g_n \in L^p([a,b])$ with $\frac1p = \frac1{p_1} + \frac1{p_2} \le 1$ (apply Theorem [[§19 The Function Spaces Lᵖ(Ω)#^thm-19-1\|§19.1]] to $\vert f_n\vert ^p$ and $\vert g_n\vert ^p$ with the conjugate exponents $\frac{p_1}{p}$ and $\frac{p_2}{p}$). Assume $f_n \rightharpoonup f_0$ in $L^{p_1}$ and $g_n \rightharpoonup g_0$ in $L^{p_2}$. Does $f_n g_n \rightharpoonup f_0 g_0$ in $L^p$? Usually not: weak limits are not closed under nonlinearity; for instance $f_n \rightharpoonup f$ does not imply $f_n^2 \rightharpoonup f^2$.

*The example.* Take $[a, b] = [0, 2\pi]$, $p_1 = p_2 = 2$, so $p = 1$, and $f_n(x) = g_n(x) = \sin(nx)$. By Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-2\|§43.2]], $f_n \rightharpoonup 0$ and $g_n \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^2([0, 2\pi])$, so $f_0 = g_0 = 0$ and $f_0 g_0 = 0$. The product is

$$
f_n(x)\, g_n(x) = \sin^2(nx) = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 \cos(2nx) .
$$

*Claim: $\sin^2(nx) \rightharpoonup \frac12$ in $L^1([0, 2\pi])$,* where $\frac12$ is the constant function. Let $\ell \in (L^1([0, 2\pi]))'$. As in Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-5\|§43.5]], $\ell(f) = \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\, g(x)\, dx$ for some $g \in L^\infty([0, 2\pi]) \subset L^2([0, 2\pi])$. The functions $\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi} \cos(kx)$, $k = 1, 2, \ldots$, form an orthonormal set in $L^2([0, 2\pi])$: for $k, j \ge 1$, $\int_0^{2\pi} \cos(kx) \cos(jx)\, dx = \frac12 \int_0^{2\pi} \bigl( \cos((k-j)x) + \cos((k+j)x) \bigr) dx$, which is $0$ for $k \neq j$ and $\pi$ for $k = j$. By Proposition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^prop-43-3\|§43.3]], applied as in Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-2\|§43.2]], $\int_0^{2\pi} \cos(kx)\, g(x)\, dx \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$, in particular along $k = 2n$. Hence

$$
\ell(\sin^2(nx)) = \tfrac12 \int_0^{2\pi} g(x)\, dx - \tfrac12 \int_0^{2\pi} \cos(2nx)\, g(x)\, dx \longrightarrow \tfrac12 \int_0^{2\pi} g(x)\, dx = \ell\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr) \qquad (n \to \infty) .
$$

As $\ell$ is arbitrary, the claim follows.

*Conclusion.* The constant function $\frac12$ is not the zero element of $L^1([0, 2\pi])$, and weak limits are unique (Proposition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^prop-43-1\|§43.1]](2)). So $f_n g_n$ does not converge weakly to $f_0 g_0 = 0$ in $L^1([0, 2\pi])$, although $f_n \rightharpoonup f_0$ and $g_n \rightharpoonup g_0$ in $L^2([0, 2\pi])$. The same computation shows that $f_n \rightharpoonup 0$ does not imply $f_n^2 \rightharpoonup 0^2$.
\end{exbox}

Weak limits are useful but limited. A bounded sequence in infinite dimensions need not have a convergent subsequence; under certain circumstances it will have a weakly convergent one, but weak convergence does not commute with nonlinear operations.

\subsection{Weak-\texorpdfstring{$\ast$}{*} Convergence}

Does every bounded sequence have a weakly convergent subsequence? This is a difficult question; to answer it, the weak limit must be weakened even further.

\begin{defbox}[Weak-$\ast$ Convergence]\label{def:weak-star}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space and let $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ and $\ell_0$ be in $X'$. We say $\ell_n$ **converges to $\ell_0$ in the weak-$\ast$ sense**, and write $\mathop{\mathrm{w}^{\ast}\text{-}\mathrm{lim}}_{n \to \infty} \ell_n = \ell_0$ or $\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$, if for every $x \in X$

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \ell_n(x) = \ell_0(x) .
$$

\laxref{§10.3, definition (there $X$ is a Banach space)}
\end{defbox}

On $X'$ two notions are available: weak convergence ($X'$ is a normed space, test against $(X')' = X''$) and weak-$\ast$ convergence (test against $X$).

\begin{propbox}[Weak Implies Weak-$\ast$]\label{prop:weak-implies-weak-star-dual}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space, and $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$, $\ell_0$ in $X'$.

1. If $\ell_n \rightharpoonup \ell_0$ in $X'$, then $\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$ in $X'$. So weak-$\ast$ is the weaker notion.
2. *(Not from lecture.)* If $X$ is reflexive (Definition [[§37 Separable Duals and Reflexive Spaces#^def-37-1\|§37.1]]), the two notions coincide.

\laxref{§10.3, Remark 1 (part (2))}
\end{propbox}

\begin{proof}
(1) Since $X \subset X''$, isometrically embedded by $i(x)(\ell) = \ell(x)$ (Proposition [[§37 Separable Duals and Reflexive Spaces#^prop-37-5\|§37.5]]), each $x \in X$ gives the test element $i(x) \in X''$, and weak convergence in $X'$ gives

$$
\ell_n(x) = i(x)(\ell_n) \longrightarrow i(x)(\ell_0) = \ell_0(x) .
$$

As $x \in X$ is arbitrary, $\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$. Weak convergence tests against every element of $X''$, weak-$\ast$ only against those in $X$.

(2) If $X$ is reflexive, every element of $X''$ is in $X$: for $\eta \in X''$ there is $x \in X$ with $\eta = i(x)$. So if $\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$, then $\eta(\ell_n) = \ell_n(x) \to \ell_0(x) = \eta(\ell_0)$ for every $\eta \in X''$, i.e. $\ell_n \rightharpoonup \ell_0$. With (1), the two notions coincide.
\end{proof}

\begin{exbox}[Weak-$\ast$ but Not Weak]\label{ex:weak-star-not-weak}
$\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$ does not imply $\ell_n \rightharpoonup \ell_0$. Let $\ell^\infty_0 = c_0 = \{ (a_1, a_2, \ldots) : \lim_n a_n = 0 \}$ (Definition [[§29 Sequence and Function Spaces#^def-29-1\|§29.1]]). Then $(c_0)' = \ell^1$ (Proposition [[§36 Duality in Normed Spaces#^prop-36-8\|§36.8]]) and $(\ell^1)' = \ell^\infty$ (Theorem [[§36 Duality in Normed Spaces#^thm-36-4\|§36.4]]). Consider $e_n = (0, \ldots, 0, 1, 0, \ldots) \in \ell^1$.
- $e_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} 0$ in $\ell^1 = (c_0)'$: for every $a = (a_1, a_2, \ldots) \in c_0$, $\langle a, e_n \rangle = a_n \to 0$.
- $e_n \not\rightharpoonup 0$ in $\ell^1$: for $a = (a_1, a_2, \ldots) \in \ell^\infty = (\ell^1)'$, $\langle e_n, a \rangle = a_n$, which need not converge, since $a$ is only bounded.

For instance $a = (1, 1, 1, \ldots)$ gives $\langle e_n, a \rangle = 1 \not\to 0$, and $a = (1, -1, 1, -1, \ldots)$ gives a sequence with no limit at all, so $\{e_n\}$ has no weak limit in $\ell^1$ (any weak limit would also be a weak-$\ast$ limit by Proposition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^prop-43-11\|§43.11]], hence $0$, since a weak-$\ast$ limit is a pointwise limit and therefore unique). (The pairings are the bar-free ones, $\langle x, b \rangle = \sum_j x_j b_j$, of Corollary [[§36 Duality in Normed Spaces#^cor-36-5\|§36.5]].)

*(Not from lecture.)* Lax's example (§10.3, Example 5) is of the same kind: the averages $m_n(h) = \frac{n}{2} \int_{-1/n}^{1/n} h(t)\, dt$ are functionals on $C[-1, 1]$ that converge weak-$\ast$ to the point evaluation $h \mapsto h(0)$, but not weakly, since against a bounded function with a jump at $0$ the averages do not converge to its value at $0$.
\end{exbox}

![[m556-43-5.svg]]

The same sequence $e_n \in \ell^1$ seen by two families of test vectors. Weak-$\ast$ convergence tests only against $c_0$ (left): the pairings are the entries $a_n$ of a null sequence, so they tend to $0$. Weak convergence must also pass the tests by $\ell^\infty = (\ell^1)'$ (right), and a bounded sequence that does not converge defeats it.

As for weak convergence, Banach–Steinhaus gives a criterion, and the norm of the limit is controlled.

\begin{thmbox}[{{Criterion for Weak-$\ast$ Convergence}}]\label{thm:weak-star-criterion}
Let $X$ be a Banach space, $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset X'$ and $\ell_0 \in X'$. Then $\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$ in $X'$ if and only if

1. $\sup_n \Vert \ell_n\Vert  < \infty$, and
2. $\lim_{n \to \infty} \ell_n(x) = \ell_0(x)$ for all $x \in D$, where $D$ is a dense subset of $X$.

\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}
Banach–Steinhaus (Theorem [[§42 Uniform Boundedness and Approximation#^thm-42-7\|§42.7]]) for the bounded linear maps $\ell_n, \ell_0 : X \to \mathbb{F}$: pointwise convergence on $X$ holds if and only if the $\ell_n$ are uniformly bounded and converge on a dense subset.
\end{proof}

\begin{rembox}[{{The Weak-$\ast$ Criteria Need $X$ Complete}}]\label{rem:weak-star-completeness}
In the lecture the theorem above, and the next one, were stated for a normed linear space $X$; Lax (§10.3) assumes $X$ Banach from the start. Banach–Steinhaus, however, needs the domain of the maps to be complete, and here the domain is $X$ (in Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-5\|§43.5]] the domain was $X'$, which is always complete). The direction ($\Leftarrow$) holds for any normed $X$ (the $\varepsilon/2$ argument does not use completeness), but ($\Rightarrow$), weak-$\ast$ convergent implies bounded, can fail (Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-8\|§43.8]]). So in both theorems $X$ is assumed to be a Banach space; the bound $\Vert \ell_0\Vert  \le \liminf \Vert \ell_n\Vert $ holds for any normed $X$.
\end{rembox}

\begin{exbox}[{An Unbounded Weak-$\ast$ Null Sequence on $c_{00}$}]\label{ex:weak-star-null-unbounded-c00}

*(Not from lecture.)* Let $X = c_{00}$, the finitely supported sequences with the maximum norm (not complete, Example [[§42 Uniform Boundedness and Approximation#^ex-42-2\|§42.2]]), and $\ell_n(x) = n\, x_n$. These are the functionals $A_n$ of Example [[§42 Uniform Boundedness and Approximation#^ex-42-2\|§42.2]], so $\ell_n \in X'$ with $\Vert \ell_n\Vert  = n$.

1. *$\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} 0$.* For each $x \in c_{00}$, $\ell_n(x) = 0$ once $n$ exceeds the support of $x$, so $\ell_n(x) \to 0$.
2. *Not bounded.* $\sup_n \Vert \ell_n\Vert  = \sup_n n = \infty$.

So for the normed space $c_{00}$ the direction ($\Rightarrow$) of Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-12\|§43.12]] fails, and so does the boundedness in Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-13\|§43.13]].
\end{exbox}

\begin{thmbox}[{{The Norm of a Weak-$\ast$ Limit}}]\label{thm:weak-star-lsc}
Let $X$ be a Banach space, $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset X'$, $\ell_0 \in X'$, and assume $\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$ in $X'$. Then $\sup_n \Vert \ell_n\Vert  < \infty$, and

$$
\|\ell_0\| \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \|\ell_n\| .
$$

\laxref{§10.3, Thm 11 (bounded) and Exercise 6 (the norm bound)}
\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}
*Bounded:* to converge in the weak-$\ast$ sense, the sequence is pointwise bounded on $X$; this is the uniform boundedness principle (Theorem [[§42 Uniform Boundedness and Approximation#^thm-42-6\|§42.6]]).

*The norm bound.* The proof is almost the same as the previous one, but now the norm of $\ell_0$ is computed by its definition, without Hahn–Banach:

$$
\|\ell_0\| = \sup_{x \in X,\ \|x\| \le 1} |\ell_0(x)| , \qquad \ell_0(x) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \ell_n(x) .
$$

For $\Vert x\Vert  \le 1$, $\vert \ell_0(x)\vert  = \lim_n \vert \ell_n(x)\vert  = \liminf_n \vert \ell_n(x)\vert  \le \liminf_n \Vert \ell_n\Vert \, \Vert x\Vert  \le \liminf_n \Vert \ell_n\Vert $. Take the supremum over $\Vert x\Vert  \le 1$.
\end{proof}

The remaining question is under what conditions a bounded sequence has a weak-$\ast$ convergent subsequence; it is taken up in [[§44 Weak Sequential Compactness\|§44]].

\subsection{The Question}

Three modes of convergence are now available, in decreasing strength. In a normed linear space $X$, strong convergence implies weak convergence (Proposition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^prop-43-1\|§43.1]](1)). In a dual space $X'$, weak convergence implies weak-$\ast$ convergence (Proposition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^prop-43-11\|§43.11]](1)), and the two coincide when $X$ is reflexive, $X = X''$ (part (2) of that proposition).

The question left open at the end of [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence\|§43]]: let $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ be a bounded sequence in $X$. Is there a subsequence $\{x_{n_j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ that converges weakly, or (when $X$ is a dual space) in the weak-$\ast$ sense, to some $x_0 \in X$? For strong convergence the answer is no as soon as $X$ is infinite-dimensional, since the closed unit ball is then not compact (Theorem [[§20 Compactness and the Unit Ball#^thm-20-5\|§20.5]]). In $\ell^2$, for instance, the bounded sequence $\{e_n\}$ of Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-1\|§43.1]] has $\Vert e_n - e_m\Vert  = \sqrt{2}$ for $n \neq m$, so none of its subsequences is Cauchy.

\begin{defbox}[Weakly Sequentially Compact Set]\label{def:weakly-seq-compact}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space and $C$ a subset of $X$. $C$ is **weakly sequentially compact** if every sequence $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset C$ has a subsequence $\{x_{n_j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ and a point $x_0 \in C$ such that $x_{n_j} \rightharpoonup x_0$ as $j \to \infty$ (Definition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^def-43-1\|§43.1]]).
\laxref{§10.2, definition}
\end{defbox}

\begin{defbox}[{{Weak-$\ast$ Sequentially Compact Set}}]\label{def:weak-star-seq-compact}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space and $C$ a subset of $X'$. $C$ is **weak-$\ast$ sequentially compact** if every sequence $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset C$ has a subsequence $\{\ell_{n_j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ and a point $\ell_0 \in C$ such that $\ell_{n_j} \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$ as $j \to \infty$ (Definition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^def-43-2\|§43.2]]).
\laxref{§10.3, definition}
\end{defbox}

Both definitions copy Definition [[§20 Compactness and the Unit Ball#^def-20-1\|§20.1]], with strong convergence replaced by a weaker one. In both the limit is required to lie in $C$.

\subsection{Helly's Theorem}

\begin{thmbox}[Helly's Theorem]\label{thm:helly}
Let $X$ be a separable normed linear space (Definition [[§28 Existence of Orthonormal Bases and Separability#^def-28-1\|§28.1]]), and let $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ be a bounded sequence in $X'$, with $M = \sup_n \Vert \ell_n\Vert  < \infty$. Then there are a subsequence $\{\ell_{n_j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ and some $\ell_0 \in X'$ such that

$$
\ell_{n_j} \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0 \qquad \text{as } j \to \infty .
$$

Moreover $\Vert \ell_0\Vert  \le M$.
\laxref{§10.3, Thm 12 (for a separable Banach space, stated for the closed unit ball of $X'$; the name of Helly is Lax's)}
\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}
Step 1 produces the subsequence by a diagonal process, so that it converges at every point of a countable dense set. Step 2 extends the convergence to all of $X$, and Step 3 shows that the limit is a bounded linear functional.

Let $D = \{y_k\}_{k=1}^\infty$ be a countable dense subset of $X$, listed as a sequence (with repetitions if $D$ is finite).

**Step 1: a subsequence that converges on $D$.** For each fixed $y \in X$ the numbers $\ell_n(y)$ are bounded:

$$
|\ell_n(y)| \le \|\ell_n\|\, \|y\| \le M\, \|y\| \qquad \text{for all } n
$$

(Proposition [[§30 Boundedness and Continuity#^prop-30-1\|§30.1]](b)). A bounded sequence in $\mathbb{F}$ has a convergent subsequence, by the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem; for $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{C}$ apply it to the real parts and then, along the subsequence obtained, to the imaginary parts.

Apply this to $\{\ell_n(y_1)\}_{n}$: there are indices $n_{1,1} < n_{1,2} < n_{1,3} < \cdots$ such that $\{\ell_{n_{1,j}}(y_1)\}_{j=1}^\infty$ converges in $\mathbb{F}$. The sequence $\{\ell_{n_{1,j}}(y_2)\}_{j=1}^\infty$ is again bounded in $\mathbb{F}$, so the index sequence $\{n_{1,j}\}_j$ has a subsequence $n_{2,1} < n_{2,2} < \cdots$ such that $\{\ell_{n_{2,j}}(y_2)\}_{j=1}^\infty$ converges. Continue in the same way: if $\{n_{k,j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ has been formed, then $\{\ell_{n_{k,j}}(y_{k+1})\}_{j=1}^\infty$ is bounded in $\mathbb{F}$, and $\{n_{k+1,j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ is a subsequence of $\{n_{k,j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ along which $\ell_{n_{k+1,j}}(y_{k+1})$ converges. This gives index sequences, each a subsequence of the one before,

$$
\{n_{1,j}\}_j \supset \{n_{2,j}\}_j \supset \cdots \supset \{n_{k,j}\}_j \supset \{n_{k+1,j}\}_j \supset \cdots .
$$

Put $z_k = \lim_{j \to \infty} \ell_{n_{k,j}}(y_k) \in \mathbb{F}$. Since a subsequence of a convergent sequence converges to the same limit,

$$
\lim_{j \to \infty} \ell_{n_{k,j}}(y_i) = z_i \qquad \text{for each } i = 1, \ldots, k .
$$

Now take the diagonal:

$$
n_j = n_{j,j}, \qquad \ell_{n_j} = \ell_{n_{j,j}}, \qquad j = 1, 2, \ldots .
$$

![[m556-44-1.svg]]

The diagonal process (the picture in the notes). Row $k$ is the index sequence $\{n_{k,j}\}_j$, a subsequence of the row above, along which $\ell_n(y_1), \ldots, \ell_n(y_k)$ converge. The circled diagonal $n_j = n_{j,j}$ is, from its $k$-th term on, a subsequence of row $k$, for every $k$.

*The $n_j$ increase strictly.* Since $\{n_{j+1,i}\}_i$ is a subsequence of $\{n_{j,i}\}_i$, its $(j+1)$-st term is the $m$-th term of $\{n_{j,i}\}_i$ for some $m \ge j+1$. So $n_{j+1} = n_{j+1,j+1} = n_{j,m} \ge n_{j,j+1} > n_{j,j} = n_j$.

*For each $k$, $\{n_j\}_{j \ge k}$ is a subsequence of $\{n_{k,j}\}_{j \ge 1}$.* For $j \ge k$ the number $n_j = n_{j,j}$ is a term of the $j$-th index sequence, hence of the $k$-th. As the $n_j$ increase strictly and $\{n_{k,i}\}_i$ is strictly increasing, the numbers $n_k < n_{k+1} < \cdots$ occur in $\{n_{k,i}\}_i$ at increasing positions.

Consequently, for each fixed $k$,

$$
\ell_{n_{k,j}}(y_k) \to z_k \ \text{ as } j \to \infty \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \ell_{n_j}(y_k) \to z_k \ \text{ as } j \to \infty ,
$$

because $\{\ell_{n_j}(y_k)\}_{j \ge k}$ is a subsequence of the convergent sequence on the left. So $\{\ell_{n_j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ is a subsequence of $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ with

$$
\ell_{n_j}(y_k) \to z_k \in \mathbb{F} \qquad \text{for each } k, \text{ as } j \to \infty .
$$

**Step 2: the subsequence converges at every point of $X$.** We show that for every $x_0 \in X$ the sequence $\{\ell_{n_j}(x_0)\}_{j=1}^\infty$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb{F}$; since $\mathbb{F}$ is complete, it then converges. A bounded sequence of functionals that converges at every point of a dense set converges at every point.

Let $x_0 \in X$ and $\varepsilon > 0$. We need $N$ such that $\vert \ell_{n_j}(x_0) - \ell_{n_k}(x_0)\vert  < \varepsilon$ for all $j, k \ge N$. For every $y \in D$, by the triangle inequality, linearity, and $\Vert \ell_n\Vert  \le M$ for all $n$,
\begin{align*}
|\ell_{n_j}(x_0) - \ell_{n_k}(x_0)| &\le |\ell_{n_j}(y) - \ell_{n_k}(y)| + |\ell_{n_j}(x_0 - y) - \ell_{n_k}(x_0 - y)| |
| [[§48 The Resolvent and the Spectrum\|§48]] The resolvent and the spectrum | 0 \le t < 1, \ 1, | t = 1 . \end{cases}
\]
If $x_n \rightharpoonup x$ for some $x \in C[0,1]$, then by Proposition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^prop-43-4\|§43.4]] $x(t) = 0$ for $t < 1$ and $x(1) = 1$, which is not a continuous function. So $\{x_n\}$ does not converge weakly in $C[0,1]$. The same argument applies to every subsequence $\{t^{n_k}\}$, which has the same pointwise limit: the bounded sequence $\{t^n\}$ has no weakly convergent subsequence in $C[0,1]$.
\end{exbox}

\subsection{Criteria and the Norm of a Weak Limit}

\begin{thmbox}[{{Criterion for Weak Convergence}}]\label{thm:weak-criterion}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space, $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset X$ and $x_0 \in X$. Then $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$ in $X$ if and only if

1. $\sup_n \Vert x_n\Vert  < \infty$, and
2. $\lim_{n \to \infty} \ell(x_n) = \ell(x_0)$ for all $\ell \in D$, where $D$ is a dense subset of $X'$.

\laxref{§10.1, Thm 1 (the direction $\Leftarrow$, Exercise 3) and Thm 4$'$ (weakly convergent sequences are bounded)}
\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}
This is Banach–Steinhaus (Theorem [[§42 Uniform Boundedness and Approximation#^thm-42-7\|§42.7]]). Think of $x_n$ not as an element of $X$ but as an element of the dual of $X'$: $x_n \in (X')' = \mathcal{L}(X', \mathbb{F})$, via $x_n(\ell) = \langle x_n, \ell \rangle = \ell(x_n)$. So $\{x_n\}$ is a sequence of bounded linear maps from $X'$ to $\mathbb{F}$, and

$X'$ is a Banach space even if $X$ is not (Corollary [[§31 Dual Spaces#^cor-31-1\|§31.1]]), while $\mathbb{F}$ is a normed space. The norm of $x_n$ as an element of $(X')'$ is $\Vert x_n\Vert $ (Proposition [[§37 Separable Duals and Reflexive Spaces#^prop-37-5\|§37.5]]), and $x_0$ is the candidate limit map $A \in \mathcal{L}(X', \mathbb{F})$. Weak convergence says: for all $\ell \in X'$, $\ell(x_n) = \langle x_n, \ell \rangle \to \langle x_0, \ell \rangle$, i.e. the maps $x_n$ converge pointwise on $X'$ to $x_0$. Banach–Steinhaus says this happens if and only if (1) $\sup_n \Vert x_n\Vert  < \infty$ and (2) the convergence holds for all $\ell$ in a dense subset $D \subset X'$.
\end{proof}

\begin{thmbox}[{{The Norm of a Weak Limit}}]\label{thm:weak-lsc}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space, $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset X$, $x_0 \in X$, and $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$ in $X$. Then $\{x_n\}$ is a bounded sequence, and

$$
\|x_0\| \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \|x_n\| .
$$

\laxref{§10.1, Thms 4$'$ and 5}
\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}
*Bounded:* this is part (1) of Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-5\|§43.5]], i.e. the uniform boundedness principle applied to the $x_n \in \mathcal{L}(X', \mathbb{F})$, which are pointwise bounded because each $\{\ell(x_n)\}$ converges. Just as every convergent sequence is bounded, every weakly convergent sequence is bounded.

*The norm bound.* Since $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$, for every $\ell \in X'$ we have $\ell(x_0) = \lim_n \ell(x_n)$, hence $\vert \ell(x_0)\vert  = \lim_n \vert \ell(x_n)\vert $. Also $\vert \ell(x_n)\vert  \le \Vert \ell\Vert \, \Vert x_n\Vert $. Take the $\liminf$ on both sides: the left side has a limit, equal to $\vert \ell(x_0)\vert $; the right side may not have a limit, but the $\liminf$ always makes sense. So

$$
|\ell(x_0)| = \lim_{n \to \infty} |\ell(x_n)| = \liminf_{n \to \infty} |\ell(x_n)| \le \|\ell\| \liminf_{n \to \infty} \|x_n\| .
$$

Now take the supremum over all $\ell \in X'$ with $\Vert \ell\Vert  \le 1$: by Theorem [[§36 Duality in Normed Spaces#^thm-36-2\|§36.2]] the left side becomes $\Vert x_0\Vert $, so

$$
\|x_0\| = \sup_{\ell \in X',\ \|\ell\| \le 1} |\ell(x_0)| \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \|x_n\| .
$$

\end{proof}

In Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-1\|§43.1]], $\Vert e_n\Vert  = 1$ for all $n$ while the weak limit $0$ has norm $0$: the inequality can be strict. Norm can be lost in a weak limit, but not gained.

\begin{propbox}[{{Weak Convergence and Convergence of the Norms in a Hilbert Space}}]\label{prop:weak-norm-strong}
Let $H$ be a Hilbert space, $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset H$ and $x_0 \in H$. Then $x_n \to x_0$ strongly if and only if

$$
\|x_n\| \to \|x_0\| \qquad \text{and} \qquad x_n \rightharpoonup x_0 \quad \text{in } H .
$$

\end{propbox}

\begin{proof}
*(HW10, Problem 5. Not from lecture; filled in.)* ($\Rightarrow$) Assume $\Vert x_n - x_0\Vert  \to 0$. Then $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$ by Proposition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^prop-43-1\|§43.1]](1), and by the reverse triangle inequality $\bigl\vert  \Vert x_n\Vert  - \Vert x_0\Vert  \bigr\vert  \le \Vert x_n - x_0\Vert  \to 0$.

($\Leftarrow$) Assume $\Vert x_n\Vert  \to \Vert x_0\Vert $ and $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$. Expanding the square of the norm with the inner product,

$$
\|x_n - x_0\|^2 = (x_n - x_0, x_n - x_0) = \|x_n\|^2 - (x_n, x_0) - (x_0, x_n) + \|x_0\|^2 = \|x_n\|^2 - 2 \operatorname{Re} (x_n, x_0) + \|x_0\|^2 ,
$$

because $(x_0, x_n) = \overline{(x_n, x_0)}$. The map $\ell(x) = (x, x_0)$ is a bounded linear functional on $H$ (Theorem [[§26 Bounded Linear Functionals and the Riesz Representation Theorem#^thm-26-5\|§26.5]](1)), so weak convergence gives

$$
(x_n, x_0) = \ell(x_n) \to \ell(x_0) = (x_0, x_0) = \|x_0\|^2 .
$$

Together with $\Vert x_n\Vert ^2 \to \Vert x_0\Vert ^2$,

$$
\|x_n - x_0\|^2 \to \|x_0\|^2 - 2 \|x_0\|^2 + \|x_0\|^2 = 0 .
$$

\end{proof}

So in a Hilbert space the only thing that separates weak from strong convergence is a loss of norm in the limit: by Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-6\|§43.6]], $\Vert x_0\Vert  \le \liminf_n \Vert x_n\Vert $ always, and the convergence is strong exactly when no norm is lost, $\Vert x_n\Vert  \to \Vert x_0\Vert $. For $e_n \rightharpoonup 0$ in $\ell^2$ the norms are $1$ and the norm of the limit is $0$. The proof does not use the completeness of $H$; it holds in every inner product space.

\subsection{Mazur's Theorem}

The norm bound says how large the weak limit can be; Mazur's theorem says where it is. Its proof uses the hyperplane separation theorem of Chapter 2, in a form adapted to normed spaces, which comes first.

\begin{thmbox}[{{Hyperplane Separation in a Normed Space}}]\label{thm:separation-normed}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space over $\mathbb{R}$, $K$ a closed convex set in $X$, and $x_0 \notin K$. Then there exist $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\ell \in X'$ such that

$$
\ell(x_0) > a > \ell(x) \qquad \text{for all } x \in K .
$$

\laxref{§8.4, in the proof of Thm 17 (a ball about $x_0$ is separated from $K$ by the extended hyperplane separation theorem, Ch. 3 Thm 6)}
\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}

(The proof was left to the class and then set as HW10, Problem 4; it is filled in here.) If $K = \varnothing$, take $\ell = 0$ and $a = -1$. Otherwise let $d = \inf_{y \in K} \Vert x_0 - y\Vert $; then $d > 0$, since otherwise there are $y_k \in K$ with $y_k \to x_0$, and $K$ closed would give $x_0 \in K$. Fix $0 < \varepsilon < d$ and let

$$
K_\varepsilon = \{ x \in X : \text{there is } y \in K \text{ with } \|x - y\| < \varepsilon \} .
$$

*$K_\varepsilon$ is convex.* If $x_1, x_2 \in K_\varepsilon$ with $\Vert x_i - y_i\Vert  < \varepsilon$, $y_i \in K$, and $0 \le t \le 1$, then $y = t y_1 + (1-t) y_2 \in K$ and $\Vert t x_1 + (1-t) x_2 - y\Vert  \le t\Vert x_1 - y_1\Vert  + (1-t)\Vert x_2 - y_2\Vert  < \varepsilon$.

*Every point of $K_\varepsilon$ is interior.* If $\Vert x - y\Vert  < \varepsilon$ with $y \in K$, put $\rho = \varepsilon - \Vert x - y\Vert  > 0$; then $B(x, \rho) \subset K_\varepsilon$ by the triangle inequality, so $x$ is a metric interior point, hence an interior point (Proposition [[§11 Normed Linear Spaces#^prop-11-9\|§11.9]]).

*$x_0 \notin K_\varepsilon$,* since every point of $K_\varepsilon$ is within $\varepsilon < d$ of $K$.

By Theorem [[§8 The Hyperplane Separation Theorem#^thm-8-1\|§8.1]] there are a linear $\ell : X \to \mathbb{R}$ and $c_0$ with $\ell(x_0) = c_0$ and $\ell(x) < c_0$ for all $x \in K_\varepsilon$. Fix $y_1 \in K$. For $\Vert u\Vert  < 1$, $y_1 + \varepsilon u \in K_\varepsilon$, so $\ell(y_1) + \varepsilon\, \ell(u) < c_0$, i.e. $\ell(u) < (c_0 - \ell(y_1))/\varepsilon =: C$ for all $\Vert u\Vert  < 1$; applied to $-u$ as well, $\vert \ell(u)\vert  \le C$ on the open unit ball, so $\ell$ is bounded, $\ell \in X'$. And $\ell \neq 0$, since $\ell(x_0) = c_0 > \ell(y_1)$. Now for $y \in K$ and $\Vert u\Vert  < 1$, $y + \varepsilon u \in K_\varepsilon$, so $\ell(y) + \varepsilon\, \ell(u) < c_0$; taking the supremum over $\Vert u\Vert  < 1$, $\ell(y) + \varepsilon \Vert \ell\Vert  \le c_0$. Thus

$$
\ell(y) \le c_0 - \varepsilon \|\ell\| < c_0 - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \|\ell\| =: a < c_0 = \ell(x_0) \qquad \text{for all } y \in K .
$$

\end{proof}

It is important that $\ell$ is *bounded*, and that $K$ is *closed* and convex. The version proved in Chapter 2 (Theorem [[§8 The Hyperplane Separation Theorem#^thm-8-1\|§8.1]]) was for a linear space without a norm, for a convex set $K$ consisting of interior points (“open”) with $y_0 \notin K$; it produced a linear functional, not necessarily bounded. The idea of the proof: since $K$ is closed and $x_0 \notin K$, the distance from $x_0$ to $K$ is positive, so $K$ can be made slightly bigger, to the set $K_\varepsilon$ of the proof, without reaching $x_0$. Every point of $K_\varepsilon$ is an interior point, $K_\varepsilon$ is convex, and Theorem [[§8 The Hyperplane Separation Theorem#^thm-8-1\|§8.1]] applies; or redo its proof from scratch.

![[m556-43-3.svg]]

\begin{corbox}[Hyperplane Separation over $\mathbb{C}$]\label{cor:separation-normed-complex}
*(Not from lecture.)* Let $X$ be a normed linear space over $\mathbb{C}$, $K$ a closed convex set in $X$, and $x_0 \notin K$. Then there exist $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\ell \in X'$ such that

$$
\operatorname{Re} \ell(x_0) > a > \operatorname{Re} \ell(x) \qquad \text{for all } x \in K .
$$

\end{corbox}

\begin{proof}

Regard $X$ as a real normed space $X_{\mathbb{R}}$ (the same vectors and norm, with scalars restricted to $\mathbb{R}$). $K$ is still closed (the norm is the same) and convex (convexity only uses real coefficients $t \in [0,1]$), and $x_0 \notin K$. Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-8\|§43.8]] gives $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and a real-linear bounded $\ell_r : X \to \mathbb{R}$ with $\ell_r(x_0) > a > \ell_r(x)$ for all $x \in K$. Define, as in the complex Hahn–Banach theorem (Theorem [[§9 The Complex Hahn–Banach Theorem#^thm-9-2\|§9.2]]),

$$
\ell(x) = \ell_r(x) - i\, \ell_r(ix) .
$$

*$\ell$ is complex-linear.* It is additive and real-homogeneous because $\ell_r$ is, and $\ell(ix) = \ell_r(ix) - i\,\ell_r(-x) = \ell_r(ix) + i\,\ell_r(x) = i\bigl( \ell_r(x) - i\,\ell_r(ix) \bigr) = i\,\ell(x)$; together, $\ell((s + it)x) = s\,\ell(x) + t\,\ell(ix) = (s + it)\,\ell(x)$.

*$\operatorname{Re} \ell = \ell_r$,* by construction.

*$\ell$ is bounded.* Given $x$, write $\ell(x) = \vert \ell(x)\vert \, e^{i\varphi}$ and put $c = e^{-i\varphi}$. Then $\ell(cx) = \vert \ell(x)\vert $ is real, so $\vert \ell(x)\vert  = \operatorname{Re} \ell(cx) = \ell_r(cx) \le \Vert \ell_r\Vert \, \Vert cx\Vert  = \Vert \ell_r\Vert \, \Vert x\Vert $. Hence $\ell \in X'$.

Finally $\operatorname{Re} \ell(x_0) = \ell_r(x_0) > a > \ell_r(x) = \operatorname{Re} \ell(x)$ for $x \in K$.
\end{proof}

The proof of Mazur's theorem below goes through over $\mathbb{C}$ with $\operatorname{Re} \ell$ in place of $\ell$, using Corollary [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^cor-43-9\|§43.9]]. Lecture 20 opened with this point: Mazur's theorem holds over $\mathbb{C}$ as well, so the hyperplane separation theorem has to be adapted to complex scalars; the adaptation was left to the class, and the corollary is that adaptation.

\begin{thmbox}[Mazur's Theorem]\label{thm:mazur}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space, $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset X$, $x_0 \in X$, and $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$ in $X$. Then $x_0$ belongs to the closed convex hull of $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$. That is, for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there are finitely many terms $x_1, \ldots, x_j$ of the sequence and $\lambda_k \ge 0$, $k = 1, \ldots, j$, with $\sum_{k=1}^j \lambda_k = 1$, such that

$$
\Bigl\| x_0 - \sum_{k=1}^j \lambda_k x_k \Bigr\| < \varepsilon .
$$

\laxref{§10.1, Thm 6 (for a closed convex $K$ containing the $x_n$; proved with support functions, §8.4 Thm 17)}
\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Mazur's theorem]
Assume $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$, but $x_0$ is not in the closed convex hull of $\{x_n\}$; call the closed convex hull $K$.

($K$ is closed by definition, and convex: if $z_k \to z$ and $w_k \to w$ with $z_k, w_k$ in the convex hull, then $t z_k + (1-t) w_k$ is in the convex hull and tends to $t z + (1-t) w$.) By the hyperplane separation theorem (Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-8\|§43.8]]) there are $\ell \in X'$ and $a \in \mathbb{R}$ with

$$
\ell(x_0) > a \qquad \text{and} \qquad \ell(x_n) < a \quad \text{for all } n ,
$$

since every $x_n \in K$. But $x_n \rightharpoonup x_0$ implies $\ell(x_n) \to \ell(x_0)$, and a sequence of numbers all strictly less than $a$ cannot converge to something strictly bigger than $a$: the limit would satisfy $\ell(x_0) \le a$. This is a contradiction.
\end{proof}

In other words, the weak limit can be approximated in norm by convex combinations of the terms of the sequence.

(The convex hull of $\{x_n\}$ (Definition [[§3 Linear Maps, Convexity, and Linear Functionals#^def-3-4\|§3.4]]) consists of the finite convex combinations $\sum_k \lambda_k x_{n_k}$, $\lambda_k \ge 0$, $\sum_k \lambda_k = 1$: they form a convex set containing every $x_n$, and every convex set containing the $x_n$ contains them, by induction on the number of terms. The closed convex hull is the closure of this set; $x_0$ lies in it exactly when every ball about $x_0$ contains such a combination. Taking the first $j$ terms, with some $\lambda_k = 0$ allowed, loses nothing.)

![[m556-43-4.svg]]

Mazur's theorem for $e_n \rightharpoonup 0$ in $\ell^2$ (Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-1\|§43.1]]): no $e_n$ comes near $0$, since $\Vert e_n\Vert  = 1$, but the convex combinations $\frac1N (e_1 + \cdots + e_N)$, with $N$ entries $\frac1N$, have norm $\bigl( N \cdot \frac{1}{N^2} \bigr)^{1/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt N} \to 0$. Spreading the mass over many orthogonal directions is what the weak limit sees; it also shows the strict inequality $\Vert 0\Vert  < \liminf \Vert e_n\Vert  = 1$ of Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-6\|§43.6]].

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\sin(mx)$}{sin(mx)} in \texorpdfstring{$L^p$}{Lp}}

\begin{exbox}[{{$\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^p[0, 2\pi]$ for $1 < p < \infty$}}]\label{ex:sin-weak-Lp}
We know $\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^2([0, 2\pi])$ (Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-2\|§43.2]]). Since $\vert \sin(mx)\vert  \le 1$, also $\sin(mx) \in L^p([0, 2\pi])$ for $1 \le p < \infty$. *Question:* does $\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^p([0, 2\pi])$ for all $1 < p < \infty$? Yes. (The case $p = 1$ needs a different argument and is Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-5\|§43.5]].)

Recall $(L^p)' = L^{p'}$ with $\frac1p + \frac1{p'} = 1$, so $1 < p' < \infty$ (Theorem [[§31 Dual Spaces#^thm-31-4\|§31.4]]). We check the two conditions of Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-5\|§43.5]].

1. *Bounded:* $\sup_m \Vert \sin(mx)\Vert _{L^p([0, 2\pi])} \le \bigl( \int_0^{2\pi} 1\, dx \bigr)^{1/p} = (2\pi)^{1/p} < \infty$.
2. *Convergence on a dense set:* for every $g \in L^2([0, 2\pi])$, $\int_0^{2\pi} g(x) \sin(mx)\, dx \to 0$ as $m \to \infty$. Continuous functions are in $L^2$, so for all $g \in C[0, 2\pi]$, $\int_0^{2\pi} g(x) \sin(mx)\, dx \to 0$. And $C[0, 2\pi]$ is dense in $L^{p'}([0, 2\pi])$, because $p' < \infty$: it contains $C_c^\infty((0, 2\pi))$, which is dense by Theorem [[§19 The Function Spaces Lᵖ(Ω)#^thm-19-4\|§19.4]] (the endpoints have measure zero, so $L^{p'}([0, 2\pi]) = L^{p'}((0, 2\pi))$).

By Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-5\|§43.5]], $\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^p([0, 2\pi])$.
\end{exbox}

\begin{exbox}[{{$\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^1[0, 2\pi]$}}]\label{ex:sin-weak-L1}
*(Posed in lecture as a homework question. The answer is not from lecture; filled in.)* Does $\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^1([0, 2\pi])$? Here $(L^1)' = L^\infty$, and continuous functions are not dense in $L^\infty$ (Proposition [[§19 The Function Spaces Lᵖ(Ω)#^prop-19-5\|§19.5]]), so the argument of Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-4\|§43.4]] does not apply; one has to test against $L^\infty$ functions. The answer is yes.

Let $\ell \in (L^1([0, 2\pi]))'$. By Theorem [[§31 Dual Spaces#^thm-31-4\|§31.4]] with $p = 1$ there is $g \in L^\infty([0, 2\pi])$ with $\ell(f) = \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\, g(x)\, dx$ for all $f \in L^1([0, 2\pi])$. Since $\vert g(x)\vert  \le \Vert g\Vert _{L^\infty}$ for almost every $x$ and the interval has finite length,

$$
\int_0^{2\pi} |g(x)|^2\, dx \le 2\pi\, \|g\|_{L^\infty}^2 < \infty ,
$$

so $g \in L^2([0, 2\pi])$. By Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-2\|§43.2]],

$$
\ell(\sin(mx)) = \int_0^{2\pi} \sin(mx)\, g(x)\, dx \longrightarrow 0 = \ell(0) \qquad (m \to \infty) .
$$

As $\ell \in (L^1([0, 2\pi]))'$ is arbitrary, $\sin(mx) \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^1([0, 2\pi])$. No dense subset is needed: on a bounded interval every $L^\infty$ function already lies in $L^2$, where the convergence is known.
\end{exbox}

\begin{exbox}[Weak Limits Do Not Respect Products]\label{ex:weak-product-fails}
*(Posed in lecture as a homework question, then set as HW10, Problem 2. The example is not from lecture; filled in.)* If $\{f_n\} \subset L^{p_1}([a,b])$ and $\{g_n\} \subset L^{p_2}([a,b])$, Hölder gives $f_n g_n \in L^p([a,b])$ with $\frac1p = \frac1{p_1} + \frac1{p_2} \le 1$ (apply Theorem [[§19 The Function Spaces Lᵖ(Ω)#^thm-19-1\|§19.1]] to $\vert f_n\vert ^p$ and $\vert g_n\vert ^p$ with the conjugate exponents $\frac{p_1}{p}$ and $\frac{p_2}{p}$). Assume $f_n \rightharpoonup f_0$ in $L^{p_1}$ and $g_n \rightharpoonup g_0$ in $L^{p_2}$. Does $f_n g_n \rightharpoonup f_0 g_0$ in $L^p$? Usually not: weak limits are not closed under nonlinearity; for instance $f_n \rightharpoonup f$ does not imply $f_n^2 \rightharpoonup f^2$.

*The example.* Take $[a, b] = [0, 2\pi]$, $p_1 = p_2 = 2$, so $p = 1$, and $f_n(x) = g_n(x) = \sin(nx)$. By Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-2\|§43.2]], $f_n \rightharpoonup 0$ and $g_n \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^2([0, 2\pi])$, so $f_0 = g_0 = 0$ and $f_0 g_0 = 0$. The product is

$$
f_n(x)\, g_n(x) = \sin^2(nx) = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 \cos(2nx) .
$$

*Claim: $\sin^2(nx) \rightharpoonup \frac12$ in $L^1([0, 2\pi])$,* where $\frac12$ is the constant function. Let $\ell \in (L^1([0, 2\pi]))'$. As in Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-5\|§43.5]], $\ell(f) = \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\, g(x)\, dx$ for some $g \in L^\infty([0, 2\pi]) \subset L^2([0, 2\pi])$. The functions $\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi} \cos(kx)$, $k = 1, 2, \ldots$, form an orthonormal set in $L^2([0, 2\pi])$: for $k, j \ge 1$, $\int_0^{2\pi} \cos(kx) \cos(jx)\, dx = \frac12 \int_0^{2\pi} \bigl( \cos((k-j)x) + \cos((k+j)x) \bigr) dx$, which is $0$ for $k \neq j$ and $\pi$ for $k = j$. By Proposition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^prop-43-3\|§43.3]], applied as in Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-2\|§43.2]], $\int_0^{2\pi} \cos(kx)\, g(x)\, dx \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$, in particular along $k = 2n$. Hence

$$
\ell(\sin^2(nx)) = \tfrac12 \int_0^{2\pi} g(x)\, dx - \tfrac12 \int_0^{2\pi} \cos(2nx)\, g(x)\, dx \longrightarrow \tfrac12 \int_0^{2\pi} g(x)\, dx = \ell\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr) \qquad (n \to \infty) .
$$

As $\ell$ is arbitrary, the claim follows.

*Conclusion.* The constant function $\frac12$ is not the zero element of $L^1([0, 2\pi])$, and weak limits are unique (Proposition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^prop-43-1\|§43.1]](2)). So $f_n g_n$ does not converge weakly to $f_0 g_0 = 0$ in $L^1([0, 2\pi])$, although $f_n \rightharpoonup f_0$ and $g_n \rightharpoonup g_0$ in $L^2([0, 2\pi])$. The same computation shows that $f_n \rightharpoonup 0$ does not imply $f_n^2 \rightharpoonup 0^2$.
\end{exbox}

Weak limits are useful but limited. A bounded sequence in infinite dimensions need not have a convergent subsequence; under certain circumstances it will have a weakly convergent one, but weak convergence does not commute with nonlinear operations.

\subsection{Weak-\texorpdfstring{$\ast$}{*} Convergence}

Does every bounded sequence have a weakly convergent subsequence? This is a difficult question; to answer it, the weak limit must be weakened even further.

\begin{defbox}[Weak-$\ast$ Convergence]\label{def:weak-star}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space and let $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ and $\ell_0$ be in $X'$. We say $\ell_n$ **converges to $\ell_0$ in the weak-$\ast$ sense**, and write $\mathop{\mathrm{w}^{\ast}\text{-}\mathrm{lim}}_{n \to \infty} \ell_n = \ell_0$ or $\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$, if for every $x \in X$

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \ell_n(x) = \ell_0(x) .
$$

\laxref{§10.3, definition (there $X$ is a Banach space)}
\end{defbox}

On $X'$ two notions are available: weak convergence ($X'$ is a normed space, test against $(X')' = X''$) and weak-$\ast$ convergence (test against $X$).

\begin{propbox}[Weak Implies Weak-$\ast$]\label{prop:weak-implies-weak-star-dual}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space, and $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$, $\ell_0$ in $X'$.

1. If $\ell_n \rightharpoonup \ell_0$ in $X'$, then $\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$ in $X'$. So weak-$\ast$ is the weaker notion.
2. *(Not from lecture.)* If $X$ is reflexive (Definition [[§37 Separable Duals and Reflexive Spaces#^def-37-1\|§37.1]]), the two notions coincide.

\laxref{§10.3, Remark 1 (part (2))}
\end{propbox}

\begin{proof}
(1) Since $X \subset X''$, isometrically embedded by $i(x)(\ell) = \ell(x)$ (Proposition [[§37 Separable Duals and Reflexive Spaces#^prop-37-5\|§37.5]]), each $x \in X$ gives the test element $i(x) \in X''$, and weak convergence in $X'$ gives

$$
\ell_n(x) = i(x)(\ell_n) \longrightarrow i(x)(\ell_0) = \ell_0(x) .
$$

As $x \in X$ is arbitrary, $\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$. Weak convergence tests against every element of $X''$, weak-$\ast$ only against those in $X$.

(2) If $X$ is reflexive, every element of $X''$ is in $X$: for $\eta \in X''$ there is $x \in X$ with $\eta = i(x)$. So if $\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$, then $\eta(\ell_n) = \ell_n(x) \to \ell_0(x) = \eta(\ell_0)$ for every $\eta \in X''$, i.e. $\ell_n \rightharpoonup \ell_0$. With (1), the two notions coincide.
\end{proof}

\begin{exbox}[Weak-$\ast$ but Not Weak]\label{ex:weak-star-not-weak}
$\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$ does not imply $\ell_n \rightharpoonup \ell_0$. Let $\ell^\infty_0 = c_0 = \{ (a_1, a_2, \ldots) : \lim_n a_n = 0 \}$ (Definition [[§29 Sequence and Function Spaces#^def-29-1\|§29.1]]). Then $(c_0)' = \ell^1$ (Proposition [[§36 Duality in Normed Spaces#^prop-36-8\|§36.8]]) and $(\ell^1)' = \ell^\infty$ (Theorem [[§36 Duality in Normed Spaces#^thm-36-4\|§36.4]]). Consider $e_n = (0, \ldots, 0, 1, 0, \ldots) \in \ell^1$.
- $e_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} 0$ in $\ell^1 = (c_0)'$: for every $a = (a_1, a_2, \ldots) \in c_0$, $\langle a, e_n \rangle = a_n \to 0$.
- $e_n \not\rightharpoonup 0$ in $\ell^1$: for $a = (a_1, a_2, \ldots) \in \ell^\infty = (\ell^1)'$, $\langle e_n, a \rangle = a_n$, which need not converge, since $a$ is only bounded.

For instance $a = (1, 1, 1, \ldots)$ gives $\langle e_n, a \rangle = 1 \not\to 0$, and $a = (1, -1, 1, -1, \ldots)$ gives a sequence with no limit at all, so $\{e_n\}$ has no weak limit in $\ell^1$ (any weak limit would also be a weak-$\ast$ limit by Proposition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^prop-43-11\|§43.11]], hence $0$, since a weak-$\ast$ limit is a pointwise limit and therefore unique). (The pairings are the bar-free ones, $\langle x, b \rangle = \sum_j x_j b_j$, of Corollary [[§36 Duality in Normed Spaces#^cor-36-5\|§36.5]].)

*(Not from lecture.)* Lax's example (§10.3, Example 5) is of the same kind: the averages $m_n(h) = \frac{n}{2} \int_{-1/n}^{1/n} h(t)\, dt$ are functionals on $C[-1, 1]$ that converge weak-$\ast$ to the point evaluation $h \mapsto h(0)$, but not weakly, since against a bounded function with a jump at $0$ the averages do not converge to its value at $0$.
\end{exbox}

![[m556-43-5.svg]]

The same sequence $e_n \in \ell^1$ seen by two families of test vectors. Weak-$\ast$ convergence tests only against $c_0$ (left): the pairings are the entries $a_n$ of a null sequence, so they tend to $0$. Weak convergence must also pass the tests by $\ell^\infty = (\ell^1)'$ (right), and a bounded sequence that does not converge defeats it.

As for weak convergence, Banach–Steinhaus gives a criterion, and the norm of the limit is controlled.

\begin{thmbox}[{{Criterion for Weak-$\ast$ Convergence}}]\label{thm:weak-star-criterion}
Let $X$ be a Banach space, $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset X'$ and $\ell_0 \in X'$. Then $\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$ in $X'$ if and only if

1. $\sup_n \Vert \ell_n\Vert  < \infty$, and
2. $\lim_{n \to \infty} \ell_n(x) = \ell_0(x)$ for all $x \in D$, where $D$ is a dense subset of $X$.

\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}
Banach–Steinhaus (Theorem [[§42 Uniform Boundedness and Approximation#^thm-42-7\|§42.7]]) for the bounded linear maps $\ell_n, \ell_0 : X \to \mathbb{F}$: pointwise convergence on $X$ holds if and only if the $\ell_n$ are uniformly bounded and converge on a dense subset.
\end{proof}

\begin{rembox}[{{The Weak-$\ast$ Criteria Need $X$ Complete}}]\label{rem:weak-star-completeness}
In the lecture the theorem above, and the next one, were stated for a normed linear space $X$; Lax (§10.3) assumes $X$ Banach from the start. Banach–Steinhaus, however, needs the domain of the maps to be complete, and here the domain is $X$ (in Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-5\|§43.5]] the domain was $X'$, which is always complete). The direction ($\Leftarrow$) holds for any normed $X$ (the $\varepsilon/2$ argument does not use completeness), but ($\Rightarrow$), weak-$\ast$ convergent implies bounded, can fail (Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-8\|§43.8]]). So in both theorems $X$ is assumed to be a Banach space; the bound $\Vert \ell_0\Vert  \le \liminf \Vert \ell_n\Vert $ holds for any normed $X$.
\end{rembox}

\begin{exbox}[{An Unbounded Weak-$\ast$ Null Sequence on $c_{00}$}]\label{ex:weak-star-null-unbounded-c00}

*(Not from lecture.)* Let $X = c_{00}$, the finitely supported sequences with the maximum norm (not complete, Example [[§42 Uniform Boundedness and Approximation#^ex-42-2\|§42.2]]), and $\ell_n(x) = n\, x_n$. These are the functionals $A_n$ of Example [[§42 Uniform Boundedness and Approximation#^ex-42-2\|§42.2]], so $\ell_n \in X'$ with $\Vert \ell_n\Vert  = n$.

1. *$\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} 0$.* For each $x \in c_{00}$, $\ell_n(x) = 0$ once $n$ exceeds the support of $x$, so $\ell_n(x) \to 0$.
2. *Not bounded.* $\sup_n \Vert \ell_n\Vert  = \sup_n n = \infty$.

So for the normed space $c_{00}$ the direction ($\Rightarrow$) of Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-12\|§43.12]] fails, and so does the boundedness in Theorem [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^thm-43-13\|§43.13]].
\end{exbox}

\begin{thmbox}[{{The Norm of a Weak-$\ast$ Limit}}]\label{thm:weak-star-lsc}
Let $X$ be a Banach space, $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset X'$, $\ell_0 \in X'$, and assume $\ell_n \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$ in $X'$. Then $\sup_n \Vert \ell_n\Vert  < \infty$, and

$$
\|\ell_0\| \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \|\ell_n\| .
$$

\laxref{§10.3, Thm 11 (bounded) and Exercise 6 (the norm bound)}
\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}
*Bounded:* to converge in the weak-$\ast$ sense, the sequence is pointwise bounded on $X$; this is the uniform boundedness principle (Theorem [[§42 Uniform Boundedness and Approximation#^thm-42-6\|§42.6]]).

*The norm bound.* The proof is almost the same as the previous one, but now the norm of $\ell_0$ is computed by its definition, without Hahn–Banach:

$$
\|\ell_0\| = \sup_{x \in X,\ \|x\| \le 1} |\ell_0(x)| , \qquad \ell_0(x) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \ell_n(x) .
$$

For $\Vert x\Vert  \le 1$, $\vert \ell_0(x)\vert  = \lim_n \vert \ell_n(x)\vert  = \liminf_n \vert \ell_n(x)\vert  \le \liminf_n \Vert \ell_n\Vert \, \Vert x\Vert  \le \liminf_n \Vert \ell_n\Vert $. Take the supremum over $\Vert x\Vert  \le 1$.
\end{proof}

The remaining question is under what conditions a bounded sequence has a weak-$\ast$ convergent subsequence; it is taken up in [[§44 Weak Sequential Compactness\|§44]].

With Mazur's theorem and the norm bound this produces closest points.}

\subsection{The Question}

Three modes of convergence are now available, in decreasing strength. In a normed linear space $X$, strong convergence implies weak convergence (Proposition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^prop-43-1\|§43.1]](1)). In a dual space $X'$, weak convergence implies weak-$\ast$ convergence (Proposition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^prop-43-11\|§43.11]](1)), and the two coincide when $X$ is reflexive, $X = X''$ (part (2) of that proposition).

The question left open at the end of [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence\|§43]]: let $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ be a bounded sequence in $X$. Is there a subsequence $\{x_{n_j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ that converges weakly, or (when $X$ is a dual space) in the weak-$\ast$ sense, to some $x_0 \in X$? For strong convergence the answer is no as soon as $X$ is infinite-dimensional, since the closed unit ball is then not compact (Theorem [[§20 Compactness and the Unit Ball#^thm-20-5\|§20.5]]). In $\ell^2$, for instance, the bounded sequence $\{e_n\}$ of Example [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^ex-43-1\|§43.1]] has $\Vert e_n - e_m\Vert  = \sqrt{2}$ for $n \neq m$, so none of its subsequences is Cauchy.

\begin{defbox}[Weakly Sequentially Compact Set]\label{def:weakly-seq-compact}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space and $C$ a subset of $X$. $C$ is **weakly sequentially compact** if every sequence $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset C$ has a subsequence $\{x_{n_j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ and a point $x_0 \in C$ such that $x_{n_j} \rightharpoonup x_0$ as $j \to \infty$ (Definition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^def-43-1\|§43.1]]).
\laxref{§10.2, definition}
\end{defbox}

\begin{defbox}[{{Weak-$\ast$ Sequentially Compact Set}}]\label{def:weak-star-seq-compact}
Let $X$ be a normed linear space and $C$ a subset of $X'$. $C$ is **weak-$\ast$ sequentially compact** if every sequence $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty \subset C$ has a subsequence $\{\ell_{n_j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ and a point $\ell_0 \in C$ such that $\ell_{n_j} \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0$ as $j \to \infty$ (Definition [[§43 Weak and Weak-Star Convergence#^def-43-2\|§43.2]]).
\laxref{§10.3, definition}
\end{defbox}

Both definitions copy Definition [[§20 Compactness and the Unit Ball#^def-20-1\|§20.1]], with strong convergence replaced by a weaker one. In both the limit is required to lie in $C$.

\subsection{Helly's Theorem}

\begin{thmbox}[Helly's Theorem]\label{thm:helly}
Let $X$ be a separable normed linear space (Definition [[§28 Existence of Orthonormal Bases and Separability#^def-28-1\|§28.1]]), and let $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ be a bounded sequence in $X'$, with $M = \sup_n \Vert \ell_n\Vert  < \infty$. Then there are a subsequence $\{\ell_{n_j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ and some $\ell_0 \in X'$ such that

$$
\ell_{n_j} \overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup} \ell_0 \qquad \text{as } j \to \infty .
$$

Moreover $\Vert \ell_0\Vert  \le M$.
\laxref{§10.3, Thm 12 (for a separable Banach space, stated for the closed unit ball of $X'$; the name of Helly is Lax's)}
\end{thmbox}

\begin{proof}
Step 1 produces the subsequence by a diagonal process, so that it converges at every point of a countable dense set. Step 2 extends the convergence to all of $X$, and Step 3 shows that the limit is a bounded linear functional.

Let $D = \{y_k\}_{k=1}^\infty$ be a countable dense subset of $X$, listed as a sequence (with repetitions if $D$ is finite).

**Step 1: a subsequence that converges on $D$.** For each fixed $y \in X$ the numbers $\ell_n(y)$ are bounded:

$$
|\ell_n(y)| \le \|\ell_n\|\, \|y\| \le M\, \|y\| \qquad \text{for all } n
$$

(Proposition [[§30 Boundedness and Continuity#^prop-30-1\|§30.1]](b)). A bounded sequence in $\mathbb{F}$ has a convergent subsequence, by the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem; for $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{C}$ apply it to the real parts and then, along the subsequence obtained, to the imaginary parts.

Apply this to $\{\ell_n(y_1)\}_{n}$: there are indices $n_{1,1} < n_{1,2} < n_{1,3} < \cdots$ such that $\{\ell_{n_{1,j}}(y_1)\}_{j=1}^\infty$ converges in $\mathbb{F}$. The sequence $\{\ell_{n_{1,j}}(y_2)\}_{j=1}^\infty$ is again bounded in $\mathbb{F}$, so the index sequence $\{n_{1,j}\}_j$ has a subsequence $n_{2,1} < n_{2,2} < \cdots$ such that $\{\ell_{n_{2,j}}(y_2)\}_{j=1}^\infty$ converges. Continue in the same way: if $\{n_{k,j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ has been formed, then $\{\ell_{n_{k,j}}(y_{k+1})\}_{j=1}^\infty$ is bounded in $\mathbb{F}$, and $\{n_{k+1,j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ is a subsequence of $\{n_{k,j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ along which $\ell_{n_{k+1,j}}(y_{k+1})$ converges. This gives index sequences, each a subsequence of the one before,

$$
\{n_{1,j}\}_j \supset \{n_{2,j}\}_j \supset \cdots \supset \{n_{k,j}\}_j \supset \{n_{k+1,j}\}_j \supset \cdots .
$$

Put $z_k = \lim_{j \to \infty} \ell_{n_{k,j}}(y_k) \in \mathbb{F}$. Since a subsequence of a convergent sequence converges to the same limit,

$$
\lim_{j \to \infty} \ell_{n_{k,j}}(y_i) = z_i \qquad \text{for each } i = 1, \ldots, k .
$$

Now take the diagonal:

$$
n_j = n_{j,j}, \qquad \ell_{n_j} = \ell_{n_{j,j}}, \qquad j = 1, 2, \ldots .
$$

![[m556-44-1.svg]]

The diagonal process (the picture in the notes). Row $k$ is the index sequence $\{n_{k,j}\}_j$, a subsequence of the row above, along which $\ell_n(y_1), \ldots, \ell_n(y_k)$ converge. The circled diagonal $n_j = n_{j,j}$ is, from its $k$-th term on, a subsequence of row $k$, for every $k$.

*The $n_j$ increase strictly.* Since $\{n_{j+1,i}\}_i$ is a subsequence of $\{n_{j,i}\}_i$, its $(j+1)$-st term is the $m$-th term of $\{n_{j,i}\}_i$ for some $m \ge j+1$. So $n_{j+1} = n_{j+1,j+1} = n_{j,m} \ge n_{j,j+1} > n_{j,j} = n_j$.

*For each $k$, $\{n_j\}_{j \ge k}$ is a subsequence of $\{n_{k,j}\}_{j \ge 1}$.* For $j \ge k$ the number $n_j = n_{j,j}$ is a term of the $j$-th index sequence, hence of the $k$-th. As the $n_j$ increase strictly and $\{n_{k,i}\}_i$ is strictly increasing, the numbers $n_k < n_{k+1} < \cdots$ occur in $\{n_{k,i}\}_i$ at increasing positions.

Consequently, for each fixed $k$,

$$
\ell_{n_{k,j}}(y_k) \to z_k \ \text{ as } j \to \infty \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \ell_{n_j}(y_k) \to z_k \ \text{ as } j \to \infty ,
$$

because $\{\ell_{n_j}(y_k)\}_{j \ge k}$ is a subsequence of the convergent sequence on the left. So $\{\ell_{n_j}\}_{j=1}^\infty$ is a subsequence of $\{\ell_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ with

$$
\ell_{n_j}(y_k) \to z_k \in \mathbb{F} \qquad \text{for each } k, \text{ as } j \to \infty .
$$

**Step 2: the subsequence converges at every point of $X$.** We show that for every $x_0 \in X$ the sequence $\{\ell_{n_j}(x_0)\}_{j=1}^\infty$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb{F}$; since $\mathbb{F}$ is complete, it then converges. A bounded sequence of functionals that converges at every point of a dense set converges at every point.

Let $x_0 \in X$ and $\varepsilon > 0$. We need $N$ such that $\vert \ell_{n_j}(x_0) - \ell_{n_k}(x_0)\vert  < \varepsilon$ for all $j, k \ge N$. For every $y \in D$, by the triangle inequality, linearity, and $\Vert \ell_n\Vert  \le M$ for all $n$,
\begin{align*}
|\ell_{n_j}(x_0) - \ell_{n_k}(x_0)| &\le |\ell_{n_j}(y) - \ell_{n_k}(y)| + |\ell_{n_j}(x_0 - y) - \ell_{n_k}(x_0 - y)| |
