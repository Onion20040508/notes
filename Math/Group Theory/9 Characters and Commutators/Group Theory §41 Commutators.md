> [!definition] Definition §41.1: Commutator
> An element $h \in G$ is a **commutator** if $h = g_1 g_2 g_1^{-1} g_2^{-1}$ for some $g_1, g_2 \in G$. The identity is always a commutator (take $g_1 = g_2 = e$), and $G$ is abelian if and only if $e$ is the only commutator.

^def-41-1

> [!theorem] Proposition §41.1: Characters Kill Commutators
> Let $\chi: G \to A$ be a character and $h \in G$ a commutator. Then $\chi(h) = e_A$.
>
> *Source: PS 2.4(2)*

^prop-41-1

> [!proof]+ Proof
> Write $h = g_1g_2g_1^{-1}g_2^{-1}$. Since $\chi$ is a homomorphism and $A$ is abelian,
>
> $$ \chi(h) = \chi(g_1)\chi(g_2)\chi(g_1^{-1})\chi(g_2^{-1}) = \chi(g_1)\chi(g_1^{-1})\,\chi(g_2)\chi(g_2^{-1}) = \chi(g_1g_1^{-1})\,\chi(g_2g_2^{-1}) = \chi(e)\chi(e) = e_A. $$

^pf-41-1

*Uses:* [[Group Theory §40 Characters#^def-40-1|Def. §40.1]], [[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^def-41-1|Def. §41.1]], [[Group Theory §15 Homomorphisms#^prop-15-1|§15.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The Contrapositive as a Tool
> This gives a practical test: to show an element $h$ is *not* a commutator, exhibit one character $\chi$ with $\chi(h) \neq e_A$. [[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^prop-41-3|PS 2.4(4)]] below is exactly this, with $\chi = \operatorname{sgn}$.

^rem-41-1

> [!theorem] Proposition §41.2: $3$-Cycles Are Commutators
> Let $n \geq 3$ and let $c = (i\,j\,k) \in S_n$ with $i, j, k$ distinct. Then $c$ is a commutator: with $g_1 = (j\,k)$ and $g_2 = (i\,j)$,
>
> $$ g_1 g_2 g_1^{-1} g_2^{-1} = (j\,k)(i\,j)(j\,k)(i\,j) = (i\,j\,k). $$
>
> *Source: PS 2.4(3)*

^prop-41-2

> [!proof]+ Proof
> Transpositions are their own inverses, so $g_1^{-1} = g_1$ and $g_2^{-1} = g_2$ and the displayed product is $g_1g_2g_1^{-1}g_2^{-1}$. To evaluate it, first note $(j\,k)(i\,j) = (i\,k\,j)$: applying the right factor first,
>
> $$ i \mapsto j \mapsto k, \qquad j \mapsto i \mapsto i, \qquad k \mapsto k \mapsto j, $$
>
> so the product sends $i \mapsto k \mapsto j \mapsto i$, which is $(i\,k\,j)$. Hence
>
> $$ (j\,k)(i\,j)(j\,k)(i\,j) = (i\,k\,j)^2 = (i\,k\,j)^{-1} = (i\,j\,k), $$
>
> using that a $3$-cycle $\gamma$ satisfies $\gamma^3 = e$, so $\gamma^2 = \gamma^{-1}$, and that reversing the loop of $(i\,k\,j)$ gives $(i\,j\,k)$.

^pf-41-2

*Uses:* [[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^def-41-1|Def. §41.1]], [[Group Theory §12 Multiplying and Conjugating Cycles#^ex-12-1|Ex. §12.1]], [[Group Theory §11 Disjoint Cycle Decomposition#^lem-11-1|§11.1]]

> [!theorem] Proposition §41.3: Transpositions Are Not Commutators
> Let $n \geq 2$ and let $s = (i\,j) \in S_n$ with $i \neq j$. Then $s$ is not a commutator.
>
> *Source: PS 2.4(4)*

^prop-41-3

> [!proof]+ Proof
> The sign $\operatorname{sgn}: S_n \to \{\pm 1\}$ is a character (the target is abelian). By [[Group Theory §20 The Sign Homomorphism and the Alternating Group#^cor-20-4|§20.4]], $\operatorname{sgn}(s) = \det M(s) = -1$, since $M(s)$ is the identity matrix with two columns interchanged. If $s$ were a commutator, [[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^prop-41-1|Characters Kill Commutators]] would force $\operatorname{sgn}(s) = 1$, a contradiction.

^pf-41-3

*Uses:* [[Group Theory §20 The Sign Homomorphism and the Alternating Group#^thm-20-3|§20.3]], [[Group Theory §20 The Sign Homomorphism and the Alternating Group#^def-20-2|Def. §20.2]], [[Group Theory §20 The Sign Homomorphism and the Alternating Group#^cor-20-4|§20.4]], [[Linear Algebra 9C Determinants#^ladr-9-57|LADR 9.57]], [[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^prop-41-1|§41.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §41.2: Commutator Subgroup
> The **commutator subgroup** of $G$, written $[G,G]$, is the subgroup generated by all commutators $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$, $a, b \in G$. (A product of commutators need not itself be a commutator, which is why the generated subgroup is taken.)

^def-41-2

> [!theorem] Proposition §41.4: Properties of the Commutator Subgroup
> 1. $[G,G]$ is a normal subgroup of $G$ ([[Group Theory — 8 Normal Subgroups and Quotient Groups|Chapter 8]]).
> 2. Every character $\chi: G \to A$ is trivial on $[G,G]$, i.e. $[G,G] \subseteq \operatorname{Ker}\chi$.
> 3. $[G,G] = \{e\}$ if and only if $G$ is abelian.

^prop-41-4

> [!proof]+ Proof
> **(1)** Conjugation carries commutators to commutators:
>
> $$ g\,(aba^{-1}b^{-1})\,g^{-1} = (gag^{-1})(gbg^{-1})(gag^{-1})^{-1}(gbg^{-1})^{-1}. $$
>
> The inverse of a commutator is a commutator, $(aba^{-1}b^{-1})^{-1} = bab^{-1}a^{-1}$, so every element of $[G,G]$ is a product of commutators $c_1 \cdots c_m$, and since [[Group Theory §18 Conjugation, Products, and Pointwise Products#^prop-18-1|conjugation is a homomorphism]],
>
> $$ g(c_1 \cdots c_m)g^{-1} = (gc_1g^{-1}) \cdots (gc_mg^{-1}) \in [G,G]. $$
>
> **(2)** $\chi$ kills every commutator ([[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^prop-41-1|Characters Kill Commutators]]), and $\operatorname{Ker}\chi$ is a subgroup, so it contains the subgroup they generate. **(3)** $G$ is abelian iff every commutator equals $e$, iff $[G,G] = \{e\}$.

^pf-41-4

*Uses:* [[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^def-41-2|Def. §41.2]], [[Group Theory §4 Subgroups#^def-4-4|Def. §4.4]], [[Group Theory §4 Subgroups#^prop-4-7|§4.7]], [[Group Theory §18 Conjugation, Products, and Pointwise Products#^prop-18-1|§18.1]], [[Group Theory §35 Normal Subgroups#^def-35-1|Def. §35.1]], [[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^prop-41-1|§41.1]], [[Group Theory §15 Homomorphisms#^prop-15-2|§15.2]], [[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^def-41-1|Def. §41.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §41.5: The Commutator Subgroup of $S_n$
> For $n \geq 2$, $[S_n, S_n] = A_n$. Consequently every character $\chi: S_n \to A$ is trivial on $A_n$ and is determined by its value $c = \chi(\tau)$ on any transposition $\tau$, which satisfies $c^2 = e_A$: $\chi(\sigma) = e_A$ for even $\sigma$ and $\chi(\sigma) = c$ for odd $\sigma$.

^thm-41-5

> [!proof]+ Proof
> ($\subseteq$) The sign $\operatorname{sgn}$ is a character, so by (2) ([[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^prop-41-4|§41.4]]) $[S_n, S_n] \subseteq \operatorname{Ker}\operatorname{sgn} = A_n$. ($\supseteq$) For $n = 2$, $A_2 = \{e\}$. For $n \geq 3$, $A_n$ is generated by $3$-cycles ([[Group Theory §20 The Sign Homomorphism and the Alternating Group#^thm-20-9|§20.9]]), each of which is a commutator ([[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^prop-41-2|§41.2]]); so $A_n \subseteq [S_n, S_n]$. For the consequence: $\chi$ is trivial on $[S_n, S_n] = A_n$ by (2). All transpositions are conjugate ([[Group Theory §31 Conjugacy Classes#^prop-31-1|§31.1]]), so $\chi$ takes a single value $c$ on them ([[Group Theory §40 Characters#^prop-40-1|Characters Are Constant on Conjugacy Classes]]), and $c^2 = \chi(\tau^2) = \chi(e) = e_A$. An odd $\sigma$ equals $\tau(\tau\sigma)$ with $\tau\sigma$ even, so $\chi(\sigma) = \chi(\tau)\chi(\tau\sigma) = c$.

^pf-41-5

> [!remark] Remark: What the Theorem Says About $S_n$
> In $S_n$ all $3$-cycles are commutators and no transposition is, and [[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^thm-41-5|the theorem]] shows that characters of $S_n$ can only distinguish even from odd permutations: $\operatorname{sgn}$ is essentially the only nontrivial one.

^rem-41-2

> [!definition] Definition §41.3: Abelianization
> The **abelianization** of $G$ is the quotient group $G/[G,G]$, defined because $[G,G]$ is normal ([[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^prop-41-4|Properties of the Commutator Subgroup]]).
>
> *Source: not from class*

^def-41-3

> [!theorem] Proposition §41.6: The Abelianization Is Abelian
> $G/[G,G]$ is abelian.
>
> *Source: not from class*

^prop-41-6

> [!proof]+ Proof
> Let $N = [G,G]$. For $a, b \in G$, $(aN)(bN)(aN)^{-1}(bN)^{-1} = aba^{-1}b^{-1}N = N$, since the commutator $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$ lies in $N$. So $(aN)(bN) = (bN)(aN)$.

^pf-41-6

*Uses:* [[Group Theory §37 Quotient Groups#^def-37-1|Def. §37.1]], [[Group Theory §37 Quotient Groups#^thm-37-1|§37.1]], [[Group Theory §26 Left and Right Cosets#^prop-26-2|§26.2]], [[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^def-41-2|Def. §41.2]]

> [!example] Example §41.1: The Abelianization of $S_n$
> For $n \geq 2$, $S_n/[S_n, S_n] = S_n/A_n \cong \{\pm 1\}$: by The Commutator Subgroup of $S_n$ ([[Group Theory §41 Commutators#^thm-41-5|§41.5]]), $[S_n, S_n] = A_n$, and the [[Group Theory §38 The First Isomorphism Theorem#^thm-38-1|First Isomorphism Theorem]] applied to the surjective homomorphism $\operatorname{sgn}$, whose kernel is $A_n$, gives $S_n/A_n \cong \{\pm 1\}$ ([[Group Theory §38 The First Isomorphism Theorem#^ex-38-1|Ex. §38.1]]).
>
> *Source: not from class*
