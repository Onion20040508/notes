*Stage: bundles — The trivial fibrations, the Möbius band with its two trivializations and their half-twist, the normal bundle of a submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and its focal points, and where the other fibrations of the course appear.*

*The examples of [[§38 Fibrations|§38]], collected: the product fibrations, which every fibration imitates locally, and the Möbius band (Lecture 12), the first fibration that is not a product. The non-examples — a submersion that is not a fibration, and one whose fibres are all diffeomorphic but which is still not a fibration — stay with Ehresmann's theorem in [[§38 Fibrations|§38]], whose hypotheses they explain.*

> [!remark] Remark: Where Explicit Trivializations Come From
> *(Not from lecture.)* In Definition [[§38 Fibrations#^def-38-1|§38.1]] the local trivializations $\phi_\alpha$ are part of the data: a fibration has some, and nothing in the definition says how to find them — the charts of the base $B$ carry no information about the total space or the fibre. So a fibration is a map that is, over each $U_\alpha$, a product fibration (Example [[§39 Examples of Fibrations#^ex-39-1|§39.1]]), but there is no general formula for $\phi_\alpha$. Explicit trivializations come from extra structure:
> - *The tangent and cotangent bundles* ([[§49 The Tangent Bundle|§49]], [[§50 The Cotangent Bundle|§50]]) reverse the order: $TM$ and $T^{\ast}M$ start out as sets (Definitions [[§49 The Tangent Bundle#^def-49-1|§49.1]] and [[§50 The Cotangent Bundle#^def-50-1|§50.1]]), with no topology, and the trivializations are written down first. A chart $(U, \varphi = (x^1, \ldots, x^m))$ of $M$ gives every fibre a basis, $\partial/\partial x^i|_p$ of $T_pM$ and $dx^i|_p$ of $T_p^{\ast}M$ (Theorem [[§29 Coordinate Derivations and the Basis Theorem#^thm-29-6|§29.6]], Lemma [[§33 Covectors in Coordinates#^lem-33-1|§33.1]]), and writing a tangent vector $v \in T_pM$ or a covector $\xi \in T_p^{\ast}M$ in that basis,
>
> $$
> v = \sum_{i=1}^m v^i\, \frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\Big|_p, \quad v^i = v[x^i], \qquad\qquad \xi = \sum_{i=1}^m \xi_i\, dx^i\big|_p, \quad \xi_i = \xi\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\Big|_p\Big)
> $$
>
> (Theorem [[§29 Coordinate Derivations and the Basis Theorem#^thm-29-6|§29.6]], Corollary [[§33 Covectors in Coordinates#^cor-33-2|§33.2]]), is the trivialization: $(p, v) \mapsto (p, v^1, \ldots, v^m)$ (Definition [[§49 The Tangent Bundle#^def-49-4|§49.4]]) and $(p, \xi) \mapsto (p, \xi_1, \ldots, \xi_m)$ (Definition [[§50 The Cotangent Bundle#^def-50-4|§50.4]]). The topology is then *defined* from them: it is the unique topology for which every $TU$ is open and every chart $\tilde\varphi = (\varphi \times \mathrm{id}) \circ \Phi : TU \to \varphi(U) \times \mathbb{R}^m$ is a homeomorphism (Proposition [[§49 The Tangent Bundle#^prop-49-1|§49.1]]; for $T^{\ast}M$, Proposition [[§50 The Cotangent Bundle#^prop-50-1|§50.1]]), and the smooth structure is the one generated by these charts (Propositions [[§49 The Tangent Bundle#^prop-49-2|§49.2]] and [[§50 The Cotangent Bundle#^prop-50-2|§50.2]]). So the trivializations make the total space a manifold, and are local trivializations in the sense of Definition [[§38 Fibrations#^def-38-1|§38.1]] by construction (Corollaries [[§49 The Tangent Bundle#^cor-49-3|§49.3]] and [[§50 The Cotangent Bundle#^cor-50-3|§50.3]]). For a general fibration the order is the other way: the total space is a manifold to begin with, and trivializations have to be found.
> - *A quotient description* of the total space lies in between, as for the Möbius band (Proposition [[§39 Examples of Fibrations#^prop-39-1|§39.1]]): the topology is the quotient topology, given in advance, but the smooth structure is defined by the same charts that give the trivializations.
> - *Local sections* (Definition [[§38 Fibrations#^def-38-4|§38.4]]) give them for the Hopf fibration (Proposition [[§44 Projective Spaces and the Hopf Fibration#^prop-44-1|§44.1]]), whose total space $S^{2n+1}$ is a manifold in advance.
>
> Ehresmann's theorem (Theorem [[§38 Fibrations#^thm-38-3|§38.3]]) produces trivializations for proper submersions, but not by a formula.

^rem-39-1

## Product Fibrations

> [!example] Example §39.1: Product Fibrations
> *(Not from lecture.)* For smooth manifolds $B$ and $F$, the projection $\mathrm{pr}_1 : B \times F \to B$, $\mathrm{pr}_1(b, y) = b$, is a trivial fibration with fibre $F$, with one open set $U = B$ and one local trivialization $\phi = \mathrm{id}_{B \times F}$. Every trivial fibration is this one up to the diffeomorphism $\phi$: if $\phi : M \to B \times F$ is a global trivialization of $\pi$, then $\pi = \mathrm{pr}_1 \circ \phi$.

^ex-39-1

> [!proof]+ Proof
> Check Definition [[§38 Fibrations#^def-38-1|§38.1]] with $M = B \times F$ and $\pi = \mathrm{pr}_1$. The map $\mathrm{pr}_1$ is smooth (Proposition [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-11|§19.11]]). The single set $U = B$ covers $B$, and $\mathrm{pr}_1^{-1}(B) = B \times F$. The identity $\mathrm{id}_{B \times F}$ is a diffeomorphism $\mathrm{pr}_1^{-1}(B) \to B \times F$, and $\mathrm{pr}_1 \circ \mathrm{id}_{B \times F} = \mathrm{pr}_1 = \pi|_{\pi^{-1}(B)}$. So $\mathrm{pr}_1$ is a fibration with fibre $F$, trivial by Definition [[§38 Fibrations#^def-38-2|§38.2]]. The last sentence is the condition $\mathrm{pr}_1 \circ \phi = \pi$ of that definition.

^pf-ex-39-1

## The Möbius Band

> [!example] Example §39.2: The Möbius Band
> The Möbius band fibres over its core circle, with an interval as fibre: “no matter where you cut it, it just looks like a [strip]. But globally, it's not a product.” Concretely, let $E = \big(\mathbb{R} \times (-1, 1)\big)/{\sim}$ with $(s, t) \sim (s + n, (-1)^n t)$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, and $\pi[s, t] = [s] \in \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} \cong S^1$.
>
> *Lee: Example 10.3*

^ex-39-2

*Status.* In lecture this was a picture, not a proof: the claim that $\pi$ is a fibration with fibre $(-1, 1)$ is proved, with explicit local trivializations, in [[§39 Examples of Fibrations#^prop-39-1|Proposition §39.1]] below; the claim that $E$ is not globally the product $S^1 \times (-1, 1)$ is stated, not proved, here. Over any arc of the circle shorter than the whole, the twist can be undone, which is the local product structure; the obstruction is global, and it is visible in the picture: the band has a single boundary curve, while the product $S^1 \times (-1, 1)$ closed up would have two. Lee's Möbius bundle takes the fibre $\mathbb{R}$ in place of $(-1, 1)$.

*Left: the strip $[0, 1] \times (-1, 1)$, ruled by its fibres, with the core circle in orange; glue the two ends with a half-twist, matching the arrows. Right: the band itself. Each grey segment is a fibre, lying over one point of the orange core circle, and the boundary is a single curve.*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $E$ is the orbit space of $\mathbb{Z}$ acting on $\mathbb{R} \times (-1,1)$: [[§13 Group Actions and Orbit Spaces#^def-13-5|Def. §13.5]]; quotient spaces in 590: [[§13 Quotient Topology#^def-13-3|590 Def. §13.3]].

> [!theorem] Proposition §39.1: The Möbius Band Is a Fibration
> Let $E = \big(\mathbb{R} \times (-1, 1)\big)/{\sim}$ and $\pi[s, t] = [s]$ be as in Example [[§39 Examples of Fibrations#^ex-39-2|§39.2]], let $q : \mathbb{R} \times (-1, 1) \to E$ be the quotient map, and identify $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ with $S^1$ by $h[s] = (\cos 2\pi s, \sin 2\pi s)$. For $a \in \{0, \tfrac12\}$ put $I_a = (a, a + 1)$, $U_a = \{[s] : s \in I_a\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ and $W_a = q\big(I_a \times (-1, 1)\big) \subseteq E$.
>
> 1. $E$ is a topological $2$-manifold, and the two maps $\psi_a = \big(q|_{I_a \times (-1,1)}\big)^{-1} : W_a \to I_a \times (-1, 1)$ form a smooth atlas, with transition
>
> $$
> \psi_{1/2} \circ \psi_0^{-1}(s, t) = \begin{cases} (s, t), & \tfrac12 < s < 1, \\ (s + 1, -t), & 0 < s < \tfrac12. \end{cases}
> $$
>
> 2. With this smooth structure $\pi$ is smooth, $\pi^{-1}(U_a) = W_a$, and
>
> $$
> \phi_a : \pi^{-1}(U_a) \to U_a \times (-1, 1), \qquad \phi_a[s, t] = \big([s], t\big) \quad (s \in I_a),
> $$
>
> are local trivializations. So $\pi$ is a fibration with fibre $(-1, 1)$.
> 3. On $U_0 \cap U_{1/2}$ the two trivializations differ by a sign on one arc:
>
> $$
> \phi_{1/2} \circ \phi_0^{-1}\big([s], t\big) = \begin{cases} \big([s], t\big), & \tfrac12 < s < 1, \\ \big([s], -t\big), & 0 < s < \tfrac12. \end{cases}
> $$

^prop-39-1

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from lecture; filled in.)* Write $g_n(s, t) = \big(s + n, (-1)^n t\big)$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, so that $(s, t) \sim (s', t')$ exactly when $(s', t') = g_n(s, t)$ for some $n$. Each $g_n$ is a homeomorphism of $\mathbb{R} \times (-1, 1)$, with inverse $g_{-n}$, since $g_n \circ g_m = g_{n+m}$ and $g_0 = \mathrm{id}$.
>
> *$q$ is open.* For $O$ open in $\mathbb{R} \times (-1, 1)$, the saturation $q^{-1}(q(O)) = \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} g_n(O)$ is a union of open sets, so $q(O)$ is open in the quotient topology (Definition [[§4 Quotient Spaces and Open Maps#^def-4-2|§4.2]]).
>
> *$q$ restricted to $I_a \times (-1, 1)$.* It is injective: if $q(s, t) = q(s', t')$ with $s, s' \in I_a$, then $(s', t') = g_n(s, t)$, so $s' - s = n$ is an integer with $|s' - s| < 1$, hence $n = 0$ and $(s', t') = (s, t)$. It is continuous, as a restriction of $q$, and open, since an open subset of $I_a \times (-1, 1)$ is open in $\mathbb{R} \times (-1, 1)$ and $q$ is open. So it is a homeomorphism onto the open set $W_a$, and $\psi_a$ is a homeomorphism of $W_a$ onto the open subset $I_a \times (-1, 1)$ of $\mathbb{R}^2$.
>
> *(1) $E$ is a topological $2$-manifold.* The sets $W_0, W_{1/2}$ cover $E$: every $s \in \mathbb{R}$ has $s - n \in (0, 1)$ for some $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ unless $s$ is an integer, and then $s - n = 1 \in I_{1/2}$ for $n = s - 1$; and $q(s, t) = q\big(g_{-n}(s, t)\big)$. *Hausdorff:* let $x \ne y$ in $E$. If both lie in one $W_a$, separate them there, $W_a$ being homeomorphic to a subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$ and open in $E$. Otherwise one lies in $W_0 \setminus W_{1/2} = q\big(\{\tfrac12\} \times (-1, 1)\big)$ and the other in $W_{1/2} \setminus W_0 = q\big(\{1\} \times (-1, 1)\big)$; then $q\big((\tfrac14, \tfrac34) \times (-1, 1)\big)$ and $q\big((\tfrac34, \tfrac54) \times (-1, 1)\big)$ are open neighbourhoods of them, disjoint because two of their points with first coordinates $s \in (\tfrac14, \tfrac34)$, $s' \in (\tfrac34, \tfrac54)$ have $0 < s' - s < 1$, not an integer. *Second countable:* each $W_a$ has a countable basis of sets open in $W_a$, hence in $E$, and the union of the two bases is a countable basis of $E$. With the charts $\psi_a$, $E$ is a topological $2$-manifold (Definition [[§2 Topological Manifolds#^def-2-2|§2.2]]).
>
> *(1) The transition.* $W_0 \cap W_{1/2} = q\big(((0, \tfrac12) \cup (\tfrac12, 1)) \times (-1, 1)\big)$. Let $(s, t) \in \psi_0(W_0 \cap W_{1/2})$. If $\tfrac12 < s < 1$, then $s \in I_{1/2}$ and $\psi_{1/2}(q(s, t)) = (s, t)$. If $0 < s < \tfrac12$, then $q(s, t) = q\big(g_1(s, t)\big) = q(s + 1, -t)$ with $s + 1 \in I_{1/2}$, so $\psi_{1/2}(q(s, t)) = (s + 1, -t)$. Both branches are smooth, and so is the inverse, $(s, t) \mapsto (s, t)$ or $(s - 1, -t)$. So the two charts are compatible (Definition [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^def-17-4|§17.4]]) and form a smooth atlas (Theorem [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^thm-17-5|§17.5]]).
>
> *The base.* $h$ is well defined and bijective because $(\cos 2\pi s, \sin 2\pi s)$ depends exactly on $s$ modulo $1$; $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ carries the topology and smooth structure of $S^1$ transported along $h$ (Proposition [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-8|§19.8]]). For $s \in I_0 = (0, 1)$ the angle $2\pi s$ lies in $(0, 2\pi)$, and for $s \in I_{1/2}$ in $(\pi, 3\pi)$; so the maps $\chi_a : U_a \to I_a$, $\chi_a[s] = s$ ($s \in I_a$), are $\chi_0 = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\,\varphi_a \circ h$ and $\chi_{1/2} = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\,\varphi_b \circ h$, with $\varphi_a, \varphi_b$ the angle charts of Example [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^ex-17-4|§17.4]]. Rescaling by the diffeomorphism $\theta \mapsto \theta/2\pi$ of $\mathbb{R}$ keeps a chart in the smooth structure (as in the proof of Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-2|§37.2]]), so $\chi_0, \chi_{1/2}$ are smooth charts of $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ with domains $U_0, U_{1/2}$.
>
> *(2) $\pi$ is smooth and $\pi^{-1}(U_a) = W_a$.* $\pi$ is well defined, since $\pi[g_n(s, t)] = [s + n] = [s]$. If $[s] \in U_a$ then $s - n \in I_a$ for some $n$ and $q(s, t) = q(g_{-n}(s, t)) \in W_a$; conversely $\pi(W_a) \subseteq U_a$. So $\pi^{-1}(U_a) = W_a$, and in the charts $\psi_a$ and $\chi_a$,
>
> $$
> \chi_a \circ \pi \circ \psi_a^{-1}(s, t) = \chi_a[s] = s \qquad \big((s, t) \in I_a \times (-1, 1)\big),
> $$
>
> a smooth map. The $W_a$ cover $E$, so $\pi$ is smooth (Definition [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^def-19-3|§19.3]], Proposition [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-2|§19.2]]).
>
> *(2) The trivializations.* $\phi_a = (\chi_a^{-1} \times \mathrm{id}) \circ \psi_a$, since $\psi_a[s, t] = (s, t)$ and $\chi_a^{-1}(s) = [s]$ for $s \in I_a$. Here $\psi_a : W_a \to I_a \times (-1, 1)$ is a diffeomorphism, a chart of the open submanifold $W_a$ (Proposition [[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^prop-28-9|§28.9]]), and $\chi_a^{-1} \times \mathrm{id} : I_a \times (-1, 1) \to U_a \times (-1, 1)$ is a diffeomorphism, with inverse $\chi_a \times \mathrm{id}$, both having the identity as coordinate representation in the product chart $(U_a \times (-1, 1), \chi_a \times \mathrm{id})$. So $\phi_a$ is a diffeomorphism, and $\mathrm{pr}_1 \circ \phi_a[s, t] = [s] = \pi[s, t]$. With the open cover $\{U_0, U_{1/2}\}$ of $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, Definition [[§38 Fibrations#^def-38-1|§38.1]] holds with fibre $(-1, 1)$.
>
> *(3)* Let $[s] \in U_0 \cap U_{1/2}$ with $s \in I_0$. Then $\phi_0^{-1}([s], t) = q(s, t)$, and by the transition in (1), $q(s, t) = q(s, t)$ with $s \in I_{1/2}$ if $\tfrac12 < s < 1$, and $q(s, t) = q(s + 1, -t)$ with $s + 1 \in I_{1/2}$ if $0 < s < \tfrac12$. Applying $\phi_{1/2}$ gives $([s], t)$ in the first case and $([s + 1], -t) = ([s], -t)$ in the second.

^pf-39-1

*Uses:* [[§4 Quotient Spaces and Open Maps#^def-4-2|Def. §4.2]], [[§2 Topological Manifolds#^def-2-2|Def. §2.2]], [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^def-17-4|Def. §17.4]], [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^thm-17-5|§17.5]], [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-8|§19.8]], [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^ex-17-4|Ex. §17.4]], [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-2|§37.2]], [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^def-19-3|Def. §19.3]], [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-2|§19.2]], [[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^prop-28-9|§28.9]], [[§38 Fibrations#^def-38-1|Def. §38.1]]

The sign in (3) is the half-twist: going once around the circle, the two trivializations agree on one arc and differ by $t \mapsto -t$ on the other. Over either arc alone the band is a product; the sign is what a global product would have to remove.

## The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space

*Assignment 5, Problem 5.* A submanifold $M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ has, at each of its points, the vectors perpendicular to it; collecting all pairs (point, perpendicular vector) gives the *normal bundle*. Part (a) of the problem shows that it is a manifold of dimension $n$, whatever the dimension of $M$; part (b) introduces the endpoint map and finds the focal points of a parabola. The normal bundle is the Euclidean shadow of the conormal bundle (the remark *The Conormal Bundle* in [[§37 Regular Submanifolds|§37]]): Uribe's “if we had a metric \ldots this would be the normal space” (the remark *No Normal Vectors Without a Metric* in [[§37 Regular Submanifolds|§37]]) is realized here by the dot product of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and the remark *Normal and Conormal* below makes the identification explicit. That the projection to $M$ is a fibration (Proposition [[§40 The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space#^prop-40-2|§40.2]]), and what focal points mean geometrically (the remark *Focal Points Are Centres of Curvature* below), are added.

*Setting.* Throughout this subsection $M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is a regular submanifold of dimension $m < n$, that is, of codimension $n - m$ (Definition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^def-37-2|§37.2]]), and $v \cdot w = \sum_l v^l w^l$ is the dot product of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Tangent spaces are the geometric ones: for $p \in M$, $T^{\mathrm{geo}}_pM \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is the set of velocities $\gamma'(0)$ of smooth curves $\gamma$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $\gamma(0) = p$ that stay in $M$ (Definition [[§25 The Geometric Tangent Space#^def-25-1|§25.1]], whose formula makes sense verbatim for any subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$). This is the assignment's “$T_pM$ identified with the ambient tangent space”; its identification with $\iota_{\ast p}(T_pM)$ is the remark *The Tangent Spaces Match* in [[§37 Regular Submanifolds|§37]]. It is computed in an adapted chart, as follows. Let $(U, \varphi = (x^1, \ldots, x^n))$ be a smooth chart of $\mathbb{R}^n$ adapted to $M$ (Definition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^def-37-3|§37.3]]), so that

$$
U \cap M = \{\, q \in U : x^{m+1}(q) = \cdots = x^n(q) = 0 \,\},
$$

and put

$$
G = (G^1, \ldots, G^{n-m}) = (x^{m+1}, \ldots, x^n) : U \to \mathbb{R}^{n-m}, \qquad J(q) = G'(q) \quad (q \in U),
$$

the $(n-m) \times n$ Jacobian of $G$ in the standard coordinates of $\mathbb{R}^n$, whose $s$-th row is the gradient $\nabla G^s(q)$ (Definition [[§7 The Regular Value Theorem#^def-7-1|§7.1]]). Every point of $M$ lies in such a chart (Definition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^def-37-2|§37.2]]). Two facts are used throughout.

1. *$\varphi'(q)$ is invertible and $J(q)$ has rank $n - m$, for every $q \in U$.* The chart $\varphi : U \to \varphi(U)$ is a diffeomorphism of open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (Propositions [[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^prop-28-9|§28.9]] and [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-4|§19.4]]), and the chain rule applied to $\varphi \circ \varphi^{-1} = \mathrm{id}_{\varphi(U)}$ at $\varphi(q)$ gives

$$
\varphi'(q)\, (\varphi^{-1})'\big(\varphi(q)\big) = I_n ,
$$

so $\varphi'(q)$ is invertible, with inverse $(\varphi^{-1})'(\varphi(q))$. The rows of an invertible matrix are linearly independent, and row $l$ of $\varphi'(q)$ is $\nabla x^l(q)$ (Definition [[§7 The Regular Value Theorem#^def-7-1|§7.1]]); the rows of $J(q)$ are the last $n - m$ of these, $\nabla x^{m+1}(q), \ldots, \nabla x^n(q)$, so they are independent and $J(q)$ has rank $n - m$ (Definition [[§7 The Regular Value Theorem#^def-7-2|§7.2]]). This is the computation in the proof of Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-9|§37.9]] (slice $\Rightarrow$ external, with $m$ and $n - m$ in place of $n$ and $k$).
2. *For every $q \in U \cap M$,*

$$
T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM = T^{\mathrm{geo}}_q(U \cap M) = \ker J(q), \qquad \dim T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM = m. \tag{$\sharp$}
$$

By (1), $\vec 0$ is a regular value of $G$ on the open set $U$ (Definition [[§7 The Regular Value Theorem#^def-7-4|§7.4]]), and $U \cap M = G^{-1}(\vec 0)$ by the displayed description of $U \cap M$. The first equality is Lemma [[§25 The Geometric Tangent Space#^lem-25-1|§25.1]], since $U \cap M$ is open in $M$ (its proof uses only that $U \cap M$ is open in the subset $M$ of the Euclidean space). The second, and the dimension, are Theorem [[§25 The Geometric Tangent Space#^thm-25-3|§25.3]] for the regular level set $U \cap M = G^{-1}(\vec 0)$, with $W = U$, $F = G$, $c = \vec 0$ and $m$, $n - m$ in place of $n$, $k$: the kernel of the surjective $J(q) : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^{n-m}$ has dimension $n - (n - m) = m$ (Proposition [[§21 Linear Algebra Toolkit#^prop-21-1|§21.1]](1)).

> [!definition] Definition §40.1: Normal Bundle
> Let $M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a regular submanifold of dimension $m < n$. For $p \in M$ write
>
> $$
> (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_pM)^\perp = \{\, v \in \mathbb{R}^n : v \cdot w = 0 \text{ for all } w \in T^{\mathrm{geo}}_pM \,\}
> $$
>
> for the orthogonal complement of the geometric tangent space with respect to the dot product. The **normal bundle** of $M$ is
>
> $$
> \mathcal{N} = \{\, (p, v) \in \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n : p \in M \text{ and } v \in (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_pM)^\perp \,\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{2n},
> $$
>
> with the **projection** $\pi : \mathcal{N} \to M$, $\pi(p, v) = p$. Its fibre over $p$ is $\pi^{-1}(p) = \{p\} \times (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_pM)^\perp$, a copy of the normal space at $p$.
>
> *Lee: Ch. 6, Tubular Neighborhoods*

^def-40-1

> [!theorem] Proposition §40.1: The Normal Bundle Is a Submanifold
> $\mathcal{N}$ is a regular submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^{2n} = \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n$ of codimension $n$, hence a smooth manifold of dimension $n$ with the induced structure (Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-3|§37.3]]).
>
> *Lee: Ch. 6, Tubular Neighborhoods*

^prop-40-1

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Assignment 5, Problem 5(a); the student's route, following the hint. Two steps are completed: that the first $m$ coordinate vectors span the tangent space is proved from $(\sharp)$, and the Jacobian, which the hint computes in the coordinates $(x, v)$, is converted to the standard coordinates of $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$, so that the external description of [[§20 Manifolds in Euclidean Space|§20]] applies.)* By the last statement of Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-9|§37.9]], applied to $X = \mathcal{N} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{2n} = \mathbb{R}^{n+k}$ with $k = n$, it suffices to describe $\mathcal{N}$ externally near each of its points (Definition [[§20 Manifolds in Euclidean Space#^def-20-1|§20.1]]): for each $(p_0, v_0) \in \mathcal{N}$ we need an open $W \ni (p_0, v_0)$ in $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ and a smooth $F : W \to \mathbb{R}^n$ having $\vec 0$ as a regular value with $\mathcal{N} \cap W = F^{-1}(\vec 0)$. Fix $(p_0, v_0) \in \mathcal{N}$. Then $p_0 \in M$, so there is an adapted chart $(U, \varphi = (x^1, \ldots, x^n))$ at $p_0$ as in the Setting; put $W = U \times \mathbb{R}^n$, open in $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ as a product of open sets, and containing $(p_0, v_0)$.
>
> *Step 1: the vector fields $X_j$.* For $q \in U$ and $j = 1, \ldots, n$ put
>
> $$
> X_j(q) = \frac{\partial \varphi^{-1}}{\partial r^j}\big(\varphi(q)\big) \in \mathbb{R}^n ,
> $$
>
> the $j$-th column of the Jacobian $(\varphi^{-1})'(\varphi(q))$, with components $X_j^l(q)$, $l = 1, \ldots, n$. This is the hint's coordinate field $\partial/\partial x^j$ “regarded as a vector field on $U$ in the sense of Calc III”: the velocity of the $j$-th coordinate line $t \mapsto \varphi^{-1}(\varphi(q) + t e_j)$ at $t = 0$, by the chain rule. Each $X_j^l$ is smooth on $U$, as the composite of the smooth $\varphi$ with the partial derivative $\partial(\varphi^{-1})^l/\partial r^j$ of the smooth map $\varphi^{-1}$.
>
> 1. [(i)] *$X_1(q), \ldots, X_n(q)$ form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and $\varphi'(q)\, X_j(q) = e_j$.* By fact (1) of the Setting, $(\varphi^{-1})'(\varphi(q))$ is the inverse of $\varphi'(q)$; so it is invertible, its columns $X_1(q), \ldots, X_n(q)$ form a basis, and $\varphi'(q)\, X_j(q)$, the $j$-th column of $\varphi'(q)\,(\varphi^{-1})'(\varphi(q)) = I_n$, is $e_j$.
> 2. [(ii)] *For $q \in U \cap M$, $X_1(q), \ldots, X_m(q)$ form a basis of $T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM$.* The rows of $J(q)$ are the last $n - m$ rows of $\varphi'(q)$ (fact (1) of the Setting), so $J(q)\, X_j(q)$ consists of the last $n - m$ entries of $\varphi'(q)\, X_j(q) = e_j$, by (i). For $j \le m$ these entries are all $0$, so $X_j(q) \in \ker J(q) = T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM$ by $(\sharp)$. The $m$ vectors $X_1(q), \ldots, X_m(q)$ are linearly independent, as part of the basis in (i), and lie in the $m$-dimensional space $T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM$ $(\sharp)$; $m$ independent vectors in an $m$-dimensional space form a basis. So
>
> $$
> T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM = \operatorname{Span}\{X_1(q), \ldots, X_m(q)\}.
> $$
>
> The submitted solution quotes this span from the computation of Problem 2 for the inclusion of a regular submanifold in an adapted chart: $\iota_{\ast q}(T_qM) = \operatorname{Span}\{\partial/\partial x^1|_q, \ldots, \partial/\partial x^m|_q\}$ (Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-6|§37.6]]), read in $\mathbb{R}^n$ through the remark *The Tangent Spaces Match* in [[§37 Regular Submanifolds|§37]].
>
> *Step 2: the map $F$.* Define $F : W = U \times \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ by
>
> $$
> F(q, v) = \big( x^{m+1}(q), \ldots, x^n(q),\ y^1(q, v), \ldots, y^m(q, v) \big), \qquad y^j(q, v) = X_j(q) \cdot v = \sum_{l=1}^n X_j^l(q)\, v^l .
> $$
>
> It is smooth: its first $n - m$ components are components of the smooth chart $\varphi$, and each $y^j$ is a sum of products of the smooth functions $X_j^l(q)$ and the coordinates $v^l$.
>
> *Step 3: $F^{-1}(\vec 0) = \mathcal{N} \cap W$.* For $(q, v) \in W$,
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}F(q, v) = \vec 0 &\iff x^{m+s}(q) = 0 \ (1 \le s \le n - m) \ \text{ and } \ X_j(q) \cdot v = 0 \ (1 \le j \le m) \\
> &\iff q \in U \cap M \ \text{ and } \ X_j(q) \cdot v = 0 \ (1 \le j \le m) \\
> &\iff q \in U \cap M \ \text{ and } \ v \in (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM)^\perp \\
> &\iff (q, v) \in \mathcal{N} \cap W .\end{aligned}
> $$
>
> The first equivalence is the definition of $F$. The second is the adapted-chart description $U \cap M = \{x^{m+1} = \cdots = x^n = 0\}$, with $q \in U$. For the third, $q \in U \cap M$, so $T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM = \operatorname{Span}\{X_1(q), \ldots, X_m(q)\}$ by Step 1(ii); a vector $v$ is orthogonal to every element $\sum_j c_j X_j(q)$ of this span if and only if it is orthogonal to each $X_j(q)$, because $v \cdot \sum_j c_j X_j(q) = \sum_j c_j\, \big(v \cdot X_j(q)\big)$ by linearity of the dot product (“if” by this formula, “only if” by taking $c = e_j$). The fourth is Definition [[§40 The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space#^def-40-1|§40.1]], together with $q \in U$ and $W = U \times \mathbb{R}^n$.
>
> *Step 4: the Jacobian in the coordinates $(x, v)$ (the submitted computation).* On $W$ use the coordinates $(x, v) = (x^1(q), \ldots, x^n(q), v^1, \ldots, v^n)$, i.e. the map $\varphi \times \mathrm{id} : W \to \varphi(U) \times \mathbb{R}^n$, $(q, v) \mapsto (\varphi(q), v)$, a bijection with inverse $\varphi^{-1} \times \mathrm{id}$; in them the $x$'s and the $v$'s are independent variables. The coordinate representation of $F$ is $\tilde F = F \circ (\varphi^{-1} \times \mathrm{id}) : \varphi(U) \times \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$,
>
> $$
> \tilde F(r, v) = \Big( r^{m+1}, \ldots, r^n,\ \tilde X_1(r) \cdot v, \ldots, \tilde X_m(r) \cdot v \Big), \qquad \tilde X_j(r) = \frac{\partial \varphi^{-1}}{\partial r^j}(r),
> $$
>
> using $x^{m+s}(\varphi^{-1}(r)) = r^{m+s}$ and $X_j(\varphi^{-1}(r)) = \tilde X_j(r)$. Its partial derivatives at $(r, v)$, for $1 \le i, l \le n$, are as follows.
> - The component $r^{m+s}$ ($1 \le s \le n - m$) depends only on $r$, and is the $(m+s)$-th coordinate:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial r^{m+s}}{\partial r^i} = \delta^{m+s}_i, \qquad \frac{\partial r^{m+s}}{\partial v^l} = 0 .
> $$
>
> - The component $\tilde y^j(r, v) = \tilde X_j(r) \cdot v = \sum_l \tilde X_j^l(r)\, v^l$: differentiating each term by the product rule, with $v$ independent of $r$ and $\tilde X_j$ independent of $v$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial \tilde y^j}{\partial r^i} = \sum_{l=1}^n v^l\, \frac{\partial^2 (\varphi^{-1})^l}{\partial r^i\, \partial r^j}(r), \qquad \frac{\partial \tilde y^j}{\partial v^l} = \tilde X_j^l(r) .
> $$
>
> Grouping the columns as $(r^1, \ldots, r^m)$, $(r^{m+1}, \ldots, r^n)$, $(v^1, \ldots, v^n)$, the $n \times 2n$ Jacobian at $(\varphi(q), v)$ is therefore
>
> $$
> \tilde F'\big(\varphi(q), v\big) = \begin{pmatrix} 0_{(n-m) \times m} & I_{n-m} & 0_{(n-m) \times n} \\ \ast & \ast & X(q) \end{pmatrix},
> $$
>
> where the entries $\ast$ are the second-derivative sums above, and $X(q)$ is the $m \times n$ matrix whose $j$-th row is $X_j(q)^{\mathsf T} = \big(X_j^1(q), \ldots, X_j^n(q)\big)$ (recall $\tilde X_j(\varphi(q)) = X_j(q)$).
>
> *It has rank $n$.* Write the $s$-th of the first $n - m$ rows as $(0, e_s, 0)$, with $e_s \in \mathbb{R}^{n-m}$, and the $j$-th of the last $m$ rows as $(\ast, \ast, X_j(q)^{\mathsf T})$, and suppose a linear combination of the rows vanishes:
>
> $$
> \sum_{s=1}^{n-m} c_s\, (0, e_s, 0) + \sum_{j=1}^m b_j\, \big(\ast, \ast, X_j(q)^{\mathsf T}\big) = 0, \qquad c_s, b_j \in \mathbb{R}.
> $$
>
> In the last $n$ columns the first $n - m$ rows have zeros, so these columns give $\sum_j b_j X_j(q)^{\mathsf T} = 0$; since $X_1(q), \ldots, X_m(q)$ are linearly independent (Step 1(i)), $b_1 = \cdots = b_m = 0$. With all $b_j = 0$, columns $m+1, \ldots, n$ give $\sum_s c_s e_s = 0$, so $c_1 = \cdots = c_{n-m} = 0$. So the $n$ rows are linearly independent, and $\tilde F'(\varphi(q), v)$ has rank $n$ (Definition [[§7 The Regular Value Theorem#^def-7-2|§7.2]]).
>
> *Step 5: back to the standard coordinates.* On $W$, $F = \tilde F \circ (\varphi \times \mathrm{id})$, and $\varphi \times \mathrm{id}$ is smooth with the block-diagonal Jacobian
>
> $$
> (\varphi \times \mathrm{id})'(q, v) = \begin{pmatrix} \varphi'(q) & 0 \\ 0 & I_n \end{pmatrix},
> $$
>
> which is invertible, with inverse $\operatorname{diag}\big((\varphi^{-1})'(\varphi(q)), I_n\big)$, by fact (1) of the Setting. By the chain rule,
>
> $$
> F'(q, v) = \tilde F'\big(\varphi(q), v\big)\, (\varphi \times \mathrm{id})'(q, v) .
> $$
>
> Multiplying on the right by an invertible matrix does not change the image: $\operatorname{im}(AB) = A(\operatorname{im} B) = A(\mathbb{R}^{2n}) = \operatorname{im} A$ when $B$ is onto. So $F'(q, v)$ has the same image as $\tilde F'(\varphi(q), v)$, which is all of $\mathbb{R}^n$ by Step 4. Thus every point of $W$ is a regular point of $F$ (Definition [[§7 The Regular Value Theorem#^def-7-3|§7.3]]), and in particular $\vec 0$ is a regular value of $F$ on $W$ (Definition [[§7 The Regular Value Theorem#^def-7-4|§7.4]], applied on the open set $W$ as in Corollary [[§7 The Regular Value Theorem#^cor-7-4|§7.4]]).
>
> *Step 6: conclusion.* By Steps 2, 3 and 5, $W$ is an open neighbourhood of $(p_0, v_0)$ in $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$, $F : W \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is smooth with $\vec 0$ as a regular value, and $\mathcal{N} \cap W = F^{-1}(\vec 0)$: $\mathcal{N}$ is described externally near $(p_0, v_0)$ (Definition [[§20 Manifolds in Euclidean Space#^def-20-1|§20.1]], with $n + k = 2n$ and $k = n$). As $(p_0, v_0) \in \mathcal{N}$ was arbitrary, Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-9|§37.9]] gives that $\mathcal{N}$ is a regular submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ of codimension $n$, and by Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-3|§37.3]] it is a smooth manifold of dimension $2n - n = n$.
>
> *The submitted ending.* The solution as submitted concludes without leaving the coordinates $(x, v)$. By Theorem [[§30 The Differential in Coordinates#^thm-30-2|§30.2]], $\tilde F'(\varphi(q), v)$ is the matrix of $F_{\ast(q,v)}$, so by Step 4 $F$ is a submersion on the open submanifold $W$ of $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$, and the regular value theorem for manifolds (Theorem [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^thm-37-12|§37.12]]) makes $F^{-1}(\vec 0) = \mathcal{N} \cap W$ a regular submanifold of $W$ of codimension $n$. An adapted chart $(W', \theta)$ of $W$ at $(p_0, v_0)$ is a smooth chart of $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ (Proposition [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^prop-17-7|§17.7]](2)), and since $W' \subseteq W$,
>
> $$
> W' \cap \mathcal{N} = W' \cap \mathcal{N} \cap W = W' \cap F^{-1}(\vec 0) ,
> $$
>
> so it is adapted to $\mathcal{N}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ (Definition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^def-37-3|§37.3]]), with $n$ vanishing coordinates. The two endings are the same argument: Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-9|§37.9]] packages the passage from a regular level set to an adapted chart.

> [!theorem] Proposition §40.2: The Normal Bundle Is a Fibration
> Let $(U, \varphi = (x^1, \ldots, x^n))$ be a chart of $\mathbb{R}^n$ adapted to $M$, with $G = (x^{m+1}, \ldots, x^n)$ and $J(q) = G'(q)$ as in the Setting, and let $\mathcal{N}$ carry the smooth structure of Proposition [[§40 The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space#^prop-40-1|§40.1]].
>
> 1. For $q \in U \cap M$ the gradients $\nabla G^1(q), \ldots, \nabla G^{n-m}(q)$, the rows of $J(q)$, form a basis of $(T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM)^\perp$; and for every $q \in U$ the $(n-m) \times (n-m)$ matrix $J(q) J(q)^{\mathsf T}$ is invertible.
> 2. The map
>
> $$
> \Phi_U : \pi^{-1}(U \cap M) \longrightarrow (U \cap M) \times \mathbb{R}^{n-m}, \qquad \Phi_U(q, v) = \Big(q,\ \big(J(q) J(q)^{\mathsf T}\big)^{-1} J(q)\, v\Big),
> $$
>
> is a diffeomorphism, with inverse $(q, c) \mapsto \big(q, J(q)^{\mathsf T} c\big)$, and $\mathrm{pr}_1 \circ \Phi_U = \pi|_{\pi^{-1}(U \cap M)}$. On each fibre $\pi^{-1}(q) = \{q\} \times (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM)^\perp$ it is a linear isomorphism $(T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM)^\perp \to \mathbb{R}^{n-m}$.
> 3. $\pi : \mathcal{N} \to M$ is a fibration with fibre $\mathbb{R}^{n-m}$ (Definition [[§38 Fibrations#^def-38-1|§38.1]]), the maps $\Phi_U$ of (2), over all adapted charts, being local trivializations.

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from the assignment; filled in.)* Throughout, $M$ carries its induced structure as a regular submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^n$, $U \cap M$ is open in $M$ (subspace topology, $U$ being open in $\mathbb{R}^n$) and is an open submanifold of $M$ (Definition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^def-37-1|§37.1]]), and $(U \cap M) \times \mathbb{R}^{n-m}$ is a product manifold (Definition [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^def-19-7|§19.7]]).
>
> *$\pi$ is smooth, and $\pi^{-1}(U \cap M)$.* Let $\iota_{\mathcal{N}} : \mathcal{N} \to \mathbb{R}^{2n}$ be the inclusion, smooth by Lemma [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^lem-37-5|§37.5]](1), and $\mathrm{pr} : \mathbb{R}^{2n} \to \mathbb{R}^n$, $\mathrm{pr}(p, v) = p$, which is linear, hence smooth. Then $\pi = \mathrm{pr} \circ \iota_{\mathcal{N}}$ as maps into $\mathbb{R}^n$, smooth as a composite (Lemma [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^lem-19-5|§19.5]]); its values lie in $M$ by Definition [[§40 The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space#^def-40-1|§40.1]], so $\pi$ is smooth as a map into $M$ (Lemma [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^lem-37-5|§37.5]](2)). Next,
>
> $$
> \mathcal{N}_U := \pi^{-1}(U \cap M) = \{\, (q, v) \in \mathcal{N} : q \in U \,\} = \mathcal{N} \cap (U \times \mathbb{R}^n),
> $$
>
> the first equality because $\pi(q, v) = q$ and $q \in M$ for every $(q, v) \in \mathcal{N}$. It is open in $\mathcal{N}$, being the intersection of $\mathcal{N}$ with an open set of $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$, and is an open submanifold of $\mathcal{N}$.
>
> *(1) The gradients span the normal space.* Fix $q \in U \cap M$. *They are normal:* for $w \in T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM = \ker J(q)$ $(\sharp)$, the $s$-th entry of $J(q)\, w$ is $\nabla G^s(q) \cdot w$ (the $s$-th row of $J(q)$ is $\nabla G^s(q)$, Definition [[§7 The Regular Value Theorem#^def-7-1|§7.1]]), and $J(q)\, w = 0$; so $\nabla G^s(q) \cdot w = 0$, and $\nabla G^s(q) \in (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM)^\perp$. *They are independent:* $J(q)$ has rank $n - m$ (fact (1) of the Setting), so its $n - m$ rows are linearly independent (Definition [[§7 The Regular Value Theorem#^def-7-2|§7.2]]). *The normal space has dimension $n - m$:* choose a basis $w_1, \ldots, w_m$ of $T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM$ ($\dim = m$ by $(\sharp)$), and let $B$ be the $m \times n$ matrix with rows $w_1^{\mathsf T}, \ldots, w_m^{\mathsf T}$. A vector is orthogonal to $T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM$ if and only if it is orthogonal to each $w_i$ (linearity of the dot product, as in Step 3 of the previous proof), that is, iff $Bv = 0$; so $(T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM)^\perp = \ker B$. The rows of $B$ are independent, so $B$ has rank $m$, and rank–nullity (Proposition [[§21 Linear Algebra Toolkit#^prop-21-1|§21.1]](1)) gives $\dim \ker B = n - m$. So the $n - m$ independent vectors $\nabla G^s(q)$ lie in the $(n-m)$-dimensional space $(T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM)^\perp$, and form a basis of it. In matrix form, since $J(q)^{\mathsf T} c = \sum_s c^s\, \nabla G^s(q)$ for $c = (c^1, \ldots, c^{n-m}) \in \mathbb{R}^{n-m}$ (the columns of $J(q)^{\mathsf T}$ are the rows of $J(q)$):
>
> $$
> (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM)^\perp = \{\, J(q)^{\mathsf T} c : c \in \mathbb{R}^{n-m} \,\}, \qquad c \mapsto J(q)^{\mathsf T} c \ \text{ injective}. \tag{$\flat$}
> $$
>
> *The Gram matrix.* Now let $q \in U$ be arbitrary; $J(q)$ still has rank $n - m$, so $c \mapsto J(q)^{\mathsf T} c$ is injective (its columns are the independent rows of $J(q)$). If $J(q) J(q)^{\mathsf T} c = 0$, then
>
> $$
> 0 = c \cdot \big(J(q) J(q)^{\mathsf T} c\big) = c^{\mathsf T} J(q) J(q)^{\mathsf T} c = \big(J(q)^{\mathsf T} c\big) \cdot \big(J(q)^{\mathsf T} c\big) = \big|J(q)^{\mathsf T} c\big|^2 ,
> $$
>
> so $J(q)^{\mathsf T} c = 0$, and $c = 0$ by injectivity. A square matrix with zero kernel is invertible (Proposition [[§21 Linear Algebra Toolkit#^prop-21-1|§21.1]](1)). ($J J^{\mathsf T}$ is the Gram matrix of the gradients: its $(s, s')$ entry is $\nabla G^s(q) \cdot \nabla G^{s'}(q)$.)
>
> *(2) The map and its inverse.* For $q \in U$ put $A(q) = \big(J(q) J(q)^{\mathsf T}\big)^{-1} J(q)$, an $(n-m) \times n$ matrix. Its entries are smooth functions on $U$: the entries of $J(q)$ are partial derivatives $\partial x^{m+s}/\partial r^l(q)$ of components of the smooth chart $\varphi$; those of $J J^{\mathsf T}$ are sums of products of these; and by the cofactor formula $(J J^{\mathsf T})^{-1} = \operatorname{adj}(J J^{\mathsf T}) / \det(J J^{\mathsf T})$, each entry of the inverse is a polynomial in the entries of $J J^{\mathsf T}$ divided by $\det(J J^{\mathsf T})$, which is smooth and, by (1), nowhere zero on $U$. Define
>
> $$
> \Phi_U(q, v) = \big(q, A(q)\, v\big) \quad \big((q, v) \in \mathcal{N}_U\big), \qquad \Theta_U(q, c) = \big(q, J(q)^{\mathsf T} c\big) \quad \big((q, c) \in (U \cap M) \times \mathbb{R}^{n-m}\big).
> $$
>
> *Targets.* For $(q, v) \in \mathcal{N}_U$, $q \in U \cap M$, so $\Phi_U(q, v) \in (U \cap M) \times \mathbb{R}^{n-m}$. For $q \in U \cap M$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}^{n-m}$, $J(q)^{\mathsf T} c \in (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM)^\perp$ by $(\flat)$, so $(q, J(q)^{\mathsf T} c) \in \mathcal{N}$, and it lies in $\mathcal{N}_U$ since $q \in U$.
>
> *Mutually inverse.* For $(q, c) \in (U \cap M) \times \mathbb{R}^{n-m}$,
>
> $$
> \Phi_U\big(\Theta_U(q, c)\big) = \Big(q,\ \big(J J^{\mathsf T}\big)^{-1} J\, J^{\mathsf T} c\Big) = \Big(q,\ \big(J J^{\mathsf T}\big)^{-1} \big(J J^{\mathsf T}\big) c\Big) = (q, c),
> $$
>
> with $J = J(q)$. For $(q, v) \in \mathcal{N}_U$, $v \in (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM)^\perp$, so by $(\flat)$ $v = J^{\mathsf T} c$ for some $c \in \mathbb{R}^{n-m}$, and
>
> $$
> \Theta_U\big(\Phi_U(q, v)\big) = \Big(q,\ J^{\mathsf T} \big(J J^{\mathsf T}\big)^{-1} J\, v\Big) = \Big(q,\ J^{\mathsf T} \big(J J^{\mathsf T}\big)^{-1} \big(J J^{\mathsf T}\big) c\Big) = \big(q, J^{\mathsf T} c\big) = (q, v).
> $$
>
> So $\Phi_U$ is a bijection with inverse $\Theta_U$.
>
> *Commuting triangle and linearity on fibres.* $\mathrm{pr}_1\big(\Phi_U(q, v)\big) = q = \pi(q, v)$ for every $(q, v) \in \mathcal{N}_U$. On the fibre $\pi^{-1}(q) = \{q\} \times (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_qM)^\perp$, $\Phi_U$ is $v \mapsto A(q)\, v$ in the second component, a linear map; it is a bijection onto $\mathbb{R}^{n-m}$, because $\Phi_U$ and $\Theta_U$ are mutually inverse and both keep the first component $q$, so that $c \mapsto J(q)^{\mathsf T} c$ is its inverse.
>
> *$\Phi_U$ is smooth.* Let $\varphi_M = (x^1, \ldots, x^m)|_{U \cap M}$ be the induced chart of $M$ (Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-3|§37.3]]); its domain is all of $U \cap M$, so it is also a chart of the open submanifold $U \cap M$ (Proposition [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^prop-17-7|§17.7]](2)), with image $V_M = \varphi_M(U \cap M)$ open in $\mathbb{R}^m$. With the identity chart of $\mathbb{R}^{n-m}$ it gives the product chart $\big((U \cap M) \times \mathbb{R}^{n-m}, \varphi_M \times \mathrm{id}\big)$, whose domain is the whole product (Proposition [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-10|§19.10]]); so $\varphi_M \times \mathrm{id} : (U \cap M) \times \mathbb{R}^{n-m} \to V_M \times \mathbb{R}^{n-m}$ is a diffeomorphism (Proposition [[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^prop-28-9|§28.9]]). Now
>
> $$
> (\varphi_M \times \mathrm{id}) \circ \Phi_U(q, v) = \big(x^1(q), \ldots, x^m(q),\ A(q)\, v\big) = \hat\Phi(q, v), \qquad \hat\Phi : U \times \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m \times \mathbb{R}^{n-m},
> $$
>
> where $\hat\Phi$ is defined by the same formula on all of $U \times \mathbb{R}^n$ and is smooth, its components being components of $\varphi$ and sums of products of the smooth entries of $A(q)$ with the coordinates $v^l$. The inclusion $\iota_U : \mathcal{N}_U \to U \times \mathbb{R}^n$ is smooth: the inclusion $\mathcal{N}_U \to \mathcal{N}$ is smooth (Proposition [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-6|§19.6]](1)), and so is $\iota_{\mathcal{N}}$; their composite $\mathcal{N}_U \to \mathbb{R}^{2n}$ takes values in the open set $U \times \mathbb{R}^n$, hence is smooth into it (Proposition [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-6|§19.6]](3)). So $\hat\Phi \circ \iota_U$ is smooth (Lemma [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^lem-19-5|§19.5]]); it takes values in the open set $V_M \times \mathbb{R}^{n-m}$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$, hence is smooth into it (Proposition [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-6|§19.6]](3)); and
>
> $$
> \Phi_U = (\varphi_M \times \mathrm{id})^{-1} \circ \big(\hat\Phi \circ \iota_U\big)
> $$
>
> is a composite of smooth maps, hence smooth.
>
> *$\Theta_U$ is smooth.* Recall $\varphi_M^{-1}(r) = \varphi^{-1}(r, \vec 0)$ for $r \in V_M$ (the proof of Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-3|§37.3]]). So for $(r, c) \in V_M \times \mathbb{R}^{n-m}$,
>
> $$
> \Theta_U \circ (\varphi_M \times \mathrm{id})^{-1}(r, c) = \Big(\varphi^{-1}(r, \vec 0),\ J\big(\varphi^{-1}(r, \vec 0)\big)^{\mathsf T} c\Big) =: \hat\Theta(r, c) \in \mathbb{R}^{2n},
> $$
>
> and $\hat\Theta : V_M \times \mathbb{R}^{n-m} \to \mathbb{R}^{2n}$ is smooth: $r \mapsto (r, \vec 0)$ is linear with values in $\varphi(U)$ for $r \in V_M$, $\varphi^{-1}$ is smooth on $\varphi(U)$, and the entries of $J$ are smooth on $U$. Hence $\Theta_U = \hat\Theta \circ (\varphi_M \times \mathrm{id})$ is smooth as a map into $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ (Lemma [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^lem-19-5|§19.5]]). Its values lie in $\mathcal{N}$ (*Targets* above), so it is smooth into $\mathcal{N}$ (Lemma [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^lem-37-5|§37.5]](2)), and they lie in the open subset $\mathcal{N}_U$, so it is smooth into $\mathcal{N}_U$ (Proposition [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-6|§19.6]](3)).
>
> So $\Phi_U$ is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse $\Theta_U$, a diffeomorphism (Definition [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^def-19-4|§19.4]]).
>
> *(3) The fibration.* The sets $U \cap M$, over all charts $(U, \varphi)$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ adapted to $M$, are open in $M$ and cover $M$, since every point of $M$ lies in an adapted chart (Definition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^def-37-2|§37.2]]). The projection $\pi$ is smooth (first paragraph), and over each $U \cap M$ the map $\Phi_U$ of (2) is a diffeomorphism $\pi^{-1}(U \cap M) \to (U \cap M) \times \mathbb{R}^{n-m}$ with $\mathrm{pr}_1 \circ \Phi_U = \pi|_{\pi^{-1}(U \cap M)}$. This is Definition [[§38 Fibrations#^def-38-1|§38.1]] with total space $\mathcal{N}$, base $M$, fibre $\mathbb{R}^{n-m}$ and the cover $\{U \cap M\}$.

The trivialization is the recipe of the remark *Where Explicit Trivializations Come From* at the start of this section: a basis of each fibre that depends smoothly on the base point — here the gradients $\nabla G^s(q)$ — and, for a vector $v$ of the fibre, its components $c = (J J^{\mathsf T})^{-1} J v$ in that basis, $v = \sum_s c^s\, \nabla G^s(q)$. The gradients are the vectors that the dot product attaches to the covectors $dG^s|_q = dx^{m+s}|_q$: for $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $dG^s|_q(D_w|_q) = D_w|_q[G^s] = \nabla G^s(q) \cdot w$ (Proposition [[§32 The Cotangent Space#^prop-32-2|§32.2]] and Proposition [[§30 The Differential in Coordinates#^prop-30-5|§30.5]]). Those covectors form a basis of the conormal space $N_qM$ (Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-8|§37.8]]; for a general defining map, Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-19|§37.19]]). So (1) is the basis of the conormal space, read through the metric; the remark *Normal and Conormal* below says this for the whole bundle.

> [!definition] Definition §40.2: Endpoint Map
> The **endpoint map** of $M$ is
>
> $$
> E : \mathcal{N} \to \mathbb{R}^n, \qquad E(p, v) = p + v ,
> $$
>
> the endpoint of the normal vector $v$ drawn from $p$. It is smooth: $E = L \circ \iota_{\mathcal{N}}$, where $\iota_{\mathcal{N}} : \mathcal{N} \to \mathbb{R}^{2n}$ is the inclusion, smooth by Lemma [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^lem-37-5|§37.5]](1), and $L(p, v) = p + v$ is linear, hence smooth; a composite of smooth maps is smooth (Lemma [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^lem-19-5|§19.5]]).
>
> *Lee: Ch. 6, Tubular Neighborhoods*

> [!definition] Definition §40.3: Focal Point
> A point $q \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is a **focal point** of $M$ if $q = E(p, v)$ for some critical point $(p, v) \in \mathcal{N}$ of the endpoint map $E$ (Definition [[§40 The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space#^def-40-2|§40.2]]), that is, for some $(p, v)$ at which $E_{\ast(p,v)} : T_{(p,v)}\mathcal{N} \to T_q\mathbb{R}^n$ (written $dE_{(p,v)}$ in the assignment) is not of maximal rank. Since $\dim \mathcal{N} = n = \dim \mathbb{R}^n$ (Proposition [[§40 The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space#^prop-40-1|§40.1]]), maximal rank means rank $n$, so $(p, v)$ is critical exactly when $E_{\ast(p,v)}$ is not onto — a critical point in the sense of Definition [[§36 Submersions#^def-36-3|§36.3]] — equivalently (Proposition [[§21 Linear Algebra Toolkit#^prop-21-1|§21.1]](1)) not bijective.

> [!example] Example §40.1: Focal Points of the Parabola
> Let $M = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : y = x^2\}$. The set of focal points of $M$ is the image of the smooth curve
>
> $$
> \mathbf{r} : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2, \qquad \mathbf{r}(a) = \Big(-4a^3,\ 3a^2 + \tfrac12\Big),
> $$
>
> where $\mathbf{r}(a) = E(p, v)$ for the unique critical point $(p, v)$ of $E$ with $p = (a, a^2)$: the focal point on the normal line to the parabola at $(a, a^2)$.

^ex-40-1

> [!proof]+ Working out the example
> *(Assignment 5, Problem 5(b), as submitted; the smoothness of the parametrization and the passage from $E_\ast$ to a Jacobian are made explicit.)* In this example $(x, y)$ are the standard coordinates of $\mathbb{R}^2$, so $n = 2$ and $m = 1$.
>
> *$M$ is a submanifold.* $M = g^{-1}(0)$ for $g(x, y) = y - x^2$, $g : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$, whose Jacobian $g'(x, y) = (-2x, 1)$ is nonzero, i.e. has rank $1$, at every point. So $0$ is a regular value (Definition [[§7 The Regular Value Theorem#^def-7-4|§7.4]]), and $M = g^{-1}(0)$ is an external description of $M$ near each of its points with $W = \mathbb{R}^2$ (Definition [[§20 Manifolds in Euclidean Space#^def-20-1|§20.1]]). By Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-9|§37.9]], $M$ is a regular submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^2$ of codimension $1$, i.e. of dimension $1$; and by Proposition [[§40 The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space#^prop-40-1|§40.1]], $\mathcal{N}$ is a regular submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^4$ of dimension $2$.
>
> *Tangent and normal spaces.* Every $p \in M$ is $p = (a, a^2)$ for a unique $a \in \mathbb{R}$, namely its first coordinate. By Theorem [[§25 The Geometric Tangent Space#^thm-25-3|§25.3]] with $W = \mathbb{R}^2$, $F = g$, $c = 0$,
>
> $$
> T^{\mathrm{geo}}_pM = \ker g'(p) = \{\, (u, w) : -2a\, u + w = 0 \,\} = \{\, (u, 2a\, u) : u \in \mathbb{R} \,\} = \operatorname{Span}\{(1, 2a)\}.
> $$
>
> (The submission finds the same line as the velocity of the curve $t \mapsto (a + t, (a + t)^2)$ in $M$ at $t = 0$, which is $(1, 2a)$, together with $\dim M = 1$.) The vector $(-2a, 1)$ is nonzero and
>
> $$
> (-2a, 1) \cdot (1, 2a) = -2a + 2a = 0 ,
> $$
>
> so it lies in $(T^{\mathrm{geo}}_pM)^\perp$; and $\dim (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_pM)^\perp = 2 - 1 = 1$, as in the proof of Proposition [[§40 The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space#^prop-40-2|§40.2]](1). Hence
>
> $$
> (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_pM)^\perp = \operatorname{Span}\{(-2a, 1)\}, \qquad \mathcal{N} = \Big\{\, \big((a, a^2),\ \lambda(-2a, 1)\big) : a, \lambda \in \mathbb{R} \,\Big\}.
> $$
>
> Note that $(-2a, 1) = \nabla g(p)$, as Proposition [[§40 The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space#^prop-40-2|§40.2]](1) predicts: $\varphi(x, y) = (x, y - x^2)$ is a chart of $\mathbb{R}^2$ adapted to $M$ with $U = \mathbb{R}^2$ and $G = g$ (the chart $\Phi$ of the proof of Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-9|§37.9]], graph $\Rightarrow$ slice, for $h(x) = x^2$).
>
> *A global parametrization of $\mathcal{N}$.* Define
>
> $$
> \Psi : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathcal{N}, \qquad \Psi(a, \lambda) = \big((a, a^2),\ \lambda(-2a, 1)\big) = \big(a,\ a^2,\ -2\lambda a,\ \lambda\big).
> $$
>
> - *Smooth.* As a map into $\mathbb{R}^4$ its components are polynomials, so it is smooth; its values lie in $\mathcal{N}$ by the description of $\mathcal{N}$ just found, so it is smooth into $\mathcal{N}$ (Lemma [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^lem-37-5|§37.5]](2)).
> - *Bijective.* It is onto $\mathcal{N}$ by that description. It is injective: if $\Psi(a, \lambda) = \Psi(a', \lambda')$, the first coordinates give $a = a'$ and the fourth give $\lambda = \lambda'$.
> - *Its differential is bijective.* The Jacobian of $\Psi$ as a map into $\mathbb{R}^4$,
>
> $$
> D\Psi(a, \lambda) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2a & 0 \\ -2\lambda & -2a \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
> $$
>
> has rank $2$, since its first and fourth rows form $I_2$; so $D\Psi(a, \lambda) u = 0$ forces $u = 0$. With $\iota_{\mathcal{N}} : \mathcal{N} \to \mathbb{R}^4$ the inclusion, $\iota_{\mathcal{N}} \circ \Psi$ is $\Psi$ regarded as a map into $\mathbb{R}^4$, and its differential corresponds to $D\Psi(a, \lambda)$ (Proposition [[§30 The Differential in Coordinates#^prop-30-5|§30.5]]). By the Chain Rule (Theorem [[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^thm-28-6|§28.6]]), $(\iota_{\mathcal{N}})_{\ast} \circ \Psi_{\ast(a,\lambda)} = (\iota_{\mathcal{N}} \circ \Psi)_{\ast(a,\lambda)}$, which is injective; so $\Psi_{\ast(a,\lambda)}$ is injective (if $\Psi_{\ast}u = 0$ then $(\iota_{\mathcal{N}} \circ \Psi)_{\ast}u = 0$, so $u = 0$). It is a linear map between spaces of the same dimension $2$, $T_{(a,\lambda)}\mathbb{R}^2$ and $T_{\Psi(a,\lambda)}\mathcal{N}$ (Theorem [[§29 Coordinate Derivations and the Basis Theorem#^thm-29-9|§29.9]]), hence bijective (Proposition [[§21 Linear Algebra Toolkit#^prop-21-1|§21.1]](1)).
>
> By Corollary [[§35 Local Diffeomorphisms#^cor-35-3|§35.3]] (a smooth bijection with bijective differential everywhere; this is where the inverse function theorem, Theorem [[§35 Local Diffeomorphisms#^thm-35-1|§35.1]], enters), $\Psi : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathcal{N}$ is a diffeomorphism, and $\Psi^{-1}$ is a global chart of $\mathcal{N}$: its transition maps with any smooth chart $\chi$ of $\mathcal{N}$ are $\chi \circ \Psi$ and $\Psi^{-1} \circ \chi^{-1}$, smooth as composites of smooth maps. (In the language of Proposition [[§40 The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space#^prop-40-2|§40.2]], $\Psi(a, \lambda) = \Theta_U\big((a, a^2), \lambda\big)$ with $G = g$: the normal bundle of the parabola is trivial.)
>
> *The endpoint map in coordinates.* Put
>
> $$
> \hat E = E \circ \Psi : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2, \qquad \hat E(a, \lambda) = (a, a^2) + \lambda(-2a, 1) = \big(a - 2\lambda a,\ a^2 + \lambda\big).
> $$
>
> By the Chain Rule, $\hat E_{\ast(a,\lambda)} = E_{\ast\Psi(a,\lambda)} \circ \Psi_{\ast(a,\lambda)}$. Since $\Psi_{\ast(a,\lambda)}$ is a bijection onto $T_{\Psi(a,\lambda)}\mathcal{N}$, the two maps $\hat E_{\ast(a,\lambda)}$ and $E_{\ast\Psi(a,\lambda)}$ have the same image; so $E_{\ast\Psi(a,\lambda)}$ is onto iff $\hat E_{\ast(a,\lambda)}$ is onto, iff (Proposition [[§30 The Differential in Coordinates#^prop-30-5|§30.5]]) the $2 \times 2$ Jacobian $D\hat E(a, \lambda)$ has rank $2$, iff $\det D\hat E(a, \lambda) \neq 0$.
>
> *Critical points of $E$.* Differentiating the components $a - 2\lambda a$ and $a^2 + \lambda$ with respect to $a$ and $\lambda$,
>
> $$
> D\hat E(a, \lambda) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - 2\lambda & -2a \\ 2a & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det D\hat E(a, \lambda) = (1 - 2\lambda)\cdot 1 - (-2a)(2a) = 1 - 2\lambda + 4a^2 .
> $$
>
> So $\Psi(a, \lambda)$ is a critical point of $E$ (Definition [[§40 The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space#^def-40-3|§40.3]]) if and only if $1 - 2\lambda + 4a^2 = 0$, that is,
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{4a^2 + 1}{2} .
> $$
>
> For each $a$ there is exactly one such $\lambda$, and every point of $\mathcal{N}$ is $\Psi(a, \lambda)$ for exactly one $(a, \lambda)$; so the critical points of $E$ are exactly the points $\Psi\big(a, \tfrac{4a^2+1}{2}\big)$, one over each $p = (a, a^2) \in M$.
>
> *The focal points.* A point of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is a focal point iff it is $E(\Psi(a, \lambda)) = \hat E(a, \lambda)$ with $\lambda = \frac{4a^2+1}{2}$, for some $a \in \mathbb{R}$. Substituting,
>
> $$
> a - 2\lambda a = a - a(4a^2 + 1) = -4a^3, \qquad a^2 + \lambda = a^2 + 2a^2 + \tfrac12 = 3a^2 + \tfrac12 .
> $$
>
> So the set of focal points is the image of $\mathbf{r}(a) = \big(-4a^3, 3a^2 + \frac12\big)$, a smooth curve, its components being polynomials in $a$; and $\mathbf{r}(a) = E(p, v)$ for the critical point over $p = (a, a^2)$.
>
> *Shape.* $\mathbf{r}(-a) = \big(4a^3, 3a^2 + \frac12\big)$ is the reflection of $\mathbf{r}(a)$ in the $y$-axis, so the curve is symmetric about the axis of the parabola. Its velocity $\mathbf{r}'(a) = (-12a^2, 6a)$ vanishes only at $a = 0$, where the curve has a cusp at $\mathbf{r}(0) = (0, \frac12)$ (the remark *Focal Points Are Centres of Curvature* gives its equation, $(y - \frac12)^3 = \frac{27}{16}x^2$).

> [!remark] Remark: Focal Points Are Centres of Curvature
> *(Not from the assignment.)* At the critical point over $p = (a, a^2)$ the normal vector is $v = \lambda(-2a, 1)$ with $\lambda = \frac{1 + 4a^2}{2}$, of length
>
> $$
> |v| = \lambda\, \big|(-2a, 1)\big| = \frac{1 + 4a^2}{2}\, \sqrt{1 + 4a^2} = \frac{(1 + 4a^2)^{3/2}}{2} .
> $$
>
> This is the radius of curvature of the graph $y = f(x)$ at $x = a$, $R = (1 + f'(a)^2)^{3/2} / |f''(a)|$, for $f(x) = x^2$: $f'(a) = 2a$ and $f''(a) = 2$ give $R = (1 + 4a^2)^{3/2}/2$. And $v$ points to the concave (upper) side, since $\lambda > 0$ and $(-2a, 1)$ has positive second component. So the focal point $p + v$ on the normal line at $p$ is the *centre of curvature*, the centre of the osculating circle at $p$, and the curve $\mathbf{r}$ of focal points is the *evolute* of the parabola. As a check, the classical formula for the centre of curvature of a graph, $\big(a - f'(1 + f'^2)/f'',\ f + (1 + f'^2)/f''\big)$, gives
>
> $$
> \Big(a - \frac{2a(1 + 4a^2)}{2},\ a^2 + \frac{1 + 4a^2}{2}\Big) = \Big(-4a^3,\ 3a^2 + \tfrac12\Big) = \mathbf{r}(a).
> $$
>
> *The evolute as a curve.* From $x = -4a^3$ and $y - \frac12 = 3a^2$: $(y - \frac12)^3 = 27a^6$ and $x^2 = 16a^6$, so
>
> $$
> \Big(y - \tfrac12\Big)^3 = \tfrac{27}{16}\, x^2 ,
> $$
>
> a semicubical parabola with its cusp at $(0, \frac12)$. Conversely every point of this curve is on $\mathbf{r}$: given $(x, y)$ with $(y - \frac12)^3 = \frac{27}{16}x^2$, let $a$ be the real cube root of $-x/4$; then $-4a^3 = x$, and $y - \frac12 = \big(\frac{27}{16}x^2\big)^{1/3} = 3\,(x/4)^{2/3} = 3a^2$. The cusp is the centre of curvature at the vertex, where the radius of curvature is $R = \frac12$: the osculating circle there is $x^2 + (y - \frac12)^2 = \frac14$.
>
> *In general* (stated, not proved here): for a submanifold $M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, $(p, v) \in \mathcal{N}$ is a critical point of $E$ if and only if $1$ is an eigenvalue of the shape operator (second fundamental form) of $M$ at $p$ in the normal direction $v$, and the dimension of $\ker E_{\ast(p,v)}$ is the multiplicity of that eigenvalue (Milnor, *Morse Theory*, §6). For a curve in the plane the shape operator in the direction of a normal $v$ is multiplication by $\kappa\, (v \cdot \mathbf{n})$, with $\kappa$ the curvature and $\mathbf{n}$ the unit normal toward the concave side; so the focal point on each normal line is at distance $|v| = 1/\kappa$ on the concave side, the centre of curvature, as found above. For the parabola, $\kappa = 2/(1 + 4a^2)^{3/2}$.

> [!remark] Remark: Normal and Conormal
> *(Not from the assignment.)* Fix $p \in M$. By Corollary [[§30 The Differential in Coordinates#^cor-30-6|§30.6]] every tangent vector of $\mathbb{R}^n$ at $p$ is $D_w|_p$ for a unique $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$, so the dot product defines a linear map
>
> $$
> \mathbb{R}^n \to T_p^{\ast}\mathbb{R}^n, \qquad v \longmapsto \xi_v, \qquad \xi_v\big(D_w|_p\big) = v \cdot w .
> $$
>
> It is injective, since $\xi_v(D_v|_p) = |v|^2 \neq 0$ for $v \neq 0$, and both spaces have dimension $n$, so it is an isomorphism (Proposition [[§21 Linear Algebra Toolkit#^prop-21-1|§21.1]](1)): the metric identifies vectors with covectors. Under $w \mapsto D_w|_p$ the subspace $\iota_{\ast p}(T_pM)$ is $T^{\mathrm{geo}}_pM$ (the remark *The Tangent Spaces Match* in [[§37 Regular Submanifolds|§37]]; Proposition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-14|§37.14]](2) applied to the level set $U \cap M = G^{-1}(\vec 0)$ of the Setting). So $\xi_v$ lies in the conormal space $N_pM$ (Definition [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^def-37-4|§37.4]]) iff $v \cdot w = 0$ for all $w \in T^{\mathrm{geo}}_pM$, iff $v \in (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_pM)^\perp$:
>
> $$
> (T^{\mathrm{geo}}_pM)^\perp \xrightarrow{\ \cong\ } N_pM, \qquad v \mapsto \xi_v ,
> $$
>
> an isomorphism between spaces of dimension $n - m$ (Propositions [[§40 The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space#^prop-40-2|§40.2]](1) and [[§37 Regular Submanifolds#^prop-37-8|§37.8]]). It sends the gradient $\nabla G^s(p)$ to the differential $dG^s|_p$, as computed after Proposition [[§40 The Normal Bundle of a Submanifold of Euclidean Space#^prop-40-2|§40.2]]. Hence $(p, v) \mapsto (p, \xi_v)$ maps the normal bundle $\mathcal{N}$ bijectively onto the conormal bundle $\{(p, \xi) : p \in M,\ \xi \in N_pM\}$ of the remark *The Conormal Bundle* in [[§37 Regular Submanifolds|§37]], fibre by fibre and linearly on each fibre: $\mathcal{N}$ is the conormal bundle read through the metric of $\mathbb{R}^n$. This is Uribe's remark that with an inner product the conormal space “would be the normal space” (the remark *No Normal Vectors Without a Metric* in [[§37 Regular Submanifolds|§37]]), and it is why both bundles have dimension $n$: $m$ dimensions of base point, plus $n - m$ of normal vector or conormal covector.

- *The tangent and cotangent bundles* $TM \to M$ and $T^{\ast}M \to M$, with fibre $\mathbb{R}^m$, whose trivializations come from charts of $M$ (Corollaries [[§49 The Tangent Bundle#^cor-49-3|§49.3]] and [[§50 The Cotangent Bundle#^cor-50-3|§50.3]]).
- *Two-to-one local diffeomorphisms*: $S^n \to \mathbb{RP}^n$ (Example [[§44 Projective Spaces and the Hopf Fibration#^ex-44-1|§44.1]]) and $\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)$ (Theorem [[§46 SU(2) → SO(3)꞉ The Double Cover#^thm-46-4|§46.4]]). These are fibrations with a two-point fibre (two-sheeted coverings); the notes prove the local-diffeomorphism and two-to-one statements, not the fibration property.
