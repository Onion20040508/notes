*Stage: fields — Vector fields and multiplications by functions generate a ring of operators on $C^\infty(M)$; its elements have an order, and in a chart they are the linear partial differential operators with smooth coefficients.*

*Lecture 15 introduced the ring of differential operators in passing, as an aside to the locality of derivations ([[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-6|Lemma §56.6]]): its definition, the fact that its elements are local, and Peetre's theorem “FYI”. Those are collected here. Everything else in this subsection — the algebra of multiplications and vector fields, the order of an operator, the coordinate form and the commutator estimate in [[§58 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^prop-58-6|§58]] — is not from lecture; it makes precise the remark that differential operators “are given by differential expressions in local coordinates.”*

> [!definition] Definition §57.1: Multiplication Operator
> For $g \in C^\infty(M)$, the **multiplication operator** $m_g : C^\infty(M) \to C^\infty(M)$ is $m_g f = g f$.

^def-57-1

> [!theorem] Proposition §57.1: Multiplication Operators
> Let $g, h \in C^\infty(M)$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $m_g$ ([[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-1|Definition §57.1]]) is an $\mathbb{R}$-linear [[§56 Vector Fields#^def-56-4|operator]], and
> 1. $m_{g + h} = m_g + m_h$, $m_{gh} = m_g \circ m_h$, $m_1 = \mathrm{id}$ and $m_c = c\, \mathrm{id}$ (writing $c$ also for the constant function);
> 2. $m_g$ determines $g$: $g = m_g(1)$, so $m_g = m_h$ only if $g = h$.
>
> So $g \mapsto m_g$ is an injective ring homomorphism from $C^\infty(M)$ into the linear operators, whose image $\{ m_g \}$ is a *commutative* subring: $m_g \circ m_h = m_{gh} = m_{hg} = m_h \circ m_g$.

^prop-57-1

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from lecture; filled in.)* Two operators are equal when they give the same function on every $f \in C^\infty(M)$, and two functions are equal when they agree at every point; operators are added pointwise, $(P + Q)f = Pf + Qf$, and composed by $(P \circ Q)f = P(Qf)$ (Definition [[§56 Vector Fields#^def-56-4|§56.4]] and the paragraph after it). So each claim is checked at a function $f$ and a point $p$.
>
> *$m_g$ is a linear operator.* $m_g f = gf$ is a product of smooth functions, so $m_g$ maps $C^\infty(M)$ to $C^\infty(M)$. For $f_1, f_2 \in C^\infty(M)$ and $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, by distributivity in $\mathbb{R}$,
>
> $$
> \big(m_g(af_1 + bf_2)\big)(p) = g(p)\big(a f_1(p) + b f_2(p)\big) = a\, g(p) f_1(p) + b\, g(p) f_2(p) = \big(a\, m_g f_1 + b\, m_g f_2\big)(p) .
> $$
>
> *(1)* The four identities, by the arithmetic of $\mathbb{R}$ at the point $p$:
>
> $$
> (m_{g+h} f)(p) = \big(g(p) + h(p)\big) f(p) = g(p) f(p) + h(p) f(p) = (m_g f)(p) + (m_h f)(p) = \big((m_g + m_h) f\big)(p),
> $$
>
> $$
> (m_{gh} f)(p) = \big(g(p) h(p)\big) f(p) = g(p)\big(h(p) f(p)\big) = g(p)\,(m_h f)(p) = \big(m_g(m_h f)\big)(p) = \big((m_g \circ m_h) f\big)(p),
> $$
>
> $$
> (m_1 f)(p) = 1 \cdot f(p) = f(p) = (\mathrm{id}\, f)(p), \qquad (m_c f)(p) = c\, f(p) = \big((c\,\mathrm{id}) f\big)(p) .
> $$
>
> *(2)* Applying $m_g$ to the constant function $1$: $m_g(1) = g \cdot 1 = g$. So if $m_g = m_h$, then $g = m_g(1) = m_h(1) = h$.
>
> *The final sentence.* By (1), $g \mapsto m_g$ sends sums to sums, products to composites, and the unit $1$ to the unit $\mathrm{id}$, so it is a ring homomorphism; by (2) it is injective. Its image is commutative because $C^\infty(M)$ is, $gh = hg$ pointwise: by (1), $m_g \circ m_h = m_{gh} = m_{hg} = m_h \circ m_g$.

> [!definition] Definition §57.2: Differential Operators
> The **ring of differential operators** on $M$, written $\mathrm{Diff}(M)$ here, is the subring of the $\mathbb{R}$-linear maps $C^\infty(M) \to C^\infty(M)$ (with sum and composition) generated by
>
> $$
> D_\mathbf{X} \quad (\mathbf{X} \in \mathfrak{X}(M)) \qquad \text{and} \qquad m_g \quad (g \in C^\infty(M)) ,
> $$
>
> the operators of [[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-3|Lemma §56.3]] and [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-1|Definition §57.1]]. Its elements, the **differential operators**, are the finite sums of finite composites of such operators.

^def-57-2

Uribe: “I can take a vector field, apply it … I can compose that with multiplication by a function, or compose it with another vector field, and do finitely many of these things, and add linear combinations of such things.” (The name $\mathrm{Diff}(M)$ is not from lecture.)

> [!theorem] Proposition §57.2: Vector Fields and Multiplication Operators
> Let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ and $g \in C^\infty(M)$. Then
> 1. $m_g \circ D_\mathbf{X} = D_{g\mathbf{X}}$, for the vector field $g\mathbf{X}$ of [[§56 Vector Fields#^def-56-3|Definition §56.3]];
> 2. $D_\mathbf{X} \circ m_g = m_g \circ D_\mathbf{X} + m_{\mathbf{X}g}$, where $\mathbf{X}g = D_\mathbf{X} g$.
>
> In words: a [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-1|multiplication]] can always be moved to the *left* of a vector field, at the cost of the extra term $m_{\mathbf{X}g}$, which contains no vector field.

^prop-57-2

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from lecture; filled in.)* Both parts are equalities of operators on $C^\infty(M)$: two operators are equal when they give the same function on every $f$, and two functions are equal when they agree at every point. So let $f \in C^\infty(M)$ and $p \in M$.
>
> *(1)* $g\mathbf{X}$ is a vector field by Proposition [[§56 Vector Fields#^prop-56-2|§56.2]](4), so $D_{g\mathbf{X}}$ is defined (Lemma [[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-3|§56.3]]). Using in turn Definition [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-1|§57.1]], the definition of $D_\mathbf{X}$ (Lemma [[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-3|§56.3]]), the pointwise scalar multiplication of derivations (Definition [[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^def-28-2|§28.2]]), and Definition [[§56 Vector Fields#^def-56-3|§56.3]]:
>
> $$
> \big((m_g \circ D_\mathbf{X}) f\big)(p) = g(p)\,(D_\mathbf{X} f)(p) = g(p)\, \mathbf{X}_p[f] = \big(g(p)\, \mathbf{X}_p\big)[f] = (g\mathbf{X})_p[f] = (D_{g\mathbf{X}} f)(p) .
> $$
>
> *(2)* By the product rule for the derivation $D_\mathbf{X}$ (Lemma [[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-3|§56.3]]), applied to the product $gf$:
>
> $$
> D_\mathbf{X}(m_g f) = D_\mathbf{X}(gf) = g\, D_\mathbf{X} f + f\, D_\mathbf{X} g = (m_g \circ D_\mathbf{X}) f + m_{\mathbf{X}g} f = \big(m_g \circ D_\mathbf{X} + m_{\mathbf{X}g}\big) f .
> $$
>
> The third equality uses $g\, D_\mathbf{X} f = m_g(D_\mathbf{X} f)$ and $f\, D_\mathbf{X} g = (\mathbf{X}g)\, f = m_{\mathbf{X}g} f$, with $\mathbf{X}g = D_\mathbf{X} g \in C^\infty(M)$; the last is the pointwise sum of operators. As $f$ was arbitrary, $D_\mathbf{X} \circ m_g = m_g \circ D_\mathbf{X} + m_{\mathbf{X}g}$.

> [!example] Example §57.1: Differential Operators in Coordinates
> *(Not from lecture.)* On $M = \mathbb{R}$ with coordinate $x$, write $\partial = D_{\partial/\partial x}$. Then $P = \partial \circ \partial + m_x \circ \partial$ is a differential operator, $Pf = f'' + x f'$, of [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-3|order]] $2$ ([[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-3|Definition §57.3]] below). On $M = \mathbb{R}^2$ the Laplacian $\Delta = \partial_x \circ \partial_x + \partial_y \circ \partial_y$ is one, also of order $2$. Composites can always be rewritten with the multiplications in front, because of the product rule $\partial \circ m_g = m_{\partial g} + m_g \circ \partial$ ([[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-2|Proposition §57.2]] (2)): for instance $\partial \circ m_x \circ \partial = \partial + m_x \circ \partial \circ \partial$, that is, $(x f')' = f' + x f''$. Rewriting every composite in this way is how [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-6|Proposition §57.6]] below puts any differential operator, in a chart, in the form $\sum_\alpha a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha$ with smooth coefficients $a_\alpha$ — “they are given by differential expressions in local coordinates.”

^ex-57-1

*Uses:* [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-2|§57.2]], [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-3|Def. §57.3]], [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-6|§57.6]]

> [!definition] Definition §57.3: Order of a Differential Operator
> For an integer $k \ge 0$, $\mathrm{Diff}_k(M)$ is the set of finite sums of composites $A_1 \circ \cdots \circ A_r$ of generators (each $A_i$ a $D_\mathbf{X}$ or an $m_g$, [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-2|Definition §57.2]]) in which *at most $k$* of the factors are of the form $D_\mathbf{X}$; the empty composite ($r = 0$) is $\mathrm{id}$. Set $\mathrm{Diff}_{-1}(M) = \{0\}$. A [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-2|differential operator]] $P$ has **order at most $k$** if $P \in \mathrm{Diff}_k(M)$, and **order $k$** if moreover $P \notin \mathrm{Diff}_{k-1}(M)$.

^def-57-3

The order counts the fewest vector-field factors with which $P$ can be written, not the number in a given expression: the next example is written with one vector field but has order $0$.

> [!example] Example §57.2: A Commutator in One Variable
> *(Not from lecture.)* On $M = \mathbb{R}$, with $\partial = D_{\partial/\partial x}$ and $m_x$ multiplication by the coordinate $x$,
>
> $$
> \partial \circ m_x - m_x \circ \partial = \mathrm{id}, \qquad \text{that is,} \quad (xf)' - x f' = f .
> $$
>
> So $\mathrm{Diff}(\mathbb{R})$ is not commutative, unlike its subring of multiplications ([[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-1|Proposition §57.1]]); and the operator on the left, written with one vector-field factor in each term, has [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-3|order]] $0$. The operators with polynomial coefficients, generated by $\partial$ and $m_x$, form the *Weyl algebra*; in quantum mechanics the same relation, for $i\hbar\,\partial$ and $m_x$, is the [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|canonical commutation relation]].

^ex-57-2

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *The identity.* By Proposition [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-2|§57.2]](2) with $\mathbf{X} = \partial/\partial x$ and $g = x$: $\partial \circ m_x = m_x \circ \partial + m_{\mathbf{X}x}$. Here $\mathbf{X}x = D_{\partial/\partial x}\, x = x' = 1$, the derivative of the coordinate function (Lemma [[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-3|§56.3]] with the single coefficient $1$). So
>
> $$
> \partial \circ m_x = m_x \circ \partial + m_{x'} = m_x \circ \partial + m_1 ,
> $$
>
> and $m_1 = \mathrm{id}$ (Proposition [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-1|§57.1]](1)). Subtracting $m_x \circ \partial$ gives $\partial \circ m_x - m_x \circ \partial = \mathrm{id}$. Applied to $f$ this reads $(xf)' - xf' = f$, the product rule $(xf)' = f + xf'$.
>
> *The two consequences.* $\mathrm{Diff}(\mathbb{R})$ is not commutative, because $\partial \circ m_x \ne m_x \circ \partial$: their difference $\mathrm{id}$ sends the constant function $1$ to $1 \ne 0$. And the operator $\partial \circ m_x - m_x \circ \partial$ has order $0$ (Definition [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-3|§57.3]]): it equals $\mathrm{id}$, the empty composite ($r = 0$), so it lies in $\mathrm{Diff}_0(\mathbb{R})$; and it is not in $\mathrm{Diff}_{-1}(\mathbb{R}) = \{0\}$, being nonzero.

^pf-ex-57-2

*Uses:* [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-2|§57.2]], [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-1|§57.1]], [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-3|Def. §57.3]], [[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-3|§56.3]]

> [!theorem] Proposition §57.3: The Order Filtration
> Let $M$ be a smooth manifold.
>
> 1. $\mathrm{Diff}_0(M) \subseteq \mathrm{Diff}_1(M) \subseteq \mathrm{Diff}_2(M) \subseteq \cdots$, each $\mathrm{Diff}_k(M)$ is closed under sums, and $\bigcup_k \mathrm{Diff}_k(M) = \mathrm{Diff}(M)$.
> 2. $\mathrm{Diff}_k(M) \circ \mathrm{Diff}_l(M) \subseteq \mathrm{Diff}_{k+l}(M)$: orders add, at most, under composition.

^prop-57-3

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from lecture; filled in.)* Throughout, “composite” means a composite $A_1 \circ \cdots \circ A_r$ of generators, each $A_i$ a $D_\mathbf{X}$ or an $m_g$, and $\mathrm{Diff}_k(M)$ consists of the finite sums of composites with at most $k$ factors of the form $D_\mathbf{X}$ (Definition [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-3|§57.3]]). The empty sum is the operator $0 = m_0$.
>
> *(1)* *Inclusions.* A composite with at most $k$ vector-field factors has at most $k + 1$; so a finite sum of such composites is also a finite sum of composites with at most $k + 1$, and $\mathrm{Diff}_k(M) \subseteq \mathrm{Diff}_{k+1}(M)$. *Sums.* The sum of two finite sums of composites with at most $k$ vector-field factors is again a finite sum of such composites. *Union.* Each $\mathrm{Diff}_k(M)$ consists of finite sums of finite composites of generators, so lies in $\mathrm{Diff}(M)$ (Definition [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-2|§57.2]]). Conversely, let $P = C_1 + \cdots + C_s \in \mathrm{Diff}(M)$ with composites $C_i$, and let $k_i$ be the number of vector-field factors of $C_i$. With $k = \max_i k_i$ (and $k = 0$ if $s = 0$), every $C_i$ has at most $k$ vector-field factors, so $P \in \mathrm{Diff}_k(M)$.
>
> *(2)* Let $P = \sum_i C_i \in \mathrm{Diff}_k(M)$ and $Q = \sum_j E_j \in \mathrm{Diff}_l(M)$, with composites $C_i$ having at most $k$ and $E_j$ at most $l$ vector-field factors. Composition distributes over sums on both sides: for every $f$,
>
> $$
> \big((P_1 + P_2) \circ Q\big) f = P_1(Qf) + P_2(Qf), \qquad \big(Q \circ (P_1 + P_2)\big) f = Q(P_1 f + P_2 f) = Q(P_1 f) + Q(P_2 f),
> $$
>
> the first by the pointwise sum of operators, the second because $Q$ is linear. Hence $P \circ Q = \sum_i \sum_j C_i \circ E_j$. Each $C_i \circ E_j$ is a composite of generators — the factors of $C_i$ followed by those of $E_j$ — whose vector-field factors number at most $k + l$. So $P \circ Q$ is a finite sum of composites with at most $k + l$ vector-field factors: $P \circ Q \in \mathrm{Diff}_{k+l}(M)$. (If $k = -1$ or $l = -1$, then $P = 0$ or $Q = 0$, so $P \circ Q = 0$, which lies in $\mathrm{Diff}_{k+l}(M)$ whenever $k + l \ge -1$.)

> [!theorem] Proposition §57.4: Differential Operators of Order 0 and 1
> Let $M$ be a smooth manifold.
>
> 1. $\mathrm{Diff}_0(M) = \{ m_g : g \in C^\infty(M) \}$.
> 2. $\mathrm{Diff}_1(M) = \{ m_g + D_\mathbf{X} : g \in C^\infty(M),\ \mathbf{X} \in \mathfrak{X}(M) \}$, and $g$ and $\mathbf{X}$ are determined by the operator $P = m_g + D_\mathbf{X}$: $g = P(1)$ and $D_\mathbf{X} = P - m_{P(1)}$.
>
> In particular the derivations of $C^\infty(M)$ are exactly the $P \in \mathrm{Diff}_1(M)$ with $P(1) = 0$.

^prop-57-4

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from lecture; filled in.)* As in the proof of Proposition [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-3|§57.3]], “composite” means a composite of generators, each a $D_\mathbf{X}$ or an $m_g$, and $\mathrm{Diff}_k(M)$ consists of the finite sums of composites with at most $k$ factors of the form $D_\mathbf{X}$ (Definition [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-3|§57.3]]).
>
> *(1)* A composite with no vector-field factor is $m_{g_1} \circ \cdots \circ m_{g_r}$. By $m_{gh} = m_g \circ m_h$ (Proposition [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-1|§57.1]](1)), applied $r - 1$ times, it equals $m_{g_1 \cdots g_r}$; for $r = 0$ it is $\mathrm{id} = m_1$. A finite sum of multiplication operators is again one, $\sum_i m_{h_i} = m_{\sum_i h_i}$, by $m_{g+h} = m_g + m_h$ (same proposition). So every element of $\mathrm{Diff}_0(M)$ is some $m_g$. Conversely $m_g$ is a composite of one generator with no vector-field factor, so $m_g \in \mathrm{Diff}_0(M)$.
>
> *(2)* *Every element has the form $m_g + D_\mathbf{Y}$.* A composite with no vector-field factor is $m_g$ by (1), and $m_g = m_g + D_\mathbf{0}$, since $(D_\mathbf{0} f)(p) = 0_p[f] = 0$. A composite with exactly one vector-field factor $D_\mathbf{X}$ is $m_a \circ D_\mathbf{X} \circ m_b$, where $m_a$ is the composite of the multiplications to the left of it and $m_b$ that of those to the right (by (1); $a = 1$ or $b = 1$ if there are none). Then, by Proposition [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-2|§57.2]](2) with $g = b$, the linearity of $m_a$, Proposition [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-1|§57.1]](1), and Proposition [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-2|§57.2]](1) with $g = ab$,
>
> $$
> m_a \circ D_\mathbf{X} \circ m_b = m_a \circ \big( m_b \circ D_\mathbf{X} + m_{\mathbf{X}b} \big) = m_a \circ m_b \circ D_\mathbf{X} + m_a \circ m_{\mathbf{X}b} = m_{ab} \circ D_\mathbf{X} + m_{a\,\mathbf{X}b} = D_{ab\mathbf{X}} + m_{a\,\mathbf{X}b} ,
> $$
>
> where $ab\mathbf{X} \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ by Proposition [[§56 Vector Fields#^prop-56-2|§56.2]](4). Finally, a sum of two operators of this form is again of this form:
>
> $$
> (m_g + D_\mathbf{X}) + (m_h + D_\mathbf{Y}) = m_{g+h} + D_{\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{Y}} ,
> $$
>
> because $m_g + m_h = m_{g+h}$ (Proposition [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-1|§57.1]](1)), and $D_\mathbf{X} + D_\mathbf{Y} = D_{\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{Y}}$: for every $f$ and $p$, $\mathbf{X}_p[f] + \mathbf{Y}_p[f] = (\mathbf{X}_p + \mathbf{Y}_p)[f] = (\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{Y})_p[f]$ (Definitions [[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^def-28-2|§28.2]] and [[§56 Vector Fields#^def-56-2|§56.2]]), with $\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{Y} \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ (Proposition [[§56 Vector Fields#^prop-56-2|§56.2]](1)). The empty sum is $0 = m_0 + D_\mathbf{0}$. So every element of $\mathrm{Diff}_1(M)$ is of the form $m_g + D_\mathbf{Y}$. Conversely $m_g + D_\mathbf{X}$ is a sum of two composites of one generator each, with at most one vector-field factor, so it lies in $\mathrm{Diff}_1(M)$.
>
> *$g$ and $\mathbf{X}$ are determined by $P$.* First, $D_\mathbf{X}$ kills the constant function $1$: for every $p \in M$, $(D_\mathbf{X} 1)(p) = \mathbf{X}_p[1] = 0$ (Lemma [[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-3|§56.3]]), because the derivation $\mathbf{X}_p$ at $p$ annihilates constants (Lemma [[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^lem-28-2|§28.2]](1)).
> Hence for $P = m_g + D_\mathbf{X}$,
>
> $$
> P(1) = m_g(1) + D_\mathbf{X}(1) = g \cdot 1 + 0 = g ,
> $$
>
> and then $D_\mathbf{X} = P - m_g = P - m_{P(1)}$. If also $P = m_h + D_\mathbf{Y}$, the same computation gives $h = P(1) = g$ and $D_\mathbf{Y} = P - m_{P(1)} = D_\mathbf{X}$, and $D_\mathbf{Y} = D_\mathbf{X}$ forces $\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}$ by the uniqueness in Proposition [[§56 Vector Fields#^prop-56-7|§56.7]].
>
> For the last sentence. *Derivations lie in $\mathrm{Diff}_1(M)$ and kill $1$:* a derivation of $C^\infty(M)$ is $D_\mathbf{X}$ for some $\mathbf{X} \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ (Proposition [[§56 Vector Fields#^prop-56-7|§56.7]]); $D_\mathbf{X} = m_0 + D_\mathbf{X} \in \mathrm{Diff}_1(M)$ by (2), and $D_\mathbf{X}(1) = 0$ as just shown. *Conversely*, if $P \in \mathrm{Diff}_1(M)$ has $P(1) = 0$, write $P = m_g + D_\mathbf{X}$ by (2); then $g = P(1) = 0$, so $P = m_0 + D_\mathbf{X} = D_\mathbf{X}$ (as $m_0 f = 0$), a derivation (Lemma [[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-3|§56.3]]).

> [!theorem] Lemma §57.5: Coordinate Derivations on a Chart
> Let $(U, \varphi = (x^1, \ldots, x^m))$ be a chart and $\partial_i = \partial/\partial x^i : C^\infty(U) \to C^\infty(U)$ (Lemma [[§29 Coordinate Derivations and the Basis Theorem#^lem-29-1|§29.1]]).
>
> 1. Each $\partial_i$ is a derivation of $C^\infty(U)$: $\partial_i(bu) = (\partial_i b)\, u + b\, \partial_i u$.
> 2. The $\partial_i$ commute: $\partial_i \circ \partial_j = \partial_j \circ \partial_i$. Hence $\partial_i \circ \partial^\alpha = \partial^{\alpha + e_i}$ for every multi-index $\alpha$ (Definition [[§55 Morse Functions and the Zero Section#^def-55-3|§55.3]]).

^lem-57-5

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from lecture; filled in.)* *(1)* $\partial_i$ is the operator $D_{\partial/\partial x^i}$ of the vector field $\partial/\partial x^i$ on the manifold $U$ (a vector field by Proposition [[§56 Vector Fields#^prop-56-1|§56.1]], its coefficients being the constants $0$ and $1$), so it is a derivation of $C^\infty(U)$ (Lemma [[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-3|§56.3]]): $\partial_i(bu) = (\partial_i b)\, u + b\, \partial_i u$. *(2)* The $\partial_i$ commute: by Definition [[§29 Coordinate Derivations and the Basis Theorem#^def-29-2|§29.2]], $(\partial_i u) \circ \varphi^{-1} = \partial u_\varphi/\partial r^i$, so $(\partial_i \partial_j u) \circ \varphi^{-1} = \partial^2 u_\varphi/\partial r^i \partial r^j$, and mixed partials of the smooth function $u_\varphi = u \circ \varphi^{-1}$ are equal (Clairaut's theorem); hence $\partial_i \circ \partial_j = \partial_j \circ \partial_i$. Finally, $\partial^\alpha = \partial_1^{\alpha_1} \circ \cdots \circ \partial_m^{\alpha_m}$, and moving the extra factor $\partial_i$ past $\partial_1^{\alpha_1}, \ldots, \partial_{i-1}^{\alpha_{i-1}}$ one factor at a time gives $\partial_i \circ \partial^\alpha = \partial_1^{\alpha_1} \circ \cdots \circ \partial_i^{\alpha_i + 1} \circ \cdots \circ \partial_m^{\alpha_m} = \partial^{\alpha + e_i}$.

> [!theorem] Proposition §57.6: Differential Operators in a Chart
> Let $P \in \mathrm{Diff}_k(M)$ ([[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-3|Definition §57.3]]) and let $(U, (x^1, \ldots, x^m))$ be a chart. Then there are *unique* functions $a_\alpha \in C^\infty(U)$, one for each [[§55 Morse Functions and the Zero Section#^def-55-3|multi-index]] $\alpha$ with $|\alpha| \le k$, such that
>
> $$
> (Pf)\big|_U = \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha \big(f|_U\big) \qquad \text{for all } f \in C^\infty(M).
> $$

^prop-57-6

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from lecture; filled in.)* Write $\varphi$ for the chart map. By Lemma [[§57 Differential Operators#^lem-57-5|§57.5]], the $\partial_i$ are commuting derivations of $C^\infty(U)$, and $\partial_i \circ \partial^\alpha = \partial^{\alpha + e_i}$.
>
> *Existence.* Call an operator $Q$ on $C^\infty(U)$ *of form $j$* if $Q = \sum_{|\alpha| \le j} b_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha$ with $b_\alpha \in C^\infty(U)$. An operator of form $j$ is of form $k$ for $j \le k$ (take $b_\alpha = 0$ for $j < |\alpha| \le k$), and a sum of operators of form $k$ is of form $k$ (add the coefficients of equal $\partial^\alpha$). Now $P$ is a finite sum $\sum_s C_s$ of composites $C_s$ of generators with at most $k$ vector-field factors (Definition [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-3|§57.3]]), and $(Pf)|_U = \sum_s (C_s f)|_U$. So it suffices to show: if $A_1 \circ \cdots \circ A_r$ is a composite of generators with $j$ vector-field factors, then $(A_1 \cdots A_r f)|_U = Q(f|_U)$ for all $f \in C^\infty(M)$, for some $Q$ of form $j$. Induct on $r$. For $r = 0$ take $Q = \mathrm{id} = \partial^{(0, \ldots, 0)}$, of form $0$. For $r \ge 1$ let $B = A_2 \circ \cdots \circ A_r$, with $j'$ vector-field factors; by the induction hypothesis $(Bf)|_U = Q'(f|_U)$ with $Q' = \sum_{|\alpha| \le j'} b_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha$ of form $j'$. Write $u = f|_U$.
> - If $A_1 = m_g$ then $j = j'$, and restricting the product $g\,(Bf)$ to $U$,
>
> $$
> (m_g B f)|_U = g|_U\, (Bf)|_U = g|_U \sum_{|\alpha| \le j'} b_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha u = \sum_{|\alpha| \le j'} \big(g|_U\, b_\alpha\big)\, \partial^\alpha u ,
> $$
>
> of form $j$ with the smooth coefficients $g|_U\, b_\alpha$.
> - If $A_1 = D_\mathbf{X}$ then $j = j' + 1$. For $h \in C^\infty(M)$, Lemma [[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-3|§56.3]] gives $(D_\mathbf{X} h)|_U = \sum_i \mathbf{X}^i\, \partial_i (h|_U)$; here $\partial h/\partial x^i(q) = \partial_i(h|_U)(q)$, both being computed from $h \circ \varphi^{-1}$ on $\varphi(U)$ (Definition [[§29 Coordinate Derivations and the Basis Theorem#^def-29-2|§29.2]]). With $h = Bf$, the product rule for $\partial_i$, and $\partial_i \circ \partial^\alpha = \partial^{\alpha + e_i}$,
>
> $$
> (D_\mathbf{X} B f)|_U = \sum_i \mathbf{X}^i\, \partial_i \Big( \sum_\alpha b_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha u \Big) = \sum_i \sum_\alpha \mathbf{X}^i \Big( (\partial_i b_\alpha)\, \partial^\alpha u + b_\alpha\, \partial_i \partial^\alpha u \Big) = \sum_i \sum_\alpha \Big( \mathbf{X}^i\, (\partial_i b_\alpha)\, \partial^\alpha + \mathbf{X}^i\, b_\alpha\, \partial^{\alpha + e_i} \Big) u ,
> $$
>
> the sums over $|\alpha| \le j'$. The last step uses $\partial_i \circ \partial^\alpha = \partial^{\alpha + e_i}$, which reorders partial derivatives (Lemma [[§57 Differential Operators#^lem-57-5|§57.5]]). The coefficients $\mathbf{X}^i\, \partial_i b_\alpha$ and $\mathbf{X}^i\, b_\alpha$ are smooth on $U$ ($\mathbf{X}^i$ by Proposition [[§56 Vector Fields#^prop-56-1|§56.1]]), and the multi-indices occurring have $|\alpha| \le j'$ and $|\alpha + e_i| = |\alpha| + 1 \le j' + 1$. Collecting the terms with the same multi-index, the right side has form $j' + 1 = j$.
>
> *Uniqueness.* Suppose $\sum_{|\alpha| \le k} c_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha (f|_U) = 0$ for all $f \in C^\infty(M)$, with $c_\alpha \in C^\infty(U)$; we show every $c_\beta = 0$. Fix $p \in U$, let $c = (x^1(p), \ldots, x^m(p)) = \varphi(p)$, and fix $\beta$ with $|\beta| \le k$. The function $(x - c)^\beta/\beta!$ is smooth on $U$, so by Corollary [[§56 Vector Fields#^cor-56-5|§56.5]] there is $f_\beta \in C^\infty(M)$ that agrees with it on an open $W$ with $p \in W \subseteq U$. Partial derivatives on $U$ are computed from the coordinate representation on open subsets (Definition [[§29 Coordinate Derivations and the Basis Theorem#^def-29-2|§29.2]]), so two functions that agree on $W$ have all their iterated partial derivatives equal on $W$; hence $\partial^\alpha (f_\beta|_U)(p) = \partial^\alpha\big((x - c)^\beta/\beta!\big)(p)$ for every $\alpha$. By Lemma [[§55 Morse Functions and the Zero Section#^lem-55-11|§55.11]], $\partial^\alpha (x - c)^\beta(p)$ vanishes unless $\alpha = \beta$, where it is $\beta!$. Applying the hypothesis to $f = f_\beta$ and evaluating at $p$,
>
> $$
> 0 = \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} c_\alpha(p)\, \partial^\alpha (f_\beta|_U)(p) = \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} c_\alpha(p)\, \partial^\alpha\Big(\frac{(x - c)^\beta}{\beta!}\Big)(p) = c_\beta(p)\, \frac{\beta!}{\beta!} = c_\beta(p) .
> $$
>
> As $p$ and $\beta$ were arbitrary, all $c_\beta = 0$. Applied to the difference of two expressions for $P$, $(Pf)|_U = \sum a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha (f|_U) = \sum a'_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha (f|_U)$, with $c_\alpha = a_\alpha - a'_\alpha$, this gives $a_\alpha = a'_\alpha$ for every $\alpha$: uniqueness.

> [!theorem] Corollary §57.7: Differential Operators on Euclidean Space
> On $\mathbb{R}^m$, with its standard coordinates and $\partial_i = D_{\partial/\partial x^i}$,
>
> $$
> \mathrm{Diff}_k(\mathbb{R}^m) = \Big\{ \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha \;:\; a_\alpha \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^m) \Big\},
> $$
>
> with the coefficients $a_\alpha$ uniquely determined by the operator. In particular $\mathrm{Diff}(\mathbb{R}^m)$ is exactly the set of linear partial differential operators with smooth coefficients, and the [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-3|order]] is the usual order: the largest $|\alpha|$ with $a_\alpha \ne 0$.

^cor-57-7

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from lecture; filled in.)* Here $\partial_i$ in the sense of Definition [[§55 Morse Functions and the Zero Section#^def-55-3|§55.3]] for the identity chart, $f \mapsto \partial f/\partial x^i$, is the operator $D_{\partial/\partial x^i}$ of the vector field $\partial/\partial x^i$ on $\mathbb{R}^m$ (Lemma [[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-3|§56.3]], with coefficients $\delta^j_i$); $\partial/\partial x^i$ is a vector field by Proposition [[§56 Vector Fields#^prop-56-1|§56.1]], its coefficients being constant.
>
> *$\subseteq$ and uniqueness.* The identity chart $(\mathbb{R}^m, \mathrm{id})$ covers $\mathbb{R}^m$, so Proposition [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-6|§57.6]] with $U = \mathbb{R}^m$ (where $f|_U = f$) gives, for $P \in \mathrm{Diff}_k(\mathbb{R}^m)$, unique $a_\alpha \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^m)$ with $Pf = \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha f$ for all $f$.
>
> *$\supseteq$.* Conversely, each term is
>
> $$
> a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha = m_{a_\alpha} \circ \underbrace{\partial_1 \circ \cdots \circ \partial_1}_{\alpha_1} \circ \cdots \circ \underbrace{\partial_m \circ \cdots \circ \partial_m}_{\alpha_m} ,
> $$
>
> a composite of generators with $\alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_m = |\alpha|$ vector-field factors, so it lies in $\mathrm{Diff}_{|\alpha|}(\mathbb{R}^m) \subseteq \mathrm{Diff}_k(\mathbb{R}^m)$ for $|\alpha| \le k$ (Proposition [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-3|§57.3]](1)). A finite sum of such terms lies in $\mathrm{Diff}_k(\mathbb{R}^m)$, which is closed under sums (same proposition).
>
> *$\mathrm{Diff}(\mathbb{R}^m)$.* Since $\mathrm{Diff}(\mathbb{R}^m) = \bigcup_k \mathrm{Diff}_k(\mathbb{R}^m)$ (Proposition [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-3|§57.3]](1)), the differential operators are exactly the finite expressions $\sum_\alpha a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha$ with smooth coefficients.
>
> *The order.* Let $P \in \mathrm{Diff}_k(\mathbb{R}^m)$ with its unique coefficients $a_\alpha$, $|\alpha| \le k$. For $-1 \le j \le k$: $P \in \mathrm{Diff}_j(\mathbb{R}^m)$ if and only if $a_\alpha = 0$ for all $|\alpha| > j$. Indeed, if $P \in \mathrm{Diff}_j(\mathbb{R}^m)$, its expansion with $|\alpha| \le j$ (by $\subseteq$; for $j = -1$, $P = 0$ with the empty expansion), padded with zero coefficients for $j < |\alpha| \le k$, is an expansion with $|\alpha| \le k$, so by uniqueness it equals $(a_\alpha)$, and $a_\alpha = 0$ for $|\alpha| > j$. Conversely, if $a_\alpha = 0$ for $|\alpha| > j$, then $P = \sum_{|\alpha| \le j} a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha \in \mathrm{Diff}_j(\mathbb{R}^m)$ by $\supseteq$ (for $j = -1$, $P = 0$). So for $P \ne 0$, with $\ell$ the largest $|\alpha|$ such that $a_\alpha \ne 0$, $P \in \mathrm{Diff}_\ell(\mathbb{R}^m)$ and $P \notin \mathrm{Diff}_{\ell - 1}(\mathbb{R}^m)$: $P$ has order $\ell$ (Definition [[§57 Differential Operators#^def-57-3|§57.3]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Differential Operators Are Local
> Every differential operator is a local operator ([[§56 Vector Fields#^def-56-6|Definition §56.6]]). *(Lecture 15: “these are all local operators”; the reason is filled in.)* The generators are local: $(m_g f)(p) = g(p) f(p)$ depends only on $f(p)$, and $(D_\mathbf{X} f)(p) = \mathbf{X}_p[f]$ depends only on the germ of $f$ at $p$, because $\mathbf{X}_p$ is a derivation at $p$, acting on germs ([[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^def-28-1|Definition §28.1]]). Sums and composites of local operators are local: if $f|_U = g|_U$ then $(Pf)|_U = (Pg)|_U$, and applying $Q$ gives $(QPf)|_U = (QPg)|_U$.

^rem-57-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §57.8: Local Operators Are Differential Operators
> Let $P : C^\infty(M) \to C^\infty(M)$ be an $\mathbb{R}$-linear local operator. Then $P$ is a differential operator near each point: every $p \in M$ has a chart neighbourhood $U$ on which $(Pf)|_U = \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha (f|_U)$ for all $f \in C^\infty(M)$, for some $k$ and some $a_\alpha \in C^\infty(U)$ — the form of Proposition [[§57 Differential Operators#^prop-57-6|§57.6]]. Conversely, every differential operator is local (the remark *[[§57 Differential Operators#^rem-57-1|Differential Operators Are Local]]* in [[§57 Differential Operators|§57]]).

^thm-57-8

> [!proof]+ Proof (not given in this course)
> Stated in Lecture 15 “FYI”: “There's a theorem which is that every local operator … is a differential operator, and conversely, of course, the converse is easy. … We're not going to prove this theorem, but it's true.” It is Peetre's theorem. The statement is local because on a non-compact $M$ the order $k$ need not be bounded as $p$ varies, and then $P$ is not a finite sum of composites on all of $M$.
