*Stage: fields — Vector fields and multiplications by functions generate a ring of operators on $C^\infty(M)$; its elements have an order, and in a chart they are the linear partial differential operators with smooth coefficients.*

*Lecture 15 introduced the ring of differential operators in passing, as an aside to the locality of derivations ([[§48 Vector Fields#^lem-48-6|Lemma §48.6]]): its definition, the fact that its elements are local, and Peetre's theorem “FYI”. Those are collected here. Everything else in this subsection — the algebra of multiplications and vector fields, the order of an operator, the coordinate form and the commutator estimate in [[§50 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^prop-50-5|§50]] — is not from lecture; it makes precise the remark that differential operators “are given by differential expressions in local coordinates.”*

> [!definition] Definition §49.1: Multiplication Operator
> For $g \in C^\infty(M)$, the **multiplication operator** $m_g : C^\infty(M) \to C^\infty(M)$ is $m_g f = g f$.

^def-49-1

> [!theorem] Proposition §49.1: Multiplication Operators
> Let $g, h \in C^\infty(M)$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $m_g$ ([[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-1|Definition §49.1]]) is an $\mathbb{R}$-linear [[§48 Vector Fields#^def-48-4|operator]], and
> 1. $m_{g + h} = m_g + m_h$, $m_{gh} = m_g \circ m_h$, $m_1 = \mathrm{id}$ and $m_c = c\, \mathrm{id}$ (writing $c$ also for the constant function);
> 2. $m_g$ determines $g$: $g = m_g(1)$, so $m_g = m_h$ only if $g = h$.
>
> So $g \mapsto m_g$ is an injective ring homomorphism from $C^\infty(M)$ into the linear operators, whose image $\{ m_g \}$ is a *commutative* subring: $m_g \circ m_h = m_{gh} = m_{hg} = m_h \circ m_g$.

^prop-49-1

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from lecture; filled in.)* Linearity: $g(af_1 + bf_2) = a\, gf_1 + b\, gf_2$. For (1), evaluate at a function $f$ and a point $p$: $(g + h)(p) f(p) = g(p) f(p) + h(p) f(p)$, $(gh)(p) f(p) = g(p)\big(h(p) f(p)\big)$, $1 \cdot f(p) = f(p)$ and $c f(p) = c\, f(p)$. For (2), $m_g(1) = g \cdot 1 = g$.

^pf-49-1

*Uses:* [[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-1|Def. §49.1]], [[§48 Vector Fields#^def-48-4|Def. §48.4]]

> [!definition] Definition §49.2: Differential Operators
> The **ring of differential operators** on $M$, written $\mathrm{Diff}(M)$ here, is the subring of the $\mathbb{R}$-linear maps $C^\infty(M) \to C^\infty(M)$ (with sum and composition) generated by
>
> $$
> D_\mathbf{X} \quad (\mathbf{X} \in \mathfrak{X}(M)) \qquad \text{and} \qquad m_g \quad (g \in C^\infty(M)) ,
> $$
>
> the operators of [[§48 Vector Fields#^lem-48-3|Lemma §48.3]] and [[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-1|Definition §49.1]]. Its elements, the **differential operators**, are the finite sums of finite composites of such operators.

^def-49-2

Uribe: “I can take a vector field, apply it … I can compose that with multiplication by a function, or compose it with another vector field, and do finitely many of these things, and add linear combinations of such things.” (The name $\mathrm{Diff}(M)$ is not from lecture.)

> [!theorem] Proposition §49.2: Vector Fields and Multiplication Operators
> Let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ and $g \in C^\infty(M)$. Then
> 1. $m_g \circ D_\mathbf{X} = D_{g\mathbf{X}}$, for the vector field $g\mathbf{X}$ of [[§48 Vector Fields#^def-48-3|Definition §48.3]];
> 2. $D_\mathbf{X} \circ m_g = m_g \circ D_\mathbf{X} + m_{\mathbf{X}g}$, where $\mathbf{X}g = D_\mathbf{X} g$.
>
> In words: a [[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-1|multiplication]] can always be moved to the *left* of a vector field, at the cost of the extra term $m_{\mathbf{X}g}$, which contains no vector field.

^prop-49-2

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from lecture; filled in.)* Let $f \in C^\infty(M)$ and $p \in M$. (1) $g\mathbf{X}$ is a vector field by [[§48 Vector Fields#^prop-48-2|Proposition §48.2]] (4), and $(m_g D_\mathbf{X} f)(p) = g(p)\, \mathbf{X}_p[f] = \big(g(p)\, \mathbf{X}_p\big)[f] = (g\mathbf{X})_p[f] = (D_{g\mathbf{X}} f)(p)$. (2) By the product rule for the [[§48 Vector Fields#^def-48-5|derivation]] $D_\mathbf{X}$ ([[§48 Vector Fields#^lem-48-3|Lemma §48.3]]),
>
> $$
> D_\mathbf{X}(m_g f) = D_\mathbf{X}(gf) = g\, D_\mathbf{X} f + f\, D_\mathbf{X} g = (m_g \circ D_\mathbf{X}) f + m_{\mathbf{X}g} f .
> $$

^pf-49-2

*Uses:* [[§48 Vector Fields#^def-48-3|Def. §48.3]], [[§48 Vector Fields#^prop-48-2|§48.2]], [[§48 Vector Fields#^def-48-5|Def. §48.5]], [[§48 Vector Fields#^lem-48-3|§48.3]], [[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-1|Def. §49.1]]

> [!example] Example §49.1: Differential Operators in Coordinates
> *(Not from lecture.)* On $M = \mathbb{R}$ with coordinate $x$, write $\partial = D_{\partial/\partial x}$. Then $P = \partial \circ \partial + m_x \circ \partial$ is a differential operator, $Pf = f'' + x f'$, of [[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-3|order]] $2$ ([[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-3|Definition §49.3]] below). On $M = \mathbb{R}^2$ the Laplacian $\Delta = \partial_x \circ \partial_x + \partial_y \circ \partial_y$ is one, also of order $2$. Composites can always be rewritten with the multiplications in front, because of the product rule $\partial \circ m_g = m_{\partial g} + m_g \circ \partial$ ([[§49 Differential Operators#^prop-49-2|Proposition §49.2]] (2)): for instance $\partial \circ m_x \circ \partial = \partial + m_x \circ \partial \circ \partial$, that is, $(x f')' = f' + x f''$. Rewriting every composite in this way is how [[§49 Differential Operators#^prop-49-4|Proposition §49.4]] below puts any differential operator, in a chart, in the form $\sum_\alpha a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha$ with smooth coefficients $a_\alpha$ — “they are given by differential expressions in local coordinates.”

^ex-49-1

*Uses:* [[§49 Differential Operators#^prop-49-2|§49.2]], [[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-3|Def. §49.3]], [[§49 Differential Operators#^prop-49-4|§49.4]]

> [!definition] Definition §49.3: Order of a Differential Operator
> For an integer $k \ge 0$, $\mathrm{Diff}_k(M)$ is the set of finite sums of composites $A_1 \circ \cdots \circ A_r$ of generators (each $A_i$ a $D_\mathbf{X}$ or an $m_g$, [[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-2|Definition §49.2]]) in which *at most $k$* of the factors are of the form $D_\mathbf{X}$; the empty composite ($r = 0$) is $\mathrm{id}$. Set $\mathrm{Diff}_{-1}(M) = \{0\}$. A [[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-2|differential operator]] $P$ has **order at most $k$** if $P \in \mathrm{Diff}_k(M)$, and **order $k$** if moreover $P \notin \mathrm{Diff}_{k-1}(M)$.

^def-49-3

The order counts the fewest vector-field factors with which $P$ can be written, not the number in a given expression: the next example is written with one vector field but has order $0$.

> [!example] Example §49.2: A Commutator in One Variable
> *(Not from lecture.)* On $M = \mathbb{R}$, with $\partial = D_{\partial/\partial x}$ and $m_x$ multiplication by the coordinate $x$,
>
> $$
> \partial \circ m_x - m_x \circ \partial = \mathrm{id}, \qquad \text{that is,} \quad (xf)' - x f' = f .
> $$
>
> So $\mathrm{Diff}(\mathbb{R})$ is not commutative, unlike its subring of multiplications ([[§49 Differential Operators#^prop-49-1|Proposition §49.1]]); and the operator on the left, written with one vector-field factor in each term, has [[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-3|order]] $0$. The operators with polynomial coefficients, generated by $\partial$ and $m_x$, form the *Weyl algebra*; in quantum mechanics the same relation, for $i\hbar\,\partial$ and $m_x$, is the [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|canonical commutation relation]].

^ex-49-2

> [!proof]+ Proof
> By [[§49 Differential Operators#^prop-49-2|Proposition §49.2]] (2) with $\mathbf{X} = \partial/\partial x$ and $g = x$: $\partial \circ m_x = m_x \circ \partial + m_{x'} = m_x \circ \partial + m_1$, and $m_1 = \mathrm{id}$.

^pf-ex-49-2

*Uses:* [[§49 Differential Operators#^prop-49-2|§49.2]], [[§49 Differential Operators#^prop-49-1|§49.1]], [[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-3|Def. §49.3]]

> [!theorem] Proposition §49.3: The Order Filtration
> Let $M$ be a smooth manifold.
> 1. $\mathrm{Diff}_0(M) \subseteq \mathrm{Diff}_1(M) \subseteq \mathrm{Diff}_2(M) \subseteq \cdots$, each $\mathrm{Diff}_k(M)$ is closed under sums, and $\bigcup_k \mathrm{Diff}_k(M) = \mathrm{Diff}(M)$.
> 2. $\mathrm{Diff}_k(M) \circ \mathrm{Diff}_l(M) \subseteq \mathrm{Diff}_{k+l}(M)$: orders add, at most, under composition.
> 3. $\mathrm{Diff}_0(M) = \{ m_g : g \in C^\infty(M) \}$.
> 4. $\mathrm{Diff}_1(M) = \{ m_g + D_\mathbf{X} : g \in C^\infty(M),\ \mathbf{X} \in \mathfrak{X}(M) \}$, and $g$ and $\mathbf{X}$ are determined by the operator $P = m_g + D_\mathbf{X}$: $g = P(1)$ and $D_\mathbf{X} = P - m_{P(1)}$.
>
> In particular the [[§48 Vector Fields#^def-48-5|derivations]] of $C^\infty(M)$ are exactly the $P \in \mathrm{Diff}_1(M)$ with $P(1) = 0$.

^prop-49-3

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from lecture; filled in.)* (1) A composite with at most $k$ vector-field factors has at most $k + 1$; sums of finite sums are finite sums; and every composite has some finite number of vector-field factors.
>
> (2) Composition distributes over sums, $(P_1 + P_2) \circ Q = P_1 \circ Q + P_2 \circ Q$, and, because the operators are linear, $Q \circ (P_1 + P_2) = Q \circ P_1 + Q \circ P_2$. So it suffices to compose two composites with at most $k$ and at most $l$ vector-field factors; the result is a composite with at most $k + l$.
>
> (3) A composite with no vector-field factor is $m_{g_1} \circ \cdots \circ m_{g_r} = m_{g_1 \cdots g_r}$, and a sum $\sum_i m_{h_i} = m_{\sum_i h_i}$ ([[§49 Differential Operators#^prop-49-1|Proposition §49.1]] (1)); conversely $m_g \in \mathrm{Diff}_0(M)$.
>
> (4) A composite with exactly one vector-field factor $D_\mathbf{X}$ is $m_a \circ D_\mathbf{X} \circ m_b$, where $m_a$ is the composite of the multiplications to the left of it and $m_b$ that of those to the right (by (3); $a = 1$ or $b = 1$ if there are none). By [[§49 Differential Operators#^prop-49-2|Proposition §49.2]],
>
> $$
> m_a \circ D_\mathbf{X} \circ m_b = m_a \circ \big( m_b \circ D_\mathbf{X} + m_{\mathbf{X}b} \big) = D_{ab\mathbf{X}} + m_{a\,\mathbf{X}b} .
> $$
>
> So every composite with at most one vector-field factor is of the form $m_g + D_\mathbf{Y}$, and so is a sum of such: $(m_g + D_\mathbf{X}) + (m_h + D_\mathbf{Y}) = m_{g+h} + D_{\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{Y}}$, since $(\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{Y})_p[f] = \mathbf{X}_p[f] + \mathbf{Y}_p[f]$ and $\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{Y} \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ ([[§48 Vector Fields#^prop-48-2|Proposition §48.2]]). Conversely $m_g + D_\mathbf{X} \in \mathrm{Diff}_1(M)$. For the uniqueness, a derivation kills constants: $D(1) = D(1 \cdot 1) = 2\, D(1)$, so $D(1) = 0$. Hence $P(1) = g \cdot 1 + D_\mathbf{X}(1) = g$, then $D_\mathbf{X} = P - m_g$, and $D_\mathbf{X}$ determines $\mathbf{X}$ by the uniqueness in [[§48 Vector Fields#^prop-48-7|Proposition §48.7]].
>
> For the last sentence: $D_\mathbf{X}$ lies in $\mathrm{Diff}_1(M)$ and kills $1$; conversely if $P = m_g + D_\mathbf{X}$ has $P(1) = 0$ then $g = 0$ and $P = D_\mathbf{X}$, a derivation ([[§48 Vector Fields#^lem-48-3|Lemma §48.3]]).

> [!definition] Definition §49.4: Multi-Index Notation
> A **multi-index** is an $m$-tuple $\alpha = (\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_m)$ of integers $\alpha_i \ge 0$, with **order** $|\alpha| = \alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_m$ and $\alpha! = \alpha_1! \cdots \alpha_m!$. In a chart $(U, (x^1, \ldots, x^m))$, with $\partial_i = \partial/\partial x^i$ acting on $C^\infty(U)$,
>
> $$
> \partial^\alpha = \partial_1^{\alpha_1} \circ \cdots \circ \partial_m^{\alpha_m}, \qquad (x - c)^\alpha = (x^1 - c^1)^{\alpha_1} \cdots (x^m - c^m)^{\alpha_m} \quad (c \in \mathbb{R}^m),
> $$
>
> and $\partial^{(0, \ldots, 0)} = \mathrm{id}$. Write $\beta \le \alpha$ if $\beta_i \le \alpha_i$ for every $i$, and $e_j$ for the multi-index with $1$ in place $j$ and $0$ elsewhere.

^def-49-4

> [!theorem] Proposition §49.4: Differential Operators in a Chart
> Let $P \in \mathrm{Diff}_k(M)$ ([[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-3|Definition §49.3]]) and let $(U, (x^1, \ldots, x^m))$ be a chart. Then there are *unique* functions $a_\alpha \in C^\infty(U)$, one for each [[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-4|multi-index]] $\alpha$ with $|\alpha| \le k$, such that
>
> $$
> (Pf)\big|_U = \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha \big(f|_U\big) \qquad \text{for all } f \in C^\infty(M).
> $$

^prop-49-4

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from lecture; filled in.)* *Existence.* Call an operator $Q$ on $C^\infty(U)$ *of form $j$* if $Q = \sum_{|\alpha| \le j} b_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha$ with $b_\alpha \in C^\infty(U)$. By linearity it suffices to show: if $A_1 \circ \cdots \circ A_r$ is a composite of generators with $j$ vector-field factors, then $(A_1 \cdots A_r f)|_U = Q(f|_U)$ for some $Q$ of form $j$ (and form $j$ is form $k$ for $j \le k$). Induct on $r$. For $r = 0$ take $Q = \mathrm{id} = \partial^{(0, \ldots, 0)}$. For $r \ge 1$ let $B = A_2 \circ \cdots \circ A_r$, with $(Bf)|_U = Q'(f|_U)$, $Q' = \sum b_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha$ of form $j'$.
> - If $A_1 = m_g$ then $j = j'$ and $(gBf)|_U = g|_U\, Q'(f|_U)$, of form $j$ with coefficients $g|_U\, b_\alpha$.
> - If $A_1 = D_\mathbf{X}$ then $j = j' + 1$, and on $U$, $D_\mathbf{X} h = \sum_i \mathbf{X}^i\, \partial_i h$ ([[§48 Vector Fields#^lem-48-3|Lemma §48.3]]), so
>
> $$
> (D_\mathbf{X} B f)|_U = \sum_i \mathbf{X}^i\, \partial_i \Big( \sum_\alpha b_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha (f|_U) \Big) = \sum_i \sum_\alpha \Big( \mathbf{X}^i\, (\partial_i b_\alpha)\, \partial^\alpha + \mathbf{X}^i\, b_\alpha\, \partial^{\alpha + e_i} \Big)(f|_U),
> $$
>
> by the product rule and $\partial_i \circ \partial^\alpha = \partial^{\alpha + e_i}$. The last step reorders the partial derivatives, which is allowed because mixed partials of smooth functions are equal ([[Schwarz–Clairaut Theorem|Clairaut's theorem]], applied to $f \circ \varphi^{-1}$). The right side has form $j' + 1 = j$.
>
> *Uniqueness.* Suppose $\sum_{|\alpha| \le k} c_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha (f|_U) = 0$ for all $f \in C^\infty(M)$, with $c_\alpha \in C^\infty(U)$; we show every $c_\beta = 0$. Fix $p \in U$, let $c = (x^1(p), \ldots, x^m(p))$, and fix $\beta$ with $|\beta| \le k$. By [[§48 Vector Fields#^cor-48-5|Corollary §48.5]] there is $f_\beta \in C^\infty(M)$ equal to $(x - c)^\beta / \beta!$ near $p$. For the polynomial $(x - c)^\beta$, $\partial^\alpha (x - c)^\beta = \frac{\beta!}{(\beta - \alpha)!} (x - c)^{\beta - \alpha}$ if $\alpha \le \beta$, and $0$ otherwise; at $x = c$ it vanishes unless $\alpha = \beta$, where it is $\beta!$. Since $f_\beta$ agrees with $(x - c)^\beta/\beta!$ near $p$, its derivatives at $p$ are the same, and
>
> $$
> 0 = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha(p)\, \partial^\alpha f_\beta(p) = c_\beta(p) .
> $$
>
> As $p$ and $\beta$ were arbitrary, all $c_\beta = 0$; applied to the difference of two expressions for $P$, this is uniqueness.

> [!theorem] Corollary §49.5: Differential Operators on Euclidean Space
> On $\mathbb{R}^m$, with its standard coordinates and $\partial_i = D_{\partial/\partial x^i}$,
>
> $$
> \mathrm{Diff}_k(\mathbb{R}^m) = \Big\{ \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha \;:\; a_\alpha \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^m) \Big\},
> $$
>
> with the coefficients $a_\alpha$ uniquely determined by the operator. In particular $\mathrm{Diff}(\mathbb{R}^m)$ is exactly the set of linear partial differential operators with smooth coefficients, and the [[§49 Differential Operators#^def-49-3|order]] is the usual order: the largest $|\alpha|$ with $a_\alpha \ne 0$.

^cor-49-5

> [!proof]+ Proof
> *(Not from lecture; filled in.)* The identity chart covers $\mathbb{R}^m$, so [[§49 Differential Operators#^prop-49-4|Proposition §49.4]] with $U = \mathbb{R}^m$ gives the inclusion $\subseteq$ and the uniqueness. Conversely, each $\partial_i$ is the operator of the global vector field $\partial/\partial x^i$, so $a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha = m_{a_\alpha} \circ \partial_1^{\alpha_1} \circ \cdots \circ \partial_m^{\alpha_m}$ is a composite with $|\alpha|$ vector-field factors, in $\mathrm{Diff}_{|\alpha|}(\mathbb{R}^m) \subseteq \mathrm{Diff}_k(\mathbb{R}^m)$. For the order: $P$ lies in $\mathrm{Diff}_j$ exactly when its unique expansion has $a_\alpha = 0$ for $|\alpha| > j$.

> [!remark] Remark: Differential Operators Are Local
> Every differential operator is a local operator ([[§48 Vector Fields#^def-48-6|Definition §48.6]]). *(Lecture 15: “these are all local operators”; the reason is filled in.)* The generators are local: $(m_g f)(p) = g(p) f(p)$ depends only on $f(p)$, and $(D_\mathbf{X} f)(p) = \mathbf{X}_p[f]$ depends only on the germ of $f$ at $p$, because $\mathbf{X}_p$ is a derivation at $p$, acting on germs ([[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^def-28-1|Definition §28.1]]). Sums and composites of local operators are local: if $f|_U = g|_U$ then $(Pf)|_U = (Pg)|_U$, and applying $Q$ gives $(QPf)|_U = (QPg)|_U$.

^rem-49-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §49.6: Local Operators Are Differential Operators
> Let $P : C^\infty(M) \to C^\infty(M)$ be an $\mathbb{R}$-linear local operator. Then $P$ is a differential operator near each point: every $p \in M$ has a chart neighbourhood $U$ on which $(Pf)|_U = \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} a_\alpha\, \partial^\alpha (f|_U)$ for all $f \in C^\infty(M)$, for some $k$ and some $a_\alpha \in C^\infty(U)$ — the form of [[§49 Differential Operators#^prop-49-4|Proposition §49.4]]. Conversely, every differential operator is local ([[§49 Differential Operators#^rem-49-1|Remark: Differential Operators Are Local]]).

^thm-49-6

> [!proof]+ Proof (not given in this course)
> Stated in Lecture 15 “FYI”: “There's a theorem which is that every local operator … is a differential operator, and conversely, of course, the converse is easy. … We're not going to prove this theorem, but it's true.” It is Peetre's theorem. The statement is local because on a non-compact $M$ the order $k$ need not be bounded as $p$ varies, and then $P$ is not a finite sum of composites on all of $M$.

^pf-49-6
