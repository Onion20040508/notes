What happens to the quantum Dirac field if the names "particle" and "antiparticle" are exchanged? The field is the mode expansion of [[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra#^thm-c5b-2-1|Theorem §C5b.2.1]], with $\hat a$ annihilating particles and $\hat b^\dagger$ creating antiparticles, and spinors $u^s(p)$, $v^s(p)$ of [[§C5a.9 Plane-Wave Solutions#^thm-c5a-9-9|Theorem §C5a.9.9]] and [[§C5a.9 Plane-Wave Solutions#^thm-c5a-9-11|Theorem §C5a.9.11]]. Lecture 12 follows the method used for parity in [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field|§C9.3]]: transform the operators, rewrite the wave functions, and require that the pieces reassemble into a field multiplied by a $4\times4$ matrix. For charge conjugation the reassembled object is not $\hat\psi$ but its "star" $\hat\psi^{\ast}$, and rewriting the wave functions needs the complex conjugate of a spinor, which flips its spin. That fixes the two-spinors $\eta^s$ of $v^s(p)$, left free in [[§C5a.9 Plane-Wave Solutions#^def-c5a-9-3|Def. §C5a.9.3]]: $\eta^s = \xi^{-s}$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-5|Def. §C9.5.5]]), whose physical meaning is [[§C9.6 Physical Spin versus Wave-Function Spin|§C9.6]]. In any basis of $\gamma$'s the matrix $-i\gamma^2$ of the lecture becomes a charge-conjugation matrix $B$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-7|Theorem §C9.5.7]]), so charge conjugation is a basis-free conjugation of spinors that reduces to complex conjugation only in a Majorana basis. The section ends with the two bilinears the lecture transformed. Notation: hats on Hilbert-space operators, none on $U(\mathcal C)$, spinors or $\gamma$'s ([[Larsen PHY 513]]); chiral basis ([[§C5a.1 Spinor Space and the Clifford Action#^def-c5a-1-3|Def. §C5a.1.3]]), $\gamma^\mu = \begin{pmatrix}0 & \sigma^\mu\\ \bar\sigma^\mu & 0\end{pmatrix}$ (slide 2).

## Charge conjugation on the oscillators

> [!definition] Definition §C9.5.1: Charge Conjugation on the Hilbert Space
> **Charge conjugation** interchanges particles and antiparticles. It is represented by a unitary operator $U(\mathcal C)$ on the Fock space of the Dirac field ([[§C5b.4 Fermions, Fock Space and the Pauli Principle#^thm-c5b-4-1|Theorem §C5b.4.1]]), defined by its action on the mode operators ([[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra#^thm-c5b-2-4|Theorem §C5b.2.4]]) and on the vacuum:
>
> $$
> U(\mathcal C)\,\hat a^s_{\mathbf p}\,U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = \hat b^s_{\mathbf p}, \qquad U(\mathcal C)\,\hat b^s_{\mathbf p}\,U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = \hat a^s_{\mathbf p}, \qquad U(\mathcal C)|0\rangle = |0\rangle .
> $$
>
> The momentum $\mathbf p$ and the spin label $s$ are unchanged; possible phases on the right-hand sides are set to $1$. Conjugation order as for parity ([[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^cau-c9-3-1|§C9.3, Caution: Which side the inverse is on, and the names of the phases]]).
>
> *Parity analogue:* [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^def-c9-3-1|Def. §C9.3.1]].
> *Source: Lecture 12, slide 5 ("Phases on RHS possible. Set to 1 without loss of generality") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.1 (Definition "Charge conjugation on the Hilbert space", eq. (Cosc)) · PS §3.6, eq. (3.143) · Yu §9.2.1, eqs. (9.97)–(9.100) (helicity basis, phases $\zeta_C$)*

^def-c9-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the definition
> - *What C is.* Which species is called the particle is a convention. Changing one's mind should be a transformation under which every formula works the same way, forwards and backwards (Lecture 12). It is sometimes called "charge symmetry", but it is a transformation; whether nature respects it is a separate question ([[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity#^rem-c9-4-3|§C9.4, Remark: Mandatory and optional symmetries]]).
> - *The phases.* For parity the phases of particle and antiparticle could not both be $1$ ([[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^thm-c9-3-6|Theorem §C9.3.6]]). Here they can, and the field confirms it ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-8|Theorem §C9.5.8]] needs no condition on phases).
> - *Spin.* A particle of spin $s$ becomes the antiparticle of the same spin $s$ (transcript). What "the same spin" means for an antiparticle is [[§C9.6 Physical Spin versus Wave-Function Spin|§C9.6]].
> - *Consistency (written here).* The definition respects the anticommutators of [[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra#^thm-c5b-2-4|Theorem §C5b.2.4]]: conjugation by a unitary preserves products, and $\{\hat b^r_{\mathbf p}, \hat b^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf q}\} = \{\hat a^r_{\mathbf p}, \hat a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf q}\}$, while the mixed anticommutators all vanish on both sides. Applying the definition twice gives back $\hat a$ and $\hat b$, so $U(\mathcal C)^2$ commutes with every mode operator and fixes $|0\rangle$; every Fock state is a polynomial in creators applied to $|0\rangle$, hence $U(\mathcal C)^2 = \mathbb 1$ (stated in the user's notes).
>
> *Source: Lecture 12, slide 5, and transcript · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.1 (paragraph "Phases, and what C is") · the consistency check written here*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.5.1: Charge Conjugation on Creation Operators and One-Particle States
> Under $U(\mathcal C)$ of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-1|Def. §C9.5.1]],
>
> $$
> U(\mathcal C)\,\hat a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}\,U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = \hat b^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}, \qquad U(\mathcal C)\,\hat b^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}\,U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = \hat a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p},
> $$
>
> and on the one-particle states of [[§C5b.4 Fermions, Fock Space and the Pauli Principle#^thm-c5b-4-1|Theorem §C5b.4.1]], $U(\mathcal C)|\mathbf p, s\rangle = |\mathbf p, s\rangle^c$ and $U(\mathcal C)|\mathbf p, s\rangle^c = |\mathbf p, s\rangle$.
>
> *Parity analogue:* [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^thm-c9-3-1|Theorem §C9.3.1]].
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.1 (Definition "Charge conjugation on the Hilbert space", last sentence) · Lecture 12, slide 5*

^thm-c9-5-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Adjoint of the defining relation.** As in step 1 of [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^der-c9-3-1|Derivation §C9.3.1]]: $(U\hat a^s_{\mathbf p}U^{-1})^\dagger = (U^{-1})^\dagger\hat a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}U^\dagger = U\hat a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}U^{-1}$ for unitary $U$, and the right side gives $(\hat b^s_{\mathbf p})^\dagger = \hat b^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}$; no phase to conjugate. The same with $\hat a \leftrightarrow \hat b$.
>
> **2. The states.** $U|\mathbf p, s\rangle = \sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}\,(U\hat a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}U^{-1})\,U|0\rangle = \sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}\,\hat b^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle = |\mathbf p, s\rangle^c$, inserting $U^{-1}U = \mathbb 1$ before the vacuum and using $U|0\rangle = |0\rangle$. The momentum and the label $s$ are the same on both sides, so no relabelling is needed (unlike parity). The other state the same way.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - $U(\mathcal C)$ maps creators to creators and annihilators to annihilators; this is what lets it pass through normal ordering (used in Theorems §C9.5.11–12).
> - Used next: the field (Theorem §C9.5.8).

> [!remark] Remark: The image of the field needs a conjugation
> $U(\mathcal C)$ acts on the operators only; for it the spinors and exponentials are numbers. Applied to the mode expansion it replaces $\hat a$ by $\hat b$ and $\hat b^\dagger$ by $\hat a^\dagger$ (step 1 of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^der-c9-5-8|Derivation §C9.5.8]]). The result has $\hat b$ without a dagger and $\hat a$ with one: as far as the operators go it looks like the Hermitian conjugate of $\hat\psi$, but the four components must stay a column, so the wave functions must not be Hermitian conjugated. What is needed lies between Hermitian and complex conjugation (slide 6: "† on operators but something like complex conjugation on wave functions").
>
> *Source: Lecture 12, slides 6 and 9, and transcript · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.1 (paragraph "The field needs a conjugation")*

> [!definition] Definition §C9.5.2: The Star of a Field Operator
> For the column of operators $\hat\psi$ (components $\hat\psi_a$, $a = 1, \dots, 4$; [[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra#^rem-c5b-2-3|§C5b.2, Remark: A spinor-valued operator, component by component]]),
>
> $$
> \hat\psi^{\ast} \equiv (\hat\psi^\dagger)^{\mathsf T}, \qquad (\hat\psi^{\ast})_a = (\hat\psi_a)^\dagger :
> $$
>
> the dagger on the operators, complex conjugation on the numbers (spinor components and exponentials), and the four components still arranged as a column. $\hat\psi^\dagger$ is the row of [[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra#^thm-c5b-2-2|Theorem §C5b.2.2]].
>
> *Source: Lecture 12, slides 6 and 9 ("$\ast$ on $\hat\psi$ is $\dagger$ on ladder operators but complex conjugate on $u$, $v$") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.1 (Definition "The star of a field operator") · PS §3.6, eq. (3.145) ($\psi^{\ast}(x) = (\psi^\dagger)^{\mathsf T}$)*

^def-c9-5-2

The lecture preferred the star to a dagger because "it reminds me that columns stay columns"; the handwritten notes asked which symbol to use and settled on the star (the user's notes).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.5.2: Mode Expansion of the Starred Field
> For $\hat\psi^{\ast}$ of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-2|Def. §C9.5.2]],
>
> $$
> \hat\psi^{\ast}(x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_{s=1,2}\Bigl(\hat a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}\,u^s(p)^{\ast}\,e^{ip\cdot x} + \hat b^s_{\mathbf p}\,v^s(p)^{\ast}\,e^{-ip\cdot x}\Bigr), \qquad p^0 = E_{\mathbf p} .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.1 (Definition "The star of a field operator", display) · Lecture 12, slide 9 (third line, read backwards)*

^thm-c9-5-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Component a of the row.** By [[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra#^thm-c5b-2-2|Theorem §C5b.2.2]], $\hat\psi^\dagger = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_s\bigl(\hat a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}u^{s\dagger}(p)e^{ip\cdot x} + \hat b^s_{\mathbf p}v^{s\dagger}(p)e^{-ip\cdot x}\bigr)$. The $a$-th entry of the row $u^{s\dagger}$ is $(u^s_a)^{\ast}$, the complex conjugate of the $a$-th component of the column $u^s$; the same for $v$. So $(\hat\psi^\dagger)_a = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_s\bigl(\hat a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}(u^s_a)^{\ast}e^{ip\cdot x} + \hat b^s_{\mathbf p}(v^s_a)^{\ast}e^{-ip\cdot x}\bigr)$.
>
> **2. Transpose.** Transposing arranges the four entries $(\hat\psi^\dagger)_a$ as a column; the column with entries $(u^s_a)^{\ast}$ is $u^s(p)^{\ast}$. No operators are reordered: each term contains one operator.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The operator content of $\hat\psi^{\ast}$ is that of $U(\mathcal C)\hat\psi U(\mathcal C)^{-1}$: $\hat b$ with $e^{-ip\cdot x}$ and $\hat a^\dagger$ with $e^{ip\cdot x}$. The spinors are the wrong ones, $v^{\ast}$ where $U\hat\psi U^{-1}$ has $u$, and $u^{\ast}$ where it has $v$. Charge conjugation works if $u$ can be written through $v^{\ast}$ and $v$ through $u^{\ast}$: Theorems §C9.5.5–6.

## Complex conjugation of two-spinors

The spinors contain two-spinors $\xi^s$ (and $\eta^s$) and the matrices $\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma}$, $\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}$; the lecture works "from the inside", starting with the two-spinors.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.5.3: Conjugation Reverses the Spin of a Two-Spinor
> Define $J : \mathbb C^2 \to \mathbb C^2$ by $J\xi \equiv -i\sigma^2\xi^{\ast}$, with $-i\sigma^2 = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ ([[§CB.0 Linear Algebra in Components꞉ Bases, Changes of Basis, Duals and Tensors#^def-cb-0-19|Def. §CB.0.19]]). Then
> 1. $J$ is antilinear, $J(c\xi) = c^{\ast}J\xi$, and $(J\xi)^\dagger(J\chi) = (\xi^\dagger\chi)^{\ast}$; so $J$ maps orthonormal bases to orthonormal bases;
> 2. $J$ commutes with every $A \in SU(2)$: $J(A\xi) = A\,J\xi$;
> 3. $(-i\sigma^2)\bigl(\tfrac12\sigma^i\bigr)^{\ast}(-i\sigma^2)^{-1} = -\tfrac12\sigma^i$; hence if $(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\xi = \lambda\xi$ for a real unit vector $\hat{\mathbf n}$ and $\lambda = \pm1$, then $(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)J\xi = -\lambda J\xi$: $J$ reverses the spin component along every axis;
> 4. $J^2 = -\mathbb 1$.
>
> *Source: Lecture 12, slide 7 ("$-i\sigma_2(\xi^s)^{\ast} = \xi^{-s}$ is general result"; "Proof (optional): rotation generators of two sides agree") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.2 (Derivation "Conjugating a two-spinor flips the spin", paragraph "Why it holds in every basis"; Caution "The sign in $\xi^{-s}$": "applying the operation twice gives $-1$") · PS §3.6, p. 68 (the identity $\boldsymbol\sigma\sigma^2 = \sigma^2(-\boldsymbol\sigma^{\ast})$ and "two successive spin flips return a spin to $(-1)$ times the original state") · parts 1–2 written here*

^thm-c9-5-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Antilinearity and inner products.** $J(c\xi) = -i\sigma^2c^{\ast}\xi^{\ast} = c^{\ast}J\xi$. For the inner product, $(J\xi)^\dagger = (\xi^{\ast})^\dagger(-i\sigma^2)^\dagger = \xi^{\mathsf T}(i\sigma^2)$, since $\sigma^2$ is Hermitian and $(-i)^{\ast} = i$; then $(J\xi)^\dagger(J\chi) = \xi^{\mathsf T}(i\sigma^2)(-i\sigma^2)\chi^{\ast} = \xi^{\mathsf T}(\sigma^2)^2\chi^{\ast} = \xi^{\mathsf T}\chi^{\ast} = \sum_a\xi_a\chi_a^{\ast} = (\xi^\dagger\chi)^{\ast}$, with $(\sigma^2)^2 = \mathbb 1$ ([[§CB.0 Linear Algebra in Components꞉ Bases, Changes of Basis, Duals and Tensors#^thm-cb-0-21|Theorem §CB.0.21]], 1). Orthonormal vectors therefore go to orthonormal vectors.
>
> **2. Commuting with SU(2).** With $\varepsilon = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix} = i\sigma^2$ and $\varepsilon^{-1} = -\varepsilon = -i\sigma^2$, part 3 of [[§CB.8 New Representations from Old꞉ Tensor Products, Duals, Conjugates and Invariant Tensors#^thm-cb-8-15|Theorem §CB.8.15]] gives $A^{\ast} = \varepsilon A\varepsilon^{-1}$ for $A \in SU(2)$. Then $J(A\xi) = -i\sigma^2A^{\ast}\xi^{\ast} = \varepsilon^{-1}\,\varepsilon A\varepsilon^{-1}\,\xi^{\ast} = A\,\varepsilon^{-1}\xi^{\ast} = A\,J\xi$.
>
> **3. The generators (slide 7).** $(-i\sigma^2)^{-1} = i\sigma^2$, because $(-i\sigma^2)(i\sigma^2) = (\sigma^2)^2 = \mathbb 1$. So $(-i\sigma^2)(\frac12\sigma^i)^{\ast}(i\sigma^2) = \frac12\sigma^2(\sigma^i)^{\ast}\sigma^2 = -\frac12\sigma^i$ by [[§CB.0 Linear Algebra in Components꞉ Bases, Changes of Basis, Duals and Tensors#^thm-cb-0-22|Theorem §CB.0.22]], 3: $\sigma^1$, $\sigma^3$ are real and anticommute with $\sigma^2$, while $\sigma^2$ is imaginary and commutes with itself, so each case gives one minus sign. Now let $(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\xi = \lambda\xi$ with $\hat{\mathbf n}$, $\lambda$ real. Complex conjugation gives $(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^{\ast}\xi^{\ast} = \lambda\xi^{\ast}$. Insert $\mathbb 1 = (i\sigma^2)(-i\sigma^2)$ and multiply by $-i\sigma^2$ on the left:
>
> $$
> (-i\sigma^2)(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^{\ast}(i\sigma^2)\,(-i\sigma^2)\xi^{\ast} = \lambda\,(-i\sigma^2)\xi^{\ast}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad -(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\,J\xi = \lambda\,J\xi .
> $$
>
> So $J\xi$ is an eigenvector of $\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with eigenvalue $-\lambda$: spin $+\frac12$ along $\hat{\mathbf n}$ becomes spin $-\frac12$ along $\hat{\mathbf n}$, for every axis at once. (Part 2 is the finite form of the same statement: rotating and then conjugating is conjugating and then rotating, so the conjugate of a spinor pointing along $\hat{\mathbf n}$ again has a definite direction.)
>
> **4. Twice.** $(J\xi)^{\ast} = (-i\sigma^2)^{\ast}\xi = (i)(\sigma^2)^{\ast}\xi = (i)(-\sigma^2)\xi = -i\sigma^2\xi$, since $\sigma^2$ is imaginary. Hence $J^2\xi = -i\sigma^2(J\xi)^{\ast} = (-i\sigma^2)^2\xi = (-i)^2(\sigma^2)^2\xi = -\xi$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The matrix is $-i\sigma^2$ rather than a plain swap of the two components because only $-i\sigma^2$ makes the operation covariant, the same in every frame (Lecture 12: "the covariant form"); it is the matrix of the equivalence between spin $\frac12$ and its complex conjugate.
> - ⚑ By-product: $J^2 = -1$, not $+1$ → [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^cau-c9-5-1|Caution: The sign in ξ^{−s}]], and the label rule $X^{-(-s)} = -X^s$ of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-4|Def. §C9.5.4]].
> - Part 1 says that $J$ preserves transition probabilities while conjugating amplitudes: it is an antiunitary map of $\mathbb C^2$, the kind of operator time reversal will need on the Hilbert space ([[§C9.7 Time Reversal|§C9.7]]).

> [!example] Example §C9.5.1: Conjugating the Helicity and the Standard Two-Spinors
> For the two-spinors along $\hat{\mathbf n} = (\sin\theta\cos\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\theta)$ ([[§C5a.10 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^thm-c5a-10-11|Theorem §C5a.10.11]]; slide 7 writes them $\xi^\uparrow$, $\xi^\downarrow$),
>
> $$
> \xi^\uparrow = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac\theta2\\ e^{i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2\end{pmatrix}, \quad \xi^\downarrow = \begin{pmatrix}-e^{-i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2\\ \cos\frac\theta2\end{pmatrix} : \qquad -i\sigma^2(\xi^\uparrow)^{\ast} = \xi^\downarrow, \qquad -i\sigma^2(\xi^\downarrow)^{\ast} = -\xi^\uparrow .
> $$
>
> For the standard basis $\xi^1 = (1, 0)^{\mathsf T}$, $\xi^2 = (0, 1)^{\mathsf T}$ (the case $\theta = 0$): $-i\sigma^2(\xi^1)^{\ast} = (0, 1)^{\mathsf T} = \xi^2$ and $-i\sigma^2(\xi^2)^{\ast} = (-1, 0)^{\mathsf T} = -\xi^1$.
>
> *Computation.* $(\xi^\uparrow)^{\ast} = (\cos\frac\theta2, e^{-i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2)^{\mathsf T}$: complex conjugation turns $e^{i\phi}$ into $e^{-i\phi}$, and the result has the components of $\xi^\downarrow$ in the wrong slots and one sign wrong. $\begin{pmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ repairs both: it sends $(a, b)^{\mathsf T}$ to $(-b, a)^{\mathsf T}$, so $-i\sigma^2(\xi^\uparrow)^{\ast} = (-e^{-i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2, \cos\frac\theta2)^{\mathsf T} = \xi^\downarrow$. Next $(\xi^\downarrow)^{\ast} = (-e^{i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2, \cos\frac\theta2)^{\mathsf T} \mapsto (-\cos\frac\theta2, -e^{i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2)^{\mathsf T} = -\xi^\uparrow$. At $\theta = 0$, $\xi^\uparrow = (1, 0)^{\mathsf T}$, $\xi^\downarrow = (0, 1)^{\mathsf T}$, and the two results are the standard-basis lines. ## Complex conjugation of Dirac spinors

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.5.4: Transposes and Conjugates of the Chiral Dirac Matrices
> For the chiral Dirac matrices ([[§C5a.1 Spinor Space and the Clifford Action#^def-c5a-1-3|Def. §C5a.1.3]]; [[§CB.11 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Universal Property and Clifford Modules#^ex-cb-11-13|Example §CB.11.13]]):
> 1. $\gamma^0$ and $\gamma^2$ are symmetric, $\gamma^1$ and $\gamma^3$ antisymmetric: $(\gamma^\mu)^{\mathsf T} = \gamma^\mu$ for $\mu = 0, 2$ and $-\gamma^\mu$ for $\mu = 1, 3$;
> 2. $\gamma^0$, $\gamma^1$, $\gamma^3$ are real and $\gamma^2$ is imaginary, $(\gamma^2)^{\ast} = -\gamma^2$; so $i\gamma^2$ is real;
> 3. $(\gamma^2)^\dagger = -\gamma^2$ and $(-i\gamma^2)^2 = \mathbb 1$;
> 4. $\gamma^0\gamma^2$ anticommutes with $\gamma^0$ and $\gamma^2$ and commutes with $\gamma^1$ and $\gamma^3$; hence $(\gamma^\mu)^{\mathsf T}\gamma^0\gamma^2 = -\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^\mu$ for every $\mu$;
> 5. $(\gamma^0\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T} = \gamma^2\gamma^0$ and $\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^0\gamma^2 = \mathbb 1$.
>
> *Source: Lecture 12, slides 8 ("$i\gamma_2$ is real and $(-i\gamma_2)^2 = 1$"), 10 ("$\gamma^2$ symmetric", "$\gamma^0$ symmetric") and 11 ("Used $(\gamma^\mu)^{\mathsf T} = -\gamma^\mu$ for $\mu = 1, 3$ and $(\gamma^\mu)^{\mathsf T} = \gamma^\mu$ for $\mu = 0, 2$") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.3 (Derivation "Alternative forms", Derivation "Bilinears under C", paragraph "Vector") · PS §3.6, p. 70 ("$\gamma^0$ and $\gamma^2$ are symmetric matrices while $\gamma^1$ and $\gamma^3$ are antisymmetric")*

^thm-c9-5-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> The tools: in $2\times2$ blocks $\gamma^0 = \begin{pmatrix}0 & \mathbb 1\\ \mathbb 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, $\gamma^i = \begin{pmatrix}0 & \sigma^i\\ -\sigma^i & 0\end{pmatrix}$; $\sigma^1$, $\sigma^3$ are real and symmetric, $\sigma^2$ is imaginary and antisymmetric, $(\sigma^2)^{\mathsf T} = -\sigma^2$ ([[§CB.0 Linear Algebra in Components꞉ Bases, Changes of Basis, Duals and Tensors#^def-cb-0-19|Def. §CB.0.19]]); the Clifford relations $(\gamma^0)^2 = \mathbb 1$, $(\gamma^i)^2 = -\mathbb 1$, $\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu = -\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu$ for $\mu \ne \nu$ ([[§CB.11 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Universal Property and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-11-12|Theorem §CB.11.12]], 1).
>
> **1. Transposes.** The transpose of a block matrix transposes each block and exchanges the off-diagonal blocks: $\begin{pmatrix}0 & B\\ C & 0\end{pmatrix}^{\mathsf T} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & C^{\mathsf T}\\ B^{\mathsf T} & 0\end{pmatrix}$. For $\gamma^0$: $B = C = \mathbb 1$, so $(\gamma^0)^{\mathsf T} = \gamma^0$. For $\gamma^i$: $B = \sigma^i$, $C = -\sigma^i$, so $(\gamma^i)^{\mathsf T} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -(\sigma^i)^{\mathsf T}\\ (\sigma^i)^{\mathsf T} & 0\end{pmatrix}$. With $(\sigma^{1,3})^{\mathsf T} = \sigma^{1,3}$ this is $-\gamma^{1,3}$; with $(\sigma^2)^{\mathsf T} = -\sigma^2$ it is $\begin{pmatrix}0 & \sigma^2\\ -\sigma^2 & 0\end{pmatrix} = \gamma^2$. ($\gamma^2$ looks antisymmetric, with $\pm\sigma^2$ in its blocks, but $\sigma^2$ is itself antisymmetric, so the two signs cancel.)
>
> **2. Conjugates.** The entries of $\gamma^0$, $\gamma^1$, $\gamma^3$ are $0$, $\pm1$; those of $\gamma^2$ are $0$, $\pm i$, so $(\gamma^2)^{\ast} = -\gamma^2$, and $(i\gamma^2)^{\ast} = (-i)(-\gamma^2) = i\gamma^2$.
>
> **3. Adjoint and square.** $(\gamma^2)^\dagger = ((\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T})^{\ast} = (\gamma^2)^{\ast} = -\gamma^2$ by parts 1–2. $(-i\gamma^2)^2 = (-i)^2(\gamma^2)^2 = (-1)(-\mathbb 1) = \mathbb 1$.
>
> **4. Commuting with γ⁰γ².** Each move of one $\gamma$ past a different $\gamma$ costs a sign. $\gamma^0(\gamma^0\gamma^2) = \gamma^2$ and $(\gamma^0\gamma^2)\gamma^0 = -\gamma^0\gamma^0\gamma^2 = -\gamma^2$: anticommute. $\gamma^2(\gamma^0\gamma^2) = -\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^2 = \gamma^0$ and $(\gamma^0\gamma^2)\gamma^2 = -\gamma^0$: anticommute. For $\gamma^1$ (and $\gamma^3$), moving it through $\gamma^0\gamma^2$ costs two signs: $\gamma^1\gamma^0\gamma^2 = -\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2 = \gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^1$: commute. Now combine with part 1. For $\mu = 0, 2$: $(\gamma^\mu)^{\mathsf T}\gamma^0\gamma^2 = \gamma^\mu\gamma^0\gamma^2 = -\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^\mu$. For $\mu = 1, 3$: $(\gamma^\mu)^{\mathsf T}\gamma^0\gamma^2 = -\gamma^\mu\gamma^0\gamma^2 = -\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^\mu$. The same result in all four cases (slide 11: "definitely awkward … but this is not entirely random", transcript).
>
> **5. The product.** $(\gamma^0\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T} = (\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T}(\gamma^0)^{\mathsf T} = \gamma^2\gamma^0$. $\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^0\gamma^2 = -\gamma^0\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^2 = -(\mathbb 1)(-\mathbb 1) = \mathbb 1$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Every property singles out $\gamma^2$, the only imaginary Dirac matrix of the chiral basis; this is why charge conjugation involves $\gamma^2$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^cau-c9-5-3|Caution: −iγ² is a statement about the chiral basis]]).
> - Used in: Theorems §C9.5.5–9.5.12.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.5.5: The Conjugate of u Is a v
> For the spinors $u^s(p)$ of [[§C5a.9 Plane-Wave Solutions#^thm-c5a-9-9|Theorem §C5a.9.9]], on-shell $p$ ($p^0 = E_{\mathbf p}$), and $v^s(p)$ of [[§C5a.9 Plane-Wave Solutions#^thm-c5a-9-11|Theorem §C5a.9.11]] with $\eta^s = \xi^{-s}$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-5|Def. §C9.5.5]]),

> $$
> u^s(p)^{\ast} = -i\gamma^2\,v^s(p) ;
> $$
>
> without that choice, the right side is $-i\gamma^2$ times the negative-frequency spinor $\bigl(\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma}\,\xi^{-s}, -\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}\,\xi^{-s}\bigr)$ built on $\xi^{-s}$, which slide 8 writes $v^{-s}(p)$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^cau-c9-5-2|Caution: Which v Is the Conjugate of u?]]).

> *Source: Lecture 12, slide 8 ("$u^s(p)^{\ast} = -i\gamma^2v^{-s}(p)$") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.2 (Derivation "$u^s(p)^{\ast} = -i\gamma^2v^s(p)$", eq. ^thm-c9-5-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Conjugate the column.** By [[§C5a.9 Plane-Wave Solutions#^thm-c5a-9-9|Theorem §C5a.9.9]], $u^s(p) = \bigl(\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma}\,\xi^s, \sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}\,\xi^s\bigr)$. Complex conjugation acts entry by entry, so $(M\xi)^{\ast} = M^{\ast}\xi^{\ast}$ for a matrix $M$, and
>
> $$
> u^s(p)^{\ast} = \begin{pmatrix}\bigl(\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma}\bigr)^{\ast}(\xi^s)^{\ast}\\ \bigl(\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}\bigr)^{\ast}(\xi^s)^{\ast}\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> **2. The conjugate of a square root is the square root of the conjugate.** $p\cdot\sigma$ is Hermitian and positive semidefinite for on-shell $p$ with $p^0 > 0$ ([[§C5a.9 Plane-Wave Solutions#^def-c5a-9-5|Def. §C5a.9.5]]); so is its entrywise conjugate $(p\cdot\sigma)^{\ast} = (p\cdot\sigma)^{\mathsf T}$, and $\bigl(\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma}\bigr)^{\ast}$ is positive semidefinite with square $(p\cdot\sigma)^{\ast}$. By uniqueness of the positive square root ([[§25 Positive Operators#^ladr-7-39|LADR 7.39]]), $\bigl(\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma}\bigr)^{\ast} = \sqrt{(p\cdot\sigma)^{\ast}}$, which the slide writes $\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma^{\ast}}$. The same for $\bar\sigma$.
>
> **3. The two-spinor.** By [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-3|Def. §C9.5.3]], $\xi^{-s} = -i\sigma^2(\xi^s)^{\ast}$; multiply by $(-i\sigma^2)^{-1} = i\sigma^2$: $(\xi^s)^{\ast} = i\sigma^2\xi^{-s}$. So
>
> $$
> u^s(p)^{\ast} = \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma^{\ast}}\;i\sigma^2\,\xi^{-s}\\ \sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma^{\ast}}\;i\sigma^2\,\xi^{-s}\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> **4. $\sigma^{\ast}\to\bar\sigma$: move $i\sigma^2$ to the left.** By [[§CB.0 Linear Algebra in Components꞉ Bases, Changes of Basis, Duals and Tensors#^thm-cb-0-22|Theorem §CB.0.22]], 3, $\sigma^2(\sigma^\mu)^{\ast}\sigma^2 = \bar\sigma^\mu$, hence $\sigma^2(p\cdot\sigma)^{\ast}\sigma^2 = p\cdot\bar\sigma$ (the coefficients $p_\mu$ are real): conjugation flips the sign of $\sigma^2$ only, and moving $\sigma^2$ past $\sigma^1$ and $\sigma^3$ flips theirs (slide 8). Equivalently $(p\cdot\sigma)^{\ast} = \sigma^2(p\cdot\bar\sigma)\sigma^2$ (using $(\sigma^2)^2 = \mathbb 1$). Conjugation by the unitary $\sigma^2 = (\sigma^2)^{-1}$ maps positive semidefinite matrices to positive semidefinite matrices and squares to squares, so by uniqueness again $\sqrt{(p\cdot\sigma)^{\ast}} = \sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}\,\sigma^2$. Then
>
> $$
> \sqrt{p\cdot\sigma^{\ast}}\;i\sigma^2 = \sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}\,\sigma^2\,i\sigma^2 = i\sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}\,(\sigma^2)^2 = i\sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma} ,
> $$
>
> and in the same way $\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma^{\ast}}\;i\sigma^2 = i\sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma}$. So
>
> $$
> u^s(p)^{\ast} = \begin{pmatrix}i\sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}\;\xi^{-s}\\ i\sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma}\;\xi^{-s}\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> The square roots now stand in the order of $u$ with the halves exchanged: $\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}$ on top.
>
> **5. Recognize −iγ² and a v.** Exchanging the halves back and creating the relative minus sign of a $v$ costs an off-diagonal matrix with a minus sign. Check by block multiplication:
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix}0 & -i\sigma^2\\ i\sigma^2 & 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma}\;\xi^{-s}\\ -\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}\;\xi^{-s}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}(-i\sigma^2)(-\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}\;\xi^{-s})\\ i\sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma}\;\xi^{-s}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}i\sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}\;\xi^{-s}\\ i\sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma}\;\xi^{-s}\end{pmatrix} = u^s(p)^{\ast} .
> $$
>
> The matrix is $-i\gamma^2 = -i\begin{pmatrix}0 & \sigma^2\\ -\sigma^2 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, and the column is the negative-frequency spinor of [[§C5a.9 Plane-Wave Solutions#^thm-c5a-9-11|Theorem §C5a.9.11]] with two-spinor $\xi^{-s}$, i.e. $v^s(p)$ when $\eta^s = \xi^{-s}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Two operations, conjugation and moving $i\sigma^2$ across, together turn $\sigma$ into $\bar\sigma$; for charge conjugation the halves must then be exchanged back, which is what $\gamma^2$ (off-diagonal) does. Time reversal will keep them in place ([[§C9.7 Time Reversal|§C9.7]]).
> - ⚑ By-product: the conjugate of a particle spinor with $\xi^s$ is an antiparticle spinor with the flipped two-spinor $\xi^{-s}$. The spin flip sits in the two-spinor; the choice $\eta^s = \xi^{-s}$ absorbs it so that the labels match → [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-5|Def. §C9.5.5]], [[§C9.6 Physical Spin versus Wave-Function Spin|§C9.6]].
> - The user's notes report the identity checked numerically.

> [!caution] Caution: −iγ² is a statement about the chiral basis
> The matrix in [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-8|Theorem §C9.5.8]] depends on the basis of $\gamma$'s; the physics does not. ## Charge conjugation on the field

The lecture derived the law in the chiral basis (Theorem §C9.5.8 below). In any basis of $\gamma$'s the matrix $-i\gamma^2$ is replaced by a charge-conjugation matrix $B$ ([[§CB.18 The Lorentz Case III꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^def-cb-18-18|Def. §CB.18.18]]), and the same steps go through:

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.5.7: Charge Conjugation on the Dirac Field in Any Basis
> Let the Dirac matrices $\gamma^\mu$ be given in any Hermitian basis of spinor space ([[§CB.13 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^def-cb-13-11|Def. §CB.13.11]]), $B$ a unitary charge-conjugation matrix for them, $B(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}B^{-1} = -\gamma^\mu$ ([[§CB.18 The Lorentz Case III꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^def-cb-18-18|Def. §CB.18.18]], [[§CB.18 The Lorentz Case III꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^thm-cb-18-19|Theorem §CB.18.19]]), and $\hat\psi$ the Dirac field ([[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra#^thm-c5b-2-1|Theorem §C5b.2.1]]) with its spinors $u^s(p)$, $v^s(p)$ written in this basis. If
>
> $$
> v^s(p) = B\,u^s(p)^{\ast} \qquad (s = 1, 2;\ \text{every on-shell } p),
> $$
>
> that is $v^s(p) = c(u^s(p))$ for the charge-conjugation map $c$ ([[§CB.18 The Lorentz Case III꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^def-cb-18-20|Def. §CB.18.20]]), then, with $U(\mathcal C)$ of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-1|Def. §C9.5.1]] and the star of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-2|Def. §C9.5.2]],
> 1. $U(\mathcal C)\,\hat\psi(x)\,U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = B\,\hat\psi^{\ast}(x)$, and conversely this law holds only if $v^s(p) = B\,u^s(p)^{\ast}$;
> 2. $B\hat\psi^{\ast}$ obeys the Dirac equation, $(i\slashed\partial - m)\,B\hat\psi^{\ast} = 0$, because $\hat\psi$ does ([[§C5b.6 The Heisenberg Dirac Field#^thm-c5b-6-2|Theorem §C5b.6.2]]).
>
> In the chiral basis, with $B = -i\gamma^2$ ([[§CB.18 The Lorentz Case III꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^ex-cb-18-22|Example §CB.18.22]], 1), the condition is the choice $\eta^s = \xi^{-s}$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-5|Def. §C9.5.5]]) and part 1 is [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-8|Theorem §C9.5.8]].
>

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The condition defines admissible v's.** The $u^s(p)$ solve $(\slashed p - m)u = 0$ and the $v^s(p)$ must solve $(\slashed p + m)v = 0$ ([[§C5a.9 Plane-Wave Solutions#^thm-c5a-9-1|Theorem §C5a.9.1]]). Complex-conjugate the first ($p_\mu$ real): $\bigl(p_\mu(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast} - m\bigr)u^{\ast} = 0$. Multiply by $B$ on the left and insert $B^{-1}B = \mathbb 1$: $\bigl(p_\mu B(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}B^{-1} - m\bigr)Bu^{\ast} = (-\slashed p - m)Bu^{\ast} = 0$, so $Bu^{s\ast}$ is a negative-frequency spinor. Its normalization is right: by [[§CB.18 The Lorentz Case III꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^thm-cb-18-21|Theorem §CB.18.21]], 1, $(Bu^{r\ast})^\dagger(Bu^{s\ast}) = (u^{r\dagger}u^s)^{\ast} = 2E_{\mathbf p}\delta^{rs}$ and $\overline{Bu^{r\ast}}\,Bu^{s\ast} = -(\bar u^ru^s)^{\ast} = -2m\delta^{rs}$, the values of [[§C5a.10 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^thm-c5a-10-3|Theorem §C5a.10.3]] and [[§C5a.10 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^thm-c5a-10-4|Theorem §C5a.10.4]] (from [[§C5a.10 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^thm-c5a-10-1|Theorem §C5a.10.1]], [[§C5a.10 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^thm-c5a-10-2|Theorem §C5a.10.2]]; both forms are the same in every basis reached by a unitary change, [[§C5a.2 The Dirac Form#^thm-c5a-2-2|Theorem §C5a.2.2]]). ⚑ By-product: $v^s = c(u^s)$ is a legitimate choice of the antiparticle spinors in every basis.
>
> **2. In the chiral basis it is the choice $\eta^s = \xi^{-s}$.** With $B = -i\gamma^2$ the condition reads $v^s(p) = -i\gamma^2u^s(p)^{\ast}$, the third form of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-6|Theorem §C9.5.6]], which holds when $\eta^s = \xi^{-s}$. Conversely, [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-5|Theorem §C9.5.5]] gives, for any $\eta^s$, $u^s(p)^{\ast} = -i\gamma^2w^s(p)$ with $w^s(p)$ the negative-frequency spinor built on $\xi^{-s}$; multiplying by $-i\gamma^2$ and using $(-i\gamma^2)^2 = \mathbb 1$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-4|Theorem §C9.5.4]], 3), $-i\gamma^2u^s(p)^{\ast} = w^s(p)$. So the condition says $v^s(p) = w^s(p)$, which at $\mathbf p = 0$ is $\sqrt m(\eta^s, -\eta^s) = \sqrt m(\xi^{-s}, -\xi^{-s})$ ([[§C5a.9 Plane-Wave Solutions#^thm-c5a-9-3|Theorem §C5a.9.3]]), i.e. $\eta^s = \xi^{-s}$ (for $m > 0$; for $m = 0$ use any $\mathbf p \ne 0$). With $e^{i\alpha}B$ in place of $B$ the condition gives $\eta^s = e^{i\alpha}\xi^{-s}$: the phase of $B$ is the phase convention of the $v$'s.
>
> **3. Every other basis.** In a basis $\psi' = M\psi$ the spinors are $Mu^s$, $Mv^s$ ([[§CB.0 Linear Algebra in Components꞉ Bases, Changes of Basis, Duals and Tensors#^thm-cb-0-5|Theorem §CB.0.5]]) and $B' = MB(M^{\ast})^{-1}$ ([[§CB.18 The Lorentz Case III꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^thm-cb-18-19|Theorem §CB.18.19]], 4). Then $B'(Mu^s)^{\ast} = MB(M^{\ast})^{-1}M^{\ast}u^{s\ast} = M\,Bu^{s\ast}$, so the condition holds in the new basis iff it holds in the old one: it is the basis-free statement $v^s = c(u^s)$ ([[§CB.18 The Lorentz Case III꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^thm-cb-18-21|Theorem §CB.18.21]], 5), equivalent to $\eta^s = \xi^{-s}$ by step 2.
>
> **4. Act on the operators only.** As in step 1 of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^der-c9-5-8|Derivation §C9.5.8]]: $U(\mathcal C)$ is linear, the spinors, exponentials and prefactor are numbers for it, and by [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-1|Def. §C9.5.1]] and [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-1|Theorem §C9.5.1]]
>
> $$
> U(\mathcal C)\,\hat\psi(x)\,U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_{s=1,2}\Bigl(\hat b^s_{\mathbf p}\,u^s(p)\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + \hat a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}\,v^s(p)\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **5. u through v: where the sign of $BB^{\ast}$ enters.** Complex-conjugate the condition: $v^s(p)^{\ast} = B^{\ast}u^s(p)$. Multiply by $B$: $Bv^s(p)^{\ast} = BB^{\ast}u^s(p) = u^s(p)$, since $BB^{\ast} = \mathbb 1$ for unitary $B$ ([[§CB.18 The Lorentz Case III꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^thm-cb-18-19|Theorem §CB.18.19]], 5). So
>
> $$
> u^s(p) = B\,v^s(p)^{\ast}, \qquad v^s(p) = B\,u^s(p)^{\ast} .
> $$
>
> ⚑ By-product: this is where $BB^{\ast} = +\mathbb 1$ is used. Were it $-\mathbb 1$, the first relation would read $u^s = -Bv^{s\ast}$, the two terms of step 6 would carry opposite signs, and the image of $\hat\psi$ would not be a matrix times $\hat\psi^{\ast}$.
>
> **6. Rewrite and pull out B.** Insert step 5 into step 4. $B$ is a constant $4\times4$ matrix on the spinor index; it commutes with the operators (which carry no spinor index), the sum and the integral:
>
> $$
> = B\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_{s}\Bigl(\hat b^s_{\mathbf p}\,v^s(p)^{\ast}\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + \hat a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}\,u^s(p)^{\ast}\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **7. Recognize the star.** The integral is $\hat\psi^{\ast}(x)$ of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-2|Theorem §C9.5.2]], whose derivation uses only Def. §C9.5.2 and the mode expansion, in any basis. This proves part 1, "if".
>
> **8. Only if.** If $U(\mathcal C)\hat\psi U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = B\hat\psi^{\ast}$, compare the coefficients of $\hat b^s_{\mathbf p}$ in step 4 and in $B\hat\psi^{\ast}$ (extracted as in step 5 of Derivation §C9.5.8, by [[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra#^thm-c5b-2-3|Theorem §C5b.2.3]]): $u^s(p) = Bv^s(p)^{\ast}$ for all $\mathbf p$, $s$. Conjugate, $u^s(p)^{\ast} = B^{\ast}v^s(p)$, and multiply by $B$: $Bu^s(p)^{\ast} = BB^{\ast}v^s(p) = v^s(p)$.
>
> **9. Sense.** As in step 6 of Derivation §C9.5.8: smeared with $f \in \mathcal S(\mathbb R^4)$, $U(\mathcal C)\hat\psi_a(f)U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = B_{ab}\,\hat\psi_b(\bar f)^\dagger$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-11|Def. §CA.2.11]]).
>
> **10. The Dirac equation (part 2).** Component $a$ of the Dirac equation is $\sum_b\bigl(i(\gamma^\mu)_{ab}\partial_\mu - m\,\delta_{ab}\bigr)\hat\psi_b = 0$. Take the adjoint: it conjugates the numbers, daggers the operators, and commutes with $\partial_\mu$ (smeared with a real test function $f$, $(\partial_\mu\hat\psi_b)(f) = -\hat\psi_b(\partial_\mu f)$, and the adjoint of $\hat\psi_b(f)$ is $\hat\psi_b^\dagger(f)$, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-11|Def. §CA.2.11]]):
>
> $$
> \sum_b\bigl(-i(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}_{ab}\partial_\mu - m\,\delta_{ab}\bigr)\hat\psi_b^\dagger = 0, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \bigl(-i(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}\partial_\mu - m\bigr)\hat\psi^{\ast} = 0 .
> $$
>
> Multiply by $B$ and insert $B^{-1}B$: $\bigl(-iB(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}B^{-1}\partial_\mu - m\bigr)B\hat\psi^{\ast} = (i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)B\hat\psi^{\ast} = 0$. ⚑ By-product: the minus sign in $B(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}B^{-1} = -\gamma^\mu$ restores the $i$ that the adjoint flipped; a matrix with $B(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}B^{-1} = +\gamma^\mu$ would give a solution with $m \to -m$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Charge conjugation is defined on the oscillators (Def. §C9.5.1), not as complex conjugation. On the field it acts as $\dagger$ on the operators together with the basis-free antilinear map $c = B\circ K$ on the spinor index ([[§CB.18 The Lorentz Case III꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^thm-cb-18-21|Theorem §CB.18.21]]). It is plain complex conjugation only in a Majorana basis, where $B = \mathbb 1$ (Example §CB.18.22, 3), and $-i\gamma^2$ times complex conjugation in the chiral and Dirac bases.
> - Steps 4–9 are the lecture's computation (Derivation §C9.5.8) with $-i\gamma^2$ replaced by $B$. Two facts of $B$ are used: $BB^{\ast} = +\mathbb 1$ (step 5) and $B(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}B^{-1} = -\gamma^\mu$ (steps 1 and 10).
> - The condition on the $v$'s is $v^s = c(u^s)$, the basis-free form of $\eta^s = \xi^{-s}$; its meaning for the spin of antiparticles is [[§C9.6 Physical Spin versus Wave-Function Spin|§C9.6]].

> [!derivation]- Derivation

As columns: $-i\gamma^2\hat\psi^{\ast} = -i(\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T}(\hat\psi^\dagger)^{\mathsf T} = -i(\hat\psi^\dagger\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T}$ (slide 10).

>
> **3. Combine with Theorem §C9.5.8.**

^thm-c9-5-10

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Conjugation commutes with the adjoint.** For unitary $U$, $U\hat A^\dagger U^{-1} = (U\hat AU^{-1})^\dagger$ (step 1 of [[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity#^der-c9-4-1|Derivation §C9.4.1]]), applied to each component.
>
> **2. Component a.** By [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-8|Theorem §C9.5.8]], $U\hat\psi_aU^{-1} = -i\sum_b(\gamma^2)_{ab}\hat\psi_b^\dagger$. Its adjoint conjugates the numbers and daggers the operators: $U\hat\psi_a^\dagger U^{-1} = \sum_b\bigl(-i(\gamma^2)_{ab}\bigr)^{\ast}\hat\psi_b = \sum_b i\,(\gamma^2)^{\ast}_{ab}\hat\psi_b = -i\sum_b(\gamma^2)_{ab}\hat\psi_b = -i(\gamma^2\hat\psi)_a$, by $(\gamma^2)^{\ast} = -\gamma^2$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-4|Theorem §C9.5.4]], 2). Arranged as the row $\hat\psi^\dagger$ is: $-i(\gamma^2\hat\psi)^{\mathsf T}$. In the slide's form: $(-i\gamma^2\hat\psi^{\ast})^\dagger = i\hat\psi^{\mathsf T}(\gamma^2)^\dagger = -i\hat\psi^{\mathsf T}\gamma^2 = -i\hat\psi^{\mathsf T}(\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T} = -i(\gamma^2\hat\psi)^{\mathsf T}$, with $(\gamma^2)^\dagger = -\gamma^2$ and $(\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T} = \gamma^2$ (Theorem §C9.5.4, 1 and 3).
>
> **3. Right-multiply by γ⁰.** $\gamma^0$ is a c-number matrix, untouched by $U$: $U\hat{\bar\psi}U^{-1} = (U\hat\psi^\dagger U^{-1})\gamma^0 = -i(\gamma^2\hat\psi)^{\mathsf T}\gamma^0 = -i(\gamma^2\hat\psi)^{\mathsf T}(\gamma^0)^{\mathsf T} = \bigl(-i\gamma^0\gamma^2\hat\psi\bigr)^{\mathsf T}$, using $(\gamma^0)^{\mathsf T} = \gamma^0$ and $(AB)^{\mathsf T} = B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}$ for a c-number matrix $A$ and an operator column $B$ (no operators are reordered).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - "Transposes are harmless": transposing a product reverses the order and removes the transposes (Lecture 12); with one operator per entry nothing is reordered.
> - Used next: the bilinears, which need both laws side by side.

## Two bilinears under charge conjugation

The lecture transformed the scalar $\mathsf S = \hat{\bar\psi}\hat\psi$ and the vector current $\mathsf V^\mu = \hat{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\hat\psi$ (names of [[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity#^thm-c9-4-4|Theorem §C9.4.4]]). As there, a product of two fields at one point is understood normal-ordered and smeared ([[§C5a.6 The Dirac Conjugate and the Bilinears#^rem-c5a-6-2|§C5a.6, Remark: The quantized bilinears are Hermitian]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.5.11: The Scalar Bilinear Is Charge-Conjugation Invariant
>
> $$
> U(\mathcal C)\,\hat{\bar\psi}\hat\psi(x)\,U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = +\hat{\bar\psi}\hat\psi(x) .
> $$
>
> *Parity analogue:* [[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity#^thm-c9-4-4|Theorem §C9.4.4]], row 1.
> *Source: Lecture 12, slide 11 (first item; "'−' from anticommutation of the fields") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.3 (Derivation "Bilinears under C", "Scalar") · PS §3.6, eq. (3.147) · Yu §9.2.1, eqs. (9.119)–(9.121)*

^thm-c9-5-11

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Insert U⁻¹U.** In components $\mathsf S = \sum_c\hat{\bar\psi}_c\hat\psi_c$, so $U\mathsf SU^{-1} = \sum_c(U\hat{\bar\psi}_cU^{-1})(U\hat\psi_cU^{-1})$.
>
> **2. Insert both laws.** By [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-10|Theorem §C9.5.10]], $U\hat{\bar\psi}_cU^{-1} = -i\sum_a(\gamma^0\gamma^2)_{ca}\hat\psi_a$, a component of the "row"; by [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-9|Theorem §C9.5.9]], $U\hat\psi_cU^{-1} = -i\sum_b\hat{\bar\psi}_b(\gamma^0\gamma^2)_{bc}$, a component of the "column" (slide 11). So
>
> $$
> U\mathsf SU^{-1} = (-i)^2\sum_{a,b,c}(\gamma^0\gamma^2)_{ca}(\gamma^0\gamma^2)_{bc}\,\hat\psi_a\hat{\bar\psi}_b = -\sum_{a,b}\bigl(\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^0\gamma^2\bigr)_{ba}\,\hat\psi_a\hat{\bar\psi}_b ,
> $$
>
> the sum over $c$ being the matrix product $\sum_c(\gamma^0\gamma^2)_{bc}(\gamma^0\gamma^2)_{ca}$. ⚑ By-product: a minus sign from $(-i)^2$. The result is a number (a row times a column), but with $\hat\psi$ on the left.
>
> **3. Exchange the fermion fields.** To restore the order of a bilinear, exchange the two field operators. They are fermion operators: $\hat\psi_a\hat{\bar\psi}_b = -\hat{\bar\psi}_b\hat\psi_a + \{\hat\psi_a, \hat{\bar\psi}_b\}$, and at coincident points the anticommutator is a c-number proportional to $\delta^3(\mathbf 0)$ ([[§C5b.1 Canonical Quantization of the Dirac Field#^pr-c5b-1-6|Principle §C5b.1.6]]). Inside a normal-ordered product it does not appear: $U(\mathcal C)$ maps annihilators to annihilators and creators to creators ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^der-c9-5-1|Derivation §C9.5.1]]), so it maps a normal-ordered product to the normal-ordered product of the images, and within normal ordering fermion operators anticommute ([[§C5b.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^def-c5b-3-1|Def. §C5b.3.1]]). So $\hat\psi_a\hat{\bar\psi}_b \to -\hat{\bar\psi}_b\hat\psi_a$: ⚑ By-product: a second minus sign, from fermion statistics (slide 11: "'−' from anticommutation of the fields"). Then
>
> $$
> U\mathsf SU^{-1} = +\sum_{a,b}\hat{\bar\psi}_b\bigl(\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^0\gamma^2\bigr)_{ba}\hat\psi_a = \hat{\bar\psi}\,\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^0\gamma^2\,\hat\psi .
> $$
>
> **4. The matrix.** $\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^0\gamma^2 = \mathbb 1$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-4|Theorem §C9.5.4]], 5), so $U\mathsf SU^{-1} = \hat{\bar\psi}\hat\psi$, at the same point $x$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Two minus signs, $(-i)^2$ and the fermion exchange, cancel; with commuting fields the scalar bilinear would have flipped sign.
> - Without normal ordering the exchange leaves a c-number behind; it is dropped with the normal-ordering constant, and for the charge density it is a vacuum charge ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^rem-c9-5-6|Remark: The number current flips]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.5.12: The Vector Current Changes Sign under Charge Conjugation
>
> $$
> U(\mathcal C)\,\hat{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\hat\psi(x)\,U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = -\hat{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\hat\psi(x), \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \mathsf V^\mu(x) \to -\mathsf V^\mu(x)\quad(\mu = 0, 1, 2, 3) .
> $$
>
> *Parity analogue:* [[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity#^thm-c9-4-4|Theorem §C9.4.4]], row 3 (only the spatial components flip there).
> *Source: Lecture 12, slide 11 (second item) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.3 (Derivation "Bilinears under C", "Vector") · PS §3.6, eq. (3.149) · Yu §9.2.1, eqs. (9.119)–(9.121)*

^thm-c9-5-12

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1–2. As for the scalar, with γ^μ between the fields.** $\mathsf V^\mu = \sum_{c,d}\hat{\bar\psi}_c(\gamma^\mu)_{cd}\hat\psi_d$. Steps 1–2 of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^der-c9-5-11|Derivation §C9.5.11]] give
>
> $$
> U\mathsf V^\mu U^{-1} = (-i)^2\sum_{a,b,c,d}(\gamma^0\gamma^2)_{ca}(\gamma^\mu)_{cd}(\gamma^0\gamma^2)_{bd}\,\hat\psi_a\hat{\bar\psi}_b = -\sum_{a,b}N_{ab}\,\hat\psi_a\hat{\bar\psi}_b, \qquad N = (\gamma^0\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T}\gamma^\mu(\gamma^0\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T},
> $$
>
> since $\sum_{c,d}(\gamma^0\gamma^2)_{ca}(\gamma^\mu)_{cd}(\gamma^0\gamma^2)_{bd} = \sum_{c,d}\bigl((\gamma^0\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T}\bigr)_{ac}(\gamma^\mu)_{cd}\bigl((\gamma^0\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T}\bigr)_{db}$.
>
> **3. Exchange the fields.** As in step 3 there, $\hat\psi_a\hat{\bar\psi}_b \to -\hat{\bar\psi}_b\hat\psi_a$ under normal ordering: $U\mathsf V^\mu U^{-1} = +\sum_{a,b}\hat{\bar\psi}_bN_{ab}\hat\psi_a = \hat{\bar\psi}N^{\mathsf T}\hat\psi$, with $N^{\mathsf T} = \gamma^0\gamma^2(\gamma^\mu)^{\mathsf T}\gamma^0\gamma^2$ (transpose of a product of three matrices; $((\gamma^0\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T} = \gamma^0\gamma^2$). This is slide 11's $\bar\psi\gamma^0\gamma^2(\gamma^\mu)^{\mathsf T}\gamma^0\gamma^2\psi$.
>
> **4. Remove the transpose.** $(\gamma^\mu)^{\mathsf T}\gamma^0\gamma^2 = -\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^\mu$ for every $\mu$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-4|Theorem §C9.5.4]], 4): for $\mu = 0, 2$ the transpose does nothing and moving $\gamma^\mu$ through $\gamma^0\gamma^2$ costs a sign; for $\mu = 1, 3$ the transpose costs the sign and the move is free. So $N^{\mathsf T} = -\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^\mu$.
>
> **5. The matrix.** $\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^0\gamma^2 = \mathbb 1$ (Theorem §C9.5.4, 5; slide 11: "square my $\gamma^0$'s … square $\gamma^2$, which is minus the identity"), so $N^{\mathsf T} = -\gamma^\mu$ and $U\mathsf V^\mu U^{-1} = -\hat{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\hat\psi$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The same two signs as for the scalar cancel; the one sign left is that of step 4, the same for all four components, so the whole four-vector flips.
> - Used in: [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^rem-c9-5-6|Remark: The number current flips]].

> [!remark] Remark: The number current flips
> $\mathsf V^\mu = \hat{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\hat\psi$ is the number current, the Noether current of the phase symmetry $\psi \to e^{i\alpha}\psi$ ([[§C5a.8 Canonical Structure and Noether Currents of the Dirac Field#^thm-c5a-8-3|Theorem §C5a.8.3]]), proportional to the electric current; its charge $\hat Q = \int d^3x\,\mathsf V^0$ counts fermions minus antifermions ([[§C5b.5 The U(1) Charge, Particles and Antiparticles#^thm-c5b-5-3|Theorem §C5b.5.3]]). Under charge conjugation the whole four-vector flips ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-12|Theorem §C9.5.12]]): the charge density, because particles and antiparticles carry opposite charge, and the spatial current with it. Integrating $\mu = 0$, $U(\mathcal C)\hat QU(\mathcal C)^{-1} = -\hat Q$, which is also immediate in modes, $\sum_s\int(\hat a^\dagger\hat a - \hat b^\dagger\hat b) \to \sum_s\int(\hat b^\dagger\hat b - \hat a^\dagger\hat a)$. Compare parity, which flips only the spatial current ([[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity#^thm-c9-4-4|Theorem §C9.4.4]], row 3), and time reversal, which also flips only the spatial current ([[§C9.7 Time Reversal|§C9.7]]). The c-number dropped in the fermion exchange is, for $\mu = 0$, the vacuum charge of the unordered density ([[§C5b.5 The U(1) Charge, Particles and Antiparticles#^rem-c5b-5-1|§C5b.5, Remark: The vacuum charge, and why the order is forced]]): a vacuum with nonzero charge would not be invariant under $C$, which is why normal ordering is physics here and not convenience. Peskin–Schroeder make the same point: $C$ exchanges $\psi$ and $\bar\psi$ but does not change the order of creation and annihilation operators, so the subtracted constant does not reappear (p. 71).
>
> *Source: Lecture 12, slide 11 ("Interpretation: $\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi$ = number current (electric current) flips sign under interchange of particles and antiparticles"), and transcript · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.3 (paragraph "Interpretation") · PS §3.6, p. 71 · the mode form of $\hat Q$ written here*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The matrix $-i\gamma^2 = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -i\sigma^2\\ i\sigma^2 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ exchanges the two Weyl halves, each with $\pm i\sigma^2$ and a complex conjugation: complex conjugation maps $(\frac12, 0)$ to $(0, \frac12)$ and $\sigma^2$ repairs the matrices, the fact behind Peskin–Schroeder's $\sigma^2\psi_L^{\ast}$ — [[§C5a.5 Chirality and Weyl Spinors#^thm-c5a-5-4|Theorem §C5a.5.4]], [[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)#^thm-cb-17-13|Theorem §CB.17.13]], [[§CB.0 Linear Algebra in Components꞉ Bases, Changes of Basis, Duals and Tensors#^thm-cb-0-22|Theorem §CB.0.22]].
> - The flip $\xi \mapsto -i\sigma^2\xi^{\ast}$ is the spin-$\frac12$ time-reversal operator of Quantum Mechanics up to a phase ($\Theta = \eta e^{-i\pi S_y/\hbar}K$, with $e^{-i\pi\sigma^2/2} = -i\sigma^2$): the same antiunitary map reverses spin there and here — [[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^thm-c8-3-6|QM Theorem §C8.3.6]], [[§C9.7 Time Reversal|§C9.7]].
> - Parity needed opposite phases for particle and antiparticle; charge conjugation needs none but fixes the antiparticle two-spinors instead. In both cases the condition comes from asking the transformed pieces to reassemble into a field — [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^thm-c9-3-6|Theorem §C9.3.6]], [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-8|Theorem §C9.5.8]].
> - $\hat{\bar\psi}$ is $\hat\psi$ with particles and antiparticles exchanged; charge conjugation is the operator that makes this symmetry explicit — [[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra#^rem-c5b-2-2|§C5b.2, Remark: ψ̄ is ψ with particles and antiparticles interchanged]], [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-10|Theorem §C9.5.10]].
> - The opposite charges of the two species ([[§C5b.5 The U(1) Charge, Particles and Antiparticles#^thm-c5b-5-4|Theorem §C5b.5.4]]) are the content of $\mathsf V^\mu \to -\mathsf V^\mu$; for the complex scalar the analogous exchange $\hat a \leftrightarrow \hat b$ reverses $\hat Q$ in the same way — [[§C2a.5 The Complex Scalar Field and Its Charge#^thm-c2a-5-7|Theorem §C2a.5.7]].
> - Charge conjugation is not complex conjugation by definition: it is defined on the oscillators, and on the spinor index it acts by an antilinear map $c = B\circ K$ that exists in every basis because complex conjugation of the $\gamma$'s gives another Clifford module (Pauli's theorem); $c^2 = +1$ is the algebraic fact that lets Majorana spinors exist — [[§CB.18 The Lorentz Case III꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^thm-cb-18-19|Theorem §CB.18.19]], [[§CB.18 The Lorentz Case III꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^thm-cb-18-21|Theorem §CB.18.21]], [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-7|Theorem §C9.5.7]].
> - Combined with parity and time reversal, charge conjugation enters $CPT$, the one discrete symmetry every local Lorentz-invariant theory has — [[§C9.8 CPT and the CPT Theorem|§C9.8]].
