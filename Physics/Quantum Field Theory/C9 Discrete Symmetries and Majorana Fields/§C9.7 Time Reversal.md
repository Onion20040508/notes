How does running the movie backwards act on the quantum Dirac field? Time reversal is the second discrete element of the Lorentz group, $\mathcal T = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ ([[§C9.1 Discrete Lorentz Transformations#^def-c9-1-1|Def. §C9.1.1]]; [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-2|REL Def. §B1.2.2]]). Lecture 12, Part C, follows the method of parity ([[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field|§C9.3]]) and of charge conjugation ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation|§C9.5]]): define the action on the ladder operators, rewrite the wave functions, reassemble the field. One thing is new and, in the lecture's words, "goes against everything said so far": the operator $U(\mathcal T)$ must complex-conjugate every number it passes, so it is antilinear and antiunitary. The wave-function identity behind time reversal reuses the conjugation of spinors of §C9.5, with the halves of the Dirac spinor kept in place; the label rule $X^{-2} = -X^1$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-4|Def. §C9.5.4]]) turns out to be forced. The section ends with the vector current, the bilinear the lecture transformed. Notation as in [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation|§C9.5]].

## What time reversal should do

> [!remark] Remark: A movie run backwards
> A particle seen in a movie run backwards is not an antiparticle. It is the same particle with opposite momentum, and its spin turns the other way: spin is an angular momentum, and under $t \to -t$ with $\mathbf x$ fixed, $\mathbf p \to -\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf x\times\mathbf p$ flips (slide 19: "$\mathcal T$ reverses $\vec p$ and $\vec s = \vec x\times\vec p$"). Compare parity, which flips $\mathbf p$ but not the spin ([[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^rem-c9-3-1|§C9.3, Remark: Momentum flips, spin does not]]), and charge conjugation, which flips neither but exchanges the species ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-1|Def. §C9.5.1]]). If $\mathcal T$ were a symmetry, predicting the future from initial conditions and the past from final conditions would be equivalent ([[§C9.1 Discrete Lorentz Transformations#^rem-c9-1-2|§C9.1, Remark: What Lecture 11 adds to the four pieces]]).
>
> *Source: Lecture 12, slide 19, and transcript · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.5 (paragraph "What it should do") · PS §3.6, p. 67 (figure: $T$ flips momentum and spin)*

^def-c9-7-1

> [!definition] Definition §C9.7.2: Time Reversal on the Oscillators
> **Time reversal** on the Fock space of the Dirac field is an operator $U(\mathcal T)$ with
>
> $$
> U(\mathcal T)\,\hat a^s_{\mathbf p}\,U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = \hat a^{-s}_{-\mathbf p}, \qquad U(\mathcal T)\,\hat b^s_{\mathbf p}\,U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = \hat b^{-s}_{-\mathbf p}, \qquad U(\mathcal T)|0\rangle = |0\rangle ,
> $$
>
> with $-s$ in the sense of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-4|Def. §C9.5.4]] ($\hat a^{-1} = \hat a^2$, $\hat a^{-2} = -\hat a^1$, the same for $\hat b$) and further phases set to $1$. Momentum and spin both flip. The goal is a law of the form $U(\mathcal T)\hat\psi(t, \mathbf x)U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = M\,\hat\psi(-t, \mathbf x)$ with a constant $4\times4$ matrix $M$ (slide 19).
>
> *Parity analogue:* [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^def-c9-3-1|Def. §C9.3.1]].
> *Source: Lecture 12, slide 19 ("Phases on RHS chosen to 1 without loss of generality") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.5 (Definition "Time reversal on the oscillators", eq. (Tosc)) · PS §3.6, eqs. (3.137)–(3.138) · Yu §9.2.4, eqs. (9.203)–(9.205), (9.208) (helicity basis, phases $\zeta_T$)*

## Time reversal is antilinear

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.7.1: Time Reversal Must Be Antilinear
> Let $U(\mathcal T)$ commute with the Hamiltonian $\hat H$ and satisfy $U(\mathcal T)\hat\psi(t, \mathbf x)U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = M\hat\psi(-t, \mathbf x)$ for an invertible constant matrix $M$. Then $U(\mathcal T)$ is not linear. The lecture's way out: $U(\mathcal T)$ commutes with $\hat H$ but not with numbers,
>
> $$
> U(\mathcal T)\,c\,U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = c^{\ast}\quad\text{for every } c \in \mathbb C, \qquad\text{so}\qquad U(\mathcal T)\,e^{-i\hat Ht}\,U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = e^{i\hat Ht} :
> $$
>
> $U(\mathcal T)$ is **antilinear** ([[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries#^def-cb-19-3|Def. §CB.19.3]]). The alternative $U\hat HU^{-1} = -\hat H$ is excluded because the spectrum of $\hat H$ is bounded below and not above.
>
> *Source: Lecture 12, slide 21 ("$U(\mathcal T)$ commutes with the Hamiltonian. Conclusion: $U(\mathcal T)$ does not commute with numbers"), and transcript · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.5 (Derivation "Time reversal must be antilinear", eq. (antilinear)) · PS §3.6, p. 67, eq. (3.133) · Yu §9.1.2, eqs. (9.49)–(9.52)*

^thm-c9-7-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Where the time dependence comes from.** The Heisenberg field is $\hat\psi(t, \mathbf x) = e^{i\hat Ht}\hat\psi(0, \mathbf x)e^{-i\hat Ht}$ ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^def-c2b-1-1|Def. §C2b.1.1]]; [[§C5b.6 The Heisenberg Dirac Field#^thm-c5b-6-1|Theorem §C5b.6.1]]), with $\hat\psi(0, \mathbf x)$ the Schrödinger-picture field (slide 21: "Schrödinger wave fct."). What is needed is $\hat\psi(-t, \mathbf x) = e^{-i\hat Ht}\hat\psi(0, \mathbf x)e^{i\hat Ht}$.
>
> **2. A linear U gives the wrong sign of t.** Insert $U^{-1}U = \mathbb 1$ twice: $U\hat\psi(t, \mathbf x)U^{-1} = (Ue^{i\hat Ht}U^{-1})(U\hat\psi(0, \mathbf x)U^{-1})(Ue^{-i\hat Ht}U^{-1})$. If $U$ is linear and commutes with $\hat H$, it commutes with every power series in $\hat H$ with complex coefficients, so $Ue^{\pm i\hat Ht}U^{-1} = e^{\pm i\hat Ht}$, and with the assumed law at $t = 0$, $U\hat\psi(0, \mathbf x)U^{-1} = M\hat\psi(0, \mathbf x)$:
>
> $$
> U\hat\psi(t, \mathbf x)U^{-1} = e^{i\hat Ht}M\hat\psi(0, \mathbf x)e^{-i\hat Ht} = M\hat\psi(t, \mathbf x) .
> $$
>
> Equating with $M\hat\psi(-t, \mathbf x)$ and multiplying by $M^{-1}$ gives $\hat\psi(t, \mathbf x) = \hat\psi(-t, \mathbf x)$ for all $t$. That is false: by [[§C5b.6 The Heisenberg Dirac Field#^thm-c5b-6-1|Theorem §C5b.6.1]] the coefficient of $\hat a^s_{\mathbf p}$ in $\hat\psi(t, \mathbf x)$ carries $e^{-iE_{\mathbf p}t}$, in $\hat\psi(-t, \mathbf x)$ it carries $e^{+iE_{\mathbf p}t}$, and the coefficients can be extracted ([[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra#^thm-c5b-2-3|Theorem §C5b.2.3]]). No $4\times4$ matrix can repair the time dependence (transcript: "I have no problem with you having a $\gamma^1$, $\gamma^3$ … But this is still not going to work").
>
> **3. Way out 1, excluded.** If instead $U\hat HU^{-1} = -\hat H$ with $U$ linear, then $Ue^{i\hat Ht}U^{-1} = e^{-i\hat Ht}$ and the time dependence would come out right; but an eigenstate $|E\rangle$ of $\hat H$ would go to $U|E\rangle$ with $\hat HU|E\rangle = -U\hat H|E\rangle = -EU|E\rangle$. The spectrum of $:\!\hat H\!:$ is $[0, \infty)$ ([[§C5b.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^thm-c5b-3-5|Theorem §C5b.3.5]]), so it would have to contain $(-\infty, 0]$ as well, which it does not. (Yu (9.49) makes the same argument: $T^{-1}HT = \mathcal T^0{}_0P^0 = -H$ for a linear unitary $T$.)
>
> **4. Way out 2.** Keep $[U, \hat H] = 0$ but let $U$ conjugate numbers, $UcU^{-1} = c^{\ast}$. Then in the power series every coefficient $(-it)^n/n!$ is conjugated while $\hat H^n$ passes through: $Ue^{-i\hat Ht}U^{-1} = \sum_n\frac{(it)^n}{n!}\hat H^n = e^{i\hat Ht}$ (slide 21), and step 2 now gives $U\hat\psi(t, \mathbf x)U^{-1} = e^{-i\hat Ht}(U\hat\psi(0, \mathbf x)U^{-1})e^{i\hat Ht}$: the time dependence of $\hat\psi(-t, \mathbf x)$. The relation $UcU^{-1} = c^{\ast}$ means $U(c\Psi) = c^{\ast}U\Psi$ for every state: $U$ is antilinear.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ By-product: antilinearity is forced by $[U, \hat H] = 0$ together with $t \to -t$; it is a consequence of the time dependence $e^{-i\hat Ht}$ of quantum mechanics, not an extra postulate → [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-2|Theorem §C9.7.2]].
> - Assumptions used: a lower bound on the energy, and a field law with a constant matrix.

^der-c9-7-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation (second route: on the vacuum, Peskin–Schroeder)
> Assume $T$ linear, $[T, \hat H] = 0$, $\hat H|0\rangle = 0$, $T|0\rangle = |0\rangle$, and write PS's $T\cdots T$ for $U\cdots U^{-1}$. Then $T\hat\psi(t, \mathbf x)T|0\rangle = e^{i\hat Ht}\bigl[T\hat\psi(\mathbf x)T\bigr]|0\rangle$. The right side is a sum of negative-frequency terms only ($e^{i\hat Ht}$ acting on states of positive energy). If $T$ is to reverse the time dependence, the left side is, up to a constant matrix, $\hat\psi(-t, \mathbf x)|0\rangle = e^{-i\hat Ht}\hat\psi(\mathbf x)|0\rangle$, a sum of positive-frequency terms. So $T$ cannot be a linear unitary operator; the way out is $T(\text{c-number}) = (\text{c-number})^{\ast}T$, PS eq. (3.133).
>
> *Source: PS §3.6, p. 67*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.7.2: Time Reversal Is Antiunitary
> If time reversal is a symmetry, i.e. maps rays to rays preserving transition probabilities ([[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries#^def-cb-19-2|Def. §CB.19.2]]), then $U(\mathcal T)$ is **antiunitary** ([[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries#^def-cb-19-4|Def. §CB.19.4]]): antilinear, and
>
> $$
> \langle U(\mathcal T)\Psi_1, U(\mathcal T)\Psi_2\rangle = \langle\Psi_1, \Psi_2\rangle^{\ast}\qquad\text{for all states } \Psi_1, \Psi_2 .
> $$
>
> It is fixed by [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^def-c9-7-2|Def. §C9.7.2]]: on a Fock state $\sum c\,\hat a^\dagger\cdots\hat b^\dagger|0\rangle$ it conjugates the coefficients $c$ and transforms each creator.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.5 (Derivation "Time reversal must be antilinear": "It is antiunitary if in addition … which keeps probabilities") · Yu §9.1.2, eqs. (9.49)–(9.53) · PS §3.6, p. 67 ("$T$ is referred to as an antilinear or antiunitary operator")*

^thm-c9-7-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Wigner.** A symmetry is induced by an operator that is unitary or antiunitary ([[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries#^thm-cb-19-7|Theorem §CB.19.7]]; the Fock space has dimension $\ge 2$, so the kind is determined).
>
> **2. Not unitary.** A unitary operator is linear, which [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-1|Theorem §C9.7.1]] excludes. So $U(\mathcal T)$ is antiunitary, unique up to a phase; the phase is fixed by $U(\mathcal T)|0\rangle = |0\rangle$.
>
> **3. Fixed by the oscillators.** Every Fock state is a linear combination of products of creators applied to $|0\rangle$ ([[§C5b.4 Fermions, Fock Space and the Pauli Principle#^thm-c5b-4-1|Theorem §C5b.4.1]]). Antilinearity and $U\hat A^\dagger U^{-1} = (U\hat AU^{-1})^\dagger$ ([[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries#^thm-cb-19-16|Theorem §CB.19.16]], 3; for the creators, with real coefficients in [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^def-c9-7-2|Def. §C9.7.2]]) give $U\bigl(c\,\hat a^{s_1\dagger}_{\mathbf p_1}\cdots|0\rangle\bigr) = c^{\ast}\,\hat a^{-s_1\dagger}_{-\mathbf p_1}\cdots|0\rangle$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - By [[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries#^thm-cb-19-6|Theorem §CB.19.6]], $U(\mathcal T) = VK$ with $K$ the complex conjugation in the Fock basis and $V$ unitary: "it is kind of as if it acts not on the kets but on the bras" (Lecture 12) → [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^cau-c9-7-1|Caution: This goes against everything said so far]].
> - Quantum Mechanics reached the same conclusion for one particle ([[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^thm-c8-3-3|QM Theorem §C8.3.3]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.7.3: Time Reversal Conjugates Every Number in the Field
> Under the antiunitary $U(\mathcal T)$ ([[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-2|Theorem §C9.7.2]]), in any expression built from field or mode operators:
> 1. every c-number coefficient is complex-conjugated: for a constant matrix $M$ on the spinor index of an operator column $\hat\Psi$, $U(\mathcal T)(M\hat\Psi)U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = M^{\ast}\,U(\mathcal T)\hat\Psi U(\mathcal T)^{-1}$; the spinors $u^s(p)$, $v^s(p)$ multiplying $\hat a$, $\hat b^\dagger$ become $u^s(p)^{\ast}$, $v^s(p)^{\ast}$; plane waves $e^{\mp ip\cdot x}$ become $e^{\pm ip\cdot x}$ — slide 21's shorthand "$U(\mathcal T)\gamma^\mu U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = (\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}$, $U(\mathcal T)u^s(p)U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = u^s(p)^{\ast}$";
> 2. products of operators keep their order;
> 3. adjoints are preserved: $U(\mathcal T)\hat A^\dagger U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = \bigl(U(\mathcal T)\hat AU(\mathcal T)^{-1}\bigr)^\dagger$.
>
> *Source: Lecture 12, slide 21 ("Examples") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.5 (Derivation "Time reversal must be antilinear", last sentences: "The matrices and spinors are arrays of numbers, and every number is conjugated"; Derivation "Bilinears under T": "$U(T)$ conjugates numbers but does not reverse the order of operators") · Yu §9.1.2, eqs. (9.51)–(9.52)*

^thm-c9-7-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Numbers.** [[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries#^thm-cb-19-16|Theorem §CB.19.16]], 1, with the column $\hat\Psi$ and the matrix $M$. In the mode expansion each term is a number (spinor component times plane wave times $1/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$) times one mode operator; the numbers are conjugated term by term, the real prefactor unchanged. The slide's "$U\gamma^\mu U^{-1} = (\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}$" means exactly this: $\gamma^\mu$ is an array of numbers standing in front of operators, not an operator on the Hilbert space.
>
> **2–3. Products and adjoints.** Parts 2 and 3 of Theorem §CB.19.16.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - In the field this hits the spinors, the $\gamma$'s, the plane waves and every explicit $i$; it does not touch $t$ and $\mathbf x$, which are labels of the operator, not coefficients.

*Uses:* [[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries#^thm-cb-19-16|Theorem §CB.19.16]], [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-2|Theorem §C9.7.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: This goes against everything said so far
> Until now the $U$ operators acted on the Hilbert space, on the $\hat a$'s and $\hat b$'s, and went straight through numbers, spinors and $\gamma$'s ([[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^cau-c9-3-3|§C9.3, Caution: U(𝒫) and γ⁰ are different objects]]). $U(\mathcal T)$ does not: it conjugates them ([[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-3|Theorem §C9.7.3]]). ## Time reversal on the spinors

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.7.4: The Matrix γ¹γ³
> In the chiral basis ([[§C5a.1 Spinor Space and the Clifford Action#^def-c5a-1-3|Def. §C5a.1.3]]),
> 1. $\gamma^1\gamma^3 = \begin{pmatrix}i\sigma^2 & 0\\ 0 & i\sigma^2\end{pmatrix}$, a real matrix;
> 2. $(\gamma^1\gamma^3)^2 = -\mathbb 1$, and $(\gamma^1\gamma^3)^\dagger = \gamma^3\gamma^1 = -\gamma^1\gamma^3 = (\gamma^1\gamma^3)^{-1}$;
> 3. $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ commutes with $\gamma^0$ and with $\gamma^2$, and anticommutes with $\gamma^1$ and $\gamma^3$;
> 4. $(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}\,\gamma^1\gamma^3 = \gamma^1\gamma^3\,\gamma_\mu$ for each $\mu$, with the *lower* index ($\gamma_0 = \gamma^0$, $\gamma_i = -\gamma^i$).
>
> *Source: Lecture 12, slides 20 ("$\begin{pmatrix}i\sigma_2 & 0\\ 0 & i\sigma_2\end{pmatrix} = \gamma^1\gamma^3$") and 23 ("$\ast$ flips sign on $\gamma^2$ only so $(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}\gamma^1\gamma^3 = \gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma_\mu$ (lower $\mu$)") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.5 (Derivations "$u^s(p)^{\ast} = \gamma^1\gamma^3u^{-s}(\tilde p)$" and "Bilinears under T", "Vector") · Yu §9.2.4, eq. (9.198) ($\mathcal C\gamma^5 = \operatorname{diag}(i\sigma^2, i\sigma^2)$)*

^thm-c9-7-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The blocks.** The spatial $\gamma$'s are off-diagonal, $\gamma^i = \begin{pmatrix}0 & \sigma^i\\ -\sigma^i & 0\end{pmatrix}$, so their product is block diagonal: $\gamma^1\gamma^3 = \begin{pmatrix}\sigma^1(-\sigma^3) & 0\\ 0 & (-\sigma^1)\sigma^3\end{pmatrix} = \operatorname{diag}(-\sigma^1\sigma^3, -\sigma^1\sigma^3)$. By the product rule ([[§CB.0 Linear Algebra in Components꞉ Bases, Changes of Basis, Duals and Tensors#^thm-cb-0-21|Theorem §CB.0.21]], 1), $\sigma^3\sigma^1 = i\sigma^2$ and $\sigma^1\sigma^3 = -i\sigma^2$, so $-\sigma^1\sigma^3 = i\sigma^2 = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, real. ("We have to check the $i$'s and all that. Yup, it turns out that just works", Lecture 12.)
>
> **2. Square, adjoint, inverse.** $(\gamma^1\gamma^3)^2 = \gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^1\gamma^3 = -\gamma^1\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^3 = -(-\mathbb 1)(-\mathbb 1) = -\mathbb 1$ ([[§CB.11 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Universal Property and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-11-12|Theorem §CB.11.12]], 1). $(\gamma^1\gamma^3)^\dagger = (\gamma^3)^\dagger(\gamma^1)^\dagger = (-\gamma^3)(-\gamma^1) = \gamma^3\gamma^1 = -\gamma^1\gamma^3$ ([[§CB.11 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Universal Property and Clifford Modules#^ex-cb-11-13|Example §CB.11.13]]: $\gamma^{i\dagger} = -\gamma^i$), and $(-\gamma^1\gamma^3)(\gamma^1\gamma^3) = -(\gamma^1\gamma^3)^2 = \mathbb 1$.
>
> **3. Commutation.** Moving $\gamma^0$ or $\gamma^2$ through $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ costs two signs; moving $\gamma^1$: $\gamma^1(\gamma^1\gamma^3) = -\gamma^3$ and $(\gamma^1\gamma^3)\gamma^1 = -\gamma^1\gamma^1\gamma^3 = \gamma^3$, opposite; $\gamma^3$ likewise.
>
> **4. Conjugates.** $\gamma^0$, $\gamma^1$, $\gamma^3$ are real and $(\gamma^2)^{\ast} = -\gamma^2$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-4|Theorem §C9.5.4]], 2). Case by case, with part 3: $\mu = 0$: $\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^3 = \gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^0 = \gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma_0$. $\mu = 1$: $\gamma^1\gamma^1\gamma^3 = -\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^1 = \gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma_1$. $\mu = 2$: $(-\gamma^2)\gamma^1\gamma^3 = -\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^2 = \gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma_2$. $\mu = 3$: $\gamma^3\gamma^1\gamma^3 = -\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^3 = \gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma_3$. Complex conjugation flips the sign of $\gamma^2$ only; commuting through $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ flips $\gamma^1$ and $\gamma^3$; together every spatial $\gamma$ is flipped.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ is block diagonal, so it keeps the Weyl halves in place (unlike $\gamma^0$ for parity and $-i\gamma^2$ for charge conjugation, both off-diagonal); its blocks are the $i\sigma^2$ of two-spinor conjugation ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-3|Theorem §C9.5.3]], with $i\sigma^2 = -(-i\sigma^2)$).
> - Part 4 is what makes the vector current transform with $\mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu$ (Theorem §C9.7.9).

> **1. Copied from charge conjugation.** Steps 1–4 of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^der-c9-5-5|Derivation §C9.5.5]] (slide 20: "the first step copied from analysis of charge conjugation") gave
>
> $$
> u^s(p)^{\ast} = \begin{pmatrix}i\sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}\;\xi^{-s}\\ i\sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma}\;\xi^{-s}\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> **2. Keep the halves in place.** For charge conjugation the halves were exchanged back, because $\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}$ stood on top. Now read the upper root through the parity identity $p\cdot\bar\sigma = \tilde p\cdot\sigma$ and the lower through $p\cdot\sigma = \tilde p\cdot\bar\sigma$, valid also for the positive roots ([[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^thm-c9-3-2|Theorem §C9.3.2]]): the order is that of a $u$ with flipped three-momentum (transcript: "$p\cdot\bar\sigma$ is actually sort of the $\tilde p\cdot\sigma$ … That's why I wanted it"):
>
> $$
> u^s(p)^{\ast} = \begin{pmatrix}i\sigma^2\sqrt{\tilde p\cdot\sigma}\;\xi^{-s}\\ i\sigma^2\sqrt{\tilde p\cdot\bar\sigma}\;\xi^{-s}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}i\sigma^2 & 0\\ 0 & i\sigma^2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\tilde p\cdot\sigma}\;\xi^{-s}\\ \sqrt{\tilde p\cdot\bar\sigma}\;\xi^{-s}\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> **3. Recognize.** The column is $u$ of [[§C5a.9 Plane-Wave Solutions#^thm-c5a-9-9|Theorem §C5a.9.9]] at momentum $\tilde p$ (on shell, $\tilde p^0 = p^0$) with two-spinor $\xi^{-s}$, i.e. $u^{-s}(\tilde p)$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-4|Def. §C9.5.4]]: $u^{-1} = u^2$, $u^{-2} = -u^1$, since $u$ is linear in its two-spinor and $\xi^{-1} = \xi^2$, $\xi^{-2} = -\xi^1$). The block matrix is $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ ([[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-4|Theorem §C9.7.4]], 1).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Conjugation flips the spin ($\xi^s \to \xi^{-s}$) and, together with moving $i\sigma^2$ across, exchanges $\sigma$ and $\bar\sigma$, which is the same as flipping the three-momentum: exactly what a time-reversed particle needs (Remark: A movie run backwards).

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> The slide says "similarly"; the steps, with the differences.
>
> **1. Conjugating η^s.** With $J\xi = -i\sigma^2\xi^{\ast}$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-3|Theorem §C9.5.3]]), $\eta^s = J\xi^s$, so $-i\sigma^2(\eta^s)^{\ast} = J\eta^s = J^2\xi^s = -\xi^s$ (part 4). > **2. >

> v^s(p)^{\ast} = \begin{pmatrix}i\sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\bar\sigma}\;\eta^{-s}\\ -i\sigma^2\sqrt{p\cdot\sigma}\;\eta^{-s}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}i\sigma^2 & 0\\ 0 & i\sigma^2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\tilde p\cdot\sigma}\;\eta^{-s}\\ -\sqrt{\tilde p\cdot\bar\sigma}\;\eta^{-s}\end{pmatrix} ,
> $$
>
> using $p\cdot\bar\sigma = \tilde p\cdot\sigma$, $p\cdot\sigma = \tilde p\cdot\bar\sigma$ ([[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^thm-c9-3-2|Theorem §C9.3.2]]). *Difference:* the relative minus sign of $v$ rides along in the lower half; nothing is exchanged.

> - The same matrix $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ works for $u$ and $v$, so the field law will have no relative sign between the particle and antiparticle terms; the relative minus sign that Peskin–Schroeder point to is "implicit in the twice-flipped spinor in $v^{-s}$" (PS p. 69), i.e. in $\eta^{-s} = -\xi^s$.

## Time reversal on the field

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.7.7: Time Reversal on the Dirac Field
> With $U(\mathcal T)$ antiunitary as in [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^def-c9-7-2|Def. §C9.7.2]] and [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-2|Theorem §C9.7.2]], and $\hat\psi$ the Dirac field ([[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra#^thm-c5b-2-1|Theorem §C5b.2.1]]) with $\eta^s = \xi^{-s}$,
>
> $$
> U(\mathcal T)\,\hat\psi(t, \mathbf x)\,U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = \gamma^1\gamma^3\,\hat\psi(-t, \mathbf x), \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad U(\mathcal T)\,\hat\psi(x)\,U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = \gamma^1\gamma^3\,\hat\psi(x_T) ,
> $$
>
> with $x_T$ of [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^def-c9-7-1|Def. §C9.7.1]]. As operator-valued distributions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-11|Def. §CA.2.11]]), antilinearity conjugates the test function: $U(\mathcal T)\hat\psi_a(f)U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = (\gamma^1\gamma^3)_{ab}\,\hat\psi_b(\bar f\circ\mathcal T)$.
>
> *Parity analogue:* [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^thm-c9-3-5|Theorem §C9.3.5]].
> *Source: Lecture 12, slide 22 · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.5 (Derivation "$U(T)\hat\psi(t, \vec x)U(T)^{-1} = \gamma^1\gamma^3\hat\psi(-t, \vec x)$", eq. (Tfield)) · PHY 513, Problem Set 6, conventions before Problem 1 ("$\mathcal T\psi\mathcal T^\dagger = \gamma^1\gamma^3\psi(-t, \mathbf x)$") · PS §3.6, eq. (3.139) (with $-\gamma^1\gamma^3$: [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^cau-c9-7-4|Caution: Signs and names in Peskin–Schroeder and Yu]]) · Yu §9.2.4, eqs. (9.206)–(9.209)*

^thm-c9-7-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Act antilinearly.** Insert the mode expansion. By [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-3|Theorem §C9.7.3]], 1, $U(\mathcal T)$ conjugates every number, the real prefactor $1/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$ unchanged, the spinors to $u^s(p)^{\ast}$, $v^s(p)^{\ast}$, and the plane waves $e^{\mp ip\cdot x}$ to $e^{\pm ip\cdot x}$ (same $t$, $\mathbf x$: $U$ acts on operators, not on the point). On the operators [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^def-c9-7-2|Def. §C9.7.2]] gives $\hat a^s_{\mathbf p} \to \hat a^{-s}_{-\mathbf p}$, and with part 3 of Theorem §C9.7.3, $\hat b^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p} \to (\hat b^{-s}_{-\mathbf p})^\dagger \equiv \hat b^{-s\dagger}_{-\mathbf p}$ (for $s = 2$: $(-\hat b^1_{-\mathbf p})^\dagger = -\hat b^{1\dagger}_{-\mathbf p}$, a real coefficient). So
>
> $$
> U(\mathcal T)\,\hat\psi(t, \mathbf x)\,U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_{s=1,2}\Bigl(\hat a^{-s}_{-\mathbf p}\,u^s(p)^{\ast}\,e^{ip\cdot x} + \hat b^{-s\dagger}_{-\mathbf p}\,v^s(p)^{\ast}\,e^{-ip\cdot x}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **2. The spinors.** By [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-5|Theorem §C9.7.5]] and [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-6|Theorem §C9.7.6]], $u^s(p)^{\ast} = \gamma^1\gamma^3u^{-s}(\tilde p)$ and $v^s(p)^{\ast} = \gamma^1\gamma^3v^{-s}(\tilde p)$. Pull the constant matrix out of the integral (it commutes with the operators, the sum and the integral):
>
> $$
> = \gamma^1\gamma^3\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_s\Bigl(\hat a^{-s}_{-\mathbf p}\,u^{-s}(\tilde p)\,e^{ip\cdot x} + \hat b^{-s\dagger}_{-\mathbf p}\,v^{-s}(\tilde p)\,e^{-ip\cdot x}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **3. Relabel s → −s.** Write out the sum: $\sum_s\hat a^{-s}u^{-s} = \hat a^{-1}u^{-1} + \hat a^{-2}u^{-2} = \hat a^2u^2 + (-\hat a^1)(-u^1) = \sum_s\hat a^su^s$, and likewise $\sum_s\hat b^{-s\dagger}v^{-s} = \hat b^{2\dagger}v^2 + (-\hat b^{1\dagger})(-v^1) = \sum_s\hat b^{s\dagger}v^s$. The relabelling is harmless because the two signs of $X^{-2} = -X^1$ meet, once on the operator and once on the spinor. ⚑ By-product: had the oscillators been flipped without the sign ($\hat a^{-2} = +\hat a^1$), the $s = 2$ terms would come back as $-\hat a^1u^1$, with a relative minus sign, and would not reassemble into $\hat\psi$: the sign in [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^def-c9-7-2|Def. §C9.7.2]] is forced by the spinors.
>
> **4. Change variables p → −p.** The integral runs over all of $\mathbb R^3$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-7|Theorem §CA.3.7]], 1; read after smearing in $\mathbf p$, [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^def-c2a-2-2|Def. §C2a.2.2]]). What changes: the Jacobian is $1$ and $E_{-\mathbf p} = E_{\mathbf p}$ ([[§C9.1 Discrete Lorentz Transformations#^thm-c9-1-1|Theorem §C9.1.1]], 5); the labels $\hat a^s_{-\mathbf p} \to \hat a^s_{\mathbf p}$, $\hat b^{s\dagger}_{-\mathbf p} \to \hat b^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}$; the on-shell $\tilde p = (E_{\mathbf p}, -\mathbf p)$ becomes $(E_{\mathbf p}, \mathbf p) = p$ in the spinors; and $p$ in the exponents becomes $\tilde p$:
>
> $$
> = \gamma^1\gamma^3\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_s\Bigl(\hat a^s_{\mathbf p}\,u^s(p)\,e^{i\tilde p\cdot x} + \hat b^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}\,v^s(p)\,e^{-i\tilde p\cdot x}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **5. The exponent.** $i\tilde p\cdot x = i\bigl(p^0t + \mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x\bigr) = -i\bigl(p^0(-t) - \mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x\bigr) = -ip\cdot x_T$ (slide 22). So $e^{i\tilde p\cdot x} = e^{-ip\cdot x_T}$ and $e^{-i\tilde p\cdot x} = e^{ip\cdot x_T}$, and the integral is the mode expansion at the point $x_T = (-t, \mathbf x)$: $\hat\psi(x_T)$.
>
> **6. Sense.** For $f \in \mathcal S(\mathbb R^4)$, antilinearity gives $U\bigl(\int d^4x\,f(x)\hat\psi_a(x)\bigr)U^{-1} = \int d^4x\,\bar f(x)\,U\hat\psi_a(x)U^{-1} = (\gamma^1\gamma^3)_{ab}\int d^4x\,\bar f(x)\hat\psi_b(x_T)$, and the substitution $y = x_T$ (Jacobian $|\det\mathcal T| = 1$, [[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-1|Theorem §C1a.4.1]]) turns the last integral into $\hat\psi_b(\bar f\circ\mathcal T)$, with $(\bar f\circ\mathcal T)(y) = \bar f(y_T)$ again a test function.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ By-product: antilinearity flips the sign of the whole four-vector in the exponent: $p^0t$ because time is reversed and $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x$ because the momentum is (transcript: "the entire four-vector is flipping sign, and that is because it's antilinear").
> - The matrix $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ is block diagonal: time reversal does not exchange the Weyl halves.
> - Used next: $\hat{\bar\psi}$ and the vector current (Theorems §C9.7.8–9).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.7.9: The Vector Current under Time Reversal

>
> $$
> U(\mathcal T)\,\mathsf V^\mu(t, \mathbf x)\,U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = \mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu\,\mathsf V^\nu(-t, \mathbf x) :
> $$
>
> $\mathsf V^0(t, \mathbf x) \to \mathsf V^0(-t, \mathbf x)$ and $\mathsf V^i(t, \mathbf x) \to -\mathsf V^i(-t, \mathbf x)$, with $\mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu$ of [[§C9.1 Discrete Lorentz Transformations#^def-c9-1-2|Def. §C9.1.2]]. Slide 23 writes $\mathsf V_\mu$ ([[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^cau-c9-7-3|Caution: Slide 23's V_μ and its argument]]).
>

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. No fermion exchange.** In components $\mathsf V^\mu = \sum_{a,b}\hat{\bar\psi}_a(\gamma^\mu)_{ab}\hat\psi_b$. By [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-3|Theorem §C9.7.3]], 1–2, $U$ keeps the order of the two field operators and conjugates the numbers $(\gamma^\mu)_{ab}$:
>
> $$
> U\mathsf V^\mu U^{-1} = \sum_{a,b}(U\hat{\bar\psi}_aU^{-1})\,(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}_{ab}\,(U\hat\psi_bU^{-1}) = \hat{\bar\psi}(x_T)\,\gamma^3\gamma^1\,(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}\,\gamma^1\gamma^3\,\hat\psi(x_T) ,
> $$
>
> by [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-8|Theorem §C9.7.8]] and [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-7|Theorem §C9.7.7]] (slide 23: "$(\bar\psi\gamma^3\gamma^1)(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}(\gamma^1\gamma^3\psi)$"). Unlike charge conjugation ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^der-c9-5-11|Derivation §C9.5.11]]), $\hat{\bar\psi}$ stays on the left, so no exchange of fermion fields and no sign arise. Normal ordering is preserved: $U$ maps annihilators to annihilators and creators to creators ([[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^def-c9-7-2|Def. §C9.7.2]]).
>
> **2. The matrix.** $(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}\gamma^1\gamma^3 = \gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma_\mu$ ([[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-4|Theorem §C9.7.4]], 4), so $\gamma^3\gamma^1(\gamma^\mu)^{\ast}\gamma^1\gamma^3 = \gamma^3\gamma^1\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma_\mu = \gamma_\mu$, since $\gamma^3\gamma^1\gamma^1\gamma^3 = \gamma^3(-\mathbb 1)\gamma^3 = -(\gamma^3)^2 = \mathbb 1$.
>
> **3. Lower index as a matrix.** $\gamma_\mu = g_{\mu\nu}\gamma^\nu$ has $\gamma_0 = \gamma^0$, $\gamma_i = -\gamma^i$; numerically, component by component, $\gamma_\mu = \mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu\gamma^\nu$ ($\mathcal P = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$; no sum over the repeated $\mu$ on the left). So $U\mathsf V^\mu(x)U^{-1} = \mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu\,\hat{\bar\psi}(x_T)\gamma^\nu\hat\psi(x_T) = \mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu\,\mathsf V^\nu(x_T)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Complex conjugation flips $\gamma^2$, $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ flips $\gamma^1$ and $\gamma^3$: every spatial index gets one sign, the time index none.
> - The same $\mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu$ as parity, but at $x_T$ instead of $\tilde x$.

> [!remark] Remark: The charge density stays, the current flips
> $\mathsf V^\mu$ is the number (electric) current ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^rem-c9-5-6|§C9.5, Remark: The number current flips]]). Time reversal leaves $\mu = 0$, the charge density, invariant and flips the spatial components, the current: when the movie is run backwards the charges stay where they are and move the other way. Parity flips the current too ([[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity#^thm-c9-4-4|Theorem §C9.4.4]], row 3), because the direction of motion is reflected; charge conjugation flips the density as well ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-12|Theorem §C9.5.12]]).

> [!remark] Remark: What the field theory adds to time reversal in Quantum Mechanics
> Quantum Mechanics has time reversal as an antiunitary operator on kets that reverses momentum and spin ([[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^thm-c8-3-3|QM Theorem §C8.3.3]], [[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^thm-c8-3-6|QM Theorem §C8.3.6]]), and on Dirac wave functions as $\Psi \mapsto \gamma^1\gamma^3\Psi^{\ast}(\mathbf x, -t)$ ([[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-7|QM Theorem §C13.2.7]], 3). The field theory adds: the action on the ladder operators with the flipped labels $\hat a^{-s}_{-\mathbf p}$ ([[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^def-c9-7-2|Def. §C9.7.2]]); the derivation of antilinearity from $[U, \hat H] = 0$ and a spectrum bounded below ([[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-1|Theorem §C9.7.1]]); and the field law, in which the conjugation of the wave functions and the flip of the operators reassemble into $\gamma^1\gamma^3\hat\psi(x_T)$, with no star on the field ([[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-7|Theorem §C9.7.7]]): the star of the wave-function version is supplied by the antilinear $U(\mathcal T)$ acting on the mode functions.
>

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The $2\times2$ blocks $i\sigma^2$ of $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ are, up to a sign, the spin-$\frac12$ time reversal $e^{-i\pi S_y}K$ of Quantum Mechanics and the two-spinor conjugation $J$ of charge conjugation; one antiunitary map of spin $\frac12$ serves both — [[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^thm-c8-3-6|QM Theorem §C8.3.6]], [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-3|Theorem §C9.5.3]].
> - Continuous symmetries are always unitary; only discrete ones can be antiunitary, and time reversal is the one that must be — [[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries#^thm-cb-19-8|Theorem §CB.19.8]], [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-2|Theorem §C9.7.2]].
> - The positivity of the energy, which forced antiunitarity here, is the same that ruled out commutators for the Dirac field — [[§C5b.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^thm-c5b-3-3|Theorem §C5b.3.3]], [[§C9.7 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-7-1|Theorem §C9.7.1]].
> - The sign rule $X^{-2} = -X^1$, introduced for the conjugation of two-spinors, is what lets the time-reversed field reassemble; the same sign makes two spin flips equal to $-1$ — [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^def-c9-5-4|Def. §C9.5.4]], [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation#^thm-c9-5-3|Theorem §C9.5.3]].
> - Equations of motion symmetric under $t \to -t$ let one postdict the past as well as predict the future; causality is the prior statement that the two light cones differ — [[§C1a.3 Causal Structure and the Causality of a Single Particle|§C1a.3]].
> - Combined with $C$ and $P$, time reversal gives the antiunitary $\Theta$ of the CPT theorem — [[§C9.8 CPT and the CPT Theorem|§C9.8]].
