Which Lorentz transformations does the construction "start small and integrate up" miss, and what notation does parity need? The geometry is already proved: the four pieces of the Lorentz group ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-4|REL Theorem §B1.2.4]]), their coset structure ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-2|Theorem §C1a.4.2]]), the connectedness of the identity piece ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-3|Theorem §C1a.4.3]]) and the fact that every exponential of generators is proper orthochronous ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-6-4|Theorem §C1a.6.4]]). This section adds what Lecture 11 built on them: the name *discrete* for what the exponentials miss, the rotation group as the model case (with its dependence on the dimension), and the parity images $\tilde x$, $\tilde p$ with the identities that every parity computation of [[§C9.2★ Parity of the Scalar Field|§C9.2★]] (the scalar field, beyond the lecture), [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field|§C9.3]] and [[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity|§C9.4]] uses. Natural units, $g = \operatorname{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1)$ ([[Larsen PHY 513]]).

## What "discrete" means

> [!definition] Definition §C9.1.1: Discrete Lorentz Transformation
> A Lorentz transformation $\Lambda \in O(1,3)$ ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^def-c1a-4-4|Def. §C1a.4.4]]) is **discrete** if it cannot be joined to the identity by a continuous path of Lorentz transformations, i.e. if it lies outside the identity component $SO^+(1,3)$ ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-3|Theorem §C1a.4.3]]). The transformations $\Lambda = \exp\bigl(-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}\bigr)$ built from the generators ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-1|Def. §C1a.6.1]]) are never discrete ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-6-4|Theorem §C1a.6.4]]).
>
> *Source: Lecture 11, slide 5 ("Elements of the Lorentz group not continuously connected to the identity are discrete Lorentz transformations") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 11 §11.1 (paragraph "What 'discrete' means") · PS §3.6, p. 64 · Yu, Ch. 9 opening (分立变换)*

^def-c9-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: A definition by what it is not
> The lecture admitted that defining something by what it is not ("an animal that is not an elephant") says little; the examples below fill the definition in. The positive description is [[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-2|Theorem §C1a.4.2]]: each discrete $\Lambda$ is one of $\mathcal P$, $\mathcal T$, $\mathcal P\mathcal T$ times an element of $SO^+(1,3)$, so the discrete transformations are exactly the three cosets other than $SO^+(1,3)$. The reason the generators miss them is the one in [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-6-4|Theorem §C1a.6.4]]: a path $s \mapsto e^{s\omega}$ starts at $\mathbb 1$ and never leaves its component.
>
> *Source: Lecture 11 (transcript) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 11 §11.1*

## The model case: rotations

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.1.1: The Discrete Element of the Rotation Group
> Let $O(3)$ be the real $3\times3$ matrices with $R^{\mathsf T}R = \mathbb 1$, the transformations preserving $\mathbf x^2$ ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^def-c1a-4-4|Def. §C1a.4.4]]).
> 1. Every $R \in O(3)$ has $\det R = \pm1$ ([[§11 Topological Groups and Classical Matrix Groups#^prop-11-5|591 Prop. §11.5]]).
> 2. The inversion $R = -\mathbb 1$, $\mathbf x \mapsto -\mathbf x$, lies in $O(3)$ and has $\det(-\mathbb 1) = -1$.
> 3. Every $R$ on a continuous path in $O(3)$ starting at $\mathbb 1$ has $\det R = +1$; so $-\mathbb 1$ cannot be reached from the identity continuously: it is a discrete element.
>
> *Source: Lecture 11, slide 6 ("integers do not jump") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 11 §11.1 (Derivation "The discrete element of the rotation group")*

^thm-c9-1-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The determinant is $\pm1$.** Take determinants of $R^{\mathsf T}R = \mathbb 1$: $\det(R^{\mathsf T}R) = \det R^{\mathsf T}\det R = (\det R)^2$, using $\det(AB) = \det A\det B$ ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]) and $\det R^{\mathsf T} = \det R$ ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-56|LADR Thm. 9.56]]); the right side is $\det\mathbb 1 = 1$. So $(\det R)^2 = 1$ and $\det R \in \{+1, -1\}$.
>
> **2. The inversion.** $(-\mathbb 1)^{\mathsf T}(-\mathbb 1) = (-1)^2\mathbb 1 = \mathbb 1$, so $-\mathbb 1 \in O(3)$; it sends $(x, y, z) \mapsto (-x, -y, -z)$. Its determinant is the product of its three diagonal entries, $(-1)^3 = -1$. ⚑ By-product: the exponent $3$ is the number of space dimensions; in $d$ dimensions $\det(-\mathbb 1_d) = (-1)^d$ → [[§C9.1 Discrete Lorentz Transformations#^cau-c9-1-1|Caution: Parity depends on the number of space dimensions]].
>
> **3. Small rotations.** The identity has $\det\mathbb 1 = +1$. A rotation by angle $\theta$ about $z$ has determinant $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$; the same holds about every axis, and a product of determinant-one matrices has determinant one (step 1's rule). Intuitively: a rotation by $\pi$ about $z$ flips $x$ and $y$, but no rotation flips all three coordinates (Lecture 11).
>
> **4. Integers do not jump.** Along a continuous path $s \mapsto R(s)$, $s \in [0, 1]$, $R(0) = \mathbb 1$, the function $s \mapsto \det R(s)$ is continuous (a polynomial in the entries, [[§11 Topological Groups and Classical Matrix Groups#^prop-11-3|591 Prop. §11.3]]) and by step 1 takes values in $\{+1, -1\}$. The image of the interval $[0, 1]$ under a continuous map is connected ([[§15 Connected Spaces#^thm-15-3|590 Thm. §15.3]]), and the only connected subsets of $\{+1, -1\}$ are single points; so $\det R(s) = \det R(0) = +1$ for all $s$. Since $\det(-\mathbb 1) = -1$ (step 2), no such path ends at $-\mathbb 1$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The argument is topological: a quantity that can only take integer values is constant along continuous paths, so it is enough to check it on small transformations (the lecture's "simplest instance of topology in the course").
> - Only step 2 used the dimension; steps 1, 3, 4 hold in any dimension.
> - The same argument with the two labels $\det\Lambda$ and $\operatorname{sgn}\Lambda^0{}_0$ is [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-4|REL Theorem §B1.2.4]], 4, for the Lorentz group.

*Uses:* [[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^def-c1a-4-4|Def. §C1a.4.4]], [[§11 Topological Groups and Classical Matrix Groups#^prop-11-5|591 Prop. §11.5]], [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]], [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-56|LADR Thm. 9.56]], [[§11 Topological Groups and Classical Matrix Groups#^prop-11-3|591 Prop. §11.3]], [[§15 Connected Spaces#^thm-15-3|590 Thm. §15.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Parity depends on the number of space dimensions
> With $d$ spatial dimensions, $\det(-\mathbb 1_d) = (-1)^d$. For $d = 3$ the inversion $\mathbf x \to -\mathbf x$ is improper (Theorem §C9.1.1). For $d = 2$ it is the rotation by $\pi$, continuously connected to the identity; the improper element must then be taken as the reflection of a single axis. Statements about parity in these notes are statements about three space dimensions (four spacetime dimensions). The orbit picture of the Lorentz group depends on the dimension in the same way ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^cau-c1a-4-4|§C1a.4, Caution: The orbit picture depends on the dimension]]).
>
> *Source: Lecture 11 (transcript, a question from the class) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 11 §11.1 (Caution "This depends on the number of space dimensions")*

## The discrete elements of the Lorentz group

The four pieces, with their labels $(\det\Lambda, \operatorname{sgn}\Lambda^0{}_0)$ and representatives $\mathbb 1$, $\mathcal P$, $\mathcal T$, $\mathcal P\mathcal T$, are proved in [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-4|REL Theorem §B1.2.4]]; the matrices are defined there:

![[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-2]]

In the natural units of these notes the two maps read $\mathcal P: (t, \mathbf x) \mapsto (t, -\mathbf x)$ and $\mathcal T: (t, \mathbf x) \mapsto (-t, \mathbf x)$, and $\mathcal P\mathcal T = -\mathbb 1$: $(t, \mathbf x) \mapsto (-t, -\mathbf x)$. Every Lorentz transformation is one of $\mathbb 1, \mathcal P, \mathcal T, \mathcal P\mathcal T$ times an element of $SO^+(1,3)$ ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-2|Theorem §C1a.4.2]]). Lecture 11, slide 7, names the four sectors after these representatives, $\mathcal I$, $\mathcal P$, $\mathcal T$, $\mathcal P\mathcal T$, with $\mathcal I$ the sector of the identity $\mathbb 1$.

> [!remark] Remark: What Lecture 11 adds to the four pieces
> - **Two invariants, not one.** For rotations the determinant alone separates the pieces. For the Lorentz group there is a second label: every $\Lambda$ has $|\Lambda^0{}_0| \ge 1$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-4|REL Theorem §B1.2.4]], 2), and the sign of $\Lambda^0{}_0$ cannot jump across the gap $(-1, 1)$, by step 4 of Derivation §C9.1.1 with $\mathbb R \setminus (-1, 1)$ in place of $\{\pm1\}$.
> - **No contraction of time.** A boost has $\Lambda^0{}_0 = \cosh\eta \ge 1$ ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^def-c1a-4-3|Def. §C1a.4.3]]), and products of boosts and rotations keep $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$ ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-2|Theorem §C1a.4.2]], step 2): time is always dilated, never contracted, and no continuous transformation turns it around. That is why $\mathcal T$ must be considered separately from the boosts.
> - **What $\mathcal T$ would mean.** If $\mathcal T$ were a symmetry, predicting the future from initial conditions and the past from final conditions would be equivalent (Lecture 11). Time reversal is Lecture 12 (QFT C9, later sections).
> - **The word "Lorentz transformation".** People are often imprecise about whether it includes $\mathcal P$, $\mathcal T$ and $\mathcal P\mathcal T$. In this chapter it does; elsewhere in these notes "Lorentz invariant" means invariant under $SO^+(1,3)$ ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^rem-c1a-4-1|§C1a.4, Remark: What "Lorentz invariant" means in field theory]]).
>
> *Source: Lecture 11, slide 7, and transcript · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 11 §11.1 (paragraph "The Lorentz group has four pieces")*

^rem-c9-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Slide 7's determinants

Both have $\det\Lambda = -1$: $\mathcal P = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$ has three entries $-1$, $\mathcal T = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ one. It is $\mathcal P\mathcal T = -\mathbb 1$ that has determinant $+1$ (REL Theorem §B1.2.4, table). The same slide's second statement, "$\mathcal T$, $\mathcal{PT}$ have $\Lambda^0{}_0 = -1$", is correct. The slides and Yu (eqs. (1.58)–(1.59), (9.1)) write the matrices $\mathcal P$, $\mathcal T$, and so do these notes; the matrices $P$ and $T$ of [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-2|REL Def. §B1.2.2]], embedded above, are the same matrices, $P = \mathcal P$, $T = \mathcal T$, $PT = \mathcal P\mathcal T$.

## Parity images

> [!definition] Definition §C9.1.2: Parity Matrix and Parity Images
> The **parity matrix** acting on four-vectors is $\mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-2|REL Def. §B1.2.2]]). The **parity image** of a point and of a momentum are
>
> $$
> \tilde x^\mu \equiv \mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu = (t, -\mathbf x), \qquad \tilde p^\mu \equiv \mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu p^\nu = (p^0, -\mathbf p) .
> $$
>
> For an on-shell momentum $p = (E_{\mathbf p}, \mathbf p)$, $\tilde p = (E_{\mathbf p}, -\mathbf p)$ is the on-shell momentum with label $-\mathbf p$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 11 §11.1 (end: "Write $\tilde x \equiv (t, -\mathbf x)$ … and $P^\mu{}_\nu = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$") · Lecture 11, slide 12 ("$p = (p^0, \mathbf p) \to \tilde p = (p^0, -\mathbf p)$") · PS §3.6, p. 65 · Yu §9.1.1, eq. (9.1) ($\mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu$)*

^def-c9-1-2

The tilde on momenta was used before parity had a name: $v^s(\tilde p)$ in the mode expansion of the Dirac field ([[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra#^thm-c5b-2-1|Theorem §C5b.2.1]], derivation step 3).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.1.2: Identities of the Parity Matrix
> For $\mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu$ and the images of [[§C9.1 Discrete Lorentz Transformations#^def-c9-1-2|Def. §C9.1.2]], and any four-vectors $p$, $q$, $x$:
> 1. $\mathcal P^\mu{}_\rho \mathcal P^\rho{}_\nu = \delta^\mu{}_\nu$, so $\mathcal P^{-1} = \mathcal P$ and $\tilde{\tilde x} = x$;
> 2. $\tilde p\cdot\tilde q = p\cdot q$ (parity is a Lorentz transformation);
> 3. $\tilde p\cdot x = p\cdot\tilde x = p^0t + \mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x$;
> 4. for a function $f$ on spacetime, $g(x) \equiv f(\tilde x)$ has $\partial_\mu g(x) = \mathcal P^\nu{}_\mu\,(\partial_\nu f)(\tilde x)$, and $\mathcal P^\mu{}_\rho \mathcal P^\nu{}_\mu = \delta^\nu{}_\rho$;
> 5. $E_{-\mathbf p} = E_{\mathbf p}$ and $d^3p$ is unchanged under $\mathbf p \to -\mathbf p$ (Jacobian $|\det(-\mathbb 1_3)| = 1$).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 11 §11.4 (Derivation "$U(P)\hat\psi(x)U(P)^{-1} = \eta_a\gamma^0\hat\psi(\tilde x)$", steps 2–3: "$\tilde p\cdot x = p^0t + \mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x = p\cdot\tilde x$"), §11.5 (paragraph "The free Dirac Lagrangian is parity invariant": "$\partial_\mu = P^\nu{}_\mu\tilde\partial_\nu$ … since $P^\mu{}_\rho P^\nu{}_\mu = \delta^\nu{}_\rho$") · Lecture 11, slide 14 ("$\tilde px = p\tilde x$") · PS §3.6, p. 65 ("$p\cdot x = \tilde p\cdot(t, -\mathbf x)$") · Yu §9.1.1, eq. (9.1), and the text after it ($\partial'_\mu = (\mathcal P^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu\partial_\nu$)*

^thm-c9-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. $\mathcal P^2 = \mathbb 1$.** $\mathcal P$ is diagonal, so $(\mathcal P^2)^\mu{}_\nu = \mathcal P^\mu{}_\mu \mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu$ (no sum) $= (\pm1)^2\delta^\mu{}_\nu = \delta^\mu{}_\nu$: each diagonal entry is $+1$ or $-1$ and squares to $1$. Hence $\mathcal P^{-1} = \mathcal P$ and $\tilde{\tilde x}^\mu = \mathcal P^\mu{}_\rho \mathcal P^\rho{}_\nu x^\nu = x^\mu$.
>
> **2. Invariance of the product.** Write both products in components with $g = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$: $\tilde p\cdot\tilde q = \tilde p^0\tilde q^0 - \tilde{\mathbf p}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf q} = p^0q^0 - (-\mathbf p)\cdot(-\mathbf q) = p^0q^0 - \mathbf p\cdot\mathbf q = p\cdot q$. (Equivalently $\mathcal P^{\mathsf T}g\mathcal P = g$: $\mathcal P$ is in $O(1,3)$, [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-4|REL Theorem §B1.2.4]], 4.)
>
> **3. Moving the tilde.** $\tilde p\cdot x = \tilde p^0x^0 - \tilde{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf x = p^0t - (-\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf x = p^0t + \mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x$, and $p\cdot\tilde x = p^0\tilde x^0 - \mathbf p\cdot\tilde{\mathbf x} = p^0t - \mathbf p\cdot(-\mathbf x) = p^0t + \mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x$. The two agree. (Equivalently: by item 2 with $q = \tilde x$, $\tilde p\cdot x = \tilde p\cdot\tilde{\tilde x} = p\cdot\tilde x$, using item 1.)
>
> **4. Chain rule.** $g(x) = f(y)$ with $y^\nu = \tilde x^\nu = \mathcal P^\nu{}_\rho x^\rho$. Then $\partial y^\nu/\partial x^\mu = \mathcal P^\nu{}_\mu$, a constant, and the chain rule gives $\partial_\mu g(x) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y^\nu}\Big|_{y = \tilde x}\frac{\partial y^\nu}{\partial x^\mu} = \mathcal P^\nu{}_\mu(\partial_\nu f)(\tilde x)$. The lecture and the user's notes write this as $\partial_\mu = \mathcal P^\nu{}_\mu\tilde\partial_\nu$, with $\tilde\partial_\nu$ the derivative with respect to the argument $\tilde x^\nu$. In components: $\partial_0 g = (\partial_0 f)(\tilde x)$ and $\partial_i g = -(\partial_i f)(\tilde x)$. The contraction $\mathcal P^\mu{}_\rho \mathcal P^\nu{}_\mu = \delta^\nu{}_\rho$ is item 1 with the factors written in the other order (diagonal matrices commute).
>
> **5. Energies and measure.** $E_{\mathbf p} = \sqrt{\mathbf p^2 + m^2}$ depends on $\mathbf p$ only through $\mathbf p^2 = (-\mathbf p)^2$. The substitution $\mathbf p = -\mathbf p'$ is linear with matrix $-\mathbb 1_3$, Jacobian $|\det(-\mathbb 1_3)| = |(-1)^3| = 1$, and maps $\mathbb R^3$ onto itself ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-7|Theorem §CA.3.7]], 1).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Every identity is a component computation with one sign per spatial index; nothing beyond $\mathcal P = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$ is used.
> - Item 3 is what lets a flip of the integration momentum be traded for a flip of the spacetime point ([[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field|§C9.3]]); item 4 is what makes $\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi$ parity invariant ([[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity|§C9.4]]).
> - Item 2 is the statement "parity is a Lorentz transformation"; the volume elements are invariant under it too ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-1|Theorem §C1a.4.1]]).
