What do charge conjugation, parity and time reversal do together, and why is the combination special? Parity multiplies the Dirac field by $\gamma^0$ ([[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^thm-c9-3-5|Theorem §C9.3.5]]), charge conjugation by $-i\gamma^2$ after starring it ([[§C9.6 Charge Conjugation on the Field and Bilinears#^thm-c9-6-2|Theorem §C9.6.2]]), time reversal by $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ ([[§C9.8 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-8-7|Theorem §C9.8.7]]): all four $\gamma$'s once each, and the product is $\gamma^5$. The combined operator $\Theta$ multiplies the field by $\gamma^5$ and reverses the whole four-vector $x$. Lecture 11 had stated that $P$, $C$ and $T$ are optional symmetries but $CPT$ is mandatory ([[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity#^rem-c9-4-3|§C9.4, Remark: Mandatory and optional symmetries]]); this section states the CPT theorem behind that, with the lecture's idea of proof, collects the transformation of every bilinear under $C$, $P$, $T$ and $CPT$ in one table, and collects the four transformation laws of the Dirac field. Notation as in [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation on Oscillators and Spinors|§C9.5]] and [[§C9.8 Time Reversal|§C9.8]].

## Three matrices, one γ⁵

> [!remark] Remark: Each γ once
> Collecting the three laws of the Dirac field, with their points:
>
> $$
> P:\ \eta_a\gamma^0\,\hat\psi(\tilde x), \qquad C:\ -i\gamma^2\,\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(x), \qquad T:\ \gamma^1\gamma^3\,\hat\psi(x_T) .
> $$
>
> They use $\gamma^0$, then $\gamma^2$, then $\gamma^1$ and $\gamma^3$: all four $\gamma$'s, each once, and their product is $\gamma^5$ ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^thm-c9-9-1|Theorem §C9.9.1]]). This suggests that doing all three is a more symmetric operation than any one of them (Lecture 12: "somehow all three of them is a more symmetric operation than any one of them, and that's the statement … the CPT symmetry").
>
> *Source: Lecture 12 (transcript) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 opening paragraph and §12.6 (paragraph "Three matrices")*

^rem-c9-9-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.9.1: The Three Matrices Multiply to γ⁵
> For the chiral Dirac matrices and $\gamma^5 = i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$ ([[§CB.12 Clifford Algebras꞉ Grading, Basis and the Volume Element#^def-cb-12-14|Def. §CB.12.14]]),
>
> $$
> \gamma^0\,(-i\gamma^2)\,(\gamma^1\gamma^3) = \gamma^5, \qquad \gamma^1\gamma^3\,\gamma^0\,(-i\gamma^2) = \gamma^5 ,
> $$
>
> and the product of the three matrices $\gamma^0$, $-i\gamma^2$, $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ in any order is $\pm\gamma^5$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 opening ("Their product is $\gamma^5$") and §12.6 (Derivation "$\Theta\hat\psi(x)\Theta^{-1} = \eta_a\gamma^5\hat\psi^{\ast}(-x)$": "$\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^0\gamma^2 = \gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^2 = -\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3 = i\gamma^5$") · Lecture 12 (transcript: "if you multiply them together, you get gamma five") · the first product written here*

^thm-c9-9-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> The tools: distinct $\gamma$'s anticommute ([[§CB.11 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Universal Property and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-11-12|Theorem §CB.11.12]], 1), so exchanging two neighbours costs a sign; and $\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3 = \gamma^5/i = -i\gamma^5$ (Def. §CB.12.14).
>
> **1. First order.** $\gamma^0(-i\gamma^2)\gamma^1\gamma^3 = -i\,\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^1\gamma^3 = -i\,(-\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3) = i\,\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3 = i(-i\gamma^5) = \gamma^5$ (one exchange, $\gamma^2\gamma^1 = -\gamma^1\gamma^2$).
>
> **2. Second order (the one the field produces, Theorem §C9.9.3).** Move $\gamma^0$ to the front of $\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^0\gamma^2$: two exchanges, $\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^0 = \gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^3$; then $\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^2 = -\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3 = -(-i\gamma^5) = i\gamma^5$. With the factor $-i$: $\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^0(-i\gamma^2) = (-i)(i\gamma^5) = \gamma^5$.
>
> **3. Any order.** Every ordering of the three factors is a product of the four distinct $\gamma$'s times $-i$, in some order; reordering four anticommuting factors into $\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$ costs $(-1)^k$ for $k$ exchanges. So every ordering gives $(-1)^k(-i)(-i\gamma^5) = \pm\gamma^5$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - $\gamma^5$ is the volume element: the one product that uses every direction of spacetime once, the spinor counterpart of reversing all four coordinates.
> - The order of the three transformations changes only a sign (and, through antilinearity, the phases); slide 24's "any order will do".

## The CPT transformation

> [!definition] Definition §C9.9.1: The CPT Operator Θ
> The **CPT transformation** is the consecutive application of time reversal, parity and charge conjugation,
>
> $$
> \Theta \equiv U(\mathcal C)\,U(\mathcal P)\,U(\mathcal T), \qquad \Theta\,\hat{\mathcal O}\,\Theta^{-1} = U(\mathcal C)\Bigl(U(\mathcal P)\bigl(U(\mathcal T)\,\hat{\mathcal O}\,U(\mathcal T)^{-1}\bigr)U(\mathcal P)^{-1}\Bigr)U(\mathcal C)^{-1} :
> $$
>
> first $\mathcal T$, then $\mathcal P$, then $\mathcal C$, since the innermost operator acts on $\hat{\mathcal O}$ first. "In fact any order will do" (slide 24): other orders change only signs and phases. $U(\mathcal C)$, $U(\mathcal P)$, $U(\mathcal T)$ are those of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation on Oscillators and Spinors#^def-c9-5-1|Def. §C9.5.1]], [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^def-c9-3-1|Def. §C9.3.1]] and [[§C9.8 Time Reversal#^def-c9-8-2|Def. §C9.8.2]].
>
> *Source: Lecture 12, slide 24 ("Definition: $\mathcal{CPT}$ = consecutive discrete transformations (first $\mathcal T$, then $\mathcal P$, then $\mathcal C$ — although in fact any order will do)") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.6 (Derivation "$\Theta\hat\psi(x)\Theta^{-1} = \eta_a\gamma^5\hat\psi^{\ast}(-x)$": "Let $\Theta \equiv U(C)U(P)U(T)$") · Yu §9.5, eq. (9.320) ($\Theta \equiv CPT$)*

^def-c9-9-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.9.2: Θ Is Antiunitary
> $\Theta$ of [[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^def-c9-9-1|Def. §C9.9.1]] is antiunitary ([[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries#^def-cb-19-4|Def. §CB.19.4]]), with
>
> $$
> \Theta^{-1} = U(\mathcal T)^{-1}U(\mathcal P)^{-1}U(\mathcal C)^{-1}, \qquad \Theta\,c\,\Theta^{-1} = c^{\ast}\quad\text{for every } c \in \mathbb C .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.6 ("$\Theta$ is antilinear, since $T$ is") and §12.7 (Principle "Discrete transformations of the Dirac field": "$U(T)$ and $\Theta$ are antiunitary") · Yu §9.5, eqs. (9.321)–(9.322) and p. 404 ("Θ跟T一样是反线性反幺正算符")*

^thm-c9-9-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Antiunitary.** $U(\mathcal T)$ is antiunitary ([[§C9.8 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-8-2|Theorem §C9.8.2]]), $U(\mathcal P)$ and $U(\mathcal C)$ are unitary ([[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^def-c9-3-1|Def. §C9.3.1]], [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation on Oscillators and Spinors#^def-c9-5-1|Def. §C9.5.1]]). By [[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries#^thm-cb-19-6|Theorem §CB.19.6]], 2, $U(\mathcal P)U(\mathcal T)$ is antiunitary (a unitary times an antiunitary), and then so is $U(\mathcal C)\bigl(U(\mathcal P)U(\mathcal T)\bigr)$.
>
> **2. Inverse.** The inverse of a product reverses the order: $\Theta\,U(\mathcal T)^{-1}U(\mathcal P)^{-1}U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = \mathbb 1$ by cancelling from the middle out (Yu (9.321)).
>
> **3. Numbers.** $U(\mathcal T)cU(\mathcal T)^{-1} = c^{\ast}$ ([[§C9.8 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-8-1|Theorem §C9.8.1]]), and the linear $U(\mathcal P)$, $U(\mathcal C)$ pass $c^{\ast}$ unchanged: $\Theta c\Theta^{-1} = U(\mathcal C)U(\mathcal P)c^{\ast}U(\mathcal P)^{-1}U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = c^{\ast}$ (Yu (9.322), with $\Theta^{-1}i\Theta = -i$).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - One antilinear factor makes the whole product antilinear: the CPT transformation inherits antiunitarity from $\mathcal T$, whatever the order.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.9.3: CPT on the Dirac Field
> With $\Theta$ of [[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^def-c9-9-1|Def. §C9.9.1]], the parity phase $\eta_a$ of [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^def-c9-3-1|Def. §C9.3.1]] and $\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}$ of [[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation on Oscillators and Spinors#^def-c9-5-2|Def. §C9.5.2]],
>
> $$
> \Theta\,\hat\psi(x)\,\Theta^{-1} = \eta_a\,\gamma^5\,\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x), \qquad \Theta\,\hat\psi_c(x)\,\Theta^{-1} = \eta_a\sum_d(\gamma^5)_{cd}\,\bigl(\hat\psi_d(-x)\bigr)^\dagger :
> $$
>
> multiplication by $\gamma^5$, a star, and a sign flip of the entire four-vector $x$. As operator-valued distributions: $\Theta\hat\psi_a(f)\Theta^{-1} = \eta_a(\gamma^5)_{ab}\,\hat\psi_b(f_-)^\dagger$ with $f_-(y) = f(-y)$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.6 (Derivation "$\Theta\hat\psi(x)\Theta^{-1} = \eta_a\gamma^5\hat\psi^{\ast}(-x)$", eq. (CPTfield)) and §12.7 (Principle "Discrete transformations of the Dirac field") · Lecture 12 (transcript: "CPT on my field. I get a gamma five, and my entire four vector flips sign") · PHY 513, Problem Set 7, Problem 3 (the case $\eta_a = 1$, "$\Theta\psi(x)\Theta^\dagger = \gamma^5\psi^{\ast}(-x)$", as the user wrote it: second route below)*

^thm-c9-9-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. T first.** By [[§C9.8 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-8-7|Theorem §C9.8.7]], $U(\mathcal T)\hat\psi(x)U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = \gamma^1\gamma^3\hat\psi(x_T)$, $x_T = (-t, \mathbf x)$.
>
> **2. Then P.** $U(\mathcal P)$ is linear, so the c-number matrix $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ passes through it, and [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^thm-c9-3-5|Theorem §C9.3.5]] holds at every point, in particular at $x_T$:
>
> $$
> U(\mathcal P)\,\gamma^1\gamma^3\hat\psi(x_T)\,U(\mathcal P)^{-1} = \gamma^1\gamma^3\,\eta_a\gamma^0\,\hat\psi(\widetilde{x_T}) = \eta_a\,\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^0\,\hat\psi(-x) ,
> $$
>
> since the parity image of $x_T = (-t, \mathbf x)$ is $(-t, -\mathbf x) = -x$ ([[§C9.1 Discrete Lorentz Transformations#^def-c9-1-2|Def. §C9.1.2]]; $\mathcal P\mathcal T = -\mathbb 1$, [[§C9.1 Discrete Lorentz Transformations|§C9.1]]). The phase $\eta_a$ is not conjugated: the antilinear $U(\mathcal T)$ has already acted.
>
> **3. Then C.** $U(\mathcal C)$ is linear as well; by [[§C9.6 Charge Conjugation on the Field and Bilinears#^thm-c9-6-2|Theorem §C9.6.2]] at the point $-x$:
>
> $$
> U(\mathcal C)\,\eta_a\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^0\hat\psi(-x)\,U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = \eta_a\,\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^0\,(-i\gamma^2)\,\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x) .
> $$
>
> **4. The matrix.** $\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^0(-i\gamma^2) = \gamma^5$ ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^thm-c9-9-1|Theorem §C9.9.1]], second product). So $\Theta\hat\psi(x)\Theta^{-1} = \eta_a\gamma^5\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x)$.
>
> **5. Sense.** $\Theta$ is antilinear ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^thm-c9-9-2|Theorem §C9.9.2]]): $\Theta\bigl(\int d^4x\,f(x)\hat\psi_a(x)\bigr)\Theta^{-1} = \int d^4x\,\bar f(x)\,\eta_a(\gamma^5)_{ab}\hat\psi_b^\dagger(-x) = \eta_a(\gamma^5)_{ab}\int d^4y\,\bar f(-y)\hat\psi_b^\dagger(y) = \eta_a(\gamma^5)_{ab}\,\hat\psi_b(f_-)^\dagger$, with the substitution $y = -x$ (Jacobian $|\det(-\mathbb 1_4)| = 1$) and $(\hat\psi_b(g))^\dagger = \int\bar g\,\hat\psi_b^\dagger$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Two things happen: multiplication by $\gamma^5$, and $x \to -x$ (slide 24: "Idea of proof: invariance under 4-vector inversion $x \to -x$"). $\Theta$ contains $C$, so it maps $\hat\psi$, which annihilates particles, into $\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}$, which annihilates antiparticles → [[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^cau-c9-9-1|Caution: The transposed adjoint in CPT]].
> - The user's notes report the result checked numerically.

> [!derivation]- Derivation (second route: PHY 513, Problem Set 7, Problem 3)
> The user's solution, with $\eta_a = 1$ as the problem prescribes, $\Theta \equiv \mathcal C\mathcal P\mathcal T$ for $U(\mathcal C)U(\mathcal P)U(\mathcal T)$, $\Theta^\dagger = \Theta^{-1}$, and the problem's $\psi^{\ast} \equiv (\psi^\dagger)^{\mathsf T}$ written $\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation on Oscillators and Spinors#^cau-c9-5-1|§C9.5, Caution: Names for the transposed adjoint]]).
>
> **1. Inside out.** $\Theta^\dagger = \mathcal T^\dagger\mathcal P^\dagger\mathcal C^\dagger$, so $\Theta\hat\psi(x)\Theta^\dagger = \mathcal C\mathcal P\bigl(\mathcal T\hat\psi(x)\mathcal T^\dagger\bigr)\mathcal P^\dagger\mathcal C^\dagger$: first $\mathcal T$, then $\mathcal P$, then $\mathcal C$. The matrices produced at each step are c-numbers; $\mathcal P$ and $\mathcal C$ are unitary and pass through them unchanged.
>
> **2. T and P.** $\mathcal T\hat\psi(t, \mathbf x)\mathcal T^\dagger = \gamma^1\gamma^3\hat\psi(-t, \mathbf x)$, then $\mathcal P\gamma^1\gamma^3\hat\psi(-t, \mathbf x)\mathcal P^\dagger = \gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^0\hat\psi(-t, -\mathbf x) = \gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^0\hat\psi(-x)$ (the conventions of Problem Set 6: [[§C9.8 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-8-7|Theorem §C9.8.7]], [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^thm-c9-3-5|Theorem §C9.3.5]]).
>
> **3. C from the convention.** With $\hat{\bar\psi}\gamma^0\gamma^2 = \hat\psi^\dagger\gamma^0\gamma^0\gamma^2 = \hat\psi^\dagger\gamma^2$ and $(\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T} = \gamma^2$: $\mathcal C\hat\psi(-x)\mathcal C^\dagger = \bigl(-i\hat{\bar\psi}(-x)\gamma^0\gamma^2\bigr)^{\mathsf T} = -i\bigl(\hat\psi^\dagger(-x)\gamma^2\bigr)^{\mathsf T} = -i(\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T}\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x) = -i\gamma^2\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x)$ ([[§C9.6 Charge Conjugation on the Field and Bilinears#^thm-c9-6-3|Theorem §C9.6.3]]). So $\Theta\hat\psi(x)\Theta^\dagger = -i\,\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^0\gamma^2\,\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x)$.
>
> **4. The matrix.** $\gamma^0$ and $\gamma^2$ each anticommute with both $\gamma^1$ and $\gamma^3$, so $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ commutes with $\gamma^0$ and with $\gamma^2$: $\gamma^1\gamma^3\gamma^0\gamma^2 = \gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^1\gamma^3$. Swapping $\gamma^2$ and $\gamma^1$ and using $\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3 = -i\gamma^5$ ([[§CB.12 Clifford Algebras꞉ Grading, Basis and the Volume Element#^def-cb-12-14|Def. §CB.12.14]]): $\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^1\gamma^3 = -\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3 = i\gamma^5$.
>
> **5. Result.** $\Theta\hat\psi(x)\Theta^\dagger = -i(i\gamma^5)\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x) = \gamma^5\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x)$: [[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^thm-c9-9-3|Theorem §C9.9.3]] with $\eta_a = 1$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The same three steps as the first route; the user's route takes charge conjugation in the form of the conventions, $-i(\hat{\bar\psi}\gamma^0\gamma^2)^{\mathsf T}$, and converts it, and reorders the matrix by moving the pair $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ as a block instead of $\gamma^0$ alone ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^der-c9-9-1|Derivation §C9.9.1]], step 2). No sign differs.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.9.4: CPT in Another Order
> With the order reversed, $\Theta' \equiv U(\mathcal T)\,U(\mathcal P)\,U(\mathcal C)$ (first $\mathcal C$, then $\mathcal P$, then $\mathcal T$),
>
> $$
> \Theta'\,\hat\psi(x)\,\Theta'^{-1} = -\eta_a\,\gamma^5\,\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x) :
> $$
>
> the same as [[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^thm-c9-9-3|Theorem §C9.9.3]] up to the sign, a phase ("any order will do", slide 24). $\Theta'$ is antiunitary as well.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.6 (Derivation "$\Theta\hat\psi(x)\Theta^{-1} = \eta_a\gamma^5\hat\psi^{\ast}(-x)$", last sentence: "$U(T)U(P)U(C)$ gives $-\eta_a\gamma^5\hat\psi^{\ast}(-x)$ … checked numerically") · Lecture 12, slide 24*

^thm-c9-9-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. C first.** $U(\mathcal C)\hat\psi(x)U(\mathcal C)^{-1} = -i\gamma^2\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(x)$, componentwise $-i\sum_b(\gamma^2)_{ab}\hat\psi_b^\dagger(x)$ ([[§C9.6 Charge Conjugation on the Field and Bilinears#^thm-c9-6-2|Theorem §C9.6.2]]).
>
> **2. Then P, on the adjoint field.** $U(\mathcal P)$ is unitary, so $U(\mathcal P)\hat\psi_b^\dagger(x)U(\mathcal P)^{-1} = \bigl(U(\mathcal P)\hat\psi_b(x)U(\mathcal P)^{-1}\bigr)^\dagger = \bigl(\eta_a\sum_c\gamma^0_{bc}\hat\psi_c(\tilde x)\bigr)^\dagger = \eta_a^{\ast}\sum_c\gamma^0_{bc}\hat\psi_c^\dagger(\tilde x)$ ([[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^thm-c9-3-5|Theorem §C9.3.5]]; $\gamma^0$ real). As a column: $\eta_a^{\ast}\gamma^0\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(\tilde x)$, and the number matrix $-i\gamma^2$ passes the linear $U(\mathcal P)$: the field is now $-i\eta_a^{\ast}\gamma^2\gamma^0\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(\tilde x)$.
>
> **3. Then T, which conjugates the numbers.** By [[§C9.8 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-8-3|Theorem §C9.8.3]], $U(\mathcal T)$ conjugates the coefficients: $(-i)^{\ast} = i$, $(\eta_a^{\ast})^{\ast} = \eta_a$, $(\gamma^2)^{\ast} = -\gamma^2$, $(\gamma^0)^{\ast} = \gamma^0$ ([[§C9.5 Charge Conjugation on Oscillators and Spinors#^thm-c9-5-4|Theorem §C9.5.4]], 2). On the operators, $U(\mathcal T)\hat\psi_c^\dagger(y)U(\mathcal T)^{-1} = \bigl(U(\mathcal T)\hat\psi_c(y)U(\mathcal T)^{-1}\bigr)^\dagger = \sum_d(\gamma^1\gamma^3)_{cd}\hat\psi_d^\dagger(y_T)$ ([[§C9.8 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-8-7|Theorem §C9.8.7]]; $\gamma^1\gamma^3$ real, [[§C9.8 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-8-4|Theorem §C9.8.4]], 1). With $y = \tilde x = (t, -\mathbf x)$, $y_T = (-t, -\mathbf x) = -x$. Altogether:
>
> $$
> \Theta'\hat\psi(x)\Theta'^{-1} = i\,\eta_a\,(-\gamma^2)\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^3\,\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x) = -i\eta_a\,\gamma^2\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^3\,\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x) .
> $$
>
> **4. The matrix.** Two exchanges bring $\gamma^2\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^3$ to $\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$: $\gamma^2\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^3 = -\gamma^0\gamma^2\gamma^1\gamma^3 = +\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3 = -i\gamma^5$ ([[§CB.12 Clifford Algebras꞉ Grading, Basis and the Volume Element#^def-cb-12-14|Def. §CB.12.14]]). So $\Theta'\hat\psi\Theta'^{-1} = -i\eta_a(-i\gamma^5)\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x) = -\eta_a\gamma^5\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x)$.
>
> **5. Antiunitary.** As in [[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^der-c9-9-2|Derivation §C9.9.2]], step 1, with the factors in the other order ([[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries#^thm-cb-19-6|Theorem §CB.19.6]], 2).
>

> [!caution] Caution: The transposed adjoint in CPT
> The handwritten notes record the result as $CPT\,\psi(x)\,CPT^{-1} = \gamma^5\psi(-x)$. The transposed adjoint is essential, and so is the phase $\eta_a$ for the conventions used here ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^thm-c9-9-3|Theorem §C9.9.3]]). $\Theta$ contains $C$, so it must map $\hat\psi$, which annihilates particles, into something that annihilates antiparticles; $\gamma^5\hat\psi(-x)$ does not, $\gamma^5\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x)$ does. For the field, $U(\mathcal T)$ conjugates the c-number coefficients, not the operators ([[§C9.8 Time Reversal#^thm-c9-8-3|Theorem §C9.8.3]]), so the transposed adjoint brought in by $C$ survives (comparison written here). The form without the transposed adjoint, $\gamma^5\hat\psi(-x)$, is what the complexified Lorentz group gives ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^der-c9-9-6b|★ Derivation: x → −x as a complex Lorentz transformation]]); $\Theta$ is that transformation combined with Hermitian conjugation, which is why it is antilinear (the user's notes).

## The bilinears under C, P, T and CPT

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.9.5: The Bilinears under C, P, T and CPT
> For the five bilinears of [[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity#^thm-c9-4-4|Theorem §C9.4.4]] (normal-ordered), with $\hat O \to U\hat OU^{-1}$ for $U(\mathcal C)$, $U(\mathcal P)$, $U(\mathcal T)$, and $\hat O \to \Theta\hat O\Theta^{-1}$ ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^def-c9-9-1|Def. §C9.9.1]]), and $\mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$ ([[§C9.1 Discrete Lorentz Transformations#^def-c9-1-2|Def. §C9.1.2]]):
>
> | bilinear | $C$ (at $x$) | $P$ (at $\tilde x$) | $T$ (at $x_T$) | $CPT$ (at $-x$) |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | $\mathsf S = \hat{\bar\psi}\hat\psi$ | $+\mathsf S$ | $+\mathsf S$ | $+\mathsf S$ | $+\mathsf S$ |
> | $\mathsf P = \hat{\bar\psi}\,i\gamma^5\hat\psi$ | $+\mathsf P$ | $-\mathsf P$ | $-\mathsf P$ | $+\mathsf P$ |
> | $\mathsf V^\mu = \hat{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\hat\psi$ | $-\mathsf V^\mu$ | $+\mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu\mathsf V^\nu$ | $+\mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu\mathsf V^\nu$ | $-\mathsf V^\mu$ |
> | $\mathsf A^\mu = \hat{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\gamma^5\hat\psi$ | $+\mathsf A^\mu$ | $-\mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu\mathsf A^\nu$ | $+\mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu\mathsf A^\nu$ | $-\mathsf A^\mu$ |
> | $\mathsf T^{\mu\nu} = \hat{\bar\psi}\sigma^{\mu\nu}\hat\psi$ | $-\mathsf T^{\mu\nu}$ | $+\mathcal P^\mu{}_\rho\mathcal P^\nu{}_\sigma\mathsf T^{\rho\sigma}$ | $-\mathcal P^\mu{}_\rho\mathcal P^\nu{}_\sigma\mathsf T^{\rho\sigma}$ | $+\mathsf T^{\mu\nu}$ |
> | $\partial_\mu$ (on a field's argument) | $\partial_\mu$ | $\mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu\partial_\nu$ | $-\mathcal P^\mu{}_\nu\partial_\nu$ | $-\partial_\mu$ |
>
> each entry evaluated at the point named in the column head, $\tilde x = (t, -\mathbf x)$, $x_T = (-t, \mathbf x)$. The $CPT$ column is the product of the other three, and it follows one rule: **under $\Theta$ a bilinear with $n$ Lorentz indices is multiplied by $(-1)^n$** and moved to $-x$; a derivative counts as an index.

## The CPT theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.9.6: The CPT Theorem
> Every local, Lorentz-invariant quantum field theory is invariant under $CPT$. "Local" means finitely many fields and finitely many derivatives in the Lagrangian. Precisely (Yu): if a local theory has a Hermitian Lagrangian density $\hat{\mathcal L}(x)$, invariant under proper orthochronous Lorentz transformations ($SO^+(1,3)$, [[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^rem-c1a-4-1|§C1a.4, Remark: What "Lorentz invariant" means in field theory]]), and its fields are quantized according to the spin–statistics theorem ([[§C5b.9 Spin and Statistics|§C5b.9]]), then
>
> $$
> \Theta\,\hat{\mathcal L}(x)\,\Theta^{-1} = \hat{\mathcal L}(-x), \qquad \Theta\,\hat S\,\Theta^{-1} = \hat S, \quad \hat S = \int d^4x\,\hat{\mathcal L}(x) ,
> $$
>
> with $\Theta$ of [[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^def-c9-9-1|Def. §C9.9.1]].
>
> *Layer:* the user's notes state this as a principle; it is a theorem (Lüders, Pauli 1954; Jost), whose proof is beyond the course. Below: the lecture's idea of proof only.
> *Source: Lecture 12, slides 24–25 ("All local Lorentz invariant QFTs are invariant under $\mathcal{CPT}$"; "'local' means: finite # of fields, finite # of derivatives") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.6 (Principle "The CPT theorem") · Yu §9.5, eq. (9.323) (written $\Theta^{-1}\mathcal L(x)\Theta = +\mathcal L(-x)$, with the inverse on the other side) and p. 404 · PS §3.6, p. 71 ("one cannot build a Lorentz-invariant quantum field theory with a Hermitian Hamiltonian that violates $CPT$"; proof: Streater and Wightman)*

> [!derivation]- Idea of proof (Lecture 12)
> **1. What Θ does to a field.** It multiplies the Dirac field by $\gamma^5$ and reverses the whole four-vector: $\Theta\hat\psi(x)\Theta^{-1} = \eta_a\gamma^5\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x)$ ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^thm-c9-9-3|Theorem §C9.9.3]]). Both are sign flips (transcript: "Then gamma five. It's also something like flipping sign. Yeah, that's exactly right").
>
> **2. Vector indices flip.** In the example of [[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^ex-c9-9-1|Example §C9.9.1]], a vector index gives one sign, whether it sits on the field or on the argument; derivatives count, since $\partial_\mu \to -\partial_\mu$ under $x \to -x$.
>
> **3. Invariant terms have indices in pairs.** A Lorentz-invariant term of a Lagrangian has all its indices contracted, so the signs come in pairs and cancel: "You cannot write down any term where you have contracted the indices correctly and violate CPT. You just can't" (Lecture 12). Behind it lies invariance under the four-vector inversion $x \to -x$ (slide 24). That element is in the component of $\mathcal P\mathcal T$, not connected to the identity in the real Lorentz group ([[§C9.1 Discrete Lorentz Transformations|§C9.1]]), but it is reached continuously once the parameters are allowed to be complex (★ Derivation below).
>
> **4. The general statement (Yu).** With suitable phases, every Hermitian operator built from fields and derivatives picks up $(-1)^n$ under $\Theta$, $n$ being its number of uncontracted Lorentz indices (Yu §9.5, p. 404); the table ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^thm-c9-9-5|Theorem §C9.9.5]]) and Example §C9.9.1 are instances. $\hat{\mathcal L}$ is a Hermitian Lorentz scalar, so $n = 0$ and $\Theta\hat{\mathcal L}(x)\Theta^{-1} = \hat{\mathcal L}(-x)$; integrating over $x$ (substitution $y = -x$, Jacobian $1$) gives $\Theta\hat S\Theta^{-1} = \hat S$.
>
> **5. The full proof** considers all possible terms (slide 25) and is not given in the course; it was the optional last problem announced for Problem Set 7, which in the submitted set became a qualitative argument (Problem 4(c); [[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^ex-c9-9-2|Example §C9.9.2]]).
>
> **What the idea shows**
> - $CPT$ invariance comes "for free" with Lorentz invariance and locality; it is not an extra assumption, which is why it is mandatory where $C$, $P$, $T$ separately are not ([[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity#^rem-c9-4-3|§C9.4, Remark: Mandatory and optional symmetries]]).

> [!derivation]- ★ Derivation (beyond the lecture): x → −x as a complex Lorentz transformation
> **1. On four-vectors.** A boost along $z$ with rapidity $\eta$ and a rotation about $z$ by $\theta$ commute (they act on the separate planes $(t, z)$ and $(x, y)$). The boost has the block $\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\eta & \sinh\eta\\ \sinh\eta & \cosh\eta\end{pmatrix}$ on $(t, z)$, up to the sign convention of $\sinh\eta$, the rotation the block $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ on $(x, y)$. Continue the rapidity to $\eta = i\pi$: $\cosh(i\pi) = \cos\pi = -1$ and $\sinh(i\pi) = i\sin\pi = 0$, so the boost becomes $(t, z) \to (-t, -z)$; with $\theta = \pi$ the rotation gives $(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$. Together $\Lambda(\eta = i\pi, \theta = \pi) = -\mathbb 1 = \mathcal P\mathcal T$ ([[§C9.1 Discrete Lorentz Transformations#^def-c9-1-1|Def. §C9.1.1]]).
>
> **2. A path through complex Lorentz transformations.** Along $\eta = i\pi\tau$, $\theta = \pi\tau$, $0 \le \tau \le 1$, every matrix satisfies $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}g\Lambda = g$, because $\cosh^2 - \sinh^2 = 1$ and $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$ hold for complex arguments. So within the complexified Lorentz group $x \to -x$ is connected to the identity. In the real group it is not: $\det\Lambda = +1$ all along, but $\Lambda^0{}_0 = \cos(\pi\tau)$ passes through $0$ at $\tau = \frac12$, which no real Lorentz transformation can do ($|\Lambda^0{}_0| \ge 1$, [[§C9.1 Discrete Lorentz Transformations|§C9.1]]); the path leaves the real group.
>
> **3. On Dirac spinors.** The same complex parameters in the spinor matrices of [[§C5a.4 SL(2,C) and the Group Action on Spinor Space#^thm-c5a-4-3|Theorem §C5a.4.3]]: the boost gives $e^{\mp\eta\sigma^3/2} = e^{\mp i\pi\sigma^3/2} = \cos\frac\pi2\,\mathbb 1 \mp i\sin\frac\pi2\,\sigma^3 = \mp i\sigma^3$ on the upper ($\Lambda_L$) and lower ($\Lambda_R$) block, the rotation $e^{-i\pi\sigma^3/2} = -i\sigma^3$ on both. The products are $(-i\sigma^3)(-i\sigma^3) = -(\sigma^3)^2 = -\mathbb 1$ and $(i\sigma^3)(-i\sigma^3) = +\mathbb 1$, so
>
> $$
> \Lambda_{1/2}(\eta = i\pi, \theta = \pi) = \begin{pmatrix}-\mathbb 1 & 0\\ 0 & \mathbb 1\end{pmatrix} = \gamma^5
> $$
>
> in the chiral basis ([[§C5a.5 Chirality and Weyl Spinors#^thm-c5a-5-1|Theorem §C5a.5.1]], 1). Continuing to $\eta = -i\pi$ instead gives $-\gamma^5$, a phase.
>
> **4. The γ⁵ of Θ.** This is the $\gamma^5$ of [[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^thm-c9-9-3|Theorem §C9.9.3]]: the field multiplied by $\gamma^5$ at the point $-x$ is the complexified Lorentz transformation of the field. $\Theta$ is that transformation combined with Hermitian conjugation, which is why it is antilinear and carries the transposed adjoint ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^cau-c9-9-1|Caution: The transposed adjoint in CPT]]).
>
> **5. The Jost / Pauli–Lüders argument, sketched only.** A theory whose correlation functions are analytic functions of the Lorentz parameters (which positivity of the energy guarantees) inherits invariance under the complex path from ordinary Lorentz invariance, in particular under $x \to -x$. To turn it into a symmetry of the Hilbert space one combines it with Hermitian conjugation, which reverses the order of operators; the spin–statistics connection makes the reordering harmless ([[§C5b.9 Spin and Statistics|§C5b.9]]). The proof is not part of the course.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - $x \to -x$ is "almost" a Lorentz transformation: it lies in the component of $\mathcal P\mathcal T$, not connected to the identity among real transformations, but reachable through complex ones. The course's index counting (every index flips) is the real shadow of this: under $\Lambda = -\mathbb 1$ a tensor with $n$ indices picks up $(-1)^n$.
>

> [!example] Example §C9.9.2: Why a Hermitian, Local Lorentz Scalar Is CPT Invariant (a Qualitative Argument)
> Let $\hat O(x)$ be a Hermitian, local, Lorentz-scalar operator built from a Dirac field $\hat\psi$, a complex scalar field $\hat\phi$ and their conjugates: a sum of normal-ordered products of $\hat\phi$, $\hat\phi^\dagger$, $\hat\psi$, $\hat{\bar\psi}$ and their derivatives, all at the same point $x$, with c-number coefficients and every Lorentz and spinor index contracted (spinor indices inside bilinears $\hat{\bar\psi}\Gamma\hat\psi$, Lorentz indices with $g_{\mu\nu}$ or $\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$; "Lorentz scalar" refers to rotations and boosts, so pseudoscalars such as $\hat{\bar\psi}i\gamma^5\hat\psi$ are included). Then
>
> $$
> \Theta\,\hat O(x)\,\Theta^{-1} = \hat O(-x), \qquad \Theta\,\hat S\,\Theta^{-1} = \hat S\quad\text{for}\quad \hat S = \int d^4x\,\hat{\mathcal L}(x) .
> $$
>
> *Argument* (PHY 513, Problem Set 7, Problem 4(c), as the user wrote it; the problem asks for "a qualitative argument that captures why the conclusion is difficult to avoid", not a proof). *Building blocks*: composing $T$, $P$, $C$, $\Theta\hat\phi(x)\Theta^{-1} = \hat\phi^\dagger(-x)$ ([[§C9.10 C, T and CPT for the Complex Scalar Field#^thm-c9-10-6|Theorem §C9.10.6]]), and $\Theta\hat\psi(x)\Theta^{-1} = \gamma^5\hat\psi^{\dagger\mathsf T}(-x)$ ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^thm-c9-9-3|Theorem §C9.9.3]], $\eta_a = 1$). So $\Theta$ does three things to $\hat O$:
> 1. *$PT$ sends $x \to -x$.* $\hat O$ is local, so all its factors sit at the same point, and the whole operator is evaluated at $-x$.
> 2. *Each Lorentz index contributes $-1$.* On four-vectors $\mathcal P\mathcal T = -\mathbb 1$, so each index of a tensor changes sign; for derivatives this is the chain rule, $\partial_\mu \to -\partial_\mu$ under $x \to -x$; for fermion bilinears the $\gamma^5$ of the field law gives the same rule, a bilinear with $n$ indices acquiring $(-1)^n$, the fermion reordering sign included ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^thm-c9-9-5|Theorem §C9.9.5]]); likewise the scalar current, $\Theta\hat j^\mu(x)\Theta^{-1} = -\hat j^\mu(-x)$ (Theorem §C9.10.6, 3).
> 3. *$C$, together with the antilinearity of $T$, Hermitian-conjugates.* Write $\hat O$ as a sum of terms $c\,\hat X_1\cdots\hat X_k$, $c$ a number and each $\hat X_j$ one of the blocks $\hat\phi$, $\hat\phi^\dagger$, a bilinear $\hat{\bar\psi}\Gamma\hat\psi$, or a derivative of these. By the building blocks and item 2, $\Theta\hat X_j(x)\Theta^{-1} = (-1)^{n_j}\hat X_j^\dagger(-x)$, $n_j$ the number of Lorentz indices on $\hat X_j$, and $\Theta$ conjugates $c$ ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^thm-c9-9-2|Theorem §C9.9.2]]): $\Theta\,c\hat X_1\cdots\hat X_k(x)\,\Theta^{-1} = (-1)^nc^{\ast}\hat X_1^\dagger\cdots\hat X_k^\dagger(-x)$, $n = \sum_jn_j$. Hermitian conjugation reverses the order, $(c\hat X_1\cdots\hat X_k)^\dagger = c^{\ast}\hat X_k^\dagger\cdots\hat X_1^\dagger$; but the blocks are bosonic (a bilinear contains two fermion fields), so inside a normal-ordered product the two orders agree, and $\Theta$ acts on each term as Hermitian conjugation.
>
> Together: $\Theta\hat O(x)\Theta^{-1} = (-1)^n\hat O^\dagger(-x)$. In a Lorentz scalar every index is contracted with an invariant tensor of even rank ($g_{\mu\nu}$ two indices, $\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ four), so $n$ is even; $\hat O$ is Hermitian, so $\Theta\hat O(x)\Theta^{-1} = \hat O(-x)$: the same operator at the reflected point, invariant in the sense in which a Lorentz scalar satisfies $U\hat O(x)U^{-1} = \hat O(\Lambda^{-1}x)$. For a Lagrangian density, $\Theta\hat S\Theta^{-1} = \int d^4x\,\hat{\mathcal L}(-x) = \int d^4x\,\hat{\mathcal L}(x) = \hat S$ (substitution $y = -x$, Jacobian $1$). The conclusion is difficult to avoid because each assumption removes exactly one obstruction: locality puts every factor at the common point $-x$, Lorentz invariance makes the index signs cancel, and Hermiticity undoes the conjugation; no property of the particular interaction enters. Behind items 1 and 2 lies the fact that $x \to -x$ is connected to the identity in the complexified Lorentz group, a rotation by $\pi$ in both the $(t, z)$ and $(x, y)$ planes ([[§C9.9 CPT and the CPT Theorem#^der-c9-9-6b|★ Derivation: x → −x as a complex Lorentz transformation]]).
>

> [!remark] Remark: Consequences of the CPT theorem, and the status of the discrete symmetries
> - **Equal masses and lifetimes.** $\Theta$ maps a particle at rest to the antiparticle at rest (with reversed spin); $CPT$ invariance therefore implies that particles and antiparticles have exactly equal masses and lifetimes. These equalities are among the most precise tests in physics, for example with neutral kaons and with trapped protons and antiprotons (the user's notes; Yu §9.5, p. 405). For a free field the equal masses were already visible, both species sitting on one mass shell ([[§C5b.4 Fermions, Fock Space and the Pauli Principle#^thm-c5b-4-1|Theorem §C5b.4.1]]); the theorem guarantees them with interactions.
> - **The logic of the status.** $C$, $P$, $T$ separately are optional; nature violates $P$ and $C$ (the weak interaction) and $CP$ slightly; $CPT$ is mandatory. Hence $CP$ violation is equivalent to $T$ violation ([[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity#^rem-c9-4-3|§C9.4, Remark: Mandatory and optional symmetries]], [[§C9.4 Fermion Bilinears under Parity#^rem-c9-4-4|§C9.4, Remark: Discrete symmetries in nature]]).
> - **Testing it.** Because no consistent local Lorentz-invariant theory violates $CPT$, there is no "wrong theory" to compare with; tests look for violations of its consequences, such as the mass equalities.
> - **Role.** The transformations play a central role in particle physics whether or not they are realized as symmetries of nature, and they "provide good, instructive exercises" (slide 26).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 12 §12.6 (paragraph "Consequences, and status"), §12.7 (closing paragraph) · Lecture 12, slide 26 · Lecture 11 (as in §C9.4) · Yu §9.5, pp. 404–405*
