What is the classical theory of a free massive vector field, and how does it get rid of the spin-0 component that every four-vector carries ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]])? The field strength and its units are at home in [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form|§C1a.7]] ([[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^def-c1a-7-2|Def. §C1a.7.2]], Heaviside–Lorentz: [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^def-c1a-7-1|Def. §C1a.7.1]]), the Euler–Lagrange equations in [[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1b-2-4|Theorem §C1b.2.4]], the canonical formalism and its failure for vector fields in [[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory|§C1b.4]] ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^rem-c1b-4-3|§C1b.4, Remark: Constraints and first-order Lagrangians]]); Electromagnetism treats the same Lagrangian in SI units as a worked example ([[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^ex-c1-3-2|EM ★ Example §C1.3.2]]). This section derives the Proca equation, shows that the Lorenz condition is a *consequence* of it, identifies $A^0$ as a constrained, non-dynamical variable, and writes the energy and the momentum in the three canonical pairs that remain, both in field form: $H$ by the Legendre transform, $\mathbf P$ from $T^{0i}$ index by index, with the statement that $\mathbf P$ generates spatial translations (the vector analogues of [[§C1b.6 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^thm-c1b-6-7|Theorem §C1b.6.7]] and [[§C1b.6 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^thm-c1b-6-9|Theorem §C1b.6.9]]). These are the classical homes that the quantization recalls. The quantization is [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra|§C4.4★]]–[[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field|§C4.5★]]; the plane waves are [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves|§C4.3★]]; the procedure is [[P1 Canonical Quantization#^p1-1|P1, steps 1–3]].

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): $\hbar = c = 1$, Heaviside–Lorentz units; $F^{\mu\nu} = \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu$, $A^\mu = (A^0, \mathbf A)$, $\mathbf E = -\nabla A^0 - \partial_t\mathbf A$, $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$, so $F^{i0} = E^i$, $F^{ij} = -\varepsilon_{ijk}B^k$ ([[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^def-c1a-7-2|Def. §C1a.7.2]]). SI formulas follow from the dictionary of [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1a-7-4|Theorem §C1a.7.4]] with $m \to mc/\hbar$; Electromagnetism's range $\ell = \hbar/mc$ is $1/m$ here.

## The Lagrangian and the field equation

> [!model] Model §C4.2.1: The Free Proca Field
> A real vector field $A^\mu(x)$ with field strength $F^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^def-c1a-7-2|Def. §C1a.7.2]]) and Lagrangian density
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = -\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} + \frac12m^2A_\mu A^\mu, \qquad m > 0 :
> $$
>
> the Maxwell kinetic term plus a mass term.
>
> *Assumptions:* free (quadratic, no sources); $m > 0$; $A^\mu$ real and of class $C^3$ (three derivatives are used in [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]); flat spacetime; fields falling off at spatial infinity wherever a surface term is dropped. No gauge invariance: the mass term changes under $A^\mu \to A^\mu + \partial^\mu\chi$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3, eqs. (4.44)–(4.46) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 · Proca (1936), as cited by Yu*

^mod-c4-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why F² plus a mass term
> Since total derivatives do not change the field equations ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1b-2-5|Theorem §C1b.2.5]]), every Lorentz-invariant quadratic Lagrangian with first derivatives can be written $-\frac a2\,\partial_\mu A_\nu\partial^\mu A^\nu + \frac b2\,\partial_\mu A_\nu\partial^\nu A^\mu + \frac12m^2A_\mu A^\mu$ (the remaining invariant $(\partial_\mu A^\mu)^2$ differs from the $b$-term by the divergence $\partial_\nu(A_\mu\partial^\mu A^\nu) - \partial_\mu(A^\mu\partial_\nu A^\nu)$). In this form $\dot A_0$ occurs only in $-\frac a2\dot A_0\dot A^0 + \frac b2\dot A_0\dot A^0 = \frac{b - a}{2}\dot A_0^2$, while the spatial components get $+\frac a2\sum_i\dot A_i^2$. For $b \ne a$ the spin-0 component $A^0$ is a dynamical field whose energy has the wrong sign — through its kinetic term if $b < a$, through the mass term $-\frac12m^2A_0^2$ in the energy if $b > a$ — so the energy is unbounded below, or after quantization there are states of negative norm. The choice $b = a = 1$ is exactly $-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^der-c4-2-2|Derivation §C4.2.2]], step 1), the one in which $\dot A^0$ does not appear: $A^0$ is then not a degree of freedom at all ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]]). The mass term is the only place where the theory differs from Maxwell's, and it is what breaks gauge invariance ([[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^rem-c1a-7-2|§C1a.7, Remark: Electrodynamics as the template for field theory]], "Three statements, one fact"; the gauge-invariant quadratic Lagrangians: [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^thm-c1-1-2|EM Theorem §C1.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §4 (introduction: the mismatch between four components and three polarizations); the comparison of kinetic terms written here*

^rem-c4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.2: Derivatives of the Proca Lagrangian
> Treating the sixteen $\partial_\mu A_\nu$ and the four $A_\nu$ as independent variables,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu A_\nu)} = -F^{\mu\nu}, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial A_\nu} = m^2A^\nu .
> $$
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3, eqs. (4.47)–(4.48) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.3 (Derivation "The Euler–Lagrange equation for other kinds of field", vector field)*

^thm-c4-2-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Expand the kinetic term into its four terms.** With $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ (indices of the field strength renamed $\alpha$, $\beta$ before differentiating, [[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^cau-c1b-2-3|§C1b.2, Caution: Rename the dummy index before differentiating]]),
>
> $$
> -\tfrac14F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta} = -\tfrac14\bigl[(\partial_\alpha A_\beta)\partial^\alpha A^\beta - (\partial_\alpha A_\beta)\partial^\beta A^\alpha - (\partial_\beta A_\alpha)\partial^\alpha A^\beta + (\partial_\beta A_\alpha)\partial^\beta A^\alpha\bigr] = -\tfrac12(\partial_\alpha A_\beta)\partial^\alpha A^\beta + \tfrac12(\partial_\alpha A_\beta)\partial^\beta A^\alpha ,
> $$
>
> the fourth term equal to the first and the third to the second after renaming $\alpha \leftrightarrow \beta$.
>
> **2. Differentiate the first term.** $(\partial_\alpha A_\beta)\partial^\alpha A^\beta = g^{\alpha\gamma}g^{\beta\delta}(\partial_\alpha A_\beta)(\partial_\gamma A_\delta)$ depends on $\partial_\mu A_\nu$ through both factors; each factor gives $\delta_\alpha{}^\mu\delta_\beta{}^\nu$ ([[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-12|Theorem §C1a.5.12]], 1), so its derivative is $2\partial^\mu A^\nu$ and $-\frac12$ of it is $-\partial^\mu A^\nu$.
>
> **3. Differentiate the second term.** $(\partial_\alpha A_\beta)\partial^\beta A^\alpha$: the first factor gives $\delta_\alpha{}^\mu\delta_\beta{}^\nu\,\partial^\beta A^\alpha = \partial^\nu A^\mu$; the second, $\partial^\beta A^\alpha = g^{\beta\gamma}g^{\alpha\delta}\partial_\gamma A_\delta$, gives $(\partial_\alpha A_\beta)g^{\beta\mu}g^{\alpha\nu} = \partial^\nu A^\mu$. Sum $2\partial^\nu A^\mu$; $+\frac12$ of it is $+\partial^\nu A^\mu$.
>
> **4. Combine.** $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu A_\nu) = -\partial^\mu A^\nu + \partial^\nu A^\mu = -F^{\mu\nu}$; the mass term has no derivatives and contributes nothing. The same result in one line: $\partial(F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta})/\partial(\partial_\mu A_\nu) = 4F^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-12|Theorem §C1a.5.12]], 4).
>
> **5. The mass term.** $\frac12m^2A_\alpha A^\alpha = \frac12m^2g^{\alpha\beta}A_\alpha A_\beta$; both factors depend on $A_\nu$, giving $\frac12m^2(g^{\nu\beta}A_\beta + g^{\alpha\nu}A_\alpha) = m^2A^\nu$, the factor of two of [[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-11|Theorem §C1a.5.11]]. $F$ contains no undifferentiated $A$, so the kinetic term contributes nothing here.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The kinetic term is Maxwell's; only $\partial\mathcal L/\partial A_\nu$ is new, and it plays the role that $-J^\nu$ plays in electrodynamics ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^rem-c4-2-2|Remark: Proca is Maxwell with the current −m²A]]).
> - Used next: the field equation ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-3|Theorem §C4.2.3]]) and the canonical momenta, the $\mu = 0$ row of the first formula ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]]).

*Uses:* [[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-12|Theorem §C1a.5.12]], [[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-11|Theorem §C1a.5.11]], [[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^cau-c1b-2-3|§C1b.2, Caution: Rename the dummy index before differentiating]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.3: The Proca Equation
> The Euler–Lagrange equations ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1b-2-4|Theorem §C1b.2.4]]) of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^mod-c4-2-1|Model §C4.2.1]] are
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + m^2A^\nu = 0 ,
> $$
>
> four equations, one per component $A_\nu$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3, eqs. (4.49)–(4.50) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3*

^thm-c4-2-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Insert the derivatives.** [[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1b-2-4|Theorem §C1b.2.4]] for the field $A_\nu$ reads $\partial_\mu\bigl(\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu A_\nu)\bigr) - \partial\mathcal L/\partial A_\nu = 0$. By [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-2|Theorem §C4.2.2]] this is $\partial_\mu(-F^{\mu\nu}) - m^2A^\nu = 0$.
>
> **2. Multiply by $-1$.** $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + m^2A^\nu = 0$. The free index $\nu$ of the equation is the index of the varied component: a vector equation from a vector field ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^rem-c1b-2-7|§C1b.2, Remark: One equation for every kind of field]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Varying $A_\nu$ (lower) gives the equation with $\nu$ upper; varying $A^\nu$ would give the same equation with $\nu$ lowered, since $g$ is invertible.
> - Used next: its divergence ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]) and its time component ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-8|Theorem §C4.2.8]]).

^der-c4-2-3

*Uses:* [[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1b-2-4|Theorem §C1b.2.4]], [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-2|Theorem §C4.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Proca is Maxwell with the current −m²A
> Comparing with $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = J^\nu$ ([[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1a-7-1|Theorem §C1a.7.1]]), the Proca equation is Maxwell's equation with the field's own current $J^\nu = -m^2A^\nu$: Gauss's law reads $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = -m^2A^0$ and Ampère–Maxwell $\nabla\times\mathbf B - \partial_t\mathbf E = -m^2\mathbf A$; the homogeneous pair is unchanged because $F$ still comes from a potential. Maxwell's equations are consistent only for a conserved current; here the current is $-m^2A^\nu$, and its conservation is the Lorenz condition — which the field equation therefore *implies* ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]). For a static point source the same equations give the Yukawa potential $e^{-mr}/4\pi r$ ([[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^ex-c1-3-2|EM ★ Example §C1.3.2]], (c)).
>
> *Source: written here from Theorem §C4.2.3 and Theorem §C1a.7.1; the SI version is Zangwill Problem 24.9 as worked in EM ★ Example §C1a.3.2*

## The constraint: the Lorenz condition and A⁰

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.4: The Lorenz Condition Is a Consequence of the Field Equation
> Every solution of the Proca equation ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-3|Theorem §C4.2.3]]) with $m \ne 0$ satisfies
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu A^\mu = 0 .
> $$
>
> It is a constraint the dynamics imposes, not a gauge choice: the Proca theory has no gauge freedom to choose with.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3, eqs. (4.51)–(4.53) and footnote 2 (Lorenz, not Lorentz) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 ("here a consequence of the equation of motion, not an imposed choice")*

^thm-c4-2-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The double divergence of $F$ vanishes.** Rename the dummy indices $\mu \leftrightarrow \nu$ in $\partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}$: it equals $\partial_\mu\partial_\nu F^{\nu\mu}$. Antisymmetry, $F^{\nu\mu} = -F^{\mu\nu}$, gives $-\partial_\mu\partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu}$, and for a $C^3$ field ($F$ is $C^2$) the derivatives commute, $\partial_\mu\partial_\nu = \partial_\nu\partial_\mu$, so this is $-\partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}$. A quantity equal to its negative is zero: $\partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0$, identically, whatever $A$ is.
>
> **2. Take the divergence of the field equation.** Apply $\partial_\nu$ to $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + m^2A^\nu = 0$: $0 = \partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + m^2\partial_\nu A^\nu = m^2\partial_\nu A^\nu$, by step 1.
>
> **3. Divide by $m^2$.** ⚑ By-product: this step uses $m \ne 0$. For $m = 0$ step 2 is the empty statement $0 = 0$, $\partial_\nu A^\nu$ is unconstrained, and its freedom is the gauge freedom of electrodynamics → [[§C4.6 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^rem-c4-6-4|§C4.6, Remark: The Lorenz condition: derived for Proca, chosen for Maxwell]]; [[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^thm-c1-3-2|EM Theorem §C1.3.2]] (gauge invariance makes the field equations dependent).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Of the four Proca equations only three are independent evolution equations; the divergence identity of step 1 is the same that makes Maxwell's equations require a conserved current ([[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1a-7-1|Theorem §C1a.7.1]]).
> - On plane waves the condition is $p\cdot\varepsilon = 0$, which removes exactly the spin-0 direction $\varepsilon \propto p$ ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]], 3).
> - Assumptions used: $C^3$ (or the distributional reading, where derivatives always commute, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-4|Theorem §CA.2.4]]) and $m \ne 0$.

^der-c4-2-4

*Uses:* [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-3|Theorem §C4.2.3]], [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-4|Theorem §CA.2.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.5: Proca Equals Klein–Gordon plus the Lorenz Condition
> For $m \ne 0$, $A^\mu$ solves the Proca equation if and only if
>
> $$
> (\partial^2 + m^2)A^\mu = 0 \quad (\mu = 0, 1, 2, 3) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \partial_\mu A^\mu = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3, eqs. (4.54)–(4.55) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 ("a constrained Klein–Gordon equation")*

^thm-c4-2-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Rewrite the divergence of $F$.** $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial_\mu\partial^\nu A^\mu = \partial^2A^\nu - \partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu)$, commuting $\partial_\mu$ past $\partial^\nu$ in the second term.
>
> **2. ⇒.** By [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]], $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$, so the second term of step 1 is zero and the Proca equation reads $\partial^2A^\nu + m^2A^\nu = 0$.
>
> **3. ⇐.** If both conditions hold, step 1 gives $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + m^2A^\nu = (\partial^2 + m^2)A^\nu - \partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu) = 0 - 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Each component is a Klein–Gordon field of mass $m$ ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-5|REL Theorem §B4.1.5]]), so the plane waves are on the mass shell $p^2 = m^2$; the Lorenz condition is the extra input. ⚑ By-product: four Klein–Gordon fields alone would carry four polarizations per momentum, one of them the spin-0 direction $\varepsilon \propto p$; the Lorenz condition removes it → [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]].
> - The scalar mode expansion ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-2|Theorem §C2a.2.2]]) applies component by component; the constraint couples the components through their polarization vectors ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-5|Theorem §C4.4.5]]).

*Uses:* [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]], [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-5|REL Theorem §B4.1.5]]

## Canonical variables

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.6: The Canonical Momenta; π⁰ = 0
> The momentum densities $\pi^\nu \equiv \partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_0A_\nu)$ ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^def-c1b-4-1|Def. §C1b.4.1]]) of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^mod-c4-2-1|Model §C4.2.1]] are
>
> $$
> \pi^\nu = -F^{0\nu} : \qquad \pi^0 = 0, \qquad \pi^i = F^{i0} = E^i .
> $$
>
> $\pi^0 = 0$ identically: $\dot A^0$ does not occur in $\mathcal L$, the velocity $\dot A^0$ cannot be solved for, and $A^0$ is not a canonical coordinate. The momentum conjugate to $A^i$ (upper index) is $\pi_i \equiv \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot A^i = -\pi^i = -E^i$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3, eqs. (4.56)–(4.57), (4.59)–(4.60) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.4 ("Other fields": $\pi^\mu = F^{\mu0}$, $\pi^0 = 0$)*

^thm-c4-2-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The $\mu = 0$ row.** $\partial_0 = \partial_t$, so $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_0A_\nu)$ is the $\mu = 0$ row of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-2|Theorem §C4.2.2]]: $\pi^\nu = -F^{0\nu}$.
>
> **2. The time component.** $F^{00} = \partial^0A^0 - \partial^0A^0 = 0$, so $\pi^0 = 0$. Equivalently, in step 1 of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^der-c4-2-2|Derivation §C4.2.2]] the terms with $(\partial_0A_0)^2$ from $-\frac12(\partial_\alpha A_\beta)\partial^\alpha A^\beta$ and $+\frac12(\partial_\alpha A_\beta)\partial^\beta A^\alpha$ cancel ($\alpha = \beta = 0$ in both); $\dot A_0$ enters $\mathcal L$ only through $F_{0i}$, where it does not occur either ($F_{0i} = \partial_0A_i - \partial_iA_0$).
>
> **3. The spatial components.** $\pi^i = -F^{0i} = F^{i0} = E^i$ by antisymmetry and [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^def-c1a-7-2|Def. §C1a.7.2]].
>
> **4. Upper versus lower.** $A^i = g^{ij}A_j = -A_i$, so as functions of the velocities $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot A^i = -\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot A_i = -\pi^i$ (chain rule with $\partial\dot A_i/\partial\dot A^i = -1$). So $\pi_i = -E^i$ is conjugate to $A^i$, and $\pi^i = E^i$ to $A_i$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ By-product: $\pi^0 = 0$ is a constraint on the canonical data (a *primary constraint*): the Legendre map of [[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^def-c1b-4-2|Def. §C1b.4.2]] is not invertible, as anticipated for vector fields in [[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^rem-c1b-4-3|§C1b.4, Remark: Constraints and first-order Lagrangians]] → [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-8|Theorem §C4.2.8]] removes $A^0$.
> - The momentum is the electric field, as in electrodynamics; the mass term contains no velocities and does not change it.

> [!caution] Caution: Index placement of the momentum across sources
> The canonical pairs are $(A^i, \pi_i)$ with $\pi_i = -E^i$, or equivalently $(A_i, \pi^i)$ with $\pi^i = E^i$. Yu writes $\pi_\mu = \partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial^0A^\mu) = -F_{0\mu}$ and the commutator $[A^i, \pi_j] = i\delta^i{}_j\delta^3$; the user's pre-course notes write $\pi^\mu = \partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_0A_\mu) = -F^{0\mu}$ and $[A^i, \pi^j] = -i\delta^{ij}\delta^3$; the user's PHY 513 notes write $\pi^\mu = F^{\mu0}$. These agree ($\partial^0 = \partial_0$, $\pi_j = -\pi^j$); a sign error appears only if $\pi^i$ is paired with $A^i$ as if it were its conjugate. Electromagnetism, in SI units, has $\vec\pi = -\varepsilon_0\vec E$ conjugate to $\vec A$ ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^rem-c1-4-2|EM ★ Remark: Dirac's constraints]]), the same statement as $\pi_i = -E^i$. Boldface $\boldsymbol\pi$ in this chapter means $(\pi^1, \pi^2, \pi^3) = \mathbf E$, as in Yu (4.60).
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3, eqs. (4.56)–(4.60) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 (ETCR "the upper-index form of the canonical pairing")*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.7: The Spin Charge in Canonical Variables

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.8: A⁰ Is Fixed by the Constraint
> The $\nu = 0$ component of the Proca equation contains no time derivative of $A^0$; it is the constraint
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi + m^2A^0 = 0, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad A^0 = -\frac{1}{m^2}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi = -\frac1{m^2}\nabla\cdot\mathbf E ,
> $$
>
> and the definition of the momentum reads $\dot{\mathbf A} = -\boldsymbol\pi - \nabla A^0$. So $A^0$ is a local function of the canonical momenta on the same time slice, not an independent variable.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3, eqs. (4.61)–(4.62) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3*

^thm-c4-2-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The time component of the field equation.** $\nu = 0$ in $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + m^2A^\nu = 0$: $\partial_0F^{00} + \partial_iF^{i0} + m^2A^0 = 0$. The first term vanishes ($F^{00} = 0$); $F^{i0} = \pi^i$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]]). So $\partial_i\pi^i + m^2A^0 = 0$.
>
> **2. Solve.** ⚑ By-product: dividing by $m^2$ uses $m \ne 0$ again; for $m = 0$ the equation becomes Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = 0$, a constraint on $\boldsymbol\pi$ alone that leaves $A^0$ undetermined → [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^rem-c4-2-5|★ Remark: Second-class constraints, and why Maxwell differs]].
>
> **3. The velocities.** $\pi^i = E^i = -\partial_iA^0 - \dot A^i$ (the definition of $\mathbf E$, [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^def-c1a-7-2|Def. §C1a.7.2]]), so $\dot A^i = -\pi^i - \partial_iA^0$.
>
> **4. The same constraint in terms of velocities.** Inserting step 3 into step 1, $\partial_i\pi^i = -\nabla^2A^0 - \nabla\cdot\dot{\mathbf A}$, so $(-\nabla^2 + m^2)A^0 = \nabla\cdot\dot{\mathbf A}$: in the Lagrangian variables $A^0$ is fixed by an elliptic equation at each instant, i.e. by convolution with the Yukawa kernel $e^{-m|\mathbf x - \mathbf y|}/4\pi|\mathbf x - \mathbf y|$, the decaying fundamental solution of $-\nabla^2 + m^2$ ([[§CA.6 Boundary Values, iε Limits and Fundamental Solutions#^def-ca-6-1|Def. §CA.6.1]], [[§CA.6 Boundary Values, iε Limits and Fundamental Solutions#^thm-ca-6-8|Theorem §CA.6.8]]). ⚑ By-product: nonlocal in the velocities, local in the momenta; the canonical variables make the constraint algebraic → [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-9|Theorem §C4.2.9]] uses the local form.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - $A^0$ carries no independent data: given $\boldsymbol\pi$ on a slice it is known on that slice. Its time derivative is not independent either: by the Lorenz condition $\dot A^0 = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf A$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]).
> - Used next: elimination of $A^0$ from the Hamiltonian ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-9|Theorem §C4.2.9]]), and the equal-time commutators of $A^0$ ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-3|Theorem §C4.4.3]]).

## Energy, momentum and degrees of freedom

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.9: The Proca Hamiltonian
> With $A^0$ eliminated by [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-8|Theorem §C4.2.8]], the Hamiltonian $H = \int d^3x\,(\pi_i\dot A^i - \mathcal L)$ of the Proca field is
>
> $$
> H = \frac12\int d^3x\,\Bigl[\boldsymbol\pi^2 + \frac{1}{m^2}(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)^2 + (\nabla\times\mathbf A)^2 + m^2\mathbf A^2\Bigr], \qquad \boldsymbol\pi = \mathbf E ,
> $$
>
> a functional of the three canonical pairs $(A^i, \pi_i)$ alone. Pointwise, $\mathcal H = \frac12\bigl[\mathbf E^2 + \mathbf B^2 + m^2(A^0)^2 + m^2\mathbf A^2\bigr] + \nabla\cdot(A^0\mathbf E)$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3, eqs. (4.128)–(4.136) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 (Note "Reducing H to mode form", first part)*

^thm-c4-2-9

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The Legendre transform.** Only the spatial components have momenta ($\pi_0 = 0$, [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]]), so $\mathcal H = \pi_i\dot A^i - \mathcal L$, summed over $i$ (a term $\pi_0\dot A^0$ would be $0$ anyway).
>
> **2. The Lagrangian in three-vector form.** $-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = \frac12(\mathbf E^2 - \mathbf B^2)$ ([[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1a-7-2|Theorem §C1a.7.2]], 2; Yu splits it as $\frac12F_{0i}F^{0i} + \frac14F_{ij}F^{ij} = -\frac12\boldsymbol\pi^2 + \frac12(\nabla\times\mathbf A)^2$ for $\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$, (4.131)–(4.134)), and $A_\mu A^\mu = (A^0)^2 - \mathbf A^2$. So
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = \tfrac12\mathbf E^2 - \tfrac12\mathbf B^2 + \tfrac12m^2(A^0)^2 - \tfrac12m^2\mathbf A^2 .
> $$
>
> **3. The term $\pi_i\dot A^i$.** $\pi_i = -E^i$ and $\dot A^i = -E^i - \partial_iA^0$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-8|Theorem §C4.2.8]], step 3), so $\pi_i\dot A^i = (-E^i)(-E^i - \partial_iA^0) = \mathbf E^2 + \mathbf E\cdot\nabla A^0$.
>
> **4. Subtract.** $\mathcal H = \mathbf E^2 + \mathbf E\cdot\nabla A^0 - \frac12\mathbf E^2 + \frac12\mathbf B^2 - \frac12m^2(A^0)^2 + \frac12m^2\mathbf A^2 = \frac12\mathbf E^2 + \frac12\mathbf B^2 + \mathbf E\cdot\nabla A^0 - \frac12m^2(A^0)^2 + \frac12m^2\mathbf A^2$.
>
> **5. Integrate by parts pointwise.** $\mathbf E\cdot\nabla A^0 = \nabla\cdot(A^0\mathbf E) - A^0\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf E$, and the constraint $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = -m^2A^0$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-8|Theorem §C4.2.8]]) turns $-A^0\nabla\cdot\mathbf E$ into $+m^2(A^0)^2$. Then
>
> $$
> \mathcal H = \tfrac12\mathbf E^2 + \tfrac12\mathbf B^2 + \tfrac12m^2(A^0)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\mathbf A^2 + \nabla\cdot(A^0\mathbf E) ,
> $$
>
> the pointwise form of the statement.
>
> **6. Eliminate $A^0$.** $m^2(A^0)^2 = m^2\cdot\frac{(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)^2}{m^4} = \frac{(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)^2}{m^2}$, and $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$, $\mathbf E = \boldsymbol\pi$.
>
> **7. Drop the divergence.** $\int d^3x\,\nabla\cdot(A^0\mathbf E) = \lim_{R\to\infty}\oint_{|\mathbf x| = R}A^0\,\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S = 0$ for fields with $A^0\mathbf E = o(1/R^2)$ (the fall-off of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^mod-c4-2-1|Model §C4.2.1]]; for the quantum field the statement holds between wave-packet states, where the mode integrals converge, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-3|Theorem §CA.2.3]]). ⚑ By-product: the dropped surface term is the only difference between the canonical $\mathcal H$ and the manifestly positive density of step 5, which is the $T^{00}$ of the symmetric (Belinfante) tensor of the Proca field, $T^{\mu\nu} = -F^{\mu\rho}F^\nu{}_\rho + \frac14g^{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}F^{\rho\sigma} + m^2\bigl(A^\mu A^\nu - \frac12g^{\mu\nu}A_\rho A^\rho\bigr)$ ([[§C1b.7 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor#^thm-c1b-7-4|Theorem §C1b.7.4]]; its $00$ component checked here: $-F^{0\rho}F^0{}_\rho = \mathbf E^2$, $\frac14F^2 = -\frac12(\mathbf E^2 - \mathbf B^2)$, mass part $\frac12m^2((A^0)^2 + \mathbf A^2)$) → [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-10|Theorem §C4.2.10]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The constraint does real work twice: it converts the cross term into $+m^2(A^0)^2$ (step 5) and lets $A^0$ be eliminated (step 6). The two $(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)^2$ contributions, $+1/m^2$ from step 5 and $-1/2m^2$ from the mass term, combine to $+1/2m^2$.
> - $H$ depends only on $(A^i, \pi_i)$: the phase space is that of three fields ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-14|Theorem §C4.2.14]]).
> - Used next: Hamilton's equations ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-11|Theorem §C4.2.11]]) and the mode form ([[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-2|Theorem §C4.5.2]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.10: The Proca Energy Is Positive
> For canonical data falling off at infinity, $H \ge 0$, and $H = 0$ only for $\boldsymbol\pi = 0$, $\mathbf A = 0$ (hence $A^0 = 0$): the classical Proca field has no configuration of negative energy, and the vacuum $A^\mu = 0$ is its unique minimum.
>
> *Source: read off from Yu §4.3, eq. (4.136); stated here*

^thm-c4-2-10

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Each term is nonnegative.** In [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-9|Theorem §C4.2.9]] the integrand is a sum of squares of real fields with positive coefficients $\frac12$, $\frac1{2m^2}$, $\frac12$, $\frac{m^2}2$ (here $m^2 > 0$ is used).
>
> **2. Equality.** $H = 0$ forces each square to vanish almost everywhere, and for continuous fields everywhere: $\boldsymbol\pi = 0$ and $m^2\mathbf A^2 = 0$, so $\mathbf A = 0$; then $A^0 = -\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi/m^2 = 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Positivity rests on the constraint: with $A^0$ independent, the term $-\frac12m^2(A^0)^2$ of step 4 of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^der-c4-2-9|Derivation §C4.2.9]] would be negative. ⚑ By-product: commutators will therefore give a positive Hamiltonian and positive norms at once, unlike the Dirac field, whose classical energy is unbounded below ([[§C5b.5 The U(1) Charge, Particles and Antiparticles#^thm-c5b-5-2|Theorem §C5b.5.2]]) → [[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-6|Theorem §C4.5.6]].
> - The covariant photon keeps all four components dynamical and pays with indefinite norms ([[§C4.7 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-7-17|Theorem §C4.7.17]]): the price of not solving the constraint.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.11: Hamilton's Equations Reproduce the Proca Equation
> With $H$ of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-9|Theorem §C4.2.9]] as a functional of $A^i$ and their conjugates $\pi_i = -E^i$, and $A^0 \equiv -\nabla\cdot\mathbf E/m^2$, Hamilton's equations ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1b-4-4|Theorem §C1b.4.4]]) are
>
> $$
> \dot{\mathbf A} = -\mathbf E - \nabla A^0, \qquad \dot{\mathbf E} = \nabla\times\mathbf B + m^2\mathbf A ,
> $$
>
> and together with the definition of $A^0$ they are equivalent to the Proca equation; the Lorenz condition $\dot A^0 = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf A$ follows from them.
>
> *Source: derived here from Yu (4.136) and Theorem §C1b.4.4*

^thm-c4-2-11

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. $H$ in the canonical variables.** With $E^i = -\pi_i$: $\mathbf E^2 = \pi_i\pi_i$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = -\partial_i\pi_i$, so $H = \frac12\int d^3x\,\bigl[\pi_i\pi_i + \frac1{m^2}(\partial_j\pi_j)^2 + (\nabla\times\mathbf A)^2 + m^2A^iA^i\bigr]$.
>
> **2. Vary $\pi_i$.** $\delta\int\frac12\pi_j\pi_j = \int\pi_i\,\delta\pi_i$; $\delta\int\frac1{2m^2}(\partial_j\pi_j)^2 = \int\frac1{m^2}(\partial_j\pi_j)\,\partial_i\delta\pi_i = -\int\frac1{m^2}\partial_i(\partial_j\pi_j)\,\delta\pi_i$, integrating by parts with a compactly supported $\delta\pi_i$ (no surface term). So $\delta H/\delta\pi_i = \pi_i - \frac1{m^2}\partial_i(\partial_j\pi_j)$.
>
> **3. The first equation.** $\dot A^i = \delta H/\delta\pi_i = \pi_i - \frac1{m^2}\partial_i(\partial_j\pi_j)$. Here $\pi_i = -E^i$ and $\partial_j\pi_j = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = m^2A^0$ (the definition of $A^0$), so $-\frac1{m^2}\partial_i(\partial_j\pi_j) = -\partial_iA^0$ and $\dot A^i = -E^i - \partial_iA^0$: the definition of $\mathbf E$, i.e. the time–space components of $F^{\mu\nu}$.
>
> **4. Vary $A^i$.** $\delta\int\frac12(\nabla\times\mathbf A)^2 = \int\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\delta\mathbf A) = \int\delta\mathbf A\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)$, by $\nabla\cdot(\delta\mathbf A\times\mathbf B) = \mathbf B\cdot\nabla\times\delta\mathbf A - \delta\mathbf A\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf B$ and the vanishing of the surface term; $\delta\int\frac{m^2}2\mathbf A^2 = \int m^2\mathbf A\cdot\delta\mathbf A$. So $\dot\pi_i = -\delta H/\delta A^i = -(\nabla\times\mathbf B)^i - m^2A^i$, and with $\pi_i = -E^i$: $\dot{\mathbf E} = \nabla\times\mathbf B + m^2\mathbf A$.
>
> **5. Compare with Proca, $\nu = i$.** $\partial_0F^{0i} + \partial_jF^{ji} + m^2A^i = 0$ with $F^{0i} = -E^i$ and $F^{ji} = -\varepsilon_{jik}B^k = \varepsilon_{ijk}B^k$, so $\partial_jF^{ji} = \varepsilon_{ijk}\partial_jB^k = (\nabla\times\mathbf B)^i$: the equation is $-\dot E^i + (\nabla\times\mathbf B)^i + m^2A^i = 0$, which is step 4. The $\nu = 0$ equation is the definition of $A^0$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-8|Theorem §C4.2.8]]). So Hamilton's equations plus the definition are the four Proca equations.
>
> **6. The Lorenz condition.** $\dot A^0 = -\frac1{m^2}\nabla\cdot\dot{\mathbf E} = -\frac1{m^2}\bigl(\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B) + m^2\nabla\cdot\mathbf A\bigr) = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf A$, the divergence of a curl vanishing: $\partial_\mu A^\mu = \dot A^0 + \nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 0$, recovering [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]] inside the Hamiltonian system.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The reduced system — three canonical pairs and $A^0$ defined by the constraint — loses nothing: it is the Proca theory. This is what licenses quantizing only $(A^i, \pi_i)$ ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^pr-c4-4-2|Principle §C4.4.2]]).
> - The second equation is Ampère–Maxwell with current $-m^2\mathbf A$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^rem-c4-2-2|Remark: Proca is Maxwell with the current −m²A]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.12: The Proca Momentum
> The momentum of the Proca field, the Noether charge of spatial translations ([[§C1b.6 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^thm-c1b-6-6|Theorem §C1b.6.6]]), has the density $T^{0i} = E^l\,\partial_iA^l$ (summed over $l$), so
>
> $$
> \mathbf P = \bigl(P^1, P^2, P^3\bigr) = \int d^3x\;E^l\,\nabla A^l = -\int d^3x\;\pi_l\,\nabla A^l ,
> $$
>
> a functional of the three canonical pairs $(A^l, \pi_l)$ alone ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]]). With $H = P^0 = \int d^3x\,T^{00}$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-9|Theorem §C4.2.9]]) it forms $P^\mu = \int d^3x\,T^{0\mu}$, conserved on solutions whose fields fall off at spatial infinity.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3, eq. (4.138) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 (Note "Reducing H to mode form": "$\mathbf P = -\int\pi^i\nabla A_i$ ($= +\int\pi^i\nabla A^i$)") · the derivation from $T^{0i}$ index by index written here, as for the scalar ([[§C1b.6 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^thm-c1b-6-7|Theorem §C1b.6.7]])*

^thm-c4-2-12

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the coefficient of the current index). Treating the $\partial_\mu A_\rho$ as independent variables, $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu A_\rho) = -F^{\mu\rho}$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-2|Theorem §C4.2.2]]); the mass term has no derivatives and contributes nothing here. For $\mu = 0$: $-F^{0\rho} = \pi^\rho$, the momenta of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]].
>
> **Step 2** (the tensor). Insert Step 1 into [[§C1b.6 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^def-c1b-6-2|Def. §C1b.6.2]], with the four field components $A_\rho$ summed over:
>
> $$
> T^{\mu\nu} = \sum_\rho\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu A_\rho)}\,\partial^\nu A_\rho - g^{\mu\nu}\mathcal L = -F^{\mu\rho}\,\partial^\nu A_\rho - g^{\mu\nu}\mathcal L ,
> $$
>
> the vector row of [[§C1b.6 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^rem-c1b-6-3|§C1b.6, Remark: The canonical tensor of the standard fields]] with $\mathcal L$ now containing the mass term. Only the row $\mu = 0$ enters the charges.
>
> **Step 3** ($T^{0i}$, the $\rho$-sum written out). Put $\mu = 0$, $\nu = i \in \{1, 2, 3\}$. The metric is diagonal, $g^{0i} = 0$, so the $\mathcal L$ term drops, mass term included:
>
> $$
> T^{0i} = -F^{00}\,\partial^iA_0 - \sum_{l=1}^3F^{0l}\,\partial^iA_l .
> $$
>
> The $\rho = 0$ term vanishes because $F^{00} = 0$: $A^0$, which has no momentum ($\pi^0 = 0$), carries no momentum density either.
>
> **Step 4** (the signs, index by index). Three facts: $-F^{0l} = E^l$ ([[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^def-c1a-7-2|Def. §C1a.7.2]]); $\partial^i = g^{ii}\partial_i = -\partial_i$ (no sum; $g^{ii} = -1$, as in Step 4 of [[§C1b.6 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^der-c1b-6-7|Derivation §C1b.6.7]]); $A_l = g_{ll}A^l = -A^l$. Hence $\partial^iA_l = (-\partial_i)(-A^l) = +\partial_iA^l$, and
>
> $$
> T^{0i} = \sum_{l=1}^3E^l\,\partial_iA^l .
> $$
>
> ⚑ By-product: the two metric signs cancel here, unlike the scalar's single $\partial^i = -\partial_i$; the sign reappears in the canonical momentum, $\pi_l = -E^l$, so that in canonical variables $T^{0i} = -\sum_l\pi_l\,\partial_iA^l$ has exactly the scalar form $-\pi\,\partial_i\phi$ once per spatial component → [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^rem-c4-2-3|Remark: Why P is the momentum, for the Proca field]].
>
> **Step 5** (the momentum vector). $P^i = \int d^3x\,T^{0i} = \int d^3x\,E^l\partial_iA^l$; the $\partial_i$ are the Cartesian components of $\nabla$, so the three charges form $\mathbf P = \int d^3x\,E^l\nabla A^l = -\int d^3x\,\pi_l\nabla A^l$.
>
> **Step 6** (conservation). On solutions $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C1b.6 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^thm-c1b-6-5|Theorem §C1b.6.5]]): $\frac{d}{dt}\int_{r<R}d^3x\,T^{0\nu} = -\oint_{r=R}dS_i\,T^{i\nu}$, and $T^{i\nu} = -F^{i\rho}\partial^\nu A_\rho - g^{i\nu}\mathcal L$ is quadratic in the fields and their first derivatives; for fields falling off faster than $1/r$ the surface integral vanishes as $R \to \infty$ (as in Step 6 of Derivation §C1b.6.7), and $dP^\nu/dt = 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Like $H$, $\mathbf P$ involves only the canonical pairs: the constrained $A^0$ drops out of $T^{0i}$ by $F^{00} = 0$.
> - The derivation is the scalar one ([[§C1b.6 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^thm-c1b-6-7|Theorem §C1b.6.7]]) with a sum over the field index; the mass term enters only through $g^{0i}\mathcal L = 0$.
> - Used next: $\mathbf P$ generates spatial translations ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-13|Theorem §C4.2.13]]); the quantum model ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra|§C4.4★]]) and the mode form ([[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-4|Theorem §C4.5.4]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.13: The Proca Momentum Generates Spatial Translations
> Take Poisson brackets of the three canonical pairs $(A^l, \pi_l)$ ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^def-c1b-4-3|Def. §C1b.4.3]]), fields differentiable and falling off at spatial infinity, and $\mathbf P = -\int d^3x\,\pi_l\nabla A^l$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-12|Theorem §C4.2.12]]). Then, as identities after smearing with test functions:
> 1. $\{A^l(\mathbf x), \mathbf P\} = -\nabla A^l(\mathbf x)$ and $\{\pi_l(\mathbf x), \mathbf P\} = -\nabla\pi_l(\mathbf x)$;
> 2. the constrained component follows suit: $\{A^0(\mathbf x), \mathbf P\} = -\nabla A^0(\mathbf x)$, with $A^0 = -\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi/m^2$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-8|Theorem §C4.2.8]]);
> 3. with $\{A^l, H\} = \dot A^l$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-11|Theorem §C4.2.11]]) and $P^0 = H$, in covariant form $\{A^l(x), P^\mu\} = \partial^\mu A^l(x)$;
> 4. $\{\mathbf P, H\} = 0$.
>
> *Source: the scalar statement, [[§C1b.6 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^thm-c1b-6-9|Theorem §C1b.6.9]] (for particles [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-5|CM Theorem §B8.1.5]]), applied here to the Proca canonical pairs; item 2 derived here*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Write $P^j = -\int d^3z\sum_l\pi_l(\mathbf z)\,\partial_jA^l(\mathbf z)$ on one time slice: the form of [[§C1b.6 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^thm-c1b-6-9|Theorem §C1b.6.9]] with the three pairs $(\phi_c, \pi_c) = (A^l, \pi_l)$, which are unconstrained canonical coordinates of the Proca theory ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-11|Theorem §C4.2.11]]).
>
> **Step 1** ($\delta P^j/\delta\pi_l$). Vary $\pi_l \to \pi_l + \varepsilon\zeta_l$, $\zeta_l$ a test function, $\mathbf A$ fixed. $P^j$ is linear in $\pi$: $\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}P^j\big|_0 = -\int d^3z\sum_l\zeta_l\,\partial_jA^l$, so $\delta P^j/\delta\pi_l(\mathbf z) = -\partial_jA^l(\mathbf z)$ ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^def-c1b-2-4|Def. §C1b.2.4]], on a slice).
>
> **Step 2** ($\delta P^j/\delta A^l$). Vary $A^l \to A^l + \varepsilon\eta^l$, $\boldsymbol\pi$ fixed: $\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}P^j\big|_0 = -\int d^3z\sum_l\pi_l\,\partial_j\eta^l$. With $\pi_l\,\partial_j\eta^l = \partial_j(\pi_l\eta^l) - (\partial_j\pi_l)\eta^l$ and the first term integrating to zero ($\eta^l$ has compact support; [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-3|Theorem §CA.2.3]]), $\delta P^j/\delta A^l(\mathbf z) = +\partial_j\pi_l(\mathbf z)$.
>
> **Step 3** (item 1). For $A^l(f) = \int d^3x\,fA^l$: $\delta A^l(f)/\delta A^k = \delta^l{}_kf$, $\delta A^l(f)/\delta\pi_k = 0$. The bracket of [[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^def-c1b-4-3|Def. §C1b.4.3]] gives $\{A^l(f), P^j\} = \sum_k\int d^3z\,\bigl(\delta^l{}_kf\cdot(-\partial_jA^k) - 0\bigr) = -\int d^3z\,f\,\partial_jA^l$. For $\pi_l(g) = \int d^3x\,g\pi_l$ only the second term survives: $\{\pi_l(g), P^j\} = -\sum_k\int d^3z\,\delta_{lk}\,g\,\partial_j\pi_k = -\int d^3z\,g\,\partial_j\pi_l$. These are Steps 3–4 of [[§C1b.6 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^der-c1b-6-9|Derivation §C1b.6.9]] with the label $l$.
>
> **Step 4** (item 2). $A^0 = -\partial_k\pi^k/m^2 = +\partial_k\pi_k/m^2$ ($\pi^k = -\pi_k$), a linear function of the momenta with constant coefficients. The bracket is linear and the spatial derivative passes through it (smeared: it moves onto the test function, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|Def. §CA.2.4]]), so by Step 3
>
> $$
> \{A^0(\mathbf x), P^j\} = \frac1{m^2}\,\partial_k\{\pi_k(\mathbf x), P^j\} = -\frac1{m^2}\,\partial_k\partial_j\pi_k(\mathbf x) = -\partial_j\Bigl(\frac{\partial_k\pi_k}{m^2}\Bigr)(\mathbf x) = -\partial_jA^0(\mathbf x),
> $$
>
> the derivatives commuting.
>
> ⚑ By-product: the constraint is translation covariant: $\mathbf P$ moves the constrained $A^0$ exactly as it moves the canonical fields, so the reduced phase space carries the full four-vector field.
>
> **Step 5** (item 3). By [[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1b-4-6|Theorem §C1b.4.6]], $\{A^l, H\} = \dot A^l = \partial_0A^l = \partial^0A^l$ (Hamilton's equations of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-11|Theorem §C4.2.11]]; $g^{00} = 1$). With $\partial^j = -\partial_j$, Step 3 reads $\{A^l, P^j\} = \partial^jA^l$. Together, $\{A^l(x), P^\mu\} = \partial^\mu A^l(x)$.
>
> **Step 6** (item 4). By [[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1b-4-6|Theorem §C1b.4.6]], $\{P^j, H\} = dP^j/dt$, and $\mathbf P$ is conserved on solutions with the stated fall-off (Step 6 of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^der-c4-2-12|Derivation §C4.2.12]]); equivalently $\mathcal H$ of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-9|Theorem §C4.2.9]] has no explicit $\mathbf z$-dependence, so every term of $\{P^j, H\}$ is a total derivative, as in Step 7 of Derivation §C1b.6.9.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - $\mathbf P$ acts on the canonical data as $-\nabla$, as $H$ acts as $\partial_t$: the Proca momentum is the generator of spatial translations, as for the scalar.
> - Assumptions: differentiable $\boldsymbol\pi$ (Step 2) and fall-off at infinity (Steps 2 and 6).
> - ⚑ By-product: quantized with $\{F, G\} \to -i[F, G]$ ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^rem-c1b-4-5|§C1b.4, Remark: From brackets to commutators]]), item 3 becomes $[A^l, P^\mu] = i\partial^\mu A^l$, which in modes is $[\mathbf P, a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,\lambda}] = \mathbf p\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,\lambda}$ → [[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-5|Theorem §C4.5.5]].

> [!remark] Remark: Why P is the momentum, for the Proca field
> - *On a wave.* Take a transverse solution: $A^0 = 0$, $\mathbf A = a\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon\cos\theta$, $\theta = \omega t - \mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x$, $\omega = \sqrt{\mathbf k^2 + m^2}$, with $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ a real unit vector, $\boldsymbol\varepsilon\cdot\mathbf k = 0$ (each component solves Klein–Gordon, $\partial_\mu A^\mu = a\,(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\cdot\mathbf k)\sin\theta = 0$, and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = 0 = -m^2A^0$: a Proca solution by [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-5|Theorem §C4.2.5]]). Then $\mathbf E = -\partial_t\mathbf A = a\omega\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon\sin\theta$, $\partial_iA^l = a\,\varepsilon^lk_i\sin\theta$ (since $\partial_i\cos\theta = k_i\sin\theta$), and the momentum density of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-12|Theorem §C4.2.12]] is $E^l\partial_iA^l = a^2\omega\,k_i\,(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon)\sin^2\theta = a^2\omega\,k_i\sin^2\theta$. The energy density of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-9|Theorem §C4.2.9]] (positive form) has $\mathbf E^2 = a^2\omega^2\sin^2\theta$, $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A = a\,(\mathbf k\times\boldsymbol\varepsilon)\sin\theta$ with $|\mathbf k\times\boldsymbol\varepsilon|^2 = \mathbf k^2$, and $m^2\mathbf A^2 = m^2a^2\cos^2\theta$. Averaged over a period ($\langle\sin^2\rangle = \langle\cos^2\rangle = \frac12$): $\langle\mathcal H\rangle = \frac14a^2(\omega^2 + \mathbf k^2 + m^2) = \frac12a^2\omega^2$ and $\langle E^l\nabla A^l\rangle = \frac12a^2\omega\,\mathbf k$. Momentum per energy is $\mathbf k/\omega$, as for the scalar wave of [[§C1b.6 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^rem-c1b-6-2|§C1b.6, Remark: Why P is the momentum]]: per quantum $E = \omega$, $\mathbf p = \mathbf k$ ([[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-6|Theorem §C4.5.6]]).
> - *Not the canonical momentum.* $\pi_l = -E^l$ is conjugate to the field value $A^l(\mathbf x)$; the momentum carried through space is $T^{0i} = -\pi_l\,\partial_iA^l$, the canonical momentum contracted with the generator $\partial_iA^l$ of the translation, once per spatial component. $A^0$ contributes nothing: it has no momentum.
> - *Not the Poynting vector.* For the Proca field the canonical $\mathbf P$ differs from $\int\mathbf E\times\mathbf B$ by a term proportional to $m^2$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^rem-c4-2-4|Remark: The Poynting vector is not the momentum here]]).
> - *No ordering choice after quantization.* In modes $\mathbf P = \sum_\lambda\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\mathbf p\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,\lambda}a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}$ with no constant ([[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-4|Theorem §C4.5.4]]), unlike $H$ ([[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-3|Theorem §C4.5.3]]).
>
> *Source: the scalar remark of §C1b.6 (the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.5, eq. (Pfield)) · the plane-wave averages for the Proca field worked out here*

> [!remark] Remark: The Poynting vector is not the momentum here
> In electrodynamics the field momentum is $\int\mathbf E\times\mathbf B$ ([[§C1.5 The Electromagnetic Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c1-5-3|EM Theorem §C1.5.3]]). For the Proca field the canonical $\mathbf P$ differs from it: $(\mathbf E\times(\nabla\times\mathbf A))^j = E^i\partial_jA^i - E^i\partial_iA^j$, and $\int E^i\partial_iA^j = -\int(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)A^j = m^2\int A^0A^j$ (by parts, then the constraint), so
>
> $$
> \mathbf P = \int d^3x\,\mathbf E\times\mathbf B + m^2\int d^3x\,A^0\mathbf A .
> $$
>
> The extra term is the momentum carried by the "charge density" $-m^2A^0$ of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^rem-c4-2-2|Remark: Proca is Maxwell with the current −m²A]]; for the free Maxwell field ($m^2A^0 \to$ Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = 0$) the two coincide.
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 (Note "Reducing H to mode form": "The Poynting-like form … is not equal to $\vec P$ here")*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.14: Three Degrees of Freedom per Point
> A solution of the Proca equation is determined by the six functions $\mathbf A(\mathbf x)$, $\mathbf E(\mathbf x)$ on one time slice, which are unconstrained; $A^0$ and $\dot A^0$ on the slice are fixed by them ($A^0 = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf E/m^2$, $\dot A^0 = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf A$). The Proca field has three degrees of freedom per point, one more than the Maxwell field ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-4|EM Theorem §C1.4.4]]) and one fewer than four Klein–Gordon fields.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3, after eq. (4.57) ("$A^\mu$ has only 3 independent components") · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 ("The count matches Wigner") · the Cauchy-data count written here*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Data for four Klein–Gordon fields.** By [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-5|Theorem §C4.2.5]] each component obeys a second-order equation in time, so a priori the data are $A^\mu$ and $\dot A^\mu$: eight functions.
>
> **2. Two are fixed.** On the slice, $A^0 = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf E/m^2$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-8|Theorem §C4.2.8]]) with $\mathbf E = -\nabla A^0 - \dot{\mathbf A}$, and $\dot A^0 = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf A$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]). Trading $\dot{\mathbf A}$ for $\mathbf E$ (an invertible change once $A^0$ is known), the free data are $(\mathbf A, \mathbf E)$: six functions.
>
> **3. They evolve freely.** [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-11|Theorem §C4.2.11]] gives $\dot{\mathbf A}$ and $\dot{\mathbf E}$ in terms of $(\mathbf A, \mathbf E)$ and their spatial derivatives, with no further condition; every choice of $(\mathbf A, \mathbf E)$ is admissible (unlike Maxwell, where $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = 0$ restricts the data). Six first-order data are three canonical pairs: three degrees of freedom per point.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The count $3 = 2s + 1$ for $s = 1$ matches the massive little group ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-8|Theorem §C3.6.8]]) and the three polarizations of [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]].
> - ⚑ By-product: the counts differ at $m = 0$ (Maxwell: two) although the Lagrangians differ only by the mass term: the limit $m \to 0$ is discontinuous in the number of degrees of freedom, and the missing one is the longitudinal polarization, whose vector diverges like $1/m$ → [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-10|Theorem §C4.3.10]], [[§C4.6 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-6-1|Theorem §C4.6.1]].

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Second-class constraints, and why Maxwell differs
> In Dirac's language, $\pi_0 \approx 0$ is a *primary* constraint; requiring it to hold at all times gives the *secondary* constraint $\chi \equiv \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi + m^2A^0 \approx 0$ (Gauss's law with source $-m^2A^0$). Their Poisson bracket ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^def-c1b-4-3|Def. §C1b.4.3]], with $A^0$ and $\pi_0$ temporarily kept as a canonical pair) is $\{\pi_0(\mathbf x), \chi(\mathbf y)\} = -m^2\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y) \ne 0$: the pair is *second class*. Second-class constraints are solved, not gauged: they remove the pair $(A^0, \pi_0)$ and leave the three pairs of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-14|Theorem §C4.2.14]], and the brackets of what remains are Dirac brackets — which is why $A^0$, now a function of $\boldsymbol\pi$, fails to commute with $A^j$ after quantization ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-3|Theorem §C4.4.3]]). At $m = 0$ the bracket vanishes: $\pi_0 \approx 0$ and $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi \approx 0$ are *first class*, they generate gauge transformations, and each removes one degree of freedom from $(\mathbf A, \boldsymbol\pi)$ in addition, leaving two ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^rem-c1-4-2|EM ★ Remark: Dirac's constraints, and the Coulomb gauge as the true variables]]; [[§C4.6 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-6-5|Theorem §C4.6.5]]).
>
