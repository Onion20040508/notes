What are the plane-wave solutions of the Proca equation, and how is the general field built from them? Each component is a Klein–Gordon field ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-5|Theorem §C4.2.5]]), so the scalar mode expansion ([[§C2.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]) applies component by component; what is new is the index, carried by a **polarization vector**, and the Lorenz condition ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]), which confines it to the three-dimensional space transverse to $p$ found in [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]]. This section constructs two bases of that space — the spin basis, obtained by boosting the rest frame with the standard boost of [[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-9|Theorem §C3.5.9]], and Yu's helicity basis — proves orthonormality, transversality and the **polarization sum** $\sum_\lambda\varepsilon^\mu\varepsilon^{\nu\ast} = -g^{\mu\nu} + p^\mu p^\nu/m^2$, isolates the longitudinal vector's growth $\sim p/m$, and writes the mode expansion of $A^\mu$ and of its momentum. It is the vector analogue of the spinor solutions and spin sums of [[§C5.5 Plane-Wave Solutions|§C5.5]]–[[§C5.6 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity|§C5.6]]; the step is [[P1 Canonical Quantization#^p1-3|P1, step 3]].

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): $p^\mu = (E_{\mathbf p}, \mathbf p)$ on shell, $E_{\mathbf p} = \sqrt{\mathbf p^2 + m^2}$; $p\cdot\varepsilon$ is the Minkowski product, $\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ the Euclidean one of the spatial parts; $\varepsilon^{\ast}\cdot\varepsilon' = \varepsilon^{\ast}_\mu\varepsilon'^\mu$. Polarization labels: $\lambda = +, 0, -$ (helicity), $j = 1, 2, 3$ (Cartesian spin basis).

## Plane waves

> [!definition] Definition §C4.3.1: Vector Plane Waves and Polarization Vectors
> A **positive-frequency vector plane wave** of momentum $\mathbf p$ is $\varphi^\mu(x) = \varepsilon^\mu(p)\,e^{-ip\cdot x}$ with $p^0 = E_{\mathbf p}$; its complex conjugate $\varepsilon^{\mu\ast}(p)\,e^{ip\cdot x}$ is the corresponding negative-frequency wave. The constant complex four-vector $\varepsilon^\mu(p)$ is its **polarization vector**: a c-number, not an operator, labelled by the momentum and by a polarization index.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3.1, eq. (4.63) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3*

^def-c4-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.1: Plane-Wave Solutions of the Proca Equation
> For $m > 0$ and any $p$, the plane wave $\varepsilon^\mu e^{-ip\cdot x}$ ($\varepsilon \ne 0$) solves the Proca equation ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-3|Theorem §C4.2.3]]) if and only if
>
> $$
> p^2 = m^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad p\cdot\varepsilon = 0 .
> $$
>
> For each on-shell $p$ the allowed $\varepsilon$ form the three-dimensional space $p^\perp$ of [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]]: three polarizations, carrying spin $1$ under the little group; the spin-0 direction $\varepsilon \propto p$ never solves the equation.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3.1, eqs. (4.88)–(4.89) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 ("Only three physical polarizations") · the direct substitution written here*

^thm-c4-3-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Derivatives.** For $A^\nu = \varepsilon^\nu e^{-ip\cdot x}$, $\partial^\mu A^\nu = -ip^\mu A^\nu$, so $F^{\mu\nu} = -i(p^\mu\varepsilon^\nu - p^\nu\varepsilon^\mu)e^{-ip\cdot x}$ and
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = (-ip_\mu)(-i)(p^\mu\varepsilon^\nu - p^\nu\varepsilon^\mu)e^{-ip\cdot x} = -\bigl(p^2\varepsilon^\nu - p^\nu(p\cdot\varepsilon)\bigr)e^{-ip\cdot x} .
> $$
>
> **2. The equation.** Dividing the Proca equation by $e^{-ip\cdot x} \ne 0$: $(m^2 - p^2)\varepsilon^\nu + p^\nu(p\cdot\varepsilon) = 0$.
>
> **3. Contract with $p_\nu$.** $(m^2 - p^2)(p\cdot\varepsilon) + p^2(p\cdot\varepsilon) = m^2(p\cdot\varepsilon) = 0$, so $p\cdot\varepsilon = 0$ ($m \ne 0$): the plane-wave form of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]].
>
> **4. Then the mass shell.** With $p\cdot\varepsilon = 0$, step 2 reads $(m^2 - p^2)\varepsilon^\nu = 0$, and $\varepsilon \ne 0$ forces $p^2 = m^2$, i.e. $p^0 = \pm E_{\mathbf p}$ ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-5|REL Theorem §B4.1.5]]). Conversely, $p^2 = m^2$ and $p\cdot\varepsilon = 0$ make step 2 hold.
>
> **5. Counting.** For on-shell $p$, $p^2 = m^2 > 0$ and $p^\perp$ is three-dimensional ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]], 1). The candidate $\varepsilon = p$ has $p\cdot\varepsilon = m^2 \ne 0$: excluded.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The constraint and the mass shell come out of one equation: the transversality is forced at every $p$, not imposed.
> - ⚑ By-product: four free Klein–Gordon fields would have four polarizations per momentum; the Proca field has three, and the missing one is the spin-0 direction → [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^rem-c4-1-2|§C4.1, Remark: Four components, three or two states]].
> - Used next: bases of $p^\perp$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-3|Def. §C4.3.3]], [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-4|Def. §C4.3.4]]) and the mode expansion ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-11|Theorem §C4.3.11]]).

## Tetrads and completeness

> [!definition] Definition §C4.3.2: Polarization Tetrad
> For timelike $p$ ($p^2 = m^2 > 0$), a **polarization tetrad** is a set of four real four-vectors $e^\mu(p, \sigma)$, $\sigma = 0, 1, 2, 3$, with
>
> $$
> e_\mu(p, \sigma)\,e^\mu(p, \sigma') = g_{\sigma\sigma'}, \qquad e^\mu(p, 0) = \frac{p^\mu}{m} :
> $$
>
> one timelike vector along $p$ and three spacelike ones, Minkowski-orthonormal; the spacelike ones are then transverse, $p_\mu e^\mu(p, \sigma) = m\,g_{0\sigma} = 0$ for $\sigma = 1, 2, 3$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3.1, eqs. (4.64)–(4.65), (4.83)–(4.85) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3, eq. (polarization-complete) ("a vierbein adapted to $p$")*

^def-c4-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.2: An Orthonormal Tetrad Is Complete
> Every polarization tetrad satisfies
>
> $$
> \sum_{\sigma = 0}^3g_{\sigma\sigma}\,e^\mu(p, \sigma)\,e^\nu(p, \sigma) = g^{\mu\nu}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad V^\mu = \sum_\sigma g_{\sigma\sigma}\bigl(e(p, \sigma)\cdot V\bigr)\,e^\mu(p, \sigma)\ \text{ for every } V .
> $$
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3.1, eqs. (4.66)–(4.67), verified there entry by entry for his tetrad, (4.87) · the general matrix argument written here*

^thm-c4-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Matrix form.** Let $E$ be the real $4\times4$ matrix with entries $E^\mu{}_\sigma = e^\mu(p, \sigma)$ (column $\sigma$ is the vector $e(p, \sigma)$), and $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$ the matrix $g_{\sigma\sigma'}$ on the labels. Orthonormality reads $\sum_{\mu,\nu}E^\mu{}_\sigma\,g_{\mu\nu}\,E^\nu{}_{\sigma'} = \eta_{\sigma\sigma'}$, i.e. $E^{\mathsf T}gE = \eta$.
>
> **2. $E$ is invertible.** Taking determinants, $(\det E)^2\det g = \det\eta = -1$, so $\det E \ne 0$.
>
> **3. Invert.** From $E^{\mathsf T}gE = \eta$: $g = (E^{\mathsf T})^{-1}\eta E^{-1}$, hence $g^{-1} = E\,\eta^{-1}E^{\mathsf T} = E\,\eta\,E^{\mathsf T}$ ($\eta^{-1} = \eta$). In entries, with $(g^{-1})^{\mu\nu} = g^{\mu\nu}$: $g^{\mu\nu} = \sum_\sigma E^\mu{}_\sigma\eta_{\sigma\sigma}E^\nu{}_\sigma = \sum_\sigma g_{\sigma\sigma}e^\mu(p, \sigma)e^\nu(p, \sigma)$.
>
> **4. Expansion of a vector.** Contract step 3 with $V_\nu$: $V^\mu = \sum_\sigma g_{\sigma\sigma}\,e^\mu(p, \sigma)\,\bigl(e(p, \sigma)\cdot V\bigr)$. Lowering both indices of step 3 gives Yu's form $\sum_\sigma g_{\sigma\sigma}e_\mu e_\nu = g_{\mu\nu}$ (4.66).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Completeness is not a separate requirement: for four vectors in four dimensions it follows from orthonormality, as for an orthonormal basis of a Euclidean space; the weights $g_{\sigma\sigma} = \pm1$ are the signs of the norms.
> - Used next: the polarization sum, by subtracting the timelike term ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-8|Theorem §C4.3.8]]).

## The spin basis: boosting the rest frame

> [!definition] Definition §C4.3.3: Spin-Basis Polarization Vectors
> At rest, $k = (m, \mathbf 0)$, let $\varepsilon^\mu(k, j) = (0, \hat{\mathbf e}_j)$, $j = 1, 2, 3$, with $\hat{\mathbf e}_j$ the Cartesian unit vectors. For momentum $p$ define
>
> $$
> \varepsilon^\mu(p, j) \equiv L(p)^\mu{}_\nu\,\varepsilon^\nu(k, j) ,
> $$
>
> with $L(p)$ the pure boost along $\hat{\mathbf p}$ taking $k$ to $p$, the standard transformation of [[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-9|Theorem §C3.5.9]] ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]]). Complex bases are unitary combinations $\varepsilon(p, \lambda) = \sum_j\varepsilon(p, j)\,U_{j\lambda}$.
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 (Note "Transformation of the states": polarization vectors and $W = V^{-1}(\Lambda p)\Lambda V(p)$) · the boosted-rest-frame construction written here, as for the Dirac spinors ([[§C5.5 Plane-Wave Solutions#^thm-c5-5-8|Theorem §C5.5.8]])*

^def-c4-3-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.3: The Boosted Polarization Vectors Explicitly
> For any three-vector $\mathbf n$,
>
> $$
> L(p)\,(0, \mathbf n) = \Bigl(\frac{\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf n}{m},\ \mathbf n + \frac{(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf n)\,\mathbf p}{m(E_{\mathbf p} + m)}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> In particular $\varepsilon(p, j)$ is $(p^j/m,\ \hat{\mathbf e}_j + p^j\mathbf p/m(E_{\mathbf p} + m))$, smooth in $\mathbf p$ on all of $\mathbb R^3$ with components growing at most like $|\mathbf p|/m$; vectors $\mathbf n \perp \mathbf p$ are unchanged, and $\mathbf n = \hat{\mathbf p}$ goes to $\bigl(|\mathbf p|/m,\ E_{\mathbf p}\hat{\mathbf p}/m\bigr)$.
>
> *Source: computed here (the boost matrix as in the standard form; Yu's longitudinal vector (4.79) is the case $\mathbf n = \hat{\mathbf p}$)*

^thm-c4-3-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The pure boost.** $L(p)$ is the active boost along $\hat{\mathbf p}$ with rapidity $\eta$, $\cosh\eta = E_{\mathbf p}/m$, $\sinh\eta = |\mathbf p|/m$ ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^der-c3-5-9|Derivation §C3.5.9]], step 1). Decompose $x = (x^0, x_\parallel\hat{\mathbf p} + \mathbf x_\perp)$ with $\mathbf x_\perp\cdot\hat{\mathbf p} = 0$. As for the boost along $z$ ([[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1-6-3|Theorem §C1.6.3]], 2, with $\hat{\mathbf z} \to \hat{\mathbf p}$ by a rotation, which conjugates the boost),
>
> $$
> x'^0 = \cosh\eta\,x^0 + \sinh\eta\,x_\parallel, \qquad x'_\parallel = \sinh\eta\,x^0 + \cosh\eta\,x_\parallel, \qquad \mathbf x'_\perp = \mathbf x_\perp .
> $$
>
> Check: $k = (m, \mathbf 0)$ goes to $(m\cosh\eta, m\sinh\eta\,\hat{\mathbf p}) = (E_{\mathbf p}, \mathbf p)$.
>
> **2. Apply to $(0, \mathbf n)$.** Here $x^0 = 0$, $x_\parallel = \hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf n$, $\mathbf x_\perp = \mathbf n - (\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf n)\hat{\mathbf p}$. So the time component is $\sinh\eta\,(\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf n) = \mathbf p\cdot\mathbf n/m$, and the spatial part is
>
> $$
> \mathbf n - (\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf n)\hat{\mathbf p} + \frac{E_{\mathbf p}}{m}(\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf n)\hat{\mathbf p} = \mathbf n + \frac{E_{\mathbf p} - m}{m}\,\frac{(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf n)\,\mathbf p}{\mathbf p^2} .
> $$
>
> **3. Simplify.** $\mathbf p^2 = E_{\mathbf p}^2 - m^2 = (E_{\mathbf p} - m)(E_{\mathbf p} + m)$, so $\frac{E_{\mathbf p} - m}{\mathbf p^2} = \frac{1}{E_{\mathbf p} + m}$: the statement. The form has no $1/|\mathbf p|$ left, so it is smooth at $\mathbf p = \mathbf 0$ (where $L = \mathbb 1$) and everywhere else ($E_{\mathbf p} + m \ge 2m > 0$); its entries are bounded by $1 + 2|\mathbf p|/m$.
>
> **4. Special cases.** $\mathbf n \perp \mathbf p$: $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf n = 0$ and nothing changes. $\mathbf n = \hat{\mathbf p}$: time component $|\mathbf p|/m$, spatial $\hat{\mathbf p}\bigl(1 + \frac{\mathbf p^2}{m(E_{\mathbf p} + m)}\bigr) = \hat{\mathbf p}\,\frac{mE_{\mathbf p} + m^2 + E_{\mathbf p}^2 - m^2}{m(E_{\mathbf p} + m)} = \frac{E_{\mathbf p}}{m}\hat{\mathbf p}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - A boost along $\hat{\mathbf p}$ leaves transverse polarizations untouched and stretches the longitudinal one into $(|\mathbf p|, E_{\mathbf p}\hat{\mathbf p})/m$, which grows with the energy. ⚑ By-product: the longitudinal vector approaches $p^\mu/m$ → [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-10|Theorem §C4.3.10]].
> - Smoothness and polynomial growth make $\varepsilon(p, j)/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$ times a Schwartz function again a Schwartz function: the spin basis is the one in which the smeared field is manifestly well defined ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^rem-c4-3-1|Remark: In what sense the Proca field is a distribution]]).

^der-c4-3-3

*Uses:* [[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^der-c3-5-9|Derivation §C3.5.9]], [[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1-6-3|Theorem §C1.6.3]], [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-3|Def. §C4.3.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.4: Transversality and Orthonormality
> The spin-basis vectors of [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-3|Def. §C4.3.3]], and every unitary combination $\varepsilon(p, \lambda)$ of them, satisfy
>
> $$
> p_\mu\,\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda) = 0, \qquad \varepsilon^*_\mu(p, \lambda)\,\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda') = -\delta_{\lambda\lambda'} ;
> $$
>
> together with $e(p, 0) = p/m$ the real vectors $\varepsilon(p, j)$ form a polarization tetrad ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-2|Def. §C4.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3.1, eqs. (4.94), (4.99) (for his basis) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 · the boost argument written here*

^thm-c4-3-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Invariance of the product.** $L(p)$ is real with $L^{\mathsf T}gL = g$ ([[§C1.4 The Lorentz Group#^def-c1-4-1|Def. §C1.4.1]]), so $(Lu)^{\ast}\cdot(Lv) = u^{\ast}\cdot v$ for complex $u$, $v$.
>
> **2. At rest.** $k\cdot\varepsilon(k, j) = m\cdot0 - \mathbf 0\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_j = 0$, $\varepsilon(k, j)\cdot\varepsilon(k, j') = 0 - \hat{\mathbf e}_j\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_{j'} = -\delta_{jj'}$, $\frac km\cdot\frac km = 1$.
>
> **3. Boost.** By step 1 with $L(p)k = p$: $p\cdot\varepsilon(p, j) = 0$, $\varepsilon(p, j)\cdot\varepsilon(p, j') = -\delta_{jj'}$, $\frac pm\cdot\frac pm = 1$: a tetrad.
>
> **4. Unitary combinations.** $p\cdot\varepsilon(p, \lambda) = \sum_jU_{j\lambda}\,p\cdot\varepsilon(p, j) = 0$, and, the $\varepsilon(p, j)$ being real,
>
> $$
> \varepsilon^*(p, \lambda)\cdot\varepsilon(p, \lambda') = \sum_{j,j'}\overline{U_{j\lambda}}\,U_{j'\lambda'}\,\varepsilon(p, j)\cdot\varepsilon(p, j') = -\sum_j\overline{U_{j\lambda}}\,U_{j\lambda'} = -(U^\dagger U)_{\lambda\lambda'} = -\delta_{\lambda\lambda'} .
> $$
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Everything is inherited from the rest frame, where it is Euclidean orthonormality of three unit vectors with the sign of the spatial metric.
> - ⚑ By-product: the sign $-\delta$ is the negative-definite metric on $p^\perp$ ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]], 1); after quantization it reappears with the opposite sign as the positive norm of the one-particle states → [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Massive Vector Field#^thm-c4-4-9|Theorem §C4.4.9]].

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.5: Polarization Vectors Transform by the Wigner Rotation
> For every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$, with the Wigner rotation $W(\Lambda, p) = L(\Lambda p)^{-1}\Lambda L(p)$ ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]], [[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]]), a rotation acting on $\mathbb R^3$ as the matrix $\mathcal R$,
>
> $$
> \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,\varepsilon^\nu(p, j) = \sum_{j'}\varepsilon^\mu(\Lambda p, j')\,\mathcal R_{j'j}(\Lambda, p) .
> $$
>
> For a rotation $\Lambda = R$, $\mathcal R = R$ itself ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-9|Theorem §C3.5.9]]). The matrices $\mathcal R$ are the spin-1 representation $D^{(1)}$ in the Cartesian basis ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]): the polarization index transforms exactly as the label of a massive spin-1 particle ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-8|Theorem §C3.5.8]]).
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 (Note "Transformation of the states": "the intertwining property of the polarization vectors … [stated]") · derived here for the spin basis*

^thm-c4-3-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Factor the transformation.** By the definition of $W$, $\Lambda L(p) = L(\Lambda p)\,W(\Lambda, p)$, and $W$ fixes $k$, so it is a rotation ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]], [[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-7|Theorem §C3.5.7]]), acting as $(v^0, \mathbf v) \mapsto (v^0, \mathcal R\mathbf v)$.
>
> **2. Act on the rest-frame vector.** $\Lambda\varepsilon(p, j) = \Lambda L(p)\varepsilon(k, j) = L(\Lambda p)\,W\,(0, \hat{\mathbf e}_j) = L(\Lambda p)\,(0, \mathcal R\hat{\mathbf e}_j)$.
>
> **3. Expand.** $\mathcal R\hat{\mathbf e}_j = \sum_{j'}\hat{\mathbf e}_{j'}\mathcal R_{j'j}$ (column $j$ of the matrix), so by linearity $L(\Lambda p)(0, \mathcal R\hat{\mathbf e}_j) = \sum_{j'}\mathcal R_{j'j}L(\Lambda p)\varepsilon(k, j') = \sum_{j'}\varepsilon(\Lambda p, j')\mathcal R_{j'j}$.
>
> **4. Rotations.** For $\Lambda = R$, $W(R, p) = R$ ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-9|Theorem §C3.5.9]]); this is where the pure-boost choice of $L(p)$ is used.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The field's four-vector index and the particle's spin label are related by the polarization vectors: $\Lambda$ on the index equals $D^{(1)}(W)$ on the label. This is how a finite-dimensional non-unitary representation (the index) carries the unitary spin-1 representation (the states): the polarization vectors are the intertwiners.
> - ⚑ By-product: with the helicity basis instead of the spin basis, $\mathcal R$ is replaced by its matrix in that basis; for a rotation about $\hat{\mathbf p}$ it is diagonal, $e^{-i\lambda\theta}$ ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-1|Theorem §C4.1.1]]) → [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Massive Vector Field#^thm-c4-4-12|Theorem §C4.4.12]].
> - Used next: the action of rotations on the mode operators ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Massive Vector Field#^thm-c4-4-10|Theorem §C4.4.10]]).

## The helicity basis

![[ph-qft-c4-3-1.svg]]
*Yu's helicity triad at momentum $\mathbf p$: the two transverse linear polarizations are the spherical unit vectors $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$, $\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}$ of the direction $\hat{\mathbf p}$, the longitudinal one points along $\hat{\mathbf p}$ (and acquires a time component). At the poles $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}$ are undefined.*

> [!definition] Definition §C4.3.4: Helicity Polarization Vectors
> For $\mathbf p$ with $|\mathbf p_T| \equiv \sqrt{(p^1)^2 + (p^2)^2} \ne 0$, Yu's vectors are
>
> $$
> e^\mu(p, 1) = \Bigl(0,\ \frac{(p^1p^3,\ p^2p^3,\ -|\mathbf p_T|^2)}{|\mathbf p|\,|\mathbf p_T|}\Bigr), \quad e^\mu(p, 2) = \Bigl(0,\ \frac{(-p^2,\ p^1,\ 0)}{|\mathbf p_T|}\Bigr), \quad e^\mu(p, 3) = \Bigl(\frac{|\mathbf p|}{m},\ \frac{E_{\mathbf p}\hat{\mathbf p}}{m}\Bigr),
> $$
>
> and the **helicity polarization vectors** are
>
> $$
> \varepsilon^\mu(p, \pm) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(e^\mu(p, 1) \pm i\,e^\mu(p, 2)\bigr), \qquad \varepsilon^\mu(p, 0) = e^\mu(p, 3) .
> $$
>
> In spherical angles of $\hat{\mathbf p}$, $\mathbf e(p, 1) = \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ and $\mathbf e(p, 2) = \hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3.1, eqs. (4.68)–(4.70), (4.79), (4.92)–(4.93), (4.104) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 · the identification with $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$, $\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}$ computed here*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.6: The Helicity Vectors Are a Transverse Orthonormal Basis
> $(\mathbf e(p, 1), \mathbf e(p, 2), \hat{\mathbf p})$ is a right-handed orthonormal triad of $\mathbb R^3$, $e(p, \sigma) = L(p)\,(0, \mathbf e(p, \sigma))$ for $\sigma = 1, 2$ and $e(p, 3) = L(p)(0, \hat{\mathbf p})$, and with $e(p, 0) = p/m$ the four $e(p, \sigma)$ form a polarization tetrad. Hence $p\cdot\varepsilon(p, \lambda) = 0$ and $\varepsilon^*(p, \lambda)\cdot\varepsilon(p, \lambda') = -\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}$ for $\lambda, \lambda' = \pm, 0$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3.1, eqs. (4.71)–(4.86), (4.94)–(4.99) · the boost reading computed here*

^thm-c4-3-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Spherical form.** Write $\mathbf p = |\mathbf p|(\sin\theta\cos\varphi, \sin\theta\sin\varphi, \cos\theta)$, so $|\mathbf p_T| = |\mathbf p|\sin\theta$ and $|\mathbf p|\,|\mathbf p_T| = \mathbf p^2\sin\theta$. Then $\mathbf e(p, 1) = (\cos\theta\cos\varphi, \cos\theta\sin\varphi, -\sin\theta) = \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ (dividing $p^1p^3 = \mathbf p^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\cos\varphi$ etc. by $\mathbf p^2\sin\theta$, and $-|\mathbf p_T|^2/(|\mathbf p||\mathbf p_T|) = -\sin\theta$), and $\mathbf e(p, 2) = (-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi, 0) = \hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}$.
>
> **2. Orthonormal and transverse (Yu's direct check).** $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf e(p, 1) = \frac{(p^1)^2p^3 + (p^2)^2p^3 - p^3|\mathbf p_T|^2}{|\mathbf p||\mathbf p_T|} = 0$; $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf e(p, 2) = \frac{-p^1p^2 + p^2p^1}{|\mathbf p_T|} = 0$; $\mathbf e(p, 1)^2 = \frac{|\mathbf p_T|^2(p^3)^2 + |\mathbf p_T|^4}{\mathbf p^2|\mathbf p_T|^2} = 1$; $\mathbf e(p, 2)^2 = 1$; $\mathbf e(p, 1)\cdot\mathbf e(p, 2) = \frac{-p^1p^3p^2 + p^2p^3p^1}{|\mathbf p||\mathbf p_T|^2} = 0$ (Yu (4.71)–(4.76)).
>
> **3. Right-handed.** With $\mathbf a = (p^1p^3, p^2p^3, -|\mathbf p_T|^2)$, $\mathbf b = (-p^2, p^1, 0)$: $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b = \bigl(0 - (-|\mathbf p_T|^2)p^1,\ (-|\mathbf p_T|^2)(-p^2) - 0,\ (p^1)^2p^3 + (p^2)^2p^3\bigr) = |\mathbf p_T|^2\,\mathbf p$. Dividing by $|\mathbf p||\mathbf p_T|\cdot|\mathbf p_T|$: $\mathbf e(p, 1)\times\mathbf e(p, 2) = \hat{\mathbf p}$.
>
> **4. Boost reading.** By [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]], vectors $\perp\mathbf p$ are unchanged by $L(p)$, so $e(p, 1)$, $e(p, 2)$ are the boosts of $(0, \mathbf e(p, 1))$, $(0, \mathbf e(p, 2))$, and $L(p)(0, \hat{\mathbf p}) = (|\mathbf p|, E_{\mathbf p}\hat{\mathbf p})/m = e(p, 3)$. The rest-frame vectors $(0, \mathbf e(p, \sigma))$ are a Minkowski-orthonormal transverse triad at $k$, so by [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^der-c4-3-4|Derivation §C4.3.4]], steps 1–3, the $e(p, \sigma)$, $\sigma = 1, 2, 3$, with $p/m$ form a tetrad. Yu checks the longitudinal one directly: $p_\mu e^\mu(p, 3) = E_{\mathbf p}\frac{|\mathbf p|}m - \mathbf p\cdot\frac{E_{\mathbf p}\hat{\mathbf p}}{m} = 0$ and $e(p, 3)\cdot e(p, 3) = \frac{\mathbf p^2 - E_{\mathbf p}^2}{m^2} = -1$ (4.80)–(4.81).
>
> **5. The complex combinations.** $\varepsilon(p, \lambda) = \sum_\sigma e(p, \sigma)U_{\sigma\lambda}$ with $U = \begin{pmatrix} 1/\sqrt2 & 0 & 1/\sqrt2 \\ i/\sqrt2 & 0 & -i/\sqrt2 \\ 0 & 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ (columns $\lambda = +, 0, -$), which is unitary: its columns are orthonormal in $\mathbb C^3$. Step 4 of [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^der-c4-3-4|Derivation §C4.3.4]] gives transversality and $-\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}$. Written out as Yu does: $\varepsilon^{\ast}(\pm)\cdot\varepsilon(\pm) = \frac12(e_1\cdot e_1 + e_2\cdot e_2) = \frac12(-1 - 1) = -1$, $\varepsilon^{\ast}(\pm)\cdot\varepsilon(\mp) = \frac12(e_1\cdot e_1 - e_2\cdot e_2 \mp 2i\,e_1\cdot e_2) = 0$, $\varepsilon^{\ast}(\pm)\cdot\varepsilon(0) = 0$ (4.95)–(4.98).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Yu's basis is the boost of the rest-frame triad $(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}, \hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}, \hat{\mathbf p})$: it differs from the spin basis by a $\mathbf p$-dependent rotation in the rest frame, the rotation taking $(\hat{\mathbf x}, \hat{\mathbf y}, \hat{\mathbf z})$ to $(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}, \hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}, \hat{\mathbf p})$. ⚑ By-product: that rotation is undefined at $\theta = 0, \pi$ → [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^cau-c4-3-1|Caution: Phase conventions and the z-axis]].
> - The real vectors $e(p, 1)$, $e(p, 2)$ are linear polarizations, the complex $\varepsilon(p, \pm)$ circular ones (relative phase $\pm i = e^{\pm i\pi/2}$, Yu after (4.102)).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.7: The Helicity Vectors Are Helicity Eigenvectors
> With the helicity matrix of [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-1|Theorem §C4.1.1]] for $\hat{\mathbf n} = \hat{\mathbf p}$,
>
> $$
> (\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal J})^\mu{}_\nu\,\varepsilon^\nu(p, \lambda) = \lambda\,\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda), \qquad \lambda = +1, 0, -1 :
> $$

> $\varepsilon(p, \pm)$ are the transverse polarizations of helicity $\pm1$ (right and left circular), $\varepsilon(p, 0)$ the longitudinal polarization of helicity $0$.

^thm-c4-3-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Transverse vectors.** $(\mathbf e(p, 1), \mathbf e(p, 2), \hat{\mathbf p})$ is a right-handed orthonormal triad ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]]), so by [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-1|Theorem §C4.1.1]] (with $\mathbf e_1 = \mathbf e(p, 1)$, $\mathbf e_2 = \mathbf e(p, 2)$) the vectors $\varepsilon(p, \pm) = \frac1{\sqrt2}(0, \mathbf e(p, 1) \pm i\mathbf e(p, 2))$ have eigenvalues $\pm1$.
>
> **2. Longitudinal vector.** $\varepsilon(p, 0) = \frac{|\mathbf p|}{m}(1, \mathbf 0) + \frac{E_{\mathbf p}}{m}(0, \hat{\mathbf p})$, a combination of the two eigenvectors with eigenvalue $0$ of Theorem §C4.1.1: eigenvalue $0$. Yu verifies all three by multiplying out the matrix (4.103) with the column vectors (4.104), (4.105)–(4.107).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Helicity is measured by the spin matrix of the index along $\hat{\mathbf p}$; the time component, although nonzero in $\varepsilon(p, 0)$, is inert under it. The matrix $\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal J}$ does not commute with boosts along $\hat{\mathbf p}$, but $L(p)$ maps its eigenvectors at rest to eigenvectors at $p$ with the same eigenvalue because both lie in the span of $(1, \mathbf 0)$, $(0, \hat{\mathbf p})$ or in the plane $\perp\hat{\mathbf p}$, which $L(p)$ preserves.

So $\varepsilon(p, +)$ is minus the $m = +1$ spherical vector; matrix elements of ladder operators between helicity states pick up the sign.
> - **Frame dependence.** The helicity of a massive particle is not Lorentz invariant ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^rem-c3-5-4|§C3.5★, Remark: Helicity of a massive particle]]); $\varepsilon(p, \lambda)$ is a helicity eigenvector at the momentum $p$ of the frame in which it is written.

## The polarization sum

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.8: The Massive Polarization Sum
> For any orthonormal basis $\varepsilon(p, \lambda)$ of the transverse space $p^\perp$ (spin basis, helicity basis, or any unitary combination; [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]], [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]]),
>
> $$
> \sum_{\lambda = \pm, 0}\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda)\,\varepsilon^{\nu*}(p, \lambda) = -g^{\mu\nu} + \frac{p^\mu p^\nu}{m^2} ,
> $$
>
> a real symmetric tensor, so the same holds with the complex conjugate on the other factor. It is Lorentz covariant and transverse: $p_\mu\sum_\lambda\varepsilon^\mu\varepsilon^{\nu*} = 0$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3.1, eqs. (4.90)–(4.91), (4.100)–(4.102) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3, eq. (massive-pol-sum)*

^thm-c4-3-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation (first route: subtract the timelike vector from the tetrad, Yu)
> **1. Completeness.** By [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]] for the tetrad $e(p, \sigma)$ of [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]], with $g_{00} = 1$, $g_{\sigma\sigma} = -1$ ($\sigma = 1, 2, 3$):
>
> $$
> g^{\mu\nu} = e^\mu(p, 0)e^\nu(p, 0) - \sum_{\sigma = 1}^3e^\mu(p, \sigma)e^\nu(p, \sigma) .
> $$
>
> **2. Isolate the spatial vectors.** $e(p, 0) = p/m$, so $\sum_{\sigma = 1}^3e^\mu(p, \sigma)e^\nu(p, \sigma) = -g^{\mu\nu} + p^\mu p^\nu/m^2$ (Yu (4.90)–(4.91)). ⚑ By-product: the timelike vector is the only place where $m$ enters; the $p^\mu p^\nu/m^2$ term is the removed spin-0 direction, subtracted → [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-9|Theorem §C4.3.9]].
>
> **3. Pass to the helicity vectors.** Expand all four products in $\sum_\lambda\varepsilon^\mu\varepsilon^{\nu\ast}$ with $\varepsilon(\pm) = (e_1 \pm ie_2)/\sqrt2$ (writing $e_\sigma$ for $e(p, \sigma)$):
>
> $$
> \tfrac12(e_1 + ie_2)^\mu(e_1 - ie_2)^\nu = \tfrac12\bigl(e_1^\mu e_1^\nu - ie_1^\mu e_2^\nu + ie_2^\mu e_1^\nu + e_2^\mu e_2^\nu\bigr), \quad \tfrac12(e_1 - ie_2)^\mu(e_1 + ie_2)^\nu = \tfrac12\bigl(e_1^\mu e_1^\nu + ie_1^\mu e_2^\nu - ie_2^\mu e_1^\nu + e_2^\mu e_2^\nu\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The imaginary cross terms cancel between the two; the sum is $e_1^\mu e_1^\nu + e_2^\mu e_2^\nu$, and $\varepsilon(0)\varepsilon(0)^* = e_3^\mu e_3^\nu$ (real). So $\sum_\lambda\varepsilon^\mu\varepsilon^{\nu*} = \sum_{\sigma=1}^3e_\sigma^\mu e_\sigma^\nu$ (Yu (4.100)), and step 2 gives the statement.
>
> **4. Transversality.** $p_\mu(-g^{\mu\nu} + p^\mu p^\nu/m^2) = -p^\nu + p^2p^\nu/m^2 = -p^\nu + p^\nu = 0$ on the mass shell (Yu (4.102)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The sum is the tetrad's completeness relation minus its timelike member; all dependence on the particular spacelike vectors drops out.
> - Used next: the mode algebra, second route ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Massive Vector Field#^der-c4-4-4b|Derivation §C4.4.4, second route]]), and the massive propagator numerator ([[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-8-1|Theorem §C4.8.1]], [[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-8-3|Theorem §C4.8.3]]).

^der-c4-3-8

*Uses:* [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]], [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]]

> [!derivation]- Derivation (second route: rest frame and covariance, spin basis)
> **1. At rest.** $\sum_j\varepsilon^\mu(k, j)\varepsilon^\nu(k, j) = \sum_j(0, \hat{\mathbf e}_j)^\mu(0, \hat{\mathbf e}_j)^\nu = \operatorname{diag}(0, 1, 1, 1)$. On the other side, $-g^{\mu\nu} + k^\mu k^\nu/m^2 = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1) + \operatorname{diag}(1, 0, 0, 0) = \operatorname{diag}(0, 1, 1, 1)$. Equal.
>
> **2. Boost.** $\varepsilon(p, j) = L(p)\varepsilon(k, j)$ with $L = L(p)$ real, so $\sum_j\varepsilon^\mu(p, j)\varepsilon^\nu(p, j) = L^\mu{}_\alpha L^\nu{}_\beta\sum_j\varepsilon^\alpha(k, j)\varepsilon^\beta(k, j) = L^\mu{}_\alpha L^\nu{}_\beta\bigl(-g^{\alpha\beta} + k^\alpha k^\beta/m^2\bigr)$.
>
> **3. The two terms.** $L^\mu{}_\alpha L^\nu{}_\beta g^{\alpha\beta} = (Lg^{-1}L^{\mathsf T})^{\mu\nu} = g^{\mu\nu}$, because $L^{\mathsf T}gL = g$ implies $Lg^{-1}L^{\mathsf T} = g^{-1}$ (invert both sides: $L^{-1}g^{-1}(L^{\mathsf T})^{-1} = g^{-1}$, then multiply by $L$ on the left and $L^{\mathsf T}$ on the right). And $L^\mu{}_\alpha k^\alpha = p^\mu$. So the sum is $-g^{\mu\nu} + p^\mu p^\nu/m^2$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The sum is a tensor built from $g$ and $p$ alone because it is one in the rest frame and both sides transform alike: the covariance of the sum is the covariance of its two ingredients, as for the Dirac spin sums ([[§C5.6 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^thm-c5-6-9|Theorem §C5.6.9]]).

^der-c4-3-8b

*Uses:* [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-3|Def. §C4.3.3]], [[§C1.4 The Lorentz Group#^def-c1-4-1|Def. §C1.4.1]]

> [!derivation]- Derivation (third route: the sum does not depend on the basis)
> **1. Change of basis.** Two orthonormal bases of $p^\perp$ (in the sense $\varepsilon^{\ast}\cdot\varepsilon' = -\delta$) are related by a unitary matrix: $\varepsilon(p, \lambda) = \sum_j\varepsilon(p, j)U_{j\lambda}$, where $U_{j\lambda} = -\varepsilon(p, j)\cdot\varepsilon(p, \lambda)$ by the orthonormality of the $\varepsilon(p, j)$, and $U^\dagger U = \mathbb 1$ by that of the $\varepsilon(p, \lambda)$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^der-c4-3-4|Derivation §C4.3.4]], step 4, read backwards).
>
> **2. Insert.** $\sum_\lambda\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda)\varepsilon^{\nu\ast}(p, \lambda) = \sum_{j,j'}\varepsilon^\mu(p, j)\varepsilon^\nu(p, j')\sum_\lambda U_{j\lambda}\overline{U_{j'\lambda}} = \sum_{j,j'}\varepsilon^\mu(p, j)\varepsilon^\nu(p, j')(UU^\dagger)_{jj'} = \sum_j\varepsilon^\mu(p, j)\varepsilon^\nu(p, j)$, using $UU^\dagger = \mathbb 1$ (a square matrix with $U^\dagger U = \mathbb 1$).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The polarization sum is a property of the subspace $p^\perp$, not of a basis: it is minus the orthogonal projector onto it ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-9|Theorem §C4.3.9]]). This is why phase conventions and the singularity of the helicity basis on the $z$-axis never reach physical results ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^cau-c4-3-1|Caution: Phase conventions and the z-axis]]).

*Uses:* [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^der-c4-3-4|Derivation §C4.3.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.9: The Polarization Sum Is Minus the Projector Transverse to p
> The mixed tensor $P^\mu{}_\nu(p) \equiv -\sum_\lambda\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda)\,\varepsilon^*_\nu(p, \lambda) = \delta^\mu{}_\nu - p^\mu p_\nu/m^2$ satisfies
>
> $$
> P^\mu{}_\rho P^\rho{}_\nu = P^\mu{}_\nu, \qquad P^\mu{}_\nu\,p^\nu = 0, \qquad P^\mu{}_\nu\,v^\nu = v^\mu\ \ (p\cdot v = 0), \qquad P^\mu{}_\mu = 3 :
> $$
>
> it is the projector onto $p^\perp$ along $p$, and $P v = -\sum_\lambda\varepsilon(p, \lambda)\,\bigl(\varepsilon^*(p, \lambda)\cdot v\bigr)$ is the expansion of the transverse part of $v$ in the polarization basis.
>
> *Source: written here from Yu (4.91), (4.101) and the user's pre-course notes, §4.3*

^thm-c4-3-9

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Lower one index.** From [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-8|Theorem §C4.3.8]], $-\sum_\lambda\varepsilon^\mu\varepsilon^{\ast}_\nu = g_{\nu\beta}(g^{\mu\beta} - p^\mu p^\beta/m^2) = \delta^\mu{}_\nu - p^\mu p_\nu/m^2$.
>
> **2. Idempotent.** Expand the product into its four terms: $(\delta^\mu{}_\rho - p^\mu p_\rho/m^2)(\delta^\rho{}_\nu - p^\rho p_\nu/m^2) = \delta^\mu{}_\nu - p^\mu p_\nu/m^2 - p^\mu p_\nu/m^2 + p^\mu(p_\rho p^\rho)p_\nu/m^4$, and $p_\rho p^\rho = m^2$ turns the last into $+p^\mu p_\nu/m^2$: the sum is $\delta^\mu{}_\nu - p^\mu p_\nu/m^2$.
>
> **3. Kernel and range.** $P p = p - p\,(p^2/m^2) = 0$. For $p\cdot v = 0$, $Pv = v - p\,(p\cdot v)/m^2 = v$. Trace: $\delta^\mu{}_\mu - p^2/m^2 = 4 - 1 = 3 = \dim p^\perp$.
>
> **4. Expansion.** $Pv = -\sum_\lambda\varepsilon(p, \lambda)\,(\varepsilon^{\ast}(p, \lambda)\cdot v)$ by the definition; for $v = \varepsilon(p, \lambda')$ orthonormality gives $-\sum_\lambda\varepsilon(\lambda)(-\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}) = \varepsilon(\lambda')$, consistent with step 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The minus sign in front of the sum is the negative metric on $p^\perp$; $P$ itself is a positive projector.
> - ⚑ By-product: the projector contains $1/m^2$; its entries grow like $E_{\mathbf p}^2/m^2$ at high energy and have no limit as $m \to 0$. This term is the numerator $p^\mu p^\nu/m^2$ of the massive vector propagator, responsible for its bad high-energy behaviour → [[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-8-3|Theorem §C4.8.3]]; and it is what fails in the massless limit → [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-1|Theorem §C4.5.1]].

*Uses:* [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-8|Theorem §C4.3.8]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.10: The Longitudinal Polarization Grows Like p/m
> The longitudinal vector of [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-4|Def. §C4.3.4]] is
>
> $$
> \varepsilon^\mu(p, 0) = \frac{p^\mu}{m} - \frac{m}{E_{\mathbf p} + |\mathbf p|}\,\bigl(1, -\hat{\mathbf p}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> so $\varepsilon^\mu(p, 0) = p^\mu/m + O(m/E_{\mathbf p})$ at high energy: its components grow like $E_{\mathbf p}/m$, it becomes parallel to $p$, and at fixed $\mathbf p$ it has no limit as $m \to 0$. The transverse vectors $\varepsilon(p, \pm)$ do not depend on $m$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.1 (start: "(4.79) is clearly not a good definition" for $m = 0$) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 (Note "Massive versus massless") · the expansion computed here*

^thm-c4-3-10

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Subtract.** $\varepsilon(p, 0) - p/m = \frac1m\bigl(|\mathbf p| - E_{\mathbf p},\ E_{\mathbf p}\hat{\mathbf p} - |\mathbf p|\hat{\mathbf p}\bigr) = \frac{E_{\mathbf p} - |\mathbf p|}{m}\,(-1, \hat{\mathbf p})$, using $\mathbf p = |\mathbf p|\hat{\mathbf p}$.
>
> **2. Rationalize.** $E_{\mathbf p} - |\mathbf p| = \frac{E_{\mathbf p}^2 - \mathbf p^2}{E_{\mathbf p} + |\mathbf p|} = \frac{m^2}{E_{\mathbf p} + |\mathbf p|}$. So $\varepsilon(p, 0) - p/m = -\frac{m}{E_{\mathbf p} + |\mathbf p|}(1, -\hat{\mathbf p})$.
>
> **3. Sizes.** $|\mathbf p| \le E_{\mathbf p}$ gives $\frac{m}{E_{\mathbf p} + |\mathbf p|} \le \frac{m}{E_{\mathbf p}}$, the remainder is $O(m/E_{\mathbf p})$, while $p^\mu/m$ has components of size $E_{\mathbf p}/m$. At fixed $\mathbf p \ne 0$ and $m \to 0$, $p^\mu/m$ diverges and the remainder vanishes. $\varepsilon(p, \pm)$ involve only $\mathbf p$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-4|Def. §C4.3.4]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ By-product: amplitudes with an external longitudinal vector boson contain $p^\mu/m$ and grow with energy unless $p^\mu$ is contracted into something that vanishes, such as a conserved current ($p_\mu J^\mu = 0$); this is the massive-vector version of the Ward identity → [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^rem-c4-5-1|§C4.5, Remark: The longitudinal mode decouples from conserved currents]], [[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-8-10|Theorem §C4.8.10]]; and the reason the massless limit of the Proca theory is singular → [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-1|Theorem §C4.5.1]].
> - The remainder $(1, -\hat{\mathbf p})$ is lightlike and points opposite to $\mathbf p$ in space: at high energy the longitudinal polarization is "$p/m$ plus a small backward null vector".

## The mode expansion

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.11: Mode Expansion of the Proca Field
> Every real solution of the Proca equation is
>
> $$
> A^\mu(x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_{\lambda = \pm, 0}\Bigl(\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda)\,a_{\mathbf p, \lambda}\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + \varepsilon^{\mu*}(p, \lambda)\,a^*_{\mathbf p, \lambda}\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigr)\Big|_{p^0 = E_{\mathbf p}} ,
> $$
>
> and its momentum $\pi^i = E^i = -\pi_i$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]]) is
>
> $$
> \pi^i(x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_\lambda\Bigl(i\,W^i(p, \lambda)\,a_{\mathbf p, \lambda}\,e^{-ip\cdot x} - i\,W^{i*}(p, \lambda)\,a^*_{\mathbf p, \lambda}\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigr), \qquad W^i \equiv E_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^i - p^i\varepsilon^0 .
> $$
>
> After quantization $a_{\mathbf p, \lambda}$ are operators, $a^*_{\mathbf p, \lambda} \to a^\dagger_{\mathbf p, \lambda}$, and $A^\mu$, $\pi^i$ are Hermitian. Yu's form of the momentum uses $\tilde\varepsilon_i \equiv \varepsilon_i - \frac{p_i}{p_0}\varepsilon_0 = -W^i/E_{\mathbf p}$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3.1, eqs. (4.109), (4.122)–(4.126) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3, eq. (massive-vector-expansion) and Note "Reducing H to mode form" ($W^i$)*

^thm-c4-3-11

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Component by component.** By [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-5|Theorem §C4.2.5]] each $A^\mu$ solves the Klein–Gordon equation. Steps 1–3 of [[§C2.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^der-c2-2-2|Derivation §C2.2.2]], applied to each component (with complex coefficients, reality not yet imposed), give
>
> $$
> A^\mu(x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\Bigl(\alpha^\mu(\mathbf p)\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + \beta^\mu(\mathbf p)\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigr), \qquad p^0 = E_{\mathbf p},
> $$
>
> with the substitution $\mathbf k = -\mathbf p$ in the second term (Jacobian $1$, $E_{-\mathbf p} = E_{\mathbf p}$; [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-7|Theorem §CA.3.7]], 1) already made, as there.
>
> **2. Reality.** $A^{\mu\ast} = A^\mu$ and the linear independence of $e^{-ip\cdot x}$ and $e^{+ip\cdot x}$ (uniqueness of the Fourier transform in $\mathcal S'$, [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-2|Theorem §CA.3.2]], 2, as in [[§C2.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^der-c2-2-2|Derivation §C2.2.2]], step 4) give $\beta^\mu(\mathbf p) = \alpha^\mu(\mathbf p)^{\ast}$.
>
> **3. The Lorenz condition.** Differentiate under the integral (for wave-packet coefficients; [[§CA.1 Exchanging Limits, Derivatives and Integrals#^thm-ca-1-1|Theorem §CA.1.1]]): $\partial_\mu A^\mu = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\bigl(-i\,p\cdot\alpha\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + i\,p\cdot\alpha^{\ast}\,e^{ip\cdot x}\bigr)$. It vanishes ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]) iff both coefficient functions vanish, by the same independence: $p\cdot\alpha(\mathbf p) = 0$ for every $\mathbf p$.
>
> **4. Expand in the polarization basis.** $\alpha(\mathbf p) \in p^\perp$, so $\alpha = P\alpha = -\sum_\lambda\varepsilon(p, \lambda)\bigl(\varepsilon^{\ast}(p, \lambda)\cdot\alpha\bigr)$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-9|Theorem §C4.3.9]]). Define $a_{\mathbf p, \lambda} \equiv -\varepsilon^{\ast}(p, \lambda)\cdot\alpha(\mathbf p)$; then $\alpha^\mu = \sum_\lambda\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda)a_{\mathbf p, \lambda}$ and $\alpha^{\mu\ast} = \sum_\lambda\varepsilon^{\mu\ast}(p, \lambda)a^{\ast}_{\mathbf p, \lambda}$: the first statement. Conversely every such sum is a real Proca solution by [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]] and superposition.
>
> **5. The momentum.** $\pi^i = E^i = -\partial_iA^0 - \partial_0A^i$. On the positive-frequency term, $\partial_\mu e^{-ip\cdot x} = -ip_\mu e^{-ip\cdot x}$ with $p_i = -p^i$, $p_0 = E_{\mathbf p}$: $-\partial_i(\varepsilon^0e^{-ip\cdot x}) - \partial_0(\varepsilon^ie^{-ip\cdot x}) = (ip_i\varepsilon^0 + ip_0\varepsilon^i)e^{-ip\cdot x} = i(E_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^i - p^i\varepsilon^0)e^{-ip\cdot x} = iW^ie^{-ip\cdot x}$. The negative-frequency term is its complex conjugate, $-iW^{i\ast}e^{ip\cdot x}$. Since $\varepsilon_i = -\varepsilon^i$, $p_i/p_0 = -p^i/E_{\mathbf p}$, $\varepsilon_0 = \varepsilon^0$: $\tilde\varepsilon_i = -\varepsilon^i + p^i\varepsilon^0/E_{\mathbf p} = -W^i/E_{\mathbf p}$, so Yu's $\pi_i = \frac{ip_0}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}(\tilde\varepsilon_ia\,e^{-ip\cdot x} - \dots)$ (4.125) is $-\pi^i$, as it must be.
>
> **6. Check: the constraint in modes.** Transversality $p_\mu\varepsilon^\mu = E_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^0 - \mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon = 0$ gives $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf W = E_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon) - \mathbf p^2\varepsilon^0 = (E_{\mathbf p}^2 - \mathbf p^2)\varepsilon^0 = m^2\varepsilon^0$. With $\partial_ie^{-ip\cdot x} = ip^ie^{-ip\cdot x}$, the positive-frequency part of $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi$ is $(ip^i)(iW^i) = -m^2\varepsilon^0$ times the mode, i.e. $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi = -m^2A^0$ mode by mode: the constraint of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-7|Theorem §C4.2.7]] holds identically. ⚑ By-product: the expansion has eliminated $A^0$ as an independent variable already: it is fixed by the same $a_{\mathbf p, \lambda}$ through $\varepsilon^0$ → [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Massive Vector Field#^thm-c4-4-3|Theorem §C4.4.3]], where it drops out of the extraction.
>
> **7. Quantize.** As in [[§C2.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^der-c2-2-2|Derivation §C2.2.2]], step 7: numbers become operators, $\ast$ becomes $\dagger$; each term of $A^\mu$ and of $\pi^i$ is the adjoint of the other, so both are Hermitian (Yu (4.45), (4.126)). ⚑ By-product: the operator coefficients make $A^\mu$ an operator-valued distribution → [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^rem-c4-3-1|Remark: In what sense the Proca field is a distribution]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The vector analogue of [[§C2.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]: one scalar expansion per polarization, the four components tied together by $\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda)$; the Lorenz condition is what reduces four coefficient functions to three.
> - The momentum is not $\dot A$: it contains $\nabla A^0$, which is why $W^i$ mixes $\varepsilon^i$ and $\varepsilon^0$.
> - Used next: extraction of $a_{\mathbf p, \lambda}$ and its algebra ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Massive Vector Field#^thm-c4-4-3|Theorem §C4.4.3]], [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Massive Vector Field#^thm-c4-4-4|Theorem §C4.4.4]]).

> [!remark] Remark: In what sense the Proca field is a distribution
> As for the scalar field ([[§C2.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2-2-7|Theorem §C2.2.7]]), $A^\mu(x)$ is an operator-valued distribution ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-8|Def. §CA.2.8]]): only smeared fields are operators. For real test functions $f_\mu \in \mathcal S(\mathbb R^3)$ at $t = 0$, doing the $\mathbf x$-integral first (Fubini, [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-6|Theorem §CA.3.6]]) with $\int d^3x\,f_\mu e^{\pm i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x} = \tilde f_\mu(\mp\mathbf p)$ and $\tilde f_\mu(-\mathbf p) = \overline{\tilde f_\mu(\mathbf p)}$ for real $f_\mu$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-4|Theorem §CA.3.4]], 4), as in [[§C2.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^der-c2-2-7|Derivation §C2.2.7]], steps 4–5,
>
> $$
> A(f) \equiv \int d^3x\,f_\mu(\mathbf x)A^\mu(0, \mathbf x) = \sum_\lambda\bigl(a_\lambda(g_\lambda) + a^\dagger_\lambda(g_\lambda)\bigr), \qquad g_\lambda(\mathbf p) = \frac{\varepsilon^{\mu*}(p, \lambda)\,\tilde f_\mu(\mathbf p)}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} ,
> $$
>
> with the smeared mode operators of [[§C2.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]], one per polarization. In the spin basis $g_j$ is a Schwartz function, because $\varepsilon(p, j)$ is smooth and grows at most linearly ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]]) and $m > 0$; in the helicity basis the individual $g_\pm$ are discontinuous on the $z$-axis, but their sum over $\lambda$ is the same operator ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^der-c4-3-8c|Derivation §C4.3.8, third route]] applied to $\sum_\lambda\varepsilon\,\varepsilon^*$). The longitudinal vector grows like $|\mathbf p|/m$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-10|Theorem §C4.3.10]]): the Proca field is one power of momentum more singular at short distances than a scalar field, which is where the $p^\mu p^\nu/m^2$ of its two-point function comes from ([[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-8-1|Theorem §C4.8.1]]).
>
> *Source: the scalar case, the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.8 (Caution "Two honesty points"); the vector case written here*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The polarization vectors are to the vector field what $u(p, s)$, $v(p, s)$ are to the Dirac field: the boosted rest-frame solutions, normalized covariantly, whose sums over the label give the propagator numerators — $\sum\varepsilon\varepsilon^* = -g + pp/m^2$ here, $\sum u\bar u = \slashed p + m$ there — [[§C5.5 Plane-Wave Solutions#^thm-c5-5-9|Theorem §C5.5.9]], [[§C5.6 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^thm-c5-6-7|Theorem §C5.6.7]], [[§C5.6 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^thm-c5-6-9|Theorem §C5.6.9]].
> - The helicity vectors $(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} \pm i\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi})/\sqrt2$ are the circular polarizations of a light wave travelling along $\hat{\mathbf p}$; the massive field adds the longitudinal one, which electromagnetic waves lack — [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-3|EM Theorem §C1.4.3]].
> - Spherical unit vectors $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$, $\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}$ cannot be defined continuously at the poles for the same reason a helicity basis cannot: there is no nowhere-vanishing tangent field on the sphere — [[§C5.6 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^cau-c5-6-2|§C5.6, Caution: No phase convention is smooth on the whole sphere]].
> - The polarization index carries the spin-1 representation of the little group through the Wigner rotation, exactly as the one-particle states do; the polarization vectors are the intertwiners between the field's $(\frac12, \frac12)$ and the particle's $D^{(1)}$ — [[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-8|Theorem §C3.5.8]], [[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^rem-c3-5-5|§C3.5★, Remark: What a field must contain]].
> - The tetrad completeness relation is the resolution of the identity for a Minkowski-orthonormal basis, with the metric signs as weights — the analogue of $\sum_n|n\rangle\langle n| = 1$ for an indefinite form; the Klein–Gordon product's indefiniteness plays the same role for mode functions — [[§C2.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^rem-c2-2-3|§C2.2, Remark: Why this inner product, and why it is indefinite]].
> - The massless polarization vectors and the failure of $\varepsilon(p, 0) \sim p/m$ as $m \to 0$ are taken up in [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-1|Theorem §C4.5.1]] and [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-3|Def. §C4.5.3]]; for a massless particle the little-group translations shift $\varepsilon$ by multiples of $k$ — [[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-6-8|Theorem §C3.6.8]].
