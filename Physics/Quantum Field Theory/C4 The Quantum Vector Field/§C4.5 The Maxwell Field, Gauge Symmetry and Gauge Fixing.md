What goes wrong when the mass of a vector field is set to zero, and what replaces the constraint that made the massive field quantizable? The massive field was built from the Proca Lagrangian, whose divergence equation forces $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$ and whose Gauss law fixes $A^0$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]], [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-7|Theorem §C4.2.7]]), with three polarization vectors and the polarization sum $-g^{\mu\nu} + p^\mu p^\nu/m^2$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-8|Theorem §C4.3.8]]). At $m = 0$ the longitudinal vector and the sum diverge, the Lagrangian becomes Maxwell's ([[§C1.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form|§C1.7]]), and gauge symmetry makes both the propagator equation and the canonical relations impossible. This section states the gauge symmetry, the gauge-fixed Lagrangian that restores a momentum to every component, the massless polarization basis, and what gauge symmetry and gauge fixing do to the field's spin; the quantization is [[§C4.6 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric|§C4.6]], the propagators [[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators|§C4.8]]. The classical theory of gauge conditions is at home in Electromagnetism ([[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation|EM §C1.3]], [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations|EM §C1.4]]); the reason a massless spin-1 particle needs it is [[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-6-8|Theorem §C3.6.8]].

For a massless momentum $p^0 = |\mathbf p| > 0$. Names of polarization vectors follow Yu (Caution below).

## Why the massive construction fails at m = 0

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.5.1: What Survives the Massless Limit
> Let $m \to 0$ at fixed $\mathbf p \ne 0$, so that $p^\mu = (E_{\mathbf p}, \mathbf p) \to p_0^\mu \equiv (|\mathbf p|, \mathbf p)$, a null vector.
> 1. The timelike vector $e(p, 0) = p/m$ of the massive tetrad ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-2|Def. §C4.3.2]]), the longitudinal vector $\varepsilon(p, 0)$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-10|Theorem §C4.3.10]]) and the polarization sum $-g^{\mu\nu} + p^\mu p^\nu/m^2$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-8|Theorem §C4.3.8]]) have no limit; their singular parts point along the null momentum: $m\,e(p, 0) \to p_0$, $m\,\varepsilon(p, 0) \to p_0$, $m^2\sum_\lambda\varepsilon^\mu\varepsilon^{\nu*} \to p_0^\mu p_0^\nu$.
> 2. The transverse vectors $e(p, 1)$, $e(p, 2)$ and $\varepsilon(p, \pm)$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-4|Def. §C4.3.4]]) do not depend on $m$, and at $m = 0$ they are still orthonormal and four-transverse, $p_0\cdot e(p, i) = 0$.
> 3. A massless basis must therefore keep the transverse pair and replace the timelike and longitudinal vectors ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-3|Def. §C4.5.3]]); the direction that blows up, $p_0$, lies in the transverse space of the null momentum ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]).
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.1, p. 138 ("(4.79) 式显然不是纵向极化矢量 e^μ(p,3) 的良好定义") · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4 ("$e^\mu(p,3) = (|\vec p|/m, \dots)$ and $e^\mu(p,0) = p^\mu/m$ are not [well defined]") · the limits written out here from [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-10|Theorem §C4.3.10]]*

^thm-c4-5-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The timelike vector.** $m\,e(p, 0) = p = (E_{\mathbf p}, \mathbf p)$ and $E_{\mathbf p} = \sqrt{\mathbf p^2 + m^2} \to |\mathbf p|$: the limit is $p_0$, nonzero, so $e(p, 0)$ grows like $|\mathbf p|/m$.
>
> **2. The longitudinal vector.** By [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-10|Theorem §C4.3.10]], $\varepsilon(p, 0) = p/m - \frac{m}{E_{\mathbf p} + |\mathbf p|}(1, -\hat{\mathbf p})$. Multiply by $m$: $m\,\varepsilon(p, 0) = p - \frac{m^2}{E_{\mathbf p} + |\mathbf p|}(1, -\hat{\mathbf p}) \to p_0 - 0$, since $\frac{m^2}{E_{\mathbf p} + |\mathbf p|} \le \frac{m^2}{2|\mathbf p|} \to 0$.
>
> **3. The sum.** $m^2\bigl(-g^{\mu\nu} + p^\mu p^\nu/m^2\bigr) = -m^2g^{\mu\nu} + p^\mu p^\nu \to p_0^\mu p_0^\nu \ne 0$.
>
> **4. The transverse pair.** Yu's $e(p, 1)$, $e(p, 2)$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-4|Def. §C4.3.4]]) are built from the components of $\mathbf p$ alone and have zero time component. Their orthonormality $\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf e_j = \delta_{ij}$ and $\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf e_i = 0$ do not involve $m$, so at $m = 0$, $p_0\cdot e(p, i) = |\mathbf p|\cdot0 - \mathbf p\cdot\mathbf e_i = 0$.
>
> ⚑ By-product: the singular direction $p_0$ is null and orthogonal to itself, the direction of $k^\perp$ that no covariant transversality condition can remove ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]); its couplings to conserved currents vanish → [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^rem-c4-5-1|Remark: The longitudinal mode decouples from conserved currents]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Nothing is wrong with the massive basis at any $m > 0$; the trouble is the limit, and it sits entirely in the direction of the momentum.
> - The two transverse vectors survive unchanged; the massless basis replaces only $e(p, 0)$ and $e(p, 3) = \varepsilon(p, 0)$.

> [!remark] Remark: The longitudinal mode decouples from conserved currents
> If a source is conserved, $\partial_\mu j^\mu = 0$, its transform obeys $p_\mu\tilde j^\mu(p) = 0$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-4|Theorem §CA.3.4]]: $\partial_\mu \to -ip_\mu$). By the decomposition of [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-10|Theorem §C4.3.10]] the longitudinal polarization then couples as $e(p, 3)\cdot\tilde j = -\frac{m}{E_{\mathbf p} + |\mathbf p|}\,\tilde n\cdot\tilde j = O(m/E_{\mathbf p})$: the divergent piece $p^\mu/m$ drops out, and the helicity-zero state of a fast or light vector is emitted with an amplitude suppressed by $m/E$. In the same way the term $p^\mu p^\nu/m^2$ of the polarization sum, and of the massive propagator, never reaches a conserved current ([[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-8-9|Theorem §C4.8.9]]). This is why the $m \to 0$ limit of a massive vector coupled to a conserved current is smooth although its polarization vectors are not, and it is the massive shadow of the Ward identity ([[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-8-10|Theorem §C4.8.10]]).
>
> *Source: derived here from [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-10|Theorem §C4.3.10]] · PS §5.5, eq. (5.79) (the Ward identity, for the massless case)*

^rem-c4-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Three polarizations become two
> A four-vector field carries $(\frac12, \frac12)$, spin $0 \oplus 1$ under rotations ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^rem-c4-1-2|§C4.1, Remark: Four components, three or two states]]; [[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-5|Theorem §C3.3.5]]). The massive field removes the spin-0 component by the constraint $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$ and keeps three states, the spin-1 triplet of the little group $SO(3)$ ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group|§C3.5★]]). A massless particle has the little group $ISO(2)$ and only helicities, here $\pm1$ ([[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-6-6|Theorem §C3.6.6]]): the count drops discontinuously from three to two. The vector field must therefore lose one more component at $m = 0$, and the mechanism is gauge symmetry ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^rem-c4-5-3|Remark: Why a massless vector field must be a gauge field]]); classically the count is $4 - 1 - 1 = 2$ of [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-3|EM Theorem §C1.4.3]].
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.2, p. 142 · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4 ("Gauge fixing and physical degrees of freedom") and "Where this leaves us"*

## The Maxwell field and its gauge symmetry

> [!model] Model §C4.5.2: The Free Maxwell Field
> A real vector field $A^\mu(x)$ with the Lagrangian density
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = -\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}, \qquad F^{\mu\nu} = \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu ,
> $$
>
> with $F$ the field tensor of [[§C1.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^def-c1-7-2|Def. §C1.7.2]]: the $m = 0$ case of the Proca model ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^mod-c4-2-1|Model §C4.2.1]]). Its Euler–Lagrange equation is the source-free Maxwell equation $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^rem-c1-9-5|§C1.9, Remark: One equation for every kind of field]]; [[§C1.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1-7-1|Theorem §C1.7.1]]). Its quanta are photons, massless spin-1 bosons.
>
> *Assumptions:* free (no source; a conserved source enters through $-J^\mu A_\mu$, Theorem §C1.7.1); flat spacetime; Heaviside–Lorentz units (the SI action is [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^pr-c1-1-4|EM Principle §C1.1.4]], converted by [[§C1.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1-7-4|Theorem §C1.7.4]]).
> *Source: Yu §4.4.2, eqs. (4.199)–(4.200) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4 · PS §9.4, eq. (9.51)*

^mod-c4-5-2

> [!definition] Definition §C4.5.1: Gauge Transformation
> For a real smooth function $\chi(x)$ on spacetime, the **gauge transformation** of the vector field is
>
> $$
> A'^\mu(x) = A^\mu(x) + \partial^\mu\chi(x) .
> $$
>
> It is **local**: the parameter $\chi$ may take a different value at every point. A Lagrangian density unchanged by every such transformation has **gauge symmetry**; a quantity unchanged by it is **gauge invariant**. (The SI version, with the distinction between global and local transformations, is [[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^def-c1-3-1|EM Def. §C1.3.1]].)
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.2, eq. (4.201) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4*

^def-c4-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.5.3: Gauge Invariance of F and of the Maxwell Lagrangian; the Mass Term Breaks It
> 1. Under [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-1|Def. §C4.5.1]], $F'^{\mu\nu} = F^{\mu\nu}$. Hence the Maxwell Lagrangian of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^mod-c4-5-2|Model §C4.5.2]], the Maxwell equation, and $\mathbf E$, $\mathbf B$ (the components of $F$, [[§C1.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^def-c1-7-2|Def. §C1.7.2]]) are gauge invariant.
> 2. The Proca mass term $\frac12m^2A_\mu A^\mu$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^mod-c4-2-1|Model §C4.2.1]]) is not: the Proca equation ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-3|Theorem §C4.2.3]]) changes by $m^2\partial^\nu\chi$. A massive vector field has no gauge symmetry.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.2, eq. (4.202) and p. 141 · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4*

^thm-c4-5-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The field tensor.** Substitute $A' = A + \partial\chi$:
>
> $$
> F'^{\mu\nu} = \partial^\mu(A^\nu + \partial^\nu\chi) - \partial^\nu(A^\mu + \partial^\mu\chi) = \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu + \partial^\mu\partial^\nu\chi - \partial^\nu\partial^\mu\chi = F^{\mu\nu} ,
> $$
>
> the last two terms cancelling because mixed partial derivatives of a smooth function commute ([[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]; for distributions they commute by definition, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|Def. §CA.2.4]]).
>
> **2. Everything built from $F$.** $\mathcal L = -\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$, $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}$, $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ are functions of $F$ alone, so they are unchanged.
>
> **3. The Proca equation.** It reads $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + m^2A^\nu = 0$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-3|Theorem §C4.2.3]]). After the transformation the first term is unchanged (step 1) and the second becomes $m^2A^\nu + m^2\partial^\nu\chi$: the equation acquires $m^2\partial^\nu\chi$, nonzero for every non-constant $\chi$. A change of $\mathcal L$ by a total derivative would leave the equations unchanged ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1-9-5|Theorem §C1.9.5]]); so the change of the mass term, $m^2A_\mu\partial^\mu\chi + \frac12m^2\partial_\mu\chi\,\partial^\mu\chi$ (expanding $\frac12m^2(A + \partial\chi)_\mu(A + \partial\chi)^\mu$ into its three terms), is not a total derivative.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Gauge invariance of the Maxwell theory is a property of $F$; a vector theory has it exactly when $A$ enters only through $F$ (and through couplings to conserved currents, [[§C1.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^rem-c1-7-2|§C1.7, Remark: Electrodynamics as the template for field theory]]).
> - "Massless" and "gauge invariant" are one statement for a free vector field.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.5.4: The Maxwell Kinetic Operator Is Singular
> 1. For fields that decay at infinity, the Maxwell action is
>
> $$
> S = \int d^4x\,\Bigl(-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\Bigr) = \frac12\int d^4x\,A_\mu\bigl(\partial^2g^{\mu\nu} - \partial^\mu\partial^\nu\bigr)A_\nu = \frac12\int\frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\,\tilde A_\mu(k)\,K^{\mu\nu}(k)\,\tilde A_\nu(-k), \qquad K^{\mu\nu}(k) = -k^2g^{\mu\nu} + k^\mu k^\nu ,
> $$
>
> and the field equation is $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \partial^2A^\nu - \partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu) = 0$.
> 2. $K^{\mu\nu}(k)\,k_\nu = 0$ for every $k$: the $4\times4$ matrix $K(k)$ is singular, and its kernel contains the pure-gauge configurations $\tilde A_\nu(k) = k_\nu\alpha(k)$ ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-1|Def. §C4.5.1]]).
> 3. Hence no $\tilde D^{\nu\rho}$ solves $\bigl(-k^2g_{\mu\nu} + k_\mu k_\nu\bigr)\tilde D^{\nu\rho}(k) = i\delta_\mu{}^\rho$, i.e. $(\partial^2g_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\mu\partial_\nu)D^{\nu\rho}(x - y) = i\delta_\mu{}^\rho\delta^4(x - y)$ has no solution: the Maxwell Lagrangian has no propagator.
>
> *Source: PS §9.4, eqs. (9.51)–(9.52) and p. 295 ("has no solution, since the 4 × 4 matrix $(-k^2g_{\mu\nu} + k_\mu k_\nu)$ is singular") · Yu §4.4.2, eq. (4.214) (the expansion of $F^2$)*

^thm-c4-5-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Expand $F^2$.** By step 1 of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^der-c4-2-2|Derivation §C4.2.2]] (all four terms of the product, two pairs equal after renaming the dummy indices), $-\frac14F^2 = -\frac12\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu + \frac12\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\nu A^\mu$ (Yu (4.214) at $1/\xi = 0$).
>
> **2. Integrate by parts.** By the product rule,
>
> $$
> -\tfrac12\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu = -\tfrac12\partial_\mu\bigl(A_\nu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu\bigr) + \tfrac12A_\nu\,\partial^2A^\nu, \qquad \tfrac12\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\nu A^\mu = \tfrac12\partial_\mu\bigl(A_\nu\,\partial^\nu A^\mu\bigr) - \tfrac12A_\nu\,\partial^\nu\partial_\mu A^\mu .
> $$
>
> Dropped: the two divergences, whose integrals are surface terms at infinity that vanish for decaying fields ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-8|Theorem §CA.3.8]]). What remains is $\frac12A_\nu\bigl(\partial^2A^\nu - \partial^\nu\partial_\mu A^\mu\bigr) = \frac12A_\mu\bigl(\partial^2g^{\mu\nu} - \partial^\mu\partial^\nu\bigr)A_\nu$ (renaming the free index and writing $A^\nu = g^{\nu\mu}A_\mu$).
>
> **3. The field equation.** $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial_\mu\partial^\nu A^\mu = \partial^2A^\nu - \partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu)$, the order of derivatives being immaterial; it is the variation of step 2 (the operator is symmetric under exchange of the two factors after integration by parts).
>
> **4. Fourier transform.** Insert $A_\mu(x) = \int\frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\tilde A_\mu(k)e^{-ik\cdot x}$ and, with a second variable, $A_\nu(x) = \int\frac{d^4k'}{(2\pi)^4}\tilde A_\nu(k')e^{-ik'\cdot x}$. Acting on $e^{-ik'\cdot x}$, $\partial^\mu \to -ik'^\mu$, so $(\partial^2g^{\mu\nu} - \partial^\mu\partial^\nu)e^{-ik'\cdot x} = (-k'^2g^{\mu\nu} + k'^\mu k'^\nu)e^{-ik'\cdot x}$. Then
>
> $$
> S = \frac12\int\frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{d^4k'}{(2\pi)^4}\,\tilde A_\mu(k)K^{\mu\nu}(k')\tilde A_\nu(k')\int d^4x\,e^{-i(k + k')\cdot x}, \qquad \int d^4x\,e^{-i(k + k')\cdot x} = (2\pi)^4\delta^4(k + k') ,
> $$
>
> the plane-wave delta, an identity in $\mathcal S'$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-3|Theorem §CA.3.3]]), with the $x$-integral moved inside by [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-6|Theorem §CA.3.6]] (exact for wave packets). Integrating over $k'$ eliminates it, $k' = -k$, and $K(-k) = K(k)$ (both terms are even): the third form of the action.
>
> **5. The null vector.** $K^{\mu\nu}(k)k_\nu = -k^2k^\mu + k^\mu(k^\nu k_\nu) = -k^2k^\mu + k^2k^\mu = 0$. So for every $k \ne 0$ the matrix $K(k)$ has the nonzero null vector $k$ and determinant zero. A configuration $\tilde A_\nu(k) = k_\nu\alpha(k)$ is $A_\nu(x) = \int\frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}k_\nu\alpha(k)e^{-ik\cdot x} = \partial_\nu\chi(x)$ with $\chi = i\int\frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\alpha(k)e^{-ik\cdot x}$, a pure gauge transformation of $A = 0$; for it the action vanishes ⚑ By-product: the action does not see pure-gauge directions, so a functional integral over $A$ diverges along them (PS p. 294) → [[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators#^rem-c4-8-3|§C4.8, Remark: Why the ξ-term is allowed: Faddeev–Popov]].
>
> **6. No inverse.** Suppose $(-k^2g_{\mu\nu} + k_\mu k_\nu)\tilde D^{\nu\rho} = i\delta_\mu{}^\rho$. Contract with $k^\mu$: the left side is $(-k^2k_\nu + k^2k_\nu)\tilde D^{\nu\rho} = 0$, the right side $ik^\rho$, nonzero for $k \ne 0$: a contradiction. The position-space equation transforms into this one ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-5|Theorem §CA.3.5]]: $\partial \to -ik$, $\delta^4 \to 1$), so it has no solution either.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The singularity of $K$ is gauge invariance written in momentum space; it is not a failure of a particular boundary condition, so no contour or $i\varepsilon$ can repair it.
> - The massive kinetic operator $(-k^2 + m^2)g^{\mu\nu} + k^\mu k^\nu$ has no null vector for $k^2 \ne m^2$ and is inverted in [[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-8-4|Theorem §C4.8.4]]; the gauge-fixed one in [[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-8-8|Theorem §C4.8.8]].

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.5.5: No Canonical Momentum for A⁰
> For the Maxwell Lagrangian ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^mod-c4-5-2|Model §C4.5.2]]) the momentum densities are those of the Proca field at $m = 0$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]]): $\pi^0 = \pi_0 = 0$, and $\pi_i = -\pi^i = -E^i$ conjugate to $A^i$. Therefore:
> 1. the equal-time relation $[A^0(\mathbf x), \pi_0(\mathbf y)] = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$ of [[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]], applied to each component, cannot hold;
> 2. the $\nu = 0$ Maxwell equation is Gauss's law $\partial_i\pi^i = \nabla\cdot\mathbf E = 0$, a condition on the momenta that contains no $A^0$; unlike the Proca case, where it reads $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = -m^2A^0$ and fixes $A^0$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-7|Theorem §C4.2.7]]), nothing at one time determines $A^0$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.2, pp. 142–143, eq. (4.210) and the remark after it · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4 ("For $m = 0$ the relation $A^0 = -\frac{1}{m^2}\nabla\cdot\vec\pi$ fails") · [[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^rem-c1-10-3|§C1.10, Remark: Constraints and first-order Lagrangians]]*

^thm-c4-5-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The momenta do not depend on $m$.** The mass term $\frac12m^2A_\mu A^\mu$ contains no derivatives, so $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu A_\nu) = -F^{\mu\nu}$ for every $m$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-2|Theorem §C4.2.2]]), and the momenta $\pi^\nu = -F^{0\nu}$ of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]] hold at $m = 0$. In particular $\pi^0 = -F^{00} = 0$ by antisymmetry. Lowering the index, $\pi_\mu = \partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_0A^\mu) = g_{\mu\nu}\pi^\nu = -F^0{}_\mu = -F_{0\mu}$ (lowering $0$ costs $g_{00} = 1$); this is the index placement used for the gauge-fixed momenta of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-9|Theorem §C4.5.9]].
>
> **2. The commutator.** The relation would require $i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y) = [A^0(\mathbf x), 0] = 0$, false (smeared with test functions $f$, $g$ the left side is $i\int fg \ne 0$, [[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^thm-c2-1-2|Theorem §C2.1.2]]). ⚑ By-product: $A^0$ has no canonical partner; the Legendre map from velocities to momenta is not invertible, since $\partial_0A^0$ cannot be recovered from $\pi$ → [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-9|Theorem §C4.5.9]], where the gauge-fixing term restores it.
>
> **3. Gauss's law.** The $\nu = 0$ component of $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0$ is $\partial_0F^{00} + \partial_iF^{i0} = \partial_iF^{i0} = \partial_i\pi^i$. No time derivative and no $A^0$ appears. In the Proca equation ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-3|Theorem §C4.2.3]]) the same component is $\partial_iF^{i0} + m^2A^0 = 0$, which solves for $A^0$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-7|Theorem §C4.2.7]]); at $m = 0$ the term that did so is gone.
>
> ⚑ By-product: what is not determined is exactly what a gauge transformation changes freely: $A^0 \to A^0 + \partial_0\chi$ with $\partial_0\chi$ arbitrary at each time → [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-1|Def. §C4.5.1]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Canonical quantization of all four components fails at its first step, the commutation relations, before any question of states arises.
> - The Proca field avoided the failure by eliminating $A^0$ through the constraint, a second-class pair that is solved; the Maxwell constraint is first class, generates gauge transformations, and must be fixed or circumvented ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^rem-c4-2-4|§C4.2★, ★ Remark: Second-class constraints, and why Maxwell differs]]; [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^rem-c1-4-2|EM ★ Remark: Dirac's constraints, and the Coulomb gauge as the true variables]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Why a massless vector field must be a gauge field
> A massless spin-1 particle has two helicity states, $\lambda = \pm1$, and its polarization vectors transform under Lorentz transformations as four-vectors only up to a multiple of the momentum ([[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-6-8|Theorem §C3.6.8]]). A four-component field built from two states therefore changes by $\partial^\mu\Omega$ under a Lorentz transformation, i.e. by a gauge transformation, and its theory is consistent only if such changes are unobservable ([[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^rem-c3-6-3|§C3.6★, Remark: Two polarizations, and why a massless vector field needs gauge invariance]]). Conversely, gauge symmetry reduces the four components to two: the Lorenz condition removes one, the residual freedom $\partial^2\tilde\chi = 0$ another (Yu p. 142; [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-3|EM Theorem §C1.4.3]]). The gauge-invariant Lagrangian $-\frac14F^2$ is the one that does this; that no non-gauge-invariant local vector Lagrangian does is stated, not proved, in the user's pre-course notes (with a reference to Weinberg, *The Quantum Theory of Fields* I, §5.9). The redundancy is not a physical symmetry: it relates descriptions, not states, which is why it is fixed rather than conserved ([[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^rem-c1-3-1|EM §C1.3, Remark: What gauge symmetry is, and is not]]).
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.2, p. 142 · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4 ("Gauge fixing and physical degrees of freedom") and "Where this leaves us"*

## Gauge conditions and the gauge-fixed Lagrangian

The classical theory of gauge conditions is [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations|EM §C1.4]]: gauge orbits, attainable conditions and residual transformations ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^def-c1-4-1|EM Def. §C1.4.1]]); the Lorenz gauge $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$, attainable by solving $\partial^2\chi = -\partial_\mu A^\mu$ and preserved by $\partial^2\chi = 0$ ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-1|EM Theorem §C1.4.1]], Yu (4.207)–(4.209)); the count of two polarizations per plane wave ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-3|EM Theorem §C1.4.3]]) and of two oscillators per wave vector in the Coulomb gauge ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-4|EM Theorem §C1.4.4]]). Yu lists the Lorenz ($\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$), Coulomb ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 0$), temporal ($A^0 = 0$) and axial ($A^3 = 0$) conditions, eqs. (4.203)–(4.206); only the Lorenz condition is Lorentz invariant. What the quantum theory needs beyond these is a Lagrangian in which every component has a momentum.

> [!remark] Remark: The Lorenz condition: derived for Proca, chosen for Maxwell
> The same equation $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$ plays two different roles. For the massive field it is a consequence of the field equation, a constraint that every solution obeys ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]); for the massless field it is a choice of representative in each gauge orbit, which the field equation neither implies nor forbids. In the quantum theory this difference becomes decisive: the massive constraint holds as an operator identity, the massless choice cannot ([[§C4.6 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-6-5|Theorem §C4.6.5]]).
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.2, p. 142 ("在研究有质量矢量场时它是从运动方程推导出来的必须满足的约束条件，而在研究无质量矢量场时它只是一种人为选择")*

^rem-c4-5-4

> [!definition] Definition §C4.5.2: Gauge-Fixed Lagrangian; Feynman and Landau Gauges
> For a real constant $\xi \ne 0$, the **gauge-fixed** Lagrangian of the vector field is
>
> $$
> \mathcal L_\xi = -\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2\xi}\bigl(\partial_\mu A^\mu\bigr)^2 ,
> $$
>
> the Maxwell Lagrangian ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^mod-c4-5-2|Model §C4.5.2]]) plus the **gauge-fixing term**, which is not gauge invariant ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-1|Def. §C4.5.1]]). The choice $\xi = 1$ is the **Feynman gauge**; the limit $\xi \to 0$ (taken in the propagator, [[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-8-8|Theorem §C4.8.8]]) is the **Landau gauge**. The parameter $\xi$ is the **gauge parameter**.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.2, eqs. (4.211), (4.223) · PS §9.4, eqs. (9.56)–(9.57) and p. 297 · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4*

The Electromagnetism note's Fermi example (after Zangwill) writes $-\frac{\xi}{2\mu_0}(\partial_\mu A^\mu)^2$ ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^ex-c1-4-2|EM Example §C1.4.2]]): its $\xi$ is $1/\xi$ here. The Feynman gauge is $\xi = 1$ in both; the Landau gauge $\xi \to 0$ here is $\xi \to \infty$ there.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.5.6: Field Equation of the Gauge-Fixed Lagrangian
> The Euler–Lagrange equation of $\mathcal L_\xi$ ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-2|Def. §C4.5.2]]) is
>
> $$
> \partial^2A^\mu - \Bigl(1 - \frac1\xi\Bigr)\partial^\mu\partial_\nu A^\nu = 0 .
> $$
>
> In the Feynman gauge it is the d'Alembert equation $\partial^2A^\mu = 0$: each component obeys the massless Klein–Gordon equation ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^mod-b4-1-3|REL Model §B4.1.3]] at $m = 0$).
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.2, eqs. (4.219)–(4.222) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4 · PS §4.8, eq. (4.130)*

^thm-c4-5-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The divergence as a function of $\partial_\mu A_\nu$.** $\partial_\rho A^\rho = g^{\rho\sigma}\partial_\rho A_\sigma$, so $\partial(\partial_\rho A^\rho)/\partial(\partial_\mu A_\nu) = g^{\mu\nu}$.
>
> **2. Derivative of $\mathcal L_\xi$.** With [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-2|Theorem §C4.2.2]] at $m = 0$ for the Maxwell part and the chain rule $\partial(\partial\cdot A)^2/\partial(\partial_\mu A_\nu) = 2(\partial\cdot A)\,g^{\mu\nu}$ for the new term,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\mathcal L_\xi}{\partial(\partial_\mu A_\nu)} = -F^{\mu\nu} - \frac1\xi\,g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\rho A^\rho = -\partial^\mu A^\nu + \partial^\nu A^\mu - \frac1\xi\,g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\rho A^\rho, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L_\xi}{\partial A_\nu} = 0 .
> $$
>
> **3. Euler–Lagrange** ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1-9-4|Theorem §C1.9.4]]). $\partial_\mu$ of step 2:
>
> $$
> 0 = -\partial^2A^\nu + \partial^\nu\partial_\mu A^\mu - \frac1\xi\,\partial^\nu\partial_\rho A^\rho = -\partial^2A^\nu + \Bigl(1 - \frac1\xi\Bigr)\partial^\nu\partial_\rho A^\rho ,
> $$
>
> using $g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu = \partial^\nu$ and the commuting of $\partial_\mu$ with $\partial^\nu$. Multiplying by $-1$ and renaming $\nu \to \mu$ gives the statement.
>
> **4. Feynman gauge.** At $\xi = 1$ the coefficient $1 - 1/\xi$ vanishes.
>
> ⚑ By-product: in momentum space the operator is $K_\xi^{\mu\nu}(k) = -k^2g^{\mu\nu} + (1 - \frac1\xi)k^\mu k^\nu$, and $K_\xi^{\mu\nu}k_\nu = -\frac{k^2}{\xi}k^\mu \ne 0$ for $k^2 \ne 0$: the gauge-fixing term has removed the null vector of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-4|Theorem §C4.5.4]] → [[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-8-8|Theorem §C4.8.8]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - For $\xi \ne 1$ the components stay coupled through $\partial^\mu(\partial\cdot A)$; only the Feynman gauge decouples them into four massless Klein–Gordon fields, which is why the covariant quantization is done there.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.5.7: Equivalence with Maxwell's Equations on the Lorenz Subspace
> Let $\xi \ne 0$ be finite and $\Lambda \equiv \partial_\mu A^\mu$.
> 1. If $\Lambda = 0$, then $\mathcal L_\xi = \mathcal L$ and the equation of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-6|Theorem §C4.5.6]] is the Maxwell equation $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0$.
> 2. Every solution of the equation of Theorem §C4.5.6 has $\partial^2\Lambda = 0$: $\Lambda$ is a free massless field.
> 3. If $\Lambda$ and $\partial_0\Lambda$ vanish at one time (and $\Lambda$ decays at spatial infinity), then $\Lambda \equiv 0$ and $A$ solves Maxwell's equation in the Lorenz gauge; every Maxwell solution is gauge equivalent to such an $A$ ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-1|EM Theorem §C1.4.1]], 2).
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.2, p. 143 ("在 A^μ(x) 满足 Lorenz 规范条件 ∂_μA^μ = 0 的情况下，由 (4.211) 式定义的 ℒ₁ 等价于由 (4.199) 式定义的 ℒ") · EM Example §C1.4.2 (the classical version, Zangwill Problem 24.14)*

^thm-c4-5-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Item 1.** Put $\Lambda = 0$ into $\mathcal L_\xi$: the gauge-fixing term is $-\frac{1}{2\xi}\cdot0$. Put it into the equation: $\partial^2A^\mu - (1 - \frac1\xi)\partial^\mu\Lambda = \partial^2A^\mu = \partial^2A^\mu - \partial^\mu\Lambda = \partial_\nu F^{\nu\mu}$ (step 3 of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^der-c4-5-4|Derivation §C4.5.4]]).
>
> **2. Item 2.** Apply $\partial_\mu$ to the equation: $\partial_\mu\partial^2A^\mu - (1 - \frac1\xi)\partial_\mu\partial^\mu\Lambda = \partial^2\Lambda - \partial^2\Lambda + \frac1\xi\partial^2\Lambda = \frac1\xi\partial^2\Lambda = 0$. Since $\xi$ is finite, $\partial^2\Lambda = 0$.
>
> **3. Item 3.** A solution of $\partial^2\Lambda = 0$ with vanishing Cauchy data and decay at infinity vanishes identically: the energy argument of step 6 of [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^der-c1-4-1|EM Derivation §C1.4.1]] (in natural units). Then item 1 applies. The last sentence is the attainability of the Lorenz gauge, [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-1|EM Theorem §C1.4.1]], 2, followed by the restriction of the residual freedom $\partial^2\chi = 0$ to make $\partial_0\Lambda = 0$ as well.
>
> ⚑ By-product: the gauge-fixing term trades the gauge freedom for an initial condition on the free field $\Lambda$. In the quantum theory $\Lambda$ is an operator whose commutator with $A^0$ is a nonzero c-number, so the initial condition cannot be imposed on operators ([[§C4.6 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-6-5|Theorem §C4.6.5]]); it is imposed on states instead ([[§C4.7 The Gupta–Bleuler Condition and Physical Photons#^pr-c4-7-1|Principle §C4.7.1]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The gauge-fixed theory contains the Maxwell theory as the subspace $\Lambda = 0$, which the dynamics preserves; outside it, $\Lambda$ is an extra free massless field that must be removed by hand.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.5.8: The Feynman-Gauge Lagrangian up to a Total Derivative
> At $\xi = 1$,
>
> $$
> \mathcal L_1 = -\frac12\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu + \partial_\mu K^\mu, \qquad K^\mu = \frac12\bigl(A_\nu\,\partial^\nu A^\mu - A^\mu\,\partial_\nu A^\nu\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> so $\mathcal L_2 \equiv -\frac12\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu$ has the same field equation $\partial^2A^\mu = 0$ ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1-9-5|Theorem §C1.9.5]]). The time component $K^0 = \frac12\bigl(A_i\,\partial^iA^0 - A^0\,\partial_iA^i\bigr)$ contains no time derivatives.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.2, eqs. (4.224)–(4.225) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4 ("$\mathcal L_1$ then differs from $\mathcal L_2 = -\frac12(\partial_\mu A_\nu)\partial^\mu A^\nu$ by a total derivative") · the statement about $K^0$ added here*

^thm-c4-5-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Write out $\mathcal L_1$.** By step 1 of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^der-c4-5-4|Derivation §C4.5.4]], relabelling $\mu \leftrightarrow \nu$ in its second term, and writing the gauge-fixing term with the index on the first factor raised,
>
> $$
> \mathcal L_1 = -\frac12\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu + \frac12\partial_\nu A_\mu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu - \frac12\bigl(\partial^\mu A_\mu\bigr)\bigl(\partial_\nu A^\nu\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **2. Second term by the product rule.** $\frac12\partial_\nu A_\mu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu = \frac12\partial_\nu\bigl(A_\mu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu\bigr) - \frac12A_\mu\,\partial_\nu\partial^\mu A^\nu$.
>
> **3. Third term by the product rule.** $-\frac12(\partial^\mu A_\mu)(\partial_\nu A^\nu) = -\frac12\partial^\mu\bigl(A_\mu\,\partial_\nu A^\nu\bigr) + \frac12A_\mu\,\partial^\mu\partial_\nu A^\nu$.
>
> **4. The remainders cancel.** $-\frac12A_\mu\,\partial_\nu\partial^\mu A^\nu + \frac12A_\mu\,\partial^\mu\partial_\nu A^\nu = 0$, since the derivatives commute.
>
> **5. Collect the divergences.** In step 2 relabel $\mu \leftrightarrow \nu$: $\frac12\partial_\mu(A_\nu\,\partial^\nu A^\mu)$. In step 3 move the index of $\partial^\mu$ down and that of $A_\mu$ up: $-\frac12\partial_\mu(A^\mu\,\partial_\nu A^\nu)$. Their sum is $\partial_\mu K^\mu$ with the stated $K^\mu$.
>
> **6. No velocities in $K^0$.** $K^0 = \frac12(A_\nu\,\partial^\nu A^0 - A^0\,\partial_\nu A^\nu)$. The $\nu = 0$ terms are $A_0\,\partial^0A^0 - A^0\,\partial_0A^0 = 0$, because $A_0 = A^0$ and $\partial^0 = \partial_0$. What is left has only spatial derivatives.
>
> **7. Same equations.** A total divergence does not change the Euler–Lagrange equations ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1-9-5|Theorem §C1.9.5]]); the equation of $\mathcal L_1$ is that of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-6|Theorem §C4.5.6]] at $\xi = 1$.
>
> ⚑ By-product: the divergence $\partial_\mu K^\mu$ contains $\partial_0K^0$, so the two Lagrangians give different canonical momenta (difference in [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-9|Theorem §C4.5.9]]); because $K^0$ has no velocities the difference is a canonical transformation, and the commutation relations agree → [[§C4.6 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-6-4|Theorem §C4.6.4]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - In the Feynman gauge the vector field is, up to a boundary term, four copies of the massless scalar Lagrangian $\frac12\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi$, three with the right sign ($-\frac12\partial A_i\,\partial A_i = +\frac12\partial A^i\,\partial A^i$) and the timelike one with the wrong sign ($-\frac12\partial A_0\,\partial A^0$): the origin of the negative norms of [[§C4.6 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-6-14|Theorem §C4.6.14]].

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.5.9: Canonical Momenta of the Gauge-Fixed Field
> 1. For $\mathcal L_\xi$ ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-2|Def. §C4.5.2]]) the momentum conjugate to $A^\mu$ is
>
> $$
> \pi_\mu = \frac{\partial\mathcal L_\xi}{\partial(\partial_0A^\mu)} = -F_{0\mu} - \frac1\xi\,g_{0\mu}\,\partial_\nu A^\nu: \qquad \pi_0 = -\frac1\xi\,\partial_\mu A^\mu, \qquad \pi_i = -\partial_0A_i + \partial_iA_0 .
> $$
>
> 2. For $\mathcal L_2 = -\frac12\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu$ ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-8|Theorem §C4.5.8]]) it is $\pi_\mu = -\partial_0A_\mu$.
> 3. In both cases every velocity $\partial_0A^\mu$ is a function of the momenta and of $A$ with its spatial derivatives: all four components are canonical variables. At $\xi = 1$ the two momenta differ by $\pi^{(1)}_0 - \pi^{(2)}_0 = -\partial_iA^i$, $\pi^{(1)}_i - \pi^{(2)}_i = \partial_iA^0$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.2, eqs. (4.215)–(4.216), (4.226) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4 (with $\pi^\mu$ upper: $\pi^0 = -\frac1\xi\partial_\mu A^\mu$) · item 3 written out here*

^thm-c4-5-9

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. $\mathcal L_\xi$.** By step 2 of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^der-c4-5-6|Derivation §C4.5.6]] at $\mu = 0$, $\partial\mathcal L_\xi/\partial(\partial_0A_\beta) = -F^{0\beta} - \frac1\xi g^{0\beta}\partial\cdot A$. Lowering as in step 1 of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^der-c4-5-5|Derivation §C4.5.5]] (the chain rule $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_0A^\mu) = g_{\mu\beta}\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_0A_\beta)$): $\pi_\mu = g_{\mu\beta}\bigl(-F^{0\beta} - \frac1\xi g^{0\beta}\partial\cdot A\bigr) = -F_{0\mu} - \frac1\xi\,\delta^0{}_\mu\,\partial\cdot A$, and $\delta^0{}_\mu = g_{0\mu}$ in this metric.
>
> **2. Components.** $\mu = 0$: $F_{00} = 0$, so $\pi_0 = -\frac1\xi\partial\cdot A$. $\mu = i$: $g_{0i} = 0$ and $F_{0i} = \partial_0A_i - \partial_iA_0$.
>
> **3. $\mathcal L_2$.** $\partial\mathcal L_2/\partial(\partial_\alpha A_\beta) = -\partial^\alpha A^\beta$ (one quadratic term, factor 2 times $\frac12$). At $\alpha = 0$ and lowered: $\pi_\mu = -g_{\mu\beta}\,\partial^0A^\beta = -\partial_0A_\mu$.
>
> **4. Velocities.** For $\mathcal L_2$: $\partial_0A_\mu = -\pi_\mu$. For $\mathcal L_\xi$: $\partial_0A_i = -\pi_i + \partial_iA_0$, and $\pi_0 = -\frac1\xi(\partial_0A^0 + \partial_iA^i)$ gives $\partial_0A^0 = -\xi\pi_0 - \partial_iA^i$. ⚑ By-product: the Legendre map is invertible for every finite $\xi$, unlike the Maxwell case ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-5|Theorem §C4.5.5]]); as $\xi \to \infty$ the term vanishes and $\pi_0 \to 0$ again.
>
> **5. The difference at $\xi = 1$.** $\pi^{(1)}_\mu - \pi^{(2)}_\mu = -\partial_0A_\mu + \partial_\mu A_0 - g_{0\mu}\partial\cdot A + \partial_0A_\mu = \partial_\mu A_0 - g_{0\mu}\partial_\nu A^\nu$. For $\mu = 0$: $\partial_0A_0 - \partial_0A^0 - \partial_iA^i = -\partial_iA^i$. For $\mu = i$: $\partial_iA_0 = \partial_iA^0$. No time derivative remains.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The gauge-fixing term gives $A^0$ a partner proportional to the Lorenz expression itself; the operator that should vanish is a momentum, which is why it cannot vanish ([[§C4.6 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-6-5|Theorem §C4.6.5]]).
> - The two Feynman-gauge momenta differ by functions of the coordinates alone, the mark of a canonical transformation ([[§C4.6 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-6-4|Theorem §C4.6.4]]).

## Polarization vectors of the massless field

> [!definition] Definition §C4.5.3: Massless Polarization Basis
> Let $p^\mu = (|\mathbf p|, \mathbf p)$, $\mathbf p \ne 0$, and fix an inertial frame with timelike unit vector $n^\mu = (1, 0, 0, 0)$, $n^2 = 1$. The **massless polarization basis** is
>
> $$
> e^\mu(p, 0) = n^\mu, \qquad e^\mu(p, 3) = \frac{p^\mu - (p\cdot n)\,n^\mu}{p\cdot n} = \bigl(0, \hat{\mathbf p}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> together with the transverse vectors $e^\mu(p, 1)$, $e^\mu(p, 2)$ of the massive basis ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-4|Def. §C4.3.4]]), which do not involve $m$, and the helicity vectors $\varepsilon^\mu(p, \pm) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[e^\mu(p, 1) \pm ie^\mu(p, 2)\bigr]$ as in the massive case ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-4|Def. §C4.3.4]]). In another frame $e(p, 0)$ and $e(p, 3)$ are obtained by the Lorentz transformation; they depend on the choice of $n$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.1, eqs. (4.184)–(4.188), (4.196) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4 ("Polarization vectors and gauge symmetry")*

^def-c4-5-3

> [!caution] Caution: The transverse basis along the z-axis, and the phase of ε(p, ±)
> The transverse vectors are the massive ones and inherit their conventions: undefined on the $z$-axis, no phase choice continuous on the whole sphere, no Condon–Shortley sign ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^cau-c4-3-1|§C4.3★, Caution: Phase conventions and the z-axis]]). For a photon along $\hat{\mathbf z}$, PS take $e(k, 1) = (0, 1, 0, 0)$, $e(k, 2) = (0, 0, 1, 0)$ (PS p. 160) and $\varepsilon(k, \pm) = (0, 1, \pm i, 0)/\sqrt2$ (PS p. 124), the vectors of [[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-6-8|Theorem §C3.6.8]]. Phases of $\varepsilon$ change phases of amplitudes, never $|\mathcal M|^2$ or polarization sums.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3.1, eqs. (4.69)–(4.70) · PS §4.8, p. 124; §5.5, p. 160*

^cau-c4-5-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.5.10: Only Two Massless Polarizations Can Be Transverse
> Let $p^\mu = (|\mathbf p|, \mathbf p)$, $\mathbf p \ne 0$.
> 1. No vector $e$ with $e\cdot e = -1$ and spatial part parallel to $\mathbf p$ satisfies $p\cdot e = 0$.
> 2. No vector $e$ with $e\cdot e = +1$ satisfies $p\cdot e = 0$.
> 3. $p\cdot p = 0$, so the massive choice $e(p, 0) = p/m$ has no normalizable massless analogue.
> 4. For the basis of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-3|Def. §C4.5.3]]: $p\cdot e(p, 1) = p\cdot e(p, 2) = 0$, $p\cdot e(p, 0) = p\cdot n = |\mathbf p| > 0$, $p\cdot e(p, 3) = -|\mathbf p| < 0$, and $e^\mu(p, 0) + e^\mu(p, 3) = p^\mu/|\mathbf p|$.
>
> So only the two transverse vectors are four-transverse, $p_\mu e^\mu = 0$, the condition the Lorenz gauge imposes on a plane wave ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-3|EM Theorem §C1.4.3]], 2); the sum of the timelike and longitudinal vectors points along the momentum, the pure-gauge direction.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.1, p. 139, eq. (4.193), and Exercise 4.7 (items 1–2, solved here from [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4 · item 4's last formula added here*

^thm-c4-5-10

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The transverse space of a null vector.** By [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]], the vectors $v$ with $p\cdot v = 0$ form a three-dimensional space containing $p$ itself, on which $-v^*\cdot v \ge 0$ with equality only on multiples of $p$.
>
> **2. Item 2.** A vector with $e\cdot e = +1$ would have $-e\cdot e = -1 < 0$: it cannot lie in that space.
>
> **3. Item 1.** A vector $e = (e^0, c\,\hat{\mathbf p})$ with $p\cdot e = |\mathbf p|(e^0 - c) = 0$ has $e^0 = c$, so $e = (c/|\mathbf p|)\,p$, a multiple of $p$, with $e\cdot e = 0 \ne -1$ (Yu's Exercise 4.7(a); (b) is item 2).
>
> **4. Item 3.** $p\cdot p = |\mathbf p|^2 - |\mathbf p|^2 = 0$; no multiple of $p$ has norm $\pm1$.
>
> **5. Item 4.** $e(p, 1)$, $e(p, 2)$ have zero time component and spatial parts perpendicular to $\mathbf p$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-4|Def. §C4.3.4]]), so $p\cdot e(p, i) = -\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf e(p, i) = 0$. $p\cdot n = p^0 = |\mathbf p|$. $p\cdot e(p, 3) = \bigl(p\cdot p - (p\cdot n)^2\bigr)/(p\cdot n) = (0 - |\mathbf p|^2)/|\mathbf p| = -|\mathbf p|$. Finally $n^\mu + (0, \hat{\mathbf p}) = (1, \hat{\mathbf p}) = p^\mu/|\mathbf p|$.
>
> ⚑ By-product: $e(p, 0) + e(p, 3) \propto p$ is the polarization of a pure-gauge plane wave ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-3|EM Theorem §C1.4.3]], 3); the operator that removes it in the quantum theory is $b_{\mathbf p,0} - b_{\mathbf p,3}$ → [[§C4.7 The Gupta–Bleuler Condition and Physical Photons#^thm-c4-7-3|Theorem §C4.7.3]], [[§C4.7 The Gupta–Bleuler Condition and Physical Photons#^thm-c4-7-6|Theorem §C4.7.6]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - A complete basis of four vectors must contain non-transverse members; covariance forces the extra two on the theory, and they are removed by a condition on states, not on the basis.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.5.11: Orthonormality and Completeness of the Massless Basis
> The basis of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-3|Def. §C4.5.3]] obeys the same relations as the massive basis ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]]):
>
> $$
> e_\mu(p, \sigma)\,e^\mu(p, \sigma') = g_{\sigma\sigma'}, \qquad \sum_{\sigma = 0}^3g_{\sigma\sigma}\,e_\mu(p, \sigma)\,e_\nu(p, \sigma) = g_{\mu\nu} ,
> $$
>
> the completeness of a $g$-orthonormal tetrad ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]]) holding without the massive condition $e(p, 0) = p/m$. As for the massive field, $(\mathbf e(p, 1), \mathbf e(p, 2), \hat{\mathbf p})$ is a right-handed orthonormal triad ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]]).
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.1, eqs. (4.189)–(4.192)*

^thm-c4-5-11

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Orthonormality, entry by entry.** $e(0)\cdot e(0) = n^2 = 1$; $e(3)\cdot e(3) = -\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\hat{\mathbf p} = -1$; $e(0)\cdot e(3) = 1\cdot0 - 0 = 0$; $e(0)\cdot e(i) = 0$ for $i = 1, 2$ (no time components in $e(i)$); $e(3)\cdot e(i) = -\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf e(p, i) = 0$ (transversality); $e(i)\cdot e(j) = -\mathbf e(p, i)\cdot\mathbf e(p, j) = -\delta_{ij}$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^def-c4-3-4|Def. §C4.3.4]], Yu (4.71)–(4.75)). Together: $g_{\sigma\sigma'}$.
>
> **2. Completeness.** The derivation of [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]] uses only the orthonormality $e(\sigma)\cdot e(\sigma') = g_{\sigma\sigma'}$ (which makes the four vectors independent and lets $\sum_\sigma g_{\sigma\sigma}e_\mu(\sigma)e_\nu(\sigma)$ act as $g_{\mu\nu}$ on each of them), never $e(p, 0) = p/m$; with step 1 it applies verbatim. Yu (4.192) checks the same by multiplying out the $4\times4$ matrices.
>
> **3. Orientation.** The vectors $\mathbf e(p, 1)$, $\mathbf e(p, 2)$ are the massive ones, whose triad with $\hat{\mathbf p}$ is right handed ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]]). ⚑ By-product: the orientation fixes which of $\varepsilon(p, \pm)$ has helicity $+1$: a rotation by $\theta$ about $\hat{\mathbf p}$ multiplies $\varepsilon(p, \pm)$ by $e^{\mp i\theta}$ (as [[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-6-8|Theorem §C3.6.8]], 1, for $\hat{\mathbf p} = \hat{\mathbf z}$) → [[§C4.7 The Gupta–Bleuler Condition and Physical Photons#^thm-c4-7-8|Theorem §C4.7.8]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Completeness is a consequence of orthonormality alone, so it holds for both bases; what differs between them is transversality ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-10|Theorem §C4.5.10]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.5.12: The Transverse Polarization Sum
> For the massless basis of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-3|Def. §C4.5.3]],
>
> $$
> \sum_{\lambda = \pm}\varepsilon^*_\mu(p, \lambda)\,\varepsilon_\nu(p, \lambda) = \sum_{\sigma = 1}^2e_\mu(p, \sigma)\,e_\nu(p, \sigma) = -g_{\mu\nu} - \frac{p_\mu p_\nu}{(p\cdot n)^2} + \frac{p_\mu n_\nu + p_\nu n_\mu}{p\cdot n} ,
> $$
>
> and $p^\nu\sum_{\lambda = \pm}\varepsilon^*_\mu(p, \lambda)\varepsilon_\nu(p, \lambda) = 0$. It is the massless counterpart of the massive sum $-g_{\mu\nu} + p_\mu p_\nu/m^2$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-8|Theorem §C4.3.8]]), finite but dependent on $n$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.4.1, eqs. (4.194)–(4.198) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.4, eq. (massless-pol-sum)*

^thm-c4-5-12

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Solve completeness for the transverse pair.** [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-11|Theorem §C4.5.11]] with $g_{00} = 1$, $g_{ii} = -1$ reads $e_\mu(0)e_\nu(0) - \sum_{i=1}^3e_\mu(i)e_\nu(i) = g_{\mu\nu}$, so
>
> $$
> \sum_{\sigma = 1}^2e_\mu(\sigma)e_\nu(\sigma) = -g_{\mu\nu} + n_\mu n_\nu - e_\mu(3)e_\nu(3) .
> $$
>
> **2. The longitudinal product.** From the definition, with all four terms of the product kept,
>
> $$
> e_\mu(3)e_\nu(3) = \frac{\bigl(p_\mu - (p\cdot n)n_\mu\bigr)\bigl(p_\nu - (p\cdot n)n_\nu\bigr)}{(p\cdot n)^2} = \frac{p_\mu p_\nu}{(p\cdot n)^2} - \frac{p_\mu n_\nu + n_\mu p_\nu}{p\cdot n} + n_\mu n_\nu .
> $$
>
> **3. Substitute.** The $n_\mu n_\nu$ terms cancel: $\sum_{\sigma=1}^2e_\mu e_\nu = -g_{\mu\nu} - \frac{p_\mu p_\nu}{(p\cdot n)^2} + \frac{p_\mu n_\nu + p_\nu n_\mu}{p\cdot n}$.
>
> **4. Helicity form.** With $\varepsilon(\pm) = (e_1 \pm ie_2)/\sqrt2$ and $e_1$, $e_2$ real, expand both products into their four terms:
>
> $$
> \varepsilon^*_\mu(+)\varepsilon_\nu(+) = \tfrac12\bigl(e_{1\mu}e_{1\nu} + ie_{1\mu}e_{2\nu} - ie_{2\mu}e_{1\nu} + e_{2\mu}e_{2\nu}\bigr), \qquad \varepsilon^*_\mu(-)\varepsilon_\nu(-) = \tfrac12\bigl(e_{1\mu}e_{1\nu} - ie_{1\mu}e_{2\nu} + ie_{2\mu}e_{1\nu} + e_{2\mu}e_{2\nu}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The imaginary cross terms cancel in the sum, leaving $e_{1\mu}e_{1\nu} + e_{2\mu}e_{2\nu}$ (Yu (4.196)).
>
> **5. Transversality.** Contract step 3 with $p^\nu$, using $p^\nu p_\nu = 0$: $-p_\mu - \frac{p_\mu\cdot0}{(p\cdot n)^2} + \frac{p_\mu(n\cdot p) + 0\cdot n_\mu}{p\cdot n} = -p_\mu + p_\mu = 0$ (Yu (4.198)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The physical sum differs from $-g_{\mu\nu}$ only by terms proportional to $p_\mu$ or $p_\nu$; contracted with amplitudes that satisfy the Ward identity these vanish, which is the polarization-sum replacement of [[§C4.8 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-8-10|Theorem §C4.8.10]].
> - At $m > 0$ the same role is played by $+p_\mu p_\nu/m^2$, which is covariant but singular as $m \to 0$ ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-1|Theorem §C4.5.1]]): covariance and finiteness cannot both be kept at $m = 0$ without dropping the $p$-terms.

## Angular momentum of the Maxwell field and gauge invariance

The Noether spin and orbital angular momentum of every vector field with $\Pi^{\lambda\rho} = -F^{\lambda\rho}$ are [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-5|Theorem §C4.1.5]] and [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]]. For the Maxwell field both contain the potential itself, and the gauge-fixed Lagrangians have their own spin currents.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.5.13: Spin and Orbital Angular Momentum Are Not Separately Gauge Invariant
> Under a gauge transformation $A^\mu \to A^\mu + \partial^\mu\chi$ ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-1|Def. §C4.5.1]]), i.e. $A^0 \to A^0 + \partial_t\chi$, $\mathbf A \to \mathbf A - \nabla\chi$, with $\chi$ smooth and of compact support in space at the time considered, the spin $\mathbf S = \int d^3x\,\mathbf E\times\mathbf A$ and the orbital part $\mathbf L = \int d^3x\,E^l(\mathbf x\times\nabla)A^l$ ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-5|Theorem §C4.1.5]], [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]]) change by
>
> $$
> \Delta\mathbf S = -\int d^3x\;\chi\,\nabla\times\mathbf E = \int d^3x\;\chi\,\partial_t\mathbf B, \qquad \Delta\mathbf L = \int d^3x\,\bigl[\chi\,\nabla\times\mathbf E + (\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)\,\mathbf x\times\nabla\chi\bigr] .
> $$
>
> For the free Maxwell field ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = 0$, [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^mod-c4-5-2|Model §C4.5.2]]) the total $\mathbf J = \mathbf L + \mathbf S$ is gauge invariant; $\mathbf S$ and $\mathbf L$ separately are not, at every time at which $\nabla\times\mathbf E \ne 0$ somewhere (every radiation field).
>
> *Source: derived here (Yu and the user's pre-course notes do not discuss the gauge dependence of the split; the pre-course notes flag that only $J^{\nu\rho}$, not the split, is covariant, §1.7)*

^thm-c4-5-13

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. What changes.** $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ are gauge invariant ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-3|Theorem §C4.5.3]]), so only the bare $\mathbf A$ in $\mathbf S$ and $\mathbf L$ changes: $A^i \to A^i + \partial^i\chi = A^i - \partial_i\chi$, using $\partial^i = -\partial_i$.
>
> **2. The spin.** $\Delta\mathbf S = \int d^3x\,\mathbf E\times(-\nabla\chi) = -\int d^3x\,\mathbf E\times\nabla\chi$.
>
> **3. Integrate by parts.** $(\mathbf E\times\nabla\chi)^i = \varepsilon_{ijk}E^j\partial_k\chi = \partial_k\bigl(\varepsilon_{ijk}E^j\chi\bigr) - \varepsilon_{ijk}(\partial_kE^j)\chi$. The divergence integrates to a surface term, zero because $\chi$ vanishes outside a bounded region ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-8|Theorem §CA.3.8]]). In the remainder swap the last two indices of $\varepsilon$: $-\varepsilon_{ijk}\partial_kE^j = \varepsilon_{ikj}\partial_kE^j = (\nabla\times\mathbf E)^i$. Hence $\int d^3x\,\mathbf E\times\nabla\chi = \int d^3x\,\chi\,\nabla\times\mathbf E$, and $\Delta\mathbf S = -\int d^3x\,\chi\,\nabla\times\mathbf E$.
>
> **4. Faraday's law.** From $\mathbf E = -\nabla A^0 - \partial_t\mathbf A$ ([[§C1.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^def-c1-7-2|Def. §C1.7.2]]): $\nabla\times\mathbf E = -\nabla\times\nabla A^0 - \partial_t(\nabla\times\mathbf A) = 0 - \partial_t\mathbf B$, the curl of a gradient vanishing because mixed partial derivatives commute ([[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]). So $\Delta\mathbf S = \int d^3x\,\chi\,\partial_t\mathbf B$.
>
> **5. The orbital part.** $\Delta L^i = \int d^3x\,E^l\varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\partial_k(-\partial_l\chi) = -\int d^3x\,E^l\varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\,\partial_l\partial_k\chi$ (the derivatives commute). Integrate by parts in $\partial_l$ (surface term zero as in step 3): $\Delta L^i = \int d^3x\,\partial_l\bigl(E^l\varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\bigr)\partial_k\chi$. The product rule gives two terms: $(\partial_lE^l)\,\varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\partial_k\chi = (\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)(\mathbf x\times\nabla\chi)^i$, and $E^l\varepsilon_{ijk}(\partial_lx^j)\partial_k\chi = E^l\varepsilon_{ijk}\delta_{lj}\partial_k\chi = \varepsilon_{ilk}E^l\partial_k\chi = (\mathbf E\times\nabla\chi)^i$. With step 3 the second integrates to $\int\chi\,(\nabla\times\mathbf E)^i$: the stated $\Delta\mathbf L$.
>
> **6. The total.** $\Delta\mathbf J = \Delta\mathbf L + \Delta\mathbf S = \int d^3x\,(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)\,\mathbf x\times\nabla\chi$. The $\nu = 0$ component of the source-free Maxwell equation $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0$ is $\partial_iF^{i0} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf E = 0$ ($F^{00} = 0$, $F^{i0} = E^i$), so $\Delta\mathbf J = 0$.
>
> **7. The parts do change.** Suppose $(\nabla\times\mathbf E)^i(t, \mathbf x_0) \ne 0$. By continuity it has one sign on a ball around $\mathbf x_0$; take $\chi \ge 0$ supported in that ball and not identically zero. Then $\int\chi\,(\nabla\times\mathbf E)^i \ne 0$: $\Delta S^i \ne 0$, and for the free field $\Delta L^i = -\Delta S^i \ne 0$. For a radiation field $\nabla\times\mathbf E = -\partial_t\mathbf B \ne 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ By-product: the decomposition $\mathbf J = \mathbf L + \mathbf S$ of the photon field depends on the gauge; only the sum, equal to $\int\mathbf x\times(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)$ ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^rem-c4-1-3|§C4.1, Remark: Spin hidden in the Poynting vector]]), is an observable of the classical field. In the Coulomb gauge $\mathbf S = \int\mathbf E\times\mathbf A_\perp$, the form whose quanta are counted in [[§C4.7 The Gupta–Bleuler Condition and Physical Photons#^thm-c4-7-10|Theorem §C4.7.10]]; the quantum shadow of the dependence is [[§C4.7 The Gupta–Bleuler Condition and Physical Photons#^thm-c4-7-12|Theorem §C4.7.12]].
> - Assumption actually used for the total: Gauss's law without charge. With a charge density, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \rho$ and $\Delta\mathbf J = \int\rho\,\mathbf x\times\nabla\chi \ne 0$ in general; the field's angular momentum is then not gauge invariant alone, and the angular momentum of the charged matter must compensate (QED, QFT C8, planned; stated, not derived here).
> - The Proca field has no gauge symmetry ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-3|Theorem §C4.5.3]], 2): its $\mathbf L$ and $\mathbf S$ are unambiguous, but its $\mathbf S$ is not conserved alone ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Massive Vector Field#^thm-c4-4-14|Theorem §C4.4.14]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.5.14: Spin Charges of the Gauge-Fixed Lagrangians
> 1. For $\mathcal L_\xi$ ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-2|Def. §C4.5.2]]), $\Pi^{\lambda\rho} = -F^{\lambda\rho} - \frac1\xi g^{\lambda\rho}\,\partial\cdot A$, and the spin current of [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]] becomes $\mathcal S_\xi^{\lambda\mu\nu} = F^{\lambda\nu}A^\mu - F^{\lambda\mu}A^\nu - \frac1\xi(\partial\cdot A)\bigl(g^{\lambda\mu}A^\nu - g^{\lambda\nu}A^\mu\bigr)$. The spin vector is unchanged, $\mathbf S_\xi = \int d^3x\,\mathbf E\times\mathbf A$; the boost spin gains a term, $S_\xi^{0k} = -\int d^3x\,\bigl(A^0E^k + \frac1\xi(\partial\cdot A)A^k\bigr)$, which vanishes on the Lorenz subspace ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-7|Theorem §C4.5.7]]).
> 2. For $\mathcal L_2 = -\frac12\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu$ ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-8|Theorem §C4.5.8]]; the Lagrangian quantized in [[§C4.6 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^mod-c4-6-1|Model §C4.6.1]]), $\Pi^{\lambda\rho} = -\partial^\lambda A^\rho$, $\mathcal S_2^{\lambda\mu\nu} = A^\mu\partial^\lambda A^\nu - A^\nu\partial^\lambda A^\mu$, and
>
> $$
> \mathbf S_2 = -\int d^3x\;\partial_t\mathbf A\times\mathbf A = \mathbf S - \int d^3x\;A^0\,\mathbf B .
> $$
>
> Two Lagrangians with the same field equation, differing by a total derivative, have spin charges differing by $\int A^0\mathbf B$, which vanishes only where $A^0 = 0$.
>

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. $\Pi$ for $\mathcal L_\xi$.** Step 2 of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^der-c4-5-6|Derivation §C4.5.6]]: $\partial\mathcal L_\xi/\partial(\partial_\lambda A_\rho) = -F^{\lambda\rho} - \frac1\xi g^{\lambda\rho}\,\partial_\sigma A^\sigma$.
>
> **2. The extra spin current.** Insert the extra piece $-\frac1\xi g^{\lambda\rho}(\partial\cdot A)$ into $\Pi^{\lambda\mu}A^\nu - \Pi^{\lambda\nu}A^\mu$ ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]]): $-\frac1\xi(\partial\cdot A)\bigl(g^{\lambda\mu}A^\nu - g^{\lambda\nu}A^\mu\bigr)$. The rest is the Maxwell current $F^{\lambda\nu}A^\mu - F^{\lambda\mu}A^\nu$.
>
> **3. Its charges.** At $\lambda = 0$: for $(\mu, \nu) = (j, k)$ the extra term is $-\frac1\xi(\partial\cdot A)(g^{0j}A^k - g^{0k}A^j) = 0$, since $g^{0j} = g^{0k} = 0$; so $S^{jk}$ and $\mathbf S$ are those of [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-5|Theorem §C4.1.5]]. For $(\mu, \nu) = (0, k)$ it is $-\frac1\xi(\partial\cdot A)(g^{00}A^k - g^{0k}A^0) = -\frac1\xi(\partial\cdot A)A^k$; add it to $\mathcal S^{00k} = -A^0E^k$ (Theorem §C4.1.5) and integrate. On the Lorenz subspace $\partial\cdot A = 0$ and the extra term vanishes.
>
> **4. $\Pi$ for $\mathcal L_2$.** Step 3 of [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^der-c4-5-9|Derivation §C4.5.9]]: $\partial\mathcal L_2/\partial(\partial_\lambda A_\rho) = -\partial^\lambda A^\rho$. Theorem §C4.1.4: $\mathcal S_2^{\lambda\mu\nu} = -\partial^\lambda A^\mu\,A^\nu + \partial^\lambda A^\nu\,A^\mu$, the stated form.
>
> **5. Its spin vector.** At $\lambda = 0$, $\partial^0 = \partial_t$: $\mathcal S_2^{0jk} = A^j\partial_tA^k - A^k\partial_tA^j$. Dualizing as in step 2 of [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^der-c4-1-5|Derivation §C4.1.5]], $S_2^i = \int d^3x\,\varepsilon_{ijk}A^j\partial_tA^k = \int d^3x\,(\mathbf A\times\partial_t\mathbf A)^i = -\int d^3x\,(\partial_t\mathbf A\times\mathbf A)^i$.
>
> **6. Compare with $\mathbf S$.** $\mathbf S - \mathbf S_2 = \int d^3x\,(\mathbf E + \partial_t\mathbf A)\times\mathbf A = -\int d^3x\,\nabla A^0\times\mathbf A$, by $\mathbf E = -\nabla A^0 - \partial_t\mathbf A$. Integrate by parts: $(\nabla A^0\times\mathbf A)^i = \varepsilon_{ijk}(\partial_jA^0)A^k = \partial_j\bigl(\varepsilon_{ijk}A^0A^k\bigr) - A^0\varepsilon_{ijk}\partial_jA^k$; the divergence gives a surface term, dropped for fields falling off at infinity ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-8|Theorem §CA.3.8]]), and $\varepsilon_{ijk}\partial_jA^k = B^i$. So $-\int\nabla A^0\times\mathbf A = +\int A^0\mathbf B$, and $\mathbf S_2 = \mathbf S - \int A^0\mathbf B$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ By-product: Noether's spin charge is not a property of the field equation alone. $\mathcal L_1$ and $\mathcal L_2$ differ by $\partial_\mu K^\mu$ ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-8|Theorem §C4.5.8]]) and have different spin charges, the non-uniqueness of Noether currents ([[§C1.11 Noether's Theorem#^cau-c1-11-2|§C1.11, Caution: What the theorem does and does not say]], [[§C1.11 Noether's Theorem#^thm-c1-11-4|Theorem §C1.11.4]]) met in a physical charge.
> - The gauge-fixing term only touches the boost components: the rotational spin of $\mathcal L_\xi$ is Maxwell's for every $\xi$.
> - Used next: the photon's quantum spin is that of $\mathcal L_2$, the quantized Lagrangian; on transverse fields ($A^0 = 0$) $\mathbf S_2 = \mathbf S$ → [[§C4.7 The Gupta–Bleuler Condition and Physical Photons#^thm-c4-7-11|Theorem §C4.7.11]].
