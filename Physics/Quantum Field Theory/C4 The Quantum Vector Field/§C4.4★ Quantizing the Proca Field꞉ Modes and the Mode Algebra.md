How is the Proca field quantized? The classical theory has been reduced to three canonical pairs $(A^i, \pi_i)$, with $A^0$ fixed by a constraint and $H$ and $\mathbf P$ written in field form ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field|§C4.2★]]), and its plane waves and polarization vectors are known ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves|§C4.3★]]). This section follows the scalar sequence of [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields|§C2a.1]]–[[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra|§C2a.2]] and the Dirac one of [[§C5b.1 Canonical Quantization of the Dirac Field|§C5b.1]]–[[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra|§C5b.2]]: it recalls the classical homes as embedded boxes, states the quantum model and the equal-time relations of the three canonical pairs, shows that the Heisenberg equations are the Proca equation, and only then expands the operator field in modes, extracts the mode operators and derives their algebra. The vector index changes the scalar template in three places: the constraint survives as a non-vanishing equal-time commutator of $A^0$, transversality removes $A^0$ from the mode extraction, and the polarization sum is equivalent to the canonical commutator. Energy, momentum, the vacuum and the spin-one quanta are [[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field|§C4.5★]]; the procedure is [[P1 Canonical Quantization|P1]], steps 2–4.

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): as in [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves|§C4.3★]]; canonical pairs $(A^i, \pi_i)$ with $\pi_i = -E^i$, and $\pi^i = E^i$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^cau-c4-2-1|§C4.2★, Caution: Index placement of the momentum across sources]]); all operators at one time $t$ unless a time is shown; $\varepsilon(p, \lambda)$ any orthonormal transverse basis, the spin basis where smoothness in $\mathbf p$ is used.

## The quantum model

> [!model] Model §C4.4.1: The Free Proca Quantum Field
> Three Hermitian fields $A^i(\mathbf x)$ and their conjugate momenta $\pi_i(\mathbf x) = -E^i(\mathbf x)$ satisfying the equal-time relations of [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^pr-c4-4-2|Principle §C4.4.2]], with $A^0 \equiv -\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi/m^2$ the operator of the constraint, Hamiltonian
>
> $$
> H = \frac12\int d^3x\,\Bigl[\boldsymbol\pi^2 + \frac{1}{m^2}(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)^2 + (\nabla\times\mathbf A)^2 + m^2\mathbf A^2\Bigr], \qquad \boldsymbol\pi = \mathbf E ,
> $$
>
> and field momentum $\mathbf P = -\int d^3x\,\pi_l\nabla A^l$: the quantization of the Proca Lagrangian ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^mod-c4-2-1|Model §C4.2.1]]), with the classical charges of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-9|Theorem §C4.2.9]] and [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-12|Theorem §C4.2.12]] (recalled above) read as operators. That $\mathbf P$ generates translations of the field is [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-13|Theorem §C4.2.13]] with brackets replaced by commutators; in modes, [[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-5|Theorem §C4.5.5]].
>
> *Assumptions:* free (quadratic $\mathcal L$, no interactions); $m > 0$; flat spacetime with an inertial time; infinite space, continuum (delta-function) normalization; the covariance law of a vector field ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^rem-c4-1-1|§C4.1, Remark: The quantum vector field, recalled]]) is assumed; the operator ordering in $H$ is fixed in [[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-3|Theorem §C4.5.3]]; $\mathbf P$ needs no ordering choice ([[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-4|Theorem §C4.5.4]]).
> *Source: Yu §4.3, eqs. (4.58), (4.136), (4.138) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 ("Quantization") · modelled on [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^mod-c2a-1-1|Model §C2a.1.1]]*

## The postulate

> [!principle] Principle §C4.4.2: Equal-Time Commutation Relations of the Proca Field
> The three independent components $A^i$ and their conjugates $\pi_i$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]]) are Hermitian operator-valued distributions with
>
> $$
> [A^i(\mathbf x, t), \pi_j(\mathbf y, t)] = i\delta^i{}_j\,\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y), \qquad [A^i(\mathbf x, t), A^j(\mathbf y, t)] = [\pi_i(\mathbf x, t), \pi_j(\mathbf y, t)] = 0 ;
> $$
>
> equivalently $[A^i(\mathbf x), \pi^j(\mathbf y)] = [A^i(\mathbf x), E^j(\mathbf y)] = -i\delta^{ij}\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$. $A^0$ is not quantized independently: it is the operator $A^0 \equiv -\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi/m^2$ of the constraint ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-8|Theorem §C4.2.8]]).
>
> *Domain:* the free Proca field ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^mod-c4-2-1|Model §C4.2.1]]), in the reduced canonical variables whose Hamiltonian system is the Proca theory ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-11|Theorem §C4.2.11]]). As for the scalar field, the relations hold for smeared fields, $[A^i(f), \pi_j(g)] = i\delta^i{}_j\int d^3x\,fg$ ([[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^def-c2a-1-1|Def. §C2a.1.1]], [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^thm-c2a-1-3|Theorem §C2a.1.3]]). Layer: the scalar postulate [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2a-1-2|Principle §C2a.1.2]] applied to the pairs that survive the constraint, not to all four components.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3, eq. (4.58) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 (ETCR, "the upper-index form of the canonical pairing")*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.4.3: A⁰ Does Not Commute with A^j at Equal Times
> At equal times,
>
> $$
> [A^0(\mathbf x), A^j(\mathbf y)] = -\frac{i}{m^2}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}\,\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y), \qquad [A^0(\mathbf x), \pi_j(\mathbf y)] = 0, \qquad [A^0(\mathbf x), A^0(\mathbf y)] = 0 ,
> $$
>
> the first as the gradient of a distribution: smeared, $[A^0(f), A^j(g)] = \frac{i}{m^2}\int d^3x\,(\partial_jf)\,g$.
>
> *Source: derived here from Principle §C4.4.2 and the constraint (Yu and the user's pre-course notes do not state it); checked against the mode expansion in [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^der-c4-4-7b|Derivation §C4.4.7, second route]], step 5*

^thm-c4-4-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Write $A^0$ in canonical variables.** $A^0(\mathbf x) = -\frac1{m^2}\partial^x_i\pi^i(\mathbf x) = \frac1{m^2}\partial^x_i\pi_i(\mathbf x)$, since $\pi^i = -\pi_i$.
>
> **2. Pull the derivative out.** The commutator is linear, and $\partial^x_i$ acts on the first argument only: $[A^0(\mathbf x), A^j(\mathbf y)] = \frac1{m^2}\partial^x_i[\pi_i(\mathbf x), A^j(\mathbf y)]$. *Sense:* the derivative of an operator-valued distribution ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|Def. §CA.2.4]]); smeared with $f(\mathbf x)$ it moves onto the test function with a minus sign.
>
> **3. Use the canonical relation.** $[\pi_i(\mathbf x), A^j(\mathbf y)] = -[A^j(\mathbf y), \pi_i(\mathbf x)] = -i\delta^j{}_i\delta^3(\mathbf y - \mathbf x)$, and $\delta^3$ is even. So $[A^0(\mathbf x), A^j(\mathbf y)] = -\frac i{m^2}\partial^x_j\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$, the gradient of the delta function ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-8|Theorem §CA.3.8]]).
>
> **4. Smeared.** $\int d^3x\,d^3y\,f(\mathbf x)g(\mathbf y)\bigl(-\frac i{m^2}\bigr)\partial^x_j\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y) = \frac i{m^2}\int d^3x\,d^3y\,(\partial_jf)(\mathbf x)g(\mathbf y)\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y) = \frac i{m^2}\int d^3x\,(\partial_jf)g$.
>
> **5. The other two.** $[A^0(\mathbf x), \pi_j(\mathbf y)] = \frac1{m^2}\partial^x_i[\pi_i(\mathbf x), \pi_j(\mathbf y)] = 0$ and $[A^0, A^0] \propto \partial^x_i\partial^y_k[\pi_i, \pi_k] = 0$ by Principle §C4.4.2.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ By-product: the constraint does not commute with everything: $A^0$ is a function of the momenta, so it inherits their non-commutativity with $\mathbf A$. The four components do not obey $[A^\mu, A^\nu] = 0$ at equal times, as four independent fields would; this is the quantum form of the second-class constraint ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^rem-c4-2-5|§C4.2★, ★ Remark: Second-class constraints, and why Maxwell differs]]) and it reappears as a non-covariant equal-time piece in the massive vector propagator ([[§C4.9 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-9-3|Theorem §C4.9.3]]).
> - The size $1/m^2$ diverges as $m \to 0$: another face of the singular massless limit ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-10|Theorem §C4.3.10]]).

## The field equation and the mode expansion

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.4.4: The Heisenberg Equations Are the Proca Equation
> Let $A^i(t, \mathbf x) = e^{iHt}A^i(\mathbf x)e^{-iHt}$ and $\pi_i(t, \mathbf x)$ likewise be the Heisenberg fields ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^def-c2b-1-1|Def. §C2b.1.1]], per component), with $H$ of [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^mod-c4-4-1|Model §C4.4.1]]. The Heisenberg equation $i\partial_t\mathcal O = [\mathcal O, H]$ gives, with $E^i = -\pi_i$, $A^0 \equiv -\nabla\cdot\mathbf E/m^2$ and $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$,
>
> $$
> \dot{\mathbf A} = -\mathbf E - \nabla A^0, \qquad \dot{\mathbf E} = \nabla\times\mathbf B + m^2\mathbf A ,
> $$
>
> Hamilton's equations of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-11|Theorem §C4.2.11]] as operator equations. Hence the operator field $A^\mu$ obeys the Proca equation $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + m^2A^\nu = 0$, the Lorenz condition $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$ and $(\partial^2 + m^2)A^\mu = 0$, as identities of operator-valued distributions.
>
> *Source: derived here, as [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-2|Theorem §C2b.1.2]] for the scalar (the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.2), with the Proca Hamiltonian of Yu §4.3, eq. (4.136)*

^thm-c4-4-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the relations at every time). Conjugation by $e^{iHt}$ preserves the relations of [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^pr-c4-4-2|Principle §C4.4.2]] at each common time $t$, exactly as in [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-1|Theorem §C2b.1.1]]: $[A^i(t, \mathbf x), \pi_j(t, \mathbf y)] = i\delta^i{}_j\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$, $[A^i, A^j] = [\pi_i, \pi_j] = 0$. The constraint $A^0 = \partial_k\pi_k/m^2$ (from $A^0 = -\partial_k\pi^k/m^2$ and $\pi^k = -\pi_k$) is linear with constant coefficients, so it holds at every time.
>
> **Step 2** ($H$ in the canonical variables). $\boldsymbol\pi^2 = \pi^j\pi^j = \pi_j\pi_j$ and $(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)^2 = (\partial_j\pi^j)^2 = (\partial_j\pi_j)^2$, so at the time $t$ of the operator it is commuted with,
>
> $$
> H = \frac12\int d^3y\,\Bigl[\pi_j\pi_j + \frac{1}{m^2}(\partial_j\pi_j)^2 + (\nabla\times\mathbf A)^2 + m^2A^jA^j\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Products at one point are understood normal-ordered ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^def-c2a-3-1|Def. §C2a.3.1]]); the subtracted constant drops out of every commutator, as in Step 2 of [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^der-c2b-1-2|Derivation §C2b.1.2]].
>
> **Step 3** ($A^i$ against the four terms). Write $A^i = A^i(t, \mathbf x)$ and fields at $\mathbf y$ in the integrand.
> - $[A^i, \frac12\int\pi_j\pi_j] = \frac12\int d^3y\,\bigl(\pi_j[A^i, \pi_j] + [A^i, \pi_j]\pi_j\bigr) = i\pi_i(\mathbf x)$: the commutator is a number, so the two terms are equal.
> - $[A^i, \frac{1}{2m^2}\int(\partial_j\pi_j)^2] = \frac1{m^2}\int d^3y\,(\partial_j\pi_j)(\mathbf y)\,\partial^{\mathbf y}_k[A^i(\mathbf x), \pi_k(\mathbf y)] = \frac{i}{m^2}\int d^3y\,(\partial_j\pi_j)(\mathbf y)\,\partial^{\mathbf y}_i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y) = -\frac{i}{m^2}\,\partial_i(\partial_j\pi_j)(\mathbf x)$, moving the derivative off the delta function ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|Def. §CA.2.4]]; the surface term vanishes, [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-8|Theorem §CA.3.8]]).
> - $[A^i, (\nabla\times\mathbf A)^2] = 0$ and $[A^i, A^jA^j] = 0$, since $[A^i, A^j] = 0$ for all $\mathbf y$ (and so for its derivatives).
>
> By the Heisenberg equation ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-4|QM Theorem §C3.3.4]]) $i\dot A^i = [A^i, H] = i\pi_i - \frac{i}{m^2}\partial_i(\partial_j\pi_j)$. With $\pi_i = -E^i$ and $\partial_j\pi_j = m^2A^0$: $\dot A^i = -E^i - \partial_iA^0$.
>
> **Step 4** ($\pi_i$ against the four terms), with $[\pi_i(\mathbf x), A^j(\mathbf y)] = -i\delta_{ij}\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$.
> - The two $\pi$ terms commute with $\pi_i$.
> - $[\pi_i, \frac12m^2\int A^jA^j] = \frac12m^2\int d^3y\,2A^j(\mathbf y)(-i\delta_{ij})\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y) = -im^2A^i(\mathbf x)$.
> - With $(\nabla\times\mathbf A)^2 = B^jB^j$, $B^j = \varepsilon_{jkl}\partial_kA^l$: $[\pi_i(\mathbf x), \frac12\int B^jB^j] = \int d^3y\,B^j(\mathbf y)\,\varepsilon_{jkl}\,\partial^{\mathbf y}_k[\pi_i(\mathbf x), A^l(\mathbf y)] = -i\,\varepsilon_{jki}\int d^3y\,B^j(\mathbf y)\,\partial^{\mathbf y}_k\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y) = i\,\varepsilon_{jki}\,\partial_kB^j(\mathbf x)$. Since $\varepsilon_{jki} = -\varepsilon_{ikj}$ and $(\nabla\times\mathbf B)^i = \varepsilon_{ikj}\partial_kB^j$, this is $-i(\nabla\times\mathbf B)^i$.
>
> So $i\dot\pi_i = [\pi_i, H] = -i(\nabla\times\mathbf B)^i - im^2A^i$, and with $\pi_i = -E^i$: $\dot{\mathbf E} = \nabla\times\mathbf B + m^2\mathbf A$.
>
> **Step 5** (the Proca equation). Steps 3–4 with the definition of $A^0$ are the four Proca equations and imply the Lorenz condition, by the algebra of Steps 5–6 of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^der-c4-2-11|Derivation §C4.2.11]]; $(\partial^2 + m^2)A^\mu = 0$ follows as in [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-5|Theorem §C4.2.5]]. Every step is linear with derivatives, so it holds for operator-valued distributions with the derivatives moved onto test functions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|Def. §CA.2.4]]; the scalar case: [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-6|Theorem §C2b.1.6]], 3).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The field equation of the quantum field follows from the equal-time relations and $H$ alone, without the mode expansion; the mode expansion is its general solution ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-5|Theorem §C4.4.5]]).
> - The Lorenz condition holds here as an operator identity, because it is a consequence of the field equation; for the photon it cannot ([[§C4.7 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-7-7|Theorem §C4.7.7]]).
> - Assumption: fall-off of the fields at spatial infinity (the dropped surface terms).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.4.5: Mode Expansion of the Proca Field
> Every real solution of the Proca equation is
>
> $$
> A^\mu(x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_{\lambda = \pm, 0}\Bigl(\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda)\,a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + \varepsilon^{\mu*}(p, \lambda)\,a^*_{\mathbf p, \lambda}\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigr)\Big|_{p^0 = E_{\mathbf p}} ,
> $$
>
> and its momentum $\pi^i = E^i = -\pi_i$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]]) is
>
> $$
> \pi^i(x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_\lambda\Bigl(i\,W^i(p, \lambda)\,a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}\,e^{-ip\cdot x} - i\,W^{i*}(p, \lambda)\,a^*_{\mathbf p, \lambda}\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigr), \qquad W^i \equiv E_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^i - p^i\varepsilon^0 .
> $$
>
> The quantum field, which obeys the Proca equation as an operator ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-4|Theorem §C4.4.4]]), has the same form with operators: $a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}$ are operators, $a^*_{\mathbf p, \lambda} \to a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,\lambda}$, and $A^\mu$, $\pi^i$ are Hermitian. Yu's form of the momentum uses $\tilde\varepsilon_i \equiv \varepsilon_i - \frac{p_i}{p_0}\varepsilon_0 = -W^i/E_{\mathbf p}$.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3.1, eqs. (4.109), (4.122)–(4.126) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3, eq. (massive-vector-expansion) and Note "Reducing H to mode form" ($W^i$)*

^thm-c4-4-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Component by component.** By [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-5|Theorem §C4.2.5]] each $A^\mu$ solves the Klein–Gordon equation. Steps 1–3 of [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^der-c2a-2-2|Derivation §C2a.2.2]], applied to each component (with complex coefficients, reality not yet imposed), give
>
> $$
> A^\mu(x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\Bigl(\alpha^\mu(\mathbf p)\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + \beta^\mu(\mathbf p)\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigr), \qquad p^0 = E_{\mathbf p},
> $$
>
> with the substitution $\mathbf k = -\mathbf p$ in the second term (Jacobian $1$, $E_{-\mathbf p} = E_{\mathbf p}$; [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-7|Theorem §CA.3.7]], 1) already made, as there.
>
> **2. Reality.** $A^{\mu\ast} = A^\mu$ and the linear independence of $e^{-ip\cdot x}$ and $e^{+ip\cdot x}$ (uniqueness of the Fourier transform in $\mathcal S'$, [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-2|Theorem §CA.3.2]], 2, as in [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^der-c2a-2-2|Derivation §C2a.2.2]], step 4) give $\beta^\mu(\mathbf p) = \alpha^\mu(\mathbf p)^{\ast}$.
>
> **3. The Lorenz condition.** Differentiate under the integral (for wave-packet coefficients; [[§CA.1 Exchanging Limits, Derivatives and Integrals#^thm-ca-1-1|Theorem §CA.1.1]]): $\partial_\mu A^\mu = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\bigl(-i\,p\cdot\alpha\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + i\,p\cdot\alpha^{\ast}\,e^{ip\cdot x}\bigr)$. It vanishes ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]) iff both coefficient functions vanish, by the same independence: $p\cdot\alpha(\mathbf p) = 0$ for every $\mathbf p$.
>
> **4. Expand in the polarization basis.** $\alpha(\mathbf p) \in p^\perp$, so $\alpha = P\alpha = -\sum_\lambda\varepsilon(p, \lambda)\bigl(\varepsilon^{\ast}(p, \lambda)\cdot\alpha\bigr)$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-9|Theorem §C4.3.9]]). Define $a_{\mathbf p,\lambda} \equiv -\varepsilon^{\ast}(p, \lambda)\cdot\alpha(\mathbf p)$; then $\alpha^\mu = \sum_\lambda\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda)a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}$ and $\alpha^{\mu\ast} = \sum_\lambda\varepsilon^{\mu\ast}(p, \lambda)a^{\ast}_{\mathbf p, \lambda}$: the first statement. Conversely every such sum is a real Proca solution by [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]] and superposition.
>
> **5. The momentum.** $\pi^i = E^i = -\partial_iA^0 - \partial_0A^i$. On the positive-frequency term, $\partial_\mu e^{-ip\cdot x} = -ip_\mu e^{-ip\cdot x}$ with $p_i = -p^i$, $p_0 = E_{\mathbf p}$: $-\partial_i(\varepsilon^0e^{-ip\cdot x}) - \partial_0(\varepsilon^ie^{-ip\cdot x}) = (ip_i\varepsilon^0 + ip_0\varepsilon^i)e^{-ip\cdot x} = i(E_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^i - p^i\varepsilon^0)e^{-ip\cdot x} = iW^ie^{-ip\cdot x}$. The negative-frequency term is its complex conjugate, $-iW^{i\ast}e^{ip\cdot x}$. Since $\varepsilon_i = -\varepsilon^i$, $p_i/p_0 = -p^i/E_{\mathbf p}$, $\varepsilon_0 = \varepsilon^0$: $\tilde\varepsilon_i = -\varepsilon^i + p^i\varepsilon^0/E_{\mathbf p} = -W^i/E_{\mathbf p}$, so Yu's $\pi_i = \frac{ip_0}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}(\tilde\varepsilon_ia\,e^{-ip\cdot x} - \dots)$ (4.125) is $-\pi^i$, as it must be.
>
> **6. Check: the constraint in modes.** Transversality $p_\mu\varepsilon^\mu = E_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^0 - \mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon = 0$ gives $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf W = E_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon) - \mathbf p^2\varepsilon^0 = (E_{\mathbf p}^2 - \mathbf p^2)\varepsilon^0 = m^2\varepsilon^0$. With $\partial_ie^{-ip\cdot x} = ip^ie^{-ip\cdot x}$, the positive-frequency part of $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi$ is $(ip^i)(iW^i) = -m^2\varepsilon^0$ times the mode, i.e. $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\pi = -m^2A^0$ mode by mode: the constraint of [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-8|Theorem §C4.2.8]] holds identically. ⚑ By-product: the expansion has eliminated $A^0$ as an independent variable already: it is fixed by the same $a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}$ through $\varepsilon^0$ → [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-6|Theorem §C4.4.6]], where it drops out of the extraction.
>
> **7. Quantize.** The Heisenberg field obeys the Proca equation as an operator ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-4|Theorem §C4.4.4]]), so steps 1–6 hold for it in every wave-packet matrix element. As in [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^der-c2a-2-2|Derivation §C2a.2.2]], step 7: numbers become operators, $\ast$ becomes $\dagger$; each term of $A^\mu$ and of $\pi^i$ is the adjoint of the other, so both are Hermitian (Yu (4.45), (4.126)). ⚑ By-product: the operator coefficients make $A^\mu$ an operator-valued distribution → [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^rem-c4-4-1|Remark: In what sense the Proca field is a distribution]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The vector analogue of [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-2|Theorem §C2a.2.2]]: one scalar expansion per polarization, the four components tied together by $\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda)$; the Lorenz condition is what reduces four coefficient functions to three.
> - The momentum is not $\dot A$: it contains $\nabla A^0$, which is why $W^i$ mixes $\varepsilon^i$ and $\varepsilon^0$.
> - Used next: extraction of $a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}$ and its algebra ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-6|Theorem §C4.4.6]], [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-7|Theorem §C4.4.7]]).

> [!remark] Remark: In what sense the Proca field is a distribution
> As for the scalar field ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-7|Theorem §C2a.2.7]]), $A^\mu(x)$ is an operator-valued distribution ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-8|Def. §CA.2.8]]): only smeared fields are operators. For real test functions $f_\mu \in \mathcal S(\mathbb R^3)$ at $t = 0$, doing the $\mathbf x$-integral first (Fubini, [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-6|Theorem §CA.3.6]]) with $\int d^3x\,f_\mu e^{\pm i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x} = \tilde f_\mu(\mp\mathbf p)$ and $\tilde f_\mu(-\mathbf p) = \overline{\tilde f_\mu(\mathbf p)}$ for real $f_\mu$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-4|Theorem §CA.3.4]], 4), as in [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^der-c2a-2-7|Derivation §C2a.2.7]], steps 4–5,
>
> $$
> A(f) \equiv \int d^3x\,f_\mu(\mathbf x)A^\mu(0, \mathbf x) = \sum_\lambda\bigl(a_\lambda(g_\lambda) + a^\dagger_\lambda(g_\lambda)\bigr), \qquad g_\lambda(\mathbf p) = \frac{\varepsilon^{\mu*}(p, \lambda)\,\tilde f_\mu(\mathbf p)}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} ,
> $$
>
> with the smeared mode operators of [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^def-c2a-2-2|Def. §C2a.2.2]], one per polarization. In the spin basis $g_j$ is a Schwartz function, because $\varepsilon(p, j)$ is smooth and grows at most linearly ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]]) and $m > 0$; in the helicity basis the individual $g_\pm$ are discontinuous on the $z$-axis, but their sum over $\lambda$ is the same operator ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^der-c4-3-8c|Derivation §C4.3.8, third route]] applied to $\sum_\lambda\varepsilon\,\varepsilon^*$). The longitudinal vector grows like $|\mathbf p|/m$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-10|Theorem §C4.3.10]]): the Proca field is one power of momentum more singular at short distances than a scalar field, which is where the $p^\mu p^\nu/m^2$ of its two-point function comes from ([[§C4.9 Vector-Field Propagators#^thm-c4-9-1|Theorem §C4.9.1]]).

## Mode extraction and the mode algebra

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.4.6: Mode Extraction
> On any time slice, the mode operators of [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-5|Theorem §C4.4.5]] are
>
> $$
> a_{\mathbf p,\lambda} = \frac{-i}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\,\varepsilon^*_\mu(p, \lambda)\int d^3x\,e^{ip\cdot x}\bigl(\partial_0A^\mu - iE_{\mathbf p}A^\mu\bigr) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\int d^3x\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigl(-i\,\varepsilon^{i*}(p, \lambda)\,\pi^i(x) + W^{i*}(p, \lambda)\,A^i(x)\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> with $W^i = E_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^i - p^i\varepsilon^0$, and $a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,\lambda} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\int d^3x\,e^{-ip\cdot x}\bigl(i\,\varepsilon^i\pi^i + W^iA^i\bigr)$. The second form contains only the canonical variables: $A^0$ and $\partial_0A^0$ drop out.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3.2, eqs. (4.139)–(4.148) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 (Note "Where δ_{λλ′} comes from", eq. (massive-inversion))*

^thm-c4-4-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Fourier transform of the field at time $t$.** Insert the expansion with integration variable $\mathbf q$, $q^0 = E_{\mathbf q}$, and do the $d^3x$ integral first ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^rem-c2a-2-2|§C2a.2, Remark: Two bookkeeping rules]]; the plane-wave delta, an identity in $\mathcal S'$, [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-3|Theorem §CA.3.3]], with the time phase moved out by [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-6|Theorem §CA.3.6]]; valid after smearing in $\mathbf p$):
>
> $$
> \int d^3x\,e^{ip\cdot x}A^\mu(x) = \int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf q}}}\sum_\lambda\Bigl(\varepsilon^\mu(q, \lambda)a_{\mathbf q,\lambda}e^{i(E_{\mathbf p} - E_{\mathbf q})t}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q) + \varepsilon^{\mu*}(q, \lambda)a^\dagger_{\mathbf q,\lambda}e^{i(E_{\mathbf p} + E_{\mathbf q})t}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p + \mathbf q)\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> The first delta eliminates $\mathbf q = \mathbf p$, the second $\mathbf q = -\mathbf p$ ($E_{-\mathbf p} = E_{\mathbf p}$):
>
> $$
> \int d^3x\,e^{ip\cdot x}A^\mu = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_\lambda\Bigl(\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda)a_{\mathbf p,\lambda} + \varepsilon^{\mu*}(-p, \lambda)a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p,\lambda}e^{2iE_{\mathbf p}t}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> where $-p$ means the on-shell momentum $(E_{\mathbf p}, -\mathbf p)$. (Yu (4.139).)
>
> **2. The same for $\partial_0A^\mu$.** $\partial_0$ brings down $-iE_{\mathbf q}$ on the first term and $+iE_{\mathbf q}$ on the second: $\int d^3x\,e^{ip\cdot x}\partial_0A^\mu = \frac{-iE_{\mathbf p}}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_\lambda\bigl(\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda)a_{\mathbf p,\lambda} - \varepsilon^{\mu\ast}(-p, \lambda)a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p,\lambda}e^{2iE_{\mathbf p}t}\bigr)$ (Yu (4.140)).
>
> **3. Combine to cancel the creators.** $\int e^{ip\cdot x}(\partial_0A^\mu - iE_{\mathbf p}A^\mu) = \frac{-iE_{\mathbf p}}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_\lambda\bigl(\varepsilon^\mu a - \varepsilon^{\mu\ast}(-p)a^\dagger e^{2iEt} + \varepsilon^\mu a + \varepsilon^{\mu\ast}(-p)a^\dagger e^{2iEt}\bigr) = \frac{-2iE_{\mathbf p}}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_\lambda\varepsilon^\mu(p, \lambda)a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}$: the $a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}$ terms and all $t$-dependence cancel, as in [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^der-c2a-2-5|Derivation §C2a.2.5]], step 6.
>
> **4. Project on one polarization.** Contract with $\varepsilon^{\ast}_\mu(p, \lambda)$ and use $\varepsilon^{\ast}(p, \lambda)\cdot\varepsilon(p, \lambda') = -\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]]): $\varepsilon^{\ast}_\mu\int e^{ip\cdot x}(\partial_0A^\mu - iEA^\mu) = \frac{-2iE_{\mathbf p}}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}(-a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}) = i\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}\,a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}$. Dividing by $i\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$ gives the first form (Yu (4.141), (4.144)).
>
> **5. Remove the non-canonical velocities.** Split $\varepsilon^{\ast}_\mu X^\mu = \varepsilon^{0\ast}X^0 - \varepsilon^{i\ast}X^i$ ($\varepsilon^{\ast}_0 = \varepsilon^{0\ast}$, $\varepsilon^{\ast}_i = -\varepsilon^{i\ast}$). Replace $\partial_0A^0 = -\partial_iA^i$ (Lorenz condition, [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]) and $\partial_0A^i = -\pi^i - \partial_iA^0$ ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-8|Theorem §C4.2.8]]). The integrand becomes, term by term,
>
> $$
> \varepsilon^{0*}(-\partial_iA^i - iE_{\mathbf p}A^0) - \varepsilon^{i*}(-\pi^i - \partial_iA^0 - iE_{\mathbf p}A^i) = -\varepsilon^{0*}\partial_iA^i - iE_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^{0*}A^0 + \varepsilon^{i*}\pi^i + \varepsilon^{i*}\partial_iA^0 + iE_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^{i*}A^i .
> $$
>
> **6. Integrate the gradients by parts.** $\partial_ie^{ip\cdot x} = -ip^ie^{ip\cdot x}$ (because $p\cdot x = E_{\mathbf p}t - \mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x$), so $\int d^3x\,e^{ip\cdot x}\partial_iF = +ip^i\int d^3x\,e^{ip\cdot x}F$; the surface term is dropped (operator-valued distributions paired with the smooth $e^{ip\cdot x}$, valid after smearing in $\mathbf p$; [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-8|Theorem §CA.3.8]]). Thus $-\varepsilon^{0\ast}\partial_iA^i \to -ip^i\varepsilon^{0\ast}A^i$ and $\varepsilon^{i\ast}\partial_iA^0 \to ip^i\varepsilon^{i\ast}A^0$ (Yu (4.145)).
>
> **7. Collect by field.** Coefficient of $A^0$: $-iE_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^{0\ast} + ip^i\varepsilon^{i\ast} = -i\bigl(E_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^{0\ast} - \mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\ast}\bigr) = -i\,p_\mu\varepsilon^{\mu\ast}(p, \lambda) = 0$ by transversality ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]]). ⚑ By-product: $A^0$ drops out *because* the physical polarizations are transverse; with the timelike vector $p/m$ it would survive → [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^rem-c4-4-2|Remark: Why A⁰ drops out]]. Coefficient of $\pi^i$: $\varepsilon^{i\ast}$. Coefficient of $A^i$: $iE_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^{i\ast} - ip^i\varepsilon^{0\ast} = iW^{i\ast}$. So $a_{\mathbf p,\lambda} = \frac{-i}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\int e^{ip\cdot x}\bigl(\varepsilon^{i\ast}\pi^i + iW^{i\ast}A^i\bigr)$, the second form (Yu (4.146)–(4.147), there with $\pi_i = -\pi^i$ and $\tilde\varepsilon_i = -W^i/E_{\mathbf p}$).
>
> **8. The adjoint.** $A^i$, $\pi^i$ are Hermitian; conjugate every number: $a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,\lambda} = \frac{i}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\int e^{-ip\cdot x}\bigl(\varepsilon^i\pi^i - iW^iA^i\bigr) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\int e^{-ip\cdot x}\bigl(i\varepsilon^i\pi^i + W^iA^i\bigr)$ (Yu (4.148)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Steps 1–4 are the scalar extraction ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-5|Theorem §C2a.2.5]]) per component, followed by a projection with the polarization vector; steps 5–7 trade the non-canonical $\partial_0A^0$ and $A^0$ for canonical data using both constraints, and transversality makes $A^0$ disappear.
> - The extracted $a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}$ carries no $t$ (step 3): the same operator on every slice.
> - Used next: the commutator of two extracted operators ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-7|Theorem §C4.4.7]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Why A⁰ drops out
> The first form of [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-6|Theorem §C4.4.6]] contains $\partial_0A^0$, which has no conjugate momentum, so the canonical relations cannot be applied to it directly. Two facts remove it: the Lorenz condition trades $\partial_0A^0$ for $-\nabla\cdot\mathbf A$, and transversality $p_\mu\varepsilon^\mu = 0$ makes the coefficient of $A^0$ vanish after integration by parts. The condition that removed the spin-0 component from the field ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]]) is the same that removes the non-canonical $A^0$ from the extraction — the user's notes call it the condition that "protects the whole derivation". In the covariant photon theory no constraint removes $A^0$, all four components are canonical, and the timelike mode returns with a negative norm ([[§C4.7 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-7-10|Theorem §C4.7.10]], [[§C4.7 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-7-17|Theorem §C4.7.17]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.4.7: The Mode Algebra of the Proca Field
> The equal-time relations of [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^pr-c4-4-2|Principle §C4.4.2]] hold if and only if
>
> $$
> [a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf q,\lambda'}] = (2\pi)^3\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}\,\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q), \qquad [a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}, a_{\mathbf q,\lambda'}] = [a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,\lambda}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf q,\lambda'}] = 0 :
> $$
>
> three independent oscillator algebras per momentum, one per polarization, each the scalar one ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-6|Theorem §C2a.2.6]]); as there, an identity of distributions in $(\mathbf p, \mathbf q)$, made into one between operators by smearing ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-7|Theorem §C2a.2.7]], per polarization).
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3, eq. (4.127); §4.3.2, eqs. (4.149)–(4.160) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 (Note "Where δ_{λλ′} comes from")*

^thm-c4-4-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation (⇒: from the canonical relations to the mode algebra)
> **1. Write both operators.** By [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-6|Theorem §C4.4.6]], on one slice, with $\mathbf x$ for $a$ and $\mathbf y$ for $a^\dagger$,
>
> $$
> [a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf q,\lambda'}] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4E_{\mathbf p}E_{\mathbf q}}}\int d^3x\,d^3y\;e^{ip\cdot x - iq\cdot y}\Bigl[-i\varepsilon^{i*}(p, \lambda)\pi^i(\mathbf x) + W^{i*}(p, \lambda)A^i(\mathbf x),\ i\varepsilon^j(q, \lambda')\pi^j(\mathbf y) + W^j(q, \lambda')A^j(\mathbf y)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Sense:* as in [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^der-c2a-2-6|Derivation §C2a.2.6]], step 2: the commutators are distributions in $(\mathbf x, \mathbf y)$, and after smearing in $\mathbf p$, $\mathbf q$ with test functions the coefficient functions become Schwartz functions (spin basis, [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]]), so every step is exact.
>
> **2. Expand into all four terms.** $[\pi^i, \pi^j] = 0$ and $[A^i, A^j] = 0$ remove the first and fourth. With $[\pi^i(\mathbf x), A^j(\mathbf y)] = i\delta^{ij}\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$ and $[A^i(\mathbf x), \pi^j(\mathbf y)] = -i\delta^{ij}\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$ (Principle §C4.4.2 with $\pi^j = -\pi_j$):
>
> $$
> [-i\varepsilon^{i*}\pi^i, W^jA^j] = -i\varepsilon^{i*}W^j\,(i\delta^{ij}\delta^3) = \varepsilon^{i*}(p, \lambda)W^i(q, \lambda')\,\delta^3, \qquad [W^{i*}A^i, i\varepsilon^j\pi^j] = iW^{i*}\varepsilon^j\,(-i\delta^{ij}\delta^3) = W^{i*}(p, \lambda)\varepsilon^i(q, \lambda')\,\delta^3 .
> $$
>
> **3. Integrate the delta.** The $\mathbf y$ integral sets $\mathbf y = \mathbf x$ (δ paired with the test functions of step 1). The $\mathbf x$ integral of $e^{i(p - q)\cdot x}$ is $e^{i(E_{\mathbf p} - E_{\mathbf q})t}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-3|Theorem §CA.3.3]], [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-6|Theorem §CA.3.6]]). On the support of the delta, $\mathbf q = \mathbf p$: the phase is $1$, $\sqrt{4E_{\mathbf p}E_{\mathbf q}} = 2E_{\mathbf p}$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-7|Theorem §CA.2.7]], 1, the prefactors being smooth in the spin basis):
>
> $$
> [a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf q,\lambda'}] = \frac{(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)}{2E_{\mathbf p}}\Bigl(\varepsilon^{i*}(p, \lambda)W^i(p, \lambda') + W^{i*}(p, \lambda)\varepsilon^i(p, \lambda')\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **4. The polarization factor.** Transversality gives $\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon = E_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^0$ (and its conjugate). Then
>
> $$
> \varepsilon^{i*}(\lambda)W^i(\lambda') = E_{\mathbf p}\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon^*(\lambda)\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon(\lambda') - (\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon^*(\lambda))\varepsilon^0(\lambda') = E_{\mathbf p}\bigl(\boldsymbol\varepsilon^*(\lambda)\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon(\lambda') - \varepsilon^{0*}(\lambda)\varepsilon^0(\lambda')\bigr) = -E_{\mathbf p}\,\varepsilon^*(\lambda)\cdot\varepsilon(\lambda') = E_{\mathbf p}\delta_{\lambda\lambda'} ,
> $$
>
> using orthonormality ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]]); likewise $W^{i*}(\lambda)\varepsilon^i(\lambda') = E_{\mathbf p}\boldsymbol\varepsilon^*\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon' - \varepsilon^{0*}(\lambda)(\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon(\lambda')) = E_{\mathbf p}\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}$. The bracket is $2E_{\mathbf p}\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}$, and $[a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf q,\lambda'}] = (2\pi)^3\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ (Yu (4.149)–(4.152)). ⚑ By-product: at $\mathbf q = \mathbf p$, $\lambda' = \lambda$ this is $(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf 0)$, the volume, once per polarization → [[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-3|Theorem §C4.5.3]]. For the helicity basis, whose vectors are discontinuous on the $z$-axis, read steps 3–4 in the spin basis and transform with the unitary $U(\mathbf p)$ of [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^der-c4-3-8c|Derivation §C4.3.8, third route]]: $a_{\mathbf p,\lambda} = \sum_j\overline{U_{j\lambda}}\,a_{\mathbf p,j}$, so $[a_\lambda, a^\dagger_{\lambda'}] = (U^\dagger U)_{\lambda\lambda'}(2\pi)^3\delta^3 = \delta_{\lambda\lambda'}(2\pi)^3\delta^3$.
>
> **5. Two annihilators.** $a_{\mathbf q,\lambda'} = \frac1{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf q}}}\int d^3y\,e^{iq\cdot y}\bigl(-i\varepsilon^{j\ast}(q, \lambda')\pi^j + W^{j\ast}(q, \lambda')A^j\bigr)$. The two surviving terms are $[-i\varepsilon^{i\ast}\pi^i(\mathbf x), W^{j\ast}A^j(\mathbf y)] = \varepsilon^{i\ast}(p, \lambda)W^{i\ast}(q, \lambda')\delta^3$ and $[W^{i\ast}A^i(\mathbf x), -i\varepsilon^{j\ast}\pi^j(\mathbf y)] = -W^{i\ast}(p, \lambda)\varepsilon^{i\ast}(q, \lambda')\delta^3$. The $\mathbf x$ integral of $e^{i(p + q)\cdot x}$ gives $e^{i(E_{\mathbf p} + E_{\mathbf q})t}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p + \mathbf q)$, whose support is $\mathbf q = -\mathbf p$:
>
> $$
> [a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}, a_{\mathbf q,\lambda'}] = \frac{(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p + \mathbf q)\,e^{2iE_{\mathbf p}t}}{2E_{\mathbf p}}\,B, \qquad B = \varepsilon^{i*}(p, \lambda)W^{i*}(-p, \lambda') - W^{i*}(p, \lambda)\varepsilon^{i*}(-p, \lambda') .
> $$
>
> **6. The polarization factor vanishes pointwise.** $W^i(-p, \lambda') = E_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^i(-p, \lambda') + p^i\varepsilon^0(-p, \lambda')$ (the spatial momentum of $-p$ is $-\mathbf p$), and transversality at $-p$ reads $E_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^0(-p) + \mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon(-p) = 0$. First term: $E_{\mathbf p}\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\ast}(p, \lambda)\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\ast}(-p, \lambda') + (\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\ast}(p, \lambda))\varepsilon^{0\ast}(-p, \lambda') = E_{\mathbf p}\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\ast}\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon'^{\ast} + E_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^{0\ast}(p, \lambda)\varepsilon^{0\ast}(-p, \lambda')$. Second term: $E_{\mathbf p}\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\ast}\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon'^{\ast} - \varepsilon^{0\ast}(p, \lambda)(\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\ast}(-p, \lambda')) = E_{\mathbf p}\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\ast}\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon'^{\ast} + E_{\mathbf p}\varepsilon^{0\ast}(p, \lambda)\varepsilon^{0\ast}(-p, \lambda')$. So $B = 0$ for every $\mathbf p$, $\lambda$, $\lambda'$, and $[a, a] = 0$. ⚑ By-product: the time-dependent phase $e^{2iE_{\mathbf p}t}$ is harmless only because $B$ vanishes; the user's notes add a second argument — a $t$-dependent c-number cannot equal the $t$-independent commutator of the $t$-independent mode operators unless it is zero. Yu instead uses the relabelling identity (4.159), obtained by $\mathbf p \leftrightarrow -\mathbf p$, $\lambda \leftrightarrow \lambda'$; the pointwise computation needs no such identity.
>
> **7. Two creators.** $[a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,\lambda}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf q,\lambda'}] = -[a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}, a_{\mathbf q,\lambda'}]^\dagger = 0$, as in [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^der-c2a-2-6|Derivation §C2a.2.6]], step 7.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The commutator of two extracted operators is a polarization contraction times the scalar delta; the contraction is $-\varepsilon^*\cdot\varepsilon' = \delta_{\lambda\lambda'}$, so the indefinite Minkowski product of polarization vectors becomes a positive-definite oscillator algebra: two minus signs (the metric on $p^\perp$, and $\pi^j = -\pi_j$) cancel.
> - Only equal-time relations and the two constraints were used; the slice drops out.
> - Used next: $H$ and $\mathbf P$ in modes, and the positive norms of the states ([[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-6|Theorem §C4.5.6]]).

> [!derivation]- Derivation (second route, ⇐: from the mode algebra back to the fields, by the polarization sum)
> **1. Expand.** With the expansions of [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-5|Theorem §C4.4.5]] (label $\mathbf p$ for $A^i(x)$, $\mathbf q$ for $\pi^j(y)$, equal times) and the mode algebra, only $[a, a^\dagger]$ and $[a^\dagger, a] = -[a, a^\dagger]$ survive. The coefficient of $a_{\mathbf p,\lambda}$ in $A^i$ is $\varepsilon^i(p, \lambda)$ and that of $a^\dagger_{\mathbf q,\lambda'}$ in $\pi^j$ is $-iW^{j\ast}(q, \lambda')$; the coefficient of $a^\dagger_{\mathbf p,\lambda}$ in $A^i$ is $\varepsilon^{i\ast}$ and that of $a_{\mathbf q,\lambda'}$ in $\pi^j$ is $iW^j$. After the deltas set $\mathbf q = \mathbf p$, $\lambda' = \lambda$:
>
> $$
> [A^i(x), \pi^j(y)] = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{2E_{\mathbf p}}\sum_\lambda\Bigl(-i\,\varepsilon^iW^{j*}\,e^{-ip\cdot(x - y)} - i\,\varepsilon^{i*}W^j\,e^{ip\cdot(x - y)}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **2. The polarization sums.** With [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-8|Theorem §C4.3.8]], $g^{ij} = -\delta^{ij}$, $g^{i0} = 0$, $p^0 = E_{\mathbf p}$:
>
> $$
> \sum_\lambda\varepsilon^iW^{j*} = E_{\mathbf p}\sum_\lambda\varepsilon^i\varepsilon^{j*} - p^j\sum_\lambda\varepsilon^i\varepsilon^{0*} = E_{\mathbf p}\Bigl(\delta^{ij} + \frac{p^ip^j}{m^2}\Bigr) - p^j\,\frac{p^iE_{\mathbf p}}{m^2} = E_{\mathbf p}\,\delta^{ij} ,
> $$
>
> and the same for $\sum_\lambda\varepsilon^{i*}W^j$ (the sums are real). ⚑ By-product: the $p^ip^j/m^2$ parts cancel between the two terms of $W$: the longitudinal growth of [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-10|Theorem §C4.3.10]] never reaches the canonical commutator.
>
> **3. Equal times.** $[A^i(\mathbf x), \pi^j(\mathbf y)] = -\frac i2\delta^{ij}\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\bigl(e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r} + e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r}\bigr) = -\frac i2\delta^{ij}\cdot2\delta^3(\mathbf r) = -i\delta^{ij}\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$, $\mathbf r = \mathbf x - \mathbf y$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-4|Theorem §CA.3.4]]; an identity in $\mathcal S'$, [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-3|Theorem §CA.3.3]]): Principle §C4.4.2.
>
> **4. The other two.** $[A^i, A^j]$ has the kernel $\frac1{2E_{\mathbf p}}\bigl(\delta^{ij} + \frac{p^ip^j}{m^2}\bigr)\bigl(e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r} - e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r}\bigr)$; the coefficient is even in $\mathbf p$, so the substitution $\mathbf p \to -\mathbf p$ in the second term ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-7|Theorem §CA.3.7]], 1) cancels it against the first. $[\pi^i, \pi^j]$ has $\sum_\lambda W^iW^{j\ast} = E_{\mathbf p}^2\bigl(\delta^{ij} + \frac{p^ip^j}{m^2}\bigr) - 2E_{\mathbf p}^2\frac{p^ip^j}{m^2} + p^ip^j\bigl(\frac{E_{\mathbf p}^2}{m^2} - 1\bigr) = E_{\mathbf p}^2\delta^{ij} - p^ip^j$, again even in $\mathbf p$: zero.
>
> **5. The constrained component.** The same computation for $[A^0(x), A^j(y)]$ has the sum $\sum_\lambda\varepsilon^0\varepsilon^{j\ast} = -g^{0j} + p^0p^j/m^2 = E_{\mathbf p}p^j/m^2$ (real), so at equal times $[A^0, A^j] = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{p^j}{2m^2}\bigl(e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r} - e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r}\bigr)$. With $\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}p^je^{\pm i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r} = \mp i\,\partial_j\delta^3(\mathbf r)$ (differentiating the plane-wave delta, [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-4|Theorem §CA.3.4]]) this is $\frac{1}{2m^2}(-2i)\partial_j\delta^3(\mathbf r) = -\frac{i}{m^2}\partial_j\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$: [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Proca Field꞉ Modes and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-4-3|Theorem §C4.4.3]], recovered from the modes. Its non-vanishing is the $p^0p^j/m^2$ entry of the projector ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-9|Theorem §C4.3.9]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The polarization sum is the canonical commutator in disguise: completeness of the three polarizations is what makes $[A^i, \pi^j]$ a pure $\delta^3$, the vector version of the Dirac spin sums reproducing the anticommutator ([[§C5b.2 Mode Expansion and the Anticommutator Algebra#^der-c5b-2-4b|Derivation §C5b.2.4, second route]]).
> - Had the timelike vector been included, $\sum_\sigma g_{\sigma\sigma}e\,e = g$ would have appeared instead, and with it the indefinite metric of the covariant photon ([[§C4.7 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-7-11|Theorem §C4.7.11]]).
