What does a field with one Lorentz index carry, and what must a theory of such a field get rid of? Everything about the vector representation is already at home: its generators ([[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1-6-1|Def. §C1.6.1]]), its label $(\frac12, \frac12)$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and Its Finite-Dimensional Representations#^thm-c3-2-7|Theorem §C3.2.7]]), its rotation content spin $0 \oplus 1$ ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]), the classical field law ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-5|Theorem §C3.3.5]]) and the quantum one ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-4-8|Principle §C3.4.8]], [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-10|Theorem §C3.4.10]]); this note recalls them in one place (Lecture 7 material) and adds only what is specific to vector fields: the helicity matrix, and the covariant split of a four-vector by a momentum into the spin-0 direction that every vector theory must remove and the spin-1 (massive) or helicity-$\pm1$ (massless) directions it keeps. The particle side is [[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group|§C3.5★]] and [[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity|§C3.6★]]; the theories are the Proca field ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field|§C4.2★]]) and the Maxwell field ([[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing|§C4.5]]).

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): active transformations, $\Lambda = \exp(-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$; the $4\times4$ rotation generators of the vector representation are written $\mathcal J^i = \frac12\varepsilon^{ijk}\mathcal J^{jk}$ (the matrices $J_i$ of [[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1-6-2|Def. §C1.6.2]], renamed so that $\mathbf J$ stays the Hilbert-space angular momentum, as in Yu).

## The transformation law, recalled

> [!remark] Remark: The quantum vector field, recalled
> A vector field is a field whose index carries $D(\Lambda) = \Lambda$ ([[§C1.8 Scalar Field Theory꞉ Fields, Locality and Symmetry#^ex-c1-8-1|Example §C1.8.1]], [[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-5|Theorem §C3.3.5]]). As a quantum field it obeys the covariance law of [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-4-8|Principle §C3.4.8]] with that $D$, and infinitesimally [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-10|Theorem §C3.4.10]]:
>
> $$
> U^{-1}(\Lambda)\,A^\mu(x)\,U(\Lambda) = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,A^\nu(\Lambda^{-1}x), \qquad [A^\mu(x), J^{\rho\sigma}] = \hat L^{\rho\sigma}A^\mu(x) + (\mathcal J^{\rho\sigma})^\mu{}_\nu\,A^\nu(x) ,
> $$
>
> with $\hat L^{\rho\sigma} = i(x^\rho\partial^\sigma - x^\sigma\partial^\rho)$ ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]). In three-dimensional form $[A^\mu(x), \mathbf J] = -i\,\mathbf x\times\nabla A^\mu(x) + \boldsymbol{\mathcal J}^\mu{}_\nu A^\nu(x)$: orbital angular momentum plus spin. The spin matrices vanish on the time row and column and are $(\mathcal J^k)^l{}_m = -i\varepsilon_{klm}$ on the spatial block, so $\boldsymbol{\mathcal J}^2 = \operatorname{diag}(0, 2, 2, 2)$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and Its Finite-Dimensional Representations#^thm-c3-2-7|Theorem §C3.2.7]]): $A^0$ is spin $0$ and $\mathbf A$ spin $1$ under rotations ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]), while boosts mix them, so the four components form one irreducible $(\frac12, \frac12)$. One object, three actions: $J^{\rho\sigma}$ acts on $A^\mu(x)$ as an operator on the Hilbert space, $\hat L^{\rho\sigma}$ on its argument, $\mathcal J^{\rho\sigma}$ on its index (Yu's "three identities").
>
> *Layer.* For the vector fields of this chapter the covariance law is assumed, as for every field of the course ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-4-8|Principle §C3.4.8]], domain); for the free scalar field it was derived ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-12|Theorem §C3.4.12]]), and the same construction for the Proca field is not carried out here. What the law gives for the Proca quanta is derived in [[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Massive Vector Field|§C4.4★]].
>
> *Source: Yu §4.2.2, eqs. (4.28)–(4.43) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.2 (eq. (vector-J); Keypoint "The vector field has spin 1") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.3 (Derivation "The spin content of a four-vector"), §7.5*

## The helicity matrix

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.1: The Helicity Matrix of the Vector Representation
> For a unit vector $\hat{\mathbf n}$, the $4\times4$ matrix $\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal J}$ (spin matrices of [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^rem-c4-1-1|Remark: The quantum vector field, recalled]]) is
>
> $$
> \hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal J} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -in^3 & in^2 \\ 0 & in^3 & 0 & -in^1 \\ 0 & -in^2 & in^1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad (\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal J})(v^0, \mathbf v) = (0,\ i\,\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf v) .
> $$
>
> It is Hermitian. For a right-handed orthonormal triad $(\mathbf e_1, \mathbf e_2, \hat{\mathbf n})$ its eigenvectors are $\varepsilon_\pm = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(0, \mathbf e_1 \pm i\mathbf e_2)$ with eigenvalues $\pm1$, and $(1, \mathbf 0)$, $(0, \hat{\mathbf n})$ with eigenvalue $0$. The active rotation by $\theta$ about $\hat{\mathbf n}$, $R(\hat{\mathbf n}, \theta) = e^{-i\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal J}}$ ([[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1-6-3|Theorem §C1.6.3]], 3), multiplies $\varepsilon_\pm$ by $e^{\mp i\theta}$ and fixes the other two.
>
> *Source: Yu §4.3.1, eq. (4.103) · the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 (Note "Checking the helicity eigenvalue equation"); the eigenvalue statement for a general triad and the rotation written out here*

^thm-c4-1-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The entries.** $(\mathcal J^k)^\mu{}_\nu$ is zero if $\mu = 0$ or $\nu = 0$, and $(\mathcal J^k)^l{}_m = -i\varepsilon_{klm}$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and Its Finite-Dimensional Representations#^thm-c3-2-7|Theorem §C3.2.7]], step 2). So $(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal J})^l{}_m = -i\,n^k\varepsilon_{klm}$. Row by row: $(l, m) = (1, 2)$: $-in^3\varepsilon_{312} = -in^3$; $(1, 3)$: $-in^2\varepsilon_{213} = +in^2$; $(2, 1)$: $-in^3\varepsilon_{321} = +in^3$; $(2, 3)$: $-in^1\varepsilon_{123} = -in^1$; $(3, 1)$: $-in^2\varepsilon_{231} = -in^2$; $(3, 2)$: $-in^1\varepsilon_{132} = +in^1$; the diagonal is zero because $\varepsilon_{kll} = 0$. This is the displayed matrix (Yu (4.103) with $\hat{\mathbf n} = \hat{\mathbf p}$).
>
> **2. As a cross product.** $(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal J}\,v)^l = -i\,n^k\varepsilon_{klm}v^m$. Since $(\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf v)^l = \varepsilon_{lkm}n^kv^m = -\varepsilon_{klm}n^kv^m$ (one transposition of indices), $(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal J}\,v)^l = i(\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf v)^l$; the time component of the image is $0$.
>
> **3. Hermitian.** Each entry $-in^k\varepsilon_{klm}$ is purely imaginary ($n^k$ real) and antisymmetric in $(l, m)$, so the complex conjugate of the transpose is $\overline{-in^k\varepsilon_{kml}} = in^k\varepsilon_{kml} = -in^k\varepsilon_{klm}$: the matrix equals its adjoint.
>
> **4. The cross products in the triad.** Right-handed means $\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_2 = \hat{\mathbf n}$, hence (cyclically) $\mathbf e_2\times\hat{\mathbf n} = \mathbf e_1$ and $\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf e_1 = \mathbf e_2$; so $\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf e_1 = \mathbf e_2$ and $\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf e_2 = -\mathbf e_1$, and $\hat{\mathbf n}\times\hat{\mathbf n} = 0$.
>
> **5. Eigenvectors.** By step 4, $\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf e_1 \pm i\mathbf e_2) = \mathbf e_2 \mp i\mathbf e_1$. For the upper sign, $\mathbf e_2 - i\mathbf e_1 = -i(\mathbf e_1 + i\mathbf e_2)$; for the lower, $\mathbf e_2 + i\mathbf e_1 = +i(\mathbf e_1 - i\mathbf e_2)$. So $\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf e_1 \pm i\mathbf e_2) = \mp i\,(\mathbf e_1 \pm i\mathbf e_2)$, and by step 2 $(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal J})\varepsilon_\pm = i(\mp i)\varepsilon_\pm = \pm\varepsilon_\pm$. Further $(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal J})(0, \hat{\mathbf n}) = (0, i\hat{\mathbf n}\times\hat{\mathbf n}) = 0$, and $(1, \mathbf 0)$ is sent to $0$ by the zero time column. The four vectors are linearly independent (two lie in the span of $\mathbf e_1$, $\mathbf e_2$, one is $\hat{\mathbf n}$, one is time), so the spectrum is $\{+1, -1, 0, 0\}$.
>
> **6. The finite rotation.** On an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$ the exponential series gives $e^{-i\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal J}}v = \sum_k\frac{(-i\theta\lambda)^k}{k!}v = e^{-i\lambda\theta}v$: $e^{\mp i\theta}$ on $\varepsilon_\pm$, $1$ on the other two. That $e^{-i\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal J}}$ is the active rotation by $\theta$ about $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is [[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1-6-3|Theorem §C1.6.3]], 3, for $\hat{\mathbf n}$ along an axis, and for general $\hat{\mathbf n}$ follows by conjugating with a rotation taking $\hat{\mathbf z}$ to $\hat{\mathbf n}$ (conjugation passes through the exponential series, as in [[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^der-c3-5-9|Derivation §C3.5.9]], step 3). Check with $\hat{\mathbf n} = \hat{\mathbf z}$, $\mathbf e_1 = \hat{\mathbf x}$, $\mathbf e_2 = \hat{\mathbf y}$: the active rotation sends $\hat{\mathbf x} \pm i\hat{\mathbf y}$ to $e^{\mp i\theta}(\hat{\mathbf x} \pm i\hat{\mathbf y})$ ([[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^der-c3-6-8|Derivation §C3.6.8]], step 2).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - On the spatial block the helicity matrix is $i\,\hat{\mathbf n}\times$; circular combinations of the two directions transverse to $\hat{\mathbf n}$ have helicity $\pm1$, the direction $\hat{\mathbf n}$ itself has $0$. ⚑ By-product: the eigenvalue $0$ is doubly degenerate, once on the spatial vector $\hat{\mathbf n}$ (the longitudinal spin-1 state) and once on the time direction (the spin-0 component) → [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]] separates them covariantly.
> - The convention $\varepsilon_\pm = (\mathbf e_1 \pm i\mathbf e_2)/\sqrt2$ carries no Condon–Shortley sign; the spherical basis of Quantum Mechanics has $\mp(\mathbf e_1 \pm i\mathbf e_2)/\sqrt2$ ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-3|QM Theorem §C5.3.3]]).
> - Used next: the helicity polarization vectors ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion|§C4.3★]]) and the helicity of the Proca quanta ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Massive Vector Field|§C4.4★]]).

## What a momentum does to a four-vector

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.2: A Timelike Momentum Splits a Four-Vector into Spin 0 and Spin 1
> Let $p^2 = m^2 > 0$, $p^0 > 0$, and $p^\perp = \{v \in \mathbb C^4 : p\cdot v = 0\}$. Then
> 1. $\mathbb C^4 = \mathbb Cp \oplus p^\perp$, and $-v^*\cdot v > 0$ for every nonzero $v \in p^\perp$;
> 2. the little group $G_p$ of $p$ ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]]; $\cong SO(3)$, [[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-7|Theorem §C3.5.7]]) maps both pieces to themselves, fixes $p$ (spin $0$) and acts on $p^\perp$ as the three-dimensional rotation group on $\mathbb C^3$ (spin $1$);
> 3. a plane wave $A^\mu = \varepsilon^\mu e^{-ip\cdot x}$ obeys $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$ if and only if $\varepsilon \in p^\perp$.
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 ("the $s = 0$ piece identified in the keypoint above is precisely what the constraint removes"; "Only three physical polarizations") · Yu §4.3.1, eqs. (4.85)–(4.89) · the covariant split stated and proved here*

^thm-c4-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The rest frame.** Let $L(p)$ be the pure boost with $L(p)k = p$, $k = (m, \mathbf 0)$ ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-9|Theorem §C3.5.9]]). For $k$: $k\cdot v = mv^0$, so $k^\perp = \{(0, \mathbf v)\}$, and $-v^*\cdot v = -(|v^0|^2 - |\mathbf v|^2) = |\mathbf v|^2 > 0$ for $v = (0, \mathbf v) \ne 0$. Every $v = (v^0, \mathbf v)$ is $\frac{v^0}{m}k + (0, \mathbf v)$, uniquely: $\mathbb C^4 = \mathbb Ck \oplus k^\perp$.
>
> **2. Transport to $p$.** $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$ preserves the Minkowski product of complex vectors ($\Lambda$ is real and $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}g\Lambda = g$, [[§C1.4 The Lorentz Group#^def-c1-4-1|Def. §C1.4.1]]), so $L(p)$ maps $k^\perp$ onto $p^\perp$ ($p\cdot L(p)w = L(p)k\cdot L(p)w = k\cdot w$) and preserves $v^*\cdot v$. Applying $L(p)$ to the decomposition of step 1 gives part 1.
>
> **3. The little group at rest.** $G_k$ consists of the rotations $R$ ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-7|Theorem §C3.5.7]]), which act on $(v^0, \mathbf v)$ as $(v^0, R\mathbf v)$: they fix $k$, map $k^\perp$ to itself, and on $k^\perp \cong \mathbb C^3$ act as the defining representation of $SO(3)$, spin $1$ ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]).
>
> **4. The little group at $p$.** $G_p = L(p)G_kL(p)^{-1}$ ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-6|Theorem §C3.5.6]], step 3). For $W = L(p)RL(p)^{-1}$: $Wp = L(p)Rk = p$, and $W$ maps $p^\perp = L(p)k^\perp$ to $L(p)Rk^\perp = p^\perp$, acting there as $R$ does on $k^\perp$, transported by $L(p)$: an equivalent representation, again spin $1$. This is part 2.
>
> **5. The plane wave.** $\partial_\mu e^{-ip\cdot x} = -ip_\mu e^{-ip\cdot x}$, so $\partial_\mu(\varepsilon^\mu e^{-ip\cdot x}) = -i(p\cdot\varepsilon)e^{-ip\cdot x}$, which vanishes identically iff $p\cdot\varepsilon = 0$. This is part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The rotation split $A^0 \oplus \mathbf A$ of [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^rem-c4-1-1|Remark: The quantum vector field, recalled]] is frame dependent; for a given momentum the covariant split is "along $p$" plus "Minkowski-orthogonal to $p$", which reduces to it in the rest frame.
> - ⚑ By-product: the spin-0 direction of a plane wave is $\varepsilon \propto p$, the "timelike polarization", and the condition that removes it is the Lorenz condition; for the Proca field it is a consequence of the field equation → [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]], and the remaining three directions are the physical polarizations → [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]].
> - The sign $-v^*\cdot v > 0$ is the positivity that makes the Proca quanta have positive norm ([[§C4.4★ Quantizing the Massive Vector Field#^thm-c4-4-9|Theorem §C4.4.9]]); on $\mathbb Cp$ the sign is the opposite one, $p\cdot p > 0$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.3: The Transverse Space of a Lightlike Momentum Contains the Momentum
> Let $k^2 = 0$, $k^0 > 0$, and $k^\perp = \{v \in \mathbb C^4 : k\cdot v = 0\}$. Then $k^\perp$ is three-dimensional and contains $k$; on it $-v^*\cdot v \ge 0$, with equality exactly on $\mathbb Ck$. So no covariant condition $k\cdot\varepsilon = 0$ can remove the direction of $k$: the space with positive norm is the two-dimensional quotient $k^\perp/\mathbb Ck$, on which rotations about $\hat{\mathbf k}$ act with helicities $\pm1$ ([[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-6-8|Theorem §C3.6.8]]).
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §4.3 (Note "Massive versus massless: three versus two polarizations") · computed here*

^thm-c4-1-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Choose the frame.** Rotate so that $k = \kappa(1, 0, 0, 1)$, $\kappa > 0$ (a rotation preserves the Minkowski product, so the statements are frame independent, as in [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^der-c4-1-2|Derivation §C4.1.2]], step 2).
>
> **2. Solve the condition.** $k\cdot v = \kappa(v^0 - v^3) = 0$ iff $v^3 = v^0$, so $k^\perp = \{(a, b, c, a) : a, b, c \in \mathbb C\}$, three-dimensional, and $k$ (with $a = \kappa$, $b = c = 0$) lies in it because $k\cdot k = 0$.
>
> **3. The norm on it.** For $v = (a, b, c, a)$: $v^*\cdot v = |a|^2 - |b|^2 - |c|^2 - |a|^2 = -(|b|^2 + |c|^2)$. So $-v^*\cdot v = |b|^2 + |c|^2 \ge 0$, zero iff $b = c = 0$, i.e. iff $v = (a/\kappa)\,k$.
>
> **4. The quotient.** Two elements of $k^\perp$ differing by a multiple of $k$ have the same $b$, $c$, so $-v^*\cdot v$ is a positive-definite form on $k^\perp/\mathbb Ck \cong \{(0, b, c, 0)\}$, spanned by $\varepsilon(k, \pm) = \frac1{\sqrt2}(0, 1, \pm i, 0)$, the helicity $\pm1$ vectors of [[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-6-8|Theorem §C3.6.8]], 1 (and of [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-1|Theorem §C4.1.1]] with $\hat{\mathbf n} = \hat{\mathbf z}$).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - For $m > 0$ the transversality condition removes one of four directions and leaves a positive space; for $m = 0$ it removes one and leaves a null direction, $k$ itself, which only an identification $\varepsilon \sim \varepsilon + ck$ can remove. ⚑ By-product: that identification is the gauge freedom $A^\mu \to A^\mu + \partial^\mu\Lambda$ on plane waves → [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-5-1|Def. §C4.5.1]]; the little-group translations shift polarization vectors by exactly such multiples of $k$ ([[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-6-8|Theorem §C3.6.8]], 2).
> - The degenerate norm on $k^\perp$ is the classical shadow of the zero-norm states of covariant photon quantization ([[§C4.7 The Gupta–Bleuler Condition and Physical Photons#^thm-c4-7-6|Theorem §C4.7.6]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Four components, three or two states
> A vector field has $(2j_+ + 1)(2j_- + 1) = 4$ components ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and Its Finite-Dimensional Representations#^thm-c3-2-7|Theorem §C3.2.7]]); a massive spin-1 particle has $3$ states per momentum ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-8|Theorem §C3.5.8]]), a massless helicity-$\pm1$ particle $2$ ([[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-6-6|Theorem §C3.6.6]]). The bookkeeping is $4 = 1 + 3$ and $4 = 1 + 1 + 2$:
> - **Massive** ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]]): the spin-0 direction $\varepsilon \propto p$ is removed by $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$, which the Proca equation implies ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]); in canonical terms $A^0$ has no momentum and is fixed by a constraint ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-7|Theorem §C4.2.7]]), leaving three canonical pairs ([[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-12|Theorem §C4.2.12]]) and three polarizations ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]]).
> - **Massless** ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]): the Lorenz condition removes one direction and gauge invariance identifies another, $\varepsilon \sim \varepsilon + ck$ ([[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^rem-c3-6-3|§C3.6★, Remark: Two polarizations, and why a massless vector field needs gauge invariance]]; [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-10|Theorem §C4.5.10]], [[§C4.7 The Gupta–Bleuler Condition and Physical Photons#^pr-c4-7-1|Principle §C4.7.1]]); classically this is the count of two degrees of freedom per point of electrodynamics ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-4|EM Theorem §C1.4.4]]).
>
> The general principle is [[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^rem-c3-5-5|§C3.5★, Remark: What a field must contain]]: the mismatch between the components of a field and the states of its particle is why field equations carry constraints. The Dirac field meets the same mismatch with the spin $\frac12$ contained twice ([[§C5.5 Plane-Wave Solutions#^rem-c5-5-2|§C5.5, Remark: Two of each kind, and what they will describe]]).
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §4 (introduction: "the mismatch between the four components of $A^\mu$ and the two or three physical polarizations is where all the new structure … comes from"), §4.3 · Yu §4.3, after eq. (4.57); §4.3.1, after eq. (4.89)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The four-vector is irreducible under the Lorentz group but splits under the little group of a momentum, exactly as it splits into $A^0 \oplus \mathbf A$ under the rotation group of a fixed frame ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]); [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]] is that split made frame-independent by choosing the frame of the particle — [[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]].
> - The helicity matrix $i\,\hat{\mathbf n}\times$ is the angular-momentum operator of a classical vector field's polarization: circular polarizations of light are its eigenvectors, and the eigenvalue $\pm1$ is the photon's spin along its momentum — [[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^def-c3-6-1|Def. §C3.6.1]], [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^ex-c5-1-2|QM Example §C5.1.2]].
> - The spinor analogue of the helicity matrix is $\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf S$ on Dirac spinors, with eigenvalues $\pm\frac12$; the helicity bases of the two fields are built the same way, by diagonalizing the spin along $\hat{\mathbf p}$ — [[§C5.6 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^def-c5-6-2|Def. §C5.6.2]], [[§C5.6 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^thm-c5-6-11|Theorem §C5.6.11]].
> - The null direction in $k^\perp$ and the identification $\varepsilon \sim \varepsilon + ck$ are the plane-wave form of the residual gauge freedom of the Lorenz gauge, which classically leaves two transverse polarizations — [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-3|EM Theorem §C1.4.3]], [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-1|EM Theorem §C1.4.1]].
> - The rotation content of $(\frac12, \frac12)$, $\frac12\otimes\frac12 = 0\oplus1$, is the addition of two spins one-half; the spin-0 piece is the singlet — [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and Its Finite-Dimensional Representations#^thm-c3-2-8|Theorem §C3.2.8]], [[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^rem-c7-1-2|QM Remark: Reducible and irreducible, checked for two spins one-half]].
> - Linear algebra: the split $\mathbb C^4 = \mathbb Cp \oplus p^\perp$ is the orthogonal decomposition with respect to a nondegenerate form; for a null vector the decomposition fails because $p \in p^\perp$, the familiar degeneracy of isotropic vectors — [[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and the Plane-Wave Expansion#^thm-c4-3-9|Theorem §C4.3.9]] is the projector of the massive case, and $1/m^2$ in it is where the massless limit fails.
