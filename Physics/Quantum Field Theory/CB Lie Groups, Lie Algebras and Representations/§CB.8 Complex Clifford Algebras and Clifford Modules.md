Why do the Dirac matrices have to be $4\times4$, and why are any two sets of them conjugate? After complexification every Clifford algebra of an $n$-dimensional nondegenerate space is one complex algebra $\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)$, and that algebra is a full matrix algebra ($n$ even) or a sum of two ($n$ odd). A matrix algebra has exactly one irreducible module, which gives the dimension $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ of spinors and Pauli's fundamental theorem as corollaries. The section builds on [[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element|§CB.7]] and on Schur and Burnside ([[§CB.3 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^thm-cb-3-8|Theorem §CB.3.8]], [[§CB.3 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^thm-cb-3-9|Theorem §CB.3.9]]); the course's statements (Pauli's theorem, uniqueness of spinor space, the $4\times4$ bound) are shown as embeds at the points where they become corollaries.

## Complexification

> [!definition] Definition §CB.8.1: Complexification of a Real Algebra
> The **complexification** of a real associative algebra $A$ ([[§CB.3 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^def-cb-3-5|Def. §CB.3.5]]) is the complex vector space $A_{\mathbb C}$ ([[§CB.2 Real Lie Algebras, the Physicists' i and Complexification#^def-cb-2-2|Def. §CB.2.2]]) with the complex-bilinear product $(a + ib)(a' + ib') = (aa' - bb') + i(ab' + ba')$. A representation of $A$ on a complex space extends uniquely to a complex-linear representation of $A_{\mathbb C}$, and conversely, with the same invariant subspaces and intertwiners (as in [[§CB.2 Real Lie Algebras, the Physicists' i and Complexification#^thm-cb-2-6|Theorem §CB.2.6]]).
>

> [!definition] Definition §CB.8.2: Complex Clifford Algebra
> $\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C) = \mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb C^n, z_1^2 + \cdots + z_n^2)$ ([[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^def-cb-7-6|Def. §CB.7.6]] over $\mathbb K = \mathbb C$): generated by $e_1, \dots, e_n$ with $e_ie_j + e_je_i = 2\delta_{ij}$.
>
> *Source: Woit, §28.1 (definition of $\mathrm{Cliff}(n, \mathbb C)$ by $[\gamma_j, \gamma_k]_+ = 2\delta_{jk}$)*

^def-cb-8-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §CB.8.3: Every Real Clifford Algebra Complexifies to Clₙ(ℂ)
> For every nondegenerate real quadratic space $(V, q)$ of dimension $n$, $\mathrm{Cl}(V, q)_{\mathbb C} \cong \mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)$ ([[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^def-cb-8-1|Def. §CB.8.1]], [[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^def-cb-8-2|Def. §CB.8.2]]): for an orthonormal basis with $q(e_j) = -1$ for $j > r$, send $e_j \mapsto e_j$ ($j \le r$) and $e_j \mapsto ie_j$ ($j > r$). Hence the complex Clifford modules of $(V, q)$ ([[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^def-cb-7-9|Def. §CB.7.9]]) are the same as the modules of $\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)$; e.g. $\mathrm{Cl}(1,3)_{\mathbb C} \cong \mathrm{Cl}(3,1)_{\mathbb C} \cong \mathrm{Cl}_4(\mathbb C)$.
>
> *Source: Figueroa-O'Farrill, Spin Geometry, Prop. 2.2 and eq. (48) · Woit, §29.1 (the basis change by factors $i$)*

^thm-cb-8-3

> [!proof]- Proof
> *Source: J. Figueroa-O'Farrill, Spin Geometry, §2.2, Prop. 2.2 and eq. (48) (the Clifford functor commutes with complexification; "upon complexification we lose the information about the signature"), proof by universality, surjectivity and a dimension count · P. Woit, Quantum Theory, Groups and Representations, §29.1 (multiply the $s$ negative basis vectors by $i$). Written out here in an orthonormal basis.*
>
> **Step 1** (a Clifford map into the complexification). Let $e_1, \dots, e_n$ be an orthonormal basis of $(V, q)$ with $q(e_j) = +1$ for $j \le r$ and $-1$ for $j > r$, and $\mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb C} = \mathrm{Cl}(V, q)_{\mathbb C}$ ([[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^def-cb-8-1|Def. §CB.8.1]]), a complex algebra. Define the complex-linear map $f : \mathbb C^n \to \mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb C}$ on the standard basis $\varepsilon_j$ by $f(\varepsilon_j) = e_j$ ($j \le r$), $f(\varepsilon_j) = ie_j$ ($j > r$). Then $f(\varepsilon_j)^2 = q(e_j) = 1$ ($j \le r$), $f(\varepsilon_j)^2 = i^2q(e_j) = (-1)(-1) = 1$ ($j > r$), and distinct $f(\varepsilon_j)$ anticommute ([[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-8|Theorem §CB.7.8]]). For $z = \sum z^j\varepsilon_j$, expanding into all terms,
>
> $$
> f(z)^2 = \sum_j(z^j)^2f(\varepsilon_j)^2 + \sum_{j<k}z^jz^k\bigl(f(\varepsilon_j)f(\varepsilon_k) + f(\varepsilon_k)f(\varepsilon_j)\bigr) = \sum_j(z^j)^2 .
> $$
>
> By [[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-7|Theorem §CB.7.7]] over $\mathbb K = \mathbb C$, $f$ extends to a homomorphism of complex algebras $\Phi : \mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C) \to \mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb C}$ ([[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^def-cb-8-2|Def. §CB.8.2]]).
>
> **Step 2** (surjective). The image is a complex subalgebra containing every $e_j$ ($= f(\varepsilon_j)$ or $-if(\varepsilon_j)$), hence every product $e_I$. The $e_I$ are a real basis of $\mathrm{Cl}(V, q)$ ([[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-16|Theorem §CB.7.16]]), hence a complex basis of $\mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb C}$ ([[§CB.2 Real Lie Algebras, the Physicists' i and Complexification#^def-cb-2-2|Def. §CB.2.2]]); so $\Phi$ is onto.
>
> **Step 3** (bijective). $\dim_{\mathbb C}\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C) = 2^n$ (Theorem §CB.7.16 over $\mathbb C$) and $\dim_{\mathbb C}\mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb C} = \dim_{\mathbb R}\mathrm{Cl}(V, q) = 2^n$. A surjective linear map between spaces of equal finite dimension is injective, so $\Phi$ is an isomorphism.
>
> **Step 4** (modules). A complex Clifford module $\gamma : \mathrm{Cl}(V, q) \to \operatorname{End}_{\mathbb C}(W)$ ([[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^def-cb-7-9|Def. §CB.7.9]]) extends to $\gamma_{\mathbb C}(a + ib) = \gamma(a) + i\gamma(b)$, which is complex-linear and multiplicative: $\gamma_{\mathbb C}\bigl((a + ib)(a' + ib')\bigr) = \gamma(aa' - bb') + i\gamma(ab' + ba') = (\gamma(a) + i\gamma(b))(\gamma(a') + i\gamma(b'))$. Conversely a complex-linear module of $\mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb C}$ restricts to one of $\mathrm{Cl}(V, q)$, and the two operations are inverse. A complex subspace is invariant under $\gamma(\mathrm{Cl})$ iff under its complex span $\gamma_{\mathbb C}(\mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb C})$, and likewise for intertwiners. Composing with $\Phi$ identifies these modules with the modules of $\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)$. For $\mathbb R^{1,3}$ and $\mathbb R^{3,1}$, $n = 4$.
>
> **What the proof shows.**
> - ⚑ By-product: the signature is lost after complexification; this is why the complex Dirac matrices of the $(+,-,-,-)$ and $(-,+,+,+)$ conventions differ only by a factor $i$, and why reality questions (Majorana spinors) must be asked of the real algebra ([[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^rem-cb-7-1|§CB.7, ★ Remark: The real classification (not used in the course)]]).
> - Used next: Theorems §CB.8.5–§CB.8.7 reduce every complex module question to $\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)$.

> [!definition] Definition §CB.8.4: Complex Volume Element
> For a nondegenerate $\mathbb R^{r,s}$ with orthonormal basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$ ($n = r + s$), the **complex volume element** is $\omega_{\mathbb C} = i^{\,n(n-1)/2 + s}\,e_1\cdots e_n \in \mathrm{Cl}(V, q)_{\mathbb C}$, normalized so that $\omega_{\mathbb C}^2 = 1$ ([[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-19|Theorem §CB.7.19]], 2). For $\mathbb R^{1,3}$: $\omega_{\mathbb C} = i\,e_0e_1e_2e_3$; the Dirac module $e_\mu \mapsto \gamma^\mu$ sends it to $\gamma^5$, the course's module $e_\mu \mapsto \gamma_\mu$ to $-\gamma^5$ ([[§CB.12 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^cau-cb-12-1|Caution: Two Dirac modules, and the sign of γ⁵]]).

## Structure of the complex Clifford algebras

> [!theorem] Theorem §CB.8.5: The Complex Clifford Algebras Are Matrix Algebras
> 1. $n = 2m$: $\mathrm{Cl}_{2m}(\mathbb C) \cong M_{2^m}(\mathbb C)$.
> 2. $n = 2m + 1$: $\mathrm{Cl}_{2m+1}(\mathbb C) \cong M_{2^m}(\mathbb C)\oplus M_{2^m}(\mathbb C)$; the two summands are $\mathrm{Cl}\cdot\frac12(1 \pm \omega_{\mathbb C})$, with $\omega_{\mathbb C}$ ([[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^def-cb-8-4|Def. §CB.8.4]]) central and acting as $\pm1$ on them.
>
> In particular $\mathrm{Cl}(1,3)_{\mathbb C} \cong M_4(\mathbb C)$, and $\mathrm{Cl}(3,0)_{\mathbb C} \cong M_2(\mathbb C)\oplus M_2(\mathbb C)$.
>
> *Source: Figueroa-O'Farrill, Spin Geometry, Prop. 2.10, Cor. 2.11 · Meinrenken, Clifford Algebras and Lie Groups, Prop. 2.5 · Woit, §28.1 (statement) · the $n = 4$ case: [[§C5a.1 Spinor Space and the Clifford Action#^rem-c5a-1-5|§C5a.1, ★ Remark: The complexified Clifford algebra is the full matrix algebra]]*

^thm-cb-8-5

> [!proof]- Proof
> *Source: J. Figueroa-O'Farrill, Spin Geometry, §2.4, Prop. 2.10 (complex Bott periodicity $C\ell(n+2) \cong C\ell(n)\otimes_{\mathbb C}\mathbb C(2)$, by an explicit Clifford map, surjectivity and dimension) and Cor. 2.11 (induction from $C\ell(0) \cong \mathbb C$, $C\ell(1) \cong \mathbb C\oplus\mathbb C$) · E. Meinrenken, Clifford Algebras and Lie Groups, §2.4, Prop. 2.5 (same result via $\mathrm{Cl}(2m) \cong \operatorname{End}(\Lambda\mathbb C^m)$) · P. Woit, Quantum Theory, Groups and Representations, §28.1 (statement). Here $\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)\otimes M_2(\mathbb C)$ is written as $M_2(\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C))$, $2\times2$ matrices with entries in $\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)$, and the generators are adapted to the sign $vv = +q(v)$; part 2 written here.*
>
> **Step 1** (start). $\mathrm{Cl}_0(\mathbb C) = \mathbb C$ (no generators, $T(0) = \mathbb C$, $I_q = 0$). $\mathrm{Cl}_1(\mathbb C)$ has basis $1, e$ with $e^2 = 1$; $e \mapsto (1, -1)$ is Clifford into $\mathbb C\oplus\mathbb C$, onto since $(1,1)$, $(1,-1)$ span, so $\mathrm{Cl}_1(\mathbb C) \cong \mathbb C\oplus\mathbb C$ (as in Step 1 of the proof of [[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-21|Theorem §CB.7.21]]).
>
> **Step 2** (a Clifford map $\mathbb C^{n+2} \to M_2(\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C))$). Write $\mathbb C^{n+2} = \mathbb C^n\oplus\mathbb C\varepsilon_{n+1}\oplus\mathbb C\varepsilon_{n+2}$ and set, for $x \in \mathbb C^n \subset \mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)$,
>
> $$
> \phi(x) = \begin{pmatrix}x & 0\\ 0 & -x\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \phi(\varepsilon_{n+1}) = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \phi(\varepsilon_{n+2}) = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -i\\ i & 0\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> Squares: $\phi(x)^2 = \operatorname{diag}(x^2, x^2) = q(x)\mathbb 1$; $\phi(\varepsilon_{n+1})^2 = \mathbb 1$; $\phi(\varepsilon_{n+2})^2 = \operatorname{diag}((-i)i, i(-i)) = \mathbb 1$. Anticommutators: $\phi(x)\phi(\varepsilon_{n+1}) = \begin{pmatrix}0 & x\\ -x & 0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\phi(\varepsilon_{n+1})\phi(x) = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -x\\ x & 0\end{pmatrix}$ add to $0$; $\phi(x)\phi(\varepsilon_{n+2}) = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -ix\\ -ix & 0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\phi(\varepsilon_{n+2})\phi(x) = \begin{pmatrix}0 & ix\\ ix & 0\end{pmatrix}$ add to $0$; and $\phi(\varepsilon_{n+1})$, $\phi(\varepsilon_{n+2})$ are $\sigma^1$, $\sigma^2$, which anticommute. Expanding $\phi(x + a\varepsilon_{n+1} + b\varepsilon_{n+2})^2$ into its nine terms, the cross terms cancel in pairs and the result is $(q(x) + a^2 + b^2)\mathbb 1$. By [[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-7|Theorem §CB.7.7]] we get an algebra homomorphism $\Phi : \mathrm{Cl}_{n+2}(\mathbb C) \to M_2(\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C))$.
>
> **Step 3** (surjective). Let $E = \phi(\varepsilon_{n+1})$, $F = \phi(\varepsilon_{n+2})$. Then $EF = \sigma^1\sigma^2 = i\sigma^3$, so $H = -iEF = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$ is in the image, and $\phi(x)H = \operatorname{diag}(x, x)$. The map $a \mapsto \operatorname{diag}(a, a)$ is an algebra homomorphism, so the image contains $\operatorname{diag}(a, a)$ for every $a$ in the algebra generated by $\mathbb C^n$, which is all of $\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)$. It also contains the matrix units $E_{11} = \frac12(\mathbb 1 + H)$, $E_{22} = \frac12(\mathbb 1 - H)$, $E_{12} = EE_{22}$, $E_{21} = EE_{11}$, and
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix}a & b\\ c & d\end{pmatrix} = \operatorname{diag}(a, a)E_{11} + \operatorname{diag}(b, b)E_{12} + \operatorname{diag}(c, c)E_{21} + \operatorname{diag}(d, d)E_{22} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (isomorphism). $\dim M_2(\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)) = 4\cdot2^n = 2^{n+2} = \dim\mathrm{Cl}_{n+2}(\mathbb C)$ ([[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-16|Theorem §CB.7.16]]), so $\Phi$ is an isomorphism: $\mathrm{Cl}_{n+2}(\mathbb C) \cong M_2(\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C))$.
>
> **Step 5** (induction). Block matrices identify $M_2(M_k(\mathbb C)) = M_{2k}(\mathbb C)$, and $M_2(\mathbb C\oplus\mathbb C) = M_2(\mathbb C)\oplus M_2(\mathbb C)$ (take components entrywise). From Steps 1 and 4, $\mathrm{Cl}_{2m}(\mathbb C) \cong M_2(\cdots M_2(\mathbb C)\cdots) = M_{2^m}(\mathbb C)$ and $\mathrm{Cl}_{2m+1}(\mathbb C) \cong M_{2^m}(\mathbb C\oplus\mathbb C) = M_{2^m}(\mathbb C)\oplus M_{2^m}(\mathbb C)$. This is part 1 and the first half of part 2.
>
> **Step 6** (part 2: the central idempotents). Let $n = 2m + 1$ and $\omega_{\mathbb C} = i^{n(n-1)/2}e_1\cdots e_n$ ([[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^def-cb-8-4|Def. §CB.8.4]] with $s = 0$). By [[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-19|Theorem §CB.7.19]], 3 ($n$ odd; the proof is the same over $\mathbb C$) it is central, and by part 2 there $\omega_{\mathbb C}^2 = i^{n(n-1)}(-1)^{n(n-1)/2} = 1$. Put $P_\pm = \frac12(1 \pm \omega_{\mathbb C})$: central, $P_+ + P_- = 1$, $P_\pm^2 = \frac14(1 \pm 2\omega_{\mathbb C} + 1) = P_\pm$, $P_+P_- = \frac14(1 - \omega_{\mathbb C}^2) = 0$, and $\omega_{\mathbb C}P_\pm = \frac12(\omega_{\mathbb C} \pm 1) = \pm P_\pm$. Neither is $0$, since $\omega_{\mathbb C}$ is a multiple of the basis element $e_{\{1, \dots, n\}} \ne e_\varnothing$. Transport them by the isomorphism $\psi$ of Step 5. The centre of $M_N(\mathbb C)$ is $\mathbb C\mathbb 1$: if $XE_{ab} = E_{ab}X$, comparing entries $(i, b)$ and $(a, j)$ gives $X_{ia} = \delta_{ia}X_{bb}$; so the centre of $M_N\oplus M_N$ is $\{(\lambda, \mu)\}$, whose idempotents have $\lambda, \mu \in \{0, 1\}$. Two nonzero central idempotents with sum $(1, 1)$ and product $0$ must be $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$. So $\psi(\mathrm{Cl}\,P_\pm)$ are the two summands, and $\omega_{\mathbb C}$ acts on them as $\pm1$.
>
> **Step 7** (examples). $\mathrm{Cl}(1,3)_{\mathbb C} \cong \mathrm{Cl}_4(\mathbb C) \cong M_4(\mathbb C)$ and $\mathrm{Cl}(3,0)_{\mathbb C} \cong \mathrm{Cl}_3(\mathbb C) \cong M_2(\mathbb C)\oplus M_2(\mathbb C)$, by [[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-3|Theorem §CB.8.3]] and Step 5.
>
> **What the proof shows.**
> - ⚑ By-product: complex Bott periodicity $\mathrm{Cl}_{n+2}(\mathbb C) \cong M_2(\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C))$: adding two dimensions doubles the spinor dimension. Unrolled from $n = 4$, the generators of Step 2 are the Dirac matrices built as Kronecker products of Pauli matrices.
> - The course's $n = 4$ argument ([[§C5a.1 Spinor Space and the Clifford Action#^rem-c5a-1-5|§C5a.1, ★ Remark: The complexified Clifford algebra is the full matrix algebra]]) gets the same result from a $4\times4$ module and a dimension count; here no module is assumed.

> [!theorem] Theorem §CB.8.6: Modules of a Full Matrix Algebra
> Every finite-dimensional complex representation of $M_N(\mathbb C)$ ([[§CB.3 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^def-cb-3-7|Def. §CB.3.7]]) is equivalent to a direct sum of copies of the defining representation on $\mathbb C^N$. So $\mathbb C^N$ is the only irreducible representation up to equivalence, and an irreducible representation has dimension $N$.
>
> *Source: Figueroa-O'Farrill, Spin Geometry, Thm. 3.8 (statement, from Lang, Algebra, Ch. XVII) · proof via the matrix units $E_{ab}$ written here*

^thm-cb-8-6

> [!proof]- Proof
> *Source: the statement is J. Figueroa-O'Farrill, Spin Geometry, §3.3, Thm. 3.8 (which says it "can be extracted from" Lang, Algebra, Ch. XVII, and gives no proof). The matrix-unit proof is written here.*
>
> Let $\gamma : M_N(\mathbb C) \to \operatorname{End}(W)$ be a representation ([[§CB.3 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^def-cb-3-7|Def. §CB.3.7]]) and $E_{ab}$ the matrix units ($1$ in place $(a, b)$, $0$ elsewhere), so that
>
> $$
> E_{ab}E_{cd} = \delta_{bc}E_{ad}, \qquad \sum_aE_{aa} = \mathbb 1, \qquad X = \sum_{a,b}X_{ab}E_{ab} .
> $$
>
> **Step 1** (splitting $W$). Put $P_a = \gamma(E_{aa})$ and $W_a = P_aW$. Then $P_aP_b = \delta_{ab}P_a$ and $\sum_aP_a = \gamma(\mathbb 1) = \mathbb 1$, so $w = \sum_aP_aw$ for every $w$, and if $\sum_aw_a = 0$ with $w_a \in W_a$, applying $P_b$ gives $w_b = 0$: $W = W_1\oplus\cdots\oplus W_N$.
>
> **Step 2** (the pieces are isomorphic). $\gamma(E_{a1})$ maps $W_1$ into $W_a$ (since $E_{aa}E_{a1} = E_{a1}$), and $\gamma(E_{1a})$ maps $W_a$ into $W_1$; on $W_1$, $\gamma(E_{1a})\gamma(E_{a1}) = \gamma(E_{11}) = \mathbb 1$, and on $W_a$, $\gamma(E_{a1})\gamma(E_{1a}) = \gamma(E_{aa}) = \mathbb 1$. So $\gamma(E_{a1}) : W_1 \to W_a$ is an isomorphism.
>
> **Step 3** (copies of $\mathbb C^N$). Let $w_1, \dots, w_k$ be a basis of $W_1$ and $\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_N$ the standard basis of $\mathbb C^N$, on which $E_{bc}\varepsilon_a = \delta_{ca}\varepsilon_b$. For each $t$ define $J_t : \mathbb C^N \to W$ by $J_t\varepsilon_a = \gamma(E_{a1})w_t$. It is an intertwiner:
>
> $$
> \gamma(E_{bc})J_t\varepsilon_a = \gamma(E_{bc}E_{a1})w_t = \delta_{ca}\gamma(E_{b1})w_t = J_t(\delta_{ca}\varepsilon_b) = J_t(E_{bc}\varepsilon_a),
> $$
>
> and by linearity in $X = \sum X_{bc}E_{bc}$, $\gamma(X)J_t = J_tX$.
>
> **Step 4** (direct sum). By Steps 1–2, the vectors $\gamma(E_{a1})w_t$ ($a = 1, \dots, N$, $t = 1, \dots, k$) are a basis of $W$: for fixed $a$ they are a basis of $W_a$, and $W = \bigoplus_aW_a$. Hence each $J_t$ is injective and $W = \bigoplus_tJ_t(\mathbb C^N)$, a direct sum of $k$ invariant subspaces each equivalent to $\mathbb C^N$.
>
> **Step 5** (irreducibility of $\mathbb C^N$, uniqueness). If $U \subset \mathbb C^N$ is invariant and contains $u = \sum u_a\varepsilon_a \ne 0$, say $u_c \ne 0$, then $E_{bc}u = u_c\varepsilon_b \in U$ for every $b$, so $U = \mathbb C^N$: the defining representation is irreducible. If $W$ is irreducible, Step 4 forces $k = 1$, so $W \cong \mathbb C^N$ and $\dim W = N$.
>
> **What the proof shows.**
> - ⚑ By-product: every module of $M_N(\mathbb C)$ has dimension divisible by $N$ — the general form of "every representation of the Dirac algebra has dimension $4k$" ([[§C5a.1 Spinor Space and the Clifford Action#^rem-c5a-1-5|§C5a.1, ★ Remark: The complexified Clifford algebra is the full matrix algebra]]).
> - No inner product and no averaging is used: complete reducibility here is forced by the matrix units, not by unitarity.

## Irreducible Clifford modules and Pauli's theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §CB.8.7: Irreducible Clifford Modules
> Let $(V, q)$ be nondegenerate real (or complex) of dimension $n$.
> 1. $n$ even: there is exactly one irreducible complex Clifford module up to equivalence; its dimension is $2^{n/2}$.
> 2. $n$ odd: there are exactly two, of dimension $2^{(n-1)/2}$, on which $\omega_{\mathbb C}$ acts as $+1$ and as $-1$.
> 3. Every finite-dimensional complex Clifford module is a direct sum of irreducible ones.
>
> *Source: Figueroa-O'Farrill, Spin Geometry, §1.4.3 and §3.3 · written here (from Theorems §CB.8.3, §CB.8.5, §CB.8.6) · the Minkowski bound: [[§C5a.0 Why Spinors꞉ the Square Root of the Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-c5a-0-3|Theorem §C5a.0.3]]*

^thm-cb-8-7

> [!proof]- Proof
> *Source: J. Figueroa-O'Farrill, Spin Geometry, §1.4.3 ("for $V$ finite-dimensional and $Q$ nondegenerate there are (up to equivalence) either one or two irreducible representations") and §3.3 (the volume element distinguishes the two for $d$ odd: "$\omega = \pm1$ on $P_\pm$") · assembled here from Theorems §CB.8.3, §CB.8.5, §CB.8.6.*
>
> **Step 1** (reduction to $\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)$). By [[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-3|Theorem §CB.8.3]] (for complex $(V, q)$: an orthonormal basis with $q(e_j) = 1$ exists, Meinrenken, Ch. 1, Prop. 1.3, and Theorem §CB.7.7 gives $\mathrm{Cl}(V, q) \cong \mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)$ directly), complex Clifford modules of $(V, q)$, with their invariant subspaces and intertwiners, are the modules of $\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)$; the image of the complex volume element is the $\omega_{\mathbb C}$ of Step 6 of the proof of [[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-5|Theorem §CB.8.5]] up to the sign fixed by the basis.
>
> **Step 2** ($n$ even). $\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C) \cong M_{2^{n/2}}(\mathbb C)$ (Theorem §CB.8.5, 1). By [[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-6|Theorem §CB.8.6]], every finite-dimensional module is a direct sum of copies of $\mathbb C^{2^{n/2}}$, which is the only irreducible one: parts 1 and 3.
>
> **Step 3** ($n$ odd: splitting a module). $\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C) \cong M\oplus M$ with $M = M_{2^{(n-1)/2}}(\mathbb C)$, the summands being $\mathrm{Cl}\,P_\pm$, $P_\pm = \frac12(1 \pm \omega_{\mathbb C})$ central idempotents (Theorem §CB.8.5, 2). In a module $\gamma$ on $W$, $\gamma(P_+) + \gamma(P_-) = \mathbb 1$, $\gamma(P_\pm)^2 = \gamma(P_\pm)$, $\gamma(P_+)\gamma(P_-) = 0$, so $W = W_+\oplus W_-$ with $W_\pm = \gamma(P_\pm)W$ (as in Step 1 of the proof of Theorem §CB.8.6). Each $W_\pm$ is invariant ($P_\pm$ is central) and $\gamma(\omega_{\mathbb C}) = \gamma(P_+) - \gamma(P_-)$ is $\pm1$ on $W_\pm$. On $W_+$ the summand $\mathrm{Cl}\,P_-$ acts as $0$ ($xP_-\cdot P_+ = 0$) and $\mathrm{Cl}\,P_+ \cong M$ acts unitally ($P_+$ acts as $\mathbb 1$); so $W_+$ is a module of $M$, and likewise $W_-$.
>
> **Step 4** ($n$ odd: parts 2 and 3). By Theorem §CB.8.6, $W_+$ and $W_-$ are direct sums of copies of $\mathbb C^{2^{(n-1)/2}}$; so every module is a direct sum of irreducible ones on each of which $\omega_{\mathbb C} = +1$ or $-1$ (part 3). An irreducible module has $W_+ = 0$ or $W_- = 0$, hence is one copy, of dimension $2^{(n-1)/2}$, with $\omega_{\mathbb C} = +1$ or $-1$; both occur (let $M\oplus M$ act through either projection). The two are inequivalent: an intertwiner $S$ from the first to the second satisfies $S = S\gamma_+(\omega_{\mathbb C}) = \gamma_-(\omega_{\mathbb C})S = -S$, so $S = 0$. Two irreducible modules with the same sign are both the defining module of the same summand, hence equivalent.
>
> **What the proof shows.**
> - The dimension $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ of spinors is the size of the matrix algebra; for $n = 4$ it is the bound $n \ge 4$ of [[§C5a.0 Why Spinors꞉ the Square Root of the Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-c5a-0-3|Theorem §C5a.0.3]] with equality.
> - For $n$ odd the label of an irreducible module is the eigenvalue of the central element $\omega_{\mathbb C}$; for $\mathbb R^3$ this is the sign choice $e_i \mapsto \pm\sigma^i$ ([[§CB.10 Three Dimensions꞉ Cl(3), Spin(3) = SU(2) and Spin One-Half#^thm-cb-10-1|Theorem §CB.10.1]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §CB.8.8: Pauli's Theorem, General Form
> Let $n = \dim V$ be even and $\gamma$, $\gamma'$ two Clifford modules of $(V, q)$ ([[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-10|Theorem §CB.7.10]]) on spaces $W$, $W'$ of the same dimension. Then there is an invertible $S : W \to W'$ with $\gamma'(v) = S\gamma(v)S^{-1}$ for all $v \in V$. If the modules are irreducible ($\dim W = 2^{n/2}$), $S$ is unique up to a nonzero factor ([[§CB.3 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^thm-cb-3-8|Theorem §CB.3.8]]). For $n$ odd the same holds for modules on which $\omega_{\mathbb C}$ acts by the same sign.
>
> *Source: written here (corollary of Theorem §CB.8.7) · Peskin & Schroeder, §3.2 (statement for $n = 4$)*

^thm-cb-8-8

> [!proof]- Proof
> *Source: written here as a corollary of Theorem §CB.8.7 (Figueroa-O'Farrill, Spin Geometry, §1.4.3, states the uniqueness of the irreducible representations; Peskin & Schroeder, §3.2, p. 41, state the $4\times4$ case). The course proves the $n = 4$ case directly, by averaging over the sixteen products ([[§C5a.1 Spinor Space and the Clifford Action#^thm-c5a-1-12|Theorem §C5a.1.12]], Derivation); both proofs are kept.*
>
> **Step 1** (both modules are sums of the same irreducible). Let $n$ be even, and $S_0$ the irreducible module of dimension $N = 2^{n/2}$ ([[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-7|Theorem §CB.8.7]], 1). By part 3 there, $W \cong S_0^{\oplus k}$ and $W' \cong S_0^{\oplus k'}$ as modules; $\dim W = kN$ and $\dim W' = k'N$, so $\dim W = \dim W'$ gives $k = k'$.
>
> **Step 2** (the intertwiner). Composing the equivalence $W \to S_0^{\oplus k}$ with the inverse of $W' \to S_0^{\oplus k}$ gives an invertible linear $S : W \to W'$ with $S\gamma(x) = \gamma'(x)S$ for all $x \in \mathrm{Cl}(V, q)$; for $x = v \in V$, $\gamma'(v) = S\gamma(v)S^{-1}$. (By [[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-10|Theorem §CB.7.10]] it is enough that this holds on $V$: the modules are determined by the $\gamma(v)$.)
>
> **Step 3** (uniqueness up to a factor). If $W$, $W'$ are irreducible and $S$, $S'$ are two such maps, $S^{-1}S'$ is an invertible intertwiner from the irreducible $W$ to itself, hence $S^{-1}S' = \lambda\mathbb 1$ by Schur ([[§CB.3 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^thm-cb-3-8|Theorem §CB.3.8]]), $\lambda \ne 0$: $S' = \lambda S$.
>
> **Step 4** ($n$ odd). If $\omega_{\mathbb C}$ acts by the same sign on $W$ and $W'$, Step 4 of the proof of Theorem §CB.8.7 shows both are sums of copies of the same irreducible module of dimension $2^{(n-1)/2}$; Steps 1–3 apply verbatim.
>
> **What the proof shows.**
> - A change of Dirac matrices is a change of basis of spinor space and nothing else ([[§C5a.1 Spinor Space and the Clifford Action#^thm-c5a-1-13|Theorem §C5a.1.13]]); the only freedom left is the overall factor $\lambda$, fixed up to a phase by a unitarity requirement (Theorem §C5a.1.12, 2).
> - For $n$ odd the hypothesis on $\omega_{\mathbb C}$ cannot be dropped: $\sigma^i$ and $-\sigma^i$ are not conjugate, since conjugation preserves $\sigma^1\sigma^2\sigma^3 = i\mathbb 1$ while $(-\sigma^1)(-\sigma^2)(-\sigma^3) = -i\mathbb 1$.

## The even subalgebra acting on a module

> [!theorem] Theorem §CB.8.9: The Complexified Even Subalgebra
> For nondegenerate real $(V, q)$ of dimension $n \ge 1$, $\mathrm{Cl}^0(V, q)_{\mathbb C} \cong \mathrm{Cl}_{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ ([[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-20|Theorem §CB.7.20]], [[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-3|Theorem §CB.8.3]]). So $\mathrm{Cl}^0_{\mathbb C}$ is a full matrix algebra $M_{2^{(n-1)/2}}(\mathbb C)$ for $n$ odd and $M_{2^{n/2-1}}(\mathbb C)\oplus M_{2^{n/2-1}}(\mathbb C)$ for $n$ even ([[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-5|Theorem §CB.8.5]]).
>
> *Source: Figueroa-O'Farrill, Spin Geometry, Prop. 2.8, Cor. 2.9 · Meinrenken, Clifford Algebras and Lie Groups, eq. (19)*

^thm-cb-8-9

> [!proof]- Proof
> *Source: J. Figueroa-O'Farrill, Spin Geometry, §2.3.1, Prop. 2.8 and Cor. 2.9 (real form), with §2.2, Prop. 2.2 (complexification) · E. Meinrenken, Clifford Algebras and Lie Groups, §2.4, eq. (19) ($\mathrm{Cl}(n) \cong \mathrm{Cl}^{\bar0}(n+1)$ over $\mathbb C$).*
>
> **Step 1** (real level). Pick $e_0 \in V$ with $q(e_0) = \epsilon = \pm1$ (it exists: $q \not\equiv 0$ for nondegenerate $V \ne 0$, then rescale). [[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-20|Theorem §CB.7.20]] gives a real algebra isomorphism $\Phi : \mathrm{Cl}(V', q') \to \mathrm{Cl}^0(V, q)$ with $\dim V' = n - 1$, $q'$ nondegenerate.
>
> **Step 2** (complexify). $\Phi_{\mathbb C}(a + ib) = \Phi(a) + i\Phi(b)$ is a complex-linear bijection $\mathrm{Cl}(V', q')_{\mathbb C} \to \mathrm{Cl}^0(V, q)_{\mathbb C}$, multiplicative by the computation in Step 4 of the proof of [[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-3|Theorem §CB.8.3]] (with $\Phi$ in place of $\gamma$). Here $\mathrm{Cl}^0(V, q)_{\mathbb C} = \mathrm{Cl}^0 + i\,\mathrm{Cl}^0$ is the even part of $\mathrm{Cl}(V, q)_{\mathbb C}$.
>
> **Step 3** (identify). Theorem §CB.8.3 for $(V', q')$ gives $\mathrm{Cl}(V', q')_{\mathbb C} \cong \mathrm{Cl}_{n-1}(\mathbb C)$; compose. By [[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-5|Theorem §CB.8.5]]: $n$ odd means $n - 1 = 2m$ even, $\mathrm{Cl}_{n-1}(\mathbb C) \cong M_{2^{(n-1)/2}}(\mathbb C)$; $n$ even means $n - 1 = 2m + 1$ with $m = n/2 - 1$, $\mathrm{Cl}_{n-1}(\mathbb C) \cong M_{2^{n/2-1}}(\mathbb C)\oplus M_{2^{n/2-1}}(\mathbb C)$. For $n = 1$, $\mathrm{Cl}_0(\mathbb C) = \mathbb C = M_1(\mathbb C)$.
>
> **What the proof shows.**
> - The parity flips: an even-dimensional space has a split even subalgebra (two Weyl blocks), an odd-dimensional one a simple even subalgebra. Theorem §CB.8.10 reads this off on a module.

^pf-cb-8-9

*Uses:* [[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-20|Theorem §CB.7.20]], [[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-3|Theorem §CB.8.3]], [[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-5|Theorem §CB.8.5]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §CB.8.10: An Irreducible Clifford Module under the Even Subalgebra
> Let $S$ be an irreducible complex Clifford module of nondegenerate $(V, q)$, $\dim V = n$.
> 1. $n$ even: $\omega_{\mathbb C}$ commutes with $\mathrm{Cl}^0$ and anticommutes with $V$ ([[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-19|Theorem §CB.7.19]], 3); its eigenspaces $S^\pm$ ($\omega_{\mathbb C} = \pm1$) are invariant under $\mathrm{Cl}^0$, each of dimension $2^{n/2-1}$, irreducible and mutually inequivalent as $\mathrm{Cl}^0$-modules; and $\gamma(v)$ maps $S^\pm$ isomorphically onto $S^\mp$ whenever $q(v) \ne 0$.
> 2. $n$ odd: $S$ is irreducible as a $\mathrm{Cl}^0$-module, and the two inequivalent irreducible Clifford modules ([[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-7|Theorem §CB.8.7]], 2) are equivalent as $\mathrm{Cl}^0$-modules.
> 3. In both cases, every irreducible complex $\mathrm{Cl}^0$-module is equivalent to $S$ ($n$ odd) or to $S^+$ or $S^-$ ($n$ even).
>
> *Source: Figueroa-O'Farrill, Spin Geometry, §3.3 · written here (from Theorems §CB.8.6–§CB.8.9)*

^thm-cb-8-10

> [!proof]- Proof
> *Source: J. Figueroa-O'Farrill, Spin Geometry, §3.3 (for $d$ even "the volume element commutes with $C\ell(s,t)^0$, whence its eigenspaces in the pinor representation will correspond to the spinor representations"; for $d$ odd $C\ell = C\ell^0\oplus C\ell^0\omega$ and the two pinor representations restrict to the same spinor one) and Def. 3.7 · details written here from Theorems §CB.8.6–§CB.8.9.*
>
> Let $\gamma : \mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb C} \to \operatorname{End}(S)$ be the complex-linear extension of the irreducible module (Step 4 of the proof of [[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-3|Theorem §CB.8.3]]); complex subspaces invariant under $\gamma(\mathrm{Cl}^0)$ are those invariant under $\gamma(\mathrm{Cl}^0_{\mathbb C})$, and likewise for intertwiners, so we may work with $\mathrm{Cl}^0_{\mathbb C}$.
>
> **Step 1** ($n$ even: the splitting). $\omega_{\mathbb C}$ is a multiple of $\omega$, so it commutes with $\mathrm{Cl}^0$ and anticommutes with $V$ ([[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-19|Theorem §CB.7.19]], 3), and $\omega_{\mathbb C}^2 = 1$ ([[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^def-cb-8-4|Def. §CB.8.4]]). With $\Pi_\pm = \frac12(\mathbb 1 \pm \gamma(\omega_{\mathbb C}))$, as in Step 1 of the proof of [[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-6|Theorem §CB.8.6]], $S = S^+\oplus S^-$ with $S^\pm = \Pi_\pm S$ the $\pm1$ eigenspaces. For $x \in \mathrm{Cl}^0$, $\gamma(x)$ commutes with $\gamma(\omega_{\mathbb C})$, so it preserves each eigenspace. For $v \in V$ and $s \in S^\pm$: $\gamma(\omega_{\mathbb C})\gamma(v)s = -\gamma(v)\gamma(\omega_{\mathbb C})s = \mp\gamma(v)s$, so $\gamma(v)S^\pm \subset S^\mp$.
>
> **Step 2** ($n$ even: equal dimensions). If $q(v) \ne 0$, $\gamma(v)^2 = q(v)\mathbb 1$ is invertible, so $\gamma(v) : S^+ \to S^-$ and $\gamma(v) : S^- \to S^+$ are both injective; hence $\dim S^+ = \dim S^-$ and each map is an isomorphism. Such $v$ exist ($q \not\equiv 0$). With $\dim S = 2^{n/2}$ ([[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-7|Theorem §CB.8.7]], 1), $\dim S^\pm = 2^{n/2-1}$.
>
> **Step 3** ($n$ even: irreducible and inequivalent). $\gamma : \mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb C} \to \operatorname{End}(S)$ is surjective: $S$ is the defining module of $\mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb C} \cong M_{2^{n/2}}(\mathbb C)$ ([[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-5|Theorem §CB.8.5]], Theorem §CB.8.6), so $\gamma$ is that isomorphism up to conjugation. Write $\operatorname{End}(S) = \mathcal D\oplus\mathcal O$, block-diagonal and block-off-diagonal operators for $S = S^+\oplus S^-$. By Step 1, $\gamma(\mathrm{Cl}^0_{\mathbb C}) \subset \mathcal D$ and $\gamma(\mathrm{Cl}^1_{\mathbb C}) \subset \mathcal O$ (an odd element is a sum of products of an odd number of vectors). Since $\gamma(\mathrm{Cl}^0_{\mathbb C}) + \gamma(\mathrm{Cl}^1_{\mathbb C}) = \operatorname{End}(S) = \mathcal D\oplus\mathcal O$, comparing components gives $\gamma(\mathrm{Cl}^0_{\mathbb C}) = \mathcal D = \operatorname{End}(S^+)\oplus\operatorname{End}(S^-)$. So $\mathrm{Cl}^0$ acts on $S^+$ through all of $\operatorname{End}(S^+)$, and a subspace invariant under all of $\operatorname{End}(S^+)$ is $0$ or $S^+$: $S^+$ is irreducible, and likewise $S^-$. The element $x = \frac12(1 + \omega_{\mathbb C}) \in \mathrm{Cl}^0_{\mathbb C}$ acts as $\mathbb 1$ on $S^+$ and $0$ on $S^-$; an intertwiner $T : S^+ \to S^-$ satisfies $T = T\gamma(x)|_{S^+} = \gamma(x)|_{S^-}T = 0$, so $S^+$, $S^-$ are inequivalent.
>
> **Step 4** ($n$ odd: irreducible). $\omega_{\mathbb C}$ is central ([[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-19|Theorem §CB.7.19]], 3) and acts on the irreducible $S$ as $+1$ or $-1$ (Theorem §CB.8.7, 2). $\omega$ is odd and invertible, so $y \mapsto y\omega$ maps $\mathrm{Cl}^0$ onto $\mathrm{Cl}^1$ (inverse $y \mapsto y\omega^{-1}$, by Theorem §CB.7.13), whence $\mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb C} = \mathrm{Cl}^0_{\mathbb C} + \mathrm{Cl}^0_{\mathbb C}\,\omega_{\mathbb C}$ and $\gamma(\mathrm{Cl}_{\mathbb C}) = \gamma(\mathrm{Cl}^0_{\mathbb C}) \pm \gamma(\mathrm{Cl}^0_{\mathbb C}) = \gamma(\mathrm{Cl}^0_{\mathbb C})$. A subspace invariant under $\mathrm{Cl}^0$ is therefore invariant under $\mathrm{Cl}$, hence $0$ or $S$.
>
> **Step 5** ($n$ odd: the two modules agree on $\mathrm{Cl}^0$). Given $\gamma$ with $\omega_{\mathbb C} = +1$, $\gamma^-(v) = -\gamma(v)$ is again a Clifford module ($(-\gamma(v))^2 = q(v)\mathbb 1$, [[§CB.7 Clifford Algebras꞉ Definition, Grading, Basis and Volume Element#^thm-cb-7-10|Theorem §CB.7.10]]), with $\gamma^-(e_1\cdots e_n) = (-1)^n\gamma(e_1\cdots e_n) = -\gamma(e_1\cdots e_n)$, so $\omega_{\mathbb C} = -1$ on it: it represents the other class (Theorem §CB.8.7, 2). On a product of an even number of vectors the signs cancel, so $\gamma^-(x) = \gamma(x)$ for $x \in \mathrm{Cl}^0$; the identity map intertwines the two restrictions.
>
> **Step 6** (part 3). By [[§CB.8 Complex Clifford Algebras and Clifford Modules#^thm-cb-8-9|Theorem §CB.8.9]], $\mathrm{Cl}^0_{\mathbb C} \cong \mathrm{Cl}_{n-1}(\mathbb C)$. For $n$ odd this is $M_{2^{(n-1)/2}}(\mathbb C)$, which has exactly one irreducible module, of dimension $2^{(n-1)/2}$ (Theorem §CB.8.6); $S$ is irreducible of that dimension (Step 4, Theorem §CB.8.7), so it is that module. For $n$ even, $\mathrm{Cl}_{n-1}(\mathbb C) \cong M\oplus M$ has exactly two irreducible modules up to equivalence (Steps 3–4 of the proof of Theorem §CB.8.7, with $\mathrm{Cl}_{n-1}(\mathbb C)$ in place of $\mathrm{Cl}_n(\mathbb C)$); $S^+$ and $S^-$ are two inequivalent irreducible ones (Step 3), so they are those two.
>
> **What the proof shows.**
> - ⚑ By-product: for $n$ even, $\gamma(\mathrm{Cl}^0) = \operatorname{End}(S^+)\oplus\operatorname{End}(S^-)$ exactly, so the only operators commuting with all of $\gamma(\mathrm{Cl}^0)$ are $a\Pi_+ + b\Pi_-$: the chirality projectors $\frac12(1 \pm \gamma^5)$ are, up to combination, the only invariants of the even algebra on the Dirac module.
> - Restricted to $\mathrm{Spin}(V)_0$, which generates $\mathrm{Cl}^0$ ([[§CB.9 The Spin Group and the Double Cover Spin(V) → SO(V)#^thm-cb-9-15|Theorem §CB.9.15]]), Steps 3–6 become parts 1–2 of the TA's theorem ([[§CB.9 The Spin Group and the Double Cover Spin(V) → SO(V)#^thm-cb-9-20|Theorem §CB.9.20]]).
