How does a free field respond to a source, $(\partial^2 + m^2)\phi = j$? One solves the equation once, with a delta function on the right, and the answer is a Green's function. Fourier transformation makes this a division by $m^2 - p^2$ ([[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-4|Theorem §CA.1.4]]), which is impossible on the mass shell; the integral needs a contour in the complex $p^0$-plane ([[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-2-9|Theorem §CA.2.9]], [[P2 Green's Functions by Contour Integration|P2]]), and the choice of contour turns out to be the choice of boundary condition. The results are built from the two-point functions of [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality|§C2.5]]: the retarded function is the causal commutator, and the Feynman propagator is the vacuum expectation value of the time-ordered product, defined here. The section ends with the $i\varepsilon$ prescription, the Wick rotation to the Euclidean propagator, and the whole family of two-point functions in one table ([[§C2.6 Green's Functions and the Feynman Propagator#^def-c2-6-5|Def. §C2.6.5]]).

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): Green's functions are normalized by $(\partial^2 + m^2)D_C = -i\delta^4$ (PS), so that $D_F = \langle0|T\phi\phi|0\rangle$ with no prefactor and $\tilde D_F = i/(p^2 - m^2 + i\varepsilon)$; Fourier conventions as in [[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^def-ca-1-1|Def. §CA.1.1]]; $\xi = x - y$, $t = \xi^0$.

## Sources and Green's functions

> [!model] Model §C2.6.1: Scalar Field with a Classical Source
> A real scalar field coupled to a prescribed real function $j(x)$, smooth and nonzero only for a finite time:
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = \tfrac12(\partial_\mu\phi)^2 - \tfrac12m^2\phi^2 + j\phi, \qquad (\partial^2 + m^2)\,\phi(x) = j(x) .
> $$
>
> *Assumptions:* $j$ is external and classical, prepared in the laboratory; it acts on the field, and the field does not act back on it. (Later the source is itself made of fields: interactions, QFT C6, planned.)
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.1 · PHY 513 Lecture 6, Part A · PS §2.4, eqs. (2.61)–(2.62)*

^mod-c2-6-1

> [!definition] Definition §C2.6.1: Green's Function of the Klein–Gordon Operator
> A **Green's function** $D_C$ solves
>
> $$
> (\partial_x^2 + m^2)\,D_C(x - y) = -i\,\delta^4(x - y) .
> $$
>
> Then for every source $\phi(x) = \phi_0(x) + i\int d^4y\,D_C(x - y)\,j(y)$, with $\phi_0$ any homogeneous solution, solves $(\partial^2 + m^2)\phi = j$. The label $C$ names the boundary condition, equivalently the contour of [[§C2.6 Green's Functions and the Feynman Propagator#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]].
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.1 · PHY 513 Lecture 6, Part A · PS §2.4, eq. (2.56)*

^def-c2-6-1

> [!remark] Remark: What a Green's "function" is, and why it is not unique
> *Check:* $\partial_x^2 + m^2$ acts on $x$ while the integral is over $y$, so it passes inside and gives $i\cdot(-i)\int d^4y\,\delta^4(x - y)j(y) = j(x)$: solving once with a delta function solves every source. In the language of [[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^def-ca-1-2|Def. §CA.1.2]], the sources of physics are test functions, and what is defined is the *linear map* $j \mapsto \phi$; "$D_C(x - y)$" is its kernel, not a function with values ([[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^rem-ca-1-4|Remark: What the language buys in field theory]]). Adding a homogeneous solution to $D_C$ gives another Green's function: a differential equation has no unique solution without boundary conditions, so there is one map, and one kernel, per boundary condition (Problem Set 4, Problem 0). The factor $-i$ is PS's: Problem Set 4 writes $(-\Delta)G = \delta$ with $D_C = -iG$. (Its "$-\Delta = -\partial_\mu\partial^\mu + m^2$" has the sign of $\partial^2$ reversed for the metric $(+, -, -, -)$; the Klein–Gordon operator is $\partial_\mu\partial^\mu + m^2$.)
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §§6.1–6.2 · PHY 513, Problem Set 4, Problem 0*

^rem-c2-6-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.6.2: Free Fields Live on the Mass Shell
> If $(\partial^2 + m^2)f = 0$, the transform of $f$ is supported on $p^2 = m^2$, $\tilde f(p) = 2\pi\,\delta(p^2 - m^2)\,g(p)$, and
>
> $$
> f(x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_{\mathbf p}}\Bigl[g(p)\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + g(-p)\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigr]_{p^0 = E_{\mathbf p}} ,
> $$
>
> the mode expansion, with $g(p) = \sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}\,a_{\mathbf p}$ for the free field. A Green's function, by contrast, has $\tilde D_C(p) = i/(p^2 - m^2)$ wherever $p^2 \ne m^2$: it lives off the shell.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.3 (Principle "Free fields live on the mass shell"), §6.4 · PS §2.4, eqs. (2.57)–(2.58)*

^thm-c2-6-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> By [[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-4|Theorem §CA.1.4]], 2, the equation becomes $(m^2 - p^2)\tilde f(p) = 0$, so $\tilde f$ vanishes off the shell; for the transforms met here it is a smooth function times $\delta(p^2 - m^2)$. With $\delta(p^2 - m^2) = [\delta(p^0 - E_{\mathbf p}) + \delta(p^0 + E_{\mathbf p})]/2E_{\mathbf p}$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-1|EM Theorem §B1.2.1]]) the $p^0$ integral leaves two terms; relabel $\mathbf p \to -\mathbf p$ in the negative-energy one. Comparing with [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]] identifies $g$. For $D_C$ the same rule gives $(m^2 - p^2)\tilde D_C = -i$.

^der-c2-6-2

*Uses:* [[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-4|Theorem §CA.1.4]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-1|EM Theorem §B1.2.1]], [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §C2.6.2: The Green's Function of a Contour
> Let $C$ be a contour in the complex $p^0$-plane that follows the real axis from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ except near $p^0 = \pm E_{\mathbf p}$, each of which it passes above or below. Then
>
> $$
> D_C(\xi) \equiv \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\xi}\int_C\frac{dp^0}{2\pi}\,\frac{i\,e^{-ip^0\xi^0}}{(p^0)^2 - E_{\mathbf p}^2} .
> $$
>
> Without a contour, $\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{i\,e^{-ip\cdot\xi}}{p^2 - m^2}$ means nothing: $1/(p^0 \mp E_{\mathbf p})$ is not integrable at the pole.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.4 (eq. (DCdef)) · PHY 513 Lecture 6, "Important Mathematical Limitation"*

^def-c2-6-2

> [!remark] Remark: The pole problem; two problems that are one
> Unlike every integral of [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field|§C2.2]]–[[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality|§C2.5]], this one is four-dimensional: $p^0$ is now a variable, not $E_{\mathbf p}$ ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^rem-c2-5-2|Remark: What is covariant and what is not yet]]), and the integrand blows up exactly at the energy one "knows in one's heart" is the right one. Two problems were swept under the rug: one cannot divide by $p^2 - m^2$ on the shell, and one cannot solve a differential equation without boundary conditions. Theorem §C2.6.3 shows they are the same problem. The lecture's insistence is worth keeping: without a $C$, the expression is not yet anything.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.4 · PHY 513 Lecture 6*

^rem-c2-6-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.6.3: Contours Are Boundary Conditions
> 1. For every contour $C$ that passes the two poles, $(\partial^2 + m^2)D_C = -i\delta^4$.
> 2. Two such contours differ by closed loops around poles, and $D_{C_1} - D_{C_2}$ is a homogeneous solution built from the on-shell waves $e^{\mp iE_{\mathbf p}\xi^0 + i\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\xi}$.
> 3. For $\xi^0 > 0$ the $p^0$ contour may be closed downward, for $\xi^0 < 0$ upward; the arc contributes nothing.
>
> Choosing a contour is choosing which homogeneous solution to add: a boundary condition.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.8 (Principle "Contours are boundary conditions"), §6.5 (Derivation "Closing the contour for the Green's function") · PS §2.4, p. 31*

^thm-c2-6-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *1.* $\partial^2 + m^2$ acting on $e^{-ip\cdot\xi}$ brings down $m^2 - p^2$, which cancels the denominator; the integrand is then entire in $p^0$, so by Cauchy's theorem ([[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-2-2|Theorem §CA.2.2]]) the contour may be moved back to the real axis, where $\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4}e^{-ip\cdot\xi} = \delta^4(\xi)$ ([[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-4|Theorem §CA.1.4]]); the factor is $i\cdot(-1) = -i$.
>
> *2.* $C_1 - C_2$ is a sum of loops around $\pm E_{\mathbf p}$; each picks up $\pm2\pi i$ times a residue $\propto e^{\mp iE_{\mathbf p}\xi^0}$ ([[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-2-4|Theorem §CA.2.4]], 3), an on-shell plane wave, and on shell $(\partial^2 + m^2)e^{-ip\cdot\xi} = 0$.
>
> *3.* $|e^{-ip^0\xi^0}| = e^{\xi^0\operatorname{Im}p^0} \le 1$ on the lower semicircle when $\xi^0 > 0$, on the upper one when $\xi^0 < 0$. There the integrand is bounded by $1/(R^2 - E^2)$, and the arc by $\frac{\pi R}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{R^2 - E^2} \to 0$ (ML, [[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-2-6|Theorem §CA.2.6]]). The $1/(p^0)^2$ decay makes Jordan's lemma, which Yu invokes here, unnecessary. "Move the contour up to infinity" is the same argument ([[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^rem-ca-2-2|§CA.2, Remark: Move the contour up to infinity]]).

## Retarded and advanced functions

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.6.4: Retarded and Advanced Green's Functions
> The **retarded** contour $C_R$ passes above both poles, the **advanced** contour $C_A$ below both. Then
>
> $$
> D_R(\xi) = \theta(\xi^0)\bigl[D_W(\xi) - D_W(-\xi)\bigr] = \theta(\xi^0)\,\langle0|[\phi(x), \phi(y)]|0\rangle = \theta(\xi^0)\,iD(\xi), \qquad D_A(\xi) = -\theta(-\xi^0)\,iD(\xi) .
> $$
>
> $D_R$ vanishes unless $x$ lies in the causal future of $y$ (inside or on the forward light cone), $D_A$ unless it lies in the causal past. Mode by mode, $D_R$ is $\theta(t)\,(-i\sin E_{\mathbf p}t)/E_{\mathbf p}$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.6, §6.8 (table) · PHY 513 Lecture 6, "Retarded Green's Function: Explicit Evaluation" · PS §2.4, eqs. (2.54)–(2.56)*

^thm-c2-6-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Follow [[P2 Green's Functions by Contour Integration|P2]]. *Retarded, $t < 0$:* close upward ([[P2 Green's Functions by Contour Integration#^p2-4|P2, step 4]]); no pole is enclosed, so the integral is zero. *$t > 0$:* close downward, clockwise, around both poles ([[P2 Green's Functions by Contour Integration#^p2-5|P2, step 5]]). With the residues $\pm e^{\mp iEt}/2E$ ([[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-2-4|Theorem §CA.2.4]], 3),
>
> $$
> \int_{C_R}\frac{dp^0}{2\pi}\,\frac{i\,e^{-ip^0t}}{(p^0)^2 - E^2} = -2\pi i\cdot\frac{i}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{2E}\bigl(e^{-iEt} - e^{iEt}\bigr) = -\frac{i\sin Et}{E} .
> $$
>
> The residue at $+E$ carries the wave with the right energy; the one at $-E$ carries $e^{+iEt}$. Relabel $\mathbf p \to -\mathbf p$ in that term ([[P2 Green's Functions by Contour Integration#^p2-6|P2, step 6]]): $e^{iEt + i\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\xi} \to e^{-ip\cdot(y - x)}$, a mode of $D_W(-\xi)$. So $D_R = \theta(t)[D_W(\xi) - D_W(-\xi)]$, which is $\theta(t)\,iD$ by [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-9|Theorem §C2.5.9]]. Since $D = 0$ outside the light cone ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-10|Theorem §C2.5.10]]), $D_R$ is supported in the closed forward cone.
>
> *Advanced:* for $t > 0$ close downward, nothing enclosed; for $t < 0$ close upward, counterclockwise around both: $+2\pi i\cdot\frac{i}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{2E}(e^{-iEt} - e^{iEt})$, giving $-[D_W(\xi) - D_W(-\xi)]$.
>
> *Check, mode by mode* ([[P2 Green's Functions by Contour Integration#^p2-7|P2, step 7]]): $G(t) = \theta(t)(-i\sin Et)/E$ is continuous at $0$, and $G'$ jumps by $-i$ there, so $G'' + E^2G = -i\delta(t)$. In operator form (PS eq. (2.56)): $\partial^2$ on $\theta(x^0 - y^0)\langle[\phi(x), \phi(y)]\rangle$ gives $-\delta(x^0 - y^0)\langle[\pi(x), \phi(y)]\rangle + 2\delta(x^0 - y^0)\langle[\pi, \phi]\rangle + 0 = -i\delta^4(x - y)$, with the equal-time commutator of [[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]].

*Uses:* [[P2 Green's Functions by Contour Integration|P2]], [[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-2-4|Theorem §CA.2.4]], [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-9|Theorem §C2.5.9]], [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-10|Theorem §C2.5.10]], [[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Retarded means late; the sign of the exponent carries causality
> The source at $y$ affects the field at $x$ only if $y$ comes first: causality in its plainest form, and since the commutator vanishes outside the cone, the retarded function is causal in both senses at once. Why not take complex conjugates and halve the bookkeeping? Conjugation flips $e^{-ip^0t} \to e^{+ip^0t}$, i.e. $t \to -t$, exchanging "$y$ before $x$" with "$y$ after $x$". The sign of the exponent decides where the contour may be closed, so it carries the information about what comes first ([[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^ex-ca-2-2|Example §CA.2.2]] is the same mechanism in one line).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.6 (Caution "Why the sign of the exponent is not innocent") · PHY 513 Lecture 6, "Retarded Contour: Qualitative Behavior"*

^rem-c2-6-3

> [!remark] Remark: Measurable response is a retarded commutator
> Couple the quantum field to a classical source as in [[§C2.6 Green's Functions and the Feynman Propagator#^mod-c2-6-1|Model §C2.6.1]], which adds $-\int d^3x\,j\phi$ to $H$. The retarded solution changes the expectation value of the field by
>
> $$
> \delta\langle0|\phi(x)|0\rangle = i\int d^4y\,D_R(x - y)\,j(y) = i\int d^4y\;\theta(x^0 - y^0)\,\langle0|[\phi(x), \phi(y)]|0\rangle\,j(y) :
> $$
>
> the response of the field is the retarded unequal-time commutator. This is the simplest case of the Kubo formula of linear response, $\delta\langle\mathcal O(x)\rangle = i\int d^4y\,\theta(x^0 - y^0)\langle[\mathcal O(x), \phi(y)]\rangle\,j(y)$, and the reason the retarded function is the central object of condensed matter. Influence travels only through the commutator ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^rem-c2-5-8|§C2.5, Remark: Influence and correlation]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.6 ("Measurable response is a retarded commutator"), Ch. 5 §5.7*

## Time ordering and the Feynman propagator

> [!definition] Definition §C2.6.3: Time-Ordered Product
> For field operators at $x$ and $y$,
>
> $$
> T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\} \equiv \theta(x^0 - y^0)\,\phi(x)\phi(y) + \theta(y^0 - x^0)\,\phi(y)\phi(x) ,
> $$
>
> the later operator to the left, with $\theta(0) = \frac12$. For $n$ fields, $T\{\phi(x_1)\cdots\phi(x_n)\} = \phi(x_{\sigma(1)})\cdots\phi(x_{\sigma(n)})$ with $x^0_{\sigma(1)} \ge \cdots \ge x^0_{\sigma(n)}$. The **anti-time-ordered** product $\bar T$ puts the earlier operator to the left. (Fermion fields will add a sign per exchange, QFT C5, planned.)
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.6 (Definition "The time-ordered product"), Ch. 6 §6.10 ($\bar T$) · PS §2.4, eq. (2.60) · Yu §6.2, eq. (6.108)*

^def-c2-6-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.6.5: Properties of Time Ordering
> 1. $T$ reorders by time labels, so $T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\} = T\{\phi(y)\phi(x)\}$.
> 2. At equal times both orders give the same operator for $\phi\phi$, so the value of $\theta(0)$ does not matter there; for $\phi$ and $\pi$ it does.
> 3. $T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}$ is the same in every inertial frame, because of microcausality.
> 4. For any $A(x)$, $B(y)$: $\partial_{x^0}T\{A(x)B(y)\} = T\{\partial_0A(x)\,B(y)\} + \delta(x^0 - y^0)\,[A(x), B(y)]$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.6 (Derivation "Properties of time ordering"), Ch. 6 §6.7 (eq. (dT)) · Yu §6.2, eqs. (6.108)–(6.113)*

^thm-c2-6-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *1.* The definition sorts its arguments by their time labels, whatever order they are written in.
>
> *2.* $[\phi(t, \mathbf x), \phi(t, \mathbf y)] = 0$ ([[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]]); $[\phi, \pi] \ne 0$ at equal times, and there the ordering is ambiguous, the origin of contact terms (part 4).
>
> *3.* For timelike $x - y$ the sign of $x^0 - y^0$ is the same in every frame related by $SO^+(1,3)$ ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|REL Theorem §B1.3.1]]). For spacelike separation observers disagree on the order, but there $[\phi(x), \phi(y)] = 0$ ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-10|Theorem §C2.5.10]]), so both orders give the same product. Without microcausality the definition would depend on the observer.
>
> *4.* Differentiate the step functions: $\partial_{x^0}\theta(\pm(x^0 - y^0)) = \pm\delta(x^0 - y^0)$, and the two delta terms combine into the commutator ([[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-7|Theorem §CA.1.7]], 2).

*Uses:* [[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]], [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|REL Theorem §B1.3.1]], [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-10|Theorem §C2.5.10]], [[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-7|Theorem §CA.1.7]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why the later operator stands to the left
> In $\langle0|\cdots|0\rangle$ operators act on the state from right to left, so reading right to left is reading the history: the earliest acts first. Time evolution composes the same way, $U(t_3, t_2)U(t_2, t_1) = U(t_3, t_1)$ ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation|QM §C3.1]]), and Dyson's formula for interacting fields will order its exponential this way (QFT C6, planned). In the lecture's words: time starts on the right, things happen, and time ends on the left.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.6*

^rem-c2-6-5

> [!caution] Caution: The step function at zero
> The step-function form must not double-count at $x^0 = y^0$, which needs $\theta(0) = \frac12$. Problem Set 3 uses $\theta(0) = 1$; with it the definition gives $\phi(x)\phi(y) + \phi(y)\phi(x)$ at equal times, twice too much. For $\phi\phi$ this never matters (Theorem §C2.6.5, 2), and a single instant carries no weight once integrated.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.6, property 3*

^cau-c2-6-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.6.6: The Feynman Propagator
> The **Feynman** contour $C_F$ passes below $-E_{\mathbf p}$ and above $+E_{\mathbf p}$. Then
>
> $$
> D_F(\xi) = \theta(\xi^0)\,D_W(\xi) + \theta(-\xi^0)\,D_W(-\xi) = \langle0|T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}|0\rangle .
> $$
>
> $D_F$ is even in $\xi$ and Lorentz invariant, and $D_F = D_R + D_W(-\xi) = D_A + D_W(\xi)$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.7 · PHY 513 Lecture 6, "Evaluation of the Feynman Propagator" · PS §2.4, eqs. (2.59)–(2.60) · Yu §6.4.1, eqs. (6.194)–(6.211) · PHY 513, Problem Set 4, Problem 4(a)*

^thm-c2-6-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *$t > 0$:* close downward, clockwise; only $+E$ is enclosed: $-2\pi i\cdot\frac{i}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{e^{-iEt}}{2E} = \frac{e^{-iEt}}{2E}$, a mode of $D_W(\xi)$. *$t < 0$:* close upward, counterclockwise; only $-E$ is enclosed: $+2\pi i\cdot\frac{i}{2\pi}\cdot\bigl(-\frac{e^{iEt}}{2E}\bigr) = \frac{e^{iEt}}{2E}$, which after $\mathbf p \to -\mathbf p$ is a mode of $D_W(-\xi)$. The leftover loop is counterclockwise here, clockwise in the retarded case, and the orientation turns the minus sign of the partial fractions into a plus.
>
> *The same function from the operator side:* by [[§C2.6 Green's Functions and the Feynman Propagator#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]] and [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^def-c2-5-2|Def. §C2.5.2]], $\langle0|T\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle = \theta(t)D_W(\xi) + \theta(-t)D_W(-\xi)$: the contour and the time-ordered product arrive independently at the same function. Evenness is [[§C2.6 Green's Functions and the Feynman Propagator#^thm-c2-6-5|Theorem §C2.6.5]], 1, invariance is part 3 of it. The relations: for $t > 0$, $D_R + D_W(-\xi) = D_W(\xi) - D_W(-\xi) + D_W(-\xi)$; for $t < 0$, $0 + D_W(-\xi)$. Likewise for $D_A$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.6.7: The Free Schwinger–Dyson Equation
> For distinct times $y_1^0, \dots, y_n^0$,
>
> $$
> (\partial_x^2 + m^2)\,T\{\phi(x)\phi(y_1)\cdots\phi(y_n)\} = -i\sum_{k=1}^n\delta^4(x - y_k)\,T\{\phi(y_1)\cdots\widehat{\phi(y_k)}\cdots\phi(y_n)\},
> $$
>
> the hat marking the omitted field. For $n = 1$ the vacuum expectation value is $(\partial^2 + m^2)D_F = -i\delta^4$: the Feynman propagator is a Green's function.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.7 (Derivations "$D_F$ is a Green's function: the operator derivation", "Generalization: the free Schwinger–Dyson equation") · PHY 513, Problem Set 4, Problem 4(c)*

^thm-c2-6-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *$n = 1$.* Write $D_F = \theta(t)D_W(\xi) + \theta(-t)D_W(-\xi)$, derivatives in $x$. Spatial derivatives and the mass act only on the Wightman functions. *First time derivative:* $\partial_0D_F = \delta(t)[D_W(\xi) - D_W(-\xi)] + \theta(t)\partial_0D_W(\xi) + \theta(-t)\partial_0D_W(-\xi)$; the bracket is $\langle[\phi(x), \phi(y)]\rangle$ at equal times, zero. *Second:* the new contact term is $\delta(t)\,\partial_{x^0}\langle[\phi(x), \phi(y)]\rangle = \delta(t)\langle[\pi(x), \phi(y)]\rangle_{t=0} = \delta(t)(-i)\delta^3(\boldsymbol\xi) = -i\delta^4(\xi)$ ([[§C2.6 Green's Functions and the Feynman Propagator#^thm-c2-6-5|Theorem §C2.6.5]], 4; [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-12|Theorem §C2.5.12]]). Both Wightman functions are homogeneous solutions, so $(\partial^2 + m^2)D_F = -i\delta^4$. The two pieces of $D_F$ each satisfy the source-free equation; the source is produced entirely by the switch between them at $t = 0$, with strength set by the canonical commutator. (Taking both time derivatives at once produces $\delta'(t)$, reduced to this by $\delta'(t)f(t) = f(0)\delta'(t) - f'(0)\delta(t)$; that is the route of Problem Set 4, Problem 4(c).)
>
> *General $n$.* Between consecutive times $y_k^0$ the field $\phi(x)$ sits in a fixed slot, and $(\partial_x^2 + m^2)$ acting on it gives zero by the field equation ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]]). At $x^0 = y_k^0$, $\phi(x)$ moves past $\phi(y_k)$: the first derivative produces $\delta(x^0 - y_k^0)[\phi(x), \phi(y_k)] = 0$, the second $\delta(x^0 - y_k^0)[\pi(x), \phi(y_k)] = -i\delta^4(x - y_k)$, a $c$-number, which leaves the remaining fields in time order.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.6.8: Contractions: Normal Ordering against Time Ordering
> With $\phi = \phi^+ + \phi^-$ ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-3|Theorem §C2.5.3]]) and normal ordering $:\;:$ ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]),
>
> $$
> \phi(x)\phi(y) = \,:\!\phi(x)\phi(y)\!:\, + D_W(x - y), \qquad T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\} = \,:\!\phi(x)\phi(y)\!:\, + D_F(x - y) .
> $$
>
> $D_F$ is the number that separates the time-ordered from the normal-ordered product: the **contraction** of the two fields.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.7 (Derivation "Normal ordering and time ordering: a first look at Wick's theorem") · Yu §6.3, eqs. (6.132)–(6.133)*

^thm-c2-6-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Of the four terms of $\phi(x)\phi(y)$ only $\phi^+(x)\phi^-(y)$ is out of normal order; moving it costs $[\phi^+(x), \phi^-(y)]$, a number, and taking the vacuum expectation value of both sides (a normal-ordered product has none, since $\phi^+|0\rangle = 0 = \langle0|\phi^-$) identifies it as $D_W(x - y)$. For boson fields $:\phi(x)\phi(y): = :\phi(y)\phi(x):$, so applying the first identity in each time order gives the second, with $\theta(t)D_W(\xi) + \theta(-t)D_W(-\xi) = D_F$ ([[§C2.6 Green's Functions and the Feynman Propagator#^thm-c2-6-6|Theorem §C2.6.6]]).

## The $i\varepsilon$ prescription

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.6.9: The $i\varepsilon$ Prescriptions
> Keeping the $p^0$ integral on the real axis and moving the poles off it is equivalent to deforming the contour. With $\varepsilon \to 0^+$ at the end,
>
> $$
> \tilde D_F = \frac{i}{p^2 - m^2 + i\varepsilon}, \quad \tilde D_{\bar F} = \frac{i}{p^2 - m^2 - i\varepsilon}, \quad \tilde D_R = \frac{i}{(p^0 + i\varepsilon)^2 - E_{\mathbf p}^2} = \frac{i}{p^2 - m^2 + i\varepsilon\operatorname{sgn}p^0}, \quad \tilde D_A = \frac{i}{(p^0 - i\varepsilon)^2 - E_{\mathbf p}^2} .
> $$
>
> $\tilde D_F$ depends on $p$ only through $p^2$ ($m^2 \to m^2 - i\varepsilon$) and is manifestly Lorentz invariant; $\tilde D_R$ is invariant under orthochronous transformations.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.9 · PHY 513 Lecture 6, "Deforming Integrand: the Feynman Propagator", "Relativistic Form of Feynman Propagator" · PS §2.4, eq. (2.59) · Yu §6.4.1, eq. (6.210)*

^thm-c2-6-9

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Passing above a pole at $E$ is the same, by Cauchy's theorem, as integrating along $\mathbb R$ with the pole moved to $E - i\varepsilon$ ([[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-2-7|Theorem §CA.2.7]]). *Feynman:* poles at $p^0 = \pm(E - i\varepsilon)$, and $(p^0)^2 - (E - i\varepsilon)^2 = p^2 - m^2 + 2iE\varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2)$; only the sign of the infinitesimal matters and $E > 0$, so $2E\varepsilon \to \varepsilon$. *Anti-Feynman:* the mirror image. *Retarded:* both poles below, at $\pm E - i\varepsilon$, i.e. $(p^0 + i\varepsilon)^2 - E^2 = p^2 - m^2 + 2ip^0\varepsilon$. *Advanced:* both above. The sign of $p^0$ is invariant under $SO^+(1,3)$ for timelike $p$ ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|REL Theorem §B1.3.1]]), and only near the shell, where $p$ is timelike, does the infinitesimal matter.

## Wick rotation and the Euclidean propagator

> [!definition] Definition §C2.6.4: Euclidean Propagator
> With $x_E = (\tau, \mathbf x)$, $p_E = (p_4, \mathbf p)$ and Euclidean products,
>
> $$
> D_E(x_E) \equiv \int\frac{d^4p_E}{(2\pi)^4}\,\frac{e^{ip_E\cdot x_E}}{p_E^2 + m^2} .
> $$
>
> Its denominator never vanishes, and it is invariant under four-dimensional rotations; its value, $mK_1(mR)/4\pi^2R$ with $R = |x_E|$, is [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-6|Theorem §C2.5.6]].
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 2 §2.2 (eq. (DE)), Ch. 6 §6.10 · PHY 513 Lecture 6, "Another perspective: the Euclidean Green's Function"*

^def-c2-6-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.6.11: Wick Rotation
> 1. In a momentum integral whose integrand decays like $1/(p^0)^2$, the real $p^0$-axis may be rotated counterclockwise onto $p^0 = ip_4$ without crossing a Feynman pole, and then $\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{i\,F(p)}{p^2 - m^2 + i\varepsilon} = \int\frac{d^4p_E}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{F}{p_E^2 + m^2}$ for $F$ analytic in the swept quadrants.
> 2. In position space the time must rotate too: $D_F(-i\tau, \boldsymbol\xi) = D_E(\tau, \boldsymbol\xi)$ for $\tau > 0$.
> 3. Consequently, for all real $\xi$,
>
> $$
> D_F(\xi) = \frac{m\,K_1(ms)}{4\pi^2s}, \qquad s = \sqrt{-\xi^2 + i\varepsilon} ,
> $$
>
> even in $\xi$, real at spacelike separation, with the same branch for both signs of $\xi^0$ inside the cone: the Feynman prescription is the infinitesimal Wick rotation $t \to t(1 - i\varepsilon)$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.10 (Caution "When the rotation is legitimate", "The Feynman function in position space") · PHY 513 Lecture 6*

^thm-c2-6-11

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *1.* The Feynman poles $\pm(E - i\varepsilon)$ lie in the fourth and second quadrants; rotating the real axis counterclockwise by $90°$ sweeps the first and third, crossing nothing, and the arcs vanish by the ML estimate ([[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-2-6|Theorem §CA.2.6]]). On $p^0 = ip_4$: $dp^0 = i\,dp_4$ and $p^2 - m^2 = -(p_E^2 + m^2)$, so $i\,dp^0/(p^2 - m^2) = dp_4/(p_E^2 + m^2)$.
>
> *2.* With $e^{-ip^0t}$ present, $|e^{-ip^0t}| = e^{t\operatorname{Im}p^0}$ grows in one of the swept quadrants for every real $t \ne 0$, so the rotation of $p^0$ alone is not allowed. Rotate $t = -i\tau$ as well, keeping $p^0t = p_4\tau$ real. Directly: for $\operatorname{Re}\xi^0 > 0$, $D_F = D_W(\xi)$, the boundary value of $W(z, \boldsymbol\xi)$ from the lower half $z$-plane ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-5|Theorem §C2.5.5]]), and on the ray $z = -i\tau$ it is $D_E$ by [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-6|Theorem §C2.5.6]].
>
> *3.* For $\xi^0 > 0$, $D_F = D_W(\xi)$ from $\xi^0 - i\varepsilon$, so $-\xi^2 \to -\xi^2 + 2i\varepsilon\xi^0$; for $\xi^0 < 0$, $D_F = D_W(-\xi)$ from $-\xi^0 - i\varepsilon$, so $-\xi^2 \to -\xi^2 - 2i\varepsilon\xi^0$. In both cases the infinitesimal is $+i\varepsilon$; insert into [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-7|Theorem §C2.5.7]]. And $t \to t(1 - i\varepsilon)$ turns $-t^2$ into $-t^2 + 2i\varepsilon t^2$, the same sign.

> [!remark] Remark: Why the Feynman contour is singled out
> On the imaginary axis $p^2 - m^2 = -(p_4^2 + \mathbf p^2 + m^2) < 0$: the pole problem disappears, and energy and momentum enter symmetrically. In the slides' words, the Feynman prescription is the Euclidean prescription, analytically continued to Lorentzian signature: four-dimensional rotation symmetry, which is relativity continued to imaginary time, is manifest at every step. That is how loop integrals will be done (QFT C7, planned). (The slides write $p^0_E = ip^0_M$; $p^0 = ip_4$ here differs by $p_4 \to -p_4$, which the integral does not see.) The imaginary-time function is also the vacuum two-point function at imaginary time ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-6|Theorem §C2.5.6]]), so Euclidean, Wightman and Feynman functions are three boundary values of one analytic function.

## The two-point functions, all together

> [!definition] Definition §C2.6.5: The Two-Point Family
> With $\xi = x - y$, vacuum expectation values, "homogeneous" meaning $(\partial^2 + m^2)f = 0$ and "Green's" meaning $(\partial^2 + m^2)f = -i\delta^4$:
>
> | name | definition | type | momentum space (times $\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4}e^{-ip\cdot\xi}$) |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | Wightman $D_W(\xi)$ | $\langle0\vert\phi(x)\phi(y)\vert0\rangle$ | homogeneous | $2\pi\theta(p^0)\delta(p^2 - m^2)$ |
> | reversed $D_W(-\xi)$ | $\langle0\vert\phi(y)\phi(x)\vert0\rangle$ | homogeneous | $2\pi\theta(-p^0)\delta(p^2 - m^2)$ |
> | commutator $iD(\xi)$ | $\langle0\vert[\phi(x), \phi(y)]\vert0\rangle$ | homogeneous | $2\pi\operatorname{sgn}(p^0)\delta(p^2 - m^2)$ |
> | Hadamard $D_1(\xi)$ | $\langle0\vert\{\phi(x), \phi(y)\}\vert0\rangle$ | homogeneous | $2\pi\delta(p^2 - m^2)$ |
> | retarded $D_R(\xi)$ | $\theta(\xi^0)\,iD(\xi)$ | Green's | $\frac{i}{(p^0 + i\varepsilon)^2 - E_{\mathbf p}^2}$ |
> | advanced $D_A(\xi)$ | $-\theta(-\xi^0)\,iD(\xi)$ | Green's | $\frac{i}{(p^0 - i\varepsilon)^2 - E_{\mathbf p}^2}$ |
> | principal $\bar D(\xi)$ | $\frac12(D_R + D_A)$ | Green's | $i\,\mathcal P\frac{1}{p^2 - m^2}$ |
> | Feynman $D_F(\xi)$ | $\langle0\vert T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}\vert0\rangle$ | Green's | $\frac{i}{p^2 - m^2 + i\varepsilon}$ |
> | anti-Feynman $D_{\bar F}(\xi)$ | $-\langle0\vert\bar T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}\vert0\rangle$ | Green's | $\frac{i}{p^2 - m^2 - i\varepsilon}$ |
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.11 (Definition "The family"), §6.8 (table of contours)*

^def-c2-6-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.6.12: Relations in the Two-Point Family
> 1. $D_R - D_A = iD$, and $\bar D = \frac12\operatorname{sgn}(\xi^0)\,iD$.
> 2. $D_F = \frac12D_1 + \bar D$ and $D_{\bar F} = -\frac12D_1 + \bar D = -\overline{D_F}$; hence $D_F - D_{\bar F} = D_1$, $\operatorname{Re}D_F = \frac12D_1$, $\operatorname{Im}D_F = \frac12\operatorname{sgn}(\xi^0)D$.
> 3. $D_F = D_R + D_W(-\xi) = D_A + D_W(\xi)$.
> 4. At spacelike separation $D_R = D_A = \bar D = 0$ and $D_F = D_W = \frac12D_1 = \dfrac{m\,K_1(mr)}{4\pi^2r}$, $r = \sqrt{-\xi^2}$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.11 (Derivations "Relations in position space", "Relations in momentum space", "At spacelike separation"), §6.8 (eq. (Grelations))*

^thm-c2-6-12

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Everything follows from $D_W(\pm\xi) = \frac12\bigl(D_1(\xi) \pm iD(\xi)\bigr)$ with $D_1$ even, $D$ odd and both real ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-9|Theorem §C2.5.9]]).
>
> *1.* $D_R - D_A = [\theta(\xi^0) + \theta(-\xi^0)]\,iD$; $\bar D = \frac12[\theta(\xi^0) - \theta(-\xi^0)]\,iD$.
>
> *2.* $D_F = \theta(\xi^0)\frac12(D_1 + iD) + \theta(-\xi^0)\frac12(D_1 - iD) = \frac12D_1 + \frac12\operatorname{sgn}(\xi^0)iD$. The anti-time-ordered product gives $-[\theta(\xi^0)D_W(-\xi) + \theta(-\xi^0)D_W(\xi)] = -\frac12D_1 + \frac12\operatorname{sgn}(\xi^0)iD$, and since $D_W(-\xi) = \overline{D_W(\xi)}$ this is $-\overline{D_F}$. Real and imaginary parts follow because $D$, $D_1$ are real.
>
> *3.* [[§C2.6 Green's Functions and the Feynman Propagator#^thm-c2-6-6|Theorem §C2.6.6]].
>
> *4.* $D = 0$ there ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-10|Theorem §C2.5.10]]), so every function built on the commutator vanishes, and $D_W = \frac12D_1$ is [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-7|Theorem §C2.5.7]], 1.

> [!derivation]- Derivation (second route: momentum space)
> By Sokhotski–Plemelj ([[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-8|Theorem §CA.1.8]]), each retarded or advanced denominator $p^2 - m^2 \pm i\varepsilon\operatorname{sgn}p^0$ is $\mathcal P\frac{1}{p^2 - m^2} \mp i\pi\operatorname{sgn}(p^0)\delta(p^2 - m^2)$; the delta terms cancel in the average, so $\tilde{\bar D} = i\mathcal P\frac{1}{p^2 - m^2}$. And
>
> $$
> \tilde D_F = \frac{i}{p^2 - m^2 + i\varepsilon} = i\,\mathcal P\frac{1}{p^2 - m^2} + \pi\,\delta(p^2 - m^2) = \tilde{\bar D} + \tfrac12\tilde D_1 ,
> $$
>
> which is part 2 again: the principal value is the Green's-function part, the delta function the homogeneous part. The position-space derivation used only operator algebra, this one only distribution theory.

> [!remark] Remark: Which Green's function when
> There is no single right answer: the choice is a boundary condition, and the physical question imposes it. A source switched on in the laboratory and measured afterwards calls for the retarded function: classical radiation (the retarded potentials and Liénard–Wiechert fields are the massless retarded function, [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|EM Theorem §B11.1.4]]), linear response in condensed matter (Remark: Measurable response is a retarded commutator), and particle production by a classical source ([[§C2.7 Particle Production by a Classical Source|§C2.7]]). Quantum amplitudes will turn out to involve time-ordered products, hence the Feynman function; with interactions this will be derived, not chosen (QFT C6, planned). The Feynman function treats the two orderings symmetrically, positive frequency forward and negative frequency backward in time, the reading of the two terms of the commutator in [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^rem-c2-5-7|§C2.5, Remark: Two orderings that cancel]].
>

> [!remark] Remark: Preview: the propagator as a Gaussian covariance
> In the path integral (QFT C11, planned; PS §9.1–9.2; Yu §11.1–11.2) time-ordered vacuum correlators are averages over field histories, $\langle0|T\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle = \int\mathcal D\phi\,\phi(x)\phi(y)e^{iS}/\int\mathcal D\phi\,e^{iS}$, with $S = \frac12\int\phi(-\partial^2 - m^2)\phi$. In momentum space this is a Gaussian with kernel $K = p^2 - m^2$, which converges only with a damping $e^{-\frac\varepsilon2\int\phi^2}$; its covariance is then $i(K + i\varepsilon)^{-1} = \tilde D_F$. The $i\varepsilon$ prescribed here becomes the condition for the integral to exist, time ordering is produced rather than imposed, and with $t = -i\tau$ the weight becomes $e^{-S_E}$, whose covariance is $D_E$. Choosing the vacuum as initial and final state selects the Feynman inverse of the Klein–Gordon operator, not the retarded one: contours are boundary conditions once more. The integral of a total derivative gives $(\partial^2 + m^2)D_F = -i\delta^4$ in one line, the free Schwinger–Dyson equation.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.13 (Principle "Looking ahead: the path integral (Lectures 23–24)")*

^rem-c2-6-10

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Exponential clustering
> At spacelike separation every function that involves the commutator vanishes, and what remains is the vacuum correlation $D_1$, decaying like $r^{-3/2}e^{-mr}$. That the correlation length equals the Compton wavelength is not special to the free field: in any theory with a mass gap $m$, connected vacuum correlations at spacelike separation decay at least as fast as $e^{-mr}$ (exponential clustering, a theorem of axiomatic field theory). The Euclidean continuation shows why: in imaginary time a gap is a correlation length, as in statistical mechanics.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.11 ("At spacelike separation")*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Mode by mode the retarded function is $\theta(t)(-i\sin Et)/E$, the impulse response of an undamped oscillator; the damped version $\theta(t)e^{-\gamma t/2}\sin\omega_dt/m\omega_d$ is [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-6|WO Theorem §B4.4.6]], whose transfer function $\chi(\omega)$ has its poles in one half-plane exactly as $\tilde D_R$ does. A field is one such oscillator per $\mathbf p$.
> - The massless retarded function, $D_R = -\frac{i}{4\pi r}\delta(t - r)$ for $t > 0$ ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-11|Theorem §C2.5.11]]), turns $i\int D_Rj$ into $\int d^3y\,j(t - |\mathbf x - \mathbf y|, \mathbf y)/4\pi|\mathbf x - \mathbf y|$: the retarded potentials of [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|EM Theorem §B11.1.4]] (with $\partial^2 = -\Box$ in EM's sign).
> - A static point source $j = g\,\delta^3(\mathbf y)$ gives, through the retarded function integrated over $y^0$, $\phi = g\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}}{\mathbf p^2 + m^2} = \frac{g}{4\pi}\frac{e^{-mr}}{r}$: the Yukawa field of [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^rem-b4-1-4|REL Remark: Static solutions and the Yukawa potential]], the $p^0 = 0$ slice of $\tilde D$.
> - The $\pm i\varepsilon$ as boundary conditions has a nonrelativistic twin: the outgoing and incoming resolvents $(E - H_0 \pm i\varepsilon)^{-1}$ of scattering theory, whose $k$-plane contour is closed exactly as here ([[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-3|QM Theorem §C10.1.3]], [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^rem-c10-1-2|QM Remark: Why the sign of iε matters]]), and the causal energy transform of the propagator ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-7|QM Theorem §C4.1.7]]).
> - Wick rotation is the field version of imaginary time in quantum statistical mechanics: $e^{-iHt} \to e^{-H\tau}$ links the propagator to the partition function ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-6|QM Theorem §C4.1.6]]) and the Euclidean action to a Boltzmann weight; the Euclidean propagator is the correlation function of a statistical field.
> - The contraction $D_F$ is the building block of Wick's theorem and of every internal line of a Feynman diagram (QFT C6–C7, planned); for spinor and vector fields the numerator changes but the $i\varepsilon$ does not (QFT C4–C5, planned; Yu eq. (6.262)).
> - Normal ordering ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]) and time ordering meet in Theorem §C2.6.8; the zero-point energy is the same divergence as the light-cone singularity of $D_W$.
