What are the states of the free real scalar field, and what do they describe? [[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields|§C2.1]] supplied the postulate ([[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]]) and showed that the classical field is a set of oscillators ([[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^thm-c2-1-2|Theorem §C2.1.2]]); Quantum Mechanics supplies the oscillator algebra ([[§C3.4 The Harmonic Oscillator and Coherent States Revisited#^thm-c3-4-1|QM Theorem §C3.4.1]]) and the Fock space of identical particles ([[§C12.2★ Second Quantization#^def-c12-2-1|QM Def. §C12.2.1]]). This section derives the mode expansion and its algebra, reduces $H$ and $\mathbf P$ to one oscillator per momentum, and reads off particles. What the field adds to second quantization is the continuum and relativity: one-particle states labelled by a continuous momentum, normalized so that the normalization is Lorentz invariant, with the invariant measure $d^3p/(2\pi)^32E_{\mathbf p}$; the tool that extracts the oscillators is the Klein–Gordon inner product. The steps are [[P1 Canonical Quantization#^p1-3|P1, steps 3–7]].

> [!model] Model §C2.2.1: The Free Real Scalar Quantum Field
> A Hermitian field $\phi(\mathbf x)$ and momentum density $\pi(\mathbf x)$ satisfying the equal-time relations of [[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]], with Hamiltonian
>
> $$
> H = \int d^3x\,\Bigl[\tfrac12\pi^2 + \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\phi^2\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> the quantization of $\mathcal L = \frac12\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi - \frac12m^2\phi^2$, for which classically $\pi = \dot\phi$.
>
> *Assumptions:* free (quadratic $\mathcal L$: linear field equation, no interactions); $m > 0$; flat spacetime with an inertial time; infinite space, continuum (delta-function) normalization; the operator ordering in $H$ is fixed in [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]].
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.4 and Ch. 4 §4.2 · PS §2.3 · Yu §2.3, eqs. (2.79), (2.123)–(2.124)*

^mod-c2-2-1

The classical model is [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^mod-b4-1-3|REL Model §B4.1.3]] with $c = \hbar = 1$; Quantum Mechanics quantized it in a box ([[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^def-c13-1-1|QM Def. §C13.1.1]]). The general integration rules (plane-wave delta function, derivatives under the integral, integration by parts, angular integrals) are in [[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-4|Theorems §CA.1.4]]–[[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-6|§CA.1.6]], and the interchanges of limits and integrals are licensed by [[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-1|Theorem §CA.1.1]]; two rules are specific to mode expansions:

> [!remark] Remark: Two bookkeeping rules for mode integrals
> $$
> \int d^3x\;e^{\mp i(p \pm q)\cdot x} = e^{\mp i(E_{\mathbf p} \pm E_{\mathbf q})t}\,(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p \pm \mathbf q) \qquad (p^0 = E_{\mathbf p},\ q^0 = E_{\mathbf q}).
> $$
>
> - **Split exponentials.** A fixed-time $d^3x$ integral touches only the spatial factor, as displayed; the delta then equates the energies, so the phase is $e^{\mp2iE_{\mathbf p}t}$ (for $p + q$) or $1$ (for $p - q$). This is the only place time and space are separated.
> - **Relabel.** In an integral over all $\mathbf p$ one may substitute $\mathbf p \to -\mathbf p$ in a single term: the Jacobian is $1$ and $E_{-\mathbf p} = E_{\mathbf p}$; only the exponent and the operator index change. Keep the index $-\mathbf p$ on creation operators until the delta has been used, and give the two integration variables of a product different names ($\mathbf p$, $\mathbf q$); a dropped sign turns $[a_{\mathbf p}, a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}]$ into $[a_{\mathbf p}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}]$ and produces a $\delta^3(0)$ where none belongs.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.3 (rules 2 and 3 of "The six rules"; Caution "Where the errors live")*

## The mode expansion

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.2: Mode Expansion of the Real Field
> Every real solution of $(\partial^2 + m^2)\phi = 0$ is
>
> $$
> \phi(x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\Bigl(a_{\mathbf p}\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + a_{\mathbf p}^*\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigr)\Big|_{p^0 = E_{\mathbf p}}, \qquad \pi = \dot\phi = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}(-i)\sqrt{\frac{E_{\mathbf p}}{2}}\Bigl(a_{\mathbf p}\,e^{-ip\cdot x} - a_{\mathbf p}^*\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Accordingly the operators of [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^mod-c2-2-1|Model §C2.2.1]] are written at $t = 0$ with operators $a_{\mathbf p}$, $a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger$:
>
> $$
> \phi(\mathbf x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\bigl(a_{\mathbf p} + a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}\bigr)e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}, \qquad \pi(\mathbf x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}(-i)\sqrt{\frac{E_{\mathbf p}}{2}}\bigl(a_{\mathbf p} - a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}\bigr)e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x} .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.4 · PHY 513 Lecture 4, Part B · PS §2.3, eqs. (2.25)–(2.28) · Yu §2.3.1, eqs. (2.97)–(2.105)*

^thm-c2-2-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> A plane wave $e^{-ik\cdot x}$ solves the equation iff $k^2 = m^2$, i.e. $k^0 = \pm E_{\mathbf k}$ ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-5|REL Theorem §B4.1.5]]): two solutions per $\mathbf k$, both needed to match arbitrary initial data $\phi$, $\dot\phi$ (each mode obeys $\ddot{\tilde\phi} + E_{\mathbf k}^2\tilde\phi = 0$, [[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^thm-c2-1-2|Theorem §C2.1.2]]). So
>
> $$
> \phi(x) = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}}}\Bigl(a_{\mathbf k}\,e^{-iE_{\mathbf k}t + i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} + \tilde a_{\mathbf k}\,e^{+iE_{\mathbf k}t + i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}\Bigr),
> $$
>
> the factor $1/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}}$ being a choice made for [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]. Relabel $\mathbf k \to -\mathbf k$ in the second term ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^rem-c2-2-1|Remark: Two bookkeeping rules]]): both exponents become $\mp ip\cdot x$ with the same on-shell $p$, and the coefficient of $e^{+ip\cdot x}$ is $\tilde a_{-\mathbf p}$. Reality, $\phi^* = \phi$, compares the coefficients of the independent functions $e^{\mp ip\cdot x}$ and forces $\tilde a_{-\mathbf p} = a_{\mathbf p}^*$. Differentiating, $\partial_te^{\mp ip\cdot x} = \mp iE_{\mathbf p}e^{\mp ip\cdot x}$ and $E_{\mathbf p}/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}} = \sqrt{E_{\mathbf p}/2}$ give $\pi$. At $t = 0$, $e^{-ip\cdot x} = e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}$; relabelling the $a^*$ terms puts both on $e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}$. Quantization replaces the coefficients by operators and complex conjugation by Hermitian conjugation, which keeps $\phi$ and $\pi$ Hermitian.

## The Klein–Gordon inner product

> [!definition] Definition §C2.2.1: Klein–Gordon Inner Product
> For complex solutions $f$, $g$ of the Klein–Gordon equation,
>
> $$
> (f, g) = i\int d^3x\,\bigl(f^*\,\partial_tg - (\partial_tf^*)\,g\bigr) \equiv i\int d^3x\;f^*\overleftrightarrow{\partial_t}g ,
> $$
>
> evaluated on any time slice; it is antilinear in $f$ and linear in $g$. The **positive-frequency modes** are $f_{\mathbf p}(x) = e^{-ip\cdot x}/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$, $p^0 = E_{\mathbf p}$; their conjugates $f^*_{\mathbf p}$ are the negative-frequency modes.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.5 · PHY 513 Problem Set 3, Problem 3*

^def-c2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.3: Conservation, Orthonormality and Mode Extraction
> 1. For solutions $f$, $g$, $(f, g)$ does not depend on $t$, and $(f^*, g^*) = -(f, g)^*$.
> 2. $(f_{\mathbf p}, f_{\mathbf q}) = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$, $(f^*_{\mathbf p}, f^*_{\mathbf q}) = -(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$, $(f_{\mathbf p}, f^*_{\mathbf q}) = 0$: the product is indefinite, positive on positive frequencies and negative on negative ones.
> 3. In terms of the field of [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], $\phi = \int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3}\bigl(a_{\mathbf q}f_{\mathbf q} + a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}f^*_{\mathbf q}\bigr)$, the mode operators are extracted as
>
> $$
> a_{\mathbf p} = (f_{\mathbf p}, \phi) = \frac{i}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\int d^3x\;e^{ip\cdot x}\bigl(\pi(x) - iE_{\mathbf p}\,\phi(x)\bigr), \qquad a^\dagger_{\mathbf p} = -(f^*_{\mathbf p}, \phi) ,
> $$
>
> with $\partial_t\phi = \pi$; the explicit $t$ drops out.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.5, eqs. (KG inner product, orthonormality, selection rule) · PHY 513 Problem Set 3, Problem 3(a)–(b) and course solutions · PS §2.3, p. 21 ("the inverse expressions … are easy to derive") · Yu §2.3.2*

^thm-c2-2-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *1.* The bilinear current $j^\mu(f, g) = i\bigl(f^*\partial^\mu g - (\partial^\mu f^*)g\bigr)$ has $\partial_\mu j^\mu = i\bigl(f^*\partial^2g - (\partial^2f^*)g\bigr)$, the cross terms cancelling, and the Klein–Gordon equation makes this $i(-m^2 + m^2)f^*g = 0$. So $(f, g) = \int d^3x\,j^0$ is a conserved charge for fields that fall off at infinity (integrate $\partial_tj^0 = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf j$ over space). Directly: $\partial_t(f, g) = i\int(f^*\ddot g - \ddot f^*g) = i\int(f^*\nabla^2g - (\nabla^2f^*)g) = 0$ by integrating by parts twice. For the conjugation identity, $(f, g)^* = -i\int(f\,\partial_tg^* - \partial_tf\,g^*) = -(f^*, g^*)$.
>
> *2.* With $\partial_tf_{\mathbf q} = -iE_{\mathbf q}f_{\mathbf q}$ and $\partial_tf^*_{\mathbf p} = iE_{\mathbf p}f^*_{\mathbf p}$, and the split-exponential rule ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^rem-c2-2-1|Remark: Two bookkeeping rules]]),
>
> $$
> (f_{\mathbf p}, f_{\mathbf q}) = \frac{E_{\mathbf p} + E_{\mathbf q}}{2\sqrt{E_{\mathbf p}E_{\mathbf q}}}\,e^{i(E_{\mathbf p} - E_{\mathbf q})t}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q) = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q),
> $$
>
> since the delta sets $E_{\mathbf q} = E_{\mathbf p}$. For $(f_{\mathbf p}, f^*_{\mathbf q})$ the prefactor is $\propto (E_{\mathbf q} - E_{\mathbf p})$ while the delta is $\delta^3(\mathbf p + \mathbf q)$, which also sets $E_{\mathbf q} = E_{\mathbf p}$: it vanishes. The middle relation is part 1 applied to the first.
>
> *3.* Apply $(f_{\mathbf p}, \cdot)$ to the expansion term by term: $(f_{\mathbf p}, f_{\mathbf q})$ collapses the integral and $(f_{\mathbf p}, f^*_{\mathbf q}) = 0$ removes the creation part. For $-(f^*_{\mathbf p}, \phi)$, use $(f^*_{\mathbf p}, f_{\mathbf q}) = -(f_{\mathbf p}, f^*_{\mathbf q})^* = 0$ and $(f^*_{\mathbf p}, f^*_{\mathbf q}) = -(2\pi)^3\delta^3$. Writing out $(f_{\mathbf p}, \phi) = i\int\bigl(f^*_{\mathbf p}\pi - iE_{\mathbf p}f^*_{\mathbf p}\phi\bigr)$ gives the explicit form. As a check by direct Fourier inversion: $\int d^3x\,e^{ip\cdot x}\phi = (a_{\mathbf p} + e^{2iE_{\mathbf p}t}a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p})/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$ and $\int d^3x\,e^{ip\cdot x}\pi = -iE_{\mathbf p}(a_{\mathbf p} - e^{2iE_{\mathbf p}t}a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p})/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$; the combination $\pi - iE_{\mathbf p}\phi$ cancels the $a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}$ terms and their time dependence, leaving $-2iE_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p}/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$.

> [!remark] Remark: Why this inner product, and why it is indefinite
> - **A projection.** Extracting one coefficient from a superposition is what an inner product does, $c_n = \langle e_n|f\rangle$; the field is a superposition of plane waves with operator coefficients, and $(f_{\mathbf p}, \cdot)$ is the inner product that does it. Part 3 is the inversion formula of Peskin–Schroeder and Yu in three symbols.
> - **A Noether charge.** $(f, g)$ is the charge of a conserved current, so it is the same on every slice; that is why the extracted $a_{\mathbf p}$ carries no $t$, and why it is the same operator in the Schrödinger and Heisenberg pictures. For $f = g = \phi$ the current is minus the U(1) Noether current of the complex field ([[§C2.3 The Complex Scalar Field and Its Charge#^cau-c2-3-1|Caution: Normalization and sign of the charge]]).
> - **Indefinite, and that is the point.** Unlike a Hilbert-space inner product, $(f, f)$ can be negative. Quantum Mechanics met the same density as the would-be probability of a Klein–Gordon wave function and had to give it up for that reason ([[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^thm-c13-1-1|QM Theorem §C13.1.1]]). In the field theory the sign is the basis-free statement of the positive/negative-frequency split: positive-norm modes carry annihilators, negative-norm modes creators.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.5*

^rem-c2-2-3

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Other mode functions, Bogoliubov transformations and the vacuum
> Nothing in Theorem §C2.2.3 used plane waves except to check part 2. Any complete set of solutions $\{u_i\}$ with $(u_i, u_j) = \delta_{ij}$, $(u^*_i, u^*_j) = -\delta_{ij}$, $(u_i, u^*_j) = 0$ gives $\phi = \sum_i(a_iu_i + a_i^\dagger u^*_i)$ with $a_i = (u_i, \phi)$ and, by the computation of [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]], $[a_i, a_j^\dagger] = \delta_{ij}$. So a complete set of positive-norm modes *is* a choice of annihilation operators, hence of a vacuum and a Fock space. Two such choices are related by a Bogoliubov transformation, whose mixing is measured by the overlaps $(u_i, \bar u^*_j) \ne 0$; the two vacua then differ, and for infinitely many modes they can be unitarily inequivalent. In flat space with an inertial time the split by the sign of the frequency is canonical, which is why it can be taken for granted here; for a field in a box or a background it is replaced by the appropriate modes, and in curved spacetime, for accelerated observers (the Unruh effect) or in interacting theories (Haag's theorem) the choice genuinely matters (Wald, *Quantum Field Theory in Curved Spacetime*, Ch. 4; Streater and Wightman).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.5 ("The general statement") and §4.8 (Principle "Why |0⟩ is the vacuum")*

## The mode algebra

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.4: The Mode Algebra
> For the field and momentum of [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], the equal-time relations of [[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]] hold if and only if
>
> $$
> [a_{\mathbf p}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}] = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q), \qquad [a_{\mathbf p}, a_{\mathbf q}] = [a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}] = 0 :
> $$
>
> one oscillator algebra per momentum, the $(2\pi)^3$ accompanying the measure $d^3p/(2\pi)^3$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.5 · PHY 513 Lecture 4, Part B (⇐ in the lecture, ⇒ in Problem Set 3, Problem 3(c), with the course solutions) · PS §2.3, eqs. (2.29)–(2.30) · Yu §2.3.2, eqs. (2.115)–(2.122)*

^thm-c2-2-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *⇒, through the inner product.* By [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]], $a_{\mathbf p} = i\int d^3x\,(f^*_{\mathbf p}\pi - \dot f^*_{\mathbf p}\phi)$ and $a^\dagger_{\mathbf q} = -i\int d^3y\,(f_{\mathbf q}\pi - \dot f_{\mathbf q}\phi)$, at a common time. Only $[\pi(\mathbf x), \phi(\mathbf y)] = -i\delta^3$ and $[\phi(\mathbf x), \pi(\mathbf y)] = i\delta^3$ survive:
>
> $$
> [a_{\mathbf p}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}] = \int d^3x\,d^3y\,\Bigl(-f^*_{\mathbf p}(x)\dot f_{\mathbf q}(y)\,[\pi(\mathbf x), \phi(\mathbf y)] - \dot f^*_{\mathbf p}(x)f_{\mathbf q}(y)\,[\phi(\mathbf x), \pi(\mathbf y)]\Bigr) = i\int d^3x\,\bigl(f^*_{\mathbf p}\dot f_{\mathbf q} - \dot f^*_{\mathbf p}f_{\mathbf q}\bigr) = (f_{\mathbf p}, f_{\mathbf q}) ,
> $$
>
> which is $(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ by Theorem §C2.2.3, 2: the commutator of two extracted operators is the inner product of their mode functions. The same steps give $[a_{\mathbf p}, a_{\mathbf q}] = -(f_{\mathbf p}, f^*_{\mathbf q}) = 0$, and $[a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}] = -[a_{\mathbf p}, a_{\mathbf q}]^\dagger = 0$. Written with the explicit forms, the first computation reads $[\pi - iE_{\mathbf p}\phi,\ \pi + iE_{\mathbf q}\phi] = (E_{\mathbf p} + E_{\mathbf q})\,\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$ and $[a_{\mathbf p}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}] = \frac{E_{\mathbf p} + E_{\mathbf q}}{2\sqrt{E_{\mathbf p}E_{\mathbf q}}}e^{i(E_{\mathbf p} - E_{\mathbf q})t}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$; for $[a_{\mathbf p}, a_{\mathbf q}]$ the prefactor is $\propto (E_{\mathbf p} - E_{\mathbf q})$ on the support of $\delta^3(\mathbf p + \mathbf q)$.

*Uses:* [[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]], [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]

> [!derivation]- Derivation (second route: from the oscillator algebra back to the fields, as in Lecture 4)
> Use the $t = 0$ forms with both terms on $e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}$, integration variables $\mathbf p$ for $\phi(\mathbf x)$ and $\mathbf p'$ for $\pi(\mathbf y)$. In $[a_{\mathbf p} + a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p},\ a_{\mathbf p'} - a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p'}]$ two terms survive, $[a_{\mathbf p}, -a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p'}]$ and $[a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}, a_{\mathbf p'}]$, each $-(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p + \mathbf p')$; they add. The delta sets $\mathbf p' = -\mathbf p$, cancels one $(2\pi)^3$, makes $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sqrt{\frac{E_{\mathbf p}}{2}} = \frac12$ and combines the exponentials into $e^{i\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)}$. The factors are $(-i)\cdot\frac12\cdot(-2) = i$:
>
> $$
> [\phi(\mathbf x), \pi(\mathbf y)] = i\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,e^{i\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)} = i\,\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y) .
> $$
>
> In $[\phi(\mathbf x), \phi(\mathbf y)]$ the two surviving terms $[a_{\mathbf p}, a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p'}]$ and $[a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}, a_{\mathbf p'}]$ have opposite signs and cancel, and likewise in $[\pi, \pi]$.

## The Hamiltonian, the momentum and normal ordering

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.5: Hamiltonian and Momentum in Mode Form
> With $H$ of [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^mod-c2-2-1|Model §C2.2.1]], in the order written, and the momentum $\mathbf P = -\int d^3x\,\pi\nabla\phi$ (the Noether charge of spatial translations; QFT C1, planned),
>
> $$
> H = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{E_{\mathbf p}}{2}\bigl(a_{\mathbf p}a^\dagger_{\mathbf p} + a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p}\bigr) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,E_{\mathbf p}\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p} + E_0, \qquad E_0 = V\!\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{E_{\mathbf p}}{2} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \mathbf P = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,\mathbf p\;a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p} ,
> $$
>
> where $V = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf 0) = \int d^3x$. $\mathbf P$ needs no reordering and has no constant.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §§4.6–4.7, eqs. (Hfinal), (Pfinal) · PHY 513 Lecture 4, Part C · PS §2.3, eqs. (2.31), (2.33) · Yu §2.3.3, eqs. (2.130)–(2.134), (2.141)–(2.144)*

^thm-c2-2-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *Hamiltonian.* Insert the $t = 0$ forms of [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]] with variables $\mathbf p$, $\mathbf p'$; the $d^3x$ integral gives $(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p + \mathbf p')$, so $\mathbf p' = -\mathbf p$ and $E_{\mathbf p'} = E_{\mathbf p}$. The term $\frac12\pi^2$ carries $(-i)^2\frac{E_{\mathbf p}}{2}\cdot\frac12$; the gradient term carries $\frac12(i\mathbf p)\cdot(-i\mathbf p)/2E_{\mathbf p} = \mathbf p^2/4E_{\mathbf p}$ and the mass term $m^2/4E_{\mathbf p}$, together $(\mathbf p^2 + m^2)/4E_{\mathbf p} = E_{\mathbf p}/4$. So
>
> $$
> H = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{E_{\mathbf p}}{4}\Bigl[-\bigl(a_{\mathbf p} - a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}\bigr)\bigl(a_{-\mathbf p} - a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}\bigr) + \bigl(a_{\mathbf p} + a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}\bigr)\bigl(a_{-\mathbf p} + a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}\bigr)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> The $a_{\mathbf p}a_{-\mathbf p}$ and $a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$ terms appear with opposite signs and cancel; the others add, giving $\frac{E_{\mathbf p}}{2}\bigl(a_{\mathbf p}a^\dagger_{\mathbf p} + a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}a_{-\mathbf p}\bigr)$, and relabelling the second term gives the first form. Then $a_{\mathbf p}a^\dagger_{\mathbf p} = a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p} + (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf 0)$ ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]]), and $\int d^3x\,e^{i\mathbf 0\cdot\mathbf x} = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf 0)$ identifies it with $V$. Computed instead at time $t$ with the time-dependent expansion, the $aa$ and $a^\dagger a^\dagger$ terms carry $e^{\mp2iE_{\mathbf p}t}$ and the coefficient $-E_{\mathbf p}^2 + \mathbf p^2 + m^2 = 0$: the mass shell is what makes $H$ time independent (Yu eq. (2.130)).
>
> *Momentum.* In $-\pi\nabla\phi$ the factors are $(-i)\sqrt{E_{\mathbf p}/2}\cdot(i\mathbf p')/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p'}} \to -\mathbf p/2$ at $\mathbf p' = -\mathbf p$, so
>
> $$
> \mathbf P = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{\mathbf p}{2}\bigl(a_{\mathbf p}a_{-\mathbf p} + a_{\mathbf p}a^\dagger_{\mathbf p} - a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}a_{-\mathbf p} - a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The terms $\mathbf p\,a_{\mathbf p}a_{-\mathbf p}$ and $\mathbf p\,a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$ are odd under $\mathbf p \to -\mathbf p$ (the operators commute) and integrate to zero; relabelling $-\mathbf p\,a^\dagger_{-\mathbf p}a_{-\mathbf p}$ gives $+\mathbf p\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p}$; and the reordering constant $\frac12V\int\mathbf p\,d^3p$ is odd as well.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.2.2: Normal Ordering
> The **normal-ordered** product $:\!X\!:$ of creation and annihilation operators is the product rearranged with all creation operators to the left of all annihilation operators, as if they commuted, e.g. $:\!a_{\mathbf p}a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}\!: = a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}a_{\mathbf p}$; it is extended linearly to products of fields, $:\!\phi(x)\phi(y)\!:$. For the free field, the Hamiltonian is taken normal ordered:
>
> $$
> :\!H\!: = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,E_{\mathbf p}\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p}, \qquad :\!H\!:|0\rangle = 0 .
> $$
>

> [!remark] Remark: Why the zero-point energy is dropped
> $E_0$ is the sum of the oscillators' $\frac12E_{\mathbf p}$ ([[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^rem-c2-1-1|Remark: The oscillator, recalled]]) over infinitely many modes, and it is doubly infinite: $V$ diverges with the volume, $\int d^3p\,E_{\mathbf p}$ at large $\mathbf p$. Only energy differences are measured, and $|0\rangle$ is the lowest state whatever the constant, with excitations spaced by $E_{\mathbf p}$; so the lecture "sweeps it under the rug" and defines $H|0\rangle = 0$. The one interaction that sees absolute energy is gravity, and there the discrepancy is the cosmological-constant problem, outside the course. (Boundaries change the mode sum, and differences of zero-point energies are physical: the Casimir force, mentioned in [[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^rem-c13-1-3|QM Remark: What the field repairs, and what it costs]].)
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.6 · PHY 513 Lecture 4, Part C · PS §2.3, pp. 21–22*

^rem-c2-2-4

> [!remark] Remark: Normal ordering is a choice of quantization
> Classically $a_{\mathbf p}a^*_{\mathbf p} = a^*_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p}$, and $\int E_{\mathbf p}\,a^*_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p}$ has no zero-point term. Writing $H$ in $\phi$ and $\pi$ and *then* promoting to operators produces the symmetric ordering $\frac12(aa^\dagger + a^\dagger a)$; reordering the classical expression first quantizes the same classical theory with no constant (Problem Set 3's prescription "without taking the commutator into account"; its course solution computed the commutator and showed the two routes agree). [[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]] does not fix the order, and normal ordering is a choice made at quantization, not a subtraction afterwards. For the energy the choice is harmless; for the U(1) charge it is forced by physics ([[§C2.3 The Complex Scalar Field and Its Charge#^rem-c2-3-2|Remark: Why the vacuum must be neutral]]). The constant itself is $\langle0|H|0\rangle$, built from products of fields at one point: the vacuum two-point function at coincident points ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^def-c2-5-2|Def. §C2.5.2]]); applied to time-ordered products, the same reordering produces the Feynman propagator ([[§C2.6 Green's Functions and the Feynman Propagator#^thm-c2-6-8|Theorem §C2.6.8]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.6 · PHY 513 Problem Set 3, Problem 2(b), course solution*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.6: Ladder Relations
>
> $$
> [H, a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}] = E_{\mathbf p}\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}, \quad [H, a_{\mathbf p}] = -E_{\mathbf p}\,a_{\mathbf p}, \qquad [\mathbf P, a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}] = \mathbf p\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}, \quad [\mathbf P, a_{\mathbf p}] = -\mathbf p\,a_{\mathbf p} .
> $$
>
> So if $|\psi\rangle$ has energy $E$ and momentum $\mathbf P$, then $a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|\psi\rangle$ has $E + E_{\mathbf p}$ and $\mathbf P + \mathbf p$, and $a_{\mathbf p}|\psi\rangle$ has $E - E_{\mathbf p}$ and $\mathbf P - \mathbf p$, unless zero.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.8 · PS §2.3, eq. (2.32) · Yu §2.3.3, eqs. (2.135)–(2.147)*

^thm-c2-2-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> The constant $E_0$ commutes with everything. $[a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}a_{\mathbf q}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}] = a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}[a_{\mathbf q}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}] = a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf q - \mathbf p)$, so $[H, a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}] = \int d^3q\,E_{\mathbf q}\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}\delta^3(\mathbf q - \mathbf p) = E_{\mathbf p}a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$; similarly $[a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}a_{\mathbf q}, a_{\mathbf p}] = -(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf q - \mathbf p)\,a_{\mathbf q}$, and the same with $\mathbf q$ in place of $E_{\mathbf q}$ for $\mathbf P$. Then $Ha^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|\psi\rangle = a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}(H + E_{\mathbf p})|\psi\rangle$, as for one oscillator.

## The Fock space and particles

> [!principle] Principle §C2.2.7: The Vacuum and the Fock Space
> There is a normalized state $|0\rangle$, unique up to a phase, with $a_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle = 0$ for all $\mathbf p$, and the space of states is the **Fock space** it generates,
>
> $$
> \mathcal F = \overline{\operatorname{span}}\bigl\{|0\rangle,\ a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle,\ a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_1}a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_2}|0\rangle,\ \dots\bigr\} = \bigoplus_{n=0}^\infty\mathcal H_n, \qquad \mathcal H_0 = \mathbb C|0\rangle, \quad \mathcal H_n = \operatorname{Sym}^n\mathcal H_1 ,
> $$
>
> with inner products computed from [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]] by moving annihilators to the right, e.g. $\langle0|a_{\mathbf q}a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$.
>
> *Domain:* free fields, given the split of $\phi$ into $a$ and $a^\dagger$ of [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]; that $\mathcal F$ is the whole space (irreducibility) is asserted, not derived, at this level (Streater–Wightman). In interacting theories the vacuum is not this one (QFT C10, planned).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.8 (Principle "What space the operators act on") · PS §2.3, p. 22 · Yu §2.3.4, eqs. (2.148)–(2.149)*

^pr-c2-2-7

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.8: The Spectrum: Particles
> With $:\!H\!:$ and $\mathbf P$ of [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-5|Theorem §C2.2.5]]:
> 1. $:\!H\!: \ge 0$; $|0\rangle$ is the ground state, $:\!H\!:|0\rangle = \mathbf P|0\rangle = 0$.
> 2. $a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_1}\cdots a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_n}|0\rangle$ has energy $E_{\mathbf p_1} + \dots + E_{\mathbf p_n}$ and momentum $\mathbf p_1 + \dots + \mathbf p_n$: $n$ free particles of mass $m$, $E_{\mathbf p} = \sqrt{\mathbf p^2 + m^2}$.
> 3. These states are symmetric under exchange of any two momenta (Bose–Einstein statistics), and one mode can hold any number of quanta, $(a^\dagger_{\mathbf p})^N|0\rangle$.
> 4. $N = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p}$ counts particles: its eigenvalue on the state of part 2 is $n$, and $[N, H] = [N, \mathbf P] = 0$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.8 (Principle "Fields give particles"; "Bose statistics"; "Macroscopic occupation") · PHY 513 Lecture 4, Part C · PS §2.3, p. 22 · Yu §2.3.4, eqs. (2.150)–(2.172)*

^thm-c2-2-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *1.* $\langle\psi|a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p}|\psi\rangle = \|a_{\mathbf p}\psi\|^2 \ge 0$ for every $\mathbf p$, and $E_{\mathbf p} > 0$, so $\langle\psi|{:}H{:}|\psi\rangle \ge 0$ with equality for $|0\rangle$; both operators end in an annihilator. *2.* Push $H$ through the creation operators one at a time with [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-6|Theorem §C2.2.6]]: $Ha^\dagger_{\mathbf p_1}\cdots|0\rangle = a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_1}(H + E_{\mathbf p_1})a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_2}\cdots|0\rangle = \dots = (E_{\mathbf p_1} + \dots + E_{\mathbf p_n})\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_1}\cdots|0\rangle$, and likewise for $\mathbf P$. *3.* $[a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}] = 0$; the two-particle overlap has the two terms $\delta^3(\mathbf p_1 - \mathbf q_1)\delta^3(\mathbf p_2 - \mathbf q_2) + \delta^3(\mathbf p_1 - \mathbf q_2)\delta^3(\mathbf p_2 - \mathbf q_1)$ (Yu eq. (2.171)). *4.* $[N, a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}] = a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$ by the computation of Theorem §C2.2.6 with $1$ in place of $E_{\mathbf q}$; $N$, $H$ and $\mathbf P$ are all built from the commuting $a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p}$.

> [!remark] Remark: What the spectrum says
> - **The Lagrangian's $m$ is the particle's mass.** $a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle$ has exactly the energy and momentum of a relativistic particle of mass $m$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-3|REL Theorem §B2.1.3]]), and the label $\mathbf p$, introduced as a Fourier variable, is the eigenvalue of $\mathbf P$. The isolated hyperbola in the figure is how a particle is recognized in a spectrum; with interactions it returns as an isolated pole of the two-point function ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^rem-c2-5-13|★ Remark: With interactions, the Källén–Lehmann representation]]; QFT C10, planned).
> - **Statistics were not imposed.** Bose symmetry comes from $[\phi(\mathbf x), \phi(\mathbf y)] = 0$, inherited by the $a^\dagger$'s; fermions need anticommutators, a different quantization (QFT C5, planned). Many quanta in one mode, $(a^\dagger_{\mathbf p})^N|0\rangle$, is the beginning of the laser; whether such a state is a classical wave is [[§C2.4 Coherent States and the Classical Field|§C2.4]].
> - **One field, any number of particles.** This is the reason for field theory: particle number is not conserved in relativistic processes, and one field operator creates and destroys any number of quanta. In $\phi$ the plane wave $e^{-ip\cdot x}$ (the wave) multiplies $a_{\mathbf p}$ (the particle): wave–particle duality in one expression.
> - **Not a density.** $\mathbf x$ is a label on the field operator, not an eigenvalue; there is a momentum operator and a Hamiltonian but no position or time operator.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §§4.7–4.9 · PHY 513 Lecture 4, Part C ("Comments") · PS §2.3, p. 22*

^rem-c2-2-6

> [!remark] Remark: Why |0⟩ is the vacuum
> - **Ground state.** By Theorem §C2.2.8, 1, no state has lower energy; $H|0\rangle = 0$ is the normal-ordering choice of zero, not a physical statement.
> - **Poincaré invariant.** $P^\mu|0\rangle = 0$ and $J^{\mu\nu}|0\rangle = 0$, since every generator is normal ordered and bilinear, with an annihilator on the right. This is what makes $\langle0|\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle$ a function of $x - y$ alone ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-4|Theorem §C2.5.4]]).
> - **Unique, given the $a$'s.** Any state annihilated by all $a_{\mathbf p}$ is a multiple of $|0\rangle$ (irreducibility). What is not unique is the choice of the $a$'s ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^rem-c2-2-8|★ Remark: Other mode functions]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.8 (Principle "Why |0⟩ is the vacuum")*

^rem-c2-2-7

> [!caution] Caution: Plane-wave states are not normalizable
> $\langle\mathbf p|\mathbf p\rangle = 2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf 0)$ is infinite: like $|x'\rangle$ and $|p'\rangle$ in quantum mechanics ([[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^pr-c2-1-2|QM Principle §C2.1.2]], [[§26 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-26-4|556 Prop. §26.4]]), $|\mathbf p\rangle$ is not in $\mathcal F$. Genuine one-particle states are wave packets $\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_{\mathbf p}}\,\psi(\mathbf p)\,|\mathbf p\rangle$ with $\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_{\mathbf p}}|\psi(\mathbf p)|^2 < \infty$ (Yu, Exercise 2.3). Correspondingly $a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$ and $\phi(\mathbf x)$ are operator-valued distributions, meaningful after smearing with test functions; that is why products of fields at one point need normal ordering and why $\delta^3(\mathbf 0)$ appeared in $H$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.8 (Caution "Two honesty points") · Yu §2.3.4, after eq. (2.163)*

## Relativistic normalization

> [!definition] Definition §C2.2.3: Relativistic Normalization and the Invariant Measure
> The relativistically normalized states are
>
> $$
> |\mathbf p\rangle = \sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle, \qquad \langle\mathbf p|\mathbf q\rangle = 2E_{\mathbf p}\,(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q), \qquad |\mathbf p_1, \dots, \mathbf p_n\rangle = \sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p_1}}\cdots\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p_n}}\;a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_1}\cdots a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_n}|0\rangle ,
> $$
>
> and the **invariant measure** and the one-particle resolution of the identity are
>
> $$
> \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_{\mathbf p}} = \int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4}\,2\pi\,\delta(p^2 - m^2)\,\theta(p^0), \qquad \mathbb 1_{1\text{-particle}} = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_{\mathbf p}}\,|\mathbf p\rangle\langle\mathbf p| .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.9, eqs. (relnorm), (invmeasure) · PHY 513 Lecture 4, Part C · PS §2.3, eqs. (2.35)–(2.36), (2.39)–(2.40) · Yu §2.3.4, eqs. (2.152), (2.157), (2.161), (2.167)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.9: Lorentz Invariance of the Measure and the Normalization
> Under proper orthochronous Lorentz transformations, for $m > 0$:
> 1. $\theta(p^0)$ is invariant on the mass shell $p^2 = m^2$;
> 2. for every scalar $F$, $\displaystyle\int\frac{d^3p}{2E_{\mathbf p}}\,F(E_{\mathbf p}, \mathbf p) = \int d^4p\;\delta(p^2 - m^2)\,\theta(p^0)\,F(p)$, so $d^3p/2E_{\mathbf p}$ is an invariant measure;
> 3. $2E_{\mathbf p}\,\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ is invariant, hence so is $\langle\mathbf p|\mathbf q\rangle$, while $\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ alone is not: $\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q) = (E_{\mathbf p'}/E_{\mathbf p})\,\delta^3(\mathbf p' - \mathbf q')$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 4 §4.9 · PHY 513 Lecture 4 ("Details on Normalization"); Problem Set 3, Problem 1, with the course solution · PS §2.3, eqs. (2.34)–(2.40) · Yu §2.3.4, eqs. (2.158)–(2.163)*

^thm-c2-2-9

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *1.* For $m > 0$ an on-shell $p$ is timelike, $|\mathbf p| < p^0$. Rotations leave $p^0$ alone; a boost with velocity $\mathbf v$, $|\mathbf v| < 1$, gives $p'^0 = \gamma(p^0 - \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf p) \ge \gamma(p^0 - |\mathbf v||\mathbf p|) > \gamma(p^0 - |\mathbf p|) > 0$. The same argument for $\Lambda^{-1}$ gives the converse, so $\operatorname{sgn}p^0$ is invariant: the same fact that makes "future" a frame-independent notion for timelike intervals ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|REL Theorem §B1.3.1]]).
>
> *2.* $d^4p$ is invariant ($|\det\Lambda| = 1$), and so are $\delta(p^2 - m^2)$ and, by 1, $\theta(p^0)$ on its support. Do the $p^0$ integral with $\delta(f(p^0)) = \sum_i\delta(p^0 - p_i^0)/|f'(p^0_i)|$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|WO Theorem §B4.4.3]]): $f = (p^0)^2 - E_{\mathbf p}^2$ has roots $\pm E_{\mathbf p}$, $\theta$ keeps $+E_{\mathbf p}$, and $f'(E_{\mathbf p}) = 2E_{\mathbf p}$. The left side of 2 equals the manifestly invariant right side.
>
> *3.* For a scalar $g$, $g(\mathbf q) = \int d^3p\,\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)g(\mathbf p) = \int\frac{d^3p}{2E_{\mathbf p}}\bigl[2E_{\mathbf p}\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)\bigr]g(\mathbf p)$; the outer expressions and the measure are invariant, so the bracket is. Then $\langle\mathbf p|\mathbf q\rangle = 2E_{\mathbf p}\langle0|a_{\mathbf p}a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}|0\rangle = 2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ by [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]].

> [!derivation]- Derivation (second route: boosting the delta function directly)
> Boost along $z$: $p_3' = \gamma(p_3 + \beta E)$, $E' = \gamma(E + \beta p_3)$, transverse components unchanged, and $dE/dp_3 = p_3/E$ on the mass shell. By the delta-of-a-function rule in the variable $p_3$,
>
> $$
> \delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q) = \delta^3(\mathbf p' - \mathbf q')\,\frac{dp_3'}{dp_3} = \delta^3(\mathbf p' - \mathbf q')\,\gamma\Bigl(1 + \beta\frac{dE}{dp_3}\Bigr) = \delta^3(\mathbf p' - \mathbf q')\,\frac{\gamma(E + \beta p_3)}{E} = \delta^3(\mathbf p' - \mathbf q')\,\frac{E'}{E} ,
> $$
>
> so $E\,\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ is invariant. The physical reason: a box of volume $V$ in its rest frame has volume $V/\gamma$ in a boosted frame, so a density per unit volume cannot be invariant, and $E$ compensates.

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> In $\phi(\mathbf x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\bigl(a_{\mathbf p}e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x} + a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}\bigr)$ the annihilators kill $|0\rangle$, and $a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle = |\mathbf p\rangle/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$ ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]). Then $\langle\mathbf q|\phi(\mathbf x)|0\rangle = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_{\mathbf p}}e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}\,2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q) = e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}$; the third relation is the complex conjugate, $\phi$ being Hermitian.

*Uses:* [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Sharp momentum or sharp position; why √(2E_p)
> - **Fourier conjugates.** $a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle$ has sharp momentum and is not localized (its "wave function" is a plane wave); $\phi(\mathbf x)|0\rangle$ is localized at $\mathbf x$ and contains all momenta. The two are related as $|\mathbf p\rangle$ and $|\mathbf x\rangle$ are in quantum mechanics ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-3|QM Theorem §C2.3.3]]), with the difference that $\mathbf x$ labels an operator. The localization is approximate: compared with $|\mathbf x\rangle$, the weight carries an extra $1/2E_{\mathbf p}$, nearly constant only for $|\mathbf p| \ll m$ (PS p. 24).
> - **Why the factor.** The $1/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$ placed in the mode expansion and the $\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$ in $|\mathbf p\rangle$ are the same choice: they make $\langle\mathbf p|\mathbf q\rangle$, $\langle\mathbf p|\phi|0\rangle$ and the completeness relation invariant, at the price of dividing by $2E_{\mathbf p}$ elsewhere. With it a Lorentz transformation acts on states without extra factors, $U(\Lambda)|\mathbf p\rangle = |\Lambda\mathbf p\rangle$ (PS eq. (2.37)); that, and the spin-0 particle as a representation of the Poincaré group, is QFT C3 (planned).
>
