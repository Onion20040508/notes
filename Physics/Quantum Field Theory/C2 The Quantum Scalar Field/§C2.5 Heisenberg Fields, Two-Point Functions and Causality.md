The free field of [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field|§C2.2]] lives at one instant: a mode expansion, a commutator and a spectrum, with no time in them. This section restores time in the Heisenberg picture ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^def-c3-3-1|QM Def. §C3.3.1]]) and asks three questions: how the field operator depends on time, what the negative-frequency half of it means, and whether the theory is causal. The answer to the third is a function, the vacuum two-point function $D_W$, whose explicit Bessel forms are worked out here once for the whole subject ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-6|Theorem §C2.5.6]], [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-7|Theorem §C2.5.7]]); its antisymmetric part, the commutator function, vanishes outside the light cone, and the Green's functions of [[§C2.6 Green's Functions and the Feynman Propagator|§C2.6]] are built from both.

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): $\hbar = c = 1$, $g = \operatorname{diag}(+, -, -, -)$, $E_{\mathbf p} = \sqrt{\mathbf p^2 + m^2}$, $p\cdot x = E_{\mathbf p}t - \mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x$ whenever $p$ is on shell, $[a_{\mathbf p}, a_{\mathbf q}^\dagger] = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$, $|\mathbf p\rangle = \sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}\,a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger|0\rangle$. Lecture 5 writes $\omega_{\mathbf p}$ for $E_{\mathbf p}$ ([[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^cau-c2-1-1|Caution: Eₚ, not ωₚ; π, not Π]]).

## The Heisenberg field

> [!definition] Definition §C2.5.1: Heisenberg Field
> Let $\phi_S(\mathbf x)$, $\pi_S(\mathbf x)$ be the Schrödinger-picture field operators and $H$ the Hamiltonian. The **Heisenberg fields** are
>
> $$
> \phi(x) = \phi(t, \mathbf x) \equiv e^{iHt}\,\phi_S(\mathbf x)\,e^{-iHt}, \qquad \pi(x) \equiv e^{iHt}\,\pi_S(\mathbf x)\,e^{-iHt},
> $$
>
> so that $\phi(0, \mathbf x) = \phi_S(\mathbf x)$. From here on a field with a spacetime argument is a Heisenberg operator, and states are frozen at $t = 0$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.1 · PS §2.4, eq. (2.43)*

^def-c2-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why field theory works in the Heisenberg picture
> The definition is QM's Heisenberg picture ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^def-c3-3-1|QM Def. §C3.3.1]]) applied to an operator labelled by $\mathbf x$, and the two pictures give the same matrix elements ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-3|QM Theorem §C3.3.3]]). What is new is the reason for preferring it: a time-dependent *state* obeying a Schrödinger equation singles out time, while a time-dependent *operator* $\phi(t, \mathbf x)$ is a function on spacetime that can transform covariantly. Canonical quantization starts in the Schrödinger picture because diagonalizing $H$ at one instant is the continuation of ordinary quantum mechanics ([[P1 Canonical Quantization|P1]]); it then switches.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.1*

^rem-c2-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.1: Time Dependence of the Modes; the Covariant Mode Expansion
> 1. The Schrödinger ladder operators acquire phases: $e^{iHt}a_{\mathbf p}e^{-iHt} = a_{\mathbf p}\,e^{-iE_{\mathbf p}t}$, $e^{iHt}a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger e^{-iHt} = a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger\,e^{iE_{\mathbf p}t}$.
> 2. Hence, with $p^0 = E_{\mathbf p}$,
>
> $$
> \phi(x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\Bigl(a_{\mathbf p}\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigr), \qquad \pi(x) = \partial_t\phi(x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}(-i)\sqrt{\frac{E_{\mathbf p}}{2}}\Bigl(a_{\mathbf p}\,e^{-ip\cdot x} - a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger\,e^{ip\cdot x}\Bigr).
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.1 · PS §2.4, eqs. (2.46)–(2.47)*

^thm-c2-5-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *1.* With $H = \int\frac{d^3p'}{(2\pi)^3}E_{\mathbf p'}\,a_{\mathbf p'}^\dagger a_{\mathbf p'}$ ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-5|Theorem §C2.2.5]]; the integration variable renamed),
>
> $$
> [H, a_{\mathbf p}] = \int\frac{d^3p'}{(2\pi)^3}E_{\mathbf p'}\,[a_{\mathbf p'}^\dagger, a_{\mathbf p}]\,a_{\mathbf p'} = -E_{\mathbf p}\,a_{\mathbf p},
> $$
>
> since $[a_{\mathbf p'}^\dagger, a_{\mathbf p}] = -(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf p')$. Read as $Ha_{\mathbf p} = a_{\mathbf p}(H - E_{\mathbf p})$: the Hamiltonian passes through $a_{\mathbf p}$ at the price of a number. Repeating, $H^na_{\mathbf p} = a_{\mathbf p}(H - E_{\mathbf p})^n$; summing the exponential series, $e^{iHt}a_{\mathbf p} = a_{\mathbf p}e^{i(H - E_{\mathbf p})t}$, which is the first claim. The second is its adjoint. (This is the Baker–Hausdorff lemma for $[G, A] = \kappa A$, [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-2|QM Theorem §C3.3.2]], exactly as for one oscillator in [[§C3.4 The Harmonic Oscillator and Coherent States Revisited#^thm-c3-4-2|QM Theorem §C3.4.2]].)
>
> *2.* Conjugate the Schrödinger expansion ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]) term by term: $e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}a_{\mathbf p}$ becomes $e^{-iE_{\mathbf p}t + i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}a_{\mathbf p} = e^{-ip\cdot x}a_{\mathbf p}$, and the creation term becomes $e^{ip\cdot x}a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger$. For $\pi$, either conjugate its Schrödinger expansion the same way, or differentiate $\phi$: $\partial_t$ on $e^{\mp ip\cdot x}$ brings down $\mp iE_{\mathbf p}$, and $E_{\mathbf p}/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}} = \sqrt{E_{\mathbf p}/2}$. The two agree, so $\pi = \partial_t\phi$ in the Heisenberg picture. The sign check the lecture recommended: $\partial_t$ of the $a$ term must give $-iE_{\mathbf p}$.

> [!remark] Remark: What is covariant and what is not yet
> The spatial Fourier factors have become four-dimensional plane waves, the first manifestly covariant-looking expression of the quantum theory. But the integral still runs over three momenta: $p^0$ is not a variable, it means $E_{\mathbf p}$, a square root of the integration variable. The remaining non-covariance hides there, and the invariant measure $d^3p/(2\pi)^32E_{\mathbf p}$ ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]) is the bookkeeping that keeps it honest. In [[§C2.6 Green's Functions and the Feynman Propagator|§C2.6]], $p^0$ becomes an integration variable, and the poles at $p^0 = \pm E_{\mathbf p}$ are the price.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.1, §5.7 (closing paragraph)*

^rem-c2-5-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.2: The Heisenberg Equations Are the Klein–Gordon Equation
> 1. The equal-time commutation relations hold at every common time: $[\phi(t, \mathbf x), \pi(t, \mathbf y)] = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$, $[\phi(t, \mathbf x), \phi(t, \mathbf y)] = [\pi(t, \mathbf x), \pi(t, \mathbf y)] = 0$.
> 2. With $H = \int d^3x\,\bigl[\tfrac12\pi^2 + \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\phi^2\bigr]$, the Heisenberg equation $i\partial_t\mathcal O = [\mathcal O, H]$ gives $\partial_t\phi = \pi$ and $\partial_t\pi = (\nabla^2 - m^2)\phi$, hence the operator equation
>
> $$
> (\partial_\mu\partial^\mu + m^2)\,\phi(x) = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.2 · PS §2.4, eqs. (2.44)–(2.45)*

^thm-c2-5-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *1.* Conjugate both sides of the Schrödinger-picture relations ([[§C2.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]]) with $e^{iHt}$. One unitary sandwiches the whole product, so the *same* $t$ enters both operators: $e^{iHt}[\phi_S(\mathbf x), \pi_S(\mathbf y)]e^{-iHt} = [\phi(t, \mathbf x), \pi(t, \mathbf y)]$, while the right side $i\delta^3$ is a number.
>
> *2.* $H$ is time-independent, so write its integration variable at the same time $t$ as the operator it is commuted with; then part 1 applies to every commutator. *For $\phi$:* $\phi(t, \mathbf x)$ commutes with $\phi(t, \mathbf x')$ and with its gradient, so only $\tfrac12\pi^2$ contributes, and its two factors give equal terms that cancel the $\tfrac12$:
>
> $$
> i\partial_t\phi(t, \mathbf x) = \Bigl[\phi(t, \mathbf x), \int d^3x'\,\tfrac12\pi^2(t, \mathbf x')\Bigr] = \int d^3x'\,\pi(t, \mathbf x')\,i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf x') = i\pi(t, \mathbf x) .
> $$
>
> *For $\pi$:* integrate the gradient term by parts ([[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-5|Theorem §CA.1.5]]), $\tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\phi^2 \to \tfrac12\phi\,(-\nabla^2 + m^2)\phi$. Now $\pi(t, \mathbf x)$ meets $\phi(t, \mathbf x')$ twice, on each side of the symmetric operator $-\nabla'^2 + m^2$, and the two terms are equal:
>
> $$
> i\partial_t\pi(t, \mathbf x) = \int d^3x'\,(-i)\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf x')\,(-\nabla'^2 + m^2)\phi(t, \mathbf x') = -i(-\nabla^2 + m^2)\phi(t, \mathbf x) .
> $$
>
> *Combining*, $\partial_t^2\phi = \partial_t\pi = (\nabla^2 - m^2)\phi$, which is $(\partial_t^2 - \nabla^2 + m^2)\phi = 0$. The two first-order equations are Hamilton's equations of the classical field (QFT C1, planned) with operators in place of functions.

> [!caution] Caution: Equal times only
> - The canonical relations were postulated at one instant. Part 1 extends them to every *common* time; at different times nothing has been assumed, and the answer is a theorem ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-12|Theorem §C2.5.12]]).
> - $\phi(\mathbf x)$ commutes with $\nabla\phi(\mathbf y)$ because $[\phi(\mathbf x), \phi(\mathbf y)]$ vanishes for *all* $\mathbf x, \mathbf y$, and the difference quotient of an identically zero function is zero. The same move on $[\phi, \pi]$ gives $\nabla\delta^3 \neq 0$.
> - Inside the commutator with $H$, $(-\nabla^2 + m^2)\phi$ may not be replaced by $-\partial_t^2\phi$: the equality is true, but a time derivative involves nearby *different* times, about which the postulates say nothing. Compute at equal times, then read off the time dependence.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.2 (Cautions "Equal times", "Two questions from the lecture")*

> [!remark] Remark: Two routes that meet
> Theorem §C2.5.2 never used the mode expansion. One could start from it: postulate the commutators and $H$, derive the Klein–Gordon equation, expand its solutions in $e^{\mp ip\cdot x}$, name the coefficients $a_{\mathbf p}$, $a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger$, and compute their algebra. That is the "field first" route of Yu §2.3 and of the user's notes; the lecture went "oscillators first", diagonalizing $H$ and discovering afterwards that the result solves a wave equation ([[P1 Canonical Quantization|P1]]). That the two routes meet is Theorem §C2.5.1 against Theorem §C2.5.2. Level change: QM's preview asserted the operator Klein–Gordon equation for the mode sum it wrote down ([[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^thm-c13-1-6|QM Theorem §C13.1.6]], 1); here it follows from the canonical structure alone, and $\pi = \partial_t\phi$, a definition in classical field theory, becomes a consequence of the commutators.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.2 ("The logic run backwards")*

^rem-c2-5-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.3: Positive and Negative Frequency
> Split $\phi = \phi^+ + \phi^-$ with
>
> $$
> \phi^+(x) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{a_{\mathbf p}\,e^{-ip\cdot x}}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\quad\text{(positive frequency)}, \qquad \phi^- = (\phi^+)^\dagger\quad\text{(negative frequency)} .
> $$
>
> Then $\phi^+|0\rangle = 0$, $\langle0|\phi^- = 0$, and for the one-particle states $|\mathbf p\rangle$, with $p^0 = E_{\mathbf p} > 0$,
>
> $$
> \langle0|\phi(x)|\mathbf p\rangle = e^{-ip\cdot x}, \qquad \langle\mathbf p|\phi(x)|0\rangle = e^{ip\cdot x} .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.3, Ch. 6 §6.6 (the split $\hat\phi^\pm$) · PS §2.4, p. 26 · Yu §6.3, eqs. (6.132)–(6.133)*

^thm-c2-5-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> $a_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle = 0$ gives $\phi^+|0\rangle = 0$ and, by adjoints, $\langle0|\phi^- = 0$. Then $\langle0|\phi(x)|\mathbf p\rangle = \langle0|\phi^+(x)|\mathbf p\rangle = \int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{e^{-iq\cdot x}}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf q}}}\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}\,\langle0|a_{\mathbf q}a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger|0\rangle$ with $\langle0|a_{\mathbf q}a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger|0\rangle = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf q - \mathbf p)$ ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]], [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]); the delta does the integral and the square roots cancel. The second formula is the complex conjugate of the first.

^der-c2-5-3

*Uses:* [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]], [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]], [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-1|Theorem §C2.5.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Negative frequency is creation, not negative energy
> A complete set of solutions of the Klein–Gordon equation has both $e^{-iE t}$ and $e^{+iEt}$, and a Hermitian field needs both. Read as single-particle wave functions they would be states of energy $\pm E$, the old difficulty of relativistic wave mechanics ([[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^thm-c13-1-2|QM Theorem §C13.1.2]]). In the field, the coefficient of the positive-frequency solution is the operator that *destroys* a quantum, and $\langle0|\phi|\mathbf p\rangle = e^{-ip\cdot x}$ is a positive-energy wave function; the coefficient of the negative-frequency solution *creates* one. No state of negative energy exists in the spectrum ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-8|Theorem §C2.2.8]]). Wave and particle sit in one formula: the plane waves are the wave aspect, the operator coefficients with $[a, a^\dagger] = (2\pi)^3\delta^3$ the particle aspect. Notation: $\phi^{(+)}$ in PS and Yu is the positive-frequency, annihilating part, as here.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.3 · PS §2.4, p. 26*

## The Wightman function

> [!definition] Definition §C2.5.2: Wightman Function
> The **Wightman function** of the free field is the vacuum expectation value
>
> $$
> D_W(x - y) \equiv \langle0|\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle ,
> $$
>
> the amplitude for a quantum created at $y$ to be found at $x$. Peskin & Schroeder call it $D(x - y)$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.4 · PS §2.4, eq. (2.50) · PHY 513, Problem Set 4, eq. (8)*

^def-c2-5-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.4: The Wightman Function as a Mode Integral
> With $\xi = x - y$ and $p^0 = E_{\mathbf p}$:
> 1. $\displaystyle D_W(\xi) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_{\mathbf p}}\,e^{-ip\cdot\xi} = \int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4}\,2\pi\,\theta(p^0)\,\delta(p^2 - m^2)\,e^{-ip\cdot\xi}$: the transform of the positive-energy mass shell.
> 2. $D_W$ is Lorentz invariant: a function of $\xi^2$ alone at spacelike or null $\xi$, of $\xi^2$ and $\operatorname{sgn}\xi^0$ inside the light cone.
> 3. $(\partial^2 + m^2)D_W = 0$, and $D_W(-\xi) = \overline{D_W(\xi)}$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.4, Ch. 6 §6.9 · PS §2.4, eq. (2.50)*

^thm-c2-5-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *1.* Of the four terms of $\phi(x)\phi(y)$ only $a_{\mathbf p}e^{-ip\cdot x}$ in $\phi(x)$ times $a_{\mathbf p'}^\dagger e^{ip'\cdot y}$ in $\phi(y)$ survives between vacua, and $\langle0|a_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p'}^\dagger|0\rangle = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf p')$. The delta does one integral, $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E}\sqrt{2E}} = \frac{1}{2E}$, and the exponentials combine into $e^{-ip\cdot(x - y)}$. For the four-dimensional form, $\theta(p^0)\delta(p^2 - m^2) = \delta(p^0 - E_{\mathbf p})/2E_{\mathbf p}$ ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-9|Theorem §C2.2.9]]; composition rule [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-1|EM Theorem §B1.2.1]]), and the $p^0$ integral sets $p^0 = E_{\mathbf p}$.
>
> *2.* In the four-dimensional form, $d^4p$, $\delta(p^2 - m^2)$, $\theta(p^0)$ (for timelike $p$, [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|REL Theorem §B1.3.1]]) and $p\cdot\xi$ are invariant under $SO^+(1,3)$, so substituting $p \to \Lambda p$ gives $D_W(\Lambda\xi) = D_W(\xi)$. An invariant function is constant on the orbits of the group, and the orbits are the one-sheeted hyperboloids $\xi^2 = -r^2$, each sheet of $\xi^2 = \tau^2 > 0$ separately, and the two halves of the cone ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^rem-b1-3-1|REL Remark: Normal forms, and the orbits of the Lorentz group]]).
>
> *3.* Each mode is on shell: $(\partial^2 + m^2)e^{-ip\cdot\xi} = (m^2 - p^2)e^{-ip\cdot\xi} = 0$. For the conjugate, $\phi$ is Hermitian and $\overline{\langle0|AB|0\rangle} = \langle0|B^\dagger A^\dagger|0\rangle$, so $\overline{D_W(x - y)} = \langle0|\phi(y)\phi(x)|0\rangle = D_W(y - x)$.

^der-c2-5-4

*Uses:* [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]], [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-9|Theorem §C2.2.9]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-1|EM Theorem §B1.2.1]], [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|REL Theorem §B1.3.1]], [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^rem-b1-3-1|REL Remark: Normal forms, and the orbits of the Lorentz group]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.5: The Wightman Function Is a Boundary Value
> At real $\xi$ the integral of Theorem §C2.5.4 converges for no $\xi$. Because every energy is positive, the function
>
> $$
> W(z, \boldsymbol\xi) \equiv \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_{\mathbf p}}\,e^{-iE_{\mathbf p}z + i\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\xi}, \qquad \operatorname{Im}z < 0,
> $$
>
> converges absolutely and is analytic in the lower half $z$-plane, and $D_W(\xi) = \lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}W(\xi^0 - i\varepsilon, \boldsymbol\xi)$ in the sense of generalized functions. This limit *is* the definition of the physicist's "$\xi^0 \to \xi^0 - i\varepsilon$".
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.4 (Principle "The Wightman function is a boundary value") · PHY 513, Problem Set 4, Problem 2(a) (the prescription $t \to t - i\varepsilon$)*

^thm-c2-5-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> At real $\xi$ the integrand has modulus $1/2E_{\mathbf p}$, and $\int d^3p/E_{\mathbf p}$ diverges quadratically. For $z = a - ib$ with $b > 0$ the modulus is $e^{-bE_{\mathbf p}}/2E_{\mathbf p}$, integrable; on every compact subset of the half-plane $b$ is bounded below, so the convergence is uniform and the integral may be differentiated under the integral sign ([[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-1|Theorem §CA.1.1]]): $W$ is analytic. The limit $\varepsilon \to 0^+$ exists when tested against Schwartz functions of $\xi$, which is the meaning of a boundary value ([[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^def-ca-1-3|Def. §CA.1.3]]); Theorem §C2.5.7 exhibits it explicitly. What makes the *lower* half-plane the right one is the spectrum condition, $p^0 = E_{\mathbf p} > 0$: for negative energies, $e^{-iEz}$ would grow there. At equal times the prescription is the convergence factor $e^{-\varepsilon E_{\mathbf p}}$, and the regulated integral is not an approximation but exactly $W(-i\varepsilon, \boldsymbol\xi)$.

*Uses:* [[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-1|Theorem §CA.1.1]], [[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^def-ca-1-3|Def. §CA.1.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.6: The Wightman Function at Imaginary Time
> For $\tau > 0$, with $p_E = (p_4, \mathbf p)$, $x_E = (\tau, \boldsymbol\xi)$, Euclidean products and $R = \sqrt{\tau^2 + \boldsymbol\xi^2}$,
>
> $$
> W(-i\tau, \boldsymbol\xi) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{e^{-E_{\mathbf p}\tau + i\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\xi}}{2E_{\mathbf p}} = \int\frac{d^4p_E}{(2\pi)^4}\,\frac{e^{ip_E\cdot x_E}}{p_E^2 + m^2} = \frac{m}{4\pi^2R}\,K_1(mR) ,
> $$
>
> a function of the four-dimensional Euclidean distance alone; for $m = 0$ it is $1/4\pi^2R^2$. This is the single evaluation of the Bessel-$K$ integral in these notes.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 2 §2.2 (Derivation "The relativistic amplitude in closed form", Steps 1–4, where it is the auxiliary function $D_E$), Ch. 5 §5.4*

^thm-c2-5-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *Step 1: a fourth momentum.* For $\tau > 0$ and $E > 0$, close the $p_4$ contour upward ([[P2 Green's Functions by Contour Integration#^p2-4|P2, step 4]]); the only enclosed pole is $p_4 = iE$, with residue $e^{-E\tau}/2iE$ ([[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-2-4|Theorem §CA.2.4]]), and the arc vanishes by the ML estimate ([[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-2-6|Theorem §CA.2.6]]):
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dp_4}{2\pi}\,\frac{e^{ip_4\tau}}{p_4^2 + E^2} = \frac{e^{-E\tau}}{2E} .
> $$
>
> Inserting this for each $\mathbf p$ gives the second equality; the four-dimensional form is manifestly invariant under rotations of $(\tau, \boldsymbol\xi)$, so the result depends only on $R$.
>
> *Step 2: Schwinger parametrization.* $\frac{1}{p_E^2 + m^2} = \int_0^\infty ds\,e^{-s(p_E^2 + m^2)}$ ([[§CA.3 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^thm-ca-3-1|Theorem §CA.3.1]]). The order of the $s$ and $p_E$ integrals may be exchanged (the integrand times $|e^{ip_E\cdot x_E}| = 1$ is positive and integrable: [[Tonelli's Theorem|551 Tonelli's theorem]]). The $p_E$ integral is then a four-dimensional Gaussian (Theorem §CA.3.1 with $n = 4$):
>
> $$
> W = \int_0^\infty ds\,e^{-sm^2}\,\frac{e^{-R^2/4s}}{(4\pi s)^2} = \frac{1}{16\pi^2}\int_0^\infty\frac{ds}{s^2}\,\exp\Bigl(-sm^2 - \frac{R^2}{4s}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Step 3: recognize $K_1$.* Substitute $s = \frac{R}{2m}e^u$, $u \in \mathbb R$: $sm^2 + R^2/4s = mR\cosh u$ and $ds/s^2 = \frac{2m}{R}e^{-u}\,du$, so
>
> $$
> W = \frac{m}{8\pi^2R}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\,(\cosh u - \sinh u)\,e^{-mR\cosh u} = \frac{m}{4\pi^2R}\,K_1(mR) ,
> $$
>
> the odd $\sinh u$ term integrating to zero and the integral representation ([[§CA.3 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^def-ca-3-1|Def. §CA.3.1]]) giving $2K_1$. For $m \to 0$, $K_1(z) \simeq 1/z$ ([[§CA.3 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^thm-ca-3-3|Theorem §CA.3.3]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.7: Explicit Forms of the Wightman Function
> For $\operatorname{Im}z < 0$, $W(z, \boldsymbol\xi) = \dfrac{m\,K_1(ms)}{4\pi^2s}$ with $s = \sqrt{\boldsymbol\xi^2 - z^2}$ (principal branch, $\operatorname{Re}s > 0$). Its boundary values, with $r = \sqrt{-\xi^2}$ and $\tau = \sqrt{\xi^2}$:
> 1. *spacelike:* $D_W = \dfrac{m\,K_1(mr)}{4\pi^2r}$, real and positive; for $mr \gg 1$, $D_W \simeq \dfrac{\sqrt m}{2(2\pi r)^{3/2}}\,e^{-mr}\Bigl(1 + \dfrac{3}{8mr} + \cdots\Bigr)$;
> 2. *timelike:* $D_W = \dfrac{m}{8\pi\tau}\bigl[Y_1(m\tau) + i\operatorname{sgn}(\xi^0)\,J_1(m\tau)\bigr]$; at $\boldsymbol\xi = 0$, $D_W \simeq \dfrac{\sqrt m}{4\pi^2}\sqrt{\dfrac\pi2}\,e^{-3\pi i/4}\,t^{-3/2}e^{-imt}$ for $mt \gg 1$;
> 3. *near the cone:* $D_W \to -\dfrac{1}{4\pi^2\bigl[(\xi^0 - i\varepsilon)^2 - \boldsymbol\xi^2\bigr]}$, the massless Wightman function, singular on the whole cone.
>

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *The formula in the half-plane.* Write $z = a - ib$, $b > 0$. Then $\operatorname{Im}(\boldsymbol\xi^2 - z^2) = 2ab$ vanishes only at $a = 0$, where $\boldsymbol\xi^2 - z^2 = \boldsymbol\xi^2 + b^2 > 0$; so the lower half-plane is mapped into $\mathbb C \setminus (-\infty, 0]$, $s$ is analytic with $\operatorname{Re}s > 0$, and $mK_1(ms)/4\pi^2s$ is analytic there ([[§CA.3 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^def-ca-3-1|Def. §CA.3.1]]). $W$ is analytic there ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-5|Theorem §C2.5.5]]), and the two agree on the ray $z = -i\tau$ ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-6|Theorem §C2.5.6]]). By the identity theorem ([[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-2-3|Theorem §CA.2.3]]) they agree on the whole half-plane.
>
> *Boundary values.* Put $z = t - i\varepsilon$, $t = \xi^0$: $\boldsymbol\xi^2 - z^2 = -\xi^2 + 2i\varepsilon t$.
> 1. If $\xi^2 < 0$, the argument tends to the positive number $r^2$, the limit is smooth and real. The asymptotic form is [[§CA.3 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^thm-ca-3-3|Theorem §CA.3.3]] with $\nu = 1$, $(4\nu^2 - 1)/8 = 3/8$, and $\frac{m}{4\pi^2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2m}} = \frac{\sqrt m}{2(2\pi)^{3/2}}$.
> 2. If $\xi^2 > 0$, the argument approaches the negative axis from above for $t > 0$ and from below for $t < 0$, so $s \to i\operatorname{sgn}(t)\,\tau$. For $t > 0$ the continuation formula $K_1(ix) = -\frac\pi2\bigl[J_1(x) - iY_1(x)\bigr]$, $x > 0$ ([[§CA.3 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^thm-ca-3-4|Theorem §CA.3.4]]), gives $\frac{m}{4\pi^2i\tau}\bigl(-\frac\pi2\bigr)(J_1 - iY_1) = \frac{m}{8\pi\tau}(Y_1 + iJ_1)$; for $t < 0$ take the complex conjugate ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-4|Theorem §C2.5.4]], 3). At $\boldsymbol\xi = 0$, $s = it$ in $K_1(z) \simeq \sqrt{\pi/2z}\,e^{-z}$ gives the phase $(1/i)(i)^{-1/2} = e^{-3\pi i/4}$.
> 3. As $s \to 0$, $K_1(ms) \simeq 1/ms$ gives $1/4\pi^2s^2 = -1/4\pi^2(z^2 - \boldsymbol\xi^2)$; this limit is a genuine distribution, and the boundary-value definition is indispensable there.

> [!derivation]- Derivation (second route: the contour around the branch cut, at equal times)
> This is the lecture's and PS's computation (PS eq. (2.52), Fig. 2.3), with the three points that need care made explicit.
>
> *Reduce to one dimension.* By the angular rule ([[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-6|Theorem §CA.1.6]]) and evenness of the integrand in $p = |\mathbf p|$,
>
> $$
> D_W(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi^2r}\int_0^\infty dp\,\frac{p\sin pr}{\sqrt{p^2 + m^2}} = -\frac{i}{8\pi^2r}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp\,\frac{p\,e^{ipr}}{\sqrt{p^2 + m^2}} .
> $$
>
> *Why the contour cannot be closed yet.* $p/\sqrt{p^2 + m^2} \to 1$, so Jordan's lemma does not apply, and keeping the regulator $e^{-\varepsilon E}$ on the arc fails near $\arg p = \pi$. The fix: $p\,e^{ipr} = -i\partial_re^{ipr}$, so $D_W(r) = -\frac{1}{8\pi^2r}\frac{d}{dr}I(r)$ with $I(r) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ipr}\,dp}{\sqrt{p^2 + m^2}}$, which converges (Dirichlet's test, [[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-2|Theorem §CA.1.2]]) and has a kernel that tends to zero.
>
> *The branch and the keyhole* ([[§CA.2 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-2-8|Theorem §CA.2.8]] and its figure). Take $\sqrt{p^2 + m^2}$ positive on the real axis with cuts from $\pm im$ along the imaginary axis to $\pm i\infty$. Just right of the upper cut, at $p = i\rho$ with $\rho > m$, it equals $+i\sqrt{\rho^2 - m^2}$; just left, $-i\sqrt{\rho^2 - m^2}$. Close upward around the cut: the arcs vanish by Jordan's lemma, the small circle round $im$ contributes $O(\delta^{1/2})$, and on the lips $dp = i\,d\rho$, $e^{ipr} = e^{-\rho r}$. Both lips give $-\int_m^\infty e^{-\rho r}d\rho/\sqrt{\rho^2 - m^2}$, so they *add*, and Cauchy's theorem gives
>
> $$
> I(r) = 2\int_m^\infty\frac{e^{-\rho r}\,d\rho}{\sqrt{\rho^2 - m^2}} = 2K_0(mr)
> $$
>
> ([[§CA.3 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^thm-ca-3-4|Theorem §CA.3.4]]). *Differentiate:* with $K_0' = -K_1$, $D_W(r) = -\frac{2m}{8\pi^2r}K_0'(mr) = \frac{m}{4\pi^2r}K_1(mr)$. Before the derivative this is PS's $\frac{1}{4\pi^2r}\int_m^\infty\frac{\rho\,e^{-\rho r}d\rho}{\sqrt{\rho^2 - m^2}}$, whose value $mK_1(mr)$ PS leave unevaluated.
>
> *The lecture's shortcut, reread.* Rotating $p \to i\rho$ on the half-line form is this computation compressed: on $0 < \rho < m$ the integrand is analytic and that stretch cancels against its conjugate; only the cut survives, with the two lips adding. What the shortcut takes for granted, a vanishing quarter arc for the non-decaying kernel $p/\sqrt{p^2 + m^2}$, is what the derivative secures.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.4 (Derivation "Equal time, directly: the lecture's contour done carefully") · PS §2.4, eq. (2.52)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation (third route: real variables only)
> No contours: a substitution, a table integral, and the interchange theorems of [[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms|§CA.1]].
>
> *Regulate.* From the radial form, $D_W(r) = -\frac{i}{2(2\pi)^2r}\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}\Delta_\varepsilon(r)$ with $\Delta_\varepsilon = \int_0^\infty dp\,\frac{p\,e^{-\varepsilon p}}{\sqrt{p^2 + m^2}}\bigl(e^{ipr} - e^{-ipr}\bigr)$, absolutely convergent for $\varepsilon > 0$ (the problem set's $r \to r \pm i\varepsilon$).
>
> *Substitute* $p = m\sinh v$: $\Delta_\varepsilon = -2i\,\partial_rF_\varepsilon(r)$ with $F_\varepsilon(r) = \int_0^\infty dv\,e^{-m\varepsilon\sinh v}\cos(mr\sinh v)$; the derivative leaves the integral by [[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-1|Theorem §CA.1.1]] (dominated by $m\sinh v\,e^{-m\varepsilon\sinh v}$).
>
> *Limit of $F_\varepsilon$.* $F_\varepsilon = \int_0^\infty\frac{\cos pr}{\sqrt{p^2 + m^2}}e^{-\varepsilon p}dp$; the unregulated integral converges conditionally (Dirichlet) and equals $K_0(mr)$ ([[§CA.3 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^thm-ca-3-4|Theorem §CA.3.4]]), so $F_\varepsilon \to K_0(mr)$ by Abel's theorem ([[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-2|Theorem §CA.1.2]]).
>
> *Limit of $F_\varepsilon'$, the step that needs care.* Write $p/\sqrt{p^2 + m^2} = 1 - g(p)$ with $0 \le g \le \min\bigl(1, m^2/2p^2\bigr)$ integrable. With $\int_0^\infty\sin(pr)e^{-\varepsilon p}dp = r/(r^2 + \varepsilon^2)$,
>
> $$
> F_\varepsilon'(r) = -\frac{r}{r^2 + \varepsilon^2} + \int_0^\infty g(p)\sin(pr)\,e^{-\varepsilon p}\,dp .
> $$
>
> On $[r_0, \infty)$ the first term is within $\varepsilon^2/r_0^3$ of $-1/r$, and the second within $\int g\,(1 - e^{-\varepsilon p})$ of its $\varepsilon = 0$ value, which tends to zero by dominated convergence. So $F_\varepsilon'$ converges uniformly, and by [[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-3|Theorem §CA.1.3]] its limit is $\partial_rK_0(mr) = -mK_1(mr)$.
>
> *Assemble:* $\lim\Delta_\varepsilon = 2imK_1(mr)$ and $D_W(r) = \frac{m}{4\pi^2r}K_1(mr)$. (The regulator $e^{-\varepsilon p}$ differs from the covariant $e^{-\varepsilon E_{\mathbf p}}$; at spacelike separation both limits are the same smooth function.)
>

^rem-c2-5-5

## Microcausality and the commutator function

> [!principle] Principle §C2.5.8: Microcausality
> Local observables at spacelike separation commute:
>
> $$
> [\mathcal O_1(x), \mathcal O_2(y)] = 0 \qquad \text{for } (x - y)^2 < 0 .
> $$
>
> When the local observables are built from $\phi$ and its derivatives, it suffices that $[\phi(x), \phi(y)] = 0$ for $(x - y)^2 < 0$ (for a complex field also $[\phi(x), \phi^\dagger(y)] = 0$).
>
> *Domain:* relativistic quantum field theory; free or interacting (with interactions it is the locality axiom).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.5 (Principle "The causality condition") · PS §2.4, p. 28*

^pr-c2-5-8

> [!remark] Remark: Why the criterion is a commutator, not an amplitude
> Spacelike-separated events have no invariant order ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|REL Theorem §B1.3.1]], [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^rem-b1-3-2|REL Remark: Causality as a statement about cones]]), so neither may influence the other: measurements there must be compatible, which in quantum mechanics means commuting operators. An *amplitude* to propagate outside the cone, such as $D_W \neq 0$ (Theorem §C2.5.7) or the single-particle amplitude of QFT C1 (planned), is not a signal. The condition is an operator identity, true in every state, not a vacuum expectation value. Derivatives pass through commutators and $[AB, C] = A[B, C] + [A, C]B$, which is why the field commutator suffices. Microcausality is a principle: the free field satisfies it as a theorem (Theorem §C2.5.10), an interacting theory must be built so that it does.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.5 · PS §2.4, p. 28*

> $$
> iD(x - y) \equiv \langle0|[\phi(x), \phi(y)]|0\rangle, \qquad D_1(x - y) \equiv \langle0|\{\phi(x), \phi(y)\}|0\rangle .

^thm-c2-5-10

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *Spacelike.* The one-sheeted hyperboloid through $\xi$ is a single orbit of $SO^+(1,3)$, and it contains $-\xi$: in the frame where $\xi^0 = 0$, a rotation by $\pi$ about an axis perpendicular to $\boldsymbol\xi$ reverses it ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^rem-b1-3-1|REL Remark: Normal forms, and the orbits of the Lorentz group]]). $D_W$ is invariant ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-4|Theorem §C2.5.4]], 2), so $D_W(\xi) = D_W(-\xi)$ and $iD = 0$ by [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-9|Theorem §C2.5.9]], 1. Concretely, at equal times both terms are $D_W(r)$.
>
> *Timelike.* The future and past sheets are different orbits, so nothing forces $D_W(\xi) = D_W(-\xi)$, and indeed the phases $e^{\mp imt}$ of [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-7|Theorem §C2.5.7]], 2 are conjugates, not equal: $D = \operatorname{sgn}(\xi^0)\,mJ_1(m\tau)/4\pi\tau \neq 0$. The orbit structure of the Lorentz group places the boundary between "commute" and "do not commute" exactly on the light cone.

> [!derivation]- Derivation (second route: causality is the reality of the Wightman function)
> By [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-9|Theorem §C2.5.9]], 2, $[\phi(x), \phi(y)] = 2i\operatorname{Im}D_W(\xi)$, so microcausality says exactly that $D_W$ is real at spacelike separation. [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-7|Theorem §C2.5.7]], 1 shows that it is: there the boundary value of the analytic function $W$ is approached from a region where $s^2 = \boldsymbol\xi^2 - z^2$ tends to a positive number, and $K_1$ of a positive argument is real. Inside the cone the two signs of $\xi^0$ approach the cut of $s$ from opposite sides, the values are complex conjugates, and the commutator is the discontinuity of one analytic function across its cut. In this language causality is not a cancellation to be checked but a property of analyticity: $D_W$, as a function of $\xi^2$, has its cut only along the timelike axis.

> [!remark] Remark: Two orderings that cancel; why antiparticles must exist
> The commutator contains an $aa^\dagger$ term, creating at $y$ and annihilating at $x$, and an $a^\dagger a$ term, creating at $x$ and annihilating at $y$. At spacelike separation observers disagree on which point comes first; the theory includes both amplitudes, and they cancel. For a complex field ([[§C2.3 The Complex Scalar Field and Its Charge#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]) the two terms are visibly different:
>
> $$
> [\phi(x), \phi^\dagger(y)] = \underbrace{D_W(x - y)}_{\text{particle } y \to x\ (a,\,a^\dagger)} - \underbrace{D_W(y - x)}_{\text{antiparticle } x \to y\ (b^\dagger,\,b)} = 0 \qquad ((x - y)^2 < 0) .
> $$
>
> The cancellation needs both species, with the same mass: causality requires every particle to have an antiparticle with opposite charge (for the real field, itself). This is the honest content of "antiparticles are particles moving backwards in time"; the lecture's advice was not to be too disturbed by such narrations of the second term.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.5 (Caution "What the cancellation means, and what it does not") · PS §2.4, p. 29*

> [!remark] Remark: Influence and correlation
> At spacelike separation $D = 0$ but, by Theorem §C2.5.7, 1,
>
> $$
> D_1(\xi) = 2D_W(\xi) = \frac{m}{2\pi^2r}K_1(mr) \simeq \sqrt{\frac{m}{8\pi^3}}\;r^{-3/2}e^{-mr} \qquad (\xi^2 = -r^2,\ mr \gg 1).
> $$
>
> The two functions answer different questions. $D$ measures *influence*: a disturbance at $y$ changes what is measured at $x$ only through the commutator (made precise by the retarded response of [[§C2.6 Green's Functions and the Feynman Propagator#^rem-c2-6-4|§C2.6]]). $D_1$ measures *correlation*: it is the covariance of the vacuum fluctuations at the two points. Outside the cone the vacuum is correlated over the Compton length, yet nothing done at one point can affect the other, the field-theory version of correlation without signalling for entangled systems ([[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^thm-c11-3-5|QM Theorem §C11.3.5]]); it is also why the vacuum restricted to a region is a mixed state.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.5 ("At spacelike separation: correlated, not causally connected") · PHY 513, Problem Set 4, Problem 3(b)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.11: Explicit Forms of the Commutator and Hadamard Functions
> Write $t = \xi^0$, $r = |\boldsymbol\xi|$, $\tau = \sqrt{\xi^2}$ inside the cone.
> 1. *Massless:* $D(\xi) = -\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\operatorname{sgn}(\xi^0)\,\delta(\xi^2) = -\dfrac{1}{4\pi r}\bigl[\delta(t - r) - \delta(t + r)\bigr]$ and $D_1(\xi) = -\dfrac{1}{2\pi^2}\,\mathcal P\dfrac{1}{\xi^2}$: $D$ lives on the light cone only.
> 2. *Massive:* $D(\xi) = -\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\operatorname{sgn}(\xi^0)\Bigl[\delta(\xi^2) - \dfrac{m}{2\sqrt{\xi^2}}\,\theta(\xi^2)\,J_1\bigl(m\sqrt{\xi^2}\bigr)\Bigr]$; inside the cone $D = \operatorname{sgn}(\xi^0)\dfrac{mJ_1(m\tau)}{4\pi\tau}$ and $D_1 = \dfrac{mY_1(m\tau)}{4\pi\tau}$; outside, $D = 0$ and $D_1 = \dfrac{mK_1(m\sqrt{-\xi^2})}{2\pi^2\sqrt{-\xi^2}}$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.7 (Derivation "Explicit forms"), App. A §A.5 (Caution "What the formula does and does not say", example) · PHY 513, Problem Set 4, Problems 2(b), 3(b)*

^thm-c2-5-11

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *Massless.* By [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-7|Theorem §C2.5.7]], 3 (exact for $m = 0$), with partial fractions in $t$,
>
> $$
> D_W = -\frac{1}{4\pi^2}\,\frac{1}{(t - i\varepsilon)^2 - r^2} = -\frac{1}{8\pi^2r}\Bigl[\frac{1}{t - r - i\varepsilon} - \frac{1}{t + r - i\varepsilon}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> The Sokhotski–Plemelj formula $\frac{1}{s - i\varepsilon} \to \mathcal P\frac1s + i\pi\delta(s)$ ([[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-8|Theorem §CA.1.8]]) applied to each term gives $\operatorname{Im}D_W = -\frac{1}{8\pi r}[\delta(t - r) - \delta(t + r)]$, and $D = 2\operatorname{Im}D_W$ ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-9|Theorem §C2.5.9]]). With $\delta(t^2 - r^2) = [\delta(t - r) + \delta(t + r)]/2r$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-1|EM Theorem §B1.2.1]]), $\operatorname{sgn}(t)\delta(\xi^2) = [\delta(t - r) - \delta(t + r)]/2r$. The real parts give $D_1 = 2\operatorname{Re}D_W = -\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\mathcal P\frac{1}{t^2 - r^2}$.
>
> *Massive.* Outside the cone, Theorem §C2.5.10 and §C2.5.7, 1. Inside, $D = 2\operatorname{Im}D_W$ and $D_1 = 2\operatorname{Re}D_W$ with Theorem §C2.5.7, 2. On the cone, $K_1(z) = \frac1z + \frac z2\ln\frac z2 + \cdots$: the leading term is the massless function and gives the massless $\delta(\xi^2)$ term; the logarithm gives no delta function, only a finite jump, since $\ln s$ changes by $i\pi$ between $s = \pm i\tau$: $D \to m^2/8\pi$ just inside the cone, the $\tau \to 0$ limit of $mJ_1(m\tau)/4\pi\tau$. Assembling the three regions gives the stated formula.

> [!remark] Remark: Sharp and blurred propagation
> For $m = 0$ the commutator is supported on the cone alone: a massless disturbance travels exactly at the speed of light and leaves nothing behind, Huygens' principle in its sharp form, which holds for the wave equation in three (more generally odd, $\ge 3$) space dimensions (compare [[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle|WO §A4.4]] and the retarded potentials of [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|EM Theorem §B11.1.4]]). For $m > 0$ a $J_1$ tail fills the inside of the cone: the components of a massive wave travel at all group velocities below 1 ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-6|REL Theorem §B4.1.6]]), so the signal arrives on the cone and keeps arriving afterwards.
>

## Commutators at unequal times

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.12: Commutators at Unequal Times
> 1. All commutators of the free field are $c$-numbers fixed by $D$:
>
> $$
> [\phi(x), \phi(y)] = iD(x - y), \qquad [\phi(x), \pi(y)] = i\,\partial_{y^0}D(x - y) = i\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\cos\bigl(p\cdot(x - y)\bigr), \qquad [\pi(x), \pi(y)] = i\,\partial_{x^0}\partial_{y^0}D(x - y) .
> $$
>
> 2. $D$ is the unique solution of $(\partial^2 + m^2)D = 0$ with Cauchy data $D(0, \boldsymbol\xi) = 0$, $\partial_0D(0, \boldsymbol\xi) = -\delta^3(\boldsymbol\xi)$.
>
> Only the equal-time relations are postulated; the equation of motion carries them to every pair of times.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.7 (Principle "What is postulated and what is derived" and the derivations after it)*

^thm-c2-5-12

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *1.* $\pi(y) = \partial_{y^0}\phi(y)$ ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]]) and $\phi(x)$ does not depend on $y$, so $\partial_{y^0}[\phi(x), \phi(y)] = [\phi(x), \pi(y)]$; likewise with $\partial_{x^0}$. In the mode integral of [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-9|Theorem §C2.5.9]], 3, $\partial_{y^0}(p\cdot\xi) = -E_{\mathbf p}$ and $\partial_{x^0}(p\cdot\xi) = +E_{\mathbf p}$, which gives the cosine form and $[\pi(x), \pi(y)] = -i\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}E_{\mathbf p}\sin(p\cdot\xi)$. *Check at equal times:* the phase is $-\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$, the sine integrals vanish by oddness, and the cosine integral is $\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$ ([[§CA.1 Generalized Functions and Fourier Transforms#^thm-ca-1-4|Theorem §CA.1.4]]): the canonical relations are recovered. At unequal times the phases $E_{\mathbf p}(x^0 - y^0)$ smear the delta function out.
>
> *2.* Every mode of $D$ is on shell, so it solves the equation. Its data at $\xi^0 = 0$ are the canonical relations: $D = 0$ there, and $\partial_{x^0}D = -i[\pi(x), \phi(y)] = -i(-i)\delta^3 = -\delta^3$. Uniqueness: a solution with a spatial Fourier transform has the form $f = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\bigl(\alpha_{\mathbf p}e^{-iE_{\mathbf p}t} + \beta_{\mathbf p}e^{iE_{\mathbf p}t}\bigr)$, whose data at $t = 0$ are $\tilde f = (\alpha + \beta)/\sqrt{2E}$ and $\tilde{\dot f} = -iE(\alpha - \beta)/\sqrt{2E}$; since $E_{\mathbf p} \ge m > 0$ this $2\times2$ system determines $\alpha_{\mathbf p}$, $\beta_{\mathbf p}$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.13: The Commutator Function Propagates the Field
> For every time $y^0$,
>
> $$
> \phi(x) = -\int d^3y\;D(x - y)\overleftrightarrow{\partial}_{y^0}\phi(y) = -\int d^3y\,\Bigl[D(x - y)\,\pi(y) - \partial_{y^0}D(x - y)\,\phi(y)\Bigr],
> $$
>
> with $f\overleftrightarrow{\partial}_0g = f\partial_0g - (\partial_0f)g$. Since $D$ vanishes outside the cone, $\phi(x)$ is fixed by the data $(\phi, \pi)$ on any earlier slice inside the backward light cone of $x$, and for $m = 0$ by the data on the surface of that cone alone.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.7 (Derivation "The commutator function propagates the field")*

^thm-c2-5-13

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *Independent of $y^0$.* $D(x - y)$ and $\phi(y)$ both solve the Klein–Gordon equation in $y$, and the $y^0$-derivative of $\int d^3y\,(f\partial_0g - \partial_0f\,g)$ is $\int d^3y\,(f\nabla^2g - \nabla^2f\,g)$, the mass terms cancelling; it vanishes after two integrations by parts. This is the conservation of the Klein–Gordon product ([[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]).
>
> *Correct at $y^0 = x^0$.* There $D = 0$ and $\partial_{y^0}D(x - y) = -\partial_{x^0}D = +\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$ ([[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-12|Theorem §C2.5.12]], 2), so the right side is $\int d^3y\,\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)\phi(x^0, \mathbf y) = \phi(x)$. Being independent of $y^0$, it equals $\phi(x)$ for every $y^0$. The support statements follow from [[§C2.5 Heisenberg Fields, Two-Point Functions and Causality#^thm-c2-5-11|Theorem §C2.5.11]].

^rem-c2-5-10

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Other states: what belongs to the dynamics and what to the state
> In any state $\rho$, $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho[\phi(x), \phi(y)]) = iD(x - y)$, because the commutator is a $c$-number (Theorem §C2.5.9, 1): $D$, and with it causality and the retarded response, belong to the dynamics. Everything state-dependent is in the symmetric part $D_1^\rho(x, y) = \operatorname{Tr}(\rho\{\phi(x), \phi(y)\})$, which need not depend on $x - y$ alone.
> - *Thermal state* $\rho = e^{-\beta H}/Z$: the modes are independent oscillators with $\langle a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger a_{\mathbf q}\rangle_\beta = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)\,n(E_{\mathbf p})$, $n(E) = (e^{\beta E} - 1)^{-1}$ ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|TH Theorem §B10.1.2]]), and with $1 + 2n = \coth(\beta E/2)$,
>
> $$
> D_1^\beta(\xi) = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_{\mathbf p}}\,\coth\frac{\beta E_{\mathbf p}}{2}\,\bigl(e^{-ip\cdot\xi} + e^{ip\cdot\xi}\bigr) \;\xrightarrow{\beta\to\infty}\; D_1(\xi):
> $$
>
> heating enlarges the fluctuations of each mode by $\coth(\beta E/2) \ge 1$ and leaves the commutator untouched.
> - *Coherent state* ([[§C2.4 Coherent States and the Classical Field#^def-c2-4-1|Def. §C2.4.1]]): $\phi = \bar\phi + \tilde\phi$, with $\bar\phi$ the classical field and $\tilde\phi$ carrying vacuum correlations, so $D_1^{\mathrm{coh}}(x, y) = 2\bar\phi(x)\bar\phi(y) + D_1(x - y)$: the vacuum's fluctuations riding on a classical wave.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 6 §6.11 (Derivation "Generalization: other states")*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Why "Wightman function": the axiomatic view
> The $n$-point functions $W_n = \langle0|\phi(x_1)\cdots\phi(x_n)|0\rangle$ are tempered distributions, and Wightman's axioms characterize a field theory by them. For $n = 2$ every axiom has been seen here: Poincaré invariance (Theorem §C2.5.4, 2), the spectrum condition as analyticity in the forward tube (Theorem §C2.5.5), locality as the symmetry of $W_2$ at spacelike separation (Theorem §C2.5.10), positivity from the Hilbert-space norm and clustering from the uniqueness of the vacuum. The reconstruction theorem runs the logic backwards: distributions with these properties determine a Hilbert space, a unique invariant vacuum and a field, so the Fock space of [[§C2.2 The Real Scalar Field#^pr-c2-2-7|Principle §C2.2.7]] is a consequence of the correlation functions. At imaginary times the $W_n$ become the Euclidean Schwinger functions (Theorem §C2.5.6 is the $n = 2$ one), and the Osterwalder–Schrader theorem (reflection positivity) is the rigorous basis of the Euclidean path integral and of lattice field theory. For the free field every $W_n$ is fixed by $W_2 = D_W$ through Wick's theorem (QFT C6, planned).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 5 §5.4 (Derivation "Why 'Wightman function': the axiomatic view")*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: With interactions: the Källén–Lehmann representation
> Unequal-time commutators of an interacting field are genuine operators. Two things survive: the equal-time relations remain the postulate, and microcausality becomes an axiom. The vacuum expectation value of the commutator becomes a superposition of free commutator functions over masses, $\langle0|[\phi(x), \phi(y)]|0\rangle = \int d\mu^2\,\rho(\mu^2)\,iD_\mu(x - y)$, and applying the equal-time relation to both sides (Theorem §C2.5.12, 2: $\partial_0D_\mu = -\delta^3$ for every $\mu$) gives the sum rule $\int d\mu^2\,\rho(\mu^2) = 1$. PS §7.1 derives the representation.

The same $e^{-mr}$ sets the range of the correlations $D_1$ and of meson exchange, QFT C7 (planned).

> - The light-cone boundary between commuting and non-commuting points comes from the orbit structure of $SO^+(1,3)$ ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^rem-b1-3-1|REL Remark: Normal forms, and the orbits of the Lorentz group]]); the invariance of $\operatorname{sgn}p^0$ that makes $\theta(p^0)\delta(p^2 - m^2)$ invariant is the same fact for momenta ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|REL Theorem §B1.3.1]]).
> - The massless commutator $-\frac{1}{4\pi r}[\delta(t - r) - \delta(t + r)]$ is, up to the factor $i$ and the step function, the retarded kernel of the wave equation behind the retarded potentials ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|EM Theorem §B11.1.4]]); the full statement is the retarded Green's function of [[§C2.6 Green's Functions and the Feynman Propagator#^thm-c2-6-4|Theorem §C2.6.4]].
