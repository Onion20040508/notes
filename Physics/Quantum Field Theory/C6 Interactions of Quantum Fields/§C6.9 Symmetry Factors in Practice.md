The coefficient of a diagram is $1/S$, with $S$ the order of its symmetry group ([[§C6.8 Position-Space Feynman Rules#^thm-c6-8-1|Theorem §C6.8.1]]). In practice $S$ is read off the drawing as a product of local factors (loops, bundles of parallel lines) and a global one (permutations of the vertices), a rule of thumb that is in fact exact ([[§CA.7 Pairings, Graph Symmetries and the Exponential Formula#^thm-ca-7-6|Theorem §CA.7.6]]). This section states the slides' rules in that language and works the examples of the lecture, the user's notes and PS, each both by the product rule and by counting contractions; every count was checked by enumerating the pairings. Yu treats symmetry factors in §7.3 for the self-energy contributions $\langle\mathbf k|iT|\mathbf k\rangle$ of the $S$-operator, counting contractions with the initial and final states rather than with fixed external points; the factors agree.

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): a diagram's value includes $-i\lambda\int d^4z$ per vertex and $1/S$ ([[§C6.8 Position-Space Feynman Rules#^thm-c6-8-2|Theorem §C6.8.2]]); "$k$ of $N$" means $k$ of the $N = n!\,(4!)^n$ relabellings, i.e. $k$ complete contractions produce the diagram, and $S = N/k$ ([[§CA.7 Pairings, Graph Symmetries and the Exponential Formula#^thm-ca-7-4|Theorem §CA.7.4]]).

> [!remark] Remark: The slides' general rule in this language
> Slide 17 states the principle: "Reordering of legs = topologically distinct diagram ⇒ S = 1"; "If symmetry ⇒ same diagram then S = order of symmetry." The first sentence is the case $\operatorname{Aut} = 1$: every one of the $n!\,(4!)^n$ relabellings gives a different contraction, and the prefactor cancels completely. The second is [[§C6.8 Position-Space Feynman Rules#^def-c6-8-1|Def. §C6.8.1]]. The user's notes' "in practice, multiply: a factor 2 for each line that begins and ends at the same vertex; $k!$ for each set of $k$ lines joining the same two vertices; the number of permutations of internal vertices that map the diagram to itself" is [[§CA.7 Pairings, Graph Symmetries and the Exponential Formula#^thm-ca-7-6|Theorem §CA.7.6]], with one clarification: $k$ loops at the same vertex count as a set of $k$ lines joining the same two vertices (figure-eight: $2\cdot2\cdot2!$). With external points held fixed, a vertex joined to an external point cannot be moved, so "more symmetric diagrams have larger $S$; diagrams anchored to many external points have smaller $S$."
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 14, slide 17, and transcript · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 14 §14.5 (Principle "The symmetry factor")*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C6.9.1: The First-Order Diagrams
> - *The tadpole* (the slides' "self-energy"): $x$–$z$, $z$–$y$, one loop at $z$. Product rule: one loop, $S = 2$ ("reflection of internal line a symmetry", slide 18). Counting: $x$ to one of the four legs (4), $y$ to one of the remaining three (3), the last two together: 12 of $4! = 24$, $\frac{12}{24} = \frac12$.
> - *The figure-eight*: two loops at $z$. Product rule: $2\cdot2$ for reversing each loop, $2!$ for exchanging them, $S = 2^3 = 8$ ("three reflection symmetries", slide 18). Counting: the four legs paired among themselves, $3!! = 3$ of 24, $\frac{3}{24} = \frac18$.
>
> These are the two coefficients of [[§C6.7 Feynman Diagrams for Correlation Functions#^thm-c6-7-1|Theorem §C6.7.1]] (slide 17: "In the example (two point function at first order): S = 8 and 2 ⇒ factors $\frac18$, $\frac12$").
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 14, slides 17–18 · PS §4.4, p. 93 ($S = 2$, $S = 2\cdot2\cdot2 = 8$) · Yu §7.3, eqs. (7.124) ($S = 2$, Fig. 7.13) and (7.125) ($S = 8$, Fig. 7.14) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 14 §14.5, Figure "Symmetry factors" ("12 of 4!", "3 of 4!")*

> [!example] Example §C6.9.2: The Melon: S = 48 by Counting
> Two vertices $z_1$, $z_2$, no external points, four lines from $z_1$ to $z_2$ (the slides' "melonic vacuum bubble (pumpkin diagram)").
>
> **Step 1** (prefactor). Second order: $\frac{1}{2!}\bigl(\frac{1}{4!}\bigr)^2 = \frac{1}{1152}$: $\frac12$ from expanding the exponential to second order, $\frac{1}{4!}$ from each vertex.
>
> **Step 2** (contractions). The first leg of $z_1$ pairs with one of the four legs of $z_2$ (4), the second with one of the remaining three (3), the third with one of two (2), the fourth with the last (1): $4! = 24$ contractions.
>
> **Step 3** (ratio). $\frac{24}{1152} = \frac{1}{48}$: one $4!$ cancels, the other and the $\frac12$ do not ("only 4! cancel", slide 18).
>
> **Step 4** (as a symmetry). Product rule: the four lines form one bundle ($4!$), and the two vertices can be exchanged ($|\Gamma| = 2$): $S = 4!\cdot2 = 48$. The lecture's test: describe the diagram to a friend who is not looking at the blackboard, "from vertex one to vertex two, four times." The friend has $4!$ orders in which to draw the lines and can put either vertex on top; the prescription does not distinguish these, which is why they count as one diagram.
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 14, slide 18 ("Permutation symmetry of all lines ⇒ $\frac12\frac{1}{4!^2}$ but only 4! cancel"), and transcript · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 14 §14.5 (Derivation "The melon: S = 48 by counting")*

> [!example] Example §C6.9.3: The Other Second-Order Vacuum Bubbles
> - *The dumbbell*: one loop at each of $z_1$, $z_2$ and two lines between them. Product rule: $2\cdot2$ (two loops) $\cdot\,2!$ (the double line) $\cdot\,2$ (exchanging the vertices) $= 16$. Counting: choose the two legs of $z_1$ that form its loop ($\binom42 = 6$), the same at $z_2$ (6), and join the remaining two legs of $z_1$ to those of $z_2$ (2 ways): 72 of 1152, $\frac{72}{1152} = \frac{1}{16}$.
> - *Two figure-eights*: a figure-eight at each vertex. Product rule: $8\cdot8$ for the two figure-eights, $\cdot\,2$ for exchanging them, $= 128$. Counting: $3\cdot3 = 9$ of 1152, $\frac{9}{1152} = \frac{1}{128}$.
>
> With the melon ([[§C6.9 Symmetry Factors in Practice#^ex-c6-9-2|Example §C6.9.2]]) these exhaust the eight legs of two vertices: $24 + 72 + 9 = 105 = 7!!$ ([[§CA.7 Pairings, Graph Symmetries and the Exponential Formula#^ex-ca-7-1|Example §CA.7.1]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 14 §14.5, Figure "Symmetry factors" ($S = 2\cdot2\cdot2!\cdot2 = 16$, "72 of $2\cdot4!^2$"; $S = 8\cdot8\cdot2 = 128$, "9 of $2\cdot4!^2$") · counts checked by enumeration*

> [!example] Example §C6.9.4: Two Second-Order Corrections to the Two-Point Function
> - *The snowman*: $x$ and $y$ joined to $z_1$, two lines from $z_1$ to $z_2$, one loop at $z_2$. Product rule: $2$ (the loop) $\cdot\,2!$ (the double line); the vertices cannot be exchanged ($z_1$ is anchored at $x$ and $y$); $S = 4$. Counting: $x$ to one of the 8 legs (8, which fixes the vertex called $z_1$), $y$ to one of the 3 other legs of that vertex (3), the two remaining legs of $z_1$ to two of the four legs of $z_2$ in order ($4\cdot3$), the last two legs of $z_2$ together: $8\cdot3\cdot4\cdot3 = 288$ of 1152, $\frac{288}{1152} = \frac14$.
> - *The chain of two tadpoles*: $x$–$z_1$, a loop at $z_1$, $z_1$–$z_2$, a loop at $z_2$, $z_2$–$y$. Product rule: $2\cdot2$ (two loops), no vertex symmetry (one vertex is anchored at $x$, the other at $y$); $S = 4$. Counting: $x$ to one of 8 legs (8), $y$ to one of the 4 legs of the other vertex (4), one of the 3 free legs of $z_1$ to one of the 3 free legs of $z_2$ ($3\cdot3$), the remaining pairs at each vertex form the loops: $8\cdot4\cdot3\cdot3 = 288$ of 1152, $\frac14$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 14 §14.5, Figure "Symmetry factors" ($S = 2\cdot2! = 4$, $S = 2\cdot2 = 4$, "288 of $2\cdot4!^2$") · Yu §7.3, eqs. (7.131)–(7.132) and Fig. 7.15(c) (the snowman, $S = 4$), eqs. (7.127)–(7.128) and Fig. 7.15(a) (the chain, $S = 4$) · counts checked by enumeration*

> [!example] Example §C6.9.5: PS's Cactus, a Third-Order Example
> PS's third-order contraction (eq. (4.45)): $x$–$z$, a loop at $z$, $z$–$w$, $w$–$y$, two lines from $w$ to $u$, a loop at $u$, with value $\frac{1}{3!}\bigl(\frac{-i\lambda}{4!}\bigr)^3\int d^4z\,d^4w\,d^4u\,D_F(x - z)D_F(z - z)D_F(z - w)D_F(w - y)D_F^2(w - u)D_F(u - u)$ per contraction.
> - *Counting* (PS): $3!$ (interchange of vertices) $\times\;4\cdot3$ (placement of contractions into the $z$ vertex) $\times\;4\cdot3\cdot2$ (into the $w$ vertex) $\times\;4\cdot3$ (into the $u$ vertex) $\times\;\frac12$ (interchange of the $w$–$u$ contractions) $= 10\,368$, "roughly 1/13 of the total of 135,135 possible full contractions of the 14 operators". The relabelling group has $3!\,(4!)^3 = 82\,944$ elements, and $\frac{10\,368}{82\,944} = \frac18$.
> - *Product rule*: $2$ (loop at $z$) $\cdot\,2$ (loop at $u$) $\cdot\,2!$ (double line $w$–$u$), no vertex symmetry: $S = 8 = 2\cdot2\cdot2$ (PS p. 93: "Two factors of 2 come from lines that start and end on the same vertex … The other factor of 2 comes from the two propagators connecting $w$ to $u$").
>
> *Source: PS §4.4, pp. 92–93, eq. (4.45) and the "cactus" diagram · enumeration of the 135 135 pairings of 14 fields (10 368 produce the cactus)*

> [!remark] Remark: How much this matters
> Slide 18: "Details of symmetry factors are not a high priority in this course." The lecture: "this will drive you crazy, especially the night before the exam … don't lose sleep over this … don't overthink" (Problem Set 8, posted the evening before, has some). PS: "Most people never need to evaluate a diagram with a symmetry factor greater than 2, so there's no need to worry too much about these technicalities … When in doubt, you can always determine the symmetry factor by counting equivalent contractions" (p. 93). Large symmetry factors belong to diagrams with few external points, above all the vacuum bubbles, which drop out of correlation functions ([[§C6.11 The iε Limit and Vacuum Bubbles#^thm-c6-11-5|Theorem §C6.11.5]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 14, slide 18, and transcript · PS §4.4, p. 93 · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 14 §14.5 (paragraph "How much this matters")*

> [!remark] Remark: Symmetry factors in QED
> Slide 18: "In QED the symmetry factor is always $S = 1$." The lecture's reason: "if it was a more complex theory in such a way that the $\phi$'s here weren't all the same, they actually truly meant something different, then we wouldn't have these annoying factors … that is exactly what's going to happen in QED. One of them has charge plus, the other has charge minus, one has a photon, and you can't just exchange them. They have different roles … By starting with this more pedagogical example, we unfortunately get the particular complexity that's not representative of some of the theories."
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 14, slide 18, and transcript*

> [!remark] Remark: Yu's route
> Yu computes symmetry factors for matrix elements of the $S$-operator: the first-order self-energy $\langle\mathbf k|iT_2^{(1)}|\mathbf k\rangle$ (eq. (7.124)), where $C_4^2 = 6$ ways of contracting two of the four fields at the vertex and $A_2^2 = 2$ ways of contracting the other two with the initial and final states reduce $\frac{1}{4!}$ to $\frac12$; the first-order vacuum bubble (eq. (7.125), $\frac18$); and the second-order self-energies (eqs. (7.126)–(7.132)), among them the chain ((7.127)–(7.128), $S = 4$) and the snowman ((7.131)–(7.132), $S = 4$). In each case the two ways of contracting with the initial and final states cancel the $\frac{1}{2!}$ of the second order. He states the principle in a box: "the factor obtained by analysing the symmetry of the Feynman diagram always agrees with the factor left over in the $T$-matrix computation" (p. 293), and remarks that tree diagrams contributing to scattering or decay have $S = 1$, while loop diagrams may have nontrivial symmetry factors (p. 294).
>
> *Source: Yu §7.3, pp. 292–296*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $S$ is the order of a stabilizer, and the product rule is the first isomorphism theorem applied to the map that forgets the legs: local symmetries form the kernel, vertex permutations the image — [[§CA.7 Pairings, Graph Symmetries and the Exponential Formula#^thm-ca-7-6|Theorem §CA.7.6]], [[§41 The First and Second Isomorphism Theorems#^thm-41-1|493 Thm. §41.1]].
> - Identical particles in quantum mechanics are counted by the same permutation groups; a symmetry factor is the field-theory trace of indistinguishability — [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate|QM §C12.1★]].
> - The vacuum bubbles carry the largest symmetry factors and are removed by the denominator of the master formula — [[§C6.11 The iε Limit and Vacuum Bubbles#^thm-c6-11-5|Theorem §C6.11.5]].
