What an experiment observes is an event rate, and the cross section is that rate with the set-up divided out ([[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-1|Def. §C6.13.1]]); classically it is an area in the plane of impact parameters ([[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-2|Def. §C6.13.2]]). The goal is a quantum, relativistic formula for it in terms of the amplitude $\mathcal M$ of [[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^def-c6-12-3|Def. §C6.12.3]]. Momentum eigenstates never "come in" from anywhere, and squaring amplitudes between them produces squares of delta functions; so the incoming particles are wave packets, one of them displaced by an impact parameter $\mathbf b$, and the probability of scattering is integrated over $\mathbf b$. The result, after fourteen integrals, one Jacobian and an argument from smoothness, is "the longest formula we have in the entire class", [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^thm-c6-13-6|Theorem §C6.13.6]]: initial kinematics × $|\mathcal M|^2$ × final phase space. It is done once and never again. Yu reaches the same formula without wave packets: he normalizes the states in a box of volume $\tilde V$, squares the delta function honestly, $(2\pi)^4\delta^4(0) = \tilde V\tilde T$, and divides by the duration and the flux (§6.5.1–§6.5.2, eqs. (6.276)–(6.313)); Remark: Yu's route, at the end.

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): beams along the $z$-axis; $\perp$ marks the components $(x, y)$ transverse to it; $E_A \equiv E_{\mathbf k_A} = \sqrt{\mathbf k_A^2 + m_A^2}$ inside integrals and $E_A = E_{\mathbf p_A}$ in final formulas; $\mathbf v = \mathbf k/E$ is the velocity; barred momenta $\bar{\mathbf k}_A$, $\bar{\mathbf k}_B$ label the second, complex-conjugated copy of an amplitude.

## The cross section: a universal event rate

> [!definition] Definition §C6.13.1: The Cross Section as a Universal Event Rate
> The observable of a scattering experiment is an event rate. The **cross section** $\sigma$ is a universal event rate: the rate with the factors of the experimental set-up divided out, independent of the set-up. For a beam of particles $B$ with flux $j_B$ (particles per unit area per unit time) on a target of $N_A$ particles $A$, the number of events in a time $t$ is
>
> $$
> N_{\rm events} = \sigma\,N_A\,j_B\,t ,
> $$
>
> so $\sigma$ has the dimension of an area. A separate, set-up-dependent step turns $\sigma$ back into a count of events.
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 8 ("Observable in scattering experiment: event rate. Cross-section $= \sigma$: is a universal event rate. It is independent of experimental set-up."), and transcript ("how many clicks do you get per time … I mean divided by that … the cross section is that universal event rate") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.2 (paragraph "Event counting (filled in)") · Yu §6.5.2, eq. (6.308) · PS §4.5, eqs. (4.59)–(4.61)*

> [!definition] Definition §C6.13.2: The Classical Differential Cross Section
> In classical, non-relativistic mechanics a particle aimed at the target with impact parameter $\mathbf b$ (its transverse offset from a head-on hit, an area element $d^2b$ in **impact-parameter space**) leaves in a definite direction. The differential cross section is the area giving scattering into $d\Omega$:
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{d^2b}{d\Omega} .
> $$
>
> This is the classical-mechanics definition ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^def-b4-2-1|CM Def. §B4.2.1]], the number scattered into $d\Omega$ per unit time divided by the incident flux) rewritten with the deflection function ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-2|CM Theorem §B4.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 8 ("Definition in classical, non-relativistic mechanics: $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}$ = area giving scattering into $d\Omega = \frac{d^2b}{d\Omega}$. $d^2b$ is an area in impact parameter space."), and transcript ("The cross section is literally what kind of area should you hit … if you want it out in this final angle") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.2, eq. (classicalxsec)*

> [!remark] Remark: The goal: a quantum, relativistic version
> "What we want to do is to have a quantum and relativistic version of that, where it's not actually literally an area like that, but it's something that has the same properties." It should strip out "the kinematics of this situation and the quantum stuff with squared wave functions … that are completely universal. Nothing to do with the actual interaction", and leave a formula in terms of $\mathcal M$: "if you learn how to compute $M$ … you can compute the cross section." Quantum mechanics already did this for a fixed potential, with a transition rate divided by a probability current ([[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^def-c10-1-1|QM Def. §C10.1.1]]); the new features are two moving particles and relativistic kinematics.
>

## Wave packets and the scattering probability

"You're telling me these initial particles have [definite] momentum … What is their position then? There was this Heisenberg business." A momentum eigenstate is spread over all space and is not normalizable ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^cau-c2a-4-1|§C2a.4, Caution: Plane-wave states are not normalizable]]); by "momentum $\mathbf p$" we mean "as close as I can get to momentum $\mathbf p$".

> [!definition] Definition §C6.13.3: Wave Packet
> A smeared initial state: the **wave packet** peaked at $\mathbf k = \mathbf p$ is
>
> $$
> |\phi_{\mathbf p}\rangle = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}}}\,\phi(\mathbf k)\,|\mathbf k\rangle , \qquad \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,|\phi(\mathbf k)|^2 = 1 ,
> $$
>
> with a smooth profile $\phi(\mathbf k)$ sharply peaked at $\mathbf k = \mathbf p$. It is the wave packet $|g\rangle$ of [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-3|Def. §C2a.4.3]] with the profile $g(\mathbf k) = \sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}}\,\phi(\mathbf k)$.
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 11 ("Smeared initial state: wave packet peaked at $\vec k = \vec p$ … Normalization $\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}|\phi(\vec k)|^2 = 1$"), and transcript ("I'm going to take my energy normalizations and make them part of this") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.3, eq. (wavepacket) · PS §4.5, eqs. (4.65)–(4.66)*

^def-c6-13-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.13.1: The Normalization Condition Is the Unit Norm
> For the wave packet of [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-3|Def. §C6.13.3]],
>
> $$
> \langle\phi_{\mathbf p}|\phi_{\mathbf p}\rangle = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,|\phi(\mathbf k)|^2 ,
> $$
>
> so the normalization of the profile is $\langle\phi_{\mathbf p}|\phi_{\mathbf p}\rangle = 1$: the factor $1/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}}$ absorbs the relativistic normalization of $|\mathbf k\rangle$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.3 (the check after Definition "Wave packets") · PS §4.5, eq. (4.66)*

^thm-c6-13-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (two copies). With a second integration variable $\mathbf k'$ for the bra,
>
> $$
> \langle\phi_{\mathbf p}|\phi_{\mathbf p}\rangle = \int\frac{d^3k\,d^3k'}{(2\pi)^6}\,\frac{\phi^\ast(\mathbf k)\,\phi(\mathbf k')}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}\,2E_{\mathbf k'}}}\,\langle\mathbf k|\mathbf k'\rangle .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the overlap). Insert $\langle\mathbf k|\mathbf k'\rangle = 2E_{\mathbf k}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf k - \mathbf k')$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-1|Def. §C2a.4.1]]) and do the $\mathbf k'$ integral with the delta function, $\mathbf k' = \mathbf k$, $E_{\mathbf k'} = E_{\mathbf k}$:
>
> $$
> \langle\phi_{\mathbf p}|\phi_{\mathbf p}\rangle = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{\phi^\ast(\mathbf k)}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}}}\,2E_{\mathbf k}\int\frac{d^3k'}{(2\pi)^3}\,(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf k - \mathbf k')\,\frac{\phi(\mathbf k')}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k'}}} = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{\phi^\ast(\mathbf k)}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}}}\,2E_{\mathbf k}\,\frac{\phi(\mathbf k)}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (collect). $\frac{2E_{\mathbf k}}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}}\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}}} = 1$ and $\phi^\ast\phi = |\phi|^2$:
>
> $$
> \langle\phi_{\mathbf p}|\phi_{\mathbf p}\rangle = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,|\phi(\mathbf k)|^2 .
> $$
>
> The pairing of δ with the smooth, rapidly decreasing profiles is a pairing of a tempered distribution with a test function ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-5|Theorem §C2a.4.5]], 1).
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - With the course's profile $\phi$, probabilities are computed with the plain measure $\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}$; with the profile $g = \sqrt{2E}\phi$ of [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-3|Def. §C2a.4.3]] the same norm reads $\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^32E_{\mathbf k}}|g|^2$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.13.2: The Phase Displaces the Packet by b
> Multiplying a momentum profile by $e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf k}$ translates the packet by $+\mathbf b$ in space: if $\psi(\mathbf x) = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\phi(\mathbf k)\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}$, then the profile $e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf k}\phi(\mathbf k)$ has position-space form $\psi(\mathbf x - \mathbf b)$. Equivalently, $e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot\hat{\mathbf P}}$ is the translation by $\mathbf b$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.3 (paragraph "Why $e^{-i\vec b\cdot\vec k_B}$ shifts $B$ by $+\vec b$ (filled in)") · PS §4.5, p. 103*

^thm-c6-13-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (substitute and combine the exponents). With $e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf k}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} = e^{i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf x - \mathbf b)}$,
>
> $$
> \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\bigl[e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf k}\phi(\mathbf k)\bigr]\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\phi(\mathbf k)\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf x - \mathbf b)} = \psi(\mathbf x - \mathbf b) ,
> $$
>
> the definition of $\psi$ at the point $\mathbf x - \mathbf b$.
>
> **Step 2** (the peak moves by $+\mathbf b$). If $|\psi|$ is largest at $\mathbf x_0$, then $|\psi(\mathbf x - \mathbf b)|$ is largest where $\mathbf x - \mathbf b = \mathbf x_0$, i.e. at $\mathbf x = \mathbf x_0 + \mathbf b$.
>
> **Step 3** (operator form). With $\hat{\mathbf P}|\mathbf k\rangle = \mathbf k|\mathbf k\rangle$, the exponential series gives $e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot\hat{\mathbf P}}|\mathbf k\rangle = e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf k}|\mathbf k\rangle$, so on a packet
>
> $$
> e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot\hat{\mathbf P}}\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{\phi(\mathbf k)}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}}}|\mathbf k\rangle = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf k}\phi(\mathbf k)}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}}}|\mathbf k\rangle :
> $$
>
> multiplying the profile by the phase is acting with $e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot\hat{\mathbf P}}$, the translation operator with the sign of [[§C3.5 Covariant Quantum Fields and the Free Scalar's Representation#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Since $\mathbf b \perp \hat{\mathbf z}$, $b^z = 0$, only $\mathbf k_B^\perp$ enters the phase: $\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf k_B = b^xk_B^x + b^yk_B^y + 0\cdot k_B^z = \mathbf b\cdot\mathbf k_B^\perp$. This is why the $\mathbf b$-integral below constrains only the transverse momenta.

> [!definition] Definition §C6.13.5: The Scattering Probability at Fixed Impact Parameter
> The probability of scattering into the final phase-space element $\prod_i\frac{d^3p_i}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{2E_i}$, for a particular impact parameter $\mathbf b$, is
>
> $$
> \mathcal P(\mathbf b) = \prod_{\text{final }i}\frac{d^3p_i}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{2E_i}\;\bigl|{}_{\rm out}\langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|\phi_A\phi_B\rangle_{\rm in}\bigr|^2 .

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.13.3: The Quantum Cross Section Is the Event Rate per Unit Flux
> Let the $B$ particles arrive at random transverse positions with density $n_B = j_Bt$ per unit area, constant over the range of $\mathbf b$ where $\mathcal P(\mathbf b) \ne 0$. Then the expected number of events per target particle in the final-state element of [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-5|Def. §C6.13.5]] is
>
> $$
> N_{\rm events} = n_B\int d^2b\;\mathcal P(\mathbf b) = d\sigma\;j_B\,t ,
> $$
>
> [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-1|Def. §C6.13.1]] with $N_A = 1$ and $d\sigma$ of [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-6|Def. §C6.13.6]]. If $\mathcal P(\mathbf b) \in \{0, 1\}$ (classical trajectories), $d\sigma$ is the area of the set of impact parameters that end in $d\Omega$, [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-2|Def. §C6.13.2]].
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.3 (paragraph "Why integrate over $\vec b$ (filled in)") · PS §4.5, p. 105 · PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 12, and transcript*

^thm-c6-13-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (sum over the incident particles). Each $B$ particle $i$, at impact parameter $\mathbf b_i$, scatters into the element with probability $\mathcal P(\mathbf b_i)$; the expected number of events is the sum, and the expected number of $B$ particles in $d^2b$ is $n_B\,d^2b$:
>
> $$
> N_{\rm events} = \Bigl\langle\sum_{\text{incident }i}\mathcal P(\mathbf b_i)\Bigr\rangle = \int d^2b\;n_B\,\mathcal P(\mathbf b) = n_B\int d^2b\;\mathcal P(\mathbf b) ,
> $$
>
> $n_B$ taken out of the integral because it is constant where $\mathcal P \ne 0$.
>
> **Step 2** (insert the definitions). $\int d^2b\,\mathcal P(\mathbf b) = d\sigma$ ([[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-6|Def. §C6.13.6]]) and $n_B = j_Bt$ (particles per area = flux × time):
>
> $$
> N_{\rm events} = d\sigma\cdot j_B\,t = d\sigma\cdot N_A\cdot j_B\,t\big|_{N_A = 1} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (classical limit). If $\mathcal P(\mathbf b) = 1$ for $\mathbf b \in R$ (the impact parameters whose trajectories end in $d\Omega$) and $0$ otherwise,
>
> $$
> d\sigma = \int d^2b\;\mathcal P(\mathbf b) = \int_Rd^2b = \operatorname{area}(R) ,
> $$
>
> the $d^2b$ of $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{d^2b}{d\Omega}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Integrating over $\mathbf b$ is what turns a probability into an area. Quantum mechanically every $\mathbf b$ contributes with its weight $\mathcal P(\mathbf b) \in [0, 1]$; the lecture calls it "very quantum": "it's not a specific $d^2b$ that gives me into my particular final … angle. It's all of them. All of them with a certain probability."

> [!theorem] Lemma §C6.13.4: Away from the Forward Direction Only iT̂ Contributes
> If the final particles differ from the initial ones in number or species, or have momenta $\{\mathbf p_1, \mathbf p_2\} \ne \{\mathbf k_A, \mathbf k_B\}$, then
>
> $$
> {}_{\rm out}\langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|\mathbf k_A\mathbf k_B\rangle_{\rm in} = \langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|i\hat T|\mathbf k_A\mathbf k_B\rangle = (2\pi)^4\delta^4\Bigl(k_A + k_B - \sum_ip_i\Bigr)\,i\mathcal M .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.3 (paragraph "Which final states (filled in)") · PS §4.5, p. 105 ("we can drop the $\mathbf 1$")*

^lem-c6-13-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (split $\hat S$). By [[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^def-c6-12-1|Def. §C6.12.1]] and [[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^def-c6-12-2|Def. §C6.12.2]],
>
> $$
> {}_{\rm out}\langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|\mathbf k_A\mathbf k_B\rangle_{\rm in} = \langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|\hat S|\mathbf k_A\mathbf k_B\rangle = \langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|\mathbf k_A\mathbf k_B\rangle + \langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|i\hat T|\mathbf k_A\mathbf k_B\rangle .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the "1" term). States with different particle numbers or species are orthogonal, so the first term is 0 unless the final state has two particles of the species of $A$, $B$. Then, for $A$, $B$ of one species ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-2|Theorem §C2a.4.2]], factors $\sqrt{2E}$ of [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-1|Def. §C2a.4.1]]),
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2|\mathbf k_A\mathbf k_B\rangle = 2E_{\mathbf k_A}2E_{\mathbf k_B}(2\pi)^6\bigl[\delta^3(\mathbf p_1 - \mathbf k_A)\delta^3(\mathbf p_2 - \mathbf k_B) + \delta^3(\mathbf p_1 - \mathbf k_B)\delta^3(\mathbf p_2 - \mathbf k_A)\bigr] ,
> $$
>
> (one term only if $A \ne B$), which vanishes unless $\{\mathbf p_1, \mathbf p_2\} = \{\mathbf k_A, \mathbf k_B\}$.
>
> **Step 3** (the amplitude). The remaining term is [[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^def-c6-12-3|Def. §C6.12.3]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - "Assuming that we are not interested in the trivial case of forward scattering where no interaction takes place, we can drop the $\mathbf 1$" (PS p. 105): measured final states are always of this kind (away from the forward direction, or different particles).

## Doing the integrals

Inserting [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-4|Def. §C6.13.4]] into [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-5|Def. §C6.13.5]] and [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-6|Def. §C6.13.6]], the square brings in a second, complex-conjugated copy of the packet integral, with its own variables $\bar{\mathbf k}_A$, $\bar{\mathbf k}_B$ (slide 13):

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\sigma = {}&\Bigl(\prod_i\frac{d^3p_i}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{2E_i}\Bigr)\int d^2b\,\frac{d^3k_A}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{d^3k_B}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{d^3\bar k_A}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{d^3\bar k_B}{(2\pi)^3}\;e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot(\mathbf k_B - \bar{\mathbf k}_B)}\\
&\times\frac{\phi_A(\mathbf k_A)}{\sqrt{2E_A}}\frac{\phi_B(\mathbf k_B)}{\sqrt{2E_B}}\frac{\phi_A^\ast(\bar{\mathbf k}_A)}{\sqrt{2\bar E_A}}\frac{\phi_B^\ast(\bar{\mathbf k}_B)}{\sqrt{2\bar E_B}}\;\langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|\mathbf k_A\mathbf k_B\rangle\,\langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|\bar{\mathbf k}_A\bar{\mathbf k}_B\rangle^\ast .
\end{aligned}
$$

"What a mess!" It comes from "the inconvenient fact" that probabilities are squares of amplitudes. There are 14 integrals, $3 + 3 + 3 + 3$ momenta and 2 components of $\mathbf b$: "six of them will give delta functions, and then that will help us with getting rid of six more. 14 minus 12 is two." The fourteen, and where each goes in [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^der-c6-13-6|Derivation §C6.13.6]]:

| integrals | number | done by | step |
|---|---|---|---|
| $d^2b$ | 2 | $\int d^2b\,e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot(\mathbf k_B - \bar{\mathbf k}_B)} = (2\pi)^2\delta^2(\mathbf k_B^\perp - \bar{\mathbf k}_B^\perp)$: produces 2 deltas | 1 |
| $d^2\bar k_B^\perp$ | 2 | $\delta^2(\mathbf k_B^\perp - \bar{\mathbf k}_B^\perp)$ from $d^2b$ | 3 |
| $d^2\bar k_A^\perp$ | 2 | transverse part of $\delta^4(\bar k_A + \bar k_B - \sum p_i)$ | 3 |
| $d\bar k_A^z\,d\bar k_B^z$ | 2 | $z$ and energy parts of $\delta^4(\bar k_A + \bar k_B - \sum p_i)$, one Jacobian | 4 |
| $d^3k_A\,d^3k_B$ | 6 | smoothness and the normalization of the packets | 7–8 |

Six deltas ($2$ from $\mathbf b$, $4$ from the barred amplitude) do the six $\bar k$ integrals; the remaining six, over $\mathbf k_A$ and $\mathbf k_B$, collapse by the normalization of the packets (the user's notes, Ch. 15 §15.4, paragraph "Counting"); the $\delta^4$ of the unbarred amplitude is never used up.

> [!caution] Caution: The conjugated profiles on slide 13
> Slide 13 writes the factors of the second copy as $\phi(\bar k_A)$, $\phi(\bar k_B)$. They come from the complex conjugate of the amplitude and are $\phi_A^\ast(\bar{\mathbf k}_A)$, $\phi_B^\ast(\bar{\mathbf k}_B)$ (the phase $e^{+i\mathbf b\cdot\bar{\mathbf k}_B}$ in the slide's exponent is conjugated correctly). With the conjugates the smoothness step produces $|\phi_A(\mathbf k_A)|^2|\phi_B(\mathbf k_B)|^2$, as slide 15 says.
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slides 13, 15 (page images) · PS §4.5, eq. (4.76) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.4, eq. (dsigmafull)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.13.5: The Longitudinal Integrals Give the Velocity Difference
> Fix the transverse momenta, let $P^z$ and $E$ be fixed, and write $\bar E_A = \sqrt{\bar{\mathbf k}_A^2 + m_A^2}$, $\bar E_B = \sqrt{\bar{\mathbf k}_B^2 + m_B^2}$. For a smooth $g$, if the constraints have simple zeros,
>
> $$
> \int d\bar k_A^z\,d\bar k_B^z\;\delta\bigl(\bar k_A^z + \bar k_B^z - P^z\bigr)\,\delta\bigl(\bar E_A + \bar E_B - E\bigr)\,g = \sum_{\text{zeros}}\frac{g}{\bigl|\bar k_A^z/\bar E_A - \bar k_B^z/\bar E_B\bigr|} = \sum_{\text{zeros}}\frac{g}{|\bar v_A^z - \bar v_B^z|} .
> $$
>
> At the zero $\bar{\mathbf k} = \mathbf k$ of collinear beams along $z$ the factor is $1/|\mathbf v_A - \mathbf v_B|$.
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 14 ("$= \frac{1}{|\frac{\bar k_A^z}{E_A} - \frac{\bar k_B^z}{E_B}|} = \frac{1}{|\vec v_A - \vec v_B|}$ … Final equality: $\vec k = \gamma m\vec v$, $E = \gamma m$"), and transcript · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.4, eq. (jacobian), paragraphs "Integrating over $\bar k_B^z$ instead", "A second zero (filled in)" · PS §4.5, eq. (4.77)*

^thm-c6-13-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the linear delta). $\delta(\bar k_A^z + \bar k_B^z - P^z)$ does the $\bar k_B^z$ integral with Jacobian 1 ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-6|Theorem §CA.2.6]], 1): "$\vec{\bar k}_B$ as determined by momentum conservation" (slide 14),
>
> $$
> \int d\bar k_B^z\;\delta\bigl(\bar k_B^z - (P^z - \bar k_A^z)\bigr)\,\delta\bigl(\bar E_A + \bar E_B - E\bigr)\,g = \delta\bigl(\bar E_A + \bar E_B - E\bigr)\,g\Big|_{\bar k_B^z = P^z - \bar k_A^z} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the remaining delta). What is left is $\int d\bar k_A^z\,\delta(f(\bar k_A^z))\,g$ with
>
> $$
> f(x) = \sqrt{\bar{\mathbf k}_A^{\perp2} + x^2 + m_A^2} + \sqrt{\bar{\mathbf k}_B^{\perp2} + (P^z - x)^2 + m_B^2} - E ,
> $$
>
> "quite a nonlinear function": both square roots depend on $x = \bar k_A^z$, the second through $\bar k_B^z = P^z - x$.
>
> **Step 3** (the derivative). $\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{c + x^2} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{c + x^2}}$; for the second root, with $c' = \bar{\mathbf k}_B^{\perp2} + m_B^2$ and the chain rule, $\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{c' + (P^z - x)^2} = \frac{(P^z - x)}{\sqrt{c' + (P^z - x)^2}}\cdot\frac{d(P^z - x)}{dx} = -\frac{\bar k_B^z}{\bar E_B}$. So
>
> $$
> f'(x) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\bar{\mathbf k}_A^{\perp2} + x^2 + m_A^2}} - \frac{P^z - x}{\sqrt{\bar{\mathbf k}_B^{\perp2} + (P^z - x)^2 + m_B^2}} = \frac{\bar k_A^z}{\bar E_A} - \frac{\bar k_B^z}{\bar E_B} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (δ of a function). By [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-6|Theorem §CA.2.6]], 2, over the simple zeros $x_0$ of $f$: "you should evaluate it with a one over $f'$, and then put it to zero."
>
> $$
> \int dx\;\delta\bigl(f(x)\bigr)\,g(x) = \int dx\sum_{x_0}\frac{\delta(x - x_0)}{|f'(x_0)|}\,g(x) = \sum_{x_0}\frac{g(x_0)}{|f'(x_0)|} = \sum_{x_0}\frac{g(x_0)}{\bigl|\bar k_A^z/\bar E_A - \bar k_B^z/\bar E_B\bigr|_{x_0}} .
> $$
>
> The absolute value is the orientation of the substitution $u = f(x)$; $f'$ is negative whenever $B$ moves faster in the $+z$ direction than $A$.
>
> **Step 5** (velocities). For a particle of mass $m$, $\mathbf k = \gamma m\mathbf v$ and $E = \gamma m$, so "once you take the ratio, it's just the velocity":
>
> $$
> \frac{\mathbf k}{E} = \frac{\gamma m\mathbf v}{\gamma m} = \mathbf v , \qquad |f'| = \bigl|\bar v_A^z - \bar v_B^z\bigr| .
> $$
>
> At the zero $\bar{\mathbf k} = \mathbf k$ with both momenta along $z$, $\mathbf v_A^\perp = \mathbf v_B^\perp = 0$ and $|\mathbf v_A - \mathbf v_B| = \sqrt{0^2 + 0^2 + (v_A^z - v_B^z)^2} = |v_A^z - v_B^z|$.
>
> ⚑ By-product: with the transverse momenta fixed, conservation of $k^z$ and of energy for two particles has in general *two* solutions $\bar k_A^z$: $\bar k_A^z = k_A^z$, and a "reflected" configuration ($A$ and $B$ swapped in direction). In the centre-of-mass frame with $\bar{\mathbf k}^\perp = 0$, $P^z = 0$ and
>
> $$
> f(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + m_A^2} + \sqrt{x^2 + m_B^2} - E = f(-x) ,
> $$
>
> so $f(k_A^z) = 0$ implies $f(-k_A^z) = 0$: the second root is $\bar k_A^z = -k_A^z$. The reflected root lies far from the peak of $\phi_A$, so $\phi_A^\ast(\bar{\mathbf k}_A) \approx 0$ there and it does not contribute for sharply peaked packets (numerical check in the user's notes and `qft-C/tools/l15/checks.py`: $m_A = 1$, $m_B = 1.7$, $\mathbf k_A = (0.3, -0.2, 1.1)$, $\mathbf k_B = (0.1, 0.25, -0.8)$ give the roots $1.1$ and $-0.847$, and $1/|f'| = 1/|v_A - v_B| = 0.8767$ at the first).
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The only non-trivial step of the whole computation is one Jacobian, and it is the kinematic velocity difference of the two beams: "you should not actually be shocked that that is part of the kinematics of doing scattering."
> - Integrating over $\bar k_B^z$ instead, eliminating $\bar k_A^z$, gives $-f'$ and, with the absolute value, the same result.

^thm-c6-13-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Start from the 14-fold integral above (the definitions inserted, the square written as the product of the amplitude and its conjugate). The integrals are exchanged freely: the packets are smooth and rapidly decreasing, and every delta function is used against them (the physicists' route of [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-6|Theorem §CA.3.6]]).
>
> **Step 1** (the impact parameter; slide 13, item 1). $\mathbf b$ has only transverse components, so $\mathbf b\cdot(\mathbf k_B - \bar{\mathbf k}_B) = \mathbf b\cdot(\mathbf k_B^\perp - \bar{\mathbf k}_B^\perp)$ and, by [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-3|Theorem §CA.3.3]] in two dimensions,
>
> $$
> \int d^2b\;e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot(\mathbf k_B - \bar{\mathbf k}_B)} = (2\pi)^2\,\delta^2\bigl(\mathbf k_B^\perp - \bar{\mathbf k}_B^\perp\bigr) .
> $$
>
> "Because the $A$ particle and the $B$ particle were shifted by $b$ relative to each other … since we integrate all $b$, [the two copies] have to be the same … but only in the transverse directions."
>
> **Step 2** (the amplitudes; slide 13, item 2). The final states differ from the initial one, so only $i\hat T$ contributes ([[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^lem-c6-13-4|Lemma §C6.13.4]]), and by [[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^def-c6-12-3|Def. §C6.12.3]]
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf p_1\cdots|\mathbf k_A\mathbf k_B\rangle = i\mathcal M\,(2\pi)^4\delta^4\Bigl(k_A + k_B - \sum_ip_i\Bigr), \qquad \langle\mathbf p_1\cdots|\bar{\mathbf k}_A\bar{\mathbf k}_B\rangle^\ast = -i\bar{\mathcal M}^\ast\,(2\pi)^4\delta^4\Bigl(\bar k_A + \bar k_B - \sum_ip_i\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> with $\mathcal M \equiv \mathcal M(k_A, k_B \to \{p_i\})$, $\bar{\mathcal M} \equiv \mathcal M(\bar k_A, \bar k_B \to \{p_i\})$; the delta function is real, and $(i)(-i) = 1$. All four momenta $k_A, k_B, \bar k_A, \bar k_B$ are on the mass shell, $k^0 = E_{\mathbf k}$. Write $P \equiv \sum_ip_i$ (so $P^0 = \sum_iE_i$), $d\Phi_f \equiv \prod_i\frac{d^3p_i}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{2E_i}$ and
>
> $$
> \Phi \equiv \frac{\phi_A(\mathbf k_A)}{\sqrt{2E_A}}\frac{\phi_B(\mathbf k_B)}{\sqrt{2E_B}} , \qquad \bar\Phi \equiv \frac{\phi_A^\ast(\bar{\mathbf k}_A)}{\sqrt{2\bar E_A}}\frac{\phi_B^\ast(\bar{\mathbf k}_B)}{\sqrt{2\bar E_B}} .
> $$
>
> Steps 1 and 2 inserted into the 14-fold integral:
>
> $$
> d\sigma = d\Phi_f\int\frac{d^3k_A\,d^3k_B}{(2\pi)^6}\int\frac{d^3\bar k_A\,d^3\bar k_B}{(2\pi)^6}\;(2\pi)^2\delta^2\bigl(\mathbf k_B^\perp - \bar{\mathbf k}_B^\perp\bigr)\;\Phi\,\bar\Phi\;\mathcal M\bar{\mathcal M}^\ast\;(2\pi)^4\delta^4(k_A + k_B - P)\;(2\pi)^4\delta^4(\bar k_A + \bar k_B - P) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the transverse $\bar k$ integrals; slide 14, first bullet). Split the barred delta function by components,
>
> $$
> (2\pi)^4\delta^4(\bar k_A + \bar k_B - P) = (2\pi)^2\delta^2\bigl(\bar{\mathbf k}_A^\perp + \bar{\mathbf k}_B^\perp - \mathbf P^\perp\bigr)\cdot(2\pi)^2\delta\bigl(\bar k_A^z + \bar k_B^z - P^z\bigr)\,\delta\bigl(\bar E_A + \bar E_B - P^0\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> and write $d^3\bar k = d^2\bar k^\perp\,d\bar k^z$. The two transverse deltas do the four transverse integrals, $d^2\bar k_B^\perp$ first: for any smooth $G$,
>
> $$
> \int\frac{d^2\bar k_A^\perp}{(2\pi)^2}\frac{d^2\bar k_B^\perp}{(2\pi)^2}\;(2\pi)^2\delta^2\bigl(\mathbf k_B^\perp - \bar{\mathbf k}_B^\perp\bigr)\,(2\pi)^2\delta^2\bigl(\bar{\mathbf k}_A^\perp + \bar{\mathbf k}_B^\perp - \mathbf P^\perp\bigr)\,G = \int\frac{d^2\bar k_A^\perp}{(2\pi)^2}(2\pi)^2\delta^2\bigl(\bar{\mathbf k}_A^\perp + \mathbf k_B^\perp - \mathbf P^\perp\bigr)\,G\Big|_{\bar{\mathbf k}_B^\perp = \mathbf k_B^\perp} = G\Big|_{\bar{\mathbf k}_B^\perp = \mathbf k_B^\perp,\ \bar{\mathbf k}_A^\perp = \mathbf P^\perp - \mathbf k_B^\perp} .
> $$
>
> Factors of $2\pi$: $(2\pi)^{-2-2+2+2} = 1$.
>
> **Step 4** (the longitudinal $\bar k$ integrals; slide 14). What remains of the barred delta function, with the last two barred measures, is [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^thm-c6-13-5|Theorem §C6.13.5]] with $E = P^0$:
>
> $$
> \int\frac{d\bar k_A^z\,d\bar k_B^z}{(2\pi)^2}\;(2\pi)^2\delta\bigl(\bar k_A^z + \bar k_B^z - P^z\bigr)\,\delta\bigl(\bar E_A + \bar E_B - P^0\bigr)\,H = \frac{H}{\bigl|\bar v_A^z - \bar v_B^z\bigr|}\bigg|_{\text{contributing zero}} ,
> $$
>
> $(2\pi)^{-2+2} = 1$. All six $\bar k$ integrals are done, and no factor $2\pi$ is left from them.
>
> **Step 5** (the barred momenta equal the unbarred ones; slide 15). On the support of the integrand three sets of equations hold:
>
> $$
> \text{(i)}\ k_A + k_B = P\ \ \text{(kept }\delta^4\text{)}, \qquad \text{(ii)}\ \bar k_A + \bar k_B = P\ \ \text{(Steps 3–4)}, \qquad \text{(iii)}\ \bar{\mathbf k}_B^\perp = \mathbf k_B^\perp\ \ \text{(Step 3)} .
> $$
>
> (i) and (ii) give $\bar k_A + \bar k_B = k_A + k_B$; its transverse part with (iii) gives $\bar{\mathbf k}_A^\perp = \mathbf k_A^\perp$. Its $z$ and energy parts,
>
> $$
> \bar k_A^z + \bar k_B^z = k_A^z + k_B^z , \qquad \sqrt{\mathbf k_A^{\perp2} + (\bar k_A^z)^2 + m_A^2} + \sqrt{\mathbf k_B^{\perp2} + (\bar k_B^z)^2 + m_B^2} = E_A + E_B ,
> $$
>
> are the two equations that $(k_A^z, k_B^z)$ itself satisfies; their contributing solution is $\bar k_A^z = k_A^z$, $\bar k_B^z = k_B^z$ (the reflected zero is suppressed, ⚑ of [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^der-c6-13-5|Derivation §C6.13.5]]). Hence "simply take $\bar k_A = k_A$, $\bar k_B = k_B$":
>
> $$
> \bar{\mathbf k}_A = \mathbf k_A , \quad \bar{\mathbf k}_B = \mathbf k_B , \quad \bar E_{A,B} = E_{A,B} , \quad \bar v^z_{A,B} = v^z_{A,B} , \quad \bar{\mathcal M} = \mathcal M .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (collect). At $\bar k = k$,
>
> $$
> \Phi\,\bar\Phi = \frac{\phi_A(\mathbf k_A)\phi_A^\ast(\mathbf k_A)\,\phi_B(\mathbf k_B)\phi_B^\ast(\mathbf k_B)}{\sqrt{2E_A\,2E_B\,2E_A\,2E_B}} = \frac{|\phi_A(\mathbf k_A)|^2|\phi_B(\mathbf k_B)|^2}{2E_A\,2E_B} , \qquad \mathcal M\bar{\mathcal M}^\ast = |\mathcal M(k_A, k_B \to \{p_i\})|^2
> $$
>
> (PS eq. (4.78)), and with Steps 3–4 the cross section is
>
> $$
> d\sigma = d\Phi_f\int\frac{d^3k_A}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{d^3k_B}{(2\pi)^3}\;|\phi_A(\mathbf k_A)|^2|\phi_B(\mathbf k_B)|^2\;F(k_A, k_B) , \qquad F(k_A, k_B) \equiv \frac{|\mathcal M(k_A, k_B \to \{p_i\})|^2}{2E_A\,2E_B\,|v_A^z - v_B^z|}\;(2\pi)^4\delta^4\Bigl(k_A + k_B - \sum_ip_i\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 7** (smoothness; slide 15). $|\phi_A(\mathbf k_A)|^2$ is smooth and sharply peaked at $\mathbf p_A$, $|\phi_B(\mathbf k_B)|^2$ at $\mathbf p_B$, while $\mathcal M$, the energies and the velocities in $F$ vary slowly on the scale of the packet widths. So "evaluate all other factors at $p_A$, $p_B$ rather than $k_A$, $k_B$":
>
> $$
> \int\frac{d^3k_A}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{d^3k_B}{(2\pi)^3}\;|\phi_A(\mathbf k_A)|^2|\phi_B(\mathbf k_B)|^2\,F(k_A, k_B) \simeq F(p_A, p_B)\int\frac{d^3k_A}{(2\pi)^3}|\phi_A(\mathbf k_A)|^2\int\frac{d^3k_B}{(2\pi)^3}|\phi_B(\mathbf k_B)|^2 .
> $$
>
> ⚑ By-product: the same is done inside the remaining delta function, $\delta^4(k_A + k_B - \sum p_i) \to \delta^4(p_A + p_B - \sum p_i)$; this assumes that the final-state acceptance is smooth on the scale of the packet widths, as for any real detector, which does not resolve the momentum spread of the initial packets (PS p. 106).
>
> **Step 8** (normalization; slide 15, last bullet). By [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-3|Def. §C6.13.3]] each remaining integral is 1, so the right side of Step 7 is $F(p_A, p_B)\cdot1\cdot1$. "It completely collapses, and it's because of smoothness."
>
> **Step 9** (read off). With beams along $z$, $|v_A^z - v_B^z| = |\mathbf v_A - \mathbf v_B|$ ([[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^der-c6-13-5|Derivation §C6.13.5]], Step 5), and
>
> $$
> d\sigma = d\Phi_f\,F(p_A, p_B) = \frac{1}{2E_A\,2E_B\,|\mathbf v_A - \mathbf v_B|}\,|\mathcal M(p_A, p_B \to \{p_i\})|^2\,\Bigl(\prod_i\frac{d^3p_i}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{2E_i}\Bigr)(2\pi)^4\delta^4\Bigl(p_A + p_B - \sum_ip_i\Bigr) :
> $$
>
> $\frac{1}{2E_A2E_B}$ from the packet factors, the Jacobian, $|\mathcal M|^2$, the final-state measure, and "one of the two delta functions … still there". That is the statement.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Of the two $\delta^4$ of the squared amplitude, one is used up on the packets (steps 3–4) and the other becomes part of the final phase space: no $\delta^4(0)$ ever appears.
> - The details of the packets drop out "entirely for free"; only their normalization and their narrowness were used.
> - The assumptions: narrow packets, smooth $\mathcal M$, forward scattering excluded, a smooth detector acceptance, collinear beams.

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the formula
> "Longest formula, not most important." The first factor is the kinematics of the initial state, the energies and the velocity difference of what came in; "it's actually the same kinematics that appears" in mechanics books, classical or non-relativistic, and "has nothing to do with quantum field [theory]". The last factor, the $n$-particle relativistic phase space ([[§C6.14 Lorentz Invariance and Two-Body Phase Space#^def-c6-14-1|Def. §C6.14.1]]), is the kinematics of the final state at fixed total momentum; which features of the final state are measured and which are integrated over "is a separate story". The middle factor is the only place the theory enters: "morally speaking, if you know $M$, it's the thing that you should square and get the probability, because you take your initial state, you expand [it] on a complete set of final states, take the coefficient … and you square it, and then you sum over all the possible final states that you're kind of willing to accept." From now on the work is to compute $\mathcal M$; the conversion to $d\sigma$ is "let me just look it up".
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 16, and transcript · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.5 (paragraph "Reading the formula")*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.13.7: The Cross Section Is an Area for Every n
> With $N = 2 + n$ external particles, $[d\sigma] = -2$ for every $n$: the dimension of an area.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.5, Derivation "Dimension check (filled in)"*

^thm-c6-13-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the amplitude). By [[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^thm-c6-12-3|Theorem §C6.12.3]] with $N = 2 + n$,
>
> $$
> [\mathcal M] = 4 - (2 + n) = 2 - n , \qquad [|\mathcal M|^2] = 2(2 - n) = 4 - 2n .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (phase space). With $[d^3p] = 3$, $[E] = 1$, $[\delta^4] = -4$ and $(2\pi)$'s dimensionless,
>
> $$
> \Bigl[\prod_{i=1}^n\frac{d^3p_i}{(2\pi)^32E_i}\,(2\pi)^4\delta^4\Bigr] = n(3 - 1) + (-4) = 2n - 4 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (flux). Velocities are dimensionless, so
>
> $$
> \Bigl[\frac{1}{2E_A\,2E_B\,|\mathbf v_A - \mathbf v_B|}\Bigr] = -(1 + 1 + 0) = -2 .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (sum).
>
> $$
> [d\sigma] = -2 + (4 - 2n) + (2n - 4) = -2 .
> $$
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The growth of $|\mathcal M|^2$'s dimension with $n$ is exactly compensated by the phase space; checked for $n = 1, \dots, 5$ in `qft-C/tools/l15/checks.py`.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.13.8: Identical Particles in the Final State
> [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^thm-c6-13-6|Theorem §C6.13.6]] holds as it stands whether or not the final state contains identical particles. If it contains $n_k$ identical particles of species $k$, integrating it over *all* final momenta counts every physical final state $S_{\rm id} = \prod_kn_k!$ times, and the total cross section is
>
> $$
> \sigma = \frac{1}{S_{\rm id}}\int d\sigma .
> $$
>
> At a given point of phase space (e.g. $d\sigma/d\Omega$ at a given angle) there is no factor.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.5 (paragraph "Identical particles (filled in)") · PS §4.5, p. 108 ("divide by $n!$ after integrating over all sets of momenta") · Yu §6.5.1, eqs. (6.305)–(6.306); §6.5.2, eq. (6.321)*

^thm-c6-13-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the states are symmetric). For identical bosons the creation operators commute ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-2|Theorem §C2a.4.2]]), so for every permutation $\pi \in S_n$
>
> $$
> |\mathbf p_{\pi(1)}\cdots\mathbf p_{\pi(n)}\rangle = \sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p_1}}\cdots\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p_n}}\;\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_{\pi(1)}}\cdots\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_{\pi(n)}}|0\rangle = |\mathbf p_1\cdots\mathbf p_n\rangle .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the resolution of the identity). Take $n$ identical particles, the sector of the Fock space they span, and distinct momenta $\mathbf q_1, \dots, \mathbf q_n$. For $n = 2$, [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-2|Theorem §C2a.4.2]] with the factors $\sqrt{2E}$ (which agree on the support of the deltas) gives
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2|\mathbf q_1\mathbf q_2\rangle = 2E_{\mathbf q_1}2E_{\mathbf q_2}(2\pi)^6\bigl[\delta^3(\mathbf p_1 - \mathbf q_1)\delta^3(\mathbf p_2 - \mathbf q_2) + \delta^3(\mathbf p_1 - \mathbf q_2)\delta^3(\mathbf p_2 - \mathbf q_1)\bigr] ,
> $$
>
> so, each delta doing one integral and $|\mathbf q_2\mathbf q_1\rangle = |\mathbf q_1\mathbf q_2\rangle$ by Step 1,
>
> $$
> \int\frac{d^3p_1}{(2\pi)^32E_{\mathbf p_1}}\frac{d^3p_2}{(2\pi)^32E_{\mathbf p_2}}\;|\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\rangle\langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2|\mathbf q_1\mathbf q_2\rangle = |\mathbf q_1\mathbf q_2\rangle + |\mathbf q_2\mathbf q_1\rangle = 2\,|\mathbf q_1\mathbf q_2\rangle .
> $$
>
> For general $n$ the commutation relations give $\langle\mathbf p_1\cdots\mathbf p_n|\mathbf q_1\cdots\mathbf q_n\rangle = \sum_{\pi\in S_n}\prod_j2E_{\mathbf q_{\pi(j)}}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p_j - \mathbf q_{\pi(j)})$, one term per way of matching the $\mathbf p$'s to the $\mathbf q$'s, $n!$ terms. So
>
> $$
> \int\prod_j\frac{d^3p_j}{(2\pi)^32E_{\mathbf p_j}}\;|\mathbf p_1\cdots\mathbf p_n\rangle\langle\mathbf p_1\cdots\mathbf p_n|\mathbf q_1\cdots\mathbf q_n\rangle = \sum_{\pi\in S_n}|\mathbf q_{\pi(1)}\cdots\mathbf q_{\pi(n)}\rangle = n!\,|\mathbf q_1\cdots\mathbf q_n\rangle ,
> $$
>
> each delta function doing one integral and, by step 1, every permuted state equal to $|\mathbf q_1\cdots\mathbf q_n\rangle$. The identity on the sector is therefore $\frac{1}{n!}\int\prod_j\frac{d^3p_j}{(2\pi)^32E_{\mathbf p_j}}|\mathbf p_1\cdots\rangle\langle\mathbf p_1\cdots|$ (coincident momenta have measure zero).
>
> **Step 3** (the total probability). For the scattered state $|\psi\rangle$, the probability of ending in this sector is, with $\mathbb 1_n$ of Step 2 and $\mathcal P$ of [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-5|Def. §C6.13.5]],
>
> $$
> \langle\psi|\mathbb 1_n|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{n!}\int\prod_j\frac{d^3p_j}{(2\pi)^32E_{\mathbf p_j}}\;\bigl|\langle\mathbf p_1\cdots\mathbf p_n|\psi\rangle\bigr|^2 = \frac{1}{n!}\int\mathcal P .
> $$
>
> For several species the identity on the sector is the product of the factors, $\prod_k\frac{1}{n_k!} = \frac{1}{S_{\rm id}}$. The integrated cross section inherits the factor through [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-6|Def. §C6.13.6]]: $\sigma = \frac{1}{S_{\rm id}}\int d\sigma$.
>
> **Step 4** (no factor locally). In a small region $R$ of phase space around distinct momenta, the permuted region $\pi R$ ($\pi \ne \mathrm{id}$) is disjoint from $R$, so no two points of $R$ describe the same state (Step 1), and $d\sigma$ there is the probability per unit flux without correction.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The factor comes from counting final *states*, not from the amplitude: the amplitude for identical particles is computed separately and is already symmetric.
> - For two identical particles at $2 \to 2$ in the centre-of-mass frame, $\mathbf p_2 = -\mathbf p_1$, and the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf n}(\theta, \varphi) = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi, \sin\theta\sin\varphi, \cos\theta)$ satisfies $-\hat{\mathbf n}(\theta, \varphi) = \hat{\mathbf n}(\pi - \theta, \varphi + \pi)$; so $|\mathbf p\,\hat{\mathbf n}, -\mathbf p\,\hat{\mathbf n}\rangle = |-\mathbf p\,\hat{\mathbf n}, \mathbf p\,\hat{\mathbf n}\rangle$ (Step 1): the directions $(\theta, \varphi)$ and $(\pi - \theta, \varphi + \pi)$ of $\mathbf p_1$ are the same state, integrating over the full sphere counts it twice, and $S_{\rm id} = 2$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.13.9: The Scattering Probabilities Add Up to One
> Let $|\psi\rangle$ be a normalized state and assume the out states are complete. Summing over the particle contents of the final state, with $S_{\rm id}$ of [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^thm-c6-13-8|Theorem §C6.13.8]] for each content,
>
> $$
> \sum_{\text{contents}}\frac{1}{S_{\rm id}}\int\prod_i\frac{d^3p_i}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{2E_i}\;\bigl|{}_{\rm out}\langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|\psi\rangle\bigr|^2 = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1 .
> $$
>
> For the two-packet state of [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-4|Def. §C6.13.4]] with profiles peaked at $\mathbf p_A \ne \mathbf p_B$ (or $A \ne B$), $\langle\phi_A\phi_B|\phi_A\phi_B\rangle = 1$ for every $\mathbf b$, so the $\mathcal P(\mathbf b)$ of [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-5|Def. §C6.13.5]], integrated over all final momenta and summed over contents with $\frac{1}{S_{\rm id}}$, is 1.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.3 (after eq. (Pb): "the completeness relation for relativistically normalized states") · PS §4.5, p. 103 · the identical-particle factor: PS p. 108, Yu §6.5.1, eq. (6.305)*

^thm-c6-13-9

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (completeness by sectors). The out states of different particle contents are orthogonal, and in the sector of a content with $S_{\rm id} = \prod_kn_k!$ the identity is ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-2|Def. §C2a.4.2]] for one particle; [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^der-c6-13-8|Derivation §C6.13.8]], Step 2, for $n$ identical ones)
>
> $$
> \mathbb 1_{\rm content} = \frac{1}{S_{\rm id}}\int\prod_i\frac{d^3p_i}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_i}\;|\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots\rangle_{\rm out}\,{}_{\rm out}\langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots| , \qquad \mathbb 1 = \sum_{\text{contents}}\mathbb 1_{\rm content} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (sandwich). $\langle\psi|\mathbb 1|\psi\rangle = \sum\langle\psi|\mathbb 1_{\rm content}|\psi\rangle$, and each term is
>
> $$
> \langle\psi|\mathbb 1_{\rm content}|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{S_{\rm id}}\int\prod_i\frac{d^3p_i}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_i}\;\langle\psi|\mathbf p_1\cdots\rangle_{\rm out}\,{}_{\rm out}\langle\mathbf p_1\cdots|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{S_{\rm id}}\int\prod_i\frac{d^3p_i}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_i}\;\bigl|{}_{\rm out}\langle\mathbf p_1\cdots|\psi\rangle\bigr|^2 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the two-packet norm). With $\Phi(\mathbf k_A, \mathbf k_B) = e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf k_B}\frac{\phi_A(\mathbf k_A)\phi_B(\mathbf k_B)}{\sqrt{2E_A2E_B}}$ and the two-particle overlap of [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-2|Theorem §C2a.4.2]] (factors $\sqrt{2E}$ of [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-1|Def. §C2a.4.1]]),
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \langle\phi_A\phi_B|\phi_A\phi_B\rangle ={}& \int\frac{d^3k_A\,d^3k_B\,d^3k_A'\,d^3k_B'}{(2\pi)^{12}}\;\Phi^\ast(\mathbf k_A, \mathbf k_B)\,\Phi(\mathbf k_A', \mathbf k_B')\;2E_A2E_B(2\pi)^6\bigl[\delta^3(\mathbf k_A - \mathbf k_A')\delta^3(\mathbf k_B - \mathbf k_B') + \delta^3(\mathbf k_A - \mathbf k_B')\delta^3(\mathbf k_B - \mathbf k_A')\bigr]\\
> ={}& \int\frac{d^3k_A}{(2\pi)^3}|\phi_A(\mathbf k_A)|^2\int\frac{d^3k_B}{(2\pi)^3}|\phi_B(\mathbf k_B)|^2 + \Bigl|\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf k}\phi_A^\ast(\mathbf k)\phi_B(\mathbf k)\Bigr|^2 = 1\cdot1 + 0 ,
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> the exchange term (present only for $A = B$) vanishing because $\phi_A$ and $\phi_B$ are peaked at different momenta and do not overlap. The phase $e^{-i\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf k_B}$ has modulus 1 in the direct term, so the norm is 1 for every $\mathbf b$.
>
> **Step 4** (read off). With $|\psi\rangle = |\phi_A\phi_B\rangle_{\rm in}$, Steps 2–3 and [[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^def-c6-13-5|Def. §C6.13.5]]: $\sum\frac{1}{S_{\rm id}}\int\mathcal P(\mathbf b) = 1$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - $\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^32E}$ is the measure that makes $\mathcal P$ a probability density; $\frac{1}{S_{\rm id}}$ is needed so that identical final particles are not counted $S_{\rm id}$ times.
> - The assumption is asymptotic completeness: every state is a superposition of out states.
