Lectures 13–14 built the Feynman rules for correlation functions $\langle\Omega|T\{\hat\phi_H(x_1)\cdots\hat\phi_H(x_n)\}|\Omega\rangle$ ([[§C6.11 The iε Limit and Vacuum Bubbles#^thm-c6-11-5|Theorem §C6.11.5]]). "It's completely the right idea. It's just that … that's not what you really want": nobody measures a correlation function. Lecture 15 asks what we do want to compute, and the answer of particle physics is the cross section and the decay rate. This section sets up the object they are made from. Particles that are widely separated early evolve into particles that are widely separated late; the linear map between the two is the $S$-matrix ([[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^def-c6-12-1|Def. §C6.12.1]]). Removing the part in which nothing happens and the overall delta function of four-momentum conservation leaves one function, the invariant amplitude $\mathcal M$ ([[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^def-c6-12-3|Def. §C6.12.3]]), the only place where the dynamics enters. The next sections turn $\mathcal M$ into cross sections ([[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets|§C6.13]], [[§C6.14 Lorentz Invariance and Two-Body Phase Space|§C6.14]]) and decay rates ([[§C6.15 Decay Rates and Widths|§C6.15]]), and [[§C6.16 Amplitudes from Correlation Functions|§C6.16]] states how $\mathcal M$ is read off from the correlation functions of Lecture 14. Yu treats the $S$-matrix in §6.2, eqs. (6.122)–(6.127), defining it as the interaction-picture evolution operator $U(+\infty, -\infty)$ between asymptotic states, and the split $S = \mathbb I + iT$ with the invariant amplitude in §6.5, eqs. (6.272)–(6.275), in the setting of the Dyson series of the $S$-operator.

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): $\hbar = c = 1$; states normalized relativistically, $\langle\mathbf p|\mathbf q\rangle = 2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-1|Def. §C2a.4.1]]); the $S$-matrix and the $T$-matrix are operators and carry hats, $\hat S$, $\hat T$ (the slides write $\mathcal S$, $\mathcal T$: Caution: Names for the S-matrix, the T-matrix and the amplitude, below); $\mathcal M$ is a number.

## The S-matrix

> [!definition] Definition §C6.12.1: The S-Matrix
> A general scattering process takes free initial particles, momenta $\mathbf p_A, \mathbf p_B, \dots$, to free final particles, momenta $\mathbf p_1, \mathbf p_2, \dots$; "free" means widely separated. The initial time is $-T$, the final time $+T$, with $T$ large and positive: the time evolution is over $\Delta t = 2T$. The amplitude of the process is
>
> $$
> {}_{\rm out}\langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|\mathbf p_A\mathbf p_B\cdots\rangle_{\rm in} = \lim_{T\to\infty}\langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|e^{-i\hat H\,2T}|\mathbf p_A\mathbf p_B\cdots\rangle \equiv \langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|\hat S|\mathbf p_A\mathbf p_B\cdots\rangle ,
> $$
>
> with $\hat H$ the full Hamiltonian. The **$S$-matrix** $\hat S$ is the map between free particles early and late, a linear operator.
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 4 ("A general scattering process: Free initial particles to free final particles … 'Initial' time $= -T$, 'final' time $+T$. $T$ large and positive. Time evolution over $\Delta t = 2T$ … The $\mathcal S$-matrix = map between free particles early and late. Comment: 'matrix' is a historical term = linear operator"), and transcript ("what I mean by free is not even necessarily that … the guys were created by a dagger … it's more just that they started out being widely separated") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.1, eq. (Smatrixdef) · PS §4.5, eqs. (4.70)–(4.71) · Yu §6.2, eqs. (6.123)–(6.124)*

^def-c6-12-1

> [!caution] Caution: "Free" means widely separated, not created by $\hat a^\dagger$ from the free vacuum
> The in and out states of [[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^def-c6-12-1|Def. §C6.12.1]] are states of the *interacting* theory that look like free, widely separated particles at $-T$ (in) or at $+T$ (out): "they don't know each other, and then they come together." They are eigenstates of $\hat H$, not of $\hat H_0$, and "in" and "out" states with the same labels are different states, "because there's been all this time evolution". PS say the same: $|\mathbf k\rangle$ in their wave packet (4.65) "is a one-particle state of momentum $\mathbf k$ in the interacting theory", and in the free theory one would have $|\mathbf k\rangle = \sqrt{2E_{\mathbf k}}\,\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf k}|0\rangle$. Relating these states to the free states $\hat a^\dagger\cdots|0\rangle$ is the same problem that the $T \to \infty(1 - i\varepsilon)$ limit solved for the vacuum ([[§C6.4 The Interacting Vacuum and the Two-Point Function#^thm-c6-4-1|Theorem §C6.4.1]]); for particle states it is solved by the LSZ formula (Lecture 16 and QFT C10, planned).
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, transcript · PS §4.5, p. 102 (after eq. (4.65)), p. 108 (eqs. (4.87)–(4.88)) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.1*

^cau-c6-12-1

> [!remark] Remark: "Matrix" is a historical term
> "It's back when people … consider[ed] matrices advanced math … Linear operators, they are matrices … We should call it a linear operator. That's really what we mean." Its matrix elements between basis states, $S_{fi} = \langle f|\hat S|i\rangle$, are what Yu arranges into a matrix with $f$ as the row index and $i$ as the column index (eq. (6.123)). The letter is Heisenberg's (*Streumatrix*, scattering matrix).
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 4, and transcript · Yu §6.2, after eq. (6.123) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.1*

^rem-c6-12-1

> [!remark] Remark: The fundamental observable?
> "If I give you the input, you compute the output. It's sort of what the theory does": the $S$-matrix describes all possible scattering processes, and slide 5 calls it "the fundamental observable in quantum field theory". The lecture qualified this at once: it is "a justifiable answer because there are other justifiable answers". In condensed matter physics one rarely scatters particles; one has a material in some quantum state, "wiggles" it and asks how it responds. Those observables are **response functions**, Green's functions, not the $S$-matrix, so calling the $S$-matrix *the* observable is "a little bit prejudiced", though natural in high-energy physics. The correlation functions of Lecture 14 are the Green's functions of that other point of view ([[§C6.4 The Interacting Vacuum and the Two-Point Function#^thm-c6-4-7|Theorem §C6.4.7]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 5, and transcript · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.1 (paragraph "The fundamental observable?")*

^rem-c6-12-2

The interaction picture of Lecture 13 gives a second expression for the same matrix elements, between free states, which is Yu's starting point.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.12.1: S-Matrix Elements and the Interaction-Picture Evolution Operator
> Let $|i\rangle$, $|f\rangle$ be eigenstates of $\hat H_0$ with energies $E_i$, $E_f$, and let $U(t, t')$ be the evolution operator of [[§C6.3 The Time-Evolution Operator and Time Ordering#^def-c6-3-2|Def. §C6.3.2]]. Then
>
> $$
> \langle f|U(T, -T)|i\rangle = e^{i(E_f + E_i)T}\,\langle f|e^{-i\hat H\,2T}|i\rangle :
> $$
>
> between eigenstates of $\hat H_0$ the two matrix elements differ by a phase, which drops out of every probability.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.1 (paragraph "Relation to Chapter 13 (filled in)") · Yu §6.2, eqs. (6.122)–(6.125) (the $S$-operator $S = U(+\infty, -\infty)$)*

^thm-c6-12-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the definition at $t = T$, $t' = -T$). [[§C6.3 The Time-Evolution Operator and Time Ordering#^def-c6-3-2|Def. §C6.3.2]] with $t - t' = 2T$ gives
>
> $$
> U(T, -T) = e^{i\hat H_0T}\,e^{-i\hat H\,2T}\,e^{-i\hat H_0(-T)} = e^{i\hat H_0T}\,e^{-i\hat H\,2T}\,e^{i\hat H_0T} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (act on the eigenstates). $e^{i\hat H_0T}|i\rangle = e^{iE_iT}|i\rangle$, and, taking the adjoint of $e^{-i\hat H_0T}|f\rangle = e^{-iE_fT}|f\rangle$, $\langle f|e^{i\hat H_0T} = e^{iE_fT}\langle f|$ ($E_f$ real).
>
> **Step 3** (combine). $\langle f|U(T, -T)|i\rangle = e^{iE_fT}e^{iE_iT}\langle f|e^{-i\hat H\,2T}|i\rangle$. The prefactor has modulus 1.
>
> ⚑ By-product: with $T \to \infty$ this suggests $\hat S = U(+\infty, -\infty) = T\{\exp[-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,\hat H_I(t)]\}$ ([[§C6.3 The Time-Evolution Operator and Time Ordering#^thm-c6-3-5|Theorem §C6.3.5]]), Yu's definition (eqs. (6.125)–(6.126)), which makes Dyson's series a series for $\hat S$. It is heuristic: the phase oscillates without a limit as $T \to \infty$, and the true in and out states are eigenstates of $\hat H$, not $\hat H_0$ (Caution: "Free" means widely separated, above). → Remark: Yu's route, below.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Between free states, the $S$-matrix of the course and Yu's $U(+\infty, -\infty)$ agree up to a phase.
> - What the phase hides, the difference between free and interacting asymptotic states, is the subject of LSZ (Lecture 16; QFT C10, planned).

## S = 1 + iT and the amplitude

> [!definition] Definition §C6.12.2: The T-Matrix
> If in = out, nothing happened; if in ≠ out, the interactions did something. The two outcomes are separated by removing the "1":
>
> $$
> \hat S = \mathbb 1 + i\hat T .
> $$
>
> $\mathbb 1$ is the "nothing happened" part and the **$T$-matrix** $\hat T$ the "stuff happened" part.
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 6 ("in = out ⇒ nothing happens ⇒ remove '1': $\mathcal S = 1 + i\mathcal T$; in ≠ out ⇒ interactions ⇒ $i\mathcal T$"), and transcript ("one is that sometimes maybe literally nothing happens … the $T$ is the stuff happened part") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.1, eq. (SoneiT) · PS §4.5, eq. (4.72) · Yu §6.5, eqs. (6.272)–(6.274)*

^def-c6-12-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.12.2: The S-Matrix Conserves Four-Momentum
> Assume the theory is translation invariant: the four-momentum $\hat P^\mu = (\hat H, \hat{\mathbf P})$ satisfies $[\hat H, \hat P^\mu] = 0$, and the in and out states are eigenstates of $\hat P^\mu$ with eigenvalues $p_i = \sum_{A, B, \dots}p$ and $p_f = \sum_{1, 2, \dots}p$. Then $[\hat S, \hat P^\mu] = 0$, and every matrix element of $i\hat T$ between such states, as a distribution in the momenta, is of the form
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|i\hat T|\mathbf p_A\mathbf p_B\cdots\rangle = \delta^4(p_i - p_f)\;C(p_A, p_B, \dots; p_1, p_2, \dots) ,
> $$
>
> with a coefficient $C$ assumed smooth in the momenta on the surface $p_i = p_f$.
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 6 ("$\mathcal T$ conserves energy-momentum so remove $\delta$-function"), and transcript ("this is a relativistic theory, and therefore, the final outcome has the same total four momentum as the incoming … this whole thing here will be proportional to a delta function") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.1, Derivation "Three properties of $\mathcal M$ (filled in)", first paragraph · PS §4.5, p. 104 · stated as a distribution here*

^thm-c6-12-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** ($\hat S$ commutes with $\hat P^\mu$). $[\hat H, \hat P^\mu] = 0$ gives $[\hat H^k, \hat P^\mu] = 0$ for every power, hence $e^{-i\hat H\,2T}\hat P^\mu = \hat P^\mu e^{-i\hat H\,2T}$ for every $T$, and in the limit $\hat S\hat P^\mu = \hat P^\mu\hat S$ (matrix elements between the states of [[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^def-c6-12-1|Def. §C6.12.1]]). $\hat P^\mu$ is conserved because the theory is invariant under translations in space and time (Noether, [[§C1b.7 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum|§C1b.7]]; the generator form [[§C3.5 Covariant Quantum Fields and the Free Scalar's Representation#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]]).
>
> **Step 2** (matrix elements). Sandwich $\hat S\hat P^\mu - \hat P^\mu\hat S = 0$ between $\langle f|$ and $|i\rangle$, using $\hat P^\mu|i\rangle = p_i^\mu|i\rangle$ and $\langle f|\hat P^\mu = p_f^\mu\langle f|$ ($\hat P^\mu$ self-adjoint, eigenvalues real):
>
> $$
> (p_i - p_f)^\mu\,\langle f|\hat S|i\rangle = 0 \qquad (\mu = 0, 1, 2, 3) .
> $$
>
> The same holds for $\langle f|\mathbb 1|i\rangle$ ($\mathbb 1$ commutes with everything), hence for $\langle f|i\hat T|i\rangle = \langle f|\hat S|i\rangle - \langle f|i\rangle$.
>
> **Step 3** (support). Change variables from the momenta to $\Delta = p_i - p_f$ and the remaining independent momenta (a linear change of variables with constant Jacobian, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-6|Theorem §CA.2.6]], 1). By step 2, $\Delta^\mu\,\langle f|i\hat T|i\rangle = 0$ for every $\mu$: as a distribution in $\Delta$ it vanishes against every test function supported away from $\Delta = 0$, so its support is $\{\Delta = 0\}$.
>
> **Step 4** (only δ itself survives). A distribution supported at $\Delta = 0$ is a finite sum $\sum_\alpha c_\alpha\,\partial^\alpha\delta^4(\Delta)$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-2|Theorem §CA.2.2]]; the remaining momenta are parameters). Multiplying by $\Delta^\mu$: $\Delta^\mu\delta^4(\Delta) = 0$ and $\Delta^\mu\,\partial_\mu\delta^4(\Delta) = -\delta^4(\Delta)$ (no sum; integrate by parts, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-3|Theorem §CA.2.3]]), and in general $\Delta^\mu\partial^\alpha\delta = -\alpha_\mu\,\partial^{\alpha - e_\mu}\delta$. Taking the highest order $|\alpha| = N \ge 1$ present and a $\mu$ with $\alpha_\mu \ge 1$, $\Delta^\mu$ times the sum contains $-\alpha_\mu c_\alpha\partial^{\alpha - e_\mu}\delta$, which nothing else cancels; so $c_\alpha = 0$ for all $|\alpha| \ge 1$, and only $c_0\,\delta^4(\Delta)$ remains. Its coefficient $C$ depends on the remaining momenta; that it is smooth there is the assumption in the statement.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The delta function is forced by symmetry alone, "before we even discuss what theory we're talking about"; only its coefficient knows the dynamics.
> - The assumption that $C$ is smooth excludes further delta functions, which come from parts of the process in which some particles do not interact at all (disconnected pieces, [[§C6.16 Amplitudes from Correlation Functions|§C6.16]]).

> [!definition] Definition §C6.12.3: The Invariant Amplitude
> Since $\hat T$ conserves energy-momentum ([[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^thm-c6-12-2|Theorem §C6.12.2]]), the delta function is removed:
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\cdots|i\hat T|\mathbf p_A\mathbf p_B\cdots\rangle = (2\pi)^4\delta^4\Bigl(\sum_{A, B, \dots}p - \sum_{1, 2, \dots}p\Bigr)\;i\mathcal M(p_A, p_B, \dots \to p_1, p_2, \dots) .
> $$
>
> The **amplitude** $\mathcal M$, the "invariant matrix element", is dynamical data.
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 6 ("$\langle\vec p_1\vec p_2\ldots|i\mathcal T|\vec p_A\vec p_B\ldots\rangle = (2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(\sum_{A,B,\ldots}p - \sum_{1,2,\ldots}p)\,i\mathcal M$. Amplitude $\mathcal M$ = 'invariant matrix element'. The amplitude is dynamical data."), and transcript ("The coefficient of that we call $M$") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.1, eq. (Mdef) · PS §4.5, eq. (4.73) · Yu §6.5, eq. (6.275)*

^def-c6-12-3

> [!remark] Remark: What "dynamical" means
> "By dynamical data, we mean things that depend on … the potentials. It's not something you can just get by energy conservation. It should depend on … what theory [we are talking] about." Everything else in a cross section or a decay rate, the $\mathbb 1$, the delta function, the normalization of the states, the phase space, is kinematics and is the same in every theory. So $\mathcal M$ "is kind of the proper matrix element that we actually really want", and the Feynman rules are rules for computing it ([[§C6.16 Amplitudes from Correlation Functions|§C6.16]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, transcript · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.1*

^rem-c6-12-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.12.3: The Mass Dimension of the Amplitude
> For a process with $N$ external particles in total (initial plus final),
>
> $$
> [\mathcal M] = 4 - N .
> $$
>
> For $2 \to 2$ scattering $\mathcal M$ is dimensionless, as is the coupling $\lambda$ of $\phi^4$ theory ([[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^thm-c6-1-2|Theorem §C6.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.1, Derivation "Three properties of $\mathcal M$ (filled in)", second paragraph*

^thm-c6-12-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (one-particle states). In $\langle\mathbf p|\mathbf q\rangle = 2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-1|Def. §C2a.4.1]]) the right side has dimension $1 + (-3) = -2$ ($[E] = 1$, $[\delta^3(\mathbf p)] = -3$). So $[|\mathbf p\rangle] = -1$, and a state of $k$ particles, a product of $k$ such factors ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-1|Def. §C2a.4.1]]), has dimension $-k$.
>
> **Step 2** (the left side of Def. §C6.12.3). $\hat T$ is dimensionless, like $\hat S = e^{-i\hat H\,2T}$ in the limit. With $k_i$ initial and $k_f$ final particles, $N = k_i + k_f$, the matrix element has dimension $-k_i - k_f = -N$.
>
> **Step 3** (the right side). $[\delta^4(p)] = -4$ and $(2\pi)^4$ is a number, so $-N = -4 + [\mathcal M]$, i.e. $[\mathcal M] = 4 - N$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The dimension of $\mathcal M$ is fixed by the number of external particles alone; for $N = 4$ the amplitude is dimensionless, so at lowest order in $\phi^4$ theory it can only be a number times $\lambda$ ([[§C6.16 Amplitudes from Correlation Functions#^ex-c6-16-1|Example §C6.16.1]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.12.4: The Amplitude of Spinless Particles Is Lorentz Invariant
> If $\hat S$ commutes with the unitary representation $U(\Lambda)$ of the proper orthochronous Lorentz group on the in and out states, and the particles are spinless, then
>
> $$
> \mathcal M(\Lambda p_A, \Lambda p_B, \dots \to \Lambda p_1, \Lambda p_2, \dots) = \mathcal M(p_A, p_B, \dots \to p_1, p_2, \dots) \qquad \text{for every } \Lambda \in SO^+(1, 3) .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.1, Derivation "Three properties of $\mathcal M$ (filled in)", third paragraph · PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 6 ("invariant matrix element") · Yu §6.5, after eq. (6.275) ("$\mathcal M$ 是 Lorentz 不变的")*

^thm-c6-12-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (states). For a spinless particle $U(\Lambda)|\mathbf p\rangle = |\Lambda\mathbf p\rangle$ ([[§C3.5 Covariant Quantum Fields and the Free Scalar's Representation#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]], at $a = 0$), and for several particles each momentum is transformed.
>
> **Step 2** (invariance of the matrix element). $U(\Lambda)$ is unitary and $U(\Lambda)^\dagger\hat SU(\Lambda) = \hat S$, hence also $U(\Lambda)^\dagger i\hat TU(\Lambda) = i\hat T$ ($\mathbb 1$ is invariant). So
>
> $$
> \langle\Lambda\mathbf p_1\cdots|i\hat T|\Lambda\mathbf p_A\cdots\rangle = \langle\mathbf p_1\cdots|U(\Lambda)^\dagger\,i\hat T\,U(\Lambda)|\mathbf p_A\cdots\rangle = \langle\mathbf p_1\cdots|i\hat T|\mathbf p_A\cdots\rangle .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the delta function is invariant). $\delta^4(\Lambda\Delta) = |\det\Lambda|^{-1}\delta^4(\Delta) = \delta^4(\Delta)$ with $\Delta = p_i - p_f$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-6|Theorem §CA.2.6]], 1; $\det\Lambda = 1$), and $\sum_i\Lambda p - \sum_f\Lambda p = \Lambda\Delta$ by linearity.
>
> **Step 4** (divide). Write both sides of step 2 with [[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^def-c6-12-3|Def. §C6.12.3]]: $(2\pi)^4\delta^4(\Lambda\Delta)\,i\mathcal M(\Lambda p) = (2\pi)^4\delta^4(\Delta)\,i\mathcal M(p)$; by step 3 the delta functions agree, and their coefficients on the surface $\Delta = 0$ agree.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The relativistic normalization ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-4|Theorem §C2a.4.4]]) is what makes the coefficient invariant: with non-relativistic normalization, factors $E/E'$ would appear.
> - With spin, $\mathcal M$ carries spinor or polarization labels, and it transforms with the representation matrices of the external particles instead of being invariant.

> [!remark] Remark: A 2 → 2 amplitude is a function of s and t

In the centre-of-mass frame, $s = E_{\rm cm}^2$ and $t$ fixes the scattering angle.

> [!remark] Remark: The big picture
> - Spectrum and interactions (a Lagrangian) ⇒ Feynman rules. "Next few lectures: Feynman rules in different theories."
> - Feynman rules compute amplitudes $i\mathcal M$, "usually the most laborious step when analyzing a process".
> - The amplitude $i\mathcal M$ and general principles (such as kinematics) ⇒ physical variables: cross sections, decay widths, … "The focus of this lecture."
>
> The Feynman rules are the content of the dynamical theory: which lines exist, how they attach, "sometimes … oriented lines … that all have an arrow". Lecture 14 gave one example ([[§C6.10 Feynman Rules in Momentum Space#^thm-c6-10-2|Theorem §C6.10.2]]). Lecture 15 assumes $\mathcal M$ is known, "which you don't really, but you will on Monday", and asks what to do with it: $\mathcal M$ is "similar to a wave function", its square "something like a probability, but it's not exactly that": the cross section "is not a probability, but it's kind of more probability".
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slide 7 ("The Big Picture"), and transcript · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 15 §15.1, Principle "Dynamics versus kinematics" and Figure "bigpicture" (a programmatic statement, kept here as a remark)*

> [!caution] Caution: Four S's
> From this section on, four different objects are called $S$:
> 1. the **$S$-matrix** $\hat S$ ([[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^def-c6-12-1|Def. §C6.12.1]]), an operator, hatted here;
> 2. the **symmetry factor** $S = |\operatorname{Aut}|$ of a Feynman diagram ([[§C6.8 Position-Space Feynman Rules#^def-c6-8-1|Def. §C6.8.1]]), a positive integer from overcounted contractions;
> 3. the **identical-particle factor** $S_{\rm id} = \prod_kn_k!$ for $n_k$ identical particles of species $k$ in a final state ([[§C6.13 Cross Sections from Wave Packets#^thm-c6-13-6|Theorem §C6.13.6]]), a positive integer from overcounted final states; the user's notes write $S$, Yu $\mathcal S$ (eq. (6.305));
> 4. the **scalar bilinear** $\mathsf S = \bar\psi\psi$ ([[§C5a.6 The Dirac Conjugate and the Bilinears#^def-c5a-6-2|Def. §C5a.6.2]]), sans-serif.
>
> The action $S = \int d^4x\,\mathcal L$ ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^def-c1b-2-2|Def. §C1b.2.2]]) is a fifth; the lecture: "probably with a fancy calligraphic $S$ … not to be confused with action". Factors 2 and 3 can occur in the same calculation ([[§C6.16 Amplitudes from Correlation Functions#^ex-c6-16-2|Example §C6.16.2]]: $S = 1$ for the diagram, $S_{\rm id} = 2$ for the final state).
>

> [!caution] Caution: Names for the S-matrix, the T-matrix and the amplitude
> - Slides: calligraphic $\mathcal S = 1 + i\mathcal T$, $\mathcal M$ (slides 4, 6). In these notes the calligraphic $\mathcal T$ is time reversal ([[Larsen PHY 513]], row "Discrete Lorentz transformations") and $\mathcal S$ the Schwartz space of test functions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-1|Def. §CA.2.1]]), so the operators are written $\hat S$, $\hat T$.
> - PS: $S$, $T$, $S = \mathbf 1 + iT$ (eqs. (4.71)–(4.73)), $\mathcal M$ "invariant matrix element"; $\delta^{(4)}(k_{\mathcal A} + k_{\mathcal B} - \sum p_f)$.
> - Yu: $S$-operator $S$, $S = \mathbb I + iT$ (eq. (6.272)), $T_{fi} = \langle f|T|i\rangle$ (eq. (6.273)), $iT_{fi} = (2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(p_i - p_f)\,i\mathcal M$ (eq. (6.275)); $\mathcal M$ is 不变矩阵元 or 不变散射振幅 (invariant scattering amplitude).
> - The user's notes: $S$, $T$, $\mathbb 1$; "a third use of $T$, after large time and time ordering".
> - Quantum Mechanics (Sakurai) normalizes differently: $S_{ni} = \delta_{ni} - 2\pi i\,\delta(E_n - E_i)\,T_{ni}$ ([[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-1|QM Theorem §C10.1.1]]): only energy is conserved (a fixed potential breaks translation invariance), and the sign and the $2\pi$ differ. The scattering amplitude $f$ of quantum mechanics is the analogue of $\mathcal M$ (PS p. 104).
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 15, slides 4, 6 · PS §4.5, eqs. (4.71)–(4.73), p. 104 · Yu §6.5, eqs. (6.272)–(6.275) · the user's PHY 513 notes, PART 2 (e)*

> [!remark] Remark: Yu's route
> Yu introduces the $S$-matrix in §6.2 directly in the interaction picture. Particles prepared far apart are "asymptotic states" (渐近态), of the same form as multiparticle states of the free theory; the state $|\Psi(t)\rangle = \lim_{t_0\to-\infty}U(t, t_0)|i\rangle$ (eq. (6.122)) is projected on a final state in the far future, $S_{fi} = \lim\langle f|U(t, t_0)|i\rangle$ (eq. (6.123)), so $S_{fi} = \langle f|S|i\rangle$ (eq. (6.124)) with $S = U(+\infty, -\infty)$ (eq. (6.125)), Dyson's series (eq. (6.126)) and unitarity $S^\dagger S = \mathbb I$ (eq. (6.127)). The course defines $\hat S$ by the full evolution $e^{-i\hat H\,2T}$ between in and out states, as PS do (eqs. (4.70)–(4.71)); the two agree up to a phase between free states ([[§C6.12 The S-Matrix and the Invariant Amplitude#^thm-c6-12-1|Theorem §C6.12.1]]). Yu then splits $S = \mathbb I + iT$ in §6.5 because the $n = 0$ term of the Dyson series is $\mathbb I$ ("$iT$ 包含所有 $n \ge 1$ 的项"), which is the course's "nothing happened" part read off the series.
>
> *Source: Yu §6.2, pp. 216–217, eqs. (6.122)–(6.127); §6.5, p. 238, eqs. (6.272)–(6.275)*
