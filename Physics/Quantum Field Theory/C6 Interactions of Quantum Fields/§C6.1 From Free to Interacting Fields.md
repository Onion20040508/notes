Until now every field in this subject was free: its Lagrangian was quadratic, its field equation linear, and its quantization exact, because the field decomposed into independent oscillators ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-1|Theorem §C2a.2.1]]). Lecture 13 begins the study of interactions with the simplest interacting theory, a real scalar field with a quartic self-interaction. This section sets the theory up classically, as a Lagrangian ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations|§C1b.2]]) whose coupling is dimensionless ([[§C1b.3 Mass Dimension, Locality and Power Counting|§C1b.3]]) and whose Hamiltonian splits into a free part and an interaction ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory|§C1b.4]]). It then sets it up quantum mechanically at one reference time, where nothing changes from the free field, and locates the one thing that does change: the time dependence. The interaction picture of [[§C6.2 The Interaction Picture|§C6.2]] is built on that observation. Fermions return later; the structure is the same, and the scalar field carries no extra luggage.

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): $\hbar = c = 1$, $g = \operatorname{diag}(+, -, -, -)$, $E_{\mathbf p} = \sqrt{\mathbf p^2 + m^2}$, $[\hat a_{\mathbf p}, \hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}] = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$, $(\partial_\mu\phi)^2 \equiv \partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi$; reference time $t_0 = 0$ throughout this chapter ([[§C6.2 The Interaction Picture#^cau-c6-2-2|§C6.2, Caution: The reference time]]); hats on quantum operators only.

## $\phi^4$ theory, classically

> [!model] Model §C6.1.1: $\phi^4$ Theory
> A real scalar field $\phi$ with the Lagrangian density
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = \underbrace{\tfrac12\,\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi - \tfrac12m^2\phi^2}_{\mathcal L_0\text{: free scalar particle}}\;\underbrace{-\;\frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^4}_{\mathcal L_{\text{int}}\text{: interaction}} ,
> $$
>
> where $\mathcal L_0$ is the free real scalar field ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^mod-c1b-2-6|Model §C1b.2.6]]) and the real number $\lambda$ is the **coupling constant**.
>
> *Assumptions:* $\lambda > 0$ (the potential $\frac12m^2\phi^2 + \frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4$ is then bounded below); $m > 0$; the interaction is local, a product of fields at one spacetime point ([[§C1b.3 Mass Dimension, Locality and Power Counting#^pr-c1b-3-4|Principle §C1b.3.4]]), and contains no derivatives; $\lambda$ small enough that $\lambda = 0$ is a useful starting point (the strategy of perturbation theory in $\lambda$, slide 5).
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 13, slide 5 ("Simplest interacting theory") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 13 §13.1 (Definition "$\phi^4$ theory") · PS §4.1, eq. (4.1) · Yu Ch. 6, eq. (6.5)*

^mod-c6-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the Lagrangian
> - *The minus sign.* $\mathcal L$ is kinetic minus potential energy ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^rem-c1b-2-5|§C1b.2, Remark: Kinetic minus potential, read term by term]]), so the interaction, which enters $\mathcal L$ with a minus sign, is a positive potential energy $\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4$; the lecture: "this one here is an extra energy."
> - *The $4!$.* A convention. Its use appears with Wick's theorem (QFT C6, planned): the $4!$ cancels the $4!$ ways of attaching four identical fields to one vertex. Here it only leaves a $3!$ in the field equation ([[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]]), as the $3!$ of a cubic coupling leaves $\frac{\lambda}{2}$ ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^ex-c1b-2-2|Example §C1b.2.2]]).
> - *Why quartic.* A $\phi^3$ interaction would be simpler, but the energy would not be bounded below unless an even higher power were added (PS p. 77); $\phi^n$ with $n > 4$ has a coupling of negative mass dimension ([[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^thm-c6-1-2|Theorem §C6.1.2]]).
> - *Where it occurs.* Pedagogically it is the simplest interacting theory; in nature, the self-interaction of the Higgs field is of this form, and the same Lagrangian describes critical phenomena in statistical mechanics (PS p. 77).
> - *What makes it hard.* $\mathcal L$ is no longer quadratic, so the field is no longer a collection of independent oscillators: the quartic term couples every Fourier mode to every other ([[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]], ⚑ in Step 4). The strategy is perturbation theory: if $\lambda$ is small, $\lambda = 0$ should be a good first approximation. "It'll be successful at some level, but as we move further and further in, we'll see" — the course will come back to how far.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 13 §13.1 (Definition "$\phi^4$ theory", paragraph "The strategy") · PHY 513 Lecture 13, slide 5, and transcript · PS §4.1, p. 77*

^rem-c6-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.1.2: The Coupling of $\phi^4$ Theory Is Dimensionless
> In four spacetime dimensions $[\phi] = 1$ and $[\lambda] = 0$: the $\phi^4$ interaction is marginal ([[§C1b.3 Mass Dimension, Locality and Power Counting#^def-c1b-3-1|Def. §C1b.3.1]]). Among the interactions $\phi^n$ of one real scalar field, only $n = 3$ (coupling of dimension $1$) and $n = 4$ (dimension $0$) have couplings of non-negative mass dimension, and with the symmetry $\phi \to -\phi$ only $\phi^4$ remains.
>
> *Source: PS §4.1, p. 80 (allowed interactions $\mu\phi^3$ and $\lambda\phi^4$, "$\lambda$ is dimensionless") · Yu Ch. 6, eqs. (6.1)–(6.2) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 2 §2.4*

^thm-c6-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the Lagrangian density). The action is dimensionless and $[d^4x] = -4$ ([[§C1b.3 Mass Dimension, Locality and Power Counting#^thm-c1b-3-1|Theorem §C1b.3.1]]):
>
> $$
> S = \int d^4x\,\mathcal L, \qquad 0 = [S] = [d^4x] + [\mathcal L] = -4 + [\mathcal L] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad [\mathcal L] = 4 .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the field). Each derivative has dimension $1$, and the kinetic term has dimension $4$ ([[§C1b.3 Mass Dimension, Locality and Power Counting#^thm-c1b-3-2|Theorem §C1b.3.2]] at $d = 4$):
>
> $$
> \bigl[\tfrac12\,\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi\bigr] = [\partial_\mu] + [\phi] + [\partial^\mu] + [\phi] = 2 + 2[\phi] = 4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad [\phi] = 1 .
> $$
>
> Check on the mass term: $[\tfrac12m^2\phi^2] = 2[m] + 2[\phi] = 2 + 2 = 4$.
>
> **Step 3** (the coupling). The number $4!$ is dimensionless:
>
> $$
> \Bigl[\frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^4\Bigr] = [\lambda] + 4[\phi] = [\lambda] + 4 = 4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad [\lambda] = 0 ,
> $$
>
> [[§C1b.3 Mass Dimension, Locality and Power Counting#^thm-c1b-3-3|Theorem §C1b.3.3]] with $n = 4$, $d = 4$.
>
> **Step 4** (the other powers). The same equation for a term $g_n\phi^n$:
>
> $$
> [g_n] + n[\phi] = 4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad [g_n] = 4 - n: \qquad [g_3] = 1, \quad [g_4] = 0, \quad [g_5] = -1, \quad [g_6] = -2, \quad \dots
> $$
>
> For $n \ge 5$ write $g_n = \tilde g_n\Lambda^{4-n}$ with $\tilde g_n$ dimensionless ([[§C1b.3 Mass Dimension, Locality and Power Counting#^def-c1b-3-1|Def. §C1b.3.1]]). At energy $E$, measured against a marginal term,
>
> $$
> g_n\,E^{n-4} = \tilde g_n\Bigl(\frac{E}{\Lambda}\Bigr)^{n-4} \;\xrightarrow{\;E \ll \Lambda\;}\; 0 :
> $$
>
> irrelevant at low energy, and, PS p. 80, non-renormalizable.
>
> **Step 5** (the symmetry). Under $\phi \to -\phi$ the kinetic term is unchanged and
>
> $$
> g_n\phi^n \;\longmapsto\; g_n(-\phi)^n = (-1)^ng_n\phi^n, \qquad\text{invariance:}\quad (-1)^ng_n = g_n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g_n = 0 \ \text{ for } n \text{ odd} .
> $$
>
> The powers with $[g_n] \ge 0$, $n = 1, \dots, 4$:
> - $n = 1$: $g_1\phi$, odd, removed;
> - $n = 2$: $\frac12m^2\phi^2$, the mass term, $[m^2] = 2$;
> - $n = 3$: $\frac{g}{3!}\phi^3$, odd, removed;
> - $n = 4$: $\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4$, $[\lambda] = 0$
>
> (a constant, $n = 0$, does not enter the field equation). What is left, with the potential smallest at $\phi = 0$, is [[§C1b.3 Mass Dimension, Locality and Power Counting#^thm-c1b-3-6|Theorem §C1b.3.6]] with $g = 0$:
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = \tfrac12\,\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi - \tfrac12m^2\phi^2 - \frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^4 ,
> $$
>
> which is Model §C6.1.1.
>
> ⚑ By-product: $\phi^4$ theory is not one choice among many but, given locality, Lorentz invariance, power counting and $\phi \to -\phi$, the *only* interacting theory of one real scalar field → [[§C1b.3 Mass Dimension, Locality and Power Counting#^thm-c1b-3-6|Theorem §C1b.3.6]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Only dimensional analysis was used; no quantum theory.
> - A dimensionless coupling makes "small $\lambda$" a meaningful statement without reference to a scale: $\lambda \ll 1$ is the regime of perturbation theory.
> - Used next: the interaction Hamiltonian ([[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^thm-c6-1-5|Theorem §C6.1.5]]) carries the same $\lambda$, and every order of perturbation theory is a power of it ([[§C6.3 The Time-Evolution Operator and Time Ordering#^ex-c6-3-1|Example §C6.3.1]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.1.3: The Field Equation of $\phi^4$ Theory
> The Euler–Lagrange equation ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1b-2-4|Theorem §C1b.2.4]]) of Model §C6.1.1 is the nonlinear Klein–Gordon equation
>
> $$
> (\partial_\mu\partial^\mu + m^2)\,\phi = -\frac{\lambda}{3!}\,\phi^3 .
> $$
>
> In Fourier space it couples every mode to every triple of modes whose momenta add up to its own.
>
> *Source: PS §4.1, eq. (4.2) ("which cannot be solved by Fourier analysis as the free Klein–Gordon equation could") · PHY 513 Lecture 13, slide 17 ("All $\vec p$ are coupled")*

^thm-c6-1-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the equation to evaluate). [[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1b-2-4|Theorem §C1b.2.4]]:
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)} - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi} = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the derivative term). Only the kinetic term contains $\partial_\mu\phi$. With the dummy indices renamed ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^cau-c1b-2-3|§C1b.2, Caution: Rename the dummy index before differentiating]]),
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}\Bigl(\tfrac12\,g^{\alpha\beta}\,\partial_\alpha\phi\,\partial_\beta\phi\Bigr) = \tfrac12\,g^{\alpha\beta}\bigl(\delta^\mu_\alpha\,\partial_\beta\phi + \partial_\alpha\phi\,\delta^\mu_\beta\bigr) = \tfrac12\bigl(\partial^\mu\phi + \partial^\mu\phi\bigr) = \partial^\mu\phi, \qquad \partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)} = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu\phi ,
> $$
>
> exactly as for the free field ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^mod-c1b-2-6|Model §C1b.2.6]]).
>
> **Step 3** (the potential terms).
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi} = \frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\Bigl(-\tfrac12m^2\phi^2 - \frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^4\Bigr) = -m^2\phi - \frac{4\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^3 = -m^2\phi - \frac{\lambda}{3!}\,\phi^3 \qquad\Bigl(\frac{4}{4!} = \frac{4}{4\cdot3!} = \frac{1}{3!}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Insert Steps 2 and 3 into Step 1:
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu\partial^\mu\phi - \Bigl(-m^2\phi - \frac{\lambda}{3!}\,\phi^3\Bigr) = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu\phi + m^2\phi + \frac{\lambda}{3!}\,\phi^3 = 0 ,
> $$
>
> which is the statement.
>
> **Step 4** (Fourier space: the left side). Write $\phi(x) = \int\frac{d^4q}{(2\pi)^4}\tilde\phi(q)e^{-iq\cdot x}$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^def-ca-3-1|Def. §CA.3.1]]). With $\partial_\mu e^{-iq\cdot x} = -iq_\mu e^{-iq\cdot x}$, so $\partial_\mu\partial^\mu e^{-iq\cdot x} = (-i)^2q_\mu q^\mu e^{-iq\cdot x} = -q^2e^{-iq\cdot x}$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-4|Theorem §CA.3.4]], 2),
>
> $$
> (\partial_\mu\partial^\mu + m^2)\,\phi(x) = \int\frac{d^4q}{(2\pi)^4}\,(m^2 - q^2)\,\tilde\phi(q)\,e^{-iq\cdot x} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (the right side). The cube is a triple integral with three distinct variables,
>
> $$
> \phi(x)^3 = \int\frac{d^4q_1}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{d^4q_2}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{d^4q_3}{(2\pi)^4}\,\tilde\phi(q_1)\tilde\phi(q_2)\tilde\phi(q_3)\,e^{-i(q_1 + q_2 + q_3)\cdot x} .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (project onto the mode $p$). Multiply both sides by $e^{ip\cdot x}$ and integrate over $d^4x$, using $\int d^4x\,e^{ik\cdot x} = (2\pi)^4\delta^4(k)$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-4|Theorem §CA.3.4]], 1; the $x$ integral is exchanged with the $q$ integrals in the sense of [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-6|Theorem §CA.3.6]], for a field whose transform is a test function). Left:
>
> $$
> \int\frac{d^4q}{(2\pi)^4}\,(m^2 - q^2)\,\tilde\phi(q)\,(2\pi)^4\delta^4(p - q) = (m^2 - p^2)\,\tilde\phi(p) .
> $$
>
> Right, then the $q_3$ integral done by the delta, $q_3 = p - q_1 - q_2$, its $(2\pi)^4$ cancelling the $(2\pi)^{-4}$ of $d^4q_3$:
>
> $$
> -\frac{\lambda}{3!}\int\frac{d^4q_1}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{d^4q_2}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{d^4q_3}{(2\pi)^4}\,\tilde\phi(q_1)\tilde\phi(q_2)\tilde\phi(q_3)\,(2\pi)^4\delta^4(p - q_1 - q_2 - q_3) = -\frac{\lambda}{3!}\int\frac{d^4q_1}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{d^4q_2}{(2\pi)^4}\,\tilde\phi(q_1)\,\tilde\phi(q_2)\,\tilde\phi(p - q_1 - q_2) .
> $$
>
> Together:
>
> $$
> (m^2 - p^2)\,\tilde\phi(p) = -\frac{\lambda}{3!}\int\frac{d^4q_1}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{d^4q_2}{(2\pi)^4}\,\tilde\phi(q_1)\,\tilde\phi(q_2)\,\tilde\phi(p - q_1 - q_2) .
> $$
>
> ⚑ By-product: at $\lambda = 0$ each $\tilde\phi(p)$ obeys its own equation and is supported on the mass shell ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-3|Theorem §C2b.1.3]]); at $\lambda \ne 0$ the mode $p$ is driven by all pairs $q_1, q_2$, the modes no longer decouple, and the superposition principle fails → [[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^rem-c6-1-2|Remark: The challenge]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The derivative part is untouched by the interaction; only $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\phi$ changes. This is why the canonical momentum is also untouched ([[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^thm-c6-1-4|Theorem §C6.1.4]]).
> - The coefficient $\frac{\lambda}{3!}$ is the $4!$ of the convention divided by the $4$ from differentiating $\phi^4$.
> - Used next: the quantum field obeys the same equation as a Heisenberg equation (PS p. 78, stated there as an exercise); the interaction picture avoids solving it ([[§C6.2 The Interaction Picture|§C6.2]]).

*Uses:* [[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1b-2-4|Theorem §C1b.2.4]], [[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^mod-c1b-2-6|Model §C1b.2.6]], [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^def-ca-3-1|Def. §CA.3.1]], [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-4|Theorem §CA.3.4]], [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-6|Theorem §CA.3.6]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.1.4: The Conjugate Momentum Is Unchanged by the Interaction
> For Model §C6.1.1 the momentum density conjugate to $\phi$ ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^def-c1b-4-1|Def. §C1b.4.1]]) is
>
> $$
> \pi = \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\phi} = \dot\phi ,
> $$
>
> the same as for the free field ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1b-4-2|Theorem §C1b.4.2]]). The canonical data $(\phi(\mathbf x), \pi(\mathbf x))$ on a time slice are therefore those of the free field.
>
> *Source: PS §4.1, p. 78 ("which are unaffected by $\mathcal L_{\text{int}}$. (Note, however, that if $\mathcal L_{\text{int}}$ contained $\partial_\mu\phi$, the definition of $\pi(\mathbf x)$ would change.)") · Yu §6.1.1, eq. (6.39)*

^thm-c6-1-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the density without indices). With $g = \operatorname{diag}(+, -, -, -)$, $\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi = \dot\phi^2 - (\nabla\phi)^2$, so ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^mod-c1b-2-6|Model §C1b.2.6]] plus the interaction)
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = \tfrac12\dot\phi^2 - \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 - \tfrac12m^2\phi^2 - \frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^4 .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (differentiate term by term, $\phi$ and $\nabla\phi$ held fixed).
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\phi} = \frac{\partial}{\partial\dot\phi}\bigl(\tfrac12\dot\phi^2\bigr) - \frac{\partial}{\partial\dot\phi}\bigl(\tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2\bigr) - \frac{\partial}{\partial\dot\phi}\bigl(\tfrac12m^2\phi^2\bigr) - \frac{\partial}{\partial\dot\phi}\Bigl(\frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^4\Bigr) = \dot\phi - 0 - 0 - 0 = \dot\phi .
> $$
>
> ⚑ By-product: this holds because $\mathcal L_{\text{int}}$ contains no derivatives. A derivative coupling changes $\pi$: for instance $\mathcal L_{\text{int}} = c\,\phi^2\,\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi = c\,\phi^2\bigl(\dot\phi^2 - (\nabla\phi)^2\bigr)$ gives $\pi = \dot\phi + 2c\,\phi^2\dot\phi$ (likewise the $eA^\mu\phi\,\partial_\mu\phi^\ast$ of scalar QED, PS p. 80), and then $\mathcal H_{\text{int}} \ne -\mathcal L_{\text{int}}$ → [[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^thm-c6-1-5|Theorem §C6.1.5]], Step 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The interaction does not touch the kinematics of the canonical data: the equal-time commutators of the quantum theory can be imposed exactly as before ([[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^pr-c6-1-6|Principle §C6.1.6]]).

*Uses:* [[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^def-c1b-4-1|Def. §C1b.4.1]], [[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^mod-c1b-2-6|Model §C1b.2.6]]

*Procedure:* [[P1 Canonical Quantization#^p1-1|P1, step 1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.1.5: The Hamiltonian of $\phi^4$ Theory and Its Split
> The Hamiltonian density ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^def-c1b-4-2|Def. §C1b.4.2]]) of Model §C6.1.1 is
>
> $$
> \mathcal H = \underbrace{\tfrac12\pi^2 + \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\phi^2}_{\mathcal H_0} + \underbrace{\frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^4}_{\mathcal H_{\text{int}}}, \qquad \mathcal H_{\text{int}} = -\mathcal L_{\text{int}} ,
> $$
>
> so the Hamiltonian splits as
>
> $$
> H = H_0 + H_{\text{int}}, \qquad H_0 = \int d^3x\,\mathcal H_0, \qquad H_{\text{int}} = \int d^3x\,\frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^4(\mathbf x) ,
> $$
>
> with $H_0$ the Hamiltonian of the free field ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1b-4-2|Theorem §C1b.4.2]]). For $\lambda > 0$, $H \ge H_0 \ge 0$.
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 13, slide 5 ($H_{\text{int}} = \int d^3x\,\mathcal H_{\text{int}}[\phi(x)] = \int d^3x\,\frac{1}{4!}\lambda\phi^4(x)$) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 13 §13.1, eq. (phi4) · PS §4.1, p. 77; §4.2, eq. (4.12) · Yu Ch. 6, eq. (6.13); §6.1.1, eqs. (6.40)–(6.43)*

^thm-c6-1-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the Legendre transform). With $\pi = \dot\phi$ ([[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^thm-c6-1-4|Theorem §C6.1.4]]),
>
> $$
> \pi\dot\phi - \mathcal L = \dot\phi^2 - \tfrac12\dot\phi^2 + \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\phi^2 + \frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4 = \tfrac12\dot\phi^2 + \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\phi^2 + \frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4 :
> $$
>
> every potential term changes sign, the kinetic term keeps its sign with half the coefficient of $\pi\dot\phi$, as in [[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^der-c1b-4-2|Derivation §C1b.4.2]], step 3.
>
> **Step 2** (eliminate the velocity). Substitute $\dot\phi = \pi$:
>
> $$
> \mathcal H = \underbrace{\tfrac12\pi^2 + \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\phi^2}_{\mathcal H_0\text{, the free density of Theorem §C1b.4.2}} + \underbrace{\frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^4}_{\mathcal H_{\text{int}}}, \qquad \mathcal H_{\text{int}} = \frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^4 = -\Bigl(-\frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^4\Bigr) = -\mathcal L_{\text{int}}
> $$
>
> ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1b-4-2|Theorem §C1b.4.2]]).
>
> **Step 3** (integrate over space). By linearity of the integral,
>
> $$
> H = \int d^3x\,\mathcal H = \int d^3x\,\mathcal H_0 + \int d^3x\,\mathcal H_{\text{int}} = H_0 + H_{\text{int}} .
> $$
>
> Positivity, pointwise: $\mathcal H_0$ is a sum of squares ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^der-c1b-4-2|Derivation §C1b.4.2]], step 5) and $\phi^4 \ge 0$, so for $\lambda > 0$
>
> $$
> \mathcal H_0 \ge 0, \qquad \mathcal H_{\text{int}} = \frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^4 \ge 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H - H_0 = \int d^3x\,\frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\phi^4 \ge 0, \qquad H \ge H_0 \ge 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (why $\mathcal H_{\text{int}} = -\mathcal L_{\text{int}}$). For any interaction without derivatives, $\mathcal L = \mathcal L_0(\phi, \partial\phi) + \mathcal L_{\text{int}}(\phi)$ and
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\mathcal L_{\text{int}}}{\partial\dot\phi} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \pi = \frac{\partial\mathcal L_0}{\partial\dot\phi}, \qquad \mathcal H = \pi\dot\phi - \mathcal L_0 - \mathcal L_{\text{int}} = \underbrace{\bigl(\pi\dot\phi - \mathcal L_0\bigr)}_{\mathcal H_0} - \mathcal L_{\text{int}} :
> $$
>
> the interaction enters only through $-\mathcal L$ and does not change the relation between $\pi$ and $\dot\phi$ used in Step 2. PS states the rule, $H_{\text{int}} = \int d^3x\,\mathcal H_{\text{int}} = -\int d^3x\,\mathcal L_{\text{int}}$ (p. 77).
>
> ⚑ By-product: the Hamiltonian is a space integral at one instant, never a time integral; the time integrals that appear in [[§C6.3 The Time-Evolution Operator and Time Ordering|§C6.3]] come from solving an equation of motion, not from $H$ → [[§C6.3 The Time-Evolution Operator and Time Ordering#^rem-c6-3-1|§C6.3, Remark: Space integrals and time integrals]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The split $H = H_0 + H_{\text{int}}$ is exact; it is the decomposition the whole of perturbation theory is built on.
> - $H_0$ is quadratic in $(\phi, \pi)$ and is diagonalized by the free mode expansion; $H_{\text{int}}$ is quartic.
> - Used next: the quantum operators $\hat H_0$, $\hat H_{\text{int}}$ ([[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]]).

## The quantum theory at the reference time

> [!principle] Principle §C6.1.6: Canonical Quantization of $\phi^4$ Theory at a Reference Time
> At the reference time $t_0 = 0$ the field $\hat\phi_S(\mathbf x)$ and momentum density $\hat\pi_S(\mathbf x)$ of $\phi^4$ theory obey the equal-time relations of [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2a-1-2|Principle §C2a.1.2]], and they are realized exactly as for the free field: by the mode expansion of [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-2|Theorem §C2a.2.2]] with ladder operators obeying $[\hat a_{\mathbf p}, \hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}] = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-6|Theorem §C2a.2.6]]), acting on the Fock space of [[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^pr-c2a-3-3|Principle §C2a.3.3]], with $E_{\mathbf p} = \sqrt{\mathbf p^2 + m^2}$. The Hamiltonian is the classical $H$ of [[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^thm-c6-1-5|Theorem §C6.1.5]] read as an operator.
>
> *Domain:* any interaction without derivatives (so that $\pi$ is unchanged, [[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^thm-c6-1-4|Theorem §C6.1.4]]); here $\mathbf p$ is only the Fourier variable conjugate to $\mathbf x$ at the reference time, and $m$ the parameter of $\mathcal L$, not yet the measured mass. That the interacting theory lives on this Fock space at all is assumed at this level ([[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^rem-c6-1-4|★ Remark: In what sense φ̂⁴(x) is defined]]).

> [!definition] Definition §C6.1.1: Free and Interaction Hamiltonians
> In $\phi^4$ theory the **free Hamiltonian** $\hat H_0$ and the **interaction Hamiltonian** $\hat H_{\text{int}}$ are the operators, built from the Schrödinger field at the reference time,
>
> $$
> \hat H_0 = \;:\!\int d^3x\,\Bigl[\tfrac12\hat\pi_S^2 + \tfrac12(\nabla\hat\phi_S)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\hat\phi_S^2\Bigr]\!:\; = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,E_{\mathbf p}\,\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}\hat a_{\mathbf p}, \qquad \hat H_{\text{int}} = \int d^3x\;\hat{\mathcal H}_{\text{int}}(\mathbf x) = \int d^3x\;\frac{\lambda}{4!}\,\hat\phi_S(\mathbf x)^4 ,
> $$
>
> and the full Hamiltonian is $\hat H = \hat H_0 + \hat H_{\text{int}}$. $\hat H_0$ is normal ordered ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^def-c2a-3-1|Def. §C2a.3.1]]), so $\hat H_0|0\rangle = 0$: the zero of energy is the free vacuum. Neither operator depends on time.
>

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.1.7: The Ladder Operators Diagonalize the Free Hamiltonian, Not the Full One
> 1. $[\hat H_0, \hat a_{\mathbf p}] = -E_{\mathbf p}\hat a_{\mathbf p}$, hence for every real $t$
>
> $$
> e^{i\hat H_0t}\,\hat a_{\mathbf p}\,e^{-i\hat H_0t} = \hat a_{\mathbf p}\,e^{-iE_{\mathbf p}t}, \qquad e^{i\hat H_0t}\,\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}\,e^{-i\hat H_0t} = \hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}\,e^{iE_{\mathbf p}t} .
> $$
>
> 2. The full Hamiltonian does not act this way: for $\lambda \ne 0$,
>
> $$
> [\hat H, \hat a_{\mathbf p}] = -E_{\mathbf p}\hat a_{\mathbf p} - \frac{\lambda}{3!}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\int d^3x\;e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}\,\hat\phi_S(\mathbf x)^3 ,
> $$
>
> which is not a multiple of $\hat a_{\mathbf p}$, so $e^{i\hat Ht}\hat a_{\mathbf p}e^{-i\hat Ht}$ is not $\hat a_{\mathbf p}$ times a phase.
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 13, slide 6 ("Ladder operators $a_{\vec p}$, $a^\dagger_{\vec p}$ diagonalize free Hamiltonian $= H_0$ … Challenge: $H_{\text{int}}$ acts in a complicated way on $a_{\vec p}$, $a^\dagger_{\vec p}$") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 13 §13.1 · Yu §6.1.1, eqs. (6.47)–(6.48)*

^thm-c6-1-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1: the commutator with $\hat H_0$). As in [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^der-c2b-1-4|Derivation §C2b.1.4]], Steps 1–6, from the mode form of $\hat H_0$ ([[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]]), $[AB, C] = A[B, C] + [A, C]B$ and the mode algebra ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-6|Theorem §C2a.2.6]]):
>
> $$
> [\hat H_0, \hat a_{\mathbf p}] = \int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3}E_{\mathbf q}\Bigl(\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}\underbrace{[\hat a_{\mathbf q}, \hat a_{\mathbf p}]}_{0} + \underbrace{[\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}, \hat a_{\mathbf p}]}_{-(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf q - \mathbf p)}\hat a_{\mathbf q}\Bigr) = -\int d^3q\,E_{\mathbf q}\,\delta^3(\mathbf q - \mathbf p)\,\hat a_{\mathbf q} = -E_{\mathbf p}\,\hat a_{\mathbf p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (powers, by induction). $\hat H_0\hat a_{\mathbf p} = \hat a_{\mathbf p}\hat H_0 + [\hat H_0, \hat a_{\mathbf p}] = \hat a_{\mathbf p}(\hat H_0 - E_{\mathbf p})$; if $\hat H_0^n\hat a_{\mathbf p} = \hat a_{\mathbf p}(\hat H_0 - E_{\mathbf p})^n$, then
>
> $$
> \hat H_0^{n+1}\hat a_{\mathbf p} = \hat H_0\,\hat a_{\mathbf p}(\hat H_0 - E_{\mathbf p})^n = \hat a_{\mathbf p}(\hat H_0 - E_{\mathbf p})(\hat H_0 - E_{\mathbf p})^n = \hat a_{\mathbf p}(\hat H_0 - E_{\mathbf p})^{n+1} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the exponential). Sum the series term by term; $E_{\mathbf p}\mathbf 1$ commutes with $\hat H_0$, so the exponents split ([[§CB.1 Matrix Lie Groups, the Exponential Map and Lie Algebras#^thm-cb-1-5|Theorem §CB.1.5]], 3):
>
> $$
> e^{i\hat H_0t}\hat a_{\mathbf p} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(it)^n}{n!}\hat H_0^n\hat a_{\mathbf p} = \hat a_{\mathbf p}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(it)^n}{n!}(\hat H_0 - E_{\mathbf p})^n = \hat a_{\mathbf p}\,e^{i(\hat H_0 - E_{\mathbf p})t} = \hat a_{\mathbf p}\,e^{i\hat H_0t}\,e^{-iE_{\mathbf p}t} .
> $$
>
> Multiply by $e^{-i\hat H_0t}$ on the right: $e^{i\hat H_0t}\hat a_{\mathbf p}e^{-i\hat H_0t} = \hat a_{\mathbf p}e^{-iE_{\mathbf p}t}$. Take the adjoint ($t$ real, $\hat H_0$ Hermitian): $e^{i\hat H_0t}\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}e^{-i\hat H_0t} = (\hat a_{\mathbf p}e^{-iE_{\mathbf p}t})^\dagger = \hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}e^{iE_{\mathbf p}t}$. Part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (part 2: the field against $\hat a_{\mathbf p}$). $[\hat H, \hat a_{\mathbf p}] = [\hat H_0, \hat a_{\mathbf p}] + [\hat H_{\text{int}}, \hat a_{\mathbf p}]$, the first being Step 1. For the second, write $\hat\phi \equiv \hat\phi_S(\mathbf x)$ and $c(\mathbf x) \equiv [\hat\phi_S(\mathbf x), \hat a_{\mathbf p}]$. From the mode expansion of $\hat\phi_S$ and [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-6|Theorem §C2a.2.6]],
>
> $$
> c(\mathbf x) = \int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf q}}}\Bigl(e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}\underbrace{[\hat a_{\mathbf q}, \hat a_{\mathbf p}]}_{0} + e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}\underbrace{[\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}, \hat a_{\mathbf p}]}_{-(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf q - \mathbf p)}\Bigr) = -\int d^3q\,\frac{e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf q}}}\,\delta^3(\mathbf q - \mathbf p) = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\,e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x} ,
> $$
>
> a number (a c-number function of $\mathbf x$), so it commutes with everything.
>
> **Step 5** (the fourth power). $[AB, C] = A[B, C] + [A, C]B$, applied for $\hat\phi^2$, $\hat\phi^3$, $\hat\phi^4$ in turn:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> [\hat\phi^2, \hat a_{\mathbf p}] &= \hat\phi c + c\hat\phi ,\\
> [\hat\phi^3, \hat a_{\mathbf p}] &= \hat\phi[\hat\phi^2, \hat a_{\mathbf p}] + c\hat\phi^2 = \hat\phi^2c + \hat\phi c\hat\phi + c\hat\phi^2 ,\\
> [\hat\phi^4, \hat a_{\mathbf p}] &= \hat\phi[\hat\phi^3, \hat a_{\mathbf p}] + c\hat\phi^3 = \hat\phi^3c + \hat\phi^2c\hat\phi + \hat\phi c\hat\phi^2 + c\hat\phi^3 = 4c\,\hat\phi^3 ,
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> the four terms being equal because $c$ is a number.
>
> **Step 6** (assemble). With $\frac{4}{4!} = \frac{1}{3!}$,
>
> $$
> [\hat H_{\text{int}}, \hat a_{\mathbf p}] = \int d^3x\,\frac{\lambda}{4!}\,4c(\mathbf x)\,\hat\phi_S(\mathbf x)^3 = -\frac{\lambda}{3!}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\int d^3x\,e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}\,\hat\phi_S(\mathbf x)^3 .
> $$
>
> Adding Step 1 gives part 2.
>
> **Step 7** (why it is not a phase: sort by particle number). Write $\hat\phi_S = \hat A + \hat A^\dagger$, $\hat A(\mathbf x) = \int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{\hat a_{\mathbf q}e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf q}}}$, and $\hat N = \int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3}\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}\hat a_{\mathbf q}$, with $[\hat N, \hat A] = -\hat A$, $[\hat N, \hat A^\dagger] = \hat A^\dagger$ (as in Step 1 with $E_{\mathbf q} \to 1$). The cube has $2^3 = 8$ terms; a term with $k$ factors $\hat A^\dagger$ and $3 - k$ factors $\hat A$ obeys $[\hat N, X] = (2k - 3)X$:
>
> $$
> \hat\phi_S^3 = \underbrace{\hat A\hat A\hat A}_{\Delta N = -3} + \underbrace{\hat A^\dagger\hat A\hat A + \hat A\hat A^\dagger\hat A + \hat A\hat A\hat A^\dagger}_{\Delta N = -1} + \underbrace{\hat A^\dagger\hat A^\dagger\hat A + \hat A^\dagger\hat A\hat A^\dagger + \hat A\hat A^\dagger\hat A^\dagger}_{\Delta N = +1} + \underbrace{\hat A^\dagger\hat A^\dagger\hat A^\dagger}_{\Delta N = +3} .
> $$
>
> $\kappa\,\hat a_{\mathbf p}$ has $\Delta N = -1$ only. The $\Delta N = +3$ part of the extra term is not zero: with $\int d^3x\,e^{-i(\mathbf p + \mathbf q_1 + \mathbf q_2 + \mathbf q_3)\cdot\mathbf x} = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p + \mathbf q_1 + \mathbf q_2 + \mathbf q_3)$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-4|Theorem §CA.3.4]], 1),
>
> $$
> -\frac{\lambda}{3!}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\int d^3x\,e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}\,\hat A^\dagger(\mathbf x)^3 = -\frac{\lambda}{3!}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\int\prod_{k=1}^3\frac{d^3q_k}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf q_k}}}\;(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p + \mathbf q_1 + \mathbf q_2 + \mathbf q_3)\;\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf q_1}\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf q_2}\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf q_3} ,
> $$
>
> which maps $|0\rangle$ to a nonzero three-particle state. Parts with different $\Delta N$ cannot cancel (they are eigenoperators of $[\hat N, \cdot\,]$ with different eigenvalues), so $[\hat H, \hat a_{\mathbf p}] = \kappa\,\hat a_{\mathbf p}$ fails for every number $\kappa$, and the Baker–Hausdorff step of Steps 2–3 ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-2|QM Theorem §C3.3.2]]) does not apply.
>
> ⚑ By-product: in the interacting theory $\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle$ is not an eigenstate of $\hat H$, and $|0\rangle$ itself is not: $\hat H|0\rangle = \hat H_0|0\rangle + \hat H_{\text{int}}|0\rangle = 0 + \frac{\lambda}{4!}\int d^3x\,\hat\phi_S(\mathbf x)^4|0\rangle$, whose $\Delta N = +4$ part $\frac{\lambda}{4!}\int d^3x\,\hat A^\dagger(\mathbf x)^4|0\rangle$ is a nonzero four-particle state → the interacting vacuum, [[§C6.4 The Interacting Vacuum and the Two-Point Function#^def-c6-4-1|Def. §C6.4.1]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The ladder operators were designed for $\hat H_0$: they encode the commutators *and* diagonalize the free energy. The first job survives the interaction, the second does not.
> - One cannot "just put in the correct energy" in $e^{-iE_{\mathbf p}t}$: the operator does not acquire a phase at all.
> - Used next: the free time dependence of [[§C6.2 The Interaction Picture#^thm-c6-2-1|Theorem §C6.2.1]], which keeps part 1 and gives up the full $\hat H$.

^rem-c6-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: What p means in the interacting theory
> Asked in the lecture: if the interacting field is still expanded in $\hat a_{\mathbf p}$, what is $\mathbf p$? It is the Fourier label at the reference time, nothing more: at one instant there is one degree of freedom per point $\mathbf x$, and these can be traded for one per momentum $\mathbf p$. Everything at a fixed time is quantum mechanically correct, because the commutators are equal-time ones ([[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^pr-c6-1-6|Principle §C6.1.6]]). What is lost is the link between $\mathbf p$ and energy: $\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$ no longer creates an eigenstate of $\hat H$ with energy $E_{\mathbf p}$ ([[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^der-c6-1-7|Derivation §C6.1.7]], ⚑). The difficulty is only how things at different times are related; without interactions the free evolution would be exact.
>

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: In what sense φ̂⁴(x) is defined
> $\hat\phi_S(\mathbf x)$ is an operator-valued distribution, not an operator ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-11|Def. §CA.2.11]]; [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-7|Theorem §C2a.4.7]]), and the product of distributions at one point has in general no value ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-9|Theorem §CA.2.9]]). Already $\hat\phi_S(\mathbf x)^2$ contains the divergent constant $\langle0|\hat\phi_S(\mathbf x)^2|0\rangle = D_W(0)$, the coincident limit of the Wightman function; normal ordering removes it ([[§C2b.6 Time Ordering, the Feynman Propagator and the iε Prescription#^thm-c2b-6-7|Theorem §C2b.6.7]], [[§C2b.6 Time Ordering, the Feynman Propagator and the iε Prescription#^thm-c2b-6-8|Theorem §C2b.6.8]]). $\hat\phi_S^4$ contains such constants and also operator terms multiplied by them (from contracting two of the four fields). So $\hat H_{\text{int}}$ of [[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]] is defined only after a regularization: a finite box and a momentum cutoff turn the field into finitely many oscillators, where every formula of this chapter is a statement about matrices (the user's notes check them numerically on one anharmonic oscillator, [[§C6.4 The Interacting Vacuum and the Two-Point Function#^ex-c6-4-2|Example §C6.4.2]]). Taking $\hat H_{\text{int}}$ normal ordered, $:\!\hat\phi_S^4\!:$, removes the vacuum constants; the remaining dependence on the cutoff is absorbed into the parameters $m$ and $\lambda$ by renormalization, beyond this course. In PS this is the "formal" level of §4.2; the vacuum-energy constants drop out of the master formula anyway ([[§C6.4 The Interacting Vacuum and the Two-Point Function#^thm-c6-4-6|Theorem §C6.4.6]]). Haag's theorem, which says that an interacting field cannot be unitarily equivalent to a free one in infinite volume, is the precise form of the warning that [[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^pr-c6-1-6|Principle §C6.1.6]] is an assumption.
>

> - Problem Set 7 already wrote the field equation of a cubic interaction; the quartic one differs only in the power and the factorial — [[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^ex-c1b-2-2|Example §C1b.2.2]], [[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]].
> - The interaction couples all Fourier modes, which is the field-theory version of an anharmonic oscillator: the lecture's analogy for the interacting vacuum rests on it — [[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^der-c6-1-3|Derivation §C6.1.3]], [[§C6.4 The Interacting Vacuum and the Two-Point Function#^rem-c6-4-2|§C6.4, Remark: Two complications]].
> - What survives the interaction is everything at one instant: commutators, mode operators, Fock space. This is why the canonical relations were postulated at equal times in the first place — [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2a-1-2|Principle §C2a.1.2]], [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^cau-c2b-1-1|§C2b.1, Caution: Equal times only]], [[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^pr-c6-1-6|Principle §C6.1.6]].
> - The commutator $[\hat H_{\text{int}}, \hat a_{\mathbf p}]$ changes the particle number by $\pm1$ and $\pm3$: interactions create and destroy particles, the reason a field theory is needed at all — [[§C1a.1 Why Quantum Field Theory|§C1a.1]], [[§C6.1 From Free to Interacting Fields#^thm-c6-1-7|Theorem §C6.1.7]].
> - The QED and Yukawa interactions of PS §4.1 have the same structure, $\mathcal H_{\text{int}} = -\mathcal L_{\text{int}}$ without derivatives (QED in Coulomb gauge has extra terms) — QFT C8 (planned); the Yukawa coupling is dimensionless, [[§C1b.3 Mass Dimension, Locality and Power Counting#^ex-c1b-3-2|Example §C1b.3.2]].
> - Quantum mechanics treats an anharmonic term the same way, as a perturbation of a solved $H_0$ — [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory|QM §B8.1]] (time-independent), [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits|QM §C9.4]] (time-dependent).
