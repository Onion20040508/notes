How does a field theory look in the form quantum mechanics needs, with coordinates, momenta and a Hamiltonian? The particle version is the Legendre transform and Hamilton's equations ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-2|CM Def. §B7.1.2]], [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-3|CM Theorem §B7.1.3]]) and the Poisson bracket ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-1|CM Def. §B8.1.1]]); the field's Lagrangian is [[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations|§C1.9]]. This section is the home of the momentum density $\pi$, the Hamiltonian density $\mathcal H$, Hamilton's equations for fields and the field Poisson brackets. What is new relative to mechanics: one canonical pair per point of space, a gradient term that turns $\partial H/\partial q$ into a functional derivative, and brackets that are delta functions. Canonical quantization, which promotes these brackets to commutators, is [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields|§C2a.1]] and [[P1 Canonical Quantization#^p1-1|P1, step 1]].

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): natural units; $g = \operatorname{diag}(+,-,-,-)$; a dot is $\partial_t$; the momentum density is written $\pi$, as in PS and on the board (the slides and Problem Set 3 write $\Pi$: [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^cau-c2a-1-1|§C2a.1, Caution: Eₚ, not ωₚ; π, not Π]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Why a Hamiltonian, and what it costs
> Quantum mechanics is built on a Hamiltonian: the Schrödinger equation is $H$ acting on a state, and canonical quantization turns brackets of canonical pairs into commutators. The course takes this familiar route first. The price is manifest Lorentz invariance: $H$ generates time translations only, so time is singled out, and the formalism treats $\dot\phi$ differently from $\nabla\phi$. Invariance is recovered at the end (the Heisenberg field obeys a covariant equation, [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-3|Theorem §C2b.1.3]]). The alternative, the path integral, keeps the Lagrangian central and invariance manifest throughout, at the cost of an unfamiliar formalism (QFT C11, planned).
>
> *Source: PHY 513 Lecture 3, Part B ("Towards Quantization: Strategy in the Course") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.4 · PS §2.2, p. 16*

## Momentum density and Hamiltonian

> [!definition] Definition §C1.10.1: Canonical Momentum Density; Hamiltonian Density
> For a density $\mathcal L(\phi_a, \partial_\mu\phi_a)$ the **momentum density conjugate to** $\phi_a$ is
>
> $$
> \pi_a(x) = \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\phi_a(x)} ,
> $$
>
> taken with the other velocities, the fields and their spatial derivatives held fixed. If these relations can be solved for the velocities, the **Hamiltonian density** and the **Hamiltonian** are
>
> $$
> \mathcal H(\phi_a, \nabla\phi_a, \pi_a) = \sum_a\pi_a\dot\phi_a - \mathcal L \quad\text{(every } \dot\phi_a \text{ eliminated)}, \qquad H[\phi, \pi] = \int d^3x\;\mathcal H ,
> $$
>
> a functional of the **canonical data** $\phi_a(\mathbf x)$, $\pi_a(\mathbf x)$ on one time slice.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.4 (Definition "Canonical momentum density and Hamiltonian density", eq. (pifield); Derivation "Several fields in the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formalisms") · PHY 513 Lecture 3, Part B ("Momentum density ('one per spatial point')") · PS §2.2, eqs. (2.4)–(2.5) · Yu §1.6.2, eqs. (1.168)–(1.170)*

^def-c1-10-1

This is the Legendre transform of mechanics ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-2|CM Def. §B7.1.2]]) done pointwise, with the label $i$ of $q_i$ made continuous, $q_i \to \phi(\mathbf x)$. Two features have no particle analogue: $\pi$ is a density, and $H$ integrates over space, not spacetime, whereas $S$ integrates $\mathcal L$ over $d^4x$. The units confirm it: $[\mathcal L] = [\mathcal H] = 4$, $[S] = 0$, $[H] = [L] = 1$ (an energy), $[\pi] = 2$ for a scalar ([[§C1.2 Natural Units and Dimensional Analysis#^thm-c1-2-4|Theorem §C1.2.4]], [[§C1.2 Natural Units and Dimensional Analysis#^thm-c1-2-5|Theorem §C1.2.5]]). The invertibility assumption fails for gauge fields ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^rem-c1-10-3|Remark: Constraints and first-order Lagrangians]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.10.1: The Momentum Density Is a Functional Derivative
> Regard the Lagrangian $L[\phi, \dot\phi] = \int d^3y\,\mathcal L$ as a functional of the field and the velocity field on one slice, independent of each other. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{\delta L}{\delta\dot\phi_a(\mathbf x)} = \pi_a(\mathbf x) .
> $$
>
> Equivalently, with space divided into cells of volume $\Delta V$ around points $\mathbf x_j$, the momentum conjugate to the cell value $\phi_j$ is $p_j = \partial L/\partial\dot\phi_j = \pi(\mathbf x_j)\,\Delta V$: $\pi$ is momentum per unit volume.
>
> *Source: PS §2.2, p. 16 (the derivation of eq. (2.4) by "pretending that the spatial points are discretely spaced") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.4 ("q_i → φ(x)") · the functional-derivative form written out here*

^thm-c1-10-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Vary the velocity alone.** In the Lagrangian formalism the coordinates and velocities at one instant are independent variables, as $q$ and $\dot q$ are in $L(q, \dot q)$. Replace $\dot\phi_a \to \dot\phi_a + \varepsilon\zeta_a$ with $\zeta$ a test function on $\mathbb R^3$, keeping $\phi_a$, hence $\nabla\phi_a$, fixed.
>
> **2. Differentiate.** For a configuration on which $L$ converges (fields and velocities falling off at infinity), the derivative at $\varepsilon = 0$ passes under the integral ([[§CA.1 Exchanging Limits, Derivatives and Integrals#^thm-ca-1-1|Theorem §CA.1.1]]; the $\varepsilon$-dependence sits on the compact support of $\zeta$):
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}L[\phi, \dot\phi + \varepsilon\zeta]\Big|_{\varepsilon = 0} = \int d^3y\,\sum_a\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\phi_a}(\mathbf y)\,\zeta_a(\mathbf y) .
> $$
>
> No spatial integration by parts is needed, because $\mathcal L$ contains $\dot\phi$ but not its spatial derivatives.
>
> **3. Read off the kernel.** By [[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^def-c1-9-2|Def. §C1.9.2]] (on a slice), $\delta L/\delta\dot\phi_a(\mathbf y) = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\phi_a(\mathbf y) = \pi_a(\mathbf y)$, a continuous function and so unique ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-1|Theorem §CA.2.1]], 2).
>
> **4. Cells.** Approximate $L \approx \sum_i\Delta V\,\mathcal L_i$, where $\mathcal L_i$ is the density in cell $i$ with $\phi_i$, $\dot\phi_i$ and finite differences of neighbouring $\phi$'s in place of $\nabla\phi$. The velocity $\dot\phi_j$ occurs only in the term $i = j$ (finite differences involve the $\phi$'s, not their velocities), so $p_j = \partial L/\partial\dot\phi_j = \Delta V\,(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\phi)(\mathbf x_j) = \pi(\mathbf x_j)\,\Delta V$. Then $H = \sum_jp_j\dot\phi_j - L \approx \sum_j\Delta V\,(\pi_j\dot\phi_j - \mathcal L_j) \to \int d^3x\,\mathcal H$. ⚑ By-product: a cell momentum is $\pi\,\Delta V$, so the cell bracket $\{\phi_i, \pi_j\} = \delta_{ij}/\Delta V$ becomes $\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$ in the limit → [[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1-10-5|Theorem §C1.10.5]], [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^rem-c2a-1-2|§C2a.1, Remark: Why a delta function]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - "Momentum density" is literal: momentum per unit volume of the cell variables.
> - Assumption: the fields fall off fast enough for $L$ to exist; otherwise the statement is local and holds for $L$ restricted to any bounded region containing $\operatorname{supp}\zeta$.
> - Used next: Hamilton's equations ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1-10-4|Theorem §C1.10.4]]), where the Hamiltonian is differentiated functionally in the same way.

> [!caution] Caution: What the conjugate momentum is not
> - $\pi = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\phi$ is the derivative with respect to the time component of $\partial_\mu\phi$ only: the $\mu = 0$ component of $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)$, which for the free field is $\partial^\mu\phi$, so $\pi = \partial^0\phi = \dot\phi$.
> - It is not the momentum carried by the field. That is a different quantity built from $\pi$, $P^i = \int d^3x\,T^{0i}$, which for the scalar is $-\int d^3x\,\pi\,\partial_i\phi$ ([[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum#^thm-c1-12-6|Theorem §C1.12.6]]; in modes [[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^thm-c2a-3-4|Theorem §C2a.3.4]]).
> - It is not "mass times velocity" in general: for a charged particle $\mathbf p = m\dot{\mathbf x} + e\mathbf A$; for a Dirac field $\pi = i\psi^\dagger$, no velocity at all; and for a gauge field $A_0$ has no conjugate momentum.
> - $H$ must be a function of $\phi$ and $\pi$: before $\dot\phi$ is eliminated, the expression $\pi\dot\phi - \mathcal L$ is not wrong but wrongly formatted, and Hamilton's equations cannot be applied to it ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^cau-b7-1-1|CM Caution: Eliminate the velocities first]]).
>

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.10.2: Hamiltonian Density of the Free Real Field
> For the free real scalar field ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^mod-c1-9-6|Model §C1.9.6]]),
>
> $$
> \pi = \dot\phi, \qquad \mathcal H = \tfrac12\pi^2 + \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\phi^2, \qquad H = \int d^3x\,\Bigl[\tfrac12\pi^2 + \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\phi^2\Bigr] \ge 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.4, eqs. (piscalar), (Hscalar) · PHY 513 Lecture 3, Part B ("Hamiltonian of Free Scalar Theory") · PS §2.2, eq. (2.8)*

^thm-c1-10-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The density without indices.** $\mathcal L = \tfrac12\dot\phi^2 - \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 - \tfrac12m^2\phi^2$ ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^mod-c1-9-6|Model §C1.9.6]]).
>
> **2. The momentum.** Only the first term contains $\dot\phi$: $\pi = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\phi = \dot\phi$. No mass multiplies $\dot\phi$, because the kinetic term is $\frac12\dot\phi^2$ (each point has unit inertia). The relation inverts trivially: $\dot\phi = \pi$.
>
> **3. The Legendre transform.** $\pi\dot\phi - \mathcal L = \dot\phi\cdot\dot\phi - \tfrac12\dot\phi^2 + \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\phi^2 = \tfrac12\dot\phi^2 + \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\phi^2$: the two signs of the potential terms flip, the kinetic term keeps its sign with half the coefficient of $\pi\dot\phi$.
>
> **4. Eliminate the velocity.** Substitute $\dot\phi = \pi$ (step 2): $\mathcal H = \tfrac12\pi^2 + \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac12m^2\phi^2$. Before this step the expression is not a Hamiltonian ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^cau-c1-10-1|Caution: What the conjugate momentum is not]]).
>
> **5. Positivity.** Each term is a square times $\frac12$, so $\mathcal H \ge 0$ pointwise and $H \ge 0$. ⚑ By-product: $H = 0$ only for $\pi = 0$ and $\phi = 0$ (for $m > 0$; for $m = 0$, any constant $\phi$): the classical ground state. The kinetic term of [[§C1.8 Scalar Field Theory꞉ Fields, Locality and Symmetry#^thm-c1-8-6|Theorem §C1.8.6]] had to be positive for this. ⚑ By-product: $\mathcal H$ is not a scalar; it is the $00$ component of the energy–momentum tensor $T^{\mu\nu}$, and $H$ the time component of a four-vector → [[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum#^rem-c1-12-1|§C1.12, Remark: Why the 00 component is the Hamiltonian density]], [[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum#^ex-c1-12-1|Example §C1.12.1]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The Lagrangian had negative pieces, the price of Lorentz invariance; the Hamiltonian is a sum of squares and pays by not being invariant.
> - The three terms are the energy cost of moving in time, of shearing in space, and of having the field at all (PS); in Fourier space they make one oscillator per momentum ([[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^thm-c2a-1-3|Theorem §C2a.1.3]]).
> - Used next: Hamilton's equations ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^ex-c1-10-1|Example §C1.10.1]]), canonical quantization ([[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2a-1-1|Principle §C2a.1.1]]), the Hamiltonian in modes and its zero-point energy ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^thm-c2a-3-1|Theorem §C2a.3.1]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.10.3: Hamiltonian Density of the Free Complex Field
> For the free complex scalar field ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^mod-c1-9-7|Model §C1.9.7]]), with $\phi$ and $\phi^*$ as independent coordinates,
>
> $$
> \pi \equiv \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\phi} = \dot\phi^*, \qquad \pi^* \equiv \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\phi^*} = \dot\phi, \qquad \mathcal H = \pi^*\pi + \nabla\phi^*\cdot\nabla\phi + m^2\phi^*\phi ,
> $$
>
> the sum of two real-field densities ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1-10-2|Theorem §C1.10.2]]) for $\phi = (\phi_1 + i\phi_2)/\sqrt2$, $\pi = (\pi_1 - i\pi_2)/\sqrt2$. The momentum conjugate to $\phi$ is the velocity of $\phi^*$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.4 (Derivation "Several fields …", "The complex scalar, worked") · PHY 513 Problem Set 2, Problem 3(b), course solution (with $V = 0$) · PS Problem 2.2(a) (the Hamiltonian, as stated there)*

^thm-c1-10-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The density without indices.** $\partial_\mu\phi^{\ast}\,\partial^\mu\phi = \dot\phi^{\ast}\dot\phi - \sum_i\partial_i\phi^{\ast}\,\partial_i\phi$, so $\mathcal L = \dot\phi^{\ast}\dot\phi - \nabla\phi^{\ast}\cdot\nabla\phi - m^2\phi^{\ast}\phi$.
>
> **2. The momenta.** With $\phi$, $\phi^{\ast}$ independent ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1-9-9|Theorem §C1.9.9]]), $\dot\phi$ appears once, multiplied by $\dot\phi^{\ast}$: $\pi = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\phi = \dot\phi^{\ast}$; likewise $\pi^{\ast} = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\phi^{\ast} = \dot\phi$, which is indeed the complex conjugate of $\pi$. Inverted: $\dot\phi = \pi^{\ast}$, $\dot\phi^{\ast} = \pi$.
>
> **3. The Legendre transform in both velocities.** The transform trades all velocities at once, so it has one term per coordinate: $\pi\dot\phi + \pi^{\ast}\dot\phi^{\ast} - \mathcal L = \dot\phi^{\ast}\dot\phi + \dot\phi\dot\phi^{\ast} - \dot\phi^{\ast}\dot\phi + \nabla\phi^{\ast}\cdot\nabla\phi + m^2\phi^{\ast}\phi = \dot\phi^{\ast}\dot\phi + \nabla\phi^{\ast}\cdot\nabla\phi + m^2\phi^{\ast}\phi$.
>
> **4. Eliminate the velocities.** $\dot\phi^{\ast}\dot\phi = \pi\,\pi^{\ast}$ by step 2, which gives $\mathcal H$ of the statement. No factor $\frac12$ appears anywhere.
>
> **5. Real components.** With $\pi_j = \dot\phi_j$ the momenta of the real fields ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1-9-8|Theorem §C1.9.8]], [[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1-10-2|Theorem §C1.10.2]]), $\pi = \dot\phi^{\ast} = (\dot\phi_1 - i\dot\phi_2)/\sqrt2 = (\pi_1 - i\pi_2)/\sqrt2$. Then $\pi^{\ast}\pi = \frac12(\pi_1 + i\pi_2)(\pi_1 - i\pi_2) = \frac12(\pi_1^2 + \pi_2^2)$, $\nabla\phi^{\ast}\cdot\nabla\phi = \frac12\bigl((\nabla\phi_1)^2 + (\nabla\phi_2)^2\bigr)$ and $m^2\phi^{\ast}\phi = \frac12m^2(\phi_1^2 + \phi_2^2)$, the cross terms cancelling as in [[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^der-c1-9-8|Derivation §C1.9.8]]. Also $\pi\dot\phi + \pi^{\ast}\dot\phi^{\ast} = 2\operatorname{Re}(\pi\dot\phi) = \pi_1\dot\phi_1 + \pi_2\dot\phi_2$: the two Legendre transforms are the same. ⚑ By-product: the pairing crosses over, $\pi = \dot\phi^{\ast}$: the canonical partner of $\phi$ is the velocity of $\phi^{\ast}$, while $\pi^{\ast} = \dot\phi$ is built from the same variables as $\phi$; after quantization $\phi$ and $\pi^\dagger$ both contain $a_{\mathbf p}$ and $b^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$, and $\pi$ contains $a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$ and $b_{\mathbf p}$ → [[§C2a.5 The Complex Scalar Field and Its Charge#^mod-c2a-5-1|Model §C2a.5.1]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - $\mathcal H \ge 0$, a sum of squared moduli, and it equals two real Hamiltonians of the same mass.
> - Assumption: $\phi$ and $\phi^*$ treated as independent coordinates, an invertible change of variables.
> - Used next: the quantized complex field ([[§C2a.5 The Complex Scalar Field and Its Charge#^thm-c2a-5-4|Theorem §C2a.5.4]]); for the dagger notation of the quantum field, $\phi^* \to \phi^\dagger$.

> [!remark] Remark: Several fields, and a non-canonical normalization
> Every construction is "one of each per independent field component": one momentum per component, differentiating with respect to that component's velocity only; one Legendre term per component; one pair of Hamilton's equations per component; cross terms in $\mathcal L$ put pieces of several fields into one $\pi_a$. If a kinetic term is written $\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi$ without the $\frac12$ (Problem Set 2, Problem 2), then $\pi = 2\dot\phi$, $\mathcal H = \frac14\pi^2 + (\nabla\phi)^2 + \dots$, and the Euler–Lagrange equation carries an overall factor $2$ that must be divided out before masses and couplings are read off. Nothing is wrong, but every later factor of $2$ traces to the choice; rescaling $\phi \to \phi/\sqrt2$ restores the canonical form, which is why the $\frac12$ is standard.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.4 (Derivation "Several fields in the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formalisms": "Non-canonical normalization (Problem Set 2)") · PHY 513 Problem Set 2, Problem 2(b)–(c), course solution*

## Hamilton's equations

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.10.4: Hamilton's Equations for Fields
> Let $\mathcal H = \mathcal H(\phi_a, \nabla\phi_a, \pi_a)$ be $C^2$. Stationarity of the phase-space action $S[\phi, \pi] = \int d^4x\,\bigl(\sum_a\pi_a\dot\phi_a - \mathcal H\bigr)$ under independent variations of $\phi_a$ and $\pi_a$ is equivalent to
>
> $$
> \dot\phi_a = \frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial\pi_a} = \frac{\delta H}{\delta\pi_a(\mathbf x)}, \qquad \dot\pi_a = -\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial\phi_a} + \nabla\cdot\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial(\nabla\phi_a)} = -\frac{\delta H}{\delta\phi_a(\mathbf x)} ,
> $$
>
> and, when $\mathcal H$ is the Legendre transform of $\mathcal L$ ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^def-c1-10-1|Def. §C1.10.1]]), these $2n$ first-order equations are equivalent to the $n$ Euler–Lagrange equations ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1-9-4|Theorem §C1.9.4]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.4 (Derivation "Hamilton's equations for fields", eq. (Hamiltonfield); Derivation "Euler–Lagrange and Hamilton's equations in mechanics") · Yu §1.6.2, eqs. (1.171)–(1.175) · the user's pre-course notes, §1.6 ("canonical field equations")*

^thm-c1-10-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Vary.** Take a slab $R$ and test functions $\eta_a$, $\zeta_a$ supported inside it; $\phi_a \to \phi_a + \varepsilon\eta_a$, $\pi_a \to \pi_a + \varepsilon\zeta_a$ independently. At fixed argument, $\delta\dot\phi_a = \partial_t\delta\phi_a$ and $\delta(\partial_i\phi_a) = \partial_i\delta\phi_a$ ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1-9-2|Theorem §C1.9.2]]); $\mathcal H$ contains no derivatives of $\pi$. Differentiating under the integral ([[§CA.1 Exchanging Limits, Derivatives and Integrals#^thm-ca-1-1|Theorem §CA.1.1]]),
>
> $$
> \delta S = \int_Rd^4x\,\sum_a\Bigl[\dot\phi_a\,\zeta_a + \pi_a\,\partial_t\eta_a - \frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial\phi_a}\eta_a - \frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial(\partial_i\phi_a)}\partial_i\eta_a - \frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial\pi_a}\zeta_a\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> **2. Move the time derivative.** $\pi_a\,\partial_t\eta_a = \partial_t(\pi_a\eta_a) - \dot\pi_a\,\eta_a$.
>
> **3. Move the space derivatives.** $\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial(\partial_i\phi_a)}\partial_i\eta_a = \partial_i\Bigl(\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial(\partial_i\phi_a)}\eta_a\Bigr) - \Bigl(\partial_i\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial(\partial_i\phi_a)}\Bigr)\eta_a$, summed over $i$.
>
> **4. Drop the divergences.** $\partial_t(\pi_a\eta_a)$ and $\partial_i(\cdots\eta_a)$ are derivatives of functions with compact support inside $R$; their integrals vanish ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-1|452 Thm. §17.1]]; as in [[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^der-c1-9-4|Derivation §C1.9.4]], step 4). What remains is
>
> $$
> \delta S = \int_Rd^4x\,\sum_a\Bigl[\Bigl(\dot\phi_a - \frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial\pi_a}\Bigr)\zeta_a - \Bigl(\dot\pi_a + \frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial\phi_a} - \partial_i\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial(\partial_i\phi_a)}\Bigr)\eta_a\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> **5. Localize.** $\zeta_a$ and $\eta_a$ are independent and arbitrary, so by the fundamental lemma ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^der-c1-9-4|Derivation §C1.9.4]], step 6) both brackets vanish: the two equations, with $\partial_i(\partial\mathcal H/\partial(\partial_i\phi_a)) = \nabla\cdot\partial\mathcal H/\partial(\nabla\phi_a)$. ⚑ By-product: the divergence term has no particle analogue; it is there because $\mathcal H$ couples neighbouring points through $\nabla\phi$.
>
> **6. Functional derivatives of H.** On one slice, vary $\phi_a \to \phi_a + \varepsilon\eta_a$ with $\eta$ a test function on $\mathbb R^3$ and $\pi$ fixed: $\delta H = \int d^3x\,\sum_a\bigl[\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial\phi_a}\eta_a + \frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial(\partial_i\phi_a)}\partial_i\eta_a\bigr] = \int d^3x\,\sum_a\bigl[\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial\phi_a} - \partial_i\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial(\partial_i\phi_a)}\bigr]\eta_a$, the surface term vanishing by the three-dimensional rule ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-8|Theorem §CA.3.8]]) because $\eta$ has compact support. So $\delta H/\delta\phi_a = \partial\mathcal H/\partial\phi_a - \nabla\cdot\partial\mathcal H/\partial(\nabla\phi_a)$; varying $\pi$ needs no integration by parts, $\delta H/\delta\pi_a = \partial\mathcal H/\partial\pi_a$. Both are continuous functions, so the kernels are unique ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-1|Theorem §CA.2.1]], 2).
>
> **7. Back to Euler–Lagrange: the first equation.** Let $\mathcal H(\pi) = \sum_b\pi_b\dot\phi_b(\pi) - \mathcal L(\dot\phi(\pi))$ with $\dot\phi(\pi)$ the inverse of $\pi = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\phi$ (other arguments passive). By the chain rule $\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial\pi_a} = \dot\phi_a + \sum_b\pi_b\frac{\partial\dot\phi_b}{\partial\pi_a} - \sum_b\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\phi_b}\frac{\partial\dot\phi_b}{\partial\pi_a} = \dot\phi_a$, since $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\phi_b = \pi_b$. So the first Hamilton equation says that $\dot\phi$ is the velocity belonging to $\pi$, i.e. $\pi_a = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\phi_a$ along the solution ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-1|CM Theorem §B7.1.1]]).
>
> **8. The second equation.** With the same chain rule at fixed $\pi$, the passive arguments $u = \phi_a$ or $\partial_i\phi_a$ give $\frac{\partial\mathcal H}{\partial u}\Big|_\pi = \sum_b\pi_b\frac{\partial\dot\phi_b}{\partial u} - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial u}\Big|_{\dot\phi} - \sum_b\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\phi_b}\frac{\partial\dot\phi_b}{\partial u} = -\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial u}\Big|_{\dot\phi}$. The second equation becomes $\partial_t\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\phi_a} = \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi_a} - \partial_i\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_i\phi_a)}$, i.e. $\partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)} - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi_a} = 0$ with $\mu = 0$ and $\mu = i$ recombined. Conversely, a solution of the Euler–Lagrange equations with $\pi \equiv \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\phi$ satisfies both Hamilton equations by steps 7–8 read backwards.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Two first-order equations in twice the variables replace one second-order equation, as in mechanics; the novelty is the divergence term, i.e. that the force is the functional derivative $-\delta H/\delta\phi$.
> - Assumptions: $\mathcal H \in C^2$; the Legendre map invertible; the variations of the phase-space action have compact support (in $\pi$ as well as $\phi$, a slight strengthening of the particle case, where $p$ is free at the ends: [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-3|CM Theorem §B7.1.3]]).
> - Used next: the bracket form ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1-10-6|Theorem §C1.10.6]]) and the Heisenberg equations of the quantum field, which are these equations with operators in place of functions ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-3|Theorem §C2b.1.3]]).

> [!example] Example §C1.10.1: Hamilton's Equations of the Free Real Field
> For $\mathcal H = \frac12\pi^2 + \frac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \frac12m^2\phi^2$ ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1-10-2|Theorem §C1.10.2]]): $\partial\mathcal H/\partial\pi = \pi$, $\partial\mathcal H/\partial\phi = m^2\phi$, $\partial\mathcal H/\partial(\partial_i\phi) = \partial_i\phi$, so by [[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1-10-4|Theorem §C1.10.4]]
>
> $$
> \dot\phi = \pi, \qquad \dot\pi = -m^2\phi + \sum_i\partial_i\partial_i\phi = \nabla^2\phi - m^2\phi .
> $$
>
> The gradient term contributed through the divergence: $\delta H/\delta\phi = m^2\phi - \nabla^2\phi$, the integration by parts of [[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^der-c1-10-4|Derivation §C1.10.4]], step 6. Differentiating the first equation in time and inserting the second: $\ddot\phi = \dot\pi = \nabla^2\phi - m^2\phi$, i.e. $(\partial_t^2 - \nabla^2 + m^2)\phi = 0$, the Klein–Gordon equation again, now as two first-order equations: the form quantum mechanics wants.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.4 (the scalar-field example after eq. (Hamiltonfield))*

## Poisson brackets

> [!definition] Definition §C1.10.2: Poisson Bracket of Field Functionals
> For functionals $F$, $G$ of the canonical data on one slice whose functional derivatives exist ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^def-c1-9-2|Def. §C1.9.2]]), the **Poisson bracket** is
>
> $$
> \{F, G\} = \sum_a\int d^3z\,\Bigl(\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi_a(\mathbf z)}\,\frac{\delta G}{\delta\pi_a(\mathbf z)} - \frac{\delta F}{\delta\pi_a(\mathbf z)}\,\frac{\delta G}{\delta\phi_a(\mathbf z)}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> defined whenever the products can be integrated (for instance, one factor of each product a test function). The sign convention is that of [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-1|CM Def. §B8.1.1]], with the sum over $i$ replaced by $\sum_a\int d^3z$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.4 (Derivation "Several fields …": "Canonical brackets") · the user's pre-course notes, §1.6 (field Poisson bracket, referenced) · the functional form written out here*

^def-c1-10-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.10.5: Fundamental Brackets of Fields
> For real test functions $f$, $g$ on $\mathbb R^3$ and the smeared data $\phi_a(f) = \int d^3x\,f\,\phi_a$, $\pi_b(g) = \int d^3x\,g\,\pi_b$,
>
> $$
> \{\phi_a(f), \pi_b(g)\} = \delta_{ab}\int d^3x\,f(\mathbf x)\,g(\mathbf x), \qquad \{\phi_a(f), \phi_b(g)\} = \{\pi_a(f), \pi_b(g)\} = 0 ;
> $$
>
> in kernel form, $\{\phi_a(\mathbf x), \pi_b(\mathbf y)\} = \delta_{ab}\,\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$, an identity of distributions in $(\mathbf x, \mathbf y)$. For the complex field, $\{\phi, \pi\} = \{\phi^*, \pi^*\} = \delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$ and $\{\phi, \pi^*\} = \{\phi, \phi^*\} = 0$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.4 (Derivation "Several fields …": "One bracket per conjugate pair, all others zero: {φᵢ(x), πⱼ(y)} = δᵢⱼδ³(x − y)"; "The nonvanishing brackets are {φ, π} = {φ*, π*} = δ³, with {φ, π*} = 0") · the smeared form written out here*

^thm-c1-10-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Derivatives of the smeared data.** $\phi_a(f)$ is linear in $\phi$: replacing $\phi_c \to \phi_c + \varepsilon\eta_c$ changes it by $\varepsilon\,\delta_{ac}\int f\eta_c$, and $\pi$ does not enter. So $\delta\phi_a(f)/\delta\phi_c(\mathbf z) = \delta_{ac}\,f(\mathbf z)$ and $\delta\phi_a(f)/\delta\pi_c(\mathbf z) = 0$. Likewise $\delta\pi_b(g)/\delta\pi_c(\mathbf z) = \delta_{bc}\,g(\mathbf z)$, $\delta\pi_b(g)/\delta\phi_c(\mathbf z) = 0$.
>
> **2. The mixed bracket.** Insert into [[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^def-c1-10-2|Def. §C1.10.2]]: $\{\phi_a(f), \pi_b(g)\} = \sum_c\int d^3z\,\bigl(\delta_{ac}f(\mathbf z)\,\delta_{bc}g(\mathbf z) - 0\cdot0\bigr) = \delta_{ab}\int d^3z\,f\,g$. The sum over $c$ eliminates $c$ against $\delta_{ac}$.
>
> **3. The other two.** In $\{\phi_a(f), \phi_b(g)\}$ every term contains a derivative with respect to $\pi$ of a functional of $\phi$ alone, which is zero; likewise for $\{\pi_a(f), \pi_b(g)\}$.
>
> **4. Kernel form.** The bilinear map $(f, g) \mapsto \delta_{ab}\int f\,g$ is the pairing of $\delta_{ab}\,\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$ with $f(\mathbf x)g(\mathbf y)$ (change of variables $\mathbf u = \mathbf x - \mathbf y$, Jacobian $1$: [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^der-c2a-1-2|Derivation §C2a.1.2]], step 2, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-5|Def. §CA.2.5]]); by the kernel theorem ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-16|Theorem §CA.2.16]]) this kernel is unique. Unsmeared, the same follows formally from $\delta\phi_a(\mathbf x)/\delta\phi_c(\mathbf z) = \delta_{ac}\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf z)$ ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^rem-c1-9-2|Remark: The functional derivative is a distribution]]): $\int d^3z\,\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf z)\delta^3(\mathbf y - \mathbf z) = \delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$, a convolution of two deltas, which is defined. ⚑ By-product: at coinciding points the bracket would be $\delta^3(\mathbf 0)$, which has no value ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-8|Theorem §CA.2.8]]); the field at a point is not a canonical coordinate, only its smearings are → [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^thm-c2a-1-2|Theorem §C2a.1.2]].
>
> **5. The complex field.** In real components, $\{\phi, \pi\} = \frac12\{\phi_1 + i\phi_2, \pi_1 - i\pi_2\} = \frac12\bigl(\{\phi_1, \pi_1\} - i\{\phi_1, \pi_2\} + i\{\phi_2, \pi_1\} + \{\phi_2, \pi_2\}\bigr) = \frac12(\delta^3 - 0 + 0 + \delta^3) = \delta^3$, using bilinearity and $i\cdot(-i) = 1$. And $\{\phi, \pi^{\ast}\} = \frac12\{\phi_1 + i\phi_2, \pi_1 + i\pi_2\} = \frac12(\{\phi_1, \pi_1\} + i^2\{\phi_2, \pi_2\}) = \frac12(\delta^3 - \delta^3) = 0$; $\{\phi, \phi^{\ast}\} = 0$ since it contains no momentum. $\{\phi^{\ast}, \pi^{\ast}\}$ is the conjugate of $\{\phi, \pi\}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The continuum bracket is the discrete $\{q_i, p_j\} = \delta_{ij}$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-1|CM Theorem §B8.1.1]]) with the cells' $\delta_{ij}/\Delta V$ become a delta function ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^der-c1-10-1|Derivation §C1.10.1]], step 4); it is an identity between smeared quantities.
> - For a complex field the bracket pairs $\phi$ with $\pi = \dot\phi^*$; $\phi$ and $\pi^*$ have zero bracket.
> - Used next: canonical quantization replaces these brackets by $-i$ times commutators ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^rem-c1-10-5|Remark: From brackets to commutators]], [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2a-1-1|Principle §C2a.1.1]], [[§C2a.5 The Complex Scalar Field and Its Charge#^mod-c2a-5-1|Model §C2a.5.1]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.10.6: Time Evolution by the Bracket
> Along a solution of Hamilton's equations, every functional $F[\phi, \pi]$ without explicit time dependence, whose functional derivatives exist and fall off at infinity, evolves by
>
> $$
> \frac{dF}{dt} = \{F, H\} .
> $$
>
> In particular $\frac{d}{dt}\phi_a(f) = \{\phi_a(f), H\}$ and $\frac{d}{dt}\pi_a(g) = \{\pi_a(g), H\}$, in kernel form $\dot\phi_a(\mathbf x) = \{\phi_a(\mathbf x), H\}$, $\dot\pi_a(\mathbf x) = \{\pi_a(\mathbf x), H\}$; and $dH/dt = \{H, H\} = 0$.
>
> *Source: the particle version [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|CM Theorem §B8.1.3]] · the user's pre-course notes, §1.6 ("written with functional derivatives and the field Poisson bracket, the two equations collapse to the same form as their mechanics parents") · the field form written out here*

^thm-c1-10-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Chain rule for functionals.** Along a solution, $\phi_a(t + h) = \phi_a(t) + h\,\dot\phi_a(t) + o(h)$ and likewise $\pi_a$; by the definition of the first variation, with the direction $(\dot\phi, \dot\pi)$ in place of $(\eta, \zeta)$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dF}{dt} = \sum_a\int d^3z\,\Bigl(\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi_a(\mathbf z)}\,\dot\phi_a(\mathbf z) + \frac{\delta F}{\delta\pi_a(\mathbf z)}\,\dot\pi_a(\mathbf z)\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> The direction $(\dot\phi, \dot\pi)$ is not a test function; the formula holds when the functional derivatives of $F$ fall off fast enough for the integrals to converge (for smeared data they are test functions, step 3).
>
> **2. Insert Hamilton's equations.** $\dot\phi_a = \delta H/\delta\pi_a$, $\dot\pi_a = -\delta H/\delta\phi_a$ ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1-10-4|Theorem §C1.10.4]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{dF}{dt} = \sum_a\int d^3z\,\Bigl(\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi_a}\frac{\delta H}{\delta\pi_a} - \frac{\delta F}{\delta\pi_a}\frac{\delta H}{\delta\phi_a}\Bigr) = \{F, H\}
> $$
>
> by [[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^def-c1-10-2|Def. §C1.10.2]].
>
> **3. The smeared field.** With $\delta\phi_a(f)/\delta\phi_c = \delta_{ac}f$, $\delta\phi_a(f)/\delta\pi_c = 0$ ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^der-c1-10-5|Derivation §C1.10.5]], step 1): $\{\phi_a(f), H\} = \int d^3z\,f\,\delta H/\delta\pi_a = \int d^3z\,f\,\dot\phi_a$. For the free real field this is $\int f\pi$, i.e. $\dot\phi = \pi$. With $\delta\pi_a(g)/\delta\pi_c = \delta_{ac}g$ and $\delta\pi_a(g)/\delta\phi_c = 0$ (same step 1), only the second term of the bracket survives: $\{\pi_a(g), H\} = -\int d^3z\,g\,\delta H/\delta\phi_a = \int d^3z\,g\,\dot\pi_a$, which for the free field is $\int g\,(\nabla^2\phi - m^2\phi)$ ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^ex-c1-10-1|Example §C1.10.1]]).
>
> **4. Conservation of H.** The bracket is antisymmetric, so $\{H, H\} = 0$. ⚑ By-product: the energy is conserved because $\mathcal H$ has no explicit time dependence; in the language of [[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum#^thm-c1-12-6|Theorem §C1.12.6]] it is the Noether charge of time translations, and $H$ generates them through the bracket, as in mechanics ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-5|CM Theorem §B8.1.5]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The field bracket reproduces the particle structure exactly: dynamics is the bracket with $H$.
> - Assumption: the functional derivatives of $F$ fall off at infinity; for $H$ itself the falloff of the fields is what makes $H$ finite.
> - Used next: quantized, $\{F, H\} \to -i[F, H]$ becomes the Heisenberg equation $\dot F = i[H, F]$ ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-3|Theorem §C2b.1.3]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Constraints and first-order Lagrangians
> "One momentum per component" can fail in two instructive ways. For the vector field, $\pi^\mu = \partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_0A_\mu) = F^{\mu0}$ for $\mathcal L = -\frac14F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}$, so the momentum conjugate to $A_i$ is $\pi^i = F^{i0} = E^i$ by [[§C1.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^def-c1-7-2|Def. §C1.7.2]] (conjugate to $A^i = -A_i$ it is $-E^i$), and $\pi^0 = 0$: $A_0$ has no velocity in $\mathcal L$, the Legendre map is not invertible, and Gauss's law is a constraint. Gauge fields are where "one per component" miscounts the physical degrees of freedom: components related by a gauge transformation are not independent, which is why the photon has two polarizations, not four (QFT C4: [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-5|Theorem §C4.5.5]], [[§C4.5 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-5-10|Theorem §C4.5.10]]; for the massive field [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]], [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-12|Theorem §C4.2.12]]; C8 planned). For a Dirac field the Lagrangian is first order in time and $\pi = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\psi = i\psi^\dagger$: the momentum conjugate to $\psi$ is essentially $\psi^\dagger$, the crossover of the complex scalar ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1-10-3|Theorem §C1.10.3]]) taken one step further ([[§C5a.3 The Dirac Equation and Its Lagrangian#^thm-c5a-3-8|Theorem §C5a.3.8]]; [[§C5b.1 Quantizing the Dirac Field#^rem-c5b-1-1|§C5b.1, Remark: A first-order system: the momentum is ψ† itself]]).
>

> [!remark] Remark: π multiplies the endpoint variation
> In mechanics the boundary term of the varied action is $[p\,\delta q]_{t_1}^{t_2}$; the conjugate momentum is what multiplies the endpoint variation, and along true paths $\partial S/\partial q(t_2) = p(t_2)$, the starting point of Hamilton–Jacobi theory ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation|CM §B8.3]]). For fields the time components of the surface term of [[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^der-c1-9-4|Derivation §C1.9.4]], step 4, are $\int d^3x\,\pi\,\delta\phi\big|_{t_1}^{t_2}$: on a solution, the action as a functional of the final configuration has $\delta S/\delta\phi(t_2, \mathbf x) = \pi(t_2, \mathbf x)$. This is the mechanical root of why $\pi$ appears in the surface terms of field theory, among them the Noether charges ([[§C1.11 Noether's Theorem#^def-c1-11-4|Def. §C1.11.4]]): the field form of $\partial S/\partial q = p$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 3 §3.4 (Derivation "Euler–Lagrange and Hamilton's equations in mechanics": "the boundary term is p δq … the mechanical root of why π appears in the surface terms of field theory") · Yu §1.6.1, eq. (1.155)*

> [!remark] Remark: From brackets to commutators
> Canonical quantization keeps the bracket algebra and changes the objects: $\{F, G\} \to -i[F, G]$ with $\hbar = 1$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^rem-b8-1-5|CM Remark: From brackets to commutators]]). Applied to [[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1-10-5|Theorem §C1.10.5]] it gives $[\phi_a(\mathbf x), \pi_b(\mathbf y)] = i\delta_{ab}\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$, the postulate of [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2a-1-1|Principle §C2a.1.1]], with the same reading as an identity after smearing ([[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^thm-c2a-1-2|Theorem §C2a.1.2]]); applied to [[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1-10-6|Theorem §C1.10.6]] it gives the Heisenberg equations $\dot\phi = i[H, \phi]$, which for the free field are the two equations of [[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^ex-c1-10-1|Example §C1.10.1]] with operators ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-3|Theorem §C2b.1.3]]). As in mechanics this is a prescription, not a derivation: products such as $\pi^2$ have no unique ordering once they are operators, which is where the zero-point energy and normal ordering enter ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^thm-c2a-3-3|Theorem §C2a.3.3]], [[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^def-c2a-3-1|Def. §C2a.3.1]]).
