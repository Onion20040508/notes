What exactly happens to a scalar, a four-vector, a tensor or a spinor field under a Lorentz transformation, and which of the representations $(j_+, j_-)$ is each? The general law $\phi'_a(x) = D(\Lambda)_a{}^b\phi_b(\Lambda^{-1}x)$, its group property and its infinitesimal form are [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]], [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^thm-c1b-1-1|Theorem §C1b.1.1]] and [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^thm-c1b-1-2|Theorem §C1b.1.2]] (with the scalar case [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-2|Def. §C1b.1.2]]); they are linked, not repeated. The representations themselves are [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields|§C3.2]], with their mathematics in [[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)|§CB.17]]; the further facts from CB used here (representations on functions, tensor products, the spinor generators, the double cover) are linked where they enter and listed at the end, and the rule for products of fields, which decides what a product of fields carries, is stated as a physics box before the two-index tensors. This section adds the representation-specific content: the orbital generators that move the argument and their algebra, the total generator as orbital plus spin, and, field by field, the matrix $D(\Lambda)$, its label $(j_+, j_-)$ and its rotation content, ending with the field strength's split into $\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B$. How unitary operators on states implement these laws for quantum fields is [[§C3.5 Covariant Quantum Fields and the Free Scalar's Representation#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]]; the spinor matrices are QFT C5a ([[§C5a.3 The Lorentz Action on Spinor Space|§C5a.3]], [[§C5a.4 SL(2,C) and the Group Action on Spinor Space|§C5a.4]]).

**Setup.** No dynamics and no Model box: every law here is a statement about a field $\phi_a(x)$ with a transformation law [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]], its index and its argument, for any field equation, free or interacting, and is exact. Read classically, $\phi_a$ is a function; for a quantum field operator the same $D(\Lambda)$ and the same move of the argument enter the operator law [[§C3.5 Covariant Quantum Fields and the Free Scalar's Representation#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]], which is where states and the vacuum come in. The field strength of Theorem §C3.3.8 is the classical $F^{\mu\nu}$ of [[§C1a.8 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^def-c1a-8-2|Def. §C1a.8.2]].

*Conventions* as in [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields|§C3.2]]: active reading, $\Lambda = e^\omega = \exp(-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$, $K_i = \mathcal J^{0i}$, $\theta_i = \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\omega_{jk}$, $\eta_i = \omega_{0i}$ ([[Larsen PHY 513]]).

## Orbital and spin generators

> [!definition] Definition §C3.3.1: Orbital Generators
> On functions of $x$, the **orbital generators** are the six differential operators
>
> $$
> L^{\mu\nu} = i\bigl(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu\bigr) = -L^{\nu\mu} ,
> $$
>
> three rotations $L_i = \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}L^{jk}$ and three boosts $L^{0i}$. They act on the argument of a field, not on its components.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.3, eq. (Jorbital) · PHY 513 Lecture 7, Part B ("Lorentz Algebra: Defining Representation") · PS §3.1, eq. (3.16) · Yu §4.2.1, eq. (4.22)*

^def-c3-3-1

The slides introduce these operators under the heading "defining representation"; they act on functions (an infinite-dimensional representation), while the defining representation on four-vectors is the $4\times4$ matrices $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-9|Theorem §C3.2.9]]). Peskin–Schroeder use them, as here, to compute the algebra. The three-dimensional $J^{ij} = -i(x^i\partial_j - x^j\partial_i)$ of the slides is the spatial part with Euclidean derivatives ([[§C3.1 Index Slots, Rotations and Spin in Field Theory#^cau-c3-1-2|§C3.1, Caution: The sign of the orbital generator]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.1: The Orbital Generators Move the Argument
> 1. For $\Lambda = e^\omega$ and a $C^2$ function $\phi$, to first order in $\omega$,
>
> $$
> \phi(\Lambda^{-1}x) = \Bigl(1 - \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}L^{\mu\nu}\Bigr)\phi(x) + O(\omega^2) ,
> $$
>
> with the same parameters and the same sign as $v \mapsto \Lambda v = (1 - \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})v$ on vectors.
> 2. $L_i = -i(\mathbf x\times\nabla)_i$, the orbital angular momentum $(\mathbf x\times\mathbf p)_i$ with the momentum acting on functions as $-i\nabla$; $L^{0i} = -i(t\,\partial_i + x^i\partial_t)$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.5 (Derivation "The generator of the scalar transformation law is the orbital operator"; checked symbolically there) · Yu §4.2.1, eqs. (4.19)–(4.26)*

^thm-c3-3-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The scalar law to first order.** Steps 1–5 of [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^der-c1b-1-2|Derivation §C1b.1.2]] with $a = 0$ give $\Lambda^{-1} = 1 - \omega + O(\omega^2)$, the Taylor expansion of $\phi$ at $x$, the lowering $\omega^\mu{}_\nu\partial_\mu = \omega_{\rho\nu}\partial^\rho$, and the antisymmetrization:
>
> $$
> \phi(\Lambda^{-1}x) = \phi(x) - \omega^\mu{}_\nu\,x^\nu\partial_\mu\phi(x) + O(\omega^2) = \phi(x) + \tfrac12\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu\bigr)\phi(x) + O(\omega^2) .
> $$
>
> **2. Insert the orbital generators.** By Def. §C3.3.1, $x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu = -iL^{\mu\nu}$ (multiply $L^{\mu\nu} = i(\cdots)$ by $-i$). So $\frac12\omega_{\mu\nu}(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu) = -\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}L^{\mu\nu}$, which is part 1.
>
> **3. Same sign as the vector representation.** By [[§C1a.7 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-7-2|Theorem §C1a.7.2]], $-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu} = \omega$ as a matrix, and $\Lambda v = v + \omega v + O(\omega^2)$. Both laws are "$1 - \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}(\text{generator})$" with the same $\omega_{\mu\nu}$; the inverse in $\phi(\Lambda^{-1}x)$ is absorbed by the sign of $L$. ⚑ By-product: the convention $L = +i(x\partial - x\partial)$, with $p_\mu = i\partial_\mu$, is forced by requiring the orbital and the matrix generators to enter the field law with the same $\omega$ → Theorem §C3.3.3.
>
> **4. Rotations.** $L_i = \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\,i(x^j\partial^k - x^k\partial^j)$. Rename $j \leftrightarrow k$ in the second term and use $\varepsilon_{ikj} = -\varepsilon_{ijk}$: $L_i = \frac i2(\varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\partial^k + \varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\partial^k) = i\varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\partial^k$. With $\partial^k = -\partial_k$ (one spatial index raised), $L_i = -i\varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\partial_k = -i(\mathbf x\times\nabla)_i$, with $-i\nabla$ the momentum acting on functions.
>
> **5. Boosts.** $L^{0i} = i(x^0\partial^i - x^i\partial^0) = i(-t\,\partial_i - x^i\partial_t)$, using $\partial^i = -\partial_i$, $\partial^0 = \partial_t$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The spatial generators are Quantum Mechanics' orbital angular momentum, which generates $\psi(\mathbf x) \mapsto \psi(R^{-1}\mathbf x)$ on wave functions ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-1|QM Theorem §C5.4.1]]); the boosts are new and mix $t$ with $\mathbf x$.
> - $s \mapsto \phi(e^{-s\omega}x)$ is a one-parameter group of operators on functions with generator $-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}L^{\mu\nu}$; for a plane wave, $e^{-ip\cdot\Lambda^{-1}x} = e^{-i(\Lambda p)\cdot x}$, so the orbital operators rotate and boost momenta.
> - Used next: the algebra of the $L^{\mu\nu}$ (Theorem §C3.3.2) and the total generator of a field (Theorem §C3.3.3).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.2: The Orbital Generators Obey the Lorentz Algebra
> On $C^2$ functions (in particular on $\mathcal S(\mathbb R^4)$, which the $L^{\mu\nu}$ map into itself),
>
> $$
> [L^{\mu\nu}, L^{\rho\sigma}] = i\bigl(g^{\nu\rho}L^{\mu\sigma} - g^{\mu\rho}L^{\nu\sigma} - g^{\nu\sigma}L^{\mu\rho} + g^{\mu\sigma}L^{\nu\rho}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> These are the relations of [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]: the orbital generators are one more realization of the Lorentz algebra, on the infinite-dimensional space of functions (moving the argument is a representation on functions, [[§CB.7 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^thm-cb-7-23|Theorem §CB.7.23]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.3 (Derivation "Realization on functions: the orbital generators", "They obey the algebra") · PS §3.1, eqs. (3.16)–(3.17) ("simply compute the commutators of the differential operators")*

^thm-c3-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Write $\ell^{\mu\nu} = x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu$, so $L^{\mu\nu} = i\ell^{\mu\nu}$; act on a $C^2$ function $f$.
>
> **1. One elementary commutator.** By the product rule and $\partial^\nu x^\rho = g^{\nu\rho}$,
>
> $$
> x^\mu\partial^\nu\bigl(x^\rho\partial^\sigma f\bigr) = g^{\nu\rho}x^\mu\partial^\sigma f + x^\mu x^\rho\partial^\nu\partial^\sigma f, \qquad x^\rho\partial^\sigma\bigl(x^\mu\partial^\nu f\bigr) = g^{\sigma\mu}x^\rho\partial^\nu f + x^\rho x^\mu\partial^\sigma\partial^\nu f .
> $$
>
> Subtract: the second-derivative terms cancel ($f \in C^2$, so $\partial^\nu\partial^\sigma f = \partial^\sigma\partial^\nu f$), leaving
>
> $$
> C(\mu\nu; \rho\sigma) \equiv [x^\mu\partial^\nu, x^\rho\partial^\sigma] = g^{\nu\rho}x^\mu\partial^\sigma - g^{\mu\sigma}x^\rho\partial^\nu .
> $$
>
> **2. Expand into four commutators.** $[\ell^{\mu\nu}, \ell^{\rho\sigma}] = C(\mu\nu; \rho\sigma) - C(\mu\nu; \sigma\rho) - C(\nu\mu; \rho\sigma) + C(\nu\mu; \sigma\rho)$. From step 1, with the labels substituted in each:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> C(\mu\nu; \rho\sigma) &= g^{\nu\rho}x^\mu\partial^\sigma - g^{\mu\sigma}x^\rho\partial^\nu, & -C(\mu\nu; \sigma\rho) &= -g^{\nu\sigma}x^\mu\partial^\rho + g^{\mu\rho}x^\sigma\partial^\nu, \\
> -C(\nu\mu; \rho\sigma) &= -g^{\mu\rho}x^\nu\partial^\sigma + g^{\nu\sigma}x^\rho\partial^\mu, & C(\nu\mu; \sigma\rho) &= g^{\mu\sigma}x^\nu\partial^\rho - g^{\nu\rho}x^\sigma\partial^\mu .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> **3. Collect the eight terms by their metric factor.** $g^{\nu\rho}(x^\mu\partial^\sigma - x^\sigma\partial^\mu) = g^{\nu\rho}\ell^{\mu\sigma}$; $g^{\mu\sigma}(x^\nu\partial^\rho - x^\rho\partial^\nu) = g^{\mu\sigma}\ell^{\nu\rho}$; $g^{\nu\sigma}(x^\rho\partial^\mu - x^\mu\partial^\rho) = -g^{\nu\sigma}\ell^{\mu\rho}$; $g^{\mu\rho}(x^\sigma\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\sigma) = -g^{\mu\rho}\ell^{\nu\sigma}$. Hence
>
> $$
> [\ell^{\mu\nu}, \ell^{\rho\sigma}] = g^{\nu\rho}\ell^{\mu\sigma} - g^{\mu\rho}\ell^{\nu\sigma} - g^{\nu\sigma}\ell^{\mu\rho} + g^{\mu\sigma}\ell^{\nu\rho} .
> $$
>
> **4. Restore i.** $[L^{\mu\nu}, L^{\rho\sigma}] = i^2[\ell^{\mu\nu}, \ell^{\rho\sigma}] = i\bigl(g^{\nu\rho}\,i\ell^{\mu\sigma} - \cdots\bigr) = i\bigl(g^{\nu\rho}L^{\mu\sigma} - g^{\mu\rho}L^{\nu\sigma} - g^{\nu\sigma}L^{\mu\rho} + g^{\mu\sigma}L^{\nu\rho}\bigr)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The real operators $\ell^{\mu\nu}$ obey exactly the relations of the real matrices $M^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§CB.5 The Lorentz Algebra꞉ Generators and Commutation Relations#^der-cb-5-7b|Derivation §CB.5.7, second route]], step 6), as the group-law derivation predicts: functions of $x$ carry a representation, and the algebra does not care that it is infinite-dimensional.
> - Assumption used: equality of mixed second derivatives. For distributions the same identity holds by transposition ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^rem-c3-3-2|Remark: The laws for fields that are distributions]]).
> - The rotation part reproduces Quantum Mechanics' $[\hat L_i, \hat L_j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat L_k$ for $\hat L_i = \varepsilon_{ijk}\hat x_j\hat p_k$ ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-1|QM Theorem §C5.4.1]]).

What the representation theory adds is the Hermitian form of the same law: the generators of the two parts as a single operator obeying the Lorentz algebra, with the dictionary between $S^{\mu\nu}$ and $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.3: Orbital Plus Spin
> For a field $\phi_a$ transforming with $D$ ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]]) and $\Lambda = e^\omega$, the infinitesimal law of [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^thm-c1b-1-2|Theorem §C1b.1.2]] (with $a = 0$) reads, with Hermitian generators,
>
> $$
> \phi'_a(x) = \phi_a(x) - \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl(\mathbb J^{\mu\nu}\phi\bigr)_a(x), \qquad \mathbb J^{\mu\nu} = L^{\mu\nu}\,\delta_a{}^b + D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b ,
> $$
>
> an **orbital** part acting on the argument and a **spin** part acting on the index. The two parts commute, $\mathbb J^{\mu\nu}$ obeys the Lorentz algebra, and the spin generators of Def. §C1b.1.3 are $S^{\mu\nu} = -iD(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$. Under rotations, $\mathbb J_i = L_i + J_i$: total angular momentum is orbital plus spin.
>
> *Model:* any field with a transformation law ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]]); *applies:* as in the opening.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.5 (paragraph "Fields with indices": "an orbital part acting on the argument plus a spin part acting on the indices") · PHY 513 Lecture 7, Part C · Yu §4.2.2, eqs. (4.31)–(4.32)*

^thm-c3-3-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The general law to first order.** [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^thm-c1b-1-2|Theorem §C1b.1.2]] with $a = 0$: $\phi'_a = \phi_a + \frac12\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl[(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu)\delta_a{}^b + (S^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b\bigr]\phi_b + O(\omega^2)$, where $D(\Lambda) = 1 + \frac12\omega_{\mu\nu}S^{\mu\nu} + O(\omega^2)$.
>
> **2. Orbital part.** $\frac12\omega_{\mu\nu}(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu) = -\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}L^{\mu\nu}$ (Theorem §C3.3.1, step 2).
>
> **3. Spin part.** By [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]], $D(\Lambda) = 1 - \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}) + O(\omega^2)$. Comparing with step 1, $\frac12 S^{\mu\nu} = -\frac i2D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ (the coefficients of the arbitrary antisymmetric $\omega_{\mu\nu}$, both antisymmetric, must agree), so $S^{\mu\nu} = -iD(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$, i.e. $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}) = iS^{\mu\nu}$: the same relation as $\mathcal J = iM$ for the vector ([[§C1a.7 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-7-1|Def. §C1a.7.1]]).
>
> **4. The parts commute.** $D(\mathcal J^{\rho\sigma})$ is a constant matrix on the index, $L^{\mu\nu}$ a differential operator in $x$: $L^{\mu\nu}\bigl(D(\mathcal J^{\rho\sigma})_a{}^b\phi_b\bigr) = D(\mathcal J^{\rho\sigma})_a{}^b\,L^{\mu\nu}\phi_b$.
>
> **5. The algebra.** Expand $[L^{\mu\nu} + D^{\mu\nu}, L^{\rho\sigma} + D^{\rho\sigma}]$ (with $D^{\mu\nu} \equiv D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$) into four commutators; the two mixed ones vanish by step 4, leaving $[L^{\mu\nu}, L^{\rho\sigma}] + [D^{\mu\nu}, D^{\rho\sigma}]$. Each obeys the algebra ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]]; [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]] for $D$), and the right side of the algebra is linear in the generators, so the sum is $i(g^{\nu\rho}(L^{\mu\sigma} + D^{\mu\sigma}) - \cdots)$.
>
> **6. Rotations.** $\mathbb J_i = \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\mathbb J^{jk} = L_i + J_i$ by linearity, with $L_i = -i(\mathbf x\times\nabla)_i$ (Theorem §C3.3.1, 2). ⚑ By-product: the total generator is the generator of a tensor product, functions $\otimes$ components, and the generators of a product add, as for finite-dimensional products ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-6|Theorem §C3.3.6]], 1) → [[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^rem-c3-3-1|Remark: Three realizations of one algebra]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Only the spin part distinguishes one kind of field from another; the orbital part is universal.
> - The factor $i$ between $S^{\mu\nu}$ (real convention, used in Noether's theorem) and $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ (Hermitian convention, used for representations) is the only translation needed between §C1b.1, §C1b.8 and this chapter.
> - Used next: field by field below; the Noether current's spin part ([[§C1b.8 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor#^thm-c1b-8-1|Theorem §C1b.8.1]]); the quantum version ([[§C3.5 Covariant Quantum Fields and the Free Scalar's Representation#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Three realizations of one algebra
> The same six generators appear three times. $L^{\mu\nu}$ acts on the argument $x$ (infinite-dimensional); $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ acts on the index $a$ (finite-dimensional, not unitary, [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-7|Theorem §C3.2.7]]); and, once the field is an operator, the Hilbert-space generators $\hat J^{\mu\nu}$ act on states (unitary, [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations and the Poincaré Algebra#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]). A classical field feels the first two together ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^rem-c1b-1-2|§C1b.1, Remark: Two representations at once]]); a quantum field feels all three, which meet in one commutator, $[\hat\Phi_a(x), \hat J^{\mu\nu}] = L^{\mu\nu}\hat\Phi_a(x) + D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b\hat\Phi_b(x)$ (the hat marks the operator on the Hilbert space; the unhatted $L^{\mu\nu}$ and $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ act on the argument and on the index; Yu's "three identities" of a quantum field; derived in [[§C3.5 Covariant Quantum Fields and the Free Scalar's Representation#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]]). The list of symbols for the realizations is [[§C3.1 Index Slots, Rotations and Spin in Field Theory#^rem-c3-1-1|§C3.1, Remark: One symbol for each realization of the generators]].
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.1 (Principle "One symbol for each realization of the generators"), §7.5 (Derivation "The three identities of a quantum field") · Yu §4.2.2, after eq. (4.34)*

> [!remark] Remark: The laws for fields that are distributions
> Quantum fields, and classical objects such as $\delta^4(x)$ or the Wightman function, are generalized functions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-2|Def. §CA.2.2]]; operator-valued for quantum fields, [[§CA.3 Products of Distributions and Operator-Valued Distributions#^def-ca-3-1|Def. §CA.3.1]]); the laws of this section then hold as identities in $\mathcal S'$, defined by moving each operation onto the test function (a derivative: [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-7|Def. §CA.2.7]]; multiplication by the smooth function $x^\mu$: [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-8|Def. §CA.2.8]]). The argument change is the linear change of variables of [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-11|Def. §CA.2.11]] with $|\det\Lambda| = 1$: $\phi(\Lambda^{-1}\cdot)[f] = \phi[f\circ\Lambda]$. The orbital operators act by transposition, $(L^{\mu\nu}\phi)[f] = -\phi[L^{\mu\nu}f]$: from $(x^\mu\partial^\nu\phi)[f] = -\phi[\partial^\nu(x^\mu f)] = -\phi[g^{\mu\nu}f + x^\mu\partial^\nu f]$, the $g^{\mu\nu}$ terms cancel in the antisymmetric combination. $L^{\mu\nu}$ maps $\mathcal S$ into itself, so Theorem §C3.3.2 holds on $\mathcal S'$ with the order of products reversed twice by the transpositions. The scalar law's fixed points, $\phi(\Lambda^{-1}x) = \phi(x)$, are the Lorentz-invariant distributions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-12|Def. §CA.2.12]]; those on the mass shell and the light cone: [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-7|Theorem §CA.2.7]]), annihilated by every $L^{\mu\nu}$; the Wightman function is one ([[§C2b.2 The Wightman Function|§C2b.2]]).
>
> *Source: standard; written out here (Hörmander I, §§3.1, 6.1, as cited in CA)*

## The scalar field

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.4: A One-Component Field Carries the Trivial Representation
> Every one-dimensional representation of the Lorentz algebra is zero ([[§CB.5 The Lorentz Algebra꞉ Generators and Commutation Relations#^thm-cb-5-9|Theorem §CB.5.9]]), so a one-component field has $D(\Lambda) = 1$ for every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$: the representation $(0, 0)$. Its total generator is $L^{\mu\nu}$ alone, and under rotations it carries orbital angular momentum only.
>
> *Model:* any field with a transformation law ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]]); *applies:* as in the opening.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.5 (Principle "Components and functions: the scalar field carries the trivial representation") · PHY 513, Problem Set 5, Problem 1(b) (spin 0: "the generators are numbers, which commute", as the user wrote it) · Yu §4.2.1, eq. (4.27)*

^thm-c3-3-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Numbers commute.** In one dimension the six generators $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ are complex numbers, so every commutator of two of them vanishes.
>
> **2. The rotation generators vanish.** The relations $[J_i, J_j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}J_k$ of [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]] have vanishing left sides (step 1): $[J_1, J_2] = iJ_3$, $[J_2, J_3] = iJ_1$, $[J_3, J_1] = iJ_2$ give $J_3 = J_1 = J_2 = 0$, i.e. $\mathbf J = 0$.
>
> **3. The boost generators vanish.** The relations $[J_i, K_j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}K_k$ of the same theorem have vanishing left sides: $[J_1, K_2] = iK_3$, $[J_2, K_3] = iK_1$, $[J_3, K_1] = iK_2$ give $\mathbf K = 0$. Steps 1–3 are the derivation of [[§CB.5 The Lorentz Algebra꞉ Generators and Commutation Relations#^thm-cb-5-9|Theorem §CB.5.9]] (one-dimensional representations of the Lorentz algebra are zero).
>
> **4. The group elements.** $D(e^\omega) = \exp(-i\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf J - i\boldsymbol\eta\cdot\mathbf K) = e^0 = 1$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]]); every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$ is a product of exponentials ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-2|Theorem §C1a.4.2]] with [[§C1a.7 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-7-3|Theorem §C1a.7.3]]), so $D(\Lambda) = 1$. In [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]] this is $j_+ = j_- = 0$.
>
> **5. Total generator.** With $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}) = 0$, Theorem §C3.3.3 leaves $\mathbb J^{\mu\nu} = L^{\mu\nu}$ and $\mathbb J_i = L_i$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - "The scalar does not transform" refers to the index representation only; the field still changes through its argument, $\phi \mapsto \phi(\Lambda^{-1}x)$, generated by $L^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^cau-c1b-1-1|§C1b.1, Caution: What "the scalar does not change" does not mean]]).
> - Only the proper orthochronous group is constrained; parity may still act by a sign ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^rem-c3-3-3|Remark: Scalars, pseudoscalars and spin zero]]).
> - Used next: the free scalar field as a quantum scalar ([[§C3.5 Covariant Quantum Fields and the Free Scalar's Representation#^thm-c3-5-5|Theorem §C3.5.5]]) and spin-0 particles ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-9|Theorem §C3.6.9]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Scalars, pseudoscalars and spin zero
> The trivial representation fixes only the behaviour under $SO^+(1,3)$. Under parity a one-component field may still change sign, $D(\mathcal P) = +1$ for a scalar and $-1$ for a pseudoscalar; both are $(0, 0)$ ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^rem-c1b-1-2|§C1b.1, Remark: Two representations at once]]; among fermion bilinears $\bar\psi\psi$ is a scalar and $\bar\psi\,i\gamma^5\psi$ a pseudoscalar, [[§C5a.6 The Dirac Conjugate and the Bilinears#^def-c5a-6-3|Def. §C5a.6.3]], [[§C5a.6 The Dirac Conjugate and the Bilinears#^thm-c5a-6-3|Theorem §C5a.6.3]]). The quanta of a scalar field have spin $0$ (made precise for states in [[§C3.5 Covariant Quantum Fields and the Free Scalar's Representation#^thm-c3-5-5|Theorem §C3.5.5]] and [[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-9|Theorem §C3.6.9]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.5: Vector Fields, Upper and Lower Index
> 1. A vector field transforms with $D = \Lambda$: $A'^\mu(x) = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu A^\nu(\Lambda^{-1}x)$, with spin generators $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$, so infinitesimally
>
> $$
> \delta A^\alpha = -\frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl(L^{\mu\nu}A^\alpha + (\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})^\alpha{}_\beta A^\beta\bigr) ;
> $$
>
> its representation is $(\frac12, \frac12)$, and under rotations $A^0$ is spin $0$ and $\mathbf A$ spin $1$.
> 2. Lower components $A_\mu = g_{\mu\nu}A^\nu$ transform with $\Lambda_\mu{}^\nu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu$, the matrix $g\Lambda g^{-1} = (\Lambda^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$: a representation equivalent to the upper one through $g$, hence also $(\frac12, \frac12)$.
>
> *Model:* any field with a transformation law ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]]); *applies:* as in the opening.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.5, eq. (fieldtransforms); §7.3 (Derivation "The spin content of a four-vector") · PHY 513 Lecture 7, Part C ("Transformation of Vector and Spinor Fields") · PS §3.1, p. 37 · Yu §4.2.2, eqs. (4.28)–(4.37)*

^thm-c3-3-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The law and its generators.** The four-vector field is [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^ex-c1b-1-1|Example §C1b.1.1]], $D = \Lambda$; the generators of $D(\Lambda) = \Lambda$ are $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1a.7 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-7-2|Theorem §C1a.7.2]]). Theorem §C3.3.3 with $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}) = \mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$ gives the infinitesimal law.
>
> **2. Its label and rotation content.** [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-9|Theorem §C3.2.9]]: $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$ is $(\frac12, \frac12)$, with $\mathbf J^2 = \operatorname{diag}(0, 2, 2, 2)$, so $s(s + 1) = 0$ on $A^0$ and $2$ on $\mathbf A$.
>
> **3. Lower components.** $A'_\mu = g_{\mu\alpha}A'^\alpha = g_{\mu\alpha}\Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta A^\beta = g_{\mu\alpha}\Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta g^{\beta\nu}A_\nu$ (argument $\Lambda^{-1}x$ suppressed), so the matrix acting on lower components is $(g\Lambda g^{-1})_\mu{}^\nu = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu$.
>
> **4. It is the inverse transpose.** From $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}g\Lambda = g$ ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^def-c1a-4-1|Def. §C1a.4.1]]): $g\Lambda = (\Lambda^{\mathsf T})^{-1}g$, so $g\Lambda g^{-1} = (\Lambda^{\mathsf T})^{-1} = (\Lambda^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$, entrywise $\Lambda_\mu{}^\nu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|REL Theorem §B1.2.3]]).
>
> **5. Equivalence.** $\Lambda \mapsto g\Lambda g^{-1}$ is the vector representation conjugated by the fixed invertible matrix $g$, hence equivalent to it ([[§CB.3 Homomorphisms, Representations, the Identity Component and Coverings#^def-cb-3-8|Def. §CB.3.8]]); its generators are $g\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}g^{-1}$, which are the lower-index spin generators of Example §C1b.1.1 times $i$. Equivalent representations have the same Casimirs, so the label is again $(\frac12, \frac12)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Upper and lower indices are two bases of one representation, related by the invariant tensor $g$: raising and lowering *is* the equivalence. For the rotation group the same statement is trivial ($R = (R^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$); for the Lorentz group it costs the metric.
> - $A^0$ and $\mathbf A$ are not separate representations of the Lorentz group: boosts mix them. The extra spin-$0$ component of a vector field is what gauge invariance or a constraint must remove for a spin-$1$ particle (QFT C4: [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]], the Lorenz condition [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]], gauge invariance [[§C4.6 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^rem-c4-6-3|§C4.6, Remark: Why a massless vector field must be a gauge field]]).

> [!example] Example §C3.3.1: The Gradient of a Scalar Field
> If $\phi$ is a scalar field, show that $\partial_\mu\phi$ is a vector field with lower index.
>
> By the chain rule applied to $\phi'(x) = \phi(\Lambda^{-1}x)$, with $y^\nu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu x^\mu$ so that $\partial y^\nu/\partial x^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu$,
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu\phi'(x) = \frac{\partial y^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}\,(\partial_\nu\phi)(\Lambda^{-1}x) = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu\,(\partial_\nu\phi)(\Lambda^{-1}x) = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu\,(\partial_\nu\phi)(\Lambda^{-1}x) ,
> $$
>
> the law of Theorem §C3.3.5, 2. So differentiation maps $(0, 0)$ fields to $(\frac12, \frac12)$ fields: $\partial_\mu$ itself carries the vector representation, which is why every derivative in a Lagrangian must be contracted.
>

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.6: Products of Fields Add Each Copy Separately
> 1. A product of two fields at one point, $\phi_a(x)\chi_{a'}(x)$, with laws $D$ and $D'$, transforms with $D\otimes D'$, whose generators add:
>
> $$
> D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})\otimes\mathbb 1 + \mathbb 1\otimes D'(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}), \qquad \mathbf J_\pm^{\rm tot} = \mathbf J_\pm\otimes\mathbb 1 + \mathbb 1\otimes\mathbf J'_\pm .
> $$
>
> 2. Each copy adds as two angular momenta ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]):
>
> $$
> (j_+, j_-)\otimes(j'_+, j'_-) = \bigoplus_{J_+ = |j_+ - j'_+|}^{j_+ + j'_+}\ \bigoplus_{J_- = |j_- - j'_-|}^{j_- + j'_-}(J_+, J_-) ,
> $$
>
> each label once. Examples: $(\frac12, \frac12)\otimes(\frac12, \frac12) = (1, 1)\oplus(1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)\oplus(0, 0)$, $16 = 9 + 3 + 3 + 1$ (the two-index tensor, [[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]]); $(\frac12, 0)\otimes(0, \frac12) = (\frac12, \frac12)$; $(\frac12, 0)\otimes(\frac12, 0) = (1, 0)\oplus(0, 0)$.
>
> *How to use it:* a monomial of fields and derivatives ($\partial_\mu$ counts as $(\frac12, \frac12)$, [[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^ex-c3-3-1|Example §C3.3.1]]) can enter a Lorentz-invariant Lagrangian only through its $(0, 0)$ pieces, one invariant contraction for each $(0, 0)$ in the product ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^rem-c3-3-5|Remark: Invariant Lagrangians are (0, 0) pieces]]); the rule within each copy is the Clebsch–Gordan series of Quantum Mechanics ([[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-2|QM Theorem §C7.1.2]]), and the rotation content of each piece is [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-5|Theorem §C3.2.5]].
>
> *Model:* any fields with transformation laws ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]]), classical or quantum, at one spacetime point; *applies:* exact.
>

> A field $T^{\mu\nu}$ transforms as $T'^{\mu\nu}(x) = \Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta T^{\alpha\beta}(\Lambda^{-1}x)$, i.e. with $D = \Lambda\otimes\Lambda$ and spin generators $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}\otimes\mathbb 1 + \mathbb 1\otimes\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$. > \underbrace{\tfrac14g^{\mu\nu}T^\rho{}_\rho}_{(0,\,0)}\ \oplus\ \underbrace{T^{[\mu\nu]}}_{(1,\,0)\oplus(0,\,1)}\ \oplus\ \underbrace{T^{(\mu\nu)} - \tfrac14g^{\mu\nu}T^\rho{}_\rho}_{(1,\,1)} ,

> [!derivation]- Derivation (second route: rotation content first, then match)
> **1. Rotation content of the blocks.** Under rotations a four-vector is $0\oplus1$, $v^0$ and $\mathbf v$ ([[§C3.1 Index Slots, Rotations and Spin in Field Theory#^ex-c3-1-1|Example §C3.1.1]]), so $T^{\mu\nu}$ splits into the rotation-invariant blocks $T^{00}$ ($0\otimes0 = 0$), $T^{0j}$ ($0\otimes1 = 1$), $T^{i0}$ ($1\otimes0 = 1$) and $T^{ij}$ ($1\otimes1 = 0\oplus1\oplus2$, [[§CB.10 SU(2) and SO(3)꞉ Spin j, Symmetric Powers and Clebsch–Gordan#^thm-cb-10-17|Theorem §CB.10.17]]): the trace $\delta_{ij}T^{ij}$ (spin $0$), the antisymmetric part $T^{[ij]} \leftrightarrow \varepsilon_{ijk}$ (spin $1$) and the symmetric traceless part (spin $2$) ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-2|QM Theorem §C7.2.2]]).
>
> **2. Antisymmetry block by block.** $T^{00} = -T^{00}$ forces $T^{00} = 0$: nothing survives. $T^{i0} = -T^{0i}$ ties the two vector blocks: one spin $1$ survives (for the field strength, $\mathbf E$). In $T^{ij}$ the trace and the symmetric traceless part vanish: the spin $1$ survives ($\mathbf B$). So the six antisymmetric components have rotation content $1\oplus1$.
>
> **3. The candidates.** By [[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-6|Theorem §C3.3.6]], $T^{\mu\nu}$ carries $(1, 1)\oplus(1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)\oplus(0, 0)$, each once, with rotation contents $0\oplus1\oplus2$, $1$, $1$, $0$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-5|Theorem §C3.2.5]]).
>
> **4. Match.** The antisymmetric part is invariant ([[§CB.8 New Representations from Old꞉ Tensor Products, Duals, Conjugates and Invariant Tensors#^thm-cb-8-19|Theorem §CB.8.19]]); with an invariant complement it decomposes $T^{\mu\nu}$, so by the uniqueness of multiplicities ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-4|Theorem §C3.2.4]]) it is equivalent to a sum of some of the four labels of step 3, each at most once. Its rotation content is the union of theirs, and must be $1\oplus1$ (step 2): $(1, 1)$ would bring spin $2$ and $(0, 0)$ spin $0$, so the subset is $\{(1, 0), (0, 1)\}$: $T^{[\mu\nu]} \cong (1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)$.
>
> **5. The symmetric part.** The ten symmetric components have $T^{00}$ ($0$), $T^{0i} = T^{i0}$ ($1$) and symmetric $T^{ij}$ ($0\oplus2$): content $0\oplus0\oplus1\oplus2$, which among the remaining labels is $(1, 1)\oplus(0, 0)$. Removing the four-dimensional trace $\frac14g^{\mu\nu}T^\rho{}_\rho$, the $(0, 0)$, removes one spin $0$ and leaves $0\oplus1\oplus2$, $1 + 3 + 5 = 9$ components: $(1, 1)$, whose rotation content is $1\otimes1$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The route needs from [[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-6|Theorem §C3.3.6]] only the list of labels; it decides by rotation content. That works here because the contents of $(1, 1)$, $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$, $(0, 0)$ differ enough; it is not decisive in general ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-12|Theorem §C3.2.12]]).
> - ⚑ A rotation block is not a Lorentz label: in step 1, "$1\otimes0$" is the block $T^{i0}$ (a product of the rotation contents of the two indices), while in $(1, 0)$ the comma separates the $\mathbf J_+$ and $\mathbf J_-$ slots. $T^{i0}$ alone is not invariant (a boost mixes it with $T^{00}$ and $T^{ij}$); the piece $(1, 0)$ draws on $T^{0i}$, $T^{i0}$ and $T^{ij}$ together, as $\mathbf E - i\mathbf B$ (Theorem §C3.3.8). Both counts give the same totals: two spins $0$, three spins $1$, one spin $2$. Since boosts mix $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$, the irreducible halves are $\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B$, not $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ separately.
>

> [!derivation]- Derivation (third route: the swap operator and Schur's lemma)
> Why is each piece wholly symmetric or wholly antisymmetric?
>
> **1. The swap commutes with the action.** Let $P(u\otimes w) = w\otimes u$, i.e. $(PT)^{\mu\nu} = T^{\nu\mu}$. Since both indices carry the same $\Lambda$, $(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda)P(u\otimes w) = \Lambda w\otimes\Lambda u = P(\Lambda u\otimes\Lambda w) = P(\Lambda\otimes\Lambda)(u\otimes w)$: $P$ is an intertwiner of $T^{\mu\nu}$ with itself, and $P^2 = \mathbb 1$.
>
> **2. P preserves each piece.** Write $\mathbb C^{16} = W_9\oplus W_3\oplus W_3'\oplus W_1$ with $W_9 \cong (1, 1)$, $W_3 \cong (1, 0)$, $W_3' \cong (0, 1)$, $W_1 \cong (0, 0)$ invariant ([[§CB.18 The Lorentz Case III꞉ the Dirac Module, Half-Spin Representations and γ⁵#^thm-cb-18-12|Theorem §CB.18.12]]); they are irreducible and mutually inequivalent ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-4|Theorem §C3.2.4]]). The projections $p_b$ onto the pieces commute with the action, so for $a \ne b$, $p_b\circ P$ restricted to $W_a$ is an intertwiner $W_a \to W_b$ between inequivalent irreducible representations, hence $0$ ([[§CB.7 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^thm-cb-7-4|Theorem §CB.7.4]], 1). So $P(W_a) \subset W_a$.
>
> **3. A sign on each piece.** $P|_{W_a}$ intertwines $W_a$ with itself, so it is a number $c_a$ (Theorem §CB.7.4, 2); $P^2 = \mathbb 1$ gives $c_a = \pm1$. It suffices to test one nonzero element per piece.
>
> **4. The tests.** $W_1$ is spanned by $g^{\mu\nu}$, which is symmetric: $c_1 = +1$. $W_9$ is the only piece containing rotation spin $2$ (step 3 of the second route), so every element of rotation spin $2$ lies in $W_9$; the element $T^{11} = 1$, $T^{22} = -1$, all other components $0$, is spatial, symmetric and traceless, hence of spin $2$: $c_9 = +1$. The symmetric tensors form a ten-dimensional space containing $W_9\oplus W_1$ ($9 + 1 = 10$), so it is exactly $W_9\oplus W_1$, and $c_3 = c_3' = -1$ (otherwise it would have dimension at least $13$).
>
> **5. Conclusion.** The antisymmetric tensors are $W_3\oplus W_3' \cong (1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)$; the symmetric ones are $(1, 1)\oplus(0, 0)$, with $(0, 0)$ the trace.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Schur's lemma turns "symmetric or antisymmetric" into a sign attached to each irreducible piece; one test vector per piece decides it, with no projection computed. The spinor-index form (Example §C3.3.2) gives the same signs as products of the signs of the two slots.

> | sym | sym | $S_{(aa')(bb')}$ | $(1, 1)$ | $9$ | symmetric |

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The parameters.** For a rotation, $\omega_{jk} = \varepsilon_{jkl}\theta_l$ ([[§C1a.7 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-7-2|Def. §C1a.7.2]]), so $\omega^j{}_k = g^{jj}\omega_{jk} = -\varepsilon_{jkl}\theta_l$ and $\omega^0{}_\mu = 0$. For a boost, $\omega_{0i} = \eta_i = -\omega_{i0}$, so $\omega^0{}_i = \eta_i$, $\omega^i{}_0 = g^{ii}\omega_{i0} = \eta_i$, and $\omega^i{}_j = 0$. The fields are $F^{0i} = -E^i$, $F^{ij} = -\varepsilon_{ijk}B^k$, $B^i = -\frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}F^{jk}$.
>
> **2. E under a rotation.** $\delta_sF^{0j} = \omega^0{}_\alpha F^{\alpha j} + \omega^j{}_\beta F^{0\beta} = 0 + \omega^j{}_kF^{0k}$ ($\omega^j{}_0 = 0$). So $-\delta E^j = -\varepsilon_{jkl}\theta_l(-E^k)$, i.e. $\delta E^j = -\varepsilon_{jkl}\theta_lE^k = \varepsilon_{jlk}\theta_lE^k = (\boldsymbol\theta\times\mathbf E)_j$.
>
> **3. B under a rotation.** $\delta_sF^{jk} = \omega^j{}_lF^{lk} + \omega^k{}_lF^{jl} = (-\varepsilon_{jln}\theta_n)(-\varepsilon_{lkm}B^m) + (-\varepsilon_{kln}\theta_n)(-\varepsilon_{jlm}B^m)$. Contract: $\varepsilon_{jln}\varepsilon_{lkm} = \varepsilon_{lnj}\varepsilon_{lkm} = \delta_{nk}\delta_{jm} - \delta_{nm}\delta_{jk}$ and $\varepsilon_{kln}\varepsilon_{jlm} = \varepsilon_{lnk}\varepsilon_{lmj} = \delta_{nm}\delta_{kj} - \delta_{nj}\delta_{km}$, so $\delta_sF^{jk} = \theta_kB^j - \delta_{jk}\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf B + \delta_{jk}\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf B - \theta_jB^k = \theta_kB^j - \theta_jB^k$. Then $\delta B^i = -\frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}(\theta_kB^j - \theta_jB^k) = -\frac12\bigl((\mathbf B\times\boldsymbol\theta)_i - (\boldsymbol\theta\times\mathbf B)_i\bigr) = (\boldsymbol\theta\times\mathbf B)_i$.
>
> **4. E under a boost.** $\delta_sF^{0j} = \omega^0{}_kF^{kj} + \omega^j{}_0F^{00} = \eta_k(-\varepsilon_{kjm}B^m) + 0$. So $\delta E^j = \varepsilon_{kjm}\eta_kB^m = -\varepsilon_{jkm}\eta_kB^m = -(\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf B)_j$.
>
> **5. B under a boost.** $\delta_sF^{jk} = \omega^j{}_0F^{0k} + \omega^k{}_0F^{j0} = \eta_j(-E^k) + \eta_kE^j$ (using $F^{j0} = -F^{0j} = E^j$). Then $\delta B^i = -\frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}(\eta_kE^j - \eta_jE^k) = -\frac12\bigl((\mathbf E\times\boldsymbol\eta)_i - (\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf E)_i\bigr) = (\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf E)_i$. ⚑ By-product: for a boost along $z$ this is $\delta E_x = \eta B_y$, the first-order part of $E'_x = \gamma(E_x + vB_y)$ of [[§C1a.8 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^ex-c1a-8-1|Example §C1a.8.1]] — a check on every sign above.
>
> **6. The complex combinations.** Add steps 2–5 for a general $\omega$ and form $\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B$: $\delta(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B) = \boldsymbol\theta\times(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B) - \boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf B \mp i\,\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf E$. Since $\mp i\boldsymbol\eta\times(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B) = \mp i\,\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf E + (\mp i)(\mp i)\,\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf B = \mp i\,\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf E - \boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf B$, this is $(\boldsymbol\theta \mp i\boldsymbol\eta)\times(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B)$.
>
> **7. Compare with (1, 0) and (0, 1).** The Cartesian spin-1 matrices $(J^{(1)}_k)_{lm} = -i\varepsilon_{klm}$ obey the rotation algebra with $\mathbf J^{(1)2} = 2$ ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^ex-c5-1-2|QM Example §C5.1.2]]), so they are a spin-1 multiplet in another basis, and $(-i\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf J^{(1)}\mathbf v)_l = -i\alpha_k(-i\varepsilon_{klm})v_m = -\varepsilon_{klm}\alpha_kv_m = (\boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf v)_l$. On $(1, 0)$, [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]] to first order is $1 - i(\boldsymbol\theta - i\boldsymbol\eta)\cdot\mathbf J^{(1)}$, i.e. $\delta\mathbf v = (\boldsymbol\theta - i\boldsymbol\eta)\times\mathbf v$; on $(0, 1)$, $\delta\mathbf v = (\boldsymbol\theta + i\boldsymbol\eta)\times\mathbf v$. Step 6 matches the first for $\mathbf E - i\mathbf B$ and the second for $\mathbf E + i\mathbf B$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - A Lorentz transformation acts on $\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B$ as a rotation through a complex angle, so it preserves $(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B)\cdot(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B)$, the complex invariant of [[§C1a.8 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1a-8-4|Theorem §C1a.8.4]]: the complex orthogonal $O(\Lambda)$ found there is $e^{(\boldsymbol\theta + i\boldsymbol\eta)\times}$.
> - The labels depend on the sign conventions for $\mathbf K$ and for $\mathbf B$ ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^cau-c3-3-1|Caution: Which half of F is called (1, 0)]]).
> - Used next: the photon field ([[§C4.6 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^rem-c4-6-3|§C4.6, Remark: Why a massless vector field must be a gauge field]], [[§C4.8 Covariant Quantization꞉ Modes and the Indefinite Mode Algebra#^thm-c4-8-1|Theorem §C4.8.1]]); parity exchanges the halves ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-6|Theorem §C3.2.6]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Why a spinor's matrix is fixed only up to sign
> For $(\frac12, 0)$, $(0, \frac12)$ and their sum, $j_+ + j_- = \frac12$, and a rotation by $2\pi$, the identity of $SO^+(1,3)$, acts as $-1$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-5|Theorem §C3.2.5]]). So the law $\psi \mapsto D(\Lambda)\psi(\Lambda^{-1}x)$ is defined up to $\psi \mapsto -\psi$: a two-valued representation ([[§CB.10 SU(2) and SO(3)꞉ Spin j, Symmetric Powers and Clebsch–Gordan#^def-cb-10-10|Def. §CB.10.10]]), single-valued on $SL(2, \mathbb C)$ ([[§CB.10 SU(2) and SO(3)꞉ Spin j, Symmetric Powers and Clebsch–Gordan#^rem-cb-10-3|§CB.10, Remark: The same pattern for the Lorentz group]]; [[§CB.16 The Lorentz Case I꞉ Spin(1,3), SL(2,C) and the Double Cover#^thm-cb-16-5|Theorem §CB.16.5]], [[§CB.16 The Lorentz Case I꞉ Spin(1,3), SL(2,C) and the Double Cover#^thm-cb-16-9|Theorem §CB.16.9]]; in the course's matrices, [[§C5a.4 SL(2,C) and the Group Action on Spinor Space#^thm-c5a-4-2|Theorem §C5a.4.2]] and [[§C5a.4 SL(2,C) and the Group Action on Spinor Space#^thm-c5a-4-3|Theorem §C5a.4.3]]). Nothing observable is affected: observables are built from bilinears in $\psi$ (currents, $\bar\psi\psi$, the Lagrangian), in which the sign cancels, just as the overall phase of a quantum state is unobservable ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-5|QM Theorem §C5.2.5]] measures only a relative sign). ## Invariant Lagrangians

> [!remark] Remark: Invariant Lagrangians are (0, 0) pieces
> An action is Lorentz invariant when its Lagrangian density transforms as a scalar ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1b-2-1|Theorem §C1b.2.1]]). A product of fields and derivatives at one point transforms in the tensor product of their representations ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-6|Theorem §C3.3.6]]; $\partial_\mu$ counts as $(\frac12, \frac12)$, [[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^ex-c3-3-1|Example §C3.3.1]]), and only its $(0, 0)$ components are numbers that every observer agrees on. "Contract all indices" is the rule that extracts them. The three Lagrangians previewed on the slides:
> - *scalar*, $\frac12\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi - \frac12m^2\phi^2$: $g^{\mu\nu}$ picks the $(0, 0)$ of $(\frac12, \frac12)\otimes(\frac12, \frac12)$, the trace piece of Theorem §C3.3.7;
> - *vector*, $-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$: $F\otimes F$ with $F = (1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)$ contains $(0, 0)$ twice, once from $(1, 0)\otimes(1, 0)$ and once from $(0, 1)\otimes(0, 1)$; the two invariants are $(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B)^2$, i.e. $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ and $F_{\mu\nu}\tilde F^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1a.8 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1a-8-3|Theorem §C1a.8.3]]); parity keeps the first and reverses the second;
> - *Dirac*, $\bar\psi(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)\psi$: since $D$ is not unitary, $\psi^\dagger$ does not transform with $D^{-1}$ and $\psi^\dagger\psi$ is not invariant ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-7|Theorem §C3.2.7]]); $\psi_L^\dagger$ is $(0, \frac12)$ (complex conjugation, [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-6|Theorem §C3.2.6]]), so $\psi_L^\dagger\otimes\psi_L = (\frac12, \frac12)$ is a vector and must be contracted with $\partial_\mu$. > [!remark]- ★ Remark: Larger tensors: the gradient of a vector field and the Riemann tensor
> The same bookkeeping sorts any tensor built from four-vectors.
> - $\partial_\mu A_\nu \in (\frac12, \frac12)\otimes(\frac12, \frac12) = (1, 1)\oplus(1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)\oplus(0, 0)$ ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-6|Theorem §C3.3.6]]): the trace $\partial\cdot A$ is the $(0, 0)$ (the quantity set to zero by the Lorenz condition), the antisymmetric part $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ is $(1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)$, the symmetric traceless part is $(1, 1)$ (Theorem §C3.3.7).
> - A symmetric $T^{\mu\nu}$ (such as the energy–momentum tensor) is $(1, 1)\oplus(0, 0)$, the $(0, 0)$ being $T^\mu{}_\mu$.
> - The Riemann tensor of general relativity, $R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$, is antisymmetric in $\mu\nu$ and in $\rho\sigma$, symmetric under exchange of the two pairs, and obeys $R_{\mu[\nu\rho\sigma]} = 0$: $20$ components. Antisymmetric pairs are $\Lambda^2 = (1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)$, and the pair-symmetric part of $\Lambda^2\otimes\Lambda^2$ is $\operatorname{Sym}^2\bigl((1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)\bigr) = \operatorname{Sym}^2(1, 0)\oplus\operatorname{Sym}^2(0, 1)\oplus(1, 0)\otimes(0, 1) = \bigl((2, 0)\oplus(0, 0)\bigr)\oplus\bigl((0, 2)\oplus(0, 0)\bigr)\oplus(1, 1)$, of dimension $6 + 6 + 9 = 21$ (in one copy, $1\otimes1 = 2\oplus1\oplus0$ with $2\oplus0$ symmetric, $5 + 1 = 6 = \frac{3\cdot4}2$). The cyclic identity removes the totally antisymmetric part, $\Lambda^4 \cong (0, 0)$ (one component, $\propto\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$), leaving $(2, 0)\oplus(0, 2)\oplus(1, 1)\oplus(0, 0)$, $5 + 5 + 9 + 1 = 20$: the Weyl tensor ($10$), the traceless Ricci tensor ($9$) and the Ricci scalar ($1$). The Weyl tensor, like $F$, splits over $\mathbb C$ into self-dual and anti-self-dual halves.
