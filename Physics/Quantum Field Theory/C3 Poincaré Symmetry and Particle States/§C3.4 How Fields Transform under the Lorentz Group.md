What exactly happens to a scalar, a four-vector, a tensor or a spinor field under a Lorentz transformation, and which of the representations $(j_+, j_-)$ is each? The general law $\phi'_a(x) = D(\Lambda)_a{}^b\phi_b(\Lambda^{-1}x)$, its group property and its infinitesimal form are [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]], [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^thm-c1b-1-1|Theorem §C1b.1.1]] and [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^thm-c1b-1-2|Theorem §C1b.1.2]] (with the scalar case [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-2|Def. §C1b.1.2]]); they are linked, not repeated. The representations themselves are [[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra|§C3.3]]. This section adds the representation-specific content: the orbital generators that move the argument and their algebra, the total generator as orbital plus spin, and, field by field, the matrix $D(\Lambda)$, its label $(j_+, j_-)$ and its rotation content, ending with the field strength's split into $\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B$. How unitary operators on states implement these laws for quantum fields is [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-5-8|Principle §C3.5.8]]; the spinor matrices are QFT C5a ([[§C5a.3 The Lorentz Action on Spinor Space|§C5a.3]], [[§C5a.4 SL(2,C) and the Group Action on Spinor Space|§C5a.4]]).

*Conventions* as in [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra|§C3.2]]: active reading, $\Lambda = e^\omega = \exp(-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$, $K_i = \mathcal J^{0i}$, $\theta_i = \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\omega_{jk}$, $\eta_i = \omega_{0i}$ ([[Larsen PHY 513]]).

## Orbital and spin generators

> [!definition] Definition §C3.4.1: Orbital Generators
> On functions of $x$, the **orbital generators** are the six differential operators
>
> $$
> L^{\mu\nu} = i\bigl(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu\bigr) = -L^{\nu\mu} ,
> $$
>
> three rotations $L_i = \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}L^{jk}$ and three boosts $L^{0i}$. They act on the argument of a field, not on its components.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.3, eq. (Jorbital) · PHY 513 Lecture 7, Part B ("Lorentz Algebra: Defining Representation") · PS §3.1, eq. (3.16) · Yu §4.2.1, eq. (4.22)*

^def-c3-4-1

The slides introduce these operators under the heading "defining representation"; they act on functions (an infinite-dimensional representation), while the defining representation on four-vectors is the $4\times4$ matrices $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]). Peskin–Schroeder use them, as here, to compute the algebra. The three-dimensional $J^{ij} = -i(x^i\partial_j - x^j\partial_i)$ of the slides is the spatial part with Euclidean derivatives ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^cau-c3-1-2|§C3.1, Caution: The sign of the orbital generator]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.1: The Orbital Generators Move the Argument
> 1. For $\Lambda = e^\omega$ and a $C^2$ function $\phi$, to first order in $\omega$,
>
> $$
> \phi(\Lambda^{-1}x) = \Bigl(1 - \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}L^{\mu\nu}\Bigr)\phi(x) + O(\omega^2) ,
> $$
>
> with the same parameters and the same sign as $v \mapsto \Lambda v = (1 - \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})v$ on vectors.
> 2. $L_i = -i(\mathbf x\times\nabla)_i$, the orbital angular momentum $(\mathbf x\times\mathbf p)_i$ with the momentum acting on functions as $-i\nabla$; $L^{0i} = -i(t\,\partial_i + x^i\partial_t)$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.5 (Derivation "The generator of the scalar transformation law is the orbital operator"; checked symbolically there) · Yu §4.2.1, eqs. (4.19)–(4.26)*

^thm-c3-4-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The scalar law to first order.** Steps 1–5 of [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^der-c1b-1-2|Derivation §C1b.1.2]] with $a = 0$ give $\Lambda^{-1} = 1 - \omega + O(\omega^2)$, the Taylor expansion of $\phi$ at $x$, the lowering $\omega^\mu{}_\nu\partial_\mu = \omega_{\rho\nu}\partial^\rho$, and the antisymmetrization:
>
> $$
> \phi(\Lambda^{-1}x) = \phi(x) - \omega^\mu{}_\nu\,x^\nu\partial_\mu\phi(x) + O(\omega^2) = \phi(x) + \tfrac12\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu\bigr)\phi(x) + O(\omega^2) .
> $$
>
> **2. Insert the orbital generators.** By Def. §C3.4.1, $x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu = -iL^{\mu\nu}$ (multiply $L^{\mu\nu} = i(\cdots)$ by $-i$). So $\frac12\omega_{\mu\nu}(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu) = -\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}L^{\mu\nu}$, which is part 1.
>
> **3. Same sign as the vector representation.** By [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-6-2|Theorem §C1a.6.2]], $-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu} = \omega$ as a matrix, and $\Lambda v = v + \omega v + O(\omega^2)$. Both laws are "$1 - \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}(\text{generator})$" with the same $\omega_{\mu\nu}$; the inverse in $\phi(\Lambda^{-1}x)$ is absorbed by the sign of $L$. ⚑ By-product: the convention $L = +i(x\partial - x\partial)$, with $p_\mu = i\partial_\mu$, is forced by requiring the orbital and the matrix generators to enter the field law with the same $\omega$ → Theorem §C3.4.3.
>
> **4. Rotations.** $L_i = \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\,i(x^j\partial^k - x^k\partial^j)$. Rename $j \leftrightarrow k$ in the second term and use $\varepsilon_{ikj} = -\varepsilon_{ijk}$: $L_i = \frac i2(\varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\partial^k + \varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\partial^k) = i\varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\partial^k$. With $\partial^k = -\partial_k$ (one spatial index raised), $L_i = -i\varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\partial_k = -i(\mathbf x\times\nabla)_i$, with $-i\nabla$ the momentum acting on functions.
>
> **5. Boosts.** $L^{0i} = i(x^0\partial^i - x^i\partial^0) = i(-t\,\partial_i - x^i\partial_t)$, using $\partial^i = -\partial_i$, $\partial^0 = \partial_t$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The spatial generators are Quantum Mechanics' orbital angular momentum, which generates $\psi(\mathbf x) \mapsto \psi(R^{-1}\mathbf x)$ on wave functions ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-1|QM Theorem §C5.4.1]]); the boosts are new and mix $t$ with $\mathbf x$.
> - $s \mapsto \phi(e^{-s\omega}x)$ is a one-parameter group of operators on functions with generator $-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}L^{\mu\nu}$; for a plane wave, $e^{-ip\cdot\Lambda^{-1}x} = e^{-i(\Lambda p)\cdot x}$, so the orbital operators rotate and boost momenta.
> - Used next: the algebra of the $L^{\mu\nu}$ (Theorem §C3.4.2) and the total generator of a field (Theorem §C3.4.3).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.2: The Orbital Generators Obey the Lorentz Algebra
> On $C^2$ functions (in particular on $\mathcal S(\mathbb R^4)$, which the $L^{\mu\nu}$ map into itself),
>
> $$
> [L^{\mu\nu}, L^{\rho\sigma}] = i\bigl(g^{\nu\rho}L^{\mu\sigma} - g^{\mu\rho}L^{\nu\sigma} - g^{\nu\sigma}L^{\mu\rho} + g^{\mu\sigma}L^{\nu\rho}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.3 (Derivation "Realization on functions: the orbital generators", "They obey the algebra") · PS §3.1, eqs. (3.16)–(3.17) ("simply compute the commutators of the differential operators")*

^thm-c3-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Write $\ell^{\mu\nu} = x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu$, so $L^{\mu\nu} = i\ell^{\mu\nu}$; act on a $C^2$ function $f$.
>
> **1. One elementary commutator.** By the product rule and $\partial^\nu x^\rho = g^{\nu\rho}$,
>
> $$
> x^\mu\partial^\nu\bigl(x^\rho\partial^\sigma f\bigr) = g^{\nu\rho}x^\mu\partial^\sigma f + x^\mu x^\rho\partial^\nu\partial^\sigma f, \qquad x^\rho\partial^\sigma\bigl(x^\mu\partial^\nu f\bigr) = g^{\sigma\mu}x^\rho\partial^\nu f + x^\rho x^\mu\partial^\sigma\partial^\nu f .
> $$
>
> Subtract: the second-derivative terms cancel ($f \in C^2$, so $\partial^\nu\partial^\sigma f = \partial^\sigma\partial^\nu f$), leaving
>
> $$
> C(\mu\nu; \rho\sigma) \equiv [x^\mu\partial^\nu, x^\rho\partial^\sigma] = g^{\nu\rho}x^\mu\partial^\sigma - g^{\mu\sigma}x^\rho\partial^\nu .
> $$
>
> **2. Expand into four commutators.** $[\ell^{\mu\nu}, \ell^{\rho\sigma}] = C(\mu\nu; \rho\sigma) - C(\mu\nu; \sigma\rho) - C(\nu\mu; \rho\sigma) + C(\nu\mu; \sigma\rho)$. From step 1, with the labels substituted in each:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> C(\mu\nu; \rho\sigma) &= g^{\nu\rho}x^\mu\partial^\sigma - g^{\mu\sigma}x^\rho\partial^\nu, & -C(\mu\nu; \sigma\rho) &= -g^{\nu\sigma}x^\mu\partial^\rho + g^{\mu\rho}x^\sigma\partial^\nu, \\
> -C(\nu\mu; \rho\sigma) &= -g^{\mu\rho}x^\nu\partial^\sigma + g^{\nu\sigma}x^\rho\partial^\mu, & C(\nu\mu; \sigma\rho) &= g^{\mu\sigma}x^\nu\partial^\rho - g^{\nu\rho}x^\sigma\partial^\mu .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> **3. Collect the eight terms by their metric factor.** $g^{\nu\rho}(x^\mu\partial^\sigma - x^\sigma\partial^\mu) = g^{\nu\rho}\ell^{\mu\sigma}$; $g^{\mu\sigma}(x^\nu\partial^\rho - x^\rho\partial^\nu) = g^{\mu\sigma}\ell^{\nu\rho}$; $g^{\nu\sigma}(x^\rho\partial^\mu - x^\mu\partial^\rho) = -g^{\nu\sigma}\ell^{\mu\rho}$; $g^{\mu\rho}(x^\sigma\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\sigma) = -g^{\mu\rho}\ell^{\nu\sigma}$. Hence
>
> $$
> [\ell^{\mu\nu}, \ell^{\rho\sigma}] = g^{\nu\rho}\ell^{\mu\sigma} - g^{\mu\rho}\ell^{\nu\sigma} - g^{\nu\sigma}\ell^{\mu\rho} + g^{\mu\sigma}\ell^{\nu\rho} .
> $$
>
> **4. Restore i.** $[L^{\mu\nu}, L^{\rho\sigma}] = i^2[\ell^{\mu\nu}, \ell^{\rho\sigma}] = i\bigl(g^{\nu\rho}\,i\ell^{\mu\sigma} - \cdots\bigr) = i\bigl(g^{\nu\rho}L^{\mu\sigma} - g^{\mu\rho}L^{\nu\sigma} - g^{\nu\sigma}L^{\mu\rho} + g^{\mu\sigma}L^{\nu\rho}\bigr)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The real operators $\ell^{\mu\nu}$ obey exactly the relations of the real matrices $M^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^der-c3-2-1b|Derivation §C3.2.1, second route]], step 6), as the group-law derivation predicts: functions of $x$ carry a representation, and the algebra does not care that it is infinite-dimensional.
> - Assumption used: equality of mixed second derivatives. For distributions the same identity holds by transposition ([[§C3.4 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^rem-c3-4-2|Remark: The laws for fields that are distributions]]).
> - The rotation part reproduces Quantum Mechanics' $[\hat L_i, \hat L_j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat L_k$ for $\hat L_i = \varepsilon_{ijk}\hat x_j\hat p_k$ ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-1|QM Theorem §C5.4.1]]).

The infinitesimal field law, with its orbital and spin parts in the real convention of Noether's theorem, has its home in [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws|§C1b.1]]:

![[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^thm-c1b-1-2]]

What the representation theory adds is the Hermitian form of the same law: the generators of the two parts as a single operator obeying the Lorentz algebra, with the dictionary between $S^{\mu\nu}$ and $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.3: Orbital Plus Spin
> For a field $\phi_a$ transforming with $D$ ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]]) and $\Lambda = e^\omega$, the infinitesimal law of [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^thm-c1b-1-2|Theorem §C1b.1.2]] (with $a = 0$) reads, with Hermitian generators,
>
> $$
> \phi'_a(x) = \phi_a(x) - \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl(\mathbb J^{\mu\nu}\phi\bigr)_a(x), \qquad \mathbb J^{\mu\nu} = L^{\mu\nu}\,\delta_a{}^b + D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b ,
> $$
>
> an **orbital** part acting on the argument and a **spin** part acting on the index. The two parts commute, $\mathbb J^{\mu\nu}$ obeys the Lorentz algebra, and the spin generators of Def. §C1b.1.3 are $S^{\mu\nu} = -iD(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$. Under rotations, $\mathbb J_i = L_i + J_i$: total angular momentum is orbital plus spin.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.5 (paragraph "Fields with indices": "an orbital part acting on the argument plus a spin part acting on the indices") · PHY 513 Lecture 7, Part C · Yu §4.2.2, eqs. (4.31)–(4.32)*

^thm-c3-4-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The general law to first order.** [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^thm-c1b-1-2|Theorem §C1b.1.2]] with $a = 0$: $\phi'_a = \phi_a + \frac12\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl[(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu)\delta_a{}^b + (S^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b\bigr]\phi_b + O(\omega^2)$, where $D(\Lambda) = 1 + \frac12\omega_{\mu\nu}S^{\mu\nu} + O(\omega^2)$.
>
> **2. Orbital part.** $\frac12\omega_{\mu\nu}(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu) = -\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}L^{\mu\nu}$ (Theorem §C3.4.1, step 2).
>
> **3. Spin part.** By [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]], $D(\Lambda) = 1 - \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}) + O(\omega^2)$. Comparing with step 1, $\frac12 S^{\mu\nu} = -\frac i2D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ (the coefficients of the arbitrary antisymmetric $\omega_{\mu\nu}$, both antisymmetric, must agree), so $S^{\mu\nu} = -iD(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$, i.e. $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}) = iS^{\mu\nu}$: the same relation as $\mathcal J = iM$ for the vector ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-1|Def. §C1a.6.1]]).
>
> **4. The parts commute.** $D(\mathcal J^{\rho\sigma})$ is a constant matrix on the index, $L^{\mu\nu}$ a differential operator in $x$: $L^{\mu\nu}\bigl(D(\mathcal J^{\rho\sigma})_a{}^b\phi_b\bigr) = D(\mathcal J^{\rho\sigma})_a{}^b\,L^{\mu\nu}\phi_b$.
>
> **5. The algebra.** Expand $[L^{\mu\nu} + D^{\mu\nu}, L^{\rho\sigma} + D^{\rho\sigma}]$ (with $D^{\mu\nu} \equiv D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$) into four commutators; the two mixed ones vanish by step 4, leaving $[L^{\mu\nu}, L^{\rho\sigma}] + [D^{\mu\nu}, D^{\rho\sigma}]$. Each obeys the algebra ([[§C3.4 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]]; [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]] for $D$), and the right side of the algebra is linear in the generators, so the sum is $i(g^{\nu\rho}(L^{\mu\sigma} + D^{\mu\sigma}) - \cdots)$.
>
> **6. Rotations.** $\mathbb J_i = \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\mathbb J^{jk} = L_i + J_i$ by linearity, with $L_i = -i(\mathbf x\times\nabla)_i$ (Theorem §C3.4.1, 2). ⚑ By-product: the total generator is the generator of a tensor product, functions $\otimes$ components, and the generators of a product add, as for finite-dimensional products ([[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]], step 1) → [[§C3.4 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^rem-c3-4-1|Remark: Three realizations of one algebra]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Only the spin part distinguishes one kind of field from another; the orbital part is universal.
> - The factor $i$ between $S^{\mu\nu}$ (real convention, used in Noether's theorem) and $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ (Hermitian convention, used for representations) is the only translation needed between §C1b.1, §C1b.8 and this chapter.
> - Used next: field by field below; the Noether current's spin part ([[§C1b.8 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor#^thm-c1b-8-1|Theorem §C1b.8.1]]); the quantum version ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-10|Theorem §C3.5.10]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Three realizations of one algebra
> The same six generators appear three times. $L^{\mu\nu}$ acts on the argument $x$ (infinite-dimensional); $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ acts on the index $a$ (finite-dimensional, not unitary, [[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-10|Theorem §C3.3.10]]); and, once the field is an operator, the Hilbert-space generators $\hat J^{\mu\nu}$ act on states (unitary, [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]]). A classical field feels the first two together ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^rem-c1b-1-2|§C1b.1, Remark: Two representations at once]]); a quantum field feels all three, which meet in one commutator, $[\hat\Phi_a(x), \hat J^{\mu\nu}] = L^{\mu\nu}\hat\Phi_a(x) + D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b\hat\Phi_b(x)$ (the hat marks the operator on the Hilbert space; the unhatted $L^{\mu\nu}$ and $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ act on the argument and on the index; Yu's "three identities" of a quantum field; derived in [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-10|Theorem §C3.5.10]]). The list of symbols for the realizations is [[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^rem-c3-1-1|§C3.1, Remark: One symbol for each realization of the generators]].
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.1 (Principle "One symbol for each realization of the generators"), §7.5 (Derivation "The three identities of a quantum field") · Yu §4.2.2, after eq. (4.34)*

^rem-c3-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: The laws for fields that are distributions
> Quantum fields, and classical objects such as $\delta^4(x)$ or the Wightman function, are generalized functions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-11|Def. §CA.2.11]]); the laws of this section then hold as identities in $\mathcal S'$, defined by moving each operation onto the test function ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|Def. §CA.2.4]]). The argument change is the linear change of variables of [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-8|Def. §CA.2.8]] with $|\det\Lambda| = 1$: $\phi(\Lambda^{-1}\cdot)[f] = \phi[f\circ\Lambda]$. The orbital operators act by transposition, $(L^{\mu\nu}\phi)[f] = -\phi[L^{\mu\nu}f]$: from $(x^\mu\partial^\nu\phi)[f] = -\phi[\partial^\nu(x^\mu f)] = -\phi[g^{\mu\nu}f + x^\mu\partial^\nu f]$, the $g^{\mu\nu}$ terms cancel in the antisymmetric combination. $L^{\mu\nu}$ maps $\mathcal S$ into itself, so Theorem §C3.4.2 holds on $\mathcal S'$ with the order of products reversed twice by the transpositions. The scalar law's fixed points, $\phi(\Lambda^{-1}x) = \phi(x)$, are the Lorentz-invariant distributions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-7|Theorem §CA.2.7]]), annihilated by every $L^{\mu\nu}$; the Wightman function is one ([[§C2b.2 The Wightman Function|§C2b.2]]).
>
> *Source: standard; written out here (Hörmander I, §§3.1, 6.1, as cited in CA)*

## The scalar field

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.4: A One-Component Field Carries the Trivial Representation
> Every one-dimensional representation of the Lorentz algebra is zero, so a one-component field has $D(\Lambda) = 1$ for every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$: the representation $(0, 0)$. Its total generator is $L^{\mu\nu}$ alone, and under rotations it carries orbital angular momentum only.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.5 (Principle "Components and functions: the scalar field carries the trivial representation") · PHY 513, Problem Set 5, Problem 1(b) (spin 0: "the generators are numbers, which commute", as the user wrote it) · Yu §4.2.1, eq. (4.27)*

^thm-c3-4-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Numbers commute.** In one dimension the six generators are complex numbers, so every commutator vanishes.
>
> **2. The rotation generators vanish.** $[J_1, J_2] = iJ_3$, $[J_2, J_3] = iJ_1$, $[J_3, J_1] = iJ_2$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]) with left sides $0$ give $\mathbf J = 0$.
>
> **3. The boost generators vanish.** $[J_1, K_2] = iK_3$, $[J_2, K_3] = iK_1$, $[J_3, K_1] = iK_2$ (the relation $[J_i, K_j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}K_k$) with left sides $0$ give $\mathbf K = 0$.
>
> **4. The group elements.** $D(e^\omega) = \exp(-i\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf J - i\boldsymbol\eta\cdot\mathbf K) = e^0 = 1$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-2-4|Theorem §C3.2.4]]); every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$ is a product of exponentials ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-3|Theorem §C1a.4.3]] with [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-6-3|Theorem §C1a.6.3]]), so $D(\Lambda) = 1$. In Def. §C3.3.1 this is $j_+ = j_- = 0$.
>
> **5. Total generator.** With $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}) = 0$, Theorem §C3.4.3 leaves $\mathbb J^{\mu\nu} = L^{\mu\nu}$ and $\mathbb J_i = L_i$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - "The scalar does not transform" refers to the index representation only; the field still changes through its argument, $\phi \mapsto \phi(\Lambda^{-1}x)$, generated by $L^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^cau-c1b-1-1|§C1b.1, Caution: What "the scalar does not change" does not mean]]).
> - Only the proper orthochronous group is constrained; parity may still act by a sign ([[§C3.4 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^rem-c3-4-3|Remark: Scalars, pseudoscalars and spin zero]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.5: Vector Fields, Upper and Lower Index
> 1. A vector field transforms with $D = \Lambda$: $A'^\mu(x) = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu A^\nu(\Lambda^{-1}x)$, with spin generators $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$, so infinitesimally
>
> $$
> \delta A^\alpha = -\frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl(L^{\mu\nu}A^\alpha + (\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})^\alpha{}_\beta A^\beta\bigr) ;
> $$
>
> its representation is $(\frac12, \frac12)$, and under rotations $A^0$ is spin $0$ and $\mathbf A$ spin $1$.
> 2. Lower components $A_\mu = g_{\mu\nu}A^\nu$ transform with $\Lambda_\mu{}^\nu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu$, the matrix $g\Lambda g^{-1} = (\Lambda^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$: a representation equivalent to the upper one through $g$, hence also $(\frac12, \frac12)$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.5, eq. (fieldtransforms); §7.3 (Derivation "The spin content of a four-vector") · PHY 513 Lecture 7, Part C ("Transformation of Vector and Spinor Fields") · PS §3.1, p. 37 · Yu §4.2.2, eqs. (4.28)–(4.37)*

^thm-c3-4-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The law and its generators.** The four-vector field is [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^ex-c1b-1-1|Example §C1b.1.1]], $D = \Lambda$; the generators of $D(\Lambda) = \Lambda$ are $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-6-2|Theorem §C1a.6.2]]). Theorem §C3.4.3 with $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}) = \mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$ gives the infinitesimal law.
>
> **2. Its label and rotation content.** [[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]: $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$ is $(\frac12, \frac12)$, with $\mathbf J^2 = \operatorname{diag}(0, 2, 2, 2)$, so $s(s + 1) = 0$ on $A^0$ and $2$ on $\mathbf A$.
>
> **3. Lower components.** $A'_\mu = g_{\mu\alpha}A'^\alpha = g_{\mu\alpha}\Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta A^\beta = g_{\mu\alpha}\Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta g^{\beta\nu}A_\nu$ (argument $\Lambda^{-1}x$ suppressed), so the matrix acting on lower components is $(g\Lambda g^{-1})_\mu{}^\nu = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu$.
>
> **4. It is the inverse transpose.** From $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}g\Lambda = g$ ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^def-c1a-4-1|Def. §C1a.4.1]]): $g\Lambda = (\Lambda^{\mathsf T})^{-1}g$, so $g\Lambda g^{-1} = (\Lambda^{\mathsf T})^{-1} = (\Lambda^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$, entrywise $\Lambda_\mu{}^\nu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|REL Theorem §B1.2.3]]).
>
> **5. Equivalence.** $\Lambda \mapsto g\Lambda g^{-1}$ is the vector representation conjugated by the fixed invertible matrix $g$, hence equivalent to it ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^def-c3-1-7|Def. §C3.1.7]]); its generators are $g\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}g^{-1}$, which are the lower-index spin generators of Example §C1b.1.1 times $i$. Equivalent representations have the same Casimirs, so the label is again $(\frac12, \frac12)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Upper and lower indices are two bases of one representation, related by the invariant tensor $g$: raising and lowering *is* the equivalence. For the rotation group the same statement is trivial ($R = (R^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$); for the Lorentz group it costs the metric.
> - $A^0$ and $\mathbf A$ are not separate representations of the Lorentz group: boosts mix them. The extra spin-$0$ component of a vector field is what gauge invariance or a constraint must remove for a spin-$1$ particle (QFT C4: [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]], the Lorenz condition [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]], gauge invariance [[§C4.6 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^rem-c4-6-3|§C4.6, Remark: Why a massless vector field must be a gauge field]]).

> [!example] Example §C3.4.1: The Gradient of a Scalar Field
> If $\phi$ is a scalar field, show that $\partial_\mu\phi$ is a vector field with lower index.
>
> By the chain rule applied to $\phi'(x) = \phi(\Lambda^{-1}x)$, with $y^\nu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu x^\mu$ so that $\partial y^\nu/\partial x^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu$,
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu\phi'(x) = \frac{\partial y^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}\,(\partial_\nu\phi)(\Lambda^{-1}x) = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu\,(\partial_\nu\phi)(\Lambda^{-1}x) = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu\,(\partial_\nu\phi)(\Lambda^{-1}x) ,
> $$
>
> the law of Theorem §C3.4.5, 2. So differentiation maps $(0, 0)$ fields to $(\frac12, \frac12)$ fields: $\partial_\mu$ itself carries the vector representation, which is why every derivative in a Lagrangian must be contracted.
>

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.6: Two-Index Tensor Fields
> A field $T^{\mu\nu}$ transforms as $T'^{\mu\nu}(x) = \Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta T^{\alpha\beta}(\Lambda^{-1}x)$, i.e. with $D = \Lambda\otimes\Lambda$ and spin generators $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}\otimes\mathbb 1 + \mathbb 1\otimes\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$. Over $\mathbb C$ its invariant pieces ([[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-6|Theorem §C1a.5.6]]) are
>
> $$
> \underbrace{\tfrac14g^{\mu\nu}T^\rho{}_\rho}_{(0,\,0)}\ \oplus\ \underbrace{T^{[\mu\nu]}}_{(1,\,0)\oplus(0,\,1)}\ \oplus\ \underbrace{T^{(\mu\nu)} - \tfrac14g^{\mu\nu}T^\rho{}_\rho}_{(1,\,1)} ,
> $$
>
> the two halves of the antisymmetric part being the eigenspaces $\star A = \pm iA$ of duality ([[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-7|Theorem §C1a.5.7]]). Over $\mathbb R$, the six-dimensional real antisymmetric tensors are irreducible.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.4.5 (Derivation "Products: two-index tensors are (1,1)⊕(1,0)⊕(0,1)⊕(0,0)"; checked numerically there: "This proves the irreducibility that Section [tensors] could only quote") · the matching argument and the real case written out here*

^thm-c3-4-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The representation.** Each upper index transforms with $\Lambda$ ([[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-3|Theorem §C1a.5.3]]), so $D = \Lambda\otimes\Lambda$ on $\mathbb C^4\otimes\mathbb C^4$; its generators add ([[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]], step 1).
>
> **2. Its decomposition.** With $\Lambda \cong (\frac12, \frac12)$ (Theorem §C3.3.4), Theorem §C3.3.7 gives $(1, 1)\oplus(1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)\oplus(0, 0)$, each once, of dimensions $9, 3, 3, 1$.
>
> **3. Four invariant subspaces.** By Theorem §C1a.5.6 the trace part (dimension $1$), the antisymmetric part ($6$) and the symmetric traceless part ($9$) are each mapped into themselves by every $\Lambda$; by Theorem §C1a.5.7, 3, the antisymmetric part is, over $\mathbb C$, the sum of two invariant three-dimensional eigenspaces of $\star$. So $\mathbb C^{16} = W_1\oplus W_3\oplus W_3'\oplus W_9$ with invariant $W_d$ of dimension $d$.
>
> **4. Match by dimension.** Each $W_d$ is a representation, hence a sum of pieces $(j_+, j_-)$ ([[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]]); joining these decompositions decomposes $\mathbb C^{16}$, and multiplicities are unique (Theorem §C3.3.2, step 9), so the pieces of the four $W_d$ together are exactly $(1, 1)$, $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$, $(0, 0)$, once each. The only piece of dimension $1$ is $(0, 0)$, so $W_1 = (0, 0)$. A three-dimensional $W_3$ must be made of pieces from $\{9, 3, 3\}$ with dimensions summing to $3$: a single $(1, 0)$ or $(0, 1)$, and $W_3$, $W_3'$ take one each. What remains, $W_9$, is $(1, 1)$.
>
> **5. Real irreducibility.** Let $U$ be a real subspace of the real antisymmetric tensors, invariant under every $\Lambda$. Its complexification $U_{\mathbb C} = U + iU$ is an invariant subspace of $(1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)$ and is closed under complex conjugation. The invariant subspaces of a sum of two inequivalent irreducible representations are $0$, either summand, or the whole (an invariant subspace is a sum of pieces, and with multiplicity one each piece is a fixed subspace). Complex conjugation maps the $(+i)$-eigenspace of the real map $\star$ to the $(-i)$-eigenspace (Theorem §C1a.5.7, 3), so neither summand alone is closed under it. Hence $U_{\mathbb C} = 0$ or everything, i.e. $U = 0$ or all six dimensions.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The decomposition of Theorem §C1a.5.6 is the finest possible over $\mathbb R$; over $\mathbb C$ only the antisymmetric part splits further, and only by complex combinations (the duality eigenvalues are $\pm i$ because $\star^2 = -1$).
> - The symmetric traceless tensor contains spin $0\oplus1\oplus2$ under rotations ([[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-5|Theorem §C3.3.5]]); the field of a massless spin-2 particle starts from such a tensor.
> - Which half is $(1, 0)$: Theorem §C3.4.7.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.7: The Field Strength Is (1, 0) ⊕ (0, 1)
> Under the spin part of the tensor law, $\delta_sF^{\mu\nu} = \omega^\mu{}_\alpha F^{\alpha\nu} + \omega^\nu{}_\beta F^{\mu\beta}$, the fields of [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^def-c1a-7-2|Def. §C1a.7.2]] change by
>
> $$
> \delta\mathbf E = \boldsymbol\theta\times\mathbf E - \boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf B, \qquad \delta\mathbf B = \boldsymbol\theta\times\mathbf B + \boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf E, \qquad \delta(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B) = (\boldsymbol\theta \mp i\boldsymbol\eta)\times(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B) .
> $$
>
> Hence $\mathbf E - i\mathbf B$ transforms in $(1, 0)$ and $\mathbf E + i\mathbf B$ in $(0, 1)$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.4.5 ("the antisymmetric part is (1,0)⊕(0,1), its two halves being … E + iB and E − iB for the field strength"; which half is which is not stated there) · the computation written out here*

^thm-c3-4-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The parameters.** For a rotation, $\omega_{jk} = \varepsilon_{jkl}\theta_l$ ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-2|Def. §C1a.6.2]]), so $\omega^j{}_k = g^{jj}\omega_{jk} = -\varepsilon_{jkl}\theta_l$ and $\omega^0{}_\mu = 0$. For a boost, $\omega_{0i} = \eta_i = -\omega_{i0}$, so $\omega^0{}_i = \eta_i$, $\omega^i{}_0 = g^{ii}\omega_{i0} = \eta_i$, and $\omega^i{}_j = 0$. The fields are $F^{0i} = -E^i$, $F^{ij} = -\varepsilon_{ijk}B^k$, $B^i = -\frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}F^{jk}$.
>
> **2. E under a rotation.** $\delta_sF^{0j} = \omega^0{}_\alpha F^{\alpha j} + \omega^j{}_\beta F^{0\beta} = 0 + \omega^j{}_kF^{0k}$ ($\omega^j{}_0 = 0$). So $-\delta E^j = -\varepsilon_{jkl}\theta_l(-E^k)$, i.e. $\delta E^j = -\varepsilon_{jkl}\theta_lE^k = \varepsilon_{jlk}\theta_lE^k = (\boldsymbol\theta\times\mathbf E)_j$.
>
> **3. B under a rotation.** $\delta_sF^{jk} = \omega^j{}_lF^{lk} + \omega^k{}_lF^{jl} = (-\varepsilon_{jln}\theta_n)(-\varepsilon_{lkm}B^m) + (-\varepsilon_{kln}\theta_n)(-\varepsilon_{jlm}B^m)$. Contract: $\varepsilon_{jln}\varepsilon_{lkm} = \varepsilon_{lnj}\varepsilon_{lkm} = \delta_{nk}\delta_{jm} - \delta_{nm}\delta_{jk}$ and $\varepsilon_{kln}\varepsilon_{jlm} = \varepsilon_{lnk}\varepsilon_{lmj} = \delta_{nm}\delta_{kj} - \delta_{nj}\delta_{km}$, so $\delta_sF^{jk} = \theta_kB^j - \delta_{jk}\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf B + \delta_{jk}\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf B - \theta_jB^k = \theta_kB^j - \theta_jB^k$. Then $\delta B^i = -\frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}(\theta_kB^j - \theta_jB^k) = -\frac12\bigl((\mathbf B\times\boldsymbol\theta)_i - (\boldsymbol\theta\times\mathbf B)_i\bigr) = (\boldsymbol\theta\times\mathbf B)_i$.
>
> **4. E under a boost.** $\delta_sF^{0j} = \omega^0{}_kF^{kj} + \omega^j{}_0F^{00} = \eta_k(-\varepsilon_{kjm}B^m) + 0$. So $\delta E^j = \varepsilon_{kjm}\eta_kB^m = -\varepsilon_{jkm}\eta_kB^m = -(\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf B)_j$.
>
> **5. B under a boost.** $\delta_sF^{jk} = \omega^j{}_0F^{0k} + \omega^k{}_0F^{j0} = \eta_j(-E^k) + \eta_kE^j$ (using $F^{j0} = -F^{0j} = E^j$). Then $\delta B^i = -\frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}(\eta_kE^j - \eta_jE^k) = -\frac12\bigl((\mathbf E\times\boldsymbol\eta)_i - (\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf E)_i\bigr) = (\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf E)_i$. ⚑ By-product: for a boost along $z$ this is $\delta E_x = \eta B_y$, the first-order part of $E'_x = \gamma(E_x + vB_y)$ of [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^ex-c1a-7-1|Example §C1a.7.1]] — a check on every sign above.
>
> **6. The complex combinations.** Add steps 2–5 for a general $\omega$ and form $\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B$: $\delta(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B) = \boldsymbol\theta\times(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B) - \boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf B \mp i\,\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf E$. Since $\mp i\boldsymbol\eta\times(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B) = \mp i\,\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf E + (\mp i)(\mp i)\,\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf B = \mp i\,\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf E - \boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf B$, this is $(\boldsymbol\theta \mp i\boldsymbol\eta)\times(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B)$.
>
> **7. Compare with (1, 0) and (0, 1).** The Cartesian spin-1 matrices $(J^{(1)}_k)_{lm} = -i\varepsilon_{klm}$ obey the rotation algebra with $\mathbf J^{(1)2} = 2$ ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^ex-c5-1-2|QM Example §C5.1.2]]), so they are a spin-1 multiplet in another basis, and $(-i\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf J^{(1)}\mathbf v)_l = -i\alpha_k(-i\varepsilon_{klm})v_m = -\varepsilon_{klm}\alpha_kv_m = (\boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf v)_l$. On $(1, 0)$, Theorem §C3.2.4 to first order is $1 - i(\boldsymbol\theta - i\boldsymbol\eta)\cdot\mathbf J^{(1)}$, i.e. $\delta\mathbf v = (\boldsymbol\theta - i\boldsymbol\eta)\times\mathbf v$; on $(0, 1)$, $\delta\mathbf v = (\boldsymbol\theta + i\boldsymbol\eta)\times\mathbf v$. Step 6 matches the first for $\mathbf E - i\mathbf B$ and the second for $\mathbf E + i\mathbf B$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - A Lorentz transformation acts on $\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B$ as a rotation through a complex angle, so it preserves $(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B)\cdot(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B)$, the complex invariant of [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1a-7-3|Theorem §C1a.7.3]]: the complex orthogonal $O(\Lambda)$ found there is $e^{(\boldsymbol\theta + i\boldsymbol\eta)\times}$.
> - The labels depend on the sign conventions for $\mathbf K$ and for $\mathbf B$ ([[§C3.4 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^cau-c3-4-1|Caution: Which half of F is called (1, 0)]]).
> - Used next: the photon field ([[§C4.6 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^rem-c4-6-3|§C4.6, Remark: Why a massless vector field must be a gauge field]], [[§C4.7 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-7-1|Theorem §C4.7.1]]); parity exchanges the halves ([[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-8|Theorem §C3.3.8]]).

> [!definition] Definition §C3.4.2: Spinor Fields

In the Dirac case the lecture writes $D(\Lambda) = \Lambda_{1/2} = \exp(-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}S^{\mu\nu})$ with $S^{\mu\nu} = \frac i4[\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu]$; in its chiral basis $S^{ij} = \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^k, \sigma^k)$ and $S^{0i} = -\frac i2\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^i, -\sigma^i)$, so the upper block has $\mathbf J = \frac12\boldsymbol\sigma$, $\mathbf K = -\frac i2\boldsymbol\sigma$ and the lower $\mathbf K = +\frac i2\boldsymbol\sigma$: exactly $(\frac12, 0)$ and $(0, \frac12)$ of Theorem §C3.3.3, stacked. > [!caution] Caution: Two meanings of S^μν
> In §C1b.1 and §C1b.8 the spin generators $S^{\mu\nu}$ are real-convention, $D = 1 + \frac12\omega_{\mu\nu}S^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]]); the Dirac $S^{\mu\nu} = \frac i4[\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu]$ of the lecture and Peskin–Schroeder is Hermitian-convention, $\Lambda_{1/2} = 1 - \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}S^{\mu\nu}$, i.e. it is $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$. By Theorem §C3.4.3 the §C1b.1 spin generator of the Dirac field is therefore $-i$ times the lecture's $S^{\mu\nu}$. Yu writes $I^{\mu\nu} = iS^{\mu\nu}$ for the Hermitian-convention matrices ([[§C1b.8 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor|§C1b.8]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Why a spinor's matrix is fixed only up to sign
> For $(\frac12, 0)$, $(0, \frac12)$ and their sum, $j_+ + j_- = \frac12$, and a rotation by $2\pi$, the identity of $SO^+(1,3)$, acts as $-1$ ([[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-5|Theorem §C3.3.5]]). So the law $\psi \mapsto D(\Lambda)\psi(\Lambda^{-1}x)$ is defined up to $\psi \mapsto -\psi$: a two-valued representation ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^def-c3-1-14|Def. §C3.1.14]]), single-valued on $SL(2, \mathbb C)$ ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^rem-c3-1-8|§C3.1, Remark: The same pattern for the Lorentz group]]; [[§C5a.4 SL(2,C) and the Group Action on Spinor Space#^thm-c5a-4-5|Theorem §C5a.4.5]], [[§C5a.4 SL(2,C) and the Group Action on Spinor Space#^thm-c5a-4-7|Theorem §C5a.4.7]]). Nothing observable is affected: observables are built from bilinears in $\psi$ (currents, $\bar\psi\psi$, the Lagrangian), in which the sign cancels, just as the overall phase of a quantum state is unobservable ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-4|QM Theorem §C5.2.4]] measures only a relative sign). The general law [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^thm-c1b-1-1|Theorem §C1b.1.1]] assumed $D$ to be a representation; for spinors it is one up to sign, and every statement proved from it holds up to that sign.
>

## Invariant Lagrangians

> [!remark] Remark: Invariant Lagrangians are (0, 0) pieces
> An action is Lorentz invariant when its Lagrangian density transforms as a scalar ([[§C1b.2 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1b-2-1|Theorem §C1b.2.1]]). A product of fields and derivatives at one point transforms in the tensor product of their representations ([[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]]; $\partial_\mu$ counts as $(\frac12, \frac12)$, [[§C3.4 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^ex-c3-4-1|Example §C3.4.1]]), and only its $(0, 0)$ components are numbers that every observer agrees on. "Contract all indices" is the rule that extracts them. The three Lagrangians previewed on the slides:
> - *scalar*, $\frac12\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi - \frac12m^2\phi^2$: $g^{\mu\nu}$ picks the $(0, 0)$ of $(\frac12, \frac12)\otimes(\frac12, \frac12)$, the trace piece of Theorem §C3.4.6;
> - *vector*, $-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$: $F\otimes F$ with $F = (1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)$ contains $(0, 0)$ twice, once from $(1, 0)\otimes(1, 0)$ and once from $(0, 1)\otimes(0, 1)$; the two invariants are $(\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B)^2$, i.e. $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ and $F_{\mu\nu}\tilde F^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1a-7-2|Theorem §C1a.7.2]]); parity keeps the first and reverses the second;
> - *Dirac*, $\bar\psi(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)\psi$: since $D$ is not unitary, $\psi^\dagger$ does not transform with $D^{-1}$ and $\psi^\dagger\psi$ is not invariant ([[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-10|Theorem §C3.3.10]]); $\psi_L^\dagger$ is $(0, \frac12)$ (complex conjugation, [[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-9|Theorem §C3.3.9]]), so $\psi_L^\dagger\otimes\psi_L = (\frac12, \frac12)$ is a vector and must be contracted with $\partial_\mu$. The details, $\bar\psi$ and the covariance of $\gamma^\mu$, are QFT C5a ([[§C5a.2 The Dirac Form#^def-c5a-2-5|Def. §C5a.2.5]], [[§C5a.6 The Dirac Conjugate and the Bilinears#^thm-c5a-6-2|Theorem §C5a.6.2]], [[§C5a.4 SL(2,C) and the Group Action on Spinor Space#^thm-c5a-4-11|Theorem §C5a.4.11]], [[§C5a.7 The Dirac Equation and Its Lagrangian#^thm-c5a-7-1|Theorem §C5a.7.1]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.5 ("Preview: invariant Lagrangians"; Principle "Components and functions", paragraph "Where (0,0) returns") · PHY 513 Lecture 7, Part C ("Preview: Invariant Lagrangians")*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The inverse in $\phi(\Lambda^{-1}x)$ and the orbital operator $\mathbf L = -i\,\mathbf x\times\nabla$ are Quantum Mechanics' rotated wave function $\psi(R^{-1}\mathbf x)$ and its generator, extended from rotations to boosts — [[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-1|QM Theorem §C5.4.1]], [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^rem-c1b-1-1|§C1b.1, Remark: Why the inverse]].
> - "Total = orbital + spin" for a field is the same tensor-product rule as $\hat{\mathbf J} = \hat{\mathbf L} + \hat{\mathbf S}$ for a particle with spin in Quantum Mechanics; the field's components play the role of the spin space — [[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-5|QM Theorem §C7.1.5]], [[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]].
> - The orbital and spin generators are what Noether's theorem turns into the orbital and spin parts of the conserved Lorentz current; the boost part $L^{0i} = -i(t\partial_i + x^i\partial_t)$ becomes the conserved boost charge that makes the centre of energy move uniformly — [[§C1b.8 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor#^thm-c1b-8-1|Theorem §C1b.8.1]], [[§C1b.8 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor#^thm-c1b-8-3|Theorem §C1b.8.3]].
> - For a quantum field the classical law becomes $U(\Lambda)^{-1}\hat\Phi_a(x)U(\Lambda) = D(\Lambda)_a{}^b\hat\Phi_b(\Lambda^{-1}x)$, and its infinitesimal form puts the Hilbert-space generators $\hat J^{\mu\nu}$, the differential operators $L^{\mu\nu}$ and the matrices $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ into one commutator — [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-5-8|Principle §C3.5.8]], [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-10|Theorem §C3.5.10]].
> - Lorentz-invariant distributions such as $\delta^4(x)$, $D_W$, $D_F$ are the scalar fields annihilated by every $L^{\mu\nu}$; that is why the two-point functions of a scalar field can depend only on $x^2$ and, inside the cone, on the sign of $x^0$ — [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-7|Theorem §CA.2.7]], [[§C2b.2 The Wightman Function|§C2b.2]].
> - The field strength's halves $\mathbf E \mp i\mathbf B$, the duality eigenspaces of $\star$, and the generator halves $\mathbf J \pm i\mathbf K$ are the same split seen in two representations (the antisymmetric tensor is the adjoint representation) — [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^rem-c3-2-3|§C3.2, Remark: The algebra is the infinitesimal tensor law]], [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1a-7-3|Theorem §C1a.7.3]].
> - A vector field carries one spin-$0$ component besides spin $1$; removing it (by the Lorenz condition for a massive field, by gauge invariance for a massless one) is the central problem of the vector field; for massless particles it reappears as the little-group translations that shift a polarization vector by the momentum — [[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]], [[§C4.7 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-7-1|Theorem §C4.7.1]], null states [[§C4.8 The Gupta–Bleuler Condition and Physical Photons#^thm-c4-8-6|Theorem §C4.8.6]], [[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-7-8|Theorem §C3.7.8]].
> - The spinor law $\psi'(x) = \Lambda_{1/2}\psi(\Lambda^{-1}x)$ acts on the same index as a change of basis $\psi \to U\psi$ of spinor space, but is a different operation (physical versus descriptive), and $\gamma^\mu$ is fixed by the first and not by the second — [[§C5a.4 SL(2,C) and the Group Action on Spinor Space#^cau-c5a-4-1|§C5a.4, Caution: Three different transformations]], [[§C5a.4 SL(2,C) and the Group Action on Spinor Space#^rem-c5a-4-4|§C5a.4, Remark: Why γ is fixed under a Lorentz transformation but not under a change of basis]].
