Why does a massless particle of spin $s$ have two polarization states, not $2s + 1$, and why is its helicity the same for every observer? Wigner's construction ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-4|Theorem §C3.6.4]]) reduces the question to the little group of a lightlike momentum, which is not the rotation group: a massless particle has no rest frame ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-6|Theorem §C3.6.6]]). This section finds the little group, the Euclidean group of a plane, shows that its "translations" must act trivially on physical states, and derives the invariance and quantization of helicity from the algebra and the topology of the Lorentz group ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]], [[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations|§C3.1]]). The same translations, acting on polarization vectors, are where gauge invariance of a massless vector field comes from ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]], [[§C4.6 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^rem-c4-6-3|§C4.6, Remark: Why a massless vector field must be a gauge field]]; null states, [[§C4.8 The Gupta–Bleuler Condition and Physical Photons#^thm-c4-8-6|Theorem §C4.8.6]]).

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): as in [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations|§C3.5]] and [[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group|§C3.6★]]; rotations are active, $U(R_z(\theta)) = e^{-i\theta J^3}$, $K^i = J^{0i}$. The standard momentum is $k = \kappa(1, 0, 0, 1)$, $\kappa > 0$, with $\tilde k \equiv k/\kappa = (1, 0, 0, 1)$ and $\tilde t \equiv (1, 0, 0, 0)$. Yu's angle is passive, so his phases $e^{+i\lambda\theta}$ ((3.208)–(3.214)) are $e^{-i\lambda\theta}$ here; his matrix $S(\alpha, \beta)$ and generators $A$, $B$ are the same.

## The little group of a lightlike momentum

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.7.1: The Massless Little Group Is ISO(2)
> Every $W \in SO^+(1,3)$ with $Wk = k$ is, uniquely, $W = S(\alpha, \beta)\,R_z(\theta)$ with $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb R$, $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$, where $R_z(\theta)$ is the rotation by $\theta$ about the $z$-axis and
>
> $$
> S(\alpha, \beta) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + \zeta & \alpha & \beta & -\zeta \\ \alpha & 1 & 0 & -\alpha \\ \beta & 0 & 1 & -\beta \\ \zeta & \alpha & \beta & 1 - \zeta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \zeta = \tfrac12(\alpha^2 + \beta^2) .
> $$
>
> The $S$'s compose by adding parameters, $S(\alpha_1, \beta_1)S(\alpha_2, \beta_2) = S(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \beta_1 + \beta_2)$, and a rotation turns them like a vector of the plane, $R_z(\theta)\,S(\mathbf v)\,R_z(\theta)^{-1} = S(r_\theta\mathbf v)$ for $\mathbf v = (\alpha, \beta)$ and $r_\theta$ the rotation of $\mathbb R^2$ by $\theta$. So $G_k \cong ISO(2)$, the isometries $\mathbf x \mapsto r_\theta\mathbf x + \mathbf v$ of the Euclidean plane, with $(\alpha, \beta)$ as translations.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.7 (Step 1 of the massless derivation) · Yu §3.3.2, eqs. (3.178)–(3.190) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.3 ("The little group is ISO(2)"); the exponential form and the conjugation rule in the active convention written out here*

^thm-c3-7-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (what $W$ does to a timelike reference vector). Let $t \equiv W\tilde t$. A Lorentz transformation preserves Minkowski products and $W\tilde k = \tilde k$, so $t\cdot\tilde k = (W\tilde t)\cdot(W\tilde k) = \tilde t\cdot\tilde k = 1$ and $t\cdot t = \tilde t\cdot\tilde t = 1$; $W$ is orthochronous, so $t^0 > 0$.
>
> **Step 2** (solve for $t$). $t\cdot\tilde k = t^0 - t^3 = 1$, so write $t^3 = \zeta$, $t^0 = 1 + \zeta$, $t^1 = \alpha$, $t^2 = \beta$. Then $t\cdot t = (1 + \zeta)^2 - \alpha^2 - \beta^2 - \zeta^2 = 1 + 2\zeta - \alpha^2 - \beta^2$, and $t\cdot t = 1$ gives $\zeta = \frac12(\alpha^2 + \beta^2)$, with $\alpha$, $\beta$ unrestricted: $t = (1 + \zeta, \alpha, \beta, \zeta)$.
>
> **Step 3** ($S(\alpha, \beta)$ is an exponential). Let $N(\alpha, \beta)$ be the matrix with rows $(0, \alpha, \beta, 0)$, $(\alpha, 0, 0, -\alpha)$, $(\beta, 0, 0, -\beta)$, $(0, \alpha, \beta, 0)$. Multiplying row by column, $N^2$ has rows $2\zeta(1, 0, 0, -1)$, $0$, $0$, $2\zeta(1, 0, 0, -1)$ (row 0 times column 0 is $\alpha^2 + \beta^2$, times column 3 is $-\alpha^2 - \beta^2$, all other entries cancel in pairs), and $N^3 = N^2N = 0$ because $(1, 0, 0, -1)N$ is row 0 minus row 3 of $N$, which is zero. So the exponential series stops:
>
> $$
> e^{N} = \mathbb 1 + N + \tfrac12N^2 = S(\alpha, \beta) .
> $$
>
> Lowering the first index, $\omega_{\mu\nu} = g_{\mu\rho}N^\rho{}_\nu$ has $\omega_{01} = \omega_{13} = \alpha$, $\omega_{02} = \omega_{23} = \beta$, all others fixed by antisymmetry or zero: it is antisymmetric, so $S(\alpha, \beta) \in SO^+(1,3)$ ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-6-4|Theorem §C1a.6.4]]).
>
> **Step 4** ($S$ fixes $k$ and carries $\tilde t$ to $t$). $N\tilde k$: row 0 gives $0$, rows 1 and 2 give $\alpha - \alpha = 0$ and $\beta - \beta = 0$, row 3 gives $0$; so $N\tilde k = 0$ and $S\tilde k = \tilde k$, $Sk = k$. $S\tilde t$ is the first column of $S$, $(1 + \zeta, \alpha, \beta, \zeta) = t$.
>
> **Step 5** (what is left is a rotation about $z$). $Q \equiv S(\alpha, \beta)^{-1}W$ satisfies $Q\tilde t = S^{-1}t = \tilde t$ and $Q\tilde k = \tilde k$. Fixing $\tilde t = (1, \mathbf 0)$ makes $Q = \operatorname{diag}(1, R)$ with $R \in SO(3)$ (Steps 1–3 of [[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^der-c3-6-7|Derivation §C3.6.7]] with $m = 1$). Fixing $\tilde k$ then says $R\hat{\mathbf z} = \hat{\mathbf z}$, so $R$ maps the $xy$-plane to itself orthogonally with determinant 1: $R = R_z(\theta)$ for one $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$. Hence $W = S(\alpha, \beta)R_z(\theta)$. Conversely every such product fixes $k$ ($R_z$ does, and $S$ by Step 4).
>
> **Step 6** (uniqueness). If $S(\alpha, \beta)R_z(\theta) = S(\alpha', \beta')R_z(\theta')$, apply both sides to $\tilde t$, which $R_z$ fixes: the first columns of $S$ agree, so $(\alpha, \beta) = (\alpha', \beta')$; cancelling $S$, $R_z(\theta) = R_z(\theta')$.
>
> **Step 7** (composition of the $S$'s). $N$ is linear in $(\alpha, \beta)$: $N = \alpha N_1 + \beta N_2$. The products $N_1N_2$ and $N_2N_1$ vanish (each row of $N_1$ is $0$, $\pm$ row 1 of $N_2$, or row 0 minus row 3 of $N_2$, all zero rows, and symmetrically), so $N_1$ and $N_2$ commute and exponents add: $S(\alpha_1, \beta_1)S(\alpha_2, \beta_2) = e^{N(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2,\, \beta_1 + \beta_2)} = S(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \beta_1 + \beta_2)$.
>
> **Step 8** (conjugation by a rotation). In block form for the coordinates $(t; x, y; z)$, $N(\mathbf v)$ has $\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}$ in the $t$- and $z$-rows, $\mathbf v$ in the $t$-column and $-\mathbf v$ in the $z$-column, and $R_z(\theta) = \operatorname{diag}(1, r_\theta, 1)$. Then $R_zN(\mathbf v)R_z^{-1}$ has $\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}r_\theta^{-1} = (r_\theta\mathbf v)^{\mathsf T}$ in those rows and $r_\theta\mathbf v$ in those columns: $R_zN(\mathbf v)R_z^{-1} = N(r_\theta\mathbf v)$, and conjugation passes through the exponential. So $R_z(\theta)S(\mathbf v)R_z(\theta)^{-1} = S(r_\theta\mathbf v)$; equivalently $R_z(\theta)^{-1}S(\alpha, \beta)R_z(\theta) = S(\alpha\cos\theta + \beta\sin\theta,\ -\alpha\sin\theta + \beta\cos\theta)$ (checked numerically).
>
> **Step 9** (the group law is that of ISO(2)). By Steps 7–8, $S(\mathbf v_1)R_z(\theta_1)\,S(\mathbf v_2)R_z(\theta_2) = S(\mathbf v_1)\bigl(R_z(\theta_1)S(\mathbf v_2)R_z(\theta_1)^{-1}\bigr)R_z(\theta_1)R_z(\theta_2) = S(\mathbf v_1 + r_{\theta_1}\mathbf v_2)\,R_z(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$, which is the composition $(\mathbf v_1, r_{\theta_1})(\mathbf v_2, r_{\theta_2}) = (\mathbf v_1 + r_{\theta_1}\mathbf v_2, r_{\theta_1}r_{\theta_2})$ of the plane isometries $\mathbf x \mapsto r_\theta\mathbf x + \mathbf v$. With Steps 5–6 the map $(\mathbf v, \theta) \mapsto S(\mathbf v)R_z(\theta)$ is an isomorphism $ISO(2) \to G_k$, and $\{S(\mathbf v)\}$ is a normal subgroup.
>
> ⚑ By-product: the "translations" $S(\alpha, \beta)$ are not translations of spacetime; each is a combination of a boost and a rotation that happens to fix one null direction ([[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]] gives its generators). The little group is not compact, unlike $SO(3)$ → [[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^rem-c3-7-1|Remark: No rest frame, a noncompact little group]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Only $W\tilde k = \tilde k$, the invariance of Minkowski products and orthochronicity were used; the massive argument of Theorem §C3.6.7 reappears for the leftover rotation.
> - Used next: the generators of $G_k$ (Theorem §C3.7.2) and the action on polarization vectors ([[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-7-8|Theorem §C3.7.8]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.7.2: Generators of the Massless Little Group
> With $J^i$ and $K^i = J^{0i}$ the Hilbert-space generators ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]]), let
>
> $$
> A \equiv J^2 - K^1, \qquad B \equiv -J^1 - K^2 .
> $$
>
> 1. $U(S(\alpha, \beta)) = e^{i(\alpha A + \beta B)}$ and $U(R_z(\theta)) = e^{-i\theta J^3}$; in the vector representation the same formulas hold with the matrices $\mathcal J^i$, $\mathcal K^i$.
> 2. $[J^3, A] = iB$, $[J^3, B] = -iA$, $[A, B] = 0$: the algebra of $ISO(2)$, the same as that of $(J^3, P^1, P^2)$ in the Poincaré algebra ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-5|Theorem §C3.5.5]]).
> 3. $U(R_z(\theta))^{-1}\,A\,U(R_z(\theta)) = A\cos\theta - B\sin\theta$ and $U(R_z(\theta))^{-1}\,B\,U(R_z(\theta)) = A\sin\theta + B\cos\theta$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.7 (Step 2 of the massless derivation; Weinberg's $A = J_2 + K_1$, $B = -J_1 + K_2$ with his $K = J^{i0}$) · Yu §3.3.2, eqs. (3.191)–(3.203) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.3 ("Generators"; Note "Checking")*

^thm-c3-7-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the parameters of $S$). From Step 3 of [[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^der-c3-7-1|Derivation §C3.7.1]], $S(\alpha, \beta) = e^{N}$ with lowered components $\omega_{01} = \omega_{13} = \alpha$, $\omega_{02} = \omega_{23} = \beta$, $\omega_{03} = \omega_{12} = 0$. Its generator, by [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-1|Def. §C1a.6.1]], is $-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu} = -i\sum_{\mu<\nu}\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$ (the $\frac12$ removes the double count):
>
> $$
> -i\bigl(\alpha\mathcal J^{01} + \beta\mathcal J^{02} + \alpha\mathcal J^{13} + \beta\mathcal J^{23}\bigr) = -i\bigl(\alpha\mathcal K^1 + \beta\mathcal K^2 - \alpha\mathcal J^2 + \beta\mathcal J^1\bigr) = i\alpha(\mathcal J^2 - \mathcal K^1) + i\beta(-\mathcal J^1 - \mathcal K^2),
> $$
>
> using $\mathcal J^{13} = -\mathcal J^{31} = -\mathcal J^2$ and $\mathcal J^{23} = \mathcal J^1$ ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-2|Def. §C1a.6.2]]). So $S(\alpha, \beta) = e^{i(\alpha\mathcal A + \beta\mathcal B)}$ in the vector representation (checked numerically). The rotation $R_z(\theta)$ has $\omega_{12} = \theta$ and generator $-i\theta\mathcal J^3$. On the Hilbert space the same $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ give $U = \exp(-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu})$ ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]], 4): part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (the commutators). From the Lorentz algebra in $J$, $K$ form, $[J^i, J^j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}J^k$, $[J^i, K^j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}K^k$, $[K^i, K^j] = -i\varepsilon_{ijk}J^k$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]):
> - $[J^3, A] = [J^3, J^2] - [J^3, K^1] = i\varepsilon_{321}J^1 - i\varepsilon_{312}K^2 = -iJ^1 - iK^2 = iB$;
> - $[J^3, B] = -[J^3, J^1] - [J^3, K^2] = -i\varepsilon_{312}J^2 - i\varepsilon_{321}K^1 = -iJ^2 + iK^1 = -iA$;
> - $[A, B] = -[J^2, J^1] - [J^2, K^2] + [K^1, J^1] + [K^1, K^2]$. The four terms are $-(-iJ^3) = iJ^3$; $-i\varepsilon_{22k}K^k = 0$; $-[J^1, K^1] = -i\varepsilon_{11k}K^k = 0$; and $-i\varepsilon_{123}J^3 = -iJ^3$. They sum to zero: the rotation–rotation and boost–boost terms cancel.
>
> Renaming $A \to P^1$, $B \to P^2$ gives $[J^3, P^1] = iP^2$, $[J^3, P^2] = -iP^1$, $[P^1, P^2] = 0$, which are $[J^i, P^j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}P^k$ and $[P^i, P^j] = 0$ of Theorem §C3.5.5 for $i, j \in \{1, 2\}$, $k = 3$.
>
> **Step 3** (conjugation by a finite rotation). Let $F(\theta) = e^{i\theta J^3}Ae^{-i\theta J^3}$ and $G(\theta) = e^{i\theta J^3}Be^{-i\theta J^3}$, i.e. $U(R_z(\theta))^{-1}AU(R_z(\theta))$ and likewise for $B$. Differentiating, $F' = e^{i\theta J^3}\,i[J^3, A]\,e^{-i\theta J^3} = -G$ and $G' = e^{i\theta J^3}\,i[J^3, B]\,e^{-i\theta J^3} = F$ by Step 2. So $F'' = -F$ with $F(0) = A$, $F'(0) = -B$: $F = A\cos\theta - B\sin\theta$, and $G = -F' = A\sin\theta + B\cos\theta$: part 3. (The same relation follows from Step 8 of Derivation §C3.7.1 expanded to first order in $(\alpha, \beta)$.)
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - $A$ and $B$ mix a rotation with a boost; their commutator vanishes only because the minus sign of $[K^i, K^j]$ cancels the plus sign of $[J^i, J^j]$, the Lorentzian feature of the algebra ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^rem-c3-2-2|§C3.2, Remark: Two boosts make a rotation]]).
> - Under rotations about $\hat{\mathbf k}$, $(A, B)$ turn like a vector in the plane, as $(P^1, P^2)$ do.
> - Used next: Theorem §C3.7.3.

> [!remark] Remark: No rest frame, a noncompact little group
> A massive particle's little group was the rotation group of its rest frame ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-7|Theorem §C3.6.7]]), compact, with only finite-dimensional irreducible unitary representations. A massless particle has no rest frame, and the only transformations that keep its momentum fixed are the rotations about $\hat{\mathbf k}$ and the two-parameter family $S(\alpha, \beta)$: a boost transverse to $\hat{\mathbf k}$ tilts the momentum, and the compensating rotation brings it back. The resulting group $ISO(2)$ is noncompact, like the Poincaré group itself, and it has infinite-dimensional irreducible unitary representations as well as one-dimensional ones. Which of them nature uses is decided by Theorem §C3.7.3 and Law §C3.7.4.
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §2.3 ("there is no rest frame to standardize on — the technical novelty of this case") · Yu §3.3.2, after eq. (3.190)*

## Continuous spin, and why it is excluded

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.7.3: Nonzero Translation Eigenvalues Come in Circles
> $A$ and $B$ commute and are Hermitian, so the states at the standard momentum can be chosen as joint eigenstates, $A|k; a, b\rangle = a|k; a, b\rangle$, $B|k; a, b\rangle = b|k; a, b\rangle$.
> 1. $U(R_z(\theta))|k; a, b\rangle$ is again a state at momentum $k$, with eigenvalues $(a\cos\theta - b\sin\theta,\ a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta)$.
> 2. Hence if $(a, b) \neq 0$, the states at $k$ include one for every point of the circle of radius $\rho = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, mutually orthogonal: infinitely many internal states ("continuous spin").
> 3. If the little-group representation $D$ is finite-dimensional, then $A = B = 0$ on every state at $k$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.7 (Step 3 of the massless derivation) · Yu §3.3.2, eqs. (3.200)–(3.206) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.3 ("Helicity")*

^thm-c3-7-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the rotated state stays at $k$). $R_z(\theta) \in G_k$, so $U(R_z(\theta))$ maps momentum $k$ to $R_z(\theta)k = k$ ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]], 2).
>
> **Step 2** (its eigenvalues). Write $U = U(R_z(\theta))$. By [[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]], 3,
>
> $$
> A\,U|k; a, b\rangle = U\,(U^{-1}AU)|k; a, b\rangle = U(A\cos\theta - B\sin\theta)|k; a, b\rangle = (a\cos\theta - b\sin\theta)\,U|k; a, b\rangle ,
> $$
>
> and likewise $B\,U|k; a, b\rangle = (a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta)\,U|k; a, b\rangle$: part 1. The new pair is the old one rotated by $\theta$ in the plane.
>
> **Step 3** (a circle of orthogonal states). For $(a, b) \neq 0$, distinct $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ give distinct eigenvalue pairs on the circle of radius $\rho$. Eigenvectors of a Hermitian operator for different eigenvalues are orthogonal, so the states (kernels, as the eigenvalues form a continuum) are mutually orthogonal: part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (finite dimension forces zero). The states at $k$ carry the representation $D$ of $G_k$ ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-4|Theorem §C3.6.4]], 1). If $D$ is finite-dimensional, $A$ and $B$ restricted to it are commuting Hermitian matrices with finitely many joint eigenvalue pairs. By Step 3 any pair $(a, b) \neq 0$ would bring a whole circle of distinct pairs. So the only pair is $(0, 0)$; a Hermitian matrix with only the eigenvalue $0$ is zero: $A = B = 0$ there.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The irreducible unitary representations of $ISO(2)$ split into the one-dimensional ones ($A = B = 0$, labelled by the $J^3$ eigenvalue) and infinite-dimensional ones labelled by $\rho > 0$ (functions on the circle), as for the Euclidean group of the plane acting on a free particle in two dimensions, whose momenta of fixed magnitude form a circle.
> - The Pauli–Lubanski Casimir distinguishes them: $W^2 = -\kappa^2\rho^2$ ([[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-7-7|Theorem §C3.7.7]]).

> [!law] Law §C3.7.4: Massless Particles Have No Continuous Spin
> Every observed massless particle has finitely many states at each momentum. Hence $A = B = 0$ on its states at the standard momentum ([[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], 3), and its states at $k$ are labelled by the eigenvalue of $J^3$ alone.
>
> *Valid when: for the photon and the gluons (massless, the latter confined), and for gravitons if they exist; the continuous-spin representations ($\rho > 0$) are mathematically consistent unitary representations of the Poincaré group, but no particle carrying one has been observed.*
>

## Helicity

> [!definition] Definition §C3.7.1: Helicity
> The **helicity** operator on states of nonzero momentum is $h \equiv \mathbf J\cdot\mathbf P/|\mathbf P|$, the angular momentum along the direction of motion. For a massless particle obeying Law §C3.7.4, the states at $k$ are $|k, \lambda\rangle$ with $J^3|k, \lambda\rangle = \lambda|k, \lambda\rangle$, and at a general $p$
>
> $$
> |p, \lambda\rangle \equiv U\bigl(V(p)\bigr)|k, \lambda\rangle, \qquad V(p) = R(\hat{\mathbf p})\,L_z(\eta), \quad e^\eta = |\mathbf p|/\kappa ,
> $$
>
> with $L_z(\eta) = e^{-i\eta\mathcal K^3}$ the boost along $z$ (it takes $k$ to $|\mathbf p|(1, 0, 0, 1)$) and $R(\hat{\mathbf p})$ a rotation with $R(\hat{\mathbf p})\hat{\mathbf z} = \hat{\mathbf p}$, chosen continuously away from $\hat{\mathbf p} = -\hat{\mathbf z}$ (e.g. the rotation about $\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf p}$; no choice is continuous on the whole sphere).
>

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.7.5: Helicity Is Lorentz Invariant
> For a massless particle obeying Law §C3.7.4:
> 1. the little group acts on $|k, \lambda\rangle$ by a phase: $U\bigl(S(\alpha, \beta)R_z(\theta)\bigr)|k, \lambda\rangle = e^{-i\lambda\theta}|k, \lambda\rangle$;
> 2. for every $\Lambda$ and $p$, with $W(\Lambda, p) = S(\alpha, \beta)R_z(\theta(\Lambda, p))$,
>
> $$
> U(\Lambda)\,|p, \lambda\rangle = e^{-i\lambda\theta(\Lambda, p)}\,|\Lambda p, \lambda\rangle ;
> $$
>
> 3. $h|p, \lambda\rangle = \lambda|p, \lambda\rangle$ for every $p$.
>
> So the label $\lambda$ is the helicity of the state in every frame, no Lorentz transformation changes it, and each $\lambda$ is a separate irreducible representation with a single state at each momentum.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.7 (Step 3 of the massless derivation: "a Lorentz transformation only multiplies a massless state by a phase, $D(W) = e^{-i\lambda\theta(\Lambda, p)}$") · Yu §3.3.2, eqs. (3.206)–(3.214) (passive sign) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.3*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the little group, factor by factor). By [[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]], 1, $U(S(\alpha, \beta)R_z(\theta)) = e^{i(\alpha A + \beta B)}e^{-i\theta J^3}$. First $e^{-i\theta J^3}|k, \lambda\rangle = e^{-i\lambda\theta}|k, \lambda\rangle$ (the exponential of an operator on its eigenvector). This is again a state at $k$ on which $A = B = 0$ (Law §C3.7.4), so $e^{i(\alpha A + \beta B)}$ acts as $e^0 = 1$ on it. Part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (insert into Wigner's law). [[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-4|Theorem §C3.6.4]], 2 with the one-dimensional $D(W) = e^{-i\lambda\theta}$ gives part 2. Each $\lambda$ alone is invariant, hence irreducible (one state per momentum; there is no smaller nonzero invariant subspace of labels).
>
> **Step 3** ($J^3$ commutes with boosts along $z$). $[J^3, K^3] = i\varepsilon_{33k}K^k = 0$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]), so $U(L_z(\eta)) = e^{-i\eta K^3}$ commutes with $J^3$ and $J^3U(L_z(\eta))|k, \lambda\rangle = \lambda\,U(L_z(\eta))|k, \lambda\rangle$: the boosted state, of momentum $|\mathbf p|(1, 0, 0, 1)$, still has $J^3 = \lambda$, and $J^3$ is its helicity since its momentum points along $z$.
>
> **Step 4** (rotate the axis). For a rotation $R$, $U(R)\,(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf J)\,U(R)^{-1} = (R\hat{\mathbf n})\cdot\mathbf J$: this is the $J$-law of [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]] restricted to rotations, i.e. $\mathbf J$ is a vector operator ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-5|QM Theorem §C5.1.5]]; used the same way in [[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^der-c5-3-4|QM Derivation §C5.3.4]]). With $R = R(\hat{\mathbf p})$, $\hat{\mathbf n} = \hat{\mathbf z}$: $(\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf J)\,U(R)\,U(L_z)|k, \lambda\rangle = U(R)\,J^3\,U(L_z)|k, \lambda\rangle = \lambda\,|p, \lambda\rangle$ by Step 3.
>
> **Step 5** ($h$ on a momentum eigenstate). $\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf P = \sum_iJ^iP^i$ and $[J^i, P^i] = i\varepsilon_{iik}P^k = 0$ for each $i$, so the order is immaterial; on $|p, \lambda\rangle$ the factor $\mathbf P/|\mathbf P|$ is the number $\hat{\mathbf p}$, and $h|p, \lambda\rangle = (\hat{\mathbf p}\cdot\mathbf J)|p, \lambda\rangle = \lambda|p, \lambda\rangle$: part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The little-group "translations" act trivially and the rotation about $\hat{\mathbf k}$ by a phase, so nothing can mix different helicities: they are different particles as far as the Poincaré group is concerned.
> - Contrast with a massive particle, whose helicity flips under a boost that overtakes it ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^rem-c3-6-4|§C3.6★, Remark: Helicity of a massive particle]]); a massless particle cannot be overtaken.
> - Used next: the quantization of $\lambda$ (Theorem §C3.7.6) and the covariant form $W^\mu = \lambda P^\mu$ (Theorem §C3.7.7).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.7.6: Helicity Is Quantized
> The helicity of a massless particle is an integer or a half-integer,
>
> $$
> \lambda \in \{0, \pm\tfrac12, \pm1, \pm\tfrac32, \dots\} ,
> $$
>
> integer when $U$ is a true representation of $SO^+(1,3)$ and half-odd when it is a representation of the cover only (a rotation by $2\pi$ about $\hat{\mathbf k}$ acting as $e^{-2\pi i\lambda} = -1$).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.7 (Step 4 of the massless derivation: "Nothing in the algebra of ISO(2) quantizes λ. The topology does") · Yu §3.3.2, eqs. (3.215)–(3.217) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.3 ("Quantization of helicity")*

^thm-c3-7-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the algebra does not quantize λ). The rotation part of $G_k$ is the one-parameter group $\theta \mapsto R_z(\theta)$; on $|k, \lambda\rangle$ it is represented by $e^{-i\lambda\theta}$ (Theorem §C3.7.5, 1), which is a representation of the additive group $\mathbb R$ for *every* real $\lambda$. Unlike the massive case, no Casimir of a compact algebra bounds or discretizes $\lambda$ ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^thm-c3-1-6|Theorem §C3.1.6]] does not apply to the abelian rotation subgroup alone).
>
> **Step 2** (the $2\pi$ rotation is the identity of the group). $R_z(2\pi) = \mathbb 1$ in $SO^+(1,3)$. If $U$ represents $SO^+(1,3)$ itself, $U(R_z(2\pi)) = U(\mathbb 1) = \mathbb 1$, so $e^{-2\pi i\lambda} = 1$ and $\lambda \in \mathbb Z$.
>
> **Step 3** (the topology allows a sign, and only a sign). In general $U$ represents the covering group ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-5-1|§C3.5, Remark: Why unitary, and why the covering group]]). Every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$ is a boost times a rotation ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-6|REL Theorem §B1.2.6]]) and the boosts can be shrunk continuously to $\mathbb 1$ (rapidity $\to 0$), so loops in $SO^+(1,3)$ deform into loops in $SO(3)$ and $\pi_1(SO^+(1,3)) = \pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb Z_2$ ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^thm-c3-1-8|Theorem §C3.1.8]]; [[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^rem-c3-1-8|§C3.1, Remark: The same pattern for the Lorentz group]]). The loop $\theta \mapsto R_z(\theta)$, $\theta \in [0, 4\pi]$, is twice the generator and is contractible, so it lifts to a closed loop in the simply connected cover: the element of the cover reached at $\theta = 4\pi$ is the identity. Its representative is $e^{-4\pi iJ^3}$, the one-parameter group of the generator $J^3$ at $\theta = 4\pi$. Hence $e^{-4\pi i\lambda} = 1$ and $2\lambda \in \mathbb Z$.
>
> **Step 4** (the sign of a 2π rotation). Then $e^{-2\pi i\lambda} = (-1)^{2\lambda}$: $+1$ for integer, $-1$ for half-odd $\lambda$, the same dichotomy as for massive spin ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-4|QM Theorem §C5.3.4]], [[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^thm-c3-1-7|Theorem §C3.1.7]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The quantization of helicity is topological, not algebraic: it comes from the double connectedness of the Lorentz group, the same fact that allows spin $\frac12$.
> - The spin of a massless particle is $s = |\lambda|$; the values are those of massive spins, but $\lambda$ and $-\lambda$ are a priori different particles.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.7.7: The Pauli–Lubanski Vector of a Massless Particle
> On states of momentum $k = \kappa(1, 0, 0, 1)$ the Pauli–Lubanski vector ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^def-c3-6-3|Def. §C3.6.3]]) acts as
>
> $$
> W^\mu = \kappa\,\bigl(J^3,\ -B,\ A,\ J^3\bigr), \qquad W^2 = -\kappa^2\,(A^2 + B^2) .
> $$
>
> So $W^2 = -\kappa^2\rho^2 < 0$ on a continuous-spin representation and $W^2 = 0$ on a helicity representation, where moreover
>
> $$
> W^\mu|p, \lambda\rangle = \lambda\,p^\mu|p, \lambda\rangle \quad\text{for every } p :
> $$
>
> the helicity is the constant of proportionality between the two commuting four-vectors $W$ and $P$, a frame-independent definition.
>
> *Source: derived here from Theorems §C3.6.10 and §C3.7.5 (the user's pre-course notes, §2.3, state the Pauli–Lubanski Casimir without the massless evaluation)*

^thm-c3-7-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (evaluate at $k$). By [[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-10|Theorem §C3.6.10]], 1, put $P_\sigma$ to the right; on a state at $k$ it becomes $k_\sigma = \kappa(1, 0, 0, -1)$ (lower index). So $W^\mu = -\frac\kappa2\bigl(\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho0} - \varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho3}\bigr)J_{\nu\rho}$ there.
>
> **Step 2** (the four components). With $\varepsilon^{0123} = +1$, $J_{ij} = J^{ij}$, $J_{0i} = -J^{0i} = -K^i$, $J^{12} = J^3$, $J^{23} = J^1$, $J^{31} = J^2$:
> - $\mu = 0$: only the $\sigma = 3$ term survives, $\varepsilon^{0ij3}J_{ij} = \varepsilon^{0123}J_{12} + \varepsilon^{0213}J_{21} = 2J^3$, so $W^0 = -\frac\kappa2\cdot(-2J^3) = \kappa J^3$.
> - $\mu = 3$: only the $\sigma = 0$ term survives, $\varepsilon^{3ij0}J_{ij}$ with $\varepsilon^{3120} = -\varepsilon^{0123}$ (one transposition of the first and last places) $= -1$, so $\varepsilon^{3ij0}J_{ij} = -2J^3$ and $W^3 = -\frac\kappa2(-2J^3) = \kappa J^3$.
> - $\mu = 1$: the $\sigma = 0$ term has $\nu\rho \in \{2, 3\}$, $\varepsilon^{1230} = -1$ (a cyclic shift of four places, three transpositions), giving $-2J_{23} = -2J^1$; the $\sigma = 3$ term has $\nu\rho \in \{0, 2\}$, $\varepsilon^{1023} = -1$, giving $-2J_{02} = 2K^2$, which enters with the minus sign of Step 1 as $-2K^2$. So $W^1 = -\frac\kappa2(-2J^1 - 2K^2) = \kappa(J^1 + K^2) = -\kappa B$.
> - $\mu = 2$: the $\sigma = 0$ term has $\varepsilon^{2310} = -1$ times $2J_{31} = 2J^2$, i.e. $-2J^2$; the $\sigma = 3$ term has $\varepsilon^{2013} = +1$ times $2J_{01} = -2K^1$, entering with the minus sign of Step 1 as $+2K^1$. So $W^2 = -\frac\kappa2(-2J^2 + 2K^1) = \kappa(J^2 - K^1) = \kappa A$.
>
> (Checked symbolically.)
>
> **Step 3** ($W^2$). $J^3$, $A$, $B$ map states at $k$ to states at $k$ (they generate $G_k$), so the second factor of $W_\mu W^\mu$ is evaluated by the same rule: $W_\mu W^\mu = \kappa^2\bigl((J^3)^2 - B^2 - A^2 - (J^3)^2\bigr) = -\kappa^2(A^2 + B^2)$; the $(J^3)^2$ terms cancel exactly because $W^0 = W^3$. On a joint eigenstate this is $-\kappa^2(a^2 + b^2) = -\kappa^2\rho^2$, constant on the circle of [[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]]; under Law §C3.7.4 it is $0$.
>
> **Step 4** ($W = \lambda P$ at $k$). With $A = B = 0$ and $J^3 = \lambda$: $W^\mu|k, \lambda\rangle = \kappa(\lambda, 0, 0, \lambda)|k, \lambda\rangle = \lambda k^\mu|k, \lambda\rangle$.
>
> **Step 5** (transport). By Theorem §C3.6.10, 3, $U(V)^{-1}W^\mu U(V) = V^\mu{}_\nu W^\nu$, so
>
> $$
> W^\mu|p, \lambda\rangle = U(V(p))\,V(p)^\mu{}_\nu W^\nu|k, \lambda\rangle = \lambda\,V(p)^\mu{}_\nu k^\nu\;U(V(p))|k, \lambda\rangle = \lambda\,p^\mu|p, \lambda\rangle .
> $$
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - On a massless helicity state $P^2 = 0$, $W\cdot P = 0$ and $W^2 = 0$: two orthogonal null vectors are parallel, so $W \propto P$ was forced; $\lambda$ is the proportionality constant.
> - The three Casimir classes of physical representations: $P^2 = m^2 > 0$ with $W^2 = -m^2s(s + 1)$ ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-11|Theorem §C3.6.11]]); $P^2 = 0$, $W^2 = 0$, labelled by $\lambda$; $P^2 = 0$, $W^2 = -\kappa^2\rho^2$, continuous spin (excluded).

> [!remark] Remark: Parity pairs λ with −λ: photon, graviton, neutrino
> A parity transformation reverses $\mathbf p$ but not the axial vector $\mathbf J$, so it reverses helicity and maps the representation $\lambda$ to $-\lambda$ (parity: QFT C9, planned). A theory with parity symmetry must contain both, and the two one-dimensional representations become the two polarizations of one particle: a massless particle of spin $s \ge \frac12$ has **two** states, $\lambda = \pm s$, not $2s + 1$. The photon has $\lambda = \pm1$, the left and right circular polarizations of light; a graviton would have $\pm2$. A theory without parity need not pair them: in the massless idealization the neutrinos of the weak interaction have $\lambda = -\frac12$ and the antineutrinos $+\frac12$, which a single two-component Weyl field describes ([[§C5a.4 Bilinears, Chirality and the Weyl Equations#^mod-c5a-4-7|Model §C5a.4.7]], [[§C5a.4 Bilinears, Chirality and the Weyl Equations#^thm-c5a-4-8|Theorem §C5a.4.8]], [[§C5a.7 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^thm-c5a-7-14|Theorem §C5a.7.14]]). A massless scalar, $\lambda = 0$, is its own partner.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.7 ("Consequence") · Yu §3.3.2, after eq. (3.218) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.3 ("Photon, graviton, neutrino")*

## Polarization vectors and gauge invariance

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.7.8: Little-Group Translations Shift a Polarization Vector by the Momentum
> Let $\varepsilon^\mu(k, \pm) = \frac1{\sqrt2}(0, 1, \pm i, 0)$, and transport them, $\varepsilon(p, \pm) \equiv V(p)\,\varepsilon(k, \pm)$ with $V(p)$ of [[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^def-c3-7-1|Def. §C3.7.1]]. Then $k\cdot\varepsilon(k, \pm) = 0$, $\varepsilon(k, \pm)\cdot\varepsilon(k, \pm)^* = -1$, and
> 1. $R_z(\theta)\,\varepsilon(k, \pm) = e^{\mp i\theta}\,\varepsilon(k, \pm)$: these are the helicity $\pm1$ vectors of the vector representation;
> 2. $S(\alpha, \beta)\,\varepsilon(k, \pm) = \varepsilon(k, \pm) + \dfrac{\alpha \pm i\beta}{\sqrt2\,\kappa}\,k$;
> 3. for every $\Lambda$ and $p$, with $W(\Lambda, p) = S(\alpha, \beta)R_z(\theta)$,
>
> $$
> \Lambda\,\varepsilon(p, \pm) = e^{\mp i\theta}\Bigl[\varepsilon(\Lambda p, \pm) + \frac{\alpha \pm i\beta}{\sqrt2\,\kappa}\,\Lambda p\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> So the polarization vectors of a massless helicity-$\pm1$ particle transform as four-vectors only up to a multiple of the momentum.
>
> *Source: computed here with Yu's matrix $S(\alpha, \beta)$, eq. (3.182) (checked numerically for a random Λ and p); the reading as the origin of gauge invariance is stated in the user's pre-course notes, §2.3 ("the origin of gauge invariance for the photon"), and developed in their §4 (massless vector field)*

^thm-c3-7-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (transversality and norm). $k\cdot\varepsilon = \kappa(1\cdot0 - 0\cdot1 - 0\cdot(\pm i) - 1\cdot0) = 0$. $\varepsilon\cdot\varepsilon^{\ast} = \frac12(0 - 1 - (\pm i)(\mp i) - 0) = \frac12(-1 - 1) = -1$.
>
> **Step 2** (rotation, part 1). The active rotation by $\theta$ about $z$ sends $\hat{\mathbf x} \mapsto \cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf x} + \sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf y}$ and $\hat{\mathbf y} \mapsto -\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf x} + \cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf y}$. So $\hat{\mathbf x} \pm i\hat{\mathbf y} \mapsto (\cos\theta \mp i\sin\theta)\hat{\mathbf x} + (\sin\theta \pm i\cos\theta)\hat{\mathbf y} = e^{\mp i\theta}(\hat{\mathbf x} \pm i\hat{\mathbf y})$. Comparing with $e^{-i\theta\mathcal J^3}$ ([[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]], 1): $\mathcal J^3\varepsilon(k, \pm) = \pm\varepsilon(k, \pm)$, helicity $\pm1$ along $\hat{\mathbf k} = \hat{\mathbf z}$.
>
> **Step 3** (translation, part 2). $S(\alpha, \beta)\varepsilon$ uses only the columns 1 and 2 of $S$ ([[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-7-1|Theorem §C3.7.1]]), $(\alpha, 1, 0, \alpha)$ and $(\beta, 0, 1, \beta)$:
>
> $$
> S(\alpha, \beta)\,\varepsilon(k, \pm) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(\alpha \pm i\beta,\ 1,\ \pm i,\ \alpha \pm i\beta\bigr) = \varepsilon(k, \pm) + \tfrac{\alpha \pm i\beta}{\sqrt2}\,(1, 0, 0, 1) ,
> $$
>
> and $(1, 0, 0, 1) = k/\kappa$.
>
> **Step 4** (any Λ, part 3). By the definition of the Wigner element, $\Lambda V(p) = V(\Lambda p)\,W(\Lambda, p)$, so
>
> $$
> \Lambda\,\varepsilon(p, \pm) = \Lambda V(p)\,\varepsilon(k, \pm) = V(\Lambda p)\,S(\alpha, \beta)\,R_z(\theta)\,\varepsilon(k, \pm) = e^{\mp i\theta}\,V(\Lambda p)\Bigl[\varepsilon(k, \pm) + \frac{\alpha \pm i\beta}{\sqrt2\,\kappa}\,k\Bigr]
> $$
>
> by Steps 2–3, and $V(\Lambda p)\varepsilon(k, \pm) = \varepsilon(\Lambda p, \pm)$, $V(\Lambda p)k = \Lambda p$.
>
> ⚑ By-product: the extra term is proportional to $p^\mu$. A vector field built from these modes, $A^\mu(x) = \sum_\pm\int d\mu(p)\,\bigl(\varepsilon^\mu(p, \pm)\,\alpha_{\mathbf p, \pm}e^{-ip\cdot x} + \text{h.c.}\bigr)$, therefore transforms under $U(\Lambda)$ as a four-vector *plus* a term $\partial^\mu\Omega(x)$: a gauge transformation → [[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^rem-c3-7-3|Remark: Two polarizations, and why a massless vector field needs gauge invariance]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The helicity states are covariant (Theorem §C3.7.5); their polarization *vectors* are not, because the little-group translations act trivially on the states but not on the four-vector index.
> - For a massive vector the little group $SO(3)$ acts on $\varepsilon(k, \sigma)$, $k = (m, \mathbf 0)$, by honest rotations and no inhomogeneous term arises.

> [!remark] Remark: Two polarizations, and why a massless vector field needs gauge invariance
> A four-vector field has four components, carrying $(\frac12, \frac12)$, whose rotation content is spin 0 plus spin 1 ([[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]). A massive spin-1 particle uses three of them, and the constraint $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$ removes the fourth. A massless helicity-$\pm1$ particle has only two states, and Theorem §C3.7.8 shows that no four-vector field can be built from them covariantly: the field must be allowed to change by $\partial_\mu\Omega$ under a Lorentz transformation. Either one uses a field that is not a four-vector, such as $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$, which is blind to the extra term and carries $(1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)$ ([[§C3.4 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-4-7|Theorem §C3.4.7]]), or one couples $A_\mu$ only in gauge-invariant ways. This is why the quantum theory of the photon is a gauge theory, and why the longitudinal polarization of a massive vector, $\varepsilon(p, 0) \sim p^\mu/m$, misbehaves as $m \to 0$ ([[§C4.3★ Massive Polarization Vectors and Plane Waves#^thm-c4-3-10|Theorem §C4.3.10]], [[§C4.6 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^thm-c4-6-1|Theorem §C4.6.1]]; the two transverse polarizations, [[§C4.7 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^thm-c4-7-1|Theorem §C4.7.1]]).
>

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The little group $ISO(2)$ has the same algebra as $(J^3, P^1, P^2)$, the rotations and translations of the transverse plane inside the Poincaré algebra ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-5|Theorem §C3.5.5]]); its unitary representations mirror those of a free particle in two dimensions: momentum magnitude $\rho > 0$ gives a circle of states (continuous spin), $\rho = 0$ gives only the angular-momentum label (helicity).
> - The orbit of $k$ is the forward null cone $C^+$ of [[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-4|Theorem §C1a.4.4]], the homogeneous space $SO^+(1,3)/ISO(2)$; the invariant measure on it is the $m \to 0$ limit $d^3p/(2\pi)^3\,2|\mathbf p|$ of [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-2|Def. §C2a.4.2]] ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-7|Theorem §CA.2.7]] for the cone).
> - The quantization of helicity uses the same topological fact as half-integer spin, $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb Z_2$ ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^thm-c3-1-8|Theorem §C3.1.8]], [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^rem-c5-2-6|QM §C5.2, ★ Remark: Why the sign cannot be removed]]), now with no Casimir to help.
> - $W^\mu = \lambda P^\mu$ (Theorem §C3.7.7) and $-W^2 = m^2s(s + 1)$ ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-11|Theorem §C3.6.11]]) are the two Casimir characterizations of the physical particles; the three-way split by $(P^2, W^2)$ is the content of the orbit table in [[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^rem-c3-6-2|§C3.6★, Remark: The orbits at a glance]].
> - The polarization vectors $\varepsilon(k, \pm)$ are the spherical basis vectors $\mp(\hat{\mathbf x} \pm i\hat{\mathbf y})/\sqrt2$ of the spin-1 representation of Quantum Mechanics up to the Condon–Shortley sign ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-3|QM Theorem §C5.3.3]]); for light they are the circular polarizations of electromagnetic waves.
> - Helicity enters the course as a computational tool: helicity spinors of the Dirac field (PHY 513, Problem Set 5, Problem 6; [[§C5a.7 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^thm-c5a-7-11|Theorem §C5a.7.11]], [[§C5a.7 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^thm-c5a-7-12|Theorem §C5a.7.12]], [[§C5a.7 Normalization, Spin Sums and Helicity#^thm-c5a-7-14|Theorem §C5a.7.14]]) and the helicity structure of QED amplitudes (Lecture 20; QFT C8, planned); for massless fermions helicity equals chirality, a statement about the Weyl representations $(\frac12, 0)$ and $(0, \frac12)$ of [[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]].
> - Weinberg's form of the generators, $A = J_2 + K_1$, $B = -J_1 + K_2$, uses $K_i = J^{i0}$, the opposite boost convention ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^cau-c3-5-1|§C3.5, Caution: Signs and names across the sources]]); with the Noether boost charge of [[§C1b.7 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor#^def-c1b-7-1|Def. §C1b.7.1]] it is the same pair of operators.
> - Math: $ISO(2)$ is the semidirect product $\mathbb R^2 \rtimes SO(2)$, the translations forming a normal subgroup ([[§38 Normal Subgroups|493 §38]]); $G_k$ is the stabilizer of a point on the null orbit ([[§26 Stabilizers and Fixed Points#^def-26-1|493 Def. §26.1]]); the exponential of a nilpotent matrix ($N^3 = 0$) terminates, which is why $S(\alpha, \beta)$ is polynomial in its parameters.
