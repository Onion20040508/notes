How does a Lorentz transformation or a translation act on the *states* of a quantum field theory, and how is that action tied to the field operators and to the conserved charges? Chapters C1a–C1b let the Poincaré group act on coordinates ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group|§C1a.4]]), on classical field configurations ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^thm-c1b-1-1|Theorem §C1b.1.1]]) and, through Noether's theorem, produced ten conserved charges ([[§C1b.7 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum|§C1b.7]]–[[§C1b.8 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor|§C1b.8]]); [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra|§C3.2]]–[[§C3.4 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group|§C3.4]] studied the finite-dimensional representations carried by field *indices*. This section puts the group on the Hilbert space, as unitary operators $U(\Lambda, a)$, in the way Quantum Mechanics put translations, rotations and time evolution there ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-4|QM Principle §C2.2.4]], [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|QM Principle §C5.1.2]], [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^pr-c3-1-1|QM Principle §C3.1.1]]). New here: the ten generators as one tensor-and-vector family, the Poincaré algebra, the representation on states being *infinite-dimensional and unitary* against the finite-dimensional non-unitary one on indices, the Heisenberg equation as translation invariance ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields|§C2b.1]]), an explicit unitary representation for the free scalar field ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization|§C2a.4]]), and the identification of the Noether charges with the generators.

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): $\hbar = c = 1$, $g = \operatorname{diag}(+, -, -, -)$; Lorentz transformations are read actively and are proper orthochronous unless stated, $\Lambda = \exp(-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ with the matrices $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu} = iM^{\mu\nu}$ of [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-1|Def. §C1a.6.1]]. Three different objects carry the letters $J^{\mu\nu}$ in the sources: here $\hat J^{\mu\nu}$ is an operator on the Hilbert space, $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$ a $4\times4$ matrix (and $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ its image in a representation $D$, [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]), and $L^{\mu\nu} = i(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu)$ a differential operator on the argument ([[§C3.4 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]); only the Hilbert-space operator carries a hat; Peskin & Schroeder write $J^{\mu\nu}$ for all three. A Poincaré transformation is the pair $(\Lambda, a)$ acting by $x \mapsto \Lambda x + a$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-4|REL Def. §B1.2.4]]).

## The Poincaré group on states

> [!principle] Principle §C3.5.1: Poincaré Invariance on the Hilbert Space
> A relativistic quantum theory has, for every proper orthochronous Poincaré transformation $(\Lambda, a)$, a unitary operator $U(\Lambda, a)$ on its Hilbert space, $|\Psi\rangle \mapsto U(\Lambda, a)|\Psi\rangle$, such that
> 1. $U(\mathbb 1, 0) = \mathbb 1$, and $U(\Lambda, a)|\Psi\rangle$ depends continuously on $(\Lambda, a)$;
> 2. the operators compose as the transformations do:
>
> $$
> U(\Lambda_2, a_2)\,U(\Lambda_1, a_1) = U(\Lambda_2\Lambda_1,\ \Lambda_2a_1 + a_2) ;
> $$
>
> 3. the vacuum is invariant, $U(\Lambda, a)|0\rangle = |0\rangle$.
>
> $U(\Lambda) \equiv U(\Lambda, 0)$ is a quantum Lorentz transformation and $U(\mathbb 1, a)$ a quantum translation.
>
> *Domain: every relativistic theory of the course. For fields of half-integer spin, item 2 holds for the covering group (each $\Lambda$ replaced by one of its two preimages in $SL(2, \mathbb C)$) and on $SO^+(1,3)$ only up to a sign ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-5-1|Remark: Why unitary, and why the covering group]]). Parity and time reversal are not included; parity on the scalar field: [[§C9.2★ Parity of the Scalar Field#^def-c9-2-1|Def. §C9.2.1]], [[§C9.2★ Parity of the Scalar Field#^thm-c9-2-2|Theorem §C9.2.2]] (★); parity on the Dirac field: [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^def-c9-3-1|Def. §C9.3.1]], [[§C9.3 Parity on States, Spinors and the Dirac Field#^thm-c9-3-5|Theorem §C9.3.5]]; time reversal: QFT C9, later sections.*
>

> [!remark] Remark: Why unitary, and why the covering group
> A symmetry must preserve every transition probability $|\langle\Phi|\Psi\rangle|^2$, so by Wigner's theorem it acts by a unitary or an antiunitary operator, determined up to a phase ([[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-1|QM Theorem §C8.1.1]]). Every element of the proper orthochronous group lies on a continuous path from the identity ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-3|Theorem §C1a.4.3]], 2), so it is a product of elements near the identity, each the square of another element; the square of an antiunitary operator is unitary, so, by the argument of item 2 of Wigner's theorem, it is represented unitarily; antiunitary operators are left for time reversal. Because states are rays, item 2 of Principle §C3.5.1 need only hold up to a phase, a projective representation ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^def-c3-1-14|Def. §C3.1.14]]). For the rotation group the phase that survives every redefinition is a sign, removed by passing to $SU(2)$ ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^thm-c3-1-9|Theorem §C3.1.9]]); the same holds for $SO^+(1,3)$ with its double cover $SL(2, \mathbb C)$ ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^rem-c3-1-8|§C3.1, Remark: The same pattern for the Lorentz group]]), and for the Poincaré group with the cover of its Lorentz part (Bargmann's theorem, stated: [[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^rem-c3-1-9|§C3.1, ★ Remark: Infinite dimensions, Bargmann, and a phase topology does not explain]]). Working with the cover from the start, as Yu and the user's notes do, is why item 2 can be written without a phase.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6 (the parenthesis in the Definition) · Yu §3.3.1, eqs. (3.110)–(3.117), and the closing paragraph of §3.3.1 · the user's pre-course notes, §2.1 (Wigner's theorem, stated)*

## Generators

> [!definition] Definition §C3.5.1: Generators of Quantum Poincaré Transformations
> The ten **generators** $\hat J^{\mu\nu} = -\hat J^{\nu\mu}$ and $\hat P^\mu$ are the operators in the expansion near the identity,
>
> $$
> U(\mathbb 1 + \omega, \varepsilon) = \mathbb 1 - \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\hat J^{\mu\nu} + i\,\varepsilon_\mu \hat P^\mu + O(\omega^2, \varepsilon^2, \omega\varepsilon), \qquad \hat J^{\mu\nu} = i\,\frac{\partial U}{\partial\omega_{\mu\nu}}\Big|_0, \quad \hat P^\mu = -i\,\frac{\partial U}{\partial\varepsilon_\mu}\Big|_0 ,
> $$
>
> with $\omega_{\mu\nu} = -\omega_{\nu\mu}$ ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-6-1|Theorem §C1a.6.1]]). Their names: $\hat H \equiv \hat P^0$, momentum $\hat{\mathbf P} = (\hat P^1, \hat P^2, \hat P^3)$, angular momentum $\hat J^i \equiv \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat J^{jk}$, boost generators $\hat K^i \equiv \hat J^{0i}$, exactly as for the matrices of [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-2|Def. §C1a.6.2]].
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6, eq. (poincaregen) and Derivation "Expanding U(Λ, a) near the identity" · Yu §3.1, eqs. (3.10)–(3.12); §3.2, eqs. (3.37)–(3.38) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.1*

> [!caution] Caution: Signs and names across the sources
> - **The signs** $-\frac i2\omega \hat J$ and $+i\varepsilon \hat P$ are those of the lecture, PS and Yu (3.10). Weinberg (2.4.3) writes $\mathbb 1 + \frac i2\omega_{\rho\sigma}\hat J^{\rho\sigma} - i\varepsilon_\rho \hat P^\rho$; formulas from the two families cannot be mixed. The $\frac12$ removes the double count of each independent pair ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^rem-c1a-6-1|§C1a.6, Remark: The factor ½ and why ω may be taken antisymmetric]]).
> - **Angles.** Yu reads transformations passively and names the angle $\theta_i = -\frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\omega_{jk}$, rapidity $\xi_i = -\omega_{0i}$, so his $U[R_z(\theta)] = e^{+i\theta \hat J^3}$ (3.46); here, actively, $U[R_z(\theta)] = e^{-i\theta \hat J^3}$. Every formula written with $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ is the same in both ([[Larsen PHY 513]]).
> - **The boost.** The *generator* $\hat K^i = \hat J^{0i}$ is minus the Noether *boost charge* $\hat K^i_{\rm N} \equiv \hat J^{i0}$ of [[§C1b.8 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor#^def-c1b-8-1|Def. §C1b.8.1]]; [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-13|Theorem §C3.5.13]] proves the dictionary.
> - **Which side the inverse is on.** This note writes $U^{-1}\hat\Phi(x)U = \hat\Phi(\Lambda^{-1}x)$; PS and the user's pre-course notes also write $U\hat\Phi(x)U^{-1} = \hat\Phi(\Lambda x)$. They are one law, with $\Lambda \to \Lambda^{-1}$.
>

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.2: The Generators Are Hermitian
> All ten generators are Hermitian: $(\hat J^{\mu\nu})^\dagger = \hat J^{\mu\nu}$ and $(\hat P^\mu)^\dagger = \hat P^\mu$, the boost generators $\hat K^i$ included. Hence $U(\mathbb 1 + \omega, \varepsilon)^{-1} = \mathbb 1 + \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\hat J^{\mu\nu} - i\varepsilon_\mu \hat P^\mu$ to first order.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6 (Derivation "Unitarity makes every generator Hermitian") · Yu §3.1, eqs. (3.13)–(3.15)*

^thm-c3-5-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the adjoint of the expansion). The parameters $\omega_{\mu\nu}$, $\varepsilon_\mu$ are real, so taking the adjoint of [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]] term by term,
>
> $$
> U^\dagger = \mathbb 1 + \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}(\hat J^{\mu\nu})^\dagger - i\,\varepsilon_\mu(\hat P^\mu)^\dagger + O(2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (multiply). Expand $U^\dagger U$ into its nine products; the four products of two first-order terms are second order and are dropped:
>
> $$
> U^\dagger U = \mathbb 1 + \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl[(\hat J^{\mu\nu})^\dagger - \hat J^{\mu\nu}\bigr] - i\,\varepsilon_\mu\bigl[(\hat P^\mu)^\dagger - \hat P^\mu\bigr] + O(2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (unitarity). [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]] gives $U^\dagger U = \mathbb 1$ for every $(\omega, \varepsilon)$, so each first-order coefficient vanishes. The ten parameters $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ ($\mu < \nu$) and $\varepsilon_\mu$ are independent, and $(\hat J^{\mu\nu})^\dagger - \hat J^{\mu\nu}$ is antisymmetric in $\mu\nu$, as is $\omega_{\mu\nu}$, so the coefficient of each independent $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ is $i[(\hat J^{\mu\nu})^\dagger - \hat J^{\mu\nu}]$ and must vanish. Hence $(\hat J^{\mu\nu})^\dagger = \hat J^{\mu\nu}$, $(\hat P^\mu)^\dagger = \hat P^\mu$.
>
> **Step 4** (the inverse). $U^{-1} = U^\dagger$; insert Step 3 into Step 1.
>
> ⚑ By-product: the boost generators $\hat K^i = \hat J^{0i}$ are Hermitian on the Hilbert space, while in every finite-dimensional representation their matrices are not ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^rem-c1a-6-2|§C1a.6, Remark: Hermitian generators do not make boosts unitary]]) → [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-5-2|Remark: Two kinds of representation]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Only unitarity was used; the factor $i$ in the definition is what turns "unitary" into "Hermitian", so the generators are candidate observables: energy, momentum, angular momentum, boost.
> - *Sense:* $\hat P^\mu$ and $\hat J^{\mu\nu}$ are unbounded (the spectrum of $\hat H$ is unbounded above), so "to first order" means on vectors where the derivatives of $U$ exist, a dense set (Stone's theorem, as for time evolution, [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^rem-c3-1-1|QM §C3.1, Remark: Layer change from level B]]); on wave packets of the free field everything is explicit (Theorem §C3.5.11).
> - Used next: the inverse expansion in every commutator below.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.3: Finite Transformations as Exponentials
> 1. $U(\Lambda, a)^{-1} = U(\Lambda^{-1}, -\Lambda^{-1}a)$ and $U(\Lambda)^{-1} = U(\Lambda^{-1})$.
> 2. $U(\Lambda, a) = U(\mathbb 1, a)\,U(\Lambda)$.
> 3. Translations: $U(\mathbb 1, a) = e^{i\hat P\cdot a} = e^{i\hat Ha^0 - i\hat{\mathbf P}\cdot\mathbf a}$.
> 4. Lorentz transformations: for $\Lambda = \exp(-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$, $\ U(\Lambda) = \exp(-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\hat J^{\mu\nu})$; a rotation by $\theta$ about $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is $e^{-i\theta\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\hat{\mathbf J}}$, a boost of rapidity $\eta$ along $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is $e^{-i\eta\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\hat{\mathbf K}}$.
>
> The order in 2 matters, since $\hat P$ and $\hat J$ do not commute ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-5|Theorem §C3.5.5]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6 (Step 4 of Derivation "Expanding U(Λ, a)") · Yu §3.1, eqs. (3.8)–(3.9); §3.2, eqs. (3.45)–(3.46), (3.74)–(3.79)*

^thm-c3-5-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (inverse in the group). By the composition law of [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]] with $(\Lambda_2, a_2) = (\Lambda^{-1}, -\Lambda^{-1}a)$, $(\Lambda_1, a_1) = (\Lambda, a)$: $U(\Lambda^{-1}, -\Lambda^{-1}a)\,U(\Lambda, a) = U(\Lambda^{-1}\Lambda,\ \Lambda^{-1}a - \Lambda^{-1}a) = U(\mathbb 1, 0) = \mathbb 1$. The product in the other order gives $U(\Lambda\Lambda^{-1}, \Lambda(-\Lambda^{-1}a) + a) = \mathbb 1$ as well. This is part 1; $a = 0$ gives the second statement.
>
> **Step 2** (factorize). The composition law with $(\Lambda_2, a_2) = (\mathbb 1, a)$, $(\Lambda_1, a_1) = (\Lambda, 0)$ gives $U(\mathbb 1, a)U(\Lambda, 0) = U(\Lambda, \mathbb 1\cdot0 + a) = U(\Lambda, a)$: part 2. In the opposite order, $U(\Lambda, 0)U(\mathbb 1, a) = U(\Lambda, \Lambda a)$, a different element.
>
> **Step 3** (translations form a one-parameter group in each direction). For fixed $a$, $s \mapsto V(s) \equiv U(\mathbb 1, sa)$ satisfies $V(s)V(t) = U(\mathbb 1, (s + t)a) = V(s + t)$ and $V(0) = \mathbb 1$. Hence $V(s + ds) = V(ds)V(s)$, and with Def. §C3.5.1 at $\varepsilon = a\,ds$, $V(ds) = \mathbb 1 + i\,ds\,a_\mu \hat P^\mu$:
>
> $$
> \frac{dV}{ds} = i\,(a_\mu \hat P^\mu)\,V(s), \qquad V(0) = \mathbb 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad V(s) = e^{is\,a\cdot \hat P} ,
> $$
>
> the exponential defined by its spectral resolution (for a self-adjoint generator this is Stone's theorem; the power series converges on eigenvectors). At $s = 1$, $U(\mathbb 1, a) = e^{i\hat P\cdot a}$, with $\hat P\cdot a = \hat P^0a^0 - \hat{\mathbf P}\cdot\mathbf a$. The four $\hat P^\mu$ commute ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-5|Theorem §C3.5.5]]), so $e^{i\hat P\cdot a} = e^{i\hat Ha^0}e^{-i\hat{\mathbf P}\cdot\mathbf a}$ splits without correction terms.
>
> **Step 4** (Lorentz transformations). For fixed antisymmetric $\omega$, $s \mapsto \Lambda(s) = \exp(-\frac{is}2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}) = e^{s\omega}$ is a one-parameter subgroup of $SO^+(1,3)$ ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-6-4|Theorem §C1a.6.4]]), so $W(s) \equiv U(\Lambda(s))$ satisfies $W(s)W(t) = W(s + t)$. As in Step 3, $W(ds) = \mathbb 1 - \frac i2\,ds\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\hat J^{\mu\nu}$ gives $W(s) = \exp(-\frac{is}2\omega_{\mu\nu}\hat J^{\mu\nu})$; set $s = 1$. With $\theta_i = \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\omega_{jk}$ and $\eta_i = \omega_{0i}$ the exponent is $-i\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\hat{\mathbf J} - i\boldsymbol\eta\cdot\hat{\mathbf K}$, by the same regrouping as in [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-2|Def. §C1a.6.2]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The finite operators are fixed by the generators and the group law; the only input beyond Def. §C3.5.1 is that each one-parameter subgroup is represented by a one-parameter group of unitaries.
> - $U(\Lambda, a)$ is "first Lorentz-transform, then translate"; reversing the order translates by $\Lambda a$.
> - Used next: $U(\mathbb 1, a) = e^{i\hat P\cdot a}$ moves fields ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-9|Theorem §C3.5.9]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Two kinds of representation
> Quantum field theory uses two representations of the Lorentz group at once, and they are of opposite kinds.
>
> | | on field indices ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra\|§C3.2]]–[[§C3.4 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group\|§C3.4]]) | on states (this section) |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | acts on | the components $\hat\Phi_a$ at one point, by $D(\Lambda)$ | the Hilbert space, by $U(\Lambda, a)$ |
> | dimension | finite: $(2j_+ + 1)(2j_- + 1)$ | infinite |
> | unitary? | no, unless trivial ([[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-10\|Theorem §C3.3.10]]) | yes ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-5-1\|Principle §C3.5.1]]) |
> | boost generators | not Hermitian | Hermitian ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-2\|Theorem §C3.5.2]]) |
> | labels | $(j_+, j_-)$ | mass and spin, or helicity ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group\|§C3.6★]], [[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity\|§C3.7★]]) |
> | for the scalar | the number $1$, i.e. $(0, 0)$ | $L^2$ of the mass hyperboloid ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-11\|Theorem §C3.5.11]]) |
>
> The two statements are one theorem read twice: the noncompact simple group $SO^+(1,3)$ has no nontrivial finite-dimensional unitary representation ([[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-10|Theorem §C3.3.10]]), so the representation on states, which must be unitary, must be infinite-dimensional; and the one on indices, which is finite-dimensional, cannot be unitary. Noncompactness alone is not the reason: the translations and the massless little group $ISO(2)$ are noncompact and have one-dimensional unitary representations, $e^{ip\cdot a}$ and the helicity phases of [[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-7-5|Theorem §C3.7.5]]; it is the boosts, whose commutator is a rotation, that forbid it. Both representations satisfy the same Lorentz algebra ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]), which is derived from the group law alone and so holds for $U$ as well ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^der-c3-2-1|Derivation §C3.2.1]]). They meet in [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-10|Theorem §C3.5.10]], where $\hat J^{\mu\nu}$ acts on the operator nature of a field, $L^{\mu\nu}$ on its argument and $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ on its index; the vocabulary for both uses is [[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^rem-c3-1-5|§C3.1, Remark: Two uses of one theory: field indices and quantum states]]. Quantum Mechanics never had to separate them: rotations are compact, and there one finite-dimensional unitary representation serves both purposes (spin matrices on a spinor's components *are* the action on its states).
>

## How the generators transform: the Poincaré algebra

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.4: How the Generators Transform
> For every $(\Lambda, a)$,
>
> $$
> U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\,\hat P^\rho\,U(\Lambda, a) = \Lambda^\rho{}_\mu \hat P^\mu, \qquad U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\,\hat J^{\rho\sigma}\,U(\Lambda, a) = \Lambda^\rho{}_\mu\Lambda^\sigma{}_\nu \hat J^{\mu\nu} - a^\sigma\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu \hat P^\mu + a^\rho\Lambda^\sigma{}_\mu \hat P^\mu .
> $$
>
> $\hat P^\mu$ is a four-vector operator; $\hat J^{\mu\nu}$ is a tensor under Lorentz transformations and shifts by $a^\rho \hat P'^\sigma - a^\sigma \hat P'^\rho$ (with $\hat P' = \Lambda \hat P$) under translations. Consequently $\langle\Psi'|\hat P^\rho|\Psi'\rangle = \Lambda^\rho{}_\mu\langle\Psi|\hat P^\mu|\Psi\rangle$ for $|\Psi'\rangle = U(\Lambda)|\Psi\rangle$, and likewise for $\hat J$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6, eq. (poincaretensor) · Yu §3.1, eqs. (3.16)–(3.29) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.1, eqs. (J-transform), (P-transform)*

^thm-c3-5-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (conjugate in the group). With the inverse of [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]], 1, and the composition law twice:
>
> $$
> (\mathbb 1 + \omega, \varepsilon)(\Lambda, a) = \bigl((\mathbb 1 + \omega)\Lambda,\ (\mathbb 1 + \omega)a + \varepsilon\bigr),
> $$
>
> $$
> (\Lambda^{-1}, -\Lambda^{-1}a)\bigl((\mathbb 1 + \omega)\Lambda,\ (\mathbb 1 + \omega)a + \varepsilon\bigr) = \bigl(\Lambda^{-1}(\mathbb 1 + \omega)\Lambda,\ \Lambda^{-1}[(\mathbb 1 + \omega)a + \varepsilon] - \Lambda^{-1}a\bigr) = \bigl(\mathbb 1 + \Lambda^{-1}\omega\Lambda,\ \Lambda^{-1}(\omega a + \varepsilon)\bigr),
> $$
>
> the $\Lambda^{-1}a$ terms cancelling. Applying $U$ and the composition law of [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]],
>
> $$
> U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\,U(\mathbb 1 + \omega, \varepsilon)\,U(\Lambda, a) = U\bigl(\mathbb 1 + \Lambda^{-1}\omega\Lambda,\ \Lambda^{-1}(\omega a + \varepsilon)\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the conjugated parameter is again antisymmetric). From $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}g\Lambda = g$, $(\Lambda^{-1})^\alpha{}_\beta = g^{\alpha\tau}\Lambda^\lambda{}_\tau g_{\lambda\beta}$. So, lowering the first index with $g_{\mu\alpha}$ and using $g_{\mu\alpha}g^{\alpha\tau} = \delta_\mu{}^\tau$, $g_{\lambda\beta}\omega^\beta{}_\gamma = \omega_{\lambda\gamma}$:
>
> $$
> (\Lambda^{-1}\omega\Lambda)_{\mu\nu} = g_{\mu\alpha}(\Lambda^{-1})^\alpha{}_\beta\,\omega^\beta{}_\gamma\,\Lambda^\gamma{}_\nu = \Lambda^\lambda{}_\mu\,\omega_{\lambda\gamma}\,\Lambda^\gamma{}_\nu ,
> $$
>
> antisymmetric in $\mu\nu$ because $\omega_{\lambda\gamma}$ is. So the right side of Step 1 is an infinitesimal Poincaré transformation and Def. §C3.5.1 applies to it.
>
> **Step 3** (expand the left side). With [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]] inside and the fixed $U(\Lambda, a)$ outside,
>
> $$
> U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\Bigl(\mathbb 1 - \frac i2\,\omega_{\rho\sigma}\hat J^{\rho\sigma} + i\,\varepsilon_\rho \hat P^\rho\Bigr)U(\Lambda, a) = \mathbb 1 - \frac i2\,\omega_{\rho\sigma}\,U^{-1}\hat J^{\rho\sigma}U + i\,\varepsilon_\rho\,U^{-1}\hat P^\rho U .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (expand the right side, Lorentz part). By Step 2, with the dummy pairs renamed $\lambda\gamma \to \rho\sigma$ and $\mu\nu$ kept,
>
> $$
> -\frac i2\,(\Lambda^{-1}\omega\Lambda)_{\mu\nu}\hat J^{\mu\nu} = -\frac i2\,\omega_{\rho\sigma}\,\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu\Lambda^\sigma{}_\nu \hat J^{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (expand the right side, translation part). For $v = \omega a + \varepsilon$, i.e. $v^\beta = \omega^\beta{}_\sigma a^\sigma + \varepsilon^\beta$, the same index move as in Step 2 gives $(\Lambda^{-1}v)_\mu = g_{\mu\alpha}(\Lambda^{-1})^\alpha{}_\beta v^\beta = \Lambda^\rho{}_\mu v_\rho$, so
>
> $$
> i\,(\Lambda^{-1}v)_\mu \hat P^\mu = i\,\varepsilon_\rho\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu \hat P^\mu + i\,\omega_{\rho\sigma}a^\sigma\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu \hat P^\mu .
> $$
>
> The last term has the parameter $\omega_{\rho\sigma}$ multiplying a coefficient that is not antisymmetric; only its antisymmetric part survives the contraction, so write $\omega_{\rho\sigma}X^{\rho\sigma} = \frac12\omega_{\rho\sigma}(X^{\rho\sigma} - X^{\sigma\rho})$:
>
> $$
> i\,\omega_{\rho\sigma}a^\sigma\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu \hat P^\mu = \frac i2\,\omega_{\rho\sigma}\bigl(a^\sigma\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu - a^\rho\Lambda^\sigma{}_\mu\bigr)\hat P^\mu .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (compare coefficients). Steps 3–5 are first-order expansions of the same operator in the ten independent parameters $\varepsilon_\rho$ and $\omega_{\rho\sigma}$ ($\rho < \sigma$), with coefficients of $\omega_{\rho\sigma}$ antisymmetric on both sides; so the coefficients agree. From $\varepsilon_\rho$: $i\,U^{-1}\hat P^\rho U = i\,\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu \hat P^\mu$. From $\omega_{\rho\sigma}$:
>
> $$
> -\frac i2\,U^{-1}\hat J^{\rho\sigma}U = -\frac i2\,\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu\Lambda^\sigma{}_\nu \hat J^{\mu\nu} + \frac i2\bigl(a^\sigma\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu - a^\rho\Lambda^\sigma{}_\mu\bigr)\hat P^\mu ,
> $$
>
> and multiplying by $2i$ gives the stated law for $\hat J$.
>
> **Step 7** (expectation values). For $|\Psi'\rangle = U(\Lambda)|\Psi\rangle$, $\langle\Psi'|\hat P^\rho|\Psi'\rangle = \langle\Psi|U^\dagger \hat P^\rho U|\Psi\rangle = \Lambda^\rho{}_\mu\langle\Psi|\hat P^\mu|\Psi\rangle$, using $U^\dagger = U^{-1}$.
>
> ⚑ By-product: with $a = 0$ the $\hat J$-law is the tensor law of [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]] for the representation $D = U$; the translation terms are new → [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-5-3|Remark: Angular momentum about a shifted origin]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The whole content is the group law: the indices of a generator are spacetime indices because conjugating an infinitesimal transformation by a finite one gives the transformed infinitesimal one.
> - The right side of the $\hat J$-law is antisymmetric in $\rho\sigma$, as it must be; that is why Step 5 antisymmetrized before comparing.
> - Used next: the Poincaré algebra ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-5|Theorem §C3.5.5]]), the Casimir $\hat P^2$ (Theorem §C3.5.6), and the momentum of a transformed state ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Angular momentum about a shifted origin
> With $\Lambda = \mathbb 1$, $U^{-1}\hat J^{ij}U = \hat J^{ij} - a^j\hat P^i + a^i\hat P^j$: in three-dimensional language $\hat{\mathbf J} \to \hat{\mathbf J} + \mathbf a\times\hat{\mathbf P}$ for $a^0 = 0$ (e.g. $\hat J^3 = \hat J^{12} \to \hat J^{12} + a^1\hat P^2 - a^2\hat P^1$): the angular momentum about the origin of the state translated by $\mathbf a$, $U|\Psi\rangle$, equals that of $|\Psi\rangle$ about the point $-\mathbf a$, as for a classical system ($\mathbf L = \mathbf r\times\mathbf p$ with $\mathbf r \to \mathbf r + \mathbf a$; checked numerically in the $5\times5$ representation of Derivation §C3.5.5). The boost generators shift by the time translation as well, $\hat K^i \to \hat K^i - a^i\hat H + a^0\hat P^i$, which is the operator form of $\hat K^i = t\hat P^i - \int d^3x\,x^i\hat T^{00}$ ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-13|Theorem §C3.5.13]]): the boost generator contains the time explicitly. Only $\hat P^\mu$, and the Casimirs built from it, ignore the origin.
>

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.5: The Poincaré Algebra
> Together with the Lorentz algebra $[\hat J^{\mu\nu}, \hat J^{\rho\sigma}] = i(g^{\nu\rho}\hat J^{\mu\sigma} - g^{\mu\rho}\hat J^{\nu\sigma} - g^{\nu\sigma}\hat J^{\mu\rho} + g^{\mu\sigma}\hat J^{\nu\rho})$ of [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]], the generators obey
>
> $$
> [\hat J^{\mu\nu}, \hat P^\rho] = i\bigl(g^{\nu\rho}\hat P^\mu - g^{\mu\rho}\hat P^\nu\bigr), \qquad [\hat P^\mu, \hat P^\nu] = 0 .
> $$
>
> In three-dimensional form, with $\hat H = \hat P^0$, $\hat J^i$ and $\hat K^i = \hat J^{0i}$:
>
> $$
> [\hat J^i, \hat P^j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat P^k, \quad [\hat J^i, \hat H] = 0, \quad [\hat P^i, \hat H] = 0, \quad [\hat P^i, \hat P^j] = 0, \quad [\hat K^i, \hat P^j] = -i\delta_{ij}\hat H, \quad [\hat K^i, \hat H] = -i\hat P^i .
> $$
>
> The ten generators span the **Poincaré algebra**, the Lie algebra of the Poincaré group; the Lorentz algebra is a subalgebra, and the $\hat P^\mu$ span an Abelian ideal.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6, eq. (poincarealgebra) and Step 3 ("in three-dimensional language") · Yu §3.2, eqs. (3.33)–(3.34), (3.66)–(3.73) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.2*

^thm-c3-5-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 0** (the Lorentz algebra on states). [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^der-c3-2-1|Derivation §C3.2.1]] uses only the multiplication law of a representation and the definition of its generators; [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]] and [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]] supply both for $U(\Lambda)$, so the $\hat J^{\mu\nu}$ on the Hilbert space obey Theorem §C3.2.1. It is also the infinitesimal form of the $\hat J$-law of [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]] at $a = 0$.
>
> **Step 1** ($[\hat J, \hat P]$: set up). Take the $\hat P$-law of Theorem §C3.5.4 with $a = 0$ and $\Lambda = \mathbb 1 + \omega$. By Def. §C3.5.1 and [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]], $U = \mathbb 1 - \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\hat J^{\mu\nu}$, $U^{-1} = \mathbb 1 + \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\hat J^{\mu\nu}$, so the left side is
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\mathbb 1 + \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\hat J^{\mu\nu}\Bigr)\hat P^\rho\Bigl(\mathbb 1 - \frac i2\omega_{\alpha\beta}\hat J^{\alpha\beta}\Bigr) = \hat P^\rho + \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\hat J^{\mu\nu}\hat P^\rho - \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\hat P^\rho \hat J^{\mu\nu} + O(\omega^2) = \hat P^\rho + \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}[\hat J^{\mu\nu}, \hat P^\rho] + O(\omega^2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the right side, antisymmetrized). $(\delta^\rho{}_\mu + \omega^\rho{}_\mu)\hat P^\mu = \hat P^\rho + g^{\rho\alpha}\omega_{\alpha\mu}\hat P^\mu$. Antisymmetrize the coefficient of $\omega_{\alpha\mu}$ as in Step 5 of Derivation §C3.5.4 and rename $\alpha\mu \to \mu\nu$:
>
> $$
> g^{\rho\alpha}\omega_{\alpha\mu}\hat P^\mu = \frac12\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl(g^{\rho\mu}\hat P^\nu - g^{\rho\nu}\hat P^\mu\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (compare). The coefficients of $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ give $\frac i2[\hat J^{\mu\nu}, \hat P^\rho] = \frac12(g^{\rho\mu}\hat P^\nu - g^{\rho\nu}\hat P^\mu)$; multiply by $-2i$: $[\hat J^{\mu\nu}, \hat P^\rho] = -i(g^{\rho\mu}\hat P^\nu - g^{\rho\nu}\hat P^\mu) = i(g^{\nu\rho}\hat P^\mu - g^{\mu\rho}\hat P^\nu)$.
>
> **Step 4** ($[\hat P, \hat P]$). Take the $\hat P$-law with $\Lambda = \mathbb 1$, $a = \varepsilon$: the right side is $\hat P^\rho$. The left side, with $U = \mathbb 1 + i\varepsilon_\nu \hat P^\nu$, $U^{-1} = \mathbb 1 - i\varepsilon_\nu \hat P^\nu$, is $\hat P^\rho - i\varepsilon_\nu \hat P^\nu \hat P^\rho + i\varepsilon_\nu \hat P^\rho \hat P^\nu + O(\varepsilon^2) = \hat P^\rho - i\varepsilon_\nu[\hat P^\nu, \hat P^\rho] + O(\varepsilon^2)$. Each $\varepsilon_\nu$ is arbitrary: $[\hat P^\nu, \hat P^\rho] = 0$.
>
> **Step 5** (three-dimensional form). Insert $g^{00} = 1$, $g^{ij} = -\delta_{ij}$, $g^{0i} = 0$ into Step 3.
> - $[\hat J^i, \hat P^j] = \frac12\varepsilon_{ikl}[\hat J^{kl}, \hat P^j] = \frac i2\varepsilon_{ikl}(g^{lj}\hat P^k - g^{kj}\hat P^l) = \frac i2(-\varepsilon_{ikj}\hat P^k + \varepsilon_{ijl}\hat P^l)$. Since $-\varepsilon_{ikj} = \varepsilon_{ijk}$, both terms are $\frac i2\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat P^k$ after renaming $l \to k$: $[\hat J^i, \hat P^j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat P^k$.
> - $[\hat J^i, \hat H] = \frac12\varepsilon_{ikl}[\hat J^{kl}, \hat P^0] = \frac i2\varepsilon_{ikl}(g^{l0}\hat P^k - g^{k0}\hat P^l) = 0$.
> - $[\hat K^i, \hat P^j] = [\hat J^{0i}, \hat P^j] = i(g^{ij}\hat P^0 - g^{0j}\hat P^i) = -i\delta_{ij}\hat H$.
> - $[\hat K^i, \hat H] = [\hat J^{0i}, \hat P^0] = i(g^{i0}\hat P^0 - g^{00}\hat P^i) = -i\hat P^i$.
> - $[\hat P^i, \hat H] = [\hat P^i, \hat P^j] = 0$ are Step 4.
>
> ⚑ By-product: $[\hat K^i, \hat H] = -i\hat P^i \neq 0$: the boost generators are not conserved in the sense of commuting with $\hat H$ → [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-5-4|Remark: What the algebra says: conservation, and no boost quantum number]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The commutators are the second-order content of the group law: the first-order expansion *defines* the generators, the transformation law of the generators *is* the algebra.
> - The same steps in the $5\times5$ matrices acting on $(x, 1)$, where $(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ fills the $4\times4$ block and $(P^\mu)^\alpha{}_4 = -ig^{\mu\alpha}$, reproduce every relation (a finite-dimensional, non-unitary representation of the Poincaré group; checked numerically).
> - Used next: $\hat P^2$ commutes with everything ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-6|Theorem §C3.5.6]]); the little-group generators of a massless particle form the same algebra as $(\hat J^3, \hat P^1, \hat P^2)$ ([[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]).

*Uses:* [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]], [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]], [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]], [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the algebra says: conservation, and no boost quantum number
> $[\hat H, \hat{\mathbf P}] = [\hat H, \hat{\mathbf J}] = 0$: momentum and angular momentum are conserved, the quantum form of [[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-2|QM Theorem §C8.1.2]]. $[\hat K^i, \hat H] = -i\hat P^i$ says the boost generators do not commute with $\hat H$, yet they are conserved in the sense of Noether, because they contain the time explicitly: with $\hat K^i = t\hat P^i - \int d^3x\,x^i\hat T^{00}$ ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-13|Theorem §C3.5.13]]), the Heisenberg equation for an explicitly time-dependent operator gives $\frac{d\hat K^i}{dt} = \partial_t\hat K^i + i[\hat H, \hat K^i] = \hat P^i - \hat P^i = 0$, the uniform motion of the centre of energy ([[§C1b.8 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor#^thm-c1b-8-3|Theorem §C1b.8.3]]). A conserved quantity that does not commute with $\hat H$ cannot label stationary states, so there is no "boost quantum number": among the ten generators, the largest commuting set that classifies particles is $\hat P^\mu$ together with one rotation-type label ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group|§C3.6★]]). The relations $[\hat K^i, \hat K^j] = -i\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat J^k$ (two boosts make a rotation, [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^rem-c3-2-2|§C3.2, Remark: Two boosts make a rotation]]) and $[\hat K^i, \hat P^j] = -i\delta_{ij}\hat H$ (a boost mixes energy into momentum) are what distinguish the Poincaré algebra from the Galilean one.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6 (Step 3 of the Poincaré-algebra derivation) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.2 (Remark "No boost quantum number")*

## The Casimir P² and the vacuum

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.6: P² Is a Casimir Operator
> $\hat P^2 \equiv \hat P_\mu \hat P^\mu = \hat H^2 - \hat{\mathbf P}^2$ commutes with all ten generators, $[\hat P^2, \hat P^\mu] = [\hat P^2, \hat J^{\mu\nu}] = 0$, and is invariant under every Poincaré transformation, $U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\,\hat P^2\,U(\Lambda, a) = \hat P^2$. On a momentum eigenstate its eigenvalue $p^2$ is unchanged by $U(\Lambda, a)$.
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §2.3 (Note "P² as a Casimir operator") · Yu §3.3, eqs. (3.88)–(3.96) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.7 ("Invariants")*

^thm-c3-5-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (lower an index in $[\hat P, \hat J]$). By [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-5|Theorem §C3.5.5]], $[\hat P^\mu, \hat J^{\rho\sigma}] = -[\hat J^{\rho\sigma}, \hat P^\mu] = -i(g^{\sigma\mu}\hat P^\rho - g^{\rho\mu}\hat P^\sigma)$. Multiply by $g_{\mu\lambda}$ (a number, so it passes through the commutator): $[\hat P_\lambda, \hat J^{\rho\sigma}] = i(\delta^\rho{}_\lambda \hat P^\sigma - \delta^\sigma{}_\lambda \hat P^\rho)$.
>
> **Step 2** (product rule). $[AB, C] = A[B, C] + [A, C]B$ with $A = \hat P^\mu$, $B = \hat P_\mu$, $C = \hat J^{\rho\sigma}$, summed over $\mu$:
>
> $$
> [\hat P^2, \hat J^{\rho\sigma}] = \hat P^\mu[\hat P_\mu, \hat J^{\rho\sigma}] + [\hat P^\mu, \hat J^{\rho\sigma}]\hat P_\mu = i\bigl(\hat P^\rho \hat P^\sigma - \hat P^\sigma \hat P^\rho\bigr) + i\bigl(g^{\rho\mu}\hat P^\sigma \hat P_\mu - g^{\sigma\mu}\hat P^\rho \hat P_\mu\bigr) .
> $$
>
> In the first bracket the Kronecker deltas set $\mu = \rho$ and $\mu = \sigma$; in the second, $g^{\rho\mu}\hat P_\mu = \hat P^\rho$ and $g^{\sigma\mu}\hat P_\mu = \hat P^\sigma$, giving $i(\hat P^\sigma \hat P^\rho - \hat P^\rho \hat P^\sigma)$.
>
> **Step 3** (the momenta commute). $[\hat P^\rho, \hat P^\sigma] = 0$ (Theorem §C3.5.5), so both brackets vanish: $[\hat P^2, \hat J^{\rho\sigma}] = 0$. And $[\hat P^2, \hat P^\nu] = \hat P^\mu[\hat P_\mu, \hat P^\nu] + [\hat P^\mu, \hat P^\nu]\hat P_\mu = 0$.
>
> **Step 4** (finite form, directly). By [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]], $U^{-1}\hat P^\mu \hat P^\nu U = U^{-1}\hat P^\mu U\,U^{-1}\hat P^\nu U = \Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta \hat P^\alpha \hat P^\beta$, and $g_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta = g_{\alpha\beta}$, so $U^{-1}\hat P^2U = \hat P^2$.
>
> **Step 5** (eigenvalues). If $\hat P^\mu|p\rangle = p^\mu|p\rangle$, then $\hat P^2U|p\rangle = U\,U^{-1}\hat P^2U|p\rangle = U\hat P^2|p\rangle = p^2\,U|p\rangle$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Only $[\hat P, \hat P] = 0$ and the vector character of $\hat P$ enter; the invariance of $p^2$ is the operator version of [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-2|REL Theorem §B1.2.2]].
> - $\hat P^2$ is a Casimir operator in the sense of [[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^def-c3-1-11|Def. §C3.1.11]], so it is a number on every irreducible representation ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]]): the mass squared $m^2$ of a particle.
> - Used next: one particle, one orbit ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]).

^der-c3-5-6

*Uses:* [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-5|Theorem §C3.5.5]], [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]]

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: A second Casimir
> $\hat P^2$ fixes the mass but cannot tell a scalar from a vector particle of the same mass. The Poincaré algebra has one more independent Casimir, $\hat W^2 = \hat W_\mu \hat W^\mu$, built from the Pauli–Lubanski vector $\hat W^\mu = -\frac12\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\hat J_{\nu\rho}\hat P_\sigma$ ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^def-c3-6-3|Def. §C3.6.3]]); on a massive particle of spin $s$ it equals $-m^2s(s+1)$, on a massless particle of helicity $\lambda$ it vanishes and $\hat W^\mu = \lambda \hat P^\mu$ ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-11|Theorem §C3.6.11]], [[§C3.7★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-7-7|Theorem §C3.7.7]]). Mass and spin are the two Casimir labels of an irreducible representation.
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §2.3 (Note "P² as a Casimir operator", where $\hat W^\mu$ is stated)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.7: An Invariant Vacuum Has Zero Four-Momentum
> Let $|0\rangle$ be an eigenvector of the four $\hat P^\mu$, $\hat P^\mu|0\rangle = p_0^\mu|0\rangle$, and let $U(\Lambda)|0\rangle = c(\Lambda)|0\rangle$ for every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$, $|c(\Lambda)| = 1$. Then $p_0^\mu = 0$. In particular, for the free scalar field the generators of a Poincaré representation with an invariant vacuum are the *normal-ordered* $\hat H$ and $\hat{\mathbf P}$: a zero-point energy $E_0 \neq 0$ with $\hat{\mathbf P}|0\rangle = 0$ is incompatible with Lorentz invariance of the vacuum.
>
> *Source: derived here from Theorem §C3.5.4 and the orbit theorem; the vacuum's invariance is item 3 of Principle §C3.5.1 (the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.7, table: "p = 0 … the vacuum"; Yu §3.3, after eq. (3.109))*

^thm-c3-5-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the transformed vacuum is an eigenvector of the transformed momentum). By [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]] with $a = 0$, $\hat P^\mu U(\Lambda) = U(\Lambda)\,U^{-1}(\Lambda)\hat P^\mu U(\Lambda) = U(\Lambda)\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu \hat P^\nu$. Acting on $|0\rangle$:
>
> $$
> \hat P^\mu U(\Lambda)|0\rangle = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,p_0^\nu\;U(\Lambda)|0\rangle .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (invariance). The left side is $c(\Lambda)\hat P^\mu|0\rangle = c(\Lambda)p_0^\mu|0\rangle$, the right side $c(\Lambda)\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu p_0^\nu|0\rangle$. Divide by $c(\Lambda) \neq 0$ and compare the coefficients of $|0\rangle \neq 0$: $\Lambda p_0 = p_0$ for every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$.
>
> **Step 3** (only the origin is fixed by every Λ). The orbit of $p_0$ is then the single point $\{p_0\}$. By [[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-4|Theorem §C1a.4.4]] the orbits are the hyperboloid sheets, the one-sheeted hyperboloids, the two null half-cones and the origin, and only the origin is a single point. So $p_0 = 0$.
>
> **Step 4** (the zero-point energy). The unordered Hamiltonian has $\hat H|0\rangle = E_0|0\rangle$ with $E_0 = V\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{E_{\mathbf p}}2$ ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^thm-c2a-3-4|Theorem §C2a.3.4]]) and $\hat{\mathbf P}|0\rangle = 0$ ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^thm-c2a-3-5|Theorem §C2a.3.5]]), so $p_0 = (E_0, \mathbf 0)$, which Step 3 forbids unless $E_0 = 0$. ⚑ By-product: normal ordering ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^def-c2a-3-1|Def. §C2a.3.1]]) is not only convenient but required for $\hat H = \hat P^0$ to be the time component of a four-vector of generators with an invariant vacuum → [[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^rem-c2a-3-1|§C2a.3, Remark: Why the zero-point energy is dropped]].
>

> [!principle] Principle §C3.5.8: Covariance of Quantum Fields
> A field $\hat\Phi_a$ whose index carries the finite-dimensional representation $D$ of [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]] is transformed by the operators of [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]] according to the classical law:
>
> $$
> U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\,\hat\Phi_a(x)\,U(\Lambda, a) = D(\Lambda)_a{}^b\,\hat\Phi_b\bigl(\Lambda^{-1}(x - a)\bigr) .
> $$
>
> As operator-valued distributions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-11|Def. §CA.2.11]]) this means, for test functions $f^a$, $U^{-1}\hat\Phi(f)U = \int d^4x\,f^a(x)D(\Lambda)_a{}^b\hat\Phi_b(\Lambda^{-1}(x - a))$, i.e. the smeared field with the test function moved by $(\Lambda, a)$.
>
> *Domain: every relativistic field theory of the course; for spinor fields $D$ is a representation of the cover ([[§C5a.4 SL(2,C) and the Group Action on Spinor Space#^thm-c5a-4-8|Theorem §C5a.4.8]]; the Dirac field: [[§C5b.4 Fermions, Fock Space and the Pauli Principle#^rem-c5b-4-1|§C5b.4, Remark: Lorentz covariance of the quantized field]]). For the free scalar field it is a theorem ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-12|Theorem §C3.5.12]]).*
>

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.9: Translations of a Field; the Heisenberg Equation
> Under [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-5-8|Principle §C3.5.8]], translations move a field without touching its index, $U^{-1}(\mathbb 1, a)\hat\Phi_a(x)U(\mathbb 1, a) = \hat\Phi_a(x - a)$, and equivalently
>
> $$
> [\hat\Phi_a(x), \hat P^\mu] = i\,\partial^\mu\hat\Phi_a(x), \qquad \hat\Phi_a(x) = e^{i\hat P\cdot x}\,\hat\Phi_a(0)\,e^{-i\hat P\cdot x} .
> $$
>
> The time component is the Heisenberg equation $i\partial_t\hat\Phi_a = [\hat\Phi_a, \hat H]$; the space components are $[\hat\Phi_a, \hat{\mathbf P}] = -i\nabla\hat\Phi_a$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6 (Derivation "Translations of a field: the Heisenberg equation is translation invariance"), eq. (PPhi) · Yu §3.2, eqs. (3.80)–(3.86) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.2, eq. (field-P-commutator)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the law for a translation). In Principle §C3.5.8 put $\Lambda = \mathbb 1$: $D(\mathbb 1) = \mathbb 1$ and $\Lambda^{-1}(x - a) = x - a$, so $U^{-1}(\mathbb 1, a)\hat\Phi_a(x)U(\mathbb 1, a) = \hat\Phi_a(x - a)$.
>
> **Step 2** (first order in a). For $a = \varepsilon$, [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]] and [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]] give $U = \mathbb 1 + i\varepsilon_\mu \hat P^\mu$, $U^{-1} = \mathbb 1 - i\varepsilon_\mu \hat P^\mu$. Expanding the product, dropping the $\varepsilon^2$ term:
>
> $$
> (\mathbb 1 - i\varepsilon_\mu \hat P^\mu)\,\hat\Phi_a\,(\mathbb 1 + i\varepsilon_\mu \hat P^\mu) = \hat\Phi_a + i\varepsilon_\mu\hat\Phi_a\hat P^\mu - i\varepsilon_\mu \hat P^\mu\hat\Phi_a + O(\varepsilon^2) = \hat\Phi_a + i\varepsilon_\mu[\hat\Phi_a, \hat P^\mu] + O(\varepsilon^2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (Taylor on the right). $\hat\Phi_a(x - \varepsilon) = \hat\Phi_a(x) - \varepsilon^\mu\partial_\mu\hat\Phi_a(x) + O(\varepsilon^2) = \hat\Phi_a(x) - \varepsilon_\mu\partial^\mu\hat\Phi_a(x) + O(\varepsilon^2)$ ([[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-10|Theorem §C1a.5.10]]; for an operator-valued distribution the derivative is moved onto the test function, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|Def. §CA.2.4]], and the expansion is that of the smooth function $f(\cdot + \varepsilon)$).
>
> **Step 4** (compare). The four $\varepsilon_\mu$ are independent: $i[\hat\Phi_a, \hat P^\mu] = -\partial^\mu\hat\Phi_a$, i.e. $[\hat\Phi_a, \hat P^\mu] = i\partial^\mu\hat\Phi_a$.
>
> **Step 5** (finite form). Step 1 with [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]], 3, reads $e^{-i\hat P\cdot a}\hat\Phi_a(x)e^{i\hat P\cdot a} = \hat\Phi_a(x - a)$. Put $a = x$: $e^{-i\hat P\cdot x}\hat\Phi_a(x)e^{i\hat P\cdot x} = \hat\Phi_a(0)$; multiply by $e^{i\hat P\cdot x}$ on the left and $e^{-i\hat P\cdot x}$ on the right.
>
> **Step 6** (time and space components). $\mu = 0$: $\hat P^0 = \hat H$, $\partial^0 = \partial_t$, so $[\hat\Phi_a, \hat H] = i\partial_t\hat\Phi_a$. $\mu = j$: $\partial^j = -\partial_j$, so $[\hat\Phi_a, \hat P^j] = -i\partial_j\hat\Phi_a$. With $x = (t, \mathbf 0) + (0, \mathbf x)$, Step 5 at $\mathbf x$ fixed gives $\hat\Phi(t, \mathbf x) = e^{i\hat Ht}\hat\Phi(0, \mathbf x)e^{-i\hat Ht}$: [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^def-c2b-1-1|Def. §C2b.1.1]].
>
> ⚑ By-product: the Heisenberg picture of [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields|§C2b.1]], introduced there as a choice of picture, is the time-translation part of Principle §C3.5.8, and the Heisenberg equation is not an extra postulate → [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-5-6|Remark: Principle or theorem]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - A translation acts the same way on every field, whatever its index: $D$ represents the Lorentz part only.
> - For the free scalar field both sides were computed independently: $[\hat\phi, \hat H] = i\partial_t\hat\phi$ from the canonical commutators ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-2|Theorem §C2b.1.2]]) and $[\hat\phi, \hat P^\mu] = i\partial^\mu\hat\phi$ from the Noether charges ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-13|Theorem §C3.5.13]]).
> - Used next: $\langle0|\hat\phi(x)|\mathbf p\rangle = e^{-ip\cdot x}\langle0|\hat\phi(0)|\mathbf p\rangle$, the positive-frequency plane wave of [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-5|Theorem §C2b.1.5]], follows from Step 5 and $\hat P^\mu|\mathbf p\rangle = p^\mu|\mathbf p\rangle$, $\langle0|\hat P^\mu = 0$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.10: Lorentz Transformations of a Field: Orbital plus Spin
> Under [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-5-8|Principle §C3.5.8]], with the orbital generators $L^{\mu\nu} = i(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu)$ ([[§C3.4 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]) and the representation matrices $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ of the field's index ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]),

> $$
> [\hat\Phi_a(x), \hat J^{\mu\nu}] = L^{\mu\nu}\hat\Phi_a(x) + D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b\,\hat\Phi_b(x) .
> $$
>
> In three-dimensional form, $[\hat\Phi_a, \hat{\mathbf J}] = -i\,\mathbf x\times\nabla\,\hat\Phi_a + D(\boldsymbol{\mathcal J})_a{}^b\hat\Phi_b$: orbital angular momentum plus spin. For a scalar field $[\hat\phi(x), \hat J^{\mu\nu}] = L^{\mu\nu}\hat\phi(x)$; for a vector field the spin part is $(\mathcal J^{\rho\sigma})^\mu{}_\nu \hat A^\nu$.
>

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the law for a Lorentz transformation). Principle §C3.5.8 with $a = 0$ and $\Lambda = \mathbb 1 + \omega$: $U^{-1}(\Lambda)\hat\Phi_a(x)U(\Lambda) = D(\Lambda)_a{}^b\hat\Phi_b(\Lambda^{-1}x)$.

>

> U^{-1}\hat\Phi_aU = \hat\Phi_a + \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl(\hat J^{\mu\nu}\hat\Phi_a - \hat\Phi_a\hat J^{\mu\nu}\bigr) = \hat\Phi_a - \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\,[\hat\Phi_a, \hat J^{\mu\nu}] .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the argument). $\hat\Phi_b(\Lambda^{-1}x) = \hat\Phi_b(x) - \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}L^{\mu\nu}\hat\Phi_b(x) + O(\omega^2)$: this is [[§C3.4 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]] (equivalently the scalar line of [[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^thm-c1b-1-2|Theorem §C1b.1.2]], whose operator is $\frac12\omega_{\mu\nu}(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu) = -\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}L^{\mu\nu}$).
>
> **Step 4** (the index). $D(\mathbb 1 + \omega)_a{}^b = \delta_a{}^b - \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b + O(\omega^2)$ (Def. §C3.2.1; in the notation of Def. §C1b.1.3, $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}) = iS^{\mu\nu}$). The product of Steps 3 and 4 has four terms: $\delta\cdot\hat\Phi_b \to \hat\Phi_a$; $\delta\cdot(-\frac i2\omega L\hat\Phi_b) \to -\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}L^{\mu\nu}\hat\Phi_a$; $(-\frac i2\omega D)\cdot\hat\Phi_b \to -\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b\hat\Phi_b$; and the product of the two first-order terms, second order, dropped.
>
> **Step 5** (compare). Both sides have coefficients of $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ antisymmetric in $\mu\nu$ ($\hat J^{\mu\nu}$, $L^{\mu\nu}$ and $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ all are), so $-\frac i2[\hat\Phi_a, \hat J^{\mu\nu}] = -\frac i2L^{\mu\nu}\hat\Phi_a - \frac i2D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b\hat\Phi_b$. Multiply by $2i$.
>
> **Step 6** (three-dimensional form). $L^i \equiv \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}L^{jk} = \frac i2\varepsilon_{ijk}(x^j\partial^k - x^k\partial^j) = i\varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\partial^k$ (the two terms are equal after $j \leftrightarrow k$), and $\partial^k = -\partial_k$, so $\mathbf L = -i\,\mathbf x\times\nabla$, the orbital angular momentum of Quantum Mechanics with $\hbar = 1$ ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-1|QM Theorem §C5.4.1]]).

## The free scalar field: an explicit representation

For the free real scalar field of [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^mod-c2a-1-1|Model §C2a.1.1]] everything above can be constructed rather than postulated. Write $d\mu(p) \equiv \frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_{\mathbf p}}$ for the invariant measure on the mass shell $H^+_m$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-2|Def. §C2a.4.2]]), $\hat\alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p} \equiv \sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}\,\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$ for the creation operator of the relativistically normalized state, $|\mathbf p\rangle = \hat\alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-1|Def. §C2a.4.1]]), and $\Lambda\mathbf p$ for the spatial part of $\Lambda p$, $p^0 = E_{\mathbf p}$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.11: The Free Scalar Field Carries a Unitary Representation
> 1. On the one-particle space of wave packets $|g\rangle = \int d\mu(p)\,g(\mathbf p)\,|\mathbf p\rangle$, $g \in L^2(H^+_m, d\mu)$, the operators
>
> $$
> \bigl(U(\Lambda, a)g\bigr)(\mathbf p) = e^{ip\cdot a}\,g(\Lambda^{-1}\mathbf p)
> $$
>
> form a unitary representation of the Poincaré group; in kernel form $U(\Lambda, a)|\mathbf p\rangle = e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\,|\Lambda\mathbf p\rangle$.
> 2. On Fock space, $U(\Lambda, a)|\mathbf p_1, \dots, \mathbf p_n\rangle = e^{i(\Lambda p_1 + \dots + \Lambda p_n)\cdot a}\,|\Lambda\mathbf p_1, \dots, \Lambda\mathbf p_n\rangle$ and $U(\Lambda, a)|0\rangle = |0\rangle$ define a unitary representation satisfying [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]].
> 3. The mode operators transform as
>
> $$
> U(\Lambda, a)\,\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}\,U(\Lambda, a)^{-1} = e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\sqrt{\frac{E_{\Lambda\mathbf p}}{E_{\mathbf p}}}\;\hat a^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p}, \qquad U(\Lambda, a)\,\hat a_{\mathbf p}\,U(\Lambda, a)^{-1} = e^{-i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\sqrt{\frac{E_{\Lambda\mathbf p}}{E_{\mathbf p}}}\;\hat a_{\Lambda\mathbf p} .
> $$
>
> 4. The translation generators are the normal-ordered energy and momentum, $\hat P^\mu = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,p^\mu\,\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}\hat a_{\mathbf p}$.
>
> *Source: PS §2.3, eqs. (2.37)–(2.38) (stated) · the user's pre-course notes, §3.3 (Remark "Realization of the massive spin-0 representation") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.7 (the parenthesis after eq. (wignerlaw)); the wave-packet form and the proofs written out here*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the measure is invariant, and Λ maps the shell onto itself). For $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$, $p \mapsto \Lambda p$ maps $H^+_m$ onto itself (an orbit, [[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-4|Theorem §C1a.4.4]]), and $\int d\mu(p)\,F(\Lambda p) = \int d\mu(p)\,F(p)$ for every integrable $F$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-3|Theorem §C2a.4.3]], 2, applied to $F\circ\Lambda$).
>
> **Step 2** (norm of a wave packet). By $\langle\mathbf p|\mathbf q\rangle = 2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-4|Theorem §C2a.4.4]]), the $\mathbf q$-integral against the delta cancels one measure exactly: $\langle g|h\rangle = \int d\mu(p)\,\overline{g(\mathbf p)}\,h(\mathbf p)$. So the one-particle space is $L^2(H^+_m, d\mu)$.
>
> **Step 3** (unitarity). The phase has modulus 1, and by Step 1 with $F = |g|^2$ and $\Lambda^{-1}$ in place of $\Lambda$:
>
> $$
> \|U(\Lambda, a)g\|^2 = \int d\mu(p)\,|g(\Lambda^{-1}\mathbf p)|^2 = \int d\mu(p)\,|g(\mathbf p)|^2 = \|g\|^2 .
> $$
>
> $U(\Lambda, a)$ is onto, since $U(\Lambda^{-1}, -\Lambda^{-1}a)$ undoes it (Step 4 with $g_2 = g_1^{-1}$); an onto isometry is unitary ([[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-51|LADR Def. 7.51]]).
>
> **Step 4** (composition). Apply $(\Lambda_1, a_1)$ and then $(\Lambda_2, a_2)$:
>
> $$
> \bigl(U(\Lambda_2, a_2)U(\Lambda_1, a_1)g\bigr)(\mathbf p) = e^{ip\cdot a_2}\bigl(U(\Lambda_1, a_1)g\bigr)(\Lambda_2^{-1}\mathbf p) = e^{ip\cdot a_2}\,e^{i(\Lambda_2^{-1}p)\cdot a_1}\,g(\Lambda_1^{-1}\Lambda_2^{-1}\mathbf p) .
> $$
>
> The Minkowski product is invariant, $(\Lambda_2^{-1}p)\cdot a_1 = p\cdot(\Lambda_2a_1)$, and $\Lambda_1^{-1}\Lambda_2^{-1} = (\Lambda_2\Lambda_1)^{-1}$, so the result is $e^{ip\cdot(\Lambda_2a_1 + a_2)}g((\Lambda_2\Lambda_1)^{-1}\mathbf p) = \bigl(U(\Lambda_2\Lambda_1, \Lambda_2a_1 + a_2)g\bigr)(\mathbf p)$: the composition law. Continuity in $(\Lambda, a)$ holds for continuous $g$ of compact support and extends to $L^2$ by density.
>
> **Step 5** (kernel form). Insert the definition into $U|g\rangle = \int d\mu(q)\,e^{iq\cdot a}g(\Lambda^{-1}\mathbf q)\,|\mathbf q\rangle$ and substitute $\mathbf q = \Lambda\mathbf p$: by Step 1, $d\mu(q) = d\mu(p)$, and $g(\Lambda^{-1}\mathbf q) = g(\mathbf p)$:
>
> $$
> U(\Lambda, a)|g\rangle = \int d\mu(p)\,g(\mathbf p)\;e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\,|\Lambda\mathbf p\rangle .
> $$
>
> Since this holds for every $g$, $U(\Lambda, a)|\mathbf p\rangle = e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}|\Lambda\mathbf p\rangle$ as kernels. (*Sense:* $|\mathbf p\rangle$ is not a vector, [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^cau-c2a-4-1|§C2a.4, Caution: Plane-wave states are not normalizable]]; the kernel statement is the identity of the maps $g \mapsto U|g\rangle$, [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-5|Theorem §C2a.4.5]].)
>
> **Step 6** (Fock space). On $n$-particle wave packets $g(\mathbf p_1, \dots, \mathbf p_n)$, symmetric in their arguments, define $(Ug)(\mathbf p_1, \dots) = e^{i(p_1 + \dots + p_n)\cdot a}g(\Lambda^{-1}\mathbf p_1, \dots, \Lambda^{-1}\mathbf p_n)$. Steps 1–5 go through factor by factor with the product measure $d\mu(p_1)\cdots d\mu(p_n)$, because the $n$-particle overlap $\langle\mathbf p_1 \dots \mathbf p_n|\mathbf q_1 \dots \mathbf q_n\rangle$ is a sum over permutations of products of the invariant kernels $2E(2\pi)^3\delta^3$ (by the mode algebra, [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-6|Theorem §C2a.2.6]], and [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-2|Theorem §C2a.4.2]]); symmetric functions go to symmetric functions. For $n = 0$ the definition is $U|0\rangle = |0\rangle$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 7** (the mode operators). $|\mathbf p, \mathbf q_1, \dots, \mathbf q_n\rangle = \hat\alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|\mathbf q_1, \dots, \mathbf q_n\rangle$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-1|Def. §C2a.4.1]]). Apply $U$ and use part 2 twice:
>
> $$
> U\hat\alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|\mathbf q_1 \dots\rangle = e^{i(\Lambda p + \sum_i\Lambda q_i)\cdot a}|\Lambda\mathbf p, \Lambda\mathbf q_1, \dots\rangle = e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\,\hat\alpha^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p}\;e^{i\sum_i(\Lambda q_i)\cdot a}|\Lambda\mathbf q_1, \dots\rangle = e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\,\hat\alpha^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p}\,U|\mathbf q_1 \dots\rangle .
> $$
>
> The states $U|\mathbf q_1 \dots\rangle$ span Fock space ($U$ is onto), so $U\hat\alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p}U^{-1} = e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\hat\alpha^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p}$ (as an identity of operator-valued distributions in $\mathbf p$, smeared with a test function as in Step 5). Divide by $\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$ and write $\hat\alpha^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p} = \sqrt{2E_{\Lambda\mathbf p}}\,\hat a^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p}$: the first formula of part 3. The adjoint, with $(UXU^{-1})^\dagger = UX^\dagger U^{-1}$ for unitary $U$, gives the second.
>
> **Step 8** (the generators of translations). With $\Lambda = \mathbb 1$, $U(\mathbb 1, a)$ multiplies $|\mathbf p_1 \dots \mathbf p_n\rangle$ by $e^{i(p_1 + \dots + p_n)\cdot a}$; by Def. §C3.5.1, $\hat P^\mu = -i\,\partial U/\partial a_\mu|_0$ has eigenvalue $p_1^\mu + \dots + p_n^\mu$ on it, and $0$ on $|0\rangle$. The operator $\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,p^\mu \hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}\hat a_{\mathbf p}$ has the same eigenvalues on the same states, by the ladder relations ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^thm-c2a-3-7|Theorem §C2a.3.7]]) and $\hat a_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle = 0$: for $\mu = 0$ it is $:\!\hat H\!:$ ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^def-c2a-3-1|Def. §C2a.3.1]]), for $\mu = j$ it is $\hat{\mathbf P}$ ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^thm-c2a-3-5|Theorem §C2a.3.5]]). ⚑ By-product: the unordered $\hat H$ would add $E_0$ on every state and spoil $U|0\rangle = |0\rangle$ → [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-7|Theorem §C3.5.7]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Unitarity is exactly the invariance of the measure ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-3|Theorem §C2a.4.3]]) and of the overlap $\langle\mathbf p|\mathbf q\rangle$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-4|Theorem §C2a.4.4]]); this is why the relativistic normalization was chosen.
> - The representation is infinite-dimensional: functions on a three-dimensional hyperboloid. The one-particle part has no label besides $\mathbf p$: it is Wigner's massive spin-0 representation ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-8|Theorem §C3.6.8]] with $s = 0$).
> - The factor $\sqrt{E_{\Lambda\mathbf p}/E_{\mathbf p}}$ in part 3 is the price of the non-invariant normalization $[\hat a_{\mathbf p}, \hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}] = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$; the relativistically normalized $\hat\alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$ transform without it.
> - Used next: the field transforms as a scalar (Theorem §C3.5.12).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.12: The Free Scalar Field Transforms as a Scalar
> With $U(\Lambda, a)$ of [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-11|Theorem §C3.5.11]] and the Heisenberg field $\hat\phi(x)$ of [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-4|Theorem §C2b.1.4]],
>
> $$
> U(\Lambda, a)\,\hat\phi(x)\,U(\Lambda, a)^{-1} = \hat\phi(\Lambda x + a), \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\,\hat\phi(x)\,U(\Lambda, a) = \hat\phi\bigl(\Lambda^{-1}(x - a)\bigr),
> $$
>
> as operator-valued distributions: $U\hat\phi(f)U^{-1} = \hat\phi(f_{(\Lambda, a)})$ with $f_{(\Lambda, a)}(y) = f(\Lambda^{-1}(y - a))$. So [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-5-8|Principle §C3.5.8]] holds for the free scalar field with $D = 1$.
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §3.3 (Remark "Realization of the massive spin-0 representation", run in the opposite direction) and §4.2, eq. (scalar-transform) · PS §2.3, eq. (2.38) with §3.1, eq. (3.2) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6 (Step 5 of Derivation "Expanding U(Λ, a)")*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the field over the invariant measure). Since $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} = \frac{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}{2E_{\mathbf p}}$, Theorem §C2b.1.4 reads
>
> $$
> \hat\phi(x) = \int d\mu(p)\,\bigl(\hat\alpha_{\mathbf p}\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + \hat\alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p}\,e^{ip\cdot x}\bigr), \qquad p^0 = E_{\mathbf p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (conjugate term by term). $U$ passes through the numbers $e^{\mp ip\cdot x}$ and through the integral (*sense:* after smearing with $f \in \mathcal S(\mathbb R^4)$ the integral has Schwartz coefficients, [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-6|Theorem §C2b.1.6]], 1, and is a limit of finite sums in every wave-packet matrix element). By Theorem §C3.5.11, 3, written for $\alpha$:
>
> $$
> U\hat\phi(x)U^{-1} = \int d\mu(p)\,\bigl(e^{-i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\,\hat\alpha_{\Lambda\mathbf p}\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\,\hat\alpha^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p}\,e^{ip\cdot x}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (change variables on the shell). Substitute $\mathbf q = \Lambda\mathbf p$, i.e. $q = \Lambda p$ on $H^+_m$: the measure is unchanged, $d\mu(p) = d\mu(q)$ (Step 1 of Derivation §C3.5.11; the change of variables of an invariant distribution, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-8|Def. §CA.2.8]]), and the domain $H^+_m$ is mapped onto itself. In the exponent, $p\cdot x = (\Lambda^{-1}q)\cdot x = q\cdot(\Lambda x)$ by invariance of the Minkowski product. So $-i(\Lambda p)\cdot a - ip\cdot x = -iq\cdot(\Lambda x + a)$ and
>
> $$
> U\hat\phi(x)U^{-1} = \int d\mu(q)\,\bigl(\hat\alpha_{\mathbf q}\,e^{-iq\cdot(\Lambda x + a)} + \hat\alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf q}\,e^{iq\cdot(\Lambda x + a)}\bigr) = \hat\phi(\Lambda x + a) .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (the inverse form). Apply Step 3 to $(\Lambda, a)^{-1} = (\Lambda^{-1}, -\Lambda^{-1}a)$, whose operator is $U(\Lambda, a)^{-1}$ ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]], 1): $U^{-1}\hat\phi(x)U = \hat\phi(\Lambda^{-1}x - \Lambda^{-1}a) = \hat\phi(\Lambda^{-1}(x - a))$.
>
> **Step 5** (smeared form). $U\hat\phi(f)U^{-1} = \int d^4x\,f(x)\,\hat\phi(\Lambda x + a)$; substitute $y = \Lambda x + a$, $d^4y = |\det\Lambda|\,d^4x = d^4x$ ([[§C1a.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1a-4-1|Theorem §C1a.4.1]]): $= \int d^4y\,f(\Lambda^{-1}(y - a))\,\hat\phi(y) = \hat\phi(f_{(\Lambda, a)})$.
>
> ⚑ By-product: the canonical construction singled out a time (equal-time commutators, $\hat\pi = \partial_t\hat\phi$, a Hamiltonian), yet the result is covariant; this theorem is the check → [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-5-6|Remark: Principle or theorem]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The invariant measure, not $d^3p$, is what makes the substitution $q = \Lambda p$ free of Jacobians; the relativistic normalization of $\hat\alpha^\dagger$ is what makes the operators transform without factors.
> - With $U|0\rangle = |0\rangle$ it gives at once $\langle0|\hat\phi(\Lambda x)\hat\phi(\Lambda y)|0\rangle = \langle0|\hat\phi(x)\hat\phi(y)|0\rangle$: the Lorentz invariance of the Wightman function ([[§C2b.2 The Wightman Function#^thm-c2b-2-3|Theorem §C2b.2.3]]) is vacuum invariance plus this theorem.
> - $\hat\pi = \partial_t\hat\phi$ is not a scalar: it is the time component of the covector $\partial_\mu\hat\phi$: differentiating Step 3 with the chain rule, $\partial y^\nu/\partial x^\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu$ for $y = \Lambda x + a$, gives $U\partial_\mu\hat\phi(x)U^{-1} = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu(\partial_\nu\hat\phi)(\Lambda x + a)$, equivalently $U^{-1}\partial_\mu\hat\phi(x)U = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu(\partial_\nu\hat\phi)(\Lambda^{-1}(x - a))$, the law of Principle §C3.5.8 for a lower index.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.13: The Noether Charges Are the Generators
> For the free real scalar field, the Noether charges of translations and Lorentz transformations, $\hat P^\mu = \int d^3x\,\hat T^{0\mu}$ ([[§C1b.7 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^thm-c1b-7-6|Theorem §C1b.7.6]]) and $\hat J^{\mu\nu} = \int d^3x\,\hat{\mathcal M}^{0\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1b.8 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor#^def-c1b-8-1|Def. §C1b.8.1]]), taken as operators (normal ordered), satisfy
>
> $$
> [\hat\phi(x), \hat P^\mu] = i\,\partial^\mu\hat\phi(x), \qquad [\hat\phi(x), \hat J^{\mu\nu}] = L^{\mu\nu}\hat\phi(x),
> $$
>
> the relations of Theorems §C3.5.9–§C3.5.10 with $D = 1$. So they are the generators of Def. §C3.5.1, with $\hat P^0 = :\!\hat H\!:$. In particular the boost generator is
>
> $$
> \hat K^i = \hat J^{0i} = t\,\hat P^i - \int d^3x\,x^i\,\hat T^{00} = -\hat K^i_{\rm N},
> $$
>
> minus the boost charge $\hat K^i_{\rm N} \equiv \hat J^{i0}$ of Def. §C1b.8.1.
>

(3.86) (stated; checked for $\hat P^\mu$ in Yu (2.129)) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.2 (Note "Agreement with the canonical construction", stated); the equal-time computation written out here*

^thm-c3-5-13

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> All commutators are at equal times: each charge is conserved, so it may be evaluated as an integral over the slice $y^0 = x^0 \equiv t$ of the field it is commuted with. Inputs: the equal-time relations $[\hat\phi(t, \mathbf x), \hat\pi(t, \mathbf y)] = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$, $[\hat\phi(t, \mathbf x), \hat\phi(t, \mathbf y)] = 0$ ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-1|Theorem §C2b.1.1]]), and $\hat\pi = \partial_t\hat\phi$ ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-4|Theorem §C2b.1.4]], 3). For the scalar field, $\hat T^{0k} = \hat\pi\,\partial^k\hat\phi$ and $\hat T^{00} = \hat{\mathcal H} = \frac12\hat\pi^2 + \frac12(\nabla\hat\phi)^2 + \frac12m^2\hat\phi^2$ (Theorem §C1b.7.6), and $\hat{\mathcal M}^{0\mu\nu} = x^\mu \hat T^{0\nu} - x^\nu \hat T^{0\mu}$ ([[§C1b.8 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor#^thm-c1b-8-1|Theorem §C1b.8.1]], no spin part). Normal ordering changes each charge by a c-number, which drops out of every commutator. Write $\hat\phi = \hat\phi(t, \mathbf x)$ and primes for fields at $(t, \mathbf y)$.
>
> **Step 1** ($[\hat\phi, \hat\pi'\,\partial'^k\hat\phi']$). By $[A, BC] = [A, B]C + B[A, C]$: $[\hat\phi, \hat\pi'\partial'^k\hat\phi'] = [\hat\phi, \hat\pi']\,\partial'^k\hat\phi' + \hat\pi'\,\partial'^k[\hat\phi, \hat\phi'] = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)\,\partial'^k\hat\phi'$. The second term vanishes because $[\hat\phi, \hat\phi']$ is zero for *all* $\mathbf y$, so its $\mathbf y$-derivative is zero ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^cau-c2b-1-1|§C2b.1, Caution: Equal times only]]).
>
> **Step 2** ($[\hat\phi, \hat{\mathcal H}']$). $[\hat\phi, \frac12\hat\pi'^2] = \frac12(\hat\pi'[\hat\phi, \hat\pi'] + [\hat\phi, \hat\pi']\hat\pi') = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)\,\hat\pi'$ (the commutator is a number); $[\hat\phi, \frac12(\nabla'\hat\phi')^2] = 0$ and $[\hat\phi, \frac12m^2\hat\phi'^2] = 0$ as in Step 1. So $[\hat\phi, \hat{\mathcal H}'] = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)\,\hat\pi'$.
>
> **Step 3** (momentum). Integrate Step 1 over $\mathbf y$; the delta sets $\mathbf y = \mathbf x$ (*sense:* smeared in $\mathbf x$ with $g \in \mathcal S(\mathbb R^3)$, the $\mathbf y$-integral of $g(\mathbf x)\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$ against the operator-valued distribution $\partial'^k\hat\phi'$ is $\partial^k\hat\phi(t, g)$, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-2|Def. §CA.2.2]]): $[\hat\phi, \hat P^k] = i\,\partial^k\hat\phi$. Integrating Step 2: $[\hat\phi, \hat H] = i\pi = i\partial_t\hat\phi = i\partial^0\hat\phi$. Together, $[\hat\phi, \hat P^\mu] = i\partial^\mu\hat\phi$.
>
> **Step 4** (rotations, $\mu\nu = jk$). $\hat J^{jk} = \int d^3y\,(y^j\hat T^{0k} - y^k\hat T^{0j})$. By Step 1, $[\hat\phi, y^j\hat\pi'\partial'^k\hat\phi'] = iy^j\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)\partial'^k\hat\phi'$, and the delta sets $y^j = x^j$:
>
> $$
> [\hat\phi, \hat J^{jk}] = i\bigl(x^j\partial^k - x^k\partial^j\bigr)\hat\phi = L^{jk}\hat\phi .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (boosts, $\mu\nu = 0k$). $\hat J^{0k} = \int d^3y\,(y^0\hat T^{0k} - y^k\hat T^{00})$ with $y^0 = t$ on the slice: $\hat J^{0k} = t\hat P^k - \int d^3y\,y^k\,\hat{\mathcal H}'$. By Steps 3 and 2,
>
> $$
> [\hat\phi, \hat J^{0k}] = t\cdot i\partial^k\hat\phi - i\,x^k\,\hat\pi = i\bigl(x^0\partial^k - x^k\partial^0\bigr)\hat\phi = L^{0k}\hat\phi ,
> $$
>
> using $\hat\pi = \partial_t\hat\phi = \partial^0\hat\phi$ and $t = x^0$. ⚑ By-product: $\hat J^{0k}$ contains $t$ explicitly, and $[\hat\phi, \hat J^{0k}]$ involves $\partial_t\hat\phi$ although only equal-time relations were used: the boost needs the dynamics ($\hat\pi = \partial_t\hat\phi$), rotations and translations in space do not → [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-5-6|Remark: Principle or theorem]].
>
> **Step 6** (they are the generators). Steps 3–5 are the relations that the generators of Def. §C3.5.1 satisfy by Theorems §C3.5.9–§C3.5.10 (with $D = 1$, which [[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-12|Theorem §C3.5.12]] establishes for this field). Let $X$ be the difference of a Noether charge and the corresponding generator. It commutes with every $\hat\phi(x)$, hence with $\hat\pi = \partial_t\hat\phi$, hence with every $\hat a_{\mathbf p}$ and $\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$, which are extracted from $\hat\phi$ and $\hat\pi$ by [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-5|Theorem §C2a.2.5]]. Then $\hat a_{\mathbf p}X|0\rangle = X\hat a_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle = 0$ for every $\mathbf p$, so $X|0\rangle = c|0\rangle$ (the vacuum is the only state annihilated by every $\hat a_{\mathbf p}$, [[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^pr-c2a-3-3|Principle §C2a.3.3]]), and $X\,\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_1}\cdots \hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_n}|0\rangle = \hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_1}\cdots \hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_n}X|0\rangle = c\,\hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_1}\cdots \hat a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_n}|0\rangle$: $X = c\mathbb 1$ on Fock space (the operator form of Schur's lemma, [[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]]). The constant is $c = \langle0|X|0\rangle$: a normal-ordered charge has vanishing vacuum expectation value, and a generator annihilates the invariant vacuum ($U|0\rangle = |0\rangle$, differentiated). So $c = 0$. For $\hat P^\mu$ the identification is also Theorem §C3.5.11, 4.
>
> **Step 7** (the boost dictionary). $\hat K^i \equiv \hat J^{0i}$ (Def. §C3.5.1) $= t\hat P^i - \int d^3x\,x^i\hat T^{00}$ by Step 5, while Def. §C1b.8.1 names $\hat K^i_{\rm N} \equiv \hat J^{i0} = -\hat J^{0i}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Noether's theorem and the representation theory meet: the classical charges, quantized canonically, are the generators of the unitary representation, with the same signs. This resolves [[§C1b.8 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor#^cau-c1b-8-1|§C1b.8, Caution: Notation in Yu and in the conventions table]].
> - Assumptions: the charges are conserved (so any slice may be used) and finite, i.e. the fields fall off at spatial infinity in every matrix element considered; normal ordering only shifts c-numbers.
> - Used next: the algebra of these charges is the Poincaré algebra (Theorem §C3.5.5) because they are the generators.

> [!remark] Remark: Principle or theorem
> Principle §C3.5.8 is what a relativistic quantum field theory *requires*, and in the axiomatic formulation it is an axiom. For the free scalar field it is a theorem twice over: from the explicit $U$ (Theorem §C3.5.12) and from the Noether charges (Theorem §C3.5.13). The layer change matters because canonical quantization is not manifestly covariant: it fixes a time slice, postulates commutators at equal times ([[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2a-1-2|Principle §C2a.1.2]]) and evolves with $\hat H$. Space translations and rotations act within the slice and are automatic; boosts tilt the slice, and their correctness rests on the dynamics, as Step 5 of Derivation §C3.5.13 shows. For an interacting theory the check is that the Noether charges, built with the interacting $\hat{\mathcal H}$, still close into the Poincaré algebra; the relation $[\hat K^i, \hat H] = -i\hat P^i$ is the nontrivial one, and it holds when the interaction density is a Lorentz scalar (QFT C6, planned). The Heisenberg equation, an independent input in [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields|§C2b.1]], is the time-translation part of the same principle ([[§C3.5 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-5-9|Theorem §C3.5.9]]).
>

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Quantum Mechanics built three generator constructions separately, translations ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-4|QM Principle §C2.2.4]]), rotations ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|QM Principle §C5.1.2]]) and time evolution ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^pr-c3-1-1|QM Principle §C3.1.1]]); Principle §C3.5.1 is the single relativistic one, and boosts are the generators QM never needed, the ones that mix $\hat H$ with $\hat{\mathbf P}$.
> - The Hermiticity argument of Theorem §C3.5.2 is the one that made $\hat{\mathbf J}$ Hermitian in [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|QM Principle §C5.1.2]]; what fails relativistically is only the finite-dimensional *matrix* form of the boost, [[§C3.3 Finite-Dimensional Representations of the Lorentz Algebra#^thm-c3-3-10|Theorem §C3.3.10]].
> - The ten generators match the ten parameters of the Poincaré group and the ten classical conservation laws — [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^rem-b1-2-5|REL Remark: Ten parameters, ten conservation laws]], [[§C1b.7 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum|§C1b.7]]–[[§C1b.8 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor|§C1b.8]]; Theorem §C3.5.13 is the quantum statement that the charges generate the symmetry, as Poisson brackets with $H$ generate time evolution classically ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1b-4-6|Theorem §C1b.4.6]]).
> - The Heisenberg field of [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^def-c2b-1-1|Def. §C2b.1.1]] is $\hat\phi(x) = e^{i\hat P\cdot x}\hat\phi(0)e^{-i\hat P\cdot x}$ restricted to time; the plane wave $\langle0|\hat\phi(x)|\mathbf p\rangle = e^{-ip\cdot x}$ of [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-5|Theorem §C2b.1.5]] is translation covariance applied to one matrix element, and the field creating a particle at a point ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-6|Theorem §C2a.4.6]]) is the same matrix element at $t = 0$.
