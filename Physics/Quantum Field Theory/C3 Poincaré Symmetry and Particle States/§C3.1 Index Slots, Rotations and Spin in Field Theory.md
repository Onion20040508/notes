How do rotations act on the indices of a field and on its states, and why does half-integer spin force $SU(2)$ instead of $SO(3)$? Quantum Mechanics already holds everything about rotations themselves: the algebra $[J_i, J_j] = i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}J_k$ as the multiplication table of $SO(3)$ near the identity ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-4|QM Theorem §C5.1.4]]), $SU(2)$ as the double cover ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-8|QM Theorem §C5.2.8]]), the multiplets and the rotation matrices ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices]]); the matrix groups and the generators of the vector representation are [[§CB.1 Matrix Lie Groups, the Exponential Map and Lie Algebras#^def-cb-1-1|Def. §CB.1.1]] and [[§C1a.7 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-7-1|Def. §C1a.7.1]]. The representation theory that makes these facts reusable — groups, Lie algebras and their representations, irreducibility and Casimirs, why compactness gives unitarity, the highest-weight classification proved without an inner product, the passage from the algebra back to $SU(2)$ and $SO(3)$ and the topology behind it, and projective representations — is mathematics, proved in [[§CB.1 Matrix Lie Groups, the Exponential Map and Lie Algebras|§CB.1]]–[[§CB.7 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs|§CB.7]], [[§CB.10 SU(2) and SO(3)꞉ Spin j, Symmetric Powers and Clebsch–Gordan|§CB.10]] and [[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries|§CB.19]]. This section states the three facts of it that physics judges with, in the form the calculations use and with the proofs linked: the spin-$j$ multiplets and the Casimir test that names them, integer versus half-integer spin (the sign of a $2\pi$ rotation), and why states, being rays, admit $SU(2)$; the CB items it rests on are listed at the end. Besides these it keeps what belongs to field theory: what each index of a field labels, the course's symbols and sign conventions for the generators, the spin content of a four-vector, and why states are rays. Every item is used again, with the rotation algebra replaced by the Lorentz algebra, in [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields|§C3.2]] and on quantum states in [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations and the Poincaré Algebra|§C3.4]].

**Setup.** No dynamics and no Model box: the section is about the matrices by which rotations act on the components of a field (any field, classical or quantum, free or interacting; no Lagrangian is used) and on state vectors (any quantum system, states as rays). Its statements hold for every field with the index structure named, independent of the field equations.

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): $\hbar = 1$; spatial indices $i, j, k \in \{1, 2, 3\}$, whose position (up or down) carries no meaning in Euclidean space; $\varepsilon^{123} = +1$; generators are Hermitian and a rotation by $\theta$ about $\hat n$ is $e^{-i\theta\,\hat n\cdot\mathbf J}$, as in [[§C1a.7 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-7-2|Def. §C1a.7.2]].

## What each index labels

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.1: Index Slots
> An index names a **slot**, a vector space on which a Lorentz transformation acts by that space's own rule. Three kinds occur:
> 1. **spacetime indices** $\mu, \nu, \alpha, \beta \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$: components on $V = \mathbb R^{1,3}$, transformed by $\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$, raised and lowered with $g$;
> 2. **generator labels**: the antisymmetric pair in $J^{\mu\nu}$, $S^{\mu\nu}$, $\omega_{\mu\nu}$, saying which of the six basis elements of the Lie algebra is meant; it transforms as an antisymmetric tensor on $V$, so the rules of item 1 apply to it;
> 3. **matrix indices**: row and column of a representation matrix, belonging to the carrier space of the representation ([[§CB.3 Homomorphisms, Representations, the Identity Component and Coverings#^def-cb-3-6|Def. §CB.3.6]]) and obeying its rules: spacetime indices in the vector representation, $m = -j, \dots, j$ for spin $j$, spinor indices for Dirac spinors ([[§C5a.5 Chirality and Weyl Spinors#^rem-c5a-5-4|§C5a.5, Remark: What each Dirac index labels]]).
>
> An index obeys the rules of special relativity exactly when it is a spacetime slot.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.1 (Principle "Every index is a slot, and each slot has its own transformation rule") · PS §3.1, p. 39 (the remark after eq. (3.18))*

| symbol | what it is | its indices |
|---|---|---|
| $x^\mu$, $p^\mu$, $v^\mu$ | a vector in $V$ | $\mu$: spacetime |
| $g_{\mu\nu}$, $\delta^\mu{}_\nu$, $\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ | invariant tensors on $V$ | all spacetime |
| $\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$ | a group element, as a linear map $V \to V$ | row and column: spacetime |
| $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ | six numbers: which element of the algebra | antisymmetric pair: spacetime |
| $J^{\mu\nu}$ (abstract) | a basis element of the Lie algebra | pair: generator label |
| $(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})^\alpha{}_\beta$ | that generator as a $4\times4$ matrix on $V$ | $\mu\nu$: label; $\alpha, \beta$: matrix $=$ spacetime |
| $L^{\mu\nu} = i(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu)$ | a generator acting on functions of $x$ | $\mu\nu$: label; no matrix indices |
| $(\sigma^i)_{ab}$ | a 3-vector whose components are $2\times2$ matrices | $i$: spatial; $a, b$: spin $\tfrac12$ |
| $\hat J^{\mu\nu}$ on the Hilbert space | the Noether charges, acting on states | $\mu\nu$: label; acts on states, not on indices |

*The practical test.* Ask what happens to an index under a Lorentz transformation: if it picks up $\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$, it is a spacetime index and $g$ may be used freely; if it labels the six generators, it is an antisymmetric pair of spacetime indices; if it picks up another matrix, it belongs to another carrier space and follows that space's rules.

> [!caution] Caution: Four spacetime indices in two roles
> In the vector representation all four indices of $(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})^\alpha{}_\beta$ are spacetime indices, but $\mu\nu$ says *which* generator and $\alpha\beta$ *which matrix entry*; they look alike only because here the carrier space is spacetime itself. PS's $(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_{\alpha\beta}$ (eq. (3.18)), with both matrix indices down, is a legitimate tensor but not the matrix that acts on $v^\beta$: one matrix index must first be raised with $g$ ([[§C1a.7 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^rem-c1a-7-2|§C1a.7, Remark: Hermitian generators do not make boosts unitary]]). Problem Set 4's product rule $(AB)_{\alpha\gamma} = A_{\alpha\beta}B^\beta{}_\gamma$ is the contraction rule of item 1 applied to matrix indices that happen to be spacetime indices ([[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^cau-c1a-5-2|§C1a.5, Caution: Index bookkeeping]]). The Pauli matrices carry one slot of each other kind: $U^\dagger\sigma^iU = R^i{}_j\sigma^j$ says that transforming the two spinor slots by $U \in SU(2)$ is the same as transforming the vector slot by $R = R(U)$ ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-8|QM Theorem §C5.2.8]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.1 (paragraph "The two cases that cause confusion")*

> [!remark] Remark: Why rotations felt simpler
> In three dimensions an antisymmetric pair can be traded for one index, because there are three pairs and three axes: $J^{ij} = \varepsilon^{ijk}J^k$ and $J^k = \frac12\varepsilon^{kij}J^{ij}$ (insert the second into the first and use $\varepsilon^{ijk}\varepsilon^{klm} = \delta^{il}\delta^{jm} - \delta^{im}\delta^{jl}$, [[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-4|Theorem §C1a.5.4]]: $\varepsilon^{ijk}\cdot\frac12\varepsilon^{klm}J^{lm} = \frac12(J^{ij} - J^{ji}) = J^{ij}$). $J^3$ generates rotations *about* the $z$ axis, $J^{12}$ rotations *in* the $(12)$ plane. In four dimensions there are six generators and four directions, and the pair $\mu\nu$ cannot be traded for anything: the two-index form is the one that generalizes ([[§C1a.7 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-7-2|Def. §C1a.7.2]] splits it into $\mathbf J$ and $\mathbf K$ only by singling out time).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.1 (paragraph "Why rotations felt simpler"), §7.2, eq. (Jtwoways) · PHY 513 Lecture 7, Part A ("Classical angular momentum") · PS §3.1, p. 39*

## The rotation algebra, from the field-theory side

The algebra $[J^i, J^j] = i\varepsilon^{ijk}J^k$ has its home in Quantum Mechanics: for the rotation generators of any system it is the multiplication table of $SO(3)$ near the identity ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-4|QM Theorem §C5.1.4]]), and for the orbital $\hat L^i = \varepsilon^{ijk}\hat x^j\hat p^k$ it follows from $[\hat x^i, \hat p^j] = i\delta^{ij}$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-1|QM Theorem §B5.2.1]]); the latter is the computation of Lecture 7, of PS eq. (3.15) and of the user's 513 notes (Derivation "The rotation algebra"). Nothing is added to it here. What changes is the object of study: not a particular set of operators obeying the algebra, but the algebra itself and all the ways it can be realized.

> [!caution] Caution: The sign of the orbital generator
> With the Euclidean derivative $\partial_j = \partial/\partial x^j$ and $p^j = -i\partial_j$, $J^{ij} = x^ip^j - x^jp^i = -i(x^i\partial_j - x^j\partial_i)$, as on the Lecture 7 slide and in PS (3.15); a handwritten version recorded in the user's notes has $+i$, which is the wrong sign. The four-dimensional orbital generator $L^{\mu\nu} = i(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu)$ ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]) has a plus sign and *upper* derivatives; on spatial indices the two agree because $\partial^j = -\partial_j$ in the signature $(+,-,-,-)$: $i(x^i\partial^j - x^j\partial^i) = -i(x^i\partial_j - x^j\partial_i)$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.2 (Caution "A sign to watch") · PHY 513 Lecture 7 ("Quantum angular momentum")*

Yu writes $U(\mathbb 1 + \omega) = \mathbb 1 - \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu} = \mathbb 1 + i\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf J + i\boldsymbol\xi\cdot\mathbf K$ with $\theta_i \equiv -\frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\omega_{jk}$ (Yu (3.42), (3.44)) and $U[R_z(\theta^3)] = \exp(i\theta^3J^3)$ (Yu (3.46)): the same operator $e^{-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu}}$, with Yu's angle of opposite sign to the $\theta$ of [[§C1a.7 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-7-2|Def. §C1a.7.2]] ([[§C1a.7 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^cau-c1a-7-1|§C1a.7, Caution: Which ω is a rotation by θ]]). Georgi writes $D = 1 + i\alpha^aX_a$ (his (2.4)). Reversing the parameter leaves $[T_a, T_b] = if_{ab}{}^cT_c$ unchanged; reversing the generator instead ($T \to -T$) flips the sign of every $f_{ab}{}^c$.

> [!remark] Remark: Two uses of one theory: field indices and quantum states
> Field theory uses representations of the Lorentz group twice. Finite-dimensional ones act on the *indices of fields* ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]]; [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields|§C3.2]]–[[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group|§C3.3]]); they need not be unitary, and for the Lorentz group cannot be. Unitary (projective) ones act on the *Hilbert space of states*, where probabilities must be preserved; for the Lorentz group they are infinite-dimensional ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations and the Poincaré Algebra|§C3.4]], [[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group|§C3.6★]]). For rotations the two coincide, because $SO(3)$ is compact: every finite-dimensional representation is unitary in a suitable inner product and splits completely ([[§CB.7 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^thm-cb-7-16|Theorem §CB.7.16]]): this is why Quantum Mechanics never had to keep them apart, and why the rotation group is the right place to learn the vocabulary.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 (Principle "Hermitian generators do not make boosts unitary", §7.2)*

## Spin j and the four-vector

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.1: Spin j: the Multiplets of the Rotation Algebra and the Casimir Test
> 1. For each $j \in \{0, \frac12, 1, \frac32, \dots\}$ the rotation algebra $[J_i, J_j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}J_k$ has one irreducible representation $D^{(j)}$, on $\mathbb C^{2j+1}$ with basis $|j, m\rangle$, $m = j, j - 1, \dots, -j$, and
>
> $$
> J_3|j, m\rangle = m\,|j, m\rangle, \qquad J_\pm|j, m\rangle = \sqrt{(j \mp m)(j \pm m + 1)}\;|j, m \pm 1\rangle, \qquad \mathbf J^2 = j(j + 1)\,\mathbb 1 ,
> $$
>
> with $J_\pm = J_1 \pm iJ_2$ (Condon–Shortley phases; Yu's differ by signs: [[§CB.10 SU(2) and SO(3)꞉ Spin j, Symmetric Powers and Clebsch–Gordan#^cau-cb-10-1|§CB.10, Caution: Phase conventions for the ladder operators]]). Different $j$ give inequivalent representations, and every finite-dimensional irreducible one is equivalent to some $D^{(j)}$.
> 2. Every finite-dimensional representation of $SU(2)$ or $SO(3)$ (the rotation law of a field index, or of a multiplet of states) is a direct sum of $D^{(j)}$'s with unique multiplicities; in a suitable inner product the $J_i$ are Hermitian and the rotations unitary.
> 3. *How to use it:* the invariant pieces are those of the three generators $J_i$; compute $\mathbf J^2$ on the components, each eigenvalue $j(j + 1)$ names a spin, its eigenspace carries $D^{(j)}$ with $2j + 1$ components, and the dimensions add up to the number of components. Example §C3.1.1 runs the test on the four-vector: $0\oplus1$.
>
> *Proof:* [[§CB.10 SU(2) and SO(3)꞉ Spin j, Symmetric Powers and Clebsch–Gordan#^thm-cb-10-3|Theorem §CB.10.3]] (part 1, by highest weight, without an inner product), [[§CB.7 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^thm-cb-7-13|Theorem §CB.7.13]] (the Casimir is constant on each irreducible piece), [[§CB.7 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^thm-cb-7-16|Theorem §CB.7.16]] (part 2: compactness gives unitarity and complete reducibility), [[§CB.3 Homomorphisms, Representations, the Identity Component and Coverings#^thm-cb-3-10|Theorem §CB.3.10]] (invariant subspaces from the generators). The same catalogue in Quantum Mechanics: [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-4|QM Theorem §B5.2.4]], [[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-1|QM Theorem §C5.3.1]].
>
> *Source: Yu §3.3.1, eqs. (3.118), (3.134)–(3.138), (3.141), (3.166), pp. 91–96 · Srednicki §33, p. 213 ("we can find three $(2j+1)\times(2j+1)$ hermitian matrices … these matrices constitute all of the inequivalent, irreducible representations of the Lie algebra of SO(3)") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.2 (Derivations "Constructing every irreducible representation: ladder operators", "The representations of the rotation algebra") · PHY 513 Lecture 7 ("Representations of angular momentum": spin $j$, $2j + 1$ states)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.2: Integer and Half-Integer Spin: SO(3) or Only SU(2)
> 1. Vectors and spin-$\frac12$ objects obey one algebra: $(J_k)_{lm} = -i\varepsilon_{klm}$ on $\mathbb C^3$ and $\mathbf J = \frac12\boldsymbol\sigma$ on $\mathbb C^2$ both satisfy $[J_i, J_j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}J_k$, because $SO(3)$ and $SU(2)$ have the same Lie algebra; the map $U \mapsto R(U)$, $U(\mathbf x\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)U^\dagger = (R(U)\mathbf x)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-8|QM Theorem §C5.2.8]]), is two-to-one with kernel $\{\pm\mathbb 1\}$.
> 2. Every $D^{(j)}$ ([[§C3.1 Index Slots, Rotations and Spin in Field Theory#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]]) exponentiates to a representation $U \mapsto D^{(j)}(U)$ of $SU(2)$, and a rotation by $2\pi$ about any axis acts as
>
> $$
> e^{-2\pi i\,\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf J} = D^{(j)}(-\mathbb 1) = (-1)^{2j}\,\mathbb 1 .
> $$
>
> 3. For integer $j$, $D^{(j)}$ is a representation of $SO(3)$; for half-integer $j$ it is defined on $SO(3)$ only up to sign (a two-valued representation). On a sum of integer and half-integer spins the $2\pi$ rotation is $+1$ on one part and $-1$ on the other, not a multiple of $\mathbb 1$.
> 4. The reason is topological: $SU(2)$ is simply connected, $SO(3)$ is not; the loop of rotations by $0 \to 2\pi$ about a fixed axis cannot be shrunk to a point in $SO(3)$, and its lift to $SU(2)$ runs from $\mathbb 1$ to $-\mathbb 1$; traversed twice it can be shrunk.
>
> *How to read it:* the sign of a $2\pi$ rotation sorts tensors (integer $j$, back to themselves after $2\pi$) from spinors (half-integer $j$, back after $4\pi$); the Lorentz form of the same test is [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-5|Theorem §C3.2.5]].
>
> *Proof:* [[§CB.2 SU(2) and SO(3) as Matrix Lie Groups꞉ Structure and the Double Cover#^thm-cb-2-4|Theorem §CB.2.4]] (part 1), [[§CB.10 SU(2) and SO(3)꞉ Spin j, Symmetric Powers and Clebsch–Gordan#^thm-cb-10-13|Theorem §CB.10.13]] (parts 2–3), [[§CB.10 SU(2) and SO(3)꞉ Spin j, Symmetric Powers and Clebsch–Gordan#^thm-cb-10-11|Theorem §CB.10.11]] (part 4). In Quantum Mechanics: [[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-4|QM Theorem §C5.3.4]], [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-4|QM Theorem §C5.2.4]].
>
> *Source: Yu §3.3.1, p. 96 (below eq. (3.169): for integer $s$, $D^{(s)}$ is also a linear representation of SO(3); for half-integer $s$, a faithful representation of SU(2) and a double-valued representation of SO(3)) · Srednicki §33, p. 213 ("when $j$ is a half integer, a rotation by $2\pi$ results in an overall minus sign … the half-integer representations of this Lie algebra do qualify as representations of the group SU(2)") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.2 (Derivations "The representations of the rotation algebra", "Half-integer spin and the group", "Why the sign appears: the topology of SO(3) and SU(2)") · the user's pre-course notes, §2.2 ("SU(2) and SO(3)"), §2.3 ("Spin")*

> [!remark] Remark: "One representation for each dimension" needs two qualifiers
> Lecture 7 says the rotation algebra has one representation for each dimension. The precise statement is [[§C3.1 Index Slots, Rotations and Spin in Field Theory#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], 1 (proved as [[§CB.10 SU(2) and SO(3)꞉ Spin j, Symmetric Powers and Clebsch–Gordan#^thm-cb-10-3|Theorem §CB.10.3]], 2), and it needs both qualifiers: without *irreducible* it is false (two copies of spin $0$ form a two-dimensional representation, $J^i = 0$ on $\mathbb C^2$), and without *up to equivalence* it is false (every change of basis produces another one).

> [!example] Example §C3.1.1: The Four-Vector under Rotations Is 0 ⊕ 1
> Restrict the vector representation $(\mathcal J^{\alpha\beta})^\mu{}_\nu = i(g^{\alpha\mu}\delta^\beta{}_\nu - g^{\beta\mu}\delta^\alpha{}_\nu)$ ([[§C1a.7 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-7-1|Def. §C1a.7.1]]) to rotations, $J_k = \frac12\varepsilon_{kij}\mathcal J^{ij}$ ([[§C1a.7 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-7-2|Def. §C1a.7.2]]), and decompose it into irreducible representations of $\mathfrak{so}(3)$.
>
> *The time component.* $(\mathcal J^{ij})^0{}_\nu = i(g^{i0}\delta^j{}_\nu - g^{j0}\delta^i{}_\nu) = 0$ and $(\mathcal J^{ij})^\mu{}_0 = i(g^{i\mu}\delta^j{}_0 - g^{j\mu}\delta^i{}_0) = 0$, since $g^{i0} = 0$ and $\delta^j{}_0 = 0$: the line of $e_0$ is invariant, with $J_k = 0$ on it. Spin $0$.
>
> *The spatial components.* With $g^{il} = -\delta^{il}$, $(\mathcal J^{ij})^l{}_m = -i(\delta^{il}\delta^{jm} - \delta^{jl}\delta^{im})$, so
>
> $$
> (J_k)^l{}_m = \tfrac12\varepsilon_{kij}(-i)(\delta^{il}\delta^{jm} - \delta^{jl}\delta^{im}) = -\tfrac i2(\varepsilon_{klm} - \varepsilon_{kml}) = -i\varepsilon_{klm} ,
> $$
>
> the defining representation of the rotation algebra, the same algebra as spin $\frac12$'s $\frac12\boldsymbol\sigma$ ([[§C3.1 Index Slots, Rotations and Spin in Field Theory#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]]). Its Casimir is $\mathbf J^2 = 2\,\mathbb 1 = 1(1+1)\mathbb 1$ and $J_3$ has eigenvalues $1, 0, -1$ on $\mp(e_1 \pm ie_2)/\sqrt2$, $e_3$ ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^ex-c5-1-2|QM Example §C5.1.2]]): highest weight $1$, so this block is $D^{(1)}$ ([[§C3.1 Index Slots, Rotations and Spin in Field Theory#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]]). Spin $1$.
>
> Hence, under rotations, a four-vector is $D^{(0)}\oplus D^{(1)}$, the $1 + 3$ split of $(V^0, \mathbf V)$. The boost generators $K_i = \mathcal J^{0i}$ map $e_0$ into the spatial block and back, so neither subspace is invariant under the Lorentz group: the four-vector is irreducible under $SO^+(1,3)$ but reducible under its rotation subgroup. This is the rotation content $\frac12\otimes\frac12 = 0\oplus1$ of the vector representation $(\frac12, \frac12)$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-9|Theorem §C3.2.9]], [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-5|Theorem §C3.2.5]]; for two spins $\frac12$: [[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^rem-c7-1-2|QM Remark: Reducible and irreducible, checked for two spins one-half]]), and the reason a massive vector field carries spin $1$ after its time component is removed ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]], [[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-8|Theorem §C4.5.8]]).
>

## States are rays

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.3: States Allow a Sign: Projective Rotations Are Representations of SU(2)
> States are rays: $|\psi\rangle$ and $e^{i\alpha}|\psi\rangle$ are the same state, so rotations need act on state vectors only up to phases, $U(R_2)U(R_1) = e^{i\varphi(R_2, R_1)}U(R_2R_1)$ (a projective representation, [[§CB.10 SU(2) and SO(3)꞉ Spin j, Symmetric Powers and Clebsch–Gordan#^def-cb-10-8|Def. §CB.10.8]]). For every continuous finite-dimensional projective representation of $SO(3)$:
> 1. there is a genuine representation $\Sigma$ of $SU(2)$ with $U(R(V)) = c(V)\,\Sigma(V)$, $|c(V)| = 1$, for all $V \in SU(2)$;
> 2. if it is irreducible, $\Sigma$ is some $D^{(j)}$ ([[§C3.1 Index Slots, Rotations and Spin in Field Theory#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]]), and the phases can be chosen so that
>
> $$
> U(R_2)\,U(R_1) = \pm\,U(R_2R_1) ,
> $$
>
> always $+$ for integer $j$; for half-integer $j$, $-$ exactly when the loop $\mathbb 1 \to R_1 \to R_2R_1 \to \mathbb 1$ along the paths that define the chosen operators is not contractible. No other phase occurs.
>
> *How to read it:* quantum mechanics allows half-integer spin, at the price of the sign of [[§C3.1 Index Slots, Rotations and Spin in Field Theory#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]] and of nothing else; the states of a particle at rest are therefore classified by the $D^{(j)}$ of $SU(2)$ ([[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-6-9|Theorem §C3.6.9]]). For the Lorentz group the same lift goes to $SL(2, \mathbb C)$ ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations and the Poincaré Algebra#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]]).
>
> *Proof:* [[§CB.19 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries#^thm-cb-19-11|Theorem §CB.19.11]].
>

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Rays, the Bloch sphere, and the word "projective"
> "Projective" in projective representation and in $\mathbb{RP}^3$ is the same word: physical states are rays, points of the projective space of the Hilbert space. For one spin $\frac12$ the rays form $\mathbb{CP}^1 \cong S^2$, the Bloch sphere, on which $SO(3)$ acts as an honest group of rotations; only the lift from rays to state vectors requires $SU(2)$ and produces the sign. The interferometer of [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-5|QM Theorem §C5.2.5]] measures that sign by making it relative between two parts of one state.

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: What spin means for a three-dimensional tensor
> A two-index tensor $T_{ij}$ is a bilinear form, $T(\mathbf u, \mathbf v) = T_{ij}u^iv^j$; rotating the form, $T'(\mathbf u, \mathbf v) = T(R^{-1}\mathbf u, R^{-1}\mathbf v)$, means $T' = RTR^{\mathsf T}$. Its three invariant pieces ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-2|QM Theorem §C7.2.2]]) respond as spin $0$, $1$, $2$:
>
> | piece of $T_{ij}$ | bilinear form | under rotation | spin |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | trace, $T = c\,\mathbb 1$ | $c\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v$ | unchanged | $0$ |
> | antisymmetric, $T_{ij} = \varepsilon_{ijk}w_k$ | $\mathbf w\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)$ | $\mathbf w$ turns as an arrow | $1$ |
> | symmetric traceless | a quadrupole (an ellipsoid) | the shape turns | $2$ |
>
> Spin counts how fast a component's phase turns: under the rotation by $\theta$ about $z$ a component of weight $m$ picks up $e^{-im\theta}$ (the convention in which $A^1 - iA^2$ has $m = +1$, [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^ex-c3-2-1|Example §C3.2.1]]). For the $xy$ block $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & -a\end{pmatrix}$ of a symmetric traceless $T$ and $R = \begin{pmatrix} c & -s \\ s & c\end{pmatrix}$, multiplying out $RTR^{\mathsf T}$ gives $a' = (c^2 - s^2)a - 2cs\,b = a\cos2\theta - b\sin2\theta$ and $b' = 2cs\,a + (c^2 - s^2)b = a\sin2\theta + b\cos2\theta$, so $a \pm ib \mapsto e^{\pm2i\theta}(a \pm ib)$: $a - ib$ has $m = +2$ and $a + ib$ has $m = -2$. The ellipse is back after a rotation by $\pi$.
>
> | object | returns to itself after | $m$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | scalar | any angle | $0$ |
> | vector (an arrow) | $2\pi$ | $\pm1$ |
> | quadrupole (an ellipse) | $\pi$ | $\pm2$ |
> | spinor | $4\pi$ | $\pm\frac12$ |
>
> Examples: the velocity gradient $\partial_iv_j$ splits into $\nabla\cdot\mathbf v$ (spin $0$, uniform expansion), the antisymmetric part $\leftrightarrow\nabla\times\mathbf v$ (spin $1$, rigid rotation) and the symmetric traceless part (spin $2$, shear); strain is bulk ($0$) plus shear ($2$); a moment of inertia is its average ($0$) plus its anisotropy ($2$). A symmetric traceless rank-$l$ tensor is spin $l$, with $2l + 1$ components like $Y_{lm}$: monopole (charge), dipole $\mathbf p$, quadrupole $Q_{ij}$ (the five $d$ orbitals $xy, yz, zx, x^2 - y^2, 3z^2 - r^2$; the tidal tensor $\partial_i\partial_j\Phi$), octupole ($f$ orbitals).
>
> *Source: the user's Lorentz-representation review (2026-10-14), §9 (Principles "Tensors as bilinear forms", "Examples"; there the exponent of $a \pm ib$ is printed as $e^{\mp2i\theta}$, here recomputed) · beyond the course*
