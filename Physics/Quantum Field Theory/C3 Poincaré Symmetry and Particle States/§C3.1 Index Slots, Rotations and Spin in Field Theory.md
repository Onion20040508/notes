How do rotations act on the indices of a field and on its states, and why does half-integer spin force $SU(2)$ instead of $SO(3)$? Quantum Mechanics already holds everything about rotations themselves: the algebra $[J_i, J_j] = i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}J_k$ as the multiplication table of $SO(3)$ near the identity ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-4|QM Theorem §C5.1.4]]), $SU(2)$ as the double cover ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-6|QM Theorem §C5.2.6]]), the multiplets and the rotation matrices ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices]]); the matrix groups and the generators of the vector representation are [[§CB.1 Matrix Lie Groups, the Exponential Map and Lie Algebras#^def-cb-1-1|Def. §CB.1.1]] and [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-1|Def. §C1a.6.1]]. The representation theory that makes these facts reusable — groups, Lie algebras and their representations, irreducibility and Casimirs, why compactness gives unitarity, the highest-weight classification proved without an inner product, the passage from the algebra back to $SU(2)$ and $SO(3)$ and the topology behind it, and projective representations — is mathematics: it lives in [[§CB.1 Matrix Lie Groups, the Exponential Map and Lie Algebras|§CB.1]]–[[§CB.6 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs|§CB.6]], [[§CB.9 SU(2) and SO(3)꞉ Spin j, Symmetric Powers and Clebsch–Gordan|§CB.9]] and [[§CB.18 Projective Representations, Wigner's Theorem and Antiunitary Symmetries|§CB.18]], and the statements this section uses are shown where they are needed. This section keeps what belongs to field theory: what each index of a field labels, the course's symbols and sign conventions for the generators, the spin content of a four-vector, and why states are rays. Every item is used again, with the rotation algebra replaced by the Lorentz algebra, in [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields|§C3.2]] and on quantum states in [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations and the Poincaré Algebra|§C3.4]].

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): $\hbar = 1$; spatial indices $i, j, k \in \{1, 2, 3\}$, whose position (up or down) carries no meaning in Euclidean space; $\varepsilon^{123} = +1$; generators are Hermitian and a rotation by $\theta$ about $\hat n$ is $e^{-i\theta\,\hat n\cdot\mathbf J}$, as in [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-2|Def. §C1a.6.2]].

## What each index labels

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.1: Index Slots
> An index names a **slot**, a vector space on which a Lorentz transformation acts by that space's own rule. Three kinds occur:
> 1. **spacetime indices** $\mu, \nu, \alpha, \beta \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$: components on $V = \mathbb R^{1,3}$, transformed by $\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$, raised and lowered with $g$;
> 2. **generator labels**: the antisymmetric pair in $J^{\mu\nu}$, $S^{\mu\nu}$, $\omega_{\mu\nu}$, saying which of the six basis elements of the Lie algebra is meant; it transforms as an antisymmetric tensor on $V$, so the rules of item 1 apply to it;
> 3. **matrix indices**: row and column of a representation matrix, belonging to the carrier space of the representation ([[§CB.2 Homomorphisms, Representations, the Identity Component and Coverings#^def-cb-2-4|Def. §CB.2.4]]) and obeying its rules: spacetime indices in the vector representation, $m = -j, \dots, j$ for spin $j$, spinor indices for Dirac spinors ([[§C5a.5 Chirality and Weyl Spinors#^rem-c5a-5-4|§C5a.5, Remark: What each Dirac index labels]]).
>
> An index obeys the rules of special relativity exactly when it is a spacetime slot.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.1 (Principle "Every index is a slot, and each slot has its own transformation rule") · PS §3.1, p. 39 (the remark after eq. (3.18))*

^def-c3-1-1

| symbol | what it is | its indices |
|---|---|---|
| $x^\mu$, $p^\mu$, $v^\mu$ | a vector in $V$ | $\mu$: spacetime |
| $g_{\mu\nu}$, $\delta^\mu{}_\nu$, $\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ | invariant tensors on $V$ | all spacetime |
| $\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$ | a group element, as a linear map $V \to V$ | row and column: spacetime |
| $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ | six numbers: which element of the algebra | antisymmetric pair: spacetime |
| $J^{\mu\nu}$ (abstract) | a basis element of the Lie algebra | pair: generator label |
| $(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})^\alpha{}_\beta$ | that generator as a $4\times4$ matrix on $V$ | $\mu\nu$: label; $\alpha, \beta$: matrix $=$ spacetime |
| $L^{\mu\nu} = i(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu)$ | a generator acting on functions of $x$ | $\mu\nu$: label; no matrix indices |
| $(\sigma^i)_{ab}$ | a 3-vector whose components are $2\times2$ matrices | $i$: spatial; $a, b$: spin $\tfrac12$ |
| $\hat J^{\mu\nu}$ on the Hilbert space | the Noether charges, acting on states | $\mu\nu$: label; acts on states, not on indices |

*The practical test.* Ask what happens to an index under a Lorentz transformation: if it picks up $\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$, it is a spacetime index and $g$ may be used freely; if it labels the six generators, it is an antisymmetric pair of spacetime indices; if it picks up another matrix, it belongs to another carrier space and follows that space's rules.

> [!caution] Caution: Four spacetime indices in two roles
> In the vector representation all four indices of $(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})^\alpha{}_\beta$ are spacetime indices, but $\mu\nu$ says *which* generator and $\alpha\beta$ *which matrix entry*; they look alike only because here the carrier space is spacetime itself. PS's $(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_{\alpha\beta}$ (eq. The Pauli matrices carry one slot of each other kind: $U^\dagger\sigma^iU = R^i{}_j\sigma^j$ says that transforming the two spinor slots by $U \in SU(2)$ is the same as transforming the vector slot by $R = R(U)$ ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-6|QM Theorem §C5.2.6]]).
>

> [!remark] Remark: Why rotations felt simpler
> In three dimensions an antisymmetric pair can be traded for one index, because there are three pairs and three axes: $J^{ij} = \varepsilon^{ijk}J^k$ and $J^k = \frac12\varepsilon^{kij}J^{ij}$ (insert the second into the first and use $\varepsilon^{ijk}\varepsilon^{klm} = \delta^{il}\delta^{jm} - \delta^{im}\delta^{jl}$, [[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-3|Theorem §C1a.5.3]]: $\varepsilon^{ijk}\cdot\frac12\varepsilon^{klm}J^{lm} = \frac12(J^{ij} - J^{ji}) = J^{ij}$). $J^3$ generates rotations *about* the $z$ axis, $J^{12}$ rotations *in* the $(12)$ plane. In four dimensions there are six generators and four directions, and the pair $\mu\nu$ cannot be traded for anything: the two-index form is the one that generalizes ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-2|Def. §C1a.6.2]] splits it into $\mathbf J$ and $\mathbf K$ only by singling out time).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.1 (paragraph "Why rotations felt simpler"), §7.2, eq. (Jtwoways) · PHY 513 Lecture 7, Part A ("Classical angular momentum") · PS §3.1, p. 39*

## The rotation algebra, from the field-theory side

The algebra $[J^i, J^j] = i\varepsilon^{ijk}J^k$ has its home in Quantum Mechanics: for the rotation generators of any system it is the multiplication table of $SO(3)$ near the identity ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-4|QM Theorem §C5.1.4]]), and for the orbital $\hat L^i = \varepsilon^{ijk}\hat x^j\hat p^k$ it follows from $[\hat x^i, \hat p^j] = i\delta^{ij}$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-1|QM Theorem §B5.2.1]]); the latter is the computation of Lecture 7, of PS eq. (3.15) and of the user's 513 notes (Derivation "The rotation algebra"). Nothing is added to it here. What changes is the object of study: not a particular set of operators obeying the algebra, but the algebra itself and all the ways it can be realized.

> [!caution] Caution: The sign of the orbital generator
> With the Euclidean derivative $\partial_j = \partial/\partial x^j$ and $p^j = -i\partial_j$, $J^{ij} = x^ip^j - x^jp^i = -i(x^i\partial_j - x^j\partial_i)$, as on the Lecture 7 slide and in PS (3.15); a handwritten version recorded in the user's notes has $+i$, which is the wrong sign. The four-dimensional orbital generator $L^{\mu\nu} = i(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu)$ ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]) has a plus sign and *upper* derivatives; on spatial indices the two agree because $\partial^j = -\partial_j$ in the signature $(+,-,-,-)$: $i(x^i\partial^j - x^j\partial^i) = -i(x^i\partial_j - x^j\partial_i)$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.2 (Caution "A sign to watch") · PHY 513 Lecture 7 ("Quantum angular momentum")*

> [!caution] Caution: The sign in the exponent across sources
> Larsen, PS (3.13), the user's notes and Quantum Mechanics write a rotation as $e^{-i\theta\hat n\cdot\mathbf J}$. Yu writes $U(\mathbb 1 + \omega) = \mathbb 1 - \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu} = \mathbb 1 + i\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf J + i\boldsymbol\xi\cdot\mathbf K$ with $\theta_i \equiv -\frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\omega_{jk}$ (Yu (3.42), (3.44)) and $U[R_z(\theta^3)] = \exp(i\theta^3J^3)$ (Yu (3.46)): the same operator $e^{-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu}}$, with Yu's angle of opposite sign to the $\theta$ of [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-2|Def. §C1a.6.2]] ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^cau-c1a-6-1|§C1a.6, Caution: Which ω is a rotation by θ]]). Georgi writes $D = 1 + i\alpha^aX_a$ (his (2.4)). Reversing the parameter leaves $[T_a, T_b] = if_{ab}{}^cT_c$ unchanged; reversing the generator instead ($T \to -T$) flips the sign of every $f_{ab}{}^c$.

^cau-c3-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Two uses of one theory: field indices and quantum states
> Field theory uses representations of the Lorentz group twice. Finite-dimensional ones act on the *indices of fields* ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]]; [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields|§C3.2]]–[[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group|§C3.3]]); they need not be unitary, and for the Lorentz group cannot be. Unitary (projective) ones act on the *Hilbert space of states*, where probabilities must be preserved; for the Lorentz group they are infinite-dimensional ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations and the Poincaré Algebra|§C3.4]], [[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group|§C3.6★]]). For rotations the two coincide, because $SO(3)$ is compact (Theorem §CB.6.16): this is why Quantum Mechanics never had to keep them apart, and why the rotation group is the right place to learn the vocabulary.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 (Principle "Hermitian generators do not make boosts unitary", §7.2)*

## Spin j and the four-vector

> [!remark] Remark: "One representation for each dimension" needs two qualifiers
> Lecture 7 says the rotation algebra has one representation for each dimension. The precise statement is [[§CB.9 SU(2) and SO(3)꞉ Spin j, Symmetric Powers and Clebsch–Gordan#^thm-cb-9-3|Theorem §CB.9.3]], 2, and it needs both qualifiers: without *irreducible* it is false (two copies of spin $0$ form a two-dimensional representation, $J^i = 0$ on $\mathbb C^2$), and without *up to equivalence* it is false (every change of basis produces another one).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.2 (Derivation "The representations of the rotation algebra", "The classification")*

^rem-c3-1-4

> [!example] Example §C3.1.1: The Four-Vector under Rotations Is 0 ⊕ 1
> Restrict the vector representation $(\mathcal J^{\alpha\beta})^\mu{}_\nu = i(g^{\alpha\mu}\delta^\beta{}_\nu - g^{\beta\mu}\delta^\alpha{}_\nu)$ ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-1|Def. §C1a.6.1]]) to rotations, $J_k = \frac12\varepsilon_{kij}\mathcal J^{ij}$ ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-2|Def. §C1a.6.2]]), and decompose it into irreducible representations of $\mathfrak{so}(3)$.
>
> *The time component.* $(\mathcal J^{ij})^0{}_\nu = i(g^{i0}\delta^j{}_\nu - g^{j0}\delta^i{}_\nu) = 0$ and $(\mathcal J^{ij})^\mu{}_0 = i(g^{i\mu}\delta^j{}_0 - g^{j\mu}\delta^i{}_0) = 0$, since $g^{i0} = 0$ and $\delta^j{}_0 = 0$: the line of $e_0$ is invariant, with $J_k = 0$ on it. Spin $0$.
>
> *The spatial components.* With $g^{il} = -\delta^{il}$, $(\mathcal J^{ij})^l{}_m = -i(\delta^{il}\delta^{jm} - \delta^{jl}\delta^{im})$, so
>
> $$
> (J_k)^l{}_m = \tfrac12\varepsilon_{kij}(-i)(\delta^{il}\delta^{jm} - \delta^{jl}\delta^{im}) = -\tfrac i2(\varepsilon_{klm} - \varepsilon_{kml}) = -i\varepsilon_{klm} ,
> $$
>
> the defining representation of Theorem §CB.1.21. Its Casimir is $\mathbf J^2 = 2\,\mathbb 1 = 1(1+1)\mathbb 1$ and $J_3$ has eigenvalues $1, 0, -1$ on $\mp(e_1 \pm ie_2)/\sqrt2$, $e_3$ ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^ex-c5-1-2|QM Example §C5.1.2]]): highest weight $1$, so this block is $D^{(1)}$. Spin $1$.
>
> Hence, under rotations, a four-vector is $D^{(0)}\oplus D^{(1)}$, the $1 + 3$ split of $(V^0, \mathbf V)$. The boost generators $K_i = \mathcal J^{0i}$ map $e_0$ into the spatial block and back, so neither subspace is invariant under the Lorentz group: the four-vector is irreducible under $SO^+(1,3)$ but reducible under its rotation subgroup. This is the rotation content $\frac12\otimes\frac12 = 0\oplus1$ of the vector representation $(\frac12, \frac12)$ ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]], [[§CB.16 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)#^thm-cb-16-8|Theorem §CB.16.8]]; for two spins $\frac12$: [[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^rem-c7-1-2|QM Remark: Reducible and irreducible, checked for two spins one-half]]), and the reason a massive vector field carries spin $1$ after its time component is removed ([[§C4.1 The Vector Field and Its Lorentz Transformation#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]], [[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-8|Theorem §C4.5.8]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 1 §1.6, Ch. 7 §7.3 (vector-representation generators); worked here*

## States are rays

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Rays, the Bloch sphere, and the word "projective"
> "Projective" in projective representation and in $\mathbb{RP}^3$ is the same word: physical states are rays, points of the projective space of the Hilbert space. For one spin $\frac12$ the rays form $\mathbb{CP}^1 \cong S^2$, the Bloch sphere, on which $SO(3)$ acts as an honest group of rotations; only the lift from rays to state vectors requires $SU(2)$ and produces the sign. The interferometer of [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-4|QM Theorem §C5.2.4]] measures that sign by making it relative between two parts of one state.
