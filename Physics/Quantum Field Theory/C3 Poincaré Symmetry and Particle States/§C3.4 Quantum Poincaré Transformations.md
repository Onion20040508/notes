How does a Lorentz transformation or a translation act on the *states* of a quantum field theory, and how is that action tied to the field operators and to the conserved charges? Chapter C1 let the Poincaré group act on coordinates ([[§C1.4 The Lorentz Group|§C1.4]]), on classical field configurations ([[§C1.8 Scalar Field Theory꞉ Fields, Locality and Symmetry#^thm-c1-8-1|Theorem §C1.8.1]]) and, through Noether's theorem, produced ten conserved charges ([[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum|§C1.12]]); [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and Its Finite-Dimensional Representations|§C3.2]]–[[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group|§C3.3]] studied the finite-dimensional representations carried by field *indices*. This section puts the group on the Hilbert space, as unitary operators $U(\Lambda, a)$, in the way Quantum Mechanics put translations, rotations and time evolution there ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-4|QM Principle §C2.2.4]], [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|QM Principle §C5.1.2]], [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^pr-c3-1-1|QM Principle §C3.1.1]]). New here: the ten generators as one tensor-and-vector family, the Poincaré algebra, the representation on states being *infinite-dimensional and unitary* against the finite-dimensional non-unitary one on indices, the Heisenberg equation as translation invariance ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields|§C2b.1]]), an explicit unitary representation for the free scalar field ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization|§C2a.4]]), and the identification of the Noether charges with the generators.

*Conventions* ([[Larsen PHY 513]]): $\hbar = c = 1$, $g = \operatorname{diag}(+, -, -, -)$; Lorentz transformations are read actively and are proper orthochronous unless stated, $\Lambda = \exp(-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ with the matrices $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu} = iM^{\mu\nu}$ of [[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1-6-1|Def. §C1.6.1]]. Three different objects carry the letters $J^{\mu\nu}$ in the sources: here $J^{\mu\nu}$ is an operator on the Hilbert space, $\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}$ a $4\times4$ matrix (and $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ its image in a representation $D$, [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and Its Finite-Dimensional Representations#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]), and $\hat L^{\mu\nu} = i(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu)$ a differential operator ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]); Peskin & Schroeder write $J^{\mu\nu}$ for all three. A Poincaré transformation is the pair $(\Lambda, a)$ acting by $x \mapsto \Lambda x + a$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-4|REL Def. §B1.2.4]]).

## The Poincaré group on states

> [!principle] Principle §C3.4.1: Poincaré Invariance on the Hilbert Space
> A relativistic quantum theory has, for every proper orthochronous Poincaré transformation $(\Lambda, a)$, a unitary operator $U(\Lambda, a)$ on its Hilbert space, $|\Psi\rangle \mapsto U(\Lambda, a)|\Psi\rangle$, such that
> 1. $U(\mathbb 1, 0) = \mathbb 1$, and $U(\Lambda, a)|\Psi\rangle$ depends continuously on $(\Lambda, a)$;
> 2. the operators compose as the transformations do:
>
> $$
> U(\Lambda_2, a_2)\,U(\Lambda_1, a_1) = U(\Lambda_2\Lambda_1,\ \Lambda_2a_1 + a_2) ;
> $$
>
> 3. the vacuum is invariant, $U(\Lambda, a)|0\rangle = |0\rangle$.
>
> $U(\Lambda) \equiv U(\Lambda, 0)$ is a quantum Lorentz transformation and $U(\mathbb 1, a)$ a quantum translation.
>
> *Domain: every relativistic theory of the course. For fields of half-integer spin, item 2 holds for the covering group (each $\Lambda$ replaced by one of its two preimages in $SL(2, \mathbb C)$) and on $SO^+(1,3)$ only up to a sign ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-4-1|Remark: Why unitary, and why the covering group]]). Parity and time reversal are not included (QFT C9, planned).*
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6 (Definition "Quantum Poincaré transformations") · Yu §3.1, eqs. (3.2)–(3.7) · PS §2.3, p. 23 ("a Lorentz transformation Λ will be implemented as some unitary operator U(Λ)") · the user's pre-course notes, §2.1*

> [!remark] Remark: Why unitary, and why the covering group
> A symmetry must preserve every transition probability $|\langle\Phi|\Psi\rangle|^2$, so by Wigner's theorem it acts by a unitary or an antiunitary operator, determined up to a phase ([[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-1|QM Theorem §C8.1.1]]). Every element of the proper orthochronous group lies on a continuous path from the identity ([[§C1.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]], 2), so it is a product of elements near the identity, each the square of another element; the square of an antiunitary operator is unitary, so, by the argument of item 2 of Wigner's theorem, it is represented unitarily; antiunitary operators are left for time reversal. Because states are rays, item 2 of Principle §C3.4.1 need only hold up to a phase, a projective representation ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^def-c3-1-7|Def. §C3.1.7]]). For the rotation group the phase that survives every redefinition is a sign, removed by passing to $SU(2)$ ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^thm-c3-1-9|Theorem §C3.1.9]]); the same holds for $SO^+(1,3)$ with its double cover $SL(2, \mathbb C)$ ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^rem-c3-1-8|§C3.1, Remark: The same pattern for the Lorentz group]]), and for the Poincaré group with the cover of its Lorentz part (Bargmann's theorem, stated: [[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^rem-c3-1-9|§C3.1, ★ Remark: Infinite dimensions, Bargmann, and a phase topology does not explain]]). Working with the cover from the start, as Yu and the user's notes do, is why item 2 can be written without a phase.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6 (the parenthesis in the Definition) · Yu §3.3.1, eqs. (3.110)–(3.117), and the closing paragraph of §3.3.1 · the user's pre-course notes, §2.1 (Wigner's theorem, stated)*

## Generators

> [!definition] Definition §C3.4.1: Generators of Quantum Poincaré Transformations
> The ten **generators** $J^{\mu\nu} = -J^{\nu\mu}$ and $P^\mu$ are the operators in the expansion near the identity,
>
> $$
> U(\mathbb 1 + \omega, \varepsilon) = \mathbb 1 - \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu} + i\,\varepsilon_\mu P^\mu + O(\omega^2, \varepsilon^2, \omega\varepsilon), \qquad J^{\mu\nu} = i\,\frac{\partial U}{\partial\omega_{\mu\nu}}\Big|_0, \quad P^\mu = -i\,\frac{\partial U}{\partial\varepsilon_\mu}\Big|_0 ,
> $$
>
> with $\omega_{\mu\nu} = -\omega_{\nu\mu}$ ([[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1-6-1|Theorem §C1.6.1]]). Their names: $H \equiv P^0$, momentum $\mathbf P = (P^1, P^2, P^3)$, angular momentum $J^i \equiv \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}J^{jk}$, boost generators $K^i \equiv J^{0i}$, exactly as for the matrices of [[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1-6-2|Def. §C1.6.2]].
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6, eq. (poincaregen) and Derivation "Expanding U(Λ, a) near the identity" · Yu §3.1, eqs. (3.10)–(3.12); §3.2, eqs. (3.37)–(3.38) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.1*

> [!caution] Caution: Signs and names across the sources
> - **The signs** $-\frac i2\omega J$ and $+i\varepsilon P$ are those of the lecture, PS and Yu (3.10). Weinberg (2.4.3) writes $\mathbb 1 + \frac i2\omega_{\rho\sigma}J^{\rho\sigma} - i\varepsilon_\rho P^\rho$; formulas from the two families cannot be mixed. The $\frac12$ removes the double count of each independent pair ([[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^rem-c1-6-1|§C1.6, Remark: The factor ½ and why ω may be taken antisymmetric]]).
> - **Angles.** Yu reads transformations passively and names the angle $\theta_i = -\frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\omega_{jk}$, rapidity $\xi_i = -\omega_{0i}$, so his $U[R_z(\theta)] = e^{+i\theta J^3}$ (3.46); here, actively, $U[R_z(\theta)] = e^{-i\theta J^3}$. Every formula written with $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ is the same in both ([[Larsen PHY 513]]).
> - **The boost.** The *generator* $K^i = J^{0i}$ is minus the Noether *boost charge* $K^i_{\rm N} \equiv J^{i0}$ of [[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum#^def-c1-12-3|Def. §C1.12.3]]; [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-13|Theorem §C3.4.13]] proves the dictionary.
> - **Which side the inverse is on.** This note writes $U^{-1}\Phi(x)U = \Phi(\Lambda^{-1}x)$; PS and the user's pre-course notes also write $U\Phi(x)U^{-1} = \Phi(\Lambda x)$. They are one law, with $\Lambda \to \Lambda^{-1}$.
>

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.2: The Generators Are Hermitian
> All ten generators are Hermitian: $(J^{\mu\nu})^\dagger = J^{\mu\nu}$ and $(P^\mu)^\dagger = P^\mu$, the boost generators $K^i$ included. Hence $U(\mathbb 1 + \omega, \varepsilon)^{-1} = \mathbb 1 + \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu} - i\varepsilon_\mu P^\mu$ to first order.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6 (Derivation "Unitarity makes every generator Hermitian") · Yu §3.1, eqs. (3.13)–(3.15)*

^thm-c3-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the adjoint of the expansion). The parameters $\omega_{\mu\nu}$, $\varepsilon_\mu$ are real, so taking the adjoint of [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]] term by term,
>
> $$
> U^\dagger = \mathbb 1 + \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}(J^{\mu\nu})^\dagger - i\,\varepsilon_\mu(P^\mu)^\dagger + O(2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (multiply). Expand $U^\dagger U$ into its nine products; the four products of two first-order terms are second order and are dropped:
>
> $$
> U^\dagger U = \mathbb 1 + \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl[(J^{\mu\nu})^\dagger - J^{\mu\nu}\bigr] - i\,\varepsilon_\mu\bigl[(P^\mu)^\dagger - P^\mu\bigr] + O(2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (unitarity). [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-4-1|Principle §C3.4.1]] gives $U^\dagger U = \mathbb 1$ for every $(\omega, \varepsilon)$, so each first-order coefficient vanishes. The ten parameters $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ ($\mu < \nu$) and $\varepsilon_\mu$ are independent, and $(J^{\mu\nu})^\dagger - J^{\mu\nu}$ is antisymmetric in $\mu\nu$, as is $\omega_{\mu\nu}$, so the coefficient of each independent $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ is $i[(J^{\mu\nu})^\dagger - J^{\mu\nu}]$ and must vanish. Hence $(J^{\mu\nu})^\dagger = J^{\mu\nu}$, $(P^\mu)^\dagger = P^\mu$.
>
> **Step 4** (the inverse). $U^{-1} = U^\dagger$; insert Step 3 into Step 1.
>
> ⚑ By-product: the boost generators $K^i = J^{0i}$ are Hermitian on the Hilbert space, while in every finite-dimensional representation their matrices are not ([[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^rem-c1-6-2|§C1.6, Remark: Hermitian generators do not make boosts unitary]]) → [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-4-2|Remark: Two kinds of representation]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Only unitarity was used; the factor $i$ in the definition is what turns "unitary" into "Hermitian", so the generators are candidate observables: energy, momentum, angular momentum, boost.
> - *Sense:* $P^\mu$ and $J^{\mu\nu}$ are unbounded (the spectrum of $H$ is unbounded above), so "to first order" means on vectors where the derivatives of $U$ exist, a dense set (Stone's theorem, as for time evolution, [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^rem-c3-1-1|QM §C3.1, Remark: Layer change from level B]]); on wave packets of the free field everything is explicit (Theorem §C3.4.11).
> - Used next: the inverse expansion in every commutator below.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.3: Finite Transformations as Exponentials
> 1. $U(\Lambda, a)^{-1} = U(\Lambda^{-1}, -\Lambda^{-1}a)$ and $U(\Lambda)^{-1} = U(\Lambda^{-1})$.
> 2. $U(\Lambda, a) = U(\mathbb 1, a)\,U(\Lambda)$.
> 3. Translations: $U(\mathbb 1, a) = e^{iP\cdot a} = e^{iHa^0 - i\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf a}$.
> 4. Lorentz transformations: for $\Lambda = \exp(-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$, $\ U(\Lambda) = \exp(-\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu})$; a rotation by $\theta$ about $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is $e^{-i\theta\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf J}$, a boost of rapidity $\eta$ along $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is $e^{-i\eta\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf K}$.
>
> The order in 2 matters, since $P$ and $J$ do not commute ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-5|Theorem §C3.4.5]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6 (Step 4 of Derivation "Expanding U(Λ, a)") · Yu §3.1, eqs. (3.8)–(3.9); §3.2, eqs. (3.45)–(3.46), (3.74)–(3.79)*

^thm-c3-4-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (inverse in the group). By the composition law of [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-4-1|Principle §C3.4.1]] with $(\Lambda_2, a_2) = (\Lambda^{-1}, -\Lambda^{-1}a)$, $(\Lambda_1, a_1) = (\Lambda, a)$: $U(\Lambda^{-1}, -\Lambda^{-1}a)\,U(\Lambda, a) = U(\Lambda^{-1}\Lambda,\ \Lambda^{-1}a - \Lambda^{-1}a) = U(\mathbb 1, 0) = \mathbb 1$. The product in the other order gives $U(\Lambda\Lambda^{-1}, \Lambda(-\Lambda^{-1}a) + a) = \mathbb 1$ as well. This is part 1; $a = 0$ gives the second statement.
>
> **Step 2** (factorize). The composition law with $(\Lambda_2, a_2) = (\mathbb 1, a)$, $(\Lambda_1, a_1) = (\Lambda, 0)$ gives $U(\mathbb 1, a)U(\Lambda, 0) = U(\Lambda, \mathbb 1\cdot0 + a) = U(\Lambda, a)$: part 2. In the opposite order, $U(\Lambda, 0)U(\mathbb 1, a) = U(\Lambda, \Lambda a)$, a different element.
>
> **Step 3** (translations form a one-parameter group in each direction). For fixed $a$, $s \mapsto V(s) \equiv U(\mathbb 1, sa)$ satisfies $V(s)V(t) = U(\mathbb 1, (s + t)a) = V(s + t)$ and $V(0) = \mathbb 1$. Hence $V(s + ds) = V(ds)V(s)$, and with Def. §C3.4.1 at $\varepsilon = a\,ds$, $V(ds) = \mathbb 1 + i\,ds\,a_\mu P^\mu$:
>
> $$
> \frac{dV}{ds} = i\,(a_\mu P^\mu)\,V(s), \qquad V(0) = \mathbb 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad V(s) = e^{is\,a\cdot P} ,
> $$
>
> the exponential defined by its spectral resolution (for a self-adjoint generator this is Stone's theorem; the power series converges on eigenvectors). At $s = 1$, $U(\mathbb 1, a) = e^{iP\cdot a}$, with $P\cdot a = P^0a^0 - \mathbf P\cdot\mathbf a$. The four $P^\mu$ commute ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-5|Theorem §C3.4.5]]), so $e^{iP\cdot a} = e^{iHa^0}e^{-i\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf a}$ splits without correction terms.
>
> **Step 4** (Lorentz transformations). For fixed antisymmetric $\omega$, $s \mapsto \Lambda(s) = \exp(-\frac{is}2\omega_{\mu\nu}\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}) = e^{s\omega}$ is a one-parameter subgroup of $SO^+(1,3)$ ([[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1-6-4|Theorem §C1.6.4]]), so $W(s) \equiv U(\Lambda(s))$ satisfies $W(s)W(t) = W(s + t)$. As in Step 3, $W(ds) = \mathbb 1 - \frac i2\,ds\,\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu}$ gives $W(s) = \exp(-\frac{is}2\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu})$; set $s = 1$. With $\theta_i = \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\omega_{jk}$ and $\eta_i = \omega_{0i}$ the exponent is $-i\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf J - i\boldsymbol\eta\cdot\mathbf K$, by the same regrouping as in [[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1-6-2|Def. §C1.6.2]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The finite operators are fixed by the generators and the group law; the only input beyond Def. §C3.4.1 is that each one-parameter subgroup is represented by a one-parameter group of unitaries.
> - $U(\Lambda, a)$ is "first Lorentz-transform, then translate"; reversing the order translates by $\Lambda a$.
> - Used next: $U(\mathbb 1, a) = e^{iP\cdot a}$ moves fields ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-9|Theorem §C3.4.9]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Two kinds of representation
> Quantum field theory uses two representations of the Lorentz group at once, and they are of opposite kinds.
>
> | | on field indices ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and Its Finite-Dimensional Representations\|§C3.2]]–[[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group\|§C3.3]]) | on states (this section) |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | acts on | the components $\Phi_a$ at one point, by $D(\Lambda)$ | the Hilbert space, by $U(\Lambda, a)$ |
> | dimension | finite: $(2j_+ + 1)(2j_- + 1)$ | infinite |
> | unitary? | no, unless trivial ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and Its Finite-Dimensional Representations#^thm-c3-2-12\|Theorem §C3.2.12]]) | yes ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-4-1\|Principle §C3.4.1]]) |
> | boost generators | not Hermitian | Hermitian ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-2\|Theorem §C3.4.2]]) |
> | labels | $(j_+, j_-)$ | mass and spin, or helicity ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group\|§C3.5★]], [[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity\|§C3.6★]]) |
> | for the scalar | the number $1$, i.e. $(0, 0)$ | $L^2$ of the mass hyperboloid ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-11\|Theorem §C3.4.11]]) |
>
> The two statements are one theorem read twice: the noncompact simple group $SO^+(1,3)$ has no nontrivial finite-dimensional unitary representation ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and Its Finite-Dimensional Representations#^thm-c3-2-12|Theorem §C3.2.12]]), so the representation on states, which must be unitary, must be infinite-dimensional; and the one on indices, which is finite-dimensional, cannot be unitary. Noncompactness alone is not the reason: the translations and the massless little group $ISO(2)$ are noncompact and have one-dimensional unitary representations, $e^{ip\cdot a}$ and the helicity phases of [[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-6-5|Theorem §C3.6.5]]; it is the boosts, whose commutator is a rotation, that forbid it. Both representations satisfy the same Lorentz algebra ([[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1-6-5|Theorem §C1.6.5]]), which is derived from the group law alone and so holds for $U$ as well ([[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^der-c1-6-5|Derivation §C1.6.5]]). They meet in [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-10|Theorem §C3.4.10]], where $J^{\mu\nu}$ acts on the operator nature of a field, $\hat L^{\mu\nu}$ on its argument and $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ on its index; the vocabulary for both uses is [[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^rem-c3-1-5|§C3.1, Remark: Two uses of one theory: field indices and quantum states]]. Quantum Mechanics never had to separate them: rotations are compact, and there one finite-dimensional unitary representation serves both purposes (spin matrices on a spinor's components *are* the action on its states).
>

## How the generators transform: the Poincaré algebra

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.4: How the Generators Transform
> For every $(\Lambda, a)$,
>
> $$
> U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\,P^\rho\,U(\Lambda, a) = \Lambda^\rho{}_\mu P^\mu, \qquad U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\,J^{\rho\sigma}\,U(\Lambda, a) = \Lambda^\rho{}_\mu\Lambda^\sigma{}_\nu J^{\mu\nu} - a^\sigma\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu P^\mu + a^\rho\Lambda^\sigma{}_\mu P^\mu .
> $$
>
> $P^\mu$ is a four-vector operator; $J^{\mu\nu}$ is a tensor under Lorentz transformations and shifts by $a^\rho P'^\sigma - a^\sigma P'^\rho$ (with $P' = \Lambda P$) under translations. Consequently $\langle\Psi'|P^\rho|\Psi'\rangle = \Lambda^\rho{}_\mu\langle\Psi|P^\mu|\Psi\rangle$ for $|\Psi'\rangle = U(\Lambda)|\Psi\rangle$, and likewise for $J$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6, eq. (poincaretensor) · Yu §3.1, eqs. (3.16)–(3.29) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.1, eqs. (J-transform), (P-transform)*

^thm-c3-4-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (conjugate in the group). With the inverse of [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]], 1, and the composition law twice:
>
> $$
> (\mathbb 1 + \omega, \varepsilon)(\Lambda, a) = \bigl((\mathbb 1 + \omega)\Lambda,\ (\mathbb 1 + \omega)a + \varepsilon\bigr),
> $$
>
> $$
> (\Lambda^{-1}, -\Lambda^{-1}a)\bigl((\mathbb 1 + \omega)\Lambda,\ (\mathbb 1 + \omega)a + \varepsilon\bigr) = \bigl(\Lambda^{-1}(\mathbb 1 + \omega)\Lambda,\ \Lambda^{-1}[(\mathbb 1 + \omega)a + \varepsilon] - \Lambda^{-1}a\bigr) = \bigl(\mathbb 1 + \Lambda^{-1}\omega\Lambda,\ \Lambda^{-1}(\omega a + \varepsilon)\bigr),
> $$
>
> the $\Lambda^{-1}a$ terms cancelling. Applying $U$ and the composition law of [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-4-1|Principle §C3.4.1]],
>
> $$
> U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\,U(\mathbb 1 + \omega, \varepsilon)\,U(\Lambda, a) = U\bigl(\mathbb 1 + \Lambda^{-1}\omega\Lambda,\ \Lambda^{-1}(\omega a + \varepsilon)\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the conjugated parameter is again antisymmetric). From $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}g\Lambda = g$, $(\Lambda^{-1})^\alpha{}_\beta = g^{\alpha\tau}\Lambda^\lambda{}_\tau g_{\lambda\beta}$. So, lowering the first index with $g_{\mu\alpha}$ and using $g_{\mu\alpha}g^{\alpha\tau} = \delta_\mu{}^\tau$, $g_{\lambda\beta}\omega^\beta{}_\gamma = \omega_{\lambda\gamma}$:
>
> $$
> (\Lambda^{-1}\omega\Lambda)_{\mu\nu} = g_{\mu\alpha}(\Lambda^{-1})^\alpha{}_\beta\,\omega^\beta{}_\gamma\,\Lambda^\gamma{}_\nu = \Lambda^\lambda{}_\mu\,\omega_{\lambda\gamma}\,\Lambda^\gamma{}_\nu ,
> $$
>
> antisymmetric in $\mu\nu$ because $\omega_{\lambda\gamma}$ is. So the right side of Step 1 is an infinitesimal Poincaré transformation and Def. §C3.4.1 applies to it.
>
> **Step 3** (expand the left side). With [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]] inside and the fixed $U(\Lambda, a)$ outside,
>
> $$
> U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\Bigl(\mathbb 1 - \frac i2\,\omega_{\rho\sigma}J^{\rho\sigma} + i\,\varepsilon_\rho P^\rho\Bigr)U(\Lambda, a) = \mathbb 1 - \frac i2\,\omega_{\rho\sigma}\,U^{-1}J^{\rho\sigma}U + i\,\varepsilon_\rho\,U^{-1}P^\rho U .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (expand the right side, Lorentz part). By Step 2, with the dummy pairs renamed $\lambda\gamma \to \rho\sigma$ and $\mu\nu$ kept,
>
> $$
> -\frac i2\,(\Lambda^{-1}\omega\Lambda)_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu} = -\frac i2\,\omega_{\rho\sigma}\,\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu\Lambda^\sigma{}_\nu J^{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (expand the right side, translation part). For $v = \omega a + \varepsilon$, i.e. $v^\beta = \omega^\beta{}_\sigma a^\sigma + \varepsilon^\beta$, the same index move as in Step 2 gives $(\Lambda^{-1}v)_\mu = g_{\mu\alpha}(\Lambda^{-1})^\alpha{}_\beta v^\beta = \Lambda^\rho{}_\mu v_\rho$, so
>
> $$
> i\,(\Lambda^{-1}v)_\mu P^\mu = i\,\varepsilon_\rho\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu P^\mu + i\,\omega_{\rho\sigma}a^\sigma\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu P^\mu .
> $$
>
> The last term has the parameter $\omega_{\rho\sigma}$ multiplying a coefficient that is not antisymmetric; only its antisymmetric part survives the contraction, so write $\omega_{\rho\sigma}X^{\rho\sigma} = \frac12\omega_{\rho\sigma}(X^{\rho\sigma} - X^{\sigma\rho})$:
>
> $$
> i\,\omega_{\rho\sigma}a^\sigma\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu P^\mu = \frac i2\,\omega_{\rho\sigma}\bigl(a^\sigma\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu - a^\rho\Lambda^\sigma{}_\mu\bigr)P^\mu .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (compare coefficients). Steps 3–5 are first-order expansions of the same operator in the ten independent parameters $\varepsilon_\rho$ and $\omega_{\rho\sigma}$ ($\rho < \sigma$), with coefficients of $\omega_{\rho\sigma}$ antisymmetric on both sides; so the coefficients agree. From $\varepsilon_\rho$: $i\,U^{-1}P^\rho U = i\,\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu P^\mu$. From $\omega_{\rho\sigma}$:
>
> $$
> -\frac i2\,U^{-1}J^{\rho\sigma}U = -\frac i2\,\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu\Lambda^\sigma{}_\nu J^{\mu\nu} + \frac i2\bigl(a^\sigma\Lambda^\rho{}_\mu - a^\rho\Lambda^\sigma{}_\mu\bigr)P^\mu ,
> $$
>
> and multiplying by $2i$ gives the stated law for $J$.
>
> **Step 7** (expectation values). For $|\Psi'\rangle = U(\Lambda)|\Psi\rangle$, $\langle\Psi'|P^\rho|\Psi'\rangle = \langle\Psi|U^\dagger P^\rho U|\Psi\rangle = \Lambda^\rho{}_\mu\langle\Psi|P^\mu|\Psi\rangle$, using $U^\dagger = U^{-1}$.
>
> ⚑ By-product: with $a = 0$ the $J$-law is the tensor law of [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and Its Finite-Dimensional Representations#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]] for the representation $D = U$; the translation terms are new → [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-4-3|Remark: Angular momentum about a shifted origin]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The whole content is the group law: the indices of a generator are spacetime indices because conjugating an infinitesimal transformation by a finite one gives the transformed infinitesimal one.
> - The right side of the $J$-law is antisymmetric in $\rho\sigma$, as it must be; that is why Step 5 antisymmetrized before comparing.
> - Used next: the Poincaré algebra ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-5|Theorem §C3.4.5]]), the Casimir $P^2$ (Theorem §C3.4.6), and the momentum of a transformed state ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Angular momentum about a shifted origin
> With $\Lambda = \mathbb 1$, $U^{-1}J^{ij}U = J^{ij} - a^jP^i + a^iP^j$: in three-dimensional language $\mathbf J \to \mathbf J + \mathbf a\times\mathbf P$ for $a^0 = 0$ (e.g. $J^3 = J^{12} \to J^{12} + a^1P^2 - a^2P^1$): the angular momentum about the origin of the state translated by $\mathbf a$, $U|\Psi\rangle$, equals that of $|\Psi\rangle$ about the point $-\mathbf a$, as for a classical system ($\mathbf L = \mathbf r\times\mathbf p$ with $\mathbf r \to \mathbf r + \mathbf a$; checked numerically in the $5\times5$ representation of Derivation §C3.4.5). The boost generators shift by the time translation as well, $K^i \to K^i - a^iH + a^0P^i$, which is the operator form of $K^i = tP^i - \int d^3x\,x^iT^{00}$ ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-13|Theorem §C3.4.13]]): the boost generator contains the time explicitly. Only $P^\mu$, and the Casimirs built from it, ignore the origin.
>

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.5: The Poincaré Algebra
> Together with the Lorentz algebra $[J^{\mu\nu}, J^{\rho\sigma}] = i(g^{\nu\rho}J^{\mu\sigma} - g^{\mu\rho}J^{\nu\sigma} - g^{\nu\sigma}J^{\mu\rho} + g^{\mu\sigma}J^{\nu\rho})$ of [[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1-6-5|Theorem §C1.6.5]], the generators obey
>
> $$
> [J^{\mu\nu}, P^\rho] = i\bigl(g^{\nu\rho}P^\mu - g^{\mu\rho}P^\nu\bigr), \qquad [P^\mu, P^\nu] = 0 .
> $$
>
> In three-dimensional form, with $H = P^0$, $J^i$ and $K^i = J^{0i}$:
>
> $$
> [J^i, P^j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}P^k, \quad [J^i, H] = 0, \quad [P^i, H] = 0, \quad [P^i, P^j] = 0, \quad [K^i, P^j] = -i\delta_{ij}H, \quad [K^i, H] = -iP^i .
> $$
>
> The ten generators span the **Poincaré algebra**, the Lie algebra of the Poincaré group; the Lorentz algebra is a subalgebra, and the $P^\mu$ span an Abelian ideal.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6, eq. (poincarealgebra) and Step 3 ("in three-dimensional language") · Yu §3.2, eqs. (3.33)–(3.34), (3.66)–(3.73) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.2*

^thm-c3-4-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 0** (the Lorentz algebra on states). [[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^der-c1-6-5|Derivation §C1.6.5]] uses only the multiplication law of a representation and the definition of its generators; [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-4-1|Principle §C3.4.1]] and [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]] supply both for $U(\Lambda)$, so the $J^{\mu\nu}$ on the Hilbert space obey Theorem §C1.6.5. It is also the infinitesimal form of the $J$-law of [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]] at $a = 0$.
>
> **Step 1** ($[J, P]$: set up). Take the $P$-law of Theorem §C3.4.4 with $a = 0$ and $\Lambda = \mathbb 1 + \omega$. By Def. §C3.4.1 and [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]], $U = \mathbb 1 - \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu}$, $U^{-1} = \mathbb 1 + \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu}$, so the left side is
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\mathbb 1 + \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu}\Bigr)P^\rho\Bigl(\mathbb 1 - \frac i2\omega_{\alpha\beta}J^{\alpha\beta}\Bigr) = P^\rho + \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu}P^\rho - \frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}P^\rho J^{\mu\nu} + O(\omega^2) = P^\rho + \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}[J^{\mu\nu}, P^\rho] + O(\omega^2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the right side, antisymmetrized). $(\delta^\rho{}_\mu + \omega^\rho{}_\mu)P^\mu = P^\rho + g^{\rho\alpha}\omega_{\alpha\mu}P^\mu$. Antisymmetrize the coefficient of $\omega_{\alpha\mu}$ as in Step 5 of Derivation §C3.4.4 and rename $\alpha\mu \to \mu\nu$:
>
> $$
> g^{\rho\alpha}\omega_{\alpha\mu}P^\mu = \frac12\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl(g^{\rho\mu}P^\nu - g^{\rho\nu}P^\mu\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (compare). The coefficients of $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ give $\frac i2[J^{\mu\nu}, P^\rho] = \frac12(g^{\rho\mu}P^\nu - g^{\rho\nu}P^\mu)$; multiply by $-2i$: $[J^{\mu\nu}, P^\rho] = -i(g^{\rho\mu}P^\nu - g^{\rho\nu}P^\mu) = i(g^{\nu\rho}P^\mu - g^{\mu\rho}P^\nu)$.
>
> **Step 4** ($[P, P]$). Take the $P$-law with $\Lambda = \mathbb 1$, $a = \varepsilon$: the right side is $P^\rho$. The left side, with $U = \mathbb 1 + i\varepsilon_\nu P^\nu$, $U^{-1} = \mathbb 1 - i\varepsilon_\nu P^\nu$, is $P^\rho - i\varepsilon_\nu P^\nu P^\rho + i\varepsilon_\nu P^\rho P^\nu + O(\varepsilon^2) = P^\rho - i\varepsilon_\nu[P^\nu, P^\rho] + O(\varepsilon^2)$. Each $\varepsilon_\nu$ is arbitrary: $[P^\nu, P^\rho] = 0$.
>
> **Step 5** (three-dimensional form). Insert $g^{00} = 1$, $g^{ij} = -\delta_{ij}$, $g^{0i} = 0$ into Step 3.
> - $[J^i, P^j] = \frac12\varepsilon_{ikl}[J^{kl}, P^j] = \frac i2\varepsilon_{ikl}(g^{lj}P^k - g^{kj}P^l) = \frac i2(-\varepsilon_{ikj}P^k + \varepsilon_{ijl}P^l)$. Since $-\varepsilon_{ikj} = \varepsilon_{ijk}$, both terms are $\frac i2\varepsilon_{ijk}P^k$ after renaming $l \to k$: $[J^i, P^j] = i\varepsilon_{ijk}P^k$.
> - $[J^i, H] = \frac12\varepsilon_{ikl}[J^{kl}, P^0] = \frac i2\varepsilon_{ikl}(g^{l0}P^k - g^{k0}P^l) = 0$.
> - $[K^i, P^j] = [J^{0i}, P^j] = i(g^{ij}P^0 - g^{0j}P^i) = -i\delta_{ij}H$.
> - $[K^i, H] = [J^{0i}, P^0] = i(g^{i0}P^0 - g^{00}P^i) = -iP^i$.
> - $[P^i, H] = [P^i, P^j] = 0$ are Step 4.
>
> ⚑ By-product: $[K^i, H] = -iP^i \neq 0$: the boost generators are not conserved in the sense of commuting with $H$ → [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-4-4|Remark: What the algebra says: conservation, and no boost quantum number]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The commutators are the second-order content of the group law: the first-order expansion *defines* the generators, the transformation law of the generators *is* the algebra.
> - The same steps in the $5\times5$ matrices acting on $(x, 1)$, where $(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ fills the $4\times4$ block and $(P^\mu)^\alpha{}_4 = -ig^{\mu\alpha}$, reproduce every relation (a finite-dimensional, non-unitary representation of the Poincaré group; checked numerically).
> - Used next: $P^2$ commutes with everything ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-6|Theorem §C3.4.6]]); the little-group generators of a massless particle form the same algebra as $(J^3, P^1, P^2)$ ([[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]).

*Uses:* [[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1-6-5|Theorem §C1.6.5]], [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]], [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]], [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the algebra says: conservation, and no boost quantum number
> $[H, \mathbf P] = [H, \mathbf J] = 0$: momentum and angular momentum are conserved, the quantum form of [[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-2|QM Theorem §C8.1.2]]. $[K^i, H] = -iP^i$ says the boost generators do not commute with $H$, yet they are conserved in the sense of Noether, because they contain the time explicitly: with $K^i = tP^i - \int d^3x\,x^iT^{00}$ ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-13|Theorem §C3.4.13]]), the Heisenberg equation for an explicitly time-dependent operator gives $\frac{dK^i}{dt} = \partial_tK^i + i[H, K^i] = P^i - P^i = 0$, the uniform motion of the centre of energy ([[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum#^thm-c1-12-9|Theorem §C1.12.9]]). A conserved quantity that does not commute with $H$ cannot label stationary states, so there is no "boost quantum number": among the ten generators, the largest commuting set that classifies particles is $P^\mu$ together with one rotation-type label ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group|§C3.5★]]). The relations $[K^i, K^j] = -i\varepsilon_{ijk}J^k$ (two boosts make a rotation, [[§C1.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^rem-c1-6-4|§C1.6, Remark: Two boosts make a rotation]]) and $[K^i, P^j] = -i\delta_{ij}H$ (a boost mixes energy into momentum) are what distinguish the Poincaré algebra from the Galilean one.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6 (Step 3 of the Poincaré-algebra derivation) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.2 (Remark "No boost quantum number")*

## The Casimir P² and the vacuum

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.6: P² Is a Casimir Operator
> $P^2 \equiv P_\mu P^\mu = H^2 - \mathbf P^2$ commutes with all ten generators, $[P^2, P^\mu] = [P^2, J^{\mu\nu}] = 0$, and is invariant under every Poincaré transformation, $U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\,P^2\,U(\Lambda, a) = P^2$. On a momentum eigenstate its eigenvalue $p^2$ is unchanged by $U(\Lambda, a)$.
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §2.3 (Note "P² as a Casimir operator") · Yu §3.3, eqs. (3.88)–(3.96) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.7 ("Invariants")*

^thm-c3-4-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (lower an index in $[P, J]$). By [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-5|Theorem §C3.4.5]], $[P^\mu, J^{\rho\sigma}] = -[J^{\rho\sigma}, P^\mu] = -i(g^{\sigma\mu}P^\rho - g^{\rho\mu}P^\sigma)$. Multiply by $g_{\mu\lambda}$ (a number, so it passes through the commutator): $[P_\lambda, J^{\rho\sigma}] = i(\delta^\rho{}_\lambda P^\sigma - \delta^\sigma{}_\lambda P^\rho)$.
>
> **Step 2** (product rule). $[AB, C] = A[B, C] + [A, C]B$ with $A = P^\mu$, $B = P_\mu$, $C = J^{\rho\sigma}$, summed over $\mu$:
>
> $$
> [P^2, J^{\rho\sigma}] = P^\mu[P_\mu, J^{\rho\sigma}] + [P^\mu, J^{\rho\sigma}]P_\mu = i\bigl(P^\rho P^\sigma - P^\sigma P^\rho\bigr) + i\bigl(g^{\rho\mu}P^\sigma P_\mu - g^{\sigma\mu}P^\rho P_\mu\bigr) .
> $$
>
> In the first bracket the Kronecker deltas set $\mu = \rho$ and $\mu = \sigma$; in the second, $g^{\rho\mu}P_\mu = P^\rho$ and $g^{\sigma\mu}P_\mu = P^\sigma$, giving $i(P^\sigma P^\rho - P^\rho P^\sigma)$.
>
> **Step 3** (the momenta commute). $[P^\rho, P^\sigma] = 0$ (Theorem §C3.4.5), so both brackets vanish: $[P^2, J^{\rho\sigma}] = 0$. And $[P^2, P^\nu] = P^\mu[P_\mu, P^\nu] + [P^\mu, P^\nu]P_\mu = 0$.
>
> **Step 4** (finite form, directly). By [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]], $U^{-1}P^\mu P^\nu U = U^{-1}P^\mu U\,U^{-1}P^\nu U = \Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta P^\alpha P^\beta$, and $g_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta = g_{\alpha\beta}$, so $U^{-1}P^2U = P^2$.
>
> **Step 5** (eigenvalues). If $P^\mu|p\rangle = p^\mu|p\rangle$, then $P^2U|p\rangle = U\,U^{-1}P^2U|p\rangle = UP^2|p\rangle = p^2\,U|p\rangle$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Only $[P, P] = 0$ and the vector character of $P$ enter; the invariance of $p^2$ is the operator version of [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-2|REL Theorem §B1.2.2]].
> - $P^2$ is a Casimir operator in the sense of [[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]], so it is a number on every irreducible representation ([[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]]): the mass squared $m^2$ of a particle.
> - Used next: one particle, one orbit ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]]).

^der-c3-4-6

*Uses:* [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-5|Theorem §C3.4.5]], [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]]

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: A second Casimir
> $P^2$ fixes the mass but cannot tell a scalar from a vector particle of the same mass. The Poincaré algebra has one more independent Casimir, $W^2 = W_\mu W^\mu$, built from the Pauli–Lubanski vector $W^\mu = -\frac12\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}J_{\nu\rho}P_\sigma$ ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^def-c3-5-2|Def. §C3.5.2]]); on a massive particle of spin $s$ it equals $-m^2s(s+1)$, on a massless particle of helicity $\lambda$ it vanishes and $W^\mu = \lambda P^\mu$ ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-11|Theorem §C3.5.11]], [[§C3.6★ Massless Particles and Helicity#^thm-c3-6-7|Theorem §C3.6.7]]). Mass and spin are the two Casimir labels of an irreducible representation.
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §2.3 (Note "P² as a Casimir operator", where $W^\mu$ is stated)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.7: An Invariant Vacuum Has Zero Four-Momentum
> Let $|0\rangle$ be an eigenvector of the four $P^\mu$, $P^\mu|0\rangle = p_0^\mu|0\rangle$, and let $U(\Lambda)|0\rangle = c(\Lambda)|0\rangle$ for every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$, $|c(\Lambda)| = 1$. Then $p_0^\mu = 0$. In particular, for the free scalar field the generators of a Poincaré representation with an invariant vacuum are the *normal-ordered* $H$ and $\mathbf P$: a zero-point energy $E_0 \neq 0$ with $\mathbf P|0\rangle = 0$ is incompatible with Lorentz invariance of the vacuum.
>
> *Source: derived here from Theorem §C3.4.4 and the orbit theorem; the vacuum's invariance is item 3 of Principle §C3.4.1 (the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.7, table: "p = 0 … the vacuum"; Yu §3.3, after eq. (3.109))*

^thm-c3-4-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the transformed vacuum is an eigenvector of the transformed momentum). By [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]] with $a = 0$, $P^\mu U(\Lambda) = U(\Lambda)\,U^{-1}(\Lambda)P^\mu U(\Lambda) = U(\Lambda)\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu P^\nu$. Acting on $|0\rangle$:
>
> $$
> P^\mu U(\Lambda)|0\rangle = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,p_0^\nu\;U(\Lambda)|0\rangle .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (invariance). The left side is $c(\Lambda)P^\mu|0\rangle = c(\Lambda)p_0^\mu|0\rangle$, the right side $c(\Lambda)\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu p_0^\nu|0\rangle$. Divide by $c(\Lambda) \neq 0$ and compare the coefficients of $|0\rangle \neq 0$: $\Lambda p_0 = p_0$ for every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$.
>
> **Step 3** (only the origin is fixed by every Λ). The orbit of $p_0$ is then the single point $\{p_0\}$. By [[§C1.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1-4-4|Theorem §C1.4.4]] the orbits are the hyperboloid sheets, the one-sheeted hyperboloids, the two null half-cones and the origin, and only the origin is a single point. So $p_0 = 0$.
>
> **Step 4** (the zero-point energy). The unordered Hamiltonian has $H|0\rangle = E_0|0\rangle$ with $E_0 = V\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{E_{\mathbf p}}2$ ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^thm-c2a-3-3|Theorem §C2a.3.3]]) and $\mathbf P|0\rangle = 0$ ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^thm-c2a-3-4|Theorem §C2a.3.4]]), so $p_0 = (E_0, \mathbf 0)$, which Step 3 forbids unless $E_0 = 0$. ⚑ By-product: normal ordering ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^def-c2a-3-1|Def. §C2a.3.1]]) is not only convenient but required for $H = P^0$ to be the time component of a four-vector of generators with an invariant vacuum → [[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^rem-c2a-3-1|§C2a.3, Remark: Why the zero-point energy is dropped]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The argument uses Lorentz invariance only; translation invariance of $|0\rangle$ would give $p_0 = 0$ directly, since $e^{iP\cdot a}|0\rangle = e^{ip_0\cdot a}|0\rangle = |0\rangle$ for all $a$.
> - Caveat: $E_0$ is infinite (proportional to the volume and quartic in a cutoff), and a box or a cutoff itself breaks boosts, so Step 4 is a statement about the structure of the generators, not a comparison of numbers. A vacuum *energy density* with $\langle T^{\mu\nu}\rangle = \rho\,g^{\mu\nu}$ is Lorentz invariant locally; it is its total charge $\int d^3x\,T^{0\mu}$ that cannot be a finite four-vector. Gravity, which couples to $\rho$, is outside the course.
> - Used next: the vacuum is the orbit $p = 0$ of Wigner's classification ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-6|Theorem §C3.5.6]]).

## Fields

> [!principle] Principle §C3.4.8: Covariance of Quantum Fields
> A field $\Phi_a$ whose index carries the finite-dimensional representation $D$ of [[§C1.8 Scalar Field Theory꞉ Fields, Locality and Symmetry#^def-c1-8-3|Def. §C1.8.3]] is transformed by the operators of [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-4-1|Principle §C3.4.1]] according to the classical law:
>
> $$
> U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\,\Phi_a(x)\,U(\Lambda, a) = D(\Lambda)_a{}^b\,\Phi_b\bigl(\Lambda^{-1}(x - a)\bigr) .
> $$
>
> As operator-valued distributions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-8|Def. §CA.2.8]]) this means, for test functions $f^a$, $U^{-1}\Phi(f)U = \int d^4x\,f^a(x)D(\Lambda)_a{}^b\Phi_b(\Lambda^{-1}(x - a))$, i.e. the smeared field with the test function moved by $(\Lambda, a)$.
>

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.9: Translations of a Field; the Heisenberg Equation
> Under [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-4-8|Principle §C3.4.8]], translations move a field without touching its index, $U^{-1}(\mathbb 1, a)\Phi_a(x)U(\mathbb 1, a) = \Phi_a(x - a)$, and equivalently

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the law for a translation). In Principle §C3.4.8 put $\Lambda = \mathbb 1$: $D(\mathbb 1) = \mathbb 1$ and $\Lambda^{-1}(x - a) = x - a$, so $U^{-1}(\mathbb 1, a)\Phi_a(x)U(\mathbb 1, a) = \Phi_a(x - a)$.
>
> **Step 2** (first order in a). For $a = \varepsilon$, [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]] and [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]] give $U = \mathbb 1 + i\varepsilon_\mu P^\mu$, $U^{-1} = \mathbb 1 - i\varepsilon_\mu P^\mu$. Expanding the product, dropping the $\varepsilon^2$ term:
>
> $$
> (\mathbb 1 - i\varepsilon_\mu P^\mu)\,\Phi_a\,(\mathbb 1 + i\varepsilon_\mu P^\mu) = \Phi_a + i\varepsilon_\mu\Phi_aP^\mu - i\varepsilon_\mu P^\mu\Phi_a + O(\varepsilon^2) = \Phi_a + i\varepsilon_\mu[\Phi_a, P^\mu] + O(\varepsilon^2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (Taylor on the right). $\Phi_a(x - \varepsilon) = \Phi_a(x) - \varepsilon^\mu\partial_\mu\Phi_a(x) + O(\varepsilon^2) = \Phi_a(x) - \varepsilon_\mu\partial^\mu\Phi_a(x) + O(\varepsilon^2)$ ([[§C1.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1-5-10|Theorem §C1.5.10]]; for an operator-valued distribution the derivative is moved onto the test function, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|Def. §CA.2.4]], and the expansion is that of the smooth function $f(\cdot + \varepsilon)$).
>
> **Step 4** (compare). The four $\varepsilon_\mu$ are independent: $i[\Phi_a, P^\mu] = -\partial^\mu\Phi_a$, i.e. $[\Phi_a, P^\mu] = i\partial^\mu\Phi_a$.
>
> **Step 5** (finite form). Step 1 with [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]], 3, reads $e^{-iP\cdot a}\Phi_a(x)e^{iP\cdot a} = \Phi_a(x - a)$. Put $a = x$: $e^{-iP\cdot x}\Phi_a(x)e^{iP\cdot x} = \Phi_a(0)$; multiply by $e^{iP\cdot x}$ on the left and $e^{-iP\cdot x}$ on the right.
>
> **Step 6** (time and space components). $\mu = 0$: $P^0 = H$, $\partial^0 = \partial_t$, so $[\Phi_a, H] = i\partial_t\Phi_a$. $\mu = j$: $\partial^j = -\partial_j$, so $[\Phi_a, P^j] = -i\partial_j\Phi_a$. With $x = (t, \mathbf 0) + (0, \mathbf x)$, Step 5 at $\mathbf x$ fixed gives $\Phi(t, \mathbf x) = e^{iHt}\Phi(0, \mathbf x)e^{-iHt}$: [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^def-c2b-1-1|Def. §C2b.1.1]].
>
> ⚑ By-product: the Heisenberg picture of [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields|§C2b.1]], introduced there as a choice of picture, is the time-translation part of Principle §C3.4.8, and the Heisenberg equation is not an extra postulate → [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-4-6|Remark: Principle or theorem]].
>

> - A translation acts the same way on every field, whatever its index: $D$ represents the Lorentz part only.
> - For the free scalar field both sides were computed independently: $[\phi, H] = i\partial_t\phi$ from the canonical commutators ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-3|Theorem §C2b.1.3]]) and $[\phi, P^\mu] = i\partial^\mu\phi$ from the Noether charges ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-13|Theorem §C3.4.13]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.10: Lorentz Transformations of a Field: Orbital plus Spin
> Under [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-4-8|Principle §C3.4.8]], with the orbital generators $\hat L^{\mu\nu} = i(x^\mu\partial^\nu - x^\nu\partial^\mu)$ ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]) and the representation matrices $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ of the field's index ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and Its Finite-Dimensional Representations#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]),
>
> $$
> [\Phi_a(x), J^{\mu\nu}] = \hat L^{\mu\nu}\Phi_a(x) + D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b\,\Phi_b(x) .
> $$
>
> In three-dimensional form, $[\Phi_a, \mathbf J] = -i\,\mathbf x\times\nabla\,\Phi_a + D(\boldsymbol{\mathcal J})_a{}^b\Phi_b$: orbital angular momentum plus spin. For a scalar field $[\phi(x), J^{\mu\nu}] = \hat L^{\mu\nu}\phi(x)$; for a vector field the spin part is $(\mathcal J^{\rho\sigma})^\mu{}_\nu A^\nu$.

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the law for a Lorentz transformation). Principle §C3.4.8 with $a = 0$ and $\Lambda = \mathbb 1 + \omega$: $U^{-1}(\Lambda)\Phi_a(x)U(\Lambda) = D(\Lambda)_a{}^b\Phi_b(\Lambda^{-1}x)$.

>
> $$
> U^{-1}\Phi_aU = \Phi_a + \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\bigl(J^{\mu\nu}\Phi_a - \Phi_aJ^{\mu\nu}\bigr) = \Phi_a - \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\,[\Phi_a, J^{\mu\nu}] .
> $$

>
> **Step 4** (the index). $D(\mathbb 1 + \omega)_a{}^b = \delta_a{}^b - \frac i2\,\omega_{\mu\nu}D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b + O(\omega^2)$ (Def. §C3.2.1; in the notation of Def. §C1.8.3, $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu}) = iS^{\mu\nu}$). The product of Steps 3 and 4 has four terms: $\delta\cdot\Phi_b \to \Phi_a$; $\delta\cdot(-\frac i2\omega\hat L\Phi_b) \to -\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}\hat L^{\mu\nu}\Phi_a$; $(-\frac i2\omega D)\cdot\Phi_b \to -\frac i2\omega_{\mu\nu}D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b\Phi_b$; and the product of the two first-order terms, second order, dropped.
>
> **Step 5** (compare). Both sides have coefficients of $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ antisymmetric in $\mu\nu$ ($J^{\mu\nu}$, $\hat L^{\mu\nu}$ and $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ all are), so $-\frac i2[\Phi_a, J^{\mu\nu}] = -\frac i2\hat L^{\mu\nu}\Phi_a - \frac i2D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})_a{}^b\Phi_b$. Multiply by $2i$.
>
> **Step 6** (three-dimensional form). $\hat L^i \equiv \frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat L^{jk} = \frac i2\varepsilon_{ijk}(x^j\partial^k - x^k\partial^j) = i\varepsilon_{ijk}x^j\partial^k$ (the two terms are equal after $j \leftrightarrow k$), and $\partial^k = -\partial_k$, so $\hat{\mathbf L} = -i\,\mathbf x\times\nabla$, the orbital angular momentum of Quantum Mechanics with $\hbar = 1$ ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-1|QM Theorem §C5.4.1]]).
>
> ⚑ By-product: three different actions on one object: $J^{\mu\nu}$ on its operator nature (by commutator), $\hat L^{\mu\nu}$ on its argument, $D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ on its index → [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-4-2|Remark: Two kinds of representation]]; the classical total generator is [[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]], and the three realizations are compared in [[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^rem-c3-3-1|§C3.3, Remark: Three realizations of one algebra]].

## The free scalar field: an explicit representation

For the free real scalar field of [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^mod-c2a-2-1|Model §C2a.2.1]] everything above can be constructed rather than postulated. Write $d\mu(p) \equiv \frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_{\mathbf p}}$ for the invariant measure on the mass shell $H^+_m$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-2|Def. §C2a.4.2]]), $\alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p} \equiv \sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$ for the creation operator of the relativistically normalized state, $|\mathbf p\rangle = \alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-1|Def. §C2a.4.1]]), and $\Lambda\mathbf p$ for the spatial part of $\Lambda p$, $p^0 = E_{\mathbf p}$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.11: The Free Scalar Field Carries a Unitary Representation
> 1. On the one-particle space of wave packets $|g\rangle = \int d\mu(p)\,g(\mathbf p)\,|\mathbf p\rangle$, $g \in L^2(H^+_m, d\mu)$, the operators
>
> $$
> \bigl(U(\Lambda, a)g\bigr)(\mathbf p) = e^{ip\cdot a}\,g(\Lambda^{-1}\mathbf p)
> $$
>
> form a unitary representation of the Poincaré group; in kernel form $U(\Lambda, a)|\mathbf p\rangle = e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\,|\Lambda\mathbf p\rangle$.
> 2. On Fock space, $U(\Lambda, a)|\mathbf p_1, \dots, \mathbf p_n\rangle = e^{i(\Lambda p_1 + \dots + \Lambda p_n)\cdot a}\,|\Lambda\mathbf p_1, \dots, \Lambda\mathbf p_n\rangle$ and $U(\Lambda, a)|0\rangle = |0\rangle$ define a unitary representation satisfying [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-4-1|Principle §C3.4.1]].
> 3. The mode operators transform as
>
> $$
> U(\Lambda, a)\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}\,U(\Lambda, a)^{-1} = e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\sqrt{\frac{E_{\Lambda\mathbf p}}{E_{\mathbf p}}}\;a^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p}, \qquad U(\Lambda, a)\,a_{\mathbf p}\,U(\Lambda, a)^{-1} = e^{-i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\sqrt{\frac{E_{\Lambda\mathbf p}}{E_{\mathbf p}}}\;a_{\Lambda\mathbf p} .
> $$
>
> 4. The translation generators are the normal-ordered energy and momentum, $P^\mu = \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,p^\mu\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p}$.
>
> *Source: PS §2.3, eqs. (2.37)–(2.38) (stated) · the user's pre-course notes, §3.3 (Remark "Realization of the massive spin-0 representation") · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.7 (the parenthesis after eq. (wignerlaw)); the wave-packet form and the proofs written out here*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the measure is invariant, and Λ maps the shell onto itself). For $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$, $p \mapsto \Lambda p$ maps $H^+_m$ onto itself (an orbit, [[§C1.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1-4-4|Theorem §C1.4.4]]), and $\int d\mu(p)\,F(\Lambda p) = \int d\mu(p)\,F(p)$ for every integrable $F$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-4|Theorem §C2a.4.4]], 2, applied to $F\circ\Lambda$).
>
> **Step 2** (norm of a wave packet). By $\langle\mathbf p|\mathbf q\rangle = 2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-5|Theorem §C2a.4.5]]), the $\mathbf q$-integral against the delta cancels one measure exactly: $\langle g|h\rangle = \int d\mu(p)\,\overline{g(\mathbf p)}\,h(\mathbf p)$. So the one-particle space is $L^2(H^+_m, d\mu)$.
>
> **Step 3** (unitarity). The phase has modulus 1, and by Step 1 with $F = |g|^2$ and $\Lambda^{-1}$ in place of $\Lambda$:
>
> $$
> \|U(\Lambda, a)g\|^2 = \int d\mu(p)\,|g(\Lambda^{-1}\mathbf p)|^2 = \int d\mu(p)\,|g(\mathbf p)|^2 = \|g\|^2 .
> $$
>
> $U(\Lambda, a)$ is onto, since $U(\Lambda^{-1}, -\Lambda^{-1}a)$ undoes it (Step 4 with $g_2 = g_1^{-1}$); an onto isometry is unitary ([[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-51|LADR Def. 7.51]]).
>
> **Step 4** (composition). Apply $(\Lambda_1, a_1)$ and then $(\Lambda_2, a_2)$:
>
> $$
> \bigl(U(\Lambda_2, a_2)U(\Lambda_1, a_1)g\bigr)(\mathbf p) = e^{ip\cdot a_2}\bigl(U(\Lambda_1, a_1)g\bigr)(\Lambda_2^{-1}\mathbf p) = e^{ip\cdot a_2}\,e^{i(\Lambda_2^{-1}p)\cdot a_1}\,g(\Lambda_1^{-1}\Lambda_2^{-1}\mathbf p) .
> $$
>
> The Minkowski product is invariant, $(\Lambda_2^{-1}p)\cdot a_1 = p\cdot(\Lambda_2a_1)$, and $\Lambda_1^{-1}\Lambda_2^{-1} = (\Lambda_2\Lambda_1)^{-1}$, so the result is $e^{ip\cdot(\Lambda_2a_1 + a_2)}g((\Lambda_2\Lambda_1)^{-1}\mathbf p) = \bigl(U(\Lambda_2\Lambda_1, \Lambda_2a_1 + a_2)g\bigr)(\mathbf p)$: the composition law. Continuity in $(\Lambda, a)$ holds for continuous $g$ of compact support and extends to $L^2$ by density.
>
> **Step 5** (kernel form). Insert the definition into $U|g\rangle = \int d\mu(q)\,e^{iq\cdot a}g(\Lambda^{-1}\mathbf q)\,|\mathbf q\rangle$ and substitute $\mathbf q = \Lambda\mathbf p$: by Step 1, $d\mu(q) = d\mu(p)$, and $g(\Lambda^{-1}\mathbf q) = g(\mathbf p)$:
>
> $$
> U(\Lambda, a)|g\rangle = \int d\mu(p)\,g(\mathbf p)\;e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\,|\Lambda\mathbf p\rangle .
> $$
>
> Since this holds for every $g$, $U(\Lambda, a)|\mathbf p\rangle = e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}|\Lambda\mathbf p\rangle$ as kernels. (*Sense:* $|\mathbf p\rangle$ is not a vector, [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^cau-c2a-4-1|§C2a.4, Caution: Plane-wave states are not normalizable]]; the kernel statement is the identity of the maps $g \mapsto U|g\rangle$, [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-6|Theorem §C2a.4.6]].)
>
> **Step 6** (Fock space). On $n$-particle wave packets $g(\mathbf p_1, \dots, \mathbf p_n)$, symmetric in their arguments, define $(Ug)(\mathbf p_1, \dots) = e^{i(p_1 + \dots + p_n)\cdot a}g(\Lambda^{-1}\mathbf p_1, \dots, \Lambda^{-1}\mathbf p_n)$. Steps 1–5 go through factor by factor with the product measure $d\mu(p_1)\cdots d\mu(p_n)$, because the $n$-particle overlap $\langle\mathbf p_1 \dots \mathbf p_n|\mathbf q_1 \dots \mathbf q_n\rangle$ is a sum over permutations of products of the invariant kernels $2E(2\pi)^3\delta^3$ (by the mode algebra, [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-6|Theorem §C2a.2.6]], and [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-3|Theorem §C2a.4.3]]); symmetric functions go to symmetric functions. For $n = 0$ the definition is $U|0\rangle = |0\rangle$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 7** (the mode operators). $|\mathbf p, \mathbf q_1, \dots, \mathbf q_n\rangle = \alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|\mathbf q_1, \dots, \mathbf q_n\rangle$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-1|Def. §C2a.4.1]]). Apply $U$ and use part 2 twice:
>
> $$
> U\alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p}|\mathbf q_1 \dots\rangle = e^{i(\Lambda p + \sum_i\Lambda q_i)\cdot a}|\Lambda\mathbf p, \Lambda\mathbf q_1, \dots\rangle = e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\,\alpha^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p}\;e^{i\sum_i(\Lambda q_i)\cdot a}|\Lambda\mathbf q_1, \dots\rangle = e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\,\alpha^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p}\,U|\mathbf q_1 \dots\rangle .
> $$
>
> The states $U|\mathbf q_1 \dots\rangle$ span Fock space ($U$ is onto), so $U\alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p}U^{-1} = e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\alpha^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p}$ (as an identity of operator-valued distributions in $\mathbf p$, smeared with a test function as in Step 5). Divide by $\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$ and write $\alpha^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p} = \sqrt{2E_{\Lambda\mathbf p}}\,a^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p}$: the first formula of part 3. The adjoint, with $(UXU^{-1})^\dagger = UX^\dagger U^{-1}$ for unitary $U$, gives the second.
>
> **Step 8** (the generators of translations). With $\Lambda = \mathbb 1$, $U(\mathbb 1, a)$ multiplies $|\mathbf p_1 \dots \mathbf p_n\rangle$ by $e^{i(p_1 + \dots + p_n)\cdot a}$; by Def. §C3.4.1, $P^\mu = -i\,\partial U/\partial a_\mu|_0$ has eigenvalue $p_1^\mu + \dots + p_n^\mu$ on it, and $0$ on $|0\rangle$. The operator $\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\,p^\mu a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p}$ has the same eigenvalues on the same states, by the ladder relations ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^thm-c2a-3-6|Theorem §C2a.3.6]]) and $a_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle = 0$: for $\mu = 0$ it is $:\!H\!:$ ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^def-c2a-3-1|Def. §C2a.3.1]]), for $\mu = j$ it is $\mathbf P$ ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^thm-c2a-3-4|Theorem §C2a.3.4]]). ⚑ By-product: the unordered $H$ would add $E_0$ on every state and spoil $U|0\rangle = |0\rangle$ → [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-7|Theorem §C3.4.7]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Unitarity is exactly the invariance of the measure ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-4|Theorem §C2a.4.4]]) and of the overlap $\langle\mathbf p|\mathbf q\rangle$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-5|Theorem §C2a.4.5]]); this is why the relativistic normalization was chosen.
> - The representation is infinite-dimensional: functions on a three-dimensional hyperboloid. The one-particle part has no label besides $\mathbf p$: it is Wigner's massive spin-0 representation ([[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group#^thm-c3-5-8|Theorem §C3.5.8]] with $s = 0$).
> - The factor $\sqrt{E_{\Lambda\mathbf p}/E_{\mathbf p}}$ in part 3 is the price of the non-invariant normalization $[a_{\mathbf p}, a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}] = (2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$; the relativistically normalized $\alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$ transform without it.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.12: The Free Scalar Field Transforms as a Scalar
> With $U(\Lambda, a)$ of [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-11|Theorem §C3.4.11]] and the Heisenberg field $\phi(x)$ of [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-1|Theorem §C2b.1.1]],
>
> $$
> U(\Lambda, a)\,\phi(x)\,U(\Lambda, a)^{-1} = \phi(\Lambda x + a), \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad U^{-1}(\Lambda, a)\,\phi(x)\,U(\Lambda, a) = \phi\bigl(\Lambda^{-1}(x - a)\bigr),
> $$
>
> as operator-valued distributions: $U\phi(f)U^{-1} = \phi(f_{(\Lambda, a)})$ with $f_{(\Lambda, a)}(y) = f(\Lambda^{-1}(y - a))$. So [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^pr-c3-4-8|Principle §C3.4.8]] holds for the free scalar field with $D = 1$.
>
> *Source: the user's pre-course notes, §3.3 (Remark "Realization of the massive spin-0 representation", run in the opposite direction) and §4.2, eq. (scalar-transform) · PS §2.3, eq. (2.38) with §3.1, eq. (3.2) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6 (Step 5 of Derivation "Expanding U(Λ, a)")*

^thm-c3-4-12

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the field over the invariant measure). Since $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}} = \frac{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}{2E_{\mathbf p}}$, Theorem §C2b.1.1 reads
>
> $$
> \phi(x) = \int d\mu(p)\,\bigl(\alpha_{\mathbf p}\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + \alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf p}\,e^{ip\cdot x}\bigr), \qquad p^0 = E_{\mathbf p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (conjugate term by term). $U$ passes through the numbers $e^{\mp ip\cdot x}$ and through the integral (*sense:* after smearing with $f \in \mathcal S(\mathbb R^4)$ the integral has Schwartz coefficients, [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-2|Theorem §C2b.1.2]], 1, and is a limit of finite sums in every wave-packet matrix element). By Theorem §C3.4.11, 3, written for $\alpha$:
>
> $$
> U\phi(x)U^{-1} = \int d\mu(p)\,\bigl(e^{-i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\,\alpha_{\Lambda\mathbf p}\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\,\alpha^\dagger_{\Lambda\mathbf p}\,e^{ip\cdot x}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (change variables on the shell). Substitute $\mathbf q = \Lambda\mathbf p$, i.e. $q = \Lambda p$ on $H^+_m$: the measure is unchanged, $d\mu(p) = d\mu(q)$ (Step 1 of Derivation §C3.4.11; the change of variables of an invariant distribution, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-5|Def. §CA.2.5]]), and the domain $H^+_m$ is mapped onto itself. In the exponent, $p\cdot x = (\Lambda^{-1}q)\cdot x = q\cdot(\Lambda x)$ by invariance of the Minkowski product. So $-i(\Lambda p)\cdot a - ip\cdot x = -iq\cdot(\Lambda x + a)$ and
>
> $$
> U\phi(x)U^{-1} = \int d\mu(q)\,\bigl(\alpha_{\mathbf q}\,e^{-iq\cdot(\Lambda x + a)} + \alpha^\dagger_{\mathbf q}\,e^{iq\cdot(\Lambda x + a)}\bigr) = \phi(\Lambda x + a) .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (the inverse form). Apply Step 3 to $(\Lambda, a)^{-1} = (\Lambda^{-1}, -\Lambda^{-1}a)$, whose operator is $U(\Lambda, a)^{-1}$ ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]], 1): $U^{-1}\phi(x)U = \phi(\Lambda^{-1}x - \Lambda^{-1}a) = \phi(\Lambda^{-1}(x - a))$.
>
> **Step 5** (smeared form). $U\phi(f)U^{-1} = \int d^4x\,f(x)\,\phi(\Lambda x + a)$; substitute $y = \Lambda x + a$, $d^4y = |\det\Lambda|\,d^4x = d^4x$ ([[§C1.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1-4-1|Theorem §C1.4.1]]): $= \int d^4y\,f(\Lambda^{-1}(y - a))\,\phi(y) = \phi(f_{(\Lambda, a)})$.
>
> ⚑ By-product: the canonical construction singled out a time (equal-time commutators, $\pi = \partial_t\phi$, a Hamiltonian), yet the result is covariant; this theorem is the check → [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-4-6|Remark: Principle or theorem]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The invariant measure, not $d^3p$, is what makes the substitution $q = \Lambda p$ free of Jacobians; the relativistic normalization of $\alpha^\dagger$ is what makes the operators transform without factors.
> - With $U|0\rangle = |0\rangle$ it gives at once $\langle0|\phi(\Lambda x)\phi(\Lambda y)|0\rangle = \langle0|\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle$: the Lorentz invariance of the Wightman function ([[§C2b.2 The Wightman Function#^thm-c2b-2-3|Theorem §C2b.2.3]]) is vacuum invariance plus this theorem.
> - $\pi = \partial_t\phi$ is not a scalar: it is the time component of the covector $\partial_\mu\phi$: differentiating Step 3 with the chain rule, $\partial y^\nu/\partial x^\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu$ for $y = \Lambda x + a$, gives $U\partial_\mu\phi(x)U^{-1} = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu(\partial_\nu\phi)(\Lambda x + a)$, equivalently $U^{-1}\partial_\mu\phi(x)U = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu(\partial_\nu\phi)(\Lambda^{-1}(x - a))$, the law of Principle §C3.4.8 for a lower index.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.13: The Noether Charges Are the Generators
> For the free real scalar field, the Noether charges of translations and Lorentz transformations, $P^\mu = \int d^3x\,T^{0\mu}$ ([[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum#^thm-c1-12-6|Theorem §C1.12.6]]) and $J^{\mu\nu} = \int d^3x\,\mathcal M^{0\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum#^def-c1-12-3|Def. §C1.12.3]]), taken as operators (normal ordered), satisfy
>
> $$
> [\phi(x), P^\mu] = i\,\partial^\mu\phi(x), \qquad [\phi(x), J^{\mu\nu}] = \hat L^{\mu\nu}\phi(x),
> $$
>
> the relations of Theorems §C3.4.9–§C3.4.10 with $D = 1$. So they are the generators of Def. §C3.4.1, with $P^0 = :\!H\!:$. In particular the boost generator is
>
> $$
> K^i = J^{0i} = t\,P^i - \int d^3x\,x^i\,T^{00} = -K^i_{\rm N},
> $$
>
> minus the boost charge $K^i_{\rm N} \equiv J^{i0}$ of Def. §C1.12.3.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.6 ("the Noether charges are the generators of U(Λ, a)"; Step 3 of the Poincaré-algebra derivation for $K^i$) · Yu §3.2, after eq. (3.86) (stated; checked for $P^\mu$ in Yu (2.129)) · the user's pre-course notes, §2.2 (Note "Agreement with the canonical construction", stated); the equal-time computation written out here*

^thm-c3-4-13

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> All commutators are at equal times: each charge is conserved, so it may be evaluated as an integral over the slice $y^0 = x^0 \equiv t$ of the field it is commuted with. Inputs: the equal-time relations $[\phi(t, \mathbf x), \pi(t, \mathbf y)] = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$, $[\phi(t, \mathbf x), \phi(t, \mathbf y)] = 0$ ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-3|Theorem §C2b.1.3]], 1), and $\pi = \partial_t\phi$ ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-1|Theorem §C2b.1.1]], 3). For the scalar field, $T^{0k} = \pi\,\partial^k\phi$ and $T^{00} = \mathcal H = \frac12\pi^2 + \frac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \frac12m^2\phi^2$ (Theorem §C1.12.6), and $\mathcal M^{0\mu\nu} = x^\mu T^{0\nu} - x^\nu T^{0\mu}$ ([[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum#^thm-c1-12-7|Theorem §C1.12.7]], no spin part). Normal ordering changes each charge by a c-number, which drops out of every commutator. Write $\phi = \phi(t, \mathbf x)$ and primes for fields at $(t, \mathbf y)$.
>
> **Step 1** ($[\phi, \pi'\,\partial'^k\phi']$). By $[A, BC] = [A, B]C + B[A, C]$: $[\phi, \pi'\partial'^k\phi'] = [\phi, \pi']\,\partial'^k\phi' + \pi'\,\partial'^k[\phi, \phi'] = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)\,\partial'^k\phi'$. The second term vanishes because $[\phi, \phi']$ is zero for *all* $\mathbf y$, so its $\mathbf y$-derivative is zero ([[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^cau-c2b-1-1|§C2b.1, Caution: Equal times only]]).
>
> **Step 2** ($[\phi, \mathcal H']$). $[\phi, \frac12\pi'^2] = \frac12(\pi'[\phi, \pi'] + [\phi, \pi']\pi') = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)\,\pi'$ (the commutator is a number); $[\phi, \frac12(\nabla'\phi')^2] = 0$ and $[\phi, \frac12m^2\phi'^2] = 0$ as in Step 1. So $[\phi, \mathcal H'] = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)\,\pi'$.
>
> **Step 3** (momentum). Integrate Step 1 over $\mathbf y$; the delta sets $\mathbf y = \mathbf x$ (*sense:* smeared in $\mathbf x$ with $g \in \mathcal S(\mathbb R^3)$, the $\mathbf y$-integral of $g(\mathbf x)\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$ against the operator-valued distribution $\partial'^k\phi'$ is $\partial^k\phi(t, g)$, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-2|Def. §CA.2.2]]): $[\phi, P^k] = i\,\partial^k\phi$. Integrating Step 2: $[\phi, H] = i\pi = i\partial_t\phi = i\partial^0\phi$. Together, $[\phi, P^\mu] = i\partial^\mu\phi$.
>
> **Step 4** (rotations, $\mu\nu = jk$). $J^{jk} = \int d^3y\,(y^jT^{0k} - y^kT^{0j})$. By Step 1, $[\phi, y^j\pi'\partial'^k\phi'] = iy^j\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)\partial'^k\phi'$, and the delta sets $y^j = x^j$:
>
> $$
> [\phi, J^{jk}] = i\bigl(x^j\partial^k - x^k\partial^j\bigr)\phi = \hat L^{jk}\phi .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (boosts, $\mu\nu = 0k$). $J^{0k} = \int d^3y\,(y^0T^{0k} - y^kT^{00})$ with $y^0 = t$ on the slice: $J^{0k} = tP^k - \int d^3y\,y^k\,\mathcal H'$. By Steps 3 and 2,
>
> $$
> [\phi, J^{0k}] = t\cdot i\partial^k\phi - i\,x^k\,\pi = i\bigl(x^0\partial^k - x^k\partial^0\bigr)\phi = \hat L^{0k}\phi ,
> $$
>
> using $\pi = \partial_t\phi = \partial^0\phi$ and $t = x^0$. ⚑ By-product: $J^{0k}$ contains $t$ explicitly, and $[\phi, J^{0k}]$ involves $\partial_t\phi$ although only equal-time relations were used: the boost needs the dynamics ($\pi = \partial_t\phi$), rotations and translations in space do not → [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^rem-c3-4-6|Remark: Principle or theorem]].
>
> **Step 6** (they are the generators). Steps 3–5 are the relations that the generators of Def. §C3.4.1 satisfy by Theorems §C3.4.9–§C3.4.10 (with $D = 1$, which [[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-12|Theorem §C3.4.12]] establishes for this field). Let $X$ be the difference of a Noether charge and the corresponding generator. It commutes with every $\phi(x)$, hence with $\pi = \partial_t\phi$, hence with every $a_{\mathbf p}$ and $a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}$, which are extracted from $\phi$ and $\pi$ by [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-5|Theorem §C2a.2.5]]. Then $a_{\mathbf p}X|0\rangle = Xa_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle = 0$ for every $\mathbf p$, so $X|0\rangle = c|0\rangle$ (the vacuum is the only state annihilated by every $a_{\mathbf p}$, [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^pr-c2a-4-1|Principle §C2a.4.1]]), and $X\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_1}\cdots a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_n}|0\rangle = a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_1}\cdots a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_n}X|0\rangle = c\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_1}\cdots a^\dagger_{\mathbf p_n}|0\rangle$: $X = c\mathbb 1$ on Fock space (the operator form of Schur's lemma, [[§C3.1 Groups, Algebras and Representations of Rotations#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]]). The constant is $c = \langle0|X|0\rangle$: a normal-ordered charge has vanishing vacuum expectation value, and a generator annihilates the invariant vacuum ($U|0\rangle = |0\rangle$, differentiated). So $c = 0$. For $P^\mu$ the identification is also Theorem §C3.4.11, 4.
>
> **Step 7** (the boost dictionary). $K^i \equiv J^{0i}$ (Def. §C3.4.1) $= tP^i - \int d^3x\,x^iT^{00}$ by Step 5, while Def. §C1.12.3 names $K^i_{\rm N} \equiv J^{i0} = -J^{0i}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Noether's theorem and the representation theory meet: the classical charges, quantized canonically, are the generators of the unitary representation, with the same signs. This resolves [[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum#^cau-c1-12-2|§C1.12, Caution: Notation in Yu and in the conventions table]].
> - Assumptions: the charges are conserved (so any slice may be used) and finite, i.e. the fields fall off at spatial infinity in every matrix element considered; normal ordering only shifts c-numbers.
> - Used next: the algebra of these charges is the Poincaré algebra (Theorem §C3.4.5) because they are the generators.

> [!remark] Remark: Principle or theorem
> Principle §C3.4.8 is what a relativistic quantum field theory *requires*, and in the axiomatic formulation it is an axiom. For the free scalar field it is a theorem twice over: from the explicit $U$ (Theorem §C3.4.12) and from the Noether charges (Theorem §C3.4.13). The layer change matters because canonical quantization is not manifestly covariant: it fixes a time slice, postulates commutators at equal times ([[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2a-1-1|Principle §C2a.1.1]]) and evolves with $H$. Space translations and rotations act within the slice and are automatic; boosts tilt the slice, and their correctness rests on the dynamics, as Step 5 of Derivation §C3.4.13 shows. For an interacting theory the check is that the Noether charges, built with the interacting $\mathcal H$, still close into the Poincaré algebra; the relation $[K^i, H] = -iP^i$ is the nontrivial one, and it holds when the interaction density is a Lorentz scalar (QFT C6, planned). The Heisenberg equation, an independent input in [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields|§C2b.1]], is the time-translation part of the same principle ([[§C3.4 Quantum Poincaré Transformations#^thm-c3-4-9|Theorem §C3.4.9]]).
>

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Quantum Mechanics built three generator constructions separately, translations ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-4|QM Principle §C2.2.4]]), rotations ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|QM Principle §C5.1.2]]) and time evolution ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^pr-c3-1-1|QM Principle §C3.1.1]]); Principle §C3.4.1 is the single relativistic one, and boosts are the generators QM never needed, the ones that mix $H$ with $\mathbf P$.
> - The Hermiticity argument of Theorem §C3.4.2 is the one that made $\mathbf J$ Hermitian in [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|QM Principle §C5.1.2]]; what fails relativistically is only the finite-dimensional *matrix* form of the boost, [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and Its Finite-Dimensional Representations#^thm-c3-2-12|Theorem §C3.2.12]].
> - The ten generators match the ten parameters of the Poincaré group and the ten classical conservation laws — [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^rem-b1-2-5|REL Remark: Ten parameters, ten conservation laws]], [[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum|§C1.12]]; Theorem §C3.4.13 is the quantum statement that the charges generate the symmetry, as Poisson brackets with $H$ generate time evolution classically ([[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^thm-c1-10-6|Theorem §C1.10.6]]).
> - The Heisenberg field of [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^def-c2b-1-1|Def. §C2b.1.1]] is $\phi(x) = e^{iP\cdot x}\phi(0)e^{-iP\cdot x}$ restricted to time; the plane wave $\langle0|\phi(x)|\mathbf p\rangle = e^{-ip\cdot x}$ of [[§C2b.1 Heisenberg Fields#^thm-c2b-1-4|Theorem §C2b.1.4]] is translation covariance applied to one matrix element, and the field creating a particle at a point ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-7|Theorem §C2a.4.7]]) is the same matrix element at $t = 0$.
> - The Lorentz invariance of the Wightman function ([[§C2b.2 The Wightman Function#^thm-c2b-2-3|Theorem §C2b.2.3]]) is vacuum invariance plus Theorem §C3.4.12; combined with microcausality it is why the commutator function vanishes outside the light cone ([[§C2b.4 Microcausality and the Commutator Function#^thm-c2b-4-5|Theorem §C2b.4.5]]): an invariant function cannot distinguish $x$ from $-x$ at spacelike separation.
> - Theorem §C3.4.7 gives the zero-point energy a second reason to be dropped ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^rem-c2a-3-1|§C2a.3, Remark: Why the zero-point energy is dropped]]): not only is it unobservable without gravity, it is incompatible with an invariant vacuum whose energy is the time component of a four-vector.
> - The orbit theorem ([[§C1.4 The Lorentz Group#^thm-c1-4-4|Theorem §C1.4.4]]) enters twice: the only invariant momentum is zero (Theorem §C3.4.7), and the one-particle space of the scalar is $L^2$ of one orbit (Theorem §C3.4.11); in [[§C3.5★ Particle States and the Little Group|§C3.5★]] every particle lives on one orbit.
> - The orbital-plus-spin split of Theorem §C3.4.10 is the operator image of the orbital and spin parts of the Lorentz current ([[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum#^thm-c1-12-7|Theorem §C1.12.7]]) and of $\mathbf J = \mathbf L + \mathbf S$ in Quantum Mechanics ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-1|QM Theorem §C5.4.1]]).
> - The three relations $[\Phi, P^\mu] = i\partial^\mu\Phi$, $[\Phi, J^{\mu\nu}] = \hat L^{\mu\nu}\Phi + D\Phi$, and the field algebra, with unitarity and an invariant vacuum, are the content of the Wightman axioms on covariance (★, Streater & Wightman, Ch. 3); the field being an operator-valued distribution ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-8|Def. §CA.2.8]]) is why every identity here is stated after smearing.
> - [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-5|Def. §CA.2.5]] (linear change of variables of a distribution) and the invariant measure ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-4|Theorem §C2a.4.4]]) license the substitution $q = \Lambda p$ of Derivation §C3.4.12 and the unitarity of Theorem §C3.4.11; [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|Def. §CA.2.4]] (derivatives of distributions) licenses the Taylor steps of Theorems §C3.4.9–§C3.4.10.
> - Math: $U$ is a group homomorphism into the unitary group ([[§15 Homomorphisms#^def-15-1|493 Def. §15.1]]; unitary operators, [[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-51|LADR Def. 7.51]]); the Poincaré group is a semidirect product of translations and Lorentz transformations, visible in the composition law $(\Lambda_2, a_2)(\Lambda_1, a_1) = (\Lambda_2\Lambda_1, \Lambda_2a_1 + a_2)$ and in the translations forming a normal subgroup ([[§38 Normal Subgroups|493 §38]]).
