Which finite-dimensional representations can the index of a field carry, what do the labels $(j_+, j_-)$ mean physically, and why are they not unitary? A field's law $\phi'_a(x) = D(\Lambda)_a{}^b\phi_b(\Lambda^{-1}x)$ ([[§C1b.1 Fields and Their Transformation Laws#^def-c1b-1-3|Def. §C1b.1.3]]) asks for a finite-dimensional representation $D$ of the Lorentz group on the index; the generators and the rotation and boost generators $\mathbf J$, $\mathbf K$ of the vector representation are [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-1|Def. §C1a.6.1]]–[[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-2|§C1a.6.2]]. The mathematics — the generators of a representation and their commutation relations with three derivations (from the group law, in the vector representation as in Problem Set 4, and in rotation–boost form as in Problem Set 5), the finite form of the algebra (the generators transform as a tensor), the reduction of every representation to two commuting angular momenta $\mathbf J_\pm$ with the factorization of every Lorentz transformation that follows, and the list $(j_+, j_-)$ of all finite-dimensional representations with their rotation content, products, parity, conjugation and non-unitarity — is [[§CB.5 The Lorentz Algebra꞉ Generators and Commutation Relations|§CB.5]]–[[§CB.6 The Lorentz Algebra꞉ the Split J±, Real Forms and 𝔰𝔩(2,ℂ) as a Real Lie Algebra|§CB.6]] and [[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)|§CB.17]]; the statements used here are shown where they are needed. This section (Lecture 7, Parts B–C) keeps the physical reading: what the third commutator says about boosts, the slide's word "reducible", the explicit spinor and vector representations used in calculations, parity on the indices of fields, the rotation and boost weights of components (the four-vector and $(1, 0)$ component by component, and the test that tells the four-vector from $(1, 0)\oplus(0, 0)$), and the catalogue of the fields of the course with their labels. The vocabulary is [[§C3.1 Index Slots, Rotations and Spin in Field Theory|§C3.1]]; the field laws themselves are [[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group|§C3.3]].

## The Lorentz algebra on the indices of fields

> [!remark] Remark: Two boosts make a rotation
> The first relation is the rotation algebra of Quantum Mechanics ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-1|QM Theorem §C5.1.1]]); the second says $\mathbf K$ transforms as a vector under rotations; the third is the Lorentz-specific one. Its minus sign is the whole difference between $SO(1,3)$ and the rotation group $SO(4)$ of four Euclidean dimensions, where the same computation gives $+i\varepsilon_{ijk}J_k$. It says that boosts do not close among themselves: two infinitesimal boosts in different directions, applied in opposite orders, differ by a rotation. Its finite form is the Wigner rotation of two perpendicular boosts ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^ex-b1-2-2|REL Example §B1.2.2]]), which along a curved path accumulates into Thomas precession. Because of the $\mathbf J_\pm$ split, finite-dimensional representations are labelled by two spins $(j_+, j_-)$: $(0, 0)$ the scalar, $(\frac12, 0)\oplus(0, \frac12)$ the Dirac spinor, $(\frac12, \frac12)$ the vector ([[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)#^def-cb-17-3|Def. §CB.17.3]], [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^rem-c3-2-3|§C3.2, Remark: The representations met in the course]]; spinor fields: [[§C5a.3 The Lorentz Action on Spinor Space#^def-c5a-3-1|Def. §C5a.3.1]], [[§C5a.3 The Lorentz Action on Spinor Space#^thm-c5a-3-2|Theorem §C5a.3.2]]).
>

> [!caution] Caution: The algebra decomposes; it is not "reducible"
> The slides (Lecture 7, "Transformation of General Fields") say the Lorentz algebra "is reducible = 2 copies of the angular momentum algebra". Reducibility is a property of a representation ([[§CB.3 Homomorphisms, Representations, the Identity Component and Coverings#^def-cb-3-7|Def. §CB.3.7]]); what Theorem §CB.6.2 shows is that the *complexified* Lorentz algebra, in which the combinations $\mathbf J_\pm = \frac12(\mathbf J \pm i\mathbf K)$ live, is a direct sum of two commuting copies of the *complexified* rotation algebra $\mathfrak{sl}(2, \mathbb C)$: $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)_{\mathbb C} \cong \mathfrak{sl}(2, \mathbb C)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2, \mathbb C)$ (complexification, [[§CB.4 Real Lie Algebras, the Physicists' i and Complexification#^def-cb-4-3|Def. §CB.4.3]]; [[§CB.6 The Lorentz Algebra꞉ the Split J±, Real Forms and 𝔰𝔩(2,ℂ) as a Real Lie Algebra#^thm-cb-6-1|Theorem §CB.6.1]]). The real algebra itself does not split into two real rotation algebras: $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ is simple as a real Lie algebra, so it is not $\mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)$ ([[§CB.6 The Lorentz Algebra꞉ the Split J±, Real Forms and 𝔰𝔩(2,ℂ) as a Real Lie Algebra#^thm-cb-6-5|Theorem §CB.6.5]]). That real split happens for the Euclidean $\mathfrak{so}(4)$, whose boosts-analogues obey $[K_i, K_j] = +i\varepsilon_{ijk}J_k$ ([[§CB.6 The Lorentz Algebra꞉ the Split J±, Real Forms and 𝔰𝔩(2,ℂ) as a Real Lie Algebra#^thm-cb-6-4|Theorem §CB.6.4]]).
>

## The simplest cases

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.2.1: The Representations (½, 0) and (0, ½)
> Both act on $\mathbb C^2$, and with the Pauli matrices $\boldsymbol\sigma$:
>
> | | $\mathbf J_+$ | $\mathbf J_-$ | $\mathbf J$ | $\mathbf K$ | $D(e^\omega)$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $(\frac12, 0)$ | $\frac12\boldsymbol\sigma$ | $0$ | $\frac12\boldsymbol\sigma$ | $-\frac i2\boldsymbol\sigma$ | $e^{-i\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2 - \boldsymbol\eta\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2}$ |
> | $(0, \frac12)$ | $0$ | $\frac12\boldsymbol\sigma$ | $\frac12\boldsymbol\sigma$ | $+\frac i2\boldsymbol\sigma$ | $e^{-i\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2 + \boldsymbol\eta\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2}$ |
>
> To first order, $D = 1 - i\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\frac{\boldsymbol\sigma}{2} \mp \boldsymbol\eta\cdot\frac{\boldsymbol\sigma}{2}$: the transformation laws of Peskin–Schroeder's left- and right-handed Weyl spinors $\psi_L$, $\psi_R$ (eq. (3.37), with their $\boldsymbol\beta = \boldsymbol\eta$).
>
> *Source: PHY 513, Problem Set 5, Problem 1(b) (as the user wrote it, with $K_i = \mathcal J^{i0}$; converted here) · PHY 513, Problem Set 7, Problem 1(d) ("left-handed Weyl spinor: $(\frac12, 0)$", in Problem Set 5's convention: [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^der-c3-2-2b|Derivation §C3.2.2b]]) · the user's PHY 513 notes, Ch. 7 §7.4.3 (Derivation "What a label (j₊, j₋) means, worked out") · PS §3.2, eq. > [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. They are representations.** Spin $\frac12$ is $\mathbb C^2$ with $\mathbf J^{(1/2)} = \frac12\boldsymbol\sigma$: from $[\sigma_i, \sigma_j] = 2i\varepsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k$, $[\frac12\sigma_i, \frac12\sigma_j] = \frac14\cdot2i\varepsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k = i\varepsilon_{ijk}\frac12\sigma_k$. Spin $0$ is $\mathbb C$ with $\mathbf J^{(0)} = 0$: a $1\times1$ matrix is a number, numbers commute, so $i\varepsilon_{ijk}J_k = [J_i, J_j] = 0$ forces $\mathbf J = 0$. The relations involving the zero copy hold trivially; $\mathbb C^2\otimes\mathbb C = \mathbb C^2$.
>
> **2. J and K.** By Def. §CB.17.3 with $\mathbf J_\mp = 0$: on $(\frac12, 0)$, $\mathbf J = \frac12\boldsymbol\sigma + 0$ and $\mathbf K = -i(\frac12\boldsymbol\sigma - 0) = -\frac i2\boldsymbol\sigma$; on $(0, \frac12)$, $\mathbf J = 0 + \frac12\boldsymbol\sigma$ and $\mathbf K = -i(0 - \frac12\boldsymbol\sigma) = +\frac i2\boldsymbol\sigma$.
>
> **3. The group elements.** $D(e^\omega) = e^{-i\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf J - i\boldsymbol\eta\cdot\mathbf K}$ (Theorem §CB.6.3). On $(\frac12, 0)$ the boost term is $-i\boldsymbol\eta\cdot(-\frac i2\boldsymbol\sigma) = (-i)(-i)\frac12\boldsymbol\eta\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = -\frac12\boldsymbol\eta\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$; on $(0, \frac12)$ it is $-i\boldsymbol\eta\cdot(\frac i2\boldsymbol\sigma) = +\frac12\boldsymbol\eta\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$. The same follows from the factorization of Theorem §CB.6.3 with one factor switched off: on $(\frac12, 0)$, $e^{-i(\boldsymbol\theta - i\boldsymbol\eta)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2} = e^{-i\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2 - \boldsymbol\eta\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2}$.
>
> **4. First order and Peskin–Schroeder.** Expanding the exponential to first order gives $1 - i\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\frac{\boldsymbol\sigma}{2} \mp \boldsymbol\eta\cdot\frac{\boldsymbol\sigma}{2}$. Peskin–Schroeder's $\boldsymbol\beta$ in (3.37) is the boost parameter of (3.21), $\omega_{01} = \beta$, i.e. $\boldsymbol\beta = \boldsymbol\eta$ with the generator $K_i = \mathcal J^{0i}$ used here; so $(\frac12, 0)$ is $\psi_L$ and $(0, \frac12)$ is $\psi_R$.
>
> **5. A boost along z, finite.** On $(\frac12, 0)$, $\boldsymbol\theta = 0$, $\boldsymbol\eta = \eta\hat{\mathbf z}$: $D = e^{-\eta\sigma_3/2} = \operatorname{diag}(e^{-\eta/2}, e^{\eta/2})$, real, Hermitian and not unitary. ⚑ By-product: the boost matrices of a finite-dimensional representation are not unitary → [[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)#^thm-cb-17-15|Theorem §CB.17.15]]. A rotation by $2\pi$ about $z$ is $e^{-i\pi\sigma_3} = -1$ ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-3|QM Theorem §C5.2.3]]), although $e^{2\pi M^{12}} = \mathbb 1$ in $SO^+(1,3)$ → Theorem §CB.17.9.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The two representations have the same rotation generators $\frac12\boldsymbol\sigma$ (both are spin $\frac12$) and opposite boost generators; they differ only in how boosts act.
> - The $2\times2$ matrices are those of a spin-$\frac12$ rotation at a complex angle; their group, $SL(2, \mathbb C)$, the matrices $\sigma^\mu$ and the Weyl equations are QFT C5a ([[§CB.1 Matrix Lie Groups, the Exponential Map and Lie Algebras#^def-cb-1-2|Def. §CB.1.2]], [[§C5a.4 SL(2,C) and the Group Action on Spinor Space#^def-c5a-4-1|Def. §C5a.4.1]], [[§C5a.1 Spinor Space and the Clifford Action#^def-c5a-1-2|Def. §C5a.1.2]], [[§C5a.7 The Dirac Equation and Its Lagrangian#^mod-c5a-7-11|Model §C5a.7.11]]).
> - Used next: parity and conjugation exchange the two (Theorems §CB.17.13–§CB.17.14), and the Dirac spinor is their sum ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^def-c3-3-2|Def. §C3.3.2]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Neither label is the spin
> "Spin $\frac12$" for rotations means a choice: the space $\mathbb C^2$ and $\mathbf J = \frac12\boldsymbol\sigma$. > [!derivation]- Derivation
> Write $E_{ab}$ for the $4\times4$ matrix with $1$ in row $a$, column $b$ ($a, b = 0, \dots, 3$) and $0$ elsewhere, so $E_{ab}E_{cd} = \delta_{bc}E_{ad}$.
>
> **1. The boost generators.** $(K_i)^\mu{}_\nu = (\mathcal J^{0i})^\mu{}_\nu = i(g^{0\mu}\delta^i{}_\nu - g^{i\mu}\delta^0{}_\nu)$. Nonzero entries: $(\mu, \nu) = (0, i)$ gives $ig^{00} = i$; $(\mu, \nu) = (i, 0)$ gives $-ig^{ii} = +i$. So $K_i = i(E_{0i} + E_{i0})$.
>
> **2. The rotation generators.** For spatial $j, k$, $(\mathcal J^{jk})^\mu{}_\nu = i(g^{j\mu}\delta^k{}_\nu - g^{k\mu}\delta^j{}_\nu)$ vanishes unless $\mu$ is spatial, and then $g^{jl} = -\delta_{jl}$: $(\mathcal J^{jk})^l{}_m = -i(\delta_{jl}\delta_{km} - \delta_{kl}\delta_{jm})$. Contract with $\frac12\varepsilon_{ijk}$: $(J_i)^l{}_m = -\frac i2(\varepsilon_{ilm} - \varepsilon_{iml}) = -i\varepsilon_{ilm}$. So $J_i = -i\varepsilon_{ilm}E_{lm}$ (sum over spatial $l, m$), the computation of [[§C3.1 Index Slots, Rotations and Spin in Field Theory#^ex-c3-1-1|Example §C3.1.1]]: on the spatial components these are Quantum Mechanics' vector-representation matrices ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^ex-c5-1-2|QM Example §C5.1.2]]).
>
> **3. J².** $(J_iJ_i)^l{}_n = \sum_{i,m}(-i\varepsilon_{ilm})(-i\varepsilon_{imn}) = -\sum_{i,m}\varepsilon_{ilm}\varepsilon_{imn}$. With $\varepsilon_{imn} = -\varepsilon_{inm}$ and $\sum_{i,m}\varepsilon_{ilm}\varepsilon_{inm} = 2\delta_{ln}$ ([[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-3|Theorem §C1a.5.3]], three-dimensional form), this is $+2\delta_{ln}$; the time row and column are zero. So $\mathbf J^2 = \operatorname{diag}(0, 2, 2, 2)$.
>
> **4. K².** $K_i^2 = i^2(E_{0i} + E_{i0})^2 = -(E_{0i}E_{0i} + E_{0i}E_{i0} + E_{i0}E_{0i} + E_{i0}E_{i0}) = -(0 + E_{00} + E_{ii} + 0)$, since $i \ne 0$. Summing over $i = 1, 2, 3$: $\mathbf K^2 = -(3E_{00} + E_{11} + E_{22} + E_{33}) = -\operatorname{diag}(3, 1, 1, 1)$.
>
> **5. J·K and K·J.** $J_iK_i = (-i\varepsilon_{ilm}E_{lm})\,i(E_{0i} + E_{i0}) = \varepsilon_{ilm}(E_{lm}E_{0i} + E_{lm}E_{i0}) = \varepsilon_{ilm}(0 + \delta_{mi}E_{l0}) = \varepsilon_{ili}E_{l0} = 0$ ($m$ is spatial, so $E_{lm}E_{0i} = 0$). $K_iJ_i = i(E_{0i} + E_{i0})(-i\varepsilon_{ilm}E_{lm}) = \varepsilon_{ilm}(\delta_{il}E_{0m} + 0) = \varepsilon_{iim}E_{0m} = 0$.
>
> **6. The Casimirs.** Expand $\mathbf J_\pm^2 = \frac14(\mathbf J \pm i\mathbf K)\cdot(\mathbf J \pm i\mathbf K)$ into its four terms: $\frac14\bigl(\mathbf J^2 \pm i\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf K \pm i\mathbf K\cdot\mathbf J - \mathbf K^2\bigr) = \frac14\bigl(\operatorname{diag}(0, 2, 2, 2) + \operatorname{diag}(3, 1, 1, 1)\bigr) = \frac34\,\mathbb 1$, by steps 3–5.
>
> **7. Identify.** By Theorem §CB.17.6 the vector representation is a sum of pieces $(j_+, j_-)$, and on each piece $\mathbf J_\pm^2 = j_\pm(j_\pm + 1)$ (Theorem §CB.17.5). Step 6 forces $j_\pm(j_\pm + 1) = \frac34$ on every piece, i.e. $j_+ = j_- = \frac12$; each such piece has dimension $2\cdot2 = 4 = \dim\mathbb C^4$, so there is exactly one. ⚑ By-product: step 3 is the rotation content directly: $s(s+1) = 0$ on $v^0$ and $2$ on $\mathbf v$, so a four-vector is spin $0 \oplus 1$ under rotations ([[§C3.1 Index Slots, Rotations and Spin in Field Theory#^ex-c3-1-1|Example §C3.1.1]]), as Theorem §CB.17.9 predicts for $\frac12\otimes\frac12$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The four-vector is irreducible under the Lorentz group (a single $(\frac12, \frac12)$) although it is reducible under rotations (time plus space): boosts mix $v^0$ with $\mathbf v$.
> - Time and space are not $\mathbf J_+$ and $\mathbf J_-$: every vector of $\mathbb C^4$ has $\mathbf J_+^2 = \mathbf J_-^2 = \frac34$; the split into two spins $\frac12$ is a tensor product $\mathbb C^2\otimes\mathbb C^2$, realized by $v^\mu \mapsto v_\mu\sigma^\mu$ ([[§C5a.5 Chirality and Weyl Spinors#^thm-c5a-5-5|Theorem §C5a.5.5]]).
> - Used next: vector fields ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-5|Theorem §C3.3.5]]) and two-index tensors as $(\frac12, \frac12)\otimes(\frac12, \frac12)$ (Theorem §CB.17.12).

## Parity on the indices of fields

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.2.3: Parity on Four-Vectors; Parity-Invariant Fields Contain Both Copies
> 1. In the vector representation the parity automorphism $\pi$: $\mathbf J \mapsto \mathbf J$, $\mathbf K \mapsto -\mathbf K$ of [[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)#^thm-cb-17-13|Theorem §CB.17.13]] is conjugation by $\mathcal P = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$: $\mathcal P\mathcal J^{ij}\mathcal P^{-1} = \mathcal J^{ij}$, $\mathcal P\mathcal J^{0i}\mathcal P^{-1} = -\mathcal J^{0i}$.
> 2. If parity acts on the index of a field by $Q = D(\mathcal P)$, parity in a representation $D$ of the full Lorentz group, then $Q\mathbf JQ^{-1} = \mathbf J$ and $Q\mathbf KQ^{-1} = -\mathbf K$, so the field contains $(j_+, j_-)$ and $(j_-, j_+)$ with equal multiplicity ([[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)#^thm-cb-17-13|Theorem §CB.17.13]], 3).
>

## Rotation and boost weights of field components

In every representation $(j_+, j_-)$ the vectors $|m_+\rangle\otimes|m_-\rangle$ of [[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)#^def-cb-17-3|Def. §CB.17.3]] are joint eigenvectors of $J_{+3}$ and $J_{-3}$; a field's component along such a vector (the coefficient in the expansion, an array entry, not a state: [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^cau-c3-2-3|Caution: Components, not states]]) is multiplied by $e^{-i\theta(m_+ + m_-)}$ under a rotation by $\theta$ about $z$ and by $e^{-\eta(m_+ - m_-)}$ under a boost of rapidity $\eta$ along $z$ ([[§CB.6 The Lorentz Algebra꞉ the Split J±, Real Forms and 𝔰𝔩(2,ℂ) as a Real Lie Algebra#^thm-cb-6-10|Theorem §CB.6.10]]). Here this is read on the four-vector, on $(1, 0)$, and used to tell the four-vector from $(1, 0)\oplus(0, 0)$.

> [!example] Example §C3.2.1: The Four-Vector under a Rotation about z and a Boost along z, Component by Component

> | entry | $(m_+, m_-)$ | rotation weight $m_+ + m_-$ | boost weight $m_+ - m_-$ | rotation by $\theta$ about $z$ | boost $\eta$ along $z$ | components |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $c_{11}$ | $(\frac12, \frac12)$ | $1$ | $0$ | $e^{-i\theta}$ | $1$ | $A^1 - iA^2$ |
> | $c_{12}$ | $(\frac12, -\frac12)$ | $0$ | $1$ | $1$ | $e^{-\eta}$ | $A^0 - A^3$ |

> | $c_{22}$ | $(-\frac12, -\frac12)$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $e^{i\theta}$ | $1$ | $-(A^1 + iA^2)$ |

> Find the matrix of a boost of rapidity $\eta$ along $z$ on $(1, 0)$, and compare it with the same boost on the four-vector.
>
> *The representation.* $(1, 0)$ is $\mathbb C^3$ with $\mathbf J_+$ the spin-1 matrices and $\mathbf J_- = 0$ ([[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)#^def-cb-17-3|Def. §CB.17.3]]); in the Cartesian basis $(J^{(1)}_k)_{lm} = -i\varepsilon_{klm}$ ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^ex-c5-1-2|QM Example §C5.1.2]]; the spatial block of [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-6-5|Theorem §C1a.6.5]]). So $\mathbf J = \mathbf J^{(1)}$, $\mathbf K = -i\mathbf J^{(1)}$, and by [[§CB.6 The Lorentz Algebra꞉ the Split J±, Real Forms and 𝔰𝔩(2,ℂ) as a Real Lie Algebra#^thm-cb-6-3|Theorem §CB.6.3]] with the second factor trivial, $D(e^\omega) = e^{-i(\boldsymbol\theta - i\boldsymbol\eta)\cdot\mathbf J^{(1)}}$: the rotation matrix at the complex angle $\boldsymbol\theta - i\boldsymbol\eta$. Infinitesimally $\delta\mathbf v = (\boldsymbol\theta - i\boldsymbol\eta)\times\mathbf v$, since $-i\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf J^{(1)}\mathbf v = \boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf v$.
>
> *The boost along z.* With $\boldsymbol\theta = 0$, $\boldsymbol\eta = \eta\hat{\mathbf z}$: $D = e^{-i(-i\eta)J^{(1)}_3}$, which is $R_z(\theta) = e^{-i\theta J^{(1)}_3}$ at $\theta = -i\eta$. Step 4 of [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^der-c1a-6-6|Derivation §C1a.6.6]] gives $e^{-i\theta J^{(1)}_3} = P + \cos\theta\,Q + \sin\theta\,A$ ($P = \operatorname{diag}(0, 0, 1)$, $Q = \operatorname{diag}(1, 1, 0)$, $A = E_{21} - E_{12}$) using only $A^2 = -Q$, $A^3 = -A$, so the identity of power series holds for complex $\theta$ too. With $\cos(-i\eta) = \cosh\eta$ and $\sin(-i\eta) = -i\sinh\eta$:
>
> $$
> R_z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} \ \xrightarrow{\ \theta = -i\eta\ }\ D_{(1,0)}(\text{boost}) = \begin{pmatrix} \cosh\eta & i\sinh\eta & 0 \\ -i\sinh\eta & \cosh\eta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> *Weights.* $(Dv)_x - i(Dv)_y = (\cosh\eta\,v_x + i\sinh\eta\,v_y) - i(-i\sinh\eta\,v_x + \cosh\eta\,v_y) = (\cosh\eta - \sinh\eta)(v_x - iv_y) = e^{-\eta}(v_x - iv_y)$; likewise $v_x + iv_y \mapsto e^{\eta}(v_x + iv_y)$, and $v_z$ is unchanged. Under $R_z(\theta)$, $v_x \mp iv_y \mapsto e^{\mp i\theta}(v_x \mp iv_y)$: rotation weights $m = \pm1$, and with $m_- = 0$ the boost weights $m_+ - m_- = \pm1$, as [[§CB.6 The Lorentz Algebra꞉ the Split J±, Real Forms and 𝔰𝔩(2,ℂ) as a Real Lie Algebra#^thm-cb-6-10|Theorem §CB.6.10]] requires.
>
> | the same boost along $z$ | four-vector $(\frac12, \frac12)$ (Example §C3.2.1) | $(1, 0)$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | components | $4$: $t, x, y, z$ | $3$: $x, y, z$ |
> | the boost mixes | $t$ with $z$ | $x$ with $y$ |
> | it leaves alone | $x$, $y$ | $z$ |
> | matrix entries | real | complex |
> | $m = \pm1$ components | $A^1 \mp iA^2$ unchanged | $v_x \mp iv_y$ scaled by $e^{\mp\eta}$ |
> | $m = 0$ components | $A^0 \mp A^3$ scaled by $e^{\mp\eta}$ | $v_z$ unchanged |
>
> A real $\mathbf v$ cannot stay real: for real $\mathbf v$, $\delta\mathbf v = -i\boldsymbol\eta\times\mathbf v$ is imaginary. The field that carries $(1, 0)$ is complex: $\mathbf E - i\mathbf B$, whose real partner $\mathbf E + i\mathbf B$ is $(0, 1)$ ([[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]]). Check: the real part of $(\mathbf E - i\mathbf B)'_x = \cosh\eta\,(E_x - iB_x) + i\sinh\eta\,(E_y - iB_y)$ is $E'_x = \cosh\eta\,E_x + \sinh\eta\,B_y$, the boost of [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^ex-c1a-7-1|Example §C1a.7.1]] ($E'_x = \gamma(E_x + vB_y)$).
>
> *Source: the user's Lorentz-representation review (2026-10-14), §7 (Derivation "$(1, 0)$: a boost is a rotation by an imaginary angle", with its comparison table) · the power-series argument and the checks written out here*

^ex-c3-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.2.4: The Four-Vector Is Not (1, 0) ⊕ (0, 0): Boosts Decide What Rotations Cannot
> The vector representation ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^def-c1a-6-1|Def. §C1a.6.1]]) and $(1, 0)\oplus(0, 0)$ have the same dimension $4$, the same rotation content $0\oplus1$ and the same sign $+1$ of a $2\pi$ rotation ([[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)#^thm-cb-17-9|Theorem §CB.17.9]]), but they are not equivalent. Two tests separate them:
> 1. **Boost weights.** $J_3$ and $K_3$ commute; their joint eigenvalue pairs $(m_+ + m_-,\ -i(m_+ - m_-))$ ([[§CB.6 The Lorentz Algebra꞉ the Split J±, Real Forms and 𝔰𝔩(2,ℂ) as a Real Lie Algebra#^thm-cb-6-10|Theorem §CB.6.10]]) are $(\pm1, 0)$, $(0, \pm i)$ on the vector and $(\pm1, \mp i)$, $(0, 0)$, $(0, 0)$ on $(1, 0)\oplus(0, 0)$. In components: a boost along $z$ mixes $A^0$ with $A^3$ and leaves $A^1$, $A^2$ unchanged, while on $(1, 0)\oplus(0, 0)$ it leaves both $m = 0$ components unchanged and rescales the $m = \pm1$ ones.
> 2. **Complex conjugation.** The vector representation is real, hence equivalent to its complex conjugate; the conjugate of $(1, 0)\oplus(0, 0)$ is $(0, 1)\oplus(0, 0)$ ([[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)#^thm-cb-17-14|Theorem §CB.17.14]]), which is not equivalent to it.
>
> The same holds with $(0, 1)$ in place of $(1, 0)$.
>
> *Source: the user's Lorentz-representation review (2026-10-14), §7 (Principle "Tests": dimension, rotations cannot decide, boosts decide, reality and parity) · the invariance of the joint eigenvalue pairs and the conjugation argument written out here*

^thm-c3-2-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The common invariants.** Dimensions: $(2\cdot\frac12 + 1)^2 = 4$ and $3\cdot1 + 1\cdot1 = 4$ (Theorem §CB.17.5). Rotation content: $\frac12\otimes\frac12 = 0\oplus1$, and $1\otimes0\ \oplus\ 0\otimes0 = 1\oplus0$ (Theorem §CB.17.9, 1). The $2\pi$ rotation: $(-1)^{2(j_+ + j_-)} = +1$ on $(\frac12, \frac12)$, $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 0)$ (Theorem §CB.17.9, 2). None of these separates the two.
>
> **2. Joint pairs on the vector.** By [[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-6-5|Theorem §C1a.6.5]] (step 5 of its derivation), $J_3$ has eigenvalues $\pm1$ on $e_1 \pm ie_2$ and $0$ on $e_0$, $e_3$; $K_3$ has $\pm i$ on $e_0 \pm e_3$ and $0$ on $e_1$, $e_2$. The basis $e_1 \pm ie_2$, $e_0 \pm e_3$ diagonalizes both: $J_3(e_1 \pm ie_2) = \pm(e_1 \pm ie_2)$, $K_3(e_1 \pm ie_2) = 0$; $J_3(e_0 \pm e_3) = 0$, $K_3(e_0 \pm e_3) = \pm i(e_0 \pm e_3)$. Pairs: $(\pm1, 0)$ and $(0, \pm i)$. In the language of Example §C3.2.1: $(m_+, m_-) = (\pm\frac12, \pm\frac12)$ and $(\mp\frac12, \pm\frac12)$, with $K_3 = -i(m_+ - m_-)$.
>
> **3. Joint pairs on (1, 0) ⊕ (0, 0).** On $(1, 0)$, $\mathbf J_- = 0$, so $m_- = 0$ and $m_+ \in \{1, 0, -1\}$ on the vectors $|m_+\rangle\otimes|0\rangle$; with $J_3 = J_{+3}$ and $K_3 = -iJ_{+3}$ (Def. §CB.17.3), the pairs are $(1, -i)$, $(0, 0)$, $(-1, i)$. On $(0, 0)$ all generators vanish: the pair $(0, 0)$.
>
> **4. The pairs are invariants.** Suppose an invertible $U$ intertwines, $UJ_3 = J_3'U$ and $UK_3 = K_3'U$ ([[§CB.7 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^def-cb-7-2|Def. §CB.7.2]]). If $J_3v = av$ and $K_3v = bv$, then $J_3'(Uv) = UJ_3v = a\,Uv$ and $K_3'(Uv) = b\,Uv$: $U$ maps the joint eigenspace for $(a, b)$ into that for $(a, b)$, injectively, and $U^{-1}$ maps back. So equivalent representations have the same joint eigenvalue pairs with the same multiplicities. The lists of steps 2 and 3 differ (e.g. $(0, i)$ occurs in the first and not in the second), so the representations are not equivalent. ⚑ By-product: the spectra of $J_3$ alone ($1, 0, 0, -1$) and of $K_3$ alone ($i, -i, 0, 0$) are the same in both; only the pairing of rotation weight with boost weight differs.
>
> **5. In components.** For the vector, $e^{-i\eta K_3} = \Lambda_z(\eta)$ mixes $A^0$ and $A^3$ ([[§C1a.6 Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations and Generators#^thm-c1a-6-7|Theorem §C1a.6.7]]; Example §C3.2.1). On $(1, 0)\oplus(0, 0)$, by Theorem §CB.6.10 the two $m = 0$ vectors have boost weight $0$ and are fixed by every boost along $z$, while the $m = \pm1$ vectors are multiplied by $e^{\mp\eta}$ (Example §C3.2.2): a would-be "time component" in the $(0, 0)$ piece would be boost invariant.
>
> **6. Part 2.** The matrices $\Lambda$ are real, so the conjugate representation $\Lambda \mapsto \Lambda^\ast = \Lambda$ ([[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)#^thm-cb-17-14|Theorem §CB.17.14]], step 1) is the vector representation itself. By Theorem §CB.17.14 the conjugate of $(1, 0)\oplus(0, 0)$ is equivalent to $(0, 1)\oplus(0, 0)$, and by the uniqueness of multiplicities ([[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)#^thm-cb-17-6|Theorem §CB.17.6]]) $(0, 1)\oplus(0, 0) \not\cong (1, 0)\oplus(0, 0)$. If the vector were equivalent to $(1, 0)\oplus(0, 0)$ by $U$, conjugating entrywise, $\bar U$ would make it equivalent to $(0, 1)\oplus(0, 0)$ too, a contradiction.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Rotation content does not determine a Lorentz representation; the pairs (rotation weight, boost weight) do separate these two. The four-vector is $(\frac12, \frac12)$ by [[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]]; this theorem shows directly that the other candidate with the same rotation content fails. The other two four-dimensional candidates, $(\frac32, 0)$ and $(0, \frac32)$, fail the $2\pi$ test ([[§C3.2 The Lorentz Algebra and the Representations (j₊, j₋) of Fields#^der-c3-2-2b|Derivation §C3.2.2b]]), and $(\frac12, 0)\oplus(0, \frac12)$ fails it too ([[§CB.17 The Lorentz Case II꞉ the Representations (j₊, j₋)#^rem-cb-17-2|§CB.17, Remark: Sums versus products]]).
> - The freedom is in the particle, not in the field: a massive spin-1 particle can be described by a vector field $A^\mu \in (\frac12, \frac12)$ or by an antisymmetric tensor field in $(1, 0)\oplus(0, 1)$; each field's label is fixed by its own transformation law.

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The Casimirs of the two copies are the Casimirs of the Lorentz algebra in disguise: since $[J_i, K_i] = 0$, $\mathbf J_+^2 + \mathbf J_-^2 = \frac12(\mathbf J^2 - \mathbf K^2) = \frac14 D(\mathcal J_{\mu\nu})D(\mathcal J^{\mu\nu})$ and $\mathbf J_+^2 - \mathbf J_-^2 = i\,\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf K$, the same pattern as the two invariants $\mathbf E^2 - \mathbf B^2$ and $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B$ of the field strength, which combine into the single complex invariant $(\mathbf E + i\mathbf B)^2$ — [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1a-7-2|Theorem §C1a.7.2]], [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1a-7-3|Theorem §C1a.7.3]].
> - The split of the generators into $\mathbf J \pm i\mathbf K$ and the split of the field strength into $\mathbf E \pm i\mathbf B$ are one phenomenon: the duality $\star$ squares to $-1$ on antisymmetric tensors, so its eigenvalues are $\pm i$, and the generators themselves form an antisymmetric tensor (the adjoint representation) — [[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-6|Theorem §C1a.5.6]], [[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]].
> - The spinor representations defined here by the sign of the $2\pi$ rotation are realized concretely on spinor space: the Clifford action builds the generators, and the Dirac representation is the smallest parity-symmetric spinor representation, $(\frac12, 0)\oplus(0, \frac12)$ — [[§C5a.3 The Lorentz Action on Spinor Space|QFT §C5a.3]], [[§C5a.3 The Lorentz Action on Spinor Space#^thm-c5a-3-2|QFT Theorem §C5a.3.2]].
> - Every tool of the classification comes from Quantum Mechanics' angular momentum: the spectrum and ladders for irreducibility, the Clebsch–Gordan series for rotation content and products, the $(-1)^{2j}$ of a $2\pi$ rotation for statistics' sign; only the doubling into two commuting copies is new — [[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-4|QM Theorem §C5.3.4]], [[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-2|QM Theorem §C7.1.2]].
> - The nine-plus-three-plus-three-plus-one decomposition of a two-index Lorentz tensor is the relativistic upgrade of Quantum Mechanics' decomposition of a Cartesian dyadic into ranks $0$, $1$, $2$ ($9 = 1 + 3 + 5$): the symmetric traceless $(1, 1)$ contains spin $2$ — [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-2|QM Theorem §C7.2.2]], [[§C1a.5 Vectors, Tensors and Index Notation#^thm-c1a-5-5|Theorem §C1a.5.5]].
> - That $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ is a compact group with the same complexified algebra as the noncompact Lorentz group is what makes every finite-dimensional representation completely reducible; the same trick fails for the unitary representations on states, which need the infinite-dimensional theory of Wigner — [[§CB.7 Representations꞉ Intertwiners, Schur's Lemma, Complete Reducibility and Casimirs#^thm-cb-7-16|Theorem §CB.7.16]], [[§C3.6★ Particle States and the Little Group#^pr-c3-6-1|Principle §C3.6.1]].
> - The $(-1)^{2(j_+ + j_-)}$ of a $2\pi$ rotation is the sign that the spin–statistics theorem ties to Fermi statistics: half-integer representations will be quantized with anticommutators ([[§C5b.1 Canonical Quantization of the Dirac Field#^pr-c5b-1-7|Principle §C5b.1.7]]) — [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-3|QM Theorem §C5.2.3]].
> - Non-unitarity of the index representations and unitarity of the state representation are reconciled in the field law $U(\Lambda)^{-1}\hat\Phi U(\Lambda) = D(\Lambda)\hat\Phi(\Lambda^{-1}x)$, where a unitary operator on states produces a non-unitary matrix on indices — [[§C3.5 Covariant Quantum Fields and the Free Scalar's Representation#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]], [[§C3.3 How Fields Transform under the Lorentz Group#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]].
