Chapters A1–A6 dealt with charges at rest and the fields they make, and with steady currents driven through circuits by those fields. A charge that *moves* feels a second force, which does not exist for a charge at rest: the magnetic force. This section states that force, defines the magnetic field through it, and describes how magnetic fields are pictured.

## Magnets and currents

> [!remark] Remark: What was known before the field
> - Every magnet has two poles, north (N) and south (S). Like poles repel, unlike poles attract, and both poles attract unmagnetized iron. A pole cannot be isolated: cutting a magnet gives two smaller magnets, each with an N and an S pole.
> - A compass needle is a thin bar magnet free to turn in a horizontal plane. Its north pole is, by definition, the end attracted toward Earth's geographic North Pole.
> - In 1819–1820 Oersted saw a compass needle deflect when a current flowed in a nearby wire: currents act on magnets. Within a few years Biot and Savart measured the forces of currents on magnets, Ampère the forces between currents, and Faraday found that a moving magnet induces a current (Chapter A9).
> - Ampère proposed that all magnetism comes from currents, permanent magnets containing aligned microscopic current loops. The modern picture (aligned electron spins, [[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter|§A8.6]]) is close to his.

^rem-a7-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Earth's "north magnetic pole" is a south pole
> The north pole of a compass is attracted to the magnetic pole near the geographic North Pole, and unlike poles attract. So the magnetic pole in the Arctic is a magnetic **south** pole; calling it the "north magnetic pole" names its location, not its polarity. (Earth's field also reverses over long geological times.) The PHY 182 slides add that N and S name *regions* of a dipole field, not its sources; the sources are currents ([[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop|§A8.3]]).

## The magnetic force and the field

> [!definition] Definition §A7.1.1: Magnetic Field; Tesla
> At a point where a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ feels a force that, after the gravitational and electric forces have been accounted for, is
> - proportional to $q$, to the speed $v$ and to $\sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec v$ and a fixed direction, and
> - perpendicular both to $\vec v$ and to that direction,
>
> the **magnetic field** $\vec B$ is the vector along that line whose magnitude and sense make the force equal to $q\,\vec v \times \vec B$. Its SI unit is the **tesla**,
>
> $$
> 1\ \mathrm T = 1\ \frac{\mathrm N}{\mathrm{A\cdot m}} = 1\ \frac{\mathrm{N\cdot s}}{\mathrm{C\cdot m}} ;
> $$
>
> a smaller unit is the **gauss**, $1\ \mathrm G = 10^{-4}\ \mathrm T$. Earth's field at its surface is about $5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm T$ ($0.5\ \mathrm G$); the strongest permanent magnets give about $2\ \mathrm T$, superconducting electromagnets $10\ \mathrm T$ or more.
>
> *Source: UP2 §11.2, eqs. (11.1)–(11.3)*

^def-a7-1-1

> [!principle] Principle §A7.1.1: Magnetic Force on a Moving Charge
> A charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ in a magnetic field $\vec B$ feels the force
>
> $$
> \vec F = q\,\vec v \times \vec B, \qquad F = |q|\,vB\sin\theta ,
> $$
>
> where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec v$ and $\vec B$. Its direction is given by the right-hand rule for the cross product: curl the fingers of the right hand from $\vec v$ toward $\vec B$ through the smaller angle; the thumb points along $\vec F$ if $q > 0$ and opposite to it if $q < 0$. If an electric field is also present, the forces add: $\vec F = q\vec E + q\,\vec v\times\vec B$ (the Lorentz force; $q\vec E$ from [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^def-a1-3-1|Def. §A1.3.1]]).
>
> *Domain: any source of $\vec B$ (magnets, currents); $\vec v$ and $\vec B$ measured in the same inertial frame; speeds well below $c$ when combined with $\vec F = m\vec a$ ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-2|REL Def. §A3.1.2]]).*
> *Source: UP2 §11.2, eqs. (11.1)–(11.2); PHY 182, week 10 slides*

^pr-a7-1-1

![[ph-em-a7-1-2.svg]]
*The magnetic force is perpendicular to the grey plane spanned by $\vec v$ and $\vec B$: fingers from $\vec v$ to $\vec B$, thumb along $\vec F$ (red) for a positive charge; for a negative charge the force is reversed (dashed).*

> [!remark] Remark: Electric and magnetic forces side by side
> | | electric | magnetic |
> |---|---|---|
> | feels it | any charge | a *moving* charge |
> | direction | along $\vec E$ (or against it) | perpendicular to both $\vec v$ and $\vec B$ |
> | size | $\lvert q\rvert E$ | $\lvert q\rvert vB\sin\theta$: zero at rest and for motion along $\vec B$ |
> | field unit | $\mathrm{N/C}$ | $\mathrm{T} = \mathrm{N/(C\cdot m/s)}$ |
>
> The PHY 182 slides summarize magnetism in two parallel sentences: moving charges *make* magnetic fields ([[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law|§A8.1]]), and moving charges *feel* them (this section). The extra factor $v$ is why $\vec B$ has a different unit from $\vec E$.
>
> Everything else about magnetic forces is derived from Principle §A7.1.1: the circular and helical orbits ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]]), the force on a current-carrying wire ([[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]) and the torque on a loop built from it, the Hall effect, and the motional emf of a conductor moving through a field ([[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-2|Theorem §A9.3.2]]).

^rem-a7-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Order, sign and angle
> - $\vec v \times \vec B = -\vec B \times \vec v$: sweep from $\vec v$ to $\vec B$, not the other way ([[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^cau-a1-5-1|CM §A1.5]]).
> - For a negative charge (an electron) reverse the direction found with the right hand. Using the left hand by mistake also reverses it.
> - $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec v$ and $\vec B$. A component of $\vec v$ along $\vec B$ contributes nothing; only $v_\perp = v\sin\theta$ counts.
> - A particle moving in a straight line need not be in zero field: it may move along $\vec B$, or an electric force may cancel the magnetic one ([[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^thm-a7-5-1|Theorem §A7.5.1]]).

^cau-a7-1-2

## Field lines

> [!definition] Definition §A7.1.2: Magnetic Field Lines
> **Magnetic field lines** are curves drawn so that
> 1. the field at each point is tangent to the line through it (a small compass needle points along it);
> 2. the field's magnitude is proportional to the number of lines per unit area perpendicular to them;
> 3. lines never cross, since the field has one direction at each point;
> 4. lines are continuous and form closed loops: outside a magnet they run from the N pole to the S pole, inside it from S back to N.
>
> *Source: UP2 §11.2*

^def-a7-1-2

![[ph-em-a7-1-1.svg]]
*Computed field lines of a bar magnet (a uniformly magnetized cylinder four times as long as it is wide), in a plane through its axis. Outside, the lines leave the N end and enter the S end; inside, they run from S to N, so every line closes on itself. The lines are crowded inside the magnet, where the field is strongest.*

> [!remark] Remark: No magnetic charges
> Property 4 is the real difference from electric field lines, which begin on positive and end on negative charges ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^def-a1-3-3|Def. §A1.3.3]]). Isolated magnetic poles (**magnetic monopoles**) would be magnetic charges on which lines could begin and end; none have been found, which is the content of "a pole cannot be isolated". In the language of flux ([[§A2.1 Electric Flux#^def-a2-1-3|Def. §A2.1.3]]): every line that enters a closed surface also leaves it, so the net magnetic flux through any closed surface is zero, $\oint \vec B\cdot d\vec A = 0$ — "Gauss's law for magnetism", true but of no use for computing fields. It becomes one of Maxwell's equations, [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|Principle §A12.1.3]].

## Example

> [!example] Example §A7.1.1: An Alpha Particle in a Uniform Field
> An alpha particle, $q = 3.2\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm C$, moves in the field $\vec B = 1.5\ \mathrm T\ \hat k$. Find the force for several velocities, with [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]] and $\hat\imath\times\hat k = -\hat\jmath$, $\hat\jmath\times\hat k = \hat\imath$, $\hat k\times\hat k = \vec 0$.
> - $\vec v = 5.0\times10^4\ \mathrm{m/s}\ \hat\imath$: $\ \vec F = qvB\,(\hat\imath\times\hat k) = -(3.2\times10^{-19})(5.0\times10^4)(1.5)\ \mathrm N\ \hat\jmath = -2.4\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm N\ \hat\jmath$.
> - $\vec v = -5.0\times10^4\ \mathrm{m/s}\ \hat\jmath$: $\ \vec F = -2.4\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm N\ \hat\imath$. Same size, since again $\theta = 90^\circ$.
> - $\vec v$ along $+\hat k$: $\vec F = \vec 0$.
> - $\vec v = (2.0\,\hat\imath - 3.0\,\hat\jmath + 1.0\,\hat k)\times10^4\ \mathrm{m/s}$: the $\hat k$ part drops out, and
>
> $$
> \vec F = q\,(1.5\times10^4)\bigl[2.0\,(-\hat\jmath) - 3.0\,\hat\imath\bigr]\ \mathrm N = (-14.4\,\hat\imath - 9.6\,\hat\jmath)\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm N ,
> $$
>
> of magnitude $1.7\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm N$, in the $xy$-plane at $214^\circ$ from the $+x$ axis ($34^\circ$ below the $-x$ axis). Check: $v_\perp = \sqrt{2.0^2+3.0^2}\times10^4\ \mathrm{m/s} = 3.6\times10^4\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $qv_\perp B = 1.7\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm N$.
>
> The force is always perpendicular to $\vec B$, whatever $\vec v$ is; and it is about $4\times10^{11}$ times the alpha particle's weight ($m = 6.64\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$), so gravity is negligible for such particles.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 11.1*
