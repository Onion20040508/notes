A closed current loop in a uniform field feels no net force ([[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-2|Theorem §A7.3.2]]), but it does feel a torque that turns it to face the field. This section computes that torque, packs the loop's current, area and orientation into one vector, the magnetic dipole moment, and finds the orientation energy that goes with the torque. A loop on an axle whose current is reversed every half turn is a direct-current motor.

## Torque on a rectangular loop

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.4.1: Torque on a Rectangular Loop
> A rectangular loop with sides $a$ and $b$, area $A = ab$, carrying current $I$, can turn about an axis through its centre parallel to the sides of length $b$; a uniform field $\vec B$ is perpendicular to that axis. Let $\theta$ be the angle between $\vec B$ and the normal to the loop. Then the net force on the loop is zero and the magnetic torque has magnitude
>
> $$
> \tau = IAB\sin\theta ,
> $$
>
> the same about every axis parallel to the given one. It turns the loop so as to reduce $\theta$: the loop turns until its plane is perpendicular to $\vec B$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §11.5, eqs. (11.14)–(11.19)*

^thm-a7-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply $\vec F = I\,\vec l\times\vec B$ ([[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]) to each side.
> - The two sides of length $a$ carry opposite currents in the plane perpendicular to the axis. Their forces are equal and opposite, along the axis, and act along the same line, so they contribute neither net force nor torque.
> - The two sides of length $b$ (sides 2 and 4, parallel to the axis) are perpendicular to $\vec B$ and carry opposite currents: forces of equal magnitude $IbB$ in opposite directions, both perpendicular to $\vec B$ and to the axis. Net force: zero.
> - Each of these two forces acts at distance $a/2$ from the axis. Seen along the axis, the side's position vector lies in the plane of the loop, at angle $90^\circ - \theta$ to $\vec B$, and the force is perpendicular to $\vec B$, so the lever arm is $\tfrac a2\sin\theta$. Both torques turn the loop the same way:
>
> $$
> \tau = 2\,(IbB)\,\frac a2\sin\theta = IabB\sin\theta = IAB\sin\theta .
> $$
>
> With no net force, the torque is the same about any parallel axis (UP2 checks this by putting the axis at an arbitrary distance $x$ from side 2: the $x$-dependence cancels).

^der-a7-4-1

## Magnetic dipole moment

> [!definition] Definition §A7.4.1: Magnetic Dipole Moment
> A flat loop of area $A$ carrying current $I$ is a **magnetic dipole**. Its **magnetic dipole moment** is
>
> $$
> \vec\mu = IA\,\hat n ,
> $$
>
> where the unit normal $\hat n$ is given by the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of the right hand along the current, and the thumb points along $\hat n$. A flat coil of $N$ turns has $\vec\mu = NIA\,\hat n$. The unit is $\mathrm{A\cdot m^2}$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §11.5, eqs. (11.20)–(11.21)*

^def-a7-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.4.2: Torque on a Magnetic Dipole
> A flat current loop of any shape with moment $\vec\mu$ in a uniform field $\vec B$ feels zero net force and the torque
>
> $$
> \vec\tau = \vec\mu\times\vec B, \qquad \tau = \mu B\sin\theta ,
> $$
>
> with $\theta$ the angle between $\vec\mu$ and $\vec B$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §11.5, eq. (11.22)*

^thm-a7-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Rectangle.* With $\mu = IA$, [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-1|Theorem §A7.4.1]] gives the magnitude $\mu B\sin\theta$. For the direction, in the figure $\vec\mu$ points down-right and $\vec B$ right, so $\vec\mu\times\vec B$ points out of the page: a counterclockwise torque, the sense in which the forces turn the loop. For $N$ turns every force, and so the torque, is $N$ times larger.
>
> *Rectangle, field in any direction.* Split $\vec B$ into its components along $\hat n$ and along the two pairs of sides. The normal component gives forces in the plane of the loop, equal and opposite on opposite sides and acting along the same line through the centre: no torque, and also $\hat n\times\hat n = \vec 0$. A component along one pair of sides is perpendicular to the other pair, so [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-1|Theorem §A7.4.1]] applies with the axis along that other pair and $\theta = 90^\circ$, giving $IA\,\hat n\times\vec B_{\text{comp}}$ (the direction checked as above). The force $I\,\vec l\times\vec B$ is linear in $\vec B$, so these torques add to $IA\,\hat n\times\vec B = \vec\mu\times\vec B$.
>
> *Any flat loop.* Cover the area inside the loop with a fine grid of small rectangles and imagine the current $I$ circulating around each in the same sense as around the loop. On every interior edge two neighbouring rectangles carry opposite currents, whose forces cancel; what remains is the current around the outer boundary, which approximates the loop as the grid is refined. So the torque on the loop is the sum of the torques on the small rectangles, $\sum_i I A_i\,\hat n\times\vec B = I\bigl(\sum_i A_i\bigr)\hat n\times\vec B = \vec\mu\times\vec B$. The net force is zero by [[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-2|Theorem §A7.3.2]].

^der-a7-4-2

> [!caution] Caution: Which angle
> $\theta$ is measured between $\vec\mu$ (the normal) and $\vec B$, not between the plane of the loop and $\vec B$. The torque is largest, $\mu B$, when the plane of the loop is *parallel* to $\vec B$, and zero when the plane is perpendicular to $\vec B$ ($\vec\mu$ parallel or antiparallel to $\vec B$).

^cau-a7-4-1

## Orientation energy

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.4.3: Potential Energy of a Magnetic Dipole
> A magnetic dipole in a uniform field has the orientation potential energy
>
> $$
> U = -\vec\mu\cdot\vec B = -\mu B\cos\theta ,
> $$
>
> with the zero at $\theta = 90^\circ$. It is lowest, $-\mu B$, when $\vec\mu$ is along $\vec B$ (stable equilibrium) and highest, $+\mu B$, when $\vec\mu$ is opposite to $\vec B$ (unstable equilibrium).
>
> *Source: UP2 §11.5, eq. (11.23)*

^thm-a7-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The torque of [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-2|Theorem §A7.4.2]] acts to decrease $\theta$, so when the angle increases by $d\theta$ the magnetic torque does the rotational work $dW = -\mu B\sin\theta\,d\theta$. The torque depends on $\theta$ alone, so this work defines a potential energy with $dU = -dW$:
>
> $$
> U(\theta) - U(90^\circ) = \int_{90^\circ}^{\theta}\mu B\sin\theta'\,d\theta' = -\mu B\cos\theta .
> $$
>
> With $U(90^\circ) = 0$, $U = -\mu B\cos\theta = -\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$. Its minimum at $\theta = 0$ is a stable equilibrium, its maximum at $\theta = 180^\circ$ an unstable one, and the torque $-dU/d\theta = -\mu B\sin\theta$ always points downhill in $U$.

^der-a7-4-3

> [!remark] Remark: Electric and magnetic dipoles; motors
> - The formulas copy those of an electric dipole $\vec p$ in a field $\vec E$, $\vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E$ ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^thm-a1-5-1|Theorem §A1.5.1]]): in both cases a uniform field exerts no net force, only a torque that aligns the moment with the field, and the same integration of the torque gives the electric orientation energy $U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E$. The PHY 182 slides set the two side by side: $\vec\mu$ = current × area (direction by the right-hand rule), $\vec p$ = charge × separation (from $-$ to $+$). The analogy extends to the fields the dipoles *make*: far away, a current loop's field is fixed by $\vec\mu$ exactly as an electric dipole's is fixed by $\vec p$ ([[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop#^thm-a8-3-2|Theorem §A8.3.2]], [[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]).
> - In a **dc motor** the loop would come to rest with $\vec\mu$ along $\vec B$. A commutator and brushes reverse the current each time the loop passes that position, so $\vec\mu$ flips and the torque keeps the same sense; the loop keeps turning, converting electrical energy into mechanical work. The aligning of atomic moments in a field is the basis of magnetism in matter ([[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter|§A8.6]]).

^rem-a7-4-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A7.4.1: Moment, Torque and Energy of a Small Loop
> A circular loop of radius $2.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ carries $2.0\ \mathrm{mA}$, and its moment makes $30^\circ$ with a uniform field of $0.50\ \mathrm T$.
>
> $$
> \mu = IA = (2.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm A)\,\pi(0.020\ \mathrm m)^2 = 2.5\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{A\cdot m^2},
> $$
>
> $$
> \tau = \mu B\sin30^\circ = 6.3\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}, \qquad U = -\mu B\cos30^\circ = -1.1\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm J .
> $$
>
> Turning the moment into alignment would lower the energy to $-\mu B = -1.3\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm J$.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 11.7*

^ex-a7-4-1

> [!example] Example §A7.4.2: A Square Loop on an Axle, Two Ways
> A square loop $2.0\ \mathrm m$ on a side carries $2.0\ \mathrm A$ and turns on an axle through its centre, perpendicular to a $0.10\ \mathrm T$ field.
> - Forces: each side parallel to the axle feels $F = IlB = (2.0)(2.0)(0.10)\ \mathrm N = 0.40\ \mathrm N$, perpendicular to $\vec B$, at $1.0\ \mathrm m$ from the axle; the torque is $\tau = 2(0.40\ \mathrm N)(1.0\ \mathrm m)\sin\theta = 0.80\sin\theta\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}$.
> - Moment: $\mu = IA = (2.0\ \mathrm A)(4.0\ \mathrm{m^2}) = 8.0\ \mathrm{A\cdot m^2}$ and $\tau = \mu B\sin\theta = 0.80\sin\theta\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}$, the same.
>
> The torque is greatest ($0.80\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}$) when the plane of the loop contains $\vec B$, and vanishes when $\vec\mu$ is along $\vec B$ ($U = -0.80\ \mathrm J$, where the loop "wants" to be) or opposite to it ($U = +0.80\ \mathrm J$, balanced but unstable).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 17 and TBL 18*

^ex-a7-4-2

> [!example] Example §A7.4.3: The Current Behind Earth's Field
> Earth's magnetic dipole moment is about $8\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{A\cdot m^2}$, produced by charge flowing in the liquid part of its interior. Model that flow, very roughly, as a single current loop of radius $R \approx 1000\ \mathrm{km}$. Then
>
> $$
> I = \frac{\mu}{\pi R^2} = \frac{8\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{A\cdot m^2}}{\pi\,(1\times10^{6}\ \mathrm m)^2} \approx 2.5\times10^{10}\ \mathrm A .
> $$
>
> A huge current, but spread over a huge area: the dipole moment grows with the area, so even a large moment needs no exotic current densities.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 18*

^ex-a7-4-3
