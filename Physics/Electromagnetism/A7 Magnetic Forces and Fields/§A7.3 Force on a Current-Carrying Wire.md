A current is charge in motion, so a wire carrying a current in a magnetic field feels the sum of the forces on its moving carriers ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge|§A7.1]]). This section adds them up: the result, $I\,d\vec l\times\vec B$ per element of wire, is the working formula behind motors, loudspeakers and current balances, and it shows that a closed loop in a uniform field feels no net force.

## Currents make fields, fields push currents

> [!remark] Remark: Two effects, and two right-hand rules
> A current-carrying wire both *makes* a magnetic field and *feels* one. Oersted's compass needles line up tangent to circles around a straight wire: the field lines of a long straight current are circles centred on the wire. Their sense is given by a second right-hand rule (RHR-2): with the thumb along the current, the fingers curl in the direction of $\vec B$. This field is computed in [[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents|§A8.2]]; here the field is external and given. Notation in drawings: $\odot$ is a vector pointing out of the page (the tip of an arrow coming at you), $\otimes$ one pointing into the page (the tail feathers of an arrow flying away).

^rem-a7-3-1

## The force on a wire

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.3.1: Force on a Current Element; Straight Wire
> An element $d\vec l$ of a wire carrying current $I$, with $d\vec l$ pointing along the current, feels in a magnetic field $\vec B$ the force
>
> $$
> d\vec F = I\,d\vec l\times\vec B .
> $$
>
> The force on a whole wire is $\vec F = I\int_{\text{wire}} d\vec l\times\vec B$. For a straight wire of length $l$ in a uniform field, with $\vec l$ the vector of length $l$ along the current,
>
> $$
> \vec F = I\,\vec l\times\vec B, \qquad F = IlB\sin\theta ,
> $$
>
> where $\theta$ is the angle between the wire and $\vec B$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §11.4, eqs. (11.10)–(11.13)*

^thm-a7-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let the wire have cross-sectional area $A$ and $n$ carriers per unit volume, each of charge $q$, moving with drift velocity $\vec v_d$. The element of length $dl$ holds $nA\,dl$ carriers, each feeling $q\,\vec v_d\times\vec B$, so
>
> $$
> d\vec F = (nA\,dl)\,q\,\vec v_d\times\vec B .
> $$
>
> The current is $I = n|q|v_dA$ ([[§A5.1 Electric Current|§A5.1]]), and $q\vec v_d$ points along the conventional current whatever the sign of $q$ (negative carriers drift against the current). So $nA\,dl\,q\,\vec v_d = n|q|v_dA\,d\vec l = I\,d\vec l$, which gives $d\vec F = I\,d\vec l\times\vec B$. For a straight wire in a uniform field, $d\vec l$ and $\vec B$ are the same along the wire and the integral is $I\,\vec l\times\vec B$. The force acts on the carriers; they pass it on to the lattice of the wire, so it is the wire that moves.

^der-a7-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: The current's direction, not the carriers'
> The force on a wire depends only on the direction of the conventional current: electrons drifting left give the same force as positive charges drifting right. Use the right-hand rule with the fingers along $I$ (not along the electrons' velocity) and sweep to $\vec B$. The sign of the carriers shows up only in the Hall effect ([[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^thm-a7-5-2|Theorem §A7.5.2]]).

## Curved wires and closed loops

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.3.2: Curved Wire in a Uniform Field
> In a uniform field $\vec B$, the force on a wire carrying current $I$ from point $a$ to point $b$ along any path equals the force on a straight wire from $a$ to $b$:
>
> $$
> \vec F = I\,\vec l_{ab}\times\vec B, \qquad \vec l_{ab} = \vec r_b - \vec r_a .
> $$
>
> In particular, the net force on a **closed** current loop of any shape in a uniform field is zero.
>
> *Source: UP2 §11.4 Example 11.6, §11.5; PHY 182, TBL 17*

^thm-a7-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\vec B$ is constant, so it comes out of the integral of [[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]: $\vec F = I\bigl(\int_a^b d\vec l\bigr)\times\vec B$. The sum of the small displacements $d\vec l$ along any path from $a$ to $b$ is the net displacement $\vec r_b - \vec r_a$. For a closed loop $b = a$, so $\vec l_{ab} = \vec 0$ and $\vec F = \vec 0$.

^der-a7-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Zero net force is not zero effect
> A loop in a uniform field feels no net force, but the forces on its parts generally have different lines of action and produce a torque: that is how a motor turns ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop|§A7.4]]). The theorem needs a uniform field: in a non-uniform field the forces on the parts of a loop need not cancel.

^cau-a7-3-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A7.3.1: Levitating a Wire
> A horizontal wire of length $l = 50\ \mathrm{cm}$ and mass $m = 10\ \mathrm g$ hangs from two flexible leads in a uniform horizontal field $B = 0.50\ \mathrm T$ perpendicular to the wire. What current removes the tension in the leads? Viewed from the front with $\vec B$ pointing away from the viewer, a current to the right gives $\vec F = I\,l\,\hat\imath\times(-B\hat k) = IlB\,\hat\jmath$, straight up. The leads go slack when this balances the weight:
>
> $$
> IlB = mg \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I = \frac{mg}{lB} = \frac{(0.010\ \mathrm{kg})(9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2})}{(0.50\ \mathrm m)(0.50\ \mathrm T)} = 0.39\ \mathrm A ,
> $$
>
> flowing to the right. Reversing the current would double the tension instead.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 11.4*

^ex-a7-3-1

> [!example] Example §A7.3.2: Only the Perpendicular Component Counts
> A long straight wire along the $y$-axis carries $I = 5.0\ \mathrm A$ in the $+y$ direction. Find the force per unit length (a) for $\vec B = 0.30\ \mathrm T\ \hat\imath$, (b) for a $0.30\ \mathrm T$ field at $30^\circ$ from the $+x$ axis toward $+y$. With $\vec F/l = I\,\hat\jmath\times\vec B$:
>
> $$
> \text{(a)}\ \ \frac{\vec F}{l} = (5.0\ \mathrm A)(0.30\ \mathrm T)\,\hat\jmath\times\hat\imath = -1.5\ \mathrm{N/m}\ \hat k ;
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{(b)}\ \ \frac{\vec F}{l} = (5.0\ \mathrm A)(0.30\ \mathrm T)\,\hat\jmath\times(\cos30^\circ\,\hat\imath + \sin30^\circ\,\hat\jmath) = -1.3\ \mathrm{N/m}\ \hat k .
> $$
>
> The field's component along the wire ($\sin30^\circ\,\hat\jmath$) contributes nothing; the force shrinks as the field turns toward the wire.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 11.5*

^ex-a7-3-2

> [!example] Example §A7.3.3: A D-Shaped Loop
> A loop made of a semicircle of radius $R = 1.0\ \mathrm m$ and its diameter carries $I = 2.0\ \mathrm A$ counterclockwise in a uniform field $B = 0.10\ \mathrm T$ into the page. Put the diameter on the $y$-axis and the arc on the right, at angles $\phi$ from $-\pi/2$ to $\pi/2$ measured from $+x$.
> - Arc: $d\vec l = R\,d\phi\,(-\sin\phi\,\hat\imath + \cos\phi\,\hat\jmath)$, and $d\vec F = I\,d\vec l\times(-B\hat k) = -IBR\,d\phi\,(\cos\phi\,\hat\imath + \sin\phi\,\hat\jmath)$: each piece is pushed radially inward. Integrating, the $\hat\jmath$ parts cancel and
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{arc}} = -IBR\,\hat\imath\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos\phi\,d\phi = -2IBR\,\hat\imath = -0.40\ \mathrm N\ \hat\imath .
> $$
>
> - Diameter: the current runs down, $\vec l = -2R\,\hat\jmath$, and $\vec F = I(-2R\hat\jmath)\times(-B\hat k) = +2IBR\,\hat\imath = +0.40\ \mathrm N\ \hat\imath$.
>
> The total is zero, as [[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-2|Theorem §A7.3.2]] predicts: the arc alone already gives the force on the straight chord from its bottom end to its top end, $I(2R\hat\jmath)\times(-B\hat k) = -2IBR\,\hat\imath$.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 17; UP2 Example 11.6*

^ex-a7-3-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The integral of $d\vec l$ along a path is the net displacement, the vector version of $\int_a^b g'(x)\,dx = g(b) - g(a)$ ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|Theorem §34.1]]) applied to each component of the path's parametrization.
