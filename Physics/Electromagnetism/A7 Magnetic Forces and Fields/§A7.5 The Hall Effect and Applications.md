When electric and magnetic forces act on a charge together, they can cancel for one particular speed. That balance is the working principle of three devices treated here: the velocity selector, the Hall effect (which reveals the sign of the charge carriers in a conductor and measures fields), and the mass spectrometer. The cyclotron, finally, exploits the speed-independent period of [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]].

## Crossed fields

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.5.1: Velocity Selector
> In **crossed** uniform fields, $\vec E$ perpendicular to $\vec B$, a charged particle moving perpendicular to both passes undeflected exactly when its speed is
>
> $$
> v = \frac EB ,
> $$
>
> provided the directions are such that the electric and magnetic forces oppose each other. Faster particles are deflected by the magnetic force, slower ones by the electric force. The selected speed is the same for all charges and masses.
>
> *Source: UP2 §11.6, eqs. (11.24)–(11.25)*

^thm-a7-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The net force is $q\vec E + q\,\vec v\times\vec B$ ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]]). With $\vec v\perp\vec B$, the magnetic term has magnitude $|q|vB$; if it is antiparallel to $q\vec E$, the net force vanishes when $|q|E = |q|vB$, that is, $v = E/B$. The charge cancels, so the condition does not depend on $q$ or $m$. With zero net force, the particle continues in a straight line.

^der-a7-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]]

## The Hall effect

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.5.2: Hall Potential
> A conducting strip of width $l$ and cross-sectional area $A$ carries a current $I$ in a uniform field $\vec B$ perpendicular to the strip. The magnetic force pushes the carriers toward one edge until the charge piled up at the edges produces a transverse field $E$ that balances it. In equilibrium a **Hall potential** difference appears across the width,
>
> $$
> V = Blv_d = \frac{IBl}{n|q|A} ,
> $$
>
> where $n$ is the number density of carriers of charge $q$ and $v_d$ their drift speed. Its magnitude is the same for either sign of carriers with the same current; its **polarity** reverses with the sign of the carriers.
>
> *Source: UP2 §11.6, eqs. (11.26)–(11.30)*

^thm-a7-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The carriers drift along the strip at $v_d$, perpendicular to $\vec B$. Charge accumulates at the edges until the transverse electric force balances the magnetic force, as in a velocity selector ([[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^thm-a7-5-1|Theorem §A7.5.1]]): $|q|E = |q|v_dB$, so $E = v_dB$. This field is uniform across the width, so $V = El = Blv_d$. The current is $I = n|q|v_dA$ ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]]); substituting $v_d = I/(n|q|A)$ gives $V = IBl/(n|q|A)$.
>
> Polarity: for the same current direction, positive carriers move along $I$ and negative carriers against it, so $q\vec v_d$ is the same for both and both are pushed toward the *same* edge. That edge therefore becomes positive for positive carriers and negative for negative carriers.

^der-a7-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^thm-a7-5-1|Theorem §A7.5.1]], [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]], [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]]

![[ph-em-a7-5-1.svg]]
*A metal strip with current $I$ to the right and $\vec B$ out of the page. The electrons drift to the left; the magnetic force $\vec F_B$ (red) pushes them to the lower edge, leaving the upper edge positive. The resulting Hall field $\vec E$ (green) pulls the electrons back with $\vec F_E$, and in equilibrium the two forces balance. With positive carriers and the same current, the lower edge would be positive instead.*

> [!law] Law §A7.5.3: Sign of the Charge Carriers
> Hall measurements show that in most metals the majority charge carriers are negative (electrons); in a few metals, such as tungsten and beryllium, and in many semiconductors, they are positive. Positive conduction is carried by **holes**, missing-electron sites that move through the material like positive charges. Hall's 1879 experiment was the first to show that the carriers in most metals are negative.
>
> *Valid when:* read off from the polarity of the Hall potential in a given material; the sign is a property of the material (its explanation, conduction by holes, belongs to condensed-matter physics, beyond level A).
> *Source: UP2 §11.6*

^law-a7-5-3

> [!remark] Remark: Using the Hall effect
> - **Measuring fields.** For a strip of known $n$ (a Hall probe), measuring $V$ and $I$ gives $B = n|q|AV/(Il)$; Hall probes regulate the fields of laboratory electromagnets.
> - **Small for good conductors.** $V \propto 1/n$: metals, with $n \sim 10^{28}\text{–}10^{29}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, give microvolts (Example §A7.5.1), while poor conductors with few carriers give much larger Hall potentials.

^rem-a7-5-1

## Mass spectrometer and cyclotron

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.5.4: Mass Spectrometer
> In a (Bainbridge) **mass spectrometer**, ions pass a velocity selector with fields $E$ and $B$, then enter a region of uniform field $B_0$ in which they move on a semicircle of radius $R$ to a detector. Their charge-to-mass ratio is
>
> $$
> \frac qm = \frac{E}{BB_0R} .
> $$
>
> Ions of the same charge but different mass land at different radii, $R \propto m$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §11.7, eq. (11.31)*

^thm-a7-5-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Only ions with $v = E/B$ leave the selector ([[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^thm-a7-5-1|Theorem §A7.5.1]]). In the field $B_0$ they circle with radius $R = mv/(qB_0)$ ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]]). Substituting $v$: $R = mE/(qBB_0)$, so $q/m = E/(BB_0R)$.

^der-a7-5-4

*Uses:* [[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^thm-a7-5-1|Theorem §A7.5.1]], [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.5.5: Cyclotron
> In a **cyclotron**, particles of charge $q$ and mass $m$ circle in a uniform field $B$ inside two hollow D-shaped electrodes (**dees**) and are accelerated each time they cross the gap between them by an alternating voltage of period
>
> $$
> T = \frac{2\pi m}{qB} ,
> $$
>
> the same for every orbit. A particle extracted at the maximum orbit radius $R$ has speed and kinetic energy
>
> $$
> v_{\max} = \frac{qBR}{m}, \qquad K_{\max} = \tfrac12 mv_{\max}^2 = \frac{q^2B^2R^2}{2m} .
> $$
>
> This works only while the period does not depend on the energy, that is, at non-relativistic speeds; UP2 puts the practical limit of this simple design at roughly $30\ \mathrm{MeV}$, beyond which the period grows with energy ([[§A8.3 Particle Accelerators and Detectors#^def-a8-3-1|QM Def. §A8.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §11.7, eqs. (11.32)–(11.35)*

^thm-a7-5-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Inside a dee the metal shields the electric field, so only $\vec B$ acts and the particle runs a semicircle of radius $r = mv/(qB)$ in the time $T/2$, with $T = 2\pi m/(qB)$ independent of $v$ and $r$ ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]]). If the voltage reverses every $T/2$, the particle meets an accelerating voltage $V$ at every gap crossing and gains $qV$ each time; it spirals outward on ever larger semicircles, still in step with the voltage. At the edge, $R = mv_{\max}/(qB)$ gives $v_{\max} = qBR/m$ and $K_{\max} = q^2B^2R^2/(2m)$.

^der-a7-5-5

*Uses:* [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Beyond the cyclotron
> Up to several hundred MeV the relativistic lengthening of the period can be compensated by letting $B$ grow with radius. Higher energies need other machines: **linear accelerators**, which push particles along a straight evacuated tube with an electromagnetic wave (SLAC: about $3.3\ \mathrm{km}$, electrons and positrons to $50\ \mathrm{GeV}$), and **synchrotrons**, whose bending field grows with the particles' speed so that the orbit radius stays fixed (CERN's, near Geneva, accelerates bunches of about $10^{13}$ protons to about $10^3\ \mathrm{GeV}$).

^rem-a7-5-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A7.5.1: Hall Potential in a Silver Ribbon
> A silver ribbon $1.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ wide and $0.20\ \mathrm{cm}$ thick ($A = 2.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2}$) carries $100\ \mathrm A$ in a perpendicular field of $1.5\ \mathrm T$. Silver has $n = 5.9\times10^{28}$ free electrons per $\mathrm{m^3}$. The Hall potential across the width is
>
> $$
> V = \frac{IBl}{neA} = \frac{(100\ \mathrm A)(1.5\ \mathrm T)(1.0\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm m)}{(5.9\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}})(1.6\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm C)(2.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2})} = 7.9\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm V .
> $$
>
> Even with a large current and a strong field, the Hall potential of a metal is only microvolts, because $n$ is enormous; its polarity (the edge toward which electrons are pushed is negative) identifies the carriers as electrons.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 11.9*

^ex-a7-5-1

> [!example] Example §A7.5.2: Separating Helium from Air in a Mass Spectrometer
> Singly charged ions ($q = +e$) of He, N$_2$ and O$_2$ (molar masses 4, 28 and $32\ \mathrm{g/mol}$) are accelerated from rest through $\Delta V = 1.0\ \mathrm{kV}$ and then bent in a field $B = 0.10\ \mathrm T$.
> - Masses: $m = M/N_A$, so $m_{\text{He}} = 6.6\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $m_{\text{N}_2} = 4.7\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $m_{\text{O}_2} = 5.3\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$.
> - Speeds, from energy conservation $\tfrac12 mv^2 = e\Delta V$: $v = \sqrt{2e\Delta V/m} = 2.2\times10^5$, $8.3\times10^4$ and $7.8\times10^4\ \mathrm{m/s}$.
> - Radii, from [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]]:
>
> $$
> r = \frac{mv}{eB} = \frac1B\sqrt{\frac{2m\,\Delta V}{e}} : \qquad r_{\text{He}} = 9.1\ \mathrm{cm},\quad r_{\text{N}_2} = 24\ \mathrm{cm},\quad r_{\text{O}_2} = 26\ \mathrm{cm}.
> $$
>
> With an accelerating voltage instead of a velocity selector, $r \propto \sqrt m$; helium lands far from the air molecules and is easy to pick out, which is how helium leak detectors work.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 20*

^ex-a7-5-2

> [!example] Example §A7.5.3: A Cyclotron for Alpha Particles
> A cyclotron of radius $R = 0.50\ \mathrm m$ with $B = 1.8\ \mathrm T$ accelerates alpha particles ($m = 6.64\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $q = 3.2\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm C$). By [[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^thm-a7-5-5|Theorem §A7.5.5]],
>
> $$
> T = \frac{2\pi m}{qB} = \frac{2\pi(6.64\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg})}{(3.2\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm C)(1.8\ \mathrm T)} = 7.2\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm s ,
> $$
>
> so the dee voltage alternates at $1/T = 14\ \mathrm{MHz}$, and
>
> $$
> K_{\max} = \frac{q^2B^2R^2}{2m} = \frac{(3.2\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm C)^2(1.8\ \mathrm T)^2(0.50\ \mathrm m)^2}{2(6.64\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg})} = 6.2\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm J = 39\ \mathrm{MeV}.
> $$
>
> The energy grows as $(BR)^2$: a bigger magnet, not a bigger voltage, sets the final energy. ($39\ \mathrm{MeV}$ is above the "about $30\ \mathrm{MeV}$" limit quoted in [[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^thm-a7-5-5|Theorem §A7.5.5]]; a single figure cannot fit all particles. The period lengthens by the fraction $K/(mc^2)$ of the kinetic energy to the rest energy, so the limit grows with the mass: at $39\ \mathrm{MeV}$ an alpha particle ($mc^2 \approx 3.7\ \mathrm{GeV}$) is off by only about 1%, a proton at $30\ \mathrm{MeV}$ ($mc^2 \approx 0.94\ \mathrm{GeV}$) by about 3%. Relativity, planned.)
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 11.10; the remark on the energy limit is an outside fact (special relativity), not in UP2*

^ex-a7-5-3
