*Sources: Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (4th ed.), §4.3 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 6.*

Gauss's law, $\varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]), counts all charge, and in matter part of it is bound charge, which depends on the field it helps to produce. This section separates the charge we put in place, the free charge, from the bound charge that "comes along for the ride", and folds the bound part into a new field $\vec D$ whose divergence is the free charge alone. Level A used the result without the field: Gauss's law with $\varepsilon_0$ replaced by $\kappa\varepsilon_0$ in a region filled with dielectric ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^rem-a4-4-2|§A4.4, Remark: Permittivity]]), and Waves and Optics took $\oint\vec D\cdot d\vec A = Q_{\text{free}}$ as given ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-1|WO Theorem §B9.1.1]]). New here: the definition of $\vec D$, Gauss's law for it as a theorem, the reason it is *not* an electric field of the free charge (its curl), and the static boundary conditions with dielectrics. The magnetic counterpart is $\vec H$ ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media|§B7.2]]).

## Free charge and the displacement

> [!definition] Definition §B5.2.1: Free Charge
> In the presence of a dielectric the charge density is split as (Griffiths eq. (4.20))
>
> $$
> \rho = \rho_b + \rho_f ,
> $$
>
> where $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\vec P$ is the bound charge of [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]] and the **free charge** $\rho_f$ is everything else: electrons on a conductor, ions embedded in the dielectric, any charge that is not the result of polarization. Likewise $\sigma_f$ for free surface charge.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.3.1, eq. (4.20)*

^def-b5-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §B5.2.2: Electric Displacement
> The **electric displacement** is (Griffiths eq. (4.21))
>
> $$
> \vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec E$ the total macroscopic field ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]]). Its unit is that of $\vec P$, $\mathrm{C/m^2}$; outside matter $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.3.1, eq. (4.21) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 6, eq. (6.8)*

^def-b5-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.1: Gauss's Law for the Displacement
> In electrostatics, with or without dielectrics (Griffiths eqs. (4.22)–(4.23)),
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f , \qquad \oint_{\mathcal S}\vec D\cdot d\vec a = Q_{f,\text{enc}} ,
> $$
>
> where $Q_{f,\text{enc}}$ is the free charge inside the closed surface $\mathcal S$. The bound charge does not appear: it is accounted for by $\vec P$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.3.1, eqs. (4.22)–(4.23)*

^thm-b5-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Gauss's law for the total field ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]) reads $\varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho_b + \rho_f = -\nabla\cdot\vec P + \rho_f$; collecting the divergences, $\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P) = \rho_f$. The integral form follows from the divergence theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]). The surface bound charge seems to be left out, and Gauss's law in differential form cannot be applied exactly *at* a dielectric surface, where $\rho_b$ blows up. Picture the edge as a thin skin in which $\vec P$ tapers to zero: then there is no surface bound charge, $\rho_b$ is large but finite in the skin, and the argument holds everywhere (Griffiths §4.3.1). The integral form is free of the difficulty in any case.

^der-b5-2-1

*Uses:* [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^def-b5-2-1|Def. §B5.2.1]], [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^def-b5-2-2|Def. §B5.2.2]], [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]], [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why it is useful, and when
> Free charge is what we control; the bound charge follows from it through the polarization, which is unknown until the field is known. Gauss's law for $\vec D$ breaks this circle *when there is enough symmetry*: with spherical, cylindrical or planar symmetry, $\vec D$ follows from the free charge alone by the methods of level A ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^def-a2-3-1|Def. §A2.3.1]]), and $\vec E$ follows wherever $\vec P$ is known. Griffiths' Example 4.4: a wire with line charge $\lambda$ inside rubber insulation of radius $a$ has $\vec D = \lambda\hat s/(2\pi s)$ inside and outside the rubber, hence $\vec E = \lambda\hat s/(2\pi\varepsilon_0s)$ outside; inside the rubber $\vec E$ cannot be found until $\vec P$ is known. Without such symmetry, the free charge does not determine $\vec D$ (next theorem).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.3.1, Example 4.4, eq. (4.24); §4.3.2 ("Advice")*

^rem-b5-2-1

## A deceptive parallel

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.2: Curl of the Displacement
> In electrostatics (Griffiths eq. (4.25))
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\vec D = \nabla\times\vec P ,
> $$
>
> which is not zero in general. Consequently $\vec D$ is *not* determined by the free charge alone, there is no Coulomb's law for it,
>
> $$
> \vec D(\vec r) \ne \frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\,\rho_f(\vec r\,')\,d\tau' \quad\text{in general,}
> $$
>
> and $\vec D$ is not the gradient of a scalar potential. $\vec D$ is the field of the free charge only where $\nabla\times\vec P = \vec 0$ everywhere, for example when symmetry forces it.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.3.2, eq. (4.25)*

^thm-b5-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\nabla\times\vec D = \varepsilon_0\nabla\times\vec E + \nabla\times\vec P$, and $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ in electrostatics ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]). A field that vanishes at infinity is fixed by its divergence *and* its curl ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]); the divergence of $\vec D$ is $\rho_f$, but its curl is $\nabla\times\vec P$, so the Coulomb integral of $\rho_f$, which has zero curl, gives $\vec D$ only when $\nabla\times\vec P = \vec 0$. A gradient has zero curl ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]), so a $\vec D$ with nonzero curl has no potential.

^der-b5-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^def-b5-2-2|Def. §B5.2.2]], [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: "Forget the bound charge and call the answer D" is wrong
> Since $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f$ looks like Gauss's law, it is tempting to compute the field of the free charges as if no dielectric were there, multiply it by $\varepsilon_0$ and call the result $\vec D$. This works only when $\nabla\times\vec P = \vec 0$. A bar electret ([[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^ex-b5-2-2|Example §B5.2.2]]) has no free charge anywhere, so the rule would give $\vec D = \vec 0$, hence $\vec E = -\vec P/\varepsilon_0$ inside and $\vec E = \vec 0$ outside, which is plainly wrong: the electret has a dipole field outside.

^cau-b5-2-1

## Boundary conditions

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.3: Boundary Conditions with Dielectrics
> At a surface carrying free surface charge $\sigma_f$, with $\perp$ the component along the normal from "below" to "above" and $\parallel$ the components tangent to the surface (Griffiths eqs. (4.26)–(4.29)),
>
> $$
> D^\perp_{\text{above}} - D^\perp_{\text{below}} = \sigma_f , \qquad \vec D^\parallel_{\text{above}} - \vec D^\parallel_{\text{below}} = \vec P^\parallel_{\text{above}} - \vec P^\parallel_{\text{below}} ,
> $$
>
> together with the conditions on $\vec E$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]), in which the *total* surface charge $\sigma = \sigma_f + \sigma_b$ appears:
>
> $$
> E^\perp_{\text{above}} - E^\perp_{\text{below}} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} , \qquad \vec E^\parallel_{\text{above}} - \vec E^\parallel_{\text{below}} = \vec 0 .
> $$
>
> In particular, at an interface between insulators ($\sigma_f = 0$) the normal component of $\vec D$ is continuous while that of $\vec E$ jumps by $\sigma_b/\varepsilon_0$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.3.3, eqs. (4.26)–(4.29)*

^thm-b5-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Normal.* Apply [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]] to a flat pillbox straddling the surface, faces of area $A$ on either side, height $\to 0$: the side wall contributes nothing and the enclosed free charge is $\sigma_fA$, so $(D^\perp_{\text{above}} - D^\perp_{\text{below}})A = \sigma_fA$. *Tangential.* By [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]], $\nabla\times(\vec D - \vec P) = \varepsilon_0\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$; Stokes' theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]) on a thin rectangle straddling the surface, short sides $\to 0$, gives continuity of the tangential part of $\vec D - \vec P = \varepsilon_0\vec E$. That is the second $\vec E$ condition, and it is equivalent to the second $\vec D$ condition. The first $\vec E$ condition is the same pillbox with Gauss's law for the total charge.

^der-b5-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]], [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]], [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Lines of E and lines of D
> Lines of $\vec E$ begin and end on charges of either kind; lines of $\vec D$ begin and end only on free charge (Griffiths Problem 4.17, hint). Where there is no free charge, lines of $\vec D$ do not end at all, yet they need not be absent, because $\vec D$ can circulate ([[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]]). Across an insulating interface $D^\perp$ is continuous, so the number of $\vec D$ lines is conserved, while some $\vec E$ lines end on the bound charge. Theorem §B5.2.3 with $\sigma_f = 0$ is the electric part of the boundary conditions that Waves and Optics uses for light ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-1|WO Theorem §B9.1.1]]). The general conditions ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-6|Theorem §B8.4.6]], with medium 1 "above", medium 2 "below" and the same normal from below to above) show that the conditions on $D^\perp$ and $\vec E^\parallel$ hold unchanged for time-dependent fields.

^rem-b5-2-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B5.2.1: The Radially Polarized Shell, Again
> The shell of [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^ex-b5-1-1|Example §B5.1.1]], $\vec P = (k/r)\hat r$ for $a < r < b$, contains no free charge. Spherical symmetry makes $\vec D = D(r)\hat r$ (and $\nabla\times\vec P = \vec 0$), and [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]] on a sphere of any radius gives $4\pi r^2D(r) = 0$. So $\vec D = \vec 0$ everywhere, and
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{\vec D - \vec P}{\varepsilon_0} = -\frac{\vec P}{\varepsilon_0} = -\frac{k}{\varepsilon_0r}\,\hat r \quad (a < r < b), \qquad \vec E = \vec 0 \text{ elsewhere,}
> $$
>
> as before, "much faster, and without any explicit reference to the bound charges" (Griffiths).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 4.15(b)*

^ex-b5-2-1

> [!example] Example §B5.2.2: The Bar Electret
> A short cylinder (radius $a$, length $L$) carries a frozen-in uniform polarization $\vec P = P\hat z$ along its axis, and there is no free charge anywhere (Griffiths Problems 4.11, 4.17). Bound charge: $\rho_b = 0$, and $\sigma_b = +P$ on the top face, $-P$ on the bottom face, zero on the side. So $\vec E$ is the field of two oppositely charged disks; at the centre, adding the on-axis fields of the disks, for $L = 2a$,
>
> $$
> \vec E(0) = -\frac{P}{\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(1 - \frac{L/2}{\sqrt{(L/2)^2 + a^2}}\Bigr)\hat z = -\Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigr)\frac{P}{\varepsilon_0}\hat z \approx -0.29\,\frac{P}{\varepsilon_0}\hat z, \qquad \vec D(0) = \varepsilon_0\vec E(0) + \vec P = \frac{P}{\sqrt2}\hat z .
> $$
>
> Inside, $\vec E$ opposes $\vec P$ and $\vec D$ points along it. Where is the curl of $\vec P$? $\vec P$ jumps from $P\hat z$ to zero across the side wall, a tangential discontinuity: $\nabla\times\vec P = P\,\delta(s - a)\,\hat\varphi$ for $|z| < L/2$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]). With $\nabla\cdot\vec D = 0$, $\vec D$ thus has the divergence and curl of $\vec B/\mu_0$ for a solenoid with surface current $K = P$ around the side: the electret's $\vec D$ is the field of a finite solenoid, which is why its lines close on themselves (and $D(0) = P\cos 45^\circ$ is the solenoid's centre field). This is the electrostatic twin of a bar magnet ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 4.11, 4.17, and §4.3.2 (the electret as a counterexample)*

> [!example] Example §B5.2.3: Cavities, and What E and D Mean
> Deep inside a large dielectric with frozen-in polarization $\vec P$ the field is $\vec E_0$ and $\vec D_0 = \varepsilon_0\vec E_0 + \vec P$, all uniform. Hollow out a small cavity and find the field at its centre (Griffiths Problem 4.16). Removing material is the same as superimposing an object of the cavity's shape with polarization $-\vec P$, whose field is that of its bound charges ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^rem-b5-1-3|§B5.1, Remark: The field inside depends on the shape]]):
> - *Sphere:* the added field inside is $+\vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]), so $\vec E = \vec E_0 + \vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ and $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E = \vec D_0 - \frac23\vec P$.
> - *Long needle along $\vec P$:* its bound charges sit on two tiny distant tips, so $\vec E = \vec E_0$ and $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E_0 = \vec D_0 - \vec P$.
> - *Thin wafer perpendicular to $\vec P$:* its faces carry $\mp P$, two sheets with field $+\vec P/\varepsilon_0$ between them, so $\vec E = \vec E_0 + \vec P/\varepsilon_0 = \vec D_0/\varepsilon_0$ and $\vec D = \vec D_0$.
>
> So $\vec E$ in the medium is what a needle-shaped cavity along the field measures, and $\vec D/\varepsilon_0$ what a wafer-shaped cavity across it measures: the tangential and normal continuity of [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]] at the cavity walls. The magnetic version is [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^rem-b7-2-3|§B7.2, Remark: Cavities in a magnet]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 4.16*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Pillbox and thin-loop arguments are the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]) and Stokes' theorem ([[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]]) on vanishing regions; a field is fixed by divergence and curl ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]).

> - Level C: the curl of D (Theorem §B5.2.2; Caution: "Forget the bound charge and call the answer D" is wrong) as the source of the part of D that free charge does not fix, with sheets on interfaces where the tangential polarization jumps — [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-5|Theorem §C5.2.5]].
