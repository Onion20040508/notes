How does a dielectric respond to a field? Level A described the response by one number, the dielectric constant $\kappa = E_0/E$ of a capacitor filled with the material, and by the empirical rule that the field drops by $1/\kappa$ ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]], [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^law-a4-4-1|Law §A4.4.1]]), which in a filled region amounts to replacing $\varepsilon_0$ by $\kappa\varepsilon_0$ ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^rem-a4-4-2|§A4.4, Remark: Permittivity]]). Here the empirical law becomes local, $\vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E$ at each point, with $\vec E$ the total field. Together with Gauss's law for $\vec D$ ([[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement|§B5.2]]) it explains when level A's rule holds, and replaces it where it fails: boundary-value problems with dielectrics, the energy of dielectric systems and the force that pulls a dielectric into a field. The same constitutive structure at optical frequencies is Waves and Optics' linear dielectric ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|WO Model §B8.1.1]]); the magnetic counterpart is [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media|§B7.2]].

## Susceptibility and permittivity

> [!law] Law §B5.3.1: Linear Dielectric Response
> In many materials the polarization is proportional to the field (Griffiths eq. (4.30)):
>
> $$
> \vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E ,
> $$
>
> where $\vec E$ is the **total** macroscopic field at the point, due in part to free charges and in part to the polarization itself, and the dimensionless **electric susceptibility** $\chi_e$ depends on the material and on conditions such as temperature, not on $\vec E$. For ordinary materials $\chi_e > 0$.
>
> *Valid when:* the field is weak compared with the fields that hold atoms and molecules together, so that the first term of the Taylor expansion of $\vec P(\vec E)$ suffices (Griffiths, footnote 7; nonlinear optical materials need a cubic term), and well below the dielectric strength ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^def-a4-4-2|Def. §A4.4.2]]); the fields are static or vary slowly enough for the material to follow; the material is isotropic (for a crystal $\chi_e$ becomes a tensor, eq. (4.38)); and there is no frozen-in polarization (electrets).
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.1, eq. (4.30), eq. (4.38) and footnotes 7, 12 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 6, eq. (6.10)*

^law-b5-3-1

> [!definition] Definition §B5.3.1: Susceptibility; Permittivity; Dielectric Constant
> A material obeying [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^law-b5-3-1|Law §B5.3.1]] is a **linear dielectric**. Then $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P = \varepsilon_0(1 + \chi_e)\vec E$, so (Griffiths eqs. (4.31)–(4.34))
>
> $$
> \vec D = \varepsilon\vec E, \qquad \varepsilon = \varepsilon_0(1 + \chi_e), \qquad \varepsilon_r = 1 + \chi_e = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0} ,
> $$
>
> with $\varepsilon$ the **permittivity** of the material and $\varepsilon_r$ its **relative permittivity** or **dielectric constant**; vacuum has $\chi_e = 0$, $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0$. A relation giving $\vec D$ in terms of $\vec E$ is a **constitutive relation**. A linear dielectric is **isotropic** if $\chi_e$ is the same for every direction of $\vec E$ and **homogeneous** if $\chi_e$ does not vary with position. Values at 1 atm and 20 °C (Griffiths Table 4.2): dry air 1.000536, benzene 2.28, diamond 5.7–5.9, silicon 11.7, methanol 33.0, water 80.1, KTaNbO$_3$ at 0 °C 34,000.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.1, eqs. (4.31)–(4.34), Table 4.2 and footnotes 8, 10, 12*

^def-b5-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: κ, ε_r, χ_e
> - Level A, following University Physics, writes $\kappa$ for the dielectric constant; Griffiths writes $\varepsilon_r$. They are the same number, $\kappa = \varepsilon_r = 1 + \chi_e$.
> - Level A *defines* $\kappa$ as $E_0/E$, the factor by which a material filling a capacitor lowers the field. That ratio equals $\varepsilon_r$ only when the material fills all the space where the field is ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-3|Theorem §B5.3.3]] (3)). For a dielectric sphere in a uniform field $E_0/E = (\varepsilon_r + 2)/3$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-4|Theorem §B5.3.4]]), for a thin slab along the field it is 1. $\varepsilon_r$ is the material constant; $E_0/E$ depends on the geometry.
> - $\chi_e$ is defined with a factor $\varepsilon_0$ taken out, so it is dimensionless; do not confuse it with the magnetic susceptibility, written $\chi$ at level A ([[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^def-a8-6-2|Def. §A8.6.2]]) and $\chi_m$ here ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media|§B7.2]]).

^cau-b5-3-1

> [!model] Model §B5.3.2: Linear Isotropic Homogeneous Dielectric (Static)
> A static configuration of free charges, conductors and pieces of dielectric, in which each piece fills its region with a constant $\chi_e > 0$ obeying [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^law-b5-3-1|Law §B5.3.1]], the pieces are separated from each other and from vacuum by sharp interfaces, and $\vec E$ is the macroscopic field ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]]). Griffiths' phrase "linear dielectric" usually means this model.
>
> - *Assumes:* the conditions of Law §B5.3.1 (weak static fields, isotropy, no frozen-in polarization) and uniform material in each region.
> - *Compared with Waves and Optics:* [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|WO Model §B8.1.1]] has the same constitutive relation, but for fields oscillating at one frequency, with $\chi(\omega)$ in place of the static $\chi_e$, no free charge and $\mu = \mu_0$. The two susceptibilities are different numbers for the same material: water has $\varepsilon_r = 80$ but refractive index 1.33 ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^rem-b13-1-4|WO §B13.1, Remark: Water, with dielectric constant 80 and index 1.33]]).
> - *Fails:* in strong fields (nonlinearity, breakdown), in crystals (tensor $\chi_e$), in electrets and ferroelectrics, and for rapidly varying fields.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.1–4.4.2 and footnotes 10, 12*

## Fields in linear dielectrics

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.3: Field Equations in Linear Dielectrics
> In the model of [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^mod-b5-3-2|Model §B5.3.2]]:
> 1. Inside a homogeneous region the bound charge is proportional to the free charge (Griffiths eq. (4.39)),
>
> $$
> \rho_b = -\frac{\chi_e}{1 + \chi_e}\,\rho_f ;
> $$
>
>    so where no free charge is embedded, the region has no volume bound charge and the potential obeys Laplace's equation there. Any bound charge sits on surfaces.
> 2. At an interface carrying free charge $\sigma_f$ (Griffiths eqs. (4.40)–(4.42)),
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_{\text{above}}E^\perp_{\text{above}} - \varepsilon_{\text{below}}E^\perp_{\text{below}} = \sigma_f , \quad\text{i.e.}\quad \varepsilon_{\text{above}}\frac{\partial V_{\text{above}}}{\partial n} - \varepsilon_{\text{below}}\frac{\partial V_{\text{below}}}{\partial n} = -\sigma_f, \qquad V_{\text{above}} = V_{\text{below}} .
> $$
>
> 3. If one homogeneous linear dielectric fills all the space where the field is nonzero, then $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E_{\text{vac}}$ and (Griffiths eq. (4.35))
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_r}\vec E_{\text{vac}} ,
> $$
>
>    where $\vec E_{\text{vac}}$ is the field the same free charges would produce in vacuum. A free point charge embedded in the dielectric has $\vec E = q\hat r/(4\pi\varepsilon r^2)$ (eq. (4.36)), and a filled capacitor has $C = \varepsilon_rC_{\text{vac}}$ (eq. (4.37)).
> 4. At an interface without free charge the field lines bend (Griffiths Problem 4.36, eq. (4.68)): with $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$ the angles between the field and the normal on the two sides,
>
> $$
> \frac{\tan\theta_2}{\tan\theta_1} = \frac{\varepsilon_2}{\varepsilon_1} ,
> $$
>
>    so a line entering the medium of larger permittivity turns away from the normal.
> 5. *Uniqueness.* If the free charge in a volume $\mathcal V$ and the potential on its boundary (or $V \to 0$ at infinity) are given, the potential throughout $\mathcal V$ is uniquely determined, whatever pieces of linear dielectric are inside (Griffiths Problem 4.38).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.1–4.4.2, eqs. (4.35)–(4.42); Problems 4.36, 4.38*

^thm-b5-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E = (\varepsilon_0\chi_e/\varepsilon)\vec D$ with a constant factor, so $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\vec P = -\frac{\chi_e}{1 + \chi_e}\nabla\cdot\vec D = -\frac{\chi_e}{1 + \chi_e}\rho_f$ by [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]]. Then $\rho = \rho_f + \rho_b = 0$ where $\rho_f = 0$, and $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0 = 0$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]). The argument fails on a boundary, where $\chi_e$ jumps (Griffiths, footnote 13).
>
> *2.* Substitute $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$ into the normal condition of [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]], with $E^\perp = -\partial V/\partial n$; $V$ is continuous across any surface carrying no dipole layer ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]]).
>
> *3.* With $\varepsilon$ constant everywhere the field is nonzero, $\nabla\times\vec D = \varepsilon\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ and $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f$; these are the equations obeyed by $\varepsilon_0\vec E_{\text{vac}}$, and a field vanishing at infinity is fixed by its divergence and curl ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]). So $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E_{\text{vac}}$ and $\vec E = \vec D/\varepsilon$. (Regions where the field vanishes anyway do not need to be filled.) For a capacitor at fixed $Q$ the field, hence $V$, drops by $\varepsilon_r$, and $C = Q/V$ rises by $\varepsilon_r$.
>
> *4.* $E^\parallel$ is continuous and so is $\varepsilon E^\perp$ (item 2 with $\sigma_f = 0$). Hence $\tan\theta = E^\parallel/E^\perp$ satisfies $\tan\theta_2/\tan\theta_1 = E^\perp_1/E^\perp_2 = \varepsilon_2/\varepsilon_1$.
>
> *5.* Let $V_1$, $V_2$ be two solutions and $V_3 = V_1 - V_2$, $\vec D_3 = -\varepsilon\nabla V_3$. Then $\nabla\cdot\vec D_3 = 0$ in each region, $V_3$ and $D_3^\perp$ are continuous across every interface (item 2 with the same $\sigma_f$ for both), and $V_3 = 0$ on the boundary. By product rule (iii) of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], $\nabla\cdot(V_3\vec D_3) = V_3\nabla\cdot\vec D_3 + \vec D_3\cdot\nabla V_3 = -\varepsilon|\nabla V_3|^2$. Integrate over each region and add: the interface terms cancel (continuity of $V_3D_3^\perp$), the outer boundary term vanishes, and $\int\varepsilon|\nabla V_3|^2\,d\tau = 0$. Since $\varepsilon > 0$, $\nabla V_3 = 0$, and $V_3 = 0$. This is the uniqueness theorem of [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]], weighted by $\varepsilon$.

> [!remark] Remark: What becomes of level A
> Level A's dielectric is the special case "one material fills the capacitor":
> - [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^law-a4-4-1|Law §A4.4.1]] ($E = E_0/\kappa$) and the permittivity rule ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^rem-a4-4-2|§A4.4, Remark: Permittivity]]) are Theorem §B5.3.3 (3): no longer an empirical law about capacitors, but a consequence of the local law $\vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E$ and Gauss's law for $\vec D$, valid only when the dielectric fills the field region.
> - [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^thm-a4-4-2|Theorem §A4.4.2]] ($C = \kappa C_0$) is eq. (4.37).
> - The induced charge of [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^thm-a4-4-4|Theorem §A4.4.4]] is the bound charge: with $\sigma_f = Q/A$ on a plate, $E = \sigma_f/\varepsilon$ in the filled gap and $\sigma_b = P = \varepsilon_0\chi_eE = \frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r}\,\sigma_f$, which is $Q_i = (1 - 1/\kappa)Q$.
> - The half-filled capacitors of [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^ex-a4-4-2|Example §A4.4.2]] are boundary-value problems solved by item 2: side by side, $E^\parallel$ is continuous across the vertical interface and the field is $V/d$ in both halves; stacked, $\varepsilon E^\perp$ is continuous across the horizontal one.

^rem-b5-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Screening, and why P can still curl
> - A free charge $q$ inside a large dielectric is surrounded by bound charge $-\frac{\chi_e}{1 + \chi_e}q$ piled up next to it (item 1), which partially *shields* it; Coulomb's law is not wrong, the polarization reduces the field (Griffiths Fig. 4.22). A dielectric is "rather like an imperfect conductor" (Griffiths Example 4.5), and a conductor behaves in many respects as the limit $\chi_e \to \infty$ (footnote 16): check it on Theorem §B5.3.4 and Example §B5.3.1 below.
> - In a linear medium $\vec P \propto \vec E$, but $\nabla\times\vec P = -\varepsilon_0\vec E\times\nabla\chi_e$ (footnote 9) is not zero where $\chi_e$ changes, at every interface not perpendicular to $\vec E$. So the deceptive parallel of [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^cau-b5-2-1|§B5.2, Caution]] survives in linear media; only all space filled with one homogeneous dielectric escapes it (item 3).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.1, Example 4.5, Figures 4.21–4.22 and footnotes 9, 11, 16*

^rem-b5-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.4: Dielectric Sphere in a Uniform Field
> A sphere of radius $R$ of homogeneous linear dielectric, placed in an otherwise uniform field $\vec E_0$, has a **uniform** field inside (Griffiths eq. (4.49)),
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{3}{\varepsilon_r + 2}\,\vec E_0, \qquad \vec P = 3\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 2}\,\vec E_0 ,
> $$
>
> and outside, the field is $\vec E_0$ plus that of a point dipole at the centre with $\vec p = \frac43\pi R^3\vec P = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 2}R^3\vec E_0$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.2, Example 4.7, eqs. (4.43)–(4.49); Problem 4.23*

^thm-b5-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Self-consistent polarization.* Try a uniform polarization $\vec P$. By [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]] it adds the uniform field $-\vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ inside and a dipole field outside, so the total inside field is $\vec E = \vec E_0 - \vec P/3\varepsilon_0$. The law $\vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E$ then requires $\vec E = \vec E_0 - \chi_e\vec E/3$, that is $\vec E = \vec E_0/(1 + \chi_e/3) = 3\vec E_0/(\varepsilon_r + 2)$, and $\vec P = \varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)\vec E$. The resulting potential is continuous, satisfies Laplace's equation inside and outside, has continuous $D^\perp$ (it is the field of an actual polarized sphere plus a uniform field, and no free charge sits on the surface) and tends to $-E_0z$ far away; by uniqueness (Theorem §B5.3.3 (5)) it is the solution. Griffiths' Problem 4.23 reaches the same answer by successive approximations: $\vec E_0$ polarizes the sphere, whose field $-\chi_e\vec E_0/3$ changes the polarization, and so on; the geometric series $\vec E_0\sum_n(-\chi_e/3)^n$ sums to the same result for $\chi_e < 3$.
>
> *Separation of variables* (Griffiths' route). With $V_{\text{in}} = \sum A_\ell r^\ell P_\ell(\cos\theta)$ and $V_{\text{out}} = -E_0r\cos\theta + \sum B_\ell r^{-\ell - 1}P_\ell(\cos\theta)$ ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-3|Theorem §B3.4.3]]), the conditions at $r = R$, $V_{\text{in}} = V_{\text{out}}$ and $\varepsilon\,\partial_rV_{\text{in}} = \varepsilon_0\,\partial_rV_{\text{out}}$ (eq. (4.43)), give $A_\ell = B_\ell = 0$ for $\ell \ne 1$ and $A_1 = -3E_0/(\varepsilon_r + 2)$, $B_1 = \frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 2}R^3E_0$ (eq. (4.48)), so $V_{\text{in}} = -\frac{3E_0}{\varepsilon_r + 2}z$.

^der-b5-3-4

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]], [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^law-b5-3-1|Law §B5.3.1]], [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-3|Theorem §B5.3.3]], [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-3|Theorem §B3.4.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: The sphere between the extremes
> The inside field $3E_0/(\varepsilon_r + 2)$ lies between $E_0$ (vacuum, $\varepsilon_r = 1$) and $0$, the conductor of [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]] reached as $\chi_e \to \infty$. It is not $E_0/\varepsilon_r$: the sphere does not fill the field region, so Theorem §B5.3.3 (3) does not apply, and the bound charge on its surface screens the inside only partially. The factor $\frac13$ is the sphere's shape factor ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^rem-b5-1-3|§B5.1, Remark: The field inside depends on the shape]]); a long cylinder across the field gives $\vec E = 2\vec E_0/(\varepsilon_r + 1)$ in the same way (Griffiths Problem 4.22), the electric twin of [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^ex-b7-2-2|Example §B7.2.2]].

## Energy

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.5: Energy in Dielectric Systems
> With all dielectric material held fixed, the work done in increasing the free charge density by $\Delta\rho_f$ is (Griffiths eqs. (4.56)–(4.57))
>
> $$
> \Delta W = \int(\Delta\vec D)\cdot\vec E\,d\tau ,
> $$
>
> for any material. If all dielectrics are linear, the work needed to bring the free charge in from infinity, starting from an unpolarized system, is (eq. (4.58))
>
> $$
> W = \frac12\int\vec D\cdot\vec E\,d\tau = \frac12\int\rho_fV\,d\tau ,
> $$
>
> integrals over all space; for a single linear medium the energy density is $\frac12\varepsilon E^2$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.3, eqs. (4.55)–(4.58) and footnote 17 · PHY 422, Final formula sheet (linear dielectric energy)*

^thm-b5-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Bringing in $\Delta\rho_f$ against the potential $V$ costs $\Delta W = \int(\Delta\rho_f)V\,d\tau$ (eq. (4.56)). By [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]], $\Delta\rho_f = \nabla\cdot(\Delta\vec D)$, and by product rule (iii) of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], $(\nabla\cdot\Delta\vec D)V = \nabla\cdot(V\Delta\vec D) + \Delta\vec D\cdot\vec E$, with $\vec E = -\nabla V$. Over all space the divergence term becomes a surface integral at infinity, which vanishes for localized charges. This gives eq. (4.57), with no assumption about the material. If $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$ with $\varepsilon$ fixed, then $\frac12\Delta(\vec D\cdot\vec E) = \frac12\Delta(\varepsilon E^2) = \varepsilon\vec E\cdot\Delta\vec E = (\Delta\vec D)\cdot\vec E$ for infinitesimal increments, so $\Delta W = \Delta\bigl(\frac12\int\vec D\cdot\vec E\,d\tau\bigr)$; adding up from zero gives eq. (4.58). Integrating by parts backwards, $\frac12\int\vec D\cdot(-\nabla V)\,d\tau = \frac12\int(\nabla\cdot\vec D)V\,d\tau = \frac12\int\rho_fV\,d\tau$.

^der-b5-3-5

*Uses:* [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]], [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^def-b5-3-1|Def. §B5.3.1]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Two energies, and the springs
> The vacuum formula $W = \frac{\varepsilon_0}2\int E^2\,d\tau$ ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]], [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]]; notation, [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^cau-b2-3-1|§B2.3, Caution: Notation for energy]]) is not wrong in matter; it answers a different question (Griffiths §4.4.3). It is the work to assemble *all* charges, free and bound, one by one into their final places, and it leaves out the energy of stretching and twisting the molecules. $\frac12\int\vec D\cdot\vec E\,d\tau$ is the work to bring in the *free* charges while the dielectric responds, and it includes that "spring" energy, since the force needed to move a free charge depends on where the bound charges are. Griffiths' Example 4.9, a sphere of radius $R$ with embedded free charge $\rho_f$:
>
> $$
> W_1 = \frac{2\pi}{9\varepsilon_0}\rho_f^2R^5\Bigl(\frac{1}{5\varepsilon_r^2} + 1\Bigr), \qquad W_2 = \frac{2\pi}{9\varepsilon_0}\rho_f^2R^5\Bigl(\frac{1}{5\varepsilon_r} + 1\Bigr), \qquad W_2 - W_1 = \frac{2\pi}{45\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r^2}\rho_f^2R^5(\varepsilon_r - 1) ,
> $$
>
> and the difference is exactly $\sum\frac12kd^2$ for molecules modelled as charges $\pm q$ on springs of constant $k$, stretched by the macroscopic field ($qE = kd$). Nelson puts it as two springs in series: per unit volume the stored energy is $\frac12\varepsilon_0E^2 + \frac12kd^2n = \frac12\varepsilon E^2$, and at fixed free charge the system minimizes it by cancelling part, but not all, of the free charge with bound charge. Total cancellation would make the field energy zero but the spring energy larger. That is why the polarization is partial, and why the energy at fixed free charge, $\sigma_f^2/2\varepsilon$ per volume for a capacitor, drops when a dielectric enters.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.3, Example 4.9 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 6, §6.3.2, eqs. (6.14)–(6.16)*

^rem-b5-3-4

> [!caution] Caution: Only for linear dielectrics
> Eq. (4.57) holds for any material, but the step to $\frac12\int\vec D\cdot\vec E\,d\tau$ needs linearity. For a dissipative material (molecular "springs" with friction, hysteresis) the work depends on how the final state was reached, and "stored energy" loses its meaning; applied to an electret with frozen-in polarization, eq. (4.58) gives nonsense (Griffiths Problem 4.27).

^cau-b5-3-2

## Forces on dielectrics

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.6: Force on a Dielectric in a Capacitor
> The energy method of [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-4|Theorem §B2.4.4]] holds unchanged when linear dielectrics are present, with $C$ the capacitance including the dielectric: a piece of linear dielectric in a capacitor, positioned by one coordinate $x$, feels $F = \frac12V^2\,dC/dx$, pulling it toward larger capacitance. For a slab of width $w$ filling the gap $d$ of a parallel-plate capacitor of length $l$, with a length $x$ not yet inserted, $C = \frac{\varepsilon_0w}{d}(\varepsilon_rl - \chi_ex)$ (Griffiths eq. (4.62)) and
>
> $$
> F = -\frac{\varepsilon_0\chi_ew}{2d}\,V^2 \qquad \text{(eq. (4.65))},
> $$
>
> independent of how far the slab is in: it is pulled into the capacitor.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.4, eqs. (4.59)–(4.67) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 6, §6.3.2*

^thm-b5-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Theorem §B2.4.4 rests on energy conservation in a slow displacement and on $W = Q^2/2C$. What is new with a dielectric is which energy to use. The molecular "springs" store their energy reversibly in a linear dielectric, and the work needed to set up the free charges, springs included, is $W = \frac12\int\vec D\cdot\vec E\,d\tau = \frac12\int\rho_fV\,d\tau$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]), which for two conductors carrying $\pm Q$ is $\frac12QV = Q^2/2C$; the vacuum expression $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$ would leave out the springs ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^rem-b5-3-4|Remark: Two energies, and the springs]]). With this $W$, Theorem §B2.4.4 gives $F = \frac12V^2\,dC/dx$ at fixed charge or fixed voltage alike; the force is fixed by the distribution of free and bound charge. For the slab, the filled length $l - x$ and the empty length $x$ are capacitors in parallel ([[§A4.2 Capacitors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]), with the filled part multiplied by $\varepsilon_r$ (Theorem §B5.3.3 (3)): $C = \frac{\varepsilon_0w}{d}[\varepsilon_r(l - x) + x]$, so $dC/dx = -\varepsilon_0\chi_ew/d$.

> [!remark] Remark: The fringing field does the pulling
> In the idealized capacitor the field is uniform inside, zero outside and perpendicular to the plates, and could exert no sideways force at all. The force comes from the fringing field at the edge, which must exist: if the field stopped abruptly, $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l$ around a loop half inside and half outside would not vanish (Griffiths Fig. 4.31). The bound charge it induces on the slab's edge sits next to free charge of the opposite sign and is attracted. The energy method gets the force "without knowing anything about the fringing fields", by exploiting $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$; the fringing energy stays constant as the slab moves, and what changes is the energy deep inside. Level A stated the result for an isolated capacitor ($U_0 \to U_0/\kappa$, [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^rem-a4-4-3|§A4.4, Remark: The dielectric is pulled in]]) and found at fixed voltage that the battery supplies twice the gain in stored energy ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^ex-a4-4-3|Example §A4.4.3]]): the other half is the work $\int F\,dx$ just computed. A spherical version, a conducting sphere surrounded by a dielectric pulled inward by the field, is PHY 422's Midterm 2, Problem 2 ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^ex-b2-4-3|Example §B2.4.3]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.4, Figure 4.31 · PHY 422, Midterm 2 (Problem 2, Capacitor compaction)*

^rem-b5-3-5

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: From molecules to the dielectric constant
> - *Nonpolar molecules (Clausius–Mossotti).* If each molecule has polarizability $\alpha$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^law-b4-2-3|Law §B4.2.3]]) and there are $N$ per volume, $\vec P = N\alpha\vec E_{\text{else}}$, where $\vec E_{\text{else}}$ is the field due to everything *except* the molecule itself, not the macroscopic $\vec E$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^rem-b5-1-4|§B5.1, Remark: Macroscopic, not local]]). Give each molecule a sphere of its own volume; its average contribution to $\vec E$ is $-\vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]), so $\vec E_{\text{else}} = \vec E + \vec P/3\varepsilon_0$. Then $\vec P(1 - N\alpha/3\varepsilon_0) = N\alpha\vec E$, i.e. $\vec E = (1 - N\alpha/3\varepsilon_0)\vec E_{\text{else}}$ (Griffiths eq. (4.71)), and
>
> $$
> \chi_e = \frac{N\alpha/\varepsilon_0}{1 - N\alpha/3\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{3\varepsilon_0}{N}\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 2}\Bigr)
> $$
>
>   (Griffiths eq. (4.72), the Clausius–Mossotti formula; in optics, with $\varepsilon_r = n^2$, the Lorentz–Lorenz equation). At low density it reduces to $\chi_e = N\alpha/\varepsilon_0$ (eq. (4.70)). Nelson derives it the same way with a spherical cavity cut around one molecule (§6.3.3), and notes that real materials follow it only approximately, with factors of order one in place of $\frac13$.
> - *Polar molecules (Langevin).* Molecules with permanent moment $p$, distributed by the Boltzmann factor $e^{pE\cos\theta/kT}$, give $P = Np[\coth(pE/kT) - kT/pE]$ (Griffiths eq. (4.73), Problem 4.43), which for $pE \ll kT$ is linear with $\chi_e = Np^2/3\varepsilon_0kT$. The $1/T$ law is the electric analogue of Curie's law for paramagnets ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^law-b6-2-10|TH Law §B6.2.10]]). Alignment is limited by entropy rather than by a restoring spring, which is why liquid water is so polarizable ($\varepsilon_r \approx 80$), and why the orientational response is too slow to follow optical fields (Nelson §6.6). Nelson adds that ice, whose molecules cannot turn freely, has a much smaller permittivity; that holds only for fields that change too fast for the molecules of ice to follow, since its static dielectric constant is even larger than water's (Griffiths Table 4.2: $\varepsilon_r = 104$ at $-30$ °C).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 4.41–4.43, eqs. (4.70)–(4.73) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 6, §6.3.3, eq. (6.18), and §6.6*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B5.3.1: A Point Charge above a Dielectric Half-Space
> The region $z < 0$ is filled with a linear dielectric of susceptibility $\chi_e$, and a point charge $q$ sits at height $d$ above it (Griffiths Example 4.8). By Theorem §B5.3.3 (1) there is no volume bound charge; the surface charge is $\sigma_b = \vec P\cdot\hat z = \varepsilon_0\chi_eE_z$, with $E_z$ the total field just inside, at $z = 0^-$. That field is the field of $q$, $-qd/[4\pi\varepsilon_0(r^2 + d^2)^{3/2}]$ with $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, plus the field $-\sigma_b/2\varepsilon_0$ of the bound sheet just below itself. Solving the resulting linear equation for $\sigma_b$ (Griffiths eqs. (4.50)–(4.51)),
>
> $$
> \sigma_b = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\Bigl(\frac{\chi_e}{\chi_e + 2}\Bigr)\frac{qd}{(r^2 + d^2)^{3/2}}, \qquad q_b = \int\sigma_b\,da = -\frac{\chi_e}{\chi_e + 2}\,q .
> $$
>
> *Images* (as in [[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]]). For $z > 0$ take $q$ plus an image $q_b$ at $(0, 0, -d)$; for $z < 0$ take a single charge $q + q_b$ at the position of $q$ (eqs. (4.52)–(4.53)). Each image lies outside the region it serves, both potentials agree on $z = 0$, and the normal derivatives jump as $\sigma_b$ requires; equivalently $\varepsilon_0E_z(0^+) = \varepsilon E_z(0^-)$, which reads $-q + q_b = -\varepsilon_r(q + q_b)$ and gives the same $q_b = -\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 1}q$. By uniqueness (Theorem §B5.3.3 (5)) this is the solution. The charge is attracted with (eq. (4.54))
>
> $$
> \vec F = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\frac{\chi_e}{\chi_e + 2}\Bigr)\frac{q^2}{4d^2}\,\hat z ,
> $$
>
> which tends to the image force on a grounded conducting plane as $\chi_e \to \infty$. Below the surface the field is the vacuum field of a single point charge $q + q_b = \frac{2}{\varepsilon_r + 1}q$ at the position of $q$: the field lines there are straight and radiate from $q$'s position, and they bend at the surface according to Theorem §B5.3.3 (4).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.2, Example 4.8, eqs. (4.50)–(4.54)*

*Example §B5.3.1 with $\varepsilon_r = 4$: computed field lines in a plane through $q$. Above the surface they are the lines of $q$ and its image $q_b = -\frac35q$; below, they are straight lines from the position of $q$, bent away from the normal on entering the dielectric. Drawn this way the curves are lines of $\vec D$, which pass through the surface without ending; the lines of $\vec E$ have the same shape, but below the surface only a fraction $1/\varepsilon_r$ of them continue, the rest ending on the negative bound charge (marked) that pulls $q$ down.*

> [!example] Example §B5.3.2: A Dielectric Liquid Rising between Coaxial Tubes
> Two long coaxial metal tubes (inner radius $a$, outer radius $b$) stand vertically in a pool of dielectric liquid of susceptibility $\chi_e$ and mass density $\rho$; the inner tube is held at potential $V$, the outer one grounded. How high does the liquid rise between the tubes? (Griffiths Problem 4.28; PHY 422, HW 8 (2023 typed solutions), Problem 1, the dielectric column; the problem is not in the 2024 HW 8.)
>
> The coaxial capacitor per unit length has capacitance $2\pi\varepsilon/\ln(b/a)$ ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-5|Theorem §A4.1.5]] with $\varepsilon_0 \to \varepsilon$, Theorem §B5.3.3 (3)). The filled and empty lengths are capacitors in parallel, so when the liquid column rises by $dh$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dC}{dh} = \frac{2\pi(\varepsilon - \varepsilon_0)}{\ln(b/a)} = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\chi_e}{\ln(b/a)}, \qquad F_{\uparrow} = \frac12V^2\frac{dC}{dh} = \frac{\pi\varepsilon_0\chi_eV^2}{\ln(b/a)}
> $$
>
> by [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-6|Theorem §B5.3.6]], independent of $h$. It balances the weight of the raised column, $\rho g\,\pi(b^2 - a^2)h$, at
>
> $$
> h = \frac{\varepsilon_0\chi_eV^2}{\rho g\,(b^2 - a^2)\ln(b/a)} .
> $$
>
> This agrees with the instructor's typed solution, $h = V_0^2(\varepsilon - \varepsilon_0)/[\rho g(R_2^2 - R_1^2)\ln(R_2/R_1)]$, which minimizes the total energy $\frac12\pi(R_2^2 - R_1^2)\rho gh^2 + Q^2/2C$ at fixed $Q$; as its note explains, holding $Q$ fixed avoids having to add the battery's energy, and the result cannot depend on that choice (Theorem §B5.3.6). With illustrative values $\chi_e = 1.3$, $\rho = 900\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $a = 2$ mm, $b = 4$ mm and $V = 10$ kV,
>
> $$
> h = \frac{(8.85\times10^{-12})(1.3)(10^4)^2}{(900)(9.8)(1.2\times10^{-5})(\ln 2)}\ \mathrm m = \frac{1.15\times10^{-3}}{7.3\times10^{-2}}\ \mathrm m \approx 1.6\ \mathrm{cm} .
> $$
>
> Capillarity is ignored. Nelson describes the same effect for parallel plates: a fluid dielectric "will be pulled into the space between charged plates, even if it must overcome gravity to do so" (§6.3.2).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 4.28 · PHY 422, HW 8 (2023 typed solutions), Problem 1 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 6, §6.3.2*
