What field does a piece of polarized matter produce? Level A answered for one geometry: a slab between capacitor plates, whose aligned molecular dipoles leave induced charges $\mp Q_i$ on its two faces ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^mod-a4-4-3|Model §A4.4.3]]), with $Q_i$ fixed by the empirical dielectric constant ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^thm-a4-4-4|Theorem §A4.4.4]]). This section describes the state of the material by a vector field, the polarization $\vec P$, and proves for a body of any shape and any polarization, uniform or not, that its field is that of a surface charge $\vec P\cdot\hat n$ and a volume charge $-\nabla\cdot\vec P$. The induced and aligned dipoles that make up $\vec P$ are those of [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole|§B4.2]]; how $\vec P$ depends on the field is the subject of [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics|§B5.3]]. The magnetic counterpart of everything here is [[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents|§B7.1]].

## Polarization

> [!definition] Definition §B5.1.1: Dielectric; Polarization
> A **dielectric** (insulator) is a material in which every charge is attached to a particular atom or molecule and can move only a little within it. In an electric field each atom or molecule acquires a dipole moment along the field, either because the field stretches it (an induced dipole) or because it turns a molecule that already has one (a permanent dipole; [[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]). The **polarization** is the dipole moment per unit volume (Griffiths §4.1.4),
>
> $$
> \vec P = \frac{d\vec p}{d\tau} ,
> $$
>
> so that the molecules in a volume element $d\tau$ have total dipole moment $\vec P\,d\tau$. It is a macroscopic field: $d\tau$ is large compared with molecular sizes and small compared with the distances over which $\vec P$ changes. Its SI unit is $\mathrm{C/m^2}$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.1.1, §4.1.4*

^def-b5-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Where the polarization comes from
> - *Stretching.* A neutral atom in a field $\vec E$ acquires $\vec p = \alpha\vec E$, with the atomic polarizability $\alpha$ (Griffiths eq. (4.1)); a crude model gives $\alpha = 4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3$ for an atom of radius $a$ (Example 4.1), and an asymmetric molecule needs a polarizability tensor (eq. (4.3)). *Turning.* A polar molecule feels the torque $\vec p\times\vec E$, which tends to line it up, while thermal collisions oppose the alignment. Both mechanisms belong to [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole|§B4.2]] ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^law-b4-2-3|Law §B4.2.3]], [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]]); for polar molecules turning usually dominates, because it is easier to rotate a molecule than to stretch it.
> - Either way the result is "a lot of little dipoles pointing along the direction of the field" (Griffiths). In some materials the polarization can be **frozen in** and persists after the field is removed (electrets; barium titanate is the familiar example, Griffiths Problem 4.11).
> - From here to the end of [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement|§B5.2]] $\vec P$ is taken as given, whatever caused it, and the question is purely electrostatic: what field does it produce?

^rem-b5-1-1

## The field of a polarized object

> [!definition] Definition §B5.1.2: Bound Charge Densities
> Let a dielectric occupy the volume $\mathcal V$ with boundary surface $\mathcal S$, and let $\vec P$ be continuously differentiable inside $\mathcal V$. The **bound surface charge density** on $\mathcal S$ and the **bound volume charge density** in $\mathcal V$ are (Griffiths eqs. (4.11)–(4.12))
>
> $$
> \sigma_b = \vec P\cdot\hat n , \qquad \rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\vec P ,
> $$
>
> where $\hat n$ is the unit normal pointing out of the dielectric.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.2.1, eqs. (4.11)–(4.12)*

^def-b5-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.1: Potential of a Polarized Object
> The potential produced by the polarization itself (not by whatever caused it) is, with $\vec{\mathcal R} = \vec r - \vec r\,'$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]),
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}\frac{\vec P(\vec r\,')\cdot\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\,d\tau'
> = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint_{\mathcal S}\frac{\sigma_b}{\mathcal R}\,da' + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}\frac{\rho_b}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau'
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (4.9), (4.13)). A polarized body therefore produces exactly the field of its bound charges, which can be found by every method that works for ordinary charge: Coulomb integrals, Gauss's law, separation of variables. The derivation uses the perfect-dipole potential, which is accurate at points outside the body; at points inside it, the same formula gives the macroscopic field ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.2.1, eqs. (4.8)–(4.13)*

^thm-b5-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A point dipole $\vec p$ at $\vec r\,'$ has the potential $\vec p\cdot\hat{\mathcal R}/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\mathcal R^2)$ (Griffiths eq. (4.8); [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]]). The element $d\tau'$ carries $\vec p = \vec P\,d\tau'$, and potentials superpose; this is the first form. Differentiating with respect to the source coordinates, $\nabla'(1/\mathcal R) = \hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2$, so by product rule (iii) of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]
>
> $$
> \frac{\vec P\cdot\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2} = \vec P\cdot\nabla'\Bigl(\frac{1}{\mathcal R}\Bigr) = \nabla'\cdot\Bigl(\frac{\vec P}{\mathcal R}\Bigr) - \frac{1}{\mathcal R}\,\nabla'\cdot\vec P .
> $$
>
> The divergence theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]) turns the volume integral of the first term into $\oint_{\mathcal S}\vec P\cdot\hat n\,da'/\mathcal R$ (Griffiths eq. (4.10)), which is the $\sigma_b$ term; the second term is the $\rho_b$ term.

^der-b5-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Bound charge is real charge
> The bound charges came out of a manipulation of an integral, but they are "perfectly genuine accumulations of charge" (Griffiths §4.2.2). In a chain of aligned dipoles the head of each cancels the tail of the next, and only the two ends are left charged. Cut a tube of cross-section $A$ parallel to $\vec P$: a chunk of length $d$ has dipole moment $P A d$, which is $q d$ with $q$ the charge at its end, so $q = PA$ piles up at a perpendicular end face, $\sigma_b = P$. An oblique cut at angle $\theta$ to $\vec P$ has area $A/\cos\theta$, so $\sigma_b = P\cos\theta = \vec P\cdot\hat n$. Nelson counts the same layer directly: molecules of number density $n$, each with charges $\pm q_1$ displaced by $d_1$, leave an uncancelled layer of thickness $d_1$ at each face, with charge per area $n q_1d_1 = P$ (Nelson §6.3.1). If $\vec P$ is not uniform, more charge is pushed out of a small region on one side than is pushed in on the other, and a volume charge remains inside ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]]). "Bound" only says that the charge cannot be removed: every electron stays with its molecule, but no single electron makes the whole trip.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.2.2, Figures 4.11–4.14 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 6, §6.3.1, eq. (6.4), and §6.4*

^rem-b5-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.2: Bound Charge in a Region; Total Bound Charge
> 1. For every region $\mathcal V'$ inside the dielectric, with boundary $\mathcal S'$, the bound charge it contains is minus the outward flux of the polarization, the charge pushed out through its surface:
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V'}\rho_b\,d\tau = -\oint_{\mathcal S'}\vec P\cdot d\vec a .
> $$
>
> 2. The total bound charge of a dielectric body vanishes:
>
> $$
> \oint_{\mathcal S}\sigma_b\,da + \int_{\mathcal V}\rho_b\,d\tau = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.2.2 and Problem 4.14*

^thm-b5-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Both are the divergence theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]) applied to $\vec P$: $\int_{\mathcal V'}\nabla\cdot\vec P\,d\tau = \oint_{\mathcal S'}\vec P\cdot d\vec a$, with $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\vec P$. For 2 take $\mathcal V' = \mathcal V$; the flux $\oint_{\mathcal S}\vec P\cdot\hat n\,da$ is then the total surface charge. Physically: polarizing a neutral body moves charge a little but creates none.

^der-b5-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Normals, interfaces and the surface layer
> - $\hat n$ points *out of* the polarized material. Where two dielectrics touch, both faces contribute: with $\hat n$ pointing from medium 1 into medium 2, the interface carries $\sigma_b = \vec P_1\cdot\hat n - \vec P_2\cdot\hat n$.
> - $\sigma_b$ is not a separate kind of charge. $\vec P$ drops to zero across the surface, so its divergence there is a delta function ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]) whose weight is $\vec P\cdot\hat n$; the surface charge is that part of $\rho_b$, handled separately for convenience (Griffiths, footnote 6). If the edge is pictured as a thin skin in which $\vec P$ tapers off, $\rho_b$ is large but finite there and no surface term is needed.

^cau-b5-1-1

## The uniformly polarized sphere

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.3: Field of a Uniformly Polarized Sphere
> A sphere of radius $R$ with uniform polarization $\vec P$ carries no volume bound charge and the surface charge $\sigma_b = P\cos\theta$ ($\theta$ measured from $\vec P$). Its field is **uniform** inside,
>
> $$
> \vec E = -\frac{1}{3\varepsilon_0}\vec P \qquad (r < R),
> $$
>
> and outside it is exactly the field of a point dipole at the centre (Griffiths eqs. (4.14)–(4.16)):
>
> $$
> V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\vec p\cdot\hat r}{r^2}, \qquad \vec p = \frac43\pi R^3\vec P \qquad (r \ge R) .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.2.1, Example 4.2, eqs. (4.14)–(4.16); §4.2.2, Example 4.3*

^thm-b5-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\nabla\cdot\vec P = 0$ and $\vec P\cdot\hat r = P\cos\theta$. Following Griffiths' Example 4.3, picture the sphere as two balls of charge density $\pm\rho$, the positive one displaced from the negative one by a small vector $\vec d$: then the dipole moment per volume is $\vec P = \rho\vec d$, and where the balls do not overlap, a cap of thickness $d\cos\theta$, they leave the surface charge $\rho\,d\cos\theta = P\cos\theta$. Inside a uniformly charged ball the field is $\rho\,\vec s/(3\varepsilon_0)$, with $\vec s$ the vector from its centre ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]). In the overlap, with the centres at $\vec c_\pm$,
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{\rho}{3\varepsilon_0}(\vec r - \vec c_+) - \frac{\rho}{3\varepsilon_0}(\vec r - \vec c_-) = -\frac{\rho}{3\varepsilon_0}(\vec c_+ - \vec c_-) = -\frac{\rho\vec d}{3\varepsilon_0} = -\frac{\vec P}{3\varepsilon_0} .
> $$
>
> Outside, each ball acts as a point charge $\pm q = \pm\frac43\pi R^3\rho$ at its centre, a pair with dipole moment $q\vec d = \frac43\pi R^3\vec P$; in the limit $d \to 0$ at fixed $\vec P$ the quadrupole and higher terms vanish and the dipole potential is exact. (Griffiths' first route, Example 4.2, solves for the potential of the surface charge $P\cos\theta$ by separation of variables: $V = (P/3\varepsilon_0)\,r\cos\theta$ inside and $(P/3\varepsilon_0)(R^3/r^2)\cos\theta$ outside; [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-5|Theorem §B3.4.5]].)

^der-b5-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]], [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]], [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The field inside depends on the shape
> In a body with uniform polarization the bound charge sits on the surface, and the field it makes inside opposes $\vec P$ with a strength set by the shape. For a slab polarized across its faces (the capacitor of [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^mod-a4-4-3|Model §A4.4.3]]) the faces carry $\sigma_b = \pm P$, two opposite sheets, and $\vec E = -\vec P/\varepsilon_0$; this is level A's induced field $\vec E_i$, with $Q_i = PA$. For a sphere $\vec E = -\vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ (Theorem §B5.1.3), for a long cylinder polarized across its axis $-\vec P/2\varepsilon_0$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^ex-b5-1-2|Example §B5.1.2]]), and for a long needle polarized along its axis almost zero, because its bound charges sit on the two distant tips (the fact behind the needle-shaped cavity of Griffiths Problem 4.16, [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^ex-b5-2-3|Example §B5.2.3]]). Level A's induced surface charge is thus the special case of a theorem that holds for any shape, and the empirical factor $1/\kappa$ will reappear as a consequence of $\vec P \propto \vec E$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics|§B5.3]]).

^rem-b5-1-3

## The field inside matter

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.4: The Macroscopic Field inside a Dielectric
> Inside matter the true (microscopic) field varies wildly over atomic distances and in time. The **macroscopic field** at a point is its average over a sphere of radius $R$ about the point, with $R$ large compared with molecular sizes but small enough that $\vec P$ is nearly constant within the sphere. At points inside a dielectric the macroscopic field is $-\nabla V$, with $V$ given by [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]] integrated over the **entire** dielectric (Griffiths eq. (4.19)), even though the perfect-dipole potential used there is wrong close to each molecule. The derivation averages over a sphere; since the final formula makes no reference to the averaging region, Griffiths concludes that its shape does not matter (a cube or an ellipsoid would need a harder calculation with the same result), but this is argued, not proved here.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.2.3, eqs. (4.17)–(4.19)*

^thm-b5-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Split the charges into those outside the averaging sphere and those inside. (a) The average over a sphere of the field of charges outside it equals their field at the centre (Griffiths Problem 3.47(d); for the potential this is the mean-value property, [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]). Seen from the centre, those molecules are far away, so their field is accurately given by the perfect-dipole integral over the region outside the sphere (Griffiths eq. (4.17)). (b) The average field over a sphere due to all the charge inside it is $-\vec p/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3)$, with $\vec p$ the total dipole moment of that charge, *whatever its arrangement* (Griffiths eq. (3.105); [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^rem-b4-2-1|§B4.2, Remark: The contact term at the dipole's position]]). With $\vec p = \frac43\pi R^3\vec P$ this is $-\vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ (eq. (4.18)). (c) The term left out of the integral in (a), the sphere's interior treated as uniformly polarized, contributes at the centre exactly $-\vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]). So (a) + (b) equals the perfect-dipole integral over the whole dielectric.

^der-b5-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]], [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]], [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]], [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^rem-b4-2-1|§B4.2, Remark: The contact term at the dipole's position]]

> [!remark] Remark: Macroscopic, not local
> "The field inside matter" always means the macroscopic field, just as the density of a material is an average over many molecules (Griffiths, footnote 5). It is not the field that acts on one particular molecule, which excludes that molecule's own contribution; the difference matters for dense media ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^rem-b5-3-6|§B5.3, ★ Remark: From molecules to the dielectric constant]]).

^rem-b5-1-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B5.1.1: A Shell with Frozen-In Radial Polarization
> A thick spherical shell, $a < r < b$, carries the frozen-in polarization $\vec P = (k/r)\,\hat r$, $k$ a constant; there is no free charge (Griffiths Problem 4.15(a)). The bound charges (Def. §B5.1.2, divergence in spherical coordinates, [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]]) are
>
> $$
> \rho_b = -\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\Bigl(r^2\,\frac kr\Bigr) = -\frac{k}{r^2}, \qquad \sigma_b(b) = \vec P\cdot\hat r = \frac kb, \qquad \sigma_b(a) = \vec P\cdot(-\hat r) = -\frac ka .
> $$
>
> The arrangement is spherically symmetric, so Gauss's law with a sphere of radius $r$ gives the field ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]). For $a < r < b$ the enclosed charge is $-\frac ka\,4\pi a^2 + \int_a^r\bigl(-\frac{k}{r'^2}\bigr)4\pi r'^2\,dr' = -4\pi kr$, hence
>
> $$
> \vec E = -\frac{k}{\varepsilon_0r}\,\hat r = -\frac{\vec P}{\varepsilon_0} \quad (a < r < b), \qquad \vec E = \vec 0 \quad (r < a \text{ and } r > b) .
> $$
>
> Outside, the enclosed charge is $-4\pi kb + \frac kb\,4\pi b^2 = 0$, as [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]] requires. The field lives only inside the material; [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^ex-b5-2-1|Example §B5.2.1]] gets it in one line, without locating any bound charge.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 4.15(a)*

^ex-b5-1-1

> [!example] Example §B5.1.2: A Long Cylinder Polarized across Its Axis
> A very long cylinder of radius $a$ carries a uniform polarization $\vec P$ perpendicular to its axis (Griffiths Problem 4.13). Then $\rho_b = 0$ and $\sigma_b = \vec P\cdot\hat s = P\cos\phi$, with $\phi$ measured from $\vec P$. The field is
>
> $$
> \vec E = -\frac{\vec P}{2\varepsilon_0} \quad (s < a), \qquad \vec E = \frac{a^2}{2\varepsilon_0s^2}\bigl[2(\vec P\cdot\hat s)\,\hat s - \vec P\bigr] \quad (s > a) .
> $$
>
> As for the sphere, use two cylinders of charge density $\pm\rho$ displaced by $\vec d$, with $\vec P = \rho\vec d$. Inside a uniformly charged cylinder the field is $\rho\,\vec s/(2\varepsilon_0)$, with $\vec s$ the perpendicular vector from its axis ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-3|Theorem §A2.3.3]]); in the overlap the two terms combine to $-\rho\vec d/2\varepsilon_0 = -\vec P/2\varepsilon_0$. Outside, each cylinder acts as a line charge $\pm\lambda$, $\lambda = \pi a^2\rho$, with field $\lambda\vec s/(2\pi\varepsilon_0s^2)$. Displacing the positive one by $+\vec d/2$ and the negative one by $-\vec d/2$ gives, to first order in $d$, $-(\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)(\vec d\cdot\nabla)(\vec s/s^2)$, and
>
> $$
> (\vec d\cdot\nabla)\frac{\vec s}{s^2} = \frac{\vec d}{s^2} - \frac{2(\vec d\cdot\vec s)\,\vec s}{s^4}
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> \vec E = \frac{\pi a^2\rho}{2\pi\varepsilon_0s^2}\bigl[2(\vec d\cdot\hat s)\hat s - \vec d\bigr] ,
> $$
>
> which is the stated result: the field of a two-dimensional dipole of moment $\pi a^2\vec P$ per unit length. The internal factor $\frac12$ (sphere $\frac13$) is the shape dependence of [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^rem-b5-1-3|Remark: The field inside depends on the shape]]; the same geometry returns for a magnetizable cylinder in a transverse field ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^ex-b7-2-2|Example §B7.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 4.13*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The step from dipoles to bound charges is integration by parts: product rule plus divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]; [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]).
> - At optical frequencies $\vec P$ oscillates with the field and follows it with a frequency-dependent, complex susceptibility ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-1|WO Def. §B13.1.1]]), computed there from the Lorentz model of bound electrons.
> - Magnetic counterpart: bound currents $\nabla\times\vec M$ and $\vec M\times\hat n$, [[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents|§B7.1]].
