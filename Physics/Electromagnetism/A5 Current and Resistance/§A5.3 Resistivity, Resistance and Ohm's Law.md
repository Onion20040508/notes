A potential difference across a conductor creates a field inside it, and the field drives a current. How much current flows depends on the material and on the shape of the object. This section separates the two: resistivity is a property of the material, resistance a property of a particular piece of it. Ohm's law, the empirical statement that the current is proportional to the voltage, holds for metals and resistors but not for every device.

## Conductivity and resistivity

> [!law] Law §A5.3.1: Ohm's Law, Microscopic Form
> In many materials, metals in particular, the current density at each point is proportional to the electric field there:
>
> $$
> \vec J = \sigma\,\vec E ,
> $$
>
> where the **conductivity** $\sigma$ is a constant of the material at a given temperature, independent of $\vec E$.
>
> *Valid when: metals and many other conductors at fixed temperature, for fields of ordinary strength; it fails for semiconductor devices such as diodes, for gas discharges, and in superconductors.*
> *Source: UP2 §9.3–9.4*

^law-a5-3-1

> [!definition] Definition §A5.3.1: Conductivity; Resistivity; Ohm
> The **conductivity** $\sigma = J/E$ measures how readily a material conducts, and the **resistivity** is its reciprocal,
>
> $$
> \rho = \frac{1}{\sigma} = \frac{E}{J} ,
> $$
>
> the field needed per unit current density. The unit of resistance is the **ohm**, $1\ \Omega = 1\ \mathrm{V/A}$; resistivity is measured in $\Omega\cdot\mathrm m$ and conductivity in $(\Omega\cdot\mathrm m)^{-1}$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.3, eq. (9.6)*

^def-a5-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why the microscopic law holds
> For a metal, [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-4|Theorem §A5.2.4]] explains the law: collisions with the lattice keep the drift velocity proportional to the field, and $\sigma = nq^2\tau/m$. The empirical law and the model agree; the model also says what $\sigma$ depends on (carrier density and collision time).

^rem-a5-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Conductors, semiconductors, insulators
> Resistivities at $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ span more than 24 orders of magnitude:
>
> | Material | $\rho\ (\Omega\cdot\mathrm m)$ | $\alpha\ (^\circ\mathrm C^{-1})$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | silver | $1.59\times 10^{-8}$ | $0.0038$ |
> | copper | $1.68\times 10^{-8}$ | $0.0039$ |
> | aluminium | $2.65\times 10^{-8}$ | $0.0039$ |
> | tungsten | $5.60\times 10^{-8}$ | $0.0045$ |
> | iron | $9.71\times 10^{-8}$ | $0.0065$ |
> | manganin (Cu, Mn, Ni) | $4.82\times 10^{-7}$ | $0.000002$ |
> | nichrome (Ni, Fe, Cr) | $1.00\times 10^{-6}$ | $0.0004$ |
> | carbon (pure) | $3.5\times 10^{-5}$ | $-0.0005$ |
> | germanium (pure) | $0.60$ | $-0.048$ |
> | silicon (pure) | $2300$ | $-0.075$ |
> | glass | $10^{9}$–$10^{14}$ | |
> | fused quartz | $7.5\times 10^{17}$ | |
>
> Conductors have many free charges; in insulators nearly all charges are bound; semiconductors are in between, with a carrier density that depends strongly on impurities. The temperature coefficient $\alpha$ is defined in Law §A5.3.2.
>
> *Source: UP2 Table 9.1*

^rem-a5-3-2

> [!law] Law §A5.3.2: Temperature Dependence of Resistivity
> Over a moderate temperature range the resistivity depends linearly on temperature,
>
> $$
> \rho \approx \rho_0\bigl[1 + \alpha\,(T - T_0)\bigr],
> $$
>
> where $\rho_0$ is the resistivity at the reference temperature $T_0$ (usually $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$) and $\alpha$ is the **temperature coefficient of resistivity** of the material. For metals $\alpha > 0$: hotter lattices vibrate more and scatter electrons more. For semiconductors $\alpha < 0$: heating frees more carriers.
>
> *Valid when: $|T - T_0|$ up to roughly $100\ ^\circ\mathrm C$; for tungsten it works well over thousands of degrees. Values depend on purity, strongly so for semiconductors.*
> *Source: UP2 §9.3, eq. (9.7)*

^law-a5-3-2

## Resistance

> [!definition] Definition §A5.3.2: Resistance
> The **resistance** of a component (a wire, a resistor, a lamp) is the ratio of the voltage across it to the current through it,
>
> $$
> R = \frac{V}{I} .
> $$
>
> Its unit is the ohm. Resistivity belongs to a material; resistance belongs to a particular object made of it. A **resistor** is a component built to have a definite resistance.
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.3, eq. (9.8)*

^def-a5-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.3.3: Resistance of a Uniform Conductor
> A conductor of length $L$, uniform cross-sectional area $A$ and resistivity $\rho$, with the current flowing along its length, has resistance
>
> $$
> R = \frac{\rho L}{A} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.3, eq. (9.9)*

^thm-a5-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A potential difference $V$ between the ends sets up a uniform field along the conductor of magnitude $E = V/L$ ([[§A3.4 Field from Potential|§A3.4]]), and the current spreads uniformly, so $J = I/A$. The microscopic law, $E = \rho J$, then reads
>
> $$
> \frac{V}{L} = \rho\,\frac{I}{A} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V = \frac{\rho L}{A}\,I ,
> $$
>
> so $V/I = \rho L/A$. This ratio does not depend on $V$: a material obeying the microscopic Ohm's law makes components obeying the macroscopic one (Law §A5.3.5).

^der-a5-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-1|Law §A5.3.1]], [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^def-a5-3-1|Def. §A5.3.1]], [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^def-a5-3-2|Def. §A5.3.2]], [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^def-a5-2-3|Def. §A5.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: The pipe picture
> Resistance grows with length and shrinks with cross-section, like the resistance of a pipe to the flow of water. Typical values: a metre of thick copper wire, about $10^{-5}\ \Omega$; the human heart, about $10^3\ \Omega$; a dry person from hand to foot, about $10^5\ \Omega$; the ceramic insulators that hold power lines, $10^{12}\ \Omega$ or more.

^rem-a5-3-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.3.4: Temperature Dependence of Resistance
> For a component whose dimensions change little with temperature,
>
> $$
> R = R_0\,(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T),
> $$
>
> with $R_0$ the resistance at the reference temperature and $\alpha$ the coefficient of Law §A5.3.2.
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.3, eq. (9.10)*

^thm-a5-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^thm-a5-3-3|Theorem §A5.3.3]], $R = \rho L/A$. Thermal expansion changes $L$ and $A$ about a hundred times less than the temperature changes $\rho$, so $L/A$ may be treated as constant, and $R$ inherits the temperature dependence of $\rho$ from [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-2|Law §A5.3.2]]. Thermistor thermometers use exactly this effect, with a semiconductor of large $|\alpha|$.

^der-a5-3-4

*Uses:* [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^thm-a5-3-3|Theorem §A5.3.3]], [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-2|Law §A5.3.2]]

## Ohm's law

> [!law] Law §A5.3.5: Ohm's Law
> For many components the current is proportional to the voltage applied,
>
> $$
> V = IR \quad\text{with } R \text{ independent of } V \text{ and } I .
> $$
>
> Such components are called **ohmic**; the others are **nonohmic**. Reversing the voltage reverses the current, with the same magnitude.
>
> *Valid when: metal wires and resistors at constant temperature. Not for diodes and other semiconductor devices, and only approximately for an incandescent filament, whose temperature (hence $R$) rises with the current.*
> *Source: UP2 §9.4, eq. (9.11)*

^law-a5-3-5

> [!caution] Caution: $R = V/I$ is not Ohm's law
> - $R = V/I$ defines resistance for *any* component at a given operating point. Ohm's law is the separate, empirical claim that this ratio is the same at every voltage. It is not a law of nature like Newton's laws: it is a property that some materials have.
> - A **diode** is nonohmic. In reverse bias the current is essentially zero (until a large breakdown voltage is reached); in forward bias it stays near zero until about $0.7\ \mathrm V$ and then rises steeply, so that the diode's voltage stays near $0.7\ \mathrm V$ over a wide range of currents. A resistor in series limits the current.
> - Ohm (1827) found $I \propto V$ for metal wires. Kirchhoff later recast the law in the microscopic form $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ of Law §A5.3.1, which is the more basic one.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.3.1: A Length of Copper Wire
> A copper wire $L = 5.00\ \mathrm m$ long, of cross-section $A = 3.31\ \mathrm{mm^2}$ (12-gauge), carries $I = 10.0\ \mathrm{mA}$. With $\rho = 1.68\times 10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot\mathrm m$,
>
> $$
> J = \frac{I}{A} = 3.02\times 10^{3}\ \mathrm{A/m^2}, \qquad R = \frac{\rho L}{A} = 2.54\times 10^{-2}\ \Omega, \qquad E = \rho J = 5.08\times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{V/m} .
> $$
>
> The voltage across the wire is $V = IR = 0.254\ \mathrm{mV}$. The current density and the field do not depend on the length of the wire; the resistance and the voltage do. Copper's low resistivity, strength and ductility make it the standard wire.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 9.5*

^ex-a5-3-1

> [!example] Example §A5.3.2: A Lamp Filament Heats Up
> A tungsten filament has $R_0 = 0.350\ \Omega$ at $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. At its operating temperature of $2850\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ ($\Delta T = 2830\ ^\circ\mathrm C$), with $\alpha = 4.5\times 10^{-3}\ ^\circ\mathrm C^{-1}$, [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^thm-a5-3-4|Theorem §A5.3.4]] gives
>
> $$
> R = R_0(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T) = (0.350\ \Omega)\bigl[1 + (4.5\times 10^{-3})(2830)\bigr] = 4.8\ \Omega .
> $$
>
> The resistance grows by a factor of almost 14. At a fixed voltage, the current when the lamp is switched on cold is therefore much larger than in steady operation, which is when filaments tend to fail.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 9.6*

^ex-a5-3-2

> [!example] Example §A5.3.3: Leakage Resistance of a Coaxial Cable
> A coaxial cable of length $L$ has an inner conductor of radius $r_i$ and an outer conductor of radius $r_o$, separated by an insulator of resistivity $\rho$. A small leakage current flows radially from one conductor to the other. $R = \rho L/A$ cannot be used directly, because the area crossed grows with $r$. Divide the insulator into thin cylindrical shells of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$. Each is a conductor of length $dr$ (along the current) and area $2\pi r L$, with resistance $dR = \rho\,dr/(2\pi r L)$. The current passes through the shells one after the other, so their resistances add ([[§A6.2 Resistors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a6-2-1|Theorem §A6.2.1]]):
>
> $$
> R = \int_{r_i}^{r_o} \frac{\rho\,dr}{2\pi r L} = \frac{\rho}{2\pi L}\,\ln\frac{r_o}{r_i} .
> $$
>
> The leakage resistance falls as the cable gets longer (more parallel paths) and depends on the radii only through their ratio. Since it is finite, some signal leaks away; good insulators keep it small.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 9.7*

^ex-a5-3-3

One thin shell of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ (red) acts as a resistor of length $dr$ and area $2\pi rL$; the shells are in series.*

## Superconductors

> [!remark] Remark: Zero resistance
> In 1911 Heike Kamerlingh Onnes cooled mercury with liquid helium. Its resistance fell linearly with temperature, as Law §A5.3.2 predicts, and then at about $4.2\ \mathrm K$, the **critical temperature** $T_c$, dropped abruptly to exactly zero. This is **superconductivity**.
> - *Consequences.* With $R = 0$ there are no $I^2R$ losses, and a current set up in a superconducting loop persists without any source; such currents have been observed to last for years. A superconductor also expels magnetic fields (the **Meissner effect**), so a small magnet levitates above it.
> - *Materials.* Many good conductors (copper, silver, gold) never become superconducting. Type I superconductors are about 30 pure metals, with low critical fields above which superconductivity is destroyed. Type II materials tolerate much higher fields and currents; they include alloys such as niobium–titanium and the ceramic high-temperature superconductors, starting with yttrium barium copper oxide (YBCO, $T_c = 92\ \mathrm K$, 1986), which can be cooled with liquid nitrogen.
> - *Mechanism.* In BCS theory (Bardeen, Cooper, Schrieffer) electrons near the Fermi energy pair up through lattice vibrations into Cooper pairs, which behave like bosons and condense into one state separated from the excited states by a gap of order $10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}$. Below $T_c$ thermal energy cannot bridge the gap, so the collisions that cause resistance do not occur. The details are quantum mechanics (UP Vol. 3, planned).
> - *Uses.* Superconducting magnets in MRI scanners and in particle accelerators such as the LHC; SQUIDs (superconducting loops with Josephson junctions), which detect fields as small as $10^{-14}\ \mathrm T$; maglev trains.

^rem-a5-3-4

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Example §A5.3.3 sums infinitesimal series resistances into an integral, evaluated by the [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|Fundamental Theorem of Calculus]] with the antiderivative $\ln r$ of $1/r$.
> - The microscopic law $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ is the constitutive relation that, together with Maxwell's equations, determines currents in conductors at level B.
