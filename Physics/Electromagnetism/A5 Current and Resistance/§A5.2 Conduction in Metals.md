[[§A5.1 Electric Current|§A5.1]] measured the flow of charge through a whole cross-section. This section looks inside the conductor: what carries the current in a metal, how fast the carriers move, and how to describe the flow point by point with a vector, the current density. A simple model of collisions then explains why the current density is proportional to the field, which is the content of Ohm's law ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law|§A5.3]]).

## Free electrons and drift

> [!definition] Definition §A5.2.1: Free Charges; Carrier Density
> In a metal the outermost electrons are not bound to individual atoms but move through the lattice of ions as a "sea" of **free electrons**. More generally, the **free charges** (charge carriers) of a material are the charges able to move through it. Their **number density** $n$ is the number of carriers per unit volume; it depends on the material. Insulators have essentially no free charges.
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.2*

^def-a5-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §A5.2.2: Drift Velocity
> The **drift velocity** $\vec v_d$ is the average velocity of the free charges in a conductor. Between collisions with the lattice and with each other the carriers move fast and in all directions; the drift is the small systematic part of the motion that a field adds. Positive carriers drift along $\vec E$, negative carriers against it.
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.2*

^def-a5-2-2

![[ph-em-a5-2-1.svg]]
*(a) A computed path of one electron: straight free flights in random directions between collisions (grey dots), each shifted slightly against the field $\vec E$ (blue). The net displacement (red) is along $\vec v_d$, opposite to $\vec E$. In a real metal the drift is far slower relative to the random motion than drawn. (b) Carriers drifting with speed $v_d$: in a time $dt$, all those in the shaded slab of length $v_d\,dt$ cross the cross-section $A$. The current density $\vec J$ (red) points along $\vec E$ although the electrons (blue) move the other way.*

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.2.1: Current and Drift Speed
> In a wire of cross-sectional area $A$ with carriers of charge $q$, number density $n$ and drift speed $v_d$,
>
> $$
> I = n\,|q|\,A\,v_d, \qquad\text{so}\qquad v_d = \frac{I}{n\,|q|\,A} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.2, eq. (9.4)*

^thm-a5-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In a time $dt$ every carrier moves a distance $v_d\,dt$ along the wire, so the carriers that cross a given cross-section are those in a slab of length $v_d\,dt$ next to it (figure (b)). The slab has volume $A v_d\,dt$ and contains $nAv_d\,dt$ carriers, of total charge $dQ = |q|\,nAv_d\,dt$. Dividing by $dt$ gives $I = dQ/dt = n|q|Av_d$.

^der-a5-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^def-a5-1-1|Def. §A5.1.1]], [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^def-a5-2-1|Def. §A5.2.1]], [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^def-a5-2-2|Def. §A5.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Slow carriers, fast signals
> Drift speeds in household wiring are of order $10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (Example §A5.2.1), yet a lamp lights the moment the switch closes, and electrical signals travel at about $10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$. The wire is already full of free charges. Charge pushed in at one end repels the charges ahead of it, and since the charge density of a conductor can hardly be raised, an equal amount leaves almost at once further down the line. What travels fast is the change in the electric field, not the carriers themselves, much as water comes out of a full hose at once although each drop moves slowly.

^rem-a5-2-1

## Current density

> [!definition] Definition §A5.2.3: Current Density
> The **current density** $\vec J$ at a point is the vector whose direction is that of the net flow of positive charge there and whose magnitude is the current per unit area through a small surface perpendicular to the flow. The current through a small oriented area $d\vec A$ is $dI = \vec J \cdot d\vec A = J\,dA\cos\theta$, and through a surface $S$
>
> $$
> I = \iint_S \vec J \cdot d\vec A .
> $$
>
> The SI unit of $J$ is $\mathrm{A/m^2}$. For a current spread uniformly over a cross-section $A$ perpendicular to the flow, $J = I/A$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.2, eq. (9.5)*

^def-a5-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.2.2: Current Density and Drift Velocity
> For carriers of charge $q$, number density $n$ and drift velocity $\vec v_d$,
>
> $$
> \vec J = n\,q\,\vec v_d .
> $$
>
> If several kinds of carriers move (positive and negative ions in a solution), their contributions $n_i q_i \vec v_{d,i}$ add. Whatever the sign of $q$, $\vec J$ points along the field that drives the flow.
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.2*

^thm-a5-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Dividing [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]] by the area gives the magnitude $J = I/A = n|q|v_d$. For the direction: positive carriers drift along $\vec E$, which is then the direction of positive flow; negative carriers drift against $\vec E$, which is again equivalent to positive flow along $\vec E$ ([[§A5.1 Electric Current#^def-a5-1-2|Def. §A5.1.2]]). In both cases $nq\vec v_d$ points along $\vec E$ and has magnitude $n|q|v_d$, so it is $\vec J$. For several species the charges crossing a surface simply add.

^der-a5-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]], [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^def-a5-2-3|Def. §A5.2.3]], [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^def-a5-1-2|Def. §A5.1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Signs for electrons
> With $q = -e$, the formula $v_d = I/(nqA)$ returns a negative number: the electrons move against the conventional current. Use $|q|$ for the drift *speed*, and the vector form $\vec J = nq\vec v_d$ when the direction matters. Current $I$ is a scalar; $\vec J$ is the vector.

^cau-a5-2-1

## Why the current is proportional to the field

> [!model] Model §A5.2.3: Drude Model of Conduction
> The free electrons of a metal are treated as classical point particles of mass $m$ and charge $q = -e$ that
> - move freely between collisions, accelerating only under the applied field, $\vec a = q\vec E/m$;
> - collide with the lattice, each collision giving the electron a new velocity in a random direction that has no memory of its velocity before;
> - have an average time $\tau$ since the last collision (equivalently between collisions) that does not depend on $\vec E$, because the drift is tiny compared with the random motion.
>
> The energy the field gives the electrons between collisions is handed to the lattice in the collisions, which is why a current heats a conductor ([[§A5.4 Electrical Power|§A5.4]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.2 (qualitative picture); PHY 182, week 9 slides*

^mod-a5-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.2.4: Drift Velocity and Conductivity in the Drude Model
> In the Drude model,
>
> $$
> \vec v_d = \frac{q\tau}{m}\,\vec E, \qquad \vec J = \frac{n q^2 \tau}{m}\,\vec E .
> $$
>
> The current density is proportional to the field, with a constant $\sigma = nq^2\tau/m$ that depends only on the material (and, through $\tau$, on its temperature).
>
> *Source: derived here from Model §A5.2.3; UP2 §9.2 and §9.4 state the proportionality qualitatively*

^thm-a5-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Consider an electron a time $t$ after its last collision, when its velocity was $\vec v_0$. Since then it has accelerated uniformly, so $\vec v = \vec v_0 + (q\vec E/m)\,t$. Average over all the electrons at one instant. The velocities just after collisions point in random directions, so $\langle \vec v_0 \rangle = \vec 0$, and the average time since the last collision is $\tau$. Hence
>
> $$
> \vec v_d = \langle \vec v \rangle = \frac{q\vec E}{m}\,\tau .
> $$
>
> Inserting this in [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]] gives $\vec J = nq\vec v_d = (nq^2\tau/m)\,\vec E$. Note $q^2 > 0$: the sign of the carriers drops out, as it must.

^der-a5-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^mod-a5-2-3|Model §A5.2.3]], [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Collisions act like linear drag
> The same result follows if the collisions are replaced by an average resistive force $-(m/\tau)\vec v$ on each electron: then $m\,d\vec v/dt = q\vec E - (m/\tau)\vec v$, and the velocity relaxes to the terminal value $q\tau\vec E/m$, exactly as a body falling with linear drag relaxes to its terminal speed ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^thm-a3-10-4|CM Theorem §A3.10.4]]). This is the precise sense of the book's comparison of the drift velocity with a terminal velocity. The relaxation time is $\tau \sim 10^{-14}\ \mathrm s$ (Example §A5.2.2), so in practice the drift adjusts to the field instantly.

^rem-a5-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: Limits of the model
> The Drude model is classical. It gets the proportionality $\vec J \propto \vec E$ right and gives sensible orders of magnitude, but the true motion of conduction electrons is quantum mechanical, and the collision time it extracts from data is only an effective parameter. The quantum treatment belongs to condensed-matter physics ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^rem-a6-2-4|QM Remark in §A6.2]]). In this subject the model is used only to understand why Ohm's law holds.

^cau-a5-2-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.2.1: Drift Speed in a Household Wire
> A copper wire of diameter $2.053\ \mathrm{mm}$ (12-gauge) carries $I = 20.0\ \mathrm A$. Copper has density $8.80 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and molar mass $63.54\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, with one free electron per atom.
>
> The carrier density equals the number density of atoms:
>
> $$
> n = \frac{(8.80\times 10^3\ \mathrm{kg/m^3})(6.02\times 10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}})}{0.06354\ \mathrm{kg/mol}} = 8.34 \times 10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}} .
> $$
>
> With $A = \pi (1.0265\ \mathrm{mm})^2 = 3.31 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]] gives
>
> $$
> v_d = \frac{I}{n e A} = \frac{20.0\ \mathrm A}{(8.34\times 10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}})(1.60\times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm C)(3.31\times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2})} = 4.53 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m/s},
> $$
>
> directed against the current. The current density is $J = I/A = 6.04 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{A/m^2}$. An electron needs about $37$ minutes to drift one metre, while the signal covers the same metre in about $10^{-8}\ \mathrm s$: the huge carrier density is what lets a slow drift carry a large current.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 9.3*

^ex-a5-2-1

> [!example] Example §A5.2.2: Collision Time in Copper
> Copper's conductivity at $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ is $\sigma = 5.95 \times 10^7\ (\Omega\cdot\mathrm m)^{-1}$ ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^def-a5-3-1|Def. §A5.3.1]]). Solving [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-4|Theorem §A5.2.4]] for $\tau$, with $n$ from Example §A5.2.1 and $m = 9.11\times 10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$,
>
> $$
> \tau = \frac{m\sigma}{ne^2} = \frac{(9.11\times 10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg})(5.95\times 10^{7}\ (\Omega\cdot\mathrm m)^{-1})}{(8.34\times 10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}})(1.60\times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm C)^2} = 2.5 \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm s .
> $$
>
> Check: the field in the wire of Example §A5.2.1 is $E = J/\sigma = 0.102\ \mathrm{V/m}$, and $v_d = e\tau E/m = 4.5 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m/s}$, as found there. Each electron collides some $10^{13}$–$10^{14}$ times per second.
>
> *Source: data from UP2 Table 9.1 and Example 9.3; model of §A5.2*

^ex-a5-2-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $I = \iint_S \vec J\cdot d\vec A$ is the flux of $\vec J$ through $S$, a [[§18 Surface Integrals#^def-18-6|flux integral (452 Def. §18.6)]]; $\vec J\cdot d\vec A$ is a [[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-1|dot product (LADR Def. 6.1)]].
> - The Drude relaxation is the same first-order linear differential equation as falling with linear drag ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^thm-a3-10-4|CM Theorem §A3.10.4]]) and as the charging RC circuit ([[§A6.5 RC Circuits#^thm-a6-5-1|Theorem §A6.5.1]]).
