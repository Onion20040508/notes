Chapters A1–A4 dealt with charges at rest, or with charges that moved only until they reached equilibrium. From here on, charges flow steadily through conductors. The first step is to measure that flow: current is the amount of charge that crosses a surface per unit time. A source such as a battery keeps a conductor out of electrostatic equilibrium, and the result is a steady current.

## Current and the ampere

> [!definition] Definition §A5.1.1: Electric Current; Ampere
> Let $\Delta Q$ be the net charge that crosses a given surface (for instance a cross-section of a wire) in a time $\Delta t$. The **average current** through the surface is
>
> $$
> I_{\text{ave}} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t},
> $$
>
> and the (instantaneous) **current** is its limit,
>
> $$
> I = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = \frac{dQ}{dt} .
> $$
>
> The SI unit is the **ampere**, $1\ \mathrm A = 1\ \mathrm{C/s}$. Since $e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm C$, a current of $1\ \mathrm A$ carried by electrons means $6.25 \times 10^{18}$ electrons crossing the surface each second.
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.1, eqs. (9.1)–(9.3)*

^def-a5-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: What kind of quantity current is
> Current belongs to a surface, not to a point: it is a rate of flow *through* something. It is a scalar, and its sign only says whether the net flow crosses the surface along or against the orientation chosen for it. The arrow drawn on a wire in a circuit diagram is this bookkeeping choice. The local, vector description of the flow is the current density of [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^def-a5-2-3|Def. §A5.2.3]].

^rem-a5-1-1

## Direction of current

> [!definition] Definition §A5.1.2: Conventional Current
> The direction of a current is, by convention, the direction in which **positive** charge would flow. Positive carriers moving one way and negative carriers moving the other way both give a current in the first direction. Inside a wire or resistor this is the direction of the electric field that drives the flow, from higher to lower potential.
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.1*

^def-a5-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Carriers and current point in different directions
> - In metal wires the carriers are electrons, which move *opposite* to the conventional current: in an external circuit they leave the negative terminal of a battery and return to the positive one.
> - In salt water and in nerve cells both positive and negative ions move; their contributions to the current add.
> - A beam of antiprotons is a current pointing against the beam's motion; a beam of protons, along it.
>
> The convention goes back to Benjamin Franklin, who had to choose a sign for "electrical fluid" long before the electron was known. Nothing in the physics depends on it, as long as one sticks to it.

^cau-a5-1-1

## Circuits

> [!definition] Definition §A5.1.3: Circuit; Schematic
> A **circuit** is a closed conducting path along which charge can flow, for example from the positive terminal of a battery, through a switch and a lamp, back to the negative terminal. A **schematic** (circuit diagram) represents each component by a standard symbol and the connecting wires by lines. In this subject the symbols are the American (ANSI) ones: a zigzag for a resistor, long and short plates for a battery (the long plate is the positive terminal).
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.1*

^def-a5-1-3

*A battery, a switch and a lamp. With the switch closed the path is complete, and the conventional current $I$ (red arrows) leaves the positive terminal (long plate), passes through the lamp and returns to the negative terminal. The electrons in the wires move the other way.*

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.1.1: Steady Current Is the Same Along an Unbranched Path
> If no charge accumulates anywhere (a **steady state**), the current through every cross-section of an unbranched conductor is the same.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.3; PHY 182, week 9 slides*

^thm-a5-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the piece of conductor between two cross-sections $S_1$ and $S_2$, with current $I_1$ entering through $S_1$ and $I_2$ leaving through $S_2$. Charge is conserved ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]]), so the charge $Q_{\text{in}}$ inside the piece changes only by flow across its ends: $dQ_{\text{in}}/dt = I_1 - I_2$. In a steady state $Q_{\text{in}}$ is constant, hence $I_1 = I_2$.

^der-a5-1-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]], [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^def-a5-1-1|Def. §A5.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Nothing is "used up" along a circuit
> In the course's river picture, the water level (the potential) drops along the way, steeply where the bed is narrow (a large resistance) and gently where it is wide, but the flow is the same everywhere along the river. A lamp does not consume current; it converts the electric potential energy of the charges passing through it into heat and light ([[§A5.4 Electrical Power|§A5.4]]). What a battery supplies is energy, not charge. At a branch point the same reasoning gives Kirchhoff's junction rule ([[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 9 slides*

^rem-a5-1-2

## The field inside a current-carrying conductor

> [!remark] Remark: Out of equilibrium on purpose
> In electrostatic equilibrium the field inside a conductor is zero ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]]). A battery connected across a wire prevents that equilibrium from ever being reached: it keeps the ends at different potentials, so a field exists inside the wire and pushes the free charges along. The field does work on them, but their average kinetic energy does not grow. Collisions hand the energy to the atoms of the conductor, and the charges settle at a constant average **drift velocity**, much as a body falling through air reaches a terminal velocity ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^def-a3-10-2|CM Def. §A3.10.2]]). This is the subject of [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals|§A5.2]].

^rem-a5-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: A current-carrying wire is neutral
> A wire can carry a current and still have zero net charge: free electrons enter one end at the same rate as they leave the other, and the positive ions of the metal stay in place. The current is a motion of charge, not an excess of it.

^rem-a5-1-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.1.1: Starting a Truck
> While starting the engine, a truck battery sets $\Delta Q = 720\ \mathrm C$ of charge in motion in $\Delta t = 4.00\ \mathrm s$. The average current is
>
> $$
> I_{\text{ave}} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = \frac{720\ \mathrm C}{4.00\ \mathrm s} = 180\ \mathrm A ,
> $$
>
> so $1.00\ \mathrm C$ flows in $\Delta t = (1.00\ \mathrm C)/(180\ \mathrm A) = 5.56\ \mathrm{ms}$, and $(180\ \mathrm A)/(1.60\times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm C) = 1.13 \times 10^{21}$ electrons pass any cross-section of the cable per second. Starter motors need such large currents for a short time; a calculator runs on a fraction of a milliampere.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 9.1*

^ex-a5-1-1

> [!example] Example §A5.1.2: Current from a Charge History
> The charge that has passed a cross-section of a wire by time $t$ is $Q(t) = Q_M\bigl(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\bigr)$, with constants $Q_M$ (the charge passed after a long time) and $\tau$ (a time). By [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^def-a5-1-1|Def. §A5.1.1]] and the chain rule,
>
> $$
> I(t) = \frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{Q_M}{\tau}\, e^{-t/\tau} .
> $$
>
> The current starts at $Q_M/\tau$ and decays exponentially to zero while $Q$ levels off at $Q_M$: the current is the slope of the $Q(t)$ graph. This is exactly how the current behaves while a capacitor charges through a resistor ([[§A6.5 RC Circuits#^thm-a6-5-1|Theorem §A6.5.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 9.2*

^ex-a5-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Example §A5.1.2 differentiates with the chain rule, [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]; conversely $Q = \int I\,dt$ by the [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|Fundamental Theorem of Calculus]].
> - The current through a surface is the flux of the current density ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^def-a5-2-3|Def. §A5.2.3]]), a [[§18 Surface Integrals#^def-18-6|flux integral (452 Def. §18.6)]]. In that language the derivation of Theorem §A5.1.1 is the integral form of the continuity equation, which is level B.
