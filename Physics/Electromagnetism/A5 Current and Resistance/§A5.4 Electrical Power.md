Charges that pass through a potential difference $V$ lose potential energy $qV$ ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]]). In a conductor this energy does not become kinetic energy of the carriers, which keep their drift velocity ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals|§A5.2]]); it becomes thermal energy, light or mechanical work. This section finds the rate of that conversion, the electric power, and the energy unit used on electricity bills.

## Power in a circuit element

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.4.1: Electric Power
> When a current $I$ passes through a device across which the potential drops by $V$ (in the direction of the current), electric potential energy is converted into other forms at the rate
>
> $$
> P = IV .
> $$
>
> If the current instead leaves the device at the higher-potential terminal, as in a battery that is discharging, the device supplies power $IV$ to the charges.
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.5, eq. (9.12)*

^thm-a5-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In a time $dt$ a charge $dQ = I\,dt$ enters the device at potential $V_1$ and an equal charge leaves at $V_2 = V_1 - V$ (the current is steady, [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]). The net effect is that $dQ$ has been moved from $V_1$ to $V_2$, so its electric potential energy has changed by $dU = dQ\,(V_2 - V_1) = -I V\,dt$. The drift velocity is the same at entry and exit, so the kinetic energy of the carriers has not changed, and by conservation of energy ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]) the energy $IV\,dt$ has gone into other forms inside the device. Dividing by $dt$ gives the rate $P = IV$ ([[§A4.3 Power#^def-a4-3-1|CM Def. §A4.3.1]]).

^der-a5-4-1

*Uses:* [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^def-a5-1-1|Def. §A5.1.1]], [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]] (potential energy $qV$), [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]], [[§A4.3 Power#^def-a4-3-1|CM Def. §A4.3.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.4.2: Power Dissipated in a Resistor
> In a resistor obeying Ohm's law the power converted into thermal energy is
>
> $$
> P = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.5, eq. (9.13)*

^thm-a5-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Substitute $V = IR$ ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-5|Law §A5.3.5]]) into $P = IV$: $P = I(IR) = I^2R$, or with $I = V/R$, $P = (V/R)V = V^2/R$.

^der-a5-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A5.4 Electrical Power#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]], [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-5|Law §A5.3.5]]

> [!caution] Caution: Which formula, and what is held fixed
> The three forms are the same quantity, but they suggest different things depending on what is held constant.
> - At fixed voltage (a wall outlet, a battery), $P = V^2/R$: a *smaller* resistance draws *more* power. A "heavy load" is a small resistance.
> - At fixed current (resistors in series), $P = I^2R$: the *larger* resistance dissipates more.
> - Doubling the voltage on a lamp would quadruple its power if $R$ stayed fixed; a $25\ \mathrm W$ bulb would burn at about $100\ \mathrm W$ and fail. In fact the hotter filament has a larger $R$ ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^ex-a5-3-2|Example §A5.3.2]]), so the power rises somewhat less.
> - $P = I^2R$ and $P = V^2/R$ are for resistors. For a motor or a charging battery only $P = IV$ gives the total power taken in.
> - With alternating current, $I$ and $V$ vary in time and need not be in phase; the average power is then $I_{\text{rms}}V_{\text{rms}}\cos\phi$, and for a resistor the formulas above hold with rms values ([[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-3-2|Theorem §A11.3.2]]).
>
> All the relations between $P$, $I$, $V$ and $R$ for an ohmic device come from just two equations, $V = IR$ and $P = IV$.

^cau-a5-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Power per unit volume
> In a small piece of conductor of length $dl$ along the current and cross-section $dA$, the current is $J\,dA$ and the potential drop is $E\,dl$, so the power converted is $J E\,dA\,dl$: the power per unit volume is $\vec J\cdot\vec E$. Microscopically, the field does work on each carrier at the rate $q\vec E\cdot\vec v_d$ ([[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|CM Theorem §A4.3.1]]); multiplied by the $n$ carriers per unit volume this is $nq\vec v_d\cdot\vec E = \vec J\cdot\vec E$ ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]). For an ohmic material it equals $\rho J^2 = \sigma E^2$, always positive: a resistor can only absorb energy. Collisions pass this energy to the lattice, which warms up ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^mod-a5-2-3|Model §A5.2.3]]).

^rem-a5-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Fuses and circuit breakers
> A **fuse** is a short wire with a low melting point. The $I^2R$ heating melts it when the current exceeds its rating, opening the circuit before the wiring overheats; it must then be replaced. A **circuit breaker** also opens at a rated current, acts faster, and can be reset. The ground fault circuit interrupter works by magnetic induction ([[§A6.4 Meters and Electrical Safety#^rem-a6-4-3|Remark in §A6.4]], [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^pr-a9-1-1|Principle §A9.1.1]]).

^rem-a5-4-2

## Energy and its cost

> [!definition] Definition §A5.4.1: Kilowatt-Hour
> The energy used by a device that draws power $P(t)$ is $E = \int P\,dt$, or $E = Pt$ at constant power. Utilities bill energy in **kilowatt-hours**: $1\ \mathrm{kW\cdot h}$ is the energy used in one hour at $1\ \mathrm{kW}$,
>
> $$
> 1\ \mathrm{kW\cdot h} = (10^3\ \mathrm W)(3600\ \mathrm s) = 3.6\times 10^6\ \mathrm J .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §9.5*

^def-a5-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Efficient lighting
> A $100\ \mathrm W$ incandescent bulb and a $20\ \mathrm W$ LED bulb give about the same light (lighting output is measured in lumens; about $1600\ \mathrm{lm}$ here). Burning $3\ \mathrm h$ a day for a year, they use $(0.100\ \mathrm{kW})(1095\ \mathrm h) = 109.5\ \mathrm{kW\cdot h}$ and $21.9\ \mathrm{kW\cdot h}$; at $\$0.10$ per $\mathrm{kW\cdot h}$ that is $\$10.95$ against $\$2.19$. The LED also lasts about $50\,000\ \mathrm h$ against $1200\ \mathrm h$. Most of an incandescent bulb's power leaves as heat, the price of making light by heating a filament.

^rem-a5-4-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.4.1: Where the Energy Goes
> A $1.5\ \mathrm V$ battery drives current through a $10\ \Omega$ resistor (wires and battery of negligible resistance). The current is $I = V/R = 0.15\ \mathrm A$, leaving the battery's positive terminal: conventional current flows from high to low potential through the resistor, while the electrons in the wires go the other way. In one hour $Q = It = (0.15\ \mathrm A)(3600\ \mathrm s) = 540\ \mathrm C$ passes through the resistor, each coulomb losing $1.5\ \mathrm J$, so $E = QV = 810\ \mathrm J$ are released. Per second this is
>
> $$
> P = IV = (0.15\ \mathrm A)(1.5\ \mathrm V) = 0.225\ \mathrm W = I^2R ,
> $$
>
> all of it ending up as thermal energy in the resistor. It came from chemical energy in the battery ([[§A6.1 Electromotive Force|§A6.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 15*

^ex-a5-4-1

> [!example] Example §A5.4.2: A Winch Motor
> A DC winch motor draws $I = 20.0\ \mathrm A$ at $V = 115\ \mathrm V$ and lifts a $4900\ \mathrm N$ load $10.0\ \mathrm m$ in $30.0\ \mathrm s$ at constant speed. The electric power taken in is
>
> $$
> P_{\text{in}} = IV = (20.0\ \mathrm A)(115\ \mathrm V) = 2300\ \mathrm W .
> $$
>
> At constant speed the motor's upward force equals the weight, so the mechanical power is ([[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|CM Theorem §A4.3.1]])
>
> $$
> P_{\text{mech}} = Fv = (4900\ \mathrm N)\,\frac{10.0\ \mathrm m}{30.0\ \mathrm s} = 1633\ \mathrm W .
> $$
>
> If the difference, $667\ \mathrm W$, is dissipated in the resistance of the windings, then $R = P/I^2 = (667\ \mathrm W)/(20.0\ \mathrm A)^2 = 1.67\ \Omega$. The motor is about $71\%$ efficient; because the loss grows as $I^2$, motors drawing large currents need windings of low resistance.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 9.9*

^ex-a5-4-2
