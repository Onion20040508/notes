Each conductor in electrostatics is at one potential ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]). When charge $Q$ is moved from one conductor to another, a potential difference $V$ appears between them, and it is proportional to $Q$; the constant of proportionality depends only on the geometry. This section names that constant, the capacitance, and computes it for the three standard shapes.

## Capacitance

> [!definition] Definition §A4.1.1: Capacitor; Capacitance; Farad
> A **capacitor** is a pair of conductors (its **plates**), insulated from each other, carrying charges $+Q$ and $-Q$. If $V$ is the magnitude of the potential difference between them, the **capacitance** is
>
> $$
> C = \frac{Q}{V} .
> $$
>
> Its unit is the **farad**, $1\ \mathrm F = 1\ \mathrm{C/V}$. Practical capacitances range from picofarads ($10^{-12}$ F) to millifarads ($10^{-3}$ F). The space between the plates is vacuum or, usually, an insulator called a **dielectric** ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics|§A4.4]]); in this section it is vacuum.
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.1, eq. (8.1)*

^def-a4-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.1: Capacitance Depends Only on Geometry
> For given conductors in vacuum, $V$ is proportional to $Q$, so $C = Q/V$ does not depend on $Q$ or $V$; it is fixed by the shapes, sizes and arrangement of the conductors (and, in [[§A4.4 Dielectrics|§A4.4]], by the material between them).
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.1*

^thm-a4-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Suppose charges $\pm Q$ sit on the plates in some equilibrium distribution. Multiply every surface charge density by a number $\lambda$. Coulomb's law with superposition is linear in the charges, so every field vector is multiplied by $\lambda$ ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]); the field inside each conductor is still zero, so the scaled distribution is again in equilibrium, and by [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]] the potential difference is $\lambda V$. Hence the ratio $(\lambda Q)/(\lambda V)$ is the same for every charge. (This assumes that the equilibrium distribution for a given $Q$ is unique, which is proved at the upper level: [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], with what it does and does not fix in [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^cau-b3-1-1|Caution: What the theorems do not say]].)

^der-a4-1-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Computing a capacitance
> The recipe is always the same: (1) put $\pm Q$ on the conductors; (2) find $\vec E$ between them, usually from Gauss's law and symmetry; (3) integrate, $V = \bigl|\int \vec E\cdot d\vec l\bigr|$, from one conductor to the other; (4) $C = Q/V$, in which $Q$ cancels. A useful picture from the lecture: the potential is like a pressure, and the capacitance is the size of the container: the larger $C$, the more charge is pushed in by a given voltage.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 8 slides*

^rem-a4-1-1

## Three standard capacitors

> [!model] Model §A4.1.2: Parallel-Plate Capacitor
> Two flat parallel conducting plates, each of area $A$, separated by a distance $d$ that is small compared with their width, carry charges $\pm Q$ spread uniformly over their inner faces, with surface densities $\pm\sigma = \pm Q/A$. Edge effects (fringing) are neglected: the field is taken to be uniform between the plates, of magnitude
>
> $$
> E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} ,
> $$
>
> directed from the positive to the negative plate, and zero outside, as for two infinite oppositely charged planes ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-4|Theorem §A2.3.4]] with superposition).
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.1*

^mod-a4-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.3: Capacitance of a Parallel-Plate Capacitor
> In vacuum,
>
> $$
> C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}, \qquad \varepsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.1, eq. (8.3)*

^thm-a4-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^mod-a4-1-2|Model §A4.1.2]] the field is uniform, so by [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]] $V = Ed = \sigma d/\varepsilon_0 = Qd/(\varepsilon_0 A)$. Then $C = Q/V = \varepsilon_0 A/d$. Larger plates hold more charge at a given voltage, and closer plates hold more, as expected.

^der-a4-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^mod-a4-1-2|Model §A4.1.2]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.4: Spherical Capacitor; Isolated Sphere
> Two concentric conducting spherical shells of radii $R_1 < R_2$ have
>
> $$
> C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\frac{R_1 R_2}{R_2 - R_1} .
> $$
>
> As $R_2 \to \infty$ this becomes the capacitance of an isolated sphere of radius $R$ (relative to infinity), $C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 R$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.1, eq. (8.4); Example 8.3*

^thm-a4-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $+Q$ on the inner shell and $-Q$ on the outer. Gauss's law on a sphere of radius $r$ between them, which encloses $+Q$, gives $\vec E = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)\,\hat r$ ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]). Integrating along a radius,
>
> $$
> V = V_1 - V_2 = \int_{R_1}^{R_2} \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\,dr = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{R_2 - R_1}{R_1 R_2},
> $$
>
> and $C = Q/V$. For $R_2 \to \infty$, $R_1R_2/(R_2 - R_1) \to R_1$; equivalently $V = kQ/R$ for an isolated sphere ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]]).

^der-a4-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.5: Cylindrical Capacitor
> Two coaxial conducting cylinders of radii $R_1 < R_2$ and length $l \gg R_2$ (edge effects neglected) have
>
> $$
> C = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\, l}{\ln(R_2/R_1)}, \qquad \frac{C}{l} = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\ln(R_2/R_1)} .
> $$
>
> The second form is the capacitance per unit length of a coaxial cable.
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.1, eqs. (8.5)–(8.6)*

^thm-a4-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $+Q$ on the inner cylinder, Gauss's law on a coaxial cylinder of radius $r$ and length $l$ gives $E\,(2\pi r l) = Q/\varepsilon_0$, so $\vec E = Q/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 l r)\,\hat r$ between the conductors ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-3|Theorem §A2.3.3]]). Then
>
> $$
> V = \int_{R_1}^{R_2} \frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 l}\,\frac{dr}{r} = \frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 l}\,\ln\frac{R_2}{R_1},
> $$
>
> and $C = Q/V$.

^der-a4-1-5

*Uses:* [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-3|Theorem §A2.3.3]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Real capacitors
> One farad is huge: a 1.0 F parallel-plate capacitor with a 1.0 mm gap would need $A = Cd/\varepsilon_0 = 1.1\times10^8\ \mathrm{m^2}$, plates about 10 km on a side. Practical capacitors gain capacitance from a very small gap and a dielectric ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics|§A4.4]]): rolled metal foils separated by thin insulating film (mica, ceramic, paper, Teflon), or **electrolytic capacitors** (an oxide film on metal in a conducting paste), which reach up to about 1 F but work only with one polarity and so cannot be used on alternating current. A **variable air capacitor** changes the overlap area of interleaved plates and is used to tune radios. In circuit diagrams a capacitor is drawn as two parallel lines; a curved line marks the negative terminal of an electrolytic one. A cell membrane, about 70 mV across 7–10 nm, is a natural capacitor whose field, about $7\times10^6$ V/m, exceeds the breakdown field of air.

^rem-a4-1-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.1.1: A Square-Metre Parallel-Plate Capacitor
> Plates of area $A = 1.00\ \mathrm{m^2}$ are $d = 1.00$ mm apart in vacuum. By [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]],
>
> $$
> C = \frac{(8.85\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m})(1.00\ \mathrm{m^2})}{1.00\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm m} = 8.85\ \mathrm{nF}.
> $$
>
> At $V = 3.00$ kV it holds $Q = CV = 26.6\ \mu$C. The field is then $V/d = 3.0\times10^6$ V/m, the breakdown field of air: in air no higher voltage, and so no more charge, is possible. Even a square metre of plate gives only nanofarads.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 8.1*

^ex-a4-1-1

> [!example] Example §A4.1.2: Two Distant Spheres
> Two conducting spheres of radii $R_A$ and $R_B$ are a distance $L \gg R_A, R_B$ apart, and charge $+q$ has been moved from $B$ to $A$. Because the spheres are far apart, each keeps a nearly uniform charge, and the other sphere acts on it like a point charge at distance $L$. By superposition ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]) the potentials of the two spheres are
>
> $$
> V_A = \frac{kq}{R_A} - \frac{kq}{L}, \qquad V_B = -\frac{kq}{R_B} + \frac{kq}{L},
> $$
>
> so $V_A - V_B = kq\bigl(1/R_A + 1/R_B - 2/L\bigr)$ and
>
> $$
> C = \frac{q}{V_A - V_B} = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0}{1/R_A + 1/R_B - 2/L} .
> $$
>
> The charge cancels, as [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]] says it must. For $R_A = R_B = 1.0$ cm and $L = 1.0$ m, $C \approx 0.56$ pF. As $L \to \infty$, $1/C \to 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_A) + 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_B)$: the two isolated-sphere capacitances combined in series ([[§A4.2 Capacitors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 12*

^ex-a4-1-2
