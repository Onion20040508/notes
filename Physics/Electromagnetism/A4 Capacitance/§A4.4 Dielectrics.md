So far the space between capacitor plates was empty. Filling it with an insulator raises the capacitance and allows higher voltages, which is how practical capacitors are built ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance|§A4.1]]). This section states the empirical rule, derives its consequences for capacitors, and explains it by the polarization of the molecules.

## The dielectric constant

> [!definition] Definition §A4.4.1: Dielectric; Dielectric Constant
> A **dielectric** is an insulating material placed in an electric field, typically between the plates of a capacitor. Let $\vec E_0$ be the field that the free charges (on the plates) would produce in vacuum, and $\vec E$ the net field inside the dielectric when it fills the space where the field is. The **dielectric constant** of the material is
>
> $$
> \kappa = \frac{E_0}{E} .
> $$
>
> For vacuum $\kappa = 1$ exactly.
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.4–8.5, eq. (8.14)*

^def-a4-4-1

> [!law] Law §A4.4.1: Linear Dielectric Response
> In a given insulating material the net field is proportional to the applied field, $\vec E = \vec E_0/\kappa$, with a constant $\kappa > 1$ that depends on the material (and its temperature) but not on the field strength. Equivalently: filling the gap of an isolated charged capacitor with the material lowers its voltage by the factor $\kappa$, $V = V_0/\kappa$.
>
> *Valid when:* the field is static and well below the material's dielectric strength ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^def-a4-4-2|Def. §A4.4.2]]); for ordinary insulators. Air at 1 atm has $\kappa = 1.00059$, so an air-filled capacitor behaves as a vacuum one.
> *Source: UP2 §8.4–8.5, Table 8.1*

^law-a4-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Representative values
> At room temperature (dielectric strength $E_c$ in $10^6$ V/m):
>
> | Material | $\kappa$ | $E_c$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | vacuum | 1 | $\infty$ |
> | dry air (1 atm) | 1.00059 | 3.0 |
> | Teflon | 2.1 | 60–173 |
> | paper | 3.7 | 16 |
> | diamond | 5.5 | 2000 |
> | Pyrex glass | 5.6 | 14 |
> | mica | 6.0 | 118 |
> | water | 80 | – |
> | strontium titanate | 310 | 8 |
>
> Water has a large $\kappa$ because its molecules are strongly polar, but it is useless between capacitor plates: as a good solvent it always contains ions, which conduct.

^rem-a4-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.4.2: Capacitor Filled with a Dielectric
> Filling the whole space between the plates of a capacitor with a dielectric of constant $\kappa$ multiplies its capacitance by $\kappa$:
>
> $$
> C = \kappa C_0 .
> $$
>
> If the capacitor is isolated (charge $Q_0$ fixed), $V = V_0/\kappa$ and $U = U_0/\kappa$. If it stays connected to a battery (voltage $V_0$ fixed), $Q = \kappa Q_0$ and $U = \kappa U_0$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.4, eqs. (8.11)–(8.12); Example 8.12*

^thm-a4-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At fixed free charge $Q_0$ the field everywhere between the plates drops from $\vec E_0$ to $\vec E_0/\kappa$ ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^law-a4-4-1|Law §A4.4.1]]), so the line integral from plate to plate drops by the same factor: $V = V_0/\kappa$ ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]). Hence $C = Q_0/V = \kappa Q_0/V_0 = \kappa C_0$; since $C$ depends only on the geometry and the material ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]]), this holds however the capacitor is later charged. The energies follow from [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]: at fixed $Q_0$, $U = Q_0^2/(2\kappa C_0) = U_0/\kappa$; at fixed $V_0$, $Q = \kappa C_0V_0 = \kappa Q_0$ and $U = \tfrac12\kappa C_0V_0^2 = \kappa U_0$.

^der-a4-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^law-a4-4-1|Law §A4.4.1]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]], [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Permittivity
> In a region uniformly filled with a dielectric, every field of the free charges is weaker by $\kappa$, so all the vacuum formulas hold with $\varepsilon_0$ replaced by the **permittivity** $\varepsilon = \kappa\varepsilon_0$ (and $k$ by $k/\kappa$). Gauss's law reads $\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A = Q_{\text{free,enc}}/\varepsilon$, a filled parallel-plate capacitor has $C = \varepsilon A/d$, a filled coaxial cable $C/l = 2\pi\varepsilon/\ln(R_2/R_1)$, and the energy density becomes $u_E = \tfrac12\varepsilon E^2$ (with a battery attached, $E$ stays $E_0$ and $U = \tfrac12\kappa\varepsilon_0E_0^2\,(Ad) = \kappa U_0$, in agreement with Theorem §A4.4.2).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 8 slides; TBL 13*

^rem-a4-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: The dielectric is pulled in
> When a dielectric slab approaches an isolated charged capacitor, its near face acquires charge opposite to the nearby plate ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^mod-a4-4-3|Model §A4.4.3]]) and is attracted into the gap. The stored energy drops from $U_0$ to $U_0/\kappa$; the difference is the work the field does pulling the slab in. A stud finder uses the same effect in reverse: its capacitance changes when it passes over a wooden stud, whose $\kappa$ differs from that of the wall.

^rem-a4-4-3

## Molecular picture

> [!model] Model §A4.4.3: Polarization of a Dielectric
> A dielectric is made of neutral molecules. **Polar** molecules (water) have permanent dipole moments, randomly oriented when there is no field; an applied field $\vec E_0$ partly aligns them against thermal agitation. **Nonpolar** molecules (oxygen) have no dipole moment of their own but acquire an **induced dipole moment** along $\vec E_0$. In either case the material becomes **polarized**: inside the bulk the neighbouring dipoles' charges cancel, but at the two faces bordering the plates uncancelled **induced surface charges** $-Q_i$ (next to the positive plate) and $+Q_i$ (next to the negative plate) remain. They produce an **induced field** $\vec E_i$ opposite to $\vec E_0$, and the net field is
>
> $$
> \vec E = \vec E_0 + \vec E_i .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.5, eq. (8.13)*

^mod-a4-4-3

![[ph-em-a4-4-1.svg]]
*A polarized dielectric between charged plates (dipoles drawn in part of the slab only). The dipoles point along the applied field, leaving induced charge $-Q_i$ at the face next to the positive plate and $+Q_i$ at the face next to the negative one. The induced field $\vec E_i$ (green) opposes the field $\vec E_0$ of the free charges (blue), so the net field $\vec E$ (red) is weaker. In a conductor the induced charges would cancel $\vec E_0$ completely; in a dielectric only partly.*

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.4.4: Induced Field and Induced Charge
> In a dielectric of constant $\kappa$ that fills the space between the plates,
>
> $$
> \vec E_i = \left(\frac1\kappa - 1\right)\vec E_0, \qquad Q_i = \left(1 - \frac1\kappa\right) Q ,
> $$
>
> where $Q$ is the free charge on the plates (for parallel plates). Since $\kappa > 1$, $0 < Q_i < Q$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.5, eq. (8.15); Examples 8.11–8.12*

^thm-a4-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^law-a4-4-1|Law §A4.4.1]], $\vec E = \vec E_0/\kappa$, and by [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^mod-a4-4-3|Model §A4.4.3]], $\vec E_i = \vec E - \vec E_0 = (1/\kappa - 1)\vec E_0$. Between parallel plates the net field is that of the effective charge $Q - Q_i$ on each side, $E = (Q - Q_i)/(\varepsilon_0 A)$, while $E_0 = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)$ ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^mod-a4-1-2|Model §A4.1.2]]). Then $E = E_0/\kappa$ gives $Q - Q_i = Q/\kappa$.

^der-a4-4-4

*Uses:* [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]], [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^law-a4-4-1|Law §A4.4.1]], [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^mod-a4-4-3|Model §A4.4.3]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^mod-a4-1-2|Model §A4.1.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §A4.4.2: Dielectric Breakdown; Dielectric Strength
> In a strong enough field the molecules of an insulator are ionized: electrons are torn free and the material conducts, a **dielectric breakdown** (a spark through air, branching tracks in plastic). The critical field $E_c$ at which this starts is the **dielectric strength** of the material. It limits the voltage across a capacitor of plate separation $d$ to $V = E_c d$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.5*

^def-a4-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Why fill a capacitor
> A dielectric helps twice: it raises $C$ by $\kappa$, and it usually withstands a far stronger field than air. A capacitor with a 1.00 mm gap can take $(3.0\ \mathrm{MV/m})(1.00\ \mathrm{mm}) = 3.0$ kV in air but about 60 kV filled with Teflon ($E_c \approx 60$ MV/m). The maximum charge $Q = \kappa C_0 V_{\max}$ therefore grows by $(2.1/1.00059)(60/3.0) \approx 42$.

^rem-a4-4-4

> [!caution] Caution: Dielectric constant versus dielectric strength
> $\kappa$ (dimensionless) says how much the material weakens the field and raises $C$; $E_c$ (in V/m) says how strong a field it can take before breaking down. They are independent: water has a huge $\kappa$ and is useless as an insulator, diamond has a modest $\kappa = 5.5$ and an enormous $E_c$.

^cau-a4-4-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.4.1: Teflon in an Isolated Capacitor
> An empty capacitor, $C_0 = 20.0$ pF, plates of area $4.5\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ and $d = 2.0$ mm apart, is charged to $V_0 = 40.0$ V; the battery is disconnected and Teflon ($\kappa = 2.1$) is slid in to fill the gap. By [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^thm-a4-4-2|Theorem §A4.4.2]] with $Q$ fixed:
> - $C = \kappa C_0 = 42$ pF; $Q = Q_0 = C_0V_0 = 0.80$ nC, unchanged since the charge has nowhere to go;
> - $V = V_0/\kappa = 19$ V; $U_0 = \tfrac12 C_0V_0^2 = 16.0$ nJ drops to $U = U_0/\kappa = 7.6$ nJ;
> - $E_0 = V_0/d = 2.0\times10^4$ V/m drops to $E = E_0/\kappa = 9.5\times10^3$ V/m;
> - induced charge ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^thm-a4-4-4|Theorem §A4.4.4]]): $Q_i = Q_0 - \varepsilon_0AE = 0.80\ \mathrm{nC} - (8.85\times10^{-12})(4.5\times10^{-3})(9.5\times10^3)\ \mathrm C = 0.42$ nC, which is $(1 - 1/2.1)Q_0$.
>
> *Source: UP2 Examples 8.10–8.11*

^ex-a4-4-1

> [!example] Example §A4.4.2: Half-Filled Two Ways
> A parallel-plate capacitor ($C_0 = \varepsilon_0A/d$) is half filled with a dielectric $\kappa$ in two ways. **Side by side**, the left half of the area empty and the right half filled: two capacitors of area $A/2$ and gap $d$, in parallel ([[§A4.2 Capacitors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]):
>
> $$
> C_a = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{2d} + \frac{\kappa\varepsilon_0 A}{2d} = \frac{1+\kappa}{2}\,C_0 .
> $$
>
> **Stacked**, the lower half of the gap filled: two capacitors of area $A$ and gap $d/2$, capacitances $2C_0$ and $2\kappa C_0$, in series ([[§A4.2 Capacitors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{C_b} = \frac{1}{2C_0} + \frac{1}{2\kappa C_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_b = \frac{2\kappa}{1+\kappa}\,C_0 .
> $$
>
> $C_a \ge C_b$, since $(1+\kappa)^2 \ge 4\kappa$ is $(\kappa - 1)^2 \ge 0$; they are equal only for $\kappa = 1$. For Teflon, $C_a = 1.55\,C_0$ and $C_b = 1.35\,C_0$.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 14*

^ex-a4-4-2

> [!example] Example §A4.4.3: Inserting a Dielectric with the Battery Connected
> A capacitor $C_0$ stays connected to a battery $V_0$ while a dielectric $\kappa$ is slid in to fill it. Now $V = V_0$ is fixed, so $E = V_0/d = E_0$ is unchanged, while by [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^thm-a4-4-2|Theorem §A4.4.2]] $Q = \kappa Q_0$ and $U = \kappa U_0$. The field is unchanged because the extra free charge $(\kappa - 1)Q_0$ drawn from the battery exactly offsets the induced charge, $Q_i = (1 - 1/\kappa)\kappa Q_0 = (\kappa - 1)Q_0$. Energy: the battery delivers $V_0(\kappa - 1)Q_0 = 2(\kappa - 1)U_0$; half of it raises the stored energy by $(\kappa - 1)U_0$, the other half is the work done by the field pulling the dielectric in.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 8.12 · PHY 182, TBL 13*

^ex-a4-4-3
