Charging a capacitor means moving charge from one plate to the other against a growing potential difference, so it takes work, which is stored as electric potential energy ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]]). This section computes that energy and shows that it can equally be regarded as stored in the field between the plates.

## Energy of a charged capacitor

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.3.1: Energy Stored in a Capacitor
> A capacitor of capacitance $C$ carrying charge $Q$ at voltage $V = Q/C$ stores the energy
>
> $$
> U_C = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac12 QV = \frac12 CV^2 .
> $$
>
> This is the work needed to charge it from zero, whatever its geometry.
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.3, eq. (8.10)*

^thm-a4-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Charge the capacitor in small steps. When the plates carry $\pm q$, the voltage is $v = q/C$ ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]). Moving a further $dq$ from the negative to the positive plate raises its potential energy by $dU = v\,dq$ ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]]), so it takes the work $dW = (q/C)\,dq$. Each step costs more than the last, because $v$ grows. In total
>
> $$
> U_C = \int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{Q^2}{2C} ,
> $$
>
> and $Q = CV$ gives the other two forms.

^der-a4-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|FTC I (451 Thm. §34.1)]]

![[ph-em-a4-3-1.svg]]
*Charging a capacitor: the voltage $v = q/C$ (blue) grows linearly with the charge moved. The work for one more $dq$ is the thin red strip $v\,dq$; the total, the stored energy, is the shaded triangle of area $\tfrac12 QV$, not $QV$.*

> [!caution] Caution: Which formula when something changes
> The three forms are equal, but when the capacitance changes (plates moved, a dielectric inserted) use the one whose variable stays fixed. An **isolated** capacitor keeps its charge: use $U = Q^2/(2C)$. A capacitor **connected to a battery** keeps its voltage: use $U = \tfrac12 CV^2$, and remember that the battery also exchanges energy $V\,\Delta Q$ with the circuit.

^cau-a4-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Energy of a network
> The energy stored in a network is the sum of the energies of its capacitors, and equals the energy $\tfrac12 C V^2$ of the equivalent capacitor ([[§A4.2 Capacitors in Series and Parallel|§A4.2]]). For the network of [[§A4.2 Capacitors in Series and Parallel#^ex-a4-2-1|Example §A4.2.1]]: $\tfrac12(12.0\ \mu\mathrm F)(4.0\ \mathrm V)^2 + \tfrac12(2.0\ \mu\mathrm F)(8.0\ \mathrm V)^2 + \tfrac12(4.0\ \mu\mathrm F)(8.0\ \mathrm V)^2 = 96 + 64 + 128 = 288\ \mu$J, and $\tfrac12(4.0\ \mu\mathrm F)(12.0\ \mathrm V)^2 = 288\ \mu$J.

^rem-a4-3-1

## Energy in the field

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.3.2: Energy Density of the Electric Field
> The energy of an electrostatic configuration can be located in the field, with energy per unit volume
>
> $$
> u_E = \frac12 \varepsilon_0 E^2 .
> $$
>
> For a capacitor, integrating $u_E$ over the space between the plates gives $U_C$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.3, eq. (8.9)*

^thm-a4-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Parallel plates* ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^mod-a4-1-2|Model §A4.1.2]]). The field is uniform in the volume $Ad$ between the plates and zero outside. With $V = Ed$ and $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$ ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]]),
>
> $$
> U_C = \frac12 CV^2 = \frac12\,\frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}\,(Ed)^2 = \frac12\varepsilon_0 E^2\,(Ad),
> $$
>
> so the energy per volume is $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$.
>
> *Spherical capacitor* (a non-uniform check). Between the shells $E = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$; integrating over spherical shells of volume $4\pi r^2\,dr$,
>
> $$
> \int_{R_1}^{R_2} \frac12\varepsilon_0\left(\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\right)^2 4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right) = \frac{Q^2}{2C},
> $$
>
> with $C$ from [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]]. These cases support the general statement; at this level it is not derived in general (level B derives it for any charge distribution: [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]], [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^def-b2-3-1|Definition §B2.3.1]]). It is taken up again with electromagnetic waves, where the field carries energy away from its sources and the electric and magnetic energy densities add ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-1|Theorem §A12.3.1]]).

^der-a4-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^mod-a4-1-2|Model §A4.1.2]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Two pictures of the same energy
> In the first picture the energy belongs to the charges, which were pushed "uphill" against each other's potential. In the second it belongs to the field in empty space, $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ per cubic metre, even where there is no charge. For static charges the two give the same total. The field picture is the one that survives when fields move on their own, as in light.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 13*

^rem-a4-3-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.3.1: A Defibrillator
> A heart defibrillator delivers $U = 4.00\times10^2$ J by discharging a capacitor initially at $V = 1.00\times10^4$ V. By [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]],
>
> $$
> C = \frac{2U}{V^2} = \frac{2(4.00\times10^2\ \mathrm J)}{(1.00\times10^4\ \mathrm V)^2} = 8.00\ \mu\mathrm F ,
> $$
>
> holding $Q = CV = 80.0$ mC. A modest capacitance stores hundreds of joules because $U$ grows as $V^2$.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 8.9*

^ex-a4-3-1

> [!example] Example §A4.3.2: Pulling the Plates Apart
> A parallel-plate capacitor $C_0 = \varepsilon_0 A/d$ is charged to $V_0$, $Q_0 = C_0V_0$. Its plate separation is then doubled. Since $C = \varepsilon_0A/d$, the capacitance halves in both cases below.
> - **Isolated** (battery removed first). $Q = Q_0$ stays, so $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ is unchanged, $V = Ed$ doubles, and $U = Q^2/(2C)$ **doubles**. The extra $\tfrac12 Q_0V_0$ is the work done by whoever pulls the attracting plates apart; equivalently, the same field now fills twice the volume.
> - **Battery connected.** $V = V_0$ stays, so $Q = CV$ halves, $E = V/d$ halves, and $U = \tfrac12 CV^2$ **halves**, from $\tfrac12 C_0V_0^2$ to $\tfrac14 C_0V_0^2$. The charge $\tfrac12 Q_0$ flows back into the battery, which gains $V_0 \cdot \tfrac12 Q_0 = \tfrac12 C_0V_0^2$. Energy balance: the puller's work $= \Delta U + \Delta U_{\text{battery}} = -\tfrac14 C_0V_0^2 + \tfrac12 C_0V_0^2 = \tfrac14 C_0V_0^2 > 0$, positive because the plates still attract.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 12*

^ex-a4-3-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $U_C = \int_0^Q (q/C)\,dq$ is the area under the line $v = q/C$, evaluated with the antiderivative $q^2/(2C)$ by the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]], Single Variable Analysis).
> - Level B: the energy of any static charge distribution as $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$, which proves the energy density of Theorem §A4.3.2 in general — [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]], [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^def-b2-3-1|Definition §B2.3.1]].
> - Level B: Caution: Which formula when something changes, made precise. The force on a part of a system is $-\partial W/\partial x$ at fixed charge or $+\partial W/\partial x$ at fixed potential, and both give the same force — [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-4|Theorem §B2.4.4]].
> - Level C: Caution: Which formula when something changes, for any number of conductors: the naive derivative at fixed potentials has the wrong sign, the batteries' work restores it, and the forces at fixed charges and at fixed potentials agree — [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^rem-c4-4-1|Remark: The naive sign error]], [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-5|Theorem §C4.4.5]], [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-4|Theorem §C4.4.4]].
