Capacitors are rarely used alone. A network of them, connected to the rest of a circuit at two terminals, behaves like a single capacitor ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance|§A4.1]]); this section finds its capacitance for the two basic connections, from which more complicated networks are reduced step by step.

## Equivalent capacitance

> [!definition] Definition §A4.2.1: Series and Parallel Connection; Equivalent Capacitance
> Capacitors are connected **in series** when they are joined one after another, each plate joined to exactly one plate of the next capacitor and to nothing else, so that only the two end plates connect to the terminals. They are connected **in parallel** when each has one plate joined to one terminal and the other plate to the other terminal. The **equivalent capacitance** of a network between two terminals is $C = Q/V$, where $V$ is the voltage across the terminals and $Q$ the charge that has entered through the positive terminal.
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.2*

^def-a4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.2.1: Capacitors in Series
> Capacitors in series all carry the same charge $Q$, their voltages add up to the total, and
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{C_S} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \frac{1}{C_3} + \cdots .
> $$
>
> The equivalent capacitance is smaller than the smallest of the $C_i$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.2, eq. (8.7)*

^thm-a4-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The battery puts $+Q$ on the first plate and $-Q$ on the last. Each pair of joined plates in between (the right plate of one capacitor and the left plate of the next, with their wire) is an isolated conductor that was neutral, so by conservation of charge ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge|§A1.1]]) it stays neutral: $-Q$ on one side forces $+Q$ on the other. Hence every capacitor carries $\pm Q$ and has voltage $V_i = Q/C_i$ ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]). Each conductor is at one potential ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]), so going from one terminal to the other the potential drops are $V_1, V_2, \dots$ in turn and add to $V$. With $V = Q/C_S$:
>
> $$
> \frac{Q}{C_S} = \frac{Q}{C_1} + \frac{Q}{C_2} + \cdots,
> $$
>
> and $Q$ cancels. Each term $1/C_i$ is less than the sum $1/C_S$, so $C_S < C_i$ for every $i$.

^der-a4-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Electric Charge|conservation of charge (§A1.1)]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]], [[§A4.2 Capacitors in Series and Parallel#^def-a4-2-1|Def. §A4.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.2.2: Capacitors in Parallel
> Capacitors in parallel all have the same voltage $V$, their charges add up to the total, and
>
> $$
> C_P = C_1 + C_2 + C_3 + \cdots .
> $$
>
> The equivalent capacitance is larger than the largest of the $C_i$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §8.2, eq. (8.8)*

^thm-a4-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> All the plates on one side are joined into one conductor, at one potential, and likewise on the other side ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]), so every capacitor has the terminal voltage $V$ and charge $Q_i = C_i V$. The charge that entered through the terminal is shared among them: $Q = Q_1 + Q_2 + \cdots$, that is $C_P V = C_1 V + C_2 V + \cdots$. Divide by $V$.

^der-a4-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A4.2 Capacitors in Series and Parallel#^def-a4-2-1|Def. §A4.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reducing a network
> A network built from series and parallel pieces is reduced step by step: find a group that is purely in series or purely in parallel, replace it by its equivalent capacitor, and repeat until one capacitor is left. To find the individual charges and voltages, run the steps backwards, using $Q = CV$ at each stage: series pieces share the charge, parallel pieces share the voltage. Compare resistors ([[§A6.2 Resistors in Series and Parallel|§A6.2]]), where the two rules are interchanged.

^rem-a4-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Series means one path, not "in a row on the page"
> Two capacitors are in series only if nothing else is connected to the junction between them; then they must carry the same charge. Two capacitors are in parallel only if both of their ends are joined directly; then they must have the same voltage. Capacitors drawn next to each other may be neither.

^cau-a4-2-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.2.1: A Mixed Network
> $C_1 = 12.0\ \mu$F is in series with the parallel pair $C_2 = 2.0\ \mu$F, $C_3 = 4.0\ \mu$F, and 12.0 V is applied across the whole network. Reduce it: the parallel pair is $C_{23} = 6.0\ \mu$F ([[§A4.2 Capacitors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]), and in series with $C_1$ ([[§A4.2 Capacitors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]])
>
> $$
> \frac1C = \frac{1}{12.0\ \mu\mathrm F} + \frac{1}{6.0\ \mu\mathrm F} = \frac{1}{4.0\ \mu\mathrm F}, \qquad C = 4.0\ \mu\mathrm F .
> $$
>
> Now go back. The total charge $Q = CV = 48.0\ \mu$C sits on $C_1$ and on the pair (series), so $V_1 = Q/C_1 = 4.0$ V and the pair has $V_2 = V_3 = 12.0 - 4.0 = 8.0$ V. Then $Q_2 = C_2 V_2 = 16.0\ \mu$C and $Q_3 = C_3V_3 = 32.0\ \mu$C, which add to $48.0\ \mu$C as they must.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 8.7*

^ex-a4-2-1

> [!example] Example §A4.2.2: Cutting a Capacitor in Two
> Take a parallel-plate capacitor $C_0 = \varepsilon_0 A/d$ and cut both plates in half, then move the two halves apart. Each half is a capacitor $\varepsilon_0(A/2)/d = C_0/2$, and the two are in parallel between the same two terminals, so the total is $C_0/2 + C_0/2 = C_0$: nothing has changed, since the field and the charge per area are the same as before. Cutting the *gap* instead, by sliding a thin metal sheet midway between the plates, makes two capacitors of gap $d/2$, each $2C_0$, in series: $1/C = 1/(2C_0) + 1/(2C_0)$, again $C = C_0$. The series and parallel rules agree with the geometry: capacitance adds with area (parallel) and its inverse adds with gap (series).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 13*

^ex-a4-2-2
