How much charge does a conductor hold per volt, and when does a pair of conductors have a single capacitance? Level A defined $C = Q/V$ for a capacitor ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]), and level B added the single conductor, the uniqueness behind the proportionality, and $C$ read off the field energy ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^rem-b2-4-4|§B2.4, Remark: Capacitance at level B]]). Level C writes the self-capacitance as a property of one harmonic function, the characteristic potential, which gives both a flux formula and an energy (Dirichlet-integral) formula and with it $C > 0$; computes the disk from the charge density of [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]]; makes "grounding" precise; and shows, with the two-dimensional electron gas of HW 9, how a non-electric energy adds a "quantum capacitance" in series. The general $N$-conductor theory, of which a capacitor is a special charge state, is [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix|§C4.3]].

**Notation.** Potential $V(\vec r)$ (Zangwill $\varphi$; [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: The potential is V]]); the potential of a conductor is a number, $V$ or $V_k$; $\Omega$ is the vacuum region outside the conductors; $U_E$ the electrostatic energy. Capacitances in this note are in farads; "per area" quantities are marked.

## Self-capacitance

> [!definition] Definition §C4.2.1: Characteristic Potential; Self-Capacitance
> For an isolated conductor with surface $S$, the **characteristic potential** $\tilde V$ is the solution of Laplace's equation in the exterior region $\Omega$ with $\tilde V = 1$ on $S$ and $\tilde V \to 0$ at infinity. If the conductor carries charge $Q$ at potential $V$ (zero at infinity), its **self-capacitance** is (Zangwill eqs. (5.35)–(5.36); Likharev eq. (2.13))
>
> $$
> C = \frac{Q}{V} .
> $$
>
> Likharev calls $p = 1/C$ the reciprocal capacitance (elastance).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.1, eqs. (5.35)–(5.36) · Likharev §2.2, eqs. (2.12)–(2.13)*

^def-c4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.1: Self-Capacitance Is Geometric and Positive
> The potential of a conductor at potential $V$ is $V(\vec r) = V\,\tilde V(\vec r)$ ([[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^def-c4-2-1|Def. §C4.2.1]]), and
>
> $$
> C = -\varepsilon_0\oint_S\nabla\tilde V\cdot d\vec S = \varepsilon_0\int_\Omega\lvert\nabla\tilde V\rvert^2\,d^3r > 0 ,
> $$
>
> with $d\vec S = \hat n\,dS$ outward from the conductor. $C$ depends only on the shape and size of the conductor.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.1, eqs. (5.35)–(5.36) (flux form); the energy form and positivity added here*

^thm-c4-2-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Scaling.** $V\tilde V$ is harmonic in $\Omega$, equals $V$ on $S$ and vanishes at infinity. The true potential has the same three properties, and the exterior Dirichlet problem has at most one solution ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]; level C [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]). So $V(\vec r) = V\tilde V(\vec r)$ (Zangwill's use of "Thomson's theorem and Coulomb's law" amounts to the same uniqueness, [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]]).
>
> **2. Flux form.** The surface charge is $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\,\partial V/\partial n = -\varepsilon_0V\,\hat n\cdot\nabla\tilde V$ ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-1|Theorem §B2.4.1]]). Integrating over $S$: $Q = -\varepsilon_0V\oint_S\nabla\tilde V\cdot d\vec S$ (Zangwill eq. (5.35)); divide by $V$.
>
> **3. Energy form: Green's first identity on $\Omega$.** Cut $\Omega$ off at a large sphere $S_R$ of radius $R$, giving the region $\Omega_R$. Green's first identity with $u = v = \tilde V$ ([[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-28-2|452 Thm. §28.2]]) reads $\int_{\Omega_R}(\lvert\nabla\tilde V\rvert^2 + \tilde V\nabla^2\tilde V)\,d^3r = \oint_{\partial\Omega_R}\tilde V\,\nabla\tilde V\cdot\hat n_\Omega\,dS$, with $\hat n_\Omega$ pointing *out of* $\Omega_R$. The term $\tilde V\nabla^2\tilde V$ vanishes (harmonic). On $S$, $\hat n_\Omega = -\hat n$ (into the conductor) and $\tilde V = 1$, giving $-\oint_S\nabla\tilde V\cdot d\vec S$. On $S_R$: far away the conductor looks like a point charge $Q = CV$, so $\tilde V \approx C/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$ and $\partial\tilde V/\partial r \approx -C/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$; the integrand is $O(R^{-3})$ on an area $4\pi R^2$, so this contribution is $O(1/R)$ and drops as $R \to \infty$. Hence $\int_\Omega\lvert\nabla\tilde V\rvert^2\,d^3r = -\oint_S\nabla\tilde V\cdot d\vec S = C/\varepsilon_0$.
>
> **4. Positivity.** $\tilde V$ is not constant on $\Omega$ (it is $1$ on $S$ and tends to $0$), so $\nabla\tilde V$ is not identically zero and, being continuous, $\int_\Omega\lvert\nabla\tilde V\rvert^2 > 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Capacitance is a property of one dimensionless harmonic function; $Q \propto V$ is uniqueness plus linearity.
> - ⚑ By-product: $C = \varepsilon_0\int_\Omega\lvert\nabla\tilde V\rvert^2$ says $\tfrac12CV^2 = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int\lvert\vec E\rvert^2$, the field energy ([[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-3|Theorem §C4.2.3]]). Since $\tilde V$ minimizes the Dirichlet integral among functions with the same boundary values, any trial function with $\tilde V_{\text{trial}} = 1$ on $S$ and $\to 0$ at infinity gives an *upper* bound $C \le \varepsilon_0\int\lvert\nabla\tilde V_{\text{trial}}\rvert^2$ (the variational principle, [[§C6.4★ The Complex Potential and the Variational Principle#^thm-c6-4-3|Theorem §C6.4.3]]).
> - Used next: the $N$-conductor version, where the same Green identity makes the capacitance matrix a Gram matrix ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.2: Self-Capacitance of a Sphere and of a Disk
> $$
> C_{\text{sphere}} = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R, \qquad C_{\text{disk}} = 8\varepsilon_0R ,
> $$
>
> for a sphere and for a thin disk of radius $R$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.1, eqs. (5.37)–(5.40) · Likharev §2.2, eq. (2.17)*

^thm-c4-2-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Sphere.** By symmetry the charge is uniform, the exterior potential is that of a point charge, $Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$, and at $r = R$ it is $V = Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0R$ (Zangwill eq. (5.2)). So $C = Q/V = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R$. Equivalently $\tilde V = R/r$, and $-\varepsilon_0\oint\partial_r(R/r)\,dS = \varepsilon_0\,(R/R^2)\,4\pi R^2 = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R$.
>
> **2. Disk: the potential at the centre.** The conductor's potential equals its value at any point of the disk, in particular at the centre. Each face carries $\sigma(\rho) = Q/4\pi R\sqrt{R^2 - \rho^2}$ ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]]), so the total density in the plane is $2\sigma$, and the distance from the centre to the point at $\rho$ is $\rho$ (Zangwill eq. (5.38)):
>
> $$
> V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!d\phi\int_0^R\rho\,d\rho\,\frac{2\sigma(\rho)}{\rho} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{2Q}{4\pi R}\cdot2\pi\int_0^R\frac{d\rho}{\sqrt{R^2 - \rho^2}} .
> $$
>
> **3. The integral.** Substitute $\rho = R\sin\theta$, $d\rho = R\cos\theta\,d\theta$, $\sqrt{R^2 - \rho^2} = R\cos\theta$, limits $0$ to $\pi/2$: $\int_0^R d\rho/\sqrt{R^2 - \rho^2} = \int_0^{\pi/2}d\theta = \pi/2$. The integrand diverges at $\rho = R$, but integrably ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^der-c4-1-6|§C4.1, Derivation of Theorem §C4.1.6]], step 7), so the integral is an ordinary convergent improper integral.
>
> **4. Result.** $V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{2Q}{4\pi R}\cdot2\pi\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{Q}{8\varepsilon_0R}$ (Zangwill eq. (5.39)), so $C = 8\varepsilon_0R$ (eq. (5.40)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The disk holds $8/4\pi = 2/\pi \approx 0.64$ of the charge of a sphere of the same radius at the same potential, with half the area of the sphere's surface ($2\pi R^2$ for both faces versus $4\pi R^2$): capacitance is not proportional to area.

^der-c4-2-2

*Uses:* [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]]

> [!remark] Remark: How big is a capacitance?
> - *Dimension.* $C$ is $\varepsilon_0$ times a length of the order of the body's size (Likharev eq. (2.18)). Since the charge sits on the surface, Zangwill quotes the estimate $C \approx \varepsilon_0\sqrt{4\pi A}$, $A$ the surface area (eq. (5.41), Chow and Yovanovich): exact for a sphere ($\sqrt{4\pi\cdot4\pi R^2} = 4\pi R$), and for the disk ($A = 2\pi R^2$) it gives $2\sqrt2\,\pi\,\varepsilon_0R \approx 8.89\,\varepsilon_0R$, 11% above $8\varepsilon_0R$; for spheroids it stays good over two decades of aspect ratio (Zangwill Figure 5.6).
> - *Units.* The farad is huge: the Earth ($R = 6.37\times10^6$ m) has $C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R \approx 7.1\times10^{-4}$ F, below a millifarad (Zangwill Problem 5.14; Likharev §2.2). In Gaussian units $C_{\text{sphere}} = R$, a length; $1\ \mathrm{pF} \approx 0.90$ cm, the capacitance of a ball of about 1 cm radius (Likharev §2.2, footnote 13).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.1, eq. (5.41), Figure 5.6, Problem 5.14 · Likharev §2.2, eqs. (2.17)–(2.18) (Earth's capacitance computed here)*

^rem-c4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.3: Charging Energy of a Conductor
> An isolated conductor with charge $Q$ and self-capacitance $C$ stores
>
> $$
> U_E = \tfrac12QV = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \tfrac12CV^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.5, eq. (5.62) · Likharev §2.2, eqs. (2.14)–(2.15)*

^thm-c4-2-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1.** The energy of a charge distribution is $U_E = \tfrac12\int\rho V\,d^3r$ ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]; Zangwill eq. (5.58)). For the conductor $\rho = \sigma\,\delta_S$, and the pairing of this surface distribution with the continuous function $V$ is $\oint_S\sigma V\,dS$ ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^rem-c4-1-1|§C4.1, Remark: What changes from levels A and B]]).
>
> **2.** $V$ is constant on $S$ and comes out: $U_E = \tfrac12V\oint_S\sigma\,dS = \tfrac12QV$.
>
> **3.** Substitute $V = Q/C$ or $Q = CV$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Level A had $Q^2/2C$ for a capacitor from the work of charging ([[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]); here it is the general energy formula evaluated on an equipotential. With [[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-1|Theorem §C4.2.1]] it equals $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int\lvert\vec E\rvert^2$.

^der-c4-2-3

*Uses:* [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]], [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^rem-c4-1-1|§C4.1, Remark: What changes from levels A and B]]

## Grounding

> [!definition] Definition §C4.2.2: Ground; Grounding a Conductor
> The **ground** is a conductor of self-capacitance so large that finite charges added to or taken from it leave its potential at zero; the Earth is the standard example ($C_{\text{Earth}} \to \infty$, $V_{\text{Earth}} \to 0$). To **ground** a conductor is to connect it to the ground by a fine conducting wire: its potential is then fixed at $V = 0$, and its charge becomes an unknown, fixed by equilibrium. In electrical engineering any constant-potential conductor is called ground (Likharev).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.2 · Likharev §2.2, footnote 19*

^def-c4-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: What grounding does
> Grounding lets charge flow until the conductor and the ground share one potential, and the flow lowers the energy of the joined system (Zangwill Figure 5.7). A conductor with charge $Q$ loses it all: its final state is $Q = V = 0$ (the reservoir's potential does not change). An uncharged conductor near a charge $q$ draws charge of the opposite sign *up from ground*; for a point charge outside a grounded sphere the drawn charge is the image charge $-qR/a$ ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]). Grounding exchanges a known charge for a known potential: in the $N$-conductor theory each conductor has one of the two given ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix|§C4.3]]), and the energy that matters for forces changes accordingly ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors|§C4.4]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.2, Figure 5.7*

^rem-c4-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: A thin wire is not chargeless for free
> Zangwill assumes that the grounding wires are "sufficiently thin" to carry no charge and can be removed after equilibrium without changing anything ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^der-c4-3-1|§C4.3, Derivation of Theorem §C4.3.1]], step 4). A wire of length $L$ and radius $a$, however, has a self-capacitance of order $2\pi\varepsilon_0L/\ln(L/a)$ (its potential, at charge $\lambda$ per length, is about $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln(L/a)$), which decreases only *logarithmically* as the wire is made thinner (Likharev §2.2, footnote 14: for thin, long conductors the capacitance is set mostly by the larger size). The idealization needs $\ln(L/a)$ large, or wires short compared with the conductors.
>
> *Source: Likharev §2.2, footnote 14 (the estimate of the wire's capacitance worked out here)*

## Capacitors

> [!definition] Definition §C4.2.3: Capacitor; Mutual Capacitance
> A **capacitor** is a pair of conductors in the charge state $Q_1 = Q$, $Q_2 = -Q$. Its **capacitance** (Likharev: mutual capacitance) is (Zangwill eq. (5.53), first equality; Likharev eq. (2.26))
>
> $$
> C = \frac{Q}{V_1 - V_2} .
> $$
>
> The definition needs the charges to be equal and opposite: for general $Q_1$, $Q_2$ two conductors need three coefficients, not one ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.4, eq. (5.53) · Likharev §2.2, eqs. (2.25)–(2.26)*

^def-c4-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.4: Capacitance as Flux over Voltage; Energy; Positivity
> For any closed surface $S$ enclosing conductor 1 but not conductor 2, and any path from conductor 1 to conductor 2,
>
> $$
> C = \frac{\varepsilon_0\oint_S\vec E\cdot d\vec S}{\int_1^2\vec E\cdot d\vec\ell}, \qquad U_E = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \tfrac12C\,(V_1 - V_2)^2, \qquad C > 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.4, eq. (5.56); §5.5, eq. (5.61) · Likharev §2.2, eq. (2.27)*

^thm-c4-2-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Numerator.** Gauss's law over $S$, which encloses exactly the charge $Q$ of conductor 1: $\varepsilon_0\oint_S\vec E\cdot d\vec S = Q$.
>
> **2. Denominator.** $\vec E = -\nabla V$, so $\int_1^2\vec E\cdot d\vec\ell = V_1 - V_2$, the same for every path (the field is conservative, [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-1|Theorem §C2.1.1]]). The ratio is $Q/(V_1 - V_2)$, [[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^def-c4-2-3|Def. §C4.2.3]].
>
> **3. Energy.** As in [[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-3|Theorem §C4.2.3]], $U_E = \tfrac12\int\rho V = \tfrac12(Q_1V_1 + Q_2V_2) = \tfrac12Q(V_1 - V_2)$; substituting $V_1 - V_2 = Q/C$ gives the two forms.
>
> **4. Positivity.** $U_E = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int\lvert\vec E\rvert^2\,d^3r > 0$ for $Q \ne 0$ ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^rem-c2-3-1|§C2.3, Remark: Positive energy, and where it sits]]; Zangwill §3.6.1), and $U_E = \tfrac12Q(V_1 - V_2) = Q^2/2C$, so $C > 0$ (Zangwill's argument after eq. (5.61)): the positive conductor is always at the higher potential.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The capacitance is geometric for the same reason as in [[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-1|Theorem §C4.2.1]]: by uniqueness the field scales with $Q$, so numerator and denominator scale together.
> - In terms of the capacitance matrix, $C = (C_{11}C_{22} - C_{12}^2)/(C_{11} + C_{22} + 2C_{12})$ ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-7|Theorem §C4.3.7]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C4.2.1: Fringing Fields Raise the Capacitance of Parallel Plates
> Two identical flat plates of area $A$ and arbitrary shape face each other at separation $d$. For $d \ll \sqrt A$ the uniform-field estimate is $C_0 = \varepsilon_0A/d$ (Zangwill eq. (5.57); [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]]). Show that the true capacitance is larger, $C > C_0$, for every $d$.
>
> *Set-up.* Hold the plates at $\pm V/2$ (plate 1 at $z = d$, plate 2 at $z = 0$). The arrangement is symmetric under reflection in the midplane combined with $V \to -V$, so by uniqueness ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]) the reflected solution is the solution with the signs reversed, and the charges are $\pm Q$: a capacitor state, with $U_E = \tfrac12CV^2$ ([[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]). Let $\mathcal V$ be the slab between the plates (the plate shape times $(0, d)$), $\vec E = -\nabla V$ the true field and $\vec E_0 = -\nabla V_0$ with $V_0 = -V/2 + Vz/d$ the uniform field of infinite plates.
>
> *Identity.* With $\delta\vec E = \vec E - \vec E_0$, expanding $\lvert\vec E_0 + \delta\vec E\rvert^2$ (Zangwill Problem 5.20(a)):
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V}\lvert\vec E\rvert^2 - \int_{\mathcal V}\lvert\vec E_0\rvert^2 = \int_{\mathcal V}\lvert\delta\vec E\rvert^2 + 2\int_{\mathcal V}\vec E_0\cdot\delta\vec E .
> $$
>
> *The cross term vanishes.* $\delta\vec E = -\nabla\delta V$ with $\delta V = V - V_0$, and $\nabla\cdot\vec E_0 = 0$, so $\vec E_0\cdot\delta\vec E = -\nabla\cdot(\delta V\,\vec E_0)$ and, by the divergence theorem, $\int_{\mathcal V}\vec E_0\cdot\delta\vec E = -\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\delta V\,\vec E_0\cdot\hat n\,dS$. On the two faces $\delta V = 0$ (both $V$ and $V_0$ equal $\pm V/2$ there); on the side wall $\vec E_0 \parallel \hat z$ is tangent, $\vec E_0\cdot\hat n = 0$. So the cross term is zero.
>
> *Bound.* Hence $\int_{\mathcal V}\lvert\vec E\rvert^2 \ge \int_{\mathcal V}\lvert\vec E_0\rvert^2 = (V/d)^2Ad$, and
>
> $$
> \tfrac12CV^2 = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int_{\text{all space}}\lvert\vec E\rvert^2 \;\ge\; \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int_{\mathcal V}\lvert\vec E\rvert^2 \;\ge\; \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\,\frac{V^2}{d^2}\,Ad = \tfrac12C_0V^2 .
> $$
>
> The first inequality is strict, since the field outside the slab cannot vanish (the potential is $+V/2$ on the outer face of plate 1 and tends to $0$ at infinity), so $C > C_0$. Zangwill's heuristic reads the same from Figure 5.11: fringe lines put some charge on the outer faces, so the field between the plates is slightly below $Q/A\varepsilon_0$ and the voltage for a given $Q$ is lower. Likharev quotes the fringe ("stray") capacitance as of order $\varepsilon_0\sqrt A$, against $C_0 \sim \varepsilon_0\sqrt A\,(\sqrt A/d)$; for two coaxial disks of radius $R$ it is $\varepsilon_0R[\ln(16\pi R/d) - 1]$ (Kirchhoff's result).
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 5.20 (solved here), §5.4.4, eq. (5.57), Figure 5.11 · Likharev §2.2, eq. (2.28) and footnote 17*

^ex-c4-2-1

> [!example] Example §C4.2.2: The Two-Dimensional Electron Gas Capacitor and Quantum Capacitance
> Two infinite parallel perfectly conducting plates are a distance $d$ apart. The lower surface of the upper plate carries charge per area $\sigma_1 > 0$, the upper surface of the lower plate $\sigma_2$, and at height $L$ above the lower plate lies a sheet with $\sigma_0 = -(\sigma_1 + \sigma_2)$: a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) whose energy per area is $u_0 = \pi\hbar^2\sigma_0^2/2me^2$. Fix $\sigma_1$ and minimize the total energy per area over $\sigma_2$ (charge flows between the lower plate and the 2DEG).
>
> *The 2DEG energy.* Spin-½ fermions in a plane have the constant density of states $m/\pi\hbar^2$ per area, Fermi energy $\varepsilon_F = \pi\hbar^2n/m$ at areal density $n$, and mean energy $\varepsilon_F/2$ per particle at $T = 0$ ([[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^ex-b13-1-3|TH Example §B13.1.3]]). So the kinetic energy per area is $n\varepsilon_F/2 = \pi\hbar^2n^2/2m$, and with $n = \lvert\sigma_0\rvert/e$ this is $u_0$.
>
> *Fields.* The total charge per area is $\sigma_1 + \sigma_2 + \sigma_0 = 0$, so the field vanishes outside the plates (and in the metal). Gauss's law with pillboxes from inside the lower plate: $E_z = \sigma_2/\varepsilon_0$ for $0 < z < L$, and $E_z = (\sigma_2 + \sigma_0)/\varepsilon_0 = -\sigma_1/\varepsilon_0$ for $L < z < d$.
>
> *Total energy per area.*
>
> $$
> u(\sigma_2) = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{\sigma_2}{\varepsilon_0}\Bigr)^2L + \Bigl(\frac{\sigma_1}{\varepsilon_0}\Bigr)^2(d - L)\Bigr] + \frac{\pi\hbar^2}{2me^2}(\sigma_1 + \sigma_2)^2 .
> $$
>
> *Minimize.* With $\sigma_1$ fixed, $du/d\sigma_2 = \sigma_2L/\varepsilon_0 + \frac{\pi\hbar^2}{me^2}(\sigma_1 + \sigma_2) = 0$, and $d^2u/d\sigma_2^2 = L/\varepsilon_0 + \pi\hbar^2/me^2 > 0$ (a minimum). Define the geometric and the **quantum capacitance** per area
>
> $$
> C_2 = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{L}, \qquad C_0 = \frac{me^2}{\pi\hbar^2} = e^2\times(\text{density of states per area}) .
> $$
>
> The condition reads $\sigma_2(1/C_2 + 1/C_0) = -\sigma_1/C_0$, so
>
> $$
> \sigma_2 = -\sigma_1\,\frac{C_2}{C_2 + C_0}, \qquad \sigma_0 = -\sigma_1\,\frac{C_0}{C_2 + C_0} .
> $$
>
> *Reading.* $\sigma_2 < 0$: the lower plate carries charge of the same sign as the 2DEG. The 2DEG screens the upper plate's field only partly, and the part that leaks through ends on the lower plate. The inverse capacitances add: $1/C_2 + 1/C_0$ is a geometric gap in series with a "quantum gap" $\varepsilon_0/C_0 = \pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2/me^2 = a_B/4$ (with $a_B = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2/me^2$): the 2DEG behaves like a perfect conductor whose surface sits a quarter Bohr radius behind the sheet, the two-dimensional cousin of the Thomas–Fermi length ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^rem-c4-1-6|§C4.1, ★ Remark: Real conductors and the screening length]]).
>
> *Classical limit.* $\hbar \to 0$ gives $C_0 \to \infty$ (compressing the electrons costs nothing), $\sigma_2 \to 0$ and $\sigma_0 \to -\sigma_1$: the sheet screens completely, as a perfect conductor would, and no field reaches the lower plate. In a semiconductor, $m \to m^{\ast}$ and $\varepsilon_0 \to \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0$ make the quantum gap $a_B^{\ast}/4$ with $a_B^{\ast} = a_B\,\varepsilon_r\,m/m^{\ast}$; for GaAs ($m^{\ast} = 0.067\,m$, $\varepsilon_r \approx 12.9$) $a_B^{\ast} \approx 10$ nm, so the quantum gap of about 2.5 nm is comparable to typical gate distances and is measured.
>
> *The statement's sign.* Zangwill's statement says $\sigma_2 > 0$; the result it asks for has $\sigma_2 < 0$ for $\sigma_1 > 0$, so the sign condition on $\sigma_2$ should be dropped (or read as $\sigma_2$ of either sign).
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 9, Problem 4 (Zangwill Problem 5.17), solved here (the GaAs numbers computed here)*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Coulomb blockade
> For a conductor of microscopic size (a quantum dot, $R \sim 10$ nm) the charging energy $Q^2/2C \propto 1/R$ of [[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-3|Theorem §C4.2.3]] is large compared with $kT$ at low temperature. Let a dot be coupled through a thin tunnel barrier to a large reservoir and held at an external potential $V$; with $N$ carriers of charge $e$ added to the dot (dot charge $Ne$), the relevant energy is (Zangwill eq. (5.63))
>
> $$
> U_E(N) = \frac{(Ne)^2}{2C} - NeV ,
> $$
>
> with $C \approx C_{11}$, the dot's self-capacitance, since the reservoir's capacitance is much larger ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-7|Theorem §C4.3.7]] with $C_{22} \gg C_{11}, \lvert C_{12}\rvert$; Zangwill's wording, although $C_{11}$ is not strictly the self-capacitance, [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^cau-c4-3-1|§C4.3, Caution: Capacitance needs a qualifier]]). Adding $M$ electrons changes it by $U_E(N + M) - U_E(N) = \frac{(2NM + M^2)e^2}{2C} - MeV = Me\bigl[(N + \tfrac M2)\frac eC - V\bigr]$ (eq. (5.64)), which first turns negative for $M = 1$ at $VC/e = N + \tfrac12$. The dot's charge is therefore a staircase in $V$, with plateaus ("Coulomb blockade") of width $e/C$; thermal excitation over the barrier at $kT \gg e^2/2C$ smears it into the classical line $Q = CV$. Counting electrons on such a staircase gives a capacitance standard.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Application 5.2, eqs. (5.63)–(5.64), Figure 5.12*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $C = \varepsilon_0\int\lvert\nabla\tilde V\rvert^2$ is a Dirichlet energy: minimizing it over functions with fixed boundary values is the Dirichlet principle ([[§C6.4★ The Complex Potential and the Variational Principle#^thm-c6-4-3|Theorem §C6.4.3]]), and Green's first identity ([[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-28-2|452 Thm. §28.2]]) is what turns the flux into the energy.
> - The quantum capacitance $e^2\times$(density of states) is the inverse of $\partial\mu/\partial(\text{charge})$, the same thermodynamic derivative $\partial n/\partial\mu$ that sets the Thomas–Fermi length; for the 2D gas the density of states is constant ([[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^ex-b13-1-3|TH Example §B13.1.3]]), so $C_0$ does not depend on the density.
> - The Coulomb staircase is charge quantization made visible by a large charging energy, the same competition between an electrostatic and a quantum energy that the 2DEG capacitor shows in its continuum form; the tunnelling that lets electrons on and off the dot is barrier penetration ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling|QM §A4.6]]).
