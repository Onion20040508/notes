*Sources: Zangwill, Modern Electrodynamics, §5.4.3–§5.4.4, §5.5 (Examples 5.3–5.4), Problems 5.2, 5.10, 5.13, 5.15 · Likharev, Essential Graduate Physics: EM, §2.2 · PHY 505 (Wells, Fall 2025), HW 9, Problems 1–3.*

How do $N$ conductors influence one another, when each may be held at a potential or carry a charge? One number per conductor is not enough: the charge on each depends linearly on all the potentials, through a matrix of geometric coefficients. Level A and B had only the capacitor, a single charge state of two conductors ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^def-c4-2-3|Def. §C4.2.3]]). This section builds the capacitance matrix from elementary solutions ([[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-1|Theorem §C4.2.1]] for $N$ conductors), proves it symmetric (Green's reciprocity, [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]]) and positive definite (energy; [[§24 Positive Operators|LADR §24]]), derives Maxwell's sign inequalities from the maximum principle, recovers the capacitor, and uses reciprocity to find induced charges without solving for any field (HW 9).

**Notation.** Conductors $k = 1, \dots, N$ with surfaces $S_k$, potentials $V_k$ (zero at infinity), charges $Q_k$; column vectors $\mathbf V$, $\mathbf Q$; $\Omega$ is the vacuum outside all conductors (it may have several components: the unbounded one and the cavities); $\hat n$ points out of the conductor. Zangwill writes $\varphi_k$ for the potentials ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: The potential is V]]); Likharev writes $p_{jj'}$ for Zangwill's $P_{ij}$.

## The matrix and its existence

> [!definition] Definition §C4.3.1: Elementary Potentials; Capacitance Matrix; Coefficients of Potential
> The **elementary potential** $\tilde V_j$ of conductor $j$ is the harmonic function in $\Omega$ with $\tilde V_j = \delta_{ij}$ on $S_i$ for every $i$ and $\tilde V_j \to 0$ at infinity: conductor $j$ at unit potential, all others grounded. The **capacitance matrix** has entries (Zangwill eq. (5.42))
>
> $$
> C_{ij} = -\varepsilon_0\oint_{S_i}\nabla\tilde V_j\cdot d\vec S ,
> $$
>
> the charge on conductor $i$ in that state: the **coefficients of capacitance** $C_{kk}$ and the **mutual** (cross) **capacitances** $C_{ij}$, $i \ne j$. The **coefficients of potential** are the entries of $P = C^{-1}$ (Zangwill eq. (5.51); it exists by [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]]); Likharev calls $P$ the reciprocal capacitance matrix.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.3, eqs. (5.42)–(5.44), Figure 5.8; §5.4.4, eq. (5.51) · Likharev §2.2, eqs. (2.19), (2.34)*

^def-c4-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.1: Charges Are Linear in the Potentials
> For $N$ conductors at potentials $V_j$ with no other charges present, the potential is $V(\vec r) = \sum_jV_j\tilde V_j(\vec r)$ and
>
> $$
> Q_i = \sum_{j=1}^NC_{ij}V_j, \qquad \mathbf Q = C\,\mathbf V ,
> $$
>
> with $C_{ij}$ ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^def-c4-3-1|Def. §C4.3.1]]) depending only on the shapes and the arrangement of the conductors.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.3, eqs. (5.42)–(5.45) · Likharev §2.2, eq. (2.19)*

^thm-c4-3-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Elementary problems.** For each $j$, $\tilde V_j$ solves an exterior Dirichlet problem; it is unique ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]; [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]], which also allows charges instead of potentials as data, [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^rem-c6-1-2|§C6.1, Remark: Potentials or charges]]). Physically: hold conductor $j$ at unit potential with a battery and ground the others ([[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^def-c4-2-2|Def. §C4.2.2]]); the grounded conductors draw charge $C_{ij}$ up from ground (Zangwill eqs. (5.43)–(5.44)). Its value depends on the geometry alone, because $\tilde V_j$ does.
>
> **2. Superposition.** $W = \sum_jV_j\tilde V_j$ is harmonic in $\Omega$ (a finite sum of harmonic functions), equals $\sum_jV_j\delta_{ij} = V_i$ on $S_i$, and tends to $0$ at infinity. The true potential has the same data, so by uniqueness $V = W$ (Zangwill's superposition of the $N$ grounding scenarios, eq. (5.45)).
>
> **3. Charges.** On $S_i$, $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\,\hat n\cdot\nabla V = \sum_jV_j\bigl(-\varepsilon_0\,\hat n\cdot\nabla\tilde V_j\bigr)$ ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-1|Theorem §B2.4.1]]). Integrating over $S_i$ and exchanging the finite sum with the integral gives $Q_i = \sum_jC_{ij}V_j$.
>
> **4. The grounding wires.** If the wires used to fix the potentials are thin enough to hold no charge, removing them after equilibrium changes no charge and no potential (Zangwill); see [[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^cau-c4-2-1|§C4.2, Caution: A thin wire is not chargeless for free]] for what "thin enough" costs.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Linearity is superposition plus uniqueness, exactly as for one conductor ([[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-1|Theorem §C4.2.1]]).
> - $C_{kk}$ is the charge on $k$ with *all other conductors grounded*, which is not the self-capacitance of $k$ alone: the charge drawn onto the grounded neighbours lowers $k$'s potential for a given charge ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^cau-c4-3-1|Caution: Capacitance needs a qualifier]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.2: The Capacitance Matrix Is a Gram Matrix
> $$
> C_{ij} = \varepsilon_0\int_\Omega\nabla\tilde V_i\cdot\nabla\tilde V_j\,d^3r .
> $$
>
> So $C$ is the Gram matrix of the fields $-\nabla\tilde V_i$ in the inner product $\langle\vec E, \vec E'\rangle = \varepsilon_0\int_\Omega\vec E\cdot\vec E'\,d^3r$; in particular $C_{ij} = C_{ji}$.
>
> *Source: derived here (Green's first identity applied to Zangwill's eq. (5.42)); it gives Zangwill's eqs. (5.46) and (5.59) in one line*

^thm-c4-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Green's first identity.** On $\Omega$ cut off by a large sphere $S_R$, with $u = \tilde V_i$, $v = \tilde V_j$ ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-2|452 Thm. §17.2]]): $\int_{\Omega_R}\bigl(\nabla\tilde V_i\cdot\nabla\tilde V_j + \tilde V_i\nabla^2\tilde V_j\bigr)\,d^3r = \oint_{\partial\Omega_R}\tilde V_i\,\nabla\tilde V_j\cdot\hat n_\Omega\,dS$, with $\hat n_\Omega$ pointing out of $\Omega_R$. If $\Omega$ has several components (cavities), apply it to each and add.
>
> **2. Drop the Laplacian.** $\nabla^2\tilde V_j = 0$ in $\Omega$.
>
> **3. Conductor surfaces.** On $S_k$, $\tilde V_i = \delta_{ik}$ and $\hat n_\Omega = -\hat n$, so the surfaces contribute $\sum_k\delta_{ik}\bigl(-\oint_{S_k}\nabla\tilde V_j\cdot d\vec S\bigr) = -\oint_{S_i}\nabla\tilde V_j\cdot d\vec S = C_{ij}/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^def-c4-3-1|Def. §C4.3.1]]).
>
> **4. Sphere at infinity.** Far away each $\tilde V$ is a monopole field, $O(1/R)$, with gradient $O(1/R^2)$; the integrand on $S_R$ is $O(R^{-3})$ on area $4\pi R^2$, so the contribution is $O(1/R) \to 0$.
>
> **5. Result.** $\int_\Omega\nabla\tilde V_i\cdot\nabla\tilde V_j = C_{ij}/\varepsilon_0$. The integrand is symmetric in $i, j$, hence $C_{ij} = C_{ji}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Symmetry and positivity of $C$ are one fact: $C$ is a Gram matrix ([[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-62|LADR Def. 7.62]], Connections: Gram matrices of independent lists are positive definite; the independence is [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]]).
> - ⚑ By-product: $\mathbf V^{\mathsf T}C\,\mathbf V = \varepsilon_0\int_\Omega\lvert\nabla\sum_iV_i\tilde V_i\rvert^2 = \varepsilon_0\int\lvert\vec E\rvert^2$, twice the field energy → [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]].

^der-c4-3-2

*Uses:* [[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-2|452 Thm. §17.2]], [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^def-c4-3-1|Def. §C4.3.1]]

## Reciprocity and symmetry

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.3: Green's Reciprocity for Conductors
> Take the same $N$ conductors in two states, unprimed and primed, each with possible extra charge $\rho_{\text{ex}}$, $\rho'_{\text{ex}}$ in $\Omega$. Then
>
> $$
> \sum_{i=1}^NV_iQ'_i + \int\rho'_{\text{ex}}V\,d^3r = \sum_{i=1}^NV'_iQ_i + \int\rho_{\text{ex}}V'\,d^3r ,
> $$
>
> provided each extra charge sits where the other state's potential is smooth. This is Green's reciprocity relation ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]]) evaluated on conductors. Without extra charges, $\sum_iV_iQ'_i = \sum_iV'_iQ_i$ (Zangwill eq. (5.48)), and with $\mathbf Q = C\mathbf V$ this is equivalent to $C_{ij} = C_{ji}$ (eq. (5.46); Likharev eq. (2.20)).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.3, eqs. (5.46)–(5.49), Example 5.3; Problem 5.15(a) · Likharev §2.2, eqs. (2.20)–(2.22)*

^thm-c4-3-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Green's reciprocity.** For two charge distributions with potentials $V$ and $V'$, $\int\rho'V\,d^3r = \int\rho V'\,d^3r$: both equal $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\!\!\int\rho(\vec r)\rho'(\vec r\,')/\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert$, by exchanging the order of integration in the symmetric kernel (Zangwill eq. (3.70); [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]]).
>
> **2. Sense for distributions.** The exchange is legitimate when the double integral converges absolutely: for finite-energy distributions (surface charges included), and for a point charge $q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)$ or a point dipole $-\vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)$ in one state if the other state's potential is smooth near $\vec r_0$. Then $\int q\,\delta^3V' = qV'(\vec r_0)$ and, by the definition of the derivative of a distribution, $\int(-\vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3)V' = \vec p\cdot\nabla V'(\vec r_0)$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]; point dipole as a distribution [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^mod-c3-1-2|Model §C3.1.2]]). Both states containing a point charge at the same place would bring in its infinite self-term; that case is excluded.
>
> **3. Insert the conductors.** In each state $\rho = \sum_i\sigma_i\,\delta_{S_i} + \rho_{\text{ex}}$. Pair the primed density with $V$: $\int\rho'V = \sum_i\oint_{S_i}\sigma'_iV\,dS + \int\rho'_{\text{ex}}V$. On $S_i$, $V = V_i$ is constant and comes out of the integral: $\oint_{S_i}\sigma'_iV\,dS = V_iQ'_i$. Likewise $\int\rho V' = \sum_iV'_iQ_i + \int\rho_{\text{ex}}V'$. Equating gives the theorem (Zangwill eq. (5.48) without extra charge).
>
> **4. Symmetry of $C$.** With $\mathbf Q = C\mathbf V$, $\mathbf Q' = C\mathbf V'$: $\sum_{i,j}V_iC_{ij}V'_j = \sum_{i,j}V'_iC_{ij}V_j$ (eq. (5.49)). Rename $i \leftrightarrow j$ on the right: $\sum_{i,j}V_iC_{ji}V'_j$. Choosing $\mathbf V = \mathbf e_a$, $\mathbf V' = \mathbf e_b$ gives $C_{ab} = C_{ba}$. Conversely, $C = C^{\mathsf T}$ gives $\mathbf V^{\mathsf T}C\mathbf V' = \mathbf V'^{\mathsf T}C\mathbf V$, the theorem without extra charge (Zangwill Problem 5.15(a)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Two proofs of the symmetry: reciprocity (Zangwill, Likharev) and the Gram form ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]]). Physically, the charge drawn onto grounded conductor $i$ by unit potential on $j$ equals the charge drawn onto $j$ by unit potential on $i$, however different the two conductors are.
> - The version with extra charges is a computational tool: an unknown induced charge in a hard problem is traded for a potential in an easy comparison problem (Examples §C4.3.2–§C4.3.4). Zangwill treats a point charge as an "infinitesimal conductor"; the distributional pairing of step 2 does the same without that device and covers dipoles.

## Energy, positivity and Maxwell's inequalities

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.4: Energy of a System of Conductors
> $$
> U_E = \frac12\sum_kQ_kV_k = \frac12\sum_{i,j}V_iC_{ij}V_j = \frac12\sum_{i,j}Q_iP_{ij}Q_j .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.5, eqs. (5.58)–(5.60) · Likharev §2.2, eq. (2.24)*

^thm-c4-3-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1.** $U_E = \tfrac12\int\rho V\,d^3r$ ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]) with $\rho = \sum_k\sigma_k\,\delta_{S_k}$; on $S_k$, $V = V_k$ comes out of the surface integral: $U_E = \tfrac12\sum_kV_k\oint_{S_k}\sigma_k\,dS = \tfrac12\sum_kQ_kV_k$ (Zangwill eq. (5.58)), as for one conductor ([[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-3|Theorem §C4.2.3]]).
>
> **2.** Substitute $Q_k = \sum_jC_{kj}V_j$ (eq. (5.59)) or $V_k = \sum_jP_{kj}Q_j$ (eq. (5.60)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - $U_E$ is a quadratic form in the potentials with matrix $\tfrac12C$, or in the charges with matrix $\tfrac12P$; the equality with $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int\lvert\vec E\rvert^2$ is [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]].

^der-c4-3-4

*Uses:* [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]], [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.5: C and P Are Positive Definite
> The capacitance matrix is symmetric positive definite ([[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-62|LADR Def. 7.62]]): $\mathbf V^{\mathsf T}C\mathbf V > 0$ for $\mathbf V \ne \mathbf 0$. Hence it is invertible ([[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-61|LADR Thm. 7.61]]), $P = C^{-1}$ exists and is positive definite, and the charges fix the potentials, $V_i = \sum_jP_{ij}Q_j$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.5 (from $U_E \ge 0$, §3.6.1) and §5.4.4, footnote 7; the complete argument is given here*

^thm-c4-3-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Quadratic form.** For $\mathbf V \ne \mathbf 0$ let $W = \sum_iV_i\tilde V_i$; by [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]], $\mathbf V^{\mathsf T}C\mathbf V = \varepsilon_0\int_\Omega\lvert\nabla W\rvert^2 \ge 0$.
>
> **2. Zero only for $\mathbf V = \mathbf 0$.** Suppose the integral vanishes. Then $\nabla W = \vec 0$, so $W$ is constant on each component of $\Omega$. On the unbounded component $W \to 0$, so $W = 0$ there, and every conductor whose surface touches it has $V_i = 0$. A cavity is bounded by the inner wall of one conductor and the outer surfaces of the conductors inside it; $W$ is constant in the cavity, so all of them share the potential of the wall. Working inward, from the conductors that touch the unbounded component to those in their cavities, and so on, every $V_i = 0$. Hence $\mathbf V^{\mathsf T}C\mathbf V > 0$ for $\mathbf V \ne \mathbf 0$; with $C = C^{\mathsf T}$ ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]]) $C$ is positive definite.
>
> **3. Inverse.** A positive definite matrix is injective ($C\mathbf V = \mathbf 0$ would give $\mathbf V^{\mathsf T}C\mathbf V = 0$), hence invertible ([[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-61|LADR Thm. 7.61]]). $P = C^{-1}$ is symmetric, and for $\mathbf Q \ne \mathbf 0$, writing $\mathbf Q = C\mathbf V$, $\mathbf Q^{\mathsf T}P\mathbf Q = \mathbf V^{\mathsf T}C\mathbf V > 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Physically: any nonzero set of potentials (or charges) stores positive energy, $U_E = \tfrac12\mathbf V^{\mathsf T}C\mathbf V = \tfrac12\mathbf Q^{\mathsf T}P\mathbf Q > 0$ (Zangwill uses this in the $2\times2$ case to show $C > 0$ for a capacitor).
> - Invertibility means the charges determine the potentials: the second uniqueness theorem ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]) in matrix form.

^der-c4-3-5

*Uses:* [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]], [[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-61|LADR Thm. 7.61]], [[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-62|LADR Def. 7.62]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.6: Maxwell's Inequalities
> For $N$ conductors,
>
> $$
> C_{kk} > 0, \qquad C_{jk} \le 0\ \ (j \ne k), \qquad \sum_jC_{kj} \ge 0 .
> $$
>
> $C_{jk} < 0$ whenever one vacuum region touches both conductors; $C_{jk} = 0$ when they are screened from each other. $\sum_jC_{kj} = 0$ exactly when conductor $k$ does not touch the unbounded vacuum region (it sits in a cavity of another conductor).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.3, eq. (5.50) (with a field-line argument; the maximum-principle proof and the equality cases are given here)*

^thm-c4-3-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Bounds.** $\tilde V_k$ is harmonic on each component of $\Omega$, with boundary values $0$ or $1$ and limit $0$ at infinity. By the maximum principle ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]; with the limit at infinity as boundary value on the unbounded component, [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]], part 3), $0 \le \tilde V_k \le 1$ in $\Omega$.
>
> **2. Off-diagonal.** On $S_j$, $j \ne k$, $\tilde V_k = 0$ is its minimum value. Moving from $S_j$ into $\Omega$ along $\hat n$ (out of conductor $j$), $\tilde V_k$ cannot decrease, so $\partial\tilde V_k/\partial n \ge 0$ and $\sigma_j = -\varepsilon_0\,\partial\tilde V_k/\partial n \le 0$ at every point. Integrating, $C_{jk} \le 0$. If a component of $\Omega$ touches both $S_j$ and $S_k$, $\tilde V_k$ is not constant on it ($0$ on one, $1$ on the other), and Hopf's boundary-point lemma (for smooth surfaces: a non-constant harmonic function has a nonzero normal derivative at a boundary minimum; stated, not proved here; Mathematical Methods, planned) makes $\partial\tilde V_k/\partial n > 0$ on $S_j$, so $C_{jk} < 0$. If no component touches both, then $\tilde V_k = 0$ on every component touching $S_j$ (its boundary values there are all $0$), so $\sigma_j = 0$ and $C_{jk} = 0$.
>
> **3. Diagonal.** $C_{kk} = \varepsilon_0\int_\Omega\lvert\nabla\tilde V_k\rvert^2 > 0$ ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]]), since $\tilde V_k$ is not constant on the component next to $S_k$ (it is $1$ on $S_k$ and either tends to $0$ or reaches a grounded wall).
>
> **4. Row sums.** $\sum_jC_{kj}$ is the charge on $k$ when every conductor is at unit potential, i.e. in the state $W = \sum_j\tilde V_j$. $W = 1$ on all conductors, so $0 \le W \le 1$ by step 1, and $W$ is constant ($= 1$) on every cavity. On $S_k$, $W = 1$ is its maximum, so $\partial W/\partial n \le 0$ and $\sigma_k \ge 0$: $\sum_jC_{kj} \ge 0$. Surfaces facing a cavity get $\sigma = 0$; surfaces facing the unbounded component, where $W$ falls to $0$, get $\sigma > 0$ by Hopf's lemma. So the sum vanishes exactly when $k$ touches only cavities.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The signs say: raise one conductor's potential and every other conductor in view of it acquires charge of the opposite sign, while the raised conductor acquires at least as much positive charge as all the others negative ($C_{kk} \ge \sum_{j\ne k}\lvert C_{kj}\rvert$); the excess is the flux that escapes to infinity. Zangwill's field-line version: all lines start on $k$ and end on the others or at infinity, and none connect two grounded conductors.
> - ⚑ By-product: $C$ is weakly diagonally dominant with positive diagonal → [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^rem-c4-3-1|Remark: Diagonal dominance and footnote 7]].
> - Zangwill states $C_{kj} < 0$ (strict, and without "$j \ne k$"); strictness fails for screened pairs, e.g. two conductors in separate cavities of a third.

> [!remark] Remark: Diagonal dominance and footnote 7
> Maxwell's inequalities make $C$ weakly diagonally dominant with a positive diagonal, so by Gershgorin's theorem every eigenvalue lies in some disk $\lvert\lambda - C_{kk}\rvert \le \sum_{j\ne k}\lvert C_{kj}\rvert \le C_{kk}$, which does not reach below $0$: $C$ is positive *semi*definite from the inequalities alone. Zangwill's footnote 7 says that the inequalities "guarantee that this inverse exists"; that needs a little more (a strictly dominant row reached from every conductor through nonzero couplings, which the conductors touching the unbounded region provide). The energy argument of [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]] gives invertibility directly.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.4, footnote 7*

## The capacitor and the meaning of "capacitance"

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.7: The Capacitor from the Matrix
> For two conductors in the capacitor state $Q_1 = -Q_2 = Q$ ([[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^def-c4-2-3|Def. §C4.2.3]]),
>
> $$
> C = \frac{1}{P_{11} + P_{22} - 2P_{12}} = \frac{C_{11}C_{22} - C_{12}^2}{C_{11} + C_{22} + 2C_{12}} .
> $$
>
> If $C_{12} \ll C_{11}, C_{22}$, then $1/C \approx 1/C_{11} + 1/C_{22}$: the two coefficients combine **in series**.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.4, eqs. (5.51)–(5.55) · Likharev §2.2, eq. (2.26)*

^thm-c4-3-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Potentials.** $V_1 = P_{11}Q_1 + P_{12}Q_2 = (P_{11} - P_{12})Q$ and $V_2 = P_{21}Q_1 + P_{22}Q_2 = (P_{12} - P_{22})Q$, using $P_{21} = P_{12}$ (Zangwill eq. (5.52)).
>
> **2. Voltage.** $V_1 - V_2 = (P_{11} + P_{22} - 2P_{12})Q$, so $C = Q/(V_1 - V_2) = 1/(P_{11} + P_{22} - 2P_{12})$.
>
> **3. Inverse of a $2\times2$ matrix.** With $D = C_{11}C_{22} - C_{12}^2 > 0$ (positive definiteness, [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]]), $P = \frac1D\begin{pmatrix}C_{22} & -C_{12}\\ -C_{12} & C_{11}\end{pmatrix}$ (eq. (5.54)). So $P_{11} + P_{22} - 2P_{12} = (C_{22} + C_{11} + 2C_{12})/D$, and inverting gives the second form.
>
> **4. Weak coupling.** For $C_{12} \to 0$, $C \to C_{11}C_{22}/(C_{11} + C_{22})$, i.e. $1/C = 1/C_{11} + 1/C_{22}$, the series rule ([[§A4.2 Capacitors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]]). Zangwill writes that this "identifies the two-conductor capacitance as the two self-capacitances added in parallel"; the formula is the series combination.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - For two distant spheres of radius $R$ at distance $d$, $P_{11} = P_{22} \approx 1/4\pi\varepsilon_0R$ and $P_{12} \approx 1/4\pi\varepsilon_0d$ (Likharev eqs. (2.29)–(2.31)), so $C \approx 4\pi\varepsilon_0/(2/R - 2/d) \approx 2\pi\varepsilon_0R$: the level-A result [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^ex-a4-1-2|Example §A4.1.2]], which hardly depends on $d$. The coupling is carried by $P_{12}$, not by $C$.

^der-c4-3-7

*Uses:* [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]], [[§A4.2 Capacitors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Capacitance needs a qualifier
> For one conductor among others there are several "capacitances", all different (Likharev §2.2). For conductor 1 of a pair:
> - **self-capacitance** of conductor 1 alone, the second conductor absent ([[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^def-c4-2-1|Def. §C4.2.1]]);
> - **partial capacitance** $1/P_{11}$: conductor 2 present, isolated and neutral (Likharev eq. (2.32));
> - **effective capacitance** $C_{11} = P_{22}/(P_{11}P_{22} - P_{12}^2)$: conductor 2 grounded (Likharev eq. (2.33)), the diagonal entry of the capacitance matrix;
> - **mutual capacitance** $C$ of the pair as a capacitor ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-7|Theorem §C4.3.7]]).
>
> For two equal spheres far apart, to lowest orders in $R/d$: self and partial $4\pi\varepsilon_0R$, effective $4\pi\varepsilon_0R/(1 - R^2/d^2)$, mutual $\approx 2\pi\varepsilon_0R$. Zangwill's warning is the third item: $C_{kk}$ is not the self-capacitance (Zangwill §5.4.3), and for many conductors even a mutual capacitance depends on what the others are doing.
>
> *Source: Likharev §2.2, eqs. (2.29)–(2.34) · Zangwill §5.4.3 (the effective value for two spheres computed here)*

^cau-c4-3-1

> [!remark]- Remark: The triode as a three-conductor problem
> In a vacuum triode the cathode charge is $Q_C = C_{CC}V_C + C_{CG}V_G + C_{CA}V_A$ ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]]). The field just outside the cathode is $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$, so it, and with it the electron current, is modulated by the grid voltage through $C_{CG}$, whatever the cathode–anode voltage. Where the grid sits physically does not enter the argument (Zangwill, after Marcus 1939).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.4.3, box "The Triode"*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C4.3.1: A Ball inside a Shell
> A conducting ball of radius $a$ (conductor 1) sits at the centre of a thin conducting spherical shell of radius $b > a$ (conductor 2). Find the $2\times2$ capacitance matrix.
>
> *Field and potentials for given charges.* Let the ball carry $Q_1$ and the shell $Q_2$. By spherical symmetry and Gauss's law, $E = Q_1/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$ for $a < r < b$ and $E = (Q_1 + Q_2)/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$ for $r > b$; the shell's inner face carries $-Q_1$ (no field in the metal) and its outer face $Q_1 + Q_2$. Integrating in from infinity:
>
> $$
> V_2 = \frac{Q_1 + Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0b}, \qquad V_1 = V_2 + \frac{Q_1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\frac1a - \frac1b\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Coefficients of potential.* Reading off $V_i = \sum_jP_{ij}Q_j$: $P_{11} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a}$, $P_{12} = P_{21} = P_{22} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0b}$.
>
> *Capacitance matrix.* Solve for the charges: from the second equation $Q_1 = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0ab}{b - a}(V_1 - V_2)$; from the first $Q_2 = 4\pi\varepsilon_0bV_2 - Q_1$. With $\kappa = 4\pi\varepsilon_0ab/(b - a)$:
>
> $$
> C = \begin{pmatrix}\kappa & -\kappa\\ -\kappa & \kappa + 4\pi\varepsilon_0b\end{pmatrix} = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0}{b - a}\begin{pmatrix}ab & -ab\\ -ab & b^2\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> *Checks.* Inverse: $\det P = \frac{1}{(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2}\bigl(\frac{1}{ab} - \frac{1}{b^2}\bigr) = \frac{b - a}{(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2ab^2}$, and $P^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det P}\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\begin{pmatrix}1/b & -1/b\\ -1/b & 1/a\end{pmatrix}$ reproduces $C$. Symmetric ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]]); $\det C = \frac{(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2ab^2}{b - a} > 0$ and $C_{11} > 0$, positive definite ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]]); $C_{12} < 0$; row sums $0$ for the ball (it touches only the cavity) and $4\pi\varepsilon_0b > 0$ for the shell ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]]). See the figure after [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]].
>
> *Zangwill's two capacitances.* (a) Shell grounded, ball charged: $C = Q_1/V_1 = C_{11} = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0ab}{b - a}$, the spherical capacitor ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]]). (b) Ball grounded, shell charged: $C = Q_2/V_2 = C_{22} = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0b^2}{b - a}$, larger than the shell's self-capacitance $4\pi\varepsilon_0b$ by $\kappa$: the grounded ball draws up $-\kappa V_2$ and adds a capacitor in parallel with the shell's exterior. The mutual capacitance ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-7|Theorem §C4.3.7]]) is $\frac{(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2ab^2/(b - a)}{(4\pi\varepsilon_0/(b - a))(ab + b^2 - 2ab)} = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0ab}{b - a}$, the same as (a), because in state (a) the shell's outer face is uncharged anyway.
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 9, Problem 1 (Zangwill Problem 5.2, asked for the full matrix), solved here*

> [!example] Example §C4.3.2: A Point Charge between Grounded Plates
> Infinite grounded plates at $z = 0$ (L) and $z = d$ (R); a point charge $q$ at height $z_0$ between them. How much charge does each plate draw up from ground?
>
> Apply [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]] (for large finite plates, then let them grow). *Unprimed:* $V_L = V_R = 0$, unknown $Q_L$, $Q_R$, extra charge $q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)$. *Primed* (a comparison state with no extra charge): $V'_L = 0$, $V'_R = V$; between infinite plates $V'$ is linear, $V'(z) = Vz/d$ (Zangwill Figure 5.9). The theorem reads
>
> $$
> V_LQ'_L + V_RQ'_R + 0 = V'_LQ_L + V'_RQ_R + qV'(z_0) \;\Longrightarrow\; 0 = VQ_R + q\,\frac{z_0}{d}V ,
> $$
>
> so $Q_R = -qz_0/d$, and the comparison state with the roles swapped gives $Q_L = -q(1 - z_0/d)$. The plate farther from the charge draws less, in proportion to the distance from the *other* plate; together they draw $-q$, so every field line from $q$ ends on a plate, and none escapes sideways to infinity. The infinite image series gives the same result with much more work ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-10|Theorem §C7.1.10]]; by Green functions, [[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^ex-c7-2-3|Example §C7.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill Example 5.3, Figure 5.9*

> [!example] Example §C4.3.3: Charge Induced on a Grounded Sphere by a Dipole
> A point dipole $\vec p$ sits at $\vec r_0$ outside a grounded conducting sphere of radius $R$ centred at the origin. Find the charge drawn up from ground.
>
> *Unprimed state.* Sphere at $V = 0$ with unknown charge $Q$; extra charge $\rho_{\text{ex}} = -\vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)$, the point dipole as a distribution ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^mod-c3-1-2|Model §C3.1.2]]).
>
> *Comparison state* (zero volume charge, as the problem suggests): the sphere alone at potential $V'$, so $Q' = 4\pi\varepsilon_0RV'$ and $V'(\vec r) = V'R/r$ outside, smooth near $\vec r_0$.
>
> *Reciprocity* ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]], step 2 for the dipole pairing):
>
> $$
> 0\cdot Q' + 0 = V'Q + \int\rho_{\text{ex}}V'\,d^3r = V'Q + \vec p\cdot\nabla\Bigl(\frac{V'R}{r}\Bigr)\Big\rvert_{\vec r_0} = V'Q - V'R\,\frac{\vec p\cdot\vec r_0}{r_0^3} .
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> Q = R\,\frac{\vec p\cdot\vec r_0}{r_0^3} = \frac{Rp\cos\gamma}{r_0^2} ,
> $$
>
> with $\gamma$ the angle between $\vec p$ and $\vec r_0$. A dipole pointing away from the sphere ($\cos\gamma > 0$) has its negative end nearer, and the sphere draws up positive charge; a dipole perpendicular to $\vec r_0$ draws none. *Check by images:* a charge $q$ at $\vec r$ induces $-qR/\lvert\vec r\rvert$ on a grounded sphere ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]); for $+q$ at $\vec r_0 + \vec s/2$ and $-q$ at $\vec r_0 - \vec s/2$, the induced total is $-qR\bigl[\lvert\vec r_0 + \vec s/2\rvert^{-1} - \lvert\vec r_0 - \vec s/2\rvert^{-1}\bigr] \to -R\,\vec p\cdot\nabla(1/r)\rvert_{\vec r_0} = R\,\vec p\cdot\vec r_0/r_0^3$ as $s \to 0$ with $q\vec s = \vec p$ (the dipole's full image: [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-9|Theorem §C7.1.9]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 9, Problem 2 (Zangwill Problem 5.10), solved here*

> [!example] Example §C4.3.4: The Conducting Disk, on Its Axis and as an Inductor of Charge
> A thin conducting disk of radius $R$ in the plane $z = 0$, centred on the $z$ axis. (a) Held at potential $V$, find the potential on the axis. (b) Grounded, with a point charge $q_0$ on the axis at $z = d$, find the charge drawn up from ground.
>
> *(a)* At $V$ the disk holds $Q = 8\varepsilon_0RV$ ([[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-2|Theorem §C4.2.2]]) with $\sigma(\rho) = Q/4\pi R\sqrt{R^2 - \rho^2}$ on each face ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]]). A ring at radius $\rho$ is at distance $\sqrt{\rho^2 + z^2}$ from the axis point, so
>
> $$
> V(z) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^R\frac{2\sigma(\rho)\,2\pi\rho\,d\rho}{\sqrt{\rho^2 + z^2}} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R}\int_0^R\frac{\rho\,d\rho}{\sqrt{R^2 - \rho^2}\sqrt{\rho^2 + z^2}} .
> $$
>
> Substitute $u = \rho^2$ ($\rho\,d\rho = du/2$), then $u + z^2 = (R^2 + z^2)\sin^2\theta$: $R^2 - u = (R^2 + z^2)\cos^2\theta$, $du = 2(R^2 + z^2)\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta$, so $du/\sqrt{(R^2 - u)(u + z^2)} = 2\,d\theta$, with $\theta$ from $\theta_0 = \arcsin(\lvert z\rvert/\sqrt{R^2 + z^2})$ to $\pi/2$. The integral is $\frac12\cdot2(\pi/2 - \theta_0) = \arctan(R/\lvert z\rvert)$, and with $Q = 8\varepsilon_0RV$:
>
> $$
> V(z) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R}\arctan\frac{R}{\lvert z\rvert} = \frac{2V}{\pi}\arctan\frac{R}{\lvert z\rvert} .
> $$
>
> Checks: $V(0) = V$; for $\lvert z\rvert \gg R$, $V \approx 2VR/\pi\lvert z\rvert = Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0\lvert z\rvert$.
>
> *(b)* Reciprocity ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]]) with the grounded disk plus $q_0$ (unknown induced $Q_{\text{ind}}$) as the unprimed state and part (a) as the comparison state: $0\cdot Q' + 0 = V\,Q_{\text{ind}} + q_0V(d)$, so
>
> $$
> Q_{\text{ind}} = -\frac{q_0V(d)}{V} = -\frac{2q_0}{\pi}\arctan\frac Rd .
> $$
>
> As $d \to 0$ the disk draws $-q_0$; far away, $-q_0\cdot\frac{8\varepsilon_0R}{4\pi\varepsilon_0d}$, the charge a sphere of capacitance $8\varepsilon_0R$ would draw from the potential $q_0/4\pi\varepsilon_0d$ of the charge. The general rule: *the charge induced on a grounded conductor by a unit charge at $\vec r$ is minus the potential at $\vec r$ of that conductor held at unit potential*, $Q_{\text{ind}} = -q_0\tilde V(\vec r)$.
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 9, Problem 3 (Zangwill Problem 5.13), solved here*

> [!example]- ★ Example §C4.3.5: A Chain of Weakly Coupled Conductors
> $N + 1$ identical conductors $m = 0, \dots, N$ in a line have $C_{ii} = C_0$, $C_{i,i\pm1} = -C$, all others zero, with $C \ll C_0$ (Zangwill Example 5.4). Conductor 0 carries $Q$, the others are neutral. Then $Q = C_0V_0 - CV_1$, $0 = C_0V_m - C(V_{m+1} + V_{m-1})$ for $0 < m < N$, and $0 = C_0V_N - CV_{N-1}$. The last gives $V_N = (C/C_0)V_{N-1}$; inserted in the equation for $m = N - 1$, $(C_0/C - C/C_0)V_{N-1} = V_{N-2}$, so $V_{N-1} \approx (C/C_0)V_{N-2}$ to leading order, and so on down the chain: $V_m \approx (C/C_0)V_{m-1}$. With $Q \approx C_0V_0$,
>
> $$
> V_m \approx \frac{Q}{C_0}\Bigl(\frac{C}{C_0}\Bigr)^m = \frac{Q}{C_0}\,e^{-m\ln(C_0/C)} :
> $$
>
> the potential of a single charge decays exponentially along the chain, over $1/\ln(C_0/C)$ sites, a discrete screening length. (The chain is a simplified single-electron tunnel-junction array, Likharev et al. 1989.)
>
> *Source: Zangwill Example 5.4*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $C$ is the Gram matrix of the elementary fields in the energy inner product ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]]); positive definiteness is [[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-62|LADR Def. 7.62]] and the square root and diagonalization of $C$ are [[§24 Positive Operators#^ladr-7-38|LADR Thm. 7.38]]: in the eigenbasis of $C$ the conductors decouple into independent "normal capacitances".
> - Reciprocity in electrostatics is the symmetry of the Green function, $G(\vec r, \vec r\,') = G(\vec r\,', \vec r)$ ([[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]]); the rule $Q_{\text{ind}} = -q_0\tilde V(\vec r)$ of Example §C4.3.4 is the Green-function statement that the induced charge is a normal derivative of $G$. The magnetic counterpart is the symmetry of mutual inductance, $M_{12} = M_{21}$ ([[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-1|Theorem §B8.3.1]]).
> - Maxwell's inequalities are those of a weighted graph Laplacian plus a "leak to ground": $C$ has the sign pattern of a discretized $-\nabla^2$ with a positive mass term, which is why the chain of Example §C4.3.5 screens exponentially like the Yukawa potential of [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^rem-c4-1-6|§C4.1, ★ Remark: Real conductors and the screening length]].
