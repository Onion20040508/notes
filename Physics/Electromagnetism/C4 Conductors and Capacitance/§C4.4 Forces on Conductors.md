What force does the field exert on a conductor, and how is it computed when charges or potentials, rather than fields, are known? Level B gave the outward pressure $\sigma^2/2\varepsilon_0$ on a conductor's surface and the forces on a capacitor from its stored energy, at fixed charge or fixed voltage ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-3|EM Theorem §B2.4.3]], [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-4|EM Theorem §B2.4.4]]). Level C does both for $N$ conductors: the force as a surface integral of the stress tensor ([[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor|§C2.4]]), and the force as a derivative of an energy whose natural variables are the ones held fixed: $U_E(Q, R)$ at fixed charges, its Legendre transform $\hat U_E(V, R)$ at fixed potentials, built on the matrices of [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix|§C4.3]]. The two formulas give the same force, and the battery bookkeeping explains the sign difference that trips up the naive calculation.

**Notation.** Conductor $k$ has centre-of-mass position $\vec R_k$, charge $Q_k$, potential $V_k$ (Zangwill $\varphi_k$; [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: The potential is V]]); $C$ and $P = C^{-1}$ as in [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^def-c4-3-1|Def. §C4.3.1]]; a subscript on a partial derivative lists what is held fixed, with $Q'$ meaning "all charges except the one differentiated" (Zangwill's convention).

## The force from the surface charge

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.4.1: Force on a Conductor
> The electric force on a conductor with surface $S$ is (Zangwill eq. (5.65))
>
> $$
> \vec F = \frac12\oint_S\sigma\vec E_{\text{out}}\,dS = \frac{1}{2\varepsilon_0}\oint_S\sigma^2\,\hat n\,dS = \oint_S\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E_{\text{out}}^2\,\hat n\,dS ,
> $$
>
> an outward pressure $\sigma^2/2\varepsilon_0$ on every surface element. It equals the flux $\oint\hat n\cdot\mathsf T\,dS$ of the Maxwell stress tensor through any closed surface in vacuum that encloses this conductor and no other charge, and it vanishes for an isolated conductor.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.6, eq. (5.65); §3.4.3, eq. (3.52); §3.7, eqs. (3.93)–(3.97)*

^thm-c4-4-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Force per area.** A surface charge feels $\vec f = \sigma\vec E_{\text{avg}} = \tfrac12\sigma(\vec E_{\text{in}} + \vec E_{\text{out}})$, the average of the fields on its two sides, since a surface element exerts no force on itself (Zangwill eq. (3.52); [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-2|EM Theorem §B2.4.2]]; level C [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^thm-c2-4-3|Theorem §C2.4.3]]).
>
> **2. Conductor.** $\vec E_{\text{in}} = \vec 0$ and $\vec E_{\text{out}} = \hat n\,\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^rem-c4-1-3|§C4.1, Remark: The field at a conductor's surface]]), so $\vec f = \tfrac12\sigma\vec E_{\text{out}} = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\hat n = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E_{\text{out}}^2\hat n$ ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-3|EM Theorem §B2.4.3]]). Integrating over $S$ gives the three forms.
>
> **3. Stress tensor on $S$.** With $T_{ij} = \varepsilon_0(E_iE_j - \tfrac12\delta_{ij}E^2)$, the force on the charge inside a closed surface is $\vec F = \oint dS\,\varepsilon_0\bigl[(\hat n\cdot\vec E)\vec E - \tfrac12E^2\hat n\bigr]$ (Zangwill eq. (3.97); [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]]; SI at level B [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^def-b9-2-1|EM Def. §B9.2.1]]). On a surface just outside the conductor $\vec E = E\hat n$, so $(\hat n\cdot\vec E)\vec E = E^2\hat n$ and the bracket is $E^2\hat n - \tfrac12E^2\hat n = \tfrac12E^2\hat n$: the same integrand as step 2.
>
> **4. Any enclosing surface.** In vacuum $\partial_iT_{ij} = \rho E_j = 0$ (Zangwill eq. (3.92)). For two closed surfaces that both enclose the conductor and no other charge, the divergence theorem on the region between them gives equal fluxes.
>
> **5. Isolated conductor.** Take the surface to be a sphere of radius $R \to \infty$. There $E = O(R^{-2})$, so $T_{ij} = O(R^{-4})$, on an area $4\pi R^2$: the flux is $O(R^{-2}) \to 0$. Hence $\vec F = \vec 0$: the outward pressures on an isolated conductor cancel, and it cannot push itself (no self-force, [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-4|Theorem §C2.1.4]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The pressure always points out of the metal, whatever the sign of $\sigma$: like charges on each surface element repel, and the conductor is pulled into the field region. Non-electric cohesive forces hold the metal together (Zangwill).
> - Net forces on a conductor come only from other charges and conductors; the surface integral is the way to compute them when the field is known (Zangwill computes it for a charge distribution near a conductor in ch. 8 → [[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation|§C7.2]]). When only charges or potentials are known, use the energy (Theorems §C4.4.2–§C4.4.3).

> [!example] Example §C4.4.1: The Force between Two Halves of a Charged Shell
> (a) A thin spherical metal shell of radius $R$ is at potential $V$ (charge $Q = 4\pi\varepsilon_0RV$). Cut it in half and pull the halves infinitesimally apart. With what force do they repel? (b) The same for a spherical capacitor, concentric shells of radii $a < b$ with charges $\pm Q$, cut in half through the centre.
>
> *(a)* The charge is uniform, $\sigma = Q/4\pi R^2$, and by [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-1|Theorem §C4.4.1]] each element of the upper hemisphere feels the outward pressure $\sigma^2/2\varepsilon_0$ along $\hat r$. By symmetry the net force on the upper half is along $z$; the $z$ component of $\hat r\,dS$ is $\cos\theta\,dS$, and $\int_{\text{upper}}\cos\theta\,dS = \pi R^2$ (the projection of the hemisphere onto the equatorial plane). Hence
>
> $$
> F_z = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\,\pi R^2 = \frac{Q^2}{16\pi^2R^4}\cdot\frac{\pi R^2}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{Q^2}{32\pi\varepsilon_0R^2} = \frac{\pi\varepsilon_0}{2}V^2 .
> $$
>
> The infinitesimal cut changes the charge distribution only near the cut, an area that vanishes with the gap. The force at fixed $V$ does not depend on $R$.
>
> *(b)* The field lives only between the shells. The inner shell's charge $+Q$ sits on its outer face, $\sigma_a = Q/4\pi a^2$, and the pressure pushes its upper half up with $Q^2/32\pi\varepsilon_0a^2$, as in (a). The outer shell's charge $-Q$ sits on its inner face, $\sigma_b = -Q/4\pi b^2$; the pressure $\sigma_b^2/2\varepsilon_0$ points out of the metal into the gap, i.e. towards the centre, and pulls its upper half *down* with $Q^2/32\pi\varepsilon_0b^2$. The upper half of the capacitor (both upper hemispheres) feels
>
> $$
> F_z = \frac{Q^2}{32\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\frac{1}{a^2} - \frac{1}{b^2}\Bigr) > 0 :
> $$
>
> the halves repel, because the inner hemisphere is pushed harder than the outer one is pulled.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 5.23, solved here*

## Forces at fixed charges

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.4.2: Forces at Fixed Charges
> For isolated conductors the electrostatic energy is a function $U_E(Q_1, \dots, Q_N, \vec R_1, \dots, \vec R_N)$ with
>
> $$
> dU_E = \sum_kV_k\,dQ_k - \sum_k\vec F_k\cdot d\vec R_k ,
> $$
>
> so $V_k = (\partial U_E/\partial Q_k)_{Q', R}$ and
>
> $$
> \vec F_k = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial U_E}{\partial\vec R_k}\Bigr)_{Q, R'} = -\frac12\sum_{i,j}Q_iQ_j\,\frac{\partial P_{ij}}{\partial\vec R_k} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.6.1, eqs. (5.66)–(5.70)*

^thm-c4-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Natural variables.** For isolated conductors of fixed shapes, the charges and positions fix the state: the charges determine the potentials, $V_i = \sum_jP_{ij}Q_j$ ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]]), and with them the field. So $U_E = U_E(Q, R)$ (Zangwill eq. (5.66)), and its total differential is $dU_E = \sum_k(\partial U_E/\partial Q_k)_{Q', R}\,dQ_k + \sum_k(\partial U_E/\partial\vec R_k)_{Q, R'}\cdot d\vec R_k$ (eq. (5.68)).
>
> **2. Charging.** Bringing a charge $dQ_k$ from infinity (potential $0$) to conductor $k$ (potential $V_k$) at fixed positions costs the work $V_k\,dQ_k$, by the definition of the potential as work per charge ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-1|EM Theorem §B2.3.1]]); it is stored as field energy.
>
> **3. Moving.** Displacing conductor $k$ rigidly and slowly by $d\vec R_k$ at fixed charges, the electric force does work $\vec F_k\cdot d\vec R_k$ on it. The conductors form a closed system (no charge enters or leaves), so this work comes out of the field energy: $dU_E = -\vec F_k\cdot d\vec R_k$. The minus sign makes the force push towards lower energy. Steps 2 and 3 together give Zangwill's eq. (5.67).
>
> **4. Compare.** Matching the coefficients of the independent differentials $dQ_k$ and $d\vec R_k$ in steps 1 and 2–3 gives $V_k = (\partial U_E/\partial Q_k)_{Q', R}$ and $\vec F_k = -(\partial U_E/\partial\vec R_k)_{Q, R'}$ (eq. (5.69)).
>
> **5. Check with the matrix form.** With $U_E = \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}Q_iP_{ij}Q_j$ ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]]), differentiating with respect to $Q_k$ hits each factor once: $\partial U_E/\partial Q_k = \tfrac12\sum_jP_{kj}Q_j + \tfrac12\sum_iQ_iP_{ik} = \sum_jP_{kj}Q_j = V_k$, using $P_{ik} = P_{ki}$. ⚑ By-product: read backwards, $V_k = \partial U_E/\partial Q_k$ forces $\partial V_k/\partial Q_j = \partial^2U_E/\partial Q_j\partial Q_k = \partial V_j/\partial Q_k$, i.e. $P_{kj} = P_{jk}$: the symmetry of the coefficients of potential is a Maxwell relation, like those of thermodynamics ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-1|TH Theorem §B3.2.1]]), a third proof besides reciprocity and the Gram form ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]], [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]]); it presupposes that an energy function exists.
>
> **6. Force formula.** At fixed charges only $P(R)$ depends on $\vec R_k$, so $\vec F_k = -\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}Q_iQ_j\,\partial P_{ij}/\partial\vec R_k$ (eq. (5.70)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - $Q_k$ and $V_k$ are conjugate variables, as volume and pressure are in thermodynamics ($dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$, Zangwill): $U_E(Q, R)$ is to electrostatics what $U(S, V)$ is to a closed system ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^def-b3-1-1|TH Def. §B3.1.1]]).
> - The multiplier of Thomson's theorem was $\partial U_E/\partial Q_k$ ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^der-c4-1-3|§C4.1, Derivation of Theorem §C4.1.3]], step 5); here it is identified with the potential.

## Forces at fixed potentials

> [!definition] Definition §C4.4.1: The Energy at Fixed Potentials
> The Legendre transform of $U_E$ with respect to the charges is (Zangwill eq. (5.71))
>
> $$
> \hat U_E = U_E - \sum_{i=1}^NQ_iV_i ,
> $$
>
> regarded as a function of the potentials and positions, $\hat U_E(V_1, \dots, V_N, \vec R_1, \dots, \vec R_N)$. With $U_E = \tfrac12\sum Q_iV_i$, numerically $\hat U_E = -\tfrac12\sum_iQ_iV_i = -U_E$ (eq. (5.75)). The sign convention is that of thermodynamics, $F = U - TS$ ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|TH Def. §B1.2.2]]); mechanics' $H = p\dot q - L$ has the opposite overall sign ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-1|CM Def. §B7.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.6.2, eqs. (5.71), (5.75), footnote 10*

^def-c4-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.4.3: Forces at Fixed Potentials
> $$
> d\hat U_E = -\sum_iQ_i\,dV_i - \sum_i\vec F_i\cdot d\vec R_i ,
> $$
>
> so $Q_i = -(\partial\hat U_E/\partial V_i)_{V', R}$ and
>
> $$
> \vec F_k = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial\hat U_E}{\partial\vec R_k}\Bigr)_{V, R'} = +\frac12\sum_{i,j}V_iV_j\,\frac{\partial C_{ij}}{\partial\vec R_k} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.6.2, eqs. (5.72)–(5.76)*

^thm-c4-4-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Differential.** From [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^def-c4-4-1|Def. §C4.4.1]], $d\hat U_E = dU_E - \sum_i(V_i\,dQ_i + Q_i\,dV_i)$ (product rule). Insert $dU_E$ from [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-2|Theorem §C4.4.2]]: the terms $\sum V_i\,dQ_i$ cancel, leaving $d\hat U_E = -\sum_iQ_i\,dV_i - \sum_i\vec F_i\cdot d\vec R_i$ (eq. (5.72)). So the natural variables of $\hat U_E$ are the potentials and positions (eq. (5.73)).
>
> **2. Partial derivatives.** Matching coefficients gives $Q_i = -(\partial\hat U_E/\partial V_i)_{V', R}$ and $\vec F_i = -(\partial\hat U_E/\partial\vec R_i)_{V, R'}$ (eq. (5.74)).
>
> **3. As a function of $V$.** $\hat U_E = -U_E = -\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}V_iC_{ij}V_j$ ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]]). *Check:* $-\partial\hat U_E/\partial V_i = \tfrac12\sum_jC_{ij}V_j + \tfrac12\sum_jV_jC_{ji} = \sum_jC_{ij}V_j = Q_i$, using $C_{ji} = C_{ij}$.
>
> **4. Force.** At fixed potentials only $C(R)$ depends on $\vec R_k$: $\vec F_k = -\partial\hat U_E/\partial\vec R_k = +\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}V_iV_j\,\partial C_{ij}/\partial\vec R_k$ (eq. (5.76)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - At fixed potentials the conductors are not a closed system (batteries or ground exchange charge with them), and the energy to differentiate is $\hat U_E$, not $U_E$; numerically $\hat U_E = -U_E$, which is the whole source of the sign difference ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-5|Theorem §C4.4.5]]).

^der-c4-4-3

*Uses:* [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^def-c4-4-1|Def. §C4.4.1]], [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-2|Theorem §C4.4.2]], [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.4.4: The Two Force Formulas Agree
> In any state $\mathbf Q = C\mathbf V$,
>
> $$
> -\frac12\sum_{i,j}Q_iQ_j\,\frac{\partial P_{ij}}{\partial\vec R_k} = \frac12\sum_{i,j}V_iV_j\,\frac{\partial C_{ij}}{\partial\vec R_k} :
> $$
>
> the force on a conductor is a property of the instantaneous configuration, not of what is imagined held fixed. This generalizes the capacitor case $F = \tfrac12(V_1 - V_2)^2\,\partial C/\partial x$ ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-4|EM Theorem §B2.4.4]]) to $N$ conductors.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.6.3, eqs. (5.83)–(5.84); Problem 5.25*

^thm-c4-4-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Derivative of an inverse.** Differentiate $PC = \mathbb 1$ with respect to one component of $\vec R_k$ (written $\partial$): $(\partial P)C + P(\partial C) = 0$. Multiply on the right by $P = C^{-1}$: $\partial P = -P(\partial C)P$.
>
> **2. Insert.** $-\tfrac12\mathbf Q^{\mathsf T}(\partial P)\mathbf Q = \tfrac12\mathbf Q^{\mathsf T}P(\partial C)P\mathbf Q = \tfrac12(P\mathbf Q)^{\mathsf T}(\partial C)(P\mathbf Q)$, using $P^{\mathsf T} = P$ ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]]). With $P\mathbf Q = \mathbf V$ this is $\tfrac12\mathbf V^{\mathsf T}(\partial C)\mathbf V$.
>
> **3. The capacitor.** For two conductors in the capacitor state, $U_E = Q^2/2C$ gives at fixed charge $\vec F_1 = -\frac{Q^2}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec R_1}\frac1C = \frac{Q^2}{2C^2}\frac{\partial C}{\partial\vec R_1} = \frac{U_E}{C}\frac{\partial C}{\partial\vec R_1}$ (Zangwill eq. (5.83)), and at fixed voltage $\hat U_E = -\tfrac12C(V_1 - V_2)^2$ gives $\vec F_1 = \tfrac12(V_1 - V_2)^2\,\partial C/\partial\vec R_1 = \frac{U_E}{C}\frac{\partial C}{\partial\vec R_1}$ (eq. (5.84)), the same.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The displacements are virtual: in reality nothing moves, and the force is whatever the field exerts now (Zangwill). Holding $Q$ or $V$ fixed is a choice of bookkeeping, and both bookkeepings must agree; the algebraic reason is $\partial(C^{-1}) = -C^{-1}(\partial C)C^{-1}$.

^der-c4-4-4

*Uses:* [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]], [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-2|Theorem §C4.4.2]], [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-3|Theorem §C4.4.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.4.5: Energy Bookkeeping at Fixed Potentials
> When a conductor moves by $\delta\vec R$ while batteries hold all potentials fixed and the charges change by $\Delta Q_k$, the batteries deliver $W_{\text{batt}} = \sum_kV_k\,\Delta Q_k$, of which exactly half raises the field energy and half is the work done by the electric force:
>
> $$
> \Delta U_E = \tfrac12\sum_kV_k\,\Delta Q_k, \qquad \vec F\cdot\delta\vec R = \tfrac12\sum_kV_k\,\Delta Q_k = \Delta U_E = -\Delta\hat U_E .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.6.3, eqs. (5.77)–(5.82), Figure 5.14*

^thm-c4-4-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Closed system.** Conductors, field and batteries together form an isolated system. Energy conservation for the displacement reads $\vec F\cdot\delta\vec R + \Delta U_E + \Delta E_{\text{batt}} = 0$ (Zangwill eq. (5.77)), where $\vec F\cdot\delta\vec R$ is the work done by the electric force (energy leaving as mechanical work), $\Delta U_E$ the change of the field energy and $\Delta E_{\text{batt}}$ that of the batteries.
>
> **2. Field energy.** $U_E = \tfrac12\sum_kQ_kV_k$ ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]]) with the $V_k$ fixed: $\Delta U_E = \tfrac12\sum_kV_k(Q_k - Q_k^{(0)})$ (eq. (5.79)).
>
> **3. Batteries.** A battery holding conductor $k$ at $V_k$ does work $V_k\,dQ_k$ to push $dQ_k$ onto it ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^der-c4-4-2|Derivation of Theorem §C4.4.2]], step 2), and its own energy falls by as much: $\Delta E_{\text{batt}} = -\sum_k\int_{Q_k^{(0)}}^{Q_k}V_k\,dQ_k = -\sum_kV_k(Q_k - Q_k^{(0)})$ (eq. (5.80)).
>
> **4. Solve.** $\vec F\cdot\delta\vec R = -\Delta U_E - \Delta E_{\text{batt}} = -\tfrac12\sum_kV_k\Delta Q_k + \sum_kV_k\Delta Q_k = \tfrac12\sum_kV_k\Delta Q_k = \Delta U_E$.
>
> **5. Link to $\hat U_E$.** By [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-3|Theorem §C4.4.3]] at fixed $V$, $\vec F\cdot\delta\vec R = -\Delta\hat U_E$; hence $\Delta\hat U_E = -\Delta U_E = \Delta U_E + \Delta E_{\text{batt}}$ (Zangwill eqs. (5.78), (5.81)): $\hat U_E$ is the energy of conductors *plus batteries*, up to a constant, and integrating gives $\hat U_E = -\tfrac12\sum_kV_kQ_k$ (eq. (5.82)), in agreement with Def. §C4.4.1.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ By-product: at fixed potentials the force moves the system *up* in field energy, $\vec F\cdot\delta\vec R = +\Delta U_E$, the opposite of the closed case ($\vec F\cdot\delta\vec R = -\Delta U_E$); the batteries pay twice: once for the work, once for the stored energy.
> - Zangwill's text uses $\hat U_E$ and $\tilde U_E$ for the same quantity in eqs. (5.78), (5.81)–(5.82) and writes $U_B$ for $U_E$ in eq. (5.79); typographical only.

> [!remark] Remark: The naive sign error
> Differentiating $U_E = \tfrac12\mathbf V^{\mathsf T}C\mathbf V$ at fixed potentials, $-\partial U_E/\partial\vec R_k = -\tfrac12\mathbf V^{\mathsf T}(\partial C/\partial\vec R_k)\mathbf V$, gives the *negative* of the correct force (Zangwill §5.6.3): $U_E$ is not a natural function of the potentials, and a fixed-potential system is not closed. Level A and B met the same trap for a capacitor ([[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^cau-a4-3-1|EM §A4.3, Caution: Which formula when something changes]]; [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^der-b2-4-4|EM §B2.4, Derivation of Theorem §B2.4.4]]); Theorem §C4.4.5 is the general bookkeeping.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.6.3*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C4.4.2: A Metal Sheet Drawn into a Capacitor
> A parallel-plate capacitor (gap $d$, width $w$ perpendicular to the page) is held at voltage $V$. A metal sheet of thickness $t < d$, parallel to the plates, is inserted a distance $x$. Neglecting fringe fields in the energy, find the force on the sheet.
>
> *Which energy.* The potentials are fixed, so $F_x = -\partial\hat U_E/\partial x = +\partial U_E/\partial x$ ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-3|Theorem §C4.4.3]], $\hat U_E = -U_E$).
>
> *Fields.* Inside the metal $E = 0$; the voltage $V = \int_0^d\vec E\cdot d\vec\ell$ across the gap is then taken by the two vacuum layers of total thickness $d - t$, so $E = V/(d - t)$ above and below the sheet, and $E = V/d$ where there is no sheet.
>
> *Energy change.* Advancing the sheet from $x$ to $x + \epsilon$ converts a strip of width $\epsilon$ from "no sheet" to "sheet"; nothing changes beyond $x + \epsilon$ or behind $x$ (the fringe region just moves along). With $U_E = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int\lvert\vec E\rvert^2$:
>
> $$
> U_E(x + \epsilon) - U_E(x) = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\Bigl(\frac{V}{d - t}\Bigr)^2w\epsilon(d - t) - \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\Bigl(\frac Vd\Bigr)^2w\epsilon d = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}V^2w\epsilon\Bigl(\frac{1}{d - t} - \frac1d\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Force.* Dividing by $\epsilon$:
>
> $$
> \vec F = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}V^2w\Bigl(\frac{1}{d - t} - \frac1d\Bigr)\hat x ,
> $$
>
> positive: the sheet is drawn in. The pressure picture ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-1|Theorem §C4.4.1]]) gives the same force only if the fringe field at the sheet's inner edge is kept: the uniform-field pressures on the top and bottom faces are vertical and cancel, and the $+x$ force comes from the charge induced by field lines that bend round and end on the sheet's vertical edge. The energy method needs no knowledge of that fringe field, which is its advantage.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Example 5.5, Figure 5.13*

> [!example] Example §C4.4.3: Pulling Apart a Capacitor That Shares Its Charge
> A parallel-plate capacitor $C = \varepsilon_0A/s$ carries $\pm q$; a second capacitor $C'$ carries $\pm q'$. (a) With the connecting switches open, find the energy change when the plate separation grows from $s$ to $s + \delta s$, and the force. (b) The same with the switches closed (the two capacitors in parallel).
>
> *(a)* $C$ is isolated: $U_E = q^2/2C$ at fixed $q$, and $\delta C = -\varepsilon_0A\,\delta s/s^2$, so $\delta U_E = -\frac12\bigl(\frac qC\bigr)^2\delta C = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\varepsilon_0}A\,\delta s$ with $\sigma = q/A$. The energy grows as the plates separate, so the force $F = -\delta U_E/\delta s = -\sigma^2A/2\varepsilon_0$ is attractive, the pressure of [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-1|Theorem §C4.4.1]] times the area.
>
> *(b)* Now $U_E = q^2/2C + q'^2/2C'$, and charge flows between the capacitors, $\delta q' = -\delta q$:
>
> $$
> \delta U_E = -\frac12\Bigl(\frac qC\Bigr)^2\delta C + \frac qC\,\delta q + \frac{q'}{C'}\,\delta q' = -\frac12\Bigl(\frac qC\Bigr)^2\delta C + \Bigl(\frac qC - \frac{q'}{C'}\Bigr)\delta q .
> $$
>
> The bracket vanishes, since both capacitors are at the common voltage $V = q/C = q'/C'$. What remains is the energy change of (a): the same force. *Reservoir limit* $C' \to \infty$: $C$ is at fixed voltage $V$, and the total energy change is $\delta U_E = -\tfrac12V^2\delta C$, which equals $\delta\hat U_E$ of capacitor $C$ ($\hat U_E = -\tfrac12CV^2$, [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^def-c4-4-1|Def. §C4.4.1]]) because the big capacitor plays the battery ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-5|Theorem §C4.4.5]], step 5). Zangwill states that it is $-\delta\hat U_E$; with his definitions the sign is $+$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Example 5.6, Figure 5.15 (the sign in the reservoir limit checked here)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $U_E(Q, R)$ and $\hat U_E(V, R)$ are a Legendre pair, as $U(S, V)$ and $F(T, V)$ in thermodynamics ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^rem-b3-1-3|TH §B3.1, Remark: Each potential is a Legendre transform]]); the battery is the heat bath of the analogy, and Theorem §C4.4.5 is the electrostatic counterpart of the work done by a system in contact with a reservoir. The symmetry $P_{ij} = P_{ji}$ is the corresponding Maxwell relation ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-1|TH Theorem §B3.2.1]]).
> - Forces from a virtual displacement at fixed constraints are the principle of virtual work of analytical mechanics; holding $V$ instead of $Q$ is a change of constraint, paid for by an external system.
> - The same two energies, with $\vec D$ and $\vec E$ in place of charge and potential, govern the forces on dielectric matter ([[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics|§C5.4]]; Zangwill §6.7.5, §6.8).
