Why does the charge on a conductor arrange itself so that the interior is field-free, and what does that arrangement look like? Levels A and B took the ideal conductor as a model whose properties follow from "charges free to move" ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^mod-a2-4-1|Model §A2.4.1]], [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^rem-b2-4-1|§B2.4, Remark: The properties of a conductor]]), and proved that the equilibrium distribution is unique ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]). Level C defines the perfect conductor by the vanishing of the macroscopic field and charge in its interior, writes induction as a self-consistency condition on the surface charge, and derives the field-free interior from an energy principle, Thomson's theorem, using the electrostatic energy of [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy|§C2.3]]. It then computes one surface charge that no symmetry gives away, that of a conducting ellipsoid and disk, which [[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors|§C4.2]] and [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix|§C4.3]] use, and ends with shielding and (★) the screening length of a real metal.

**Notation.** The potential is $V(\vec r)$ (Zangwill writes $\varphi$; [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: The potential is V]]); the volume of a body is $\mathcal V$ with surface $S$ and outward normal $\hat n$; $U_E$ is the electrostatic energy and $U_{\text{self}}$ a self-energy (EM B writes $W$; [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^cau-c2-3-1|§C2.3, Caution: Symbols for energy]]).

## The perfect conductor and electrostatic induction

> [!model] Model §C4.1.1: Perfect Conductor
> A **perfect conductor** occupying the volume $\mathcal V$ is a body in which the macroscopic (Lorentz-averaged) field and charge density vanish at every interior point (Zangwill eq. (5.1)):
>
> $$
> \vec E(\vec r) = \vec 0, \qquad \rho(\vec r) = 0 \qquad (\vec r \in \mathcal V) .
> $$
>
> All excess charge sits on $S$ as a surface charge $\sigma$, i.e. the singular distribution $\rho = \sigma\,\delta_S$, $\int\rho f\,d^3r = \oint_S\sigma f\,dS$.
>
> *Assumptions:* electrostatic equilibrium; macroscopic fields (averaged over many atoms); the screening length of the real material is negligible against every length of the problem (★ Remark below); the body's shape is held fixed by non-electric forces.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.1, eq. (5.1), §5.2*

^mod-c4-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from levels A and B
> - *Layer.* At level A the conductor was a model with "free charges", and "no field inside" a theorem derived from equilibrium ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]]). At level C, following Zangwill, $\vec E = \rho = 0$ is the defining property of the model, and the question becomes *why* nature realizes it: Thomson's theorem ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]) shows that it is the state of least electrostatic energy.
> - *Macroscopic.* Induction in a real metal does not move charge over long distances. The conduction-electron wave functions are distorted slightly at every atom; averaged over a region containing many atoms (the Lorentz average, Zangwill §2.3.2) the perturbed density vanishes inside and leaves tiny excesses and deficits at the surface, which average to $\sigma$ (Zangwill §5.2).
> - *The surface charge is a distribution.* $\sigma\,\delta_S$ is a singular distribution supported on $S$ in the sense of [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-2|QFT Def. §CA.2.2]] and [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-1|QFT Theorem §CA.2.1]]: it has no value at a point, only the pairing $\oint_S\sigma f\,dS$ with smooth $f$. Its potential is the ordinary surface integral $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint_S\sigma(\vec r\,')\,dS'/\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert$, continuous across $S$, while $\hat n\cdot\vec E$ jumps by $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$, the jump that Gauss's law in distributional form, $\varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E = \sigma\,\delta_S$, demands ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]] for the point-charge version). The energy of a surface charge is finite; that of a point charge is not ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-7|Theorem §C2.3.7]]), which is why Thomson's theorem lives in the class of finite-energy densities.

^rem-c4-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §C4.1.1: Electrostatic Induction; Self-Field; Induced Dipole Moment
> Let a conductor be placed in an external field $\vec E_{\text{ext}} = -\nabla V_{\text{ext}}$ produced by fixed charges outside it. **Electrostatic induction** is the rearrangement of the conductor's charge into the surface density $\sigma$ that makes [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^mod-c4-1-1|Model §C4.1.1]] hold. The field of $\sigma$ is the **self-field**
>
> $$
> \vec E_{\text{self}}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint_S dS'\,\sigma(\vec r\,')\,\frac{\vec r - \vec r\,'}{\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert^3},
> $$
>
> and for a neutral conductor its far field is dipolar, with the **induced dipole moment** $\vec p = \oint_S\vec r\,\sigma\,dS$ (Zangwill eqs. (5.3)–(5.4)).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.2, eqs. (5.3)–(5.4), Figure 5.1*

^def-c4-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.2: Induction as a Self-Consistency Condition
> The induced surface charge $\sigma$ on a conductor of volume $\mathcal V$ (connected) solves the integral equation
>
> $$
> V_{\text{ext}}(\vec r) + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint_S\frac{\sigma(\vec r\,')\,dS'}{\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert} = V_c \qquad (\vec r \in \mathcal V),
> $$
>
> with a constant $V_c$, the potential of the conductor, and the side condition $\oint_S\sigma\,dS = Q$ ($Q$ its net charge, [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^mod-c4-1-1|Model §C4.1.1]]). Equivalently $\vec E_{\text{self}} = -\vec E_{\text{ext}}$ inside.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.2, eq. (5.3)*

^thm-c4-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Total field inside.** The field at an interior point is the superposition $\vec E = \vec E_{\text{ext}} + \vec E_{\text{self}}$ (superposition of the Coulomb integrals, [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]]), and the model requires $\vec E = \vec 0$ there. This is Zangwill's eq. (5.3), $\vec E_{\text{ext}} + \vec E_{\text{self}} = \vec 0$ in $\mathcal V$.
>
> **2. Potential form.** Both fields are gradients: $\vec E_{\text{ext}} = -\nabla V_{\text{ext}}$ and $\vec E_{\text{self}} = -\nabla V_{\text{self}}$ with $V_{\text{self}}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint_S\sigma(\vec r\,')\,dS'/\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert$ (differentiation under the integral is allowed for $\vec r$ off $S$, where the integrand is smooth in $\vec r$). So $\nabla(V_{\text{ext}} + V_{\text{self}}) = \vec 0$ in $\mathcal V$.
>
> **3. Constant.** A function with zero gradient on a connected open set is constant (for two points join them by a polygonal path in $\mathcal V$ and integrate the gradient along it). Call the constant $V_c$. Both $V_{\text{ext}}$ and $V_{\text{self}}$ are continuous up to $S$ (the potential of a surface charge is continuous, [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^rem-c4-1-1|Remark: What changes from levels A and B]]), so the equation also holds on $S$.
>
> **4. Net charge.** $\rho = 0$ inside, so the whole net charge is on $S$: $\oint_S\sigma\,dS = Q$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Induction is not a dynamical statement but a linear integral equation for $\sigma$ (a Fredholm equation of the first kind, with kernel $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert$). Zangwill's iterative picture (the induced charge changes the field, which changes the induced charge, …) describes how the solution is reached, not what it is.
> - The equation is linear in $(V_{\text{ext}}, Q)$ and $\sigma$, the root of all linear relations of [[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors|§C4.2]]–[[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix|§C4.3]]. That it has exactly one solution is a theorem ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]]); existence is part of potential theory ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness|§C6.1]]).

## Thomson's theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.3: Thomson's Theorem
> Let $N$ bodies of fixed shapes $\mathcal V_1, \dots, \mathcal V_N$ carry fixed net charges $Q_1, \dots, Q_N$, in the potential $V_{\text{ext}}$ of fixed external charges. Among all finite-energy charge distributions $\rho$ on the bodies with these net charges, the electrostatic energy ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]], plus the potential energy in $V_{\text{ext}}$)
>
> $$
> U_E[\rho] = \frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\!\!\int d^3r\,d^3r'\,\frac{\rho(\vec r)\rho(\vec r\,')}{\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert} + \int d^3r\,\rho(\vec r)\,V_{\text{ext}}(\vec r)
> $$
>
> is stationary exactly when the total potential is constant on each body, $V = V_k$ on $\mathcal V_k$; that distribution is the unique global minimum. So $\vec E = \vec 0$ inside each body: [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^mod-c4-1-1|Model §C4.1.1]] is the minimum-energy state.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.2.1, eqs. (5.5)–(5.10) (one body; stated here for $N$ bodies)*

^thm-c4-1-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The functional.** $U_E[\rho]$ is the total electrostatic energy of the bodies' charge (self-energy, first term) plus its potential energy in the external field (second term) ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]], [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]]; Zangwill eq. (3.84)); the energy of the fixed external charges among themselves is a constant and is dropped. Both integrals run over $\mathcal V = \mathcal V_1\cup\dots\cup\mathcal V_N$ (closed sets, so surface charges are included). Write $V_\rho(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r'\,\rho(\vec r\,')/\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert$ for the potential of $\rho$.
>
> **2. Admissible variations.** A competitor is $\rho + \eta$ with $\int_{\mathcal V_k}\eta\,d^3r = 0$ for each $k$ (the net charges are fixed) and $\eta$ of finite energy. Zangwill enforces the constraints with Lagrange multipliers $\lambda_k$, minimizing $I[\rho] = U_E[\rho] - \sum_k\lambda_k\int_{\mathcal V_k}\rho\,d^3r$ without constraint (eq. (5.6); finite-dimensional version [[§17 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-17-3|452 Thm. §17.3]]). Both routes are carried along below.
>
> **3. Expand exactly.** Insert $\rho + \eta$. The double integral contains $(\rho + \eta)(\vec r)\,(\rho + \eta)(\vec r\,') = \rho\rho' + \rho\,\eta' + \eta\,\rho' + \eta\,\eta'$ (primes: argument $\vec r\,'$). Hence
>
> $$
> U_E[\rho + \eta] - U_E[\rho] = \frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\!\!\int\frac{\rho(\vec r)\eta(\vec r\,') + \eta(\vec r)\rho(\vec r\,')}{\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert} + \int\eta\,V_{\text{ext}} + \frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\!\!\int\frac{\eta(\vec r)\eta(\vec r\,')}{\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert} .
> $$
>
> There are no further terms: $U_E$ is a quadratic functional, so the expansion stops at second order (Zangwill "neglects terms of order $(\delta\rho)^2$"; here they are kept, because they decide minimum versus maximum).
>
> **4. Symmetrize the cross term.** Rename $\vec r \leftrightarrow \vec r\,'$ in the first cross term; the kernel $1/\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert$ is symmetric, so it equals the second (Zangwill eq. (5.9)). The two together give $\frac{2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r\,\eta(\vec r)\int d^3r'\,\rho(\vec r\,')/\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert = \int\eta\,V_\rho$. With $V = V_\rho + V_{\text{ext}}$ the total potential:
>
> $$
> U_E[\rho + \eta] - U_E[\rho] = \int_{\mathcal V}\eta(\vec r)\,V(\vec r)\,d^3r + U_{\text{self}}[\eta], \qquad U_{\text{self}}[\eta] = \frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\!\!\int\frac{\eta(\vec r)\eta(\vec r\,')}{\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert} .
> $$
>
> **5. Stationarity.** The first-order term must vanish for every admissible $\eta$. If $V$ is constant, $V = V_k$, on each $\mathcal V_k$, it does: $\int_{\mathcal V}\eta V = \sum_kV_k\int_{\mathcal V_k}\eta = 0$. Conversely, if $V$ took values $V(\vec a) > V(\vec b)$ at two points of the same body, the variation $\eta$ that removes a small charge $\epsilon > 0$ from a small ball around $\vec a$ and adds it in a small ball around $\vec b$ (smooth bumps, zero net charge, finite energy) gives a first-order change $\approx \epsilon\,[V(\vec b) - V(\vec a)] < 0$: moving charge from high to low potential lowers the energy, so $\rho$ was not stationary. With multipliers: the first-order change of $I$ is $\int\eta\,(V - \lambda(\vec r))$, $\lambda(\vec r) = \lambda_k$ on $\mathcal V_k$, now for arbitrary $\eta$, and its vanishing gives $V = \lambda_k$ on $\mathcal V_k$ (Zangwill eq. (5.10)). ⚑ By-product: the multiplier is the potential of the body, $\lambda_k = V_k$; it is the rate $\partial U_E/\partial Q_k$ at which the minimum energy grows with the body's charge → [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-2|Theorem §C4.4.2]] (charge and potential as conjugate variables).
>
> **6. Minimum, exactly.** For an admissible $\eta$ at the stationary $\rho$, step 5 kills the first-order term, and step 4 gives $U_E[\rho + \eta] - U_E[\rho] = U_{\text{self}}[\eta]$. The Coulomb energy of any finite-energy distribution is $U_{\text{self}}[\eta] = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int\lvert\vec E_\eta\rvert^2\,d^3r \ge 0$, zero only if $\vec E_\eta \equiv \vec 0$, i.e. $\eta = \varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E_\eta = 0$ ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^rem-c2-3-1|§C2.3, Remark: Positive energy, and where it sits]]; Zangwill §3.6.1, eq. (3.80)). So every other admissible distribution has strictly higher energy: a global, strict minimum. ⚑ By-product: two distinct stationary distributions are impossible → [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]].
>
> **7. The minimizer lives on the surfaces.** In the interior of $\mathcal V_k$, $V = V_k$ is constant, so $\vec E = -\nabla V = \vec 0$ and $\rho = -\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V = 0$ there. All charge of the minimizer is on the surfaces $S_k$. ⚑ By-product: the minimum is never attained by a smooth volume density; the admissible class must contain surface distributions $\sigma\,\delta_S$, which have finite energy, while point charges (infinite self-energy) are excluded ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^rem-c4-1-1|Remark: What changes from levels A and B]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The field-free interior is not postulated but is the energy minimum at fixed charges; "free to move" means "free to minimize $U_E$".
> - The minimum is global and strict because $U_E$ is quadratic with a positive-definite quadratic part, the self-energy; no higher-order terms are neglected.
> - Assumptions actually used: fixed shapes (non-electric forces hold the bodies together), fixed net charge per body, fixed external charges, and no non-electric energy that depends on how the charge is arranged (the "perfect" in perfect conductor).
> - Used next: uniqueness ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]]), the potential as $\partial U_E/\partial Q$ ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-2|Theorem §C4.4.2]]), and the energy form of capacitance ([[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-1|Theorem §C4.2.1]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.4: The Equilibrium Charge Distribution Is Unique
> For conductors of given shapes, given net charges $Q_k$ and given external charges there is exactly one equilibrium surface charge, and hence one field. In the language of [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]]: the integral equation with the side conditions $\oint_{S_k}\sigma\,dS = Q_k$ has at most one solution.
>
> *Source: consequence of Zangwill §5.2.1 (the positivity of the second variation), drawn here*

^thm-c4-1-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1.** An equilibrium distribution makes each conductor an equipotential, so by [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]] (step 5) it is a stationary point of $U_E$ in the admissible class, and by step 6 a strict global minimum.
>
> **2.** Let $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$ both be equilibria. Then $\eta = \rho_2 - \rho_1$ has zero net charge on each body, and step 6 at $\rho_1$ gives $U_E[\rho_2] - U_E[\rho_1] = U_{\text{self}}[\eta] \ge 0$, while step 6 at $\rho_2$ (with $-\eta$) gives $U_E[\rho_1] - U_E[\rho_2] = U_{\text{self}}[-\eta] = U_{\text{self}}[\eta] \ge 0$. Adding the two equalities, the left sides cancel: $0 = 2U_{\text{self}}[\eta]$, so $U_{\text{self}}[\eta] = 0$ and, by the strict positivity of step 6, $\eta = 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The same result as the second uniqueness theorem ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]; at level C [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]), now as strict convexity of the energy. EM B's proof integrates $E_3^2$ of the difference field over the vacuum region; here the same positive quantity appears as the self-energy of the difference charge.

^der-c4-1-4

*Uses:* [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: What Thomson's theorem says about matter
> All systems in mechanical equilibrium arrange their charge to minimize their *total* energy; they differ in how much non-electric (quantum-mechanical) energy competes with the electrostatic part. A perfect conductor is the limit in which only the electrostatic energy depends on the arrangement of the charge (Zangwill §5.2.1). The competition returns twice in this chapter: the screening length of a real metal (★ Remark below), where the electrons' kinetic energy resists compressing the induced charge into a sheet, and the "quantum capacitance" of a two-dimensional electron gas ([[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^ex-c4-2-2|Example §C4.2.2]], HW 9, Problem 4), where it is the whole point. Thomson minimizes over charges at fixed charge; the complementary Dirichlet principle minimizes the field energy over potentials at fixed boundary potentials (the static action of [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-2|Theorem §C2.1.2]] is of this kind) ([[§C6.4★ The Complex Potential and the Variational Principle#^thm-c6-4-3|Theorem §C6.4.3]]); together they give the two-sided bounds on capacitances.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.2.1*

## Surface charge and induced moments

> [!remark] Remark: The field at a conductor's surface
> Just outside a conductor $\hat n\times\vec E_{\text{out}} = \vec 0$ and $\vec E_{\text{out}} = \hat n\,\sigma/\varepsilon_0$, so $\sigma = \varepsilon_0\,\hat n\cdot\vec E_{\text{out}} = -\varepsilon_0\,\partial V/\partial n$ (Zangwill eqs. (5.11)–(5.15)). This is the same result and the same argument as level B, the matching conditions with $\vec E_{\text{in}} = \vec 0$: [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-1|Theorem §B2.4.1]]. The charge density at a conductor is read off the field just outside it.

^rem-c4-1-3

> [!example] Example §C4.1.1: The Induced Dipole of a Conducting Sphere
> A neutral conducting sphere of radius $R$ sits in a uniform field $\vec E_0 = E_0\hat z$. Its potential and surface charge, $V = -E_0(r - R^3/r^2)\cos\theta$ and $\sigma = 3\varepsilon_0E_0\cos\theta$, were found by separation of variables in [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]]. Zangwill gets the exterior self-potential faster from [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]]: inside, $V_{\text{self}} = +E_0r\cos\theta$ must cancel $V_{\text{ext}} = -E_0r\cos\theta$; outside, $V_{\text{self}}$ is a sum $\sum_\ell B_\ell r^{-\ell - 1}P_\ell(\cos\theta)$, and continuity at $r = R$ for all $\theta$ leaves only $\ell = 1$, $B_1 = E_0R^3$: an exact dipole. The level-C step is to compute $\vec p$ from Definition §C4.1.1 directly. With $\vec r = R(\sin\theta\cos\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\theta)$ on the surface and $dS = R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$, the $x$ and $y$ components contain $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\phi\,d\phi = \int_0^{2\pi}\sin\phi\,d\phi = 0$, and
>
> $$
> p_z = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!d\phi\int_0^\pi R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\;R\cos\theta\;3\varepsilon_0E_0\cos\theta = 2\pi R^3\cdot3\varepsilon_0E_0\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = 2\pi R^3\cdot3\varepsilon_0E_0\cdot\tfrac23 = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3E_0 .
> $$
>
> Zangwill writes $\vec p = \alpha\varepsilon_0\vec E_0$ with $\alpha = 4\pi R^3$, a volume; EM B writes $\vec p = \alpha\vec E$ with $\alpha = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^law-b4-2-3|Law §B4.2.3]]). Both say the same thing; the polarizability convention has its caution home in [[§C5.4 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^cau-c5-4-1|§C5.4, Caution: Two polarizabilities]]. The exactly dipolar exterior is special to the sphere; other shapes add quadrupole and higher terms.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Example 5.1 and eqs. (5.16)–(5.17)*

^ex-c4-1-1

> [!example] Example §C4.1.2: Two Distant Spheres Joined by a Wire
> Two uncharged metal spheres, radii $R_1$, $R_2$, centred at $\vec r_1$, $\vec r_2$ with $\vec d = \vec r_2 - \vec r_1$, $d \gg R_1, R_2$, are joined by a thin wire that holds no charge, and the whole conductor sits in a uniform field $\vec E_0$. Find the induced dipole moment.
>
> *Charges.* The wire carries none, so sphere 2 gets $+Q$ and sphere 1 gets $-Q$.
>
> *Potential of each sphere = potential at its centre.* The centre is an interior point. The sphere's own surface charge contributes exactly $Q_k/4\pi\varepsilon_0R_k$ there, *whatever its distribution*, since every surface point is at distance $R_k$ from the centre. The external field contributes $-\vec E_0\cdot\vec r_k$. The other sphere contributes its monopole $\pm Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0d$ plus induced multipoles of relative size $(R/d)^3$, dropped. So
>
> $$
> V_1 = -\vec E_0\cdot\vec r_1 - \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_1} + \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0d}, \qquad V_2 = -\vec E_0\cdot\vec r_2 + \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_2} - \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0d} .
> $$
>
> *Equipotential.* The wire makes one conductor, $V_1 = V_2$:
>
> $$
> \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} - \frac{2}{d}\Bigr) = \vec E_0\cdot\vec d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\vec E_0\cdot\vec d}{1/R_1 + 1/R_2 - 2/d} \approx 4\pi\varepsilon_0\frac{R_1R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\,\vec E_0\cdot\vec d .
> $$
>
> *Dipole.* $\vec p \approx Q\vec d = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\frac{R_1R_2}{R_1 + R_2}(\vec E_0\cdot\vec d)\,\vec d$; the spheres' own induced dipoles $4\pi\varepsilon_0R_k^3\vec E_0$ (Example §C4.1.1) are smaller by $(R/d)^2$.
>
> Two readings. The coefficient of $\vec E_0\cdot\vec d$ is exactly the capacitance of two distant spheres, $4\pi\varepsilon_0/(1/R_1 + 1/R_2 - 2/d)$ ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^ex-a4-1-2|Example §A4.1.2]]): the external field drives the "voltage" $\vec E_0\cdot\vec d$ between the two ends, and the conductor responds as a capacitor. And the polarizability is a tensor $\propto\vec d\,\vec d$, growing like (length)$^2\times$radius: an elongated conductor is far more polarizable along its length than a sphere of the same volume. Zangwill keeps only the leading term (no $2/d$); the correction is of relative order $R/d$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Example 5.2 (the $2/d$ correction and the capacitor reading added here)*

## The charge on an ellipsoid and a disk

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.5: Newton's Homoeoid Theorem
> A **homoeoid** is the shell between two similar, similarly placed, concentric ellipsoids, $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1$ and $= (1 + \epsilon)^2$. A homoeoid filled with uniform charge density produces no electric field in its hollow interior.
>
> *Source: derived here (Newton's shell argument for the sphere, carried to the ellipsoid by a linear map); it yields Zangwill's eqs. (5.31)–(5.33) for prolate and oblate shapes at once*

^thm-c4-1-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Equal chords.** Take a straight line through a point $P$ of the hollow interior. It cuts the inner and the outer ellipsoid twice each; call the two segments of the line that lie in the shell $\ell_1$ (on one side of $P$) and $\ell_2$ (on the other). *Claim: $\ell_1 = \ell_2$.* For two concentric spheres this holds by reflection symmetry about the foot of the perpendicular from the centre to the line: both chords are bisected there. The linear map $(x, y, z) \mapsto (ax, by, cz)$ sends concentric spheres of radii $1$ and $1 + \epsilon$ to the two ellipsoids, sends lines to lines, and preserves ratios of lengths along any one line. Equal segments on a line therefore go to equal segments, and the claim holds for the ellipsoids.
>
> **2. Cone pairs.** Around the line, take a thin double cone with apex $P$ and solid angle $d\Omega$ on each side. On side $i$ it cuts out of the shell a piece at distance $r_i$ from $P$, of thickness $\ell_i$ along the line and cross-section $r_i^2\,d\Omega$ (to first order in $d\Omega$ and in the thin-piece limit; for a thick shell integrate along the cone, as in step 4). Its charge is $\rho_0\,r_i^2\,d\Omega\,\ell_i$.
>
> **3. Cancellation.** The two pieces lie in opposite directions from $P$ and contribute fields of magnitude $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\rho_0r_i^2d\Omega\,\ell_i/r_i^2 = \frac{\rho_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ell_i\,d\Omega$; with $\ell_1 = \ell_2$ these are equal, and the directions are opposite: the pair contributes nothing.
>
> **4. Thick shells.** For finite $\epsilon$ write the charge on side $i$ as $\int\rho_0\,r^2\,d\Omega\,dr$ over the shell part of the cone; its field at $P$ is $\frac{\rho_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}d\Omega\int dr = \frac{\rho_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ell_i\,d\Omega$ exactly (the $r^2$ of the volume cancels the $1/r^2$ of Coulomb's law), so step 3 holds for any thickness.
>
> **5. Sum.** The double cones in all directions tile the shell, so $\vec E(P) = \vec 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Only two facts enter: the inverse-square law (the $r^2$ of a cone's cross-section cancels the $1/r^2$ of the field) and the equal-chord property, which is affine geometry. For any other force law the hollow would not be field-free; the same cancellation tests Coulomb's law ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^rem-a2-4-3|§A2.4, Remark: A precision test of Coulomb's law]]).

^der-c4-1-5

*Uses:* [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.6: Charge on a Conducting Ellipsoid and a Conducting Disk
> An isolated conducting ellipsoid with semi-axes $a, b, c$ and charge $Q$ carries the surface charge
>
> $$
> \sigma(x, y, z) = \frac{Q}{4\pi abc}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2/a^4 + y^2/b^4 + z^2/c^4}} .
> $$
>
> In the limit $c \to 0$ (a disk of radius $a$, $a = b$) each face carries
>
> $$
> \sigma(\rho) = \frac{Q}{4\pi a\sqrt{a^2 - \rho^2}} ,
> $$
>
> which diverges at the rim like $(a - \rho)^{-1/2}$ and is still integrable: each face holds $Q/2$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Application 5.1, eqs. (5.31)–(5.33) (spheroid of revolution; Zangwill derives the prolate case and quotes Stratton for the oblate one); the general ellipsoid and the homoeoid route are added here*

^thm-c4-1-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. A thin homoeoid as a candidate.** Let the shell between $F = 1$ and $F = (1 + \epsilon)^2$, $F(\vec r) = x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 + z^2/c^2$, carry uniform density $\rho_0$, and let $\epsilon \to 0$ with the total charge fixed at $Q$. By [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-5|Theorem §C4.1.5]] the field vanishes in the hollow, so the potential is constant there; the potential of a shell whose thickness tends to zero is continuous across it, so in the limit the ellipsoid's surface is an equipotential, and so is its interior.
>
> **2. It is the conductor's charge.** A conductor's equilibrium charge is the unique distribution with net charge $Q$ that makes the body an equipotential ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]]). The limiting surface charge of step 1 has both properties, so it *is* the conductor's charge.
>
> **3. Total charge in terms of $\epsilon$.** The map $(x, y, z) \mapsto (ax, by, cz)$ has Jacobian determinant $abc$, so the shell's volume is $abc\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3}[(1 + \epsilon)^3 - 1] = 4\pi abc\,\epsilon + O(\epsilon^2)$. Hence $\rho_0 = Q/(4\pi abc\,\epsilon)$ to leading order.
>
> **4. Local thickness.** At a surface point, move along the outward normal $\hat n = \nabla F/\lvert\nabla F\rvert$. Since $F$ rises from $1$ to $(1 + \epsilon)^2 = 1 + 2\epsilon + O(\epsilon^2)$ across the shell and $dF = \lvert\nabla F\rvert\,dh$ along the normal, the thickness is $h = 2\epsilon/\lvert\nabla F\rvert$. With $\nabla F = 2(x/a^2, y/b^2, z/c^2)$, $\lvert\nabla F\rvert = 2\sqrt{x^2/a^4 + y^2/b^4 + z^2/c^4}$ and $h = \epsilon/\sqrt{x^2/a^4 + y^2/b^4 + z^2/c^4}$.
>
> **5. Surface density.** $\sigma = \rho_0h$; inserting steps 3 and 4 gives the first formula, the $\epsilon$ cancelling. *Check:* for a sphere, $a = b = c = R$ and $\sqrt{r^2/R^4} = 1/R$ on the surface, so $\sigma = Q/4\pi R^2$.
>
> **6. Flatten.** Take $a = b$, so $\rho^2 = x^2 + y^2$, and use the surface equation to eliminate $z$: $z^2/c^4 = (1 - \rho^2/a^2)/c^2$. Then
>
> $$
> \sigma = \frac{Q}{4\pi a^2c}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^2/a^4 + (1 - \rho^2/a^2)/c^2}} = \frac{Q}{4\pi a^2}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^2c^2/a^4 + 1 - \rho^2/a^2}}
> $$
>
> (Zangwill eq. (5.32), multiplying numerator and denominator by $c$). As $c \to 0$ at fixed $\rho < a$, the first term under the root vanishes: $\sigma \to \frac{Q}{4\pi a^2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \rho^2/a^2}} = \frac{Q}{4\pi a\sqrt{a^2 - \rho^2}}$, on the upper and on the lower face (the two halves of the flattened surface).
>
> **7. Charge per face and the rim.** $\int_0^a\sigma\,2\pi\rho\,d\rho = \frac{Q}{4\pi a}\cdot2\pi\int_0^a\frac{\rho\,d\rho}{\sqrt{a^2 - \rho^2}} = \frac{Q}{2a}\bigl[-\sqrt{a^2 - \rho^2}\bigr]_0^a = \frac{Q}{2}$. Near the rim $\sigma \approx \frac{Q}{4\pi a\sqrt{2a}}(a - \rho)^{-1/2}$. ⚑ By-product: a locally integrable singularity, so $\sigma$ (and $\sigma\,\delta_S$) is still a regular distribution on the surface ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-1|QFT Theorem §CA.2.1]]), with finite total charge and finite energy; the field near a knife edge diverges like the inverse square root of the distance, the edge singularity derived in general in [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-5|Theorem §C6.3.5]] (Zangwill §7.9.1).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The equipotential condition is solved by a geometric construction plus uniqueness, without solving Laplace's equation in ellipsoidal coordinates.
> - The disk's density is the projection of a uniformly charged sphere onto its equatorial plane: a uniform density $Q/4\pi a^2$ on the sphere, projected, is divided by $\cos\theta = \sqrt{a^2 - \rho^2}/a$ (Zangwill's geometric reading). A conductor piles charge at its sharpest parts.
> - Used next: the self-capacitance $C = 8\varepsilon_0a$ of the disk ([[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-2|Theorem §C4.2.2]]) and the induction on a grounded disk (HW 9, Problem 3; [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^ex-c4-3-4|Example §C4.3.4]]).

The sphere ($c = a$) is uniform; as the spheroid is flattened the charge moves to the rim, and the disk ($c = 0$) has the integrable $1/\sqrt{a^2 - \rho^2}$ edge singularity.*

> [!remark]- Remark: Zangwill's route, from a charged needle
> Zangwill (after Green, 1828) builds the conducting *prolate* spheroid from the equipotentials of a uniformly charged segment $-L < z < L$: with $r_\pm$ the distances to the ends and $u = \frac12(r_+ + r_-)$, the segment's potential is $V(u) = \frac{Q}{8\pi\varepsilon_0L}\ln\frac{u + L}{u - L}$ (Zangwill eq. (5.20); [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-6|Theorem §C2.1.6]]), so the surfaces $u = b$ are equipotentials, and they are the prolate spheroids with foci at the ends ($L^2 = b^2 - a^2$). Then $\sigma = \varepsilon_0\lvert\partial V/\partial u\rvert\,\lvert\nabla u\rvert$ on $u = b$, with $\lvert\partial V/\partial u\rvert_{u = b} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{1}{b^2 - L^2} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a^2}$ (eq. (5.24)), reproduces Theorem §C4.1.6 for semi-axes $(a, a, b)$ (in this remark $a$ is the equatorial and $b$ the polar semi-axis, as in Zangwill). This route needs a separate argument for the oblate case, which is the one the disk requires; the homoeoid treats both at once.
>

## Screening and shielding

> [!remark] Remark: Shielding at level C
> - *Empty cavity.* The field in a charge-free cavity inside a conductor vanishes, whatever charges lie outside, and the cavity wall carries no charge: same result and same proof as level B, uniqueness for the cavity with constant boundary value ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^ex-b3-1-1|Example §B3.1.1]]; the maximum principle [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]).
> - *Zangwill's loop argument.* Suppose a field line in the cavity ran from a wall point $A$ to a wall point $B$. Close the loop through the metal: $0 = \oint\vec E\cdot d\vec\ell = \int_A^B\lvert\vec E_{\text{cavity}}\rvert\,d\ell + \int_B^A\vec E_{\text{metal}}\cdot d\vec\ell = \int_A^B\lvert\vec E_{\text{cavity}}\rvert\,d\ell$, impossible for a nonzero field (Zangwill eq. (5.34)). The argument assumes that a field line through any point of nonzero field reaches the wall at both ends. It cannot close on itself ($\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec\ell = 0$) or end inside (no charge there), but it could a priori accumulate inside the cavity; the maximum principle closes that gap, since $V$ decreases strictly along a field line and a harmonic $V$ cannot have its extrema inside. Earnshaw's theorem gives a third route (Zangwill Problem 5.1; [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]; [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^rem-b3-1-3|§B3.1, ★ Remark: Earnshaw's theorem]]).
> - *Charge in the cavity.* A charge $q$ inside induces $-q$ on the cavity wall and, on a neutral conductor, $+q$ on the outer surface; the exterior field depends only on the total, not on where $q$ sits ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^ex-b3-1-1|Example §B3.1.1]]; Zangwill proves it with uniqueness when conductors are present, §7.3.1 → [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]). A conductor shields its inside from the outside, not the outside from its inside.
> - *Mesh.* A wall of metal mesh shields almost as well as a solid wall: the field leaking through a periodic grid decays exponentially with the distance from it, on the scale of the mesh period (Faraday's cage, Zangwill §7.5.1 → [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^ex-c6-2-2|Example §C6.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.3, eq. (5.34), Figure 5.5; Problem 5.1*

^rem-c4-1-5

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Real conductors and the screening length
> In a real conductor the induced surface sheet spreads into a layer of thickness $\ell$, the **screening length**: $\sim10^{-10}$ m in a good metal, $\sim10^{-8}$ m in an electrolyte or a biological plasma, $\sim10^{-5}$ m in a laboratory plasma (Zangwill §5.7). A model: a point charge $q$ at the origin in a uniform positive background $en_0$ (fixed ions) with mobile electrons of density $n(\vec r)$.
> 1. *Poisson.* $\rho = e[n_0 - n(\vec r)] + q\,\delta^3(\vec r)$, so $\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V = -e[n_0 - n] - q\,\delta^3(\vec r)$ (Zangwill eq. (5.85)); $q\,\delta^3$ is the point charge as a distribution ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]).
> 2. *Local response.* The electrons (charge $-e$, potential energy $-eV$) follow the local electrochemical balance $n(\vec r) = n_0(\mu + eV(\vec r))$, with $n_0(\mu)$ the uniform density at chemical potential $\mu$, a non-decreasing function. To first order, $n - n_0 \approx e\,\frac{\partial n_0}{\partial\mu}V$, so $-e[n_0 - n] \approx e^2\frac{\partial n_0}{\partial\mu}V$ (eq. (5.87)).
> 3. *Screened Poisson equation.* With $1/\ell^2 = \frac{e^2}{\varepsilon_0}\frac{\partial n_0}{\partial\mu}$ (eq. (5.88)) and dividing by $\varepsilon_0$:
>
> $$
> \nabla^2V - \frac{V}{\ell^2} = -\frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}\,\delta^3(\vec r) .
> $$
>
>    Zangwill's eq. (5.89) prints the right side as $-q\,\delta(\vec r)$, without the $1/\varepsilon_0$; his solution (5.92) is that of the equation above.
> 4. *Solution.* $V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}e^{-r/\ell}$ (eq. (5.92)). Check, as a distributional identity: [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^ex-b2-2-3|Example §B2.2.3]] computed $\nabla^2(Ae^{-\lambda r}/r) = A[-4\pi\delta^3(\vec r) + \lambda^2e^{-\lambda r}/r]$; with $A = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ and $\lambda = 1/\ell$ this is $V/\ell^2 - (q/\varepsilon_0)\delta^3$.
> 5. *Complete screening.* The induced charge is $\rho_{\text{ind}} = -e(n - n_0) = -\frac{\varepsilon_0}{\ell^2}V$, with total $-\frac{\varepsilon_0}{\ell^2}\cdot\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-r/\ell}}{r}4\pi r^2\,dr = -\frac{q}{\ell^2}\cdot\ell^2 = -q$. Zangwill's eq. (5.93) writes this as $\int d^3r\,n(\vec r) = -q$; the correct statement is $-e\int d^3r\,[n(\vec r) - n_0] = -q$ ($\int n$ itself diverges, since $n \to n_0$).
> 6. *Two limits.* Classical (Boltzmann) carriers, $n_0 \propto e^{\mu/kT}$ ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-3|TH Theorem §B10.1.3]]): $\partial n_0/\partial\mu = n_0/kT$ and $\ell_{\mathrm{DH}} = \sqrt{\varepsilon_0kT/e^2n_0}$ (Debye–Hückel, eq. (5.95)). Degenerate electrons at $T = 0$ ([[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^def-b13-1-1|TH Def. §B13.1.1]]), $n_0 = k_F^3/3\pi^2$ with $\mu = \hbar^2k_F^2/2m$: $\partial n_0/\partial\mu = \frac{3n_0}{2\mu} = \frac{mk_F}{\pi^2\hbar^2}$, and with $a_B = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2/me^2$, $\ell_{\mathrm{TF}}^2 = \frac{\varepsilon_0\pi^2\hbar^2}{e^2mk_F} = \frac{\pi a_B}{4k_F}$ (Thomas–Fermi, eq. (5.97)). For copper, $k_F = 1.36\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$ gives $\ell_{\mathrm{TF}} \approx 0.55$ Å.
>
> A perfect conductor is the limit $\ell \to 0$, i.e. $\partial n_0/\partial\mu \to \infty$: charge can be compressed into a sheet at no cost. What resists compression is configurational entropy in a classical plasma and the kinetic (zero-point) energy of confined electrons in a metal: the non-electric energy that Thomson's theorem sets to zero ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^rem-c4-1-2|Remark: What Thomson's theorem says about matter]]). The ratio $\ell/D$, $D$ the size of the sample, measures how perfect a conductor is. The nonlinear version (Poisson–Boltzmann) is [[§C7.5★ Logarithmic Potentials and the Poisson–Boltzmann Equation#^mod-c7-5-3|Model §C7.5.3]].
>
> *Source: Zangwill §5.7, eqs. (5.85)–(5.97) (the copper number computed here) · Likharev §2.1*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Thomson's theorem is a constrained minimization of a quadratic functional with a positive-definite quadratic part, the infinite-dimensional analogue of minimizing $\tfrac12x^{\mathsf T}Ax - b^{\mathsf T}x$ under linear constraints with Lagrange multipliers ([[§17 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-17-3|452 Thm. §17.3]]); the multipliers become the conductors' potentials, as in thermodynamics multipliers become intensive variables.
> - The homoeoid theorem is the electrostatic twin of Newton's shell theorem for gravity ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits|CM §A8.2]]): any inverse-square force gives a field-free hollow inside a uniform spherical or ellipsoidal shell.
> - The screened equation $(\nabla^2 - 1/\ell^2)V = -q\delta^3/\varepsilon_0$ is the static Klein–Gordon (Yukawa) equation: its fundamental solution $e^{-r/\ell}/4\pi r$ is the static propagator of a field of mass $\hbar/\ell c$ ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation|REL §B4.1]]; fundamental solutions [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-7|QFT Def. §CA.2.7]]).
