*Sources: Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (4th ed.), §9.4 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 47, 53.*

What happens to an electromagnetic wave that enters a conductor, and why does the index of any material depend on frequency? Waves and Optics answers both for light: metals through the Drude model at optical frequencies, with the skin depth and the reflectance of a metal ([[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media|WO §B13.2]]), and dispersion through the Lorentz model of bound electrons ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model|WO §B13.1]]); those are the homes of the skin depth, the complex index, the plasma frequency and the dispersion formulas. This section adds Griffiths' treatment of an Ohmic conductor with arbitrary $\varepsilon$, $\mu$ and constant conductivity $\sigma$, derived from Maxwell's equations with Ohm's law ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^law-b8-1-1|Law §B8.1.1]]): the complex wave number for all ratios $\sigma/\varepsilon\omega$ (good and poor conductors alike), the lag of $\vec B$ behind $\vec E$, the energy balance, and reflection at a conducting surface, ending with the perfect conductor that guided waves need ([[§B10.4★ Guided Waves|§B10.4★]]). For dispersion it gives the dictionary to Waves and Optics.

## Waves in an Ohmic conductor

> [!model] Model §B10.3.1: Ohmic Linear Conductor
> A homogeneous linear medium ($\varepsilon$, $\mu$; [[§B10.2 Waves in Linear Media, Reflection and Transmission#^mod-b10-2-1|Model §B10.2.1]]) in which the free current obeys Ohm's law $\vec J_f = \sigma\vec E$ with a real constant conductivity $\sigma$ (Griffiths eq. (9.117)). Any free charge density inside decays as $e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = \varepsilon/\sigma$ ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]; Griffiths eq. (9.120)); once it has gone, $\rho_f = 0$ and Maxwell's equations in the medium are (Griffiths eq. (9.121))
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec E = 0, \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec B = 0, \qquad \nabla\times\vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla\times\vec B = \mu\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t} + \mu\sigma\vec E .
> $$
>
> - *Assumes:* Ohm's law with the static $\sigma$, which needs fields that change slowly compared with the collision time of the carriers, $\omega\tau_c \ll 1$ ($\tau_c \sim 10^{-14}\ \mathrm s$ in metals, [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^ex-a5-2-2|Example §A5.2.2]]); $\varepsilon$, $\mu$, $\sigma$ real and independent of frequency over the range considered.
> - *Fails:* for metals at optical frequencies, where $\omega\tau_c \gg 1$ and the conductivity becomes complex, $\sigma/(1 - i\omega\tau_c)$ ([[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-2|WO Theorem §B13.2.2]]); on times shorter than $\tau_c$, where also the charge relaxation is not exponential with $\varepsilon/\sigma$ ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^rem-b8-1-3|§B8.1, Remark: What the relaxation time measures]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.4.1, eqs. (9.117)–(9.121) and footnote 17*

^mod-b10-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.3.2: Plane Waves in an Ohmic Conductor
> In the conductor of Model §B10.3.1, $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ obey the modified wave equations (Griffiths eq. (9.122))
>
> $$
> \nabla^2\vec E = \mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\vec E}{\partial t^2} + \mu\sigma\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla^2\vec B = \mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\vec B}{\partial t^2} + \mu\sigma\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t} ,
> $$
>
> and admit plane waves $\tilde{\vec E} = \tilde{\vec E}_0e^{i(\tilde kz - \omega t)}$ with complex wave number $\tilde k = k + i\kappa$ (Griffiths eqs. (9.123)–(9.126)):
>
> $$
> \tilde k^2 = \mu\varepsilon\omega^2 + i\mu\sigma\omega, \qquad k = \omega\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon\mu}{2}}\Bigl[\sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon\omega}\Bigr)^2} + 1\Bigr]^{1/2}, \qquad \kappa = \omega\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon\mu}{2}}\Bigl[\sqrt{1 + \Bigl(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon\omega}\Bigr)^2} - 1\Bigr]^{1/2} .
> $$
>
> 1. *Attenuation.* $\tilde{\vec E} = \tilde{\vec E}_0e^{-\kappa z}e^{i(kz - \omega t)}$: the amplitude falls by $1/e$ in the **skin depth** $d = 1/\kappa$ (9.128) (Waves and Optics' $\delta$, [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^def-b13-2-1|WO Def. §B13.2.1]]); $k$ fixes the wavelength $2\pi/k$, phase velocity $\omega/k$ and index $ck/\omega$ (9.129).
> 2. *Fields.* The fields are transverse; for $\tilde{\vec E}_0 = \tilde E_0\hat x$, $\tilde{\vec B} = (\tilde k/\omega)\tilde E_0e^{-\kappa z}e^{i(kz - \omega t)}\hat y$ (9.131). With $\tilde k = Ke^{i\phi}$, $K = \omega\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}\,[1 + (\sigma/\varepsilon\omega)^2]^{1/4}$, $\phi = \tan^{-1}(\kappa/k)$, the real fields are (9.133)–(9.138)
>
> $$
> \vec E = E_0e^{-\kappa z}\cos(kz - \omega t + \delta_E)\,\hat x, \qquad \vec B = \frac{K}{\omega}E_0e^{-\kappa z}\cos(kz - \omega t + \delta_E + \phi)\,\hat y :
> $$
>
>    $\vec B$ **lags** $\vec E$ by the phase $\phi$, and $B_0/E_0 = K/\omega = \sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}\,[1 + (\sigma/\varepsilon\omega)^2]^{1/4}$.
> 3. *Energy.* The time-averaged energy density and intensity are (Griffiths Problem 9.21)
>
> $$
> \langle u\rangle = \frac{k^2}{2\mu\omega^2}E_0^2e^{-2\kappa z}, \qquad I = \frac{k}{2\mu\omega}E_0^2e^{-2\kappa z} ,
> $$
>
>    with magnetic and electric parts in the ratio $\sqrt{1 + (\sigma/\varepsilon\omega)^2} \ge 1$; the intensity lost per unit length, $-dI/dz$, equals the Joule heating $\langle\vec J\cdot\vec E\rangle = \tfrac12\sigma E_0^2e^{-2\kappa z}$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.4.1, eqs. (9.122)–(9.138), Figure 9.18, Problem 9.21 (solved here)*

^thm-b10-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Wave equations.* As in [[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]]: the curl of Faraday's law with $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ gives $-\nabla^2\vec E = -\partial_t(\nabla\times\vec B) = -\mu\varepsilon\,\partial_t^2\vec E - \mu\sigma\,\partial_t\vec E$; the curl of the last equation gives $-\nabla^2\vec B = \mu\varepsilon\,\partial_t(\nabla\times\vec E) + \mu\sigma\nabla\times\vec E = -\mu\varepsilon\,\partial_t^2\vec B - \mu\sigma\,\partial_t\vec B$.
>
> *Dispersion relation.* On $e^{i(\tilde kz - \omega t)}$, $\nabla^2 \to -\tilde k^2$ and $\partial_t \to -i\omega$, so $-\tilde k^2 = -\mu\varepsilon\omega^2 - i\mu\sigma\omega$. Write $\tilde k = k + i\kappa$ and $x = \sigma/\varepsilon\omega$: $k^2 - \kappa^2 = \mu\varepsilon\omega^2$, $2k\kappa = \mu\sigma\omega$, and $k^2 + \kappa^2 = |\tilde k^2| = \mu\varepsilon\omega^2\sqrt{1 + x^2}$. Adding and subtracting, $k^2 = \tfrac12\mu\varepsilon\omega^2(\sqrt{1 + x^2} + 1)$ and $\kappa^2 = \tfrac12\mu\varepsilon\omega^2(\sqrt{1 + x^2} - 1)$; the roots with $k, \kappa > 0$ describe a wave moving along $+z$ and decaying in that direction ($k\kappa > 0$ as required). Then $e^{i\tilde kz} = e^{-\kappa z}e^{ikz}$, and $K^2 = k^2 + \kappa^2$ gives the stated $K$.
>
> *Fields.* $\nabla\cdot\tilde{\vec E} = i\tilde k\tilde E_{0z}e^{i(\cdots)} = 0$ forces $\tilde E_{0z} = 0$ (as $\tilde k \ne 0$), and likewise for $\vec B$. Faraday's law gives $i\tilde k\,\hat z\times\tilde{\vec E} = i\omega\tilde{\vec B}$; for $\tilde{\vec E}$ along $\hat x$ this is (9.131), and the last Maxwell equation gives the same. Writing $\tilde E_0 = E_0e^{i\delta_E}$, $\tilde B_0 = B_0e^{i\delta_B}$, (9.131) says $B_0e^{i\delta_B} = (K/\omega)e^{i\phi}E_0e^{i\delta_E}$, so $\delta_B - \delta_E = \phi$; taking real parts gives the real fields. At fixed $z$, $\cos(\cdots - \omega t + \delta_E + \phi)$ reaches its maximum a time $\phi/\omega$ after $\cos(\cdots - \omega t + \delta_E)$: a lag.
>
> *Energy.* By $\langle\operatorname{Re}\tilde a\operatorname{Re}\tilde b\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(\tilde a\tilde b^*)$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|WO Theorem §B1.1.6]]), $\langle u_E\rangle = \tfrac14\varepsilon E_0^2e^{-2\kappa z}$ and $\langle u_B\rangle = \tfrac{1}{4\mu}(K^2/\omega^2)E_0^2e^{-2\kappa z}$, whose ratio is $K^2/\mu\varepsilon\omega^2 = \sqrt{1 + x^2}$. Using $\varepsilon = (k^2 - \kappa^2)/\mu\omega^2$ and $K^2 = k^2 + \kappa^2$, the sum is $\tfrac{1}{4\mu\omega^2}(2k^2)E_0^2e^{-2\kappa z}$. With $\vec S = \mu^{-1}\vec E\times\vec B$ ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]]), $I = \tfrac{1}{2\mu}\operatorname{Re}(\tilde E\tilde B^*) = \tfrac{1}{2\mu}\operatorname{Re}(\tilde k^*/\omega)E_0^2e^{-2\kappa z} = \tfrac{k}{2\mu\omega}E_0^2e^{-2\kappa z}$. Finally $-dI/dz = 2\kappa\,\tfrac{k}{2\mu\omega}E_0^2e^{-2\kappa z} = \tfrac{\mu\sigma\omega}{2\mu\omega}E_0^2e^{-2\kappa z} = \tfrac12\sigma E_0^2e^{-2\kappa z}$, by $2k\kappa = \mu\sigma\omega$, which is $\langle\sigma E^2\rangle$: Poynting's theorem in the steady state.

> [!caution] Caution: κ, α and d in Griffiths and in Waves and Optics
> Griffiths writes the complex **wave number** as $\tilde k = k + i\kappa$, so his $\kappa$ (in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$) is the amplitude attenuation constant, his absorption coefficient is $\alpha = 2\kappa$ (eq. (9.167)), and his skin depth is $d = 1/\kappa$. Waves and Optics, following Fowles, writes $\tilde k = k + i\alpha$ and the complex **index** $\tilde n = n + i\kappa$ ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-3|WO Def. §B13.1.3]]), so its $\kappa$ is dimensionless and its $\alpha$ is Griffiths' $\kappa$: $\kappa_{\text{Griffiths}} = \alpha_{\text{WO}} = \kappa_{\text{WO}}\,\omega/c$, and $d_{\text{Griffiths}} = \delta_{\text{WO}}$. The intensity absorption coefficient is the same quantity in both, written $\alpha = 2\kappa$ by Griffiths and $2\alpha$ by Waves and Optics. Level A's $\kappa$ is yet another quantity, the dielectric constant ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^cau-b5-3-1|§B5.3, Caution: κ, ε_r, χ_e]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.4.1, eqs. (9.125)–(9.128), and §9.4.3, eq. (9.167)*

^cau-b10-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.3.3: Good and Poor Conductors
> The ratio $\sigma/\varepsilon\omega$, conduction current over displacement current ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^ex-b8-4-2|Example §B8.4.2]]), equivalently $1/\omega\tau$ with $\tau = \varepsilon/\sigma$, separates two regimes of Theorem §B10.3.2:
> 1. **Good conductor**, $\sigma \gg \varepsilon\omega$:
>
> $$
> k \approx \kappa \approx \sqrt{\frac{\mu\sigma\omega}{2}}, \qquad d \approx \sqrt{\frac{2}{\mu\sigma\omega}} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi}, \qquad \phi \approx 45^\circ, \qquad \frac{B_0}{E_0} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\mu\sigma}{\omega}} ,
> $$
>
>    with $\lambda = 2\pi/k$ the wavelength inside the conductor: the wave dies out within a fraction $1/2\pi$ of its own wavelength, $\vec B$ lags $\vec E$ by an eighth of a period, and nearly all the energy is magnetic.
> 2. **Poor conductor**, $\sigma \ll \varepsilon\omega$:
>
> $$
> k \approx \omega\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}, \qquad d \approx \frac2\sigma\sqrt{\frac\varepsilon\mu}, \qquad \phi \approx \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon\omega} \approx 0 ,
> $$
>
>    an almost undisturbed wave of [[§B10.2 Waves in Linear Media, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]] that is attenuated over a length independent of frequency.
>
> For $\mu = \mu_0$, $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0$, case 1 is [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-4|WO Theorem §B13.2.4]] (1).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 9.20 (solved here)*

^thm-b10-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $x = \sigma/\varepsilon\omega$. *1.* For $x \gg 1$, $\sqrt{1 + x^2} \pm 1 \approx x$, so $k \approx \kappa \approx \omega\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu x/2} = \sqrt{\mu\sigma\omega/2}$, $d = 1/\kappa = \sqrt{2/\mu\sigma\omega}$, and since $k \approx \kappa$, $d = 1/k = \lambda/2\pi$. Then $\phi = \tan^{-1}(\kappa/k) \to \tan^{-1}1 = 45^\circ$, and $K/\omega \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}\,x^{1/2} = \sqrt{\mu\sigma/\omega}$. The energy ratio $\sqrt{1 + x^2} \approx x \gg 1$. *2.* For $x \ll 1$, $\sqrt{1 + x^2} \approx 1 + x^2/2$, so $k \approx \omega\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}$ and $\kappa \approx \omega\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu/2}\,(x/\sqrt2) = \tfrac\sigma2\sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon}$, whose inverse is $d$; $\phi \approx \kappa/k = \sigma/2\varepsilon\omega$.

^der-b10-3-3

*Uses:* [[§B10.3★ Conductors and Dispersion#^thm-b10-3-2|Theorem §B10.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: In a good conductor the field diffuses
> When $\sigma \gg \varepsilon\omega$ the displacement current is negligible and the modified wave equation becomes $\nabla^2\vec B = \mu\sigma\,\partial_t\vec B$: a **diffusion equation** for the field, with diffusion constant $D = 1/\mu\sigma$. A field oscillating at a surface penetrates as a damped wave $e^{-z/d}\cos(z/d - \omega t)$ with $d = \sqrt{2D/\omega}$, exactly as a temperature oscillation penetrates the ground with its thermal skin depth ([[§B5.3★ The Thermal Diffusion Equation#^thm-b5-3-4|TH Theorem §B5.3.4]]); this is the "electromagnetic analogue" that Thermal Physics points to. The equal real and imaginary parts of $\tilde k$, the $45^\circ$ lag and $d \propto \omega^{-1/2}$ are all signatures of diffusion rather than propagation. Magnetic fields "soak" into a good conductor slowly: a field change at the surface reaches depth $L$ in a time of order $\mu\sigma L^2$.
>
> *Source: added here, from Griffiths eq. (9.122) with the displacement current dropped*

## Reflection at a conductor

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.3.4: Reflection at a Conducting Surface; the Perfect Conductor
> Let the plane $z = 0$ separate a nonconducting linear medium 1 ($\mu_1$, speed $v_1$) from an Ohmic conductor 2 ($\mu_2$, complex wave number $\tilde k_2$), and let a plane wave polarized along $\hat x$ arrive from medium 1 at normal incidence. An Ohmic conductor carries no free surface current, and at normal incidence no free surface charge is formed. Then (Griffiths eqs. (9.143)–(9.147))
>
> $$
> \tilde E_{0_R} = \frac{1 - \tilde\beta}{1 + \tilde\beta}\,\tilde E_{0_I}, \qquad \tilde E_{0_T} = \frac{2}{1 + \tilde\beta}\,\tilde E_{0_I}, \qquad \tilde\beta = \frac{\mu_1v_1}{\mu_2\,\omega}\,\tilde k_2 ,
> $$
>
> formally [[§B10.2 Waves in Linear Media, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]] with a complex $\tilde\beta$, so that the reflected wave is shifted in phase by an angle other than $0$ or $\pi$. In the limit of a **perfect conductor**, $\sigma \to \infty$, $|\tilde k_2| \to \infty$ and (Griffiths eq. (9.148))
>
> $$
> \tilde E_{0_R} = -\tilde E_{0_I}, \qquad \tilde E_{0_T} = 0 :
> $$
>
> total reflection with a phase change of $\pi$, the fields vanishing inside ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-7|Theorem §B8.4.7]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.4.2, eqs. (9.139)–(9.148)*

^thm-b10-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *No surface current.* A finite surface current density would be a finite current in a layer of zero thickness, requiring infinite $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$, hence infinite $\vec E$; so $\vec K_f = \vec 0$ for finite $\sigma$. The boundary conditions are then those of [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-6|Theorem §B8.4.6]] for linear media with $\vec K_f = \vec 0$ (Griffiths eq. (9.139)). All fields are tangential at normal incidence, so the normal $\vec D$ condition gives $\sigma_f = 0$ and the normal $\vec B$ condition is empty.
>
> *Amplitudes.* Incident and reflected waves are as in Theorem §B10.2.3; the transmitted wave is $\tilde E_{0_T}e^{i(\tilde k_2z - \omega t)}\hat x$ with $\tilde{\vec B}_T = (\tilde k_2/\omega)\tilde E_{0_T}e^{i(\cdots)}\hat y$ ([[§B10.3★ Conductors and Dispersion#^thm-b10-3-2|Theorem §B10.3.2]]). Continuity of $E^\parallel$: $\tilde E_{0_I} + \tilde E_{0_R} = \tilde E_{0_T}$; continuity of $B^\parallel/\mu$: $(\tilde E_{0_I} - \tilde E_{0_R})/\mu_1v_1 = (\tilde k_2/\mu_2\omega)\tilde E_{0_T}$, i.e. $\tilde E_{0_I} - \tilde E_{0_R} = \tilde\beta\tilde E_{0_T}$ (9.144)–(9.146). Solving as in Theorem §B10.2.3 gives (9.147). As $\sigma \to \infty$, $|\tilde k_2| \ge \kappa_2 \approx \sqrt{\mu_2\sigma\omega/2} \to \infty$ (Theorem §B10.3.3), so $|\tilde\beta| \to \infty$, $(1 - \tilde\beta)/(1 + \tilde\beta) \to -1$ and $2/(1 + \tilde\beta) \to 0$.

^der-b10-3-4

*Uses:* [[§B10.2 Waves in Linear Media, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]], [[§B10.3★ Conductors and Dispersion#^thm-b10-3-2|Theorem §B10.3.2]], [[§B10.3★ Conductors and Dispersion#^thm-b10-3-3|Theorem §B10.3.3]], [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-6|Theorem §B8.4.6]], [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-7|Theorem §B8.4.7]]

> [!remark] Remark: How this connects to the optics of metals
> If medium 1 is vacuum and $\mu_2 = \mu_0$, then $\tilde\beta = c\tilde k_2/\omega = \tilde n$, the complex index, and $r = (1 - \tilde n)/(1 + \tilde n)$ is [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-5|WO Theorem §B13.2.5]], with the reflectance $R = |r|^2$ and its phase worked out there. For a good conductor $\tilde n \approx (1 + i)\,c/\omega d$, and $R \approx 1 - 2\omega d/c$, the Hagen–Rubens formula ([[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-6|WO Theorem §B13.2.6]]). Griffiths' remark that the glass of a mirror only supports and protects a silver film, which does the reflecting, rests on the skin depth being far smaller than any practical film. The perfect conductor, inside which $\vec E = \vec B = \vec 0$, is the limit used for the walls of wave guides ([[§B10.4★ Guided Waves|§B10.4★]]) and for the pressure of light on a mirror ([[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^ex-b10-1-1|Example §B10.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.4.2, after eq. (9.148)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B10.3.1: Skin Depths of Copper and Sea Water
> Use Griffiths' Table 7.1 resistivities: copper $1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m$ ($\sigma = 5.95\times10^7\ \mathrm{S/m}$), sea water $0.2\ \Omega\,\mathrm m$ ($\sigma = 5\ \mathrm{S/m}$), with $\varepsilon = 81\varepsilon_0$ for water (Griffiths Problem 7.40) and $\mu = \mu_0$. The exact $d = 1/\kappa$ of [[§B10.3★ Conductors and Dispersion#^thm-b10-3-2|Theorem §B10.3.2]] gives
>
> | $f$ | copper | sea water |
> |---|---|---|
> | $60\ \mathrm{Hz}$ | $8.4\ \mathrm{mm}$ | $29\ \mathrm m$ |
> | $1\ \mathrm{kHz}$ | $2.1\ \mathrm{mm}$ | $7.1\ \mathrm m$ |
> | $1\ \mathrm{MHz}$ | $65\ \mu\mathrm m$ | $23\ \mathrm{cm}$ |
> | $1\ \mathrm{GHz}$ | $2.1\ \mu\mathrm m$ | $1.07\ \mathrm{cm}$ |
> | $10\ \mathrm{GHz}$ | $0.65\ \mu\mathrm m$ | $0.96\ \mathrm{cm}$ |
>
> The copper column is the good-conductor skin depth of [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^ex-b13-2-1|WO Example §B13.2.1]] (there with $\sigma = 5.8\times10^7\ \mathrm{S/m}$), repeated as the reference line; what is new here is the sea water. Copper is a good conductor throughout: $\sigma/\varepsilon_0\omega = 1$ only at $\omega \sim 7\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so $d = \sqrt{2/\mu_0\sigma\omega} \approx 6.5\ \mathrm{cm}/\sqrt{f/\mathrm{Hz}}$. Sea water is a good conductor only below $\sigma/2\pi\varepsilon = 1.1\ \mathrm{GHz}$ (the crossover of [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^ex-b8-4-2|Example §B8.4.2]]); above it the skin depth stops shrinking and approaches the poor-conductor value $(2/\sigma)\sqrt{\varepsilon/\mu} = 9.6\ \mathrm{mm}$ (Theorem §B10.3.3). Radio reaches a submerged receiver only at very low frequencies, where $d$ is metres or more. For a microwave experiment at $10\ \mathrm{GHz}$, a silver coating ($1.59\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m$) has $d = 0.63\ \mu\mathrm m$, so a few micrometres of silver carry all of the current (Griffiths Problem 9.19(b)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Table 7.1, Problems 7.40, 9.19, 9.20 (computed here)*

> [!example] Example §B10.3.2: The Silver Mirror, and the Limits of the Model
> Griffiths Problem 9.22 asks for the reflectance of silver ($\sigma = 6\times10^7\ \mathrm{S/m}$, $\mu = \mu_0$) at $\omega = 4\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, from air; take $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0$ for the silver as well (immaterial, since $\sigma \gg \varepsilon_0\omega$). Here $\sigma/\varepsilon_0\omega = 1.7\times10^3$, a good conductor in the sense of Theorem §B10.3.3: $k \approx \kappa \approx \sqrt{\mu_0\sigma\omega/2} = 3.9\times10^8\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $d = 2.6\ \mathrm{nm}$, and
>
> $$
> \tilde\beta = \frac{c}{\omega}\,\tilde k_2 = 29.1\,(1 + i), \qquad R = \Bigl|\frac{1 - \tilde\beta}{1 + \tilde\beta}\Bigr|^2 = \frac{28.1^2 + 29.1^2}{30.1^2 + 29.1^2} = 0.934
> $$
>
> by [[§B10.3★ Conductors and Dispersion#^thm-b10-3-4|Theorem §B10.3.4]] (the Hagen–Rubens approximation gives $0.931$). This is the answer within Model §B10.3.1, but the model does not apply: at $\omega = 4\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ the field changes many times during one collision time of the electrons, $\omega\tau_c \gg 1$, and the conductivity is mostly imaginary. The free-electron (Drude) model of Waves and Optics, which keeps the electrons' inertia, puts silver in the reflecting regime below its plasma frequency, with $R$ close to $1$ and a penetration depth of tens of nanometres ($R = 0.998$, $\delta = 45\ \mathrm{nm}$ at $1\ \mu\mathrm m$ in [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^ex-b13-2-2|WO Example §B13.2.2]]; [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-4|WO Theorem §B13.2.4]] (2)). The static-$\sigma$ model thus underestimates both the reflectance and the penetration of visible light into silver; Griffiths' statement that the skin depth of silver at optical frequencies is below $100\ \text{Å}$ is a result of that model.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 9.22 and §9.4.2 (p. 417) (solved here); comparison with WO Example §B13.2.2*

> [!remark] Remark: A collisionless plasma, and the ionosphere
> For free electrons without collisions (a dilute plasma) the induced current is a quarter period out of phase with $\vec E$ and dissipates nothing; the medium behaves as a dielectric with $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} = \varepsilon_0(1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2)$ and the dispersion relation $c^2k^2 = \omega^2 - \omega_p^2$ (Nelson eqs. (53.7)–(53.10); [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-4|WO Theorem §B13.2.4]] (3), [[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^thm-b6-2-1|WO Theorem §B6.2.1]]). Above $\omega_p$ waves propagate with phase velocity $c/\sqrt{1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2} > c$ and group velocity $c\sqrt{1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2} < c$, whose product is $c^2$; below $\omega_p$, $k$ is imaginary, the wave cannot enter and, with no dissipation, it is reflected. Nelson's example is the ionosphere, with electron density $n_e \sim 10^{11}\ \mathrm m^{-3}$: by [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-2|WO Def. §B13.1.2]], $\omega_p = 56.4\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\times\sqrt{10^{11}} = 1.8\times10^7\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $f_p \approx 3\ \mathrm{MHz}$. AM broadcasts near $1\ \mathrm{MHz}$ are reflected back to the ground (long-distance "skip"), while FM, television and microwaves, including the cosmic microwave background, pass through. The same dispersion relation, with a cutoff frequency in place of $\omega_p$, governs waves in a guide ([[§B10.4★ Guided Waves|§B10.4★]]).
>
> *Source: Nelson ch. 53, §53.3.1–53.3.3, eqs. (53.7)–(53.10)*
