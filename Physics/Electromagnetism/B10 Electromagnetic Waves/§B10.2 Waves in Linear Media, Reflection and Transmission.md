How do electromagnetic waves travel through glass or water, and how much of a wave is reflected where two such media meet? Level A's plane-wave model stops at the surface of matter ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^mod-a12-2-1|Model §A12.2.1]], *Fails*), and Waves and Optics treats light in transparent, non-magnetic dielectrics: plane harmonic waves ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-2|WO Theorem §B8.1.2]]), the laws of reflection and refraction from phase matching ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-2|WO Theorem §B9.1.2]]) and the Fresnel equations ([[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|WO Theorem §B9.2.1]]). This section starts from Maxwell's equations in matter ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-5|Theorem §B8.4.5]]) for media that may also be magnetic, gets the speed, energy and intensity of waves in them from the general theorems of Chapter B9, and derives reflection and transmission at normal incidence in Griffiths' form, with $R + T = 1$ checked from the Poynting vector. For oblique incidence it points to Waves and Optics and records what the permeability changes.

## Waves in a linear medium

> [!model] Model §B10.2.1: Linear Homogeneous Medium without Free Sources
> A **linear homogeneous medium** has $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$ and $\vec H = \vec B/\mu$ with constants $\varepsilon$, $\mu$ (Griffiths eq. (9.66); constitutive relations: [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^law-b5-3-1|Law §B5.3.1]], [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^law-b7-2-5|Law §B7.2.5]], [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^rem-b8-4-3|§B8.4, Remark: Constitutive relations in time-dependent fields]]), and in the region considered no free charge and no free current. Maxwell's equations in matter then reduce to the vacuum equations with $\mu_0\varepsilon_0$ replaced by $\mu\varepsilon$ (Griffiths eqs. (9.65), (9.67)):
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec E = 0, \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec B = 0, \qquad \nabla\times\vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla\times\vec B = \mu\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> - *Assumes:* linear isotropic response at the frequency considered, with real $\varepsilon$ and $\mu$ (no absorption), and $\varepsilon$, $\mu$ independent of position and time.
> - *Fails:* in absorbing media and conductors ([[§B10.3★ Conductors and Dispersion|§B10.3★]]), in crystals ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-1|WO Model §B8.3.1]]), and across frequencies, since $\varepsilon$ and $\mu$ depend on $\omega$ (dispersion, [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model|WO §B13.1]]). With $\mu = \mu_0$ this is [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|WO Model §B8.1.1]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.3.1, eqs. (9.65)–(9.67) · Nelson ch. 47, §47.3.3*

^mod-b10-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.2: Plane Waves in a Linear Medium
> In the medium of Model §B10.2.1, all results of [[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum|§B10.1]] hold with $\varepsilon_0 \to \varepsilon$, $\mu_0 \to \mu$, $c \to v$. In particular, waves travel at (Griffiths eqs. (9.68)–(9.70))
>
> $$
> v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}} = \frac cn, \qquad n = \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon\mu}{\varepsilon_0\mu_0}} \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\quad(\mu \approx \mu_0) ,
> $$
>
> a monochromatic plane wave has $\omega = kv$, transverse fields and $\tilde{\vec B}_0 = \hat k\times\tilde{\vec E}_0/v$, so $B_0 = E_0/v$; and its energy density, Poynting vector and intensity are (Griffiths eqs. (9.71)–(9.73))
>
> $$
> u = \frac12\Bigl(\varepsilon E^2 + \frac1\mu B^2\Bigr), \qquad \vec S = \frac1\mu\,\vec E\times\vec B, \qquad I = \frac12\varepsilon vE_0^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.3.1, eqs. (9.68)–(9.73) · Nelson ch. 47, §47.3.3*

^thm-b10-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The equations of Model §B10.2.1 are those used in the derivations of [[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]] and [[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]] with the single constant $\mu_0\varepsilon_0$ replaced by $\mu\varepsilon$; every step goes through, giving $\nabla^2\vec E = \mu\varepsilon\,\partial_t^2\vec E$, speed $1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}$, and $\tilde{\vec B}_0 = (k/\omega)\hat k\times\tilde{\vec E}_0$ with $k/\omega = 1/v$. Since $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$, $v = c\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0/\mu\varepsilon} = c/n$; with $\mu = \mu_0$, $n = \sqrt{\varepsilon/\varepsilon_0} = \sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$. The energy density and Poynting vector are those of Poynting's theorem in linear media ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]]): $u = \tfrac12(\vec E\cdot\vec D + \vec B\cdot\vec H) = \tfrac12(\varepsilon E^2 + B^2/\mu)$ and $\vec S = \vec E\times\vec H = \mu^{-1}\vec E\times\vec B$. For the plane wave, $B^2/\mu = E^2/\mu v^2 = \varepsilon E^2$, so $u = \varepsilon E^2$, $S = \mu^{-1}E(E/v) = \varepsilon vE^2 = uv$, and averaging $\cos^2$ gives $I = \tfrac12\varepsilon vE_0^2$.

^der-b10-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B10.2 Waves in Linear Media, Reflection and Transmission#^mod-b10-2-1|Model §B10.2.1]], [[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]], [[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]], [[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]]

> [!definition] Definition §B10.2.1: Impedance of a Linear Medium
> The (wave) **impedance** of a linear medium is the ratio of the amplitudes of $\vec E$ and $\vec H$ in a travelling plane wave,
>
> $$
> Z = \frac{E_0}{H_0} = \mu v = \sqrt{\frac\mu\varepsilon}, \qquad Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0}} = 376.7\ \Omega \ \text{(vacuum)} .
> $$
>
> For a non-magnetic medium $Z = Z_0/n$, the optical impedance of [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-2|WO Theorem §B9.2.2]]; in general $Z = Z_0\sqrt{\mu_r/\varepsilon_r}$ while $n = \sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r}$, so index and impedance are independent properties of a magnetic medium.
>
> *Source: added here, from Griffiths eqs. (9.66), (9.68) (Griffiths uses $\beta = Z_1/Z_2$ without naming $Z$) · WO Theorem §B8.1.2 for $Z_0$*

^def-b10-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Which ε belongs in n?
> $\varepsilon$ depends on frequency, and $n = \sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$ holds only with $\varepsilon_r$ at the frequency of the wave. Water has static dielectric constant $80.1$ (Griffiths Table 4.2), which would give $n = 8.9$, while for visible light $n = 1.33$ (Griffiths' footnote 11): the alignment of the permanent dipoles of water molecules is far too slow to follow optical fields (Nelson ch. 47, §47.3.5; [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^rem-b13-1-4|WO §B13.1, Remark: Water, with dielectric constant 80 and index 1.33]]). Static tables ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics|§B5.3]]) do not give optical indices.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.3.1, footnote 11 · Nelson ch. 47, §47.3.5*

## Reflection and transmission at normal incidence

At an interface between two linear media without free surface charge or current, the fields obey (Griffiths eq. (9.74)) $\varepsilon_1E_1^\perp = \varepsilon_2E_2^\perp$, $B_1^\perp = B_2^\perp$, $\vec E_1^\parallel = \vec E_2^\parallel$ and $\vec B_1^\parallel/\mu_1 = \vec B_2^\parallel/\mu_2$: the linear-media form of the general boundary conditions ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-6|Theorem §B8.4.6]]; for $\mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu_0$, [[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-1|WO Theorem §B9.1.1]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.3: Reflection and Transmission at Normal Incidence
> Let the plane $z = 0$ separate medium 1 ($z < 0$; $\varepsilon_1$, $\mu_1$, speed $v_1$) from medium 2 ($z > 0$; $\varepsilon_2$, $\mu_2$, $v_2$), and let a plane wave polarized along $\hat x$ arrive from the left. Write (Griffiths eqs. (9.75)–(9.77))
>
> $$
> \tilde{\vec E}_I = \tilde E_{0_I}e^{i(k_1z - \omega t)}\hat x, \qquad \tilde{\vec E}_R = \tilde E_{0_R}e^{i(-k_1z - \omega t)}\hat x, \qquad \tilde{\vec E}_T = \tilde E_{0_T}e^{i(k_2z - \omega t)}\hat x ,
> $$
>
> with $\tilde{\vec B} = \hat k\times\tilde{\vec E}/v$ for each wave ($\hat k = \hat z$, $-\hat z$, $\hat z$). Then the reflected and transmitted waves have the same frequency and polarization as the incident one, and (Griffiths eqs. (9.78)–(9.82))
>
> $$
> \tilde E_{0_R} = \frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}\,\tilde E_{0_I}, \qquad \tilde E_{0_T} = \frac{2}{1 + \beta}\,\tilde E_{0_I}, \qquad \beta = \frac{\mu_1v_1}{\mu_2v_2} = \frac{\mu_1n_2}{\mu_2n_1} = \frac{Z_1}{Z_2} .
> $$
>
> The **reflection** and **transmission coefficients**, the fractions of the incident intensity reflected and transmitted, are
>
> $$
> R = \frac{I_R}{I_I} = \Bigl(\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}\Bigr)^2, \qquad T = \frac{I_T}{I_I} = \frac{\varepsilon_2v_2}{\varepsilon_1v_1}\Bigl(\frac{2}{1 + \beta}\Bigr)^2 = \frac{4\beta}{(1 + \beta)^2}, \qquad R + T = 1 .
> $$
>
> For $\mu_1 = \mu_2$ (most media), $\beta = n_2/n_1 = v_1/v_2$ and (Griffiths eqs. (9.83)–(9.88))
>
> $$
> \tilde E_{0_R} = \frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1 + n_2}\,\tilde E_{0_I}, \qquad \tilde E_{0_T} = \frac{2n_1}{n_1 + n_2}\,\tilde E_{0_I}, \qquad R = \Bigl(\frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1 + n_2}\Bigr)^2, \qquad T = \frac{4n_1n_2}{(n_1 + n_2)^2} .
> $$
>
> The transmitted wave is always in phase with the incident one; the reflected wave is in phase if $Z_2 > Z_1$ (for equal $\mu$: $n_2 < n_1$, $v_2 > v_1$) and inverted if $Z_2 < Z_1$. For air to glass, $n_2 = 1.5$: $R = 0.04$, $T = 0.96$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.3.2, eqs. (9.75)–(9.88), Problems 9.14 and 9.15 (solved here)*

^thm-b10-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Frequency.* All three waves have the time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$ of the source; a different frequency could not satisfy the boundary conditions at all times ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-2|WO Theorem §B9.1.2]]).
>
> *Fields.* For the incident and transmitted waves $\hat z\times\hat x = \hat y$ gives $\tilde{\vec B} = (\tilde E/v)\hat y$; for the reflected wave $(-\hat z)\times\hat x = -\hat y$ gives $\tilde{\vec B}_R = -(\tilde E_{0_R}/v_1)e^{i(-k_1z - \omega t)}\hat y$, the minus sign that makes its Poynting vector point back to the left. No field has a component normal to the interface, so the normal conditions hold trivially. At $z = 0$, after cancelling $e^{-i\omega t}$, continuity of $E^\parallel$ and of $B^\parallel/\mu$ give
>
> $$
> \tilde E_{0_I} + \tilde E_{0_R} = \tilde E_{0_T}, \qquad \frac{1}{\mu_1v_1}\bigl(\tilde E_{0_I} - \tilde E_{0_R}\bigr) = \frac{1}{\mu_2v_2}\tilde E_{0_T} \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \tilde E_{0_I} - \tilde E_{0_R} = \beta\tilde E_{0_T}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (9.78)–(9.80)). Adding: $2\tilde E_{0_I} = (1 + \beta)\tilde E_{0_T}$; then $\tilde E_{0_R} = \tilde E_{0_T} - \tilde E_{0_I} = \tfrac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}\tilde E_{0_I}$. Since $v = c/\sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r}$, $\mu v = \sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon} = Z$ and $\beta = Z_1/Z_2$; with $v_j = c/n_j$, $\beta = \mu_1n_2/\mu_2n_1$.
>
> *Polarization.* Suppose the reflected and transmitted waves also had components along $\hat y$, amplitudes $\tilde E_{R,y}$, $\tilde E_{T,y}$. For a field $E_y\hat y$ travelling along $\pm\hat z$, $\tilde{\vec B} = \mp(E_y/v)\hat x$, so the $y$-components of $E^\parallel$ and the $x$-components of $B^\parallel/\mu$ give $\tilde E_{R,y} = \tilde E_{T,y}$ and $\tilde E_{R,y}/\mu_1v_1 = -\tilde E_{T,y}/\mu_2v_2$, that is $(1 + \beta)\tilde E_{T,y} = 0$: both vanish, since $\beta > 0$. The polarization is unchanged.
>
> *Energy.* By [[§B10.2 Waves in Linear Media, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]], $I = \tfrac12\varepsilon vE_0^2$ for each real amplitude $E_0 = |\tilde E_0|$. The reflected wave is in medium 1, so $R = |\tilde E_{0_R}/\tilde E_{0_I}|^2$; the transmitted wave carries the factor $\varepsilon_2v_2/\varepsilon_1v_1$. Now $\varepsilon v = \varepsilon/\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu} = \sqrt{\varepsilon/\mu} = 1/Z$, so $\varepsilon_2v_2/\varepsilon_1v_1 = Z_1/Z_2 = \beta$ and $T = 4\beta/(1 + \beta)^2$. Then $R + T = [(1 - \beta)^2 + 4\beta]/(1 + \beta)^2 = (1 + \beta)^2/(1 + \beta)^2 = 1$: the energy per unit time arriving at a patch of the interface equals that leaving it, as Poynting's theorem ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]]) requires for a surface without sources. For $\mu_1 = \mu_2$, $\beta = n_2/n_1$, and multiplying numerators and denominators by $n_1$ gives the second set of formulas. Since $\beta > 0$, $\tilde E_{0_T}/\tilde E_{0_I} > 0$, and the sign of $1 - \beta$ is that of $Z_2 - Z_1$.

> [!remark] Remark: How this relates to Waves and Optics and to strings
> - For $\mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu_0$ the amplitude and power formulas are [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-2|WO Theorem §B9.2.2]], with the same sign convention (reflected $\vec E$ measured along the incident $\hat x$; [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^def-b9-2-1|WO Def. §B9.2.1]]). What is added here is the permeability, which enters only through the impedances: $r = (Z_2 - Z_1)/(Z_2 + Z_1)$ in every case, and $\beta = Z_1/Z_2$ is Griffiths' name for the impedance ratio.
> - Griffiths notes that (9.83) is identical to the reflection of a wave on a string at a knot (his eq. (9.30)), with the wave speeds in the same places; Waves and Optics develops this impedance analogy ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-3|WO Theorem §B5.2.3]], [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^rem-b9-2-1|WO §B9.2, Remark: Light at normal incidence is a wave on a string]]).
> - Where reflection matters in practice (lens surfaces, thin films, multilayer mirrors) the optics is in Waves and Optics: a single layer between two media, Griffiths' Problem 9.36, is the case of one layer in [[§B10.5 Multilayer Films#^thm-b10-5-3|WO Theorem §B10.5.3]] ([[§B10.5 Multilayer Films#^thm-b10-5-2|WO Theorem §B10.5.2]] for any number of layers).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.3.2, after eq. (9.82)*

> [!remark] Remark: Oblique incidence
> Griffiths §9.3.3 derives, for a plane wave hitting the interface at an angle, the three laws of geometrical optics from the requirement that the exponential factors agree all over the interface at all times, $\vec k_I\cdot\vec r = \vec k_R\cdot\vec r = \vec k_T\cdot\vec r$ at $z = 0$ (his eqs. (9.93)–(9.100)): the wave vectors are coplanar with the normal, $\theta_R = \theta_I$, and $\sin\theta_T/\sin\theta_I = n_1/n_2$. That argument, and the Fresnel equations for both polarizations, Brewster's angle and total internal reflection, are developed in Waves and Optics ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-2|WO Theorem §B9.1.2]], [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|WO Theorem §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-4|WO Theorem §B9.2.4]], [[§B9.3 Total Internal Reflection and the Evanescent Wave#^thm-b9-3-1|WO Theorem §B9.3.1]]). Griffiths' version keeps the permeabilities: for polarization in the plane of incidence (his eq. (9.109))
>
> $$
> \tilde E_{0_R} = \frac{\alpha - \beta}{\alpha + \beta}\,\tilde E_{0_I}, \qquad \tilde E_{0_T} = \frac{2}{\alpha + \beta}\,\tilde E_{0_I}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\cos\theta_T}{\cos\theta_I}, \quad \beta = \frac{\mu_1n_2}{\mu_2n_1} ,
> $$
>
> which for $\mu_1 = \mu_2$ is the $r_p$, $t_p$ of WO Theorem §B9.2.1 in the same sign convention, and the reflection vanishes at Brewster's angle $\sin^2\theta_B = (1 - \beta^2)/[(n_1/n_2)^2 - \beta^2]$ (9.111), which reduces to $\tan\theta_B = n_2/n_1$ for equal permeabilities. The reflectance and transmittance carry the factor $\cos\theta_T/\cos\theta_I$ of the projected areas, $T = \alpha\beta\bigl(\tfrac{2}{\alpha + \beta}\bigr)^2$, and again $R + T = 1$ (Griffiths eqs. (9.113)–(9.116)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.3.3, eqs. (9.89)–(9.116)*

## Example

> [!example] Example §B10.2.1: A Magnetic Medium That Does Not Reflect
> A medium with $\varepsilon_r = \mu_r = 4$ has index $n = \sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r} = 4$, but impedance $Z = Z_0\sqrt{\mu_r/\varepsilon_r} = Z_0$ (Def. §B10.2.1). A wave arriving from vacuum at normal incidence therefore has $\beta = Z_0/Z = 1$, and by [[§B10.2 Waves in Linear Media, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]]
>
> $$
> R = 0, \qquad T = 1 ,
> $$
>
> although the wave slows to $c/4$ and its wavelength shrinks fourfold inside. A non-magnetic medium of the same index reflects $R = (3/5)^2 = 0.36$. Equal relative permittivity and permeability make the medium indistinguishable from vacuum as far as the ratio $E/H$ is concerned, and reflection is caused by a mismatch of that ratio, not of the speed. The ordinary formulas in terms of $n$ alone are therefore a property of non-magnetic media. At optical frequencies $\mu_r \approx 1$ for natural materials; magnetic responses at high frequency can be engineered in composites, for example from small conducting rings embedded in an insulator (Nelson ch. 47, §47.3.4), and materials with $\varepsilon_r \approx \mu_r$ over some band act as impedance-matched layers.
>
> *Source: worked out here from Griffiths eqs. (9.82), Problem 9.14 · Nelson ch. 47, §47.3.4 (composites with magnetic response)*
