*Sources: Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (4th ed.), §9.1–9.2 · PHY 422 (Thamm, Spring 2024) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 20 · Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lecture 13.*

What do Maxwell's equations predict in empty space, and how much energy and momentum does the prediction carry? Level A derived the wave equation for a plane wave along one axis with thin loops and boxes, found $E = cB$ with both fields transverse and in phase, and computed energy density, intensity and radiation pressure ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves|§A12.2]]–[[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure|§A12.4]]); Waves and Optics B owns plane harmonic waves and polarization in a transparent dielectric ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|WO Model §B8.1.1]], [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-2|WO Theorem §B8.1.2]]). This section derives the three-dimensional wave equation for every field component from the differential Maxwell equations, without assuming a plane wave, and then reads the energy, momentum and stress of a plane wave off the general conservation laws of Chapter B9. Radiation pressure, which level A obtained from the force on one charge plus a momentum-conservation argument, becomes a consequence of the field's momentum current.

## Conventions

> [!caution] Caution: Complex fields, and Waves and Optics' two conventions
> As in Griffiths, a monochromatic field is written as the real part of a complex field with time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$ (Griffiths eqs. (9.17)–(9.18)):
>
> $$
> \tilde f(z, t) = \tilde Ae^{i(kz - \omega t)}, \qquad f = \operatorname{Re}\tilde f, \qquad \tilde A = Ae^{i\delta} ,
> $$
>
> so that $\partial_t \to -i\omega$ and $\nabla \to i\vec k$ on a plane wave. This is the convention of Waves and Optics' optics chapters B8–B13; its Chapters B1–B7 use $e^{+i\omega t}$, with complex-conjugate amplitudes, and every complex coefficient or phase imported from there must be conjugated ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^cau-b8-1-1|WO §B8.1, Caution: The optics sign convention]]). Real, measured quantities (fields, intensities, pressures) are the same in both. Griffiths' one-dimensional preliminaries (§9.1: the wave equation on a string, sinusoidal waves, reflection at a junction, polarization) are developed in Waves and Optics ([[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^thm-a2-1-1|WO Theorem §A2.1.1]], [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-3|WO Theorem §B5.2.3]], [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^def-b8-1-1|WO Def. §B8.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.1.2, eqs. (9.15)–(9.18)*

## The wave equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.1: Wave Equations for E and B
> In a region of empty space without charge or current, every Cartesian component of $\vec E$ and of $\vec B$ obeys the three-dimensional wave equation (Griffiths eqs. (9.41)–(9.42))
>
> $$
> \nabla^2\vec E = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial^2\vec E}{\partial t^2}, \qquad \nabla^2\vec B = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial^2\vec B}{\partial t^2}, \qquad \text{with speed}\quad c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\mu_0}} = 3.00\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> The converse is false: a solution of the wave equations solves Maxwell's equations only if it also satisfies the first-order equations, which tie $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ together (Theorem §B10.1.3).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.2.1, eqs. (9.40)–(9.42) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 13 §2*

^thm-b10-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $\rho = 0$ and $\vec J = \vec 0$, Maxwell's equations ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]) read $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$, $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$, $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial_t\vec B$, $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E$ (Griffiths eq. (9.40)). Take the curl of Faraday's law and use $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec E) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec E) - \nabla^2\vec E$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]) and the exchange of time and space derivatives:
>
> $$
> \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec E) - \nabla^2\vec E = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\vec B) = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec E}{\partial t^2} .
> $$
>
> With $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ this is the equation for $\vec E$. The curl of the Ampère–Maxwell law gives in the same way $\nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec B) - \nabla^2\vec B = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t(\nabla\times\vec E) = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t^2\vec B$. In Cartesian components $\nabla^2\vec E = (\nabla^2E_x, \nabla^2E_y, \nabla^2E_z)$, so each component separately obeys $\nabla^2f = v^{-2}\partial_t^2f$ with $v = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$; numerically $1/\sqrt{(8.854\times10^{-12})(4\pi\times10^{-7})}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 2.998\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

^der-b10-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]], [[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level A
> [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|Theorem §A12.2.4]] obtained $\partial_x^2E_y = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t^2E_y$ for fields depending on $x$ and $t$ only, by applying the integral laws to thin loops. Here no shape of the wave is assumed: every component of every source-free field obeys the full wave equation, so plane waves, spherical waves and beams of any profile are all governed by $\nabla^2f = c^{-2}\partial_t^2f$ ([[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^thm-b7-1-4|WO Theorem §B7.1.4]]). The layer of the statement is the same (a theorem of Maxwell's equations), only its scope widens. The derivation also shows where the displacement current enters: without $\mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E$ in Ampère's law the curl of $\vec B$ would vanish in empty space and there would be no wave. The constants $\varepsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ come from Coulomb's law and the force between currents, measured with batteries and wires; that their combination is the measured speed of light is Maxwell's discovery (Griffiths §9.2.1; [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^rem-a12-2-1|§A12.2, Remark: What was assumed, and what was not]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.2.1*

## Monochromatic plane waves

> [!model] Model §B10.1.2: Monochromatic Plane Wave
> A **monochromatic plane wave** in vacuum with **propagation vector** $\vec k$ ($|\vec k| = k$, direction $\hat k$) and frequency $\omega = ck$ is a field of the form (Griffiths eqs. (9.43), (9.49))
>
> $$
> \tilde{\vec E}(\vec r, t) = \tilde{\vec E}_0\,e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)}, \qquad \tilde{\vec B}(\vec r, t) = \tilde{\vec B}_0\,e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)} ,
> $$
>
> with constant complex amplitudes, the physical fields being the real parts. The fields are uniform on every plane $\hat k\cdot\vec r = \text{const}$. A **linearly polarized** wave has $\tilde{\vec E}_0 = \tilde E_0\,\hat n$ with a real unit vector $\hat n$, the **polarization vector** (by convention the direction of $\vec E$); a complex $\tilde{\vec E}_0$ describes elliptical and circular polarization ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^def-b8-1-1|WO Def. §B8.1.1]], [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-3|WO Theorem §B8.1.3]]).
> - *Assumes:* empty space, one frequency, infinite extent of the wave fronts.
> - *Fails:* near sources and for beams of finite width, which are superpositions of plane waves; but over a region small compared with the radius of curvature of the wave fronts, any wave looks plane (Griffiths, footnote 5). By Fourier analysis any wave is a superposition of monochromatic ones (Griffiths eq. (9.20)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.2.2, eqs. (9.43), (9.49) and footnote 5 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 13 §3*

^mod-b10-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.3: Transversality and the Relation between E and B
> A monochromatic plane wave (Model §B10.1.2) solves Maxwell's equations in vacuum if and only if
>
> $$
> \vec k\cdot\tilde{\vec E}_0 = 0, \qquad \tilde{\vec B}_0 = \frac{1}{\omega}\,\vec k\times\tilde{\vec E}_0 = \frac1c\,\hat k\times\tilde{\vec E}_0
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (9.44)–(9.46), (9.49)–(9.50)). Then $\vec B$ is transverse too, $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ are perpendicular and in phase, $\vec E\times\vec B$ points along $\hat k$, and the real amplitudes satisfy $B_0 = E_0/c$ (9.47). For a linearly polarized wave (Griffiths eqs. (9.51)–(9.52))
>
> $$
> \vec E(\vec r, t) = E_0\cos(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t + \delta)\,\hat n, \qquad \vec B(\vec r, t) = \frac{E_0}{c}\cos(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t + \delta)\,(\hat k\times\hat n) .
> $$
>
> Only Gauss's law and Faraday's law are needed; the Ampère–Maxwell law then adds nothing new.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.2.2, eqs. (9.44)–(9.52) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 13 §3*

^thm-b10-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> On $e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)}$, $\nabla$ acts as multiplication by $i\vec k$ and $\partial_t$ by $-i\omega$, so $\nabla\cdot\tilde{\vec E} = i\vec k\cdot\tilde{\vec E}$ and $\nabla\times\tilde{\vec E} = i\vec k\times\tilde{\vec E}$. Since the real and imaginary parts of $\tilde{\vec E}$ differ only by a shift of phase, the complex fields solve Maxwell's equations exactly when the real ones do (Griffiths, footnote 6). Then: $\nabla\cdot\tilde{\vec E} = 0$ gives $\vec k\cdot\tilde{\vec E}_0 = 0$ (for $\hat k = \hat z$: $(\tilde E_0)_z = 0$, (9.44)); Faraday's law gives $i\vec k\times\tilde{\vec E}_0 = i\omega\tilde{\vec B}_0$, the relation for $\tilde{\vec B}_0$ (for $\hat k = \hat z$ in components: $-k(\tilde E_0)_y = \omega(\tilde B_0)_x$, $k(\tilde E_0)_x = \omega(\tilde B_0)_y$, (9.45)), with $k/\omega = 1/c$ by [[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]]. Conversely, given these, $\nabla\cdot\tilde{\vec B} = i\vec k\cdot(\vec k\times\tilde{\vec E}_0)/\omega\,e^{i(\cdots)} = 0$, and $\nabla\times\tilde{\vec B} = (i/\omega)\vec k\times(\vec k\times\tilde{\vec E}) = (i/\omega)\bigl[\vec k(\vec k\cdot\tilde{\vec E}) - k^2\tilde{\vec E}\bigr] = -(ik^2/\omega)\tilde{\vec E} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0(-i\omega)\tilde{\vec E} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\tilde{\vec E}$, using $k^2 = \omega^2\mu_0\varepsilon_0$: the Ampère–Maxwell law holds automatically. For real $\hat k$ and $\hat n$, taking real parts of $\tilde E_0e^{i(\cdots)}\hat n$ and $(\tilde E_0/c)e^{i(\cdots)}\hat k\times\hat n$ gives the real fields, which share the phase $\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t + \delta$; $\hat k\times\hat n \perp \hat n$, and $\hat n\times(\hat k\times\hat n) = \hat k$.

> For $\hat k = \hat x$ and $\hat n = \hat y$ this is [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-5|Theorem §A12.2.5]] and transversality is [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-2|Theorem §A12.2.2]], there obtained from a Gaussian box. Griffiths uses only real $\hat n$, linear polarization (his footnote 3).

## Energy, momentum and pressure

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.4: Energy, Momentum and Stress of a Plane Wave
> For the linearly polarized plane wave of Theorem §B10.1.3 travelling along $\hat k = \hat z$ with $\hat n = \hat x$, the energy density, Poynting vector, momentum density and stress tensor ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^def-b9-1-1|Def. §B9.1.1]], [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^def-b9-2-1|Def. §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^def-b9-2-2|Def. §B9.2.2]]) are (Griffiths eqs. (9.53)–(9.59))
>
> $$
> u = \varepsilon_0E^2 = \varepsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2(kz - \omega t + \delta), \qquad \vec S = cu\,\hat z, \qquad \vec g = \frac{u}{c}\,\hat z, \qquad T_{ij} = -u\,\delta_{iz}\delta_{jz} :
> $$
>
> the electric and magnetic energies are equal, energy moves with the wave at speed $c$, the momentum density is the energy density divided by $c$, and the momentum current $-\overleftrightarrow{T}$ has the single component $u$: $z$-momentum flowing along $z$. Averaged over a cycle (Griffiths eqs. (9.60)–(9.63)),
>
> $$
> \langle u\rangle = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E_0^2, \qquad \langle\vec S\rangle = \tfrac12c\varepsilon_0E_0^2\,\hat z, \qquad \langle\vec g\rangle = \frac{1}{2c}\varepsilon_0E_0^2\,\hat z, \qquad I \equiv \langle S\rangle = \tfrac12c\varepsilon_0E_0^2 ,
> $$
>
> with $I$ the **intensity**. A beam of intensity $I$ carries momentum $I/c$ per unit area per unit time; energy $U$ comes with momentum $U/c$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.2.3, eqs. (9.53)–(9.63), Problem 9.13 (the stress tensor, solved here) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 13 §4, eqs. (28)–(30)*

^thm-b10-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $\vec E = E\hat x$ and $\vec B = (E/c)\hat y$ (Theorem §B10.1.3), $B^2/\mu_0 = E^2/(\mu_0c^2) = \varepsilon_0E^2$, so the two halves of $u$ are equal and $u = \varepsilon_0E^2$. $\vec S = \mu_0^{-1}E(E/c)\,\hat x\times\hat y = (\varepsilon_0E^2c)\hat z = cu\hat z$ (using $1/\mu_0c = \varepsilon_0c$), and $\vec g = \vec S/c^2 = (u/c)\hat z$. For the stress tensor, only $E_x$ and $B_y$ are nonzero, so all off-diagonal elements vanish and
>
> $$
> T_{xx} = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2 - \frac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2 = 0, \qquad T_{yy} = -\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2 + \frac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2 = 0, \qquad T_{zz} = -\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2 - \frac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2 = -u .
> $$
>
> The average of $\cos^2$ over a period is $\tfrac12$ ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|Theorem §A12.3.3]]; for complex amplitudes $\langle fg\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(\tilde f\tilde g^*)$, Griffiths Problem 9.12, [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|WO Theorem §B1.1.6]]). The momentum crossing area $A$ in time $\Delta t$ is that in the cylinder $Ac\Delta t$, $\langle g\rangle Ac\Delta t = (I/c)A\Delta t$.

^der-b10-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]], [[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^def-b9-1-1|Def. §B9.1.1]], [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^def-b9-2-1|Def. §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^def-b9-2-2|Def. §B9.2.2]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|WO Theorem §B1.1.6]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.5: Radiation Pressure
> Let plane waves of intensity $I$ fall normally on a body in vacuum, which reflects intensity $I_R$ straight back and transmits $I_T$ out of its far side. The time-averaged force per unit area along the direction of incidence is
>
> $$
> P = \frac{I + I_R - I_T}{c} .
> $$
>
> In particular (Griffiths eq. (9.64)): a **perfect absorber** feels $P = I/c$, a **perfect reflector** $P = 2I/c$, and a lossless partial reflector ($I_T = I - I_R$) $P = 2I_R/c$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.2.3, eq. (9.64) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 13 §4, eq. (30) (absorber) · PHY 422, practice final, Problem 3(b); the general form worked out here from Griffiths eqs. (8.27), (9.58)*

^thm-b10-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Enclose the body in a box with faces of area $A$ perpendicular to $\hat z$, one in front and one behind. Apply conservation of momentum ([[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]]) and average over a period: in the steady state the averages of the field momentum and of everything else in the box do not change, so the average force on the body is $\langle\oint\overleftrightarrow{T}\cdot d\vec a\rangle$. In front, the field is the sum of incident and reflected waves, both transverse; for any transverse $\vec E$ and $\vec B$, $T_{zz} = -\tfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2 - \tfrac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2 = -u$ and $T_{xz} = T_{yz} = 0$. The time-averaged $u$ of two counter-propagating waves is the sum of their separate averages: the cross terms in $\varepsilon_0E^2$ and in $B^2/\mu_0$ are equal and opposite, because the reflected wave's $\vec B$ has the opposite sign relative to its $\vec E$ (Theorem §B10.1.3 with $-\hat k$). So in front $\langle u\rangle = (I + I_R)/c$ and behind $\langle u\rangle = I_T/c$ (Theorem §B10.1.4). The outward normals are $-\hat z$ in front and $+\hat z$ behind, so
>
> $$
> \langle F_z\rangle = \langle T_{zz}\rangle_{\text{front}}(-A) + \langle T_{zz}\rangle_{\text{behind}}(+A) = A\,\frac{I + I_R}{c} - A\,\frac{I_T}{c} .
> $$
>
> Side faces contribute nothing for wide beams. The special cases follow from $I_R = I_T = 0$, from $I_R = I$, and from energy conservation $I_T = I - I_R$.

^der-b10-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]], [[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]], [[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-4|Theorem §B10.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the field picture adds to level A, and why absorption matters
> - [[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure#^thm-a12-4-2|Theorem §A12.4.2]] got the absorber's pressure from the force on a charge driven by the undisturbed wave, and the reflector's by assuming that the reflected wave carries momentum $U/c$. Here both follow from the field's momentum current, whatever the absorber is made of, and the reflected wave's momentum is a theorem (Theorem §B10.1.4). The same argument handles a window ([[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^ex-b9-2-1|Example §B9.2.1]]).
> - The microscopic mechanism is the one of [[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure#^thm-a12-4-1|Theorem §A12.4.1]] and Griffiths' qualitative account: $\vec E$ drives the charges along $\hat x$, and $q\vec v\times\vec B$ pushes them along $\hat z$. If the charge is free and undamped, its velocity is a quarter period out of phase with $\vec E$ and the magnetic force averages to zero (Griffiths Problem 9.11); a damping force $-\gamma m\vec v$ makes the charge absorb, and only then is there a net push. Nelson takes the opposite limit of a strongly damped charge and finds that the ratio of the rates of energy and momentum extraction is $c$, independent of charge, damping and amplitude: the field's $g = u/c$ seen from the absorber's side.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.2.3 and Problem 9.11 · Nelson ch. 20, §20.2–20.3, eq. (20.8)*

^rem-b10-1-3

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Circularly polarized light carries angular momentum
> Let the wave be circularly polarized, $\vec E = E_0(\cos\omega t\,\hat x + \sin\omega t\,\hat y)$ at $z = 0$, and let it act on a charge held in the $xy$ plane by strong viscous friction $-\zeta\vec v$, so that $\vec v = q\vec E/\zeta$ (Nelson's model, inertia neglected). The charge moves on a circle of radius $r = qE_0/\zeta\omega$ with $\vec v \parallel \vec E$, a quarter turn ahead of its position vector, so the torque about the centre is $N_z = rqE_0 = q^2E_0^2/\zeta\omega$, while the absorbed power is $P = q\vec E\cdot\vec v = q^2E_0^2/\zeta$. Hence
>
> $$
> \frac{N_z}{P} = \frac1\omega :
> $$
>
> a circularly polarized wave delivers angular momentum $U/\omega$ with energy $U$, along $\pm\hat k$ according to its handedness. With the quantum of energy $\hbar\omega$ this is $\pm\hbar$ per photon (Nelson Problem 20.2): the spin of the photon. A plane wave of infinite extent has $\vec\ell = \vec r\times\vec g$ with $\vec g \parallel \hat k$ and so no angular momentum along $\hat k$ in the sense of Def. §B9.2.2; the angular momentum sits at the edges of any real, finite beam, where $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ acquire small longitudinal components.
>
> *Source: Nelson ch. 20, §20.4–20.5 and Problem 20.2 (the ratio worked out here); the remark on finite beams added here*

## Example

> [!example] Example §B10.1.1: Pressure on a Perfect Conductor, from the Lorentz Force
> A plane wave in vacuum travelling along $+z$ meets a perfect conductor filling $z \ge 0$, inside which $\vec E = \vec B = \vec 0$ ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-7|Theorem §B8.4.7]]), and is reflected (Planck's account of radiation pressure):
>
> $$
> \vec E(z, t) = E_0\bigl[\cos(kz - \omega t) - \cos(kz + \omega t)\bigr]\hat x = 2E_0\sin kz\,\sin\omega t\,\hat x \qquad (z < 0) .
> $$
>
> *Magnetic field.* The incident wave has $\vec B = (E_0/c)\cos(kz - \omega t)\hat y$; the reflected wave, $-E_0\cos(kz + \omega t)\hat x$ travelling along $-\hat z$, has $\vec B = \tfrac1c(-\hat z)\times\vec E = (E_0/c)\cos(kz + \omega t)\hat y$ (Theorem §B10.1.3). So
>
> $$
> \vec B(z, t) = \frac{2E_0}{c}\cos kz\,\cos\omega t\,\hat y \qquad (z < 0) ,
> $$
>
> a standing wave with a node of $\vec E$ and an antinode of $\vec B$ at the surface (figure). The tangential $\vec E$ vanishes at $z = 0$, as it must.
>
> *Surface current.* With $\vec B = \vec 0$ inside, the boundary condition $\vec K = \hat n\times\vec H$ ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-7|Theorem §B8.4.7]], $\hat n = -\hat z$ the outward normal of the conductor) gives $\vec K = -\hat z\times(2E_0/\mu_0c)\cos\omega t\,\hat y = (2E_0/\mu_0c)\cos\omega t\,\hat x$.
>
> *Force.* The force per unit area on a surface current is $\vec K\times\vec B_{\text{av}}$ with the average of the fields on the two sides ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]], [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^cau-b6-1-2|Caution: The field at a surface current]]), $\vec B_{\text{av}} = (E_0/c)\cos\omega t\,\hat y$:
>
> $$
> \vec f = \frac{2E_0}{\mu_0c}\,\frac{E_0}{c}\cos^2\omega t\;(\hat x\times\hat y) = 2\varepsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2\omega t\,\hat z, \qquad \langle f\rangle = \varepsilon_0E_0^2 = \frac{2I}{c} ,
> $$
>
> with $I = \tfrac12c\varepsilon_0E_0^2$: the perfect-reflector pressure of [[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-5|Theorem §B10.1.5]]. The stress tensor gives the same at once: at the surface $\vec E = \vec 0$ and $\vec B$ is tangential, so the momentum flux into the conductor is $-T_{zz} = B^2/2\mu_0$, the **magnetic pressure**, $= 2\varepsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2\omega t$. Here, unlike the absorber of level A, the push is purely magnetic: the conductor's electrons carry the current $\vec K$, and $\vec K\times\vec B$ is the $q\vec v\times\vec B$ of level A summed over the surface.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 9.34 (solved here)*
