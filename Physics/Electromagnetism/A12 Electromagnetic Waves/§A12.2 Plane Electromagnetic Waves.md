[[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves|§A12.1]] argued in words that changing electric and magnetic fields can keep each other going through empty space. This section shows it from Maxwell's equations for the simplest case, a wave travelling along one axis. Three conclusions come out: the fields are perpendicular to the direction of travel, they obey a wave equation whose speed is $1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = c$, and the magnetic field is the electric field divided by $c$, in phase with it and perpendicular to it.

## The plane-wave model

> [!model] Model §A12.2.1: Plane Wave in Free Space
> A **plane electromagnetic wave** travelling along the $x$-axis is a field configuration in which $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ depend only on $x$ and $t$ (they are uniform over every plane $x = \text{const}$), in a region of free space: no charges and no currents, $Q_{\text{in}} = 0$ and $I = 0$ in Maxwell's equations.
>
> - *Assumes:* distances from the source large compared with the wavelength and with the region considered; far from an antenna the wavefronts are nearly spherical, and over a small patch they are nearly flat.
> - *Fails:* near the source, where the fields are not of this form; in matter (charges and currents respond to the wave; level B, planned; light in a dielectric: [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|WO Model §B8.1.1]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-1|WO Theorem §B13.1.1]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-4|WO Theorem §B13.1.4]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.2*

## Transversality

> [!theorem] Theorem §A12.2.2: Electromagnetic Waves Are Transverse
> In a plane wave travelling along $x$, the components $E_x$ and $B_x$ cannot vary with $x$; any such uniform part is not part of the wave and is set to zero. The electric and magnetic fields of the wave are perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.2, eq. (16.13)*

^thm-a12-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply Gauss's law ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|Principle §A12.1.3]] (1)) to a closed box with faces of area $A = l^2$ at $x$ and $x + \Delta x$ and four side faces parallel to the $x$-axis. Since $E_y$ and $E_z$ do not depend on $y$ or $z$, they have equal values on opposite side faces, so their flux in and out cancels. Only $E_x$ contributes:
>
> $$
> -E_x(x,t)\,A + E_x(x + \Delta x, t)\,A = \frac{Q_{\text{in}}}{\varepsilon_0} = 0 .
> $$
>
> So $E_x(x + \Delta x, t) = E_x(x,t)$ for every $\Delta x$: $E_x$ is uniform in space. A uniform field (such as that of a pair of charged plates) can be superposed on the wave, but it does not travel and is not part of it; for the wave, $E_x = 0$. The same argument with Gauss's law for magnetism (2) gives $B_x = 0$.

## The field equations and the wave equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §A12.2.3: Coupled Field Equations
> In a plane wave along $x$ with $\vec E = E_y\,\hat\jmath$ and $\vec B = B_z\,\hat k$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial t}, \qquad \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x} = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> The first is Faraday's law (a changing $\vec B$ makes a spatially varying $\vec E$), the second the Ampère–Maxwell law (a changing $\vec E$ makes a spatially varying $\vec B$).
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.2, eqs. (16.16), (16.19)*

^thm-a12-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> **Faraday.** Take a thin rectangle in the $xy$-plane, with sides $\Delta x$ (along $x$) and $l$ (along $y$), normal $+\hat k$, so it is traversed counterclockwise seen from $+z$. The sides along $x$ contribute nothing ($E_x = 0$); going up the side at $x + \Delta x$ and down the side at $x$,
>
> $$
> \oint\vec E\cdot d\vec s = \bigl[E_y(x + \Delta x,t) - E_y(x,t)\bigr]\,l \approx \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x}\,l\,\Delta x .
> $$
>
> The magnetic flux through the rectangle is $B_z\,l\,\Delta x$ (to first order in $\Delta x$). Faraday's law (3) gives $\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x}\,l\Delta x = -\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial t}\,l\Delta x$; cancel $l\Delta x$ and let $\Delta x \to 0$.
>
> **Ampère–Maxwell.** Take a thin rectangle in the $xz$-plane with normal $+\hat\jmath$; by the right-hand rule ($\hat k\times\hat\imath = \hat\jmath$) it runs along $+z$ at $x$ and along $-z$ at $x + \Delta x$. With $B_x = 0$,
>
> $$
> \oint\vec B\cdot d\vec s = \bigl[B_z(x,t) - B_z(x + \Delta x,t)\bigr]\,l \approx -\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x}\,l\,\Delta x ,
> $$
>
> and the electric flux through it is $E_y\,l\,\Delta x$. Law (4) with $I = 0$ gives $-\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial t}$.

^der-a12-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|Principle §A12.1.3]], [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-2|Theorem §A12.2.2]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|Taylor's theorem]] (Single Variable Analysis)

> [!theorem] Theorem §A12.2.4: Wave Equation and the Speed of Light
> Each field component of the plane wave obeys the **wave equation**
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2 E_y}{\partial x^2} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial^2 E_y}{\partial t^2}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 B_z}{\partial x^2} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial^2 B_z}{\partial t^2} .
> $$
>
> Any function $E_y = f(x - vt)$ (a fixed shape moving along $+x$ at speed $v$) solves it exactly when $v = c$, with
>
> $$
> c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(4\pi \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\cdot m/A})(8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{C^2/N\cdot m^2})}} = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> Electromagnetic waves in vacuum travel at the speed of light, whatever their frequency or shape.
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.2, eqs. (16.20)–(16.22); PHY 182, week 14 slides*

^thm-a12-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate the first equation of [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-3|Theorem §A12.2.3]] with respect to $x$, exchange the order of the mixed partial derivatives, and use the second equation:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2 E_y}{\partial x^2} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial^2 E_y}{\partial t^2} .
> $$
>
> Starting from the second equation instead gives the same equation for $B_z$. For $E_y = f(\xi)$ with $\xi = x - vt$, the chain rule gives $\partial^2E_y/\partial x^2 = f''(\xi)$ and $\partial^2E_y/\partial t^2 = v^2 f''(\xi)$, so the wave equation holds (for a shape with $f'' \neq 0$) iff $1 = \mu_0\varepsilon_0 v^2$. The wave equation says nothing about $f$ itself, so every shape, in particular every frequency, travels at the same speed $c$.

^der-a12-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-3|Theorem §A12.2.3]], [[Schwarz–Clairaut Theorem]] (Multivariable Analysis), [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|Chain Rule]] (Single Variable Analysis)

> [!remark] Remark: What was assumed, and what was not
> Nothing about light went into the derivation: only the four laws measured with charges, currents, magnets and coils, and the two constants $\varepsilon_0$ (from Coulomb's law) and $\mu_0$ (from the force between currents). That they combine into the measured speed of light is Maxwell's discovery. Maxwell (1873) showed in general, not only for plane waves, that electromagnetic waves in free space travel at $c$. The general theory of the wave equation and of wave kinematics is in [[Oscillations, Waves and Optics]] ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-4|WO Def. §A2.2.4]], [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^thm-a2-1-1|WO Theorem §A2.1.1]]).

## How E and B are related

> [!definition] Definition §A12.2.1: Sinusoidal Wave Quantities
> A sinusoidal plane wave has the form $E_y = E_0\cos(kx - \omega t)$, a function of $x - ct$ alone when $\omega = ck$. Its **amplitude** is $E_0$; its **angular frequency** $\omega$, **frequency** $f$ and **period** $T$ are related by $\omega = 2\pi f$, $f = 1/T$; its **wavelength** $\lambda$ (the length of one cycle) and **wave number** $k$ by $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. Hence
>
> $$
> c = f\lambda = \frac{\omega}{k} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.2, eq. (16.23)*

^def-a12-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A12.2.5: Fields of a Plane Electromagnetic Wave
> In a plane wave travelling along $+x$ with $\vec E = E_y\,\hat\jmath$, the magnetic field is
>
> $$
> B_z(x,t) = \frac{E_y(x,t)}{c} .
> $$
>
> For the sinusoidal wave: $E_y = E_0\cos(kx - \omega t)$, $B_z = B_0\cos(kx - \omega t)$ with $E_0/B_0 = c$. So
> - $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation, and $\vec E\times\vec B$ points along the propagation;
> - they are in phase: peaks and troughs coincide;
> - at every point and instant, $E = cB$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.2, eqs. (16.24)–(16.26)*

^thm-a12-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $E_y = f(x - ct)$ ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|Theorem §A12.2.4]]). The first field equation gives $\partial B_z/\partial t = -\partial E_y/\partial x = -f'(x - ct)$. Since $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigl[f(x - ct)/c\bigr] = -f'(x - ct)$, we get $B_z = f(x - ct)/c + \text{const}$, and a constant field is not part of the wave. So $B_z = E_y/c$. For $f = E_0\cos(kx - \omega t)$ this is $B_0 = E_0/c$. Finally $\hat\jmath\times\hat k = \hat\imath$: with $E_y$ and $B_z$ of the same sign, $\vec E\times\vec B$ points along $+x$; when both change sign it still does.

^der-a12-2-5

> - Fix the direction of travel from $\vec E\times\vec B$, not from either field alone. A wave with $\vec E$ along $z$ travelling along $+x$ has $\vec B$ along $-y$ ($\hat k\times(-\hat\jmath) = \hat\imath$).
> - $E = cB$ makes $B$ numerically tiny in SI units, but it does not make the magnetic part unimportant: it carries exactly half the wave's energy ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves|§A12.3]]).
> - The arrows in the figure show field *values* along the $x$-axis; nothing moves sideways. The wave moves along $x$.

## Producing and detecting the waves

> [!remark] Remark: Antennas
> A steady current makes a constant magnetic field that does not propagate; **accelerating charges** radiate ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|WO Theorem §B13.3.1]]). In a **dipole antenna** a transmitter drives charge back and forth between two straight conductors at the desired frequency, and the radiated fields have the same frequency. The total length is typically half a wavelength (a half-wave antenna), which sets up standing waves on it and radiates efficiently. The radiation is strongest perpendicular to the antenna axis (horizontally, for a vertical antenna) and zero along the axis. Far away the wavefronts become spherical and then, over small regions, plane, which is Model §A12.2.1. A receiving antenna works in reverse: the wave drives currents in it, and a tuned resonant circuit ([[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits|§A11.4]]) picks out the wanted frequency, as Hertz did ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^rem-a12-1-6|Remark: Hertz's experiment]]).

^rem-a12-2-2

## Example

> [!example] Example §A12.2.1: The Magnetic Field of a Strong Wave
> An electromagnetic wave has peak electric field $E_0 = 1000\ \mathrm{V/m}$. Its peak magnetic field is
>
> $$
> B_0 = \frac{E_0}{c} = \frac{1000\ \mathrm{V/m}}{3.00 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}} = 3.33 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm T ,
> $$
>
> less than a tenth of Earth's field. If the wave travels along $+x$ with $\vec E$ along $+y$ at some point, $\vec B$ there points along $+z$. A strong electric field in a wave comes with a weak-looking magnetic field; such fields are detected not by their size but by resonance in a tuned receiver.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 16.2, Figure 16.8*

^ex-a12-2-1
