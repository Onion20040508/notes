Sunlight warms, microwaves cook: electromagnetic waves carry energy away from their sources, whether or not anything absorbs it. This section asks how much energy a wave holds per unit volume and how fast it delivers that energy through a surface. The energy densities of the electric and magnetic fields ([[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]], [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-2|Theorem §A10.3.2]]) combined with the plane-wave relation $E = cB$ ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-5|Theorem §A12.2.5]]) answer both questions.

## Energy density

> [!theorem] Theorem §A12.3.1: Energy Density of an Electromagnetic Wave
> The energy per unit volume of the fields is
>
> $$
> u = u_E + u_B = \frac12\varepsilon_0E^2 + \frac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2 .
> $$
>
> In a plane electromagnetic wave the two parts are equal, $u_E = u_B$, so
>
> $$
> u = \varepsilon_0E^2 = \frac{B^2}{\mu_0} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.3, eq. (16.27)*

^thm-a12-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The energy densities of the electric field ([[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]) and the magnetic field ([[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-2|Theorem §A10.3.2]]) add. In the wave $B = E/c$ and $c^2 = 1/(\mu_0\varepsilon_0)$ ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-5|Theorem §A12.2.5]], [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|Theorem §A12.2.4]]), so
>
> $$
> u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} = \frac{E^2}{2\mu_0c^2} = \frac{\varepsilon_0\mu_0E^2}{2\mu_0} = \frac12\varepsilon_0E^2 = u_E .
> $$

^der-a12-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]], [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-2|Theorem §A10.3.2]], [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|Theorem §A12.2.4]], [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-5|Theorem §A12.2.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: Energy in the field itself
> The two densities were found where the energy could equally be credited to charges and currents: $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ for a parallel-plate capacitor (checked for a spherical one), $B^2/2\mu_0$ for a long solenoid. In a wave far from its source there are no charges or currents nearby, so the energy can only be in the fields: the wave is where the field picture of [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^rem-a4-3-2|Remark: Two pictures of the same energy]] becomes necessary. That the same densities hold for any field, static or not, follows from Maxwell's equations (Poynting's theorem, level B: [[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]); here it is taken over from the two special cases.

^rem-a12-3-1

## Energy flux: the Poynting vector

> [!definition] Definition §A12.3.1: Energy Flux; Poynting Vector
> The **energy flux** $S$ of a wave is the energy per unit time per unit area crossing a surface perpendicular to its direction of travel (unit $\mathrm{W/m^2}$). The **Poynting vector**
>
> $$
> \vec S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\vec E\times\vec B
> $$
>
> carries both the size and the direction of the energy flow.
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.3, eq. (16.28)*

^def-a12-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A12.3.2: Energy Flux of a Plane Wave
> In a plane electromagnetic wave the energy moves with the wave at speed $c$, and the energy flux is
>
> $$
> S = uc = c\,\varepsilon_0E^2 = \frac{1}{\mu_0}EB = \bigl\lvert\vec S\bigr\rvert ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec S$ pointing along the direction of propagation. For the sinusoidal wave, $S(x,t) = c\varepsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2(kx - \omega t)$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.3, eq. (16.29)*

^thm-a12-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The field pattern, and with it the energy density, moves along $x$ at speed $c$. In a time $\Delta t$, all the energy in a cylinder of cross-section $A$ and length $c\Delta t$ just behind a plane area $A$ crosses that area: energy $u\,A\,c\,\Delta t$ (for $\Delta t$ short enough that $u$ is nearly constant along the cylinder). Dividing by $A\,\Delta t$ gives $S = uc$. With [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-1|Theorem §A12.3.1]], $uc = c\varepsilon_0E^2$, and with $E = cB$ and $c^2\varepsilon_0 = 1/\mu_0$, $c\varepsilon_0E^2 = c^2\varepsilon_0EB = EB/\mu_0$. Since $\vec E \perp \vec B$, $\lvert\vec E\times\vec B\rvert = EB$, and $\vec E\times\vec B$ points along the propagation ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-5|Theorem §A12.2.5]]).

^der-a12-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-1|Theorem §A12.3.1]], [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^def-a12-3-1|Def. §A12.3.1]], [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-5|Theorem §A12.2.5]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^def-a1-5-2|CM Def. §A1.5.2]]

![[ph-em-a12-3-1.svg]]
*The energy $uAc\Delta t$ in the cylinder (blue) of cross-section $A$ and length $c\Delta t$ crosses the area $A$ (red) in the time $\Delta t$; per unit area and time that is $S = uc$, flowing along $\vec S$ (green).*

## Intensity

> [!definition] Definition §A12.3.2: Intensity
> The **intensity** $I$ of an electromagnetic wave is the time average of its energy flux, $I = S_{\text{avg}} = \langle S\rangle$: the average power per unit area. (Eyes and most detectors respond only to this average, since visible light oscillates about $10^{14}$ times a second.)
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.3*

^def-a12-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A12.3.3: Intensity of a Sinusoidal Plane Wave
> For a sinusoidal plane wave of amplitudes $E_0$, $B_0 = E_0/c$,
>
> $$
> I = \frac12 c\varepsilon_0E_0^2 = \frac{cB_0^2}{2\mu_0} = \frac{E_0B_0}{2\mu_0} .
> $$
>
> The intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude. The peak flux is twice the average, $S_{\max} = 2I$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.3, eqs. (16.30)–(16.33)*

^thm-a12-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Average $S = c\varepsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2(kx - \omega t)$ ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-2|Theorem §A12.3.2]]) over one period at a fixed point. $\cos^2$ and $\sin^2$ differ only by a shift in phase, so over a full cycle they have the same average, and $\langle\cos^2\rangle = \frac12\langle\cos^2 + \sin^2\rangle = \frac12$. Hence $I = \frac12c\varepsilon_0E_0^2$. Substituting $E_0 = cB_0$ and $c^2\varepsilon_0 = 1/\mu_0$ gives the other two forms.

^der-a12-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-2|Theorem §A12.3.2]], [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^def-a12-3-2|Def. §A12.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Same average as in ac circuits
> The factor $\frac12$ is the same $\langle\sin^2\rangle = \frac12$ that turned peak values into rms values in [[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-3-2|Theorem §A11.3.2]]. With $E_{\text{rms}} = E_0/\sqrt2$, the intensity reads $I = c\varepsilon_0E_{\text{rms}}^2$, just as $P_{\text{ave}} = V_{\text{rms}}^2/R$.

^rem-a12-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A12.3.4: Inverse-Square Law for a Point Source
> A source radiating power $P$ uniformly in all directions, with no absorption on the way, produces at distance $r$ the intensity
>
> $$
> I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2} .
> $$
>
> The field amplitudes therefore fall as $1/r$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.3, Examples 16.4–16.5*

^thm-a12-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the steady state, the energy emitted per unit time crosses every sphere centred on the source (energy conservation, [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]). By symmetry the intensity is the same everywhere on a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$, so $I\cdot 4\pi r^2 = P$. With $I \propto E_0^2$ ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|Theorem §A12.3.3]]), $E_0 \propto 1/r$.

^der-a12-3-4

*Uses:* [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^def-a12-3-2|Def. §A12.3.2]], [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|Theorem §A12.3.3]], [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Intensity, amplitude, and "average field"
> - "Intensity" is the *time-averaged* flux; the instantaneous flux swings between $0$ and $2I$.
> - $I$ goes with the square of the amplitude: doubling $E_0$ (and so $B_0$) quadruples $u$ and $I$.
> - The time average of $E$ itself is zero. Statements about "the field" of a beam of given intensity mean the amplitude $E_0$ (or $E_{\text{rms}}$), and one should say which.

^cau-a12-3-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A12.3.1: Fields of the Light from a Bulb
> A bulb emits $P = 5.00\ \mathrm W$ of visible light, assumed uniform in all directions. At $r = 3.0\ \mathrm m$, by [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-4|Theorem §A12.3.4]] and [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|Theorem §A12.3.3]],
>
> $$
> I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2} = 4.42 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{W/m^2}, \qquad
> E_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2I}{c\varepsilon_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(4.42 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{W/m^2})}{(3.00 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s})(8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{C^2/N\cdot m^2})}} = 5.77\ \mathrm{N/C},
> $$
>
> and $B_0 = E_0/c = 1.92 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm T$. These are the amplitudes of the oscillating fields (the book calls them "average" fields); the time averages of $E$ and $B$ themselves are zero.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 16.4*

^ex-a12-3-1

> [!example] Example §A12.3.2: Range of a Radio Transmitter
> A $60\ \mathrm{kW}$ transmitter reaches a satellite $100\ \mathrm{km}$ away. At what distance in the same direction would the field amplitude be the same if the output were raised to $90\ \mathrm{kW}$? Equal amplitude means equal intensity $I \propto P/r^2$ (the power in a given direction spreads over an area growing as $r^2$), so
>
> $$
> \frac{r_2^2}{r_1^2} = \frac{P_2}{P_1} = \frac{90\ \mathrm{kW}}{60\ \mathrm{kW}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_2 = \sqrt{1.5}\,(100\ \mathrm{km}) = 122\ \mathrm{km} .
> $$
>
> Range grows only as the square root of the power: doubling the range of a transmitter takes four times the power (ignoring reflections and absorption).
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 16.5*

^ex-a12-3-2
