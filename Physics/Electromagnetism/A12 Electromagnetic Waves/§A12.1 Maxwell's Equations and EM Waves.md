The previous chapters found four laws for the electric and magnetic fields: Gauss's law, the absence of magnetic charges, Faraday's law and Ampère's law. This section asks whether they are consistent and complete. They are not quite: Ampère's law fails as soon as currents change in time. Maxwell's repair, the displacement current, says that a changing electric field makes a magnetic field. The four corrected laws, Maxwell's equations, together with the Lorentz force are the complete classical theory of electromagnetism, and they predict electromagnetic waves that travel at the speed of light.

## Ampère's law fails for a charging capacitor

> [!remark] Remark: Two surfaces, two answers
> Ampère's law ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-1|Theorem §A8.4.1]]) says $\oint_C \vec B\cdot d\vec s = \mu_0 I$, where $I$ is the current through *any* surface bounded by the loop $C$; for steady currents the choice of surface does not matter. Now charge a parallel-plate capacitor, and take $C$ around the wire leading to one plate.
> - Through a flat surface $S_1$ spanning $C$, the wire carries the current $I$: the law gives $\mu_0 I$.
> - Through a surface $S_2$ with the same boundary that bulges out between the plates, no charge flows: the law gives $0$.
>
> The left side is the same integral around the same loop, so one of the two answers is wrong: in this form Ampère's law is inconsistent once the current changes in time. What *does* pass through $S_2$ is a growing electric field. If a changing magnetic field makes an electric field (Faraday), perhaps a changing electric field makes a magnetic field.

*A capacitor being charged. The loop $C$ bounds the flat surface $S_1$ (blue), pierced by the wire carrying $I$, and the bag-shaped surface $S_2$ (green), which passes between the plates where there is no current but a growing field $\vec E$ (red). Together $S_1$ and $S_2$ enclose the left plate.*

> [!definition] Definition §A12.1.1: Displacement Current
> The **displacement current** through a surface $S$ is
>
> $$
> I_d = \varepsilon_0\,\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}, \qquad \Phi_E = \int_S \vec E\cdot\hat n\,dA ,
> $$
>
> with $\Phi_E$ the electric flux through $S$ ([[§A2.1 Electric Flux#^def-a2-1-3|Def. §A2.1.3]]). It has the units of a current, but it is not a motion of charge: it is a changing electric field, and it exists in empty space.
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.1, eq. (16.3)*

^def-a12-1-1

> [!principle] Principle §A12.1.1: Ampère–Maxwell Law
> For any closed loop $C$ and any surface $S$ bounded by it,
>
> $$
> \oint_C \vec B\cdot d\vec s = \mu_0\bigl(I + I_d\bigr) = \mu_0 I + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt},
> $$
>
> where $I$ is the conduction current and $\Phi_E$ the electric flux through $S$, both counted positive along the normal that goes with the sense of $C$ by the right-hand rule. Magnetic fields are produced by currents *and* by changing electric fields.
>
> *Domain: all of classical electromagnetism in vacuum. For steady currents $d\Phi_E/dt = 0$ and it reduces to Ampère's law, [[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-1|Theorem §A8.4.1]].*
> *Source: UP2 §16.1, eqs. (16.2), (16.4); PHY 182, week 13–14 slides*

^pr-a12-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A12.1.2: The Ampère–Maxwell Law Does Not Depend on the Surface
> If $S_1$ and $S_2$ are two surfaces bounded by the same loop $C$, the right side of the Ampère–Maxwell law has the same value for both, as charge conservation requires.
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.1, eqs. (16.5)–(16.7) (the book treats the capacitor case with $\vec E = \vec 0$ on $S_1$)*

^thm-a12-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Orient both surfaces by the right-hand rule from $C$. Together they form a closed surface; its outward normal agrees with the normal of one of them, say $S_2$, and is opposite to that of $S_1$. Let $Q$ be the charge enclosed.
> - Gauss's law ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]) for the closed surface: $\Phi_E(S_2) - \Phi_E(S_1) = Q/\varepsilon_0$.
> - Charge conservation ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]]): $Q$ grows by the current entering through $S_1$ minus the current leaving through $S_2$, $dQ/dt = I_1 - I_2$.
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> \Bigl[\mu_0 I_2 + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E(S_2)}{dt}\Bigr] - \Bigl[\mu_0 I_1 + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E(S_1)}{dt}\Bigr]
> = \mu_0\Bigl[(I_2 - I_1) + \frac{dQ}{dt}\Bigr] = 0 .
> $$
>
> For the capacitor of the figure, $I_2 = 0$ and $\vec E \approx \vec 0$ on $S_1$: the whole $\mu_0 I$ through $S_1$ reappears as $\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,d\Phi_E/dt = \mu_0\,dQ/dt$ through $S_2$. Without the displacement-current term the difference would be $\mu_0\,dQ/dt \neq 0$: Ampère's original law contradicts charge conservation whenever charge accumulates somewhere.

^der-a12-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-1|Principle §A12.1.1]], [[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]]

> [!example] Example §A12.1.1: Displacement Current in a Charging Capacitor
> A parallel-plate capacitor (area $A$, separation $d$, so $C = \varepsilon_0A/d$, [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]]) is charged through a resistor $R$ by a battery of emf $V_0$, starting at $t = 0$. Take $z$ from the positive to the negative plate.
> - Displacement current between the plates. The capacitor voltage is $V_C = V_0(1 - e^{-t/RC})$ ([[§A6.5 RC Circuits#^thm-a6-5-1|Theorem §A6.5.1]]), so $E_z = V_C/d$ and
>
> $$
> I_d = \varepsilon_0 A\frac{dE_z}{dt} = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A V_0}{d}\,\frac{1}{RC}\,e^{-t/RC} = \frac{V_0}{R}\,e^{-t/RC} .
> $$
>
> - Conduction current in the wires. $Q = CV_C = CV_0(1 - e^{-t/RC})$, so $I = dQ/dt = (V_0/R)\,e^{-t/RC}$.
>
> The two are equal at every instant: the displacement current "continues" the wire current across the gap. Both, and hence the magnetic field they produce around the capacitor, are largest at $t = 0$, when the charging starts.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 16.1*

^ex-a12-1-1

## Maxwell's equations

> [!principle] Principle §A12.1.3: Maxwell's Equations
> For any closed surface $S$ (outward normal) and any closed loop $C$ bounding an open surface (normal by the right-hand rule),
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> &\text{(1) Gauss's law:} && \oint_S \vec E\cdot d\vec A = \frac{Q_{\text{in}}}{\varepsilon_0},\\
> &\text{(2) Gauss's law for magnetism:} && \oint_S \vec B\cdot d\vec A = 0,\\
> &\text{(3) Faraday's law:} && \oint_C \vec E\cdot d\vec s = -\frac{d\Phi_m}{dt},\\
> &\text{(4) Ampère–Maxwell law:} && \oint_C \vec B\cdot d\vec s = \mu_0 I + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}.
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> They determine the fields from the charges and currents. The fields act on a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ by the **Lorentz force** $\vec F = q\vec E + q\vec v\times\vec B$ ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^def-a1-3-1|Def. §A1.3.1]], [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]]). Maxwell's equations, the Lorentz force law and conservation of charge together contain all of classical electromagnetism.
>
> *Domain: classical electromagnetism, charges and currents in vacuum, at all speeds (the equations are already consistent with special relativity). In matter, polarization and magnetization can be absorbed into modified forms (level B, planned). Fails at the quantum level (photons: [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|QM Principle §A1.2.3]]).*
> *Source: UP2 §16.1, eqs. (16.8)–(16.12); PHY 182, week 13–14 slides*

^pr-a12-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Where each piece came from
> | Law | What it says | Introduced in |
> |---|---|---|
> | (1) Gauss | electric field lines begin on positive and end on negative charge | [[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2\|Theorem §A2.2.2]], from Coulomb's law [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2\|Principle §A1.2.2]] and superposition |
> | (2) Gauss for magnetism | magnetic field lines have no beginning or end; there are no magnetic monopoles | [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^def-a7-1-2\|Def. §A7.1.2]], [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^rem-a7-1-3\|Remark: No magnetic charges]]; the fields of the Biot–Savart law [[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^pr-a8-1-1\|Principle §A8.1.1]] obey it |
> | (3) Faraday | a changing magnetic flux induces a circulating electric field, against the change | [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^pr-a9-1-1\|Principle §A9.1.1]], [[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^pr-a9-4-1\|Principle §A9.4.1]], Lenz [[§A9.2 Lenz's Law#^thm-a9-2-1\|Theorem §A9.2.1]] |
> | (4) Ampère–Maxwell | currents and changing electric fields make circulating magnetic fields | [[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-1\|Theorem §A8.4.1]] (from Biot–Savart, [[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^pr-a8-1-1\|Principle §A8.1.1]]) + [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-1\|Principle §A12.1.1]] |
> | Lorentz force | how fields push charges | $q\vec E$: [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^def-a1-3-1\|Def. §A1.3.1]]; $q\vec v\times\vec B$: [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1\|Principle §A7.1.1]] |
>
> At level A, Gauss's law was *derived* from Coulomb's law, and Ampère's law from the Biot–Savart law, for static sources. Maxwell's set turns the logic around: the four equations are the fundamental statements, valid for moving charges and changing currents as well, and Coulomb's and the Biot–Savart laws are their solutions in the static case.

^rem-a12-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: The static limit, and the symmetry
> When nothing changes in time, the equations split into an electric pair and a magnetic pair:
>
> $$
> \oint\vec E\cdot d\vec A = \frac{Q_{\text{in}}}{\varepsilon_0},\quad \oint\vec E\cdot d\vec s = 0; \qquad \oint\vec B\cdot d\vec A = 0,\quad \oint\vec B\cdot d\vec s = \mu_0 I .
> $$
>
> In words: electric fields diverge from charge and do not curl (the static field is conservative, which is why it has a potential, [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]; contrast [[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^thm-a9-4-2|Theorem §A9.4.2]]); magnetic fields never diverge and curl around currents. Time dependence couples the two pairs through the two "field makes field" laws:
>
> $$
> \oint\vec E\cdot d\vec s = -\frac{d\Phi_m}{dt}, \qquad \oint\vec B\cdot d\vec s = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \quad (\text{no currents}).
> $$
>
> They differ only in the sign (which reverses the sense of circulation) and the factor $\mu_0\varepsilon_0$. The missing terms (no magnetic charge in (2), no magnetic current in (3)) are the only asymmetry, and it reflects the absence of magnetic monopoles.

^rem-a12-1-3

## How the fields sustain each other

> [!remark] Remark: The mechanism of an electromagnetic wave
> Drive a high-frequency alternating current in a wire. It makes a changing magnetic field $\vec B_0(t)$ around the wire. By Faraday's law, the changing $\vec B_0$ induces a changing electric field $\vec E_0(t)$ curling around it; by the Ampère–Maxwell law, the changing $\vec E_0$ makes a new changing magnetic field $\vec B_1(t)$ farther out, which induces $\vec E_1(t)$, and so on. The pattern of alternating electric and magnetic fields detaches from the source and spreads outward through empty space, with no medium needed. In empty space ($Q_{\text{in}} = 0$, $I = 0$) these two coupled laws alone drive the wave.

^rem-a12-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: The speed hidden in the constants
> In the two "field makes field" laws, the only constant is $\mu_0\varepsilon_0$, and it has the units of an inverse speed squared:
>
> $$
> \mu_0\varepsilon_0 = \Bigl(4\pi \times 10^{-7}\ \tfrac{\mathrm{T\cdot m}}{\mathrm A}\Bigr)\Bigl(8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \tfrac{\mathrm{C^2}}{\mathrm{N\cdot m^2}}\Bigr) = 1.11 \times 10^{-17}\ \mathrm{s^2/m^2},
> \qquad \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> This is the measured speed of light. [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves|§A12.2]] shows that it is indeed the speed of the waves of the previous remark. Light was already known to be a wave (Young's interference experiment, 1801; Fresnel, Foucault), so the conclusion was that light is an electromagnetic wave, and that such waves should exist at every other frequency as well. With $c$ in place of $\mu_0$ the Ampère–Maxwell law reads $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec s = \mu_0 I + \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}$.

^rem-a12-1-5

> [!remark] Remark: Hertz's experiment
> Heinrich Hertz (from 1887) drove a spark gap in a loop with a resonant RLC circuit of known frequency $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ ([[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits|§A11.4]]). Across the room a second loop with a gap, tuned to the same frequency, sparked: electromagnetic waves had been sent and received. He also observed reflection, refraction and interference of these waves, measured their wavelength $\lambda$ from interference patterns, and from $v = f\lambda$ found $v = c$. The unit of frequency is named after him. (Wave kinematics, $v = f\lambda$ in general: [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^thm-a2-1-1|WO Theorem §A2.1.1]]; the needed facts for electromagnetic waves are in [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves|§A12.2]].)

^rem-a12-1-6

> [!remark] Remark: Electric or magnetic depends on the observer
> The magnet-and-loop example of [[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^rem-a9-3-3|Remark: Magnetic or electric? It depends on the frame]] shows that what one observer calls a magnetic force ([[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-2|Theorem §A9.3.2]]) another calls an electric one (Faraday's law, [[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^pr-a9-4-1|Principle §A9.4.1]]). With the complete set of equations in hand, the conclusion can be stated: only $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ together are independent of the observer, and the magnetic force can be understood as the electric force combined with relativity (length contraction of a current-carrying wire). Maxwell's equations needed no change to fit relativity; Newton's mechanics did. (Relativity subject, planned.)
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 14 slides; UP2 §16.1*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - With [[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|Stokes' theorem]] and the [[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|divergence theorem]] (Multivariable Analysis), the integral equations become the local, differential form of Maxwell's equations, $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$, $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial t$, $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial\vec E/\partial t$ (Electromagnetism level B, planned). Taking the divergence of the last one and using $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B) = 0$ gives the continuity equation for charge: the differential version of [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^thm-a12-1-2|Theorem §A12.1.2]].
> - In the language of [[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms|differential forms]] (Multivariable Analysis §22), the two source-free laws (2) and (3) combine into one statement that a 2-form on spacetime is closed; the remaining two are its sourced partner. This is where electromagnetism and relativity meet (level C, planned).
