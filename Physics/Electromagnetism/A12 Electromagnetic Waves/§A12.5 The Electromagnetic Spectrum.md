Maxwell's theory puts no limit on the frequency of an electromagnetic wave ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves|§A12.2]]), and waves are found from fractions of a hertz to beyond $10^{22}\ \mathrm{Hz}$. This section asks how this range is organized: which names go with which wavelengths, how each kind is produced, and what it is used for. Physically all of them are the same thing, differing only in frequency.

## One law for all wavelengths

> [!theorem] Theorem §A12.5.1: Wavelength and Frequency in Vacuum
> Every electromagnetic wave in vacuum travels at the same speed $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$, and its frequency and wavelength are related by
>
> $$
> c = f\lambda .
> $$
>
> Higher frequency means shorter wavelength, in inverse proportion.
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.5*

^thm-a12-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|Theorem §A12.2.4]] the wave equation lets every shape, hence every frequency, travel at the one speed $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$. For a sinusoid, the pattern advances one wavelength in one period, $c = \lambda/T = f\lambda$ ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^def-a12-2-1|Def. §A12.2.1]]).

^der-a12-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|Theorem §A12.2.4]], [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^def-a12-2-1|Def. §A12.2.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A12.5.1: Electromagnetic Spectrum
> The **electromagnetic spectrum** is the full range of frequencies (equivalently, wavelengths) of electromagnetic waves. It is divided into bands by wavelength, source and use. Properties change smoothly from one band to the next; the boundaries are conventions and the bands overlap.
>
> | Band | Wavelength (approx.) | Main sources | Some uses |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Radio | $\gtrsim 1\ \mathrm{mm}$ (up to $\sim 100\ \mathrm{km}$); the narrower usage is $\gtrsim 0.1\ \mathrm m$ | accelerating charges: ac currents in antennas and circuits; astronomical sources | AM ($540\text{–}1600\ \mathrm{kHz}$) and FM ($88\text{–}108\ \mathrm{MHz}$) broadcasting, TV, MRI, remote controls; ELF ($3\ \mathrm{Hz}\text{–}3\ \mathrm{kHz}$) for submarines |
> | Microwaves | $1\ \mathrm{mm}$ to $1\ \mathrm m$ ($\sim 10^9$ to $10^{12}\ \mathrm{Hz}$) | currents in macroscopic devices (the highest frequencies these can make); thermal agitation; cosmic background | radar, satellite links, cell phones, WiFi ($2.4\text{–}5\ \mathrm{GHz}$), Bluetooth, RFID, ovens ($2.45\ \mathrm{GHz}$) |
> | Infrared | $0.74\ \mu\mathrm m$ to $300\ \mu\mathrm m$ | thermal agitation; molecular vibration and rotation; electronic transitions | thermal imaging, heating, remote controls |
> | Visible | about $400$ to $750\ \mathrm{nm}$ | thermal agitation; atomic and molecular vibrations and electronic transitions | vision, photosynthesis |
> | Ultraviolet | $400\ \mathrm{nm}$ down to about $10\ \mathrm{nm}$ | electronic transitions; thermal agitation (the Sun) | sterilization, vitamin D production, fluorescence |
> | X-rays | about $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-12}\ \mathrm m$ | inner-shell electronic transitions; fast electrons hitting a target | medical and security imaging, cancer therapy |
> | Gamma rays | overlapping X-rays and shorter | nuclear decay and other nuclear processes | nuclear medicine, cancer therapy, security |
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.5, Table 16.1 and chapter glossary*

## A tour of the bands

> [!remark] Remark: Radio and microwaves: made by circuits
> - **Radio waves** come from alternating currents in antennas ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^rem-a12-2-2|Remark: Antennas]]). Information is carried by **modulating** the wave: its amplitude (AM; each station allowed $\pm 5\ \mathrm{kHz}$ about its carrier), its frequency (FM; $\pm 100\ \mathrm{kHz}$) or its phase. The higher the carrier frequency, the faster it can be varied, and the more data per second it can carry. Power lines radiate in the ELF range (their frequency is 50 or 60 Hz), which also penetrates sea water.
> - **Microwaves** are the highest frequencies that currents in macroscopic circuits can produce. Radar times their echoes to find distances. In an oven, the oscillating electric field twists the polar water molecules back and forth ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles|§A1.5]]), and collisions turn this into heat; reflections off the walls set up standing waves with hot spots (Example §A12.5.1). Cell phones, WiFi, Bluetooth and RFID tags all use microwave bands; NFC uses magnetic induction between two nearby coils over a very short range.

^rem-a12-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Infrared to gamma: made by atoms and nuclei
> - **Infrared** ("below red") is emitted by every warm object through thermal agitation, with a power that grows as the fourth power of the absolute temperature; about half the solar energy reaching Earth is infrared and most of the rest visible. Water absorbs it strongly.
> - **Visible light**, about $400$–$750\ \mathrm{nm}$, is the narrow band the eye detects; red has the longest wavelength and violet the shortest. Sunlight peaks in the visible.
> - **Ultraviolet** ("above violet") is subdivided into UV-A ($320$–$400\ \mathrm{nm}$), UV-B ($290$–$320\ \mathrm{nm}$) and UV-C ($220$–$290\ \mathrm{nm}$). Ozone absorbs most UV-B and all UV-C, so 99% of the solar UV at the ground is UV-A. UV-B and UV-C cause sunburn and raise the risk of skin cancer; UV-B also drives vitamin D production.
> - **X-rays and gamma rays** transfer more energy per atomic event the shorter their wavelength, so they damage living cells and penetrate matter; both are used to image the inside of opaque objects and to treat cancer.
>
> That a shorter wavelength means a larger energy change for an atom that absorbs it is a quantum fact (Quantum Mechanics, planned); classically, the intensity of a wave depends on its amplitude, not its frequency ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves|§A12.3]]).

^rem-a12-5-2

> [!caution] Caution: Names are not physics
> - X-rays and gamma rays of the same frequency are identical; the names record the source (electrons striking a target versus a nucleus).
> - "Micro" in *microwave* does not mean micrometres: microwave wavelengths run from about a millimetre to a metre. They are "micro" only compared with other radio waves.
> - All bands travel at the same speed $c$ in vacuum; they differ in frequency, wavelength and how they interact with matter, not in speed.

^cau-a12-5-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A12.5.1: Hot Spots in a Microwave Oven
> A microwave oven runs at $f = 2.45\ \mathrm{GHz}$. Waves reflected between opposite walls form a standing wave whose intensity maxima (antinodes) are half a wavelength apart:
>
> $$
> d = \frac{\lambda}{2} = \frac{c}{2f} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}}{2(2.45 \times 10^9\ \mathrm{Hz})} = 6.12\ \mathrm{cm} .
> $$
>
> The spacing of the hot spots is set by the wavelength, which is why ovens use turntables and rotating reflectors to move the food through them.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 16.8 (the book prints 6.02 cm; the arithmetic gives 6.12 cm)*

^ex-a12-5-1

> [!example] Example §A12.5.2: Wavelengths across the Spectrum
> From $\lambda = c/f$ ([[§A12.5 The Electromagnetic Spectrum#^thm-a12-5-1|Theorem §A12.5.1]]):
> - FM station at $100\ \mathrm{MHz}$: $\lambda = 3.00\ \mathrm m$, so a half-wave receiving antenna is $1.5\ \mathrm m$ long.
> - 60 Hz power line: $\lambda = 5.0 \times 10^6\ \mathrm m$, comparable to Earth's radius: deep in the ELF range.
> - Green light, $\lambda = 500\ \mathrm{nm}$: $f = 6.0 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$.
>
> Antenna sizes scale with the wavelength they are meant to send or receive.
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.2, §16.5 (data from the text)*

^ex-a12-5-2
