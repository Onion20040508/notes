An electromagnetic wave carries energy ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves|§A12.3]]). Maxwell predicted that it also carries momentum, so that it pushes on whatever absorbs or reflects it. This section asks how large that push is. The Lorentz force on the charges of the absorber gives the answer: the momentum delivered is the energy delivered divided by $c$. The resulting radiation pressure is tiny, yet it shapes comet tails and can propel a light sail.

## Why light pushes

> [!theorem] Theorem §A12.4.1: Momentum Delivered with Absorbed Energy
> Let a plane wave travelling along $+x$ act on a charge $q$. At every instant, the $x$-component of the force on the charge equals the power the wave delivers to it divided by $c$:
>
> $$
> F_x = \frac{P}{c} .
> $$
>
> Hence a body that absorbs energy $U$ from the wave receives momentum $U/c$ along the direction of propagation.
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.4, Figure 16.13 (the book argues the direction qualitatively; the relation $F_x = P/c$ is worked out here from the same setup)*

^thm-a12-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take $\vec E = E_y\hat\jmath$ and $\vec B = B_z\hat k$ with $B_z = E_y/c$ ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-5|Theorem §A12.2.5]]), and let the charge move with velocity $\vec v = v_x\hat\imath + v_y\hat\jmath$. The Lorentz force ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^def-a1-3-1|Def. §A1.3.1]], [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]]; [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|Principle §A12.1.3]]) is
>
> $$
> \vec F = qE_y\,\hat\jmath + q\,(v_x\hat\imath + v_y\hat\jmath)\times B_z\hat k = q v_y B_z\,\hat\imath + q\,(E_y - v_xB_z)\,\hat\jmath ,
> $$
>
> so $F_x = qv_yB_z = qv_yE_y/c$. The magnetic force is perpendicular to $\vec v$ and does no work ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-1|Theorem §A7.2.1]]), so the power delivered to the charge is that of the electric force, $P = \vec F\cdot\vec v = qE_yv_y$ ([[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|CM Theorem §A4.3.1]]). Comparing, $F_x = P/c$. Integrating over time, the momentum received along $x$ is the energy received divided by $c$ ([[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^thm-a5-1-1|CM Theorem §A5.1.1]]).
>
> *Level:* this treats the field at the charge as the undisturbed incident plane wave. The general statement, that the fields carry momentum with density $\vec S/c^2$ wherever they are, is level B (planned).

> [!remark] Remark: Why absorption is needed
> If the charge moves in phase with the electric force, as for an electron whose motion is damped by the resistance of a metal, $qE_yv_y > 0$ throughout the cycle and the push along $x$ is always forward (figure). If instead the charge oscillated a quarter cycle out of phase, like a capacitor in [[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-3-1|Theorem §A11.3.1]], the power and hence $F_x$ would average to zero: no net absorption, no net push.

## Radiation pressure

> [!definition] Definition §A12.4.1: Radiation Pressure
> The **radiation pressure** $p_{\text{rad}}$ is the force per unit area that an electromagnetic wave exerts on a surface, along its direction of propagation.
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.4*

^def-a12-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A12.4.2: Radiation Pressure on Absorbers and Reflectors
> For a plane wave of energy density $u$ and intensity $I$ falling along the normal of a surface,
>
> $$
> p_{\text{rad}} = u, \qquad \langle p_{\text{rad}}\rangle = \frac{I}{c} \qquad \text{(perfect absorber)},
> $$
>
> $$
> p_{\text{rad}} = 2u, \qquad \langle p_{\text{rad}}\rangle = \frac{2I}{c} \qquad \text{(perfect reflector)} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §16.4, eqs. (16.34)–(16.36)*

^thm-a12-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> **Absorber.** An area $A$ absorbs the whole energy flux, power $SA$ ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-2|Theorem §A12.3.2]]). By [[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure#^thm-a12-4-1|Theorem §A12.4.1]] the force on it is $SA/c$, so $p_{\text{rad}} = S/c = u$; averaging, $\langle p_{\text{rad}}\rangle = I/c$.
>
> **Reflector.** The incoming wave brought momentum $U/c$ along $+x$ with each energy $U$; the reflected wave carries the same energy, hence momentum $U/c$, along $-x$. The change of the wave's momentum is $-2U/c$, so by momentum conservation ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|CM Theorem §A5.2.2]]) the surface receives $+2U/c$: twice the force, $p_{\text{rad}} = 2u$, $\langle p_{\text{rad}}\rangle = 2I/c$. (Here "the reflected wave carries momentum $U/c$" uses Theorem §A12.4.1 read as a statement about the wave, which is the level-B result mentioned there.)
>
> **Units:** $[u] = \mathrm{J/m^3} = \mathrm{N\cdot m/m^3} = \mathrm{N/m^2}$, a pressure.

^der-a12-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure#^thm-a12-4-1|Theorem §A12.4.1]], [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-2|Theorem §A12.3.2]], [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^def-a12-3-2|Def. §A12.3.2]], [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|CM Theorem §A5.2.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Absorbed or reflected; normal incidence
> - A reflector feels twice the push of an absorber, like a ball that bounces compared with one that sticks ([[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^thm-a5-1-1|CM Theorem §A5.1.1]]).
> - These formulas are for normal incidence on an idealized perfect absorber or perfect reflector; real surfaces lie in between.
> - $p_{\text{rad}} = u$ and $2u$ are instantaneous; with intensity use the averaged forms $I/c$ and $2I/c$.

^cau-a12-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Evidence: torsion balances and comets
> Nichols and Hull confirmed Maxwell's prediction in 1903: light shone on one of two mirrors hung from a fibre in an evacuated glass vessel twisted the suspension by the predicted amount. In astronomy, a comet near the Sun develops two tails. The **ion tail** of ionized gas is pushed by the solar wind (charged particles from the Sun) and points almost straight away from the Sun. The **dust tail** is electrically neutral and is pushed by the radiation pressure of sunlight, which displaces it from the comet's path.

^rem-a12-4-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A12.4.1: Sunlight on a Fragment of Halley's Comet
> At closest approach (9 February 1986) Halley's comet was $r = 9.0 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm m$ from the Sun, whose output is $P = 3.8 \times 10^{26}\ \mathrm W$. Assume a fully reflecting surface. By [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-4|Theorem §A12.3.4]] and [[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure#^thm-a12-4-2|Theorem §A12.4.2]],
>
> $$
> I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2} = 3.7 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{W/m^2}, \qquad p = \frac{2I}{c} = 2.5 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{N/m^2} .
> $$
>
> On a $10\ \mathrm{kg}$ chunk of cross-section $4.0 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ that gives $F = pA = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm N$, against the Sun's gravity ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]])
>
> $$
> F_g = \frac{GM_{\odot}m}{r^2} = \frac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2})(2.0 \times 10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg})(10\ \mathrm{kg})}{(9.0 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm m)^2} = 0.16\ \mathrm N .
> $$
>
> Radiation loses by a factor of about $10^5$. Both forces fall as $1/r^2$, so their ratio does not depend on the distance; it is $F/F_g = 2PA/(4\pi c\,GM_{\odot}m)$, proportional to $A/m$. That is why only fine dust (large area per unit mass) is blown into a tail.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 16.6*

^ex-a12-4-1

> [!example] Example §A12.4.2: Acceleration of a Light Sail
> The LightSail spacecraft has sails of total area $32\ \mathrm{m^2}$ and a total mass of $5.0\ \mathrm{kg}$. At 1 AU from the Sun, sunlight has $I = 1370\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$. Facing the Sun with reflecting sails,
>
> $$
> F = \frac{2I}{c}A = \frac{2(1370\ \mathrm{W/m^2})(32\ \mathrm{m^2})}{3.00 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}} = 2.92 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm N, \qquad
> a = \frac{F}{m} = 5.8 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}
> $$
>
> by Newton's second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]]). Tiny, but it needs no fuel and does not stop: sustained for a year it would add about $1.8\ \mathrm{km/s}$. A usable sail needs, as in the previous example, a large area per unit mass.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 16.7*

^ex-a12-4-2
