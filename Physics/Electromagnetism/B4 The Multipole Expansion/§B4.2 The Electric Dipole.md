The dipole term dominates the far field of every neutral charge distribution, and neutral atoms and molecules interact with fields mainly through their dipole moments. Level A defined the dipole moment ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^def-a1-5-1|Def. §A1.5.1]]) and found the torque and the vanishing force in a *uniform* field ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^thm-a1-5-1|Theorem §A1.5.1]]). It gave the dipole's field only on the axis and the bisector ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]) and its potential far away ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]); induced dipoles appeared only in words ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]). This section is the home of the dipole's field in coordinate-free form, of the force, torque and energy of a dipole in an arbitrary field, of the interaction of two dipoles, and of the atomic polarizability. These are the building blocks of polarized matter ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge|§B5.1]]).

## The pure dipole and its field

> [!model] Model §B4.2.1: Pure (Point) Dipole
> A **pure**, **point** or **ideal dipole** of moment $\vec p$ at $\vec r_0$ is the limit of a physical dipole ($\pm q$ a distance $d$ apart) as $d \to 0$ and $q \to \infty$ with $qd = p$ held fixed. Its potential is, *exactly*, the dipole term of [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]],
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\vec p\cdot\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}, \qquad \vec{\mathcal R} = \vec r - \vec r_0 ,
> $$
>
> for $\vec r \ne \vec r_0$. *Assumptions:* it represents a neutral distribution of size $a$ at distances $\mathcal R \gg a$ (or a physical dipole with $d \ll \mathcal R$), and in an external field it represents a body small compared with the distance over which that field changes. A physical dipole also has octupole and higher terms ([[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]]); these vanish in the limit. When someone says "dipole", assume $d$ is small enough for the pure-dipole formulas.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4.2, pp. 155–156 · Nelson ch. 3, §3.3.7*

^mod-b4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.2: The Field of a Dipole
> A pure dipole $\vec p = p\hat z$ at the origin has the field (Griffiths eq. (3.103))
>
> $$
> \vec E_{\text{dip}}(r, \theta) = \frac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}\bigl(2\cos\theta\,\hat r + \sin\theta\,\hat\theta\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> or, in a form that does not depend on coordinates (eq. (3.104)),
>
> $$
> \vec E_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{r^3}\bigl[3(\vec p\cdot\hat r)\,\hat r - \vec p\bigr] .
> $$
>
> It falls off as $1/r^3$; in general a $2^n$-pole field falls off as $1/r^{n + 2}$. On the axis ($\hat r = \pm\hat z$) the bracket is $2\vec p$; on the perpendicular bisector ($\hat r \perp \vec p$) it is $-\vec p$. These are the two special cases of [[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]], which level A found from Coulomb's law. The theorem gives the field in every direction, and exactly for a pure dipole.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4.4, eqs. (3.102)–(3.104); Problem 3.36*

^thm-b4-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $V = \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}$ (eq. (3.102)) and the gradient in spherical coordinates ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]]; [[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^rem-a3-4-2|§A3.4, Remark: Gradient in cylindrical and spherical coordinates]]), $\vec E = -\nabla V$ has the components
>
> $$
> E_r = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial r} = \frac{2p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}, \qquad E_\theta = -\frac1r\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta} = \frac{p\sin\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}, \qquad E_\varphi = -\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\varphi} = 0 .
> $$
>
> For the coordinate-free form, decompose $\hat z = \cos\theta\,\hat r - \sin\theta\,\hat\theta$, so $\vec p = p\cos\theta\,\hat r - p\sin\theta\,\hat\theta$ and $\vec p\cdot\hat r = p\cos\theta$:
>
> $$
> 3(\vec p\cdot\hat r)\,\hat r - \vec p = 3p\cos\theta\,\hat r - p\cos\theta\,\hat r + p\sin\theta\,\hat\theta = p\bigl(2\cos\theta\,\hat r + \sin\theta\,\hat\theta\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The right side no longer refers to the $z$ axis, so it holds for any direction of $\vec p$. (Alternatively, $-\nabla(\vec p\cdot\vec r/r^3) = -\vec p/r^3 + 3(\vec p\cdot\vec r)\vec r/r^5$ by the product rules of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]].) Each $2^n$-pole potential falls as $1/r^{n + 1}$, and the gradient adds one more power of $1/r$.

> [!remark] Remark: The contact term at the dipole's position
> Formula (3.104) is valid except at $r = 0$, where it blows up. The average of $\vec E$ over a ball of radius $R$ due to all the charge inside is $-\vec p/4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3$, for any distribution (Griffiths eq. (3.105), Problem 3.47). The angular average of (3.104) is zero, and its radial integral is undefined, so the missing average must sit at the origin. The full field of a pure dipole is (eq. (3.106))
>
> $$
> \vec E_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{r^3}\bigl[3(\vec p\cdot\hat r)\hat r - \vec p\bigr] - \frac{1}{3\varepsilon_0}\,\vec p\,\delta^3(\vec r) ,
> $$
>
> with the three-dimensional delta function of [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]. The delta term is the limit of the field *inside* the source, which points against $\vec p$, as in panel (b) above or inside the shell with $\sigma = k\cos\theta$ ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-5|Theorem §B3.4.5]] (3), whose $R \to 0$ limit reproduces it; Griffiths Problem 3.49). It matters only for quantities that probe the dipole's own location.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 3.47–3.49, eqs. (3.105)–(3.106)*

## Induced dipoles

> [!law] Law §B4.2.3: Linear Polarizability
> A neutral atom in an electric field $\vec E$ is polarized: the nucleus is pushed along $\vec E$, the electron cloud the other way, until their mutual attraction balances the field. The induced dipole moment is proportional to the field (Griffiths eq. (4.1)),
>
> $$
> \vec p = \alpha\vec E ,
> $$
>
> with the **atomic polarizability** $\alpha$ (SI unit $\mathrm{C^2\,m/N}$; $\alpha/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ is a volume). Measured values of $\alpha/4\pi\varepsilon_0$, in $10^{-30}\ \mathrm{m^3}$: H $0.667$, He $0.205$, Li $24.3$, Na $24.1$, K $43.4$, Cs $59.4$ (Griffiths Table 4.1). A molecule may polarize more easily along some directions: then $p_i = \sum_j\alpha_{ij}E_j$ (eq. (4.3)), with the **polarizability tensor** $\alpha_{ij}$, which is diagonal in suitable principal axes. For CO$_2$, $\alpha = 4.5 \times 10^{-40}\ \mathrm{C^2\,m/N}$ along the molecule's axis and $2 \times 10^{-40}$ across it, so $\vec p$ need not be parallel to $\vec E$.
>
> *Valid when:* the field is weak compared with the atom's internal fields (Griffiths calls it a rule of thumb; strong fields ionize); static or slowly varying fields. For oscillating fields $\alpha$ depends on frequency ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^def-b13-3-1|WO Def. §B13.3.1]]), and the Lorentz model's $\alpha_{\text{pol}}(\omega)$ tends to the static value $e^2/m\omega_0^2$ as $\omega \to 0$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.1.2, eqs. (4.1), (4.3), Table 4.1 · Nelson ch. 3, §3.4.4*

^law-b4-2-3

> [!example] Example §B4.2.1: The Polarizability of a Crude Atom, and of a Metal Ball
> Model an atom as a point nucleus $+q$ inside a uniformly charged spherical cloud $-q$ of radius $a$. In a field $\vec E$ the nucleus moves a distance $d$ from the cloud's centre, and the cloud keeps its shape (the displacements are tiny). Inside a uniformly charged ball the field grows linearly from the centre, $E_e = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qd}{a^3}$ ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]). Equilibrium, $E = E_e$, gives $p = qd = 4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3E$:
>
> $$
> \alpha = 4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3 = 3\varepsilon_0v ,
> $$
>
> with $v$ the atom's volume (Griffiths eq. (4.2)). For hydrogen with the Bohr radius $a = 0.529\ \text{Å}$, $\alpha/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = a^3 = 0.148 \times 10^{-30}\ \mathrm{m^3}$, a factor of about $4.5$ below the measured $0.667 \times 10^{-30}\ \mathrm{m^3}$: crude, but the right order of magnitude. A conducting ball of radius $R$ in a uniform field acquires $\vec p = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3\vec E_0$ ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]]), the same polarizability: on this measure, an atom polarizes like a metal ball of roughly its own size.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 4.1, eq. (4.2), Table 4.1; the comparison with the metal sphere and the hydrogen numbers are worked out here*

## A dipole in an external field

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.4: Torque, Force and Energy of a Dipole in a Field
> Let a pure dipole $\vec p$ at $\vec r$ sit in an external electrostatic field $\vec E$, produced by other charges and smooth near $\vec r$.
> 1. *Torque* about the dipole's centre (Griffiths eq. (4.4)): $\vec N = \vec p \times \vec E$, in any field. About another point $O$, $\vec N_O = \vec p \times \vec E + \vec r \times \vec F$, with $\vec r$ measured from $O$.
> 2. *Force* (eq. (4.5)): $\vec F = (\vec p\cdot\nabla)\vec E$. For a rigid dipole, with $\vec p$ held fixed, this equals $\nabla(\vec p\cdot\vec E)$.
> 3. *Energy* (eq. (4.6)): $U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E$, the work needed to bring the rigid dipole in from far away (where $\vec E = \vec 0$) and turn it into position. It is lowest for $\vec p$ parallel to $\vec E$; $\vec F = -\nabla U$ at fixed $\vec p$, and $-\partial U/\partial\psi$ is the torque about the axis of a rotation by the angle $\psi$.
> 4. *Induced dipole*, $\vec p = \alpha\vec E$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^law-b4-2-3|Law §B4.2.3]]): $\vec F = \tfrac12\alpha\,\nabla(E^2)$, the negative gradient of $U = -\tfrac12\alpha E^2$. The body is drawn toward stronger field, whatever the sign of $\vec E$.
>
> In a uniform field the force vanishes and the torque is level A's [[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^thm-a1-5-1|Theorem §A1.5.1]]. Level A obtained $U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E$ only by integrating that torque ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles|§A1.5]], Connections); here it comes with the force in a nonuniform field, which pulls an aligned dipole toward the stronger field ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^ex-a1-5-1|Example §A1.5.1]], [[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^rem-a1-5-1|§A1.5, Remark: Alignment]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.1.3, eqs. (4.4)–(4.6), footnote 2; Problem 4.7 · Nelson ch. 3, §3.4.1–3.4.2, eqs. (3.10)–(3.15); §3.4.4*

^thm-b4-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Model the dipole as $-q$ at $\vec r$ and $+q$ at $\vec r + \vec d$, and let $d \to 0$ with $q\vec d = \vec p$ fixed.
>
> *Force.* $\vec F = q[\vec E(\vec r + \vec d) - \vec E(\vec r)]$. For small $\vec d$, each component changes by $\Delta E_i = (\nabla E_i)\cdot\vec d + O(d^2)$ (first-order Taylor expansion; Griffiths eq. (1.35)), that is, $\Delta\vec E = (\vec d\cdot\nabla)\vec E$. So $\vec F = (q\vec d\cdot\nabla)\vec E + O(qd^2) \to (\vec p\cdot\nabla)\vec E$, since $qd^2 = pd \to 0$. For constant $\vec p$, product rule (ii) of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]] gives $\nabla(\vec p\cdot\vec E) = (\vec p\cdot\nabla)\vec E + \vec p \times (\nabla \times \vec E)$, and $\nabla \times \vec E = \vec 0$ in electrostatics ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]).
>
> *Torque.* About the centre $\vec r + \vec d/2$, the charges sit at $\pm\vec d/2$, and
>
> $$
> \vec N = \frac{\vec d}{2} \times q\vec E(\vec r + \vec d) + \Bigl(-\frac{\vec d}{2}\Bigr) \times \bigl(-q\vec E(\vec r)\bigr) = \frac{q\vec d}{2} \times \bigl[\vec E(\vec r + \vec d) + \vec E(\vec r)\bigr] \to \vec p \times \vec E ,
> $$
>
> because the bracket tends to $2\vec E$ and the correction is $O(qd^2)$. Moving the reference point by $-\vec r$ adds $\vec r \times \vec F$, as for any force system.
>
> *Energy.* Bringing the charges in from infinity against the external field costs $U = qV(\vec r + \vec d) - qV(\vec r) = q\,\vec d\cdot\nabla V + O(qd^2) \to \vec p\cdot\nabla V = -\vec p\cdot\vec E$. Nelson's route is the same in general form: expanding the external potential about a point inside a small rigid body gives $U = Q\,V + \vec p\cdot\nabla V + \dots$, the multipole expansion run the other way. The gradient at fixed $\vec p$ reproduces part 2. Turning $\vec p$ by a small angle $\delta\psi$ about the unit vector $\hat n$ changes it by $\delta\psi\,\hat n \times \vec p$, so $\delta U = -\delta\psi\,(\hat n \times \vec p)\cdot\vec E = -\delta\psi\,\hat n\cdot(\vec p \times \vec E)$: the torque component along $\hat n$ is $-\partial U/\partial\psi$, consistent with part 1.
>
> *Induced dipole.* With $\vec p = \alpha\vec E$ in part 2 and product rule (ii) for $\vec A = \vec B = \vec E$: $\alpha(\vec E\cdot\nabla)\vec E = \alpha\bigl[\tfrac12\nabla(E^2) - \vec E \times (\nabla \times \vec E)\bigr] = \tfrac12\alpha\nabla(E^2)$.

> [!caution] Caution: Torque is N here; do not differentiate p
> - Griffiths writes the torque as $\vec N$, because $\tau$ is taken by the volume element $d\tau$; level A and Classical Mechanics write $\vec\tau$.
> - $\vec F = \nabla(\vec p\cdot\vec E)$ holds only if $\vec p$ is *not* differentiated (Griffiths footnote 2). For an induced dipole, differentiating $\vec p = \alpha\vec E$ as well would give $\alpha\nabla(E^2)$, twice the true force $\tfrac12\alpha\nabla(E^2)$. The factor $\tfrac12$, also in $U = -\tfrac12\alpha E^2$, is the same as for the induced charge on a conductor ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^cau-b3-2-1|§B3.2, Caution: The fields agree, the energies do not]]): the moment builds up as the body moves into the field. Inside a polarized material $\vec p$ per unit volume varies from point to point, which is why the safer form $(\vec p\cdot\nabla)\vec E$ is kept ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge|§B5.1]]).

^cau-b4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.5: Interaction Energy of Two Dipoles
> Two pure dipoles $\vec p_1$ and $\vec p_2$, separated by $\vec r$ (from one to the other), have the interaction energy (Griffiths eq. (4.7))
>
> $$
> U = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{r^3}\bigl[\vec p_1\cdot\vec p_2 - 3(\vec p_1\cdot\hat r)(\vec p_2\cdot\hat r)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> At fixed distance the head-to-tail arrangement, both moments along $\hat r$, has the lowest energy, $-2p_1p_2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3$; side by side, both perpendicular to $\hat r$, antiparallel moments have $-p_1p_2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3$ and parallel ones $+p_1p_2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 4.8, eq. (4.7) · Nelson ch. 3, §3.4.1 (Your Turn 3H)*

^thm-b4-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]] (3), $U = -\vec p_2\cdot\vec E_1(\vec r)$, with $\vec E_1$ the field of $\vec p_1$ at the position of $\vec p_2$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]]):
>
> $$
> U = -\vec p_2\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}\bigl[3(\vec p_1\cdot\hat r)\hat r - \vec p_1\bigr] = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}\bigl[\vec p_1\cdot\vec p_2 - 3(\vec p_1\cdot\hat r)(\vec p_2\cdot\hat r)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> The expression is symmetric in $1 \leftrightarrow 2$ and even in $\hat r$, as an interaction energy must be. The three special cases follow by inserting $\vec p_1 \parallel \vec p_2 \parallel \hat r$, and $\vec p_1 = \mp\vec p_2 \perp \hat r$.

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]], [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Polar molecules
> Some molecules carry a permanent dipole moment ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]). In water the electrons crowd toward the oxygen, and the molecule is bent at $105^\circ$, which leaves $p = 6.1 \times 10^{-30}\ \mathrm{C\,m}$, unusually large (Griffiths), hence its strength as a solvent. In a field such molecules feel the torque $\vec p \times \vec E$, which swings them toward $\vec E$, while thermal motion disorders them. Induced moments, by contrast, are created already aligned. Either way a material in a field becomes a collection of small dipoles pointing along the field: it is polarized ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge|§B5.1]]; level A's dielectric, [[§A4.4 Dielectrics|§A4.4]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.1.3, Figure 4.4; §4.1.4*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B4.2.2: A Point Charge and a Neutral Atom
> A point charge $q$ is a large distance $r$ from a neutral atom of polarizability $\alpha$. What force does each exert on the other?
>
> The charge's field at the atom has magnitude $E = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$, and the atom acquires $\vec p = \alpha\vec E$. By [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]] (4) the force on the atom is
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{atom}} = \frac12\alpha\,\nabla\Bigl(\frac{q^2}{(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2r^4}\Bigr) = -\frac{2\alpha q^2}{(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2}\,\frac{\hat r}{r^5} ,
> $$
>
> with $\hat r$ pointing from the charge to the atom: an attraction, independent of the sign of $q$. Computed the other way, as the force $q\vec E_{\text{dip}}$ of the induced dipole's field (Theorem §B4.2.2) on the charge, it is equal and opposite, $+\frac{2\alpha q^2}{(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2}\frac{\hat r}{r^5}$, toward the atom. The $1/r^5$ law has two sources: the induced moment falls as $1/r^2$, and a dipole's force in the $1/r^2$ field falls as $p/r^3$. Level A's [[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^ex-a1-5-1|Example §A1.5.1]] took $p$ as given and found $1/r^3$. A neutral conducting sphere ($\alpha = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3$) gives $-2q^2R^3/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^5$, the far limit of the exact image result ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^ex-b2-4-2|Example §B2.4.2]]; [[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^rem-b3-2-2|§B3.2, Remark: A sphere at fixed potential, or neutral]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 7, Problem 4 (Force on a neutral atom) and its typed solutions; Griffiths Problem 4.4*

^ex-b4-2-2

> [!example] Example §B4.2.3: A Dipole above a Conducting Plane
> A pure dipole $\vec p = p\sin\theta_0\,\hat x + p\cos\theta_0\,\hat z$ sits at height $d$ above a grounded conducting plane $z = 0$.
>
> *(a) Image.* Mirror each charge of a small physical dipole in the plane and reverse its sign, as in [[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]]. The image is a dipole at $(0, 0, -d)$ with the $x$ component reversed and the $z$ component kept: $\vec p\,' = -p\sin\theta_0\,\hat x + p\cos\theta_0\,\hat z$. On $z = 0$ the two dipole potentials,
>
> $$
> V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{p\,x\sin\theta_0 + p(z - d)\cos\theta_0}{[x^2 + y^2 + (z - d)^2]^{3/2}} + \frac{-p\,x\sin\theta_0 + p(z + d)\cos\theta_0}{[x^2 + y^2 + (z + d)^2]^{3/2}}\right] ,
> $$
>
> cancel, so by [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]] this is the potential for $z > 0$.
>
> *(b) Energy and preferred orientation.* As for a point charge, the energy is half the interaction energy of the dipole with its image, because the image follows the dipole ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^cau-b3-2-1|§B3.2, Caution: The fields agree, the energies do not]]). With $\vec r = 2d\,\hat z$ in [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]], $\vec p\cdot\vec p\,' = p^2(\cos^2\theta_0 - \sin^2\theta_0)$ and $(\vec p\cdot\hat z)(\vec p\,'\cdot\hat z) = p^2\cos^2\theta_0$, so
>
> $$
> U = \frac12\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(2d)^3}\,p^2\bigl(\cos^2\theta_0 - \sin^2\theta_0 - 3\cos^2\theta_0\bigr) = -\frac{p^2\,(1 + \cos^2\theta_0)}{64\pi\varepsilon_0d^3} .
> $$
>
> It is lowest for $\theta_0 = 0$ or $\pi$: the dipole prefers to stand perpendicular to the plane. As a check, the torque computed directly from the image's field at the dipole, $\vec E' = \frac{p}{32\pi\varepsilon_0d^3}(\sin\theta_0\,\hat x + 2\cos\theta_0\,\hat z)$, is $\vec N = \vec p \times \vec E' = -\frac{p^2\sin2\theta_0}{64\pi\varepsilon_0d^3}\,\hat y$, which is $-\partial U/\partial\theta_0$ about the $\hat y$ axis, as [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]] (3) requires; it turns the dipole toward the nearer of the two perpendicular orientations. (The course's typed solution has $32\pi\varepsilon_0$ in place of $64\pi\varepsilon_0$ in the final line; the torque check confirms $64$.)
>
> *(c) Far away.* Seen from distances $r \gg d$, dipole and image merge into one dipole $\vec p + \vec p\,' = 2p\cos\theta_0\,\hat z$: the components parallel to the plane cancel, the perpendicular ones add. Setting $d = 0$ in the potential of (a) gives $V = \frac{2p\cos\theta_0\,z}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}$, which is this dipole's potential ([[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 422, Midterm 2, Problem 1 (Dipole above a conducting plane) and its typed solutions; Griffiths Problem 4.6 (the torque)*

^ex-b4-2-3

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Forces between neutral molecules
> Molecules with permanent dipoles attract one another even when thermal motion randomizes their orientations: the average of $\vec p_1\cdot\vec p_2$ is not zero, because neighbouring dipoles partly align each other, and [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]] then gives a net attraction (the Keesom interaction). Atoms with no permanent moment, such as neon, still liquefy. Their instantaneous dipole moments fluctuate quantum mechanically and the fluctuations of neighbours correlate (the London or dispersion interaction). Together these are the van der Waals forces. The induced-dipole force $\tfrac12\alpha\nabla(E^2)$ does not care about the sign of $\vec E$, so even the rapidly oscillating field of a focused laser pulls a polarizable particle into the beam: this is the principle of optical tweezers.
>
> *Source: Nelson ch. 3, §3.4.3–3.4.4*
