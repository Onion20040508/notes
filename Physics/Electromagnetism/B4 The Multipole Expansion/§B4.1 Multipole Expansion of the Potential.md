*Sources: Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (4th ed.), §3.4.1–3.4.3 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 3 · PHY 422 (Thamm, Spring 2024).*

Far from a localized charge distribution the potential looks like that of a point charge, $Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$. But what if $Q = 0$, and how good is the approximation anyway? Level A answered this for one configuration, a pair $\pm q$, by a first-order Taylor expansion ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]), and left "quadrupole, octupole" to later ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^rem-a3-3-3|§A3.3, Remark: What the dipole formula says]]). Here every localized distribution gets an exact series in powers of $1/r$. Its terms are products of a few numbers distilled from the distribution, the multipole moments, with universal functions of position, and the series follows from the generating function of the Legendre polynomials ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-4|Theorem §B3.4.4]]). The total charge does not depend on the origin; the higher moments in general do.

## The expansion

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.1: Multipole Moments
> Let a charge density $\rho(\vec r\,')$ be confined to a ball $r' \le a$ about an origin $O$. Its **monopole moment** is the total charge, and its **dipole moment** is the first moment of the charge (Griffiths eqs. (3.97)–(3.98)):
>
> $$
> Q = \int\rho(\vec r\,')\,d\tau', \qquad \vec p = \int\vec r\,'\,\rho(\vec r\,')\,d\tau' .
> $$
>
> For point charges $q_i$ at $\vec r_i'$, $\vec p = \sum_iq_i\vec r_i'$ (eq. (3.100)); for a physical dipole, $\pm q$ with $\vec d$ pointing from $-q$ to $+q$, $\vec p = q\vec d$ (eq. (3.101)), as in [[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^def-a1-5-1|Def. §A1.5.1]]. The term of order $1/r^{n + 1}$ in [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]] is the **$2^n$-pole** term: **monopole**, **dipole**, **quadrupole**, **octupole** for $n = 0, 1, 2, 3$. Its potential falls off as $1/r$, $1/r^2$, $1/r^3$, $1/r^4$, and its field one power faster.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4.1–3.4.2, eqs. (3.97)–(3.101), Figure 3.27 · Nelson ch. 3, §3.2.1, eq. (3.3)*

^def-b4-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.1: Multipole Expansion
> For a charge distribution confined to $r' \le a$ and a field point with $r > a$, the potential is (Griffiths eqs. (3.95)–(3.96))
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{n = 0}^\infty\frac{1}{r^{n + 1}}\int(r')^n\,P_n(\cos\alpha)\,\rho(\vec r\,')\,d\tau' ,
> $$
>
> where $\alpha$ is the angle between $\vec r$ and $\vec r\,'$; explicitly,
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac1r\int\rho\,d\tau' + \frac{1}{r^2}\int r'\cos\alpha\,\rho\,d\tau' + \frac{1}{r^3}\int(r')^2\Bigl(\frac32\cos^2\alpha - \frac12\Bigr)\rho\,d\tau' + \dots\right] .
> $$
>
> The series converges, and its $n$th term is at most $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{a^n}{r^{n + 1}}\int\lvert\rho\rvert\,d\tau'$ in absolute value. The integrals depend on the direction $\hat r$ of the field point through $\alpha$; on the $z'$ axis, $\alpha$ is the polar angle $\theta'$ of the source point.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4.1, eqs. (3.91)–(3.96)*

^thm-b4-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The potential of a localized distribution is $V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\rho(\vec r\,')/\mathcal R\,d\tau'$ (eq. (3.91); [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]], [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]]). For $r' \le a < r$, [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-4|Theorem §B3.4.4]] expands the separation:
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{\mathcal R} = \sum_{n = 0}^\infty\frac{(r')^n}{r^{n + 1}}P_n(\cos\alpha) .
> $$
>
> Since $\lvert P_n\rvert \le 1$ (Theorem §B3.4.4 (3)), the $n$th term is bounded by $a^n/r^{n + 1}$ uniformly in $\vec r\,'$, and $\sum(a/r)^n$ converges. By the M-test the series converges uniformly in $\vec r\,'$ ([[§25 More on Uniform Convergence#^thm-25-3|451 Thm. §25.3]]), so it may be integrated term by term against $\rho$ ([[§25 More on Uniform Convergence#^thm-25-1|451 Thm. §25.1]]); $r$ is a constant for the integration. The bound on the $n$th term follows in the same way. Griffiths reaches the same series by writing $\mathcal R = r\sqrt{1 + \epsilon}$ with $\epsilon = (r'/r)(r'/r - 2\cos\alpha)$ and expanding $(1 + \epsilon)^{-1/2}$ binomially (eqs. (3.92)–(3.93)).

^der-b4-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-4|Theorem §B3.4.4]], [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]], [[§25 More on Uniform Convergence#^thm-25-3|451 Thm. §25.3]], [[§25 More on Uniform Convergence#^thm-25-1|451 Thm. §25.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.2: Monopole and Dipole Terms
> The first two terms of the multipole expansion are (Griffiths eqs. (3.97), (3.99))
>
> $$
> V_{\text{mon}}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac Qr, \qquad V_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\vec p\cdot\hat r}{r^2} .
> $$
>
> At large $r$ the monopole term dominates unless $Q = 0$; then the dipole term dominates unless $\vec p = \vec 0$, and so on: far away, only the lowest nonvanishing moment matters. For a point charge at the origin the monopole term is exact. For a physical dipole, $\vec p = q\vec d$, the dipole term is [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]], now the first term of an exact series. The quadrupole term of that pair vanishes, and the first correction is the octupole term, smaller by a factor of order $(d/r)^2$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4.2, eqs. (3.97)–(3.101), Example 3.10; Problem 3.31 (the octupole correction, worked out here)*

^thm-b4-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $n = 0$, $P_0 = 1$ and the integral is $Q$. For $n = 1$, $r'\cos\alpha = \hat r\cdot\vec r\,'$, and $\hat r$ is constant in the integration, so $\int r'\cos\alpha\,\rho\,d\tau' = \hat r\cdot\int\vec r\,'\rho\,d\tau' = \hat r\cdot\vec p$. For the physical dipole, put $+q$ at $\tfrac d2\hat z$ and $-q$ at $-\tfrac d2\hat z$. Then $\cos\alpha = \pm\cos\theta$ for the two charges, and with $P_n(-x) = (-1)^nP_n(x)$ ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-2|Theorem §B3.4.2]] (1)) the $n$th term is $\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{(d/2)^n}{r^{n + 1}}\bigl[1 - (-1)^n\bigr]P_n(\cos\theta)$. The even terms vanish, and
>
> $$
> V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{qd\cos\theta}{r^2} + \frac{qd^3}{4r^4}P_3(\cos\theta) + \dots\right] .
> $$

^der-b4-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]], [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]], [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-2|Theorem §B3.4.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the expansion organizes
> - *A hierarchy in the ratio size/distance.* Each step up in $n$ brings one more factor $r'$ into the moment and one more $1/r$ into the potential, so successive terms shrink by about $a/r$. The lowest nonvanishing term gives the far potential, and the next ones say how to improve it (Griffiths). Nelson's summary: a term falling as $1/r$ is spherically symmetric; a $1/r^2$ term is never spherically symmetric, and its angular dependence is fixed up to a vector; a $1/r^3$ term is not spherical either.
> - *Building multipoles from smaller ones.* Two equal and opposite charges make a dipole; two equal and opposite dipoles make a quadrupole (four charges on the corners of a square, $\pm$ alternating, have $Q = 0$ and $\vec p = \vec 0$); two opposite quadrupoles make an octupole (Griffiths Figure 3.27).
> - *Singular terms, regular potential.* Every term is singular at $r = 0$, but that only marks where the expansion stops being valid ($r < a$). A smooth distribution has a smooth potential (Nelson §3.3.3).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4.1–3.4.2, Figures 3.27, 3.30 · Nelson ch. 3, §3.3.1, §3.3.3*

^rem-b4-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Where the expansion holds
> The series in powers of $r'/r$ converges only for $r$ larger than the extent $a$ of the charge. Inside, use the other half of [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-4|Theorem §B3.4.4]], with $r_<$ and $r_>$ interchanged, for the charge farther out than the field point. A source "at infinity" cannot be expanded at all. The potential $-E_0z$ of the uniform field around a conducting sphere ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]]) grows with $r$, and no multipole series reproduces it: the charges that make the field are not in a bounded region (Nelson §5.4, footnote 4).

## Origin dependence

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.3: Origin Dependence of the Moments
> Move the origin by $\vec a$, so that a source point has the new position $\bar{\vec r}\,' = \vec r\,' - \vec a$. The monopole moment does not change, and the dipole moment becomes
>
> $$
> \bar{\vec p} = \vec p - Q\vec a .
> $$
>
> Hence **if $Q = 0$, the dipole moment does not depend on the origin**. If $Q \ne 0$, the origin $\vec a = \vec p/Q$, the centre of charge, makes the dipole moment vanish.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4.3, Figures 3.32–3.34 · Nelson ch. 3, §3.3.4, eq. (3.8)*

^thm-b4-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\bar Q = \int\rho\,d\tau' = Q$, since the total charge does not care about coordinates. And $\bar{\vec p} = \int(\vec r\,' - \vec a)\,\rho\,d\tau' = \int\vec r\,'\rho\,d\tau' - \vec a\int\rho\,d\tau' = \vec p - Q\vec a$. For $Q \ne 0$, setting $\bar{\vec p} = \vec 0$ gives $\vec a = \vec p/Q$.

^der-b4-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Moving a charge off the origin
> A single charge $q$ at $(0, d, 0)$ has the exact potential $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{\mathcal R}$, but expanded about the origin it has the dipole moment $qd\,\hat y$ and every higher moment as well. The expansion is in powers of the distance from the *origin*, and moving the charge (or the origin) changes all moments except the first one. For the pair $\pm q$ the moment $q\vec d$ is unambiguous because $Q = 0$. For three charges $q, q, -q$ at the corners of a triangle the question "what is the dipole moment?" has to be answered with "about which origin?" (Griffiths Figure 3.34). In general the lowest nonvanishing moment is independent of the origin and all higher ones depend on it (Griffiths Problem 3.52(c); Nelson §3.3.4). A positive mass distribution therefore always has zero dipole moment about its centre of mass ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|CM Def. §A5.4.1]]), which is why one never hears of a gravitational dipole (Nelson, ch. 3, footnote 5).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4.3, Figures 3.32, 3.34; Problem 3.52(c) · Nelson ch. 3, §3.3.4, footnote 5*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B4.1.1: A Ball Whose Far Field Is a Quadrupole
> A ball of radius $R$ centred at the origin carries $\rho(r, \theta) = k\,\frac{R}{r^2}(R - 2r)\sin\theta$. Find the potential on the $z$ axis far from the ball.
>
> On the positive $z$ axis $\hat r = \hat z$ and $\alpha = \theta'$, so the moments needed are $\int(r')^nP_n(\cos\theta')\rho\,d\tau'$ with $d\tau' = r'^2\sin\theta'\,dr'\,d\theta'\,d\varphi'$. The factor $r'^2$ cancels the $1/r'^2$ in $\rho$. Every moment factorizes into $kR\int_0^R(R - 2r')\,r'^n\,dr'$ times $2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin^2\theta'\,P_n(\cos\theta')\,d\theta'$:
> - $n = 0$: $\int_0^R(R - 2r')\,dr' = R^2 - R^2 = 0$, so $Q = 0$;
> - $n = 1$: $\int_0^\pi\sin^2\theta'\cos\theta'\,d\theta' = 0$, so $p_z = 0$; and $p_x = p_y = 0$ from the $\varphi'$ integral;
> - $n = 2$: $\int_0^R(R - 2r')\,r'^2\,dr' = \frac{R^4}{3} - \frac{R^4}{2} = -\frac{R^4}{6}$ and $\int_0^\pi\sin^2\theta'\bigl(\frac32\cos^2\theta' - \frac12\bigr)d\theta' = \frac32\cdot\frac\pi8 - \frac12\cdot\frac\pi2 = -\frac{\pi}{16}$, so the moment is $kR\cdot\bigl(-\frac{R^4}{6}\bigr)\cdot2\pi\cdot\bigl(-\frac{\pi}{16}\bigr) = \frac{k\pi^2R^5}{48}$.
>
> The leading term is the quadrupole:
>
> $$
> V(z) \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{k\pi^2R^5}{48\,\lvert z\rvert^3} \qquad (\lvert z\rvert \gg R) .
> $$
>
> On the negative axis $\cos\alpha = -\cos\theta'$, and since $P_2$ is even the moment is the same, with $r = \lvert z\rvert$. The potential falls as $1/r^3$, although the ball carries charge of both signs in bulk.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 7, Problem 2 (Multipole expansion) and its typed solutions; Griffiths Problem 3.27*

^ex-b4-1-1

> [!example] Example §B4.1.2: A Dipole Moment Pointing Sideways
> The ball $r \le R$ now carries $\rho = \frac kr\sin\theta\cos\varphi$. Find the lowest nonvanishing moment and the potential far away in every direction.
>
> $Q = \int_0^Rk\,r\,dr\int_0^\pi\sin^2\theta\,d\theta\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\varphi\,d\varphi = 0$, because of the $\varphi$ integral. For the dipole moment, $x\rho\,d\tau = kr^2\sin^3\theta\cos^2\varphi\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi$, so
>
> $$
> p_x = k\cdot\frac{R^3}{3}\cdot\frac43\cdot\pi = \frac{4\pi kR^3}{9} ,
> $$
>
> while $p_y$ (with $\cos\varphi\sin\varphi$) and $p_z$ (with $\cos\varphi$ alone) vanish. By [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]], with $\hat x\cdot\hat r = \sin\theta\cos\varphi$,
>
> $$
> V \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\vec p\cdot\hat r}{r^2} = \frac{kR^3}{9\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\sin\theta\cos\varphi}{r^2} .
> $$
>
> The distribution has no azimuthal symmetry, so the moment had to be computed as a vector; a calculation on the $z$ axis alone would have found nothing at order $1/r^2$. Since $Q = 0$, $\vec p$ is the same about every origin ([[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 7, Problem 3 (Multipole moments) and its typed solutions*

^ex-b4-1-2

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The quadrupole moment tensor
> The $n = 2$ term can be written without reference to $\alpha$ (Griffiths Problem 3.52):
>
> $$
> V_{\text{quad}}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{r^3}\sum_{i,j = 1}^3\hat r_i\hat r_j\,Q_{ij}, \qquad Q_{ij} = \frac12\int\bigl(3r_i'r_j' - (r')^2\delta_{ij}\bigr)\rho(\vec r\,')\,d\tau' .
> $$
>
> Indeed $\sum_{ij}\hat r_i\hat r_j\cdot\frac12\bigl(3r_i'r_j' - r'^2\delta_{ij}\bigr) = \frac12\bigl(3(\hat r\cdot\vec r\,')^2 - r'^2\bigr) = r'^2P_2(\cos\alpha)$. The **quadrupole moment** $Q_{ij}$ is a symmetric, traceless second-rank tensor with five independent components. The hierarchy reads: scalar $Q$, vector $\vec p$, tensor $Q_{ij}$. The tensor is origin-independent when $Q$ and $\vec p$ vanish. Its eigenvalues are unchanged by rotations; if two of them coincide the quadrupole is uniaxial, otherwise biaxial (Nelson §3.3.8). Conventions differ by a factor $\tfrac12$: Nelson defines the moment without the $\tfrac12$ and puts $1/8\pi\varepsilon_0$ into the potential instead (his eqs. (3.3)–(3.4)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 3.52 · Nelson ch. 3, §3.2.1, §3.3.8, eqs. (3.3)–(3.4)*

^rem-b4-1-3

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Symmetry kills moments; molecules
> Symmetry often shows without calculation that moments vanish (Nelson §3.3.5–3.3.6):
> - A spherically symmetric distribution has only its monopole moment: outside it, $V = Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$ exactly.
> - Inversion symmetry about a point gives $\vec p = \vec 0$ about that point. A mirror plane forces $\vec p$ to lie in the plane, and an axis of symmetry forces $\vec p$ along the axis.
> - If the distribution is unchanged by inverting all positions *and* reversing all charges, then $Q = 0$ and every even moment (quadrupole, $2^4$-pole, …) vanishes; the pair $\pm q$ is an example.
>
> Nelson's list of molecules: NaCl is ions (monopoles); H$_2$O and HCl are neutral with dipole moments; CO$_2$ is linear and inversion-symmetric, so its first moment is the quadrupole; methane, a tetrahedron, starts at the octupole; neon is spherical. The list is also ordered by boiling (for CO$_2$, sublimation) point: the higher the leading multipole, the faster the attraction between molecules falls with distance and the more easily they separate.
>
> *Source: Nelson ch. 3, §3.3.5–3.3.6, §3.4.3, eq. (3.9)*

^rem-b4-1-4

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The expansion is the generating function of the Legendre polynomials ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-4|Theorem §B3.4.4]]) integrated against $\rho$; its convergence is the M-test ([[§25 More on Uniform Convergence#^thm-25-3|451 Thm. §25.3]]), and level A's dipole formula is its first-order Taylor term ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]).
> - The same expansion run the other way, an *external* potential expanded about a point inside a small body, gives the energy $QV + \vec p\cdot\nabla V + \dots$ of the body in a field ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]]). For gravity, its quadrupole term is the tidal potential ([[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^thm-b9-2-6|CM Theorem §B9.2.6]]).
> - The magnetic counterpart is the multipole expansion of the vector potential, which has no monopole term ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-5|Theorem §B6.4.5]]). Radiating charges have a multipole expansion too, led by electric dipole radiation ([[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation|§B11.3★]]).
