Stacking many current loops along a common axis gives a solenoid: inside, the fields of the turns add up to a strong, nearly uniform field proportional to the current; outside, they nearly cancel. Bending the stack into a ring gives a toroid, whose field is confined entirely to the windings. These are the standard ways of making a known magnetic field, from laboratory coils to MRI and fusion magnets. Both fields follow from the loop field of [[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop|§A8.3]] or, much faster, from Ampère's law ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-1|Theorem §A8.4.1]]).

## Solenoids

> [!model] Model §A8.5.1: Ideal Solenoid
> A **solenoid** is a wire wound in a tight helix on a cylinder of radius $R$, with $n$ turns per unit length carrying current $I$. The **ideal solenoid** treats it as
> - a stack of circular loops (the slight advance of the helix along the axis is ignored), so that a length $dy$ of the solenoid carries the circling current $nI\,dy$;
> - infinitely long, so that every cross-section looks the same: the field depends at most on the distance from the axis and, by symmetry, points along the axis.
>
> *Fails when:* the point is near an end or the solenoid is not much longer than wide (use [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-2|Theorem §A8.5.2]]); the winding is loose; and just outside, where the net current $I$ running along the helix gives a weak circling field like that of a straight wire.
> *Source: UP2 §12.6*

^mod-a8-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.5.2: Field on the Axis of a Finite Solenoid
> On the axis of a solenoid of radius $R$ with $n$ turns per unit length, at a point $P$ from which the ends lie at axial positions $y_1 < y_2$ (measured along the field direction),
>
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0 nI}{2}\left[\frac{y_2}{\sqrt{y_2^2+R^2}} - \frac{y_1}{\sqrt{y_1^2+R^2}}\right] = \frac{\mu_0 nI}{2}\,(\sin\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1),
> $$
>
> where $\sin\theta_i = y_i/\sqrt{y_i^2+R^2}$. Deep inside a long solenoid ($\theta_1 \to -90^\circ$, $\theta_2 \to 90^\circ$) this tends to $\mu_0 nI$; at one end of a long solenoid it is half that.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.6, eqs. (12.24)–(12.27)*

^thm-a8-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Cut the solenoid into slices of thickness $dy$, each a loop carrying $dI = nI\,dy$ ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^mod-a8-5-1|Model §A8.5.1]]). A slice at axial distance $y$ from $P$ contributes, by [[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop#^thm-a8-3-1|Theorem §A8.3.1]], $dB = \mu_0R^2\,nI\,dy/\bigl(2(y^2+R^2)^{3/2}\bigr)$ along the axis. All slices give the same direction, so
>
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0 nI}{2}\int_{y_1}^{y_2}\frac{R^2\,dy}{(y^2+R^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\mu_0 nI}{2}\left[\frac{y}{\sqrt{y^2+R^2}}\right]_{y_1}^{y_2},
> $$
>
> the same integral as for the straight segment ([[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-1|Theorem §A8.2.1]]), here multiplied by $R$. Each fraction tends to $\pm1$ for a far-away end, giving $\mu_0nI$ in the middle and $\mu_0nI/2$ at an end.

^der-a8-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop#^thm-a8-3-1|Theorem §A8.3.1]], [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^mod-a8-5-1|Model §A8.5.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.5.3: Field of an Ideal Solenoid
> Inside an ideal solenoid the field is uniform and along the axis,
>
> $$
> B = \mu_0 nI ,
> $$
>
> everywhere inside, not only on the axis; outside it is zero. Its direction follows the right-hand rule: curl the fingers along the current in the turns, and the thumb points along $\vec B$ inside.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.6, eqs. (12.28)–(12.30)*

^thm-a8-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^mod-a8-5-1|Model §A8.5.1]], $\vec B$ is along the axis and depends only on the distance from it. Apply Ampère's law to rectangles with two sides of length $l$ parallel to the axis; on the other two sides $\vec B\perp d\vec l$ (or $\vec B = \vec 0$), so they contribute nothing.
> - *Outside.* A rectangle entirely outside encloses no current, so $B(r_1)\,l - B(r_2)\,l = 0$: the outside field is the same at every distance. Far from the solenoid it must vanish, so it vanishes everywhere outside. (The PHY 182 picture: just outside, the fields of the turns on the near and far sides of the solenoid nearly cancel.)
> - *Inside.* Take side 1 inside and side 3 outside (figure). Only side 1 contributes, $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = Bl$, and the rectangle is pierced by $nl$ turns each carrying $I$: $Bl = \mu_0 nlI$, so $B = \mu_0nI$. Side 1 can be at any distance from the axis, so the field is the same everywhere inside. On the axis this agrees with the limit of [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-2|Theorem §A8.5.2]].

^der-a8-5-3

> [!caution] Caution: What a loop around the whole solenoid encloses
> A circle around the outside of a solenoid, in a plane perpendicular to its axis, is pierced once by the wire running along the helix, so it encloses the net current $I$, not zero. That is why a real solenoid has a weak field circling outside it, $\mu_0 I/(2\pi r)$, like a single straight wire; it is negligible next to $\mu_0 nI$ inside whenever $2\pi r n \gg 1$. (UP2's argument that such a loop "would enclose current flowing in both directions" and hence zero net current is not right; the outside-field argument above avoids it.)

^cau-a8-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Finite solenoids and MRI
> A finite solenoid's field lines leave one end, spread around the outside and re-enter the other end: the same pattern as a bar magnet ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^def-a7-1-2|Def. §A7.1.2]], whose figure was computed as exactly such a solenoid). The large uniform field inside a big solenoid is what an MRI scanner applies to the patient's body; superconducting wires carry the large currents.

^rem-a8-5-1

## Toroids

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.5.4: Field of a Toroid
> A **toroid** is a doughnut-shaped coil of $N$ turns closely wound with one wire carrying current $I$. Inside the windings, at distance $r$ from the toroid's central axis,
>
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0 NI}{2\pi r} ,
> $$
>
> directed along circles about the central axis (right-hand rule with the fingers along the current in the turns). Outside the windings, both in the central hole and beyond the outer wall, $B = 0$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.6, eqs. (12.31)–(12.33)*

^thm-a8-5-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For a tightly wound toroid all points on a circle about the central axis are equivalent (cylindrical symmetry), so $\vec B$ is tangent to such circles and constant in magnitude on each: $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = B\,2\pi r$. A circle in the hole encloses no current; a circle beyond the outer wall is pierced by each turn twice, in opposite directions, so the net current is zero; in both cases $B = 0$. A circle inside the windings is pierced $N$ times by the inner side of the turns, all in the same direction: $B\,2\pi r = \mu_0 NI$.

^der-a8-5-4

*Uses:* [[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-1|Theorem §A8.4.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: The toroid's field is not uniform
> $B$ falls off as $1/r$ across the windings: it is larger at the inner wall than at the outer. Only if the central radius $R$ is much larger than the cross-section of the coil does the field vary little, and then $B \approx \mu_0NI/(2\pi R) = \mu_0 nI$ with $n = N/(2\pi R)$, a solenoid bent into a ring. As with the solenoid, the helical winding leaves a small field outside.

^cau-a8-5-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A8.5.1: A Laboratory Solenoid, Ideal and Finite
> A solenoid has 300 turns on a cylinder of diameter $1.20\ \mathrm{cm}$ and length $14.0\ \mathrm{cm}$, with $I = 0.410\ \mathrm A$. Here $n = 300/(0.140\ \mathrm m) = 2.14\times10^3\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, so in the ideal model
>
> $$
> B = \mu_0 nI = (4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\cdot m/A})(2.14\times10^3\ \mathrm{m^{-1}})(0.410\ \mathrm A) = 1.10\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm T .
> $$
>
> Check the model at the centre with [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-2|Theorem §A8.5.2]]: $y_{1,2} = \mp7.0\ \mathrm{cm}$, $R = 0.60\ \mathrm{cm}$, so the bracket is $2\times7.0/\sqrt{7.0^2+0.60^2} = 2\times0.996$: the finite solenoid's central field is only $0.4\%$ below $\mu_0nI$. A length of about 12 diameters is "infinite" for practical purposes.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 12.9*

^ex-a8-5-1

> [!example] Example §A8.5.2: An MRI Magnet
> An MRI magnet has a bore $1\ \mathrm m$ in diameter and an outer diameter of $3\ \mathrm m$; the space between is filled with close-packed wires $1\ \mathrm{mm}$ thick, all carrying the same current $I$ around the bore. What $I$ gives $7\ \mathrm T$ in the bore? The windings are many solenoids nested inside each other, $(1.5 - 0.5)\ \mathrm m / 1\ \mathrm{mm} = 1000$ layers, each with $1000$ turns per metre. Take an Amperian rectangle with one long side of length $l$ in the bore and the other outside the magnet: it encloses $1000\times1000\,l = 10^6\,l$ turns per metre of length, so
>
> $$
> Bl = \mu_0\,(10^6\ \mathrm{m^{-1}})\,l\,I \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I = \frac{7\ \mathrm T}{(4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\cdot m/A})(10^6\ \mathrm{m^{-1}})} = 5.6\ \mathrm A .
> $$
>
> A modest current per wire, but a million wires per metre: the total current through the rectangle is about $5.6\times10^6\ \mathrm A$ per metre of magnet.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 21*

^ex-a8-5-2

> [!example] Example §A8.5.3: A Tokamak's Toroidal Field
> A fusion reactor's toroidal coil has $N = 1.0\times10^6$ turns, with inner radius $4\ \mathrm m$ and outer radius $30\ \mathrm m$. What current gives $10\ \mathrm T$ at $r = 5\ \mathrm m$? From [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-4|Theorem §A8.5.4]],
>
> $$
> I = \frac{2\pi rB}{\mu_0 N} = \frac{2\pi(5\ \mathrm m)(10\ \mathrm T)}{(4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\cdot m/A})(1.0\times10^6)} = 250\ \mathrm A .
> $$
>
> Since $B \propto 1/r$, the same current gives only $B = 10\ \mathrm T\times(5/30) = 1.7\ \mathrm T$ at the outer wall: in such a wide toroid the field varies strongly.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 21*

^ex-a8-5-3
