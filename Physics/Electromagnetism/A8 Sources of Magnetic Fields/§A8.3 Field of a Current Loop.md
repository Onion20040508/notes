A circular loop is the other basic shape of current, and the one that occurs in nature: charge cannot pile up anywhere in a steady state, so currents close on themselves. The Biot–Savart law gives the loop's field on its axis in closed form. Far away this field falls off as $1/y^3$ and is fixed by the loop's dipole moment alone, the same moment that measured the torque on the loop in [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop|§A7.4]].

## Field on the axis

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.3.1: Field on the Axis of a Circular Loop
> A circular loop of radius $R$ carrying current $I$ produces, at a point on its axis a distance $y$ from its centre, the field
>
> $$
> \vec B = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2\,(y^2+R^2)^{3/2}}\,\hat n = \frac{\mu_0\,\vec\mu}{2\pi\,(y^2+R^2)^{3/2}} ,
> $$
>
> along the axis in the direction $\hat n$ of the right-hand rule (fingers along the current, thumb along $\hat n$); $\vec\mu = I\pi R^2\,\hat n$ is the loop's dipole moment. At the centre ($y = 0$), $B = \mu_0 I/(2R)$; a flat coil of $N$ turns gives $N$ times these values.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.4, eqs. (12.13)–(12.17)*

^thm-a8-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let the loop lie in the $xz$-plane with centre at the origin and $P$ at height $y$ on the $y$-axis. Every element $I\,d\vec l$ is at distance $r = \sqrt{y^2+R^2}$ from $P$ and perpendicular to $\hat r$, so $dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,dl}{y^2+R^2}$, directed perpendicular to $\hat r$ at an angle $\theta$ to the axis with $\cos\theta = R/\sqrt{y^2+R^2}$.
>
> The element diametrically opposite gives a $d\vec B'$ of the same size whose component perpendicular to the axis is opposite, so these components cancel in pairs and only the axial components $dB\cos\theta$ survive. All factors are the same for every element, so
>
> $$
> B = \oint dB\cos\theta = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\frac{R}{(y^2+R^2)^{3/2}}\oint dl = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\frac{R\cdot2\pi R}{(y^2+R^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\mu_0 IR^2}{2(y^2+R^2)^{3/2}} .
> $$
>
> Writing $IR^2 = \mu/\pi$ gives the second form; $y = 0$ gives $\mu_0 I/(2R)$, in agreement with [[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]].

^der-a8-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]], [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^def-a7-4-1|Def. §A7.4.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Turns, not turns per length
> For a flat coil the factor is the total number of turns $N$: $B = N\mu_0 I/(2R)$ at the centre. (UP2 writes "$\mu_0 nI/(2R)$ for a flat coil of $n$ loops per length", which mixes this up with the solenoid's turns per unit length, [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids|§A8.5]].)

^cau-a8-3-1

## The dipole field

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.3.2: Far Field on the Axis
> Far from the loop ($y \gg R$) the axial field is
>
> $$
> \vec B \approx \frac{\mu_0\,\vec\mu}{2\pi y^3} ,
> $$
>
> falling off as $1/y^3$ and depending on the loop only through its dipole moment $\vec\mu$, not on $I$ and $R$ separately.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.4, eq. (12.19)*

^thm-a8-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $y \gg R$, $(y^2+R^2)^{3/2} = y^3(1+R^2/y^2)^{3/2} \approx y^3$ in [[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop#^thm-a8-3-1|Theorem §A8.3.1]].

^der-a8-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop#^thm-a8-3-1|Theorem §A8.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Dipoles everywhere
> - The off-axis field of a loop needs harder mathematics (the figure was computed numerically), but its shape far away is the same as that of an electric dipole's field ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles|§A1.5]]): a current loop (current × area) and a pair of separated charges (charge × separation) both produce a *dipole field*, scaled by their dipole moment.
> - Most fields in nature are dipole fields at large distance. Charges are usually separated only slightly, leaving objects neutral; and steady currents always close into loops ("charge motion bites its own tail"). Earth's field (charge flowing in the liquid core) and a store-bought magnet (aligned electron spins, [[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter|§A8.6]]) are both, from far away, magnetic dipoles.
> - The names N and S label the regions where the lines leave and enter; the loop of current is the source.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.4; PHY 182, week 11 slides*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A8.3.1: Two Coaxial Loops
> Two coaxial loops carry $I = 10\ \mathrm{mA}$ in opposite senses. Loop 1 has radius $R_1 = 0.50\ \mathrm m$ and is $y_1 = 0.25\ \mathrm m$ from the point $P$ on the axis; loop 2 has $R_2 = 1.0\ \mathrm m$ and is $y_2 = 0.75\ \mathrm m$ from $P$. The axial fields are opposite, so they subtract:
>
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0 IR_1^2}{2(y_1^2+R_1^2)^{3/2}} - \frac{\mu_0 IR_2^2}{2(y_2^2+R_2^2)^{3/2}} = (8.99 - 3.22)\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm T = 5.8\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm T ,
> $$
>
> along the axis in the direction of loop 1's field. The nearer, smaller loop wins. (With equal radii and currents in the *same* sense, two such loops give a nearly uniform field between them: Helmholtz coils.)
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 12.5*

^ex-a8-3-1

> [!example] Example §A8.3.2: A Spinning Charged Ring
> A thin ring of radius $R$ carries charge $q > 0$ spread uniformly, and it spins so that its material moves with speed $v$. Find $B$ at the centre.
> - As a current: the linear charge density is $\lambda = q/(2\pi R)$, and the charge passing a fixed point per unit time is $I = \lambda v = qv/(2\pi R)$. By [[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop#^thm-a8-3-1|Theorem §A8.3.1]], $B = \mu_0 I/(2R) = \mu_0 qv/(4\pi R^2)$.
> - As moving charges: each piece $dq$ has $\vec v\perp\hat r$ at distance $R$, so $dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{v\,dq}{R^2}$, all along the axis; summing, $B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{qv}{R^2}$, the same.
>
> If the ring turns clockwise as seen by the viewer, so does the current, and $\vec B$ at the centre points away from the viewer. "Current × length" and "charge × velocity" are two bookkeepings of the same source.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 19*
