Why do some materials stick to magnets, and how can a lump of iron be a magnet with no wires at all? The answer is Ampère's: atoms carry tiny current loops, that is, magnetic dipoles ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^def-a7-4-1|Def. §A7.4.1]]). How strongly these dipoles line up in an applied field, and whether they stay lined up, sorts materials into three classes, and an aligned array of dipoles acts like a current flowing around the surface of the sample, the solenoid of [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids|§A8.5]].

## Atomic dipoles

> [!remark] Remark: Every atom is a set of current loops
> An electron in an atom both orbits and spins on its own axis; each motion amounts to a current loop and hence a magnetic dipole. An atom's net moment is the vector sum: zero for some atoms (He), and typically about $10^{-23}\ \mathrm{A\cdot m^2}$ otherwise (H, Li, Na: $9.27\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{A\cdot m^2}$; O, S: $13.9\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{A\cdot m^2}$). A handful of matter has about $10^{26}$ atoms. With no applied field their moments point at random and the sample has no net moment; in an applied field the torque $\vec\mu\times\vec B$ ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-2|Theorem §A7.4.2]]) tends to align them. (The quantum theory of spin: [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^pr-a5-2-6|QM Principle §A5.2.6]].)

^rem-a8-6-1

> [!definition] Definition §A8.6.1: Paramagnetic, Diamagnetic and Ferromagnetic Materials
> - In a **paramagnetic** material the atoms have permanent moments, and an applied field aligns a small fraction of them, adding a weak field in the same direction as the applied one; the alignment disappears when the field is removed.
> - In a **ferromagnetic** material the moments align strongly and lock together, so the material can stay magnetized when the applied field is turned off or reversed.
> - In a **diamagnetic** material the atoms have no permanent moment; the applied field induces moments opposite to it, producing a weak field that opposes the applied field.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.7*

^def-a8-6-1

## Susceptibility and permeability

> [!definition] Definition §A8.6.2: Magnetic Susceptibility; Permeability
> Fill a long solenoid with a sample. The field in the material is $\vec B = \vec B_0 + \vec B_m$, where $\vec B_0$ is the field the solenoid current alone would produce and $\vec B_m$ is the field of the aligned (or induced) atomic dipoles. If $\vec B_m$ is proportional to $\vec B_0$,
>
> $$
> \vec B_m = \chi\,\vec B_0 ,
> $$
>
> the dimensionless constant $\chi$ is the **magnetic susceptibility** of the material, and
>
> $$
> \mu = (1+\chi)\,\mu_0
> $$
>
> is its **magnetic permeability**. $\chi > 0$ for paramagnets, $\chi < 0$ for diamagnets.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.7, eqs. (12.34)–(12.35), (12.38)*

^def-a8-6-2

> [!law] Law §A8.6.1: Linear Magnetic Response
> In paramagnetic and diamagnetic materials the induced field is proportional to the applied field, with a susceptibility that is a property of the material (and its temperature), so
>
> $$
> \vec B = (1+\chi)\,\vec B_0 , \qquad B = (1+\chi)\,\mu_0 nI = \mu nI \ \ \text{(filled long solenoid)} .
> $$
>
> Typical values at room temperature: aluminium $2.2\times10^{-5}$, chromium $3.1\times10^{-4}$, liquid oxygen (90 K) $3.5\times10^{-3}$, air $3.6\times10^{-7}$; bismuth $-1.7\times10^{-5}$, copper $-9.7\times10^{-6}$, water $-9.1\times10^{-6}$. All are tiny: inserting a para- or diamagnetic core changes a solenoid's field by a fraction of a percent at most.
>
> *Valid when:* the material is para- or diamagnetic and the temperature is fixed; it fails for ferromagnets, whose $\chi$ is large and depends on the field and on the material's history (below).
> *Source: UP2 §12.7, eqs. (12.36)–(12.39), Table 12.2*

^law-a8-6-1

> [!remark] Remark: Compare the dielectric
> The structure parallels a dielectric in a capacitor ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^law-a4-4-1|Law §A4.4.1]], [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^mod-a4-4-3|Model §A4.4.3]]): an applied field orients microscopic dipoles, and their own field changes the total by a material constant. The signs differ: a dielectric always *weakens* the electric field, while a paramagnet *strengthens* the magnetic field and only a diamagnet weakens it.

^rem-a8-6-2

> [!model] Model §A8.6.2: Magnetization as a Surface Current
> Treat each aligned atomic dipole in a sample filling a solenoid as a small current loop, all in the same sense. Neighbouring loops carry opposite currents along their shared sides, which cancel inside the material; what remains is an effective current $I_m$ circulating around the **surface** of the sample, like an extra solenoid winding. Its field is $\vec B_m$: along $\vec B_0$ for paramagnets and ferromagnets (surface current in the sense of the solenoid current), opposite to it for diamagnets (surface current against it).
>
> *Fails when:* one asks about fields on the atomic scale; the model describes the average field over many atoms.
> *Source: UP2 §12.7, Figure 12.23*

^mod-a8-6-2

## Para- and diamagnetism

> [!remark] Remark: Why paramagnetism is weak, and why diamagnetism is everywhere
> - In a paramagnet the aligning torque competes with thermal collisions that randomize the moments. At room temperature the thermal energy per atom is a few hundred times the magnetic alignment energy even in a strong field (Example §A8.6.1), so only a small fraction of the dipoles is aligned at any instant; hence $\chi \sim 10^{-5}$–$10^{-3}$.
> - A field always *induces* an opposing moment in an atom. In para- and ferromagnetic materials this is masked by the much stronger permanent moments; in diamagnetic materials, which have none, it is what remains. Water is diamagnetic, and frogs and mice have been levitated in strong non-uniform fields, their water repelled from the strong-field region.

^rem-a8-6-3

## Ferromagnetism

> [!definition] Definition §A8.6.3: Magnetic Domains; Hysteresis
> A ferromagnet consists of **magnetic domains**, regions of about $10^{-12}$ to $10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m^3}$ containing $10^{17}$ to $10^{21}$ atoms whose moments are rigidly aligned by a quantum-mechanical coupling too strong for thermal agitation at room temperature to break. In an unmagnetized sample the domains point at random. An applied field rotates the domains' moments toward it and makes aligned domains grow at the expense of the others, producing a large net moment; $\chi$ is of order $10^3$ to $10^4$ and depends on $B_0$. The field $B$ in the material then depends on the history of the applied field, not only on its present value: this is **hysteresis**, and the closed curve traced by $B$ as $B_0$ is swept back and forth is the **hysteresis loop**. In particular $B \ne 0$ when $B_0$ returns to zero: the material has become a permanent magnet.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.7*

^def-a8-6-3

![[ph-em-a8-6-1.svg]]
*A schematic hysteresis loop (blue): field $B$ in a ferromagnet versus the applied field $B_0$. An unmagnetized sample follows the dashed red curve from $0$ to $a$. Reducing $B_0$ and reversing it leads along the upper branch to $b$, and increasing it again along the lower branch back to $a$. At $B_0 = 0$ on the upper branch the material keeps a large field (blue dot): it is permanently magnetized. The horizontal scale is in reality thousands of times finer than the vertical one.*

> [!remark] Remark: Ferromagnets in practice
> - Besides iron, only cobalt, nickel, gadolinium and dysprosium have domains, together with many of their alloys; some materials are ferromagnetic only at low temperatures.
> - A bar magnet is, by [[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^mod-a8-6-2|Model §A8.6.2]], a short solenoid with a large surface current, and the two have the same field pattern ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge|§A7.1]], figure). Outside, lines leave the N pole and enter the S pole; inside, they run from S to N.
> - Data storage uses layered ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic films whose electrical resistance changes markedly with an applied field (giant magnetoresistance, Nobel Prize 2007), so that magnetically stored bits can be read electrically.

^rem-a8-6-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A8.6.1: Thermal Energy against Alignment Energy
> Compare, for atomic hydrogen ($\mu = 9.3\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{A\cdot m^2}$) in $B = 1.0\ \mathrm T$ at $27\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, the energy needed to flip a dipole from aligned to anti-aligned with the thermal energy. By [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-3|Theorem §A7.4.3]], the difference between $U = -\mu B$ and $U = +\mu B$ is
>
> $$
> U_B = 2\mu B = 2(9.3\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{A\cdot m^2})(1.0\ \mathrm T) = 1.9\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm J ,
> $$
>
> while the thermal energy per atom is about $kT = (1.38\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K})(300\ \mathrm K) = 4.1\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm J$, some 220 times larger. Collisions easily knock dipoles out of alignment, which is why paramagnetic susceptibilities are so small.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.7*

^ex-a8-6-1

> [!example] Example §A8.6.2: An Iron Core
> A long coil with $n = 20$ turns per centimetre is wound tightly on an iron cylinder and carries $I_0 = 0.20\ \mathrm A$. The applied field is
>
> $$
> B_0 = \mu_0 nI_0 = (4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\cdot m/A})(2000\ \mathrm{m^{-1}})(0.20\ \mathrm A) = 5.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm T .
> $$
>
> The measured magnetization curve of this (annealed) iron gives $B = 1.4\ \mathrm T$ at this $B_0$, so
>
> $$
> \chi = \frac{B}{B_0} - 1 = \frac{1.4\ \mathrm T}{5.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm T} - 1 = 2.8\times10^3 .
> $$
>
> The iron multiplies the field almost three thousandfold. On the same curve, $B_0 = 1.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm T$ gives $B = 0.60\ \mathrm T$ ($\chi \approx 6.0\times10^3$) and $B_0 = 6.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm T$ gives $1.5\ \mathrm T$ ($\chi \approx 2.5\times10^3$): for a ferromagnet $\chi$ is not a constant, and the linear law fails.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 12.10, §12.7*

^ex-a8-6-2
