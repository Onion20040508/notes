For the electric field, Gauss's law ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]) turned symmetric problems into one-line calculations by relating a flux to the enclosed charge. Its magnetic analogue fails: the flux of $\vec B$ through every closed surface is zero ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^rem-a7-1-3|§A7.1]]), because magnetic field lines curl around their sources instead of leaving them. The useful integral goes *around* the current instead: the circulation of $\vec B$ around a closed path measures the current that passes through it.

## Circulation and enclosed current

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.4.1: Ampère's Law
> For steady currents and any closed path $C$,
>
> $$
> \oint_C \vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} ,
> $$
>
> where $I_{\text{enc}}$ is the net current through any open surface bounded by $C$. Sign: curl the fingers of the right hand along the direction of integration; a current through the surface along the thumb counts positive, against it negative. Currents outside the path do not appear on the right, although they contribute to $\vec B$ on the path.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.5, eqs. (12.20)–(12.23)*

^thm-a8-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *One long straight wire.* Let the current $I$ run along the $z$-axis, out of the page. Its field is $B = \mu_0I/(2\pi r)$ along the counterclockwise circles around the wire ([[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-2|Theorem §A8.2.2]]). Split a step $d\vec l$ of any closed path into radial, axial and azimuthal parts; only the azimuthal part, of length $r\,d\varphi$ ($\varphi$ the angle around the wire), is along $\vec B$. So
>
> $$
> \oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \oint\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\,r\,d\varphi = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi}\oint d\varphi .
> $$
>
> A path that goes once around the wire counterclockwise has $\oint d\varphi = 2\pi$, giving $\mu_0 I$; one that does not enclose the wire comes back to its starting angle with $\oint d\varphi = 0$ (it sweeps through positive and negative $d\varphi$ alike), giving $0$; going clockwise gives $-\mu_0 I$, the sign rule.
>
> *Several parallel wires.* The fields add, so the circulations add: each enclosed wire contributes $\pm\mu_0 I_i$, each outside wire $0$.
>
> *In general* (any shape of steady current) the law can be derived from the Biot–Savart law, but the proof needs vector calculus (the curl and Stokes' theorem) and is done at level B ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]). At this level it is used as stated.

^der-a8-4-1

*Uses:* [[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-2|Theorem §A8.2.2]], [[§27 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^def-27-2|452 Def. §27.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the law says about $\vec B$
> - $\vec B$ is **not conservative**: its circulation around a path enclosing current is not zero. This is the sharp contrast with the electrostatic field, whose circulation $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l$ is always zero, so that it has a potential ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]]).
> - Gauss's law for $\vec E$ captures how field lines spread out from charge; $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec A = 0$ says that magnetic lines never spread out; Ampère's law captures how they curl around current. The PHY 182 summary: "magnetic field lines do not converge or diverge; they do curl around their source."
> - The law holds for steady currents. For changing fields Maxwell added a second source term, the displacement current ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-1|Principle §A12.1.1]]).

^rem-a8-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: When it computes $\vec B$
> Ampère's law is always true, but it *gives* $B$ only when symmetry lets $B$ come out of the integral. Choose a path on which $\vec B$ is, piece by piece, either parallel to $d\vec l$ with constant magnitude (contributing $Bl$) or perpendicular to it or zero (contributing nothing). Then $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = B\,l$ and $B = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}/l$. This works for the infinite straight wire, a long cylinder, an infinite sheet, the ideal solenoid and the toroid ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids|§A8.5]]). For a finite wire or a single loop no such path exists, and the Biot–Savart law is needed.

^rem-a8-4-2

## Cylindrical conductors

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.4.2: Field of a Long Cylindrical Conductor
> A long straight wire of radius $a$ carrying current $I_0$ spread uniformly over its cross-section produces a field circling the axis with magnitude
>
> $$
> B = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\mu_0 I_0}{2\pi a^2}\,r, & r \le a,\\[2ex] \dfrac{\mu_0 I_0}{2\pi r}, & r \ge a. \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Outside, the wire acts like a thin wire; inside, $B$ grows linearly from zero on the axis.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.5, Examples 12.6–12.7*

^thm-a8-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Shape of the field.* Every current element points along the axis, so by the Biot–Savart law its field $d\vec l\times\hat r$ is perpendicular to the axis: $\vec B$ has no axial component. By symmetry, the magnitudes of the remaining components $B_r$ (radial) and $B_\theta$ (circling) are the same at all points of a circle of radius $r$ about the axis. A nonzero $B_r$, all outward or all inward, would give a net flux through a closed cylinder around the axis, which is impossible ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^rem-a7-1-3|§A7.1]]); so $\vec B$ circles the axis.
>
> *Ampère's law* on that circle, traversed along $\vec B$: $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = B\,2\pi r$. For $r \ge a$ the path encloses $I_0$, so $B = \mu_0I_0/(2\pi r)$. For $r \le a$ it encloses the fraction of the cross-section inside it; the current density $J = I_0/(\pi a^2)$ is uniform, so $I_{\text{enc}} = J\pi r^2 = I_0 r^2/a^2$ and $B\,2\pi r = \mu_0 I_0 r^2/a^2$.

^der-a8-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-1|Theorem §A8.4.1]], [[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]], [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^def-a5-2-3|Def. §A5.2.3]]

![[ph-em-a8-4-1.svg]]
*The field of a uniform current in a wire of radius $a$ (blue): linear inside (grey region), $1/r$ outside, largest at the surface. The dashed red curve is the $1/r$ field a thin wire with the same current would have there.*

> [!remark] Remark: Same pattern as a uniformly charged cylinder
> The result mirrors the electric field of a long uniformly charged cylinder found with Gauss's law ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-3|Theorem §A2.3.3]]): linear inside, $1/r$ outside, because in both cases only the source enclosed by the chosen path or surface matters and it grows as $r^2$ inside. The PHY 182 slides also let the current density vary with $r$; then $I_{\text{enc}} = \int_0^r J(r')\,2\pi r'\,dr'$ and the same steps apply.

^rem-a8-4-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A8.4.1: Counting Enclosed Current
> A closed path, traversed counterclockwise, encloses four long wires: $3\ \mathrm A$ and $4\ \mathrm A$ out of the page, $2\ \mathrm A$ and $5\ \mathrm A$ into it. With the thumb out of the page as positive,
>
> $$
> \oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0(3 + 4 - 2 - 5)\ \mathrm A = 0 .
> $$
>
> The field on the path is not zero (each wire's field is there), but its circulation is; no detail of the path's shape matters, only what it encloses. Likewise a power cord carries equal currents to and from a device, so a path around the whole cord encloses no net current: ideally there is no circulating field around the cord.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 12 slides; UP2 Example 12.8*

^ex-a8-4-1

> [!example] Example §A8.4.2: Coaxial Cable
> A coaxial cable has an inner conductor of radius $a$ and an outer conducting shell from $b$ to $c$; a current $I$ flows one way in the inner conductor and back the other way in the shell, each spread uniformly. Circular paths of radius $r$ about the axis give $B\,2\pi r = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}$:
>
> | region | $I_{\text{enc}}$ | $B$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | $r < a$ | $I r^2/a^2$ | $\mu_0 I r/(2\pi a^2)$ |
> | $a < r < b$ | $I$ | $\mu_0 I/(2\pi r)$ |
> | $b < r < c$ | $I\,\dfrac{c^2 - r^2}{c^2 - b^2}$ | $\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\,\dfrac{c^2-r^2}{c^2-b^2}$ |
> | $r > c$ | $0$ | $0$ |
>
> In the shell, the enclosed return current is $I(r^2-b^2)/(c^2-b^2)$ (uniform density over the area $\pi(c^2-b^2)$). The field lives only inside the cable: outside, the two currents cancel.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 21*

^ex-a8-4-2

> [!example] Example §A8.4.3: An Infinite Sheet of Current
> A large flat conducting sheet carries current out of the page with $K = 0.01\ \mathrm A$ per millimetre of width ($K = 10\ \mathrm{A/m}$). Think of it as many parallel wires side by side. Adding the circular fields of neighbouring wires, the components perpendicular to the sheet cancel and those parallel to it add: $\vec B$ is parallel to the sheet and perpendicular to the current, pointing left above the sheet and right below it, with the same magnitude at equal distances. Take a rectangle straddling the sheet, with sides of length $l$ parallel to the sheet above and below it; the short sides contribute nothing ($\vec B\perp d\vec l$). Then
>
> $$
> 2Bl = \mu_0 Kl \quad\Longrightarrow\quad B = \frac{\mu_0K}{2} = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(10)}{2}\ \mathrm T = 6.3\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm T ,
> $$
>
> independent of the distance from the sheet, like the electric field of an infinite charged plane ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-9|Theorem §A1.4.9]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 21*

^ex-a8-4-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l$ is a line integral of a vector field around a closed curve ([[§27 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^def-27-2|452 Def. §27.2]]); its dependence on the direction of traversal is the sign rule of Ampère's law ([[§27 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^rem-27-1|452 §27]]).
> - With Stokes' theorem ([[§34 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-34-1|452 Thm. §34.1]]), $\oint_{\partial S}\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \iint_S(\nabla\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec A$; writing $I_{\text{enc}} = \iint_S\vec J\cdot d\vec A$ for every surface turns Ampère's law into $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ (level B: [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]). Stokes' right-hand orientation convention ([[§34 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^rem-34-1|452 §34]]) is exactly Ampère's sign rule.
> - The field of a straight wire along the $z$-axis, $\vec B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi}\,\frac{(-y,\,x)}{x^2+y^2}$, is the 1-form of [[§39 Closed and Exact Forms#^prop-39-6|452 Prop. §39.6]]: curl-free away from the wire, yet with circulation $\mu_0 I$ around it — closed but not exact, because the region outside the wire has a hole, where the Poincaré lemma ([[§39 Closed and Exact Forms#^prop-39-7|452 Prop. §39.7]]) does not apply. The $\oint d\varphi = 2\pi$ in the derivation above is that form's winding integral.
