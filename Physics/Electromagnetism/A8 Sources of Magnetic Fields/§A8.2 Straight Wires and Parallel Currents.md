The first application of the Biot–Savart law ([[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]]) is the simplest current there is: a straight wire. Its field circles the wire and falls off as $1/R$. Put a second wire in that field and the force of [[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]] follows: parallel currents attract, which is how the ampere used to be defined.

## Field of a straight wire

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.2.1: Field of a Straight Segment
> A straight segment carrying current $I$ produces at a point $P$ at perpendicular distance $R$ from its line a field of magnitude
>
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi R}\left[\frac{y_2}{\sqrt{y_2^2+R^2}} - \frac{y_1}{\sqrt{y_1^2+R^2}}\right],
> $$
>
> where $y_1 < y_2$ are the positions of the segment's ends measured along the line, in the direction of the current, from the foot of the perpendicular through $P$. The field is perpendicular to the plane containing the segment and $P$, with sense given by the right-hand rule (thumb along $I$, fingers curl along $\vec B$). For a segment of length $L$ seen from its midpoint ($y_{1,2} = \mp L/2$),
>
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi R}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + 4R^2/L^2}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.2, eqs. (12.5)–(12.7); PHY 182, week 11 slides*

^thm-a8-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put the wire on the $y$-axis with the current along $+\hat\jmath$ and $P$ at distance $R$ on the $x$-axis. For the element $I\,dy$ at height $y$, $d\vec l\times\hat r$ points the same way (perpendicular to the plane of wire and $P$) for every $y$, so magnitudes add. With $r^2 = y^2 + R^2$ and $\sin\theta = R/r$ (the angle between $d\vec l$ and $\hat r$),
>
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\int_{y_1}^{y_2}\frac{\sin\theta\,dy}{r^2} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\int_{y_1}^{y_2}\frac{R\,dy}{(y^2+R^2)^{3/2}} .
> $$
>
> An antiderivative is $y/\bigl(R\sqrt{y^2+R^2}\bigr)$: its derivative is $\frac1R\bigl[(y^2+R^2)^{-1/2} - y^2(y^2+R^2)^{-3/2}\bigr] = R/(y^2+R^2)^{3/2}$. Evaluating between $y_1$ and $y_2$ gives the result; with $y_{1,2} = \mp L/2$ the bracket is $L/\sqrt{L^2/4 + R^2}$, which rearranges to the midpoint form.

^der-a8-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.2.2: Field of a Long Straight Wire
> An infinitely long straight wire carrying current $I$ produces at distance $R$ the field
>
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi R} .
> $$
>
> The field lines are circles centred on the wire, in planes perpendicular to it, with the sense of the right-hand rule (RHR-2). In practice "infinitely long" means $L \gg R$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.2, eq. (12.8)*

^thm-a8-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $y_1 \to -\infty$ and $y_2 \to +\infty$ in [[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-1|Theorem §A8.2.1]]: each fraction $y/\sqrt{y^2+R^2}$ tends to $\pm1$, the bracket to $2$, and $B = \mu_0 I/(2\pi R)$. (Equivalently, $1/\sqrt{1+4R^2/L^2} \to 1$ as $L\to\infty$.) The field has the same magnitude at all points at distance $R$ and is tangent to the circle of radius $R$ around the wire, by the symmetry of the wire under rotations about itself.

^der-a8-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-1|Theorem §A8.2.1]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-2|451 Def. §36.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Wire of current, line of charge
> Compare the field of an infinite line of charge, $E = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 R)$ ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-5|Theorem §A1.4.5]]). The magnitudes match under $\lambda \leftrightarrow I$ and $1/\varepsilon_0 \leftrightarrow \mu_0$; only the direction differs, radial for $\vec E$ and circling for $\vec B$. Many magnetic results can be read off the electric ones this way, as long as the direction is fixed by the right-hand rule. The PHY 182 slides note the curiosity that $\lambda$ (charge per length) turns into $I$ (charge per time), a hint of the length–time connection of relativity.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 11 slides*

^rem-a8-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: $1/R$, not $1/R^2$
> Each element contributes a $1/r^2$ field, but an infinite line of elements gives $1/R$, as for the line of charge. The field of a *finite* segment far away ($R \gg L$) goes back to $1/R^2$: the midpoint formula then gives $B \approx \mu_0 IL/(4\pi R^2)$, the field of a single element.

^cau-a8-2-1

## Force between parallel currents

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.2.3: Force between Parallel Currents
> Two long parallel wires a distance $r$ apart carrying currents $I_1$ and $I_2$ exert on each other forces per unit length
>
> $$
> \frac Fl = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi r} ,
> $$
>
> attractive if the currents are in the same direction and repulsive if they are opposite. The forces on the two wires are equal and opposite.
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.3, eqs. (12.9)–(12.11)*

^thm-a8-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Wire 1 produces at wire 2 the field $B_1 = \mu_0 I_1/(2\pi r)$ ([[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-2|Theorem §A8.2.2]]), perpendicular to wire 2 and uniform along it. By [[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]] a length $l$ of wire 2 feels $F = I_2lB_1$, so $F/l = \mu_0 I_1I_2/(2\pi r)$. Direction: with both currents out of the page and wire 2 to the right of wire 1, $\vec B_1$ at wire 2 points up the page (RHR-2), and $I_2\hat k\times\hat\jmath = -I_2\hat\imath$, toward wire 1. Reversing one current reverses the force. Exchanging the roles of the wires gives the force on wire 1, of the same size and opposite direction.

^der-a8-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-2|Theorem §A8.2.2]], [[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]

![[ph-em-a8-2-1.svg]]
*Left: field lines of a long wire with current out of the page are counterclockwise circles; the spacing grows with distance because $B \propto 1/R$ (the radii grow by a constant factor). Right: wire 2 sits in the field $\vec B_1$ of wire 1 (blue); with both currents out of the page the forces (red) pull the wires together.*

> [!remark] Remark: The pinch effect
> The attraction does not need wires: any two parallel streams of charge attract magnetically. It is noticeable only when the net charge density is zero, as in a wire or a plasma; otherwise the Coulomb repulsion dominates (Example §A8.1.2). In an electric arc the parallel currents squeeze themselves into a thinner channel (the **pinch effect**): in large circuit breakers this can concentrate an arc enough to burn holes in the contacts, and in the Sun magnetic forces shape jets of ionized gas.

^rem-a8-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: The ampere from a force
> For $I_1 = I_2 = 1\ \mathrm A$ and $r = 1\ \mathrm m$, $F/l = (4\pi\times10^{-7})(1)^2/(2\pi\cdot1)\ \mathrm{N/m} = 2\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{N/m}$. In the SI as used in UP2, this *defined* the ampere: the current that, flowing in two long parallel wires $1\ \mathrm m$ apart, gives a force of exactly $2\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm N$ per metre; then $1\ \mathrm C = 1\ \mathrm{A\cdot s}$. In practice a current balance measures the force between coils a few centimetres apart. (Since 2019 the ampere is defined through the elementary charge instead, [[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^cau-a8-1-1|§A8.1]].)

^rem-a8-2-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A8.2.1: A Hexagonal Loop
> A regular hexagon with sides $L = 1.0\ \mathrm m$ carries $I = 0.10\ \mathrm A$ clockwise. Find $\vec B$ at its centre. The centre is at distance $R = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}L = 0.866\ \mathrm m$ from each side (a 30–60–90 triangle), opposite each side's midpoint, so by [[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-1|Theorem §A8.2.1]] each side gives
>
> $$
> B_1 = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi R}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+4R^2/L^2}} = \frac{(2\times10^{-7})(0.10)}{0.866}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+3}}\ \mathrm T = 1.15\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm T .
> $$
>
> All six sides give fields into the page (clockwise current), so $B = 6B_1 = 6.9\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm T$, into the page. Check: a circular loop has $B = \mu_0I/(2a)$ ([[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]]), which gives $6.3\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm T$ for $a = 1.0\ \mathrm m$ (circle through the corners) and $7.3\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm T$ for $a = 0.866\ \mathrm m$ (circle touching the sides); the hexagon lies between. Its dipole moment is $\mu = IA = (0.10\ \mathrm A)\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}(1.0\ \mathrm m)^2 = 0.26\ \mathrm{A\cdot m^2}$, also into the page.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 19*

^ex-a8-2-1

> [!example] Example §A8.2.2: Three Wires at the Corners of a Square
> Three long wires carry $2.0\ \mathrm A$ each into the page at three corners of a square of side $a = 1.0\ \mathrm{cm}$. Find $\vec B$ at the fourth corner $P$. Put $P$ at the origin and the wires at $(a,0)$, $(a,a)$, $(0,a)$. For a current into the page the field circles clockwise, so at $P$:
> - wire at $(a,0)$: $B = \mu_0I/(2\pi a) = 4.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm T$, along $+\hat\jmath$;
> - wire at $(0,a)$: $4.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm T$ along $-\hat\imath$;
> - wire at $(a,a)$, distance $\sqrt2 a$: $2.83\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm T$ along $(-\hat\imath+\hat\jmath)/\sqrt2$, i.e. $(-2.0\,\hat\imath + 2.0\,\hat\jmath)\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm T$.
>
> Sum: $\vec B = (-6.0\,\hat\imath + 6.0\,\hat\jmath)\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm T$, of magnitude $8.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm T$, perpendicular to the diagonal through $P$. The fields from different wires point in different directions, so they must be added as vectors, by components.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 12.3*

^ex-a8-2-2

> [!example] Example §A8.2.3: Forces between Two Wires
> Two long wires carry $5.0\ \mathrm{mA}$ each out of the page, wire 1 at $(0, 3.0\ \mathrm{cm})$ and wire 2 at $(4.0\ \mathrm{cm}, 0)$. Their separation is $r = 5.0\ \mathrm{cm}$, so
>
> $$
> \frac Fl = \frac{\mu_0 I^2}{2\pi r} = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(5.0\times10^{-3})^2}{2\pi(0.050)}\ \mathrm{N/m} = 1.0\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{N/m} .
> $$
>
> The currents are parallel, so the force on wire 2 points toward wire 1, along $(-4.0\,\hat\imath + 3.0\,\hat\jmath)/5.0$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\vec F_2}{l} = (-8.0\,\hat\imath + 6.0\,\hat\jmath)\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N/m}, \qquad \frac{\vec F_1}{l} = (8.0\,\hat\imath - 6.0\,\hat\jmath)\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N/m}.
> $$
>
> Equal and opposite, as Newton's third law requires; tiny, because milliamperes are small currents.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 12.4 (whose solution has the two directions interchanged)*

^ex-a8-2-3
