Chapter A7 took the magnetic field as given and asked what it does to moving charges. This chapter asks where the field comes from. Mass makes and feels gravity, charge makes and feels the electric field; moving charge, which feels the magnetic field, also makes it. The Biot–Savart law gives the field of each small piece of current, as Coulomb's law gives the field of each small piece of charge.

## The law

> [!principle] Principle §A8.1.1: Biot–Savart Law
> A current element $I\,d\vec l$ ($d\vec l$ along the current) produces at a point $P$ the magnetic field
>
> $$
> d\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec l\times\hat r}{r^2}, \qquad dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,dl\,\sin\theta}{r^2},
> $$
>
> where $\vec r = r\hat r$ points from the element to $P$ and $\theta$ is the angle between $d\vec l$ and $\hat r$. Fields of different elements add as vectors, so a wire produces
>
> $$
> \vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int_{\text{wire}}\frac{I\,d\vec l\times\hat r}{r^2} .
> $$
>
> Equivalently, a point charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ produces $\vec B = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\dfrac{q\,\vec v\times\hat r}{r^2}$ (replace $I\,d\vec l$ by $dq\,\vec v$).
>
> *Domain: steady currents (magnetostatics); the moving-charge form for speeds well below $c$. Changing currents and fields need Chapters A9 and A12.*
> *Source: UP2 §12.1, eqs. (12.1)–(12.4); PHY 182, week 10 and week 12 slides*

^pr-a8-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A8.1.1: Permeability of Free Space
> The constant in the Biot–Savart law is the **permeability of free space**,
>
> $$
> \mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\cdot m/A}, \qquad \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} = 1\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\cdot m/A},
> $$
>
> exactly in the SI as UP2 uses it, where the ampere was defined through the force between parallel currents ([[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^rem-a8-2-3|§A8.2]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.1, eq. (12.2)*

^def-a8-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: $\mu_0$ after 2019
> Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the ampere is defined by fixing the value of the elementary charge, and $\mu_0$ is a measured constant. It still equals $4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\cdot m/A}$ to better than one part in $10^{9}$, so nothing changes in calculations; only the word "exactly" is out of date.
>
> *Source: outside fact (2019 SI revision), not in UP2*

^cau-a8-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Coulomb's law, magnetic version
> The PHY 182 slides build the law by analogy with the field of a charge element, $d\vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{r^2}\hat r$ ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-2|Theorem §A1.4.2]]):
> - the source $dq$ becomes an amount of *moving* charge, $dq\,\vec v = I\,d\vec l$ (charge × length/time = charge/time × length);
> - the field still falls off as $1/r^2$;
> - the constant $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ becomes $\mu_0/(4\pi)$;
> - the direction $\hat r$ becomes $d\vec l\times\hat r$, perpendicular to both the current and the line to $P$.
>
> The last change is what makes the field lines *curl around* the current instead of pointing away from it. At each point $d\vec B$ has no component along $d\vec l$, and an element produces no field at all at points on its own line ($\theta = 0$ or $180^\circ$).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 10 slides*

^rem-a8-1-1

![[ph-em-a8-1-1.svg]]
*The Biot–Savart law: the element $I\,d\vec l$ (blue) and the unit vector $\hat r$ (red) toward the field point $P$ span the page, so $d\vec B \propto d\vec l\times\hat r$ points into the page at $P$.*

> [!remark] Remark: How to use it
> 1. Draw an element $d\vec l$ along the current and the vector $\vec r$ from it to the field point.
> 2. Find the direction of $d\vec l\times\hat r$ for a typical element. If all elements give the same direction (as for a straight wire, or at the centre of an arc), add magnitudes; otherwise split $d\vec B$ into components and use symmetry to see which cancel ([[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop|§A8.3]]).
> 3. Express $r$ and $\sin\theta$ through one integration variable, take constants out, integrate.
> 4. Check the direction with the right-hand rule. For symmetric current distributions Ampère's law is usually quicker ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law|§A8.4]]).

^rem-a8-1-2

## Field at the centre of an arc

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.1.2: Field at the Centre of a Circular Arc
> A wire bent into a circular arc of radius $R$ subtending the angle $\phi$ (in radians) and carrying current $I$ produces at the centre of the arc the field
>
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0 I\phi}{4\pi R} ,
> $$
>
> perpendicular to the plane of the arc, with direction given by the right-hand rule (fingers along the current, thumb along $\vec B$). For a full circle, $\phi = 2\pi$:
>
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §12.1, Example 12.2*

^thm-a8-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Every element of the arc is at distance $R$ from the centre, and $d\vec l$ is perpendicular to $\hat r$ there, so $\sin\theta = 1$. All the $d\vec l\times\hat r$ point the same way, perpendicular to the plane, so the magnitudes add. With $dl = R\,d\phi'$,
>
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{I\,dl}{R^2} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi R^2}\int_0^{\phi}R\,d\phi' = \frac{\mu_0 I\phi}{4\pi R} .
> $$

^der-a8-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Superposition of arcs and straight pieces
> Field at a point from a circuit made of arcs and straight segments: add the contributions, each with its own sign. A straight segment whose line passes through the point contributes nothing; an arc centred on the point contributes $\mu_0I\phi/(4\pi R)$; other straight segments are handled in [[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-1|Theorem §A8.2.1]].

^rem-a8-1-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A8.1.1: A Short Current Segment
> A wire segment of length $\Delta l = 1.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ carries $I = 2.0\ \mathrm A$ upward; the rest of the circuit is shielded. Find $B$ at $P$, $1.0\ \mathrm m$ from the segment along the perpendicular through its middle. Since $\Delta l \ll r$, treat the segment as one element with $r = 1.0\ \mathrm m$ and $\theta \approx 90^\circ$:
>
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,\Delta l\sin\theta}{r^2} = (1\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\cdot m/A})\,\frac{(2.0\ \mathrm A)(0.010\ \mathrm m)}{(1.0\ \mathrm m)^2} = 2.0\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm T ,
> $$
>
> into the page if $P$ lies to the right of the upward current ($\hat\jmath\times\hat\imath = -\hat k$). Doubling the distance would quarter the field; the approximation fails once $r$ is comparable to $\Delta l$, and then the integral is needed.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 12.1*

^ex-a8-1-1

> [!example] Example §A8.1.2: Two Moving Charges
> Two charges of $+1.0\ \mathrm C$ move side by side to the right at $1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$, one $1.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ above the other. Field of the lower charge at the upper one ($\vec v = v\hat\imath$, $\hat r = \hat\jmath$):
>
> $$
> \vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{qv}{r^2}\,(\hat\imath\times\hat\jmath) = (10^{-7})\frac{(1.0)(1.0)}{(0.010)^2}\ \mathrm T\ \hat k = 1.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm T\ \hat k ,
> $$
>
> out of the page. The magnetic force on the upper charge is $q\vec v\times\vec B = (1.0)(1.0)(10^{-3})\ \mathrm N\ (\hat\imath\times\hat k) = -1.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm N\ \hat\jmath$: toward the lower charge, an *attraction*. The Coulomb force is a repulsion of $kq^2/r^2 = 9.0\times10^{13}\ \mathrm N$. The ratio is
>
> $$
> \frac{F_B}{F_E} = \frac{(\mu_0/4\pi)\,q^2v^2/r^2}{q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,v^2 = 1.1\times10^{-17} ,
> $$
>
> and $1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 3.0\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$ is the speed of light ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|Theorem §A12.2.4]]), so $F_B/F_E = v^2/c^2$. Between moving charges the magnetic force is a tiny correction to the electric one. In a wire the carriers' charge is neutralized by the lattice, the electric force disappears, and the magnetic force between currents is all that is left ([[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-3|Theorem §A8.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 20*

^ex-a8-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Whose velocity?
> If the two charges of Example §A8.1.2 sit at rest in your hands on a moving train, an observer on the platform sees them moving and computes a magnetic force, while you see none. Both are right: what one observer calls a magnetic force another partly calls electric. The magnetic field depends on the frame, and sorting this out is the business of special relativity ([[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^rem-a1-1-4|REL Remark in §A1.1]], [[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-1|REL Principle §A1.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 20 and week 11 slides*
