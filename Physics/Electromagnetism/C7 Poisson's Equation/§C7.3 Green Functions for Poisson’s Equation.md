*Sources: Zangwill, Modern Electrodynamics, §8.4–§8.5.1 (Problems 8.13–8.15) · PHY 505 (Wells, Fall 2025) · Likharev, Essential Graduate Physics: EM, §2.9–§2.10.*

An image solution of [[§C7.1 The Method of Images|§C7.1]] answers one question: the potential of one charge with the boundaries grounded. Green's method turns that answer into the solution for *every* charge distribution and *every* set of boundary values in the same region. The potential of a unit charge with grounded walls, as a function of both the field point and the charge's position, is the Dirichlet Green function. Green's second identity ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-3|452 Thm. §17.3]]) then expresses any potential through its sources and its boundary values. Chapter C2 already read the Coulomb potential as a fundamental solution ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^rem-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Remark: The Coulomb potential as a fundamental solution]]); level B used only the free-space kernel $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0\mathcal R$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]) and promised the rest to level C ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images|§B3.2]], Connections). New here: Green functions as fundamental solutions with boundary conditions, in the sense of distributions, and the representation formula they satisfy; reciprocity; Dirichlet and Neumann solutions and why Neumann needs a modified condition; the force and energy of a charge near grounded conductors; positivity bounds. Three ways to construct Green functions (eigenfunctions, direct integration with a jump condition, splitting), with worked cases, are [[§C7.4 Constructing Green Functions|§C7.4]]. The general theory of fundamental solutions is in QFT ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-7|QFT Def. §CA.2.7]]), where the same objects for the Klein–Gordon operator need contours instead of walls ([[§C2.11 Green's Functions and Contours|QFT §C2.11]]).

*Notation.* As in [[§C7.1 The Method of Images|§C7.1]]: potential $V$, region $\mathcal V$ with boundary $S$ (area $A$ if bounded) and outward normal $\hat n$; $\partial/\partial n'$ acts on the variable $\vec r\,'$. Green functions are normalized as in Zangwill, $\varepsilon_0\nabla^2G = -\delta^3$, so that $G$ is the potential of a unit charge ([[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^cau-c7-3-1|Caution: Normalizations of the Green function]]).

## Green functions as fundamental solutions

> [!definition] Definition §C7.3.1: Green Function of Poisson's Equation; Dirichlet and Neumann
> A **Green function** for the region $\mathcal V$ is a function $G(\vec r, \vec r\,')$, defined for $\vec r \ne \vec r\,'$ in $\mathcal V$, such that for each $\vec r\,' \in \mathcal V$
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_0\nabla^2_{\vec r}\,G(\vec r, \vec r\,') = -\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,')
> $$
>
> as distributions in $\vec r$ on $\mathcal V$: $\int G(\vec r, \vec r\,')\,\nabla^2\varphi(\vec r)\,d^3r = -\varphi(\vec r\,')/\varepsilon_0$ for every test function $\varphi$ vanishing outside a bounded closed subset of $\mathcal V$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]). It is
> - a **Dirichlet Green function** $G_D$ if $G_D(\vec r_S, \vec r\,') = 0$ for $\vec r_S \in S$;
> - a **Neumann Green function** $G_N$ if $\partial G_N(\vec r_S, \vec r\,')/\partial n = -1/\varepsilon_0A$ for $\vec r_S \in S$ (bounded $\mathcal V$; $\partial G_N/\partial n = 0$ on a part at infinity).
>
> For unbounded $\mathcal V$ also $G \to 0$ as $|\vec r| \to \infty$. Physically, $G_D(\vec r, \vec r\,')$ is the potential at $\vec r$ of a unit point charge at $\vec r\,'$ when every boundary is a grounded conductor. In QFT's normalization ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-7|QFT Def. §CA.2.7]], $P(\partial)G = -i\delta$): $\nabla^2(i\varepsilon_0G) = -i\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,')$, so $i\varepsilon_0G_0 = i/4\pi\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert$ is a fundamental solution of $\nabla^2$ on $\mathbb R^3$ in exactly that sense, and $i\varepsilon_0G$ for a region obeys the same equation with the delta at $\vec r\,'$ and test functions supported in $\mathcal V$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.4, eqs. (8.49)–(8.50), §8.4.1–§8.4.2, eqs. (8.53), (8.55), §8.5 · Likharev §2.10, eqs. (2.202)–(2.205)*

> [!caution] Caution: Normalizations of the Green function
> The factor in front of $\delta^3$ is a convention, and the sources differ:
>
> | Source | Equation | Free-space $G$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | Zangwill, here | $\varepsilon_0\nabla^2G = -\delta^3$ | $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0\vert\vec r - \vec r\,'\vert$ |
> | Likharev, Jackson | $\nabla^2G = -4\pi\delta^3$ | $1/\vert\vec r - \vec r\,'\vert$ |
> | mathematics, 452 | $\nabla^2E = \delta^3$ | $-1/4\pi\vert\vec r - \vec r\,'\vert$ |
> | QFT (Peskin–Schroeder) | $P(\partial)G = -i\delta$ | for $\nabla^2$: $i/4\pi\vert\vec r - \vec r\,'\vert$ |
>
> Formulas such as the representation formula change their prefactors accordingly (Likharev's eq. (2.210) has $1/4\pi$ in front of the surface term). Likharev also takes $\partial/\partial n'$ along the normal pointing *into* the conductors, i.e. out of the field region, as here; some books use the opposite normal and flip the sign of the surface term (Likharev, footnote 72).
>
> *Source: Zangwill eq. (8.50) · Likharev §2.10, eqs. (2.202)–(2.210), footnotes 68, 72*

^cau-c7-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.1: The Free-Space Green Function; Every Green Function Is Coulomb Plus Harmonic
> 1. $G_0(\vec r, \vec r\,') = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}$ is the Green function of all space, and the only one vanishing at infinity.
> 2. A function $G$ is a Green function for $\mathcal V$ if and only if $G = G_0 + \Lambda$ with $\Lambda(\cdot, \vec r\,')$ smooth and harmonic in all of $\mathcal V$ for each $\vec r\,'$. The boundary conditions are conditions on $\Lambda$ alone: for $G_D$, $\Lambda(\vec r_S, \vec r\,') = -G_0(\vec r_S, \vec r\,')$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill eqs. (8.59), (8.64)–(8.65), (8.87) · Likharev eq. (2.204)*

^thm-c7-3-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** ($G_0$). By [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], $\nabla^2(1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|) = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,')$ as distributions; divide by $4\pi\varepsilon_0$. It vanishes at infinity.
>
> **Step 2** (only one in free space). If $G$ is another Green function of $\mathbb R^3$ vanishing at infinity, $H = G - G_0$ satisfies $\nabla^2H = 0$ as a distribution on $\mathbb R^3$ (the deltas cancel, as in [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-14|QFT Theorem §CA.2.14]], part 1), so $H$ is a smooth harmonic function (Weyl's lemma, as in [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^der-c7-1-1|Derivation of Theorem §C7.1.1]]). It is bounded, being continuous and tending to zero at infinity, hence constant by Liouville's theorem ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-4|Theorem §C6.1.4]]), hence zero.
>
> **Step 3** (any region). For a Green function $G$ of $\mathcal V$, $\Lambda = G - G_0$ satisfies $\nabla^2\Lambda = 0$ as a distribution on $\mathcal V$, so it is smooth and harmonic there. Conversely, adding such a $\Lambda$ to $G_0$ keeps the delta. Since $G_0$ is a fixed function, every boundary condition on $G$ is a condition on $\Lambda$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The singular part of every Green function is the same Coulomb potential; the region and its boundary conditions live entirely in the smooth part $\Lambda$, the potential of the charge induced by a unit charge ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-1|Theorem §C7.1.1]] with $q = 1$).
> - For the Laplacian the free-space choice is forced by decay. The Fourier symbol $-|\vec k|^2$ vanishes only at $\vec k = 0$, so free-space fundamental solutions differ only by harmonic functions, and decay removes them. The Klein–Gordon symbol $m^2 - p^2$ vanishes on the whole mass shell, so there a choice remains even without walls, and it is made with contours ([[§C2.11 Green's Functions and Contours#^thm-c2-11-3|QFT Theorem §C2.11.3]]). In electrostatics the walls make the choice.

*Uses:* [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-14|QFT Theorem §CA.2.14]], [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-4|Theorem §C6.1.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.2: The Representation Formula
> Let $V$ be twice continuously differentiable on $\mathcal V$ up to $S$, with $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$ and $\rho$ bounded, and let $G$ be any Green function for $\mathcal V$. For $\vec r \in \mathcal V$,
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \int_{\mathcal V}d^3r'\,\rho(\vec r\,')\,G(\vec r\,', \vec r) - \varepsilon_0\oint_SdS'\,V(\vec r\,')\,\frac{\partial G(\vec r\,', \vec r)}{\partial n'} + \varepsilon_0\oint_SdS'\,G(\vec r\,', \vec r)\,\frac{\partial V(\vec r\,')}{\partial n'} .
> $$
>
> With $G = G_0$ this says: in $\mathcal V$, $V$ is the Coulomb potential of $\rho$ in $\mathcal V$, plus that of a surface charge $\varepsilon_0\partial V/\partial n'$ on $S$, plus that of a dipole layer on $S$ with moment density $\varepsilon_0V$ per area along the inward normal $-\hat n'$ ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]], [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-8|Theorem §C3.1.8]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.4, eqs. (8.51)–(8.52) · Likharev §2.10, eqs. (2.207)–(2.209)*

^thm-c7-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (cut out the singularity). Green's second identity ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-3|452 Thm. §17.3]]) needs $C^2$ functions, and $G(\cdot, \vec r)$ is singular at $\vec r$. Let $B_\epsilon$ be the ball of radius $\epsilon$ about $\vec r$ and $\mathcal V_\epsilon = \mathcal V\setminus\overline{B_\epsilon}$. With $u = V$ and $v = G(\cdot, \vec r)$ as functions of $\vec r\,'$:
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V_\epsilon}\bigl(V\nabla'^2G - G\nabla'^2V\bigr)\,d^3r' = \oint_S\Bigl(V\frac{\partial G}{\partial n'} - G\frac{\partial V}{\partial n'}\Bigr)dS' + \oint_{\partial B_\epsilon}\Bigl(V\frac{\partial G}{\partial n'} - G\frac{\partial V}{\partial n'}\Bigr)dS' ,
> $$
>
> where on $\partial B_\epsilon$ the outward normal of $\mathcal V_\epsilon$ points *into* the ball: $\hat n' = -\hat e$, $\hat e = (\vec r\,' - \vec r)/\epsilon$. (For unbounded $\mathcal V$ add a large sphere; its contribution vanishes if $V$, $G = O(1/r')$ and their gradients are $O(1/r'^2)$.)
>
> **Step 2** (left side). In $\mathcal V_\epsilon$, $\nabla'^2G = 0$ and $\nabla'^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$, so the left side is $\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V_\epsilon}\rho\,G\,d^3r'$. As $\epsilon \to 0$ it tends to $\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}\rho\,G\,d^3r'$, because $\rho$ is bounded and $G$ is integrable near $\vec r$ ($\int_{B_\epsilon}d^3r'/|\vec r\,' - \vec r| = 2\pi\epsilon^2$).
>
> **Step 3** (small sphere). By [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], $G = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\epsilon} + \Lambda$ on $\partial B_\epsilon$ with $\Lambda$ smooth, so
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial G}{\partial n'} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon}\Bigl(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\epsilon}\Bigr) - \hat e\cdot\nabla'\Lambda = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\epsilon^2} + O(1) .
> $$
>
> With $dS' = \epsilon^2d\Omega$:
>
> $$
> \oint_{\partial B_\epsilon}V\frac{\partial G}{\partial n'}dS' = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint V\,d\Omega + O(\epsilon^2) \to \frac{V(\vec r)}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \Bigl|\oint_{\partial B_\epsilon}G\frac{\partial V}{\partial n'}dS'\Bigr| \le \Bigl(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\epsilon} + O(1)\Bigr)\max|\nabla V|\,4\pi\epsilon^2 \to 0 ,
> $$
>
> the first by continuity of $V$ (its average over a shrinking sphere tends to $V(\vec r)$).
>
> **Step 4** (assemble). $\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int\rho G = \oint_S(V\partial_{n'}G - G\partial_{n'}V)\,dS' + V(\vec r)/\varepsilon_0$; multiply by $\varepsilon_0$ and solve for $V(\vec r)$.
>
> **Step 5** (the layers, $G = G_0$). $\varepsilon_0\oint G_0\,\partial_{n'}V\,dS' = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint\frac{\varepsilon_0\partial_{n'}V}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}dS'$ is a single layer of charge density $\varepsilon_0\partial V/\partial n'$. Using $\nabla'|\vec r\,' - \vec r|^{-1} = (\vec r - \vec r\,')/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3$,
>
> $$
> -\varepsilon_0\oint V\,\frac{\partial G_0}{\partial n'}\,dS' = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint\bigl(\varepsilon_0V\bigr)(-\hat n')\cdot\frac{\vec r - \vec r\,'}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3}\,dS' ,
> $$
>
> the potential of point dipoles $\varepsilon_0V\,dS'$ pointing along $-\hat n'$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - In distribution language the formula is the pairing of $\varepsilon_0\nabla'^2G = -\delta^3$ with $V$; the excision in Step 1 is needed because $V$ is not a test function (it does not vanish on $S$), and the boundary terms are exactly what the pairing leaves behind.
> - ⚑ Cauchy data overdetermine: the formula wants both $V$ and $\partial V/\partial n$ on $S$, but by uniqueness either one fixes the solution ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^cau-c6-1-1|§C6.1, Caution: Too much data, too little data, infinite energy]]). A good Green function removes the term whose data are not given (Theorems §C7.3.4 and §C7.3.6).
> - ⚑ The single and dipole layers of Step 5 reproduce $V$ inside $\mathcal V$ and give zero outside it (repeat Steps 1–4 for a point $\vec r$ outside $\mathcal V$: no small sphere, no $V(\vec r)$ term). Every potential in a region can be replaced by charges and dipoles on its boundary.

*Uses:* [[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-3|452 Thm. §17.3]], [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.3: Reciprocity
> For $\vec r_1 \ne \vec r_2$ in $\mathcal V$ (bounded, or unbounded with $G_D \to 0$ at infinity),
>
> $$
> G_D(\vec r_1, \vec r_2) = G_D(\vec r_2, \vec r_1) .
> $$
>
> The potential at $\vec r_1$ of a unit charge at $\vec r_2$, with the walls grounded, equals the potential at $\vec r_2$ of a unit charge at $\vec r_1$. The same holds for $\Lambda = G_D - G_0$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.5, eq. (8.58), Problem 8.13 · Likharev §2.10, footnote 71*

^thm-c7-3-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1.** Let $u = G_D(\cdot, \vec r_1)$, $v = G_D(\cdot, \vec r_2)$. Apply Green's second identity on $\mathcal V$ minus small balls $B_1$, $B_2$ of radius $\epsilon$ about $\vec r_1$, $\vec r_2$ (and plus a large sphere if $\mathcal V$ is unbounded, whose contribution vanishes as in [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^der-c7-3-2|Derivation of Theorem §C7.3.2]], Step 1). Both functions are harmonic there, so
>
> $$
> 0 = \oint_S(u\,\partial_nv - v\,\partial_nu)\,dS + \oint_{\partial B_1}(\dots) + \oint_{\partial B_2}(\dots) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2.** On $S$, $u = v = 0$: the first integral vanishes.
>
> **Step 3.** On $\partial B_1$, $u$ has the singular part $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0\epsilon$ and $v$ is smooth. As in Step 3 of Theorem §C7.3.2, $\oint u\,\partial_nv \to 0$ and $-\oint v\,\partial_nu \to -v(\vec r_1)/\varepsilon_0$. On $\partial B_2$ the roles swap: the integral tends to $+u(\vec r_2)/\varepsilon_0$.
>
> **Step 4.** Hence $0 = -G_D(\vec r_1, \vec r_2)/\varepsilon_0 + G_D(\vec r_2, \vec r_1)/\varepsilon_0$. $G_0$ is symmetric, so $\Lambda$ is too.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Symmetry comes from the self-adjointness of the Laplacian with Dirichlet conditions: the boundary terms of Green's identity vanish. It is the point-charge form of Green's reciprocity ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]]; for conductors [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]]).
> - For Neumann conditions the boundary term does not vanish, and symmetry needs a normalization ([[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-6|Theorem §C7.3.6]]).

## Dirichlet and Neumann problems

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.4: The Dirichlet Problem Solved ("Magic Rule")
> The Dirichlet Green function of $\mathcal V$ is unique. For any charge density $\rho$ in $\mathcal V$ and boundary values $V_S$ on $S$, the potential is
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \int_{\mathcal V}d^3r'\,G_D(\vec r, \vec r\,')\,\rho(\vec r\,') - \varepsilon_0\oint_SdS'\,V_S(\vec r\,')\,\frac{\partial G_D(\vec r, \vec r\,')}{\partial n'} .
> $$
>
> The **Poisson kernel** $P(\vec r, \vec r\,') = -\varepsilon_0\,\partial G_D(\vec r, \vec r\,')/\partial n'$ ($\vec r\,' \in S$) is harmonic in $\vec r$ and turns boundary values into the potential they produce in a charge-free region. One calculation of $G_D$ serves all $\rho$ and all $V_S$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.4.1, eq. (8.54); §8.5.1, eq. (8.60) (Barton's "magic rule") · Likharev §2.10, eq. (2.210)*

^thm-c7-3-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (uniqueness). Two Dirichlet Green functions differ, for fixed $\vec r\,'$, by a harmonic function of $\vec r$ vanishing on $S$ (and at infinity) ([[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]]), which is zero by [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]. Existence is assumed ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^rem-c6-1-4|§C6.1, Remark: Uniqueness is not existence]]); for planes and spheres images construct it ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]], [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]).
>
> **Step 2** (formula). Put $G = G_D$ in [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]]: $G_D(\vec r\,', \vec r) = 0$ for $\vec r\,' \in S$, so the last term drops and $V = V_S$ on $S$ enters the middle term. Then use [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]] to write $G_D(\vec r\,', \vec r) = G_D(\vec r, \vec r\,')$ in both terms.
>
> **Step 3** (point sources). For $\rho = q\,\delta^3(\vec r\,' - \vec s)$, read as a distribution, the volume term is $q\,G_D(\vec r, \vec s)$: the Green function acting on the source, as in [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-15|QFT Theorem §CA.2.15]] (there with the factor $i$ that undoes its $-i$). The derivation of Theorem §C7.3.2 assumed bounded $\rho$; point charges are reached by superposing narrow smooth distributions and passing to the limit, which commutes with the integral against the continuous function $G_D(\vec r, \cdot)$ away from $\vec r$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The Dirichlet Green function is the inverse of $-\varepsilon_0\nabla^2$ with zero boundary values, as a linear map $\rho \mapsto V$; $G_D(\vec r, \vec r\,')$ is its kernel.
> - Boundary values act like sources on the boundary, with the Poisson kernel as their Green function.

*Uses:* [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.5: The Poisson Kernel Is a Nascent Delta Function
> For the half-space $z > 0$, $G_D$ is the potential of a unit charge and its image ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]]), and its Poisson kernel is
>
> $$
> P_z(x - x', y - y') = -\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial G_D}{\partial n'}\Big|_{z' = 0} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{z}{\bigl[(x - x')^2 + (y - y')^2 + z^2\bigr]^{3/2}} .
> $$
>
> $P_z \ge 0$, $\int P_z\,dx'dy' = 1$, and $P_z \to \delta(x - x')\,\delta(y - y')$ as $z \to 0^+$, in the sense that $\int P_z\,f\,dx'dy' \to f(x, y)$ for every bounded continuous $f$. So the magic rule returns the prescribed boundary values.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.5.1, eqs. (8.61)–(8.63)*

^thm-c7-3-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the kernel). With $\vec r\,'$ the source and $\bar{\vec r}\,' = (x', y', -z')$ its mirror point, $G_D = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\bigl[|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^{-1} - |\vec r - \bar{\vec r}\,'|^{-1}\bigr]$. The outward normal of $z > 0$ is $-\hat z$, so $-\varepsilon_0\partial/\partial n' = +\varepsilon_0\partial/\partial z'$. With $D^2 = (x - x')^2 + (y - y')^2 + z^2$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial}{\partial z'}\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}\Big|_{z' = 0} = \frac{z}{D^3}, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial z'}\frac{1}{|\vec r - \bar{\vec r}\,'|}\Big|_{z' = 0} = -\frac{z}{D^3} ,
> $$
>
> so $\varepsilon_0\partial_{z'}G_D = \frac{1}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{2z}{D^3}$, which is $P_z$.
>
> **Step 2** (unit integral). In polar coordinates about $(x, y)$, $\varrho^2 = (x - x')^2 + (y - y')^2$, and substituting $\varrho = z\sigma$ (Jacobian $\varrho\,d\varrho = z^2\sigma\,d\sigma$):
>
> $$
> \int P_z\,dx'dy' = \int_0^\infty\frac{z\,\varrho\,d\varrho}{(\varrho^2 + z^2)^{3/2}} = \int_0^\infty\frac{\sigma\,d\sigma}{(\sigma^2 + 1)^{3/2}} = 1 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (limit). With the same substitution in both coordinates, $\vec\xi = (x' - x, y' - y) = z\vec\eta$, $d^2\xi = z^2d^2\eta$:
>
> $$
> \int P_z\,f\,d^2\xi = \int\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{f(x + z\eta_1, y + z\eta_2)}{(1 + \eta^2)^{3/2}}\,d^2\eta \to f(x, y)\int\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d^2\eta}{(1 + \eta^2)^{3/2}} = f(x, y) ,
> $$
>
> by dominated convergence ($|f| \le M$ and $(1 + \eta^2)^{-3/2}$ is integrable). This is the limit in the sense of distributions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-4|QFT Theorem §CA.2.4]]); $P_z$ is a nascent delta function of the kind used at level B ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|WO Def. §B4.4.2]]), the three-dimensional cousin of the Lorentzian.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - For a curved smooth $S$, Zangwill argues that near a boundary point $S$ looks flat, so the Poisson kernel has the same nascent-delta behaviour; that is the right picture, and it is a theorem of potential theory for smooth bounded domains (stated, not proved here).
> - ⚑ The boundary values are attained only as a limit: on $S$ itself the magic rule's surface term is undefined, and continuity of $V_S$ is what makes the limit equal $V_S$.

> [!example] Example §C7.3.1: A Patch at Fixed Voltage in a Grounded Plane
> The plane $z = 0$ is grounded except for a patch $S_0$ held at $V_0$, insulated from the rest by a negligible gap. With no charges, [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-4|Theorem §C7.3.4]] and the kernel of [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-5|Theorem §C7.3.5]] give, on either side of the plane (by symmetry $z \to -z$),
>
> $$
> V(x, y, z) = \frac{V_0\,|z|}{2\pi}\int_{S_0}\frac{d^2r'}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3} = \frac{V_0}{2\pi}\,\Omega(\vec r) ,
> $$
>
> where $\Omega$ is the solid angle the patch subtends at $\vec r$, since $|z|\,d^2r'/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3$ is the solid angle of $d^2r'$ ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]]).
>
> *Disc of radius $a$, on the axis.* $\Omega = 2\pi\bigl(1 - |z|/\sqrt{z^2 + a^2}\bigr)$, so
>
> $$
> V(0, 0, z) = V_0\Bigl[1 - \frac{|z|}{\sqrt{z^2 + a^2}}\Bigr] \to V_0 \ (z \to 0), \qquad V \approx \frac{V_0a^2}{2z^2} \ (|z| \gg a) .
> $$
>
> The far field is that of a dipole $p = 2\pi\varepsilon_0a^2V_0$ perpendicular to the plane. Indeed the patch acts like a dipole layer of moment density $2\varepsilon_0V_0$ over the area $\pi a^2$ ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-8|Theorem §C3.1.8]]); compare the layers of [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]].
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 8.15 · Likharev §2.10, eqs. (2.211)–(2.215) (the disc, worked there with Likharev's normalization)*

^ex-c7-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.6: The Neumann Problem
> Let $\mathcal V$ be bounded.
> 1. No Green function can have $\partial G/\partial n = 0$ on all of $S$: always $\oint_S\partial G(\vec r_S, \vec r\,')/\partial n\,dS = -1/\varepsilon_0$. The simplest admissible condition is $\partial G_N/\partial n = -1/\varepsilon_0A$.
> 2. With it, for $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$ and given $\partial V/\partial n$ on $S$,
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \langle V\rangle_S + \int_{\mathcal V}d^3r'\,\rho(\vec r\,')\,G_N(\vec r\,', \vec r) + \varepsilon_0\oint_SdS'\,\frac{\partial V(\vec r\,')}{\partial n'}\,G_N(\vec r\,', \vec r), \qquad \langle V\rangle_S = \frac1A\oint_SV\,dS ,
> $$
>
> so $V$ is fixed up to the constant $\langle V\rangle_S$, and the data must satisfy $\varepsilon_0\oint_S\partial V/\partial n\,dS = -\int_{\mathcal V}\rho\,d^3r$ (Gauss's law).
> 3. $G_N(\vec r_2, \vec r_1) - G_N(\vec r_1, \vec r_2) = \langle G_N(\cdot, \vec r_1)\rangle_S - \langle G_N(\cdot, \vec r_2)\rangle_S$; $G_N$ is symmetric if it is normalized by $\langle G_N(\cdot, \vec r\,')\rangle_S = 0$ (always possible: add a function of $\vec r\,'$ alone).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.4.2, eqs. (8.55)–(8.57) and footnote 6 (the symmetry in part 3 worked here)*

^thm-c7-3-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the flux of a Green function). Integrate $\nabla^2G = 0$ over $\mathcal V$ minus a small ball about $\vec r\,'$ and use the divergence theorem: $0 = \oint_S\partial_nG\,dS + \oint_{\partial B_\epsilon}\partial_nG\,dS$, where on $\partial B_\epsilon$ the normal points into the ball and $\partial_nG = 1/4\pi\varepsilon_0\epsilon^2 + O(1)$ ([[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^der-c7-3-2|Derivation of Theorem §C7.3.2]], Step 3). So $\oint_{\partial B_\epsilon} \to 1/\varepsilon_0$ and $\oint_S\partial_nG\,dS = -1/\varepsilon_0$: the unit charge's flux must leave through $S$. A constant normal derivative then has to be $-1/\varepsilon_0A$.
>
> **Step 2** (formula). Put $G = G_N$ in [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]]: the middle term becomes $-\varepsilon_0\oint V\cdot(-1/\varepsilon_0A)\,dS' = \langle V\rangle_S$.
>
> **Step 3** (compatibility). Integrating $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$ over $\mathcal V$ with the divergence theorem gives $\oint\partial_nV\,dS = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int\rho$; Neumann data that violate it have no solution ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^cau-c6-1-1|§C6.1, Caution: Too much data, too little data, infinite energy]]).
>
> **Step 4** (symmetry). Repeat [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^der-c7-3-3|Derivation of Theorem §C7.3.3]] with $u = G_N(\cdot, \vec r_1)$, $v = G_N(\cdot, \vec r_2)$. The small balls contribute $\bigl[G_N(\vec r_2, \vec r_1) - G_N(\vec r_1, \vec r_2)\bigr]/\varepsilon_0$ as before, but now the $S$ term is
>
> $$
> \oint_S(u\,\partial_nv - v\,\partial_nu)\,dS = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0A}\oint_S(u - v)\,dS = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\bigl[\langle u\rangle_S - \langle v\rangle_S\bigr] .
> $$
>
> Setting the sum to zero gives part 3. Adding $c(\vec r\,')$ to $G_N(\vec r, \vec r\,')$ changes neither the equation nor the boundary condition, and $c(\vec r\,') = -\langle G_N(\cdot, \vec r\,')\rangle_S$ makes all averages zero.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The Neumann Green function is not "the potential with insulating walls": a unit charge in a closed region must send its flux somewhere, and the condition spreads it uniformly over $S$.
> - Neumann data are rarely natural in electrostatics. On a conductor $\varepsilon_0\partial V/\partial n = \sigma$ is part of the answer, not of the data. They occur in steady currents and waveguides (Zangwill, footnote 7). Mixed conditions (finite plates, apertures) are a hard problem of their own.
> - (In Zangwill's eq. (8.56) the surface average is printed with $d^3r$; it is $\frac1A\oint_SdS\,\varphi$.)

## What the Dirichlet Green function says about conductors

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.7: Direct and Indirect Effect of a Charge
> Let the boundary of $\mathcal V$ be one conductor held at $V_0$. If $\mathcal V$ is bounded (a cavity), then
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}\frac{\rho(\vec r\,')\,d^3r'}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} + \int_{\mathcal V}\Lambda(\vec r, \vec r\,')\,\rho(\vec r\,')\,d^3r' + V_0 ,
> $$
>
> the **direct effect** of $\rho$, its **indirect effect** through the charge it induces on the walls ($\Lambda = G_D - G_0$ of [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]]: $\Lambda(\vec r, \vec r\,')$ is the potential at $\vec r$ of the charge induced by a unit charge at $\vec r\,'$), and the constant $V_0$. If $\mathcal V$ is the exterior of the conductor, the constant is replaced by $V_0\,h(\vec r)$, where $h$ is the potential of the conductor at unit potential with no other charge ($h = R/r$ for a sphere).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.5.1, eqs. (8.64)–(8.66) (the exterior case worked here)*

^thm-c7-3-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1.** Split $G_D = G_0 + \Lambda$ ([[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]]) in the volume term of [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-4|Theorem §C7.3.4]].
>
> **Step 2** (surface term, cavity). With $V_S = V_0$ constant, the surface term is $V_0\cdot\bigl(-\varepsilon_0\oint_S\partial_{n'}G_D(\vec r, \vec r\,')\,dS'\bigr)$. By reciprocity ([[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]) $G_D(\vec r, \cdot) = G_D(\cdot, \vec r)$ is a Green function in the variable $\vec r\,'$, so by [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-6|Theorem §C7.3.6]], part 1, its flux through $S$ is $-1/\varepsilon_0$, and the bracket is $1$.
>
> **Step 3** (surface term, exterior). Now $S$ and the sphere at infinity share the flux, so the bracket $h(\vec r) = -\varepsilon_0\oint_S\partial_{n'}G_D\,dS'$ is not $1$. It is harmonic in $\vec r$ (differentiate under the integral), tends to $1$ as $\vec r \to S$ ([[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-5|Theorem §C7.3.5]] for the kernel near the boundary, applied to the data $1$), and tends to $0$ at infinity with $G_D$. By uniqueness it is the potential of the conductor at unit potential alone. For a sphere this is $R/r$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Inside a closed conductor its potential only adds a constant; outside, it adds a field. This is the Green-function form of the Faraday cage and of the capacitance of an isolated body ($h$ falls off as $C/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$; [[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-1|Theorem §C4.2.1]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.8: Image Force and Image Energy near Grounded Conductors
> A point charge $q$ at $\vec s \in \mathcal V$, all boundaries grounded. The charge it induces produces $V_{\text{ind}}(\vec r) = q\,\Lambda(\vec r, \vec s)$ in $\mathcal V$ ($\Lambda = G_D - G_0$, [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]]; symmetric by [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]), and the force on $q$ is
>
> $$
> \vec F(\vec s) = q\,\vec E_{\text{ind}}(\vec s) = -q^2\nabla_{\vec r}\Lambda(\vec r, \vec s)\big|_{\vec r = \vec s} = -\nabla_{\vec s}U, \qquad U(\vec s) = \tfrac12q^2\Lambda(\vec s, \vec s) = \tfrac12q\,V_{\text{ind}}(\vec s) .
> $$
>
> $U$ is the work an external agent does to bring $q$ in from where $\Lambda \to 0$ (from infinity, for unbounded $\mathcal V$). If some conductors are held at fixed potentials instead, their field $V_{\text{fix}}$ (with $q$ absent) adds $q\,V_{\text{fix}}(\vec s)$ to $U$, without a factor $\tfrac12$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.5.1, eq. (8.67) (the force); the energy worked here, generalizing [[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^cau-b3-2-1|§B3.2, Caution]] and Zangwill eq. (8.9) · Likharev §2.9 (energy with an applied field)*

^thm-c7-3-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (force). By [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-1|Theorem §C7.1.1]] with $q$, $V_{\text{ind}} = q\Lambda(\cdot, \vec s)$ is the smooth part; by [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^der-c7-1-2|Derivation of Theorem §C7.1.2]], Step 4, $\vec F = -q\nabla V_{\text{ind}}(\vec s) = -q^2\nabla_{\vec r}\Lambda(\vec r, \vec s)|_{\vec r = \vec s}$.
>
> **Step 2** (chain rule on the diagonal). Write $\nabla_1$, $\nabla_2$ for the gradients in the first and second arguments. Then $\nabla_{\vec s}\bigl[\Lambda(\vec s, \vec s)\bigr] = (\nabla_1\Lambda)(\vec s, \vec s) + (\nabla_2\Lambda)(\vec s, \vec s)$. Reciprocity ([[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]), $\Lambda(\vec a, \vec b) = \Lambda(\vec b, \vec a)$, gives $(\nabla_2\Lambda)(\vec a, \vec b) = (\nabla_1\Lambda)(\vec b, \vec a)$, so on the diagonal the two terms are equal:
>
> $$
> \nabla_{\vec s}\bigl[\Lambda(\vec s, \vec s)\bigr] = 2\,(\nabla_1\Lambda)(\vec s, \vec s) .
> $$
>
> Hence $\vec F = -q^2(\nabla_1\Lambda)(\vec s, \vec s) = -\nabla_{\vec s}\bigl[\tfrac12q^2\Lambda(\vec s, \vec s)\bigr]$.
>
> **Step 3** (fixed potentials). By superposition the potential is $V_{\text{fix}} + q\,G_D(\cdot, \vec s)$, and $V_{\text{fix}}$ does not depend on $\vec s$, so it adds $-q\nabla V_{\text{fix}}(\vec s) = -\nabla_{\vec s}\bigl[qV_{\text{fix}}(\vec s)\bigr]$ to the force: a term linear in $q$, without the $\tfrac12$.
>
> **Step 4** (checks). Plane: $\Lambda(\vec s, \vec s) = -1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\cdot2z_0)$, so $U = -q^2/16\pi\varepsilon_0z_0$ (Zangwill eq. (8.9)). Sphere, charge outside: $\Lambda(\vec s, \vec s) = -\frac{R/s}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(s - R^2/s)} = -\frac{R}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(s^2 - R^2)}$, so $U = -\frac{q^2R}{8\pi\varepsilon_0(s^2 - R^2)}$ and $-dU/ds = -\frac{q^2Rs}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(s^2 - R^2)^2}$, the attraction of [[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - ⚑ The $\tfrac12$ is reciprocity: moving $q$ moves its induced charge, and the symmetry of $\Lambda$ makes the two changes contribute equally. The interaction energy with the images held fixed, $qV_{\text{ind}}(\vec s)$, is therefore twice the work actually needed to bring $q$ in.
> - An applied field enters at full strength, as in Likharev's image-plus-field barrier ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^rem-c7-1-1|§C7.1, Remark: The image energy is half the interaction with the images]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.9: Green-Function Inequalities
> For $\vec r \ne \vec r\,'$ in $\mathcal V$ (bounded, or unbounded with $G_D \to 0$ at infinity),
>
> $$
> 0 < G_D(\vec r, \vec r\,') < G_0(\vec r, \vec r\,'), \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \Lambda(\vec r, \vec r\,') < 0 .
> $$
>
> Consequences: the image energy $\tfrac12q^2\Lambda(\vec s, \vec s)$ is negative for either sign of $q$, and the Poisson kernel is non-negative, $-\varepsilon_0\,\partial G_D/\partial n' \ge 0$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 8.14 (solved here)*

^thm-c7-3-9

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** ($\Lambda < 0$). For fixed $\vec r\,'$, $\Lambda(\cdot, \vec r\,')$ is harmonic in $\mathcal V$ ([[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]]). On $S$ it equals $-G_0(\vec r_S, \vec r\,') < 0$, and at infinity it tends to $0$. By the maximum principle ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]]) its supremum over $\mathcal V$ is at most $0$, and it is not attained inside unless $\Lambda$ is constant, which the negative boundary values exclude. So $\Lambda < 0$ in $\mathcal V$.
>
> **Step 2** ($G_D > 0$). $G_D(\cdot, \vec r\,')$ is harmonic in $\mathcal V\setminus\{\vec r\,'\}$, vanishes on $S$ (and at infinity), and tends to $+\infty$ at $\vec r\,'$. On $\mathcal V$ minus a small ball about $\vec r\,'$, where it is large and positive on the small sphere, the minimum principle puts its infimum on the boundary, so $G_D \ge 0$, and strictly $> 0$ inside since it is not constant.
>
> **Step 3** (kernel). $G_D \ge 0$ in $\mathcal V$ and $= 0$ on $S$, so its derivative along the outward normal is $\le 0$, and $-\varepsilon_0\partial_{n'}G_D \ge 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Physically: a positive unit charge in a grounded enclosure raises the potential everywhere (Step 2, the comparison principle of §C6.1 for a positive source), and the negative charge it induces lowers it everywhere below the free-space value (Step 1).
> - The positive Poisson kernel says that the potential in a charge-free region is a weighted average of its boundary values, the integral form of the mean-value and extremum properties.

*Uses:* [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]]

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Existence of the Dirichlet Green function
> This section, like Zangwill, assumes that $G_D$ exists. For a bounded region with a reasonable boundary, existence follows from Hilbert-space methods: the Dirichlet energy $\int\nabla u\cdot\nabla v$ is a bounded, coercive form on the space of functions with square-integrable gradient that vanish on $S$ (coercive by the Poincaré inequality), so by Lax–Milgram ([[§28 Sesquilinear Forms and the Lax–Milgram Theorem#^thm-28-2|556 Thm. §28.2]]) the equation $-\varepsilon_0\nabla^2u = f$ has a unique weak solution for every $f$. Then $G_D$ is the kernel of $f \mapsto u$, and $\Lambda$ is obtained by solving for the harmonic correction with smooth boundary data $-G_0$. Dirichlet's principle ([[§C6.4★ The Complex Potential and the Variational Principle#^thm-c6-4-3|Theorem §C6.4.3]]) is the same statement as an energy minimum.
>
> *Source: standard; stated here*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The Green function of electrostatics is the zero-frequency member of a family: the Klein–Gordon Green functions of [[§C2.11 Green's Functions and Contours#^def-c2-11-1|QFT Def. §C2.11.1]] differ by solutions of the free equation, exactly as Dirichlet, Neumann and free Green functions here differ by harmonic functions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-14|QFT Theorem §CA.2.14]]); there contours, here walls, pick the member ([[§C2.11 Green's Functions and Contours#^rem-c2-11-1|QFT §C2.11, Remark: What a Green's "function" is, and why it is not unique]]).
> - Reciprocity is the symmetry of a self-adjoint operator's inverse; for conductors it is Green's reciprocity theorem and the symmetry of the capacitance matrix ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]]).
> - The retarded potential of [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials|§B11.1★]] is the Green function of the wave operator chosen by causality; its static limit is $G_0$.
> - Constructions of $G_D$ for a box, a slab, a sphere and a cylinder (eigenfunctions, direct integration, splitting) are [[§C7.4 Constructing Green Functions|§C7.4]]; the image-force rule of [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-8|Theorem §C7.3.8]] is applied there to a charge near a grounded tube ([[§C7.4 Constructing Green Functions#^ex-c7-4-3|Example §C7.4.3]]).
