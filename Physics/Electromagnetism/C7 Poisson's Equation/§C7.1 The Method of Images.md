*Sources: Zangwill, Modern Electrodynamics, §8.1–§8.3 (Examples 8.1–8.2, Application 8.1; Problems 8.4, 8.11) · PHY 505 (Wells, Fall 2025), HW 11, Problems 2–3 · Likharev, Essential Graduate Physics: EM, §2.9.*

When a charge sits near conductors or dielectrics, the charge they acquire is part of the unknown, and Coulomb's integral cannot be used. Level B solved the classic cases by images: the grounded plane and sphere, the right-angled corner, a line over a plane ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]], [[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]], [[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^ex-b3-2-1|Example §B3.2.1]]), a charge over a dielectric ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^ex-b5-3-1|Example §B5.3.1]]) and a dipole over a plane ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^ex-b4-2-3|Example §B4.2.3]]); those results are linked here, not repeated. This section says what the method is: the potential is a Coulomb term plus a harmonic function, and images are a way to write the harmonic function ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-1|Theorem §C7.1.1]], [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]]). It then adds what level B lacked: why the plane image appears from separation of variables, charges inside spheres and Kelvin's inversion (images for every charge distribution and a sphere), cylinders, images at dielectric interfaces between two media, image dipoles, and infinite image series with the question of their convergence. The energy of a charge near a grounded conductor, which is *not* its interaction energy with the images, is proved in general in [[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-2-8|Theorem §C7.2.8]].

*Notation.* The potential is $V$ (Zangwill writes $\varphi$; [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: The potential is V; Zangwill writes φ]]); the region where the potential is wanted is $\mathcal V$ (Zangwill: $V$), with boundary $S$ and **outward** normal $\hat n$ (out of $\mathcal V$), $\partial V/\partial n = \hat n\cdot\nabla V$, as in [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness|§C6.1]]. The source charge sits at $\vec s$, the field point is $\vec r$; the dielectric constant is $\varepsilon_r$ (Zangwill $\kappa$; [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^cau-c5-3-1|§C5.3, Caution]]). For a sphere of radius $R$ centred at the origin, the **inverse point** of $\vec s$ is $\vec s^{\,*} = (R^2/s^2)\,\vec s$, at distance $s^* = R^2/s$ on the same ray.

## A Coulomb term plus a harmonic function

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.1: A Point Charge in a Region with Given Boundary Values
> Let $\mathcal V$ be a region with boundary $S$ (part of which may be at infinity, where $V \to 0$), and let a point charge $q$ sit at $\vec s \in \mathcal V$, its density $\rho = q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec s)$ read as a distribution ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]; [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-2|QFT Def. §CA.2.2]]). The potential with $V = V_S$ on $S$ is
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec s|} + \Phi(\vec r) ,
> $$
>
> where $\Phi$ is smooth and harmonic in all of $\mathcal V$, also at $\vec s$, with $\Phi = V_S - q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec s|)$ on $S$. Poisson's equation $\nabla^2V = -(q/\varepsilon_0)\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec s)$ holds as an identity of distributions on $\mathcal V$: $\int V\,\nabla^2\varphi\,d^3r = -q\,\varphi(\vec s)/\varepsilon_0$ for every test function $\varphi$ vanishing outside a bounded closed subset of $\mathcal V$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.2, eq. (8.2)*

^thm-c7-1-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the Coulomb term is a particular solution). By [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], $\nabla^2(1/|\vec r - \vec s|) = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec r - \vec s)$ as distributions: the derivative is moved onto the test function ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]), and the identity says
>
> $$
> \int\frac{\nabla^2\varphi(\vec r)}{|\vec r - \vec s|}\,d^3r = -4\pi\,\varphi(\vec s) \qquad\text{for every test function } \varphi .
> $$
>
> Multiplying by $q/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ gives the stated identity for the first term alone.
>
> **Step 2** (the remainder is an ordinary harmonic function). Let $V$ be any distributional solution in $\mathcal V$ and $W = V - q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec s|)$. The Laplacian is linear, so $\nabla^2W = -(q/\varepsilon_0)\delta^3 + (q/\varepsilon_0)\delta^3 = 0$ as a distribution on $\mathcal V$. By Weyl's lemma (standard; a distribution annihilated by $\nabla^2$ on an open set is a smooth harmonic function there), $W$ is a classical harmonic function on $\mathcal V$, including the point $\vec s$.
>
> **Step 3** (boundary values). $\vec s$ is not on $S$, so on $S$ the Coulomb term is a known smooth function and $W = V_S - q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec s|)$ there. Call $W = \Phi$.
>
> **Step 4** (one solution). Two solutions differ by a harmonic function that vanishes on $S$ (and at infinity), which is zero by the uniqueness theorem ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]). That $\Phi$ exists is assumed here, as everywhere in electrostatics ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^rem-c6-1-4|§C6.1, Remark: Uniqueness is not existence]]); Zangwill argues for it from the completeness of the separated solutions.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The singular part of the potential is local and universal: near a point charge the potential is Coulomb's, whatever the boundaries; the boundaries add a smooth harmonic function, the potential of the charge they acquire.
> - Images are one way to write $\Phi$: as the potential of point charges placed *outside* $\mathcal V$ ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]]). Separated solutions are another ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-3|Theorem §C7.1.3]]).
> - With $q = 1$, $V_S = 0$ and $\vec s$ treated as a second variable, $V$ is the Dirichlet Green function, and $\Phi$ its harmonic part $\Lambda$ ([[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]], [[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]).

*Uses:* [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]], [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.2: The Image Principle
> Let **image charges** $q_1, q_2, \dots$ sit at points $\vec s_i$ outside the closure of $\mathcal V$ (finitely many, or a convergent series as in [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-10|Theorem §C7.1.10]]), and suppose the potential of the real charge and the images in empty space,
>
> $$
> V_{\text{im}}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\frac{q}{|\vec r - \vec s|} + \sum_i\frac{q_i}{|\vec r - \vec s_i|}\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> takes the prescribed values on $S$ (and tends to zero at infinity if $\mathcal V$ is unbounded). Then, in $\mathcal V$ only:
> 1. $V = V_{\text{im}}$, and the image terms are the potential of the charge induced on $S$ ($\Phi$ of [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-1|Theorem §C7.1.1]]). Outside $\mathcal V$ the image system says nothing about the real field.
> 2. On a conductor bounding $\mathcal V$ the induced charge is $\sigma = \varepsilon_0\,\partial V_{\text{im}}/\partial n$, with $\hat n$ the outward normal of $\mathcal V$ (pointing into the conductor).
> 3. The force on the real charge is the Coulomb force of the images: $\vec F = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\displaystyle\sum_i q_i\,\frac{\vec s - \vec s_i}{|\vec s - \vec s_i|^3}$.
>
> At a dielectric interface the boundary values are replaced by the matching conditions of [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]], and each region gets its own image system ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-7|Theorem §C7.1.7]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.3 (introduction), §8.3.1, eqs. (8.3)–(8.8)*

^thm-c7-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Poisson's equation in $\mathcal V$). Each image potential $q_i/4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec s_i|$ is smooth and harmonic away from $\vec s_i$, hence in all of $\mathcal V$; so $V_{\text{im}}$ has the form of [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-1|Theorem §C7.1.1]] with $\Phi = $ (image terms). It satisfies the boundary condition by hypothesis, so $V = V_{\text{im}}$ in $\mathcal V$ by uniqueness ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]).
>
> **Step 2** (the image terms are the induced charge's potential). In the real problem the only charges are $q$ and the charge on the conductors, which sits on their surfaces $S$. So in $\mathcal V$, $V = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec s| + V_\sigma$, with $V_\sigma$ the potential of the induced charge. Comparing with Step 1, $V_\sigma = $ (image terms) in $\mathcal V$. Outside $\mathcal V$ (inside a conductor, say) the real potential is constant while the image terms are not: the equality holds only where it was proved.
>
> **Step 3** (induced charge). Just outside a conductor, on the field side, $\sigma = \varepsilon_0\vec E\cdot\hat m$ with $\hat m$ the normal pointing from the conductor into the field region ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-1|Theorem §B2.4.1]]). Here $\hat m = -\hat n$, so $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\hat n\cdot\vec E = \varepsilon_0\,\hat n\cdot\nabla V = \varepsilon_0\,\partial V/\partial n$. Check with the plane $z = 0$ and $\mathcal V = \{z > 0\}$: $\hat n = -\hat z$, so $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\,\partial V/\partial z$, as in [[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]].
>
> **Step 4** (force). The force on $q$ is exerted by the induced charge; the field of $q$ itself exerts no force on $q$ (no self-force, [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-4|Theorem §C2.1.4]]; the self-energy is a separate, divergent constant, [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-7|Theorem §C2.3.7]]). The induced charge's field at $\vec s$ is $-\nabla V_\sigma(\vec s)$, which exists because $V_\sigma$ is smooth near $\vec s$. In a neighbourhood of $\vec s$, inside $\mathcal V$, $V_\sigma$ equals the image terms (Step 2), so their gradients agree there:
>
> $$
> \vec F = -q\,\nabla V_\sigma(\vec s) = -q\,\nabla\Bigl[\sum_i\frac{q_i}{4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec s_i|}\Bigr]_{\vec r = \vec s} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_i q_i\,\frac{\vec s - \vec s_i}{|\vec s - \vec s_i|^3} .
> $$
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Images are bookkeeping for the induced charge, valid only in the region where they were constructed; no image may lie in $\mathcal V$, or one would be solving Poisson's equation with the wrong source.
> - The force rule needs only that $\vec s$ is in $\mathcal V$. The energy is a different matter: the images move when $q$ moves (next remark).

> [!remark] Remark: The image energy is half the interaction with the images
> For a charge at height $z_0$ above a grounded plane, integrating the image force from infinity gives the interaction energy $-q^2/16\pi\varepsilon_0z_0$ (Zangwill eq. (8.9)), while $q$ times the image potential at $\vec s$ is $-q^2/8\pi\varepsilon_0z_0$ (eq. (8.10)); level B explained the factor $\tfrac12$ ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^cau-b3-2-1|§B3.2, Caution: The fields agree, the energies do not]]). Zangwill's reason: no work is needed to bring the induced charge from ground onto the plane (it moves on an equipotential), while assembling the image system would cost work on the image too. The rule $U = \tfrac12 q V_\sigma(\vec s)$ for any grounded conductor is [[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-2-8|Theorem §C7.2.8]], where it follows from the reciprocity of the Green function. Two measured consequences:
> - *Image-potential states.* An electron outside a metal feels $-e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\cdot4z)$: Coulomb's attraction with $e^2 \to e^2/4$. The one-dimensional problem on $z > 0$ with $\psi(0) = 0$ is the hydrogen $s$-wave radial problem, whose levels scale as the square of the coupling, so $E_n = -(13.6\ \text{eV})/16n^2 = -0.85\ \text{eV}/n^2$; such Rydberg series are seen in photoemission from noble-metal surfaces (Zangwill, box in §8.3.1).
> - *Work function.* Integrating the image force from an atomic distance $d \approx 10^{-10}$ m outwards gives $e^2/16\pi\varepsilon_0d \approx 3.6$ eV, close to measured work functions of 4–5 eV; a field $E_0$ pulling electrons out adds $-eE_0z$ and lowers the barrier, which leads to field emission (Likharev §2.9; Zangwill Problem 8.9 for Debye's version with a sphere).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.3.1, eqs. (8.9)–(8.10) and the box "Image-Potential States" · Likharev §2.9*

## The plane image as a sum of modes

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.3: The Plane Image from Separation of Variables
> For a charge $q$ at height $z_0$ above the grounded plane $z = 0$ (cylindrical coordinates $\rho$, $z$ about the vertical through $q$), the harmonic part of [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-1|Theorem §C7.1.1]] that is regular for $z > 0$, symmetric about the axis and decaying as $z \to \infty$ is a superposition of the separated solutions $J_0(k\rho)e^{-kz}$; the boundary condition fixes it uniquely to
>
> $$
> \Phi(\rho, z) = -\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^\infty dk\,J_0(k\rho)\,e^{-k(z + z_0)} = -\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^2 + (z + z_0)^2}} ,
> $$
>
> the potential of the image $-q$ at $z = -z_0$. The resummation uses
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^2 + z^2}} = \int_0^\infty dk\,J_0(k\rho)\,e^{-k|z|} \qquad (z \ne 0) .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.3.2, eqs. (8.11)–(8.13) and footnote 1*

^thm-c7-1-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the integral identity). Use Bessel's integral $J_0(x) = \frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\cos(x\sin\theta)\,d\theta$ ([[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], part 6). For $z > 0$ the double integral $\int_0^\infty dk\int_0^\pi d\theta\,e^{-kz}|\cos(k\rho\sin\theta)|$ is at most $\pi/z$, so the order of integration may be exchanged (Fubini; [[§CA.1 Exchanging Limits, Derivatives and Integrals|QFT §CA.1]]):
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty dk\,e^{-kz}J_0(k\rho) = \frac1\pi\int_0^\pi d\theta\int_0^\infty dk\,e^{-kz}\cos(k\rho\sin\theta) = \frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\frac{z\,d\theta}{z^2 + \rho^2\sin^2\theta} ,
> $$
>
> using $\int_0^\infty e^{-kz}\cos(bk)\,dk = z/(z^2 + b^2)$. With $\int_0^\pi d\theta/(a + b\sin^2\theta) = \pi/\sqrt{a(a + b)}$ for $a > 0$, $b \ge 0$, here $a = z^2$, $b = \rho^2$:
>
> $$
> \frac1\pi\cdot z\cdot\frac{\pi}{z\sqrt{z^2 + \rho^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^2 + z^2}} .
> $$
>
> For $z < 0$ replace $z$ by $|z|$ (the left side depends on $z^2$ only).
>
> **Step 2** (the ansatz). Separated, axially symmetric solutions of Laplace's equation are $J_0(k\rho)e^{\pm kz}$, $k \ge 0$ ([[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]); regularity on the axis excludes the second solution $Y_0$, and decay as $z \to \infty$ excludes $e^{+kz}$. So
>
> $$
> V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^2 + (z - z_0)^2}} + \int_0^\infty dk\,A(k)\,J_0(k\rho)\,e^{-kz} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the boundary condition). At $z = 0$, Step 1 with $z \to z - z_0 = -z_0$ writes the Coulomb term as $\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^\infty dk\,J_0(k\rho)e^{-kz_0}$. So
>
> $$
> V(\rho, 0) = \int_0^\infty dk\,\Bigl[A(k) + \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}e^{-kz_0}\Bigr]J_0(k\rho) = 0 \quad\text{for all } \rho
> $$
>
> holds for $A(k) = -\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}e^{-kz_0}$. Any other choice that also works gives the same $V$ by uniqueness ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]), so this is the solution.
>
> **Step 4** (resummation). With this $A$, $\int_0^\infty dk\,A(k)J_0(k\rho)e^{-kz} = -\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^\infty dk\,J_0(k\rho)e^{-k(z + z_0)}$, and Step 1 with $|z + z_0| = z + z_0 > 0$ gives the image term.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Nobody has to guess the image: the boundary condition determines the mode amplitudes, and the modes resum to a point charge.
> - The image sits where the mode sum stops converging: $\int dk\,e^{-k(z + z_0)}J_0$ converges for $z > -z_0$ and becomes singular at the image point. An image is the singularity of the analytic continuation of $\Phi$ out of $\mathcal V$.

## Spheres: images by inversion

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.4: A Point Charge Inside or Outside a Grounded Sphere
> A charge $q$ at $\vec s$, $s \ne R$, and a grounded conducting sphere of radius $R$ centred at the origin. On the side of the sphere where $q$ is, the potential is that of $q$ and an image
>
> $$
> q' = -\frac Rs\,q \quad\text{at the inverse point}\quad \vec s^{\,*} = \frac{R^2}{s^2}\,\vec s, \qquad V(\vec r) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec s|} - \frac{R/s}{|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> With $\gamma$ the angle between $\vec r$ and $\vec s$ and $D^2 = R^2 + s^2 - 2Rs\cos\gamma$, the induced surface charge is $\sigma = -\dfrac{q}{4\pi R}\,\dfrac{|s^2 - R^2|}{D^3}$ in both cases. Its total is $-qR/s$ for a charge outside (level B, [[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]) but $-q$ for a charge inside. A charge inside is pushed towards the nearest wall with
>
> $$
> \vec F = \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Rs}{(R^2 - s^2)^2}\,\hat s .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.3.5, eqs. (8.26)–(8.32) (the inside case stated there, worked here)*

^thm-c7-1-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the key identity). For $|\vec r| = R$,
>
> $$
> |\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|^2 = R^2 + \frac{R^4}{s^2} - 2\frac{R^2}{s^2}\,\vec r\cdot\vec s = \frac{R^2}{s^2}\bigl(s^2 + R^2 - 2\vec r\cdot\vec s\bigr) = \frac{R^2}{s^2}\,|\vec r - \vec s|^2 ,
> $$
>
> so $|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}| = (R/s)|\vec r - \vec s|$ on the sphere, whether $s < R$ or $s > R$. Hence $\frac{R/s}{|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|} = \frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec s|}$ there, and $V = 0$ on the sphere.
>
> **Step 2** (the image is outside the region). $s^* = R^2/s$: if $s > R$ then $s^* < R$, and if $s < R$ then $s^* > R$. So the image is always on the far side, and [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]] applies (for $s > R$ also $V \to 0$ at infinity).
>
> **Step 3** (normal derivative). Write $c = \cos\gamma$ and $\partial_r$ for the radial derivative at fixed direction. $\partial_r|\vec r - \vec s|^{-1} = -(r - sc)/|\vec r - \vec s|^3$ and $\partial_r|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|^{-1} = -(r - s^*c)/|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|^3$. At $r = R$, with $|\vec r - \vec s| = D$ and $|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}| = (R/s)D$,
>
> $$
> \partial_rV\big|_R = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0D^3}\Bigl[-(R - sc) + \frac Rs\Bigl(R - \frac{R^2}{s}c\Bigr)\frac{s^3}{R^3}\Bigr] = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0D^3}\bigl[-R + sc + s^2/R - sc\bigr] = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{s^2 - R^2}{R\,D^3} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (induced charge, [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]] (2)). Outside ($\hat n = -\hat r$): $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\partial_rV = -\frac{q}{4\pi R}\frac{s^2 - R^2}{D^3}$. Inside ($\hat n = +\hat r$): $\sigma = +\varepsilon_0\partial_rV = -\frac{q}{4\pi R}\frac{R^2 - s^2}{D^3}$. Both are $-\frac{q}{4\pi R}\frac{|s^2 - R^2|}{D^3}$.
>
> **Step 5** (totals). With $dA = 2\pi R^2\,dc$ and $\int_{-1}^1(A - Bc)^{-3/2}dc = \frac2B\bigl[(A - B)^{-1/2} - (A + B)^{-1/2}\bigr]$, $A = R^2 + s^2$, $B = 2Rs$:
>
> $$
> \oint\sigma\,dA = -\frac{q\,|s^2 - R^2|\,R}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{Rs}\Bigl[\frac{1}{|R - s|} - \frac{1}{R + s}\Bigr] = -\frac{q\,|s^2 - R^2|}{2s}\cdot\frac{(R + s) - |R - s|}{|R - s|(R + s)} .
> $$
>
> For $s > R$ the bracket is $2R/(s^2 - R^2)$ and the total is $-qR/s$; for $s < R$ it is $2s/(R^2 - s^2)$ and the total is $-q$. The second also follows from Gauss's law on a surface inside the metal, where $\vec E = 0$: the enclosed charge $q + Q_{\text{ind}}$ vanishes.
>
> **Step 6** (force inside). The image $-qR/s$ sits on the ray of $\vec s$ beyond it, at distance $s^* - s = (R^2 - s^2)/s$. By [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]] (3) the force is an attraction towards it, along $+\hat s$, of size
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q\cdot qR/s}{(R^2 - s^2)^2/s^2} = \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Rs}{(R^2 - s^2)^2} .
> $$
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - One formula serves both sides; the image is always at the inverse point, on the other side.
> - ⚑ The image's charge equals the induced charge only when the image lies in the bounded component cut off by $S$, so that Gauss's law at infinity counts both. For a charge inside, $q' = -qR/s \ne -q$: the image is a device for the field in $\mathcal V$, and its charge is not the conductor's.
> - At the centre the force vanishes and grows towards the wall: an unstable equilibrium, as it must be for a charge in a charge-free cavity ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^rem-c6-1-3|§C6.1, Remark: Earnshaw's theorem and zero curvature]]).

*Uses:* [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]], [[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.5: Kelvin Inversion: Images in a Sphere for Any Charge Distribution
> 1. If $\Phi$ is harmonic in a region $D$ not containing the origin, its **Kelvin transform** $\Psi(\vec r) = \dfrac Rr\,\Phi\Bigl(\dfrac{R^2}{r^2}\,\vec r\Bigr)$ is harmonic in the inverted region $D^* = \{R^2\vec r/r^2 : \vec r \in D\}$; more generally $\nabla^2\Psi(\vec r) = (R/r)^5\,(\nabla^2\Phi)(R^2\vec r/r^2)$, and $\Psi = \Phi$ on the sphere $r = R$.
> 2. Let charge $\rho$ lie entirely outside (or entirely inside) the grounded sphere, with potential $\Phi$ in empty space. Then on that side $V = \Phi - \Psi$: the sphere acts as the **image charge distribution**
>
> $$
> \rho^*(\vec r) = -\Bigl(\frac Rr\Bigr)^5\rho\Bigl(\frac{R^2}{r^2}\,\vec r\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> located on the other side, of total $-\int(R/s)\,\rho(\vec s)\,d^3s$. A point charge $q$ at $\vec s$ is mapped to $-qR/s$ at $\vec s^{\,*}$ ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill Example 8.2 (part 1 and the point charge); part 2 for a general distribution worked here*

^thm-c7-1-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (radial part). Write the Laplacian in spherical coordinates as $\nabla^2 = \frac1r\partial_r^2(r\,\cdot) + \frac{1}{r^2}L^2$, with $L^2$ the angular part ([[§19 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-19-1|452 Thm. §19.1]]; Zangwill's notation: $L^2 = \Delta_S$ of [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]], with eigenvalues $-\ell(\ell + 1)$, not the positive quantum operator $\mathbf L^2/\hbar^2 = -\Delta_S$). Put $u = R^2/r$, so $\partial_r = \frac{du}{dr}\partial_u = -\frac{u^2}{R^2}\partial_u$. Then $r\Psi = R\,\Phi(u)$ and
>
> $$
> \partial_r\Phi(u) = -\frac{u^2}{R^2}\Phi_u, \qquad \partial_r^2\Phi(u) = -\frac{u^2}{R^2}\partial_u\Bigl(-\frac{u^2}{R^2}\Phi_u\Bigr) = \frac{u^3}{R^4}\bigl(2\Phi_u + u\Phi_{uu}\bigr) = \frac{u^3}{R^4}\,\partial_u^2(u\Phi) .
> $$
>
> So $\frac1r\partial_r^2(r\Psi) = \frac{R}{r}\cdot\frac{u^3}{R^4}\partial_u^2(u\Phi) = \frac{u^4}{R^5}\partial_u^2(u\Phi)$, using $R/r = u/R$.
>
> **Step 2** (angular part). $\frac{1}{r^2}L^2\Psi = \frac{u^2}{R^4}\cdot\frac uR\,L^2\Phi(u) = \frac{u^3}{R^5}L^2\Phi$.
>
> **Step 3** (compare). $(R/r)^5 = (u/R)^5$, and $(u/R)^5\bigl[\frac1u\partial_u^2(u\Phi) + \frac{1}{u^2}L^2\Phi\bigr] = \frac{u^4}{R^5}\partial_u^2(u\Phi) + \frac{u^3}{R^5}L^2\Phi$, which is the sum of Steps 1–2. Hence $\nabla^2\Psi(\vec r) = (R/r)^5(\nabla^2\Phi)(R^2\vec r/r^2)$, and $\Psi$ is harmonic wherever $\Phi$ is harmonic at the inverted point. At $r = R$, $u = R$ and $\Psi = \Phi$. (In Zangwill's Example 8.2 the angular term in the transformed Laplacian is printed with a minus sign; Step 2 gives a plus.)
>
> **Step 4** (point charge). For $\Phi = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec s|$ with $s > R$, the same algebra as in [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^der-c7-1-4|Derivation of Theorem §C7.1.4]], Step 1, gives $|R^2\vec r/r^2 - \vec s| = (s/r)|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|$, so
>
> $$
> \Psi(\vec r) = \frac Rr\cdot\frac{q\,r}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,s\,|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|} = \frac{qR/s}{4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|} .
> $$
>
> $\Psi$ is harmonic for $r > R$ (its singularity is at $\vec s^{\,*}$, inside), equals $\Phi$ on the sphere, and vanishes at infinity, so $\Phi - \Psi$ is the potential of [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]].
>
> **Step 5** (distributions of charge). By linearity, each element $\rho(\vec s)\,d^3s$ outside contributes an image $-(R/s)\rho(\vec s)\,d^3s$ at $\vec s^{\,*}$. Change variables $\vec s \to \vec s^{\,*} = R^2\vec s/s^2$. Inversion is conformal with length scale factor $R^2/s^2$ (and reverses orientation), so $d^3s^* = (R/s)^6\,d^3s$. The image density at $\vec s^{\,*}$ is therefore
>
> $$
> \rho^*(\vec s^{\,*}) = -\frac Rs\,\rho(\vec s)\,\frac{d^3s}{d^3s^*} = -\frac Rs\Bigl(\frac sR\Bigr)^6\rho(\vec s) = -\Bigl(\frac sR\Bigr)^5\rho(\vec s) ,
> $$
>
> and with $s/R = R/s^*$, $\vec s = R^2\vec s^{\,*}/s^{*2}$, this is the stated formula. The same steps hold with inside and outside exchanged.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - "Images in a sphere" is not a lucky guess for point charges: inversion maps harmonic functions to harmonic functions, so every charge distribution has an image, and its density follows from the Jacobian.
> - ⚑ As a distribution, a point charge maps to a point charge: $q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec s)$ goes to $-(qR/s)\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec s^{\,*})$, as Step 4 shows directly.
> - Charges far away become images near the centre: a uniform field $E_0$ (charges $\pm Q$ at $\mp L\hat z$ with $Q/L^2$ fixed, $L \to \infty$) becomes an image dipole $4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3E_0$ at the centre, the conducting sphere of [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]].

^der-c7-1-5

*Uses:* [[§19 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-19-1|452 Thm. §19.1]], [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: A sphere at a fixed potential, or isolated with a charge
> A charge $Q_0$ at the centre adds a constant $Q_0/4\pi\varepsilon_0R$ on the sphere and keeps $V \to 0$ at infinity, so it changes the sphere's potential without spoiling the boundary condition ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^rem-b3-2-2|§B3.2, Remark: A sphere at fixed potential, or neutral]]). Outside the sphere (Zangwill eqs. (8.33)–(8.34)):
> - sphere held at $V_0$: add $RV_0/r$;
> - isolated sphere carrying charge $Q$: add $(Q + qR/s)/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$, so that the image system outside has total charge $q + Q$.
>
> For a neutral sphere the net force on $q$ is always attractive ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^ex-c7-1-1|Example §C7.1.1]]). The same trick fails inside a shell: the field inside a closed conductor does not depend on its charge or potential (only $\Phi$ shifts by a constant).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.3.6, eqs. (8.33)–(8.34)*

^rem-c7-1-2

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: A neutral conductor can repel a charge
> Is a point charge always attracted by a neutral conductor? Far away, yes: the conductor responds as an induced dipole pointing away from $q$. But let the conductor coincide with part of an equipotential of $q$, for instance a neutral hemispherical shell whose surface lies on a sphere centred at $q$ (Levin and Johnson 2011). Its interaction energy is zero at infinity and zero again in that position (a neutral conductor placed on an equipotential changes nothing). Since the energy first decreases from zero as the conductor approaches, it must rise again before the special position, and there the force is repulsive.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.3.6, Figure 8.10*

^rem-c7-1-3

> [!example] Example §C7.1.1: A Point Charge and a Conducting Sphere
> A conducting sphere of radius $R$, a charge $q$ at distance $r_0 > R$ from its centre, on the $z$ axis. Find the potential everywhere outside the sphere.
>
> *Grounded sphere.* By [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]], with $b = R^2/r_0$ and $\theta$ the polar angle,
>
> $$
> V_0(r, \theta) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2 + r_0^2 - 2rr_0\cos\theta}} - \frac{R/r_0}{\sqrt{r^2 + b^2 - 2rb\cos\theta}}\Bigr] \qquad (r \ge R) ,
> $$
>
> and $V = 0$ in the metal ($r \le R$). As a check by separation of variables ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-4|Theorem §B3.4.4]]), expand both terms for $R \le r < r_0$:
>
> $$
> V_0 = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{\ell = 0}^\infty\Bigl[\frac{r^\ell}{r_0^{\ell + 1}} - \frac{R}{r_0}\frac{b^\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}\Bigr]P_\ell(\cos\theta) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{\ell = 0}^\infty\frac{1}{r_0^{\ell + 1}}\Bigl[r^\ell - \frac{R^{2\ell + 1}}{r^{\ell + 1}}\Bigr]P_\ell(\cos\theta) ,
> $$
>
> each bracket vanishing at $r = R$: the image is the resummed separated solution, as in [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-3|Theorem §C7.1.3]].
>
> *Sphere at potential $V_s$, or isolated with charge $Q$* ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^rem-c7-1-2|Remark: A sphere at a fixed potential, or isolated with a charge]]):
>
> $$
> V = V_0 + \frac{RV_s}{r}, \qquad\text{or}\qquad V = V_0 + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q + qR/r_0}{r} \qquad (r \ge R) ,
> $$
>
> and inside the metal $V = V_s$, respectively $V = (Q + qR/r_0)/4\pi\varepsilon_0R$. For the neutral sphere ($Q = 0$) it floats at $q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r_0$, the potential $q$ alone produces at the centre.
>
> *Force on $q$, neutral sphere.* The image $-qR/r_0$ at $b$ attracts, the central image $+qR/r_0$ repels:
>
> $$
> F_r = \frac{q^2R}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\frac{1}{r_0^3} - \frac{r_0}{(r_0^2 - R^2)^2}\Bigr] < 0 ,
> $$
>
> negative because $(r_0^2 - R^2)^2 < r_0^4$. For $r_0 \gg R$, $r_0/(r_0^2 - R^2)^2 = r_0^{-3}(1 + 2R^2/r_0^2 + \dots)$ and $F_r \approx -2q^2R^3/4\pi\varepsilon_0r_0^5$: the force between $q$ and the dipole it induces in a sphere of polarizability $4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^ex-b4-2-2|Example §B4.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 11, Problem 2 · Zangwill §8.3.5–§8.3.6, eqs. (8.31)–(8.34)*

## Cylinders

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.6: A Line Charge and a Conducting Cylinder
> An infinite line charge $\lambda$ parallel to the axis of an infinite conducting cylinder of radius $R$, at distance $s \ne R$ from the axis. On the side of the line, the potential is that of the line and an **image line $-\lambda$** at the inverse point $\vec s^{\,*}$ ($s^* = R^2/s$), plus a constant:
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{|\vec r - \vec s|}{|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|} + C, \qquad V\big|_{\text{cylinder}} = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac sR + C ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec r$, $\vec s$ two-dimensional. The cylinder is grounded for $C = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac sR$. For a line outside, the induced charge per length is $-\lambda$: every field line of the wire ends on the cylinder.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.3.7, eqs. (8.35)–(8.39)*

^thm-c7-1-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (equipotential). The two-dimensional version of [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^der-c7-1-4|Derivation of Theorem §C7.1.4]], Step 1 (the same algebra in the plane), gives $|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}| = (R/s)|\vec r - \vec s|$ on the circle $|\vec r| = R$. So the logarithm there is $\ln(s/R)$, a constant. Zangwill reaches the same circle by asking when the ratio of the two distances is constant (Apollonius); that works only if the image has strength exactly $-\lambda$, since for $\lambda' \ne -\lambda$ the level set of $\lambda\ln\mathcal R + \lambda'\ln\mathcal R'$ is not of the form $\mathcal R/\mathcal R' = $ const.
>
> **Step 2** (sources). The potential of a line $\lambda$ is $-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln|\vec r - \vec s|$ up to a constant ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^ex-b3-2-2|Example §B3.2.2]]); its Laplacian is $-\frac{\lambda}{\varepsilon_0}\delta^2(\vec r - \vec s)$ in the plane, because $\ln|\vec x|/2\pi$ is the fundamental solution of the two-dimensional Laplacian ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-5|452 Thm. §17.5]]). The image is on the other side of the circle, so the region keeps the right source.
>
> **Step 3** (the constant). In two dimensions the potential of a line charge does not vanish at infinity, so the potential is fixed only up to $C$; choosing $C$ sets the cylinder's potential. For a line outside, far away the two logarithms cancel ($V \to C$), so the net charge of wire and cylinder is zero and the cylinder carries $-\lambda$ per length.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Unlike the sphere, the image has the full strength $-\lambda$, and no flux escapes to infinity.
> - The charge and the potential of a cylinder *can* be set independently here by the free constant; for a sphere they cannot, because a point charge's potential vanishes at infinity (Zangwill §8.3.7).

^der-c7-1-6

## Dielectric images

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.7: A Point Charge at a Plane Interface between Two Dielectrics
> Simple dielectrics $\varepsilon_{rL}$ fill $z < 0$ and $\varepsilon_{rR}$ fill $z > 0$ ([[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^mod-c5-3-1|Model §C5.3.1]]); a free charge $q$ sits at $z = -d$. With $\varepsilon_L = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{rL}$, $\varepsilon_R = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{rR}$ and two separate image problems, each with all of space filled by one medium,
>
> $$
> V_L = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_L}\Bigl[\frac{q}{\sqrt{\rho^2 + (z + d)^2}} + \frac{q_R}{\sqrt{\rho^2 + (z - d)^2}}\Bigr], \qquad V_R = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_R}\,\frac{q_L}{\sqrt{\rho^2 + (z + d)^2}} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> q_R = \frac{\varepsilon_{rL} - \varepsilon_{rR}}{\varepsilon_{rL} + \varepsilon_{rR}}\,q, \qquad q_L = \frac{2\varepsilon_{rR}}{\varepsilon_{rL} + \varepsilon_{rR}}\,q, \qquad \vec F = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_L}\,\frac{q\,q_R}{4d^2}\,\hat z .
> $$
>
> The charge is pushed away from a medium of smaller permittivity and pulled towards one of larger permittivity; $\varepsilon_{rR} \to \infty$ gives the grounded conductor ($q_R = -q$).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.3.3, eqs. (8.14)–(8.19)*

^thm-c7-1-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (matching conditions, as distributions). In each medium $\varepsilon_r\nabla^2V = -\rho_f/\varepsilon_0$, and on the interface $V_L = V_R$, $\varepsilon_{rL}\partial_zV_L = \varepsilon_{rR}\partial_zV_R$ (no free surface charge) ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]]; the two conditions are what $\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon\nabla V) = -\rho_f$ says, as an identity of distributions, about the surface $\delta$ term on the interface). $V_L$ has the right source in $z < 0$ (the image $q_R$ is at $z = +d$), and $V_R$ has no source in $z > 0$ (its charge $q_L$ sits at $z = -d$).
>
> **Step 2** (continuity). At $z = 0$ all three distances are $\sqrt{\rho^2 + d^2}$, so $V_L = V_R$ for all $\rho$ requires
>
> $$
> \frac{q + q_R}{\varepsilon_{rL}} = \frac{q_L}{\varepsilon_{rR}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (normal $D$). At $z = 0$, $\partial_z(\rho^2 + (z \pm d)^2)^{-1/2} = \mp d/(\rho^2 + d^2)^{3/2}$, so
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_{rL}\partial_zV_L = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{(-q + q_R)\,d}{(\rho^2 + d^2)^{3/2}}, \qquad \varepsilon_{rR}\partial_zV_R = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{-q_L\,d}{(\rho^2 + d^2)^{3/2}} ,
> $$
>
> and equality for all $\rho$ requires $q - q_R = q_L$.
>
> **Step 4** (solve). Substituting $q_L = q - q_R$ into Step 2: $\varepsilon_{rR}(q + q_R) = \varepsilon_{rL}(q - q_R)$, so $q_R(\varepsilon_{rL} + \varepsilon_{rR}) = q(\varepsilon_{rL} - \varepsilon_{rR})$, and $q_L = q - q_R = 2\varepsilon_{rR}q/(\varepsilon_{rL} + \varepsilon_{rR})$. Uniqueness with dielectric interfaces ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]) makes this the solution.
>
> **Step 5** (force). In $z < 0$ the field is that of $q$ and $q_R$ in a medium $\varepsilon_L$; $q$'s own field exerts no force on it, and the field of $q_R$ at $(0, 0, -d)$, a distance $2d$ away along $-\hat z$, is $\frac{q_R}{4\pi\varepsilon_L(2d)^2}(-\hat z)$. The force on a free charge embedded in a simple dielectric is $q$ times the macroscopic field at its position, minus its own (Zangwill eq. (6.130); the force density $\rho_f\vec E$ of [[§C5.6 Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-6-2|Theorem §C5.6.2]]), which gives $\vec F$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Each region has its own image system, and neither describes the other region: the method is two auxiliary vacuum-like problems glued by two conditions.
> - $q_R$ has the sign of $q$ when $\varepsilon_{rL} > \varepsilon_{rR}$: an ion in water ($\varepsilon_r \approx 80$) is repelled from oil or protein ($\varepsilon_r \approx 2$).
> - With $\varepsilon_{rL} = 1$ this is the charge above a dielectric of level B ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^ex-b5-3-1|Example §B5.3.1]]: $q_R = q_b$, $q_L = q + q_b$), and the same result as [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^ex-c5-3-4|Example §C5.3.4]] and the force of [[§C5.6 Forces in Dielectrics#^ex-c5-6-3|Example §C5.6.3]] (Zangwill Example 6.7), obtained there without images.

^der-c7-1-7

*Uses:* [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]], [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]], [[§C5.6 Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-6-2|Theorem §C5.6.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.8: A Line Charge in a Dielectric Cylinder
> A line charge $\lambda$ at distance $b$ from the axis inside an infinite cylinder of radius $R > b$ and dielectric constant $\varepsilon_{r2}$, embedded in a medium $\varepsilon_{r1}$. With $\rho_2 = |\vec r - \vec b|$, $\rho_1 = |\vec r - \vec b^{\,*}|$ (inverse point, $b^* = R^2/b$) and $\rho = |\vec r|$:
>
> $$
> V_{\text{in}} = -\frac{\lambda\ln\rho_2 + \lambda'\ln\rho_1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{r2}} + C_{\text{in}}, \qquad V_{\text{out}} = -\frac{\lambda''\ln\rho_2 + (\lambda - \lambda'')\ln\rho}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{r1}} + C_{\text{out}} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \lambda' = \frac{\varepsilon_{r2} - \varepsilon_{r1}}{\varepsilon_{r2} + \varepsilon_{r1}}\,\lambda, \qquad \lambda'' = \frac{2\varepsilon_{r1}}{\varepsilon_{r2} + \varepsilon_{r1}}\,\lambda :
> $$
>
> inside, an image $\lambda'$ at the inverse point; outside, an image $\lambda''$ on the real line and $\lambda - \lambda''$ on the axis (the cylinder is neutral). The ratios are those of the plane interface ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-7|Theorem §C7.1.7]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.3.8, eqs. (8.40)–(8.48)*

^thm-c7-1-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the logarithm in polar harmonics). For $\vec r = (r, \varphi)$, $\vec r\,' = (r', \varphi')$ and $r_< = \min(r, r')$, $r_> = \max(r, r')$, write $|\vec r - \vec r\,'| = r_>\,|1 - w|$ with $w = (r_</r_>)e^{\pm i(\varphi - \varphi')}$, $|w| < 1$. Then $\ln|1 - w| = \operatorname{Re}\ln(1 - w) = -\sum_{m \ge 1}\operatorname{Re}(w^m)/m$, so
>
> $$
> \ln|\vec r - \vec r\,'| = \ln r_> - \sum_{m = 1}^\infty\frac1m\Bigl(\frac{r_<}{r_>}\Bigr)^m\cos m(\varphi - \varphi') \qquad (r \ne r')
> $$
>
> (Zangwill Problem 8.18). Put the line on the $x$ axis, $\varphi' = 0$.
>
> **Step 2** (values on the boundary $r = R$). Since $b < R < b^*$: $\ln\rho_2 = \ln R - \sum_m\frac1m(b/R)^m\cos m\varphi$; $\ln\rho_1 = \ln b^* - \sum_m\frac1m(R/b^*)^m\cos m\varphi$, and $R/b^* = b/R$; $\ln\rho = \ln R$. So with $\beta_m = \frac1m(b/R)^m$,
>
> $$
> V_{\text{in}}(R) = \text{const} + \frac{\lambda + \lambda'}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{r2}}\sum_m\beta_m\cos m\varphi, \qquad V_{\text{out}}(R) = \text{const} + \frac{\lambda''}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{r1}}\sum_m\beta_m\cos m\varphi .
> $$
>
> Continuity of $V$ for all $\varphi$ requires $(\lambda + \lambda')/\varepsilon_{r2} = \lambda''/\varepsilon_{r1}$ (Zangwill eq. (8.42)); the constants are matched by $C_{\text{in}} - C_{\text{out}}$.
>
> **Step 3** (radial derivatives at $r = R$). Differentiating the series of Step 1 in $r$: for the source at $b < R$ (so $r_> = r$ near $R$), $\partial_r\ln\rho_2 = \frac1R + \frac1R\sum_m(b/R)^m\cos m\varphi$ on both sides; for the image at $b^* > R$ ($r_< = r$), $\partial_r\ln\rho_1 = -\frac1R\sum_m(b/R)^m\cos m\varphi$; and $\partial_r\ln\rho = 1/R$. Hence
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_{r2}\partial_rV_{\text{in}} = -\frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0R}\Bigl[\lambda + (\lambda - \lambda')\sum_m(b/R)^m\cos m\varphi\Bigr], \qquad \varepsilon_{r1}\partial_rV_{\text{out}} = -\frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0R}\Bigl[\lambda + \lambda''\sum_m(b/R)^m\cos m\varphi\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> The constant terms agree (both media see total charge $\lambda$ inside); continuity of the normal component of $\vec D$ requires $\lambda - \lambda' = \lambda''$ (eq. (8.47)).
>
> **Step 4** (solve). Inserting $\lambda'' = \lambda - \lambda'$ into Step 2: $\varepsilon_{r1}(\lambda + \lambda') = \varepsilon_{r2}(\lambda - \lambda')$, so $\lambda'(\varepsilon_{r1} + \varepsilon_{r2}) = \lambda(\varepsilon_{r2} - \varepsilon_{r1})$, and $\lambda'' = \lambda - \lambda' = 2\varepsilon_{r1}\lambda/(\varepsilon_{r1} + \varepsilon_{r2})$. Uniqueness ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]) completes the argument.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Every Fourier mode $m$ on the circle must match separately, and the inverse point makes all modes match with *one* pair of conditions: the image at $b^*$ reproduces the source's harmonics with the ratio $(b/R)^m$ inverted.
> - Zangwill matches $D_n$ instead with a stream function $\psi$, $\vec D = \nabla\times(\psi\hat z)$, which is continuous wherever $D_n$ is; the Fourier route above uses only the potential.

^der-c7-1-8

*Uses:* [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]], [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]], [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-7|Theorem §C7.1.7]]

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: A dielectric sphere has no point image
> For a point charge near a dielectric sphere the image is not a point charge but a line segment from the centre to the inverse point, with a continuous, non-uniform charge density (Bussemer 1994). The reason shows up in the Legendre expansion. Matching $V$ and $\varepsilon_r\partial_rV$ at $r = R$ order by order, the source's $\ell$-th term outside the sphere comes back with the ratio $B_\ell = -\ell(\varepsilon_r - 1)/(\ell\varepsilon_r + \ell + 1)$ times the reflected harmonic $R^{2\ell + 1}/(s^{\ell + 1}r^{\ell + 1})$. A single charge at the inverse point gives exactly these harmonics with an $\ell$-*independent* ratio ($-1$ for the grounded conductor, [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-5|Theorem §C7.1.5]]). Here the ratio depends on $\ell$, so the image must be spread out. For the plane and the cylinder the ratio is the same for every mode, which is why a point (or line) image works there.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.3.6 (last paragraph) and footnote 5; the ratio $B_\ell$ worked here*

## Image dipoles

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.9: The Image of a Point Dipole in a Grounded Sphere
> A point dipole $\vec p$ at $\vec s$ ($s \ne R$; density $-\vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3(\vec r - \vec s)$, [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^mod-c3-1-2|Model §C3.1.2]]) near a grounded sphere of radius $R$. Its image, at the inverse point $\vec s^{\,*}$, is a point charge and a point dipole:
>
> $$
> q^* = \frac{R\,(\vec p\cdot\hat s)}{s^2}, \qquad \vec p^{\,*} = \Bigl(\frac Rs\Bigr)^3\bigl[2(\vec p\cdot\hat s)\hat s - \vec p\bigr] .
> $$
>
> The component of $\vec p$ along $\hat s$ is imaged with its sign kept, the transverse components reversed. For a plane ($R \to \infty$ at fixed distance from the surface) $q^* \to 0$ and this becomes the mirror dipole of [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^ex-b4-2-3|Example §B4.2.3]].
>
> *Source: worked here from Zangwill §8.3.5 (the point-charge image) and Example 8.1 (images of the two charges of a dipole)*

^thm-c7-1-9

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (a dipole is a derivative with respect to the source point). The potential of a point dipole is $\vec p\cdot\nabla_{\vec s}$ applied to the potential of a unit charge at $\vec s$: $(\vec p\cdot\nabla_{\vec s})|\vec r - \vec s|^{-1} = \vec p\cdot(\vec r - \vec s)/|\vec r - \vec s|^3$. This is the limit $q \to \infty$, $\vec\delta \to 0$, $q\vec\delta = \vec p$ of $+q$ at $\vec s + \vec\delta/2$ and $-q$ at $\vec s - \vec\delta/2$; as distributions the density is $-\vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3(\vec r - \vec s)$.
>
> **Step 2** (image of a unit charge as a function of $\vec s$). By [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]] the image potential of a unit charge is $-\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}g(\vec s)$ with $g(\vec s) = (R/s)/|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}(\vec s)|$. The grounded-sphere problem is linear in the source, so the image potential of the dipole is $-\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}(\vec p\cdot\nabla_{\vec s})g$.
>
> **Step 3** (differentiate). $g$ depends on $\vec s$ through the prefactor and through $\vec s^{\,*}$:
> - $\nabla_{\vec s}(R/s) = -R\hat s/s^2$;
> - $\partial s^*_j/\partial s_i = \frac{R^2}{s^2}(\delta_{ij} - 2\hat s_i\hat s_j)$, so $(\vec p\cdot\nabla_{\vec s})\vec s^{\,*} = \frac{R^2}{s^2}\bigl[\vec p - 2(\vec p\cdot\hat s)\hat s\bigr]$;
> - $\partial/\partial s^*_j\,|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|^{-1} = (\vec r - \vec s^{\,*})_j/|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|^3$.
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> (\vec p\cdot\nabla_{\vec s})g = -\frac{R(\vec p\cdot\hat s)}{s^2}\,\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|} + \frac{R^3}{s^3}\bigl[\vec p - 2(\vec p\cdot\hat s)\hat s\bigr]\cdot\frac{\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}}{|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|^3} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (read off). Multiplying by $-1/4\pi\varepsilon_0$, the image potential is $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\bigl[q^*/|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}| + \vec p^{\,*}\cdot(\vec r - \vec s^{\,*})/|\vec r - \vec s^{\,*}|^3\bigr]$ with $q^*$ and $\vec p^{\,*}$ as stated. It vanishes on the sphere because each charge-image pair of Step 1 does, before the limit.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Images of multipoles follow from images of charges by differentiating with respect to the source point; the image of an $\ell$-pole is a superposition of image multipoles of order up to $\ell$.
> - ⚑ A radial dipole has a *charged* image: the two charges of the dipole sit at different distances, so their images $-qR/s_\pm$ do not cancel.

^der-c7-1-9

*Uses:* [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]], [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^mod-c3-1-2|Model §C3.1.2]]

> [!example] Example §C7.1.2: A Dipole at the Centre of a Grounded Shell
> A point dipole $\vec p = p\hat z$ at the centre of a grounded conducting spherical shell of radius $R$. Show by images that the field inside is that of $\vec p$ plus the uniform field $\vec E = \vec p/4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3$.
>
> *Images.* Place the dipole at $\vec s$ with $s \to 0$ and use [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-9|Theorem §C7.1.9]]: the image charge $q^* = R(\vec p\cdot\hat s)/s^2$ and dipole $\vec p^{\,*}$ sit at distance $L = R^2/s \to \infty$. For $r \ll L$ expand both about the origin, with $|\vec r - L\hat s|^{-1} = L^{-1} + (\vec r\cdot\hat s)L^{-2} + O(r^2L^{-3})$:
> - charge: $\frac{q^*}{L} = \frac{\vec p\cdot\hat s}{Rs}$ (diverges), $\frac{q^*}{L^2}(\vec r\cdot\hat s) = \frac{(\vec p\cdot\hat s)(\vec r\cdot\hat s)}{R^3}$, remainder $O(sr^2/R^5) \to 0$;
> - dipole: $\frac{\vec p^{\,*}\cdot(\vec r - L\hat s)}{|\vec r - L\hat s|^3} = -\frac{\vec p^{\,*}\cdot\hat s}{L^2} + \frac{\vec p^{\,*}\cdot\vec r - 3(\vec p^{\,*}\cdot\hat s)(\vec r\cdot\hat s)}{L^3} + \dots$, where $-\frac{\vec p^{\,*}\cdot\hat s}{L^2} = -\frac{\vec p\cdot\hat s}{Rs}$ cancels the divergent constant, and $\frac{1}{L^3}\vec p^{\,*} = \frac{1}{R^3}\bigl[2(\vec p\cdot\hat s)\hat s - \vec p\bigr]$ gives $\frac{1}{R^3}\bigl[-(\vec p\cdot\hat s)(\vec r\cdot\hat s) - \vec p\cdot\vec r\bigr]$.
>
> The $\hat s$-dependent terms cancel, and as $s \to 0$
>
> $$
> V_{\text{im}}(\vec r) = -\frac{\vec p\cdot\vec r}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3}, \qquad \vec E_{\text{im}} = -\nabla V_{\text{im}} = \frac{\vec p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3} .
> $$
>
> (Equivalently, follow the course hint: the charges $\pm q$ at $\pm\frac\delta2\hat z$ have images $\mp 2qR/\delta$ at $\pm(2R^2/\delta)\hat z$, a pair receding to infinity whose field at the centre is $2\cdot\frac{2qR/\delta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(2R^2/\delta)^2} = \frac{q\delta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3}$, along $+\hat z$.)
>
> *Check by uniqueness.* Inside, $V = \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2} + \Phi$ with $\Phi$ harmonic and $\Phi(R, \theta) = -\frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2}$; the regular $\ell = 1$ solution $\Phi = -\frac{pr\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3}$ fits, and it is the image potential above ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]).
>
> *Induced charge and energy.* With $\hat n = \hat r$, $\sigma = \varepsilon_0\partial_rV\big|_R = \varepsilon_0\bigl[-\frac{2p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3} - \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3}\bigr] = -\frac{3p\cos\theta}{4\pi R^2}$, of total zero, as Gauss's law requires for a neutral source. A $\cos\theta$ layer makes a uniform field inside, as a uniformly polarized sphere does ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]). The image field is parallel to $\vec p$ (no torque), and the dipole's energy in the field of its own induced charge is $-\frac12\vec p\cdot\vec E_{\text{im}} = -p^2/8\pi\varepsilon_0R^3$, by the rule of [[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-2-8|Theorem §C7.2.8]] applied to the two charges of the dipole.
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 11, Problem 3 (Zangwill 8.11)*

> [!remark] Remark: A dipole near a plane
> Zangwill's Example 8.1 (a dipole at distance $d$ from a grounded metal, the work $p^2/64\pi\varepsilon_0d^3$ to turn it from perpendicular to parallel) is the same problem, with the same answer, as [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^ex-b4-2-3|Example §B4.2.3]]: there $U(\theta_0) = -p^2(1 + \cos^2\theta_0)/64\pi\varepsilon_0d^3$, and $U(\pi/2) - U(0) = p^2/64\pi\varepsilon_0d^3$. The torque from the image field, $\frac{p^2}{32\pi\varepsilon_0d^3}\sin\alpha\cos\alpha$, integrated over $\alpha$ from $0$ to $\pi/2$ gives the same number.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Example 8.1*

## Infinitely many images

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.10: A Charge between Two Grounded Plates: the Image Series
> A charge $q$ at $z = z_0$ between grounded plates $z = 0$ and $z = d$. The images are $+q$ at $z = 2md + z_0$ ($m \ne 0$) and $-q$ at $z = 2md - z_0$ (all $m \in \mathbb Z$). Grouped in pairs,
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \sum_{m \in \mathbb Z}v_m(\vec r), \qquad v_m = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\frac{1}{|\vec r - (2md + z_0)\hat z|} - \frac{1}{|\vec r - (2md - z_0)\hat z|}\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> the series converges absolutely, with $|v_m| = O(1/m^2)$ uniformly on bounded sets away from the charges, and is the potential in the slab. The positive and negative images summed separately diverge (like $\sum 1/|m|$). The force on $q$ is
>
> $$
> F_z = \frac{q^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\sum_{m = 1}^\infty\frac{1}{(md - z_0)^2} - \sum_{m = 0}^\infty\frac{1}{(md + z_0)^2}\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> directed to the nearer plate, zero at $z_0 = d/2$, and $\to -q^2/16\pi\varepsilon_0z_0^2$ as $z_0 \to 0$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §8.3.4 (the image set, for $z_0 = d/2$); convergence, general $z_0$ and the force worked here*

^thm-c7-1-10

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (bound). The pair $v_m$ is $\pm q$ at $2md \pm z_0$, separation $2z_0$, centred at $2md$. For $|m|d \gg r$ expand in $z_0$ (mean-value form of Taylor's theorem): $v_m = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,2z_0\,\frac{z - 2md}{|\vec r - 2md\hat z|^3} + O(z_0^3/m^4d^4)$, which is $O(1/m^2)$ with a constant uniform for $r$ in a bounded set. So $\sum|v_m| < \infty$, and the sum may be reordered freely. Separately, $\sum_m q/|\vec r - (2md + z_0)\hat z|$ diverges like $\sum 1/2|m|d$.
>
> **Step 2** (harmonic in the slab). The only charge in $0 < z < d$ is $q$ itself: $2md + z_0 \in (0, d)$ only for $m = 0$; $2md - z_0 < 0$ for $m \le 0$ and $> d$ for $m \ge 1$. A uniformly convergent series of harmonic functions is harmonic (the mean-value property passes to the limit; [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]), so $V - q/4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - z_0\hat z|$ is harmonic in the slab.
>
> **Step 3** (plate $z = 0$). Under $z \to -z$ the positive charge of pair $m$ goes to $-(2md + z_0) = 2(-m)d - z_0$, the position of the negative charge of pair $-m$, and vice versa. So $v_m(x, y, -z) = -v_{-m}(x, y, z)$. Reindexing the absolutely convergent sum, $V(x, y, -z) = -V(x, y, z)$, hence $V = 0$ at $z = 0$.
>
> **Step 4** (plate $z = d$). Under $z \to 2d - z$, $2md + z_0 \to 2(1 - m)d - z_0$ and $2md - z_0 \to 2(1 - m)d + z_0$: pair $m$ goes to pair $1 - m$ with signs reversed, $v_m(x, y, 2d - z) = -v_{1 - m}(x, y, z)$. Reindexing, $V(2d - z) = -V(z)$, hence $V = 0$ at $z = d$.
>
> **Step 5** (infinity and uniqueness). $V \to 0$ as $\rho \to \infty$ in the slab (exponentially, [[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^ex-c7-2-3|Example §C7.2.3]]). By [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]] and uniqueness, $V$ is the potential.
>
> **Step 6** (force). By [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]] (3). The positive images at $2md + z_0$, $m \ne 0$, are at distance $2|m|d$ above and below $q$; their forces $\mp\frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(2md)^2}$ cancel between $m$ and $-m$. The negative image at $2md - z_0$ is a distance $|2z_0 - 2md|$ away and attracts $q$: upwards with $\frac{q^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0(md - z_0)^2}$ for $m \ge 1$, downwards with $\frac{q^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0(z_0 + |m|d)^2}$ for $m \le 0$. Both sums converge absolutely. At $z_0 = d/2$ the substitution $m \to m + 1$ maps one sum onto the other; as $z_0 \to 0$ the $m = 0$ term $-q^2/16\pi\varepsilon_0z_0^2$ dominates, the single-plane force of [[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - An infinite image set defines a potential only together with a rule for summing it; here the rule is "pair each image with its partner", which makes both reflection symmetries exact term by term.
> - ⚑ What may be computed from the series term by term, and what not, is a separate question: the induced charge on a plate may not ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^cau-c7-1-1|Caution: Induced charge from an image series]]).

> [!caution] Caution: Induced charge from an image series
> For the slab of [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-10|Theorem §C7.1.10]] each pair $v_m$ lies entirely on one side of the plate $z = d$, so its flux through that infinite plane is zero; summing pair by pair would give zero induced charge on the plate. The true induced charge is $-qz_0/d$ ([[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^ex-c7-2-3|Example §C7.2.3]]; also Green's reciprocity, [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^ex-c4-3-2|Example §C4.3.2]]). The flux through a disc of radius $L$ in the plate, summed over all images, does give $-qz_0/d$ once $L$ is a few $d$ (checked numerically for $z_0 = 0.3d$). The two limits do not commute: a pair at height $2|m|d$ spreads its flux over a radius $\sim 2|m|d$, so the convergence in $m$ is not uniform over the infinite plate, and $\lim_{L \to \infty}\sum_m \ne \sum_m\lim_{L \to \infty}$ ([[§CA.1 Exchanging Limits, Derivatives and Integrals|QFT §CA.1]]).
>
> *Source: worked here; the value $-qz_0/d$ from Zangwill Example 8.4*

> [!remark] Remark: When image series converge fast
> When each reflection carries a factor of modulus less than one, the image series converges geometrically and causes no trouble. A charge in front of a dielectric slab of thickness $c$ sees, beyond the slab, images of strength $(1 - \beta^2)\beta^{2n}q$, $\beta = (\varepsilon_r - 1)/(\varepsilon_r + 1)$, shifted by $2nc$ (Zangwill Problem 8.4, the transmission factors $1 \pm \beta$ of [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-7|Theorem §C7.1.7]] and one factor $\beta^2$ per round trip). Two spheres generate image sequences whose charges shrink by the ratios $R/s$ at each inversion (Zangwill Problems 8.7–8.8). Conducting plates reflect with factor $-1$, which is why the slab of [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-10|Theorem §C7.1.10]] needs care.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 8.4, §8.3.4*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: An ion in a membrane channel
> A channel of radius $a$ and length $L \gg a$ through a protein wall ($\varepsilon_r \approx 2$) joins two water reservoirs ($\varepsilon_{rW} \approx 80$). By [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-7|Theorem §C7.1.7]] an ion in the water-filled channel is repelled by the walls, so its field lines run along the channel: Gauss's law gives the field $E_0 = q/2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{rW}a^2$ on either side, as for a sheet $q/\pi a^2$. Modelling the reservoirs as grounded plates at $\pm L/2$ and the ion at $z$ as such a sheet, zero voltage across the channel and charge $-q$ drawn from ground give $E_{L,R} = 2E_0(\tfrac12 \mp z/L)$, and the energy $\frac12\int\vec E\cdot\vec D$ over the channel volume $\mathcal V_c = \pi a^2L$ is
>
> $$
> U(z) = 2\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{rW}E_0^2\,\mathcal V_c\Bigl(\frac14 - \frac{z^2}{L^2}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> a barrier $q^2L/8\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{rW}a^2$ at the centre, far above $kT$. (Zangwill's eq. (8.25) prints $2\pi\varepsilon_0$ in place of $2\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{rW}$; the computation from his eqs. (8.22)–(8.24) gives the latter.)
>
> *Source: Zangwill Application 8.1, eqs. (8.22)–(8.25)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The image principle is uniqueness used as a licence to guess ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^rem-b3-1-3|§B3.1, Remark: Uniqueness is a licence to guess]]); its general form, the Dirichlet Green function, is the potential of a unit charge with its images, and [[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation|§C7.2]] turns every image solution here into a formula for arbitrary charge and boundary data.
> - Kelvin inversion is the three-dimensional cousin of the conformal maps of [[§C6.4★ The Complex Potential and the Variational Principle|§C6.4★]]; in the plane, inversion in a circle, $w \mapsto R^2/\bar w$, is anticonformal and maps harmonic functions to harmonic functions without any prefactor, which is why the cylinder's image has full strength while the sphere's carries $R/s$.
> - The mode sum of [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-3|Theorem §C7.1.3]] and the Legendre check in [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^ex-c7-1-1|Example §C7.1.1]] are the same statement in two coordinate systems: an image is a resummed series of separated solutions ([[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates|§C6.2]], [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates|§C6.3]]).
> - The repeated reflections in two parallel plates generate the infinite dihedral group, as the reflections in the two faces of a wedge of angle $\pi/n$ generate the dihedral group of order $2n$ ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^ex-b3-2-1|Example §B3.2.1]]); for the infinite group, convergence of the orbit sum becomes the issue of [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-10|Theorem §C7.1.10]].
> - The image of a dipole by differentiation with respect to the source point is the same move that builds the dipole layer from a charge layer ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]).
