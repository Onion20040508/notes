The circuit rules of [[§A6.2 Resistors in Series and Parallel|§A6.2]]–[[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules|§A6.3]] say how a meter must be connected to measure a current or a voltage, and why connecting it disturbs the circuit as little as possible only if its resistance is very small or very large. The same rules, together with Joule heating ([[§A5.4 Electrical Power|§A5.4]]), explain the two hazards of electricity, overheating and shock, and the wiring that guards against them.

## Measuring current, voltage and resistance

> [!definition] Definition §A6.4.1: Ammeter, Voltmeter, Ohmmeter
> - An **ammeter** measures the current through a component. It is connected **in series** with the component, so that the same current passes through both, and it should have a very small resistance (a fraction of a milliohm).
> - A **voltmeter** measures the potential difference across a component. It is connected **in parallel** with the component, so that both have the same potential difference, and it should have a very large resistance ($\sim 10\ \mathrm{M\Omega}$ for inexpensive meters, up to $\sim 10\ \mathrm{G\Omega}$ for precise ones).
> - An **ohmmeter** measures a resistance as $R = V/I$, either by applying a known voltage from an internal source and measuring the current, or (in digital meters) by passing a known current and measuring the voltage. The component must be isolated from the rest of the circuit, and never connected to a live circuit.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.4*

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.4.1: Loading by a Meter
> 1. An ammeter of resistance $R_A$ inserted into a branch of resistance $R$ driven by a fixed voltage $V$ changes its current from $V/R$ to $V/(R + R_A)$, a relative change of about $R_A/R$ when $R_A \ll R$.
> 2. A voltmeter of resistance $R_M$ connected across a resistor $R$ replaces it by the parallel combination
>
>    $$
>    \frac{R R_M}{R + R_M} = \frac{R}{1 + R/R_M} \approx R\Bigl(1 - \frac{R}{R_M}\Bigr) \qquad (R \ll R_M),
>    $$
>
>    so it disturbs the circuit by a relative amount of about $R/R_M$.
>
> A meter therefore reads accurately only if $R_A \ll R$ for an ammeter and $R_M \gg R$ for a voltmeter.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.4 (qualitative); derived here from §A6.2*

^thm-a6-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. The ammeter is in series with the branch, so the branch resistance becomes $R + R_A$ ([[§A6.2 Resistors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a6-2-1|Theorem §A6.2.1]]), and $V/(R + R_A) = (V/R)\,(1 + R_A/R)^{-1}$.
> 2. The voltmeter is in parallel with $R$, so the pair has resistance $(1/R + 1/R_M)^{-1} = RR_M/(R + R_M)$ ([[§A6.2 Resistors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]]). For $x = R/R_M \ll 1$, $1/(1 + x) \approx 1 - x$.

*Uses:* [[§A6.2 Resistors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a6-2-1|Theorem §A6.2.1]], [[§A6.2 Resistors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]], [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-5|Law §A5.3.5]]

> [!caution] Caution: Wrong connections
> - An ammeter connected *in parallel*, for instance straight across a battery, is nearly a short circuit: a huge current flows through it. Ammeters usually contain a fuse for this reason.
> - A voltmeter connected *in series* puts a huge resistance in the circuit: the current almost stops, and the meter reads nearly the full source voltage.
> - A voltmeter across a battery under load reads the terminal voltage $\mathcal E - Ir$, never the emf alone ([[§A6.1 Electromotive Force#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]]).

^cau-a6-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Analog and digital meters
> An analog meter is built around a **galvanometer**, a coil of small resistance in a magnetic field that rotates when current passes through it, moving a pointer along a scale ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop|§A7.4]]). With a small **shunt** resistance in parallel it becomes an ammeter (most of the current bypasses the coil); with a large resistance in series it becomes a voltmeter. A digital meter instead converts the analog voltage into a number with an analog-to-digital converter; it can respond to much smaller currents and has largely replaced analog meters.

^rem-a6-4-1

## Electrical hazards

> [!definition] Definition §A6.4.2: Thermal Hazard; Shock Hazard; Short Circuit
> A **thermal hazard** is an excessive current causing unwanted heating, for instance a fire inside a wall. A **shock hazard** is a current passing through a person. A **short circuit** is an unintended low-resistance path between the terminals of a source; since $P = V^2/r$ is large for small $r$, it converts energy into heat very quickly. A circuit is **overloaded** when its current exceeds the rating of its wires, whose resistance $R_w$ then dissipates a dangerous $I^2R_w$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.6*

^def-a6-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: What determines the severity of a shock
> The severity depends on the current, its path through the body (the heart is the danger), its duration and its frequency ($f = 0$ for DC). The current is the main factor, and it is fixed by the voltage *and* the body's resistance, $I = V/R$. Approximate thresholds:
>
> | current | effect |
> |---|---|
> | about $1\ \mathrm{mA}$ | threshold of sensation |
> | below $5\ \mathrm{mA}$ | unpleasant but harmless; the usual safety limit |
> | $5$–$30\ \mathrm{mA}$ and above | sustained muscle contraction: the victim may be unable to let go |
> | above $300\ \mathrm{mA}$ | heart and diaphragm held contracted for the duration of the shock; through the heart it may be fatal |
>
> The body conducts fairly well because of its water content. Danger arises when it touches a live conductor and "ground" at the same time; hence insulating shoes, and working with one hand to keep current away from the heart. Most electrocutions kill through ventricular fibrillation, a massively irregular beating of the heart; conversely, a controlled shock from a defibrillator can stop fibrillation.

^rem-a6-4-2

> [!example] Example §A6.4.1: Dry Skin and Wet Skin
> A person with dry skin has a resistance of about $R = 200\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$. Touching $120\ \mathrm V$, the current through them is
>
> $$
> I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{120\ \mathrm V}{200\times 10^3\ \Omega} = 0.6\ \mathrm{mA},
> $$
>
> below the threshold of sensation. Soaking wet, the same person may have $R = 10.0\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$, and the current becomes $12\ \mathrm{mA}$: above the "can't let go" threshold and potentially dangerous. The voltage is the same; the resistance decides. This is why bathrooms and kitchens get special protection.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.6*

^ex-a6-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: The three-wire system
> Household circuits use alternating current, but the safety logic is that of DC circuits ([[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits|§A11.1]]).
> - The **live** (hot) wire carries the source voltage. A fuse or circuit breaker in series with it opens when the current exceeds its rating, preventing thermal overload ([[§A5.4 Electrical Power#^rem-a5-4-2|Remark on fuses]]).
> - The **neutral** wire is the return path. It is connected to ground at the source and again at the building, which holds it at zero volts relative to ground.
> - The **ground** wire (green) connects the metal case of the appliance to ground. If worn insulation lets the live wire touch the case, the fault current runs to ground through this low-resistance wire instead of through a person, and the breaker trips. Without it, the case would sit at the live voltage, and the appliance might even keep working normally, until someone touches it.
> - A **ground fault circuit interrupter** (GFCI) compares the currents in the live and neutral wires. If they differ by more than about $5\ \mathrm{mA}$, some current is returning by another path, possibly through a person, and the GFCI opens the circuit within a fraction of a second. It senses the difference by electromagnetic induction ([[§A9.1 Faraday's Law|§A9.1]]).
>
> Three-prong plugs fit only one way, so that these connections cannot be swapped. The colour codes of the insulation vary between countries.
