Most circuits contain more than one resistor. When resistors are connected one after another, or side by side between the same two points, the combination behaves like a single resistor whose value follows from two facts: charge is conserved, and the potential has one value at each point. Repeating the two rules reduces many networks to a single equivalent resistance; the networks that cannot be reduced this way need Kirchhoff's rules ([[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules|§A6.3]]).

## Series and parallel

> [!definition] Definition §A6.2.1: Series and Parallel Connection; Equivalent Resistance
> Resistors are connected **in series** when the current passes through them one after another, with no branch point between them, so that the same current must flow through each. They are connected **in parallel** when each has one end joined to a common point and the other end joined to a second common point, by wires of negligible resistance, so that all have the same potential difference across them. The **equivalent resistance** $R_{\text{eq}}$ of a combination is the resistance of the single resistor that, connected between the same two terminals, would draw the same current at the same voltage.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.2*

^def-a6-2-1

![[ph-em-a6-2-1.svg]]
*Left: three resistors in series carry the same current $I$ (red) and share the source voltage, $V = V_1 + V_2 + V_3$. Right: three resistors in parallel have the same voltage $V$ across them and share the current, $I = I_1 + I_2 + I_3$. The wires (blue) along each side are at a single potential.*

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.2.1: Resistors in Series
> For $N$ resistors in series,
>
> $$
> R_{\text{eq}} = R_1 + R_2 + \dots + R_N = \sum_{i=1}^N R_i .
> $$
>
> The same current $I$ flows through each; the total voltage $V$ divides in proportion to the resistances, $V_i = IR_i = V\,R_i/R_{\text{eq}}$. The equivalent resistance exceeds the largest of the $R_i$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.2, eq. (10.2)*

^thm-a6-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With no branch points, the steady current is the same through every resistor ([[§A5.1 Electric Current#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]). The potential drop across the whole chain is the sum of the drops across its parts, because the potential has one value at each junction between them ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference|§A3.2]]): $V = V_1 + \dots + V_N$. By Ohm's law $V_i = IR_i$, so $V = I(R_1 + \dots + R_N)$, and $V/I = \sum R_i$.

^der-a6-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference|§A3.2]], [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-5|Law §A5.3.5]], [[§A6.2 Resistors in Series and Parallel#^def-a6-2-1|Def. §A6.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.2.2: Resistors in Parallel
> For $N$ resistors in parallel,
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{R_N} = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{R_i} .
> $$
>
> Each has the full voltage $V$ across it; the total current divides in inverse proportion to the resistances, $I_i = V/R_i$. For two resistors, $R_{\text{eq}} = R_1R_2/(R_1 + R_2)$ and $I_1/I_2 = R_2/R_1$. The equivalent resistance is less than the smallest of the $R_i$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.2, eq. (10.3)*

^thm-a6-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> All the resistors connect the same two points, so they have the same potential difference $V$ across them, and $I_i = V/R_i$. Charge is conserved at the branch points, so the current $I$ entering the combination equals the sum of the branch currents ([[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]]):
>
> $$
> I = \sum_i I_i = V \sum_i \frac{1}{R_i} ,
> $$
>
> and $1/R_{\text{eq}} = I/V = \sum_i 1/R_i$. Since each term is positive, $1/R_{\text{eq}} > 1/R_i$ for every $i$.

^der-a6-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A6.2 Resistors in Series and Parallel#^def-a6-2-1|Def. §A6.2.1]], [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-5|Law §A5.3.5]], [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why doesn't all the current take the easy path?
> Put a $1\ \Omega$ and a $100\ \Omega$ resistor in parallel. Charge does not "choose" the easier path: the same potential difference pushes current through both, like gas escaping from a pressurised bottle through a wide and a narrow opening at once. Each path carries $V/R$, so the $1\ \Omega$ path carries 100 times more current, and some still goes through the $100\ \Omega$ path. In terms of the **conductance** $G = 1/R$ (the size of the opening), parallel conductances simply add, $G_{\text{eq}} = G_1 + G_2$, which is Theorem §A6.2.2. Adding a parallel path always lowers the total resistance and raises the total current.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 9 slides*

^rem-a6-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Compare capacitors
> The rules are reversed for capacitors ([[§A4.2 Capacitors in Series and Parallel|§A4.2]]): capacitances add in parallel and their reciprocals add in series. For resistors the current is the shared quantity in series and the voltage the shared quantity in parallel, and the same is true for capacitors (with charge in place of current); what flips is that $V = IR$ puts $R$ in the numerator, while $V = Q/C$ puts $C$ in the denominator.
>
> | | series | parallel |
> |---|---|---|
> | resistors | $R_{\text{eq}} = \sum R_i$ | $1/R_{\text{eq}} = \sum 1/R_i$ |
> | capacitors | $1/C_{\text{eq}} = \sum 1/C_i$ | $C_{\text{eq}} = \sum C_i$ |

^rem-a6-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: Recognising series and parallel
> - Two resistors are in series only if *nothing* branches off between them; sharing one end point is not enough.
> - Two resistors are in parallel if *both* ends connect, through wires alone, to the same two points. A wire of negligible resistance makes its two ends one point, however long it is drawn; redrawing the circuit often makes the structure obvious.
> - Many circuits are neither (a bridge, or anything with sources in several branches). Then use Kirchhoff's rules.

^cau-a6-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Series and parallel in practice
> In series, one failure affects everything: if one lamp in a series string burns out, the whole string goes dark. Household wiring and a car's headlights and radio are in parallel, so each device gets the full source voltage and works independently. The connecting wires are, however, in series with the parallel loads. When a large appliance is switched on, the large current through the wire resistance $R_w$ costs a voltage $IR_w$ and the other loads get less: lights dim when a refrigerator motor starts. For instance, with $R_w = 1.00\ \Omega$ in series with a $6.00\ \Omega$ and a $13.0\ \Omega$ load in parallel across $12.0\ \mathrm V$, $R_{\text{eq}} = 5.10\ \Omega$, $I = 2.35\ \mathrm A$, and the loads get $12.0\ \mathrm V - 2.35\ \mathrm V = 9.65\ \mathrm V$; the $6.00\ \Omega$ load dissipates $15.5\ \mathrm W$ instead of the $24.0\ \mathrm W$ it would receive with ideal wires.

^rem-a6-2-3

## Example

> [!example] Example §A6.2.1: Reducing a Network Step by Step
> In the circuit below, $R_1 = 7\ \Omega$ is in series with a parallel combination of $R_2 = 10\ \Omega$ and the series pair $R_3 = 6\ \Omega$, $R_4 = 4\ \Omega$; the source supplies $V = 24\ \mathrm V$.
>
> *Reduce.* $R_3$ and $R_4$ are in series: $R_{34} = 10\ \Omega$. $R_2$ and $R_{34}$ are in parallel: $R_{234} = (1/10 + 1/10)^{-1}\ \Omega = 5\ \Omega$. $R_1$ and $R_{234}$ are in series: $R_{\text{eq}} = 12\ \Omega$.
>
> *Expand back.* The source current is $I = V/R_{\text{eq}} = 2\ \mathrm A$, all of it through $R_1$, which drops $V_1 = (2\ \mathrm A)(7\ \Omega) = 14\ \mathrm V$. The remaining $10\ \mathrm V$ is across both parallel branches, so $I_2 = 10\ \mathrm V/10\ \Omega = 1\ \mathrm A$ and $I_3 = I_4 = 2\ \mathrm A - 1\ \mathrm A = 1\ \mathrm A$, with $V_3 = 6\ \mathrm V$ and $V_4 = 4\ \mathrm V$.
>
> *Check.* The resistors dissipate $I_1^2R_1 + I_2^2R_2 + I_3^2R_3 + I_4^2R_4 = 28 + 10 + 6 + 4 = 48\ \mathrm W$, equal to the power $IV = (2\ \mathrm A)(24\ \mathrm V) = 48\ \mathrm W$ supplied by the source, as energy conservation requires. Checking the power balance is a cheap test of any circuit solution.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.2, Fig. 10.15*
