A steady current needs something that keeps pushing charge "uphill": in a resistor charges only lose potential energy ([[§A5.4 Electrical Power|§A5.4]]), and the electrostatic field cannot return it to them around a closed path. A battery, generator or solar cell does this work. Its strength is the electromotive force, and every real source also has an internal resistance, which is why a battery's output voltage sags under load and as the battery runs down.

## Electromotive force

> [!definition] Definition §A6.1.1: Source of emf; Electromotive Force
> A **source of emf** is a two-terminal device that keeps one terminal (marked $+$) at a higher potential than the other ($-$) by doing non-electrostatic work on charges inside it: chemical work in a battery, mechanical work in a generator. Its **electromotive force** (emf) $\mathcal E$ is the work it does per unit charge moved through it from the $-$ to the $+$ terminal,
>
> $$
> \mathcal E = \frac{dW}{dq} ,
> $$
>
> measured in volts ($1\ \mathrm V = 1\ \mathrm{J/C}$). When no current flows, the potential difference between the terminals equals $\mathcal E$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.1*

^def-a6-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: A charge pump
> Despite the name (Volta's, 1800s), emf is not a force but an energy per charge. The course's picture: a battery is a pump, or the lift hill of a roller coaster. It raises charges to a state of high potential energy, from which they "fall" through the external circuit, giving up that energy in lamps and resistors, and are lifted again. Inside a lead–acid cell, chemical reactions move electrons onto the negative plate and remove them from the positive one, against the electric force; the reaction proceeds only when the external circuit is closed.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 9 slides; UP2 §10.1*

^rem-a6-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why a non-electrostatic source is needed
> The electrostatic field is conservative: its work on a charge around any closed path is zero ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference|§A3.2]]; compare [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-1|CM Theorem §A4.5.1]]). But around a circuit the resistors take energy from the charges at every pass. So some agent other than the electrostatic field must supply energy around the loop, and the emf is exactly the energy per charge it supplies. Inside the battery the charges move *against* the electrostatic field, from $-$ to $+$.

^rem-a6-1-2

## Internal resistance and terminal voltage

> [!model] Model §A6.1.2: Battery with Internal Resistance
> An **ideal battery** keeps the potential difference between its terminals equal to $\mathcal E$ whatever the current. A **real battery** is modelled as an ideal emf $\mathcal E$ in series with an **internal resistance** $r$. Both are treated as constants.
>
> In reality $r$ comes from the resistance of the chemicals and plates; it grows as the battery is depleted (oxidised plates, weaker acid) and can depend on the current, the temperature and the battery's history. In nickel–cadmium cells, for instance, it depends on how often and how deeply they have been discharged. An emf cannot be produced without some internal resistance.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.1*

^mod-a6-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.3: Terminal Voltage
> For a battery modelled as in Model §A6.1.2, with current $I$ leaving the positive terminal, the potential difference between the terminals is
>
> $$
> V_{\text{terminal}} = \mathcal E - Ir .
> $$
>
> Connected to an external load resistance $R$, the battery drives the current
>
> $$
> I = \frac{\mathcal E}{R + r} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.1, eq. (10.1)*

^thm-a6-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Follow the potential through the battery from the $-$ terminal to the $+$ terminal, along the current. The ideal emf raises it by $\mathcal E$, and the internal resistance, crossed in the direction of the current, lowers it by $Ir$ ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-5|Law §A5.3.5]]). Hence $V_+ - V_- = \mathcal E - Ir$. With a load $R$ across the terminals, the same potential difference drives the current through $R$, so $V_{\text{terminal}} = IR$. Equating, $\mathcal E - Ir = IR$, which gives $I = \mathcal E/(R + r)$.

^der-a6-1-3

> [!caution] Caution: Terminal voltage is not the emf
> - The terminal voltage equals $\mathcal E$ only when no current flows. Under load it is smaller by $Ir$; a "heavy" load (a small $R$) draws a large current and pulls the terminal voltage well below $\mathcal E$. (UP2 defines the terminal voltage in one place as the voltage with no load attached; that is the emf.)
> - When a battery is **charged**, a charger forces current into the positive terminal, $I < 0$ in the formula, and $V_{\text{terminal}} = \mathcal E + |I|\,r$ exceeds the emf. The charger's voltage must exceed $\mathcal E$ to reverse the current.
> - A voltmeter connected to the terminals can never read $\mathcal E$ alone under load: the internal resistance is always included.

^cau-a6-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Power bookkeeping
> Multiplying $\mathcal E = Ir + IR$ by $I$: the source converts chemical energy at the rate $\mathcal E I$; of this, $I^2r$ heats the battery itself and $I V_{\text{terminal}} = I^2R$ is delivered to the load ([[§A5.4 Electrical Power#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]). This is why a shorted or heavily loaded battery gets hot. Battery testers draw a known current through a small load and check that the terminal voltage stays acceptable, which indirectly measures $r$.

^rem-a6-1-3

## Example

> [!example] Example §A6.1.1: Light and Heavy Loads
> A battery has $\mathcal E = 12.00\ \mathrm V$ and $r = 0.100\ \Omega$.
>
> (a) With a $10.00\ \Omega$ load, $I = \mathcal E/(R + r) = 12.00\ \mathrm V/10.10\ \Omega = 1.188\ \mathrm A$ and $V_{\text{terminal}} = 12.00\ \mathrm V - (1.188\ \mathrm A)(0.100\ \Omega) = 11.88\ \mathrm V$, barely below the emf.
>
> (b) With a $0.500\ \Omega$ load, $I = 12.00\ \mathrm V/0.600\ \Omega = 20.0\ \mathrm A$ and $V_{\text{terminal}} = 12.00\ \mathrm V - 2.00\ \mathrm V = 10.0\ \mathrm V$. The load dissipates $I^2R = 200\ \mathrm W$, and the battery itself $I^2 r = 40\ \mathrm W$; together they account for $\mathcal E I = 240\ \mathrm W$.
>
> (c) If the internal resistance of the aged battery rises to $r = 0.500\ \Omega$, the same $0.500\ \Omega$ load gets $I = 12.00\ \mathrm V/1.00\ \Omega = 12.0\ \mathrm A$, $V_{\text{terminal}} = 6.00\ \mathrm V$ and only $I^2R = 72.0\ \mathrm W$.
>
> Internal resistance matters only when it is not small compared with the load. As a battery ages, $r$ grows and the delivered current, voltage and power all fall: car headlights dim gradually instead of going out at once.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 10.1*

^ex-a6-1-1

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The statement "the electrostatic field does no work around a closed loop" is the vanishing circulation of a [[§11 The Three Differential Operators꞉ Gradient, Curl, Divergence#^def-11-1|conservative (gradient) field (Def. §11.1)]]. The emf is the circulation of the non-electrostatic force per charge, which is how it is defined at level B and how induced emfs appear in [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law|§A9.1]].
