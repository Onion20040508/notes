Series and parallel reduction fails as soon as a circuit has sources in several branches, or resistors that are neither in series nor in parallel. Two rules then analyse any DC circuit. The junction rule expresses conservation of charge, the loop rule the fact that the potential has a single value at each point; together with Ohm's law they give a system of linear equations for the unknown currents.

## The two rules

> [!definition] Definition §A6.3.1: Junction, Branch, Loop
> A **junction** (node) is a point where three or more wires meet. A **branch** is a path between two junctions containing no other junction; in steady state it carries one current. A **loop** is any closed path through the circuit.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.3*

^def-a6-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.3.1: Kirchhoff's Junction Rule
> In a steady state, the total current flowing into any junction equals the total current flowing out of it:
>
> $$
> \sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.3, eq. (10.4)*

^thm-a6-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Surround the junction by a small closed surface. Charge is conserved ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge|§A1.1]]), so the charge inside changes at the rate $\sum I_{\text{in}} - \sum I_{\text{out}}$. In a steady state charge does not pile up at the junction, so this rate is zero. This is [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]] with more than two wires.

^der-a6-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Electric Charge|§A1.1]] (conservation of charge), [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^def-a5-1-1|Def. §A5.1.1]], [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.3.2: Kirchhoff's Loop Rule
> The algebraic sum of the changes in potential across all the elements around any closed loop is zero:
>
> $$
> \sum_{\text{loop}} \Delta V = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.3, eq. (10.5)*

^thm-a6-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In a DC circuit the electric field is electrostatic, so it is conservative and the potential $V$ is a single-valued function of position ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference|§A3.2]]). Going around a loop element by element, the potential changes by $\Delta V_1, \Delta V_2, \dots$ and returns to the value it started from, so the changes add to zero. In terms of energy: per unit charge, the energy supplied by the sources of emf in a loop equals the energy converted in its resistors ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]).

^der-a6-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference|§A3.2]], [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-1|CM Theorem §A4.5.1]], [[§11 The Three Differential Operators꞉ Gradient, Curl, Divergence#^def-11-1|Def. §11.1]] (conservative field)

> [!caution] Caution: When the loop rule fails
> The derivation needs a single-valued potential. When a changing magnetic flux threads the loop, the induced electric field is not conservative and the sum of potential drops around the loop is no longer zero ([[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields|§A9.4]]). Circuits with inductors are still analysed with a loop equation, by treating the induced emf of each inductor as one more element ([[§A10.4 RL Circuits|§A10.4]]).

^cau-a6-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Sign rules for the loop rule
> Traverse each element from $a$ to $b$ in the direction of the loop:
>
> | element | traversed | $\Delta V = V_b - V_a$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | resistor | along the assumed current $I$ | $-IR$ |
> | resistor | against the assumed current $I$ | $+IR$ |
> | source of emf | from $-$ to $+$ | $+\mathcal E$ |
> | source of emf | from $+$ to $-$ | $-\mathcal E$ |
>
> Current flows "downhill" through a resistor, so going with it the potential drops. A source's sign depends only on the direction of traversal, not on the current through it; its internal resistance is a resistor like any other.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.3, Fig. 10.23*

^rem-a6-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Method, and how many equations
> 1. Label points and assign a current, with an arbitrary direction, to every branch. A current that comes out negative simply flows the other way.
> 2. Write the junction rule at the junctions. With $N$ junctions only $N - 1$ of the equations are independent: the last one follows from the others (in a two-junction circuit the two equations are identical).
> 3. Write the loop rule for enough loops that every element lies in at least one loop and each new loop contains an element not in the earlier ones; for a circuit drawn flat, the "window panes" of the diagram are a good choice. Further loops give nothing new.
> 4. Solve the linear system, then check that the power supplied by the sources equals the power dissipated.
>
> Series and parallel rules are special cases: they are what the two Kirchhoff rules give for those connections ([[§A6.2 Resistors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a6-2-1|Theorem §A6.2.1]], [[§A6.2 Resistors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]]). The course's summary: three rules, "how resistors work" ($I = \Delta V/R$) and the two "logic" rules, untangle any circuit.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.3; PHY 182, TBL 16*

^rem-a6-3-2

## Several sources

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.3.3: Batteries in Series and in Parallel
> 1. For $N$ batteries in series (the $+$ terminal of each joined to the $-$ terminal of the next), carrying current $I$,
>
>    $$
>    V_{\text{terminal}} = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathcal E_i - I \sum_{i=1}^N r_i :
>    $$
>
>    the emfs add, and so do the internal resistances.
> 2. For $N$ batteries of equal emf $\mathcal E$ in parallel (all $+$ terminals joined, all $-$ terminals joined), delivering a total current $I$,
>
>    $$
>    V_{\text{terminal}} = \mathcal E - I r_{\text{eq}}, \qquad \frac{1}{r_{\text{eq}}} = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{r_i} .
>    $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.3, eqs. (10.6)–(10.7)*

^thm-a6-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. The same current passes through every battery ([[§A5.1 Electric Current#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]). By [[§A6.1 Electromotive Force#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]] each contributes $\mathcal E_i - Ir_i$ to the potential difference, and in series these add.
> 2. All batteries have the same terminal voltage $V$, so the current from battery $i$ is $I_i = (\mathcal E - V)/r_i$ (Theorem §A6.1.3 again). By the junction rule $I = \sum_i I_i = (\mathcal E - V)\sum_i 1/r_i = (\mathcal E - V)/r_{\text{eq}}$, which rearranges to $V = \mathcal E - Ir_{\text{eq}}$.

^der-a6-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A6.1 Electromotive Force#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]], [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]], [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why connect sources in series or in parallel
> Cells go in series to raise the voltage (two $1.5\ \mathrm V$ cells for a $3.0\ \mathrm V$ flashlight), at the price of adding their internal resistances. Equal cells go in parallel to deliver more current at the same voltage, since the equivalent internal resistance is smaller: some diesel trucks use two $12\ \mathrm V$ batteries in parallel to start the engine. Solar cells, about $0.5\ \mathrm V$ each and roughly $100\ \mathrm{mA/cm^2}$ in bright sun, are wired in series and parallel combinations into modules of 36 to 72 cells giving $50$ to $140\ \mathrm W$. Like batteries they give a direct current; household appliances mostly need alternating current ([[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits|§A11.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.3*

^rem-a6-3-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.3.1: A Two-Loop Circuit
> In the circuit below, a source $V_1 = 24\ \mathrm V$ and $R_1 = 3.0\ \Omega$ are in the left branch, $R_2 = 3.0\ \Omega$ in the middle branch, and $R_3 = 7.0\ \Omega$ and a source $V_2 = 29\ \mathrm V$ in the right branch. Assign $I_1$ (left branch, toward junction $b$), $I_2$ (middle, from $b$ down to $e$) and $I_3$ (right, from $b$ toward $c$).
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \text{junction } b:&\quad I_1 = I_2 + I_3,\\
> \text{loop } abefa:&\quad -I_1R_1 - I_2R_2 + V_1 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 3I_1 + 3I_2 = 24,\\
> \text{loop } ebcde:&\quad +I_2R_2 - I_3R_3 - V_2 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 3I_2 - 7I_3 = 29,
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> with currents in amperes. (In loop $ebcde$, $R_2$ is crossed against $I_2$ and $V_2$ from $+$ to $-$.) Eliminating $I_2 = I_1 - I_3$ gives $6I_1 - 3I_3 = 24$ and $3I_1 - 10I_3 = 29$, so
>
> $$
> I_1 = 3.00\ \mathrm A, \qquad I_3 = -2.00\ \mathrm A, \qquad I_2 = I_1 - I_3 = 5.00\ \mathrm A .
> $$
>
> $I_3 < 0$: $2.00\ \mathrm A$ actually flows up the right branch, out of the positive terminal of $V_2$. Both sources therefore supply power, $I_1V_1 = 72\ \mathrm W$ and $|I_3|V_2 = 58\ \mathrm W$, a total of $130\ \mathrm W$; the resistors dissipate $I_1^2R_1 + I_2^2R_2 + I_3^2R_3 = 27 + 75 + 28 = 130\ \mathrm W$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.3, Figs. 10.26–10.28 (the right branch's two series resistors combined into $R_3$)*

> [!example] Example §A6.3.2: A Battery Being Charged
> A single loop contains a $24\ \mathrm V$ source $V_2$, a $12\ \mathrm V$ battery $V_1$ connected with the opposite polarity, and resistors $R_1 = 10.0\ \Omega$, $R_2 = 30.0\ \Omega$, $R_3 = 20.0\ \Omega$. Take the current $I$ clockwise, the direction $V_2$ drives it, so the loop crosses $V_2$ from $-$ to $+$ and $V_1$ from $+$ to $-$:
>
> $$
> -IR_1 - V_1 - IR_2 + V_2 - IR_3 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I = \frac{V_2 - V_1}{R_1 + R_2 + R_3} = \frac{12\ \mathrm V}{60.0\ \Omega} = 0.200\ \mathrm A .
> $$
>
> $V_2$ supplies $IV_2 = 4.80\ \mathrm W$. The resistors dissipate $(0.200\ \mathrm A)^2(60.0\ \Omega) = 2.40\ \mathrm W$, and the remaining $IV_1 = 2.40\ \mathrm W$ goes *into* $V_1$, through which the current flows from $+$ to $-$: it is being charged. A source of emf need not deliver power; it absorbs power whenever the current is forced through it backwards.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 10.7 (resistances as implied by its power values)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The loop rule is the vanishing circulation $\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$ of a [[§11 The Three Differential Operators꞉ Gradient, Curl, Divergence#^def-11-1|conservative field (Def. §11.1)]]; by [[Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³]] it is equivalent to $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ on a simply connected region, which fails exactly when magnetic flux changes ([[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields|§A9.4]]).
> - Kirchhoff's rules give a square linear system for the branch currents. For a square system, a unique solution for every choice of emfs exists exactly when the homogeneous system (all emfs zero) has only the zero solution: injectivity and surjectivity coincide ([[§10 Invertibility and Isomorphisms#^ladr-3-65|LADR 3.65]]). Physically, with no sources and positive resistances no current can flow, since all the dissipated power $\sum I^2R$ would have to come from nowhere.
