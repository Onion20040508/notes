So far all currents were steady. A capacitor in a circuit changes that: as it charges, its voltage $q/C$ grows and opposes the source, so the current dies away; when it discharges through a resistor, the current falls as the charge runs out. The loop rule turns each process into a first-order differential equation whose solution is an exponential with a single time scale, the time constant $RC$.

## The circuit and its time constant

> [!definition] Definition §A6.5.1: RC Circuit; Time Constant
> An **RC circuit** contains a resistance $R$ and a capacitance $C$ (and possibly a DC source). Its **time constant** is
>
> $$
> \tau = RC .
> $$
>
> It is a time: $\Omega\cdot\mathrm F = (\mathrm V/\mathrm A)(\mathrm C/\mathrm V) = \mathrm C/\mathrm A = \mathrm s$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.5, eq. (10.9)*

## Charging

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.5.1: Charging a Capacitor
> An initially uncharged capacitor $C$ connected at $t = 0$ in series with a resistor $R$ to a source of emf $\mathcal E$ (switch at A) has charge
>
> $$
> q(t) = C\mathcal E\bigl(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\bigr) = Q\bigl(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\bigr), \qquad Q = C\mathcal E ,
> $$
>
> and the current is
>
> $$
> I(t) = \frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{\mathcal E}{R}\,e^{-t/\tau} = I_0\,e^{-t/\tau} .
> $$
>
> The voltages across the capacitor and the resistor are $V_C = \mathcal E(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ and $V_R = \mathcal E e^{-t/\tau}$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.5, eqs. (10.8), (10.10)*

^thm-a6-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Go around the loop in the direction of the current. The source raises the potential by $\mathcal E$, the resistor lowers it by $IR$ and the capacitor by $q/C$ ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]); by the loop rule
>
> $$
> \mathcal E - IR - \frac{q}{C} = 0, \qquad I = \frac{dq}{dt} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{C\mathcal E - q}{RC} .
> $$
>
> Separate the variables and integrate from $q(0) = 0$:
>
> $$
> \int_0^{q} \frac{dq'}{C\mathcal E - q'} = \int_0^t \frac{dt'}{RC} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -\ln\frac{C\mathcal E - q}{C\mathcal E} = \frac{t}{RC} ,
> $$
>
> so $C\mathcal E - q = C\mathcal E\,e^{-t/RC}$, which is the formula for $q(t)$. Differentiating gives $I(t)$; then $V_C = q/C$ and $V_R = IR$.

^der-a6-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-2|Theorem §A6.3.2]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^def-a5-1-1|Def. §A5.1.1]], [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-5|Law §A5.3.5]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]] (chain rule, for the antiderivative of $1/(C\mathcal E - q)$)

![[ph-em-a6-5-2.svg]]
*Charging an RC circuit, with time in units of $\tau = RC$. The charge (blue) rises to $Q = C\mathcal E$ and the current (red) decays from $I_0 = \mathcal E/R$. After one time constant $q = 0.632\,Q$ and $I = 0.368\,I_0$ (dots). The initial tangent of $q$ (grey, dashed) would reach $Q$ at $t = \tau$. > [!remark] Remark: Reading the solution
> - At $t = 0$ the uncharged capacitor has no voltage across it, so it behaves like a plain wire: the whole emf is across $R$ and $I_0 = \mathcal E/R$. The capacitor's charge, hence its voltage, cannot jump, because the current is finite.
> - As $t \to \infty$ the capacitor is full, $V_C \to \mathcal E$, and it behaves like a break in the circuit: $I \to 0$.
> - At $t = \tau$ the capacitor has $1 - e^{-1} = 63.2\%$ of its final charge and the current is down to $e^{-1} = 36.8\%$; after $5\tau$ both are within $1\%$ of their final values. A larger $R$ (less current) or a larger $C$ (more charge to deliver) means slower charging.
> - At every instant $V_R + V_C = \mathcal E$: the source voltage is handed over from the resistor to the capacitor.
> - An inductor in place of the capacitor gives the same exponentials with time constant $L/R$ ([[§A10.4 RL Circuits#^def-a10-4-1|Def. §A10.4.1]]); there it is the current, not the charge, that cannot jump.

## Discharging

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.5.2: Discharging a Capacitor
> A capacitor with initial charge $Q$, connected at $t = 0$ across a resistor $R$ (switch at B), has
>
> $$
> q(t) = Q\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad I(t) = \frac{dq}{dt} = -\frac{Q}{RC}\,e^{-t/\tau} ,
> $$
>
> and the capacitor's voltage decays as $V_C(t) = (Q/C)\,e^{-t/\tau}$. The minus sign means the current flows opposite to the charging current.
>
> *Source: UP2 §10.5, eqs. (10.11)–(10.12)*

^thm-a6-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With the source removed, the loop rule gives $-IR - q/C = 0$ with $I = dq/dt$ (measured in the charging direction), that is,
>
> $$
> \frac{dq}{dt} = -\frac{q}{RC} .
> $$
>
> The rate of loss of charge is proportional to the charge itself; in the course's words, "$Q$ is proportional to $dQ/dt$, with proportionality constant $RC$". Separating, $\int_Q^q dq'/q' = -\int_0^t dt'/RC$, so $\ln(q/Q) = -t/RC$ and $q = Qe^{-t/RC}$. Check: $RC\,dq/dt = RC\cdot(-1/RC)\,Qe^{-t/RC} = -q$. Differentiating gives $I(t)$, and $V_C = q/C$.

^der-a6-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-2|Theorem §A6.3.2]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-5|Law §A5.3.5]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Where the stored energy goes
> While discharging, $q$, $V_C$ and $I$ are all proportional to one another, so all three decay with the same factor $e^{-t/\tau}$. The power in the resistor is not:
>
> $$
> P(t) = I^2R = \frac{Q^2}{RC^2}\,e^{-2t/\tau} ,
> $$
>
> which falls twice as fast, with time constant $\tau/2$. The total energy dissipated is
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty P\,dt = \frac{Q^2}{RC^2}\cdot\frac{\tau}{2} = \frac{Q^2}{2C} ,
> $$
>
> exactly the energy stored in the capacitor at the start ([[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]), whatever the value of $R$. A large $R$ only makes the release slower.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 16*

^rem-a6-5-2

> [!remark] Remark: Building the exponential step by step
> The course first met the discharge numerically. Hold the current fixed at its value at the start of a short step $\Delta t$: the charge lost in the step is $I\Delta t = (q/RC)\,\Delta t$, so each step multiplies the charge by $1 - \Delta t/RC$. After $n$ steps of total duration $t = n\Delta t$,
>
> $$
> q = Q\Bigl(1 - \frac{t}{nRC}\Bigr)^{n} \;\longrightarrow\; Q\,e^{-t/RC} \qquad (n \to \infty).
> $$
>
> With $RC = 1.7\ \mathrm s$ and steps of $1\ \mathrm s$, the procedure gives $q/Q = 0.41,\ 0.17,\ 0.07$ after $1$, $2$, $3\ \mathrm s$, against the exact $0.56,\ 0.31,\ 0.17$: it drains the capacitor too fast, because in each step it uses the current from the start of the step, the largest one. Steps of $0.25\ \mathrm s$ already come much closer. This is Euler's method for the differential equation of Theorem §A6.5.2.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 15–16*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.5.1: Discharging a Pair of Spherical Shells
> A capacitor made of two concentric spherical shells (radii $1\times 10^6\ \mathrm m$ and $3\times 10^6\ \mathrm m$; deliberately silly numbers) has $C = 170\ \mu\mathrm F$ and carries $Q = 1\ \mathrm C$. At $t = 0$ the shells are connected through $R = 10\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$. Then
>
> $$
> \tau = RC = (1.0\times 10^4\ \Omega)(1.70\times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm F) = 1.7\ \mathrm s, \qquad V_0 = \frac{Q}{C} = 5.9\ \mathrm{kV}, \qquad I_0 = \frac{V_0}{R} = 0.59\ \mathrm A .
> $$
>
> The charge falls to a tenth when $e^{-t/\tau} = 0.1$, at $t = \tau\ln 10 = 3.9\ \mathrm s$. The resistor dissipates $P_0 = I_0V_0 = 3.5\ \mathrm{kW}$ at first, and in all $Q^2/2C = 2.9\ \mathrm{kJ}$. Doubling $R$ or $C$ doubles $\tau$ and stretches every curve along the time axis by a factor of 2.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 15–16*

^ex-a6-5-1

> [!example] Example §A6.5.2: A Relaxation Oscillator
> A source of $\mathcal E = 100\ \mathrm V$ charges a capacitor $C = 50\ \mathrm{mF}$ through a total resistance $R + r = 101\ \Omega$ (resistor plus internal resistance). A neon lamp across the capacitor acts as an open circuit until its voltage reaches $80\ \mathrm V$; then it conducts, the capacitor discharges through it in a flash (assume instantly and completely), and the charging starts again. The time constant is $\tau = (101\ \Omega)(50\times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm F) = 5.05\ \mathrm s$. From $V_C = \mathcal E(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$,
>
> $$
> t = -\tau \ln\Bigl(1 - \frac{V_C}{\mathcal E}\Bigr) = -(5.05\ \mathrm s)\ln(1 - 0.80) = 8.13\ \mathrm s .
> $$
>
> The lamp flashes every $8.13\ \mathrm s$, a frequency of $0.123\ \mathrm{Hz}$, set by $R$ and $C$. With a transistor in place of the lamp, the same circuit times blinking turn signals, and with an adjustable resistor, intermittent windshield wipers (a $10.00\ \mathrm{mF}$ capacitor, a $12.00\ \mathrm V$ source and a $10.00\ \mathrm V$ switching threshold give a $10.00\ \mathrm s$ period for $R = 558\ \Omega$).
>
> *Source: UP2 Examples 10.8–10.9*

^ex-a6-5-2

> [!remark] Remark: Applications
> A camera flash charges slowly through the battery's internal resistance and discharges in a fraction of a second through the small resistance of the flash tube; as the battery ages, its growing internal resistance makes charging slower still. RC circuits time pacemaker pulses and the popular 555 timer chip, and they filter unwanted frequencies out of signals and smooth the output of power supplies ([[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits|§A11.1]]).

^rem-a6-5-4

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Both differential equations are solved by separating variables, using the [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|Fundamental Theorem of Calculus]] and the chain rule ([[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]). That the solution is the only one with the given initial charge follows from [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]: $\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(q\,e^{t/\tau}\bigr) = 0$ for any solution of $dq/dt = -q/\tau$.
> - Charging is the same equation as a body falling with linear drag ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^thm-a3-10-4|CM Theorem §A3.10.4]]): $q \leftrightarrow v$, $C\mathcal E \leftrightarrow v_T$, $RC \leftrightarrow m/b$. Replacing the capacitor by an inductor gives the RL circuit of [[§A10.4 RL Circuits#^thm-a10-4-1|Theorem §A10.4.1]], with time constant $L/R$ ([[§A10.4 RL Circuits#^def-a10-4-1|Def. §A10.4.1]]).
