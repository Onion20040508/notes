A potential can be drawn as a contour map, just as a field is drawn with field lines. Because the field is minus the gradient of the potential ([[§A3.4 Field from Potential|§A3.4]]), the two pictures are tied together: field lines cross the contours at right angles. Applied to conductors, this explains where charge collects on them and leads to capacitors in Chapter A4.

## Equipotential surfaces

> [!definition] Definition §A3.5.1: Equipotential Surface
> An **equipotential surface** is a surface on which the potential has one constant value; in a two-dimensional drawing it appears as an **equipotential line**. The word *equipotential* is also used as a noun for either.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.5*

^def-a3-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.1: Field Lines Cross Equipotentials at Right Angles
> At every point the electric field is perpendicular to the equipotential surface through that point and points toward lower potential. No work is done on a charge moved along an equipotential. If neighbouring equipotentials differ by a fixed step $\Delta V$, their local spacing $\Delta s$ satisfies $E \approx \Delta V/\Delta s$: crowded equipotentials mean a strong field.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.5*

^thm-a3-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For a small displacement $d\vec l$ within an equipotential surface, $dV = 0$, so $-\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$ by [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]: $\vec E$ is perpendicular to every direction in the surface. The work on a charge $q$ is $W = -q\,\Delta V = 0$. Across the surfaces, $E = -dV/ds$ along the normal ([[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]), which is positive in the direction of decreasing $V$ and approximately $\Delta V/\Delta s$ for a small step.

^der-a3-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]

![[ph-em-a3-5-1.svg]]
*Computed field lines (red, with arrows) and equipotentials (blue) in a plane through two charges; neighbouring equipotentials differ by equal steps of $V$. (a) $+q$ and $-q$: lines run from $+$ to $-$, and the plane midway is the equipotential $V = 0$ (dashed). (b) Two charges $+q$: all lines go off to infinity; at the black dot between the charges $\vec E = \vec 0$, and the figure-eight equipotential crosses itself there. In both panels the lines meet the equipotentials at right angles and both crowd together where the field is strong.*

> [!remark] Remark: Drawing one picture from the other
> Given the field lines, sketch curves that cross every line at right angles; given the equipotentials, draw lines perpendicular to them, pointing from high to low $V$. Equipotentials of different values never meet, since $V$ has one value at each point; two parts of the same equipotential can touch only where $\vec E = \vec 0$, as in panel (b). The map is like a topographic map: the potential is the height, the field the downhill slope.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 6 slides; TBL 9*

^rem-a3-5-1

## Conductors

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.2: A Conductor in Equilibrium Is an Equipotential
> In electrostatic equilibrium, the whole of a conductor, its interior and its surface, is at a single potential. Its surface is an equipotential surface, and the field just outside is perpendicular to it.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.5*

^thm-a3-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In equilibrium $\vec E = \vec 0$ inside the material of a conductor ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium|§A2.4]]). Any two points of the conductor are joined by a path inside it, along which $-\int\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$, so they have the same potential. The perpendicularity of the outside field then follows from [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]], in agreement with §A2.4: a tangential component would push the surface charges along the surface.

^der-a3-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium|E = 0 in a conductor (§A2.4)]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A3.5.2: Grounding
> **Grounding** a conductor means connecting it to the Earth by a good conductor. The two then form one conductor and share one potential, which is taken as zero. Grounding the metal case of an appliance keeps it at 0 V relative to the Earth.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.5*

^def-a3-5-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.3: Potential of a Charged Conducting Sphere
> An isolated conducting sphere of radius $R$ carrying charge $q$ has, with $V = 0$ at infinity,
>
> $$
> V(r) = \frac{kq}{r} \quad (r \ge R), \qquad V(r) = \frac{kq}{R} \quad (r \le R).
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.5*

^thm-a3-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By symmetry and Gauss's law ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law|§A2.3]]), $\vec E = (kq/r^2)\,\hat r$ outside and $\vec E = \vec 0$ inside. Outside, the field and hence the potential (integrated in from infinity, [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]]) are those of a point charge $q$ at the centre. Inside, $\vec E = \vec 0$, so $V$ keeps its surface value $kq/R$.

^der-a3-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law|Gauss's law, spherical symmetry (§A2.3)]], [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.4: Charge Density and Curvature
> Two conducting spheres of radii $R_1$ and $R_2$, far apart and joined by a thin wire, share their charge so that
>
> $$
> \frac{q_1}{R_1} = \frac{q_2}{R_2}, \qquad \sigma_1 R_1 = \sigma_2 R_2 .
> $$
>
> The smaller sphere has the larger surface charge density and the larger surface field.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.5*

^thm-a3-5-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Far apart, each sphere is effectively isolated, so by [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]] its potential is $kq_i/R_i$. Joined by the wire they form one conductor, at one potential ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]): $kq_1/R_1 = kq_2/R_2$. With $q_i = 4\pi R_i^2\sigma_i$ this becomes $\sigma_1 R_1 = \sigma_2 R_2$. The field just outside each surface is $\sigma_i/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium|§A2.4]]), so it also scales as $1/R_i$.

^der-a3-5-4

*Uses:* [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]], [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]], [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium|field at a conductor's surface (§A2.4)]]

> [!remark] Remark: Sharp points and lightning rods
> Two joined spheres are not one conductor of varying curvature, but the result shows the trend: on any charged conductor, $\sigma$ and the surface field are largest where the surface is most sharply curved (small radius of curvature) and smallest where it is flat. Near a sharp point the field can exceed the dielectric strength of air, about $3.0\times10^6$ N/C, and ionise it. A lightning rod is a grounded rod with a sharp tip: under a charged cloud the field at the tip ionises the air and lets charge leak away gradually, which keeps the field between cloud and ground below the value that would produce a strike nearby.

^rem-a3-5-2

> [!remark] Remark: A conductor can replace an equipotential
> Since a conductor's surface is an equipotential, the region inside any equipotential surface can be replaced by a conductor of that shape carrying the enclosed charge, and the field outside does not change. A charged conducting sphere thus reproduces the outside field of a point charge at its centre. (That the outside field is then *uniquely* determined is a theorem of the upper level, Chapter B, planned.)

^rem-a3-5-3

> [!remark] Remark: Applications of electrostatics
> - A **Van de Graaff generator** carries charge on an insulating belt into a large metal sphere; the charge moves to the outer surface, so more can be added, up to potentials of order 15 MV, limited by ionisation of the surroundings.
> - **Xerography** (photocopiers) and **laser printers** charge a photoconducting selenium drum, discharge it where light falls, let negatively charged toner stick to the remaining positive image and transfer it to more strongly charged paper. **Ink-jet printers** steer charged droplets between charged plates.
> - **Electrostatic painting** charges paint droplets so that they fly to the object, arriving perpendicular to it if it is a conductor. **Electrostatic precipitators** charge smoke and dust particles and collect them on an oppositely charged grid, removing over 99% of the particles from power-plant stacks.

^rem-a3-5-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.5.1: Equipotentials of a Point Charge
> Where are the equipotentials $V = 100$, 50, 20 and 10 V around a charge $q = +10$ nC? Setting $V = kq/r$ equal to a constant gives a sphere of radius $r = kq/V$, with $kq = (8.99\times10^9)(10\times10^{-9}) = 89.9\ \mathrm{V\,m}$:
>
> $$
> r = 0.90\ \mathrm m,\quad 1.8\ \mathrm m,\quad 4.5\ \mathrm m,\quad 9.0\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> Halving $V$ doubles $r$, so equipotentials in equal steps of $V$ spread out with distance: the field weakens.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 7.19*

^ex-a3-5-1

> [!example] Example §A3.5.2: Equipotentials Between Charged Plates
> Two large conducting plates carry surface charge densities $\pm6.81\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{C/m^2}$ and are $l = 6.50$ mm apart. Treat them as infinite planes ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law|§A2.3]]): between them the field is uniform, from $+$ to $-$, with
>
> $$
> E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{6.81\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{C/m^2}}{8.85\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}} = 7.69\times10^4\ \mathrm{V/m}.
> $$
>
> Integrating from the negative to the positive plate, against the field, $\Delta V = El = (7.69\times10^4\ \mathrm{V/m})(6.50\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm m) = 500$ V. The equipotentials are planes parallel to the plates; since $E$ is constant, those 100 V apart are evenly spaced, $l/5 = 1.3$ mm apart.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 7.20*

^ex-a3-5-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Equipotentials are level sets of $V$, and the gradient is perpendicular to level sets ([[§7 Directional Derivatives#^rem-7-3|§7]], Multivariable Analysis; see its contour figure): this is Theorem §A3.5.1 in mathematical form.
