A potential can be drawn as a contour map, just as a field is drawn with field lines. Because the field is minus the gradient of the potential ([[§A3.4 Field from Potential|§A3.4]]), the two pictures are tied together: field lines cross the contours at right angles. Applied to conductors, this explains where charge collects on them and leads to capacitors in Chapter A4.

## Equipotential surfaces

> [!definition] Definition §A3.5.1: Equipotential Surface
> An **equipotential surface** is a surface on which the potential has one constant value; in a two-dimensional drawing it appears as an **equipotential line**. The word *equipotential* is also used as a noun for either.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.5*

^def-a3-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.1: Field Lines Cross Equipotentials at Right Angles
> At every point the electric field is perpendicular to the equipotential surface through that point and points toward lower potential. No work is done on a charge moved along an equipotential. If neighbouring equipotentials differ by a fixed step $\Delta V$, their local spacing $\Delta s$ satisfies $E \approx \Delta V/\Delta s$: crowded equipotentials mean a strong field.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.5*

^thm-a3-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For a small displacement $d\vec l$ within an equipotential surface, $dV = 0$, so $-\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$ by [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]: $\vec E$ is perpendicular to every direction in the surface. The work on a charge $q$ is $W = -q\,\Delta V = 0$. Across the surfaces, $E = -dV/ds$ along the normal ([[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]), which is positive in the direction of decreasing $V$ and approximately $\Delta V/\Delta s$ for a small step.

^der-a3-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]

![[ph-em-a3-5-1.svg]]
*Computed field lines (red, with arrows) and equipotentials (blue) in a plane through two charges; neighbouring equipotentials differ by equal steps of $V$. (a) $+q$ and $-q$: lines run from $+$ to $-$, and the plane midway is the equipotential $V = 0$ (dashed). (b) Two charges $+q$: all lines go off to infinity; at the black dot between the charges $\vec E = \vec 0$, and the figure-eight equipotential crosses itself there. In both panels the lines meet the equipotentials at right angles and both crowd together where the field is strong.*

> [!remark] Remark: Drawing one picture from the other
> Given the field lines, sketch curves that cross every line at right angles; given the equipotentials, draw lines perpendicular to them, pointing from high to low $V$. Equipotentials of different values never meet, since $V$ has one value at each point; two parts of the same equipotential can touch only where $\vec E = \vec 0$, as in panel (b). The map is like a topographic map: the potential is the height, the field the downhill slope.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 6 slides; TBL 9*

^rem-a3-5-1

## Conductors

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.2: A Conductor in Equilibrium Is an Equipotential
> In electrostatic equilibrium, the whole of a conductor, its interior and its surface, is at a single potential. Its surface is an equipotential surface, and the field just outside is perpendicular to it.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.5*

^thm-a3-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In equilibrium $\vec E = \vec 0$ inside the material of a conductor ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]]). Any two points of the conductor are joined by a path inside it, along which $-\int\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$, so they have the same potential. The perpendicularity of the outside field then follows from [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]], in agreement with [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-4|Theorem §A2.4.4]]: a tangential component would push the surface charges along the surface.

^der-a3-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Grounding fixes the zero of potential
> **Grounding** a conductor connects it to the Earth by a good conductor ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^def-a1-1-4|Def. §A1.1.4]]). The two then form one conductor and so share one potential ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]), which is conventionally taken as zero. Grounding the metal case of an appliance keeps it at 0 V relative to the Earth.

^rem-a3-5-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.3: Potential of a Charged Conducting Sphere
> An isolated conducting sphere of radius $R$ carrying charge $q$ has, with $V = 0$ at infinity,
>
> $$
> V(r) = \frac{kq}{r} \quad (r \ge R), \qquad V(r) = \frac{kq}{R} \quad (r \le R).
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.5*

^thm-a3-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The charge lies on the surface ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]]), spread uniformly by symmetry. By Gauss's law for a spherical distribution ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]), $\vec E = (kq/r^2)\,\hat r$ outside and $\vec E = \vec 0$ inside, as in [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^ex-a2-4-1|Example §A2.4.1]]. Outside, the field and hence the potential (integrated in from infinity, [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]]) are those of a point charge $q$ at the centre. Inside, $\vec E = \vec 0$, so $V$ keeps its surface value $kq/R$.

^der-a3-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]], [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]], [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.4: Charge Density and Curvature
> Two conducting spheres of radii $R_1$ and $R_2$, far apart and joined by a thin wire, share their charge so that
>
> $$
> \frac{q_1}{R_1} = \frac{q_2}{R_2}, \qquad \sigma_1 R_1 = \sigma_2 R_2 .
> $$
>
> The smaller sphere has the larger surface charge density and the larger surface field.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.5*

^thm-a3-5-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Far apart, each sphere is effectively isolated, so by [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]] its potential is $kq_i/R_i$. Joined by the wire they form one conductor, at one potential ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]): $kq_1/R_1 = kq_2/R_2$. With $q_i = 4\pi R_i^2\sigma_i$ this becomes $\sigma_1 R_1 = \sigma_2 R_2$. The field just outside each surface is $\sigma_i/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-4|Theorem §A2.4.4]]), so it also scales as $1/R_i$.

^der-a3-5-4

*Uses:* [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]], [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]], [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-4|Theorem §A2.4.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Sharp points and lightning rods
> Two joined spheres are not one conductor of varying curvature, but the result shows the trend: on any charged conductor, $\sigma$ and the surface field are largest where the surface is most sharply curved (small radius of curvature) and smallest where it is flat. Near a sharp point the field can exceed the dielectric strength of air ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^def-a4-4-2|Def. §A4.4.2]]), about $3.0\times10^6$ N/C, and ionise it. A lightning rod is a grounded rod with a sharp tip: under a charged cloud the field at the tip ionises the air and lets charge leak away gradually, which keeps the field between cloud and ground below the value that would produce a strike nearby.

^rem-a3-5-3

> [!remark] Remark: A conductor can replace an equipotential
> Since a conductor's surface is an equipotential, the region inside any equipotential surface can be replaced by a conductor of that shape carrying the enclosed charge, and the field outside does not change. A charged conducting sphere thus reproduces the outside field of a point charge at its centre. (That the outside field is then *uniquely* determined is a theorem of the upper level: [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]; the replacement argument itself — [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^rem-b3-1-4|Remark: Uniqueness is a licence to guess]].)

^rem-a3-5-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.5: Empty Cavity in a Conductor
> Let a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium contain a cavity with no charge in it. Then the field is zero everywhere in the cavity, and the cavity wall carries no charge at any point, whatever charges lie outside the conductor. The whole cavity is at the conductor's potential. A closed metal shell therefore shields its empty interior from outside fields.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.4 (Figure 6.44(b))*

^thm-a3-5-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The cavity wall belongs to the conductor, so it is an equipotential at the conductor's potential $V_0$ ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]). Suppose $\vec E \ne \vec 0$ at some point of the cavity, and follow the field line through it in both directions. Along a field line $V$ strictly decreases ([[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]: $dV/ds = -E < 0$ in the direction of $\vec E$). So the line cannot close on itself, since it would return to its starting point at a lower potential. It cannot start or end inside the empty cavity either, since field lines start and end only on charges (a small closed surface around such an end point would have nonzero flux but enclose no charge, against [[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]). Hence it runs from one point of the wall to another, and its end is at a lower potential than its start, which contradicts $V = V_0$ on the whole wall. So $\vec E = \vec 0$ throughout the cavity, and $V = V_0$ there ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]). The field just off the wall is then zero, so the wall's surface density $\sigma = \varepsilon_0 E_n$ vanishes everywhere ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-4|Theorem §A2.4.4]]). The argument takes for granted that a field line in the bounded cavity must reach the wall. The rigorous version is a uniqueness theorem for Laplace's equation (level B, Chapter B2, planned).

^der-a3-5-5

*Uses:* [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]], [[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]], [[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-4|Theorem §A2.4.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Applications of electrostatics
> - A **Van de Graaff generator** carries charge on an insulating belt into a large metal sphere; the charge moves to the outer surface, so more can be added, up to potentials of order 15 MV, limited by ionisation of the surroundings.
> - **Xerography** (photocopiers) and **laser printers** charge a photoconducting selenium drum, discharge it where light falls, let negatively charged toner stick to the remaining positive image and transfer it to more strongly charged paper. **Ink-jet printers** steer charged droplets between charged plates.
> - **Electrostatic painting** charges paint droplets so that they fly to the object, arriving perpendicular to it if it is a conductor. **Electrostatic precipitators** charge smoke and dust particles and collect them on an oppositely charged grid, removing over 99% of the particles from power-plant stacks.

^rem-a3-5-5

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.5.1: Equipotentials of a Point Charge
> Where are the equipotentials $V = 100$, 50, 20 and 10 V around a charge $q = +10$ nC? Setting $V = kq/r$ equal to a constant gives a sphere of radius $r = kq/V$, with $kq = (8.99\times10^9)(10\times10^{-9}) = 89.9\ \mathrm{V\,m}$:
>
> $$
> r = 0.90\ \mathrm m,\quad 1.8\ \mathrm m,\quad 4.5\ \mathrm m,\quad 9.0\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> Halving $V$ doubles $r$, so equipotentials in equal steps of $V$ spread out with distance: the field weakens.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 7.19*

^ex-a3-5-1

> [!example] Example §A3.5.2: Equipotentials Between Charged Plates
> Take the two large conducting plates of [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^ex-a2-4-2|Example §A2.4.2]], with surface charge densities $\pm6.81\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{C/m^2}$ on their facing sides, $l = 6.50$ mm apart. There the field between them was found to be uniform, from $+$ to $-$, with $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0 = 7.69\times10^4\ \mathrm{V/m}$. Going from the negative to the positive plate, against the uniform field ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]]), $\Delta V = El = (7.69\times10^4\ \mathrm{V/m})(6.50\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm m) = 500$ V. The equipotentials are planes parallel to the plates; since $E$ is constant, those 100 V apart are evenly spaced, $l/5 = 1.3$ mm apart.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 7.20*

^ex-a3-5-2
