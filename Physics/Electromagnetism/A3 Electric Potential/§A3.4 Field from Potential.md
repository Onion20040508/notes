The potential is obtained from the field by integration ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference|§A3.2]]); this section goes the other way, from the potential to the field by differentiation. Since potentials of charge distributions are easy to add up ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions|§A3.3]]), "find $V$, then differentiate" is often the quickest route to $\vec E$.

## The field is minus the gradient of the potential

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.4.1: Field as the Negative Gradient of the Potential
> The component of $\vec E$ along any direction $s$ is minus the rate of change of $V$ in that direction,
>
> $$
> E_s = -\frac{dV}{ds} .
> $$
>
> In Cartesian components $E_x = -\partial V/\partial x$, $E_y = -\partial V/\partial y$, $E_z = -\partial V/\partial z$, that is
>
> $$
> \vec E = -\vec\nabla V, \qquad \vec\nabla = \hat\imath\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \hat\jmath\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \hat k\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.4, eqs. (7.13)–(7.15)*

^thm-a3-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For a short displacement $d\vec l$ of length $ds$, [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]] gives $dV = -\vec E\cdot d\vec l = -E_s\,ds$, where $E_s$ is the component of $\vec E$ along $d\vec l$. Divide by $ds$. Choosing $d\vec l$ along $\hat\imath$, $\hat\jmath$, $\hat k$ in turn, with the other coordinates fixed, gives the three partial derivatives. This is the mechanics result $\vec F = -\vec\nabla U$ ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|CM Theorem §A4.5.2]]) divided by the test charge.

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|CM Theorem §A4.5.2]], [[§4 Partial Derivatives#^def-4-1|452 Def. §4.1]] (partial derivatives), [[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^def-17-2|452 Def. §17.2]] (gradient)

> [!remark] Remark: Reading a potential map
> Think of $V(x, y)$ as the height of a landscape. The gradient $\vec\nabla V$ points straight uphill, along the steepest ascent, and its length is the slope in that direction. The field $\vec E = -\vec\nabla V$ points straight downhill, the way a positive charge would roll, and it is strong where the contour lines of $V$ are packed close together. In a uniform field the slope is constant, $E = -\Delta V/\Delta s$ ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 7 slides; TBL 11*

^rem-a3-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Gradient in cylindrical and spherical coordinates
> For problems with cylindrical or spherical symmetry, use the gradient in the matching coordinates:
>
> $$
> \vec\nabla = \hat r\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r} + \hat\varphi\,\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial \varphi} + \hat z\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z} \quad\text{(cylindrical)}, \qquad
> \vec\nabla = \hat r\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r} + \hat\theta\,\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} + \hat\varphi\,\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial \varphi} \quad\text{(spherical)}.
> $$
>
> The factors $1/r$ and $1/(r\sin\theta)$ convert an angle change into a distance. For a point charge, $V = kq/r$ depends on $r$ only, so $\vec E = -\hat r\,\dfrac{d}{dr}\dfrac{kq}{r} = \dfrac{kq}{r^2}\,\hat r$, Coulomb's field again, pointing toward decreasing $V$.

^rem-a3-4-2

> [!caution] Caution: V and E at a single point
> The field depends on how $V$ *changes*, not on its value. $V = 0$ does not imply $\vec E = \vec 0$: on the midplane of a dipole $V = 0$ but the field is strong. $\vec E = \vec 0$ does not imply $V = 0$: midway between two equal charges $+q$ at distance $a$ from it, $\vec E = \vec 0$ but $V = 2kq/a$. In a region where $\vec E = \vec 0$, $V$ is constant, not necessarily zero. Conversely $\vec E$ fixes $V$ only up to an additive constant.

^cau-a3-4-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.4.1: Two Equal Charges, Potential First
> Charges $+q$ sit at $(-a, 0)$ and $(a, 0)$. Write $r_1 = \sqrt{(x+a)^2 + y^2}$, $r_2 = \sqrt{(x-a)^2 + y^2}$. Superposition of potentials ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]) gives the scalar
>
> $$
> V(x, y) = kq\left(\frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2}\right).
> $$
>
> Since $\partial(1/r_1)/\partial x = -(x+a)/r_1^3$ and $\partial(1/r_1)/\partial y = -y/r_1^3$ (and likewise for $r_2$), [[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]] gives
>
> $$
> \vec E = kq\left[\frac{x+a}{r_1^3} + \frac{x-a}{r_2^3}\right]\hat\imath + kq\left[\frac{y}{r_1^3} + \frac{y}{r_2^3}\right]\hat\jmath .
> $$
>
> Check: at the origin $\vec E = \vec 0$, and on the $y$-axis $\vec E = 2kqy/(a^2 + y^2)^{3/2}\,\hat\jmath$, the result of adding the two Coulomb fields as vectors ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^ex-a1-3-1|Example §A1.3.1]](a)). Vectors entered only in the last step. The field lines and equipotentials of this pair are drawn with [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]].
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 11*

^ex-a3-4-1

> [!example] Example §A3.4.2: Field on the Axis of a Ring
> On the axis of a ring of radius $R$ and charge $Q$, $V(z) = kQ/\sqrt{z^2 + R^2}$ ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^ex-a3-3-2|Example §A3.3.2]]). By symmetry the field on the axis points along it, so only $E_z$ is needed:
>
> $$
> E_z = -\frac{dV}{dz} = \frac{kQz}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}} .
> $$
>
> This is [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-6|Theorem §A1.4.6]] in one line. $E_z = 0$ at the centre, where $V$ has its maximum, and $|E_z|$ is largest at $z = \pm R/\sqrt2$, where $V$ falls most steeply. Knowing $V$ only on the axis gives only $E_z$ there; that $E_x = E_y = 0$ on the axis comes from symmetry, not from the formula.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 7.18*

^ex-a3-4-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $\vec E = -\vec\nabla V$ says that the electrostatic field is a gradient field ([[§11 The Three Differential Operators꞉ Gradient, Curl, Divergence#^def-11-1|452 Def. §11.1]], Multivariable Analysis) with potential $-V$. Gradient fields are curl-free, $\vec\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$, and by Stokes' theorem ([[Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³|452 Thm. §20.1]]) this matches $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$; the converse on regions without holes is the [[Poincaré Lemma]].
> - The gradient is the direction of steepest ascent and is perpendicular to level sets ([[§7 Directional Derivatives#^rem-7-3|452 §7]]); in curvilinear coordinates its components carry the scale factors of [[§19 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^rem-19-3|452 §19]].
> - In the language of forms, $dV = -\vec E\cdot d\vec l$ is the statement that the 1-form $E_x\,dx + E_y\,dy + E_z\,dz$ is exact, equal to $-dV$ ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-6|452 Prop. §22.6]]).
