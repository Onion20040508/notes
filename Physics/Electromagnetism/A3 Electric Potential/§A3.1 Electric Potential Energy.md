Chapters A1 and A2 described the interaction of charges by forces and fields; this chapter describes the same physics by energy. The work of the electrostatic force depends only on where a charge starts and ends, so the force has a potential energy, as gravity and springs do in mechanics ([[§A4.4 Potential Energy|CM §A4.4]]). This section proves the path independence and computes the potential energy of point charges.

## The electrostatic force is conservative

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.1.1: The Electrostatic Force Is Conservative
> Let a test charge $Q$ move from $P_1$ to $P_2$ in the field of a fixed point charge $q$, at distances $r_1$ and $r_2$ from it. Along every path, the work done on $Q$ by the electric force is
>
> $$
> W_{12} = \int_{P_1}^{P_2} \vec F_e \cdot d\vec l = kqQ\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right).
> $$
>
> The same path independence holds in the field of any fixed (static) distribution of charges. Equivalently, around every closed path
>
> $$
> \oint \vec E \cdot d\vec l = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.1*

^thm-a3-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $q$ at the origin. By Coulomb's law ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|Principle §A1.2.2]]), $\vec F_e = (kqQ/r^2)\,\hat r$. Split any small displacement into a radial part and a part along a sphere about $q$: $d\vec l = dr\,\hat r + (\text{part}\perp\hat r)$. Only the radial part contributes, $\vec F_e \cdot d\vec l = (kqQ/r^2)\,dr$, so
>
> $$
> W_{12} = \int_{r_1}^{r_2} \frac{kqQ}{r^2}\,dr = kqQ\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right),
> $$
>
> which depends on the endpoints only. Pieces of a path that run along a sphere about $q$ contribute nothing, since there the force is perpendicular to the motion; a radial path and a path made of arcs and radial segments give the same work. For several fixed source charges the force on $Q$ is the vector sum of the individual Coulomb forces ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]), so the work is the sum of works, each path independent. The force is therefore conservative ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^def-a4-5-1|CM Def. §A4.5.1]]), and by the closed-path criterion ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-1|CM Theorem §A4.5.1]]) its work around a closed loop vanishes. Dividing by the constant $Q$ ($\vec F_e = Q\vec E$, [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^def-a1-3-1|Def. §A1.3.1]]) gives $\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$.

^der-a3-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Only for static charges
> The argument uses the Coulomb field of charges at rest. A changing magnetic field produces an electric field whose loop integral is *not* zero (the induced field of Faraday's law, [[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^thm-a9-4-2|Theorem §A9.4.2]]); for such fields there is no potential energy. Everything in Chapters A3–A4 concerns electrostatics.

^rem-a3-1-1

## Potential energy of two point charges

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.1: Electric Potential Energy
> The **electric potential energy** $U$ of a system of charges is the potential energy of [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^def-a4-4-1|CM Def. §A4.4.1]], applied to the electrostatic forces between the charges, which are conservative by [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]]. As there, only its changes are defined: when the configuration changes from $A$ to $B$,
>
> $$
> \Delta U = U_B - U_A = -W_{AB} = -\int_A^B \vec F_e \cdot d\vec l ,
> $$
>
> where $W_{AB}$ is the work done by the electric forces. Its unit is the joule.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.1, eq. (7.1)*

^def-a3-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.1.2: Potential Energy of Two Point Charges
> Two point charges $q$ and $Q$ at separation $r$ have electric potential energy
>
> $$
> U(r) = \frac{kqQ}{r} \qquad (U = 0 \text{ at } r = \infty).
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.1, eq. (7.2)*

^thm-a3-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]] and the radial integral of [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]], moving from a reference separation $r_{\text{ref}}$ to $r$ changes the potential energy by
>
> $$
> U(r) - U(r_{\text{ref}}) = -\int_{r_{\text{ref}}}^{r} \frac{kqQ}{r'^2}\,dr' = kqQ\left(\frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{r_{\text{ref}}}\right).
> $$
>
> Infinitely separated charges do not interact, so choose $U = 0$ there and let $r_{\text{ref}} \to \infty$ (an improper integral, which converges because the force falls off as $1/r^2$).

^der-a3-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-1|451 Def. §36.1]] (improper integral)

> [!remark] Remark: Sign, symmetry and gravity
> - $U > 0$ for like charges: an external agent must do positive work to push them together, and the energy is released when they fly apart. $U < 0$ for unlike charges: they pull together, and separating them costs work. In both cases $U$ decreases when the charges move the way the force pushes them.
> - The formula is symmetric in $q$ and $Q$: the energy belongs to the pair, not to either charge ([[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^rem-a4-4-1|CM §A4.4, remark]]).
> - It has the same $1/r$ form as the gravitational energy $-GMm/r$ ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-1|CM Theorem §A8.2.1]]), but gravity only attracts, so its $U$ is never positive.

^rem-a3-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Work by whom?
> Two works appear in these problems and they have opposite signs. The electric field does $W_{\text{field}} = -\Delta U$. An external agent that moves the charge slowly (no change of kinetic energy) does $W_{\text{ext}} = +\Delta U$. It is safest to reason with $\Delta U$ itself: did the system gain or lose potential energy, and how much?
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 6 slides*

^cau-a3-1-1

## Systems of charges

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.1.3: Potential Energy of a System of Point Charges
> The potential energy of point charges $q_1, \dots, q_N$, with $U = 0$ when all are infinitely far apart, is the sum over all pairs,
>
> $$
> U = \sum_{i<j} \frac{k\,q_i q_j}{r_{ij}} = \frac{k}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\substack{j=1\\ j\ne i}}^{N} \frac{q_i q_j}{r_{ij}} .
> $$
>
> It equals the work an external agent must do to assemble the configuration, bringing the charges in slowly from infinity.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.1, eq. (7.3)*

^thm-a3-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Bring the charges in one at a time. The first costs nothing (no other charge is nearby). While the $j$-th charge is brought in, the force on it is the sum of the Coulomb forces of $q_1, \dots, q_{j-1}$, so the work needed is the sum of the pair energies of [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]]: $W_j = \sum_{i<j} kq_iq_j/r_{ij}$. Adding all steps counts every pair exactly once. The order in which the charges are brought in does not matter, because each pair appears once in any order. In the double sum each pair appears twice ($ij$ and $ji$), hence the factor $\tfrac12$.

^der-a3-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]], [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.1.1: Energy Released by Repulsion
> A charge $Q = +3.0$ nC is released from rest $r_1 = 10$ cm from a fixed charge $q = +5.0$ nC and is pushed away. What is its kinetic energy at $r_2 = 15$ cm? Take the pair as the system; only the electric force does work. By [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]],
>
> $$
> \Delta U = kqQ\left(\frac{1}{r_2} - \frac{1}{r_1}\right) = (8.99\times10^9\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2})(5.0\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm C)(3.0\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm C)\left(\frac{1}{0.15\ \mathrm m} - \frac{1}{0.10\ \mathrm m}\right) = -4.5\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm J,
> $$
>
> so $K_2 = -\Delta U = 4.5\times10^{-7}$ J. The same number is the work done by the field on $Q$, computed directly from $\int \vec F_e\cdot d\vec r$. If $Q$ is released and allowed to go to infinity, all of $U(r_1) = 1.35\times10^{-6}$ J becomes kinetic energy.
>
> *Source: UP2 Examples 7.1–7.2*

^ex-a3-1-1

![[ph-em-a3-1-1.svg]]
*Example §A3.1.1: the potential energy $U(r) = kqQ/r$ (blue) falls as the charges separate and the kinetic energy $K(r)$ (red) rises by the same amount; their sum (grey dashed) stays at $U(r_1)$. At $r_2 = 15$ cm, $K_2 = 4.5\times10^{-7}$ J.*

> [!example] Example §A3.1.2: Assembling Four Charges on a Square
> How much work does it take to place $+2.0$, $+3.0$, $+4.0$ and $+5.0\ \mu$C, in that order around a square of side $a = 1.0$ cm, starting each from infinity? By [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-3|Theorem §A3.1.3]] the answer is the sum over the six pairs: four sides of length $a$ and two diagonals of length $\sqrt2\,a$. With charges in $\mu$C,
>
> $$
> W = \frac{k\,(10^{-6}\ \mathrm C)^2}{a}\left[(2\cdot3 + 3\cdot4 + 4\cdot5 + 5\cdot2) + \frac{2\cdot4 + 3\cdot5}{\sqrt2}\right] = (0.90\ \mathrm J)\,(48 + 16.3) = 57.8\ \mathrm J .
> $$
>
> Bringing the charges in one at a time gives the steps $0$, $5.4$, $15.9$ and $36.5$ J, with the same total. The work on each new charge involves only its pair interactions with the charges already in place.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 7.3*
