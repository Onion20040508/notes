The potential energy of a test charge in a given field is proportional to the test charge, just as the force is ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy|§A3.1]]). Dividing by the charge gives the potential, a scalar property of the field alone, in the way that the field is the force per charge ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field|§A1.3]]). This section defines it, relates it to the field by a line integral and uses it with energy conservation.

## Potential

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.1: Electric Potential; Potential Difference; Volt
> The **electric potential** at a point is the electric potential energy per unit charge of a test charge $q$ placed there,
>
> $$
> V = \frac{U}{q} .
> $$
>
> The **potential difference** between points $A$ and $B$ is $\Delta V = V_B - V_A = \Delta U / q$, so a charge $q$ moved from $A$ to $B$ changes its potential energy by
>
> $$
> \Delta U = q\,\Delta V .
> $$
>
> The unit is the **volt**, $1\ \mathrm V = 1\ \mathrm{J/C}$. **Voltage** is the everyday name for a potential difference. Only differences are physical: the point of zero potential is a convention.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.2, eqs. (7.4)–(7.5)*

^def-a3-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Voltage is not energy
> Two 12.0 V batteries, a motorcycle battery that can move 5000 C and a car battery that can move 60 000 C, have the same voltage. The energy each delivers is $q\,\Delta V$: $6.00\times10^4$ J and $7.20\times10^5$ J. Voltage is energy *per charge*; it says how much each coulomb gains or loses, not how many coulombs there are. The physical picture of the lecture: the potential is a landscape, like the height of a hill, and $U = qV$ is the energy of a particular object placed on it, like $mgh$ for a particular sled.

^rem-a3-2-1

## Potential from the field

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.1: Potential Difference as a Line Integral of the Field
> For any two points $A$ and $B$ in an electrostatic field,
>
> $$
> V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B \vec E \cdot d\vec l ,
> $$
>
> and the integral is the same along every path from $A$ to $B$. With a reference point $R$ where $V_R = 0$, the potential at $P$ is $V_P = -\int_R^P \vec E\cdot d\vec l$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.2, eqs. (7.6)–(7.7)*

^thm-a3-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]], $U_B - U_A = -\int_A^B \vec F_e\cdot d\vec l$ for a test charge $q$. Substitute $\vec F_e = q\vec E$ and divide by $q$ ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]]). Path independence is [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]], so one may choose the most convenient path.

^der-a3-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]], [[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^def-16-2|Work line integral (Def. §16.2)]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.2: Uniform Field
> In a uniform field $\vec E$, the potential drops by $Ed$ over a distance $d$ measured along the field. For two points $A$ and $B$ with $B$ a distance $d$ downstream of $A$,
>
> $$
> V_{AB} = V_A - V_B = Ed, \qquad E = \frac{V_{AB}}{d} .
> $$
>
> In particular the units agree: $1\ \mathrm{N/C} = 1\ \mathrm{V/m}$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.2*

^thm-a3-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Integrate along the straight path from $A$ to $B$, parallel to $\vec E$: $\vec E\cdot d\vec l = E\,dl$, so by [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]] $V_B - V_A = -\int_0^d E\,dl = -Ed$. A displacement perpendicular to $\vec E$ contributes nothing, so only the separation along the field matters.

^der-a3-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The field points downhill
> The field points from high potential to low potential, and its strength is how fast $V$ falls per metre (made precise in [[§A3.4 Field from Potential|§A3.4]]). Between parallel conducting plates the field is nearly uniform, so $E = V_{AB}/d$ gives the voltage needed for a given field. Dry air breaks down (becomes conducting) at about $3.0\times10^6$ V/m, so plates 2.5 cm apart in air hold at most about $(3.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{V/m})(0.025\ \mathrm m) = 75$ kV before a spark jumps.

^rem-a3-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: Which way does a charge go?
> Released from rest, every charge moves so that its potential energy $U = qV$ decreases. A positive charge therefore moves toward **lower** $V$ (along $\vec E$), a negative charge toward **higher** $V$ (against $\vec E$). "Downhill" in $V$ is downhill only for positive charges.

^cau-a3-2-1

## Energy units and energy conservation

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.2: Electron-Volt
> The **electron-volt** is the energy gained by a particle of charge $e$ accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V:
>
> $$
> 1\ \mathrm{eV} = (1.60\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm C)(1\ \mathrm V) = 1.60\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm J .
> $$
>
> A charge $ne$ accelerated through $\Delta V$ volts gains $n\,\Delta V$ eV (a doubly charged ion through 100 V gains 200 eV). Chemical bond energies are a few eV; nuclear decay energies are of order 1 MeV.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.2*

^def-a3-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.3: Energy Conservation for a Charge in an Electrostatic Field
> If only electrostatic forces do work on a particle of charge $q$, then between any two points
>
> $$
> K_i + qV_i = K_f + qV_f, \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad \Delta K = -q\,\Delta V .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.2*

^thm-a3-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The electrostatic force is conservative ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]]) with potential energy $U = qV$ ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]]). Conservation of mechanical energy ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|CM Theorem §A4.6.2]]) gives $K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f$.

^der-a3-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]], [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|CM Theorem §A4.6.2]]

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.2.1: Choosing a Path Near a Point Charge
> A charge $q = +2.0$ nC sits at the origin. Point $P_1$ is $a = 4.0$ cm from it and $P_2$ is $b = 12.0$ cm from it, $24^\circ$ around. Use [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]] with $\vec E = (kq/r^2)\,\hat r$ along a convenient two-leg path: radially out from $r = a$ to $r = b$ ($d\vec l = \hat r\,dr$), then along an arc of radius $b$ ($d\vec l = b\,\hat\varphi\,d\varphi$). On the arc $\hat r\cdot\hat\varphi = 0$, so only the radial leg counts:
>
> $$
> V_{P_2} - V_{P_1} = -\int_a^b \frac{kq}{r^2}\,dr = kq\left(\frac1b - \frac1a\right) = (8.99\times10^9)(2.0\times10^{-9})\left(\frac{1}{0.12} - \frac{1}{0.040}\right)\ \mathrm V = -300\ \mathrm V .
> $$
>
> $P_1$, closer to the positive charge, is 300 V higher. Points at the same distance from $q$ are at the same potential, whatever their direction.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 7.9*

> [!example] Example §A3.2.2: An Electron in a Dark-Matter Detector
> In the LZ detector a plane near the bottom is held at $-50\,000$ V and one near the top at $+10\,000$ V. An electron is freed somewhere between them. Which way does it drift, and how much potential energy does it lose crossing the whole gap?
>
> A positive charge would have its highest $U$ near the top plane and would move down. The electron's charge is negative, so it moves **up**, toward higher potential ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^cau-a3-2-1|Caution]]). Crossing the gap, $\Delta V = +60\,000$ V and
>
> $$
> \Delta U = q\,\Delta V = (-1.60\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm C)(+6.0\times10^4\ \mathrm V) = -9.6\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm J = -60\ \mathrm{keV}.
> $$
>
> In electron-volts the answer is read off the voltage directly: crossing 60 kV, the electron's potential energy drops by 60 keV.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 9*

^ex-a3-2-2

> [!example] Example §A3.2.3: Speed of an Accelerated Electron
> An electron starts from rest and is accelerated through a potential difference of 100 V in vacuum (gravity is negligible). By [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-3|Theorem §A3.2.3]] its kinetic energy is $K = -q\,\Delta V = e(100\ \mathrm V) = 100\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.60\times10^{-17}$ J, so
>
> $$
> v = \sqrt{\frac{2K}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(1.60\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm J)}{9.11\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}}} = 5.93\times10^6\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> That is 2% of the speed of light from only 100 V: electrons are easy to accelerate because their mass is so small. Much higher voltages make $v$ comparable to $c$, and then $\tfrac12 mv^2$ no longer applies (Relativity, planned).
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 7.6*

^ex-a3-2-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B \vec E\cdot d\vec l$ is a work line integral ([[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^def-16-2|Def. §16.2]], Multivariable Analysis) of the vector field $-\vec E$; path independence is the statement that $\vec E$ is conservative ([[§11 The Three Differential Operators꞉ Gradient, Curl, Divergence#^def-11-1|Def. §11.1]]).
