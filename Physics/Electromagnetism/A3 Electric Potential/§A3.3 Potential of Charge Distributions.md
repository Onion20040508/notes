With the potential defined as a line integral of the field ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference|§A3.2]]), this section computes it for the standard sources: a point charge, a set of point charges, a dipole and continuous distributions. Because potentials are scalars, they add as numbers, which makes them much easier to sum than fields.

## Point charges

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.1: Potential of a Point Charge
> With $V = 0$ at infinity, a point charge $q$ produces at distance $r$ the potential
>
> $$
> V = \frac{kq}{r}, \qquad k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^9\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}.
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.2–7.3, eq. (7.8)*

^thm-a3-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]] with the reference point at infinity and a radial path, on which $\vec E\cdot d\vec l = (kq/r'^2)\,dr'$:
>
> $$
> V(r) = -\int_\infty^r \frac{kq}{r'^2}\,dr' = \frac{kq}{r} .
> $$
>
> Equivalently, $V = U/Q$ for the pair energy $U = kqQ/r$ of [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]].

^der-a3-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Potential versus field of a point charge
> The potential falls as $1/r$, the field as $1/r^2$; $V$ has the sign of $q$ and no direction. Outside any spherically symmetric charge, such as a charged metal sphere, the field is that of a point charge at the centre ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]), so the potential there is also $kq/r$. A demonstration Van de Graaff sphere of radius 12.5 cm at 100 kV therefore carries only $q = rV/k = (0.125\ \mathrm m)(1.00\times10^5\ \mathrm V)/(8.99\times10^9\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}) = 1.39\ \mu$C: a small charge makes a large voltage.

^rem-a3-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.2: Superposition of Potentials
> The potential of point charges $q_1, \dots, q_N$ at a point $P$, at distances $r_1, \dots, r_N$ from them, is the sum of their individual potentials:
>
> $$
> V_P = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{kq_i}{r_i} .
> $$
>
> A test charge $q_t$ at $P$ then has potential energy $U = q_t V_P$, the work needed to bring it in from infinity.
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.3, eq. (7.9)*

^thm-a3-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The field is the vector sum of the individual fields, $\vec E = \sum_i \vec E_i$ ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^thm-a1-3-1|Theorem §A1.3.1]]). The line integral is linear, so by [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]] $V_P = -\int_\infty^P \sum_i \vec E_i\cdot d\vec l = \sum_i \bigl(-\int_\infty^P \vec E_i\cdot d\vec l\bigr) = \sum_i V_i$, and each term is given by [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]].

^der-a3-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^thm-a1-3-1|Theorem §A1.3.1]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Add numbers, not arrows
> To get $\vec E$ of several charges one must resolve every field into components and add them; to get $V$ one only needs the distances. This is why it often pays to find $V$ first and get $\vec E$ from it afterwards by differentiation ([[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 7 slides*

^rem-a3-3-2

## The dipole

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.3: Potential of a Dipole Far Away
> Let charges $+q$ and $-q$ sit at $z = +d/2$ and $z = -d/2$, with dipole moment $\vec p = q\vec d$ pointing from $-q$ to $+q$ ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^def-a1-5-1|Def. §A1.5.1]]). At a distance $r \gg d$ from the centre, in the direction $\hat r$ at angle $\theta$ from $\vec p$,
>
> $$
> V \approx \frac{k\,qd\cos\theta}{r^2} = \frac{k\,\vec p\cdot\hat r}{r^2} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.3, eqs. (7.10)–(7.11)*

^thm-a3-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Exactly, $V = kq\,(1/r_+ - 1/r_-)$ with $r_\pm^2 = r^2 \mp rd\cos\theta + d^2/4$ (law of cosines). For $r \gg d$ drop the $d^2$ term:
>
> $$
> r_\pm \approx r\Bigl(1 \mp \frac{d}{r}\cos\theta\Bigr)^{1/2}, \qquad \frac{1}{r_\pm} \approx \frac1r\Bigl(1 \pm \frac{d}{2r}\cos\theta\Bigr),
> $$
>
> using $(1 \mp \alpha)^{-1/2} \approx 1 \pm \alpha/2$ for small $\alpha$ (first-order Taylor expansion). Then
>
> $$
> V \approx \frac{kq}{r}\left[\Bigl(1 + \frac{d\cos\theta}{2r}\Bigr) - \Bigl(1 - \frac{d\cos\theta}{2r}\Bigr)\right] = \frac{kqd\cos\theta}{r^2} .
> $$

^der-a3-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]] (Taylor's theorem)

> [!remark] Remark: What the dipole formula says
> The dipole potential falls as $1/r^2$, one power faster than that of a single charge: from far away the two charges almost cancel. It depends on the charges only through $\vec p$, so a pair $\pm Q/2$ at separation $2d$ looks the same as $\pm Q$ at separation $d$. It vanishes on the whole plane $\theta = 90^\circ$ through the centre, is positive on the side of $+q$ and negative on the side of $-q$. Molecules such as water are modelled this way; higher terms (quadrupole, octupole) follow in more advanced courses.

^rem-a3-3-3

## Continuous distributions

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.4: Potential of a Continuous Charge Distribution
> A charge distribution of finite extent produces at $P$ the potential
>
> $$
> V_P = k\int \frac{dq}{r}, \qquad dq = \lambda\,dl,\ \ \sigma\,dA\ \ \text{or}\ \ \rho\,dV ,
> $$
>
> where $r$ is the distance from the element $dq$ to $P$ and $\lambda$, $\sigma$, $\rho$ are the line, surface and volume charge densities ($V = 0$ at infinity).
>
> *Source: UP2 §7.3, eq. (7.12)*

^thm-a3-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Cut the distribution into small elements $\Delta q_i$, each small enough to act as a point charge at distance $r_i$ from $P$. By [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], $V_P \approx \sum_i k\,\Delta q_i/r_i$. This is a Riemann sum; refining the subdivision turns it into the integral.

^der-a3-3-4

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: When infinity cannot be the reference
> $V = k\int dq/r$ assumes $V(\infty) = 0$, which is consistent only for charge confined to a finite region. For idealised infinite distributions (an infinite line, an infinite plane) the integral diverges. Then go back to $V_P - V_R = -\int_R^P \vec E\cdot d\vec l$ with a reference point $R$ at a finite place ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^ex-a3-3-3|Example §A3.3.3]]).

^cau-a3-3-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.3.1: Potential of a Dipole, Exactly and Far Away
> Charges $\pm3.0$ nC sit at $z = \pm2.0$ cm ($d = 4.0$ cm, $+q$ on top). By [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], with $kq = (9.0\times10^9)(3.0\times10^{-9}) = 27\ \mathrm{V\,m}$:
> - at $(0, 0, 1.0\ \mathrm{cm})$: $r_+ = 1.0$ cm, $r_- = 3.0$ cm, $V = 27\,(1/0.010 - 1/0.030)\ \mathrm V = 1.8\times10^3$ V;
> - at $(0, 0, -5.0\ \mathrm{cm})$: $r_+ = 7.0$ cm, $r_- = 3.0$ cm, $V = -5.1\times10^2$ V;
> - at $(3.0\ \mathrm{cm}, 0, 2.0\ \mathrm{cm})$: $r_+ = 3.0$ cm, $r_- = 5.0$ cm, $V = 3.6\times10^2$ V.
>
> No components were needed. At the second point, $r = 5.0$ cm is only $1.25\,d$, and the far-field formula of [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]] gives $-kp/r^2 = -(9.0\times10^9)(1.2\times10^{-10})/(0.050)^2 = -4.3\times10^2$ V, 16% off: the approximation needs $r \gg d$.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 7.12*

^ex-a3-3-1

> [!example] Example §A3.3.2: Ring and Disk on the Axis
> **Ring.** A ring of radius $R$ carries total charge $Q$. Every element is the same distance $\sqrt{z^2 + R^2}$ from a point on the axis at height $z$, so [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]] gives at once
>
> $$
> V_{\text{ring}}(z) = \frac{kQ}{\sqrt{z^2 + R^2}} .
> $$
>
> **Disk.** A disk of radius $R$ with uniform surface charge density $\sigma$ is a set of rings of radius $r'$ and width $dr'$, each with charge $dq = \sigma\,2\pi r'\,dr'$. Adding their potentials,
>
> $$
> V_{\text{disk}}(z) = \int_0^R \frac{k\,\sigma\,2\pi r'\,dr'}{\sqrt{z^2 + r'^2}} = 2\pi k\sigma\left(\sqrt{z^2 + R^2} - |z|\right).
> $$
>
> Check: for $|z| \gg R$, $\sqrt{z^2 + R^2} - |z| \approx R^2/(2|z|)$, so $V \approx k(\sigma\pi R^2)/|z| = kQ/|z|$, the point-charge value. The vector version of this calculation (adding field components ring by ring) is much longer.
>
> *Source: UP2 Examples 7.14–7.15; PHY 182, TBL 12*

> [!example] Example §A3.3.3: Infinite Line of Charge
> For a finite rod of length $L$ the integral of [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]] gives, at distance $x$ from its midpoint on the perpendicular bisector, $V = k\lambda \ln\bigl[(L + \sqrt{L^2 + 4x^2})/(-L + \sqrt{L^2 + 4x^2})\bigr]$, which grows without bound as $L \to \infty$. For an infinite line use its field instead, $\vec E = (2k\lambda/s)\,\hat s$ at distance $s$ ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-5|Theorem §A1.4.5]]), and a radial path from a reference distance $s_R$:
>
> $$
> V_P - V_R = -\int_{s_R}^{s_P} \frac{2k\lambda}{s}\,ds = -2k\lambda \ln\frac{s_P}{s_R} .
> $$
>
> Choosing $V = 0$ at $s_R = 1$ m gives $V(s) = -2k\lambda\ln(s/1\ \mathrm m)$. Only differences are meaningful, and the logarithm makes infinity unusable as a reference.
>
> *Source: UP2 Examples 7.13, 7.16*

^ex-a3-3-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The dipole approximation is the first-order Taylor expansion of $(1+\alpha)^{-1/2}$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]], Single Variable Analysis).
> - $1/r$ is the fundamental solution of the Laplacian in $\mathbb R^3$ ([[§19 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^rem-19-2|452 §19]], Multivariable Analysis), and $\ln r$ that in the plane ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-5|452 Thm. §17.5]]); they are exactly the potentials of a point charge and of an infinite line, which is why their sums and integrals give potentials of charge distributions (Chapter B, planned).
