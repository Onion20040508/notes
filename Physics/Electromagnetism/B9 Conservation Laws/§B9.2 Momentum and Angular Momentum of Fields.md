Does momentum stay conserved when charges push each other through fields? Level A showed that light delivers momentum $U/c$ with absorbed energy $U$ and announced that "the fields carry momentum with density $\vec S/c^2$ wherever they are" as a level-B result ([[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure#^thm-a12-4-1|Theorem §A12.4.1]] and its derivation). This section proves it. The electromagnetic force on the charges in a volume is rewritten through the fields alone, which yields the Maxwell stress tensor (forces as stresses on a surface) and the momentum density $\vec g = \varepsilon_0\vec E\times\vec B$; with them momentum, and then angular momentum, are conserved for charges and fields together. Even static fields can store momentum and angular momentum, which explains why a charged body can start to rotate when a current is switched off.

## Why the fields must carry momentum

> [!remark] Remark: Newton's third law fails in electrodynamics
> Let two equal charges move with equal speeds toward the origin, one along the $x$ axis and one along the $y$ axis, kept on their tracks by constraints. The electric forces between them are opposite, but the magnetic field of each at the other gives magnetic forces that are equal in size and **not** opposite: one points along $+x$, the other along $+y$ (Griffiths Fig. 8.3; the fields of moving charges are derived in [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-4|Theorem §B11.2.4]]). The internal forces of the pair then do not cancel, and the proof that internal forces leave the total momentum unchanged ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-1|CM Theorem §B3.1.1]], which assumes the third law) breaks down. In electrostatics and magnetostatics the third law holds; in electrodynamics momentum conservation is rescued only if the fields themselves carry momentum.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.2.1, Figs. 8.2–8.3*

## The Maxwell stress tensor

> [!definition] Definition §B9.2.1: Maxwell Stress Tensor
> The **Maxwell stress tensor** is the symmetric $3\times3$ array (Griffiths eq. (8.17))
>
> $$
> T_{ij} = \varepsilon_0\Bigl(E_iE_j - \frac12\delta_{ij}E^2\Bigr) + \frac{1}{\mu_0}\Bigl(B_iB_j - \frac12\delta_{ij}B^2\Bigr), \qquad i, j \in \{x, y, z\},
> $$
>
> for example $T_{xx} = \tfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}(E_x^2 - E_y^2 - E_z^2) + \tfrac{1}{2\mu_0}(B_x^2 - B_y^2 - B_z^2)$ and $T_{xy} = \varepsilon_0E_xE_y + \mu_0^{-1}B_xB_y$. Written $\overleftrightarrow{T}$, it acts on vectors by $(\vec a\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T})_j = \sum_ia_iT_{ij}$, and its divergence is $(\nabla\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T})_j = \sum_i\partial_iT_{ij}$ (Griffiths eq. (8.18)); since $T_{ij} = T_{ji}$, left and right products agree.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.2.2, eqs. (8.17)–(8.18)*

^def-b9-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.1: Electromagnetic Force on the Charges in a Volume
> The force per unit volume on charges and currents, $\vec f = \rho\vec E + \vec J\times\vec B$ (Griffiths eq. (8.14)), can be written through the fields alone:
>
> $$
> \vec f = \nabla\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T} - \varepsilon_0\mu_0\frac{\partial\vec S}{\partial t} ,
> $$
>
> so the total electromagnetic force on the charges in a volume $\mathcal V$ with boundary $\mathcal S$ is (Griffiths eqs. (8.19)–(8.20))
>
> $$
> \vec F = \oint_{\mathcal S}\overleftrightarrow{T}\cdot d\vec a - \varepsilon_0\mu_0\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal V}\vec S\,d\tau, \qquad \vec F = \oint_{\mathcal S}\overleftrightarrow{T}\cdot d\vec a \quad\text{(static fields)} .
> $$
>
> $T_{ij}$ is the force per unit area in the $i$ direction on a surface element oriented along $j$: diagonal elements are pressures (or tensions), off-diagonal ones shears. Any surface enclosing the charges in question, and no others, gives the same static force.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.2.2, eqs. (8.13)–(8.21)*

^thm-b9-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the Lorentz force law ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]) the force on the charges in $d\tau$ is $(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B)\rho\,d\tau$, and $\rho\vec v \to \vec J$ (summed over species as in [[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^der-b9-1-1|the derivation of Theorem §B9.1.1]]), giving $\vec f$. Eliminate the sources with Gauss's law and the Ampère–Maxwell law ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]):
>
> $$
> \vec f = \varepsilon_0(\nabla\cdot\vec E)\vec E + \Bigl(\frac{1}{\mu_0}\nabla\times\vec B - \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t}\Bigr)\times\vec B .
> $$
>
> Since $\partial_t(\vec E\times\vec B) = \partial_t\vec E\times\vec B + \vec E\times\partial_t\vec B$ and $\partial_t\vec B = -\nabla\times\vec E$, we have $\partial_t\vec E\times\vec B = \partial_t(\vec E\times\vec B) + \vec E\times(\nabla\times\vec E)$, and so (Griffiths eq. (8.15))
>
> $$
> \vec f = \varepsilon_0\bigl[(\nabla\cdot\vec E)\vec E - \vec E\times(\nabla\times\vec E)\bigr] - \frac{1}{\mu_0}\bigl[\vec B\times(\nabla\times\vec B)\bigr] - \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\vec E\times\vec B) .
> $$
>
> Add $\mu_0^{-1}(\nabla\cdot\vec B)\vec B = \vec 0$ to make $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ appear symmetrically, and use the product rule $\nabla(E^2) = 2(\vec E\cdot\nabla)\vec E + 2\vec E\times(\nabla\times\vec E)$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]), and its analogue for $\vec B$:
>
> $$
> \vec f = \varepsilon_0\bigl[(\nabla\cdot\vec E)\vec E + (\vec E\cdot\nabla)\vec E\bigr] + \frac{1}{\mu_0}\bigl[(\nabla\cdot\vec B)\vec B + (\vec B\cdot\nabla)\vec B\bigr] - \frac12\nabla\Bigl(\varepsilon_0E^2 + \frac{1}{\mu_0}B^2\Bigr) - \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\vec E\times\vec B)
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (8.16)). On the other hand, from Def. §B9.2.1,
>
> $$
> (\nabla\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T})_j = \sum_i\partial_iT_{ij} = \varepsilon_0\Bigl[(\nabla\cdot\vec E)E_j + (\vec E\cdot\nabla)E_j - \frac12\partial_jE^2\Bigr] + \frac{1}{\mu_0}\Bigl[(\nabla\cdot\vec B)B_j + (\vec B\cdot\nabla)B_j - \frac12\partial_jB^2\Bigr],
> $$
>
> because $\sum_i\partial_i(E_iE_j) = (\nabla\cdot\vec E)E_j + (\vec E\cdot\nabla)E_j$ and $\sum_i\partial_i(\delta_{ij}E^2) = \partial_jE^2$. Comparing, $\vec f = \nabla\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T} - \varepsilon_0\partial_t(\vec E\times\vec B)$, and $\varepsilon_0\vec E\times\vec B = \varepsilon_0\mu_0\vec S$ ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^def-b9-1-1|Def. §B9.1.1]]). Integrate over $\mathcal V$ and apply the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]) to each column of $\overleftrightarrow{T}$. If the fields do not depend on time, the last term vanishes. The surface may be moved through any region without charge, since there $\nabla\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T} = \vec f = \vec 0$ for static fields.

> [!remark] Remark: Tension along the field lines, pressure across them
> In a uniform field $\vec E = E\hat z$ (no $\vec B$), $\overleftrightarrow{T} = \tfrac{\varepsilon_0E^2}{2}\operatorname{diag}(-1, -1, +1)$. Across a surface element with outward normal $\hat n$, the field outside exerts the force $\overleftrightarrow{T}\cdot\hat n\,da$ on the contents: for $\hat n = \hat z$ (along the field) it is $+\tfrac{\varepsilon_0E^2}{2}\hat z\,da$, a **pull** outward, and for $\hat n \perp \hat z$ it is a **push** inward of the same size. Faraday's field lines act like stretched rubber bands that also repel sideways, with tension and pressure $\varepsilon_0E^2/2$ (and $B^2/2\mu_0$ for magnetic lines). In general $\overleftrightarrow{T}$ is symmetric and so has three orthogonal principal directions ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]); for a pure $\vec E$ they are $\hat E$ (eigenvalue $+\varepsilon_0E^2/2$) and any two directions perpendicular to it ($-\varepsilon_0E^2/2$). Two checks:
> - *Capacitor plate.* Enclose the upper plate (charge density $+\sigma$) of a large parallel-plate capacitor by a flat box. The field $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ is present only on the lower face, whose outward normal is $-\hat z$; with $T_{zz} = \sigma^2/2\varepsilon_0$ the force per unit area is $-\sigma^2/2\varepsilon_0\,\hat z$, toward the other plate: the electrostatic pressure on a conductor ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]]; Griffiths Problem 8.7).
> - *Charged sphere.* For a uniformly charged solid sphere (charge $Q$, radius $R$), integrating $\overleftrightarrow{T}$ over the surface of the northern hemisphere (the bowl $r = R$ plus the equatorial disk) gives the force of one hemisphere on the other, $\tfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\tfrac{3Q^2}{16R^2}$ (Griffiths Example 8.2); the whole plane $z = 0$, closed by a hemisphere at infinity that contributes nothing, gives the same: a volume problem done as a surface integral.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.2.2, Example 8.2, Problem 8.7*

^rem-b9-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: Stress tensor or momentum flux: the sign
> $\overleftrightarrow{T}$ is a **stress**: $\oint\overleftrightarrow{T}\cdot d\vec a$ with outward $d\vec a$ is the force on what is inside. The **momentum current density** of the field is $-\overleftrightarrow{T}$: $-\overleftrightarrow{T}\cdot d\vec a$ is the electromagnetic momentum per unit time passing outward through $d\vec a$ (Theorem §B9.2.2). Nelson works with the momentum flux tensor, which is $-T_{ij}$ in Griffiths' notation (his ch. 35, footnote 10), and texts differ in which of the two they call the stress tensor. Check a sign by the capacitor plate above: the plates must attract.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.2.3, after eq. (8.29) · Nelson ch. 35, §35.6, footnote 10*

## Conservation of momentum and angular momentum

> [!definition] Definition §B9.2.2: Momentum and Angular Momentum Densities of the Field
> The electromagnetic **momentum density** and **angular momentum density** (about the origin) are (Griffiths eqs. (8.29), (8.33))
>
> $$
> \vec g = \varepsilon_0\,\vec E\times\vec B = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\vec S = \frac{\vec S}{c^2}, \qquad \vec\ell = \vec r\times\vec g = \varepsilon_0\,\vec r\times(\vec E\times\vec B) ,
> $$
>
> and $\vec p_{\text{em}} = \int\vec g\,d\tau$, $\vec L_{\text{em}} = \int\vec\ell\,d\tau$ are the momentum and angular momentum stored in the fields (Griffiths eq. (8.28)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.2.3–8.2.4, eqs. (8.28)–(8.29), (8.32)–(8.33)*

^def-b9-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.2: Conservation of Momentum
> If only electromagnetic forces act on the charges in $\mathcal V$, their mechanical momentum $\vec p_{\text{mech}}$ changes as (Griffiths eq. (8.27))
>
> $$
> \frac{d\vec p_{\text{mech}}}{dt} + \frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal V}\vec g\,d\tau = \oint_{\mathcal S}\overleftrightarrow{T}\cdot d\vec a .
> $$
>
> The total momentum of charges and fields in $\mathcal V$ changes only by the momentum carried in through the boundary by the momentum current $-\overleftrightarrow{T}$. Where there are no charges, (Griffiths eq. (8.30))
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\vec g}{\partial t} = \nabla\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T} ,
> $$
>
> the continuity equation for field momentum. Integrated over all space, with fields that vanish fast enough at infinity, $\vec p_{\text{mech}} + \vec p_{\text{em}}$ is constant.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.2.3, eqs. (8.27)–(8.30)*

^thm-b9-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By Newton's second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-3|CM Principle §A3.3.3]], applied to all the charges in $\mathcal V$; internal mechanical forces cancel, [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-1|CM Theorem §B3.1.1]]), $d\vec p_{\text{mech}}/dt$ is the total electromagnetic force $\vec F$ on them, given by [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]]; rewrite $\varepsilon_0\mu_0\vec S$ as $\vec g$. Without charges $\vec f = \vec 0$, so $\nabla\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T} = \partial_t\vec g$ pointwise. For all space the surface integral vanishes when $\overleftrightarrow{T} \propto$ (field)$^2$ falls faster than $1/r^2$.

> [!remark] Remark: What is new compared with level A
> - The momentum density $\vec S/c^2$ announced in the derivation of [[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure#^thm-a12-4-1|Theorem §A12.4.1]] is now a theorem: whatever momentum the charges gain, the fields lose or import through the boundary. For a plane wave it gives momentum $U/c$ per energy $U$ ([[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-4|Theorem §B10.1.4]]), and radiation pressure follows from the momentum current without modelling the absorber.
> - The Poynting vector has two roles: $\vec S$ is the energy flux, $\vec S/c^2$ the momentum density, and $\overleftrightarrow{T}$ likewise is both a stress and (with a minus sign) a momentum flux. That the two roles of $\vec S$ match is no accident (Griffiths, footnote 6): in relativity both belong to one tensor ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem|§B9.1]], Connections).
> - Static fields can hold momentum: a coaxial cable carrying a steady current $I$ from a battery to a resistor at voltage $V$ has, by Def. §B9.2.2, $\vec p_{\text{em}} = (IVl/c^2)\hat z$ in its fields; when the current is reduced, the induced electric field pushes the two conductors' charges $\pm\lambda$ and delivers exactly this momentum to the cable (Griffiths Example 8.3).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.2.3, Example 8.3, footnote 6*

^rem-b9-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.3: Conservation of Angular Momentum
> If only electromagnetic forces act on the charges in $\mathcal V$, their mechanical angular momentum about the origin changes as
>
> $$
> \frac{d\vec L_{\text{mech}}}{dt} + \frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal V}\vec\ell\,d\tau = \oint_{\mathcal S}\vec r\times\bigl(d\vec a\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec\ell = \vec r\times\vec g$ (Def. §B9.2.2). For an isolated system, $\vec L_{\text{mech}} + \vec L_{\text{em}}$ is constant.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.2.4, eq. (8.33) (the density); the balance law worked out here from eqs. (8.19)–(8.20)*

^thm-b9-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The torque on the charges in $d\tau$ is $\vec r\times\vec f\,d\tau$ with $\vec f = \nabla\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T} - \partial_t\vec g$ ([[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]]); $\vec r\times\partial_t\vec g = \partial_t\vec\ell$ since $\vec r$ does not depend on time. In components, with the Levi-Civita symbol $\epsilon_{ijk}$ and summation over repeated indices,
>
> $$
> \bigl[\vec r\times(\nabla\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T})\bigr]_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\,r_j\,\partial_lT_{lk} = \partial_l\bigl(\epsilon_{ijk}\,r_j\,T_{lk}\bigr) - \epsilon_{ijk}\,\delta_{lj}\,T_{lk} = \partial_l\bigl(\epsilon_{ijk}\,r_j\,T_{lk}\bigr) - \epsilon_{ijk}T_{jk} .
> $$
>
> The last term vanishes: $T_{jk}$ is symmetric and $\epsilon_{ijk}$ antisymmetric in $j, k$. The divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]) turns the first term into $\oint\epsilon_{ijk}r_j\,(da_l\,T_{lk}) = \oint[\vec r\times(d\vec a\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T})]_i$. The total torque on the charges is $d\vec L_{\text{mech}}/dt$ ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-2|CM Theorem §A6.6.2]]; internal mechanical forces exert no net torque, [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-1|CM Theorem §B3.1.1]]), which gives the balance law. For an isolated system, take $\mathcal S$ far away where the fields have died out.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.4: Momentum and Angular Momentum of Static Fields from the Potentials
> Let the fields be static and produced by a localized charge density $\rho$ and steady currents, with $\vec E = -\nabla V$ and $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$, $\vec A$ in the Coulomb gauge $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$ ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^def-b6-4-2|Def. §B6.4.2]]) and $V$, $\vec A$ vanishing at infinity. Then the field momentum and angular momentum are
>
> $$
> \vec p_{\text{em}} = \int\rho\,\vec A\,d\tau, \qquad \vec L_{\text{em}} = \int\rho\,\bigl(\vec r\times\vec A\bigr)\,d\tau .
> $$
>
> Each charge $q$ at $\vec r$ contributes $q\vec A(\vec r)$ and $q\,\vec r\times\vec A(\vec r)$, although the momentum resides in the fields, often far from the charge. In particular, for a uniformly charged ring or cylinder of total charge $q$ at radius $s$ about the axis of an azimuthal $\vec A = A_\varphi(s)\hat\varphi$,
>
> $$
> L_{\text{em},z} = q\,s\,A_\varphi(s) = \frac{q\,\Phi(s)}{2\pi} ,
> $$
>
> with $\Phi(s)$ the magnetic flux through the circle of radius $s$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 8.20 (its hint, the equivalent form $\vec p = \varepsilon_0\mu_0\int V\vec J\,d\tau$); the forms with $\rho\vec A$ worked out here*

^thm-b9-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Momentum.* In components (summation over repeated indices), $(\vec E\times\vec B)_i = (\vec E\times(\nabla\times\vec A))_i = E_j\partial_iA_j - E_j\partial_jA_i$. Integrate by parts, dropping surface terms (below): with $E_j = -\partial_jV$, $\int E_j\partial_iA_j\,d\tau = \int V\,\partial_i(\partial_jA_j)\,d\tau = 0$ by the Coulomb gauge, and $-\int E_j\partial_jA_i\,d\tau = \int(\partial_jE_j)A_i\,d\tau = \int(\rho/\varepsilon_0)A_i\,d\tau$ by Gauss's law. Multiply by $\varepsilon_0$.
>
> *Angular momentum.* $[\vec r\times(\vec E\times\vec B)]_a = \epsilon_{abi}r_b\,(E_j\partial_iA_j - E_j\partial_jA_i)$. First term: $-\int\epsilon_{abi}r_b\,\partial_jV\,\partial_iA_j = \int V\,\partial_j(\epsilon_{abi}r_b\partial_iA_j) = \int V\epsilon_{abi}\partial_iA_b = -\int V B_a$, using $\partial_jr_b = \delta_{jb}$, $\partial_jA_j = 0$ and $\epsilon_{abi}\partial_iA_b = -(\nabla\times\vec A)_a$. Second term: $-\int\epsilon_{abi}r_bE_j\partial_jA_i = \int\partial_j(\epsilon_{abi}r_bE_j)A_i = \int\bigl[(\vec E\times\vec A)_a + (\rho/\varepsilon_0)(\vec r\times\vec A)_a\bigr]$. Finally $-V\vec B + \vec E\times\vec A = -V\nabla\times\vec A - \nabla V\times\vec A = -\nabla\times(V\vec A)$, whose volume integral is a surface term. Multiply by $\varepsilon_0$.
>
> *Surface terms.* For localized static sources $V = O(1/r)$, $\vec E = O(1/r^2)$, $\vec A = O(1/r^2)$ and $\partial\vec A = O(1/r^3)$, so every surface integrand above is at most $O(1/r^3)$ (or $O(1/r^4)$) on a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$, and the surface terms vanish as $r \to \infty$.
>
> *Ring.* For $\vec r = s\hat s + z\hat z$ and $\vec A = A_\varphi\hat\varphi$, $(\vec r\times\vec A)_z = sA_\varphi$; on the circle of radius $s$, $2\pi sA_\varphi = \oint\vec A\cdot d\vec l = \Phi(s)$ ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]], Stokes' theorem [[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Canonical momentum, and Feynman's disk paradox
> - $m\vec v + q\vec A$ is the canonical momentum of a charge ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^rem-b7-1-5|CM Remark: The charged particle]]). Theorem §B9.2.4 says that its second part is the charge's share of the field momentum. In an axially symmetric situation the induced torque on a charge $q$ at radius $s$ is $qsE_\varphi = -qs\,\partial_tA_\varphi$ plus a magnetic part, and together they give exactly $\tfrac{d}{dt}\bigl(msv_\varphi + qsA_\varphi\bigr) = 0$ for every charge: the angular momentum a charge picks up when a flux changes is the field angular momentum it gives up.
> - *Feynman's disk paradox* (Griffiths Example 8.4). A long solenoid (radius $R$, $n$ turns per length, current $I$) is surrounded by two coaxial nonconducting cylinders of length $l$, charge $+Q$ at radius $a < R$ and $-Q$ at radius $b > R$. Between them $\vec E$ is radial and inside the solenoid $\vec B$ is axial, so $\vec g = -(\mu_0nIQ/2\pi ls)\hat\varphi$ for $a < s < R$ and (Griffiths eq. (8.34))
>
> $$
> \vec L_{\text{em}} = -\tfrac12\mu_0nIQ(R^2 - a^2)\,\hat z .
> $$
>
>   When $I$ is switched off, the induced field $E_\varphi$ makes the cylinders turn: the inner one gains $+\tfrac12\mu_0nIQa^2\hat z$, the outer one $-\tfrac12\mu_0nIQR^2\hat z$, and the sum is $\vec L_{\text{em}}$. Nothing seemed to rotate before, yet angular momentum appears; it was stored in the static fields. Theorem §B9.2.4 assigns the shares directly: $Q\,\Phi(a)/2\pi = \tfrac12\mu_0nIQa^2$ to the inner cylinder and $-Q\,\Phi(R)/2\pi = -\tfrac12\mu_0nIQR^2$ to the outer one.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 8.4 and footnote 8*

^rem-b9-2-4

> [!remark] Remark: Magnetic forces do no work
> A crane magnet lifts a junked car; who does the work? Model the car's magnetization as a ring of charge spinning in the magnet's nonuniform field. The vertical component of the magnetic force lifts the ring, but as it rises a motional emf slows its spinning by exactly the energy gained in height: the magnetic force, perpendicular to each charge's velocity, only redirects energy ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]]; the same mechanism as for the loop lifted by a battery in [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^cau-b6-1-1|§B6.1, Caution: When a magnetic force seems to do work]]). With two current loops held at constant currents by power supplies, the supply of the lifted loop provides the work of lifting and the other supply an equal amount stored in the field energy $MI_aI_b$ ([[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-2|Theorem §B8.3.2]]); when the magnet itself is hauled up on a rope, the person pulling does the work and the field merely transmits it. An exception would require a force law other than Lorentz's, for example magnetic monopoles.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.3, eqs. (8.35)–(8.44)*

^rem-b9-2-5

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Hidden momentum, and momentum in matter
> - The static coaxial cable of [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^rem-b9-2-3|Remark: What is new compared with level A]] has field momentum $IVl/c^2$ although nothing moves, which seems to violate the rule that a system at rest has zero total momentum. The resolution is relativistic: the current-carrying conductors carry an equal and opposite **hidden mechanical momentum** (Griffiths, Example 12.12); see Electromagnetism level C (planned).
> - In linear media the field momentum density is $\vec D\times\vec B$ (Minkowski) or $\varepsilon_0\mu_0\vec E\times\vec H$ (Abraham), a question debated for over a century (Griffiths Problem 8.23(b), footnote 25). Net forces on bodies in vacuum, such as radiation pressure, do not depend on the choice.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 8.3, Problem 8.23(b), footnotes 7 and 25*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B9.2.1: Radiation Pressure on a Window
> Sunlight of intensity $I$ falls normally on a transparent window floating in space; it reflects intensity $I_R < I$ and absorbs nothing. What is transmitted, and what is the pressure? Energy conservation (Poynting's theorem in the steady state, [[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]) gives $I_T = I - I_R$. Light of intensity $I$ carries momentum density $I/c^2$ along its direction ($\vec g = \vec S/c^2$, Def. §B9.2.2, averaged over a cycle) and moves at $c$, so through each unit area per unit time it carries momentum $I/c$. In a time $\Delta t$, the window of area $A$ receives the momentum of the incident slab $Ac\Delta t$ and emits the transmitted and reflected slabs:
>
> $$
> \Delta\vec p = \frac{A\Delta t}{c}\bigl[I - I_T - (-I_R)\bigr]\hat k = \frac{A\Delta t}{c}\,2I_R\,\hat k, \qquad P = \frac{2I_R}{c} ,
> $$
>
> by [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]] in the steady state. Transmitted light leaves with the momentum it brought; only the reflected part pushes, twice as hard as if it were absorbed. For $I_R = 0$ there is no force, for $I_R = I$ the perfect-reflector value $2I/c$ of [[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure#^thm-a12-4-2|Theorem §A12.4.2]]. For uncoated glass at normal incidence $I_R \approx 0.04\,I$ from each surface ([[§B10.2 Waves in Linear Media, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]]), so the push is less than a tenth of a mirror's. The same result follows from the stress tensor on the two faces ([[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-5|Theorem §B10.1.5]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 422, practice final, Problem 3(b) (Radiation pressure on a window)*

> [!example] Example §B9.2.2: A Charged Cylinder Spun Up by a Solenoid
> A long cylindrical shell of radius $R_2$, length $\ell$, surface charge density $\sigma$ and mass per unit area $\rho_m$ is free to rotate about the axis of a coaxial solenoid of radius $R_1 < R_2$ with $n$ turns per length. Initially the current is $I_0$ and the shell is at rest; then the current is switched off. Find the final angular velocity $\omega_f$. (Parts (a)–(b) of the problem, the fields $\vec B$ and $\vec E_{\text{ind}}$ in the three regions, are in [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^ex-b8-2-1|Example §B8.2.1]].)
>
> *Angular momentum bookkeeping.* Fields and shell form an isolated system ([[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]]), and its initial and final states are static, so [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^thm-b9-2-4|Theorem §B9.2.4]] gives the field angular momentum as $Q\Phi/2\pi$, with $Q = 2\pi R_2\ell\sigma$ the shell's charge and $\Phi$ the total flux inside the shell:
> - before: $\Phi_i = \mu_0nI_0\,\pi R_1^2$ (solenoid, [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]]), shell at rest;
> - after: the rotating shell is a solenoid with surface current $K = \sigma\omega_fR_2$, so $\Phi_f = \mu_0\sigma\omega_fR_2\,\pi R_2^2$, and its mechanical angular momentum is $I_{\text{shell}}\omega_f = (2\pi R_2\ell\rho_m)R_2^2\,\omega_f$.
>
> Conservation, $Q\Phi_i/2\pi = I_{\text{shell}}\omega_f + Q\Phi_f/2\pi$, reads
>
> $$
> \sigma R_2\ell\,\mu_0nI_0\pi R_1^2 = 2\pi\rho_mR_2^3\ell\,\omega_f + \pi\mu_0\sigma^2R_2^4\ell\,\omega_f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega_f = \frac{\mu_0\sigma nR_1^2I_0}{2\rho_mR_2^2 + \mu_0\sigma^2R_2^3} ,
> $$
>
> positive: for $\sigma > 0$ the shell turns in the sense of the solenoid current, so its own field partly replaces the vanishing flux (Lenz).
>
> *Torque route (the course solution).* On the shell, $E_\varphi(R_2) = -\tfrac{\mu_0}{2R_2}\bigl(nR_1^2\dot I + \sigma R_2^3\dot\omega\bigr)$ (from [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^ex-b8-2-1|Example §B8.2.1]]), so the torque is $N_z = \sigma(2\pi R_2\ell)R_2E_\varphi = -\pi\mu_0\sigma R_2\ell\,(nR_1^2\dot I + \sigma R_2^3\dot\omega)$. Setting $N_z = I_{\text{shell}}\dot\omega$ and integrating from $(I_0, 0)$ to $(0, \omega_f)$ ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]) gives the same $\omega_f$. The term $\sigma R_2^3\dot\omega$ is the shell's own back emf; without it one finds $\mu_0\sigma nR_1^2I_0/2\rho_mR_2^2$, correct only for a heavy shell, $\rho_m \gg \mu_0\sigma^2R_2/2$. The two routes are one equation: $N_z = -\tfrac{d}{dt}(Q\Phi/2\pi)$ is the canonical-momentum statement of [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^rem-b9-2-4|Remark: Canonical momentum, and Feynman's disk paradox]].
>
> *Where was the angular momentum?* In the infinitely long idealization $\vec E$ of the shell exists only outside it and $\vec B$ only inside, so $\vec E\times\vec B = \vec 0$ everywhere, yet $L_{\text{em}} \ne 0$. For any real, finite shell and solenoid the magnetic flux returns outside the shell, where the shell's electric field is not zero, and Theorem §B9.2.4 (valid for localized sources) guarantees that the field angular momentum, located where the returning flux meets the shell's electric field, is $Q\Phi/2\pi$. In Feynman's two-cylinder version the net charge is zero and no field reaches the return flux, which is why there the angular momentum sits between the cylinders.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, practice final, Problem 1(c) (Rotating cylinder), checked against the instructor's solution; the angular-momentum route worked out here*

> [!example] Example §B9.2.3: Angular Momentum of a Charged, Magnetized Sphere
> A sphere of radius $R$ is uniformly magnetized, $\vec M = M\hat z$, with dipole moment $m = \tfrac43\pi R^3M$; outside, its field is that of a dipole $m\hat z$ at the centre ([[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]]). (i) Alone, it has no electric field, so $\vec g = \vec 0$ and no field angular momentum. (The electron spins that make up $\vec M$ carry angular momentum of their own, [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field|QM §B6.2]]; that is not field angular momentum and is not asked for.) (ii) Now spread a charge $Q$ uniformly over its surface. Inside, $\vec E = \vec 0$; outside, $\vec E = Q\hat r/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$ and $\vec B = (\mu_0m/4\pi r^3)(2\cos\theta\,\hat r + \sin\theta\,\hat\theta)$. Since $\hat r\times\hat\theta = \hat\varphi$,
>
> $$
> \vec g = \varepsilon_0\vec E\times\vec B = \frac{\mu_0Qm\sin\theta}{16\pi^2r^5}\,\hat\varphi ,
> $$
>
> circulating about the $z$ axis in the sense of the magnetizing currents (at the equator $\vec B$ points along $-\hat z$ and $\vec E$ outward, so $\vec E\times\vec B$ is along $+\hat\varphi$). With $\hat r\times\hat\varphi = -\hat\theta$ and $\hat\theta\cdot\hat z = -\sin\theta$, $\ell_z = rg\sin\theta = \mu_0Qm\sin^2\theta/16\pi^2r^4$, and
>
> $$
> L_z = \frac{\mu_0Qm}{16\pi^2}\int_R^\infty\frac{r^2\,dr}{r^4}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\sin^2\theta\,\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi = \frac{\mu_0Qm}{16\pi^2}\cdot\frac1R\cdot\frac{8\pi}{3} = \frac{\mu_0Qm}{6\pi R} = \frac29\,\mu_0MQR^2 .
> $$
>
> The charged, magnetized sphere carries angular momentum $\tfrac29\mu_0MQR^2$ along $+\hat z$ (for $Q > 0$), in its fields. *Check* with [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^thm-b9-2-4|Theorem §B9.2.4]]: on the surface $\vec A = \tfrac{\mu_0}{3}\vec M\times\vec r$ (the vector potential of the uniformly magnetized sphere, the spinning shell of Griffiths Example 5.11 with $\sigma\omega R \to M$, [[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]]; it equals the dipole potential $\mu_0\vec m\times\hat r/4\pi R^2$ there), so $\vec r\times\vec A = \tfrac{\mu_0}{3}[R^2\vec M - \vec r(\vec r\cdot\vec M)]$, and averaging $\vec r(\vec r\cdot\vec M)$ over the sphere gives $\tfrac13R^2\vec M$; hence $\vec L = Q\cdot\tfrac{\mu_0}{3}\cdot\tfrac23R^2\vec M = \tfrac29\mu_0QR^2\vec M$. If the sphere is demagnetized or discharged, this angular momentum is handed to the sphere, which starts to turn.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, practice final, Problem 3(c) (Charged magnetized sphere), direction as in the instructor's solution · Griffiths Problem 8.10 (answer $\tfrac29\mu_0MQR^2$; magnitude worked out here)*
