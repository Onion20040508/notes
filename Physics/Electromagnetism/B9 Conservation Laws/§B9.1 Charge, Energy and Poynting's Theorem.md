Where is the energy of an electromagnetic system, and how does it move? Level A met the two energy densities $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ and $B^2/2\mu_0$ in a capacitor and a solenoid ([[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]], [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-2|Theorem §A10.3.2]]), added them for a plane wave and defined the Poynting vector for waves ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-1|Theorem §A12.3.1]], [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^def-a12-3-1|Def. §A12.3.1]]); level B derived the static energies $W_{\text e}$ and $W_{\text m}$ as integrals over the fields ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]], [[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-3|Theorem §B8.3.3]]). This section proves from Maxwell's equations and the Lorentz force law that the same density $u$ and the flux $\vec S$ obey a local conservation law for every field, static or not, with the work done on the charges as the only source: Poynting's theorem. The level-A formulas for waves become special cases.

## Local conservation

> [!remark] Remark: Charge conservation as the template
> Conservation of charge ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]]) holds **locally**: the charge in a volume $\mathcal V$ changes only by what flows through its boundary, $dQ/dt = -\oint_{\mathcal S}\vec J\cdot d\vec a$, which by the divergence theorem ([[§32 Flux Integrals and the Divergence Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-32-1|452 Thm. §32.1]]) is equivalent to the continuity equation $\partial\rho/\partial t = -\nabla\cdot\vec J$ (Griffiths eqs. (8.1)–(8.4); home and derivation: [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]; it also follows from Maxwell's equations, [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-3|Theorem §B8.4.3]]). Global conservation alone would allow charge to vanish here and reappear far away at the same instant; local conservation forbids it. Energy and momentum are to be put into the same form: a **density** whose rate of change is minus the divergence of a **flux density**, plus a source term that describes the exchange with the charges. Griffiths' footnote: the continuity equation is the only constraint the sources of Maxwell's equations must satisfy. Nelson stresses that local conservation is what is needed when the interaction between distant charges takes time to arrive.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.1.1 · Nelson ch. 35, §35.1–35.3*

^rem-b9-1-1

## Work on the charges

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.1: Power Delivered to the Charges
> Let fields $\vec E$, $\vec B$ act on charges with current density $\vec J$. The rate at which the electromagnetic force does work on all the charges in a volume $\mathcal V$ is (Griffiths eq. (8.6))
>
> $$
> \frac{dW}{dt} = \int_{\mathcal V}\vec E\cdot\vec J\,d\tau .
> $$
>
> $\vec E\cdot\vec J$ is the power delivered per unit volume; the magnetic field does not appear.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.1.2, eq. (8.6) and footnote 2*

^thm-b9-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In a time $dt$ a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ is displaced by $d\vec l = \vec v\,dt$, and the Lorentz force ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]) does the work $\vec F\cdot d\vec l = q(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B)\cdot\vec v\,dt = q\vec E\cdot\vec v\,dt$, since $(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot\vec v = 0$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]]). For several species of carriers with densities $\rho_\alpha$ and velocities $\vec v_\alpha$, the charge in $d\tau$ of species $\alpha$ receives the power $\rho_\alpha\,d\tau\,\vec E\cdot\vec v_\alpha$; summing over species, the power per unit volume is $\vec E\cdot\sum_\alpha\rho_\alpha\vec v_\alpha = \vec E\cdot\vec J$. (Summing the species first matters: in a wire the net $\rho$ vanishes while $\vec J$ does not, so "$\rho\vec v$" for the net charge would be meaningless; Griffiths' footnote 2.) Integrate over $\mathcal V$.

## Poynting's theorem

> [!definition] Definition §B9.1.1: Electromagnetic Energy Density; Poynting Vector
> The **energy density** of the electromagnetic field and the **Poynting vector** (energy flux density) are (Griffiths eqs. (8.5), (8.10))
>
> $$
> u = \frac12\Bigl(\varepsilon_0E^2 + \frac{1}{\mu_0}B^2\Bigr), \qquad \vec S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\vec E\times\vec B .
> $$
>
> They generalize the densities $u_E + u_B$ of level A (written there with separate symbols; [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^cau-b2-3-1|§B2.3, Caution: Notation for energy]]) and the Poynting vector of [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^def-a12-3-1|Def. §A12.3.1]] to arbitrary fields. $u$ is measured in $\mathrm{J/m^3}$, $\vec S$ in $\mathrm{W/m^2}$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.1.2, eqs. (8.5), (8.10)*

^def-b9-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.2: Poynting's Theorem
> For fields obeying Maxwell's equations in vacuum with arbitrary sources $\rho$, $\vec J$ ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]), and any volume $\mathcal V$ with boundary $\mathcal S$ (outward $d\vec a$),
>
> $$
> \frac{dW}{dt} = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal V}u\,d\tau - \oint_{\mathcal S}\vec S\cdot d\vec a ,
> $$
>
> with $dW/dt$ the power delivered to the charges in $\mathcal V$ ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]]) (Griffiths eqs. (8.9), (8.11)). Equivalently, at every point,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\vec S = -\vec E\cdot\vec J ,
> $$
>
> and where there are no currents, $\partial u/\partial t = -\nabla\cdot\vec S$ (Griffiths eq. (8.12)): field energy is then locally conserved. In words: the work done on the charges equals the decrease of the energy stored in the fields minus the energy that flows out through the surface.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.1.2, eqs. (8.7)–(8.12) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 13 §4, eqs. (26)–(28) (the current-free case)*

^thm-b9-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Eliminate $\vec J$ with the Ampère–Maxwell law, $\vec J = \mu_0^{-1}\nabla\times\vec B - \varepsilon_0\,\partial\vec E/\partial t$:
>
> $$
> \vec E\cdot\vec J = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec E\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B) - \varepsilon_0\vec E\cdot\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> The product rule $\nabla\cdot(\vec E\times\vec B) = \vec B\cdot(\nabla\times\vec E) - \vec E\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B)$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]) and Faraday's law $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial t$ give
>
> $$
> \vec E\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B) = -\vec B\cdot\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t} - \nabla\cdot(\vec E\times\vec B) .
> $$
>
> With $\vec B\cdot\partial_t\vec B = \tfrac12\partial_t(B^2)$ and $\vec E\cdot\partial_t\vec E = \tfrac12\partial_t(E^2)$ (Griffiths eq. (8.7)),
>
> $$
> \vec E\cdot\vec J = -\frac12\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Bigl(\varepsilon_0E^2 + \frac{1}{\mu_0}B^2\Bigr) - \frac{1}{\mu_0}\nabla\cdot(\vec E\times\vec B) = -\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} - \nabla\cdot\vec S ,
> $$
>
> which is the local form (Griffiths eq. (8.8)). Integrate over $\mathcal V$; the time derivative comes out of the integral over a fixed volume, and the divergence theorem ([[§32 Flux Integrals and the Divergence Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-32-1|452 Thm. §32.1]]) turns $\int_{\mathcal V}\nabla\cdot\vec S\,d\tau$ into $\oint_{\mathcal S}\vec S\cdot d\vec a$. By [[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]] the left side is $dW/dt$.

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level A
> - *The layer.* At level A the energy densities were inferred from two special devices and taken over to waves ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^rem-a12-3-1|§A12.3, Remark: Energy in the field itself]]), and the Poynting vector was defined for plane waves, where $S = uc$ followed from a moving-cylinder picture ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-2|Theorem §A12.3.2]]). Here both are fixed by a theorem of Maxwell's equations plus the Lorentz force law, valid for every field configuration. This delivers the "Poynting's theorem, level B" announced in [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves|§A12.3]].
> - *Field energy is real energy.* In electrostatics $W_{\text e} = \tfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$ could be read either as energy of the charges or of the field ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^rem-b2-3-1|§B2.3, Remark: What changed from level A, and where the energy is]]). Poynting's theorem makes the field reading necessary once energy travels: it leaves a source, is in transit in empty space, and arrives later. Nelson shows how the older picture survives: for two point charges the cross term of $\tfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int\lvert\vec E_1 + \vec E_2\rvert^2\,d\tau$ is exactly the Coulomb potential energy $q_1q_2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$, while the two self-energy terms are infinite but independent of $r$; this is why field energy does not superpose ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-4|Theorem §B2.3.4]]).
> - *The same structure as for strings.* For a wave on a string, $\partial_t\varepsilon + \partial_xP = 0$ with energy density $\varepsilon$ and power $P$ ([[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^thm-b5-3-1|WO Theorem §B5.3.1]]); Poynting's theorem is the three-dimensional field version, with a source term $-\vec E\cdot\vec J$ because the field also exchanges energy with matter.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.1.2 · Nelson ch. 35, §35.6, eq. (35.16)*

^rem-b9-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: What the theorem does and does not fix
> - It fixes $\oint\vec S\cdot d\vec a$ over **closed** surfaces. Reading $\vec S\cdot d\vec a$ as the power through an open patch is the natural interpretation, but adding to $\vec S$ any field with zero divergence (a curl) leaves the theorem unchanged; the location of energy flow is, strictly, not determined (Griffiths' footnote 3; on the location of field energy see [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^rem-b2-3-1|§B2.3, Remark: What changed from level A, and where the energy is]]).
> - Static fields can have $\vec S \ne \vec 0$: a bar magnet next to a charge has $\vec E\times\vec B \ne \vec 0$, with energy circulating in closed loops ($\nabla\cdot\vec S = 0$ and nothing changes). Such circulations matter for momentum and angular momentum ([[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields|§B9.2]]), not for energy balances.
> - Energy is conserved only for fields and charges together: $u$ alone changes whenever $\vec E\cdot\vec J \ne 0$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.1.2 and footnote 3*

^cau-b9-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.3: Poynting's Theorem in Linear Media
> In matter, let $\vec J_f$ be the free current and $\vec D$, $\vec H$ the auxiliary fields ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-5|Theorem §B8.4.5]]). The power delivered to the free charges in $\mathcal V$ is $\int_{\mathcal V}\vec E\cdot\vec J_f\,d\tau$, and
>
> $$
> \vec E\cdot\vec J_f = -\Bigl(\vec E\cdot\frac{\partial\vec D}{\partial t} + \vec H\cdot\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}\Bigr) - \nabla\cdot\vec S, \qquad \vec S = \vec E\times\vec H
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (8.46)). If the media are linear, $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$ and $\vec H = \vec B/\mu$ with $\varepsilon$, $\mu$ independent of time ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^law-b5-3-1|Law §B5.3.1]], [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^law-b7-2-5|Law §B7.2.5]]), the bracket is $\partial u/\partial t$ with
>
> $$
> u = \frac12\bigl(\vec E\cdot\vec D + \vec B\cdot\vec H\bigr)
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (8.47)), and Theorem §B9.1.2 holds with $u$, $\vec S = \vec E\times\vec H$ and $\vec J_f$ in place of $\vec J$. The energy given to the bound charges (polarizing and magnetizing the medium) is counted in $u$, as in the energy of a dielectric ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 8.23(a), eqs. (8.46)–(8.47), and footnote 24*

^thm-b9-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Repeat the derivation of [[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]] with Ampère's law in matter, $\vec J_f = \nabla\times\vec H - \partial\vec D/\partial t$: $\vec E\cdot\vec J_f = \vec E\cdot(\nabla\times\vec H) - \vec E\cdot\partial_t\vec D$. The product rule gives $\vec E\cdot(\nabla\times\vec H) = \vec H\cdot(\nabla\times\vec E) - \nabla\cdot(\vec E\times\vec H) = -\vec H\cdot\partial_t\vec B - \nabla\cdot(\vec E\times\vec H)$ by Faraday's law, which holds unchanged in matter. For linear media with constant $\varepsilon$, $\mu$: $\vec E\cdot\partial_t\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E\cdot\partial_t\vec E = \tfrac12\partial_t(\varepsilon E^2) = \tfrac12\partial_t(\vec E\cdot\vec D)$, and likewise $\vec H\cdot\partial_t\vec B = \tfrac12\partial_t(\vec B\cdot\vec H)$. Integrate as before.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B9.1.1: Energy Flow into a Resistor
> A straight cylindrical resistor of radius $a$ and length $L$ carries a steady current $I$ with a potential difference $V$ between its ends. Inside, the field is uniform, $E = V/L$ along the axis, and by continuity of tangential $\vec E$ the same axial field is present just outside. The magnetic field circles the axis, $B = \mu_0I/2\pi a$ at the surface ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-2|Theorem §A8.4.2]]). So at the surface
>
> $$
> S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\frac{V}{L}\,\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi a} = \frac{VI}{2\pi aL}, \qquad \oint\vec S\cdot d\vec a = -S\,(2\pi aL) = -VI ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec S = \mu_0^{-1}E\hat z\times B\hat\varphi = -S\hat s$ pointing **radially inward**: the power $VI$ enters through the side of the wire, not along it. The fields are steady, $\partial u/\partial t = 0$, so by [[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]] all of it goes into the charges, as Joule heat. Check from the inside: $\vec E\cdot\vec J = \sigma E^2$ for an Ohmic conductor ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^law-b8-1-1|Law §B8.1.1]]), and $\int\sigma E^2\,d\tau = \sigma(V/L)^2\pi a^2L = V^2/R = VI$ with $R = L/\sigma\pi a^2$ ([[§A5.4 Electrical Power#^thm-a5-4-2|Theorem §A5.4.2]]). Inside the wire $B(s) = \mu_0Is/2\pi a^2$, so the flux through a coaxial cylinder of radius $s < a$ is $VI\,s^2/a^2$, exactly the heat produced within that radius: the energy is used up evenly as it flows inward.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 8.1 (the interior check worked out here)*

> [!example] Example §B9.1.2: Energy Flow into a Charging Capacitor
> A fat wire of radius $a$ carries a constant current $I$ and has a narrow gap of width $w \ll a$, a parallel-plate capacitor that is uncharged at $t = 0$ (Griffiths Problem 7.34). In the gap, well inside the edge ($s \le b < a$, no fringing), $Q = It$ and
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{It}{\varepsilon_0\pi a^2}\,\hat z, \qquad \vec B = \frac{\mu_0Is}{2\pi a^2}\,\hat\varphi ,
> $$
>
> the second from the Ampère–Maxwell law around a circle of radius $s$, which encloses the displacement current $I s^2/a^2$ ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^ex-a12-1-1|Example §A12.1.1]]). Then
>
> $$
> u = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\Bigl(\frac{It}{\varepsilon_0\pi a^2}\Bigr)^2 + \frac{1}{2\mu_0}\Bigl(\frac{\mu_0Is}{2\pi a^2}\Bigr)^2, \qquad \vec S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec E\times\vec B = -\frac{I^2t\,s}{2\pi^2\varepsilon_0a^4}\,\hat s .
> $$
>
> $\vec S$ again points inward, from the sides of the gap, not along the wire. *Local check* ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]] with $\vec J = \vec 0$ in the gap): $\partial u/\partial t = I^2t/\pi^2\varepsilon_0a^4$ (the magnetic term is constant), and $\nabla\cdot\vec S = s^{-1}\partial_s(sS_s) = -I^2t/\pi^2\varepsilon_0a^4$; the sum vanishes. *Global check:* the energy in the cylinder $s < b$ grows at the rate
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl[\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\Bigl(\frac{It}{\varepsilon_0\pi a^2}\Bigr)^2\pi b^2w\Bigr] + 0 = \frac{I^2t\,b^2w}{\pi\varepsilon_0a^4} ,
> $$
>
> and the power flowing in through its side is $|S(b)|\,2\pi bw = I^2t\,b^2w/\pi\varepsilon_0a^4$, the same. The energy $\tfrac12Q^2/C$ that ends up in the capacitor ([[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]) is delivered by the fields around the wire, through the open rim of the gap.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 8.2 (solved here)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The local and integral forms are equivalent by the divergence theorem ([[§32 Flux Integrals and the Divergence Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-32-1|452 Thm. §32.1]]), exactly as for the continuity equation of fluid mechanics ([[§29 Conservation of Mass and Laplace's Equation#^thm-29-1|452 Thm. §29.1]]).
> - For monochromatic fields the time averages of $u$ and $\vec S$ are taken with $\langle\operatorname{Re}\hat a\operatorname{Re}\hat b\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(\hat a\hat b^*)$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|WO Theorem §B1.1.6]]); the irradiance of a plane wave in a dielectric is [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-4|WO Theorem §B8.1.4]], and plane waves in vacuum are treated in [[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum|§B10.1]].
> - In relativity $u$, $\vec S/c$ and the momentum flux of [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields|§B9.2]] are the components of one energy–momentum tensor, and Poynting's theorem and momentum conservation are the four components of one conservation law (Nelson ch. 35, eq. (35.13)); that formulation belongs to Electromagnetism level C — [[§C1.5 The Electromagnetic Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c1-5-3|Theorem §C1.5.3]], [[§C1.5 The Electromagnetic Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]]; for the free field, the tensor and its conservation are in Quantum Field Theory C1 — [[§C1b.7 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor#^ex-c1b-7-2|QFT Example §C1b.7.2]]. Its model, the conservation of charge as one Lorentz-invariant equation $\partial_\mu J^\mu = 0$, is [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-6|REL Theorem §B4.2.6]].
