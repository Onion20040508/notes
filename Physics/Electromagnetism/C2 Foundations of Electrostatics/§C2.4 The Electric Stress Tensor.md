Can the electrostatic force on a body be computed from the field on a surface around it, without knowing where the charge sits inside? Level B answered yes for electrodynamics: the Lorentz force on the charges in a volume is the flux of the Maxwell stress tensor through its surface minus the rate of change of the field momentum inside ([[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^def-b9-2-1|Def. §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]]), with tension along the field lines and pressure across them ([[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^rem-b9-2-2|Remark: Tension along the field lines, pressure across them]]). In statics only the electric part survives, and Zangwill derives it from the two electrostatic field equations alone. This section adds what the static case makes precise: the force per area across any surface element (the $2\theta$ rule), the force on a surface charge as a jump of stress (which settles the meaning of "$\sigma$ times a discontinuous field"), and the force on a point charge, which the stress tensor delivers without ever evaluating the charge's own infinite field.

## The stress tensor and the force on a volume

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.4.1: Electrostatic Force from the Electric Stress Tensor
> Let $\vec E$ be the total static field and $S$ a closed surface, bounding $\mathcal V$, that carries no charge. With the **electric stress tensor** (Zangwill eq. (3.93); the $\vec B = \vec 0$ case of [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^def-b9-2-1|Def. §B9.2.1]])
>
> $$
> T_{ij}(\vec E) = \varepsilon_0\Bigl(E_iE_j - \frac12\delta_{ij}E^2\Bigr),
> $$
>
> the force exerted on the charge inside $\mathcal V$ by the charge outside is (Zangwill eqs. (3.90)–(3.97))
>
> $$
> F_j = \int_{\mathcal V}d^3r\,\rho E_j = \int_{\mathcal V}d^3r\,\partial_iT_{ij} = \oint_SdS\,\hat n_iT_{ij}, \qquad \vec F = \varepsilon_0\oint_SdS\,\Bigl[(\hat n\cdot\vec E)\vec E - \frac12E^2\hat n\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> summed over repeated indices, $\hat n$ outward. Since $\partial_iT_{ij} = \rho E_j = 0$ where there is no charge, $S$ may be deformed through charge-free space without changing $\vec F$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.7, eqs. (3.90)–(3.97)*

^thm-c2-4-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Whose field.** $\int_{\mathcal V}\rho\vec E$ uses the total field. The part produced by the charge inside $\mathcal V$ exerts no net force on that charge ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-4|Theorem §C2.1.4]]), so the integral is the force from the charge outside (eq. (3.90)).
>
> **2. Eliminate $\rho$.** Gauss's law, $\rho = \varepsilon_0\partial_iE_i$ (Einstein summation over $i = 1, 2, 3$), gives $F_j = \varepsilon_0\int E_j\,\partial_iE_i$ (eq. (3.91)).
>
> **3. Product rule.** $E_j\,\partial_iE_i = \partial_i(E_iE_j) - E_i\,\partial_iE_j$.
>
> **4. Curl-free.** $(\nabla\times\vec E)_k = \epsilon_{kij}\partial_iE_j = 0$ for all $k$ is equivalent to $\partial_iE_j = \partial_jE_i$. Hence $E_i\,\partial_iE_j = E_i\,\partial_jE_i = \frac12\partial_j(E_iE_i) = \partial_i\bigl(\frac12\delta_{ij}E^2\bigr)$ (eq. (3.92)).
>
> **5. Collect.** $F_j = \varepsilon_0\int\partial_i\bigl(E_iE_j - \frac12\delta_{ij}E^2\bigr) = \int\partial_iT_{ij}$.
>
> **6. Divergence theorem**, for each fixed $j$ applied to the vector field $(T_{1j}, T_{2j}, T_{3j})$ ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]): $F_j = \oint dS\,\hat n_iT_{ij}$ (eq. (3.94)). Steps 2–6 need $\vec E$ differentiable in $\mathcal V$, true for smooth $\rho$; bounded densities follow by approximation, point charges by [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]]. In dyadic notation $\overleftrightarrow{T} = \hat e_iT_{ij}\hat e_j$, $\vec F = \oint dS\,\hat n\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T}$ (eqs. (3.95)–(3.96)), and substituting $T_{ij}$ gives the vector form (eq. (3.97)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Only the two electrostatic field equations were used: Gauss's law to remove $\rho$, $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ to make the integrand a total divergence. Level B needed the full Maxwell set and obtained an extra momentum term, absent in statics.
> - The force on what is inside is transmitted through each element of $S$ as $f_j = \hat n_iT_{ij}$, a force per area that depends only on the field there ([[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]]); $S$ need not follow the boundary of the charge.

> [!remark] Remark: The electric stress tensor in the larger picture
> - **Level B.** $T_{ij}(\vec E)$ is the electric half of the Maxwell stress tensor of [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^def-b9-2-1|Def. §B9.2.1]]; with time-dependent fields the force on the charges also contains $-\varepsilon_0\mu_0\frac{d}{dt}\int\vec S$ ([[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]]), which vanishes in statics. The definition is not repeated here; Theorem §C2.4.1 is its static derivation.
> - **The action.** The spatial block of the energy–momentum tensor of the field ([[§C1.5 The Electromagnetic Energy–Momentum Tensor|§C1.5]]) is the momentum current, which is *minus* this stress; the sign convention is the one of [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^cau-b9-2-1|§B9.2, Caution: Stress tensor or momentum flux: the sign]]. Zangwill's $T_{ij}$ is a stress: $\oint\hat n\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T}$ with outward $\hat n$ is the force on the contents.
> - **The medium picture.** That $T_{ij}$ is defined at every point, with or without charge, suggested to Faraday, Maxwell and their contemporaries that the vacuum is an elastic medium carrying stresses (Zangwill §3.7.1). The bookkeeping survives without the medium; in dielectrics the stress tensor is modified ([[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^def-c5-4-3|Def. §C5.4.3]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.7, §3.7.1*

## The force per area

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.4.2: Force per Area across a Surface Element
> Let the field at a surface element with outward normal $\hat n$ make the angle $\theta$ with $\hat n$, and let $\hat t$ be the unit tangent in the plane of $\hat n$ and $\vec E$, so $\vec E = E(\hat n\cos\theta + \hat t\sin\theta)$. The force per area that the field outside exerts across the element on the contents is (Zangwill eqs. (3.98)–(3.99))
>
> $$
> \vec f = \hat n\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T} = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2\bigl(\hat n\cos2\theta + \hat t\sin2\theta\bigr) :
> $$
>
> always of size $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2$, at the angle $2\theta$ from $\hat n$, so that $\vec E$ bisects the angle between $\hat n$ and $\vec f$. Field along the normal ($\theta = 0$): a pull outward, tension. Field tangent ($\theta = \pi/2$): a push inward, pressure. At $\theta = \pi/4$: pure shear.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.7.1, eqs. (3.98)–(3.99), Fig. 3.16*

^thm-c2-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1.** From Theorem §C2.4.1, $\vec f = \varepsilon_0[(\hat n\cdot\vec E)\vec E - \frac12E^2\hat n]$.
>
> **2.** Insert $\vec E = E(\hat n\cos\theta + \hat t\sin\theta)$, so $\hat n\cdot\vec E = E\cos\theta$ and $(\hat n\cdot\vec E)\vec E = E^2(\cos^2\theta\,\hat n + \cos\theta\sin\theta\,\hat t)$.
>
> **3.** Subtract $\frac12E^2\hat n$ and use $\cos^2\theta - \frac12 = \frac12\cos2\theta$, $\cos\theta\sin\theta = \frac12\sin2\theta$: $\vec f = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2(\cos2\theta\,\hat n + \sin2\theta\,\hat t)$.
>
> **4.** $|\vec f| = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2$ since $\hat n \perp \hat t$; $\vec f$ is $\hat n$ rotated by $2\theta$ toward $\hat t$, and $\vec E$ is $\hat n$ rotated by $\theta$, half-way.
>
> **What the derivation shows:** the sign of $\vec E$ does not matter (only $E^2$ and $2\theta$ enter), so a field-line picture without arrows determines the forces. Reading this on the bisector plane of two charges gives repulsion for like charges (field tangent, pressure) and attraction for unlike charges (field normal, tension): [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^ex-c2-4-1|Example §C2.4.1]].

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.4.3: Force on a Surface Charge from the Stress Jump
> A surface with charge density $\sigma$ separates regions 1 and 2; $\hat n$ is its unit normal pointing into region 1, and $\vec E_1$, $\vec E_2$ are the fields on the two sides. The force per area on the surface charge is (Zangwill eq. (3.52))
>
> $$
> \vec f = \hat n\cdot\bigl[\overleftrightarrow{T}(\vec E_1) - \overleftrightarrow{T}(\vec E_2)\bigr] = \frac{\sigma}{2}\bigl(\vec E_1 + \vec E_2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The same value is obtained for a charged layer of finite thickness with *any* profile across it, in the limit of zero thickness. At a conductor ($\vec E_2 = \vec 0$) it is the outward pressure $\frac{\sigma^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\hat n$ ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.4.3, eq. (3.52); §3.7.1 (the balloon) · the stress-jump and finite-layer derivations are worked out here*

^thm-c2-4-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Pillbox.** Enclose a patch $dA$ of the surface in a flat box with faces in regions 1 and 2. By [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]] the force on the enclosed charge $\sigma\,dA$ is the stress flux out of the box. As the box flattens, the side walls drop out; the outward normal is $+\hat n$ on face 1 and $-\hat n$ on face 2. Dividing by $dA$: $\vec f = \hat n\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T}(\vec E_1) - \hat n\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T}(\vec E_2)$.
>
> **2. Decompose.** Write $\vec E_k = E_{kn}\hat n + \vec E_{t}$; the tangential part is the same on both sides and $E_{1n} - E_{2n} = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]; Zangwill eqs. (3.50)–(3.51)). Then $\hat n\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T}(\vec E_k) = \varepsilon_0\bigl[E_{kn}\vec E_k - \frac12E_k^2\hat n\bigr]$ with $E_k^2 = E_{kn}^2 + E_t^2$.
>
> **3. Subtract.** The $E_t^2$ terms cancel. $E_{1n}\vec E_1 - E_{2n}\vec E_2 = (E_{1n}^2 - E_{2n}^2)\hat n + (E_{1n} - E_{2n})\vec E_t$. So
>
> $$
> \vec f = \varepsilon_0\Bigl[\tfrac12(E_{1n}^2 - E_{2n}^2)\hat n + (E_{1n} - E_{2n})\vec E_t\Bigr] = \varepsilon_0(E_{1n} - E_{2n})\Bigl[\tfrac12(E_{1n} + E_{2n})\hat n + \vec E_t\Bigr] = \frac{\sigma}{2}(\vec E_1 + \vec E_2) .
> $$
>
> **4. A layer of finite thickness.** Replace the sheet by a layer with density $\rho(\xi)$ across it, $\xi$ the normal coordinate, $\int\rho\,d\xi = \sigma$. As the thickness $h \to 0$, derivatives along the layer are negligible against $\partial_\xi$: Gauss's law becomes $\varepsilon_0\partial_\xi E_n = \rho$ and $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ gives $\partial_\xi\vec E_t = \vec 0$. The force per area is
>
> $$
> \int\rho\,\vec E\,d\xi = \varepsilon_0\int\partial_\xi E_n\,(E_n\hat n + \vec E_t)\,d\xi = \varepsilon_0\Bigl[\tfrac12E_n^2\Big|_2^1\,\hat n + E_n\Big|_2^1\,\vec E_t\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> the expression of step 3, whatever the profile $\rho(\xi)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The surface force is the jump of the stress across the sheet. Zangwill's own argument (the patch exerts no force on itself, so only the field of the rest acts, and that field is the average) is the one level B gave ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]]).
> - ⚑ In distribution language the force density of an infinitely thin sheet, $\sigma\delta(\xi)\vec E$ with $\vec E$ jumping at $\xi = 0$, is a product of $\delta$ with a function discontinuous at its support, which by itself has no value ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-8|QFT Theorem §CA.2.8]], 1). Step 4 shows why physics gives it one: every finite-thickness profile yields the same limit, the average field. Unlike the $\theta\cdot\delta$ of QFT Theorem §CA.2.8, here the answer does not depend on the regularization, because $\rho E_n = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\partial_\xi(E_n^2)$ is an exact derivative.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.4.4: Force on a Point Charge from the Stress Tensor
> Let a point charge $q$ sit at $\vec r_0$, and let $\vec E = \vec E_q + \vec E_{\text{ext}}$, with $\vec E_{\text{ext}}$ the field of all the other charges. For every ball $B_a$ of radius $a$ about $\vec r_0$ that contains no other charge,
>
> $$
> \oint_{\partial B_a}dS\,\hat n\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T}(\vec E) = q\,\vec E_{\text{ext}}(\vec r_0), \qquad \oint_{\partial B_a}dS\,\hat n\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T}(\vec E_q) = \vec 0 .
> $$
>
> The stress tensor thus gives the force on a point charge exactly and without regularization: the charge's own field, infinite at $\vec r_0$, is never evaluated there, and its stress integrates to zero. This is the rule of [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-4|Theorem §C2.1.4]], and the force of [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]].
>
> *Source: derived here from Zangwill §3.7, eq. (3.97)*

^thm-c2-4-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Expand the quadratic.** $\overleftrightarrow{T}$ is quadratic in $\vec E$: $T_{ij}(\vec E_q + \vec E_{\text{ext}}) = T_{ij}(\vec E_q) + T_{ij}(\vec E_{\text{ext}}) + C_{ij}$, with the cross term $C_{ij} = \varepsilon_0\bigl(E_{q,i}E_{\text{ext},j} + E_{\text{ext},i}E_{q,j} - \delta_{ij}\vec E_q\cdot\vec E_{\text{ext}}\bigr)$.
>
> **2. Self term.** On the sphere $\vec E_q = E_a\hat n$ with $E_a = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0a^2$, so $\hat n\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T}(\vec E_q) = \varepsilon_0[E_a^2\hat n - \frac12E_a^2\hat n] = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E_a^2\hat n$, a constant times $\hat n$. Each Cartesian component of $\hat n$ averages to zero over a sphere (e.g. $\int\sin\theta\cos\phi\,\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi = 0$), so the integral is $\vec 0$.
>
> **3. External term.** $\vec E_{\text{ext}}$ is smooth in $B_a$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec E_{\text{ext}} = 0$ and $\nabla\times\vec E_{\text{ext}} = \vec 0$ there; steps 2–6 of [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^der-c2-4-1|the derivation of Theorem §C2.4.1]] with $\rho = 0$ give $\oint\hat n\cdot\overleftrightarrow{T}(\vec E_{\text{ext}}) = \vec 0$.
>
> **4. Cross term.** $\hat n_iC_{ij} = \varepsilon_0\bigl[(\hat n\cdot\vec E_q)E_{\text{ext},j} + (\hat n\cdot\vec E_{\text{ext}})E_{q,j} - \hat n_j\,\vec E_q\cdot\vec E_{\text{ext}}\bigr]$. With $\vec E_q = E_a\hat n$ the last two terms are $E_a(\hat n\cdot\vec E_{\text{ext}})\hat n_j - E_a\hat n_j(\hat n\cdot\vec E_{\text{ext}}) = 0$, leaving $\varepsilon_0E_aE_{\text{ext},j}$. Hence
>
> $$
> \oint\hat n\cdot\overleftrightarrow{C}\,dS = \varepsilon_0\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a^2}\oint\vec E_{\text{ext}}\,dS = q\,\langle\vec E_{\text{ext}}\rangle_{\partial B_a} .
> $$
>
> **5. Mean value.** In $B_a$ each Cartesian component of $\vec E_{\text{ext}}$ is harmonic: $\nabla^2\vec E_{\text{ext}} = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec E_{\text{ext}}) - \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec E_{\text{ext}}) = \vec 0$. By the mean-value property ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]) its average over the sphere is its value at the centre: $q\vec E_{\text{ext}}(\vec r_0)$, for every admissible $a$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The force on a point charge is well defined although its self-field and self-stress are singular at the charge: the self-stress is isotropic about the charge and has zero net flux through any sphere around it.
> - The result is exact for every radius, not only as $a \to 0$; no limit, no smearing, no subtraction is needed. In this sense the stress tensor is the cleanest definition of "the force on a point charge", and it agrees with every symmetric regularization ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^der-c2-1-4|derivation of Theorem §C2.1.4]], step 6).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C2.4.1: Coulomb's Law from the Bisector Plane
> Charges sit at $z = -d$ and $z = +d$. Find the force on the one at $z = -d$ from the stress on the plane $z = 0$, for (a) two equal charges $q$, (b) charges $q$ (at $-d$) and $-q$ (at $+d$).
>
> *The surface.* Close the plane $z = 0$ by a hemisphere at infinity on the side $z < 0$ (Zangwill's bowl, Fig. 3.18). The field there falls at least like $1/r^2$, so $E^2\,dS \sim r^{-2} \to 0$ and the hemisphere contributes nothing; on the plane the outward normal is $\hat n = +\hat z$. A point $P$ of the plane at distance $s = d\tan\theta$ from the axis is at distance $d/\cos\theta$ from each charge, where $\theta$ is the angle between the axis and the line from a charge to $P$; each charge's field there has size $\frac{q\cos^2\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0d^2}$. The area element is $dS = s\,ds\,d\phi = d^2\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos^3\theta}\,d\theta\,d\phi$.
>
> (a) *Like charges.* By mirror symmetry the normal components cancel and the in-plane ones add: $\vec E\cdot\hat n = 0$, $E_\parallel = 2\cdot\frac{q\cos^2\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0d^2}\sin\theta$. The field is tangent ($\theta = \pi/2$ in [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]]): pure pressure, $\vec f = -\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E_\parallel^2\hat z$. Then
>
> $$
> \vec F = -\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\hat z\Bigl(\frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0d^2}\Bigr)^2\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\int_0^{\pi/2}d\theta\,\cos^4\theta\sin^2\theta\cdot d^2\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos^3\theta} = -\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\hat z\Bigl(\frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0d}\Bigr)^2\cdot2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin^3\theta\,d\theta .
> $$
>
> With $u = \sin\theta$, $\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin^3\theta\,d\theta = \int_0^1u^3du = \frac14$, and $\vec F = -\hat z\,\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\cdot\frac{q^2}{4\pi^2\varepsilon_0^2d^2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = -\hat z\,\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{(2d)^2}$: repulsion, Coulomb's law (Zangwill Example 3.6).
>
> (b) *Unlike charges.* Now the in-plane components cancel and the normal ones add: $\vec E = E_z\hat z$ with $E_z = 2\cdot\frac{q\cos^2\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0d^2}\cos\theta$, pointing from $+q$ to $-q$. The field is normal: pure tension, $\vec f = +\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E_z^2\hat z$, and
>
> $$
> \vec F = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\hat z\Bigl(\frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0d}\Bigr)^2\cdot2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^6\theta\,\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos^3\theta}\,d\theta = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\hat z\Bigl(\frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0d}\Bigr)^2\cdot2\pi\cdot\frac14 = +\hat z\,\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{(2d)^2} ,
> $$
>
> using $\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^3\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac14$: attraction, with the same magnitude. The two signs come from the field lines alone: parallel to the plane, they push; through it, they pull (Zangwill Fig. 3.17).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.7.1, Example 3.6 (part (a)); part (b) worked here*

^ex-c2-4-1

> [!example] Example §C2.4.2: Forces and Energies without Integrals
> (a) Two non-overlapping spherically symmetric distributions with charges $Q_1$, $Q_2$ and centres $R$ apart: show with the stress tensor that their interaction energy is $Q_1Q_2/4\pi\varepsilon_0R$ (Zangwill Problem 3.18). (b) Show that the slab-and-sheet distribution of [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^ex-c2-2-3|Example §C2.2.3]] exerts no net force on itself.
>
> (a) Two disjoint balls can be separated by a plane. Close it with a hemisphere at infinity around ball 1. This surface lies outside both distributions, where each produces the field of a point charge at its centre ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-5|Theorem §C2.2.5]]). The stress integral therefore equals the one for two point charges $Q_1$, $Q_2$ at the centres, and by [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]] and [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]] that is $Q_1$ times the field of $Q_2$ at the centre of ball 1: the Coulomb force $\frac{Q_1Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2}$, repulsive for like signs, at every separation. The interaction energy is the work against it to bring the balls in from infinity along the line of centres, $U_{\text{int}} = -\int_\infty^R\frac{Q_1Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R'^2}dR' = \frac{Q_1Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R}$ ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]], [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]]). Only the trivial radial integral was done; [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]] reached the same result by reciprocity.
>
> (b) Per unit area, enclose all the charge between the planes $x = x_-< 0$ and $x = x_+ > b$. The force on the whole distribution is the stress flux through these two faces. There $\vec E = \pm E_\infty\hat x$ with $E_\infty = (\sigma_0 + \rho_0b)/2\varepsilon_0$: equal and opposite on the two sides. On $x = x_+$, $\hat n = \hat x$ and $\vec f = \varepsilon_0[E_\infty^2 - \frac12E_\infty^2]\hat x = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E_\infty^2\hat x$; on $x = x_-$, $\hat n = -\hat x$ and $\vec E = -E_\infty\hat x$, so $\vec f = \varepsilon_0[(E_\infty)(-E_\infty\hat x) + \frac12E_\infty^2\hat x] = -\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E_\infty^2\hat x$. The sum is zero, with no reference to the inside, the sheet's own field included.
