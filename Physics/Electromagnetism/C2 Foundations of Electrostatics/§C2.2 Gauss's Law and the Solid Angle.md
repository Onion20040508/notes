What does Gauss's law add once the field of every charge distribution is known, and what does the solid angle have to do with it? Gauss's law in both forms has its home at level B, derived from $\nabla\cdot(\hat r/r^2) = 4\pi\delta^3$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]), and level A proved the closed-surface form by counting solid angles ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]); both are linked here, not repeated. Level C makes the solid angle a function of the observation point, defined for open surfaces too, and reads off what it does: it fixes the flux of a point charge through any surface, it jumps by $4\pi$ across the surface, and its level curves draw the field lines of axisymmetric fields. The second half asks why symmetry plus Gauss's law gives *the* field, and writes the potentials of planar, line-like and spherical distributions as integrals over the one- and two-dimensional fundamental solutions. The matching conditions that Zangwill rederives here (his §3.4.2) are [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]; the force on a charged surface (his §3.4.3) is [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]], derived anew in [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^thm-c2-4-3|Theorem §C2.4.3]].

## The solid angle

> [!definition] Definition §C2.2.1: Solid Angle Subtended by an Oriented Surface
> Let $S$ be a surface with a chosen unit normal $\hat n$ (an orientation) and $\vec r$ a point not on $S$. The **solid angle** that $S$ subtends at $\vec r$ is (Zangwill eq. (3.53))
>
> $$
> \Omega_S(\vec r) = \int_S d\Omega_S, \qquad d\Omega_S = dS\;\frac{\hat n\cdot(\vec r_S - \vec r)}{|\vec r_S - \vec r|^3} ,
> $$
>
> the area that the rays from $\vec r$ to the element $dS$ at $\vec r_S$ cut out of the unit sphere about $\vec r$, counted positive where $\hat n$ points away from $\vec r$ and negative where it points toward it. For a sphere of radius $R$ about $\vec r$ with outward normal, $d\Omega_S = \sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$ (eq. (3.54)). Level A used the same element for small patches ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^der-a2-2-2|§A2.2, derivation of Theorem §A2.2.2]], step 1).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.4.4, eqs. (3.53)–(3.54), Fig. 3.10*

^def-c2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.1: Properties of the Solid Angle
> 1. *Closed surfaces.* If $S$ bounds a region $\mathcal V$ and $\hat n$ points outward, $\Omega_S(\vec r) = 4\pi$ for $\vec r$ inside $\mathcal V$ and $0$ for $\vec r$ outside (Zangwill eq. (3.55)).
> 2. *Only the rim matters, up to $4\pi$.* If $S_1$ and $S_2$ have the same boundary curve and together bound a region $\mathcal V$, with $\hat n$ pointing out of $\mathcal V$ on $S_1$ and into $\mathcal V$ on $S_2$, then $\Omega_{S_1} - \Omega_{S_2} = 4\pi$ inside $\mathcal V$ and $0$ outside.
> 3. *The jump.* Crossing an open surface $S$ at a regular point in the direction of $\hat n$, $\Omega_S$ jumps by $-4\pi$.
> 4. *Harmonic.* Off $S$, $\Omega_S(\vec r) = \int_SdS\,\hat n\cdot\nabla_{\vec r}\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r_S|}$ is smooth and $\nabla^2\Omega_S = 0$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.4.4, eq. (3.55), Fig. 3.11 (item 1); items 2–4 derived here*

^thm-c2-2-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Closed surfaces.** As a function of the integration point $\vec r\,'$, the field $\vec F(\vec r\,') = (\vec r\,' - \vec r)/|\vec r\,' - \vec r|^3$ has zero divergence for $\vec r\,' \ne \vec r$ and $\nabla'\cdot\vec F = 4\pi\delta^3(\vec r\,' - \vec r)$ as a distribution ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]), and $\Omega_S(\vec r) = \oint_S\vec F\cdot\hat n\,dS$. Done without distributions:
> - $\vec r$ outside $\mathcal V$: $\vec F$ is smooth on $\mathcal V$, and the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]) gives $\oint_S\vec F\cdot\hat n\,dS = \int_{\mathcal V}\nabla'\cdot\vec F = 0$.
> - $\vec r$ inside $\mathcal V$: remove a small ball $B_\epsilon$ about $\vec r$. On $\mathcal V\setminus B_\epsilon$, $\vec F$ is smooth and divergence-free, so the flux out through $S$ equals the flux out of $B_\epsilon$ (normal pointing away from $\vec r$): $\int\frac{\epsilon\,\hat r\cdot\hat r}{\epsilon^3}\,\epsilon^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi = 4\pi$.
>
> Zangwill's version counts rays (Fig. 3.11): from an outside point every ray meets $S$ an even number of times, with entries and exits cancelling; from an inside point an odd number, leaving one exit per direction, $4\pi$ in all.
>
> **2. Two caps.** $S_1$ with its $\hat n$ and $S_2$ with $-\hat n$ together form $\partial\mathcal V$ with outward normal. Since reversing $\hat n$ reverses the sign of $\Omega$, $\Omega_{\partial\mathcal V} = \Omega_{S_1} - \Omega_{S_2}$, and item 1 gives the result.
>
> **3. The jump.** Near a regular point $p$ of $S$, let $S'$ be $S$ with a small dent pushed out in front of $S$ (the side $\hat n$ points to), same rim, same orientation, and let $\mathcal V$ be the thin region between them. On $\mathcal V$'s boundary the outward normal is $+\hat n$ on $S'$ and $-\hat n$ on $S$, so item 2 (with $S_1 = S'$, $S_2 = S$) gives $\Omega_{S'} - \Omega_S = 4\pi$ in $\mathcal V$ and $0$ outside it. Points just in front of $S$ near $p$ lie in $\mathcal V$, points just behind do not, and $\Omega_{S'}$ is continuous near $p$ because $S'$ stays away from $p$. Hence $\Omega_S(\text{front}) - \Omega_S(\text{back}) = [\Omega_{S'} - 4\pi] - \Omega_{S'} = -4\pi$.
>
> **4. Harmonic.** $\nabla_{\vec r}\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r_S|} = -\frac{\vec r - \vec r_S}{|\vec r - \vec r_S|^3} = \frac{\vec r_S - \vec r}{|\vec r_S - \vec r|^3}$, which gives the stated form. For $\vec r$ off $S$ the integrand is smooth in $\vec r$, derivatives pass under the integral ([[§CA.1 Exchanging Limits, Derivatives and Integrals#^thm-ca-1-1|QFT Theorem §CA.1.1]]), and $\nabla^2$ commutes with $\hat n\cdot\nabla$, while $\nabla^2(1/|\vec r - \vec r_S|) = 0$ for $\vec r \ne \vec r_S$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The $4\pi$ of item 1 is the $4\pi\delta^3$ of the inverse-square field: the solid-angle proof of Gauss's law and the divergence proof are the same identity, integrated or local.
> - $\Omega_S$ is a harmonic function determined by the rim up to multiples of $4\pi$, with a $-4\pi$ jump across whichever surface is used. Up to a constant factor it is the potential of a uniform dipole layer on $S$, $V = -\tau\Omega_S/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-8|Theorem §C3.1.8]]), and it returns in magnetostatics as the scalar potential of a current loop (Zangwill §3.4.4: "the solid angle will return").

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.2: Flux through Any Surface; Gauss's Law by Solid Angles
> The flux of the field of a point charge $q$ at $\vec r_q$ through an oriented surface $S$ not containing $\vec r_q$ is (Zangwill eq. (3.56))
>
> $$
> \Phi_E(S) = \int_SdS\,\hat n\cdot\vec E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\Omega_S(\vec r_q) .
> $$
>
> Consequences: (a) for a closed surface, by [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]] (1) and superposition, $\varepsilon_0\oint_SdS\,\hat n\cdot\vec E = Q_{\mathcal V}$ (eq. (3.39)); (b) a localized distribution of total charge $Q$ lying entirely in the half-space $z < 0$ sends the flux $\int_{z=0}dS\,\hat z\cdot\vec E = Q/2\varepsilon_0$ through the plane $z = 0$ (Zangwill Problem 3.9).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.4.4, eqs. (3.39), (3.55)–(3.56); Problem 3.9 (solved here)*

^thm-c2-2-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. One charge.** At $\vec r_S$ the field is $\vec E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\vec r_S - \vec r_q}{|\vec r_S - \vec r_q|^3}$ ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]]), so $dS\,\hat n\cdot\vec E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,d\Omega_S(\vec r_q)$ by [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]]. Integrate over $S$. The exact inverse square is used here: with $|\vec r_S - \vec r_q|^{-n}$, $n \ne 3$, the flux element would depend on the distance, not only on the solid angle (Zangwill: "Gauss' law is not valid for other laws of force").
>
> **2. Closed surfaces.** Theorem §C2.2.1 (1) gives $q/\varepsilon_0$ for a charge inside and $0$ outside. For a continuous $\rho$ with no charge on $S$, $\Phi_E = \int d^3r'\,\rho(\vec r\,')\,\Omega_S(\vec r\,')/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ (exchanging the $S$ and $d^3r'$ integrals is allowed: the kernel is bounded when the charge stays a positive distance from $S$, [[Fubini's Theorem]]), and $\Omega_S/4\pi$ is the indicator of $\mathcal V$, so $\Phi_E = Q_{\mathcal V}/\varepsilon_0$.
>
> **3. The plane.** Orient $z = 0$ by $\hat z$. From a point at depth $h > 0$ below it, every element faces away, and with $dS = 2\pi s\,ds$ for rings about the foot of the perpendicular,
>
> $$
> \Omega = \int_0^\infty\frac{h\,2\pi s\,ds}{(s^2 + h^2)^{3/2}} = 2\pi h\Bigl[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{s^2 + h^2}}\Bigr]_0^\infty = 2\pi ,
> $$
>
> half of the full sphere, for every depth. The integral converges absolutely, so step 2's exchange applies, and the flux is $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\rho\cdot2\pi\,d^3r' = Q/2\varepsilon_0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows:** the flux of a point charge through a surface depends on the surface only through its solid angle, and that is a property of the inverse square. A plane seen from one side is half the sky, whatever the distance.

> [!remark] Remark: What is new compared with levels A and B
> - **Layer.** Gauss's law is a theorem at every level. Level A proved it for closed surfaces by solid angles, with Coulomb's law as a principle ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]); level B by the divergence of the inverse-square field, for every distribution ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]). At level C, $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ is the time component of the field equations of the action, a constraint on the initial data ([[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]]), and needs no proof; the solid-angle argument becomes a statement about Coulomb's field, Zangwill's "derivation of (3.39) that does not make use of $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$".
> - **New content.** The solid angle as a function with a jump ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]]), fluxes through open surfaces ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]), and field lines as level curves of a flux ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.4, introduction and §3.4.4*

## Field lines from the flux

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.3: Axisymmetric Field Lines Are Level Curves of the Flux
> Let $\vec E = E_s\hat s + E_z\hat z$ be symmetric about the $z$ axis in a charge-free region. For a point $P = (s, z)$, let $\Psi(P)$ be the flux of $\vec E$ through a surface $\Sigma_P$ spanning the circle through $P$ about the axis, oriented toward $+z$, with the surfaces for nearby points chosen so that the region between them contains no charge. Then $\Psi$ is constant along each field line, and the field lines are the curves $\Psi = \text{const}$. For flat disks, $\Psi(s, z) = 2\pi\int_0^sE_z(s', z)\,s'\,ds'$ and
>
> $$
> \nabla\Psi = 2\pi s\,\bigl(E_z\,\hat s - E_s\,\hat z\bigr) \perp \vec E .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.4.4, Application 3.1, eqs. (3.57)–(3.59), Fig. 3.12 · the local form derived here*

^thm-c2-2-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Global (Zangwill's argument).** Let $P$ and $P'$ lie on one field line, and let $S_R$ be the surface of revolution swept by the arc $PP'$ about the axis. On $S_R$ the field is tangent, so its flux through $S_R$ is zero. $\Sigma_P$, $S_R$ and $\Sigma_{P'}$ form a closed surface enclosing no charge, so its total outward flux is zero ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]] (a)). With $P'$ on the $+z$ side, the outward normal is $+\hat z$-like on $\Sigma_{P'}$ and $-\hat z$-like on $\Sigma_P$, so $\Psi(P') - \Psi(P) + 0 = 0$ (Zangwill eq. (3.59), where one of the two fluxes is taken with the outward normal).
>
> **2. Local (for disks).** $\partial_s\Psi = 2\pi sE_z(s, z)$ by the fundamental theorem of calculus. Differentiating under the integral in $z$ and using $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac1s\partial_s(sE_s) + \partial_zE_z = 0$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates|§B1.1]], cylindrical divergence),
>
> $$
> \partial_z\Psi = 2\pi\int_0^s\partial_zE_z\,s'\,ds' = -2\pi\int_0^s\partial_{s'}(s'E_s)\,ds' = -2\pi sE_s ,
> $$
>
> since $s'E_s \to 0$ on the axis. Then $\nabla\Psi\cdot\vec E = 2\pi s(E_zE_s - E_sE_z) = 0$: $\Psi$ does not change along $\vec E$.
>
> **What the derivation shows:** in axial symmetry a charge-free field is described by one scalar, the flux $\Psi$ (Stokes' stream function), whose level curves are the field lines; the density of lines is $|\nabla\Psi|/2\pi s = |\vec E|$. Charges enter only through which spanning surface is used, since by [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]] the flux jumps by $q/\varepsilon_0$ when the surface is moved across a charge $q$.

## Symmetry and the potentials of symmetric distributions

> [!remark] Remark: Symmetry selects, uniqueness decides
> Gauss's law yields a field only together with symmetry ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law|§A2.3]]): rotation, translation and reflection invariance of $\rho$ fix the direction of $\vec E$ and the coordinates it depends on, so that $\hat n\cdot\vec E$ is constant on a well-chosen surface (Zangwill eqs. (3.40), (3.42), (3.44)). Zangwill, following Shaw, adds the logical point: a symmetry of $\rho$ only says that the symmetry operations map one solution into another. The symmetric field found with Gauss's law is *the* field because the solution is unique ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]], step 6; with conductors and dielectrics [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]). For charge that extends to infinity (infinite lines, planes, slabs) the Helmholtz route needs decay and fails; uniqueness is then restored by treating the infinite distribution as a limit of finite ones (Zangwill footnote 8), which selects, for example, the slab field that is odd about the slab's plane of symmetry.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.4.1, footnote 8*

^rem-c2-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.4: Potentials of Planar and Line-Like Distributions
> 1. A charge density depending only on $z$, with charge $\gamma(z')\,dz'$ per unit area in the layer $dz'$ and $\gamma$ localized in $z$, has, up to an additive constant (Zangwill eq. (3.48)),
>
> $$
> V(z) = -\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_0}\int dz'\,|z - z'|\,\gamma(z'), \qquad E_z(z) = \frac{1}{2\varepsilon_0}\int dz'\,\operatorname{sgn}(z - z')\,\gamma(z') .
> $$
>
> 2. A charge density depending only on $\vec s = (x, y)$, with charge $\Lambda(x', y')\,dx'\,dy'$ per unit length, has, up to an additive constant (eq. (3.47)),
>
> $$
> V(x, y) = -\frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\int dx'\int dy'\,\ln|\vec s - \vec s\,'|\;\Lambda(x', y') .
> $$
>
> The kernels $-|z|/2\varepsilon_0$ and $-\ln s/2\pi\varepsilon_0$ are fundamental solutions of $-\varepsilon_0\,d^2/dz^2$ and $-\varepsilon_0\nabla_\perp^2$, the one- and two-dimensional counterparts of $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$; neither vanishes at infinity, which is why the additive constant cannot be fixed by $V(\infty) = 0$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.4.1, eqs. (3.42)–(3.48)*

^thm-c2-2-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. One sheet.** A sheet $\sigma$ at $z = 0$: by the reflection symmetry $z \to -z$ and Gauss's law with a box of width $2|z|$ (Zangwill eq. (3.44); [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law|§A2.3]]), $E_z = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\operatorname{sgn}z$. Integrating $E_z = -dV/dz$: $V = -\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}|z|$ + const (eq. (3.45)).
>
> **2. Superpose sheets.** The layer between $z'$ and $z' + dz'$ is a sheet of charge $\gamma(z')dz'$ per area at $z'$; adding their potentials gives the formula for $V$, and $E_z = -dV/dz$ with $\frac{d}{dz}|z - z'| = \operatorname{sgn}(z - z')$ gives $E_z$ (differentiation under the integral: the integrand's derivative is bounded by $|\gamma|$, [[§CA.1 Exchanging Limits, Derivatives and Integrals#^thm-ca-1-1|QFT Theorem §CA.1.1]]).
>
> **3. Check as distributions.** For a test function $\eta(z)$, $\int|z|\,\eta''(z)\,dz = \int_0^\infty z\eta'' - \int_{-\infty}^0z\eta''$; integrating each by parts, $\int_0^\infty z\eta''\,dz = [z\eta']_0^\infty - \int_0^\infty\eta' = \eta(0)$ and $-\int_{-\infty}^0z\eta''\,dz = -[z\eta']_{-\infty}^0 + \int_{-\infty}^0\eta' = \eta(0)$. So $\frac{d^2}{dz^2}|z| = 2\delta(z)$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]), and $-\varepsilon_0\frac{d^2}{dz^2}\bigl(-\frac{|z|}{2\varepsilon_0}\bigr) = \delta(z)$: the kernel is a fundamental solution, and $V'' = -\gamma/\varepsilon_0$ follows as for [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^rem-c2-1-1|Remark: The Coulomb potential as a fundamental solution]].
>
> **4. One line, then many.** A line of charge $\lambda$ per length: by symmetry and Gauss's law with a coaxial cylinder, $\vec E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0s}\hat s$ (eq. (3.42)), and integrating, $V = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln s$ + const (eq. (3.43)); the constant is the divergent $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln2L$ of the finite segment ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-6|Theorem §C2.1.6]]). Superposing lines $\Lambda\,dx'\,dy'$ gives the formula. As distributions in the plane, $\nabla_\perp^2\ln s = 2\pi\delta^2(\vec s)$ ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-5|452 Thm. §17.5]]), so $-\varepsilon_0\nabla_\perp^2\bigl(-\frac{\ln s}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\bigr) = \delta^2$.
>
> **What the derivation shows:** the three kernels $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}$, $-\frac{\ln s}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}$, $-\frac{|z|}{2\varepsilon_0}$ are one object, the fundamental solution of the Laplacian in dimension 3, 2, 1. ⚑ In dimensions 1 and 2 it grows at infinity, so the zero of potential must be put at a finite point; the field is unaffected. Logarithmic potentials are developed in [[§C7.5★ Logarithmic Potentials and the Poisson–Boltzmann Equation|§C7.5★]].

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.5: Potential of a Spherically Symmetric Distribution
> A spherically symmetric density $\rho(r)$ of finite total charge, with $\int^\infty r\rho\,dr$ convergent, has (Zangwill Example 3.4)
>
> $$
> V(r) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0r}\int_0^rdr'\,r'^2\rho(r') + \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_r^\infty dr'\,r'\rho(r') .
> $$
>
> The charge inside radius $r$ acts as a point charge at the centre; each shell outside contributes the constant potential it has everywhere inside itself.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.4.1, Example 3.4*

^thm-c2-2-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Field.** With $Q(r) = 4\pi\int_0^rdr'\,r'^2\rho(r')$, symmetry and Gauss's law give $\vec E = \frac{Q(r)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\hat r$ (Zangwill eq. (3.40)).
>
> **2. Potential.** With $V(\infty) = 0$ and the path along $\hat r$, $V(r) = -\int_\infty^rE\,dr' = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_r^\infty\frac{Q(r')}{r'^2}\,dr'$.
>
> **3. Integrate by parts** with $\frac{d}{dr'}\bigl(-\frac{1}{r'}\bigr) = \frac{1}{r'^2}$ and $Q'(r') = 4\pi r'^2\rho(r')$:
>
> $$
> \int_r^\infty\frac{Q(r')}{r'^2}\,dr' = \Bigl[-\frac{Q(r')}{r'}\Bigr]_r^\infty + \int_r^\infty\frac{4\pi r'^2\rho(r')}{r'}\,dr' = \frac{Q(r)}{r} + 4\pi\int_r^\infty r'\rho(r')\,dr' ,
> $$
>
> the boundary term at infinity vanishing because the total charge is finite. Dividing by $4\pi\varepsilon_0$ gives the result.
>
> **What the derivation shows:** the two terms are the potential at $r$ of the inner charge, concentrated at the centre, and of the outer shells, each contributing $\frac{dq}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r'}$, its value on and inside itself. At $r = 0$ only the second term survives: $V(0) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_0^\infty r'\rho\,dr'$.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C2.2.1: A Point Charge in a Uniform Field
> A point charge $q > 0$ sits at the origin in a uniform field $E_0\hat z$. Find the field lines.
>
> Use [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]] with $\Sigma_P$ the spherical cap about $q$ through $P = (r, \theta)$, polar angles $0$ to $\theta$: these caps never cross the charge, so $\Psi$ is continuous. Its flux has two parts.
> - *The charge:* by [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], $\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Omega$ with $\Omega = \int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\int_0^\theta\sin\theta'\,d\theta' = 2\pi(1 - \cos\theta)$ (Zangwill eq. (3.58)).
> - *The uniform field:* it is divergence-free, so its flux is the same through every surface with the same rim, for instance the flat disk of radius $r\sin\theta$: $E_0\pi r^2\sin^2\theta$.
>
> So $\Psi = \frac{q}{2\varepsilon_0}(1 - \cos\theta) + \pi E_0r^2\sin^2\theta$ (eq. (3.57) with the cap's solid angle). Setting $\Psi$ = const and multiplying by $2\varepsilon_0/q$, with $a = 2\pi\varepsilon_0E_0/q$ and a constant $b$ labelling the line,
>
> $$
> a\,r^2\sin^2\theta - \cos\theta = -b \qquad\text{(Zangwill eq. (3.60))} .
> $$
>
> *Reading the family.* Solving, $r^2 = (\cos\theta - b)/(a\sin^2\theta)$, which needs $\cos\theta > b$.
> - $-1 < b < 1$: the line leaves the charge ($r \to 0$) at the angle $\theta_0 = \arccos b$ and runs off to $z \to +\infty$ at the distance $\sqrt{(1 - b)/a}$ from the axis.
> - $b < -1$: a line of the external field, arriving from $z = -\infty$ at distance $\sqrt{(-1 - b)/a}$ from the axis and leaving at $\sqrt{(1 - b)/a}$, bent around the charge.
> - $b = -1$: the separatrix, $r = 1/\sqrt{a(1 - \cos\theta)}$, together with the negative axis. It meets the axis at $r = 1/\sqrt{2a} = \sqrt{q/4\pi\varepsilon_0E_0}$, the null point where the charge's field cancels $E_0$, and widens to the radius $\sqrt{2/a}$. Check: the flux of $E_0$ through that cross-section, $\pi E_0\cdot2/a = q/\varepsilon_0$, is the whole flux of the charge.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.4.4, Application 3.1, eqs. (3.57)–(3.60), Fig. 3.12*

> [!example] Example §C2.2.2: Exponential Charge Densities in One, Two and Three Dimensions
> Find $\vec E$ for (a) $\rho = \rho_0e^{-\kappa|x|}$, (b) $\rho = \rho_0e^{-\kappa s}$, (c) $\rho = \rho_0e^{-\kappa r}$, with $\kappa > 0$.
>
> (a) *Planar.* By the symmetry $x \to -x$, $E_x(0) = 0$ and $E_x$ is odd; a box from $-x$ to $x$ with face area $A$ gives $2E_xA = \frac{A}{\varepsilon_0}\int_{-x}^x\rho_0e^{-\kappa|x'|}dx' = \frac{2A\rho_0}{\varepsilon_0\kappa}(1 - e^{-\kappa x})$ for $x > 0$, so
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{\rho_0}{\varepsilon_0\kappa}\bigl(1 - e^{-\kappa|x|}\bigr)\operatorname{sgn}(x)\,\hat x .
> $$
>
> Far away this is the field of a sheet with $\sigma = 2\rho_0/\kappa$, the total charge per area. The field does not decay, so this is the solution selected by symmetry among those allowed for an infinite distribution ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^rem-c2-2-2|Remark: Symmetry selects, uniqueness decides]]); [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]] gives the same.
>
> (b) *Cylindrical.* A coaxial cylinder of radius $s$ and length $\ell$ encloses $\ell\cdot2\pi\rho_0\int_0^ss'e^{-\kappa s'}ds' = \ell\,\frac{2\pi\rho_0}{\kappa^2}\bigl[1 - (1 + \kappa s)e^{-\kappa s}\bigr]$ (integrate by parts once), so
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{\rho_0}{\varepsilon_0\kappa^2s}\bigl[1 - (1 + \kappa s)e^{-\kappa s}\bigr]\hat s ,
> $$
>
> which grows like $\rho_0s/2\varepsilon_0$ near the axis and falls like $\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0s$ with $\lambda = 2\pi\rho_0/\kappa^2$ far away.
>
> (c) *Spherical.* $Q(r) = 4\pi\rho_0\int_0^rr'^2e^{-\kappa r'}dr' = \frac{8\pi\rho_0}{\kappa^3}\bigl[1 - e^{-\kappa r}\bigl(1 + \kappa r + \tfrac12\kappa^2r^2\bigr)\bigr]$ (integrate by parts twice), so
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{2\rho_0}{\varepsilon_0\kappa^3r^2}\Bigl[1 - e^{-\kappa r}\Bigl(1 + \kappa r + \frac{\kappa^2r^2}{2}\Bigr)\Bigr]\hat r .
> $$
>
> Near the centre the bracket is $\kappa^3r^3/6 + O(r^4)$ and $E \approx \rho_0r/3\varepsilon_0$, the field inside a uniform ball; far away $E \to Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$ with $Q = 8\pi\rho_0/\kappa^3$. [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-5|Theorem §C2.2.5]] gives the potential: the second term is $\frac{\rho_0}{\varepsilon_0}\int_r^\infty r'e^{-\kappa r'}dr' = \frac{\rho_0}{\varepsilon_0\kappa^2}(1 + \kappa r)e^{-\kappa r}$, and adding $Q(r)/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$ the $\kappa^2r^2$ and part of the $\kappa r$ terms cancel:
>
> $$
> V(r) = \frac{2\rho_0}{\varepsilon_0\kappa^3r}\Bigl[1 - \Bigl(1 + \frac{\kappa r}{2}\Bigr)e^{-\kappa r}\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> with $V(0) = \rho_0/\varepsilon_0\kappa^2$ (the bracket is $\kappa r/2 - \kappa^3r^3/12 + \dots$) and $-dV/dr$ equal to the field above.
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 7, Problem 2 (Zangwill Problem 3.5), solved here*

> [!example] Example §C2.2.3: A Charged Slab and a Charged Sheet
> Let $\rho(x, y, z) = \sigma_0\,\delta(x) + \rho_0\,\theta(x) - \rho_0\,\theta(x - b)$: a sheet at $x = 0$ and a slab $0 < x < b$ ($\theta$ the step function). (a) Find $\vec E$. (b) Show that $\rho$ exerts no net force on itself.
>
> (a) There is no reflection symmetry to use, but [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]] (1) applies directly with $\gamma(x') = \sigma_0\delta(x') + \rho_0$ on $[0, b]$:
>
> $$
> E_x(x) = \frac{\sigma_0}{2\varepsilon_0}\operatorname{sgn}x + \frac{\rho_0}{2\varepsilon_0}\int_0^b\operatorname{sgn}(x - x')\,dx' .
> $$
>
> The last integral is $-b$ for $x < 0$, $x - (b - x) = 2x - b$ for $0 < x < b$, and $b$ for $x > b$. Hence
>
> $$
> E_x = -\frac{\sigma_0 + \rho_0b}{2\varepsilon_0}\ (x < 0), \qquad E_x = \frac{\sigma_0 + \rho_0(2x - b)}{2\varepsilon_0}\ (0 < x < b), \qquad E_x = \frac{\sigma_0 + \rho_0b}{2\varepsilon_0}\ (x > b) .
> $$
>
> Checks: the jump at $x = 0$ is $\sigma_0/\varepsilon_0$; $E_x$ is continuous at $x = b$; $dE_x/dx = \rho_0/\varepsilon_0$ inside the slab; outside, the field of a sheet with the total charge per area $\sigma_0 + \rho_0b$.
>
> (b) Per unit area, split the force into four parts.
> - *Slab on itself:* $\int_0^b\rho_0\cdot\frac{\rho_0(2x - b)}{2\varepsilon_0}dx = \frac{\rho_0^2}{2\varepsilon_0}[x^2 - bx]_0^b = 0$, as [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-4|Theorem §C2.1.4]] demands for a bounded density.
> - *Sheet on slab:* $\int_0^b\rho_0\cdot\frac{\sigma_0}{2\varepsilon_0}dx = \frac{\sigma_0\rho_0b}{2\varepsilon_0}$.
> - *Slab on sheet:* the slab's field at $x = 0$ is $-\frac{\rho_0b}{2\varepsilon_0}$, giving $-\frac{\sigma_0\rho_0b}{2\varepsilon_0}$: Newton's third law.
> - *Sheet on itself:* $\sigma_0\cdot\frac{\sigma_0}{2\varepsilon_0}\operatorname{sgn}(0)$, which has no value. As a distribution it is $\delta(x)\operatorname{sgn}(x)$, the product of $\delta$ with a function that jumps at its support, the kind of product that [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-8|QFT Theorem §CA.2.8]] (1) shows to be undefined. Any physical sheet has a thickness, and then the sheet is a bounded density whose self-force is zero for every profile (Theorem §C2.1.4); equivalently the force on a surface charge uses the average field, which is $0$ here ([[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^thm-c2-4-3|Theorem §C2.4.3]]).
>
> The total is $0$. The stress tensor confirms it without any of these integrals ([[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^ex-c2-4-2|Example §C2.4.2]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 7, Problem 3 (Zangwill Problem 3.8), solved here*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The closed-surface case of Theorem §C2.2.1 is the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]) applied away from a point singularity; the two-dimensional fundamental solution in Theorem §C2.2.4 is [[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-5|452 Thm. §17.5]].
> - Gravitation obeys the same Gauss law with $1/\varepsilon_0 \to -4\pi G$; the shell result behind Theorem §C2.2.5 is [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|CM Theorem §A8.1.5]].
> - The stream function $\Psi$ of Theorem §C2.2.3 is the electrostatic version of Stokes' stream function for axisymmetric incompressible flow; in both, a divergence-free field in axial symmetry is fixed by one scalar.
