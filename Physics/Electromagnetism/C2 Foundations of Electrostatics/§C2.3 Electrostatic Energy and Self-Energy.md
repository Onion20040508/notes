What is the energy of a static charge distribution, and what is the energy of a point charge? Level B derived the total energy $\frac12\int\rho V = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int E^2$, the interaction energy and the failure of superposition for energy ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]], [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-4|Theorem §B2.3.4]]), and noted that the field form is positive while pair sums need not be ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^rem-b2-3-2|Remark: Always positive, or not? Self-energy]]). Level C separates two quantities that Zangwill keeps apart: the **potential energy** of a distribution in a field it does not produce, defined through the force, and the **total energy** of assembling a distribution, defined through work. It derives the Coulomb force from the first, Earnshaw's theorem and Green's reciprocity relation from it, assembles the second by uniform charging, shows that the interaction part of the total energy *is* the potential energy, and then asks in what sense the energy of a point charge exists. Answer: as a distribution it does not; regularized, it is a position-independent divergent constant, and the pair sum is what remains.

> [!caution] Caution: Symbols for energy
> - $U_E$: the **total electrostatic energy**, the work to assemble a distribution from charge dispersed at infinity ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]). Zangwill and the user's PHY 505 notes write $U_E$; Griffiths and level B write $W$ ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^cau-b2-3-1|§B2.3, Caution: Notation for energy]]); the 2023 typed notes compute it as the field Hamiltonian $H$.
> - $U_{\text{pot}}$: the **potential energy** of a distribution in an external potential ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]). **Zangwill writes $V_E$**, which clashes with the potential $V$ of these notes ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: The potential is V]]); the user's notes write $U = q\phi$.
> - $U_{\text{int}}$: the **interaction energy** of two parts of a distribution ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]]); level B's $W_{\text{int}}$, the user's notes' $\tilde W$, Zangwill's $V_E$ again (his eq. (3.83)), since it equals the potential energy ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]]).
> - $U_{\text{self}}$: the **self-energy** of one part in isolation (the user's notes' $U_{\text{self}}$). The letter $W$ is kept for work done by an agent (Zangwill's $W_{12}$).

## Potential energy

> [!definition] Definition §C2.3.1: Potential Energy in an External Field
> Let $V_{\text{ext}}$ be the potential of sources that are held fixed: they are neither moved nor changed when the distribution $\rho$ moves. The **potential energy** of $\rho$ in this field is (Zangwill eqs. (3.63)–(3.64))
>
> $$
> U_{\text{pot}} = \int d^3r\,\rho(\vec r)\,V_{\text{ext}}(\vec r), \qquad U_{\text{pot}} = qV_{\text{ext}}(\vec r) \ \text{ for a point charge} .
> $$
>
> It is meant as the potential energy of mechanics, $\delta U = -\vec F\cdot\delta\vec s$ for a displacement $\delta\vec s$ (eq. (3.61); [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^def-a4-4-1|CM Def. §A4.4.1]], [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|CM Theorem §A4.5.2]]); [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]] checks that it is.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.5, eqs. (3.61)–(3.64) · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part IV §44.1*

^def-c2-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.1: The Coulomb Force from the Variation of the Potential Energy
> For a rigid displacement $\delta\vec s$ of a localized distribution $\rho$ in a fixed external potential (Zangwill eqs. (3.65)–(3.69)),
>
> $$
> \delta U_{\text{pot}} = -\vec F\cdot\delta\vec s + O(|\delta\vec s|^2), \qquad \vec F = -\frac{\partial U_{\text{pot}}}{\partial\vec s} = \int d^3r\,\rho(\vec r)\,\vec E_{\text{ext}}(\vec r) ,
> $$
>
> the Coulomb force of [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-4|Theorem §C2.1.4]]. For a point charge, $\vec F = -q\nabla V_{\text{ext}} = q\vec E_{\text{ext}}$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.5.1, eqs. (3.65)–(3.69), footnote 9, Fig. 3.13 · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part IV §44.1*

^thm-c2-3-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The displaced density.** After the displacement, the charge found at $\vec r$ is the charge that was at $\vec r - \delta\vec s$, so $\rho_{\text{new}}(\vec r) = \rho(\vec r - \delta\vec s)$ (Zangwill footnote 9: with the centre of the body as a parameter, $\rho(\vec r + \delta\vec s, \vec R + \delta\vec s) = \rho(\vec r, \vec R)$). The change is $\delta\rho(\vec r) = \rho(\vec r - \delta\vec s) - \rho(\vec r)$ (eq. (3.65)).
>
> **2. The change of energy.** $V_{\text{ext}}$ does not change, so $\delta U_{\text{pot}} = \int d^3r\,V_{\text{ext}}(\vec r)\,[\rho(\vec r - \delta\vec s) - \rho(\vec r)]$ (eq. (3.66)).
>
> **3. First order.** Taylor's theorem, $\rho(\vec r - \delta\vec s) = \rho(\vec r) - \delta\vec s\cdot\nabla\rho(\vec r) + O(|\delta\vec s|^2)$, gives $\delta U_{\text{pot}} = -\int d^3r\,V_{\text{ext}}\,\nabla\rho\cdot\delta\vec s$ (eq. (3.67)).
>
> **4. Integrate by parts.** For each Cartesian component, $\int V_{\text{ext}}\,\partial_i\rho\,d^3r = -\int\rho\,\partial_iV_{\text{ext}}\,d^3r$, with no boundary term because $\rho$ is localized. So $\delta U_{\text{pot}} = \int\rho\,\nabla V_{\text{ext}}\cdot\delta\vec s = -\int\rho\,\vec E_{\text{ext}}\cdot\delta\vec s$ (eq. (3.68)); comparing with $\delta U = -\vec F\cdot\delta\vec s$ gives $\vec F$ (eq. (3.69)).
>
> **5. Point charge, as a distribution.** For $\rho = q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)$ no Taylor expansion of $\delta^3$ is needed: $\int V_{\text{ext}}(\vec r)\,q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0 - \delta\vec s)\,d^3r = qV_{\text{ext}}(\vec r_0 + \delta\vec s)$, whose first-order change is $q\nabla V_{\text{ext}}(\vec r_0)\cdot\delta\vec s$. Steps 3–4 say the same in distribution language: the derivative of the translated $\delta^3$ is $-\delta\vec s\cdot\nabla\delta^3$, acting by moving the gradient onto $V_{\text{ext}}$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-8|QFT Theorem §CA.3.8]]). This needs $V_{\text{ext}}$ smooth at $\vec r_0$, which is what "external" provides: the charge's own singular potential is not part of $V_{\text{ext}}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - $\int\rho V_{\text{ext}}$ deserves the name potential energy: its gradient with respect to the position of the body is minus the Coulomb force.
> - ⚑ "External" is essential: if the sources of $V_{\text{ext}}$ respond to the motion (induced charge on conductors, polarization), the energy bookkeeping changes ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors|§C4.4]], [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics|§C5.4]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.2: Earnshaw's Theorem: No Stable Electrostatic Equilibrium
> In a charge-free region the potential has no local maximum or minimum, except where it is constant (Zangwill §3.3.3; [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]). Consequently (Zangwill §3.5):
> 1. a point charge acted on only by the electrostatic field of fixed charges has no position of stable equilibrium;
> 2. no configuration of point charges is in stable equilibrium under their mutual electrostatic forces alone.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.3.3, eqs. (3.21)–(3.22); §3.5, after eq. (3.64)*

^thm-c2-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. No strict extremum.** Suppose $V$ had a strict local minimum at a point $P$ of a charge-free region: $V > V(P)$ on every small sphere about $P$. By the mean-value property ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]) the average of $V$ over such a sphere equals $V(P)$, but an average of values all larger than $V(P)$ is larger than $V(P)$: contradiction. The same for a maximum. Zangwill's version: at a minimum $\hat n\cdot\nabla V > 0$ on a small sphere, so $\oint dS\,\hat n\cdot\nabla V > 0$ (eq. (3.21)) and, with $\vec E = -\nabla V$ and the divergence theorem, $\int d^3r\,\nabla\cdot\vec E < 0$ (eq. (3.22)), contradicting $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0 = 0$; this assumes the radial derivative is positive all over the sphere, which the mean-value argument does not need. A non-strict extremum forces $V$ to be constant near $P$ (Theorem §B3.1.2).
>
> **2. One charge.** The potential energy of $q$ is $qV(\vec r)$ ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]), with $V$ produced by the fixed charges and harmonic where $q$ can sit. Stable equilibrium requires a strict local minimum of the potential energy ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|CM Theorem §A4.7.2]]; [[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^rem-b6-5-1|CM Remark: Why the equilibrium is stable when U has a minimum]]): a minimum of $V$ for $q > 0$, a maximum for $q < 0$. Step 1 excludes both; where $V$ is locally constant the equilibrium is at best neutral.
>
> **3. Many charges.** The energy of the configuration, $U(\vec r_1, \dots, \vec r_N) = \sum_{i<j}q_iq_j/4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r_i - \vec r_j|$, would need a strict local minimum in $\mathbb R^{3N}$. Hold all charges but $q_i$ fixed: as a function of $\vec r_i$ alone, $U = q_iV_i(\vec r_i) + \text{const}$, where $V_i$, the potential of the others, is harmonic near $\vec r_i$. A strict minimum of $U$ would be a strict minimum of $q_iV_i$, which step 1 excludes.
>
> **What the derivation shows:** stability fails because the potential is harmonic where a charge can sit, i.e. because of Gauss's law. ⚑ Zangwill draws the conclusion that no classical model of matter made of point nuclei and electrons is stable: quantum mechanics is essential for the stability of matter. Constraints (charges held on a sphere, [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^rem-c2-3-3|★ Remark: Thomson's problem]]) or non-electrostatic forces evade the theorem.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.3: Green's Reciprocity Relation
> For two localized distributions $\rho_1$, $\rho_2$ with potentials $V_1$, $V_2$ (zero at infinity), (Zangwill eq. (3.70))
>
> $$
> \int d^3r\,\rho_2(\vec r)V_1(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r\int d^3r'\,\frac{\rho_2(\vec r)\rho_1(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} = \int d^3r'\,\rho_1(\vec r\,')V_2(\vec r\,') :
> $$
>
> the potential energy of $\rho_2$ in the field of $\rho_1$ equals that of $\rho_1$ in the field of $\rho_2$. It holds for bounded densities and for point charges at distinct points.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.5.2, eq. (3.70), footnote 10*

^thm-c2-3-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1.** Insert $V_1(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r'\,\rho_1(\vec r\,')/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]]) into the left side: this is the middle expression.
>
> **2.** For bounded localized densities the double integral converges absolutely ($1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ is integrable near the diagonal), so the integrations may be done in either order ([[Fubini's Theorem]]). Doing the $d^3r$ integral first and using $|\vec r - \vec r\,'| = |\vec r\,' - \vec r|$ gives $\int d^3r'\,\rho_1(\vec r\,')\bigl[\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r\,\rho_2(\vec r)/|\vec r\,' - \vec r|\bigr] = \int d^3r'\,\rho_1V_2$.
>
> **3. Point charges.** For $\rho_1 = q_1\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_1)$, $\rho_2 = q_2\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_2)$ with $\vec r_1 \ne \vec r_2$, both sides equal $q_1q_2/4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|$; each potential is evaluated away from its own singularity.
>
> **What the derivation shows:** reciprocity is the symmetry of the kernel $1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$, i.e. of the Green function. Zangwill: "The equality of the first and last terms in (3.70) is not obvious." With conductors it becomes the symmetry of the capacitance matrix ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix|§C4.3]]).

^der-c2-3-3

*Uses:* [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]], [[Fubini's Theorem]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.4: Spherical Distributions Interact as Point Charges
> Two non-overlapping, spherically symmetric distributions with total charges $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ and centres a distance $R$ apart have the potential energy and force of two point charges at their centres (Zangwill eqs. (3.71)–(3.72)):
>
> $$
> U_{\text{pot}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q_1Q_2}{R}, \qquad |\vec F| = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|Q_1Q_2|}{R^2} \text{ along the line of centres.}
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.5.2, eqs. (3.71)–(3.72), Fig. 3.14*

^thm-c2-3-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Let $\rho_1$ lie in a ball $B_1$ about $\vec c_1$ and $\rho_2$ in a ball $B_2$ about $\vec c_2$, disjoint, with $|\vec c_2 - \vec c_1| = R$.
>
> **1. Outside itself, a spherical distribution looks like a point charge.** By [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-5|Theorem §C2.2.5]] the first term alone survives outside the charge, so $V_2(\vec r) = \frac{Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec c_2|} = V_P(\vec r)$ for $\vec r \notin B_2$, where $V_P$ is the potential of the point density $\rho_P = Q_2\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec c_2)$.
>
> **2. Replace.** $\rho_1$ vanishes inside $B_2$, so $U_{\text{pot}} = \int\rho_1V_2 = \int\rho_1V_P$ (eq. (3.71), middle).
>
> **3. Reciprocity** ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]]): $\int\rho_1V_P = \int\rho_PV_1 = Q_2V_1(\vec c_2)$, by the sifting property; $V_1$ is smooth at $\vec c_2$ because $\vec c_2 \notin B_1$.
>
> **4. Step 1 again**, now for $\rho_1$: $V_1(\vec c_2) = Q_1/4\pi\varepsilon_0R$. This gives $U_{\text{pot}}$ (eq. (3.72)). Holding $\rho_1$ fixed and moving $\rho_2$ rigidly, [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]] gives the force $-\nabla_{\vec c_2}U_{\text{pot}}$, Coulomb's law along $\vec c_2 - \vec c_1$.
>
> **What the derivation shows:** reciprocity lets one replace *each* distribution by a point charge in turn, which a direct integration would hide in a six-dimensional integral. The result is exact for every separation at which the balls do not overlap.

## Total energy

> [!definition] Definition §C2.3.2: Total Electrostatic Energy
> The **total electrostatic energy** $U_E$ of a charge distribution is the total work an external agent must do to assemble it from a state in which all the charge is dispersed at infinity, quasistatically and without dissipation, so that the process is reversible (Zangwill §3.6). For point charges it is the pair sum $U_E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i<j}q_iq_j/|\vec r_i - \vec r_j| = \frac12\sum_iq_iV(\vec r_i)$, $V(\vec r_i)$ the potential of the other charges (Zangwill eqs. (3.73)–(3.75)); this is [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-2|Theorem §B2.3.2]], with the assembly of ready-made point charges ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-7|Theorem §C2.3.7]] says what is left out).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.6, eqs. (3.73)–(3.75) · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part IV §44.2*

^def-c2-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.5: The Total Energy by Uniform Charging
> For a bounded, localized density $\rho$ with potential $V$ (Zangwill eqs. (3.76)–(3.78)),
>
> $$
> U_E = \frac12\int d^3r\,\rho(\vec r)V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r\int d^3r'\,\frac{\rho(\vec r)\rho(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} ,
> $$
>
> the same for every way of assembling $\rho$. For a uniformly charged ball of charge $Q$ and radius $R$, $U_E = \frac35\,\frac{Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R}$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.6, eqs. (3.76)–(3.78) · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part IV §44.3*

^thm-c2-3-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Charging path.** Build $\rho$ by raising all densities together: at stage $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ the density is $\lambda\rho$, and by linearity of [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]] its potential is $\lambda V$.
>
> **2. One step.** Raise $\lambda$ to $\lambda + \delta\lambda$ by bringing the charge $\delta\lambda\,\rho(\vec r)\,d^3r$ from infinity to each element. Carrying a charge $\delta q$ from infinity to $\vec r$ against the potential $\lambda V$ costs $\delta q\,\lambda V(\vec r)$ ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]]). The potential changes during the step only by $\delta\lambda\,V$, which alters this work at order $(\delta\lambda)^2$; dropped. Summing over elements, $\delta W = \lambda\,\delta\lambda\int d^3r\,\rho V$.
>
> **3. Integrate.** $U_E = \int_0^1\lambda\,d\lambda\int\rho V = \frac12\int\rho V$ (eq. (3.78)).
>
> **4. Independence of the path.** The result is a functional of the final $\rho$ alone. The element-by-element assembly of [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]] gives the same expression, as it must: quasistatic work against conservative forces depends only on the initial and final configurations.
>
> **5. Double integral.** Insert $V$ from Theorem §C2.1.3 (eq. (3.76)).
>
> **6. The ball, built in layers** (eq. (3.77)). With $\rho = Q/\frac43\pi R^3$: when the ball has radius $r$, its surface potential is $V_S = \frac{\rho\frac43\pi r^3}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r} = \frac{\rho r^2}{3\varepsilon_0}$ (Zangwill uses Example 3.4, [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-5|Theorem §C2.2.5]]). Adding the shell $dq = 4\pi r^2\rho\,dr$ costs $V_Sdq$, so
>
> $$
> U_E = \int_0^R\frac{\rho r^2}{3\varepsilon_0}\,4\pi r^2\rho\,dr = \frac{4\pi\rho^2}{3\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{R^5}{5} = \frac{4\pi}{15\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{9Q^2}{16\pi^2R^6}\,R^5 = \frac35\,\frac{Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R} .
> $$
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The $\frac12$ comes from $\int_0^1\lambda\,d\lambda$: the charge added late works against the full potential, the charge added early against almost none. It is the same $\frac12$ that corrects double counting in the pair sum.
> - ⚑ Step 2 needs $V$ finite where charge is added. For a point charge the first $\delta\lambda$ already produces an infinite potential at its own location, and the energy diverges ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-7|Theorem §C2.3.7]]); Zangwill: "$U_E$ diverges for a single point charge, whether we try to evaluate (3.75) with $N = 1$ or consider (3.77) in the limit $R \to 0$."

*Uses:* [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]], [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]], [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]], [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-5|Theorem §C2.2.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: Positive energy, and where it sits
> - **The field form.** Eliminating $\rho$ with Gauss's law and integrating by parts gives $U_E = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int|\vec E|^2 \ge 0$ (Zangwill eqs. (3.79)–(3.80)); this is [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]] and is not repeated. For smooth densities of either sign the self-energy part is finite and positive (Zangwill §3.6.1).
> - **A density is not forced by the total.** Zangwill warns that (3.80) does not by itself justify the energy density $u_E = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}|\vec E|^2$: the equally valid $\frac12\int\rho V$ would suggest the density $\frac12\rho V$, which is zero wherever there is no charge. What fixes $u_E$ is dynamics: it is the time–time component of the energy–momentum tensor of the action ([[§C1.5 The Electromagnetic Energy–Momentum Tensor|§C1.5]]), and Poynting's theorem needs it to balance energy flow ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]).
> - **The energy as a Hamiltonian.** The PHY 505 typed notes (§7.5.6) take this route for two point charges: the field energy $H = \int(\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2 + \frac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2)$ splits as $H_1 + H_2 + \int\varepsilon_0\vec E_1\cdot\vec E_2$, the first two infinite and independent of the positions, the cross term equal to $q_1q_2/4\pi\varepsilon_0R$: "the Coulomb energy … is everywhere". The integral is the one of [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^ex-b2-3-1|Example §B2.3.1]]. (Their eq. (7.148) writes $q_1^2$ in $H_2$ where $q_2^2$ is meant.)
> - **The energy as an action.** $U_E = -L_E[V]$, minus the minimum of the static action of [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-2|Theorem §C2.1.2]].
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.6.1, eqs. (3.79)–(3.81) · PHY 505 typed notes (2023 offering), §7.5.6, eqs. (7.145)–(7.156) · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part IV §44.4–§44.5*

^rem-c2-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.6: Interaction Total Energy Is Potential Energy
> For $\rho = \rho_1 + \rho_2$ (bounded, localized), (Zangwill eqs. (3.82)–(3.84))
>
> $$
> U_E[\rho_1 + \rho_2] = U_E[\rho_1] + U_E[\rho_2] + U_{\text{int}}, \qquad U_{\text{int}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r\int d^3r'\,\frac{\rho_1(\vec r)\rho_2(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} = \int\rho_1V_2 = \int\rho_2V_1 ,
> $$
>
> so the interaction part of the total energy equals the potential energy of either part in the field of the other ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]), and its gradient gives the force between them. If $\rho_2$ only serves to produce a fixed external potential $V_{\text{ext}}$, dropping its constant $U_E[\rho_2]$ leaves
>
> $$
> U_E = \frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r\int d^3r'\,\frac{\rho_1(\vec r)\rho_1(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} + \int d^3r\,\rho_1V_{\text{ext}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.6.2, eqs. (3.82)–(3.84)*

^thm-c2-3-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Expand.** In the double integral of [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-5|Theorem §C2.3.5]], $\rho(\vec r)\rho(\vec r\,') = \rho_1\rho_1' + \rho_2\rho_2' + \rho_1\rho_2' + \rho_2\rho_1'$ (primes on functions of $\vec r\,'$): four terms, each with the factor $\frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}$.
>
> **2. Self terms.** The first two are $U_E[\rho_1]$ and $U_E[\rho_2]$.
>
> **3. Cross terms.** Renaming $\vec r \leftrightarrow \vec r\,'$ in the fourth (Fubini, as in [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]]) turns it into the third, because the kernel is symmetric. Together, $2\cdot\frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon_0} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}$ times one of them: $U_{\text{int}}$ (eq. (3.83)). By Theorem §C2.3.3 it equals $\int\rho_1V_2$ and $\int\rho_2V_1$.
>
> **4. Identification.** $\int\rho_1V_2$ is the potential energy of $\rho_1$ in the field of $\rho_2$ held fixed, so by [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]] its gradient with respect to a rigid displacement of $\rho_1$ is minus the force on $\rho_1$; the self term $U_E[\rho_1]$ does not change under a rigid displacement.
>
> **5. External potential.** $U_E[\rho_2]$ does not depend on $\rho_1$; for a fixed external source it is a constant and is dropped (eq. (3.84)).
>
> **What the derivation shows:** the electrostatic bookkeeping is consistent: the work to assemble and the energy that gives forces agree on the interaction part. ⚑ Zangwill makes the point because it fails in magnetostatics, where the interaction part of the total magnetic energy is not the potential energy that gives the force (his ch. 12; the sign issue reappears for conductors at fixed potential, [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors|§C4.4]]).

## Self-energy

> [!definition] Definition §C2.3.3: Self-Energy and Interaction Energy
> Split a distribution into parts, $\rho = \sum_i\rho_i$. The **self-energy** of a part is its total energy in isolation, $U_{\text{self}}[\rho_i] = U_E[\rho_i]$, and the **interaction energy** of two parts is $U_{\text{int}}[\rho_i, \rho_j]$ of [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]]. By that theorem, applied repeatedly,
>
> $$
> U_E = \sum_iU_{\text{self}}[\rho_i] + \sum_{i<j}U_{\text{int}}[\rho_i, \rho_j] .
> $$
>
> The split is a choice; for point charges it is the natural one, and the pair sum of [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]] is the second sum alone.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, Part IV §45.3 (Definition "Interaction energy vs. total energy") · Zangwill §3.6.2, eq. (3.82)*

^def-c2-3-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.7: The Self-Energy of a Point Charge
> 1. *As a distribution it does not exist.* For $\rho = q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)$, $\frac12\int\rho V$ asks for $V(\vec r_0) = \infty$: the product of $\delta^3$ with $1/|\vec r - \vec r_0|$ has no value ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-8|QFT Theorem §CA.2.8]], 3). Equally, $\vec E \propto 1/r^2$ is locally integrable and a distribution, but $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}|\vec E|^2 \propto 1/r^4$ is not locally integrable at the charge and defines no distribution ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-1|QFT Theorem §CA.2.1]], 2).
> 2. *Regularized, it diverges like $1/a$.* Spread over a sphere of radius $a$, $U_{\text{self}} = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0a}$; over a ball, $\frac35\frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a}$. The coefficient depends on the shape.
> 3. *The pair sum is what remains.* Replace point charges $q_i$ at $\vec r_i$ by uniform spheres of radius $a < \frac12\min_{i\ne j}|\vec r_i - \vec r_j|$. Then, exactly,
>
> $$
> U_E(a) = \sum_i\frac{q_i^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0a} + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i<j}\frac{q_iq_j}{|\vec r_i - \vec r_j|} .
> $$
>
> The first sum does not depend on the positions; forces and energy differences come from the pair sum alone.
> 4. ⚑ $U_E(a) = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int|\vec E|^2 \ge 0$ for every $a$, while the pair sum can be negative: the positivity is carried entirely by the divergent self-energies.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.6, after eq. (3.78); §3.6.1 · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part IV §45.1–§45.5 · items 1, 3 and 4 derived here*

^thm-c2-3-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. No value.** $\rho V$ with $\rho = q\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)$ and $V$ containing $q/4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec r_0|$ is a product of a distribution with a function singular exactly at its support; a nascent-delta computation gives $\delta_\epsilon\cdot\frac1r \sim \frac1\epsilon \to \infty$, as for $\delta^n(0)$ in QFT Theorem §CA.2.8 (3). For the field form, near the charge $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}|\vec E|^2\,d^3r = \frac{q^2}{32\pi^2\varepsilon_0}\frac{4\pi r^2\,dr}{r^4}$, and $\int_0\frac{dr}{r^2}$ diverges: $|\vec E|^2$ fails the local integrability required of a regular distribution (QFT Theorem §CA.2.1 (2)), while $|\vec E| \propto r^{-2}$ passes it.
>
> **2. Shell and ball.** A uniform sphere of radius $a$: $\vec E = \vec 0$ inside and $\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\hat r$ outside ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-5|Theorem §C2.2.5]]), so
>
> $$
> U_{\text{self}} = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int_a^\infty\Bigl(\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\Bigr)^24\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_a^\infty\frac{dr}{r^2} = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0a} ,
> $$
>
> also $\frac12\int\sigma V = \frac12q\cdot\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a}$. The ball is [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-5|Theorem §C2.3.5]]. Both diverge as $a \to 0$, with coefficients $\frac12$ and $\frac35$.
>
> **3. Exact decomposition.** Split into the spheres (Def. §C2.3.3). Each self term is step 2. Two spheres of radius $a$ with centres more than $2a$ apart do not overlap, so by [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]] their interaction energy is exactly $q_iq_j/4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r_i - \vec r_j|$, independent of $a$. Under a displacement of the charges that keeps them apart, only the second sum changes, so by [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]] it alone gives the forces.
>
> **4. Positivity.** For every $a > 0$ the density is bounded (as a surface density its field is bounded), and [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]] gives $U_E(a) = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int|\vec E|^2 \ge 0$. For two opposite charges, $U_E(a) = \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a} - \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0d}$, positive because $a < d/2$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The divergence is a property of the point limit. The classical cure, used tacitly whenever pair sums are written, is to subtract position-independent constants: a classical renormalization (the user's notes §45.5).
> - The subtracted constant is not universal: its coefficient depends on how the charge is smeared, so "the self-energy of a point charge" is not a number that the point charge alone determines.
> - The resolution of [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^rem-b2-3-2|§B2.3, Remark: Always positive, or not? Self-energy]] is item 4.

> [!remark] Remark: The classical electron radius, and what quantum electrodynamics changes
> - Setting the self-energy of a charge $e$ smeared over a radius $a$, of order $e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0a$, equal to the electron's rest energy $m_ec^2$ gives $a \sim r_e = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0m_ec^2} = 2.82\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm m$, the classical electron radius. Below such distances the classical electrostatics of a point electron is not self-consistent: the field energy alone would exceed the mass. The same small-scale structure of a charge spoils its classical equation of motion ([[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-7|Theorem §B11.4.7]], [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^rem-b11-4-3|Remark: What the dumbbell does and does not settle]]).
> - Zangwill calls the divergence a "characteristic pathology (remedied only by quantum electrodynamics)". In quantum electrodynamics the electron's self-energy still diverges, but only logarithmically in the cutoff, and it is absorbed into the observed mass: **mass** renormalization. The user's PHY 505 notes (§45.5) say that "charge renormalization properly addresses this issue"; charge renormalization is the separate absorption of vacuum polarization into the observed charge. The notes' remark that "real particles have finite size" does not cover the electron, for which no size has been observed.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.6, after eq. (3.78); §3.6.1 · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part IV §45.5*

^rem-c2-3-2

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Thomson's problem
> Which arrangement of $N$ equal point charges, held on a sphere that carries a uniform neutralizing charge, has the lowest energy? The constraint $|\vec r_k| = R$ evades [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]; the uniform shell exerts no tangential force and drops out, leaving the pair sum to be minimized. The minima are not always the most symmetric arrangements (for $N = 8$, a twisted rectangular parallelepiped, not a cube), local minima multiply exponentially with $N$, and for $q = R = 1$ the minimum energy is fitted well by $U_E(N) \simeq \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac12(N^2 - N^{3/2})$. The $N^2$ term is the energy of the charge $Nq$ smeared uniformly over the sphere, $\frac{(Nq)^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0R}$ ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-7|Theorem §C2.3.7]], 2), which counts each charge's own smeared self-energy; the $-N^{3/2}$ term corrects for the charges keeping apart (Zangwill Problem 3.24). The same formula predicts *charge inversion*: more counter-ions bind to a large ion than neutralize it.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.6.1, box "Thomson's Problem"; Problem 3.24*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C2.3.1: The Mean-Value Property by Reciprocity
> Let $V$ be produced by a density $\rho$ that vanishes in a ball of radius $R$ about the origin. Show that the average of $V$ over the sphere $S$ of radius $R$ equals $V(0)$ ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]).
>
> Apply [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]] with $\rho_1 = \rho$, $V_1 = V$, and the reciprocal system a uniformly charged sphere:
>
> $$
> \rho_2(\vec r) = \frac{q}{4\pi R^2}\,\delta(r - R), \qquad V_2(\vec r) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\times\begin{cases}1/R & r \le R\\ 1/r & r \ge R\end{cases}
> $$
>
> ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-5|Theorem §C2.2.5]]).
> - Left side: $\int\rho_2V\,d^3r = \frac{q}{4\pi R^2}\oint_SV\,dS = q\langle V\rangle_S$.
> - Right side: $\rho$ lives only at $r' > R$, where $V_2 = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r'$, so $\int\rho V_2\,d^3r' = q\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\rho(\vec r\,')}{r'}\,d^3r' = qV(0)$ by [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]].
>
> Hence $\langle V\rangle_S = V(0)$. Level B proved the same by averaging the potential of one outside point charge over the sphere ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^rem-b3-1-2|Remark: The same from Coulomb's law]]); reciprocity turns that average into the potential of the shell at the charge, which Gauss's law gives at once. (Zangwill Problem 3.10 proves it a third way, with Green's second identity.)
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.5.2, Example 3.5*

^ex-c2-3-1

> [!example] Example §C2.3.2: The Ionization Potential of a Metal Cluster
> Experiments on clusters of $N$ atoms of a monovalent metal (potassium) give an ionization potential $I_N = W + aN^{-1/3}$, with $W$ the work function of the bulk metal (Bréchignac et al. 1989, quoted by Zangwill). Model: the cluster is a sphere of radius $R$ made of $N$ atomic spheres of radius $R_S$, $R = R_SN^{1/3}$. Before ionization, the atom to be ionized is a uniform ball of charge $+e$ (radius $R_S$, the ion core smeared out) with a point electron at its centre; the other $N - 1$ atoms are neutral and smeared out, contributing no charge. After ionization, the electron is at infinity and the charge $+e$ left behind sits uniformly on the cluster's surface.
>
> *Initial energy* ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]]), parts: the ball, the electron.
> - Ball: $U_{\text{self}} = \frac35\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_S}$ ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-5|Theorem §C2.3.5]]).
> - Ball–electron: $U_{\text{int}} = (-e)\,V_{\text{ball}}(0)$, and by [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-5|Theorem §C2.2.5]] $V_{\text{ball}}(0) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_0^{R_S}r\rho\,dr = \frac{\rho R_S^2}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac32\frac{e}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_S}$, so $U_{\text{int}} = -\frac32\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_S}$.
> - Electron: the divergent self-energy of a point charge, the same constant before and after ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-7|Theorem §C2.3.7]], 3); it cancels in $I_N$.
>
> So $U_E^{\text{initial}} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_S}\bigl(\frac35 - \frac32\bigr) = -\frac{9}{10}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_S}$ (Zangwill eq. (3.87)).
>
> *Final energy:* the charged spherical surface, $U_E^{\text{final}} = \frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0R}$ (Theorem §C2.3.7, 2; eq. (3.88)), plus the same electron constant.
>
> *Result:* with $R = R_SN^{1/3}$,
>
> $$
> I_N = U_E^{\text{final}} - U_E^{\text{initial}} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_S}\Bigl[\frac{9}{10} + \frac12N^{-1/3}\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> the observed form, with $W = \frac{9}{10}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_S}$ and $a = \frac12\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_S}$ (eq. (3.89)). The example sorts the self-energies: the electron's (infinite, unchanged) cancels; those of the smeared positive charges (finite) do not, and they produce both terms. The size dependence $N^{-1/3}$ is the energy of charging a sphere of radius $R$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.6.2, Application 3.2, eqs. (3.85)–(3.89), Fig. 3.15*
