What does electrostatics keep of electrodynamics, and how is the field of a given charge distribution found? Level B built electrostatics on Coulomb's law as an empirical law ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^law-b2-1-1|Law §B2.1.1]]) and derived the field equations from it ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]). Level C runs the argument the other way, as Zangwill does. Maxwell's equations are theorems of the action ([[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity|§C1.2]]). Their static limit gives the two electrostatic field equations and a static action for the potential. The Helmholtz theorem ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]) then returns the Coulomb integrals, and Coulomb's law of force becomes a theorem. The section fixes the notation for the potential and ends with what Zangwill calls *summation problems*, where $\rho$ is given everywhere and only an integral remains: scaling, the charged line segment, the cube and the shell with a hole. When matter is present, $\rho$ rearranges itself and the problem becomes a boundary-value problem (chapters C4–C7).

> [!caution] Caution: The potential is V; Zangwill writes φ
> - These notes write the electrostatic potential as $V(\vec r)$, as levels A and B do ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]]). **Zangwill and both sets of PHY 505 notes write $\varphi$ (or $\phi$).** Every formula quoted from them is translated $\varphi \to V$. In the four-potential, $A^\mu = (V/c, \vec A)$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-1|REL Def. §B4.2.1]]).
> - Zangwill uses $V$ for other things: a volume (written $\mathcal V$ here) and $V_E$ for the electrostatic potential energy (written $U_{\text{pot}}$ here, [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^cau-c2-3-1|§C2.3, Caution: Symbols for energy]]). He writes $\rho$ for both the charge density and the cylindrical radius; the radius is $s$ here, as in [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates|§B1.1]].
> - Volume elements are $d^3r'$, as in Zangwill (Griffiths and level B write $d\tau'$); $|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ is the length of the separation vector $\vec{\mathcal R}$ of [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]].

## The static limit

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.1.1: The Static Limit of Maxwell's Equations
> Let $\rho$ and $\vec J$ be independent of time. Then the time-independent solutions of Maxwell's equations ([[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]], [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-4|Theorem §C1.2.4]]; [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]) obey two uncoupled pairs,
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \quad \nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \nabla\cdot\vec B = 0, \quad \nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J ,
> $$
>
> which are consistent only if $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$. The potentials can be chosen time-independent, with $\vec E = -\nabla V$ and $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$; the gauge freedom left in $V$ is an additive constant. **Electrostatics** is the case $\vec J = \vec 0$ (charges at rest): for fields that vanish at infinity, $\vec B = \vec 0$, and everything is contained in the electric pair, equivalently in Poisson's equation $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.1, eqs. (3.1)–(3.2) · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part IV §42.4*

^thm-c2-1-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The full equations.** $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$, $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial_t\vec B$, $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E$.
>
> **2. Drop the time derivatives.** For $\partial_t\vec E = \partial_t\vec B = \vec 0$, Faraday's law becomes $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ and the Ampère–Maxwell law $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$. The two time derivatives were the only terms containing both fields, so the remaining equations split into a pair for $\vec E$ (sourced by $\rho$) and a pair for $\vec B$ (sourced by $\vec J$).
>
> **3. Compatibility.** The divergence of $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ gives $0 = \mu_0\nabla\cdot\vec J$, since a divergence of a curl vanishes. This is the continuity equation $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]; at level C a consequence of gauge invariance, [[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^thm-c1-3-1|Theorem §C1.3.1]]) with $\partial_t\rho = 0$. ⚑ By-product: steady currents must be divergence-free; this is stated in the theorem.
>
> **4. Static potentials.** Start from any potentials, $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A$, $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$ ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-1|Theorem §B11.1.1]]; from the Bianchi identity, [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-5|Theorem §C1.2.5]]), with the gauge transformation $V \to V + \partial_t\chi$, $\vec A \to \vec A - \nabla\chi$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-1|REL Def. §B4.2.1]]).
> - Since $\partial_t\vec B = \nabla\times\partial_t\vec A = \vec 0$ on all of $\mathbb R^3$, $\partial_t\vec A = \nabla g(\vec r, t)$ for some function $g$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]).
> - Take $\chi(\vec r, t) = \int_0^tg(\vec r, t')\,dt'$. Then $\vec A' = \vec A - \nabla\chi$ has $\partial_t\vec A' = \nabla g - \nabla g = \vec 0$, and $V' = V + g$.
> - Now $\vec E = -\nabla V'$ is time-independent, so $V'(\vec r, t) = V_0(\vec r) + c(t)$. The further gauge function $\chi_2 = -\int_0^tc(t')\,dt'$ depends on $t$ only, leaves $\vec A'$ unchanged ($\nabla\chi_2 = \vec 0$) and removes $c(t)$.
> - What remains free: gauge functions that keep both potentials static need $\nabla\chi$ static and $\partial_t\chi$ static, i.e. $\chi = \bar V t + \chi_0(\vec r)$. They shift $V$ by the constant $\bar V$ and $\vec A$ by a static gradient. ⚑ By-product: the arbitrary zero of the electrostatic potential is the residual gauge freedom; it is fixed by a boundary condition, usually $V(\infty) = 0$ (Theorem §C2.1.3).
>
> **5. Electrostatics.** With $\vec J = \vec 0$, $\vec B$ has zero divergence and zero curl and vanishes at infinity, so $\vec B = \vec 0$ by the uniqueness part of the Helmholtz theorem ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]). Inserting $\vec E = -\nabla V$ into Gauss's law gives $-\nabla^2V = \rho/\varepsilon_0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The electric and magnetic sectors are coupled only through time derivatives; statics decouples them.
> - Charge conservation enters as a consistency condition; the arbitrary constant in $V$ is gauge freedom.
> - $\vec B = \vec 0$ in electrostatics is a consequence of $\vec J = \vec 0$ *and* the condition at infinity; a uniform $\vec B$ filling space would also solve the equations.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.1.2: The Static Action of the Potential
> For static fields with $\vec J = \vec 0$ and $\vec B = \vec 0$, the Maxwell Lagrangian density $\mathcal L = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2 - \frac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2 - \rho V + \vec J\cdot\vec A$ ([[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]]) becomes, with $\vec E = -\nabla V$,
>
> $$
> L_E[V] = \int d^3r\,\Bigl[\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}|\nabla V|^2 - \rho V\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> 1. $L_E$ is stationary under all variations $\delta V$ of compact support exactly when $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$, as an identity of distributions.
> 2. The stationary point is a minimum: $L_E[V + \eta] = L_E[V] + \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int|\nabla\eta|^2\,d^3r$.
> 3. ⚑ For a localized, bounded $\rho$, the minimum value is minus the total electrostatic energy ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]], [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-5|Theorem §C2.3.5]]): $L_E[V] = -\frac12\int\rho V\,d^3r = -U_E$. For point charges it is not defined ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-7|Theorem §C2.3.7]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, Part III §28.13 (the Lagrangian "in traditional form") · the static reduction and the minimum property are worked out here*

^thm-c2-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Reduce the Lagrangian.** With $\vec B = \vec 0$, $\vec J = \vec 0$ and $\vec E = -\nabla V$: $E^2 = |\nabla V|^2$, the $B^2$ and $\vec J\cdot\vec A$ terms are zero, and $\mathcal L = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}|\nabla V|^2 - \rho V$. No time derivative of $V$ appears: $V$ is not a dynamical variable but is fixed by the sources at each instant (as in the full theory, where $\partial_tV$ never occurs, Theorem §C1.1.5). The overall sign is the one with $+\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2$; the user's notes pass through an intermediate line with the opposite sign ([[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^cau-c1-1-1|§C1.1, Caution: The sign of the Lagrangian in the user's PHY 505 notes]]).
>
> **2. Vary.** Let $\eta$ be a test function (smooth, zero outside a bounded region; [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-1|QFT Def. §CA.2.1]]) and $\epsilon$ a number. Expanding the square, $|\nabla V + \epsilon\nabla\eta|^2 = |\nabla V|^2 + 2\epsilon\,\nabla V\cdot\nabla\eta + \epsilon^2|\nabla\eta|^2$, so
>
> $$
> L_E[V + \epsilon\eta] = L_E[V] + \epsilon\int d^3r\,\bigl(\varepsilon_0\nabla V\cdot\nabla\eta - \rho\eta\bigr) + \epsilon^2\,\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int d^3r\,|\nabla\eta|^2 ,
> $$
>
> with no further terms because $L_E$ is quadratic in $V$.
>
> **3. First order.** Integrate by parts, $\int\nabla V\cdot\nabla\eta\,d^3r = -\int(\nabla^2V)\,\eta\,d^3r$: there is no boundary term because $\eta$ vanishes outside a bounded region. If $V$ is only a distribution (point charges), this equation is the definition of $\nabla^2V$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]); likewise $\int\rho\eta$ means $\rho[\eta]$, e.g. $\sum_iq_i\eta(\vec r_i)$ for point charges. The first-order term $\int(-\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V - \rho)\,\eta$ vanishes for every $\eta$ exactly when $-\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V = \rho$ as distributions.
>
> **4. Second order.** At a solution the first-order term is zero for every $\eta$ (and, by approximation, for every $\eta$ decaying with $\nabla\eta$ square-integrable), so $L_E[V + \eta] - L_E[V] = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int|\nabla\eta|^2 \ge 0$, with equality only for $\nabla\eta = \vec 0$, i.e. $\eta$ constant, and $\eta = 0$ if it decays. The solution is the unique minimizer among potentials vanishing at infinity.
>
> **5. The value.** For bounded, localized $\rho$, $V \sim 1/r$ and $|\nabla V| \sim 1/r^2$ at large $r$. Integrate the first term by parts over a ball of radius $R$: $\int|\nabla V|^2 = -\int V\nabla^2V + \oint V\,\nabla V\cdot d\vec S$; the surface term is of order $\frac1R\cdot\frac{1}{R^2}\cdot R^2 = \frac1R \to 0$. With $-\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V = \rho$,
>
> $$
> L_E[V] = \frac12\int\rho V\,d^3r - \int\rho V\,d^3r = -\frac12\int\rho V\,d^3r .
> $$
>
> For a point charge both terms of $L_E$ are infinite (step 5 needs $V$ at the charge), which is the self-energy of [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-7|Theorem §C2.3.7]].
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Poisson's equation is the Euler–Lagrange equation of the static action, in the distributional sense, so point charges are included.
> - $L_E$ is convex: the potential is its unique minimizer, and $-L_E[V]$ is the energy. Minimum principles of this kind return as Thomson's theorem ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]) and the variational principle of [[§C6.4★ The Complex Potential and the Variational Principle|§C6.4★]].
> - The sign is that of mechanics: with no "kinetic" term, $L = -U$ at the solution.

## The field and the potential of a charge distribution

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.1.3: The Coulomb Integrals from the Field Equations
> Let $\rho$ be localized, and let $\vec E$ solve the electrostatic pair of [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-1|Theorem §C2.1.1]] and fall off faster than $1/r$. Then $\vec E$ is unique and (Zangwill eqs. (3.6), (3.8), (3.10)–(3.13))
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r'\,\frac{\rho(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}, \qquad \vec E(\vec r) = -\nabla V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r'\,\rho(\vec r\,')\,\frac{\vec r - \vec r\,'}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3} ,
> $$
>
> with the zero of $V$ at infinity. Surface and line charges give $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int dS\,\sigma/|\vec r - \vec r_S|$ and $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d\ell\,\lambda/|\vec r - \vec\ell|$. For point charges and other singular densities the formula for $V$ holds as a convolution of distributions with the fundamental solution $G(\vec r) = 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$ of $-\varepsilon_0\nabla^2$ ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^rem-c2-1-1|Remark: The Coulomb potential as a fundamental solution]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.2–§3.3, eqs. (3.5)–(3.13); §1.9 · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part IV §43.2–§43.4*

^thm-c2-1-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Helmholtz.** For a field that falls off faster than $1/r$ (Zangwill §1.9; [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]], corollary), with $\nabla'$ acting on $\vec E(\vec r\,')$ alone (Zangwill eq. (3.5)),
>
> $$
> \vec E(\vec r) = -\nabla\int d^3r'\,\frac{\nabla'\cdot\vec E(\vec r\,')}{4\pi|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} + \nabla\times\int d^3r'\,\frac{\nabla'\times\vec E(\vec r\,')}{4\pi|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} .
> $$
>
> **2. Insert the field equations.** $\nabla'\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ and $\nabla'\times\vec E = \vec 0$ remove the second term and give $\vec E = -\nabla V$ with $V$ as stated (Zangwill eq. (3.6)). The integral for $V$ assigns $V \to 0$ at infinity, which fixes the constant left free in [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-1|Theorem §C2.1.1]].
>
> **3. Move the gradient inside.** $\nabla$ acts on $\vec r$ only. For bounded localized $\rho$, the differentiated integrand is bounded by $\sup|\rho|/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^2$, integrable near $\vec r$ in three dimensions, so differentiation under the integral is allowed ([[§CA.1 Exchanging Limits, Derivatives and Integrals#^thm-ca-1-1|QFT Theorem §CA.1.1]]).
>
> **4. The kernel.** $\nabla\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} = -\frac{\vec r - \vec r\,'}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3}$ (Zangwill eq. (3.7); [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], which also shows that the identity holds as distributions, with nothing extra at $\vec r = \vec r\,'$). This gives the field integral, eq. (3.8).
>
> **5. Singular densities.** For $\rho = \sum_iq_i\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_i)$ the sifting property ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]) gives $V = \sum_iq_i/4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec r_i|$. Check it directly: $\nabla^2V = \sum_i\frac{q_i}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\nabla^2\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r_i|} = \sum_i\frac{q_i}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\bigl(-4\pi\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_i)\bigr) = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$ (Theorem §B1.2.2). Surface charge $\sigma$ on $S$ is the density $\rho = \sigma\,\delta_S$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]] (4)), and $\int d^3r'\,\sigma\delta_S(\vec r\,')f(\vec r\,') = \int dS\,\sigma f$ turns the volume integral into the surface integral; the same for lines.
>
> **6. Uniqueness.** Two solutions differ by $\vec W$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec W = 0$, $\nabla\times\vec W = \vec 0$, so each Cartesian component is harmonic on $\mathbb R^3$ and tends to $0$ at infinity; it vanishes (Zangwill §1.9, via Green's first identity; or [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-4|Theorem §C6.1.4]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The Coulomb integrals follow from the field equations plus decay at infinity; no force law was assumed.
> - ⚑ The choice $V(\infty) = 0$ is built into the integral (Zangwill, after eq. (3.18)). For charge extending to infinity (infinite lines and planes) the integrals diverge and the Helmholtz route fails; those cases are limits of finite ones ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^rem-c2-2-2|§C2.2, Remark: Symmetry selects, uniqueness decides]]).

> [!caution] Caution: Where the derivative acts in the Helmholtz formula
> The user's PHY 505 notes (Part IV §43.3, "Fact 3") write the Helmholtz formula with $\nabla'\cdot\bigl[\vec E(\vec r\,')/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|\bigr]$ under the integral, the primed derivative acting on the whole quotient. Integrated over all space, that is a surface term at infinity and vanishes, so the formula as written would give $\vec E = \vec 0$. Zangwill has either the *unprimed* $\nabla$ on the quotient (his eq. (1.177)) or $\nabla'$ on $\vec E$ alone (eqs. (1.174), (3.5)); the two are related by
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\frac{\vec E(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} = \frac{\nabla'\cdot\vec E(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} - \nabla'\cdot\frac{\vec E(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}
> $$
>
> (Zangwill eq. (1.179)). In the derivation that follows in the notes (§43.4, Steps 3–4), "the second term vanishes by integration by parts" should read: the total divergence $\nabla'\cdot[\vec E/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|]$ integrates to zero, which leaves $\nabla'\cdot\vec E/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$. The final result, $\vec E = -\nabla V$, is correct.

> [!remark] Remark: The Coulomb potential as a fundamental solution
> - $G(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$ satisfies $-\varepsilon_0\nabla^2G = \delta^3$ as distributions ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]): it is a **fundamental solution** of $-\varepsilon_0\nabla^2$ in the sense of [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-7|QFT Def. §CA.2.7]], whose normalization $P(\partial)G = -i\delta$ carries a factor $-i$ that electrostatics does not use. Both $1/r$ and its gradient $\hat r/r^2$ are locally integrable on $\mathbb R^3$, so they are regular distributions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-1|QFT Theorem §CA.2.1]], 2).
> - Theorem §C2.1.3 is then the statement $V = G*\rho$, and $-\varepsilon_0\nabla^2(G*\rho) = (-\varepsilon_0\nabla^2G)*\rho = \delta^3*\rho = \rho$: derivatives of a convolution can be put on either factor ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-15|QFT Theorem §CA.2.15]] for smooth $\rho$; for point charges the convolution is the finite sum of step 5 above).
> - $G$ is fixed among all fundamental solutions (they differ by harmonic functions, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-14|QFT Theorem §CA.2.14]], 1) by the condition $G \to 0$ at infinity. With conductors, other boundary conditions select other fundamental solutions: the Green functions of [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^def-c7-3-1|Def. §C7.3.1]], each the free-space one plus a harmonic function ([[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]]); Zangwill's normalization there, $\varepsilon_0\nabla^2G = -\delta^3$, is the one used here.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.3, eq. (3.46) · the distributional reading stated here*

^rem-c2-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.1.4: Force and Torque between Charge Distributions; No Self-Force
> A static distribution $\rho$ exerts on a second distribution $\rho^\star$ the force and torque (Zangwill eqs. (3.3), (3.4), (3.9), Problem 3.6)
>
> $$
> \vec F = \int d^3r\,\rho^\star\vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r\int d^3r'\,\rho^\star(\vec r)\rho(\vec r\,')\,\frac{\vec r - \vec r\,'}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3}, \qquad \vec N = \int d^3r\,\vec r\times\rho^\star\vec E = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r\int d^3r'\,\rho^\star(\vec r)\rho(\vec r\,')\,\frac{\vec r\times\vec r\,'}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3} .
> $$
>
> Exchanging the roles gives $\vec F^\star = -\vec F$ and $\vec N^\star = -\vec N$ (torques about the same origin). Hence a bounded distribution exerts no net force and no net torque on itself. For two point charges the force is Coulomb's law ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^law-b2-1-1|Law §B2.1.1]]), here a theorem; the force on a point charge means its charge times the field of all the *other* charges.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.1–§3.2, eqs. (3.3), (3.4), (3.9), footnote 1; Problem 3.6 (solved here) · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part IV §42.3*

^thm-c2-1-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Force density.** The force on the charge in $d^3r$ is $\rho^\star\vec E\,d^3r$: the static, $\vec J = \vec 0$ case of the Lorentz force density $\rho\vec E + \vec J\times\vec B$, which follows from the action of a charge in a field ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-4|REL Theorem §B3.2.4]]; [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^ex-c1-2-2|Example §C1.2.2]]; [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]] at level B).
>
> **2. Insert the field.** Theorem §C2.1.3 for $\vec E$ gives the double integral for $\vec F$. For the torque, $\vec r\times(\vec r - \vec r\,') = \vec r\times\vec r - \vec r\times\vec r\,' = -\vec r\times\vec r\,'$.
>
> **3. Absolute convergence.** For bounded densities supported in a bounded region, $\bigl|\rho^\star(\vec r)\rho(\vec r\,')\bigr|/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^2$ is integrable over $(\vec r, \vec r\,')$: the inner integral is at most $\sup|\rho|\int_{\text{ball}}d^3r'/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^2$, finite. For the torque, $|\vec r\times\vec r\,'| = |\vec r\times(\vec r\,' - \vec r)| \le |\vec r|\,|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$, so the integrand is bounded by $|\vec r|$ times the same function. Fubini's theorem ([[Fubini's Theorem]]) therefore allows renaming and exchanging the variables.
>
> **4. Exchange the roles.** $\vec F^\star$ is the same double integral with $\rho^\star \leftrightarrow \rho$: $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\int\rho(\vec r)\rho^\star(\vec r\,')\frac{\vec r - \vec r\,'}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3}$. Rename $\vec r \leftrightarrow \vec r\,'$ (Jacobian 1): it becomes $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\int\rho^\star(\vec r)\rho(\vec r\,')\frac{\vec r\,' - \vec r}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3} = -\vec F$. For the torque the same renaming turns $\vec r\times\vec r\,'$ into $\vec r\,'\times\vec r = -\vec r\times\vec r\,'$, so $\vec N^\star = -\vec N$.
>
> **5. Self-force.** For $\rho^\star = \rho$, $\vec F = \vec F^\star = -\vec F$, so $\vec F = \vec 0$; likewise $\vec N = \vec 0$.
>
> **6. ⚑ Point charges.** For $\rho^\star = q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)$ and $\rho$ containing $q$ itself, $\int\rho^\star\vec E$ asks for the field of $q$ at its own position, $\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\vec 0}{0}$: the product of $\delta^3$ with a field singular at its support has no value, like the products of [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-8|QFT Theorem §CA.2.8]], and step 3 fails. Steps 3–5 justify the rule in the statement for smeared charges: a charge spread over a small ball exerts zero force on itself for every radius, so in the point limit only the field of the others remains. [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]] obtains the same force with no regularization.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Newton's third law and the absence of self-force and self-torque are the antisymmetry of the kernel under $\vec r \leftrightarrow \vec r\,'$; they need only absolute convergence.
> - The force on a *part* of $\rho$ by the rest is in general not zero (Zangwill, footnote 1): this is what holds a charged body together or tears it apart, and what the stress tensor of [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]] computes.

> [!remark] Remark: Coulomb's law across the levels; conservative because central
> - **Layer.** Coulomb's law is a principle at level A ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|Principle §A1.2.2]]), an empirical law with a stated range at level B ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^law-b2-1-1|Law §B2.1.1]], [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^rem-b2-1-1|Remark: Coulomb's law and superposition at level B]]), and here a theorem of the action, restricted to charges at rest: [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]] with the force density of [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-4|Theorem §C2.1.4]]. The experiments that tested the inverse square now test the action: a photon mass would add $m^2A^\mu$ terms and turn $1/r$ into the Yukawa form $e^{-r/\lambda}/r$ ([[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^ex-c1-3-2|★ Example §C1.3.2]]; [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^rem-b4-1-4|REL Remark: Static solutions and the Yukawa potential]]).
> - **Conservative because central.** The work done by $q\vec E$ between two points is $-q[V(\vec r_2) - V(\vec r_1)]$, independent of the path, and zero around every loop (Zangwill eqs. (3.15)–(3.16); [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]]). Zangwill stresses (footnote 4) that this holds because the Coulomb force is **central**, $\vec F = f(r)\hat r$ for any $f$ ([[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-4|CM Theorem §B1.2.4]]), not because $f \propto 1/r^2$. The inverse square is what Gauss's law needs ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^cau-a2-2-1|§A2.2, Caution: Closed surfaces, signs, inverse squares]]; [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]).
> - **The zero of potential** is free, as the residual gauge freedom of [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-1|Theorem §C2.1.1]] says; Zangwill (sources to §3.3) points to an argument of Wigner relating this freedom to the conservation of charge ([[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^thm-c1-3-1|Theorem §C1.3.1]] treats the field-theory version).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.3.1, eqs. (3.15)–(3.18), footnote 4; Sources to §3.3*

## Summation problems

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.1.5: Scaling of Potential and Field
> Let two distributions be related by a change of scale, $\rho_B(\lambda\vec r) = \rho_A(\vec r)$ for all $\vec r$, with a constant $\lambda > 0$ (the same density at corresponding points). Then (Zangwill Example 3.2)
>
> $$
> V_B(\lambda\vec r) = \lambda^2\,V_A(\vec r), \qquad \vec E_B(\lambda\vec r) = \lambda\,\vec E_A(\vec r) .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.3, Example 3.2*

^thm-c2-1-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Start from B.** By [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]], $V_B(\lambda\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r''\,\frac{\rho_B(\vec r\,'')}{|\lambda\vec r - \vec r\,''|}$.
>
> **2. Change variables** $\vec r\,'' = \lambda\vec r\,'$. The Jacobian is $d^3r'' = \lambda^3d^3r'$; the region of integration, all of $\mathbb R^3$, is mapped onto itself; and $|\lambda\vec r - \lambda\vec r\,'| = \lambda|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ because $\lambda > 0$. With $\rho_B(\lambda\vec r\,') = \rho_A(\vec r\,')$,
>
> $$
> V_B(\lambda\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\lambda^3d^3r'\,\frac{\rho_A(\vec r\,')}{\lambda|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} = \lambda^2V_A(\vec r) .
> $$
>
> **3. The field.** Write $\tilde{\vec r} = \lambda\vec r$, so $V_B(\tilde{\vec r}) = \lambda^2V_A(\tilde{\vec r}/\lambda)$. By the chain rule $\partial/\partial\tilde r_i$ of $V_A(\tilde{\vec r}/\lambda)$ is $\frac1\lambda(\partial_iV_A)(\tilde{\vec r}/\lambda)$, hence $\nabla_{\tilde r}V_B(\tilde{\vec r}) = \lambda(\nabla V_A)(\vec r)$ and $\vec E_B(\lambda\vec r) = \lambda\vec E_A(\vec r)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows:** the powers count dimensions, charge $\propto\lambda^3$ over distance $\propto\lambda$; and the zero of potential at infinity is used, since a constant added to $V_A$ would not scale.

^der-c2-1-5

*Uses:* [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.1.6: The Charged Line Segment
> A segment from $(0, 0, -L)$ to $(0, 0, L)$ with uniform charge $\lambda$ per length (total $Q = 2L\lambda$) has, at a point at distances $d_1$ and $d_2$ from the ends $(0, 0, L)$ and $(0, 0, -L)$, the potential (Zangwill eqs. (3.30)–(3.38))
>
> $$
> V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{u + L}{u - L}, \qquad u = \tfrac12(d_1 + d_2) .
> $$
>
> The equipotentials are the prolate spheroids $d_1 + d_2 = \text{const}$ with foci at the ends; the field lines are the confocal hyperbolas $d_2 - d_1 = \text{const}$. Far away ($R \gg L$, $R$ the distance from the midpoint) $V \approx Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0R$; near the middle ($|z|, s \ll L$) $V \approx -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln s + \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln 2L$, the infinite line plus a constant that diverges as $L \to \infty$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.3.5, eqs. (3.30)–(3.38), Fig. 3.6*

^thm-c2-1-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The integral.** At the point $(s, z)$ in cylindrical coordinates, $|\vec r - z'\hat z| = \sqrt{(z' - z)^2 + s^2}$ and Theorem §C2.1.3 gives $V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{dz'}{\sqrt{(z' - z)^2 + s^2}}$. An antiderivative in $z'$ is $\ln\bigl[(z' - z) + \sqrt{(z' - z)^2 + s^2}\bigr]$ (differentiate: $\bigl(1 + \frac{z' - z}{\sqrt{\cdot}}\bigr)/\bigl(z' - z + \sqrt{\cdot}\bigr) = 1/\sqrt{\cdot}$). With $d_1 = \sqrt{(z - L)^2 + s^2}$, $d_2 = \sqrt{(z + L)^2 + s^2}$,
>
> $$
> V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{L - z + d_1}{d_2 - L - z} \qquad\text{(Zangwill eq. (3.30))} .
> $$
>
> **2. New variables.** Let $u = \frac12(d_1 + d_2)$, $t = \frac12(d_2 - d_1)$. Then $d_1 = u - t$, $d_2 = u + t$, and $d_2^2 - d_1^2 = (z + L)^2 - (z - L)^2 = 4zL$; since also $d_2^2 - d_1^2 = (d_2 - d_1)(d_2 + d_1) = 4ut$, we get $ut = zL$. (Zangwill's $t$ has the opposite sign, so his identity reads $ut = -zL$.)
>
> **3. Eliminate $z$.** With $z = ut/L$: the numerator is $L - \frac{ut}{L} + u - t = (u + L) - \frac tL(u + L) = (u + L)(1 - t/L)$, the denominator $u + t - L - \frac{ut}{L} = (u - L) - \frac tL(u - L) = (u - L)(1 - t/L)$. The factor $1 - t/L$ cancels (it vanishes only on the axis beyond the end $z = L$, where $|d_2 - d_1| = 2L$ and the formula holds by continuity), leaving $V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{u + L}{u - L}$, a function of $u$ alone (Zangwill eq. (3.33)).
>
> **4. Geometry.** $V$ = const means $d_1 + d_2 = 2u$ = const: in each plane through the axis an ellipse with foci at the ends and semi-axes $u$ and $\sqrt{u^2 - L^2}$, hence a prolate spheroid. The curves $d_2 - d_1$ = const are hyperbolas with the same foci. They are orthogonal: $\nabla d_1 = \hat e_1$ and $\nabla d_2 = \hat e_2$ are the unit vectors from the two ends to the point, and $\nabla(d_1 + d_2)\cdot\nabla(d_2 - d_1) = |\hat e_2|^2 - |\hat e_1|^2 = 0$. Both gradients lie in the plane through the axis, so $\vec E \propto -\nabla u$ is tangent to the hyperbola through each point, which is therefore a field line.
>
> **5. Far.** For $R \gg L$, $u \approx R$, and $\ln\frac{R + L}{R - L} = \ln(1 + L/R) - \ln(1 - L/R) \approx \frac{2L}{R}$, so $V \approx \frac{2L\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R}$ (eq. (3.36)).
>
> **6. Near.** At $z = 0$, $d_1 = d_2 = u = \sqrt{L^2 + s^2}$, and $\frac{u + L}{u - L} = \frac{(u + L)^2}{u^2 - L^2} = \frac{(u + L)^2}{s^2}$. For $s \ll L$, $u + L \approx 2L$, so $V \approx \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot2\ln\frac{2L}{s}$, which is eq. (3.37); its gradient is the field $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0s}\hat s$ of an infinite line (eq. (3.38)).
>
> **What the derivation shows:** the elliptic coordinates $(u, t)$ separate the problem: $V$ depends on one of them. ⚑ The constant $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln 2L$ diverges as $L \to \infty$; it is an artefact of extending the charge to infinity and has no effect on the field (Zangwill, after eq. (3.38)). The spheroidal equipotentials are what [[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]] uses for a conducting ellipsoid and disk.

^der-c2-1-6

*Uses:* [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]]

Both families have their foci at the ends of the segment; close to the segment the equipotentials hug it like those of an infinite line, far away they become circles.*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C2.1.1: Centre and Corner of a Uniformly Charged Cube
> A cube of side $s$ is filled with uniform charge density $\rho$. Find the ratio $V_0/V_1$ of the potential at its centre to the potential at a corner ($V(\infty) = 0$).
>
> Write $V_1(s)$ for the corner potential and $V_0(s)$ for the centre potential of such a cube.
> - *Superposition.* The cube of side $s$ is the union of eight cubes of side $s/2$, each of which has one corner at the centre of the big cube. Each contributes the corner potential of a cube of side $s/2$, so $V_0(s) = 8\,V_1(s/2)$.
> - *Scaling.* The cube of side $s$ is the cube of side $s/2$ magnified by $\lambda = 2$, with the same density, and corners correspond to corners. By [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-5|Theorem §C2.1.5]], $V_1(s) = 2^2\,V_1(s/2)$.
>
> Combining, $V_0(s) = 8\cdot\frac14V_1(s) = 2V_1(s)$:
>
> $$
> \frac{V_0}{V_1} = 2 .
> $$
>
> No integral was evaluated. (Numerically, $V_1 = 0.0947\,\rho s^2/\varepsilon_0$, from $\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{[0,1]^3}d^3r/r$.) The argument uses $V(\infty) = 0$ twice: superposition adds potentials with a common zero, and scaling holds only for that zero.
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 7, Problem 4 (Zangwill Problem 3.13), solved here*

^ex-c2-1-1

> [!example] Example §C2.1.2: A Charged Spherical Shell with a Hole
> A spherical shell of radius $a$ carries uniform surface charge $\sigma$. A circular hole is cut out around the north pole; its radius, measured as arc length on the sphere, is $b$, so it subtends the polar half-angle $\alpha = b/a$. Find $\vec E$ everywhere on the $z$ axis through the hole.
>
> *Superposition* (Zangwill Example 3.3): shell with hole = complete shell + a spherical cap $0 \le \theta' \le \alpha$ carrying $-\sigma$. On the axis everything points along $\hat z$.
>
> *The complete shell* (Gauss's law, [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]): $E_z = 0$ for $|z| < a$, $E_z = +\sigma a^2/\varepsilon_0z^2$ for $z > a$ and $E_z = -\sigma a^2/\varepsilon_0z^2$ for $z < -a$.
>
> *The cap with $+\sigma$, by rings.* The ring between $\theta'$ and $\theta' + d\theta'$ has charge $dq = 2\pi a^2\sigma\sin\theta'\,d\theta'$ and lies at height $a\cos\theta'$, at distance $\sqrt{a^2 + z^2 - 2az\cos\theta'}$ from the field point. Its axial field is $dq\,(z - a\cos\theta')/4\pi\varepsilon_0(a^2 + z^2 - 2az\cos\theta')^{3/2}$ (the ring of Zangwill Example 3.1). With $u = \cos\theta'$, $du = -\sin\theta'\,d\theta'$, $c = \cos\alpha$, and the antiderivative of [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^ex-b2-1-1|Example §B2.1.1]],
>
> $$
> E^{\text{cap}}_z = \frac{\sigma a^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\int_c^1\frac{(z - au)\,du}{(a^2 + z^2 - 2azu)^{3/2}} = \frac{\sigma a^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\frac{zu - a}{z^2\sqrt{a^2 + z^2 - 2azu}}\Bigr]_{u = c}^{u = 1} = \frac{\sigma a^2}{2\varepsilon_0z^2}\Bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(z - a) - \frac{zc - a}{\sqrt{a^2 + z^2 - 2azc}}\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> using $\sqrt{(z - a)^2} = |z - a|$ at $u = 1$.
>
> *Shell minus cap.* For $z > -a$ (inside, through the hole and beyond it) the two cases $z < a$ and $z > a$ give the same expression, and for $z < -a$ the sign of the first term flips:
>
> $$
> E_z(z) = \frac{\sigma a^2}{2\varepsilon_0z^2}\Bigl[\pm1 + \frac{z\cos\alpha - a}{\sqrt{a^2 + z^2 - 2az\cos\alpha}}\Bigr], \qquad + \text{ for } z > -a, \quad - \text{ for } z < -a .
> $$
>
> *Checks.*
> - The field is continuous through the hole, at $z = a$, where there is no charge, and jumps by $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ at the south pole $z = -a$, where there is ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]).
> - At the centre of the hole, $\sqrt{2a^2(1 - \cos\alpha)} = 2a\sin\frac\alpha2$ and $a\cos\alpha - a = -2a\sin^2\frac\alpha2$, so $E_z(a) = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\bigl(1 - \sin\frac\alpha2\bigr)$: Zangwill Problem 3.12(a), with his cone angle $\theta_0$ the half-angle $\alpha$. For a small hole this is $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$, the average of the fields $0$ and $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ on the two sides of an intact shell, as it must be: removing a small disk removes the local jump and leaves the field of the rest of the shell, which is the average ([[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor#^thm-c2-4-3|Theorem §C2.4.3]]).
> - At the centre of the sphere, expanding to second order in $z$ gives $E_z(0) = \frac{\sigma}{4\varepsilon_0}\sin^2\alpha$, pointing toward the hole: the cap that is missing no longer pushes the field point away.
> - Far from the hole, $|z - a| \gg b$, the cap acts as a point charge $Q' = -2\pi a^2\sigma(1 - \cos\alpha) \approx -\pi b^2\sigma$ at the north pole, which is Zangwill's approximation $\vec E = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\hat r}{r^2}\theta(r - a) + \frac{Q'}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\vec r - \vec R}{|\vec r - \vec R|^3}$ with $Q = 4\pi a^2\sigma$.
>
> (The formula was checked against direct numerical integration of the rings.)
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 7, Problem 1 (Spherical shell with hole in it), solved here · Zangwill Example 3.3, Problem 3.12*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The Coulomb potential is the static, massless member of a family of Green's functions: the $m \to 0$ limit of the Yukawa potential ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^rem-b4-1-4|REL Remark: Static solutions and the Yukawa potential]]); in Heaviside–Lorentz units it is $1/4\pi r$ ([[§C1.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^thm-c1-7-4|QFT Theorem §C1.7.4]]).
> - The static action of Theorem §C2.1.2 is the Dirichlet energy of potential theory; its minimization is Green's first identity at work ([[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-28-2|452 Thm. §28.2]]), the same identity that proves uniqueness.
> - Exchanging the order of the two volume integrals in Theorem §C2.1.4 is Fubini's theorem for an absolutely integrable kernel ([[Fubini's Theorem]]); for point charges it fails, which is the self-energy problem of [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-7|Theorem §C2.3.7]].
