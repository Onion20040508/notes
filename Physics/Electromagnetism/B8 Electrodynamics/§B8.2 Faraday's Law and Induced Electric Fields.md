*Sources: Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (4th ed.), §7.2.1–7.2.2 · PHY 422 (Thamm, Spring 2024) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 18.*

A loop moving through a static field has an emf that the Lorentz force explains completely ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]]). But Faraday also found an emf when the loop stands still and the magnet moves, or when the field is simply turned up, and charges at rest feel no magnetic force. Level A stated the answer twice, as the circuit law $\mathcal E = -d\Phi_m/dt$ and as the field law $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = -d\Phi_m/dt$ ([[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^pr-a9-1-1|Principle §A9.1.1]], [[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^pr-a9-4-1|Principle §A9.4.1]]), showed that the induced field has no potential ([[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^thm-a9-4-2|Theorem §A9.4.2]]) and computed it for a long solenoid ([[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^ex-a9-4-1|Example §A9.4.1]]). Here Faraday's law becomes the local field equation $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial t$, a principle of electrodynamics; the universal flux rule for a moving loop in a changing field is derived from it and the Lorentz force; induced fields are computed with the whole apparatus of magnetostatics, by an exact analogy; and the quasistatic approximation that makes this legitimate is stated with its limits. Level A writes $\Phi_m$ for the magnetic flux; here it is $\Phi$, as in Griffiths.

## Faraday's law

> [!principle] Principle §B8.2.1: Faraday's Law
> A changing magnetic field is accompanied by an electric field whose curl is (Griffiths eq. (7.16))
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}
> $$
>
> at every point and every instant.
>
> *Domain: all of classical electrodynamics, for the fields at a point of a given inertial frame; one of Maxwell's equations ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]), unchanged in matter. It reduces to $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ for static fields ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]).*
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.1, eqs. (7.14)–(7.16) · Nelson ch. 18, §18.3.1, eqs. (18.8)–(18.9)*

^pr-b8-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Principle or theorem?
> - *What is assumed.* This note takes Faraday's law as a principle: an experimental fact (Faraday's "ingenious inspiration", in Griffiths' words: *a changing magnetic field induces an electric field*) promoted to a fundamental field equation. It cannot be derived from the flux rule of [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]], which is pure Lorentz force and says nothing about stationary loops. Nelson presents it the same way: before Faraday the electric field was assumed conservative, as Newtonian gravity is even with moving planets; Faraday's observations force a nonconservative contribution proportional to the rate of change of $\vec B$.
> - *Layers across levels.* The field form of level A, [[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^pr-a9-4-1|Principle §A9.4.1]], stays a principle and here becomes local; its integral version is now [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]]. The circuit form, [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^pr-a9-1-1|Principle §A9.1.1]], becomes a theorem, [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]], as level A's [[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^rem-a9-4-1|§A9.4, Remark: One principle, two statements]] anticipated.
> - *Names.* Griffiths reserves "Faraday's law" for electric fields induced by changing magnetic fields and does not count the motional emf (his Experiment 1) as an instance of it, because the mechanisms differ ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^rem-b8-2-2|Remark: Two mechanisms, one formula]]). Nelson does not call $\int\vec B\cdot d\vec a$ a "flux" at all (in his book a flux is always a transport rate); the vault follows Griffiths and level A.
> - *Consistency.* Taking the divergence of both sides gives $\partial(\nabla\cdot\vec B)/\partial t = 0$, consistent with $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]]; for changing fields Maxwell's equation (ii), [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]); the corresponding test fails for Ampère's law, which is how Maxwell's correction arises ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-1|Theorem §B8.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.1 (pp. 313–314) and footnote 8 · Nelson ch. 18, §18.3.1 and footnote 3*

^rem-b8-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.2.2: Faraday's Law in Integral Form
> For every surface $S$ at rest, with boundary loop $\mathcal P$ and $d\vec a$ oriented by the right-hand rule from the sense of $d\vec l$ (Griffiths eq. (7.15)),
>
> $$
> \oint_{\mathcal P}\vec E\cdot d\vec l = -\int_S\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}\cdot d\vec a = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} .
> $$
>
> Conversely, if this holds for every small fixed loop, the local law [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^pr-b8-2-1|Principle §B8.2.1]] follows. For a stationary circuit the left side is its emf ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]]): $\mathcal E = -d\Phi/dt$ (7.14).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.1, eqs. (7.14)–(7.16)*

^thm-b8-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Integrate the principle over $S$ and apply Stokes' theorem ([[§34 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-34-1|452 Thm. §34.1]]) to the left side: $\int_S(\nabla\times\vec E)\cdot d\vec a = \oint_{\mathcal P}\vec E\cdot d\vec l$. On the right, $S$ does not move, so the time derivative may be taken outside the integral (differentiation under the integral sign, for $\partial\vec B/\partial t$ continuous): $\int_S\partial_t\vec B\cdot d\vec a = d\Phi/dt$. Conversely, if the integral law holds for a small flat disk of area $\Delta a$ and normal $\hat n$ at a point, Stokes' theorem gives $\int(\nabla\times\vec E + \partial_t\vec B)\cdot\hat n\,da = 0$; dividing by $\Delta a$ and shrinking the disk, $(\nabla\times\vec E + \partial_t\vec B)\cdot\hat n = 0$ for every $\hat n$ (by continuity of the integrand), which is the local law. In a stationary wire the carriers' velocity is along the wire, so the magnetic force does not push them along it, and the only force per unit charge with a circulation is $\vec E$.

^der-b8-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^pr-b8-2-1|Principle §B8.2.1]], [[§34 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-34-1|452 Thm. §34.1]], [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.2.3: The Universal Flux Rule
> Let a wire loop move and deform continuously, the element $d\vec l$ having velocity $\vec v$, through a magnetic field $\vec B(\vec r, t)$ that may also change in time. Its emf, the circulation of the full Lorentz force per unit charge taken at one instant, is minus the rate of change of the flux through it (Griffiths eq. (7.17)):
>
> $$
> \mathcal E = \oint\bigl(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B\bigr)\cdot d\vec l = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt}, \qquad \Phi(t) = \int_{S(t)}\vec B(\vec r, t)\cdot d\vec a .
> $$
>
> "Whenever (and for whatever reason) the magnetic flux through a loop changes, an emf $-d\Phi/dt$ will appear in the loop." The sign is Lenz's law ([[§A9.2 Lenz's Law#^thm-a9-2-1|Theorem §A9.2.1]]): the induced current's own flux opposes the change. The limits of [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^cau-b8-1-2|§B8.1, Caution: Where the flux rule breaks down]] apply unchanged.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.1, eq. (7.17) and Figure 7.21 (the combination worked out here from the proofs of eqs. (7.13) and (7.16))*

^thm-b8-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $S(t)$ span the loop at time $t$ and let the ribbon swept by the loop during $dt$ have area elements $(\vec v\times d\vec l)\,dt$, as in the proof of [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]]. $S(t)$ plus the ribbon is bounded by the loop at $t + dt$, and since $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ at every instant (Maxwell's equation (ii), [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]) the flux of $\vec B(t + dt)$ through it equals that through $S(t + dt)$. Hence, to first order in $dt$,
>
> $$
> \Phi(t + dt) = \int_{S(t)}\vec B(t + dt)\cdot d\vec a + \int_{\text{ribbon}}\vec B\cdot d\vec a = \Phi(t) + dt\int_{S(t)}\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}\cdot d\vec a + dt\oint\vec B\cdot(\vec v\times d\vec l) ,
> $$
>
> where in the ribbon term the change of $\vec B$ during $dt$ only contributes at order $dt^2$. The first term is $-\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l$ by [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]], applied to the fixed surface $S(t)$; the second is $-\oint(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec l$ by the triple-product step of [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^der-b8-1-6|§B8.1, Derivation of Theorem §B8.1.6]]. Adding them, $d\Phi/dt = -\oint(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec l$. As there, the carriers' drift along the wire adds nothing to the circulation.

^der-b8-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]], [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]], [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Two mechanisms, one formula
> In Faraday's first experiment (loop pulled through the field) the emf is magnetic, $\oint(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec l$; in the other two (magnet moved, or field strength changed, loop at rest) it is electric, $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l$. That both give $-d\Phi/dt$, and that experiments 1 and 2 give *the same* emf for the same relative motion, is in classical electrodynamics "a peculiar accident", and Griffiths stresses how astonishing it is. Einstein's search for a deeper reason led to special relativity: what one inertial observer calls a magnetic force another calls an electric one ([[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^rem-a9-3-3|§A9.3, Remark: Magnetic or electric? It depends on the frame]], [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^rem-a12-1-7|§A12.1, Remark: Electric or magnetic depends on the observer]], [[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-1|REL Principle §A1.3.1]]). The transformation of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ between frames is Griffiths' Chapter 12, now [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-2|REL Theorem §B4.2.2]]; what it says about the two mechanisms is [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^rem-b4-2-2|REL Remark: Magnetism is electricity seen from a moving frame]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.1 (pp. 313–314)*

## The induced electric field

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.2.4: Induced Fields Are Computed Like Magnetostatic Fields
> Suppose there is no charge, so that $\vec E$ is a pure Faraday field, and that it vanishes at infinity. Then
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec E = 0, \qquad \nabla\times\vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t} ,
> $$
>
> which are the equations of magnetostatics, $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$, $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-3|Theorem §B6.3.3]]), with $\mu_0\vec J$ replaced by $-\partial\vec B/\partial t$. Accordingly:
> 1. the analogue of the Biot–Savart law gives the field (Griffiths eq. (7.18)), with $\vec{\mathcal R} = \vec r - \vec r\,'$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]):
>
> $$
> \vec E(\vec r, t) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{\partial\vec B(\vec r\,', t)/\partial t\times\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\,d\tau' = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int\frac{\vec B(\vec r\,', t)\times\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\,d\tau' ;
> $$
>
> 2. when symmetry permits, $\vec E$ follows from $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = -d\Phi/dt$ (7.19) on an "Amperian loop", with $-d\Phi/dt$ in the role of $\mu_0I_{\text{enc}}$.
>
> Unlike its model, this needs no steady-state assumption: $\partial\vec B/\partial t$ may have any time dependence (Griffiths' footnote 13).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.2, eqs. (7.18)–(7.19) and Example 7.7*

^thm-b8-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $\rho = 0$, Gauss's law ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]; Maxwell's equation (i)) gives $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$, and Faraday's law ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^pr-b8-2-1|Principle §B8.2.1]]) gives the curl. A field that vanishes at infinity is determined by its divergence and curl (Helmholtz theorem, [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]). The Biot–Savart law ([[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^law-b6-2-2|Law §B6.2.2]]) is the solution of $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$, $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ that vanishes at infinity, a statement about the two differential equations only, whatever the physical meaning of $\vec J$; replacing $\mu_0\vec J$ by $-\partial\vec B/\partial t$ gives item 1, and the time derivative can be taken outside the integral. Item 2 is [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]], used exactly as Ampère's law ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]) is used for symmetric current distributions: the symmetry fixes the direction of $\vec E$ and makes $|\vec E|$ constant along the loop.

^der-b8-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^pr-b8-2-1|Principle §B8.2.1]], [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]], [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]], [[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^law-b6-2-2|Law §B6.2.2]], [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-3|Theorem §B6.3.3]], [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The induced field is minus the rate of change of the vector potential
> The integral $(1/4\pi)\int\vec B\times\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2\,d\tau'$ in the second form of item 1 is the vector potential $\vec A$ of the current distribution in the Coulomb gauge ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]]; the same analogy as [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]]): it solves $\nabla\times\vec A = \vec B$, $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$ by the same Biot–Savart analogy. So a pure Faraday field is
>
> $$
> \vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t} ,
> $$
>
> the form it takes in the potential formulation of electrodynamics ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-1|Theorem §B11.1.1]], [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^rem-b11-1-1|§B11.1★, Remark: What changes from level A]]). This is how the figure below was computed: the vector potential of a circular loop is known in closed form (complete elliptic integrals; Mathematical Methods, planned), and a solenoid is a stack of loops. Inside an infinite solenoid with $n$ turns per length, $A_\varphi = \mu_0nIs/2$, so $E_\varphi = -\tfrac12\mu_0n\dot Is$, the result of [[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^ex-a9-4-1|Example §A9.4.1]].

^rem-b8-2-3

![[ph-em-b8-2-1.svg]]
*Induced electric field $|E_\varphi|$ of a solenoid of radius $a$ whose current changes at the rate $\dot I$, in units of $E_0 = \tfrac12\mu_0n|\dot I|a$, computed as $-\partial A_\varphi/\partial t$ by integrating the vector potential of its current loops over the length $L$. The field lines are circles about the axis. Dashed: the infinite solenoid, $E \propto s$ inside and $\propto 1/s$ outside ([[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^ex-a9-4-1|Example §A9.4.1]]). In the mid-plane of a finite solenoid the field is smaller, and outside it falls off faster, eventually as $1/s^2$, like the vector potential of a magnetic dipole. The circle of radius $s$ encloses less flux than in the long-solenoid count: the interior field of a short coil is weaker, and outside the coil part of the flux returns through the circle. In the end plane of the $L = 4a$ solenoid (dotted) the field is about half the mid-plane value, as the axial field is there.*

> [!remark] Remark: How the analogy fixes directions
> For a cylindrically symmetric $\partial\vec B/\partial t$ along the axis (a solenoid whose current changes, a rotating charged cylinder), $\vec E$ is azimuthal, $\vec E = E_\varphi(s)\,\hat\varphi$, just as the magnetic field of a long cylinder carrying an axial current is: a Gaussian cylinder about the axis with $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ rules out a radial part, and a rectangle in a plane containing the axis encloses no flux (the field is axial), so $E_z$ is the same at all $s$ and vanishes far away. If $\vec B$ points up and increases, $\vec E$ runs clockwise as seen from above (Griffiths Example 7.7): the circulation is opposite to that of a current making $\vec B$ grow, which is Lenz's law in field form. In the opposite geometry, a long straight wire with a changing current, $\vec B$ circles the wire and the induced $\vec E$ runs *parallel* to it ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^mod-b8-2-5|Model §B8.2.5]]), as the magnetic field of a solenoid runs parallel to its axis.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.2, Examples 7.7 and 7.9 · PHY 422, practice final, Problem 1(b) (instructor's typed solution)*

## The quasistatic approximation

> [!model] Model §B8.2.5: Quasistatic Approximation
> To use Faraday's law one needs $\vec B(\vec r, t)$. In the **quasistatic approximation** it is computed from the currents at the *same* instant with the laws of magnetostatics (Biot–Savart, Ampère's law in its static form), as if the currents were steady, and that $\vec B$ is inserted on the right side of Faraday's law. Assumptions:
> - the currents change slowly: if $\tau$ is the time over which they change substantially, the approximation holds only at distances $s \ll c\tau$ from the sources (Griffiths eq. (7.21));
> - at each instant the current is the same all along a wire (true for short circuits; false for long transmission lines unless they are driven in a distributed, synchronized way);
> - the displacement current is negligible in the Ampère–Maxwell law ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^def-b8-4-1|Def. §B8.4.1]]).
>
> It fails at large distances and high frequencies, where the field at a point depends on the currents at earlier times ("electromagnetic news travels at the speed of light"): electromagnetic waves ([[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum|§B10.1]]) and retarded fields ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]]; where quasistatics fails: [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^ex-b11-1-2|Example §B11.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.2 (pp. 319–320), eq. (7.21), Example 7.9 and footnote 14 · Nelson ch. 18, §18.3.2*

^mod-b8-2-5

> [!remark] Remark: A small fraud, and where it shows
> Griffiths calls this "a small fraud that tarnishes many applications of Faraday's law": induction needs changing fields, yet the fields are computed with the rules of statics. The error is usually negligible; even a wire cut with scissors ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^ex-b8-2-2|Example §B8.2.2]]) is "static enough". Where it shows is Griffiths' Example 7.9. An infinite straight wire carries a slowly varying current $I(t)$; quasistatically $\vec B = (\mu_0I/2\pi s)\hat\varphi$ circles it. The induced $\vec E$ runs along the wire, $\vec E = E(s)\hat z$ (the analogue of a solenoid's field). A rectangular loop of length $l$ in a plane through the wire, between radii $s_0$ and $s$, gives
>
> $$
> E(s_0)\,l - E(s)\,l = -\frac{d}{dt}\int\vec B\cdot d\vec a = -\frac{\mu_0l}{2\pi}\frac{dI}{dt}\int_{s_0}^{s}\frac{ds'}{s'} , \qquad \vec E(s) = \Bigl[\frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\frac{dI}{dt}\ln s + K\Bigr]\hat z
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (7.20)), with $K$ independent of $s$ (it depends on the history of $I$). The field grows without bound as $s \to \infty$, which cannot be right: at $s \gtrsim c\tau$ the approximation has been overstepped. The example is also artificial in assuming the same current at every point of an infinite wire at the same instant (Griffiths' footnote 14).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.2 (pp. 319–320), Example 7.9, eqs. (7.20)–(7.21)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B8.2.1: Rotating Charged Cylinder with a Coaxial Solenoid
> A long cylindrical shell of radius $R_2$ carries a uniform surface charge $\sigma$ and rotates with angular velocity $\omega$ about its axis ($\hat z$). Inside it, coaxial, is a long solenoid of radius $R_1 < R_2$ with $n$ turns per length carrying current $I$. Take the solenoid current and the rotation both counterclockwise as seen from $+z$.
>
> *(a) The magnetic field.* The solenoid gives $\mu_0nI\hat z$ inside and zero outside ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]]; [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^rem-b6-3-4|§B6.3, Remark: How to use Ampère's law]]). The rotating shell is a surface current $\vec K = \sigma\vec v = \sigma\omega R_2\,\hat\varphi$, a solenoid with $nI \to K$: field $\mu_0\sigma\omega R_2\,\hat z$ inside it, zero outside. Superposing,
>
> $$
> \vec B = \begin{cases} \mu_0\bigl(nI + \sigma\omega R_2\bigr)\hat z, & s < R_1,\\ \mu_0\sigma\omega R_2\,\hat z, & R_1 < s < R_2,\\ \vec 0, & s > R_2 . \end{cases}
> $$
>
> *(b) The induced electric field* when $I(t)$ and $\omega(t)$ change, in the quasistatic approximation ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^mod-b8-2-5|Model §B8.2.5]]). The field above, with $I(t)$ and $\omega(t)$, is axial and cylindrically symmetric, so $\vec E = E_\varphi(s)\hat\varphi$ ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^rem-b8-2-4|Remark: How the analogy fixes directions]]), and Faraday's law on a circle of radius $s$ ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^thm-b8-2-4|Theorem §B8.2.4]]) gives $2\pi sE_\varphi = -d\Phi_{\text{enc}}/dt$, with
>
> $$
> \Phi_{\text{enc}} = \begin{cases} \pi s^2\mu_0(nI + \sigma\omega R_2), & s < R_1,\\ \pi R_1^2\mu_0nI + \pi s^2\mu_0\sigma\omega R_2, & R_1 < s < R_2,\\ \pi R_1^2\mu_0nI + \pi R_2^2\mu_0\sigma\omega R_2, & s > R_2 . \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> \vec E = E_\varphi\,\hat\varphi, \qquad E_\varphi = -\frac{\mu_0}{2}\begin{cases} \bigl(n\dot I + \sigma R_2\dot\omega\bigr)s, & s < R_1,\\ n\dot I\,\dfrac{R_1^2}{s} + \sigma R_2\dot\omega\,s, & R_1 < s < R_2,\\ \bigl(n\dot IR_1^2 + \sigma R_2^3\dot\omega\bigr)\dfrac1s, & s > R_2 . \end{cases}
> $$
>
> $E_\varphi$ is continuous at $R_1$ and $R_2$, as it must be (tangential $\vec E$, [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-6|Theorem §B8.4.6]]). Outside everything $\vec B = \vec 0$, yet $\vec E \neq \vec 0$: it responds to the flux enclosed, as in [[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^ex-a9-4-1|Example §A9.4.1]].
>
> *Forward.* Part (c) of the problem lets the shell (mass $\rho_m$ per area, initially at rest) turn freely while the solenoid current is switched off from $I_0$. The induced field at $s = R_2$ exerts a torque on the shell's charge, including the term $\sigma R_2\dot\omega$ from the shell's own changing rotation (its back-emf), and the shell ends up spinning: the instructor's answer is $\omega_f = \mu_0\sigma nR_1^2I_0/(2\rho_mR_2^2 + \mu_0\sigma^2R_2^3)$. Where that angular momentum was before, in the fields, is worked out in [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^ex-b9-2-2|Example §B9.2.2]], which finds the same $\omega_f$; Griffiths' Example 7.8 (a charged wheel and a switched-off field) is the simpler version.
>

> [!example] Example §B8.2.2: Cutting a Wire
> A square loop of side $a$ and resistance $R$ lies in a plane with a long straight wire carrying current $I$ to the right, its near side parallel to the wire at distance $s$. Someone cuts the wire, so $I$ drops to zero. Which way does the induced current flow, and how much charge passes a point of the loop?
>
> *Flux.* Quasistatically $B = \mu_0I/2\pi s'$, out of the page above the wire, and with the counterclockwise sense
>
> $$
> \Phi(t) = \int_0^a\!\!\int_s^{s + a}\frac{\mu_0I(t)}{2\pi s'}\,ds'\,dz = \frac{\mu_0aI(t)}{2\pi}\ln\frac{s + a}{s} .
> $$
>
> *Direction.* The outward flux disappears, so the induced current flows **counterclockwise**, trying to maintain it (Lenz): $\mathcal E = -\dot\Phi > 0$ in the counterclockwise sense when $\dot I < 0$.
>
> *Charge.* $I_{\text{loop}} = \mathcal E/R = -\dot\Phi/R$, so by the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]])
>
> $$
> \Delta Q = \int I_{\text{loop}}\,dt = \frac{\Phi(\text{before}) - \Phi(\text{after})}{R} = \frac{\mu_0aI}{2\pi R}\ln\Bigl(1 + \frac{a}{s}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> whatever the time dependence. For Griffiths' gentler ramp $I(t) = (1 - \alpha t)I$ on $0 \le t \le 1/\alpha$, the emf is constant, $\mathcal E = (\mu_0a\alpha I/2\pi)\ln(1 + a/s)$, the current flows for the time $1/\alpha$, and the product is the same $\Delta Q$. Even the loop's own self-inductance $L$ does not change the answer: then $RI_{\text{loop}} + L\,dI_{\text{loop}}/dt = -\dot\Phi$, and since $I_{\text{loop}}$ is zero before and after, integrating gives $R\,\Delta Q = -\Delta\Phi$ again. A loop with a ballistic galvanometer measures a change of flux.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 10, Problem 5 (Wire cutting) · Griffiths Problem 7.18 (solved here; checked against the instructor's typed solution, 2023 HW 10, Problem 4; the remark on self-inductance worked out here)*
