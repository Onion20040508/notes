By now the divergence and curl of both fields are known: Gauss's law, $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$, Faraday's law ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^pr-b8-2-1|Principle §B8.2.1]]) and Ampère's law. Are they consistent once currents change in time? Level A met the failure of Ampère's law at a charging capacitor, introduced the displacement current and the Ampère–Maxwell law ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^def-a12-1-1|Def. §A12.1.1]], [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-1|Principle §A12.1.1]], [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^thm-a12-1-2|Theorem §A12.1.2]]) and stated Maxwell's equations in integral form ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|Principle §A12.1.3]]), leaving their differential form and their form in matter to level B. This section shows the inconsistency as an identity of vector calculus, writes Maxwell's equations as local field equations, derives charge conservation and the persistence of the divergence equations from them, adds the polarization current and writes the equations for free charges and currents in matter, and completes the boundary conditions at interfaces carrying surface charge and current, including perfect conductors. From here on, the equations are the starting point: electrodynamics postulates them, where electrostatics and magnetostatics were built from Coulomb's and the Biot–Savart laws.

## Maxwell's correction to Ampère's law

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.4.1: Ampère's Law Contradicts Charge Conservation
> The four equations of pre-Maxwell electrodynamics, $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$, $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial t$ and $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ (Griffiths' list (i)–(iv), §7.3.1), imply $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$ (7.36). This contradicts the continuity equation $\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\partial\rho/\partial t$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]; Griffiths eq. (5.29)) wherever the charge density changes. Faraday's law passes the same test.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.3.1, eq. (7.36)*

^thm-b8-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The divergence of a curl vanishes ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]). For (iii): $0 = \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec E) = -\partial(\nabla\cdot\vec B)/\partial t$, which is $0$ by (ii): consistent. For (iv): $0 = \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B) = \mu_0\nabla\cdot\vec J$, so (iv) forces $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$, i.e. $\partial\rho/\partial t = 0$ everywhere: it can only hold for steady currents, for which it was derived from the Biot–Savart law ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]). The integral version is the charging capacitor, for which "the current enclosed by the loop" depends on the surface spanned ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^rem-a12-1-1|§A12.1, Remark: Two surfaces, two answers]]).

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]], [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]], [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §B8.4.1: Displacement Current
> The **displacement current** density is (Griffiths eq. (7.38))
>
> $$
> \vec J_d = \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t} ;
> $$
>
> its flux through a surface is level A's displacement current $I_d = \varepsilon_0\,d\Phi_E/dt$ ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^def-a12-1-1|Def. §A12.1.1]]). In matter it is $\vec J_d = \partial\vec D/\partial t$ (7.59), which includes the polarization current of [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-4|Theorem §B8.4.4]]. Despite the name, it is not a motion of charge (in vacuum); it is called a current because it enters Ampère's law beside $\vec J$, and "displacement" because of $\vec D$, the electric displacement.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.3.2, eq. (7.38); §7.3.5, eq. (7.59)*

^def-b8-4-1

> [!principle] Principle §B8.4.2: Maxwell's Equations
> The electric and magnetic fields of charges with density $\rho$ and current density $\vec J$ obey (Griffiths eq. (7.40))
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> &\text{(i)}\ \ \nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} \qquad &&\text{(Gauss's law)},\\
> &\text{(ii)}\ \ \nabla\cdot\vec B = 0 &&\text{(no magnetic charge)},\\
> &\text{(iii)}\ \ \nabla\times\vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t} &&\text{(Faraday's law)},\\
> &\text{(iv)}\ \ \nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t} &&\text{(Ampère's law with Maxwell's correction)},
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> and the fields act on a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ by the Lorentz force (7.41) ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]])
>
> $$
> \vec F = q\bigl(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Together, and with suitable boundary conditions (typically: fields vanish far from a localized source), they contain the whole theoretical content of classical electrodynamics, apart from the special properties of matter. Griffiths prefers the arrangement (7.43), fields on the left and sources on the right: $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$, $\nabla\times\vec E + \partial\vec B/\partial t = \vec 0$, $\nabla\times\vec B - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial\vec E/\partial t = \mu_0\vec J$.
>
> *Domain: classical electrodynamics, with $\rho$ and $\vec J$ the total (free plus bound) densities; the microscopic fields, or their macroscopic averages in matter ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-5|Theorem §B8.4.5]]); all speeds (the equations are already consistent with special relativity). Fails where quantum effects matter (photons: [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|QM Principle §A1.2.3]]). This is the differential form of [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|Principle §A12.1.3]]; the layer is unchanged (principle), the content is the same ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-3|Theorem §B8.4.3]]).*
> *Source: Griffiths §7.3.2–7.3.3, eqs. (7.37), (7.40)–(7.41), (7.43) and footnote 23 · Nelson ch. 18, §18.4.1, eq. (18.17)*

^pr-b8-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: How Maxwell fixed Ampère's law, and the change of logic
> - *The fix.* By continuity and Gauss's law, the offending divergence is itself a divergence: $\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\partial\rho/\partial t = -\partial(\varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E)/\partial t = -\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon_0\,\partial\vec E/\partial t)$. Adding $\varepsilon_0\,\partial\vec E/\partial t$ to $\vec J$ in Ampère's law kills it (Griffiths eq. (7.37)). In magnetostatics ($\vec E$ constant) nothing changes. For the charging capacitor the flat surface collects $\mu_0I$ from the conduction current, the balloon between the plates $\mu_0\varepsilon_0\int\partial_t\vec E\cdot d\vec a = \mu_0I$ from the displacement current ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^ex-a12-1-1|Example §A12.1.1]]).
> - *Not Maxwell's route.* The consistency argument is today's; Maxwell's own reasons came from a mechanical model of the ether, his first displacement term was not quite the present one, and he changed it quietly in later work (Nelson §18.4.1′b). In his day no experiment could detect the term: $\mu_0\varepsilon_0 \approx 1.1\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm{s^2/m^2}$ makes it tiny at laboratory frequencies, which is why Faraday never saw it. Consistency and elegance were only suggestive ("there might be other ways to doctor up Ampère's law"); the confirmation was Hertz's detection of electromagnetic waves in 1888 ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^rem-a12-1-6|§A12.1, Remark: Hertz's experiment]]), and the term is essential for the wave equation ([[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum|§B10.1]]).
> - *Change of logic.* Up to Chapter B7, Coulomb's law and the Biot–Savart law were the starting points (laws) and the field equations were derived from them for static sources. From now on the logic is reversed: Maxwell's equations and the Lorentz force are the principles, and Coulomb's and the Biot–Savart laws are their static solutions ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^rem-a12-1-2|§A12.1, Remark: Where each piece came from]]).
> - *"Induce".* Just as "a changing magnetic field induces an electric field", the new term reads "a changing electric field induces a magnetic field". Griffiths warns that this is a manner of speaking: $\partial\vec B/\partial t$ and $\partial\vec E/\partial t$ are themselves produced by charges and currents, which are the ultimate sources of all fields; the arrangement (7.43) expresses exactly that.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.3.2–7.3.3 (pp. 334–338) and footnotes 8, 20, 21 · Nelson ch. 18, §18.4.1 and §18.4.1′b*

^rem-b8-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.4.3: What Maxwell's Equations Contain
> 1. **Charge conservation.** The continuity equation $\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\partial\rho/\partial t$ (Griffiths eq. (7.42)) follows from (i) and (iv); its home is [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]], where it is the local form of charge conservation.
> 2. **The divergence equations are constraints.** If $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ and $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ hold at one instant, and $\vec E$, $\vec B$ evolve by the curl equations (iii), (iv) with sources obeying the continuity equation, then (i) and (ii) hold at all times. The eight component equations for six field components are therefore not overdetermined.
> 3. **Integral form.** For a fixed closed surface $S$ and a fixed loop $\mathcal P$ bounding a surface $S'$, the equations are equivalent to
>
> $$
> \oint_S\vec E\cdot d\vec a = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}, \quad \oint_S\vec B\cdot d\vec a = 0, \quad \oint_{\mathcal P}\vec E\cdot d\vec l = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S'}\vec B\cdot d\vec a, \quad \oint_{\mathcal P}\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0I_{\text{enc}} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S'}\vec E\cdot d\vec a ,
> $$
>
> level A's [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|Principle §A12.1.3]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.3.3, eq. (7.42) · Nelson ch. 18, §18.4.1 (the counting question) · PHY 422, final formula sheet (integral forms)*

^thm-b8-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Take the divergence of (iv): $0 = \mu_0\nabla\cdot\vec J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial(\nabla\cdot\vec E)/\partial t = \mu_0\bigl(\nabla\cdot\vec J + \partial\rho/\partial t\bigr)$ by (i).
>
> *2.* From (iii), $\partial(\nabla\cdot\vec B)/\partial t = -\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec E) = 0$, so $\nabla\cdot\vec B$ keeps its initial value $0$. From (iv), $\varepsilon_0\,\partial(\nabla\cdot\vec E)/\partial t = \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B)/\mu_0 - \nabla\cdot\vec J = -\nabla\cdot\vec J = \partial\rho/\partial t$, so $\partial(\nabla\cdot\vec E - \rho/\varepsilon_0)/\partial t = 0$ and the initial value $0$ persists.
>
> *3.* Integrate (i), (ii) over the volume inside $S$ and use the divergence theorem ([[§32 Flux Integrals and the Divergence Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-32-1|452 Thm. §32.1]]); integrate (iii), (iv) over $S'$ and use Stokes' theorem ([[§34 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-34-1|452 Thm. §34.1]]), taking $d/dt$ outside the integrals over the fixed surface; $\int_{S'}\vec J\cdot d\vec a = I_{\text{enc}}$. Conversely, applying the integral equations to small volumes and loops about a point and shrinking them gives back the local equations, as in [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^der-b8-2-2|§B8.2, Derivation of Theorem §B8.2.2]]. Level A's surface-independence theorem [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^thm-a12-1-2|Theorem §A12.1.2]] is item 1 in integral form.

> [!remark] Remark: Magnetic charge
> In empty space the equations are symmetric: $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$, $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial t$, $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$, $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial\vec E/\partial t$, and the substitution $\vec E \to \vec B$, $\vec B \to -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\vec E$ turns the first pair into the second and back. The sources spoil the symmetry. With a magnetic charge density $\rho_m$ and current $\vec J_m$ it would be restored (Griffiths eq. (7.44)):
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac{\rho_e}{\varepsilon_0}, \quad \nabla\cdot\vec B = \mu_0\rho_m, \quad \nabla\times\vec E = -\mu_0\vec J_m - \frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}, \quad \nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J_e + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t} ,
> $$
>
> and both charges would be conserved (7.45), by the divergences of the two curl equations. No magnetic charge has ever been found, in spite of diligent searches (one method: a monopole passing through a superconducting loop of self-inductance $L$ would change the current by a fixed step, Griffiths Problem 7.39). This is why magnetic multipole expansions start with the dipole ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-5|Theorem §B6.4.5]]). Dirac showed that a single monopole anywhere would explain why electric charge is quantized (Griffiths Problem 8.19, through the angular momentum of the fields of a charge and a monopole; field angular momentum: [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^def-b9-2-2|Def. §B9.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.3.4, eqs. (7.44)–(7.45), Problems 7.38–7.39*

## Maxwell's equations in matter

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.4.4: Polarization Current
> When the polarization $\vec P$ of a medium changes, the bound charge moves, and this motion is a current with density (Griffiths eq. (7.49))
>
> $$
> \vec J_p = \frac{\partial\vec P}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> It conserves bound charge, $\nabla\cdot\vec J_p = -\partial\rho_b/\partial t$ with $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\vec P$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]]). It is unrelated to the bound current $\vec J_b = \nabla\times\vec M$ of magnetization ([[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]]), which comes from the spin and orbital motion of electrons and needs no such companion: a changing $\vec M$ accumulates no charge.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.3.5, eqs. (7.47)–(7.49) and Figure 7.47*

^thm-b8-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take a small cylinder of the medium along $\vec P$ with end faces $da_\perp$. Its ends carry bound surface charge $\pm\sigma_b = \pm P$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]]). If $P$ grows, the charge on the ends grows, which takes the current $dI = (\partial\sigma_b/\partial t)\,da_\perp = (\partial P/\partial t)\,da_\perp$ through the cylinder: positive charges shift a little along $\vec P$, negative ones against it. A current per unit area along $\vec P$ is $\partial\vec P/\partial t$. Continuity: $\nabla\cdot\vec J_p = \partial(\nabla\cdot\vec P)/\partial t = -\partial\rho_b/\partial t$. For magnetization, $\nabla\cdot\vec J_b = \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec M) = 0$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]) at all times, so no charge piles up.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.4.5: Maxwell's Equations in Matter
> Split the charge into free and bound parts, $\rho = \rho_f + \rho_b = \rho_f - \nabla\cdot\vec P$ (Griffiths eq. (7.50)), and the current into free, magnetization and polarization parts, $\vec J = \vec J_f + \nabla\times\vec M + \partial\vec P/\partial t$ (7.51). With $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P$ ([[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^def-b5-2-2|Def. §B5.2.2]]) and $\vec H = \vec B/\mu_0 - \vec M$ ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^def-b7-2-2|Def. §B7.2.2]]), Maxwell's equations become (7.56)
>
> $$
> \text{(i)}\ \nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f, \qquad \text{(ii)}\ \nabla\cdot\vec B = 0, \qquad \text{(iii)}\ \nabla\times\vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}, \qquad \text{(iv)}\ \nabla\times\vec H = \vec J_f + \frac{\partial\vec D}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> They are not more general than [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]], only a convenient division of the sources into those we control (free) and those the medium supplies (bound). They are in hybrid notation ($\vec E$ and $\vec D$, $\vec B$ and $\vec H$) and must be completed by constitutive relations giving $\vec D$ and $\vec H$ in terms of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.3.5, eqs. (7.50)–(7.56)*

^thm-b8-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert (7.50) into Gauss's law: $\varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho_f - \nabla\cdot\vec P$, i.e. $\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P) = \rho_f$ (7.52). Insert (7.51) into the Ampère–Maxwell law: $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0(\vec J_f + \nabla\times\vec M + \partial_t\vec P) + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t\vec E$; dividing by $\mu_0$ and collecting, $\nabla\times(\vec B/\mu_0 - \vec M) = \vec J_f + \partial_t(\varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P)$ (7.54). Faraday's law and $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ contain no sources and are unchanged. The polarization current ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-4|Theorem §B8.4.4]]) is what makes $\partial\vec D/\partial t$, not just $\varepsilon_0\partial\vec E/\partial t$, appear in (iv).

> [!remark] Remark: Constitutive relations in time-dependent fields
> For linear media the constitutive relations are (Griffiths eqs. (7.57)–(7.58)) $\vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E$, $\vec M = \chi_m\vec H$, hence $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$, $\vec H = \vec B/\mu$, with $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0(1 + \chi_e)$ and $\mu = \mu_0(1 + \chi_m)$; their homes, with their ranges of validity, are [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^law-b5-3-1|Law §B5.3.1]] and [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^law-b7-2-5|Law §B7.2.5]] (level A's dielectric constant $\kappa$ is $\varepsilon/\varepsilon_0$: [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]]; level A's linear magnetic response: [[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^law-a8-6-1|Law §A8.6.1]]). In time-dependent fields a medium cannot respond instantly, so $\chi_e$ and $\chi_m$ hold separately at each frequency and depend on it; a conductor adds $\vec J_f = \sigma\vec E$ ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^law-b8-1-1|Law §B8.1.1]]). Waves in such media are [[§B10.2 Waves in Linear Media, Reflection and Transmission|§B10.2]] and [[§B10.3★ Conductors and Dispersion|§B10.3★]]; the optics of dielectrics and metals is in Waves and Optics ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|WO Model §B8.1.1]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-1|WO Theorem §B13.1.1]]). How the microscopic fields are averaged into macroscopic ones is done for statics in [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]]; the time-dependent averaging is level C.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.3.5, eqs. (7.57)–(7.59)*

## Boundary conditions

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.4.6: General Boundary Conditions
> Let a surface separate medium 2 from medium 1, with unit normal $\hat n$ pointing from 2 to 1 (medium 1 is \"above\", medium 2 \"below\" in the static conditions), and let it carry free surface charge $\sigma_f$ and free surface current $\vec K_f$. Then, at every instant (Griffiths eqs. (7.60)–(7.63)),
>
> $$
> D_1^\perp - D_2^\perp = \sigma_f, \qquad B_1^\perp - B_2^\perp = 0, \qquad \vec E_1^\parallel - \vec E_2^\parallel = \vec 0, \qquad \vec H_1^\parallel - \vec H_2^\parallel = \vec K_f\times\hat n .
> $$
>
> For linear media (7.64): $\varepsilon_1E_1^\perp - \varepsilon_2E_2^\perp = \sigma_f$, $B_1^\perp = B_2^\perp$, $\vec E_1^\parallel = \vec E_2^\parallel$, $\vec B_1^\parallel/\mu_1 - \vec B_2^\parallel/\mu_2 = \vec K_f\times\hat n$; with no free surface charge or current, (7.65), the right sides vanish.
>
> The source-free case, derived from Maxwell's integral equations, is [[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-1|WO Theorem §B9.1.1]], which is its home and the basis of reflection and refraction. Added here: the jumps caused by surface sources, the vector form of the $\vec H$ condition, and the observation that the time derivatives drop out, so the static conditions of [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]] ($\vec E$), [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]] ($\vec D$), [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]] ($\vec B$) and [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]] ($\vec H$) hold unchanged in electrodynamics.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.3.6, eqs. (7.60)–(7.65), Figures 7.48–7.49 · Nelson ch. 18, §18.4.2*

^thm-b8-4-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Use the integral form of [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-5|Theorem §B8.4.5]] (obtained as in [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-3|Theorem §B8.4.3]] (3)): $\oint\vec D\cdot d\vec a = Q_{f,\text{enc}}$, $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec a = 0$, $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = -\frac{d}{dt}\int\vec B\cdot d\vec a$, $\oint\vec H\cdot d\vec l = I_{f,\text{enc}} + \frac{d}{dt}\int\vec D\cdot d\vec a$. Proceed exactly as in [[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^der-b9-1-1|WO Derivation of Theorem §B9.1.1]], keeping the surface sources.
>
> *Normal components.* A wafer-thin pillbox of face area $a$ straddling the surface: as its height goes to zero, the side wall contributes nothing and volume charge inside vanishes, but the surface charge $\sigma_fa$ stays inside. So $\vec D_1\cdot\hat n\,a - \vec D_2\cdot\hat n\,a = \sigma_fa$. The same box with $\vec B$ encloses no magnetic charge.
>
> *Tangential components.* A thin rectangle straddling the surface, with long sides $\vec l$ (tangent, of length $l$) in medium 1 and $-\vec l$ in medium 2. As its width goes to zero, the end segments drop out and the fluxes $\int\vec B\cdot d\vec a$ and $\int\vec D\cdot d\vec a$ through it vanish together with their time derivatives (bounded fields, area $\to 0$): $(\vec E_1 - \vec E_2)\cdot\vec l = 0$. A volume current contributes nothing to $I_{f,\text{enc}}$ in the limit, but a surface current does: the rectangle's normal is $\hat n\times\hat l$, so $I_{f,\text{enc}} = \vec K_f\cdot(\hat n\times\vec l) = (\vec K_f\times\hat n)\cdot\vec l$ by the cyclic triple product, and $(\vec H_1 - \vec H_2)\cdot\vec l = (\vec K_f\times\hat n)\cdot\vec l$. Both hold for every tangent $\vec l$, and $\vec K_f\times\hat n$ is tangent, which gives the vector conditions. Linear media: substitute $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$ and $\vec H = \vec B/\mu$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.4.7: Fields at a Perfect Conductor
> In a perfect conductor ($\sigma \to \infty$ with finite current density) $\vec E = \vec 0$ inside. Then:
> 1. the magnetic field inside cannot change, $\partial\vec B/\partial t = \vec 0$, and the flux through any closed loop of perfectly conducting material is constant in time;
> 2. at its surface, with medium 1 outside and $\hat n$ the outward normal, $\vec E_1^\parallel = \vec 0$ and $\hat n\cdot\vec D_1 = \sigma_f$;
> 3. if the field inside is zero (a superconductor, or a perfect conductor that was field-free from the start), then also $B_1^\perp = 0$ and $\vec K_f = \hat n\times\vec H_1$: the outside field is tangent to the surface, and the surface current is fixed by it.
>
> These are the boundary conditions of waveguides ([[§B10.4★ Guided Waves|§B10.4★]]) and of the perfectly conducting cylinder in a static field ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^ex-b7-2-2|Example §B7.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 7.44(a)–(b) (solved here) · Nelson ch. 18, §18.4.2*

^thm-b8-4-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\vec E = \vec J/\sigma \to \vec 0$ ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^law-b8-1-1|Law §B8.1.1]]). Faraday's law inside gives $\partial\vec B/\partial t = -\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$. For a perfectly conducting loop, possibly moving, the emf is zero because the total force per unit charge on the carriers vanishes (finite $\vec J$ with $\sigma = \infty$), so the universal flux rule ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]]) gives $d\Phi/dt = 0$.
>
> *2.* [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-6|Theorem §B8.4.6]] with $\vec E_2 = \vec 0$, $\vec D_2 = \vec 0$ inside.
>
> *3.* With $\vec B_2 = \vec 0$ (so $\vec H_2 = \vec 0$), continuity of $B^\perp$ gives $B_1^\perp = 0$, and the $\vec H$ condition gives $\vec H_1^\parallel = \vec K_f\times\hat n$. Taking $\hat n\times$ of both sides, $\hat n\times\vec H_1 = \hat n\times(\vec K_f\times\hat n) = \vec K_f(\hat n\cdot\hat n) - \hat n(\hat n\cdot\vec K_f) = \vec K_f$, since $\vec K_f$ is tangent.

> [!remark] Remark: Perfect conductor or superconductor?
> Item 1 says only that $\vec B$ inside a perfect conductor is frozen at whatever value it had. A superconductor does more: below its critical temperature the field inside is zero, even if a field was present when it was cooled (the Meissner effect; [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^rem-a5-3-4|§A5.3, Remark: Zero resistance]]). This is sometimes called perfect diamagnetism, but the screening surface currents are free currents, not the bound currents of a magnetized material (Griffiths' footnote 30). Its explanation is quantum mechanical ([[§A6.4 Superconductivity#^mod-a6-4-6|QM Model §A6.4.6]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 7.44 and footnote 30*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B8.4.1: A Leaky Spherical Capacitor Has No Magnetic Field
> Two concentric metal shells, radii $a < b$, carry charges $Q(t)$ and $-Q(t)$, and the space between them is filled with an ohmic material of conductivity $\sigma$. A radial current flows,
>
> $$
> \vec J = \sigma\vec E = \frac{\sigma Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\,\hat r, \qquad I = -\dot Q = \oint\vec J\cdot d\vec a = \frac{\sigma Q}{\varepsilon_0} ,
> $$
>
> so the charge leaks away as $Q = Q_0e^{-\sigma t/\varepsilon_0}$, the relaxation of [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]. The configuration is spherically symmetric, so $\vec B$ could only be radial, $B_r(r)$; then $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec a = 4\pi r^2B_r = 0$ forces $\vec B = \vec 0$. A current with no magnetic field? The Biot–Savart law and Ampère's law in the form $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ would say otherwise, but this is not a steady state. The displacement current
>
> $$
> \vec J_d = \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t} = \frac{\dot Q}{4\pi r^2}\,\hat r = -\frac{\sigma Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\,\hat r = -\vec J
> $$
>
> cancels the conduction current exactly, so $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0(\vec J + \vec J_d) = \vec 0$ and, with $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$, $\vec B = \vec 0$ is consistent. Without Maxwell's term the equations would demand a curl of $\vec B$ that no spherically symmetric field can have.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 7.14*

^ex-b8-4-1

> [!example] Example §B8.4.2: Conduction or Displacement Current in Sea Water?
> Sea water at $\nu = 4\times10^8\ \mathrm{Hz}$ has $\varepsilon = 81\varepsilon_0$, $\mu = \mu_0$ and resistivity $\rho = 0.23\ \Omega\,\mathrm m$. Compare the conduction and displacement currents.
>
> Drive a parallel-plate capacitor immersed in the water with $V_0\cos(2\pi\nu t)$, so that $E = E_0\cos\omega t$ between the plates, $\omega = 2\pi\nu$. Then $J_f = \sigma E_0\cos\omega t$ ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^law-b8-1-1|Law §B8.1.1]]) and $J_d = \partial D/\partial t = -\varepsilon\omega E_0\sin\omega t$ ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^def-b8-4-1|Def. §B8.4.1]]): a quarter period out of phase, with amplitude ratio
>
> $$
> \frac{J_f}{J_d} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon\omega} = \frac{1}{\rho\,\varepsilon\,2\pi\nu} = \frac{1}{(0.23)(81)(8.85\times10^{-12})(2\pi)(4\times10^8)} = \frac{1}{0.41} \approx 2.4 .
> $$
>
> At this frequency (a UHF radio frequency) sea water is neither a good conductor nor a good insulator: the two currents are comparable. The ratio is $1/\omega\tau$ with the relaxation time $\tau = \varepsilon/\sigma \approx 1.6\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm s$ of [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]: conduction dominates for $\omega\tau \ll 1$, displacement for $\omega\tau \gg 1$, with the crossover at $\nu = 1/2\pi\tau \approx 1\ \mathrm{GHz}$ (assuming $\varepsilon$ and $\sigma$ stay constant). This criterion decides how waves penetrate a conductor ([[§B10.3★ Conductors and Dispersion|§B10.3★]]; [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-4|WO Theorem §B13.2.4]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 7.40 (solved here, with its hint)*
