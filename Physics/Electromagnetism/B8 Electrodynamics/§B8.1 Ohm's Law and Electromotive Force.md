What pushes the charges around a circuit, and how much current does a given push produce? Level A answered with the empirical law $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ and its Drude explanation ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-1|Law §A5.3.1]], [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-4|Theorem §A5.2.4]]), defined the emf of a source as work per unit charge ([[§A6.1 Electromotive Force#^def-a6-1-1|Def. §A6.1.1]]), and found the motional emf of a sliding rod both from the flux and from $q\vec v\times\vec B$ ([[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-1|Theorem §A9.3.1]], [[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-2|Theorem §A9.3.2]]), leaving the general proof of the flux rule to level B ([[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^rem-a9-3-1|§A9.3, Remark: One law for two causes]]). This section states Ohm's law for an arbitrary force per unit charge, turns steady conduction into a boundary-value problem for Laplace's equation, relates resistance to capacitance and derives the charge-relaxation time, defines the emf as the circulation $\oint\vec f\cdot d\vec l$, and proves the flux rule for every loop moving through a static field, together with its limits. All of this is magnetostatics and electrostatics plus the Lorentz force; the genuinely new law, Faraday's, is the subject of [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields|§B8.2]].

## Ohm's law

> [!law] Law §B8.1.1: Ohm's Law
> In most materials the current density is proportional to the force per unit charge $\vec f$ acting on the carriers (Griffiths eq. (7.1)):
>
> $$
> \vec J = \sigma\vec f ,
> $$
>
> with the **conductivity** $\sigma$ an empirical constant of the material; its reciprocal is the **resistivity** $\rho = 1/\sigma$ ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^def-a5-3-1|Def. §A5.3.1]]). When the push is electromagnetic, $\vec f = \vec E + \vec v\times\vec B$ (Lorentz force per charge, [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]) and $\vec J = \sigma(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B)$ (7.2). The carriers' drift is ordinarily so slow that the magnetic term is negligible, leaving (7.3)
>
> $$
> \vec J = \sigma\vec E .
> $$
>
> Materials with $\sigma \to \infty$ are idealized as **perfect conductors** (then $\vec E = \vec J/\sigma = \vec 0$ inside, even while current flows), insulators as $\sigma = 0$.
>
> *Valid when: linear response, at fixed temperature, in isotropic materials (metals, electrolytes such as salt water), for fields that vary slowly compared with the carriers' collision time (at higher frequencies $\sigma$ depends on $\omega$: [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-2|WO Theorem §B13.2.2]]). The magnetic term must be kept when the carriers move fast or the conductor itself moves (plasmas; motional emf below). Fails for diodes and other semiconductor devices, gas discharges, superconductors.*
> *Source: Griffiths §7.1.1, eqs. (7.1)–(7.3), Table 7.1 · Nelson ch. 8, §8.5.1, eq. (8.8)*

^law-b8-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: What level B adds to Ohm's law
> - *Layer.* Ohm's law stays an empirical law, as in [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-1|Law §A5.3.1]]; Griffiths calls it "not really a true law, in the sense of Coulomb's or Ampère's", a rule of thumb that many materials obey. What is new is the form $\vec J = \sigma\vec f$: whatever pushes the carriers (electric, magnetic, chemical), the drift responds linearly. That form is what makes motional emfs and Ohm's law fit together below.
> - *The microscopic picture.* Why a constant force gives a constant current, not a growing one, is the collision argument of the Drude model ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^mod-a5-2-3|Model §A5.2.3]], [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-4|Theorem §A5.2.4]]; at optical frequencies [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^mod-b13-2-1|WO Model §B13.2.1]]). Griffiths adds a point that the "stop sign" version of the argument misses. If each carrier started from rest at each collision and accelerated over a fixed distance $\lambda$, the time per block would be $\sqrt{2\lambda/a}$ and the mean velocity $\sqrt{\lambda a/2}$, proportional to $\sqrt E$: a nonlinear law. Linearity comes from the thermal motion: the carriers already move at $v_{\text{thermal}}$, independent of $E$, so the time between collisions $\lambda/v_{\text{thermal}}$ does not depend on the field, and $v_{\text{ave}} = a\lambda/2v_{\text{thermal}}$. With $n$ molecules per volume and $f$ free carriers each (Griffiths eq. (7.6)),
>
> $$
> \vec J = nfq\,\vec v_{\text{ave}} = \Bigl(\frac{nf\lambda q^2}{2mv_{\text{thermal}}}\Bigr)\vec E ,
> $$
>
> which shows the right ingredients (proportional to the carrier density, decreasing with temperature) but is not an accurate formula; the real theory of conduction in metals is quantum mechanical.
> - *Dissipation.* Nelson stresses that $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ relates a quantity that changes sign under time reversal ($\vec J$) to one that does not ($\vec E$): it is irreversible, and the work done on the carriers becomes heat at the rate $\vec J\cdot\vec E = \sigma E^2$ per unit volume ([[§A5.4 Electrical Power#^rem-a5-4-1|§A5.4, Remark: Power per unit volume]]), $P = VI = I^2R$ for a resistor (Griffiths eq. (7.7), the Joule heating law). That term reappears in the energy balance of the field ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem|§B9.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.1.1 (pp. 300–301), eqs. (7.6)–(7.7) · Nelson ch. 8, §8.5.1, §8.5.3*

^rem-b8-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: σ and ρ do double duty
> In this section $\sigma$ is the conductivity and $\rho$ the resistivity, as in level A; elsewhere in Griffiths (and in [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations|§B8.4]] below) $\sigma$ is a surface charge density and $\rho$ a volume charge density. Griffiths apologizes for the clash; the meaning is always clear from the units. Where both appear, as in [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]], the charge density is written $\rho_f$.

## Steady currents in an ohmic conductor

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.2: Steady Currents in a Homogeneous Ohmic Conductor
> Let steady currents flow in a material of uniform conductivity $\sigma$ obeying $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$. Then:
> 1. the charge density inside vanishes, $\rho = 0$ (Griffiths eq. (7.5)); any unbalanced charge sits on the surfaces;
> 2. the potential satisfies Laplace's equation, $\nabla^2V = 0$, inside the material;
> 3. on an electrode (a perfect conductor) $V$ is constant; on a surface bordering an insulator, $\vec J\cdot\hat n = 0$, i.e. $\partial V/\partial n = 0$;
> 4. these conditions determine $V$ uniquely, and the total current between two electrodes is proportional to their potential difference,
>
> $$
> V = IR ,
> $$
>
> with a **resistance** $R$ fixed by the geometry and $\sigma$ alone (7.4).
>
> All the methods of Chapter B3 for Laplace's equation apply to resistors.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.1.1, eqs. (7.4)–(7.5) and Example 7.3*

^thm-b8-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Steady means $\partial\rho/\partial t = 0$, so the continuity equation ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]) gives $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$. With $\sigma$ uniform, $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \sigma^{-1}\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$, and Gauss's law ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]) gives $\rho = \varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ in the interior. Charge can only accumulate where $\sigma$ jumps, on the surfaces.
>
> *2.* Steady currents produce a static magnetic field, so $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]) and $\vec E = -\nabla V$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]). Then $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ reads $\nabla^2V = 0$.
>
> *3.* A perfect conductor is an equipotential (no field inside, $\sigma = \infty$ with finite $\vec J$). At a boundary with an insulator no charge may leak out, $\vec J\cdot\hat n = 0$, hence $\vec E\cdot\hat n = -\partial V/\partial n = 0$.
>
> *4.* Laplace's equation with $V$ given on part of the boundary and $\partial V/\partial n$ given on the rest has at most one solution (Griffiths Problem 3.5; [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]). The problem is linear: multiplying the electrode potentials by a constant multiplies $V$, hence $\vec E$, $\vec J$ and $I = \int\vec J\cdot d\vec a$, by the same constant. So $I/V$ is independent of $V$; it depends only on the shape and on $\sigma$.

^der-b8-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^law-b8-1-1|Law §B8.1.1]], [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]], [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]], [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Where the steering charge sits
> Item 1 says the interior of a current-carrying wire is neutral, as for a conductor in equilibrium ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]]), but for a different reason: there $\vec E = \vec 0$, here $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ with $\vec E \neq \vec 0$. The field inside the wire is produced by charges on its *surface*. They are what makes the current the same all around a circuit although the push comes from the battery alone (Griffiths §7.1.2): if more current flowed into a bend than out of it, charge would pile up at the bend, and its field would slow the inflow and speed the outflow until they matched. The adjustment is so fast that the current can be taken uniform around a circuit even at radio frequencies. The amounts of surface charge are tiny, and computing them is hard (Griffiths' footnotes 1 and 3).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.1.1–7.1.2 (pp. 299–300, 303), Figure 7.8*

^rem-b8-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.3: Resistance, Capacitance and Charge Relaxation
> Let two electrodes be embedded in a homogeneous medium of conductivity $\sigma$ and permittivity $\varepsilon_0$. Then:
> 1. the resistance $R$ between them and the capacitance $C$ of the same pair are related by
>
> $$
> R = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{\sigma C} ;
> $$
>
> 2. if the electrodes are charged to a potential difference $V_0$ and disconnected, the charge leaks through the medium and $V(t) = V_0e^{-t/\tau}$ with
>
> $$
> \tau = RC = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{\sigma} ;
> $$
>
> 3. more generally, any free charge density inside a homogeneous ohmic medium decays at every point with the same time constant, $\rho_f(\vec r, t) = \rho_f(\vec r, 0)\,e^{-t/\tau}$.
>
> In a linear dielectric that also conducts (salt water, a leaky capacitor), $\varepsilon_0$ is replaced by its permittivity $\varepsilon$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^law-b5-3-1|Law §B5.3.1]]): $RC = \varepsilon/\sigma = \tau$ (Nelson eq. (8.12); Griffiths eq. (9.120)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 7.3 (solved here) and §9.4.1, eq. (9.120) · Nelson ch. 8, §8.6, Idea 8.12*

^thm-b8-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Put charge $+Q$ on electrode 1 and $-Q$ on electrode 2, and enclose electrode 1 by a surface $S$ lying in the medium. Gauss's law gives $\oint_S\vec E\cdot d\vec a = Q/\varepsilon_0$, and the current leaving through $S$ is $I = \oint_S\vec J\cdot d\vec a = \sigma\oint_S\vec E\cdot d\vec a = \sigma Q/\varepsilon_0$. The field pattern, and hence $V = Q/C$, is that of the electrostatic problem with the same electrode potentials ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]]: Laplace's equation with the same values on the electrodes; the medium's outer boundary is taken far away). So
>
> $$
> R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{Q/C}{\sigma Q/\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{\sigma C} .
> $$
>
> *2.* After disconnection the only current is the leakage, $dQ/dt = -I = -(\sigma/\varepsilon_0)Q$, so $Q \propto e^{-\sigma t/\varepsilon_0}$, and $V = Q/C$ decays the same way, with $\tau = \varepsilon_0/\sigma = RC$ by part 1 (as for the discharge of [[§A6.5 RC Circuits#^thm-a6-5-2|Theorem §A6.5.2]]).
>
> *3.* Continuity, Ohm's law and Gauss's law at a point: $\partial\rho_f/\partial t = -\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\sigma\nabla\cdot\vec E = -(\sigma/\varepsilon_0)\rho_f$, an ordinary differential equation in $t$ at each fixed $\vec r$. In a linear dielectric Gauss's law for $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$ gives $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho_f/\varepsilon$ and part 1 uses $\oint\vec D\cdot d\vec a = Q$; everywhere $\varepsilon_0 \to \varepsilon$.

^der-b8-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^law-b8-1-1|Law §B8.1.1]], [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]], [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]], [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the relaxation time measures
> - *Good and poor conductors.* $\tau = \varepsilon/\sigma$ compares the medium with the other times of a problem: it is a good conductor if $\tau$ is much shorter than them (for oscillations, $\omega\tau \ll 1$), a poor one if longer (Griffiths §9.4.1; continued in [[§B10.3★ Conductors and Dispersion|§B10.3★]]). Nelson's salt water, $\sigma \approx 0.1\ \Omega^{-1}\mathrm m^{-1}$ at $100\ \mathrm{mM}$ and $\varepsilon \approx 80\varepsilon_0$, has $\tau \approx 80 \times 8.85\times10^{-12}/0.1\ \mathrm s \approx 7\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm s$: for anything slower, such as nerve impulses (milliseconds), the solution is locally neutral and $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ holds as in electrostatics. This is why the electric field of a current source in the body (an electrocardiogram, an electroencephalogram) has the shape of an electrostatic dipole field (Nelson §8.7).
> - *A limit of the model.* For copper the formula gives $\tau \approx 10^{-19}\ \mathrm s$, far shorter than the collision time $\sim 10^{-14}\ \mathrm s$ ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^ex-a5-2-2|Example §A5.2.2]]). Ohm's law does not hold on such short times, and the actual dissipation time of free charge in a good conductor is of the order of the collision time (Griffiths' footnote 17 to §9.4.1). The conclusion that free charge disappears from the interior quickly is unaffected.
> - *Measuring σ with two spheres.* Two metal spheres of radius $a$, far apart in sea water, have $C \to 2\pi\varepsilon_0a$ ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^ex-a4-1-2|Example §A4.1.2]]), so part 1 gives $R = 1/(2\pi\sigma a)$, independent of their separation: the resistance is dominated by the water next to each sphere, where the current is crowded (Griffiths Problem 7.1(c)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.4.1 (p. 412) and footnote 17; Problem 7.1 · Nelson ch. 8, §8.6–8.7*

## Electromotive force

> [!definition] Definition §B8.1.1: Electromotive Force
> Split the force per unit charge driving the current around a circuit into the force of the **source**, $\vec f_s$ (chemical in a battery, mechanical in a generator, light in a photocell, a temperature gradient in a thermocouple), usually confined to one part of the loop, and an electrostatic part $\vec E$ that smooths the flow and carries the source's influence around the circuit (Griffiths eq. (7.8)): $\vec f = \vec f_s + \vec E$. The **electromotive force** of the circuit is the line integral of $\vec f$ once around the loop, taken at one instant (7.9):
>
> $$
> \mathcal E = \oint\vec f\cdot d\vec l = \oint\vec f_s\cdot d\vec l ,
> $$
>
> the second form because $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$ for an electrostatic field. The sense of integration is a choice; a negative $\mathcal E$ drives current against it.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.1.2, eqs. (7.8)–(7.9)*

^def-b8-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: One definition for level A's two
> Level A defined the emf twice: as the work per unit charge done by a source ([[§A6.1 Electromotive Force#^def-a6-1-1|Def. §A6.1.1]]) and, for induction, as $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l$ ([[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^def-a9-4-1|Def. §A9.4.1]]). The circulation of the total force per unit charge on the carriers contains both: for a battery only $\vec f_s$ contributes; for a conductor moving through a magnetic field $\vec f_s = \vec v\times\vec B$ ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-5|Theorem §B8.1.5]]); for a loop at rest in a changing magnetic field, the induced electric field is not electrostatic and contributes through $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l \neq 0$ ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields|§B8.2]]). "Force" is a misnomer: $\mathcal E$ is the integral of a force per unit charge, measured in volts; Griffiths prefers the circulation to "work per unit charge" as the definition because the two can differ in their path of integration (Theorem §B8.1.5).

^rem-b8-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.4: Terminal Voltage of an Ideal Source
> For an ideal (resistanceless) source the potential difference between its terminals equals its emf (Griffiths eq. (7.10)):
>
> $$
> V = -\int_a^b\vec E\cdot d\vec l = \mathcal E .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.1.2, eq. (7.10)*

^thm-b8-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Inside an ideal source $\sigma = \infty$, so the net force per unit charge is zero ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^law-b8-1-1|Law §B8.1.1]] with finite $\vec J$): $\vec E = -\vec f_s$ there. Integrating from the negative terminal $a$ to the positive terminal $b$ through the source, $V = V(b) - V(a) = -\int_a^b\vec E\cdot d\vec l = \int_a^b\vec f_s\cdot d\vec l$, and since $\vec f_s = \vec 0$ outside the source the integral extends to the whole loop: $\oint\vec f_s\cdot d\vec l = \mathcal E$. Inside the source $\vec f_s$ drives the current *against* $\vec E$. With an internal resistance the terminal voltage is $\mathcal E - Ir$ ([[§A6.1 Electromotive Force#^thm-a6-1-2|Theorem §A6.1.2]]).

## Motional emf and the flux rule

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.5: Motional EMF, and Who Does the Work
> Let a wire loop move through a static magnetic field $\vec B$, the element $d\vec l$ having velocity $\vec v$. The emf is (Griffiths eq. (7.11))
>
> $$
> \mathcal E = \oint\vec f_{\text{mag}}\cdot d\vec l = \oint(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec l ,
> $$
>
> the integral taken around the loop at one instant, with $\vec v$ the velocity of the wire (not of the carriers). The magnetic force does no work. If a current flows, the agent moving the loop must exert a force per unit charge $\vec f_{\text{pull}}$ on the carriers, and the work it does per unit charge carried around the circuit equals $\mathcal E$, although this work is an integral along the carriers' actual paths, not along the wire.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.1.3, eq. (7.11) and Figures 7.10–7.12*

^thm-b8-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The emf.* A carrier moves with the wire at $\vec v$ and drifts along it at $\vec u \parallel d\vec l$, so its velocity is $\vec w = \vec v + \vec u$ and the magnetic force per unit charge is $\vec w\times\vec B$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]). Its component along the wire is $(\vec w\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec l = (\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec l + (\vec u\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec l$, and the last term vanishes because $\vec u\times\vec B \perp \vec u \parallel d\vec l$. Summing around the loop gives the formula ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]] with $\vec f_s = \vec f_{\text{mag}}$).
>
> *The work*, for Griffiths' rectangular loop pulled to the right at speed $v$ with its left side $ab$ (height $h$) in a uniform $\vec B$ into the page. In $ab$ the carriers move up the wire at $u$ and right at $v$; $\vec w\times\vec B$ has a component $vB$ up the wire (the emf, $vBh$) and a component $uB$ to the left. The wire holds the carriers in it, so the agent must supply $f_{\text{pull}} = uB$ to the right. A carrier crosses the field region along $\vec w$, at the angle $\theta$ from the wire with $\tan\theta = v/u$, over the distance $h/\cos\theta$; the work per unit charge is
>
> $$
> \int\vec f_{\text{pull}}\cdot d\vec l = uB\,\frac{h}{\cos\theta}\,\sin\theta = uBh\tan\theta = vBh = \mathcal E .
> $$
>
> The magnetic force $q\vec w\times\vec B$ is perpendicular to $\vec w$ and does no work ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-1|Theorem §A7.2.1]]); it only redirects the agent's push into the current. $\vec f_{\text{pull}}$ contributes nothing to $\mathcal E$ (it is perpendicular to the wire), $\vec f_{\text{mag}}$ nothing to the work (it is perpendicular to the motion).

^der-b8-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]], [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]], [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-1|Theorem §A7.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.6: The Flux Rule for Motional EMF
> Let a closed wire loop move, rotate, stretch or deform continuously through a magnetic field that does not change in time, and let $\Phi = \int_S\vec B\cdot d\vec a$ be the flux through any surface $S$ bounded by the loop, with $d\vec a$ oriented by the right-hand rule from the sense of $d\vec l$ (Griffiths eq. (7.12)). Then the motional emf of [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-5|Theorem §B8.1.5]] is (7.13)
>
> $$
> \mathcal E = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.1.3, eqs. (7.12)–(7.13) and the proof with Figure 7.13*

^thm-b8-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\Phi$ does not depend on the choice of $S$: two surfaces with the same boundary together enclose a volume, and $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec a = \int\nabla\cdot\vec B\,d\tau = 0$ ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]]; [[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]). Let $S$ span the loop at time $t$. During $dt$ the element $d\vec l$ at $P$ moves to $P'$ by $\vec v\,dt$ and sweeps out a thin "ribbon" whose area element is
>
> $$
> d\vec a = (\vec v\times d\vec l)\,dt .
> $$
>
> (Check of the orientation: a counterclockwise circle of radius $r$ expanding in the plane has $\vec v\times d\vec l = \dot r\,\hat s\times\hat\varphi\,r\,d\varphi = \dot r\,r\,d\varphi\,\hat z$, along the normal of $S$, as it must be for the ribbon to extend $S$.) $S$ plus the ribbon is a surface bounded by the loop at $t + dt$, so, the field being static,
>
> $$
> d\Phi = \Phi(t + dt) - \Phi(t) = \int_{\text{ribbon}}\vec B\cdot d\vec a = dt\oint\vec B\cdot(\vec v\times d\vec l) .
> $$
>
> Since $\vec u \parallel d\vec l$, $\vec v\times d\vec l = \vec w\times d\vec l$. The scalar triple product is cyclic, $\vec B\cdot(\vec w\times d\vec l) = d\vec l\cdot(\vec B\times\vec w) = -(\vec w\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec l$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{d\Phi}{dt} = -\oint(\vec w\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec l = -\oint\vec f_{\text{mag}}\cdot d\vec l = -\mathcal E .
> $$

^der-b8-1-6

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-5|Theorem §B8.1.5]], [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]], [[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The layer of the flux rule
> Level A stated the flux rule as a principle, Faraday's law in circuit form ([[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^pr-a9-1-1|Principle §A9.1.1]]), checked it for the sliding rod and the rotating rod, and promised a general proof for moving conductors ([[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^rem-a9-3-1|§A9.3, Remark: One law for two causes]]). For motional emfs in a static field it is now a theorem: it "does not contain any new physics", only the Lorentz force law and $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ (Griffiths), and it holds for any shape and any continuous motion, not only for rectangles in uniform fields. Changing fields need a genuinely new law, Faraday's ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields|§B8.2]]); the two mechanisms combine into one flux rule for a moving loop in a changing field in [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields|§B8.2]], where the sign is Lenz's law ([[§A9.2 Lenz's Law#^thm-a9-2-1|Theorem §A9.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.1.3 (p. 309)*

^rem-b8-1-5

> [!caution] Caution: Where the flux rule breaks down
> The proof assumes a single wire loop that moves or deforms *continuously*, so that "the flux through the circuit" is defined and changes smoothly. Without that the rule can mislead:
> - *Switches.* Griffiths' standard paradox: throwing a switch so that the circuit takes in a second loop in a field doubles the flux through it at once, but no conductor moves through the field, there is no emf, and no current flows.
> - *Sliding contacts and extended conductors.* In the Faraday disk (a metal disk of radius $a$ spinning at $\omega$ in a uniform axial field, with brushes at the axle and the rim; Griffiths Example 7.4) the current spreads over the disk, and the "flux through the circuit" has no clear meaning. The Lorentz force still gives the answer directly: along a radius $\vec v\times\vec B = \omega sB\,\hat s$, so $\mathcal E = \int_0^a\omega Bs\,ds = \tfrac12\omega Ba^2$, the emf of level A's rotating rod ([[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^ex-a9-3-2|Example §A9.3.2]]) with the rod smeared into a disk. Eddy currents ([[§A9.5 Eddy Currents#^def-a9-5-1|Def. §A9.5.1]]) are the extreme case: no paths are prescribed at all.
>
> When in doubt, compute $\oint\vec f\cdot d\vec l$ with the Lorentz force along an actual conducting path.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.1.3 (pp. 309–310), Figure 7.14, Example 7.4*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B8.1.1: Why the Field in a Resistor Is Uniform
> Level A computed the resistance $R = \rho L/A$ of a uniform conductor by assuming that the field inside is uniform ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^thm-a5-3-3|Theorem §A5.3.3]]). Prove it, for a cylinder of length $L$ along $z$ and arbitrary cross-section $A$, with each end face held at a constant potential ($0$ at $z = 0$, $V_0$ at $z = L$) and the side surface bordering an insulator.
>
> By [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]], $V$ solves Laplace's equation inside, with $V = 0$ and $V = V_0$ on the ends and $\partial V/\partial n = 0$ on the side. The guess
>
> $$
> V(z) = \frac{V_0z}{L}
> $$
>
> satisfies all of these: it is linear, so $\nabla^2V = 0$; it has the right end values; and its gradient points along $z$, so its normal derivative on the side surface (whose normal is perpendicular to $z$, whatever the shape of the cross-section) vanishes. By uniqueness it is *the* solution, and $\vec E = -\nabla V = -(V_0/L)\hat z$ is uniform. Then $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ is uniform, $I = \sigma AV_0/L$ and $R = L/\sigma A$.
>
> The contrast is instructive: remove the conducting material and leave only two metal end plates, and the field between them is a complicated fringing field that spills out of the cylinder (Griffiths' Figure 7.3). In the resistor, surface charge arranges itself on the side wall to produce exactly the uniform interior field ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^rem-b8-1-2|Remark: Where the steering charge sits]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Examples 7.1 and 7.3*

^ex-b8-1-1

> [!example] Example §B8.1.2: An EMF from a Capacitor?
> One side of a rectangular wire loop, of length $\ell$ and parallel to the field, lies between the plates of a charged parallel-plate capacitor, where the field $\vec E$ is large and nearly uniform; the opposite side is far outside, where the field is essentially zero. The loop has resistance $R$. What is its emf, and what current flows?
>
> The tempting answer is $\mathcal E = E\ell$ and $I = E\ell/R$: the field pushes charge along the inner side and nothing pushes back on the outer one. That would be a perpetual-motion machine, drawing Joule heat from a capacitor that never discharges. The correct answer is
>
> $$
> \mathcal E = \oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0, \qquad I = 0 ,
> $$
>
> because the field of static charges is curl-free, $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$, so its circulation around *any* closed loop vanishes by Stokes' theorem ([[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]]); there is no source term $\vec f_s$ in the loop ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]]). The flaw in the tempting answer is the assumption that the field drops abruptly to zero at the edge of the plates. It cannot: a rectangle straddling the edge would then have circulation $E\ell \neq 0$. The fringing field, outside the plates, has a component along the two connecting sides of the loop, and its contribution, $-E\ell$, exactly cancels that of the inner side.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 10, Problem 3 (Emf?) · Griffiths Problem 7.6 (solved here; checked against the instructor's typed solution, 2023 HW 10, Problem 2)*

> [!example] Example §B8.1.3: Sliding Bar
> A bar of mass $m$ slides without friction on two parallel rails a distance $\ell$ apart, joined at one end by a resistor $R$; a uniform field $\vec B$ points into the page everywhere. Take $\hat x$ to the right (along the rails, away from the resistor), $\hat y$ up the page, $\hat z$ out of the page, and let the bar be at distance $x$ from the resistor.
>
> *(a) Current.* With the counterclockwise sense and $d\vec a$ along $\hat z$, $\Phi = -B\ell x$, so by [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]] $\mathcal E = -d\Phi/dt = B\ell v$ and
>
> $$
> I = \frac{B\ell v}{R}, \quad \text{counterclockwise (up the bar, down through the resistor).}
> $$
>
> Directly: $\vec v\times\vec B = v\hat x\times(-B\hat z) = vB\,\hat y$, up the bar ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-5|Theorem §B8.1.5]]).
>
> *(b) Force.* $\vec F = I\vec\ell\times\vec B = I\ell\,\hat y\times(-B\hat z) = -\dfrac{B^2\ell^2v}{R}\,\hat x$, against the motion (Lenz).
>
> *(c) Motion.* $m\dot v = -(B^2\ell^2/R)\,v$, linear drag ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^ex-a3-10-2|CM Example §A3.10.2]]; with gravity added, the terminal speed of [[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^ex-a9-3-1|Example §A9.3.1]]), so
>
> $$
> v(t) = v_0e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{mR}{B^2\ell^2}, \qquad x(t) = x_0 + v_0\tau\bigl(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\bigr) ;
> $$
>
> the bar never quite stops, but comes to rest at the finite distance $v_0\tau = mRv_0/B^2\ell^2$ beyond its start.
>
> *(d) Energy.* The Joule power is $I^2R = (B^2\ell^2v_0^2/R)\,e^{-2t/\tau}$, and in total
>
> $$
> W_R = \int_0^\infty\frac{B^2\ell^2v_0^2}{R}\,e^{-2t/\tau}\,dt = \frac{B^2\ell^2v_0^2}{R}\cdot\frac{\tau}{2} = \frac12mv_0^2 ,
> $$
>
> all of the initial kinetic energy, as energy conservation requires; the magnetic force only transfers it ([[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^thm-b8-1-5|Theorem §B8.1.5]]).
>
> *(e) Switching the field off.* Once the bar is at rest at distance $x$, $B$ is switched off. The flux into the page through the circuit falls, so the induced current flows clockwise, *down* the bar, to oppose the change; while $B$ is still nonzero it feels $I\ell\,(-\hat y)\times(-B\hat z) = I\ell B\,\hat x$: the bar is kicked to the right and, once the field is gone, coasts on forever (no friction). This emf is not motional (the bar is at rest) but Faraday's ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields|§B8.2]]): $|\mathcal E| = \ell x\,|dB/dt|$. If the field falls fast enough that the bar barely moves meanwhile, and the circuit's self-inductance is negligible, the impulse is
>
> $$
> \Delta p = \int I\ell B\,dt = \frac{\ell^2x}{R}\int_0^{B_0}B\,dB = \frac{\ell^2xB_0^2}{2R}, \qquad v_f = \frac{\ell^2xB_0^2}{2mR} ,
> $$
>
> independent of how quickly the field is removed.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 10, Problem 4 (Sliding bar) · Griffiths Problem 7.7 (solved here; parts (a)–(e) checked against the instructor's typed solution, 2023 HW 10, Problem 3, which answers (e) qualitatively; the final speed is worked out here)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The emf is a work line integral ([[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^def-16-2|452 Def. §16.2]]); "$\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$ for every loop" is Stokes' theorem ([[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]]) applied to a curl-free field, i.e. a conservative field ([[§11 The Three Differential Operators꞉ Gradient, Curl, Divergence#^def-11-1|452 Def. §11.1]]).
> - The surface independence of $\Phi$ used in the flux rule is the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]) on the region between two caps; the ribbon computation is the derivative of a flux integral ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^def-18-6|452 Def. §18.6]]) over a moving surface, a special case of the transport theorems of continuum mechanics (Mathematical Methods, planned).
> - Steady conduction is a mixed Dirichlet–Neumann problem for Laplace's equation ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]); Green's first identity ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-2|452 Thm. §17.2]]) is what makes its solution unique.
> - Quantum Mechanics level C: time reversal as an operator that reverses momenta, and with them currents (Remark: What level B adds to Ohm's law); a fixed external magnetic field breaks the symmetry unless the currents that produce it are reversed too — [[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^pr-c8-3-2|QM Principle §C8.3.2]], [[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^rem-c8-3-2|QM Remark: No time-reversal quantum number]].
