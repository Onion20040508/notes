How strongly are two circuits coupled, and how much energy does it cost to set up currents? Level A defined mutual and self-inductance through flux linkage ([[§A10.1 Mutual Inductance#^def-a10-1-1|Def. §A10.1.1]], [[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^def-a10-2-1|Def. §A10.2.1]]), stated the reciprocity $M_{12} = M_{21}$ with only an energy sketch ([[§A10.1 Mutual Inductance#^thm-a10-1-1|Theorem §A10.1.1]]), derived $U = \tfrac12LI^2$ ([[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-1|Theorem §A10.3.1]]) and found the energy density $B^2/2\mu_0$ for a solenoid, asserting it in general ([[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-2|Theorem §A10.3.2]]); both proofs were left to level B. Here the Neumann formula writes $M$ as a double line integral, which makes reciprocity a theorem; the energy of any system of circuits is computed and shown to be a function of the state; and it is rewritten as $\tfrac12\int\vec A\cdot\vec J\,d\tau$ and as $(1/2\mu_0)\int B^2\,d\tau$ for any localized current distribution. Circuits with inductors (RL, LC, RLC, AC) are level A's ([[§A10.4 RL Circuits|§A10.4]]–[[§A10.6 RLC Circuits|§A10.6]], [[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits|A11]]) and are not repeated. Everything here is quasistatic ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^mod-b8-2-5|Model §B8.2.5]]).

## Mutual inductance and the Neumann formula

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.3.1: Neumann Formula; Reciprocity
> Let two thin closed loops be at rest, and let a steady current $I_1$ in loop 1 produce the flux $\Phi_2 = \int\vec B_1\cdot d\vec a_2$ through loop 2. Then $\Phi_2 = M_{21}I_1$ (Griffiths eq. (7.22)), with the **mutual inductance**
>
> $$
> M_{21} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint\!\!\oint\frac{d\vec l_1\cdot d\vec l_2}{\mathcal R} \qquad\text{(Neumann formula)},
> $$
>
> $\mathcal R$ the distance between the elements $d\vec l_1$ and $d\vec l_2$ (7.23). Consequently:
> 1. $M_{21}$ is purely geometrical: it depends only on the sizes, shapes and relative position of the loops;
> 2. the formula is symmetric in the two loops, so (7.24)
>
> $$
> M_{21} = M_{12} \equiv M :
> $$
>
> whatever their shapes and positions, the flux through 2 when a current $I$ runs around 1 equals the flux through 1 when the same current runs around 2.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.3, eqs. (7.22)–(7.24)*

^thm-b8-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $\vec B_1 = \nabla\times\vec A_1$ ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^def-b6-4-1|Def. §B6.4.1]]) and apply Stokes' theorem ([[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]]) to any surface spanning loop 2:
>
> $$
> \Phi_2 = \int(\nabla\times\vec A_1)\cdot d\vec a_2 = \oint\vec A_1\cdot d\vec l_2 .
> $$
>
> The vector potential of a line current (Griffiths eq. (5.66); [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]]) is $\vec A_1 = \dfrac{\mu_0I_1}{4\pi}\oint\dfrac{d\vec l_1}{\mathcal R}$. Substituting,
>
> $$
> \Phi_2 = \frac{\mu_0I_1}{4\pi}\oint\Bigl(\oint\frac{d\vec l_1}{\mathcal R}\Bigr)\cdot d\vec l_2 = I_1\,\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint\!\!\oint\frac{d\vec l_1\cdot d\vec l_2}{\mathcal R} .
> $$
>
> The double integral contains only the two curves, so $M_{21}$ is geometrical. Exchanging the labels 1 and 2 leaves the dot product and $\mathcal R = \lvert\vec r_2 - \vec r_1\rvert$ unchanged, so $M_{12} = M_{21}$.

^der-b8-3-1

*Uses:* [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^def-b6-4-1|Def. §B6.4.1]], [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]], [[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from level A
> - *Layer.* Reciprocity was a stated result at level A, supported by an energy argument ([[§A10.1 Mutual Inductance#^der-a10-1-1|§A10.1, Derivation of Theorem §A10.1.1]]); it is now a theorem of the Biot–Savart law. The two routes are logically complementary: level A assumed that the stored energy depends only on the final currents and deduced $M_{12} = M_{21}$; [[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-2|Theorem §B8.3.2]] below deduces from $M_{12} = M_{21}$ that the stored energy depends only on the final currents.
> - *Use.* The Neumann formula is rarely the practical way to compute $M$ (it is a double line integral), but reciprocity is: compute whichever flux is easy. A short solenoid on the axis of a long one has a complicated field and threads a different flux through each turn of the long one, but the long solenoid's uniform field through the short one gives at once $M = \mu_0\pi a^2n_1n_2l$ (Griffiths Example 7.10; the same trick as [[§A10.1 Mutual Inductance#^ex-a10-1-1|Example §A10.1.1]]).
> - *Induced emfs.* With Faraday's law, $\mathcal E_2 = -d\Phi_2/dt = -M\,dI_1/dt$ (Griffiths eq. (7.25); [[§A10.1 Mutual Inductance#^thm-a10-1-2|Theorem §A10.1.2]]), and a loop's own flux $\Phi = LI$ (7.26) gives the back emf $\mathcal E = -L\,dI/dt$ (7.27) ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-1|Theorem §A10.2.1]]). Griffiths stresses that (7.22) rests on the Biot–Savart law, so these hold only for currents that change slowly enough to be quasistatic ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^mod-b8-2-5|Model §B8.2.5]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.3 (pp. 323–325), Example 7.10, eqs. (7.25)–(7.27)*

^rem-b8-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Self-inductance and the thin-wire snag
> Setting both loops equal in the Neumann formula does not give $L$: where $d\vec l_1$ and $d\vec l_2$ coincide, $\mathcal R \to 0$ and the double integral diverges (logarithmically). The flux of an ideal filament through itself is infinite, because its field grows as $1/s$ near the wire. This is the "snag that is characteristic of many self-inductance calculations" of Griffiths' Problem 7.25: a definite answer needs a wire of finite radius $\epsilon$, and then $L$ depends on $\epsilon$ (logarithmically). For a current spread over a volume, different parts of the current link different amounts of flux, and the flux definition of $L$ becomes ambiguous; Griffiths' advice is then to let the energy define it, $W = \tfrac12LI^2$ ([[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-3|Theorem §B8.3.3]], [[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^ex-b8-3-2|Example §B8.3.2]]). Inductance, like capacitance, is positive ([[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^rem-b8-3-3|Remark: Energy decides signs]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.3–7.2.4 (pp. 325, 331), Problem 7.25*

## Energy in magnetic fields

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.3.2: Energy of a System of Circuits
> Let rigid circuits $k = 1, \dots, N$ at rest carry currents $I_k$, with $\Phi_k = \sum_jM_{kj}I_j$ the flux through circuit $k$ ($M_{kk} = L_k$). The work done against the induced emfs in building up the currents from zero, apart from resistive losses, is
>
> $$
> W = \frac12\sum_{j,k}M_{kj}I_kI_j = \frac12\sum_kI_k\Phi_k ,
> $$
>
> independently of the order and rate in which the currents are raised; it is recovered when they are turned off. For one loop (Griffiths eq. (7.30))
>
> $$
> W = \tfrac12LI^2 ,
> $$
>
> and for two, $W = \tfrac12L_1I_1^2 + MI_1I_2 + \tfrac12L_2I_2^2$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.4, eq. (7.30); the several-circuit form worked out here (Griffiths Problem 7.32(b) treats the cross term)*

^thm-b8-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The emf in circuit $k$ is $\mathcal E_k = -d\Phi_k/dt$ ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]]). Whatever maintains the current $I_k$ must work against it at the rate $-\mathcal E_kI_k$ (the minus sign: work done *against* the emf, Griffiths), so in total
>
> $$
> dW = \sum_kI_k\,d\Phi_k = \sum_{k,j}M_{kj}\,I_k\,dI_j .
> $$
>
> This is the differential of the quadratic form $F = \tfrac12\sum_{k,j}M_{kj}I_kI_j$, *because* $M$ is symmetric ([[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-1|Theorem §B8.3.1]]): $dF = \tfrac12\sum_{k,j}M_{kj}(I_j\,dI_k + I_k\,dI_j) = \sum_{k,j}M_{kj}I_k\,dI_j$, after renaming $k \leftrightarrow j$ in the first sum. So $W = \int dF = F(\text{final}) - F(0)$ along any path in the space of currents. For one loop, $dW/dt = -\mathcal EI = LI\,dI/dt$ and $W = \tfrac12LI^2$, as in [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-1|Theorem §A10.3.1]]. Finally $\tfrac12\sum_{k,j}M_{kj}I_kI_j = \tfrac12\sum_kI_k\bigl(\sum_jM_{kj}I_j\bigr) = \tfrac12\sum_kI_k\Phi_k$.

^der-b8-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]], [[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-1|Theorem §B8.3.1]], [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-1|Theorem §A10.3.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.3.3: Magnetic Energy in Terms of A and of B
> For a loop carrying current $I$, and for any localized distribution of (quasi)steady currents $\vec J$, the energy of [[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-2|Theorem §B8.3.2]] is (Griffiths eqs. (7.31)–(7.32))
>
> $$
> W = \frac12\oint(\vec A\cdot\vec I)\,dl, \qquad W = \frac12\int(\vec A\cdot\vec J)\,d\tau .
> $$
>
> In terms of the field, for any volume $\mathcal V$ containing all the current, with boundary $\mathcal S$ (7.34),
>
> $$
> W = \frac{1}{2\mu_0}\Bigl[\int_{\mathcal V}B^2\,d\tau - \oint_{\mathcal S}(\vec A\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec a\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> and over all space (7.35)
>
> $$
> W = \frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{all space}}B^2\,d\tau .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.4, eqs. (7.30)–(7.35) · Nelson ch. 18, §18.3.4, eqs. (18.13)–(18.14)*

^thm-b8-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Loops.* With $\vec A$ the total vector potential, $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$ and Stokes' theorem give $\Phi_k = \oint_k\vec A\cdot d\vec l_k$, so by [[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-2|Theorem §B8.3.2]] $W = \tfrac12\sum_kI_k\oint_k\vec A\cdot d\vec l_k = \tfrac12\sum_k\oint_k(\vec A\cdot\vec I_k)\,dl$, the current element $\vec I\,dl = I\,d\vec l$.
>
> *Volume currents.* A steady $\vec J$ has $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$, so it can be divided into thin closed tubes of flow, each a circuit carrying a small current. Summing the loop formula over the tubes replaces $\vec I\,dl$ by $\vec J\,d\tau$: $W = \tfrac12\int\vec A\cdot\vec J\,d\tau$.
>
> *Field form.* Ampère's law $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]; valid in the quasistatic regime) gives $W = (1/2\mu_0)\int\vec A\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B)\,d\tau$ (7.33). The product rule ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]; Griffiths' product rule (6)) $\nabla\cdot(\vec A\times\vec B) = \vec B\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A) - \vec A\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B)$ turns the integrand into $B^2 - \nabla\cdot(\vec A\times\vec B)$, and the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]) converts the second part into the surface integral of (7.34). The integration in $\tfrac12\int\vec A\cdot\vec J\,d\tau$ may extend over any volume containing the currents, since $\vec J = \vec 0$ outside. Let $\mathcal V$ be a ball of radius $r \to \infty$. A localized steady current has no monopole term in $\vec A$ ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-5|Theorem §B6.4.5]]), so at large $r$, $A \lesssim 1/r^2$ and $B \lesssim 1/r^3$, and the surface integral is at most of order $r^2\cdot r^{-5} \to 0$: (7.35).

^der-b8-3-3

*Uses:* [[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-2|Theorem §B8.3.2]], [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]], [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-5|Theorem §B6.4.5]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]], [[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Where the energy is, and why it costs work
> - *Layer.* [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-2|Theorem §A10.3.2]] found $u = B^2/2\mu_0$ for a long solenoid and asserted it in general; (7.35) proves it for every localized quasistatic current distribution. Nelson reaches it the same way (work against the induced field in a solenoid, then the observation that the result "has forgotten everything about the original geometry except the volume") and concludes that the equations seem to require energy stored in empty space; the general energy balance with the energy flux is Poynting's theorem ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem|§B9.1]]).
> - *Bookkeeping.* Whether the energy sits in the field ($B^2/2\mu_0$ per volume) or in the currents ($\tfrac12\vec A\cdot\vec J$ per volume) is, at this stage, bookkeeping; only the total is measured (Griffiths). The parallel with electrostatics is exact: $W_{\text{elec}} = \tfrac12\int V\rho\,d\tau = (\varepsilon_0/2)\int E^2\,d\tau$ ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]]) and $W_{\text{mag}} = \tfrac12\int\vec A\cdot\vec J\,d\tau = (1/2\mu_0)\int B^2\,d\tau$. As there, energy is not additive: two circuits have the cross term $MI_1I_2$.
> - *Why work at all?* Magnetic forces do no work ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-1|Theorem §A7.2.1]]). But to create a magnetic field one must change it, and a changing $\vec B$ induces an electric field ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^pr-b8-2-1|Principle §B8.2.1]]); the work is done against *that* field, which exists only while the currents change. This is also why the energy could not be found in magnetostatics (Griffiths). Nelson adds the contrast with Joule heat: resistive losses occur even at constant current and only in resistive wires, whereas the work $\tfrac12LI^2$ is needed only while the current changes, also in a superconducting coil, and comes back when the current falls; a superconducting coil stores energy as a flywheel does.
> - *Gauge.* $\int\vec A\cdot\vec J\,d\tau$ does not depend on the gauge of $\vec A$: replacing $\vec A$ by $\vec A + \nabla\lambda$ adds $\int\nabla\lambda\cdot\vec J\,d\tau = -\int\lambda\,\nabla\cdot\vec J\,d\tau = 0$ for localized steady currents (integration by parts; worked out here).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.4 (pp. 328–330), eqs. (2.43), (2.45), (7.32), (7.35) · Nelson ch. 18, §18.3.2–18.3.4, eqs. (18.12)–(18.15)*

^rem-b8-3-4

> [!remark] Remark: Energy decides signs
> Since $W = (1/2\mu_0)\int B^2\,d\tau \ge 0$ for *every* choice of currents, the quadratic form of [[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-2|Theorem §B8.3.2]] is positive semidefinite. Hence every self-inductance is positive, $L = 2W/I^2 > 0$ (the minus sign in $\mathcal E = -L\,dI/dt$ always opposes the change: Lenz), and for two circuits the discriminant condition gives
>
> $$
> M^2 \le L_1L_2 .
> $$
>
> The coupling coefficient $M/\sqrt{L_1L_2}$ lies between $-1$ and $1$ (the sign of $M$ depends on the senses chosen around the loops); the ideal transformer, in which every turn of both coils links the same flux ([[§A11.5 Transformers#^mod-a11-5-2|Model §A11.5.2]]; Griffiths Problem 7.57), is the limit $M^2 = L_1L_2$.
>
> *Source: worked out here from Griffiths eqs. (7.30) and (7.35)*

^rem-b8-3-3

> [!caution] Caution: W or U, and which energy
> Griffiths writes $W$ for the energy, level A (University Physics) $U$ ([[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-1|Theorem §A10.3.1]]); the convention is fixed in [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^cau-b2-3-1|§B2.3, Caution: Notation for energy]]. $W$ here is the reversible work against the back emfs, recoverable when the currents are turned off; the heat dissipated in the wires while the currents are on is separate and depends on how long they flow (Griffiths p. 328). All formulas assume quasistatic currents; for radiating systems the energy also leaves in the fields ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem|§B9.1]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B8.3.1: A Small Loop above a Big One
> A small loop of radius $a$ is held a distance $z$ above the centre of a large loop of radius $b$, coaxial and parallel to it, with $a \ll b$ and $a \ll z$. Find the mutual inductance both ways.
>
> *(a) Current $I$ in the big loop.* Its field on the axis is $B_z = \mu_0Ib^2/2(b^2 + z^2)^{3/2}$ ([[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop#^thm-a8-3-1|Theorem §A8.3.1]]; Griffiths eq. (5.41)), nearly uniform over the small loop, so
>
> $$
> \Phi_2 = \frac{\mu_0Ib^2}{2(b^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}\,\pi a^2 .
> $$
>
> *(b) Current $I$ in the small loop.* Treat it as a dipole $\vec m = \pi a^2I\hat z$ ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-7|Theorem §B6.4.7]]). Its field has $B_z = \dfrac{\mu_0m}{4\pi}\dfrac{3\cos^2\theta - 1}{r^3} = \dfrac{\mu_0a^2I}{4}\dfrac{3z^2 - r^2}{r^5}$; in the plane of the big loop $r^2 = s^2 + z^2$, so
>
> $$
> \Phi_1 = \int_0^b B_z\,2\pi s\,ds = \frac{\pi\mu_0a^2I}{2}\int_0^b\frac{2z^2 - s^2}{(s^2 + z^2)^{5/2}}\,s\,ds .
> $$
>
> With $u^2 = s^2 + z^2$, $s\,ds = u\,du$ and $2z^2 - s^2 = 3z^2 - u^2$, the integrand becomes $(3z^2u^{-4} - u^{-2})\,du$, with antiderivative $-z^2u^{-3} + u^{-1}$:
>
> $$
> \int_0^b\frac{(2z^2 - s^2)\,s\,ds}{(s^2 + z^2)^{5/2}} = \Bigl[-\frac{z^2}{u^3} + \frac1u\Bigr]_z^{\sqrt{b^2 + z^2}} = \frac{b^2}{(b^2 + z^2)^{3/2}} ,
> $$
>
> so $\Phi_1 = \pi\mu_0a^2b^2I/2(b^2 + z^2)^{3/2}$.
>
> *(c)* The two fluxes are equal, as [[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-1|Theorem §B8.3.1]] requires:
>
> $$
> M = \frac{\mu_0\pi a^2b^2}{2(b^2 + z^2)^{3/2}} .
> $$
>
> Route (b) is the instructive one: the dipole field through the big loop's disk points up near the axis and *down* beyond $s = \sqrt2\,z$, where its lines return, and only the net flux, a partial cancellation, reproduces (a). Reciprocity spared the work in [[§A10.1 Mutual Inductance#^ex-a10-1-1|Example §A10.1.1]]; here it is a check. The figure compares this small-loop result with the exact Neumann integral.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 7.22 (solved here; checked against the PHY 422 instructor's typed solutions, 2023 HW 10, Problem 5 (Mutual inductance))*

^ex-b8-3-1

For $a = 0.25\,b$ the approximation is within $3\%$ at all separations; for $a = 0.8\,b$ it is off by $43\%$ when the loops are coplanar, where the field of the big loop is far from uniform over the small one, and by up to $19\%$ at $z \approx b$. At large $z$ all ratios tend to $1$.*

> [!example] Example §B8.3.2: Inductance of a Coaxial Cable with a Solid Inner Wire
> A long coaxial cable has a solid inner wire of radius $R_1$ carrying current $I$ uniformly over its cross-section, returning along a thin outer shield of radius $R_2$. Find its inductance per unit length.
>
> *Fields* (PHY 422 practice final, Problem 2(b), by Ampère's law): $B = \mu_0Is/2\pi R_1^2$ inside the wire, $\mu_0I/2\pi s$ between wire and shield, $0$ outside, all along $\hat\varphi$.
>
> *Energy* per length $l$, by (7.35), in cylindrical shells of volume $2\pi sl\,ds$:
>
> $$
> \frac{W}{l} = \int_0^{R_1}\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\Bigl(\frac{\mu_0Is}{2\pi R_1^2}\Bigr)^2 2\pi s\,ds + \int_{R_1}^{R_2}\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\Bigl(\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s}\Bigr)^2 2\pi s\,ds = \frac{\mu_0I^2}{16\pi} + \frac{\mu_0I^2}{4\pi}\ln\frac{R_2}{R_1} .
> $$
>
> Setting $W = \tfrac12LI^2$,
>
> $$
> \frac{L}{l} = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\Bigl[\ln\frac{R_2}{R_1} + \frac14\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> The logarithm is the thin-shell result of [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^ex-a10-3-1|Example §A10.3.1]] (Griffiths Example 7.13); the extra $\mu_0/8\pi \approx 0.05\ \mu\mathrm H/\mathrm m$, independent of the wire's radius, is the **internal inductance** of the wire, the energy stored in the field inside the conductor (Griffiths Problem 7.30 asks for exactly this term). The flux route is ambiguous here: a filament at radius $s < R_1$ links only the flux outside it, so different parts of the current link different fluxes ([[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^rem-b8-3-2|Remark: Self-inductance and the thin-wire snag]]).
>
> *Check with $\tfrac12\int\vec A\cdot\vec J\,d\tau$.* The Coulomb-gauge potential of [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^ex-b6-4-2|Example §B6.4.2]](b) (practice final, Problem 2(c)), with $\vec A \to \vec 0$ far away, is $A_z = (\mu_0I/2\pi)\ln(R_2/R_1) + (\mu_0I/4\pi)(1 - s^2/R_1^2)$ inside the wire and $A_z = 0$ at the shield. The shield's current therefore contributes nothing, and with $J = I/\pi R_1^2$ in the wire,
>
> $$

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $\Phi = \oint\vec A\cdot d\vec l$ is Stokes' theorem ([[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]]); the passage from (7.33) to (7.34) is the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]) applied to $\vec A\times\vec B$, an integration by parts in three dimensions.
> - $W = \tfrac12\sum M_{kj}I_kI_j$ is a quadratic form with the symmetric matrix $M$; "$dW$ is exact" is the condition that a 1-form $\sum_kF_k\,dI_k$ be closed, here $\partial F_k/\partial I_j = M_{kj} = \partial F_j/\partial I_k$, and on the (star-shaped) space of currents closed implies exact ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-12|452 Prop. §22.12]]). $M^2 \le L_1L_2$ is positive semidefiniteness of a $2\times2$ symmetric matrix.
