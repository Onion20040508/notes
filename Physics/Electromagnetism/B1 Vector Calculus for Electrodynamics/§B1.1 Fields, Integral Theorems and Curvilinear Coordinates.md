Level B states the laws of electricity and magnetism as differential equations for fields, so it needs a working toolkit of vector calculus: the three first derivatives of a field, their product rules and second derivatives, the integral theorems that connect them to fluxes and circulations, and their forms in spherical and cylindrical coordinates. Level A used only the gradient, in Cartesian components with a remark on the curvilinear forms ([[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]], [[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^rem-a3-4-2|§A3.4, Remark: Gradient in cylindrical and spherical coordinates]]), and integral laws such as Gauss's law. The rigorous home of the definitions and the integral theorems is Multivariable Analysis (MATH 452); this section collects what a physicist uses, in Griffiths' notation, and points there for proofs. The delta function, which the toolkit needs at the singular points of physical fields, is the subject of [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem|§B1.2]].

## The operator ∇

> [!definition] Definition §B1.1.1: Del; Gradient, Divergence, Curl, Laplacian
> In Cartesian coordinates the **del operator** is the vector-valued differential operator (Griffiths eq. (1.39))
>
> $$
> \nabla = \hat x\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \hat y\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \hat z\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z} .
> $$
>
> Acting on a differentiable scalar field $T$ and vector field $\vec v = v_x\hat x + v_y\hat y + v_z\hat z$ it gives
> - the **gradient** $\nabla T = \dfrac{\partial T}{\partial x}\hat x + \dfrac{\partial T}{\partial y}\hat y + \dfrac{\partial T}{\partial z}\hat z$, characterized by $dT = \nabla T\cdot d\vec l$ for every displacement $d\vec l = dx\,\hat x + dy\,\hat y + dz\,\hat z$ (eqs. (1.35)–(1.37));
> - the **divergence** $\nabla\cdot\vec v = \dfrac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} + \dfrac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}$;
> - the **curl** $\nabla\times\vec v = \Bigl(\dfrac{\partial v_z}{\partial y} - \dfrac{\partial v_y}{\partial z}\Bigr)\hat x + \Bigl(\dfrac{\partial v_x}{\partial z} - \dfrac{\partial v_z}{\partial x}\Bigr)\hat y + \Bigl(\dfrac{\partial v_y}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial v_x}{\partial y}\Bigr)\hat z$;
> - the **Laplacian** $\nabla^2T = \nabla\cdot(\nabla T) = \dfrac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2 T}{\partial y^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2 T}{\partial z^2}$ (eq. (1.42)); for a vector field, $\nabla^2\vec v = (\nabla^2v_x)\hat x + (\nabla^2v_y)\hat y + (\nabla^2v_z)\hat z$ in Cartesian components (eq. (1.43)).
>
> In index notation, with summation over repeated indices and the Levi-Civita symbol $\epsilon_{ijk}$: $(\nabla T)_i = \partial_iT$, $\nabla\cdot\vec v = \partial_iv_i$, $(\nabla\times\vec v)_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\partial_jv_k$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.2.2–1.2.5, §1.2.7, eqs. (1.36)–(1.43)*

^def-b1-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: What the three derivatives measure
> - The gradient points in the direction of fastest increase of $T$, and its length is the slope in that direction (Griffiths §1.2.2); it is perpendicular to the level surfaces of $T$. This is the content of [[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]] and [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]] at level A.
> - The divergence is the outward flux per unit volume out of a small region around the point, and the curl component along $\hat n$ is the circulation per unit area around a small loop with normal $\hat n$: $\nabla\cdot\vec v = \lim \frac{1}{\Delta\tau}\oint\vec v\cdot d\vec a$ and $\hat n\cdot(\nabla\times\vec v) = \lim\frac{1}{\Delta a}\oint\vec v\cdot d\vec l$. These limits are the infinitesimal versions of the divergence theorem and Stokes' theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]) and the route to the curvilinear formulas ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]]).
> - $\nabla$ is not a vector but an instruction to differentiate what follows; it mimics a vector in almost every algebraic rule, which is why the product rules below look like the rules for dot and cross products, with the extra terms that the Leibniz rule produces.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.2.2–1.2.5, App. A*

^rem-b1-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.1: Product Rules
> For differentiable scalar fields $f, g$ and vector fields $\vec A, \vec B$ (Griffiths §1.2.6):
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> &\text{(i)} && \nabla(fg) = f\,\nabla g + g\,\nabla f ,\\
> &\text{(ii)} && \nabla(\vec A\cdot\vec B) = \vec A\times(\nabla\times\vec B) + \vec B\times(\nabla\times\vec A) + (\vec A\cdot\nabla)\vec B + (\vec B\cdot\nabla)\vec A ,\\
> &\text{(iii)} && \nabla\cdot(f\vec A) = f\,(\nabla\cdot\vec A) + \vec A\cdot\nabla f ,\\
> &\text{(iv)} && \nabla\cdot(\vec A\times\vec B) = \vec B\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A) - \vec A\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B) ,\\
> &\text{(v)} && \nabla\times(f\vec A) = f\,(\nabla\times\vec A) - \vec A\times\nabla f ,\\
> &\text{(vi)} && \nabla\times(\vec A\times\vec B) = (\vec B\cdot\nabla)\vec A - (\vec A\cdot\nabla)\vec B + \vec A\,(\nabla\cdot\vec B) - \vec B\,(\nabla\cdot\vec A) ,
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> where $(\vec A\cdot\nabla)\vec B$ has Cartesian components $A_j\partial_jB_i$. The quotient rules follow from them, for example $\nabla(f/g) = (g\nabla f - f\nabla g)/g^2$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.2.6, Problems 1.21–1.24*

^thm-b1-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Work in Cartesian components, where the unit vectors are constant, and use the one-variable product rule on each term. The tool for the cross products is the contraction identity of the Levi-Civita symbol ([[§37 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^def-37-2|452 Def. §37.2]]), checked by trying the index values,
>
> $$
> \epsilon_{kij}\,\epsilon_{klm} = \delta_{il}\delta_{jm} - \delta_{im}\delta_{jl} ,
> $$
>
> which in algebra gives the BAC-CAB rule $\vec A\times(\vec B\times\vec C) = \vec B(\vec A\cdot\vec C) - \vec C(\vec A\cdot\vec B)$ (Griffiths eq. (1.17)).
> - (i) and (iii): $\partial_i(fg) = f\partial_ig + g\partial_if$; $\partial_i(fA_i) = f\partial_iA_i + A_i\partial_if$.
> - (iv): $\partial_i(\epsilon_{ijk}A_jB_k) = B_k\,\epsilon_{kij}\partial_iA_j - A_j\,\epsilon_{jik}\partial_iB_k = \vec B\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A) - \vec A\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B)$, using the cyclic and antisymmetric properties of $\epsilon$.
> - (v): $\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_j(fA_k) = f\,\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_jA_k + \epsilon_{ijk}(\partial_jf)A_k$, and $\epsilon_{ijk}(\partial_jf)A_k = (\nabla f\times\vec A)_i = -(\vec A\times\nabla f)_i$.
> - (vi): $\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_j\epsilon_{klm}A_lB_m = (\delta_{il}\delta_{jm} - \delta_{im}\delta_{jl})\,\partial_j(A_lB_m) = \partial_m(A_iB_m) - \partial_l(A_lB_i)$, and expanding both derivatives gives the four terms.
> - (ii): $[\vec A\times(\nabla\times\vec B)]_i = \epsilon_{kij}\epsilon_{klm}A_j\partial_lB_m = A_j\partial_iB_j - A_j\partial_jB_i$. Adding this to the same expression with $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ exchanged and to $(\vec A\cdot\nabla)B_i + (\vec B\cdot\nabla)A_i$ leaves $A_j\partial_iB_j + B_j\partial_iA_j = \partial_i(\vec A\cdot\vec B)$.

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]], [[§37 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^def-37-2|452 Def. §37.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.2: Second Derivatives
> For a scalar field $T$ and a vector field $\vec v$ with continuous second partial derivatives (Griffiths eqs. (1.44), (1.46), (1.47)):
>
> $$
> \nabla\times(\nabla T) = \vec 0, \qquad \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec v) = 0, \qquad \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec v) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec v) - \nabla^2\vec v .
> $$
>
> The other two second derivatives are the Laplacian $\nabla\cdot(\nabla T) = \nabla^2T$ and the gradient of the divergence, $\nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec v)$, which is not $\nabla^2\vec v$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.2.7, eqs. (1.42)–(1.47)*

^thm-b1-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $[\nabla\times\nabla T]_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\partial_j\partial_kT$. The symbol is antisymmetric in $j, k$ and $\partial_j\partial_kT$ is symmetric by equality of mixed partials ([[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]; Griffiths eq. (1.45)), so the sum vanishes; the same argument gives $\partial_i\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_jv_k = 0$. For the curl of the curl, $\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_j\epsilon_{klm}\partial_lv_m = (\delta_{il}\delta_{jm} - \delta_{im}\delta_{jl})\partial_j\partial_lv_m = \partial_i(\partial_mv_m) - \partial_j\partial_jv_i$. The shortcut "$\nabla\times\nabla$ is the cross product of a vector with itself" is suggestive but not a proof: it is the symmetry of mixed partials that does the work, and $(\nabla T)\times(\nabla S)$, for instance, is not zero.

## The integral theorems

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.3: The Fundamental Theorems of Vector Calculus
> Let the fields be continuously differentiable on a region containing the path, surface or volume (Griffiths eqs. (1.55)–(1.57)).
> 1. **Gradient theorem.** For a path from $\mathbf a$ to $\mathbf b$, $\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf a}^{\mathbf b}(\nabla T)\cdot d\vec l = T(\mathbf b) - T(\mathbf a)$.
> 2. **Divergence theorem** (Gauss). For a volume $\mathcal V$ bounded by the closed surface $\mathcal S$ with outward area element $d\vec a$, $\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal V}(\nabla\cdot\vec v)\,d\tau = \oint_{\mathcal S}\vec v\cdot d\vec a$.
> 3. **Stokes' theorem.** For an oriented surface $\mathcal S$ with boundary curve $\mathcal P$, run in the sense fixed by the right-hand rule, $\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal S}(\nabla\times\vec v)\cdot d\vec a = \oint_{\mathcal P}\vec v\cdot d\vec l$.
>
> *Corollaries.* $\int_{\mathbf a}^{\mathbf b}\nabla T\cdot d\vec l$ is independent of the path and $\oint\nabla T\cdot d\vec l = 0$; $\int_{\mathcal S}(\nabla\times\vec v)\cdot d\vec a$ depends only on the boundary curve and vanishes for every closed surface.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.3.3–1.3.5, eqs. (1.55)–(1.57); App. A, eqs. (A.5), (A.10), (A.15)*

^thm-b1-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Gradient theorem.* Parametrize the path as $\vec r(t)$, $t_a \le t \le t_b$. By the chain rule ([[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]), $\frac{d}{dt}T(\vec r(t)) = \nabla T\cdot\vec r\,'(t)$, so the line integral is $\int_{t_a}^{t_b}\frac{d}{dt}T(\vec r(t))\,dt = T(\mathbf b) - T(\mathbf a)$ by the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]).
>
> *Divergence and Stokes' theorems* are proved in Multivariable Analysis: [[§32 Flux Integrals and the Divergence Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-32-1|452 Thm. §32.1]] (in $\mathbb R^n$: [[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-28-1|452 Thm. §28.1]]) and [[§34 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-34-1|452 Thm. §34.1]]. Griffiths' physical sketch (App. A) shows why they hold: for an infinitesimal box the net outflux is $(\nabla\cdot\vec v)\,d\tau$, which is how the divergence is defined in any coordinates ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]]); a finite volume is a union of such boxes, and the fluxes through every internal wall cancel in pairs because each wall bounds two neighbouring boxes with opposite outward normals, leaving only the outer surface. The same cancellation of shared edges turns the circulation $(\nabla\times\vec v)\cdot d\vec a$ around infinitesimal loops into Stokes' theorem.
>
> *Corollaries.* The right side of 1 does not mention the path, and a closed path has $\mathbf a = \mathbf b$. Two surfaces with the same boundary give the same right side in 3; a closed surface has no boundary (or, cut in two, its halves have the same boundary with opposite orientations).

^der-b1-1-3

*Uses:* [[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]], [[§32 Flux Integrals and the Divergence Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-32-1|452 Thm. §32.1]], [[§34 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-34-1|452 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: The hypotheses are not decoration
> The theorems need fields that are continuously differentiable everywhere on the region. Physical fields often are not: the field of a point charge blows up at the charge, and the field of a surface charge jumps across the surface. Applied blindly, the divergence theorem then gives wrong answers, such as $\int\nabla\cdot(\hat r/r^2)\,d\tau = 0$ for a ball around the origin while the flux is $4\pi$. The cure is the delta function of [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], or the jump conditions of [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]. Likewise, path independence of a curl-free field needs a region without holes ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^cau-b1-2-1|§B1.2, Caution: Curl-free is not enough on a region with a hole]]).

^cau-b1-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.4: Integration by Parts in Three Dimensions
> For a volume $\mathcal V$ with boundary $\mathcal S$, and for a surface $\mathcal S$ with boundary curve $\mathcal P$ (Griffiths eqs. (1.59)–(1.61)):
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V}f\,(\nabla\cdot\vec A)\,d\tau = -\int_{\mathcal V}\vec A\cdot(\nabla f)\,d\tau + \oint_{\mathcal S}f\vec A\cdot d\vec a ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal S}f\,(\nabla\times\vec A)\cdot d\vec a = \int_{\mathcal S}(\vec A\times\nabla f)\cdot d\vec a + \oint_{\mathcal P}f\vec A\cdot d\vec l, \qquad
> \int_{\mathcal V}\vec B\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A)\,d\tau = \int_{\mathcal V}\vec A\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B)\,d\tau + \oint_{\mathcal S}(\vec A\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec a .
> $$
>
> With $\vec A = \nabla g$ the first identity is Green's first identity, $\int_{\mathcal V}(f\nabla^2g + \nabla f\cdot\nabla g)\,d\tau = \oint_{\mathcal S}f\,\nabla g\cdot d\vec a$; antisymmetrizing in $f, g$ gives Green's second identity, $\int_{\mathcal V}(f\nabla^2g - g\nabla^2f)\,d\tau = \oint_{\mathcal S}(f\nabla g - g\nabla f)\cdot d\vec a$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.3.6, eq. (1.59), Problem 1.36, eqs. (1.60)–(1.61)*

^thm-b1-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Integrate product rule (iii) of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]] over $\mathcal V$ and turn $\int\nabla\cdot(f\vec A)\,d\tau$ into $\oint f\vec A\cdot d\vec a$ by the divergence theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]). The second identity is rule (v) integrated over $\mathcal S$ with Stokes' theorem, the third is rule (iv) with the divergence theorem. As in one dimension, the derivative moves from one factor to the other at the price of a sign and a boundary term. Green's identities, in their rigorous form, are [[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-28-2|452 Thm. §28.2]] and [[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-28-3|452 Thm. §28.3]].

## Curvilinear coordinates

> [!definition] Definition §B1.1.2: Orthogonal Curvilinear Coordinates; Spherical and Cylindrical Coordinates
> Coordinates $(u, v, w)$ are **orthogonal curvilinear coordinates** if the unit vectors $\hat u, \hat v, \hat w$ along the directions of increasing $u$, $v$, $w$ are mutually perpendicular (and, in this order, right-handed) at every point. The infinitesimal displacement then has the form (Griffiths eq. (A.1))
>
> $$
> d\vec l = f\,du\,\hat u + g\,dv\,\hat v + h\,dw\,\hat w ,
> $$
>
> with **scale factors** $f, g, h$, functions of position; the volume element is $d\tau = fgh\,du\,dv\,dw$ (eq. (A.6)). The two systems used throughout:
> - **Spherical** $(r, \theta, \varphi)$: $x = r\sin\theta\cos\varphi$, $y = r\sin\theta\sin\varphi$, $z = r\cos\theta$, with $r \ge 0$, polar angle $0 \le \theta \le \pi$ measured from the $+z$ axis, azimuth $0 \le \varphi < 2\pi$ (eq. (1.62)). Scale factors $1, r, r\sin\theta$:
>
> $$
> d\vec l = dr\,\hat r + r\,d\theta\,\hat\theta + r\sin\theta\,d\varphi\,\hat\varphi, \qquad d\tau = r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \hat r = \sin\theta\cos\varphi\,\hat x + \sin\theta\sin\varphi\,\hat y + \cos\theta\,\hat z,\quad \hat\theta = \cos\theta\cos\varphi\,\hat x + \cos\theta\sin\varphi\,\hat y - \sin\theta\,\hat z,\quad \hat\varphi = -\sin\varphi\,\hat x + \cos\varphi\,\hat y
> $$
>
> (eqs. (1.64), (1.68), (1.69)). On a sphere of radius $r$, $d\vec a = r^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi\,\hat r$.
> - **Cylindrical** $(s, \varphi, z)$: $x = s\cos\varphi$, $y = s\sin\varphi$, $z = z$, with $s \ge 0$ the distance from the $z$ axis (eq. (1.74)). Scale factors $1, s, 1$:
>
> $$
> d\vec l = ds\,\hat s + s\,d\varphi\,\hat\varphi + dz\,\hat z, \qquad d\tau = s\,ds\,d\varphi\,dz, \qquad \hat s = \cos\varphi\,\hat x + \sin\varphi\,\hat y,\quad \hat\varphi = -\sin\varphi\,\hat x + \cos\varphi\,\hat y
> $$
>
> (eqs. (1.75)–(1.78)). The azimuth $\varphi$ and $\hat\varphi$ are the same in both systems, and $s = r\sin\theta$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.4, eqs. (1.62)–(1.78); App. A, eqs. (A.1), (A.6), Table A.1*

> [!caution] Caution: Names and symbols
> - The cylindrical radius is $s$ here, as in Griffiths. Level A writes $r$ for it ([[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^rem-a3-4-2|§A3.4, Remark: Gradient in cylindrical and spherical coordinates]]), and Classical Mechanics writes $r$, or $\rho$ when both systems appear ([[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^def-b1-1-1|CM Def. §B1.1.1]]); in electrodynamics $\rho$ is the charge density and $r$ the distance from the origin, so neither is free.
> - Physics puts $\theta$ from the $z$ axis and $\varphi$ around it; the Multivariable Analysis notes mostly swap the two angle names ([[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^ex-25-3|452 Ex. §25.3]]; [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^cau-b1-1-1|CM §B1.1, Caution: Angle names]]), while 452 §33 keeps $\theta$ polar and writes $\psi$ for the azimuth.
> - The volume element is written $d\tau$, as in Griffiths, because $V$ is reserved for the potential; level A writes $dV$.

^cau-b1-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Curvilinear unit vectors depend on the point
> $\hat r$, $\hat\theta$, $\hat\varphi$ (and $\hat s$, $\hat\varphi$) change direction from point to point (Griffiths §1.4.1). Consequences:
> - Do not add components of vectors attached to different points: $\hat y$ and $-\hat y$ are both "$\hat r$" at the points $(0, \pm1, 0)$.
> - Do not take $\hat r$, $\hat\theta$, $\hat\varphi$ out of an integral over position; resolve into Cartesian components first ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^cau-b2-1-1|§B2.1, Caution: The separation vector in practice]]).
> - Differentiating a vector written in curvilinear components also differentiates the unit vectors. From [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]],
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\hat r}{\partial\theta} = \hat\theta,\quad \frac{\partial\hat r}{\partial\varphi} = \sin\theta\,\hat\varphi,\quad \frac{\partial\hat\theta}{\partial\theta} = -\hat r,\quad \frac{\partial\hat\theta}{\partial\varphi} = \cos\theta\,\hat\varphi,\quad \frac{\partial\hat\varphi}{\partial\varphi} = -\sin\theta\,\hat r - \cos\theta\,\hat\theta, \qquad \frac{\partial\hat s}{\partial\varphi} = \hat\varphi,\quad \frac{\partial\hat\varphi}{\partial\varphi} = -\hat s ,
> $$
>
> all other derivatives being zero. In particular the Laplacian of a vector field is **not** the scalar Laplacian applied to its curvilinear components (Griffiths, footnote to eq. (1.43)); compute it from $\nabla^2\vec v = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec v) - \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec v)$.

^cau-b1-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.5: Vector Derivatives in Curvilinear Coordinates
> In orthogonal curvilinear coordinates with scale factors $f, g, h$ (Griffiths eqs. (A.4), (A.8), (A.13), (A.16)):
>
> $$
> \nabla t = \frac{1}{f}\frac{\partial t}{\partial u}\,\hat u + \frac{1}{g}\frac{\partial t}{\partial v}\,\hat v + \frac{1}{h}\frac{\partial t}{\partial w}\,\hat w, \qquad
> \nabla\cdot\vec A = \frac{1}{fgh}\Bigl[\frac{\partial}{\partial u}(ghA_u) + \frac{\partial}{\partial v}(fhA_v) + \frac{\partial}{\partial w}(fgA_w)\Bigr],
> $$
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\vec A = \frac{1}{gh}\Bigl[\frac{\partial(hA_w)}{\partial v} - \frac{\partial(gA_v)}{\partial w}\Bigr]\hat u + \frac{1}{fh}\Bigl[\frac{\partial(fA_u)}{\partial w} - \frac{\partial(hA_w)}{\partial u}\Bigr]\hat v + \frac{1}{fg}\Bigl[\frac{\partial(gA_v)}{\partial u} - \frac{\partial(fA_u)}{\partial v}\Bigr]\hat w ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \nabla^2t = \frac{1}{fgh}\Bigl[\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\Bigl(\frac{gh}{f}\frac{\partial t}{\partial u}\Bigr) + \frac{\partial}{\partial v}\Bigl(\frac{fh}{g}\frac{\partial t}{\partial v}\Bigr) + \frac{\partial}{\partial w}\Bigl(\frac{fg}{h}\frac{\partial t}{\partial w}\Bigr)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> **Spherical coordinates** (eqs. (1.70)–(1.73)):
>
> $$
> \nabla T = \frac{\partial T}{\partial r}\hat r + \frac1r\frac{\partial T}{\partial\theta}\hat\theta + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\varphi}\hat\varphi, \qquad
> \nabla\cdot\vec v = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(r^2v_r) + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}(\sin\theta\,v_\theta) + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial v_\varphi}{\partial\varphi},
> $$
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\vec v = \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\Bigl[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}(\sin\theta\,v_\varphi) - \frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial\varphi}\Bigr]\hat r + \frac1r\Bigl[\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial v_r}{\partial\varphi} - \frac{\partial}{\partial r}(rv_\varphi)\Bigr]\hat\theta + \frac1r\Bigl[\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(rv_\theta) - \frac{\partial v_r}{\partial\theta}\Bigr]\hat\varphi,
> $$
>
> $$
> \nabla^2T = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Bigl(r^2\frac{\partial T}{\partial r}\Bigr) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\Bigl(\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial\theta}\Bigr) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial\varphi^2} .
> $$
>
> **Cylindrical coordinates** (eqs. (1.79)–(1.82)):
>
> $$
> \nabla T = \frac{\partial T}{\partial s}\hat s + \frac1s\frac{\partial T}{\partial\varphi}\hat\varphi + \frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\hat z, \qquad
> \nabla\cdot\vec v = \frac1s\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(sv_s) + \frac1s\frac{\partial v_\varphi}{\partial\varphi} + \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z},
> $$
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\vec v = \Bigl[\frac1s\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial\varphi} - \frac{\partial v_\varphi}{\partial z}\Bigr]\hat s + \Bigl[\frac{\partial v_s}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial s}\Bigr]\hat\varphi + \frac1s\Bigl[\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(sv_\varphi) - \frac{\partial v_s}{\partial\varphi}\Bigr]\hat z, \qquad
> \nabla^2T = \frac1s\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\Bigl(s\frac{\partial T}{\partial s}\Bigr) + \frac{1}{s^2}\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial\varphi^2} + \frac{\partial^2T}{\partial z^2} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.4, eqs. (1.70)–(1.73), (1.79)–(1.82); App. A, eqs. (A.2)–(A.16), Table A.1*

^thm-b1-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Gradient.* For a displacement from $(u, v, w)$ to $(u + du, v + dv, w + dw)$, $dt = \frac{\partial t}{\partial u}du + \frac{\partial t}{\partial v}dv + \frac{\partial t}{\partial w}dw$ (eq. (A.2)). Requiring $dt = \nabla t\cdot d\vec l = (\nabla t)_u\,f\,du + (\nabla t)_v\,g\,dv + (\nabla t)_w\,h\,dw$ for all $du, dv, dw$ fixes $(\nabla t)_u = \frac1f\frac{\partial t}{\partial u}$ and so on (eqs. (A.3)–(A.4)).
>
> *Divergence.* Take the infinitesimal box spanned by $f\,du$, $g\,dv$, $h\,dw$, of volume $d\tau = fgh\,du\,dv\,dw$. The face at $u$ has area $gh\,dv\,dw$ and outward normal $-\hat u$, so it contributes $-(ghA_u)\,dv\,dw$; the opposite face contributes the same quantity evaluated at $u + du$ with a plus sign. Together, $\frac{\partial}{\partial u}(ghA_u)\,du\,dv\,dw$. Adding the other two pairs of faces, $\oint\vec A\cdot d\vec a = \frac{1}{fgh}[\dots]\,d\tau$ (eq. (A.7)), and the divergence is defined as the coefficient of $d\tau$, the flux per unit volume of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^rem-b1-1-1|Remark: What the three derivatives measure]] (eq. (A.8)). The factors $gh$ inside the derivative are there because the face area itself changes with $u$.
>
> *Curl.* Run around the infinitesimal rectangle with sides $f\,du$ and $g\,dv$ at fixed $w$, counterclockwise about $\hat w$. The bottom and top edges give $(fA_u)|_v\,du - (fA_u)|_{v + dv}\,du = -\frac{\partial(fA_u)}{\partial v}du\,dv$, the right and left edges $+\frac{\partial(gA_v)}{\partial u}du\,dv$. Dividing by the area $fg\,du\,dv$ gives the $\hat w$ component (eqs. (A.11)–(A.13)); the others follow by cycling $(u, f) \to (v, g) \to (w, h)$.
>
> *Laplacian.* Insert the components of $\nabla t$ into the divergence (eq. (A.16)). With $(f, g, h) = (1, r, r\sin\theta)$ for spherical and $(1, s, 1)$ for cylindrical coordinates (Table A.1) all the special formulas follow; the spherical Laplacian is [[§33 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-33-1|452 Thm. §33.1]], and the general orthogonal formula is [[§33 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^rem-33-3|452 §33, Remark: General Orthogonal Coordinates]], both derived there in the same way from the divergence theorem. Every formula can be checked against the Cartesian definitions by the chain rule (Griffiths §1.4.1), which is how they were first found and which "would take an hour".

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The Laplacian from an energy integral
> Nelson derives the curvilinear Laplacian without boxes. For any $t$ and any test function $\psi$ that vanishes outside a bounded region, the divergence theorem gives $\int\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla t\,d\tau = -\int\psi\,\nabla^2t\,d\tau$, because the boundary term vanishes. Now write the left side in the coordinates $(q_1, q_2, q_3) = (u, v, w)$, with the scale factors renamed $(h_1, h_2, h_3) = (f, g, h)$ to free the letters: by [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]] for the gradient, $\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla t = \sum_i h_i^{-2}\,\partial_i\psi\,\partial_it$, and $d\tau = h_1h_2h_3\,dq_1\,dq_2\,dq_3$. Integrating by parts in each $q_i$ moves the derivative off $\psi$:
>
> $$
> \int\psi\,\nabla^2t\;h_1h_2h_3\,d^3q = -\int\sum_i\frac{h_1h_2h_3}{h_i^2}\,\partial_i\psi\,\partial_it\;d^3q = \int\psi\sum_i\partial_i\Bigl(\frac{h_1h_2h_3}{h_i^2}\,\partial_it\Bigr)d^3q .
> $$
>
> Since $\psi$ is arbitrary, $\nabla^2t = (h_1h_2h_3)^{-1}\sum_i\partial_i\bigl(h_1h_2h_3h_i^{-2}\,\partial_it\bigr)$, which is eq. (A.16). Only the metric, the matrix of dot products of the coordinate tangent vectors, enters; Nelson uses this to treat ellipsoidal coordinates (a blunt lightning rod) as easily as spherical ones, and notes that the same idea, the Laplacian determined by a metric, reappears in general relativity.
>
> *Source: Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 5, §5.3.2–5.3.5*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.1.1: A Gradient and a Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates
> Take $f(r, \theta, \varphi) = r\sin^2\varphi$. By [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]],
>
> $$
> \nabla f = \sin^2\varphi\,\hat r + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\,\frac{\partial(r\sin^2\varphi)}{\partial\varphi}\,\hat\varphi = \sin^2\varphi\,\hat r + \frac{\sin 2\varphi}{\sin\theta}\,\hat\varphi ,
> $$
>
> with no $\hat\theta$ component since $f$ does not depend on $\theta$. Its divergence is
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\nabla f = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\bigl(r^2\sin^2\varphi\bigr) + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}\Bigl(\frac{\sin2\varphi}{\sin\theta}\Bigr) = \frac{2\sin^2\varphi}{r} + \frac{2\cos 2\varphi}{r\sin^2\theta} ,
> $$
>
> and the Laplacian formula, applied directly, gives
>
> $$
> \nabla^2f = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Bigl(r^2\sin^2\varphi\Bigr) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\varphi^2}\bigl(r\sin^2\varphi\bigr) = \frac{2\sin^2\varphi}{r} + \frac{2\cos2\varphi}{r\sin^2\theta} ,
> $$
>
> the same, as it must be since $\nabla^2 = \nabla\cdot\nabla$. Note the scale factor $1/(r\sin\theta)$: $\partial f/\partial\varphi$ alone has the wrong units for a gradient. The function, $f = y^2r/s^2$ in Cartesian terms, is not smooth on the $z$ axis, and the formulas blow up there ($\sin\theta = 0$).
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 1, Problem 2 (Derivative examples), parts (b) and (e)*

^ex-b1-1-1

> [!example] Example §B1.1.2: A Flux That Measures Volume
> Let $\vec v = (x + y + z)\,\hat x + (x^2 - y)\,\hat y + (xy + z)\,\hat z$. Then $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 1 - 1 + 1 = 1$, so by the divergence theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]) the outward flux of $\vec v$ through **any** closed surface equals the volume it encloses. For the unit cube $0 \le x, y, z \le 1$ the six faces give, with outward normals,
>
> $$
> \oint\vec v\cdot d\vec a = \underbrace{-1 + 2}_{x = 0,\,1} \underbrace{-\tfrac13 - \tfrac23}_{y = 0,\,1} \underbrace{-\tfrac14 + \tfrac54}_{z = 0,\,1} = 1 ,
> $$
>
> for instance $\int_{x=1}v_x\,dy\,dz = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1(1 + y + z)\,dy\,dz = 2$. For a ball of radius $R$ the flux is $\tfrac43\pi R^3$, with no surface integral to do.
>
> The planar analogue uses Stokes' theorem: $\vec u = (x^2 - 2y)\,\hat x - (x + yz)\,\hat y - xyz\,\hat z$ has $\nabla\times\vec u = (y - xz)\,\hat x + yz\,\hat y + \hat z$, so $(\nabla\times\vec u)\cdot\hat z = 1$, and the counterclockwise circulation of $\vec u$ around any closed loop in the $xy$ plane equals the area it encloses (around the unit square: $\tfrac13 - 1 - (\tfrac13 - 2) - 0 = 1$). A field with constant divergence or constant normal curl turns a flux or a circulation into a measurement of volume or area; this is how planimeters work, and it is Green's theorem in the plane ([[§27 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^thm-27-1|452 Thm. §27.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 1, Problem 4 (Vectors and volumes and areas, oh my!)*
