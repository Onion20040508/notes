*Sources: Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (4th ed.), §1.5–1.6, App. B · PHY 422 (Thamm, Spring 2024).*

The field of a point charge, $\hat r/r^2$ up to constants, has zero divergence everywhere except at the charge, where the formula breaks down; yet its flux through every sphere around the charge is $4\pi$. Level A met this as the radius-independence of the flux ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]]) and proved Gauss's law by counting solid angles ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]). This section makes the singular point part of the calculus: the three-dimensional delta function gives $\nabla\cdot(\hat r/r^2) = 4\pi\delta^3(\vec r)$ and $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec r)$, the identities behind Gauss's law and Poisson's equation. The one-dimensional delta function is at home in Waves and Optics ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|WO Def. §B4.4.2]]). The second half answers the structural question behind Maxwell's equations: when do a divergence and a curl determine a field (the Helmholtz theorem), and when is a field the gradient of a scalar or the curl of a vector potential?

## The delta function in three dimensions

> [!definition] Definition §B1.2.1: Three-Dimensional Delta Function
> The one-dimensional delta function $\delta(x - a)$ is the distribution with $\int f(x)\,\delta(x - a)\,dx = f(a)$ for every continuous $f$ whenever the range of integration contains $a$, and $0$ otherwise ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|WO Def. §B4.4.2]]; Griffiths eqs. (1.86)–(1.92)). Two expressions containing delta functions are **equal** if their integrals against every ordinary function agree (eq. (1.93)). The **three-dimensional delta function** is (eq. (1.96))
>
> $$
> \delta^3(\vec r) = \delta(x)\,\delta(y)\,\delta(z) ,
> $$
>
> so that, over any volume containing the point $\vec a$ and for every continuous $f$,
>
> $$
> \int\delta^3(\vec r - \vec a)\,d\tau = 1, \qquad \int f(\vec r)\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec a)\,d\tau = f(\vec a)
> $$
>
> (eqs. (1.97)–(1.98)), and $0$ if the volume does not contain $\vec a$. It has units of inverse volume.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.5.2–1.5.3, eqs. (1.86)–(1.98)*

^def-b1-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.1: Working Rules for Delta Functions
> 1. *Scaling and composition.* $\delta(kx) = \delta(x)/|k|$ for $k \ne 0$ (Griffiths eq. (1.94)). If $g$ is differentiable with simple zeros $x_i$ ($g(x_i) = 0$, $g'(x_i) \ne 0$), then
>
> $$
> \delta\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \sum_i\frac{\delta(x - x_i)}{|g'(x_i)|} .
> $$
>
> 2. *Derivatives.* $x\,\dfrac{d\delta}{dx} = -\delta(x)$, and the step function $\theta(x)$ (1 for $x > 0$, 0 for $x \le 0$) has $\theta'(x) = \delta(x)$.
> 3. *Curvilinear coordinates.* Away from the coordinate singularities,
>
> $$
> \delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,') = \frac{\delta(r - r')\,\delta(\theta - \theta')\,\delta(\varphi - \varphi')}{r^2\sin\theta} = \frac{\delta(s - s')\,\delta(\varphi - \varphi')\,\delta(z - z')}{s} :
> $$
>
> the denominator is the product of the scale factors of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]].
> 4. *Charge densities of idealized charges.* A point charge $q$ at $\vec r\,'$ has volume density $\rho(\vec r) = q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,')$; a uniform spherical shell of radius $R$ and charge $Q$ has $\rho = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi R^2}\,\delta(r - R)$; a line charge $\lambda$ along the $z$ axis has $\rho = \lambda\,\delta(x)\,\delta(y)$. In general a surface charge is $\rho = \sigma\,\delta(n)$ with $n$ the signed distance from the surface.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.5.2, Example 1.15, eq. (1.94); Problems 1.46–1.47 · PHY 422, HW 1, Problem 5 and the instructor's solution (composition rule)*

^thm-b1-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. The scaling rule is the substitution $y = kx$ under the integral, with the limits reversed when $k < 0$ (Griffiths Example 1.15; [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|WO Theorem §B4.4.3]]). For the composition, only neighbourhoods of the zeros contribute, since $\delta(g(x))$ vanishes where $g \ne 0$. Near a simple zero, $g$ is monotone and $g(x) \approx g'(x_i)(x - x_i)$; substituting $y = g(x)$ in $\int f(x)\,\delta(g(x))\,dx$ over that neighbourhood gives $f(x_i)/|g'(x_i)|$, exactly what $\delta(x - x_i)/|g'(x_i)|$ gives.
> 2. Against a test function $f$ (smooth, vanishing outside a bounded interval), integrate by parts; the boundary terms vanish because $f$ does: $\int x\,\delta'(x)\,f\,dx = -\int\delta(x)\,(xf)'\,dx = -\bigl[f(0) + 0\cdot f'(0)\bigr] = -f(0)$, which is $-\int\delta\,f\,dx$. Similarly $\int\theta'f\,dx = -\int_0^\infty f'\,dx = f(0)$.
> 3. With $d\tau = r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi$, $\int f\,\frac{\delta(r - r')\delta(\theta - \theta')\delta(\varphi - \varphi')}{r^2\sin\theta}\,d\tau = f(r', \theta', \varphi')$, which is the defining property of $\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,')$; the criterion of equality in [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]] does the rest. The same with $d\tau = s\,ds\,d\varphi\,dz$.
> 4. Check the integrals: $\int q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,')\,d\tau = q$ over any region containing $\vec r\,'$, and nothing elsewhere. For the shell, $\int\frac{Q}{4\pi R^2}\,\delta(r - R)\,r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi = \frac{Q}{4\pi R^2}\,R^2\cdot 4\pi = Q$, all of it at $r = R$. For the line, $\int\lambda\,\delta(x)\delta(y)\,dx\,dy\,dz = \lambda\,L$ over a length $L$ of the axis.

## The divergence of the inverse-square field

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.2: Divergence of the Inverse-Square Field; Laplacian of 1/r
> As distributions (Griffiths eqs. (1.99)–(1.102)),
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\Bigl(\frac{\hat r}{r^2}\Bigr) = 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r), \qquad \nabla\Bigl(\frac1r\Bigr) = -\frac{\hat r}{r^2}, \qquad \nabla^2\Bigl(\frac1r\Bigr) = -4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r) .
> $$
>
> With the separation vector $\vec{\mathcal R} = \vec r - \vec r\,'$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]) and derivatives with respect to the field point $\vec r$ at fixed $\vec r\,'$,
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\Bigl(\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\Bigr) = 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,'), \qquad \nabla\Bigl(\frac{1}{\mathcal R}\Bigr) = -\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}, \qquad \nabla^2\Bigl(\frac{1}{\mathcal R}\Bigr) = -4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,') ,
> $$
>
> while derivatives with respect to the source point flip the sign of the gradient: $\nabla'(1/\mathcal R) = +\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2$. Among the radial fields $r^n\hat r$, only $n = -2$ produces a delta function: for $r \ne 0$, $\nabla\cdot(r^n\hat r) = (n + 2)\,r^{n-1}$, and for $n > -2$ this is the whole divergence.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.5.1, §1.5.3, eqs. (1.83)–(1.85), (1.99)–(1.102); Problems 1.13, 1.63, 1.64*

^thm-b1-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Away from the origin.* By the spherical divergence of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]], $\nabla\cdot(r^n\hat r) = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}(r^{n+2}) = (n + 2)\,r^{n-1}$; for $n = -2$ this is $0$ (eq. (1.84)). Yet the flux of $\hat r/r^2$ through a sphere of radius $R$ is $\int\frac{1}{R^2}\,R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi = 4\pi$ for every $R$ (eq. (1.85)): the divergence theorem would fail unless all of the divergence sits at $r = 0$, with total weight $4\pi$.
>
> *At the origin.* A distribution is known by its integrals against smooth test functions $f$ that vanish outside a bounded region, and the divergence of a distribution is defined by moving the derivative onto $f$, as in [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]] with no boundary term: $\int f\,\nabla\cdot\vec v\,d\tau := -\int\vec v\cdot\nabla f\,d\tau$. For $\vec v = \hat r/r^2$, $\vec v\cdot\nabla f = r^{-2}\,\partial f/\partial r$, and the $r^2$ of the volume element cancels it:
>
> $$
> -\int\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\,r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi = -\int\!\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi\int_0^\infty\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\,dr = \int\!\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi\;f(0) = 4\pi f(0) ,
> $$
>
> which is $\int f\cdot 4\pi\delta^3(\vec r)\,d\tau$. For $r^n\hat r$ with $n > -2$ the same steps leave a factor $r^{n+2}$, which kills the boundary value at $r = 0$, and an integration by parts in $r$ returns $\int f\,(n + 2)r^{n-1}\,d\tau$: no delta function. (For $n < -2$ the pointwise divergence $(n + 2)r^{n-1}$ is not integrable at the origin, and Griffiths calls the divergence ill-defined there. In the distributional sense it still exists for $-3 < n < -2$, where $r^n\hat r$ is integrable near the origin, but it is then neither a function nor a multiple of $\delta^3$; for $n \le -3$ the field is not even integrable near the origin and defines no distribution without further regularization.)
>
> *Smoothing check* (Griffiths Problem 1.64). Replace $r$ by $\sqrt{r^2 + \epsilon^2}$. The field $\vec v_\epsilon = \vec r/(r^2 + \epsilon^2)^{3/2} = -\nabla(r^2 + \epsilon^2)^{-1/2}$ is smooth, and with $\nabla\cdot(\vec r\,F(r)) = 3F + rF'$,
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec v_\epsilon = \frac{3\epsilon^2}{(r^2 + \epsilon^2)^{5/2}} = 4\pi D(r, \epsilon) .
> $$
>
> $D$ is a nascent delta function: $D(0, \epsilon) \to \infty$ and $D(r, \epsilon) \to 0$ for $r \ne 0$ as $\epsilon \to 0$, and with $r = \epsilon t$, $\int D\,d\tau = 3\int_0^\infty\frac{t^2\,dt}{(1 + t^2)^{5/2}} = 3\cdot\frac13 = 1$ (put $t = \tan u$: $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2u\cos u\,du = \tfrac13$). Hence $\nabla\cdot\vec v_\epsilon \to 4\pi\delta^3$ and $\nabla^2(r^2 + \epsilon^2)^{-1/2} = -4\pi D \to -4\pi\delta^3$.
>
> *The other identities.* $\nabla(1/r) = \hat r\,\frac{d}{dr}(1/r) = -\hat r/r^2$ by the spherical gradient for $r \ne 0$. This also holds as distributions, with nothing extra at the origin: against a test function, $-\int\frac1r\,\nabla f\,d\tau$ over the region $r > \epsilon$ equals $\int f\,\bigl(-\hat r/r^2\bigr)\,d\tau$ over the same region plus a surface term on the small sphere bounded by $\frac1\epsilon\,\max|f|\cdot4\pi\epsilon^2 \to 0$ (the first identity of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]], one Cartesian component at a time). Hence $\nabla^2(1/r) = \nabla\cdot\nabla(1/r) = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec r)$. Translating the origin to $\vec r\,'$ replaces $\vec r$ by $\vec{\mathcal R}$. Since $\vec{\mathcal R}$ depends on $\vec r - \vec r\,'$ only, $\partial/\partial x' = -\partial/\partial x$ on any function of it, which flips the sign of $\nabla'$.

> [!remark] Remark: What the identities are for
> - **Point charges become densities.** With $\rho = q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,')$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]] (4)), the field $q\hat{\mathcal R}/4\pi\varepsilon_0\mathcal R^2$ satisfies $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ everywhere, the charge's own location included. This is how Gauss's law in differential form is proved for any distribution in [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], replacing the solid-angle count of [[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^der-a2-2-2|§A2.2]].
> - **The inverse square is special.** Only $n = -2$ gives a field that is divergence-free away from the source and has a source at the origin. Gauss's law, and with it the experimental tests of the inverse-square law through the absence of charge inside conductors ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^rem-a2-4-3|§A2.4, Remark: A precision test of Coulomb's law]]), rest on this.
> - **Green function.** $\nabla^2(1/\mathcal R) = -4\pi\delta^3$ says that $-1/(4\pi\mathcal R)$ is the Green function of the Laplacian in $\mathbb R^3$: superposing it solves Poisson's equation ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]). Away from the origin $1/r$ is the radial harmonic function of [[§19 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^rem-19-2|452 §19, Remark: Verification: Radial Functions]]; its two-dimensional counterpart is $\ln s$ ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-5|452 Thm. §17.5]]). Green functions in general: Mathematical Methods (planned) and Electromagnetism level C.
> - **Quantum mechanics** uses the same identity to exclude a $1/r$ behaviour of $s$-wave functions at the origin: $\nabla^2(1/r)$ is not zero there but a delta function that no physical potential supplies (derivation of [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-2|QM Theorem §B5.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.5.1, §2.2.2*

## Potentials and the Helmholtz theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.3: Scalar and Vector Potentials
> Let $\vec F$ be continuously differentiable on all of $\mathbb R^3$ (or on a star-shaped region). Then (Griffiths Theorems 1 and 2, §1.6.2):
> 1. **Curl-free fields.** The following are equivalent: (a) $\nabla\times\vec F = \vec 0$; (b) $\int_{\mathbf a}^{\mathbf b}\vec F\cdot d\vec l$ is independent of the path; (c) $\oint\vec F\cdot d\vec l = 0$ for every closed loop; (d) $\vec F = -\nabla V$ for some scalar function $V$, the **scalar potential**, unique up to an additive constant.
> 2. **Divergence-free fields.** The following are equivalent: (a) $\nabla\cdot\vec F = 0$; (b) $\int\vec F\cdot d\vec a$ is independent of the surface for a given boundary curve; (c) $\oint\vec F\cdot d\vec a = 0$ for every closed surface; (d) $\vec F = \nabla\times\vec A$ for some vector function $\vec A$, the **vector potential**, unique up to adding the gradient of a scalar.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.6.2, eqs. (1.103)–(1.105), Theorems 1–2; Problems 1.51–1.52*

^thm-b1-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Curl-free.* (d) ⇒ (a): $\nabla\times\nabla V = \vec 0$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]). (a) ⇒ (c): on a region in which every loop bounds a surface lying in the region, Stokes' theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]) gives $\oint\vec F\cdot d\vec l = \int(\nabla\times\vec F)\cdot d\vec a = 0$. (c) ⇔ (b): two paths from $\mathbf a$ to $\mathbf b$ form a loop, one of them run backwards. (b) ⇒ (d): fix a reference point $\mathcal O$ and set $V(\vec r) = -\int_{\mathcal O}^{\vec r}\vec F\cdot d\vec l$, well defined by (b). Extending the path by $d\vec l$ changes $V$ by $-\vec F\cdot d\vec l$, and $dV = \nabla V\cdot d\vec l$ for every $d\vec l$ forces $\nabla V = -\vec F$. Adding a constant to $V$ changes nothing, and two potentials of the same $\vec F$ have zero gradient, so they differ by a constant on a connected region.
>
> *Divergence-free.* (d) ⇒ (a): $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A) = 0$. (a) ⇒ (c): the divergence theorem, for closed surfaces that enclose a volume within the region. (c) ⇔ (b): two surfaces with a common boundary form a closed surface, one of them with its orientation reversed. Adding $\nabla\lambda$ to $\vec A$ does not change $\nabla\times\vec A$.
>
> *The hard directions*, (a) ⇒ (d) in both parts, are the Poincaré lemma: on a star-shaped region every closed form is exact ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-12|452 Prop. §22.12]]); for 1-forms this says that an irrotational field is a gradient, for 2-forms that a solenoidal field is a curl ([[Poincaré Lemma]], "Classical form"). On all of $\mathbb R^3$, for fields that vanish fast enough at infinity, the Helmholtz theorem below constructs $V$ and $\vec A$ explicitly.

^der-b1-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]], [[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-12|452 Prop. §22.12]]

> [!caution] Caution: Curl-free is not enough on a region with a hole
> The equivalences need the region to be free of holes of the relevant kind. The field $\hat\varphi/s$ is curl-free on space with the $z$ axis removed, yet its circulation around the axis is $2\pi$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^ex-b1-2-2|Example §B1.2.2]]): it is the gradient of the azimuth $\varphi$, which has no single-valued continuous version around the axis. Likewise $\hat r/r^2$ is divergence-free on space with the origin removed, yet its flux through a sphere around the origin is $4\pi$, so it is not a curl there. Both are the standard non-exact closed forms ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-11|452 Prop. §22.11]], [[Angle form on the punctured plane]]), and both are physical fields: the magnetic field of a straight wire and the electric field of a point charge. In each case the "hole" is where the source sits, and the delta functions of this section restore the global statements: $\nabla\times(\hat\varphi/s) = 2\pi\,\delta(x)\delta(y)\,\hat z$ and $\nabla\cdot(\hat r/r^2) = 4\pi\delta^3(\vec r)$.

^cau-b1-2-1

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The potentials written down explicitly
> On a region star-shaped about the origin the Poincaré lemma has an explicit form, obtained by integrating along the rays from the origin: if $\nabla\times\vec F = \vec 0$, then $V(\vec r) = -\int_0^1\vec F(t\vec r)\cdot\vec r\,dt$, which is $-\int\vec F\cdot d\vec l$ along the straight segment from $0$ to $\vec r$; if $\nabla\cdot\vec F = 0$, then
>
> $$
> \vec A(\vec r) = \int_0^1\vec F(t\vec r)\times t\vec r\;dt
> $$
>
> has $\nabla\times\vec A = \vec F$. (The check uses product rule (vi) of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]: with $\nabla\cdot\vec F = 0$ the integrand's curl is $\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[t^2\vec F(t\vec r)\bigr]$.) For a uniform field, $\vec A = \tfrac12\vec F\times\vec r$: the vector potential of a uniform magnetic field ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole|§B6.4]]). Neither formula is unique, in keeping with the freedom of [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]].
>
> *Source: worked out here; it is the homotopy-operator construction behind [[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-12|452 Prop. §22.12]]*

^rem-b1-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.4: Helmholtz Theorem
> Let $D(\vec r)$ be a scalar and $\vec C(\vec r)$ a vector function on $\mathbb R^3$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec C = 0$, both falling off faster than $1/r^2$ as $r \to \infty$. Then there is exactly one vector field $\vec F$ with
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec F = D, \qquad \nabla\times\vec F = \vec C, \qquad \vec F \to \vec 0 \text{ as } r \to \infty ,
> $$
>
> namely (Griffiths eqs. (B.4)–(B.6))
>
> $$
> \vec F = -\nabla U + \nabla\times\vec W, \qquad U(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{D(\vec r\,')}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau', \qquad \vec W(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vec C(\vec r\,')}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau' ,
> $$
>
> integrals over all space. *Corollary* (eq. (B.10)): every differentiable $\vec F$ that falls off faster than $1/r$ is the gradient of a scalar plus the curl of a vector,
>
> $$
> \vec F(\vec r) = \nabla\Bigl(-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{\nabla'\cdot\vec F(\vec r\,')}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau'\Bigr) + \nabla\times\Bigl(\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{\nabla'\times\vec F(\vec r\,')}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau'\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.6.1, App. B, eqs. (B.1)–(B.10)*

^thm-b1-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Existence.* Differentiate under the integrals with respect to $\vec r$, which enters only through $\mathcal R$. Since the divergence of a curl vanishes, $\nabla\cdot\vec F = -\nabla^2U = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\int D(\vec r\,')\,\nabla^2\frac{1}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau' = \int D(\vec r\,')\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,')\,d\tau' = D(\vec r)$ by [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]. Since the curl of a gradient vanishes, $\nabla\times\vec F = \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec W) = -\nabla^2\vec W + \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec W)$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]), and $-\nabla^2\vec W = \vec C$ in the same way, component by component. The remaining term vanishes: using $\nabla(1/\mathcal R) = -\nabla'(1/\mathcal R)$ and integrating by parts in the primed variables ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]),
>
> $$
> 4\pi\,\nabla\cdot\vec W = \int\vec C\cdot\nabla\frac{1}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau' = -\int\vec C\cdot\nabla'\frac{1}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau' = \int\frac{1}{\mathcal R}\,\nabla'\cdot\vec C\,d\tau' - \oint\frac{1}{\mathcal R}\,\vec C\cdot d\vec a\,' = 0
> $$
>
> (eq. (B.8)): $\nabla'\cdot\vec C = 0$ by assumption, and the surface integral, taken on a sphere receding to infinity, vanishes because $\vec C$ falls off fast enough. The integrals converge: at large $r'$, $\mathcal R \approx r'$, and $\int^\infty\frac{X(r')}{r'}\,r'^2\,dr'$ is finite only if $X$ (that is, $D$ or $\vec C$) falls faster than $1/r'^2$ (eq. (B.9)); this also makes $\vec F \to \vec 0$.
>
> *Uniqueness.* If $\vec F_1$ and $\vec F_2$ both qualify, $\vec G = \vec F_1 - \vec F_2$ has zero divergence and zero curl and tends to $\vec 0$ at infinity. Then $\nabla^2\vec G = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec G) - \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec G) = \vec 0$, so each Cartesian component of $\vec G$ is a harmonic function on all of $\mathbb R^3$ that tends to $0$ at infinity; a harmonic function has no interior maximum or minimum ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]; Griffiths refers to his §3.1.5), so each component vanishes identically and $\vec F_1 = \vec F_2$.
>
> *Corollary.* Apply the theorem with $D = \nabla\cdot\vec F$ and $\vec C = \nabla\times\vec F$, which fall off faster than $1/r^2$ when $\vec F$ falls off faster than $1/r$ and has derivatives falling off one power faster.

> [!remark] Remark: Why electrodynamics is written as divergences and curls
> - Maxwell's equations specify the divergence and the curl of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|Principle §A12.1.3]], in integral form; differential form in [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations|§B8.4]]). By the Helmholtz theorem, that is exactly enough information, given that the fields vanish far from all charges and currents.
> - In electrostatics $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ and $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$, and the theorem returns $\vec E = -\nabla V$ with $V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\rho/\mathcal R\,d\tau'$ (eq. (B.11); [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]). In magnetostatics $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ and $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$, and it returns $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$ with $\vec A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\vec J/\mathcal R\,d\tau'$ (eq. (B.12); [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]]).
> - The theorem's hypotheses fail for the idealized infinite plane, line and cylinder of level A ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law|§A2.3]]): their sources do not fall off at all, and the fields do not vanish at infinity. Such fields are fixed by symmetry instead, and the integral formulas for $U$ and $\vec W$ diverge, which is the vector-calculus face of [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^cau-a3-3-1|§A3.3, Caution: When infinity cannot be the reference]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.6.1 (with footnote 14), App. B (with footnote 1)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.2.1: Integrals with Delta Functions
> (a) $\displaystyle\int_2^6\cos x\,\delta(x - \pi)\,dx = \cos\pi = -1$, since $\pi$ lies in $[2, 6]$.
>
> (b) $\displaystyle\int_0^3(x^5 - 2)\,\delta(x + 1)\,dx = 0$: the spike sits at $x = -1$, outside the range.
>
> (c) By the scaling rule of [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]], $\delta(3x + 1) = \tfrac13\,\delta(x + \tfrac13)$, so
>
> $$
> \int_{-1}^{1}9x^2\,\delta(3x + 1)\,dx = \frac13\cdot 9\Bigl(-\frac13\Bigr)^2 = \frac13 .
> $$
>
> (d) $g(x) = 1 - x^2$ has simple zeros at $x = \pm1$ with $|g'(\pm1)| = 2$, so $\delta(1 - x^2) = \tfrac12[\delta(x - 1) + \delta(x + 1)]$ and
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(x^2 - 3x + 1)\,\delta(1 - x^2)\,dx = \frac12\bigl[(1 - 3 + 1) + (1 + 3 + 1)\bigr] = 2 .
> $$
>
> (e) In three dimensions, the uniform shell $\rho = \frac{Q}{4\pi R^2}\delta(r - R)$ of Theorem §B1.2.1 (4), integrated over the ball $r < a$, encloses $0$ for $a < R$ and $Q$ for $a > R$: the delta function in $r$ reproduces the enclosed charge that Gauss's law needs ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]).
>
> The common mistake in (c) and (d) is to forget the factor $1/|g'|$: a delta function of a function is not a delta function of its argument.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 1, Problem 5 (Evaluate these integrals), parts (a)–(d), checked against the instructor's solution; part (e) after Griffiths Problem 1.47(c)*

> [!example] Example §B1.2.2: A Curl Hidden on the Axis
> Let $\vec F = \hat\varphi/s$ in cylindrical coordinates. Its arrows circle the $z$ axis, so one expects a curl along $+\hat z$. The cylindrical curl ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]]) says otherwise: with $F_\varphi = 1/s$ the only candidate is
>
> $$
> (\nabla\times\vec F)_z = \frac1s\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\Bigl(s\cdot\frac1s\Bigr) = 0 \qquad (s > 0) .
> $$
>
> But Stokes' theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]) on a disk of radius $R$ in the $xy$ plane gives, with $d\vec l = R\,d\varphi\,\hat\varphi$ on the rim,
>
> $$
> \int_{\text{disk}}(\nabla\times\vec F)\cdot d\vec a = \oint\vec F\cdot d\vec l = \int_0^{2\pi}\frac1R\,R\,d\varphi = 2\pi ,
> $$
>
> for every $R$. So the whole curl sits on the axis, where the formula divided by $s = 0$. The field is unchanged by rotations about and translations along the $z$ axis, so the curl is $f(x, y)\,\hat z$ with $f$ zero off the axis and $\int f\,dx\,dy = 2\pi$:
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\Bigl(\frac{\hat\varphi}{s}\Bigr) = 2\pi\,\delta(x)\,\delta(y)\,\hat z .
> $$
>
> The two-dimensional delta function plays the role that $\delta^3$ plays for $\hat r/r^2$. Off the axis $\vec F = \nabla\varphi$, the gradient of the azimuth (by the cylindrical gradient, $\nabla\varphi = \hat\varphi/s$), but $\varphi$ jumps by $2\pi$ around the axis, so $\vec F$ is not a gradient on any region that encircles it ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^cau-b1-2-1|Caution: Curl-free is not enough on a region with a hole]]). Physically $\vec F$ is, up to the factor $\mu_0I/2\pi$, the magnetic field of a long straight wire carrying current $I$ along $\hat z$: the result is $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0I\,\delta(x)\delta(y)\,\hat z = \mu_0\vec J$, Ampère's law in differential form for a thin wire ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 1, Problem 6 (Zero curl?), checked against the instructor's solution (2023 numbering: HW 2, Problem 1)*
