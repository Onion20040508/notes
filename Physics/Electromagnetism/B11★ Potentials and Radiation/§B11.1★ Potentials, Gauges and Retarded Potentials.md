*Sources: Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (4th ed.), §10.1–10.2 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 25, 38 · Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lecture 16.*

Given the charges and currents $\rho(\vec r, t)$ and $\vec J(\vec r, t)$, what are the fields? For static sources Coulomb's law and the Biot–Savart law answer this, through the potentials $V$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations|§B2.2]]) and $\vec A$ ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole|§B6.4]]). Level A used a potential only in electrostatics, with $\vec E = -\nabla V$ ([[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]), and showed that an induced electric field is not conservative ([[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^thm-a9-4-2|Theorem §A9.4.2]]), so that no scalar potential alone can describe it. This section rewrites the full Maxwell equations ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]; [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|Principle §A12.1.3]]) in terms of $V$ and $\vec A$ together, uses the freedom in choosing them (the gauge) to turn the equations into four inhomogeneous wave equations, solves these by the retarded potentials, and differentiates the result into Jefimenko's equations, the time-dependent Coulomb and Biot–Savart laws.

## Potentials in electrodynamics

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.1: Scalar and Vector Potentials
> Let $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ satisfy Maxwell's equations ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]; Griffiths eq. (10.1)) on all of space. Then there are a **scalar potential** $V(\vec r, t)$ and a **vector potential** $\vec A(\vec r, t)$ with
>
> $$
> \vec B = \nabla\times\vec A, \qquad \vec E = -\nabla V - \frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (10.2)–(10.3)). Conversely, fields defined by these formulas from any twice-differentiable $V$ and $\vec A$ satisfy the two homogeneous equations $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ and $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial t$ automatically, and the two inhomogeneous equations (Gauss, Ampère–Maxwell) become
>
> $$
> \nabla^2V + \frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\cdot\vec A) = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \Bigl(\nabla^2\vec A - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec A}{\partial t^2}\Bigr) - \nabla\Bigl(\nabla\cdot\vec A + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\Bigr) = -\mu_0\vec J
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (10.4)–(10.5)). These two equations contain all of Maxwell's equations, in four unknown functions instead of six. For static fields they reduce to Poisson's equation ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]), $\vec E = -\nabla V$ and $\nabla^2\vec A - \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec A) = -\mu_0\vec J$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.1.1, eqs. (10.1)–(10.5)*

^thm-b11-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Existence.* Since $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ on all of $\mathbb R^3$, at each instant $\vec B$ is a curl, $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$ ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-12|452 Prop. §22.12]]; the physicist's form is [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]), exactly as in magnetostatics ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-1|Theorem §B6.4.1]]); now $\vec A$ depends on $t$ as a parameter. Faraday's law gives $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial_t(\nabla\times\vec A) = -\nabla\times(\partial_t\vec A)$, the time and space derivatives commuting ([[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]). So $\nabla\times(\vec E + \partial_t\vec A) = \vec 0$, and the curl-free field $\vec E + \partial_t\vec A$ is a gradient, which we call $-\nabla V$ (452 Prop. §22.12 again).
>
> *Homogeneous equations.* $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A) = 0$ and $\nabla\times\nabla V = \vec 0$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]), so $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ and $\nabla\times\vec E = -\nabla\times\partial_t\vec A = -\partial_t\vec B$.
>
> *Inhomogeneous equations.* Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ with $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A$ is $-\nabla^2V - \partial_t(\nabla\cdot\vec A) = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, the first equation. The Ampère–Maxwell law $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t\vec E$ becomes
>
> $$
> \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec A) = \mu_0\vec J - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\nabla\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\Bigr) - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec A}{\partial t^2} ;
> $$
>
> with $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec A) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec A) - \nabla^2\vec A$ (Theorem §B1.1.2) and the terms rearranged, this is the second equation. In the static case $\partial_t = 0$ and the equations reduce as stated.

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level A
> The level-A relation $\vec E = -\nabla V$ ([[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]) is the special case $\partial\vec A/\partial t = \vec 0$. The extra term $-\partial\vec A/\partial t$ is the induced field of Faraday's law: around a fixed closed loop $C$ bounding a surface $S$, Stokes' theorem ([[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]]) gives
>
> $$
> \oint_C\vec E\cdot d\vec l = -\frac{d}{dt}\oint_C\vec A\cdot d\vec l = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_S\vec B\cdot d\vec a ,
> $$
>
> the gradient term contributing nothing. This is the non-conservative field of [[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^thm-a9-4-2|Theorem §A9.4.2]]; for a pure Faraday field, $\vec E = -\partial\vec A/\partial t$ with the Coulomb-gauge $\vec A$ ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^rem-b8-2-3|§B8.2, Remark: The induced field is minus the rate of change of the vector potential]]). The work per unit charge between two points now depends on the path, and $V$ is no longer potential energy per unit charge. It is a bookkeeping device, and, as the next theorem shows, not even a unique one.

## Gauge freedom

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.2: Gauge Transformations
> Two pairs of potentials $(V, \vec A)$ and $(V', \vec A')$ give the same fields $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ if and only if there is a scalar function $\lambda(\vec r, t)$, the **gauge function**, with
>
> $$
> \vec A' = \vec A + \nabla\lambda, \qquad V' = V - \frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial t}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (10.7)). Such a change of potentials is a **gauge transformation**, the time-dependent extension of the gauge freedom of magnetostatics ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-1|Theorem §B6.4.1]]); quantities unchanged by it, such as $\vec E$ and $\vec B$, are **gauge invariant**. (Griffiths writes $\lambda$ for the gauge function, Classical Mechanics writes $\chi$.)
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.1.2, eq. (10.7)*

^thm-b11-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $\vec A' = \vec A + \vec\alpha$ and $V' = V + \beta$. Equal magnetic fields mean $\nabla\times\vec\alpha = \vec 0$, so $\vec\alpha = \nabla\lambda$ for some scalar $\lambda$ ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-12|452 Prop. §22.12]]). Equal electric fields mean $\nabla\beta + \partial_t\vec\alpha = \vec 0$, that is $\nabla(\beta + \partial_t\lambda) = \vec 0$: the bracket is independent of position, $\beta = -\partial_t\lambda + k(t)$. Replacing $\lambda$ by $\lambda + \int_0^tk(t')\,dt'$ changes neither $\nabla\lambda$ nor anything else and absorbs $k$, so $\beta = -\partial_t\lambda$. Conversely, $\nabla\times\nabla\lambda = \vec 0$ leaves $\vec B$ unchanged, and $-\nabla(-\partial_t\lambda) - \partial_t\nabla\lambda = \vec 0$ leaves $\vec E$ unchanged.

> [!definition] Definition §B11.1.1: Lorenz Gauge; d'Alembertian
> Potentials are in the **Lorenz gauge** if they satisfy the **Lorenz condition**
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec A = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (10.12)). The **d'Alembertian** is the wave operator
>
> $$
> \Box^2 = \nabla^2 - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2} = \nabla^2 - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (10.15)); an equation $\Box^2f = -s$ with a given source $s$ is an **inhomogeneous wave equation**. The other gauge used below is the Coulomb gauge $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$, defined in magnetostatics ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^def-b6-4-2|Def. §B6.4.2]]) and now imposed at every instant (Griffiths eq. (10.8)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.1.3, eqs. (10.8), (10.12), (10.15) · Nelson ch. 25, §25.1 (Coulomb gauge) and ch. 38, §38.3 (Lorenz gauge)*

^def-b11-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Lorenz, not Lorentz
> The condition is named after the Danish physicist Ludvig Lorenz; it used to be credited to H. A. Lorentz, and many books still write "Lorentz gauge" (Griffiths, footnote to §10.1.3). The Lorentz force and the Lorentz transformation are Lorentz's.

^cau-b11-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.3: The Field Equations in the Coulomb and Lorenz Gauges
> 1. *Both gauges can always be reached:* for any potentials $(V, \vec A)$ there is a gauge transformation to Coulomb gauge and one to Lorenz gauge. Within the Lorenz gauge, further transformations with $\Box^2\lambda = 0$ remain possible.
> 2. *Coulomb gauge:* $V$ obeys Poisson's equation at each instant, $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$, so that (with $V \to 0$ at infinity)
>    $$
>    V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\rho(\vec r\,', t)}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau' ,
>    $$
>    while $\nabla^2\vec A - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t^2\vec A = -\mu_0\vec J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\nabla(\partial_tV)$ (Griffiths eqs. (10.9)–(10.11); $\vec{\mathcal R} = \vec r - \vec r\,'$ is the separation vector of [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]).
> 3. *Lorenz gauge:* $V$ and $\vec A$ obey four inhomogeneous wave equations of the same form,
>    $$
>    \Box^2V = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \Box^2\vec A = -\mu_0\vec J
>    $$
>    (Griffiths eqs. (10.13)–(10.14), (10.16)).
> 4. *No charge density:* if $\rho = 0$ everywhere at all times (currents with $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$, such as neutral wires and sheets), the Coulomb-gauge potentials have $V = 0$, satisfy the Lorenz condition as well, and $\Box^2\vec A = -\mu_0\vec J$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.1.3, eqs. (10.8)–(10.16); Problem 10.6 (part 1) · Nelson ch. 25, §25.1, eq. (25.3) (part 4)*

^thm-b11-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) By [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-2|Theorem §B11.1.2]], $\nabla\cdot\vec A' = \nabla\cdot\vec A + \nabla^2\lambda$; choosing $\lambda$ with $\nabla^2\lambda = -\nabla\cdot\vec A$, a Poisson equation solved by the integral of [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]], gives Coulomb gauge. For the Lorenz gauge put $L = \nabla\cdot\vec A + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_tV$ (Griffiths Problem 10.1); then
>
> $$
> L' = \nabla\cdot\vec A + \nabla^2\lambda + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_tV - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t^2\lambda = L + \Box^2\lambda ,
> $$
>
> so $L' = 0$ requires $\Box^2\lambda = -L$, an inhomogeneous wave equation, which the retarded integral of [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]] solves (with $\lambda$ in place of $V$ and $\varepsilon_0L$ in place of $\rho$). Adding a solution of $\Box^2\lambda = 0$ keeps $L' = 0$.
>
> (2) With $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$ the first equation of [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-1|Theorem §B11.1.1]] is Poisson's equation, solved at each $t$ as in electrostatics; the second loses its $\nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec A)$ term, and the remaining gradient term moves to the right.
>
> (3) The Lorenz condition removes the gradient term in the second equation, leaving $\Box^2\vec A = -\mu_0\vec J$; in the first, $\partial_t(\nabla\cdot\vec A) = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t^2V$, which turns $\nabla^2$ into $\Box^2$.
>
> (4) With $\rho \equiv 0$ the integral in (2) vanishes, so $V = 0$; then the Lorenz condition reads $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$, which holds, and the equation for $\vec A$ in (2) loses its last term. Charge conservation, $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$, is what makes $\rho \equiv 0$ compatible only with $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$.

> [!remark] Remark: The instantaneous potential of the Coulomb gauge
> In the Coulomb gauge $V$ follows the charge distribution *now*: move an electron in the laboratory and $V$ on the Moon changes at once. Nothing travels faster than light, because $V$ alone is not measurable. What a test charge feels is $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A$, and the Coulomb-gauge $\vec A$ contains a compensating instantaneous part, so that $\vec E$ changes only after the news has had time to arrive (Griffiths). Fields must be causal; potentials need not be (Nelson, ch. 38). The price of the simple $V$ is a complicated $\vec A$. The Lorenz gauge treats $V$ and $\vec A$ alike, with the same operator $\Box^2$, and all of electrodynamics reduces to solving inhomogeneous wave equations for given sources; the rest of this chapter uses it.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.1.3 (pp. 441–442) · Nelson ch. 38, footnote to §38.3.1*

^rem-b11-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: The Lorentz force in terms of potentials
> With the potentials, the Lorentz force on a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]; [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]; [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]]) is $\vec F = q[-\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A + \vec v\times(\nabla\times\vec A)]$ (Griffiths eq. (10.17)). With $\nabla(\vec v\cdot\vec A) = \vec v\times(\nabla\times\vec A) + (\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec A$ ($\vec v$ depends on $t$ only) and the **convective derivative** $d\vec A/dt = \partial_t\vec A + (\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec A$, the rate of change of $\vec A$ at the moving charge (Griffiths eq. (10.19)), Newton's law becomes
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\vec p + q\vec A\bigr) = -\nabla\bigl[q(V - \vec v\cdot\vec A)\bigr]
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (10.20)): the **canonical momentum** $\vec p + q\vec A$ changes under the gradient of the velocity-dependent potential energy $q(V - \vec v\cdot\vec A)$ (eqs. (10.21)–(10.22)). This is the Lagrange equation of $L = \tfrac12mv^2 - qV + q\vec v\cdot\vec A$ in Classical Mechanics ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^rem-b6-2-6|CM §B6.2, Remark: Velocity-dependent potentials]]), where a gauge transformation changes $L$ by $q\,d\lambda/dt$ and so does not change the motion ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^thm-b6-2-4|CM Theorem §B6.2.4]]). The vector potential acts like a "potential momentum" per unit charge, as $V$ is potential energy per unit charge.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.1.4, eqs. (10.17)–(10.22)*

^rem-b11-1-3

> [!example] Example §B11.1.1: Two Sets of Potentials for a Charge at Rest
> Find the fields, charges and currents for $V = 0$, $\vec A = -\dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{qt}{r^2}\,\hat r$, and transform with $\lambda = -\dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{qt}{r}$.
>
> *Fields.* $\vec E = -\partial_t\vec A = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{q}{r^2}\hat r$, and $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A = \vec 0$, since a radial field $f(r)\hat r$ has zero curl. Gauss's law gives $\rho = \varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E = q\,\delta^3(\vec r)$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]), and the Ampère–Maxwell law gives $\mu_0\vec J = \nabla\times\vec B - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t\vec E = \vec 0$: a point charge at rest at the origin.
>
> *Gauge transformation.* $\nabla\lambda = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{qt}{r^2}\hat r$ and $\partial_t\lambda = -\dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac qr$, so [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-2|Theorem §B11.1.2]] gives
>
> $$
> \vec A' = \vec A + \nabla\lambda = \vec 0, \qquad V' = V - \partial_t\lambda = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac qr ,
> $$
>
> the familiar Coulomb potential. The original pair is in neither gauge: $V = 0$, but $\nabla\cdot\vec A = -\dfrac{qt}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\nabla\cdot\dfrac{\hat r}{r^2} = -\dfrac{qt}{\varepsilon_0}\,\delta^3(\vec r) \ne 0$. The new pair is in both. A static field can thus have potentials that grow linearly in time without bound.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 10.3, 10.5 (solved here)*

## Retarded potentials

> [!definition] Definition §B11.1.2: Retarded Time; Advanced Time
> For a source point $\vec r\,'$ and a field point $\vec r$ at time $t$, with $\mathcal R = |\vec r - \vec r\,'|$, the **retarded time** and the **advanced time** are
>
> $$
> t_r = t - \frac{\mathcal R}{c}, \qquad t_a = t + \frac{\mathcal R}{c}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (10.25), (10.31)): the time at which a signal travelling at $c$ must have left $\vec r\,'$ to arrive at $\vec r$ at time $t$, and the time at which a signal leaving $\vec r$ at $t$ arrives at $\vec r\,'$. Both depend on $\vec r$, $\vec r\,'$ and $t$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.2.1, eqs. (10.25), (10.31)*

^def-b11-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.4: Retarded Potentials
> Let $\rho$ and $\vec J$ be twice continuously differentiable in time, confined to a bounded region (or falling off fast enough that the integrals below converge and surface terms at infinity vanish), and let them obey the continuity equation $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]). Then the **retarded potentials**
>
> $$
> V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\rho(\vec r\,', t_r)}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau', \qquad \vec A(\vec r, t) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vec J(\vec r\,', t_r)}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau'
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (10.26)) solve the inhomogeneous wave equations $\Box^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$, $\Box^2\vec A = -\mu_0\vec J$ and satisfy the Lorenz condition. Hence (by [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]] (3)) the fields they give satisfy all of Maxwell's equations. For time-independent sources they are the static potentials (Griffiths eq. (10.24)). The **advanced potentials**, the same integrals with $t_a$ in place of $t_r$ (Griffiths eq. (10.30)), solve the same equations but make the potentials depend on the future of the source; causality excludes them.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.2.1, eqs. (10.24)–(10.31); Problem 10.10 (Lorenz condition)*

^thm-b11-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The integrand depends on $\vec r$ explicitly through $1/\mathcal R$ and implicitly through $t_r = t - \mathcal R/c$. With $\nabla\mathcal R = \hat{\mathcal R}$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]) and the chain rule, writing a dot for $\partial/\partial t$ (note $\partial/\partial t_r = \partial/\partial t$ at fixed $\vec r, \vec r\,'$),
>
> $$
> \nabla\rho(\vec r\,', t_r) = \dot\rho\,\nabla t_r = -\frac1c\,\dot\rho\,\hat{\mathcal R}, \qquad \nabla\frac1{\mathcal R} = -\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2} ,
> $$
>
> so (Griffiths eqs. (10.27)–(10.29))
>
> $$
> \nabla V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\Bigl[-\frac{\dot\rho}{c}\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R} - \rho\,\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\Bigr]d\tau' .
> $$
>
> Take the divergence. Using $\nabla\dot\rho = -\tfrac1c\ddot\rho\,\hat{\mathcal R}$, $\nabla\cdot(\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R) = 1/\mathcal R^2$ (from $\nabla\cdot\vec{\mathcal R} = 3$ and $\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\nabla\mathcal R^{-2} = -2/\mathcal R^2$) and $\nabla\cdot(\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2) = 4\pi\delta^3(\vec{\mathcal R})$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]),
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\Bigl(\dot\rho\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R}\Bigr) = -\frac{\ddot\rho}{c\,\mathcal R} + \frac{\dot\rho}{\mathcal R^2}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\Bigl(\rho\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\Bigr) = -\frac{\dot\rho}{c\,\mathcal R^2} + 4\pi\rho\,\delta^3(\vec{\mathcal R}) .
> $$
>
> The $\dot\rho/\mathcal R^2$ terms cancel, and
>
> $$
> \nabla^2V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\Bigl[\frac{\ddot\rho}{c^2\mathcal R} - 4\pi\rho\,\delta^3(\vec{\mathcal R})\Bigr]d\tau' = \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial t^2} - \frac{\rho(\vec r, t)}{\varepsilon_0} ,
> $$
>
> which is $\Box^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$. Each Cartesian component of $\vec A$ is the same integral with $\rho/\varepsilon_0 \to \mu_0J_i$.
>
> *Lorenz condition* (Griffiths Problem 10.10, solved here). Let $\nabla'$ act on $\vec r\,'$, including through $t_r$. Since $\nabla'\mathcal R = -\hat{\mathcal R}$ and $\nabla'(1/\mathcal R) = -\nabla(1/\mathcal R)$,
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec J(\vec r\,', t_r) = -\frac1c\dot{\vec J}\cdot\hat{\mathcal R}, \qquad \nabla'\cdot\vec J(\vec r\,', t_r) = (\nabla'\cdot\vec J)_{t_r\text{ fixed}} + \frac1c\dot{\vec J}\cdot\hat{\mathcal R} = -\dot\rho + \frac1c\dot{\vec J}\cdot\hat{\mathcal R}
> $$
>
> by continuity. Hence
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\frac{\vec J}{\mathcal R} = \frac{\nabla\cdot\vec J}{\mathcal R} - \vec J\cdot\nabla'\frac1{\mathcal R} = \frac{\nabla\cdot\vec J + \nabla'\cdot\vec J}{\mathcal R} - \nabla'\cdot\frac{\vec J}{\mathcal R} = -\frac{\dot\rho(\vec r\,', t_r)}{\mathcal R} - \nabla'\cdot\frac{\vec J}{\mathcal R} .
> $$
>
> Integrated over $\vec r\,'$, the last term becomes a surface integral at infinity, which vanishes for a localized source ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]), so $\nabla\cdot\vec A = -\tfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\dot\rho/\mathcal R\,d\tau' = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_tV$. Nothing in either computation used the sign of $\mathcal R/c$ in $t_r$, so the advanced potentials pass the same tests.

> [!remark] Remark: Looking back along the light cone
> The retarded potentials say: to find $V$ at $(\vec r, t)$, collect each source element as it was when a light signal from it would just arrive. Writing $\rho(\vec r\,', t_r) = \int\rho(\vec r\,', t')\,\delta(t' - t + \mathcal R/c)\,dt'$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|WO Def. §B4.4.2]]) turns the solution into a superposition of point sources,
>
> $$
> V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int d\tau'\!\int dt'\;G(\vec r - \vec r\,', t - t')\,\rho(\vec r\,', t'), \qquad G(\vec{\mathcal R}, T) = \frac{\delta(T - \mathcal R/c)}{4\pi\mathcal R} ,
> $$
>
> where $G$, the **retarded Green function** of $\Box^2$, is nonzero only on the past light cone of the field event ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^def-a1-5-4|REL Def. §A1.5.4]]): effects travel at exactly $c$, in line with causality ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^rem-a1-5-4|REL §A1.5, Remark: Causality]]). Nelson derives $G$ without guessing, from Lorentz invariance, causality and dimensional analysis. For a single point source $f(t)$ at the origin the retarded solution is $f(t - r/c)/4\pi r$, the outgoing spherical wave of [[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^thm-b7-1-5|WO Theorem §B7.1.5]]; the incoming spherical wave of that theorem belongs to the advanced solution. The wave operator contains $t$ only through $\partial_t^2$, so the theory does not distinguish past from future; the arrow of time enters with the choice of retarded over advanced potentials (Griffiths). A consequence noted by Nelson: what reaches a detector depends only on the source at the retarded time, so radiation travels on autonomously after the source has stopped, as starlight from a star that no longer exists. Green functions in general belong to Mathematical Methods (planned) and to level C.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.2.1 (pp. 445–447) · Nelson ch. 25, §25.3; ch. 38, §38.3–38.4*

^rem-b11-1-4

> [!caution] Caution: Retarding the fields does not work
> Replacing $t$ by $t_r$ in Coulomb's law and the Biot–Savart law does **not** give the right fields: $\vec E(\vec r, t) \ne \tfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\rho(\vec r\,', t_r)\,\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2\,d\tau'$, and likewise for $\vec B$ (Griffiths). The simple prescription works for the potentials, because they obey wave equations with the sources as they stand; the correct fields contain extra terms ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-5|Theorem §B11.1.5]]).

> [!example] Example §B11.1.2: Switching On the Current in a Long Wire
> An infinite straight neutral wire on the $z$ axis carries $I(t) = 0$ for $t \le 0$ and $I(t) = I_0$ for $t > 0$. Find the fields.
>
> The wire is neutral, so $V = 0$ ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]] (4)). At distance $s$ from the wire, a segment at height $z$ is at $\mathcal R = \sqrt{s^2 + z^2}$ and contributes only if $t_r = t - \mathcal R/c > 0$, that is $|z| < \sqrt{(ct)^2 - s^2}$ (Griffiths eq. (10.32)). For $t < s/c$ nothing has arrived and $\vec A = \vec 0$; for $t > s/c$, by [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]],
>
> $$
> \vec A(s, t) = \frac{\mu_0I_0}{4\pi}\,\hat z\cdot2\int_0^{\sqrt{(ct)^2 - s^2}}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{s^2 + z^2}} = \frac{\mu_0I_0}{2\pi}\ln\Bigl(\frac{ct + \sqrt{(ct)^2 - s^2}}{s}\Bigr)\hat z .
> $$
>
> Differentiating, with $\sqrt{\cdot} = \sqrt{(ct)^2 - s^2}$,
>
> $$
> \vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t} = -\frac{\mu_0I_0c}{2\pi\sqrt{(ct)^2 - s^2}}\,\hat z, \qquad \vec B = -\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial s}\hat\varphi = \frac{\mu_0I_0}{2\pi s}\,\frac{ct}{\sqrt{(ct)^2 - s^2}}\,\hat\varphi ,
> $$
>
> for $s < ct$, and zero beyond the front $s = ct$. As $t \to \infty$, $\vec E \to \vec 0$ and $\vec B \to (\mu_0I_0/2\pi s)\hat\varphi$, the static field of a long wire ([[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-2|Theorem §A8.2.2]]). While the field builds up, the induced $\vec E$ points against the current, the back-emf of Lenz's law ([[§A9.2 Lenz's Law#^thm-a9-2-1|Theorem §A9.2.1]]). Both fields are infinite on the front itself; that is the price of an instantaneous switch-on (an infinite $dI/dt$), and any smooth switch-on gives finite fields. The example also shows where the quasistatic approximation ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^mod-b8-2-5|Model §B8.2.5]]) breaks down: computed quasistatically, the induced field of a long wire grows like $\ln s$ without bound ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^rem-b8-2-5|§B8.2, Remark: A small fraud, and where it shows]]), while the retarded field is zero beyond the front $s = ct$. The static, instantaneous picture holds only where the news has long arrived, $s \ll ct$ here, and $s \ll c\tau$ in general for currents that change on a time scale $\tau$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 10.2*

> [!example] Example §B11.1.3: The Field of a Current Sheet
> The plane $x = 0$ carries a uniform surface current $\vec K(t) = K(t)\hat z$, with $K = 0$ before some time $t_0$. Find the potentials and fields.
>
> The sheet is neutral: $V = 0$. At distance $x$, the ring of the sheet at radius $s$ about the foot of the perpendicular has area $2\pi s\,ds$ and is at $\mathcal R = \sqrt{x^2 + s^2}$, with $\mathcal R\,d\mathcal R = s\,ds$. By [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]], substituting $t' = t - \mathcal R/c$,
>
> $$
> \vec A(x, t) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\hat z\int_{|x|}^{\infty}\frac{K(t - \mathcal R/c)}{\mathcal R}\,2\pi\mathcal R\,d\mathcal R = \frac{\mu_0c}{2}\,\hat z\int_{-\infty}^{t - |x|/c}K(t')\,dt'
> $$
>
> (only a finite disk contributes, since $K$ vanishes before $t_0$). Hence
>
> $$
> \vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t} = -\frac{\mu_0c}{2}\,K\Bigl(t - \frac{|x|}{c}\Bigr)\hat z, \qquad \vec B = -\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x}\hat y = \frac{\mu_0}{2}\,\frac{x}{|x|}\,K\Bigl(t - \frac{|x|}{c}\Bigr)\hat y .
> $$
>
> On each side this is a plane wave travelling away from the sheet ([[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]]), with $|E| = c|B|$ and $\vec E\times\vec B$ pointing away from the sheet; $\vec B$ jumps by $\mu_0\vec K\times\hat x$ across it, the boundary condition for a surface current ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-6|Theorem §B8.4.6]]).
>
> - *Griffiths' Example 10.1.* For $K(t) = kt$ ($t > 0$), $\vec A = \tfrac{\mu_0ck}{2}\cdot\tfrac12\bigl(t - |x|/c\bigr)^2\hat z = \tfrac{\mu_0k}{4c}(ct - |x|)^2\hat z$ for $|x| < ct$ and zero beyond: exactly the potentials that Griffiths gives and then traces back to a surface current $\vec K = kt\,\hat z$. They are its retarded potentials, and $\vec E = -\tfrac{\mu_0k}{2}(ct - |x|)\hat z$.
> - *A sheet of moving charges.* If the current is carried by $\eta$ charges $q$ per unit area (Schwartz writes $\sigma$; here $\sigma$ is the surface charge density, $\sigma = \eta q$) moving along the sheet with velocity $v(t)$, then $K = \eta qv$ and, with $\mu_0c/2 = 1/2\varepsilon_0c$,
>   $$
>   E_{\text{sheet}}(x, t) = -\frac{\eta q}{2\varepsilon_0c}\,v\Bigl(t - \frac{|x|}{c}\Bigr) ,
>   $$
>   along the motion: the field that the microscopic theory of the refractive index takes as given ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-6|WO Theorem §B13.1.6]]; Schwartz's eq. (17), quoted from the Feynman Lectures).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 10.1 (generalized and derived here from the retarded potentials) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16, §4, eq. (17)*

## Jefimenko's equations

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.5: Jefimenko's Equations
> Under the hypotheses of [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]], the fields of the sources $\rho$, $\vec J$ are
>
> $$
> \vec E(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\Bigl[\frac{\rho(\vec r\,', t_r)}{\mathcal R^2}\,\hat{\mathcal R} + \frac{\dot\rho(\vec r\,', t_r)}{c\,\mathcal R}\,\hat{\mathcal R} - \frac{\dot{\vec J}(\vec r\,', t_r)}{c^2\mathcal R}\Bigr]d\tau' ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \vec B(\vec r, t) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\Bigl[\frac{\vec J(\vec r\,', t_r)}{\mathcal R^2} + \frac{\dot{\vec J}(\vec r\,', t_r)}{c\,\mathcal R}\Bigr]\times\hat{\mathcal R}\,d\tau'
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (10.36), (10.38)), dots denoting time derivatives. They are the causal solutions of Maxwell's equations for given sources: the time-dependent generalizations of Coulomb's law and the Biot–Savart law, to which they reduce for static sources.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.2.2, eqs. (10.33)–(10.38)*

^thm-b11-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The idea is to differentiate the retarded potentials, remembering that $\vec r$ enters both through $1/\mathcal R$ and through $t_r$. *Electric field.* $\nabla V$ was computed in the derivation of [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]], and $\partial_t\vec A = \tfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\dot{\vec J}/\mathcal R\,d\tau'$ (Griffiths eq. (10.35)). In $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A$ write $\mu_0 = 1/\varepsilon_0c^2$; the three terms are those stated.
>
> *Magnetic field.* $\nabla\times(\vec J/\mathcal R) = (\nabla\times\vec J)/\mathcal R - \vec J\times\nabla(1/\mathcal R)$. Since $\vec J(\vec r\,', t_r)$ depends on $\vec r$ only through $t_r$, $\partial_iJ_k = \dot J_k\,\partial_it_r = -\tfrac1c\dot J_k\hat{\mathcal R}_i$, so
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\vec J = \nabla t_r\times\dot{\vec J} = \frac1c\,\dot{\vec J}\times\hat{\mathcal R}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (10.37)), while $-\vec J\times\nabla(1/\mathcal R) = \vec J\times\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2$. Integrating, $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$ is the stated formula. For static sources $\dot\rho = 0$, $\dot{\vec J} = \vec 0$ and $t_r$ drops out, leaving Coulomb's law and the Biot–Savart law.

> [!remark] Remark: Reading Jefimenko's equations
> - *Static limit.* The first terms are Coulomb's law ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|Principle §A1.2.2]]; [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]) and the Biot–Savart law ([[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]]; [[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^law-b6-2-2|Law §B6.2.2]]) with the sources taken at the retarded time. The new terms involve $\dot\rho$ and $\dot{\vec J}$ and fall off only as $1/\mathcal R$: they carry radiation ([[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation|§B11.3]]).
> - *In practice* it is usually easier to compute the retarded potentials and differentiate them than to use Jefimenko's equations directly (Griffiths). Their value is conceptual: they close the theory, giving the fields of arbitrary sources in one formula, and they show why retardation alone fails for the fields ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^cau-b11-1-2|Caution: Retarding the fields does not work]]).
> - *Why quasistatics works so well.* If $\vec J$ changes slowly enough that $\vec J(t_r) \approx \vec J(t) - (\mathcal R/c)\dot{\vec J}(t)$, then $\vec J(t_r)/\mathcal R^2 + \dot{\vec J}(t_r)/c\mathcal R \approx \vec J(t)/\mathcal R^2$: the error from ignoring retardation and the second term cancel to first order, and the Biot–Savart law holds with the current at the present time (Griffiths Problem 10.14). Likewise, for steady currents Coulomb's law holds with the charge density at the present time (Problem 10.13). The quasistatic approximation ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^mod-b8-2-5|Model §B8.2.5]]) is better than one would expect; the expansion in $\mathcal R\dot J/cJ$ also shows its limit, distances $\mathcal R \ll c\tau$, beyond which the neglected higher terms grow and the retarded potentials must be used.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.2.2 (p. 450); Problems 10.13–10.14*

> [!remark] Remark: The four-potential
> Griffiths notes that the Lorenz gauge is natural in special relativity. There $V/c$ and $\vec A$ are the components of one four-vector $A^\mu$, the Lorenz condition is the vanishing of its four-divergence, $\Box^2$ is the invariant generalization of the Laplacian, and the equations of [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]] (3) become one equation $\Box^2A^\mu = -\mu_0J^\mu$ for the four-current $J^\mu = (c\rho, \vec J)$ (Nelson ch. 38 works in this form throughout). $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ together form the field tensor. The Coulomb gauge is not Lorentz invariant, the Lorenz gauge is. This covariant formulation (Griffiths ch. 12) is in Relativity: the four-potential and its gauge transformations, [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-1|REL Def. §B4.2.1]]; in what sense $A^\mu$ is a four-vector, and why the Lorenz gauge survives a boost while the Coulomb gauge does not, [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^rem-b4-2-1|REL Remark: In what sense the potential is a four-vector]]; the field tensor, [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-2|REL Def. §B4.2.2]]; and Maxwell's equations as two tensor equations, [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-7|REL Theorem §B4.2.7]] (Relativity writes $\Box$ with the opposite sign, [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^cau-b2-2-3|REL Caution: The sign of the box]]). Deriving Maxwell's equations from a Lagrangian belongs to Electromagnetism level C (planned).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.1.3 (p. 442) · Nelson ch. 38, §38.4.1*
