How much power does an accelerated charge radiate, in which directions, and what does the loss do back to the charge? The optics notes stated Larmor's formula from Purcell's picture of kinked field lines ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|WO Theorem §B13.3.1]]), and quantum mechanics level A used it qualitatively to show that a classical planetary atom must collapse ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^rem-a2-2-1|QM §A2.2, Remark: The classical planetary atom]]). Here the exact fields of a moving point charge ([[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]]) give Larmor's formula with its derivation, its angular pattern at any speed, and Liénard's relativistic generalization; the energy balance then forces a recoil on the charge, the Abraham–Lorentz radiation reaction, whose mechanism is the force of the charge's own field on itself.

## Power radiated by a point charge

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.4.1: Radiation Field of a Point Charge
> Surround the retarded position $\vec w(t_r)$ of a point charge by a sphere of radius $\mathcal R$ and evaluate the energy flux through it at $t = t_r + \mathcal R/c$, the retarded time of every point of the sphere. As $\mathcal R \to \infty$ only the acceleration field contributes ([[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^def-b11-2-2|Def. §B11.2.2]]): the radiation is carried by
>
> $$
> \vec E_{\text{rad}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\mathcal R}{(\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u)^3}\,\bigl[\vec{\mathcal R}\times(\vec u\times\vec a)\bigr], \qquad \vec S_{\text{rad}} = \frac{1}{\mu_0c}\,E_{\text{rad}}^2\,\hat{\mathcal R}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.66)–(11.67)). The velocity field carries energy along with the charge, but none of it to infinity.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.2.1, eqs. (11.62)–(11.67)*

^thm-b11-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $\vec B = \tfrac1c\hat{\mathcal R}\times\vec E$ ([[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]]),
>
> $$
> \vec S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec E\times\vec B = \frac{1}{\mu_0c}\,\vec E\times(\hat{\mathcal R}\times\vec E) = \frac{1}{\mu_0c}\bigl[E^2\hat{\mathcal R} - (\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec E)\vec E\bigr]
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (11.64)). At fixed direction $\vec E_v \propto 1/\mathcal R^2$ and $\vec E_a \propto 1/\mathcal R$, so in $E^2 = E_v^2 + 2\vec E_v\cdot\vec E_a + E_a^2$ the first two terms fall as $1/\mathcal R^4$ and $1/\mathcal R^3$. The area of the sphere grows as $\mathcal R^2$, so only $E_a^2$ survives the limit. Finally $\vec E_a \propto \vec{\mathcal R}\times(\cdots)$ is perpendicular to $\hat{\mathcal R}$, which removes the second term of $\vec S$.

> [!remark] Remark: Flies on a garbage truck
> Griffiths' image: some flies hover around a garbage truck on its rounds, others fly off and never come back. The velocity fields are the first kind, energy dragged along with the charge; the acceleration fields the second, energy that detaches and travels to infinity. Note also the change of viewpoint from [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation|§B11.3]]: there the sphere was centred on a fixed origin, here on the moving charge's retarded position.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.2.1 (p. 483) and footnote 8*

^rem-b11-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.4.2: Larmor's Formula
> A point charge $q$ instantaneously at rest at the retarded time ($\vec v = \vec 0$) with acceleration $\vec a$ has the radiation field and energy flux
>
> $$
> \vec E_{\text{rad}} = \frac{\mu_0q}{4\pi\mathcal R}\bigl[(\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec a)\hat{\mathcal R} - \vec a\bigr], \qquad \vec S_{\text{rad}} = \frac{\mu_0q^2a^2}{16\pi^2c}\Bigl(\frac{\sin^2\theta}{\mathcal R^2}\Bigr)\hat{\mathcal R} ,
> $$
>
> with $\theta$ the angle between $\hat{\mathcal R}$ and $\vec a$, and radiates the total power (**Larmor's formula**)
>
> $$
> P = \frac{\mu_0q^2a^2}{6\pi c} = \frac{q^2a^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.68)–(11.70)). No power goes along $\vec a$; the pattern is the $\sin^2\theta$ doughnut about the instantaneous acceleration. The formulas remain good approximations for $v \ll c$. This is the home of [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|WO Theorem §B13.3.1]], whose field $E_{\text{rad}} = qa\sin\Theta/4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2R$ is $|\vec E_{\text{rad}}|$ here.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.2.1, eqs. (11.68)–(11.70); Example 11.2 (the same formula from dipole radiation)*

^thm-b11-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $\vec v = \vec 0$, $\vec u = c\hat{\mathcal R}$ and $\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u = c\mathcal R$, so [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-1|Theorem §B11.4.1]] gives $\vec E_{\text{rad}} = \tfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2\mathcal R}\hat{\mathcal R}\times(\hat{\mathcal R}\times\vec a)$, which by BAC–CAB and $1/\varepsilon_0c^2 = \mu_0$ is the stated field. Its square is $\bigl(\tfrac{\mu_0q}{4\pi\mathcal R}\bigr)^2\bigl[a^2 - (\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec a)^2\bigr] = \bigl(\tfrac{\mu_0q}{4\pi\mathcal R}\bigr)^2a^2\sin^2\theta$, and $\vec S_{\text{rad}} = E_{\text{rad}}^2\hat{\mathcal R}/\mu_0c$. Integrating over the sphere, $d\vec a = \mathcal R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi\,\hat{\mathcal R}$ and $\int\sin^2\theta\,d\Omega = 8\pi/3$:
>
> $$
> P = \frac{\mu_0q^2a^2}{16\pi^2c}\cdot\frac{8\pi}{3} = \frac{\mu_0q^2a^2}{6\pi c} .
> $$
>
> Independently, [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-7|Theorem §B11.3.7]] with $\vec p = q\vec d(t)$, $\ddot{\vec p} = q\vec a$ gives the same result.

^der-b11-4-2

*Uses:* [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-1|Theorem §B11.4.1]], [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-7|Theorem §B11.3.7]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.4.3: Angular Distribution of the Radiation of a Moving Charge
> A point charge with velocity $\vec v$ and acceleration $\vec a$ at the retarded time loses energy to radiation at the rate, per unit retarded time and per unit solid angle about $\hat{\mathcal R}$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dP}{d\Omega} = \frac{q^2}{16\pi^2\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{|\hat{\mathcal R}\times(\vec u\times\vec a)|^2}{(\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u)^5}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (11.72)). In terms of $\hat n = \hat{\mathcal R}$ and $\vec\beta = \vec v/c$ this is $\dfrac{dP}{d\Omega} = \dfrac{q^2}{16\pi^2\varepsilon_0c^3}\,\dfrac{|\hat n\times((\hat n - \vec\beta)\times\vec a)|^2}{(1 - \hat n\cdot\vec\beta)^5}$. The rate $dW/dt$ at which the energy crosses the distant sphere differs from the rate $dW/dt_r$ at which it leaves the charge: $dW/dt_r = (1 - \hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v/c)\,dW/dt$ (Griffiths eq. (11.71)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.2.1, eqs. (11.71)–(11.72)*

^thm-b11-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Energy that leaves the charge during $dt_r$ crosses the sphere during $dt = (\partial t/\partial t_r)\,dt_r$, and by [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]] $\partial t_r/\partial t = \mathcal Rc/\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u$, so $dW/dt_r = (\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u/\mathcal Rc)\,dW/dt = (1 - \hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v/c)\,dW/dt$: the factor by which a stream of bullets fired from a moving car arrives faster than it leaves the gun, the Doppler factor (Griffiths). The power through the patch $\mathcal R^2d\Omega$ is $S_{\text{rad}}\mathcal R^2d\Omega$ by [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-1|Theorem §B11.4.1]]. With $\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u = \mathcal R(\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u)$, $|\vec E_{\text{rad}}| = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{|\hat{\mathcal R}\times(\vec u\times\vec a)|}{\mathcal R(\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u)^3}$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{dP}{d\Omega} = \frac{\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u}{c}\cdot\frac{1}{\mu_0c}\,E_{\text{rad}}^2\,\mathcal R^2 = \frac{q^2}{16\pi^2\varepsilon_0^2\mu_0c^2}\,\frac{|\hat{\mathcal R}\times(\vec u\times\vec a)|^2}{(\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u)^5} ,
> $$
>
> and $\varepsilon_0\mu_0c^2 = 1$. For the second form put $\vec u = c(\hat n - \vec\beta)$, so $\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u = c(1 - \hat n\cdot\vec\beta)$ and $\hat{\mathcal R}\times(\vec u\times\vec a) = c\,\hat n\times((\hat n - \vec\beta)\times\vec a)$.

^der-b11-4-3

*Uses:* [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]], [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-1|Theorem §B11.4.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Power emitted and power received
> Two different "powers" appear in the literature. $dP/d\Omega$ above, and Liénard's $P$ below, are energy lost by the charge per unit of its own (retarded) time; the flux measured by a fixed detector is per unit of the detector's time and is larger, by $1/(1 - \hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v/c)$, in the directions the charge is moving toward (Nelson computes this second quantity for synchrotron radiation and warns that many authors compute the first). Over a whole pulse both give the same energy. For $v \ll c$ the distinction disappears.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.2.1 (pp. 484–485) · Nelson ch. 44, §44.3.6 and footnote 4*

^cau-b11-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.4.4: Liénard's Formula
> The total power radiated by a point charge, per unit retarded time, is
>
> $$
> P = \frac{\mu_0q^2\gamma^6}{6\pi c}\Bigl(a^2 - \Bigl|\frac{\vec v\times\vec a}{c}\Bigr|^2\Bigr), \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (11.73)). It reduces to Larmor's formula for $v \ll c$. For $\vec a \parallel \vec v$ it is $\mu_0q^2a^2\gamma^6/6\pi c$, for $\vec a \perp \vec v$ it is $\mu_0q^2a^2\gamma^4/6\pi c$: near the speed of light the radiated power grows without bound.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.2.1, eq. (11.73) (quoted there without proof; the integration is worked out here) · Griffiths eq. (11.75), Problem 11.16 (special cases)*

^thm-b11-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Integrate the second form of [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-3|Theorem §B11.4.3]] over directions. Write $D = 1 - \hat n\cdot\vec\beta$ and $\vec w = \hat n - \vec\beta$, so that $\hat n\cdot\vec w = D$ and $|\vec w|^2 = 1 - 2\hat n\cdot\vec\beta + \beta^2 = 2D - \gamma^{-2}$. By BAC–CAB, $\hat n\times(\vec w\times\vec a) = \vec w(\hat n\cdot\vec a) - \vec aD$, and
>
> $$
> |\hat n\times(\vec w\times\vec a)|^2 = |\vec w|^2(\hat n\cdot\vec a)^2 - 2D(\hat n\cdot\vec a)(\vec w\cdot\vec a) + a^2D^2 = -\gamma^{-2}(\hat n\cdot\vec a)^2 + 2D(\hat n\cdot\vec a)(\vec\beta\cdot\vec a) + a^2D^2 ,
> $$
>
> the $2D(\hat n\cdot\vec a)^2$ terms cancelling. So the integrand is $a^2D^{-3} + 2(\vec\beta\cdot\vec a)(\hat n\cdot\vec a)D^{-4} - \gamma^{-2}(\hat n\cdot\vec a)^2D^{-5}$. Take $\vec\beta$ along $z$, $x = \cos\theta$, and $J_k(\beta) = \int D^{-k}d\Omega = 2\pi\int_{-1}^1(1 - \beta x)^{-k}dx$; then $dJ_k/d\beta = k\int xD^{-k-1}d\Omega$. Directly,
>
> $$
> J_3 = \frac\pi\beta\Bigl[\frac{1}{(1 - \beta)^2} - \frac{1}{(1 + \beta)^2}\Bigr] = 4\pi\gamma^4, \qquad J_5 = \frac{\pi}{2\beta}\Bigl[\frac{1}{(1 - \beta)^4} - \frac{1}{(1 + \beta)^4}\Bigr] = 4\pi(1 + \beta^2)\gamma^8 .
> $$
>
> Hence $\int xD^{-4}d\Omega = \tfrac13J_3' = \tfrac{16\pi}{3}\beta\gamma^6$ and $\int x^2D^{-5}d\Omega = \tfrac14\tfrac{d}{d\beta}\bigl(\tfrac{16\pi}{3}\beta\gamma^6\bigr) = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\gamma^8(1 + 5\beta^2)$, so $\int(1 - x^2)D^{-5}d\Omega = J_5 - \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\gamma^8(1 + 5\beta^2) = \tfrac{8\pi}{3}\gamma^6$. By symmetry about $z$, $\int\hat n\,D^{-4}d\Omega = \tfrac{16\pi}{3}\beta\gamma^6\hat z$ and
>
> $$
> \int n_in_j\,D^{-5}d\Omega = \frac{4\pi}{3}\gamma^6\,\delta_{ij} + 8\pi\beta^2\gamma^8\,\hat z_i\hat z_j
> $$
>
> (the transverse diagonal entries are each half of $\tfrac{8\pi}{3}\gamma^6$, the $zz$ entry is $\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\gamma^8(1 + 5\beta^2)$). With $\vec\beta\cdot\vec a = \beta a_z$,
>
> $$
> \int|\hat n\times(\vec w\times\vec a)|^2D^{-5}d\Omega = 4\pi\gamma^4a^2 + \frac{32\pi}{3}\gamma^6(\vec\beta\cdot\vec a)^2 - \frac{4\pi}{3}\gamma^4a^2 - 8\pi\gamma^6(\vec\beta\cdot\vec a)^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}\gamma^6\bigl[(1 - \beta^2)a^2 + (\vec\beta\cdot\vec a)^2\bigr] .
> $$
>
> Since $\beta^2a^2 - (\vec\beta\cdot\vec a)^2 = |\vec\beta\times\vec a|^2$, the bracket is $a^2 - |\vec v\times\vec a|^2/c^2$, and multiplying by $q^2/16\pi^2\varepsilon_0c^3$ and using $1/\varepsilon_0c^2 = \mu_0$ gives Liénard's formula. (Griffiths remarks that relativity gives it faster: $P$ is invariant, and Larmor's formula in the instantaneous rest frame can be transformed to the lab, his Problem 12.71; done in Relativity, [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion|REL §B3.3]], Connections, with the proper acceleration of [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-2|REL Theorem §B2.1.2]].)

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B11.4.1: Bremsstrahlung
> A charge moves along a straight line, so that $\vec v$ and $\vec a$ are collinear (an electron decelerating in a metal target, for instance). Find the angular distribution, the total power and the direction of strongest emission.
>
> With $\vec a \parallel \vec v$, $\vec u\times\vec a = c\,\hat{\mathcal R}\times\vec a$ and $|\hat{\mathcal R}\times(\hat{\mathcal R}\times\vec a)|^2 = a^2 - (\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec a)^2$. With $z$ along $\vec v$ and $\theta$ measured from it, [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-3|Theorem §B11.4.3]] gives
>
> $$
> \frac{dP}{d\Omega} = \frac{\mu_0q^2a^2}{16\pi^2c}\,\frac{\sin^2\theta}{(1 - \beta\cos\theta)^5}, \qquad P = \frac{\mu_0q^2a^2}{8\pi c}\int_{-1}^1\frac{1 - x^2}{(1 - \beta x)^5}\,dx = \frac{\mu_0q^2a^2\gamma^6}{6\pi c}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.74)–(11.75)), the integral being $\tfrac43\gamma^6$ (as in the derivation of [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-4|Theorem §B11.4.4]]), in agreement with Liénard. Setting the derivative of $(1 - x^2)/(1 - \beta x)^5$ to zero gives $3\beta x^2 + 2x - 5\beta = 0$, so the maximum lies at
>
> $$
> \cos\theta_{\max} = \frac{\sqrt{1 + 15\beta^2} - 1}{3\beta} :
> $$
>
> $38.2^\circ$ at $\beta = 0.5$, $13.4^\circ$ at $0.9$, $4.07^\circ$ at $0.99$. For $\beta \to 1$, with $\beta = 1 - \epsilon$, $\cos\theta_{\max} \approx 1 - \epsilon/4$, so $\theta_{\max} \approx \sqrt{(1 - \beta)/2} \approx 1/2\gamma$, and there $1 - \beta\cos\theta \approx \tfrac54\epsilon$, so the peak of $dP/d\Omega$ is about $8(4/5)^5\gamma^8 \approx 2.6\,\gamma^8$ times the peak for a charge at rest with the same acceleration. The pattern depends only on $a^2$: accelerating and braking radiate alike, always concentrated in the forward direction of motion. Electrons stopping in a target emit "braking radiation", **bremsstrahlung**; this is its classical theory.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 11.3, eqs. (11.74)–(11.75); Problem 11.15 (solved here)*

^ex-b11-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Synchrotron radiation
> If $\vec a \perp \vec v$, as for a charge circling in a magnetic field, Griffiths' Problem 11.16 gives, with $\vec v$ along $z$, $\vec a$ along $x$ and $\theta, \varphi$ the angles of $\hat{\mathcal R}$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dP}{d\Omega} = \frac{\mu_0q^2a^2}{16\pi^2c}\,\frac{(1 - \beta\cos\theta)^2 - (1 - \beta^2)\sin^2\theta\cos^2\varphi}{(1 - \beta\cos\theta)^5}, \qquad P = \frac{\mu_0q^2a^2\gamma^4}{6\pi c} ,
> $$
>
> consistent with [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-4|Theorem §B11.4.4]]. For $\beta \to 1$ the radiation forms a narrow forward cone of angular width of order $1/\gamma$ that sweeps around the orbit with the charge, like a locomotive's headlight. Nelson works out what a fixed observer in the orbital plane receives: one short pulse per revolution, at $\beta = 0.95$ lasting only $0.004$ of the period, linearly polarized; the short pulse is why synchrotron light reaches far into the X-ray range and is used for crystallography, and its polarization is how astronomers recognize it, as around the black hole at the centre of our Galaxy. On a circle of radius $R$, $a = v^2/R$, so at a given energy $\gamma mc^2$ the loss scales as $\gamma^4 \propto m^{-4}$: an electron radiates $(m_p/m_e)^4 \approx 10^{13}$ times more than a proton of the same energy, which limits electron synchrotrons.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 11.16 (statement and answer) · Nelson ch. 44, §44.1, §44.3*

^rem-b11-4-2

> [!example] Example §B11.4.2: The Collapse of the Classical Atom
> In a classical hydrogen atom the electron circles the proton at radius $r$, starting at the Bohr radius $a_0 = 5.29 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm m$. How long would it take to spiral into the nucleus?
>
> Write $r_e = e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0m_ec^2 = 2.82 \times 10^{-15}\ \mathrm m$ (the classical electron radius of [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-2|WO Theorem §B13.3.2]]). On a circular orbit $a = c^2r_e/r^2$, $v = c\sqrt{r_e/r}$ and $E = -\tfrac12m_ec^2r_e/r$. At $r = a_0$, $v/c = \sqrt{r_e/a_0} = 1/137$, so Larmor's formula ([[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-2|Theorem §B11.4.2]]) applies:
>
> $$
> P = \frac{2}{3}\,\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{a^2}{c^3} = \frac23\,\frac{m_ec^3r_e^3}{r^4} .
> $$
>
> The energy lost per revolution is a fraction $(8\pi/3)(r_e/r)^{3/2} \approx 3 \times 10^{-6}$ of $|E|$, so the orbit stays nearly circular and $dE/dt = -P$:
>
> $$
> \frac{m_ec^2r_e}{2r^2}\,\frac{dr}{dt} = -\frac23\,\frac{m_ec^3r_e^3}{r^4} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^2\,dr = -\frac43\,c\,r_e^2\,dt \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_{\text{fall}} = \frac{a_0^3}{4c\,r_e^2} = 1.6 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm s .
> $$
>
> (With Griffiths' rounder $r = 5 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm m$, $1.3 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm s$.) Since $dt = T\,dN$ with period $T = 2\pi r/v$, the electron would circle $\tfrac{1}{4\pi}(a_0/r_e)^{3/2} \approx 2 \times 10^5$ times on the way, radiating at a rising frequency. This is the quantitative content of the level-A statement that a classical atom collapses "in less than a microsecond" ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^rem-a2-2-1|QM §A2.2, Remark: The classical planetary atom]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 11.14 (solved here)*

## Radiation reaction

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.4.5: Energy Balance for the Radiation Reaction
> Suppose a nonrelativistic charge $q$ is acted on by a recoil force $\vec F_{\text{rad}}$ from its own field, and that over an interval $[t_1, t_2]$ at whose ends the state of the system is the same (in particular $\vec v$ and $\vec a$, and with them the energy in the velocity fields; for example whole periods of a periodic motion), the work done by $\vec F_{\text{rad}}$ equals minus the energy radiated according to Larmor's formula. Then
>
> $$
> \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Bigl(\vec F_{\text{rad}} - \frac{\mu_0q^2}{6\pi c}\,\dot{\vec a}\Bigr)\cdot\vec v\,dt = 0
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.78)–(11.79)). The instantaneous balance $\vec F_{\text{rad}}\cdot\vec v = -\mu_0q^2a^2/6\pi c$ (eq. (11.77)) is **not** correct, because energy is also exchanged with the velocity fields.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.2.2, eqs. (11.76)–(11.79) and footnotes 10–11*

^thm-b11-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The hypothesis is $\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\vec F_{\text{rad}}\cdot\vec v\,dt = -\tfrac{\mu_0q^2}{6\pi c}\int_{t_1}^{t_2}a^2\,dt$. Integrating by parts ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]),
>
> $$
> \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\dot{\vec v}\cdot\dot{\vec v}\,dt = \Bigl[\vec v\cdot\dot{\vec v}\Bigr]_{t_1}^{t_2} - \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\ddot{\vec v}\cdot\vec v\,dt ,
> $$
>
> and the boundary term vanishes because $\vec v$ and $\vec a$ are the same at both ends. Substituting gives the stated identity. Why only on average: the energy density is proportional to $E^2 = E_v^2 + 2\vec E_v\cdot\vec E_a + E_a^2$. Larmor's formula counts only $E_a^2$, the part that reaches infinity; the other two terms ("energy in the velocity fields") go up and down as the charge accelerates and decelerates, and they return to their initial value only if the state does. (For nonperiodic motion that requires $\vec v$ and $\vec a$ to agree over a short interval before $t_1$ and $t_2$, since the outer fields depend on the earlier motion.)

> [!model] Model §B11.4.6: Abraham–Lorentz Radiation Reaction
> A point charge $q$ of mass $m$ moving slowly ($v \ll c$) is acted on by its own field with the **Abraham–Lorentz force**
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{rad}} = \frac{\mu_0q^2}{6\pi c}\,\dot{\vec a} = m\tau\,\dot{\vec a}, \qquad \tau = \frac{\mu_0q^2}{6\pi mc}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.80), (11.82)), so that Newton's law reads $m\vec a = \vec F_{\text{ext}} + m\tau\dot{\vec a}$. For an electron $\tau = 2r_e/3c = 6.27 \times 10^{-24}\ \mathrm s$, about the time light takes to cross the classical electron radius.
>
> *Assumptions:* nonrelativistic motion; the simplest force consistent with energy conservation ([[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-5|Theorem §B11.4.5]]), which energy alone does not fix (it says nothing about components perpendicular to $\vec v$ or about instantaneous values); supported independently by the self-force of an extended charge in the point limit ([[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-7|Theorem §B11.4.7]]); reliable only when the external force changes little in a time $\tau$, so that $\vec F_{\text{rad}}$ is a small correction.
>
> *Fails:* taken literally, it has runaway and acausal solutions (caution below). Its relativistic generalization, obtained from Liénard's formula (Griffiths Problem 12.72), has the same difficulties; it belongs to level C (planned).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.2.2, eqs. (11.80)–(11.82) and footnote 12*

^mod-b11-4-6

> [!caution] Caution: Runaway solutions and preacceleration
> With no external force the Abraham–Lorentz equation $a = \tau\dot a$ has the solutions $a(t) = a_0e^{t/\tau}$ (Griffiths eq. (11.81)): a free charge would accelerate spontaneously and exponentially. Excluding them by demanding $a_0 = 0$, or more generally by forbidding the runaway after a force has acted, makes the particle start to respond *before* the force is applied, by a time of order $\tau$ (**preacceleration**, Griffiths Problem 11.19). The time is minute, but the acausality is a defect of the theory. Griffiths suggests the lesson may be that a classical point charge does not exist, or that quantum mechanics takes over before such scales matter.

> [!example] Example §B11.4.3: Radiation Damping of an Oscillator
> A charge on a spring of natural frequency $\omega_0$ is driven at frequency $\omega$. Find the damping caused by the radiation reaction.
>
> The equation of motion is $m\ddot x = -m\omega_0^2x + m\tau\dddot x + F_{\text{driving}}$. In steady oscillation at frequency $\omega$, $x = x_0\cos(\omega t + \delta)$ gives $\dddot x = -\omega^2\dot x$, so
>
> $$
> m\ddot x + m\gamma\dot x + m\omega_0^2x = F_{\text{driving}}, \qquad \gamma = \omega^2\tau
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.83)–(11.84)): the driven damped oscillator of [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-1|WO Theorem §B1.3.1]], with the same $\gamma$ convention, but with a damping force proportional to $\dddot x$ rather than $\dot x$; for sinusoidal motion any even number of extra derivatives would do. For an electron radiating at $\lambda = 500\ \mathrm{nm}$ ($\omega = 3.77 \times 10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$):
>
> $$
> \gamma = \omega^2\tau = 8.9 \times 10^7\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}, \qquad 1/\gamma = 11\ \mathrm{ns}, \qquad Q = \frac\omega\gamma = \frac{1}{\omega\tau} = 4.2 \times 10^7 .
> $$
>
> Radiation damping is the least damping any charged oscillator can have. Inserted into the scattering cross-section of a bound electron ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-2|WO Theorem §B13.3.2]]), whose resonance peak is $\sigma_{\mathrm T}(\omega_0/\gamma)^2$ with $\sigma_{\mathrm T} = \tfrac{8\pi}{3}r_e^2$, it gives with $\tau = 2r_e/3c$
>
> $$
> \sigma_{\max} = \frac{8\pi r_e^2}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{(\omega_0\tau)^2} = \frac{6\pi c^2}{\omega_0^2} = \frac{3\lambda_0^2}{2\pi} ,
> $$
>
> $1.2 \times 10^{-13}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ at $500\ \mathrm{nm}$, about $10^{15}$ times the Thomson cross-section and independent of the electron's charge. The width of the resonance, $\Delta\omega = \gamma$ ([[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-2|WO Theorem §B1.4.2]]), corresponds to $\Delta\lambda = \lambda^2\gamma/2\pi c = 2\pi c\tau = 4\pi r_e/3 = 1.2 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{nm}$, the same at every wavelength.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 11.4, eqs. (11.83)–(11.84) (the numbers, the peak cross-section and the width computed here)*

^ex-b11-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.4.7: Self-Force of a Charged Dumbbell
> Split the charge $q$ into two halves a distance $d$ apart, along $y$, moving rigidly along $x$ and instantaneously at rest at the retarded time. The net force of each half's field on the other half is
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{self}} = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0c^2}\,\frac{lc^2 - ad^2}{(l^2 + d^2)^{3/2}}\,\hat x = \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[-\frac{a(t)}{4c^2d} + \frac{\dot a(t)}{3c^3} + O(d)\Bigr]\hat x ,
> $$
>
> where $l = x(t) - x(t_r)$ (Griffiths eqs. (11.90), (11.96)). As $d \to 0$:
> 1. the first term adds the **electromagnetic mass** $\dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{q^2}{4dc^2}$ to the mechanical mass (eq. (11.97)), which is the electrostatic energy of the two halves (eq. (11.98)) divided by $c^2$;
> 2. the second term is the interaction part of the radiation reaction, $F^{\text{int}}_{\text{rad}} = \mu_0q^2\dot a/12\pi c$ (eq. (11.99)), half of Abraham–Lorentz;
> 3. adding the self-force of each half on itself gives the full Abraham–Lorentz force, $\vec F_{\text{rad}} = \mu_0q^2\dot{\vec a}/6\pi c$ (eq. (11.100)).
>
> The radiation reaction is the force of the charge on itself, a failure of Newton's third law between the parts of an extended charge.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.2.3, eqs. (11.85)–(11.100); Problem 11.20(b) (part 3)*

^thm-b11-4-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The exact force.* Field of half (2) at half (1), by [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]] with $\vec v = \vec 0$ at the retarded time: $\vec u = c\hat{\mathcal R}$, $\vec{\mathcal R} = l\hat x + d\hat y$, $\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u = c\mathcal R$, $\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec a = la$, $\mathcal R = \sqrt{l^2 + d^2}$ (eqs. (11.85)–(11.87)). By BAC–CAB the field is $\tfrac{q/2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\tfrac{\mathcal R}{(c\mathcal R)^3}[(c^2 + la)\,c\hat{\mathcal R} - c\mathcal R\,\vec a]$, whose $x$ component, with $\hat{\mathcal R}_x = l/\mathcal R$, is
>
> $$
> E_{1x} = \frac{q}{8\pi\varepsilon_0c^2\mathcal R^3}\bigl[(c^2 + la)\,l - \mathcal R^2a\bigr] = \frac{q}{8\pi\varepsilon_0c^2}\,\frac{lc^2 - ad^2}{(l^2 + d^2)^{3/2}}
> $$
>
> (eq. (11.89)). By symmetry $E_{2x} = E_{1x}$, the $y$ components cancel, and so do the magnetic forces; $\vec F_{\text{self}} = \tfrac q2(E_{1x} + E_{2x})\hat x$ is the first form.
>
> *Expansion in $d$.* With $T = t - t_r$, Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) gives $l = \tfrac12aT^2 + \tfrac16\dot aT^3 + \dots$ (eq. (11.91)), the coefficients at $t_r$, and the retarded-time condition is $(cT)^2 = l^2 + d^2$ (eq. (11.92)). Then $d = cT\sqrt{1 - (l/cT)^2} = cT - \tfrac{a^2}{8c}T^3 + O(T^4)$; inverting, $T = d/c + a^2d^3/8c^5 + O(d^4)$ (eq. (11.93)), and $l = \tfrac{a}{2c^2}d^2 + \tfrac{\dot a}{6c^3}d^3 + O(d^4)$ (eq. (11.94)). In the force, the denominator is $(cT)^3 = d^3(1 + O(d^2))$ and the numerator $lc^2 - ad^2 = -\tfrac a2d^2 + \tfrac{\dot a}{6c}d^3 + O(d^4)$, so
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{self}} = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0c^2}\Bigl[-\frac{a}{2d} + \frac{\dot a}{6c} + O(d)\Bigr]\hat x = \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[-\frac{a(t_r)}{4c^2d} + \frac{\dot a(t_r)}{12c^3} + O(d)\Bigr]\hat x
> $$
>
> (eq. (11.95)). Finally $a(t_r) = a(t) - \dot a(t)\,T + \dots = a(t) - \dot a(t)\,d/c + O(d^2)$, which turns $-a(t_r)/4c^2d$ into $-a(t)/4c^2d + \dot a/4c^3 + O(d)$; and $\tfrac14 + \tfrac1{12} = \tfrac13$ gives the second form.
>
> *Limits.* (1) Moving $-\tfrac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\tfrac{a}{4c^2d}$ to the other side of $2m_0a = F_{\text{ext}} + F_{\text{self}}$ adds $\tfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\tfrac{q^2}{4dc^2}$ to the inertia; the static energy of the pair is $\tfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\tfrac{(q/2)^2}{d}$, the same divided by $c^2$. (2) With $\tfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2} = \tfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}$, the $\dot a$ term is $\mu_0q^2\dot a/12\pi c$. (3) Let $F(q)$ be the $d$-independent part of the self-force on a charge $q$. It is quadratic in $q$, the field being proportional to $q$ and the force to $q\vec E$, and it is the interaction term plus the self-forces of the two halves: $F(q) = F^{\text{int}}(q) + 2F(q/2) = F^{\text{int}}(q) + \tfrac12F(q)$. So $F(q) = 2F^{\text{int}}(q) = \mu_0q^2\dot a/6\pi c$, without assuming the Abraham–Lorentz formula.

> [!remark] Remark: What the dumbbell does and does not settle
> - The numbers work out neatly only for this configuration. For a dumbbell moving along its axis the electromagnetic mass comes out half as large, and for a charged sphere it is $\tfrac43$ of $E/c^2$, a famous puzzle (Griffiths, footnote 17). As $d \to 0$ it diverges; only the total mass is observable, and in quantum electrodynamics the same infinity is absorbed by **mass renormalization** (footnote 18).
> - The radiation reaction exists even when $a = 0$ momentarily, since it depends on $\dot{\vec a}$; "field reaction" would be a better name (Griffiths).
> - For uniform circular motion $\dot{\vec a} = -\omega^2\vec v$, so $\vec F_{\text{rad}}\cdot\vec v = -m\tau\omega^2v^2 = -\mu_0q^2a^2/6\pi c$: here the balance with Larmor's power holds at every instant, because the velocity fields do not change. Nelson confirms it by computing the field of the circling charge at the charge itself, without any model of its structure: the work done by an agency that keeps the charge on its circle all ends up as radiation.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.2.3 and footnotes 17–18 · Nelson ch. 44, §44.4*
