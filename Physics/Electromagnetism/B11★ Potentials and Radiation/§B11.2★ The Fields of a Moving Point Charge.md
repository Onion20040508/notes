What field does a single charge produce when it moves along an arbitrary path? Level A knew only the Coulomb field of a charge at rest ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^thm-a1-3-1|Theorem §A1.3.1]]) and, for a moving charge, a magnetic field read off from the Biot–Savart law ([[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^ex-a8-1-2|Example §A8.1.2]]), with the warning that it depends on the observer ([[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^rem-a8-1-4|Remark: Whose velocity?]]). Here the retarded potentials ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]]) are applied to a point charge on a given trajectory. The result, the Liénard–Wiechert potentials and the fields derived from them, is exact at all speeds. It splits into a velocity field, which for uniform motion is a Coulomb field flattened along the motion, and an acceleration field, which falls off as $1/\mathcal R$ and is the radiation of [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge|§B11.4]].

## The retarded point

> [!definition] Definition §B11.2.1: Retarded Time, Separation and Kinematics of a Point Charge
> Let a point charge $q$ move on the trajectory $\vec w(t)$ (Griffiths eq. (10.39)). For a field point $\vec r$ at time $t$, the **retarded time** $t_r$ is defined by
>
> $$
> |\vec r - \vec w(t_r)| = c\,(t - t_r)
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (10.44)), and $\vec w(t_r)$ is the **retarded position**. The separation vector from the retarded position is $\vec{\mathcal R} = \vec r - \vec w(t_r)$ (Griffiths eq. (10.45)), with $\mathcal R = c(t - t_r)$. Velocity and acceleration are taken at the retarded time, $\vec v = \dot{\vec w}(t_r)$ and $\vec a = \ddot{\vec w}(t_r)$, and
>
> $$
> \vec u = c\,\hat{\mathcal R} - \vec v
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (10.71)). Note that $\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u = c\mathcal R - \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v = c\mathcal R\,(1 - \hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v/c)$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.3.1–10.3.2, eqs. (10.39), (10.44)–(10.45), (10.54)–(10.55), (10.71)*

^def-b11-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.2.1: The Retarded Point Is Unique
> 1. If the charge always moves slower than light, at most one point of its trajectory has a retarded time for a given field event $(\vec r, t)$.
> 2. If moreover $|\dot{\vec w}(t')| \le v_{\max} < c$ for all $t' \le t$, there is exactly one.
> 3. Then $t_r$ is a differentiable function of $\vec r$ and $t$, with
>    $$
>    \frac{\partial t_r}{\partial t} = \frac{c\,\mathcal R}{\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u} = \frac{1}{1 - \hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v/c}, \qquad \nabla t_r = -\frac{\vec{\mathcal R}}{\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u}
>    $$
>    (Griffiths eqs. (10.78), (10.68)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.3.1 (pp. 453–454), eqs. (10.68), (10.78); Problem 10.19 · Nelson ch. 38, §38.5 (existence and uniqueness for uniform motion)*

^thm-b11-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $f(t') = c(t - t') - |\vec r - \vec w(t')|$ for $t' \le t$; retarded times are the zeros of $f$. For $t_1 < t_2 \le t$, the triangle inequality gives $\bigl||\vec r - \vec w(t_1)| - |\vec r - \vec w(t_2)|\bigr| \le |\vec w(t_2) - \vec w(t_1)| < c(t_2 - t_1)$ if the speed is always below $c$, so
>
> $$
> f(t_1) - f(t_2) = c(t_2 - t_1) - \bigl(|\vec r - \vec w(t_1)| - |\vec r - \vec w(t_2)|\bigr) > 0 :
> $$
>
> $f$ is strictly decreasing and has at most one zero (Griffiths' argument: two retarded points would need an average speed $c$ toward $\vec r$). With the bound $v_{\max}$ the same estimate gives $f(t') \ge (c - v_{\max})(t - t') - |\vec r - \vec w(t)| \to +\infty$ as $t' \to -\infty$, while $f(t) = -|\vec r - \vec w(t)| \le 0$; $f$ is continuous, so it has a zero by the intermediate value theorem ([[§18 Properties of Continuous Functions#^thm-18-3|451 Thm. §18.3]]).
>
> For (3), the defining relation $g(\vec r, t, t_r) = |\vec r - \vec w(t_r)| - c(t - t_r) = 0$ has $\partial g/\partial t_r = -\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v + c > 0$, so $t_r$ is differentiable by the implicit function theorem ([[§12 The Implicit Function Theorem#^thm-12-1|452 Thm. §12.1]]). Differentiate $\mathcal R = c(t - t_r)$. In time: $\partial_t\mathcal R = \hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\partial_t\vec{\mathcal R} = -(\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v)\,\partial_tt_r$ equals $c(1 - \partial_tt_r)$, so $\partial_tt_r\,(c - \hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v) = c$. In space: $\partial_i\mathcal R_j = \delta_{ij} - v_j\,\partial_it_r$, so $\nabla\mathcal R = \hat{\mathcal R} - (\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v)\nabla t_r$, which equals $-c\nabla t_r$; hence $\nabla t_r\,(c - \hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v) = -\hat{\mathcal R}$. Multiplying numerator and denominator by $\mathcal R$ gives the stated forms.

^der-b11-2-1

*Uses:* [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^def-b11-2-1|Def. §B11.2.1]], [[§12 The Implicit Function Theorem#^thm-12-1|452 Thm. §12.1]], [[§18 Properties of Continuous Functions#^thm-18-3|451 Thm. §18.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: When there is no retarded point
> The bound in (2) matters. A charge in **hyperbolic motion**, $\vec w(t) = \sqrt{b^2 + (ct)^2}\,\hat x$ (the motion under a constant force $mc^2/b$ in special relativity), had a speed approaching $c$ in the remote past, and there are field events from which it cannot be seen at all: they lie behind a **horizon**, and the potentials there vanish (Griffiths Problem 10.17). An observer *sees* a slower-than-light particle in at most one place at a time; by contrast one can *hear* a bear growling from two places at once if it runs toward you faster than sound (Griffiths' footnote).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.3.1, Problems 10.17–10.18 and footnote 14*

## The Liénard–Wiechert potentials

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.2.2: Liénard–Wiechert Potentials
> A point charge $q$ on a trajectory that satisfies the hypotheses of [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]] (2) has the retarded potentials
>
> $$
> V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{qc}{\mathcal Rc - \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{\mathcal R\,(1 - \hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v/c)}, \qquad \vec A(\vec r, t) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{qc\,\vec v}{\mathcal Rc - \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v} = \frac{\vec v}{c^2}\,V(\vec r, t)
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (10.46)–(10.47)), with $\vec{\mathcal R}$ and $\vec v$ at the retarded time. They are not the naive $q/4\pi\varepsilon_0\mathcal R$: the factor $1/(1 - \hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v/c)$ survives in the point limit.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.3.1, eqs. (10.40)–(10.47) · Nelson ch. 38, §38.5.1 (derivation from the Green function)*

^thm-b11-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Following Nelson, write the retarded time as a delta function in an extra time integral ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^rem-b11-1-4|Remark: Looking back along the light cone]]):
>
> $$
> V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d\tau'\!\int dt'\,\frac{\rho(\vec r\,', t')}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}\,\delta\Bigl(t' - t + \frac{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}{c}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> For a point charge $\rho(\vec r\,', t') = q\,\delta^3(\vec r\,' - \vec w(t'))$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]; [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^def-b6-1-1|Def. §B6.1.1]]). Doing the $\tau'$ integral first,
>
> $$
> V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\delta(g(t'))}{|\vec r - \vec w(t')|}\,dt', \qquad g(t') = t' - t + \frac{|\vec r - \vec w(t')|}{c} .
> $$
>
> Here $g'(t') = 1 - \hat{\mathcal R}(t')\cdot\dot{\vec w}(t')/c \ge 1 - v_{\max}/c > 0$, so $g$ is increasing with its single zero at $t_r$ ([[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]]). Substituting $s = g(t')$, $dt' = ds/g'$, the sifting property ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|WO Def. §B4.4.2]]) gives
>
> $$
> V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{\mathcal R\,g'(t_r)} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{\mathcal R\,(1 - \hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v/c)} .
> $$
>
> The current density is $\vec J = \rho\dot{\vec w}$, so $\vec A$ is the same integral with an extra factor $\mu_0\varepsilon_0\vec v(t_r) = \vec v/c^2$.

^der-b11-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]], [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|WO Def. §B4.4.2]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]], [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^def-b6-1-1|Def. §B6.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why the factor survives: the approaching train
> Griffiths explains the factor geometrically. A train coming toward you looks longer than it is, because the light you receive now left the caboose earlier than the light from the engine, when the train was farther away: $L' = L/(1 - v/c)$, or $L/(1 - v\cos\theta/c)$ at an angle $\theta$ to the line of sight, while transverse dimensions are unchanged. Any integral over a source evaluated at retarded times sees such an "apparent volume" $\tau' = \tau/(1 - \hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v/c)$ (Griffiths eq. (10.43)), so $\int\rho(\vec r\,', t_r)\,d\tau' = q/(1 - \hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v/c)$ (eq. (10.42)). The factor does not depend on the size of the source, so it remains for a point charge. It has nothing to do with Lorentz contraction: it is a pure light-travel-time effect, akin to the classical Doppler factor ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^thm-a2-4-1|REL Theorem §A2.4.1]] contains it together with time dilation).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.3.1 (pp. 451–453), Figures 10.6–10.7 (described, not reproduced)*

## The fields

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.2.3: Fields of a Point Charge in Arbitrary Motion
> The fields of a point charge $q$ in arbitrary motion (slower than light) are
>
> $$
> \vec E(\vec r, t) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\mathcal R}{(\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u)^3}\Bigl[(c^2 - v^2)\,\vec u + \vec{\mathcal R}\times(\vec u\times\vec a)\Bigr], \qquad \vec B(\vec r, t) = \frac1c\,\hat{\mathcal R}\times\vec E(\vec r, t)
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (10.72)–(10.73)), all quantities on the right at the retarded time. The magnetic field is always perpendicular to $\vec E$ and to $\hat{\mathcal R}$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.3.2, eqs. (10.53)–(10.73); Problem 10.19 (the derivative of $\vec A$, worked out here)*

^thm-b11-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $\kappa = \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u = c\mathcal R - \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v$, so that $V = qc/4\pi\varepsilon_0\kappa$ and $\vec A = q\vec v/4\pi\varepsilon_0c\kappa$ ([[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-2|Theorem §B11.2.2]]). Everything depends on $(\vec r, t)$ through $t_r$ and, for $\vec{\mathcal R}$, also explicitly. With [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]] (3) and the chain rule, in components:
>
> $$
> \partial_i\mathcal R_j = \delta_{ij} - v_j\,\partial_it_r, \quad \partial_iv_j = a_j\,\partial_it_r, \quad \partial_i\mathcal R = -c\,\partial_it_r ; \qquad \partial_t\vec{\mathcal R} = -\vec v\,\partial_tt_r, \quad \partial_t\vec v = \vec a\,\partial_tt_r, \quad \partial_t\mathcal R = c(1 - \partial_tt_r) .
> $$
>
> *Derivatives of $\kappa$.* $\partial_i\kappa = c\,\partial_i\mathcal R - \partial_i(\mathcal R_jv_j) = -c^2\partial_it_r - v_i + v^2\partial_it_r - (\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec a)\partial_it_r$, and with $\nabla t_r = -\vec{\mathcal R}/\kappa$,
>
> $$
> \nabla\kappa = -\vec v + (c^2 - v^2 + \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec a)\,\frac{\vec{\mathcal R}}{\kappa}, \qquad \partial_t\kappa = c^2 - (c^2 - v^2 + \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec a)\,\frac{c\mathcal R}{\kappa} .
> $$
>
> *Potentials differentiated.* $\nabla V = -\tfrac{qc}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\nabla\kappa/\kappa^2$ and $\partial_t\vec A = \tfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c}\bigl[\vec a\,\partial_tt_r/\kappa - \vec v\,\partial_t\kappa/\kappa^2\bigr]$ give
>
> $$
> \nabla V = \frac{qc}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\kappa^3}\bigl[\kappa\vec v - (c^2 - v^2 + \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec a)\vec{\mathcal R}\bigr], \qquad \frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t} = \frac{qc}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\kappa^3}\Bigl[\kappa\Bigl(-\vec v + \frac{\mathcal R\vec a}{c}\Bigr) + \frac{\mathcal R}{c}(c^2 - v^2 + \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec a)\,\vec v\Bigr]
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (10.69)–(10.70)).
>
> *Electric field.* In $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A$ the $\kappa\vec v$ terms cancel, and with $\vec{\mathcal R} - \mathcal R\vec v/c = (\mathcal R/c)\vec u$,
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{qc}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\kappa^3}\,\frac{\mathcal R}{c}\Bigl[(c^2 - v^2 + \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec a)\,\vec u - \kappa\,\vec a\Bigr] = \frac{q\,\mathcal R}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\kappa^3}\Bigl[(c^2 - v^2)\vec u + (\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec a)\vec u - (\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u)\vec a\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> and the last two terms are $\vec{\mathcal R}\times(\vec u\times\vec a)$ by the BAC–CAB rule ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates|§B1.1]]).
>
> *Magnetic field.* By the product rule (v) of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], $\vec B = \nabla\times(V\vec v/c^2) = \tfrac{1}{c^2}\bigl[V\,\nabla\times\vec v - \vec v\times\nabla V\bigr]$, with $(\nabla\times\vec v)_k = \epsilon_{kij}a_j\partial_it_r$, that is $\nabla\times\vec v = \nabla t_r\times\vec a = -\vec{\mathcal R}\times\vec a/\kappa$. Inserting $\nabla V$,
>
> $$
> \vec B = -\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c\,\kappa^3}\,\vec{\mathcal R}\times\bigl[(\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u)\,\vec a + (c^2 - v^2 + \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec a)\,\vec v\bigr] .
> $$
>
> On the other hand $\vec{\mathcal R}\times\vec u = -\vec{\mathcal R}\times\vec v$, so $\tfrac1c\hat{\mathcal R}\times\vec E = \tfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c\kappa^3}\vec{\mathcal R}\times\bigl[-(c^2 - v^2 + \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec a)\vec v - (\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u)\vec a\bigr]$, which is the same.

*Uses:* [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]], [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-2|Theorem §B11.2.2]], [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-1|Theorem §B11.1.1]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates|§B1.1]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §B11.2.2: Velocity Field; Acceleration (Radiation) Field
> The **velocity field** (or generalized Coulomb field) and the **acceleration field** (or radiation field) of a point charge are the two parts of [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]],
>
> $$
> \vec E_v = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\mathcal R}{(\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u)^3}\,(c^2 - v^2)\,\vec u, \qquad \vec E_a = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\mathcal R}{(\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u)^3}\,\vec{\mathcal R}\times(\vec u\times\vec a) ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec B_{v,a} = \tfrac1c\hat{\mathcal R}\times\vec E_{v,a}$. At fixed direction, $\vec E_v$ falls off as $1/\mathcal R^2$ and does not involve $\vec a$; $\vec E_a$ falls off as $1/\mathcal R$ and is proportional to $\vec a$. For a charge at rest $\vec E_v$ is the Coulomb field.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.3.2 (p. 460)*

^def-b11-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: All of classical electrodynamics in one formula
> With [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]] and the Lorentz force law, the force of a charge $q$ on a charge $Q$ moving with velocity $\vec V$ is $\vec F = Q(\vec E + \vec V\times\vec B)$ with the fields above (Griffiths eq. (10.74)); by superposition this gives the force of any configuration on any test charge. This was the program announced with Coulomb's law at the start of electrostatics ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl|§B2.1]]), and the formula shows why one starts with Coulomb's law instead.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.3.2, eq. (10.74)*

^rem-b11-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: The acceleration field is the kink in the field lines
> For a charge momentarily at rest ($\vec v = \vec 0$, $\vec u = c\hat{\mathcal R}$) the acceleration field is $\vec E_a = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2\mathcal R}\,\hat{\mathcal R}\times(\hat{\mathcal R}\times\vec a)$, of magnitude $qa\sin\Theta/4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2\mathcal R$ at angle $\Theta$ to $\vec a$, along the part of $-\vec a$ transverse to the line of sight: exactly the radiation field that [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|WO Theorem §B13.3.1]] took from Purcell's picture of kinked field lines. J. J. Thomson's version of that picture (Nelson ch. 41) needs only Gauss's law and the finite speed of news: a charge that changes its velocity during a short time leaves field lines pointing away from its old, extrapolated position outside a shell expanding at $c$ and away from its new position inside it, and the lines must kink across the shell to stay unbroken. The kink is transverse, longest perpendicular to $\vec a$ and absent along it, proportional to $a$, and its field falls as $1/\mathcal R$. Here it follows from Maxwell's equations; the power it carries is [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge|§B11.4]].
>
> *Source: Nelson ch. 41, §41.2–41.3 · Griffiths eq. (11.68)*

^rem-b11-2-4

## Uniform motion

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.2.4: Potentials and Fields of a Uniformly Moving Charge
> Let $q$ move with constant velocity $\vec v$, $\vec w(t) = \vec vt$, and let $\vec R = \vec r - \vec vt$ be the vector from its **present** position to the field point, at angle $\theta$ to $\vec v$. Then
>
> $$
> V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{R\sqrt{1 - v^2\sin^2\theta/c^2}}, \qquad \vec A = \frac{\vec v}{c^2}\,V ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \vec E(\vec r, t) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1 - v^2/c^2}{(1 - v^2\sin^2\theta/c^2)^{3/2}}\,\frac{\hat R}{R^2}, \qquad \vec B = \frac{1}{c^2}\,\vec v\times\vec E
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (10.49)–(10.51), (10.75)–(10.76)). The electric field points along the line from the present position, although the news left from the retarded one. For $v \ll c$, $\vec E \approx \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{\hat R}{R^2}$ and $\vec B \approx \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{q\,\vec v\times\hat R}{R^2}$ (Griffiths eq. (10.77)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Examples 10.3–10.4, eqs. (10.48)–(10.51), (10.75)–(10.77); Problem 10.16 (solved here) · Nelson ch. 38, §38.5.2*

^thm-b11-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Retarded time.* Squaring $|\vec r - \vec vt_r| = c(t - t_r)$ gives a quadratic, $r^2 - 2\vec r\cdot\vec v\,t_r + v^2t_r^2 = c^2(t^2 - 2tt_r + t_r^2)$, with roots
>
> $$
> t_r = \frac{(c^2t - \vec r\cdot\vec v) \pm \sqrt{(c^2t - \vec r\cdot\vec v)^2 + (c^2 - v^2)(r^2 - c^2t^2)}}{c^2 - v^2}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (10.48)); for $v = 0$ they are $t \pm r/c$, so the minus sign is the retarded root. *Potentials.* With $\vec{\mathcal R} = \vec r - \vec vt_r$ and $\mathcal R = c(t - t_r)$,
>
> $$
> \mathcal R c - \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v = c^2(t - t_r) - \vec r\cdot\vec v + v^2t_r = (c^2t - \vec r\cdot\vec v) - (c^2 - v^2)t_r = \sqrt{(c^2t - \vec r\cdot\vec v)^2 + (c^2 - v^2)(r^2 - c^2t^2)} .
> $$
>
> Expanding the radicand, the $c^4t^2$ terms cancel and, with $R^2 = r^2 - 2t\,\vec r\cdot\vec v + v^2t^2$, it equals $c^2R^2 - \bigl(v^2r^2 - (\vec r\cdot\vec v)^2\bigr) = c^2R^2 - |\vec v\times\vec r|^2$. Since $\vec v\times\vec r = \vec v\times\vec R$, $|\vec v\times\vec r|^2 = v^2R^2\sin^2\theta$, and $\mathcal Rc - \vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec v = Rc\sqrt{1 - v^2\sin^2\theta/c^2}$. [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-2|Theorem §B11.2.2]] gives $V$ and $\vec A$.
>
> *Fields.* With $\vec a = \vec 0$ only the velocity field remains. Here $\mathcal R\vec u = c\vec{\mathcal R} - \mathcal R\vec v = c(\vec r - \vec vt_r) - c(t - t_r)\vec v = c\vec R$, and $\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u = Rc\sqrt{1 - v^2\sin^2\theta/c^2}$ from above, so
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{(c^2 - v^2)\,c\vec R}{R^3c^3(1 - v^2\sin^2\theta/c^2)^{3/2}} ,
> $$
>
> which is the stated field. For $\vec B$, $\hat{\mathcal R} = (\vec r - \vec vt_r)/\mathcal R = \vec R/\mathcal R + \vec v/c$; the first part is parallel to $\vec E$ and drops out of $\hat{\mathcal R}\times\vec E$, leaving $\vec B = \tfrac{1}{c^2}\vec v\times\vec E$. For $v \ll c$, drop $v^2/c^2$ and use $\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1/c^2$.

*Uses:* [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-2|Theorem §B11.2.2]], [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]]

![[ph-em-b11-2-1.svg]]
*Electric field lines of a charge in uniform motion to the right (Theorem §B11.2.4), in a plane containing the velocity, at $\beta = v/c = 0$, $0.6$ and $0.9$, computed. The lines are straight and radial from the present position; their directions are spaced so that the number of lines per unit angle is proportional to the field strength at a fixed distance. As $\beta$ grows the field collapses toward the plane perpendicular to the motion: along the motion it is weaker than Coulomb's by $1 - \beta^2$, across it stronger by $1/\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}$.*

> [!remark] Remark: The pancake, and what relativity says about it
> Along the motion ($\theta = 0, \pi$) the field is reduced by $1 - v^2/c^2 = 1/\gamma^2$ relative to a charge at rest, perpendicular to it ($\theta = \pi/2$) enhanced by $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^def-a1-4-2|REL Def. §A1.4.2]]): a fast charge carries a field flattened like a pancake, and the lines of $\vec B = \vec v\times\vec E/c^2$ circle the line of motion. Nelson obtains the same potentials and fields by transforming the Coulomb field of the charge at rest, and the result reads that way: in the rest frame the field point on the axis is at distance $\gamma R$, since $R$ is a length-contracted distance ([[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^thm-a2-2-2|REL Theorem §A2.2.2]]), and the longitudinal field component does not change, which accounts for $1/\gamma^2$; the transverse distance is unchanged ([[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^thm-a2-2-3|REL Theorem §A2.2.3]]) and the transverse field picks up a factor $\gamma$. That last rule, how $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ transform, is [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-2|REL Theorem §B4.2.2]], and Relativity rederives these fields from the Coulomb field in two lines ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^ex-b4-2-1|REL Example §B4.2.1]]); it is the quantitative form of [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^rem-a12-1-7|Remark: Electric or magnetic depends on the observer]]. These fields were first found by Heaviside (1888), before relativity.
>
> A uniformly moving charge does **not** radiate. Its fields move along with it; the Poynting flux is nonzero, but it only shifts the lump of field energy with the charge, and no energy escapes to infinity (Nelson). Radiation needs acceleration.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §10.3.2 (pp. 461–462), Figures 10.10–10.11 (described) · Nelson ch. 38, §38.5.2*

^rem-b11-2-5

> [!caution] Caution: The "Biot–Savart law for a point charge"
> The low-speed field $\vec B \approx \tfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}q\vec v\times\hat R/R^2$, which level A used for moving charges ([[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^ex-a8-1-2|Example §A8.1.2]]), is not exact: it is the $v \ll c$ limit of the uniform-motion field, with $\vec R$ from the present position. A point charge is not a steady current, and Griffiths warns against using the Biot–Savart law for it ([[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^law-b6-2-2|Law §B6.2.2]]); for an accelerating charge the field also has a radiation part ([[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^def-b11-2-2|Def. §B11.2.2]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B11.2.1: A Moving Line of Charge
> An infinite straight wire carries a uniform line charge $\lambda$ (measured in the lab), which slides along the wire at constant speed $v$. Find $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ at distance $d$.
>
> Superpose [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-4|Theorem §B11.2.4]] for the elements $\lambda\,dx$. Put the wire on the $x$ axis and the field point at distance $d$ above the origin; the element at $x$ is at present distance $R = \sqrt{x^2 + d^2}$ with $\sin\theta = d/R$. The components along the wire cancel in pairs; perpendicular to it,
>
> $$
> E_\perp = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{(1 - \beta^2)\,d}{R^3(1 - \beta^2d^2/R^2)^{3/2}}\,dx = \frac{\lambda(1 - \beta^2)\,d}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\bigl(x^2 + (1 - \beta^2)d^2\bigr)^{3/2}} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0d} ,
> $$
>
> using $\int_{-\infty}^\infty(x^2 + b^2)^{-3/2}dx = 2/b^2$. This is exactly the field of a line charge at rest, from Gauss's law ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-3|Theorem §A2.3.3]]): the pancake flattening of each element's field is compensated by the contributions of the far elements. Then $\vec B = \vec v\times\vec E/c^2$ has magnitude $\mu_0\lambda v/2\pi d = \mu_0I/2\pi d$ with $I = \lambda v$, circling the wire: the field of a long straight current ([[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-2|Theorem §A8.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 10.22 (solved here)*

^ex-b11-2-1

> [!example] Example §B11.2.2: On the Line of Motion
> A charge moves along the $x$ axis with arbitrary (not necessarily constant) velocity $v(t)$. Find the fields on the axis ahead of it and behind it.
>
> Ahead, $\hat{\mathcal R} = \hat x$, and $\vec v$, $\vec a$ are along $\hat x$, so $\vec u = (c - v)\hat x$, $\vec u\times\vec a = \vec 0$ and the acceleration field vanishes. With $\vec{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec u = \mathcal R(c - v)$, [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]] gives
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\mathcal R\,(c^2 - v^2)(c - v)}{\mathcal R^3(c - v)^3}\,\hat x = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\mathcal R^2}\,\frac{c + v}{c - v}\,\hat x, \qquad \vec B = \frac1c\,\hat x\times\vec E = \vec 0 .
> $$
>
> Behind, $\hat{\mathcal R} = -\hat x$ and $\vec u = -(c + v)\hat x$, giving $\vec E = -\dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\mathcal R^2}\dfrac{c - v}{c + v}\hat x$. Whatever the acceleration, nothing is radiated along the line of motion. For uniform motion this agrees with [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-4|Theorem §B11.2.4]]: ahead, while the light travels the distance $\mathcal R$ the charge covers $v\mathcal R/c$, so its present distance is $R = \mathcal R(1 - \beta)$, and $\tfrac{1 + \beta}{(1 - \beta)\mathcal R^2} = \tfrac{(1 + \beta)(1 - \beta)}{R^2} = \tfrac{1 - \beta^2}{R^2}$, the field at $\theta = 0$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 10.20 (solved here)*
