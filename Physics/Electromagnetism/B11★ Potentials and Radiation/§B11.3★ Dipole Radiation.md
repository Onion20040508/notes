How does an antenna send energy away for good? Level A described the dipole antenna qualitatively ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^rem-a12-2-2|Remark: Antennas]]) and assumed that a point source spreads its power over spheres ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-4|Theorem §A12.3.4]]); the optics notes quoted the power of an oscillating dipole for scattering ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|WO Theorem §B13.3.1]]). Here the retarded potentials ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]]) are evaluated for small sources: the oscillating electric dipole, the oscillating magnetic dipole (the time-dependent versions of the static dipoles of [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole|§B4.2]] and [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole|§B6.4]]) and finally any localized source, in the electric dipole approximation. Energy flow is measured with the Poynting vector ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^def-b9-1-1|Def. §B9.1.1]], [[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]).

## What radiation is

> [!definition] Definition §B11.3.1: Radiation; Radiated Power
> Let a source be localized near the origin. The power crossing a sphere of radius $r$ is $P(r, t) = \oint\vec S\cdot d\vec a = \tfrac{1}{\mu_0}\oint(\vec E\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec a$ (Griffiths eq. (11.1)). The energy crossing it at time $t_0 + r/c$ left the source at $t_0$, and the **radiated power** is
>
> $$
> P_{\text{rad}}(t_0) = \lim_{r\to\infty}P\Bigl(r, t_0 + \frac rc\Bigr)
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (11.2)). The source **radiates** if $P_{\text{rad}} \ne 0$: energy is carried away to infinity and never comes back. This needs $|\vec S| \propto 1/r^2$, that is fields falling off as $1/r$, at large distances. The region far from the source compared with the wavelength, $r \gg \lambda = 2\pi c/\omega$, is the **radiation zone**.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.1.1, eqs. (11.1)–(11.2)*

^def-b11-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Static sources do not radiate
> Static electric fields fall off as $1/r^2$ or faster, and static magnetic fields likewise, so for static sources $S \lesssim 1/r^4$ and $P \to 0$ as $r \to \infty$. Only the terms in Jefimenko's equations that contain $\dot\rho$ or $\dot{\vec J}$ fall off as $1/r$ ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-5|Theorem §B11.1.5]]), so radiation needs time-dependent sources, and for a point charge it needs acceleration ([[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^rem-b11-2-5|§B11.2, Remark: The pancake]]). Griffiths uses "radiation" in this narrow sense, radiation *to infinity*; everyday usage also calls any energy-carrying field radiation.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.1.1 (pp. 466–467) and footnote 1*

## The oscillating electric dipole

> [!model] Model §B11.3.2: Oscillating Electric Dipole
> Two small spheres a distance $d$ apart on the $z$ axis, joined by a fine wire, carry charges $\pm q(t)$ with $q(t) = q_0\cos\omega t$ (Griffiths eq. (11.3)), driven through the wire by the current $I(t) = \dot q = -q_0\omega\sin\omega t$ (eq. (11.15)). The dipole moment is
>
> $$
> \vec p(t) = p_0\cos(\omega t)\,\hat z, \qquad p_0 = q_0d
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (11.4)). The **perfect dipole** idealization keeps only what survives when
>
> 1. $d \ll r$ (eq. (11.7)): first order in $d$;
> 2. $d \ll \lambda$, that is $d \ll c/\omega$ (eq. (11.10)): the source is small compared with the wavelength, so retardation across it is negligible;
>
> and the **radiation zone** adds
>
> 3. $r \gg \lambda$, that is $r \gg c/\omega$ (eq. (11.13)).
>
> Together $d \ll \lambda \ll r$ (Griffiths' footnote 6). Nelson organizes the same approximations by three length scales (source size, wavelength, distance) and two small ratios, $\omega d/c$ (multipole parameter) and $c/\omega r$ (far-field parameter); dropping only the first gives the small-source regime, which includes the near zone.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.1.2, eqs. (11.3)–(11.15) · Nelson ch. 42, §42.2*

^mod-b11-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.3.3: Potentials of an Oscillating Electric Dipole
> Under approximations 1–2 of [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^mod-b11-3-2|Model §B11.3.2]], the retarded potentials of the oscillating dipole are
>
> $$
> V(r, \theta, t) = \frac{p_0\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\Bigl\{-\frac\omega c\sin\bigl[\omega(t - r/c)\bigr] + \frac1r\cos\bigl[\omega(t - r/c)\bigr]\Bigr\}, \qquad \vec A(r, \theta, t) = -\frac{\mu_0p_0\omega}{4\pi r}\sin\bigl[\omega(t - r/c)\bigr]\,\hat z
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.12), (11.17)). As $\omega \to 0$, $V$ becomes the static dipole potential $p_0\cos\theta/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$ ([[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]]); in the radiation zone (approximation 3)
>
> $$
> V(r, \theta, t) = -\frac{p_0\omega}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c}\Bigl(\frac{\cos\theta}{r}\Bigr)\sin\bigl[\omega(t - r/c)\bigr]
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (11.14)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.1.2, eqs. (11.5)–(11.17)*

^thm-b11-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Scalar potential.* By [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]] the two point charges give
>
> $$
> V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl\{\frac{q_0\cos[\omega(t - \mathcal R_+/c)]}{\mathcal R_+} - \frac{q_0\cos[\omega(t - \mathcal R_-/c)]}{\mathcal R_-}\Bigr\}, \qquad \mathcal R_\pm = \sqrt{r^2 \mp rd\cos\theta + (d/2)^2}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.5)–(11.6)). To first order in $d$ (approximation 1; [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), $\mathcal R_\pm \approx r(1 \mp \tfrac{d}{2r}\cos\theta)$ and $1/\mathcal R_\pm \approx \tfrac1r(1 \pm \tfrac{d}{2r}\cos\theta)$. In the cosine, $\omega(t - \mathcal R_\pm/c) \approx \omega(t - r/c) \pm \tfrac{\omega d}{2c}\cos\theta$; with approximation 2 the small angle $\tfrac{\omega d}{2c}\cos\theta$ allows $\cos\epsilon \approx 1$, $\sin\epsilon \approx \epsilon$:
>
> $$
> \cos[\omega(t - \mathcal R_\pm/c)] \approx \cos[\omega(t - r/c)] \mp \frac{\omega d}{2c}\cos\theta\,\sin[\omega(t - r/c)]
> $$
>
> (eq. (11.11)). Multiplying out and keeping first order in $d$, the zeroth-order terms cancel between the two charges and the first-order terms double, which gives $V$ with $p_0 = q_0d$. For $r \gg c/\omega$ the second term in the braces is smaller than the first by $c/\omega r$ and is dropped.
>
> *Vector potential.* The current $I(t)\hat z$ flows in the wire from $z = -d/2$ to $d/2$:
>
> $$
> \vec A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}\frac{-q_0\omega\sin[\omega(t - \mathcal R/c)]\,\hat z}{\mathcal R}\,dz
> $$
>
> (eq. (11.16)). The integral itself supplies a factor $d$, so to first order the integrand may be taken at the centre, $\mathcal R = r$; this needs approximations 1–2 but not 3.

*Uses:* [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]], [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^mod-b11-3-2|Model §B11.3.2]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.3.4: Electric Dipole Radiation
> In the radiation zone the fields of the oscillating electric dipole are
>
> $$
> \vec E = -\frac{\mu_0p_0\omega^2}{4\pi}\Bigl(\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Bigr)\cos\bigl[\omega(t - r/c)\bigr]\,\hat\theta, \qquad \vec B = -\frac{\mu_0p_0\omega^2}{4\pi c}\Bigl(\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Bigr)\cos\bigl[\omega(t - r/c)\bigr]\,\hat\varphi
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.18)–(11.19)). The Poynting vector, its time average (the intensity) and the time-averaged radiated power are
>
> $$
> \vec S = \frac{\mu_0}{c}\Bigl\{\frac{p_0\omega^2}{4\pi}\Bigl(\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Bigr)\cos\bigl[\omega(t - r/c)\bigr]\Bigr\}^2\hat r, \qquad \langle\vec S\rangle = \Bigl(\frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{32\pi^2c}\Bigr)\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}\,\hat r, \qquad \langle P\rangle = \frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{12\pi c}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.20)–(11.22)). Since $\mu_0 = 1/\varepsilon_0c^2$, $\langle P\rangle = p_0^2\omega^4/12\pi\varepsilon_0c^3$, the dipole power quoted in [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|WO Theorem §B13.3.1]], derived here from Maxwell's equations.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.1.2, eqs. (11.18)–(11.22)*

^thm-b11-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Use the radiation-zone $V$ and the $\vec A$ of [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-3|Theorem §B11.3.3]], with $\hat z = \cos\theta\,\hat r - \sin\theta\,\hat\theta$, and drop every term that falls off faster than $1/r$ (approximation 3); writing $\tau = \omega(t - r/c)$, differentiating the $r$ in $\tau$ brings down a factor $\omega/c$, while differentiating a $1/r$ or the angular factors gives an extra $1/r$. Thus
>
> $$
> \nabla V \approx \frac{p_0\omega^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2}\Bigl(\frac{\cos\theta}{r}\Bigr)\cos\tau\,\hat r, \qquad \frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t} = -\frac{\mu_0p_0\omega^2}{4\pi r}\cos\tau\,(\cos\theta\,\hat r - \sin\theta\,\hat\theta) .
> $$
>
> In $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A$, with $1/\varepsilon_0c^2 = \mu_0$, the $\hat r$ terms cancel and the $\hat\theta$ term remains. For $\vec B$, $A_r = A\cos\theta$, $A_\theta = -A\sin\theta$ and the curl in spherical coordinates ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]]) gives
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\vec A = \frac1r\Bigl[\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(rA_\theta) - \frac{\partial A_r}{\partial\theta}\Bigr]\hat\varphi = -\frac{\mu_0p_0\omega}{4\pi r}\Bigl\{\frac\omega c\sin\theta\cos\tau + \frac{\sin\theta}{r}\sin\tau\Bigr\}\hat\varphi ,
> $$
>
> whose second term is again dropped. Then $\vec S = \tfrac{1}{\mu_0}\vec E\times\vec B$, with $\hat\theta\times\hat\varphi = \hat r$. Averaging over a period, $\langle\cos^2\tau\rangle = \tfrac12$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|WO Theorem §B1.1.6]]). Finally, with $d\vec a = r^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi\,\hat r$ and $\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta = \tfrac43$,
>
> $$
> \langle P\rangle = \frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{32\pi^2c}\cdot2\pi\cdot\frac43 = \frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{12\pi c} ,
> $$
>
> the same for every $r$ in the radiation zone.

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the dipole fields
> - *A spherical wave.* The fields are monochromatic waves of frequency $\omega$ travelling radially at $c$, in phase, mutually perpendicular, transverse to $\hat r$ and with $E/B = c$, as for a plane wave ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-5|Theorem §A12.2.5]]). They are spherical waves whose amplitude falls as $1/r$, but over a region small compared with $r$ they are plane ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^mod-a12-2-1|Model §A12.2.1]]), as the surface of the Earth is flat locally. The $1/r^2$ fall of intensity assumed in [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-4|Theorem §A12.3.4]] is now derived, with an angular factor: the radiation is not isotropic.
> - *The doughnut.* $\langle S\rangle \propto \sin^2\theta$: nothing is radiated along the dipole axis, most in the equatorial plane, and half the maximum at $45^\circ$ from the axis (figure). The fields are polarized along $\hat\theta$, in the plane containing the axis and the line of sight.
> - *Near the dipole* ($r \ll \lambda$) the $1/r$ term in the braces of [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-3|Theorem §B11.3.3]] dominates and the field is the instantaneous static dipole field, which stores energy and returns it each cycle but does not radiate.
> - *The $\omega^4$.* For a fixed dipole amplitude the power grows as the fourth power of the frequency, which is why the sky is blue and the scattered light polarized (Griffiths Example 11.1). That application, with Rayleigh's law and the polarization of skylight, is worked out in [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-3|WO Theorem §B13.3.3]] and [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-4|WO Theorem §B13.3.4]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.1.2 (pp. 470–472), Figure 11.4 and Example 11.1 · Nelson ch. 42, §42.4, §42.7*

> [!definition] Definition §B11.3.2: Radiation Resistance
> A source fed by a current $I(t) = I_0\cos\omega t$ that radiates the time-averaged power $\langle P\rangle$ has **radiation resistance**
>
> $$
> R_{\text{rad}} = \frac{2\langle P\rangle}{I_0^2} ,
> $$
>
> the resistance that would turn the same average power $\tfrac12I_0^2R$ into heat ([[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-3-2|Theorem §A11.3.2]]). It adds to the ohmic resistance of the antenna in the circuit that drives it.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 11.3*

## The oscillating magnetic dipole

> [!model] Model §B11.3.5: Oscillating Magnetic Dipole
> A circular wire loop of radius $b$ in the $xy$ plane carries $I(t) = I_0\cos\omega t$ (Griffiths eq. (11.23)), so that its magnetic dipole moment is
>
> $$
> \vec m(t) = \pi b^2I(t)\,\hat z = m_0\cos(\omega t)\,\hat z, \qquad m_0 = \pi b^2I_0
> $$
>
> (eqs. (11.24)–(11.25)). The loop is uncharged, so $V = 0$. The **perfect** magnetic dipole keeps first order in $b$, with $b \ll r$ and $b \ll c/\omega$ (eqs. (11.29), (11.31)); the radiation zone is $r \gg c/\omega$ (eq. (11.34)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.1.3, eqs. (11.23)–(11.34) · Nelson ch. 25, §25.5 (the loop antenna)*

^mod-b11-3-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.3.6: Magnetic Dipole Radiation
> The retarded vector potential of the oscillating magnetic dipole is
>
> $$
> \vec A(r, \theta, t) = \frac{\mu_0m_0}{4\pi}\Bigl(\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Bigr)\Bigl\{\frac1r\cos\bigl[\omega(t - r/c)\bigr] - \frac\omega c\sin\bigl[\omega(t - r/c)\bigr]\Bigr\}\hat\varphi
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eq. (11.33)), which for $\omega \to 0$ is the static dipole potential $\mu_0m_0\sin\theta/4\pi r^2\,\hat\varphi$ ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-5|Theorem §B6.4.5]]). In the radiation zone
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{\mu_0m_0\omega^2}{4\pi c}\Bigl(\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Bigr)\cos\bigl[\omega(t - r/c)\bigr]\hat\varphi, \qquad \vec B = -\frac{\mu_0m_0\omega^2}{4\pi c^2}\Bigl(\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Bigr)\cos\bigl[\omega(t - r/c)\bigr]\hat\theta ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \langle\vec S\rangle = \Bigl(\frac{\mu_0m_0^2\omega^4}{32\pi^2c^3}\Bigr)\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}\,\hat r, \qquad \langle P\rangle = \frac{\mu_0m_0^2\omega^4}{12\pi c^3}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.35)–(11.40)). The pattern is the same doughnut as for the electric dipole, with the roles of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ exchanged. Compared with an electric dipole of the same size and current,
>
> $$
> \frac{P_{\text{magnetic}}}{P_{\text{electric}}} = \Bigl(\frac{m_0}{p_0c}\Bigr)^2 = \Bigl(\frac{\omega b}{c}\Bigr)^2 \qquad (d = \pi b,\ I_0 = q_0\omega)
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.41)–(11.42)), which is small by approximation 2.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.1.3, eqs. (11.26)–(11.42)*

^thm-b11-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By symmetry, compute $\vec A$ at a point in the $xz$ plane, $\vec r = r\sin\theta\,\hat x + r\cos\theta\,\hat z$, where it must point along $\hat y = \hat\varphi$: the $x$ components of symmetric elements cancel. With $d\vec l\,'$ at azimuth $\varphi'$ having $y$ component $b\cos\varphi'\,d\varphi'$ and $\mathcal R = \sqrt{r^2 + b^2 - 2rb\sin\theta\cos\varphi'}$ (eqs. (11.26)–(11.28)),
>
> $$
> \vec A = \frac{\mu_0I_0b}{4\pi}\,\hat y\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\cos[\omega(t - \mathcal R/c)]}{\mathcal R}\cos\varphi'\,d\varphi' .
> $$
>
> To first order in $b$: $1/\mathcal R \approx \tfrac1r(1 + \tfrac br\sin\theta\cos\varphi')$ and, using $b \ll c/\omega$, $\cos[\omega(t - \mathcal R/c)] \approx \cos\tau - \tfrac{\omega b}{c}\sin\theta\cos\varphi'\sin\tau$ with $\tau = \omega(t - r/c)$ (eqs. (11.30), (11.32)). The product, to first order, is
>
> $$
> \frac1r\Bigl\{\cos\tau + b\sin\theta\cos\varphi'\Bigl(\frac1r\cos\tau - \frac\omega c\sin\tau\Bigr)\Bigr\} .
> $$
>
> Against $\cos\varphi'$, the first term integrates to zero and the second picks up $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\varphi'\,d\varphi' = \pi$, which with $m_0 = \pi b^2I_0$ gives the stated $\vec A$. In the radiation zone keep only the $\omega/c$ term; then $\vec E = -\partial_t\vec A$ directly, and $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$, for $\vec A = A_\varphi\hat\varphi$, is $\tfrac{1}{r\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta A_\varphi)\hat r - \tfrac1r\partial_r(rA_\varphi)\hat\theta$, whose leading part at large $r$ is the $\hat\theta$ term with $\partial_r$ acting on $\tau$. The Poynting vector, average and power follow as in [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-4|Theorem §B11.3.4]] (now $\hat\varphi\times(-\hat\theta) = \hat r$), with $p_0$ replaced by $m_0/c$. For the ratio, $m_0/p_0c = \pi b^2I_0/(q_0dc) = \pi b^2\omega/(dc)$, which is $\omega b/c$ for $d = \pi b$.

*Uses:* [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^mod-b11-3-5|Model §B11.3.5]], [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]], [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-4|Theorem §B11.3.4]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why electric dipole radiation usually wins
> The factor $(\omega b/c)^2$ is the square of the quantity that approximation 2 makes small. For a source of a given size, magnetic dipole radiation is therefore much weaker than electric dipole radiation, and it shows up only when the electric dipole radiation is absent, as in a neutral current loop (Griffiths). The two sets of radiation fields are mapped into each other, up to signs, by exchanging $\vec E$ with $c\vec B$ and $p_0$ with $m_0/c$, the electric–magnetic duality of Griffiths Problems 7.64 and 11.7.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.1.3 (pp. 476–477)*

## Radiation from an arbitrary source

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.3.7: Radiation from a Localized Source (Electric Dipole Approximation)
> Let charges and currents be confined to a region of size $\ell$ about the origin, with total charge $Q$ (conserved) and dipole moment $\vec p(t) = \int\vec r\,'\rho(\vec r\,', t)\,d\tau'$. Assume $\ell \ll r$ (eq. (11.45)) and that the source changes little in the time light takes to cross it, $\ell \ll c/|\ddot\rho/\dot\rho|, c/|\dddot\rho/\dot\rho|^{1/2}, \dots$ (eq. (11.50); $\ell \ll c/\omega$ for an oscillating source). To first order in $\ell$, with $t_0 = t - r/c$,
>
> $$
> V(\vec r, t) \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\frac Qr + \frac{\hat r\cdot\vec p(t_0)}{r^2} + \frac{\hat r\cdot\dot{\vec p}(t_0)}{rc}\Bigr], \qquad \vec A(\vec r, t) \approx \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{\dot{\vec p}(t_0)}{r}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.51), (11.54)). Keeping only the $1/r$ parts of the fields (eq. (11.55)),
>
> $$
> \vec E(\vec r, t) \approx \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}\bigl[\hat r\times(\hat r\times\ddot{\vec p})\bigr], \qquad \vec B(\vec r, t) \approx -\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi rc}\bigl[\hat r\times\ddot{\vec p}\bigr], \qquad \vec S \approx \frac{\mu_0}{16\pi^2c}\,|\ddot{\vec p}|^2\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}\,\hat r ,
> $$
>
> with $\ddot{\vec p}$ at $t_0$ and $\theta$ the angle between $\hat r$ and $\ddot{\vec p}$, and the radiated power is
>
> $$
> P_{\text{rad}}(t_0) \approx \frac{\mu_0}{6\pi c}\,|\ddot{\vec p}(t_0)|^2
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (11.56)–(11.60)). The monopole part does not radiate; in this approximation only the second time derivative of the dipole moment does.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.1.4, eqs. (11.43)–(11.60); Problem 5.7 (the integral of $\vec J$)*

^thm-b11-3-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Potentials.* With $\mathcal R = |\vec r - \vec r\,'|$, to first order in $r'$: $\mathcal R \approx r(1 - \hat r\cdot\vec r\,'/r)$, $1/\mathcal R \approx \tfrac1r(1 + \hat r\cdot\vec r\,'/r)$ (eqs. (11.46)–(11.47)), so $t - \mathcal R/c \approx t_0 + \hat r\cdot\vec r\,'/c$, and a Taylor expansion in time ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) gives $\rho(\vec r\,', t - \mathcal R/c) \approx \rho(\vec r\,', t_0) + \dot\rho(\vec r\,', t_0)\,\hat r\cdot\vec r\,'/c$ (eq. (11.49)); the neglected terms are small by the second assumption. Multiplying and keeping first order in $r'$,
>
> $$
> V \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\Bigl[\int\rho\,d\tau' + \frac{\hat r}{r}\cdot\int\vec r\,'\rho\,d\tau' + \frac{\hat r}{c}\cdot\frac{d}{dt}\int\vec r\,'\rho\,d\tau'\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> which is the stated $V$; its first two terms are the static monopole and dipole terms of [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]], evaluated at $t_0$. For $\vec A$, the integral $\int\vec J\,d\tau'$ is already first order: for each component, $\int J_i\,d\tau' = \int\vec J\cdot\nabla'x_i'\,d\tau' = -\int x_i'\,\nabla'\cdot\vec J\,d\tau' = \int x_i'\dot\rho\,d\tau' = \dot p_i$, integrating by parts with no surface term and using continuity ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]]). So the zeroth-order $\mathcal R \approx r$, $t_r \approx t_0$ suffices.
>
> *Fields.* The $1/r$ parts come only from differentiating $t_0$, with $\nabla t_0 = -\hat r/c$ (eq. (11.48)): $\nabla V \approx -\tfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2}\,\tfrac{(\hat r\cdot\ddot{\vec p})\,\hat r}{r}$, $\partial_t\vec A = \tfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\tfrac{\ddot{\vec p}}{r}$, and $\nabla\times\vec A \approx \tfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}\nabla t_0\times\ddot{\vec p} = -\tfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi rc}\hat r\times\ddot{\vec p}$. With $1/\varepsilon_0c^2 = \mu_0$, $\vec E = \tfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}[(\hat r\cdot\ddot{\vec p})\hat r - \ddot{\vec p}] = \tfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}\hat r\times(\hat r\times\ddot{\vec p})$ (BAC–CAB). Both fields are perpendicular to $\hat r$, $|\vec E| = c|\vec B| = \tfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}|\ddot{\vec p}|\sin\theta$, and $\vec S = \tfrac{1}{\mu_0}\vec E\times\vec B$ is radial with the stated size. Integrating over a sphere as in [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-4|Theorem §B11.3.4]], $\int\sin^2\theta\,d\Omega = 8\pi/3$, gives $P(r, t) = \tfrac{\mu_0}{6\pi c}|\ddot{\vec p}(t - r/c)|^2$, and [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^def-b11-3-1|Def. §B11.3.1]] gives $P_{\text{rad}}$. The Coulomb field of $Q$ falls as $1/r^2$ and contributes nothing.

> [!remark] Remark: Monopoles, multipoles and selection
> - *Monopoles do not radiate*, because charge is conserved: $Q$ cannot depend on $t$. A charged sphere whose radius pulsates keeps the exterior field $Q\hat r/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$ by Gauss's law. (In acoustics, where the analogous "charge" is not conserved, monopoles do radiate: a bullfrog's croak.)
> - *Higher multipoles.* What [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-7|Theorem §B11.3.7]] does is the multipole expansion of the retarded potentials (the static one is [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]]) taken to the lowest order that radiates. If $\ddot{\vec p} = \vec 0$, the next order in $r'$ splits into magnetic dipole radiation (generalizing [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-6|Theorem §B11.3.6]]) and electric quadrupole radiation, each weaker by $(\omega\ell/c)^2$; then magnetic quadrupole and electric octopole, and so on (Griffiths).
> - *Selection.* A source with no oscillating electric dipole moment is a poor radiator, and by reciprocity a poor absorber. Nelson's example: $\mathrm O_2$ and $\mathrm N_2$ are symmetric under inversion and have no dipole moment even when vibrating, so they hardly absorb infrared, while $\mathrm H_2\mathrm O$ (a permanent dipole) and $\mathrm{CO_2}$ (which acquires a dipole moment in some of its vibrations) do; the quantitative version uses transition dipole moments (quantum mechanics, level C — [[§C9.6 The Interaction Picture and the Atom–Field Interaction#^thm-c9-6-8|QM Theorem §C9.6.8]]).
> - *A single charge.* For one charge at position $\vec d(t)$, $\vec p = q\vec d$ and $\ddot{\vec p} = q\vec a$, and the power is Larmor's formula $\mu_0q^2a^2/6\pi c$ (Griffiths Example 11.2), derived again from the point-charge fields in [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-2|Theorem §B11.4.2]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.1.4 (pp. 481–482), Example 11.2 · Nelson ch. 42, §42.5.2*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B11.3.1: Radiation Resistance of Short Antennas
> Find the radiation resistance ([[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^def-b11-3-2|Def. §B11.3.2]]) of (a) the dipole of [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^mod-b11-3-2|Model §B11.3.2]], (b) a short centre-fed antenna whose current falls linearly to zero at the tips, (c) the loop of [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^mod-b11-3-5|Model §B11.3.5]]. Write $\lambda = 2\pi c/\omega$ and $\mu_0c = 376.7\ \Omega$.
>
> (a) The current amplitude is $I_0 = q_0\omega$, so $p_0 = q_0d = I_0d/\omega$ and [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-4|Theorem §B11.3.4]] gives $\langle P\rangle = \mu_0I_0^2d^2\omega^2/12\pi c$:
>
> $$
> R_{\text{rad}} = \frac{\mu_0d^2\omega^2}{6\pi c} = \frac{2\pi}{3}\,\mu_0c\,\Bigl(\frac d\lambda\Bigr)^2 = 789\,\Bigl(\frac d\lambda\Bigr)^2\ \Omega
> $$
>
> (Griffiths rounds to $790$). For the wires of a radio, $d = 5\ \mathrm{cm}$, receiving at $1\ \mathrm{MHz}$ ($\lambda = 300\ \mathrm m$): $R_{\text{rad}} = 789 \times (1.67 \times 10^{-4})^2\ \Omega = 2.2 \times 10^{-5}\ \Omega$, utterly negligible next to the ohmic resistance of ordinary wiring. Radiation matters only for conductors comparable to the wavelength.
>
> (b) Nelson does not solve the antenna self-consistently; he assumes a current distribution compatible with charge conservation, valid for a short antenna ($d \ll \lambda$, so that the dipole approximation holds) fed with amplitude $I_0$ at the centre. In his model the current in each arm is $I_0\cos(\omega t)\,(1 - |z|/(d/2))$ for $|z| < d/2$, so that charge builds up along the wires. With $\dot{\vec p} = \int\vec J\,d\tau' = \hat z\int I\,dz$ (derivation of [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-7|Theorem §B11.3.7]]), $\dot{\vec p} = \tfrac12I_0d\cos(\omega t)\,\hat z$: half the dipole moment of (a) for the same feed current, a quarter of the power, and
>
> $$
> R_{\text{rad}} = \frac{\pi}{6}\,\mu_0c\,\Bigl(\frac d\lambda\Bigr)^2 = 197\,\Bigl(\frac d\lambda\Bigr)^2\ \Omega
> $$
>
> (this number is computed here from Nelson's dipole moment). The half-wave antennas of [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^rem-a12-2-2|Remark: Antennas]], $d = \lambda/2$, lie outside the dipole approximation: their current is a standing wave, and their radiation resistance needs the full retarded integral over the antenna (level C, planned).
>
> (c) With $m_0 = \pi b^2I_0$ and [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-6|Theorem §B11.3.6]], $\langle P\rangle = \mu_0\pi^2b^4I_0^2\omega^4/12\pi c^3$ and
>
> $$
> R_{\text{rad}} = \frac{\mu_0\pi b^4\omega^4}{6c^3} = \frac{8\pi^5}{3}\,\mu_0c\,\Bigl(\frac b\lambda\Bigr)^4 = 3.07 \times 10^5\,\Bigl(\frac b\lambda\Bigr)^4\ \Omega ,
> $$
>
> Griffiths' answer $3 \times 10^5(b/\lambda)^4\ \Omega$. For $d = \pi b$ the ratio to (a) is $4\pi^4b^4/(d^2\lambda^2) = (2\pi b/\lambda)^2 = (\omega b/c)^2$, the ratio of [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-6|Theorem §B11.3.6]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 11.3, 11.6 (solved here) · Nelson ch. 42, §42.5.1 (the linearly tapering current)*

> [!remark] Remark: Why power cords do not radiate, in dipole language
> Schwartz's explanation of power cords and coaxial cables ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^rem-b13-3-1|WO §B13.3, Remark: Why power cords do not broadcast]]) reads, in the language of this section: the two wires of a cord a distance $s$ apart carry opposite currents, so their dipole moments cancel and the leading radiation is of the next multipole order, weaker than one wire's by a factor of order $(s/\lambda)^2$, tiny at $60\ \mathrm{Hz}$ where $\lambda \approx 5000\ \mathrm{km}$. A coaxial cable cancels the outside field exactly by symmetry.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16, §3 (the multipole estimate added here from Theorem §B11.3.7)*

> [!example] Example §B11.3.2: The Rotating Dipole
> A dipole of constant magnitude rotates in the $xy$ plane, $\vec p = p_0\bigl[\cos(\omega t)\hat x + \sin(\omega t)\hat y\bigr]$: the superposition of two oscillating dipoles a quarter period apart. Find the intensity pattern and the power.
>
> Here $\ddot{\vec p} = -\omega^2\vec p$ and $|\ddot{\vec p}|^2 = \omega^4p_0^2$ at all times, so by [[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-7|Theorem §B11.3.7]] the power is constant, not only on average:
>
> $$
> P = \frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{6\pi c} ,
> $$
>
> twice that of one oscillating dipole of amplitude $p_0$. For the pattern, $|\hat r\times\ddot{\vec p}|^2 = |\ddot{\vec p}|^2 - (\hat r\cdot\ddot{\vec p})^2$ with $\hat r\cdot\vec p = p_0\sin\theta\cos(\varphi - \omega t_0)$; averaging $\cos^2$ over a period,
>
> $$
> \langle\vec S\rangle = \frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{16\pi^2c\,r^2}\Bigl(1 - \frac12\sin^2\theta\Bigr)\hat r = \frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{32\pi^2c\,r^2}\bigl(1 + \cos^2\theta\bigr)\hat r ,
> $$
>
> largest along the rotation axis (where the radiation is circularly polarized) and half as large in the plane of rotation (linearly polarized there). Power is quadratic in the fields, so superposition should fail, yet the power is the sum of the two dipoles' powers. The reason: the cross term between the $x$ and $y$ dipoles is proportional to $\cos\omega t\,\sin\omega t$, which averages to zero, and here even cancels at each instant once integrated over the sphere. The same arithmetic gives the $(1 + \cos^2\psi)/2$ pattern of unpolarized light scattered by a molecule ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-4|WO Theorem §B13.3.4]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 11.4, 11.9 (solved here)*
