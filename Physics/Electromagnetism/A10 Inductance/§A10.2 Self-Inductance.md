A current also threads its *own* circuit with magnetic flux. When the current changes, so does that flux, and Faraday's law induces an emf in the very circuit that carries it. This is self-inductance. It makes every circuit resist changes of current, and the inductor, a coil built to have a lot of it, is the circuit element that exploits this.

## Self-inductance

> [!definition] Definition §A10.2.1: Self-Inductance
> Let a coil of $N$ turns carry a current $I$, and let $\Phi_m$ be the flux through one turn produced by that current, with the normal tied to the current by the right-hand rule. The **self-inductance** $L$ is defined by
>
> $$
> N\Phi_m = LI .
> $$
>
> With this orientation $\Phi_m$ has the sign of $I$, so $L > 0$. Like $M$ it is measured in henries and depends only on the geometry of the conductor (for $N = 1$, $\Phi_m = LI$). Every arrangement of conductors has some self-inductance: a single loop, a long straight wire, a coaxial cable.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.2, eqs. (14.7)–(14.9)*

^def-a10-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.2.1: Self-Induced EMF
> When the current in a circuit of self-inductance $L$ changes, the emf induced in it, counted in the direction of the current, is
>
> $$
> \mathcal E = -L\,\frac{dI}{dt}.
> $$
>
> By Lenz's law it opposes the change of the current, not the current.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.2, eq. (14.10)*

^thm-a10-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By Faraday's law for a coil ([[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^thm-a9-1-2|Theorem §A9.1.2]]), with the positive sense along the current, $\mathcal E = -d(N\Phi_m)/dt = -d(LI)/dt = -L\,dI/dt$, since $L$ is constant for a coil of fixed shape ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^def-a10-2-1|Def. §A10.2.1]]).

^der-a10-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^thm-a9-1-2|Theorem §A9.1.2]], [[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^def-a10-2-1|Def. §A10.2.1]]

## The inductor as a circuit element

> [!definition] Definition §A10.2.2: Inductor
> An **inductor** is a circuit element made to provide self-inductance, usually a coil of wire. Its circuit symbol is a drawn coil labelled $L$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.2*

^def-a10-2-2

> [!model] Model §A10.2.2: Ideal Inductor
> An inductor of self-inductance $L$ with
> - zero resistance in its winding;
> - its magnetic flux produced only by its own current, and confined to the inductor, so that no changing flux threads the rest of the circuit.
>
> A real coil also has resistance, which is drawn as a separate resistor in series. The model fails when other circuits couple to the coil (mutual inductance, [[§A10.1 Mutual Inductance|§A10.1]]) or when its stray field threads the rest of the circuit.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.2*

^mod-a10-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.2.3: Voltage Across an Ideal Inductor
> If a current $I$ flows through an ideal inductor from terminal $a$ to terminal $b$, then
>
> $$
> V_a - V_b = L\,\frac{dI}{dt} .
> $$
>
> The potential drops across the inductor, in the direction of the current, while the current increases, and rises while it decreases. In Kirchhoff's loop rule an ideal inductor is therefore entered like any other element, with this voltage.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.2, Fig. 14.8*

^thm-a10-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Inside a zero-resistance winding the net force on a conduction charge must vanish (otherwise the current would be unlimited), so the total electric field there is zero: the electrostatic field of charges that pile up on the winding exactly cancels the induced field. Go from $a$ to $b$ along the winding. The induced field contributes the emf $\mathcal E = -L\,dI/dt$ ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-1|Theorem §A10.2.1]]), so the electrostatic field contributes $-\mathcal E$, which is $V_a - V_b$ ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]). Outside the inductor there is no changing flux (by the model), so the electrostatic potential is well defined there ([[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^thm-a9-4-2|Theorem §A9.4.2]]) and $V_a - V_b = L\,dI/dt$ is what a voltmeter across $a$ and $b$ reads.

^der-a10-2-3

![[ph-em-a10-2-1.svg]]
*Polarity of an inductor (red signs). (a) The current increases: the inductor acts like a small battery opposing it, with $a$ at the higher potential. (b) The current decreases: the polarity reverses, and the inductor pushes the current onward. With a steady current there is no voltage across an ideal inductor.*

> [!caution] Caution: An inductor is not a resistor
> - Its voltage depends on $dI/dt$, not on $I$. A large steady current through an ideal inductor gives zero voltage across it.
> - For the size of $L$ from measured quantities, use magnitudes: $L = \lvert\mathcal E\rvert/\lvert dI/dt\rvert$.

^cau-a10-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Inductors around us
> - *Traffic lights.* A coil buried under the stop line is part of a circuit; a car's metal body above it changes the coil's inductance, which signals the lights.
> - *Airport metal detectors.* A coil in the gate frame acts as transmitter and receiver; metal passing through changes the self-inductance of the circuit.
> - *Camera flashes.* A battery, two coupled inductors acting as a transformer and an oscillating switch boost a few volts to over $1000\ \mathrm V$, which a capacitor stores for the flash.

^rem-a10-2-1

## Inductance from geometry

> [!remark] Remark: Recipe for a self-inductance
> 1. Assume a current $I$ in the inductor.
> 2. Find the field $\vec B$ it produces, with Ampère's law if the symmetry allows ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-1|Theorem §A8.4.1]]).
> 3. Compute the flux $\Phi_m$ through one turn.
> 4. Then $L = N\Phi_m/I$; the current cancels.

^rem-a10-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.2.4: Inductance of a Long Solenoid
> A solenoid of length $l$, cross-sectional area $A$ and $N$ turns, much longer than its diameter, has
>
> $$
> L = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 A}{l} = \mu_0 n^2 A l , \qquad n = N/l,
> $$
>
> that is, $\mu_0 n^2$ times its volume $Al$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.2, eqs. (14.11)–(14.14)*

^thm-a10-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Ignoring end effects, the field inside is uniform, $B = \mu_0 (N/l) I$ ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]]). The flux through one turn is $\Phi_m = BA = \mu_0 NAI/l$, and $L = N\Phi_m/I = \mu_0N^2A/l$ ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^def-a10-2-1|Def. §A10.2.1]]). With $N = nl$ this is $\mu_0 n^2 Al$.

^der-a10-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]], [[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^def-a10-2-1|Def. §A10.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.2.5: Inductance of a Rectangular Toroid
> A toroid of $N$ turns with a rectangular cross-section of height $h$, inner radius $R_1$ and outer radius $R_2$ has
>
> $$
> L = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 h}{2\pi}\,\ln\frac{R_2}{R_1}.
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.2, eqs. (14.15)–(14.17)*

^thm-a10-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Ampère's law on a circle of radius $r$ inside the toroid gives $B = \mu_0NI/(2\pi r)$ ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-4|Theorem §A8.5.4]]). The field varies across the cross-section, so integrate over strips of area $h\,dr$:
>
> $$
> \Phi_m = \int_{R_1}^{R_2} \frac{\mu_0NI}{2\pi r}\,h\,dr = \frac{\mu_0NIh}{2\pi}\ln\frac{R_2}{R_1},
> $$
>
> and $L = N\Phi_m/I$ ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^def-a10-2-1|Def. §A10.2.1]]).

^der-a10-2-5

*Uses:* [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-4|Theorem §A8.5.4]], [[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^def-a10-2-1|Def. §A10.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why $N^2$
> Both factors in $N\Phi_m$ grow with $N$: more turns make a stronger field (so more flux per turn), and more turns collect that flux. Inductance therefore grows as $N^2$, and, as both results show, depends only on the coil's construction, never on the current.

^rem-a10-2-3

## Example

> [!example] Example §A10.2.1: Self-Inductance of a Coil
> An emf of $2.0\ \mathrm V$ is measured across a coil of $50$ closely wound turns while the current through it rises uniformly from $0$ to $5.0\ \mathrm A$ in $0.10\ \mathrm s$. Using magnitudes in [[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-1|Theorem §A10.2.1]],
>
> $$
> L = \frac{\lvert\mathcal E\rvert}{dI/dt} = \frac{2.0\ \mathrm V}{(5.0\ \mathrm A)/(0.10\ \mathrm s)} = 4.0\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm H ,
> $$
>
> and at $5.0\ \mathrm A$ the flux through each turn is $\Phi_m = LI/N = (4.0\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm H)(5.0\ \mathrm A)/50 = 4.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Wb}$. These are typical values for coils in everyday devices. Once the current stops changing, the emf vanishes even though the flux stays.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 14.2*

^ex-a10-2-1
