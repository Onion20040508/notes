Building up a current in an inductor takes work, because the self-induced emf opposes the rise. That work is not lost: it is stored, and it comes back when the current falls. Just as a capacitor stores energy in its electric field, an inductor stores it in its magnetic field, with an energy density $B^2/2\mu_0$ that parallels $\varepsilon_0E^2/2$.

## Energy of an inductor

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.3.1: Energy Stored in an Inductor
> An inductor of self-inductance $L$ carrying a current $I$ stores the energy
>
> $$
> U = \tfrac12 L I^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.3, eqs. (14.20)–(14.22)*

^thm-a10-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Raise the current from $0$ at time $0$ to $I$ at time $t$. At each instant the voltage across the inductor is $L\,di/dt$ ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-3|Theorem §A10.2.3]]), so the power delivered to it is $P = (L\,di/dt)\,i$ ([[§A5.4 Electrical Power#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]). The energy delivered is
>
> $$
> U = \int_0^t L\,i\,\frac{di}{dt'}\,dt' = L\int_0^{I} i\,di = \tfrac12 L I^2 ,
> $$
>
> since $\tfrac12 Li^2$ has derivative $L\,i\,di/dt$ ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]). An ideal inductor has no resistance, so none of this is dissipated: it is stored, and it depends only on the final current, not on how fast it was reached.

^der-a10-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-3|Theorem §A10.2.3]], [[§A5.4 Electrical Power#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: The current through an inductor cannot jump
> Changing $I$ suddenly would change the stored energy $\tfrac12LI^2$ in no time, which would take unlimited power and an unlimited voltage $L\,dI/dt$. That is why opening a switch in an inductive circuit can draw an arc across it ([[§A9.2 Lenz's Law#^rem-a9-2-3|Remark in §A9.2]]): the stored energy has to go somewhere.

^cau-a10-3-1

## Where the energy is

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.3.2: Magnetic Energy Density
> The energy stored in a magnetic field in vacuum is distributed with density
>
> $$
> u_m = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}
> $$
>
> (energy per unit volume), so that the energy in a region is $U = \int u_m\,dV$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.3, eqs. (14.18)–(14.19)*

^thm-a10-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> University Physics derives it for a long solenoid and states that it holds generally; the general proof belongs to level B. In a long solenoid the field $B = \mu_0 nI$ is uniform inside, zero outside ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]]), and $L = \mu_0n^2Al$ ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-4|Theorem §A10.2.4]]). By [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-1|Theorem §A10.3.1]]
>
> $$
> U = \tfrac12\bigl(\mu_0 n^2 Al\bigr)I^2 = \frac{(\mu_0 nI)^2}{2\mu_0}\,(Al) = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\times(\text{volume}),
> $$
>
> and since the field fills the volume $Al$ uniformly, the energy per unit volume is $B^2/2\mu_0$.

^der-a10-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]], [[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-4|Theorem §A10.2.4]], [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-1|Theorem §A10.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Capacitor and inductor side by side
> | | capacitor | inductor |
> |---|---|---|
> | stored energy | $\tfrac12 CV^2 = q^2/2C$ | $\tfrac12 LI^2$ |
> | where | electric field, $u_E = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ | magnetic field, $u_m = B^2/2\mu_0$ |
> | cannot jump | its voltage | its current |
>
> The capacitor results are [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]] and [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]; the two densities add to the energy density of an electromagnetic wave ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-1|Theorem §A12.3.1]]). The form $\tfrac12LI^2$ also mirrors kinetic energy $\tfrac12mv^2$ ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|CM Def. §A4.2.1]]): inductance is a kind of inertia for current, an analogy that becomes exact in the LC circuit ([[§A10.5 LC Oscillations|§A10.5]]).

^rem-a10-3-1

## Example

> [!example] Example §A10.3.1: Inductance of a Coaxial Cable from Its Energy
> Model a coaxial cable as two long, thin, concentric cylindrical shells of radii $R_1 < R_2$ carrying equal and opposite currents $I$. Ampère's law ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-1|Theorem §A8.4.1]]; the thin-shell limit of [[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^ex-a8-4-2|Example §A8.4.2]]) on a circle of radius $r$ gives $B = \mu_0I/(2\pi r)$ between the shells, and $B = 0$ inside the inner shell (no enclosed current) and outside the outer one (zero net current). So all the energy lies between the shells, with density
>
> $$
> u_m = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} = \frac{\mu_0I^2}{8\pi^2r^2}.
> $$
>
> A thin cylindrical shell of radius $r$, thickness $dr$ and length $l$ has volume $2\pi rl\,dr$, so
>
> $$
> U = \int_{R_1}^{R_2}\frac{\mu_0I^2}{8\pi^2r^2}\,2\pi rl\,dr = \frac{\mu_0I^2l}{4\pi}\ln\frac{R_2}{R_1}.
> $$
>
> Setting $U = \tfrac12LI^2$ gives the inductance per unit length
>
> $$
> \frac{L}{l} = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\ln\frac{R_2}{R_1}.
> $$
>
> Check: the flux through a strip of length $l$ between the shells is $\int_{R_1}^{R_2}B\,l\,dr = (\mu_0Il/2\pi)\ln(R_2/R_1) = LI$, the same. The energy route is often the easier one. $L$ depends only on the radii and vanishes as $R_2 \to R_1$.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 14.3*

^ex-a10-3-1

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The energy integral $\int_0^I Li\,di$ is the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]) applied to $\tfrac12Li^2$.
