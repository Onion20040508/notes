In [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^ex-a9-1-2|Example §A9.1.2]] a changing current in a solenoid induced an emf in a small coil nearby. Because a magnetic field is proportional to the current that makes it, that emf is proportional to the rate of change of the current, with a constant set by the geometry alone. This constant, the mutual inductance, describes how strongly two circuits are coupled and separates the layout of a circuit from what happens in it.

## Definition and reciprocity

> [!definition] Definition §A10.1.1: Mutual Inductance
> Let coils 1 and 2 have $N_1$ and $N_2$ turns. If a current $I_1$ in coil 1 produces a flux $\Phi_{21}$ through each turn of coil 2, the **mutual inductance** of coil 2 with respect to coil 1 is
>
> $$
> M_{21} = \frac{N_2\Phi_{21}}{I_1},
> $$
>
> and likewise $M_{12} = N_1\Phi_{12}/I_2$. Since the field, hence the flux, is proportional to the current that makes it, $M$ does not depend on the currents: like a capacitance, it is a geometric quantity, set by the shapes, sizes, numbers of turns and relative position of the coils. The SI unit is the **henry**, $1\ \mathrm H = 1\ \mathrm{V\cdot s/A} = 1\ \Omega\cdot\mathrm s$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.1, eqs. (14.1)–(14.2)*

^def-a10-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.1.1: Reciprocity
> The two mutual inductances are equal,
>
> $$
> M_{12} = M_{21} \equiv M .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.1, eq. (14.3)*

^thm-a10-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> University Physics states the result without proof; the general proof (from the Biot–Savart law) belongs to level B (Chapter B, planned). Here is a sketch of an energy argument. Raise the currents from zero to final values $I_1, I_2$ in two different orders.
> - First $I_1$, then $I_2$ with $I_1$ held fixed. While $I_2$ rises, coil 1 has the mutually induced emf $-d(N_1\Phi_{12})/dt = -M_{12}\,dI_2/dt$ ([[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^thm-a9-1-2|Theorem §A9.1.2]]), so whatever holds $I_1$ fixed must do the extra work $\int I_1 M_{12}\,dI_2 = M_{12}I_1I_2$.
> - First $I_2$, then $I_1$: by the same reasoning the extra work is $M_{21}I_1I_2$.
>
> Apart from these cross terms, each order also does the work $\tfrac12L_1I_1^2 + \tfrac12L_2I_2^2$ of building up each current against its own self-induced emf ([[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-1|Theorem §A10.3.1]]). Both orders end in the same state, the same currents with the same magnetic field, and the energy stored in that field, $\int u_m\,dV$, depends only on the state ([[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-2|Theorem §A10.3.2]]). The total work is therefore the same, so $M_{12} = M_{21}$.

^der-a10-1-1

## Mutually induced emfs

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.1.2: Mutually Induced EMF
> For two coils at fixed positions,
>
> $$
> \mathcal E_2 = -M\,\frac{dI_1}{dt}, \qquad \mathcal E_1 = -M\,\frac{dI_2}{dt}.
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.1, eqs. (14.4)–(14.5)*

^thm-a10-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $N_2\Phi_{21}$ is the total flux through coil 2 due to $I_1$, and equals $MI_1$ by [[§A10.1 Mutual Inductance#^def-a10-1-1|Def. §A10.1.1]]. By Faraday's law for a coil ([[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^thm-a9-1-2|Theorem §A9.1.2]]), $\mathcal E_2 = -d(N_2\Phi_{21})/dt = -d(MI_1)/dt = -M\,dI_1/dt$, since $M$ is constant when the geometry is fixed. The same argument with the coils exchanged, and [[§A10.1 Mutual Inductance#^thm-a10-1-1|Theorem §A10.1.1]], gives $\mathcal E_1$.

^der-a10-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A10.1 Mutual Inductance#^def-a10-1-1|Def. §A10.1.1]], [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^thm-a9-1-2|Theorem §A9.1.2]], [[§A10.1 Mutual Inductance#^thm-a10-1-1|Theorem §A10.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Only part of the emf
> $\mathcal E_1$ and $\mathcal E_2$ here are only the *mutually* induced emfs. A coil whose own current changes also has a self-induced emf ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance|§A10.2]]), and the total emf in it is the sum. And $M$ is constant only while the coils keep their shapes and positions.

^cau-a10-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Wanted and unwanted coupling
> - *Wanted:* a transformer needs a large mutual inductance between its coils ([[§A11.5 Transformers|§A11.5]]); so does a phone charging mat, whose coil induces an emf in a coil in the phone.
> - *Unwanted:* the heating coils of a clothes dryer can induce a dangerous emf in its metal case. Counter-winding the coils, so that their fields cancel, makes the mutual inductance with the case small. The same trick limits cross-talk in digital electronics, where the rapid on/off switching of one line could otherwise induce emfs large enough to flip the 1s and 0s on a neighbouring line.

^rem-a10-1-1

## Example

> [!example] Example §A10.1.1: Coil Around a Long Solenoid
> A long solenoid of length $l_1$, radius $R_1$ and $N_1$ turns is surrounded by a coil of $N_2$ turns and radius $R_2 > R_1$. With current $I_1$, the solenoid's field is $B_1 = \mu_0(N_1/l_1)I_1$ inside, along the axis, and zero outside ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]]). So the flux through each turn of the outer coil is only that through the solenoid's cross-section, $\Phi_{21} = B_1\pi R_1^2$, and
>
> $$
> M = \frac{N_2\Phi_{21}}{I_1} = \frac{\mu_0 N_1N_2\pi R_1^2}{l_1}.
> $$
>
> With $N_1 = 500$, $N_2 = 10$, $R_1 = 3.10\ \mathrm{cm}$ and $l_1 = 75.0\ \mathrm{cm}$,
>
> $$
> M = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\cdot m/A})(500)(10)\,\pi(0.0310\ \mathrm m)^2}{0.750\ \mathrm m} = 2.53\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm H,
> $$
>
> and a solenoid current changing at $200\ \mathrm{A/s}$ induces $\lvert\mathcal E_2\rvert = M\,dI_1/dt = 5.06\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm V$ in the coil. $M$ does not depend on $R_2$, because the solenoid's field is confined to its interior. Computing $M$ the other way, from the flux of the coil's field through the solenoid's turns, would be much harder; reciprocity gives that result for free.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 14.1*

^ex-a10-1-1
