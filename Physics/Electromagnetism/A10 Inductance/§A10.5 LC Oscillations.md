A capacitor stores energy in its electric field, an inductor in its magnetic field. Connect a charged capacitor to an inductor, with no resistance and no source, and the energy passes back and forth between the two fields without end: the circuit oscillates. The governing equation is that of simple harmonic motion, whose mathematics will have its home in the subject Oscillations, Waves and Optics (planned); here it is solved self-contained. The same exchange of energy between electric and magnetic fields reappears in electromagnetic waves (Chapter A12).

## The ideal LC circuit

> [!model] Model §A10.5.1: Ideal LC Circuit
> A capacitor $C$ and an ideal inductor $L$ ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^mod-a10-2-2|Model §A10.2.2]]) form a single loop with
> - zero resistance and no source of emf;
> - no energy lost in any other way.
>
> Sign conventions: $q$ is the charge on one plate of the capacitor, $i = dq/dt$ the current flowing onto that plate. The model fails for real circuits, which always have some resistance: the oscillations then die out ([[§A10.6 RLC Circuits|§A10.6]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.5*

^mod-a10-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.5.2: Equation of the LC Circuit
> In the ideal LC circuit
>
> $$
> L\frac{d^2q}{dt^2} + \frac{q}{C} = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.5–14.6, eq. (14.44) with $R = 0$*

^thm-a10-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Call the plate with charge $q$ plate $a$, the other plate $b$. The capacitor's voltage is $V_a - V_b = q/C$ ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance|§A4.1]]). The current $i$ flows through the inductor from $b$ to $a$, so $V_b - V_a = L\,di/dt$ ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-3|Theorem §A10.2.3]]). Adding the two (Kirchhoff's loop rule, [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules|§A6.3]]) gives $q/C + L\,di/dt = 0$, and $i = dq/dt$.

^der-a10-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance|§A4.1]], [[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-3|Theorem §A10.2.3]], [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules|§A6.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.5.3: Energy Is Conserved in an LC Circuit
> The total energy
>
> $$
> U = \frac{q^2}{2C} + \frac12 L i^2
> $$
>
> is constant in time. If the capacitor holds $q_0$ when $i = 0$, and the current is $I_0$ when $q = 0$, then $U = q_0^2/2C = \tfrac12LI_0^2$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.5, eqs. (14.33)–(14.36)*

^thm-a10-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The two terms are the energies of capacitor and inductor ([[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor|§A4.3]], [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-1|Theorem §A10.3.1]]). Differentiating and using $dq/dt = i$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dU}{dt} = \frac{q}{C}\,i + L\,i\,\frac{di}{dt} = i\Bigl(\frac{q}{C} + L\frac{di}{dt}\Bigr) = 0
> $$
>
> by [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^thm-a10-5-2|Theorem §A10.5.2]], so $U$ is constant ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|SVA Cor. §29.4]]). At an instant with $i = 0$ all of it is in the capacitor, at an instant with $q = 0$ all in the inductor.

^der-a10-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor|§A4.3]], [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-1|Theorem §A10.3.1]], [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^thm-a10-5-2|Theorem §A10.5.2]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|SVA Cor. §29.4]]

## The solution

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.5.4: LC Oscillations
> Every solution of the LC equation has the form
>
> $$
> q(t) = q_0\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad i(t) = -\omega q_0\sin(\omega t + \phi), \qquad \omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}},
> $$
>
> with amplitude $q_0 \ge 0$ and phase $\phi$ fixed by the charge and current at $t = 0$. The period is $T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi\sqrt{LC}$ and the largest current is $I_0 = \omega q_0$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.5, eqs. (14.39)–(14.42)*

^thm-a10-5-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *It is a solution.* Differentiating twice, $d^2q/dt^2 = -\omega^2 q_0\cos(\omega t + \phi) = -q/(LC)$, which is [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^thm-a10-5-2|Theorem §A10.5.2]]; and $i = dq/dt$ gives the current.
>
> *Any initial state can be matched.* At $t = 0$ we need $q(0) = q_0\cos\phi$ and $i(0) = -\omega q_0\sin\phi$. Take $q_0 = \sqrt{q(0)^2 + (i(0)/\omega)^2}$ and $\phi$ the angle with $\cos\phi = q(0)/q_0$, $\sin\phi = -i(0)/(\omega q_0)$.
>
> *There are no other solutions.* If $q_1$ and $q_2$ both solve the equation with the same $q(0)$ and $i(0)$, their difference $d = q_1 - q_2$ solves it too, with $d(0) = 0$ and $d'(0) = 0$. Its "energy" $d^2/2C + \tfrac12L(d')^2$ has zero derivative, by the computation of [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^der-a10-5-3|the energy derivation]], so it keeps its initial value $0$; a sum of two squares that is zero forces $d \equiv 0$.

^der-a10-5-4

*Uses:* [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^thm-a10-5-2|Theorem §A10.5.2]], [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^thm-a10-5-3|Theorem §A10.5.3]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|SVA Cor. §29.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: One cycle
> At $t = 0$ all the energy is in the capacitor's electric field. The capacitor discharges through the inductor; at $T/4$ it is empty and the current, now at its peak $I_0$, holds all the energy in the inductor's magnetic field. The inductor opposes any change of current, so the current keeps flowing and charges the capacitor the other way round; at $T/2$ the capacitor holds $-q_0$ (energy conservation fixes the magnitude), and the current is zero. The process then runs backwards, through $3T/4$, to the initial state at $T$.

^rem-a10-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: The mass on a spring
> The LC circuit is the mass–spring oscillator in electrical dress. Compare the energies, $\tfrac12Li^2 + q^2/2C$ and $\tfrac12mv^2 + \tfrac12kx^2$ ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|CM Def. §A4.2.1]], [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-2|CM Theorem §A4.4.2]]):
>
> | mass on a spring | LC circuit |
> |---|---|
> | position $x$ | charge $q$ |
> | velocity $v = dx/dt$ | current $i = dq/dt$ |
> | mass $m$ | inductance $L$ |
> | spring constant $k$ | $1/C$ |
> | $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ | $q = q_0\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, $\omega = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ |
>
> The inductor is the inertia, the capacitor the spring. A frictionless spring would oscillate forever, and so would an LC circuit without resistance.

^rem-a10-5-2

> [!caution] Caution: Amplitude, initial charge, frequency
> - $q_0$ is the amplitude. It equals the initial charge only if the current is zero at $t = 0$ ($\phi = 0$); if instead all the energy starts in the inductor, $q(0) = 0$ and $\phi = \pm\pi/2$.
> - $\omega$ is an *angular* frequency, in $\mathrm{rad/s}$; the frequency is $f = \omega/2\pi$.

^cau-a10-5-1

## Example

> [!example] Example §A10.5.1: An LC Circuit
> An LC circuit has $L = 2.0\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm H$ and $C = 8.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm F$; at $t = 0$ all its energy is in the capacitor, which holds $q_0 = 1.2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm C$. By [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^thm-a10-5-4|Theorem §A10.5.4]]:
>
> $$
> \omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2.0\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm H)(8.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm F)}} = 2.5\times10^3\ \mathrm{rad/s},
> \qquad I_0 = \omega q_0 = 3.0\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm A .
> $$
>
> The same $I_0$ follows from energy conservation, $\tfrac12LI_0^2 = q_0^2/2C$; both sides equal $9.0\ \mu\mathrm J$. The period is $T = 2\pi/\omega = 2.5\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm s$ (about $400\ \mathrm{Hz}$), so the capacitor is first fully discharged at $T/4 = 6.3\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm s$. Since $q(0) = q_0$ and $i(0) = 0$, $\phi = 0$ and
>
> $$
> q(t) = (1.2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm C)\cos\bigl[(2.5\times10^3\ \mathrm{rad/s})\,t\bigr].
> $$
>
> At most instants the energy is shared between capacitor and inductor; looking at the instants when one of them is empty is what makes the energy equation so simple.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 14.6*

^ex-a10-5-1

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Both the constancy of the energy and the uniqueness of the solution rest on [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|SVA Cor. §29.4]].
