A real LC circuit has resistance, which turns electromagnetic energy into heat at the rate $i^2R$. The oscillations of [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations|§A10.5]] then die away, and with enough resistance the circuit does not oscillate at all. Mathematically this is the damped harmonic oscillator, whose general theory has its home in [[Oscillations, Waves and Optics]] ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-2|WO Theorem §A1.5.2]]); here the equation is solved self-contained, in all three of its regimes. Circuits driven by an alternating source come in [[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC|§A11.2]].

## The equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.6.1: Equation of the Series RLC Circuit
> A charged capacitor $C$, a resistance $R$ and an ideal inductor $L$ in a single loop, without a source, obey
>
> $$
> L\frac{d^2q}{dt^2} + R\frac{dq}{dt} + \frac{q}{C} = 0, \qquad i = \frac{dq}{dt},
> $$
>
> and the energy $U = q^2/2C + \tfrac12Li^2$ decreases at the rate at which the resistor dissipates it:
>
> $$
> \frac{dU}{dt} = -i^2R .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.6, eqs. (14.43)–(14.44)*

^thm-a10-6-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With the conventions of [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^mod-a10-5-1|Model §A10.5.1]], the loop rule of [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^der-a10-5-2|the LC derivation]] gains the drop $iR$ across the resistor ([[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-2|Theorem §A6.3.2]], [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-5|Law §A5.3.5]]): $q/C + iR + L\,di/dt = 0$, which is the equation since $i = dq/dt$. Multiplying by $i$: $\dfrac{q}{C}\dfrac{dq}{dt} + Li\dfrac{di}{dt} = -i^2R$, and the left side is $dU/dt$. (University Physics runs the argument the other way, from the energy balance to the equation.)

^der-a10-6-1

*Uses:* [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^mod-a10-5-1|Model §A10.5.1]], [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^thm-a10-5-2|Theorem §A10.5.2]], [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-2|Theorem §A6.3.2]], [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-5|Law §A5.3.5]]

## Three regimes

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.6.2: Solutions of the RLC Equation
> Write $\gamma = R/2L$ and $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ (the frequency the circuit would have without resistance).
> - **Underdamped**, $R < 2\sqrt{L/C}$ (that is, $\gamma < \omega_0$): the charge oscillates with decaying amplitude,
>
> $$
> q(t) = A\,e^{-Rt/2L}\cos(\omega' t + \phi), \qquad \omega' = \sqrt{\frac{1}{LC} - \Bigl(\frac{R}{2L}\Bigr)^2} .
> $$
>
> - **Critically damped**, $R = 2\sqrt{L/C}$: $\;q(t) = (A + Bt)\,e^{-Rt/2L}$.
> - **Overdamped**, $R > 2\sqrt{L/C}$: with $\kappa = \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2} < \gamma$,
>
> $$
> q(t) = e^{-\gamma t}\bigl(A\,e^{\kappa t} + B\,e^{-\kappa t}\bigr),
> $$
>
> a sum of two decaying exponentials, with no oscillation.
>
> The constants $A, B$ (or $A, \phi$) are fixed by $q(0)$ and $i(0)$, and in every regime $q \to 0$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.6, eqs. (14.45)–(14.46) (underdamped case; the other two are named there and solved here)*

^thm-a10-6-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Strip off the decay: put $q = e^{-\gamma t}y$. Then $q' = e^{-\gamma t}(y' - \gamma y)$ and $q'' = e^{-\gamma t}(y'' - 2\gamma y' + \gamma^2 y)$. Substituting into [[§A10.6 RLC Circuits#^thm-a10-6-1|Theorem §A10.6.1]], with $R/L = 2\gamma$ and $1/(LC) = \omega_0^2$,
>
> $$
> L e^{-\gamma t}\Bigl[y'' - 2\gamma y' + \gamma^2 y + 2\gamma(y' - \gamma y) + \omega_0^2 y\Bigr] = L e^{-\gamma t}\Bigl[y'' + (\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2)\,y\Bigr] = 0 .
> $$
>
> The damping term has disappeared, and $y'' = (\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2)\,y$.
> - If $\gamma < \omega_0$: $y'' = -\omega'^2 y$ with $\omega'^2 = \omega_0^2 - \gamma^2$, the LC equation with $1/(LC)$ replaced by $\omega'^2$. By [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^thm-a10-5-4|Theorem §A10.5.4]], $y = A\cos(\omega't + \phi)$, and only such $y$.
> - If $\gamma = \omega_0$: $y'' = 0$, so $y'$ is constant and $y = A + Bt$ ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]], twice).
> - If $\gamma > \omega_0$: $y'' = \kappa^2 y$, solved by $y = Ae^{\kappa t} + Be^{-\kappa t}$ (differentiate twice to check). These are all the solutions: for any solution, $w = y' - \kappa y$ satisfies $w' = -\kappa w$, so $w = w(0)e^{-\kappa t}$ by the argument of [[§A10.4 RL Circuits#^der-a10-4-1|the RL derivation]], and $y$ is then fixed by $y(0)$ the same way.
>
> In each case two constants can be chosen to match $q(0)$ and $i(0)$, and multiplying back by $e^{-\gamma t}$ gives $q$. Since $\kappa < \gamma$, both exponents $-\gamma \pm \kappa$ in the overdamped case are negative.

^der-a10-6-2

> [!remark] Remark: The damped spring, and what is measured
> - The equation is that of a mass on a spring with a drag force $-bv$ proportional to velocity, $m\,x'' + b\,x' + kx = 0$, with $m \to L$, $b \to R$, $k \to 1/C$, $x \to q$. The resistor plays the role of linear drag (such as Stokes' drag, [[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^law-a3-10-2|CM Law §A3.10.2]]). The regimes are decided by comparing $\sqrt{k/m}$ with $b/2m$, here $1/\sqrt{LC}$ with $R/2L$.
> - Resistance lowers the frequency: $\omega' < \omega_0$, slightly for light damping.
> - The amplitude decays as $e^{-Rt/2L}$, so for light damping ($\gamma \ll \omega_0$) the energy, proportional to the amplitude squared, decays roughly as $e^{-Rt/L}$.
> - $q(t)$ can be watched directly: measure the voltage across the capacitor and multiply by $C$.
> - In terms of the quality factor $Q = \omega_0L/R$ of the driven circuit ([[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-4-3|Theorem §A11.4.3]]), $\gamma = \omega_0/2Q$. Critical damping is $Q = \tfrac12$, light damping $Q \gg 1$; then the energy decays as $e^{-\omega_0t/Q}$ and loses the fraction $2\pi/Q$ per cycle. A sharp resonance and a long ringing are the same property.

^rem-a10-6-1

> [!caution] Caution: The amplitude is not exactly $q_0$
> University Physics writes the underdamped solution as $q_0e^{-Rt/2L}\cos(\omega't + \phi)$. For a capacitor that starts with charge $q_0$ and no current, $i(0) = 0$ requires $\tan\phi = -\gamma/\omega'$, and then $q(0) = A\cos\phi = q_0$ gives $A = q_0\,\omega_0/\omega'$, slightly larger than $q_0$. The difference is negligible for light damping.

^cau-a10-6-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A10.6.1: Which Regime, and Where Does the Energy Go?
> An RLC circuit has $L = 5.0\ \mathrm{mH}$, $C = 6.0\ \mu\mathrm F$ and $R = 200\ \Omega$, and starts with $3.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm C$ on the capacitor and no current.
> - The critical resistance is $2\sqrt{L/C} = 2\sqrt{(5.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm H)/(6.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm F)} = 58\ \Omega$. Since $R = 200\ \Omega$ is larger, the circuit is **overdamped**: no oscillation.
> - Here $\gamma = R/2L = 2.0\times10^4\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\omega_0 = 5.8\times10^3\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, so $\kappa = 1.9\times10^4\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The slower exponential decays at the rate $\gamma - \kappa \approx 8.5\times10^2\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$: the charge drains away over about a millisecond.
> - By [[§A10.6 RLC Circuits#^thm-a10-6-1|Theorem §A10.6.1]] the resistor dissipates all the initial energy by the time the charge is gone: $q_0^2/2C = (3.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm C)^2/(2\times6.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm F) = 0.75\ \mathrm J$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.6 (data of its Check Your Understanding 14.11)*

^ex-a10-6-1

> [!example] Example §A10.6.2: Adding Resistance to an LC Circuit
> Take the circuit of [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^ex-a10-5-1|Example §A10.5.1]] ($L = 2.0\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm H$, $C = 8.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm F$, $\omega_0 = 2.5\times10^3\ \mathrm{rad/s}$) and add a resistance.
> - It stops oscillating once $R \ge 2\sqrt{L/C} = 2\sqrt{2500\ \Omega^2} = 100\ \Omega$.
> - With $R = 10\ \Omega$ (a value chosen here), $\gamma = R/2L = 250\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\omega' = \sqrt{(2500)^2 - (250)^2}\ \mathrm{rad/s} = 2.49\times10^3\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, only $0.5\%$ below $\omega_0$. The energy decays roughly as $e^{-Rt/L} = e^{-(500\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})t}$, halving in $(\ln 2)/(500\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}) = 1.4\ \mathrm{ms}$, a little over half a period.
>
> Even a small resistance kills the oscillation within a few periods, while barely changing its frequency.
>
> *Source: built on UP2 Example 14.6*

^ex-a10-6-2
