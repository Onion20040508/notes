What does an inductor do in a circuit? Since the current through it cannot jump ([[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^cau-a10-3-1|Caution in §A10.3]]), it delays every change: switch a source on or off, and the current moves to its new value exponentially, on the time scale $L/R$. This is the magnetic counterpart of the RC circuit ([[§A6.5 RC Circuits#^def-a6-5-1|Def. §A6.5.1]], [[§A6.5 RC Circuits#^thm-a6-5-1|Theorem §A6.5.1]]), and the first circuit whose analysis needs a differential equation built from the inductor's voltage.

![[ph-em-a10-4-1.svg]]
*The RL circuit. With $S_1$ closed and $S_2$ open, the source drives the current $I$ (blue) through $R$ and $L$. After the current has settled, opening $S_1$ and closing $S_2$ removes the source and leaves $R$ and $L$ in a loop on their own.*

## Switching on

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.4.1: Current Growth in an RL Circuit
> A source of emf $\mathcal E$, a resistance $R$ and an ideal inductor $L$ are connected in series at $t = 0$, with no current before. Then
>
> $$
> I(t) = \frac{\mathcal E}{R}\bigl(1 - e^{-t/\tau_L}\bigr), \qquad V_L(t) = L\frac{dI}{dt} = \mathcal E\,e^{-t/\tau_L}, \qquad \tau_L = \frac{L}{R},
> $$
>
> where $V_L$ is the voltage across the inductor, dropping in the direction of the current.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.4, eqs. (14.23)–(14.28)*

^thm-a10-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Go around the loop in the direction of the current with Kirchhoff's loop rule ([[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-2|Theorem §A6.3.2]]): the source raises the potential by $\mathcal E$, the resistor drops it by $IR$, the inductor by $L\,dI/dt$ ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-3|Theorem §A10.2.3]]):
>
> $$
> \mathcal E - IR - L\frac{dI}{dt} = 0 .
> $$
>
> Write $u = I - \mathcal E/R$. Then $du/dt = dI/dt = -(R/L)\,u = -u/\tau_L$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(u\,e^{t/\tau_L}\Bigr) = \Bigl(\frac{du}{dt} + \frac{u}{\tau_L}\Bigr)e^{t/\tau_L} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> so $u\,e^{t/\tau_L}$ is constant ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]) and $u(t) = u(0)\,e^{-t/\tau_L}$. With $I(0) = 0$, $u(0) = -\mathcal E/R$, which gives $I(t)$; the argument also shows that this is the only solution. Differentiating, $V_L = L\,dI/dt = L(\mathcal E/R)(1/\tau_L)e^{-t/\tau_L} = \mathcal E e^{-t/\tau_L}$.

^der-a10-4-1

*Uses:* [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-2|Theorem §A6.3.2]], [[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-3|Theorem §A10.2.3]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]

> [!definition] Definition §A10.4.1: Inductive Time Constant
> The **inductive time constant** of a series RL circuit is $\tau_L = L/R$. It has the dimension of time: $1\ \mathrm{H/\Omega} = 1\ \mathrm{(\Omega\cdot s)/\Omega} = 1\ \mathrm s$. After one time constant the growing current has reached $1 - e^{-1} \approx 63\%$ of its final value and the inductor voltage has fallen to $e^{-1} \approx 37\%$ of its initial value; the smaller $\tau_L$, the faster both happen. It is the counterpart of the capacitive time constant $\tau = RC$ ([[§A6.5 RC Circuits#^def-a6-5-1|Def. §A6.5.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.4, eqs. (14.25), (14.27), (14.29)*

^def-a10-4-1

## Switching off

> [!theorem] Theorem §A10.4.2: Current Decay in an RL Circuit
> If a current $I_0$ flows at $t = 0$ in a loop of $R$ and $L$ without a source, then
>
> $$
> I(t) = I_0\,e^{-t/\tau_L}, \qquad \lvert V_L(t)\rvert = I_0R\,e^{-t/\tau_L}, \qquad U_L(t) = \tfrac12LI_0^2\,e^{-2t/\tau_L}.
> $$
>
> After the growth of [[§A10.4 RL Circuits#^thm-a10-4-1|Theorem §A10.4.1]] has settled, $I_0 = \mathcal E/R$ and $\lvert V_L(0)\rvert = \mathcal E$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §14.4, eqs. (14.30)–(14.32)*

^thm-a10-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The loop rule now reads $-IR - L\,dI/dt = 0$, which is the equation of [[§A10.4 RL Circuits#^der-a10-4-1|the growth derivation]] with $\mathcal E = 0$; the same argument with $u = I$ gives $I = I_0e^{-t/\tau_L}$. Then $V_L = L\,dI/dt = -I_0R\,e^{-t/\tau_L}$: the inductor now pushes the current onward. The stored energy follows from [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-1|Theorem §A10.3.1]].

^der-a10-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-2|Theorem §A6.3.2]], [[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-3|Theorem §A10.2.3]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]], [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a10-3-1|Theorem §A10.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The two limits, and the RC comparison
> - *Just after switching on,* $I = 0$ and the whole emf appears across the inductor: it behaves like an open circuit. *Long after,* $dI/dt = 0$: it behaves like a plain wire and $I = \mathcal E/R$.
> - The capacitor is the mirror image: its *voltage* cannot jump, it acts like a wire at first and like an open circuit at the end, and its time constant is $RC$ rather than $L/R$ ([[§A6.5 RC Circuits#^def-a6-5-1|Def. §A6.5.1]]; [[§A6.5 RC Circuits#^thm-a6-5-1|Theorem §A6.5.1]], [[§A6.5 RC Circuits#^thm-a6-5-2|Theorem §A6.5.2]]). The growth and decay curves are the same exponentials.
> - The equation $L\,dI/dt = \mathcal E - RI$ has the form of a body falling with linear drag, $m\,dv/dt = mg - bv$ ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^thm-a3-10-4|CM Theorem §A3.10.4]]): inductance plays mass, resistance plays the drag coefficient, the final current $\mathcal E/R$ the terminal velocity.

^rem-a10-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Where the battery's energy goes
> Multiply the loop equation by $I$: $\mathcal EI = I^2R + \dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl(\tfrac12LI^2\bigr)$. The source's power goes partly into Joule heat and partly into the inductor's field. Once the current is steady, the total delivered by the source minus the total dissipated equals the stored $\tfrac12L(\mathcal E/R)^2$. After switching off, all of that energy becomes heat in $R$: $\int_0^\infty I_0^2R\,e^{-2t/\tau_L}\,dt = \tfrac12I_0^2R\tau_L = \tfrac12LI_0^2$.

^rem-a10-4-2

> [!caution] Caution: Signs of $V_L$
> University Physics writes $V_L$ for the induced emf $-L\,dI/dt$; here $V_L$ is the voltage drop $L\,dI/dt$ across the inductor in the direction of the current. They differ only in sign; the magnitudes agree. Fix one convention and stick to it in the loop rule.

^cau-a10-4-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A10.4.1: Switching On
> Take $\mathcal E = 2.0\ \mathrm V$, $R = 4.0\ \Omega$ and $L = 4.0\ \mathrm H$. The time constant is $\tau_L = L/R = 1.0\ \mathrm s$ and the final current $\mathcal E/R = 0.50\ \mathrm A$. By [[§A10.4 RL Circuits#^thm-a10-4-1|Theorem §A10.4.1]]:
>
> | $t$ | $I$ | $\lvert V_L\rvert$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | $0$ | $0$ | $2.0\ \mathrm V$ |
> | $2.0\,\tau_L$ | $(0.50\ \mathrm A)(1 - e^{-2.0}) = 0.43\ \mathrm A$ | $(2.0\ \mathrm V)e^{-2.0} = 0.27\ \mathrm V$ |
> | $\to\infty$ | $0.50\ \mathrm A$ | $0$ |
>
> On time scales much longer than $\tau_L$, the circuit looks like a plain resistor.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 14.4*

^ex-a10-4-1

> [!example] Example §A10.4.2: Switching Off
> After the current of Example §A10.4.1 has reached $0.50\ \mathrm A$, the source is removed ($S_1$ opened, $S_2$ closed). By [[§A10.4 RL Circuits#^thm-a10-4-2|Theorem §A10.4.2]]:
> - The current halves when $e^{-t/\tau_L} = 0.50$, at $t = \tau_L\ln 2 = 0.69\ \mathrm s$.
> - The stored energy falls to $1.0\%$ when $e^{-2t/\tau_L} = 0.010$, at $t = \tfrac12\tau_L\ln 100 = 2.3\ \mathrm s$.
>
> The energy decays twice as fast as the current, since it goes as $I^2$. Had the current not reached its final value before switching, the decay would start lower but follow the same exponential.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 14.5*

^ex-a10-4-2

![[ph-em-a10-4-2.svg]]
*(a) After switching on, the current (blue) climbs to $\mathcal E/R$, reaching 63% at $t = \tau_L$, while the inductor voltage (red) decays from $\mathcal E$, down to 37% at $\tau_L$. (b) After switching off, the current (blue) decays with time constant $\tau_L$, halving at $\tau_L\ln2$; the stored energy (red) decays twice as fast.*
