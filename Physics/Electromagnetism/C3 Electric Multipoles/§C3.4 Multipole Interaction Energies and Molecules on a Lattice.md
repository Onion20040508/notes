How does a small charge distribution couple to a field that varies slowly across it, and what order do many such molecules choose on a surface? Level B derived the force, torque and energy of a dipole from a physical dipole ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]]) and the energy of two dipoles ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]]). Level C expands the external potential about the body, which gives every multipole's coupling at once, and adds the quadrupole–quadrupole energy. It then applies these to molecules adsorbed on a square lattice: Zangwill's Problem 4.13 (HW 8, Problem 3) and the user's paper, which grew out of it. The paper's setup and results are presented with their section numbers. Its key numbers are recomputed here; where recomputation differs, both are stated. Energies follow [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^cau-c2-3-1|§C2.3, Caution: Symbols for energy]]: $U_{\text{pot}}$ is Zangwill's $V_E$.

## A small distribution in an external field

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.1: Energy, Force and Torque of a Small Distribution in an External Field
> Let ρ be confined near a reference point $\vec r$, with moments $Q$, $\vec p$, $Q_{ij}$ taken about $\vec r$ ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]), in an external field $\vec E = -\nabla V$ whose sources lie outside the body and which varies slowly across it. To quadrupole order (Zangwill eqs. (4.19), (4.24), (4.26), (4.67)–(4.69); Problem 4.16):
>
> $$
> U_{\text{pot}} = QV - \vec p\cdot\vec E - Q_{ij}\,\partial_iE_j = QV - \vec p\cdot\vec E - \tfrac13\Theta_{ij}\,\partial_iE_j ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \vec F = Q\vec E + (\vec p\cdot\nabla)\vec E + Q_{ij}\,\partial_i\partial_j\vec E, \qquad \vec N = \vec p\times\vec E + 2(\mathsf Q\cdot\nabla)\times\vec E + \vec r\times\vec F ,
> $$
>
> all fields at $\vec r$. Here $(\mathsf Q\cdot\nabla)_i = Q_{ij}\partial_j$, $\vec N$ is the torque about the origin, and $2(\mathsf Q\cdot\nabla)\times\vec E = \frac23(\Theta\cdot\nabla)\times\vec E$. For fixed moments, $\vec F = -\nabla U_{\text{pot}}$; for $\vec p$ fixed, $\vec F = \nabla(\vec p\cdot\vec E)$ (Zangwill eq. (4.20), not for induced moments). A uniform field exerts no force on a neutral body, and only field *gradients* couple to quadrupoles.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §4.2.3–§4.2.5, eqs. (4.17)–(4.29); §4.4.2, eqs. (4.67)–(4.69); Problem 4.16(b)–(d) (solved here)*

^thm-c3-4-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Energy.** $U_{\text{pot}} = \int d^3r'\,\rho(\vec r\,')V(\vec r\,')$ ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]). With $\vec s = \vec r\,' - \vec r$, Taylor's theorem ([[§9 Taylor's Theorem for Multivariable Functions#^thm-9-2|452 Thm. §9.2]]) gives $V(\vec r + \vec s) = V + s_i\partial_iV + \frac12s_is_j\partial_i\partial_jV + O(s^3)$. Integrate against ρ: $\int\rho = Q$, $\int\rho\,s_i = p_i$, $\frac12\int\rho\,s_is_j = Q_{ij}$. With $\partial_iV = -E_i$: $U_{\text{pot}} = QV - p_iE_i - Q_{ij}\partial_iE_j + O(QR^3|\partial^3V|)$. The dropped terms are octupole order.
>
> **2. Trace part.** $Q_{ij} = \frac13(\Theta_{ij} + Q_{kk}\delta_{ij})$ (Def. §C3.3.1), so $Q_{ij}\partial_iE_j = \frac13\Theta_{ij}\partial_iE_j + \frac13Q_{kk}\nabla\cdot\vec E$. ⚑ $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho_{\text{ext}}/\varepsilon_0$ vanishes on the body only because the external sources are elsewhere (Zangwill, after eq. (4.69)). If external charge overlaps the body, the trace survives as a **contact energy** $-Q_{kk}\rho_{\text{ext}}/3\varepsilon_0$: the trace moment $\frac12\int\rho r^2$, invisible from outside ([[§C3.3 Primitive and Traceless Moments#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]]), is seen by a probe that penetrates the charge. For a spherical neutron probed by an electron of charge $e$, this is Foldy's $\int U_{\text{pot}}\,d^3r = -\frac16\frac{e}{\varepsilon_0}\int s^2\rho_N\,d^3s$ (Zangwill Problem 4.19).
>
> **3. Force.** $\vec F = \int\rho(\vec r\,')\vec E(\vec r\,')$ ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-4|Theorem §C2.1.4]]); expand $\vec E$ the same way: $Q\vec E + p_i\partial_i\vec E + Q_{ij}\partial_i\partial_j\vec E$ (Zangwill eqs. (4.17)–(4.19), (4.67)). Check against step 1 at fixed moments: $-\partial_k(QV - p_jE_j - Q_{ij}\partial_iE_j) = QE_k + p_j\partial_kE_j + Q_{ij}\partial_i\partial_kE_j$, and $\partial_kE_j = \partial_jE_k$ ($\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$) turns this into the stated $\vec F$. For constant $\vec p$, $\nabla(\vec p\cdot\vec E) = (\vec p\cdot\nabla)\vec E + \vec p\times(\nabla\times\vec E)$, which gives (4.20).
>
> **4. Torque.** $\vec N = \int\rho\,(\vec r + \vec s)\times\vec E(\vec r + \vec s)$. Keep terms to second order in $s$: $\vec r\times\vec F + \int\rho\,\vec s\times\vec E + \int\rho\,\vec s\times(s_j\partial_j\vec E)$. The middle term is $\vec p\times\vec E$. The last has components $\epsilon_{klm}\int\rho\,s_ls_j\,\partial_jE_m = 2\epsilon_{klm}Q_{lj}\partial_jE_m = 2[(\mathsf Q\cdot\nabla)\times\vec E]_k$ (Zangwill eq. (4.68)). Its trace part is $\frac23Q_{kk}\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$, which leaves $\frac23(\Theta\cdot\nabla)\times\vec E$.
>
> **5. The same by point densities (Zangwill's route).** For a point dipole, $\vec F = \int\vec E\,\rho_D = \int\vec E\,(-\vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3) = (\vec p\cdot\nabla)\vec E$ by moving the derivative onto $\vec E$ (eq. (4.21); [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^mod-c3-1-2|Model §C3.1.2]]). In the same way $U_{\text{pot}} = \int V\rho_D = -\vec p\cdot\vec E$ (eqs. (4.25)–(4.26)), and $\rho_Q = Q_{ij}\partial_i\partial_j\delta^3$ ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^mod-c3-1-5|Model §C3.1.5]]) gives the quadrupole terms. Rotating a rigid dipole by $\delta\vec\alpha$, $\delta\vec p = \delta\vec\alpha\times\vec p$ and $\delta U_{\text{pot}} = -(\vec p\times\vec E)\cdot\delta\vec\alpha = -\vec N\cdot\delta\vec\alpha$ (eqs. (4.28)–(4.29)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Each moment couples to one more derivative of the external potential. The $\frac12$ in Zangwill's $Q_{ij}$ is exactly the Taylor $\frac12$, so no numerical factors appear (cf. [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^cau-c3-1-1|§C3.1, Caution: Quadrupole normalizations]]).
> - The moments are held fixed. For induced moments the energy acquires a factor $\frac12$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^cau-b4-2-1|§B4.2, Caution: Torque is N here; do not differentiate p]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.2: Energy of N Point Dipoles
> Rigid point dipoles $\vec p_1, \dots, \vec p_N$ at $\vec r_1, \dots, \vec r_N$ have the interaction energy (Zangwill eqs. (4.30)–(4.32))
>
> $$
> U_E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac12\sum_{i = 1}^N\sum_{j \ne i}\left[\frac{\vec p_i\cdot\vec p_j}{r_{ij}^3} - \frac{3(\vec p_i\cdot\vec r_{ij})(\vec p_j\cdot\vec r_{ij})}{r_{ij}^5}\right] = -\frac12\sum_{i = 1}^N\vec p_i\cdot\vec E(\vec r_i), \qquad \vec r_{ij} = \vec r_i - \vec r_j ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec E(\vec r_i)$ the field of all the *other* dipoles at $\vec r_i$. The self-energies of the point dipoles are excluded. For $N = 2$ this is level B's [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]].
>
> *Source: Zangwill §4.2.6, eqs. (4.30)–(4.32)*

^thm-c3-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Assemble one at a time.** Bringing $\vec p_1$ in costs nothing. Bringing $\vec p_k$ in from infinity, with its orientation fixed, costs $-\vec p_k\cdot\sum_{j < k}\vec E_j(\vec r_k)$ by [[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]], where $\vec E_j(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}[3\hat n(\hat n\cdot\vec p_j) - \vec p_j]/|\vec r - \vec r_j|^3$ is the dipole field off its source ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]]).
>
> **2. Pair energy.** $-\vec p_k\cdot\vec E_j(\vec r_k) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}[\vec p_j\cdot\vec p_k - 3(\vec p_j\cdot\hat r_{kj})(\vec p_k\cdot\hat r_{kj})]/r_{kj}^3$ (eq. (4.30)), which is symmetric in $j \leftrightarrow k$.
>
> **3. Sum.** Summing over $k$ and $j < k$ counts each pair once. Writing it as $\frac12\sum_i\sum_{j \ne i}$ counts each twice with a factor $\frac12$, which gives the first form; the second follows by step 2.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ The restriction $j \ne i$ is essential. The field of a point dipole at its own position is the contact term of Theorem §C3.1.4, and $\vec p\cdot\vec p\,\delta^3(\vec 0)$ has no value: the same exclusion as the self-energy of point charges ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-7|Theorem §C2.3.7]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.3: Interaction Energy of Two Point Quadrupoles
> Two point quadrupoles with traceless moments $\Theta^{(1)}$, $\Theta^{(2)}$, separated by $\vec R$, have
>
> $$
> U = \frac{1}{9\cdot4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\Theta^{(1)}_{kl}\,\Theta^{(2)}_{ij}\,\partial_i\partial_j\partial_k\partial_l\frac1R \;\propto\; \frac{1}{R^5} .
> $$
>
> For **linear** quadrupoles along unit vectors $\hat n_1$, $\hat n_2$, $\Theta^{(a)}_{ij} = \frac{\Theta_a}{2}(3n_{ai}n_{aj} - \delta_{ij})$, with $c_a = \hat n_a\cdot\hat R$ and $c_{12} = \hat n_1\cdot\hat n_2$:
>
> $$
> U = \frac{3\Theta_1\Theta_2}{4\cdot4\pi\varepsilon_0R^5}\,g, \qquad g = 1 - 5c_1^2 - 5c_2^2 - 15c_1^2c_2^2 + 2(c_{12} - 5c_1c_2)^2 .
> $$
>
> If both axes and $\vec R$ lie in one plane, with angles $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$ from $\hat R$ to the axes, then
>
> $$
> g = \tfrac34 + \tfrac54(\cos2\theta_1 + \cos2\theta_2) + \tfrac{19}{4}\cos2\theta_1\cos2\theta_2 - 4\sin2\theta_1\sin2\theta_2 \;\ge\; -4 ,
> $$
>
> with equality exactly for the **T** shape (one axis along $\hat R$, the other perpendicular). Other values: end to end $g = 8$, parallel side by side $g = 3$, crossed perpendicular to $\hat R$ and to each other $g = 1$. The sign of Θ does not matter for like molecules ($U \propto \Theta^2$). A linear $+q, -2q, +q$ molecule with spacing $d$ has $\Theta = 2qd^2$. N₂ has $Q = 0$ and $\vec p = \vec 0$, so the leading interaction of two N₂ molecules falls as $R^{-5}$ (Zangwill Problem 4.17).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §4.4.2, eq. (4.69); Problem 4.17 (solved here); the general and planar forms and the bound are derived here*

^thm-c3-4-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Potential of quadrupole 1.** By [[§C3.3 Primitive and Traceless Moments#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]], $V_1 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Theta^{(1)}_{kl}x_kx_l/R^5$. Since $\partial_k\partial_l\frac1R = 3[x_kx_l]^{\mathrm{STF}}/R^5$ and $\Theta^{(1)}$ is trace-free, this is $V_1 = \frac{1}{3\cdot4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Theta^{(1)}_{kl}\partial_k\partial_l\frac1R$, with $\vec x = \vec R$ the position of 2 relative to 1.
>
> **2. Energy of quadrupole 2 in it.** By [[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]], with the sources of $V_1$ away from 2, $U = -\frac13\Theta^{(2)}_{ij}\partial_iE_{1j} = \frac13\Theta^{(2)}_{ij}\partial_i\partial_jV_1$, which is the first formula. It is symmetric in $1 \leftrightarrow 2$, since $\partial^4(1/R)$ is even in $\vec R$.
>
> **3. Fourth derivative.** Differentiating $\partial_i\partial_j\partial_k(1/R)$ once more ([[§C3.3 Primitive and Traceless Moments#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]: leading coefficient $7!! = 105$, trace-free):
>
> $$
> \partial_i\partial_j\partial_k\partial_l\frac1R = \frac{105\,x_ix_jx_kx_l - 15R^2(\delta_{ij}x_kx_l + \delta_{ik}x_jx_l + \delta_{il}x_jx_k + \delta_{jk}x_ix_l + \delta_{jl}x_ix_k + \delta_{kl}x_ix_j) + 3R^4(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} + \delta_{il}\delta_{jk})}{R^9} .
> $$
>
> Check of the trace: contracting $\delta_{ij}$ gives $105R^2x_kx_l - 15R^2(7x_kx_l + R^2\delta_{kl}) + 15R^4\delta_{kl} = 0$.
>
> **4. Linear quadrupoles.** The δ parts of $\Theta^{(a)}$ give zero against the trace-free $\partial^4(1/R)$, so $U = \frac{1}{9\cdot4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{9\Theta_1\Theta_2}{4}\,n_{1k}n_{1l}n_{2i}n_{2j}\partial_i\partial_j\partial_k\partial_l\frac1R$. Contract step 3 term by term. The first term gives $105c_1^2c_2^2$. In the six δx x terms, $\delta_{ij}$ gives $R^2c_1^2$, $\delta_{kl}$ gives $R^2c_2^2$, and each of the four mixed ones gives $R^2c_{12}c_1c_2$. The δδ terms give $1 + 2c_{12}^2$. So
>
> $$
> U = \frac{\Theta_1\Theta_2}{4\cdot4\pi\varepsilon_0R^5}\bigl[105c_1^2c_2^2 - 15c_1^2 - 15c_2^2 - 60c_{12}c_1c_2 + 3 + 6c_{12}^2\bigr] .
> $$
>
> Factor 3 out; since $2(c_{12} - 5c_1c_2)^2 - 15c_1^2c_2^2 = 2c_{12}^2 - 20c_{12}c_1c_2 + 35c_1^2c_2^2$, this is the stated $g$. (A numerical check against the exact Coulomb energy of two $+q, -2q, +q$ triples at large separation agrees.)
>
> **5. Planar form.** With $c_a = \cos\theta_a$, $s_a = \sin\theta_a$ and $c_{12} = \cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2) = c_1c_2 + s_1s_2$: $c_{12} - 5c_1c_2 = s_1s_2 - 4c_1c_2$, and
>
> $$
> g = 1 - 5c_1^2 - 5c_2^2 + 17c_1^2c_2^2 + 2s_1^2s_2^2 - 16s_1c_1s_2c_2 .
> $$
>
> Insert $c^2 = \frac12(1 + u)$, $s^2 = \frac12(1 - u)$, $sc = \frac12\sin2\theta$ with $u_a = \cos2\theta_a$. Constant: $1 - \frac52 - \frac52 + \frac{17}{4} + \frac12 = \frac34$. Coefficient of $u_1$: $-\frac52 + \frac{17}{4} - \frac12 = \frac54$. Coefficient of $u_1u_2$: $\frac{17}{4} + \frac12 = \frac{19}{4}$. Sine term: $-16\cdot\frac14 = -4$.
>
> **6. The bound.** Let $A = 2\theta_1$, $B = 2\theta_2$. Then $g + 4 = \frac{19 + 5\cos A}{4} + \frac{5 + 19\cos A}{4}\cos B - 4\sin A\sin B$. For fixed $A$, $a\cos B + b\sin B \ge -\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, so $g + 4 \ge \frac{19 + 5\cos A}{4} - \sqrt{\frac{(5 + 19\cos A)^2}{16} + 16\sin^2A}$. Since $19 + 5\cos A > 0$, it suffices that $(19 + 5\cos A)^2 \ge (5 + 19\cos A)^2 + 256\sin^2A$. The left minus the first term on the right is $336 - 336\cos^2A = 336\sin^2A \ge 256\sin^2A$. Equality needs $\sin A = 0$. Then for $A = 0$, $g + 4 = 6 + 6\cos B$, zero iff $B = \pi$; for $A = \pi$, $g + 4 = \frac72(1 - \cos B)$, zero iff $B = 0$. Either way one axis lies along $\hat R$ and the other is perpendicular to it.
>
> **7. Special values.** $\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 0$: $\frac34 + \frac52 + \frac{19}{4} = 8$. $\theta_1 = \theta_2 = \frac\pi2$: $\frac34 - \frac52 + \frac{19}{4} = 3$. Crossed ($c_1 = c_2 = c_{12} = 0$): $g = 1$. $\Theta = \frac12\sum q(3z^2 - r^2) = \frac12\cdot q\cdot2d^2\cdot2 = 2qd^2$ for charges $q$ at $z = \pm d$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Quadrupoles prefer the T shape: the end of one, where its charge sits, points at the middle of the other, where the opposite charge sits. Head-to-tail order is the dipole's preference, not the quadrupole's.
> - The planar bound is what makes the lattice ground state of quadrupoles provable bond by bond (★ remark below).

## Molecules on a square lattice

> [!model] Model §C3.4.4: Molecules Adsorbed on a Square Lattice
> Molecules sit on the sites $\vec r_{mn} = (ma, na, 0)$, $(m, n) \in \mathbb Z^2$, of a square lattice with spacing $a$, lying flat with in-plane orientation angles $\alpha_{mn}$ (the user's paper (2025), Defs. 1.1–1.2; Zangwill Problem 4.13). CO is a point dipole $\vec p_{mn} = p(\cos\alpha_{mn}, \sin\alpha_{mn}, 0)$ (paper eq. (3)); N₂ is a linear point quadrupole along the same direction (paper §8.2). Each molecule interacts only with the neighbours in a set $\mathcal N$: the **eight nearest** (four edge neighbours at distance $a$, four diagonal ones at $a\sqrt2$; paper Def. 1.3, Zangwill), or the four edge neighbours only (the paper's N₂ model, Assumption 8.3). The pair energy is [[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]]'s, for dipoles
>
> $$
> U_{ij} = \frac{p^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r_{ij}^3}\bigl[\cos(\alpha_i - \alpha_j) - 3\cos(\alpha_i - \phi_{ij})\cos(\alpha_j - \phi_{ij})\bigr] \qquad\text{(paper eq. (6))},
> $$
>
> with $\phi_{ij}$ the direction of $\vec r_j - \vec r_i$, or [[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]]'s for quadrupoles. The total energy is $E = \frac12\sum_i\sum_{j \in \mathcal N(i)}U_{ij}$ (paper eq. (7)). The **energy per site** $u$ is $E/N^2$ on $N\times N$ blocks as $N \to \infty$ (paper Remark 1.6); it equals Zangwill's "energy/dipole". One molecule's energy with all its neighbours, $U_{\text{total}} = \sum_jU_{0j}$, is $2u$.
>
> *Assumptions* (paper Assumptions 2.1–2.3, plus what the model uses implicitly): an infinite, defect-free lattice; identical rigid molecules confined to the plane; no external field and no substrate field; electrostatics of point multipoles only; interactions truncated to $\mathcal N$. The truncation is part of the model (Zangwill: "eight nearest neighbors only"), and all comparisons below are made within it. It is not forced by convergence: in two dimensions the untruncated dipole sum converges absolutely (about $2\pi r$ sites at distance $r$, each term $\propto r^{-3}$, so the shells fall as $r^{-2}$), unlike the conditionally convergent, shape-dependent sums of a three-dimensional crystal ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-3|Theorem §C5.2.3]]). The untruncated values are quoted after [[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-8|Theorem §C3.4.8]].
>
> *Source: the user's paper (2025), §1–§2, Defs. 1.1–1.5, Remark 1.6, Assumptions 2.1–2.3, §8.2 · Zangwill Problem 4.13*

^mod-c3-4-4

> [!definition] Definition §C3.4.1: Interaction Matrix and Its Lattice Transform
> For the eight-neighbour dipole model, write $\vec p_i\cdot\mathsf J(\vec\delta)\,\vec p_j$ for the pair energy in units of $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3$, with $\vec\delta = (\vec r_j - \vec r_i)/a$ and $\mathsf J(\vec\delta) = (\mathbb 1 - 3\hat\delta\hat\delta^{\mathsf T})/|\vec\delta|^3$ (in-plane 2×2 block). Its **lattice transform** at $\vec q = (q_x, q_y)$ is the real symmetric matrix $\mathsf J(\vec q) = \sum_{\vec\delta \in \mathcal N}\mathsf J(\vec\delta)\cos(\vec q\cdot\vec\delta)$. With $c = \cos q_x$, $d = \cos q_y$:
>
> $$
> \mathsf J(\vec q) = \begin{pmatrix} -4c + 2d - \frac{cd}{\sqrt2} & \frac{3}{\sqrt2}\sin q_x\sin q_y \\ \frac{3}{\sqrt2}\sin q_x\sin q_y & 2c - 4d - \frac{cd}{\sqrt2}\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> Entries: the edge pairs $\pm\hat x$ contribute $2c\operatorname{diag}(-2, 1)$ and $\pm\hat y$ contribute $2d\operatorname{diag}(1, -2)$. The four diagonals $(\sigma_x, \sigma_y)/\sqrt2$ have $\mathsf J = \frac{1}{2\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} -\frac12 & -\frac32\sigma_x\sigma_y \\ -\frac32\sigma_x\sigma_y & -\frac12\end{pmatrix}$, with $\sum\cos(\sigma_xq_x + \sigma_yq_y) = 4cd$ and $\sum\sigma_x\sigma_y\cos(\cdot) = -4\sin q_x\sin q_y$. A configuration whose components are modulated as $\cos(\vec q\cdot\vec r/a)$ with $q_x, q_y \in \{0, \pi\}$ (uniform, stripes, checkerboard) feels exactly $\mathsf J(\vec q)$.
>

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.5: Symmetries and Equilibrium of the Lattice Energy
> 1. *(Symmetries.)* $E$ is unchanged by lattice translations, $\alpha_{mn} \to \alpha_{m - k, n - \ell}$ (paper Theorem 3.2), and by the point operations of the square lattice, $C_{4v}$ (paper Proposition 3.6), applied to positions and orientations together, e.g. $\alpha_{mn} \to \alpha_{R^{-1}(m, n)} + 90^\circ$. For dipoles $E$ is also unchanged by reversing every dipole, $\alpha \to \alpha + 180^\circ$. Each ground state therefore comes with all its images (paper Corollary 3.3).
> 2. *(Equilibrium.)* The torque on molecule $(m, n)$ about the surface normal is $-\partial E/\partial\alpha_{mn}$. Static equilibrium requires $\partial E/\partial\alpha_{mn} = 0$ for all sites (paper Proposition 4.1, eqs. (18)–(19)): necessary, not sufficient for a minimum (paper Remark 4.2).
>
> *Source: the user's paper (2025), §3–§4, Theorem 3.2, Corollary 3.3, Propositions 3.6, 4.1, Remark 4.2*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Translations** (paper eqs. (12)–(16)). Relabel the summation sites by $(m - k, n - \ell)$. The pair energy depends on the two orientations and on $\vec r_{m'n'} - \vec r_{mn}$, which a common shift leaves unchanged, and the neighbour sets shift with the sites.
>
> **2. Point operations.** A rotation $R$ by $90^\circ$ maps the lattice and each neighbour shell onto themselves. Rotating all positions *and* all dipole vectors by $R$ leaves every scalar product in $U_{ij}$ unchanged; reflections likewise. $U_{ij}$ is even in $(\vec p_i, \vec p_j) \to (-\vec p_i, -\vec p_j)$.
>
> **3. Torque.** Rotating molecule $(m, n)$ alone by $\delta\alpha$ about $\hat z$ changes $E$ by $\frac{\partial E}{\partial\alpha_{mn}}\delta\alpha$, and by $-N_z\,\delta\alpha$ ([[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]], step 5). Only the molecule's own bonds depend on $\alpha_{mn}$, so the derivative is finite.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.6: Uniform Dipole Configurations Are Stationary and Degenerate
> In the eight-neighbour dipole model, the lattice sums $\sum_{j \in \mathcal N}\cos2\phi_j/r_j^3$ and $\sum_j\sin2\phi_j/r_j^3$ vanish (separately for the edge and the diagonal shells), and $\sum_j r_j^{-3} = (4 + \sqrt2)/a^3$. Hence every uniform configuration $\alpha_{mn} = \alpha_0$ satisfies the equilibrium conditions, and all have the same energy per site (the user's paper (2025), Lemma 5.1, Theorem 5.3)
>

> u_{\text{uniform}} = -\frac{(4 + \sqrt2)\,p^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0a^3} = -\Bigl(2 + \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigr)\frac{p^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0a^3} \approx -0.338\,\frac{p^2}{\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}, \qquad U_{\text{total}} = 2u_{\text{uniform}} \approx -0.677\,\frac{p^2}{\pi\varepsilon_0a^3} ,
> $$
>
> independent of $\alpha_0$. In the language of Def. §C3.4.1, $\mathsf J(\vec 0) = -(2 + \frac{1}{\sqrt2})\mathbb 1$ is a multiple of the identity.

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Uniform pair energy** (paper eqs. (21), (36)–(38)). With $\alpha_i = \alpha_j = \alpha_0$: $U = \frac{p^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}[1 - 3\cos^2(\alpha_0 - \phi)] = \frac{p^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}\bigl[-\frac12 - \frac32\cos2(\alpha_0 - \phi)\bigr]$, and $\cos2(\alpha_0 - \phi) = \cos2\alpha_0\cos2\phi + \sin2\alpha_0\sin2\phi$.
>
> **2. Lattice sums** (paper eqs. (26)–(33), (41)–(43)). Edge shell, $\phi = 0^\circ, 90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ$: $\cos2\phi = 1, -1, 1, -1$ and $\sin2\phi = 0$. Diagonal shell, $\phi = 45^\circ, 135^\circ, 225^\circ, 315^\circ$: $\cos2\phi = 0$ and $\sin2\phi = 1, -1, 1, -1$. Both sums vanish shell by shell. $\sum r^{-3} = 4a^{-3} + 4(a\sqrt2)^{-3} = (4 + \sqrt2)a^{-3}$.
>
> **3. Stationarity** (paper eqs. (20)–(25)). $\partial E/\partial\alpha_{mn}$ is $2\cdot\frac12\sum_j\partial_{\alpha_0}U$ (each bond of the site counts twice in $E$ with weight $\frac12$), proportional to $\sin2\alpha_0\sum\cos2\phi_j/r_j^3 - \cos2\alpha_0\sum\sin2\phi_j/r_j^3 = 0$.
>
> **4. Energy.** $u = \frac12\sum_jU_{0j} = \frac{p^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\bigl(-\frac12\bigr)\frac{4 + \sqrt2}{a^3}$. Numerically $(4 + \sqrt2)/16 = 0.3384$. Paper §5.3 checks $\alpha_0 = 90^\circ$ directly: the edge sum is $\frac{p^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}(1 + 1 - 2 - 2)$ and the diagonal sum $-\frac{\sqrt2p^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}$, total $U_{\text{total}} = -\frac{(4 + \sqrt2)p^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}$ (paper eq. (49)), consistent.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The degeneracy is a symmetry statement: a quadratic form in $\vec p$ that is invariant under $90^\circ$ rotations is a multiple of $\mathbb 1$ on the plane (the $\cos2\phi$ harmonics cancel in every $C_4$-symmetric shell).
> - Stationarity does not decide stability; Theorems §C3.4.7–§C3.4.8 compare energies.

> - **checkerboard**: $\alpha_{mn} = 90^\circ \pm \varepsilon$ for $m + n$ even/odd;
> - **stripes**: $\alpha_{mn} = 90^\circ \pm \varepsilon$ for $m$ even/odd.

> $$
> u_{\text{chk}}(\varepsilon) = \frac{p^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}\Bigl[-\Bigl(2 + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigr)\cos^2\varepsilon + \Bigl(2 - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigr)\sin^2\varepsilon\Bigr], \qquad u_{\text{str}}(\varepsilon) = \frac{p^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}\bigl[4 - (8 + \sqrt2)\cos2\varepsilon\bigr] .

> Both equal $u_{\text{uniform}}$ at $\varepsilon = 0$ and increase strictly on $(0^\circ, 90^\circ]$: within these two families no modulation beats the uniform state (paper Theorems 6.4, 6.10, Corollaries 6.5, 6.11). The stripe formula is the paper's Theorem 6.8. ^thm-c3-4-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Split into Fourier modes.** For the checkerboard, $\vec p_{mn} = p\bigl(-\sin\varepsilon\,(-1)^{m + n}, \cos\varepsilon\bigr)$: a uniform $y$ component ($\vec q = \vec 0$) plus a staggered $x$ component ($\vec q = (\pi, \pi)$). For the stripes the $x$ component is $-p\sin\varepsilon\,(-1)^m$, $\vec q = (\pi, 0)$.
>
> **2. Cross terms vanish.** Per site, $u = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}\sum_{\vec\delta}\vec p_0\cdot\mathsf J(\vec\delta)\vec p_{\vec\delta}$. The mixed $x$–$y$ terms carry $J_{xy}(\vec\delta)(1 + \sigma_{\vec\delta})\sin\varepsilon\cos\varepsilon$, with $\sigma_{\vec\delta} = \pm1$ the sign of the staggered component at the neighbour. $J_{xy}$ is non-zero only on diagonals, where it is $\propto\sigma_x\sigma_y$. For the checkerboard the diagonals have $\sigma = +1$, and $\sum\sigma_x\sigma_y = 0$; for the stripes the diagonals have $\sigma = -1$, so $1 + \sigma = 0$.
>
> **3. Diagonal terms.** What remains is $u = \frac{p^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}\bigl[\cos^2\varepsilon\,J_{yy}(\vec 0) + \sin^2\varepsilon\,J_{xx}(\vec q)\bigr]$ with $J_{yy}(\vec 0) = -2 - \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and, from Def. §C3.4.1, $J_{xx}(\pi, \pi) = 4 - 2 - \frac{1}{\sqrt2} = 2 - \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and $J_{xx}(\pi, 0) = 4 + 2 + \frac{1}{\sqrt2} = 6 + \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$. For the stripes, $-(2 + \frac{1}{\sqrt2})\cos^2\varepsilon + (6 + \frac{1}{\sqrt2})\sin^2\varepsilon = 2 - (4 + \frac{1}{\sqrt2})\cos2\varepsilon$, the paper's eq. (72) after multiplying by $\frac12$.
>
> **4. Direct check for the checkerboard at ε = 90°** ($\vec p = \mp p\hat x$ alternating). Edge bonds along $x$ join antiparallel dipoles on the axis: $\frac{p^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}(-1 + 3) = +2$ each in these units. Edge bonds along $y$ are antiparallel side by side: $-1$ each. Diagonal neighbours lie on the **same** sublattice (parallel): $(1 - \frac32)/2\sqrt2 = -\frac{1}{4\sqrt2}$ each. Sum: $4 - 2 - \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, and $u = \frac12(2 - \frac{1}{\sqrt2})\frac{p^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}$, as in step 3.
>
> **5. Monotonicity.** $\frac{du_{\text{chk}}}{d\varepsilon} = \frac{p^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}\,\sin2\varepsilon\bigl[(2 + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}) + (2 - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})\bigr] = \frac{p^2}{2\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}\sin2\varepsilon > 0$ on $(0^\circ, 90^\circ)$, and $\frac{du_{\text{str}}}{d\varepsilon} = \frac{(8 + \sqrt2)p^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}\sin2\varepsilon > 0$ (paper eq. (89)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Each family mixes the uniform mode $\vec q = \vec 0$ with one staggered mode polarized along $x$, at $(\pi, \pi)$ or $(\pi, 0)$, and both of these cost energy. The staggered mode with the lowest eigenvalue, an $x$ component at $(0, \pi)$ or a $y$ component at $(\pi, 0)$, is not in either family, because a modulation symmetric about $90^\circ$ always staggers the $x$ component. This is the gap Theorem §C3.4.8 closes.

^thm-c3-4-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Quadratic form on a periodic block.** Put any periodic configuration on an $L\times L$ block with periodic boundary conditions, $L$ even and a multiple of the periods, $N = L^2$ sites. In units of $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3$, $E = \frac12\sum_i\sum_{\vec\delta \in \mathcal N}\vec p_i\cdot\mathsf J(\vec\delta)\vec p_{i + \vec\delta}$.
>
> **2. Lattice Fourier transform.** For $\vec q \in \frac{2\pi}{L}\{0, \dots, L - 1\}^2$ let $\tilde{\vec p}(\vec q) = \sum_ie^{-i\vec q\cdot\vec r_i}\vec p_i$ (positions in units of $a$). Then $\vec p_i = \frac1N\sum_{\vec q}e^{i\vec q\cdot\vec r_i}\tilde{\vec p}(\vec q)$, using $\sum_ie^{i(\vec q + \vec q\,')\cdot\vec r_i} = N\delta_{\vec q\,', -\vec q}$. Substituting,
>
> $$
> \sum_i\vec p_i\cdot\mathsf J(\vec\delta)\vec p_{i + \vec\delta} = \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{\vec q, \vec q\,'}\tilde{\vec p}(\vec q)\cdot\mathsf J(\vec\delta)\tilde{\vec p}(\vec q\,')\,e^{i\vec q\,'\cdot\vec\delta}\sum_ie^{i(\vec q + \vec q\,')\cdot\vec r_i} = \frac1N\sum_{\vec q}\tilde{\vec p}(\vec q)^*\cdot\mathsf J(\vec\delta)\tilde{\vec p}(\vec q)\,e^{i\vec q\cdot\vec\delta} ,
> $$
>
> with $\tilde{\vec p}(-\vec q) = \tilde{\vec p}(\vec q)^*$ for real $\vec p_i$. Sum over $\vec\delta$. The shell is symmetric under $\vec\delta \to -\vec\delta$ and $\mathsf J(-\vec\delta) = \mathsf J(\vec\delta)$, so the phases combine into the cosines of Def. §C3.4.1: $E = \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\vec q}\tilde{\vec p}(\vec q)^*\cdot\mathsf J(\vec q)\tilde{\vec p}(\vec q)$.
>
> **3. Bound by the lowest eigenvalue.** $\mathsf J(\vec q)$ is real symmetric, so $\tilde{\vec p}^{\ast}\cdot\mathsf J(\vec q)\tilde{\vec p} \ge \lambda_{\min}(\vec q)|\tilde{\vec p}|^2 \ge \lambda_{\ast}|\tilde{\vec p}|^2$, with $\lambda_{\ast} = \min_{\vec q}\lambda_{\min}(\vec q)$ ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]). Parseval, $\sum_{\vec q}|\tilde{\vec p}(\vec q)|^2 = N\sum_i|\vec p_i|^2 = N^2p^2$, gives $E \ge \frac12\lambda_{\ast}Np^2$, i.e. $u \ge \lambda_{\ast}\frac{p^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}$. Note that $|\vec p_i| = p$ was used only through $\sum_i|\vec p_i|^2$.
>
> **4. The lowest eigenvalue is $\frac{1}{\sqrt2} - 6$.** With $c, d \in [-1, 1]$ as in Def. §C3.4.1, $\lambda_{\min} = \frac{J_{xx} + J_{yy}}{2} - \sqrt{\bigl(\frac{J_{xx} - J_{yy}}{2}\bigr)^2 + J_{xy}^2} = -(c + d) - \frac{cd}{\sqrt2} - \sqrt{9(d - c)^2 + \frac92(1 - c^2)(1 - d^2)}$. Let $F = 6 - \frac{1}{\sqrt2} - (c + d) - \frac{cd}{\sqrt2}$. Then $F \ge 4 - \sqrt2 > 0$ on the square, and $\lambda_{\min} \ge \frac{1}{\sqrt2} - 6$ is equivalent to $G \equiv F^2 - 9(c - d)^2 - \frac92(1 - c^2)(1 - d^2) \ge 0$. As a polynomial in $c$, $G$ has the leading coefficient $(1 + \frac{d}{\sqrt2})^2 - 9 + \frac92(1 - d^2) = -4d^2 + \sqrt2\,d - \frac72$, which is negative for every $d$ (discriminant $2 - 56 < 0$). So $G$ is concave in $c$, and by the symmetry $c \leftrightarrow d$ concave in $d$. Its minimum over the square is therefore taken at a corner. At the corners, $G(1, -1) = G(-1, 1) = 6^2 - 9\cdot4 = 0$, $G(1, 1) = (4 - \sqrt2)^2 > 0$, $G(-1, -1) = (8 - \sqrt2)^2 > 0$. A concave function on a segment that is $\ge 0$ at both ends, and $> 0$ at one of them, is $> 0$ inside. So $G \ge 0$, with equality only at $(c, d) = (1, -1)$ and $(-1, 1)$, i.e. $\vec q = (0, \pi)$ and $(\pi, 0)$. There $\mathsf J = \operatorname{diag}(\frac{1}{\sqrt2} - 6, \frac{1}{\sqrt2} + 6)$ and its mirror image: the minimizing eigenvectors are $\hat x$ at $(0, \pi)$ and $\hat y$ at $(\pi, 0)$.
>
> **5. Equality.** Equality in step 3 requires $\tilde{\vec p}(\vec q) = \vec 0$ except at these two points, with $\tilde{\vec p}(0, \pi) \parallel \hat x$ and $\tilde{\vec p}(\pi, 0) \parallel \hat y$. Transforming back, $\vec p_{mn} = (A(-1)^n, B(-1)^m)$ ($e^{i\pi n} = (-1)^n$). Then $|\vec p_{mn}|^2 = A^2 + B^2$ at every site, so the constraint $|\vec p_{mn}| = p$ holds exactly when $A^2 + B^2 = p^2$. The bound is attained, and it is the minimum.
>
> **6. Infinite lattice.** A configuration on $\mathbb Z^2$ is the limit of blocks; restricting it to an $L\times L$ block and closing the boundary periodically changes $E$ only by bonds that cross the boundary, $O(L)$ of them, so $u$ changes by $O(1/L)$ and the bound holds for the limit.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The ground-state manifold is a circle, $A^2 + B^2 = p^2$. The $x$-chain pattern ($\alpha = 0$) and the $y$-chain pattern ($\alpha = 90^\circ$) are the two degenerate eigenvectors, and every superposition of them also satisfies the length constraint. Zangwill's "find the α that minimizes the energy" has the answer: every α (Example §C3.4.2).
> - ⚑ The degeneracy has the same origin as the paper's Theorem 5.3: $90^\circ$ rotation maps the $(0, \pi)$ eigenvalue onto the $(\pi, 0)$ one, as it made $\mathsf J(\vec 0)$ a multiple of $\mathbb 1$.
> - The diagonal bonds are frustrated in the ground state: they contribute $+\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, while the edge bonds give $-6$. The edge bonds alone would prefer the same pattern.
> - Tilting the dipoles out of the plane does not help. The 3×3 transform is block diagonal, since $\hat\delta$ lies in the plane, and its $zz$ entry $J_{zz}(\vec q) = 2c + 2d + \sqrt2\,cd$ is bilinear in $(c, d)$, with minimum $-4 + \sqrt2 > \frac{1}{\sqrt2} - 6$ at the corner $(\pi, \pi)$. So the bound and its minimizers are the same for three-dimensional dipoles.
> - *Beyond the model (computed here).* Without the truncation, the lattice sums converge absolutely (Model §C3.4.4). Summed to radius 400 (stable to $10^{-4}$ from radius 50), the lowest eigenvalue is still at $(0, \pi)$ and $(\pi, 0)$, now $-5.099$ in units of $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3$. So the same family $(A(-1)^n, B(-1)^m)$ is the ground state, with $u = -2.549\,p^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3 \approx -0.637\,p^2/\pi\varepsilon_0a^3$. The uniform states have $u = -2.258\,p^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3$. The truncation changes the numbers but not the order.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C3.4.1: Monolayer Dipole Drops Have a Preferred Width
> Polar molecules at an air–water interface, all pointing out of the surface, condense into long rectangular drops one molecule thick, of length $L$ and width $w$ with $L \gg w \gg a$ ($a$ the molecular spacing; Zangwill Figure 4.6). Each drop has a perimeter energy $\lambda$ per length and a dipole density $\tau = p/a^2$ ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]). Show that the equilibrium width does not depend on $L$.
>
> *Two rows.* A row of length $L$ carries dipole moment $\tau a$ per length, normal to the plane, so only the $\vec p_i\cdot\vec p_j$ term of [[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]] survives (the separations lie in the plane). Two parallel rows a distance $y$ apart interact with (Zangwill eq. (4.48))
>
> $$
> U(y) = \frac{\tau^2a^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^L\!\!\int_0^L\frac{dx_1\,dx_2}{[(x_1 - x_2)^2 + y^2]^{3/2}} = \frac{\tau^2a^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2}{y^2}\bigl(\sqrt{L^2 + y^2} - y\bigr) \approx \frac{\tau^2a^2L}{2\pi\varepsilon_0y^2} ,
> $$
>
> the double integral being $2\int_0^L(L - v)(v^2 + y^2)^{-3/2}dv = 2\bigl[\frac{Lv}{y^2\sqrt{v^2 + y^2}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{v^2 + y^2}}\bigr]_0^L$.
>
> *All rows.* With $N = w/a$ rows, $N - n$ pairs are $na$ apart, so $U_E = \frac{\tau^2L}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{n = 1}^{N - 1}\frac{N - n}{n^2}$. Zangwill replaces the sum by $\int_1^N\frac{N - n}{n^2}dn = N - 1 - \ln N$ (eq. (4.49) keeps $N - \ln N$). The perimeter energy is $U_C \approx 2L\lambda = 2\lambda A/w$ with the area $A = Lw$ fixed (eq. (4.47)). With $\kappa = \tau^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ and $L = A/w$,
>
> $$
> U_T = \frac{2A}{w}\Bigl[\lambda + \kappa\Bigl(\frac wa - 1 - \ln\frac wa\Bigr)\Bigr], \qquad \frac{dU_T}{dw} = \frac{2A}{w^2}\Bigl[\kappa\ln\frac wa - \lambda\Bigr] = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; w_0 = a\,e^{4\pi\varepsilon_0\lambda/\tau^2} ,
> $$
>
> independent of $L$ (Zangwill eq. (4.51)). The term linear in $w$ inside the bracket gives an $A$-proportional constant and does not affect $w_0$. Only the $w$-independent constants in the bracket set the prefactor. With Zangwill's $N - \ln N$ (eq. (4.50) as printed) the minimum is at $a\,e^{1 + 4\pi\varepsilon_0\lambda/\tau^2}$. The exact sum, $N\pi^2/6 - \ln N - 1 - \gamma + O(1/N)$, gives $a\,e^{-\gamma}e^{4\pi\varepsilon_0\lambda/\tau^2}$, and the continuum approximation for the nearest rows changes it again. The robust result is $w_0 = c\,a\,e^{4\pi\varepsilon_0\lambda/\tau^2}$ with $c$ of order one. The exponential dependence is typical of two-dimensional dipole systems, and further molecules form new drops of width $w_0$ instead of widening old ones (Zangwill).
>
> *Source: Zangwill Application 4.1, eqs. (4.47)–(4.51), Figures 4.6, 4.9 (the integral, the sums and the prefactor discussion are worked here)*

> [!example] Example §C3.4.2: Adsorbed CO and N₂
> (a) CO molecules, point dipoles $p$, occupy the centres of an $a\times a$ checkerboard in the pattern of Zangwill's sketch, interacting with their eight nearest neighbours. Find the α that minimizes the energy and show that the energy per dipole is $\frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p^2}{a^3}\bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt2} - 6\bigr)$. (b) N₂ has a small quadrupole moment. Sketch the orientational order.
>
> *(a)* Read off the sketch, with $(0, 0)$ its middle site, $m$ counting columns and $n$ rows: $\vec p_{mn} = p(\cos\alpha\,(-1)^n, \sin\alpha\,(-1)^m)$. Write $C = \cos\alpha$, $S = \sin\alpha$ and use units $p^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3$ with $U = \vec p_0\cdot\vec p_j - 3(\vec p_0\cdot\hat r)(\vec p_j\cdot\hat r)$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]]). Site $(0, 0)$ has $\vec p_0 = (C, S)$.
> - Edge neighbours $(\pm1, 0)$: $\vec p = (C, -S)$, $\hat r = \pm\hat x$: $C^2 - S^2 - 3C^2 = -2C^2 - S^2$ each.
> - Edge neighbours $(0, \pm1)$: $\vec p = (-C, S)$, $\hat r = \pm\hat y$: $-C^2 + S^2 - 3S^2 = -C^2 - 2S^2$ each.
> - Diagonal neighbours $(\sigma_x, \sigma_y)$, distance $\sqrt2$: $\vec p = (-C, -S) = -\vec p_0$, so $\vec p_0\cdot\vec p_j = -C^2 - S^2 = -1$ and $\vec p_j\cdot\hat r = -\vec p_0\cdot\hat r$, with $(\vec p_0\cdot\hat r)^2 = \frac12(C\sigma_x + S\sigma_y)^2 = \frac12(1 + 2CS\sigma_x\sigma_y)$. Each gives $\frac{1}{2\sqrt2}\bigl[-1 + \frac32(1 + 2CS\sigma_x\sigma_y)\bigr]$, and the $\sigma_x\sigma_y$ terms cancel over the four.
>
> Sum: $2(-2C^2 - S^2) + 2(-C^2 - 2S^2) + \frac{1}{2\sqrt2}\cdot4\cdot\frac12 = -6 + \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, **independent of α**. Per dipole (half of each bond): $u = \frac{p^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}\bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt2} - 6\bigr)$, as stated. Every α minimizes. By [[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-8|Theorem §C3.4.8]] this is the global minimum of the model, and the α-family is exactly its set of minimizers (up to sign changes of $A$, $B$ and translations).
>
> *(b)* For like linear quadrupoles the pair energy is lowest in the T shape, the end of one at the middle of the other ([[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]]). If the negative charge builds up in the bond region (Zangwill), the positive ends seek the negative middles of the neighbours, and the sign of Θ does not change the picture. On the square lattice every edge bond can be made T-shaped at once: molecules on one sublattice along $x$, on the other along $y$ (figure, panel (c)). For the edge-neighbour model this is the exact ground state (★ remark below).
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 8, Problem 3 (Zangwill Problem 4.13); solved here*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The paper's N₂ extension, recomputed
> **The paper's setup** (§8.1–§8.2). N₂ is modelled as a linear quadrupole $[+q, -2q, +q]$, with the lab-frame tensor $Q\begin{pmatrix}\cos2\alpha & \sin2\alpha & 0\\ \sin2\alpha & -\cos2\alpha & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ (eq. (99)). Only the four edge neighbours are kept, because diagonal ones are weaker by $(1/\sqrt2)^5 \approx 0.18$ (Assumption 8.3). The pair energy is taken as $U = \frac{3Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^5}f$ with $f = 1 - 5\cos^2(\alpha_1 - \phi) - 5\cos^2(\alpha_2 - \phi) + 17\cos^2(\alpha_1 - \phi)\cos^2(\alpha_2 - \phi) + 2\cos(\alpha_1 - \alpha_2)$ (eqs. (102)–(103)). The framework of §1–§4 (translations, $C_{4v}$, equilibrium conditions) transfers unchanged (§8.1.1).
>
> **The paper's results** (§8.3–§8.8; energies per site in $Q^2/\pi\varepsilon_0a^5$). Uniform: $u = \frac{3(35 + 17\cos4\alpha_0)}{16}$, lowest at $\alpha_0 = 45^\circ$ with $u = \frac{27}{8} \approx +3.38$, repulsive (Theorem 8.5, eq. (113)). The continuous degeneracy of the dipole case is broken by the non-vanishing $\cos4\alpha_0$ sum. Checkerboard about $45^\circ$: $u = \frac38(17\cos^22\varepsilon + 8\cos2\varepsilon - 16)$ (eq. (116)). The uniform state is unstable toward it ($u'' = -63$ in these units at $\varepsilon = 0$, Theorem 8.9). The optimum is at $\cos2\varepsilon_{\ast} = -\frac{4}{17}$, $\varepsilon_{\ast} \approx 51.8^\circ$, neighbours differ by about $104^\circ$, and $u = -\frac{108}{17} \approx -6.35$ (eqs. (121)–(123)). The optimal stripe is quoted as $\approx +4.9$ at $\varepsilon \approx 30.1^\circ$ (eq. (126)). Ground state: the $104^\circ$ checkerboard (Theorem 8.14), justified by the bipartite structure (Lemma 8.12, Corollary 8.13).
>
> **Recomputed from the paper's own $f$** (edge neighbours, as in the paper). The values $\frac{39}{4}$, $\frac{27}{8}$, eq. (116), $u'' = -63$, $\varepsilon_{\ast} = 51.8^\circ$ and $-\frac{108}{17}$ all follow from eq. (103). Random-start searches on periodic 2×2, 4×4 and 6×6 cells found nothing below $-\frac{108}{17}$. The stripe value does not follow. At $\alpha_0 = 45^\circ$ the two kinds of stripe are inequivalent: a site at $45^\circ + \varepsilon$ and one at $45^\circ - \varepsilon$ have different same-stripe bonds. The quoted $+4.9$ at $\varepsilon \approx 30.1^\circ$ (eq. (126)) is reproduced exactly by the energy of a site on an *even* stripe alone, the site the paper's §8.4.5 describes, and it is the **maximum** of that function (its only interior stationary point, $4.898$ at $30.13^\circ$). The energy per site of paper eq. (8) averages the two stripes. It falls monotonically from $\frac{27}{8}$ at $\varepsilon = 0$ to $\frac38$ at $\varepsilon = 90^\circ$, and $\frac38$ is also its minimum over all pairs of stripe angles. So within the paper's own model the order is checkerboard ($-6.35$) < stripe ($+0.375$) < uniform ($+3.38$), not the order checkerboard < uniform < stripe of §8.5.
>
> **Recomputed with the interaction of two linear quadrupoles.** Eq. (103) is not that interaction. Internal evidence: $f$ changes when one molecule is turned by $180^\circ$ (e.g. $f(0.3, 1.1) = -0.006$ but $f(0.3 + \pi, 1.1) = -2.79$ in radians), so it contradicts the paper's own head–tail symmetry (Remark 8.2). In the planar form of [[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]], $g = 1 - 5c_1^2 - 5c_2^2 + 17c_1^2c_2^2 + 2s_1^2s_2^2 - 16s_1c_1s_2c_2$. The first four terms agree with $f$, but the last term must be $2\sin^2\theta_1\sin^2\theta_2 - 4\sin2\theta_1\sin2\theta_2$, not $2\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)$. The general $g$ agrees with the exact Coulomb energy of two $+q, -2q, +q$ triples to $10^{-4}$ at separation $200d$, for random orientations. The prefactor is $\frac{3\Theta^2}{4\cdot4\pi\varepsilon_0r^5}$ with $\Theta = 2qd^2$. The paper does not fix the normalization of its $Q$, so its energies (in $Q^2/\pi\varepsilon_0a^5$) and those below (in $\Theta^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0a^5$) should not be compared number for number. Eq. (99) is a two-dimensional traceless tensor. The three-dimensional moment is $\Theta_{ij} = \frac\Theta2(3n_in_j - \delta_{ij})$, which has $\Theta_{zz} = -\Theta/2$ and in-plane trace $\Theta/2$. The paper never uses (99) in a calculation, so this is a point of presentation. With the correct $g$, edge neighbours only (the paper's Assumption 8.3), per site in units of $\Theta^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0a^5$:
> - Uniform: $u(\alpha_0) = \frac{27 + 105\cos4\alpha_0}{16}$, from $\frac{33}{4}$ at $\alpha_0 = 0$ down to $-\frac{39}{8}$ at $\alpha_0 = 45^\circ$: the best uniform state is **attractive**.
> - Checkerboard about $45^\circ$: $u(\varepsilon) = -\frac{39}{8} - \frac98\sin^22\varepsilon$. The uniform state is indeed unstable toward checkerboard modulation, $u''(0) = -9$, so the paper's qualitative claim stands. But the optimum is $\varepsilon = 45^\circ$: the sublattices lie along the two lattice axes ($\alpha_A = 90^\circ$, $\alpha_B = 0^\circ$), neighbours are **perpendicular** ($90^\circ$, not $104^\circ$), every bond is a T, and $u = -6$.
> - Ground state. By Theorem §C3.4.3, $g \ge -4$ for every planar pair, so every configuration has $u \ge \frac12\cdot4\cdot\frac34\cdot(-4) = -6$. The T-checkerboard attains this on every bond, so it is the exact ground state of the edge-neighbour model. This makes the paper's bipartite argument precise: on a bipartite lattice each bond can be optimized at once because the optimal bond is realized compatibly at every site.
> - Stripes do not help: the best stripe of the correct model (over all pairs of stripe angles) is the uniform $45^\circ$ state itself, $-\frac{39}{8}$.
> - Random-start minimizations on periodic $L\times L$ cells confirm $-6$ for $L = 2, 4, 6, 8$ ($L = 3$, which cannot hold a checkerboard, gives $-\frac{39}{8}$). Beyond the paper's model, with the diagonal neighbours added the minimum is still the T-checkerboard, $u = -6.862$ ($L = 2, 4, 6, 8$). With all neighbours to $|m|, |n| \le 12$ it is still the T-checkerboard, $u = -6.524$ ($L = 2, 4, 6$).
>
> The sign of N₂'s quadrupole moment is irrelevant here, since $U \propto \Theta^2$.

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The energy of a body in a field, $Q V - \vec p\cdot\vec E - \frac13\Theta_{ij}\partial_iE_j$, is the multipole expansion of [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]] run the other way, with the roles of source and field exchanged. In quantum mechanics it becomes the Stark and hyperfine-quadrupole Hamiltonians, whose matrix elements obey the Wigner–Eckart theorem ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-6|QM Theorem §C7.2.6]]).
> - Energies of many polarizable dipoles, with the factor $\frac12$ for induced moments, are the subject of [[§C5.4 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant|§C5.4]] and [[§C5.5 Energy in Dielectrics|§C5.5]]. A dipole layer's energy (Example §C3.4.1) is the two-dimensional version.
> - The Fourier diagonalization of a translation-invariant quadratic form (Theorem §C3.4.8) is the same move as the mode expansion of a free field ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra|QFT §C2a.2]]), or the normal modes of a lattice. The bound by the lowest eigenvalue is the variational principle for a quadratic form ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]).
