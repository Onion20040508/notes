*Sources: Zangwill, Modern Electrodynamics, §4.1–§4.4, §4.7 (Application 4.5), Problems 4.3, 4.9, 4.10, 4.11, 4.16 · Jackson, Classical Electrodynamics (3rd ed.), §4.1 · PHY 505, HW 8, Problem 2.*

What does a distant observer learn about a localized charge distribution, and how are pointlike and sheetlike sources described exactly? Level B expanded $1/\mathcal R$ in Legendre polynomials and read off the monopole, dipole and quadrupole terms ([[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]], [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]]), and treated the physical dipole and its field ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^mod-b4-2-1|Model §B4.2.1]], [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]]). Level C writes the same series as a Taylor series in the source point. Each term is then a tensor built from the source alone, contracted with derivatives of $1/r$ (Zangwill's *primitive Cartesian* expansion). Point multipoles become distributions, derivatives of δ, so the contact term of the dipole field is derived rather than inferred. The section also treats dipole layers, across which the potential jumps. Notation: the potential is $V$ (Zangwill writes φ; [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: The potential is V; Zangwill writes φ]]). Repeated Cartesian indices are summed, $\partial_i = \partial/\partial x_i$ acts on the field point, $r = |\vec r|$.

## The primitive Cartesian expansion

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.1: Primitive Cartesian Multipole Moments
> Let $\rho$ be confined to the ball $r \le R$ about the origin. Its **primitive Cartesian moment of order ℓ** is the symmetric tensor (Zangwill eq. (4.120))
>
> $$
> C^{(\ell)}_{i_1\cdots i_\ell} = \frac{1}{\ell!}\int d^3r\,\rho(\vec r)\,x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_\ell} .
> $$
>
> The first three have names (eqs. (4.4)–(4.6)): the **monopole moment** (total charge) $Q = C^{(0)}$, the **dipole moment** $\vec p = \vec C^{(1)}$, and the **primitive quadrupole moment**
>
> $$
> Q_{ij} = C^{(2)}_{ij} = \frac12\int d^3r\,\rho(\vec r)\,x_ix_j = \frac12\sum_\alpha q_\alpha x_{\alpha i}x_{\alpha j}\quad\text{(point charges } q_\alpha \text{ at } \vec r_\alpha) .
> $$
>
> $Q$ and $\vec p$ are those of [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]]. $Q_{ij}$ carries a factor $\tfrac12$ and **no trace subtraction**; other books normalize the quadrupole differently ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^cau-c3-1-1|Caution: Quadrupole normalizations]]); the traceless moments are defined in [[§C3.3 Primitive and Traceless Moments#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]. Order of magnitude: $p \sim QR$, $Q_{ij} \sim QR^2$, $C^{(\ell)} \sim QR^\ell$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §4.1.1, eqs. (4.4)–(4.6); §4.7, eq. (4.120)*

> [!caution] Caution: Quadrupole normalizations
> Four normalizations of the $\ell = 2$ moment are in use. With $\Theta_{ij} = 3Q_{ij} - Q_{kk}\delta_{ij}$ the traceless moment of [[§C3.3 Primitive and Traceless Moments#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]], the quadrupole potential is the same function in every row:
>
> | Source | Symbol | Definition | Quadrupole term of $4\pi\varepsilon_0V$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Zangwill, primitive (4.6) | $Q_{ij}$ | $\tfrac12\int\rho\,x_ix_j$ | $Q_{ij}\,(3x_ix_j - r^2\delta_{ij})/r^5$ |
> | Zangwill, traceless (4.63) | $\Theta_{ij}$ | $\tfrac12\int\rho\,(3x_ix_j - r^2\delta_{ij}) = 3Q_{ij} - Q_{kk}\delta_{ij}$ | $\Theta_{ij}\,x_ix_j/r^5$ |
> | Griffiths, Problem 3.52 | $Q_{ij}$ | $\tfrac12\int(3r_i'r_j' - r'^2\delta_{ij})\rho = \Theta_{ij}$ | $\sum\hat r_i\hat r_jQ_{ij}/r^3$ |
> | Jackson, eqs. (4.9)–(4.10); Nelson, eqs. (3.3)–(3.4) | $Q_{ij}$ | $\int(3x_ix_j - r^2\delta_{ij})\rho = 2\Theta_{ij}$ | $\tfrac12\sum Q_{ij}\,x_ix_j/r^5$ |
>
> - In this vault $Q_{ij}$ always means Zangwill's *primitive* moment, which has a trace, $Q_{kk} = \tfrac12\int\rho r^2$. Level B's ★ remark on the quadrupole tensor ([[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^rem-b4-1-3|§B4.1, ★ Remark: The quadrupole moment tensor]]) uses Griffiths' $Q_{ij}$, which is the $\Theta_{ij}$ here.
> - Zangwill writes the tensor in bold, $\mathbf Q$, next to the total charge $Q$ (his footnote 10), and calls the single number $\Theta_{zz}$ of an axially symmetric nucleus "$Q$" (Application 4.2).
> - Both of Zangwill's normalizations keep the factor $\tfrac12$ of the Taylor series. With it, force and energy in a field read $Q_{ij}\partial_i\partial_j\vec E$ and $-Q_{ij}\partial_iE_j$ without numerical factors ([[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]]). Jackson's and Nelson's moments are twice as large, and their formulas carry the $\tfrac12$ instead.
>
> *Source: Zangwill eqs. (4.6), (4.52), (4.63)–(4.64), footnote 10 · Griffiths Problem 3.52 (as quoted in EM §B4.1) · Jackson §4.1, eqs. (4.9)–(4.10) · Nelson ch. 3, eqs. (3.3)–(3.4) (as quoted in EM §B4.1)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.1: The Primitive Cartesian Multipole Expansion
> For $\rho$ confined to $r' \le R$ and a field point with $r > R$ (Zangwill eqs. (4.3), (4.7), (4.119), (4.121)),
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{\ell = 0}^\infty C^{(\ell)}_{i_1\cdots i_\ell}\,N^{(\ell)}_{i_1\cdots i_\ell}(\vec r), \qquad N^{(\ell)}_{i_1\cdots i_\ell}(\vec r) = (-1)^\ell\,\partial_{i_1}\cdots\partial_{i_\ell}\frac1r ,
> $$
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac Qr + \frac{\vec p\cdot\vec r}{r^3} + Q_{ij}\,\frac{3x_ix_j - r^2\delta_{ij}}{r^5} + \cdots\right] .
> $$
>
> The series converges absolutely, the ℓth term is at most $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{R^\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}\int|\rho|$, and the ℓth term equals the ℓth Legendre term of [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]]. Each term is a moment of the source (Def. §C3.1.1) contracted with a function of the field point alone.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §4.1.1, eqs. (4.2)–(4.7); §4.7, eqs. (4.119)–(4.121) · Jackson §4.1, eq. (4.10)*

^thm-c3-1-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Taylor series in the source point.** Fix $\vec r \ne \vec 0$ and let $g(t) = 1/|\vec r - t\vec r\,'|$ for real $t$ with $|t|\,r' < r$. It is smooth, and by the chain rule $g^{(\ell)}(0) = (\vec r\,'\cdot\nabla')^\ell\,|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^{-1}\big|_{\vec r\,' = 0}$, where $\nabla'$ differentiates with respect to the source point. Since $|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^{-1}$ depends on $\vec r - \vec r\,'$ only, $\nabla' = -\nabla$ on it, so (Zangwill eq. (4.2); the several-variable Taylor theorem, [[§11 Taylor's Theorem for Multivariable Functions#^thm-11-2|452 Thm. §11.2]], along the line $t\vec r\,'$)
>
> $$
> \frac{g^{(\ell)}(0)}{\ell!} = \frac{(-1)^\ell}{\ell!}\,(\vec r\,'\cdot\nabla)^\ell\,\frac1r = \frac{(-1)^\ell}{\ell!}\,x'_{i_1}\cdots x'_{i_\ell}\,\partial_{i_1}\cdots\partial_{i_\ell}\frac1r .
> $$
>
> **2. The same function as a Legendre series.** With $\cos\gamma = \hat r\cdot\hat r\,'$, $g(t) = \frac1r\bigl(1 - 2t\frac{r'}{r}\cos\gamma + t^2\frac{r'^2}{r^2}\bigr)^{-1/2} = \sum_\ell t^\ell\,\frac{r'^\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}P_\ell(\cos\gamma)$ for $|t|\,r'/r < 1$ ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-4|Theorem §B3.4.4]] (1) with $x = \cos\gamma$, $t \to tr'/r$). A power series in $t$ has the coefficients $g^{(\ell)}(0)/\ell!$ ([[§26 Differentiation and Integration of Power Series#^thm-26-4|451 Thm. §26.4]]), so for every ℓ
>
> $$
> \frac{(-1)^\ell}{\ell!}\,(\vec r\,'\cdot\nabla)^\ell\,\frac1r = \frac{r'^\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}\,P_\ell(\hat r\cdot\hat r\,') .
> $$
>
> ⚑ By-product: this identity is the generating function of the Legendre polynomials in Cartesian form; it is what makes the Cartesian and Legendre expansions agree term by term. Its traceless structure is [[§C3.3 Primitive and Traceless Moments#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]].
>
> **3. Convergence at $t = 1$.** Since $|P_\ell| \le 1$ ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-4|Theorem §B3.4.4]] (3)), the ℓth term is at most $R^\ell/r^{\ell + 1}$ for all $r' \le R$. So the series at $t = 1$ converges to $1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$, uniformly in $\vec r\,'$ on the ball (M-test, [[§25 More on Uniform Convergence#^thm-25-3|451 Thm. §25.3]]).
>
> **4. Integrate against ρ.** Uniform convergence allows term-by-term integration against $\rho$ ([[§25 More on Uniform Convergence#^thm-25-1|451 Thm. §25.1]]); $\vec r$ is a constant in the integration over $\vec r\,'$, so the derivatives of $1/r$ come out of the integral:
>
> $$
> \int d^3r'\,\rho(\vec r\,')\,\frac{(-1)^\ell}{\ell!}x'_{i_1}\cdots x'_{i_\ell}\,\partial_{i_1}\cdots\partial_{i_\ell}\frac1r = C^{(\ell)}_{i_1\cdots i_\ell}\,(-1)^\ell\partial_{i_1}\cdots\partial_{i_\ell}\frac1r = C^{(\ell)}\cdot N^{(\ell)} .
> $$
>
> With $V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\rho/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]]) this is the first formula. The bound follows from step 3.
>
> **5. The first three derivatives.** $\partial_ir = x_i/r$, so $\partial_i\frac1r = -\frac{x_i}{r^3}$, and by the product rule $\partial_j\partial_i\frac1r = -\frac{\delta_{ij}}{r^3} + \frac{3x_ix_j}{r^5} = \frac{3x_ix_j - r^2\delta_{ij}}{r^5}$ ($r \ne 0$). Hence $N^{(0)} = 1/r$, $N^{(1)}_i = x_i/r^3$, $N^{(2)}_{ij} = (3x_ix_j - r^2\delta_{ij})/r^5$, and the three terms are $Q/r$, $p_ix_i/r^3$ and $Q_{ij}(3x_ix_j - r^2\delta_{ij})/r^5$ (Zangwill eq. (4.7)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Level B's series and the primitive Cartesian series are one series written in two ways (step 2); convergence is inherited from the Legendre form.
> - The source enters only through the numbers $C^{(\ell)}$, the field point only through $N^{(\ell)}$: the moments can be computed once, in any coordinates (Zangwill: the factors $x_ix_j$ do not force Cartesian integration).
> - The ℓ = 2 term has the primitive $Q_{ij}$, with its trace. The trace drops out of the potential because $N^{(2)}$ is traceless; [[§C3.3 Primitive and Traceless Moments|§C3.3]] makes this the general rule.

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level B; origin dependence at every order
> - **Same result, new form (rule 2).** [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]] gave the series with $\int r'^\ell P_\ell(\cos\alpha)\rho$, an integral that still depends on the direction of the field point. Here the dependence on direction is moved into $N^{(\ell)}(\vec r)$, and the source is summarized by $C^{(\ell)}$, computed once. The monopole and dipole terms are level B's ([[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]]); the quadrupole term in level B's ★ remark is the traceless form of [[§C3.3 Primitive and Traceless Moments|§C3.3]].
> - **Origin dependence.** Shifting the origin by $\vec d$ ($\vec r = \bar{\vec r} + \vec d$) gives $\bar{\vec p} = \vec p - Q\vec d$ (Zangwill eq. (4.9); level B's [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]]). In general, expanding $(x - d)_{i_1}\cdots(x - d)_{i_\ell}$ by the binomial rule gives $\bar C^{(\ell)} = C^{(\ell)} + {}$(terms each containing one lower moment $C^{(k)}$, $k < \ell$, times $\ell - k$ components of $\vec d$). So **the lowest non-vanishing moment does not depend on the origin**; in particular $Q_{ij}$ is unique when $Q = 0$ and $\vec p = \vec 0$ (Zangwill §4.4).
>
> *Source: Zangwill eq. (4.9), §4.4*

## Point multipoles as distributions

> [!model] Model §C3.1.2: Point Dipole as a Distribution
> Maxwell's construction: charges $+q/s$ at $\vec r_0 + s\vec b$ and $-q/s$ at $\vec r_0$, in the limit $s \to 0$ with $\vec p = q\vec b$ fixed. The limit is a **point dipole** with charge density (Zangwill eqs. (4.13)–(4.14), Problem 4.3)
>
> $$
> \rho_D(\vec r) = -\vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0), \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \rho_D[f] = \vec p\cdot\nabla f(\vec r_0)\ \text{ for every test function } f ,
> $$
>
> a first derivative of δ in the sense of distributions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]). Its potential is, **exactly** for every $\vec r \ne \vec r_0$,
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\vec p\cdot\nabla\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r_0|} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\vec p\cdot(\vec r - \vec r_0)}{|\vec r - \vec r_0|^3} ,
> $$
>
> and $-\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V = \rho_D$ holds as an identity of distributions. About $\vec r_0$ it has $Q = 0$, dipole moment $\vec p$, and all $C^{(\ell \ge 2)} = 0$. *Assumptions:* as for level B's pure dipole ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^mod-b4-2-1|Model §B4.2.1]]): it represents a neutral body at distances large compared with its size. What is new is the charge density, which lets the point dipole enter Poisson's equation, integrals of energy and force, and sums over many dipoles. No point dipole with this density is known to exist in nature (Zangwill).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §4.2.1, eqs. (4.13)–(4.14), Figure 4.5; Problem 4.3 (solved here)*

^mod-c3-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The density of the pair.** $\rho_s = \frac qs\bigl[\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0 - s\vec b) - \delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)\bigr]$. Paired with a test function $f$ (smooth, compact support; [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-2|QFT Def. §CA.2.2]]):
>
> $$
> \rho_s[f] = \frac qs\bigl[f(\vec r_0 + s\vec b) - f(\vec r_0)\bigr] \xrightarrow{s \to 0} q\,\vec b\cdot\nabla f(\vec r_0) = \vec p\cdot\nabla f(\vec r_0) ,
> $$
>
> by the definition of the directional derivative. So $\rho_s$ converges as a distribution ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-5|QFT Theorem §CA.2.5]]).
>
> **2. Identify the limit.** By the derivative of a distribution, $(\partial_i\delta_{\vec r_0})[f] = -\delta_{\vec r_0}[\partial_if] = -\partial_if(\vec r_0)$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]). Hence $(-p_i\partial_i\delta_{\vec r_0})[f] = p_i\partial_if(\vec r_0)$, the limit of step 1 (Problem 4.3(a)).
>
> **3. The potential (Problem 4.3(b)).** For $\vec r \ne \vec r_0$ the kernel $h(\vec a) = 1/|\vec r - \vec a|$ is smooth for $\vec a$ near $\vec r_0$, and $\rho_D$ only sees a neighbourhood of $\vec r_0$, so the pairing $\rho_D[h]$ is defined. It gives $\vec p\cdot\nabla_{\vec a}h(\vec r_0) = \vec p\cdot(\vec r - \vec r_0)/|\vec r - \vec r_0|^3$, since $\nabla_{\vec a}|\vec r - \vec a|^{-1} = +(\vec r - \vec a)/|\vec r - \vec a|^3$. Times $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ this is the stated $V$, and $\nabla_{\vec a}h = -\nabla h$ gives the form $-\vec p\cdot\nabla(1/|\vec r - \vec r_0|)$. Directly from the pair: by Taylor's theorem $\frac{q}{s}[h(\vec r_0 + s\vec b) - h(\vec r_0)] = q\vec b\cdot\nabla_{\vec a}h(\vec r_0) + O(qs)$, and $O(qs) = O(ps/b) \to 0$. ⚑ By-product: all higher terms of the Taylor series vanish in the limit, so the dipole formula is exact for a point dipole (Zangwill), not an approximation.
>
> **4. Poisson's equation.** $V$ is locally integrable, so it is a distribution, and derivatives of distributions commute. With $\nabla^2(1/|\vec r - \vec r_0|) = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]):
>
> $$
> -\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V = \frac{1}{4\pi}\,\vec p\cdot\nabla\,\nabla^2\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r_0|} = \frac{1}{4\pi}\,\vec p\cdot\nabla\bigl(-4\pi\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)\bigr) = -\vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0) = \rho_D \quad\text{(Zangwill eq. (4.14))}.
> $$
>
> **5. Moments.** With $\vec y = \vec r - \vec r_0$: $Q = \rho_D[1] = \vec p\cdot\nabla1 = 0$; $\rho_D[y_i] = p_j\partial_jy_i = p_i$; for ℓ ≥ 2 the gradient of $y_{i_1}\cdots y_{i_\ell}$ vanishes at $\vec y = \vec 0$, so $C^{(\ell)} = 0$. (These pairings use polynomials, which are not compactly supported; multiply by a cutoff equal to 1 near $\vec r_0$, which $\rho_D$ does not see.)
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The point dipole is a definite distribution, the limit of the pair in the weak sense; nothing about it is approximate.
> - ⚑ $\vec E = -\nabla V$ must also be taken as a distribution, and then it contains a δ at $\vec r_0$ → [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]].

*Uses:* [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-2|QFT Def. §CA.2.2]], [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]], [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-5|QFT Theorem §CA.2.5]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.3: Second Derivatives of 1/r as a Distribution
> As distributions on $\mathbb R^3$,
>
> $$
> \partial_i\partial_j\frac1r = \operatorname{PV}\frac{3x_ix_j - r^2\delta_{ij}}{r^5} - \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta^3(\vec r), \qquad \operatorname{PV}K[f] \equiv \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0}\int_{r > \varepsilon}d^3r\,K(\vec r)\,f(\vec r) .
> $$
>
> The principal value exists because the angular average of $(3x_ix_j - r^2\delta_{ij})/r^5$ over every sphere about the origin is zero. The trace reproduces $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec r)$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]). The coefficient $-\frac{4\pi}{3}\delta_{ij}$ belongs to the *spherical* exclusion in PV. If a small region of another shape is cut out instead, the coefficient becomes $-4\pi\mathcal N_{ij}$, with $\mathcal N_{ij} = \frac{1}{4\pi}\oint n_jx_i/r^3\,dS$ over the boundary of the excised region (trace 1; $\mathcal N_{ij} = \frac13\delta_{ij}$ for a sphere), and the sum of the two parts is unchanged.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §4.2.2, eq. (4.16), footnote 1 (Zangwill obtains the δ term from the volume average, eq. (4.15)); the derivation by excision is written here*

^thm-c3-1-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Definition.** $1/r$ is locally integrable, hence a distribution, and two derivatives move onto the test function with two signs $-1$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]): $\partial_i\partial_j\frac1r[f] = \int d^3r\,\frac1r\,\partial_i\partial_jf = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0}\int_{r > \varepsilon}\frac1r\,\partial_i\partial_jf$ (the integrand is integrable, so cutting out the ball $r \le \varepsilon$ costs nothing in the limit; [[§CA.1 Exchanging Limits, Derivatives and Integrals#^thm-ca-1-1|QFT Theorem §CA.1.1]]).
>
> **2. First integration by parts on $V_\varepsilon = \{r > \varepsilon\}$.** $f$ has compact support, so the only boundary is the sphere $r = \varepsilon$, whose outward normal (out of $V_\varepsilon$) is $\vec n = -\hat r$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-3|QFT Theorem §CA.2.3]]):
>
> $$
> \int_{V_\varepsilon}\frac1r\,\partial_i(\partial_jf) = \oint_{r = \varepsilon}\frac1r\,(\partial_jf)\,n_i\,dS - \int_{V_\varepsilon}\Bigl(\partial_i\frac1r\Bigr)\partial_jf .
> $$
>
> The boundary term is at most $\frac1\varepsilon\sup|\nabla f|\cdot4\pi\varepsilon^2 \to 0$: dropped.
>
> **3. Second integration by parts.** With $\partial_i\frac1r = -x_i/r^3$, $n_j = -x_j/\varepsilon$ and $dS = \varepsilon^2d\Omega$ on the sphere:
>
> $$
> -\int_{V_\varepsilon}\Bigl(\partial_i\frac1r\Bigr)\partial_jf = -\oint_{r = \varepsilon}\Bigl(-\frac{x_i}{\varepsilon^3}\Bigr)f\Bigl(-\frac{x_j}{\varepsilon}\Bigr)\varepsilon^2d\Omega + \int_{V_\varepsilon}f\,\partial_j\partial_i\frac1r = -\oint\hat r_i\hat r_j\,f(\varepsilon\hat r)\,d\Omega + \int_{V_\varepsilon}f\,\frac{3x_ix_j - r^2\delta_{ij}}{r^5} .
> $$
>
> **4. The surface term gives the δ.** As $\varepsilon \to 0$, $f(\varepsilon\hat r) \to f(0)$ uniformly, and $\oint\hat r_i\hat r_j\,d\Omega = \frac{4\pi}{3}\delta_{ij}$ (by rotational symmetry it is a multiple of $\delta_{ij}$, and its trace is $\oint d\Omega = 4\pi$). So the surface term tends to $-\frac{4\pi}{3}\delta_{ij}f(0)$.
>
> **5. The volume term has a limit.** Write $f = f(0) + [f - f(0)]$. On each shell $\oint(3\hat r_i\hat r_j - \delta_{ij})\,d\Omega = 4\pi\delta_{ij} - 4\pi\delta_{ij} = 0$, so the $f(0)$ part vanishes for every ε. The rest is bounded by $\sup|\nabla f|\,r\cdot4/r^3$ near the origin, which is integrable, so the limit exists: it is $\operatorname{PV}K[f]$.
>
> **6. ⚑ Other excisions.** Repeat steps 2–4 with the ball replaced by a small region $S_\varepsilon$ of fixed shape. Step 2's boundary term still vanishes. In step 3 the surface term becomes $-f(0)\oint_{\partial S}\frac{x_i}{r^3}n^S_j\,dS$, with $\vec n^S$ the outward normal of $S$; this integral is scale-invariant, and it is $4\pi\mathcal N_{ij}$. Its trace is $\oint\vec n^S\cdot\vec r/r^3\,dS = 4\pi$, the solid angle of a closed surface seen from inside ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]]). The left side does not depend on the excision, so the shape-dependent principal value and the shape-dependent δ coefficient compensate. This is the mathematical root of the cavity dependence of dipole sums in matter ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-3|Theorem §C5.2.3]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The pointwise formula $(3x_ix_j - r^2\delta_{ij})/r^5$ is not the whole distribution: it is traceless, while $\nabla^2(1/r)$ is not. The missing piece is concentrated at the origin and is isotropic for a spherical excision.
> - The split "principal value + contact term" is a convention fixed by the excision shape; only the sum has a meaning.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.4: The Field of a Point Dipole, Contact Term Included
> The field of the point dipole of [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^mod-c3-1-2|Model §C3.1.2]], as a distribution, is (Zangwill eq. (4.16))
>
> $$
> \vec E(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\operatorname{PV}\frac{3\hat n(\hat n\cdot\vec p) - \vec p}{|\vec r - \vec r_0|^3} - \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\vec p\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)\right], \qquad \hat n = \frac{\vec r - \vec r_0}{|\vec r - \vec r_0|} ,
> $$
>
> with PV taken over spheres about $\vec r_0$ (Theorem §C3.1.3). Away from $\vec r_0$ it is the dipole field of [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]]. Over any ball $\mathcal V$ centred at $\vec r_0$ the principal-value part integrates to zero and $\int_{\mathcal V}\vec E\,d^3r = -\vec p/3\varepsilon_0$ (Zangwill eq. (4.15)). More generally, for any $\rho$ inside a ball $\mathcal V$, $\vec p = -3\varepsilon_0\int_{\mathcal V}\vec E\,d^3r$ (Zangwill Example 4.1; Griffiths' average-field result in [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^rem-b4-2-1|§B4.2, Remark: The contact term at the dipole's position]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §4.2.2, eqs. (4.15)–(4.16); Example 4.1*

^thm-c3-1-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The field as a derivative.** $\vec E = -\nabla V$ in the sense of distributions; with $V = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}p_j\partial_j\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r_0|}$ (Model §C3.1.2), $E_i = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,p_j\,\partial_i\partial_j\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r_0|}$.
>
> **2. Insert Theorem §C3.1.3**, translated to $\vec r_0$ (a translation of the variable, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-8|QFT Def. §CA.2.8]]): $p_j(3y_iy_j - y^2\delta_{ij})/y^5 = [3\hat n_i(\hat n\cdot\vec p) - p_i]/y^3$ with $\vec y = \vec r - \vec r_0$, and $p_j\cdot(-\frac{4\pi}{3}\delta_{ij}) = -\frac{4\pi}{3}p_i$. This is the statement.
>
> **3. Ball integral.** Integrate over the ball $|\vec y| \le a$, shell by shell. On each shell the principal-value integrand averages to zero (Theorem §C3.1.3), and the δ term gives $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\bigl(-\frac{4\pi}{3}\vec p\bigr) = -\vec p/3\varepsilon_0$. (The indicator of the ball is not smooth, but it is constant near $\vec r_0$, where the δ acts, and the principal-value part is an ordinary integral away from $\vec r_0$.)
>
> **4. Any distribution in a ball (Example 4.1).** For $\rho$ inside the ball $\mathcal V$ of radius $a$ about the origin, exchange the order of integration (Fubini; the singularity $|\vec r - \vec s|^{-2}$ is integrable): $\int_{\mathcal V}\vec E\,d^3r = -\int d^3s\,\rho(\vec s)\,\vec{\mathcal E}(\vec s)$, where $\vec{\mathcal E}(\vec s) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}\frac{\vec s - \vec r}{|\vec s - \vec r|^3}d^3r$ is the field at $\vec s$ of the ball carrying unit charge density. By Gauss's law it is $\vec s/3\varepsilon_0$ for $s < a$ (and $\frac{a^3}{3\varepsilon_0}\vec s/s^3$ outside). So $\int_{\mathcal V}\vec E = -\frac{1}{3\varepsilon_0}\int\rho\,\vec s\,d^3s = -\vec p/3\varepsilon_0$ when all of $\rho$ is inside. ⚑ By-product: if instead all of $\rho$ is outside, the same computation gives $\frac{1}{\mathcal V}\int_{\mathcal V}\vec E = \vec E(0)$, the mean-value property of the field of exterior charge (Zangwill; [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-2|Theorem §C6.1.2]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Zangwill infers the δ term from the volume average (4.15). Here it comes out of the distributional second derivative, and the volume average is a check. The two agree because both use spherical regions.
> - Physically the δ term is the field inside the source, which points against $\vec p$. It matters for anything that probes the dipole's own position: the hyperfine contact interaction, and the local field in matter ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter|§C5.2]], [[§C5.4 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant|§C5.4]]).

> [!model] Model §C3.1.5: Point Quadrupole
> Two opposite point dipoles, $+\vec p/s$ at $\vec r_0 + s\vec c$ and $-\vec p/s$ at $\vec r_0$, in the limit $s \to 0$, form a **point quadrupole** with (Zangwill eqs. (4.54)–(4.56); Problem 4.16(a))
>
> $$
> \rho_Q = Q_{ij}\,\partial_i\partial_j\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0), \qquad V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}(\vec c\cdot\nabla)(\vec p\cdot\nabla)\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r_0|} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,Q_{ij}\,\partial_i\partial_j\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r_0|}, \qquad Q_{ij} = \tfrac12(c_ip_j + c_jp_i) ,
> $$
>
> and $Q_{ij}$ is its primitive quadrupole moment (Def. §C3.1.1) about $\vec r_0$, while $Q$, $\vec p$ and all $C^{(\ell \ge 3)}$ vanish. A general point quadrupole is any symmetric $Q_{ij}$: six numbers. A real symmetric matrix is diagonal in an orthonormal eigenbasis (the real spectral theorem, [[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]), so six numbers = three principal values + three angles for the principal axes (Zangwill eqs. (4.57)–(4.59)), as for the inertia tensor ([[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^def-b10-1-1|CM Def. §B10.1.1]]). Off $\vec r_0$ the potential depends only on the five numbers of the traceless part ([[§C3.3 Primitive and Traceless Moments#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §4.4, eqs. (4.54)–(4.59), Figure 4.10, footnote 9; Problem 4.16(a) (solved here)*

^mod-c3-1-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Density.** By Model §C3.1.2, $\rho_s = \frac1s\bigl[-\vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0 - s\vec c) + \vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)\bigr]$. Paired with $f$: $\rho_s[f] = \frac1s\bigl[\vec p\cdot\nabla f(\vec r_0 + s\vec c) - \vec p\cdot\nabla f(\vec r_0)\bigr] \to c_ip_j\,\partial_i\partial_jf(\vec r_0)$. Since $\partial_i\partial_jf$ is symmetric, only the symmetric part of $c_ip_j$ survives: the limit is $Q_{ij}\partial_i\partial_jf(\vec r_0)$ with $Q_{ij} = \frac12(c_ip_j + c_jp_i)$. Two derivatives of δ, each with a sign, give $(Q_{ij}\partial_i\partial_j\delta_{\vec r_0})[f] = (+1)Q_{ij}\partial_i\partial_jf(\vec r_0)$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]).
>
> **2. Potential.** Pair $\rho_Q$ with $h(\vec a) = 1/|\vec r - \vec a|$ (smooth near $\vec r_0$ for $\vec r \ne \vec r_0$): $Q_{ij}\partial_{a_i}\partial_{a_j}h(\vec r_0) = Q_{ij}\partial_i\partial_j\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r_0|}$, the two sign changes $\nabla_{\vec a} = -\nabla$ cancelling. The form $(\vec c\cdot\nabla)(\vec p\cdot\nabla)$ is the same contraction before symmetrizing (Zangwill eq. (4.54)).
>
> **3. Moments.** About $\vec r_0$, with $\vec y = \vec r - \vec r_0$: $\rho_Q[1] = 0$, $\rho_Q[y_k] = 0$ (second derivatives of a linear function), $\rho_Q[\frac12y_ky_l] = Q_{ij}\cdot\frac12(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} + \delta_{il}\delta_{jk}) = Q_{kl}$, and third and higher powers have vanishing second derivatives at $\vec y = \vec 0$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.6: The Charge Density as a Series of Point Multipoles
> For point charges $q_\alpha$ at $\vec r_\alpha$ with $|\vec r_\alpha| \le R$ (and likewise for any $\rho$ confined to $r \le R$), (Zangwill eqs. (4.131)–(4.134))
>
> $$
> \rho(\vec r) = \sum_\alpha q_\alpha\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_\alpha) \;\simeq\; \sum_{\ell = 0}^\infty(-1)^\ell\,C^{(\ell)}_{i_1\cdots i_\ell}\,\partial_{i_1}\cdots\partial_{i_\ell}\delta^3(\vec r) = \bigl[Q - \vec p\cdot\nabla + Q_{ij}\partial_i\partial_j - \cdots\bigr]\delta^3(\vec r)
> $$
>
> in two precise senses:
> 1. *(asymptotic)* for every test function $f$, the series truncated after order $L - 1$ reproduces $\rho[f]$ up to an error $O(R^L)$, uniformly as the distribution shrinks;
> 2. *(convergent outside)* paired with the Coulomb kernel $1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ for $r > R$, the full series converges and gives [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]] term by term.
>
> The ℓth term is a point $2^\ell$-pole at the origin; ℓ = 1 and ℓ = 2 are Models §C3.1.2 and §C3.1.5. Conversely, every distribution supported at a single point is a finite sum of such terms ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-2|QFT Theorem §CA.2.2]]): a source that is exactly pointlike consists of finitely many point multipoles.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Application 4.5, eqs. (4.131)–(4.134), footnote 9*

^thm-c3-1-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. One charge.** For a test function $f$, Taylor's theorem about the origin gives $f(\vec r_\alpha) = \sum_{\ell < L}\frac{1}{\ell!}(\vec r_\alpha\cdot\nabla)^\ell f(0) + O(|\vec r_\alpha|^L)$, with the constant in $O$ bounded by $\sup|\partial^Lf|$ ([[§11 Taylor's Theorem for Multivariable Functions#^thm-11-2|452 Thm. §11.2]]).
>
> **2. Each Taylor term is a derivative of δ.** Moving ℓ derivatives onto $f$ costs $(-1)^\ell$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]), so $\bigl[\frac{1}{\ell!}(-\vec r_\alpha\cdot\nabla)^\ell\delta^3\bigr][f] = \frac{(-1)^\ell(-1)^\ell}{\ell!}(\vec r_\alpha\cdot\nabla)^\ell f(0) = \frac{1}{\ell!}(\vec r_\alpha\cdot\nabla)^\ell f(0)$. Hence $\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_\alpha) \simeq \sum_\ell\frac{1}{\ell!}(-\vec r_\alpha\cdot\nabla)^\ell\delta^3(\vec r)$ in sense 1 (Zangwill eq. (4.132)).
>
> **3. Sum over charges.** $\sum_\alpha q_\alpha\frac{1}{\ell!}(-1)^\ell x_{\alpha i_1}\cdots x_{\alpha i_\ell}\,\partial_{i_1}\cdots\partial_{i_\ell}\delta^3 = (-1)^\ell C^{(\ell)}_{i_1\cdots i_\ell}\partial_{i_1}\cdots\partial_{i_\ell}\delta^3$ by Def. §C3.1.1. For ℓ = 0, 1, 2 the coefficients are $Q$, $-p_i$, $+Q_{ij}$ (Zangwill eqs. (4.133)–(4.134)). A continuous ρ is handled by replacing $\sum_\alpha q_\alpha$ by $\int d^3r'\rho$.
>
> **4. Sense 2.** For fixed $\vec r$ with $r > R$, the kernel $k(\vec r\,') = 1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ is real-analytic on $r' < r$. Pairing the ℓth term with it gives $(-1)^\ell C^{(\ell)}\cdot(-1)^\ell\partial'^\ell k(0) = C^{(\ell)}\cdot(-1)^\ell\partial^\ell\frac1r = C^{(\ell)}\cdot N^{(\ell)}$ (each $\partial' = -\partial$). The sum converges by [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]] (step 3 there); this is Zangwill's "exercise in integration by parts".
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ The full series is **not** a convergent identity of distributions: for a test function that is smooth but not analytic, the Taylor series need not converge to $f(\vec r_\alpha)$. It is an asymptotic expansion in the size $R$, and it converges against the Coulomb kernel outside the source, which is all that the multipole expansion of the potential needs.
> - Each order is neutral beyond its own order: a $2^\ell$-pole is built from two opposite $2^{\ell - 1}$-poles (Zangwill footnote 9).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.7: Point Charges as a Monopole plus a Polarization
> In the sense of [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-6|Theorem §C3.1.6]], every cluster of charges is a point charge plus a polarization (Zangwill eqs. (4.135)–(4.136)):
>
> $$
> \rho = Q\,\delta^3(\vec r) - \nabla\cdot\vec P, \qquad P_i(\vec r) = \sum_{\ell = 1}^\infty(-1)^{\ell - 1}C^{(\ell)}_{ij\cdots m}\,\underbrace{\partial_j\cdots\partial_m}_{\ell - 1}\delta^3(\vec r) = p_i\,\delta^3(\vec r) - Q_{ij}\,\partial_j\delta^3(\vec r) + \cdots .
> $$
>
> For a neutral cluster, $\rho = -\nabla\cdot\vec P$: this supports, in the static case, the decomposition of matter's charge into free charge and polarization charge ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^def-c5-1-2|Def. §C5.1.2]]; level B's bound charge, [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]]). This $\vec P$ is highly singular; smooth polarizations come from averaging ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization|§C5.1]]), and $\vec P$ is not unique ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-1|Theorem §C5.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill Application 4.5, eqs. (4.130), (4.135)–(4.136)*

^thm-c3-1-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> In the ℓth term of Theorem §C3.1.6 with ℓ ≥ 1, pull the first derivative out (the coefficients are constants): $(-1)^\ell C^{(\ell)}_{ij\cdots m}\partial_i\partial_j\cdots\partial_m\delta^3 = -\partial_i\bigl[(-1)^{\ell - 1}C^{(\ell)}_{ij\cdots m}\partial_j\cdots\partial_m\delta^3\bigr]$. Summing over ℓ ≥ 1 gives $-\partial_iP_i$; the ℓ = 0 term is $Q\delta^3$. For ℓ = 1: $-\partial_i(p_i\delta^3)$, a point dipole, $\vec P = \vec p\,\delta^3$; for ℓ = 2: $P_i \ni -Q_{ij}\partial_j\delta^3$.

## Dipole layers

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.2: Dipole Layer; Double Layer
> A **dipole layer** is a neutral surface $S$ carrying a **dipole moment per unit area** (Zangwill eqs. (4.33)–(4.34))
>
> $$
> \vec\tau(\vec r_S) = \frac{d\vec p}{dS}, \qquad \vec r_S \in S .
> $$
>
> Its simplest model is two sheets with surface charges $\pm\sigma$ a distance $b$ apart, $+\sigma$ on the side to which $\vec\tau$ points, in the macroscopic limit $b \to 0$, $\sigma \to \infty$ with $\tau = \sigma b$ fixed (Zangwill Figure 4.7). A **double layer** is a dipole layer with $\vec\tau = \tau\,\hat n$ normal to $S$ (some authors reserve the name for this case; Zangwill footnote 5). Like σ, $\vec\tau$ is a macroscopic quantity: it arises from oriented polar molecules at an interface (Zangwill Figure 4.6), or from mobile charge near a surface, as in the electron "spill-out" at a metal surface.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §4.3, eqs. (4.33)–(4.34), Figures 4.6–4.7, footnote 5*

^def-c3-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.8: Potential of a Dipole Layer; Solid-Angle Form
> A dipole layer $\vec\tau$ on $S$ has the potential (Zangwill eq. (4.35))
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_SdS\,\vec\tau(\vec r_S)\cdot\nabla\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r_S|} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_SdS\,\frac{\vec\tau(\vec r_S)\cdot(\vec r - \vec r_S)}{|\vec r - \vec r_S|^3} .
> $$
>
> For a double layer $\vec\tau = \tau\hat n$ (Zangwill Problem 4.9(a)),
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_S\tau(\vec r_S)\,d\Omega, \qquad d\Omega = \frac{\hat n\cdot(\vec r_S - \vec r)}{|\vec r_S - \vec r|^3}\,dS ,
> $$
>
> where $d\Omega$ is the solid angle that $dS$ subtends at $\vec r$, positive when $\hat n$ points away from the observer, i.e. when the observer faces the negative side ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]]). A uniform double layer has $V = -\tau\,\Omega_S(\vec r)/4\pi\varepsilon_0$: the potential depends only on the outline of $S$ as seen from $\vec r$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §4.3.1, eq. (4.35); Problem 4.9(a) (solved here; the printed statement has $4\tau\epsilon_0$ for $4\pi\epsilon_0$)*

^thm-c3-1-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Superpose point dipoles.** The patch $dS$ at $\vec r_S$ is a point dipole $d\vec p = \vec\tau\,dS$ (Def. §C3.1.2). By [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^mod-c3-1-2|Model §C3.1.2]] its potential is $-\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}d\vec p\cdot\nabla\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r_S|}$; integrating over $S$ gives the first form.
>
> **2. Compute the gradient.** $\nabla\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r_S|} = -\frac{\vec r - \vec r_S}{|\vec r - \vec r_S|^3}$, which gives the second form.
>
> **3. Double layer.** For $\vec\tau = \tau\hat n$: $\tau\,\hat n\cdot(\vec r - \vec r_S)\,dS/|\vec r - \vec r_S|^3 = -\tau\,d\Omega$ with $d\Omega$ as defined. Check of the sign: if $\hat n$ points away from the observer, the observer sees the $-\sigma$ sheet first, nearer; the potential there is negative, and indeed $d\Omega > 0$ gives $V < 0$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.9: Equivalent Charge Density of a Dipole Layer
> A dipole layer on the plane $z = 0$, with $\vec\tau(x, y)$ smooth, has the same potential as the volume charge density (Zangwill eqs. (4.37)–(4.41))
>
> $$
> \rho_L(\vec r) = -\nabla\cdot\bigl[\vec\tau(x, y)\,\delta(z)\bigr] = -\tau_z(x, y)\,\delta'(z) + \sigma(x, y)\,\delta(z), \qquad \sigma = -\nabla_S\cdot\vec\tau = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial\tau_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\tau_y}{\partial y}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Here δ(z) and δ′(z) are distributions in $z$ multiplied by smooth functions of $(x, y)$, so $\rho_L[f] = \int dx\,dy\,\bigl[\vec\tau\cdot\nabla f\bigr]_{z = 0}$. The normal component of $\vec\tau$ gives the capacitor-like $\delta'$ layer. The tangential components act only through their surface divergence, an effective surface charge: neighbouring in-plane dipoles cancel head to tail unless $\vec\tau$ varies along the surface. Equivalently, the layer is the polarization $\vec P = \vec\tau(x, y)\,\delta(z)$ (Zangwill footnote 6; [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-7|Theorem §C3.1.7]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §4.3.1, eqs. (4.36)–(4.41), footnote 6*

^thm-c3-1-9

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Volume form.** With $\vec r\,' = (x', y', z')$, write the surface integral of Theorem §C3.1.8 with a δ in $z'$ (Zangwill eq. (4.36)): $V = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r'\,\delta(z')\,\vec\tau(x', y')\cdot\nabla\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}$.
>
> **2. Move the gradient to the source point.** $\nabla = -\nabla'$ on $|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^{-1}$, so $V = +\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r'\,\vec\tau\,\delta(z')\cdot\nabla'\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}$.
>
> **3. Integrate by parts.** As a distribution in $\vec r\,'$, $\vec\tau\delta(z')$ has the derivative $\nabla'\cdot(\vec\tau\delta)$, and for $\vec r$ off the plane the kernel is smooth where $\vec\tau\delta$ lives ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-3|QFT Theorem §CA.2.3]]; [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]): $V = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3r'\,\frac{\nabla'\cdot[\vec\tau\delta(z')]}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}$ (eq. (4.37)). Comparison with the Coulomb integral gives $\rho_L = -\nabla\cdot[\vec\tau\delta(z)]$ (eq. (4.38)).
>
> **4. Expand.** $\vec\tau$ does not depend on $z$, so $\nabla\cdot[\vec\tau\delta(z)] = (\partial_x\tau_x + \partial_y\tau_y)\,\delta(z) + \tau_z\,\delta'(z)$, which is eq. (4.41) with σ as stated (eqs. (4.39)–(4.40)).

*Uses:* [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-8|Theorem §C3.1.8]], [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-3|QFT Theorem §CA.2.3]], [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.10: Matching Conditions at a Dipole Layer
> Let $\hat n_2$ be the unit normal of a dipole layer pointing from side 2 into side 1. Then (Zangwill eqs. (4.45)–(4.46))
>
> $$
> V_1 - V_2 = \frac{\hat n_2\cdot\vec\tau}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \hat n_2\times(\vec E_1 - \vec E_2) = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\nabla\times\bigl[\hat n_2(\hat n_2\cdot\vec\tau)\bigr], \qquad \varepsilon_0\,\hat n_2\cdot(\vec E_1 - \vec E_2) = \sigma = -\nabla_S\cdot\vec\tau .
> $$
>
> The **potential jumps**, unlike across a surface charge ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]]). The tangential field is continuous only if $\hat n_2\cdot\vec\tau$ is uniform along the surface. Inside the layer the field has a δ-sheet, $-(\tau_z/\varepsilon_0)\delta(z)\hat z$ for a flat layer: the field between the capacitor plates of Def. §C3.1.2, which the macroscopic limit concentrates on the surface.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §4.3.2, eqs. (4.42)–(4.46), Figure 4.8; Problem 4.9(b)*

^thm-c3-1-10

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Take the layer flat, $z = 0$, side 1 at $z > 0$, so $\hat n_2 = \hat z$ (a curved layer looks flat on scales small compared with its radii of curvature).
>
> **1. Poisson's equation with $\rho_L$.** By Theorem §C3.1.9, $\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V = -\rho_L = \tau_z\,\delta'(z) - \sigma\,\delta(z)$ (Zangwill eqs. (4.42)–(4.43)).
>
> **2. Split off a step.** Let $\theta$ be the unit step. As distributions $\theta' = \delta$ and $\theta'' = \delta'$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-4|QFT Theorem §CA.2.4]]). Write $V = \frac{\tau_z(x, y)}{\varepsilon_0}\theta(z) + W$. Then $\varepsilon_0\partial_z^2\bigl[\frac{\tau_z}{\varepsilon_0}\theta\bigr] = \tau_z\delta'$ cancels the $\delta'$ source, and
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_0\nabla^2W = -\sigma\,\delta(z) - (\nabla_S^2\tau_z)\,\theta(z) .
> $$
>
> $W$ thus has a surface charge σ and a bounded volume density. Such a potential is continuous across $z = 0$, and its normal derivative jumps by $-\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ (level B's matching conditions, [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]]).
>
> **3. Read off the jumps.** $V(z = 0^+) - V(z = 0^-) = \tau_z/\varepsilon_0 + 0$ (eq. (4.45); Zangwill integrates (4.43) twice instead, eq. (4.44)). Tangential field: $\vec E_\parallel = -\nabla_SV$, so $\vec E_{1\parallel} - \vec E_{2\parallel} = -\nabla_S\tau_z/\varepsilon_0$; with $\nabla\times(\hat z\tau_z) = \nabla\tau_z\times\hat z = -\hat z\times\nabla_S\tau_z$ this is eq. (4.46). Normal field: $E_z = -\partial_zV = -\frac{\tau_z}{\varepsilon_0}\delta(z) - \partial_zW$; the δ term is the sheet field inside the layer, and the jump of $-\partial_zW$ is $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$.
>
> **4. Check by solid angles (Problem 4.9(b)).** For a double layer, Theorem §C3.1.8 gives $V = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\tau\,d\Omega$. When the observer crosses $S$ from side 2 to side 1, the patch just in front changes from being seen from its negative side, $d\Omega \to +2\pi$ per unit τ, to being seen from its positive side, $-2\pi$: Ω drops by $4\pi$ ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]]), and $V$ rises by $\tau/\varepsilon_0$. Same result.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The jump of $V$ comes only from the normal dipole density; in-plane dipoles act as an ordinary surface charge $-\nabla_S\cdot\vec\tau$.
> - ⚑ The potential of a real interface need not be continuous on the macroscopic scale. Contact potentials between metals and the surface-dipole part of a metal's work function are jumps of this kind (Zangwill, Sources to §4.3; Problem 4.2).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C3.1.1: A Uniformly Coated Soap Bubble
> An insulating spherical shell of radius $R$ is uniformly coated with polar molecules, a double layer $\vec\tau = \tau\hat r$. Find $V$ everywhere and check the matching condition.
>
> *Solid angles.* Take $\hat n = \hat r$ in Theorem §C3.1.8. From inside, every patch is seen with $\hat r$ pointing away from the observer, and the closed sphere subtends $\Omega = 4\pi$. From outside the solid angle of a closed surface is $0$ ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]]). So
>
> $$
> V(r) = -\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon_0}\quad(r < R), \qquad V(r) = 0\quad(r > R) ,
> $$
>
> and $\vec E = \vec 0$ everywhere off the shell. Matching: side 1 outside, $\hat n_2 = \hat r$: $V_1 - V_2 = 0 - (-\tau/\varepsilon_0) = \tau/\varepsilon_0 = \hat r\cdot\vec\tau/\varepsilon_0$, as [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-10|Theorem §C3.1.10]] requires.
>
> *Check with two sheets.* Model the layer as charge $+q$ spread on the sphere $R + b/2$ and $-q$ on $R - b/2$, with $\sigma b = \tau$, $q = 4\pi R^2\sigma$. By Gauss's law the field vanishes inside the inner sheet and outside the outer one (total charge zero), and between them it is $q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2 \approx \sigma/\varepsilon_0$, radially outward. The potential therefore rises by $(\sigma/\varepsilon_0)b = \tau/\varepsilon_0$ from inside to outside; with $V(\infty) = 0$ the interior sits at $-\tau/\varepsilon_0$. The bubble is invisible electrically from outside, and inside there is no field at all: a potential difference without a field anywhere except within the layer.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 4.10 (solved here)*

> [!example] Example §C3.1.2: Two Concentric Charged Rings
> A ring of radius $a$ with charge $Q$ and a coplanar concentric ring of radius $b > a$ with charge $-Q$ lie in the plane $z = 0$, both uniform. Find the lowest non-vanishing Cartesian moment and the asymptotic potential.
>
> *Density.* In cylindrical coordinates $(s, \varphi, z)$, $\rho = \frac{Q}{2\pi a}\delta(s - a)\delta(z) - \frac{Q}{2\pi b}\delta(s - b)\delta(z)$, so $\int\rho\,F\,d^3r = \frac{Q}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}F(a, \varphi, 0)\,d\varphi - \frac{Q}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}F(b, \varphi, 0)\,d\varphi$ for any continuous $F$.
>
> *Monopole and dipole.* $F = 1$: $Q - Q = 0$. $F = x = s\cos\varphi$, $F = y = s\sin\varphi$: the φ integrals vanish; $F = z$: zero on the plane. So $Q_{\text{tot}} = 0$ and $\vec p = \vec 0$, and the quadrupole moment is origin-independent ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^rem-c3-1-1|Remark: What changes from level B]]).
>
> *Quadrupole.* $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\varphi\,d\varphi = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\varphi\,d\varphi = \frac12$, $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\varphi\sin\varphi\,d\varphi = 0$, and $z = 0$ on both rings. With $Q_{ij} = \frac12\int\rho\,x_ix_j$:
>
> $$
> Q_{xx} = Q_{yy} = \frac12\Bigl[Q\frac{a^2}{2} - Q\frac{b^2}{2}\Bigr] = -\frac{Q(b^2 - a^2)}{4}, \qquad Q_{zz} = Q_{xy} = Q_{xz} = Q_{yz} = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Potential.* By [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], $Q_{ij}(3x_ix_j - r^2\delta_{ij}) = Q_{xx}\bigl(3x^2 + 3y^2 - 2r^2\bigr) = Q_{xx}(x^2 + y^2 - 2z^2)$. In spherical coordinates $x^2 + y^2 - 2z^2 = r^2(1 - 3\cos^2\theta) = -2r^2P_2(\cos\theta)$, so
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) \simeq \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q(b^2 - a^2)}{4}\,\frac{2z^2 - x^2 - y^2}{r^5} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q(b^2 - a^2)}{2r^3}\,P_2(\cos\theta) \qquad (r \gg b) .
> $$
>
> On the axis the inner, positive ring is relatively nearer and $V > 0$; near the plane ($P_2 = -\frac12$) the outer, negative ring wins and $V < 0$. The same coefficient follows from the azimuthal expansion of a ring, whose ℓ = 2 term is $QR^2P_2(0)P_2(\cos\theta)/r^3$ with $P_2(0) = -\frac12$ ([[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^ex-c6-2-3|Example §C6.2.3]]): $\bigl[Qa^2 - Qb^2\bigr]\bigl(-\frac12\bigr) = \frac{Q(b^2 - a^2)}{2}$. The primitive tensor has trace $Q_{kk} = -Q(b^2 - a^2)/2 \ne 0$, and the trace does not enter $V$; the traceless form is $\Theta = \frac{Q(b^2 - a^2)}{4}\operatorname{diag}(-1, -1, 2)$ ([[§C3.3 Primitive and Traceless Moments#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 8, Problem 2 (Zangwill Problem 4.11); solved here*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The dipole term's force, torque and energy in a field are level B's [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]], and the interaction of two dipoles is [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]]. The general multipole forms, which Zangwill derives with the point densities of this section, are in [[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]].
> - Point multipoles are exactly the distributions with one-point support ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-2|QFT Theorem §CA.2.2]]); their manipulation is the calculus of [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]], and the dipole's contact term is the electrostatic twin of the δ in $\nabla^2(1/r)$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]).
> - A dipole layer is a polarization concentrated on a surface ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-2|Theorem §C5.1.2]]); the jump of $V$ across it reappears in potential theory as the double-layer potential, whose solid-angle form is [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]] at work.

> - The quadrupole normalizations of the Caution are tied to one scalar, $\Theta = 2qd^2$ for a linear $+q, -2q, +q$ molecule, in the conventions note of the honors-thesis multipole notes ([[§M0.1 Conventions and Units#^thm-m0-1-1|Thesis Thm. §M0.1.1]]); the slips that mixing them caused there, a pair-energy prefactor off by 4 and Θ off by 2, are [[Multipole Lattices Edit Log#^c5|Thesis Edit Log C5]] and [[Multipole Lattices Edit Log#^c6|Thesis Edit Log C6]].
