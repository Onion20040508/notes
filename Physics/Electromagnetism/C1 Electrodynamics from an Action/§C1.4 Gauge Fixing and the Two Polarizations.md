How many of the four components of $A_\mu$ are physical, and how are the redundant ones removed? The field equations cannot determine $A_\mu$, because gauge transformations change $A_\mu$ but none of the equations ([[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^thm-c1-3-2|Theorem §C1.3.2]]); and $V$ has no conjugate momentum ([[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]]). Electromagnetism introduced the Coulomb and Lorenz gauges as conveniences and solved the resulting wave equations by retarded potentials ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]], [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]]); Relativity wrote the Lorenz-gauge equation covariantly ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-7|REL Theorem §B4.2.7]]). This note treats gauge fixing as such: what a gauge condition must achieve, what freedom survives it, why the Lorenz condition is kept by the dynamics, what the Coulomb gauge does to the current, and the count that the redundancy leaves: two polarizations of light, two degrees of freedom per point of space. Conventions as in [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action|§C1.1]]; $\Box = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu = \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2 - \nabla^2$ (Electromagnetism's $\Box^2$ is $-\Box$: [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^cau-b2-2-3|REL Caution: The sign of the box]]).

## Gauge conditions

> [!definition] Definition §C1.4.1: Gauge Orbit; Gauge Condition; Residual Transformations
> The **gauge orbit** of a potential $A_\mu$ is the set $\{A_\mu + \partial_\mu\chi\}$ of all potentials with the same $F_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-5|Theorem §C1.2.5]]). A **gauge condition** $G[A] = 0$ is **attainable** if every orbit contains a potential satisfying it; **gauge fixing** is the restriction to such potentials. The gauge transformations that preserve the condition are the **residual** ones; the gauge is fixed completely if only $\chi =$ const remains. Standard conditions: **Lorenz** $\partial_\mu A^\mu = \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_tV + \nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$ ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^def-b11-1-1|Def. §B11.1.1]]), **Coulomb** $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$ ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^def-b6-4-2|Def. §B6.4.2]]), **temporal** $V = 0$.
>
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.1.13 ("Gauge fixing") · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part III §"The Lorenz Gauge Condition" ("Residual Gauge Freedom") · the user's series, Part III, §13.1*

^def-c1-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why fix a gauge, classically and quantum mechanically
> - Classically, a gauge choice is a convention, like a choice of units: every gauge gives the same $\vec E$ and $\vec B$. It is useful because the equations for $A_\mu$ become determinate (Theorem §C1.3.2 says they are not otherwise) and, in a good gauge, decouple.
> - A gauge condition need not be Lorentz invariant (the Coulomb gauge is not) nor local in its consequences (the Coulomb-gauge $V$ responds instantaneously to distant charges): such requirements apply to observables, and $A_\mu$ is not one (the typed notes' Remarks #1 and #2).
> - In quantum theory all field configurations are summed over; gauge-equivalent potentials describe one physical configuration, and summing over all of them counts each configuration infinitely often. There the gauge must be fixed (the typed notes' bold-face note; in the quantum theory, [[§C4.6 The Maxwell Field, Gauge Symmetry and Gauge Fixing#^def-c4-6-2|QFT Def. §C4.6.2]], [[§C4.9 Vector-Field Propagators#^rem-c4-9-3|QFT §C4.9, Remark: Why the ξ-term is allowed: Faddeev–Popov]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.1.13 (Gauge fixing, NOTE), §6.1.13 Remarks #1–#2 (p. 114)*

## The Lorenz gauge

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.1: The Lorenz Gauge
> 1. *Field equation in potentials.* $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \Box A^\nu - \partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu)$, so Maxwell's equation is $\Box A^\nu - \partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu) = \mu_0J^\nu$, four coupled equations.
> 2. *Attainable.* For every $A_\mu$ there is $\chi$ with $\partial_\mu(A^\mu + \partial^\mu\chi) = 0$: a solution of $\Box\chi = -\partial_\mu A^\mu$, for instance its retarded solution.
> 3. *Residual freedom.* The Lorenz condition is preserved exactly by $\chi$ with $\Box\chi = 0$.
> 4. *Covariant.* $\partial_\mu A^\mu$ is a scalar, so the condition holds in every inertial frame if it holds in one.
> 5. *Decoupled and propagated.* In the Lorenz gauge, Maxwell's equations are $\Box A^\nu = \mu_0J^\nu$. Conversely, if $A$ solves $\Box A^\nu = \mu_0J^\nu$ with $\partial_\nu J^\nu = 0$, and $\partial_\mu A^\mu$ and its time derivative vanish at one time (and $\partial_\mu A^\mu$ decays at spatial infinity), then $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$ at all times, and $A$ solves Maxwell's equations.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §13.1, eqs. (maxwell-potential)–(wave-eq-A) · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part III §"From Maxwell's Equations to the Wave Equation", §"The Lorenz Gauge Condition" · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.1.7, §6.1.10–§6.1.13, eqs. (6.43)–(6.54) · Zangwill Problem 24.14(c)–(d) (item 5)*

^thm-c1-4-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Item 1.** Substitute $F^{\mu\nu} = \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu$: $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial_\mu\partial^\nu A^\mu = \Box A^\nu - \partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu)$, the order of derivatives being immaterial ([[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]).
>
> **2. Item 2.** Under $A^\mu \to A^\mu + \partial^\mu\chi$, $\partial_\mu A^\mu \to \partial_\mu A^\mu + \Box\chi$. Choose $\chi = -G_{\rm ret} \ast  (\partial_\mu A^\mu)$, the convolution with the retarded fundamental solution of $\Box$: $G_{\rm ret}$ is the distribution with $\Box G_{\rm ret} = \delta^4$ supported in the forward light cone ([[§CA.6 Boundary Values, iε Limits and Fundamental Solutions#^def-ca-6-1|QFT Def. §CA.6.1]], whose normalization carries an extra factor $-i$ not used here; explicitly the retarded kernel of [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]]; [[§C2b.5 Green's Functions and Contours#^thm-c2b-5-5|QFT Theorem §C2b.5.5]] at $m = 0$). Convolution with a fundamental solution solves the sourced equation, $\Box\chi = -\partial_\mu A^\mu$ ([[§CA.6 Boundary Values, iε Limits and Fundamental Solutions#^thm-ca-6-8|QFT Theorem §CA.6.8]]), whenever $\partial_\mu A^\mu$ vanishes in the far past (or has bounded support), so that the convolution is defined.
>
> **3. Item 3.** If $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$, the transformed divergence is $\Box\chi$, zero exactly when $\Box\chi = 0$. These $\chi$ are free waves, so the gauge is not completely fixed (used in [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]]).
>
> **4. Item 4.** $\partial_\mu$ is a covector and $A^\mu$ a vector (up to the gauge freedom; [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^rem-b4-2-1|REL Remark: In what sense the potential is a four-vector]]), so $\partial_\mu A^\mu$ is a scalar field ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-1|REL Theorem §B2.2.1]]); its vanishing is frame independent ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-8|REL Theorem §B2.2.8]]).
>
> **5. Item 5, decoupling.** Put $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$ into item 1.
>
> **6. Item 5, propagation.** Let $\Lambda = \partial_\mu A^\mu$. Apply $\partial_\nu$ to $\Box A^\nu = \mu_0J^\nu$: $\Box\Lambda = \mu_0\partial_\nu J^\nu = 0$, a free wave equation for $\Lambda$. With $\Lambda = \partial_t\Lambda = 0$ at $t = 0$, $\Lambda \equiv 0$: the energy $\mathcal E(t) = \int d^3x\,\bigl[\frac{1}{c^2}(\partial_t\Lambda)^2 + |\nabla\Lambda|^2\bigr]$ has $\dot{\mathcal E} = \int d^3x\,\bigl[\frac{2}{c^2}\partial_t\Lambda\,\partial_t^2\Lambda + 2\nabla\Lambda\cdot\nabla\partial_t\Lambda\bigr] = \int d^3x\,2\,\partial_t\Lambda\bigl[\frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2\Lambda - \nabla^2\Lambda\bigr] = 0$ (integrating the second term by parts; the surface term vanishes by the decay), so $\mathcal E(t) = \mathcal E(0) = 0$, hence $\nabla\Lambda = \partial_t\Lambda = 0$ and $\Lambda = 0$. Then item 1 turns $\Box A^\nu = \mu_0J^\nu$ into $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0J^\nu$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - In the Lorenz gauge electrodynamics is four massless Klein–Gordon equations with a source ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-1|REL Theorem §B4.1.1]] at $m = 0$), solved by the retarded potentials.
> - ⚑ By-product: the Lorenz condition is an initial condition that the wave equation preserves, provided $J$ is conserved; one may solve $\Box A = \mu_0J$ first and impose the condition on the data only (Zangwill Problem 24.14).
> - ⚑ By-product: the residual freedom $\Box\chi = 0$ is a set of free waves; it is what removes the second unphysical component of a plane wave (Theorem §C1.4.3).

> [!example] Example §C1.4.1: The Wave Equations in the Lorenz Gauge
> What is the wave equation for $A^\mu$ under $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$, and what are the equations for $V$ and $\vec A$ separately?
>
> *Solution.* **1.** By [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-1|Theorem §C1.4.1]], item 5, $\Box A^\nu = \mu_0J^\nu$ (the problem's normalization $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = j^\nu$ gives $\Box A^\nu = j^\nu$).
>
> **2. $\nu = 0$.** $A^0 = V/c$, $J^0 = c\rho$: $\Box(V/c) = \mu_0c\rho$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2V = \mu_0c^2\rho = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} .
> $$
>
> **3. $\nu = i$.** $A^i$ and $J^i$ are the Cartesian components of $\vec A$, $\vec J$:
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\vec A}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2\vec A = \mu_0\vec J ,
> $$
>
> each component an independent wave equation with speed $c$; together with $\frac{1}{c^2}\partial_tV + \nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$. These are [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]], item 3, with $\Box^2 = -\Box$.
>
> **4. Check from the three-vector equations.** Gauss's law with $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A$: $-\nabla^2V - \partial_t(\nabla\cdot\vec A) = \rho/\varepsilon_0$; the Lorenz condition replaces $\nabla\cdot\vec A$ by $-\frac{1}{c^2}\partial_tV$, giving step 2. Ampère–Maxwell with $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec A) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec A) - \nabla^2\vec A$: $\nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec A) - \nabla^2\vec A = \mu_0\vec J - \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t\nabla V - \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2\vec A$; the gradient terms combine to $\nabla\bigl(\nabla\cdot\vec A + \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_tV\bigr) = 0$, giving step 3 (the typed notes' route, eqs. (6.28)–(6.37)).
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 6, Problem 1 (where the condition is called the "Lorentz gauge": [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^cau-b11-1-1|Caution: Lorenz, not Lorentz]]) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.1.8–§6.1.12*

## The Coulomb gauge

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.2: The Coulomb Gauge and the Transverse Current
> 1. *Attainable.* $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$ is reached with $\chi$ solving Poisson's equation $\nabla^2\chi = \nabla\cdot\vec A$ at each time.
> 2. *Instantaneous $V$.* Gauss's law becomes $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$, so, with $V \to 0$ at infinity, $V(\vec x, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(\vec x', t)}{|\vec x - \vec x'|}$.
> 3. *Transverse source.* The vector potential obeys
>
> $$
> \Box\vec A = \mu_0\vec J_\perp, \qquad \vec J_\perp = \vec J - \varepsilon_0\nabla\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec J_\perp = 0,
> $$
>
> where $\vec J_\perp$ is the divergence-free (transverse) part of $\vec J$ and $\vec J_\parallel = \varepsilon_0\nabla\partial_tV$ its curl-free part.
> 4. *Residual freedom.* With decay at infinity, only $\chi = \chi(t)$ remains, which changes $V$ by a function of time and $\vec A$ not at all.
> 5. *Not covariant.* $\nabla\cdot\vec A$ is not a Lorentz scalar; the condition holds in one frame only.
>
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.1.11, eqs. (6.40)–(6.42); §6.1.13, eq. (6.55) and Remarks #1–#2 · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part III §"Alternative Gauge: Coulomb Gauge" · Zangwill §24.5.5, eqs. (24.137)–(24.140) · the user's series, Part III, §13.1 (Remark "The Coulomb gauge")*

^thm-c1-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Item 1.** $\vec A \to \vec A - \nabla\chi$ gives $\nabla\cdot\vec A \to \nabla\cdot\vec A - \nabla^2\chi$, zero for $\chi = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d^3x'\,\frac{(\nabla'\cdot\vec A)(\vec x', t)}{|\vec x - \vec x'|}$, the solution of Poisson's equation by the fundamental solution $-1/4\pi r$ of $\nabla^2$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]; the distributional statement: [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]]), for $\nabla\cdot\vec A$ decaying fast enough.
>
> **2. Item 2.** Gauss's law with $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A$: $-\nabla^2V - \partial_t(\nabla\cdot\vec A) = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, and the second term is zero. The integral is the unique solution vanishing at infinity ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]], 2).
>
> **3. Item 3.** The Ampère–Maxwell law in potentials (step 4 of [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^ex-c1-4-1|Example §C1.4.1]] with $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$): $-\nabla^2\vec A + \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2\vec A = \mu_0\vec J - \frac{1}{c^2}\nabla\partial_tV$, i.e. $\Box\vec A = \mu_0\bigl(\vec J - \varepsilon_0\nabla\partial_tV\bigr)$, using $\frac{1}{c^2} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0$. The vector $\vec J_\perp \equiv \vec J - \varepsilon_0\nabla\partial_tV$ has $\nabla\cdot\vec J_\perp = \nabla\cdot\vec J - \varepsilon_0\partial_t\nabla^2V = \nabla\cdot\vec J + \partial_t\rho = 0$ by item 2 and the continuity equation, and $\varepsilon_0\nabla\partial_tV$ is a gradient (curl-free). By the uniqueness of the Helmholtz decomposition for fields decaying at infinity ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]), these are the transverse and longitudinal parts of $\vec J$. In Fourier space, $\tilde{\vec J}_\perp = \bigl(1 - \hat k\hat k^{\mathsf T}\bigr)\tilde{\vec J}$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-4|QFT Theorem §CA.3.4]]): a projection that is local in $\vec k$ and therefore non-local in $\vec x$.
>
> **4. Item 4.** A residual $\chi$ has $\nabla^2\chi = 0$ at each time; a harmonic function that decays (or is bounded) at infinity is constant in space (Liouville: [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-4|Theorem §C6.1.4]]), so $\chi = \chi(t)$, $\nabla\chi = 0$, and only $V \to V + \dot\chi(t)$, which the condition $V \to 0$ at infinity removes.
>
> **5. Item 5.** Under a boost, $\nabla\cdot\vec A$ mixes with $\partial_tV$; the Lorentz scalar is $\partial_\mu A^\mu$, not its spatial part. A boosted Coulomb-gauge potential needs a further gauge transformation to return to the Coulomb gauge ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^rem-b4-2-1|REL Remark: In what sense the potential is a four-vector]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - In the Coulomb gauge the longitudinal part of the current is entirely accounted for by the instantaneous Coulomb potential; only the transverse current radiates.
> - ⚑ By-product: $V$ and $\vec J_\perp$ respond instantaneously to the charges everywhere, yet $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ do not: the instantaneous part of $-\nabla V$ is cancelled by an instantaneous part of $-\partial_t\vec A$ ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^rem-b11-1-2|Remark: The instantaneous potential of the Coulomb gauge]]).

## The two polarizations

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.3: Plane Waves and the Two Polarizations
> In vacuum let $A^\mu(x) = \mathrm{Re}\bigl[\epsilon^\mu e^{-ik\cdot x}\bigr]$, $k^\mu = (\omega/c, \vec k)$, $\omega > 0$, with constant complex polarization vector $\epsilon^\mu \ne 0$. Then:
> 1. $\Box A^\mu = 0$ requires $k_\mu k^\mu = 0$: $\omega = c|\vec k|$.
> 2. The Lorenz condition requires $k_\mu\epsilon^\mu = 0$, i.e. $\epsilon^0 = \hat k\cdot\vec\epsilon$.
> 3. The residual gauge transformation $\chi = \mathrm{Re}\bigl[i\lambda e^{-ik\cdot x}\bigr]$ ($\Box\chi = 0$ because $k^2 = 0$) changes $\epsilon^\mu \to \epsilon^\mu + \lambda k^\mu$ and leaves the fields unchanged.
> 4. The fields are $\vec E = \mathrm{Re}\bigl[i\omega\,\vec\epsilon_\perp e^{-ik\cdot x}\bigr]$, $\vec B = \hat k\times\vec E/c$, with $\vec\epsilon_\perp = \vec\epsilon - \hat k(\hat k\cdot\vec\epsilon)$: they depend on $\epsilon^\mu$ only through $\vec\epsilon_\perp \perp \vec k$. Choosing $\lambda$ makes $\epsilon^\mu = (0, \vec\epsilon_\perp)$.
>
> Of the four complex components of $\epsilon^\mu$, the Lorenz condition removes one and the residual freedom another: **two physical, transverse polarizations**.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §13.2 · the field computation of item 4 written out here*

^thm-c1-4-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Derivatives of the phase.** $k\cdot x = \omega t - \vec k\cdot\vec x$, so $\partial_\mu e^{-ik\cdot x} = -ik_\mu e^{-ik\cdot x}$ ($k_\mu = (\omega/c, -\vec k)$, $\partial_\mu = (\partial_t/c, \nabla)$): $\nabla e^{-ik\cdot x} = i\vec k\,e^{-ik\cdot x}$, $\partial_te^{-ik\cdot x} = -i\omega\,e^{-ik\cdot x}$. Taking the real part commutes with real differential operators, so it suffices to work with the complex exponential.
>
> **2. Item 1.** $\Box e^{-ik\cdot x} = (-ik_\mu)(-ik^\mu)e^{-ik\cdot x} = -k^2e^{-ik\cdot x}$; with $\epsilon \ne 0$, $\Box A^\mu = 0$ forces $k^2 = \omega^2/c^2 - |\vec k|^2 = 0$.
>
> **3. Item 2.** $\partial_\mu A^\mu = \mathrm{Re}\bigl[-ik_\mu\epsilon^\mu e^{-ik\cdot x}\bigr]$ vanishes for all $x$ exactly when $k_\mu\epsilon^\mu = 0$ (a complex number $z$ with $\mathrm{Re}[z\,e^{-i\varphi}] = 0$ for all phases $\varphi$ is $0$). $k_\mu\epsilon^\mu = \frac\omega c\epsilon^0 - \vec k\cdot\vec\epsilon = \frac\omega c\bigl(\epsilon^0 - \hat k\cdot\vec\epsilon\bigr)$, using $|\vec k| = \omega/c$.
>
> **4. Item 3.** $\partial^\mu\chi = \mathrm{Re}\bigl[i\lambda(-ik^\mu)e^{-ik\cdot x}\bigr] = \mathrm{Re}\bigl[\lambda k^\mu e^{-ik\cdot x}\bigr]$, so $A^\mu + \partial^\mu\chi$ has polarization $\epsilon^\mu + \lambda k^\mu$; $k_\mu(\epsilon^\mu + \lambda k^\mu) = k_\mu\epsilon^\mu$ since $k^2 = 0$, so the Lorenz condition survives.
>
> **5. Item 4.** With $V = cA^0$ and $\vec A$ the spatial part, $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A = \mathrm{Re}\bigl[(-i\vec k\,c\epsilon^0 + i\omega\vec\epsilon)e^{-ik\cdot x}\bigr] = \mathrm{Re}\bigl[i\omega(\vec\epsilon - \hat k\epsilon^0)e^{-ik\cdot x}\bigr]$, using $c\vec k = \omega\hat k$. By item 2, $\epsilon^0 = \hat k\cdot\vec\epsilon$, so $\vec\epsilon - \hat k\epsilon^0 = \vec\epsilon_\perp$. And $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A = \mathrm{Re}\bigl[i\vec k\times\vec\epsilon\,e^{-ik\cdot x}\bigr] = \hat k\times\mathrm{Re}\bigl[i\frac\omega c\vec\epsilon_\perp e^{-ik\cdot x}\bigr] = \hat k\times\vec E/c$, since $\hat k\times\hat k = 0$. Under $\epsilon \to \epsilon + \lambda k$, $\vec\epsilon \to \vec\epsilon + \lambda\frac\omega c\hat k$ and $\epsilon^0 \to \epsilon^0 + \lambda\frac\omega c$: $\vec\epsilon_\perp$ is unchanged. Choosing $\lambda = -c\epsilon^0/\omega$ sets $\epsilon^0 = 0$, and then $\hat k\cdot\vec\epsilon = \epsilon^0 = 0$: $\epsilon^\mu = (0, \vec\epsilon_\perp)$.
>
> **6. Count.** For $\hat k = \hat z$: $\epsilon^\mu = (\epsilon^0, \epsilon^x, \epsilon^y, \epsilon^z)$; item 2 fixes $\epsilon^0 - \epsilon^z = 0$; item 3 shifts $(\epsilon^0, \epsilon^z)$ along $(1, 1)$, so the remaining combination $\epsilon^0 = \epsilon^z$ is pure gauge; $\epsilon^x$, $\epsilon^y$ are physical. $4 - 1 - 1 = 2$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Light is transverse and has two polarization states because the description has one constraint and one redundancy; a correct count subtracts both, once for the condition imposed and once for the freedom that survives it.
> - ⚑ By-product: $\vec E\cdot\vec B = 0$ and $E = cB$: the plane wave is a null field in every frame ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-4|REL Theorem §B4.2.4]], 3); its transversality is [[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]], now derived from the potentials. The pure-gauge polarization $\epsilon^\mu \propto k^\mu$ is null as well, which is why it carries no field.
> - ⚑ By-product: for a massive field (★ Proca, [[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^ex-c1-3-2|§C1.3]]) $k^2 \ne 0$, there is no residual shift, and three polarizations survive (the quantum theory of this field: [[§C4.2★ The Proca Field#^thm-c4-2-14|QFT Theorem §C4.2.14]], [[§C4.5★ Energy, Momentum and the Spin-One Quanta of the Proca Field#^thm-c4-5-8|QFT Theorem §C4.5.8]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.4: Two Degrees of Freedom per Point
> Let the free field ($\rho = 0$, $\vec J = 0$) have Coulomb-gauge potentials decaying at infinity. Then $V = 0$, $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$, and the spatial Fourier transform $\tilde{\vec A}(\vec k, t)$ is perpendicular to $\vec k$:
>
> $$
> \tilde{\vec A}(\vec k, t) = \sum_{s = 1, 2}\vec e_s(\vec k)\,a_s(\vec k, t), \qquad \ddot a_s + c^2|\vec k|^2a_s = 0 ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec e_1$, $\vec e_2$ an orthonormal pair perpendicular to $\vec k$. The field is a collection of independent harmonic oscillators, two for each wave vector, of frequency $\omega = c|\vec k|$; the data are $(\vec A_\perp, \vec E_\perp)$ with $\vec E = -\partial_t\vec A$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §24.5.5, eqs. (24.135)–(24.138) (the true variables $\vec A_\perp$, $\vec\pi_\perp$) · the user's series, Part III, §13.2 and §14.1 ("Every field is a family of oscillators") · the Fourier computation written out here*

^thm-c1-4-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. $V = 0$.** By [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]], 2, $\nabla^2V = 0$ at each time with $V \to 0$ at infinity, so $V = 0$ (item 4 of the same theorem's derivation).
>
> **2. Fourier transform.** For each $t$ let $\tilde{\vec A}(\vec k, t) = \int d^3x\,e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec x}\vec A(\vec x, t)$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^def-ca-3-1|QFT Def. §CA.3.1]]; $\vec A(\cdot, t)$ in Schwartz space, or in $\mathcal S'$ in general). Derivatives become multiplication ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-4|QFT Theorem §CA.3.4]]): $\nabla \to i\vec k$, $\nabla^2 \to -|\vec k|^2$. The Coulomb condition becomes $i\vec k\cdot\tilde{\vec A} = 0$, so for $\vec k \ne 0$, $\tilde{\vec A}$ lies in the plane perpendicular to $\vec k$, spanned by $\vec e_1(\vec k)$, $\vec e_2(\vec k)$; the coefficients are $a_s = \vec e_s\cdot\tilde{\vec A}$. (The point $\vec k = 0$ has measure zero and does not affect a function in $L^2$.)
>
> **3. Dynamics.** With $V = 0$ and $\vec J = 0$, Theorem §C1.4.2, 3 gives $\Box\vec A = 0$, i.e. $\frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2\tilde{\vec A} + |\vec k|^2\tilde{\vec A} = 0$ for each $\vec k$. Project on $\vec e_s$ (time independent): $\ddot a_s + c^2|\vec k|^2a_s = 0$.
>
> **4. Data and constraint.** $\vec E = -\partial_t\vec A$ (since $V = 0$) is transverse too, so Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ holds automatically; $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$. Initial values $a_s$, $\dot a_s$ for each $\vec k$, i.e. $\vec A_\perp$ and $\vec E_\perp$ at $t = 0$, determine the solution. Counting per $\vec k$: four potentials, minus $V$ (no conjugate momentum; fixed by the constraint), minus the longitudinal $\hat k\cdot\tilde{\vec A}$ (gauge) leaves two.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The count of Theorem §C1.4.3 holds for every free field, not only for one plane wave: the electromagnetic field is two families of oscillators. Quantizing each oscillator is the photon (QFT: [[§C4.8 The Gupta–Bleuler Condition and Physical Photons#^thm-c4-8-7|QFT Theorem §C4.8.7]], [[§C4.8 The Gupta–Bleuler Condition and Physical Photons#^thm-c4-8-8|QFT Theorem §C4.8.8]], and for the indefinite metric of the covariant version [[§C4.7 Covariant Quantization and the Indefinite Metric#^rem-c4-7-3|QFT §C4.7, Remark: A space with an indefinite inner product]]; the scalar case: [[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^mod-c2a-1-1|QFT Model §C2a.1.1]]).
> - ⚑ By-product: the transverse projection is non-local in space, which is why the physical degrees of freedom cannot be singled out covariantly and locally at once; covariant (Lorenz) or physical (Coulomb) is a trade, and quantum theory uses both.

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Dirac's constraints, and the Coulomb gauge as the true variables
> Zangwill carries the Hamiltonian analysis through. $\pi_V = 0$ is a *primary constraint*; requiring it to persist gives Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\vec\pi + \rho = 0$ as a *secondary constraint*; its persistence needs only the continuity equation, so the chain stops ([[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]], 4). Adding the constraints with Lagrange multipliers $u(t)$, $w(\vec r)$ to the Hamiltonian leaves the equations for $\vec\pi$ and the charges unchanged but makes $\vec A$ and $V$ change by $-\nabla w\,\delta t$ and $u\,\delta t$: the multipliers generate gauge transformations. The variables whose equations contain no arbitrary function are $\vec A_\perp$ and $\vec\pi_\perp = -\varepsilon_0\vec E_\perp$, which is the Coulomb gauge; the Hamiltonian then splits into the particles' kinetic terms with $\vec p - q\vec A_\perp$, the instantaneous Coulomb energy $\frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\iint\frac{\rho\rho'}{|\vec r - \vec r'|}$, and the transverse field energy $\frac{1}{2}\int\bigl(\varepsilon_0E_\perp^2 + B^2/\mu_0\bigr)$ (Zangwill eq. (24.144)), the form used in atomic and condensed-matter physics. The naive Hamilton equation $\partial_tV = 0$ that appears before the constraints are handled is an artefact of the singular Lagrangian.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §24.5.3–§24.5.5, eqs. (24.115)–(24.144)*

> [!example]- ★ Example §C1.4.2: Fixing the Gauge in the Lagrangian (Fermi)
> Add $-\frac{\xi}{2\mu_0}(\partial_\mu A^\mu)^2$ to the Maxwell Lagrangian, $\xi > 0$ a constant (Zangwill's $\lambda$; $\partial_\mu A^\mu = \nabla\cdot\vec A + \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_tV$). (a) Find the field equations. (b) Write them as modified Maxwell equations. (c) Show that $\Lambda \equiv \partial_\mu A^\mu$ obeys a free wave equation. (d) Which initial data make the solutions Maxwell's?
>
> *Solution.* **(a)** The new term contributes $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu A_\nu) = -\frac{\xi}{\mu_0}\Lambda\,\eta^{\mu\nu}$ (since $\partial\Lambda/\partial(\partial_\mu A_\nu) = \eta^{\mu\nu}$). With [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]] the Euler–Lagrange equation is $-\frac{1}{\mu_0}\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} - \frac{\xi}{\mu_0}\partial^\nu\Lambda + J^\nu = 0$:
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + \xi\,\partial^\nu\Lambda = \mu_0J^\nu \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Box A^\nu - (1 - \xi)\,\partial^\nu\Lambda = \mu_0J^\nu ,
> $$
>
> by [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-1|Theorem §C1.4.1]], 1: modified, but determinate, wave equations; for $\xi = 1$ they are simply $\Box A^\nu = \mu_0J^\nu$.
>
> **(b)** $\nu = 0$: $\frac1c\nabla\cdot\vec E + \frac\xi c\partial_t\Lambda = \mu_0c\rho$, so $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} - \xi\,\partial_t\Lambda$. $\nu = j$: $\partial^j = -\partial_j$, so $\nabla\times\vec B - \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t\vec E - \xi\nabla\Lambda = \mu_0\vec J$.
>
> **(c)** Apply $\partial_\nu$ to (a): $\partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^thm-c1-3-2|Theorem §C1.3.2]]), so $\xi\,\Box\Lambda = \mu_0\partial_\nu J^\nu = 0$ for a conserved current: $\Box\Lambda = 0$.
>
> **(d)** If $\Lambda = 0$ and $\partial_t\Lambda = 0$ at $t = 0$, then $\Lambda \equiv 0$ (the energy argument of [[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^der-c1-4-1|Derivation §C1.4.1]], step 6), the extra terms in (b) vanish, and $\vec E$, $\vec B$ obey Maxwell's equations in the Lorenz gauge. The gauge-fixing term has replaced the gauge freedom by an initial condition. (The user's PHY 505 notes mention the related device of a multiplier field, $\mathcal L + \lambda\,\partial_\mu A^\mu$, whose variation with respect to $\lambda$ imposes the Lorenz condition directly.) In quantum field theory $\xi = 1$ is the Feynman gauge.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 24.14 · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part III §"Lagrangian Formulation for Fields" (constraint by a Lagrange multiplier)*
