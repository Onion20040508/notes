Which action describes the electromagnetic field, and how much of it is forced? Electromagnetism took Maxwell's equations as its principles ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]); Relativity showed that they are two tensor equations for the field tensor $F_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-7|REL Theorem §B4.2.7]]) and derived the Lorentz force from the action of a charge in a *given* field ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^pr-b3-2-1|REL Principle §B3.2.1]]). Here the field gets an action of its own. A short list of requirements (locality, Lorentz and gauge invariance, parity, weak and slowly varying fields) leaves exactly one field Lagrangian, $-\frac{1}{4\mu_0}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$, and one coupling, $-J^\mu A_\mu$; the two experimental numbers left over are $\varepsilon_0$ and $c$. This note is the SI home of the Maxwell action; the general machinery of field actions is [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^def-b4-1-1|REL Def. §B4.1.1]] (SI, one scalar field) and [[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations|QFT §C1.9]] (natural units, any number of fields); the field equations follow in [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity|§C1.2]].

*Conventions* (as Relativity, [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor|REL §B4.2]]): SI units with $\varepsilon_0$, $\mu_0$ and $c = 1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\mu_0}$ explicit; $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1)$; $x^\mu = (ct, \vec x)$, $\partial_\mu = (\partial_t/c, \nabla)$, $d^4x = c\,dt\,d^3x$; four-potential $A^\mu = (V/c, \vec A)$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-1|REL Def. §B4.2.1]]), four-current $J^\mu = (c\rho, \vec J)$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-4|REL Def. §B4.2.4]]), field tensor $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ with $F^{0i} = -E_i/c$, $F^{ij} = -\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-1|REL Theorem §B4.2.1]]; sign conventions: [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^cau-b4-2-1|REL Caution: Sign conventions for the field tensor]]). The scalar potential is $V$; Zangwill and the PHY 505 notes write $\phi$ or $\varphi$ ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: The potential is V]]). Gauge transformations are $A_\mu \to A_\mu + \partial_\mu\chi$, i.e. $V \to V + \partial_t\chi$, $\vec A \to \vec A - \nabla\chi$ ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^cau-b3-2-2|REL Caution: Three ways to write a gauge transformation]]; Griffiths' $\lambda$ is $-\chi$).

## The variables and the requirements

> [!definition] Definition §C1.1.1: Field, Interaction and Matter Actions
> The action of electrodynamics is the sum
>
> $$
> S = S_{\rm field}[A] + S_{\rm int}[A; J] + S_{\rm matter}, \qquad S_{\rm field} + S_{\rm int} = \int dt\int d^3x\;\bigl(\mathcal L_{\rm field} + \mathcal L_{\rm int}\bigr) = \frac1c\int d^4x\;\bigl(\mathcal L_{\rm field} + \mathcal L_{\rm int}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> $S_{\rm field}$ depends on the potential $A_\mu$ alone, $S_{\rm matter}$ on the matter variables alone (worldlines, or matter fields), and $S_{\rm int}$ on both; the matter enters $S_{\rm int}$ through its four-current $J^\mu$. The Lagrangian densities have the unit of energy per volume ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^def-b4-1-1|REL Def. §B4.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.1.1, eq. (7.1); §7.1.3, eq. (7.6) · Zangwill §24.3.6, eq. (24.50)*

^def-c1-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why the potentials, and not the fields
> The six components of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ are not independent: $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ and Faraday's law tie them together. An action in $\vec E$, $\vec B$ would need Lagrange multipliers to enforce these constraints, like a pendulum described by $x$ and $y$ instead of its angle. With $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$ and $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A$ ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-1|Theorem §B11.1.1]]) the two homogeneous equations hold identically ([[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-4|Theorem §C1.2.4]]), and the four functions $A_\mu$ can be varied freely. The price is redundancy: the potentials are fixed by the fields only up to a gauge transformation ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-2|Theorem §B11.1.2]]), so whatever the action says must not depend on the gauge. Zangwill (Problem 24.12) shows the other option: with $V$, $\vec A$, $\vec E$, $\vec B$ all treated as independent, a first-order Lagrangian yields all four equations at once, with constraints.
>
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.1.2, §7.3.2 · Zangwill §24.3.4; Problem 24.12*

^rem-c1-1-1

> [!principle] Principle §C1.1.1: Requirements on the Electromagnetic Action
> The field and interaction actions of [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^def-c1-1-1|Def. §C1.1.1]] obey:
> 1. *Variables and locality.* $\mathcal L_{\rm field} + \mathcal L_{\rm int}$ is a function of $A_\mu$, $J^\mu$ and finitely many of their derivatives at one event.
> 2. *Relativity.* It is a Lorentz scalar, so that $S$ is one ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^def-b4-1-1|REL Def. §B4.1.1]]).
> 3. *Gauge invariance.* Under $A_\mu \to A_\mu + \partial_\mu\chi$, for every smooth $\chi$ vanishing outside a bounded region, $S_{\rm field}$ and $S_{\rm int}$ change at most by boundary terms.
> 4. *Parity and time reversal.* $\mathcal L$ is even under $\vec x \to -\vec x$ and under $t \to -t$.
> 5. *Weak, slowly varying fields.* $\mathcal L$ is quadratic in $(A_\mu, J^\mu)$ jointly and contains the fewest derivatives compatible with 1–4.
>
> *Domain:* classical electrodynamics in vacuum, at field strengths and frequencies where the field equations are linear (no field–field interaction); flat spacetime. Item 5 is an expansion about the vacuum $A_\mu = 0$, $J^\mu = 0$: constant terms do not affect the equations, linear terms would make the vacuum non-stationary, and higher orders are dropped.
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.1.3–§7.1.5 · the user's series, Part III, §12 (opening), §12.1 · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part III §"Building a Gauge-Invariant Lagrangian"*

> [!remark] Remark: The layer changes
> - *Before.* Electromagnetism assumes Maxwell's equations and the Lorentz force law as principles ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]], [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]) and derives charge conservation from them ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-3|Theorem §B8.4.3]]); Relativity keeps Maxwell's equations as principles in covariant dress ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^rem-b4-2-4|REL Remark: Layers, and what comes later]]) and turns the Lorentz force into a theorem of the particle action ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-5|REL Theorem §B3.2.5]]).
> - *Here.* The principles are Principle §C1.1.1 and the action it leaves ([[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^pr-c1-1-4|Principle §C1.1.4]]), with stationary action ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^pr-c1-9-3|QFT Principle §C1.9.3]]). Gauss's law and the Ampère–Maxwell law become Euler–Lagrange equations ([[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]), the homogeneous pair becomes an identity of the variables ([[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-4|Theorem §C1.2.4]]), charge conservation becomes a consistency condition of gauge invariance ([[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^thm-c1-3-1|Theorem §C1.3.1]], [[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^thm-c1-3-2|Theorem §C1.3.2]]), and energy–momentum conservation a Noether theorem ([[§C1.5 The Electromagnetic Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]]). The Lorentz force stays the theorem it is in Relativity, now obtained by varying the worldline in the same coupling term that sources the field ([[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^ex-c1-2-2|Example §C1.2.2]]).
> - *What is not derived.* The two constants, the sign convention of the charge, and the requirements themselves.
>
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), ch. 7 (opening): "we shall derive such theory purely based on symmetry"*

## The field Lagrangian

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.1.2: The Gauge-Invariant Quadratic Lagrangians
> Under items 1–3 and 5 of [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^pr-c1-1-1|Principle §C1.1.1]], the field Lagrangian is, up to a total divergence,
>
> $$
> \mathcal L_{\rm field} = \alpha\bigl(E^2 - c^2B^2\bigr) + \theta\,\vec E\cdot\vec B = -\frac{\alpha c^2}{2}\,F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} - \frac{\theta c}{4}\,F_{\mu\nu}\tilde F^{\mu\nu}
> $$
>
> with constants $\alpha$, $\theta$ ($\tilde F$ the dual, [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-3|REL Def. §B4.2.3]]). In particular there is no mass term $A_\mu A^\mu$. The $\theta$ term is itself a total divergence, and item 4 (parity) sets $\theta = 0$.
>
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.2.1, eqs. (7.11)–(7.16) · the user's series, Part III, §12.1 · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part III §"Why Simple Terms Don't Work" · the general-coefficient computation of steps 2–5 written out here*

^thm-c1-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The catalogue.** The building blocks are $A_\mu$ (4 components) and $\partial_\mu A_\nu$ (16); the only Lorentz-invariant tensors available to contract their indices are $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ and $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-2|REL Theorem §B2.2.2]], [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-4|REL Theorem §B2.2.4]]). Quadratic terms, sorted by the number of derivatives (item 5 stops at two):
> - no derivative: $A_\mu A^\mu$;
> - one derivative: $A_\mu\,\partial_\nu A_\rho$ carries three indices; $\eta$ contracts indices in pairs and $\epsilon$ takes four, so no scalar exists (the 2+1-dimensional Chern–Simons term $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}A_\mu\partial_\nu A_\rho$ has no four-dimensional analogue);
> - two derivatives: four indices, contracted by two $\eta$'s in three ways or by one $\epsilon$:
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = m\,A_\mu A^\mu + a_1\,\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu + a_2\,\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\nu A^\mu + a_3\,(\partial_\mu A^\mu)^2 + e\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial_\rho A_\sigma .
> $$
>
> (Other placements of the $\epsilon$ indices give the same term up to sign, by relabelling dummies. Terms with both derivatives on one factor, $A_\nu\,\partial_\mu\partial_\rho A_\sigma$ contracted in any way, differ from these by a total divergence, $A_\nu\,\partial_\mu\partial_\rho A_\sigma = \partial_\mu(A_\nu\,\partial_\rho A_\sigma) - \partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial_\rho A_\sigma$, so they add nothing new once step 2 allows divergences.)
>
> **2. Trade $a_2$ for $a_3$.** By the product rule,
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu\bigl(A_\nu\partial^\nu A^\mu\bigr) - \partial_\nu\bigl(A^\nu\partial_\mu A^\mu\bigr) = \partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\nu A^\mu + A_\nu\,\partial_\mu\partial^\nu A^\mu - (\partial_\nu A^\nu)(\partial_\mu A^\mu) - A^\nu\,\partial_\nu\partial_\mu A^\mu ,
> $$
>
> and the second and fourth terms cancel ($A_\nu\partial^\nu = A^\nu\partial_\nu$, one index raised and one lowered; [[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]). So $\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\nu A^\mu = (\partial_\mu A^\mu)^2 + \partial_\mu\bigl(A_\nu\partial^\nu A^\mu - A^\mu\partial_\nu A^\nu\bigr)$ (dummies renamed in the last term). A divergence changes neither the field equations ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1-9-5|QFT Theorem §C1.9.5]]) nor gauge invariance up to boundary terms, so with $b \equiv a_2 + a_3$,
>
> $$
> \mathcal L \simeq m\,A_\mu A^\mu + a_1\,\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu + b\,(\partial_\mu A^\mu)^2 + e\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial_\rho A_\sigma ,
> $$
>
> $\simeq$ meaning "up to a total divergence".
>
> **3. The $\epsilon$ term is a divergence.** $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial_\rho A_\sigma = \partial_\mu\bigl(\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}A_\nu\,\partial_\rho A_\sigma\bigr) - \epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}A_\nu\,\partial_\mu\partial_\rho A_\sigma$, and the last term vanishes: $\partial_\mu\partial_\rho$ is symmetric, $\epsilon$ antisymmetric in $\mu\rho$ ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-5|REL Theorem §B2.2.5]]). It is also gauge invariant, since $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma} = 4\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial_\rho A_\sigma$ (expand each $F = \partial A - \partial A$; the four terms are equal by antisymmetry of $\epsilon$), so $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial_\rho A_\sigma = \frac14\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma} = \frac12F_{\mu\nu}\tilde F^{\mu\nu}$. It is kept aside.
>
> **4. Impose gauge invariance.** Replace $A_\mu \to A_\mu + \partial_\mu\chi$ with $\chi$ smooth and of bounded support. The change of the remaining terms is exactly
>
> $$
> \delta\mathcal L = m\bigl(2A^\mu\partial_\mu\chi + \partial_\mu\chi\,\partial^\mu\chi\bigr) + a_1\bigl(2\,\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\mu\partial^\nu\chi + \partial_\mu\partial_\nu\chi\,\partial^\mu\partial^\nu\chi\bigr) + b\bigl(2\,(\partial_\mu A^\mu)\,\Box\chi + (\Box\chi)^2\bigr),
> $$
>
> with $\Box = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu$. Its integral must vanish for all $A$ and $\chi$. The part linear in $A$ must vanish separately (replace $A$ by $sA$ and compare powers of $s$). Integrate it by parts, moving every derivative off $A$ (boundary terms vanish because $\chi$ has bounded support; [[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-1|452 Thm. §17.1]]):
>
> $$
> \int d^4x\;A_\nu\Bigl[2m\,\partial^\nu\chi - 2a_1\,\Box\partial^\nu\chi - 2b\,\partial^\nu\Box\chi\Bigr] = 0 \qquad\text{for every } A .
> $$
>
> Taking $A$ a test function in each component, the fundamental lemma ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|CM Lemma §B5.1.2]], in four variables) gives $m\,\partial^\nu\chi = (a_1 + b)\,\partial^\nu\Box\chi$ for every $\chi$. Rescale the argument, $\chi_\lambda(x) = \chi(\lambda x)$: the left side scales as $\lambda$, the right as $\lambda^3$, so $m\lambda f = (a_1 + b)\lambda^3g$ for all $\lambda > 0$ with $f = \partial^\nu\chi$, $g = \partial^\nu\Box\chi$ at the rescaled point. Choosing $\chi$ with $f \ne 0$ somewhere gives $m = 0$; choosing $\chi = (x^0)^3$ near the origin (times a cutoff that is $1$ there), where $\partial^0\Box\chi = 6$, gives $a_1 + b = 0$.
>
> **5. The survivor.** With $m = 0$, $b = -a_1$, the quadratic-in-$\chi$ part is $a_1\bigl[\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\chi\,\partial^\mu\partial^\nu\chi - (\Box\chi)^2\bigr] = a_1\,\partial_\mu\bigl(\partial_\nu\chi\,\partial^\mu\partial^\nu\chi - \partial^\mu\chi\,\Box\chi\bigr)$, a divergence, as it must be. Undo step 2:
>
> $$
> a_1\bigl[\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu - (\partial_\mu A^\mu)^2\bigr] \simeq a_1\bigl[\partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial_\mu A_\nu\,\partial^\nu A^\mu\bigr] = \frac{a_1}{2}\,F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu},
> $$
>
> since expanding $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = (\partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu)(\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu)$ gives four terms, equal in pairs after relabelling $\mu \leftrightarrow \nu$.
>
> **6. Three-vector form.** $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = 2(B^2 - E^2/c^2)$ and $F_{\mu\nu}\tilde F^{\mu\nu} = -\frac4c\,\vec E\cdot\vec B$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-3|REL Theorem §B4.2.3]]). So $\frac{a_1}{2}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = a_1(B^2 - E^2/c^2) = \alpha(E^2 - c^2B^2)$ with $\alpha = -a_1/c^2$, and $e\,\epsilon\,\partial A\,\partial A = \frac e2F_{\mu\nu}\tilde F^{\mu\nu} = -\frac{2e}{c}\,\vec E\cdot\vec B = \theta\,\vec E\cdot\vec B$ with $\theta = -2e/c$.
>
> **7. Parity.** Under $\vec x \to -\vec x$, $\vec E \to -\vec E$ (polar) and $\vec B \to \vec B$ (axial), so $\vec E\cdot\vec B \to -\vec E\cdot\vec B$ while $E^2 - c^2B^2$ is even; item 4 forces $\theta = 0$. Time reversal ($\vec E \to \vec E$, $\vec B \to -\vec B$) gives the same.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Lorentz invariance alone leaves three independent coefficients $m$, $a_1$, $b$ (and $e$); gauge invariance kills the mass $m$ and ties $b$ to $a_1$. ⚑ By-product: "the photon is massless", "the theory is gauge invariant" and "$F$ contains no bare $A$" are one statement ([[§C1.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^rem-c1-7-2|QFT Remark: Electrodynamics as the template for field theory]]); what a mass term would do is the ★ Proca example, [[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^ex-c1-3-2|★ Example §C1.3.2]].
> - ⚑ By-product: the gauge-invariant combination of first derivatives is $F_{\mu\nu}$, the same antisymmetric part that the particle's variation produced ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^rem-b3-2-3|REL Remark: The variation manufactures the field tensor]]).
> - The overall constant $\alpha$, including its sign, is not fixed by symmetry: [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^pr-c1-1-4|Principle §C1.1.4]].

> [!remark] Remark: Three routes to the same Lagrangian
> - *The typed notes* argue with gauge-invariant objects from the start: $\alpha A_\mu A^\mu$ changes under a gauge transformation and is excluded; terms $A\,\partial A$ give no scalar; quadratic terms in the gauge-invariant $\vec E$, $\vec B$ that are Lorentz scalars are $E^2 - c^2B^2$ and $\vec E\cdot\vec B$ (the invariants of [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-3|REL Theorem §B4.2.3]]); parity or time reversal removes $\vec E\cdot\vec B$. Theorem §C1.1.2 adds the proof that no gauge-variant combination of $\partial A\,\partial A$ survives, which "one can verify" leaves to the reader.
> - *The series* screens the same catalogue and drops $F\tilde F$ because it is a total divergence; parity is not needed for the classical equations.
> - *Zangwill* stays non-relativistic: a scalar quadratic in $\vec E$, $\vec B$ is $a\,E^2 + g\,\vec E\cdot\vec B + b\,B^2$; parity forces $g = 0$ (or $a = b = 0$, excluded because electrostatics and magnetostatics must each have a Lagrangian); the ratio $b/a = -c^2$ is then fixed only by checking the field equations. Covariance fixes it at once.
> - The Lagrangian of a charged particle in a given field is Lorentz's (1892); the full Lagrangian with $V$ and $\vec A$ as the coordinates of the field, and the derivation of both Maxwell's equations and the force law from it, is Schwarzschild's (1903) (Zangwill's historical box, §24.3.6).
>
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.2.1 · the user's series, Part III, §12.1 · Zangwill §24.3.3, eqs. (24.28)–(24.29); §24.3.6 (A Brief History of Electromagnetic Lagrangians)*

## Masses: reading the quadratic part

> [!remark] Remark: How a quadratic Lagrangian is read
> For fields $\phi_a$ with $\mathcal L = \frac12 Z_{ab}\,\partial_\mu\phi_a\,\partial^\mu\phi_b - \frac12 M^2_{ab}\,\phi_a\phi_b + \dots$ (natural units, or $M^2 \to M^2c^2/\hbar^2$ in SI):
> - the derivative term is the **kinetic term**; $Z$ must be positive definite for positive energy, and rescaling the fields makes it $\delta_{ab}$ (canonical normalization; [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^mod-b4-1-3|REL Model §B4.1.3]], [[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^mod-c1-9-6|QFT Model §C1.9.6]]);
> - the non-derivative quadratic term is the **mass term**: after $Z = 1$ and a rotation that diagonalizes $M^2$ ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]), each eigenvector is a field obeying the Klein–Gordon equation with mass$^2$ equal to its eigenvalue;
> - cubic and higher terms are **interactions**; constant solutions sit at stationary points of the potential $V(\phi)$ (everything without derivatives, with a minus sign), and the masses of fluctuations are the second derivatives of $V$ there.
>
> For the electromagnetic field the second item reads: $\mathcal L_{\rm field}$ has no non-derivative term (Theorem §C1.1.2), so its waves have the massless dispersion $\omega = c|\vec k|$ ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]]). The three PHY 505 problems below exercise this reading on scalar fields before it is applied to $A_\mu$.
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 5, Problems 1–3 (comments: "the simplest example of a classical field theory with two massive fields", "Landau's theory of phase transitions") · the user's series, Part III, §8.2*

^rem-c1-1-4

> [!example] Example §C1.1.1: Solutions of the Klein–Gordon Equation, and Why m Is a Mass
> Solve $(\partial_\mu\partial^\mu + m^2)\phi = 0$ (natural units) and say why $m$ is the mass of the associated particle.
>
> *Solution.* **1. Modes.** For initial data $\phi(0, \vec x)$, $\dot\phi(0, \vec x)$ in Schwartz space, Fourier transform in space ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^def-ca-3-1|QFT Def. §CA.3.1]]; inversion: [[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-1|QFT Theorem §CA.3.1]]): $\phi(t, \vec x) = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\tilde\phi(t, \vec k)\,e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}$. Since $\partial_\mu\partial^\mu = \partial_t^2 - \nabla^2$ and $\nabla^2e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x} = -|\vec k|^2e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-4|QFT Theorem §CA.3.4]]), each mode obeys $\ddot{\tilde\phi} + \omega_k^2\tilde\phi = 0$ with $\omega_k = \sqrt{|\vec k|^2 + m^2}$: a harmonic oscillator.
>
> **2. General solution.** $\tilde\phi(t, \vec k) = a(\vec k)\,e^{-i\omega_kt} + b(\vec k)\,e^{i\omega_kt}$; reality of $\phi$ forces $b(\vec k) = a^*(-\vec k)$. Relabelling $\vec k \to -\vec k$ in the second term,
>
> $$
> \phi(x) = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\Bigl[a(\vec k)\,e^{-ik\cdot x} + a^*(\vec k)\,e^{ik\cdot x}\Bigr]_{k^0 = \omega_k}, \qquad k\cdot x = \omega_kt - \vec k\cdot\vec x ,
> $$
>
> a superposition of the plane waves of [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-5|REL Theorem §B4.1.5]], with $a$ fixed by the initial data. Every solution with such data is of this form, because each mode's oscillator equation has a unique solution.
>
> **3. Why $m$ is a mass.** The dispersion relation $\omega^2 = |\vec k|^2 + m^2$, with $E = \hbar\omega$ and $\vec p = \hbar\vec k$ and units restored, is $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4$, the energy–momentum relation of a particle of mass $m$ ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-5|REL Theorem §B4.1.5]]). Three further checks: the $\vec k = 0$ mode oscillates at $\omega = mc^2/\hbar$, the rest energy; wave packets move at the group velocity $d\omega/dk = pc^2/E$, the particle's velocity ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-6|REL Theorem §B4.1.6]]); a static source produces a field of range $\hbar/mc$ ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^rem-b4-1-4|REL Remark: Static solutions and the Yukawa potential]]). After quantization each mode of wave number $\vec k$ carries quanta of energy $\hbar\omega_k$, which makes the identification literal ([[§C2.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2-4-2|QFT Theorem §C2.4.2]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 5, Problem 1 · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part III §"Plane Wave Solutions and Dispersion"*

> [!example] Example §C1.1.2: Constant Solutions of the Quartic Scalar Theory
> For $\mathcal L = \frac12\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi - \frac{m^2}{2}\phi^2 - \frac\lambda4\phi^4$ with $\lambda > 0$, find the equation of motion and its constant solutions for $m^2 > 0$ and $m^2 < 0$.
>
> *Solution.* **1. Equation of motion.** $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\phi = -m^2\phi - \lambda\phi^3$ and $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi) = \partial^\mu\phi$ ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^cau-c1-9-3|QFT Caution: Rename the dummy index before differentiating]]), so [[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^thm-c1-9-4|QFT Theorem §C1.9.4]] gives
>
> $$
> \Box\phi + m^2\phi + \lambda\phi^3 = 0, \qquad\text{i.e.}\quad \Box\phi = -V'(\phi), \quad V(\phi) = \frac{m^2}{2}\phi^2 + \frac\lambda4\phi^4 .
> $$
>
> **2. Constant solutions.** $\phi = v$ constant has $\Box v = 0$, so $v(m^2 + \lambda v^2) = 0$. For $m^2 > 0$ the bracket is positive and $v = 0$ is the only solution. For $m^2 < 0$ there are three: $v = 0$ and $v = \pm v_0$, $v_0 = \sqrt{-m^2/\lambda}$.
>
> **3. Stability.** Put $\phi = v + \eta$ and keep the terms linear in $\eta$: $V'(v + \eta) = V'(v) + V''(v)\eta + O(\eta^2)$ with $V'(v) = 0$, so $\Box\eta + V''(v)\eta = 0$, the Klein–Gordon equation with mass$^2$ $V''(v) = m^2 + 3\lambda v^2$. At $v = 0$ with $m^2 < 0$ the mass$^2$ is negative: modes with $|\vec k|^2 < -m^2$ have $\omega_k$ imaginary and grow as $e^{\sqrt{-m^2 - |\vec k|^2}\,t}$, so $\phi = 0$ is unstable. At $v = \pm v_0$, $V'' = m^2 + 3\lambda(-m^2/\lambda) = -2m^2 > 0$: stable, with fluctuation mass $\sqrt{2|m^2|}$. The energy density of a constant field is $V(v)$ (no gradients), and $V(\pm v_0) = -m^4/4\lambda < V(0) = 0$: the two stable solutions are the states of lowest energy.
>
> **4. Reading.** $\mathcal L$ is symmetric under $\phi \to -\phi$, but each lowest-energy solution is not: the symmetry is spontaneously broken. In Landau's theory of phase transitions $\phi$ is the order parameter and $m^2 \propto T - T_c$ changes sign at the transition; the constant classical field is the mean field.
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 5, Problem 2 (and its comment on Landau's theory)*

> [!example] Example §C1.1.3: Two Mixed Scalar Fields
> Two real fields with $\mathcal L = \frac12\partial_\mu\phi_1\,\partial^\mu\phi_1 + \frac12\partial_\mu\phi_2\,\partial^\mu\phi_2 - \frac{m_1^2}{2}\phi_1^2 - \frac{m_2^2}{2}\phi_2^2 - \frac{\mu^2}{2}\phi_1\phi_2$. Find a linear transformation to fields $\phi'_1$, $\phi'_2$ with no mixing term, and the new Lagrangian.
>
> *Statement.* The problem as set writes the kinetic part as $\frac12\partial_\mu\phi_1\partial^\mu\phi_2 + \frac12\partial_\mu\phi_1\partial^\mu\phi_2$ and asks for "no diagonal mass term"; read literally, the kinetic form $\partial_\mu\phi_1\,\partial^\mu\phi_2 = \frac12(\partial\phi_+)^2 - \frac12(\partial\phi_-)^2$, $\phi_\pm = (\phi_1 \pm \phi_2)/\sqrt2$, would contain a field with negative kinetic energy. The intended problem is the one above: remove the off-diagonal (mixing) term.
>
> *Solution.* **1. Matrix form.** With $\phi = (\phi_1, \phi_2)^{\mathsf T}$, $\mathcal L = \frac12\partial_\mu\phi^{\mathsf T}\partial^\mu\phi - \frac12\phi^{\mathsf T}M^2\phi$, where $M^2 = \begin{pmatrix} m_1^2 & \mu^2/2 \\ \mu^2/2 & m_2^2\end{pmatrix}$ (check: $\phi^{\mathsf T}M^2\phi = m_1^2\phi_1^2 + m_2^2\phi_2^2 + \mu^2\phi_1\phi_2$).
>
> **2. Rotate.** Put $\phi = R\phi'$, $R = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\vartheta & -\sin\vartheta \\ \sin\vartheta & \cos\vartheta\end{pmatrix}$. $R$ is constant, so $\partial_\mu\phi = R\,\partial_\mu\phi'$, and $R^{\mathsf T}R = 1$ leaves the kinetic term unchanged. The mass matrix becomes $R^{\mathsf T}M^2R$, whose off-diagonal entry is
>
> $$
> (R^{\mathsf T}M^2R)_{12} = (m_2^2 - m_1^2)\sin\vartheta\cos\vartheta + \frac{\mu^2}{2}\bigl(\cos^2\vartheta - \sin^2\vartheta\bigr) = \frac12\bigl[(m_2^2 - m_1^2)\sin2\vartheta + \mu^2\cos2\vartheta\bigr] .
> $$
>
> It vanishes for $\tan2\vartheta = \mu^2/(m_1^2 - m_2^2)$; take $\cos2\vartheta = (m_1^2 - m_2^2)/\Delta$, $\sin2\vartheta = \mu^2/\Delta$ with $\Delta = \sqrt{(m_1^2 - m_2^2)^2 + \mu^4}$ ($\vartheta = \pi/4$ if $m_1 = m_2$).
>
> **3. The new masses.** The diagonal entries are $(R^{\mathsf T}M^2R)_{11} = m_1^2\cos^2\vartheta + m_2^2\sin^2\vartheta + \mu^2\sin\vartheta\cos\vartheta$ and $(R^{\mathsf T}M^2R)_{22} = m_1^2\sin^2\vartheta + m_2^2\cos^2\vartheta - \mu^2\sin\vartheta\cos\vartheta$. With the half-angle forms and step 2,
>
> $$
> M_{1,2}^2 = \frac{m_1^2 + m_2^2}{2} \pm \frac12\sqrt{(m_1^2 - m_2^2)^2 + \mu^4}, \qquad \mathcal L = \sum_{a = 1}^2\Bigl[\tfrac12\partial_\mu\phi'_a\,\partial^\mu\phi'_a - \tfrac12M_a^2\,\phi'^2_a\Bigr],
> $$
>
> two decoupled Klein–Gordon fields: the eigenvalues of $M^2$ ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]). Both are positive, so both fields are stable, exactly when $\det M^2 = m_1^2m_2^2 - \mu^4/4 > 0$.
>
> **4. Reading.** The fields with definite mass, $\phi'_a$, are not the ones in which the coupling was written; this is the classical skeleton of neutral-meson and neutrino mixing. A non-trivial kinetic matrix would first be brought to $\delta_{ab}$ by a non-orthogonal rescaling, and only then rotated.
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 5, Problem 3*

## The coupling and the action

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.1.3: The Lowest-Order Coupling
> Under [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^pr-c1-1-1|Principle §C1.1.1]], items 1, 2 and 5, the only term coupling the potential to the four-current is, up to a constant factor $\alpha'$,
>
> $$
> \mathcal L_{\rm int} = -\alpha'\,J^\mu A_\mu = -\alpha'\bigl(\rho V - \vec J\cdot\vec A\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Item 3 then requires $\partial_\mu J^\mu = 0$ ([[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^thm-c1-3-1|Theorem §C1.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.2.2, eq. (7.18) · the user's series, Part III, §12.1 · Zangwill §24.3.6, eq. (24.50)*

^thm-c1-1-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Bilinear and local.** A term coupling field and matter, quadratic jointly (item 5) and vanishing without either, is bilinear in $J$ and $A$ (terms $J_\mu J^\mu$ belong to $S_{\rm matter}$; a term linear in $A$ alone, $c_\mu A^\mu$, needs a constant four-vector $c_\mu$, which is not Lorentz invariant).
>
> **2. No derivative.** $J^\mu A_\nu$ has two indices; the scalar is $J^\mu A_\mu$ ($\epsilon$ needs four indices).
>
> **3. One derivative.** $J^\mu\partial_\nu A_\rho$ and $A^\mu\partial_\nu J_\rho$ have three indices: no scalar. Two derivatives give gauge-invariant candidates such as $J^\mu\,\partial^\nu F_{\nu\mu}$, which item 5 drops as higher order in the slow variation.
>
> **4. Components.** $J^\mu A_\mu = J^0A_0 + J^iA_i = c\rho\cdot\frac Vc + J^i(-A^i) = \rho V - \vec J\cdot\vec A$, since lowering a spatial index changes the sign ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-4|REL Def. §B1.1.4]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The coupling is as forced as the field Lagrangian; its sign and size are conventions that define the charge relative to the potential ([[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^pr-c1-1-4|Principle §C1.1.4]]).
> - ⚑ By-product: under a gauge transformation it changes by $-J^\mu\partial_\mu\chi$, a divergence only if $\partial_\mu J^\mu = 0$ → [[§C1.3 Gauge Symmetry and Charge Conservation#^thm-c1-3-1|Theorem §C1.3.1]].

^der-c1-1-3

*Uses:* [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^pr-c1-1-1|Principle §C1.1.1]], [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-4|REL Def. §B1.1.4]]

> [!principle] Principle §C1.1.4: The Maxwell Action
> The electromagnetic potential $A_\mu$ coupled to matter of four-current $J^\mu$ has the action
>
> $$
> S[A] = \int dt\int d^3x\;\Bigl[-\frac{1}{4\mu_0}\,F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} - J^\mu A_\mu\Bigr] + S_{\rm matter}, \qquad \varepsilon_0\mu_0 = \frac{1}{c^2},
> $$
>
> and the physical $A_\mu$ makes it stationary under all variations $\delta A_\mu$ vanishing near the boundary of every bounded spacetime region ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^pr-c1-9-3|QFT Principle §C1.9.3]]). This is Theorems §C1.1.2–§C1.1.3 with $\theta = 0$, $\alpha' = 1$ and $\alpha = \varepsilon_0/2$.
>
> *Domain:* that of [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^pr-c1-1-1|Principle §C1.1.1]]; $J^\mu$ either prescribed and conserved, or produced by $S_{\rm matter}$. In matter the fields are averaged and the action changes ([[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter|§C5.1]]).
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.3.3, eqs. (7.42)–(7.49) · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part III §"The Complete EM Lagrangian", §"Derivation of Maxwell's Equations from the Lagrangian" · the user's series, Part III, §12.1, eq. (maxwell-lagrangian) (Heaviside–Lorentz) · Zangwill §24.3.6, eq. (24.50)*

> [!remark] Remark: What fixes the size and the sign
> - *Overall factor.* $\mathcal L$ and $k\mathcal L$ give the same field equations for any constant $k \ne 0$, so one of $\alpha$, $\alpha'$ is free. Setting $\alpha' = 1$ defines how strongly a unit current couples, i.e. it fixes the unit of the potential relative to that of charge; the typed notes choose it so that the field energy comes out in the standard form ([[§C1.5 The Electromagnetic Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c1-5-3|Theorem §C1.5.3]]).
> - *The ratio.* The field equation for $V$ is $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac{\alpha'}{2\alpha}\rho$ (the computation of [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]], item 2, with general coefficients). Coulomb's law fixes $\frac{\alpha'}{2\alpha} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}$: "the only thing that the symmetry cannot tell us". The speed $c$ enters through the Lorentz transformations; $\mu_0 = 1/\varepsilon_0c^2$ is then not a further constant.
> - *The sign.* With $\alpha' = 1$, $\alpha > 0$ is forced by $\varepsilon_0 > 0$ (like charges repel). Independently, $\alpha > 0$ makes the coefficient of $(\partial_t\vec A)^2$ positive ([[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]]) and the energy density $u \ge 0$ ([[§C1.5 The Electromagnetic Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c1-5-5|Theorem §C1.5.5]]): kinetic minus potential, as for any field ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^rem-c1-9-3|QFT Remark: Kinetic minus potential, read term by term]]). This is the separate fixing of the sign that [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^cau-b4-2-2|REL Caution: The sign of the first invariant]] refers to.
>
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.2.2 (the sign "makes α′ > 0"), §7.3.2, eq. (7.29), §7.3.3, eqs. (7.46)–(7.48) · the user's series, Part III, §12.1*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.1.5: The Maxwell Lagrangian in Three-Vector Form
> The Lagrangian density of [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^pr-c1-1-4|Principle §C1.1.4]] is
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2 - \frac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2 - \rho V + \vec J\cdot\vec A = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\bigl|\nabla V + \partial_t\vec A\bigr|^2 - \frac{1}{2\mu_0}\bigl|\nabla\times\vec A\bigr|^2 - \rho V + \vec J\cdot\vec A .
> $$
>
> $\partial_t V$ does not occur; $\partial_t\vec A$ occurs only through $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}|\partial_t\vec A|^2 + \varepsilon_0\,\partial_t\vec A\cdot\nabla V$, with positive coefficient.
>
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.3.2, eq. (7.23); §7.3.3, eq. (7.48) · the user's PHY 505 notes, Part III §"The Complete EM Lagrangian" ("In traditional form") · Zangwill §24.3.4, eq. (24.31)*

^thm-c1-1-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The field term.** $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = 2(B^2 - E^2/c^2)$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-3|REL Theorem §B4.2.3]]), so $-\frac{1}{4\mu_0}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = -\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} + \frac{E^2}{2\mu_0c^2}$, and $\frac{1}{\mu_0c^2} = \varepsilon_0$.
>
> **2. The coupling.** $J^\mu A_\mu = \rho V - \vec J\cdot\vec A$ (step 4 of [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^der-c1-1-3|Derivation §C1.1.3]]), with the minus sign of the action in front.
>
> **3. Potentials.** $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A$ and $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$ give $E^2 = |\nabla V + \partial_t\vec A|^2 = |\partial_t\vec A|^2 + 2\,\partial_t\vec A\cdot\nabla V + |\nabla V|^2$; $B^2$ contains only spatial derivatives of $\vec A$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ By-product: $V$ appears without a time derivative, so it has no conjugate momentum; its field equation (Gauss's law) constrains the data on a time slice instead of evolving them → [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]].
> - The energy-like part $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}|\partial_t\vec A|^2$ is "kinetic"; $\frac{1}{2\mu_0}|\nabla\times\vec A|^2$ is a potential energy of spatial variation, the field analogue of the spring energy of a chain ([[§C1.9 The Action Principle and the Euler–Lagrange Equations#^rem-c1-9-3|QFT Remark: Kinetic minus potential, read term by term]]).

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-3|REL Theorem §B4.2.3]], [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^der-c1-1-3|Derivation §C1.1.3]], [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-1|Theorem §B11.1.1]]

## A point charge

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.1.6: The Current of a Point Charge, and Its Coupling
> A charge $q$ on the worldline $x^\mu(\tau)$, with four-velocity $u^\mu = dx^\mu/d\tau$, has the four-current
>
> $$
> J^\mu(x) = qc\int d\tau\;u^\mu(\tau)\,\delta^4\bigl(x - x(\tau)\bigr), \qquad\text{in a frame}\quad \rho = q\,\delta^3\bigl(\vec x - \vec r(t)\bigr), \quad \vec J = q\,\vec v(t)\,\delta^3\bigl(\vec x - \vec r(t)\bigr) .
> $$
>
> It is a (vector-valued) distribution, not a function: it acts on a test function $f$ by $\frac1c\int d^4x\,J^\mu f = q\int d\tau\,u^\mu f(x(\tau))$. Its interaction action is the particle coupling of Relativity,
>
> $$
> S_{\rm int} = -\int dt\int d^3x\;J^\mu A_\mu = -q\int A_\mu\,dx^\mu = \int dt\,\bigl[-qV(\vec r, t) + q\,\vec v\cdot\vec A(\vec r, t)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §12.4, eq. (point-current) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.4.2, eqs. (7.55)–(7.60) · Zangwill §24.3.6*

^thm-c1-1-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. In what sense.** $\delta^4$ is the distribution $f \mapsto f(0)$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-2|QFT Def. §CA.2.2]]; working rules: [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]). The formula for $J^\mu$ is shorthand for the linear map $f \mapsto qc\int d\tau\,u^\mu(\tau)f(x(\tau))$ on test functions, obtained by integrating $\delta^4(x - x(\tau))f(x)$ over $x$ first. The map is continuous, and it is not $\int(\text{function})f$ for any locally integrable function, since it is supported on a curve, a set of measure zero ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-1|QFT Theorem §CA.2.1]]).
>
> **2. The time component.** Write $\delta^4(x - x(\tau)) = \delta\bigl(c\,t - c\,t(\tau)\bigr)\,\delta^3\bigl(\vec x - \vec r(t(\tau))\bigr)$ and change the integration variable from $\tau$ to $t' = t(\tau)$. The map is increasing, with $dt'/d\tau = \gamma > 0$; the Jacobian turns $u^0\,d\tau = c\gamma\,d\tau$ into $c\,dt'$. With $\delta(c(t - t')) = \frac1c\,\delta(t - t')$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-5|QFT Theorem §CA.2.5]], 1),
>
> $$
> J^0 = qc\int dt'\;c\cdot\frac1c\,\delta(t - t')\,\delta^3\bigl(\vec x - \vec r(t')\bigr) = c\,q\,\delta^3\bigl(\vec x - \vec r(t)\bigr) = c\rho .
> $$
>
> **3. The spatial components.** The same change of variables with $u^i\,d\tau = \gamma v^i\,d\tau = v^i\,dt'$ gives $J^i = qc\int dt'\,v^i(t')\frac1c\delta(t - t')\delta^3(\dots) = q\,v^i(t)\,\delta^3(\vec x - \vec r(t))$.
>
> **4. The coupling.** On a slab $t_1 \le t \le t_2$ the worldline is a compact arc $\tau_1 \le \tau \le \tau_2$, so the pairing with the smooth $A_\mu$ (not of bounded support) is the finite integral $\int dt\int d^3x\,J^\mu A_\mu = \frac1c\int d^4x\,J^\mu A_\mu = q\int_{\tau_1}^{\tau_2}d\tau\,u^\mu A_\mu(x(\tau)) = q\int A_\mu\,dx^\mu$. In a frame, $A_\mu\,dx^\mu = (V - \vec v\cdot\vec A)\,dt$ ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-2|REL Theorem §B3.2.2]]); integrating $\delta^3$ against $V$, $\vec A$ directly gives the same.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - One term of the total action, $-\int J^\mu A_\mu$, is the field-side face of the particle coupling $-q\int A_\mu dx^\mu$ of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^pr-b3-2-1|REL Principle §B3.2.1]]: varied with respect to the worldline it gives the Lorentz force, with respect to $A_\mu$ the source of Maxwell's equations ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^rem-b3-2-1|REL Remark: Why this coupling]]; [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]).
> - ⚑ By-product: the field that this source produces is singular on the worldline, and $\int\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2\,d^3x \propto \int r^{-4}\,r^2dr$ diverges at the charge. $S_{\rm field}$ of a point charge's own field is not defined: the self-energy problem ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-7|Theorem §C2.3.7]]). The action of this note is used with either the current or the field external.
> - ⚑ By-product: the reparametrization invariance of $\int d\tau\,u^\mu\delta^4$ (any increasing parameter gives the same $J^\mu$) is the field-side form of that of the worldline action ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^pr-b3-1-2|REL Principle §B3.1.2]]).
