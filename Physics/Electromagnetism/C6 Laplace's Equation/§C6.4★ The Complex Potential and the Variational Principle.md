Two further tools for Laplace's equation. In two dimensions every potential is the real part of an analytic function, the complex potential, whose imaginary part counts field lines; analytic maps carry solutions to solutions, so an awkward boundary can be mapped onto an easy one. In any dimension the solution of a Dirichlet problem is the minimizer of the field energy among all functions with the right boundary values, Dirichlet's principle, which turns trial functions into upper bounds on the energy. Level B pointed to both: the slot series summed with complex logarithms ([[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^ex-b3-3-2|Example §B3.3.2]]) and the uniqueness proofs, which were already energy arguments ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]). The complex-variable machinery itself is at home in the Complex Variables notes (Brown and Churchill, chapters 9★–11★), linked below; what is added here is its electrostatic reading.

*Notation.* As Zangwill: $w = x + iy$ is the position in the plane (the letter $z$ stays the third Cartesian coordinate), $f(w)$ the complex potential, $g$ a mapping and $g = u + iv$ the image plane. The Complex Variables notes write $z$ for the variable and $w = f(z)$ for the image: translate before combining formulas.

## The complex potential

> [!definition] Definition §C6.4.1: Complex Potential; Flux Function; Complex Field
> For a two-dimensional field ($\vec E$ independent of $z$, no $E_z$) in a charge-free region, the **complex potential** is an analytic function $f(w) = V(x, y) + i\,\psi(x, y)$ of $w = x + iy$ whose real part is the potential. Its imaginary part $\psi$, the **flux function**, satisfies the Cauchy–Riemann equations with $V$ (Zangwill eqs. (7.107)–(7.108); [[§21 Cauchy–Riemann Equations#^def-21-1|342 Def. §21.1]]):
>
> $$
> E_x = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}, \qquad E_y = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} .
> $$
>
> The **complex field** is $\mathcal E = E_x - iE_y$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.10, eqs. (7.104)–(7.110), (7.115)*

^def-c6-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.4.1: What the Complex Potential Encodes
> Let $f = V + i\psi$ be a complex potential on a region $D$.
> 1. $V$ and $\psi$ are harmonic; the curves $V =$ const (equipotentials) and $\psi =$ const are orthogonal, so the curves $\psi =$ const are the field lines (eqs. (7.109), (7.114)).
> 2. $\mathcal E = E_x - iE_y = -f'(w)$ and $\lvert\vec E\rvert = \lvert f'(w)\rvert$ (eq. (7.115)).
> 3. *Flux.* Across a curve from $P_1$ to $P_2$, with the normal pointing to the right of the direction of travel, the flux of $\vec E$ per unit length in $z$ is $\psi(P_1) - \psi(P_2)$. Around a closed counterclockwise curve that encloses charge $\lambda$ per unit length, $\psi$ is not single-valued: it changes by $\Delta\psi = -\lambda/\varepsilon_0$.
> 4. Conversely, every potential $V$ harmonic on a simply connected charge-free region is the real part of a complex potential, unique up to an imaginary constant.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.10, eqs. (7.105)–(7.115) (part 3 and the multivaluedness are added here)*

^thm-c6-4-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Harmonic, orthogonal.** The real and imaginary parts of an analytic function are harmonic ([[§27★ Harmonic Functions#^thm-27-1|342 Thm. §27.1]]); directly, $\partial_x^2V + \partial_y^2V = \partial_x\partial_y\psi - \partial_y\partial_x\psi = 0$ from the Cauchy–Riemann equations, and likewise for $\psi$. Orthogonality: $\nabla V\cdot\nabla\psi = V_x\psi_x + V_y\psi_y = \psi_y\psi_x - \psi_x\psi_y = 0$ (eq. (7.114); [[§112★ Preservation of Angles and Scale Factors#^ex-112-2|342 Ex. §112.2]]). $\vec E = -\nabla V$ is normal to the equipotentials, hence tangent to the curves $\psi =$ const.
>
> **2. The derivative.** $f'(w)$ can be computed along the real axis: $f' = \partial_xf = V_x + i\psi_x = V_x - iV_y$ (Cauchy–Riemann: $\psi_x = -V_y$) $= -E_x + iE_y = -(E_x - iE_y)$ (eqs. (7.112)–(7.113) compute it along both axes and recover the Cauchy–Riemann equations). Then $\lvert f'\rvert^2 = E_x^2 + E_y^2$.
>
> **3. Flux.** Parametrize the curve by arc length with tangent $(dx, dy)/ds$; the normal to its right is $\hat n = (dy, -dx)/ds$. Then
>
> $$
> \vec E\cdot\hat n\,ds = E_x\,dy - E_y\,dx = -\psi_y\,dy - \psi_x\,dx = -d\psi ,
> $$
>
> and integrating from $P_1$ to $P_2$ gives $\psi(P_1) - \psi(P_2)$. For a closed counterclockwise curve the right normal is the outward one, and Gauss's law in two dimensions (charge $\lambda$ per unit length) gives $\oint\vec E\cdot\hat n\,ds = \lambda/\varepsilon_0$, so $\oint d\psi = -\lambda/\varepsilon_0$. Example: a line charge, $f = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln w$, has $V = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\rho$ ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]]) and $\psi = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\varphi$, which decreases by $\lambda/\varepsilon_0$ around the origin.
>
> **4. Existence of $\psi$.** On a simply connected region a harmonic $V$ has a harmonic conjugate, unique up to a constant ([[§115★ Harmonic Conjugates#^thm-115-4|342 Thm. §115.4]], [[§115★ Harmonic Conjugates#^prop-115-2|342 Prop. §115.2]]). Zangwill's argument is the physicist's version: $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ makes $\vec E = \nabla\times\vec C$ with $\vec C = -\psi\hat z$; that step too needs the region to have no holes containing charge, as Step 3 shows ([[§115★ Harmonic Conjugates#^ex-115-4|342 Ex. §115.4]]: $\ln r$ has no single-valued conjugate around the origin).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ $\psi$ counts field lines quantitatively: between the field lines $\psi = \psi_1$ and $\psi = \psi_2$ flows the flux $\lvert\psi_2 - \psi_1\rvert$ per unit length, so the charge per unit length on a stretch of conductor is $\varepsilon_0$ times the change of $\psi$ along it. Capacitances per unit length are read off from $\psi$ alone.
> - The roles of $V$ and $\psi$ can be exchanged: $-if$ has real part $\psi$, a potential whose equipotentials are the old field lines.

> [!example] Example §C6.4.1: The Simplest Complex Potentials
> - *Uniform field.* $f = -E_0w$: $V = -E_0x$, $\psi = -E_0y$; $\mathcal E = E_0$, i.e. $\vec E = E_0\hat x$, with horizontal field lines (eq. (7.116)).
> - *Quadrupole.* $f = -\frac12w^2 = -\frac12(x^2 - y^2) - ixy$: $\vec E = x\hat x - y\hat y$ from $\mathcal E = w = x + iy$ (eqs. (7.117)–(7.118)). The equipotentials are the hyperbolas $x^2 - y^2 =$ const; four rods shaped like them make the field of an electrostatic quadrupole lens (Zangwill Figure 7.13). More generally $f = -cw^n$ is the field near the apex of a wedge of opening $\pi/n$, the complex form of [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-5|Theorem §C6.3.5]].
> - *Line charge.* $f = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln(w - w_0)$, with field lines the rays from $w_0$ (Theorem §C6.4.1, 3).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.10.1–§7.10.2, eqs. (7.116)–(7.118), Figure 7.13*

> [!example] Example §C6.4.2: A Conducting Strip in a Uniform Field
> A grounded conducting strip $-a < x < a$, $y = 0$ (infinitely long in $z$, no net charge) sits in an applied field $E_0\hat x$ parallel to it. Take
>
> $$
> f(w) = -E_0\sqrt{w^2 - a^2}, \qquad \sqrt{w^2 - a^2} \equiv w\sqrt{1 - a^2/w^2}\ \ (\text{principal root}),
> $$
>
> which is analytic off the segment $[-a, a]$, behaves as $-E_0w$ far away, and is purely imaginary on the strip. Zangwill takes $f = \sqrt{w^2 - a^2}$, the case $E_0 = -1$, with two branch cuts running to $-\infty$ that cancel each other on $x < -a$ (Figure 7.15); the result is the same function.
>
> *Boundary values.* $1 - a^2/w^2$ is a negative real number exactly for real $w$ with $0 < \lvert w\rvert < a$, so this root is analytic off the strip. On the upper face, $w = x + i0$ with $\lvert x\rvert < a$, the imaginary part of $1 - a^2/w^2$ is $2a^2\epsilon/x^3$ for $w = x + i\epsilon$, which has the sign of $x$; so the root is $\operatorname{sgn}(x)\,i\sqrt{a^2/x^2 - 1}$ and $\sqrt{w^2 - a^2} = i\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$. On the lower face it is $-i\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$. Either way $V = \operatorname{Re}f = 0$: the strip is an equipotential, and by uniqueness ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]], with the applied field as the condition at infinity) this is the solution.
>
> *Induced charge.* $f' = -E_0w/\sqrt{w^2 - a^2}$. On the upper face $f' = -E_0x/(i\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}) = iE_0x/\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$, so $\mathcal E = -f'$ gives $E_x = 0$, $E_y = E_0x/\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$, and $\sigma_{\text{top}} = \varepsilon_0E_y$. On the lower face both the root and the normal flip, and
>
> $$
> \sigma_{\text{top}}(x) = \sigma_{\text{bottom}}(x) = \frac{\varepsilon_0E_0\,x}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \qquad (7.125),
> $$
>
> negative on the upstream half, positive downstream, with total charge zero, and with the $\rho^{-1/2}$ edge singularity of [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-5|Theorem §C6.3.5]] ($\beta = 2\pi$).
>
> *Check by the far field.* For $\lvert w\rvert \gg a$, $f = -E_0w\bigl(1 - \frac{a^2}{2w^2} + \dots\bigr) = -E_0w + \frac{E_0a^2}{2w} + \dots$; the second term is a two-dimensional dipole, $\operatorname{Re}\frac{p'}{2\pi\varepsilon_0w} = \frac{p'\cos\varphi}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\rho}$, with $p' = \pi\varepsilon_0E_0a^2$ per unit length. Directly, $p' = \int_{-a}^ax(\sigma_{\text{top}} + \sigma_{\text{bottom}})\,dx = 2\varepsilon_0E_0\int_{-a}^a\frac{x^2\,dx}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} = 2\varepsilon_0E_0\cdot\frac{\pi a^2}{2}$. They agree.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.10.3, eqs. (7.119)–(7.125), Figures 7.14–7.15 (the field strength $E_0$, the branch via the principal root and the dipole check are added here)*

## Conformal mapping

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.4.2: Analytic Maps Carry Electrostatic Solutions to Solutions
> Let $g$ map a region $D$ of the $w$ plane one-to-one and analytically onto a region $D'$ of the $g = u + iv$ plane, with $g' \ne 0$. If $\Phi(u, v)$ solves a two-dimensional electrostatic problem in $D'$ (harmonic, with given potentials on the boundary pieces), then $V = \Phi\circ g$ solves the problem in $D$ with the same potentials on the corresponding pieces, and:
> 1. $\nabla^2_{x,y}V = \lvert g'(w)\rvert^2\,(\nabla^2_{u,v}\Phi)\circ g$, so harmonic goes to harmonic;
> 2. equipotentials and field lines go to equipotentials and field lines, meeting at the same angles; field strengths scale as $\lvert\vec E\rvert_w = \lvert g'\rvert\,\lvert\vec E\rvert_g$;
> 3. the charge per unit length on corresponding boundary pieces is the same, and so is every capacitance per unit length.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.10.4 (stated in words; the formulas and part 3 are added here)*

^thm-c6-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The Laplacian.** Write $g = u + iv$ and $V(x, y) = \Phi(u(x, y), v(x, y))$. The chain rule gives $V_x = \Phi_uu_x + \Phi_vv_x$ and
>
> $$
> V_{xx} = \Phi_{uu}u_x^2 + 2\Phi_{uv}u_xv_x + \Phi_{vv}v_x^2 + \Phi_uu_{xx} + \Phi_vv_{xx} ,
> $$
>
> and the same with $y$. Adding: the coefficient of $\Phi_{uu}$ is $u_x^2 + u_y^2$, that of $\Phi_{vv}$ is $v_x^2 + v_y^2$, that of $2\Phi_{uv}$ is $u_xv_x + u_yv_y$, and the first-derivative terms carry $\nabla^2u$, $\nabla^2v$. The Cauchy–Riemann equations $u_x = v_y$, $u_y = -v_x$ give $u_x^2 + u_y^2 = v_x^2 + v_y^2 = \lvert g'\rvert^2$, $u_xv_x + u_yv_y = u_xv_x - v_xu_x = 0$, and $\nabla^2u = \nabla^2v = 0$. So $\nabla^2V = \lvert g'\rvert^2(\Phi_{uu} + \Phi_{vv})$. This is [[§116★ Transformations of Harmonic Functions#^prop-116-2|342 Prop. §116.2]].
>
> **2. Boundaries, angles, field strengths.** A boundary piece where $\Phi$ is constant maps to one where $V$ has the same constant ([[§117★ Transformations of Boundary Conditions#^thm-117-2|342 Thm. §117.2]]). Analytic maps with $g' \ne 0$ preserve angles ([[§112★ Preservation of Angles and Scale Factors#^thm-112-1|342 Thm. §112.1]]). If $F$ is the complex potential in the $g$ plane, $f = F\circ g$ is one in the $w$ plane and $f' = F'(g)\,g'$, so $\lvert\vec E\rvert_w = \lvert F'\rvert\lvert g'\rvert$ by [[§C6.4★ The Complex Potential and the Variational Principle#^thm-c6-4-1|Theorem §C6.4.1]], 2.
>
> **3. Charges.** The flux function of $f = F\circ g$ is $\operatorname{Im}F\circ g$: it takes the same values at corresponding points. By Theorem §C6.4.1, 3, the charge per unit length on a boundary piece is $\varepsilon_0$ times the change of $\psi$ along it, which is therefore the same in both planes; the potentials are the same too, so $\lambda/\Delta V$ is unchanged.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ Potentials and charges are conformal invariants, fields and charge densities are not: they scale by $\lvert g'\rvert$, which is how the edge and corner singularities appear when a smooth problem is mapped onto a pointed one.
> - With a source, Poisson's equation picks up the factor $\lvert g'\rvert^2$ ([[§116★ Transformations of Harmonic Functions#^cor-116-3|342 Cor. §116.3]]): a line charge stays a line charge of the same $\lambda$ (its delta function transforms with the Jacobian $\lvert g'\rvert^2$), while a smooth charge density changes.

> [!example] Example §C6.4.3: The Split Cylinder by a Möbius Map
> A cylinder of unit radius has its upper half grounded and its lower half at $V_0$ (infinitesimal gaps at $w = \pm1$). The map $g(w) = -i\frac{w - 1}{w + 1}$ (eq. (7.126)) sends the disk onto the upper half plane: on the circle $w = e^{i\theta}$, $\frac{w - 1}{w + 1} = i\tan\frac\theta2$, so $g = \tan\frac\theta2$ is real, positive on the upper semicircle and negative on the lower; the centre goes to $g = i$, and $w = -1$ to $g = i\infty$ (on the $v$ axis; Zangwill's caption to Figure 7.16 says "on the $u$-axis"). In the upper half plane with $0$ on the positive and $V_0$ on the negative real axis, $\Phi = \frac{V_0}{\pi}\arg g$ (a polar solution, [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]], term $\varphi$). With $g = \frac{2y + i(1 - x^2 - y^2)}{\lvert w + 1\rvert^2}$,
>
> $$
> V(x, y) = \frac{V_0}{\pi}\arg\bigl(2y + i(1 - x^2 - y^2)\bigr) \in (0, V_0) ,
> $$
>
> the angle being taken in $(0, \pi)$. Zangwill writes $\frac{V_0}{\pi}\tan^{-1}\frac{1 - x^2 - y^2}{2y}$ (eq. (7.128)), which is right for $y > 0$ but needs $+\pi$ for $y < 0$ with the principal arctangent. The equipotentials $(1 - x^2 - y^2)/2y = c$ are the circles $x^2 + (y + c)^2 = 1 + c^2$ through $(\pm1, 0)$ (eq. (7.129)). This is [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]] with $\alpha = \pi/2$, rotated so that the live segment is centred on $\varphi = -\pi/2$: there $V = \frac{V_0}{\pi}(\Theta - \frac\pi2)$ with $\Theta = \arg\frac{w - 1}{w + 1}$, and $\arg(-i\zeta) = \arg\zeta - \frac\pi2$ makes the two formulas identical. Also [[§123★ Examples (Electrostatic Potential)#^ex-123-1|342 Ex. §123.1]].
>

> [!remark] Remark: Maxwell's fringing field, and maps for polygons
> Maxwell found the field at the edge of a parallel-plate capacitor from $w = \frac{d}{2\pi}(1 + g + e^g)$ (eqs. ## Dirichlet's variational principle

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.4.3: Dirichlet's Principle
> Let $V$ solve Laplace's equation in a region $\mathcal V$ with Dirichlet data on its boundary (for instance conductors at given potentials), and let $\psi$ be any function with the same boundary values and finite energy $U_E[\psi] = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int_{\mathcal V}\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^2\,d^3r$ (Zangwill eq. (7.132)). Then
>
> $$
> U_E[\psi] = U_E[V] + \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int_{\mathcal V}\lvert\nabla(\psi - V)\rvert^2\,d^3r \ \ge\ U_E[V] ,
> $$
>
> with equality only for $\psi = V$. The same holds (i) where the boundary condition is $\partial V/\partial n = 0$, which need not be imposed on $\psi$: it is the natural condition of the minimum; (ii) in linear dielectrics, with $\frac12\int\varepsilon\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^2$, the minimizer then obeying the interface conditions of [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]]. For conductors at potentials $V_i$, $U_E[V] = \frac12\sum_iQ_iV_i$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.11, eqs. (7.132)–(7.136) (the exact identity, the natural boundary condition and the dielectric case are added here)*

^thm-c6-4-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Expand around the solution.** Write $\psi = V + \delta$ (eq. (7.133)), where $\delta$ vanishes on the Dirichlet parts of the boundary. Then $\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^2 = \lvert\nabla V\rvert^2 + 2\nabla V\cdot\nabla\delta + \lvert\nabla\delta\rvert^2$, so (eq. (7.134))
>
> $$
> U_E[\psi] - U_E[V] = \varepsilon_0\int_{\mathcal V}\nabla V\cdot\nabla\delta\,d^3r + \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int_{\mathcal V}\lvert\nabla\delta\rvert^2\,d^3r .
> $$
>
> **2. The cross term vanishes.** Product rule, $\nabla\cdot(\delta\nabla V) = \nabla\delta\cdot\nabla V + \delta\nabla^2V$, and the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]):
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V}\nabla V\cdot\nabla\delta\,d^3r = \oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\delta\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial n}\,dS - \int_{\mathcal V}\delta\,\nabla^2V\,d^3r .
> $$
>
> The volume term is zero because $\nabla^2V = 0$. The surface term is zero on the Dirichlet parts ($\delta = 0$) and on the parts where $\partial V/\partial n = 0$, whatever $\delta$ does there. At infinity (if $\mathcal V$ is unbounded) $\delta\,\partial V/\partial n = O(r^{-1})\,O(r^{-2})$ for finite-energy trial functions that fall off like the solution, and the term vanishes as in [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^der-c6-1-1|Derivation §C6.1.1]], Step 4. Zangwill's eq. (7.135) is this step, written with $\vec E = -\nabla V$.
>
> **3. The remainder.** What is left is $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int\lvert\nabla\delta\rvert^2 \ge 0$ (eq. (7.136)). It is zero only if $\nabla\delta = 0$, i.e. $\delta$ constant on each connected component, and then $\delta = 0$ because it vanishes on the boundary.
>
> **4. Natural boundary condition.** Conversely, suppose $V$ minimizes $U_E$ among functions with prescribed values on a part $S_D$ of the boundary only. Then the first variation (the cross term) must vanish for all $\delta$ that are zero on $S_D$. Taking $\delta$ with compact support inside $\mathcal V$ gives $\nabla^2V = 0$; then the surface term $\int_{S\setminus S_D}\delta\,\partial V/\partial n\,dS$ must vanish for all $\delta$, which forces $\partial V/\partial n = 0$ on $S\setminus S_D$.
>
> **5. Dielectrics.** With $\frac12\int\varepsilon\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^2$, Step 2 is done in each dielectric with $\varepsilon\delta\nabla V$; inside each dielectric the volume term needs $\nabla^2V = 0$, and on an interface the two sides give $\delta(\varepsilon_1\partial V_1/\partial n_1 + \varepsilon_2\partial V_2/\partial n_2)$, which vanishes for all $\delta$ exactly when the flux condition of [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]] holds (with $\sigma_f = 0$). The interface condition is a natural condition too.
>
> **6. The minimum value.** For the solution, Step 2 with $\delta = V$ gives $\int\lvert\nabla V\rvert^2 = \oint V\,\partial V/\partial n\,dS$. On conductor $i$ (normal out of the region, into the metal) $V = V_i$ and $\varepsilon_0\oint\partial V/\partial n\,dS = Q_i$ (Gauss's law with the normal out of the metal, $-\hat n$, as in [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]]), so $U_E[V] = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\sum_iV_i\oint_{S_i}\partial V/\partial n\,dS = \frac12\sum_iQ_iV_i$ ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ The error in the energy is quadratic in the error of the trial function, $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int\lvert\nabla\delta\rvert^2$, while the trial potential itself may be off at first order: variational estimates are good for energies, capacitances and forces, less so for fields.
> - The Euler–Lagrange equation of $U_E$ is Laplace's equation, the source-free case of the static action of [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-2|Theorem §C2.1.2]]. Dirichlet's principle varies the potential at fixed boundary potentials; Thomson's theorem varies the charge at fixed total charges ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]). The two give bounds from opposite sides: for a capacitor at voltage $\Delta V$, a trial potential gives $C \le 2U_E[\psi]/\Delta V^2$, and a trial charge distribution with energy $U_{\text{trial}}$ at charge $Q$ gives $C \ge Q^2/2U_{\text{trial}}$ (energy and capacitance: [[§C4.2 Self-Capacitance and Capacitors#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]).
> - Historically, Riemann used the principle to prove that the Dirichlet problem has a solution; Weierstrass objected that a lower bound need not be attained, and Hilbert repaired the argument in 1900 ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^rem-c6-1-4|§C6.1, Remark: Uniqueness is not existence]]).

> [!example] Example §C6.4.4: A Variational Potential for the Slot
> A slot $0 < x < L$, $y > 0$ has grounded walls and the bottom held at $V(x, 0) = \phi_0x(L - x)$ ($\phi_0$ in volts per square metre). Try $\psi = \phi_0x(L - x)e^{-py}$, which meets every boundary condition but is not harmonic, and minimize $U_E(p)$ per unit length in $z$.
>
> *Energy of the trial.* $-\nabla\psi = \phi_0e^{-py}\bigl[(2x - L)\hat x + px(L - x)\hat y\bigr]$. With $\int_0^L(2x - L)^2dx = \frac{L^3}{3}$, $\int_0^Lx^2(L - x)^2dx = \frac{L^5}{30}$ and $\int_0^\infty e^{-2py}dy = \frac{1}{2p}$,
>
> $$
> U_E(p) = \frac{\varepsilon_0\phi_0^2}{2}\Bigl[\frac{L^3}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{2p} + p^2\frac{L^5}{30}\cdot\frac{1}{2p}\Bigr] = \frac{\varepsilon_0\phi_0^2L^3}{12}\Bigl[\frac1p + \frac{pL^2}{10}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> $dU_E/dp = 0$ gives $p = \sqrt{10}/L = 3.162/L$, and $U_E = \frac{\varepsilon_0\phi_0^2L^4}{6\sqrt{10}} = 0.052705\,\varepsilon_0\phi_0^2L^4$.
>
> *Exact.* By separation, $V = \sum_nA_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}L\,e^{-n\pi y/L}$ with $A_n = \frac2L\int_0^L\phi_0x(L - x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}L\,dx = \frac{8\phi_0L^2}{n^3\pi^3}$ for odd $n$ and $0$ for even $n$. The modes are orthogonal in energy (cross terms integrate to zero in $x$), and mode $n$ carries $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}A_n^2\cdot\frac{n\pi}{2}$, so
>
> $$
> U_E[V] = \frac{\varepsilon_0\pi}{4}\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}nA_n^2 = \frac{16\,\varepsilon_0\phi_0^2L^4}{\pi^5}\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac{1}{n^5} = \frac{16\cdot1.004523}{306.020}\,\varepsilon_0\phi_0^2L^4 = 0.052521\,\varepsilon_0\phi_0^2L^4 .
> $$
>
> The one-parameter trial overestimates the energy by $0.35\,\%$, as Theorem §C6.4.3 requires, and its decay constant $\sqrt{10}/L$ is within $0.7\,\%$ of the exact $\pi/L$ of the dominant mode. The potentials agree less closely: at the centre of the bottom the trial is exact by construction ($\phi_0L^2/4$), while the dominant mode alone gives $8\phi_0L^2/\pi^3 = 0.258\,\phi_0L^2$, the rest coming from $n = 3, 5, \dots$
>
> *Source: Zangwill Example 7.5 (the exact energy and the comparison are worked out here)*
