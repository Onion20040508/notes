On conductors and dielectrics the charge is not known in advance, so Coulomb's integral is useless and the potential has to be found from Poisson's equation and data on the boundaries. Level B proved the basic facts for empty space: harmonic functions have the mean-value property and no interior extrema, and the potential is unique given $V$ (or $\partial V/\partial n$) on the boundary or the charges on the conductors ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]–[[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]). This section states the uniqueness theorem in the generality the rest of level C uses: linear dielectrics with interfaces, and conductors for each of which either the potential or the charge is given ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]). It then sharpens the mean-value and extremum properties into quantitative tools that hold with charge present: the mean-value theorem with sources, the comparison principle, which fixes the sign of a potential from the sign of its charge, and Liouville's theorem, which says that a potential with no source anywhere is constant.

*Notation.* The potential is $V$ (Zangwill writes $\varphi$; [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: The potential is V]]); the dielectric constant is $\varepsilon_r$ (Zangwill writes $\kappa$; [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^cau-c5-3-1|§C5.3, Caution: κ, ε_r, χ and χ_e]]); $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$. On a boundary surface of a region, $\hat n$ is the outward normal of the region and $\partial V/\partial n = \hat n\cdot\nabla V$.

## The boundary-value problem and its uniqueness

> [!definition] Definition §C6.1.1: Boundary-Value Problem of Potential Theory
> Let $\mathcal V$ be a connected open region bounded by closed surfaces $S_1, S_2, \dots$ and an outer surface $S_0$, which may be at infinity (Zangwill Figure 7.2). Inside $\mathcal V$ the medium is vacuum or simple (linear, isotropic) dielectric with piecewise constant $\varepsilon_r > 0$ ([[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^mod-c5-3-1|Model §C5.3.1]]), the free charge density $\rho_f$ is given, and on the interfaces $\Sigma$ between dielectrics the free surface charge $\sigma_f$ is given. The potential solves
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_r\nabla^2V = -\frac{\rho_f}{\varepsilon_0} \ \text{ in each dielectric}, \qquad V_1 = V_2, \quad \varepsilon_{r1}\frac{\partial V_1}{\partial n_1} + \varepsilon_{r2}\frac{\partial V_2}{\partial n_2} = \frac{\sigma_f}{\varepsilon_0} \ \text{ on } \Sigma
> $$
>
> (Zangwill eqs. (7.2)–(7.4); $\hat n_1$, $\hat n_2$ point out of regions 1 and 2). The **boundary data** on each $S_i$ are of one of these types:
> - **Dirichlet**: $V$ is given on $S_i$;
> - **Neumann**: $\partial V/\partial n$ is given on $S_i$;
> - **mixed**: $V$ on part of $S_i$, $\partial V/\partial n$ on the rest;
> - **conductor**: $S_i$ is the surface of a perfect conductor, so $V$ is an unknown constant $V_i$ on it, and either $V_i$ is given (Dirichlet) or its total free charge $Q_i = \oint_{S_i}\varepsilon\,\partial V/\partial n\,dS$ is given (Zangwill's choices (A) and (B), eq. (7.9)).
>
> If $S_0$ is at infinity, $V \to 0$ there is a Dirichlet condition, with $V = O(1/r)$ and $\lvert\nabla V\rvert = O(1/r^2)$. A **solution** is continuous on $\mathcal V$ up to its boundary, twice continuously differentiable inside each dielectric, and continuously differentiable up to $\Sigma$ and the $S_i$ from each side. Finding $V$ for such data is **potential theory**.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.1, eqs. (7.2)–(7.4), Figure 7.1; §7.3, Figure 7.2; §7.3.1, eqs. (7.9), (7.11), footnote 2*

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level B
> [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]] posed the problem in vacuum. Two things are new. The interface conditions are the content of $\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon\nabla V) = -\rho_f$ read as an identity between distributions: a jump of $\varepsilon\,\partial V/\partial n$ across a surface makes $\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon\nabla V)$ contain a surface delta function, and $\sigma_f$ is its weight ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]], [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]]; the surface delta function is a generalized function in the sense of [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-2|QFT Def. §CA.2.2]]). And the conductor data mix: one conductor may be held at a potential while its neighbour is isolated with a known charge. Zangwill's point of view, adopted here, is that every charge in the problems of this chapter sits on surfaces, so between the surfaces $V$ is harmonic and the whole problem is one of matching harmonic functions across surfaces.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.2*

^rem-c6-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.1.1: Uniqueness with Conductors and Linear Dielectrics
> For the problem of [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]], two solutions with the same $\rho_f$, $\sigma_f$ and boundary data have the same field $\vec E = -\nabla V$ throughout $\mathcal V$. If $V$ itself is given anywhere on the boundary (a Dirichlet part, a conductor at given potential, or $V \to 0$ at infinity), the potentials agree; otherwise they differ by a constant.
>
> In particular, the potential outside a set of conductors is unique if for **each** conductor either its potential or its total charge is given.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.3, eqs. (7.6)–(7.8); §7.3.1, eqs. (7.9)–(7.10) (the interface terms and the dielectric weights are added here)*

^thm-c6-1-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The difference solves the homogeneous problem.** Let $V_1$, $V_2$ be two solutions and $\Phi = V_1 - V_2$. Subtracting the equations of [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]] term by term, the sources cancel:
>
> $$
> \nabla^2\Phi = 0 \ \text{ in each dielectric}; \qquad \Phi_1 = \Phi_2, \quad \varepsilon_{r1}\frac{\partial\Phi_1}{\partial n_1} + \varepsilon_{r2}\frac{\partial\Phi_2}{\partial n_2} = 0 \ \text{ on } \Sigma .
> $$
>
> On a Dirichlet part, $\Phi = 0$; on a Neumann part, $\partial\Phi/\partial n = 0$; on a conductor $S_i$, $\Phi$ is a constant $c_i = V_{1,i} - V_{2,i}$, with $c_i = 0$ if the potential is given, and $\oint_{S_i}\varepsilon\,\partial\Phi/\partial n\,dS = Q_i - Q_i = 0$ if the charge is given.
>
> **2. Green's first identity in each dielectric.** Let $\mathcal V_a$ be the part of $\mathcal V$ with constant $\varepsilon_a$. Product rule: $\nabla\cdot(\Phi\nabla\Phi) = \lvert\nabla\Phi\rvert^2 + \Phi\nabla^2\Phi = \lvert\nabla\Phi\rvert^2$. Multiply by $\varepsilon_a$, integrate over $\mathcal V_a$ and apply the divergence theorem (Green's first identity, Zangwill eq. (7.6) with $f = g = \Phi$; [[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-2|452 Thm. §17.2]]):
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V_a}\varepsilon_a\lvert\nabla\Phi\rvert^2\,d^3r = \oint_{\partial\mathcal V_a}\varepsilon_a\,\Phi\,\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial n_a}\,dS ,
> $$
>
> where $\hat n_a$ is the outward normal of $\mathcal V_a$. Each $\partial\mathcal V_a$ consists of pieces of interfaces and pieces of the outer boundary $\partial\mathcal V$.
>
> **3. Sum over the dielectrics; the interfaces cancel.** Add the identities of Step 2 over all $a$. A piece of interface between $\mathcal V_a$ and $\mathcal V_b$ appears twice, with the integrand
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_a\Phi\frac{\partial\Phi_a}{\partial n_a} + \varepsilon_b\Phi\frac{\partial\Phi_b}{\partial n_b} = \varepsilon_0\,\Phi\Bigl(\varepsilon_{ra}\frac{\partial\Phi_a}{\partial n_a} + \varepsilon_{rb}\frac{\partial\Phi_b}{\partial n_b}\Bigr) = 0 ;
> $$
>
> $\Phi$ could be factored out because it is continuous across $\Sigma$, and the bracket is the homogeneous flux condition of Step 1. What remains is (Zangwill eq. (7.7), now with weights)
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V}\varepsilon\,\lvert\nabla\Phi\rvert^2\,d^3r = \sum_i\oint_{S_i}\varepsilon\,\Phi\,\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial n}\,dS .
> $$
>
> **4. Every boundary term vanishes.** Dirichlet part: $\Phi = 0$. Neumann part: $\partial\Phi/\partial n = 0$. Conductor: $\Phi = c_i$ is constant on $S_i$ and comes out, $c_i\oint_{S_i}\varepsilon\,\partial\Phi/\partial n\,dS$, which is $0$ either because $c_i = 0$ (potential given) or because the flux integral is $0$ (charge given; Zangwill eq. (7.10), where it reads $(\varphi_1 - \varphi_2)(Q_1 - Q_2)/\epsilon_0$). Outer surface at infinity: take $S_0$ to be a sphere of radius $r$; there $\lvert\Phi\,\partial\Phi/\partial n\rvert = O(r^{-1})\cdot O(r^{-2})$ and the area is $4\pi r^2$, so the term is $O(1/r) \to 0$.
>
> **5. Conclusion.** $\int_{\mathcal V}\varepsilon\lvert\nabla\Phi\rvert^2\,d^3r = 0$ with $\varepsilon > 0$ and an integrand that is continuous and non-negative in each $\mathcal V_a$, so $\nabla\Phi = 0$ in each $\mathcal V_a$: $\Phi$ is constant on each dielectric. It is continuous across the interfaces and $\mathcal V$ is connected, so it is one constant $c$, and $\vec E_1 = \vec E_2$. If $\Phi = 0$ somewhere on the boundary, $c = 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The proof is an energy argument: $\frac12\int\varepsilon\lvert\nabla\Phi\rvert^2$ is the field energy of the difference ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]), and every boundary and interface condition is exactly what kills one surface term.
> - ⚑ The dielectric weights $\varepsilon_a$ are essential. Without them the interface terms would be $\Phi(\partial\Phi_a/\partial n_a + \partial\Phi_b/\partial n_b)$, which does not vanish: the matching condition fixes the jump of $\varepsilon\,\partial V/\partial n$, not of $\partial V/\partial n$.
> - ⚑ Prescribing the charge of a conductor is weaker than prescribing $\partial V/\partial n$ on it (one number instead of a function), yet enough, because the conductor's potential is constant and only the total flux enters.
> - The proof needs $\int\varepsilon\lvert\nabla V\rvert^2 < \infty$ near edges and gaps; see [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^cau-c6-1-1|Caution: Too much data, too little data, infinite energy]].

> [!caution] Caution: Too much data, too little data, infinite energy
> - *Too much.* $V$ and $\partial V/\partial n$ cannot both be prescribed on a closed boundary: by [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]] the Dirichlet data alone fix the solution, hence its normal derivative, so independent Neumann data are generically inconsistent. Zangwill says that then "no solution exists"; precisely, a solution exists only for compatible data, and even then it depends unstably on them (Hadamard's example: $V = n^{-2}\sinh(ny)\sin(nx)$ has data $V = 0$, $\partial_yV = n^{-1}\sin nx$ on $y = 0$ that tend to zero, while $V$ at $y = 1$ grows like $e^n/n^2$).
> - *Too little.* Pure Neumann data fix $V$ up to a constant, and they are not arbitrary: by Gauss's law $\oint\varepsilon\,\partial V/\partial n\,dS$ over the whole boundary must equal minus the enclosed free charge.
> - *Infinite energy at ideal gaps.* Many textbook problems join conductors at different potentials through gaps of zero width (the slot of [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^ex-c6-2-1|Example §C6.2.1]], the segmented cylinder of [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]]). Near such a gap $V \approx \Delta V\,\varphi/\pi$ in polar coordinates about the gap, so $\lvert\vec E\rvert = \Delta V/\pi\rho$ and the energy per unit length $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int\lvert\vec E\rvert^2\rho\,d\rho\,d\varphi$ diverges logarithmically; the energy proof does not apply as it stands. Uniqueness still holds among **bounded** solutions. In two dimensions: if $\Phi$ is bounded and harmonic in a bounded region $D$, continuous up to the boundary except at finitely many points $p_k$, and $0$ elsewhere on the boundary, put $\Phi_\epsilon = \Phi + \epsilon\sum_k\ln(\lvert w - p_k\rvert/d)$ with $d$ the diameter of $D$. Each logarithm is $\le 0$ on $D$ and tends to $-\infty$ at $p_k$, so $\Phi_\epsilon \le 0$ near each $p_k$ and on the rest of the boundary, and by the maximum principle ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]], applied on $D$ minus small discs about the $p_k$) $\Phi_\epsilon \le 0$ in $D$. Letting $\epsilon \to 0$ gives $\Phi \le 0$; the same for $-\Phi$ gives $\Phi = 0$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.3, p. 200 (the "mutually exclusive" choices); Hadamard's example and the logarithmic barrier are standard and added here*

^cau-c6-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Potentials or charges: the capacitance equations
> The two conductor data are interchangeable through a linear map. Solve once with all conductor potentials $V_j$ as unknown constants; the charges $Q_i = \oint_{S_i}\varepsilon\,\partial V/\partial n\,dS$ (Zangwill eq. (7.11)) are then linear in the $V_j$, $Q_i = \sum_jC_{ij}V_j$ (eq. (7.12); [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]]). Given charges, these linear equations determine the potentials, and [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]] guarantees that they have exactly one solution, i.e. that the matrix $C_{ij}$ is invertible (its positive definiteness, [[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]], says the same). In practice Neumann data rarely arise with conductors and dielectrics alone; they belong to waveguides and to steady currents in ohmic media (Zangwill §7.3.1), and all the boundary-value problems of this chapter are Dirichlet problems.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.3.1, eqs. (7.11)–(7.12)*

^rem-c6-1-2

> [!example] Example §C6.1.1: A Dipole in the Cavity of a Neutral Conductor
> An uncharged, isolated conductor of any shape contains a cavity of any shape, with a point dipole $\vec p$ inside. Outside the conductor $\vec E = \vec 0$. If the dipole is replaced by a point charge $q$, the outside field is not zero, but it depends only on $q$ and not on where $q$ sits in the cavity.
>
> *Why.* First let the conductor fill all space except the cavity. The dipole then induces a surface charge $\sigma^{\ast}$ on the cavity wall $S$ such that $\vec p$ and $\sigma^{\ast}$ together give $\vec E = \vec 0$ everywhere outside the cavity (that is what "conductor" means). Gauss's law over a surface in the metal that encloses the cavity gives $\oint_S\sigma^{\ast}\,dS + (\text{charge of }\vec p) = 0$, and a point dipole, the distribution $-\vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)$ ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^mod-c3-1-2|Model §C3.1.2]]), has total charge $\int(-\vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3)\,d^3r = 0$; so $\sigma^{\ast}$ integrates to zero. Now return to the finite conductor and put the same $\sigma^{\ast}$ on the cavity wall, no charge on the outer surface. Every condition is met: the field vanishes in the metal and outside, the metal is an equipotential, and the conductor's total charge is $0$, as required. The total charge is the datum of choice (B) in [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]], so by [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]] (applied to the region outside the conductor, with $V \to 0$ at infinity) $\vec E = \vec 0$ outside is *the* solution.
>
> For a point charge $q$ the induced cavity charge integrates to $-q$, so the outer surface must carry $+q$ to keep the conductor neutral. The outside problem then has the data "conductor charge $q$", which do not mention the position of $q$: the outside field is the same wherever $q$ sits (Zangwill's remark after eq. (7.10); [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^ex-b3-1-1|Example §B3.1.1]] proved the same for the vacuum case).
>
> *Source: Zangwill Example 7.1; §7.3.1, p. 200*

## Mean values, extrema and Liouville's theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.1.2: Mean-Value Theorem with Sources
> Let $V$ be twice continuously differentiable on a closed ball $\lvert\vec r - \vec r_0\rvert \le R$ and $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$ there. Then
>
> $$
> V(\vec r_0) = \frac{1}{4\pi R^2}\oint_{\lvert\vec r - \vec r_0\rvert = R}V\,dS + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\lvert\vec r - \vec r_0\rvert < R}\rho(\vec r)\Bigl(\frac{1}{\lvert\vec r - \vec r_0\rvert} - \frac1R\Bigr)d^3r .
> $$
>
> 1. *Harmonic case* ($\rho = 0$): the potential at the centre is the average over the sphere, and also over the solid ball ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]).
> 2. *First moment* (harmonic case, $\vec r_0 = \vec 0$): $\oint zV\,dS = \frac{4\pi}{3}R^4\,\partial V/\partial z\rvert_{\vec 0}$.
> 3. *Sign*: if $\rho \ge 0$ in the ball, the potential at the centre is at least its average over the sphere.
>
> The formula stays true when $\rho$ contains point, line or surface charges, as long as none sits at $\vec r_0$ or on the sphere.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 3.10(a) (Green's second identity), Example 3.5; Problem 7.1 (parts 1–2); the source term and part 3 are added here*

^thm-c6-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Choose the comparison function.** Put $\vec r_0 = \vec 0$ and $G(\vec r) = \frac1r - \frac1R$ for $0 < r \le R$. It is harmonic for $r > 0$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]), it vanishes on the sphere $r = R$, and $\partial G/\partial r = -1/r^2$.
>
> **2. Green's second identity on a shell.** On the shell $\epsilon < r < R$ both $V$ and $G$ are smooth, and ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-3|452 Thm. §17.3]])
>
> $$
> \int_{\epsilon < r < R}\bigl(V\nabla^2G - G\nabla^2V\bigr)\,d^3r = \oint_{r = R}\Bigl(V\frac{\partial G}{\partial n} - G\frac{\partial V}{\partial n}\Bigr)dS + \oint_{r = \epsilon}\Bigl(V\frac{\partial G}{\partial n} - G\frac{\partial V}{\partial n}\Bigr)dS .
> $$
>
> **3. The volume term.** $\nabla^2G = 0$ in the shell and $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$, so the left side is $\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_{\epsilon < r < R}\rho\,G\,d^3r$.
>
> **4. The outer sphere.** The outward normal is $\hat r$: $\partial G/\partial n = -1/R^2$ and $G = 0$. The term is $-\frac{1}{R^2}\oint_{r = R}V\,dS = -4\pi\langle V\rangle_R$, where $\langle V\rangle_R$ is the average over the sphere.
>
> **5. The inner sphere.** The outward normal of the shell is $-\hat r$: $\partial G/\partial n = +1/\epsilon^2$, so $\oint_{r = \epsilon}V\,\partial G/\partial n\,dS = 4\pi\langle V\rangle_\epsilon \to 4\pi V(\vec 0)$ as $\epsilon \to 0$, by continuity of $V$. The other piece is bounded by $\lvert G\rvert\cdot\max\lvert\nabla V\rvert\cdot4\pi\epsilon^2 \le (1/\epsilon + 1/R)\cdot C\cdot4\pi\epsilon^2 \to 0$.
>
> **6. Assemble and let $\epsilon \to 0$.** $G\rho$ is integrable ($1/r$ is integrable in three dimensions), so the shell integral tends to the ball integral:
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_{r < R}\rho\,G\,d^3r = -4\pi\langle V\rangle_R + 4\pi V(\vec 0) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V(\vec 0) = \langle V\rangle_R + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{r < R}\rho\Bigl(\frac1r - \frac1R\Bigr)d^3r .
> $$
>
> **7. The same computation as a distributional identity.** Extend $G$ by $0$ for $r > R$. This $G_R$ is continuous, and its radial derivative jumps by $+1/R^2$ at $r = R$; together with $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\delta^3$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]) this gives, as generalized functions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]),
>
> $$
> \nabla^2G_R = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec r) + \frac{1}{R^2}\,\delta(r - R) .
> $$
>
> "Integrating by parts twice", $\int V\nabla^2G_R = \int G_R\nabla^2V$, is then Steps 2–6 in one line: $-4\pi V(\vec 0) + 4\pi\langle V\rangle_R = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int G_R\rho$. In this form $\rho$ may be any distribution of charge whose support avoids $\vec 0$ and the sphere, since $G_R$ is smooth there; in particular a point charge $q$ at distance $z$ from the centre contributes $\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\bigl(\frac1z - \frac1R\bigr)$ for $z < R$ and nothing for $z > R$ ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^rem-b3-1-2|§B3.1, Remark: The same from Coulomb's law]]).
>
> **8. Part 1, solid average.** For $\rho = 0$, $\langle V\rangle_s = V(\vec 0)$ for every $s \le R$. Integrate $4\pi s^2\langle V\rangle_s\,ds$ from $0$ to $R$: $\int_{r < R}V\,d^3r = \frac{4\pi R^3}{3}V(\vec 0)$.
>
> **9. Part 2, first moment.** $\partial V/\partial z$ is harmonic too (derivatives commute with $\nabla^2$). By Step 8 applied to it, and the divergence theorem applied to $V\hat z$,
>
> $$
> \frac{4\pi R^3}{3}\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}(\vec 0) = \int_{r < R}\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\,d^3r = \oint_{r = R}V\,\hat z\cdot\hat r\,dS = \frac1R\oint_{r = R}zV\,dS ,
> $$
>
> since $\hat z\cdot\hat r = \cos\theta = z/R$ on the sphere. Multiply by $R$.
>
> **10. Part 3.** $\frac1r - \frac1R \ge 0$ for $r \le R$, so $\rho \ge 0$ makes the integral non-negative.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The correction to the mean-value property is the potential that the enclosed charge would produce at the centre if the sphere were a grounded conductor: $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}(\frac1r - \frac1R)$ is the Green function of the grounded sphere with one argument at its centre (images: [[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]; Green functions: [[§C7.4 Constructing Green Functions#^thm-c7-4-3|Theorem §C7.4.3]]).
> - ⚑ A positive charge raises the centre above the average (part 3); this one inequality drives the comparison principle ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]]).
> - Zangwill's Example 3.5 obtains part 1 from Green's reciprocity instead, with the uniformly charged shell as the "reciprocal system" ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^ex-c2-3-1|Example §C2.3.1]]); Problem 7.1 asks for parts 1–2 from the orthogonality of spherical harmonics, which [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]] makes available: only $Y_{00}$ survives $\oint V\,dS$, and only $Y_{10} \propto \cos\theta$ survives $\oint zV\,dS$.

*Uses:* [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], [[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-3|452 Thm. §17.3]], [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]], [[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.1.3: Maximum and Comparison Principle
> Let $\mathcal V$ be a bounded connected region and $V$ continuous on its closure, twice continuously differentiable inside, with $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$.
> 1. *Minimum principle.* If $\rho \ge 0$ in $\mathcal V$, then $V \ge \min_{\partial\mathcal V}V$ throughout $\mathcal V$; if $V$ attains its minimum at an interior point, $V$ is constant (and $\rho = 0$). For $\rho \le 0$ the same holds for the maximum; for $\rho = 0$ both hold ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]).
> 2. *Comparison.* If $V_1$, $V_2$ have charge densities $\rho_1 \ge \rho_2$ in $\mathcal V$ and $V_1 \ge V_2$ on $\partial\mathcal V$, then $V_1 \ge V_2$ in $\mathcal V$.
> 3. *Unbounded regions.* Both statements hold for $\mathcal V$ extending to infinity if $V \to 0$ (respectively $V_1 - V_2 \to 0$) at infinity, with the value $0$ at infinity counted among the boundary values (so in part 1, $V \ge \min(0, \min_{\partial\mathcal V}V)$).
>
> In words: in a grounded enclosure, positive charge makes the potential positive everywhere inside, and more charge, or higher boundary potentials, can only raise it.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §3.3.3 (Earnshaw's theorem, the harmonic case) and Problem 3.10(b); the inequality form and the comparison principle are added here*

^thm-c6-1-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The super-mean-value inequality.** For every closed ball $B$ in $\mathcal V$ about a point $\vec r_0$, [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-2|Theorem §C6.1.2]] (part 3) gives $V(\vec r_0) \ge \langle V\rangle_{\partial B}$ when $\rho \ge 0$, with equality only if $\int_B\rho\,(\frac{1}{\lvert\vec r - \vec r_0\rvert} - \frac1R)\,d^3r = 0$.
>
> **2. An interior minimum spreads.** $V$ is continuous on the closed bounded set $\overline{\mathcal V}$, so it attains a minimum $m$ there. Suppose $V(\vec r_0) = m$ at an interior point. For every sphere about $\vec r_0$ inside $\mathcal V$: $m = V(\vec r_0) \ge \langle V\rangle \ge m$, because $V \ge m$ on the sphere. So $\langle V\rangle = m$, and a continuous function $\ge m$ with average $m$ equals $m$ on the whole sphere. Varying the radius, $V = m$ on a ball about $\vec r_0$: the set $\{V = m\}\cap\mathcal V$ is open. It is closed in $\mathcal V$ by continuity and not empty, and $\mathcal V$ is connected, so $V \equiv m$ in $\mathcal V$; then $\rho = -\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V = 0$. If $V$ is not constant, its minimum is therefore attained only on $\partial\mathcal V$, which is part 1. For $\rho \le 0$ apply this to $-V$.
>
> **3. Comparison.** $W = V_1 - V_2$ has $\nabla^2W = -(\rho_1 - \rho_2)/\varepsilon_0$ with $\rho_1 - \rho_2 \ge 0$, and $W \ge 0$ on $\partial\mathcal V$. By part 1, $W \ge \min_{\partial\mathcal V}W \ge 0$.
>
> **4. Unbounded regions.** Given $\eta > 0$, choose $r_\eta$ so large that $W > -\eta$ for $r \ge r_\eta$ (possible since $W \to 0$). Apply part 1 to $\mathcal V\cap\{r < r_\eta\}$, whose boundary consists of parts of $\partial\mathcal V$ (where $W \ge 0$) and of the sphere $r = r_\eta$ (where $W > -\eta$): $W > -\eta$ inside. Let $\eta \to 0$. The same argument with $V$ in place of $W$, and $\min(0, \min_{\partial\mathcal V}V) - \eta$ in place of $-\eta$, gives part 1 for unbounded regions.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The extremum principle is the integrated form of a sign: positive charge makes the centre of every small sphere lie above the average of its surface. Zangwill's proof of Earnshaw's theorem (§3.3.3; [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]) uses the flux form instead: at a local minimum $\hat n\cdot\nabla V > 0$ on a small surface around it, contradicting $\oint\nabla V\cdot d\vec S = 0$. That argument tacitly assumes a strict inequality on the whole surface; the mean-value route needs no such assumption and covers non-strict extrema (his Problem 3.10(b) asks for it).
> - ⚑ Consequence for conductors: in the region outside a set of conductors (no other charge, $V \to 0$ at infinity), $V$ takes its extreme values on the conductors. The conductor at the highest potential, if that is positive, has $V$ decreasing away from it, so $\partial V/\partial n \le 0$ just outside and its surface charge $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\,\partial V/\partial n$ (normal out of the conductor) is $\ge 0$ everywhere. This fixes the signs of the capacitance coefficients, Maxwell's inequalities $C_{kk} > 0$, $C_{jk} \le 0$ ([[§C4.3 The Capacitance Matrix#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]]): raise conductor $k$ to potential $1$ and ground the others; by part 1 the potential lies between $0$ and $1$ everywhere, so conductor $k$ is the maximum and carries positive charge, and every other conductor is a minimum and carries charge $\le 0$.

> [!remark] Remark: Earnshaw's theorem and zero curvature
> In charge-free space $V$ has no local minimum or maximum ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]]), so no charge can rest in stable equilibrium under electrostatic forces alone: Earnshaw's theorem, whose level-C home is [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]] ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^rem-b3-1-3|§B3.1, ★ Remark: Earnshaw's theorem]]). Zangwill reads the same fact off Laplace's equation geometrically: $\partial_x^2V + \partial_y^2V + \partial_z^2V = 0$ says that the three curvatures of $V$ along the Cartesian axes cannot all have the same sign, so every critical point is a saddle. Separation of variables shows the same thing in its separation constants: $\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2 = 0$ forces oscillation in at least one direction and growth or decay in another ([[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates|§C6.2]]). A harmonic function therefore cannot stay bounded in every direction without being constant; that is Liouville's theorem below.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.5.2, footnote 8; §3.3.3*

^rem-c6-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.1.4: Liouville's Theorem
> A potential that is harmonic in all of space and bounded, $\lvert V\rvert \le M$, is constant. In particular, a potential with no charge anywhere that vanishes at infinity is zero, and a potential that is harmonic everywhere and grows at most like $r$, $\lvert V\rvert \le C(1 + r)$, is $V = V_0 - \vec E_0\cdot\vec r$, a uniform field.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.5.2, p. 209 ("the only possible solution to $\nabla^2\varphi = 0$ in completely empty space is $\varphi =$ const"; the boundedness hypothesis and the proof are added here)*

^thm-c6-1-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Derivatives are harmonic.** $\partial_iV$ is harmonic, so by [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-2|Theorem §C6.1.2]] (part 1, solid average) on the ball $B_R(\vec x)$ of radius $R$ about any point $\vec x$:
>
> $$
> \partial_iV(\vec x) = \frac{3}{4\pi R^3}\int_{B_R(\vec x)}\partial_iV\,d^3r = \frac{3}{4\pi R^3}\oint_{\partial B_R(\vec x)}V\,n_i\,dS ,
> $$
>
> the second step being the divergence theorem applied to $V\hat e_i$ ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]).
>
> **2. Estimate.** $\lvert V\,n_i\rvert \le M$ and the area is $4\pi R^2$, so $\lvert\partial_iV(\vec x)\rvert \le \frac{3}{4\pi R^3}\cdot M\cdot4\pi R^2 = \frac{3M}{R}$.
>
> **3. Let $R \to \infty$.** $V$ is harmonic everywhere, so $R$ is arbitrary: $\partial_iV(\vec x) = 0$ for every $\vec x$ and $i$, and $V$ is constant. If $V \to 0$ at infinity the constant is $0$.
>
> **4. Linear growth.** If $\lvert V\rvert \le C(1 + r)$, then on $\partial B_R(\vec x)$ we have $\lvert V\rvert \le C(1 + \lvert\vec x\rvert + R)$, and Steps 1–2 give $\lvert\partial_iV(\vec x)\rvert \le 3C(1 + \lvert\vec x\rvert + R)/R$. Letting $R \to \infty$, $\lvert\partial_iV(\vec x)\rvert \le 3C$ for every $\vec x$. So each $\partial_iV$ is a bounded harmonic function, hence a constant $-E_{0,i}$ by Step 3, and $V = V_0 - \vec E_0\cdot\vec r$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Zangwill's statement needs the hypothesis: $-E_0z$ is harmonic in all space and not constant. Read physically, a uniform field is the field of charges "at infinity" (the plates of an ideal capacitor), which the growth at infinity records. With a bounded potential no such hidden source is possible.
> - ⚑ The same estimate on higher derivatives shows that a harmonic function with $\lvert V\rvert \le C(1 + r)^N$ is a polynomial of degree at most $N$: the harmonic polynomials $r^\ell Y_{\ell m}$ of [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]] are all there is. This is why the "applied field" at infinity in a boundary-value problem is always one of a few low-order harmonic polynomials.

> [!remark] Remark: Uniqueness is not existence
> [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]] says there is at most one solution. That one exists is the hard half of potential theory. Physically it is clear (charges do settle), and the methods of this chapter construct solutions for special shapes; the Poisson integral does it for any continuous data on a sphere ([[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]). The classical general argument, Dirichlet's principle, minimizes the field energy over all functions with the given boundary values ([[§C6.4★ The Complex Potential and the Variational Principle#^thm-c6-4-3|Theorem §C6.4.3]]); that the minimum is attained needed a further half-century of analysis after Weierstrass's objection. Whatever the method, once a candidate meets the equation and the data, uniqueness certifies it: "if a valid solution is obtained by any means, including guesswork, it is *the* solution" (Zangwill §7.3).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.2–§7.3*
