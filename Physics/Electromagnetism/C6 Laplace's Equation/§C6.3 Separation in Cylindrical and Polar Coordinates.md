*Sources: Zangwill, Modern Electrodynamics, §7.8–§7.9; Application 7.4; Example 7.4; Problems 7.16, 7.18 · PHY 505 (Wells, Fall 2025), HW 11, Problem 1.*

Round boundaries with a preferred axis call for cylindrical coordinates $(\rho, \varphi, z)$. Separation then produces Bessel's equation in the radial variable, and the "zero-curvature" constraint of [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^rem-c6-1-3|§C6.1]] pairs oscillation along $\rho$ with exponentials along $z$, or the reverse. Level B met only the $z$-independent case, the wire in a uniform field ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]], part (b)), and left Bessel functions to the mathematics notes. This section collects what boundary-value problems need of Bessel functions, with orthogonality and the Fourier–Bessel expansion that it allows ([[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]), solves the two-tube electron lens in two ways that agree by uniqueness, and then turns to plane polar coordinates: the general two-dimensional solution, the field at conducting corners and edges, and the course's segmented cylinder. Separation in general and the completeness of eigenfunctions are [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-1|Theorem §C6.2.1]].

*Notation.* Cylindrical radius $\rho$, as Zangwill (EM B writes $s$); $V$ for the potential ([[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: The potential is V]]). Where $\rho$ could be confused with a charge density, densities are written $\sigma$ or $\lambda$.

## Cylindrical coordinates and Bessel functions

> [!definition] Definition §C6.3.1: Bessel Functions of the Four Kinds
> For $x > 0$ and real order $\alpha \ge 0$, **Bessel's equation** is $x^2R'' + xR' + (x^2 - \alpha^2)R = 0$ and the **modified Bessel equation** $x^2R'' + xR' - (x^2 + \alpha^2)R = 0$. Their standard solutions:
> - **Bessel function of the first kind** $J_\alpha(x) = \sum_{k \ge 0}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!\,\Gamma(k + \alpha + 1)}\bigl(\frac x2\bigr)^{2k + \alpha}$, the solution of Bessel's equation regular at $0$ ([[§45★ Bessel's Equation#^def-45-2|341 Def. §45.2]] for integer order).
> - **Bessel function of the second kind** (Neumann function) $N_\alpha$, the standard second solution, singular at $0$ ([[§45★ Bessel's Equation#^def-45-3|341 Def. §45.3]], where it is written $Y_\alpha$).
> - **Modified Bessel functions** $I_\alpha(x) = i^{-\alpha}J_\alpha(ix) = \sum_k\frac{1}{k!\,\Gamma(k + \alpha + 1)}\bigl(\frac x2\bigr)^{2k + \alpha}$ ([[§45★ Bessel's Equation#^def-45-new1|341 Def. §45.4]]) and $K_\alpha(x) = \frac\pi2i^{\alpha + 1}\bigl[J_\alpha(ix) + iN_\alpha(ix)\bigr]$ (Zangwill eq. (7.88)), which equals the integral $\int_0^\infty e^{-x\cosh u}\cosh\alpha u\,du$ of [[§CA.5 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^def-ca-5-1|QFT Def. §CA.5.1]].
>
> $J_\alpha$, $N_\alpha$ oscillate; $I_\alpha$ grows and $K_\alpha$ decays exponentially.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.8.1, eqs. (7.87)–(7.89); Appendix C (referred to)*

^def-c6-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: N or Y, and which K
> Zangwill and Jackson write $N_\alpha$ for the second solution; the PDE notes, DLMF and most tables write $Y_\alpha$ ([[§45★ Bessel's Equation#^def-45-3|341 Def. §45.3]]): the same function. $K_\alpha$ is normalized as in DLMF (the "Macdonald function"), so that $K_\alpha(x) \approx \sqrt{\pi/2x}\,e^{-x}$; older texts sometimes use a $K$ differing by a factor $\pi/2$. Check the large-argument form before combining formulas.

^cau-c6-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.3.1: Separated Solutions in Cylindrical Coordinates
> In cylindrical coordinates (Zangwill eq. (7.81))
>
> $$
> \nabla^2V = \frac1\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\Bigl(\rho\frac{\partial V}{\partial\rho}\Bigr) + \frac{1}{\rho^2}\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial\varphi^2} + \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial z^2} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> and $V = R(\rho)G(\varphi)Z(z)$ separates it into (eqs. (7.82)–(7.84))
>
> $$
> \rho\frac{d}{d\rho}\Bigl(\rho\frac{dR}{d\rho}\Bigr) + (k^2\rho^2 - \alpha^2)R = 0, \qquad G'' + \alpha^2G = 0, \qquad Z'' - k^2Z = 0 .
> $$
>
> 1. If the full angle $0 \le \varphi \le 2\pi$ belongs to the charge-free region, $\alpha = m = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ and $G = \cos m\varphi, \sin m\varphi$ (or $e^{\pm im\varphi}$); for $m = 0$ only the constant.
> 2. The radial solutions are (eq. (7.87); [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^def-c6-3-1|Def. §C6.3.1]] for the functions)
>
>    $$
>    R = \begin{cases}A_0 + B_0\ln\rho, & k = 0,\ \alpha = 0,\\ A\rho^\alpha + B\rho^{-\alpha}, & k = 0,\ \alpha \ne 0,\\ AJ_\alpha(k\rho) + BN_\alpha(k\rho), & k^2 > 0\ (Z = e^{\pm kz}),\\ AI_\alpha(\kappa\rho) + BK_\alpha(\kappa\rho), & k^2 = -\kappa^2 < 0\ (Z = e^{\pm i\kappa z}).\end{cases}
>    $$
>
> 3. Regularity on the axis $\rho = 0$ removes $\ln\rho$, $\rho^{-\alpha}$, $N_\alpha$ and $K_\alpha$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.8, eqs. (7.81)–(7.87); §7.8.1, p. 216*

^thm-c6-3-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Separate.** Insert $RGZ$, divide by $RGZ$ and multiply by $\rho^2$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\rho}{R}\frac{d}{d\rho}\Bigl(\rho\frac{dR}{d\rho}\Bigr) + \frac{G''}{G} + \rho^2\frac{Z''}{Z} = 0 .
> $$
>
> The middle term depends only on $\varphi$, the rest only on $(\rho, z)$, so $G''/G = -\alpha^2$. Divide what is left by $\rho^2$: $\frac{1}{\rho R}(\rho R')' - \frac{\alpha^2}{\rho^2} + \frac{Z''}{Z} = 0$; now $Z''/Z$ depends only on $z$ and the rest only on $\rho$, so $Z''/Z = k^2$, and multiplying back by $\rho^2R$ gives the radial equation.
>
> **2. The angle.** If the whole circle is charge-free, $V$ and $\partial_\varphi V$ are continuous across $\varphi = 0 \equiv 2\pi$ (Zangwill uses the matching conditions (7.3)–(7.4) there), so $G(0) = G(2\pi)$ and $G'(0) = G'(2\pi)$. For $\alpha = 0$, $G = x_0 + y_0\varphi$ needs $y_0 = 0$; for $\alpha \ne 0$, $G = x\cos\alpha\varphi + y\sin\alpha\varphi$ needs $\cos2\pi\alpha = 1$ and $\sin2\pi\alpha = 0$ (for nonzero $x$ or $y$), i.e. $\alpha$ an integer; $\alpha$ and $-\alpha$ give the same functions.
>
> **3. The radial equation.** For $k = 0$ it is $\rho(\rho R')' = \alpha^2R$, an equidimensional equation: $R = \rho^n$ gives $n^2 = \alpha^2$, and for $\alpha = 0$ the second solution is $\ln\rho$ (since $(\rho\cdot\rho^{-1})' = 0$). For $k^2 > 0$ put $x = k\rho$: $x^2R'' + xR' + (x^2 - \alpha^2)R = 0$, Bessel's equation, with the independent solutions $J_\alpha$, $N_\alpha$ ([[§45★ Bessel's Equation#^thm-45-5|341 Thm. §45.5]]). For $k = i\kappa$ put $x = \kappa\rho$: $x^2R'' + xR' - (x^2 + \alpha^2)R = 0$, the modified Bessel equation, solved by $I_\alpha$ and $K_\alpha$ ([[§45★ Bessel's Equation#^def-45-4|341 Def. §45.4]]; [[§CA.5 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^def-ca-5-1|QFT Def. §CA.5.1]]).
>
> **4. The axis.** $\ln\rho$ and $\rho^{-\alpha}$ diverge at $\rho = 0$; so do $N_\alpha$ ([[§45★ Bessel's Equation#^thm-45-4|341 Thm. §45.4]]) and $K_\alpha$ ([[§CA.5 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^thm-ca-5-3|QFT Theorem §CA.5.3]], 3).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ The sign of $k^2$ is not decided by the equation but by the boundary conditions: homogeneous conditions on the cylinder wall need the oscillating $J_\alpha$ radially and exponentials in $z$; homogeneous conditions on two cross-sections need oscillation in $z$ and the monotonic $I_\alpha$, $K_\alpha$ radially. Either way one direction oscillates and another grows or decays: the cylindrical face of $\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2 = 0$.
> - The $\ln\rho$ solution is the potential of a line charge on the axis ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]]); excluding it inside a region means there is no such charge.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.3.2: What Boundary-Value Problems Need of Bessel Functions
> Let $m = 0, 1, 2, \dots$
> 1. *Small argument.* $J_m(x) \approx \frac{(x/2)^m}{m!}$, $I_m(x) \approx \frac{(x/2)^m}{m!}$; $N_0(x) \approx \frac2\pi\bigl(\ln\frac x2 + \gamma_E\bigr)$, $K_0(x) \approx -\ln\frac x2 - \gamma_E$; for $m \ge 1$, $N_m(x) \approx -\frac{(m - 1)!}{\pi}\bigl(\frac2x\bigr)^m$ and $K_m(x) \approx \frac{(m - 1)!}{2}\bigl(\frac2x\bigr)^m$.
> 2. *Large argument* (eq. (7.89)). $J_\alpha(x) \approx \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\cos\bigl(x - \frac{\alpha\pi}2 - \frac\pi4\bigr)$, $N_\alpha(x) \approx \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\sin\bigl(x - \frac{\alpha\pi}2 - \frac\pi4\bigr)$, $I_\alpha(x) \approx \frac{e^x}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}$, $K_\alpha(x) \approx \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2x}}\,e^{-x}$.
> 3. *Zeros.* $J_m$ has infinitely many positive zeros $x_{m1} < x_{m2} < \dots$, all simple ($x_{01} = 2.405$, $x_{11} = 3.832$).
> 4. *Orthogonality and norm* (eq. (7.93)). With $k_{mn} = x_{mn}/R$,
>
>    $$
>    \int_0^R\rho\,J_m(k_{mn}\rho)\,J_m(k_{mn'}\rho)\,d\rho = \delta_{nn'}\,\frac{R^2}{2}\,J_{m+1}(x_{mn})^2 .
>    $$
>
> 5. *Fourier–Bessel completeness.* Every square-integrable $f$ on $[0, R]$ (weight $\rho$) is $f(\rho) = \sum_nc_nJ_m(k_{mn}\rho)$ in mean square, with $c_n = \frac{2}{R^2J_{m+1}(x_{mn})^2}\int_0^R\rho f(\rho)J_m(k_{mn}\rho)\,d\rho$; for piecewise smooth $f$ the series converges to the midpoint at every interior point.
> 6. *Bessel's integral.* $J_0(x) = \frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\cos(x\sin\theta)\,d\theta$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.8.1–§7.8.2, eqs. (7.89), (7.93) (parts 1–3 and 5 quoted from the mathematics notes and DLMF §10.7, §10.17, §10.21, §10.30, §10.40 where the vault has no proof; part 6 added here)*

^thm-c6-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Parts 1–3 (quoted).** The leading terms of the series of [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^def-c6-3-1|Def. §C6.3.1]] give $J_m$ and $I_m$ at small $x$. The logarithm in $N_0$ is [[§45★ Bessel's Equation#^thm-45-4|341 Thm. §45.4]] with the normalization of [[§45★ Bessel's Equation#^rem-45-3|341 §45, Remark: The Series for Y₀]]; the $K_\alpha$ forms at both ends are derived from the integral representation in [[§CA.5 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^thm-ca-5-3|QFT Theorem §CA.5.3]]; the $J$, $N$ asymptotics are the Hankel forms of [[§CA.5 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^thm-ca-5-4|QFT Theorem §CA.5.4]], 4 (for order 1; DLMF §10.17 in general), and the large-argument $I_\alpha$ is quoted (DLMF §10.40). The zeros are [[§45★ Bessel's Equation#^thm-45-6|341 Thm. §45.6]]; simplicity follows because a double zero would make $J_m$ and $J_m'$ vanish together, hence $J_m \equiv 0$ by uniqueness for the ODE.
>
> **2. Sturm–Liouville form.** Let $u(\rho) = J_m(a\rho)$, $v(\rho) = J_m(b\rho)$. Dividing Bessel's equation by $\rho$ gives
>
> $$
> (\rho u')' + \Bigl(a^2\rho - \frac{m^2}{\rho}\Bigr)u = 0, \qquad (\rho v')' + \Bigl(b^2\rho - \frac{m^2}{\rho}\Bigr)v = 0 .
> $$
>
> **3. Orthogonality.** Multiply the first by $v$, the second by $u$, subtract; the $m^2/\rho$ terms cancel and $v(\rho u')' - u(\rho v')' = \frac{d}{d\rho}[\rho(vu' - uv')]$, so
>
> $$
> (b^2 - a^2)\int_0^R\rho\,uv\,d\rho = \Bigl[\rho\bigl(vu' - uv'\bigr)\Bigr]_0^R .
> $$
>
> At $\rho = 0$ the bracket vanishes ($\rho \to 0$, $u$, $v$ and their derivatives finite); at $\rho = R$ it vanishes if $a = k_{mn}$, $b = k_{mn'}$, since then $u(R) = v(R) = 0$. For $n \ne n'$, $a^2 \ne b^2$, and the integral is zero ([[§23 Sturm–Liouville Problems#^thm-23-2|341 Thm. §23.2]] with weight $\rho$).
>
> **4. The norm.** Multiply the equation of $u$ by $2\rho u'$. Since $2\rho u'(\rho u')' = \frac{d}{d\rho}(\rho u')^2$ and $2(a^2\rho^2 - m^2)uu' = (a^2\rho^2 - m^2)\frac{d}{d\rho}u^2$,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\rho}(\rho u')^2 + (a^2\rho^2 - m^2)\frac{d}{d\rho}u^2 = 0 .
> $$
>
> Integrate from $0$ to $R$, the second term by parts: $\bigl[(\rho u')^2\bigr]_0^R + \bigl[(a^2\rho^2 - m^2)u^2\bigr]_0^R - 2a^2\int_0^R\rho u^2\,d\rho = 0$. At $\rho = 0$: $\rho u' = 0$, and $m^2u(0)^2 = 0$ (for $m \ge 1$, $u(0) = 0$; for $m = 0$, $m^2 = 0$). At $\rho = R$ with $a = k_{mn}$: $u(R) = 0$ and $Ru'(R) = Ra\,J_m'(x_{mn}) = x_{mn}J_m'(x_{mn})$. Hence
>
> $$
> \int_0^R\rho\,J_m(k_{mn}\rho)^2\,d\rho = \frac{x_{mn}^2J_m'(x_{mn})^2}{2a^2} = \frac{R^2}{2}J_m'(x_{mn})^2 = \frac{R^2}{2}J_{m+1}(x_{mn})^2 ,
> $$
>
> using the recurrence $J_m'(x) = \frac mxJ_m(x) - J_{m+1}(x)$ at a zero of $J_m$ (from the series; for $m = 0$ it is $J_0' = -J_1$, [[§45★ Bessel's Equation#^thm-45-7|341 Thm. §45.7]]).
>
> **5. Completeness (quoted).** [[§46★ Temperature in a Cylinder#^thm-46-3|341 Thm. §46.3]] for $m = 0$; for general $m$ it is the singular Sturm–Liouville case of [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-1|Theorem §C6.2.1]]. The coefficient formula is the projection with Steps 3–4.
>
> **6. Bessel's integral.** Expand $\cos(x\sin\theta) = \sum_k(-1)^k\frac{x^{2k}\sin^{2k}\theta}{(2k)!}$, uniformly convergent in $\theta$, and integrate term by term with $\int_0^\pi\sin^{2k}\theta\,d\theta = \pi\frac{(2k)!}{4^k(k!)^2}$ (the reduction formula $\int\sin^n = \frac{n-1}{n}\int\sin^{n-2}$ applied $k$ times):
>
> $$
> \frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\cos(x\sin\theta)\,d\theta = \sum_k\frac{(-1)^kx^{2k}}{(2k)!}\cdot\frac{(2k)!}{4^k(k!)^2} = \sum_k\frac{(-1)^k}{(k!)^2}\Bigl(\frac x2\Bigr)^{2k} = J_0(x) .
> $$
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ The weight $\rho$ in the orthogonality integral is the $\rho$ of the area element $\rho\,d\rho\,d\varphi$; with $e^{im\varphi}$ the functions $J_m(k_{mn}\rho)e^{im\varphi}$ are orthogonal on the disk in the ordinary area measure, with norm $\pi R^2J_{m+1}(x_{mn})^2$.
> - Bessel's integral is the bridge to plane waves: $J_0(k\rho)$ is the average of $e^{ik\rho\cos\varphi}$ over directions, which is how it enters the separated form of the image of a plane ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images#^thm-c7-1-3|Theorem §C7.1.3]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.3.3: A Cylinder with Potential Given on an End Face
> Let the region be $\rho < R$, $0 < z < L$, with the wall $\rho = R$ and the lid $z = L$ grounded and $V(\rho, \varphi, 0) = f(\rho, \varphi)$. Then (Zangwill eqs. (7.91)–(7.93))
>
> $$
> V = \sum_{m = -\infty}^\infty\sum_{n = 1}^\infty c_{mn}\,J_{\lvert m\rvert}\Bigl(\frac{x_{\lvert m\rvert n}\rho}{R}\Bigr)e^{im\varphi}\,\frac{\sinh\bigl(x_{\lvert m\rvert n}(L - z)/R\bigr)}{\sinh\bigl(x_{\lvert m\rvert n}L/R\bigr)}, \qquad c_{mn} = \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^Rf\,e^{-im\varphi}J_{\lvert m\rvert}\Bigl(\frac{x_{\lvert m\rvert n}\rho}{R}\Bigr)\rho\,d\rho\,d\varphi}{\pi R^2J_{\lvert m\rvert + 1}(x_{\lvert m\rvert n})^2} .
> $$
>
> For a semi-infinite cylinder ($L \to \infty$) the $z$ factor is $e^{-x_{\lvert m\rvert n}z/R}$: the potential dies off over the length $R/x_{01} = R/2.405$ of the slowest mode. If instead the wall carries the data and two cross-sections are grounded, the roles swap: $\sin(n\pi z/L)$ in $z$ and $I_m(n\pi\rho/L)$ in $\rho$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.8.2, eqs. (7.91)–(7.93) (the integration range "$0 \le \phi < R$" printed after (7.91) should read $0 \le \phi < 2\pi$)*

^thm-c6-3-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Homogeneous directions.** The angle is periodic, so $e^{im\varphi}$, $m \in \mathbb Z$ ([[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]], 1). Regularity on the axis leaves $J_{\lvert m\rvert}(k\rho)$ (part 3), and $V = 0$ on the wall requires $J_{\lvert m\rvert}(kR) = 0$, so $k = x_{\lvert m\rvert n}/R$ with $k^2 > 0$. Then $Z'' = k^2Z$ with $Z(L) = 0$ gives $Z \propto \sinh k(L - z)$, normalized by its value at $z = 0$.
>
> **2. The live face.** At $z = 0$: $f = \sum_{mn}c_{mn}J_{\lvert m\rvert}(x_{\lvert m\rvert n}\rho/R)e^{im\varphi}$, a Fourier series in $\varphi$ whose coefficients are Fourier–Bessel series in $\rho$; both families are complete ([[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-1|Theorem §C6.2.1]]; [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], 5). Multiply by $e^{-im'\varphi}J_{\lvert m'\rvert}(x_{\lvert m'\rvert n'}\rho/R)\rho$ and integrate over the disk. The $\varphi$ integral gives $2\pi\delta_{mm'}$ (Zangwill eq. (7.92)), and then the $\rho$ integral gives $\delta_{nn'}\frac{R^2}{2}J_{\lvert m\rvert + 1}^2$ (eq. (7.93)); the product $\pi R^2J_{\lvert m\rvert+1}^2$ is the denominator.
>
> **3. Convergence and uniqueness.** For $0 < z \le L$ the factors $\sinh(k(L - z))/\sinh(kL) \le e^{-kz}$ decay exponentially in $k$, and $x_{mn} \approx (n + m/2 - 1/4)\pi$ grows linearly, so the series and its derivatives converge uniformly for $z \ge z_0 > 0$ (M-test, [[§25 More on Uniform Convergence#^thm-25-3|451 Thm. §25.3]]), and $V$ is harmonic there. It meets all boundary conditions, so it is the solution ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The recipe of [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^def-c6-2-1|Def. §C6.2.1]] in its cylindrical form: homogeneous data on the wall make $\rho$ an eigenvalue direction, and the live end face is expanded in the eigenfunctions.
> - ⚑ Deep in a grounded tube any disturbance decays like $e^{-2.405z/R}$: the electrostatic analogue of a waveguide below cutoff ([[§B10.4★ Guided Waves|§B10.4★]]).

> [!example] Example §C6.3.1: The Two-Tube Electron Lens, Two Ways
> Two coaxial tubes of radius $R$ meet at $z = 0$ across a gap of negligible width; the left tube ($z < 0$) is at $V_L$, the right one at $V_R$ (Zangwill Application 7.4, Figure 7.10; Problem 7.18). Find $V(\rho, z)$ inside.
>
> *(a) Fourier integral with $I_0$ ($k^2 < 0$).* Axial symmetry gives $m = 0$, regularity on the axis $I_0$, and the data along all of $z$ call for a Fourier integral (eq. (7.94)): $V = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\kappa\,A(\kappa)I_0(\lvert\kappa\rvert\rho)e^{i\kappa z}$. At $\rho = R$ this must reproduce $V(R, z) = V_L\theta(-z) + V_R\theta(z)$, a function that does not decay, so its transform exists only as a tempered distribution. With the step-function transform $\int\theta(z)e^{-i\kappa z}dz = -i\,\mathcal P\frac1\kappa + \pi\delta(\kappa)$ and its mirror $\int\theta(-z)e^{-i\kappa z}dz = i\,\mathcal P\frac1\kappa + \pi\delta(\kappa)$ ([[§CA.3 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-3-10|QFT Theorem §CA.3.10]] with $\omega = -\kappa$),
>
> $$
> A(\kappa)I_0(\lvert\kappa\rvert R) = \pi(V_L + V_R)\,\delta(\kappa) + \frac{V_R - V_L}{i}\,\mathcal P\frac1\kappa .
> $$
>
> The delta term gives the constant $\frac12(V_L + V_R)$ (since $I_0(0) = 1$). The principal-value term has an even ratio $I_0(\kappa\rho)/I_0(\kappa R)$, so only $\sin\kappa z$ survives:
>
> $$
> V(\rho, z) = \frac{V_L + V_R}{2} + \frac{V_R - V_L}{\pi}\int_0^\infty\frac{d\kappa}{\kappa}\,\frac{I_0(\kappa\rho)}{I_0(\kappa R)}\,\sin\kappa z \qquad (7.98).
> $$
>
> For $\rho < R$ the integrand decays like $e^{-\kappa(R - \rho)}$ (Theorem §C6.3.2, 2), so the integral may be differentiated under the sign and $V$ is harmonic; at $\rho = R$, $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin\kappa z}{\kappa}d\kappa = \frac\pi2\operatorname{sgn}z$ returns the data. Zangwill regularizes $\int_0^{\pm\infty}e^{-i\kappa z}dz$ with $e^{\mp\alpha z}$ (footnote 12) but keeps only the $1/i\kappa$ part, and then fixes an undetermined "const" in eq. (7.97) by symmetry; the constant is exactly the $\pi\delta(\kappa)$ term of the regularized transform.
>
> *(b) Fourier–Bessel series with $J_0$ ($k^2 > 0$).* Let $W = V - \frac12(V_L + V_R)$. Its data on the wall are $\pm\frac12(V_R - V_L)$ for $z \gtrless 0$, odd under $z \to -z$, so $-W(\rho, -z)$ solves the same problem as $W$ and by uniqueness (among bounded solutions, [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^cau-c6-1-1|Caution: Too much data, too little data, infinite energy]]) equals it: $W(\rho, 0) = 0$. For $z > 0$, $V - V_R$ vanishes on the wall, decays as $z \to \infty$, and equals $-\frac12(V_R - V_L)$ at $z = 0$. So $V - V_R = \sum_nc_nJ_0(x_n\rho/R)e^{-x_nz/R}$ with $x_n = x_{0n}$, and the Fourier–Bessel coefficients of the constant, from $\int_0^1sJ_0(x_ns)\,ds = J_1(x_n)/x_n$ and the norm $\frac12J_1(x_n)^2$, are $c_n = -\frac12(V_R - V_L)\cdot\frac{2}{x_nJ_1(x_n)}$:
>
> $$
> V(\rho, z) = V_R - (V_R - V_L)\sum_{n = 1}^\infty\frac{J_0(x_n\rho/R)}{x_nJ_1(x_n)}\,e^{-x_nz/R}\ \ (z \ge 0), \qquad V(\rho, -z) = V_L + V_R - V(\rho, z) .
> $$
>
> *Agreement.* The two forms look nothing alike, yet by uniqueness they are one function; numerically, at $\rho = 0.5R$, $z = 0.3R$ with $V_L = 0$, $V_R = 1$, both give $0.73364$. Form (b) converges fast away from the gap and shows that the field penetrates each tube only a distance $R/x_{01} = 0.42\,R$; form (a) is better near the gap. On the axis at $z = 0$, (b) gives $\frac12(V_L + V_R)$ because $\sum_n2/(x_nJ_1(x_n)) = 1$, the Fourier–Bessel series of $1$ at $\rho = 0$ ([[§46★ Temperature in a Cylinder#^ex-46-2|341 Ex. §46.2]]).
>
> *Focusing.* Inside each tube far from the gap the field vanishes; near the gap the equipotentials bulge, and an electron near the axis is deflected towards the axis on one side and away from it on the other. The converging part acts where the electron is slower (one reason it wins), and the gap focuses in both directions of travel (Zangwill Figure 7.11; he leaves the general proof as an exercise).
>
> *Source: Zangwill Application 7.4, eqs. (7.94)–(7.98), footnote 12; Problem 7.18 (form (b), the distributional transform and the numbers are worked out here)*

## Plane polar coordinates

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.3.4: The General Separated Solution in Plane Polar Coordinates
> For $V = V(\rho, \varphi)$ (no $z$-dependence; [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]] with $k = 0$), every separated solution is a combination of (Zangwill eq. (7.100))
>
> $$
> V(\rho, \varphi) = (A_0 + B_0\ln\rho)(x_0 + y_0\varphi) + \sum_{\alpha \ne 0}\bigl(A_\alpha\rho^\alpha + B_\alpha\rho^{-\alpha}\bigr)\bigl(x_\alpha\sin\alpha\varphi + y_\alpha\cos\alpha\varphi\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 1. *Full angle, charge-free annulus or disk:* $y_0 = 0$ and $\alpha = m \in \{1, 2, \dots\}$. A disk ($\rho < R$) keeps $1$ and $\rho^m$; the outside of a cylinder, with no growth at infinity beyond an applied field, keeps $1$ and $\rho^{-m}$, plus $\ln\rho$ exactly when the cylinder carries net charge per unit length (then $V$ is not bounded). An applied field $E_0\hat x$ adds $-E_0\rho\cos\varphi$.
> 2. *Wedge* $0 < \varphi < \beta$ with homogeneous Dirichlet data on both rays: $\alpha = n\pi/\beta$, $n = 1, 2, \dots$, generally not integers; the term $\varphi$ (from $x_0 + y_0\varphi$) carries a potential difference between the two rays.
> 3. *Annular sector* $\rho_1 < \rho < \rho_2$ with homogeneous data on both arcs: $\alpha$ is imaginary, $\alpha = i\gamma$, and $\rho^{\pm i\gamma} = e^{\pm i\gamma\ln\rho}$ oscillates in $\ln\rho$: $\sin\bigl(n\pi\ln(\rho/\rho_2)/\ln(\rho_1/\rho_2)\bigr)$ times $\sinh$ and $\cosh$ of $n\pi\varphi/\ln(\rho_1/\rho_2)$, $n = 1, 2, \dots$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.9, eqs. (7.99)–(7.101); Example 7.4 (after eq. (7.100) Zangwill writes the uniform-field term as $+E_0\rho\cos\phi$ for $\vec E = E_0\hat x$; it is $-E_0\rho\cos\phi$)*

^thm-c6-3-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Separation.** With $Z = 1$ and $k = 0$, the radial equation is $\rho(\rho R')' = \alpha^2R$ and the angular one $G'' = -\alpha^2G$. Solutions: $\rho^{\pm\alpha}$ and $\cos\alpha\varphi$, $\sin\alpha\varphi$ for $\alpha \ne 0$; $1$, $\ln\rho$ and $1$, $\varphi$ for $\alpha = 0$ ([[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^der-c6-3-1|Derivation §C6.3.1]], Step 3). The general product solution is the sum (Zangwill writes the angular part with sines and cosines instead of $e^{\pm i\alpha\varphi}$).
>
> **2. Full angle.** Single-valuedness as in [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^der-c6-3-1|Derivation §C6.3.1]], Step 2. In a disk, regularity at $\rho = 0$ removes $\ln\rho$ and $\rho^{-m}$. Outside, the flux of $-\nabla(B_0\ln\rho)$ through a circle is $-2\pi B_0$ per unit length, so by Gauss's law $B_0 = -\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0$ with $\lambda$ the enclosed charge per length; bounded growth removes $\rho^m$ except the term fixed by the applied field.
>
> **3. Wedge.** $V(\rho, 0) = 0$ for all $\rho$ forces $y_\alpha = 0$ and $x_0 = 0$; $V(\rho, \beta) = 0$ forces $\sin\alpha\beta = 0$ and $y_0 = 0$ (if $B_0 = 0$). So $\alpha = n\pi/\beta$.
>
> **4. Annular sector.** Real $\alpha$ cannot work: $A\rho_1^\alpha + B\rho_1^{-\alpha} = 0 = A\rho_2^\alpha + B\rho_2^{-\alpha}$ forces $A = B = 0$ for real $\alpha \ne 0$ and $\rho_1 \ne \rho_2$. With $\alpha = i\gamma$, $\rho^{\pm\alpha} = e^{\pm i\gamma\ln\rho}$ and the radial factor $\sin(\gamma\ln(\rho/\rho_2))$ vanishes at $\rho_2$; vanishing at $\rho_1$ needs $\gamma\ln(\rho_1/\rho_2) = n\pi$. The angular factor becomes $\sinh$, $\cosh$ of $\gamma\varphi$. Negative $n$ only repeat the positive ones up to sign and $n = 0$ gives zero, so $n \ge 1$ suffices (Zangwill's Example 7.4 sums over all integers, which double-counts). As $\rho_1 \to 0$ the spacing $\pi/\ln(\rho_2/\rho_1)$ of the $\gamma$ goes to zero and the sum becomes a Fourier integral in the variable $\ln\rho$ (a Mellin transform).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ In two dimensions $\ln\rho$ is a natural Cartesian variable: the map $\zeta = \ln w$ turns sectors into rectangles, which is why the annular sector separates like a rectangle. This is the first trace of the complex-variable method of [[§C6.4★ The Complex Potential and the Variational Principle#^thm-c6-4-2|Theorem §C6.4.2]].
> - The single-valued case 1 is [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]], part (b); cases 2–3 are new: the angle need not be periodic.

*Uses:* [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]], [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.3.5: Fields at Conducting Corners and Edges
> Near the apex of a grounded conducting wedge whose vacuum region has opening angle $\beta$ ($0 < \varphi < \beta$; Zangwill Figure 7.12),
>
> $$
> V \approx C\rho^{\pi/\beta}\sin\frac{\pi\varphi}{\beta}, \qquad \vec E \approx -\frac{\pi C}{\beta}\rho^{\pi/\beta - 1}\Bigl(\hat\rho\sin\frac{\pi\varphi}{\beta} + \hat\varphi\cos\frac{\pi\varphi}{\beta}\Bigr), \qquad \sigma \propto \rho^{\pi/\beta - 1}
> $$
>
> (eqs. (7.101)–(7.103)), with $C$ fixed by conditions far away. In a concave corner ($\beta < \pi$) the field vanishes at the apex; at a flat surface ($\beta = \pi$) it is uniform; at a convex edge ($\beta > \pi$) it diverges, most strongly at the edge of a thin plate ($\beta = 2\pi$), where $\sigma \propto \rho^{-1/2}$. The charge near the apex and the field energy are always finite.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.9.1, eqs. (7.101)–(7.103), Figure 7.12, footnote 14*

^thm-c6-3-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The local expansion.** Near the apex the region is the wedge of [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]], 2, and $V$ is bounded, so $\rho^{-\alpha}$ and $\ln\rho$ are absent; both faces are at $0$, so the $\varphi$ term is absent too: $V = \sum_{n \ge 1}C_n\rho^{n\pi/\beta}\sin(n\pi\varphi/\beta)$. As $\rho \to 0$ the smallest power dominates: $n = 1$ (eq. (7.102)), assuming $C_1 \ne 0$, which is the generic case.
>
> **2. The field.** $\nabla = \hat\rho\,\partial_\rho + \hat\varphi\,\rho^{-1}\partial_\varphi$ gives $\partial_\rho V = \frac{\pi C}{\beta}\rho^{\pi/\beta - 1}\sin\frac{\pi\varphi}\beta$ and $\rho^{-1}\partial_\varphi V = \frac{\pi C}{\beta}\rho^{\pi/\beta - 1}\cos\frac{\pi\varphi}\beta$; $\vec E = -\nabla V$ is eq. (7.103). For $\beta = \pi$: $\vec E = -C(\hat\rho\sin\varphi + \hat\varphi\cos\varphi) = -C\hat y$, uniform.
>
> **3. Surface charge.** On the face $\varphi = 0$ the normal out of the metal into the vacuum is $\hat\varphi$, so $\sigma = \varepsilon_0E_\varphi = -\varepsilon_0\frac{\pi C}{\beta}\rho^{\pi/\beta - 1}$; on $\varphi = \beta$ the same with the opposite normal and $\cos\pi = -1$. The exponent $\pi/\beta - 1 > -1$ for every $\beta < \infty$, so $\int_0\sigma\,d\rho < \infty$. The energy density $\propto\rho^{2\pi/\beta - 2}$ is integrable against $\rho\,d\rho$ as well.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - ⚑ A conductor's edge, however sharp, carries finite charge and finite energy; the infinite energy of [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^cau-c6-1-1|Caution: Too much data, too little data, infinite energy]] comes only from gaps across which the potential *jumps*: there the term $\varphi$, with $\lvert\vec E\rvert \propto 1/\rho$, is not excluded.
> - The $\rho^{-1/2}$ edge singularity at $\beta = 2\pi$ is the same as that of the conducting disk ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]]) and the strip ([[§C6.4★ The Complex Potential and the Variational Principle#^ex-c6-4-2|Example §C6.4.2]]). Real edges are rounded at some radius $r_0$, and the formula holds for $r_0 \ll \rho \ll$ the other lengths (Zangwill footnote 14): the lightning rod.

^der-c6-3-5

*Uses:* [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: The same law for diffusing particles
> Particles diffusing towards a wedge that absorbs them reach a steady state with $\nabla^2n = 0$ and $n = 0$ on the wedge, the same problem; the particle current $-D\nabla n$ plays the role of $\vec E$. A random walker approaching a convex edge ($\beta > \pi$) is very likely to land close to the edge, so the flux concentrates there; one approaching a concave corner ($\beta < \pi$) is very likely to land on a face well before the corner. Precisely, the density of landing points along a face goes like the surface charge, $\rho^{\pi/\beta - 1}$: divergent but integrable for $\beta > \pi$, vanishing for $\beta < \pi$ (Zangwill's wording, "encounter the convex apex before any other portion", is this concentration, not a positive probability of hitting the apex line itself, which has zero area). The corner exponents are a statement about where random walks end, which is the probabilistic meaning of harmonic functions.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.9.1, box "Analogy with Gas Diffusion"*

> [!example] Example §C6.3.2: A Segmented Cylinder
> An infinitely long conducting cylindrical shell of radius $R$ is cut along two lines into two segments, insulated from each other: the segment $-\alpha < \varphi < \alpha$ is held at $V_0$, the rest is grounded (Zangwill Figure for Problem 7.16). Find $V$ inside.
>
> *Series.* By [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]], 1, $V = a_0 + \sum_{m \ge 1}(\rho/R)^m(a_m\cos m\varphi + b_m\sin m\varphi)$; the data are even in $\varphi$, so $b_m = 0$, and at $\rho = R$ this is the Fourier series of the data:
>
> $$
> a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\alpha}^\alpha V_0\,d\varphi = \frac{\alpha V_0}{\pi}, \qquad a_m = \frac1\pi\int_{-\alpha}^\alpha V_0\cos m\varphi\,d\varphi = \frac{2V_0\sin m\alpha}{m\pi}, \qquad V = \frac{\alpha V_0}{\pi} + \frac{2V_0}{\pi}\sum_{m = 1}^\infty\frac{\sin m\alpha}{m}\Bigl(\frac\rho R\Bigr)^m\cos m\varphi .
> $$
>
> *Closed form.* Write $w = \rho e^{i\varphi}/R$ and $\sin m\alpha\cos m\varphi = \frac12[\sin m(\alpha + \varphi) + \sin m(\alpha - \varphi)]$. For $\lvert t\rvert < 1$, $\sum_m\frac{t^m}{m}\sin m\theta = \operatorname{Im}\bigl(-\ln(1 - te^{i\theta})\bigr) = -\arg(1 - te^{i\theta})$. With $t = \rho/R$ and the gap points $A = e^{-i\alpha}$, $B = e^{i\alpha}$ on the unit circle, the two arguments combine into one:
>
> $$
> V(\rho, \varphi) = \frac{V_0}{\pi}\bigl(\Theta - \alpha\bigr), \qquad \Theta = \arg\frac{B - w}{A - w} \in (0, 2\pi) ,
> $$
>
> where $\Theta$ is the angle at the point $w$ between the directions to the two gaps, measured counterclockwise from $A$ to $B$. *Check:* at the centre $\Theta = 2\alpha$ and $V = \alpha V_0/\pi$, the average of the data (mean value, [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-2|Theorem §C6.1.2]]); on the grounded arc $\Theta = \alpha$ (inscribed angle: half the central angle $2\alpha$), $V = 0$; on the live arc $\Theta = \pi + \alpha$, $V = V_0$. The equipotentials $\Theta = \text{const}$ are arcs of circles through the two gaps. The series and the closed form agree numerically (e.g. $0.51737\,V_0$ at $\rho = 0.3R$, $\varphi = 0.2$ for $\alpha = \pi/3$).
>
> *Induced charge.* On the inside of the shell the normal into the vacuum is $-\hat\rho$, so $\sigma = \varepsilon_0\partial_\rho V\rvert_R$. Differentiating the series termwise at $\rho = R$ gives $\frac{2V_0}{\pi R}\sum_m\sin m\alpha\cos m\varphi$, whose terms do not even tend to zero: it converges only as a distribution. The physical $\sigma$ is the limit $\rho \to R^-$ of the convergent series for $\partial_\rho V$ (the Abel sum), and with $\sum_mt^m\sin m\theta \to \frac12\cot\frac\theta2$ as $t \to 1^-$ ($0 < \theta < 2\pi$) it is
>
> $$
> \sigma_{\text{in}}(\varphi) = \frac{\varepsilon_0V_0}{2\pi R}\Bigl[\cot\frac{\alpha + \varphi}{2} + \cot\frac{\alpha - \varphi}{2}\Bigr] = \frac{\varepsilon_0V_0\sin\alpha}{\pi R(\cos\varphi - \cos\alpha)} ,
> $$
>
> positive on the live segment, negative on the grounded one, and $\propto1/\lvert\varphi \mp \alpha\rvert$ at the gaps: the logarithmically divergent charge of an ideal gap ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^cau-c6-1-1|Caution: Too much data, too little data, infinite energy]]). The bounded solution is nevertheless unique, by the logarithmic-barrier argument there.
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 11, Problem 1 (after Zangwill 7.16, with the live segment at $V_0$ and subtending $2\alpha$); the closed form, its geometric reading and the induced charge are worked out here*
