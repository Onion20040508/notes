Level B separated Laplace's equation in Cartesian coordinates, solved the rectangular pipe and the slot, and built the Legendre polynomials and the azimuthally symmetric solution in spherical coordinates ([[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates|§B3.3]], [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates|§B3.4]]). This section states what makes the method work in any separable coordinate system: the separated equations become Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue problems, whose eigenfunctions are complete, and completeness is equivalent to a closure relation that is an identity between distributions ([[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-1|Theorem §C6.2.1]]). It then removes the azimuthal symmetry with spherical harmonics, proves Poisson's integral formula for the sphere, and works the course's problems: line charges on a grid and on a ring, where the source itself is a distribution, and spheres in contact with dielectrics. Uniqueness ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]) certifies every answer. The cylindrical and polar cases are [[§C6.3 Separation in Cylindrical and Polar Coordinates|§C6.3]].

*Notation.* $V$ for the potential (Zangwill $\varphi$; [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: The potential is V]]), $\varepsilon_r$ for the dielectric constant (Zangwill $\kappa$; [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^cau-c5-3-1|§C5.3, Caution: κ, ε_r, χ and χ_e]]); spherical harmonics $Y_{\ell m}$ orthonormal on the unit sphere with the Condon–Shortley phase, $Y_{\ell,-m} = (-1)^mY_{\ell m}^*$ (Zangwill eqs. (7.73)–(7.74); [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^def-b5-2-3|QM Def. §B5.2.3]], phase caution [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^cau-b5-2-1|QM §B5.2, Caution: Phase conventions]]).

## The method

> [!definition] Definition §C6.2.1: Separation of Variables in Orthogonal Coordinates
> Let $(u, v, w)$ be orthogonal coordinates in which the boundaries of the region are coordinate surfaces (Zangwill Figure 7.3). **Separation of variables** looks for product solutions $V = A(u)B(v)C(w)$ (eq. (7.13)). In a **separable** coordinate system, substituting the product and dividing by it splits Laplace's equation into three ordinary second-order equations, coupled only through **separation constants** $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ (not all independent). Each equation has two independent solutions, $A_\alpha$ and $\tilde A_\alpha$, and the general solution is the superposition (eq. (7.14))
>
> $$
> V(u, v, w) = \sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\bigl[a_\alpha A_\alpha(u) + \tilde a_\alpha\tilde A_\alpha(u)\bigr]\bigl[b_\beta B_\beta(v) + \tilde b_\beta\tilde B_\beta(v)\bigr]\bigl[c_\gamma C_\gamma(w) + \tilde c_\gamma\tilde C_\gamma(w)\bigr] ,
> $$
>
> a sum over the allowed values of the constants, or an integral where they are continuous. The coefficients and the allowed constants are fixed by (i) the boundary conditions, (ii) finiteness in the region, (iii) the symmetry of the problem. Laplace's equation separates in thirteen coordinate systems (Cartesian, spherical, cylindrical, conical, ellipsoidal, the two spheroidal, parabolic, paraboloidal, parabolic and elliptic cylindrical, and, with an extra prefactor, bispherical and toroidal).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.4, eqs. (7.13)–(7.14), footnote 4*

^def-c6-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level B
> [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]] defined separable solutions in Cartesian coordinates and fitted the last boundary condition with a Fourier series. The general statement adds the rule that decides which constants survive: arrange the homogeneous conditions (zero potential, zero normal derivative, periodicity, boundedness) so that all but one of the separated equations become **eigenvalue problems**; their eigenfunctions are then complete, and the one inhomogeneous condition becomes an expansion in them. This is what Zangwill means by "arranging" a complete set, and the next theorem is why it works.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.4, p. 202*

^rem-c6-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.2.1: Eigenfunctions: Orthonormality, Completeness, Closure
> Let $\psi_k$ be the eigenfunctions of a regular Sturm–Liouville problem $(p\psi')' - q\psi + \lambda w\psi = 0$ on $[a, b]$ ([[§23 Sturm–Liouville Problems#^def-23-1|341 Def. §23.1]], where $p$ is called $s$ and the weight $w$ is called $p$), with $p, w > 0$ and separated homogeneous (or periodic) boundary conditions, normalized so that
>
> 1. *(orthonormality)* $\displaystyle\int_a^b\psi_k^*(v)\,\psi_j(v)\,w(v)\,dv = \delta_{kj}$.
> 2. *(completeness)* Every $F$ with $\int_a^b\lvert F\rvert^2w\,dv < \infty$ is the mean-square limit of $\sum_kF_k\psi_k$, with $F_k = \int_a^b\psi_k^*F\,w\,dv$; for piecewise smooth $F$ the series converges to $\frac12[F(v^+) + F(v^-)]$ at every interior point.
> 3. *(closure)* As generalized functions of $v$ on $(a, b)$, for each fixed $v'$,
>
>    $$
>    \sum_k\psi_k(v)\,\psi_k^*(v')\,w(v') = \delta(v - v') .
>    $$
>
> The same holds for the singular problems used in this chapter: the Legendre polynomials on $[-1, 1]$ (finiteness at $\pm1$ replacing boundary conditions), the Bessel functions $J_m(x_{mn}\rho/R)$ on $[0, R]$ with $w = \rho$, and $e^{im\varphi}$ on a period. On an infinite interval the index becomes continuous and the sum an integral (Fourier transforms).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.4.1, eqs. (7.15)–(7.20) (stated without weight; the weight, the precise senses of convergence and the proofs are added here)*

^thm-c6-2-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Orthogonality from the Lagrange identity.** For eigenfunctions $\psi_k$, $\psi_j$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_k \ne \lambda_j$, multiply the equation of $\psi_k$ by $\psi_j^*$, the conjugate equation of $\psi_j$ by $\psi_k$, subtract and integrate:
>
> $$
> (\lambda_k - \lambda_j^*)\int_a^b\psi_j^*\psi_k\,w\,dv = -\int_a^b\bigl[\psi_j^*(p\psi_k')' - \psi_k(p\psi_j^{*\prime})'\bigr]dv = -\Bigl[p\bigl(\psi_j^*\psi_k' - \psi_k\psi_j^{*\prime}\bigr)\Bigr]_a^b = 0 .
> $$
>
> The middle step: $\psi_j^*(p\psi_k')' - \psi_k(p\psi_j^{*\prime})' = \frac{d}{dv}\bigl[p(\psi_j^*\psi_k' - \psi_k\psi_j^{*\prime})\bigr]$, the $q$ terms having cancelled. The boundary term vanishes for separated homogeneous conditions (the Wronskian of two functions obeying the same condition $c_1\psi + c_2\psi' = 0$ is zero), for periodic conditions (the two ends cancel), and at a singular end where $p = 0$ and the functions are finite (Legendre: $p = 1 - x^2$; Bessel: $p = \rho$). With $j = k$ the same identity gives $\lambda_k = \lambda_k^*$; then $\lambda_k \ne \lambda_j$ forces the integral to vanish. Normalization is a choice. This is [[§23 Sturm–Liouville Problems#^thm-23-2|341 Thm. §23.2]] with [[§23 Sturm–Liouville Problems#^prop-23-3|341 Prop. §23.3]].
>
> **2. Completeness (quoted).** That the orthonormal set has no gaps is the deep part. For the cases used here it is proved in the vault: sines and cosines ([[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-20-11|556 Thm. §20.11]]; pointwise, [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|WO Theorem §B4.1.6]]), Legendre polynomials ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-2|Theorem §B3.4.2]], 5, by Weierstrass approximation). For a general regular problem it is [[§24 Expansion in Series of Eigenfunctions#^thm-24-2|341 Thm. §24.2]] (stated there without proof); the standard proof writes the inverse of the differential operator as an integral operator whose kernel is the Green function of the problem, which is compact and self-adjoint on $L^2_w$, and applies the spectral theorem for such operators. In the language of Hilbert spaces "complete" means "orthonormal basis" ([[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-20-8|556 Thm. §20.8]]).
>
> **3. Completeness is closure.** Let $P_N$ be the projection $F \mapsto \sum_{k \le N}F_k\psi_k$. Completeness says $P_NF \to F$ for every $F$, i.e. $\sum_k\lvert\psi_k\rangle\langle\psi_k\rvert = \mathbf 1$ strongly ([[§25 The Completeness Relation#^thm-25-2|556 Thm. §25.2]]). Write $P_N$ as an integral operator:
>
> $$
> (P_NF)(v) = \sum_{k \le N}\psi_k(v)\int_a^b\psi_k^*(v')F(v')w(v')\,dv' = \int_a^b K_N(v, v')\,F(v')\,dv', \qquad K_N(v, v') = \sum_{k \le N}\psi_k(v)\psi_k^*(v')w(v') ,
> $$
>
> exchanging the finite sum and the integral. Take $F$ a smooth test function with compact support in $(a, b)$; it obeys the boundary conditions trivially, so its eigenfunction series converges uniformly (Step 2) and $\int K_N(v, v')F(v')\,dv' \to F(v)$. Since $K_N(v, v') = K_N(v', v)^*\,w(v')/w(v)$ the same holds with the roles of $v$ and $v'$ exchanged; this convergence for every test function is the definition of $K_N(\cdot, v') \to \delta(\cdot - v')$ as generalized functions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-4|QFT Theorem §CA.2.4]]).
>
> **4. Coefficients and orthonormality.** Integrating the expansion against $\psi_j^*w$ and using 1 gives $F_j = \int\psi_j^*F\,w\,dv$ (Zangwill eq. (7.19)), the term-by-term integration being justified by mean-square convergence (the inner product is continuous).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The logical order is the reverse of Zangwill's §7.4.1: orthogonality comes from self-adjointness (Step 1), completeness is a separate theorem (Step 2), and closure is completeness rewritten with a kernel (Step 3). Zangwill assumes closure and *derives* orthonormality by putting $F = \psi_j$; that step presupposes that expansion coefficients are unique, which is orthogonality again.
> - ⚑ The closure kernel converges only as a distribution: $\sum_k\psi_k(v)\psi_k^*(v')$ diverges at $v = v'$ and oscillates elsewhere, like the Dirichlet kernel; it does not converge in operator norm ([[§25 The Completeness Relation#^prop-25-3|556 Prop. §25.3]]).
> - ⚑ With a weight the closure relation carries $w(v')$ (or, equivalently, $\delta(v - v')/w(v)$). Zangwill's eq. (7.16) has no weight, which is right for sines and $e^{im\varphi}$ but not for the Bessel functions of his eq. (7.93), where $w = \rho$.

## Cartesian coordinates

The separated equations $X'' = \alpha^2X$, $Y'' = \beta^2Y$, $Z'' = \gamma^2Z$ with $\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2 = 0$ (Zangwill eqs. (7.21)–(7.27)) and the semi-infinite pipe are [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]] and [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]]; they are not repeated. The constraint says that the curvatures in the three directions add up to zero ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^rem-c6-1-3|§C6.1, Remark: Earnshaw's theorem and zero curvature]]): sinusoids in some directions force exponentials in another.

> [!remark] Remark: The closed box, and the inspection method
> For a box $0 < x < a$, $0 < y < b$, $0 < z < c$ with five grounded walls and $V(x, y, 0)$ given, the homogeneous side walls make $X$ and $Y$ sine eigenfunctions, and the grounded lid selects $\sinh$ in $z$ (Zangwill eqs. (7.29)–(7.34)):
>
> $$
> V = \sum_{m,n \ge 1}V_{mn}\sin\frac{m\pi x}{a}\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b}\,\frac{\sinh\gamma_{mn}(c - z)}{\sinh\gamma_{mn}c}, \qquad \gamma_{mn}^2 = \Bigl(\frac{m\pi}a\Bigr)^2 + \Bigl(\frac{n\pi}b\Bigr)^2, \qquad V_{mn} = \frac{4}{ab}\int_0^a\!\!\int_0^bV(x, y, 0)\sin\frac{m\pi x}a\sin\frac{n\pi y}b\,dx\,dy .
> $$
>
> Several live walls are handled by superposing one-live-wall problems (Zangwill Figure 7.5). An alternative "inspection" method keeps every elementary solution, including the zero-constant ones $A_0 + B_0x$, until a condition removes it. For a duct whose bottom $y = 0$ is grounded, whose lid $y = L$ is held at a potential $V'$ and whose two sides $x = 0, L$ are held at a common potential $V_s$ (Zangwill Application 7.1, Figure 7.6, where the side potential is called $V$), the linear term $V'y/L$ absorbs the inhomogeneous lid, and the remainder is a sine series in $y$ with $a_me^{m\pi x/L} + b_me^{-m\pi x/L}$ fixed by the two sides (eqs. (7.38)–(7.43)). By uniqueness the two methods give the same function, though the series look different.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.5, eqs. (7.28)–(7.34), Figures 7.4–7.5; Application 7.1, eqs. (7.35)–(7.43)*

^rem-c6-2-2

> [!example] Example §C6.2.1: A Conducting Slot
> An infinitely long, infinitely deep slot is formed by grounded plates at $x = 0$ and $x = a$ and a bottom plate at $z = 0$ held at $V_0$, insulated from the walls. Find $V$ inside and its behaviour for $z \gg a$.
>
> *Solution.* $V = V(x, z)$; the walls are homogeneous in $x$, so $X$ is a sine eigenfunction, $\sin(n\pi x/a)$, and the companion factor $e^{\pm n\pi z/a}$ must decay as $z \to \infty$. With $b_n = \frac2a\int_0^aV_0\sin\frac{n\pi x}a\,dx = \frac{2V_0}{n\pi}(1 - (-1)^n)$:
>
> $$
> V(x, z) = \frac{4V_0}{\pi}\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac1n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,e^{-n\pi z/a} = \frac{2V_0}{\pi}\arctan\frac{\sin(\pi x/a)}{\sinh(\pi z/a)} .
> $$
>
> This is Griffiths' slot with the axes renamed; the series is summed in closed form in [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^ex-b3-3-2|Example §B3.3.2]].
>
> *Deep in the slot.* Bound the tail using $\lvert\sin nu\rvert \le n\lvert\sin u\rvert$:
>
> $$
> \Bigl\lvert\sum_{n \ge 3\ \text{odd}}\frac1n\sin\frac{n\pi x}a\,e^{-n\pi z/a}\Bigr\rvert \le \Bigl\lvert\sin\frac{\pi x}a\Bigr\rvert\sum_{n \ge 3\ \text{odd}}e^{-n\pi z/a} = \Bigl\lvert\sin\frac{\pi x}a\Bigr\rvert\frac{e^{-3\pi z/a}}{1 - e^{-2\pi z/a}} ,
> $$
>
> so $V = \frac{4V_0}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi x}a\,e^{-\pi z/a}\bigl[1 + \eta\bigr]$ with $\lvert\eta\rvert \le e^{-2\pi z/a}/(1 - e^{-2\pi z/a})$, already below $0.2\,\%$ at $z = a$. The potential decays over the length $a/\pi$, with the profile of the lowest eigenfunction.
>
> *Induced charge.* On the wall $x = 0$ (normal $+\hat x$ into the slot) $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\,\partial_xV\rvert_{x=0}$. Differentiating the closed form,
>
> $$
> \sigma_{\text{wall}}(z) = -\frac{2\varepsilon_0V_0}{a\sinh(\pi z/a)}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{bottom}}(x) = \frac{2\varepsilon_0V_0}{a\sin(\pi x/a)} .
> $$
>
> Both behave like $\mp2\varepsilon_0V_0/\pi\rho$ near the corners, at distance $\rho$ from a gap, so the charge per unit length on each plate diverges logarithmically: the price of an ideal gap between conductors at different potentials ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^cau-c6-1-1|Caution: Too much data, too little data, infinite energy]], which also shows that the bounded solution is still unique).
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 10, Problem 2 (Zangwill 7.8); the error bound and the induced charges are worked out here*

> [!example] Example §C6.2.2: Faraday's Cage of Charged Lines
> Model a wire mesh by infinitely long line charges $\lambda$ along $y$, spaced $a$ apart in the plane $z = 0$, and a second identical row at $z = d$ (Zangwill Figure 7.7). Near one line, $V \approx -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln s$, so the lines are equipotential wires. Find the field between the rows.
>
> *One row.* $V(x, z)$ is periodic in $x$ with period $a$, even under $(x, z) \to (-x, -z)$, and looks like a sheet with $\sigma = \lambda/a$ for $\lvert z\rvert \gg a$. Separated solutions with these properties (Zangwill eq. (7.47)):
>
> $$
> V = -\frac{\lambda}{2a\varepsilon_0}\lvert z\rvert + \sum_{m = 1}^\infty C_m\cos\frac{2\pi mx}{a}\,e^{-2\pi m\lvert z\rvert/a} ;
> $$
>
> the $\lvert z\rvert$ term is the zero-constant solution, kept because far away the row is a charged sheet. The source is the distribution $\sigma(x) = \lambda\sum_p\delta(x - pa)$, and the jump condition $\partial_zV\rvert_{0^-} - \partial_zV\rvert_{0^+} = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ (eq. (7.48)) requires its Fourier series
>
> $$
> \sum_{p = -\infty}^\infty\delta(x - pa) = \frac1a + \frac2a\sum_{m = 1}^\infty\cos\frac{2\pi mx}{a} \qquad \text{in } \mathcal D'(\mathbb R) .
> $$
>
> *Why this holds, and in what sense.* Pair the partial sum with a test function $f$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-1|QFT Def. §CA.2.1]]) and let $F(x) = \sum_pf(x + pa)$, a finite sum, smooth and $a$-periodic. Cutting $\mathbb R$ into cells, $\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)\cos\frac{2\pi mx}a\,dx = \int_0^aF(x)\cos\frac{2\pi mx}a\,dx$, so the pairing is the $N$th partial Fourier sum of $F$ evaluated at $x = 0$ (the sine terms vanish there). $F$ is smooth, so its Fourier series converges to it ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|WO Theorem §B4.1.6]]), and the limit is $F(0) = \sum_pf(pa)$, which is the pairing of the comb with $f$. The series converges as a distribution and nowhere as a function. (Zangwill's eq. (7.50) is the case $a = 2\pi$.)
>
> *Matching.* The jump of the $\lvert z\rvert$ term is $\lambda/a\varepsilon_0$, matching the constant; the jump of the $m$th term is $C_m\cdot2\cdot2\pi m/a$, matching $\frac{2\lambda}{a\varepsilon_0}$, so $C_m = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0m}$ (eq. (7.51)). The series sums with $\sum_{m \ge 1}q^m\cos(m\theta)/m = -\frac12\ln(1 - 2q\cos\theta + q^2)$, and the linear term cancels:
>
> $$
> V_{\text{row}}(x, z) = -\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\Bigl[2\Bigl(\cosh\frac{2\pi z}{a} - \cos\frac{2\pi x}{a}\Bigr)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Near a line, $2(\cosh u - \cos\theta) \approx u^2 + \theta^2 = (2\pi s/a)^2$, which gives back $-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln s$ plus a constant; far away it is $-\lambda\lvert z\rvert/2a\varepsilon_0$.
>
> *The cage.* Add the second row, $V_{\text{row}}(x, z - d)$. For $0 < z < d$ the two sheet terms $-\frac{\lambda}{2a\varepsilon_0}(z + d - z)$ add to a constant, and what is left is a sum of terms $\propto e^{-2\pi z/a}$ and $e^{-2\pi(d - z)/a}$. Outside the cage the field is $\lambda/a\varepsilon_0$; inside, at more than a mesh spacing from the walls, it is smaller by factors $e^{-2\pi z/a}$: at the centre of a cage with $d = 4a$, $\lvert\vec E\rvert \lesssim 2e^{-4\pi}\,\lambda/a\varepsilon_0 \approx 7\times10^{-6}\,\lambda/a\varepsilon_0$. A mesh shields as well as a solid wall once the cage is several mesh spacings across (Faraday's 12-foot cube of 1836).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.5.1, eqs. (7.44)–(7.51), Figure 7.7 (the closed form, the distributional proof of eq. (7.50) and the number are added here)*

## Spherical coordinates

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.2.2: The General Solution in Spherical Coordinates
> 1. *Separation.* $V = R(r)Y(\theta, \varphi)$ separates Laplace's equation into $\frac{d}{dr}\bigl(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\bigr) = \ell(\ell + 1)R$ and $-\Delta_SY = \ell(\ell + 1)Y$, where $\Delta_S = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta) + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\partial_\varphi^2$ is the angular Laplacian; the solutions finite on the whole sphere are $\ell = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ and $Y = Y_{\ell m}$, $\lvert m\rvert \le \ell$ (eqs. (7.68)–(7.72)).
> 2. *Generality.* If $V$ is harmonic in a shell $r_1 < r < r_2$ (with $0 \le r_1 < r_2 \le \infty$), then there
>
>    $$
>    V(r, \theta, \varphi) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^\infty\sum_{m = -\ell}^\ell\Bigl(A_{\ell m}r^\ell + \frac{B_{\ell m}}{r^{\ell + 1}}\Bigr)Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \varphi) \qquad (7.75),
>    $$
>
>    converging in mean square on each sphere, and uniformly with all derivatives on every closed sub-shell. Regularity at the origin removes the $B_{\ell m}$; $V \to 0$ at infinity removes the $A_{\ell m}$. These are the interior and exterior multipole expansions ([[§C3.2 Spherical Multipole Expansions#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]]).
> 3. *A charged sphere.* A sphere $r = R$ carrying $\sigma(\theta, \varphi)$, with no other charge and $V \to 0$ at infinity, has
>
>    $$
>    V = \sum_{\ell m}c_{\ell m}\Bigl(\frac rR\Bigr)^\ell Y_{\ell m}\ (r \le R), \qquad V = \sum_{\ell m}c_{\ell m}\Bigl(\frac Rr\Bigr)^{\ell + 1}Y_{\ell m}\ (r \ge R), \qquad c_{\ell m} = \frac{R}{(2\ell + 1)\varepsilon_0}\oint Y_{\ell m}^*\,\sigma\,d\Omega .
>    $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.6–§7.7, eqs. (7.52)–(7.63), (7.68)–(7.77) (part 2's proof and part 3 for general $\sigma(\theta, \varphi)$ are added here; Zangwill does the azimuthal case, eq. (7.63))*

^thm-c6-2-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Separation.** In spherical coordinates $r^2\nabla^2V = \partial_r(r^2\partial_rV) + \Delta_SV$ ([[§19 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-19-1|452 Thm. §19.1]]). Insert $RY$, divide by $RY$: $\frac1R(r^2R')' = -\frac1Y\Delta_SY$; a function of $r$ equals a function of angles, so both are a constant, written $\ell(\ell + 1)$. The angular equation is the eigenvalue equation of $\mathbf L^2/\hbar^2 = -\Delta_S$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-6|QM Theorem §B5.2.6]]); its solutions that are finite and single-valued on the sphere are the $Y_{\ell m}$ with integer $\ell \ge 0$ and $\lvert m\rvert \le \ell$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-7|QM Theorem §B5.2.7]]; the azimuthal case $m = 0$ is [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]]). The radial equation has $r^\ell$ and $r^{-\ell - 1}$, as $R = r^n$ gives $n(n + 1) = \ell(\ell + 1)$.
>
> **2. Project on a harmonic.** For fixed $r$ in the shell let $c_{\ell m}(r) = \oint Y_{\ell m}^*(\Omega)V(r, \Omega)\,d\Omega$. Differentiate under the integral (V is smooth in the shell) and use Laplace's equation $\partial_r(r^2\partial_rV) = -\Delta_SV$:
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dr}\Bigl(r^2\frac{dc_{\ell m}}{dr}\Bigr) = -\oint Y_{\ell m}^*\,\Delta_SV\,d\Omega = -\oint(\Delta_SY_{\ell m}^*)\,V\,d\Omega = \ell(\ell + 1)\,c_{\ell m}(r) .
> $$
>
> The middle step moves $\Delta_S$ onto $Y^*_{\ell m}$: the divergence theorem on the sphere, which has no boundary (Green's second identity on $S^2$); the last step uses part 1 for $Y^*_{\ell m}$ (complex conjugation commutes with the real operator $\Delta_S$).
>
> **3. Solve and expand.** By Step 1, $c_{\ell m}(r) = A_{\ell m}r^\ell + B_{\ell m}r^{-\ell - 1}$. The $Y_{\ell m}$ form an orthonormal basis of $L^2(S^2)$ (quoted: restrictions of polynomials to the sphere are dense in $C(S^2)$ by the Stone–Weierstrass theorem, and the restriction of a polynomial of degree $N$ is a finite combination of $Y_{\ell m}$ with $\ell \le N$; for $m = 0$ this is the Legendre argument of [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-2|Theorem §B3.4.2]], 5). So $V(r, \cdot) = \sum c_{\ell m}(r)Y_{\ell m}$ in mean square ([[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-1|Theorem §C6.2.1]], 2).
>
> **4. Uniform convergence inside.** Suppose $r_1 = 0$ (the interior case; the exterior case is the same with the roles of $r^\ell$ and $r^{-\ell-1}$ exchanged, and in a shell the bounds on $c_{\ell m}$ at two radii bound $A_{\ell m}$ and $B_{\ell m}$ separately). Then $B_{\ell m} = 0$, because $\lvert c_{\ell m}(r)\rvert \le \sqrt{4\pi}\max_{\text{ball}}\lvert V\rvert$ by Cauchy–Schwarz stays bounded as $r \to 0$. Fix $r_0 < r_2$; the same bound at $r_0$ gives $\lvert A_{\ell m}\rvert \le \sqrt{4\pi}M\,r_0^{-\ell}$. With $\lvert Y_{\ell m}\rvert \le \sqrt{(2\ell + 1)/4\pi}$, the terms of the series at $r \le r' < r_0$ are bounded by $M(2\ell + 1)^{3/2}(r'/r_0)^\ell$ summed over $m$, a convergent geometric-type series; the same holds after differentiation (extra polynomial factors in $\ell$). By the Weierstrass M-test ([[§25 More on Uniform Convergence#^thm-25-3|451 Thm. §25.3]]) the series converges uniformly with its derivatives, hence equals $V$ pointwise.
>
> **5. Part 3.** Inside, regularity leaves $A$-terms; outside, decay leaves $B$-terms; write them as $c_{\ell m}(r/R)^\ell$ and $c'_{\ell m}(R/r)^{\ell + 1}$. Continuity of $V$ at $r = R$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]]; Zangwill eq. (7.3)) and orthonormality give $c' = c$. The jump condition $\partial_rV_{\text{in}} - \partial_rV_{\text{out}} = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ at $r = R$ (eq. (7.60)) reads
>
> $$
> \sum_{\ell m}c_{\ell m}\Bigl[\frac\ell R + \frac{\ell + 1}{R}\Bigr]Y_{\ell m} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} ,
> $$
>
> and projecting on $Y^*_{\ell m}$ gives $c_{\ell m}(2\ell + 1)/R = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\oint Y^*_{\ell m}\sigma\,d\Omega$. For $m = 0$, with $Y_{\ell0} = \sqrt{(2\ell + 1)/4\pi}\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)$, this is Zangwill's eq. (7.63) for the coefficients of $P_\ell$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The solution is general: every harmonic function in a shell is such a series, not only those built from products. The argument is level B's ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-3|Theorem §B3.4.3]]) with $P_\ell$ replaced by $Y_{\ell m}$; the only new input is that $\Delta_S$ is self-adjoint on the sphere.
> - ⚑ The interior series converges geometrically inside its sphere of definition, so a harmonic function is real-analytic, and its values on any small ball determine it everywhere in a connected region. That is why "going off the axis" works ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-2|Example §B3.4.2]]; Zangwill Application 7.2): an azimuthally symmetric potential is fixed by its values on the axis.

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Cones, and Legendre functions of non-integer order
> With azimuthal symmetry the angular equation is Legendre's equation with constant $\nu(\nu + 1)$, and its two solutions $P_\nu$, $Q_\nu$ exist for every real $\nu$ (Zangwill eqs. (7.53)–(7.57)). For non-integer $\nu$, $P_\nu$ is infinite at $\theta = \pi$, and $Q_\nu$ is infinite at both poles. They are admissible when the region excludes part of the axis: inside a cone about the positive $z$ axis only $P_\nu$ appears, with $\nu$ fixed by the condition on the cone ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^rem-b3-4-4|§B3.4, ★ Remark: Sharp points]]). The extreme case is $\nu = 0$: between a conducting cone $\theta = \theta_0$ at potential $V_1$ and the grounded plane $\theta = \pi/2$ (Zangwill Problem 7.13), nothing sets a length scale, so $V(\lambda\vec r)$ solves the same problem as $V(\vec r)$ and by uniqueness $V = V(\theta)$. Laplace's equation becomes $(\sin\theta\,V')' = 0$, so $V = A + B\ln\tan(\theta/2)$, a combination of $P_0 = 1$ and $Q_0(\cos\theta) = -\ln\tan(\theta/2)$; the conditions give $V = V_1\ln\tan(\theta/2)/\ln\tan(\theta_0/2)$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.6, eqs. (7.53)–(7.57), p. 210; Problem 7.13*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.2.3: Poisson's Integral Formula for the Sphere
> If $V$ is harmonic inside the sphere $\lvert\vec r\,'\rvert = R$ and continuous up to it, then for $r < R$
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \frac{R^2 - r^2}{4\pi R}\oint_{\lvert\vec r\,'\rvert = R}\frac{V(\vec r\,')}{\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert^3}\,dS' .
> $$
>
> Conversely, for every continuous $f$ on the sphere, the right side with $V(\vec r\,') = f(\vec r\,')$ is harmonic inside and tends to $f$ at the sphere: the Dirichlet problem for the ball has a solution. At $\vec r = \vec 0$ the formula is the mean-value theorem.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 7.3 (the converse is added here)*

^thm-c6-2-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Coefficients from the boundary.** By [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]] (interior case), $V = \sum_{\ell m}A_{\ell m}r^\ell Y_{\ell m}$, and continuity at $r = R$ with orthonormality gives $A_{\ell m}R^\ell = \oint Y^*_{\ell m}(\Omega')V(R, \Omega')\,d\Omega'$ (the limit $r \to R$ of the projection, by uniform continuity of $V$ on the closed ball). So
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \sum_\ell\Bigl(\frac rR\Bigr)^\ell\oint\Bigl[\sum_mY_{\ell m}(\Omega)Y^*_{\ell m}(\Omega')\Bigr]V(R, \Omega')\,d\Omega' = \oint\Bigl[\sum_\ell\frac{2\ell + 1}{4\pi}\Bigl(\frac rR\Bigr)^\ell P_\ell(\cos\gamma)\Bigr]V(R, \Omega')\,d\Omega' ,
> $$
>
> with $\gamma$ the angle between $\hat r$ and $\hat r\,'$. The bracket used the addition theorem $\sum_mY_{\ell m}(\Omega)Y^*_{\ell m}(\Omega') = \frac{2\ell + 1}{4\pi}P_\ell(\cos\gamma)$ ([[§C3.2 Spherical Multipole Expansions#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]], 2; [[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^rem-c5-4-6|QM ★ Remark: The addition theorem from the group property]]). Moving $\sum_\ell$ inside the integral is allowed because for fixed $r < R$ the series converges uniformly in $\gamma$: $\lvert P_\ell\rvert \le 1$ and $\sum(2\ell + 1)(r/R)^\ell < \infty$.
>
> **2. Sum the series.** Let $g(x, t) = (1 - 2xt + t^2)^{-1/2} = \sum_\ell t^\ell P_\ell(x)$ ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-4|Theorem §B3.4.4]]; Zangwill eq. (7.66)). Then $\sum(2\ell + 1)t^\ell P_\ell = (2t\,\partial_t + 1)g$, and
>
> $$
> 2t\,\partial_tg + g = \frac{2t(x - t) + (1 - 2xt + t^2)}{(1 - 2xt + t^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{1 - t^2}{(1 - 2xt + t^2)^{3/2}} .
> $$
>
> (This is Zangwill's hint: differentiating $1/\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert = \sum r^\ell P_\ell/R^{\ell + 1}$ with respect to $r$ and $R$.)
>
> **3. Back to distances.** With $t = r/R$, $x = \cos\gamma$ and $\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert^2 = r^2 + R^2 - 2rR\cos\gamma$:
>
> $$
> \frac{1 - r^2/R^2}{(1 - 2(r/R)\cos\gamma + r^2/R^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{R(R^2 - r^2)}{\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert^3} .
> $$
>
> Insert into Step 1 with $d\Omega' = dS'/R^2$: $V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\oint\frac{R(R^2 - r^2)}{\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert^3}V\,\frac{dS'}{R^2}$, the formula.
>
> **4. The converse: the Poisson kernel is an approximate identity.** Let $P(\vec r, \vec r\,') = \frac{R^2 - r^2}{4\pi R\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert^3}$. (a) It is harmonic in $\vec r$ for $r < R$ (each term $r^\ell P_\ell(\cos\gamma)$ of Step 1 is, and the series converges uniformly with derivatives), so the integral with continuous $f$ is harmonic. (b) $P > 0$. (c) $\oint P\,dS' = 1$: apply the formula to $V \equiv 1$. (d) For $\lvert\hat r\,' - \hat r\rvert \ge \delta$ and $r$ close to $R$, $\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert \ge \delta R/2$, so $P \le \frac{R^2 - r^2}{4\pi R(\delta R/2)^3} \to 0$ as $r \to R$. Hence, for $\vec r \to \vec r_0$ on the sphere, $\oint P f\,dS' - f(\vec r_0) = \oint P\,(f - f(\vec r_0))\,dS'$ splits into a part near $\vec r_0$, small by continuity of $f$ and (b)–(c), and a part away from it, small by (d). In the language of generalized functions, $P(\vec r, \cdot) \to \delta_{S^2}(\cdot - \vec r_0)$, the delta function on the sphere ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-4|QFT Theorem §CA.2.4]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The kernel is the normal derivative of the Dirichlet Green function of the sphere ([[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-2-12|Theorem §C7.2.12]]); here it is obtained by summing the separated solution, with no Green function in sight. That every such kernel is a nascent delta function is [[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-2-5|Theorem §C7.2.5]].
> - ⚑ Existence: [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]] gave uniqueness; for a ball this theorem gives existence for all continuous data. It also proves the converse of the mean-value property: a continuous function with the mean-value property on every sphere agrees, on each ball, with the Poisson integral of its boundary values (the difference has the mean-value property, hence obeys the maximum principle of [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]], and vanishes on the sphere), so it is harmonic.
> - The plane version is [[§39 Potential in a Disk#^thm-39-3|341 Thm. §39.3]], with $(R^2 - r^2)/(2\pi R\lvert\vec r - \vec r\,'\rvert^2)$.

## Examples on spheres

> [!example] Example §C6.2.3: A Charged Ring, a Delta Function on the Sphere
> A ring of radius $R$ in the plane $z = 0$ carries $Q$ uniformly. On the sphere $r = R$ its charge is the surface density $\sigma(\theta) = \frac{\lambda}{R}\,\delta(\cos\theta)$, $\lambda = Q/2\pi R$ (Zangwill, from Example 4.5): a generalized function of $\theta$, normalized by $\oint\sigma\,R^2d\Omega = \frac{\lambda}{R}R^2\cdot2\pi\int_{-1}^1\delta(x)\,dx = 2\pi R\lambda = Q$. [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]] (part 3, $m = 0$) needs only its pairing with the smooth functions $P_\ell$, $\int_{-1}^1\delta(x)P_\ell(x)\,dx = P_\ell(0)$, so the coefficient of $P_\ell$ is $\frac{R}{2\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{\lambda}{R}P_\ell(0) = \frac{QP_\ell(0)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R}$, and (Zangwill eq. (7.64))
>
> $$
> V(r, \theta) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{\ell = 0}^\infty\frac{r_<^\ell}{r_>^{\ell + 1}}P_\ell(0)P_\ell(\cos\theta), \qquad P_\ell(0) = \begin{cases}(-1)^{\ell/2}\dfrac{(\ell - 1)!!}{\ell!!}, & \ell \text{ even},\\ 0, & \ell \text{ odd},\end{cases}
> $$
>
> with $r_< = \min(r, R)$, $r_> = \max(r, R)$. The same coefficients come from the potential on the axis, $Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}$, expanded with the generating function (Zangwill Application 7.2; the method of [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-2|Example §B3.4.2]]).
>
> *How the series sees the singularity.* The Legendre series of $\delta(\cos\theta)$ itself, $\sum\frac{2\ell + 1}{2}P_\ell(0)P_\ell(x)$, is the closure relation of [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-1|Theorem §C6.2.1]] at $x' = 0$ and converges only as a distribution. The potential converges as a function off the ring, because of the factors $(r_</r_>)^\ell$. On the ring itself ($r = R$, $\theta = \pi/2$) the terms are $P_\ell(0)^2 \approx 2/\pi\ell$ for even $\ell$, and the series diverges like $\frac1\pi\ln L$ at $\ell \le L$: the logarithmic potential of a line charge, seen through its Legendre coefficients.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §7.6.1, eqs. (7.58)–(7.64); Application 7.2, eqs. (7.65)–(7.67) (the distributional reading and the divergence on the ring are added here)*

> [!example] Example §C6.2.4: Making a Field inside a Sphere
> An origin-centred sphere of radius $R$ is to produce, inside it, the field $\vec E = -\frac{2V_0x}{R^2}\hat x + \frac{2V_0y}{R^2}\hat y - \frac{V_0}{R}\hat z$. Which volume charge inside and surface charge on the sphere do it, with no other charge anywhere?
>
> *Solution.* Integrating $\vec E = -\nabla V$ component by component gives $V_{\text{in}} = \frac{V_0}{R^2}(x^2 - y^2) + \frac{V_0}{R}z$, up to a constant that we set to zero. $\nabla^2V_{\text{in}} = \frac{V_0}{R^2}(2 - 2) = 0$, so $\rho = 0$: all the charge is on the sphere. In spherical coordinates $z = r\cos\theta$ is an $\ell = 1$ harmonic polynomial and $x^2 - y^2 = r^2\sin^2\theta\cos2\varphi$ an $\ell = 2$ one (a combination of $Y_{2,\pm2}$). By [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]] (part 3), each term $(r/R)^\ell$ continues outside as $(R/r)^{\ell + 1}$:
>
> $$
> V = V_0\frac rR\cos\theta + V_0\frac{r^2}{R^2}\sin^2\theta\cos2\varphi\ (r \le R), \qquad V = V_0\frac{R^2}{r^2}\cos\theta + V_0\frac{R^3}{r^3}\sin^2\theta\cos2\varphi\ (r \ge R),
> $$
>
> and $\sigma = \varepsilon_0(\partial_rV_{\text{in}} - \partial_rV_{\text{out}})\rvert_R = \frac{\varepsilon_0V_0}{R}\bigl[(1 + 2)\cos\theta + (2 + 3)\sin^2\theta\cos2\varphi\bigr] = \frac{\varepsilon_0V_0}{R}\bigl(3\cos\theta + 5\sin^2\theta\cos2\varphi\bigr)$: the factor $2\ell + 1$ of part 3 for each multipole. (Had $V_{\text{in}}$ contained an additive constant $C$, it would continue as $CR/r$ and add a uniform $\varepsilon_0C/R$ to $\sigma$; "no other charge" leaves this freedom, which is the total charge of the sphere.)
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 11, Problem 5 (Zangwill Example 7.3)*

> [!example] Example §C6.2.5: A Perforated Conducting Shell
> A spherical conducting shell of radius $R$ with holes of any shape cut out of it (Zangwill's "Unisphere") is held at potential $V_0$, with no other conductor or charge and $V \to 0$ at infinity. Then the difference of the surface charge densities on the outer and inner faces of the metal is the same everywhere on the shell:
>
> $$
> \sigma_{\text{out}} - \sigma_{\text{in}} = \frac{\varepsilon_0V_0}{R} .
> $$
>
> *Why.* Off the shell $V$ is harmonic, regular at the origin and decaying, and continuous through the holes, so it has the form of [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]] (part 3): $\sum A_{\ell m}(r/R)^\ell Y_{\ell m}$ inside, $\sum A_{\ell m}(R/r)^{\ell + 1}Y_{\ell m}$ outside, with unknown $A_{\ell m}$ (eq. (7.78)). On the metal $\sum A_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m} = V_0$ (eq. (7.79)). The outer face has outward normal $+\hat r$ and the inner face $-\hat r$, so $\sigma_{\text{out}} = -\varepsilon_0\partial_rV_>$ and $\sigma_{\text{in}} = +\varepsilon_0\partial_rV_<$, and on the metal
>
> $$
> \sigma_{\text{out}} - \sigma_{\text{in}} = -\varepsilon_0\Bigl(\frac{\partial V_>}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial V_<}{\partial r}\Bigr)_R = -\varepsilon_0\sum_{\ell m}A_{\ell m}\frac{-(\ell + 1) + \ell}{R}Y_{\ell m} = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{R}\sum_{\ell m}A_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m} = \frac{\varepsilon_0V_0}{R} .
> $$
>
> *Check.* Without holes, $\sigma_{\text{in}} = 0$ and $\sigma_{\text{out}} = \varepsilon_0V_0/R$, the charge $4\pi\varepsilon_0RV_0$ of a sphere at potential $V_0$. Zangwill's eq. (7.80) writes $\Delta\sigma = \varepsilon_0[\partial_rV_> + \partial_rV_<]$ and finds $-\varepsilon_0V_0/R$; the sign of the first expression is reversed, and the correct result is positive (reported in the errata). The individual $\sigma_{\text{out}}$ and $\sigma_{\text{in}}$ do depend on the holes; only their difference does not.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Application 7.3, eqs. (7.78)–(7.80) (sign corrected here)*

> [!remark] Remark: A point source inside the region: Onsager's cavity
> A point dipole $\vec p$ at the centre of a spherical cavity of radius $a$ in a dielectric $\varepsilon_r$, in a uniform applied field $\vec E_0 \parallel \vec p$ (Zangwill Example 7.2; PHY 505, HW 11, Problem 4), is a separation problem with the source inside the region. The dipole, the distribution $-\vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3$ ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^mod-c3-1-2|Model §C3.1.2]]), is removed from the charge-free region and enters as the prescribed singular term $p\cos\theta/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$ at the centre; the applied field enters as $-E_0r\cos\theta$ at infinity. Both are pure $P_1(\cos\theta)$, and the matching conditions at $r = a$ ([[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]]) do not mix different $\ell$ (orthogonality, [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-1|Theorem §C6.2.1]]), so the trial form $(Ar + p/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)\cos\theta$ inside and $(-E_0r + B/r^2)\cos\theta$ outside is complete, and two matching conditions fix $A$ and $B$. The result, the cavity field $\frac{3\varepsilon_r}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}\vec E_0$ plus Onsager's reaction field $\frac{2(\varepsilon_r - 1)}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}\frac{\vec p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}$, and its use in the theory of polar liquids are at home in [[§C5.4 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^thm-c5-4-5|Theorem §C5.4.5]]; the cavity without the dipole is the case $\varepsilon_{\text{in}} = 1$ of [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-3-3|Theorem §C5.3.3]].
>
> *Source: Zangwill Example 7.2*

> [!example] Example §C6.2.6: A Conducting Sphere at a Dielectric Boundary
> A conducting sphere of radius $R$ with charge $Q$ is centred on the plane $z = 0$ that separates a dielectric $\varepsilon_{r1}$ (above) from $\varepsilon_{r2}$ (below). Find the potential outside, and the free and polarization charges.
>
> *Guess.* Try $V = \frac{C}{r}$ in both dielectrics. It is harmonic, constant on the sphere (a conductor), and continuous across $z = 0$. Its field is radial, hence tangent to the plane $z = 0$: $E_\parallel$ is continuous and $D_\perp = 0$ on both sides, so the interface conditions of [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]] hold with no free charge on the plane. The total charge fixes $C$: Gauss's law for $\vec D$ over a sphere of radius $r$, half in each dielectric ([[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]]),
>
> $$
> 2\pi r^2\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{r1}E + 2\pi r^2\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{r2}E = Q \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E = \frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_{r1} + \varepsilon_{r2})r^2}, \qquad V = \frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_{r1} + \varepsilon_{r2})\,r} .
> $$
>
> The data are "conductor with given charge" plus dielectric interfaces and $V \to 0$, so by [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]] the guess is the solution. It is the vacuum potential with $\varepsilon_0$ replaced by the average $\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_{r1} + \varepsilon_{r2})/2$.
>
> *Charges.* Free charge sits only on the sphere, $\sigma_f = D_r(R) = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{ri}E(R)$ on hemisphere $i$:
>
> $$
> \sigma_{f1} = \frac{\varepsilon_{r1}}{\varepsilon_{r1} + \varepsilon_{r2}}\,\frac{Q}{2\pi R^2}, \qquad \sigma_{f2} = \frac{\varepsilon_{r2}}{\varepsilon_{r1} + \varepsilon_{r2}}\,\frac{Q}{2\pi R^2},
> $$
>
> so the hemisphere against the stronger dielectric carries more. The polarization $\vec P = \varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_{ri} - 1)\vec E$ is radial with $\nabla\cdot\vec P = 0$ for $r > R$ (since $\nabla\cdot(\hat r/r^2) = 0$ there), so there is no volume polarization charge; on the plane $P_z = 0$, so none there either. On the sphere the dielectric's outward normal is $-\hat r$, so $\sigma_{\text{pol},i} = \vec P\cdot(-\hat r) = -\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_{ri} - 1)E(R)$. The total surface charge on each hemisphere, $\sigma_{fi} + \sigma_{\text{pol},i} = \varepsilon_0E(R)$, is the same on both: the total charge is uniform, as it must be for a radial field. The total polarization charge is $-Q\,\frac{\varepsilon_{r1} + \varepsilon_{r2} - 2}{\varepsilon_{r1} + \varepsilon_{r2}}$.
>
> *Source: PHY 505, HW 10, Problem 4 (Zangwill 7.14)*
