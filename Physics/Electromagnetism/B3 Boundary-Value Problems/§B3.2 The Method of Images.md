*Sources: Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (4th ed.), §3.2 · PHY 422 (Thamm, Spring 2024).*

A charge near a conductor induces charge on it, and the field is due to both; but how much is induced, and where, is part of the unknown. Level A described the induction only qualitatively ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^ex-a1-1-2|Example §A1.1.2]], [[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^rem-a1-1-4|§A1.1, Remark: Charged bodies attract neutral ones]]). The method of images computes it exactly for planes, spheres and a few other shapes. Fictitious charges are placed *outside* the region of interest so that, together with the real charges, they produce the right potential on the conductor. By the first uniqueness theorem ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]), the potential they produce in the region is then the true one. From it follow the induced surface charge, the force on the real charge and the energy, which, unlike the field, is *not* that of the image configuration.

## The classic image problem

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.1: Point Charge above a Grounded Conducting Plane
> A point charge $q$ is held at $(0, 0, d)$ above an infinite grounded conducting plane $z = 0$. In the region $z \ge 0$ the potential is that of $q$ together with an **image charge** $-q$ at $(0, 0, -d)$, with no conductor (Griffiths eq. (3.9)):
>
> $$
> V(x, y, z) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{q}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z - d)^2}} - \frac{q}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z + d)^2}}\right] .
> $$
>
> The induced surface charge on the plane and its total are (eqs. (3.10)–(3.11))
>
> $$
> \sigma(x, y) = \frac{-qd}{2\pi(x^2 + y^2 + d^2)^{3/2}}, \qquad Q_{\text{ind}} = \int\sigma\,da = -q .
> $$
>
> The charge is attracted to the plane with the force (eq. (3.12))
>
> $$
> \vec F = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q^2}{(2d)^2}\,\hat z ,
> $$
>
> and the work needed to bring it in from infinity, the energy of the configuration, is (eq. (3.14))
>
> $$
> W = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q^2}{4d} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.2.1–3.2.3, eqs. (3.9)–(3.14)*

^thm-b3-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Potential.* We need $V$ in $z > 0$, where the only charge is $q$, with the boundary conditions $V = 0$ on $z = 0$ (grounded plane) and $V \to 0$ far from the charge. The two-charge potential satisfies Poisson's equation in $z > 0$ with exactly this charge, since the image lies outside the region; it is a superposition of point-charge potentials ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]). On $z = 0$ the two distances are equal, so $V = 0$, and $V \to 0$ at infinity. By the corollary of [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]] (boundary: the plane together with a large hemisphere) it is the potential of the actual problem.
>
> *Induced charge.* Just above a conductor $\sigma = \varepsilon_0E_n = -\varepsilon_0\,\partial V/\partial n$ (Griffiths eq. (2.49); [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-1|Theorem §B2.4.1]], [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-4|Theorem §A2.4.4]]), with the normal along $+\hat z$. Differentiating,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial V}{\partial z} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left\{\frac{-q(z - d)}{[x^2 + y^2 + (z - d)^2]^{3/2}} + \frac{q(z + d)}{[x^2 + y^2 + (z + d)^2]^{3/2}}\right\} \xrightarrow{z = 0} \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2qd}{(x^2 + y^2 + d^2)^{3/2}} ,
> $$
>
> which gives $\sigma$. In polar coordinates on the plane, $s^2 = x^2 + y^2$ and $da = s\,ds\,d\varphi$:
>
> $$
> Q_{\text{ind}} = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty\frac{-qd}{2\pi(s^2 + d^2)^{3/2}}\,s\,ds\,d\varphi = qd\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{s^2 + d^2}}\right]_0^\infty = -q .
> $$
>
> *Force.* Near $q$ the potential, hence the field, is the same as in the two-charge problem. The field of the induced charge at $q$ is that of the image, and the force is Coulomb's attraction of $-q$ at distance $2d$.
>
> *Energy.* To bring $q$ slowly from infinity to height $d$, an external agent applies $-\vec F(z) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{4z^2}\hat z$ at height $z$, doing the work
>
> $$
> W = \int_\infty^d\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q^2}{4z^2}\,dz = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[-\frac{q^2}{4z}\right]_\infty^d = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q^2}{4d} .
> $$
>
> The induced charge moves too, but along an equipotential, which costs nothing.

^der-b3-2-1

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]], [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-1|Theorem §B2.4.1]], [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-4|Theorem §A2.4.4]]

> [!caution] Caution: The fields agree, the energies do not
> The two charges $\pm q$ without a conductor have the energy $W = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{2d}$ ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]]; Griffiths eq. (3.13)), twice the true value. Two ways to see why:
> - *Field energy.* $W = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$ ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]]). The two fields agree for $z > 0$, and by symmetry each half-space carries half of the image configuration's field energy; the real field vanishes for $z < 0$.
> - *Work.* Bringing in two real charges means doing work on both; with the conductor, only $q$ is moved.
>
> A rule that works for every grounded conductor (PHY 422, HW 5 solutions): $W = \tfrac12\,q\,V_{\text{ind}}(\vec r_q)$, where $V_{\text{ind}}$ is the potential of the induced charge (equivalently of the images) at the position of $q$. The factor $\tfrac12$ appears because the induced charge grows in proportion to $q$ as it is brought in. Adding up the Coulomb energies of all pairs of real and image charges instead can be off by more than a factor of two ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^ex-b3-2-1|Example §B3.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.2.3, eqs. (3.13)–(3.14) · PHY 422, HW 5 solutions, Problem 3(c) (Mirror mirror)*

## The grounded sphere

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.2: Point Charge outside a Grounded Conducting Sphere
> A point charge $q$ is at distance $a$ from the centre of a grounded conducting sphere of radius $R < a$. Outside the sphere the potential is that of $q$ together with the image charge
>
> $$
> q' = -\frac{R}{a}\,q \quad\text{at distance}\quad b = \frac{R^2}{a}
> $$
>
> from the centre, on the line towards $q$ (Griffiths eqs. (3.15)–(3.17)). With the $z$ axis through $q$ and spherical coordinates $(r, \theta)$ (eq. (3.19)),
>
> $$
> V(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{q}{\sqrt{r^2 + a^2 - 2ra\cos\theta}} - \frac{q}{\sqrt{R^2 + (ra/R)^2 - 2ra\cos\theta}}\right] \qquad (r \ge R) .
> $$
>
> The induced surface charge is
>
> $$
> \sigma(\theta) = -\frac{q}{4\pi R}\,\frac{a^2 - R^2}{(R^2 + a^2 - 2aR\cos\theta)^{3/2}} ,
> $$
>
> with total $q' = -qR/a$, and the charge is attracted with the force (eq. (3.18))
>
> $$
> F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{qq'}{(a - b)^2} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q^2Ra}{(a^2 - R^2)^2} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 3.2, eqs. (3.15)–(3.19); Problem 3.8 (induced charge, solved here)*

^thm-b3-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The two forms agree.* The distance from $q$ to the field point is $\mathcal R = \sqrt{r^2 + a^2 - 2ra\cos\theta}$ (law of cosines), and from $q'$ it is $\mathcal R' = \sqrt{r^2 + b^2 - 2rb\cos\theta}$. With $b = R^2/a$,
>
> $$
> \frac{q'}{\mathcal R'} = \frac{-(R/a)\,q}{\sqrt{r^2 + R^4/a^2 - 2rR^2\cos\theta/a}} = \frac{-q}{\sqrt{(ra/R)^2 + R^2 - 2ra\cos\theta}} ,
> $$
>
> which is the second term.
>
> *Boundary conditions.* At $r = R$ the second square root becomes $\sqrt{a^2 + R^2 - 2Ra\cos\theta}$, equal to the first, so $V = 0$ on the sphere; and $V \to 0$ at infinity. The image lies inside the sphere, outside the region $r > R$ where $V$ is wanted, so there the only charge is $q$. By [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]] this is the potential.
>
> *Induced charge.* With $D = \sqrt{R^2 + a^2 - 2aR\cos\theta}$ and $c = \cos\theta$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\bigg|_{r = R} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[-\frac{R - ac}{D^3} + \frac{a^2/R - ac}{D^3}\right] = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{a^2 - R^2}{R\,D^3} ,
> $$
>
> and $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\,\partial V/\partial r$ at $r = R$ gives the formula. Integrating over the sphere with $da = R^2\,d\varphi\,dc$,
>
> $$
> \oint\sigma\,da = -\frac{q(a^2 - R^2)R}{2}\int_{-1}^1\frac{dc}{(R^2 + a^2 - 2aRc)^{3/2}} = -\frac{q(a^2 - R^2)R}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{aR}\left[\frac{1}{a - R} - \frac{1}{a + R}\right] = -\frac{qR}{a} .
> $$
>
> *Force.* Outside the sphere the field of the induced charge equals that of $q'$, at distance $a - b = (a^2 - R^2)/a$ from $q$; Coulomb's law gives $F$.

> [!remark] Remark: Why a sphere has an image
> The sphere is where $q/\mathcal R + q'/\mathcal R' = 0$, that is, where the ratio $\mathcal R'/\mathcal R$ of the distances to two fixed points is the constant $R/a$. A theorem of Apollonius says that this locus is a sphere, which is what apparently led Kelvin to the solution in 1848 (Griffiths, footnote 7). The image must lie *outside* the region where $V$ is computed, here inside the sphere. An image placed in the region would change $\rho$ there, and one would be solving Poisson's equation with the wrong source.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.2.4, footnote 7*

^rem-b3-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: A sphere at fixed potential, or neutral
> Adding a second image $q''$ at the centre of the sphere of [[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]] changes the sphere's potential to the constant $q''/4\pi\varepsilon_0R$ and keeps $V \to 0$ at infinity. So $q'' = 4\pi\varepsilon_0RV_0$ holds the sphere at $V_0$, and $q'' = -q' = Rq/a$ makes it neutral (Griffiths Problem 3.9); the resulting force is worked out in [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^ex-b2-4-2|Example §B2.4.2]]. Two observations complete it.
> - The neutral sphere floats at $q''/4\pi\varepsilon_0R = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0a$, the potential that $q$ alone produces at its centre. That is the mean-value property ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^rem-b3-1-2|§B3.1, Remark: The same from Coulomb's law]]): averaged over the sphere, $q$ contributes its value at the centre, and the induced charge on the sphere, with total zero, contributes $Q_{\text{ind}}/4\pi\varepsilon_0R = 0$.
> - Far away the attraction falls as $1/a^5$. That is the force between a charge and the dipole it induces, with the polarizability $\alpha = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3$ of a conducting sphere ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]], [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^ex-b4-2-2|Example §B4.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 3.9 · PHY 422, HW 5, Problem 2 (Spherical conductor force)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.2.1: A Charge in a Right-Angled Corner
> Two grounded semi-infinite conducting plates meet at $90^\circ$ along the $z$ axis, occupying the half-planes $x = 0,\ y > 0$ and $y = 0,\ x > 0$. A charge $q$ sits at $(a, b, 0)$.
>
> *Images.* Reflect $q$ in each plate, with opposite sign, and reflect once more: $-q$ at $(-a, b, 0)$ and at $(a, -b, 0)$, $+q$ at $(-a, -b, 0)$. The four charges change sign under $x \to -x$ and under $y \to -y$, so their potential vanishes on both planes; in the quadrant $x, y > 0$ the only charge is $q$, so by [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]] the potential there is
>
> $$
> V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{\mathcal R_{++}} + \frac{1}{\mathcal R_{--}} - \frac{1}{\mathcal R_{+-}} - \frac{1}{\mathcal R_{-+}}\right], \qquad \mathcal R_{\pm\pm} = \sqrt{(x \mp a)^2 + (y \mp b)^2 + z^2} .
> $$
>
> *Force.* The images at distances $2a$, $2b$ and $2\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ give
>
> $$
> \vec F = \frac{q^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\left(\frac{a}{(a^2 + b^2)^{3/2}} - \frac{1}{a^2}\right)\hat x + \left(\frac{b}{(a^2 + b^2)^{3/2}} - \frac{1}{b^2}\right)\hat y\right] .
> $$
>
> *Work to bring $q$ in from infinity.* By the rule of [[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^cau-b3-2-1|Caution: The fields agree, the energies do not]], with $V_{\text{ind}}(a, b) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\bigl[-\frac{1}{2a} - \frac{1}{2b} + \frac{1}{2\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}\bigr]$,
>
> $$
> W = \tfrac12\,q\,V_{\text{ind}} = \frac{q^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}} - \frac1a - \frac1b\right] < 0 .
> $$
>
> Its gradient with respect to $(a, b)$ is $-\vec F$, as it must be. Summing the Coulomb energies of all six pairs among the four charges gives four times this. In field language, the four-charge field fills all four quadrants, the real field only one.
>
> *Other angles.* The construction works whenever the angle between the plates is $\pi/n$, $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$. Then the repeated reflections in the two plates (a dihedral group with $2n$ elements) produce $2n - 1$ images, all outside the physical wedge. For other angles some reflected images land inside the wedge, and the method fails.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 5, Problem 3 (Mirror mirror) and its typed solutions; Griffiths Problem 3.11*

^ex-b3-2-1

> [!example] Example §B3.2.2: A Line Charge above a Grounded Plane
> An infinite wire with charge per length $\lambda$ runs parallel to the $y$ axis at height $L$ above the grounded plane $z = 0$.
>
> *The auxiliary problem.* Two parallel lines $+\lambda$ at $(x, z) = (0, L)$ and $-\lambda$ at $(0, -L)$, without a conductor. A single line has $V = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln s + \text{const}$ at distance $s$ ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^ex-a3-3-3|Example §A3.3.3]]), so the pair, with $V = 0$ midway, has
>
> $$
> V(x, z) = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\ln\frac{x^2 + (z + L)^2}{x^2 + (z - L)^2} .
> $$
>
> *The conductor problem.* This vanishes on the whole plane $z = 0$; far from the wires the two logarithms cancel and $V \to 0$, although a single line's potential grows without bound; and above the plane its only source is the real wire. By [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]] it is the potential above the plane.
>
> *Induced charge and force.* $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\,\partial V/\partial z$ at $z = 0$:
>
> $$
> \sigma(x) = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\left[\frac{2L}{x^2 + L^2}\right] = -\frac{\lambda L}{\pi(x^2 + L^2)}, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^\infty\sigma\,dx = -\lambda .
> $$
>
> The field of the induced charge at the wire is the field of the image line at distance $2L$, $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0(2L)}$ downwards ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-5|Theorem §A1.4.5]]). The force per unit length on the wire is therefore
>
> $$
> \vec f = -\frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0L}\,\hat z ,
> $$
>
> an attraction falling as $1/L$, the line-charge counterpart of $q^2/(2d)^2$.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 5, Problem 4 (Wire above a grounded plane) and its typed solutions; Griffiths Problem 3.10*

^ex-b3-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Where images work
> Any stationary charge distribution near a grounded plane is handled by its mirror image with reversed sign: an image dipole for a dipole ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^ex-b4-2-3|Example §B4.2.3]]), a mirror image for a charged object. The sphere, the wedges of angle $\pi/n$ and line charges near planes are the other standard cases. Since the equipotentials of two opposite line charges are circular cylinders, by the theorem of Apollonius again, the same pair of lines also solves problems with conducting cylinders (Griffiths Problems 3.12, 3.40). A conducting sphere in a uniform field is the limit of two images at infinity (Griffiths Problem 3.53; solved by separation of variables in [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]]). For most shapes no finite set of images exists. The method is, in Griffiths' words, "as much an art as a science".
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.2.4; Problems 3.12, 3.40, 3.53*

^rem-b3-2-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The potential of a unit point charge and its image in a grounded plane, as a function of both positions, is the Green's function of the half-space for the Dirichlet problem: it solves every grounded-plane problem by superposition (Green's functions: level C and Mathematical Methods, planned).
> - Line charges have the two-dimensional fundamental solution $\ln s$ as their potential ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-5|452 Thm. §17.5]]); point charges have $1/r$ ([[§19 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^rem-19-2|452 §19, Remark: Verification: Radial Functions]]).
> - Images rely on reflection symmetry: Laplace's equation is unchanged by reflections, so the reflected potential of a solution is a solution. The reflections in the plates of a wedge of angle $\pi/n$ generate the dihedral group of order $2n$.
