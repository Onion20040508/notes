For round boundaries the natural coordinates are spherical. Separating Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates gives powers of $r$ times functions of the polar angle, the Legendre polynomials. This section treats the azimuthally symmetric case, $V$ independent of $\varphi$, which covers spheres in uniform fields, charged shells and disks. It is the vault's home of the Legendre polynomials: Rodrigues' formula, orthogonality, completeness, and the generating function, which expands $1/\mathcal R$ and underlies the multipole expansion of [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential|§B4.1]]. The $\varphi$-dependent case, with spherical harmonics, is in Quantum Mechanics ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics|QM §B5.2]]). Level A met a conducting sphere in a uniform field only as a picture ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium|§A2.4]]); here its field is computed. The method mirrors the Cartesian one of [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates|§B3.3]], and the first uniqueness theorem ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]) again certifies the answer.

## Separating the variables

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.4.1: Separated Solutions with Azimuthal Symmetry
> In spherical coordinates Laplace's equation reads (Griffiths eq. (3.53); [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]], [[§19 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-19-1|452 Thm. §19.1]])
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Bigl(r^2\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\Bigr) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\Bigl(\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta}\Bigr) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial\varphi^2} = 0 .
> $$
>
> Let $V$ be independent of $\varphi$ (**azimuthal symmetry**) and separable, $V = R(r)\,\Theta(\theta)$. Then, with the separation constant written $\ell(\ell + 1)$ (eq. (3.57)),
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dr}\Bigl(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\Bigr) = \ell(\ell + 1)\,R, \qquad \frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\Bigl(\sin\theta\,\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\Bigr) = -\ell(\ell + 1)\,\Theta .
> $$
>
> 1. The radial equation has the general solution $R(r) = Ar^\ell + B/r^{\ell + 1}$ (eq. (3.59)).
> 2. With $x = \cos\theta$, the angular equation becomes **Legendre's equation**
>
>    $$
>    (1 - x^2)\,\Theta'' - 2x\,\Theta' + \ell(\ell + 1)\,\Theta = 0 .
>    $$
>
>    It has a solution that is finite at both poles $\theta = 0, \pi$ only if $\ell = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ (taking $\ell \ge 0$, which loses nothing since $\ell$ and $-\ell - 1$ give the same constant). That solution is a polynomial of degree $\ell$ in $\cos\theta$, unique up to a factor: the Legendre polynomial $P_\ell(\cos\theta)$ ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^def-b3-4-1|Def. §B3.4.1]]).
>
> So the separable, physically acceptable solutions are $\bigl(Ar^\ell + B/r^{\ell + 1}\bigr)P_\ell(\cos\theta)$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.3.2, eqs. (3.53)–(3.64) · Nelson ch. 5, §5.4, eqs. (5.10)–(5.11)*

^thm-b3-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Separation.* Without $\varphi$-dependence, multiplying Laplace's equation by $r^2$ gives eq. (3.54). Inserting $V = R\Theta$ and dividing by $R\Theta$ gives $\frac1R(r^2R')' + \frac{1}{\Theta\sin\theta}(\sin\theta\,\Theta')' = 0$ (eq. (3.56)), a function of $r$ plus a function of $\theta$; as in [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]], each is constant.
>
> *1.* Try $R = r^n$: $(r^2\cdot nr^{n-1})' = n(n + 1)r^n$, so $n(n + 1) = \ell(\ell + 1)$, that is $n = \ell$ or $n = -\ell - 1$. These are two independent solutions of a second-order linear equation, so their combinations are all solutions.
>
> *2.* With $x = \cos\theta$, $\frac{d}{d\theta} = -\sin\theta\,\frac{d}{dx}$ and $\sin^2\theta = 1 - x^2$, so $\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\bigl(\sin\theta\,\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\bigr) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[(1 - x^2)\frac{d\Theta}{dx}\bigr]$, which gives Legendre's equation. Try a power series $\Theta = \sum_kc_kx^k$. Collecting the coefficient of $x^k$,
>
> $$
> c_{k + 2} = \frac{k(k + 1) - \ell(\ell + 1)}{(k + 1)(k + 2)}\,c_k .
> $$
>
> The even and the odd coefficients form two independent solutions, both converging for $\lvert x\rvert < 1$ ([[§23 Power Series#^thm-23-2|451 Thm. §23.2]]). If a series does not terminate, then $c_{k+2}/c_k = 1 - 2/k + O(1/k^2)$, so $c_k$ behaves like a constant times $1/k$ and the series grows like $\ln\frac{1}{1 - x}$ as $x \to 1$: the solution blows up at a pole. A series terminates exactly when $k(k + 1) = \ell(\ell + 1)$ for some integer $k \ge 0$ of its parity, that is, when $\ell$ is a non-negative integer. Then the series of the parity of $\ell$ is a polynomial of degree $\ell$, fixed up to the factor $c_0$ or $c_1$, and the other series still diverges. For $\ell = 0$ the second solution is $\Theta = \ln\tan(\theta/2) = -\operatorname{artanh}x$ (eq. (3.64)), infinite on the whole $z$ axis. [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-2|Theorem §B3.4.2]] (3) shows that $P_\ell$ is the polynomial solution.

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]], [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]], [[§23 Power Series#^thm-23-2|451 Thm. §23.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §B3.4.1: Legendre Polynomials
> The **Legendre polynomial** of degree $\ell = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ is given by **Rodrigues' formula** (Griffiths eq. (3.62); the same definition as [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^def-b5-2-3|QM Def. §B5.2.3]])
>
> $$
> P_\ell(x) = \frac{1}{2^\ell\,\ell!}\Bigl(\frac{d}{dx}\Bigr)^\ell(x^2 - 1)^\ell .
> $$
>
> The first five are
>
> $$
> P_0 = 1, \quad P_1 = x, \quad P_2 = \tfrac12(3x^2 - 1), \quad P_3 = \tfrac12(5x^3 - 3x), \quad P_4 = \tfrac18(35x^4 - 30x^2 + 3) .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.3.2, eqs. (3.61)–(3.62), Table 3.1*

^def-b3-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.4.2: Properties of the Legendre Polynomials
> 1. $P_\ell$ is a polynomial of degree $\ell$ with only even powers if $\ell$ is even and only odd powers if $\ell$ is odd: $P_\ell(-x) = (-1)^\ell P_\ell(x)$. Its leading coefficient is $(2\ell)!/2^\ell(\ell!)^2$.
> 2. $P_\ell(1) = 1$ and $P_\ell(-1) = (-1)^\ell$ (eq. (3.63)).
> 3. $P_\ell$ solves Legendre's equation with the constant $\ell(\ell + 1)$.
> 4. *Orthogonality* (eq. (3.68)):
>
>    $$
>    \int_{-1}^1P_\ell(x)P_{\ell'}(x)\,dx = \int_0^\pi P_\ell(\cos\theta)P_{\ell'}(\cos\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{2}{2\ell + 1}\,\delta_{\ell\ell'} .
>    $$
>
>    More strongly, $\int_{-1}^1q(x)P_\ell(x)\,dx = 0$ for every polynomial $q$ of degree less than $\ell$.
> 5. *Completeness.* Every square-integrable $f$ on $[-1, 1]$ (in particular every piecewise continuous one) is the mean-square limit of its Legendre series
>
>    $$
>    f(x) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^\infty c_\ell P_\ell(x), \qquad c_\ell = \frac{2\ell + 1}{2}\int_{-1}^1f(x)P_\ell(x)\,dx ,
>    $$
>
>    and for piecewise smooth $f$ the series converges at every point of continuity.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.3.2, eqs. (3.63), (3.68), footnote 14; completeness is stated there and argued here from the analysis notes; pointwise convergence is quoted*

^thm-b3-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $u = (x^2 - 1)^\ell = (x - 1)^\ell(x + 1)^\ell$.
>
> *1.* $u$ is even, of degree $2\ell$, with leading term $x^{2\ell}$. Each differentiation lowers the degree by one and flips the parity, and $\frac{d^\ell}{dx^\ell}x^{2\ell} = \frac{(2\ell)!}{\ell!}x^\ell$.
>
> *2.* By the Leibniz rule, $u^{(\ell)} = \sum_j\binom{\ell}{j}\bigl[(x - 1)^\ell\bigr]^{(j)}\bigl[(x + 1)^\ell\bigr]^{(\ell - j)}$. At $x = 1$ only the term $j = \ell$ survives, $\ell!\,2^\ell$, so $P_\ell(1) = 1$; parity gives $P_\ell(-1)$.
>
> *3.* $u$ satisfies $(x^2 - 1)u' = 2\ell x\,u$. Differentiate both sides $\ell + 1$ times with the Leibniz rule:
>
> $$
> (x^2 - 1)u^{(\ell + 2)} + 2(\ell + 1)x\,u^{(\ell + 1)} + \ell(\ell + 1)u^{(\ell)} = 2\ell x\,u^{(\ell + 1)} + 2\ell(\ell + 1)u^{(\ell)} .
> $$
>
> With $w = u^{(\ell)}$ this is $(1 - x^2)w'' - 2xw' + \ell(\ell + 1)w = 0$.
>
> *4.* $u$ has zeros of order $\ell$ at $\pm1$, so $u^{(k)}(\pm1) = 0$ for $k < \ell$. Integrating by parts $\ell$ times, all boundary terms vanish and
>
> $$
> \int_{-1}^1q\,u^{(\ell)}\,dx = (-1)^\ell\int_{-1}^1q^{(\ell)}\,u\,dx ,
> $$
>
> which is $0$ if $\deg q < \ell$. For $\ell' \neq \ell$, take $q$ to be the polynomial of lower degree. For the norm, only the leading term of $P_\ell$ survives against $P_\ell$, and with $q = x^\ell$, $q^{(\ell)} = \ell!$:
>
> $$
> \int_{-1}^1P_\ell^2\,dx = \frac{(2\ell)!}{2^\ell(\ell!)^2}\cdot\frac{1}{2^\ell\ell!}\cdot(-1)^\ell\,\ell!\int_{-1}^1(x^2 - 1)^\ell\,dx = \frac{(2\ell)!}{4^\ell(\ell!)^2}\int_0^\pi\sin^{2\ell + 1}\theta\,d\theta = \frac{(2\ell)!}{4^\ell(\ell!)^2}\cdot\frac{2\cdot4^\ell(\ell!)^2}{(2\ell + 1)!} = \frac{2}{2\ell + 1} ,
> $$
>
> using $x = \cos\theta$ and the reduction formula $\int_0^\pi\sin^n\theta\,d\theta = \frac{n - 1}{n}\int_0^\pi\sin^{n - 2}\theta\,d\theta$.
>
> *5.* By 1, $P_0, \dots, P_N$ span the polynomials of degree at most $N$. Polynomials are dense in the continuous functions on $[-1, 1]$ in the uniform norm (Weierstrass's approximation theorem, [[§27 Weierstrass's Approximation Theorem (Not Covered)|451 §27]]), and continuous functions are dense in $L^2[-1, 1]$. So the orthonormal set $\sqrt{(2\ell + 1)/2}\,P_\ell$ from 4 has dense span, which makes it an orthonormal basis ([[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-20-8|556 Thm. §20.8]]). The coefficient formula is the projection ("Fourier's trick"): multiply by $P_{\ell'}$ and integrate.

> [!remark] Remark: Legendre polynomials are orthogonalized powers
> Property 4 says that $P_\ell$ is orthogonal to all polynomials of lower degree. So $P_0, P_1, P_2, \dots$ are what the Gram–Schmidt process ([[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^lem-20-18|556 Lemma §20.18]]) makes of $1, x, x^2, \dots$ in $L^2[-1, 1]$, normalized by $P_\ell(1) = 1$ instead of to unit length. The sines of [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates|§B3.3]] and the Legendre polynomials play the same role: an orthogonal, complete set of solutions of the angular (or transverse) equation, which turns the remaining boundary condition into a projection.

^rem-b3-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.4.3: The General Azimuthally Symmetric Solution
> Let $V$ be harmonic and independent of $\varphi$ in a shell $r_1 < r < r_2$ (with $0 \le r_1 < r_2 \le \infty$), including the parts of the $z$ axis in the shell. Then there (Griffiths eq. (3.65))
>
> $$
> V(r, \theta) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^\infty\Bigl(A_\ell r^\ell + \frac{B_\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}\Bigr)P_\ell(\cos\theta) ,
> $$
>
> the series converging, for each $r$, in the sense of [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-2|Theorem §B3.4.2]] (5). If the region contains the origin ($r_1 = 0$), all $B_\ell = 0$; if it extends to infinity with $V \to 0$, all $A_\ell = 0$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.3.2, eq. (3.65) (stated there; the proof that the series is general is added here)*

^thm-b3-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For fixed $r$ expand $V(r, \cdot)$ in Legendre polynomials ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-2|Theorem §B3.4.2]] (5)), with coefficients
>
> $$
> c_\ell(r) = \frac{2\ell + 1}{2}\int_0^\pi V(r, \theta)\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta .
> $$
>
> Differentiate under the integral and use Laplace's equation (3.54) in the form $\partial_r(r^2\partial_rV) = -\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta V)$:
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dr}\Bigl(r^2\frac{dc_\ell}{dr}\Bigr) = -\frac{2\ell + 1}{2}\int_0^\pi\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta V)\,P_\ell\,d\theta = -\frac{2\ell + 1}{2}\int_0^\pi V\,\partial_\theta\bigl(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta P_\ell\bigr)\,d\theta = \ell(\ell + 1)\,c_\ell(r) .
> $$
>
> The middle step integrates by parts twice; the boundary terms carry a factor $\sin\theta$, which vanishes at both poles. The last step uses Legendre's equation for $P_\ell(\cos\theta)$ in the $\theta$ form of [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]]. So $c_\ell$ solves the radial equation, and $c_\ell(r) = A_\ell r^\ell + B_\ell r^{-\ell - 1}$ by Theorem §B3.4.1 (1). If the region contains the origin, $\lvert c_\ell(r)\rvert \le (2\ell + 1)\max\lvert V\rvert$ stays bounded as $r \to 0$, which forces $B_\ell = 0$. If $V \to 0$ at infinity, $c_\ell(r) \to 0$, which forces $A_\ell = 0$.

*Uses:* [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]], [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-2|Theorem §B3.4.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.4.4: Generating Function; Expansion of 1/𝓡
> 1. For $\lvert x\rvert \le 1$ and $\lvert t\rvert < 1$,
>
>    $$
>    \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 2xt + t^2}} = \sum_{\ell = 0}^\infty t^\ell P_\ell(x) .
>    $$
>
> 2. Consequently, if $\vec r$ and $\vec r\,'$ make the angle $\alpha$ and $\vec{\mathcal R} = \vec r - \vec r\,'$ (Griffiths eq. (3.94)),
>
>    $$
>    \frac{1}{\mathcal R} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2 + r'^2 - 2rr'\cos\alpha}} = \sum_{\ell = 0}^\infty\frac{r_<^\ell}{r_>^{\ell + 1}}\,P_\ell(\cos\alpha), \qquad r_< = \min(r, r'),\ r_> = \max(r, r') .
>    $$
>
> 3. $\lvert P_\ell(x)\rvert \le 1$ for $\lvert x\rvert \le 1$.
>
> The function $(1 - 2xt + t^2)^{-1/2}$, or $1/\mathcal R$, is the **generating function** of the Legendre polynomials, a second way to define them.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4.1, eqs. (3.92)–(3.94), footnote 16 (the expansion found from the binomial series to third order; the general proof is added here)*

^thm-b3-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The expansion exists.* With $x = \cos\theta$ the radicand factors, $1 - 2xt + t^2 = (1 - te^{i\theta})(1 - te^{-i\theta})$. The binomial series $(1 - w)^{-1/2} = \sum_ka_kw^k$, with $a_k = \binom{2k}{k}4^{-k} > 0$, converges absolutely for $\lvert w\rvert < 1$. Multiplying the two series (a Cauchy product of absolutely convergent series) gives, for $\lvert t\rvert < 1$,
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 2xt + t^2}} = \sum_{n = 0}^\infty t^nS_n(\theta), \qquad S_n(\theta) = \sum_{k = 0}^na_ka_{n - k}\,e^{i(2k - n)\theta} .
> $$
>
> Pairing the terms $k$ and $n - k$ shows that $S_n$ is a real combination of $\cos(n\theta), \cos((n - 2)\theta), \dots$, hence a polynomial of degree $n$ in $x = \cos\theta$.
>
> *The coefficients are the $P_n$.* Put $t = r'/r$ with $r' < r$. Then $\frac1r\sum_nt^nS_n = \sum_nr'^n\,r^{-n-1}S_n(\theta)$ is $1/\mathcal R$ for a source point on the $z$ axis, which is harmonic for $r > r'$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]; [[§19 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^rem-19-2|452 §19, Remark: Verification: Radial Functions]]). Apply the Laplacian term by term, which is allowed for a power series in $r'$ ([[§26 Differentiation and Integration of Power Series#^thm-26-4|451 Thm. §26.4]]). Since $\frac{d}{dr}\bigl(r^2\frac{d}{dr}r^{-n-1}\bigr) = n(n + 1)\,r^{-n-1}$,
>
> $$
> 0 = \sum_n r'^n\,r^{-n-3}\Bigl[n(n + 1)S_n + \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl((1 - x^2)\frac{dS_n}{dx}\Bigr)\Bigr]
> $$
>
> for all small $r'$. Every bracket vanishes, because the coefficients of a power series are unique: $S_n$ is a polynomial solution of Legendre's equation, so $S_n = c\,P_n$ ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]]). At $x = 1$ ($\theta = 0$) the left side of 1 is $(1 - t)^{-1} = \sum t^n$, so $S_n = 1 = P_n(1)$ there and $c = 1$. This proves 1.
>
> *2.* For $r' < r$, $1/\mathcal R = \frac1r(1 - 2xt + t^2)^{-1/2}$ with $t = r'/r$ and $x = \cos\alpha$ (law of cosines); for $r < r'$ exchange the roles. Griffiths obtains the first terms directly: with $\epsilon = (r'/r)(r'/r - 2\cos\alpha)$ (eq. (3.92)), expanding $(1 + \epsilon)^{-1/2} = 1 - \frac12\epsilon + \frac38\epsilon^2 - \frac{5}{16}\epsilon^3 + \dots$ (eq. (3.93)) and collecting powers of $r'/r$ produces $1$, $\cos\alpha$, $\frac12(3\cos^2\alpha - 1)$, $\frac12(5\cos^3\alpha - 3\cos\alpha)$.
>
> *3.* From the formula for $S_n$: $\lvert P_n(\cos\theta)\rvert \le \sum_ka_ka_{n - k}$, which is the value of $S_n$ at $\theta = 0$, namely $1$.

## Boundary-value problems on spheres

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.4.5: Potential Given on a Sphere, or Charge Glued to It
> Let $V$ be azimuthally symmetric.
> 1. *Inside a sphere of radius $R$ with $V = V_0(\theta)$ on its surface* and no charge inside (Griffiths eqs. (3.66), (3.69)):
>
>    $$
>    V = \sum_\ell A_\ell r^\ell P_\ell(\cos\theta), \qquad A_\ell = \frac{2\ell + 1}{2R^\ell}\int_0^\pi V_0(\theta)\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta .
>    $$
>
> 2. *Outside it*, with no charge outside and $V \to 0$ at infinity (eqs. (3.72)–(3.73)):
>
>    $$
>    V = \sum_\ell\frac{B_\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}P_\ell(\cos\theta), \qquad B_\ell = \frac{2\ell + 1}{2}\,R^{\ell + 1}\int_0^\pi V_0(\theta)\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta .
>    $$
>
> 3. *A spherical shell of radius $R$ carrying surface charge $\sigma_0(\theta)$*, and no other charge: $V = \sum A_\ell r^\ell P_\ell$ inside and $\sum B_\ell r^{-\ell - 1}P_\ell$ outside, with (eqs. (3.81), (3.84))
>
>    $$
>    B_\ell = A_\ell R^{2\ell + 1}, \qquad A_\ell = \frac{1}{2\varepsilon_0R^{\ell - 1}}\int_0^\pi\sigma_0(\theta)\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta .
>    $$
>
>    In particular, $\sigma_0 = k\cos\theta$ gives $V = \frac{k}{3\varepsilon_0}r\cos\theta$ inside, a uniform field $-\frac{k}{3\varepsilon_0}\hat z$, and $V = \frac{kR^3}{3\varepsilon_0}\frac{\cos\theta}{r^2}$ outside, a pure dipole potential (eqs. (3.86)–(3.87)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Examples 3.6, 3.7, 3.9, eqs. (3.66)–(3.87)*

^thm-b3-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1, 2.* By [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-3|Theorem §B3.4.3]], regularity at the origin removes the $B_\ell$ inside, and $V \to 0$ removes the $A_\ell$ outside. At $r = R$ the series must equal $V_0(\theta)$. Multiply by $P_{\ell'}(\cos\theta)\sin\theta$ and integrate from $0$ to $\pi$; by orthogonality ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-2|Theorem §B3.4.2]] (4)) only $\ell = \ell'$ survives, with the factor $2/(2\ell + 1)$, which gives $A_\ell R^\ell$, respectively $B_\ell/R^{\ell + 1}$.
>
> *3.* Across a charged surface $V$ is continuous and $\partial V/\partial n$ jumps by $-\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ (Griffiths eqs. (2.34), (2.36); [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]]). Continuity at $r = R$, $\sum A_\ell R^\ell P_\ell = \sum B_\ell R^{-\ell - 1}P_\ell$, gives $B_\ell = A_\ell R^{2\ell + 1}$ term by term (orthogonality again). The jump,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial V_{\text{out}}}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial V_{\text{in}}}{\partial r}\Bigr)_{r = R} = -\sum_\ell(\ell + 1)\frac{B_\ell}{R^{\ell + 2}}P_\ell - \sum_\ell\ell A_\ell R^{\ell - 1}P_\ell = -\frac{\sigma_0(\theta)}{\varepsilon_0} ,
> $$
>
> becomes $\sum_\ell(2\ell + 1)A_\ell R^{\ell - 1}P_\ell(\cos\theta) = \sigma_0/\varepsilon_0$ (eq. (3.83)), and Fourier's trick gives $A_\ell$. For $\sigma_0 = kP_1(\cos\theta)$ only $\ell = 1$ survives: $A_1 = \frac{k}{2\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac23 = \frac{k}{3\varepsilon_0}$ and $B_1 = \frac{kR^3}{3\varepsilon_0}$.

^der-b3-4-5

*Uses:* [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-2|Theorem §B3.4.2]], [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-3|Theorem §B3.4.3]], [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: Coefficients by eye
> When the boundary data are a polynomial in $\cos\theta$, the integrals are unnecessary: the degree tells the highest $\ell$, its leading coefficient fixes $A_\ell$, and subtracting that term and repeating works down to $A_0$ (Griffiths footnote 15). For example, $V_0 = k\sin^2(\theta/2) = \frac k2(1 - \cos\theta) = \frac k2(P_0 - P_1)$ on a sphere gives inside $V = \frac k2\bigl(1 - \frac rR\cos\theta\bigr)$ (eqs. (3.70)–(3.71)). Even data contain only even $\ell$, odd data only odd $\ell$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 3.6, eqs. (3.70)–(3.71), footnote 15*

^rem-b3-4-2

> [!caution] Caution: One expansion per charge-free region
> The series of [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-3|Theorem §B3.4.3]] holds in a shell that contains whole spheres and no charge. A charged sphere separates two regions with different coefficients, joined by the boundary conditions. Charge on a plane through the centre, such as a disk, cuts every sphere: inside, the upper and lower hemispheres then have different "coefficients" ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-2|Example §B3.4.2]]; Griffiths' note to Problem 3.22). If the $z$ axis itself is excluded from the region, the second solutions that blow up on the axis, such as $\ln\tan(\theta/2)$, are admissible (Griffiths footnote 13).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.4.1: A Conducting Sphere and a Wire in a Uniform Field
> *(a) Sphere.* An uncharged metal sphere of radius $R$ is placed in a uniform field $E_0\hat z$. Charge is pushed to its "northern" surface and pulled from the "southern" one, and the induced charge distorts the field. Set the sphere's potential to zero; by symmetry the plane $z = 0$ is then at zero too, and far away $V \to -E_0z = -E_0r\cos\theta$ (Griffiths eq. (3.74)). From [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-3|Theorem §B3.4.3]] in $r > R$, $V(R, \theta) = 0$ gives $B_\ell = -A_\ell R^{2\ell + 1}$ (eq. (3.75)), so $V = \sum A_\ell(r^\ell - R^{2\ell + 1}/r^{\ell + 1})P_\ell$. For $r \gg R$ this tends to $\sum A_\ell r^\ell P_\ell$, which must equal $-E_0rP_1(\cos\theta)$; hence $A_1 = -E_0$ and all other $A_\ell = 0$:
>
> $$
> V(r, \theta) = -E_0\Bigl(r - \frac{R^3}{r^2}\Bigr)\cos\theta, \qquad \sigma(\theta) = -\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\bigg|_{r = R} = 3\varepsilon_0E_0\cos\theta
> $$
>
> (eqs. (3.76)–(3.77)). The field at the poles is $3E_0$. The induced charge contributes $E_0R^3\cos\theta/r^2$, exactly the potential of a dipole $\vec p = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3\,\vec E_0$ at the centre ([[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential|§B4.1]]): a conducting sphere has polarizability $\alpha = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole|§B4.2]]). Inside the metal, the same surface charge produces the uniform field $-E_0\hat z$ of [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-5|Theorem §B3.4.5]] (3) with $k = 3\varepsilon_0E_0$, which cancels the applied field, as it must. This is the sphere drawn in [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium|§A2.4]], now computed.
>
> *(b) Wire.* A grounded wire of radius $R$ lies along $z$ in a uniform field $E_0\hat x$. Nothing depends on $z$, and in plane polar coordinates $(s, \varphi)$ separation gives $\Phi'' = -k^2\Phi$ with integer $k$ (single-valuedness) and $s(sS')' = k^2S$, hence (Griffiths Problem 3.24; Nelson §5.3.3)
>
> $$
> V(s, \varphi) = A_0 + B_0\ln s + \sum_{k = 1}^\infty\bigl(A_ks^k + B_ks^{-k}\bigr)\bigl(C_k\cos k\varphi + D_k\sin k\varphi\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Far away $V \to -E_0s\cos\varphi$, with $V + E_0s\cos\varphi$ tending to zero; this removes $\ln s$ (a net charge per length on the wire) and every growing term except $k = 1$. Symmetry under $\varphi \to -\varphi$ removes the sines, and $V(R, \varphi) = 0$ gives $A_0 = 0$, $B_1 = E_0R^2$:
>
> $$
> V(s, \varphi) = -E_0\Bigl(s - \frac{R^2}{s}\Bigr)\cos\varphi, \qquad \sigma(\varphi) = 2\varepsilon_0E_0\cos\varphi .
> $$
>
> In two dimensions the disturbance falls off as $1/s$ instead of $1/r^2$, and the field at the surface is enhanced by $2$ instead of $3$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 3.8, eqs. (3.74)–(3.77), Figure 3.24; Problems 3.24–3.25 · Nelson ch. 5, §5.3.3, §5.4 · PHY 422, HW 6, Problem 2 (Wire in an electric field) and its typed solutions*

> [!example] Example §B3.4.2: The Potential of a Charged Disk off the Axis
> A thin disk of radius $R$ with uniform surface charge $\sigma$ lies in the plane $z = 0$, centred at the origin. On the axis its potential is $V(z) = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\bigl(\sqrt{z^2 + R^2} - \lvert z\rvert\bigr)$ ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^ex-a3-3-2|Example §A3.3.2]]). Find it everywhere.
>
> *Outside, $r > R$.* This region contains whole spheres and no charge, $V$ is azimuthally symmetric and vanishes at infinity, so $V = \sum_\ell B_\ell r^{-\ell - 1}P_\ell(\cos\theta)$ ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-3|Theorem §B3.4.3]]). On the axis $P_\ell(1) = 1$, so $V(r, 0) = \sum_\ell B_\ell r^{-\ell - 1}$, a power series in $1/r$. The binomial series gives $\sqrt{r^2 + R^2} - r = r\sum_{k \ge 1}\binom{1/2}{k}(R/r)^{2k}$, and the coefficients of a power series are unique. So the odd $B_\ell$ vanish and $B_{2k - 2} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\binom{1/2}{k}R^{2k}$:
>
> $$
> V(r, \theta) = \frac{\sigma R^2}{4\varepsilon_0r}\left[1 - \frac{R^2}{4r^2}P_2(\cos\theta) + \frac{R^4}{8r^4}P_4(\cos\theta) - \dots\right] \qquad (r > R) .
> $$
>
> The first term is the monopole $Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$ with $Q = \pi R^2\sigma$; there is no dipole term, since the disk is symmetric under $z \to -z$.
>
> *Inside, $r < R$.* The disk now cuts every sphere ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^cau-b3-4-1|Caution: One expansion per charge-free region]]). Remove the cut: an infinite sheet with the same $\sigma$ has the potential $-\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\lvert z\rvert$ (its field is $\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$, [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-9|Theorem §A1.4.9]]). Inside the ball $r < R$ the sheet and the disk coincide, so $W = V + \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\lvert z\rvert$ has no source there: its kinks across $z = 0$ cancel, and it is harmonic in the whole ball. Hence $W = \sum A_\ell r^\ell P_\ell(\cos\theta)$. On the axis $W = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\sqrt{z^2 + R^2} = \frac{\sigma R}{2\varepsilon_0}\sum_k\binom{1/2}{k}(z/R)^{2k}$, so
>
> $$
> V(r, \theta) = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left[R - r\lvert\cos\theta\rvert + \frac{r^2}{2R}P_2(\cos\theta) - \frac{r^4}{8R^3}P_4(\cos\theta) + \dots\right] \qquad (r < R) .
> $$
>
> Above the disk, $-r\lvert\cos\theta\rvert = -rP_1(\cos\theta)$, below it $+rP_1(\cos\theta)$. These are the different $\ell = 1$ coefficients of the two hemispheres, and they come from the sheet-like field $\pm\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ near the centre. Outside, the disk looks like a compact charge, and no such term appears.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 7, Problem 1 (The potential of a charged disk off-axis) and its typed solutions; Griffiths Problem 3.22 (the subtraction of the sheet and the general coefficients are added here)*

> [!example] Example §B3.4.3: A Charged Shell outside a Grounded Sphere
> A grounded conducting sphere of radius $R_1$ is surrounded by a concentric thin shell of radius $R_2 > R_1$ carrying $\sigma = \sigma_0\cos\theta$. The boundary conditions are $V = 0$ at $r = R_1$, $V$ continuous at $R_2$ with $\partial V/\partial r$ jumping by $-\sigma/\varepsilon_0$, and $V \to 0$ at infinity. With $V_{\text{in}} = \sum(A_\ell r^\ell + B_\ell r^{-\ell - 1})P_\ell$ for $R_1 < r < R_2$ and $V_{\text{out}} = \sum D_\ell r^{-\ell - 1}P_\ell$, these give $B_\ell = -A_\ell R_1^{2\ell + 1}$, $D_\ell = A_\ell(R_2^{2\ell + 1} - R_1^{2\ell + 1})$ and $(2\ell + 1)A_\ell R_2^{\ell - 1} = (\sigma_0/\varepsilon_0)\,\delta_{\ell1}$, so
>
> $$
> V_{\text{in}} = \frac{\sigma_0}{3\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(r - \frac{R_1^3}{r^2}\Bigr)\cos\theta, \qquad V_{\text{out}} = \frac{\sigma_0}{3\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{R_2^3 - R_1^3}{r^2}\cos\theta, \qquad \sigma_{\text{ind}} = -\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial V_{\text{in}}}{\partial r}\bigg|_{R_1} = -\sigma_0\cos\theta .
> $$
>
> The induced charge is equal and opposite to the shell's charge pattern, whatever $R_2$. Superposition explains why. By itself the shell produces inside it the uniform field $-\frac{\sigma_0}{3\varepsilon_0}\hat z$ ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-5|Theorem §B3.4.5]] (3)), so the sphere sits in a uniform field $E_0 = -\sigma_0/3\varepsilon_0$, and [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]] gives $\sigma_{\text{ind}} = 3\varepsilon_0E_0\cos\theta = -\sigma_0\cos\theta$. Outside, the two dipole moments $\frac{4\pi}{3}\sigma_0R_2^3$ and $-\frac{4\pi}{3}\sigma_0R_1^3$ add.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, Midterm 2, Problem 3 (Charged shell outside a conducting sphere) and its typed solutions; the superposition argument is added here*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Without azimuthal symmetry
> If $V$ depends on $\varphi$, separating the full equation gives the same radial functions $r^\ell$, $r^{-\ell - 1}$. The angular factors are the spherical harmonics $Y_\ell^m(\theta, \varphi)$, $m = -\ell, \dots, \ell$, built from the associated Legendre functions ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^def-b5-2-3|QM Def. §B5.2.3]]), and
>
> $$
> V(r, \theta, \varphi) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^\infty\sum_{m = -\ell}^\ell\Bigl(A_{\ell m}r^\ell + \frac{B_{\ell m}}{r^{\ell + 1}}\Bigr)Y_\ell^m(\theta, \varphi) .
> $$
>
> The angular part of the Laplacian is $-L^2/\hbar^2r^2$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-6|QM Theorem §B5.2.6]]), and the $Y_\ell^m$ are its eigenfunctions with eigenvalue $-\ell(\ell + 1)$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-7|QM Theorem §B5.2.7]]); for $m = 0$ they reduce to $\sqrt{(2\ell + 1)/4\pi}\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)$. Griffiths leaves this case to the graduate texts (his footnote 12); in this vault it belongs to level C.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.3.2, footnote 12*

^rem-b3-4-3

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Sharp points need Legendre functions of non-integer order
> Nelson treats a charged conducting cone with its tip at the origin, occupying $\theta \ge \theta_0$, with $\theta_0 > \pi/2$. The region outside, $0 \le \theta < \theta_0$, excludes the negative $z$ axis, so the boundary condition is no longer finiteness at both poles. Instead the angular factor must vanish on the cone, $P_\nu(\cos\theta_0) = 0$, which is met by a Legendre function of non-integer order $\nu$. Near the tip $V \propto r^\nu P_\nu(\cos\theta)$, so the field grows like $r^{\nu - 1}$. A flat plane ($\theta_0 = \pi/2$) gives $\nu = 1$ and a uniform field; a sharp cone ($\theta_0 \to \pi$) gives $\nu < 1$, and the field diverges at the tip. This is the analytic face of the lightning rod ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^rem-a3-5-3|§A3.5, Remark: Sharp points and lightning rods]]). Nelson's warning: Laplace's equation separates only in a few coordinate systems, such as Cartesian, spherical, cylindrical and ellipsoidal, and blunt tips need ellipsoidal coordinates.
>
> *Source: Nelson ch. 5, §5.5*
