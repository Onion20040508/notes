Images need a lucky guess. Separation of variables attacks Laplace's equation directly, whenever $V$ is prescribed on the faces of a region whose boundaries are coordinate surfaces: here planes, in [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates|§B3.4]] spheres. The idea is to look for solutions that are products of functions of one coordinate each. This turns the partial differential equation into ordinary ones, and a sum of such products is then fitted to the boundary data with a Fourier series. Level A has no counterpart: its potentials all came from known charges. The first uniqueness theorem ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]) guarantees that a series meeting all the boundary conditions is the answer. The Fourier machinery itself is in Waves and Optics ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series|WO §B4.1]]).

## Separable solutions

> [!definition] Definition §B3.3.1: Separable Solution; Separation Constant
> A **separable** (product) solution of Laplace's equation in Cartesian coordinates has the form $V(x, y) = X(x)\,Y(y)$ in two dimensions, or $V = X(x)\,Y(y)\,Z(z)$ in three (Griffiths eqs. (3.22), (3.45)). Substituting it and dividing by $V$ splits the equation into a sum of terms, each depending on one coordinate only. Each term is then a constant, the **separation constants**, which add up to zero. Finding the potential by superposing separable solutions is the method of **separation of variables**.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.3, §3.3.1, eqs. (3.22)–(3.25), (3.45)*

^def-b3-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.1: Separable Harmonic Functions in Cartesian Coordinates
> 1. *Two dimensions.* A separable solution of $\partial^2V/\partial x^2 + \partial^2V/\partial y^2 = 0$ satisfies $X'' = k^2X$, $Y'' = -k^2Y$ for a constant $k^2$ (Griffiths eq. (3.26)). For $k^2 > 0$,
>
>    $$
>    V(x, y) = \bigl(Ae^{kx} + Be^{-kx}\bigr)\bigl(C\sin ky + D\cos ky\bigr) \qquad (3.27),
>    $$
>
>    equivalently with $\cosh kx$ and $\sinh kx$; for $k^2 < 0$ the roles of $x$ and $y$ swap; for $k = 0$, $V = (A + Bx)(C + Dy)$.
> 2. *Three dimensions.* A separable solution satisfies $X''/X = C_1$, $Y''/Y = C_2$, $Z''/Z = C_3$ with $C_1 + C_2 + C_3 = 0$; for example $X'' = (k^2 + l^2)X$, $Y'' = -k^2Y$, $Z'' = -l^2Z$ (eq. (3.46)).
> 3. *Superposition.* Laplace's equation is linear, so linear combinations of solutions are solutions, and so are infinite sums, provided the twice-differentiated series converge uniformly.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.3.1, eqs. (3.23)–(3.27), (3.46), p. 133; footnote 8*

^thm-b3-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $V = XY$ into Laplace's equation and divide by $XY$ (eq. (3.23)):
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{X}\frac{d^2X}{dx^2} + \frac{1}{Y}\frac{d^2Y}{dy^2} = 0, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad f(x) + g(y) = 0 .
> $$
>
> Hold $y$ fixed and vary $x$: the sum stays zero, so $f$ cannot change, and $f(x) = C_1$; then $g = C_2 = -C_1$. Writing $C_1 = k^2$, the equations $X'' = k^2X$, $Y'' = -k^2Y$ are linear with constant coefficients, and their general solutions are the exponentials and sinusoids of (3.27); for $k = 0$ they are straight lines. Taking $k > 0$ loses nothing, because $-k$ gives the same functions with the constants relabelled. In three dimensions the same argument applied to the three terms gives three constants that add up to zero. For part 3, $\nabla^2(\alpha_1V_1 + \alpha_2V_2 + \dots) = \alpha_1\nabla^2V_1 + \alpha_2\nabla^2V_2 + \dots = 0$. For an infinite series, termwise differentiation is allowed when the differentiated series converges uniformly, as it does away from the boundary in the examples below, where the terms decay like $e^{-n\pi x/a}$ and the M-test applies ([[§25 More on Uniform Convergence#^thm-25-3|451 Thm. §25.3]]).

^der-b3-3-1

*Uses:* [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]], [[§25 More on Uniform Convergence#^thm-25-3|451 Thm. §25.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Choosing the separation constant
> The sign of $k^2$ is decided by the boundary conditions, not by the equation. In the direction where $V$ vanishes at *both* ends, the factor must be sinusoidal, since an exponential or a straight line cannot vanish twice. In the other direction it is then exponential or hyperbolic: a decaying exponential if the region extends to infinity, $\sinh$ if $V = 0$ at one end, $\cosh$ if the problem is symmetric. Occasionally the $k = 0$ solution has to be kept as well (Griffiths footnote 8 and Problem 3.54).

## The rectangular pipe

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.2: Rectangular Pipe with One Side at a Given Potential
> An infinitely long pipe runs along $z$, with rectangular cross-section $0 < x < a$, $0 < y < b$. The sides $x = 0$, $y = 0$ and $y = b$ are grounded, and the side $x = a$ is held at the potential $V_0(y)$. Inside the pipe
>
> $$
> V(x, y) = \sum_{n = 1}^\infty C_n\,\frac{\sinh(n\pi x/b)}{\sinh(n\pi a/b)}\,\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b}, \qquad C_n = \frac2b\int_0^bV_0(y)\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b}\,dy .
> $$
>
> For a constant $V_0$ only odd $n$ contribute, with $C_n = 4V_0/n\pi$:
>
> $$
> V(x, y) = \frac{4V_0}{\pi}\sum_{n = 1, 3, 5, \dots}\frac1n\,\frac{\sinh(n\pi x/b)}{\sinh(n\pi a/b)}\,\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.3.1, eqs. (3.26)–(3.35); Problem 3.15 (with $x$ and the sides relabelled) · PHY 422, HW 6, Problem 1 (Rectangular pipe) and its typed solutions*

^thm-b3-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Nothing depends on $z$, so $V = V(x, y)$. The boundary conditions are homogeneous in $y$ ($V = 0$ at $y = 0$ and $y = b$), so by [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^cau-b3-3-1|Caution: Choosing the separation constant]] the $y$ factor is sinusoidal. $Y(0) = 0$ removes the cosine, and $Y(b) = 0$ requires $\sin kb = 0$, so $k = n\pi/b$ with $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ (Griffiths eq. (3.29)); $n = 0$ gives $V \equiv 0$, and negative $n$ repeat the positive ones. Then $X'' = (n\pi/b)^2X$ with $X(0) = 0$ gives $X \propto \sinh(n\pi x/b)$. The separable solutions meeting the three grounded sides are therefore $\sinh(n\pi x/b)\sin(n\pi y/b)$, and so is any convergent superposition. Normalizing each by $\sinh(n\pi a/b)$, the last condition reads
>
> $$
> V(a, y) = \sum_{n = 1}^\infty C_n\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b} = V_0(y) ,
> $$
>
> a Fourier sine series on $[0, b]$ ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-7|WO Theorem §B4.1.7]]). Multiply by $\sin(n'\pi y/b)$ and integrate over $[0, b]$ ("Fourier's trick", Griffiths eq. (3.32)). Orthogonality (eq. (3.33); [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-1|WO Theorem §B4.1.1]]),
>
> $$
> \int_0^b\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b}\,\sin\frac{n'\pi y}{b}\,dy = \frac b2\,\delta_{nn'} ,
> $$
>
> kills all terms but one and leaves $C_{n'}\,b/2 = \int_0^bV_0\sin(n'\pi y/b)\,dy$, which is the formula for $C_n$ (eq. (3.34)). For constant $V_0$,
>
> $$
> C_n = \frac{2V_0}{b}\int_0^b\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b}\,dy = \frac{2V_0}{n\pi}\,(1 - \cos n\pi) = \begin{cases} 0, & n \text{ even},\\ 4V_0/n\pi, & n \text{ odd} \end{cases} \qquad (3.35).
> $$
>
> The series satisfies Laplace's equation inside: for $x \le x_0 < a$ its terms and their derivatives are bounded by multiples of $n^2e^{-n\pi(a - x_0)/b}$, so [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]] (3) applies. It meets every boundary condition, so by [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]] it is the potential.

> [!remark] Remark: Completeness, orthogonality and the corners
> The method rests on two properties of the separable solutions (Griffiths eqs. (3.38)–(3.39)). *Completeness*: every reasonable $V_0(y)$, even one with finitely many jumps, is a sum of the $\sin(n\pi y/b)$ (Dirichlet's theorem; [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|WO Theorem §B4.1.6]]; in the Hilbert-space form, [[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-20-11|556 Thm. §20.11]]). *Orthogonality*: this is what makes Fourier's trick a projection onto one basis vector ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-2|WO Theorem §B4.1.2]]). At the corners $(a, 0)$ and $(a, b)$ the data jump from $V_0$ to $0$. On the side $x = a$ the series converges to the midpoint $V_0/2$ at the jumps and overshoots near them (the Gibbs phenomenon, [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-8|WO Theorem §B4.1.8]]). Inside the pipe the factor $\sinh(n\pi x/b)/\sinh(n\pi a/b) \approx e^{-n\pi(a - x)/b}$ damps the high harmonics exponentially: Laplace's equation smooths the boundary data, and a few terms suffice except very close to the live side.

^rem-b3-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Superposing boundary data
> If several sides carry nonzero potentials, solve with one live side at a time and add the results: the sum satisfies Laplace's equation and all the boundary conditions at once (Griffiths Example 3.4 and Problem 3.42). Griffiths' Example 3.4, a pipe $-b < x < b$, $0 < y < a$ with both sides $x = \pm b$ at $V_0$, has the even solution $\frac{4V_0}{\pi}\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac1n\frac{\cosh(n\pi x/a)}{\cosh(n\pi b/a)}\sin\frac{n\pi y}{a}$ (eq. (3.42)), the sum of two one-side solutions.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 3.4, eqs. (3.40)–(3.42); Problem 3.42*

^rem-b3-3-2

> [!example] Example §B3.3.1: The Pipe with a Constant Potential, and a Check by Symmetry
> Take [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]] with constant $V_0$ and a square cross-section, $a = b$. At the centre $\sin(n\pi/2) = (-1)^{(n-1)/2}$ for odd $n$, and $\sinh(n\pi/2)/\sinh(n\pi) = 1/2\cosh(n\pi/2)$, so
>
> $$
> V\Bigl(\frac a2, \frac a2\Bigr) = \frac{4V_0}{\pi}\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac{(-1)^{(n-1)/2}}{2n\cosh(n\pi/2)} = \frac{4V_0}{\pi}\bigl(0.19927 - 0.00299 + 0.00008 - \dots\bigr) = 0.2500\,V_0 .
> $$
>
> The exact value is $V_0/4$. If all four sides were at $V_0$, the potential would be $V_0$ everywhere (uniqueness). That configuration is the superposition of four copies of this problem, rotated by multiples of $90^\circ$, each contributing equally at the centre. The series reaches four-figure accuracy with two terms, because at the centre the $n$th term is damped by $e^{-n\pi/2}$.
>
> For the longer pipe $a = 1.5\,b$ drawn below, the series gives $0.119\,V_0$ at the centre. The equipotentials bunch against the live side and fan out into the corners $(a, 0)$, $(a, b)$, where the potential jumps from $V_0$ to $0$.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 6, Problem 1(b) (Rectangular pipe); the symmetry check and the numbers are worked out here*

> [!example] Example §B3.3.2: The Infinite Slot and Its Closed Form
> Two grounded plates lie at $y = 0$ and $y = a$, and the strip $x = 0$ between them is held at $V_0$, insulated from the plates (Griffiths Example 3.3; Griffiths' $a$ is the plate separation). Now $V \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$ replaces the fourth side. Equivalently, this is [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]] seen from the live side, with distance $\xi = a_{\text{pipe}} - x$, in the limit $a_{\text{pipe}} \to \infty$, where $\sinh(n\pi(a_{\text{pipe}} - \xi)/b)/\sinh(n\pi a_{\text{pipe}}/b) \to e^{-n\pi\xi/b}$. In Griffiths' notation (eqs. (3.30), (3.36)):
>
> $$
> V(x, y) = \frac{4V_0}{\pi}\sum_{n = 1, 3, 5, \dots}\frac1n\,e^{-n\pi x/a}\sin\frac{n\pi y}{a} .
> $$
>
> *Summing the series.* Put $u = \pi x/a$, $v = \pi y/a$ and $w = e^{-u + iv}$, with $|w| < 1$ for $x > 0$. Then $\frac1ne^{-nu}\sin nv = \frac1n\operatorname{Im}w^n$, and $\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}w^n/n = \operatorname{artanh}w = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1 + w}{1 - w}$, so
>
> $$
> V = \frac{4V_0}{\pi}\cdot\frac12\arg\frac{1 + w}{1 - w} .
> $$
>
> Since $(1 + w)(1 - \bar w) = 1 - |w|^2 + 2i\operatorname{Im}w$ has positive real part, its argument is $\arctan\frac{2\operatorname{Im}w}{1 - |w|^2} = \arctan\frac{2e^{-u}\sin v}{1 - e^{-2u}} = \arctan\frac{\sin v}{\sinh u}$, and (Griffiths eq. (3.37))
>
> $$
> V(x, y) = \frac{2V_0}{\pi}\arctan\left(\frac{\sin(\pi y/a)}{\sinh(\pi x/a)}\right) .
> $$
>
> *Check.* As $x \to 0^+$ with $0 < y < a$, the argument tends to $+\infty$ and $V \to V_0$; at $y = 0$ and $y = a$ it is $0$; as $x \to \infty$, $V \to 0$. $V$ is harmonic, being the imaginary part of $\frac{4V_0}{\pi}\operatorname{artanh}w$, an analytic function of $x - iy$. Near the strip ($x \ll a$) the potential is $V_0$ minus a small correction; far away it is the first term, $\frac{4V_0}{\pi}e^{-\pi x/a}\sin(\pi y/a)$, which decays over the distance $a/\pi$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 3.3, eqs. (3.20)–(3.37), Figure 3.17 (the closed form (3.37) is stated there; its derivation is worked out here)*

## Three dimensions

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.3: Rectangular Pipe Closed by a Plate at a Given Potential
> An infinitely long grounded rectangular pipe occupies $0 < y < a$, $0 < z < b$, $x > 0$; its end $x = 0$ is held at $V_0(y, z)$, and $V \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$. Then (Griffiths eqs. (3.48), (3.50))
>
> $$
> V(x, y, z) = \sum_{n, m = 1}^\infty C_{n,m}\,e^{-\pi\sqrt{(n/a)^2 + (m/b)^2}\,x}\,\sin\frac{n\pi y}{a}\,\sin\frac{m\pi z}{b}, \qquad C_{n,m} = \frac{4}{ab}\int_0^a\!\!\int_0^bV_0(y, z)\sin\frac{n\pi y}{a}\sin\frac{m\pi z}{b}\,dy\,dz .
> $$
>
> For a constant $V_0$, $C_{n,m} = 16V_0/\pi^2nm$ for $n$ and $m$ both odd and $0$ otherwise (eqs. (3.51)–(3.52)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 3.5, eqs. (3.43)–(3.52)*

^thm-b3-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]] (2) with $C_2 = -k^2$, $C_3 = -l^2$ and $C_1 = k^2 + l^2$: $X = Ae^{\sqrt{k^2 + l^2}\,x} + Be^{-\sqrt{k^2 + l^2}\,x}$, $Y = C\sin ky + D\cos ky$, $Z = E\sin lz + F\cos lz$. The condition at infinity gives $A = 0$; $V = 0$ on $y = 0$ and $z = 0$ gives $D = F = 0$; on $y = a$ and $z = b$ it gives $k = n\pi/a$ and $l = m\pi/b$ (eq. (3.47)). The general combination is the double sum. At $x = 0$ it must equal $V_0(y, z)$. Multiply by $\sin(n'\pi y/a)\sin(m'\pi z/b)$ and integrate over the end face: by the orthogonality relation used in [[§B3.3 Separation of Variables in Cartesian Coordinates#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]], applied in each variable, the left side is $(ab/4)\,C_{n',m'}$. For constant $V_0$ the integral factorizes into two one-dimensional integrals, each $2a/n\pi$ (or $2b/m\pi$) for odd index and $0$ for even.
