*Sources: Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (4th ed.), §3.1.*

Coulomb's integral gives the potential only when the charge is known everywhere. On conductors it is not: it moves until the conductor is an equipotential, so the potential has to be found from Poisson's or Laplace's equation ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]) together with conditions on the boundary. This section asks what solutions of Laplace's equation look like and which boundary data fix them. Level A used the answers without proof: a conductor is an equipotential whose empty cavity is field-free ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]], [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-5|Theorem §A3.5.5]]), a conductor may replace an equipotential surface ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^rem-a3-5-4|§A3.5, Remark: A conductor can replace an equipotential]]), and a symmetric charge distribution has a field of the same symmetry ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^rem-a2-3-1|§A2.3, Remark: Strategy]]). Here these become theorems. Harmonic functions have the mean-value property and no interior extrema, and the potential is fixed by its values on the boundary or, for conductors, by their total charges.

## Laplace's equation and harmonic functions

> [!definition] Definition §B3.1.1: Harmonic Function; Boundary-Value Problem
> Let $\mathcal V$ be a connected open region of space with boundary surface $\mathcal S$. The boundary may consist of several closed surfaces (the surfaces of "islands" inside $\mathcal V$), and its outer part may be at infinity. In $\mathcal V$ the potential obeys Poisson's equation, and where there is no charge Laplace's equation (Griffiths eqs. (3.3)–(3.5); [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]):
>
> $$
> \nabla^2V = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \nabla^2V = \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial z^2} = 0 .
> $$
>
> A twice continuously differentiable solution of Laplace's equation is a **harmonic function**. A **boundary-value problem** asks for $V$ in $\mathcal V$, given $\rho$ in $\mathcal V$ and data on $\mathcal S$. The data are of **Dirichlet type** if $V$ is given on $\mathcal S$, and of **Neumann type** if the normal derivative $\partial V/\partial n = \hat n\cdot\nabla V = -E_n$ is given there. Where $\mathcal S$ is the surface of a conductor, often only the conductor's total charge is known ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1.1, eqs. (3.3)–(3.5); §3.1.5; Problem 3.5*

^def-b3-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Laplace's equation is an averaging instruction
> - *One dimension.* $V'' = 0$ gives $V = mx + b$ (Griffiths eq. (3.6)): two constants, fixed by two boundary conditions. The line satisfies $V(x) = \tfrac12[V(x + a) + V(x - a)]$ and has no local maximum or minimum; its extreme values sit at the ends.
> - *Two dimensions.* A rubber membrane stretched over a warped frame has a height $V(x, y)$ that obeys Laplace's equation as long as its slopes are small (Griffiths, footnote 1, gives the exact equation). Its value at a point is the average over any circle around the point, $V(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi R}\oint V\,dl$, and it has no hills and no hollows: a ping-pong ball placed on it rolls off the edge. The equation has no "general solution" with finitely many constants; the boundary curve, a whole function, is what fixes the shape.
> - *Relaxation.* The averaging property is the basis of the simplest numerical method: assign values on the boundary and guesses on a grid inside, replace each interior value by the average of its neighbours, and repeat until nothing changes.

^rem-b3-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.1: Mean-Value Property
> If $V$ is harmonic in a region that contains a closed ball of radius $R$ about the point $\vec r$, then $V(\vec r)$ is the average of $V$ over the sphere bounding the ball:
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi R^2}\oint_{\text{sphere}} V\,da .
> $$
>
> In electrostatic language: the average over a sphere of the potential produced by charges outside it equals the potential they produce at its centre.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1.4; Problem 3.37*

^thm-b3-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> This is Griffiths' Problem 3.37, which uses only Laplace's equation. Put the origin at $\vec r$ and let $V_{\text{ave}}(s) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int V(s\hat n)\,d\Omega$ be the average over the sphere of radius $s \le R$, written with the solid angle $d\Omega = \sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi$. Since $V$ is continuously differentiable, we may differentiate under the integral sign:
>
> $$
> \frac{dV_{\text{ave}}}{ds} = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int\hat n\cdot\nabla V(s\hat n)\,d\Omega = \frac{1}{4\pi s^2}\oint_{|\vec r| = s}\nabla V\cdot d\vec a = \frac{1}{4\pi s^2}\int_{|\vec r| \le s}\nabla^2V\,d\tau = 0 ,
> $$
>
> by the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]). So $V_{\text{ave}}$ is constant on $(0, R]$. As $s \to 0$ the sphere shrinks to the centre and $V_{\text{ave}}(s) \to V(\vec 0)$ by continuity. Hence $V_{\text{ave}}(R) = V(\vec 0)$.

^der-b3-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]], [[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The same from Coulomb's law
> Griffiths' own proof starts from a single point charge $q$ on the $z$ axis at distance $z > R$ from the centre. The distance from $q$ to a point of the sphere is $\mathcal R = \sqrt{z^2 + R^2 - 2zR\cos\theta}$, and
>
> $$
> V_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{4\pi R^2}\,\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi}{\sqrt{z^2 + R^2 - 2zR\cos\theta}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{2zR}\Bigl[\sqrt{z^2 + R^2 - 2zR\cos\theta}\Bigr]_0^\pi = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{(z + R) - (z - R)}{2zR} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{z} ,
> $$
>
> the potential of $q$ at the centre; superposition does the rest. For a charge *inside* ($z < R$) the bracket is $(z + R) - (R - z)$ instead, and $V_{\text{ave}} = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0R$. So in general $V_{\text{ave}} = V_{\text{centre}} + Q_{\text{enc}}/4\pi\varepsilon_0R$, where $V_{\text{centre}}$ is due to the charges outside (Griffiths Problem 3.1).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1.4, p. 118; Problem 3.1*

^rem-b3-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.2: No Local Extrema
> Let $V$ be harmonic in a bounded connected region $\mathcal V$ and continuous up to its boundary $\mathcal S$. Then $V$ takes its maximum and its minimum on $\mathcal S$. If it reaches its maximum or minimum at a point inside $\mathcal V$, it is constant.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1.4, item 2*

^thm-b3-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $M$ be the maximum of $V$ on the closed bounded set $\mathcal V \cup \mathcal S$ (it exists because $V$ is continuous there), and suppose $V(\vec r_0) = M$ at an interior point. For every sphere about $\vec r_0$ whose ball lies in $\mathcal V$, $V \le M$ on the sphere while its average is $M$ ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]). A continuous function that never exceeds its average must equal it everywhere, so $V = M$ on every such sphere, hence on a whole ball about $\vec r_0$. The set where $V = M$ is therefore open; it is also closed in $\mathcal V$ by continuity, and not empty. Since $\mathcal V$ is connected ([[§13 Connected Spaces#^def-13-new1|590 Def. §13.1]]), it is all of $\mathcal V$. Applying the argument to $-V$ handles the minimum. Griffiths' shorter version: at a strict local maximum, all values on a small sphere around the point would be smaller than at its centre, and so would their average.

^der-b3-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]], [[§13 Connected Spaces#^def-13-new1|590 Def. §13.1]]

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Earnshaw's theorem
> A charged particle cannot be held in stable equilibrium by electrostatic forces alone (Griffiths Problem 3.2). For a positive test charge, stability would need a strict local minimum of the potential produced by the other charges, at a point where those charges are absent, so the potential is harmonic there; [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]] forbids it (for a negative charge, a maximum). Griffiths' example is a cube with equal charges $q$ at its corners: a positive charge at the centre seems trapped, but the potential at the centre is only a saddle, and the charge escapes through the middle of a face. Electrostatic "bottles" for a hot plasma are therefore impossible; magnetic confinement is used instead.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 3.2, Figure 3.4*

## The uniqueness theorems

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.3: First Uniqueness Theorem
> 1. A solution of Laplace's equation in $\mathcal V$ is uniquely determined by its values on the boundary $\mathcal S$.
> 2. *(Corollary.)* The potential in $\mathcal V$ is uniquely determined by the charge density $\rho$ throughout $\mathcal V$ and the value of $V$ on all boundaries.
> 3. *(Mixed data.)* If on each part of $\mathcal S$ either $V$ or $\partial V/\partial n$ is given, and $\rho$ is given in $\mathcal V$, then $\vec E$ is uniquely determined; if $V$ itself is given somewhere on $\mathcal S$, so is $V$.
>
> If the outer boundary is at infinity, the boundary condition there is part of the data (usually $V \to 0$ for a localized distribution).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1.5, eq. (3.3); Problems 3.5–3.6*

^thm-b3-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1 and 2.* Let $V_1$ and $V_2$ both satisfy $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$ in $\mathcal V$ (with $\rho = 0$ for Laplace's equation) and take the given values on $\mathcal S$. The difference $V_3 = V_1 - V_2$ satisfies $\nabla^2V_3 = -\rho/\varepsilon_0 + \rho/\varepsilon_0 = 0$ and vanishes on $\mathcal S$. By [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]] its maximum and minimum are both $0$, so $V_3 \equiv 0$. If $\mathcal V$ extends to infinity with $V_1, V_2 \to 0$ there, apply the theorem to the part of $\mathcal V$ inside a large sphere: on that sphere $\lvert V_3\rvert < \epsilon$ once the radius is large enough, so $\lvert V_3\rvert < \epsilon$ inside, for every $\epsilon > 0$.
>
> *3.* An energy argument (Griffiths Problem 3.5; his Problem 3.6 applies the same identity to the second theorem). With $V_3$ as before, product rule (iii) of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]] gives $\nabla\cdot(V_3\nabla V_3) = \lvert\nabla V_3\rvert^2 + V_3\nabla^2V_3 = \lvert\nabla V_3\rvert^2$, and the divergence theorem turns this into Green's first identity ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-2|452 Thm. §17.2]]):
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V}\lvert\nabla V_3\rvert^2\,d\tau = \oint_{\mathcal S}V_3\,\frac{\partial V_3}{\partial n}\,da = 0 ,
> $$
>
> because on each part of $\mathcal S$ either $V_3 = 0$ or $\partial V_3/\partial n = 0$. (If $\mathcal V$ extends to infinity, take $\mathcal S$ to include a large sphere of radius $r$: with $V_3 \to 0$ like $1/r$ and $\partial V_3/\partial n$ like $1/r^2$, its contribution falls like $1/r$ and vanishes in the limit.) The integrand is continuous and non-negative, so $\vec E_3 = -\nabla V_3 = \vec 0$ throughout $\mathcal V$, and $V_3$ is constant; it is zero if it vanishes anywhere on $\mathcal S$. (This argument also proves part 1.)

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-2|452 Thm. §17.2]]

> [!example] Example §B3.1.1: The Field in an Empty Cavity Is Zero
> A cavity inside a conductor contains no charge. Its wall belongs to the conductor and is therefore at one potential $V_0$ ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]). Inside, $V$ satisfies Laplace's equation with the value $V_0$ on the whole boundary. The constant $V = V_0$ is one such solution, and by [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]] it is the only one. So $\vec E = \vec 0$ in the cavity, whatever charges lie outside the conductor, and the wall carries no surface charge at any point, since $\sigma = \varepsilon_0E_n = 0$ just off it ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-4|Theorem §A2.4.4]]).
>
> This is [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-5|Theorem §A3.5.5]], now with a complete proof. Level A followed a field line through the cavity and needed it to reach the wall; the uniqueness theorem needs no picture of the field at all.
>
> The shielding also works the other way. If the cavity holds a charge $q$ and the conductor carries $Q$, the outer surface carries $Q + q$ ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-5|Theorem §A2.4.5]]). Outside the conductor the field is fixed by the second uniqueness theorem ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]] below, applied to the region outside the outer surface): its derivation uses only the flux $(Q + q)/\varepsilon_0$ through a surface enclosing the conductor, and the given outside charges, not where $q$ sits in the cavity. So neither the outside field nor the outer surface charge $\sigma = \varepsilon_0E_n$ depends on the arrangement inside, which is the "full proof" level A deferred.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 3.1*

^ex-b3-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Uniqueness is a licence to guess
> It does not matter how a solution was found: if it satisfies Poisson's equation in the region and takes the right boundary values, it is *the* solution (Griffiths). Three uses:
> - *Images.* A fictitious charge outside the region can be chosen so that the boundary values come out right ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images]]).
> - *Symmetry.* Suppose $\rho$, the boundaries and the boundary data are unchanged by a rotation, reflection or translation $T$. If $V$ is a solution, then $V\circ T^{-1}$ satisfies the same equation and the same boundary data, so it equals $V$: the potential, and with it the field, has the symmetry of the problem. This is what level A took for granted when it decided the direction of $\vec E$ from the symmetry of the charge before applying Gauss's law ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^rem-a2-3-1|§A2.3, Remark: Strategy]], step 1). The data at infinity must share the symmetry too.
> - *Replacing an equipotential by a conductor.* Outside a closed equipotential surface $S_0$ the potential solves Poisson's equation with the value $V_0$ on $S_0$ and $0$ at infinity. Filling the inside of $S_0$ with a conductor at potential $V_0$ leaves these data unchanged, so it leaves the outside field unchanged: the uniqueness promised in [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^rem-a3-5-4|§A3.5, Remark: A conductor can replace an equipotential]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1.5, p. 120*

^rem-b3-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.4: Second Uniqueness Theorem
> In a region $\mathcal V$ surrounded by conductors and containing a specified charge density $\rho$, the electric field is uniquely determined if the total charge on each conductor is given. The region as a whole may be bounded by another conductor or be unbounded.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1.6, eqs. (3.7)–(3.8), Figure 3.6*

^thm-b3-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Suppose $\vec E_1$ and $\vec E_2$ both satisfy the conditions. Both obey Gauss's law in differential form, $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, between the conductors ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]), and in integral form over a surface enclosing the $i$th conductor, $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec a = Q_i/\varepsilon_0$, and over the outer boundary, $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec a = Q_{\text{tot}}/\varepsilon_0$. Their difference $\vec E_3 = \vec E_1 - \vec E_2$ therefore satisfies
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec E_3 = 0 \ \text{ in } \mathcal V \quad (3.7), \qquad \oint\vec E_3\cdot d\vec a = 0 \ \text{ over each boundary surface} \quad (3.8).
> $$
>
> Each conductor is an equipotential for both fields ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]), so $V_3 = V_1 - V_2$ is constant on each conducting surface. The constants may differ from surface to surface and need not be zero, since the conductors' potentials are not given. With $\vec E_3 = -\nabla V_3$ and product rule (iii) of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]],
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot(V_3\vec E_3) = V_3\,(\nabla\cdot\vec E_3) + \vec E_3\cdot\nabla V_3 = -E_3^2 .
> $$
>
> Integrate over $\mathcal V$ and apply the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]):
>
> $$
> \oint_{\mathcal S}V_3\,\vec E_3\cdot d\vec a = -\int_{\mathcal V}E_3^2\,d\tau .
> $$
>
> On each conductor $V_3$ is a constant and comes out of the integral, leaving $V_3\oint\vec E_3\cdot d\vec a = 0$ by (3.8); if the outer boundary is at infinity, $V_3 \to 0$ there and $V_3E_3$ falls faster than $1/r^2$, so a large sphere contributes nothing. Hence $\int E_3^2\,d\tau = 0$, and since the integrand is never negative, $\vec E_3 = \vec 0$: $\vec E_1 = \vec E_2$.

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]

> [!example] Example §B3.1.2: Purcell's Four Conductors
> Four small conductors carry charges $+Q, -Q, +Q, -Q$ and are placed in two pairs, each positive charge close to a negative one, the two pairs facing each other. The arrangement looks comfortable: every charge sits next to an opposite charge. Now join the conductors in pairs by thin wires, so that each joined pair consists of a $+Q$ conductor of one pair and the $-Q$ conductor of the other. Will anything happen?
>
> After the joining there are effectively two conductors, each with total charge zero. One possible state is no charge anywhere and no field anywhere; it has the right total charges, so by [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]] it is the only one. The charge runs through the wires and cancels, although every charge had an opposite partner close by. The second uniqueness theorem is not obvious: intuition about where charges "like to be" fails here.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1.6, Figures 3.7–3.9 (after Purcell)*

^ex-b3-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: What the theorems do not say
> - *Existence.* They show that there is at most one solution. That a solution exists is clear on physical grounds; the mathematical proof is much harder, and Griffiths does not give it.
> - *Too much or too little data.* In one dimension, the value at one end alone is too little, and the derivatives at both ends are either redundant or inconsistent (Griffiths §3.1.5). Likewise, Neumann data everywhere fix $V$ only up to a constant, and prescribing both $V$ and $\partial V/\partial n$ on the same closed surface is in general inconsistent.
> - *Infinity is a boundary.* For charges in an applied uniform field $V$ does not go to $0$; the condition $V \to -E_0z + C$ replaces it ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]]).
> - *The second theorem does not give the potentials.* The conductors' potentials come out of the solution; only the charges are input. What it does fix is the charge distribution on every conductor, $\sigma = \varepsilon_0E_n$: for given total charges there is exactly one equilibrium arrangement. This is the uniqueness that level A assumed when it scaled all charges by $\lambda$ to show that capacitance depends only on geometry ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]]).
