[[§A1.1 Electric Charge]] said that like charges repel, unlike charges attract, and the force weakens with distance. This section makes that quantitative. Coulomb's law gives the force between two point charges, and the superposition principle gives the force exerted by any number of them. Together the two principles contain all of electrostatics. Everything in Chapters A1–A3 that is not a definition is derived from them.

## Point charges and electrostatics

> [!model] Model §A1.2.1: Point Charge
> A charged body is treated as a **point charge**, with all of its charge $q$ at a single point, when its size is negligible compared with its distances to the other charges considered.
>
> - *Assumes:* sizes $\ll$ separations; electrons and protons are the natural examples.
> - *Fails:* when the size is comparable to the distance, and the way the charge is spread matters. Then the body is cut into pieces that are point charges ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions]]). A spherically symmetric body acts as a point charge at its centre at every outside point ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.3*

^mod-a1-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.2.1: Source Charges, Test Charge; Electrostatics
> When the force on one charge $Q$ due to others $q_1, \dots, q_N$ is wanted, the $q_i$ are called **source charges** and $Q$ the **test charge**. The labels only organize the calculation; physically the two are the same kind of thing. **Electrostatics** is the study of forces and fields of source charges held fixed in place, so that their positions do not change in time. The test charge may move.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.3*

^def-a1-2-1

## Coulomb's law

> [!principle] Principle §A1.2.2: Coulomb's Law
> Two point charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ a distance $r$ apart exert forces on each other along the line joining them. The force exerted by $q_1$ on $q_2$ is
>
> $$
> \vec F_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}\,\hat r_{12},
> \qquad
> F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\lvert q_1 q_2\rvert}{r^2},
> $$
>
> where $\hat r_{12}$ is the unit vector pointing from $q_1$ toward $q_2$. If $q_1 q_2 > 0$ (like charges) the force points away from $q_1$, a repulsion. If $q_1 q_2 < 0$ (unlike charges) it points toward $q_1$, an attraction. The force on $q_1$ is $\vec F_{12} = -\vec F_{21}$. The constant $\varepsilon_0$ is the **permittivity of vacuum**, and $k$ is **Coulomb's constant**:
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \frac{\mathrm{C^2}}{\mathrm{N\cdot m^2}},
> \qquad
> k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99 \times 10^{9}\ \frac{\mathrm{N\cdot m^2}}{\mathrm{C^2}} .
> $$
>
> *Domain: point charges ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^mod-a1-2-1|Model §A1.2.1]]) at rest, in vacuum. Inside a material the force is changed by the material's own charges ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics|§A4.4]]). Moving charges also exert magnetic forces ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]]). The exponent 2 has been tested to better than one part in $10^{15}$ ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^rem-a2-4-3|§A2.4]]).*
> *Source: UP2 §5.3, eq. (5.1)*

^pr-a1-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the law
> - The law has the form of Newton's law of gravitation ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]]), with $k q_1 q_2$ in place of $G m_1 m_2$. The differences are that charge has two signs, so the force can repel, and that the force does not depend on the masses at all.
> - The two forces form a third-law pair ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|CM Principle §A3.5.1]]): equal magnitudes and opposite directions, even for very unequal charges.
> - The force depends on the separation, so it changes as the charges move. Newton's second law with a Coulomb force is often hard to solve directly, and energy methods are usually easier ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy]]).
> - At this level $\varepsilon_0$ is just an empirical constant. The factor $4\pi$ looks odd here, but it makes Gauss's law simple ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]).
> - The coulomb is a very large charge. Two charges of $1\ \mathrm C$ at $1\ \mathrm m$ repel with $9 \times 10^9\ \mathrm N$, roughly the weight of a million tonnes.

^rem-a1-2-1

> [!example] Example §A1.2.1: Electric versus Gravitational Attraction in Hydrogen
> In the ground state of hydrogen the electron's most probable distance from the proton is $r = 5.29 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm m$. Treat both as point charges. The electric attraction has magnitude
>
> $$
> F_e = \frac{k e^2}{r^2} = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^9\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C^2})(1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm C)^2}{(5.29 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm m)^2} = 8.24 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm N ,
> $$
>
> and the force on the electron points toward the proton. With $m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$, the gravitational attraction is $F_g = G m_p m_e / r^2 = 3.63 \times 10^{-47}\ \mathrm N$. The ratio
>
> $$
> \frac{F_e}{F_g} = \frac{k e^2}{G m_p m_e} = 2.27 \times 10^{39}
> $$
>
> does not depend on $r$. Gravity plays no part in atoms. It dominates on large scales only because matter is almost exactly neutral ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^ex-a1-1-1|Example §A1.1.1]]), while mass has only one sign and never cancels.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 5.1; PHY 182, TBL 1*

^ex-a1-2-1

## Superposition

> [!principle] Principle §A1.2.3: Superposition of Electric Forces
> The force that a set of point charges exerts on a test charge is the vector sum of the forces each would exert alone. Each force is computed by Coulomb's law as if the other charges were absent. For source charges $q_1, \dots, q_N$ and a test charge $Q$,
>
> $$
> \vec F = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \vec F_i = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{q_i}{r_i^2}\,\hat r_i ,
> $$
>
> where $r_i$ is the distance from $q_i$ to $Q$ and $\hat r_i$ is the unit vector pointing from $q_i$ toward $Q$.
>
> *Domain: as for Coulomb's law; an experimental fact.*
> *Source: UP2 §5.3, eq. (5.2)*

^pr-a1-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Why the sources are held fixed
> By the third law the test charge pushes back on every source charge. Left free, the sources would move, which would change the force on the test charge, which would change its motion, and so on. Electrostatics ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^def-a1-2-1|Def. §A1.2.1]]) avoids this by assuming that the sources are fixed in place somehow, for example glued down or held by much larger forces.

^rem-a1-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: Signs and directions
> Either use the signed vector form of [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|Principle §A1.2.2]] consistently, with $\hat r$ pointing from the source to the charge acted on, or do the following, which is safer by hand. Compute each magnitude from $\lvert q_1 q_2\rvert$, find each direction from "like repel, unlike attract", and only then split the forces into components. Do not do both at once: putting the negative sign of a charge into a magnitude and then also choosing the direction by attraction reverses the force.

^cau-a1-2-1

> [!example] Example §A1.2.2: Net Force from Two Source Charges
> Three small charged objects are fixed in place except the middle one: $q_2 = -3e$ sits at the origin, $q_1 = +2e$ a distance $d = 2.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm m$ directly above it, and $q_3 = -5e$ a distance $2d$ to its right. Find the net force on $q_2$.
>
> Use magnitudes and decide the directions physically. $q_1$ and $q_2$ are unlike, so $\vec F_{21}$ (the force on $q_2$ exerted by $q_1$) points up, toward $q_1$. $q_3$ and $q_2$ are alike, so $\vec F_{23}$ points left, away from $q_3$:
>
> $$
> F_{21} = \frac{k (3e)(2e)}{d^2} = 3.46 \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm N,
> \qquad
> F_{23} = \frac{k (3e)(5e)}{(2d)^2} = 2.16 \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm N .
> $$
>
> By superposition, $\vec F = (-2.16\,\hat\imath + 3.46\,\hat\jmath) \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm N$, of magnitude $\sqrt{2.16^2 + 3.46^2} \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm N = 4.08 \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm N$, at $\tan^{-1}(3.46/2.16) = 58^\circ$ above the $-x$ axis. The farther charge $q_3$ is larger, but at twice the distance its force is weaker.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 5.2*

> [!example] Example §A1.2.3: Charges on a Frictionless Ramp
> A charge $+Q$ is glued to a ramp inclined at $\theta$. A small block of mass $m$ and charge $+q$, further up the ramp, slides without friction. At what separation $r$ is the block in equilibrium?
>
> The block feels its weight $m\vec g$, the normal force, and the Coulomb repulsion of magnitude $kQq/r^2$, which points up the ramp. Along the ramp, with the normal force perpendicular to it, equilibrium ($\sum F_x = 0$ in [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^thm-a3-3-2|CM Theorem §A3.3.2]]) requires
>
> $$
> \frac{kQq}{r^2} = mg\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \sqrt{\frac{kQq}{mg\sin\theta}} .
> $$
>
> For $\theta = 30^\circ$, $m = 10\ \mathrm g$, $q = 1.0\ \mu\mathrm C$ and $Q = 10\ \mu\mathrm C$:
>
> $$
> r = \sqrt{\frac{(8.99 \times 10^9)(1.0 \times 10^{-5})(1.0 \times 10^{-6})}{(0.010)(9.80)(0.50)}}\ \mathrm m = 1.35\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> Doubling $Q$ multiplies $r$ by $\sqrt 2$, giving $1.92\ \mathrm m$. The Coulomb force enters the free-body diagram like any other force.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 3*

^ex-a1-2-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Coulomb's law and Newton's law of gravitation have the same inverse-square form. That is why Gauss's law ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law]]) has a gravitational twin, the shell theorem ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|CM Theorem §A8.1.5]]).
> - At the upper level, Coulomb's law with superposition is again the starting point of electrostatics, written with the separation vector — [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^rem-b2-1-1|Remark: Coulomb's law and superposition at level B]]; the field of a continuous distribution as one integral — [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]].
