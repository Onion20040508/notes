Electromagnetism starts from a property of matter that gravity does not see: electric charge. This section collects what is known about charge before any force law is written down. Charge comes in two kinds, in whole multiples of a smallest unit, and it is never created or destroyed. It is carried by electrons and protons, and it moves freely in some materials and not in others. The force law itself is the subject of [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law]].

## Two kinds of charge

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.1: Electric Charge
> **Electric charge** is a property of matter that makes bodies exert forces on each other without contact. It comes in two kinds, called **positive** and **negative**. Bodies carrying the same kind of charge repel; bodies carrying opposite kinds attract. The force weakens rapidly with distance: doubling the separation reduces it to one quarter.
>
> Charge is a scalar. Its SI unit is the **coulomb** (C). The **net charge** $q$ of a body is the algebraic sum of its charges, positive and negative counted with their signs.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.1*

^def-a1-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: The names are a convention
> - Franklin called an excess of his "electrical fluid" positive. The charges that actually move in metals turned out to be the ones he had called negative, the electrons. Nothing depends on the choice, as long as it is used consistently.
> - The two kinds are perfectly symmetric. If every positive charge were swapped for a negative one and vice versa, every electric force would stay the same, because it depends only on whether two charges are alike or unlike.
> - Compare gravity ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]]). Mass comes in one kind only and always attracts. Charge comes in two kinds and can attract or repel.
> - "A $3\ \mu\mathrm C$ charge" is shorthand for "a particle carrying a charge of $3\ \mu\mathrm C$". A charge is a property of a particle, not a particle.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 1 slides (the symmetry)*

^rem-a1-1-1

## Quantization and conservation

> [!principle] Principle §A1.1.1: Quantization of Charge
> Charge comes in discrete amounts. Every free particle, and therefore every body, carries a charge that is an integer multiple of the **elementary charge**
>
> $$
> e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm C, \qquad q = n e, \quad n \in \mathbb Z .
> $$
>
> The proton carries $+e$ and the electron $-e$. The two magnitudes are exactly equal.
>
> *Domain: free particles and all bodies made of them.*
> *Source: UP2 §5.1*

^pr-a1-1-1

> [!principle] Principle §A1.1.2: Conservation of Charge
> The net charge of a closed system is constant. Charge is neither created nor destroyed. It can only be transferred from one body, or one place, to another. The bookkeeping is local: if the charge in a region changes, the difference has flowed in or out across its boundary.
>
> *Domain: every experiment done so far; no violation is known.*
> *Source: UP2 §5.1; PHY 182, week 1 slides*

^pr-a1-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Charging is transfer
> - Rubbing amber with fur moves electrons from the fur to the amber. The amber becomes negative and the fur positive, with charges of equal magnitude, so the total stays zero. The same holds for a cat and a block of styrofoam, or for a thundercloud, the air and the ground.
> - When people say two charges "cancel", they mean that equal and opposite charges close together exert forces on a third charge that cancel. The charges themselves do not disappear.

^rem-a1-1-2

## Charge in matter

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.2: Electron, Proton, Neutron; Ion
> An atom has a small, massive **nucleus** of **protons** (charge $+e$) and **neutrons** (charge $0$, nearly the proton's mass). The nucleus is surrounded by a cloud of **electrons** (charge $-e$, about $1/1837$ of the proton's mass). A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons. An **ion** is an atom or molecule with more electrons than protons (a negative ion) or fewer (a positive ion).
>
> The light electrons can be added or removed fairly easily, while the protons stay bound in the nuclei. A macroscopic body is therefore charged because electrons have been added to it or taken from it.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.1*

^def-a1-1-2

> [!example] Example §A1.1.1: How Much Charge Is Hidden in Water
> How much charge do the electrons in one liter of water carry? Water has molar mass $18.0\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, and each $\mathrm{H_2O}$ molecule has $8 + 1 + 1 = 10$ electrons.
>
> $$
> N_{\text{molecules}} = \frac{1000\ \mathrm g}{18.0\ \mathrm{g/mol}} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}} = 3.34 \times 10^{25},
> \qquad
> Q_{\text{electrons}} = -10\,N_{\text{molecules}}\, e = -5.36 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm C .
> $$
>
> The protons carry $+5.36 \times 10^7\ \mathrm C$. With just 1% more electrons than protons, the water would carry $-5.36 \times 10^5\ \mathrm C$.
>
> In practice even a "highly charged" body carries only about $10^{-6}\ \mathrm C$. For a liter of water that is a fraction $10^{-6}/(5.36 \times 10^7) \approx 2 \times 10^{-14}$ of its electrons. Ordinary matter contains enormous amounts of charge, but positive and negative are paired almost perfectly. [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^ex-a1-2-1|Example §A1.2.1]] shows why: even one unpaired coulomb would exert huge forces.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 1*

^ex-a1-1-1

## Conductors and insulators

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.3: Conductor; Insulator
> In a **conductor** some electrons are only loosely bound to their atoms. These **conduction electrons** (one or two per atom in metals such as copper) wander freely through the material, so charge flows through it easily. An **insulator** has no conduction electrons. Charge flows through it only with great difficulty, so excess charge placed on an insulator stays where it was put for a long time. Most metals are conductors. Amber, fur, glass, wood and plastic are insulators.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.2*

^def-a1-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Why rubbed metals seemed uncharged
> Early experimenters could charge amber and gemstones by rubbing, but never a metal held in the hand. Excess charge on a conductor does not stay put: the like charges repel, and they spread away through the metal and the experimenter's body. Insulators hold charge in place, so their forces last. What happens to charge that stays on an isolated conductor is worked out in [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium]]. Conduction itself is the subject of [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals|§A5.2]].

^rem-a1-1-3

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.4: Polarization; Grounding
> - **Polarization** is a separation of positive and negative charge inside a body, or a molecule, that stays neutral overall. A nearby charge induces it by pulling unlike charge closer and pushing like charge away. Some molecules, such as water, are **polar**: their charges are separated even without an outside charge.
> - **Grounding** a conductor means connecting it to Earth by a conducting wire. Earth is a huge conductor and can supply or absorb practically any amount of charge.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.2*

^def-a1-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: Charged bodies attract neutral ones
> A charged comb picks up neutral bits of paper, and it does so whichever sign of charge it carries. The comb polarizes the paper's molecules, with unlike charge brought slightly nearer and like charge pushed slightly farther. The electric force decreases with distance, so the attraction of the near charges beats the repulsion of the far ones. A conductor is polarized the same way, more strongly, because its charges are free to move. The quantitative version is the force on a dipole in a nonuniform field ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^ex-a1-5-1|Example §A1.5.1]]).

^rem-a1-1-4

> [!example] Example §A1.1.2: Charging by Induction
> A neutral metal sphere on an insulating stand can be charged without touching it with a charged body:
> 1. Bring a positively charged rod near the sphere. Conduction electrons are attracted to the near side, which leaves the far side positive. The sphere is polarized but still neutral.
> 2. Ground the sphere. Electrons from Earth, attracted by the rod, flow up the wire and cancel the positive charge on the far side.
> 3. Break the ground connection while the rod is still there.
> 4. Remove the rod. The extra electrons stay on the sphere and spread over its surface.
>
> The sphere ends up with a charge opposite to the rod's. The rod never touched it and loses none of its own charge. The total charge of sphere and Earth is unchanged ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]]). The order matters: if the rod is removed before the ground connection is broken, the electrons flow back to Earth and the sphere is left neutral.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.2 (Figure 5.13)*

^ex-a1-1-2
