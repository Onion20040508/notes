[[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^thm-a1-3-1|Theorem §A1.3.1]] gives the field of a few point charges as a sum. Charged rods, rings, disks and sheets carry so many elementary charges that they are better described by a smooth charge density, and then the sum becomes an integral. This section introduces no new physics, only a calculation: Coulomb's law with superposition, for charge spread along lines, over surfaces and through volumes. Its results (line, ring, disk, plane) are used throughout the rest of the subject.

## Charge densities and the field integral

> [!definition] Definition §A1.4.1: Charge Densities
> For charge spread continuously,
> - the **linear charge density** $\lambda$ is the charge per unit length (C/m), with $dq = \lambda\,dl$ on a length element $dl$;
> - the **surface charge density** $\sigma$ is the charge per unit area (C/m²), with $dq = \sigma\,dA$;
> - the **volume charge density** $\rho$ is the charge per unit volume (C/m³), with $dq = \rho\,dV$.
>
> The densities may vary with position. The total charge is $Q = \int dq$. For example, a rod along $0 \le x \le L$ with $\lambda = Ax$ carries $Q = \int_0^L Ax\,dx = AL^2/2$. A sphere of radius $R$ with $\rho = \rho(r)$, cut into shells of volume $4\pi r^2\,dr$, carries $Q = \int_0^R \rho(r)\,4\pi r^2\,dr$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.5; PHY 182, week 3 slides (the examples)*

^def-a1-4-1

> [!model] Model §A1.4.1: Continuous Charge Distribution
> A body made of a huge number of elementary charges is described by a charge density, as if the charge were smeared out continuously.
>
> - *Assumes:* the number of elementary charges is enormous, and the field is wanted at distances much larger than their spacing. This is the same approximation as treating water as a continuous fluid rather than as molecules.
> - *Fails:* at atomic distances, where the quantization of charge ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-1|Principle §A1.1.1]]) shows.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.5*

^mod-a1-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.2: Field of a Continuous Distribution
> A continuous charge distribution produces at a point $P$ the field
>
> $$
> \vec E(P) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int \frac{dq}{r^2}\,\hat r ,
> \qquad dq = \lambda\,dl,\ \ \sigma\,dA\ \text{ or }\ \rho\,dV ,
> $$
>
> where $r$ is the distance from the element $dq$ to $P$ and $\hat r$ the unit vector from $dq$ toward $P$. Both vary over the distribution. The vector integral stands for three ordinary integrals, one for each component, for example $E_x = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int \frac{dq}{r^2}\,(\hat r\cdot\hat\imath)$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.5, eqs. (5.8)–(5.11)*

^thm-a1-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Cut the distribution into small pieces of charge $\Delta q_j$ (equal to $\lambda\,\Delta l_j$, $\sigma\,\Delta A_j$ or $\rho\,\Delta V_j$). Each piece is small compared with its distance to $P$, so it acts as a point charge. By [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^thm-a1-3-1|Theorem §A1.3.1]], $\vec E(P) \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_j \frac{\Delta q_j}{r_j^2}\hat r_j$. Each component of this is a Riemann sum. As the pieces shrink, the sums converge to the integrals.

^der-a1-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^thm-a1-3-1|Theorem §A1.3.1]], [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^def-a1-4-1|Def. §A1.4.1]], [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^mod-a1-4-1|Model §A1.4.1]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^def-15-11|452 Def. §15.11]] (the integral as a limit of sums)

> [!remark] Remark: Strategy
> 1. Pick an element $dq$ that fits the shape (a piece $dx$ of a rod, an arc $R\,d\theta$ of a ring, a ring $2\pi r'\,dr'$ of a disk), and write $dq$ in terms of the integration variable.
> 2. Write $r$, and the components of $\hat r$, in terms of the same variable. Keep the source coordinate ($x$, $r'$) distinct from the fixed field point ($z$).
> 3. Use symmetry *before* integrating to see which components cancel. Elements placed symmetrically about the field point have equal and opposite transverse components.
> 4. Integrate each surviving component.
> 5. Check limits. From far away every finite distribution must look like a point charge with the same total charge.

^rem-a1-4-1

> [!caution] Caution: Add components, not magnitudes
> The integral is a vector sum. Integrating $dE = k\,dq/r^2$ without the direction factors overestimates the field whenever contributions point in different directions ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^ex-a1-4-2|Example §A1.4.2]]). When pairing symmetric elements, integrate over half of the distribution only, or else every pair is counted twice.

^cau-a1-4-1

> [!example] Example §A1.4.1: On the Axis of a Charged Rod
> A rod lies along the $x$ axis from $x = a = 1.0\ \mathrm m$ to $x = b = 2.0\ \mathrm m$ with uniform density $\lambda = 0.10\ \mathrm{C/m}$. Find the field at the origin.
>
> The element $dq = \lambda\,dx$ at position $x$ is a distance $x$ from the origin, and its field there points along $-\hat\imath$, away from the rod. All contributions are parallel, so
>
> $$
> \vec E = -\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_a^b \frac{dx}{x^2}\,\hat\imath = -\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\frac1a - \frac1b\Bigr)\hat\imath = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q}{ab}\,\hat\imath ,
> $$
>
> with $Q = \lambda(b - a) = 0.10\ \mathrm C$. Numerically $E = (8.99 \times 10^9)(0.10)(1 - 0.5)\ \mathrm{N/C} = 4.5 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{N/C}$. Treating the whole rod as a point charge at its centre, $1.5\ \mathrm m$ away, would give $kQ/(1.5\ \mathrm m)^2 = 4.0 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{N/C}$, 11% too small. The $1/x^2$ weights the near end more heavily. The exact result is the point-charge formula with $r^2$ replaced by $ab$.
>
> With a non-uniform density $\lambda = Ax$, $A = 0.10\ \mathrm{C/m^2}$, the same steps give $Q = A(b^2 - a^2)/2 = 0.15\ \mathrm C$ and $E = kA\ln(b/a) = 6.2 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{N/C}$, again along $-\hat\imath$.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 4 and TBL 5*

^ex-a1-4-1

## Lines

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.3: Field of a Line Segment
> A straight segment of length $L$ with uniform density $\lambda$ produces, at distance $z$ from its midpoint along the perpendicular bisector, the field
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\lambda L}{z\sqrt{z^2 + L^2/4}}\,\hat k ,
> $$
>
> with $\hat k$ pointing away from the segment. For $z \gg L$ this tends to $\lambda L/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 z^2)$, the field of a point charge $q = \lambda L$.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 5.5, eq. (5.12)*

^thm-a1-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put the segment on the $x$ axis from $-L/2$ to $L/2$ and $P$ at height $z$. The element $dq = \lambda\,dx$ at $x$ is at distance $r = \sqrt{z^2 + x^2}$, and its field makes the angle $\theta$ with the $z$ axis, where $\cos\theta = z/r$. The elements at $x$ and $-x$ have equal and opposite $x$ components, so only $E_z$ survives:
>
> $$
> E_z = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{-L/2}^{L/2} \frac{\lambda\,dx}{z^2 + x^2}\,\frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2 + x^2}}
> = \frac{\lambda z}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\frac{x}{z^2\sqrt{z^2 + x^2}}\Bigr]_{-L/2}^{L/2}
> = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\lambda L}{z\sqrt{z^2 + L^2/4}} .
> $$
>
> The antiderivative can be checked by differentiating: $\frac{d}{dx}\frac{x}{z^2\sqrt{z^2+x^2}} = \frac{1}{(z^2+x^2)^{3/2}}$. For $z \gg L$ the root is $\approx z$, which gives the point-charge limit.

^der-a1-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-2|Theorem §A1.4.2]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]] (fundamental theorem of calculus)

> [!model] Model §A1.4.4: Infinite Line of Charge
> A long, thin, uniformly charged wire or rod is treated as an infinite straight line with uniform density $\lambda$.
>
> - *Assumes:* the distance from the line is much smaller than its length, and the field point is far from both ends; the thickness is negligible at that distance.
> - *Fails:* near an end, or at distances comparable to the length. There use [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-3|Theorem §A1.4.3]]; far away the wire looks like a point charge.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.5*

^mod-a1-4-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.5: Field of an Infinite Line
> An infinite line with uniform density $\lambda$ produces, at perpendicular distance $r$ from it, the field
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2\lambda}{r}\,\hat r = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\,\hat r ,
> $$
>
> where $\hat r$ points perpendicularly away from the line. The field falls off as $1/r$, not $1/r^2$.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 5.6, eq. (5.13)*

^thm-a1-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $L \to \infty$ in [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-3|Theorem §A1.4.3]]. Then $\sqrt{z^2 + L^2/4} \to L/2$, and $E \to \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\lambda L}{z\,L/2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2\lambda}{z}$. This is the same as integrating from $-\infty$ to $\infty$ directly, where the bracket in the derivation tends to $\pm 1/z^2$ at the two ends. On an infinite line every point is a midpoint, so the result holds at every point at distance $z = r$, with the direction perpendicular to the line.

^der-a1-4-5

*Uses:* [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-3|Theorem §A1.4.3]], [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^mod-a1-4-4|Model §A1.4.4]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-2|451 Def. §36.2]] (integral over the whole line)

> [!example] Example §A1.4.2: Centre of a Semicircular Arc
> A thin rod bent into a semicircle of radius $R$, lying in the upper half-plane ($0 \le \theta \le \pi$), carries uniform density $\lambda > 0$. Find the field at the centre.
>
> The arc element at angle $\theta$ has length $R\,d\theta$ and charge $dq = \lambda R\,d\theta$. It is a distance $R$ from the centre, and its field there points from the element toward the centre, along $-(\cos\theta\,\hat\imath + \sin\theta\,\hat\jmath)$, with magnitude $k\lambda\,d\theta/R$. So
>
> $$
> E_x = -\frac{k\lambda}{R}\int_0^\pi \cos\theta\,d\theta = 0, \qquad
> E_y = -\frac{k\lambda}{R}\int_0^\pi \sin\theta\,d\theta = -\frac{2k\lambda}{R},
> \qquad \vec E = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 R}\,\hat\jmath .
> $$
>
> The field points away from the middle of the arc. If the arc's charge $Q = \pi R\lambda$ were all at distance $R$ and all pushed in the same direction, the field would be $\pi k\lambda/R$. The true field is smaller by the factor $2/\pi$, because the contributions from the two halves partly cancel.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 4*

^ex-a1-4-2

## Rings, disks and planes

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.6: Field of a Ring on Its Axis
> A ring of radius $R$ with total charge $Q$ spread uniformly produces, at distance $z$ from its centre along its axis, the field
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q z}{\bigl(z^2 + R^2\bigr)^{3/2}}\,\hat k ,
> $$
>
> with $\hat k$ along the axis. For $z \gg R$ it becomes the field of a point charge $Q$. At the centre ($z = 0$) it vanishes.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 5.7*

^thm-a1-4-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The arc element between $\theta$ and $\theta + d\theta$ has charge $dq = \lambda R\,d\theta$, with $\lambda = Q/2\pi R$. Every element is the same distance $r = \sqrt{z^2 + R^2}$ from $P$. Diametrically opposite elements have equal charges, so their field components perpendicular to the axis cancel. The axial component of each contribution carries the factor $z/r$:
>
> $$
> E_z = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^{2\pi} \frac{\lambda R\,d\theta}{z^2 + R^2}\,\frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2 + R^2}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2\pi R\lambda\, z}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Qz}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}} .
> $$

^der-a1-4-6

*Uses:* [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-2|Theorem §A1.4.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.7: Field of a Disk on Its Axis
> A thin disk of radius $R$ with uniform surface density $\sigma$ produces, at height $z > 0$ above its centre, the field
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(1 - \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2 + R^2}}\Bigr)\hat k .
> $$
>
> For $z \gg R$ it tends to the field of a point charge $Q = \sigma\pi R^2$. For $z \ll R$ it tends to the constant $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 5.8, eq. (5.14)*

^thm-a1-4-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Cut the disk into rings of radius $r'$ and width $dr'$, of charge $dq = \sigma\,2\pi r'\,dr'$. Write $r'$ for the ring radius to keep it apart from the field point's height $z$. By [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-6|Theorem §A1.4.6]] each ring contributes an axial field $dE_z = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{z\,dq}{(z^2 + r'^2)^{3/2}}$. All the contributions are parallel, so
>
> $$
> E_z = \frac{\sigma z}{4\varepsilon_0}\int_0^R \frac{2r'\,dr'}{(z^2 + r'^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\sigma z}{4\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[-\frac{2}{\sqrt{z^2 + r'^2}}\Bigr]_0^R = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(1 - \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2 + R^2}}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> For $z \gg R$, the Taylor expansion $(1 + R^2/z^2)^{-1/2} = 1 - R^2/2z^2 + \dots$ gives $E_z \approx \frac{\sigma R^2}{4\varepsilon_0 z^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\sigma\pi R^2}{z^2}$.

^der-a1-4-7

*Uses:* [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-6|Theorem §A1.4.6]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-7|452 Thm. §15.7]] (polar coordinates, area element $r'\,dr'\,d\theta$), [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]] (Taylor's theorem)

> [!model] Model §A1.4.8: Infinite Plane of Charge
> A flat, uniformly charged sheet is treated as an infinite plane with uniform density $\sigma$.
>
> - *Assumes:* the distance from the sheet is much smaller than its extent, and the field point is far from the edges; the thickness is negligible.
> - *Fails:* near edges and at distances comparable to the size. The disk ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-7|Theorem §A1.4.7]]) shows the crossover to point-charge behaviour.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.5*

^mod-a1-4-8

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.9: Field of an Infinite Plane
> An infinite plane with uniform density $\sigma$ produces on each side the uniform field
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\,\hat n ,
> $$
>
> where $\hat n$ is the unit normal pointing away from the plane, toward the side where the field is evaluated. For $\sigma < 0$ the field points toward the plane. Its magnitude does not depend on the distance from the plane.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.5, eq. (5.15)*

^thm-a1-4-9

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $R \to \infty$ in [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-7|Theorem §A1.4.7]]: $z/\sqrt{z^2 + R^2} \to 0$ for every fixed $z$, which leaves $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ along $+\hat k$ above the plane. Every point of an infinite plane is a centre, so this holds everywhere above it. Below it, reflection through the plane reverses the normal component, so the field is $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ along $-\hat k$.

^der-a1-4-9

*Uses:* [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-7|Theorem §A1.4.7]], [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^mod-a1-4-8|Model §A1.4.8]]

> [!remark] Remark: $1/r^2$, $1/r$, constant
> A point charge's field falls as $1/r^2$, an infinite line's as $1/r$, and an infinite plane's not at all. Seen from above, an infinite plane looks the same at every height: it still fills the whole view. Moving away from it weakens each element's field as $1/r^2$, but the patch of plane seen within any fixed angle of the vertical grows in area as $z^2$, and the two effects cancel exactly. The constant field is an artefact of the infinite idealisation, and a very useful one. Gauss's law gives all three results in a few lines ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law]]).

^rem-a1-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.10: Field of Two Oppositely Charged Planes
> Two parallel infinite planes with densities $+\sigma$ and $-\sigma$ produce the uniform field
>
> $$
> E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\quad\text{between them, pointing from the positive to the negative plane,}\qquad E = 0\quad\text{outside.}
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 5.9*

^thm-a1-4-10

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-9|Theorem §A1.4.9]] each plane produces a field of magnitude $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ on both sides, pointing away from the positive plane and toward the negative one. Between the planes both fields point from $+$ to $-$ and add. Outside, they point in opposite directions and cancel. Superposition gives the total.

^der-a1-4-10

*Uses:* [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-9|Theorem §A1.4.9]], [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^thm-a1-3-1|Theorem §A1.3.1]] (fields add)

> [!remark] Remark: A source of uniform fields
> Two closely spaced, oppositely charged plates are the standard way to make a uniform field. This is the parallel-plate capacitor ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^mod-a4-1-2|Model §A4.1.2]]), as long as the gap is small compared with the plates' size.

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The integrals here are one-variable integrals after symmetry has removed the other components. Polar coordinates for the disk: [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-7|452 Thm. §15.7]]. The infinite line is an improper integral: [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-2|451 Def. §36.2]].
> - Every far-field check here is a first-order Taylor expansion ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]). Carried further, the expansion is the multipole expansion (Chapter B3, planned).
> - For the symmetric cases (line, plane, sphere) Gauss's law replaces the integral: [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law]].
