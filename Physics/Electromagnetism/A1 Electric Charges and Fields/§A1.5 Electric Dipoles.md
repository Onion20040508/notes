Equal and opposite charges close together, a dipole, make up the simplest neutral charge distribution and the most common one. Molecules such as water are dipoles, and any atom becomes one in a field. This section asks how a dipole responds to an external field: it feels a torque but no net force in a uniform field. It then asks what field the dipole itself produces: one that falls off as $1/r^3$. The dipole field was met as a limit in [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^ex-a1-3-1|Example §A1.3.1]].

## The dipole moment

> [!definition] Definition §A1.5.1: Electric Dipole; Dipole Moment
> An **electric dipole** is a pair of charges $+q$ and $-q$ ($q > 0$) a small distance $d$ apart. "Small" means small compared with the distances at which the dipole's field, or the variation of an external field, is considered. Let $\vec d$ be the displacement from $-q$ to $+q$. The **dipole moment** is
>
> $$
> \vec p = q\,\vec d ,
> $$
>
> a vector pointing from the negative to the positive charge, with unit $\mathrm{C\cdot m}$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.7, eq. (5.16)*

^def-a1-5-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.5.2: Permanent and Induced Dipoles
> A **permanent dipole** has its dipole moment without any external field, like an asymmetric polar molecule such as water. An **induced dipole** is created by an external field. The field pushes the positive nucleus of an atom one way and the electron cloud the other, so an atom that is spherically symmetric on its own acquires a dipole moment along the field. Induced moments are generally much smaller than permanent ones.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.7*

^def-a1-5-2

## A dipole in a uniform field

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.5.1: Force and Torque on a Dipole in a Uniform Field
> In a uniform external field $\vec E$, a rigid dipole feels no net force, but it feels the torque
>
> $$
> \vec\tau = \vec p \times \vec E, \qquad \tau = pE\sin\varphi ,
> $$
>
> where $\varphi$ is the angle between $\vec p$ and $\vec E$. The torque turns $\vec p$ toward the direction of $\vec E$, and it is the same about every point.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.7, eq. (5.17)*

^thm-a1-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The forces on the two charges are $\vec F_+ = q\vec E$ and $\vec F_- = -q\vec E$. They sum to zero. About the dipole's midpoint, $+q$ sits at $\vec d/2$ and $-q$ at $-\vec d/2$, so the net torque is
>
> $$
> \vec\tau = \frac{\vec d}{2} \times (q\vec E) + \Bigl(-\frac{\vec d}{2}\Bigr) \times (-q\vec E) = q\,\vec d \times \vec E = \vec p \times \vec E .
> $$
>
> Moving the reference point by $\vec a$ adds $\vec a \times (\vec F_+ + \vec F_-) = \vec 0$, so the torque does not depend on the point chosen. With $\vec p$ at angle $\varphi$ counterclockwise from $\vec E$, $\vec p \times \vec E$ points into the page. That is a clockwise torque, which decreases $\varphi$.

^der-a1-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Alignment
> The torque vanishes at $\varphi = 0$ and at $\varphi = \pi$. At $\varphi = 0$ ($\vec p$ along $\vec E$) a small tilt produces a torque back toward alignment, so the equilibrium is stable. At $\varphi = \pi$ the torque drives the dipole farther away, so the equilibrium is unstable. Permanent dipoles in a field therefore tend to line up with it, and induced dipoles are created already lined up. In both cases, once aligned, the dipole's own field points against the external field in the region between its charges, so it reduces the total field there. This is how a dielectric weakens the field in a capacitor ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^thm-a4-4-4|Theorem §A4.4.4]]).

^rem-a1-5-1

## The field of a dipole

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.5.2: Far Field of a Dipole
> At a distance $r \gg d$ from the centre of a dipole,
>
> $$
> \vec E_{\text{axis}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2\vec p}{r^3}
> \quad\text{(on the axis, either side)},
> \qquad
> \vec E_{\text{bisector}} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\vec p}{r^3}
> \quad\text{(on the perpendicular bisector)} .
> $$
>
> The dipole field falls off as $1/r^3$, faster than the $1/r^2$ of a single charge. On the axis it points along $\vec p$. On the bisector it points against $\vec p$ and is half as strong.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.7 and Example 5.4 (the bisector); the axis is derived here the same way*

^thm-a1-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Bisector.* In [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^ex-a1-3-1|Example §A1.3.1]](b) the charges are $+q$ at $x = -d/2$ and $-q$ at $x = +d/2$, so $\vec p = -qd\,\hat\imath$, and the field at height $z$ is $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qd}{(z^2 + d^2/4)^{3/2}}\hat\imath$. For $z \gg d$ the $d^2/4$ is negligible, which gives $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qd}{z^3}\hat\imath = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\vec p}{z^3}$.
>
> *Axis.* Put $+q$ at $z = d/2$ and $-q$ at $z = -d/2$, so $\vec p = qd\,\hat k$, and take a field point at $z > d/2$ on the axis. Both fields lie along the axis, with $kq/(z - d/2)^2$ upward and $kq/(z + d/2)^2$ downward:
>
> $$
> E_z = kq\Bigl[\frac{1}{(z - d/2)^2} - \frac{1}{(z + d/2)^2}\Bigr] = kq\,\frac{2zd}{(z^2 - d^2/4)^2} \;\approx\; \frac{2kqd}{z^3} \quad (z \gg d) .
> $$
>
> The same steps on the other side give the same direction, along $\vec p$. In both cases the leading terms of the two charges, $\pm kq/z^2$, cancel. What is left is a correction of relative size $d/z$, hence $1/z^3$.

^der-a1-5-2

> [!caution] Caution: The sign on the bisector
> On the perpendicular bisector the dipole's field is *antiparallel* to $\vec p$: it runs from the $+$ side back toward the $-$ side. Writing $\vec E = \vec p/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3)$ there without the minus sign is a common slip. The field lines below show why. Every line leaves $+q$ and curls back to $-q$, so midway between the charges it points from $+$ to $-$.

> [!example] Example §A1.5.1: Why Charged Bodies Attract Neutral Ones
> A positive point charge $Q$ at the origin polarizes a small neutral object at distance $r$ on the $x$ axis. The induced charges are $-q$ at $r - d/2$ (the near side) and $+q$ at $r + d/2$, so $\vec p = qd\,\hat\imath$ points along the local field. The net force on the object is
>
> $$
> F_x = -\frac{kQq}{(r - d/2)^2} + \frac{kQq}{(r + d/2)^2} \;\approx\; -\frac{2kQqd}{r^3} = -\frac{2kQp}{r^3} \quad (d \ll r),
> $$
>
> an attraction toward $Q$. If $Q$ is negative, the induced dipole is reversed, and the force again points toward $Q$. In a uniform field the forces on the two ends would cancel ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^thm-a1-5-1|Theorem §A1.5.1]]). Here the field is stronger at the near end, and the aligned dipole is pulled toward the stronger field. This is the comb and the bits of paper of [[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^rem-a1-1-4|§A1.1]].
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.2, §5.7 (the mechanism); the calculation is done here*

^ex-a1-5-1

> [!remark]- Connections
> - A current loop is the magnetic analogue. Its moment $\vec\mu$ = current × area ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^def-a7-4-1|Def. §A7.4.1]]) plays the role of $\vec p$ = charge × separation. In a uniform field it feels no net force, only the torque $\vec\tau = \vec\mu \times \vec B$ ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-2|Theorem §A7.4.2]]), the counterpart of [[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^thm-a1-5-1|Theorem §A1.5.1]]. Its orientation energy is $U = -\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$ ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-3|Theorem §A7.4.3]]). The same integration of the torque $pE\sin\varphi$ gives $U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E$ for the electric dipole.
> - Far away, the loop's own field is a dipole field of the same shape, $\propto 2\vec\mu/r^3$ on the axis ([[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop#^thm-a8-3-2|Theorem §A8.3.2]]), the counterpart of [[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]].
> - The $1/r^3$ field is the second term of the multipole expansion of a general charge distribution, after the monopole term $Q/r^2$ (Chapter B3, planned). The expansion is a Taylor expansion in $d/r$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]).
> - Torque as a cross product: [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^def-a1-5-2|CM Def. §A1.5.2]] and [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^def-a6-3-1|CM Def. §A6.3.1]].
> - Quantum side: the $n = 2$ states of hydrogen carry a permanent dipole moment of size $3ea$, which gives the linear Stark effect — [[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^ex-b8-2-2|QM Example §B8.2.2]].
