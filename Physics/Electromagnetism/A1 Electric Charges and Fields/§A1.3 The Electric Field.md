Superposition ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|Principle §A1.2.2]]) gives the force on one test charge, and the sum has to be done again for every new test charge. But the test charge enters only as an overall factor. Pulling it out leaves a quantity that depends on the sources alone, the electric field. This section defines the field, computes it for point charges, and introduces field lines as a way to draw it.

## The field

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.1: Electric Field
> The **electric field** $\vec E$ at a point $P$ is the electric force per unit charge on a test charge $Q$ placed at $P$, due to all the other charges:
>
> $$
> \vec E(P) = \frac{\vec F}{Q}, \qquad\text{so that}\qquad \vec F = Q\,\vec E(P) .
> $$
>
> Its unit is the newton per coulomb (N/C). By convention $\vec E$ points along the force on a *positive* test charge. A negative charge placed at $P$ feels a force opposite to $\vec E$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.4, eq. (5.3)*

^def-a1-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.3.1: Field of Point Charges
> Point charges $q_1, \dots, q_N$ produce at $P$ the field
>
> $$
> \vec E(P) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{q_i}{r_i^2}\,\hat r_i = \sum_{i=1}^N \vec E_i(P),
> $$
>
> where $r_i$ is the distance from $q_i$ to $P$ and $\hat r_i$ the unit vector from $q_i$ toward $P$. For a single point charge $q$,
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat r, \qquad E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\lvert q\rvert}{r^2} ,
> $$
>
> which points away from a positive charge and toward a negative one. The field depends only on the source charges and on $P$, not on the test charge, and fields of separate sources add as vectors.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.4, eq. (5.4)*

^thm-a1-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By superposition, the force on a test charge $Q$ at $P$ is $\vec F = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_i \dfrac{q_i}{r_i^2}\hat r_i$. Every term has the factor $Q$. Dividing by $Q$ as in [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^def-a1-3-1|Def. §A1.3.1]] removes it and leaves the stated sum. Each term is the field $\vec E_i$ that $q_i$ alone would produce. The direction rule follows from the sign of $q_i$: for $q_i > 0$ the term points along $\hat r_i$, away from the source.

^der-a1-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-1|Principle §A1.2.1]], [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|Principle §A1.2.2]], [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^def-a1-3-1|Def. §A1.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why bother with a field?
> The definition adds no new physics, since it only regroups Coulomb's law. There are two reasons to make it.
> - **Convenience.** Compute $\vec E$ once, then multiply by any charge to get the force on it. This separates the charges that *make* the force from the charge that *feels* it. Earth's gravitational field works the same way ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^def-a8-1-1|CM Def. §A8.1.1]]): $\vec g$ in N/kg is computed once and multiplied by any mass. $\vec E$ in N/C is the force per unit charge.
> - **The field is real.** One pictures the source charge altering the space around it, and other charges respond to the field where they are. Later chapters show that the field carries energy ([[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor|§A4.3]]) and that changes in it travel at the speed of light ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves|§A12.1]]). It is then more than a bookkeeping device.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 2 slides*

^rem-a1-3-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.2: Scalar Field; Vector Field
> A **field** is a physical quantity whose value depends on position. A **scalar field** assigns a number to each point, like the temperature in a room. A **vector field** assigns a vector, like the wind velocity on a weather map. The electric field (like the gravitational field) is a vector field, defined at every point of space whether or not a charge is there.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.4; PHY 182, week 2 slides*

^def-a1-3-2

> [!caution] Caution: What $r$ means, and whose field
> - In $kq/r^2$, $r$ is the distance from the source charge to the field point, not a coordinate of either. Find the magnitude from that distance first, then split the vector into components along the line from the source.
> - A charge feels only the fields of *other* charges. Its own field exerts no net force on it, just as Earth's orbit is not affected by Earth's own gravity.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 4 slides (the first point)*

^cau-a1-3-1

> [!example] Example §A1.3.1: Field above a Pair of Charges
> Two charges sit on the $x$ axis at $x = \mp d/2$. Find $\vec E$ at the point $P$ a height $z$ above their midpoint (a) for charges $+q$, $+q$, and (b) for $+q$ at $x = -d/2$ and $-q$ at $x = +d/2$.
>
> Each charge is at distance $r = \sqrt{z^2 + d^2/4}$ from $P$, and each field makes the angle $\theta$ with the $z$ axis, where $\cos\theta = z/r$ and $\sin\theta = (d/2)/r$. Both fields have magnitude $kq/r^2$.
>
> (a) By symmetry the $x$ components cancel and the $z$ components add:
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2q}{r^2}\cos\theta\,\hat k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2qz}{\bigl(z^2 + d^2/4\bigr)^{3/2}}\,\hat k .
> $$
>
> (b) The field of $+q$ points away from it, the field of $-q$ toward it. Both have $x$ components along $+\hat\imath$, and their $z$ components cancel:
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2q}{r^2}\sin\theta\,\hat\imath = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q d}{\bigl(z^2 + d^2/4\bigr)^{3/2}}\,\hat\imath .
> $$
>
> Check the limit $z \gg d$. Then (a) becomes $2q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 z^2)$, the field of a single charge $2q$, as it should be from far away. In (b) the charges nearly cancel, and the field falls off faster, as $qd/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 z^3)$. This is the field of a dipole ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 5.4*

^ex-a1-3-1

> [!example] Example §A1.3.2: A Charged Ball in a Uniform Field
> A ball of mass $m = 1.0\ \mathrm g$ and charge $q = +1.0\ \mu\mathrm C$ hangs from a light string in a uniform horizontal field $E = 1.0 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{N/C}$ pointing to the right. At what angle $\theta$ from the vertical does it hang?
>
> The forces on the ball are its weight $mg$ (down), the tension $T$ along the string, and the electric force $q\vec E$ (to the right, since $q > 0$). In equilibrium the horizontal and the vertical components balance separately:
>
> $$
> T\sin\theta = qE, \qquad T\cos\theta = mg \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tan\theta = \frac{qE}{mg} = \frac{(1.0 \times 10^{-6})(1.0 \times 10^4)\ \mathrm N}{(1.0 \times 10^{-3})(9.80)\ \mathrm N} = 1.02 ,
> $$
>
> so $\theta = 45.6^\circ$, with the ball displaced along $\vec E$. A negative charge would hang to the left. The string's length does not matter. Once the field is known, the force on any charge placed in it is just $q\vec E$.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 3*

^ex-a1-3-2

## Field lines

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.3: Field Lines
> **Electric field lines** are curves drawn so that at each of their points the field vector is tangent to the curve. An arrowhead gives the direction of $\vec E$. They are drawn so that the **field-line density**, the number of lines crossing a small area perpendicular to them divided by that area, is proportional to the magnitude $E$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.6*

^def-a1-3-3

> [!remark] Remark: Rules for field-line diagrams
> 1. Lines start on positive charges or come in from infinity. They end on negative charges or go out to infinity. They never start or stop in empty space.
> 2. The number of lines leaving or entering a charge is proportional to its magnitude: $2q$ gets twice as many lines as $q$.
> 3. At each point the field is tangent to the line through it.
> 4. The line density is proportional to the field strength.
> 5. Lines never cross. Where two lines met, the field would point two ways at once (except at a point where $\vec E = \vec 0$).
>
> Rules 3–5 restate the definition. Rules 1, 2 and 4 are compatible only because the field of a point charge falls as $1/r^2$. The $N$ lines of a charge spread over a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$, so their density is $N/4\pi r^2 \propto 1/r^2 \propto E$. Gauss's law ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law]]) turns this counting into an exact statement.
>
> A diagram can be read the other way too. Surround a group of charges with a closed surface and count the lines that go out minus the lines that come in. A positive result means a positive net charge inside.
>
> *Source: UP2 §5.6; PHY 182, week 4 slides (the counting argument)*

> [!caution] Caution: Field lines are not paths
> Field lines are a drawing aid, not physical objects, and the field also exists between them. They are not the paths of charged particles either. A positive charge released from rest starts out along the line, since its acceleration $q\vec E/m$ is tangent to it. After that its velocity lags behind the changing direction of the field, so on a curved line the particle drifts off the line.
