What does a magnetic field do to moving charges, and how is moving charge described when it is spread over a surface or a volume? Level A stated the magnetic force on a charge ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]]), followed charges around circles and helices ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field|§A7.2]]), added up the force on a wire ([[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]) and defined the current density ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^def-a5-2-3|Def. §A5.2.3]]). Level B takes the full Lorentz force as one of the two axioms of the theory (the other is the set of field equations), proves that magnetic forces never do work in general, solves the motion in crossed fields, introduces surface and volume currents with their force densities, and turns conservation of charge into a local field equation, the continuity equation, which is homed here.

## The Lorentz force law

> [!principle] Principle §B6.1.1: Lorentz Force Law
> A point charge $Q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ through an electric field $\vec E$ and a magnetic field $\vec B$ feels the force (Griffiths eqs. (5.1)–(5.2))
>
> $$
> \vec F = Q\bigl[\vec E + (\vec v\times\vec B)\bigr], \qquad \vec F_{\text{mag}} = Q(\vec v\times\vec B) .
> $$
>
> Together with the field equations (for statics: [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl|§B2.1]], [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B|§B6.3]]; in general: [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations|§B8.4]]) it is the whole of classical electrodynamics: the field equations say how charges make fields, the force law how fields move charges.
>
> *Domain: classical (non-quantum) charges; $\vec v$, $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ measured in the same inertial frame; valid at all speeds if $\vec F$ is read as $d\vec p/dt$ with the relativistic momentum ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-2|REL Def. §A3.1.2]]), and with $\vec F = m\vec a$ for $v \ll c$.*
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.2, eqs. (5.1)–(5.2) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.2*

^pr-b6-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level A
> - *Layer.* Level A had two separate entries, the electric force $q\vec E$ as the definition of $\vec E$ ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^def-a1-3-1|Def. §A1.3.1]]) and the magnetic force as a principle ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]]), and combined them into the Lorentz force only beside Maxwell's equations ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|Principle §A12.1.3]]). Here the combined law is the axiom from the start; Griffiths stresses that it is not derived but justified by experiment (the attraction of parallel currents and the repulsion of antiparallel ones in neutral wires, [[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-3|Theorem §A8.2.3]]).
> - *$\vec B$ is a pseudovector.* Nelson's version of the argument: experiment shows that the magnetic force is linear in $\vec v$ and always perpendicular to it. A linear map $\vec v \mapsto \vec F$ with $\vec v\cdot\vec F = 0$ for every $\vec v$ is antisymmetric, and an antisymmetric $3\times3$ matrix has three independent entries, which can be packed into a vector $\vec B$ so that the map is $\vec v\mapsto Q\,\vec v\times\vec B$. The packing uses the cross product, that is, a choice of right hand; under a reflection of space $\vec F$ and $\vec v$ behave as ordinary (polar) vectors, so $\vec B$ must pick up an extra sign (Griffiths, footnote 2). This is what makes the mirror arguments of [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B|§B6.3]] work.
> - Level A wrote $q$ for the moving charge; Griffiths writes $Q$ for a test charge and $q$ for sources. Here $m$ is a mass; from [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole|§B6.4]] on it denotes a magnetic moment.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.1–5.1.2 and footnote 2 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.2, eqs. (15.1)–(15.3)*

^rem-b6-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.2: Magnetic Forces Do No Work
> Under the Lorentz force alone, the kinetic energy $K$ of a charge changes only through the electric field:
>
> $$
> \frac{dK}{dt} = \vec F\cdot\vec v = Q\,\vec E\cdot\vec v , \qquad dW_{\text{mag}} = \vec F_{\text{mag}}\cdot d\vec l = 0 ,
> $$
>
> for every trajectory and every $\vec B$, uniform or not, static or not (Griffiths eq. (5.11)). For charge spread continuously with density $\rho$ and velocity $\vec v$ (current density $\vec J = \rho\vec v$, [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^def-b6-1-1|Def. §B6.1.1]]), the fields deliver to the charges in a volume $\mathcal V$ the power
>
> $$
> P = \int_{\mathcal V}\vec J\cdot\vec E\,d\tau ,
> $$
>
> with no magnetic contribution; for several species of carriers the $\vec J$'s add.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.2, eq. (5.11)*

^thm-b6-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In a time $dt$ the charge moves $d\vec l = \vec v\,dt$, and $(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot\vec v = 0$ because a cross product is perpendicular to each factor; so $dW_{\text{mag}} = Q(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot\vec v\,dt = 0$ and the work–energy theorem gives $dK/dt = Q\vec E\cdot\vec v$. For a continuous distribution, the force on the charge $\rho\,d\tau$ in a volume element is $\rho\,d\tau\,(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B) = (\rho\vec E + \vec J\times\vec B)\,d\tau$, and its power is this force dotted into $\vec v$: $(\rho\vec v\cdot\vec E + (\vec J\times\vec B)\cdot\vec v)\,d\tau = \vec J\cdot\vec E\,d\tau$, since $\vec J\times\vec B$ is perpendicular to $\vec J = \rho\vec v$. Each species of carrier gives such a term; summing and integrating gives $P$.

^der-b6-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]], [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^def-b6-1-1|Def. §B6.1.1]], [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^thm-a4-2-1|CM Theorem §A4.2.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: When a magnetic force seems to do work
> A rectangular loop hangs with its lower side, of width $a$, in a field $\vec B$; a current $I = mg/Ba$ makes the magnetic force on that side balance the weight $mg$ (Griffiths Example 5.3). Raise the current and the loop rises a height $h$: work $IBah$ is done, and it is tempting to credit the magnetic force. But once the loop moves upward with speed $u$, each carrier has velocity $\vec w + \vec u$ (along the wire plus upward), and the magnetic force $q(\vec w + \vec u)\times\vec B$ tilts back: its vertical part $qwB$ lifts the loop, while its horizontal part $quB$ pushes against the current. Whatever keeps the current flowing (the battery) must push the carriers forward against that component, and over the rise it does exactly the work $\lambda aB\int uw\,dt = IBah$. The battery does the work; the magnetic force, perpendicular to each carrier's actual velocity, only redirects it, like the normal force of a ramp on a trunk pushed up it horizontally. Level A met the same bookkeeping as the back emf of a motor ([[§A9.6 Generators, Back EMF and Applications|§A9.6]]); the energy flow is followed in the fields in [[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem|§B9.1]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.3, Example 5.3, eqs. (5.18)–(5.21) and footnote 7*

## Crossed fields

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.3: Motion in Uniform Crossed Fields; E×B Drift
> Let $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ be uniform and static, with $\vec E\perp\vec B$, and let $E/B \ll c$. Define the **drift velocity**
>
> $$
> \vec u = \frac{\vec E\times\vec B}{B^2}, \qquad u = \frac EB .
> $$
>
> Then every solution of $m\dot{\vec v} = Q(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B)$ has the form $\vec v(t) = \vec u + v_\parallel\hat B + \vec w(t)$: a constant velocity $v_\parallel$ along $\vec B$, the drift $\vec u$, perpendicular to both fields and the same for all charges and masses, and a part $\vec w\perp\vec B$ of constant magnitude that rotates at the cyclotron frequency $\omega = QB/m$ (Griffiths eq. (5.4)), in the sense of a charge circling in $\vec B$ alone ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]]). The particle circles, with radius $|\vec w|/|\omega|$, about a guiding centre that moves with $\vec u + v_\parallel\hat B$. The overall motion is perpendicular to $\vec E$, not along it.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.2, Example 5.2, eqs. (5.4)–(5.10); Problem 5.2*

^thm-b6-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $\vec v = \vec u + \vec v\,'$. With $(\vec a\times\vec b)\times\vec c = \vec b(\vec a\cdot\vec c) - \vec a(\vec b\cdot\vec c)$ and $\vec E\cdot\vec B = 0$,
>
> $$
> \vec u\times\vec B = \frac{(\vec E\times\vec B)\times\vec B}{B^2} = \frac{\vec B(\vec E\cdot\vec B) - \vec E\,B^2}{B^2} = -\vec E ,
> $$
>
> so $\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B = \vec v\,'\times\vec B$, and since $\vec u$ is constant, $m\dot{\vec v}\,' = Q\vec v\,'\times\vec B$: the motion relative to the drift is that of a charge in the magnetic field alone. Split $\vec v\,'$ into its component $v_\parallel\hat B$, which feels no force and stays constant, and $\vec w\perp\vec B$; then $\dot{\vec w} = (Q/m)\,\vec w\times\vec B$ is perpendicular to $\vec w$, so $|\vec w|$ is constant and $\vec w$ turns uniformly at the rate $QB/m$, the circular motion of [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]] and [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-3|Theorem §A7.2.3]], of radius $m|\vec w|/|Q|B$. Integrating $\vec v$ gives a circle about a point moving with $\vec u + v_\parallel\hat B$. The condition $E/B \ll c$ keeps the drift, and with it all speeds of the order of $u$, non-relativistic.

^der-b6-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]], [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]], [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-3|Theorem §A7.2.3]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates|§B1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The frame in which E disappears
> The step $\vec E + \vec u\times\vec B = \vec 0$ says that an observer moving with the drift sees no electric field, only $\vec B$: in that frame the charge just circles. The electric and magnetic fields are therefore not separately frame-independent (level A already noticed this, [[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^rem-a8-1-4|§A8.1, Remark: Whose velocity?]]). Here the field transformation is used only to first order in $u/c$; its exact form is special relativity (Griffiths ch. 12, Relativity level B).

> [!example] Example §B6.1.1: The Cycloid, and Thomson's e/m
> Take $\vec B = B\hat x$, $\vec E = E\hat z$ and a positive charge leaving the origin with $\vec v(0) = v_0\hat y$ (Griffiths Example 5.2 has $v_0 = 0$). The drift is $\vec u = (E\hat z\times B\hat x)/B^2 = (E/B)\hat y$, and $\vec w(0) = (v_0 - u)\hat y \equiv w_0\hat y$. The equation $\dot{\vec w} = \omega\,\vec w\times\hat x$ reads $\dot w_y = \omega w_z$, $\dot w_z = -\omega w_y$, so $w_y = w_0\cos\omega t$, $w_z = -w_0\sin\omega t$, and integrating $\vec v = \vec u + \vec w$ from the origin,
>
> $$
> y(t) = ut + \frac{w_0}{\omega}\sin\omega t, \qquad z(t) = \frac{w_0}{\omega}\bigl(\cos\omega t - 1\bigr) .
> $$
>
> *From rest* ($w_0 = -u$), with $R \equiv E/\omega B$ (Griffiths eq. (5.8)),
>
> $$
> y = R(\omega t - \sin\omega t), \quad z = R(1 - \cos\omega t), \qquad (y - R\omega t)^2 + (z - R)^2 = R^2 ,
> $$
>
> Griffiths eqs. (5.7) and (5.9): a point on the rim of a wheel of radius $R$ rolling along the $y$ axis at speed $u = \omega R$ (5.10), a **cycloid**. The charge comes to rest at each cusp, $y = 2\pi R, 4\pi R, \dots$, and is fastest at the top, $z = 2R$, where $v = u + |w_0| = 2u$. Energy check ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]]): only $\vec E$ works, so $\tfrac12mv^2 = QEz$; at the top $\tfrac12m(2u)^2 = 2mE^2/B^2$ and $QE\cdot2R = 2QE^2/\omega B = 2mE^2/B^2$.
>
> *Other launches.* The figure shows the three regimes: $|v_0 - u| < u$ gives a smooth wave, $|v_0 - u| > u$ a curve with loops, during which the charge moves backwards, and $v_0 = u$ a straight line, because then $\vec w = \vec0$ and the electric and magnetic forces cancel: the velocity selector of [[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^thm-a7-5-1|Theorem §A7.5.1]].
>
> *Thomson's measurement* (PHY 422, HW 8, Problem 1; Griffiths Problem 5.3). In 1897 J. J. Thomson sent cathode rays (charge $q$, mass $m$) through crossed $\vec E$ and $\vec B$, both perpendicular to the beam, and adjusted $E$ until the beam went straight: then its speed is the drift speed, $v = E/B$. With $\vec E$ switched off the beam bent on a circle of radius $R_c = mv/qB$ ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]]), so
>
> $$
> \frac qm = \frac{v}{R_cB} = \frac{E}{R_cB^2} ,
> $$
>
> in agreement with the instructor's solution. Neither measurement alone fixes $q/m$: the straight run gives $v$, the curved one $mv/q$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 5.2, Problems 5.2 and 5.3 (solved here) · PHY 422, HW 8, Problem 1 (Discovering the electron)*

## Surface and volume currents

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.1: Line, Surface and Volume Current Densities
> - A line charge $\lambda$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ along a wire is a **current** $\vec I = \lambda\vec v$, of magnitude $I = \lambda v$ (Griffiths eqs. (5.13)–(5.14)); $\lambda$ counts only the moving charge, and if both signs move, $\vec I = \lambda_+\vec v_+ + \lambda_-\vec v_-$.
> - Charge flowing over a surface is described by the **surface current density** $\vec K$: if $d\vec I$ is the current in a ribbon of infinitesimal width $dl_\perp$ running parallel to the flow, $\vec K = d\vec I/dl_\perp$, the current per unit width; for mobile surface charge $\sigma$ with velocity $\vec v$, $\vec K = \sigma\vec v$ (5.22)–(5.23). Unit: $\mathrm{A/m}$.
> - Charge flowing through a volume is described by the **volume current density** $\vec J = d\vec I/da_\perp$, the current per unit area through a tube of infinitesimal cross-section $da_\perp$ parallel to the flow; for mobile charge density $\rho$ with velocity $\vec v$, $\vec J = \rho\vec v$ (5.25)–(5.26). Unit: $\mathrm{A/m^2}$. The current through a surface $\mathcal S$ is $I = \int_{\mathcal S}\vec J\cdot d\vec a$ (5.28).
>
> The volume current density is level A's ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^def-a5-2-3|Def. §A5.2.3]]), with $\vec J = nq\vec v_d$ ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]) the case $\rho = nq$ of carriers drifting through a neutral lattice. New are $\vec K$ and the habit of treating every current as a vector.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.3, eqs. (5.13)–(5.14), (5.22)–(5.26), (5.28) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 8, §8.3*

^def-b6-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.4: Magnetic Force on Currents
> The magnetic force on a line, surface or volume current in a field $\vec B$ is (Griffiths eqs. (5.16), (5.24), (5.27))
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{mag}} = \int I\,(d\vec l\times\vec B), \qquad \vec F_{\text{mag}} = \int(\vec K\times\vec B)\,da, \qquad \vec F_{\text{mag}} = \int(\vec J\times\vec B)\,d\tau ;
> $$
>
> for a wire with the same current all along, $\vec F_{\text{mag}} = I\int d\vec l\times\vec B$ (5.17). Every formula for point charges carries over by the dictionary (5.30)
>
> $$
> \sum_{i}(\ \ )\,q_i\vec v_i \;\sim\; \int_{\text{line}}(\ \ )\,\vec I\,dl \;\sim\; \int_{\text{surface}}(\ \ )\,\vec K\,da \;\sim\; \int_{\text{volume}}(\ \ )\,\vec J\,d\tau ,
> $$
>
> the analogue of $q \sim \lambda\,dl \sim \sigma\,da \sim \rho\,d\tau$ for charge.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.3, eqs. (5.15)–(5.17), (5.24), (5.27), (5.30)*

^thm-b6-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]] to the moving charge in each element and add. In a wire element the moving charge is $dq = \lambda\,dl$, so $(\vec v\times\vec B)\,dq = (\lambda\vec v\times\vec B)\,dl = (\vec I\times\vec B)\,dl = I(d\vec l\times\vec B)$, since $\vec I$ and $d\vec l$ point the same way (5.15). On a surface $dq = \sigma\,da$ and $\sigma\vec v = \vec K$; in a volume $dq = \rho\,d\tau$ and $\rho\vec v = \vec J$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^def-b6-1-1|Def. §B6.1.1]]). The stationary charges of a neutral conductor feel no magnetic force, and the electric forces on the two signs cancel; the carriers pass the force on to the lattice, as at level A ([[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]).

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]], [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^def-b6-1-1|Def. §B6.1.1]], [[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: The field at a surface current
> $\vec B$ is discontinuous across a surface current ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole|§B6.4]], boundary conditions), so in $\int\vec K\times\vec B\,da$ the field must be the *average* of its values just above and just below, exactly as for the force on a surface charge in electrostatics ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors|§B2.4]]). A sheet does not push itself: the jump comes from the sheet's own field, which is equal and opposite on the two sides.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.3 (caveat after eq. (5.24))*

> [!example] Example §B6.1.2: Currents of Spinning Charge
> *(a)* A disk (a DVD) carries uniform static charge density $\sigma$ and spins at angular velocity $\omega$ about its axis. A point at distance $r$ from the centre moves with $\vec v = \vec\omega\times\vec r = \omega r\,\hat\phi$, so
>
> $$
> \vec K = \sigma\vec v = \sigma\omega r\,\hat\phi .
> $$
>
> Check: the current across a radius is $\int_0^R K\,dr = \sigma\omega R^2/2 = Q\omega/2\pi$ with $Q = \sigma\pi R^2$: the whole charge passes any radius once per period $2\pi/\omega$.
>
> *(b)* A ball of radius $R$ with charge $Q$ spread uniformly, $\rho = 3Q/4\pi R^3$, spins about the $z$ axis. In spherical coordinates the velocity is $\omega r\sin\theta\,\hat\phi$ (the distance from the axis is $s = r\sin\theta$), so
>
> $$
> \vec J(\vec r) = \rho\vec v = \frac{3Q\omega}{4\pi R^3}\,r\sin\theta\,\hat\phi \quad (r < R), \qquad \vec J = \vec 0 \quad (r > R) .
> $$
>
> Check: the current through a half-plane $\phi = $ const is $\int J\,ds\,dz = \rho\omega\int_0^R s\cdot2\sqrt{R^2 - s^2}\,ds = \rho\omega\cdot\tfrac23R^3 = Q\omega/2\pi$, again the whole charge once per period. Both distributions are steady ($\vec J$ has only a $\hat\phi$ component, independent of $\phi$, so $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$, [[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]]), and $\rho$ does not change in time although the charge moves: the electric field of the spinning ball is the static Coulomb field (Griffiths, footnote 8). The instructor's solution agrees (its eq. (16) labels the volume current $\vec K$, a typo). These currents are the sources of [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^ex-b6-4-3|Example §B6.4.3]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 5.6 (solved here) · PHY 422, HW 8, Problem 2 (Current density)*

## Conservation of charge

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.5: Continuity Equation
> Charge conservation ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]]), in its local form (charge in any region changes only by flow across the boundary), is equivalent to
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec J = -\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}
> $$
>
> at every point (Griffiths eq. (5.29)), for $\rho$ and $\vec J$ continuously differentiable. In integral form, for every fixed volume $\mathcal V$ with boundary $\mathcal S$,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal V}\rho\,d\tau = -\oint_{\mathcal S}\vec J\cdot d\vec a .
> $$
>
> *Domain: all of classical and quantum physics; every known interaction, particle reactions included, conserves charge at each point (Nelson, §8.3.1).*
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.3, eq. (5.29) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 8, §8.3, eqs. (8.6)–(8.7)*

^thm-b6-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The charge leaving $\mathcal V$ per unit time is the flux $\oint_{\mathcal S}\vec J\cdot d\vec a$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^def-b6-1-1|Def. §B6.1.1]]); local conservation says this equals the decrease of the charge inside, which is the integral form. By the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]) the flux is $\int_{\mathcal V}\nabla\cdot\vec J\,d\tau$, and differentiating under the integral sign (fixed $\mathcal V$, continuous $\partial\rho/\partial t$),
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V}\Bigl(\nabla\cdot\vec J + \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\Bigr)d\tau = 0 .
> $$
>
> This holds for every $\mathcal V$; if the continuous integrand were nonzero at a point, it would keep its sign on a small ball around it and the integral over that ball would not vanish. Hence the integrand is zero everywhere. Conversely, integrating the differential equation over $\mathcal V$ and using the divergence theorem gives back the integral form; with $\mathcal V$ so large that no charge crosses its boundary, the total charge is constant, which is the global law (Nelson's eq. (8.7)).

^der-b6-1-5

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]], [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^def-b6-1-1|Def. §B6.1.1]], [[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Local, not just global
> Global conservation alone would allow a charge to vanish here and reappear at the same instant on the Moon; local conservation forbids it, and the continuity equation is its precise form. Nelson derives it without the divergence theorem, by counting the charge that enters and leaves a small box in space and time; in his words, everything that enters the box must also exit, carrying its charge. Level A used the integral form in a special case, a steady current is the same along an unbranched wire ([[§A5.1 Electric Current#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]), and Kirchhoff's junction rule. The equation returns as the first conservation law of [[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem|§B9.1]], where it is derived from Maxwell's equations instead of assumed, and it is the consistency condition that forces the displacement current on Ampère's law ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^rem-b6-3-3|§B6.3, Remark: Why only statics]]).
>
> *Source: Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 8, §8.2–8.3*

^rem-b6-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.6: Total Current and the Electric Dipole Moment
> For charges and currents confined to a volume $\mathcal V$ (no current crosses its boundary),
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V}\vec J\,d\tau = \frac{d\vec p}{dt}, \qquad \vec p = \int_{\mathcal V}\rho\,\vec r\,d\tau ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec p$ the electric dipole moment ([[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential|§B4.1]]) (Griffiths eq. (5.31)). In particular, a **steady, localized** current distribution has $\int\vec J\,d\tau = \vec 0$, and a closed wire loop $\oint I\,d\vec l = I\oint d\vec l = \vec 0$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 5.7, eq. (5.31) (solved here) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 17, §17.2, eq. (17.1)*

^thm-b6-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the product rule for $\nabla\cdot(f\vec A)$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates|§B1.1]]) and $\nabla x = \hat x$,
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot(x\vec J) = J_x + x\,\nabla\cdot\vec J = J_x - x\,\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}
> $$
>
> by the continuity equation. Integrate over $\mathcal V$: the divergence theorem turns the left side into $\oint x\,\vec J\cdot d\vec a = 0$, since $\vec J$ has no normal component on the boundary. Hence $\int J_x\,d\tau = \int x\,\partial\rho/\partial t\,d\tau = \frac{d}{dt}\int x\rho\,d\tau = dp_x/dt$, and likewise for $y$ and $z$. In a steady state $\partial\rho/\partial t = 0$, so the integral vanishes.
