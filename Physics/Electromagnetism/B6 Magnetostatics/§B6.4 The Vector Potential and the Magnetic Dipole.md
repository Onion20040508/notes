Is there a potential for the magnetic field, and what does a small current loop look like from far away? Level A had no magnetic potential at all; it knew the magnetic dipole moment of a flat loop ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^def-a7-4-1|Def. §A7.4.1]]), the torque and orientation energy of a loop in a uniform field ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-2|Theorem §A7.4.2]], [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-3|Theorem §A7.4.3]]) and the loop's far field on its axis only ([[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop#^thm-a8-3-2|Theorem §A8.3.2]]). Since $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]]), $\vec B$ is the curl of a vector potential $\vec A$; this section constructs it, fixes its gauge freedom with the Coulomb gauge, reduces magnetostatics to three Poisson equations, states the boundary conditions at a current sheet, expands $\vec A$ in multipoles, and derives the complete dipole field and the force and torque on a dipole in a non-uniform field.

## The vector potential

> [!definition] Definition §B6.4.1: Magnetic Vector Potential
> A **vector potential** for $\vec B$ is a vector field $\vec A$ with (Griffiths eq. (5.61))
>
> $$
> \vec B = \nabla\times\vec A .
> $$
>
> It is the magnetic counterpart of the electrostatic potential, $\vec E = -\nabla V$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]): $V$ exists because $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$, $\vec A$ because $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$. Unit: $\mathrm{T\cdot m}$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.1, eq. (5.61) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.3*

^def-b6-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.4.1: Existence of A; Gauge Freedom
> Let $\vec B$ be continuously differentiable with $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ on all of space (or on any region star-shaped about the origin).
> 1. A vector potential exists; one is (Griffiths Problem 5.53(ii); Nelson eq. (15.11))
>
> $$
> \vec A(\vec r) = -\,\vec r\times\int_0^1\lambda\,\vec B(\lambda\vec r)\,d\lambda .
> $$
>
> 2. Two vector potentials $\vec A$, $\vec A\,'$ give the same $\vec B$ exactly when they differ by a gradient, $\vec A\,' = \vec A + \nabla\lambda$ (a **gauge transformation**).
>
> Every magnetic field therefore has potentials, and the potential is far from unique: $\vec B$ fixes the curl of $\vec A$ but says nothing about its divergence. $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ is built in, since the divergence of a curl vanishes.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.1, Problems 5.31 and 5.53 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.3.5–15.4, eqs. (15.11)–(15.14)*

^thm-b6-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]]), so $\vec B$ is a curl by [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]](2), the Poincaré lemma for 2-forms ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-12|452 Prop. §22.12]]). The explicit formula is the ray-integral construction of [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^rem-b1-2-2|§B1.2, ★ Remark: The potentials written down explicitly]], $\int_0^1\vec B(t\vec r)\times t\vec r\,dt = -\vec r\times\int_0^1t\,\vec B(t\vec r)\,dt$; its curl is $\int_0^1\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[t^2\vec B(t\vec r)\bigr]dt = \vec B(\vec r)$, by product rule (vi) of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]] and $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$. Only points on the segment from the origin to $\vec r$ enter, which is why a star-shaped region suffices; Nelson builds the same potential as his "integrability lemma" for the antisymmetric field tensor.
>
> *2.* $\nabla\times\nabla\lambda = \vec 0$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]), so a gradient changes nothing. Conversely, if $\nabla\times(\vec A\,' - \vec A) = \vec 0$ on such a region, the difference is a gradient by [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]](1).

> [!definition] Definition §B6.4.2: Coulomb Gauge
> A vector potential is in the **Coulomb gauge** if (Griffiths eq. (5.63))
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec A = 0 .
> $$
>
> For time-dependent fields the Coulomb gauge is one choice among several, beside the Lorenz gauge ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^def-b11-1-1|Def. §B11.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.1, eq. (5.63) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.4*

^def-b6-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.4.2: The Vector Potential of Steady Currents
> 1. Every vector potential can be brought into the Coulomb gauge by a gauge transformation.
> 2. In the Coulomb gauge Ampère's law $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ becomes (Griffiths eq. (5.64))
>
> $$
> \nabla^2\vec A = -\mu_0\vec J ,
> $$
>
> three Poisson equations, one for each Cartesian component.
> 3. For steady currents that vanish outside a bounded region, the solution that vanishes at infinity is (5.65)–(5.66)
>
> $$
> \vec A(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vec J(\vec r\,')}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau', \qquad \vec A = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int\frac{d\vec l\,'}{\mathcal R}, \qquad \vec A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vec K}{\mathcal R}\,da' .
> $$
>
> It is in the Coulomb gauge, and its curl is the Biot–Savart field ([[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^law-b6-2-2|Law §B6.2.2]]). Typically $\vec A$ points along the current that produces it.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.1, eqs. (5.62)–(5.66), Problem 5.28 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.5.3–15.5.4, §15.6.2, eqs. (15.18)–(15.21)*

^thm-b6-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* If $\nabla\cdot\vec A_0 \ne 0$, set $\vec A = \vec A_0 + \nabla\lambda$; then $\nabla\cdot\vec A = \nabla\cdot\vec A_0 + \nabla^2\lambda$, which vanishes if $\nabla^2\lambda = -\nabla\cdot\vec A_0$. This is Poisson's equation with $\nabla\cdot\vec A_0$ in place of $\rho/\varepsilon_0$, solved by $\lambda = \frac1{4\pi}\int\frac{\nabla'\cdot\vec A_0}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau'$ when the source falls off at infinity ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]).
>
> *2.* $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec A) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec A) - \nabla^2\vec A$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]); with $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$ Ampère's law reads $-\nabla^2\vec A = \mu_0\vec J$ (5.62).
>
> *3. Poisson.* Componentwise, $\nabla^2(1/\mathcal R) = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,')$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]) gives $\nabla^2\vec A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\vec J(\vec r\,')\nabla^2\frac1{\mathcal R}\,d\tau' = -\mu_0\vec J(\vec r)$, and $\vec A\to\vec 0$ at infinity.
> *Gauge.* Since $1/\mathcal R$ depends on $\vec r - \vec r\,'$, $\nabla(1/\mathcal R) = -\nabla'(1/\mathcal R)$, and
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec A = -\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\vec J\cdot\nabla'\frac1{\mathcal R}\,d\tau' = -\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\Bigl[\nabla'\cdot\Bigl(\frac{\vec J}{\mathcal R}\Bigr) - \frac{\nabla'\cdot\vec J}{\mathcal R}\Bigr]d\tau' = 0 :
> $$
>
> the first term becomes a surface integral where $\vec J = \vec 0$, and $\nabla'\cdot\vec J = 0$ for steady currents ([[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]]).
> *Curl.* $\nabla\times\bigl(\vec J(\vec r\,')/\mathcal R\bigr) = \nabla(1/\mathcal R)\times\vec J = -\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\times\vec J = \vec J\times\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}$, so $\nabla\times\vec A$ is the Biot–Savart integral. The line and surface forms follow from the dictionary $\vec J\,d\tau'\sim I\,d\vec l\,'\sim\vec K\,da'$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]).

> [!caution] Caution: Cartesian components only
> $\nabla^2\vec A = -\mu_0\vec J$ splits into $\nabla^2A_x = -\mu_0J_x$ and so on only in Cartesian components; curvilinear unit vectors depend on position and are differentiated too, so in general $\nabla^2A_\phi \ne -\mu_0J_\phi$ (Griffiths, footnote 19; Nelson, footnote 11). Express $\vec J$ in Cartesian components before evaluating (5.65) in curvilinear coordinates. One safe exception: a field of the form $A_z(s, \phi, z)\,\hat z$, since $\hat z$ is constant; then the scalar Laplacian of $A_z$ in cylindrical coordinates may be used.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.1, footnote 19 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, footnote 11*

^cau-b6-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: The two routes meet
> Equations (5.47), (5.64)–(5.65) and the definition $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$ close a triangle between $\vec J$, $\vec B$ and $\vec A$ (Griffiths, Fig. 5.48), as $\rho$, $\vec E$ and $V$ did in electrostatics. Theorem §B6.4.2 is Nelson's route to magnetostatics: take the field equations as given, solve them with $\vec A$, and get the Biot–Savart law as a theorem. Read in that direction, it also re-proves both field equations of [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B|§B6.3]] without differentiating a singular integrand: $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A) = 0$, and $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec A) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec A) - \nabla^2\vec A = \mu_0\vec J$. Nelson adds the counting: the six static field equations for $\vec E$, $\vec B$ become four equations for the four potentials $V$, $\vec A$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.1–5.4.2, Fig. 5.48 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.5.3–15.5.5*

^rem-b6-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: What A is, and why there is no magnetic scalar potential
> - $\vec A$ is less directly useful than $V$: it is still a vector, and since magnetic forces do no work ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]]) it is not a potential energy per unit charge. In some contexts it is a momentum per unit charge (Griffiths): the canonical momentum of a charge is $m\vec v + q\vec A$ ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^rem-b6-2-6|CM Remark: Velocity-dependent potentials]]). Its real importance comes with time-dependent fields ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials|§B11.1★]]).
> - A scalar potential $\vec B = -\nabla U$ (5.67) is incompatible with Ampère's law wherever $\vec J \ne \vec 0$. Even in current-free space it can fail: around a wire, Ampère's law gives $U(\text{after one turn}) - U(\text{start}) = -\mu_0I$, so $U$ is multivalued unless the region is cut so that no loop can encircle a current (Griffiths Problem 5.29). It is still a practical tool in current-free regions, for instance outside permanent magnets ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media|§B7.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.1, eq. (5.67), Problem 5.29*

^rem-b6-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.4.3: Circulation of A Is the Enclosed Flux
> For every closed loop $C$ bounding a surface $\mathcal S$ (Griffiths eq. (5.71)),
>
> $$
> \oint_C\vec A\cdot d\vec l = \int_{\mathcal S}\vec B\cdot d\vec a = \Phi .
> $$
>
> This is Ampère's law with $\vec B\to\vec A$ and $\mu_0I_{\text{enc}}\to\Phi$. In the Coulomb gauge ($\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$, $\nabla\times\vec A = \vec B$) the analogy is complete: $\vec A$ is to $\vec B$ what $\vec B$ is to $\mu_0\vec J$ (Griffiths Problem 5.52), so symmetric problems give $\vec A$ from the flux the way Ampère's law gives $\vec B$ from the current, and the symmetry rules of [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-4|Theorem §B6.3.4]](4) fix its direction.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.1, Example 5.12, eq. (5.71); Problem 5.52*

^thm-b6-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Stokes' theorem ([[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]]): $\oint_C\vec A\cdot d\vec l = \int_{\mathcal S}(\nabla\times\vec A)\cdot d\vec a = \int_{\mathcal S}\vec B\cdot d\vec a$.

^der-b6-4-3

*Uses:* [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^def-b6-4-1|Def. §B6.4.1]], [[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]]

> [!example] Example §B6.4.1: The Vector Potential of an Infinite Solenoid
> An infinite solenoid of radius $R$ with $n$ turns per unit length carries $I$, so $\vec B = \mu_0nI\,\hat z$ inside and $\vec 0$ outside ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]]). The current extends to infinity, so (5.65) cannot be used; use [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]] instead.
>
> *Direction.* Planes through the axis reverse the azimuthal current; by [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-4|Theorem §B6.3.4]](4) the polar vector $\vec A$ is perpendicular to them, $\vec A = A_\phi(s)\,\hat\phi$, circumferential like the current: the problem is the Ampère problem of a fat wire with uniform current, with $\vec B$ in the role of the current.
>
> *Magnitude.* On a circle of radius $s$ about the axis, $\oint\vec A\cdot d\vec l = 2\pi s\,A_\phi$ equals the flux $\mu_0nI\,\pi s^2$ for $s < R$ and $\mu_0nI\,\pi R^2$ for $s > R$, so (Griffiths eqs. (5.72)–(5.73))
>
> $$
> \vec A = \frac{\mu_0nI}{2}\,s\,\hat\phi \ \ (s \le R), \qquad \vec A = \frac{\mu_0nI}{2}\,\frac{R^2}{s}\,\hat\phi \ \ (s \ge R) .
> $$
>
> *Checks.* $\nabla\cdot\vec A = \frac1s\partial_\phi A_\phi = 0$; $\nabla\times\vec A = \frac1s\partial_s(sA_\phi)\,\hat z$ is $\mu_0nI\,\hat z$ inside and $\vec 0$ outside. Outside, $\vec A \ne \vec 0$ although $\vec B = \vec 0$ there: $\vec A$ is not fixed by the local field; its circulation records the flux enclosed elsewhere.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 5.12, eqs. (5.71)–(5.73)*

^ex-b6-4-1

> [!example] Example §B6.4.2: A Wire and a Coaxial Cable in the Coulomb Gauge
> *(a) Wire.* A long straight wire of radius $R$ carries $I$ along $\hat z$, uniformly distributed. Ampère's law gives $\vec B = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\frac{s}{R^2}\hat\phi$ inside and $\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s}\hat\phi$ outside ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-2|Theorem §A8.4.2]]). A plane perpendicular to the wire reverses the current, so by [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-4|Theorem §B6.3.4]](4) $\vec A$ is perpendicular to it, and with the translation and rotation symmetries $\vec A = A_z(s)\,\hat z$; it is automatically in the Coulomb gauge. Its curl is $-A_z'(s)\,\hat\phi$, so $A_z' = -B_\phi$ (equivalently, [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]] on a rectangle in the $sz$-plane). The current reaches infinity and $\vec A$ cannot be made to vanish there; choose $\vec A(0) = \vec 0$:
>
> $$
> A_z = -\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{s^2}{R^2} \ \ (s \le R), \qquad A_z = -\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi} - \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\ln\frac sR \ \ (s \ge R) .
> $$
>
> The two forms agree at $s = R$ ($\vec A$ is continuous, [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]]), and inside $\nabla^2A_z = \frac1s\frac{d}{ds}\bigl(s\frac{dA_z}{ds}\bigr) = -\mu_0I/\pi R^2 = -\mu_0J_z$, outside $0$ (the scalar Laplacian may be used for a $\hat z$ component, [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^cau-b6-4-1|Caution]]). The logarithm, the potential of a line source in two dimensions, grows without bound: the reason why (5.65) fails for an infinite wire.
>
> *(b) Coaxial cable.* Add a thin shield of radius $R_2 > R_1 = R$ carrying the return current $-I$. Outside the cable $\vec B = \vec 0$, so $A_z$ is constant there and can now be set to zero at infinity; integrating $A_z' = -B_\phi$ inward from $R_2$,
>
> $$
> A_z = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\ln\frac{R_2}{R_1} + \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\Bigl(1 - \frac{s^2}{R_1^2}\Bigr) \ (s < R_1), \qquad A_z = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\ln\frac{R_2}{s} \ (R_1 < s < R_2), \qquad A_z = 0 \ (s > R_2) .
> $$
>
> $\vec A$ points along the inner current, and is largest on the axis. Both results agree with the instructor's solutions (part (b) is the same as the wire's potential plus that of the shield, a constant inside it and $\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\ln\frac s{R_2}$ outside, with the overall constant fixed by $\vec A\to\vec 0$).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 5.26 (solved here) · PHY 422, HW 9, Problem 3 (Vector potential); practice final, Problem 2(c)*

## Boundary conditions

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.4.4: Magnetostatic Boundary Conditions
> Across a surface carrying the surface current $\vec K$, with $\hat n$ the unit normal pointing from "below" to "above",
>
> $$
> B^\perp_{\text{above}} = B^\perp_{\text{below}}, \qquad \vec B_{\text{above}} - \vec B_{\text{below}} = \mu_0\,(\vec K\times\hat n)
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (5.74)–(5.76)): the normal component is continuous, the tangential component perpendicular to $\vec K$ jumps by $\mu_0K$, and the tangential component along $\vec K$ is continuous. In the Coulomb gauge the vector potential is continuous and its normal derivative jumps (5.77)–(5.78):
>
> $$
> \vec A_{\text{above}} = \vec A_{\text{below}}, \qquad \frac{\partial\vec A_{\text{above}}}{\partial n} - \frac{\partial\vec A_{\text{below}}}{\partial n} = -\mu_0\vec K .
> $$
>
> These are the static conditions with surface currents; for time-dependent fields at interfaces without free surface currents they are part of [[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-1|WO Theorem §B9.1.1]], and the general set is in [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-6|Theorem §B8.4.6]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.2, eqs. (5.74)–(5.78), Problem 5.33 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.9, eqs. (15.26)–(15.27)*

^thm-b6-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Normal $\vec B$.* Apply $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec a = 0$ ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]]) to a wafer-thin pillbox straddling the surface: as its height goes to zero the side wall contributes nothing, leaving $(B^\perp_{\text{above}} - B^\perp_{\text{below}})\,\Delta a = 0$.
>
> *Tangential $\vec B$.* Apply Ampère's law ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]) to a thin rectangle crossing the surface, with long sides of length $l$ just above and below. If the rectangle is perpendicular to $\vec K$, it encloses the current $Kl$ and $(B^\parallel_{\text{above}} - B^\parallel_{\text{below}})\,l = \mu_0Kl$ for the component along the long sides; if it is parallel to $\vec K$, it encloses nothing and that component is continuous. $\vec K\times\hat n$ is tangential, perpendicular to $\vec K$, of length $K$, and points the right way: for $\vec K = K\hat x$ in the plane $z = 0$, $\vec K\times\hat z = -K\hat y$, and the current sheet's field is $-\frac{\mu_0}{2}K\hat y$ above, $+\frac{\mu_0}{2}K\hat y$ below (Griffiths eq. (5.58)).
>
> *$\vec A$ continuous.* $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$ on the pillbox makes $A^\perp$ continuous; $\oint\vec A\cdot d\vec l = \Phi$ ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]]) on the thin rectangle, whose flux vanishes with its width, makes the tangential components continuous.
>
> *Normal derivative.* Take local axes with $z$ along $\hat n$ and $x$ along $\vec K$, and write $\Delta f$ for the jump of $f$. Since $\vec A$ is continuous on the surface, so are its derivatives along the surface, $\partial_x\vec A$ and $\partial_y\vec A$. From $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$: $\Delta B_y = \Delta(\partial_zA_x) - \Delta(\partial_xA_z) = \Delta(\partial_zA_x)$ must equal $-\mu_0K$; $\Delta B_x = \Delta(\partial_yA_z) - \Delta(\partial_zA_y) = -\Delta(\partial_zA_y)$ must vanish; and $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$ gives $\Delta(\partial_zA_z) = -\Delta(\partial_xA_x + \partial_yA_y) = 0$. Together, $\Delta(\partial_z\vec A) = -\mu_0K\hat x = -\mu_0\vec K$.

*Uses:* [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]], [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]], [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]], [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^def-b6-4-2|Def. §B6.4.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Charge sheets and current sheets
> At a surface charge the normal component of $\vec E$ jumps and the tangential one is continuous ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]); at a surface current it is the other way round for $\vec B$, and the potential is continuous in both cases while its normal derivative inherits the jump. Checks: at the wall of an ideal solenoid, $\vec K = nI\hat\phi$ and $\hat n = \hat s$ give $\vec B_{\text{out}} - \vec B_{\text{in}} = \mu_0nI\,\hat\phi\times\hat s = -\mu_0nI\,\hat z$, the drop from $\mu_0nI$ inside to $0$ outside (Griffiths Problem 5.32(a)); at the end disc of the terminated cable of [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^ex-b6-3-3|Example §B6.3.3]] the radial disc current switches the circling field off. In Nelson's phrasing, the normal condition holds "without any dependence on the behaviour of charges or currents at the surface"; a superconductor, which excludes $\vec B$, therefore has $B^\perp = 0$ just outside.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.2, Problem 5.32 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.9*

## Multipole expansion and the magnetic dipole

> [!definition] Definition §B6.4.3: Magnetic Dipole Moment
> The **magnetic dipole moment** of a steady current distribution confined to a bounded region is (Griffiths eq. (5.90))
>
> $$
> \vec m = \frac12\int\vec r\times\vec J(\vec r)\,d\tau .
> $$
>
> For a current loop it is (5.86)
>
> $$
> \vec m = I\int d\vec a = I\vec a, \qquad \vec a = \frac12\oint\vec r\times d\vec l ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec a$ the **vector area** of the loop; for a flat loop, $\vec a$ is the enclosed area times the normal given by the right-hand rule, and $\vec m$ is level A's moment ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^def-a7-4-1|Def. §A7.4.1]], which writes $\vec\mu = IA\hat n$). Unit: $\mathrm{A\cdot m^2}$. Level A and quantum mechanics write $\vec\mu$; Griffiths writes $\vec m$, as here.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.3, eqs. (5.86), (5.90); Problem 5.38 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 17, §17.3, eq. (17.6)*

^def-b6-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.4.5: Multipole Expansion of the Vector Potential
> Let a steady current distribution lie within $r' \le a$ of the origin. At points $r > a$ its Coulomb-gauge vector potential (5.65) is the series (Griffiths eqs. (5.79)–(5.81) for a loop; Problem 5.38(a))
>
> $$
> \vec A(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int(r')^n P_n(\cos\alpha)\,\vec J(\vec r\,')\,d\tau' ,
> $$
>
> with $\alpha$ the angle between $\vec r$ and $\vec r\,'$ and $P_n$ the Legendre polynomials. Its terms are the monopole ($\propto 1/r$), dipole ($1/r^2$), quadrupole ($1/r^3$), … contributions, and:
> 1. the **monopole term vanishes** (5.82), for every steady localized distribution;
> 2. the **dipole term** is (5.85)
>
> $$
> \vec A_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{\vec m\times\hat r}{r^2} ;
> $$
>
> 3. $\vec m$ does not depend on the choice of origin.
>
> So, unless $\vec m = \vec 0$, every localized steady current looks like a magnetic dipole from far away, and its far field is fixed by just three numbers, the components of $\vec m$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.3, eqs. (5.79)–(5.86); Problem 5.38 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 17, §17.2–17.4, eqs. (17.1)–(17.7)*

^thm-b6-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Series.* For $r' < r$, $\frac1{\mathcal R} = \frac1r\sum_n\bigl(\frac{r'}r\bigr)^nP_n(\cos\alpha)$ (5.79), the expansion of [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-4|Theorem §B3.4.4]] and [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]], uniformly convergent for $r' \le a < r$; insert it in (5.65) and integrate term by term.
>
> *1.* The $n = 0$ term is $\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}\int\vec J\,d\tau'$, and $\int\vec J\,d\tau' = \vec 0$ for steady localized currents ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]]); for a loop, $\oint d\vec l\,' = \vec 0$ (5.82).
>
> *2.* With $P_1(\cos\alpha) = \cos\alpha$ and $r'\cos\alpha = \hat r\cdot\vec r\,'$, the $n = 1$ term needs the first moments $M_{ij} = \int x_iJ_j\,d\tau$. Since $\nabla\cdot(x_ix_j\vec J) = x_iJ_j + x_jJ_i + x_ix_j\nabla\cdot\vec J$ and $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$, the divergence theorem (no current crosses a surface enclosing the source) gives $M_{ij} + M_{ji} = 0$ (Nelson eq. (17.2)): $M$ is antisymmetric. With $m_k = \frac12\epsilon_{kij}M_{ij}$, the $k$-th component of $\frac12\int\vec r\times\vec J\,d\tau$, the identity $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{klm} = \delta_{il}\delta_{jm} - \delta_{im}\delta_{jl}$ gives $\epsilon_{ijk}m_k = \frac12(M_{ij} - M_{ji}) = M_{ij}$. Hence
>
> $$
> \int(\hat r\cdot\vec r\,')\,J_j\,d\tau' = \hat r_iM_{ij} = \epsilon_{ijk}\hat r_im_k = (\vec m\times\hat r)_j ,
> $$
>
> and the $n = 1$ term is $\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^2}\,\vec m\times\hat r$. For a loop, $\vec J\,d\tau\to I\,d\vec l$ turns $\vec m$ into $\frac I2\oint\vec r\times d\vec l$. That this is $I\int d\vec a$: applying Stokes' theorem to the field $T\hat e_k$ gives $\oint T\,d\vec l = \int d\vec a\times\nabla T$ for any scalar $T$; with $T = x_i$, $\frac12\epsilon_{kij}\oint x_i\,dl_j = \frac12\epsilon_{kij}\int(d\vec a\times\hat e_i)_j = \frac12\epsilon_{kij}\epsilon_{jli}\int da_l = \int da_k$. (Griffiths reaches the same through the identity $\oint(\hat r\cdot\vec r\,')\,d\vec l\,' = -\hat r\times\int d\vec a\,'$, eq. (5.84).)
>
> *3.* Shifting the origin by $\vec d$ changes $\vec m$ by $-\frac12\vec d\times\int\vec J\,d\tau = \vec 0$. (The electric dipole moment is origin-independent only when the total charge vanishes; here the monopole always vanishes.)

> [!model] Model §B6.4.6: Pure (Point) Magnetic Dipole
> A **pure magnetic dipole** $\vec m$ at the origin is the limit of a current loop shrunk to a point while its current grows, with $\vec m = I\vec a$ held fixed. Its vector potential is exactly the dipole term, $\vec A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{\vec m\times\hat r}{r^2}$, for all $r > 0$.
>
> *Assumptions:* distances much larger than the size of the real current distribution (then the higher multipoles are negligible); a moment that does not change. *Fails:* close to a real loop, where the field looks like that of the wire (figure), and at the location of the dipole itself, where the true field contains a delta-function term ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^rem-b6-4-5|★ Remark: The field at the dipole]]). Atoms, electrons and nuclei are treated as pure magnetic dipoles.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.3 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 17, §17.3.2*

^mod-b6-4-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.4.7: The Field of a Magnetic Dipole
> For a pure dipole $\vec m = m\hat z$ at the origin, in spherical coordinates (Griffiths eqs. (5.87)–(5.88)),
>
> $$
> \vec A_{\text{dip}} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{m\sin\theta}{r^2}\,\hat\phi, \qquad \vec B_{\text{dip}} = \frac{\mu_0m}{4\pi r^3}\bigl(2\cos\theta\,\hat r + \sin\theta\,\hat\theta\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> and in coordinate-free form (5.89)
>
> $$
> \vec B_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{1}{r^3}\bigl[3(\vec m\cdot\hat r)\hat r - \vec m\bigr] .
> $$
>
> The field lines are the curves $r = r_0\sin^2\theta$. This is the far field of every localized steady current with $\vec m \ne \vec 0$, and it has exactly the form of the electric dipole field ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole|§B4.2]]) with $\vec p/\varepsilon_0 \to \mu_0\vec m$. On the axis it reduces to level A's $\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2\vec m}{z^3}$ ([[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop#^thm-a8-3-2|Theorem §A8.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.3, eqs. (5.87)–(5.89), Problem 5.34 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 17, §17.3.2*

^thm-b6-4-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $\vec m = m\hat z$, $\vec m\times\hat r = m\sin\theta\,\hat\phi$, which gives $\vec A_{\text{dip}}$. The curl of $A_\phi\hat\phi$ in spherical coordinates ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]]) has the components
>
> $$
> B_r = \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\,\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,A_\phi) = \frac{\mu_0m}{4\pi}\frac{2\cos\theta}{r^3}, \qquad B_\theta = -\frac1r\,\partial_r(rA_\phi) = \frac{\mu_0m}{4\pi}\frac{\sin\theta}{r^3} .
> $$
>
> For the coordinate-free form, $\hat z = \cos\theta\,\hat r - \sin\theta\,\hat\theta$, so $3(\vec m\cdot\hat r)\hat r - \vec m = 3m\cos\theta\,\hat r - m\cos\theta\,\hat r + m\sin\theta\,\hat\theta = m(2\cos\theta\,\hat r + \sin\theta\,\hat\theta)$. *Field lines.* For any field $A_\phi(s, z)\hat\phi$ with $s = r\sin\theta$, $A_\phi\hat\phi = (sA_\phi)\nabla\phi$, so $\vec B = \nabla(sA_\phi)\times\nabla\phi$ is perpendicular to $\nabla(sA_\phi)$: field lines run along the curves $sA_\phi = $ const. For the dipole $sA_\phi \propto \sin^2\theta/r$. On the axis ($\theta = 0$, $r = z$), $\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{z^3}\hat z$.

> [!remark] Remark: Pure and physical dipoles, magnetic and electric
> - Far away, a current loop and a pair of opposite charges have fields of the same form; up close they differ (Griffiths compares his Figs. 5.55 and 3.37): inside a loop $\vec B$ points along $\vec m$, while between the charges of an electric dipole $\vec E$ points against $\vec p$, as level A noticed ([[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop#^rem-a8-3-1|§A8.3, Remark: Dipoles everywhere]]).
> - The old "Gilbert" model pictures a magnetic dipole as two magnetic poles; it gives the right far field and, often, the right forces and torques, but not the field close to or inside the source. Magnetic dipoles are current loops (the "Ampère" model); Griffiths' advice is to use Gilbert for intuition and never for quantitative results.
> - Nelson's tensor view explains why three numbers suffice: the first moments $\int x_iJ_j\,d\tau$ form an antisymmetric tensor, which has only three independent components. Higher moments do depend on the origin; only the first nonvanishing one does not.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.4.3, §6.1.2 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 17, §17.3–17.4*

> [!example] Example §B6.4.3: A Rotating Charged Disk
> A disk of radius $R$ with uniform surface charge $\sigma$ rotates about its axis at angular velocity $\omega$, so it carries $\vec K = \sigma\omega s\,\hat\phi$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^ex-b6-1-2|Example §B6.1.2]]). Find its dipole moment and far field, the exact field on the axis, and compare.
>
> *(a) Moment.* By [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^def-b6-4-3|Def. §B6.4.3]] with $\vec J\,d\tau\to\vec K\,da$,
>
> $$
> \vec m = \frac12\int(s\hat s)\times(\sigma\omega s\,\hat\phi)\,s\,ds\,d\phi = \frac12\sigma\omega\,\hat z\int_0^{2\pi}\!d\phi\int_0^Rs^3\,ds = \frac{\pi\sigma\omega R^4}{4}\,\hat z ,
> $$
>
> the same as adding rings of current $dI = \sigma\omega s\,ds$ and moment $\pi s^2\,dI$. Far away ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-7|Theorem §B6.4.7]])
>
> $$
> \vec B \approx \frac{\mu_0\sigma\omega R^4}{16}\,\frac{3\cos\theta\,\hat r - \hat z}{r^3}, \qquad\text{on the axis } \vec B \approx \frac{\mu_0\sigma\omega R^4}{8z^3}\,\hat z .
> $$
>
> *(b) Exact field on the axis* ($z > 0$). Each ring gives the loop field (Griffiths eq. (5.41)); with $u^2 = s^2 + z^2$,
>
> $$
> B_z = \frac{\mu_0\sigma\omega}{2}\int_0^R\frac{s^3\,ds}{(s^2 + z^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\mu_0\sigma\omega}{2}\int_z^{\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}}\Bigl(1 - \frac{z^2}{u^2}\Bigr)du = \mu_0\sigma\omega\left[\frac{R^2/2 + z^2}{\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}} - z\right] ,
> $$
>
> which is $\mu_0\sigma\omega R/2$ at the centre.
>
> *(c) Comparison.* With $\varepsilon = R^2/z^2$, $\frac{R^2/2 + z^2}{\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}} = z\,(1 + \tfrac\varepsilon2)(1 + \varepsilon)^{-1/2} = z\bigl(1 + \tfrac{\varepsilon^2}8 - \tfrac{\varepsilon^3}8 + \dots\bigr)$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), so
>
> $$
> B_z = \frac{\mu_0\sigma\omega R^4}{8z^3}\Bigl(1 - \frac{R^2}{z^2} + \dots\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the dipole field, as (a) predicts. The first correction is of relative order $R^2/z^2$, not $R/z$: there is no quadrupole term, because the disk is mirror-symmetric in its own plane, which makes $B_z$ on the axis even in $z$ ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-4|Theorem §B6.3.4]]). Numerically the dipole formula is off by $11\%$ at $z = 3R$ and $4\%$ at $z = 5R$. The instructor's solution agrees with (a)–(c).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 5.37(a) and 5.48 (solved here) · PHY 422, HW 9, Problem 4 (Dipole moment)*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The field at the dipole
> The dipole formula (5.89) cannot be the whole field of a pure dipole: its average over a ball centred on the dipole vanishes (the angular integral of $3(\vec m\cdot\hat r)\hat r - \vec m$ is zero, since $\int\hat r_i\hat r_j\,d\Omega = \frac{4\pi}3\delta_{ij}$), whereas the average field over a sphere of radius $R$ due to any steady currents inside it is (Griffiths Problem 5.59, eq. (5.93))
>
> $$
> \vec B_{\text{ave}} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{2\vec m}{R^3} .
> $$
>
> *Sketch.* $\int_{\text{ball}}\nabla\times\vec A\,d\tau = -\oint\vec A\times d\vec a$; inserting (5.65) and using $\oint d\vec a/\mathcal R = \frac{4\pi}3\vec r\,'$ for $\vec r\,'$ inside the sphere (only the $\ell = 1$ term of the expansion of $1/\mathcal R$ survives the angular integral) gives $\int\vec B\,d\tau = \frac{\mu_0}3\int\vec r\,'\times\vec J\,d\tau' = \frac{2\mu_0}3\vec m$, and dividing by $\frac43\pi R^3$ gives (5.93). The missing average sits at the origin, and the true field of a pure dipole is (5.94)
>
> $$
> \vec B_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{1}{r^3}\bigl[3(\vec m\cdot\hat r)\hat r - \vec m\bigr] + \frac{2\mu_0}{3}\,\vec m\,\delta^3(\vec r) .
> $$
>
> The electric analogue has $-\frac{1}{3\varepsilon_0}\vec p\,\delta^3(\vec r)$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole|§B4.2]]), with the opposite sign: the field inside a loop points along $\vec m$, inside a charge pair against $\vec p$. The contact term gives the hyperfine splitting of the hydrogen ground state ([[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting#^thm-b8-4-5|QM Theorem §B8.4.5]]; Griffiths, footnote 29). A check: a spinning spherical shell (charge $\sigma$, radius $R$, angular velocity $\omega$) has a uniform interior field $\frac23\mu_0\sigma R\omega$ and is a pure dipole outside with $m = \frac{4\pi}3\sigma\omega R^4$ (Griffiths Example 5.11, eqs. (5.68)–(5.70), Problem 5.37(b)); (5.93) gives $\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{R^3} = \frac23\mu_0\sigma\omega R$, the interior field.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 5.59 and 5.61, eqs. (5.93)–(5.94) and footnote 29; Example 5.11*

## A dipole in an external field

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.4.8: Force, Torque and Energy of a Magnetic Dipole
> A steady current distribution with moment $\vec m$, centred at $\vec r_0$, sits in an external magnetic field $\vec B$ (made by other currents) that varies little across it.
> 1. In a **uniform** field the net force vanishes and the torque is (Griffiths eq. (6.1))
>
> $$
> \vec N = \vec m\times\vec B ,
> $$
>
> exactly, for any localized steady distribution, about any point.
> 2. In a **non-uniform** field, to leading order in the size of the distribution, the force is (6.3)
>
> $$
> \vec F = \nabla(\vec m\cdot\vec B)\big|_{\vec r_0}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \vec F = (\vec m\cdot\nabla)\vec B + \vec m\times(\nabla\times\vec B) ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec m$ held fixed under the gradient; $\vec N = \vec m\times\vec B(\vec r_0)$ is then the exact torque of a pure dipole.
> 3. Both follow from the **orientation and position energy** (Griffiths Problem 6.21, eq. (6.34))
>
> $$
> U = -\vec m\cdot\vec B ,
> $$
>
> for a dipole of fixed moment: $\vec F = -\nabla U$, and $\vec N$ turns $\vec m$ toward lower $U$.
>
> Level A had parts 1 and 3 for flat loops in uniform fields ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-2|Theorem §A7.4.2]], [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-3|Theorem §A7.4.3]]); the force in a non-uniform field is new.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.1.2, eqs. (6.1)–(6.3); Problems 6.2, 6.4, 6.21–6.22 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 17, §17.5.1, eqs. (17.13)–(17.16)*

^thm-b6-4-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put the origin at $\vec r_0$. The force is $\vec F = \int\vec J\times\vec B\,d\tau$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]). Expand the external field across the distribution, $B_k(\vec r) = B_k(\vec 0) + x_l\,\partial_lB_k(\vec 0) + \dots$ ([[§9 Taylor's Theorem for Multivariable Functions#^thm-9-2|452 Thm. §9.2]]), and use the first moments $\int x_lJ_j\,d\tau = \epsilon_{ljn}m_n$ (derivation of [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-5|Theorem §B6.4.5]]).
>
> *Force.* The zeroth-order term is $\bigl(\int\vec J\,d\tau\bigr)\times\vec B(\vec 0) = \vec 0$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]]); in a uniform field this is all. The first-order term is
>
> $$
> F_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\,\partial_lB_k\int x_lJ_j\,d\tau = \epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{ljn}\,m_n\,\partial_lB_k = (\delta_{kn}\delta_{il} - \delta_{kl}\delta_{in})\,m_n\,\partial_lB_k = \partial_i(\vec m\cdot\vec B) - m_i\,\nabla\cdot\vec B ,
> $$
>
> and $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]]). The product rule $\nabla(\vec m\cdot\vec B) = (\vec m\cdot\nabla)\vec B + \vec m\times(\nabla\times\vec B)$ for constant $\vec m$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]) gives the second form (Nelson's eq. (17.14) is the same result).
>
> *Torque* in a uniform field: $\vec N = \int\vec r\times(\vec J\times\vec B)\,d\tau = \int\bigl[\vec J\,(\vec r\cdot\vec B) - \vec B\,(\vec r\cdot\vec J)\bigr]d\tau$. The first term is $B_l\int x_lJ_i\,d\tau = \epsilon_{lin}B_lm_n = (\vec m\times\vec B)_i$; the second is $B_i\int x_lJ_l\,d\tau = B_i\epsilon_{lln}m_n = 0$. With no net force, the torque is the same about every point.
>
> *Energy.* $-\nabla U = \nabla(\vec m\cdot\vec B) = \vec F$. Turning $\vec m$ through a small angle $\delta\theta$ about a unit axis $\hat k$ changes it by $\delta\theta\,\hat k\times\vec m$ and $U$ by $-\delta\theta\,(\hat k\times\vec m)\cdot\vec B = -\delta\theta\,\hat k\cdot(\vec m\times\vec B) = -\hat k\cdot\vec N\,\delta\theta$: the torque does work $\hat k\cdot\vec N\,\delta\theta = -\delta U$, as for level A's loop.

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]], [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]], [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-5|Theorem §B6.4.5]], [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]], [[§9 Taylor's Theorem for Multivariable Functions#^thm-9-2|452 Thm. §9.2]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Which energy, and which force formula
> - $U = -\vec m\cdot\vec B$ assumes a moment of fixed size: it counts the mechanical work of moving and turning the dipole into place, not the energy needed to keep its current constant while the flux through it changes (Griffiths Problem 6.21; Nelson, after his eq. (17.15)). That bookkeeping is induction ([[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-2|Theorem §B8.3.2]]).
> - $\nabla(\vec m\cdot\vec B)$ and $(\vec m\cdot\nabla)\vec B$ agree only where $\nabla\times\vec B = \vec 0$, that is, where no external current flows through the dipole (Griffiths Problem 6.5). For electric dipoles the two forms are always equal, since $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ in statics; the Gilbert pole model gives $(\vec m\cdot\nabla)\vec B$ and is right only "in the absence of external currents".
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.1.2 and Problems 6.5, 6.21 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 17, §17.5.1*

> [!remark] Remark: Where the force comes from, and atoms
> - *A ring above a solenoid.* In the fringing field near the end of a solenoid (figure in [[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^ex-b6-2-2|Example §B6.2.2]]) $\vec B$ has a radial component, and a coaxial ring of radius $R$ carrying $I$ feels $\int I\,d\vec l\times\vec B$, of size $F = 2\pi IRB\cos\theta$ along the axis, with $B\cos\theta$ the radial component (Griffiths eq. (6.2)). The dipole formula gives the same: near the axis $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ in cylindrical coordinates makes $B_s \approx -\frac s2\,\partial_zB_z$, so $F_z = m\,\partial_zB_z = \pi R^2I\,\partial_zB_z = -2\pi RI\,B_s(R)$.
> - *Atoms.* Atoms, electrons and nuclei carry magnetic moments: orbital, $\vec\mu = -\frac{e}{2m_e}\vec L$ ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-1|QM Theorem §A5.2.1]]; the classical ratio $q/2M$ is Griffiths Problem 5.58), and spin ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^def-b6-2-1|QM Def. §B6.2.1]]). The torque makes them precess ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-4|QM Theorem §B6.2.4]]); the force $\nabla(\vec m\cdot\vec B)$ on a moment that cannot freely reorient is the Stern–Gerlach effect ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-4|QM Theorem §A5.2.4]], [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-5|QM Theorem §B6.2.5]]; Nelson, §17.5.1). The aligning torque on atomic moments is the basis of paramagnetism ([[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents|§B7.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.1.2, eq. (6.2); Problem 5.58 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 17, §17.5*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Existence of $\vec A$ is the Poincaré lemma for 2-forms ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-12|452 Prop. §22.12]]; [[Poincaré Lemma]]); in the language of forms, $\vec B$ is a closed 2-form and $\vec A$ a 1-form with $d\vec A = \vec B$ (Nelson, §15.10εc). The Helmholtz theorem behind both potentials is [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]].
> - The potential of a straight wire, $A_z \propto -\ln s$, is the fundamental solution of the two-dimensional Laplacian ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-5|452 Thm. §17.5]]).
> - The multipole series uses the Legendre polynomials ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^def-b3-4-1|Def. §B3.4.1]]; [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^def-b5-2-3|QM Def. §B5.2.3]]) and term-by-term integration of a uniformly convergent series; the force uses the first-order Taylor expansion of $\vec B$ ([[§9 Taylor's Theorem for Multivariable Functions#^thm-9-2|452 Thm. §9.2]]).
> - $\vec A$ in mechanics: the charged-particle Lagrangian and Hamiltonian ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^rem-b6-2-6|CM Remark: Velocity-dependent potentials]], [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^rem-b7-1-5|CM Remark: The charged particle]]).
