Which local laws does the field of steady currents obey? Level A knew two facts in integral form: the magnetic flux through every closed surface is zero ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^rem-a7-1-3|§A7.1, Remark: No magnetic charges]]), and Ampère's law ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-1|Theorem §A8.4.1]]), which it proved only for straight wires and promised in general for level B. Here both are derived from the Biot–Savart law ([[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^law-b6-2-2|Law §B6.2.2]]) in differential form, $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ and $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$, and shown to determine $\vec B$ completely. The other half of the section makes the symmetry arguments of level A rigorous: because $\vec B$ is an axial vector, a mirror symmetry of the currents forces the direction of $\vec B$, which is how PHY 422 settled the direction of the field before applying Ampère's law.

## Divergence and curl

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.1: The Divergence of B Vanishes
> The magnetic field of any steady current distribution satisfies (Griffiths eq. (5.50))
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec B = 0, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \oint_{\mathcal S}\vec B\cdot d\vec a = 0 \ \text{ for every closed surface } \mathcal S .
> $$
>
> There are no point sources of $\vec B$: field lines neither begin nor end; they close on themselves, run off to infinity, or wind in endless tangles (Griffiths, footnote 17).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.2, eqs. (5.47)–(5.50); §5.3.4*

^thm-b6-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write the Biot–Savart law for a volume current (5.47), $\vec B(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\vec J(\vec r\,')\times\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\,d\tau'$, where $\vec B$ depends on the unprimed coordinates, $\vec J$ on the primed ones, and $\nabla$ acts on the unprimed. Taking $\nabla\cdot$ inside the integral and using the product rule $\nabla\cdot(\vec a\times\vec b) = \vec b\cdot(\nabla\times\vec a) - \vec a\cdot(\nabla\times\vec b)$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]),
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\Bigl(\vec J\times\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\Bigr) = \frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec J) - \vec J\cdot\Bigl(\nabla\times\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> Griffiths eq. (5.49). The first curl vanishes because $\vec J(\vec r\,')$ does not depend on $\vec r$; the second because $\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2 = -\nabla(1/\mathcal R)$ is a gradient, whose curl is zero. Hence $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$. The integral form follows from the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]). (The step under the integral sign, where the integrand is singular at $\vec r\,' = \vec r$, is avoided by the route through the vector potential, $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$ with $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A) = 0$: [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]].)

^der-b6-3-1

*Uses:* [[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^law-b6-2-2|Law §B6.2.2]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.2: Ampère's Law
> The magnetic field of a steady current distribution satisfies, in differential form (Griffiths eq. (5.56)),
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J ,
> $$
>
> and in integral form (5.57), for every closed loop $C$ and any surface $\mathcal S$ bounded by it,
>
> $$
> \oint_C\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0I_{\text{enc}}, \qquad I_{\text{enc}} = \int_{\mathcal S}\vec J\cdot d\vec a ,
> $$
>
> with the orientation of the right-hand rule (fingers along $C$, thumb along positive current). This is [[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-1|Theorem §A8.4.1]] for arbitrary steady currents, not only straight wires.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.2–5.3.3, eqs. (5.51)–(5.57)*

^thm-b6-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the curl of (5.47) under the integral. The product rule $\nabla\times(\vec a\times\vec b) = (\vec b\cdot\nabla)\vec a - (\vec a\cdot\nabla)\vec b + \vec a(\nabla\cdot\vec b) - \vec b(\nabla\cdot\vec a)$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]), with the terms that differentiate $\vec J(\vec r\,')$ dropped, gives (5.52)
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\Bigl(\vec J\times\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\Bigr) = \vec J\Bigl(\nabla\cdot\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\Bigr) - (\vec J\cdot\nabla)\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2} .
> $$
>
> *First term.* $\nabla\cdot(\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2) = 4\pi\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,')$ (5.53), the divergence of the point-charge field ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]); integrated against $\vec J(\vec r\,')$ it gives $\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,4\pi\vec J(\vec r) = \mu_0\vec J(\vec r)$.
>
> *Second term.* $\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2$ depends only on $\vec r - \vec r\,'$, so $\partial/\partial x = -\partial/\partial x'$ on it and $-(\vec J\cdot\nabla)\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2} = (\vec J\cdot\nabla')\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}$ (5.54). Its $x$ component, by the product rule for $\nabla'\cdot(f\vec J)$, is
>
> $$
> (\vec J\cdot\nabla')\frac{x - x'}{\mathcal R^3} = \nabla'\cdot\Bigl[\frac{x - x'}{\mathcal R^3}\,\vec J\Bigr] - \frac{x - x'}{\mathcal R^3}\,(\nabla'\cdot\vec J) .
> $$
>
> For steady currents $\nabla'\cdot\vec J = 0$ ([[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]]), and the divergence integrates, by the divergence theorem, to $\oint\frac{x - x'}{\mathcal R^3}\vec J\cdot d\vec a'$ over the boundary of a volume containing all the current (5.55), where $\vec J = \vec 0$. So the second term contributes nothing and $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$. (For currents reaching to infinity the surface term still typically vanishes, with more care: Griffiths, footnote 14.)
>
> *Integral form.* Integrate over $\mathcal S$ and apply Stokes' theorem ([[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]]): $\oint_C\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \int_{\mathcal S}(\nabla\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec a = \mu_0\int_{\mathcal S}\vec J\cdot d\vec a$. Stokes' orientation convention is the right-hand rule.

> [!remark] Remark: What level B adds
> - *Layers.* $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec a = 0$ was, at level A, a reading of the field-line pictures ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^def-a7-1-2|Def. §A7.1.2]]); Ampère's law was a theorem proved for infinite straight wires and assumed for everything else ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^der-a8-4-1|§A8.4, Derivation]]). Here both are theorems of the Biot–Savart law for every steady current distribution. In the dynamic theory ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]) $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ is kept as one of Maxwell's equations, a principle valid also for changing fields, while Ampère's law gains the displacement current.
> - *The straight-wire shortcut.* Griffiths first gets $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ in a few lines from level A's argument (circulation $\mu_0I$ around any loop enclosing an infinite straight wire, eq. (5.44)) and Stokes' theorem, and calls this derivation "seriously flawed": most current distributions cannot be built from infinite straight wires. The general proof above uses only the Biot–Savart law and the delta function.
> - *In words:* $\vec E$ diverges from charges and does not curl; $\vec B$ does not diverge and curls around currents (Griffiths, Fig. 5.44). $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ is the absence of magnetic charge: if monopoles existed, $\vec B$ would get a source term like $\vec E$. None has been found; one reported detection (1982) was never reproduced, and Nelson describes how observed galactic magnetic fields bound their abundance.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.1, eqs. (5.44)–(5.46); §5.3.4 and footnote 18 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.10εa*

^rem-b6-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Which surface, and why only statics
> - *Any surface.* $I_{\text{enc}}$ must not depend on which surface $\mathcal S$ spans the loop (Griffiths Problem 5.19). Two such surfaces together bound a closed region, and the difference of the two fluxes is $\oint\vec J\cdot d\vec a$ over its boundary, which vanishes for steady currents ([[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]]).
> - *Only statics.* The divergence of a curl is zero ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]), so $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ forces $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$. If charge accumulates, the continuity equation ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]) gives $\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\partial\rho/\partial t \ne 0$, and Ampère's law in this form must fail (Griffiths Problem 5.21). The two surfaces spanning the wire to a charging capacitor are level A's version of the same contradiction ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^thm-a12-1-2|Theorem §A12.1.2]]); the cure is Maxwell's displacement current ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-1|Principle §A12.1.1]], [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-1|Theorem §B8.4.1]]). The other three equations have no such defect: $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec E) = 0$ is consistent with $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$, and $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ and Gauss's law involve no curl.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 5.19 and 5.21 (solved here) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.5.5*

## The field equations of magnetostatics

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.3: The Field Equations Determine B
> Let $\vec J$ be a steady current distribution confined to a bounded region. Then the Biot–Savart field is the only vector field with
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec B = 0, \qquad \nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J, \qquad \vec B\to\vec 0 \ \text{ far from all currents.}
> $$
>
> These **Maxwell equations for magnetostatics** are therefore equivalent to the Biot–Savart law plus superposition, as $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ are to Coulomb's law ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]). With the Lorentz force law ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]) they are the complete theory of electro- and magnetostatics. For currents extending to infinity (planes, infinite solenoids) the condition at infinity fails, and symmetry has to take its place (Griffiths, footnote 16).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.4 and footnote 16 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.5*

^thm-b6-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Existence.* The Biot–Savart field satisfies both equations ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]], [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]), and it tends to zero far away: at distance $r$ much larger than the region of the currents, $\mathcal R \approx r$ and each component is bounded by $\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^2}\int|\vec J|\,d\tau'$.
>
> *Uniqueness.* If $\vec B_1$ and $\vec B_2$ both satisfy the three conditions, their difference $\vec D$ has $\nabla\cdot\vec D = 0$, $\nabla\times\vec D = \vec 0$ and $\vec D\to\vec 0$ at infinity. Then, in Cartesian components, $\nabla^2\vec D = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec D) - \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec D) = \vec 0$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]): each component is a harmonic function on all of space. A harmonic function takes its maximum and minimum over a ball on the boundary sphere ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]); letting the sphere grow, the boundary values tend to zero, so each component is zero everywhere. This is the Helmholtz theorem of [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]] in the case at hand.

> [!remark] Remark: Electrostatics and magnetostatics compared
> | | electrostatics | magnetostatics |
> |---|---|---|
> | source | charge density $\rho$ | steady current density $\vec J$, with $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$ |
> | starting law | Coulomb ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-1\|Theorem §B2.1.1]]) | Biot–Savart ([[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^law-b6-2-2\|Law §B6.2.2]]) |
> | divergence | $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ (Gauss) | $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ |
> | curl | $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ | $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ (Ampère) |
> | tool for symmetric sources | Gauss's law | Ampère's law |
> | potential | scalar $V$, $\vec E = -\nabla V$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1\|Theorem §B2.2.1]]) | vector $\vec A$, $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$ ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^def-b6-4-1\|Def. §B6.4.1]]) |
>
> Coulomb's law leads to Gauss's law as Biot–Savart leads to Ampère's. Electric forces are normally enormous compared with magnetic ones: between moving charges the ratio is of order $v^2/c^2$ (Griffiths Problems 5.13, 5.17; level A's [[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^ex-a8-1-2|Example §A8.1.2]]). Magnetism is noticeable because a wire pours huge amounts of charge past a point while staying neutral: the electric fields of carriers and lattice cancel and the small magnetic effect is left alone.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.4*

## Mirror symmetry

![[ph-em-b6-3-1.svg]]
*Reflection in a mirror plane (seen edge-on). A polar vector (left) keeps its component parallel to the mirror and reverses its normal component; an axial vector (right) does the opposite. A field that equals its own mirror image must therefore, on the mirror itself, lie in the mirror if it is polar and be perpendicular to it if it is axial.*

> [!definition] Definition §B6.3.1: Reflections; Polar and Axial Vectors
> The **reflection** $\sigma$ in a plane $\Pi$ with unit normal $\hat n$ through a point $\vec r_0$ maps $\vec r\mapsto\sigma(\vec r) = \vec r - 2[(\vec r - \vec r_0)\cdot\hat n]\,\hat n$. Its action on displacements is the linear map $R\vec v = \vec v - 2(\vec v\cdot\hat n)\hat n$: orthogonal, with $R^2 = 1$ and $\det R = -1$; it keeps components parallel to $\Pi$ and reverses the normal component, and $\sigma(\vec r) - \sigma(\vec r\,') = R(\vec r - \vec r\,')$. The mirror image of a vector field $\vec V$ is
>
> $$
> \vec V^\sigma\bigl(\sigma(\vec r)\bigr) = R\,\vec V(\vec r) \quad\text{(polar vector)}, \qquad \vec V^\sigma\bigl(\sigma(\vec r)\bigr) = -R\,\vec V(\vec r) \quad\text{(axial vector, pseudovector)}.
> $$
>
> Velocities, forces, $\vec J$, $\vec K$, $\vec E$ and the vector potential $\vec A$ (in the Coulomb gauge: part 4 of Theorem §B6.3.4; a gauge transformation can spoil any symmetry of $\vec A$) are polar; $\vec B$, angular velocity, angular momentum, torque and the magnetic moment $\vec m$ are axial. A current distribution is **mirror-symmetric** in $\Pi$ if $\vec J^\sigma = \vec J$, that is, $\vec J(\sigma(\vec r)) = R\vec J(\vec r)$ (the reflection leaves it unchanged), and **mirror-antisymmetric** if $\vec J^\sigma = -\vec J$ (the reflection reverses it).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.2, footnote 2 · PHY 422, HW 8 and HW 9 solutions, practice final solutions · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.2*

^def-b6-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.4: Mirror Symmetry of the Currents Fixes the Direction of B
> Let $\vec J$ be a steady current distribution and $\sigma$ the reflection in a plane $\Pi$.
> 1. The field of the mirrored currents is the mirror image of the field, as an axial vector: $\vec B[\vec J^\sigma] = \vec B[\vec J]^\sigma$.
> 2. If $\vec J$ is mirror-symmetric in $\Pi$, then $\vec B(\sigma(\vec r)) = -R\vec B(\vec r)$; at every point of $\Pi$, **$\vec B$ is perpendicular to $\Pi$**.
> 3. If $\vec J$ is mirror-antisymmetric in $\Pi$, then $\vec B(\sigma(\vec r)) = R\vec B(\vec r)$; at every point of $\Pi$, **$\vec B$ lies in $\Pi$**.
> 4. The vector potential $\vec A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\vec J/\mathcal R\,d\tau'$ (Griffiths eq. (5.65), [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]]) follows the polar rule: on $\Pi$ it lies in $\Pi$ for symmetric currents and is perpendicular to $\Pi$ for antisymmetric ones.
>
> For currents extending to infinity, apply the theorem to the field fixed by the field equations and the symmetries ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-3|Theorem §B6.3.3]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 8, Problem 4 and HW 9, Problems 2–3 (instructor's solutions); practice final, Problems 2(c) and 3(a) (solutions) · Griffiths Example 5.10 (the special case of a toroid, by pairing current elements)*

^thm-b6-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *A lemma.* For an orthogonal $R$ and any vectors, $(R\vec a)\times(R\vec b) = (\det R)\,R(\vec a\times\vec b)$. Indeed, for every $\vec c$, $\bigl[(R\vec a)\times(R\vec b)\bigr]\cdot R\vec c = \det[R\vec a, R\vec b, R\vec c] = \det R\,\det[\vec a,\vec b,\vec c] = \det R\,(\vec a\times\vec b)\cdot\vec c = \det R\,\bigl[R(\vec a\times\vec b)\bigr]\cdot R\vec c$, using that $R$ preserves dot products; $R\vec c$ runs through all vectors.
>
> *1.* In the Biot–Savart law for the mirrored currents at the point $\sigma(\vec r)$, substitute $\vec r\,'' = \sigma(\vec r\,')$; a reflection preserves volume, so $d\tau'' = d\tau'$. Then $\vec J^\sigma(\sigma(\vec r\,')) = R\vec J(\vec r\,')$ and $\sigma(\vec r) - \sigma(\vec r\,') = R\vec{\mathcal R}$, of the same length $\mathcal R$, so by the lemma with $\det R = -1$
>
> $$
> \vec B[\vec J^\sigma](\sigma(\vec r)) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{(R\vec J)\times(R\vec{\mathcal R})}{\mathcal R^3}\,d\tau' = -R\,\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vec J\times\vec{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^3}\,d\tau' = -R\,\vec B[\vec J](\vec r) .
> $$
>
> *2 and 3.* If $\vec J^\sigma = \pm\vec J$, then by linearity $\vec B[\vec J^\sigma] = \pm\vec B[\vec J]$, and part 1 gives $\vec B(\sigma(\vec r)) = \mp R\vec B(\vec r)$. On $\Pi$, $\sigma(\vec r) = \vec r$. In case 2, $\vec B = -R\vec B$: the components parallel to $\Pi$ equal their own negatives and vanish, and only the normal component survives. In case 3, $\vec B = R\vec B$: the normal component vanishes.
>
> *4.* The same substitution in $\int\vec J/\mathcal R\,d\tau'$, with no cross product, gives $\vec A[\vec J^\sigma](\sigma(\vec r)) = R\vec A[\vec J](\vec r)$; on $\Pi$, $\vec A = \pm R\vec A$, and the conclusions are reversed.

^der-b6-3-4

*Uses:* [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^def-b6-3-1|Def. §B6.3.1]], [[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^law-b6-2-2|Law §B6.2.2]], [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]], [[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]

> [!remark] Remark: How to use Ampère's law
> 1. *Symmetry first.* Translations and rotations that leave the currents unchanged fix what $\vec B$ can depend on; mirror planes (Theorem §B6.3.4) fix its direction. Each point should lie on a symmetry plane. For an ideal solenoid, planes through the axis reverse the azimuthal current, so $\vec B$ lies in them (no $\hat\varphi$ component), and planes perpendicular to the axis leave it unchanged, so $\vec B$ is perpendicular to them (no radial component): $\vec B = B_z(s)\hat z$. Griffiths reaches the same conclusion by reversing the current and turning the solenoid over (Example 5.9). A real helical winding is chiral, not mirror-symmetric: its net current along the axis adds the weak circling field of a straight wire ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^cau-a8-5-1|§A8.5, Caution]]).
> 2. *A loop on which $\vec B$ is constant or perpendicular,* so that $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l$ is a few products $Bl$. This works for Griffiths' four geometries: infinite lines, infinite planes, infinite solenoids, toroids.
> 3. *A reference value.* Ampère's law gives differences of $B$; the constant comes from $\vec B\to\vec 0$ far away or from a symmetry. For two coaxial solenoids with opposite currents (radii $R_1 < R_2$, $n_1$ and $n_2$ turns per length; PHY 422, HW 9, Problem 1; Griffiths Problem 5.16), a rectangle from the axis out to radius $s$ gives $h\,[B_z(0) - B_z(s)] = \mu_0I_{\text{enc}}$, and $B_z\to0$ outside fixes $B_z(0)$:
>
> $$
> \vec B = \mu_0(n_1 - n_2)I\,\hat z \ (s < R_1), \qquad -\mu_0n_2I\,\hat z \ (R_1 < s < R_2), \qquad \vec 0 \ (s > R_2),
> $$
>
> with $\hat z$ the direction of the inner solenoid's own field, the superposition of two ideal solenoids ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]]), as in the instructor's solution.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.3, Examples 5.8–5.10 and Problem 5.16 · PHY 422, HW 9, Problem 1 (Coaxial solenoids)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B6.3.1: A Slab of Current
> A slab $-L < z < L$, infinite in $x$ and $y$, carries the uniform current density $\vec J = J\hat x$. Find $\vec B$ above, inside and below it.
>
> *Symmetry.* Every plane $y = $ const is a mirror plane, and $\vec J$, parallel to it, is unchanged by the reflection: by [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-4|Theorem §B6.3.4]](2) $\vec B$ is perpendicular to it, $\vec B\parallel\hat y$, at every point (each point lies on such a plane). Translation invariance in $x$ and $y$ leaves $\vec B = B_y(z)\,\hat y$. The midplane $z = 0$ is also a mirror plane for the slab with $\vec J$ parallel to it, so $\vec B(x, y, -z) = -R\vec B(x, y, z)$ with $R$ reversing the $z$ component: $B_y(-z) = -B_y(z)$, and $B_y(0) = 0$.
>
> *Ampère's law* on a rectangle in a plane $x = $ const, with one side of length $\ell$ along $\hat y$ in the midplane and the opposite side at height $z$, traversed so that the positive current is $+\hat x$: the vertical sides are perpendicular to $\vec B$, and
>
> $$
> \ell\,B_y(0) - \ell\,B_y(z) = \mu_0I_{\text{enc}} = \mu_0J\ell\times\begin{cases} z, & |z| < L,\\ L, & z > L,\\ -L, & z < -L, \end{cases}
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> \vec B = -\mu_0Jz\,\hat y \ \ (|z| < L), \qquad \vec B = -\mu_0JL\,\hat y \ \ (z > L), \qquad \vec B = +\mu_0JL\,\hat y \ \ (z < -L) .
> $$
>
> *Checks.* Inside, $(\nabla\times\vec B)_x = -\partial_zB_y = \mu_0J$, Ampère's law in differential form ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]); $\vec B$ is continuous at $z = \pm L$, as it must be with no surface current there ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]]); and for a thin slab carrying $K = 2LJ$ the outside field is $\mu_0K/2$, the current sheet of level A ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^ex-a8-4-3|Example §A8.4.3]]; Griffiths eq. (5.58)), independent of the distance. The instructor's solution gives the same field.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 5.15 (solved here) · PHY 422, HW 8, Problem 4 (Slab of current)*

> [!example] Example §B6.3.2: A Toroidal Coil of Any Cross-Section
> A wire is wound uniformly and tightly ($N$ turns, current $I$) around a ring whose cross-section has any shape, the same all the way around; each turn is a plane closed loop lying in a half-plane through the symmetry axis $z$. Show that $\vec B$ is circumferential, inside and outside the coil.
>
> *Symmetry.* Take any point and the plane $\Pi$ through it and the $z$ axis, at azimuth $\varphi_0$. Reflection in $\Pi$ maps the turn at $\varphi_0 + \delta$ onto the turn at $\varphi_0 - \delta$, and the current in the turns has only $s$ and $z$ components, which lie parallel to every such plane and are kept by the reflection: the coil's current is mirror-symmetric in $\Pi$. By [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-4|Theorem §B6.3.4]](2), $\vec B$ is perpendicular to $\Pi$ at the point, that is, along $\hat\varphi$. Rotation symmetry about $z$ then gives
>
> $$
> \vec B = B_\varphi(s, z)\,\hat\varphi ,
> $$
>
> whatever the shape of the cross-section. Griffiths proves the same by pairing each current element at azimuth $\varphi'$ with the one at $-\varphi'$ and showing that their $x$ and $z$ contributions cancel in the Biot–Savart integral (Example 5.10): that pairing *is* the reflection.
>
> *Ampère's law* on the circle of radius $s$ about the axis at height $z$: $2\pi s\,B_\varphi = \mu_0I_{\text{enc}}$, with $I_{\text{enc}} = NI$ if the circle runs inside the coil and $0$ otherwise (it encloses no turn, or every turn twice in opposite directions). Hence (Griffiths eq. (5.60))
>
> $$
> \vec B = \frac{\mu_0NI}{2\pi s}\,\hat\varphi \ \ \text{inside the coil}, \qquad \vec B = \vec 0 \ \ \text{outside} .
> $$
>
> Level A obtained this result assuming the direction of $\vec B$ ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-4|Theorem §A8.5.4]]); the mirror argument proves it, for any cross-section. A real toroid wound as one helix also circulates the current $I$ once around the axis; that loop is not mirror-symmetric in $\Pi$ and adds a small field of a single ring ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^cau-a8-5-2|§A8.5, Caution]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 5.10, eq. (5.60) · PHY 422, HW 9, Problem 2 (Symmetries of a toroidal coil)*

> [!example] Example §B6.3.3: A Terminated Coaxial Cable
> A coaxial cable along the $z$ axis, with a solid inner wire of radius $R_1$ and a thin outer shield of radius $R_2$, ends at $z = 0$, where a thin conducting disc of radius $R_2$ is soldered across it. A steady current $I$ flows up the wire (uniformly over its section, $z < 0$), outward through the disc and back down the shield. Which symmetries remain, and does Ampère's law alone give $\vec B$?
>
> *Symmetry.* The cable is no longer invariant under translations along $z$, but it is under rotations about $z$, and every plane through the $z$ axis is a mirror plane in which the current (axial in the wire and shield, radial in the disc) is unchanged. By [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-4|Theorem §B6.3.4]](2), $\vec B$ is perpendicular to all these planes:
>
> $$
> \vec B = B_\varphi(s, z)\,\hat\varphi .
> $$
>
> *Ampère's law* on the circle of radius $s$ at height $z$ gives $2\pi s\,B_\varphi(s, z) = \mu_0I_{\text{enc}}(s, z)$, and $I_{\text{enc}}$ can be computed for every circle, through the flat disc it bounds. So yes: once the ansatz holds, Ampère's law determines $\vec B$ everywhere, as the instructor's solution concludes. Carrying it out:
>
> $$
> z < 0:\quad B_\varphi = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s}\times\begin{cases} s^2/R_1^2, & s < R_1,\\ 1, & R_1 < s < R_2,\\ 0, & s > R_2; \end{cases} \qquad\qquad z > 0:\quad \vec B = \vec 0 ,
> $$
>
> since no current crosses a plane above the disc. Below the end the field is that of an infinite coaxial cable ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^ex-a8-4-2|Example §A8.4.2]] with a thin shield); beyond it there is none. *Checks.* A cup-shaped surface on the same circle at $z > 0$, dipping below the disc, is crossed upward through its bottom by the wire's current $I s^2/R_1^2$ (or $I$) and outward through its wall by the same current flowing out along the disc, which counts negative for the cup's inward-pointing normal: net zero, as it must be ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^rem-b6-3-2|Remark: Which surface]]). And the jump of $\vec B$ across the disc is exactly the one its radial surface current $K = I_{\text{enc}}(s)/2\pi s$ requires ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]]): $\vec B_{\text{above}} - \vec B_{\text{below}} = \mu_0\vec K\times\hat z = -\mu_0K\,\hat\varphi$.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, practice final, Problem 3(a) (symmetry and ansatz as in the instructor's solution; the field worked out here)*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The end face of a solenoid
> Mirror symmetry gives an exact result that Ampère's law cannot: on the end face of a semi-infinite ideal solenoid (radius $R$, occupying $z > 0$) the axial field is
>
> $$
> B_z = \tfrac12\mu_0nI \ \ (s < R), \qquad B_z = 0 \ \ (s > R),
> $$
>
> at every radius, not only on the axis. Reflect the solenoid in the plane $z = 0$: the azimuthal current is parallel to the mirror and keeps its sense, so the image is the semi-infinite solenoid occupying $z < 0$, and together they form an infinite one. By [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-4|Theorem §B6.3.4]](1) the image's field on the plane is $-R$ times the original's: equal normal components, opposite radial ones. The sum is the infinite solenoid's field, $\mu_0nI\,\hat z$ inside and zero outside ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]]), so each half supplies exactly half of $B_z$, and their radial components cancel. The computed finite solenoid of [[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^ex-b6-2-2|Example §B6.2.2]] shows $B_z = 0.493\,\mu_0nI$ on its end faces both on the axis and at $s = 0.9R$.

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The differential laws come from the integral ones and back by the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]) and Stokes' theorem ([[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]]), with the curl as defined in [[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^def-16-5|452 Def. §16.5]].
> - Outside a straight wire $\vec B$ is curl-free but not a gradient on the region around the wire, which has a hole: the closed-but-not-exact form of [[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-11|452 Prop. §22.11]] (level A: [[§A8.4 Ampère's Law|§A8.4, Connections]]). This is why no single-valued magnetic scalar potential exists around currents ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^rem-b6-4-2|§B6.4, Remark]]).
> - The lemma $(R\vec a)\times(R\vec b) = (\det R)\,R(\vec a\times\vec b)$ uses the multiplicativity of the determinant ([[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]); axial vectors are, more properly, antisymmetric tensors (Nelson, §15.10εb).
