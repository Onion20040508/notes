Which magnetic field does a given distribution of steady currents produce? Level A answered for current elements with the Biot–Savart law ([[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]]) and applied it to straight wires, arcs, loops on their axes and solenoids on their axes ([[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents|§A8.2]], [[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop|§A8.3]], [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-2|Theorem §A8.5.2]]). Level B first makes "steady" precise, which defines magnetostatics, then states the law for line, surface and volume currents in the separation-vector notation of electrostatics, with its range of validity: it is the empirical starting point of magnetostatics, as Coulomb's law is of electrostatics, and it is *not* a law for single moving charges. The examples generalize level A's hexagon to any polygon and compute a field that has no closed form, that of a finite solenoid away from its axis.

## Steady currents

> [!definition] Definition §B6.2.1: Steady Current; Magnetostatics
> A **steady current** is a flow of charge that has been going on forever without change and without charge piling up anywhere. **Magnetostatics** is the regime (Griffiths eq. (5.32))
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial\vec J}{\partial t} = \vec 0 \qquad \text{at all places and all times,}
> $$
>
> in which steady currents produce magnetic fields constant in time. Like electrostatics, it is an idealization; it describes slowly varying currents well, household alternating current included.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.2.1, eq. (5.32) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.5.1*

^def-b6-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.1: Steady Currents Are Divergence-Free
> In magnetostatics (Griffiths eq. (5.33))
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0, \qquad \oint_{\mathcal S}\vec J\cdot d\vec a = 0 \ \text{ for every closed surface } \mathcal S .
> $$
>
> Consequently the current is the same through every cross-section of a wire, steady currents close on themselves or run to infinity, and a single moving point charge is never a steady current.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.2.1, eq. (5.33)*

^thm-b6-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $\partial\rho/\partial t = 0$ into the continuity equation ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]); the integral form follows by the divergence theorem. For a piece of wire between two cross-sections the closed surface consists of the two sections and the wire's side, through which nothing flows, so the current in equals the current out, the result of [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]. If a current line ended at a point, a small sphere around the end would have nonzero net outflow. A point charge moving past a fixed point makes $\rho$ there jump from zero to nonzero and back, so $\partial\rho/\partial t \ne 0$.

^der-b6-2-1

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]], [[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why magnetostatics starts with extended sources
> Electrostatics was built from a single point charge at rest and superposition ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]). Magnetostatics cannot start that way: a moving point charge is not a steady current and does not produce a static field (Theorem §B6.2.1). One has to begin with extended current distributions, which is why the arguments of this chapter are "bound to be more cumbersome" (Griffiths). Nelson's "river of charge" picture makes the same point: magnetostatics treats the discrete carriers as a continuous, steadily flowing fluid.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.2.1 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.5.1*

^rem-b6-2-1

## The law

> [!law] Law §B6.2.2: Biot–Savart Law
> A steady line current $I$ produces at the point $\vec r$ the magnetic field (Griffiths eq. (5.34))
>
> $$
> \vec B(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vec I\times\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\,dl' = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,I\int\frac{d\vec l\,'\times\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2} ,
> $$
>
> integrated along the wire in the direction of the current, with $\vec{\mathcal R} = \vec r - \vec r\,'$ the separation vector from the source point $\vec r\,'$ to the field point ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]). For surface and volume currents (5.42)
>
> $$
> \vec B(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vec K(\vec r\,')\times\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\,da', \qquad \vec B(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vec J(\vec r\,')\times\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\,d\tau' .
> $$
>
> Fields of several sources add (superposition). The constant is the permeability of free space, $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{N/A^2}$ (5.35) ([[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^def-a8-1-1|Def. §A8.1.1]]; exact before 2019, measured since, [[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^cau-a8-1-1|§A8.1, Caution]]), and $\vec B$ comes out in teslas, $1\ \mathrm T = 1\ \mathrm{N/(A\cdot m)}$ (5.36).
>
> *Valid when: the currents are steady (Definition §B6.2.1); approximately for slowly varying currents (quasistatics, [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^mod-b8-2-5|Model §B8.2.5]]). Not valid for a single moving point charge (Caution below).*
> *Source: Griffiths §5.2.2, eqs. (5.34)–(5.36), (5.42) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.6, eqs. (15.21)–(15.22)*

^law-b6-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Layer and route
> - *Layer change.* Level A took the Biot–Savart law as a principle ([[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]]). Here it is a law in the sense of this vault, the empirical starting point of magnetostatics with a stated range: Griffiths builds the theory from it and superposition, exactly as electrostatics from Coulomb's law, and derives the field equations $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ and $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ from it ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]], [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]). Once Maxwell's equations are the principle ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|Principle §B8.4.2]]), it becomes a theorem, their solution for steady sources.
> - *Nelson's route runs the other way.* He takes the static Maxwell equations as the hypothesis, solves them with the vector potential in the Coulomb gauge, and obtains the Biot–Savart formula for a general $\vec J$ as the curl of that solution (his eq. (15.21)), with the thin-wire form following from a current density concentrated on a curve (his eq. (15.22)). The same derivation is [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]] here. The two routes are equivalent ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-3|Theorem §B6.3.3]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.2.2, §5.3.4 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.5–15.6*

^rem-b6-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: Notation, and the moving point charge
> - Level A wrote $\hat r$ for the unit vector from the current element to the field point; here it is $\hat{\mathcal R}$, and primed coordinates always belong to the source, unprimed ones to the field point. The integral is over the primed variables.
> - Level A also gave a moving-point-charge form, $\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{q\vec v\times\hat r}{r^2}$, obtained by the substitution $I\,d\vec l\to q\vec v$ ([[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]]). Griffiths calls this (his eq. (5.43)) "simply wrong" as a matter of principle: a point charge is not a steady current, and the Biot–Savart law does not determine its field. It is only an approximation, good for $v \ll c$ when retardation can be neglected; the exact field of a uniformly moving charge is [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-4|Theorem §B11.2.4]] ([[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^cau-b11-2-1|§B11.2★, Caution: The "Biot–Savart law for a point charge"]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.2.2, eq. (5.43) and footnote 11*

^cau-b6-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Coulomb and Biot–Savart side by side
> | electrostatics | magnetostatics |
> |---|---|
> | source $\rho\,d\tau'$ (a scalar) | source $\vec J\,d\tau'$ (a vector) |
> | $\vec E = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\displaystyle\int\frac{\rho\,\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\,d\tau'$ | $\vec B = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\displaystyle\int\frac{\vec J\times\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\,d\tau'$ |
> | $\vec E$ along $\hat{\mathcal R}$ | $\vec B$ perpendicular to both $\vec J$ and $\hat{\mathcal R}$ |
>
> Both fall off as $1/\mathcal R^2$ per element and both obey superposition. The cross product is what makes magnetic field lines curl around currents instead of leaving them, and since $\vec J$ is a polar vector and $\hat{\mathcal R}$ too, it is also what makes $\vec B$ an axial one ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^rem-b6-1-1|§B6.1, Remark]]). The integrals are convolutions of the source with the field of a point source, the structure that the delta-function calculus of [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]] turns into the field equations of [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B|§B6.3]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.2.2*

^rem-b6-2-3

Level A's straight segment ([[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-1|Theorem §A8.2.1]]) reads, in Griffiths' angle form, $B = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi s}(\sin\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1)$ (5.37), with $s$ the perpendicular distance and $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$ the angles of the ends seen from the field point, measured from the perpendicular; $\sin\theta_i = y_i/\sqrt{y_i^2 + s^2}$ translates between the two. The infinite wire ($\theta_{1,2} = \mp\pi/2$) gives $\vec B = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s}\hat\varphi$ (5.39), and the loop on its axis is [[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop#^thm-a8-3-1|Theorem §A8.3.1]] (Griffiths Example 5.6, eq. (5.41)).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B6.2.1: Polygonal Loops
> A loop shaped as a regular $n$-gon carries a steady current $I$; $R$ is the distance from the centre to the midpoint of a side. Find $\vec B$ at the centre, and the limit $n\to\infty$.
>
> Each side subtends the angles $\theta_1 = -\pi/n$ to $\theta_2 = +\pi/n$ seen from the centre, at perpendicular distance $R$, and all sides give fields along the same normal $\hat z$ (right-hand rule with the current). By (5.37),
>
> $$
> B_{\text{side}} = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi R}\,2\sin\frac\pi n, \qquad \vec B_{\text{centre}} = \frac{\mu_0 nI}{2\pi R}\sin\frac\pi n\,\hat z .
> $$
>
> - *Square* ($n = 4$): $B = \dfrac{\mu_0\cdot4I}{2\pi R}\,\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2} = \dfrac{\sqrt2\,\mu_0I}{\pi R}$.
> - *Hexagon* ($n = 6$): $B = 3\mu_0I/(2\pi R)$; with level A's numbers ($R = 0.866\ \mathrm m$, $I = 0.10\ \mathrm A$) this is $6.9\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm T$, as in [[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^ex-a8-2-1|Example §A8.2.1]].
> - *Circle* ($n\to\infty$): $\sin(\pi/n) = \pi/n - (\pi/n)^3/6 + \dots$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), so $B \to \mu_0I/2R$, the centre of a circular loop of radius $R$ ([[§A8.1 The Biot–Savart Law#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]]).
>
> More precisely, $B_n/B_{\text{circle}} = \frac n\pi\sin\frac\pi n = 1 - \frac{\pi^2}{6n^2} + \dots$: $0.900$ for the square, $0.955$ for the hexagon. The polygon's corners lie outside the inscribed circle, farther from the centre, so it always gives slightly less field than the circle it circumscribes. The instructor's solution gives the same results.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 5.8 (solved here) · PHY 422, HW 8, Problem 3 (Polygonal current loop)*

^ex-b6-2-1

> [!example] Example §B6.2.2: A Finite Solenoid, On and Off the Axis
> A solenoid of radius $R$ and length $L$ with $n$ turns per unit length carrying $I$ is modelled, as in Griffiths' "aluminium foil" picture, by a surface current $\vec K = nI\,\hat\varphi$ on the cylinder $s = R$, $|z'| < L/2$. The surface-current Biot–Savart law ([[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^law-b6-2-2|Law §B6.2.2]]) cuts it into rings of current $dI = K\,dz' = nI\,dz'$.
>
> *On the axis* each ring contributes the loop field (5.41), and integrating over $z'$ gives level A's result ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-2|Theorem §A8.5.2]]; Griffiths Problem 5.11):
>
> $$
> B_z(0, z) = \frac{\mu_0nI}{2}\left[\frac{z + L/2}{\sqrt{(z + L/2)^2 + R^2}} - \frac{z - L/2}{\sqrt{(z - L/2)^2 + R^2}}\right] .
> $$
>
> For $L = 6R$ this is $3/\sqrt{10} = 0.949\,\mu_0nI$ at the centre and $0.493\,\mu_0nI$ at an end.
>
> *Off the axis* the field of a single ring is an elliptic integral (the integrals of Griffiths Problem 5.49, footnote 25), with no elementary closed form. The figure evaluates the two ring integrals numerically and sums the rings, for $L = 6R$. It shows:
> - inside, the field is flatter at $s = 0.9R$ than on the axis and slightly larger in the middle ($0.954$ against $0.949\,\mu_0nI$), then drops more steeply near the ends, where the field lines spread out of the open faces;
> - on each end face, $B_z \approx \tfrac12\mu_0nI$ at every radius inside the winding, not only on the axis (exactly one half for a semi-infinite solenoid: [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^rem-b6-3-5|§B6.3, Remark: The end face of a solenoid]]);
> - just outside the winding the field is small and points backwards ($B_z \approx -0.04\,\mu_0nI$ at $s = 1.1R$ near the middle): the flux that goes up inside returns down outside, spread over the whole plane, since the net flux through any plane $z =$ const is zero ($\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$, [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]]). Near the ends, outside, the lines leaving the end faces curl back and $B_z$ is positive.
>
> As $L\to\infty$ the interior field tends to $\mu_0nI$ at every $s < R$ and the exterior field to zero, the ideal solenoid ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]]); Griffiths Problem 5.46 asks for this limit directly from the Biot–Savart law, and Ampère's law gets it far faster ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^rem-b6-3-4|§B6.3, Remark: How to use Ampère's law]]). Far away the whole solenoid looks like a magnetic dipole of moment $nIL\cdot\pi R^2$ ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^def-b6-4-3|Def. §B6.4.3]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 5.11, 5.46, 5.49 and Examples 5.6, 5.9 (the field off the axis computed numerically here)*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Newton's third law for current loops
> The force of loop 1 on loop 2 is $\vec F_2 = I_2\oint d\vec l_2\times\vec B_1$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]) with $\vec B_1$ from the Biot–Savart law, so, with $\vec{\mathcal R}$ from $d\vec l_1$ to $d\vec l_2$,
>
> $$
> \vec F_2 = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}I_1I_2\oint\!\!\oint\frac{d\vec l_2\times(d\vec l_1\times\hat{\mathcal R})}{\mathcal R^2} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}I_1I_2\oint\!\!\oint\frac{d\vec l_1\,(d\vec l_2\cdot\hat{\mathcal R}) - \hat{\mathcal R}\,(d\vec l_1\cdot d\vec l_2)}{\mathcal R^2} .
> $$
>
> In the first term, $\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2 = -\nabla_2(1/\mathcal R)$, and $\oint d\vec l_2\cdot\nabla_2(1/\mathcal R) = 0$ around the closed loop 2 for each fixed $d\vec l_1$. What remains (Griffiths eq. (5.91)),
>
> $$
> \vec F_2 = -\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}I_1I_2\oint\!\!\oint\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\,d\vec l_1\cdot d\vec l_2 ,
> $$
>
> changes sign when 1 and 2 are exchanged ($\hat{\mathcal R}\to-\hat{\mathcal R}$), so $\vec F_1 = -\vec F_2$: closed steady loops obey the third law, although source and recipient enter the Biot–Savart and force laws very asymmetrically. For isolated current *elements* it fails, and so does the conservation of mechanical angular momentum (Nelson's puzzle, his §15.2ε); the balance is restored by the momentum of the fields ([[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields|§B9.2]]). The same double integral, with $d\vec l_1\cdot d\vec l_2/\mathcal R$, is the Neumann formula for mutual inductance ([[§B8.3 Inductance and Magnetic Energy#^thm-b8-3-1|Theorem §B8.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 5.50, eq. (5.91) (solved here) · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 15, §15.2ε*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The Biot–Savart integral is the curl of the convolution of $\vec J$ with $1/4\pi\mathcal R$, the Green's function of the Laplacian ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]; [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]]); Green's functions in general belong to Mathematical Methods (planned); those of Poisson's equation, with boundaries, are at level C — [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^def-c7-3-1|Def. §C7.3.1]], [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]].
> - The ring field off the axis leads to complete elliptic integrals of the first and second kind, special functions of Mathematical Methods (planned).
> - The limit $\frac n\pi\sin\frac\pi n\to1$ is the first-order Taylor expansion of $\sin$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]).
