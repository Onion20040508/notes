In a dc circuit an element absorbs power $P = IV$ ([[§A5.4 Electrical Power#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]). In an ac circuit current and voltage change sign many times a second, and they need not do so together ([[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC|§A11.2]]). This section asks how much energy an ac element takes on average, and finds that the answer depends on the phase angle. It also explains why ac voltages and currents are quoted as rms values.

## Instantaneous and average power

> [!definition] Definition §A11.3.1: Instantaneous and Average Power
> The **instantaneous power** delivered to a circuit element (or supplied by a source) is $p(t) = i(t)\,v(t)$, with $i$ the current through it and $v$ the voltage across it. For a periodic signal of period $T = 2\pi/\omega$, the **average power** is its time average over one cycle:
>
> $$
> P_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T p(t)\,dt .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.4*

^def-a11-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A11.3.1: Average Power of a Sinusoidal Element
> If $v(t) = V_0\sin\omega t$ and $i(t) = I_0\sin(\omega t - \phi)$, then
>
> $$
> P_{\text{ave}} = \tfrac12 I_0 V_0 \cos\phi .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.4, eq. (15.12)*

^thm-a11-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Expand $\sin(\omega t - \phi) = \sin\omega t\cos\phi - \cos\omega t\sin\phi$:
>
> $$
> P_{\text{ave}} = \frac{I_0V_0\cos\phi}{T}\int_0^T \sin^2\omega t\,dt \;-\; \frac{I_0V_0\sin\phi}{T}\int_0^T \sin\omega t\cos\omega t\,dt .
> $$
>
> Over a full period, $\sin^2\omega t = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2\omega t)$ averages to $\tfrac12$ (the cosine term integrates to zero over two whole cycles), and $\sin\omega t\cos\omega t = \tfrac12\sin 2\omega t$ averages to $0$. Only the first term survives.

^der-a11-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^def-a11-3-1|Def. §A11.3.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|FTC I]] (Single Variable Analysis)

> [!definition] Definition §A11.3.2: Power Factor
> The factor $\cos\phi$ in $P_{\text{ave}} = \frac12 I_0V_0\cos\phi$ is the **power factor**. It measures how much the average power falls short of $\frac12 I_0V_0$ because current and voltage are out of phase.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.4*

^def-a11-3-2

![[ph-em-a11-3-1.svg]]
*Instantaneous power $p(t)$ (red), in units of $I_0V_0$, with its average (dashed red), and the voltage for reference (thin blue). For the resistor $p \geq 0$ always and averages to $\frac12$. For the capacitor $p$ swings symmetrically about zero at twice the source frequency: energy goes in for a quarter cycle and comes back the next. For an RLC circuit with $\phi = \pi/3$ the average is $\frac12\cos\phi = \frac14$.*

> [!remark] Remark: Elements one by one
> - **Resistor**, $\phi = 0$: $P_{\text{ave}} = \frac12 I_0V_0$. Current and voltage always have the same sign, so $p(t) \geq 0$: the energy is dissipated as heat.
> - **Capacitor**, $\phi = -\pi/2$, and **inductor**, $\phi = +\pi/2$: $\cos\phi = 0$, so $P_{\text{ave}} = 0$. The element absorbs energy during one quarter cycle (charging the capacitor's electric field, or building up the inductor's magnetic field, [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor|§A4.3]], [[§A10.3 Energy in a Magnetic Field|§A10.3]]) and returns it during the next.
> - **Source**: $\phi$ may have any value. If $\cos\phi > 0$ the source delivers energy on average; if $\cos\phi < 0$ it absorbs energy on average. So in general the power factor runs from $-1$ to $1$; for a source driving passive elements it lies between $0$ and $1$.

^rem-a11-3-1

## RMS values

> [!definition] Definition §A11.3.3: RMS Current and Voltage
> The **root-mean-square** (rms) values of a periodic current and voltage are the square roots of the time averages of their squares:
>
> $$
> I_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac1T\int_0^T i^2(t)\,dt}, \qquad V_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac1T\int_0^T v^2(t)\,dt} .
> $$
>
> (Square, then take the mean, then the square root: the name read backwards.) Unlike the plain averages, which vanish for a sinusoid, they are positive.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.2, §15.4*

^def-a11-3-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A11.3.2: RMS Values and Power for Sinusoids
> For sinusoidal current and voltage of amplitudes $I_0$, $V_0$,
>
> $$
> I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt2}, \qquad V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt2}, \qquad P_{\text{ave}} = I_{\text{rms}}V_{\text{rms}}\cos\phi .
> $$
>
> For a resistor, $P_{\text{ave}} = I_{\text{rms}}V_{\text{rms}} = I_{\text{rms}}^2R = V_{\text{rms}}^2/R$: the dc formulas of [[§A5.4 Electrical Power#^thm-a5-4-2|Theorem §A5.4.2]], with rms values in place of the constant ones.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.2, eqs. (15.4)–(15.5); §15.4, eq. (15.13)*

^thm-a11-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $i^2 = I_0^2\sin^2(\omega t - \phi)$, and $\sin^2$ averages to $\frac12$ over a period whatever the phase (as in [[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^der-a11-3-1|the derivation above]]), so $I_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{I_0^2/2} = I_0/\sqrt2$; likewise for $V_{\text{rms}}$. Then $\frac12 I_0V_0 = (I_0/\sqrt2)(V_0/\sqrt2) = I_{\text{rms}}V_{\text{rms}}$ in [[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-3-1|Theorem §A11.3.1]]. For a resistor, $\phi = 0$ and $V_{\text{rms}} = I_{\text{rms}}R$ ([[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits#^thm-a11-1-2|Theorem §A11.1.2]]).

^der-a11-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^def-a11-3-3|Def. §A11.3.3]], [[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-3-1|Theorem §A11.3.1]], [[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits#^thm-a11-1-2|Theorem §A11.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why rms
> An rms current $I_{\text{rms}}$ heats a resistor at the same average rate as a steady direct current of the same size. That is why ac ratings and meters use rms values: a "110 V" outlet has $V_{\text{rms}} = 110\ \mathrm V$ and amplitude $110\sqrt2\ \mathrm V = 156\ \mathrm V$, and an ac voltmeter across it reads 110 V. Since every amplitude relation is linear, it holds for rms values too: $I_{\text{rms}} = V_{\text{rms}}/Z$, and the rms voltages across $R$, $C$, $L$ are $I_{\text{rms}}R$, $I_{\text{rms}}X_C$, $I_{\text{rms}}X_L$.

^rem-a11-3-2

> [!caution] Caution: Pitfalls with ac power
> - The factor $\sqrt2$ belongs to sinusoids only. For another waveform, compute $\langle i^2\rangle$ from the definition.
> - $P_{\text{ave}} = I_{\text{rms}}V_{\text{rms}}$ only when $\phi = 0$. In general the power factor reduces it: a capacitor can carry a large rms current and take no average power.
> - Sign of $\phi$: with $i = I_0\sin(\omega t - \phi)$, a capacitor has $\phi = -\pi/2$ (current leads) and an inductor $\phi = +\pi/2$. Either way $\cos\phi = 0$, so the power result does not depend on getting this right, but the phase does.

^cau-a11-3-1

## Power in the RLC series circuit

> [!theorem] Theorem §A11.3.3: Average Power Delivered to an RLC Series Circuit
> The average power supplied by the source in the series RLC circuit of [[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC|§A11.2]] is
>
> $$
> P_{\text{ave}} = I_{\text{rms}}V_{\text{rms}}\cos\phi = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}^2\,R}{Z^2} = I_{\text{rms}}^2 R ,
> $$
>
> and all of it is dissipated in the resistor.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.4, eq. (15.14)*

^thm-a11-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From [[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC#^thm-a11-2-2|Theorem §A11.2.2]], $\cos\phi = R/Z$ and $I_{\text{rms}} = V_{\text{rms}}/Z$. Substituting into [[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-3-2|Theorem §A11.3.2]] gives $P_{\text{ave}} = (V_{\text{rms}}/Z)\,V_{\text{rms}}\,(R/Z) = V_{\text{rms}}^2R/Z^2 = I_{\text{rms}}^2R$. The last form is exactly the average power taken by the resistor; the inductor and capacitor take none on average, so energy conservation balances.

^der-a11-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC#^thm-a11-2-2|Theorem §A11.2.2]], [[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-3-2|Theorem §A11.3.2]], [[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^rem-a11-3-1|Remark: Elements one by one]]

## Example

> [!example] Example §A11.3.1: Power Output of a Generator
> A generator with $v(t) = (4.00\ \mathrm V)\sin\bigl[(1.00 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{rad/s})\,t\bigr]$ drives $L = 2.00\ \mathrm{mH}$, $C = 4.00\ \mu\mathrm F$ and $R = 5.00\ \Omega$ in series. Then
>
> $$
> V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{4.00\ \mathrm V}{\sqrt2} = 2.83\ \mathrm V, \qquad X_L = \omega L = 20.0\ \Omega, \qquad X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C} = 25.0\ \Omega,
> $$
>
> $$
> Z = \sqrt{(5.00\ \Omega)^2 + (-5.0\ \Omega)^2} = 7.07\ \Omega, \qquad
> P_{\text{ave}} = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}^2 R}{Z^2} = \frac{(2.83\ \mathrm V)^2(5.00\ \Omega)}{(7.07\ \Omega)^2} = 0.800\ \mathrm W .
> $$
>
> Check by the other route: $\phi = \tan^{-1}(-5.0/5.00) = -45^\circ$ (capacitive, current leads), $I_{\text{rms}} = V_{\text{rms}}/Z = 0.400\ \mathrm A$, and $I_{\text{rms}}V_{\text{rms}}\cos\phi = (0.400\ \mathrm A)(2.83\ \mathrm V)(0.707) = 0.800\ \mathrm W$. If $R$ were much larger than the reactances, $Z \approx R$ and $P_{\text{ave}} \approx V_{\text{rms}}^2/R$, the dc formula.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 15.3*

^ex-a11-3-1

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The averages $\langle\sin^2\rangle = \frac12$ and $\langle\sin\cos\rangle = 0$ are integrals of derivatives ($\sin^2\omega t = \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[\frac{t}{2} - \frac{\sin 2\omega t}{4\omega}\bigr]$), evaluated by [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|FTC I]] (Single Variable Analysis). The same average gives the intensity of a light wave in [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves|§A12.3]].
> - Average power over a time interval is the mechanical notion of [[§A4.3 Power#^def-a4-3-1|CM Def. §A4.3.1]].
