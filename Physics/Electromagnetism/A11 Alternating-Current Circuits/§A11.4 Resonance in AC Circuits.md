The impedance of an RLC series circuit depends on the driving frequency ([[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC|§A11.2]]). If the frequency of the source is varied at fixed amplitude, the current and the power it delivers ([[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits|§A11.3]]) change with it. This section asks at which frequency the response is largest and how sharp the peak is. The answers explain how a radio picks out one station.

## The driven circuit as an oscillator

> [!theorem] Theorem §A11.4.1: Equation of the Driven RLC Circuit
> The charge $q$ on the capacitor of an RLC series circuit driven by $v(t) = V_0\sin\omega t$ obeys
>
> $$
> L\frac{d^2q}{dt^2} + R\frac{dq}{dt} + \frac{q}{C} = V_0\sin\omega t .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.5, eq. (15.16)*

^thm-a11-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Kirchhoff's loop rule ([[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-2|Theorem §A6.3.2]]): the source voltage equals the sum of the drops $L\,di/dt$ across the inductor ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-3|Theorem §A10.2.3]]), $iR$ across the resistor and $q/C$ across the capacitor. With $i = dq/dt$ this is the stated equation.

^der-a11-4-1

*Uses:* [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-2|Theorem §A6.3.2]], [[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-3|Theorem §A10.2.3]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A5.1 Electric Current#^def-a5-1-1|Def. §A5.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The driven damped oscillator
> The equation has the form $m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx = F_0\sin\omega t$ of a mass on a spring with damping and a sinusoidal driving force, with $L \leftrightarrow m$, $R \leftrightarrow b$, $1/C \leftrightarrow k$, $V_0 \leftrightarrow F_0$. Without the source it is the free RLC circuit of [[§A10.6 RLC Circuits|§A10.6]]. Everything below about resonance therefore holds for mechanical oscillators as well; that general theory is in [[Oscillations, Waves and Optics]] ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-2|WO Theorem §A1.6.2]], [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-3|WO Theorem §A1.6.3]]).

^rem-a11-4-1

## Resonance

> [!theorem] Theorem §A11.4.2: Resonance of the RLC Series Circuit
> At fixed source amplitude $V_0$, the current amplitude
>
> $$
> I_0(\omega) = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{R^2 + \bigl(\omega L - 1/\omega C\bigr)^2}}
> $$
>
> is largest at the **resonant angular frequency**
>
> $$
> \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} , \qquad f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} .
> $$
>
> At resonance $X_L = X_C$, $Z = R$, $\phi = 0$ and $I_0 = V_0/R$: the circuit is purely resistive, with current and emf in phase. The average power $P_{\text{ave}} = V_{\text{rms}}^2R/Z^2$ is also largest there, equal to $V_{\text{rms}}^2/R$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.5, eqs. (15.15), (15.17)*

^thm-a11-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $I_0 = V_0/Z$ ([[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC#^thm-a11-2-2|Theorem §A11.2.2]]) is largest where $Z^2 = R^2 + (\omega L - 1/\omega C)^2$ is smallest. The first term does not depend on $\omega$ and the second is a square, so the minimum is where the square vanishes: $\omega_0L = 1/(\omega_0C)$, i.e. $\omega_0^2 = 1/(LC)$. There $Z = R$ and $\tan\phi = (X_L - X_C)/R = 0$. The power $P_{\text{ave}} = V_{\text{rms}}^2R/Z^2$ ([[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-3-3|Theorem §A11.3.3]]) is largest for the same reason.

^der-a11-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC#^thm-a11-2-2|Theorem §A11.2.2]], [[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-3-3|Theorem §A11.3.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Which "natural frequency"?
> The resonant frequency $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ is the frequency of the undamped LC circuit ([[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^thm-a10-5-4|Theorem §A10.5.4]]). The free oscillation of the circuit *with* resistance is slightly slower, $\sqrt{1/LC - (R/2L)^2}$ ([[§A10.6 RLC Circuits#^thm-a10-6-2|Theorem §A10.6.2]]). The current amplitude of the driven series circuit peaks exactly at $\omega_0$, whatever $R$ is; $R$ only sets the height and width of the peak. In the notation of §A10.6, the damping rate is $\gamma = R/2L = \Delta\omega/2$ ([[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-4-3|Theorem §A11.4.3]]), so $Q = \omega_0/2\gamma$: the width of the resonance and the decay of the free oscillation are set by the same quantity.

^cau-a11-4-1

## Bandwidth and quality factor

> [!definition] Definition §A11.4.1: Bandwidth; Quality Factor
> The **bandwidth** $\Delta\omega$ of the resonance is the width of the range of angular frequencies over which $P_{\text{ave}}$ exceeds half its maximum value. The **quality factor** is the dimensionless ratio
>
> $$
> Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\Delta\omega} .
> $$
>
> A high $Q$ means a sharp, narrow resonance peak.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.5, eq. (15.18)*

^def-a11-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A11.4.3: Bandwidth and Q of the RLC Series Circuit
> For the series RLC circuit,
>
> $$
> \Delta\omega = \frac{R}{L}, \qquad Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R} = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} .
> $$
>
> The two half-power frequencies $\omega_\pm$ satisfy $\omega_+ - \omega_- = R/L$ and $\omega_+\omega_- = \omega_0^2$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.5, eq. (15.19) (the book states $Q = \omega_0L/R$ without derivation)*

^thm-a11-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $P_{\text{ave}} = V_{\text{rms}}^2R/Z^2$ is half its maximum $V_{\text{rms}}^2/R$ when $Z^2 = 2R^2$, i.e. when $(\omega L - 1/\omega C)^2 = R^2$, so $\omega L - 1/(\omega C) = \pm R$. Multiplying by $\omega/L$:
>
> $$
> \omega^2 \mp \frac{R}{L}\,\omega - \frac{1}{LC} = 0 .
> $$
>
> Each quadratic has exactly one positive root,
>
> $$
> \omega_\pm = \frac{1}{2}\left[\sqrt{\frac{R^2}{L^2} + \frac{4}{LC}} \pm \frac{R}{L}\right],
> $$
>
> so $\omega_+ - \omega_- = R/L$ and $\omega_+\omega_- = \frac14\bigl[(R^2/L^2 + 4/LC) - R^2/L^2\bigr] = 1/(LC) = \omega_0^2$. Between $\omega_-$ and $\omega_+$ the power exceeds half its maximum, so $\Delta\omega = R/L$ and $Q = \omega_0/\Delta\omega = \omega_0L/R$. With $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, $Q = \sqrt{L/C}/R$.

^der-a11-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits#^def-a11-4-1|Def. §A11.4.1]], [[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-3-3|Theorem §A11.3.3]], [[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-4-2|Theorem §A11.4.2]]

![[ph-em-a11-4-1.svg]]
*Average power delivered to a series RLC circuit versus driving frequency, for two circuits with the same $R$ and $\omega_0$ but different $L/C$: $Q = 2$ (blue) and $Q = 6$ (red). Both peak at $\omega_0$ with the same $P_{\max} = V_{\text{rms}}^2/R$; the half-power width $\Delta\omega$ (blue arrow) is three times narrower for the red curve. Note the asymmetry: $\omega_0$ is the geometric, not the arithmetic, mean of the half-power points.*

> [!remark] Remark: Tuning
> Resonant circuits pass a chosen band of frequencies and reject others. A radio receiver is tuned to a station by adjusting $L$ or $C$ until $\omega_0$ matches the station's frequency; with a high $Q$ the bandwidth is small, so stations at slightly different frequencies meet a high impedance and are not passed. Cell phones select their signals (around 1 GHz) with inductor-capacitor circuits in the same way. A metal detector senses the shift of $\omega_0$ when nearby metal changes the self-inductance of one of its coils. Hertz used tuned RLC circuits to produce and detect the first man-made electromagnetic waves ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves|§A12.1]]).

^rem-a11-4-2

> [!caution] Caution: Narrow versus strong
> At fixed $L$ and $C$, lowering $R$ narrows the peak ($\Delta\omega = R/L$) and also raises it ($P_{\max} = V_{\text{rms}}^2/R$). A sharper filter can therefore draw much more power at resonance, which may be undesirable. Raising $L$ (at fixed $\omega_0$) narrows the peak without changing its height.

^cau-a11-4-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A11.4.1: Driving at Resonance
> The circuit of [[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC#^ex-a11-2-1|Example §A11.2.1]] ($R = 4.00\ \Omega$, $L = 3.00\ \mathrm{mH}$, $C = 8.00 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm F$, $V_0 = 0.100\ \mathrm V$) has
>
> $$
> f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(3.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm H)(8.00 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm F)}} = 1.03 \times 10^2\ \mathrm{Hz} .
> $$
>
> Set to this frequency, the generator sees $Z = R$, so $I_0 = V_0/R = 0.100\ \mathrm V/4.00\ \Omega = 2.50 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm A$, up from $2.05 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm A$ at 200 Hz. Off resonance one needs the full impedance; at resonance the reactances drop out.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 15.4 (the book refers to "the circuit of Example 15.1"; the values are those of Example 15.2)*

^ex-a11-4-1

> [!example] Example §A11.4.2: A Sharp Resonance
> An RLC series circuit has $R = 0.200\ \Omega$, $L = 4.00\ \mathrm{mH}$, $C = 2.00\ \mu\mathrm F$, driven by a source of amplitude $4.00\ \mathrm V$ at resonance.
>
> $$
> \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(4.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm H)(2.00 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm F)}} = 1.12 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{rad/s}, \qquad
> P_{\text{ave}} = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}^2}{R} = \frac{(4.00\ \mathrm V/\sqrt2)^2}{0.200\ \Omega} = 40.0\ \mathrm W,
> $$
>
> $$
> Q = \frac{\omega_0L}{R} = \frac{(1.12 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{rad/s})(4.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm H)}{0.200\ \Omega} = 224, \qquad \Delta\omega = \frac{\omega_0}{Q} = \frac{R}{L} = 50.0\ \mathrm{rad/s} .
> $$
>
> The power stays above 20 W only within $\pm 25\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ of $1.12 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, a band less than half a percent wide.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 15.5*

^ex-a11-4-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The equation of [[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-4-1|Theorem §A11.4.1]] is a linear second-order ODE with constant coefficients; the steady state found here is its particular solution, and the transient of [[§A10.6 RLC Circuits|§A10.6]] the homogeneous part.
> - Mechanical analogue: the mass–spring system with damping and driving ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^def-a1-6-2|WO Def. §A1.6.2]]; note that there the bandwidth is $\Delta\omega = b/m$, i.e. $R/L$ here).
> - At level B the absorbed power of the driven oscillator is split into an absorbing and a non-absorbing part, with $\omega_+ - \omega_- = \gamma$ and $\omega_+\omega_- = \omega_0^2$ exactly, and the same results are written in impedance language: [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-4|WO Theorem §B1.4.4]], [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^rem-b1-4-1|WO Remark: The same results in impedance language]].
