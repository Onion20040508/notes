[[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits|§A11.1]] put one element at a time across an ac source. Here the resistor, inductor and capacitor are connected in series, so one current flows through all three, but the three element voltages are out of step with each other. The question is how large the current is and how far it is out of phase with the source. Adding the element voltages as phasors answers both at once and produces the ac analogue of resistance, the impedance.

## Setting up

> [!definition] Definition §A11.2.1: RLC Series Circuit; Phase Angle
> An **RLC series circuit** is a resistor $R$, an inductor $L$ and a capacitor $C$ in series across an ac source $v(t) = V_0\sin\omega t$. In the steady state ([[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits#^mod-a11-1-1|Model §A11.1.1]]) the current has the form
>
> $$
> i(t) = I_0 \sin(\omega t - \phi),
> $$
>
> where the **phase angle** $\phi$ is the amount by which the current lags the source emf ($\phi < 0$: the current leads).
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.3*

^def-a11-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: One current, three voltages
> In a series circuit the same current passes every element at every instant (charge does not pile up in the wires, [[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]]). Applying [[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits|§A11.1]] to each element with this common current:
> - $v_R$ is in phase with $i$, amplitude $V_R = I_0R$;
> - $v_L$ leads $i$ by $\pi/2$, amplitude $V_L = I_0X_L$;
> - $v_C$ lags $i$ by $\pi/2$, amplitude $V_C = I_0X_C$.
>
> Kirchhoff's loop rule requires $v_R(t) + v_L(t) + v_C(t) = v(t)$ at every instant. The unknowns are $I_0$ and $\phi$.

^rem-a11-2-1

## Adding sinusoids with phasors

> [!theorem] Theorem §A11.2.1: Phasor Addition
> If sinusoids of one angular frequency $\omega$ are added, the sum is a sinusoid of the same frequency, and its phasor is the vector sum of their phasors.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.3*

^thm-a11-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> All the phasors rotate at the same rate $\omega$, so their vector sum is an arrow of fixed length that also rotates at $\omega$: a phasor ([[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits#^def-a11-1-2|Def. §A11.1.2]]). The vertical component of a vector sum is the sum of the vertical components, $(\vec A + \vec B)_y = A_y + B_y$ ([[§A1.4 Vector Algebra#^thm-a1-4-1|CM Theorem §A1.4.1]]). So the projection of the sum, which is the value of the sum phasor, equals the sum of the projections, which is the sum of the instantaneous values.

^der-a11-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits#^def-a11-1-2|Def. §A11.1.2]], [[§A1.4 Vector Algebra#^thm-a1-4-1|CM Theorem §A1.4.1]]

## Impedance and phase

> [!definition] Definition §A11.2.2: Impedance
> The **impedance** of the series RLC circuit at angular frequency $\omega$ is
>
> $$
> Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}, \qquad X_L = \omega L,\quad X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C} .
> $$
>
> Its unit is the ohm. It is the ac counterpart of resistance: it combines the resistance and both reactances into one ratio of voltage to current amplitude.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.3, eq. (15.11)*

^def-a11-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A11.2.2: Current in an RLC Series Circuit
> The steady-state current in the series RLC circuit has amplitude and phase
>
> $$
> I_0 = \frac{V_0}{Z} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}}, \qquad \tan\phi = \frac{X_L - X_C}{R}, \quad -\frac{\pi}{2} < \phi < \frac{\pi}{2} .
> $$
>
> The first formula is the **ac version of Ohm's law**. Equivalently, $\cos\phi = R/Z$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.3, eqs. (15.9)–(15.11)*

^thm-a11-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Draw the phasors of [[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC#^rem-a11-2-1|the remark above]] with the current phasor along some direction (in the figure, horizontal). Then $V_R$ lies along it, $V_L$ is a quarter turn ahead and $V_C$ a quarter turn behind. $V_L$ and $V_C$ are antiparallel and combine into one phasor of signed length $V_L - V_C$, perpendicular to $V_R$. By [[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC#^thm-a11-2-1|Theorem §A11.2.1]] and the loop rule, the vector sum must be the source phasor, of length $V_0$, at angle $\phi$ ahead of the current. The right triangle gives
>
> $$
> V_0^2 = V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2 = I_0^2\bigl[R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2\bigr] = I_0^2 Z^2,
> \qquad
> \tan\phi = \frac{V_L - V_C}{V_R} = \frac{X_L - X_C}{R} .
> $$
>
> Since $V_R > 0$ the source phasor lies in the right half-plane relative to the current, so $\lvert\phi\rvert < \pi/2$ and $\cos\phi = V_R/V_0 = R/Z$.

^der-a11-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC#^thm-a11-2-1|Theorem §A11.2.1]], [[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits#^thm-a11-1-2|Theorem §A11.1.2]], [[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits#^thm-a11-1-3|Theorem §A11.1.3]], [[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits#^thm-a11-1-4|Theorem §A11.1.4]], [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-2|Theorem §A6.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the phase angle
> - $X_L > X_C$: $\phi > 0$, the current lags the emf; the circuit is **inductive**.
> - $X_L < X_C$: $\phi < 0$, the current leads; the circuit is **capacitive**.
> - $X_L = X_C$: $\phi = 0$ and $Z = R$; the reactances cancel. This is resonance ([[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits|§A11.4]]).
>
> Removing elements recovers [[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits|§A11.1]]: with $R$ alone $\phi = 0$; with $L$ alone ($R \to 0$, $X_C \to 0$) $\phi \to \pi/2$; with $C$ alone $\phi \to -\pi/2$.

^rem-a11-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: Amplitudes do not add
> - The instantaneous voltages add, $v_R + v_L + v_C = v$, but the amplitudes do not: $V_0 \neq V_R + V_L + V_C$, because the three peaks occur at different times. They add as vectors.
> - In particular $V_L$ or $V_C$ can be larger than the source amplitude $V_0$ (near resonance, much larger).
> - Likewise $Z \neq R + X_L + X_C$. The two reactances enter with opposite signs, and the result is combined with $R$ by Pythagoras.

^cau-a11-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: A mechanical picture
> A car wheel on a road with regularly spaced bumps behaves like this circuit: the bumps drive it periodically (the source), the shock absorber damps the motion (the resistor), and energy passes back and forth between the motion and the spring (between the inductor's magnetic energy and the capacitor's electric energy, as in [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations|§A10.5]]). The response is largest when the bumps come at the system's natural frequency ([[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits|§A11.4]]).

^rem-a11-2-3

## Example

> [!example] Example §A11.2.1: An RLC Series Circuit
> A generator with $f = 200\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $V_0 = 0.100\ \mathrm V$ drives $R = 4.00\ \Omega$, $L = 3.00\ \mathrm{mH}$ and $C = 8.00 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm F$ in series. With $\omega = 2\pi(200\ \mathrm{Hz}) = 1257\ \mathrm{rad/s}$:
>
> $$
> X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C} = 0.995\ \Omega, \qquad X_L = \omega L = 3.77\ \Omega, \qquad
> Z = \sqrt{(4.00\ \Omega)^2 + (2.77\ \Omega)^2} = 4.87\ \Omega,
> $$
>
> $$
> I_0 = \frac{V_0}{Z} = \frac{0.100\ \mathrm V}{4.87\ \Omega} = 2.05 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm A, \qquad
> \phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{2.77\ \Omega}{4.00\ \Omega} = 0.607\ \mathrm{rad} = 34.8^\circ .
> $$
>
> The circuit is inductive ($X_L > X_C$), so the current lags the emf. The element amplitudes are $V_R = I_0R = 0.082\ \mathrm V$, $V_L = I_0X_L = 0.077\ \mathrm V$, $V_C = I_0X_C = 0.020\ \mathrm V$: they sum to $0.18\ \mathrm V$, almost twice $V_0$, yet $\sqrt{V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2} = 0.100\ \mathrm V$ as it must.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 15.2*

^ex-a11-2-1
