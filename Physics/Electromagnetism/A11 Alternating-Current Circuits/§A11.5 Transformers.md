Power plants generate ac at modest voltages, send it over long lines at hundreds of kilovolts, and deliver it to homes at 110–240 V. This section asks why the voltage is raised for transmission and how it can be changed at all. The device is the transformer: two coils sharing one magnetic flux, so that Faraday's law ([[§A9.1 Faraday's Law|§A9.1]]) fixes the ratio of their voltages. It works only with changing currents, which is a main reason the grid runs on ac.

## Why transmit at high voltage

> [!theorem] Theorem §A11.5.1: Ohmic Loss in a Transmission Line
> A line of total resistance $R_{\text{line}}$ that delivers average power $P = I_{\text{rms}}V_{\text{rms}}$ (power factor ignored) at transmission voltage $V_{\text{rms}}$ loses
>
> $$
> P_{\text{loss}} = I_{\text{rms}}^2R_{\text{line}} = \frac{P^2}{V_{\text{rms}}^2}\,R_{\text{line}} .
> $$
>
> At fixed delivered power, the loss falls as the square of the transmission voltage.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.6*

^thm-a11-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The whole line current $I_{\text{rms}} = P/V_{\text{rms}}$ passes through the line's resistance, which dissipates $I_{\text{rms}}^2R_{\text{line}}$ on average ([[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-3-2|Theorem §A11.3.2]]). Substitute.

^der-a11-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-3-2|Theorem §A11.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The strategy
> The same power is best sent as a small current at high voltage (up to about 500 kV), which is then "stepped down" near the consumer to a safer 110–240 V. A constant (dc) voltage cannot be stepped up or down this way, because it produces no changing flux.

^rem-a11-5-1

## The ideal transformer

> [!model] Model §A11.5.2: Ideal Transformer
> A **transformer** consists of two coils (windings) on a common soft-iron core: the **primary**, of $N_P$ turns, connected to an ac voltage $v_P(t)$, and the **secondary**, of $N_S$ turns, connected to a load $R_S$. In the **ideal transformer**
> - all magnetic field lines stay inside the core, so the same flux $\Phi$ threads every turn of both windings;
> - there are no energy losses: no ohmic heating in the windings, no eddy-current heating in the core ([[§A9.5 Eddy Currents|§A9.5]]), no hysteresis losses in the iron ([[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter|§A8.6]]).
>
> - *Assumes:* a good core and windings; real transformers lose as little as about 1% of the transmitted power, so the model is close to reality.
> - *Fails:* when the flux leaks out of the core or the losses are not small, e.g. coils that merely sit near each other (then use the mutual inductance of [[§A10.1 Mutual Inductance|§A10.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.6*

^mod-a11-5-2

![[ph-em-a11-5-1.svg]]
*A step-up transformer: the primary (blue, $N_P$ turns) is driven by the source, the secondary (green, $N_S > N_P$ turns) feeds the load $R_S$. The iron core (grey) guides the flux (red dashed loop), so each turn of either winding encloses the same changing flux $\Phi$.*

> [!theorem] Theorem §A11.5.3: Transformer Equations
> For an ideal transformer, at every instant,
>
> $$
> \frac{v_S}{v_P} = \frac{N_S}{N_P}, \qquad \frac{i_S}{i_P} = \frac{N_P}{N_S} ,
> $$
>
> and the same ratios hold for peak and for rms values: $V_S/V_P = N_S/N_P$ (the **transformer equation**) and $I_S = (N_P/N_S)\,I_P$. Seen from the source, the load $R_S$ acts like a resistance
>
> $$
> R_P = \Bigl(\frac{N_P}{N_S}\Bigr)^2 R_S .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.6, eqs. (15.20)–(15.23)*

^thm-a11-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> **Voltages.** By Faraday's law for a coil ([[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^thm-a9-1-2|Theorem §A9.1.2]]) the emf induced in the primary is $-N_P\,d\Phi/dt$. The windings have no resistance, so the source voltage $v_P$ equals this induced emf (in magnitude, with signs fixed by a common winding sense). The secondary is threaded by the same flux per turn, so its emf, which is the voltage delivered to the load, is $-N_S\,d\Phi/dt$. The ratio is $v_S/v_P = N_S/N_P$: the unknown $d\Phi/dt$ cancels.
>
> **Currents.** With no losses, the power fed into the primary equals the power delivered to the load at every instant (energy conservation, [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]): $i_Pv_P = i_Sv_S$. Hence $i_S/i_P = v_P/v_S = N_P/N_S$.
>
> **Load seen by the source.** With $i_S = v_S/R_S$: $v_P = (N_P/N_S)\,v_S = (N_P/N_S)\,R_S\,i_S = (N_P/N_S)^2R_S\,i_P$.
>
> All three relations are linear and hold at every instant, so they hold for the amplitudes and for the rms values as well.

^der-a11-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A11.5 Transformers#^mod-a11-5-2|Model §A11.5.2]], [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^thm-a9-1-2|Theorem §A9.1.2]], [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]

> [!definition] Definition §A11.5.1: Step-Up and Step-Down Transformers
> A **step-up transformer** ($N_S > N_P$) raises the voltage and lowers the current; a **step-down transformer** ($N_S < N_P$) lowers the voltage and raises the current.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.6*

^def-a11-5-1

> [!caution] Caution: No free energy
> Stepping the voltage up by a factor $N_S/N_P$ steps the current down by the same factor; the power $i v$ is unchanged (ideally) or slightly reduced (in reality). A transformer changes the *form* in which power is delivered, never its amount.

^cau-a11-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why the core and why ac
> - The soft-iron core concentrates the field ([[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter|§A8.6]]) and guides it from one winding to the other, which is what makes "the same flux in every turn" a good approximation. Induced eddy currents in the iron waste energy, one of the losses neglected in the ideal model ([[§A9.5 Eddy Currents|§A9.5]]).
> - The mechanism is induction: only a *changing* flux induces an emf. A steady current in the primary gives a constant flux and nothing in the secondary.

^rem-a11-5-2

## Example

> [!example] Example §A11.5.1: A Step-Down Transformer on a Utility Pole
> A pole transformer steps the rms voltage down from $12\ \mathrm{kV}$ to $240\ \mathrm V$; the current entering it is $2.0\ \mathrm A$; the line feeding it has total resistance $200\ \Omega$.
> - Turns ratio: $N_S/N_P = V_S/V_P = 240\ \mathrm V/12\,000\ \mathrm V = 1/50$.
> - Output current: $I_S = (N_P/N_S)\,I_P = 50 \times 2.0\ \mathrm A = 100\ \mathrm A$.
> - Line loss at 12 kV: $P_{\text{loss}} = I_P^2R = (2.0\ \mathrm A)^2(200\ \Omega) = 800\ \mathrm W$.
> - Line loss if the same power had been sent at 240 V, i.e. as 100 A all the way: $(100\ \mathrm A)^2(200\ \Omega) = 2 \times 10^6\ \mathrm W$.
>
> Raising the transmission voltage by a factor 50 cuts the loss by $50^2 = 2500$ ([[§A11.5 Transformers#^thm-a11-5-1|Theorem §A11.5.1]]). Without the transformer, delivering $24\ \mathrm{kW}$ would waste megawatts in the line.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 15.6*

^ex-a11-5-1
