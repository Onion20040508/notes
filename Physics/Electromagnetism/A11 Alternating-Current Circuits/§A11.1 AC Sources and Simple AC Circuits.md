The circuits so far were driven by batteries: after a transient ([[§A6.5 RC Circuits|§A6.5]], [[§A10.4 RL Circuits|§A10.4]]) the current settles to a constant. Power grids instead supply an emf that oscillates sinusoidally, produced by generators ([[§A9.6 Generators, Back EMF and Applications|§A9.6]]). This section asks how each of the three passive elements (resistor, capacitor, inductor) responds when such an emf is placed directly across it. The answers, an amplitude ratio called the reactance and a fixed phase shift, are the building blocks for the combined circuit of [[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC|§A11.2]].

## Alternating current

> [!definition] Definition §A11.1.1: Direct and Alternating Current; AC Voltage
> **Direct current (dc)** is a flow of charge in one direction only; it is the steady state of a circuit with a constant-voltage source. **Alternating current (ac)** is a flow of charge that periodically reverses direction. An **ac voltage** varies sinusoidally in time at a fixed frequency,
>
> $$
> v(t) = V_0 \sin\omega t ,
> $$
>
> where $V_0$ is the **peak voltage** (amplitude) and $\omega = 2\pi f$ the angular frequency in rad/s. A circuit driven by such a source is an **ac circuit**; the current $i(t)$ it carries is an **ac current**.
>
> *Notation:* lowercase letters ($v$, $i$, $q$) are instantaneous values, capitals with subscript 0 ($V_0$, $I_0$) are peak values.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.1, eqs. (15.1)–(15.2)*

^def-a11-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Household ac
> Outlets supply $f = 60\ \mathrm{Hz}$ ($\omega = 120\pi\ \mathrm{rad/s}$) in the United States and $50\ \mathrm{Hz}$ ($\omega = 100\pi\ \mathrm{rad/s}$) in Europe. The familiar ratings "110–120 V" and "220 V" are not the amplitudes but rms values ([[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits|§A11.3]]); the amplitudes are larger by $\sqrt2$, about $156\text{–}170\ \mathrm V$ and $311\ \mathrm V$. The phase of $v(t)$ depends only on when the clock is started, so $v = V_0\sin\omega t$ (zero at $t = 0$) loses no generality.

^rem-a11-1-1

> [!model] Model §A11.1.1: Ideal AC Source; Steady State
> An **ideal ac source** keeps the potential difference across its terminals equal to $V_0\sin\omega t$, whatever is connected to it. We study only the **steady state**: every current and voltage in the circuit oscillates at the source frequency $\omega$, with constant amplitude.
>
> - *Assumes:* the transient that follows switching on has died out. In a circuit with resistance it decays exponentially, as in [[§A6.5 RC Circuits|§A6.5]] and [[§A10.4 RL Circuits|§A10.4]]; what remains is a sinusoid at the driving frequency.
> - *Fails:* for a real source with internal resistance (as for the battery of [[§A6.1 Electromotive Force|§A6.1]]); in the first moments after switching.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.2*

^mod-a11-1-1

## Phasors

> [!definition] Definition §A11.1.2: Phasor
> A sinusoid $a(t) = A_0\sin(\omega t + \alpha)$ is represented by a **phasor**: an arrow of length $A_0$ from the origin, at angle $\omega t + \alpha$ from the horizontal axis, rotating counterclockwise at angular frequency $\omega$. Its projection on the vertical axis is the instantaneous value $a(t)$. Several quantities of the same frequency rotate together, so their relative angles (the phase differences) stay fixed; that is what a **phasor diagram** shows.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.2*

^def-a11-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: What phasor lengths mean
> A voltage phasor and a current phasor on the same diagram have different units; the ratio of their lengths is a resistance or reactance, not a pure number, and their relative scale is arbitrary. Only the angle between them is fixed by the physics.

^cau-a11-1-1

## Resistor

> [!theorem] Theorem §A11.1.2: Resistor in an AC Circuit
> With an ac source $v = V_0\sin\omega t$ across a resistor $R$, the current is
>
> $$
> i_R(t) = I_0 \sin\omega t, \qquad I_0 = \frac{V_0}{R} .
> $$
>
> Current and voltage are **in phase**: they reach their maxima, minima and zeros at the same instants, and their phasors point the same way.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.2*

^thm-a11-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Kirchhoff's loop rule ([[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules|§A6.3]]) at each instant gives $v_R(t) = v(t) = V_0\sin\omega t$. Ohm's law ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law|§A5.3]]) holds instant by instant, so $i_R = v_R/R = (V_0/R)\sin\omega t$.

^der-a11-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits#^mod-a11-1-1|Model §A11.1.1]], [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules|§A6.3]], [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law|§A5.3]]

## Capacitor

> [!theorem] Theorem §A11.1.3: Capacitor in an AC Circuit
> With $v = V_0\sin\omega t$ across a capacitor $C$, the current is
>
> $$
> i_C(t) = \omega C V_0 \cos\omega t = I_0 \sin\!\Bigl(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\Bigr), \qquad \frac{V_0}{I_0} = \frac{1}{\omega C} .
> $$
>
> The current through a capacitor **leads** the voltage across it by $\pi/2$ rad, a quarter cycle.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.2, eq. (15.3)*

^thm-a11-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The loop rule gives $v_C = V_0\sin\omega t$. The relation $q = C v_C$ ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance|§A4.1]]) holds at every instant, so $q(t) = CV_0\sin\omega t$. The current is the rate at which charge arrives on the plate ([[§A5.1 Electric Current|§A5.1]]):
>
> $$
> i_C = \frac{dq}{dt} = \omega C V_0\cos\omega t ,
> $$
>
> and $\cos\omega t = \sin(\omega t + \pi/2)$. The peak current is $I_0 = \omega C V_0$.

^der-a11-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules|§A6.3]], [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance|§A4.1]], [[§A5.1 Electric Current|§A5.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A11.1.3: Capacitive Reactance
> The **capacitive reactance** is the ratio of voltage amplitude to current amplitude for a capacitor:
>
> $$
> X_C = \frac{V_0}{I_0} = \frac{1}{\omega C} .
> $$
>
> Its unit is the ohm. It measures the capacitor's opposition to a change in current and falls with frequency.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.2, eq. (15.3)*

^def-a11-1-3

## Inductor

> [!theorem] Theorem §A11.1.4: Inductor in an AC Circuit
> With $v = V_0\sin\omega t$ across an ideal inductor $L$ (no resistance), the steady-state current is
>
> $$
> i_L(t) = -\frac{V_0}{\omega L}\cos\omega t = I_0 \sin\!\Bigl(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\Bigr), \qquad \frac{V_0}{I_0} = \omega L .
> $$
>
> The current through an inductor **lags** the voltage across it by $\pi/2$ rad, a quarter cycle.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.2, eqs. (15.6)–(15.8)*

^thm-a11-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The self-induced emf of the inductor is $-L\,di_L/dt$ ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance|§A10.2]]), so the potential drop across it, in the direction of the current, is $v_L = +L\,di_L/dt$. The loop rule sets this equal to the source voltage:
>
> $$
> L\frac{di_L}{dt} = V_0 \sin\omega t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad i_L(t) = -\frac{V_0}{\omega L}\cos\omega t + \text{const}.
> $$
>
> The constant would be a steady current with nothing to drive it; in the steady state ([[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits#^mod-a11-1-1|Model §A11.1.1]]) it is zero. Finally $-\cos\omega t = \sin(\omega t - \pi/2)$, and the peak current is $I_0 = V_0/(\omega L)$.

^der-a11-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A10.2 Self-Inductance|§A10.2]], [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules|§A6.3]], [[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits#^mod-a11-1-1|Model §A11.1.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|FTC I]] (Single Variable Analysis)

> [!definition] Definition §A11.1.4: Inductive Reactance
> The **inductive reactance** is the ratio of voltage amplitude to current amplitude for an inductor:
>
> $$
> X_L = \frac{V_0}{I_0} = \omega L .
> $$
>
> Its unit is the ohm. It measures the inductor's opposition to a change in current and grows with frequency.
>
> *Source: UP2 §15.2, eq. (15.8)*

^def-a11-1-4

## Reading the three results together

> [!remark] Remark: Frequency limits
> The reactances explain the dc behaviour of [[§A6.5 RC Circuits|§A6.5]] and [[§A10.4 RL Circuits|§A10.4]] as the limit $\omega \to 0$:
> - **Capacitor.** $X_C \to \infty$ as $\omega \to 0$: a charged capacitor blocks dc. As $\omega \to \infty$, $X_C \to 0$: the capacitor behaves like a plain wire. The current keeps flowing in an ac circuit, although no charge crosses the gap, because the plates are charged and discharged every half cycle.
> - **Inductor.** $X_L \to 0$ as $\omega \to 0$: an ideal inductor is a wire for dc. As $\omega \to \infty$, $X_L \to \infty$: the inductor blocks fast changes.
> - **Resistor.** $R$ does not depend on $\omega$.

^rem-a11-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Reactance is not resistance
> - $X_C$ and $X_L$ relate *amplitudes* only. Because current and voltage are a quarter cycle apart, $v_C(t)/i_C(t)$ is not $X_C$ at any given instant; it runs through all values, including $0$ and $\pm\infty$.
> - A resistor dissipates energy; a capacitor or an inductor stores it and gives it back each cycle ([[§A11.3 Power in AC Circuits|§A11.3]]).
> - Which one leads? Mnemonic "ELI the ICE man": in an inductor ($L$) the emf $E$ comes before the current $I$; in a capacitor ($C$) the current $I$ comes before the emf $E$.

^cau-a11-1-2

## Example

> [!example] Example §A11.1.1: One Source, Three Elements
> An ac generator of amplitude $V_0 = 10\ \mathrm V$ and frequency $f = 60\ \mathrm{Hz}$ is connected in turn across (a) $R = 100\ \Omega$, (b) $C = 10\ \mu\mathrm F$, (c) $L = 15\ \mathrm{mH}$. In each case the element voltage is the source voltage, $v(t) = (10\ \mathrm V)\sin(120\pi t)$, with $\omega = 2\pi f = 377\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, and $I_0 = V_0/X$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \text{(a)}&\quad I_0 = \frac{10\ \mathrm V}{100\ \Omega} = 0.10\ \mathrm A, && i_R = (0.10\ \mathrm A)\sin 120\pi t;\\
> \text{(b)}&\quad X_C = \frac{1}{(377\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})(10 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm F)} = 265\ \Omega,\ I_0 = 3.8 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm A, && i_C = (3.8 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm A)\sin\bigl(120\pi t + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr);\\
> \text{(c)}&\quad X_L = (377\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})(15 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm H) = 5.7\ \Omega,\ I_0 = 1.8\ \mathrm A, && i_L = (1.8\ \mathrm A)\sin\bigl(120\pi t - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr).
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> The same voltage drives currents differing by a factor of almost 50 in amplitude, and at 60 Hz these values of $C$ and $L$ make the capacitor a strong obstacle and the inductor a weak one. Doubling $f$ would halve $X_C$ and double $X_L$.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 15.1*

^ex-a11-1-1

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The inductor current is found by integrating $di_L/dt$, i.e. [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|FTC I]] (Single Variable Analysis); the free integration constant is exactly the transient that the steady-state model discards.
> - The phasor picture of a sinusoid is the same rotating-arrow description as uniform circular motion projected on an axis ([[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-2|CM Theorem §A2.7.2]]); its full use for oscillations is in the planned Oscillations, Waves and Optics subject.
