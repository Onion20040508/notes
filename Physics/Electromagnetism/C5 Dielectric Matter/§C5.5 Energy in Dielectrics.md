*Sources: Zangwill, Modern Electrodynamics (2013), §6.7, Application 6.3, Problem 6.25.*

What is the energy of polarized matter? Level B gave the energy of linear dielectrics, $\frac12\int\vec D\cdot\vec E\,d\tau$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]). This section rebuilds it as Zangwill does, in the language of the conductor chapter ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors|§C4.4]]): an energy $U_E$ whose natural variables are $\vec D$ and the free charges, its Legendre transform $\hat U_E$ with natural variables $\vec E$ and the potentials, and the force on a dielectric body as their derivatives. What these energies imply for forces on whole bodies and on parts of bodies is [[§C5.6 Forces in Dielectrics|§C5.6]]. Everything here was taught (Zangwill §6.7); the energy of field configurations in vacuum is homed in [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy|§C2.3]].

*Notation* as in [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems|§C5.3]]; $\varepsilon(\vec r)$ the permittivity as a function of position; free charges $Q_k$ on conductors at potentials $V_k$; $\vec R$ the position of a dielectric body.

## The energy U_E

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.5.1: Energy Increment in Dielectric Matter
> For any reversible (lossless, hysteresis-free) dielectric, a small change of the free charge changes the total energy by (Zangwill eq. (6.87))
>
> $$
> \delta U_E = \int d^3r\;\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D ,
> $$
>
> integrated over all space; locally, $du_E = d\bigl(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2\bigr) + \vec E\cdot d\vec P = \vec E\cdot d\vec D$ (eq. (6.90)). The local form rests on an **assumption**: that the field energy density inside matter is the vacuum $\frac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ (Zangwill footnote 15, after Purcell and Bobbio), the rest being internal energy of the matter, $\partial u_{\text{mat}}/\partial t = \vec E\cdot\partial\vec P/\partial t$ (eq. (6.89)).
>
> Same formula as level B's $\Delta W = \int(\Delta\vec D)\cdot\vec E\,d\tau$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]); new are the second derivation, which splits the energy into field and matter, and the use of $\vec D$ as natural variable (next).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.1, eqs. (6.86)–(6.90) and footnotes 14–15*

^thm-c5-5-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *Route 1: charging a conductor* (Zangwill Fig. 6.13).
>
> **1. Work.** A conductor at potential $V$ embedded in the dielectric receives charge $\delta Q$ from infinity; the work is $V\delta Q$ ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-2|Theorem §C4.4.2]]; [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]]), and in a reversible process it is stored: $\delta U_E = V\delta Q$.
>
> **2. Gauss.** $\delta Q = \oint_{\mathcal S}\delta\vec D\cdot d\vec S$ with $d\vec S$ pointing from the conductor into the medium ([[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]]); $V$ is constant on $\mathcal S$, so $V\delta Q = \oint_{\mathcal S}V\,\delta\vec D\cdot d\vec S$.
>
> **3. Divergence theorem** on the region $\mathcal V_{\text{out}}$ outside the conductor (eq. (6.86)). Its boundary is $\mathcal S$, with outward normal pointing *into* the conductor ($-d\vec S$), and a sphere at infinity, whose contribution vanishes ($V = O(r^{-1})$, $\delta D = O(r^{-2})$, area $O(r^2)$). So $\oint_{\mathcal S}V\delta\vec D\cdot d\vec S = -\int_{\mathcal V_{\text{out}}}\nabla\cdot(V\delta\vec D)\,d^3r$.
>
> **4. Product rule.** $\nabla\cdot(V\delta\vec D) = \nabla V\cdot\delta\vec D + V\,\nabla\cdot\delta\vec D$, and $\nabla\cdot\delta\vec D = \delta\rho_f = 0$ in $\mathcal V_{\text{out}}$ (no free charge in the medium). Hence $\delta U_E = -\int_{\mathcal V_{\text{out}}}\nabla V\cdot\delta\vec D = \int_{\mathcal V_{\text{out}}}\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D$; inside the conductor $\vec E = \vec 0$, so the integral may run over all space (eq. (6.87)). The result is written in fields only, so it applies whatever the source of the field (footnote 14).
>
> *Route 2: field plus matter.*
>
> **5. Work on matter.** A changing polarization carries the current $\vec j_P = \partial\vec P/\partial t$ ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^rem-c5-1-3|§C5.1, ★ Remark]]). The Coulomb force density $\rho_P\vec E$ on polarization charge moving with velocity $\vec v$ does work at the rate $\rho_P\vec E\cdot\vec v = \vec j_P\cdot\vec E$ (eq. (6.88)), against the cohesive forces of the matter. So $\partial u_{\text{mat}}/\partial t = \vec E\cdot\partial\vec P/\partial t$ (eq. (6.89)).
>
> **6. Add the field.** ⚑ Assumption: $u_{\text{field}} = \frac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ inside matter, as in vacuum ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]) — stated in the theorem. Then $du_E = \varepsilon_0\vec E\cdot d\vec E + \vec E\cdot d\vec P = \vec E\cdot d(\varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P) = \vec E\cdot d\vec D$ (eq. (6.90)), which integrates to eq. (6.87).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Route 1 needs no model of matter; route 2 shows where the energy sits (field and "springs"), at the price of an assumption that only the total, not the split, is measurable.
> - Assumptions used: reversibility (no dissipation, so work is stored), no free charge in the medium (route 1), localized sources.

> [!definition] Definition §C5.5.1: Total Energy of Polarized Matter
> The **total energy** is the net work needed to establish $\vec D$ from zero (Zangwill eq. (6.91)):
>
> $$
> U_E = \int d^3r\int_0^{\vec D}\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D .
> $$
>
> The inner integral can be done only once a constitutive relation ties $\vec D$ to $\vec E$ in every volume element ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^def-c5-2-1|§C5.2, Def. §C5.2.1]]). Since $\delta U_E$ is linear in $\delta\vec D$, and free charge fixes $\vec D$ through $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f$, the natural variables are $\vec D$ or the free charges $Q_k$, together with the position $\vec R$ of the dielectric (eq. (6.92)): $U_E = U_E(\vec D, \vec R)$ or $U_E(Q_k, \vec R)$, the dielectric analogue of the conductor energy of [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-2|Theorem §C4.4.2]].
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.1, eqs. (6.91)–(6.92)*

^def-c5-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.5.2: Field and Force from U_E
> 1. $U_E$ is a functional of $\vec D$ whose functional derivative is the field: $\delta U_E/\delta\vec D(\vec r) = \vec E(\vec r)$ (Zangwill writes $\vec E = \frac1V(\partial U_E/\partial\vec D)_{\vec R}$ for a uniform field in a volume $V$).
> 2. The force on a dielectric body, all free charges held fixed, is (eq. (6.93))
>
> $$
> \vec F = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial U_E}{\partial\vec R}\Bigr)_{Q} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.1, eq. (6.93)*

^thm-c5-5-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Functional derivative.** [[§C5.5 Energy in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-5-1|Theorem §C5.5.1]] reads $\delta U_E = \int\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D\,d^3r$ at fixed $\vec R$, which is the definition of $\delta U_E/\delta\vec D(\vec r) = \vec E(\vec r)$. For uniform fields in a volume $V$, $\delta U_E = V\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D$, Zangwill's form.
>
> **2. Force.** Displace the body slowly by $\delta\vec R$ with every free charge fixed (isolated conductors, no batteries). No external agent supplies electrical energy, so the mechanical work done *by* the field on the body, $\vec F\cdot\delta\vec R$, is paid from $U_E$: $\vec F\cdot\delta\vec R = -\delta U_E|_Q$. Since $\delta\vec R$ is arbitrary, $\vec F = -(\partial U_E/\partial\vec R)_Q$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The same bookkeeping as for conductors at fixed charge ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-2|Theorem §C4.4.2]]); $\vec D$ (free charge) is the variable held fixed, not $\vec E$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.5.3: Energy of a Simple Dielectric
> For piecewise simple dielectrics ([[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^mod-c5-3-1|Model §C5.3.1]]), (Zangwill eq. (6.94))
>
> $$
> U_E[\vec D] = \frac12\int d^3r\,\frac{|\vec D|^2}{\varepsilon} = \frac12\int d^3r\,\vec E\cdot\vec D .
> $$
>
> The formula is level B's ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]); new is its reading as a functional of $\vec D$. Read so (with $\vec D$ fixed by the free charge), it is lowered by placing large $\varepsilon$ where $|\vec D|$ is large: **free charge is attracted toward regions of large permittivity** (it polarizes the medium and draws opposite charge toward itself), and, symmetry aside, $|\vec E|$ tends to be large where $\varepsilon$ is large (the induced dipoles, of strength $\propto\varepsilon$, gain $-\vec p\cdot\vec E$).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.2, eq. (6.94)*

^thm-c5-5-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Integrate along a path.** With $\varepsilon$ fixed, $\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D = \vec D\cdot\delta\vec D/\varepsilon = \delta\bigl(|\vec D|^2/2\varepsilon\bigr)$, an exact differential, so the inner integral of [[§C5.5 Energy in Dielectrics#^def-c5-5-1|Def. §C5.5.1]] is path-independent. Along $\vec D(\lambda) = \lambda\vec D$, $0 \le \lambda \le 1$: $\int_0^1(\lambda\vec D/\varepsilon)\cdot\vec D\,d\lambda = |\vec D|^2/2\varepsilon$.
>
> **2. Other forms.** $|\vec D|^2/\varepsilon = \vec D\cdot\vec E$; integrated by parts, $\frac12\int\vec D\cdot\vec E = \frac12\int\rho_fV$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]).
>
> **3. Attraction to large $\varepsilon$.** At fixed $\vec D$, $\partial(|\vec D|^2/2\varepsilon)/\partial\varepsilon = -|\vec D|^2/2\varepsilon^2 < 0$, largest in magnitude where $|\vec D|$ is large, i.e. near the free charge; the force formula [[§C5.5 Energy in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-5-2|Theorem §C5.5.2]] then pushes the configuration toward large $\varepsilon$ near free charge ([[§C5.6 Forces in Dielectrics#^ex-c5-6-3|Example §C5.6.3]] makes it quantitative).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Linearity makes $U_E$ a state function of $\vec D$ alone; for hysteretic media no such function exists ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^cau-b5-3-2|§B5.3, Caution: Only for linear dielectrics]]).

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: A minimum principle for D
> Zangwill Problem 6.25: among all fields $\vec D'$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec D' = \rho_f$ in $\mathcal V$ and prescribed $\hat n\cdot\vec D'$ on $\partial\mathcal V$, $U_E[\vec D'] = \frac12\int|\vec D'|^2/\varepsilon$ is *minimal* (not merely stationary) at the physical $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$, $\vec E = -\nabla V$. Proof without Lagrange multipliers: write $\vec D' = \vec D + \vec W$, so $\nabla\cdot\vec W = 0$ in $\mathcal V$ and $\hat n\cdot\vec W = 0$ on $\partial\mathcal V$. Then
>
> $$
> U_E[\vec D'] = U_E[\vec D] + \int\vec E\cdot\vec W\,d^3r + \frac12\int\frac{|\vec W|^2}{\varepsilon}\,d^3r, \qquad \int\vec E\cdot\vec W = -\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}V\,\vec W\cdot\hat n\,dS + \int V\,\nabla\cdot\vec W = 0 ,
> $$
>
> so $U_E[\vec D'] - U_E[\vec D] = \frac12\int|\vec W|^2/\varepsilon \ge 0$, with equality only for $\vec W = \vec 0$. In Zangwill's hinted route the Lagrange function enforcing $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f$ at every point turns out to be the potential $V(\vec r)$. With $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0$ this is the dielectric form of Thomson's theorem ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 6.25 (statement and hint) · the proof written here*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: A classical model of quark confinement
> Zangwill's Application 6.3 (after T. D. Lee) models the QCD vacuum as a fictitious dielectric for colour charge with $\varepsilon_r \to 0$ (here $\varepsilon_r < 1$ is allowed: it *anti*-screens, since $\rho_P = (1/\varepsilon_r - 1)\rho_f$ has the sign of $\rho_f$). A small sphere of radius $a$ with charge $q$ sits in a vacuum cavity of radius $R$ that it digs for itself, at a cost $\gamma$ per area. By symmetry $\vec D = q\hat r/4\pi r^2$ everywhere (eq. (6.95)); [[§C5.5 Energy in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-5-3|Theorem §C5.5.3]] gives
>
> $$
> U_{\text{in}} = \int_a^R\frac{D^2}{2\varepsilon_0}4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\frac1a - \frac1R\Bigr), \qquad U_{\text{out}} = \int_R^\infty\frac{D^2}{2\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rR} ,
> $$
>
> and for $\varepsilon_r \to 0$ the $-1/R$ is negligible beside $1/\varepsilon_rR$: $U_{\text{tot}} \approx \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\bigl(\frac1a + \frac1{\varepsilon_rR}\bigr) + 4\pi\gamma R^2$ (eq. (6.96)). $dU/dR = -q^2/(8\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rR^2) + 8\pi\gamma R = 0$ gives $R_*^3 = q^2/(64\pi^2\varepsilon_0\gamma\varepsilon_r)$, so $R_* \propto \varepsilon_r^{-1/3}$, and both terms of $U(R_*)$ scale as $\varepsilon_r^{-2/3} \to \infty$: an isolated "quark" costs infinite energy. A neutral pair (dipole) in a cavity has $\vec D_{\text{out}} = \vec 0$ when $\varepsilon_r = 0$, so $U_{\text{out}} = 0$, $U_{\text{in}} \propto p^2/\varepsilon_0R^3$ by dimensions, and $U_{\text{in}} + U_S$ has a finite minimum at an $\varepsilon_r$-independent $R$: a finite-energy "meson".
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.2, Application 6.3, eqs. (6.95)–(6.96), Fig. 6.14*

## The energy to polarize matter, and the energy at fixed potentials

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.5.4: Energy to Polarize Simple Matter
> Let $\vec E_0$ be the vacuum field of fixed free charges. Inserting a simple dielectric, with those charges held fixed, changes the energy by (Zangwill eq. (6.101))
>
> $$
> \Delta U_E = \frac12\int d^3r\,\bigl[\vec E\cdot\vec D - \vec E_0\cdot\vec D_0\bigr] = -\frac12\int d^3r\;\vec P\cdot\vec E_0 ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec P$ the final polarization. For a small body in a nearly uniform $\vec E_0$, $\Delta U_E = -\frac12\vec p\cdot\vec E_0 = -\frac12\varepsilon_0\alpha|\vec E_0|^2$ (eq. (6.104); $\alpha$ of [[§C5.4 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^def-c5-4-1|§C5.4, Def. §C5.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.3, eqs. (6.97)–(6.101); §6.7.4, eq. (6.104)*

^thm-c5-5-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Start** from [[§C5.5 Energy in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-5-3|Theorem §C5.5.3]] before and after: $\Delta U_E = \frac12\int(\vec E\cdot\vec D - \vec E_0\cdot\vec D_0)$, $\vec D_0 = \varepsilon_0\vec E_0$ (eq. (6.97)).
>
> **2. Add and subtract** $\frac12(\vec E\cdot\vec D_0 - \vec E_0\cdot\vec D)$ (eq. (6.98)); expanding term by term,
>
> $$
> \vec E\cdot(\vec D - \vec D_0) + (\vec D - \vec D_0)\cdot\vec E_0 + \vec E\cdot\vec D_0 - \vec D\cdot\vec E_0 = \vec E\cdot\vec D - \vec E\cdot\vec D_0 + \vec D\cdot\vec E_0 - \vec D_0\cdot\vec E_0 + \vec E\cdot\vec D_0 - \vec D\cdot\vec E_0 = \vec E\cdot\vec D - \vec E_0\cdot\vec D_0 .
> $$
>
> **3. Lemma.** For any electrostatic field $\vec E' = -\nabla V'$ and any localized $\vec D''$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec D'' = \rho''$: $\int\vec E'\cdot\vec D''\,d^3r = \int V'\rho''\,d^3r$ (product rule; the surface term at infinity vanishes; conductor surface charges are included in $\rho''$ as layers). This is eq. (6.87)'s computation run backwards.
>
> **4. Two terms vanish.** $\nabla\cdot(\vec D - \vec D_0) = \rho_f - \rho_f = 0$ because the free charges are held fixed. By the lemma, $\int\vec E\cdot(\vec D - \vec D_0) = 0$ and $\int(\vec D - \vec D_0)\cdot\vec E_0 = 0$ (eqs. (6.99)–(6.100), there written as $V(Q - Q_0) = 0$).
>
> **5. What remains.** $\vec E\cdot\vec D_0 - \vec D\cdot\vec E_0 = \varepsilon_0\vec E\cdot\vec E_0 - (\varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P)\cdot\vec E_0 = -\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$. Hence $\Delta U_E = -\frac12\int\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$.
>
> **6. Small body.** $\vec P$ vanishes outside the body, over which $\vec E_0$ is nearly constant: $\int\vec P\cdot\vec E_0 \approx \vec E_0\cdot\int\vec P = \vec E_0\cdot\vec p$ ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-3|§C5.1, Theorem §C5.1.3]]), and $\vec p = \alpha\varepsilon_0\vec E_0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Only the final polarization and the *unperturbed* field enter; the field changes $\vec E - \vec E_0$ cancel out (steps 3–4).
> - Assumptions used: linear (simple) media before and after, fixed free charges.

*Uses:* [[§C5.5 Energy in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-5-3|Theorem §C5.5.3]], [[§C5.5 Energy in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-5-1|Theorem §C5.5.1]], [[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-3|Theorem §C5.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Where the −½ comes from
> For a small linear body, the interaction energy with the external field is $U_{\text{ext}} = -\vec p\cdot\vec E_0$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]]; [[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]]), but creating $\vec p$ against the cohesive forces costs $U_{\text{int}} = \zeta p^2$ for some $\zeta > 0$ (it must be at least quadratic for a minimum to exist). Minimizing $-pE_0 + \zeta p^2$ over $p$ gives $p = E_0/2\zeta$, so $U_{\text{int}} = \frac12pE_0$ and $U = -pE_0 + \frac12pE_0 = -\frac12\vec p\cdot\vec E_0$ (eqs. (6.102)–(6.104)): half the interaction energy is spent on the "springs". For an extended body add the interaction of $\vec P$ with its own induced field $\vec E_{\text{ind}}$: $U_{\text{ext}} = -\int\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$, $U_{\text{self}} = -\frac12\int\vec P\cdot\vec E_{\text{ind}}$ (the self-energy of a dipole distribution, the $\frac12$ avoiding double counting; [[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]]), and, by linearity, $U_{\text{int}} = \int\int_0^{\vec P}\vec E\cdot\delta\vec P = \frac12\int\vec P\cdot(\vec E_0 + \vec E_{\text{ind}})$ (eqs. (6.105)–(6.108)). The $\vec E_{\text{ind}}$ terms cancel in the sum, leaving $-\frac12\int\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$, Theorem §C5.5.4 again. Level B's "springs" remark is the same physics ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^rem-b5-3-4|§B5.3, Remark: Two energies, and the springs]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.4, eqs. (6.102)–(6.108)*

> [!definition] Definition §C5.5.2: Energy Functional at Fixed Potentials
> The Legendre transform of $U_E$ with respect to $\vec D$ is (Zangwill eq. (6.109))
>
> $$
> \hat U_E = U_E - \int d^3r\,\vec D\cdot\vec E \qquad \Bigl( = -\frac12\int d^3r\,\vec D\cdot\vec E \ \text{ for simple dielectrics}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the dielectric analogue of the conductor function used at fixed potentials ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^def-c4-4-1|Def. §C4.4.1]]; forces [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors#^thm-c4-4-3|Theorem §C4.4.3]]); the thermodynamic Legendre transform is [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|TH Def. §B1.2.2]].
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.5, eq. (6.109)*

^def-c5-5-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.5.5: Field and Force from Û_E
> 1. $\delta\hat U_E = -\int d^3r\,\vec D\cdot\delta\vec E = -\sum_kQ_k\,\delta V_k$ (eqs. (6.110)–(6.111)): the natural variables of $\hat U_E$ are $\vec E$, or the conductor potentials $V_k$, and $\vec R$ (eq. (6.112)).
> 2. $\delta\hat U_E/\delta\vec E(\vec r) = -\vec D(\vec r)$, and the force on a dielectric with all conductor potentials fixed is (eq. (6.114))
>
> $$
> \vec F = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial\hat U_E}{\partial\vec R}\Bigr)_{\vec E} = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial\hat U_E}{\partial\vec R}\Bigr)_{V} .
> $$
>
> 3. For simple matter inserted at fixed potentials, $\Delta\hat U_E = -\frac12\int d^3r\,\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$, the same form as at fixed charge ([[§C5.5 Energy in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-5-4|Theorem §C5.5.4]]); Zangwill uses it in Example 6.8 ([[§C5.6 Forces in Dielectrics#^ex-c5-6-4|Example §C5.6.4]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.5, eqs. (6.109)–(6.114) · part 3 derived here*

^thm-c5-5-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Variation.** $\delta\hat U_E = \delta U_E - \int(\delta\vec D\cdot\vec E + \vec D\cdot\delta\vec E)$, and $\delta U_E = \int\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D$ ([[§C5.5 Energy in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-5-1|Theorem §C5.5.1]]); the $\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D$ terms cancel: $\delta\hat U_E = -\int\vec D\cdot\delta\vec E$ (eq. (6.110)).
>
> **2. In terms of potentials.** With $\delta\vec E = -\nabla\delta V$ and free charge only on conductors: $\int\vec D\cdot\delta\vec E = -\int_{\mathcal V_{\text{out}}}\vec D\cdot\nabla\delta V = -\int_{\mathcal V_{\text{out}}}\nabla\cdot(\delta V\vec D) + \int_{\mathcal V_{\text{out}}}\delta V\,\nabla\cdot\vec D$. The last term vanishes ($\nabla\cdot\vec D = 0$ in the medium); the divergence term becomes, as in [[§C5.5 Energy in Dielectrics#^der-c5-5-1|Derivation §C5.5.1]] step 3, $+\sum_k\delta V_k\oint_{\mathcal S_k}\vec D\cdot d\vec S = \sum_kQ_k\delta V_k$ (eq. (6.111)).
>
> **3. Force.** By the chain rule $\delta\hat U_E = (\partial\hat U_E/\partial\vec E)_{\vec R}\cdot\delta\vec E + (\partial\hat U_E/\partial\vec R)_{\vec E}\cdot\delta\vec R$ (eq. (6.113)), and comparison with step 1 gives $\delta\hat U_E/\delta\vec E = -\vec D$. For the force, take a slow displacement $\delta\vec R$ at fixed $V_k$: batteries supply $\sum_kV_k\delta Q_k$, which goes into $\delta U_E$ and mechanical work $\vec F\cdot\delta\vec R$. By the lemma of [[§C5.5 Energy in Dielectrics#^der-c5-5-4|Derivation §C5.5.4]] (step 3), $\int\vec D\cdot\vec E = \sum_kQ_kV_k$, so $U_E = \hat U_E + \sum_kQ_kV_k$ and, at fixed $V_k$, $\delta U_E = \delta\hat U_E + \sum_kV_k\delta Q_k$. Energy balance: $\sum_kV_k\delta Q_k = \delta\hat U_E + \sum_kV_k\delta Q_k + \vec F\cdot\delta\vec R$, i.e. $\vec F = -(\partial\hat U_E/\partial\vec R)_V$. Zangwill's eq. (6.114) prints the subscript of the middle expression as $\mathbf D$; by eq. (6.113) it is $\mathbf E$, the natural variable of $\hat U_E$, which corresponds to fixed potentials as $\vec D$ corresponds to fixed charges.
>
> **4. Part 3.** Simple media: $\Delta\hat U_E = -\frac12\int(\vec D\cdot\vec E - \vec D_0\cdot\vec E_0)$. Now the potentials of the conductors (where all free charge sits) are fixed, so $\vec E - \vec E_0 = -\nabla(V - V_0)$ with $V - V_0 = 0$ wherever $\rho_f$ or $\rho_{f,0}$ is nonzero; the lemma gives $\int(\vec E - \vec E_0)\cdot\vec D = \int(V - V_0)\rho_f = 0$ and likewise $\int(\vec E - \vec E_0)\cdot\vec D_0 = 0$. Hence $\int\vec D\cdot\vec E = \int\vec D\cdot\vec E_0$, and $\int\vec D\cdot\vec E - \vec D_0\cdot\vec E_0 = \int(\vec D - \vec D_0)\cdot\vec E_0 = \int[\varepsilon_0(\vec E - \vec E_0) + \vec P]\cdot\vec E_0 = 0 + \int\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$, the zero being $\int(\vec E - \vec E_0)\cdot\vec D_0$ again. So $\Delta\hat U_E = -\frac12\int\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - $U_E$ and $\hat U_E$ are the two thermodynamic potentials of electrostatics with matter: fixed free charge (natural variable $\vec D$) and fixed potentials (natural variable $\vec E$), exactly as for conductors.
> - At fixed potentials the battery supplies twice the gain in stored energy, half of which does mechanical work; $\hat U_E$ absorbs this bookkeeping.

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $U_E$ and $\hat U_E$ are a Legendre pair, like $U$ and $F$ in thermodynamics ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|TH Def. §B1.2.2]]); the two "energies of a polarizable sample" and the work terms of a magnetizable one are [[§B3.4★ Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^def-b3-4-3|TH Def. §B3.4.3]], whose caution on which energy to use applies here verbatim.
