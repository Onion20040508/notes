*Sources: Zangwill, Modern Electrodynamics (2013), §6.7–§6.8, Examples 6.5–6.8, Applications 6.3–6.4, Problems 6.24–6.25.*

What is the energy of polarized matter, and what force does a field exert on it — on a whole body and on a part of one? Level B gave the energy of linear dielectrics, $\frac12\int\vec D\cdot\vec E\,d\tau$, and the force on a slab from $dC/dx$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]], [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-6|Theorem §B5.3.6]]). This section rebuilds both as Zangwill does, in the language of the conductor chapter ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors|§C4.4]]): an energy $U_E$ whose natural variables are $\vec D$ and the free charges, its Legendre transform $\hat U_E$ with natural variables $\vec E$ and the potentials, and forces as their derivatives. Then it shows that Coulomb's law gives the force on an isolated body but *not* on a part of a body, derives the correct force density (Helmholtz) and the dielectric stress tensor, which generalizes the vacuum one of [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor|§C2.4]]. Everything here was taught (Zangwill §6.7–§6.8); the energy of field configurations in vacuum is homed in [[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy|§C2.3]].

*Notation* as in [[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems|§C5.2]]; $\varepsilon(\vec r)$ the permittivity as a function of position; free charges $Q_k$ on conductors at potentials $V_k$; $\vec R$ the position of a dielectric body.

## The energy U_E

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.4.1: Energy Increment in Dielectric Matter
> For any reversible (lossless, hysteresis-free) dielectric, a small change of the free charge changes the total energy by (Zangwill eq. (6.87))
>
> $$
> \delta U_E = \int d^3r\;\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D ,
> $$
>
> integrated over all space; locally, $du_E = d\bigl(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2\bigr) + \vec E\cdot d\vec P = \vec E\cdot d\vec D$ (eq. (6.90)). The local form rests on an **assumption**: that the field energy density inside matter is the vacuum $\frac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ (Zangwill footnote 15, after Purcell and Bobbio), the rest being internal energy of the matter, $\partial u_{\text{mat}}/\partial t = \vec E\cdot\partial\vec P/\partial t$ (eq. (6.89)).
>
> Same formula as level B's $\Delta W = \int(\Delta\vec D)\cdot\vec E\,d\tau$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]); new are the second derivation, which splits the energy into field and matter, and the use of $\vec D$ as natural variable (next).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.1, eqs. (6.86)–(6.90) and footnotes 14–15*

^thm-c5-4-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *Route 1: charging a conductor* (Zangwill Fig. 6.13).
>
> **1. Work.** A conductor at potential $V$ embedded in the dielectric receives charge $\delta Q$ from infinity; the work is $V\delta Q$ ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors|§C4.4]]; [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]]), and in a reversible process it is stored: $\delta U_E = V\delta Q$.
>
> **2. Gauss.** $\delta Q = \oint_{\mathcal S}\delta\vec D\cdot d\vec S$ with $d\vec S$ pointing from the conductor into the medium ([[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]]); $V$ is constant on $\mathcal S$, so $V\delta Q = \oint_{\mathcal S}V\,\delta\vec D\cdot d\vec S$.
>
> **3. Divergence theorem** on the region $\mathcal V_{\text{out}}$ outside the conductor (eq. (6.86)). Its boundary is $\mathcal S$, with outward normal pointing *into* the conductor ($-d\vec S$), and a sphere at infinity, whose contribution vanishes ($V = O(r^{-1})$, $\delta D = O(r^{-2})$, area $O(r^2)$). So $\oint_{\mathcal S}V\delta\vec D\cdot d\vec S = -\int_{\mathcal V_{\text{out}}}\nabla\cdot(V\delta\vec D)\,d^3r$.
>
> **4. Product rule.** $\nabla\cdot(V\delta\vec D) = \nabla V\cdot\delta\vec D + V\,\nabla\cdot\delta\vec D$, and $\nabla\cdot\delta\vec D = \delta\rho_f = 0$ in $\mathcal V_{\text{out}}$ (no free charge in the medium). Hence $\delta U_E = -\int_{\mathcal V_{\text{out}}}\nabla V\cdot\delta\vec D = \int_{\mathcal V_{\text{out}}}\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D$; inside the conductor $\vec E = \vec 0$, so the integral may run over all space (eq. (6.87)). The result is written in fields only, so it applies whatever the source of the field (footnote 14).
>
> *Route 2: field plus matter.*
>
> **5. Work on matter.** A changing polarization carries the current $\vec j_P = \partial\vec P/\partial t$ ([[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter#^rem-c5-1-3|§C5.1, ★ Remark]]). The Coulomb force density $\rho_P\vec E$ on polarization charge moving with velocity $\vec v$ does work at the rate $\rho_P\vec E\cdot\vec v = \vec j_P\cdot\vec E$ (eq. (6.88)), against the cohesive forces of the matter. So $\partial u_{\text{mat}}/\partial t = \vec E\cdot\partial\vec P/\partial t$ (eq. (6.89)).
>
> **6. Add the field.** ⚑ Assumption: $u_{\text{field}} = \frac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ inside matter, as in vacuum ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy|§C2.3]]) — stated in the theorem. Then $du_E = \varepsilon_0\vec E\cdot d\vec E + \vec E\cdot d\vec P = \vec E\cdot d(\varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P) = \vec E\cdot d\vec D$ (eq. (6.90)), which integrates to eq. (6.87).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Route 1 needs no model of matter; route 2 shows where the energy sits (field and "springs"), at the price of an assumption that only the total, not the split, is measurable.
> - Assumptions used: reversibility (no dissipation, so work is stored), no free charge in the medium (route 1), localized sources.

> [!definition] Definition §C5.4.1: Total Energy of Polarized Matter
> The **total energy** is the net work needed to establish $\vec D$ from zero (Zangwill eq. (6.91)):
>
> $$
> U_E = \int d^3r\int_0^{\vec D}\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D .
> $$
>
> The inner integral can be done only once a constitutive relation ties $\vec D$ to $\vec E$ in every volume element ([[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter#^def-c5-1-3|§C5.1, Def. §C5.1.3]]). Since $\delta U_E$ is linear in $\delta\vec D$, and free charge fixes $\vec D$ through $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f$, the natural variables are $\vec D$ or the free charges $Q_k$, together with the position $\vec R$ of the dielectric (eq. (6.92)): $U_E = U_E(\vec D, \vec R)$ or $U_E(Q_k, \vec R)$, the dielectric analogue of the conductor energy of [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors|§C4.4]].
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.1, eqs. (6.91)–(6.92)*

^def-c5-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.4.2: Field and Force from U_E
> 1. $U_E$ is a functional of $\vec D$ whose functional derivative is the field: $\delta U_E/\delta\vec D(\vec r) = \vec E(\vec r)$ (Zangwill writes $\vec E = \frac1V(\partial U_E/\partial\vec D)_{\vec R}$ for a uniform field in a volume $V$).
> 2. The force on a dielectric body, all free charges held fixed, is (eq. (6.93))
>
> $$
> \vec F = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial U_E}{\partial\vec R}\Bigr)_{Q} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.1, eq. (6.93)*

^thm-c5-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Functional derivative.** [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-1|Theorem §C5.4.1]] reads $\delta U_E = \int\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D\,d^3r$ at fixed $\vec R$, which is the definition of $\delta U_E/\delta\vec D(\vec r) = \vec E(\vec r)$. For uniform fields in a volume $V$, $\delta U_E = V\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D$, Zangwill's form.
>
> **2. Force.** Displace the body slowly by $\delta\vec R$ with every free charge fixed (isolated conductors, no batteries). No external agent supplies electrical energy, so the mechanical work done *by* the field on the body, $\vec F\cdot\delta\vec R$, is paid from $U_E$: $\vec F\cdot\delta\vec R = -\delta U_E|_Q$. Since $\delta\vec R$ is arbitrary, $\vec F = -(\partial U_E/\partial\vec R)_Q$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The same bookkeeping as for conductors at fixed charge ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors|§C4.4]]); $\vec D$ (free charge) is the variable held fixed, not $\vec E$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.4.3: Energy of a Simple Dielectric
> For piecewise simple dielectrics ([[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^mod-c5-2-1|Model §C5.2.1]]), (Zangwill eq. (6.94))
>
> $$
> U_E[\vec D] = \frac12\int d^3r\,\frac{|\vec D|^2}{\varepsilon} = \frac12\int d^3r\,\vec E\cdot\vec D .
> $$
>
> Read as a functional of $\vec D$ (fixed by the free charge), it is lowered by placing large $\varepsilon$ where $|\vec D|$ is large: **free charge is attracted toward regions of large permittivity** (it polarizes the medium and draws opposite charge toward itself), and, symmetry aside, $|\vec E|$ tends to be large where $\varepsilon$ is large (the induced dipoles, of strength $\propto\varepsilon$, gain $-\vec p\cdot\vec E$).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.2, eq. (6.94)*

^thm-c5-4-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Integrate along a path.** With $\varepsilon$ fixed, $\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D = \vec D\cdot\delta\vec D/\varepsilon = \delta\bigl(|\vec D|^2/2\varepsilon\bigr)$, an exact differential, so the inner integral of [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^def-c5-4-1|Def. §C5.4.1]] is path-independent. Along $\vec D(\lambda) = \lambda\vec D$, $0 \le \lambda \le 1$: $\int_0^1(\lambda\vec D/\varepsilon)\cdot\vec D\,d\lambda = |\vec D|^2/2\varepsilon$.
>
> **2. Other forms.** $|\vec D|^2/\varepsilon = \vec D\cdot\vec E$; integrated by parts, $\frac12\int\vec D\cdot\vec E = \frac12\int\rho_fV$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]).
>
> **3. Attraction to large $\varepsilon$.** At fixed $\vec D$, $\partial(|\vec D|^2/2\varepsilon)/\partial\varepsilon = -|\vec D|^2/2\varepsilon^2 < 0$, largest in magnitude where $|\vec D|$ is large, i.e. near the free charge; the force formula [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-2|Theorem §C5.4.2]] then pushes the configuration toward large $\varepsilon$ near free charge ([[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^ex-c5-4-3|Example §C5.4.3]] makes it quantitative).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Linearity makes $U_E$ a state function of $\vec D$ alone; for hysteretic media no such function exists ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^cau-b5-3-2|§B5.3, Caution: Only for linear dielectrics]]).

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: A minimum principle for D
> Zangwill Problem 6.25: among all fields $\vec D'$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec D' = \rho_f$ in $\mathcal V$ and prescribed $\hat n\cdot\vec D'$ on $\partial\mathcal V$, $U_E[\vec D'] = \frac12\int|\vec D'|^2/\varepsilon$ is *minimal* (not merely stationary) at the physical $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$, $\vec E = -\nabla V$. Proof without Lagrange multipliers: write $\vec D' = \vec D + \vec W$, so $\nabla\cdot\vec W = 0$ in $\mathcal V$ and $\hat n\cdot\vec W = 0$ on $\partial\mathcal V$. Then
>
> $$
> U_E[\vec D'] = U_E[\vec D] + \int\vec E\cdot\vec W\,d^3r + \frac12\int\frac{|\vec W|^2}{\varepsilon}\,d^3r, \qquad \int\vec E\cdot\vec W = -\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}V\,\vec W\cdot\hat n\,dS + \int V\,\nabla\cdot\vec W = 0 ,
> $$
>
> so $U_E[\vec D'] - U_E[\vec D] = \frac12\int|\vec W|^2/\varepsilon \ge 0$, with equality only for $\vec W = \vec 0$. In Zangwill's hinted route the Lagrange function enforcing $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f$ at every point turns out to be the potential $V(\vec r)$. With $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0$ this is the dielectric form of Thomson's theorem ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem|§C4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 6.25 (statement and hint) · the proof written here*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: A classical model of quark confinement
> Zangwill's Application 6.3 (after T. D. Lee) models the QCD vacuum as a fictitious dielectric for colour charge with $\varepsilon_r \to 0$ (here $\varepsilon_r < 1$ is allowed: it *anti*-screens, since $\rho_P = (1/\varepsilon_r - 1)\rho_f$ has the sign of $\rho_f$). A small sphere of radius $a$ with charge $q$ sits in a vacuum cavity of radius $R$ that it digs for itself, at a cost $\gamma$ per area. By symmetry $\vec D = q\hat r/4\pi r^2$ everywhere (eq. (6.95)); [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-3|Theorem §C5.4.3]] gives
>
> $$
> U_{\text{in}} = \int_a^R\frac{D^2}{2\varepsilon_0}4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\frac1a - \frac1R\Bigr), \qquad U_{\text{out}} = \int_R^\infty\frac{D^2}{2\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rR} ,
> $$
>
> and for $\varepsilon_r \to 0$ the $-1/R$ is negligible beside $1/\varepsilon_rR$: $U_{\text{tot}} \approx \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\bigl(\frac1a + \frac1{\varepsilon_rR}\bigr) + 4\pi\gamma R^2$ (eq. (6.96)). $dU/dR = -q^2/(8\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rR^2) + 8\pi\gamma R = 0$ gives $R_*^3 = q^2/(64\pi^2\varepsilon_0\gamma\varepsilon_r)$, so $R_* \propto \varepsilon_r^{-1/3}$, and both terms of $U(R_*)$ scale as $\varepsilon_r^{-2/3} \to \infty$: an isolated "quark" costs infinite energy. A neutral pair (dipole) in a cavity has $\vec D_{\text{out}} = \vec 0$ when $\varepsilon_r = 0$, so $U_{\text{out}} = 0$, $U_{\text{in}} \propto p^2/\varepsilon_0R^3$ by dimensions, and $U_{\text{in}} + U_S$ has a finite minimum at an $\varepsilon_r$-independent $R$: a finite-energy "meson".
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.2, Application 6.3, eqs. (6.95)–(6.96), Fig. 6.14*

## The energy to polarize matter, and the energy at fixed potentials

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.4.4: Energy to Polarize Simple Matter
> Let $\vec E_0$ be the vacuum field of fixed free charges. Inserting a simple dielectric, with those charges held fixed, changes the energy by (Zangwill eq. (6.101))
>
> $$
> \Delta U_E = \frac12\int d^3r\,\bigl[\vec E\cdot\vec D - \vec E_0\cdot\vec D_0\bigr] = -\frac12\int d^3r\;\vec P\cdot\vec E_0 ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec P$ the final polarization. For a small body in a nearly uniform $\vec E_0$, $\Delta U_E = -\frac12\vec p\cdot\vec E_0 = -\frac12\varepsilon_0\alpha|\vec E_0|^2$ (eq. (6.104); $\alpha$ of [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^def-c5-3-1|§C5.3, Def. §C5.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.3, eqs. (6.97)–(6.101); §6.7.4, eq. (6.104)*

^thm-c5-4-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Start** from [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-3|Theorem §C5.4.3]] before and after: $\Delta U_E = \frac12\int(\vec E\cdot\vec D - \vec E_0\cdot\vec D_0)$, $\vec D_0 = \varepsilon_0\vec E_0$ (eq. (6.97)).
>
> **2. Add and subtract** $\frac12(\vec E\cdot\vec D_0 - \vec E_0\cdot\vec D)$ (eq. (6.98)); expanding term by term,
>
> $$
> \vec E\cdot(\vec D - \vec D_0) + (\vec D - \vec D_0)\cdot\vec E_0 + \vec E\cdot\vec D_0 - \vec D\cdot\vec E_0 = \vec E\cdot\vec D - \vec E\cdot\vec D_0 + \vec D\cdot\vec E_0 - \vec D_0\cdot\vec E_0 + \vec E\cdot\vec D_0 - \vec D\cdot\vec E_0 = \vec E\cdot\vec D - \vec E_0\cdot\vec D_0 .
> $$
>
> **3. Lemma.** For any electrostatic field $\vec E' = -\nabla V'$ and any localized $\vec D''$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec D'' = \rho''$: $\int\vec E'\cdot\vec D''\,d^3r = \int V'\rho''\,d^3r$ (product rule; the surface term at infinity vanishes; conductor surface charges are included in $\rho''$ as layers). This is eq. (6.87)'s computation run backwards.
>
> **4. Two terms vanish.** $\nabla\cdot(\vec D - \vec D_0) = \rho_f - \rho_f = 0$ because the free charges are held fixed. By the lemma, $\int\vec E\cdot(\vec D - \vec D_0) = 0$ and $\int(\vec D - \vec D_0)\cdot\vec E_0 = 0$ (eqs. (6.99)–(6.100), there written as $V(Q - Q_0) = 0$).
>
> **5. What remains.** $\vec E\cdot\vec D_0 - \vec D\cdot\vec E_0 = \varepsilon_0\vec E\cdot\vec E_0 - (\varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P)\cdot\vec E_0 = -\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$. Hence $\Delta U_E = -\frac12\int\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$.
>
> **6. Small body.** $\vec P$ vanishes outside the body, over which $\vec E_0$ is nearly constant: $\int\vec P\cdot\vec E_0 \approx \vec E_0\cdot\int\vec P = \vec E_0\cdot\vec p$ ([[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-1-3|§C5.1, Theorem §C5.1.3]]), and $\vec p = \alpha\varepsilon_0\vec E_0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Only the final polarization and the *unperturbed* field enter; the field changes $\vec E - \vec E_0$ cancel out (steps 3–4).
> - Assumptions used: linear (simple) media before and after, fixed free charges.

> [!remark] Remark: Where the −½ comes from
> For a small linear body, the interaction energy with the external field is $U_{\text{ext}} = -\vec p\cdot\vec E_0$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]]; [[§C3.4 Multipole Interaction Energies and Molecules on a Lattice|§C3.4]]), but creating $\vec p$ against the cohesive forces costs $U_{\text{int}} = \zeta p^2$ for some $\zeta > 0$ (it must be at least quadratic for a minimum to exist). Minimizing $-pE_0 + \zeta p^2$ over $p$ gives $p = E_0/2\zeta$, so $U_{\text{int}} = \frac12pE_0$ and $U = -pE_0 + \frac12pE_0 = -\frac12\vec p\cdot\vec E_0$ (eqs. (6.102)–(6.104)): half the interaction energy is spent on the "springs". For an extended body add the interaction of $\vec P$ with its own induced field $\vec E_{\text{ind}}$: $U_{\text{ext}} = -\int\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$, $U_{\text{self}} = -\frac12\int\vec P\cdot\vec E_{\text{ind}}$ (the self-energy of a dipole distribution, the $\frac12$ avoiding double counting), and, by linearity, $U_{\text{int}} = \int\int_0^{\vec P}\vec E\cdot\delta\vec P = \frac12\int\vec P\cdot(\vec E_0 + \vec E_{\text{ind}})$ (eqs. (6.105)–(6.108)). The $\vec E_{\text{ind}}$ terms cancel in the sum, leaving $-\frac12\int\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$, Theorem §C5.4.4 again. Level B's "springs" remark is the same physics ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^rem-b5-3-4|§B5.3, Remark: Two energies, and the springs]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.4, eqs. (6.102)–(6.108)*

> [!definition] Definition §C5.4.2: Energy Functional at Fixed Potentials
> The Legendre transform of $U_E$ with respect to $\vec D$ is (Zangwill eq. (6.109))
>
> $$
> \hat U_E = U_E - \int d^3r\,\vec D\cdot\vec E \qquad \Bigl( = -\frac12\int d^3r\,\vec D\cdot\vec E \ \text{ for simple dielectrics}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the dielectric analogue of the conductor function used at fixed potentials ([[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors|§C4.4]]); the thermodynamic Legendre transform is [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|TH Def. §B1.2.2]].
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.5, eq. (6.109)*

^def-c5-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.4.5: Field and Force from Û_E
> 1. $\delta\hat U_E = -\int d^3r\,\vec D\cdot\delta\vec E = -\sum_kQ_k\,\delta V_k$ (eqs. (6.110)–(6.111)): the natural variables of $\hat U_E$ are $\vec E$, or the conductor potentials $V_k$, and $\vec R$ (eq. (6.112)).
> 2. $\delta\hat U_E/\delta\vec E(\vec r) = -\vec D(\vec r)$, and the force on a dielectric with all conductor potentials fixed is (eq. (6.114))
>
> $$
> \vec F = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial\hat U_E}{\partial\vec R}\Bigr)_{\vec E} = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial\hat U_E}{\partial\vec R}\Bigr)_{V} .
> $$
>
> 3. For simple matter inserted at fixed potentials, $\Delta\hat U_E = -\frac12\int d^3r\,\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$, the same form as at fixed charge ([[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-4|Theorem §C5.4.4]]); Zangwill uses it in Example 6.8 ([[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^ex-c5-4-4|Example §C5.4.4]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.7.5, eqs. (6.109)–(6.114) · part 3 derived here*

^thm-c5-4-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Variation.** $\delta\hat U_E = \delta U_E - \int(\delta\vec D\cdot\vec E + \vec D\cdot\delta\vec E)$, and $\delta U_E = \int\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D$ ([[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-1|Theorem §C5.4.1]]); the $\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D$ terms cancel: $\delta\hat U_E = -\int\vec D\cdot\delta\vec E$ (eq. (6.110)).
>
> **2. In terms of potentials.** With $\delta\vec E = -\nabla\delta V$ and free charge only on conductors: $\int\vec D\cdot\delta\vec E = -\int_{\mathcal V_{\text{out}}}\vec D\cdot\nabla\delta V = -\int_{\mathcal V_{\text{out}}}\nabla\cdot(\delta V\vec D) + \int_{\mathcal V_{\text{out}}}\delta V\,\nabla\cdot\vec D$. The last term vanishes ($\nabla\cdot\vec D = 0$ in the medium); the divergence term becomes, as in [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^der-c5-4-1|Derivation §C5.4.1]] step 3, $+\sum_k\delta V_k\oint_{\mathcal S_k}\vec D\cdot d\vec S = \sum_kQ_k\delta V_k$ (eq. (6.111)).
>
> **3. Force.** By the chain rule $\delta\hat U_E = (\partial\hat U_E/\partial\vec E)_{\vec R}\cdot\delta\vec E + (\partial\hat U_E/\partial\vec R)_{\vec E}\cdot\delta\vec R$ (eq. (6.113)), and comparison with step 1 gives $\delta\hat U_E/\delta\vec E = -\vec D$. For the force, take a slow displacement $\delta\vec R$ at fixed $V_k$: batteries supply $\sum_kV_k\delta Q_k$, which goes into $\delta U_E$ and mechanical work $\vec F\cdot\delta\vec R$. By the lemma of [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^der-c5-4-4|Derivation §C5.4.4]] (step 3), $\int\vec D\cdot\vec E = \sum_kQ_kV_k$, so $U_E = \hat U_E + \sum_kQ_kV_k$ and, at fixed $V_k$, $\delta U_E = \delta\hat U_E + \sum_kV_k\delta Q_k$. Energy balance: $\sum_kV_k\delta Q_k = \delta\hat U_E + \sum_kV_k\delta Q_k + \vec F\cdot\delta\vec R$, i.e. $\vec F = -(\partial\hat U_E/\partial\vec R)_V$. Zangwill's eq. (6.114) prints the subscript of the middle expression as $\mathbf D$; by eq. (6.113) it is $\mathbf E$, the natural variable of $\hat U_E$, which corresponds to fixed potentials as $\vec D$ corresponds to fixed charges.
>
> **4. Part 3.** Simple media: $\Delta\hat U_E = -\frac12\int(\vec D\cdot\vec E - \vec D_0\cdot\vec E_0)$. Now the potentials of the conductors (where all free charge sits) are fixed, so $\vec E - \vec E_0 = -\nabla(V - V_0)$ with $V - V_0 = 0$ wherever $\rho_f$ or $\rho_{f,0}$ is nonzero; the lemma gives $\int(\vec E - \vec E_0)\cdot\vec D = \int(V - V_0)\rho_f = 0$ and likewise $\int(\vec E - \vec E_0)\cdot\vec D_0 = 0$. Hence $\int\vec D\cdot\vec E = \int\vec D\cdot\vec E_0$, and $\int\vec D\cdot\vec E - \vec D_0\cdot\vec E_0 = \int(\vec D - \vec D_0)\cdot\vec E_0 = \int[\varepsilon_0(\vec E - \vec E_0) + \vec P]\cdot\vec E_0 = 0 + \int\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$, the zero being $\int(\vec E - \vec E_0)\cdot\vec D_0$ again. So $\Delta\hat U_E = -\frac12\int\vec P\cdot\vec E_0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - $U_E$ and $\hat U_E$ are the two thermodynamic potentials of electrostatics with matter: fixed free charge (natural variable $\vec D$) and fixed potentials (natural variable $\vec E$), exactly as for conductors.
> - At fixed potentials the battery supplies twice the gain in stored energy, half of which does mechanical work; $\hat U_E$ absorbs this bookkeeping.

## Forces on dielectric matter

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.4.6: Force on an Isolated Dielectric Body
> A dielectric body in vacuum with polarization $\vec P$ (caused by $\vec E_0$ or not) and free charge $\rho_f$ inside, in an external field $\vec E_0$, feels (Zangwill eqs. (6.115)–(6.116))
>
> $$
> \vec F = \int d^3r\,\bigl[\rho_f - \nabla\cdot\vec P\bigr]\vec E_0 + \oint dS\,(\vec P\cdot\hat n)\,\vec E_0 = \int d^3r\,\bigl[\rho_f + (\vec P\cdot\nabla)\bigr]\vec E_0 ,
> $$
>
> and in terms of the total field $\vec E = \vec E_0 + \vec E_{\text{self}}$ (eqs. (6.118)–(6.119)), with $\vec E_{\text{avg}} = \frac12(\vec E_{\text{in}} + \vec E_{\text{out}})$ on the surface,
>
> $$
> \vec F = \int d^3r\,\bigl[\rho_f - \nabla\cdot\vec P\bigr]\vec E + \oint dS\,(\vec P\cdot\hat n)\,\vec E_{\text{avg}} = \int d^3r\,\bigl[\rho_f + (\vec P\cdot\nabla)\bigr]\vec E + \frac{1}{2\varepsilon_0}\oint dS\,(\hat n\cdot\vec P)^2\,\hat n .
> $$
>
> These hold for the **whole** body only, and their bilinear macroscopic form is, strictly, an assumption of the macroscopic theory to be checked by experiment (Zangwill).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.8.1, eqs. (6.115)–(6.119); Problem 6.24*

^thm-c5-4-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Coulomb.** The body's charges are $\rho_f$, $\rho_P = -\nabla\cdot\vec P$ and $\sigma_P = \vec P\cdot\hat n$ ([[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter#^def-c5-1-2|§C5.1, Def. §C5.1.2]]); $\vec E_0$, produced by sources outside, is smooth across the body; the force is the sum of charge times field (eq. (6.115)).
>
> **2. Identity (6.117).** For smooth $\vec a$, $\vec b$ on $\mathcal V$: $\partial_i(a_ib_j) = (\partial_ia_i)b_j + a_i\partial_ib_j$; integrating and applying the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]) to the vector $a_ib_j$ (fixed $j$): $\oint dS\,(\hat n\cdot\vec a)\vec b = \int(\nabla\cdot\vec a)\vec b + \int(\vec a\cdot\nabla)\vec b$.
>
> **3. Dipole form.** With $\vec a = \vec P$, $\vec b = \vec E_0$: $\oint(\vec P\cdot\hat n)\vec E_0 = \int(\nabla\cdot\vec P)\vec E_0 + \int(\vec P\cdot\nabla)\vec E_0$; inserting, the $\nabla\cdot\vec P$ terms cancel (eq. (6.116)): the force on a continuum of point dipoles $(\vec p\cdot\nabla)\vec E_0$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]]).
>
> **4. Total field.** A body exerts no net force on itself: for volume charges, $\iint\rho(\vec r)\rho(\vec r\,')\vec{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^3 = \vec 0$ by antisymmetry under $\vec r \leftrightarrow \vec r\,'$. A surface charge element feels the field of all *other* charges, which is the average of the fields on its two sides ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]]; [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle|§C2.2]]). So replacing $\vec E_0$ by $\vec E$ in the volume and by $\vec E_{\text{avg}}$ on the surface adds zero (eq. (6.118)). ⚑ By-product: the $\frac12$ in $\vec E_{\text{avg}}$ is the value of the ill-defined product "step × delta" fixed by a consistent regularization of the layer ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-8|QFT Theorem §CA.2.8]], 1, case $\sigma = \rho$).
>
> **5. Problem 6.24: to the dipole form with the total field.** Apply step 2 with $\vec a = \vec P$, $\vec b = \vec E$ on $\mathcal V$, where $\vec E$ is smooth up to the boundary from inside (limit $\vec E_{\text{in}}$): $\int(-\nabla\cdot\vec P)\vec E = -\oint(\vec P\cdot\hat n)\vec E_{\text{in}} + \int(\vec P\cdot\nabla)\vec E$. Substituting into eq. (6.118):
>
> $$
> \vec F = \int\bigl[\rho_f + (\vec P\cdot\nabla)\bigr]\vec E + \oint(\vec P\cdot\hat n)\bigl(\vec E_{\text{avg}} - \vec E_{\text{in}}\bigr)\,dS .
> $$
>
> **6. The jump.** $\vec E_{\text{avg}} - \vec E_{\text{in}} = \frac12(\vec E_{\text{out}} - \vec E_{\text{in}})$; the tangential parts are continuous and the normal part jumps by $\sigma/\varepsilon_0 = \vec P\cdot\hat n/\varepsilon_0$ (no free surface charge), so $\vec E_{\text{avg}} - \vec E_{\text{in}} = (\vec P\cdot\hat n)\hat n/2\varepsilon_0$ and the surface term is $\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_0}\oint(\hat n\cdot\vec P)^2\hat n\,dS$ (eq. (6.119)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The dipole form with the *total* field needs a surface correction, an outward "pressure" $(\hat n\cdot\vec P)^2/2\varepsilon_0$: the dipoles at the surface sit in a field that jumps.
> - Assumptions used: the body is isolated in vacuum; no free surface charge; Newton's third law between the body's own charges. Nothing here applies to a *part* of a body (next remark).

> [!remark] Remark: Coulomb's law fails for a part of a body
> Try Coulomb's law on the thin slice containing the interface $z = 0$ between two dielectrics filling a capacitor with fixed free charge $\pm\sigma_f$ (Zangwill Fig. 6.16; $\varepsilon_{r1}$ below, $\varepsilon_{r2}$ above). The slice carries $\sigma_P = \varepsilon_0E_0\bigl(\frac1{\varepsilon_{r2}} - \frac1{\varepsilon_{r1}}\bigr)$ with $E_0 = \sigma_f/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-2-2|§C5.2, Theorem §C5.2.2]], 3). Zangwill multiplies by $E_0$ and gets $\vec f = \frac{\sigma_f^2}{\varepsilon_0}\bigl(\frac1{\varepsilon_{r2}} - \frac1{\varepsilon_{r1}}\bigr)\hat z$ (eq. (6.121), "incorrect"); multiplying instead by the field of all other charges, $\vec E_{\text{avg}} = \frac{E_0}2\bigl(\frac1{\varepsilon_{r1}} + \frac1{\varepsilon_{r2}}\bigr)\hat z$, gives $\frac{\sigma_f^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\bigl(\frac1{\varepsilon_{r2}^2} - \frac1{\varepsilon_{r1}^2}\bigr)\hat z$. Both are wrong: as $\varepsilon_{r1} \to \infty$ (a conductor below, [[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^rem-c5-2-3|§C5.2, Remark: The limit ε_r → ∞]]) the force must be the plate attraction of a capacitor filled with $\varepsilon_{r2}$, $\sigma_f^2/2\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{r2}$ (eq. (6.123), from $C = \varepsilon_{r2}\varepsilon_0A/d$ and the conductor force formula of [[§C4.4 Forces on Conductors|§C4.4]]), not $\sigma_f^2/\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{r2}$ (eq. (6.122)) nor $\sigma_f^2/2\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{r2}^2$. The reason (Zangwill §6.8.3): the boundary of a sub-volume also feels short-range, non-electrostatic forces from the adjacent matter (the forces of elasticity), which depend on the polarization; they cancel in the sum over all sub-volumes, which is why [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-6|Theorem §C5.4.6]] survives for a whole body. Brown (1951) computed them from elasticity theory with polarization; the upshot used below is that a force density exists (Helmholtz's) whose short-range surface terms integrate to zero, at least for a dielectric fluid.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.8.2–§6.8.3, eqs. (6.120)–(6.123) · the $\vec E_{\text{avg}}$ variant worked out here*

^rem-c5-4-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.4.7: Helmholtz Force Formula
> For simple dielectric matter, the force on the matter in a region (a whole body or a sub-volume of a dielectric fluid) is the integral over that region of the force density (Zangwill eq. (6.130))
>
> $$
> \vec f = \rho_f\vec E - \frac12E^2\,\nabla\varepsilon , \qquad \vec F = -\frac{\delta U_E}{\delta\vec r} = \int d^3r\,\rho_f\vec E - \frac12\int d^3r\,E^2\,\nabla\varepsilon .
> $$
>
> The field pushes only on free charge and on places where $\varepsilon$ varies; $\vec P$ and $\rho_P$ do not appear. At a sharp interface $\nabla\varepsilon$ is a surface layer, and the formula must be read with care ([[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^ex-c5-4-2|Example §C5.4.2]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.8.4, eqs. (6.124)–(6.130)*

^thm-c5-4-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Virtual work.** At fixed free charge, $\delta U_E = -\vec F\cdot\delta\vec r$ for a virtual displacement $\delta\vec r$ of the matter (eq. (6.124); [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-2|Theorem §C5.4.2]]), the analogue of the derivation of the Lorentz force from energy ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy|§C2.3]]; Zangwill §3.5.1).
>
> **2. Vary $U_E = \frac12\int|\vec D|^2/\varepsilon$ in both $\vec D$ and $\varepsilon$** (eq. (6.125)): $\delta\bigl(|\vec D|^2/2\varepsilon\bigr) = \vec D\cdot\delta\vec D/\varepsilon - |\vec D|^2\delta\varepsilon/2\varepsilon^2 = \vec E\cdot\delta\vec D - \frac12E^2\delta\varepsilon$.
>
> **3. Carried quantities.** Displacing the matter (with its free charge) by a uniform $\delta\vec r$ carries both fields with it: $\delta\vec D = \vec D(\vec r - \delta\vec r) - \vec D(\vec r) = -(\delta\vec r\cdot\nabla)\vec D$ and $\delta\varepsilon = -(\delta\vec r\cdot\nabla)\varepsilon$ to first order (eqs. (6.126)–(6.127)), so
>
> $$
> \delta U_E = -\int\vec E\cdot(\delta\vec r\cdot\nabla)\vec D + \frac12\int E^2\,\nabla\varepsilon\cdot\delta\vec r \qquad\text{(eq. (6.128))} .
> $$
>
> **4. Identity (6.129).** For constant $\delta\vec r$, $\nabla\times(\delta\vec r\times\vec D) = \delta\vec r(\nabla\cdot\vec D) - (\delta\vec r\cdot\nabla)\vec D$. Hence $-\vec E\cdot(\delta\vec r\cdot\nabla)\vec D = \vec E\cdot\nabla\times(\delta\vec r\times\vec D) - (\vec E\cdot\delta\vec r)\,\nabla\cdot\vec D$.
>
> **5. Integrate by parts.** $\int\vec E\cdot\nabla\times(\delta\vec r\times\vec D) = \int(\delta\vec r\times\vec D)\cdot(\nabla\times\vec E) + \oint(\cdots) = 0$: the bulk term vanishes because $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$, the surface term at infinity because the fields are localized. With $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f$: $\delta U_E = -\delta\vec r\cdot\bigl[\int\rho_f\vec E - \frac12\int E^2\nabla\varepsilon\bigr]$, and step 1 gives eq. (6.130).
>
> **6. Same result without carrying $\vec D$.** Steps 3–5 can be replaced by: $\int\vec E\cdot\delta\vec D = \int V\,\nabla\cdot\delta\vec D = \int V\,\delta\rho_f$ (lemma of [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^der-c5-4-4|Derivation §C5.4.4]]), with $\delta\rho_f = -\nabla\cdot(\rho_f\delta\vec r)$ for free charge carried by the matter; integrating by parts, $\int V\delta\rho_f = \int\rho_f\,\delta\vec r\cdot\nabla V = -\int\rho_f\vec E\cdot\delta\vec r$. This version allows a non-uniform $\delta\vec r(\vec r)$, nonzero only on the sub-volume of interest, and gives the same density $\vec f$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Energy, not Coulomb's law, fixes the force on parts of a dielectric; only $\rho_f$ and $\nabla\varepsilon$ carry force density.
> - Assumptions used: simple medium, $\varepsilon$ carried with the matter (no change of $\varepsilon$ by compression, i.e. no electrostriction), localized fields.

> [!definition] Definition §C5.4.3: Electric Stress Tensor of a Simple Dielectric
> (Zangwill eq. (6.133))
>
> $$
> \mathcal T_{ij}(\vec D) = D_iE_j - \frac12\delta_{ij}\,\vec D\cdot\vec E .
> $$
>
> It is symmetric ($D_iE_j = \varepsilon E_iE_j$) and in vacuum, $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E$, reduces to the electric Maxwell stress tensor ([[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor|§C2.4]]; level B: [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^def-b9-2-1|Def. §B9.2.1]], electric part).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.8.5, eq. (6.133)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.4.8: Force from the Dielectric Stress Tensor
> The force on everything inside a closed surface $\mathcal S$ that lies in a region where the fields are continuous is (Zangwill eqs. (6.132), (6.134)–(6.135))
>
> $$
> \vec F = \oint_{\mathcal S}dS\,\Bigl[(\hat n\cdot\vec D)\vec E - \frac12\hat n\,(\vec D\cdot\vec E)\Bigr] = \oint_{\mathcal S}dS\;\hat n\cdot\mathcal T(\vec D), \qquad F_j = \int_\Omega d^3r\,\partial_i\mathcal T_{ij}(\vec D) ,
> $$
>
> equivalent to the Helmholtz formula ([[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-7|Theorem §C5.4.7]]) but free of space derivatives and of the ill-defined $E^2\nabla\varepsilon$ at sharp interfaces; inside $\Omega$, $\partial_i\mathcal T_{ij}$ is understood as a distribution whose layers on interfaces are the jumps of $\hat n\cdot\mathcal T$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.8.5, eqs. (6.131)–(6.135)*

^thm-c5-4-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Identity.** $\partial_j(\vec D\cdot\vec E) = \partial_j(\varepsilon E_kE_k) = E^2\partial_j\varepsilon + 2\varepsilon E_k\partial_jE_k = E^2\partial_j\varepsilon + 2D_k\partial_jE_k$, so $\frac12E^2\partial_j\varepsilon = \frac12\partial_j(\vec D\cdot\vec E) - D_k\partial_jE_k$.
>
> **2. Curl-free field.** $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ means $\partial_jE_k = \partial_kE_j$, so $D_k\partial_jE_k = D_k\partial_kE_j = (\vec D\cdot\nabla)E_j$.
>
> **3. Force density.** With $\rho_f = \nabla\cdot\vec D$, the Helmholtz density is
>
> $$
> f_j = (\nabla\cdot\vec D)E_j - \frac12E^2\partial_j\varepsilon = (\nabla\cdot\vec D)E_j + (\vec D\cdot\nabla)E_j - \frac12\partial_j(\vec D\cdot\vec E) .
> $$
>
> Zangwill's eq. (6.131) prints a minus sign before the $(\vec D\cdot\nabla)\vec E$ term; steps 1–2 give plus, which is also what his result (6.132) requires.
>
> **4. A total divergence.** $(\nabla\cdot\vec D)E_j + (\vec D\cdot\nabla)E_j = \partial_i(D_iE_j)$, so $f_j = \partial_i\bigl(D_iE_j - \frac12\delta_{ij}\vec D\cdot\vec E\bigr) = \partial_i\mathcal T_{ij}$ (eq. (6.135)).
>
> **5. Surface form.** Integrating over $\Omega$ and applying the divergence theorem (identity (6.117) with $\vec a = \vec D$, $\vec b = \vec E$ for the first part) gives eq. (6.132). *Sense at interfaces inside $\Omega$:* $\mathcal T$ is piecewise smooth with jumps, and its distributional divergence contains the layer $[\hat n\cdot\mathcal T]\,\delta_{\text{interface}}$ ([[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-1-2|§C5.1, Theorem §C5.1.2]], same computation), so the divergence theorem holds with these layers included. Check on the capacitor interface of [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^ex-c5-4-2|Example §C5.4.2]]: $\mathcal T_{zz} = D_zE_z - \frac12D_zE_z = \frac12\sigma_f^2/\varepsilon$, whose jump $\frac12\sigma_f^2(1/\varepsilon_2 - 1/\varepsilon_1)$ is the interface force found there.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The dielectric force is a flux of stress through any surface drawn in a homogeneous region; the surface need not touch the charges.
> - Assumptions used: those of Theorem §C5.4.7; $\mathcal S$ itself must not lie on an interface.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.4.9: Force on an Embedded Volume
> Let a body (a second dielectric $\varepsilon'$, perhaps containing free charge) be embedded in a simple dielectric of permittivity $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$, and let $\Omega$ be a region infinitesimally larger than the body (Zangwill Fig. 6.17). The force on the body is (eq. (6.137))
>
> $$
> \vec F = \varepsilon_r\int_\Omega d^3r\,\rho\,\vec E ,
> $$
>
> with $\rho$ the **total** (free plus polarization) charge in $\Omega$, surface charges weighted with $\vec E_{\text{avg}}$. Coulomb's law applied to the same charges, eq. (6.118), would miss the factor $\varepsilon_r$, which is the net effect of the short-range forces exerted by the embedding medium ([[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^rem-c5-4-4|Remark: Coulomb's law fails for a part of a body]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.8.5, Application 6.4, eqs. (6.136)–(6.137)*

^thm-c5-4-9

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Stress tensor on $\partial\Omega$.** $\partial\Omega$ lies in the embedding medium, where $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$ with constant $\varepsilon$; by [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-8|Theorem §C5.4.8]], $F_j = \oint_{\partial\Omega}n_i\,\varepsilon\bigl(E_iE_j - \frac12\delta_{ij}E^2\bigr)dS = \varepsilon_r\oint_{\partial\Omega}n_i\,\varepsilon_0\bigl(E_iE_j - \frac12\delta_{ij}E^2\bigr)dS$ (eq. (6.136) in surface form).
>
> **2. Vacuum identity.** For any field with $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ and $\varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho$ (here $\rho$ is the total charge, by definition of the polarization charge), $\oint_{\partial\Omega}n_i\varepsilon_0(E_iE_j - \frac12\delta_{ij}E^2)dS = \int_\Omega\rho E_j$: the steps of [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^der-c5-4-8|Derivation §C5.4.8]] with $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0$ run in reverse (Zangwill §3.7; [[§C2.4 The Electric Stress Tensor|§C2.4]]; level B: [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]] in statics). Surface charges inside $\Omega$ contribute $\sigma\vec E_{\text{avg}}$, the layer reading of the same identity.
>
> **3. Combine:** $\vec F = \varepsilon_r\int_\Omega\rho\vec E$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The surface $\partial\Omega$ must lie in the embedding medium (not in vacuum); that is where the factor $\varepsilon_r$ enters.
> - For a body in vacuum ($\varepsilon_r = 1$) it reduces to Coulomb's law, Theorem §C5.4.6.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C5.4.1: Dielectrophoresis
> How does the force between a charge $Q$ and a small uncharged simple dielectric depend on their distance $r$, large compared with both sizes (Zangwill Example 6.5)? Over the dielectric, $\vec E_0 = Q\hat r/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$ is nearly uniform, so [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-6|Theorem §C5.4.6]] gives $\vec F = \int(\vec P\cdot\nabla)\vec E_0 \simeq (\vec p\cdot\nabla)\vec E_0$ with $\vec p = \gamma\vec E_0$, $\gamma = \alpha\varepsilon_0$ ([[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^def-c5-3-1|§C5.3, Def. §C5.3.1]]). Since $\nabla\bigl(\frac12|\vec E_0|^2\bigr) = (\vec E_0\cdot\nabla)\vec E_0 + \vec E_0\times(\nabla\times\vec E_0)$ and $\nabla\times\vec E_0 = \vec 0$,
>
> $$
> \vec F = \gamma(\vec E_0\cdot\nabla)\vec E_0 = \frac{\gamma}{2}\nabla|\vec E_0|^2 = \frac{\gamma}{2}\nabla\frac{Q^2}{16\pi^2\varepsilon_0^2r^4} = -\frac{\gamma Q^2}{8\pi^2\varepsilon_0^2}\,\frac{\hat r}{r^5} ,
> $$
>
> attractive whatever the sign of $Q$: the induced charge of opposite sign is nearer. In any nonuniform field the force $\frac12\gamma\nabla|\vec E_0|^2$ drives small dielectric bodies toward strong field; it is used to sort and move particles, including living cells (**dielectrophoresis**).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.8.1, Example 6.5*

> [!example] Example §C5.4.2: The Force on a Dielectric Interface in a Capacitor
> A capacitor with fixed free charge $\pm\sigma_f$ is filled with $\varepsilon_1$ for $z < 0$ and $\varepsilon_2$ for $z > 0$ (Zangwill Example 6.6, Fig. 6.16). Find the force per area on the interface from [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-7|Theorem §C5.4.7]], and check $\varepsilon_{r1} \to \infty$.
>
> **1. The naive integral has no value.** $\varepsilon(z) = \varepsilon_1\Theta(-z) + \varepsilon_2\Theta(z)$, so $d\varepsilon/dz = (\varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_1)\delta(z)$, while $E_z$ jumps at $z = 0$. Then $-\frac12\int E_z^2\,\frac{d\varepsilon}{dz}\,dz$ multiplies a step by a delta at the same point, a product with no canonical value ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-8|QFT Theorem §CA.2.8]], 1).
>
> **2. Rewrite with the continuous field.** $D_z$ is continuous ($\sigma_f = 0$ on the interface) and equals $\sigma_f$ throughout the gap. Since $E_z = D_z/\varepsilon$, $E_z^2\,\varepsilon' = D_z^2\,\varepsilon'/\varepsilon^2 = -D_z^2\,\frac{d}{dz}\frac1\varepsilon$, and $\frac{d}{dz}\frac1\varepsilon = \bigl(\frac1{\varepsilon_2} - \frac1{\varepsilon_1}\bigr)\delta(z)$ now multiplies the *constant* $D_z^2 = \sigma_f^2$, a well-defined product ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-7|QFT Theorem §CA.2.7]], 1):
>
> $$
> f = -\frac12\int_{0^-}^{0^+}\frac{D_z^2}{\varepsilon^2}\frac{d\varepsilon}{dz}\,dz = \frac12\int_{0^-}^{0^+}D_z^2\,\frac{d}{dz}\Bigl(\frac1\varepsilon\Bigr)dz = \frac{\sigma_f^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{r2}} - \frac{1}{\varepsilon_{r1}}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **3. Why this is the right reading.** Replace the step by any smooth increasing profile $\varepsilon(z)$ on $[-\eta, \eta]$. Gauss's law still gives $D_z = \sigma_f$ exactly, $E_z = \sigma_f/\varepsilon(z)$ is smooth, and $-\frac12\int E_z^2\varepsilon'\,dz = \frac12\sigma_f^2\bigl[1/\varepsilon\bigr]_{-\eta}^{\eta}$, the same for every profile and every $\eta$. The limit exists and equals step 2. Evaluating $E_z^2$ at "the average" of its two sides instead would give $-\frac14\sigma_f^2\bigl(\frac1{\varepsilon_1^2} + \frac1{\varepsilon_2^2}\bigr)(\varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_1)$, which is different: the physics fixes the regularization.
>
> **4. Reading and limit.** $f > 0$ if $\varepsilon_1 > \varepsilon_2$: whatever the field direction, the medium with larger $\varepsilon$ tends to expand. As $\varepsilon_{r1} \to \infty$, $f \to \sigma_f^2/2\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{r2} = \frac12(E_0/\varepsilon_{r2})\sigma_f$, the plate attraction of a capacitor filled with $\varepsilon_{r2}$ (eq. (6.123)): correct, where Coulomb's law failed ([[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^rem-c5-4-4|Remark: Coulomb's law fails for a part of a body]]). The same number is the jump of $\mathcal T_{zz}$ ([[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-8|Theorem §C5.4.8]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.8.4, Example 6.6 · steps 1 and 3 (distributional reading) written here*

> [!example] Example §C5.4.3: The Force on a Point Charge near a Dielectric Interface
> For the charge $q$ at $z = -d$ in medium L of [[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^ex-c5-2-4|§C5.2, Example §C5.2.4]] (Zangwill Example 6.7): by [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-9|Theorem §C5.4.9]] with $\Omega$ a small ball around $q$, $\vec F = \varepsilon_{rL}\cdot(q/\varepsilon_{rL})\,\vec E_\sigma(q) = q\vec E_\sigma(q)$, where $\vec E_\sigma$ is the vacuum field of the interface charge (the screened charge's own field exerts no force on it); equivalently, the first term of [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-7|Theorem §C5.4.7]]. By symmetry only $E_{\sigma z}$ survives. From an interface point at distance $s$ from the axis, $r = \sqrt{s^2 + d^2}$, the $z$-component at $q$ is $-d/r^3$ per unit charge; with $\sigma_P = K\,qd/r^3$, $K = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_{rL}}\frac{\varepsilon_{rL} - \varepsilon_{rR}}{\varepsilon_{rL} + \varepsilon_{rR}}$,
>
> $$
> E_{\sigma z}(q) = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^\infty 2\pi s\,ds\,\frac{Kqd\cdot d}{r^6} = -\frac{Kqd^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{4d^4} , \qquad \int_0^\infty\frac{s\,ds}{(s^2 + d^2)^3} = \Bigl[-\frac{1}{4(s^2 + d^2)^2}\Bigr]_0^\infty = \frac{1}{4d^4} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{on }q} = -\hat z\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{rL}}\,\frac{\varepsilon_{rL} - \varepsilon_{rR}}{\varepsilon_{rL} + \varepsilon_{rR}}\,\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\frac{q}{2d}\Bigr)^2 .
> $$
>
> Since $+\hat z$ points from $q$ toward the interface, the charge is attracted when $\varepsilon_{rR} > \varepsilon_{rL}$ and repelled when $\varepsilon_{rR} < \varepsilon_{rL}$: free charge seeks large permittivity ([[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-3|Theorem §C5.4.3]]). With vacuum on q's side ($\varepsilon_{rL} = 1$, $\varepsilon_{rR} = \varepsilon_r$) the force is $\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 1}\frac{q^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0d^2}$ toward the dielectric, level B's $\frac{\chi_e}{\chi_e + 2}\frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,4d^2}$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^ex-b5-3-1|Example §B5.3.1]]). Images give the same in one line ([[§C7.1 The Method of Images|§C7.1]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.8.5, Example 6.7*

> [!example] Example §C5.4.4: A Slab Drawn into a Capacitor at Fixed Potential
> A slab of simple dielectric fills the gap $d$ between square plates ($L \times L$) over an inserted length $s$; the potential difference $V$ is fixed (Zangwill Example 6.8). Neglecting fringing, show $\vec F = \hat x\,\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)LV^2/2d$, into the capacitor.
>
> *Method I (energy at fixed potentials).* By [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-5|Theorem §C5.4.5]], 3, $\Delta\hat U_E = -\frac12\int\vec P\cdot\vec E_0 = -\frac12\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)\int_{\text{slab}}\vec E\cdot\vec E_0$, and at fixed potential $|\vec E| = |\vec E_0| = V/d$ inside, so $\Delta\hat U_E = -\frac12\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)\frac{V^2}{d^2}\,Lds$ and $F_x = -(\partial\hat U_E/\partial s)_V = \varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)LV^2/2d$.
>
> *Method II (Helmholtz).* No free charge in the slab, so $\vec F = -\frac12\int E^2\nabla\varepsilon$ ([[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-7|Theorem §C5.4.7]]). Inside the capacitor $\varepsilon(x) = \varepsilon_0\Theta(x - s) + \varepsilon\Theta(s - x)$, so $\nabla\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0(1 - \varepsilon_r)\delta(x - s)\hat x$ (the far end $x = -b$ lies outside the field). Here $\vec E = (V/d)\hat z$ is *tangential* to the end face and continuous across it, so $E^2\delta(x - s)$ is a continuous function times a delta, well defined, unlike [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^ex-c5-4-2|Example §C5.4.2]]. Over the cross-section $Ld$: $\vec F = -\frac12Ld\,\frac{V^2}{d^2}\,\varepsilon_0(1 - \varepsilon_r)\hat x = \varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)\frac{LV^2}{2d}\hat x$.
>
> *Where the force really acts.* Both methods hide the mechanism. For the whole slab, [[§C5.4 Energy and Forces in Dielectrics#^thm-c5-4-6|Theorem §C5.4.6]] gives $\vec F = \int(\vec P\cdot\nabla)\vec E_0$, nonzero only where $\vec E_0$ is non-uniform: the force comes entirely from the fringing field at the plate edges ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^rem-b5-3-5|§B5.3, Remark: The fringing field does the pulling]]). Method II locates a force density at the slab's end face inside the capacitor, where there is no fringing field; the totals agree, but force densities from different formulas need not agree pointwise — only their integrals over a whole body. Level B's $F = \frac12V^2dC/dx$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-6|Theorem §B5.3.6]]) is a third route to the same number.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.8.5, Example 6.8 and its Remark*
