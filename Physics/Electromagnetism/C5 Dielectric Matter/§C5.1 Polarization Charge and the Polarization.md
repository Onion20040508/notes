*Sources: Zangwill, Modern Electrodynamics (2013), §6.1–§6.2.*

What is polarized matter, and in what sense is a dielectric "a continuum of dipoles"? Level B answered with Griffiths' route: polarization defined as dipole moment per volume ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^def-b5-1-1|Def. §B5.1.1]]), bound charges derived from the dipole potential ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]]), and the field inside matter justified by an averaging argument ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]]). Zangwill reverses the order, and that is the layer change of this section: the macroscopic **polarization charge** is primary, and $\vec P$ is any field whose divergence and normal component reproduce it, so it is not unique; whatever the microscopic picture, its volume integral is the dipole moment of the polarization charge. The field this charge produces, with the dipole picture as a theorem and the auxiliary field $\vec D$, is the next section, [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter|§C5.2]].

*Notation.* SI; potential $V$ (Zangwill writes $\varphi$: [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: The potential is V]]); sample volume $\mathcal V$ with boundary $\mathcal S$ and outward unit normal $\hat n$ (Zangwill writes $V$, $S$); separation vector $\vec{\mathcal R} = \vec r - \vec r\,'$, $\mathcal R = |\vec{\mathcal R}|$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]); Zangwill's $\rho_P$, $\sigma_P$ are Griffiths' $\rho_b$, $\sigma_b$.

## Polarization charge and the polarization

> [!definition] Definition §C5.1.1: Free Charge; Polarization Charge
> In the presence of dielectric matter the macroscopic charge density (the Lorentz average of the microscopic one, Zangwill §2.3; level B: [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]]) is split as (Zangwill eq. (6.2))
>
> $$
> \rho(\vec r) = \rho_f(\vec r) + \rho_P(\vec r) .
> $$
>
> The **free charge** $\rho_f$ is all charge extraneous to the dielectric (charge on capacitor plates, point charges placed in or near it); it is the source of the external field $\vec E_{\text{ext}}$. The **polarization charge** is the macroscopic charge rearranged inside the dielectric by the field, in mechanical equilibrium with the chemical-bonding forces; it has a volume part $\rho_P$ in $\mathcal V$ and a surface part $\sigma_P$ on $\mathcal S$, and its field is $\vec E_{\text{self}}$, so that $\vec E = \vec E_{\text{self}} + \vec E_{\text{ext}} \ne \vec 0$ inside and outside (eq. (6.1)). A neutral dielectric stays neutral (eq. (6.3)):
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V}d^3r\,\rho_P + \oint_{\mathcal S}dS\,\sigma_P = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.1–§6.2, eqs. (6.1)–(6.3)*

^def-c5-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §C5.1.2: Polarization
> A **polarization** of the sample is a vector field $\vec P$, continuously differentiable on $\mathcal V$ up to its boundary, such that (Zangwill eqs. (6.4)–(6.6))
>
> $$
> \rho_P = -\nabla\cdot\vec P \quad (\vec r \in \mathcal V), \qquad \sigma_P = \vec P\cdot\hat n \quad (\vec r \in \mathcal S), \qquad \vec P = \vec 0 \quad (\vec r \notin \mathcal V) .
> $$
>
> The definition is model-independent: it says nothing about dipoles. Its physical meaning is found afterwards ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-3|Theorem §C5.1.3]], [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-2|Theorem §C5.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.2, eqs. (6.4)–(6.6)*

^def-c5-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: The layer change from level B
> At level B, $\vec P$ was *defined* as dipole moment per volume ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^def-b5-1-1|Def. §B5.1.1]]) and the bound charges $-\nabla\cdot\vec P$, $\vec P\cdot\hat n$ were *derived* from the dipole potential ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]]). Here the same two formulas are the definition of $\vec P$ in terms of the charge, and the dipole interpretation is a theorem ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-2|Theorem §C5.2.2]]). The reason for the reversal is physical: for most solids the "dipole moment per volume" of a cell is ill-defined ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^rem-c5-1-2|Remark: The Lorentz model]]), while the macroscopic charge always is. The surface charge $\sigma_P$ is the same object as Griffiths' $\sigma_b$; that it is the singular part of $-\nabla\cdot\vec P$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^cau-b5-1-1|§B5.1, Caution: Normals, interfaces and the surface layer]]) is now a theorem in the sense of distributions ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-2|Theorem §C5.1.2]]).

^rem-c5-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.1.1: The Polarization Is Not Unique
> Let $\vec P$ be a polarization ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^def-c5-1-2|Def. §C5.1.2]]).
> 1. If $\vec G$ is twice continuously differentiable and vanishes on $\mathcal S$ and outside $\mathcal V$, then $\vec P' = \vec P + \nabla\times\vec G$ is a polarization with the same $\rho_P$ and $\sigma_P$.
> 2. Conversely, two polarizations with the same $\rho_P$ and $\sigma_P$ differ by a field $\vec W$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec W = 0$ in $\mathcal V$, $\vec W\cdot\hat n = 0$ on $\mathcal S$ and $\vec W = \vec 0$ outside.
>
> So the charges fix $\nabla\cdot\vec P$ and $\vec P\cdot\hat n$ but not $\nabla\times\vec P$ inside $\mathcal V$ (Zangwill: "This follows from Helmholtz' theorem", [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]). All fields computed from $\rho_P$, $\sigma_P$ are blind to the choice.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.2 (third remark after eq. (6.6))*

^thm-c5-1-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Divergence.** $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec G) = 0$ for a $C^2$ field ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]), so $-\nabla\cdot\vec P' = -\nabla\cdot\vec P = \rho_P$ in $\mathcal V$.
>
> **2. Normal component on $\mathcal S$.** $\hat n\cdot(\nabla\times\vec G)$ at a point of $\mathcal S$ involves only derivatives of $\vec G$ *along* the surface: in local coordinates with $\hat n = \hat z$, $\hat z\cdot\nabla\times\vec G = \partial_xG_y - \partial_yG_x$, and $x$, $y$ are tangent to $\mathcal S$. Since $\vec G = \vec 0$ on all of $\mathcal S$, its tangential derivatives vanish there, so $\hat n\cdot\nabla\times\vec G = 0$ and $\sigma_P' = \vec P'\cdot\hat n = \vec P\cdot\hat n = \sigma_P$.
>
> **3. Outside.** $\vec G = \vec 0$ outside $\mathcal V$, so $\nabla\times\vec G = \vec 0$ there and $\vec P' = \vec 0$.
>
> **4. Converse.** Let $\vec W = \vec P' - \vec P$. Subtracting the defining relations: $\nabla\cdot\vec W = -\rho_P + \rho_P = 0$ in $\mathcal V$, $\vec W\cdot\hat n = \sigma_P - \sigma_P = 0$ on $\mathcal S$, and $\vec W = \vec 0$ outside. ⚑ By-product: by [[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-2|Theorem §C5.1.2]] the extension of $\vec W$ by zero is then divergence-free on all of $\mathbb R^3$ in the sense of distributions (no surface layer, since $\vec W\cdot\hat n = 0$), so it carries no charge anywhere: the freedom is exactly a source-free "circulating" polarization.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The physical content of $\vec P$ is in $\nabla\cdot\vec P$ and $\vec P\cdot\hat n$; its curl inside the sample is free data.
> - Assumptions used: $\vec G \in C^2$, $\vec G = \vec 0$ on and outside $\mathcal S$; $\vec P$ of class $C^1$.
> - The modern theory fixes the freedom by a physical prescription (time-integrated polarization current, [[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^rem-c5-1-3|★ Remark: The modern theory of polarization]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.1.2: Polarization Charge as a Distribution
> Let $\Theta_{\mathcal V}$ be the indicator function of $\mathcal V$ and $\delta_{\mathcal S}$ the **surface layer** on $\mathcal S$, the generalized function ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-2|QFT Def. §CA.2.2]]) $\delta_{\mathcal S}[\varphi] = \oint_{\mathcal S}\varphi\,dS$. Extend $\vec P$ by zero, $\vec P_{\text{ext}} = \Theta_{\mathcal V}\vec P$. Then, as generalized functions on $\mathbb R^3$ (derivatives in the sense of [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]),
>
> $$
> \nabla\Theta_{\mathcal V} = -\hat n\,\delta_{\mathcal S}, \qquad -\nabla\cdot\vec P_{\text{ext}} = \rho_P\,\Theta_{\mathcal V} + \sigma_P\,\delta_{\mathcal S} .
> $$
>
> The total polarization charge is one distribution, $-\nabla\cdot\vec P_{\text{ext}}$; its surface part is a layer of weight $\vec P\cdot\hat n$. Zangwill's example (footnote 1): for $\mathcal V = \{z \le 0\}$, $\vec P_{\text{ext}} = \vec P_0\,\Theta(-z)$ gives $-\nabla\cdot\vec P_{\text{ext}} = -\Theta(-z)\nabla\cdot\vec P_0 + \delta(z)\,\vec P_0\cdot\hat z$.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.2, footnote 1 · the distributional form written here*

^thm-c5-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. The layer is a distribution.** $\varphi \mapsto \oint_{\mathcal S}\varphi\,dS$ is linear, and $|\oint_{\mathcal S}\varphi\,dS| \le (\text{area of }\mathcal S)\sup|\varphi|$, so it is continuous on test functions $\varphi \in \mathcal D(\mathbb R^3)$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-1|QFT Def. §CA.2.1]]). It is the surface analogue of the point delta ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]).
>
> **2. Gradient of the indicator.** By the definition of the distributional derivative, $\partial_i\Theta_{\mathcal V}[\varphi] = -\Theta_{\mathcal V}[\partial_i\varphi] = -\int_{\mathcal V}\partial_i\varphi\,d^3r$. The divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]) applied to the field $\varphi\,\hat e_i$ gives $\int_{\mathcal V}\partial_i\varphi\,d^3r = \oint_{\mathcal S}\varphi\,n_i\,dS$. Hence $\partial_i\Theta_{\mathcal V}[\varphi] = -\oint_{\mathcal S}n_i\varphi\,dS$, i.e. $\nabla\Theta_{\mathcal V} = -\hat n\,\delta_{\mathcal S}$.
>
> **3. Divergence of the extended polarization.** For a test function $\varphi$,
>
> $$
> \bigl(-\nabla\cdot\vec P_{\text{ext}}\bigr)[\varphi] = \vec P_{\text{ext}}[\nabla\varphi] = \int_{\mathcal V}\vec P\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d^3r .
> $$
>
> **4. Product rule and divergence theorem.** $\vec P\cdot\nabla\varphi = \nabla\cdot(\varphi\vec P) - \varphi\,\nabla\cdot\vec P$ on $\mathcal V$ (product rule (iii) of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]; $\vec P \in C^1$ up to $\mathcal S$). Integrating, the first term becomes $\oint_{\mathcal S}\varphi\,\vec P\cdot\hat n\,dS$:
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V}\vec P\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d^3r = \oint_{\mathcal S}\varphi\,(\vec P\cdot\hat n)\,dS + \int_{\mathcal V}\varphi\,(-\nabla\cdot\vec P)\,d^3r = \sigma_P\delta_{\mathcal S}[\varphi] + (\rho_P\Theta_{\mathcal V})[\varphi] .
> $$
>
> **5. Half-space.** With $\Theta(-z)$ in place of $\Theta_{\mathcal V}$: $\partial_z\Theta(-z) = -\delta(z)$ ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-3|QFT Theorem §CA.2.3]], chain rule), so $-\nabla\cdot[\vec P_0\Theta(-z)] = -\Theta(-z)\nabla\cdot\vec P_0 - \vec P_0\cdot\nabla\Theta(-z) = -\Theta(-z)\nabla\cdot\vec P_0 + \delta(z)P_{0z}$; the outward normal of $\{z \le 0\}$ is $+\hat z$, as step 2 predicts.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - $\sigma_P$ is not an independent kind of charge: it is the part of $-\nabla\cdot\vec P_{\text{ext}}$ supported on $\mathcal S$. Zangwill keeps the two parts separate by choice.
> - Every formula below that uses $\rho_P$ and $\sigma_P$ separately can be read as one formula with the distribution $-\nabla\cdot\vec P_{\text{ext}}$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.1.3: Neutrality; the Volume Integral of P Is the Dipole Moment
> For every polarization ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^def-c5-1-2|Def. §C5.1.2]]):
> 1. the neutrality condition of [[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^def-c5-1-1|Def. §C5.1.1]] holds identically;
> 2. the volume integral of $\vec P$ is the total dipole moment of the polarization charge (Zangwill eq. (6.9)),
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V}d^3r\,\vec P = \vec p \equiv \oint_{\mathcal S}dS\,\vec r\,\sigma_P + \int_{\mathcal V}d^3r\,\vec r\,\rho_P ,
> $$
>
>    with the dipole moment of a charge distribution as in [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]] ([[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]]); by 1 it does not depend on the origin ([[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]]).
>
> Part 1 is level B's total-bound-charge theorem ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]]); here it is the reason for the definition. Part 2 is the first physical meaning of $\vec P$: whatever the microscopic picture, $\vec P$ integrates to the sample's dipole moment.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.2, §6.2.1, eqs. (6.3)–(6.9)*

^thm-c5-1-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Neutrality.** $\int_{\mathcal V}\rho_P\,d^3r = -\int_{\mathcal V}\nabla\cdot\vec P\,d^3r = -\oint_{\mathcal S}\vec P\cdot\hat n\,dS = -\oint_{\mathcal S}\sigma_P\,dS$ by the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]).
>
> **2. One component.** Fix $k$. Since $\partial_ir_k = \delta_{ik}$, $\vec P\cdot\nabla r_k = P_k$ (Zangwill's step before eq. (6.7)). The product rule gives $\nabla\cdot(r_k\vec P) = r_k\,\nabla\cdot\vec P + \vec P\cdot\nabla r_k$, so
>
> $$
> P_k = \nabla\cdot(r_k\vec P) - r_k\,\nabla\cdot\vec P .
> $$
>
> **3. Integrate** over $\mathcal V$ (eq. (6.7)) and apply the divergence theorem to the field $r_k\vec P$:
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V}P_k\,d^3r = \oint_{\mathcal S}r_k\,(\vec P\cdot\hat n)\,dS + \int_{\mathcal V}r_k\,(-\nabla\cdot\vec P)\,d^3r = \oint_{\mathcal S}r_k\,\sigma_P\,dS + \int_{\mathcal V}r_k\,\rho_P\,d^3r ,
> $$
>
> which is the $k$-th component of $\vec p$ (eq. (6.8)).
>
> **4. The same in one line with distributions.** By [[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-2|Theorem §C5.1.2]], $\vec p$ is the first moment of $\rho_{\text{tot}} = -\nabla\cdot\vec P_{\text{ext}}$; since $\vec P_{\text{ext}}$ has compact support, $r_k$ may serve as test function, and $p_k = (-\nabla\cdot\vec P_{\text{ext}})[r_k] = \vec P_{\text{ext}}[\nabla r_k] = \int_{\mathcal V}P_k\,d^3r$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The dipole moment of the polarization charge is the volume integral of $\vec P$, for every choice of $\vec P$ allowed by [[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-1|Theorem §C5.1.1]] (a curl $\nabla\times\vec G$ with $\vec G = \vec 0$ on $\mathcal S$ integrates to zero).
> - Assumption used: $\vec P \in C^1$ up to $\mathcal S$ (or the distributional version of step 4).

> [!remark] Remark: The Lorentz model, and why it is unreliable
> Lorentz (1902) read eq. (6.9) cell by cell: the polarization at a macroscopic point $\vec r$ is the dipole moment of the microscopic charge in a cell of volume $\Omega$ labelled by $\vec r$, $\vec P(\vec r) = \Omega^{-1}\int_\Omega d^3s\,\vec s\,\rho_{\text{micro}}(\vec s)$ (Zangwill eq. (6.10)); as $\Omega \to 0$ the dielectric becomes a continuum of point dipoles. This works for dilute gases, non-polar liquids and van der Waals molecular solids, whose molecules keep their identity. It fails wherever chemical bonding puts "bond charge" on the cell boundaries, and it is ambiguous even without bond charge: in Purcell's checkerboard ionic crystal (Zangwill Fig. 6.1), two equally sensible cell choices give oppositely directed $\vec P$, and another gives $\vec P = \vec 0$. For NaBr, a favourable case, a quantum calculation shows that the cell dipoles of eq. (6.10) account for less than half of the true polarization; for covalent silicon the cell picture is qualitatively wrong. What survives of Lorentz's idea is exact, and is [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-2|Theorem §C5.2.2]]: once $\vec P$ is known by whatever means, its potential *is* that of a continuum of point dipoles.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.2.2–§6.2.3, eq. (6.10), Figs. 6.1–6.2*

^rem-c5-1-2

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The modern theory of polarization
> The $\rho_P = -\nabla\cdot\vec P$ relation says that $\vec P$ carries *more* information than $\rho_P$ ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-1|Theorem §C5.1.1]]), whereas a dipole moment per volume would carry *less* than $\rho_{\text{micro}}$. The resolution of the 1990s (Resta and Vanderbilt) is that $\vec P$ encodes the *phase* of the electronic wave function, not only $|\psi|^2 = \rho$. Operationally, since a changing polarization carries the current $\vec j_P = \partial\vec P/\partial t$, $\vec P$ is the cell average of the time-integrated microscopic polarization current that flows while the field is switched on (Zangwill eq. (6.11), assuming $\vec P = \vec 0$ at $t = -\infty$):
>
> $$
> \vec P(\vec r) = \frac1\Omega\int_\Omega d^3s\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,\vec j_{P,\text{micro}}(\vec s, t) .
> $$
>
> Currents flowing *between* cells, invisible to eq. (6.10), carry the majority of the polarization in NaBr. A quantum-mechanical calculation of $\vec j_{P,\text{micro}}$ is generally required.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.2.3, eq. (6.11) and footnote 4*

^rem-c5-1-3
