Given a polarization ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^def-c5-1-2|Def. §C5.1.2]]), what field does it produce? Because its polarization charge *is* the macroscopic charge ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^def-c5-1-1|Def. §C5.1.1]]), the Coulomb integrals of that charge give the field everywhere, inside matter as well as outside, with no averaging argument (contrast level B, [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]]). The dipole picture then becomes a *theorem*: exact for the potential, and ambiguous for the field inside matter unless a cavity is specified. The section ends with what is new about $\vec D$ at this level: its Helmholtz representation, whose curl sources sit on interfaces. Everything about $\vec D$ that level B proved ([[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement|§B5.2]]) is linked, not repeated.

*Notation.* SI; potential $V$ (Zangwill writes $\varphi$: [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: The potential is V]]); sample volume $\mathcal V$ with boundary $\mathcal S$ and outward unit normal $\hat n$ (Zangwill writes $V$, $S$); separation vector $\vec{\mathcal R} = \vec r - \vec r\,'$, $\mathcal R = |\vec{\mathcal R}|$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]); Zangwill's $\rho_P$, $\sigma_P$ are Griffiths' $\rho_b$, $\sigma_b$.

## The field of polarized matter

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.2.1: Potential and Field of Polarized Matter
> For a given polarization $\vec P$ ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^def-c5-1-2|Def. §C5.1.2]]), the potential and field it produces are the Coulomb integrals of its polarization charge (Zangwill eqs. (6.12)–(6.13)):
>
> $$
> V_P(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}d^3r'\,\frac{-\nabla'\cdot\vec P(\vec r\,')}{\mathcal R} + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint_{\mathcal S}dS'\,\frac{\vec P(\vec r\,')\cdot\hat n'}{\mathcal R} , \qquad
> \vec E_P = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}d^3r'\,\frac{(-\nabla'\cdot\vec P)\,\vec{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^3} + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint_{\mathcal S}dS'\,\frac{(\vec P\cdot\hat n')\,\vec{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^3} .
> $$
>
> Both integrals converge, and $\vec E_P = -\nabla V_P$, at every point not on $\mathcal S$, **inside $\mathcal V$ as well as outside**: no averaging argument is needed (contrast [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]]). $V_P$ is continuous across $\mathcal S$; $\vec E_P$ has one-sided limits there whose normal components jump by $\sigma_P/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.3, eqs. (6.12)–(6.13)*

^thm-c5-2-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Coulomb integrals apply.** $\rho_P$ and $\sigma_P$ are macroscopic charge densities by [[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^def-c5-1-1|Def. §C5.1.1]], so their potential is the Coulomb integral for volume and surface charge ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]; Zangwill's eqs. (3.10), (3.12), [[§C2.1 The Static Limit and the Field of a Charge Distribution#^thm-c2-1-3|§C2.1, Theorem §C2.1.3]]). Inserting eqs. (6.4)–(6.5) gives $V_P$.
>
> **2. The volume integrals converge everywhere.** $\vec P \in C^1$ on the compact set $\bar{\mathcal V}$, so $|\nabla\cdot\vec P| \le M$. Near $\vec r\,' = \vec r$, in spherical coordinates about $\vec r$ ($d^3r' = \mathcal R^2\,d\mathcal R\,d\Omega$; Jacobian $\mathcal R^2$):
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal R < a}\frac{d^3r'}{\mathcal R} = 4\pi\int_0^a\mathcal R\,d\mathcal R = 2\pi a^2 , \qquad \int_{\mathcal R < a}\frac{d^3r'}{\mathcal R^2} = 4\pi\int_0^a d\mathcal R = 4\pi a ,
> $$
>
> both finite: $1/\mathcal R$ and $1/\mathcal R^2$ are locally integrable in three dimensions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-1|QFT Theorem §CA.2.1]], 2). So the volume parts of $V_P$ and $\vec E_P$ converge absolutely, also for $\vec r \in \mathcal V$.
>
> **3. The surface integrals converge off $\mathcal S$.** If $\vec r \notin \mathcal S$, its distance $d$ to the compact surface $\mathcal S$ is positive, $\mathcal R \ge d$ on $\mathcal S$, and the integrands are bounded by $\max|\vec P|/d$ and $\max|\vec P|/d^2$.
>
> **4. Field from potential.** The Coulomb potential of a bounded volume density is continuously differentiable, with gradient taken under the integral (the step that makes $\vec E = -\nabla V$ for any bounded $\rho$, [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]); off $\mathcal S$ the surface integrand is smooth in $\vec r$, so the gradient also passes under it. With $-\nabla(1/\mathcal R) = \vec{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^3$ this gives $\vec E_P$.
>
> **5. On $\mathcal S$.** At a point of $\mathcal S$ the surface field integral is only conditionally convergent; its one-sided limits exist and differ by $\sigma_P\hat n/\varepsilon_0$, the standard jump across a surface charge ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]); the potential is continuous because $1/\mathcal R$ is integrable on a surface.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Because the polarization charge *is* the macroscopic charge, the field inside matter needs no separate justification; level B's averaging argument becomes unnecessary.
> - The only singular set is $\mathcal S$, where the field jumps as for any surface charge.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.2.2: Polarized Matter as a Superposition of Point Dipoles
> At **every** point $\vec r$ (inside or outside $\mathcal V$), the potential of [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]] equals the absolutely convergent integral (Zangwill eq. (6.22))
>
> $$
> V_P(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}d^3r'\,\vec P(\vec r\,')\cdot\nabla'\frac{1}{\mathcal R} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}d^3r'\,\frac{\vec P(\vec r\,')\cdot\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2} ,
> $$
>
> the potential of a continuum of point dipoles $d\vec p = \vec P\,d^3r'$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^mod-b4-2-1|Model §B4.2.1]]; [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^mod-c3-1-2|Model §C3.1.2]]). Lorentz's picture is thus exact for the potential, for any $\vec P$, whatever its microscopic origin. For the *field* at interior points the analogous dipole sum is ambiguous ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-3|Theorem §C5.2.3]]).
>
> Same formula as [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]], read in the opposite direction: there the dipole form was the start and held outside the body; here it is derived from the charges and holds everywhere.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.3.1, eqs. (6.20)–(6.22)*

^thm-c5-2-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Surface term as a volume integral, $\vec r \notin \bar{\mathcal V}$.** $\vec P/\mathcal R$ is $C^1$ on $\bar{\mathcal V}$, and the divergence theorem gives $\oint_{\mathcal S}\vec P\cdot\hat n'/\mathcal R\,dS' = \int_{\mathcal V}\nabla'\cdot(\vec P/\mathcal R)\,d^3r'$ (Zangwill eq. (6.20)).
>
> **2. The same for $\vec r \in \mathcal V$.** Now $\vec P/\mathcal R$ is singular at $\vec r\,' = \vec r$. Excise a ball $B_\eta$ of radius $\eta$ about $\vec r$ and apply the divergence theorem on $\mathcal V\setminus B_\eta$. Its boundary is $\mathcal S$ plus the sphere $\partial B_\eta$, on which the outward normal of $\mathcal V\setminus B_\eta$ points into the ball and $\mathcal R = \eta$:
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathcal V\setminus B_\eta}\nabla'\cdot\frac{\vec P}{\mathcal R}\,d^3r' = \oint_{\mathcal S}\frac{\vec P\cdot\hat n'}{\mathcal R}\,dS' - \oint_{\partial B_\eta}\frac{\vec P\cdot\hat{\mathcal R}'}{\eta}\,dS' , \qquad \Bigl|\oint_{\partial B_\eta}\frac{\vec P\cdot\hat{\mathcal R}'}{\eta}\,dS'\Bigr| \le \frac{\max|\vec P|}{\eta}\,4\pi\eta^2 \to 0 ,
> $$
>
> where $\hat{\mathcal R}'$ is the unit vector from $\vec r$ to $\vec r\,'$. The left side converges as $\eta \to 0$ because of step 3, so step 1 holds for interior points too.
>
> **3. Product rule** (eq. (6.21)): $\nabla'\cdot(\vec P/\mathcal R) = (\nabla'\cdot\vec P)/\mathcal R + \vec P\cdot\nabla'(1/\mathcal R)$; both terms are locally integrable ($1/\mathcal R$ and $|\nabla'(1/\mathcal R)| = 1/\mathcal R^2$, as in [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^der-c5-2-1|Derivation §C5.2.1]], step 2).
>
> **4. Cancellation.** Substituting into $V_P$ of [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]]:
>
> $$
> 4\pi\varepsilon_0V_P = -\int_{\mathcal V}\frac{\nabla'\cdot\vec P}{\mathcal R}\,d^3r' + \int_{\mathcal V}\frac{\nabla'\cdot\vec P}{\mathcal R}\,d^3r' + \int_{\mathcal V}\vec P\cdot\nabla'\frac1{\mathcal R}\,d^3r' = \int_{\mathcal V}\vec P\cdot\nabla'\frac1{\mathcal R}\,d^3r' .
> $$
>
> **5. Dipole form.** $\nabla'(1/\mathcal R) = \vec{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^3 = \hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2 = -\nabla(1/\mathcal R)$, and $\vec p\cdot\hat{\mathcal R}/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\mathcal R^2)$ is the potential of a point dipole $\vec p$ at $\vec r\,'$ (Zangwill eqs. (4.13), (4.35)).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The bound-charge form and the dipole form of the potential are the same integral, rearranged by one integration by parts; the cancellation of step 4 is exact.
> - The dipole form converges absolutely everywhere because the dipole *potential* kernel is $O(\mathcal R^{-2})$; the dipole *field* kernel is $O(\mathcal R^{-3})$, which is why the field needs the next theorem.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.2.3: Dipole Sums inside Matter Depend on the Cavity
> Let $\vec r$ be an interior point of $\mathcal V$ near which $\vec P$ is $C^1$. Fix a **cavity shape**: a bounded region $K$ with piecewise smooth boundary and the origin in its interior; let $C_\eta = \{\vec r + \eta\vec s : \vec s \in K\}$. Define the dipole-field sum outside the cavity (Zangwill's $\vec E_P^\dagger$, eq. (6.23)),
>
> $$
> \vec E^\dagger_\eta(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V\setminus C_\eta}d^3r'\,\frac{3\hat{\mathcal R}(\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec P) - \vec P}{\mathcal R^3} .
> $$
>
> 1. $\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\to0}\vec E^\dagger_\eta(\vec r) = \vec E_P(\vec r) + \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\,\mathsf N_K\vec P(\vec r)$, with $\vec E_P$ of [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]] and the **depolarization tensor** $(\mathsf N_K)_{ij} = \frac{1}{4\pi}\oint_{\partial K}\frac{s_i\,n_j}{|\vec s|^3}\,dS$: the field at the centre of the body $K$ uniformly polarized with $\vec P$ is $-\mathsf N_K\vec P/\varepsilon_0$, the same for every size. It is the tensor $\mathcal N_{ij}$ that fixes the contact term of the dipole kernel for a non-spherical excision ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]]).
> 2. $\operatorname{tr}\mathsf N_K = 1$ for every shape.
> 3. Sphere: $\mathsf N = \frac13\mathbb 1$; cube: $\frac13\mathbb 1$; thin pancake with normal $\hat n$: $\hat n\hat n^{\mathsf T}$; long needle along $\hat n$: $\frac12(\mathbb 1 - \hat n\hat n^{\mathsf T})$.
>
> So the dipole sum (6.23) has no value inside matter until a cavity is specified (Zangwill: the value "depends on the *shape* of the cavity"); the spherical cavity gives $\vec E_P + \vec P/3\varepsilon_0$, the Lorentz field of [[§C5.4 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^thm-c5-4-3|Theorem §C5.4.3]].
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.3.1, eq. (6.23) and footnote 6 ("cavity definitions"); Problem 6.5 · the cavity limit and the tensor $\mathsf N_K$ worked out here*

^thm-c5-2-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Outside its own body, the dipole field is exact.** Consider the polarization restricted to $\mathcal V\setminus C_\eta$ (extended by zero). The point $\vec r$ lies in the open cavity, outside this body. By [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-2|Theorem §C5.2.2]] its potential near $\vec r$ is $V_\eta(\vec x) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V\setminus C_\eta}\vec P\cdot(\vec x - \vec r\,')/|\vec x - \vec r\,'|^3\,d^3r'$, whose integrand is smooth in $\vec x$ for $\vec x$ in the cavity, so the gradient passes under the integral. Componentwise,
>
> $$
> -\partial_{x_i}\frac{P_j(x_j - r'_j)}{|\vec x - \vec r\,'|^3} = -\frac{P_i}{|\vec x - \vec r\,'|^3} + \frac{3P_j(x_j - r'_j)(x_i - r'_i)}{|\vec x - \vec r\,'|^5} ,
> $$
>
> which at $\vec x = \vec r$ is the dipole field $[3\hat{\mathcal R}(\hat{\mathcal R}\cdot\vec P) - \vec P]/\mathcal R^3$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]]). Hence the field of this body at $\vec r$ is exactly $\vec E^\dagger_\eta(\vec r)$.
>
> **2. Superposition.** $\vec P_{\text{ext}} = \vec P\,\Theta_{\mathcal V\setminus C_\eta} + \vec P\,\Theta_{C_\eta}$, and the charges of [[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-2|Theorem §C5.1.2]] are linear in $\vec P_{\text{ext}}$; the new surface layers on $\partial C_\eta$ cancel between the two pieces (outward normals opposite). So $\vec E_P(\vec r) = \vec E^\dagger_\eta(\vec r) + \vec E_{C_\eta}(\vec r)$, where $\vec E_{C_\eta}$ is the field, computed from charges ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]], exact at the interior point $\vec r$), of the polarization inside the cavity region.
>
> **3. The uniform part is size-independent.** Write $\vec P(\vec r\,') = \vec P(\vec r) + \Delta\vec P(\vec r\,')$ on $C_\eta$. The uniform part has no volume charge and the surface charge $\vec P(\vec r)\cdot\hat n'$ on $\partial C_\eta$; its field at $\vec r$ is
>
> $$
> \vec E_{\text{unif}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint_{\partial C_\eta}(\vec P\cdot\hat n')\,\frac{\vec r - \vec r\,'}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3}\,dS' .
> $$
>
> Change variables $\vec r\,' = \vec r + \eta\vec s$, $\vec s \in \partial K$. What changes: $dS' = \eta^2\,dS_s$ (areas scale by $\eta^2$), $\vec r - \vec r\,' = -\eta\vec s$, $|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3 = \eta^3|\vec s|^3$; the normal $\hat n'$ is unchanged by a dilation. The powers of $\eta$ cancel, $\eta^2\cdot\eta/\eta^3 = 1$:
>
> $$
> \vec E_{\text{unif}} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint_{\partial K}(\vec P\cdot\hat n)\,\frac{\vec s}{|\vec s|^3}\,dS , \qquad (E_{\text{unif}})_i = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}(\mathsf N_K)_{ij}P_j .
> $$
>
> **4. The non-uniform part vanishes as $\eta \to 0$.** With $L = \sup|\nabla\vec P|$ near $\vec r$ and $d_K = \max_K|\vec s|$, $|\Delta\vec P| \le L\eta d_K$ on $C_\eta$. Its charges are the volume charge $-\nabla\cdot\vec P$ (bounded by $3L$) and the surface charge $\Delta\vec P\cdot\hat n'$ (bounded by $L\eta d_K$). Their field at $\vec r$ is bounded by
>
> $$
> \frac{3L}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal R < \eta d_K}\frac{d^3r'}{\mathcal R^2} + \frac{L\eta d_K}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint_{\partial C_\eta}\frac{dS'}{\mathcal R^2} = \frac{3L\eta d_K}{\varepsilon_0} + \frac{L\eta d_K}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\oint_{\partial K}\frac{dS}{|\vec s|^2} = O(\eta) ,
> $$
>
> using step 2 of [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^der-c5-2-1|Derivation §C5.2.1]] for the first integral and the same dilation for the second (finite, since the origin has positive distance from $\partial K$).
>
> **5. Part 1.** $\vec E^\dagger_\eta(\vec r) = \vec E_P(\vec r) - \vec E_{C_\eta}(\vec r) = \vec E_P(\vec r) + \mathsf N_K\vec P(\vec r)/\varepsilon_0 + O(\eta)$.
>
> **6. Part 2: the trace is a solid angle.** $\operatorname{tr}\mathsf N_K = \frac{1}{4\pi}\oint_{\partial K}\frac{\vec s\cdot\hat n}{|\vec s|^3}\,dS$ is $\frac{1}{4\pi}$ times the solid angle subtended at the origin by the closed surface $\partial K$ around it, which is $4\pi$ (Gauss's law for a unit point charge, [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]]; [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]). So $\operatorname{tr}\mathsf N_K = 1$.
>
> **7. Part 3.** *Sphere:* the interior field of a uniformly polarized sphere is $-\vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]). *Cube:* a rotation $\mathsf R$ of the cube's symmetry group maps $K$ to itself, so $\mathsf R\mathsf N\mathsf R^{\mathsf T} = \mathsf N$; the only matrices commuting with all of them are multiples of $\mathbb 1$, and part 2 fixes the multiple, $\frac13$ ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^ex-c5-2-2|Example §C5.2.2]] checks it directly). *Pancake:* the faces carry $\pm\vec P\cdot\hat n$, two opposite sheets, whose field between them is $-(\vec P\cdot\hat n)\hat n/\varepsilon_0$; the rim's contribution vanishes as thickness/width $\to 0$. *Needle:* polarized along its axis, the charges $\pm(\vec P\cdot\hat n)A$ sit on the distant tips and give zero at the centre in the limit; polarized across, it is the long cylinder with interior field $-\vec P/2\varepsilon_0$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^ex-b5-1-2|Example §B5.1.2]]). Trace: $0 + \frac12 + \frac12 = 1$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The integral (6.23) is not absolutely convergent at interior points (its kernel is $O(\mathcal R^{-3})$), and the excluded region carries a finite, shape-dependent field that does not shrink with its size (step 3).
> - $\vec E_P$ computed from charges is the cavity-free macroscopic field; every "cavity field" differs from it by $\mathsf N_K\vec P/\varepsilon_0$.
> - Assumptions used: $\vec P \in C^1$ near $\vec r$; the origin strictly inside $K$.

> [!remark] Remark: Cavity fields, the averaging argument, and the local field
> - Griffiths' averaging over a sphere ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]]) and Theorem §C5.2.3 are two views of one fact: the dipole sum outside a small sphere plus the sphere's own average field $-\vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ is $\vec E_P$. The sphere is singled out only because it is the natural averaging region; the macroscopic field itself depends on no cavity.
> - "Cavity definitions" of the older literature (Zangwill, footnote 6) are the special cases of part 1: a needle cavity along $\vec P$ measures $\vec E_P$, a pancake cavity across $\vec P$ measures $\vec E_P + \vec P/\varepsilon_0$ (when $\vec P \parallel \hat n$); with the external field added to both, these are $\vec E$ and $\vec D/\varepsilon_0$. With a cavity actually cut into a linear medium, the polarization near the cavity responds too; the cavity fields of a linear medium are [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^rem-c5-3-2|§C5.3, Remark: Cavity fields in a linear medium]].
> - The spherical value $\vec E_P + \vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ is the field that polarizes one molecule of a dilute dielectric, the Lorentz local field ([[§C5.4 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^thm-c5-4-3|Theorem §C5.4.3]]).

^rem-c5-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.2.4: Poisson's Formula
> Let a body $\mathcal V$ carry a **uniform** polarization $\vec P$, and let $\vec{\mathcal E}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}d^3r'\,\vec{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^3$ be the field of the same body filled with uniform charge density of unit magnitude. Then, at every point not on $\mathcal S$ (Zangwill eq. (6.15)),
>
> $$
> V_P(\vec r) = \vec P\cdot\vec{\mathcal E}(\vec r), \qquad \vec E_P(\vec r) = -(\vec P\cdot\nabla)\,\vec{\mathcal E}(\vec r) .
> $$
>
> The field of any uniformly polarized body is a directional derivative of a Gauss's-law field.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.3, eqs. (6.14)–(6.15)*

^thm-c5-2-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Only the surface charge.** $\nabla\cdot\vec P = 0$, so by [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]] $4\pi\varepsilon_0V_P = \oint_{\mathcal S}(\vec P\cdot\hat n')/\mathcal R\,dS' = \vec P\cdot\oint_{\mathcal S}\hat n'/\mathcal R\,dS'$, pulling out the constant $\vec P$.
>
> **2. Gradient theorem.** $\oint_{\mathcal S}f\,\hat n\,dS = \int_{\mathcal V}\nabla f\,d^3r'$ (the divergence theorem for $f\hat e_k$, each $k$) with $f = 1/\mathcal R$. For $\vec r \in \mathcal V$ excise a ball $B_\eta$ as in [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^der-c5-2-2|Derivation §C5.2.2]], step 2: the extra term $\oint_{\partial B_\eta}\hat n/\eta\,dS$ is zero by symmetry (and in any case bounded by $4\pi\eta$). Hence $\oint_{\mathcal S}\hat n'/\mathcal R\,dS' = \int_{\mathcal V}\nabla'(1/\mathcal R)\,d^3r'$, a convergent integral.
>
> **3. Move the gradient out.** $\nabla'(1/\mathcal R) = -\nabla(1/\mathcal R)$, and the gradient of the Coulomb potential of the bounded density $\Theta_{\mathcal V}$ may be taken under the integral ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^der-c5-2-1|Derivation §C5.2.1]], step 4):
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}\nabla'\frac{1}{\mathcal R}\,d^3r' = -\nabla\Bigl[\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal V}\frac{d^3r'}{\mathcal R}\Bigr] = \vec{\mathcal E}(\vec r) ,
> $$
>
> since the bracket is the potential of the unit-density body and $\vec{\mathcal E}$ is minus its gradient. So $V_P = \vec P\cdot\vec{\mathcal E}$. ⚑ By-product: the potential of a uniformly polarized body is $\vec P\cdot\vec{\mathcal E}$; for a sphere, $\vec{\mathcal E} = \vec r/3\varepsilon_0$ inside gives $V_P = \vec P\cdot\vec r/3\varepsilon_0$, Griffiths' $(P/3\varepsilon_0)r\cos\theta$.
>
> **4. Field.** $\vec E_P = -\nabla(\vec P\cdot\vec{\mathcal E})$. For constant $\vec P$, $\nabla(\vec P\cdot\vec{\mathcal E}) = (\vec P\cdot\nabla)\vec{\mathcal E} + \vec P\times(\nabla\times\vec{\mathcal E})$, and $\nabla\times\vec{\mathcal E} = \vec 0$ (an electrostatic field); hence $\vec E_P = -(\vec P\cdot\nabla)\vec{\mathcal E}$. Off $\mathcal S$, $\vec{\mathcal E}$ is differentiable (inside, it solves $\varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec{\mathcal E} = 1$ with a constant source; outside, it is harmonic).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Zangwill's route moves $\partial'_k$ onto the kernel inside a volume integral (eq. (6.14)); routing it through the potential, as here, avoids differentiating the singular kernel $\vec{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^3$ at interior points.
> - Assumptions used: $\vec P$ uniform; $\vec r \notin \mathcal S$.

## The total field and the auxiliary field D

> [!remark] Remark: What carries over from level B unchanged
> Gauss's law for the total field reads $\varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho_P + \rho_f = -\nabla\cdot\vec P + \rho_f$ (Zangwill eq. (6.24)). The auxiliary field $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P$ (eq. (6.25); [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^def-b5-2-2|Def. §B5.2.2]]), with $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ (eq. (6.26)), obeys $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f$, $\oint\vec D\cdot d\vec S = Q_f$ (eqs. (6.27), (6.29); [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]]) and $\nabla\times\vec D = \nabla\times\vec P$ (eq. (6.28); [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]]). The matching conditions at an interface carrying free charge $\sigma_f$, with $\hat n_2$ the outward normal of region 2 (pointing into region 1), are (eqs. (6.31)–(6.33); [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]])
>
> $$
> \hat n_2\cdot(\vec D_1 - \vec D_2) = \sigma_f , \qquad \hat n_2\times(\vec D_1 - \vec D_2) = \hat n_2\times(\vec P_1 - \vec P_2) \iff \hat n_2\times(\vec E_1 - \vec E_2) = \vec 0 .
> $$
>
> Same results, same arguments (pillbox and thin loop): level B is their home. What is new here is the explicit form of $\vec D$ in terms of its sources, next.

^rem-c5-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.2.5: Helmholtz Representation of D
> For localized sources, with $\nabla\times\vec P$ understood as a generalized function (including surface layers where $\vec P$ jumps), (Zangwill eq. (6.30))
>
> $$
> \vec D(\vec r) = -\nabla\int d^3r'\,\frac{\rho_f(\vec r\,')}{4\pi\mathcal R} + \nabla\times\int d^3r'\,\frac{\nabla'\times\vec P(\vec r\,')}{4\pi\mathcal R} .
> $$
>
> If $\nabla\times\vec P = \vec 0$ inside each of several regions separated by surfaces $\mathcal S_k$ (as in piecewise simple dielectrics, [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^mod-c5-3-1|Model §C5.3.1]]), the curl sources are sheets on the interfaces (Zangwill eq. (6.42), stated there as an exercise):
>
> $$
> \vec D(\vec r) = -\nabla\int d^3r'\,\frac{\rho_f(\vec r\,')}{4\pi\mathcal R} + \sum_k\nabla\times\oint_{\mathcal S_k}dS'_k\,\frac{\vec P(\vec r\,')\times\hat n(\vec r\,')}{4\pi\mathcal R} ,
> $$
>
> where each region contributes on its own boundary with its own $\vec P$ and outward $\hat n$ (at an interface between media 1 and 2, $\hat n$ from 1 to 2: $(\vec P_1 - \vec P_2)\times\hat n$). Free charge is the only source of $\vec D$ only if these sheets vanish, i.e. if $\vec P$ is normal to every interface.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.4.1, eq. (6.30); §6.5.1, eqs. (6.41)–(6.42)*

^thm-c5-2-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Helmholtz theorem.** A field vanishing at infinity faster than $1/r$ is fixed by its divergence and curl ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]): $\vec D = -\nabla\int\frac{\nabla'\cdot\vec D}{4\pi\mathcal R}\,d^3r' + \nabla\times\int\frac{\nabla'\times\vec D}{4\pi\mathcal R}\,d^3r'$. For localized sources $\vec D = O(r^{-2})$.
>
> **2. Insert the sources.** $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f$ and $\nabla\times\vec D = \nabla\times\vec P$ ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^rem-c5-2-2|Remark: What carries over from level B unchanged]]). This is eq. (6.30). Where $\vec P$ jumps, $\nabla\times\vec P$ is a distribution, and the Helmholtz integrals are its action on $1/(4\pi\mathcal R)$.
>
> **3. Curl across a jump.** For region $k$ with indicator $\Theta_k$ and outward normal $\hat n_k$, the product rule for distributions and $\nabla\Theta_k = -\hat n_k\delta_{\mathcal S_k}$ ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-2|Theorem §C5.1.2]]) give
>
> $$
> \nabla\times(\Theta_k\vec P_k) = \Theta_k\,\nabla\times\vec P_k + (\nabla\Theta_k)\times\vec P_k = \Theta_k\,\nabla\times\vec P_k - \hat n_k\times\vec P_k\,\delta_{\mathcal S_k} = \Theta_k\,\nabla\times\vec P_k + \vec P_k\times\hat n_k\,\delta_{\mathcal S_k} .
> $$
>
> (The product of the smooth $\vec P_k$ with the layer is defined because one factor is smooth, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-7|QFT Theorem §CA.2.7]], 1.)
>
> **4. Piecewise curl-free.** With $\nabla\times\vec P_k = \vec 0$ in each region, $\nabla\times\vec P = \sum_k\vec P_k\times\hat n_k\,\delta_{\mathcal S_k}$. Acting on $1/(4\pi\mathcal R)$, each layer turns $\int d^3r'$ into $\oint_{\mathcal S_k}dS'$: this is eq. (6.42). At an interface between 1 and 2, $\hat n_2 = -\hat n_1$, so the two contributions combine into $(\vec P_1 - \vec P_2)\times\hat n_1$.
>
> **5. When the sheets vanish.** $\vec P\times\hat n = \vec 0$ on every interface iff $\vec P$ is normal to it there; then $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E_{\text{vac}}$ with $\vec E_{\text{vac}}$ the vacuum field of $\rho_f$ (e.g. concentric spheres around a central charge).
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The "deceptive parallel" ([[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^cau-b5-2-1|§B5.2, Caution]]) has a precise form: $\vec D$ has two kinds of sources, free charge and vortex sheets $\vec P\times\hat n$ on interfaces.
> - The curl term has the structure of $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$ in the Coulomb gauge (Zangwill), and the sheets $\vec P\times\hat n$ mirror the bound surface currents $\vec M\times\hat n$ of magnetized matter ([[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]]).

> [!definition] Definition §C5.2.1: Constitutive Relation; Susceptibility Tensors
> The equations $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P$, $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$, $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f$ can be solved only if $\vec P$ is given once and for all or a **constitutive relation** ties $\vec P$ to $\vec E$; it injects quantum and statistical information into classical electrodynamics. For matter unpolarized in its ground state, experiment gives the expansion (Zangwill eq. (6.34))
>
> $$
> P_i = \varepsilon_0\chi_{ij}E_j + \varepsilon_0\chi^{(2)}_{ijk}E_jE_k + \cdots ,
> $$
>
> with the **susceptibility tensor** $\chi_{ij}$ (anisotropic matter: $\vec P \not\parallel \vec E$) and **nonlinear susceptibilities** $\chi^{(2)}_{ijk}, \dots$ (important in all matter at large enough field). A **ferroelectric** has $\vec P \ne \vec 0$ at $\vec E = \vec 0$ and is not described by this expansion. The isotropic linear case is the simple dielectric of [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^mod-c5-3-1|Model §C5.3.1]] (scalar $\chi_e$; Zangwill writes $\chi$, [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^cau-c5-3-1|§C5.3, Caution: κ, ε_r, χ and χ_e]]); level B's linear law is [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^law-b5-3-1|Law §B5.3.1]].
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.4.3, eq. (6.34)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C5.2.1: The Uniformly Polarized Sphere by Poisson's Formula
> For a sphere of radius $R$ and volume $\mathcal V = \frac43\pi R^3$, Gauss's law gives the unit-density field $\vec{\mathcal E} = \vec r/3\varepsilon_0$ inside and $\mathcal V\vec r/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3)$ outside (Zangwill eq. (6.16)). [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-4|Theorem §C5.2.4]] with $\partial_ir_j = \delta_{ij}$ and $\partial_j(r_i/r^3) = \delta_{ij}/r^3 - 3r_ir_j/r^5$:
>
> $$
> \vec E_P = -\frac{(\vec P\cdot\nabla)\vec r}{3\varepsilon_0} = -\frac{\vec P}{3\varepsilon_0} \quad (r < R), \qquad \vec E_P = -\frac{\mathcal V}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\frac{\vec P}{r^3} - \frac{3(\vec P\cdot\vec r)\vec r}{r^5}\Bigr] = \frac{\mathcal V}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{3(\hat r\cdot\vec P)\hat r - \vec P}{r^3} \quad (r > R) ,
> $$
>
> level B's result ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]], its home) in two lines (eq. (6.17)). Outside: a point dipole $\vec p = \mathcal V\vec P$, as [[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-3|Theorem §C5.1.3]] requires. Inside: $\int_{\text{sphere}}\vec E_P\,d^3r = -\mathcal V\vec P/3\varepsilon_0 = -\vec p/3\varepsilon_0$, the same as the delta-function (contact) term $-\vec p\,\delta^3(\vec r)/3\varepsilon_0$ of a point dipole's field ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]]; [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^rem-b4-2-1|§B4.2, Remark: The contact term at the dipole's position]]): a point dipole is the $R \to 0$ limit of a uniformly polarized sphere, contact term included. Uniform interior fields are special: a uniformly polarized body has a uniform interior field if and only if it is an ellipsoid, and then $\vec E_P$ is generally not antiparallel to $\vec P$ (Zangwill footnote 5, Brownstein 1987).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.3, Application 6.1 (Method I), eqs. (6.16)–(6.17) and footnote 5*

^ex-c5-2-1

> [!example] Example §C5.2.2: The Field at the Centre of a Uniformly Polarized Cube
> A cube of edge $a$ is polarized uniformly along an edge, $\vec P = P\hat z$ (Zangwill Problem 6.5). Only the faces $z = \pm a/2$ carry charge, $\sigma_P = \vec P\cdot\hat n = \pm P$. The field at the centre of a uniformly charged plane face with outward normal $\hat n$ (pointing away from the centre) has normal component
>
> $$
> \vec E(0)\cdot\hat n = \frac{\sigma}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\text{face}}\frac{(\vec 0 - \vec r\,')\cdot\hat n}{r'^3}\,dS' = -\frac{\sigma}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{\text{face}}\frac{\hat r'\cdot\hat n}{r'^2}\,dS' = -\frac{\sigma\,\Omega_{\text{face}}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} ,
> $$
>
> where $\Omega_{\text{face}}$ is the solid angle the face subtends at the centre ([[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]]); its tangential components cancel by symmetry. The six faces are congruent and together subtend $4\pi$, so $\Omega_{\text{face}} = 4\pi/6$. Top face ($\hat n = \hat z$, $\sigma = P$): $E_z = -P/6\varepsilon_0$. Bottom face ($\hat n = -\hat z$, $\sigma = -P$): $-E_z = +P/6\varepsilon_0$, i.e. $E_z = -P/6\varepsilon_0$. Total:
>
> $$
> \vec E(0) = -\frac{\vec P}{3\varepsilon_0} ,
> $$
>
> the same as for a sphere: the cube's depolarization tensor is $\frac13\mathbb 1$, as cubic symmetry and $\operatorname{tr}\mathsf N = 1$ require ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-3|Theorem §C5.2.3]], 3). Unlike the sphere's, the cube's interior field is not uniform away from the centre.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 6.5 (and its hint)*

^ex-c5-2-2

> [!example] Example §C5.2.3: E and D for an Annular Dielectric
> PHY 505, HW 10, Problem 3 (Zangwill 6.8), supplementary.
>
> **(a)** The shell $R_1 < r < R_2$ carries a uniform polarization $\vec P$. Write it as a ball of radius $R_2$ polarized with $\vec P$ plus a ball of radius $R_1$ polarized with $-\vec P$; fields are linear in $\vec P$ ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]]). With [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^ex-c5-2-1|Example §C5.2.1]] ($\mathcal V_i = \frac43\pi R_i^3$, so $\mathcal V_i/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = R_i^3/3\varepsilon_0$):
>
> $$
> \vec E = \begin{cases} -\dfrac{\vec P}{3\varepsilon_0} + \dfrac{\vec P}{3\varepsilon_0} = \vec 0 & r < R_1 , \\[2mm] -\dfrac{\vec P}{3\varepsilon_0} - \dfrac{R_1^3}{3\varepsilon_0r^3}\bigl[3(\hat r\cdot\vec P)\hat r - \vec P\bigr] & R_1 < r < R_2 , \\[2mm] \dfrac{R_2^3 - R_1^3}{3\varepsilon_0r^3}\bigl[3(\hat r\cdot\vec P)\hat r - \vec P\bigr] & r > R_2 . \end{cases}
> $$
>
> The hole is field-free; outside, a dipole $\vec p = \frac43\pi(R_2^3 - R_1^3)\vec P = \int\vec P\,d^3r$ ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-3|Theorem §C5.1.3]]).
>
> **(b)** A ball of radius $R$ carries $\vec P = P\hat r/r^2$ ($P$ a constant, unit C). This $\vec P$ is locally integrable ($1/r^2$ on $\mathbb R^3$, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-1|QFT Theorem §CA.2.1]], 2), so its divergence is taken in the sense of distributions: $\nabla\cdot(\hat r/r^2) = 4\pi\delta^3(\vec r)$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]). Hence
>
> $$
> \rho_P = -4\pi P\,\delta^3(\vec r), \qquad \sigma_P = \vec P\cdot\hat r\big|_R = \frac{P}{R^2}, \qquad -4\pi P + 4\pi R^2\cdot\frac{P}{R^2} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> a point charge $-4\pi P$ at the centre and a neutralizing shell (neutrality, Theorem §C5.1.3). For $\vec D$: there is no free charge, so $\nabla\cdot\vec D = 0$; and $\nabla\times\vec D = \nabla\times\vec P = \vec 0$ as a distribution, because $\vec P_{\text{ext}} = -P\,\nabla\bigl[(1/r)\bigr]\Theta(R - r)$ has curl $\Theta\,\nabla\times\nabla(\cdot) + \nabla\Theta\times\vec P = \vec 0 + (-\hat r\delta)\times(P\hat r/R^2) = \vec 0$ (a radial jump has no tangential part). $\vec D$ vanishes at infinity, so by [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-5|Theorem §C5.2.5]]
>
> $$
> \vec D = \vec 0 \ \text{everywhere}, \qquad \vec E = \frac{\vec D - \vec P}{\varepsilon_0} = -\frac{P}{\varepsilon_0}\frac{\hat r}{r^2} \quad (r < R), \qquad \vec E = \vec 0 \quad (r > R) .
> $$
>
> Check from charges: the point charge $-4\pi P$ gives $-4\pi P\hat r/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$ inside, and outside the total enclosed charge is zero. Here $\vec D$ is the field of the free charge (none) because the vortex sheets of Theorem §C5.2.5 vanish ($\vec P \parallel \hat n$ on $r = R$), in contrast with (a), where $\vec P\times\hat r \ne \vec 0$ on both spheres and $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P \ne \vec 0$ although there is no free charge.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 6.8 · PHY 505, HW 10, Problem 3 (supplementary)*
