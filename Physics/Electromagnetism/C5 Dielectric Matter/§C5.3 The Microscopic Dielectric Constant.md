Where does the number $\varepsilon_r$ come from? Level B met the answer only in a starred remark ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^rem-b5-3-6|§B5.3, ★ Remark: From molecules to the dielectric constant]]); at this level it is course material. The key notion is the **local field**, the field that actually polarizes one molecule, which differs from the macroscopic field of [[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter|§C5.1]] because it leaves out the molecule's own field. For non-polar molecules, Lorentz's spherical cell gives the local field $(\varepsilon_r + 2)\vec E/3$ and the Clausius–Mossotti formula. For polar molecules, Onsager's cavity model (HW 11) splits the field at a molecule into a cavity field, which orients it, and a reaction field, which follows it; the two estimates differ completely for water. The cavity calculation is the sphere problem of [[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-2-3|Theorem §C5.2.3]] with a dipole added at the centre.

*Notation* as in [[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter|§C5.1]] and [[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems|§C5.2]] ($\varepsilon_r$ for Zangwill's $\kappa$: [[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^cau-c5-2-1|§C5.2, Caution]]); $n$ = number of molecules per volume; $\Omega = 1/n$ the volume per molecule.

## Polarizability

> [!definition] Definition §C5.3.1: Polarizability
> A small body in a uniform field $\vec E_0$ acquires the dipole moment (Zangwill eq. (6.74))
>
> $$
> \vec p = \alpha\,\varepsilon_0\vec E_0 ;
> $$
>
> the **polarizability** $\alpha$ (Zangwill's convention) has the dimension of a volume. For an atom or molecule the induced moment depends on the microscopic field at its position, $\vec p = \alpha\varepsilon_0\vec E_{\text{micro}}$ (eq. (6.75)), with the molecule's own field excluded ([[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^def-c5-3-2|Def. §C5.3.2]]). Example: a conducting sphere of radius $R$ has $\vec p = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3\vec E_0$ ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^ex-c4-1-1|Example §C4.1.1]]; [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^ex-b4-2-1|Example §B4.2.1]]), so $\alpha = 4\pi R^3 = 3\times(\text{its volume})$, Zangwill's estimate $\alpha \approx 3V$ for a molecule.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.6.1, eqs. (6.74)–(6.75); footnote 13 (Example 5.1)*

^def-c5-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Two polarizabilities
> Griffiths and level B write $\vec p = \alpha\vec E$ ([[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^law-b4-2-3|Law §B4.2.3]]), and Likharev does the same; Zangwill writes $\vec p = \alpha\varepsilon_0\vec E$. So $\alpha_{\text{Zangwill}} = \alpha_{\text{Griffiths}}/\varepsilon_0$ (a volume), and Gaussian tables list $\alpha_{\text{Gauss}} = \alpha_{\text{Zangwill}}/4\pi$ (the conducting sphere: $R^3$). Formulas change accordingly: Zangwill's $\varepsilon_r = 1 + n\alpha$ is Griffiths' $\chi_e = N\alpha/\varepsilon_0$. This section uses Zangwill's $\alpha$.

^cau-c5-3-1

## Dilute gases and the Lorentz local field

> [!model] Model §C5.3.1: Lorentz Model of a Dielectric Gas
> Identical molecules of polarizability $\alpha$ ([[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^def-c5-3-1|Def. §C5.3.1]]), $n$ per unit volume, each the only occupant of its own Lorentz cell of volume $\Omega = 1/n$ around the macroscopic point $\vec R$. Each molecule keeps its integrity (its charge stays inside its cell), so the cell-dipole formula of [[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter#^rem-c5-1-2|§C5.1, Remark: The Lorentz model]] is reliable here: $\vec P(\vec R) = n\,\vec p_{\vec R}$, with $\vec p_{\vec R}$ the cell-averaged dipole moment (Zangwill eq. (6.77)). The macroscopic field is the cell average of the microscopic field, $\vec E(\vec R) = \Omega^{-1}\int_\Omega d^3r\,\vec E_{\text{micro}}$ (eq. (6.76); Lorentz averaging, Zangwill §2.3.1).
> - *Assumes:* weakly interacting molecules (gases, non-polar liquids, van der Waals solids); linear response of each molecule; static fields.
> - *Fails:* for bonded solids, where the cell picture itself fails ([[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter#^rem-c5-1-2|§C5.1]]), and for molecules with permanent moments ([[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^thm-c5-3-5|Theorem §C5.3.5]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.2.2, §6.6.2, eqs. (6.76)–(6.77)*

^mod-c5-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.3.2: Dilute Limit
> If the field polarizing each molecule is approximated by the macroscopic field, the Lorentz model ([[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^mod-c5-3-1|Model §C5.3.1]]) gives (Zangwill eq. (6.79))
>
> $$
> \vec P = n\alpha\varepsilon_0\vec E, \qquad \varepsilon_r = 1 + n\alpha ,
> $$
>
> valid when $n\alpha \ll 1$. Numbers (Zangwill Table 6.1, Likharev §3.3): nitrogen at 1 atm and room temperature has $\varepsilon_r - 1 = 5.5\times10^{-4}$ with $n \approx 2.5\times10^{25}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, so $\alpha \approx 2.2\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm m^3$, the polarizability of a conducting sphere of radius $(\alpha/4\pi)^{1/3} \approx 1.2\times10^{-10}$ m, a molecular size.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.6.2, eqs. (6.78)–(6.79), Table 6.1 · Likharev §3.3, eqs. (3.49)–(3.51) and the air estimate there*

^thm-c5-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Cell average.** $\vec P = n\vec p_{\vec R}$ (Model §C5.3.1) and $\vec p = \alpha\varepsilon_0\vec E_{\text{micro}}$ at each molecule; replacing the polarizing field by its cell average $\vec E(\vec R)$ gives $\vec P = n\alpha\varepsilon_0\vec E$.
>
> **2. Constitutive relation.** $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P = \varepsilon_0(1 + n\alpha)\vec E$ (eq. (6.78)); comparing with $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r\vec E$ ([[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^mod-c5-2-1|Model §C5.2.1]]) gives $\varepsilon_r = 1 + n\alpha$.
>
> **3. The hidden assumption.** ⚑ By-product: the cell average in step 1 *includes* the molecule's own field, which cannot polarize the molecule. Dropping it is harmless only if the self-field's average is small compared with $\vec E$, which (next theorem) is the condition $n\alpha \ll 1$ → [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^def-c5-3-2|Def. §C5.3.2]].
>
> **4. Nitrogen.** $\alpha = (\varepsilon_r - 1)/n = 5.5\times10^{-4}/2.5\times10^{25}\ \mathrm{m^3} = 2.2\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm{m^3}$; $\alpha/4\pi = 1.75\times10^{-30}\ \mathrm{m^3}$, whose cube root is $1.2\times10^{-10}$ m.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The dilute formula is the Lorentz model with the self-field ignored; it holds exactly to first order in $n\alpha$.

^der-c5-3-2

*Uses:* [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^mod-c5-3-1|Model §C5.3.1]], [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^def-c5-3-1|Def. §C5.3.1]], [[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^mod-c5-2-1|Model §C5.2.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §C5.3.2: Local Field
> The **local field** at a molecule is the cell average of the microscopic field minus the cell average of the molecule's own field $\vec E_{\text{self}}$ (Zangwill eq. (6.80)):
>
> $$
> \vec E_{\text{local}}(\vec R) = \vec E(\vec R) - \frac1\Omega\int_\Omega d^3s\,\vec E_{\text{self}}(\vec s) , \qquad \vec P(\vec R) = n\alpha\varepsilon_0\,\vec E_{\text{local}}(\vec R) .
> $$
>
> It is the Lorentz model's approximation to the field that polarizes one molecule: the field of everything else, averaged over the molecule's cell.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.6.2, eq. (6.80)*

^def-c5-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.3.3: Lorentz Local Field
> For a spherical cell, in the Lorentz model,
>
> $$
> \frac1\Omega\int_\Omega d^3s\,\vec E_{\text{self}} = -\frac{\vec p}{3\varepsilon_0\Omega} = -\frac{\vec P}{3\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \vec E_{\text{local}} = \vec E + \frac{\vec P}{3\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\varepsilon_r + 2}{3}\,\vec E
> $$
>
> (Zangwill eqs. (6.81), (6.85)). Since $\varepsilon_r \ge 1$, the local field exceeds the macroscopic field. It is the spherical-cavity value of the dipole sum of [[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-1-6|§C5.1, Theorem §C5.1.6]] ($\mathsf N = \frac13\mathbb 1$), and it describes the space *between* molecules; inside a molecule its own field is large and opposite to $\vec E_{\text{local}}$. For non-spherical cells the result is a good approximation when the cell is large compared with the molecular charge cloud.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.6.2, eqs. (6.80)–(6.85)*

^thm-c5-3-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Average of a self-field over a sphere.** For any charge distribution contained in a ball of radius $a$, the average of its field over the ball is $-\vec p/(4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3)$, with $\vec p$ its dipole moment about the centre, whatever the arrangement (Zangwill Example 4.1; [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]]; level B: [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^rem-b4-2-1|§B4.2, Remark: The contact term]]). The molecule's charge lies inside its spherical cell (Model §C5.3.1), $\Omega = \frac43\pi a^3$, so
>
> $$
> \frac1\Omega\int_\Omega\vec E_{\text{self}}\,d^3s = -\frac{\vec p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3} = -\frac{\vec p}{3\varepsilon_0\Omega} .
> $$
>
> **2. Per volume.** $\vec p/\Omega = n\vec p = \vec P$ (eq. (6.77)), so the average self-field is $-\vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ (eq. (6.81)).
>
> **3. Local field.** Insert into [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^def-c5-3-2|Def. §C5.3.2]]: $\vec E_{\text{local}} = \vec E + \vec P/3\varepsilon_0$.
>
> **4. In terms of $\varepsilon_r$.** $\vec P = \varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)\vec E$ (definition of $\varepsilon_r$, [[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^mod-c5-2-1|Model §C5.2.1]]), so $\vec E_{\text{local}} = \vec E[1 + (\varepsilon_r - 1)/3] = \frac{\varepsilon_r + 2}{3}\vec E$ (eq. (6.85)).
>
> **5. Relation to cavity sums.** The field of all *other* molecules, cell-averaged, is the dipole sum outside a small sphere plus nothing from inside it; by [[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-1-6|Theorem §C5.1.6]] with $\mathsf N = \frac13\mathbb 1$ this is $\vec E + \vec P/3\varepsilon_0$, the same result from the macroscopic side.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The only electrostatic input is the theorem on the mean field in a sphere; the sphere's $\frac13$ is the depolarization factor.
> - Approximations surfaced: the polarizing field is a *cell average* (not the field at the nucleus), and the cell is spherical; both are good for well-separated molecules.

^der-c5-3-3

*Uses:* [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^def-c5-3-2|Def. §C5.3.2]], [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^mod-c5-3-1|Model §C5.3.1]], [[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-1-6|Theorem §C5.1.6]], [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^rem-b4-2-1|§B4.2, Remark: The contact term]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.3.4: Clausius–Mossotti Formula
> In the Lorentz model with the Lorentz local field (Zangwill eqs. (6.82)–(6.84)),
>
> $$
> \vec P = \frac{n\alpha\varepsilon_0}{1 - n\alpha/3}\,\vec E, \qquad \varepsilon_r = 1 + \frac{n\alpha}{1 - n\alpha/3}, \qquad \frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 2} = \frac{n\alpha}{3} .
> $$
>
> For $n\alpha \ll 1$ it reduces to the dilute limit ([[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^thm-c5-3-2|Theorem §C5.3.2]]). Non-polar gases and simple liquids (He, N₂, C₆H₆, liquid CH₄) obey it well; polar water does not.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.6.2–§6.6.3, eqs. (6.82)–(6.84)*

^thm-c5-3-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Self-consistency.** $\vec P = n\alpha\varepsilon_0\vec E_{\text{local}} = n\alpha\varepsilon_0(\vec E + \vec P/3\varepsilon_0)$ ([[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^thm-c5-3-3|Theorem §C5.3.3]]), an algebraic equation for $\vec P$: $\vec P(1 - n\alpha/3) = n\alpha\varepsilon_0\vec E$, so $\vec P = n\alpha\varepsilon_0\vec E/(1 - n\alpha/3)$ (eq. (6.82)).
>
> **2. Dielectric constant.** $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P = \varepsilon_0\bigl[1 + n\alpha/(1 - n\alpha/3)\bigr]\vec E$ (eq. (6.83)).
>
> **3. Clausius–Mossotti form.** From step 2, $\varepsilon_r - 1 = \frac{n\alpha}{1 - n\alpha/3}$ and $\varepsilon_r + 2 = 3 + \frac{n\alpha}{1 - n\alpha/3} = \frac{3 - n\alpha + n\alpha}{1 - n\alpha/3} = \frac{3}{1 - n\alpha/3}$. Dividing, $\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 2} = \frac{n\alpha}{3}$ (eq. (6.84)).
>
> **4. Local field again.** Combining steps 1 and 3 of [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^der-c5-3-3|Derivation §C5.3.3]], $\vec E_{\text{local}} = \vec E/(1 - n\alpha/3) = \frac{\varepsilon_r + 2}{3}\vec E$, consistent with Theorem §C5.3.3 (eq. (6.85); compare [[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^ex-c5-2-3|§C5.2, Example §C5.2.3]], where the same factor $3/(\varepsilon_r + 2)$ appears).
>
> **5. Expansion.** $\frac{n\alpha}{1 - n\alpha/3} = n\alpha + \frac{(n\alpha)^2}{3} + O((n\alpha)^3)$: the dilute formula is the first term, and the local-field correction starts at second order.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Clausius–Mossotti is the self-consistent (mean-field) solution with the Lorentz local field; nothing else is assumed beyond Model §C5.3.1.
> - ⚑ By-product: $\varepsilon_r \to \infty$ as $n\alpha \to 3$ (step 2), the polarization catastrophe → [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^rem-c5-3-2|★ Remark: The polarization catastrophe]].

^der-c5-3-4

*Uses:* [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^thm-c5-3-3|Theorem §C5.3.3]], [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^thm-c5-3-2|Theorem §C5.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Where Clausius–Mossotti holds, and what it is about
> - Tests usually compare $(\varepsilon_r - 1)/(\varepsilon_r + 2)$ with $n\alpha/3$, the polarizability being measured independently or estimated as $\alpha \approx 3V$ ([[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^def-c5-3-1|Def. §C5.3.1]]). Zangwill's Table 6.1: He $1.000065$, N₂ $1.00055$, CH₄ (liquid) $1.7$, SiO₂ $4.5$, Si $11.8$, H₂O $80$. CH₄ obeys the formula; H₂O does not, because water molecules carry a permanent dipole $\vec p_0$ (Zangwill §6.6.3); SiO₂ and Si are bonded solids outside the model.
> - Level B derived the same formula from Griffiths' "field of everything except the molecule" ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^rem-b5-3-6|§B5.3, ★ Remark: From molecules to the dielectric constant]]), the same physics in other words. Zangwill's literature note warns that many derivations are "not all completely legitimate"; the cell-average version here makes the approximations explicit.
> - The local field exceeding $\vec E$ resolves the puzzle left in [[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^ex-c5-2-2|§C5.2, Example §C5.2.2]]: the macroscopic field is an average of a field that is large between molecules and reversed inside them.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.6.2–§6.6.3, Table 6.1, and the Section 6.6 reading notes*

^rem-c5-3-1

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The polarization catastrophe and the Lorentz–Lorenz relation
> In the Clausius–Mossotti form $\varepsilon_r = 1 + n\alpha/(1 - n\alpha/3)$ the susceptibility diverges as $n\alpha \to 3$: the unpolarized state becomes unstable and a polarization appears without an applied field. Likharev reads this as the linear-response sign of **ferroelectricity**, which real materials show with hysteresis, remanent polarization and domains (the case set aside in [[§C5.1 The Field of Polarized Matter#^def-c5-1-3|§C5.1, Def. §C5.1.3]]); the formula itself is not quantitatively right for ferroelectrics. At optical frequencies, with $\varepsilon_r$ replaced by $n_{\text{opt}}^2$, the same formula is the Lorentz–Lorenz relation; the frequency-dependent polarizability is the Lorentz oscillator of Waves and Optics ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-1|WO Def. §B13.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Likharev §3.3, eq. (3.52), footnotes 20, 22 and Fig. 8*

^rem-c5-3-2

## Polar molecules: the Onsager cavity

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.3.5: Onsager Cavity: Cavity Field and Reaction Field
> A point dipole $\vec p$ sits at the centre of an empty spherical cavity of radius $a$ in an unbounded simple dielectric $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$, and far away the field is the uniform macroscopic field $\vec E_0 \parallel \vec p$ (Zangwill Example 7.2; PHY 505, HW 11, Problem 4). With $\theta$ from $\vec E_0$,
>
> $$
> V = \begin{cases}\Bigl(Ar + \dfrac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\Bigr)\cos\theta & r \le a , \\[2mm] \Bigl(-E_0r + \dfrac{B}{r^2}\Bigr)\cos\theta & r \ge a , \end{cases}
> \qquad A = -\frac{3\varepsilon_r}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}E_0 - \frac{2p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3}\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}, \quad B = -a^3E_0\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{2\varepsilon_r + 1} + \frac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{3}{2\varepsilon_r + 1} .
> $$
>
> The field acting on the dipole (the total field minus the dipole's own field) is uniform in the cavity and equals
>
> $$
> \vec E(0) = \vec G + \vec R_{\text{f}}, \qquad \vec G = \frac{3\varepsilon_r}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}\vec E_0 \ \ \text{(cavity field)}, \qquad \vec R_{\text{f}} = \frac{2(\varepsilon_r - 1)}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}\,\frac{\vec p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3} \ \ \text{(reaction field)} .
> $$
>
> By superposition the vector formula holds for any angle between $\vec p$ and $\vec E_0$. The reaction field is always parallel to $\vec p$, so it exerts no torque on it.
>
> *Source: Zangwill Example 7.2 · PHY 505, HW 11, Problem 4 (supplementary; its stated solution is Zangwill p. 212) · Zangwill §6.6.3*

^thm-c5-3-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Sources and form.** Off the origin there is no free charge in the cavity or the medium, so $\nabla^2V = 0$ in both ([[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-2-2|Theorem §C5.2.2]], 1). Near the origin $V$ must contain the potential of the point dipole in vacuum, $p\cos\theta/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$. *Sense:* the point dipole is the distribution $\rho = -\vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3(\vec r)$ ([[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^mod-c3-1-2|Model §C3.1.2]]; [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]), and $p\cos\theta/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2) = -\vec p\cdot\nabla\bigl[1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r)\bigr]$ solves $\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V = \vec p\cdot\nabla\delta^3$ because $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\delta^3$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]) and derivatives commute with $\nabla^2$ in the sense of distributions; what remains in the cavity is harmonic and regular. Far away $V \to -E_0r\cos\theta$. Both sources are $\ell = 1$, and the $\ell \ne 1$ coefficients vanish exactly as in [[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^der-c5-2-3|Derivation §C5.2.3]], step 2 (with $\varepsilon_{\text{in}} = \varepsilon_0$, $\varepsilon_{\text{out}} = \varepsilon$). Hence the stated form, with $p' \equiv p/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ for brevity.
>
> **2. Continuity at $r = a$.** $Aa + p'/a^2 = -E_0a + B/a^2$, i.e.
>
> $$
> \frac{B}{a^3} = A + E_0 + \frac{p'}{a^3} .
> $$
>
> **3. Normal $D$ at $r = a$** (no free charge; vacuum inside): $\varepsilon_0\,\partial_rV_{\text{in}} = \varepsilon\,\partial_rV_{\text{out}}$, with $\partial_r(Ar + p'/r^2) = A - 2p'/r^3$ and $\partial_r(-E_0r + B/r^2) = -E_0 - 2B/r^3$:
>
> $$
> A - \frac{2p'}{a^3} = \varepsilon_r\Bigl(-E_0 - \frac{2B}{a^3}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **4. Solve.** Substitute step 2: $A - 2p'/a^3 = -\varepsilon_rE_0 - 2\varepsilon_r(A + E_0 + p'/a^3)$. Collect: $A(1 + 2\varepsilon_r) = -3\varepsilon_rE_0 + 2p'/a^3 - 2\varepsilon_rp'/a^3 = -3\varepsilon_rE_0 - 2(\varepsilon_r - 1)p'/a^3$, which is $A$. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{B}{a^3} = E_0\Bigl(1 - \frac{3\varepsilon_r}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}\Bigr) + \frac{p'}{a^3}\Bigl(1 - \frac{2(\varepsilon_r - 1)}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}\Bigr) = -E_0\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{2\varepsilon_r + 1} + \frac{p'}{a^3}\frac{3}{2\varepsilon_r + 1} ,
> $$
>
> which is $B$.
>
> **5. The field acting on the dipole.** Inside, $V_{\text{in}} = Az + p'\cos\theta/r^2$. The second term is the dipole's own potential; its field (including its contact term) is the dipole's self-field, which exerts no net force or torque on the dipole and is excluded from "the field that acts on it" (Zangwill: "minus the contribution from $\vec p$ itself"). The rest, $Az$, gives the uniform field $-A\hat z$, so $\vec E(0) = -A\hat z$: the two terms of the statement. For general directions, the problem is linear in $(\vec E_0, \vec p)$; the $\vec E_0$ part is [[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-2-3|Theorem §C5.2.3]] case 2 (along $\vec E_0$), and the $\vec p$ part is the problem with $E_0 = 0$, symmetric about $\vec p$ (along $\vec p$). ⚑ By-product: $\vec R_{\text{f}} \parallel \vec p$, so $\vec p\times\vec R_{\text{f}} = \vec 0$: the reaction field cannot orient the dipole → [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^rem-c5-3-3|Remark: Lorentz versus Onsager]].
>
> **6. Checks.** $\varepsilon_r = 1$: $\vec G = \vec E_0$, $\vec R_{\text{f}} = \vec 0$, $B = p'$ (a dipole in vacuum in a uniform field). $\vec p = \vec 0$: the empty cavity of Theorem §C5.2.3. Outside, the system's dipole moment is $4\pi\varepsilon_0\times$ the $p$-part of $B$, $\frac{3}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}\vec p$: screened, like [[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^ex-c5-2-3|Example §C5.2.3]] but with a different factor because the dipole now sits in vacuum.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - The field at the molecule splits into a part caused by $\vec E_0$ (cavity field, independent of $\vec p$) and a part caused by $\vec p$ (reaction of the polarized surroundings to the molecule itself).
> - Assumptions used: the molecule is a point dipole in a spherical vacuum cavity; the surroundings are a continuum with the macroscopic $\varepsilon_r$.

> [!remark] Remark: Lorentz versus Onsager
> The Lorentz local field $\frac{\varepsilon_r + 2}{3}\vec E$ grows without bound with $\varepsilon_r$; the Onsager cavity field $\frac{3\varepsilon_r}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}\vec E$ never exceeds $\frac32\vec E$ (figure). The difference is the reaction field. Lorentz's $\vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ counts the *average* polarization of the surroundings, and part of that average is induced by the molecule itself and turns with it; for a molecule with a permanent moment, that part cannot help align it. Onsager (1936) separated it out: only the cavity field orients a permanent dipole, while the reaction field, parallel to $\vec p$, merely lowers its energy whatever its direction. This is what Zangwill means by the permanent moment "dramatically" affecting the local field needed for $\varepsilon_r$ (§6.6.3). For induced moments nothing is lost: a polarizable molecule responds to $\vec G + \vec R_{\text{f}}$, and the two descriptions agree ([[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^rem-c5-3-4|★ Remark: Onsager's formula]], 1).

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Onsager's formula
> Two consequences of [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^thm-c5-3-5|Theorem §C5.3.5]], worked out here from the theorem and the linear Langevin response; the sources give neither in this form.
> 1. *Polarizable molecules give back Clausius–Mossotti.* Let the molecule be non-polar, with induced moment $\vec p = \alpha\varepsilon_0(\vec G + \vec R_{\text{f}})$, and let the cavity be the molecule's share of volume, $\frac43\pi a^3 = 1/n$, so $\frac{1}{4\pi a^3} = \frac{n}{3}$. Write $g = \frac{3\varepsilon_r}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}$, $r_{\text{f}} = \frac{2(\varepsilon_r - 1)}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}$, $y = n\alpha/3$. Then $\vec p = \alpha\varepsilon_0g\vec E/(1 - y\,r_{\text{f}})$, and $\vec P = n\vec p = \varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)\vec E$ requires $3yg = (\varepsilon_r - 1)(1 - y\,r_{\text{f}})$. Multiplying by $2\varepsilon_r + 1$: $9y\varepsilon_r = (\varepsilon_r - 1)(2\varepsilon_r + 1) - 2y(\varepsilon_r - 1)^2$, so $y\,[2\varepsilon_r^2 + 5\varepsilon_r + 2] = (\varepsilon_r - 1)(2\varepsilon_r + 1)$. Since $2\varepsilon_r^2 + 5\varepsilon_r + 2 = (2\varepsilon_r + 1)(\varepsilon_r + 2)$, $y = \frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 2}$: exactly [[§C5.3 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^thm-c5-3-4|Theorem §C5.3.4]].
> 2. *Rigid polar molecules.* Let each molecule carry a permanent $p_0$ and no polarizability. Its orientation energy in the cavity is $-\vec p\cdot\vec G$ plus an orientation-independent reaction term. For $p_0G \ll kT$ the Boltzmann average gives $\langle\vec p\rangle = \frac{p_0^2}{3kT}\vec G$ (the linear Langevin law, [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^rem-b5-3-6|§B5.3, ★ Remark]]; its quantum analogue for spins is [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-5|TH Theorem §B7.1.5]]). Then $\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)\vec E = n\langle\vec p\rangle = \frac{np_0^2}{3kT}\frac{3\varepsilon_r}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}\vec E$, i.e.
>
> $$
> \frac{(\varepsilon_r - 1)(2\varepsilon_r + 1)}{\varepsilon_r} = \frac{np_0^2}{\varepsilon_0kT} \qquad\text{(Onsager)}, \qquad \frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 2} = \frac{np_0^2}{9\varepsilon_0kT} \qquad\text{(Lorentz field instead of } \vec G) .
> $$
>
> 3. *Water* (illustrative values: $p_0 \approx 6.2\times10^{-30}$ C·m, $n \approx 3.3\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, $T = 293$ K): $np_0^2/\varepsilon_0kT \approx 35$. The Lorentz-field version demands $(\varepsilon_r - 1)/(\varepsilon_r + 2) \approx 3.9 > 1$, impossible for any $\varepsilon_r$: it predicts a polarization catastrophe, i.e. ferroelectric water. Onsager's version gives $2\varepsilon_r^2 - 36\varepsilon_r - 1 \approx 0$, $\varepsilon_r \approx 18$: finite and of the right order, though below the measured 80; correlations between neighbouring molecules, absent from a single dipole in a continuum, make up the rest.

> [!remark] Remark: Solids
> No simple formula predicts $\varepsilon_r$ for SiO₂ or Si. The reliable route is quantum perturbation theory: apply a uniform perturbing field, which it is convenient to call $\vec D$, compute the total microscopic field $\vec E_{\text{micro}}(\vec r)$ it produces, average over the (identical) cells of the crystal to get $\bar{\vec E}$, and read off $\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r = D/\bar E$. For the group-IV crystals C, Si, Ge, Sn the computed local field varies strongly within a cell, and as one goes down the column $\varepsilon_r$ increases while the intracell variation decreases, reflecting the spreading (delocalization) of the electronic wave functions (Zangwill Fig. 6.12, after Baldereschi, Car and Tosatti 1979). The limit of complete delocalization, $\varepsilon_r \to \infty$, is the perfect conductor ([[§C5.2 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^rem-c5-2-3|§C5.2, Remark: The limit ε_r → ∞]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.6.3–§6.6.4, Fig. 6.12*
