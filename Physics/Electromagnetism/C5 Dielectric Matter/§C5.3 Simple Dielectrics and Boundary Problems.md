What does a linear, isotropic medium do to the fields of the charges placed in it, and how are boundary problems with several such media solved? Level B is the home of the linear dielectric, its field equations, the uniqueness theorem with dielectrics and the dielectric sphere in a uniform field ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^def-b5-3-1|Def. §B5.3.1]], [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^mod-b5-3-2|Model §B5.3.2]], [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-3|Theorem §B5.3.3]], [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-4|Theorem §B5.3.4]]). This section adds Zangwill's reading of those results in terms of charges: every free charge is screened by the medium it sits in, and every other medium acts only through the polarization charge on the interfaces ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]]). It then solves the sphere problem once for any pair of media, which gives the spherical cavity needed in [[§C5.4 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant|§C5.4]], and works the course's boundary problems (HW 10).

*Notation* as in [[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization|§C5.1]]; dielectric constant $\varepsilon_r$ and susceptibility $\chi_e$ (Zangwill's $\kappa$ and $\chi$, caution below).

## Simple dielectrics

> [!caution] Caution: κ, ε_r, χ and χ_e
> - Zangwill writes $\kappa$ for the **dielectric constant** and $\chi$ for the **susceptibility**: $\vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi\vec E = \varepsilon_0(\kappa - 1)\vec E$, $\vec D = \kappa\varepsilon_0\vec E$ (eqs. (6.35)–(6.37)); the PHY 505 homework statements quote him. Levels B and C of this vault write $\varepsilon_r$ and $\chi_e$ (Griffiths' notation): $\kappa_{\text{Zangwill}} = \varepsilon_r = \varepsilon/\varepsilon_0$, $\chi_{\text{Zangwill}} = \chi_e = \varepsilon_r - 1$. Level A writes $\kappa$ as University Physics does, with a different *definition* ($E_0/E$ in a filled capacitor); that A–B difference has its home in [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^cau-b5-3-1|§B5.3, Caution: κ, ε_r, χ_e]].
> - Both books write $\epsilon$ for the permittivity, here $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$; formulas quoted from Zangwill below are converted, e.g. his $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 2)$ is $(\varepsilon_r - 1)/(\varepsilon_r + 2)$.
> - Not to be confused with the $\kappa$ of optics, the imaginary part of the complex index $\tilde n = n + i\kappa$ in Waves and Optics ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^cau-b8-1-1|WO §B8.1, Caution]]), or with Zangwill's polarizability $\alpha$, which is $\alpha_{\text{Griffiths}}/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§C5.4 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^cau-c5-4-1|§C5.4, Caution: Two polarizabilities]]).

^cau-c5-3-1

> [!model] Model §C5.3.1: Simple Dielectric
> A **simple** dielectric (Zangwill's term) is linear and isotropic: the constitutive relation of [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^def-c5-2-1|Def. §C5.2.1]] reduces to its first term with $\chi_{ij} = \chi_e\delta_{ij}$,
>
> $$
> \vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E = (\varepsilon - \varepsilon_0)\vec E, \qquad \vec D = \varepsilon\vec E = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r\vec E, \qquad \varepsilon_r = 1 + \chi_e \ge 1 ,
> $$
>
> pointwise, also for macroscopic fields that vary in space. Zangwill (and this chapter) further restricts to **piecewise simple** configurations: $\varepsilon$ constant in each of finitely many regions separated by sharp interfaces (Zangwill Fig. 6.7), plus conductors.
> - *Assumes:* the linear term of eq. (6.34) suffices (weak fields), isotropy, no spontaneous polarization; static fields.
> - *Why $\chi_e \ge 0$:* a statistical-mechanical argument (Zangwill footnote 8: Sanders, Am. J. Phys. 56, 448 (1988)); plausible because a dielectric *incompletely* screens an external field.
> - *Compared with level B:* the same constitutive law as [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^law-b5-3-1|Law §B5.3.1]] and [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^mod-b5-3-2|Model §B5.3.2]]; Waves and Optics uses it at one frequency ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|WO Model §B8.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.5, eqs. (6.35)–(6.37), footnote 8; §6.5.1*

^mod-c5-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.3.2: Screening and Interface Charge in Simple Dielectrics
> In a piecewise simple configuration ([[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^mod-c5-3-1|Model §C5.3.1]]):
> 1. *Field equations.* $\varepsilon\nabla\cdot\vec E + \vec E\cdot\nabla\varepsilon = \rho_f$ (Zangwill eq. (6.38)); in each region $\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V = -\rho_f/\varepsilon_r$, with $V$ continuous and $\varepsilon_{r2}\,\partial V_2/\partial n_2 - \varepsilon_{r1}\,\partial V_1/\partial n_2 = \sigma_f/\varepsilon_0$ at interfaces ($\hat n_2$ out of region 2; eqs. (6.69)–(6.71); home: [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-3|Theorem §B5.3.3]], 1–2).
> 2. *Screening.* Inside each region $\rho_P = (1/\varepsilon_r - 1)\rho_f$, so the total charge is $\rho = \rho_f/\varepsilon_r$ (eqs. (6.43), (6.45)); a free point charge $q$ is, macroscopically, a total point charge $q/\varepsilon_r$. At a conductor in contact with the medium, $\sigma = \sigma_f/\varepsilon_r$ (eq. (6.50)).
> 3. *Interface charge.* At an interface without free charge, with $\hat n$ from medium 1 to medium 2, $\sigma_P = \hat n\cdot(\vec P_1 - \vec P_2) = \varepsilon_0D_n\Bigl(\dfrac1{\varepsilon_2} - \dfrac1{\varepsilon_1}\Bigr) = \varepsilon_0(E_{2n} - E_{1n})$ (eq. (6.57)).
> 4. *Superposition.* $\vec E$ everywhere is the **vacuum** Coulomb field of the screened charges $\rho_f/\varepsilon_r$, of the interface charges $\sigma_P$ and of the net surface charges of conductors. A free charge's own contribution is screened by the $\varepsilon_r$ of the medium it sits in, at every observation point, in that medium or not; other media act only through interface charges.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.5.1–§6.5.3, §6.5.5, eqs. (6.38)–(6.57), (6.69)–(6.71)*

^thm-c5-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Field equation.** Insert $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$ into $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f$ ([[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]]) and use the product rule: $\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon\vec E) = \varepsilon\nabla\cdot\vec E + \vec E\cdot\nabla\varepsilon$. In a region of constant $\varepsilon$ the second term is absent; with $\vec E = -\nabla V$ this is $\varepsilon\nabla^2V = -\rho_f$, i.e. $\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V = -\rho_f/\varepsilon_r$. The matching conditions are $\hat n_2\cdot(\vec D_1 - \vec D_2) = \sigma_f$ ([[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^rem-c5-2-2|§C5.2, Remark: What carries over]]) with $\vec D_i = -\varepsilon_i\nabla V_i$, and continuity of $V$.
>
> **2. Screening.** In a region of constant $\chi_e$: $\rho_P = -\nabla\cdot\vec P = -\varepsilon_0\chi_e\nabla\cdot\vec E$, and $\varepsilon\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho_f$ from step 1, so
>
> $$
> \rho_P = -\frac{\varepsilon_0\chi_e}{\varepsilon}\rho_f = -\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r}\rho_f = \Bigl(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_r} - 1\Bigr)\rho_f, \qquad \rho = \rho_f + \rho_P = \frac{\rho_f}{\varepsilon_r} .
> $$
>
> For a point charge, $\rho_f = q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)$ is a distribution ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]); multiplying it by the constant $\chi_e/\varepsilon_r$ is always defined, so $\rho_P = (1/\varepsilon_r - 1)q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_0)$ sits at the same point. ⚑ By-product: macroscopically the polarization charge *coincides* with the free charge it screens; microscopically it is the nearby polarized matter drawn toward $q$ (Zangwill: a consequence of Lorentz averaging). At a conductor surface with outward normal $\hat n$ into the dielectric, $\sigma_f = D_n$ and the dielectric's face carries $\sigma_P = -P_n = -\varepsilon_0\chi_eE_n = -\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r}\sigma_f$; the two coincide macroscopically and sum to $\sigma_f/\varepsilon_r$ (eqs. (6.49)–(6.50)).
>
> **3. Interface charge.** Both media contribute, each with its own outward normal ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^def-c5-1-2|Def. §C5.1.2]]): $\sigma_P = \vec P_1\cdot\hat n + \vec P_2\cdot(-\hat n)$. With $\vec P_i = \vec D_i - \varepsilon_0\vec E_i$ and $D_{1n} = D_{2n} \equiv D_n$ (no free charge):
>
> $$
> \sigma_P = (D_n - \varepsilon_0E_{1n}) - (D_n - \varepsilon_0E_{2n}) = \varepsilon_0(E_{2n} - E_{1n}) = \varepsilon_0D_n\Bigl(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_2} - \frac{1}{\varepsilon_1}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **4. Superposition.** The total charge is $\rho_f + \rho_P$ in the volumes, $\sigma_P$ (and $\sigma_f$) on surfaces; by [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-1|§C5.2, Theorem §C5.2.1]] and Coulomb's law the field is the vacuum field of these charges. By step 2 the volume part is $\rho_f/\varepsilon_r$, region by region.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Screening is local: the medium in contact with a free charge reduces it to $q/\varepsilon_r$; nothing else about the medium enters except through interfaces.
> - Assumptions used: constant $\varepsilon$ in each region, sharp interfaces, no free charge on the interfaces in step 3.

> [!remark] Remark: Refraction of field lines
> The law of refraction at an interface without free charge, $\varepsilon_1\cot\alpha_1 = \varepsilon_2\cot\alpha_2$ with $\alpha_i$ the angle between $\vec E$ and the normal (Zangwill Application 6.2, eq. (6.68)), is level B's $\tan\alpha_2/\tan\alpha_1 = \varepsilon_2/\varepsilon_1$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-3|Theorem §B5.3.3]], 4), from $\varepsilon_1E_1\cos\alpha_1 = \varepsilon_2E_2\cos\alpha_2$ and $E_1\sin\alpha_1 = E_2\sin\alpha_2$ (eq. (6.67)). What Zangwill adds is the limit $\varepsilon_2 \gg \varepsilon_1$: both $\alpha_1 \approx 0$ (lines leave the high-permittivity medium normally, as from a conductor, $\vec E = \sigma\hat n/\varepsilon_0$) and $\alpha_2 \approx \pi/2$ (lines inside the high-permittivity medium run nearly parallel to the interface) satisfy the law. Which one Nature picks depends on the whole geometry, including the positions of the free charges.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.5.4, Application 6.2, eqs. (6.67)–(6.68)*

## Spheres and cavities in a uniform field

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.3.3: A Dielectric Sphere inside Another Dielectric, in a Uniform Field
> A sphere of radius $R$ and permittivity $\varepsilon_{\text{in}}$ sits in an unbounded medium of permittivity $\varepsilon_{\text{out}}$, without free charge, and far away the field is the uniform $\vec E_0 = E_0\hat z$. Then, with $\theta$ measured from $\hat z$,
>
> $$
> V_{\text{in}} = -\frac{3\varepsilon_{\text{out}}}{\varepsilon_{\text{in}} + 2\varepsilon_{\text{out}}}\,E_0\,r\cos\theta , \qquad V_{\text{out}} = \Bigl(-E_0r + \beta\,\frac{R^3E_0}{r^2}\Bigr)\cos\theta , \qquad \beta = \frac{\varepsilon_{\text{in}} - \varepsilon_{\text{out}}}{\varepsilon_{\text{in}} + 2\varepsilon_{\text{out}}} :
> $$
>
> the field inside is uniform, $\vec E_{\text{in}} = \frac{3\varepsilon_{\text{out}}}{\varepsilon_{\text{in}} + 2\varepsilon_{\text{out}}}\vec E_0$; outside it is $\vec E_0$ plus a dipole field; the interface carries $\sigma_P = 3\varepsilon_0\beta E_0\cos\theta$. Special cases:
> 1. dielectric sphere in vacuum ($\varepsilon_{\text{out}} = \varepsilon_0$): $\vec E_{\text{in}} = \frac{3}{\varepsilon_r + 2}\vec E_0$, level B's [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-4|Theorem §B5.3.4]];
> 2. **spherical cavity** in a dielectric ($\varepsilon_{\text{in}} = \varepsilon_0$, $\varepsilon_{\text{out}} = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$; Zangwill Example 6.3): $\vec E_{\text{in}} = \frac{3\varepsilon_r}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}\vec E_0$, larger than $\vec E_0$;
> 3. $\varepsilon_{\text{in}} \to \infty$: $\vec E_{\text{in}} \to \vec 0$, $\beta \to 1$, the conducting sphere ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.5.5, Example 6.3 (cavity) · general $\varepsilon_{\text{in}}$, $\varepsilon_{\text{out}}$ worked out here*

^thm-c5-3-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **1. Form of the solution.** There is no free charge, so $\nabla^2V = 0$ in each region ([[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-3-2|Theorem §C5.3.2]], 1). With azimuthal symmetry about $\hat z$ the general solution in each region is $\sum_\ell(A_\ell r^\ell + B_\ell r^{-\ell - 1})P_\ell(\cos\theta)$ ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-3|Theorem §B3.4.3]]; [[§C6.2 Separation in Cartesian and Spherical Coordinates#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]]). Regularity at $r = 0$ removes $r^{-\ell - 1}$ inside; $V \to -E_0r\cos\theta$ at infinity removes $r^\ell$ outside except the given $\ell = 1$ term. So $V_{\text{in}} = \sum a_\ell r^\ell P_\ell$, $V_{\text{out}} = -E_0r\cos\theta + \sum b_\ell r^{-\ell - 1}P_\ell$.
>
> **2. Matching, $\ell \ne 1$.** Continuity of $V$ and of $\varepsilon\,\partial_rV$ at $r = R$ ($\sigma_f = 0$), Legendre component by component (orthogonality, [[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-2|Theorem §B3.4.2]]): $a_\ell R^\ell = b_\ell R^{-\ell - 1}$ and $\varepsilon_{\text{in}}\ell a_\ell R^{\ell - 1} = -\varepsilon_{\text{out}}(\ell + 1)b_\ell R^{-\ell - 2}$. Multiply the second by $R$ and substitute the first: $a_\ell R^\ell[\varepsilon_{\text{in}}\ell + \varepsilon_{\text{out}}(\ell + 1)] = 0$; the bracket is positive, so $a_\ell = b_\ell = 0$.
>
> **3. Matching, $\ell = 1$.** Write $a_1 = A$, $b_1 = B$. Continuity: $AR = -E_0R + B/R^2$, i.e. $B/R^3 = A + E_0$. Normal $D$: $\varepsilon_{\text{in}}A = \varepsilon_{\text{out}}(-E_0 - 2B/R^3)$. Substituting $B/R^3$:
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_{\text{in}}A = -\varepsilon_{\text{out}}E_0 - 2\varepsilon_{\text{out}}(A + E_0) \;\Longrightarrow\; A(\varepsilon_{\text{in}} + 2\varepsilon_{\text{out}}) = -3\varepsilon_{\text{out}}E_0 \;\Longrightarrow\; A = -\frac{3\varepsilon_{\text{out}}E_0}{\varepsilon_{\text{in}} + 2\varepsilon_{\text{out}}} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \frac{B}{R^3} = E_0 - \frac{3\varepsilon_{\text{out}}E_0}{\varepsilon_{\text{in}} + 2\varepsilon_{\text{out}}} = \frac{\varepsilon_{\text{in}} - \varepsilon_{\text{out}}}{\varepsilon_{\text{in}} + 2\varepsilon_{\text{out}}}\,E_0 = \beta E_0 .
> $$
>
> **4. Uniqueness.** The potential found is continuous, harmonic in each region, satisfies the matching conditions and the condition at infinity; by uniqueness with dielectrics ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-3|Theorem §B5.3.3]], 5; [[§C6.1 Potential Theory and Uniqueness#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]) it is the solution.
>
> **5. Interface charge.** By [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-3-2|Theorem §C5.3.2]], 3 (normal $\hat r$ from in to out), $\sigma_P = \varepsilon_0(E_{\text{out},r} - E_{\text{in},r})$ at $r = R$. $E_{\text{out},r} = -\partial_rV_{\text{out}} = (E_0 + 2\beta E_0)\cos\theta$ and $E_{\text{in},r} = -A\cos\theta$, so
>
> $$
> \sigma_P = \varepsilon_0E_0\cos\theta\Bigl[1 + 2\beta - \frac{3\varepsilon_{\text{out}}}{\varepsilon_{\text{in}} + 2\varepsilon_{\text{out}}}\Bigr] = \varepsilon_0E_0\cos\theta\,\frac{3(\varepsilon_{\text{in}} - \varepsilon_{\text{out}})}{\varepsilon_{\text{in}} + 2\varepsilon_{\text{out}}} = 3\varepsilon_0\beta E_0\cos\theta .
> $$
>
> **6. Special cases.** Substitute: (1) $\varepsilon_{\text{out}} = \varepsilon_0$: $3\varepsilon_0/(\varepsilon + 2\varepsilon_0) = 3/(\varepsilon_r + 2)$. (2) $\varepsilon_{\text{in}} = \varepsilon_0$: $3\varepsilon/(\varepsilon_0 + 2\varepsilon) = 3\varepsilon_r/(1 + 2\varepsilon_r)$, and $B = -\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}E_0R^3$, Zangwill's $A$, $B$. (3) $\varepsilon_{\text{in}} \to \infty$: $A \to 0$, $\beta \to 1$.
>
> **What the derivation shows**
> - Only the $\ell$ of the applied field survives: a uniform field induces a uniform interior field and a pure dipole outside, for any pair of media.
> - Only the ratio $\varepsilon_{\text{in}}/\varepsilon_{\text{out}}$ matters; the cavity is the "inverse" sphere, with $\beta < 0$, so the surface charge has the opposite sign and *enhances* the field inside.

> [!remark] Remark: Cavity fields in a linear medium
> - In the spherical cavity the field $\frac{3\varepsilon_r}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}\vec E_0$ exceeds $\vec E_0$ because the cavity wall carries positive polarization charge on its upstream side and negative charge downstream (Zangwill Example 6.3). Zangwill's warning: the final $\sigma_P = \vec P\cdot\hat n$ is *not* the $\vec P_0\cdot\hat n$ of the uniform polarization before the cavity was cut, since the polarization near the cavity adjusts; only the $\cos\theta$ dependence, shared by both, was used.
> - *Pancake cavity* (Zangwill Problem 6.11): a thin $L \times L \times h$ cavity, $h \ll L$, with normal $\hat n$. Away from the rim the fields are uniform; tangential $\vec E$ and normal $\vec D$ are continuous across its faces, so $\vec E_{\text{cav}}\cdot\hat n = \varepsilon_r\vec E_0\cdot\hat n$ and $\vec E_{\text{cav}}\times\hat n = \vec E_0\times\hat n$:
>
> $$
> \vec E_{\text{cav}} = \vec E_0 + (\varepsilon_r - 1)(\hat n\cdot\vec E_0)\,\hat n = \vec E_0 + \Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0} - 1\Bigr)(\hat n\cdot\vec E_0)\,\hat n .
> $$
>
>   A needle cavity along $\vec E_0$ has $\vec E_{\text{cav}} = \vec E_0$ (tangential continuity only). These are the linear-medium versions of the cavity fields of [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^thm-c5-2-3|Theorem §C5.2.3]], where $\vec P$ was held fixed; here $\vec P$ near the cavity responds, which is why the sphere gives $\frac{3\varepsilon_r}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}\vec E_0$ rather than $\vec E_0 + \vec P/3\varepsilon_0 = \frac{\varepsilon_r + 2}{3}\vec E_0$. The two differ; the difference is the core of Onsager's correction to the local field ([[§C5.4 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^thm-c5-4-5|Theorem §C5.4.5]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill Example 6.3 and its last paragraph; Problem 6.11*

^rem-c5-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: The limit ε_r → ∞
> Since $\vec E = \vec D/\varepsilon$, a medium with $\varepsilon_r \to \infty$ has $\vec E \to \vec 0$ inside when exposed to a static external field, the defining property of a perfect conductor ([[§C4.1 Induced Charge, Screening and Thomson's Theorem#^mod-c4-1-1|Model §C4.1.1]]). The $\varepsilon_r \to \infty$ limit of a dielectric result therefore often reproduces the conductor result, a useful check: case 3 of [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-3-3|Theorem §C5.3.3]], and [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^ex-c5-3-5|Example §C5.3.5]] below. It is not guaranteed when free charge is in intimate contact with the dielectric: in a capacitor held at fixed potential and filled with the dielectric, $\vec E = V/d$ inside for every $\varepsilon_r$ ([[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^ex-c5-3-2|Example §C5.3.2]]), whereas a conductor filling the gap would short the plates.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.6.4*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C5.3.1: A Point Charge in a Sphere Embedded in Another Dielectric
> A point charge $q$ sits at the centre of a sphere of radius $R$ and permittivity $\varepsilon_1$, embedded in an infinite medium $\varepsilon_2$ (Zangwill Fig. 6.7). Spherical symmetry and Gauss's law for $\vec D$ give $\vec D = q\hat r/4\pi r^2$ everywhere, hence $\vec E_1 = q\hat r/4\pi\varepsilon_1r^2$, $\vec E_2 = q\hat r/4\pi\varepsilon_2r^2$ (eq. (6.53)). The "paradox": the screened charge $q/\varepsilon_{r1}$ produces $q\hat r/4\pi\varepsilon_1r^2$ at *every* point (eq. (6.54); [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-3-2|Theorem §C5.3.2]], 4), also in medium 2. Resolution: the interface carries, by Theorem §C5.3.2, 3 with $D_n = q/4\pi R^2$,
>
> $$
> \sigma_P = \frac{q}{4\pi R^2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{r2}} - \frac{1}{\varepsilon_{r1}}\Bigr) , \qquad \vec E_\sigma = \frac{4\pi R^2\sigma_P}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\hat r = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{r2}} - \frac{1}{\varepsilon_{r1}}\Bigr)\frac{\hat r}{r^2} \quad (r > R),
> $$
>
> and zero inside (a uniform spherical shell; eqs. (6.55)–(6.58)). The sum outside is $\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\bigl[\frac1{\varepsilon_{r1}} + \frac1{\varepsilon_{r2}} - \frac1{\varepsilon_{r1}}\bigr]\hat r = \frac{q\hat r}{4\pi\varepsilon_2r^2}$ (eq. (6.59)). The field outside "knows" $\varepsilon_2$ only through the charge on the interface.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.5.3, eqs. (6.53)–(6.59)*

^ex-c5-3-1

> [!example] Example §C5.3.2: A Filled Capacitor at Fixed Charge and at Fixed Potential
> A parallel-plate capacitor (area $A$, gap $d$, $C_0 = \varepsilon_0A/d$) is filled with a simple dielectric; the lower plate is positive and $\hat z$ points up.
> - *Fixed charge* (isolated, Zangwill §6.5.2): $\sigma_f = Q/A$ is fixed; a pillbox from inside the plate gives $\vec D = \sigma_f\hat z$, so $\vec E = \vec D/\varepsilon = \vec E_0/\varepsilon_r$ with $\vec E_0 = \sigma_f\hat z/\varepsilon_0$ (eq. (6.48)): **screened**. The dielectric's lower face carries $\sigma_P = -P_z = \frac{1 - \varepsilon_r}{\varepsilon_r}\sigma_f$ (eq. (6.49)), the net charge at the interface is $\sigma_f/\varepsilon_r$ (eq. (6.50); [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-3-2|Theorem §C5.3.2]], 2), $V = Ed = E_0d/\varepsilon_r$, and $C = Q/V = \varepsilon_rC_0$ (eq. (6.52)).
> - *Fixed potential* (battery, §6.5.4): $V = \int_0^d E\,dz$ is fixed, so $\vec E = \vec E_0 = (V/d)\hat z$ (eq. (6.63)): **not screened**. Now $\sigma_P = -\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)V/d$ (eq. (6.64)) and $\sigma_f = D_z = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rV/d$ (eq. (6.65)), so again $C = \sigma_fA/V = \varepsilon_rC_0$ (eq. (6.66)). The sum $\sigma_f + \sigma_P = \varepsilon_0V/d$ is exactly the vacuum plate charge: the battery pushes onto the plates just enough extra charge to cancel the polarization charge next to them. This is how Faraday measured dielectric constants, from the extra charge drawn onto a (spherical) capacitor when a dielectric was interposed; the SI unit of capacitance, the farad, honours this work (Zangwill).
>
> The screening of Example §C5.3.1 is thus not a property of the medium alone but of what is held fixed. Macroscopically $\sigma_f$ and $\sigma_P$ coincide; microscopically they do not, so the field right next to the plates exceeds the vacuum field and must be smaller somewhere inside the dielectric, the theme of the local field ([[§C5.4 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^thm-c5-4-3|Theorem §C5.4.3]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.5.2, eqs. (6.48)–(6.52); §6.5.4, eqs. (6.62)–(6.66)*

> [!example] Example §C5.3.3: A Point Dipole at the Centre of a Dielectric Sphere
> A point dipole $\vec p_0$ sits at the centre of a dielectric sphere of volume $\mathcal V$ (Zangwill Example 6.1). What dipole moment $\vec p_{\text{tot}}$ does the whole system show from outside? The dielectric contributes $\vec p = \int_{\mathcal V}\vec P\,d^3r = \varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)\int_{\mathcal V}\vec E\,d^3r$ ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-3|§C5.1, Theorem §C5.1.3]]). The volume integral of the field over a sphere containing all the charge is $-\vec p_{\text{tot}}/3\varepsilon_0$, whatever the arrangement (Zangwill Example 4.1; [[§C3.1 Cartesian Multipole Moments and Dipole Layers#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]]; level B: [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^rem-b4-2-1|§B4.2, Remark: The contact term]]). Hence
>
> $$
> \vec p_{\text{tot}} = \vec p_0 - \frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{3}\vec p_{\text{tot}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec p_{\text{tot}} = \frac{3}{\varepsilon_r + 2}\,\vec p_0 ,
> $$
>
> screened, since $\varepsilon_r \ge 1$. *Check by separation of variables* (axis along $\vec p_0$, $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$, radius $R$): $V_{\text{in}} = \bigl[\frac{p_0}{4\pi\varepsilon r^2} + ar\bigr]\cos\theta$, the dipole screened by its own medium plus a uniform reaction field ([[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-3-2|Theorem §C5.3.2]], 2), and $V_{\text{out}} = \frac{p_{\text{tot}}\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}$. Continuity at $R$: $\frac{p_0}{4\pi\varepsilon R^2} + aR = \frac{p_{\text{tot}}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2}$. Normal $D$: $\varepsilon\bigl(-\frac{2p_0}{4\pi\varepsilon R^3} + a\bigr) = -\frac{2p_{\text{tot}}}{4\pi R^3}$. The first gives $\varepsilon a = \frac{1}{4\pi R^3}\bigl(\varepsilon_rp_{\text{tot}} - p_0\bigr)$; inserted in the second, $-2p_0 + \varepsilon_rp_{\text{tot}} - p_0 = -2p_{\text{tot}}$, i.e. $p_{\text{tot}} = 3p_0/(\varepsilon_r + 2)$. The reaction field $a$ inside is the electrostatic core of Onsager's model ([[§C5.4 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^thm-c5-4-5|Theorem §C5.4.5]]), where the dipole sits in a cavity instead.
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.5, Example 6.1 · the separation-of-variables check worked out here*

^ex-c5-3-3

> [!example] Example §C5.3.4: A Point Charge at a Planar Interface between Two Dielectrics
> A point charge $q$ sits at $z = -d$ in medium L ($\varepsilon_{rL}$, $z < 0$); medium R ($\varepsilon_{rR}$) fills $z > 0$ (Zangwill Example 6.2). Find the polarization charge on $z = 0$ without images. At an interface point at distance $r = \sqrt{s^2 + d^2}$ from $q$ ($s^2 = x^2 + y^2$), the field just to the left and right has two sources ([[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-3-2|Theorem §C5.3.2]], 4): the screened charge $q/\varepsilon_{rL}$, whose $z$-component there is $\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{rL}r^2}\frac dr$, and the surface charge, which contributes $\mp\sigma_P/2\varepsilon_0$ from its immediate neighbourhood and only tangential field from farther away (the field just off a charged surface, Zangwill §3.4.2; [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]):
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_0E_{Lz} = \frac{qd}{4\pi\varepsilon_{rL}r^3} - \frac{\sigma_P}{2}, \qquad \varepsilon_0E_{Rz} = \frac{qd}{4\pi\varepsilon_{rL}r^3} + \frac{\sigma_P}{2} .
> $$
>
> No free charge on the interface: $\varepsilon_{rL}E_{Lz} = \varepsilon_{rR}E_{Rz}$ (eq. (6.31)). With $a = qd/4\pi\varepsilon_{rL}r^3$: $\varepsilon_{rL}(a - \sigma_P/2) = \varepsilon_{rR}(a + \sigma_P/2)$, so $\sigma_P(\varepsilon_{rL} + \varepsilon_{rR})/2 = a(\varepsilon_{rL} - \varepsilon_{rR})$:
>
> $$
> \sigma_P(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_{rL}}\,\frac{\varepsilon_{rL} - \varepsilon_{rR}}{\varepsilon_{rL} + \varepsilon_{rR}}\,\frac{qd}{(x^2 + y^2 + d^2)^{3/2}}, \qquad \int\sigma_P\,dx\,dy = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_{rL}}\,\frac{\varepsilon_{rL} - \varepsilon_{rR}}{\varepsilon_{rL} + \varepsilon_{rR}} ,
> $$
>
> using $\int_0^\infty 2\pi s\,ds\,d/(s^2 + d^2)^{3/2} = 2\pi$. With vacuum on the left ($\varepsilon_{rL} = 1$, $\varepsilon_{rR} = \varepsilon_r$) this is level B's $\sigma_b = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\chi_e}{\chi_e + 2}\frac{qd}{r^3}$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^ex-b5-3-1|Example §B5.3.1]]). The usual route is images ([[§C7.2 Dielectric Images, Image Dipoles and Image Series#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]); the force on $q$ is [[§C5.6 Forces in Dielectrics#^ex-c5-6-3|§C5.6, Example §C5.6.3]].
>
> *Source: Zangwill §6.5.3, Example 6.2*

> [!example] Example §C5.3.5: A Spherical Conductor in a Dielectric Shell
> PHY 505, HW 10, Problem 1 (Zangwill 6.14). A conducting sphere of radius $R_1$ is surrounded by a simple dielectric $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$ filling $R_1 < r < R_2$; vacuum outside.
>
> **(a) Conductor with charge $Q$.** Spherical symmetry and Gauss's law for $\vec D$: $\vec D = Q\hat r/4\pi r^2$ for $r > R_1$, so
>
> $$
> \vec E = \vec 0 \ (r < R_1), \qquad \vec E = \frac{Q\,\hat r}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rr^2} \ (R_1 < r < R_2), \qquad \vec E = \frac{Q\,\hat r}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2} \ (r > R_2) .
> $$
>
> Polarization charge: $\rho_P = 0$ in the shell (no free charge there; [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-3-2|Theorem §C5.3.2]], 2). On the inner face the outward normal of the dielectric is $-\hat r$: $\sigma_P(R_1) = -P_r = -\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)E_r = -\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r}\frac{Q}{4\pi R_1^2}$, total $-\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r}Q$. On the outer face $\sigma_P(R_2) = +P_r = \frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r}\frac{Q}{4\pi R_2^2}$, total $+\frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r}Q$. The total polarization charge is zero ([[§C5.1 Polarization Charge and the Polarization#^thm-c5-1-3|§C5.1, Theorem §C5.1.3]]), and the conductor's net charge seen by the shell is $Q/\varepsilon_r$ (Theorem §C5.3.2, 2).
>
> **(b) Grounded conductor in a uniform field $\vec E_0 = E_0\hat z$.** By the argument of [[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^der-c5-3-3|Derivation §C5.3.3]], steps 1–2, only $\ell = 1$ is forced; the $\ell = 0$ part, $a + b/r$ in the shell and $c/r$ outside (zero at infinity, the ground reference), must satisfy $a + b/R_1 = 0$ (grounded), $a + b/R_2 = c/R_2$ and $\varepsilon_rb = c$ (normal $D$), whence $b[(\varepsilon_r - 1)/R_2 + 1/R_1] = 0$ and $b = 0$: no monopole term. For $\ell = 1$, with $x = R_1^3/R_2^3$:
>
> $$
> V = \begin{cases} 0 & r \le R_1 , \\ A\Bigl(r - \dfrac{R_1^3}{r^2}\Bigr)\cos\theta & R_1 \le r \le R_2 , \\ \Bigl(-E_0r + \dfrac{C}{r^2}\Bigr)\cos\theta & r \ge R_2 , \end{cases}
> $$
>
> where the shell form already vanishes at $R_1$. At $R_2$, with $c = C/R_2^3$: continuity, $A(1 - x) = -E_0 + c$; normal $D$, $\varepsilon_r\,\partial_rV_{\text{shell}} = \partial_rV_{\text{out}}$, i.e. $\varepsilon_rA(1 + 2x) = -E_0 - 2c$. Eliminating $c = E_0 + A(1 - x)$: $A[\varepsilon_r(1 + 2x) + 2(1 - x)] = -3E_0$, so
>
> $$
> A = -\frac{3E_0}{\Delta}, \qquad \frac{C}{R_2^3} = E_0\,\frac{\varepsilon_r(1 + 2x) - (1 - x)}{\Delta}, \qquad \Delta = \varepsilon_r(1 + 2x) + 2(1 - x) .
> $$
>
> The free charge on the conductor is $\sigma_f = D_r(R_1) = -\varepsilon\,\partial_rV|_{R_1} = -\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rA(1 + 2R_1^3/R_1^3)\cos\theta = \frac{9\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rE_0}{\Delta}\cos\theta$. Its total is $\propto\int_0^\pi\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = 0$: **no charge is drawn up from ground**; the field only separates charge (top positive, bottom negative). The system's dipole moment is $p = 4\pi\varepsilon_0C$.
>
> *Checks.* $\varepsilon_r = 1$: $\Delta = 3$, $A = -E_0$, $C = E_0R_1^3$, $\sigma_f = 3\varepsilon_0E_0\cos\theta$, the bare grounded sphere ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]]). $R_1 \to 0$ ($x \to 0$): $A = -3E_0/(\varepsilon_r + 2)$, $C = \frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r + 2}R_2^3E_0$, the dielectric sphere ([[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^thm-c5-3-3|Theorem §C5.3.3]], 1). $\varepsilon_r \to \infty$: $A \to 0$ and $C \to E_0R_2^3$, a grounded conductor of radius $R_2$ ([[§C5.3 Simple Dielectrics and Boundary Problems#^rem-c5-3-3|Remark: The limit ε_r → ∞]]).
>
> *Source: Zangwill Problem 6.14 · PHY 505, HW 10, Problem 1*
