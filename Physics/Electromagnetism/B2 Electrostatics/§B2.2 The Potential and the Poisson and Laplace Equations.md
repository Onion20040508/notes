How can one scalar function carry the three components of the electrostatic field, and which equation determines it? Level A defined the potential as potential energy per unit charge ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]]), showed $V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B\vec E\cdot d\vec l$ and $\vec E = -\nabla V$ ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.4 Field from Potential#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]) and summed potentials of distributions ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]]). Level B defines $V$ from the field, so that its existence becomes a consequence of $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]); Gauss's law then becomes Poisson's equation, and the Coulomb potential is its solution for localized charge. The section ends with the conditions that $\vec E$ and $V$ satisfy across a charged surface, which turn Poisson's equation into boundary-value problems ([[· B3 Boundary-Value Problems|Chapter B3]]).

## The potential

> [!definition] Definition §B2.2.1: Electric Potential
> Fix a reference point $\mathcal O$. The **electric potential** of a static field $\vec E$ is (Griffiths eq. (2.21))
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = -\int_{\mathcal O}^{\vec r}\vec E\cdot d\vec l ,
> $$
>
> the line integral along any path from $\mathcal O$ to $\vec r$; it is well defined because the field's loop integrals vanish ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]). Its unit is the volt, $1\ \mathrm V = 1\ \mathrm{N\,m/C} = 1\ \mathrm{J/C}$. For charge confined to a finite region the standard reference is a point at infinity, $V(\infty) = 0$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.1–2.3.2, eq. (2.21)*

^def-b2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.1: The Field Is Minus the Gradient of the Potential
> For any two points (Griffiths eqs. (2.22)–(2.23)),
>
> $$
> V(\mathbf b) - V(\mathbf a) = -\int_{\mathbf a}^{\mathbf b}\vec E\cdot d\vec l, \qquad \vec E = -\nabla V .
> $$
>
> Moving the reference point from $\mathcal O$ to $\mathcal O'$ adds the constant $K = -\int_{\mathcal O'}^{\mathcal O}\vec E\cdot d\vec l$ to $V$ and changes neither potential differences nor $\vec E$. Potentials of separate sources add: $V = V_1 + V_2 + \dots$, an ordinary sum.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.1–2.3.2, eqs. (2.22)–(2.23)*

^thm-b2-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Split $\int_{\mathcal O}^{\mathbf b}$ at $\mathbf a$: $V(\mathbf b) - V(\mathbf a) = -\int_{\mathcal O}^{\mathbf b}\vec E\cdot d\vec l + \int_{\mathcal O}^{\mathbf a}\vec E\cdot d\vec l = -\int_{\mathbf a}^{\mathbf b}\vec E\cdot d\vec l$ (eq. (2.22)). The gradient theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]) writes the left side as $\int_{\mathbf a}^{\mathbf b}\nabla V\cdot d\vec l$; since the two line integrals agree for all endpoints and all paths, the integrands agree, $\nabla V = -\vec E$. With the new reference, $V'(\vec r) = -\int_{\mathcal O'}^{\mathcal O}\vec E\cdot d\vec l - \int_{\mathcal O}^{\vec r}\vec E\cdot d\vec l = K + V(\vec r)$, and $\nabla K = \vec 0$. Superposition: $\vec E = \sum\vec E_i$ and the line integral is linear.

^der-b2-2-1

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]], [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from level A
> - **Order of the definitions.** Level A defined $V$ as energy per unit charge and derived the line integral; here the line integral is the definition, and the energy interpretation is a theorem, [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]]. Defining $V$ from $\vec E$ makes clear that the potential exists *because* $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$; for the induced fields of electrodynamics no such $V$ exists ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^rem-a3-1-1|§A3.1, Remark: Only for static charges]]).
> - **Why one function suffices.** $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ says $\partial E_x/\partial y = \partial E_y/\partial x$, $\partial E_z/\partial y = \partial E_y/\partial z$, $\partial E_x/\partial z = \partial E_z/\partial x$: the three components are not independent, and $V$ is the one function that encodes them (Griffiths §2.3.2 (ii)).
> - **The name.** "Potential" is not potential energy; Griffiths calls the name a hideous misnomer. The connection is [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]].
> - **Where $V$ is nailed down.** Inside a charged spherical shell $\vec E = \vec 0$ but $V = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0R \ne 0$ ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]]): the potential at a point depends on the field all the way to the reference point. For distributions that extend to infinity the reference cannot be at infinity ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^cau-a3-3-1|§A3.3, Caution: When infinity cannot be the reference]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.2, Example 2.7*

^rem-b2-2-1

## Poisson's equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.2: Poisson's and Laplace's Equations
> The potential of a static charge density $\rho$ satisfies **Poisson's equation**, and in charge-free regions **Laplace's equation** (Griffiths eqs. (2.24)–(2.25)):
>
> $$
> \nabla^2V = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \nabla^2V = 0\ \ \text{where } \rho = 0 .
> $$
>
> The second field equation, $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$, imposes no further condition on $V$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.3, eqs. (2.24)–(2.25)*

^thm-b2-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert $\vec E = -\nabla V$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]) into Gauss's law ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]): $\rho/\varepsilon_0 = \nabla\cdot\vec E = -\nabla\cdot\nabla V = -\nabla^2V$. The curl equation becomes $\nabla\times(-\nabla V) = \vec 0$, an identity ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]): it was used up in making $V$ exist. One equation for one scalar replaces two equations for a vector.

^der-b2-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.3: Potential of a Localized Charge Distribution
> If the charge is confined to a finite region and $V(\infty) = 0$, then (Griffiths eqs. (2.26)–(2.30))
>
> $$
> V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\rho(\vec r\,')}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau' ,
> $$
>
> with $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\lambda}{\mathcal R}\,dl'$ and $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\sigma}{\mathcal R}\,da'$ for line and surface charge and $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_iq_i/\mathcal R_i$ for point charges. This $V$ solves Poisson's equation, and it is the only solution that vanishes at infinity.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.4, eqs. (2.26)–(2.30); Problem 2.29; App. B, eq. (B.11)*

^thm-b2-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For a point charge at the origin, integrate inward from infinity along a radius, where $\vec E\cdot d\vec l = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dr}{r^2}$: $V = -\int_\infty^r\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r'^2}\,dr' = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}$, and $r \to \mathcal R$ for a charge at $\vec r\,'$ (eq. (2.26)). Superposition gives the sums and integrals (eqs. (2.27)–(2.30)); this is [[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]] in the new notation. *Check* (Griffiths Problem 2.29): by [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]],
>
> $$
> \nabla^2V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\rho(\vec r\,')\,\nabla^2\frac{1}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau' = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\rho(\vec r\,')\,\bigl(-4\pi\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,')\bigr)\,d\tau' = -\frac{\rho(\vec r)}{\varepsilon_0} .
> $$
>
> *Uniqueness.* Two solutions that vanish at infinity differ by a harmonic function on all of space that vanishes at infinity, which is zero ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]; [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]). The formula is the Helmholtz construction (eq. (B.11)) applied to $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$.

^der-b2-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Three quantities, six relations
> The charge density, the field and the potential are linked in both directions (Griffiths Fig. 2.35):
>
> | from → to | formula | where |
> |---|---|---|
> | $\rho \to \vec E$ | $\vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\rho\,\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2\,d\tau'$ | [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-2\|Theorem §B2.1.2]] |
> | $\vec E \to \rho$ | $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ | [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3\|Theorem §B2.1.3]] |
> | $\rho \to V$ | $V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\rho/\mathcal R\,d\tau'$ | [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-3\|Theorem §B2.2.3]] |
> | $V \to \rho$ | $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$ | [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-2\|Theorem §B2.2.2]] |
> | $V \to \vec E$ | $\vec E = -\nabla V$ | [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1\|Theorem §B2.2.1]] |
> | $\vec E \to V$ | $V = -\int_{\mathcal O}^{\vec r}\vec E\cdot d\vec l$ | [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^def-b2-2-1\|Def. §B2.2.1]] |
>
> The integral formulas are useful only when all the charge is known. In most problems it is not: the charge on conductors arranges itself, and the task is to solve Laplace's equation with conditions on boundaries ([[· B3 Boundary-Value Problems|Chapter B3]]; Nelson ch. 2, §2.4). The kernel $1/4\pi\mathcal R$ is the Green function of the Laplacian ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^rem-b1-2-1|§B1.2, Remark: What the identities are for]]); the Green functions that build in conducting boundaries belong to Electromagnetism level C — [[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]], [[§C7.2 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^thm-c7-2-4|Theorem §C7.2.4]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.5, Fig. 2.35 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 2, §2.3–2.4*

## Boundary conditions

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.4: Boundary Conditions on the Electrostatic Field
> Let a surface carry surface charge density $\sigma$, let $\hat n$ be its unit normal pointing from the side called "below" to the side called "above", and let $\vec E$ be bounded near the surface on each side. Then, at each point of the surface (Griffiths eqs. (2.31)–(2.33)),
>
> $$
> E^\perp_{\text{above}} - E^\perp_{\text{below}} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \vec E^\parallel_{\text{above}} = \vec E^\parallel_{\text{below}}, \qquad\text{together}\qquad \vec E_{\text{above}} - \vec E_{\text{below}} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\,\hat n ,
> $$
>
> where $E^\perp = \vec E\cdot\hat n$, $\vec E^\parallel$ is the tangential part, and the fields are the limiting values on the two sides. The jump is produced entirely by the local patch of charge, whose own field is $\pm(\sigma/2\varepsilon_0)\hat n$ just above and below.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.5, eqs. (2.31)–(2.33), footnote 8*

^thm-b2-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Normal component.* Apply Gauss's law in integral form ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]) to a pillbox of lid area $A$ straddling the surface, so small that $\sigma$ and the fields are nearly constant over it. As the thickness $\epsilon \to 0$ the side wall contributes nothing (bounded field, vanishing area) and any volume charge inside contributes nothing, so $(E^\perp_{\text{above}} - E^\perp_{\text{below}})A = \sigma A/\varepsilon_0$.
>
> *Tangential component.* Apply $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]) to a thin rectangle crossing the surface, with long sides of length $l$ parallel to a tangent direction. As its width $\epsilon \to 0$ the ends contribute nothing, leaving $(E^\parallel_{\text{above}} - E^\parallel_{\text{below}})\,l = 0$ for every tangent direction.
>
> *The local patch.* Close enough, a small patch looks like an infinite plane, whose field is $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ on either side, pointing away from it ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-9|Theorem §A1.4.9]]); the field of all other charges is continuous at the patch. The difference of the two sides is therefore the patch's $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$.

^der-b2-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]], [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-9|Theorem §A1.4.9]]

> [!remark] Remark: Where these conditions come from and where they go
> - Level A noticed the rule case by case: across every charged shell, cylinder and sheet the normal field jumps by $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^rem-a2-3-3|§A2.3, Remark: Continuity checks]]), and the surface of a conductor has $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ outside and $0$ inside ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^cau-a2-4-1|§A2.4, Caution: σ/ε₀ or σ/2ε₀?]]). Theorem §B2.2.4 is the general statement; the conductor is the case $\vec E_{\text{below}} = \vec 0$ ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-1|Theorem §B2.4.1]]).
> - The same pillbox and loop, applied to the macroscopic Maxwell equations with $\vec D$ and $\vec H$, give the electromagnetic boundary conditions at an interface between media ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-1|WO Theorem §B9.1.1]]); the static version in matter, with $\vec D$, is [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]], and the general one [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations|§B8.4]].

^rem-b2-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.5: Boundary Conditions on the Potential
> Under the hypotheses of [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], the potential is continuous across the surface and its normal derivative jumps (Griffiths eqs. (2.34)–(2.37)):
>
> $$
> V_{\text{above}} = V_{\text{below}}, \qquad \frac{\partial V_{\text{above}}}{\partial n} - \frac{\partial V_{\text{below}}}{\partial n} = -\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \frac{\partial V}{\partial n} = \nabla V\cdot\hat n .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.5, eqs. (2.34)–(2.37)*

^thm-b2-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Join a point just below to a point just above by a path of length $\ell$ across the surface: $V_{\text{above}} - V_{\text{below}} = -\int\vec E\cdot d\vec l$, at most $\ell$ times a bound on $|\vec E|$, which tends to zero with $\ell$. Since $\vec E = -\nabla V$ on each side ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]), the field jump becomes $\nabla V_{\text{above}} - \nabla V_{\text{below}} = -(\sigma/\varepsilon_0)\hat n$ (eq. (2.35)), whose normal component is the stated condition. The tangential derivatives agree, as they must for a function that is continuous along the surface.

^der-b2-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]

> [!caution] Caution: When the potential does jump
> Continuity of $V$ needs a bounded field near the surface. A **dipole layer**, two sheets of charge $\pm\sigma$ a distance $\ell$ apart, has the field $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ between them, so $V$ changes by $\sigma\ell/\varepsilon_0$ across the pair. In the limit $\ell \to 0$ with $\sigma\ell$ fixed the field inside becomes unbounded, and the potential jumps by $\sigma\ell/\varepsilon_0$ across a surface that carries no net charge. Contact potentials between different metals, and the surface dipole layers of matter, are of this kind ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge|§B5.1]] for bound charge); Theorem §B2.2.5 assumes there are none.
>
> *Source: worked out here from [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-10|Theorem §A1.4.10]]*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.2.1: Which Field Can Be Electrostatic?
> With a constant $k$ of suitable units, consider (1) $\vec E = k\,[xy\,\hat x + 2yz\,\hat y + 3xz\,\hat z]$ and (2) $\vec E = k\,[y^2\,\hat x + (2xy + z^2)\,\hat y + 2yz\,\hat z]$. One cannot be the field of static charges.
>
> A static field must have zero curl ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]), and by [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]] every curl-free field on all of space is $-\nabla V$, so zero curl is also sufficient:
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\vec E_{(1)} = k\,[-2y\,\hat x - 3z\,\hat y - x\,\hat z] \ne \vec 0, \qquad \nabla\times\vec E_{(2)} = k\,[(2z - 2z)\,\hat x + 0\,\hat y + (2y - 2y)\,\hat z] = \vec 0 .
> $$
>
> Field (1) is impossible. For (2), take the origin as reference ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]]) and the path $(0,0,0) \to (x,0,0) \to (x,0,z) \to (x,y,z)$. On the first leg $E_x(x', 0, 0) = 0$, on the second $E_z(x, 0, z') = 0$, and on the third
>
> $$
> V(x, y, z) = -\int_0^yE_y(x, y', z)\,dy' = -k\int_0^y(2xy' + z^2)\,dy' = -k\,(xy^2 + yz^2) .
> $$
>
> Check: $-\nabla V = k\,[y^2\,\hat x + (2xy + z^2)\,\hat y + 2yz\,\hat z] = \vec E_{(2)}$. The charge that produces it follows from Poisson's equation: $\rho = -\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V = 2k\varepsilon_0\,(x + y)$, which grows without bound, so this field is not that of a localized distribution and $V(\infty) = 0$ was not an option.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 2, Problem 5 ((Im)possible electrostatic field), checked against the instructor's solution (2023 numbering: HW 3, Problem 1) · Griffiths Problem 2.20; the charge density added here*

^ex-b2-2-1

> [!example] Example §B2.2.2: A Neutral Coaxial Cable
> A long coaxial cable has a core of radius $R_1$ with uniform volume charge density $\rho$ and a thin outer shield of radius $R_2$ with uniform surface charge $\sigma$, chosen so that the cable is neutral: per unit length $\pi R_1^2\rho + 2\pi R_2\sigma = 0$, so $\sigma = -\rho R_1^2/2R_2$.
>
> *Field.* By cylindrical symmetry and Gauss's law on coaxial cylinders ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-3|Theorem §A2.3.3]]), $\vec E = E_s(s)\,\hat s$ with
>
> $$
> E_s = \frac{\rho s}{2\varepsilon_0}\ (s < R_1), \qquad E_s = \frac{\rho R_1^2}{2\varepsilon_0 s}\ (R_1 < s < R_2), \qquad E_s = 0\ (s > R_2) .
> $$
>
> *Potential.* Because the cable is neutral the field vanishes outside, so the whole exterior is at one potential and can be the reference, $V = 0$ for $s \ge R_2$; infinity could not be used for a charged infinite cable ([[§A3.3 Potential of Charge Distributions#^ex-a3-3-3|Example §A3.3.3]]). Integrating $-E_s$ inward from $R_2$, region by region,
>
> $$
> V = \frac{\rho R_1^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{R_2}{s}\quad(R_1 \le s \le R_2), \qquad V = \frac{\rho R_1^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{R_2}{R_1} + \frac{\rho}{4\varepsilon_0}\bigl(R_1^2 - s^2\bigr)\quad(s \le R_1) .
> $$
>
> *Boundary conditions.* At $s = R_1$ there is no surface charge, and indeed both $E_s$ and $V$ are continuous ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]); $V$ even has a continuous slope. At $s = R_2$, with $\hat n = \hat s$, $E_s$ jumps from $\rho R_1^2/2\varepsilon_0R_2$ to $0$, a jump of $-\rho R_1^2/2\varepsilon_0R_2 = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$, while $V$ is continuous with a kink: $\partial V/\partial s$ jumps by $-\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]]). Inside the core, $\nabla^2V = \frac1s\frac{d}{ds}\bigl(s\,\frac{dV}{ds}\bigr) = \frac1s\frac{d}{ds}\bigl(-\frac{\rho s^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\bigr) = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$, Poisson's equation; in the gap $\ln s$ is harmonic.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 3, Problem 2 (Coaxial cable), checked against the instructor's solution · Griffiths Problems 2.16, 2.24*

> [!example] Example §B2.2.3: A Screened Point Charge
> The potential of some configuration is $V(\vec r) = A\,e^{-\lambda r}/r$, with constants $A$ and $\lambda > 0$. Find the field, the charge density and the total charge.
>
> *Field.* By the spherical gradient, $\vec E = -\frac{dV}{dr}\hat r = A\,(1 + \lambda r)\,\frac{e^{-\lambda r}}{r^2}\,\hat r$.
>
> *Charge density.* Write $V = A\,e^{-\lambda r}\cdot\frac1r$ and use the product rule $\nabla^2(fg) = f\nabla^2g + 2\nabla f\cdot\nabla g + g\nabla^2f$. With $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec r)$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]) and $e^{-\lambda\cdot 0} = 1$ at the only place the delta function acts, the singular part is $-4\pi A\,\delta^3(\vec r)$; away from the origin the radial formula $\nabla^2V = \frac1r\frac{d^2}{dr^2}(rV) = \frac{A}{r}\frac{d^2}{dr^2}e^{-\lambda r}$ gives $A\lambda^2e^{-\lambda r}/r$. Poisson's equation ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]) then gives
>
> $$
> \rho = -\varepsilon_0\nabla^2V = \varepsilon_0A\Bigl[4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r) - \lambda^2\,\frac{e^{-\lambda r}}{r}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Total charge.* $Q = \varepsilon_0A\bigl[4\pi - \lambda^2\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-\lambda r}}{r}\,4\pi r^2\,dr\bigr] = \varepsilon_0A\,[4\pi - 4\pi] = 0$.
>
> A point charge $q = 4\pi\varepsilon_0A$ sits at the origin inside a cloud of opposite charge that exactly neutralizes it; at distances $r \gg 1/\lambda$ the field is exponentially small. This is the screened (Yukawa or Debye) potential of a charge in an electrolyte or a plasma, with $1/\lambda$ the screening length. Without the delta function, Poisson's equation would have returned a negative cloud and no charge for it to screen.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.50 (solved here; the charge density agrees with the answer printed there)*
