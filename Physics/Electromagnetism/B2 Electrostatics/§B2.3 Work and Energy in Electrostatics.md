*Sources: Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (4th ed.), §2.4 · PHY 422 (Thamm, Spring 2024).*

How much energy is stored in a static charge distribution, and where is it? Level A computed the potential energy of point charges as a sum over pairs ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-3|Theorem §A3.1.3]]) and the energy of a charged capacitor ([[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]), and found in two cases that the latter equals $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ integrated over the field ([[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]), without a general proof. Level B proves the general formulas $W = \tfrac12\int\rho V\,d\tau = \tfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$, explains why the second is always positive while the pair sum can be negative (self-energy), and shows that energy, being quadratic in the fields, does not obey superposition.

> [!caution] Caution: Notation for energy
> Level A, following University Physics, writes $U$ for electric potential energy and $u_E$ for the energy density of the field ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]], [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]); level B, following Griffiths, writes $W$ for the energy of a configuration, meaning the work needed to assemble it, and $u$ for the energy density. The letter $W$ also still means the work done by an agent ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]]); for an assembly done slowly from infinity the two coincide. $U$ is kept for other uses at level B (the scalar of the Helmholtz theorem; later, mechanical potential energies).

## Work and the potential

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.1: Work to Move a Charge; Potential as Work per Charge
> Let fixed sources produce the potential $V$. The work an agent must do to carry a test charge $Q$ slowly (without giving it kinetic energy) from $\mathbf a$ to $\mathbf b$ is, along every path (Griffiths eqs. (2.38)–(2.39)),
>
> $$
> W = Q\,\bigl[V(\mathbf b) - V(\mathbf a)\bigr], \qquad\text{and from infinity}\qquad W = Q\,V(\vec r)\ \ \bigl(V(\infty) = 0\bigr) .
> $$
>
> So the potential difference is the work per unit charge, and $QV(\vec r)$ is the potential energy of $Q$ in the field of the sources: the potential of [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]] is the potential of [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.4.1, eqs. (2.38)–(2.39)*

^thm-b2-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The field exerts $Q\vec E$ on the charge; to move it without acceleration the agent exerts $-Q\vec E$ (any extra force would go into kinetic energy). Its work is $W = \int_{\mathbf a}^{\mathbf b}(-Q\vec E)\cdot d\vec l = -Q\int_{\mathbf a}^{\mathbf b}\vec E\cdot d\vec l = Q[V(\mathbf b) - V(\mathbf a)]$ by [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], independent of the path. With $\mathbf a$ at infinity and $V(\infty) = 0$ this is $QV(\vec r)$; it is the change of the potential energy of [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]], the work done by the field being $-W$.

## The energy of a charge distribution

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.2: Energy of a Set of Point Charges
> The work needed to assemble point charges $q_1, \dots, q_n$ from infinite separation is (Griffiths eqs. (2.40)–(2.42))
>
> $$
> W = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j>i}\frac{q_iq_j}{\mathcal R_{ij}} = \frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j\ne i}\frac{q_iq_j}{\mathcal R_{ij}} = \frac12\sum_{i=1}^nq_i\,V(\vec r_i) ,
> $$
>
> where $\mathcal R_{ij}$ is the distance between $q_i$ and $q_j$ and $V(\vec r_i)$ is the potential at $q_i$ due to **all the other** charges. It does not depend on the order of assembly, and it is also the work recovered when the system is dismantled.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.4.2, eqs. (2.40)–(2.42)*

^thm-b2-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The pair sum is [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-3|Theorem §A3.1.3]], obtained by bringing the charges in one at a time and applying [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]] to each with the potential of those already in place. Counting each pair twice and halving gives the double sum over $j \ne i$, which is symmetric in the charges and so independent of the order. Pulling out $q_i$, the inner sum $\sum_{j\ne i}q_j/4\pi\varepsilon_0\mathcal R_{ij}$ is the potential at $\vec r_i$ of all the other charges, now all of them rather than those present at some stage of the assembly.

^der-b2-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-3|Theorem §A3.1.3]], [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]], [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.3: Energy of a Continuous Charge Distribution
> A localized, bounded charge density $\rho$ with potential $V$ ($V(\infty) = 0$) and field $\vec E$ stores the energy (Griffiths eqs. (2.43)–(2.45))
>
> $$
> W = \frac12\int\rho V\,d\tau = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\Bigl(\int_{\mathcal V}E^2\,d\tau + \oint_{\mathcal S}V\vec E\cdot d\vec a\Bigr) = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int_{\text{all space}}E^2\,d\tau ,
> $$
>
> where $\mathcal V$ is any volume containing all the charge and $\mathcal S$ its surface. Surface and line charges contribute $\tfrac12\int\sigma V\,da$ and $\tfrac12\int\lambda V\,dl$ to the first form.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.4.3, eqs. (2.43)–(2.45)*

^thm-b2-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *First form.* Cut the distribution into elements $\rho\,d\tau$; the sum of [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-2|Theorem §B2.3.2]] becomes $\tfrac12\int\rho V\,d\tau$ (eq. (2.43)). The potential of "all the other charges" at a point is now the full potential, because the charge of an infinitesimal element contributes nothing to the potential at its own location when $\rho$ is bounded: a ball of radius $b$ around the point contributes at most $\rho_{\max}\int_0^b4\pi\mathcal R^2\,d\mathcal R/4\pi\varepsilon_0\mathcal R = \rho_{\max}b^2/2\varepsilon_0 \to 0$.
>
> *Second form.* Gauss's law gives $\rho = \varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]). Integrating by parts ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]) over a volume $\mathcal V$ that contains all the charge (enlarging the region adds nothing, since $\rho = 0$ there),
>
> $$
> W = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int_{\mathcal V}(\nabla\cdot\vec E)\,V\,d\tau = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\Bigl[-\int_{\mathcal V}\vec E\cdot\nabla V\,d\tau + \oint_{\mathcal S}V\vec E\cdot d\vec a\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> and $-\nabla V = \vec E$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]) turns the volume term into $\int E^2\,d\tau$ (eq. (2.44)).
>
> *Third form.* As $\mathcal V$ grows, the volume term can only increase and the surface term must decrease to compensate. On a sphere of radius $R$ far from the charges, $V \sim 1/R$, $E \sim 1/R^2$ and the area $\sim R^2$, so the surface term falls off like $1/R$; in the limit only $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$ over all space remains (eq. (2.45)).

*Uses:* [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-2|Theorem §B2.3.2]], [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]

> [!definition] Definition §B2.3.1: Energy Density of the Electrostatic Field
> The **energy density** of an electrostatic field is (Griffiths eq. (2.46))
>
> $$
> u = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2 ,
> $$
>
> the energy per unit volume when the energy of [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]] is ascribed to the field, so that $W = \int u\,d\tau$. Level A writes $u_E$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.4.4 (ii), eq. (2.46)*

^def-b2-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from level A, and where the energy is
> - **Layer.** $u_E = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ was a statement checked for the parallel-plate and spherical capacitors ([[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]], whose derivation said that it "is not derived in general" at that level); [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]] derives it for every localized distribution, from Gauss's law and an integration by parts.
> - **Where is the energy?** $\tfrac12\int\rho V\,d\tau$ integrates over the charge, $\tfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$ over the field, and the regions can be disjoint: for a charged spherical shell the charge sits on the surface while the field fills the outside. In electrostatics the question has no answer, since only the total is measurable; locating the energy in the field, with density $u$, is a choice of bookkeeping ([[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^rem-a4-3-2|§A4.3, Remark: Two pictures of the same energy]]). It becomes compulsory when fields carry energy away from their sources, in radiation ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem|§B9.1]]), and, Griffiths notes, in general relativity, where energy density is a source of gravity.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.4.4 (ii)*

^rem-b2-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Always positive, or not? Self-energy
> The field form gives $W \ge 0$ for every distribution, yet the pair sum of [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-2|Theorem §B2.3.2]] is negative for two opposite charges, $-q^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0\mathcal R$. Both are right; they answer different questions. The pair sum counts only the work of bringing ready-made point charges together. The field form also counts the work of making each charge, which for a point charge is infinite:
>
> $$
> W_{\text{self}} = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int\Bigl(\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\Bigr)^2 4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^\infty\frac{dr}{r^2} = \infty .
> $$
>
> The slip between the two lies in replacing "the potential of the other charges" by the full potential: harmless for a bounded density, fatal at a point charge. With point charges one uses the pair sum and leaves out the (constant, unobservable) self-energies. The infinite self-energy of a point charge is a lasting embarrassment of classical and quantum electrodynamics; it returns with radiation reaction ([[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-7|Theorem §B11.4.7]], [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^rem-b11-4-3|Remark: What the dumbbell does and does not settle]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.4.4 (i)*

^rem-b2-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.4: Energy Does Not Superpose
> If $\vec E_1$ and $\vec E_2$ are the fields of two localized distributions $\rho_1$, $\rho_2$ with potentials $V_1$, $V_2$, the energy of the combined distribution is (Griffiths eq. (2.47))
>
> $$
> W_{\text{tot}} = W_1 + W_2 + W_{\text{int}}, \qquad W_{\text{int}} = \varepsilon_0\int\vec E_1\cdot\vec E_2\,d\tau = \int\rho_1V_2\,d\tau = \int\rho_2V_1\,d\tau .
> $$
>
> The interaction energy $W_{\text{int}}$ is the work of bringing the two finished distributions together from infinite separation; it is symmetric (the energy of $\rho_1$ in the potential of $\rho_2$ equals that of $\rho_2$ in the potential of $\rho_1$). Doubling all charges quadruples the energy.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.4.4 (iii), eq. (2.47); Problem 2.37*

^thm-b2-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The fields superpose, $\vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2$ ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]), so $E^2 = E_1^2 + E_2^2 + 2\vec E_1\cdot\vec E_2$, and [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]] gives the three terms. For the cross term, write $\vec E_2 = -\nabla V_2$ and integrate by parts over all space as in Theorem §B2.3.3 (the surface term at infinity vanishes): $\varepsilon_0\int\vec E_1\cdot\vec E_2\,d\tau = \varepsilon_0\int V_2\,\nabla\cdot\vec E_1\,d\tau = \int\rho_1V_2\,d\tau$; exchanging the labels gives the last form. That $\int\rho_1V_2\,d\tau$ is the work to bring $\rho_1$ in from infinity against the fixed field of $\rho_2$ is [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]] applied to each element of $\rho_1$. Energy is quadratic in the charges, so a factor $\lambda$ on all charges multiplies it by $\lambda^2$.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.3.1: The Interaction Energy of Two Point Charges
> Put $q_1$ at the origin and $q_2$ at $a\hat z$, and compute the cross term $\varepsilon_0\int\vec E_1\cdot\vec E_2\,d\tau$ of [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-4|Theorem §B2.3.4]], which is finite although each self-energy is not.
>
> *Directly.* With $\vec E_1 = \frac{q_1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\hat r}{r^2}$ and $\vec E_2 = \frac{q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\vec r - a\hat z}{|\vec r - a\hat z|^3}$, $\hat r\cdot(\vec r - a\hat z) = r - a\cos\theta$, and in spherical coordinates with $u = \cos\theta$,
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_0\int\vec E_1\cdot\vec E_2\,d\tau = \frac{q_1q_2}{16\pi^2\varepsilon_0}\,2\pi\int_0^\infty dr\int_{-1}^1\frac{r - au}{(r^2 + a^2 - 2aru)^{3/2}}\,du .
> $$
>
> The $u$ integral is the one of [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^ex-b2-1-1|Example §B2.1.1]] with $z \to r$ and $R \to a$: it equals $2/r^2$ for $r > a$ and $0$ for $r < a$. Then $\int_a^\infty 2\,dr/r^2 = 2/a$, and
>
> $$
> W_{\text{int}} = \frac{q_1q_2}{16\pi^2\varepsilon_0}\cdot2\pi\cdot\frac2a = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q_1q_2}{a} .
> $$
>
> *Shortcut.* $W_{\text{int}} = \int\rho_1V_2\,d\tau$ with $\rho_1 = q_1\delta^3(\vec r)$ gives $q_1V_2(0) = q_1q_2/4\pi\varepsilon_0a$ at once.
>
> This is the pair energy of [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-2|Theorem §B2.3.2]]: the field form and the pair sum agree once the infinite but fixed self-energies are set aside. The cross term, unlike $E^2$, can be negative, which is how the field picture accommodates attraction.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.37 (solved here)*

^ex-b2-3-1

> [!example] Example §B2.3.2: A Shell That Feels No Force
> A spherical shell of radius $R_1$ carries charge $Q$, uniformly spread, and a concentric shell of radius $R_2 > R_1$ carries $-2Q$. Gauss's law gives $\vec E = \vec 0$ for $r < R_1$, $\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\hat r$ for $R_1 < r < R_2$ and $-\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\hat r$ for $r > R_2$. By [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]],
>
> $$
> W = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int_0^\infty E^2\,4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\int_{R_1}^{R_2}\frac{dr}{r^2} + \int_{R_2}^\infty\frac{dr}{r^2}\Bigr] = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_2}\Bigr] = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0R_1} .
> $$
>
> Check with $\tfrac12\int\sigma V\,da$: the shells sit at $V(R_1) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\bigl(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{2}{R_2}\bigr)$ and $V(R_2) = -\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_2}$, so $W = \tfrac12[QV(R_1) - 2QV(R_2)] = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\bigl[\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{2}{R_2} + \frac{2}{R_2}\bigr]$, the same.
>
> The energy does not depend on $R_2$. If the outer shell expands or contracts uniformly, with its charge fixed, no work is done, so the electric forces on it add up to zero radial force. Directly: the inner shell's attraction pulls the charge $-2Q$ inward, the outer shell's repulsion of its own charge pushes it outward, and the two pressures are both $2Q^2/16\pi^2\varepsilon_0R_2^4$; equivalently, the force per area on a charged surface, which uses the average of the fields on the two sides, vanishes because $\vec E$ just inside and just outside $R_2$ are equal and opposite ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]]). Energy derivatives as forces: [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-4|Theorem §B2.4.4]].
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 3, Problem 4 (Contracting charged shell), checked against the instructor's solution*

^ex-b2-3-2

> [!example] Example §B2.3.3: Taking a Capacitor Apart
> A conducting sphere of radius $a$ with charge $Q$ is centred inside a neutral conducting shell of inner radius $b$ and outer radius $c$. By [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-5|Theorem §A2.4.5]] the shell carries $-Q$ on its inner and $+Q$ on its outer surface, so $E = Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$ (radial) for $a < r < b$ and for $r > c$, and zero inside the metal. The stored energy is
>
> $$
> W_i = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\int_a^b\frac{dr}{r^2} + \int_c^\infty\frac{dr}{r^2}\Bigr] = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\frac1a - \frac1b + \frac1c\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Now the shell, made of two clamped halves, is unclamped and the halves are carried far away. They are neutral, so they end with no field and no energy of their own, and the sphere alone has $W_f = Q^2/8\pi\varepsilon_0a$. The work required is
>
> $$
> W_f - W_i = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\frac1b - \frac1c\Bigr) > 0 ,
> $$
>
> exactly the field energy that the metal had excluded from $b < r < c$: removing the shell lets the field fill that region, and filling it costs work. The induced charges attract the sphere's charge, so the halves must be pulled away.
>
> The same reasoning answers Griffiths Problem 2.60, a point charge $q$ at the centre of a neutral shell of radii $a < b$ carried out through a small hole: the work is $\frac{q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\bigl(\frac1a - \frac1b\bigr)$, which is not the answer printed with that problem in the 4th edition, $\frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac1a$.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 3, Problem 5 (Deconstructing a capacitor), checked against the instructor's solution (2023 numbering: HW 4, Problem 1) · Griffiths Problem 2.60*
