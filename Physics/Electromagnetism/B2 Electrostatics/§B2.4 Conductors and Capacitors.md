What do the field equations add to the level-A picture of conductors, and what forces do charges exert on the conductors that carry them? Level A established the properties of an ideal conductor in equilibrium ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^mod-a2-4-1|Model §A2.4.1]]): no field inside, charge only on the surface, the field $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ just outside, the charges induced in cavities, and a single potential throughout ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]]–[[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-5|Theorem §A2.4.5]], [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]); it then defined capacitance and the energy of a capacitor ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]). Level B restates the surface field as a boundary condition on $V$, the form used to solve boundary-value problems, and adds what level A lacked: the force on a surface charge, which for a conductor is an outward electrostatic pressure equal to the field's energy density, and forces computed as derivatives of the stored energy.

## Conductors at level B

> [!remark] Remark: The properties of a conductor, seen from level B
> For an ideal conductor in electrostatic equilibrium (Griffiths §2.5.1):
> 1. $\vec E = \vec 0$ inside: induced surface charges move until their field cancels the applied one in the metal, practically instantly ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]], [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^rem-a2-4-1|§A2.4, Remark: How the field gets cancelled]]).
> 2. $\rho = 0$ inside: at level B this is one line, $\rho = \varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]), a local form of [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]]. Charges of both signs are present; the net density vanishes.
> 3. Any net charge resides on the surface.
> 4. The conductor is an equipotential: $V(\mathbf b) - V(\mathbf a) = -\int_{\mathbf a}^{\mathbf b}\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$ along a path in the metal ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]).
> 5. Just outside, $\vec E$ is normal to the surface.
>
> In boundary-value language, a conductor is a surface on which $V$ is constant, and its charge distribution is whatever makes the field inside vanish. That there is exactly one such distribution, which level A assumed for the charges in a cavity ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^der-a2-4-5|§A2.4]]) and for the proportionality of charge and voltage in a capacitor ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^der-a4-1-1|§A4.1]]), is the second uniqueness theorem ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]); Griffiths: "electrostatics is very stingy with its options".
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.1–2.5.2*

^rem-b2-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why the charge goes to the surface
> Mutual repulsion spreads the charge out, but it is not obvious that it should all end on the surface rather than fill the volume. Energy settles it: free charges seek the configuration of least energy, and for a sphere of radius $R$ with charge $q$ the surface distribution stores $q^2/8\pi\varepsilon_0R$ ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^ex-b2-3-2|§B2.3]] with one shell; Griffiths Example 2.9) while the uniform volume distribution stores more, $3q^2/20\pi\varepsilon_0R$ (Griffiths Problem 2.34). The result depends on the dimension and on the exact inverse square: the charge on a conducting disk does not all go to its rim, nor that on a needle to its ends, and if Coulomb's law had an exponent other than 2 some charge would stay inside a conductor, which is the basis of the precision tests of [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^rem-a2-4-3|§A2.4, Remark: A precision test of Coulomb's law]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.1 (with footnote 10)*

^rem-b2-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.4.1: Field and Surface Charge at a Conductor
> Just outside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, with $\hat n$ the outward normal and $\partial V/\partial n = \nabla V\cdot\hat n$ the outward normal derivative (Griffiths eqs. (2.48)–(2.49)),
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\,\hat n, \qquad \sigma = -\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial n} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.3, eqs. (2.48)–(2.49)*

^thm-b2-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]] with "above" outside and "below" in the metal, where $\vec E_{\text{below}} = \vec 0$: $\vec E_{\text{above}} = (\sigma/\varepsilon_0)\hat n$. Equivalently, [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]] with $\nabla V = \vec 0$ in the metal gives $\partial V/\partial n = -\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ outside. The field formula is [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-4|Theorem §A2.4.4]]; the potential form is what boundary-value problems use: solve Laplace's equation with $V$ constant on the conductor, then read off the induced charge from the normal derivative ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images|§B3.2]]).

## The force on a surface charge

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.4.2: Force on a Surface Charge
> The electric force per unit area on a surface charge $\sigma$ is (Griffiths eq. (2.50))
>
> $$
> \vec f = \sigma\,\vec E_{\text{average}} = \frac{\sigma}{2}\bigl(\vec E_{\text{above}} + \vec E_{\text{below}}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the average of the fields on the two sides; the field is discontinuous at the surface ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]), and neither one-sided value is right.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.3, eq. (2.50), Fig. 2.50*

^thm-b2-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Single out a patch of the surface small enough to be flat with constant $\sigma$, and split the total field into the patch's own field and the field $\vec E_{\text{other}}$ of everything else. The patch exerts no net force on itself (a body cannot lift itself), so the force on it is $\sigma\vec E_{\text{other}}$ per area. $\vec E_{\text{other}}$ is continuous at the patch: removing the patch would leave a hole through which the other field passes smoothly. The patch's own field is $\pm(\sigma/2\varepsilon_0)\hat n$ just above and below ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-9|Theorem §A1.4.9]]), so $\vec E_{\text{above}} = \vec E_{\text{other}} + \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\hat n$ and $\vec E_{\text{below}} = \vec E_{\text{other}} - \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\hat n$; their average is $\vec E_{\text{other}}$. Averaging is a device for removing the patch's self-field.

^der-b2-4-2

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-9|Theorem §A1.4.9]], [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^def-a1-3-1|Def. §A1.3.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.4.3: Electrostatic Pressure on a Conductor
> The surface of a conductor carrying surface charge $\sigma$ feels the outward force per unit area (Griffiths eqs. (2.51)–(2.52))
>
> $$
> \vec f = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\,\hat n, \qquad\text{an electrostatic pressure}\qquad P = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\,E^2 ,
> $$
>
> with $E$ the field just outside. It points out of the conductor whatever the sign of $\sigma$: the field pulls the conductor's surface into the field region.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.3, eqs. (2.51)–(2.52)*

^thm-b2-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For a conductor $\vec E_{\text{below}} = \vec 0$ and $\vec E_{\text{above}} = (\sigma/\varepsilon_0)\hat n$ ([[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-1|Theorem §B2.4.1]]), so $\vec E_{\text{average}} = (\sigma/2\varepsilon_0)\hat n$ and [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]] gives $\vec f = (\sigma^2/2\varepsilon_0)\hat n$. With $\sigma = \varepsilon_0E$, $\sigma^2/2\varepsilon_0 = \varepsilon_0E^2/2$. *Energy check* (Griffiths Problem 2.44): let one plate of a parallel-plate capacitor with field $E$ between the plates move towards the other by $\epsilon$. The electric forces do work $PA\epsilon$; the field disappears from a volume $A\epsilon$ and with it the energy $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2A\epsilon$ ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]]). Energy conservation requires $P = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2$.

^der-b2-4-3

*Uses:* [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-1|Theorem §B2.4.1]], [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]], [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Pressure, energy density and tension in the field
> - The pressure on a conductor equals the energy density of the field just outside it. This is no coincidence: the field behaves as if it were under tension $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2$ along its field lines (Faraday's picture of field lines as stretched strings), and the field lines that end on the surface pull on it. The full statement is the Maxwell stress tensor ([[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^def-b9-2-1|Def. §B9.2.1]]).
> - For a parallel-plate capacitor the pressure on each plate is $\sigma^2/2\varepsilon_0$, so the plates attract with $Q^2/2\varepsilon_0A$, half of "charge times field between the plates": each plate feels only the field of the other, $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^cau-a2-4-1|§A2.4, Caution: σ/ε₀ or σ/2ε₀?]]).
> - Numbers: at the breakdown field of air, $E \approx 3\times10^6\ \mathrm{V/m}$, $P = \tfrac12(8.85\times10^{-12})(9\times10^{12})\ \mathrm{Pa} \approx 40\ \mathrm{Pa}$, about $4\times10^{-4}$ atmosphere. Electrostatic pressures on macroscopic conductors are small.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.3, Problems 2.41, 2.44*

## Capacitors and forces from energy

> [!remark] Remark: Capacitance at level B
> Griffiths defines capacitance as level A does, $C = Q/V$ with $V = V_+ - V_-$ the potential of the positive conductor less that of the negative one (eq. (2.53); [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]), and adds two points.
> - A **single conductor** has a capacitance too, with $V$ measured from infinity: the "second plate" is a sphere of infinite radius. For a sphere $C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R$ ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]]).
> - That $V$ is proportional to $Q$, so that $C$ depends on geometry alone ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]]), needs that doubling the charges doubles the density everywhere instead of rearranging it; the proof is uniqueness ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]).
>
> With the field energy of [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]], $W = Q^2/2C = \tfrac12CV^2$ (eq. (2.55); [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]) can be read backwards: compute $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$ and set $C = Q^2/2W$. For a coaxial capacitor with $\pm\lambda$ per length on radii $R_1 < R_2$, $E = \lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0s$ in the gap and the energy per length is $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int_{R_1}^{R_2}\bigl(\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0s}\bigr)^2 2\pi s\,ds = \frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{R_2}{R_1}$, so $C/L = 2\pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(R_2/R_1)$, as the potential difference gives ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-5|Theorem §A4.1.5]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.4, eqs. (2.53)–(2.55) · PHY 422, HW 4, Problem 1 (Cylindrical capacitor), checked against the instructor's solution*

^rem-b2-4-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.4.4: Forces from the Stored Energy
> Let the electrostatic energy $W$ of a system of conductors depend on a coordinate $x$ of one of its parts (a plate separation, a radius). The $x$ component of the electric force on that part is (Griffiths eqs. (4.59)–(4.60), (4.64), (4.66)–(4.67))
>
> $$
> F = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial W}{\partial x}\Bigr)_{Q}\quad\text{(charges held fixed)}, \qquad F = +\Bigl(\frac{\partial W}{\partial x}\Bigr)_{V}\quad\text{(potentials held fixed by batteries)} .
> $$
>
> For a capacitor both give $F = \tfrac12V^2\,dC/dx$: the force is fixed by the charge distribution and does not depend on what is held constant. The same holds with dielectrics present, where it gives the force that pulls a dielectric into a capacitor ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-6|Theorem §B5.3.6]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.4, eqs. (4.59)–(4.67)*

^thm-b2-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let an agent move the part slowly by $dx$, exerting $F_{\text{me}} = -F$ to balance the electric force. If the conductors are isolated, the only energy input is the agent's work, so $dW = F_{\text{me}}\,dx = -F\,dx$ (eq. (4.59)). If batteries hold the potentials, they also do work $V\,dQ$ moving charge onto the conductors: $dW = F_{\text{me}}\,dx + V\,dQ$ (eq. (4.66)). For a capacitor, $W = Q^2/2C$ at fixed $Q$ gives $F = \frac{Q^2}{2C^2}\frac{dC}{dx} = \frac12V^2\frac{dC}{dx}$ (eq. (4.64)); at fixed $V$, $W = \frac12CV^2$ and $dQ = V\,dC$, so $F = -\frac12V^2\frac{dC}{dx} + V^2\frac{dC}{dx} = \frac12V^2\frac{dC}{dx}$ (eq. (4.67)), the same. Using $W = \frac12CV^2$ with $V$ fixed but forgetting the battery gives the right magnitude with the wrong sign, a common error (Griffiths). *Check:* for parallel plates, $C = \varepsilon_0A/x$ gives $F = -\frac12V^2\varepsilon_0A/x^2 = -\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2A$, an attraction equal to the pressure of [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]] times the area.

^der-b2-4-4

*Uses:* [[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]], [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]], [[§A4.3 Energy Stored in a Capacitor#^cau-a4-3-1|§A4.3, Caution: Which formula when something changes]], [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]]

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Why macroscopic bodies are neutral
> Nelson turns the capacitor energy into an argument for bulk electroneutrality. A drop of radius $R$ carrying charge $Q$ on its surface stores $W = Q^2/2C = Q^2/8\pi\varepsilon_0R$. Remove one electron from 1% of the molecules of a water drop of radius $1\ \mathrm{mm}$: the drop contains $(10^3\ \mathrm{kg/m^3})(\tfrac43\pi\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m^3})/(0.018\ \mathrm{kg/mol})\times6.0\times10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}} \approx 1.4\times10^{20}$ molecules, so $Q/e \approx 1.4\times10^{18}$, and with $e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 2.3\times10^{-28}\ \mathrm{J\,m}$
>
> $$
> W = \Bigl(\frac{Q}{e}\Bigr)^2\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{2R} \approx \frac{(1.4\times10^{18})^2\,(2.3\times10^{-28}\ \mathrm{J\,m})}{2\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm m} \approx 2\times10^{11}\ \mathrm J ,
> $$
>
> some fifty tonnes of TNT. Ordinary matter therefore stays neutral to fantastic accuracy, and the charges that electrostatics deals with are tiny surpluses on enormous neutral backgrounds. The argument fails on the nanometre scale, where the energies are small enough for local charge separation (Nelson ch. 6, §6.5).
>
> *Source: Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 6, §6.2.2 (worked example), numbers checked here*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.4.1: A Charged Slab between Conducting Plates
> Two infinite, thin conducting plates at $z = \pm L$ each carry zero net charge, and the space between them is filled with uniform charge density $\rho$. Find the field, the induced charges and the force per area on each plate; then ground the plates.
>
> *Field between the plates.* Planar symmetry makes $\vec E = E_z(z)\,\hat z$, and the reflection $z \to -z$ maps the system to itself, so $E_z(-z) = -E_z(z)$ and $E_z(0) = 0$. A Gaussian pillbox from $0$ to $z$ gives $E_zA = \rho Az/\varepsilon_0$:
>
> $$
> E_z = \frac{\rho z}{\varepsilon_0} \qquad (|z| < L) .
> $$
>
> *Outside, neutral plates.* A pillbox from $0$ to beyond the top plate encloses $\rho AL$ plus the plate's zero net charge, so $E_z = \rho L/\varepsilon_0$ above and $-\rho L/\varepsilon_0$ below, as if the plates were absent. Inside the metal $\vec E = \vec 0$, and [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-1|Theorem §B2.4.1]] gives the induced charges: on the inner face of the top plate $\sigma = -\rho L$ (outward normal $-\hat z$, field $+\rho L/\varepsilon_0$), on its outer face $\sigma = +\rho L$. By [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]] each face feels an outward pressure $(\rho L)^2/2\varepsilon_0$, the inner face downwards and the outer face upwards: the net force on each plate is **zero**.
>
> *Grounded plates.* Now each plate is at the potential of infinity. A nonzero uniform field outside would make $V$ change without bound, so the field outside vanishes and the outer faces lose their charge; the inner faces keep $-\rho L$, since Gauss's law from the midplane into the metal still requires it. Each plate now carries net charge $-\rho L$ per area, drawn from the ground, and only the inner-face pressure remains:
>
> $$
> f = \frac{(\rho L)^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\quad\text{per unit area, each plate pulled towards the slab.}
> $$
>
> Check by the field of the others: at the top plate the slab produces $\rho L/\varepsilon_0$ upward and the bottom plate's $-\rho L$ produces $\rho L/2\varepsilon_0$ downward, a net $\rho L/2\varepsilon_0$ upward acting on $-\rho L$: force $-\rho^2L^2/2\varepsilon_0$ per area, downward.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 4, Problems 2 (Conducting plates) and 3(a) (Vignettes), checked against the instructor's solution*

> [!example] Example §B2.4.2: Point Charges and Conductors Attract
> (a) A charge $-2q$ sits at height $d$ above an infinite grounded conducting plane, and a charge $+q$ at height $3d$ on the same perpendicular. Find the force on $+q$.
>
> The charge induced on the plane acts on the region above it exactly like a set of image charges below it: $-Q$ at depth $h$ for each charge $Q$ at height $h$ ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]], justified by uniqueness, [[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]). Here the images are $+2q$ at $z = -d$ and $-q$ at $z = -3d$. The force on $+q$ at $z = 3d$, by Coulomb's law from the three other charges at distances $2d$, $4d$ and $6d$, is
>
> $$
> \vec F = \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0d^2}\Bigl[-\frac{2}{4} + \frac{2}{16} - \frac{1}{36}\Bigr]\hat z = -\frac{29}{72}\,\frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0d^2}\,\hat z ,
> $$
>
> towards the plane; without the conductor it would be $-\tfrac12$ instead of $-\tfrac{29}{72}$.
>
> *Check with the pressure.* For a single charge $q$ at height $d$ the method of images gives the induced density $\sigma(s) = -qd/2\pi(s^2 + d^2)^{3/2}$ ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]]). The pressure $\sigma^2/2\varepsilon_0$ of [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]] pulls the plane towards the charge with
>
> $$
> F_{\text{plane}} = \int_0^\infty\frac{\sigma^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\,2\pi s\,ds = \frac{q^2d^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^\infty\frac{s\,ds}{(s^2 + d^2)^3} = \frac{q^2d^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{1}{4d^4} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q^2}{(2d)^2} ,
> $$
>
> equal and opposite to the image force $q^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0(2d)^2$ on the charge: Newton's third law, with the conductor's share computed from the surface pressure.
>
> (b) A charge $q$ at distance $a$ from the centre of a **neutral** conducting sphere of radius $R$. A grounded sphere is represented by the image $q' = -qR/a$ at distance $R^2/a$ from the centre ([[§B3.2 The Method of Images#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]); a second image $q'' = -q' = +qR/a$ at the centre raises the sphere to a constant potential and restores neutrality. The force on $q$ is
>
> $$
> F = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\frac{q'}{(a - R^2/a)^2} + \frac{q''}{a^2}\Bigr] = -\frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{R^3\,(2a^2 - R^2)}{a^3\,(a^2 - R^2)^2} ,
> $$
>
> attractive for every $a > R$, and $\approx -2q^2R^3/4\pi\varepsilon_0a^5$ far away: the charge polarizes the sphere into an induced dipole proportional to $q/a^2$, whose field acts back with one more factor $1/a^3$ ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^ex-a1-5-1|Example §A1.5.1]] for the same mechanism at level A).
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 5, Problems 1 (Conductor force) and 2 (Spherical conductor force), checked against the instructor's solutions (2023 numbering: HW 4, Problems 3–4); the pressure check added here*

> [!example] Example §B2.4.3: Capacitor Compaction
> A conducting sphere of radius $R_1$ carries charge $Q$. The region $R_1 < r < R_2$ is vacuum, and all of space beyond $R_2$ is filled with a linear dielectric of permittivity $\varepsilon > \varepsilon_0$. Find the capacitance (relative to infinity) and the force on the dielectric's inner surface.
>
> *Field.* By symmetry and Gauss's law, $\vec E = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\hat r$ in the gap; in the dielectric the field of the free charge is reduced by $\kappa = \varepsilon/\varepsilon_0$, $\vec E = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon r^2}\hat r$ ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^law-a4-4-1|Law §A4.4.1]], [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^rem-a4-4-2|§A4.4, Remark: Permittivity]]; proved with Gauss's law for $\vec D$ in [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]]).
>
> *Capacitance.* Integrating in from infinity,
>
> $$
> V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon R_2} + \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\Bigr), \qquad \frac1C = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon R_2} + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_1} - \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_2} .
> $$
>
> *Force.* The charge is fixed, so by [[§B2.4 Conductors and Capacitors#^thm-b2-4-4|Theorem §B2.4.4]] with $W = Q^2/2C$ (valid for linear dielectrics, [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]) the total radial force on the inner surface of the dielectric is
>
> $$
> F = -\frac{\partial W}{\partial R_2} = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\varepsilon R_2^2} - \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0R_2^2}\Bigr) = -\frac{Q^2}{8\pi R_2^2}\,\frac{\varepsilon - \varepsilon_0}{\varepsilon\varepsilon_0} ,
> $$
>
> negative: inward, an inward pressure $P = \frac{|F|}{4\pi R_2^2} = \frac{Q^2(\varepsilon - \varepsilon_0)}{32\pi^2R_2^4\,\varepsilon\varepsilon_0}$ that would compact the dielectric onto the sphere. Moving the dielectric inward replaces vacuum in a strong field by dielectric, lowering $W$; the dielectric is drawn into the region of stronger field, as at level A ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^rem-a4-4-3|§A4.4, Remark: The dielectric is pulled in]]). For $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0$ the force vanishes, as it must when there is nothing there.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, Midterm 2, Problem 2 (Capacitor compaction), with the instructor's solutions (the statement is reconstructed from the typed solutions)*
