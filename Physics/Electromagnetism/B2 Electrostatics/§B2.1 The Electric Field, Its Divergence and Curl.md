Which differential equations does the electrostatic field obey, and how does one write the field of an arbitrary charge distribution in one formula? Level A built electrostatics from Coulomb's law and superposition ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|Principle §A1.2.2]], [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]), computed fields of point charges and continuous distributions as sums and integrals ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^thm-a1-3-1|Theorem §A1.3.1]], [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-2|Theorem §A1.4.2]]), proved Gauss's law by counting solid angles ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]) and showed that the field's loop integral vanishes ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]]). Level B writes all fields as one integral over source points with the separation vector, and turns the two integral laws into the local field equations $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ and $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$, proved for every distribution with the delta function of [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem|§B1.2]]. By the Helmholtz theorem these two equations determine the field.

## Source points, field points and Coulomb's law

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.1: Source Point, Field Point, Separation Vector
> In a field calculation the **source point** $\vec r\,'$ is the location of a charge (or of a charge element $dq$), and the **field point** $\vec r$ the point where the field is evaluated. The **separation vector** from source point to field point is (Griffiths eqs. (1.23)–(1.25), (2.2))
>
> $$
> \vec{\mathcal R} = \vec r - \vec r\,', \qquad \mathcal R = |\vec r - \vec r\,'|, \qquad \hat{\mathcal R} = \frac{\vec{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R} ;
> $$
>
> in Cartesian coordinates $\vec{\mathcal R} = (x - x')\hat x + (y - y')\hat y + (z - z')\hat z$. Primed coordinates and primed differentials ($dl'$, $da'$, $d\tau'$) refer to the source, unprimed ones to the field point. A charge element is $dq = \lambda\,dl'$, $\sigma\,da'$ or $\rho\,d\tau'$ for line, surface and volume charge ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^def-a1-4-1|Def. §A1.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.1.4, eqs. (1.23)–(1.28); §2.1.2–2.1.4*

^def-b2-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: The separation vector in practice
> - **Notation.** Griffiths writes the separation vector as a script letter r; these notes write $\vec{\mathcal R}$, $\mathcal R$, $\hat{\mathcal R}$. It is the vector that level A called "$r$, the distance from the source charge to the field point" with unit vector $\hat r$ "from the source toward the point" ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^cau-a1-3-1|§A1.3, Caution: What r means, and whose field]]). At level B, $\vec r$ and $r$ always mean the position of the field point and its distance from the origin, never the separation.
> - **Which variable is differentiated.** $\nabla$ acts on the field point $\vec r$ at fixed $\vec r\,'$; $\nabla'$ acts on $\vec r\,'$. On functions of $\vec{\mathcal R}$ the two differ by a sign ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]).
> - **Integrating.** $\hat{\mathcal R}$ changes direction with the source point, so it cannot be taken out of an integral over sources. Write it in Cartesian components (whose unit vectors are constant) before integrating, even when the integration itself is done in curvilinear coordinates (Griffiths, footnote to Example 2.2; [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^cau-b1-1-3|§B1.1, Caution: Curvilinear unit vectors depend on the point]]). This is the vector form of [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^cau-a1-4-1|§A1.4, Caution: Add components, not magnitudes]].

^cau-b2-1-1

> [!law] Law §B2.1.1: Coulomb's Law
> A point charge $q$ at rest at the source point $\vec r\,'$ exerts on a charge $Q$ at the field point $\vec r$ the force (Griffiths eq. (2.1))
>
> $$
> \vec F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{qQ}{\mathcal R^2}\,\hat{\mathcal R} ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec{\mathcal R} = \vec r - \vec r\,'$ ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]). Together with superposition ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]) it gives the force of any static distribution on $Q$.
>
> *Valid when:* the source charges are at rest (the test charge $Q$ may move); for moving sources the force depends on their velocities and accelerations at the retarded time ([[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge|§B11.2★]]). Level A states the same law as a principle ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|Principle §A1.2.2]]); the layer change is discussed below.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.1.1, eq. (2.1)*

^law-b2-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Coulomb's law and superposition at level B
> Coulomb's law ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^law-b2-1-1|Law §B2.1.1]]) and the superposition principle are the experimental starting point of electrostatics, as at level A ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|Principle §A1.2.2]], [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]). *Layer change:* superposition stays a principle, but Coulomb's law is at level B an empirical law with a stated range, as the Biot–Savart law is for magnetostatics ([[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law#^law-b6-2-2|Law §B6.2.2]]). *Valid when:* the source charges are at rest; the test charge $Q$ may move (Griffiths §2.1.1). For moving sources the force depends on their velocities and accelerations at the retarded time ([[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge|§B11.2★]]). Superposition is not a logical necessity: a force depending on the square of the source charge would violate it, and only experiment decides (Griffiths, footnote 1 of ch. 2). Everything else in electrostatics, apart from properties of matter, is derived from these two inputs (Griffiths §2.1.2); this note derives the field equations. The route can be reversed. Nelson starts from the static Maxwell equations, $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ and $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$, introduces the potential, and recovers Coulomb's law as the point-charge solution of Poisson's equation (Nelson ch. 2, §2.2–2.3); this is the route of electrodynamics, where Maxwell's equations become the principles ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations|§B8.4]]) and Coulomb's law a theorem about static charges.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.1.1–2.1.2 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 2, §2.1–2.3*

^rem-b2-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.2: Field of a Charge Distribution
> A static charge distribution of volume density $\rho$ produces the field (Griffiths eq. (2.8))
>
> $$
> \vec E(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\rho(\vec r\,')}{\mathcal R^2}\,\hat{\mathcal R}\,d\tau' ,
> $$
>
> and line and surface charges the fields $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\lambda(\vec r\,')}{\mathcal R^2}\hat{\mathcal R}\,dl'$ and $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\sigma(\vec r\,')}{\mathcal R^2}\hat{\mathcal R}\,da'$ (eqs. (2.6)–(2.7)). With the densities of [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]] (4), the volume formula covers all of them: point charges $q_i$ at $\vec r_i$, $\rho = \sum_iq_i\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_i)$, give $\vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_iq_i\hat{\mathcal R}_i/\mathcal R_i^2$ (eq. (2.4)). For bounded $\rho$ the integral converges also at field points inside the charge.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.1.3–2.1.4, eqs. (2.3)–(2.8)*

^thm-b2-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The field of the element $dq = \rho\,d\tau'$ at $\vec r\,'$ is that of a point charge, $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{\mathcal R^2}\hat{\mathcal R}$, and superposition adds the elements: this is [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-2|Theorem §A1.4.2]] in the new notation. Inserting $\rho = \sum q_i\delta^3(\vec r\,' - \vec r_i)$ and using the sifting property ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]) returns the sum over point charges. *Convergence.* Near the field point use spherical coordinates centred on it: $d\tau' = \mathcal R^2\,d\mathcal R\,d\Omega$, so the integrand is bounded by $|\rho|_{\max}\,d\mathcal R\,d\Omega$ and the neighbourhood contributes a finite amount, vanishing with its size. For line and surface charges this fails at points on the charge itself, where the field is indeed infinite or discontinuous.

## The field equations

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.3: Gauss's Law in Differential Form
> The electrostatic field of any charge distribution satisfies, at every point (Griffiths eqs. (2.14), (2.16)),
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} ,
> $$
>
> with point, line and surface charges included as delta-function densities. Equivalently, for every closed surface $\mathcal S$ bounding a volume $\mathcal V$ (eq. (2.13)),
>
> $$
> \oint_{\mathcal S}\vec E\cdot d\vec a = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad Q_{\text{enc}} = \int_{\mathcal V}\rho\,d\tau .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.2.1–2.2.2, eqs. (2.13)–(2.16)*

^thm-b2-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The field point enters [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]] only through $\vec{\mathcal R}$, so the divergence acts on $\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2$ (eq. (2.15)), and by [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\nabla\cdot\Bigl(\frac{\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\Bigr)\rho(\vec r\,')\,d\tau' = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,')\,\rho(\vec r\,')\,d\tau' = \frac{\rho(\vec r)}{\varepsilon_0}
> $$
>
> (eq. (2.16)). Taking the divergence inside the integral is legitimate in the distributional sense used for Theorem §B1.2.2: against a test function $f$, $\int f\,\nabla\cdot\vec E\,d\tau = -\int\vec E\cdot\nabla f\,d\tau$, and the order of the $d\tau$ and $d\tau'$ integrations may be exchanged because $1/\mathcal R^2$ is integrable; the inner integral is then $-\int(\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2)\cdot\nabla f\,d\tau = 4\pi f(\vec r\,')$, which leaves $\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int\rho f\,d\tau'$. The integral form follows by integrating over $\mathcal V$ and applying the divergence theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]); conversely, the integral form for every small volume gives back the local law (Griffiths' first route, eqs. (2.13)–(2.14)).

^der-b2-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from level A
> - **Layer and proof.** Gauss's law is a theorem at both levels. Level A proved it by a solid-angle count that assumed surfaces regular enough and no charge on the surface ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^der-a2-2-2|§A2.2]], which promised the divergence proof for level B); here it follows for every distribution, the singular ones included, from the single identity $\nabla\cdot(\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2) = 4\pi\delta^3$. The proof uses the exact inverse square, as the solid-angle argument did ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^cau-a2-2-1|§A2.2, Caution: Closed surfaces, signs, inverse squares]]).
> - **Local versus global.** The differential form relates the field near a point to the charge at that point and needs no surface. The integral form is better for surface, line and point charges and, with symmetry, for computing fields ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law|§A2.3]]; Griffiths §2.2.3).
> - **Not the whole story.** Gauss's law fixes only the divergence, which is why level A needed symmetry to extract $\vec E$ ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^rem-a2-2-2|§A2.2, Remark: What the law does and does not say]]). The curl is fixed by the next theorem, and the two together determine the field ([[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^rem-b2-1-3|Remark: The field equations of electrostatics]]).

^rem-b2-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.4: The Curl of the Electrostatic Field Vanishes
> The field of any static charge distribution satisfies (Griffiths eqs. (2.19)–(2.20))
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0, \qquad \oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0 \ \text{ for every closed loop} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.2.4, eqs. (2.18)–(2.20); Problem 2.19*

^thm-b2-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Griffiths' route.* For a point charge at the origin, $d\vec l = dr\,\hat r + r\,d\theta\,\hat\theta + r\sin\theta\,d\varphi\,\hat\varphi$ gives $\vec E\cdot d\vec l = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dr}{r^2}$, so $\int_{\mathbf a}^{\mathbf b}\vec E\cdot d\vec l = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\bigl(\frac{1}{r_a} - \frac{1}{r_b}\bigr)$ (eq. (2.18)), which vanishes for a closed path; this is [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]]. Nothing depends on where the charge sits, and by superposition the loop integral of any static field vanishes. Stokes' theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]) applied to arbitrarily small loops then gives $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$.
>
> *Directly* (Griffiths Problem 2.19). By [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], $\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2 = -\nabla(1/\mathcal R)$, so
>
> $$
> \vec E = -\nabla\Bigl[\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\rho(\vec r\,')}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau'\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> a gradient, and the curl of a gradient vanishes ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]). Unlike the divergence, the curl has no delta function at a point charge: $\hat r/r^2 = -\nabla(1/r)$ holds as distributions, and the curl of a distributional gradient is zero.

^der-b2-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^law-b2-1-1|Law §B2.1.1]], [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]], [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The field equations of electrostatics
> - The two local laws
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0
> $$
>
> are the static case of the first and third of Maxwell's equations ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|Principle §A12.1.3]]): with nothing changing in time, Faraday's law loses its source term. For charge confined to a finite region and $\vec E \to \vec 0$ at infinity, the Helmholtz theorem ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]) says they have exactly one solution, and it is the Coulomb integral of [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]. So the field equations contain Coulomb's law, and Coulomb's law contains them: the two routes of [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^rem-b2-1-1|Remark: Coulomb's law and superposition at level B]] meet.
> - A vector field with nonzero curl, such as $y\hat x$, cannot be the field of any static charges (Griffiths §2.3.1); a test of this kind is [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^ex-b2-2-1|Example §B2.2.1]].
> - $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ on all of space is what makes a potential exist ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]; [[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations|§B2.2]]). Nelson notes that this step needs a region without holes; in electrostatics the region is all of space, and the field's singular points carry no curl.
> - Both laws fail as soon as charges move: a changing magnetic field adds a curl ([[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields|§A9.4]]; [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields|§B8.2]]), while Gauss's law survives unchanged.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.2.4, §2.3.1 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 2, §2.2.1 (footnote on contractible regions), §2.2.3*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.1.1: A Charged Spherical Shell by Direct Integration
> A spherical surface of radius $R$ carries uniform surface charge $\sigma$, total $q = 4\pi R^2\sigma$. Find $\vec E$ at distance $z$ from the centre, inside and outside, from [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]] rather than from Gauss's law.
>
> Put the field point at $\vec r = z\hat z$ and the source point at polar angle $\theta'$, azimuth $\varphi'$ on the sphere. By the law of cosines $\mathcal R^2 = R^2 + z^2 - 2Rz\cos\theta'$, and the $z$ component of $\vec{\mathcal R}$ is $z - R\cos\theta'$; the $x$ and $y$ components contain $\cos\varphi'$ and $\sin\varphi'$ and integrate to zero over $\varphi'$, as symmetry demands (and only the Cartesian components may be integrated this way). With $da' = R^2\sin\theta'\,d\theta'\,d\varphi'$ and $u = \cos\theta'$,
>
> $$
> E_z = \frac{\sigma R^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{z - Ru}{(R^2 + z^2 - 2Rzu)^{3/2}}\,du = \frac{\sigma R^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\frac{zu - R}{z^2\sqrt{R^2 + z^2 - 2Rzu}}\Bigr]_{u=-1}^{u=1} .
> $$
>
> (Differentiate the bracket to check it.) At $u = -1$ the root is $R + z$ and the bracket is $-1/z^2$. At $u = 1$ the root is $\sqrt{(R - z)^2} = |z - R|$, the **positive** root, and the bracket is $(z - R)/(z^2|z - R|)$, which is $+1/z^2$ outside and $-1/z^2$ inside. Hence
>
> $$
> E_z = \frac{\sigma R^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{2}{z^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{z^2}\quad(z > R), \qquad E_z = 0\quad(z < R) .
> $$
>
> This is [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]], obtained the hard way. Two lessons: Gauss's law with symmetry saves a real integral; and the whole difference between inside and outside comes from choosing the sign of a square root, the kind of step that direct integration hides and the field equations make automatic.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.7 (solved here), Figure 2.11*

^ex-b2-1-1

> [!example] Example §B2.1.2: Two Overlapping Charged Balls
> Two balls of radius $R$ carry uniform densities $+\rho$ and $-\rho$ and partly overlap; let $\vec d$ be the vector from the centre $\vec r_+$ of the positive ball to the centre $\vec r_-$ of the negative one. Find the field in the overlap.
>
> The net charge density in the overlap is $\rho - \rho = 0$, yet the field there is not zero. Inside a uniformly charged ball centred at $\vec r_0$, Gauss's law gives $E = \rho|\vec r - \vec r_0|/3\varepsilon_0$ along the radius ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]), which in vector form is
>
> $$
> \vec E_{\text{in}}(\vec r) = \frac{\rho}{3\varepsilon_0}\,(\vec r - \vec r_0) .
> $$
>
> In the overlap both balls are "inside", and superposition gives
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{\rho}{3\varepsilon_0}(\vec r - \vec r_+) - \frac{\rho}{3\varepsilon_0}(\vec r - \vec r_-) = \frac{\rho}{3\varepsilon_0}\,(\vec r_- - \vec r_+) = \frac{\rho\,\vec d}{3\varepsilon_0} ,
> $$
>
> uniform, pointing from the positive towards the negative centre. The radii never entered, so the result holds for balls of different radii too. It is consistent with the field equations: a uniform field has zero divergence, matching the zero net density, and zero curl. In the limit $d \to 0$ with $\rho d$ fixed the overlap fills the whole ball, and this becomes the uniform field $-\vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ inside a uniformly polarized sphere with polarization $\vec P = -\rho\vec d$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 2, Problem 4 (Overlapping charged spheres), checked against the instructor's solution · Griffiths Problem 2.18*
