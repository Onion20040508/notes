An induced emf in a *stationary* loop means that something does work on the conduction electrons. It is not a battery (there is none) and not the magnetic force (it does no work). It is an electric field, induced by the changing magnetic field. This section restates Faraday's law as a law about that field. The field exists whether or not a wire is there, and it is unlike the electrostatic field in one decisive way: it has no potential.

## Faraday's law as a field law

> [!definition] Definition §A9.4.1: EMF Around a Closed Path
> The **emf** around a closed path $C$ is the work per unit charge done by the electric field on a charge carried once around $C$:
>
> $$
> \mathcal E = \oint_C \vec E\cdot d\vec l .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §13.4, eq. (13.9)*

^def-a9-4-1

> [!principle] Principle §A9.4.1: Faraday's Law, Field Form
> For every closed path $C$ at rest, bounding a surface $S$ whose normal $\hat n$ is tied to the sense of $C$ by the right-hand rule,
>
> $$
> \oint_C \vec E\cdot d\vec l = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_S \vec B\cdot\hat n\,dA = -\frac{d\Phi_m}{dt}.
> $$
>
> A changing magnetic field produces an electric field, wherever $\partial\vec B/\partial t \neq 0$, whether or not a conductor is present.
>
> *Domain: all of classical electromagnetism, for paths at rest in the chosen frame; it is one of Maxwell's equations ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves|§A12.1]]).*
> *Source: UP2 §13.4, eqs. (13.10), (13.12) and Example 13.7*

^pr-a9-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: How this fits with §A9.1
> - For a circuit at rest, the only thing that can push charges around is $\vec E$, so the emf of [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^pr-a9-1-1|Principle §A9.1.1]] is $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l$ and the two statements coincide. For a moving circuit, part or all of the emf comes from $\vec v\times\vec B$ instead ([[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-2|Theorem §A9.3.2]]).
> - The field needs no wire. In [[§A9.2 Lenz's Law#^ex-a9-2-2|Example §A9.2.2]] the induced field on the circle $r = 0.50\ \mathrm m$ is tangent to it with magnitude $E = \lvert\mathcal E\rvert/(2\pi r) = 1.9\ \mathrm{V/m}$ at $t = 0$, circulating counterclockwise; remove the coil and the field in empty space is the same.
> - Shape: the induced field *curls around* the changing flux, as a magnetic field curls around a current (Ampère's law, [[§A8.4 Ampère's Law|§A8.4]]). Charges make electric fields that diverge from them; a changing magnetic flux makes electric fields that circulate around it.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 13 slides*

^rem-a9-4-1

## No potential for the induced field

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.4.2: The Induced Electric Field Is Not Conservative
> Where the magnetic flux through some closed path changes, the electric field has nonzero circulation around that path, $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l \neq 0$. The induced field therefore does net work on a charge taken around a closed path, it has no electric potential, and the integral $\int_a^b \vec E\cdot d\vec l$ between two points depends on the path. The electrostatic field of fixed charges, by contrast, has $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$ for every closed path.
>
> *Source: UP2 §13.4, eq. (13.11)*

^thm-a9-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^pr-a9-4-1|Principle §A9.4.1]], $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = -d\Phi_m/dt \neq 0$ for such a path. The force $q\vec E$ thus does nonzero work $q\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l$ around a closed path, so it is not conservative ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-1|CM Theorem §A4.5.1]]), and no potential energy, hence no potential $V = U/q$, can be defined for it ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference|§A3.2]]). If $\int_a^b\vec E\cdot d\vec l$ were the same along every path from $a$ to $b$, going out along one path and back along another would give zero circulation; since the circulation is not zero, two such paths must give different values.

^der-a9-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^pr-a9-4-1|Principle §A9.4.1]], [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-1|CM Theorem §A4.5.1]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference|§A3.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: "Voltage" near changing flux
> - With an induced field present, "the potential at a point" is undefined, and a voltmeter's reading between two points depends on how its leads run (which flux they enclose).
> - Kirchhoff's loop rule ([[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules|§A6.3]]) rests on $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$. When changing flux threads a circuit, enter the induced emf as a source in the loop; inductors are handled this way ([[§A10.2 Self-Inductance#^thm-a10-2-3|Theorem §A10.2.3]]).
> - PHY 182 writes the law as $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = d\Phi_B/dt$ and gets the direction from Lenz's law. That is the same law for magnitudes; the minus sign is what encodes Lenz's law when the orientation convention is used.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 13 slides*

## Example

> [!example] Example §A9.4.1: Electric Field Around a Solenoid with Decaying Current
> A long solenoid of radius $R$ with $n$ turns per unit length carries $I = I_0 e^{-\alpha t}$. In the infinite-solenoid approximation the field is $B = \mu_0 n I_0 e^{-\alpha t}$ inside, along the axis (into the page in the figure), and zero outside ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids|§A8.5]]). Take as path a circle of radius $r$ centred on the axis in a cross-section. By the cylindrical symmetry $\vec E$ is tangent to the circle and has the same magnitude all along it, so $\lvert\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l\rvert = 2\pi r E$.
> - *Inside*, $r < R$: the enclosed flux is $B\pi r^2$, so $2\pi rE = \pi r^2\alpha\mu_0 n I_0 e^{-\alpha t}$ and
>
> $$
> E = \tfrac12\,\alpha\mu_0 n I_0\, r\, e^{-\alpha t}.
> $$
>
> - *Outside*, $r > R$: the enclosed flux is only $B\pi R^2$, so
>
> $$
> E = \frac{\alpha\mu_0 n I_0 R^2}{2r}\, e^{-\alpha t}.
> $$
>
> The field into the page is decreasing, so a conducting ring on either circle would carry a clockwise current (as seen in the figure), and $\vec E$ circulates clockwise. Outside the solenoid $\vec B = 0$ everywhere on the path, yet $\vec E \neq 0$: the induced field responds to the changing flux enclosed, not to the field where it sits.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 13.8*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - By Stokes' theorem ([[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|MVA Thm. §20.1]]), applying Principle §A9.4.1 to every small loop gives the differential form $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial t$ (level B, Chapter B, planned).
> - So the induced field is not irrotational, hence not a gradient field ([[§11 The Three Differential Operators꞉ Gradient, Curl, Divergence#^def-11-1|MVA Def. §11.1]]); the electrostatic field is both.
