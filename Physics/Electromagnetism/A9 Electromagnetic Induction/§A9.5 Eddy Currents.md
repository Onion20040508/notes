Induction does not need a wire. In a solid piece of metal, a changing flux drives currents that swirl through the bulk. By Lenz's law they always resist the motion that causes them, which produces a drag proportional to speed. This section explains that drag with a single loop as a model, and lists where it helps (brakes, balances, detectors) and where it hurts (energy losses in iron cores).

## Eddy currents and magnetic damping

> [!definition] Definition §A9.5.1: Eddy Current; Magnetic Damping
> An **eddy current** is a loop of current induced inside a bulk conductor by a changing magnetic flux through it, either because the conductor moves through a nonuniform field or because the field changes in time. **Magnetic damping** is the drag on a conductor moving through a magnetic field that is caused by its eddy currents.
>
> *Source: UP2 §13.5*

^def-a9-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: The pendulum demonstration
> A metal plate swinging as a pendulum bob between the poles of a strong magnet stops within a few swings. A slotted plate is hardly slowed, and an insulating one not at all.
> - As the plate *enters* the field, the flux through it grows, an eddy current circulates (Faraday, Lenz), and only the part of that current inside the field feels a force, which points against the motion.
> - While the plate is wholly inside a uniform field the flux does not change: no eddy current, no drag.
> - As it *leaves*, the flux falls, the eddy current reverses, and the force on the part still in the field again points against the motion. Swinging back is damped the same way.
>
> Slots limit the size of the current loops, and neighbouring small loops carry opposite currents whose forces largely cancel. To suppress eddy currents in a conductor, cut it into slots or build it from thin conducting layers separated by insulation (laminated cores, [[§A9.6 Generators, Back EMF and Applications#^rem-a9-6-2|§A9.6]]).

^rem-a9-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.5.1: Magnetic Drag on a Loop Crossing a Field Edge
> A rectangular loop of resistance $R$ moves with velocity $\vec v$ perpendicular to a uniform field $\vec B$ and to one of its edges, of length $l$. While that edge is inside the field and the opposite edge outside, the loop feels a net magnetic force
>
> $$
> F = \frac{B^2l^2v}{R},
> $$
>
> directed against $\vec v$; the same holds while it leaves. While the loop is wholly inside (or wholly outside) the field, the force is zero. The drag is proportional to the speed and vanishes as the motion stops.
>
> *Source: UP2 §13.5; PHY 182, TBL 23*

^thm-a9-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> While entering, the area inside the field grows at the rate $lv$, so $\lvert d\Phi_m/dt\rvert = Blv$ and the current is $I = Blv/R$, exactly as for the sliding rod ([[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-1|Theorem §A9.3.1]]). The edge of length $l$ in the field feels $IlB = B^2l^2v/R$ ([[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]); the two side edges, partly in the field, carry opposite currents and feel equal and opposite forces that cancel. By Lenz's law ([[§A9.2 Lenz's Law#^thm-a9-2-1|Theorem §A9.2.1]]) the net force opposes the motion that changes the flux. While leaving, the flux falls at the same rate, the current reverses, and the force on the trailing edge, now the one in the field, again opposes the motion. Wholly inside or outside, $d\Phi_m/dt = 0$, so $I = 0$ and $F = 0$.

^der-a9-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-1|Theorem §A9.3.1]], [[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]], [[§A9.2 Lenz's Law#^thm-a9-2-1|Theorem §A9.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Uses of eddy currents
> - *Sensitive balances.* A conducting disc turning in a fixed field damps the oscillations of the balance. Since the drag is proportional to speed, it vanishes once the balance is at rest and does not disturb the reading.
> - *Recycling.* Trash slides down a ramp over a strong magnet; metals (all metals, not only ferromagnetic ones) are slowed by magnetic drag while nonmetals move on.
> - *Metal detectors.* A primary coil carrying alternating current induces eddy currents in nearby metal, and these change the current induced in a secondary coil, which triggers the signal.
> - *Brakes* on trains and roller coasters: copper fins on the vehicle pass between rows of strong (neodymium) magnets. The braking is smooth and does not depend on rain.
> - *Induction cooktops.* A rapidly varying field from electromagnets under the surface drives eddy currents in the pot's conducting base, which heat it.

^rem-a9-5-2

> [!caution] Caution: An eddy-current brake cannot stop a vehicle
> The braking force is proportional to speed, so it fades as the vehicle slows: speed can be cut from, say, $20\ \mathrm{m/s}$ to $5\ \mathrm{m/s}$, but another brake is needed to stop. In the single-loop model the drag is linear drag with $b = B^2l^2/R$, under which the speed decays exponentially and never reaches zero in finite time ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed|CM §A3.10]]).

^cau-a9-5-1

## Example

> [!example] Example §A9.5.1: Square Loop Falling Through a Field Strip
> A square loop, $1.0\ \mathrm m$ on a side, with resistance $R$, moves straight down at a constant $v = 1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Below it is a horizontal strip, $2.0\ \mathrm m$ high, of uniform field $\vec B$ out of the page; its leading (bottom) edge reaches the strip at $t = 0$. Take clockwise current and upward force as positive.
> - $0 < t < 1\ \mathrm s$: the loop enters; $\Phi_m = B(1.0\ \mathrm m)(vt)$ grows at the rate $Blv$. The outward flux grows, so the induced field points into the page and the current $I = Blv/R$ is clockwise. The bottom edge, inside the field, feels $F = B^2l^2v/R$ upward.
> - $1\ \mathrm s < t < 2\ \mathrm s$: the loop is wholly inside; the flux is constant, $I = 0$, $F = 0$.
> - $2\ \mathrm s < t < 3\ \mathrm s$: the loop leaves; the flux falls at the rate $Blv$, the current is counterclockwise, and the top edge, still in the field, feels $F = B^2l^2v/R$, again upward.
> - Before $t = 0$ and after $t = 3\ \mathrm s$: no flux, nothing.
>
> The drag acts only while the flux changes, on entering and on leaving, just as for the pendulum plate. To keep $v$ constant, gravity or an external agent must supply the power $I^2R$ during those two intervals.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 23*
