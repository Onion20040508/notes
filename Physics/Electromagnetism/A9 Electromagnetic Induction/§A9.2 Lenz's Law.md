Faraday's law gives the size of an induced emf, and its minus sign gives the direction, but reading the sign requires the orientation convention every time. Lenz's law states the same information as a physical rule: the induced current fights the change that causes it. It also shows why the sign has to be a minus: anything else would create energy.

## The rule

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.2.1: Lenz's Law
> The induced emf drives current around a loop in the direction whose own magnetic field **opposes the change** in magnetic flux that causes the emf. If the flux through the loop is increasing, the induced field through the loop points against the applied field; if it is decreasing, along it.
>
> *Source: UP2 §13.2*

^thm-a9-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Choose $\hat n$ along the applied field, so the flux $\Phi_m$ is positive. If $\Phi_m$ increases, [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^pr-a9-1-1|Principle §A9.1.1]] gives $\mathcal E = -d\Phi_m/dt < 0$: the current flows against the positive sense. By the right-hand rule for the field of a current loop ([[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop|§A8.3]]), a current circulating against the positive sense makes a field through the loop along $-\hat n$, which reduces the flux. If $\Phi_m$ decreases, $\mathcal E > 0$, the current flows in the positive sense, and its field along $+\hat n$ props the flux up. In both cases the induced field opposes the change.

^der-a9-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^pr-a9-1-1|Principle §A9.1.1]], [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^def-a9-1-1|Def. §A9.1.1]], [[§A8.3 Field of a Current Loop|§A8.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why it must be "oppose": energy
> Suppose the induced current *aided* the change. A magnet pushed toward a loop would then be pulled in further, speeding up on its own while the induced current heats the wire: kinetic energy and heat from nothing, against [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]. With the induced field opposing the approach, whoever pushes the magnet must do work against a repulsion, and that work reappears as the energy of the current. The minus sign in Faraday's law is energy conservation.

^rem-a9-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: A recipe for directions
> 1. Sketch the situation.
> 2. Find the direction of the applied field $\vec B$ through the loop.
> 3. Decide whether its flux is increasing or decreasing.
> 4. The induced field opposes an increase or reinforces a decrease.
> 5. The right-hand rule (thumb along the induced field through the loop, fingers along the current) gives the induced current $I$.
> 6. The emf drives conventional current in that direction.
>
> Equivalently, treat the loop as a little magnet: it presents the *same* pole to an approaching pole (repelling it) and the *opposite* pole to a receding one (attracting it). Either way the loop resists the relative motion.

^rem-a9-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: "Opposes the change", not "opposes the field"
> - The induced field is *parallel* to the applied field whenever the flux is decreasing. It opposes $d\Phi_m/dt$, not $\vec B$.
> - Lenz's law gives only a direction. The magnitude comes from Faraday's law.

^cau-a9-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Sparks and jumping rings
> - Opening a switch in a circuit with a solenoid collapses its flux. The induced emf tries to keep the original current going, in the original direction, and can be large enough to draw an arc across the opening switch contacts. Sharpened leads on a coil can be made to spark this way.
> - A metal ring rests on a vertical solenoid. When the current is switched on, the ring's induced current opposes the growing flux, so the ring is repelled by the solenoid's stray field and jumps into the air.

^rem-a9-2-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A9.2.1: Magnet Falling Through a Ring
> A bar magnet, north pole down, falls along the axis of a horizontal conducting ring. On the axis the magnet's field points along its moment, here downward, both above and below the magnet.
> - *Approaching (above the ring).* The downward flux through the ring grows, so the induced field points up: the current is counterclockwise seen from above. The ring's upper face is then a north pole, which repels the magnet's approaching north pole.
> - *Receding (below the ring).* The downward flux shrinks, so the induced field points down: the current is clockwise seen from above. The ring's lower face is a north pole, which attracts the magnet's south pole (now on top) back up.
>
> The force on the magnet is upward in both phases. Flipping the magnet reverses both currents but not this conclusion: the induced current always opposes the relative motion.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 23*

^ex-a9-2-1

![[ph-em-a9-2-1.svg]]
*Example §A9.2.1. (a) The magnet approaches: its field through the ring (green) grows, so the induced current (blue) makes an upward induced field (red), and the ring repels the north pole. (b) The magnet recedes: the flux shrinks, the current reverses, the induced field points down, and the ring attracts the departing south pole. Either way the magnet is slowed.*

> [!example] Example §A9.2.2: Coil in an Exponentially Decaying Field
> A uniform field points out of the page, perpendicular to a circular coil of radius $r = 0.50\ \mathrm m$ and resistance $R = 10\ \Omega$, and decays as $B = (1.5\ \mathrm T)\,e^{-(5.0\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})t}$. With $\hat n$ out of the page,
>
> $$
> \Phi_m = B\pi r^2 = (1.18\ \mathrm{Wb})\,e^{-(5.0\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})t},
> \qquad
> \lvert\mathcal E\rvert = \left\lvert\frac{d\Phi_m}{dt}\right\rvert = (5.9\ \mathrm V)\,e^{-(5.0\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})t}.
> $$
>
> At $t = 0$, $0.050\ \mathrm s$ and $1.0\ \mathrm s$ this gives $5.9\ \mathrm V$, $4.6\ \mathrm V$ and $0.040\ \mathrm V$, and currents $I = \lvert\mathcal E\rvert/R$ of $0.59\ \mathrm A$, $0.46\ \mathrm A$ and $4.0\ \mathrm{mA}$. The outward flux is decreasing, so the induced field also points out of the page: the current circulates counterclockwise as seen from the side the field points toward, at all three times.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 13.2*

^ex-a9-2-2
