The practical way to keep a flux changing is to turn a coil in a magnetic field. That is the electric generator, and it produces a sinusoidal emf: the origin of alternating current. A motor is the same machine run the other way ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop|§A7.4]]). While its coil turns it also acts as a generator, and the emf it generates, the back emf, governs how much current the motor draws. The section closes with a survey of further applications of induction.

## Generators

> [!definition] Definition §A9.6.1: Electric Generator; Peak EMF
> An **electric generator** converts mechanical work into electrical energy by rotating a coil in a magnetic field. The largest value its sinusoidal emf reaches is the **peak emf** $\mathcal E_0$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §13.6*

^def-a9-6-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.6.1: EMF of a Rotating Coil
> A coil of $N$ turns and area $A$ rotates at constant angular velocity $\omega$ about an axis perpendicular to a uniform field $\vec B$, the angle between its normal and $\vec B$ being $\theta = \omega t$. The induced emf is
>
> $$
> \mathcal E = NBA\omega\,\sin\omega t = \mathcal E_0\sin\omega t, \qquad \mathcal E_0 = NBA\omega,
> $$
>
> with frequency $f = \omega/2\pi$ and period $T = 1/f = 2\pi/\omega$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §13.6, eqs. (13.13)–(13.18); Example 13.6*

^thm-a9-6-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *From the flux.* The flux through one turn is $\Phi_m = BA\cos\omega t$ ([[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^def-a9-1-1|Def. §A9.1.1]]), so by [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^thm-a9-1-2|Theorem §A9.1.2]]
> $\mathcal E = -N\,d\Phi_m/dt = NBA\omega\sin\omega t$.
>
> *From the motional emf.* Take a rectangular turn of width $w$ and length $l$, the sides of length $l$ parallel to the axis. They move on a circle of radius $w/2$ at speed $v = \omega w/2$, with velocity at angle $\theta$ to $\vec B$, so each gets the motional emf $Blv\sin\theta$ ([[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-2|Theorem §A9.3.2]]); the two act in the same sense around the turn. In the other two sides $\vec v\times\vec B$ is perpendicular to the wire and contributes nothing. One turn gives $2Blv\sin\theta = (lw)B\omega\sin\omega t = BA\omega\sin\omega t$, and $N$ turns in series give $N$ times this.

^der-a9-6-1

*Uses:* [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^def-a9-1-1|Def. §A9.1.1]], [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^thm-a9-1-2|Theorem §A9.1.2]], [[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-2|Theorem §A9.3.2]]

![[ph-em-a9-6-1.svg]]
*Flux linkage (blue, dashed) and emf (red) of a coil turning at constant $\omega$, each scaled to its own peak. They are a quarter period apart: the emf is largest when the flux passes through zero (the coil's plane parallel to $\vec B$) and zero when the flux is extreme.*

> [!remark] Remark: Real generators
> - The peak emf $NBA\omega$ grows with the number of turns, the area, the field and the speed of rotation; a cheap bicycle dynamo glows brighter the faster one rides.
> - Slip rings and brushes take off the sinusoidal emf as alternating current ([[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits|§A11.1]]). Split rings (commutators) reverse the connection every half turn and give a pulsed dc output.
> - The shaft is turned by falling water, by steam from burning fuel, or by wind. Generating electrical energy from mechanical energy this way is the basis of the power grid.

^rem-a9-6-1

## Back emf

> [!definition] Definition §A9.6.2: Back EMF
> The **back emf** of a motor is the emf induced in its rotating coil. By Lenz's law it opposes the emf that drives the motor. It is zero when the coil is at rest and grows in proportion to the coil's angular velocity.
>
> *Source: UP2 §13.6*

^def-a9-6-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.6.2: Current and Power of a DC Motor
> Model a dc motor as a supply emf $\mathcal E_S$, the resistance $R$ of its coils, and the back emf $\mathcal E_i$, in series. Then
>
> $$
> I = \frac{\mathcal E_S - \mathcal E_i}{R}, \qquad \mathcal E_S I = \mathcal E_i I + I^2R ,
> $$
>
> where $\mathcal E_S I$ is the power supplied, $I^2R$ the power dissipated in the coils, and $\mathcal E_i I$ the mechanical power output (including what is lost to friction).
>
> *Source: UP2 §13.6 and Example 13.10*

^thm-a9-6-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Go around the loop in the direction of $I$ with Kirchhoff's loop rule ([[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules|§A6.3]]), entering the back emf as a source opposing $\mathcal E_S$: $\mathcal E_S - IR - \mathcal E_i = 0$, which gives $I$. Multiplying by $I$, $\mathcal E_S I = I^2R + \mathcal E_i I$. The source delivers $\mathcal E_S I$ ([[§A5.4 Electrical Power|§A5.4]]) and the coils turn $I^2R$ into heat. The remaining $\mathcal E_i I$ is the work per unit time done in driving the current against the back emf; as for the sliding rod run in reverse ([[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-3|Theorem §A9.3.3]]), energy conservation ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]) requires that it leave the circuit as mechanical work done by the turning coil.

^der-a9-6-2

*Uses:* [[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules|§A6.3]], [[§A5.4 Electrical Power|§A5.4]], [[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-3|Theorem §A9.3.3]], [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]

![[ph-em-a9-6-2.svg]]
*The circuit model of [[§A9.6 Generators, Back EMF and Applications#^thm-a9-6-2|Theorem §A9.6.2]]: the supply $\mathcal E_S$ drives the current $I$ (blue) through the coil resistance $R$ against the back emf $\mathcal E_i$ (red), whose polarity opposes the supply.*

> [!remark] Remark: What the back emf does
> - *Starting.* At rest $\mathcal E_i = 0$ and the coils get the full supply voltage. Coils of $0.400\ \Omega$ on $48.0\ \mathrm V$ draw $120\ \mathrm A$ and dissipate $5.76\ \mathrm{kW}$, enough to burn them out if sustained. At operating speed, with a back emf of $40.0\ \mathrm V$, only $8.0\ \mathrm V$ is left across the coils: $20\ \mathrm A$ and $160\ \mathrm W$. This is why lights on the same circuit dim briefly when a refrigerator or washing machine starts: the large starting current makes a large $IR$ drop in the supply lines.
> - *Load.* A heavier mechanical load slows the motor, the back emf drops, and more current flows, so more work can be done. A stalled or overloaded motor can overheat.
> - *No load.* The motor speeds up until $\mathcal E_i$ nearly equals $\mathcal E_S$, drawing only enough current to overcome friction.
> - *Iron cores.* Eddy currents in the iron cores of motors waste energy; cores are built of thin, electrically insulated iron sheets, which hardly changes their magnetic properties but greatly reduces the heating ([[§A9.5 Eddy Currents|§A9.5]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A9.6.1: A Quarter Turn of a Generator Coil
> A circular coil of $N = 200$ turns and radius $5.00\ \mathrm{cm}$ in a uniform $0.80\ \mathrm T$ field turns from $\theta = 0^\circ$ (normal along $\vec B$) to $\theta = 90^\circ$ in $15.0\ \mathrm{ms}$. Its flux linkage falls from $NBA = (200)(0.80\ \mathrm T)\,\pi(0.0500\ \mathrm m)^2 = 1.26\ \mathrm{Wb}$ to zero, so the *average* emf over the quarter turn is
>
> $$
> \mathcal E_{\text{avg}} = \frac{N\,\lvert\Delta\Phi_m\rvert}{\Delta t} = \frac{1.26\ \mathrm{Wb}}{15.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm s} = 83.8\ \mathrm V .
> $$
>
> If the rotation is uniform, $\omega = (\pi/2)/(15.0\ \mathrm{ms}) = 105\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, and the emf rises from $0$ at $\theta = 0^\circ$ to its peak $NBA\omega = 132\ \mathrm V$ at $\theta = 90^\circ$. The average over a quarter period is $2/\pi$ of the peak. (University Physics quotes the $131\ \mathrm V$ it computes as the average; it is the peak value at $90^\circ$.) Either way the order of magnitude is that of household voltage.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 13.9*

^ex-a9-6-1

> [!example] Example §A9.6.2: A Series-Wound Motor
> A dc motor with total coil resistance $R = 2.0\ \Omega$ runs on $\mathcal E_S = 120\ \mathrm V$ and draws $I = 10\ \mathrm A$ at constant speed. By [[§A9.6 Generators, Back EMF and Applications#^thm-a9-6-2|Theorem §A9.6.2]]:
>
> $$
> \mathcal E_i = \mathcal E_S - IR = 100\ \mathrm V, \quad
> P_m = \mathcal E_i I = 1.0\ \mathrm{kW}, \quad
> P_R = I^2R = 200\ \mathrm W, \quad
> P_S = \mathcal E_S I = 1.2\ \mathrm{kW},
> $$
>
> and indeed $1.2\ \mathrm{kW} = 1.0\ \mathrm{kW} + 0.2\ \mathrm{kW}$. Under a heavier load the motor slows until it draws $20\ \mathrm A$: then $\mathcal E_i = 80\ \mathrm V$, $P_m = 1.6\ \mathrm{kW}$, $P_R = 800\ \mathrm W$ and $P_S = 2.4\ \mathrm{kW}$. The slower motor delivers more mechanical power, but the share lost as heat rises from one sixth to one third.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 13.10*

^ex-a9-6-2

## Further applications

> [!remark] Remark: Induction at work
> - *Hard drives.* Older drives read the magnetized coating of the spinning disc by induction; most now use giant magnetoresistance, a large change in the resistance of thin alternating ferromagnetic and nonmagnetic layers in a magnetic field.
> - *Graphics tablets* (passive ones). The pen's tip carries a small magnetic field; moving it over a grid of fine wires under the screen induces emfs in them that record the stroke.
> - *Magnetic stripes* on credit cards are read, like audio tape, by a head in which the moving stripe induces an emf. The card has to be swiped: a stationary stripe gives no changing flux.
> - *Electric and hybrid cars.* While braking, the drive motor runs as a generator, and the energy it produces recharges the battery.
> - *Transcranial magnetic stimulation.* Disorders such as depression can be traced to irregular, localized electrical activity in the brain. A rapidly varying, very localized field placed near a chosen site induces currents there; the technique is well established for diagnosis.
