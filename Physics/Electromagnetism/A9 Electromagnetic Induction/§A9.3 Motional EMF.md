So far the flux changed because the field changed. It can equally change because the circuit moves through a steady field. Then the emf can be found in two quite different ways, from Faraday's law or from the magnetic force on the charges carried along by the moving wire, and they agree. Energy bookkeeping shows where the electrical energy comes from: the agent that moves the wire.

## The sliding rod

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.3.1: Motional EMF of a Sliding Rod
> A conducting rod of length $l$ slides with speed $v$ along two conducting rails through a uniform field $\vec B$, with $\vec B$, the rod and $\vec v$ mutually perpendicular. The emf around the circuit is
>
> $$
> \lvert\mathcal E\rvert = Blv ,
> $$
>
> and if the circuit has resistance $R$, the induced current is $I = Blv/R$. Its direction follows from Lenz's law.
>
> *Source: UP2 §13.3, eqs. (13.4)–(13.5)*

^thm-a9-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *From Faraday's law.* With the rod a distance $x$ from the resistor, the enclosed area is $lx$, perpendicular to $\vec B$, so $\Phi_m = Blx$. Only $x$ changes, and $dx/dt = v$: $\lvert\mathcal E\rvert = \lvert d\Phi_m/dt\rvert = Bl\,dx/dt = Blv$.
>
> *From the magnetic force.* A charge $q$ in the rod moves with the rod at velocity $\vec v$, so it feels $q\vec v\times\vec B$ ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]]), of magnitude $qvB$, directed along the rod. Carried the length $l$ of the rod, the charge receives work $qvBl$; the work per unit charge is the emf ([[§A6.1 Electromotive Force#^def-a6-1-1|Def. §A6.1.1]]), again $Blv$. The rails and resistor are at rest and contribute nothing.
>
> Ohm's law ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-5|Law §A5.3.5]]) then gives $I = Blv/R$.

^der-a9-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^pr-a9-1-1|Principle §A9.1.1]], [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^def-a9-1-1|Def. §A9.1.1]], [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]], [[§A6.1 Electromotive Force#^def-a6-1-1|Def. §A6.1.1]], [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-5|Law §A5.3.5]]

The current in the rod feels the magnetic force $\vec F_m$ (red), against the motion; an applied force $\vec F_a$ (black) must balance it to keep $\vec v$ constant.*

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.3.2: Motional EMF from the Lorentz Force
> For a conductor moving through a static magnetic field, the emf along it is the line integral of the magnetic force per unit charge,
>
> $$
> \mathcal E = \int \bigl(\vec v\times\vec B\bigr)\cdot d\vec l ,
> $$
>
> taken along the conductor, where $\vec v$ is the velocity of the element $d\vec l$. When $\vec v$, $\vec B$ and the conductor are mutually perpendicular this is $\lvert\mathcal E\rvert = \int vB\,dl$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §13.3 and Example 13.5; PHY 182, TBL 22*

^thm-a9-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A charge $q$ carried by the element $d\vec l$ moves with it at $\vec v$ and feels the force $q\vec v\times\vec B$, by the magnetic (Lorentz) force law [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]]. The work this force does per unit charge while the charge is moved along $d\vec l$ is $(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec l$; summing along the conductor gives the work per unit charge, which is the emf ([[§A6.1 Electromotive Force#^def-a6-1-1|Def. §A6.1.1]]). For perpendicular vectors, $\vec v\times\vec B$ has magnitude $vB$ and points along the conductor.

^der-a9-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|Principle §A7.1.1]], [[§A6.1 Electromotive Force#^def-a6-1-1|Def. §A6.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: One law for two causes
> Starting from $q\vec v\times\vec B$ one can show that $\mathcal E = -d\Phi_m/dt$ for *any* flux change caused by the motion of a conductor (the general proof belongs to level B, Chapter B, planned). Faraday's law therefore holds for all flux changes: those from a changing field, from motion, or from both. The rod shows the agreement in the simplest case. How the two causes combine into the single flux rule of [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^pr-a9-1-1|Principle §A9.1.1]], with the field form of Faraday's law as the general statement, is spelled out in [[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^rem-a9-4-1|§A9.4]].

^rem-a9-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Only perpendicular components count
> $Blv$ needs $\vec B$, the rod and $\vec v$ mutually perpendicular. If $\vec v$ makes an angle $\theta$ with $\vec B$ (rod still perpendicular to both), only $v\sin\theta$ counts: $\lvert\mathcal E\rvert = Blv\sin\theta$. A rod moving *along* its own length, or parallel to $\vec B$, has no motional emf.

^cau-a9-3-1

## Where the energy comes from

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.3.3: Power Balance for the Sliding Rod
> To keep the rod of [[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-1|Theorem §A9.3.1]] moving at constant velocity, an applied force of magnitude
>
> $$
> F_a = IlB = \frac{B^2l^2v}{R}
> $$
>
> is needed, and the mechanical power it delivers equals the Joule power in the circuit:
>
> $$
> F_a v = \frac{B^2l^2v^2}{R} = I^2R .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §13.3, eqs. (13.6)–(13.7)*

^thm-a9-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The rod carries the current $I$ in the field, so it feels the magnetic force $\vec F_m = I\vec l\times\vec B$ ([[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]) of magnitude $IlB$, directed against $\vec v$ (Lenz's law; see the figure). At constant velocity the net force is zero ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]]), so $F_a = IlB = (Blv/R)\,lB = B^2l^2v/R$. The power of a force along the motion is $F_a v$ ([[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|CM Theorem §A4.3.1]]), which is $B^2l^2v^2/R$. The power dissipated in the resistance is $I^2R = (Blv/R)^2R = B^2l^2v^2/R$ ([[§A5.4 Electrical Power#^thm-a5-4-2|Theorem §A5.4.2]]). The two agree.

^der-a9-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-1|Theorem §A9.3.1]], [[§A9.2 Lenz's Law#^thm-a9-2-1|Theorem §A9.2.1]], [[§A7.3 Force on a Current-Carrying Wire#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|CM Theorem §A4.3.1]], [[§A5.4 Electrical Power#^thm-a5-4-2|Theorem §A5.4.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Converting work into current
> The energy comes from the agent pushing the rod, not from the magnetic field: a magnetic force on a charge is perpendicular to its velocity and does no work ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-1|Theorem §A7.2.1]]). The magnetic force redirects: its component along the rod drives the current, its component across the rod resists the push. A rail gun runs the same device backwards: a large current through the rails and the armature between them pushes the armature, and the projectile it carries, forward.

^rem-a9-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Magnetic or electric? It depends on the frame
> Let a magnet and a ring move relative to each other. In the magnet's rest frame, the ring's charges move through a static field and the magnetic force $q\vec v\times\vec B$ pushes them around: a motional emf. In the ring's rest frame, the charges are at rest, the field at the ring is changing, and an induced *electric* field pushes them ([[§A9.4 Induced Electric Fields#^pr-a9-4-1|Principle §A9.4.1]]). The current is the same; only the name of the force differs. That what one observer calls magnetic another calls electric troubled Maxwell and opens Einstein's 1905 paper on special relativity ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^rem-a1-3-2|REL Remark in §A1.3]]; see also [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^rem-a12-1-7|§A12.1]]). Level A sidesteps the question by always working in the laboratory frame.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 14 slides*

^rem-a9-3-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A9.3.1: Bar Falling on Vertical Rails
> A horizontal bar of mass $m$ and length $l$ slides without friction down two vertical rails joined at the top through a resistance $R$; a uniform horizontal field $\vec B$ is perpendicular to the plane of the rails. Take down as positive. At speed $v$ the induced current is $I = Blv/R$ and the magnetic force on the bar is $IlB = B^2l^2v/R$, upward (Lenz). Newton's second law:
>
> $$
> m\frac{dv}{dt} = mg - \frac{B^2l^2}{R}\,v .
> $$
>
> The bar reaches a **terminal velocity** where the forces balance:
>
> $$
> v_T = \frac{mgR}{B^2l^2}.
> $$
>
> Energy check: gravity supplies $mgv_T$ per unit time, and the resistor dissipates $I^2R = B^2l^2v_T^2/R = mgv_T$. A larger $R$ means a smaller current, a weaker brake, a faster fall, and more power burned ($mgv_T \propto R$). The equation is falling with linear drag, $b = B^2l^2/R$, so by [[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^thm-a3-10-4|CM Theorem §A3.10.4]] a bar released from rest has $v(t) = v_T\bigl(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\bigr)$ with $\tau = mR/(B^2l^2)$.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 22*

^ex-a9-3-1

> [!example] Example §A9.3.2: Rotating Rod
> A metal rod $OS$ of length $r$ turns about $O$ with constant angular velocity $\omega$ in a horizontal plane, its end $S$ sliding on a circular wire; a resistor $R$ connects the wire back to $O$. A uniform field $\vec B$ points out of the page (up). When the rod has swept an angle $\theta$ from the fixed lead $OP$, the loop $OPSO$ encloses a sector of area $r^2\theta/2$, so
>
> $$
> \Phi_m = \tfrac12 B r^2\theta, \qquad \lvert\mathcal E\rvert = \tfrac12 B r^2\omega, \qquad I = \frac{Br^2\omega}{2R}.
> $$
>
> [[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^thm-a9-3-2|Theorem §A9.3.2]] gives the same: a point at distance $s$ from $O$ moves at $\omega s$, and $\int_0^r B\omega s\,ds = \tfrac12 B\omega r^2$. The outward flux grows, so the current is clockwise seen from above. Energy: the element $ds$ at distance $s$ feels the force $IB\,ds$ and the torque $IBs\,ds$, in total $\tau = \tfrac12 IBr^2$; the power needed is $\tau\omega = B^2r^4\omega^2/(4R) = I^2R$ ([[§A6.4 Work and Power in Rotation#^thm-a6-4-3|CM Theorem §A6.4.3]]).
>
> With $r = 1\ \mathrm m$, $B = 0.1\ \mathrm T$ and $R = 1\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$, delivering $100\ \mathrm W$ needs $\lvert\mathcal E\rvert = \sqrt{PR} = 316\ \mathrm V$, so $\omega = 2\lvert\mathcal E\rvert/(Br^2) = 6.3\times10^3\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, about $1000$ revolutions per second. A single rod is a poor generator; many turns of a coil do far better ([[§A9.6 Generators, Back EMF and Applications#^thm-a9-6-1|Theorem §A9.6.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 13.5; PHY 182, TBL 22*

^ex-a9-3-2

> [!example] Example §A9.3.3: A Moving Rod with No Circuit
> A rod moves perpendicular to its length and to $\vec B$, but nothing closes the circuit. The magnetic force pushes charges toward one end until the electric field $E$ of the separated charges balances it, $qE = qvB$. There is then no current, but a potential difference $\Delta V = El = Blv$ between the ends: Faraday's law still applies to an imagined loop through the rod.
> - A $1.0\ \mathrm m$ rod moved at $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ across Earth's field of about $5.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm T$: $\Delta V = 150\ \mu\mathrm V$, unnoticeable.
> - A $20.0\ \mathrm{km}$ tether moving at orbital speed $7.80\ \mathrm{km/s}$ perpendicular to the same field: $\Delta V = (5.00\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm T)(2.00\times10^4\ \mathrm m)(7.80\times10^3\ \mathrm{m/s}) = 7.80\ \mathrm{kV}$. The shuttle tether experiments of 1992 and 1996 expected about $5\ \mathrm{kV}$, less because the motion was not perpendicular to the field; the ionosphere was to close the circuit.
> - An aeroplane's wings are such a rod. Near the north magnetic pole the field points down; for a plane flying north, $\vec v\times\vec B$ points west, so the left wing charges positive. Over Antarctica the field points up and the right wing charges positive. At the equator the field is horizontal and northward: flying north, $\vec v\parallel\vec B$ and there is no emf; flying east, $\vec v\times\vec B$ is vertical, across the wings rather than along them, and again there is no wingtip-to-wingtip emf.
>
> *Source: UP2 §13.3 and Example 13.4; PHY 182, TBL 22*

^ex-a9-3-3
