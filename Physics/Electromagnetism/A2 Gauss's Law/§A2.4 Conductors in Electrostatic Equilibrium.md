So far the charges sat fixed in insulators. In a conductor they are free to move ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^def-a1-1-3|Def. §A1.1.3]]), and they keep moving until the forces on them vanish. This section asks what that final state looks like. One idea, that the field inside the metal is zero, combined with Gauss's law gives the main results: where the charge sits on a conductor, what field it makes at the surface, and what happens to charge placed in a hollow conductor.

## The ideal conductor

> [!definition] Definition §A2.4.1: Electrostatic Equilibrium
> A conductor is in **electrostatic equilibrium** when no charge in it is moving (on average). After charge is added or an external field is applied, the free charges rearrange and reach this state very quickly.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.4*

^def-a2-4-1

> [!model] Model §A2.4.1: Ideal Conductor
> An **ideal conductor** contains an unlimited supply of charges that are completely free to move. Any force on them, however small, moves them.
>
> - *Assumes:* enough conduction electrons to cancel any field that is applied; enough time for them to settle into equilibrium.
> - *Fails:* while charge is still flowing. In a wire carrying a steady current there is a field inside the metal ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-1|Law §A5.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.4; PHY 182, week 5 slides*

^mod-a2-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.4.2: No Field inside a Conductor
> In electrostatic equilibrium the electric field is zero at every point inside the material of a conductor:
>
> $$
> \vec E = \vec 0 \quad\text{(inside the conductor)}.
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.4, eq. (6.13)*

^thm-a2-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Suppose $\vec E \ne \vec 0$ at some point inside the metal. The free electrons there feel the force $-e\vec E$ ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^def-a1-3-1|Def. §A1.3.1]]) and, being free, accelerate. Charge would then be moving, which contradicts equilibrium. Hence $\vec E = \vec 0$.

^der-a2-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^def-a1-3-1|Def. §A1.3.1]], [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^def-a2-4-1|Def. §A2.4.1]], [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^mod-a2-4-1|Model §A2.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: How the field gets cancelled
> Put a piece of metal in an external field $\vec E_{\text{applied}}$. Each conduction electron is pushed against the field and runs "upstream" until it reaches the surface, where it can go no farther. The supply is unlimited, so electrons keep piling up on that side, leaving positive charge on the other. These induced surface charges make their own field, which inside the metal points against $\vec E_{\text{applied}}$. The flow stops only when the induced field cancels the applied one everywhere inside. The metal is polarized, and in the total field the lines end on the induced negative charge and start again from the induced positive charge.

^rem-a2-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.4.3: Charge Resides on the Surface
> In electrostatic equilibrium a conductor has no net charge in its interior: $\rho = 0$ inside the metal. Any excess charge lies on its surfaces.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.4*

^thm-a2-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take any closed surface lying wholly inside the metal, for instance one just beneath the outer surface, or a tiny sphere around an interior point. By [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]], $\vec E = \vec 0$ at every point of it, so its flux is zero. By Gauss's law it encloses no net charge. Since this holds for arbitrarily small surfaces around every interior point, the interior density vanishes. The excess charge can only be on the surface.

^der-a2-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]], [[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Charge goes to the outside
> Lower a charged body into a cavity in a metal and touch it to the inner wall. All its charge ends up on the *outer* surface. Before contact, the inner wall carries induced charge of the opposite sign ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-5|Theorem §A2.4.5]]). At contact the two neutralize, and the matching induced charge on the outer surface is what remains.

^rem-a2-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: A precision test of Coulomb's law
> Theorem §A2.4.3 rests on Gauss's law, which holds only for an exactly inverse-square force ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^cau-a2-2-1|§A2.2]]). Plimpton and Lawton (1936) used this. They connected two concentric metal shells through a sensitive electrometer and charged the outer one. If the force went as $1/r^{2+\delta}$ with $\delta \ne 0$, some charge would flow to the inner shell. None was detected, which gave $|\delta| < 2 \times 10^{-9}$. Later experiments (1971) pushed this to $|\delta| < 3 \times 10^{-16}$.

^rem-a2-4-3

## The field at the surface

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.4.4: Field at the Surface of a Conductor
> Just outside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, the field is perpendicular to the surface, with
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\,\hat n ,
> $$
>
> where $\sigma$ is the local surface charge density and $\hat n$ the outward normal. Where $\sigma < 0$ the field points into the surface.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.4, eq. (6.14)*

^thm-a2-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Direction.* A component of $\vec E$ along the surface would push the surface charges along it, and they are free to move. In equilibrium there is none, so $\vec E$ is normal to the surface.
>
> *Magnitude.* Take a small pillbox of cap area $\Delta A$ straddling the surface, one cap just inside the metal and one just outside, with its sides along the normal. The inner cap is in the metal, where $\vec E = \vec 0$, so it has no flux. On the sides, the field is either zero (inside) or along the side wall (outside), so they have no flux either. The outer cap contributes $E_n\,\Delta A$. The enclosed charge is $\sigma\,\Delta A$, so Gauss's law gives $E_n = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$.

^der-a2-4-4

*Uses:* [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]], [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]], [[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]

![[ph-em-a2-4-2.svg]]
*A neutral conducting sphere in a uniform field, with field lines computed from the exact solution (a level-B result). Induced negative charge gathers on the upstream side and positive charge on the downstream side. The lines end and restart on these charges, meeting the surface at right angles. Inside, the field is zero.*

> [!caution] Caution: $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ or $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$?
> An isolated charged sheet produces $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ on *each* side ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-9|Theorem §A1.4.9]]). A conductor surface produces $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ outside and $0$ inside. There is no conflict. Close to the surface, the local patch of charge acts like a sheet: $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ outward on the outside and $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ inward on the inside. All the *other* charges on and outside the conductor arrange themselves to cancel the inward part in the metal, so near the patch they supply another $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ outward, on both sides. The sum is $0$ inside and $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ outside. The surface field $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ is produced by all the charges, not by the local patch alone. Also, $\sigma$ means the charge per unit area on the face in question, which for a plate is not the plate's total charge divided by its area.

^cau-a2-4-1

> [!example] Example §A2.4.1: A Charged Conducting Sphere
> An isolated conducting sphere of radius $R$ carries excess charge $q$. By [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]] the charge is on the surface, and by symmetry it is spread uniformly, $\sigma = q/4\pi R^2$. Apply Gauss's law on a concentric sphere of radius $r$, where $\vec E = E(r)\,\hat r$. For $r < R$ the sphere lies in the metal and encloses no charge. For $r > R$ it encloses $q$:
>
> $$
> \vec E = \vec 0 \quad (r < R), \qquad \vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat r \quad (r > R).
> $$
>
> Just outside, $E = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2 = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$, as [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-4|Theorem §A2.4.4]] requires. Outside, the sphere is indistinguishable from a point charge at its centre. Only inside does the difference show: zero field instead of $q/4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2$.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 6.11*

^ex-a2-4-1

> [!example] Example §A2.4.2: Parallel Conducting Plates
> Two large conducting plates carry equal and opposite charges. The charge sits on their facing surfaces with density of magnitude $\sigma = 6.81 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{C/m^2}$, and the plates are $6.50\ \mathrm{mm}$ apart. Between them, just outside either plate's inner face,
>
> $$
> E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{6.81 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{C/m^2}}{8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{C^2/N\,m^2}} = 7.69 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{N/C},
> $$
>
> directed from the positive to the negative plate. The separation does not enter. The same value follows from superposing two sheets of $\pm\sigma$ ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-10|Theorem §A1.4.10]]), each contributing $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$. This is the field of a parallel-plate capacitor ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^mod-a4-1-2|Model §A4.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 6.10*

^ex-a2-4-2

## Cavities

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.4.5: Charge in a Cavity
> Let a conductor with net charge $Q$ contain a cavity, and let a charge $q$ be placed inside the cavity, not touching the walls. In electrostatic equilibrium:
> - the cavity wall carries total induced charge $-q$;
> - the outer surface carries $Q + q$.
>
> If the cavity holds no charge, the net charge on its wall is zero. Gauss's law fixes these amounts but not how they are spread. The spread on the wall depends on where $q$ is. The spread on the outer surface does not, because the metal in between has no field. For an isolated spherical conductor the outer charge is uniform.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.4 (Figures 6.44–6.45); PHY 182, week 5 slides*

^thm-a2-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Draw a Gaussian surface inside the metal, surrounding the cavity. On it $\vec E = \vec 0$ ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]]), so it encloses zero net charge: $q$ plus the wall charge is zero, and the wall carries $-q$. The conductor's own charge is $Q$ in total ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]]). It lies only on surfaces ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]]), so the outer surface carries $Q - (-q) = Q + q$. That the outer distribution does not depend on the arrangement inside is the statement that, through the field-free metal, the outer charges feel only each other and outside charges. A full proof uses the uniqueness of electrostatic solutions (level B, Chapter B2, planned).

^der-a2-4-5

*Uses:* [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]], [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]], [[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]]

> [!example] Example §A2.4.3: A Charge Hidden in a Neutral Sphere
> A point charge $+q$ sits in a small spherical cavity placed off-centre inside a neutral conducting sphere of radius $R$. Where is the charge, and what is the field outside?
>
> By [[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-5|Theorem §A2.4.5]] with $Q = 0$, the cavity wall carries $-q$ and the outer surface carries $+q$. The outer charge does not "know" where the cavity is, and the outer surface is a sphere, so the charge spreads uniformly. Outside, by [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]],
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat r ,
> $$
>
> with $r$ measured from the centre of the *sphere*, not from the charge. The metal shows the outside world the total charge it contains and hides where that charge is. Had the conductor carried net charge $Q$ to begin with, the outside field would be that of $Q + q$ at the centre.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 5 slides*

^ex-a2-4-3

![[ph-em-a2-4-1.svg]]
*Example §A2.4.3 with the cavity centred on $q$, so that the $-q$ on the cavity wall is uniform. Inside the cavity the field is that of $q$. In the metal (gray) it is zero. Outside it is radial from the centre of the sphere, produced by the uniform $+q$ (red) on the outer surface.*

> [!remark] Remark: Shielding
> PHY 182 puts it this way: conductors hide any complicated geometry inside them, but not the total charge. The converse holds too. A conductor with an empty cavity has no net charge on the cavity wall (Theorem §A2.4.5). In fact it has no charge on the wall at all, and no field in the cavity, whatever charges sit outside. Proving that needs the electric potential ([[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-5|Theorem §A3.5.5]]). This is why sensitive equipment, and rooms where secret communications are handled, are enclosed in metal.

^rem-a2-4-4

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The surface of a conductor in equilibrium is an equipotential, and the field just outside is the potential's normal derivative: [[§A3.5 Equipotentials and Conductors#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]. Conductors with opposite charges make capacitors: [[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]].
> - At the upper level, conductors become boundary conditions for Laplace's equation, solved by uniqueness arguments and the method of images (Chapter B2, planned). The sphere in a uniform field drawn above is a standard example there.
