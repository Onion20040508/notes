Field lines start and end on charges ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^rem-a1-3-2|§A1.3]]). Counting the lines that leave a closed surface should therefore reveal the charge inside. Making that count precise needs one new quantity, the flux of the field through a surface, which this section defines. The next section, [[§A2.2 Gauss's Law]], turns the counting into a law.

## Flux through a flat surface

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.1: Area Vector
> A flat surface of area $A$ has the **area vector** $\vec A = A\,\hat n$. Its magnitude is the area, and its direction is a unit normal $\hat n$, perpendicular to the surface. An open surface has two possible normals. Either may be chosen, but the same side must be used over the whole surface. For a closed surface (one that encloses a volume), $\hat n$ is always the **outward** normal.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.1*

^def-a2-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.2: Electric Flux of a Uniform Field
> The **electric flux** of a uniform field $\vec E$ through a flat surface with area vector $\vec A$ is
>
> $$
> \Phi = \vec E \cdot \vec A = EA\cos\theta ,
> $$
>
> where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec E$ and the normal $\hat n$. Flux is a scalar with unit $\mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C}$. It is positive when the field crosses the surface along $\hat n$ and negative when it crosses against $\hat n$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.1, eq. (6.1)*

^def-a2-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Flux counts field lines
> - Let $N$ field lines cross a surface $S_1$ of area $A_1$ held perpendicular to a uniform field. Line density is proportional to field strength ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^def-a1-3-3|Def. §A1.3.3]]), so $N/A_1 \propto E$ and $N \propto EA_1 = \Phi$. Tilt the surface to $S_2$ so that its normal makes the angle $\theta$ with $\vec E$, keeping the same outline as seen along the field. The same $N$ lines cross it, and its area is $A_2 = A_1/\cos\theta$. So $\Phi = EA_1 = EA_2\cos\theta$, which is the definition. Flux is the number of lines crossing, counted with sign.
> - Picture a hoop in a river. The water through it depends on the current's strength, the hoop's size, and its tilt. A hoop turned edge-on catches nothing. For a fluid of speed $v$ through a perpendicular area $A$, the volume flow rate $Q = Av$ ([[§A9.4 Flow and Continuity#^def-a9-4-2|CM Def. §A9.4.2]]) is the flux of the velocity field. For the electric field nothing actually flows, but the geometry is the same.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.1; PHY 182, week 4 slides*

^rem-a2-1-1

The same lines cross both, so the fluxes are equal. $S_2$ is larger by $1/\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec E$ and its normal $\hat n_2$ (red).*

> [!caution] Caution: Measure $\theta$ from the normal
> In $EA\cos\theta$ the angle is between $\vec E$ and the *normal*, not between $\vec E$ and the surface. A field lying along the surface ($\theta = 90^\circ$) gives zero flux. A field perpendicular to the surface ($\theta = 0$) gives the full $EA$.

^cau-a2-1-1

> [!example] Example §A2.1.1: Flux through a Tilted Plane
> A uniform field of magnitude $E = 10\ \mathrm{N/C}$ is parallel to the $yz$ plane and points $30^\circ$ above the $xy$ plane, so $\vec E = E(\cos 30^\circ\,\hat\jmath + \sin 30^\circ\,\hat k)$. A flat surface of area $A = 6.0\ \mathrm{m^2}$ lies in the $xz$ plane with $\hat n = \hat\jmath$. The angle between $\vec E$ and $\hat n$ is $30^\circ$, so
>
> $$
> \Phi = EA\cos 30^\circ = (10\ \mathrm{N/C})(6.0\ \mathrm{m^2})(0.866) = 52\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C} .
> $$
>
> Only the component of $\vec E$ along the normal, $E_y = E\cos 30^\circ$, contributes. The component in the plane of the surface, $E_z$, contributes nothing. A rectangle in the $xy$ plane would catch the other component, and a field lying in the plane of the surface would give zero flux.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 6.3 (and Example 6.1)*

^ex-a2-1-1

## Flux through any surface

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.3: Electric Flux through a Surface
> For a curved surface $S$, or a field that varies over it, divide $S$ into small patches. Each patch is nearly flat, with area $dA$ and unit normal $\hat n$, and the field over it is nearly uniform. The flux is the sum of the patch fluxes, in the limit a surface integral:
>
> $$
> \Phi = \int_S \vec E \cdot \hat n\,dA = \int_S \vec E \cdot d\vec A \quad\text{(open surface)},
> \qquad
> \Phi = \oint_S \vec E \cdot \hat n\,dA \quad\text{(closed surface, } \hat n \text{ outward)}.
> $$
>
> On a closed surface, lines leaving the enclosed volume contribute positive flux and lines entering contribute negative flux.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.1, eqs. (6.2)–(6.3)*

^def-a2-1-3

> [!example] Example §A2.1.2: A Non-uniform Field
> The field $\vec E = c\,y^2\,\hat k$ crosses a rectangle in the $xy$ plane with $0 \le x \le b$ and $0 \le y \le a$. Take $\hat n = \hat k$. The field is constant along strips parallel to the $x$ axis, so use strips of area $dA = b\,dy$:
>
> $$
> \Phi = \int_S \vec E \cdot \hat n\,dA = \int_0^a c\,y^2\,b\,dy = \tfrac13\,a^3 b\,c .
> $$
>
> Choosing the element so that the field is constant on it turns the surface integral into a one-variable integral.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 6.4*

^ex-a2-1-2

> [!example] Example §A2.1.3: A Uniform Field through a Closed Cube
> A uniform field $E_0\,\hat k$ passes through a cube of face area $A$ with faces perpendicular to the axes. Through the top face $\hat n = \hat k$, giving $\Phi = +E_0 A$. Through the bottom face $\hat n = -\hat k$, giving $-E_0 A$. On the four side faces $\vec E \perp \hat n$, giving $0$. The net flux is zero. Every line that enters the cube also leaves it, because there is no charge inside for a line to end on.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 6.2*

^ex-a2-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: What a closed surface measures
> PHY 182 pictures a lawn sprinkler, a source of water. Any closed surface around the sprinkler, of any size or shape, has the same total outflow. A closed surface that does not contain the sprinkler has as much water flowing in as flowing out, so its net flux is zero. A drain inside would give negative flux. The net flux through a *closed* surface thus reports the net source inside it. An open surface has no inside, and its flux says nothing of the kind. For the electric field the sources are charges, and [[§A2.2 Gauss's Law]] gives the exact relation.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 4 slides*
