[[§A2.1 Electric Flux]] showed that a closed surface with no charge inside has zero net flux: every line that enters also leaves. This section answers the question that follows: what is the flux when there *is* charge inside? The answer, Gauss's law, is that the flux equals the enclosed charge divided by $\varepsilon_0$, whatever the shape of the surface and wherever the charges are. It is derived here from Coulomb's law and superposition, and it holds precisely because the force falls off as $1/r^2$.

## The flux of a point charge

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.1: Flux of a Point Charge through a Sphere
> The flux of the field of a point charge $q$ through any sphere centred on it is
>
> $$
> \Phi = \oint_S \vec E \cdot \hat n\,dA = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0} ,
> $$
>
> independent of the sphere's radius.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.2, eq. (6.4)*

^thm-a2-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> On a sphere of radius $R$ centred on $q$ the outward normal is $\hat n = \hat r$. The field ([[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^thm-a1-3-1|Theorem §A1.3.1]]) is $\vec E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2}\hat r$, the same at every point of the sphere. So $\vec E\cdot\hat n = q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2)$ is constant and comes out of the integral:
>
> $$
> \Phi = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2}\oint_S dA = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2}\,4\pi R^2 = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0} .
> $$
>
> The $1/R^2$ of the field cancels the $R^2$ of the area.

^der-a2-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^thm-a1-3-1|Theorem §A1.3.1]], [[§A2.1 Electric Flux#^def-a2-1-3|Def. §A2.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: The same lines cross every sphere
> In field-line terms, every line from $q$ that pierces a sphere of radius $R_1$ also pierces any larger concentric sphere $R_2$, in the same outward direction. The count, and so the flux, is the same. Nothing about this argument needs the surface to be a sphere, which is the idea behind the general law.

^rem-a2-2-1

## Gauss's law

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.1: Gaussian Surface; Enclosed Charge
> A **Gaussian surface** is any closed surface, real or imaginary, chosen to apply Gauss's law. The **enclosed charge** $q_{\text{enc}}$ is the net charge inside it: the algebraic sum of the point charges inside, or the integral of the charge density over the enclosed volume.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.2*

^def-a2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.2: Gauss's Law
> For any closed surface $S$ and any distribution of charges,
>
> $$
> \Phi = \oint_S \vec E \cdot \hat n\,dA = \frac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0} .
> $$
>
> Here $\vec E$ is the *total* field at each point of $S$, produced by the charges inside and outside, and $q_{\text{enc}}$ is the net charge *inside* $S$ only.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.2, eq. (6.5)*

^thm-a2-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *A sketch at the level of this course.* The book argues by counting field lines. The solid-angle version below makes that count quantitative.
>
> 1. *Solid angle.* A small patch $dA$ at distance $r$ from a point, with its normal $\hat n$ at angle $\alpha$ to the line of sight $\hat r$, appears from the point like a patch of area $dA\cos\alpha$ on a sphere of radius $r$. The **solid angle** it subtends is that area divided by $r^2$: $d\Omega = (\hat r\cdot\hat n)\,dA/r^2$. It is positive if the patch faces away from the point and negative if it faces toward it. The full sphere of directions has $\Omega = 4\pi$.
> 2. *One charge, one patch.* By [[§A1.3 The Electric Field#^thm-a1-3-1|Theorem §A1.3.1]] the flux of a point charge $q$ through the patch is
>    $$
>    d\Phi = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\hat r\cdot\hat n\,dA}{r^2} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,d\Omega .
>    $$
>    Because the field falls off as exactly $1/r^2$, the flux through a patch depends only on the solid angle it fills, not on its distance or tilt.
> 3. *Charge inside.* Every ray from $q$ ends up outside $S$, so it crosses $S$ an odd number of times: once, or three times (out, in, out), and so on. Each in-crossing cancels an out-crossing, since $\hat r\cdot\hat n$ changes sign there, which leaves one net outward crossing per direction. Summed over all directions, the solid angles add to the whole sphere, $4\pi$, so $\Phi = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,4\pi = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}$.
> 4. *Charge outside.* Every ray from an outside charge that meets $S$ crosses it once inward and once outward (or an even number of times in all). The contributions cancel in pairs, so $\Phi = 0$.
> 5. *Many charges.* By superposition the total field is $\vec E = \sum_i \vec E_i$ (for a continuous distribution, an integral). The flux integral is linear, so $\Phi = \sum_i \Phi_i$. Each inside charge contributes $q_i/\varepsilon_0$, and each outside charge contributes $0$.
>
> The sketch assumes a surface regular enough for solid angles to make sense, with no charge sitting exactly on it. The general proof writes the law as $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ and uses the divergence theorem. That is level-B material (Chapter B1, planned).

> [!remark] Remark: What the law does and does not say
> - The flux depends only on the enclosed charge. Any two closed surfaces that enclose the same charges have the same flux, whatever their shapes and sizes.
> - Charges outside $S$ change the field *on* $S$, but they add nothing to the net flux.
> - The law always holds, but it gives only one number, an integral over the surface. On its own it does not determine $\vec E$. In a messy arrangement the flux is known, but the field still varies over the surface in an unknown way. To extract $\vec E$, the symmetry must fix its direction and keep its magnitude constant over a suitable surface ([[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law]]).

^rem-a2-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Why $k = 1/4\pi\varepsilon_0$
> Written with Coulomb's constant, the law reads $\Phi = 4\pi k\,q_{\text{enc}}$. The $4\pi$, the solid angle of a full sphere, appears whenever a point source spreads over spheres. Writing $k = 1/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ in Coulomb's law moves the $4\pi$ out of Gauss's law. The same construction for gravity, with $-4\pi G$ in place of $1/\varepsilon_0$, gives the shell theorem ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|CM Theorem §A8.1.5]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, week 4 slides*

^rem-a2-2-3

> [!caution] Caution: Closed surfaces, signs, inverse squares
> - The surface must be closed: Gauss's law says nothing about the flux through an open surface.
> - With the outward normal, a negative enclosed charge gives negative flux, with net field lines going in.
> - Step 2 of the derivation needs the exact $1/r^2$. With any other power the flux through a sphere would depend on its radius. Gauss's law is therefore an exact test of the inverse-square law ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^rem-a2-4-3|§A2.4]]).

^cau-a2-2-1

> [!example] Example §A2.2.1: Flux from the Enclosed Charge Alone
> (a) A closed surface encloses a single charge of $2.0\ \mu\mathrm C$: $\Phi = (2.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm C)/(8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{C^2/N\,m^2}) = 2.3 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C}$.
>
> (b) A surface encloses $-4.0\ \mu\mathrm C$, $+6.0\ \mu\mathrm C$ and $-1.0\ \mu\mathrm C$. Then $q_{\text{enc}} = +1.0\ \mu\mathrm C$ and $\Phi = 1.1 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C}$.
>
> (c) A surface encloses $+4.0$, $+6.0$ and $-10.0\ \mu\mathrm C$. Then $q_{\text{enc}} = 0$ and $\Phi = 0$, although the field on the surface is not zero: the inward and outward fluxes balance.
>
> The shape of the surface never entered. For a closed surface the flux calculation reduces to adding charges.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 6.5*

^ex-a2-2-1

> [!example] Example §A2.2.2: A Charge at the Corner of a Cube
> A point charge $q$ sits at one corner of a cube. What is the flux through one of the three faces that do not touch that corner?
>
> Gauss's law needs a closed surface around the charge, so build one. Eight such cubes fit around the corner and enclose $q$ completely. By symmetry each cube gets the same flux, $q/8\varepsilon_0$. In one cube, the three faces that meet at the charge contain the radial field lines, so $\vec E \perp \hat n$ there and their flux is zero. The remaining three faces are equivalent by symmetry, so each carries
>
> $$
> \Phi_{\text{face}} = \frac13\cdot\frac{q}{8\varepsilon_0} = \frac{q}{24\varepsilon_0} .
> $$
>
> No integral was needed: symmetry plus Gauss's law fixed the answer.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, HW 4*

^ex-a2-2-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - By the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|Theorem §18.2]]), $\oint_S \vec E\cdot\hat n\,dA = \int_V \nabla\cdot\vec E\,dV$ for every volume $V$. Gauss's law is therefore equivalent to the local form $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, the first of Maxwell's equations ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves|§A12.1]]; differential form in Chapter B1, planned).
> - The gravitational version gives the shell theorem and the field inside a uniform planet ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|CM Theorem §A8.1.5]], [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-6|CM Theorem §A8.1.6]]).
> - The point-charge field $\hat r/r^2 = -\nabla(1/r)$ has zero divergence away from the origin, because $1/r$ solves Laplace's equation there. It is the radial fundamental solution of [[§19 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^rem-19-2|§19]], and the area $4\pi r^2$ noted there is the reason the flux does not depend on the radius.
