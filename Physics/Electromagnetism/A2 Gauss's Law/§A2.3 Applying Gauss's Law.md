Gauss's law ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]) is a statement about flux, a single number. When the charge distribution is symmetric enough, that number determines the field. This section shows how, for the three symmetries where it works: spherical, cylindrical and planar. It recovers the fields of [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions]] without integrating over the charge, and it handles distributions with volume, inside and outside, which the direct integration made laborious.

## Symmetry and strategy

> [!definition] Definition §A2.3.1: Spherical, Cylindrical and Planar Symmetry
> A charge distribution has
> - **spherical symmetry** if its density depends only on the distance $r$ from a centre: $\rho = \rho(r)$;
> - **cylindrical symmetry** if it is infinitely long and its density depends only on the distance $r$ from an axis, not on the position along the axis or the direction around it;
> - **planar symmetry** if it is infinite in two directions and its density depends only on the distance $z$ from a plane. For the method below it must also be mirror-symmetric about that plane: $\rho(-z) = \rho(z)$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.3*

^def-a2-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Symmetry of the charge, not of the object
> A sphere whose upper half is charged differently from its lower half has no spherical symmetry: the density depends on direction. A sphere made of concentric shells of different densities does have it. A finite cylinder or a square plate has none of the three symmetries, because the density changes at the ends and edges. For long cylinders and large plates the infinite idealisations ([[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^mod-a1-4-4|Model §A1.4.4]], [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^mod-a1-4-8|Model §A1.4.8]]) are good near the middle.

^cau-a2-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Strategy
> 1. Identify the symmetry. Then decide from it the direction of $\vec E$ and what its magnitude can depend on.
> 2. Choose a Gaussian surface that matches. Over each part of the surface, either $\vec E \parallel \hat n$ with constant magnitude, giving flux $EA$, or $\vec E \perp \hat n$, giving flux $0$.
> 3. Compute the flux in terms of the unknown $E$.
> 4. Compute $q_{\text{enc}}$. This is usually the hard part. Is it all the charge or only some? For a non-uniform density, integrate shells: $dq = \rho(r')\,4\pi r'^2\,dr'$ (sphere), $\rho(r')\,2\pi r' L\,dr'$ (cylinder), $\rho(z')\,A\,dz'$ (slab).
> 5. Solve for $E$.
>
> Keep the radius $r$ of the Gaussian surface (where the field is wanted) distinct from the radius $R$ of the charged object, and from the integration variable $r'$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.3; PHY 182, week 4–5 slides*

^rem-a2-3-1

![[ph-em-a2-3-1.svg]]
*The Gaussian surface for each symmetry. Red arrows show the field on the surface. On a sphere it is normal everywhere. On a coaxial cylinder it is normal on the curved side and lies along the end caps. On a pillbox straddling a plane it is normal on the two caps and lies along the side. In each case the flux is $E$ times the area of the parts where the field is normal.*

## Spherical symmetry

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.3.1: Field of a Spherically Symmetric Distribution
> A spherically symmetric distribution produces a radial field
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q_{\text{enc}}(r)}{r^2}\,\hat r,
> \qquad
> q_{\text{enc}}(r) = \int_0^r \rho(r')\,4\pi r'^2\,dr' ,
> $$
>
> where $q_{\text{enc}}(r)$ is the charge within radius $r$. In particular:
> - outside the distribution, the field is that of a point charge equal to the total charge, placed at the centre;
> - inside a hollow spherical shell with no charge within it, the field is zero.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.3, eqs. (6.7)–(6.10)*

^thm-a2-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Symmetry.* Rotating the distribution about any axis through the centre leaves it unchanged, so it must leave the field unchanged. Rotations about the line from the centre $O$ to a point $P$ keep $P$ fixed. A field component at $P$ perpendicular to $OP$ would be turned by them, so there is none, and $\vec E$ is radial. Rotations can carry $P$ to any other point at the same distance, so the radial component depends on $r$ alone: $\vec E = E(r)\,\hat r$.
>
> *Flux.* On a concentric sphere of radius $r$, $\hat n = \hat r$, and $E(r)$ is constant, so $\Phi = E(r)\,4\pi r^2$.
>
> *Gauss.* $E(r)\,4\pi r^2 = q_{\text{enc}}(r)/\varepsilon_0$. The charge between $r'$ and $r' + dr'$ fills a shell of volume $4\pi r'^2\,dr'$, which gives the integral. Outside, $q_{\text{enc}}$ is the total charge. In the empty interior of a shell, $q_{\text{enc}} = 0$.

^der-a2-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^def-a2-3-1|Def. §A2.3.1]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-6|Example §15.6]] (spherical coordinates: shell volume $4\pi r'^2\,dr'$)

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.3.2: Uniformly Charged Sphere
> A sphere of radius $R$ with uniform density $\rho_0$ and total charge $Q = \frac43\pi R^3\rho_0$ produces the radial field of magnitude
>
> $$
> E(r) = \frac{\rho_0\,r}{3\varepsilon_0} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q\,r}{R^3}\quad (r \le R),
> \qquad
> E(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q}{r^2}\quad (r \ge R).
> $$
>
> The field grows linearly from zero at the centre to its maximum $\rho_0 R/3\varepsilon_0$ at the surface, then falls as $1/r^2$.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 6.6*

^thm-a2-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $r \le R$, $q_{\text{enc}} = \rho_0\cdot\frac43\pi r^3$, so [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]] gives $E = \frac{\rho_0 \frac43\pi r^3}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} = \frac{\rho_0 r}{3\varepsilon_0}$. For $r \ge R$, $q_{\text{enc}} = Q$. The two expressions agree at $r = R$. Inside, the enclosed charge grows as $r^3$ while the area grows as $r^2$, so the field grows as $r$.

^der-a2-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]

> [!example] Example §A2.3.1: A Non-uniformly Charged Sphere
> An insulating sphere of radius $R$ has density $\rho(r) = a\,r^n$ ($n \ge 0$). Find the field inside and outside.
>
> The distribution is spherically symmetric, so only $q_{\text{enc}}$ is needed:
>
> $$
> q_{\text{enc}}(r) = \int_0^{r} a\,r'^n\,4\pi r'^2\,dr' = \frac{4\pi a}{n+3}\,r^{n+3}\quad (r \le R),
> \qquad q_{\text{tot}} = \frac{4\pi a}{n+3}\,R^{n+3}.
> $$
>
> By [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]],
>
> $$
> E_{\text{in}} = \frac{a}{(n+3)\,\varepsilon_0}\,r^{n+1},
> \qquad
> E_{\text{out}} = \frac{a\,R^{n+3}}{(n+3)\,\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{r^2} ,
> $$
>
> directed along $\hat r$ for $a > 0$. The two agree at $r = R$. For $n = 0$ this is the uniform sphere. PHY 182's $\rho = \rho_0 (r/R)^3$ is the case $n = 3$, $a = \rho_0/R^3$, with $E_{\text{in}} = \rho_0 r^4/6\varepsilon_0 R^3$. The more the charge is concentrated toward the surface, the more slowly the field builds up inside.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 6.7; PHY 182, TBL 6*

^ex-a2-3-1

## Cylindrical symmetry

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.3.3: Field of a Cylindrically Symmetric Distribution
> A cylindrically symmetric distribution produces a field perpendicular to the axis, of magnitude
>
> $$
> E(r) = \frac{\lambda_{\text{enc}}(r)}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,r} ,
> $$
>
> where $r$ is the distance from the axis and $\lambda_{\text{enc}}(r)$ is the charge per unit length within radius $r$. The field points away from the axis if $\lambda_{\text{enc}} > 0$. In particular:
> - a line with density $\lambda$: $E = \lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0 r$;
> - a solid cylinder of radius $R$ and uniform density $\rho$: $E = \rho r/2\varepsilon_0$ inside, $E = \rho R^2/2\varepsilon_0 r$ outside;
> - a cylindrical shell of radius $R$ and surface density $\sigma$: $E = 0$ inside, $E = R\sigma/\varepsilon_0 r$ outside.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.3 and Example 6.8; PHY 182, week 4 slides and TBL 7 (the solid cylinder)*

^thm-a2-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Symmetry.* The distribution is unchanged by rotations about the axis, by shifts along it, and by reflection in any plane perpendicular to it. So $\vec E$ has no component along the axis or around it, and its magnitude depends only on $r$.
>
> *Flux.* Take a coaxial cylinder of radius $r$ and length $L$. On the two end caps $\vec E$ lies in the surface, so their flux is zero. On the curved side $\vec E \parallel \hat n$ with constant magnitude, so $\Phi = E(r)\,2\pi r L$.
>
> *Gauss.* The enclosed charge is $\lambda_{\text{enc}} L$, so $E\,2\pi r L = \lambda_{\text{enc}} L/\varepsilon_0$, and $L$ cancels. For the special cases, $\lambda_{\text{enc}}$ is: $\lambda$ for a line; $\rho\pi r^2$ inside and $\rho\pi R^2$ outside a solid cylinder; $0$ inside and $2\pi R\sigma$ outside a shell.

^der-a2-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^def-a2-3-1|Def. §A2.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Two routes, one answer
> The line result agrees with the direct integration of [[§A1.4 Fields of Charge Distributions#^thm-a1-4-5|Theorem §A1.4.5]], which took a page. Gauss's law gets it in two lines, but only because the direction of the field was known in advance from symmetry. That knowledge is the price of the shortcut. Outside the shell, the field is that of a line of density $2\pi R\sigma$ on the axis.

^rem-a2-3-2

> [!example] Example §A2.3.2: A Rod inside a Coaxial Tube
> A long solid rod of radius $R_1$ is surrounded by a coaxial tube occupying $R_2 \le r \le R_3$, with empty space between. Both have the same uniform density $\rho > 0$. Find $E(r)$ everywhere.
>
> Only $\lambda_{\text{enc}}$ changes from region to region:
>
> | Region | $\lambda_{\text{enc}}$ | $E = \lambda_{\text{enc}}/2\pi\varepsilon_0 r$ |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | $r < R_1$ | $\rho\pi r^2$ | $\rho r/2\varepsilon_0$ |
> | $R_1 < r < R_2$ | $\rho\pi R_1^2$ | $\rho R_1^2/2\varepsilon_0 r$ |
> | $R_2 < r < R_3$ | $\rho\pi(R_1^2 + r^2 - R_2^2)$ | $\rho(R_1^2 + r^2 - R_2^2)/2\varepsilon_0 r$ |
> | $r > R_3$ | $\rho\pi(R_1^2 + R_3^2 - R_2^2)$ | $\rho(R_1^2 + R_3^2 - R_2^2)/2\varepsilon_0 r$ |
>
> All directed away from the axis. Each expression matches its neighbour at the boundaries, because the charge sits in volumes, not on surfaces. Outside, the arrangement acts like a line carrying its total charge per unit length.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 7*

^ex-a2-3-2

![[ph-em-a2-3-2.svg]]
*Example §A2.3.2 for $R_2 = 2R_1$, $R_3 = 3R_1$. The shaded bands are the charged regions. The field rises linearly in the rod, falls as $1/r$ across the empty gap, rises again through the tube as more charge is enclosed, and falls as $1/r$ outside.*

## Planar symmetry

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.3.4: Field of a Planar Distribution
> A distribution with planar symmetry about the plane $z = 0$ produces a field perpendicular to the plane. Its magnitude depends only on $|z|$:
>
> $$
> E(z) = \frac{\sigma_{\text{enc}}(z)}{2\varepsilon_0} ,
> $$
>
> where $\sigma_{\text{enc}}(z)$ is the charge per unit area between $-|z|$ and $|z|$. The field points away from the plane on both sides if $\sigma_{\text{enc}} > 0$. In particular:
> - a sheet of density $\sigma$: $E = \sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ on both sides, independent of distance;
> - a slab of thickness $t$ and uniform density $\rho$: $E = \rho|z|/\varepsilon_0$ inside ($|z| \le t/2$) and $E = \rho t/2\varepsilon_0$ outside.
>
> *Source: UP2 §6.3, eqs. (6.11)–(6.12); PHY 182, week 4 slides and TBL 8 (the slab)*

^thm-a2-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Symmetry.* The distribution is unchanged by shifts parallel to the plane and by rotations about any normal. So $\vec E$ is along the normal, with a magnitude that depends only on $z$. Reflection in $z = 0$ maps the distribution onto itself, so the field at $-z$ is the mirror image of the field at $z$: equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
>
> *Flux.* Take a pillbox (a cylinder of cap area $A$) from $-z$ to $z$. On the side, $\vec E$ lies in the surface and gives no flux. On the two caps, $\vec E$ points outward with the same magnitude, giving $\Phi = 2E(z)A$.
>
> *Gauss.* $2E(z)A = \sigma_{\text{enc}}A/\varepsilon_0$. For a sheet, $\sigma_{\text{enc}} = \sigma$. For a slab, $\sigma_{\text{enc}} = \rho\cdot 2|z|$ inside and $\rho t$ outside.

^der-a2-3-4

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^def-a2-3-1|Def. §A2.3.1]]

> [!example] Example §A2.3.3: A Thick Charged Plate
> A large plate $10\ \mathrm{cm}$ thick has uniform density $\rho = 1.0\ \mu\mathrm{C/m^3}$. Outside it,
>
> $$
> E = \frac{\rho t}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{(1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{C/m^3})(0.10\ \mathrm m)}{2(8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{C^2/N\,m^2})} = 5.6 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{N/C},
> $$
>
> pointing away from the plate on both sides, at any distance. Inside, $E_z = \rho z/\varepsilon_0$, measured from the midplane: zero in the middle, rising linearly to the same $5.6 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{N/C}$ at each face. A graph of $E_z$ against $z$ is a straight line through the origin inside the plate, joined to the constants $\pm\rho t/2\varepsilon_0$ outside. The pillbox must be centred on the midplane. Only then do both caps see the same field strength.
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 8*

^ex-a2-3-3

> [!remark] Remark: Continuity checks
> PHY 182 asks after every case whether the inside and outside formulas agree at the boundary. For charge spread through a volume they do: uniform sphere, coaxial rod, slab. For charge on a surface they do not. Across a spherical or cylindrical shell the field jumps from $0$ to $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$. Across a sheet, $E_z$ jumps from $-\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ to $+\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$, again by $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$. A jump of $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ in the normal component across a surface charge is the general rule (proved at level B, Chapter B1, planned). It reappears at conductor surfaces ([[§A2.4 Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 182, TBL 6–8*

^rem-a2-3-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The field inside a uniform charged sphere grows linearly with $r$, like gravity inside a uniform planet ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-6|CM Theorem §A8.1.6]]). Both follow from the inverse-square law through the same Gauss-type argument ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|CM Theorem §A8.1.5]]).
> - The shell integrals for $q_{\text{enc}}$ are volume integrals in spherical or cylindrical coordinates with the angles already done ([[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-6|Example §15.6]]).
> - The infinite-sheet and coaxial fields are the starting point for parallel-plate and cylindrical capacitors ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance|§A4.1]]).
