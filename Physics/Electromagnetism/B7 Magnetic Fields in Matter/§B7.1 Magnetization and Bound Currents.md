*Sources: Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (4th ed.), §6.1–6.2 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 17 · PHY 422 (Thamm, Spring 2024).*

What field does a magnetized body produce? Level A gave the picture: every atom carries tiny current loops, which a field lines up (paramagnets) or which respond against it (diamagnets), and the aligned loops of a sample in a solenoid add up to a current around its surface ([[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^rem-a8-6-1|§A8.6, Remark: Every atom is a set of current loops]], [[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^def-a8-6-1|Def. §A8.6.1]], [[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^mod-a8-6-2|Model §A8.6.2]]). This section runs exactly parallel to the electric case of [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge|§B5.1]]: polarization $\vec P$ becomes magnetization $\vec M$, the electric dipole potential becomes the vector potential of a magnetic dipole ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole|§B6.4]]), and bound charges $\vec P\cdot\hat n$ and $-\nabla\cdot\vec P$ become bound currents $\vec M\times\hat n$ and $\nabla\times\vec M$, now for a body of any shape and any magnetization. Level A's surface current becomes a theorem, and a classical model shows why diamagnetism opposes the field.

## How matter responds

> [!remark] Remark: Para-, dia- and ferromagnetism at the upper level
> - *Paramagnetism.* A magnetic dipole in a field feels the torque $\vec m\times\vec B$ (Griffiths eq. (6.1); [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole|§B6.4]]), the same form as $\vec p\times\vec E$, and it turns the dipole *parallel* to $\vec B$. Every electron is such a dipole through its spin ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^pr-a5-2-6|QM Principle §A5.2.6]], [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^def-b6-2-1|QM Def. §B6.2.1]]), but the Pauli principle ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|QM Principle §A5.3.2]]) locks the electrons of an atom in pairs with opposite spins, whose torques cancel. Paramagnetism therefore occurs mostly in atoms and molecules with an odd number of electrons, and thermal collisions keep the alignment far from complete.
> - *Force.* In a nonuniform field a dipole feels $\vec F = \nabla(\vec m\cdot\vec B)$ (eq. (6.3)). A paramagnet, magnetized along $\vec B$, is pulled into the strong-field region; a diamagnet is pushed out. For an induced moment $\vec m = \alpha_M\vec B$ the force is $\frac12\alpha_M\nabla(B^2)$ (Nelson §17.5.3), which can levitate a flake of graphite above a neodymium magnet, and a live frog in a strong enough field gradient (Griffiths, footnote 5). These forces are tiny: on a comparable sample of iron the force is $10^4$ to $10^5$ times greater (Griffiths §6.1.4), which is why the magnetization of a copper wire could be ignored in magnetostatics.
> - *Ferromagnetism* is not linear at all; it is the subject of [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media|§B7.2]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.1.1–6.1.2, §6.1.4 · Nelson, PHYS 5516 notes, ch. 17, §17.5.2–17.5.3*

^rem-b7-1-1

> [!model] Model §B7.1.1: Classical Orbiting Electron
> An electron (charge $-e$, mass $m_e$) moves on a circle of radius $R$ about the nucleus with speed $v$, held by the Coulomb attraction, $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{e^2}{R^2} = m_e\frac{v^2}{R}$ (Griffiths eq. (6.5)). Its period $2\pi R/v$ is so short that the orbit acts as a steady current loop $I = -ev/2\pi R$, with orbital dipole moment (eq. (6.4))
>
> $$
> \vec m = -\frac12evR\,\hat z ,
> $$
>
> for counterclockwise motion seen from $+\hat z$; this is $\vec m = -(e/2m_e)\vec L$ ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-1|QM Theorem §A5.2.1]]).
>
> - *Assumes:* classical circular orbit; a field $\vec B$ perpendicular to the orbit is switched on slowly, and the radius stays fixed while it is.
> - *Fails:* atoms are quantum systems, and "diamagnetism is really a quantum phenomenon"; Griffiths calls the classical model "fundamentally flawed" and uses it only for a qualitative account. Assuming fixed $v$ instead of fixed $R$ changes the result by a factor of 2.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.1.3, eqs. (6.4)–(6.5) and footnote 2*

^mod-b7-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.2: Diamagnetic Response of an Orbit
> In [[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^mod-b7-1-1|Model §B7.1.1]], switching on $\vec B$ perpendicular to the orbit changes the speed and the orbital moment by (Griffiths eqs. (6.7)–(6.8))
>
> $$
> \Delta v = \frac{eRB}{2m_e}, \qquad \Delta\vec m = -\frac{e^2R^2}{4m_e}\,\vec B ,
> $$
>
> the electron speeding up or slowing down depending on its sense of motion. In either case the change of the moment is **opposite** to $\vec B$. With orbits in random orientations the permanent orbital moments cancel, but the induced changes all point against the field: this is **diamagnetism**, present in every atom and usually masked by paramagnetism when the atom has a net permanent moment.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.1.3, eqs. (6.6)–(6.8)*

^thm-b7-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take $\vec B = B\hat z$ and the electron moving counterclockwise, $\vec v = v\hat\phi$. The magnetic force $-e\vec v\times\vec B = -evB\,\hat s$ points to the centre and adds to the Coulomb force, so at fixed $R$ the new speed $\bar v$ obeys $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{e^2}{R^2} + e\bar vB = m_e\frac{\bar v^2}{R}$ (eq. (6.6)). Subtracting eq. (6.5),
>
> $$
> e\bar vB = \frac{m_e}{R}(\bar v^2 - v^2) = \frac{m_e}{R}(\bar v + v)(\bar v - v) ,
> $$
>
> and for a small change, $\bar v + v \approx 2\bar v$, so $\Delta v = \bar v - v = eRB/2m_e$. The moment $-\frac12evR\hat z$ changes by $-\frac12e(\Delta v)R\hat z = -\frac{e^2R^2}{4m_e}B\hat z$. For clockwise motion the magnetic force points outward, the electron slows down, and its moment, now along $+\hat z$, shrinks: the change is again along $-\hat z$.

^der-b7-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^mod-b7-1-1|Model §B7.1.1]], [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: What speeds the electron up?
> Magnetic forces do no work ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]]), so $\vec B$ cannot change the electron's speed. While the field is being switched on, the changing $\vec B$ induces an electric field ([[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^pr-b8-2-1|Principle §B8.2.1]]), and that field does the work (Griffiths, footnote 3 and Problem 7.52, where the fixed-radius assumption is also justified for an atom at rest). The model's qualitative lesson is robust and empirical: induced moments oppose the field.

^cau-b7-1-1

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: How big is the diamagnetic susceptibility?
> With one electron per atom on an orbit of radius $R$ and $n$ atoms per volume, the induced magnetization is $n\,\Delta m = -ne^2R^2B/4m_e$, and with $B \approx \mu_0H$ the susceptibility of [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^def-b7-2-3|Def. §B7.2.3]] is $\chi_m \approx -\mu_0ne^2R^2/4m_e$. For copper ($n = 8.5\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$) and $R = 0.5$ Å this is
>
> $$
> \chi_m \approx -\frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(8.5\times10^{28})(1.6\times10^{-19})^2(0.5\times10^{-10})^2}{4(9.11\times10^{-31})} \approx -1.9\times10^{-6} ,
> $$
>
> the right order of magnitude next to the measured $-9.7\times10^{-6}$ (Griffiths Table 6.1). Randomly oriented orbits would lower the estimate by $\frac23$, and copper has 29 electrons per atom on orbits of many sizes, so no closer agreement is to be expected from a model that is wrong in principle (Griffiths Problem 6.19 asks for this comparison).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 6.19, Table 6.1 (the estimate is computed here)*

## Magnetization and bound currents

> [!definition] Definition §B7.1.1: Magnetization
> The **magnetization** of a material is its magnetic dipole moment per unit volume (Griffiths eq. (6.9)),
>
> $$
> \vec M = \frac{d\vec m}{d\tau} ,
> $$
>
> a macroscopic field in the same sense as $\vec P$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^def-b5-1-1|Def. §B5.1.1]]). Its SI unit is $\mathrm{A\cdot m^2/m^3} = \mathrm{A/m}$. A material with $\vec M \ne \vec 0$ is **magnetized**; how $\vec M$ arose (paramagnetism, diamagnetism, ferromagnetism) does not matter for the field it produces.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.1.4, eq. (6.9)*

^def-b7-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B7.1.2: Bound Currents
> For a body occupying the volume $\mathcal V$ with boundary $\mathcal S$ and magnetization $\vec M$, the **bound volume current density** and the **bound surface current density** are (Griffiths eqs. (6.13)–(6.14))
>
> $$
> \vec J_b = \nabla\times\vec M \quad\text{in } \mathcal V, \qquad \vec K_b = \vec M\times\hat n \quad\text{on } \mathcal S ,
> $$
>
> with $\hat n$ the outward unit normal.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.2.1, eqs. (6.13)–(6.14)*

^def-b7-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.3: Vector Potential of a Magnetized Object
> The vector potential produced by the magnetization is (Griffiths eqs. (6.11), (6.15))
>
> $$
> \vec A(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal V}\frac{\vec M(\vec r\,')\times\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2}\,d\tau'
> = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal V}\frac{\vec J_b(\vec r\,')}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau' + \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint_{\mathcal S}\frac{\vec K_b(\vec r\,')}{\mathcal R}\,da' .
> $$
>
> A magnetized body therefore produces exactly the field of its bound currents, which can be found like the field of any other current: Biot–Savart, Ampère's law, the vector potential ([[§B6.2 The Biot–Savart Law|§B6.2]]–[[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole|§B6.4]]). At points inside the material the same formula gives the macroscopic field, by the argument of [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]] (Griffiths §6.2.3 and Problem 6.11).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.2.1, eqs. (6.10)–(6.15); §6.2.3*

^thm-b7-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A point dipole $\vec m$ at $\vec r\,'$ has $\vec A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\vec m\times\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2$ (Griffiths eq. (6.10); [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole|§B6.4]]); with $\vec m = \vec M\,d\tau'$ and superposition this is the first form. With $\hat{\mathcal R}/\mathcal R^2 = \nabla'(1/\mathcal R)$ and product rule (v) of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], $\nabla'\times(\vec M/\mathcal R) = (\nabla'\times\vec M)/\mathcal R - \vec M\times\nabla'(1/\mathcal R)$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{\vec M\times\hat{\mathcal R}}{\mathcal R^2} = \frac{\nabla'\times\vec M}{\mathcal R} - \nabla'\times\Bigl(\frac{\vec M}{\mathcal R}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> For any differentiable $\vec v$, $\int_{\mathcal V}\nabla\times\vec v\,d\tau = -\oint_{\mathcal S}\vec v\times d\vec a$ (Griffiths Problem 1.61(b)): dot a constant vector $\vec c$ into it, use $\vec c\cdot(\nabla\times\vec v) = \nabla\cdot(\vec v\times\vec c)$ (product rule (iv)) and the divergence theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]), $\oint(\vec v\times\vec c)\cdot d\vec a = -\vec c\cdot\oint\vec v\times d\vec a$. Hence $-\int\nabla'\times(\vec M/\mathcal R)\,d\tau' = \oint(\vec M\times\hat n)\,da'/\mathcal R$ (eq. (6.12)), the surface term; the first term is the volume term.

> [!remark] Remark: Bound currents are real currents
> Picture a thin slab of uniformly magnetized material as a mosaic of tiny current loops all circulating the same way (Griffiths §6.2.2). Wherever two loops touch, their currents run in opposite directions and cancel; only at the edge is there no neighbour, and the whole slab is equivalent to one ribbon of current around its boundary. A loop of area $a$ and thickness $t$ has moment $m = Mat$, which is also $Ia$, so the edge carries $I = Mt$, i.e. $K_b = M$, directed as $\vec M\times\hat n$, and nothing flows on the top and bottom faces, where $\vec M \parallel \hat n$. If $\vec M$ varies, neighbouring loops no longer cancel: a larger $M_z$ at $y + dy$ than at $y$ leaves a net current $(\partial M_z/\partial y)\,dy\,dz$ along $x$, and with the analogous $-\partial M_y/\partial z$ this is $(J_b)_x = (\nabla\times\vec M)_x$. No charge travels more than an atomic distance, yet the bound current is a genuine macroscopic current with the field of any other. Level A's surface current ([[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^mod-a8-6-2|Model §A8.6.2]]) is the special case of uniform $\vec M$: a model there, a theorem here, valid for any shape and any magnetization.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.2.2, Figures 6.15–6.18*

^rem-b7-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.4: Bound Currents Are Conserved and Have No Net Flow
> 1. $\nabla\cdot\vec J_b = 0$: the bound current obeys the continuity equation of a steady current ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]).
> 2. The total bound current, volume and surface parts together, through any surface whose boundary curve lies outside the magnetized body, is zero.
>
> Part 2 is the magnetic counterpart of the vanishing total bound charge ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.2.2 (part 1); part 2 is the magnetic counterpart of Griffiths Problem 4.14, checked in PHY 422, HW 10, Problem 1*

^thm-b7-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* The divergence of a curl vanishes ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]). *2.* Let $\vec M$ taper to zero in a thin skin at the surface instead of dropping abruptly; then the surface current becomes part of $\vec J_b = \nabla\times\vec M$, as $\sigma_b$ became part of $\rho_b$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^cau-b5-1-1|§B5.1, Caution]]). For a surface $\mathcal S$ with boundary curve $\mathcal P$ outside the body, Stokes' theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]) gives $\int_{\mathcal S}(\nabla\times\vec M)\cdot d\vec a = \oint_{\mathcal P}\vec M\cdot d\vec l = 0$, because $\vec M = \vec 0$ on $\mathcal P$.

## The uniformly magnetized sphere

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.5: Field of a Uniformly Magnetized Sphere
> A sphere of radius $R$ with uniform magnetization $\vec M$ carries no volume bound current and the surface current $\vec K_b = M\sin\theta\,\hat\phi$ ($\theta$ from $\vec M$). Its field is **uniform** inside,
>
> $$
> \vec B = \frac23\mu_0\vec M \qquad (r < R) ,
> $$
>
> and outside it is exactly the field of a point dipole at the centre with $\vec m = \frac43\pi R^3\vec M$ (Griffiths eq. (6.16)).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.2.1, Example 6.1, eq. (6.16)*

^thm-b7-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\vec J_b = \nabla\times\vec M = \vec 0$, and $\vec K_b = \vec M\times\hat r = M\hat z\times\hat r = M\sin\theta\,\hat\phi$. A spherical shell of radius $R$ with surface charge $\sigma$ spinning with angular velocity $\vec\omega$ carries $\vec K = \sigma\vec v = \sigma\omega R\sin\theta\,\hat\phi$, the same distribution with $\sigma\omega R \to M$. Its field ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole|§B6.4]]; Griffiths Example 5.11, eqs. (5.68)–(5.70)) is uniform inside, $\vec B = \frac23\mu_0\sigma R\vec\omega$, and outside its vector potential $\frac{\mu_0R^4\omega\sigma}{3}\frac{\sin\theta}{r^2}\hat\phi$ is that of a dipole with $m = \frac43\pi R^4\sigma\omega$. Substituting $\sigma\omega R = M$ gives $\frac23\mu_0\vec M$ and $\vec m = \frac43\pi R^3\vec M$. (A second derivation, from the polarized sphere, is in [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]].)

^der-b7-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^thm-b7-1-3|Theorem §B7.1.3]], [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole|§B6.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Two-thirds against minus one-third
> Inside a uniformly polarized sphere $\vec E = -\vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ points *against* $\vec P$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]); inside a uniformly magnetized sphere $\vec B = +\frac23\mu_0\vec M$ points *along* $\vec M$. Outside, both are pure dipole fields. The same factors appear in the contact terms of the point-dipole fields (Griffiths, footnote 6; eqs. (3.106) and (5.94), [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole|§B4.2]], [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole|§B6.4]]): a perfect dipole is the limit $R \to 0$ of a polarized or magnetized sphere. The difference is physical. If magnetic dipoles were pairs of north and south "poles" (the Gilbert model) instead of current loops (the Ampère model), the interior field would be the electric one, $-\frac13\mu_0\vec M$, against $\vec M$. Outside the two models agree, as do torques and forces on stationary dipoles in fields from outside, which is why the pole picture is a useful shortcut; but there are no magnetic monopoles, and inside matter only the Ampère model is right (Griffiths §6.1.2, Problem 6.29).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.1.2, §6.2.1 and footnote 6; Problem 6.29*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B7.1.1: A Cylinder with Azimuthal Magnetization
> A long cylinder of radius $R$ on the $z$ axis carries the magnetization $\vec M = ks^2\,\hat\phi$, $k$ a constant (PHY 422, HW 10, Problem 1, numbered 5 in the 2023 typed solutions; Griffiths Problem 6.8). Find $\vec B$ inside and outside.
>
> *Bound currents* (curl in cylindrical coordinates, [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]]):
>
> $$
> \vec J_b = \nabla\times\vec M = \frac1s\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(sM_\phi)\,\hat z = \frac1s\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(ks^3)\,\hat z = 3ks\,\hat z, \qquad \vec K_b = \vec M\times\hat s\big|_{s = R} = kR^2\,\hat\phi\times\hat s = -kR^2\,\hat z .
> $$
>
> *Field.* All currents run along $\hat z$ and depend only on $s$, so, as for a straight wire, $\vec B = B_\phi(s)\,\hat\phi$ ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-2|Theorem §A8.4.2]]; [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B|§B6.3]]). Ampère's law on a circle of radius $s$:
>
> $$
> s < R:\quad 2\pi sB_\phi = \mu_0\int_0^s3ks'\,2\pi s'\,ds' = 2\pi\mu_0ks^3 \;\Longrightarrow\; \vec B = \mu_0ks^2\,\hat\phi = \mu_0\vec M ;
> $$
>
> $$
> s > R:\quad I_{\text{enc}} = 2\pi kR^3 + 2\pi R(-kR^2) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \vec B = \vec 0 .
> $$
>
> The surface current exactly cancels the volume current ([[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]] (2)), and the magnet has no field outside: its field lines are circles that never leave it. This agrees with the instructor's typed solution, which also notes that $\vec H = \vec B/\mu_0 - \vec M = \vec 0$; with $\vec H$ the problem takes one line ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^rem-b7-2-1|§B7.2, Remark: Using Ampère's law for H]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 10, Problem 1 (Magnetization) and its 2023 typed solution · Griffiths Problem 6.8*

> [!example] Example §B7.1.2: A Long Cylinder Magnetized along Its Axis
> An infinitely long cylinder carries a uniform magnetization $\vec M = M\hat z$ along its axis (Griffiths Problem 6.7). Then $\vec J_b = \vec 0$ and $\vec K_b = M\hat z\times\hat s = M\hat\phi$ on the side: a sheet of current going around the cylinder, exactly an ideal solenoid with $nI = M$ ([[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]]). So $\vec B = \mu_0\vec M$ inside and $\vec B = \vec 0$ outside. A short cylinder, a **bar magnet**, is likewise a short solenoid with the familiar dipole-like field (Griffiths Problem 6.9; [[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^rem-a8-6-4|§A8.6, Remark: Ferromagnets in practice]]); its $\vec B/\mu_0$ is the $\vec D$ of the bar electret drawn in [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^ex-b5-2-2|Example §B5.2.2]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 6.7, 6.9*
