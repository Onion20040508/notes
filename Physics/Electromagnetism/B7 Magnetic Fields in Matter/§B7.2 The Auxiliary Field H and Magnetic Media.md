*Sources: Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (4th ed.), §6.3–6.4 · PHY 422 (Thamm, Spring 2024) · McDonald, perfectly conducting cylinder note.*

Ampère's law, $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]), counts all current, and in matter part of it is bound current, which depends on the field it helps to produce. As $\vec D$ did for charge ([[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement|§B5.2]]), the field $\vec H$ separates the free current, which we control, from the bound current. Level A described magnetic media by a susceptibility measured in a filled solenoid, $\vec B_m = \chi\vec B_0$, and described ferromagnets by domains and hysteresis ([[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^def-a8-6-2|Def. §A8.6.2]], [[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^law-a8-6-1|Law §A8.6.1]], [[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^def-a8-6-3|Def. §A8.6.3]]). New here: $\vec H$ and Ampère's law for it, the divergence of $\vec H$, where the parallel with $\vec D$ breaks, boundary conditions, an exact electrostatic analogy for magnets, the local law $\vec M = \chi_m\vec H$ and its consequences (including a superconductor treated as $\chi_m = -1$), and ferromagnetism as the nonlinear, history-dependent case.

## The auxiliary field

> [!definition] Definition §B7.2.1: Free Current
> In magnetized matter the current density is split as (Griffiths eq. (6.17))
>
> $$
> \vec J = \vec J_b + \vec J_f ,
> $$
>
> where $\vec J_b = \nabla\times\vec M$ is the bound current of [[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]] and the **free current** $\vec J_f$ is everything else: current that involves actual transport of charge, in wires embedded in the material or through the material itself if it conducts. Likewise $\vec K_f$ for free surface current.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.3.1, eq. (6.17)*

^def-b7-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §B7.2.2: The Field H
> The **auxiliary field** is (Griffiths eq. (6.18))
>
> $$
> \vec H = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec B - \vec M ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec B$ the macroscopic magnetic field. Its unit is that of $\vec M$, A/m; outside matter $\vec H = \vec B/\mu_0$. $\vec H$ "has no sensible name: just call it H" (Griffiths). Many authors call $\vec H$ the magnetic field and $\vec B$ the flux density or magnetic induction; here, as in Griffiths, $\vec B$ is the magnetic field, because it is the fundamental quantity, the one in the Lorentz force ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.3.1, eq. (6.18) and footnote 7*

^def-b7-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.1: Ampère's Law for H
> In magnetostatics, with or without magnetized matter (Griffiths eqs. (6.19)–(6.20)),
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\vec H = \vec J_f , \qquad \oint_{\mathcal P}\vec H\cdot d\vec l = I_{f,\text{enc}} ,
> $$
>
> where $I_{f,\text{enc}}$ is the free current through the loop $\mathcal P$. The bound current does not appear. (With time-dependent fields, $\partial\vec D/\partial t$ is added: [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-5|Theorem §B8.4.5]].)
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.3.1, eqs. (6.19)–(6.20)*

^thm-b7-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Ampère's law for the total current ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]) reads $\frac{1}{\mu_0}\nabla\times\vec B = \vec J_f + \vec J_b = \vec J_f + \nabla\times\vec M$; collecting the curls, $\nabla\times\bigl(\frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec B - \vec M\bigr) = \vec J_f$. Stokes' theorem ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]) gives the integral form.

*Uses:* [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^def-b7-2-1|Def. §B7.2.1]], [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^def-b7-2-2|Def. §B7.2.2]], [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]], [[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Using Ampère's law for H
> - With cylindrical, plane, solenoidal or toroidal symmetry, $\vec H$ follows from the free current alone by the methods of level A ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-1|Theorem §A8.4.1]]). Griffiths' Example 6.2: a copper rod of radius $R$ carrying a uniformly distributed free current $I$ has $\vec H = \frac{Is}{2\pi R^2}\hat\phi$ inside and $\frac{I}{2\pi s}\hat\phi$ outside; outside, $\vec B = \mu_0\vec H$ exactly as for a nonmagnetic wire, while inside $\vec B$ needs $\vec M$, which is tiny for copper.
> - The cylinder of [[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^ex-b7-1-1|Example §B7.1.1]] (PHY 422, HW 10, Problem 1) has no free current and cylindrical symmetry, so $\vec H = \vec 0$ in one line and $\vec B = \mu_0\vec M$: inside $\mu_0ks^2\hat\phi$, outside zero. (Symmetry is essential here: see the Caution below.)
> - Why $\vec H$ is familiar in the laboratory and $\vec D$ is not: to build an electromagnet one runs a known current through a coil, which fixes $\oint\vec H\cdot d\vec l$, while $\vec B$ depends on the core material and even its history. For an electric field one sets a voltage, which fixes $\int\vec E\cdot d\vec l$, and $\vec D$ depends on the dielectric. Theoretically $\vec D$ and $\vec H$ are on an equal footing (Griffiths §6.3.1).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.3.1, Example 6.2, eqs. (6.21)–(6.22) · PHY 422, HW 10, Problem 1 (typed solution, eq. (32))*

## A deceptive parallel

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.2: Divergence of H; the Magnetic Scalar Potential
> In magnetostatics (Griffiths eq. (6.23))
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec H = -\nabla\cdot\vec M ,
> $$
>
> which is not zero in general. If there is no free current anywhere, $\vec H = -\nabla W$ with a **magnetic scalar potential** $W$ obeying $\nabla^2W = \nabla\cdot\vec M$ (Griffiths Problem 6.15); for a magnetized body of finite size, with $\vec H \to \vec 0$ at infinity,
>
> $$
> W(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\oint_{\mathcal S}\frac{\sigma_M}{\mathcal R}\,da' + \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathcal V}\frac{\rho_M}{\mathcal R}\,d\tau', \qquad \sigma_M = \vec M\cdot\hat n, \quad \rho_M = -\nabla\cdot\vec M ,
> $$
>
> so $\vec H$ is the "Coulomb field" of fictitious magnetic charges: the pole picture of a magnet.
>
> *Notation:* $W$ (Griffiths' letter, Problem 6.15) is here a magnetic scalar potential, measured in amperes, **not** the energy $W$ used everywhere else in these notes ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^cau-b2-3-1|§B2.3, Caution: Notation for energy]]); it has this meaning only in this section (this theorem, [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]] and [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^ex-b7-2-2|Example §B7.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.3.2, eq. (6.23); Problem 6.15*

^thm-b7-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]]), so $\nabla\cdot\vec H = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\nabla\cdot\vec B - \nabla\cdot\vec M = -\nabla\cdot\vec M$. With $\vec J_f = \vec 0$ everywhere, $\nabla\times\vec H = \vec 0$ on all of space ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]]), so $\vec H = -\nabla W$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]) and $-\nabla^2W = \nabla\cdot\vec H = -\nabla\cdot\vec M$. This is Poisson's equation $\nabla^2W = -\rho_M$, whose decaying solution is $\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\rho_M/\mathcal R\,d\tau'$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]); the jump of $\vec M$ at the surface contributes the delta-function layer $\sigma_M = \vec M\cdot\hat n$, exactly as $\sigma_b = \vec P\cdot\hat n$ did ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]], with $\vec P/\varepsilon_0 \to \vec M$).

^der-b7-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^def-b7-2-2|Def. §B7.2.2]], [[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]], [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]], [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: H is not the field of the free current
> $\nabla\times\vec H = \vec J_f$ looks like Ampère's law with $\mu_0\vec H$ for $\vec B$, but the curl alone does not fix a field; the divergence of $\vec H$ is $-\nabla\cdot\vec M$, not zero. A bar magnet (a short iron cylinder with permanent uniform magnetization along its axis) has no free current anywhere, yet $\vec H \ne \vec 0$: $\nabla\cdot\vec M$ is a delta layer on its two end faces. Concluding $\vec H = \vec 0$, hence $\vec B = \mu_0\vec M$ inside and $\vec B = \vec 0$ outside, is nonsense. Inside the magnet $\vec H$ points *against* $\vec M$. Only with the symmetries listed in [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^rem-b7-2-1|Remark: Using Ampère's law for H]] does $\nabla\cdot\vec M$ vanish automatically. Even in a linear medium, $\nabla\cdot\vec H = \vec B\cdot\nabla(1/\mu)$ is nonzero wherever $\mu$ changes (Griffiths, footnote 10).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.3.2 and §6.4.1, footnote 10; Problems 6.9, 6.14*

^cau-b7-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.3: Boundary Conditions with Magnetized Matter
> At a surface carrying free surface current $\vec K_f$, with $\hat n$ the unit normal from "below" to "above" (Griffiths eqs. (6.24)–(6.27)),
>
> $$
> H^\perp_{\text{above}} - H^\perp_{\text{below}} = -\bigl(M^\perp_{\text{above}} - M^\perp_{\text{below}}\bigr), \qquad \vec H^\parallel_{\text{above}} - \vec H^\parallel_{\text{below}} = \vec K_f\times\hat n ,
> $$
>
> together with the conditions on $\vec B$ ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]]), in which the *total* surface current $\vec K = \vec K_f + \vec K_b$ appears:
>
> $$
> B^\perp_{\text{above}} - B^\perp_{\text{below}} = 0, \qquad \vec B^\parallel_{\text{above}} - \vec B^\parallel_{\text{below}} = \mu_0\,\vec K\times\hat n .
> $$
>
> In particular, at a surface without free current, tangential $\vec H$ and normal $\vec B$ are continuous. The general conditions ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-6|Theorem §B8.4.6]], with medium 1 "above", medium 2 "below" and the same normal) show that the conditions on $\vec H^\parallel$ and $B^\perp$ hold unchanged for time-dependent fields; with $\vec K_f = \vec 0$ they are the magnetic part of [[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-1|WO Theorem §B9.1.1]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.3.3, eqs. (6.24)–(6.27)*

^thm-b7-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Normal.* A flat pillbox straddling the surface in $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ gives continuity of $B^\perp = \mu_0(H^\perp + M^\perp)$, which is the first $\vec H$ condition. *Tangential.* A thin rectangle straddling the surface, with its long sides along a tangent $\hat l$, in [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]]: as its width shrinks only the surface current crossing it survives, $I_{f,\text{enc}} = \vec K_f\cdot(\hat n\times\hat l)\,l = (\vec K_f\times\hat n)\cdot\hat l\,l$, so $(\vec H^\parallel_{\text{above}} - \vec H^\parallel_{\text{below}})\cdot\hat l = (\vec K_f\times\hat n)\cdot\hat l$ for every tangent $\hat l$. The $\vec B$ conditions are the same constructions with the total current.

## The electrostatic analogy

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.4: Electrostatic Analogue of a Magnet
> Where there is no free charge and no free current, the equations
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec D = 0, \quad \nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0, \quad \varepsilon_0\vec E = \vec D - \vec P \qquad\text{and}\qquad \nabla\cdot\vec B = 0, \quad \nabla\times\vec H = \vec 0, \quad \mu_0\vec H = \vec B - \mu_0\vec M
> $$
>
> have the same form under $\vec D \to \vec B$, $\vec E \to \vec H$, $\vec P \to \mu_0\vec M$, $\varepsilon_0 \to \mu_0$ (Griffiths Problem 6.25). Hence:
> 1. *Frozen-in sources.* If a polarization and a magnetization have the same profile, $\vec P = P_0\vec f(\vec r)$ and $\vec M = M_0\vec f(\vec r)$, with no free charge or current anywhere, then at every point
>
> $$
> \vec H = \frac{M_0}{P_0}\,\varepsilon_0\vec E, \qquad \vec B = \mu_0\frac{M_0}{P_0}\,\vec D .
> $$
>
> 2. *Linear media.* In regions of linear media without free sources, ($\vec E$, $\vec D$, $\varepsilon$) and ($\vec H$, $\vec B$, $\mu$) obey the same equations and the same interface conditions, so an electrostatic solution with applied field $\vec E_0$ becomes a magnetostatic one with applied $\vec H_0$ under $\vec E \to \vec H$, $\vec D \to \vec B$, $\varepsilon \to \mu$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 6.25 · PHY 422, Final formula sheet ("EM analogy") and HW 10, Problem 2*

^thm-b7-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\vec E = -\nabla V$ with $\nabla^2(\varepsilon_0V) = -\rho_b = \nabla\cdot\vec P$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]]), and $\vec H = -\nabla W$ with $\nabla^2W = \nabla\cdot\vec M$ ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-2|Theorem §B7.2.2]]). So $\varepsilon_0V/P_0$ and $W/M_0$ both solve $\nabla^2u = \nabla\cdot\vec f$ and vanish at infinity; that solution is unique ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]), so $W = (M_0/P_0)\,\varepsilon_0V$ and $\vec H = (M_0/P_0)\,\varepsilon_0\vec E$. Then $\vec B = \mu_0(\vec H + \vec M) = \mu_0(M_0/P_0)(\varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P) = \mu_0(M_0/P_0)\vec D$.
>
> *2.* The field equations correspond term by term, and so do the interface conditions without free sources: tangential $\vec E$ and normal $\vec D$ continuous ([[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]]) against tangential $\vec H$ and normal $\vec B$ continuous ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]]). Uniqueness for linear media ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-3|Theorem §B5.3.3]] (5), with $W$ and $\mu$ in place of $V$ and $\varepsilon$) makes the transcribed solution the solution.

*Uses:* [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-2|Theorem §B7.2.2]], [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]], [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]], [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]], [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-3|Theorem §B5.3.3]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the analogy gives for free
> - *Uniformly magnetized sphere.* The polarized sphere has $\varepsilon_0\vec E = -\vec P/3$ inside ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]), so $\vec H = -\vec M/3$ and $\vec B = \mu_0(\vec H + \vec M) = \frac23\mu_0\vec M$ ([[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]]) without any vector potential.
> - *Bar magnet and bar electret.* The lines of $\vec B/\mu_0$ of a bar magnet are the lines of $\vec D$ of the bar electret, and those of $\vec H$ are the lines of $\varepsilon_0\vec E$ ([[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^ex-b5-2-2|Example §B5.2.2]] and its figure): $\vec B$ circulates through the magnet and back outside, while $\vec H$ runs from the "north" end face to the "south" end face, against $\vec M$ inside.
> - *Linear sphere in a uniform field* (Griffiths Problem 6.18): from [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^thm-b5-3-4|Theorem §B5.3.4]], $\vec H = \frac{3}{\mu_r + 2}\vec H_0$ and $\vec B = \frac{3\mu_r}{\mu_r + 2}\vec B_0$ inside, with $\mu_r$ of [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^def-b7-2-3|Def. §B7.2.3]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 6.14, 6.18, 6.25*

^rem-b7-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Cavities in a magnet
> Inside a large piece of material with frozen-in $\vec M$, field $\vec B_0$ and $\vec H_0 = \vec B_0/\mu_0 - \vec M$, the field at the centre of a small cavity follows from the electric results of [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^ex-b5-2-3|Example §B5.2.3]] by Theorem §B7.2.4 (Griffiths Problem 6.13): a sphere gives $\vec B = \vec B_0 - \frac23\mu_0\vec M$; a long needle along $\vec M$ gives $\vec B = \mu_0\vec H_0$; a thin wafer across $\vec M$ gives $\vec B = \vec B_0$. A compass needle in a needle-shaped cavity measures $\vec H$, one in a wafer-shaped cavity measures $\vec B$. This is also why Griffiths' "grant proposal" to decide between current-loop and pole models of iron by measuring the field in a small cavity (Problem 6.29) cannot work: a cavity is a region without magnetization, where the two models give the same field, as they do everywhere outside magnetized matter ([[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^rem-b7-1-4|§B7.1, Remark: Two-thirds against minus one-third]]); what the compass needle reads depends on the cavity's shape, not on the model.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 6.13, 6.29*

## Linear media

> [!definition] Definition §B7.2.3: Magnetic Susceptibility; Permeability
> A **linear** magnetic medium is one in which (Griffiths eqs. (6.29)–(6.32))
>
> $$
> \vec M = \chi_m\vec H ,
> $$
>
> with a dimensionless **magnetic susceptibility** $\chi_m$. Then $\vec B = \mu_0(\vec H + \vec M) = \mu_0(1 + \chi_m)\vec H$, so
>
> $$
> \vec B = \mu\vec H, \qquad \mu = \mu_0(1 + \chi_m), \qquad \mu_r = 1 + \chi_m = \frac{\mu}{\mu_0} ,
> $$
>
> with $\mu$ the **permeability** and $\mu_r$ the **relative permeability**; vacuum has $\mu = \mu_0$, the permeability of free space. $\vec B = \mu\vec H$ is the magnetic constitutive relation.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.4.1, eqs. (6.29)–(6.32) and footnotes 8–9*

^def-b7-2-3

> [!law] Law §B7.2.5: Linear Magnetic Response
> In paramagnetic and diamagnetic materials the magnetization is sustained by the field and disappears with it, and it is proportional to it: $\vec M = \chi_m\vec H$ with a material constant $\chi_m$, positive for paramagnets and negative for diamagnets, and small (Griffiths Table 6.1, 1 atm, 20 °C):
>
> | Diamagnetic | $\chi_m$ | Paramagnetic | $\chi_m$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | bismuth | $-1.7\times10^{-4}$ | oxygen (O$_2$) | $1.7\times10^{-6}$ |
> | gold | $-3.4\times10^{-5}$ | sodium | $8.5\times10^{-6}$ |
> | copper | $-9.7\times10^{-6}$ | aluminium | $2.2\times10^{-5}$ |
> | water | $-9.0\times10^{-6}$ | platinum | $2.7\times10^{-4}$ |
> | hydrogen (H$_2$) | $-2.1\times10^{-9}$ | liquid oxygen ($-200$ °C) | $3.9\times10^{-3}$ |
>
> Level A's table ([[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^law-a8-6-1|Law §A8.6.1]], from University Physics) lists bismuth as $-1.7\times10^{-5}$, ten times smaller than Griffiths' value from the CRC Handbook used here; for copper and aluminium the two tables agree, for water to rounding.
>
> *Valid when:* the material is para- or diamagnetic, the field is not too strong (for a paramagnet, far from saturation: $mB \ll kT$ for atomic moments $m$), and the temperature is fixed: a paramagnet's $\chi_m$ grows as $1/T$ (Curie's law, [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^law-b6-2-10|TH Law §B6.2.10]]; for two-state moments $m$ at density $n$, $\chi_m \approx \mu_0nm^2/kT$ from [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^rem-b6-2-4|TH §B6.2, Remark: Curie's law derived]], using $B \approx \mu_0H$). It fails for ferromagnets ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^law-b7-2-8|Law §B7.2.8]]).
> *Source: Griffiths §6.4.1, eq. (6.29), Table 6.1*

^law-b7-2-5

> [!caution] Caution: Conventions that differ
> - *H, not B.* Consistency with $\vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E$ would suggest $\vec M = \chi_m\vec B/\mu_0$ (Griffiths eq. (6.28), "incorrect!"), but custom writes $\vec M$ in terms of $\vec H$, because experimenters control $\vec H$. For $|\chi_m| \ll 1$ the two differ only in second order.
> - *Level A's χ.* [[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^def-a8-6-2|Def. §A8.6.2]] defines $\chi$ by $\vec B_m = \chi\vec B_0$ in a long filled solenoid. There $\vec H = nI\hat z = \vec B_0/\mu_0$ and $\vec B = (1 + \chi_m)\vec B_0$ ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-6|Theorem §B7.2.6]]), so $\chi = \chi_m$, but only in that geometry: in a sphere, $\vec B - \vec B_0 \ne \chi_m\vec B_0$ ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^rem-b7-2-2|Remark: What the analogy gives for free]]). The same distinction as $\kappa = E_0/E$ versus $\varepsilon_r$ ([[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^cau-b5-3-1|§B5.3, Caution]]).
> - *Symbols.* Level A (University Physics) and Quantum Mechanics write $\mu$ for a magnetic dipole moment; Griffiths writes $\vec m$ ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^def-b6-4-3|Def. §B6.4.3]]), and $\mu$ is the permeability. Thermal physics' $M$ is the total moment of a sample, not the moment per volume.

^cau-b7-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.6: Fields in Linear Magnetic Media
> In a homogeneous linear medium:
> 1. The bound volume current is proportional to the free current (Griffiths eq. (6.33)), $\vec J_b = \chi_m\vec J_f$; unless free current flows through the material, all bound current is on its surface.
> 2. *Filled solenoid* (Griffiths Example 6.3). An infinite solenoid with $n$ turns per length and current $I$, filled with the medium, has
>
> $$
> \vec H = nI\,\hat z, \qquad \vec B = \mu_0(1 + \chi_m)nI\,\hat z = \mu nI\,\hat z, \qquad \vec K_b = \chi_mnI\,\hat\phi ,
> $$
>
>    the field enhanced by a paramagnetic and reduced by a diamagnetic core, the bound surface current running with $I$ ($\chi_m > 0$) or against it ($\chi_m < 0$).
> 3. At a boundary between media, $\nabla\cdot\vec M$ and $\nabla\cdot\vec H$ are not zero: $\vec M$ jumps (Griffiths Fig. 6.23).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.4.1, Example 6.3, eq. (6.33) and Figure 6.23*

^thm-b7-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\vec J_b = \nabla\times\vec M = \nabla\times(\chi_m\vec H) = \chi_m\nabla\times\vec H = \chi_m\vec J_f$ for constant $\chi_m$ ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]]). *2.* With solenoidal symmetry, Ampère's law for $\vec H$ on a rectangle with one long side inside and one outside, where $\vec H = \vec 0$, gives $\vec H = nI\hat z$ from the free current alone, as in [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]]; then $\vec B = \mu\vec H$, $\vec M = \chi_m\vec H$ and $\vec K_b = \vec M\times\hat s = \chi_mnI\,\hat z\times\hat s = \chi_mnI\,\hat\phi$. *3.* A pillbox straddling the end of a magnetized cylinder has $\oint\vec M\cdot d\vec a \ne 0$ because $\vec M = \vec 0$ on one side, so $\nabla\cdot\vec M$ cannot vanish everywhere inside it.

^der-b7-2-6

*Uses:* [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^def-b7-2-3|Def. §B7.2.3]], [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]], [[§A8.5 Solenoids and Toroids#^thm-a8-5-3|Theorem §A8.5.3]], [[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Level A's law, now a theorem
> [[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^law-a8-6-1|Law §A8.6.1]], $B = (1 + \chi)\mu_0nI = \mu nI$ in a filled solenoid, was an empirical statement about that one device. Here it is item 2 of Theorem §B7.2.6, a consequence of the local law $\vec M = \chi_m\vec H$ and Ampère's law for $\vec H$, and level A's surface current $I_m$ ([[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^mod-a8-6-2|Model §A8.6.2]]) is the bound current $\vec K_b = \chi_mnI\,\hat\phi$. In $\vec H$ language, level A's iron core ([[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^ex-a8-6-2|Example §A8.6.2]]) has $H = nI_0 = 400$ A/m, $\mu_0H = 5.0\times10^{-4}$ T and $B = 1.4$ T, so $B/\mu_0H = 2.8\times10^3$. But iron is not a linear medium, and that ratio changes with $H$ and with the history of the sample (below).

## Ferromagnetism

> [!model] Model §B7.2.7: Ferromagnet (Domain Picture)
> - *Exchange alignment.* As in a paramagnet, the dipoles are the spins of unpaired electrons; in a ferromagnet each dipole "likes" to point the same way as its neighbours, a coupling that is quantum mechanical in origin and so strong that it aligns virtually 100% of the unpaired spins against thermal agitation.
> - *Domains.* The alignment holds in patches, **domains**, each containing billions of dipoles; in an unmagnetized piece the domains point at random and their fields cancel ([[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^def-a8-6-3|Def. §A8.6.3]]).
> - *Wall motion.* An applied field cannot easily turn the dipoles inside a domain, but at a boundary between domains it tips the balance: domains along the field grow at the expense of the others. The process is not fully reversible. When one domain takes over, the material is **saturated**.
> - *Curie point.* Above a sharply defined temperature (770 °C for iron) thermal motion destroys the alignment and the material becomes paramagnetic; the change is a phase transition, with the magnetization as order parameter and its direction a spontaneously broken symmetry ([[§B9.3★ The Classification of Phase Transitions#^def-b9-3-3|TH Def. §B9.3.3]], [[§B9.3★ The Classification of Phase Transitions#^def-b9-3-4|TH Def. §B9.3.4]]).
> - *Assumes / fails:* the exchange coupling is put in by hand, not derived; the picture is qualitative and gives no formula for $\vec M(\vec H)$. A ferromagnet is "emphatically not linear": susceptibility and permeability, if quoted at all, mean the differential ratio $dM/dH$ (or $dB/dH$), itself a function of $H$ (Griffiths, footnote 11).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §6.4.2, Figures 6.25–6.26 and footnote 11*

^mod-b7-2-7

> [!law] Law §B7.2.8: Hysteresis
> Below its Curie point the magnetization of a ferromagnet is not determined by the present field but by its whole history. Magnetize an iron object with a coil, $H = nI$ for a long coil (Griffiths Figs. 6.27–6.28): as $I$ increases from zero, $M$ grows to saturation (point $b$); reducing $I$ to zero leaves a **remanent** magnetization ($c$: a permanent magnet); a reversed current is needed to bring $M$ back to zero ($d$); further reversal saturates the other way ($e$); switching off leaves the opposite remanence ($f$); a forward current brings $M$ to zero ($g$) and back to $b$. The closed curve is the **hysteresis loop**, usually plotted as $B$ against $\mu_0H$ (Fig. 6.29). Since $M \gg H$, $B = \mu_0(H + M) \approx \mu_0M$, and the vertical scale is about $10^4$ times the horizontal: $B \approx 1$ T for $\mu_0H$ of order $10^{-3}$ T. "A little current goes a long way, when you have ferromagnetic materials around."
>
> *Valid when:* below the Curie temperature, for slowly varying fields; the shape of the loop depends on the material and its treatment.
> *Source: Griffiths §6.4.2, Figures 6.27–6.29*

^law-b7-2-8

> [!remark] Remark: Where the electric–magnetic parallel holds, and where it breaks
> | Electric (B5) | Magnetic (B7) |
> |---|---|
> | polarization $\vec P$ | magnetization $\vec M$ |
> | $\sigma_b = \vec P\cdot\hat n$, $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\vec P$ | $\vec K_b = \vec M\times\hat n$, $\vec J_b = \nabla\times\vec M$ |
> | $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P$ | $\vec H = \vec B/\mu_0 - \vec M$ |
> | $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f$, but $\nabla\times\vec D = \nabla\times\vec P$ | $\nabla\times\vec H = \vec J_f$, but $\nabla\cdot\vec H = -\nabla\cdot\vec M$ |
> | $\vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E$, $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0(1 + \chi_e)$ | $\vec M = \chi_m\vec H$, $\mu = \mu_0(1 + \chi_m)$ |
> | uniform sphere: $\vec E = -\vec P/3\varepsilon_0$ inside | uniform sphere: $\vec B = \frac23\mu_0\vec M$ inside |
>
> Where it breaks:
> - *Which field is fundamental.* $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ are the fields that act on charges; $\vec D$ and $\vec H$ are bookkeeping. Yet in source-free regions the mathematics pairs $\vec E$ with $\vec H$ and $\vec D$ with $\vec B$ (Theorem §B7.2.4): $\vec H$ is not "the" magnetic field, and the free-source law is a divergence for $\vec D$ but a curl for $\vec H$.
> - *Signs.* Ordinary dielectrics all have $\chi_e > 0$ ($\varepsilon_r \ge 1$ in Griffiths Table 4.2); $\chi_m$ has either sign.
> - *Constitutive custom.* $\vec P$ is written in terms of $\vec E$, the fundamental field; $\vec M$ in terms of $\vec H$.
> - *Real versus fictitious sources.* Bound charge is genuine charge; the "magnetic charge" $-\nabla\cdot\vec M$ of Theorem §B7.2.2 is not, since there are no monopoles. The physical sources of $\vec B$ are the bound currents, which is why $\vec B$ inside a magnet points along $\vec M$ ([[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^rem-b7-1-4|§B7.1, Remark: Two-thirds against minus one-third]]).
> - *Nonlinearity.* Ferromagnetism, strongly nonlinear and hysteretic, is common (iron, nickel, cobalt). Its electric counterpart, permanent or ferroelectric polarization, is rare: "only very special materials … will hold a permanent electric polarization. That's why you can't buy electrets at the toy store" (Griffiths Problem 4.11).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B7.2.1: The Field outside a Capacitor as the Field of a Current Loop
> A parallel-plate capacitor has square plates $L\times L$ with charges $\pm Q$, a distance $d \ll L$ apart. Show that the electric field outside it is proportional to the magnetic field of a square $L\times L$ loop carrying a current $I$, and find the constant (PHY 422, HW 10, Problem 2, "Electromagnetic analog"; Problem 1 of the 2023 typed solutions).
>
> *Charges as polarization.* For $d \ll L$ the free charge is spread uniformly, $\sigma = \pm Q/L^2$ on the plates (neglecting its pile-up near the edges). Exactly the same charge is the bound charge of a slab of frozen-in polarization $\vec P = (Q/L^2)\hat z$ filling the gap, with $\hat z$ from the negative to the positive plate: $\sigma_b = \pm P$ on the plate faces, nothing on the sides, $\rho_b = 0$ ([[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]]). So $\vec E$ is the same everywhere.
>
> *Polarization as magnetization.* Give the same slab a uniform magnetization $\vec M = M\hat z$. By [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]], outside the slab $\vec B/\mu_0 = \vec H = (M/P)\,\varepsilon_0\vec E$. The magnetized slab's field is that of its bound current ([[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^thm-b7-1-3|Theorem §B7.1.3]]): $\vec J_b = \vec 0$, $\vec K_b = \vec M\times\hat n$ is zero on the top and bottom and equal to $M$ around the four sides, a ribbon of height $d$ carrying the total current $I_0 = Md$. For $d \ll L$ the ribbon is a square $L\times L$ loop.
>
> *Result.* Writing $\vec B_I(\vec r)$ for the field of the square loop with current $I$, so that $\vec B_{I_0} = (I_0/I)\vec B_I$,
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{P}{\varepsilon_0M}\frac{\vec B_{I_0}}{\mu_0} = \frac{Pd}{\varepsilon_0\mu_0}\frac{\vec B_I}{I} = \frac{V}{\mu_0I}\,\vec B_I, \qquad V = \frac{Qd}{\varepsilon_0L^2} ,
> $$
>
> with $V$ the voltage between the plates ([[§A4.1 Capacitors and Capacitance#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]]). Equivalently $\vec E = c^2\frac{Qd}{L^2I}\vec B_I$: the electric field outside equals the magnetic field of a loop carrying "$I = Qd/(\mu_0\varepsilon_0L^2) = V/\mu_0$", the instructor's form, which (as the typed solution notes in its footnotes) is not dimensionally a current; written as $E/B = V/\mu_0I$ the statement is dimensionally consistent. The constant has a direct reading: on a path from the positive plate around the edge to the negative plate, $\int\vec E\cdot d\vec l = V$, while for the loop the same path, closed through the thin gap, encircles the wire once and gives $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0I$ (Ampère's law). The correspondence holds outside the slab, at distances from the edges large compared with $d$; inside the gap it fails, because there $\vec M \ne \vec 0$ and $\vec B \ne \mu_0\vec H$.
>
> *Source: PHY 422, HW 10, Problem 2 (Electromagnetic analog) and its 2023 typed solution (Problem 1), eqs. (1)–(6)*

> [!example] Example §B7.2.2: A Magnetizable Cylinder in a Transverse Field; the Superconducting Limit
> An infinitely long cylinder of radius $a$ and permeability $\mu = \mu_r\mu_0$ lies along the $z$ axis in a field that far away is uniform, $\vec B_0 = \mu_0H_0\hat x$. There is no free current, so $\vec H = -\nabla W$ ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-2|Theorem §B7.2.2]]), and inside each homogeneous region $\nabla\cdot\vec H = \nabla\cdot\vec B/\mu = 0$ ([[§B6.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]]), so $\nabla^2W = 0$. In polar coordinates $(s, \phi)$ the separated solutions of the two-dimensional Laplace equation are $a_0 + b_0\ln s + \sum_n(a_ns^n + b_ns^{-n})\cos(n\phi + \alpha_n)$ (McDonald, eq. (2), who writes $(r, \theta)$ for the cylindrical $(s, \phi)$ used here; cylindrical separation: Mathematical Methods, planned). The far field $W \to -H_0s\cos\phi$ and regularity on the axis leave
>
> $$
> W_{\text{out}} = -H_0s\cos\phi + A\,\frac{\cos\phi}{s}, \qquad W_{\text{in}} = -C\,s\cos\phi .
> $$
>
> At $s = a$, with no free current ([[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]]): tangential $\vec H$ continuous means $W$ continuous, $-H_0a + A/a = -Ca$; normal $\vec B$ continuous means $\mu_0(H_0 + A/a^2) = \mu C$. Hence
>
> $$
> \vec H_{\text{in}} = \frac{2}{\mu_r + 1}\vec H_0, \qquad \vec B_{\text{in}} = \frac{2\mu_r}{\mu_r + 1}\vec B_0, \qquad \vec M = \frac{2(\mu_r - 1)}{\mu_r + 1}\vec H_0 ,
> $$
>
> uniform, and outside, with $\beta = (\mu_r - 1)/(\mu_r + 1)$,
>
> $$
> B_s = B_0\cos\phi\Bigl(1 + \beta\frac{a^2}{s^2}\Bigr), \qquad B_\phi = -B_0\sin\phi\Bigl(1 - \beta\frac{a^2}{s^2}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the applied field plus a two-dimensional dipole. The surface carries $\vec K_b = \vec M\times\hat s = 2\beta H_0\sin\phi\,\hat z$. Check by the electrostatic analogue: the cylinder's own $\vec H$ inside is $-\vec M/2$ (the $-\vec P/2\varepsilon_0$ of [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^ex-b5-1-2|Example §B5.1.2]], transcribed by Theorem §B7.2.4), so $\vec H_{\text{in}} = \vec H_0 - \chi_m\vec H_{\text{in}}/2$, the same result.
>
> *Limits.* For soft iron, $\mu_r \to \infty$: $\vec B_{\text{in}} \to 2\vec B_0$ and the field lines are drawn in. For $\mu_r \to 0$, i.e. $\chi_m = -1$, a perfect diamagnet: $\vec B_{\text{in}} = \vec 0$, $\beta = -1$,
>
> $$
> B_s = B_0\cos\phi\Bigl(1 - \frac{a^2}{s^2}\Bigr), \qquad B_\phi = -B_0\sin\phi\Bigl(1 + \frac{a^2}{s^2}\Bigr), \qquad \vec K = -\frac{2B_0}{\mu_0}\sin\phi\,\hat z ,
> $$
>
> which are McDonald's fields (his eqs. (10)–(11)) and surface current (eq. (13), $K_z = -\frac{c}{2\pi}B_0\sin\theta$ in Gaussian units) for a perfectly conducting cylinder that excludes the field. The outside field is tangent to the surface ($B_s = 0$ at $s = a$), as the perfect-conductor conditions require ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-7|Theorem §B8.4.7]]). The electric twin is McDonald's conducting cylinder in a uniform $\vec E_0$, $E_r = E_0\cos\theta(1 + a^2/r^2)$: a conductor is the limit $\chi_e \to \infty$ (field lines meet it at right angles), a superconductor the limit $\chi_m = -1$ (field lines graze it).
>
> *Caution.* The analogy describes the *fields*, not the mechanism: a superconductor's screening currents are free currents, not bound magnetization currents, and a perfect conductor excludes the field only if it was field-free when the field was applied ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^rem-b8-4-4|§B8.4, Remark: Perfect conductor or superconductor?]]).
>
> *Source: McDonald, perfectly conducting cylinder note, §2, eqs. (2)–(13) · Griffiths §6.3–6.4 (method), Problem 7.44 and footnote 30*
