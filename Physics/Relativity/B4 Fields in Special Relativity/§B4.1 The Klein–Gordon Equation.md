What is the simplest wave equation that is exactly Lorentz invariant, and what does it have to do with particles? This section is an optional bridge from relativistic particles to relativistic fields and, beyond this subject, to quantum field theory. It starts from the energy–momentum relation $E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]]), the mass shell of the four-momentum ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]; [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]]) and the de Broglie relations ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|QM Principle §A3.1.1]]). Two routes lead to the same equation, the Klein–Gordon equation: replacing $E$ and $\vec p$ by derivatives in the squared mass-shell relation, and the variational principle for a scalar field with the simplest Lorentz-invariant Lagrangian. Its plane waves have the dispersion relation of a particle of mass $m$, with a phase velocity above $c$ and a group velocity below it. The field-theory formalism (Noether's theorem for fields, the energy–momentum tensor, the Hamiltonian of a field) is in Quantum Field Theory C1 — [[§C1.11 Noether's Theorem|QFT §C1.11]], [[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum|QFT §C1.12]], [[§C1.10 Hamiltonian Field Theory|QFT §C1.10]] (also planned for Classical Mechanics level C1), and the quantization that turns the field's waves into particles is quantum field theory — [[§C2.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^mod-c2-2-1|QFT Model §C2.2.1]], [[§C2.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2-4-2|QFT Theorem §C2.4.2]] (its first step, the quantized real and charged Klein–Gordon fields, is in Quantum Mechanics level C — [[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^def-c13-1-1|QM Def. §C13.1.1]], [[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^thm-c13-1-7|QM Theorem §C13.1.7]]); this note is the vault's home of the Klein–Gordon equation for both. The field is written $\phi$; the rapidity of [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group|§B1.2]] is $\varphi$, and potentials are $V$, $\vec A$.

## From the mass shell to a wave equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.1: The Klein–Gordon Equation from the Energy–Momentum Relation
> Replace the four-momentum in the mass-shell relation $p_\mu p^\mu = m^2c^2$ by derivatives,
>
> $$
> p^\mu \to i\hbar\,\partial^\mu, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad E \to i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}, \quad \vec p \to -i\hbar\nabla ,
> $$
>
> acting on a function $\phi(x)$. The result is the **Klein–Gordon equation**
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\Box + \frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\Bigr)\phi = 0, \qquad \Box = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu = \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 ,
> $$
>
> explicitly $\dfrac{1}{c^2}\dfrac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2\phi + \dfrac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\,\phi = 0$. For $m = 0$ it is the wave equation $\Box\phi = 0$ with speed $c$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Klein-Gordon Equation", §"Connection to Energy-Momentum Relation" · the user's series, Part III, §4.4 (Remark: The square-root problem), §8.3*

^thm-b4-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $\partial^\mu = \eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu = (\partial_t/c, -\nabla)$ ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]), $p^0 = E/c \to i\hbar\,\partial_t/c$ gives $E \to i\hbar\,\partial_t$, and $p^i \to -i\hbar\,\partial_i$ gives $\vec p \to -i\hbar\nabla$, the momentum operator of quantum mechanics ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|QM Def. §B1.2.1]]). Then $p_\mu p^\mu \to (i\hbar)^2\partial_\mu\partial^\mu = -\hbar^2\Box$, and $p_\mu p^\mu\phi = m^2c^2\phi$ becomes $-\hbar^2\Box\phi = m^2c^2\phi$. In three-vector form this is $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4$ with the replacements: $-\hbar^2\partial_t^2\phi = (-\hbar^2c^2\nabla^2 + m^2c^4)\phi$; divide by $\hbar^2c^2$.

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]], [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]], [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|QM Def. §B1.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why square, and what it costs
> The Schrödinger equation is $E = p^2/2m + V$ with the same replacements ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|QM Principle §A4.1.1]]). The relativistic energy $E = \sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}$ would give the square root of a differential operator, which is non-local ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^rem-b3-1-7|§B3.1, Remark: The square-root problem]]). Squaring first gives a local equation, manifestly covariant ($\Box$ is a scalar operator, [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]]), but second order in time, and it admits $E = -\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}$ as well. Klein–Gordon was first tried as a relativistic wave equation for the electron and failed (the electron needs the Dirac equation, and spin); it was later reinterpreted as the equation of a field whose quanta are spin-0 particles, such as pions and the Higgs boson. That reinterpretation is quantum field theory; in this note $\phi$ is a classical field.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Physical Interpretation" (of the Klein–Gordon equation) · the user's series, Part III, §4.4 (Remark: The square-root problem)*

## The field as a variational problem

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.1: Lagrangian Density; Action of a Field
> A **field theory** of a real scalar field $\phi(x)$ is specified by a **Lagrangian density** $\mathcal L(\phi, \partial_\mu\phi)$, a function of the field and its first derivatives at the same event, and the **action**
>
> $$
> S[\phi] = \int\mathcal L\bigl(\phi, \partial_\mu\phi\bigr)\,d^3x\,dt = \frac1c\int\mathcal L\,d^4x, \qquad d^4x = dx^0\,dx^1\,dx^2\,dx^3 .
> $$
>
> Since $|\det\Lambda| = 1$ for every Lorentz transformation ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]), $d^4x$ is invariant, and $S$ is a Lorentz scalar exactly when $\mathcal L$ is a scalar field. $\mathcal L$ has the dimension of energy per volume.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §8.1, eq. (covariant action) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Lagrangian Formulation for Fields" · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §2.1.1, §2.2.5*

^def-b4-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.2: Covariant Euler–Lagrange Equation
> A field $\phi$ makes $S[\phi]$ stationary under all variations that vanish at the initial and final times and far away in space if and only if
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu\Bigl(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}\Bigr) - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi} = 0 .
> $$
>
> Here $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)$ carries an upper index; for a scalar $\mathcal L$ it is a four-vector field, and the equation is a scalar equation, the same in every inertial frame. Separated into time and space it reads $\partial_t\bigl(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\phi\bigr) + \sum_i\partial_i\bigl(\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_i\phi)\bigr) - \partial\mathcal L/\partial\phi = 0$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §8.1, eq. (covariant EL) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Lagrangian Formulation for Fields" · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §2.1.3*

^thm-b4-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $\phi \to \phi + \varepsilon\eta$ with $\eta$ smooth, zero at $t_1$, $t_2$ and outside a bounded region of space. To first order in $\varepsilon$, with the chain rule and $\partial_\mu(\phi + \varepsilon\eta) = \partial_\mu\phi + \varepsilon\,\partial_\mu\eta$,
>
> $$
> \delta S = \varepsilon\int\Bigl[\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi}\,\eta + \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}\,\partial_\mu\eta\Bigr]d^3x\,dt = \varepsilon\int\Bigl[\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi} - \partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}\Bigr]\eta\,d^3x\,dt + \varepsilon\int\partial_\mu\Bigl(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}\,\eta\Bigr)d^3x\,dt .
> $$
>
> In the last integral do, for each $\mu$, first the integration over $x^\mu$ (Fubini's theorem, [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-8|452 Thm. §15.8]]): by the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]) it gives the bracket at the ends, where $\eta = 0$. So $\delta S = 0$ for all such $\eta$ exactly when the first bracket vanishes everywhere, by the fundamental lemma ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|CM Lemma §B5.1.2]], whose proof works the same way in four variables). The derivative $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)$ is taken with respect to a lower-index quantity, so it transforms with an upper index, and its contraction with $\partial_\mu$ is a scalar ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]).

> [!model] Model §B4.1.3: Free Real Scalar Field
> The **free real scalar field** of mass parameter $m$ has the Lagrangian density
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = \tfrac12\,\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi - \tfrac12\Bigl(\frac{mc}{\hbar}\Bigr)^2\phi^2 = \frac{1}{2c^2}\Bigl(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\Bigr)^2 - \tfrac12|\nabla\phi|^2 - \tfrac12\Bigl(\frac{mc}{\hbar}\Bigr)^2\phi^2 .
> $$
>
> It is the most general density that is (i) a Lorentz scalar built from a real scalar field, (ii) of first order in derivatives, and (iii) at most quadratic in $\phi$, after a constant term is dropped, a linear term is removed by shifting $\phi$ by a constant, and the derivative term is normalized to $\tfrac12$ by rescaling $\phi$. With this normalization $\phi$ has the unit $\sqrt{\mathrm{J/m}}$.
>
> *Assumptions: classical real field; free (quadratic $\mathcal L$, hence linear field equation and superposition; cubic and higher terms would be interactions); no higher derivatives; flat spacetime.*
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §8.2, eqs. (KG Lagrangian), (KG 3+1) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The Simplest Relativistic Field" (SI form)*

^mod-b4-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: The building blocks, and why the signs are physics
> - *The catalogue.* A scalar $\mathcal L$ can contain $\phi^n$, $\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi$ (the only scalar from one gradient: contraction is the only way to make a scalar of a covector, [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]), products of these, and higher derivatives such as $\Box\phi$. Higher derivatives give higher-order field equations with energies unbounded below (stated in the series, not proved); terms beyond quadratic make the equation nonlinear, which is an interaction. What survives is a one-parameter family.
> - *The relative sign* of $(\partial_t\phi)^2$ and $|\nabla\phi|^2$ is the signature of the metric; it makes the field equation hyperbolic, a wave equation with propagation at most at $c$.
> - *The sign of the mass term* makes $\tfrac12(mc/\hbar)^2\phi^2$ a potential well. The opposite sign would put every long-wavelength mode on an inverted potential and make it grow exponentially (a tachyonic instability).
> - *A mattress, not a rope.* The string ([[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation|WO §B3.2]]) has $\mathcal L = \tfrac{\mu}{2}(\dot y^2 - v^2y'^2)$ and no $y^2$ term, because nothing pulls a bead back to the axis except its neighbours. Tie every bead to the axis by its own spring and the continuum limit acquires exactly a mass term: a medium whose elements oscillate even when their neighbours are held still. Phys505 meets the same structure in sound waves with a restoring term, $\omega^2 = v^2k^2 + \omega_0^2$, and calls it gapped, or massive (Phys505 §2.1.4).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §8.2 (Remark: What the mass term would mean for a string) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Requirements for Relativistic Invariance" · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §2.1.4*

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.4: The Klein–Gordon Equation from the Lagrangian
> The Euler–Lagrange equation ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]]) of the free scalar field ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^mod-b4-1-3|Model §B4.1.3]]) is the Klein–Gordon equation of [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]],
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\Box + \frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\Bigr)\phi = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §8.3, eq. (KG) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Deriving the Klein-Gordon Equation"*

^thm-b4-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write the kinetic term as $\tfrac12\eta^{\rho\sigma}\partial_\rho\phi\,\partial_\sigma\phi$ and differentiate with respect to $\partial_\mu\phi$, using $\partial(\partial_\rho\phi)/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi) = \delta^\mu_\rho$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)} = \tfrac12\eta^{\rho\sigma}\bigl(\delta^\mu_\rho\,\partial_\sigma\phi + \partial_\rho\phi\,\delta^\mu_\sigma\bigr) = \eta^{\mu\sigma}\partial_\sigma\phi = \partial^\mu\phi ,
> $$
>
> the two slots contributing equally since $\eta$ is symmetric. Also $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\phi = -(mc/\hbar)^2\phi$. The Euler–Lagrange equation is $\partial_\mu\partial^\mu\phi + (mc/\hbar)^2\phi = 0$, and $\partial_\mu\partial^\mu = \eta^{00}\partial_0^2 + \sum_i\eta^{ii}\partial_i^2 = c^{-2}\partial_t^2 - \nabla^2$.

^der-b4-1-4

## Plane waves and dispersion

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.5: Plane Waves and the Dispersion Relation
> Let $k^\mu = (\omega/c, \vec k)$, so that $k\cdot x = \omega t - \vec k\cdot\vec x$. The plane wave
>
> $$
> \phi(x) = \mathrm{Re}\bigl[\hat A\,e^{-ik\cdot x}\bigr] = \mathrm{Re}\bigl[\hat A\,e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega t)}\bigr], \qquad \hat A \in \mathbb C ,
> $$
>
> solves the Klein–Gordon equation if and only if $k_\mu k^\mu = (mc/\hbar)^2$, that is,
>
> $$
> \omega^2 = c^2|\vec k|^2 + \Bigl(\frac{mc^2}{\hbar}\Bigr)^2 .
> $$
>
> The phase $k\cdot x$ is a scalar and $k^\mu$ a four-vector, so a plane wave in one frame is a plane wave in every frame, with $k^\mu$ transformed as a four-vector ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-9|Theorem §B2.1.9]]). With $E = \hbar\omega$, $\vec p = \hbar\vec k$ ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|QM Principle §A3.1.1]]), i.e. $p^\mu = \hbar k^\mu$, the dispersion relation is the mass shell $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4$. For $m = 0$, $\omega = c|\vec k|$ and $k^\mu$ is null. Since the equation is linear, superpositions of such waves are again solutions.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Plane Wave Ansatz", §"Connection to Energy-Momentum Relation", §"Massless Limit" · the user's series, Part III, §8.4, eq. (KG dispersion)*

^thm-b4-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\partial_\mu e^{-ik\cdot x} = -ik_\mu e^{-ik\cdot x}$, since $k\cdot x = k_\nu x^\nu$ and $\partial_\mu x^\nu = \delta^\nu_\mu$. Hence $\Box e^{-ik\cdot x} = (-ik_\mu)(-ik^\mu)e^{-ik\cdot x} = -k\cdot k\,e^{-ik\cdot x}$, and
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\Box + \frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\Bigr)\hat A\,e^{-ik\cdot x} = \Bigl(-k\cdot k + \frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\Bigr)\hat A\,e^{-ik\cdot x} ,
> $$
>
> which vanishes for $\hat A \ne 0$ exactly when $k\cdot k = m^2c^2/\hbar^2$. The Klein–Gordon operator is real, so it commutes with taking the real part. With $k\cdot k = \omega^2/c^2 - |\vec k|^2$, multiply by $c^2$. The operator $i\hbar\,\partial^\mu$ of [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]] acting on $e^{-ik\cdot x}$ returns $\hbar k^\mu$, so the replacement rule and $p^\mu = \hbar k^\mu$ say the same thing. If $k\cdot x$ is the same number in all frames for all $x^\mu$, then $k_\mu$ transforms inversely to $x^\mu$, which makes $k^\mu$ a four-vector ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-9|Theorem §B2.1.9]]).

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.2: Reduced Compton Wavelength
> The **reduced Compton wavelength** of a particle of mass $m$ is
>
> $$
> \bar\lambda_C = \frac{\hbar}{mc} = \frac{\hbar c}{mc^2}, \qquad \frac{mc^2}{\hbar} = \frac{c}{\bar\lambda_C} ,
> $$
>
> so the dispersion relation reads $\omega^2 = c^2\bigl(|\vec k|^2 + \bar\lambda_C^{-2}\bigr)$. It is the Compton wavelength $h/mc$ of quantum mechanics ([[§A1.3 The Compton Effect#^def-a1-3-2|QM Def. §A1.3.2]]) divided by $2\pi$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The Simplest Relativistic Field" ($\lambda_C = \hbar/mc$) · the user's series, Part III, §8.4*

^def-b4-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.6: Phase and Group Velocity of Klein–Gordon Waves
> For $m > 0$ and $k = |\vec k|$, the phase and group velocities ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^def-b6-2-1|WO Def. §B6.2.1]]) are
>
> $$
> v_p = \frac{\omega}{k} = c\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{(k\bar\lambda_C)^2}} > c, \qquad v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{c^2k}{\omega} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{1 + (k\bar\lambda_C)^{-2}}} < c, \qquad v_pv_g = c^2 ,
> $$
>
> and $v_g = c^2p/E$ is the velocity of a particle of mass $m$ and momentum $p = \hbar k$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]]; [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]]). In the two limits
>
> $$
> k\bar\lambda_C \ll 1:\quad \omega \approx \frac{mc^2}{\hbar} + \frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}, \quad v_g \approx \frac{\hbar k}{m}; \qquad\qquad k\bar\lambda_C \gg 1:\quad \omega \approx ck, \quad v_p \approx v_g \approx c .
> $$
>
> The medium is dispersive, $d^2\omega/dk^2 = c^2(mc^2/\hbar)^2/\omega^3 > 0$, so wave packets spread; only for $m = 0$ do they keep their shape.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Massive Case", §"Massless Limit" · the user's series, Part III, §8.4 · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §2.4.3, eqs. (2.176)–(2.180)*

^thm-b4-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From $\omega = c\sqrt{k^2 + \bar\lambda_C^{-2}}$: $v_p = \omega/k = c\sqrt{1 + (k\bar\lambda_C)^{-2}}$, and $2\omega\,d\omega/dk = 2c^2k$ gives $v_g = c^2k/\omega$, so $v_pv_g = c^2$ and $v_g = c/\sqrt{1 + (k\bar\lambda_C)^{-2}}$; the two square roots exceed $1$ for $m > 0$. With $p = \hbar k$, $E = \hbar\omega$: $v_g = c^2p/E$. *Limits* by Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]): for $k\bar\lambda_C \ll 1$, $\omega = (c/\bar\lambda_C)\sqrt{1 + (k\bar\lambda_C)^2} \approx (c/\bar\lambda_C)\bigl(1 + \tfrac12k^2\bar\lambda_C^2\bigr) = mc^2/\hbar + \hbar k^2/2m$; for $k\bar\lambda_C \gg 1$, $\omega = ck\sqrt{1 + (k\bar\lambda_C)^{-2}} \approx ck$. *Curvature:* $d^2\omega/dk^2 = d(c^2k/\omega)/dk = c^2/\omega - c^2k\,v_g/\omega^2 = c^2(\omega^2 - c^2k^2)/\omega^3$, and $\omega^2 - c^2k^2 = (mc^2/\hbar)^2$. A nonzero curvature spreads packets ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^thm-b6-2-4|WO Theorem §B6.2.4]]).

> [!remark] Remark: What travels at which velocity
> A phase velocity above $c$ violates nothing: a single infinite plane wave carries no signal, since nothing distinguishes one crest from the next, and what carries energy and information is a packet, whose envelope moves at $v_g$ ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^thm-b6-2-3|WO Theorem §B6.2.3]], [[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^rem-b6-2-2|WO Remark: Faster than light?]]). Phys505 shows more directly that for a massive plane wave the ratio of energy flux to energy density is $v_g$, the velocity of energy flow (Phys505 §2.4.4; the energy density and flux of the field come from its energy–momentum tensor, Quantum Field Theory C1 — [[§C1.12 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy–Momentum and Angular Momentum#^ex-c1-12-1|QFT Example §C1.12.1]]; also planned for Classical Mechanics level C1). That $v_g$ is exactly the particle velocity $c^2p/E$ is the classical seed of the particle–wave correspondence. Light in a plasma has the same dispersion relation, with the plasma frequency in place of $mc^2/\hbar$ (WO Remark: Faster than light?). The question of what precisely can outrun light in a dispersive medium (the front velocity) is subtle; the series flags it without settling it.
>

> [!remark] Remark: Static solutions and the Yukawa potential
> A time-independent field obeys $(\nabla^2 - \bar\lambda_C^{-2})\phi = 0$. Phys505 notes that a local field theory allows only two forms of static interaction between point sources, the Coulomb $1/r$ and the Yukawa $e^{-\kappa r}/r$ (Phys505 §2.1.1, stated). For a point source of strength $g$ at the origin, $(\nabla^2 - \bar\lambda_C^{-2})\phi = -g\,\delta^3(\vec x)$, the solution that vanishes at infinity is
>
> $$
> \phi(r) = \frac{g}{4\pi}\,\frac{e^{-r/\bar\lambda_C}}{r} .
> $$
>
> *Check.* For $r > 0$, $\nabla^2(f/r) = f''/r$ ([[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^thm-b7-1-5|WO Theorem §B7.1.5]]), and $f = e^{-r/\bar\lambda_C}$ gives $f'' = f/\bar\lambda_C^2$. Near $r = 0$, $\phi = g/4\pi r + (\text{bounded})$, and $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\delta^3$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|EM Theorem §B1.2.2]]) supplies the source. For $m = 0$ this is the Coulomb potential; for $m > 0$ the field dies off over the range $\bar\lambda_C = \hbar/mc$, which is how the range of a force reveals the mass of its carrier ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^rem-a8-2-2|QM Remark: h or ħ; the Yukawa potential]]).
>
> *Source: Phys505 (Wells) notes, §2.1.1 (statement); solution worked here*

> [!caution] Caution: Signs of the exponent, and other conventions
> - These notes write plane waves as $e^{-ik\cdot x} = e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega t)}$, the convention of quantum mechanics ($e^{-iEt/\hbar}$) and of the optics chapters of Waves and Optics; the oscillation chapters WO B1–B7 use $e^{+i\omega t}$ ([[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^cau-b10-1-1|EM Caution: Complex fields, and Waves and Optics' two conventions]]). The real part is the same physical wave either way.
> - The user's PHY 505 notes write the ansatz as $\phi = Ae^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega t)} = Ae^{ik_\mu x^\mu}$ with $k^\mu = (\omega/c, \vec k)$. In the mostly-minus metric $k_\mu x^\mu = \omega t - \vec k\cdot\vec x$, so $e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega t)} = e^{-ik_\mu x^\mu}$; the second equality there has the sign of the exponent reversed. The dispersion relation is unaffected, since it depends on $k_\mu k^\mu$ only. The intermediate line "$k_\mu k^\mu = m^2$" there is in natural units; with $c$ and $\hbar$ it reads $k_\mu k^\mu = m^2c^2/\hbar^2$, as the boxed result $\omega^2 = c^2|\vec k|^2 + m^2c^4/\hbar^2$ correctly has.
> - $\Box = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu = c^{-2}\partial_t^2 - \nabla^2$ here; Griffiths and Electromagnetism write $\Box^2 = \nabla^2 - c^{-2}\partial_t^2$, the negative ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^def-b11-1-1|EM Def. §B11.1.1]]).
> - The user's series sets $c = \hbar = 1$, so its $m$ in $(\Box + m^2)\phi = 0$ is $mc/\hbar = 1/\bar\lambda_C$, an inverse length.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Plane Wave Ansatz" · the user's series, Part III, §8.3–8.4*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B4.1.1: The Pion Field
> The charged pion is a spin-0 particle, a quantum of a Klein–Gordon field, with $m_\pi c^2 = 139.6\ \mathrm{MeV}$. Find its reduced Compton wavelength and the frequency gap of its field, and the velocities of a wave packet of momentum $p = 200\ \mathrm{MeV}/c$.
>
> With $\hbar c = 197.3\ \mathrm{MeV\cdot fm}$ and $\hbar = 6.582\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{MeV\cdot s}$ ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^def-b4-1-2|Def. §B4.1.2]]),
>
> $$
> \bar\lambda_C = \frac{\hbar c}{m_\pi c^2} = \frac{197.3}{139.6}\ \mathrm{fm} = 1.41\ \mathrm{fm}, \qquad \frac{m_\pi c^2}{\hbar} = \frac{139.6\ \mathrm{MeV}}{6.582\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{MeV\cdot s}} = 2.12\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} .
> $$
>
> No wave of the field oscillates more slowly than this. The length $1.41\ \mathrm{fm}$ is the range of the Yukawa field of [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^rem-b4-1-4|Remark: Static solutions and the Yukawa potential]], comparable with the range of the nuclear force, which is how Yukawa predicted a carrier of about this mass ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^rem-a8-2-2|QM Remark: h or ħ; the Yukawa potential]]). For the packet, $k\bar\lambda_C = pc/m_\pi c^2 = 200/139.6 = 1.43$ and $E = \sqrt{200^2 + 139.6^2}\ \mathrm{MeV} = 243.9\ \mathrm{MeV}$, so by [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]
>
> $$
> \frac{v_g}{c} = \frac{pc}{E} = 0.820, \qquad \frac{v_p}{c} = \frac{E}{pc} = 1.22 .
> $$
>
> The packet moves at the speed of a pion with that momentum; its crests run through it $22\,\%$ faster than light. Neither limit of Theorem §B4.1.6 applies at $k\bar\lambda_C \approx 1.4$: the non-relativistic $v_g = \hbar k/m = p/m_\pi$ would give $1.43c$.
>
> *Source: worked here; pions as Klein–Gordon quanta: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Physical Interpretation"*
