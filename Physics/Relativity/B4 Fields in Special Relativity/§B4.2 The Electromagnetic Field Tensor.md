How do electric and magnetic fields look from a moving frame, and what about them do all observers agree on? Electromagnetism has Maxwell's equations as its principles ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|EM Principle §B8.4.2]]) and noted that they need no change to fit relativity, while what one observer calls a magnetic force another calls electric ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^rem-a12-1-7|EM Remark: Electric or magnetic depends on the observer]]); it found the field of a uniformly moving charge the long way, from retarded potentials ([[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-4|EM Theorem §B11.2.4]]), and left the transformation of the fields to this subject. [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle|§B3.2]] found that the variation of the coupling $-q\int A_\mu\,dx^\mu$ assembles an antisymmetric tensor $F_{\mu\nu}$ whose six components are $\vec E$ and $\vec B$. Here the tensor machinery of [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle|§B2.2]] is applied to it: its components and sign convention, the Lorentz transformation of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$, the two invariants $E^2 - c^2B^2$ and $\vec E\cdot\vec B$, the four-current, and Maxwell's equations as two tensor equations, from which their covariance is evident. The field of a moving charge then follows from Coulomb's field in two lines. Deriving Maxwell's equations from a Lagrangian, and the energy–momentum tensor of the field, are in Quantum Field Theory C1, in Heaviside–Lorentz natural units — [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^rem-c1a-7-2|QFT Remark: Electrodynamics as the template for field theory]], [[§C1b.8 Lorentz Symmetry꞉ Angular Momentum and the Symmetric Tensor#^ex-c1b-8-2|QFT Example §C1b.8.2]], and in SI units, with this note's conventions, in Electromagnetism C1 — [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^pr-c1-1-4|EM Principle §C1.1.4]], [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-2|EM Theorem §C1.2.2]], [[§C1.5 The Electromagnetic Energy–Momentum Tensor#^def-c1-5-1|EM Def. §C1.5.1]], [[§C1.5 The Electromagnetic Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c1-5-4|EM Theorem §C1.5.4]]; this note remains the vault's home of the field tensor and of the transformation of the fields. Conventions: $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1)$, $x^\mu = (ct, \vec x)$, $\partial_\mu = (\partial_t/c, \nabla)$, scalar potential $V$ as in Electromagnetism, $\epsilon_{ijk}$ the three-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol; $E_i$, $B_i$ are Cartesian components.

## The potential and the field tensor

> [!definition] Definition §B4.2.1: Four-Potential; Gauge Transformation
> The **four-potential** combines the scalar and vector potentials ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-1|EM Theorem §B11.1.1]]),
>
> $$
> A^\mu = \Bigl(\frac{V}{c}, \vec A\Bigr), \qquad A_\mu = \Bigl(\frac{V}{c}, -\vec A\Bigr), \qquad \vec E = -\nabla V - \frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t}, \quad \vec B = \nabla\times\vec A .
> $$
>
> A **gauge transformation** is $A_\mu \to A_\mu + \partial_\mu\chi$ for a smooth function $\chi$, i.e. $V \to V + \partial_t\chi$, $\vec A \to \vec A - \nabla\chi$ (Electromagnetism's $\lambda$ is $-\chi$: [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^cau-b3-2-2|§B3.2, Caution: Three ways to write a gauge transformation]]). It changes neither $\vec E$ nor $\vec B$. The **Lorenz gauge** is $\partial_\mu A^\mu = \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$ ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^def-b11-1-1|EM Def. §B11.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The Four-Potential" · Griffiths §12.3.5, eqs. (12.131)–(12.132), (12.135)–(12.136) · the user's series, Part III, §11.1, §13.1*

^def-b4-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: In what sense the potential is a four-vector
> In the Lorenz gauge $A^\mu$ obeys $\Box A^\mu = \mu_0J^\mu$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-7|Theorem §B4.2.7]]) with the four-vector source $J^\mu$ and the scalar operator $\Box$; its retarded solution is then a four-vector, and the Lorenz condition, a four-divergence, holds in every frame once it holds in one. In the Coulomb gauge, $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$ is not preserved by a boost, so after each change of frame a further gauge transformation is needed to restore it; in that sense $A^\mu$ is a four-vector only up to gauge (Griffiths, footnote 27 of ch. 12). The coupling $-q\int A_\mu\,dx^\mu$ of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^pr-b3-2-1|§B3.2]] is a scalar exactly when $A_\mu$ transforms as a covector, up to the gradient terms that gauge freedom allows anyway.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §12.3.5, footnote 27 · the user's series, Part III, §13.1 (Remark: The Coulomb gauge)*

^rem-b4-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §B4.2.2: Electromagnetic Field Tensor
> The **field tensor** (field strength) is the antisymmetric tensor
>
> $$
> F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu, \qquad F^{\mu\nu} = \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu = \eta^{\mu\rho}\eta^{\nu\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma} .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.3, §11.1 · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The Electromagnetic Field Tensor" · Griffiths §12.3.5, eq. (12.133)*

^def-b4-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.1: Components of the Field Tensor
> $F$ is antisymmetric, gauge invariant, and its six independent components are the fields:
>
> $$
> F_{0i} = \frac{E_i}{c}, \quad F_{ij} = -\epsilon_{ijk}B_k; \qquad F^{0i} = -\frac{E_i}{c}, \quad F^{ij} = -\epsilon_{ijk}B_k ,
> $$
>
> that is, with rows $\mu$ and columns $\nu$,
>
> $$
> F^{\mu\nu} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -E_x/c & -E_y/c & -E_z/c \\ E_x/c & 0 & -B_z & B_y \\ E_y/c & B_z & 0 & -B_x \\ E_z/c & -B_y & B_x & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad F_{\mu\nu} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & E_x/c & E_y/c & E_z/c \\ -E_x/c & 0 & -B_z & B_y \\ -E_y/c & B_z & 0 & -B_x \\ -E_z/c & -B_y & B_x & 0 \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> The mixed components used in the force law are $F^0{}_i = F^i{}_0 = E_i/c$ and $F^i{}_j = \epsilon_{ijk}B_k$. Conversely,
>
> $$
> E_i = c\,F^{i0}, \qquad B_i = -\tfrac12\,\epsilon_{ijk}F^{jk} .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §11.1, eq. (F matrix) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Explicit Matrix Representation", §"Component-by-Component Derivation" · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.1.15–6.1.16*

^thm-b4-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Time–space.* With $A_0 = V/c$ and $A_i = -A^i$ (the Cartesian components of $\vec A$ with a minus sign),
>
> $$
> F_{0i} = \frac1c\,\partial_t(-A^i) - \partial_i\frac{V}{c} = \frac1c\Bigl(-\nabla V - \frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t}\Bigr)_i = \frac{E_i}{c} .
> $$
>
> *Space–space.* $F_{ij} = \partial_i(-A^j) - \partial_j(-A^i) = -(\partial_iA^j - \partial_jA^i)$, and since $(\nabla\times\vec A)_k = \epsilon_{klm}\partial_lA^m$, $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{klm} = \delta_{il}\delta_{jm} - \delta_{im}\delta_{jl}$ gives $\epsilon_{ijk}B_k = \partial_iA^j - \partial_jA^i$, so $F_{ij} = -\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$.
>
> *Raising* ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-4|Def. §B1.1.4]]). Raising an index $0$ multiplies by $\eta^{00} = 1$, raising a spatial index by $-1$. So $F^{0i} = -F_{0i}$ and $F^{ij} = F_{ij}$; with one index raised, $F^0{}_i = \eta^{00}F_{0i} = E_i/c$, $F^i{}_0 = \eta^{ii}F_{i0} = -(-E_i/c) = E_i/c$ and $F^i{}_j = \eta^{ii}F_{ij} = \epsilon_{ijk}B_k$ (no sum over $i$ in $\eta^{ii}$).
>
> *Inversion.* $E_i = -cF^{0i} = cF^{i0}$, and $-\tfrac12\epsilon_{ijk}F^{jk} = \tfrac12\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{jkl}B_l = \tfrac12\cdot2\delta_{il}B_l = B_i$.
>
> *Gauge invariance.* $\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\chi - \partial_\nu\partial_\mu\chi = 0$ by equality of mixed partials ([[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]).

> [!caution] Caution: Sign conventions for the field tensor
> The sign of $F^{0i} = -E_i/c$ follows from three choices: $A^\mu = (V/c, \vec A)$, the mostly-minus metric, and $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$. Texts differ:
> - **Griffiths** uses the metric $(-, +, +, +)$ and $F^{\mu\nu} = \partial A^\nu/\partial x_\mu - \partial A^\mu/\partial x_\nu$, which gives $F^{01} = +E_x/c$, $F^{12} = +B_z$ (eqs. (12.119), (12.133)): his $F^{\mu\nu}$ is the negative of the one here, and so is his dual $G^{\mu\nu}$. The physics, e.g. $K^\mu = q\eta_\nu F^{\mu\nu}$, comes out the same ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^cau-b3-2-1|§B3.2, Caution: Index placement and conventions in the force law]]). Some authors also use $F^{01} = E_x$, $F^{12} = cB_z$ (Griffiths, footnote 25).
> - **Phys505** writes the same matrix as here (Phys505 §6.1.15, eq. (6.83)). Its covariant Maxwell equation (6.93), $\partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0j^\mu$, has Griffiths' index order; with this matrix the divergence must be taken on the first index, $\partial_\nu F^{\nu\mu} = \mu_0j^\mu$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-7|Theorem §B4.2.7]]), since $\partial_\nu F^{0\nu} = -\nabla\cdot\vec E/c$.
> - **The user's PHY 505 notes** give the matrix above in §"Explicit Matrix Representation" and derive $F^{01} = -E_x/c$, $F^{12} = -B_z$ correctly. Later, in §"Components of $F^{\mu\nu}$", the boxed matrix has $F^{0i} = +E_i/c$, and its derivation adds the step "$F^{0i} = -g^{00}g^{ii}F_{0i} = E_i/c$" after having computed $\partial^0A^i - \partial^iA^0 = -E_i/c$; but that expression is already the contravariant component, so no further raising applies, and the boxed matrix has the sign of the $E$ entries reversed. (Its $B$ entries are still those above, so it agrees with neither this convention nor Griffiths'.) In the same notes, §"Extracting E and B from the Field Tensor" writes $\vec E = c(F^{01}, F^{02}, F^{03})$ and $B_x = F^{23}$, $B_y = F^{31}$, $B_z = F^{12}$; with the notes' own first matrix these should read $\vec E = c(F^{10}, F^{20}, F^{30}) = -c(F^{01}, F^{02}, F^{03})$ and $B_x = -F^{23}$, $B_y = -F^{31}$, $B_z = -F^{12}$, as the formula $B_i = -\tfrac12\epsilon_{ijk}F^{jk}$ given there correctly says.

## How E and B transform

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.2: Lorentz Transformation of the Fields
> Under a Lorentz transformation $x' = \Lambda x$ the field tensor transforms as a rank-$(2, 0)$ tensor ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]]),
>
> $$
> F'^{\mu\nu}(x') = \Lambda^\mu{}_\rho\,\Lambda^\nu{}_\sigma\,F^{\rho\sigma}(x), \qquad\text{as matrices}\quad F' = \Lambda F\Lambda^{\mathsf T} .
> $$
>
> For the standard boost, $S'$ moving with velocity $v$ along $+x$ relative to $S$ ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^def-a1-4-1|Def. §A1.4.1]]), the fields at the same event are
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> E'_x &= E_x, & E'_y &= \gamma\,(E_y - vB_z), & E'_z &= \gamma\,(E_z + vB_y),\\
> B'_x &= B_x, & B'_y &= \gamma\Bigl(B_y + \frac{v}{c^2}E_z\Bigr), & B'_z &= \gamma\Bigl(B_z - \frac{v}{c^2}E_y\Bigr),
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> and for a boost with arbitrary velocity $\vec v$, splitting into components parallel and perpendicular to $\vec v$,
>
> $$
> \vec E'_\parallel = \vec E_\parallel, \quad \vec B'_\parallel = \vec B_\parallel, \qquad \vec E'_\perp = \gamma\,\bigl(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B\bigr)_\perp, \quad \vec B'_\perp = \gamma\Bigl(\vec B - \frac{\vec v\times\vec E}{c^2}\Bigr)_\perp .
> $$
>
> The inverse transformation is the same with $v \to -v$. In particular, if $\vec B = \vec 0$ in $S$, then $\vec B' = -\vec v\times\vec E'/c^2$; if $\vec E = \vec 0$, then $\vec E' = \vec v\times\vec B'$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §11.2 (table) · Griffiths §12.3.2, eqs. (12.109)–(12.111); §12.3.3, eq. (12.118) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.1.16, eqs. (6.85)–(6.88)*

^thm-b4-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The boost matrix is $\Lambda^0{}_0 = \Lambda^1{}_1 = \gamma$, $\Lambda^0{}_1 = \Lambda^1{}_0 = -\gamma\beta$, $\Lambda^2{}_2 = \Lambda^3{}_3 = 1$, all others zero ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]). Use the components of [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]].
>
> - $(01)$: both indices meet the boost block, and antisymmetry $F^{10} = -F^{01}$ gives $F'^{01} = \Lambda^0{}_0\Lambda^1{}_1F^{01} + \Lambda^0{}_1\Lambda^1{}_0F^{10} = \gamma^2(1 - \beta^2)F^{01} = F^{01}$, so $E'_x = E_x$.
> - $(02)$: $F'^{02} = \Lambda^0{}_0F^{02} + \Lambda^0{}_1F^{12} = -\gamma E_y/c + \gamma\beta B_z$, and $F'^{02} = -E'_y/c$ gives $E'_y = \gamma(E_y - vB_z)$.
> - $(03)$: $F'^{03} = \gamma F^{03} - \gamma\beta F^{13} = -\gamma E_z/c - \gamma\beta B_y$, so $E'_z = \gamma(E_z + vB_y)$.
> - $(12)$: $F'^{12} = \Lambda^1{}_0F^{02} + \Lambda^1{}_1F^{12} = \gamma\beta E_y/c - \gamma B_z = -B'_z$, so $B'_z = \gamma(B_z - vE_y/c^2)$.
> - $(13)$: $F'^{13} = \Lambda^1{}_0F^{03} + \Lambda^1{}_1F^{13} = \gamma\beta E_z/c + \gamma B_y = B'_y$, so $B'_y = \gamma(B_y + vE_z/c^2)$.
> - $(23)$: no boost index, $F'^{23} = F^{23}$, so $B'_x = B_x$.
>
> *Vector form.* For $\vec v = v\hat x$, $\vec v\times\vec B = v(0, -B_z, B_y)$ and $\vec v\times\vec E = v(0, -E_z, E_y)$, which reproduces the table; both sides are three-vector expressions, so a rotation carries them to any direction of $\vec v$. The special cases: with $\vec B = \vec 0$, $\vec B'_\perp = -\gamma\vec v\times\vec E/c^2 = -\vec v\times\vec E'/c^2$ (since $\vec v\times\vec E$ involves only $\vec E_\perp$ and $\vec E'_\perp = \gamma\vec E_\perp$), and $\vec B'_\parallel = \vec 0$; the other case likewise.

> [!remark] Remark: Magnetism is electricity seen from a moving frame
> - The table is the long-promised answer to level A's observation that whether a force is electric or magnetic depends on the observer ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^rem-a12-1-7|EM Remark: Electric or magnetic depends on the observer]]; [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^rem-b8-2-2|EM Remark: Two mechanisms, one formula]]). $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ are not two vector fields but the time–space and space–space parts of one tensor, and a boost mixes them as it mixes $t$ and $x$.
> - A charge at rest has a Coulomb field and no $\vec B$; from a moving frame the table produces $\vec B' = -\vec v\times\vec E'/c^2 \ne \vec 0$, a magnetic field from nothing but a boost ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^ex-b4-2-1|Example §B4.2.1]]). Magnetic forces between currents are large not because $v/c$ is large (drift speeds in wires are of order mm/s) but because they are the $O(v^2/c^2)$ remainder of two enormous electric forces that cancel almost exactly in a neutral wire ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^ex-b4-2-2|Example §B4.2.2]]; Griffiths §12.3.1 works the wire through length contraction of the two charge densities).
> - To first order in $v/c$ the table reduces to $\vec E' \approx \vec E + \vec v\times\vec B$, which Electromagnetism used to remove the electric field in crossed fields ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^rem-b6-1-2|EM Remark: The frame in which E disappears]]); the exact statement is [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]].
> - Historically the logic ran this way round: Einstein's 1905 paper, "On the electrodynamics of moving bodies", opens with the asymmetry between a magnet moving past a conductor and a conductor moving past a magnet, which this table removes.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §11.2 (Remark: Magnetism is boosted electricity) · Griffiths §12.3.1–12.3.2*

## The invariants

> [!definition] Definition §B4.2.3: Dual Field Tensor
> With the totally antisymmetric symbol $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$, normalized by $\epsilon^{0123} = +1$ (so $\epsilon_{0123} = -1$; [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-2|Def. §B2.2.2]]), the **dual** of $F$ is
>
> $$
> \tilde F^{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}, \qquad \tilde F^{0i} = -B_i, \quad \tilde F^{ij} = \frac{1}{c}\,\epsilon_{ijk}E_k ,
> $$
>
> obtained from $F^{\mu\nu}$ by the substitution $\vec E/c \to \vec B$, $\vec B \to -\vec E/c$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §11.3 · Griffiths §12.3.3, eq. (12.120) (his $G^{\mu\nu} = -\tilde F^{\mu\nu}$)*

^def-b4-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.3: The Two Field Invariants
> The full contractions
>
> $$
> F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = 2\Bigl(B^2 - \frac{E^2}{c^2}\Bigr), \qquad F_{\mu\nu}\tilde F^{\mu\nu} = -\frac{4}{c}\,\vec E\cdot\vec B
> $$
>
> have the same value in every inertial frame (at the same event). Equivalently, $E^2 - c^2B^2$ and $\vec E\cdot\vec B$ are Lorentz invariant. The first is a scalar; the second is a **pseudoscalar**: invariant under every Lorentz transformation with $\det\Lambda = +1$ (the proper ones, including the combined reversal $PT$), it changes sign under parity or time reversal alone.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §11.3, eqs. (invariant 1), (invariant 2) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.1.18 · Griffiths §12.3.2, Problem 12.47; §12.3.3, Problem 12.51 · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Electromagnetic Field Invariants"*

^thm-b4-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Dual components.* In $\tilde F^{0i} = \tfrac12\epsilon^{0ijk}F_{jk}$ only spatial $j, k$ contribute, and $\epsilon^{0ijk} = \epsilon_{ijk}$, so $\tilde F^{0i} = \tfrac12\epsilon_{ijk}(-\epsilon_{jkl}B_l) = -B_i$. In $\tilde F^{ij} = \tfrac12(\epsilon^{ij0k}F_{0k} + \epsilon^{ijk0}F_{k0}) = \epsilon^{ij0k}F_{0k}$, and $\epsilon^{ij0k} = \epsilon^{0ijk} = \epsilon_{ijk}$ (two transpositions), so $\tilde F^{ij} = \epsilon_{ijk}E_k/c$.
>
> *Contractions.* Split each sum into time–space and space–space terms, the time–space ones appearing twice ($0i$ and $i0$):
>
> $$
> F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = 2F_{0i}F^{0i} + F_{ij}F^{ij} = 2\,\frac{E_i}{c}\Bigl(-\frac{E_i}{c}\Bigr) + \epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{ijl}B_kB_l = -\frac{2E^2}{c^2} + 2B^2 ,
> $$
>
> using $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{ijl} = 2\delta_{kl}$, and
>
> $$
> F_{\mu\nu}\tilde F^{\mu\nu} = 2F_{0i}\tilde F^{0i} + F_{ij}\tilde F^{ij} = 2\,\frac{E_i}{c}(-B_i) + (-\epsilon_{ijk}B_k)\,\frac{\epsilon_{ijl}E_l}{c} = -\frac{4}{c}\,\vec E\cdot\vec B .
> $$
>
> *Invariance.* A full contraction of tensors is a scalar ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]). The metric is invariant by definition of the Lorentz group; $\epsilon$ transforms as $\Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta\Lambda^\rho{}_\gamma\Lambda^\sigma{}_\delta\,\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = (\det\Lambda)\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$, which is the Leibniz formula for the determinant ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-46|LADR Thm. 9.46]]; [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]). So $F\tilde F$ is invariant for $\det\Lambda = +1$ and flips sign for parity or time reversal ($\det\Lambda = -1$), as $\vec E\cdot\vec B$ visibly does: $\vec E$ is a vector and $\vec B$ an axial vector. One can also check the invariance of $E^2 - c^2B^2$ and $\vec E\cdot\vec B$ directly from [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]] (Griffiths Problem 12.47).

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]], [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-3|Def. §B4.2.3]], [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-46|LADR Thm. 9.46]]

> [!caution] Caution: The sign of the first invariant
> With the mostly-minus metric, $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = 2(B^2 - E^2/c^2)$: raising both indices flips the sign of the time–space entries only, so the $E$ terms enter negatively. The user's PHY 505 notes (§"Electromagnetic Field Invariants") state $I_1 = F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = 2(E^2/c^2 - B^2)$, writing $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = 2\sum_{\mu<\nu}(F_{\mu\nu})^2$; that step drops the metric signs ($F^{0i} = -F_{0i}$, $F^{ij} = +F_{ij}$), and the next line attaches the minus sign to the $B$ terms instead of the $E$ terms, so the result has the opposite overall sign. Invariance is unaffected, and so is the Maxwell Lagrangian built from it, whose sign is fixed separately ([[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^rem-c1-1-5|EM Remark: What fixes the size and the sign]]). Phys505 states the invariant as $E^2 - c^2B^2$, correctly up to normalization, but its eq. (6.102), $-\mathrm{tr}(gFgF) = \frac{2}{c^2}(E^2 - c^2B^2)$, has the same sign slip, since by its eq. (6.100) $-\mathrm{tr}(gFgF) = F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ (Phys505 §6.1.18).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Electromagnetic Field Invariants" · the user's series, Part III, §11.3 · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.1.18, eqs. (6.100)–(6.102)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.4: Invariant Classes of Fields
> At a given event:
> 1. If $\vec E\cdot\vec B \ne 0$ in one frame, then in every frame both fields are nonzero and not perpendicular; no frame sees a purely electric or purely magnetic field.
> 2. If $\vec E\cdot\vec B = 0$ and $E > cB$ (with $\vec B \ne \vec 0$), then in the frame moving with velocity $\vec v = c^2\,\vec E\times\vec B/E^2$ (of speed $c^2B/E < c$) the field is purely electric, $\vec B' = \vec 0$, $E' = \sqrt{E^2 - c^2B^2}$. If $\vec E\cdot\vec B = 0$ and $E < cB$, then in the frame moving with $\vec v = \vec E\times\vec B/B^2$ (speed $E/B < c$) it is purely magnetic, $\vec E' = \vec 0$, $B' = \sqrt{B^2 - E^2/c^2}$.
> 3. If $\vec E\cdot\vec B = 0$ and $E = cB$ (**null field**), then in every frame $\vec E \perp \vec B$ and $E = cB$, and unless both vanish, no frame sees a purely electric or purely magnetic field. The fields of a plane electromagnetic wave are null ([[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-3|EM Theorem §B10.1.3]]).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §11.3 (classification by the two invariants) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.1.18*

^thm-b4-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1 and 3 are restatements of [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]]: $\vec E'\cdot\vec B' = \vec E\cdot\vec B$, and if, in case 3, $\vec E' = \vec 0$ in some frame, then $c^2B'^2 = E'^2 = 0$.
>
> For 2, let $\vec E \perp \vec B$, $E > cB$, and $\vec v = c^2\vec E\times\vec B/E^2$; then $v = c^2EB/E^2 = c\cdot cB/E < c$, and $\vec v$ is perpendicular to both fields, so $\vec E_\parallel = \vec B_\parallel = \vec 0$. By the vector identity $(\vec E\times\vec B)\times\vec E = E^2\vec B - (\vec E\cdot\vec B)\vec E = E^2\vec B$,
>
> $$
> \vec v\times\vec E = \frac{c^2}{E^2}(\vec E\times\vec B)\times\vec E = c^2\vec B, \qquad \vec B'_\perp = \gamma\Bigl(\vec B - \frac{\vec v\times\vec E}{c^2}\Bigr) = \vec 0 ,
> $$
>
> by [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]], so $\vec B' = \vec 0$, and $E'^2 = E'^2 - c^2B'^2 = E^2 - c^2B^2$. For $E < cB$ take $\vec v = \vec E\times\vec B/B^2$, of speed $E/B < c$: $(\vec E\times\vec B)\times\vec B = (\vec E\cdot\vec B)\vec B - B^2\vec E = -B^2\vec E$, so $\vec v\times\vec B = -\vec E$, $\vec E'_\perp = \gamma(\vec E - \vec E) = \vec 0$, and $c^2B'^2 = c^2B^2 - E^2$.

> [!remark] Remark: The drift velocity is the frame velocity
> The velocity $\vec E\times\vec B/B^2$ of case 2 is the drift velocity of a charge in crossed fields ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-3|EM Theorem §B6.1.3]]). Electromagnetism found it to first order in $v/c$; here it is exact: for $E < cB$ there is a frame in which the charge sees only a magnetic field and circles ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-6|Theorem §B3.3.6]]), and the drift is the motion of that frame. For $E > cB$ no such frame exists, and a charge is accelerated indefinitely, as in a pure electric field ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]; covariantly, [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^ex-b3-2-2|Example §B3.2.2]]).

## Sources and Maxwell's equations

> [!law] Law §B4.2.5: Invariance of Charge
> The electric charge of a body, and the total charge of any system, has the same value in every inertial frame, whatever the motion of the charges.
>
> *Valid when:* always, as far as tested. The best evidence is the exact neutrality of atoms and molecules, whose electrons and nuclei (and the quarks inside them) move with very different speeds; a speed-dependent charge would leave them charged. Charge is both conserved ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|EM Principle §A1.1.2]]) and invariant, while energy is conserved but not invariant and mass invariant but not conserved ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^rem-a3-2-1|§A3.2, Remark: Conserved is not invariant]]).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §11.4 (experimental input) · Griffiths §12.3.2, p. 553; §12.2.2, p. 536 (invariant versus conserved)*

> [!definition] Definition §B4.2.4: Four-Current
> The **four-current** (current density four-vector) combines the charge density $\rho$ and current density $\vec J$,
>
> $$
> J^\mu = \bigl(c\rho, \vec J\bigr), \qquad J_\mu = \bigl(c\rho, -\vec J\bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The Four-Current Density" · Griffiths §12.3.4, eq. (12.124) · the user's series, Part III, §11.4*

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.6: The Four-Current Is a Four-Vector; Continuity
> For charge with proper density $\rho_0$ (the density in its local rest frame, a scalar) flowing with four-velocity $u^\mu = \gamma_u(c, \vec u)$,
>
> $$
> J^\mu = \rho_0\,u^\mu, \qquad \rho = \gamma_u\rho_0, \quad \vec J = \gamma_u\rho_0\,\vec u ,
> $$
>
> so $J^\mu$ is a four-vector; a general current, a superposition of such flows, is one too. Local charge conservation is the Lorentz-invariant statement
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu J^\mu = 0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0 ,
> $$
>
> the continuity equation ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|EM Theorem §B6.1.5]]); it holds in every frame if it holds in one.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §12.3.4, eqs. (12.121)–(12.126) · the user's series, Part III, §11.4, eqs. (convective current), (charge continuity) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Charge Conservation"*

^thm-b4-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take a small volume moving with the charge, of rest volume $V_0$, containing charge $Q$. In a frame where it moves with velocity $\vec u$, its extent along $\vec u$ is contracted and the others are not ([[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]]), so $V = V_0/\gamma_u$, while $Q$ is the same ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^law-b4-2-5|Law §B4.2.5]]). Hence $\rho = Q/V = \gamma_u\rho_0$ and $\vec J = \rho\vec u = \gamma_u\rho_0\vec u$, i.e. $(c\rho, \vec J) = \rho_0\gamma_u(c, \vec u) = \rho_0u^\mu$, a scalar times a four-vector ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]). Charges of different kinds or velocities each give such a term, and a sum of four-vectors is a four-vector. Finally $\partial_\mu J^\mu = \partial_0(c\rho) + \partial_iJ^i = \partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\vec J$, a scalar ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]), so its vanishing in one frame is its vanishing in all.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.7: Maxwell's Equations in Covariant Form
> Maxwell's equations ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|EM Principle §B8.4.2]]) are equivalent to the two tensor equations
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0J^\nu, \qquad \partial_\lambda F_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu F_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_\nu F_{\lambda\mu} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \partial_\mu\tilde F^{\mu\nu} = 0 .
> $$
>
> The first (inhomogeneous) contains Gauss's law ($\nu = 0$) and the Ampère–Maxwell law ($\nu = 1, 2, 3$); the second (homogeneous, the **Bianchi identity**) contains $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ and Faraday's law, and holds identically for every $F$ derived from a potential. Consequently:
> - Maxwell's equations have the same form in every inertial frame, provided $F$ transforms as a tensor ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]]) and $J$ as a four-vector ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]).
> - The inhomogeneous equation implies charge conservation, $\partial_\nu J^\nu = 0$ ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-3|EM Theorem §B8.4.3]]).
> - In terms of the potential, $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \Box A^\nu - \partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu)$, and in the Lorenz gauge $\Box A^\nu = \mu_0J^\nu$, four massless Klein–Gordon equations with a source ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]]).
> - The field tensor itself obeys $\Box F_{\mu\nu} = \mu_0(\partial_\mu J_\nu - \partial_\nu J_\mu)$; in vacuum $\Box F_{\mu\nu} = 0$, the covariant form of the wave equations for $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ ([[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-1|EM Theorem §B10.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Unpacking Maxwell's Equations", §"Homogeneous Maxwell Equations (Bianchi Identity)", §"From Maxwell's Equations to the Wave Equation" · the user's series, Part III, §12.3, §13.1 · Griffiths §12.3.4, eq. (12.127), Problem 12.54, eq. (12.130); §12.3.5, eqs. (12.134)–(12.138) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.1.17*

^thm-b4-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Inhomogeneous pair.* With [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]] and $J^0 = c\rho$: for $\nu = 0$, $\partial_iF^{i0} = \partial_i(E_i/c) = \nabla\cdot\vec E/c = \mu_0c\rho$, i.e. $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \mu_0c^2\rho = \rho/\varepsilon_0$. For $\nu = j$,
>
> $$
> \partial_0F^{0j} + \partial_iF^{ij} = -\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial E_j}{\partial t} - \epsilon_{ijk}\partial_iB_k = -\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial E_j}{\partial t} + (\nabla\times\vec B)_j = \mu_0J_j ,
> $$
>
> since $-\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_iB_k = \epsilon_{jik}\partial_iB_k$; with $\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1/c^2$ this is $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t\vec E$.
>
> *Homogeneous pair.* The cyclic sum is antisymmetric in all three indices, so only triples of distinct indices count, four in all. $(\lambda\mu\nu) = (123)$: $\partial_1F_{23} + \partial_2F_{31} + \partial_3F_{12} = -(\partial_xB_x + \partial_yB_y + \partial_zB_z) = -\nabla\cdot\vec B$. $(012)$: $\partial_0F_{12} + \partial_1F_{20} + \partial_2F_{01} = -\frac1c\partial_tB_z - \frac1c\partial_xE_y + \frac1c\partial_yE_x = -\frac1c\bigl[\partial_t\vec B + \nabla\times\vec E\bigr]_z$, and $(023)$, $(031)$ give the $x$ and $y$ components. In dual form, $\partial_\mu\tilde F^{\mu 0} = \partial_iB_i$ and $\partial_\mu\tilde F^{\mu j} = -\frac1c[\partial_t\vec B + \nabla\times\vec E]_j$ by [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-3|Def. §B4.2.3]]: the same four equations. If $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$, the cyclic sum is $(\partial_\lambda\partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\lambda\partial_\nu A_\mu) + (\partial_\mu\partial_\nu A_\lambda - \partial_\mu\partial_\lambda A_\nu) + (\partial_\nu\partial_\lambda A_\mu - \partial_\nu\partial_\mu A_\lambda) = 0$, the terms cancelling in pairs by equality of mixed partials ([[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]).
>
> *Covariance.* Both sides of each equation are tensors of the same rank: if they agree in one frame they agree in all ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-8|Theorem §B2.2.8]]).
>
> *Conservation.* $\partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0$, a symmetric operator contracted with an antisymmetric tensor; hence $\mu_0\partial_\nu J^\nu = 0$.
>
> *Potentials.* $\partial_\mu(\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu) = \Box A^\nu - \partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu)$, the order of derivatives being immaterial; in the Lorenz gauge ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-1|Def. §B4.2.1]]) the second term vanishes.
>
> *Wave equation for F.* Apply $\partial^\lambda$ to the cyclic identity: $\Box F_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu\partial^\lambda F_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_\nu\partial^\lambda F_{\lambda\mu} = 0$. Lowering the free index of the inhomogeneous equation gives $\partial^\lambda F_{\lambda\nu} = \mu_0J_\nu$, and $F_{\nu\lambda} = -F_{\lambda\nu}$, so $\Box F_{\mu\nu} - \mu_0\partial_\mu J_\nu + \mu_0\partial_\nu J_\mu = 0$.

> [!remark] Remark: Layers, and what comes later
> - Maxwell's equations keep their layer: they are the principle of Electromagnetism ([[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|EM Principle §B8.4.2]]), whose stated domain already included all speeds. What [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-7|Theorem §B4.2.7]] adds is that they are tensor equations, so the covariance principle ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^pr-b2-2-9|Principle §B2.2.9]]) holds for them without any change, unlike Newton's mechanics. Their compatibility with relativity, which Electromagnetism asserted, is now visible.
> - The two halves have different origins. The homogeneous pair is geometry: an identity for any $F$ built from a potential, which is why it can have no source. Conversely, on a region without holes every $F$ obeying it comes from a potential (the Poincaré lemma, [[§39 Closed and Exact Forms#^prop-39-7|452 Prop. §39.7]]; on regions with holes the exceptions are physics: magnetic monopoles, the Aharonov–Bohm effect). The inhomogeneous pair is dynamics; it follows from the Maxwell Lagrangian $-\frac{1}{4\mu_0}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} - J^\mu A_\mu$, the action whose source term is the field-side face of the coupling of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^rem-b3-2-1|§B3.2, Remark: Why this coupling]] (Quantum Field Theory C1 — [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^rem-c1a-7-2|QFT Remark: Electrodynamics as the template for field theory]]; in SI units, Electromagnetism C1 — [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^pr-c1-1-4|EM Principle §C1.1.4]], [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-2|EM Theorem §C1.2.2]], with the homogeneous pair as an identity and its converse, [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-4|EM Theorem §C1.2.4]], [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-5|EM Theorem §C1.2.5]]; derived in the user's PHY 505 notes and series, Part III, §12).
> - With the force law $dp^\mu/d\tau = qF^{\mu\nu}u_\nu$ ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-5|§B3.2, Theorem §B3.2.5]]), or its density form $f^\mu = F^{\mu\nu}J_\nu$ (Phys505 §6.2.2), electrodynamics is complete in covariant form. ## Examples

> [!example] Example §B4.2.1: The Field of a Uniformly Moving Charge
> A charge $q$ moves with constant velocity $v\hat x$ in $S$ and passes the origin at $t = 0$. Find its potentials and fields in $S$ by transforming from its rest frame, and compare with Electromagnetism.
>
> *Rest frame.* $S'$ moves with $v\hat x$ ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^def-a1-4-1|Def. §A1.4.1]]); there the charge sits at the origin, with $V' = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r'$, $\vec A' = \vec 0$, $\vec E' = q\vec r\,'/4\pi\varepsilon_0r'^3$, $\vec B' = \vec 0$. This static potential satisfies the Lorenz condition. Let $\vec R = (x - vt, y, z)$ be the vector from the charge's **present** position to the field point, at angle $\theta$ to $\hat x$. By [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]], $\vec r\,' = (\gamma R_x, R_y, R_z)$, and
>
> $$
> r'^2 = \gamma^2R^2\cos^2\theta + R^2\sin^2\theta = \gamma^2R^2\bigl(1 - \beta^2\sin^2\theta\bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Potentials, as a four-vector.* The inverse boost ($v \to -v$) of $A'^\mu = (V'/c, \vec 0)$ gives $A^0 = \gamma A'^0$, $A^1 = \gamma\beta A'^0$:
>
> $$
> V = \gamma V' = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\gamma}{\gamma R\sqrt{1 - \beta^2\sin^2\theta}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R\sqrt{1 - v^2\sin^2\theta/c^2}}, \qquad \vec A = \frac{\gamma\beta V'}{c}\,\hat x = \frac{\vec v}{c^2}\,V .
> $$
>
> *Fields, as a tensor.* The inverse of [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]] with $\vec B' = \vec 0$ gives $E_x = E'_x$, $E_y = \gamma E'_y$, $E_z = \gamma E'_z$, so
>
> $$
> \vec E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{(\gamma R_x, \gamma R_y, \gamma R_z)}{r'^3} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\gamma\vec R}{\gamma^3R^3(1 - \beta^2\sin^2\theta)^{3/2}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1 - v^2/c^2}{(1 - v^2\sin^2\theta/c^2)^{3/2}}\,\frac{\hat R}{R^2} ,
> $$
>
> and $\vec B = \gamma\,\vec v\times\vec E'/c^2 = \vec v\times\vec E/c^2$ (the special case of Theorem §B4.2.2, with the frame velocity $-\vec v$). These are exactly the potentials and fields of [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-4|EM Theorem §B11.2.4]], which Electromagnetism obtained from the Liénard–Wiechert potentials by solving a quadratic for the retarded time. Here they follow from Coulomb's law and one boost. The electric field points from the present position because $E_x$ gains its factor $\gamma$ from the coordinate transformation ($r'_x = \gamma R_x$), while $E_y$, $E_z$ gain theirs from the field transformation; the two factors balance. The flattening of the field into a "pancake" ($1 - \beta^2$ along the motion, $\gamma$ across it) is drawn in [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^rem-b11-2-5|EM Remark: The pancake, and what relativity says about it]], whose closing question this example answers.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §12.3.2, Example 12.14, eqs. (12.92)–(12.93); Example 12.15, eq. (12.112) · the user's series, Part III, §11.2 (Remark: Magnetism is boosted electricity) · Griffiths Problem 12.58 (four-potential)*

> [!example] Example §B4.2.2: A Moving Line Charge, and the Force on a Charge Moving with It
> In its rest frame $S'$, an infinite straight line along the $x$ axis carries charge $\lambda_0$ per length. In $S$ the line moves along itself with velocity $v\hat x$. Find $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ in $S$, and the force on a charge $Q$ at distance $s$ from the line that moves along with it.
>
> *Fields.* In $S'$, $\vec E' = \dfrac{\lambda_0}{2\pi\varepsilon_0s}\,\hat s$ (radial from the axis; [[§A2.3 Applying Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-3-3|EM Theorem §A2.3.3]]) and $\vec B' = \vec 0$. The boost is along the line, so $\vec E'$ is entirely transverse, and $s$ is unchanged ([[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]]). The inverse of [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]] gives
>
> $$
> \vec E = \gamma\vec E' = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0s}\,\hat s, \qquad \vec B = \frac{\vec v\times\vec E}{c^2} = \frac{\mu_0\lambda v}{2\pi s}\,\hat\varphi = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s}\,\hat\varphi ,
> $$
>
> with $\lambda = \gamma\lambda_0$, the line density in $S$ (the same charge on a length contracted by $\gamma$; [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^law-b4-2-5|Law §B4.2.5]]), and $I = \lambda v$. These are Gauss's law for the line charge measured in $S$ and the field of a long straight current ([[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-2|EM Theorem §A8.2.2]]), which Electromagnetism obtained by integrating the field of [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-4|EM Theorem §B11.2.4]] along the line ([[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^ex-b11-2-1|EM Example §B11.2.1]]).
>
> *Force.* With $\hat x\times\hat\varphi = -\hat s$, the Lorentz force on $Q$ moving at $v\hat x$ is
>
> $$
> \vec F = Q(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B) = Q\Bigl(\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0s} - \frac{\mu_0\lambda v^2}{2\pi s}\Bigr)\hat s = \frac{Q\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0s}\Bigl(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\Bigr)\hat s = \frac{1}{\gamma}\,\frac{Q\lambda_0}{2\pi\varepsilon_0s}\,\hat s = \frac{\vec F'}{\gamma} .
> $$
>
> The electric repulsion in $S$ is stronger than in $S'$ by $\gamma$; the magnetic attraction between the parallel moving charges takes away the fraction $v^2/c^2$; the net force is $1/\gamma$ times the rest-frame force. That is what the transformation of a transverse force requires: $\vec p_\perp$ is the same in both frames while $dt = \gamma\,dt'$ for a clock moving with $Q$ (time dilation, [[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^thm-a2-1-2|Theorem §A2.1.2]]; force transformation: [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]]). The magnetic force is the relativistic correction that keeps the electric force consistent with time dilation, and for drift speeds of mm/s it is a correction of order $10^{-23}$ to forces that, in a neutral wire, cancel.
>
