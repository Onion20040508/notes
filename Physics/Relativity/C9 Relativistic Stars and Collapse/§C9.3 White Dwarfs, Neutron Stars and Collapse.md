How heavy can a cold star be before gravity wins? [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations|§C9.1]] reduced the question to the TOV equations ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-4|Theorem §C9.1.4]]) and showed that, for a given cold equation of state, the stars form one family labelled by the central density ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-7|Theorem §C9.1.7]]). [[§C9.2 The Uniform-Density Star and Buchdahl's Limit|§C9.2]] bounded every static star by $R > 9M/4$ ([[§C9.2 The Uniform-Density Star and Buchdahl's Limit#^thm-c9-2-4|Theorem §C9.2.4]]) and every cold star by about $5\,M_\odot$ whatever its matter ([[§C9.2 The Uniform-Density Star and Buchdahl's Limit#^thm-c9-2-6|Theorem §C9.2.6]]). This section puts real matter into the equations: the degenerate electrons of white dwarfs and the degenerate neutrons of neutron stars, whose Newtonian theory is [[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars|TH §B13.3★]].

*The naive attempt, computed.* Newton's theory with the ideal neutron gas says neutron stars can be heavy. With the same pressure as for electrons, but neutrons supplying both pressure and mass, the Newtonian sequence of stars climbs monotonically toward $5.73\,M_\odot$ as $\rho_c \to \infty$, never turning over ([[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^thm-b13-3-7|TH Theorem §B13.3.7]] estimates $5.7\,M_\odot$). At the central density $\rho_c = 4.21\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$ Newton gives $1.65\,M_\odot$ and $R = 11.6\ \mathrm{km}$. Integrate the TOV equations instead, with the same matter and the same $\rho_c$: $0.710\,M_\odot$ and $9.16\ \mathrm{km}$. That is the heaviest star of the whole family, eight times lighter than Newton's limit. For white dwarfs the change is small in size but not in kind: Newton's masses approach $1.435\,M_\odot$ only as $\rho_c \to \infty$, while the TOV sequence has a maximum, $1.396\,M_\odot$, at the finite density $2.4\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$. In general relativity pressure weighs, and the relativistic gas cannot win.

The section builds this in order. It states the matter model ([[#^mod-c9-3-1|Model §C9.3.1]]) and its polytropic limits with the Chandrasekhar mass ([[#^thm-c9-3-2|Theorem §C9.3.2]]), computes the families of stars ([[#^thm-c9-3-3|Theorem §C9.3.3]]), says which members are stable ([[#^thm-c9-3-4|Theorem §C9.3.4]]), compares with observed neutron stars ([[#^law-c9-3-5|Law §C9.3.5]]), and shows what happens above the maximum mass: collapse, with a black hole outside ([[#^thm-c9-3-6|Theorem §C9.3.6]]).

**Setup.** Static, spherical, non-rotating stars of cold matter (temperature zero, [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^def-c9-1-2|Def. §C9.1.2]]), described by [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^mod-c9-1-1|Model §C9.1.1]]; outside them vacuum and the Schwarzschild metric. The matter is the ideal Fermi gas of [[#^mod-c9-3-1|Model §C9.3.1]], an idealisation whose limits are stated there and in [[#^cau-c9-3-1|Caution: Ideal-gas neutron stars are too light]]. The sequences of stars are computed by numerical integration of the TOV equations; every number was checked by script. The stability criterion is quoted from Hartle, not derived ([[#^thm-c9-3-4|Theorem §C9.3.4]] says which parts). Collapse is treated from the outside only: the exterior geometry and the worldline of the surface. The inside of a collapsing star and the formation of the horizon belong to C10 (planned). Observed numbers are those of the typed sources, with their dates.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$ in the structure equations, restored with [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]] ($M \to GM/c^2$, with $GM_\odot/c^2 = 1.4767\ \mathrm{km}$; $\rho \to G\rho/c^4$ for an energy density). The microphysics of the gas keeps $\hbar$ and $c$. $\rho$ is the total energy density, rest energy included; densities quoted in $\mathrm{g/cm^3}$ are $\rho/c^2$. $x = p_F/mc$ is the Fermi parameter, $m$ the mass of the fermion that supplies the pressure.

## Cold degenerate matter

> [!model] Model §C9.3.1: Cold Degenerate Matter
> *Structure.* A cold equation of state ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^def-c9-1-2|Def. §C9.1.2]]) given parametrically: a scalar $x \ge 0$ at each point of the star, the Fermi momentum $p_F$ in units of $mc$ ([[§C4b.8 The Degenerate Fermi Gas#^def-c4b-8-1|TH Def. §C4b.8.1]]), determines the number density $n$, the energy density $\rho$ and the pressure $p$ of the fluid in its rest frame ([[§C4.3 Perfect Fluids#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]]). The pressure comes from an ideal Fermi gas of spin-½ particles of mass $m$ at $T = 0$ ([[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^thm-b13-3-2|TH Theorem §B13.3.2]]): with $\lambda = \hbar/mc$ and the energy-density scale $K_m = m^4c^5/(\pi^2\hbar^3)$,
>
> $$
> n(x) = \frac{x^3}{3\pi^2\lambda^3}, \qquad \varepsilon_m(x) = \frac{K_m}{8}\Bigl[x\sqrt{1 + x^2}\,(1 + 2x^2) - \operatorname{arsinh}x\Bigr], \qquad p(x) = \frac{K_m}{24}\Bigl[x\sqrt{1 + x^2}\,(2x^2 - 3) + 3\operatorname{arsinh}x\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> where $\varepsilon_m$ is the energy density of the fermions, their rest energy included. Two versions:
> 1. **White-dwarf matter.** Electrons ($m = m_e$) supply the pressure; fully ionised nuclei supply the mass, $\mu_e$ nucleon masses per electron ($\mu_e = 2$ for helium, carbon, oxygen). Then $\rho = \mu_em_pc^2\,n + \varepsilon_{m_e}(x)$, and $x = 1$ at $\rho/c^2 = 1.96\times10^6\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$. This is [[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^mod-b13-3-1|TH Model §B13.3.1]] with the rest energy of the nuclei counted as energy density.
> 2. **Ideal neutron gas.** Neutrons ($m = m_n$) supply both pressure and energy: $\rho = \varepsilon_{m_n}(x)$. This is [[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^mod-b13-3-6|TH Model §B13.3.6]], the matter of Oppenheimer and Volkoff's 1939 calculation.
>
> In both, $p(x)$ and $\rho(x)$ increase strictly with $x$, so $p(\rho)$ is a cold equation of state, and $p < \rho/3$ (the bound is approached as $x \to \infty$).
>
> - *Assumes:* $T = 0$ (the thermal energy is far below the Fermi energy, [[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^ex-b13-3-1|TH Example §B13.3.1]]); free fermions; fixed composition.
> - *Leaves out:* inverse β decay, $e^- + p \to n + \nu_e$ (the reverse of β decay, [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^def-a7-2-4|QM Def. §A7.2.4]]), which removes electrons at high density and softens white-dwarf matter (Schutz §10.7, p. 285; Hartle Box 24.1); the strong interaction between nucleons, which dominates the pressure above nuclear density, $2.9\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$ (Hartle §24.6); the protons and electrons that remain in neutron-star matter; Coulomb corrections; rotation and magnetic fields.
> - *Ground-state matter* (Hartle Box 24.1) combines all these into one equation of state, from iron through neutron-rich nuclei and neutron drip near $4\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$ to nuclear matter; it is known reliably only up to about nuclear density.
>
> *Source: Schutz §10.7, eqs. (10.71)–(10.81), (10.87) (pp. 282–285, PDF 300–303) · Hartle §24.1, eqs. (24.6)–(24.9); §24.3; Box 24.1 (pp. 518–527, PDF 517–526) · Couzens, GR2, §3 introduction (pp. 52–53, PDF 53–54) · TH §B13.3★ for the derivation of $p(x)$*

The two limits of the gas are polytropes, and for one of them the structure equations contain only one mass scale.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.3.2: Polytropes and the Chandrasekhar Mass
> In [[#^mod-c9-3-1|Model §C9.3.1]], white-dwarf matter, with rest-mass density $\rho_0 = \mu_em_pn$:
> 1. **Two polytropes.** The pressure is a power of the density in the two limits,
>
> $$
> x \ll 1:\ \ p = \frac{K_{m_e}}{15}\,x^5 \propto \rho_0^{5/3}\ \ \bigl(n_{\rm p} = \tfrac32\bigr), \qquad x \gg 1:\ \ p = \frac{K_{m_e}}{12}\,x^4 = K_4\,\rho_0^{4/3}\ \ \bigl(n_{\rm p} = 3\bigr), \quad K_4 = \frac{\hbar c}{4}\,(3\pi^2)^{1/3}(\mu_em_p)^{-4/3} ,
> $$
>
>    with polytropic index $n_{\rm p}$ in $p = K\rho_0^{1 + 1/n_{\rm p}}$. The stiffness $\gamma = (n/p)\,dp/dn = \bigl((\rho + p)/p\bigr)\,dp/d\rho$ (Hartle (24.18)) falls from $\tfrac53$ to $\tfrac43$; at large $x$, $\gamma = \tfrac43 + \tfrac{2}{3x^2} + O(x^{-4})$.
> 2. **One mass scale.** In $G = c = 1$ the constant of the relativistic polytrope, $k = K_4G^{-1/3}c^{-4/3}$, has dimension $(\text{length})^{2/3}$, so $k^{3/2}$ is a mass, the only one in Newton's structure equations with $p = k\rho_0^{4/3}$. Every such star has the same mass, whatever its central density.
> 3. **Its value.** Schutz's order-of-magnitude balance gives $M = k^{3/2} = 0.32\,M_\odot$ ($\mu_e = 2$). The exact Newtonian star (Lane–Emden, $n_{\rm p} = 3$) has
>
> $$
> M_{\rm Ch} = \frac{4\omega_3}{\sqrt\pi}\,k^{3/2} = \frac{\omega_3\sqrt{3\pi}}{2}\,\frac{1}{\mu_e^2}\Bigl(\frac{\hbar c}{G}\Bigr)^{3/2}\frac{1}{m_p^2} = 1.435\,M_\odot \quad (\mu_e = 2), \qquad \omega_3 = 2.018 ,
> $$
>
>    the **Chandrasekhar mass** ([[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^thm-b13-3-5|TH Theorem §B13.3.5]]); $(\hbar c/G)^{3/2}/m_p^2 = 1.853\,M_\odot$, and the electron mass drops out. With the atomic mass unit for $m_p$ it is $1.456\,M_\odot$; for iron ($\mu_e = 56/26$), $1.24\,M_\odot$.
> 4. **What general relativity changes.** At white-dwarf densities the three TOV factors ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-4|Theorem §C9.1.4]]) differ from $1$ by parts in a thousand: at the heaviest white dwarf $p_c/\rho_c = 1.6\times10^{-3}$ and $2M/R = 4.0\times10^{-3}$. They still change the answer, because the relativistic gas is only marginally stiff ($\gamma - \tfrac43 \to 0$): the TOV sequence reaches a maximum $1.396\,M_\odot$ at $\rho_c = 2.4\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$, below Newton's $1.435\,M_\odot$, which is approached only as $\rho_c \to \infty$ ([[#^thm-c9-3-3|Theorem §C9.3.3]], part 1).
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c9-3-1|Model §C9.3.1]], white-dwarf matter; parts 2–3 Newtonian structure with the $n_{\rm p} = 3$ polytrope; part 4 the full TOV equations. *applies:* parts 1–3 exactly within the model; part 4 is a computed result.
>
> *Source: Schutz §10.7, eqs. (10.78)–(10.86) (pp. 283–285, PDF 301–303; on (10.85), [[#^cau-c9-3-2|Caution: Slips in the sources on compact stars]]) · Hartle Box 12.1 (p. 257, PDF 258); §24.3–24.4, eq. > [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (limits). Write $s = \sqrt{1 + x^2}$. For $x \ll 1$, $\operatorname{arsinh}x = x - \tfrac16x^3 + \tfrac{3}{40}x^5 + O(x^7)$ and $xs(2x^2 - 3) = -3x + 2x^3 - \tfrac32x^3 + O(x^5)$ expanded to fifth order give the bracket of $p$ as $\tfrac85x^5 + O(x^7)$ ([[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^der-b13-3-2|TH Derivation §B13.3.2]], where the bracket carries a factor $\tfrac13$), so $p = (K/24)\tfrac85x^5 = Kx^5/15$. For $x \gg 1$, $s = x\bigl(1 + \tfrac{1}{2x^2} - \tfrac{1}{8x^4} + \dots\bigr)$, so
>
> $$
> xs(2x^2 - 3) = x^2\Bigl(1 + \frac{1}{2x^2} - \frac{1}{8x^4}\Bigr)(2x^2 - 3) + \dots = 2x^4 - 2x^2 + O(1), \qquad p = \frac{K}{24}\bigl(2x^4 - 2x^2 + O(\ln x)\bigr) = \frac{K}{12}\bigl(x^4 - x^2\bigr) + O(\ln x) .
> $$
>
> With $n = x^3/(3\pi^2\lambda^3)$, $x = \lambda(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$ and $\rho_0 = \mu_em_pn$: $Kx^4/12 = \frac{m_e^4c^5}{12\pi^2\hbar^3}\,\frac{\hbar^4}{m_e^4c^4}(3\pi^2)^{4/3}n^{4/3} = \frac{\hbar c}{4}(3\pi^2)^{1/3}n^{4/3}$, which is $K_4\rho_0^{4/3}$. The same substitution in $Kx^5/15$ gives $\rho_0^{5/3}$.
>
> **Step 2** (stiffness). At $T = 0$ the first law $d(\rho V) = -p\,dV$ at fixed particle number $N = nV$ gives $d\rho/dn = (\rho + p)/n$; hence $\bigl((\rho + p)/p\bigr)dp/d\rho = (n/p)\,dp/dn$. From Step 1, $\ln p = \ln(x^4 - x^2) + \text{const}$ and $\ln n = 3\ln x + \text{const}$, so
>
> $$
> \gamma = \frac{d\ln p}{d\ln n} = \frac{x}{3}\,\frac{4x^3 - 2x}{x^4 - x^2} = \frac43\,\frac{1 - \tfrac{1}{2x^2}}{1 - \tfrac{1}{x^2}} = \frac43\Bigl(1 + \frac{1}{2x^2} + O(x^{-4})\Bigr) = \frac43 + \frac{2}{3x^2} + O(x^{-4}) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (dimension). In geometric units $p$ and $\rho_0$ are both $(\text{length})^{-2}$ (curvature, by Einstein's equations). From $p = k\rho_0^{4/3}$, $[k] = L^{-2}/L^{-8/3} = L^{2/3}$, so $k^{3/2}$ has dimension $L$, a mass in $G = c = 1$. Newton's structure equations, $dm/dr = 4\pi r^2\rho_0$ and $dp/dr = -\rho_0m/r^2$ ([[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^thm-b13-3-3|TH Theorem §B13.3.3]]), contain no other constant; $\rho_c$ sets only the length scale $\rho_c^{-1/3}k^{1/2}$. So $M = C\,k^{3/2}$ with a pure number $C$, the same for every $\rho_c$. Converting: $p_{\rm geo} = Gp/c^4$, $\rho_{0,\rm geo} = G\rho_0/c^2$, and $Gp/c^4 = k(G\rho_0/c^2)^{4/3}$ gives $k = K_4G^{-1/3}c^{-4/3}$.
>
> **Step 4** (Schutz's estimate). Replace the equations by orders of magnitude for a star of mass $M$, radius $R$, typical $\bar\rho$, $\bar p$ (Schutz (10.83)): $M = R^3\bar\rho$ and $\bar p/R = \bar\rho M/R^2$. With $\bar p = k\bar\rho^{4/3}$ the second gives $k\bar\rho^{1/3} = M/R$; inserting $\bar\rho = M/R^3$,
>
> $$
> \frac{kM^{1/3}}{R} = \frac MR \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M^{2/3} = k \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M = k^{3/2} = 0.465\times10^5\ \mathrm{cm} = 0.315\,M_\odot \quad (\mu_e = 2) ,
> $$
>
> $R$ cancelling, as Step 3 predicts. This is $C = 1$.
>
> **Step 5** (Lane–Emden). The exact Newtonian star with $p = k\rho_0^{4/3}$: [[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^der-b13-3-5|TH Derivation §B13.3.5]] sets $\rho_0 = \rho_c\theta^3$, $r = r_0\xi$ with $r_0^2 = (n_{\rm p} + 1)k\rho_c^{1/n_{\rm p} - 1}/4\pi = k\rho_c^{-2/3}/\pi$ ($G = 1$), solves the Lane–Emden equation numerically to its first zero $\xi_1 = 6.897$, and finds $M = 4\pi r_0^3\rho_c\,\omega_3$, $\omega_3 = -\xi_1^2\theta'(\xi_1) = 2.018$. Here $r_0^3\rho_c = (k/\pi)^{3/2}$, so
>
> $$
> M = 4\pi\omega_3\Bigl(\frac k\pi\Bigr)^{3/2} = \frac{4\omega_3}{\sqrt\pi}\,k^{3/2} = 4.555\,k^{3/2} = 1.435\,M_\odot .
> $$
>
> Restored: $M = C\,(c^2/G)\,k^{3/2}$ with $k^{3/2} = K_4^{3/2}G^{-1/2}c^{-2}$, so $M = C\,K_4^{3/2}/G^{3/2}$; and $K_4^{3/2} = (\hbar c/4)^{3/2}(3\pi^2)^{1/2}/(\mu_em_p)^2$, so $M = C\,\frac{\sqrt3\,\pi}{8}\,(\hbar c/G)^{3/2}/(\mu_em_p)^2$. With $C = 4\omega_3/\sqrt\pi$ the prefactor is $\omega_3\sqrt{3\pi}/2 = 3.098$; times $1.853\,M_\odot/\mu_e^2$ this is $1.435\,M_\odot$.
>
> **Step 6** (general relativity, part 4). The real gas has $\gamma > \tfrac43$ at every finite $x$, by $\tfrac{2}{3x^2}$ (Step 2): in Newton's theory the excess makes $M(\rho_c)$ rise toward $M_{\rm Ch}$ from below without a maximum. The TOV factors strengthen gravity by relative amounts of order $p/\rho$ and $2m/r$, which grow with $\rho_c$ (at fixed mass $R \propto \rho_c^{-1/3}$, so $M/R \propto x$), while the excess stiffness falls as $x^{-2}$. Somewhere the growing correction overtakes the shrinking excess, and beyond that point adding central density no longer adds mass. Where that happens is found by integrating the TOV equations ([[#^thm-c9-3-3|Theorem §C9.3.3]]): $x_c = 22.9$, $\rho_c = 2.4\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$. The precise analytic criterion for this onset (due to Chandrasekhar) is not in the typed sources and is not derived here.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The Chandrasekhar mass is fixed by dimensional analysis up to a number: an $n_{\rm p} = 3$ polytrope has no length scale that could set its mass.
> - The same marginality makes white dwarfs near the limit sensitive to the smallest extra attraction, so that general relativity matters there although it is a correction of $10^{-3}$.

## The families of cold stars

For a given equation of state, [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-7|Theorem §C9.1.7]] makes $M$ and $R$ functions of one number, $\rho_c$. Computing them is the content of the next theorem.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.3.3: The Mass–Radius Curve of Cold Stars
> Integrating the TOV equations ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-4|Theorem §C9.1.4]]) from a regular centre with $\rho(0) = \rho_c$ ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-7|Theorem §C9.1.7]]) to the surface $p = 0$ ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^def-c9-1-3|Def. §C9.1.3]]), for the matter of [[#^mod-c9-3-1|Model §C9.3.1]]:
> 1. **White-dwarf matter** ($\mu_e = 2$). $M(\rho_c)$ rises from $0$; $M = 1.00\,M_\odot$ at $\rho_c = 3.2\times10^7\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$ with $R = 5550\ \mathrm{km}$. It reaches a **maximum** $M = 1.396\,M_\odot$ at $\rho_c = 2.36\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$ ($x_c = 22.9$), $R = 1019\ \mathrm{km}$, $2M/R = 4.0\times10^{-3}$, and falls beyond. The radius decreases monotonically along the rising branch. Newton's hydrostatics with the same matter gives masses rising monotonically to $1.435\,M_\odot$.
> 2. **Ideal neutron gas.** $M(\rho_c)$ rises from $0$ ($0.50\,M_\odot$ at $\rho_c = 4.2\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$, $R = 15.6\ \mathrm{km}$) to a **maximum**
>
> $$
> M_{\max} = 0.710\,M_\odot, \qquad R = 9.16\ \mathrm{km}, \qquad \frac{2M}{R} = 0.229, \qquad \rho_c = 4.21\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}\ \ (x_c = 0.834,\ p_c/\rho_c = 0.096) ,
> $$
>
>    and then falls to a minimum $0.368\,M_\odot$ ($x_c = 3.55$, $R = 5.6\ \mathrm{km}$), after which it oscillates with shrinking amplitude: in the $(R, M)$ plane the curve spirals into a limit point. Newton's hydrostatics with the same matter gives $1.65\,M_\odot$ at the same $\rho_c$ and masses rising to $5.73\,M_\odot$.
> 3. **The core at infinite density.** For $p = \rho/3$, the limit $x \to \infty$ of both gases, the TOV equations have the self-similar solution
>
> $$
> \rho = \frac{3}{56\pi r^2}, \qquad m = \frac{3}{14}\,r, \qquad \frac{2m}{r} = \frac37 ,
> $$
>
>    singular at $r = 0$ and without a surface (Schutz, Exercise 10.16). The cores of the stars far out on the spiral approach it.
> 4. **⚑ A maximum at finite density is generic.** In both cases general relativity turns a Newtonian sequence that only approaches its limit into one with a maximum at finite $\rho_c$; at the maximum the number of baryons in the star, $N = \int_0^R4\pi r^2n\,(1 - 2m/r)^{-1/2}\,dr$, is maximal too (neutron gas: both at $x_c = 0.8339$; binding energy $1 - M/Nm_n = 3.6\%$).
>

>

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (variables). Use $x$ as the dependent variable instead of $p$; then the equation of state needs no inversion. Since $n\,d\mu = dp$ at $T = 0$ (Step 2 of [[#^der-c9-3-2|Derivation §C9.3.2]]: $d\rho = \mu\,dn$ with chemical potential $\mu = (\rho + p)/n = \sqrt{m^2c^4 + p_F^2c^2}$), and $\mu = mc^2\sqrt{1 + x^2}$, $n \propto x^3$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dp}{dx} = n\,\frac{d\mu}{dx} = \frac{x^3}{3\pi^2\lambda^3}\,\frac{mc^2x}{\sqrt{1 + x^2}} = \frac{K_m}{3}\,\frac{x^4}{\sqrt{1 + x^2}} ,
> $$
>
> (direct differentiation of $p(x)$ gives the same). The system integrated outward is, in $G = c = 1$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dm}{dr} = 4\pi r^2\rho(x), \qquad \frac{dx}{dr} = -\frac{(\rho + p)(m + 4\pi r^3p)}{r(r - 2m)}\Bigl/\frac{dp}{dx}, \qquad \frac{dN}{dr} = \frac{4\pi r^2n(x)}{\sqrt{1 - 2m/r}} .
> $$
>
> The last equation counts baryons in the proper volume $e^{\Psi}4\pi r^2dr$ ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^cau-c9-1-1|§C9.1, Caution: M is not the sum of the energies of the fluid elements]]).
>
> **Step 2** (start). At $r = 0$ the right-hand side of $dx/dr$ is $0/0$. Start at a small $r_1$ from the series of [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-7|Theorem §C9.1.7]], part 1: $p(r_1) = p_c - \tfrac{2\pi}{3}(\rho_c + p_c)(\rho_c + 3p_c)r_1^2$, $m(r_1) = \tfrac43\pi\rho_cr_1^3$, with $r_1$ chosen so that $p_c - p(r_1) = 10^{-8}p_c$ (Schutz's Exercise 10.15 asks for this).
>
> **Step 3** (surface). Integrate (Dormand–Prince, relative tolerance $10^{-10}$) until $x$ falls to $10^{-3}$ of its central value (or of $1$). Near the surface $\rho \propto x^3$ (white dwarfs) and $dp/dx \propto x^4$, so $x^2 \propto R - r$: stopping at $x = 10^{-3}$ misses a shell of relative thickness about $10^{-6}$, negligible. Then $M = m(R)$ ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-5|Theorem §C9.1.5]]).
>
> **Step 4** (sequences). Repeat for $x_c$ on a logarithmic grid and locate the extrema of $M(x_c)$ and $N(x_c)$ by one-dimensional minimisation. Newton's sequences use the same code with $dp/dr = -\rho_0m/r^2$, $dm/dr = 4\pi r^2\rho_0$ and the rest-mass density $\rho_0$ (Chandrasekhar's model). The numbers of parts 1–2 and 4 are the output. Checks: the Newtonian white-dwarf masses approach the Lane–Emden value $1.435\,M_\odot$ of [[#^thm-c9-3-2|Theorem §C9.3.2]] ($1.4352$ at $x_c = 300$); a second integrator, in $\ln p$ instead of $x$, reproduces the neutron-gas maximum.
>
> **Step 5** (part 3). Try $\rho = \mu/r^2$, $m = \nu r$, $p = \rho/3$. Then $dm/dr = \nu$ and $4\pi r^2\rho = 4\pi\mu$, so $\nu = 4\pi\mu$. For the pressure, $dp/dr = -2\mu/(3r^3)$, and the right side of TOV is
>
> $$
> -\frac{\bigl(\tfrac{4\mu}{3r^2}\bigr)\bigl(\nu r + \tfrac{4\pi\mu}{3}r\bigr)}{r^2(1 - 2\nu)} = -\frac{4\mu}{3r^3}\,\frac{\nu + \nu/3}{1 - 2\nu} = -\frac{4\mu}{3r^3}\,\frac{4\nu/3}{1 - 2\nu} .
> $$
>
> Equating, $\tfrac23 = \tfrac43\cdot\tfrac{4\nu/3}{1 - 2\nu}$, i.e. $1 - 2\nu = \tfrac83\nu$, so $\nu = \tfrac{3}{14}$ and $\mu = \nu/4\pi = 3/(56\pi)$. There is no constant to set a scale: if $(\rho(r), m(r))$ solves the equations with $p = \rho/3$, so does $(\lambda^2\rho(\lambda r), m(\lambda r)/\lambda)$, and this solution is the fixed point of that rescaling.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The curves are numerical solutions of exact equations; nothing beyond the equation of state is approximated.
> - The spiral is the regular solutions oscillating about the singular one of Step 5 as $\rho_c$ grows; each turn of the spiral is an extremum of $M$ where stability can change ([[#^thm-c9-3-4|Theorem §C9.3.4]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.3.4: Stability along the Sequence
> Along the family $\rho_c \mapsto (M(\rho_c), R(\rho_c))$ of cold stars of one equation of state ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-7|Theorem §C9.1.7]]):
> 1. **Radial modes** (quoted). Small spherical oscillations of a star are superpositions of normal modes $\xi_n(r)e^{\pm i\omega_nt}$, $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, where $\xi$ is the radial displacement of a fluid element, $\xi_n$ has $n$ nodes in $0 < r < R$, $\xi_n(0) = 0$, and $\omega_n^2$ is real. If $\omega_n^2 > 0$ the mode oscillates; if $\omega_n^2 < 0$ it grows as $e^{|\omega_n|t}$. The star is stable against radial perturbations exactly when all $\omega_n^2 > 0$.
> 2. **Changes of stability sit at extrema.** As $\rho_c$ varies, some $\omega_n^2$ can change sign only where $dM/d\rho_c = 0$; there $dN/d\rho_c = 0$ as well, $N$ the number of baryons.
> 3. **The rule.** Stars at low $\rho_c$ are stable. At an extremum where $R$ decreases with $\rho_c$, a mode with no node changes stability; where $R$ increases, a mode with one node does. Hence, from low density up to the first maximum of $M(\rho_c)$, every star is stable ($dM/d\rho_c > 0$); just past it ($dM/d\rho_c < 0$) the fundamental mode is unstable, and for a single family like those of [[#^thm-c9-3-3|Theorem §C9.3.3]] no star beyond the first maximum is stable.
> 4. **Applied.** White-dwarf matter: stable for $\rho_c < 2.4\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$; ideal neutron gas: stable for $\rho_c < 4.2\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$. For ground-state matter (Hartle Fig. 24.7) the curve has several extrema, and two stable families result: white dwarfs, and neutron stars with $1.2\times10^{14} < \rho_c < 3\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$. Stars of intermediate central density are unstable and do not exist in nature.
>
> *What is proved here and what is quoted.* Part 1 (the mode spectrum, which needs the pulsation equation of a relativistic star, not derived in the typed sources) and the node-counting of part 3 are quoted from Hartle §24.5. The reasoning of part 2 is given below; the coincidence of the extrema of $M$ and $N$ is verified numerically, not proved.
>
> *Model:* [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^mod-c9-1-1|Model §C9.1.1]], perturbations that keep the star spherical and its composition fixed; *applies:* to radial (spherical) modes; rotation and non-radial modes, and slow (secular) instabilities, are outside it (Hartle fn. 4).
>
> *Source: Hartle §24.5, Ex. 24.1, Figs. 24.8–24.11, eqs. (24.19)–(24.25) (pp. 528–533, PDF 527–532) · Schutz §10.7 (p. 285, PDF 303) · Couzens, GR2, §3.3, Fig. 7 (p. 63, PDF 64: "unstable" branches) · the $N$–$M$ coincidence checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation (of part 2, with the reasoning behind part 3)
> **Step 1** (mechanics). For a particle in a potential $V(x)$, small displacements $\xi$ about an equilibrium $x_A$ obey $m\ddot\xi = -V''(x_A)\xi$ (Hartle (24.19)): solutions $e^{\pm i\omega t}$ with $\omega^2 = V''(x_A)/m$. A minimum ($\omega^2 > 0$) is stable, a maximum ($\omega^2 < 0$) is not: one solution grows as $e^{|\omega|t}$, and one growing perturbation is enough to destroy the equilibrium. A star has infinitely many such degrees of freedom, its normal modes (part 1).
>
> **Step 2** (a mode changing stability has zero frequency). Along the family, $\omega_n^2(\rho_c)$ varies continuously; to change sign it passes through $\omega_n^2 = 0$. A zero-frequency mode is a time-independent displacement $\xi_n(r)$: the displaced star is again static.
>
> **Step 3** (it is a neighbour in the family). A static cold star with a regular centre is a member of the family ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-7|Theorem §C9.1.7]], part 3), so the displaced star is the member with $\rho_c + \delta\rho_c$, and its mass and baryon number change by $(dM/d\rho_c)\delta\rho_c$ and $(dN/d\rho_c)\delta\rho_c$.
>
> **Step 4** (conservation). The displacement moves the fluid of the same star: no baryon is added, so $\delta N = 0$; and a mode of zero frequency carries no energy, so $\delta M = 0$ (Hartle §24.5). With $\delta\rho_c \neq 0$ this gives $dN/d\rho_c = dM/d\rho_c = 0$. The computation of [[#^thm-c9-3-3|Theorem §C9.3.3]], part 4, finds the maxima of $M$ and $N$ at the same $x_c = 0.8339$, as this requires.
>
> **Step 5** (which mode). Take the zero mode in the direction of increasing $\rho_c$: the centre is compressed, $\xi < 0$ near $r = 0$. A mode with no node keeps that sign out to the surface, so the surface moves in, $\delta R < 0$; a mode with one node changes sign once, so $\delta R > 0$ (Hartle Fig. 24.10). At the first maximum of the curves of [[#^thm-c9-3-3|Theorem §C9.3.3]] $R$ decreases with $\rho_c$ (figure), so it is the fundamental mode that becomes unstable there. That it becomes unstable rather than stable is the quoted part: stability at low density and continuity fix it.
>
> **Step 6** (Newtonian cross-check). For a Newtonian star of uniform $\gamma$ the energy argument of [[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^rem-b13-3-1|TH §B13.3★, Remark: Why relativity is fatal]] gives stability for $\gamma > \tfrac43$ and marginality at $\tfrac43$; there the polytrope's mass is independent of $\rho_c$ ([[#^thm-c9-3-2|Theorem §C9.3.2]], part 2), $dM/d\rho_c = 0$ along the whole sequence, consistent with part 2.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The curve $M(\rho_c)$, which comes from static equations alone, carries the dynamical information that would otherwise need the pulsation equation: stability can only change where the curve is flat.

## Observed neutron stars

> [!law] Law §C9.3.5: Observed Neutron Stars
> Neutron stars are observed as radio pulsars, spinning magnetised neutron stars whose beamed emission sweeps past the Earth (Hartle Box 24.2; Schutz §10.7), and through their mergers. As stated in the typed sources, with their dates:
> 1. **Masses cluster near $1.4\,M_\odot$.** Most masses measured in binary pulsars lie near $1.4\,M_\odot$ (Schutz 2022, citing Lorimer 2008 and Stairs 2003). Examples in the vault: both stars of the Hulse–Taylor pulsar ([[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral#^law-c8-6-6|Law §C8.6.6]]); the two stars of GW170817, $1.44$ and $1.28\,M_\odot$ ([[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral#^law-c8-6-7|Law §C8.6.7]], part 2).
> 2. **The heaviest.** PSR J0740+6620, a millisecond pulsar in a binary, has $M = 2.14^{+0.10}_{-0.09}\,M_\odot$, the largest pulsar mass measured as of Schutz 2022 (citing Cromartie et al. 2020). Known neutron stars lie below about $2\,M_\odot$, black holes in binaries start around $5\,M_\odot$ (Schutz, mid-2021). GW190814 had a component of at least $2.5\,M_\odot$ that may be either (Schutz 2022).
> 3. **Radii about $10$–$12\ \mathrm{km}$.** "The physics of these stars, along with data from radio and gravitational wave observations, all suggest radii around 10–12 km" (Schutz 2022); gravitational waves from neutron-star mergers are "beginning to measure the masses and radii", with large uncertainties (Schutz 2022, citing Abbott et al. 2018). No typed source gives a numerical radius or tidal bound from GW170817.
> 4. **The maximum mass is not known.** Proposed equations of state give maxima up to about $2.5\,M_\odot$, and "it is generally accepted that the maximum is less than $3\,M_\odot$"; no causal equation of state supports more than about $3\,M_\odot$ (Schutz 2022; Couzens 2025: $M_{\rm critical} \approx 2\,M_\odot$, causal bound about $3\,M_\odot$; Hartle 2003: about $2\,M_\odot$ from ground-state matter, Fig. 24.7). d'Inverno & Vickers (2022) write "approximately … 4 solar masses", above the causal bound ([[#^cau-c9-3-2|Caution: Slips in the sources on compact stars]]).
>
> Pulsar spin frequencies range from $0.04\ \mathrm{Hz}$ to $716\ \mathrm{Hz}$ (PSR J1748−2446ad), and surface fields from $10^8$ to $10^{15}\ \mathrm G$ (Schutz 2022).
>
> *Valid when:* the masses come from the orbits of binaries, with relativistic timing effects fixing both masses (pulsars) or the chirp mass and the components (mergers); radii are inferred through models and are much less certain than masses.
>
> *Model:* observations interpreted with general relativity; *applies:* as of the sources' dates (Hartle 2003; Schutz, d'Inverno & Vickers 2022; Couzens 2025).
>
> *Source: Schutz §10.7, pp. 286–288 (PDF 304–306); §11.4, p. 339 (PDF 357) · Hartle §24.5–24.6, Box 24.2 (pp. 533–535, PDF 532–534) · Couzens, GR2, §3 introduction, p. 53 (PDF 54); §3.2, p. 61 (PDF 62) · d'Inverno & Vickers §17.11, p. 337 (PDF 352)*

^law-c9-3-5

Set the law against the computed families. The ideal neutron gas supports at most $0.710\,M_\odot$ ([[#^thm-c9-3-3|Theorem §C9.3.3]]); the observed stars of $1.4\,M_\odot$ are twice that, PSR J0740+6620 three times ($2.14/0.710 = 3.01$). The compactness of a $1.4\,M_\odot$ star of radius $12\ \mathrm{km}$ is $2M/R = 0.345$, of $2.14\,M_\odot$ at $12\ \mathrm{km}$, $0.527$: both well inside Buchdahl's bound $8/9$, and both relativistic in every TOV factor.

> [!caution] Caution: Ideal-gas neutron stars are too light
> The maximum $0.710\,M_\odot$ of [[#^thm-c9-3-3|Theorem §C9.3.3]] is a property of the ideal gas, not of neutron stars. At its central density, $4.2\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$, $14.5$ times nuclear density ($2.9\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$, Hartle §24.6), the neutrons are packed closer than the range of the strong force, whose repulsive part dominates the pressure and makes the matter much stiffer than a free gas (Hartle Box 24.1: the stiffness $\gamma$ "rises steadily … until it is well above nuclear density"). A free gas is as soft as matter gets at such densities: $p < \rho/3$, and $\gamma \to \tfrac43$. Stiffer matter supports more mass ([[#^ex-c9-3-1|Example §C9.3.1]]), up to about $3\,M_\odot$ for the stiffest causal matter. The observed $2.14\,M_\odot$ therefore rules out the free-gas equation of state, and any equation of state too soft to reach it. The ideal gas still gives the right scales: radii of ten kilometres, compactness $0.2$, densities above nuclear.

## Above the maximum mass: collapse

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.3.6: Above the Maximum Mass a Cold Star Must Collapse
> Let the matter obey a cold equation of state with maximum mass $M_{\max}$ along its family ([[#^thm-c9-3-3|Theorem §C9.3.3]]); for every equation of state known up to $\rho_0 = 5\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$, $M_{\max} \lesssim 5\,M_\odot$ ([[§C9.2 The Uniform-Density Star and Buchdahl's Limit#^thm-c9-2-6|Theorem §C9.2.6]]). Consider a spherical star of this matter with mass $M > M_{\max}$, for instance a stellar core that has cooled.
> 1. **No equilibrium.** There is no static star of mass $M$: the star cannot stay at rest.
> 2. **The outside stays Schwarzschild.** As long as the star stays spherically symmetric, the metric in the vacuum outside its surface is the Schwarzschild metric with the same constant $M$, however the inside moves ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-5|Theorem §C6.2.5]], [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-6|Theorem §C6.2.6]]). The mass can change only by matter thrown out.
> 3. **Inside $2M$ nothing can stop it.** If the surface reaches $r < 2M$, it reaches $r = 0$ within a proper time at most $\pi M$ (restored $\pi GM/c^3$), whatever the pressure; no signal sent from it afterwards reaches $r > 2M$. The region $r \le 2M$ outside the matter is then part of a black hole, with event horizon $r = 2M$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]]).
> 4. **Free fall reaches $2M$.** If the matter is pressure-free (dust), the surface falls on a radial geodesic: from rest at $R$ it crosses $r = 2M$ after a finite proper time, $r = 0$ after $\tau = \pi\sqrt{R^3/8M}$, while a distant observer sees it approach $r = 2M$ ever more slowly and redden by a factor $e$ every $4M$ ([[#^ex-c9-3-2|Example §C9.3.2]]).
>
> *What is proved here and what is not.* Parts 1–4 are proved below. That a cold star above $M_{\max}$ actually falls inside $2M$, rather than bouncing or shedding enough mass, is not: for spherical dust it is exact (part 4; Oppenheimer and Snyder, [[#^rem-c9-3-1|Remark: Collapse to a black hole]]). In general it follows once a trapped surface forms (Penrose, Hawking; Couzens fn. 12), which is C10 (planned). Couzens' conclusion is "complete gravitational collapse unless they shed some of their mass".
>
> *Model:* [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^mod-c9-1-1|Model §C9.1.1]] for part 1; any spherically symmetric matter with vacuum outside for parts 2–3; dust for part 4; *applies:* exactly within these models.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §3 introduction and fn. 12 (p. 53, PDF 54); §3.2, p. 61 (PDF 62); §3.3 (p. 62, PDF 63) · Schutz §10.7, p. 280 (PDF 298) · Hartle §12.2 (pp. 262–265, PDF 263–266: "no new source of pressure at high densities can save it") · d'Inverno & Vickers §17.8, Figs. 17.13–17.14 (pp. 332–334, PDF 347–349)*

^thm-c9-3-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). Suppose a static star of mass $M$ existed. It is described by [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^mod-c9-1-1|Model §C9.1.1]] with a regular centre ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-5|Theorem §C9.1.5]]) and some central density $\rho_c$. By [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-7|Theorem §C9.1.7]], part 3, it is the member of the family with that $\rho_c$, so $M = M(\rho_c) \le M_{\max}$, a contradiction.
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). The region outside the surface is a spherically symmetric vacuum. Birkhoff's theorem ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-5|Theorem §C6.2.5]]) makes it locally the Schwarzschild metric, static, with a constant $M$; [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-6|Theorem §C6.2.6]] states the consequence for a moving surface. Far away, $M$ is the mass that governs Keplerian orbits ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-4|Theorem §C6.2.4]]), so it is the star's mass before the collapse.
>
> **Step 3** (part 3, the surface). A fluid element on the surface moves on a timelike worldline; it lies in the exterior, on its inner edge. Inside $r = 2M$ the exterior is region II of the Schwarzschild spacetime, where $r$ decreases along every future-directed causal curve, with $|dr/d\tau| \ge \sqrt{2M/r - 1}$ ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]], parts 2 and 4). Hence, from $r_a < 2M$,
>
> $$
> \tau \le \int_0^{r_a}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{2M/r - 1}} \le \int_0^{2M}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{2M/r - 1}} = \pi M ,
> $$
>
> the integral evaluated with $r = 2M\sin^2\psi$ ([[§C7.4 Falling into a Black Hole#^thm-c7-4-4|Theorem §C7.4.4]]). The bound holds for any force on the element, so for any pressure.
>
> **Step 4** (part 3, signals). Light emitted inside $r = 2M$ also has $r$ decreasing ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]], part 4); the surface $r = 2M$ is a null surface crossed only inward ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]]). So the region inside $2M$ cannot send signals to $r > 2M$, which is the definition of the black-hole region and its horizon ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]]).
>
> **Step 5** (part 4). Dust has $p = 0$, and ∇·T = 0 then makes every dust particle move on a geodesic ([[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c4-4-5|Theorem §C4.4.5]]). The outermost particle moves in the exterior Schwarzschild metric (Step 2) on a radial geodesic. From rest at $R$, [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]] gives the cycloid $r = \tfrac12R(1 + \cos\eta)$, $\tau = \sqrt{R^3/8M}\,(\eta + \sin\eta)$, which reaches $r = 2M$ at $\cos\eta_H = 4M/R - 1$ and $r = 0$ at $\eta = \pi$. The distant view is [[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^ex-c7-1-2|Example §C7.1.2]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Part 1 uses only the family of static stars; parts 2–3 only the exterior geometry. Nothing about the inside of the collapsing star is needed to see that a black hole forms once the surface is inside $2M$.
> - The bound of Step 3 is a statement about the spacetime, not about the matter: no pressure, however large, can turn an element round inside $2M$. This is the sense of Hartle's "no new source of pressure at high densities can save it".

> [!remark] Remark: Collapse to a black hole
> Drawn in ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^ex-c7-2-2|Example §C7.2.2]]), the surface of a collapsing star is a timelike worldline that crosses $r = 2M$ at a finite advanced time $v$ and ends at $r = 0$. Only the part of the diagram outside it is Schwarzschild; inside is the star (Hartle Figs. 12.3–12.4; d'Inverno & Vickers Figs. 17.13–17.14, the second the first rotated about the time axis). Signals sent outward from the surface at equal intervals of its proper time reach a distant observer at ever longer intervals, and none sent after the crossing arrive. In the Kruskal diagram the same worldline cuts away everything to its left: no white hole, no second exterior ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^rem-c7-3-1|§C7.3, Remark: Which parts are physical]]). The interior of a collapsing ball of uniform dust was found by Oppenheimer and Snyder (1939): the inside is a piece of a contracting homogeneous dust universe, matched at the surface to Schwarzschild. None of the sources derives it, and it is not derived here. Where the event horizon forms inside the matter, the trapped surfaces of Couzens' footnote 12, and the singularity theorems that make collapse inevitable without spherical symmetry are C10 (planned).

> [!caution] Caution: Slips in the sources on compact stars
> Checked against the rendered pages and by script:
> - *Couzens, §3 introduction, pp. 52–53.* "A critical mass $M_C$, (??)" is a broken reference to the Chandrasekhar limit, $\approx 1.4\,M_\odot$, named on the next page. A star too light to fuse helium does not become "a red dwarf" (a low-mass star still burning hydrogen) but ends as a helium white dwarf (Schutz §10.7, p. 281). "$M_{\rm critical}$ $2M_\odot$" lacks its $\approx$; "Hawing" is Hawking.
> - *Schutz (10.85), p. 285.* The crude Chandrasekhar mass is printed $M = (3k^3/4\pi)^{1/2} = (1/32\mu^2m_p^2)(6h^3/\pi)^{1/2}$. The two expressions are not equal. His (10.83), $M = R^3\bar\rho$, gives $M = k^{3/2}$, which is the second expression and his number $0.32\,M_\odot$ ([[#^thm-c9-3-2|Theorem §C9.3.2]], Step 4 of the derivation); the first, $0.15\,M_\odot$, belongs to $M = \tfrac43\pi R^3\bar\rho$.
> - *Schutz, p. 285.* The white-dwarf limit is said to lie "at central densities of about $10^{10}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$". At that density the ideal sequence has only $0.80\,M_\odot$; its maximum is at $2.4\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ ($2.4\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$). Probably g/cm³ was meant. His precise limit "$\approx 1.3\,M_\odot$" fits iron cores ($\mu_e = 56/26$: $1.24\,M_\odot$) rather than carbon–oxygen ($1.44\,M_\odot$).
> - *Schutz, p. 286.* A particle at rest on a star's surface has energy at infinity $m\sqrt{1 - 2M/R}$ ([[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay#^thm-c6-6-1|Theorem §C6.6.1]]), not "$m\sqrt{1 - M/R}$"; for $M/R = 0.15$ the neutrons have given up $16\%$ of their rest energy, not "about 8%".
> - *Hartle, Figs. 24.4–24.5, pp. 524–525.* The white-dwarf models are described as electrons with "an equal number of protons". The printed numbers ($\rho_c = 10^{10}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$: $p_c = 1.06\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{dyn/cm^2}$, $R = 1314\ \mathrm{km}$, $1.42\,M_\odot$; limit $1.4\,M_\odot$) are those of two nucleons per electron, $\mu_e = 2$ (computed: $1.045\times10^{28}$, $1308\ \mathrm{km}$, $1.39\,M_\odot$). One proton per electron would give $p_c = 2.6\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{dyn/cm^2}$, $5.5\,M_\odot$ at that density, and a limit of $5.7\,M_\odot$.
> - *Couzens, §3.3, p. 62.* "An upper bound on the maximal size of any cold star" means its mass ([[§C9.2 The Uniform-Density Star and Buchdahl's Limit#^thm-c9-2-6|Theorem §C9.2.6]]); "hot starts" means hot stars.
> - *d'Inverno & Vickers, §17.11, p. 337.* The maximum neutron-star mass is given as "approximately … 4 solar masses", without a reference; the other sources say about $2$–$2.5$ and below $3\,M_\odot$, and the stiffest causal matter gives about $3\,M_\odot$ ([[#^ex-c9-3-1|Example §C9.3.1]]). Treat it as a query.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C9.3.1: A 1.4 Solar-Mass Neutron Star from the Ideal Neutron Gas?
> *Question.* The typical observed neutron star has $1.4\,M_\odot$ ([[#^law-c9-3-5|Law §C9.3.5]]). Can the ideal neutron gas of [[#^mod-c9-3-1|Model §C9.3.1]] make one, and if not, what does?
>
> *Newton's answer.* [[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^thm-b13-3-7|TH Theorem §B13.3.7]] (uniform density, nonrelativistic neutrons) gives $R \approx 12\ \mathrm{km}\,(1.4)^{-1/3} = 10.7\ \mathrm{km}$, and Newton's hydrostatics with the full ideal-gas pressure has stars up to $5.73\,M_\odot$. A $1.4\,M_\odot$ star exists comfortably.
>
> **Step 1** (TOV). The TOV family of the same gas peaks at $0.710\,M_\odot$ ([[#^thm-c9-3-3|Theorem §C9.3.3]], part 2), so the equation $M(\rho_c) = 1.4\,M_\odot$ has no solution: no star of this matter has $1.4\,M_\odot$, stable or not. Raising $\rho_c$ beyond $4.2\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$ lowers the mass: the added pressure weighs more than it supports.
>
> **Step 2** (what is missing). At the maximum, $p_c/\rho_c = 0.096$ and $2M/R = 0.229$. To hold $1.4\,M_\odot$ inside $12\ \mathrm{km}$, $2M/R = 2\times1.4\times1.4767/12 = 0.345$, the matter must give more pressure at the same density: it must be stiffer.
>
> **Step 3** (the stiffest causal matter). Keep the ideal gas below $\rho_0 = 5\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$ (Couzens' density, where $x = 0.427$ and $p_0/\rho_0 = 0.033$) and above it take $p = p_0 + (\rho - \rho_0)$: sound speed $\sqrt{dp/d\rho} = 1$, the largest a causal equation of state allows ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^def-c9-1-2|Def. §C9.1.2]]). Integrating the TOV equations with this matter:
> - the family now reaches $M_{\max} = 3.05\,M_\odot$, at $R = 13.3\ \mathrm{km}$, $\rho_c = 1.46\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$, $2M/R = 0.68$;
> - a $1.4\,M_\odot$ star exists with $R = 13.2\ \mathrm{km}$, $\rho_c = 5.66\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$, $2M/R = 0.31$, $\rho_c$ barely above $\rho_0$.
>
> *Result.* The free gas cannot make the observed stars; stiffer matter can, and the stiffest causal matter allows $3.05\,M_\odot$, in line with Couzens' "about $3\,M_\odot$" (p. 61) and Schutz's causal bound. Real nuclear matter lies between: the observed $2.14\,M_\odot$ star requires it to be much stiffer than the free gas ([[#^cau-c9-3-1|Caution: Ideal-gas neutron stars are too light]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §3.2, p. 61 (PDF 62) · Schutz §10.7, p. 288 (PDF 306); §11.4, p. 339 (PDF 357) · Hartle §24.4, Box 24.1 (pp. 525–527, PDF 524–526) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C9.3.2: The Collapsing Surface of a Dust Star
> *Question* (Hartle, Example 12.1). A ball of pressure-free matter of mass $M = 1.4\,M_\odot$ is released from rest with radius $R$. How long does its surface take, on its own clock, to reach $r = 2M$ and $r = 0$, and what does a distant observer see?
>
> *Setup.* By [[#^thm-c9-3-6|Theorem §C9.3.6]], parts 2 and 4, the surface falls on the radial geodesic of the Schwarzschild metric with $E^2 = 1 - 2M/R$: $r = \tfrac12R(1 + \cos\eta)$, $\tau = \sqrt{R^3/8M}\,(\eta + \sin\eta)$. With $M = 1.4\times1.4767\ \mathrm{km} = 2.067\ \mathrm{km}$, and $\mathrm{km}/c = 3.336\ \mu\mathrm s$.
>
> **Step 1** (to the centre). At $\eta = \pi$,
>
> $$
> \tau_0 = \pi\sqrt{\frac{R^3}{8M}} = \frac\pi2\sqrt{\frac{R^3}{2M}} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \frac\pi2\sqrt{\frac{R^3}{2GM}}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> exactly Newton's free-fall time ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^ex-b2-2-2|CM Example §B2.2.2]]), now in proper time and areal radius.
>
> **Step 2** (numbers). An iron core of $1.4\,M_\odot$ the size of the Earth, $R = 6371\ \mathrm{km}$ (Schutz p. 281: the core falls "from the size of the Earth"): $\tau_0 = \tfrac\pi2\sqrt{(6.371\times10^6)^3/(2\times6.674\times10^{-11}\times2.784\times10^{30})}\ \mathrm s = 1.31\ \mathrm s$. Of this, the last stretch from $2M$ to $0$ takes $9.20\ \mu\mathrm s$. Hartle's surface falls from rest at infinity, where $\tau(r) \propto r^{3/2}$ by (12.7a): $\tau(2M \to 0) = \tfrac23(2M)^{3/2}/\sqrt{2M} = \tfrac43M = 6.57\ \mu\mathrm s$ per solar mass ("of order $10^{-5}$ s"), $9.19\ \mu\mathrm s$ for $1.4\,M_\odot$, almost the same, since a body falling from $6371\ \mathrm{km}$ arrives near $2M$ nearly as fast as one from infinity.
>
> **Step 3** (Buchdahl's star pushed in). Couzens' star at $R = 9M/4 = 4.65\ \mathrm{km}$, given a push ([[§C9.2 The Uniform-Density Star and Buchdahl's Limit#^ex-c9-2-2|Example §C9.2.2]]), if its matter then offered no resistance, would reach $2M$ after $10.8\ \mu\mathrm s$ and $r = 0$ after $25.9\ \mu\mathrm s$; inside $2M$ it spends $15.1\ \mu\mathrm s$, below the bound $\pi M = 21.6\ \mu\mathrm s$ of [[#^thm-c9-3-6|Theorem §C9.3.6]], part 3.
>
> **Step 4** (seen from far away). The distant observer receives the light of the surface ever more redshifted, its frequency and photon rate falling by $e$ every $4M = 27.6\ \mu\mathrm s$ of reception time ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^ex-c7-1-2|Example §C7.1.2]]). Within a millisecond the star has faded by $e^{-36}$: a black hole has formed, although the observer never sees the surface cross $r = 2M$ (Hartle Fig. 12.3; d'Inverno & Vickers Fig. 17.13).
>
> *Source: Hartle §12.2, Example 12.1, eqs. (12.7a)–(12.7b), Figs. 12.3–12.4 (pp. 262–264, PDF 263–265) · Schutz §10.7, p. 281 (PDF 299) · d'Inverno & Vickers §17.8, p. 333 (PDF 348) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C9.3.3: Spin-Up of a Collapsing Core
> *Question* (Schutz, Exercise 10.19). The Sun's equator turns at about $2\ \mathrm{km/s}$. If the Sun collapsed to a neutron star of radius $10\ \mathrm{km}$, keeping its mass and angular momentum, how fast would it spin? Compare a young pulsar spinning at $30\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and the magnetic field.
>
> **Step 1** (the Sun). Rigid rotation, uniform density (a lower bound, since the interior probably turns faster): $\Omega_\odot = v/R_\odot = 2\times10^3/6.957\times10^8 = 2.875\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ (period $25.3$ days), moment of inertia $\tfrac25MR^2$ ([[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-5|CM Theorem §B3.2.5]]), so
>
> $$
> J = \tfrac25M_\odot R_\odot^2\,\Omega_\odot = 0.4\times1.988\times10^{30}\times(6.957\times10^8)^2\times2.875\times10^{-6} = 1.11\times10^{42}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (collapse). With $J$ conserved ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-4|CM Theorem §A6.6.4]]) and $I \propto R^2$, $\Omega = \Omega_\odot(R_\odot/R)^2 = 2.875\times10^{-6}\times(6.957\times10^4)^2 = 1.39\times10^4\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, a period of $0.45\ \mathrm{ms}$.
>
> **Step 3** (too fast). The ratio of centrifugal to gravitational acceleration at the equator is
>
> $$
> \frac{\Omega^2R}{GM/R^2} = \frac{\Omega^2R^3}{GM} = \frac{(1.391\times10^4)^2\times(10^4)^3}{1.327\times10^{20}} = 1.46 > 1 :
> $$
>
> the collapsed Sun would fly apart at its equator; break-up is at $\Omega = \sqrt{GM/R^3} = 1.15\times10^4\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ (period $0.54\ \mathrm{ms}$, Schutz p. 286). A collapsing core must lose most of its angular momentum on the way, or stay non-spherical; the spherical [[#^thm-c9-3-6|Theorem §C9.3.6]] is an idealisation.
>
> **Step 4** (a pulsar). At $30\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $\Omega = 188.5\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ and the ratio is $188.5^2\times10^{12}/1.327\times10^{20} = 2.7\times10^{-4}$: "slowly rotating", and the static spherical star of [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^mod-c9-1-1|Model §C9.1.1]] describes it well (Hartle Box 24.2: centrifugal forces much less than gravitational).
>
> **Step 5** (field). With the magnetic flux $BR^2$ conserved, a $1\ \mathrm G$ field becomes $(6.957\times10^4)^2\ \mathrm G = 4.8\times10^9\ \mathrm G$; the Crab pulsar has about $10^{11}\ \mathrm G$ (Schutz), and Hartle's Box 24.2 quotes amplification of a few gauss to $10^{12}\ \mathrm G$.
>
> *Source: Schutz, Exercise 10.19 (p. 295, PDF 313); §10.7, p. 286 (PDF 304) · Hartle Box 24.2 (p. 534, PDF 533) · numbers checked by script*
