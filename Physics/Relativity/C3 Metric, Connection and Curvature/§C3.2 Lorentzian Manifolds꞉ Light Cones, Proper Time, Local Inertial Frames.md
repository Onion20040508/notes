[[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor|§C3.1]] put a metric on a manifold: a symmetric, nondegenerate $(0, 2)$ tensor field ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]), called Lorentzian when its signature is $(-, +, \dots, +)$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]). Chapter C1 had already used three things such a metric is meant to supply, each in a single chart. Light cones narrow toward a mass ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]]). Clocks read the proper time of their worldlines ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]]). In a small freely falling laboratory physics is special relativity ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]], [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]). This section asks what a Lorentzian metric gives at one event and near it, when no chart is preferred. *Is the metric at a single event the Minkowski metric? How is "the future" chosen when the light cones change from event to event? And how close to Minkowski can a chart make the metric near an event?*

Two naive answers fail, and the failures shape the section. The first takes the future to be the direction in which $x^0$ increases, as in special relativity ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^def-b1-3-1|Def. §B1.3.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where "future-pointing" means $a^0 > 0$). That depends on the chart. In Minkowski spacetime the chart $t' = -t$ is as good as $t$, and in it a future-pointing vector has $a'^0 < 0$. Worse, the coordinate vector $\partial_t$ need not lie inside the light cone at all. Take the two-dimensional metric

$$
ds^2 = -dt^2 + \bigl(dx - v(x)\,dt\bigr)^2, \qquad g_{tt} = -1 + v^2, \quad g_{tx} = -v, \quad g_{xx} = 1 ,
$$

a static version of the line element of Hartle's Example 7.4. Then $g(\partial_t, \partial_t) = v^2 - 1$, so $\partial_t$ is timelike where $|v| < 1$ and spacelike where $|v| > 1$ (figure below). The future has to be fixed by the metric, as a continuous choice of one half of every light cone, and such a choice need not exist ([[#^ex-c3-2-4|Example §C3.2.4]]).

The second naive answer makes the metric Minkowski near an event by a linear change of coordinates. For the weak-field metric ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]) at a point $\vec x_0$ with $\Phi_0 = \Phi(\vec x_0)$, this is the rescaling of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]], Step 3: $\hat t = \sqrt{1 + 2\Phi_0}\,t$, $\hat x^i = \sqrt{1 - 2\Phi_0}\,x^i$. It gives $\hat g_{\mu\nu}(\vec x_0) = \eta_{\mu\nu}$, but

$$
\hat g_{00} = -\frac{1 + 2\Phi(\vec x)}{1 + 2\Phi_0}, \qquad \frac{\partial\hat g_{00}}{\partial x^i}\Big|_{\vec x_0} = -\frac{2\,\partial_i\Phi(\vec x_0)}{1 + 2\Phi_0} \neq 0 ,
$$

and a free particle released at $\vec x_0$ falls with $d^2\hat x^i/d\hat t^2 = -\partial_i\Phi$ to first order in these coordinates ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]]). These are the coordinates of an observer held at rest, who feels gravity. A linear change of chart can fix the metric at the event, but not its first derivatives; those need the quadratic terms of the change of chart, and at the next order the counting fails for good.

The section builds the answers in order. At one event an orthonormal basis makes the metric exactly $\eta_{\mu\nu}$, and all of Minkowski vector algebra holds there ([[#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]). This gives the classification of vectors and the light cone at each point ([[#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]), a time orientation as the continuous choice of a future ([[#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]), and causal curves with their proper time ([[#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]). Near an event, a count of Taylor coefficients shows that a chart can make $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ and $\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 0$ at the event, but leaves $20$ combinations of second derivatives untouched ([[#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]]). This is the mathematical content of the Einstein equivalence principle ([[#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]]), and it defines the local inertial frame of general relativity ([[#^def-c3-2-4|Def. §C3.2.4]]).

**Setup.** A Lorentzian manifold $(M, g)$ of dimension $n \ge 2$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]). A spacetime is the case $n = 4$, connected ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]]) and time-oriented ([[#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]); the examples use $n = 2$ as well. Statements at one point are linear algebra in the tangent space $T_pM$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]). Statements near a point use Taylor expansion in a chart and the inverse function theorem. Every theorem is exact differential geometry, and no field equation is used. Physics enters in two places only: part 2 of [[#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]] uses the free-fall principle ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]), and [[#^def-c3-2-4|Def. §C3.2.4]] is read through the Einstein equivalence principle. The identities and numbers in the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]), in $n$ dimensions $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, \dots, 1)$; $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]); Greek indices are coordinate indices, $\mu = 0, \dots, n - 1$, with $x^0$ the time coordinate where there is one; Latin $a, b, c$ label the vectors $e_a$ of a basis or frame, as in [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]; Latin $i, j, k = 1, 2, 3$ are spatial. In the proofs a tilde marks a given chart $\tilde x^\alpha$ and its components $\tilde g_{\alpha\beta}$, and $\tilde\partial_\gamma = \partial/\partial\tilde x^\gamma$. ## The metric at one event

At a single point $p$ the metric is one symmetric bilinear form $g_p$ on the vector space $T_pM$. The question is whether it differs from the Minkowski form $\eta$ at all, and how much freedom is left in writing it as $\eta$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.2.1: Orthonormal Basis at a Point: g_μν(p) = η_μν
> Let $(M, g)$ be a Lorentzian manifold of dimension $n$ and $p \in M$.
> 1. **Orthonormal basis.** $T_pM$ has a basis $e_0, \dots, e_{n-1}$ with $g_p(e_a, e_b) = \eta_{ab}$: exactly one basis vector has $g(e, e) = -1$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 4, Sylvester's law of inertia), and it is labelled $e_0$.
> 2. **A chart with $g_{\mu\nu}(p) = \eta_{\mu\nu}$.** If $\tilde x^\alpha$ is any chart at $p$ with $\tilde x(p) = 0$ and $e_a = A^\alpha{}_a\,\tilde\partial_\alpha|_p$, the linear change of coordinates $\tilde x^\alpha = A^\alpha{}_\mu x^\mu$ gives a chart $x^\mu$ with $\partial_\mu|_p = e_\mu$ and
>
> $$
> g_{\mu\nu}(p) = \eta_{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> 3. **The freedom is the Lorentz group.** Two orthonormal bases at $p$ are related by $e'_b = e_a\Lambda^a{}_b$ with
>
> $$
> \Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\,\Lambda = \eta ,
> $$
>
> a Lorentz transformation ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]], [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, the same matrices because the condition is unchanged by $\eta \to -\eta$), and every such $\Lambda$ gives a new one. The Lorentz group has dimension $\tfrac12n(n - 1)$: $6$ for $n = 4$ (three boosts, three rotations), $1$ for $n = 2$.
> 4. **Minkowski vector algebra holds in each tangent space.** For $X, Y \in T_pM$:
>    - if $X$ is timelike ($g(X, X) < 0$) and $g(X, Y) = 0$ with $Y \neq 0$, then $g(Y, Y) > 0$; a timelike vector is never orthogonal to a nonzero null vector;
>    - if $X$ and $Y$ are timelike, $g(X, Y)^2 \ge g(X, X)\,g(Y, Y)$ (reversed Cauchy–Schwarz), with equality only for $Y \parallel X$; in particular $g(X, Y) \neq 0$;
>    - the timelike vectors at $p$ form two disjoint open convex cones $\mathcal T_p$ and $-\mathcal T_p$, and two timelike vectors lie in the same cone exactly when $g(X, Y) < 0$.
>
> *Model:* none, linear algebra in $T_pM$; *applies:* every point of every Lorentzian manifold, exact. Part 2 makes $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ at $p$ only; at other points the components in that chart are whatever the metric gives.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.2, eq. (4.17) (p. 76; stated, "it is always possible to find an orthonormal basis") · Schutz §6.2, eq. (6.2) and the remarks after it (p. 144: the matrix that produces $\eta$ at a point "may not be a coordinate transformation" elsewhere) · Hartle §7.4, eq. (7.12) (p. 140: diagonalize, then rescale; the numbers of $+1$s and $-1$s cannot be changed) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.8 (p. 100; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (orthonormal basis). By [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 4, $g_p$ is diagonal in some basis $f_a$ ([[§35 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-12|LADR Thm. 9.12]]), $g_p(f_a, f_b) = d_a\delta_{ab}$ with every $d_a \neq 0$ (nondegeneracy), and the number of negative $d_a$ is the same in every such basis. For a Lorentzian metric it is $1$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]). Rescale, $e_a = f_a/\sqrt{|d_a|}$, so that $g_p(e_a, e_a) = d_a/|d_a| = \pm1$, and number the vectors so that the negative one is $e_0$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (the chart). The map $x \mapsto \tilde x = Ax$ is linear with invertible matrix $A$ (its columns are the components of a basis), so $x^\mu = (A^{-1})^\mu{}_\alpha\tilde x^\alpha$ is again a chart on the same domain. By [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], part 1,
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu|_p = \frac{\partial\tilde x^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}\,\tilde\partial_\alpha|_p = A^\alpha{}_\mu\,\tilde\partial_\alpha|_p = e_\mu ,
> $$
>
> and by [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 1, $g_{\mu\nu}(p) = g_p(\partial_\mu, \partial_\nu) = g_p(e_\mu, e_\nu) = \eta_{\mu\nu}$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 3** (the freedom). Let $e_a$ and $e'_b$ be orthonormal and expand $e'_b = e_a\Lambda^a{}_b$ (any two bases are related by an invertible matrix). Bilinearity gives
>
> $$
> \eta_{bc} = g(e'_b, e'_c) = \Lambda^a{}_b\Lambda^d{}_c\,g(e_a, e_d) = \Lambda^a{}_b\,\eta_{ad}\,\Lambda^d{}_c = (\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda)_{bc} .
> $$
>
> Conversely, the same line shows that $e_a\Lambda^a{}_b$ is orthonormal whenever $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta$. The dimension: near $\Lambda = \mathbb 1$ write $\Lambda = \mathbb 1 + \omega$; to first order the condition is $\omega^{\mathsf T}\eta + \eta\omega = 0$, i.e. $\omega_{ab} \equiv \eta_{ac}\omega^c{}_b = -\omega_{ba}$. An antisymmetric $n \times n$ matrix has $\tfrac12n(n - 1)$ independent entries, the $n^2$ entries of $\omega$ minus the $\tfrac12n(n + 1)$ conditions. For $n = 4$: $16 - 10 = 6$, the boosts $\omega_{0i}$ and rotations $\omega_{ij}$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]). This is part 3.
>
> **Step 4** (Minkowski algebra). Components in an orthonormal basis, $X = X^ae_a$, identify $(T_pM, g_p)$ with $\mathbf R^n$ and the form $g_p(X, Y) = \eta_{ab}X^aY^b = -X^0Y^0 + \vec X\cdot\vec Y$. On $\mathbf R^4$ with the level-B form $\eta^{\rm B} = -\eta$, the first two bullets are [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]], parts 1 and 3, with every scalar product multiplied by $-1$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]], part 3): "timelike, $a^2 > 0$" there is $g(X, X) < 0$ here, and $(a\cdot b)^2 \ge a^2b^2$ is unchanged by the sign flip since both sides are products of two scalar products. The proofs there use only the form, so they hold for any $n$. For the third bullet, a timelike $X$ has $(X^0)^2 > |\vec X|^2 \ge 0$, so $X^0 \neq 0$. Let $\mathcal T_p = \{X : g(X, X) < 0,\ X^0 > 0\}$; the timelike vectors are $\mathcal T_p \cup (-\mathcal T_p)$, disjoint. $\mathcal T_p$ is open (two strict inequalities between continuous functions) and convex: for $X, Y \in \mathcal T_p$ and $s, t > 0$ with $s + t = 1$, the reversed triangle inequality ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]], part 4) gives $sX + tY \in \mathcal T_p$. For $X, Y \in \mathcal T_p$ the rest-frame computation of that theorem gives $g(X, Y) \le -\sqrt{g(X, X)\,g(Y, Y)} < 0$. Replacing $Y$ by $-Y$ gives $g(X, Y) > 0$ for $X$ and $Y$ in opposite cones. This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** At a single event a Lorentzian metric has no features at all: in a suitable basis it is $\eta$, and the bases that do this differ by Lorentz transformations. Everything that distinguishes one spacetime from another must therefore lie in how $g$ changes from point to point. The choice of basis was made at $p$ alone. The chart of part 2 has $\partial_\mu = e_\mu$ only at $p$, and nothing has been said about the derivatives of $g_{\mu\nu}$ there; that is the subject of [[#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]].

## Light cones and the direction of time

Since every tangent space is a copy of Minkowski space, special relativity's sorting of vectors applies at every event. What is new is that each event has its own copy, and nothing identifies the copies at different events.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.2.1: Timelike, Null and Spacelike Vectors; Light Cone at a Point
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be a Lorentzian manifold, $p \in M$, and $X \in T_pM$ a nonzero tangent vector ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]). $X$ is
> - **timelike** if $g_p(X, X) < 0$,
> - **null** (lightlike) if $g_p(X, X) = 0$,
> - **spacelike** if $g_p(X, X) > 0$;
>
> **causal** if it is timelike or null. The **light cone** (null cone) at $p$ is the set of null vectors in $T_pM$. In components, $g_p(X, X) = g_{\mu\nu}(p)X^\mu X^\nu$ in any chart ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]]), a number that does not depend on the chart, so the classification does not either.
>
> *Shape.* In an orthonormal basis at $p$ ([[#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]), $g_p(X, X) = -(X^0)^2 + |\vec X|^2$, so the light cone is $|\vec X| = |X^0|$, the light cone of Minkowski space, and the timelike vectors fill its two open halves ([[#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]], part 4). The cone lives in the vector space $T_pM$, not in $M$. Drawn in the coordinates of a chart, through the basis $\partial_\mu|_p$, the cones at different events have different openings and tilts (figure below; [[#^ex-c3-2-1|Example §C3.2.1]]). These are statements about the chart. The cone at each event, measured in an orthonormal basis there, is always the same.
>
> *Layer change.* [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^def-b1-3-1|Def. §B1.3.1]] (signature $+{-}{-}{-}$: timelike is $a^2 > 0$ there) classifies four-vectors of Minkowski spacetime, where all tangent spaces are identified with one $\mathbf R^4$ and one cone serves every event. Here there is one cone per event. In a chart the same test reads $ds^2 = g_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu < 0$, $= 0$, $> 0$ for a displacement $dx^\mu$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.2 (p. 76: the three classes, light cones whose directions "vary smoothly as we move around the manifold") · Hartle §7.5, eqs. (7.18a)–(7.18c) (p. 142) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.8, eq. (6.54) (p. 100: the null cone in $T_PM$; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, timelike $X^2 > 0$ there)*

Which half of the cone is the future? In special relativity the answer is the half with $a^0 > 0$ in an inertial frame, and orthochronous Lorentz transformations preserve it ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]], part 2). On a manifold the sign of a component is a property of the chart, and in the metric of the figure no coordinate vector field is timelike everywhere: $\partial_t$ is spacelike in the band, and $\partial_x$ has $g(\partial_x, \partial_x) = 1$ everywhere. What does work in the figure is the field $e_0 = \partial_t + v\,\partial_x$, timelike at every point: $g(e_0, e_0) = (-1 + v^2) + 2v(-v) + v^2 = -1$. Choosing the half of each cone on the side of $e_0$ is a continuous choice of future. The definition keeps exactly this.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.2.2: Time Orientation; Future-Directed
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be a Lorentzian manifold.
> 1. A **time orientation** is a continuous vector field $T$ on $M$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-4|Def. §C2.2.4]], continuity instead of smoothness) that is timelike at every point, $g(T, T) < 0$ ([[#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]). Two such fields $T$, $T'$ define the same time orientation if $g(T, T') < 0$ at every point. $M$ is **time-orientable** if a time orientation exists, and **time-oriented** once one is chosen.
> 2. Given a time orientation $T$, a causal vector $X \in T_pM$ is **future-directed** if $g(T_p, X) < 0$ and **past-directed** if $g(T_p, X) > 0$.
>
> *Well defined.* By [[#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]], part 4, $g(T_p, X) \neq 0$ for every causal $X \neq 0$ (a timelike vector is orthogonal neither to a timelike nor to a nonzero null vector), so every causal vector is exactly one of the two. The future-directed timelike vectors at $p$ are the cone $\mathcal T_p$ that contains $T_p$. A $T'$ with $g(T, T') < 0$ everywhere lies in the same cone at each point and selects the same future.
>
> *Exactly two on a connected manifold.* If $T$ and $T'$ are time orientations, the function $p \mapsto g(T_p, T'_p)$ is continuous and never zero, so on a connected $M$ it has one sign. Hence $T'$ defines either the same time orientation as $T$ or the same as $-T$.
>
> *Layer change.* In Minkowski spacetime take $T = \partial_t$. Then $g(\partial_t, X) = \eta_{0\nu}X^\nu = -X^0$, and future-directed means $X^0 > 0$: the future-pointing vectors of [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^def-b1-3-1|Def. §B1.3.1]] (signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where the same vectors have $a^0 > 0$). The weak-field metric is time-oriented by $\partial_t$ ([[#^ex-c3-2-1|Example §C3.2.1]]), the condition "$t$ increases" of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]]. The metric of the figure above is time-oriented by $e_0 = \partial_t + v\,\partial_x$, although $\partial_t$ is not timelike everywhere. The same happens for the Schwarzschild black hole: $\partial_t$ is spacelike inside $r = 2M$, and another field time-orients the whole Eddington–Finkelstein chart ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-2|Theorem §C7.2.2]], part 2).
>
> *Not automatic.* At each single point the choice of a half cone is always possible; the content of the definition is that the choice varies continuously over all of $M$. [[#^ex-c3-2-4|Example §C3.2.4]] is a Lorentzian manifold on which this fails. Every spacetime in these notes is assumed time-oriented: without a global future, "the future light cone of an event" and "causality" have no meaning.
>
> *Source: none of the typed sources defines it. Couzens, GR1, §4.1.2 (p. 76) draws light cones and speaks of causal structure without choosing a future half; Hartle §7.5 (p. 142) speaks of "the local future and past light cones"; d'Inverno & Vickers §6.8 (p. 100) stop at the null cone. The definition is the standard one of the general-relativity literature, written here; the two-orientations argument is added.*

## Causal curves and proper time

Curves inherit their character from their velocities. A clock's worldline must be future-directed and timelike at every event it passes, and its reading is a length computed with $-g$.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.2.3: Causal Curves; Proper Time
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be a time-oriented Lorentzian manifold and $\gamma : [\lambda_A, \lambda_B] \to M$ a smooth curve with velocity $\dot\gamma(\lambda) \in T_{\gamma(\lambda)}M$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]), components $\dot x^\mu = dx^\mu/d\lambda$ in a chart (piecewise smooth curves are allowed, with the conditions on each piece and finitely many kinks).
> 1. $\gamma$ is **timelike**, **null** or **spacelike** if $\dot\gamma(\lambda)$ is timelike, null or spacelike ([[#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]) for every $\lambda$; **causal** if $\dot\gamma(\lambda)$ is causal for every $\lambda$; **future-directed** if $\dot\gamma(\lambda)$ is future-directed ([[#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]) for every $\lambda$. A curve whose velocity changes character along the way is none of these.
> 2. The **proper time** of a future-directed timelike curve from $A = \gamma(\lambda_A)$ to $B = \gamma(\lambda_B)$ is
>
> $$
> \tau_{AB}[\gamma] = \int_{\lambda_A}^{\lambda_B}\sqrt{-g\bigl(\dot\gamma(\lambda), \dot\gamma(\lambda)\bigr)}\;d\lambda = \int_{\lambda_A}^{\lambda_B}\sqrt{-g_{\mu\nu}\bigl(x(\lambda)\bigr)\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}\;d\lambda .
> $$
>
> 3. **Proper time as parameter; four-velocity.** The function $\tau(\lambda) = \tau_{A\gamma(\lambda)}$ has $d\tau/d\lambda = \sqrt{-g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)} > 0$, so it is an admissible parameter. The velocity with respect to it is the **four-velocity** $u = d\gamma/d\tau$, with
>
> $$
> u^\mu = \frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau} = \frac{\dot x^\mu}{d\tau/d\lambda}, \qquad g(u, u) = \frac{g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)}{-g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)} = -1 ,
> $$
>
> future-directed and timelike.
>
> *Well defined.* The integrand is a scalar, so $\tau_{AB}$ does not depend on the chart. It does not depend on the parametrization: the change-of-variables computation of [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-3|Def. §C3.1.3]] (written there for $\sqrt{g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)}$) goes through with $-g$ in place of $g$. A null curve has $\tau = 0$ between any two of its points, and no parameter is singled out on it by this integral. A spacelike curve has a length instead ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-3|Def. §C3.1.3]]).
>
> *Physical reading.* The worldline of a massive particle is a future-directed timelike curve, and an ideal clock carried along it reads $\tau$ (the clock hypothesis, [[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^mod-a2-5-1|Model §A2.5.1]]). By [[#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]], part 4, a timelike velocity lies inside the light cone at each event: the particle moves slower than light as measured at each event, whatever the coordinate speeds. Light moves on future-directed null curves.
>
> *Layer change.* In a coordinate patch this is [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]], whose condition "$t$ increases along it" is now "future-directed". In Minkowski spacetime it is the proper time of special relativity ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^def-b1-3-2|Def. §B1.3.2]]; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where $c\,d\tau = \sqrt{ds^2}$ without a minus sign). The four-velocity is the one announced in [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]], with the normalization $u\cdot u = -1$ of [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.2, eq. (4.18) (p. 77: "A curve is called timelike if its tangent vector is everywhere timelike") · Hartle §7.5, eq. (7.19) (p. 142), Example 7.3, eqs. (7.20)–(7.23) (p. 143) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.8, eq. (6.53) (p. 100; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$)*

## How flat can a chart make the metric near an event?

[[#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]] made $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ at $p$ with a linear change of chart, and the opening showed that a linear change leaves the first derivatives of the metric alone: the rescaled weak-field coordinates keep $\partial_i\hat g_{00} = -2\partial_i\Phi/(1 + 2\Phi_0)$. The reason is the transformation law ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 3). It contains the first derivatives $\partial\tilde x/\partial x$ of the change of chart, so differentiating it once brings in the second derivatives $\partial^2\tilde x/\partial x\,\partial x$, the same terms that spoiled $\partial_\mu V^\nu$ in [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]]. A linear change has none. The way forward is to count. At each order in a Taylor expansion about $p$, compare the number of metric derivatives to be removed with the number of free coefficients in the change of chart.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.2.2: Local Flatness: g(p) = η and ∂g(p) = 0 Can Be Achieved, ∂∂g(p) Cannot
> Let $(M, g)$ be a Lorentzian manifold of dimension $n$, $p \in M$, and $\tilde x^\alpha$ a chart at $p$ with $\tilde x(p) = 0$. Describe a second chart $x^\mu$ with $x(p) = 0$ by the Taylor coefficients of the change of chart,
>
> $$
> \tilde x^\alpha = A^\alpha{}_\mu x^\mu + \tfrac12B^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu}x^\mu x^\nu + \tfrac16C^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu\lambda}x^\mu x^\nu x^\lambda + O(x^4) ,
> $$
>
> with $A$ invertible, $B$ symmetric in its lower indices and $C$ totally symmetric in them. The counts are given for $n = 4$.
> 1. **Order 0: $g_{\mu\nu}(p) = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ can be achieved.** It is $\tfrac12n(n + 1) = 10$ conditions on the $n^2 = 16$ entries of $A$. Solutions exist, and they form a family of dimension $\tfrac12n(n - 1) = 6$: if $A$ is one, the others are $A\Lambda$ with $\Lambda$ a Lorentz transformation.
> 2. **Order 1: $\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$ can be achieved, in exactly one way.** For each admissible $A$ it is $n\cdot\tfrac12n(n + 1) = 40$ linear equations for the $n\cdot\tfrac12n(n + 1) = 40$ coefficients $B^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu}$, with exactly one solution. When $\tilde g_{\alpha\beta}(p) = \eta_{\alpha\beta}$ and $A = \mathbb 1$, it is
>
> $$
> B_{\sigma\mu\nu} \equiv \eta_{\sigma\alpha}B^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu} = -\tfrac12\bigl(\tilde\partial_\mu\tilde g_{\sigma\nu} + \tilde\partial_\nu\tilde g_{\sigma\mu} - \tilde\partial_\sigma\tilde g_{\mu\nu}\bigr)(p) .
> $$
>
> 3. **Order 2: $\partial_\kappa\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$ cannot be achieved in general.** There are $\bigl(\tfrac12n(n + 1)\bigr)^2 = 100$ second derivatives and $n\cdot\tfrac16n(n + 1)(n + 2) = 80$ coefficients $C^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu\lambda}$. The second derivatives depend on $C$ through an injective linear map, so exactly $80$ independent combinations of them can be set to zero, and
>
> $$
> \tfrac14n^2(n + 1)^2 - \tfrac16n^2(n + 1)(n + 2) = \tfrac1{12}n^2(n^2 - 1) = 20
> $$
>
> combinations are unchanged by every choice of $C$. Whether these vanish does not depend on the choices made in parts 1–2. For a general metric they do not vanish; if they do, all second derivatives at $p$ can be removed.
> 4. **The survivors are the curvature.** $\tfrac1{12}n^2(n^2 - 1)$ is the number of independent components of the Riemann tensor ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-4|Theorem §C3.10.4]]): $1$, $6$, $20$ for $n = 2$, $3$, $4$. The surviving combinations are the components of the Riemann tensor at $p$ ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]]; the explicit second-order form of the metric is [[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^thm-c3-8-2|Theorem §C3.8.2]]).
>
> In a chart with parts 1–2, $g_{\mu\nu}(x) = \eta_{\mu\nu} + \tfrac12\,\partial_\kappa\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}(p)\,x^\kappa x^\lambda + O(x^3)$. The same holds for a Riemannian metric with $\eta$ replaced by $\delta$ and the Lorentz group by the rotation group $O(n)$, which has the same dimension $\tfrac12n(n - 1)$.
>
> *Model:* none, differential geometry of a metric of class $C^3$ or better; *applies:* every point of every Lorentzian or Riemannian manifold, exact. The conditions hold at $p$ only; at other points of the chart nothing is claimed.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.2, eqs. (4.92)–(4.96) (pp. 89–91; the counting stated, Riemannian $\delta$ written, "$\delta \to \eta$" for Lorentzian) · Schutz §6.2, eqs. (6.3)–(6.5) (p. 145; the statement) and eqs. (6.21)–(6.29) (pp. 147–149; the counts $16$, $40$, $80$ against $10$, $40$, $100$) · Hartle §7.4, eqs. (7.12)–(7.13) (pp. 140–141). Both counting sources compare numbers only; the injectivity of the order-2 map and the uniqueness at order 1 are proved here.*

^thm-c3-2-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 0** (the coefficients are free). Any $A$, $B$, $C$ with $A$ invertible define a chart. The polynomial map $P(x) = Ax + \tfrac12Bxx + \tfrac16Cxxx$ has derivative $A$ at $0$, so by the inverse function theorem ([[§35 Local Diffeomorphisms#^thm-35-1|591 Thm. §35.1]]) it maps a neighbourhood of $0$ diffeomorphically onto a neighbourhood of $0 = \tilde x(p)$, and $x = P^{-1}\circ\tilde x$ is a chart at $p$. The coefficients are the derivatives of $\tilde x(x)$ at $0$: $A^\alpha{}_\mu = \partial\tilde x^\alpha/\partial x^\mu$, $B^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu} = \partial^2\tilde x^\alpha/\partial x^\mu\partial x^\nu$, $C^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu\lambda} = \partial^3\tilde x^\alpha/\partial x^\mu\partial x^\nu\partial x^\lambda$, all at $0$; mixed partial derivatives commute, hence the symmetries. Count for $n = 4$:
> - $A$: $4 \times 4 = 16$ entries;
> - $B^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu}$: for each $\alpha$ one entry per unordered pair $\{\mu, \nu\}$, i.e. per $00, 01, 02, 03, 11, 12, 13, 22, 23, 33$: $4 \times 10 = 40$;
> - $C^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu\lambda}$: for each $\alpha$ one entry per unordered triple, $000, 001, 002, 003, 011, 012, 013, 022, 023, 033, 111, 112, 113, 122, 123, 133, 222, 223, 233, 333$: $4 \times 20 = 80$. In general $n\binom{n + 2}{3} = \tfrac16n^2(n + 1)(n + 2)$.
>
> **Step 1** (the transformation law to second order). By [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 3, with $J^\alpha{}_\mu(x) \equiv \partial\tilde x^\alpha/\partial x^\mu$,
>
> $$
> g_{\mu\nu}(x) = J^\alpha{}_\mu(x)\,J^\beta{}_\nu(x)\,\tilde g_{\alpha\beta}\bigl(\tilde x(x)\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Differentiating the expansion of $\tilde x^\alpha$ term by term, using the symmetry of $B$ and $C$ ($\partial_\mu(\tfrac12B^\alpha{}_{\rho\sigma}x^\rho x^\sigma) = B^\alpha{}_{\mu\rho}x^\rho$ and $\partial_\mu(\tfrac16C^\alpha{}_{\rho\sigma\tau}x^\rho x^\sigma x^\tau) = \tfrac12C^\alpha{}_{\mu\rho\sigma}x^\rho x^\sigma$),
>
> $$
> J^\alpha{}_\mu = A^\alpha{}_\mu + B^\alpha{}_{\mu\rho}x^\rho + \tfrac12C^\alpha{}_{\mu\rho\sigma}x^\rho x^\sigma + O(x^3), \quad J^\alpha{}_\mu(0) = A^\alpha{}_\mu,\quad \partial_\lambda J^\alpha{}_\mu(0) = B^\alpha{}_{\mu\lambda},\quad \partial_\kappa\partial_\lambda J^\alpha{}_\mu(0) = C^\alpha{}_{\mu\kappa\lambda} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (order 0). At $x = 0$, $g_{\mu\nu}(p) = A^\alpha{}_\mu A^\beta{}_\nu\tilde g_{\alpha\beta}(p)$, or $g(p) = A^{\mathsf T}\tilde g(p)A$. Both sides are symmetric, so $g(p) = \eta$ is one equation per unordered pair $\{\mu, \nu\}$: $10$ equations for $16$ unknowns. A solution is the matrix whose columns are the components of an orthonormal basis ([[#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]], part 2). If $A$ and $A'$ are solutions, $\Lambda = A^{-1}A'$ satisfies $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = A'^{\mathsf T}\tilde g(p)A' = \eta$; conversely $A\Lambda$ is a solution for every Lorentz $\Lambda$. The family has dimension $16 - 10 = 6$ ([[#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]], part 3). This is part 1. From here on fix $A$ and replace $\tilde x$ by the linear chart $A^{-1}\tilde x$; in it $\tilde g_{\alpha\beta}(p) = \eta_{\alpha\beta}$ and $A = \mathbb 1$. Write $G_{\alpha\beta\gamma} \equiv \tilde\partial_\gamma\tilde g_{\alpha\beta}(p)$ (symmetric in $\alpha\beta$) and $H_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} \equiv \tilde\partial_\gamma\tilde\partial_\delta\tilde g_{\alpha\beta}(p)$ (symmetric in $\alpha\beta$ and in $\gamma\delta$), and lower the first index of $B$ and $C$ with $\eta$: $B_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \eta_{\sigma\alpha}B^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu}$, $C_{\sigma\mu\nu\lambda} = \eta_{\sigma\alpha}C^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu\lambda}$.
>
> **Step 3** (order 1: the equations). Differentiate Step 1 once by the product rule and set $x = 0$. The chain rule gives $\partial_\lambda[\tilde g_{\alpha\beta}(\tilde x(x))] = J^\gamma{}_\lambda\,\tilde\partial_\gamma\tilde g_{\alpha\beta}$, which is $G_{\alpha\beta\lambda}$ at $0$. So
>
> $$
> \partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}(p) = \underbrace{B^\alpha{}_{\mu\lambda}\,\delta^\beta_\nu\,\eta_{\alpha\beta}}_{\partial J\cdot J\cdot\tilde g} + \underbrace{\delta^\alpha_\mu\,B^\beta{}_{\nu\lambda}\,\eta_{\alpha\beta}}_{J\cdot\partial J\cdot\tilde g} + \underbrace{G_{\mu\nu\lambda}}_{J\cdot J\cdot\partial\tilde g} = B_{\nu\mu\lambda} + B_{\mu\nu\lambda} + G_{\mu\nu\lambda} .
> $$
>
> The condition $\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$ is $B_{\nu\mu\lambda} + B_{\mu\nu\lambda} = -G_{\mu\nu\lambda}$, symmetric in $\mu\nu$: one equation per unordered pair $\{\mu, \nu\}$ and each $\lambda$, $10 \times 4 = 40$ equations for the $40$ unknowns of Step 0.
>
> **Step 4** (order 1: the solution). Try $B_{\sigma\mu\lambda} = -\tfrac12(G_{\sigma\mu\lambda} + G_{\sigma\lambda\mu} - G_{\mu\lambda\sigma})$, which is symmetric in $\mu\lambda$ because $G$ is symmetric in its first two indices. Substituting,
>
> $$
> B_{\nu\mu\lambda} + B_{\mu\nu\lambda} = -\tfrac12\bigl(G_{\nu\mu\lambda} + G_{\nu\lambda\mu} - G_{\mu\lambda\nu} + G_{\mu\nu\lambda} + G_{\mu\lambda\nu} - G_{\nu\lambda\mu}\bigr) = -\tfrac12\bigl(G_{\nu\mu\lambda} + G_{\mu\nu\lambda}\bigr) = -G_{\mu\nu\lambda} ,
> $$
>
> where $G_{\nu\lambda\mu}$ and $G_{\mu\lambda\nu}$ cancelled in pairs and $G_{\nu\mu\lambda} = G_{\mu\nu\lambda}$. In the notation of the statement this is the displayed formula.
>
> **Step 5** (order 1: uniqueness). If $B$ and $B'$ are two solutions, $D = B - B'$ solves $D_{\nu\mu\lambda} + D_{\mu\nu\lambda} = 0$: $D$ is antisymmetric in its first two indices and symmetric in its last two. Alternating the two symmetries,
>
> $$
> D_{\sigma\mu\lambda} = -D_{\mu\sigma\lambda} = -D_{\mu\lambda\sigma} = D_{\lambda\mu\sigma} = D_{\lambda\sigma\mu} = -D_{\sigma\lambda\mu} = -D_{\sigma\mu\lambda} ,
> $$
>
> so $D = 0$. The solution is unique, and the $40 \times 40$ system is invertible: there is no freedom left at this order beyond the choice of $A$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 6** (order 2: the equations). Differentiate Step 1 twice and set $x = 0$, using Step 1 for the derivatives of $J$ and the chain rule $\partial_\kappa\partial_\lambda[\tilde g_{\alpha\beta}(\tilde x(x))]|_0 = H_{\alpha\beta\kappa\lambda} + G_{\alpha\beta\gamma}B^\gamma{}_{\kappa\lambda}$. The product rule gives nine kinds of terms. The two in which both derivatives hit one $J$ contain $C$:
>
> $$
> C^\alpha{}_{\mu\kappa\lambda}\,\eta_{\alpha\nu} + C^\beta{}_{\nu\kappa\lambda}\,\eta_{\mu\beta} = C_{\nu\mu\kappa\lambda} + C_{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda} .
> $$
>
> None of the others does, so
>
> $$
> \partial_\kappa\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}(p) = C_{\nu\mu\kappa\lambda} + C_{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda} + Q_{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> Q_{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda} = H_{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda} + G_{\mu\nu\gamma}B^\gamma{}_{\kappa\lambda} + B^\alpha{}_{\mu\kappa}G_{\alpha\nu\lambda} + B^\alpha{}_{\mu\lambda}G_{\alpha\nu\kappa} + B^\alpha{}_{\nu\kappa}G_{\mu\alpha\lambda} + B^\alpha{}_{\nu\lambda}G_{\mu\alpha\kappa} + \eta_{\alpha\beta}\bigl(B^\alpha{}_{\mu\kappa}B^\beta{}_{\nu\lambda} + B^\alpha{}_{\mu\lambda}B^\beta{}_{\nu\kappa}\bigr)
> $$
>
> (the terms: $JJ\,\partial\partial\tilde g$; $\partial J\,J\,\partial\tilde g$ and $J\,\partial J\,\partial\tilde g$ with the derivatives in both orders; $\partial J\,\partial J\,\tilde g$ in both orders). The condition $\partial_\kappa\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$ is one equation per unordered pair $\{\mu, \nu\}$ and unordered pair $\{\kappa, \lambda\}$: $10 \times 10 = 100$ equations for the $80$ unknowns $C$.
>
> **Step 7** (order 2: the map is injective). Suppose $C_{\nu\mu\kappa\lambda} + C_{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda} = 0$ for all indices. Then $C$ is antisymmetric in its first two indices and symmetric in its last three. Alternating the two,
>
> $$
> C_{\sigma\mu\kappa\lambda} = -C_{\mu\sigma\kappa\lambda} = -C_{\mu\kappa\sigma\lambda} = C_{\kappa\mu\sigma\lambda} = C_{\kappa\sigma\mu\lambda} = -C_{\sigma\kappa\mu\lambda} = -C_{\sigma\mu\kappa\lambda} ,
> $$
>
> so $C = 0$. The linear map $C \mapsto (C_{\nu\mu\kappa\lambda} + C_{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda})$ from an $80$-dimensional space into the $100$-dimensional space of arrays symmetric in $\mu\nu$ and in $\kappa\lambda$ is injective, so its image $V_C$ has dimension $80$ (rank–nullity). The second derivatives can be made zero exactly when $-Q \in V_C$. The class of $\partial\partial g(p)$ in the quotient by $V_C$, a space of dimension $100 - 80 = 20$, does not depend on $C$.
>
> **Step 8** (order 2: in general impossible). An example in which $-Q \notin V_C$: the metric $\tilde g = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(-1, 1, 1 - (\tilde x^1)^2, 1\bigr)$ near $\tilde x = 0$, that is $ds^2 = -dt^2 + dx^2 + (1 - x^2)\,dy^2 + dz^2$. Here $G = 0$, so $B = 0$ (Step 4) and $Q = H$, whose only nonzero entry is $H_{2211} = \tilde\partial_1\tilde\partial_1\tilde g_{22} = -2$. Consider the linear function of an array $F_{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda}$ (symmetric in $\mu\nu$ and in $\kappa\lambda$)
>
> $$
> \ell(F) = 2F_{1212} - F_{2211} - F_{1122} .
> $$
>
> On $V_C$ it vanishes: $F_{1212} = C_{2112} + C_{1212}$, $F_{2211} = 2C_{2211}$, $F_{1122} = 2C_{1122}$, and by the symmetry of $C$ in its last three indices $C_{2112} = C_{2211}$ and $C_{1212} = C_{1122}$, so $\ell(F) = 2C_{2211} + 2C_{1122} - 2C_{2211} - 2C_{1122} = 0$. Hence $\ell\bigl(\partial\partial g(p)\bigr) = \ell(Q) = -H_{2211} = 2$ for every choice of $C$, and the second derivatives cannot all vanish. (The checked random examples behave the same way: for a metric whose second derivatives at $p$ are chosen at random, the $100$ equations have rank $81$ with the right-hand side added, against $80$ without.)
>
> **Step 9** (independent of the choices of parts 1–2). By Step 5 every chart with parts 1–2 is, up to terms of third order, $x' = \Lambda^{-1}x$ for a Lorentz $\Lambda$, where $x$ is one fixed such chart. A linear change of chart transforms the second derivatives at $p$ as a $(0, 4)$ array, $\partial'_\kappa\partial'_\lambda g'_{\mu\nu} = \Lambda^\alpha{}_\mu\Lambda^\beta{}_\nu\Lambda^\gamma{}_\kappa\Lambda^\delta{}_\lambda\,\partial_\gamma\partial_\delta g_{\alpha\beta}$. It maps $V_C$ onto itself, since the image of $C_{\nu\mu\kappa\lambda} + C_{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda}$ is $C'_{\nu\mu\kappa\lambda} + C'_{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda}$ with $C'_{\sigma\rho\kappa\lambda} = \Lambda^\beta{}_\sigma\Lambda^\alpha{}_\rho\Lambda^\gamma{}_\kappa\Lambda^\delta{}_\lambda C_{\beta\alpha\gamma\delta}$, again symmetric in its last three indices. So it acts invertibly on the $20$-dimensional quotient, and the class of $\partial\partial g(p)$ is zero in one such chart exactly when it is zero in all. If it is zero, Step 7 gives a $C$ with $\partial\partial g(p) = 0$.
>
> **Step 10** (general $n$). With the counts of Step 0, $\tfrac14n^2(n + 1)^2 - \tfrac16n^2(n + 1)(n + 2) = \tfrac1{12}n^2(n + 1)\bigl[3(n + 1) - 2(n + 2)\bigr] = \tfrac1{12}n^2(n + 1)(n - 1)$; for $n = 4$, $100 - 80 = 20$. Steps 1–9 used $n = 4$ nowhere except in the numbers, and $\eta$ only through $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta$, so they hold for $\delta$ as well. This is part 3. Part 4 is the count of [[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-4|Theorem §C3.10.4]]. The ranks of both linear maps ($n = 2, 3, 4$) and the formula for $Q$ ($n = 4$) were checked by script.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Each order of the Taylor expansion of the change of chart controls one order of the metric at $p$: $A$ the metric, $B$ its first derivatives, $C$ its second derivatives.
> - At order 0 there is freedom left: the Lorentz group. At order 1 the count is exactly even, $40 = 40$, and the solution is unique. The combination $-\tfrac12(\partial g + \partial g - \partial g)$ that removes the first derivatives is, up to sign, the Christoffel symbol ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]). In these coordinates it vanishes at $p$ ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^def-c3-8-2|Def. §C3.8.2]]).
> - At order 2 the count is short, $80 < 100$, and Step 7 shows the shortfall is exactly $20$: no hidden dependence among the $80$ lets more be removed. What cannot be removed is a property of the metric at $p$, not of the chart. This is the first appearance of curvature.

Physically, part 1 is the choice of an observer's velocity and orientation, part 2 is the choice to fall freely, and part 3 is what falling cannot undo. The next theorem states this in the language of chapter C1.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.2.3: The Einstein Equivalence Principle in Geometric Form
> Let $(M, g)$ be a spacetime and $p \in M$.
> 1. **Local inertial coordinates exist.** There is a chart $x^\mu$ on a neighbourhood of $p$ with $x(p) = 0$, $\partial_0|_p$ future-directed ([[#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]), and
>
> $$
> g_{\mu\nu}(x) = \eta_{\mu\nu} + O(|x|^2) .
> $$
>
> Any two such charts agree, up to terms of third order in $x$, after an orthochronous Lorentz transformation ($\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$).
> 2. **Free fall is unaccelerated at $p$.** In such a chart, every timelike curve through $p$ that makes its proper time stationary ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]), parametrized by proper time ([[#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]), has $d^2x^\mu/d\tau^2 = 0$ at $p$.
> 3. **Tides survive.** The second derivatives $\partial_\kappa\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}(p)$ cannot in general be removed; $20$ combinations of them are the same in all such charts up to the Lorentz freedom ([[#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]], part 3). Within $|x^\mu| \le L$ the metric differs from $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ by terms of order $\max|\partial\partial g(p)|\,L^2$. For the weak-field metric these terms contain the tidal tensor $E_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi$ ([[#^ex-c3-2-2|Example §C3.2.2]]; [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]]).
> 4. ⚑ **What is left of the principle.** Parts 1–3 hold for every Lorentzian metric. So the local inertial frames that [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]] presupposes exist at every event of every spacetime. They are fixed up to the Lorentz group, which is the room the principle needs for local Lorentz invariance. They are inertial to first order, and they fail at second order through tides. The physical content of the principle is what remains: in such a chart, at $p$, every non-gravitational law takes its special-relativistic form, with $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ for the metric and $\partial_\mu$ for derivatives, up to corrections that involve second derivatives of $g$. This is how laws are carried into curved spacetime from chapter C4 on ([[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^pr-c4-1-1|Principle §C4.1.1]]).
>
> *Model:* parts 1 and 3, any spacetime, exact (part 3's size estimate by Taylor's theorem); part 2, test bodies obeying [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]; part 4, the Einstein equivalence principle; *applies:* at one event $p$ at a time.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.2, "The Equivalence principle" (p. 91: an observer using normal coordinates "will experience a locally flat metric"; curvature is a "non-local" observable) · Hartle §7.4 (pp. 140–142: local inertial frames give "a precise expression to the equivalence principle idea") · Schutz §6.2 (p. 145: the absence of first-derivative terms is the mathematical statement that free particles move on straight lines near $P$) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.4 (pp. 294–295; the principle, cited through [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]])*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). [[#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]], parts 1–2, gives a chart with $g_{\mu\nu}(p) = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ and $\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$. Taylor's theorem with remainder ([[§11 Taylor's Theorem for Multivariable Functions#^thm-11-2|452 Thm. §11.2]], stated there in two variables; the same expansion holds in four) gives $g_{\mu\nu}(x) = g_{\mu\nu}(0) + \partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}(0)\,x^\lambda + O(|x|^2) = \eta_{\mu\nu} + O(|x|^2)$. The vector $\partial_0|_p$ has $g(\partial_0, \partial_0)(p) = \eta_{00} = -1$, so it is timelike and either future- or past-directed. If it is past-directed, replace $x^0$ by $-x^0$. This is the Lorentz transformation $\operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$, linear with constant coefficients, so it keeps $g(p) = \eta$ and $\partial g(p) = 0$. Uniqueness: by [[#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]], parts 1–2, two such charts have $A$'s differing by a Lorentz $\Lambda$ and then equal $B$'s. Both $\partial_0|_p$ being future-directed means $g(\partial_0, \partial'_0) = \eta_{0a}\Lambda^a{}_0 = -\Lambda^0{}_0 < 0$ ([[#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]), and $(\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 = 1 + \sum_i(\Lambda^i{}_0)^2 \ge 1$ from the $00$ entry of $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta$, so $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$.
>
> **Step 2** (the stationarity condition). Write the proper time of a curve with any parameter $\lambda$ as $\int F\,d\lambda$, $F(x, \dot x) = \sqrt{-g_{\alpha\beta}(x)\,\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta}$ ([[#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]). The Euler–Lagrange equations ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|CM Theorem §B5.1.4]]; functions $x^\mu(\lambda)$) need
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial F}{\partial\dot x^\mu} = -\frac{g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\nu}{F}, \qquad \frac{\partial F}{\partial x^\mu} = -\frac{\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta}\,\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta}{2F} ,
> $$
>
> and read $\frac{d}{d\lambda}\bigl(-g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\nu/F\bigr) + \partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta}\,\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta/(2F) = 0$. After varying, parametrize the stationary curve by its proper time. Then $F = 1$ along it ([[#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]], part 3), $\dot x^\mu = u^\mu$, and multiplying by $-1$,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\tau}\bigl(g_{\mu\nu}u^\nu\bigr) - \tfrac12\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta}\,u^\alpha u^\beta = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g_{\mu\nu}\frac{du^\nu}{d\tau} + \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}\,u^\rho u^\nu - \tfrac12\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta}\,u^\alpha u^\beta = 0 ,
> $$
>
> using $\frac{d}{d\tau}g_{\mu\nu}(x(\tau)) = \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}\,u^\rho$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 2). At $p$, in the chart of part 1, $\partial g = 0$ removes the second and third terms and $g = \eta$ leaves $\eta_{\mu\nu}\,du^\nu/d\tau = 0$. $\eta$ is invertible, so $d^2x^\nu/d\tau^2 = du^\nu/d\tau = 0$ at $p$. In a general chart the last two terms are not zero; written as $\Gamma^\nu{}_{\rho\sigma}u^\rho u^\sigma$ they make the geodesic equation ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]; stationary curves of proper time are geodesics, [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-4|Theorem §C3.7.4]]).
>
> **Step 4** (part 3). The first statement is [[#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]], part 3. For the size: by Taylor's theorem the remainder after the linear terms is $g_{\mu\nu}(x) - \eta_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\partial_\kappa\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}(p)\,x^\kappa x^\lambda + O(|x|^3)$, a sum of $16$ terms each bounded by $\tfrac12\max|\partial\partial g(p)|\,L^2$ when $|x^\kappa| \le L$. In the weak field, [[#^ex-c3-2-2|Example §C3.2.2]] computes these terms in a local inertial chart: $g'_{00} = -1 - \partial_\kappa\partial_\lambda\Phi\,y^\kappa y^\lambda + \dots$, which for a static field is $-1 - E_{ij}y^iy^j$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 4). [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]] asked of a local inertial frame that free bodies released at rest stay at rest within the resolution of the experiment. Part 2 gives this at $p$ to first order, and part 3 says how the condition fails away from $p$, at the rate set by second derivatives, as [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]] found for Newtonian tides. The freedom of part 1 is the Lorentz group of [[#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]], part 3. Nothing in parts 1–3 says how matter, light or clocks behave in these coordinates. That is the remaining assumption, stated in part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The geometric content of the equivalence principle is a theorem about Lorentzian metrics, not an extra assumption: one can always fall so that gravity disappears at a point. The "uniform field" that Einstein's lift removes ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]]) is the first derivatives of $g$, which are chart artefacts at a point. The tides that no lift removes ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]]) are the second derivatives, which are not. General relativity calls them curvature ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor|§C3.9]], [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation|§C3.12]]).

The coordinates of part 1 are the relativistic replacement of the freely falling laboratory of chapter C1. They deserve their own name.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.2.4: Local Inertial Frame (Geometric)
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be a spacetime and $p \in M$. A **local inertial frame** at $p$ (also: local inertial coordinates, local Lorentz frame) is a chart $x^\mu$ on a neighbourhood $U$ of $p$ with
> 1. $x(p) = 0$;
> 2. $g_{\mu\nu}(p) = \eta_{\mu\nu}$;
> 3. $\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$;
> 4. $\partial_0|_p$ future-directed ([[#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]).
>
> Its **observer** is the curve $x^i = 0$, parametrized by $x^0$; its **axes** at $p$ are the orthonormal basis $\partial_\mu|_p$ ([[#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]). Along the observer, $g_{00} = -1 + O((x^0)^2)$, so $d\tau/dx^0 = 1 + O((x^0)^2)$, $u^\mu = \delta^\mu_0 + O((x^0)^2)$ and $du^\mu/d\tau = 0$ at $p$: the observer is momentarily in free fall ([[#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]], part 2). Local inertial frames exist at every event and are unique up to orthochronous Lorentz transformations and terms of third order ([[#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]], part 1).
>
> *How local.* The frame is inertial at $p$ only. Away from $p$ its metric differs from $\eta$ by terms of order $\max|\partial\partial g(p)|\,L^2$ ([[#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]], part 3). In the weak field these are set by the tidal tensor: of order $|E|L^2/c^2$ over a distance $L$ and $|E|T^2$ over a time $T$. Near the Earth's surface $|E| = 3.07\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$ ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]]). Over $L = 1\ \mathrm{km}$, $\tfrac12|E|L^2/c^2 = 1.71\times10^{-17}$. Over $T = 1\ \mathrm s$, $\tfrac12|E|T^2 = 1.54\times10^{-6}$, the relative drift $\tfrac12|E|LT^2/L$ of two free bodies, which is the bound of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]].
>
> *Not rotating.* Condition 3 excludes rotating axes. In Minkowski spacetime the chart $x = x'\cos\omega t - y'\sin\omega t$, $y = x'\sin\omega t + y'\cos\omega t$ (axes rotating with angular velocity $\omega$ about the $z$ axis) has
>
> $$
> g_{00} = -1 + \omega^2(x'^2 + y'^2), \qquad g_{0x'} = -\omega y', \qquad g_{0y'} = \omega x' ,
> $$
>
> so $g(p) = \eta$ at the origin but $\partial_{y'}g_{0x'}(p) = -\omega \neq 0$. The non-rotation demanded of a freely falling frame in [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]] is part of condition 3.
>
> *Layer change.* [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]] defined a local inertial frame in Newtonian gravity as a freely falling, non-rotating frame used in a region small enough that tides are not detected. Here it is a chart at one event. Free fall and non-rotation are both contained in condition 3, and "small enough" is the size estimate above. Normal coordinates ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^def-c3-8-2|Def. §C3.8.2]]) are one systematic choice of local inertial frame.
>
> *Model:* any spacetime; *applies:* at one event; physically, through [[#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]], part 4, for non-gravitational experiments of size $L$ and duration $T$ in which the second-order terms are below the resolution.
>
> *Source: Hartle §7.4, eq. (7.13) (p. 141: "A coordinate system that satisfies these two conditions at a point P is called a local inertial frame at the point P") · Schutz §6.2, eqs. (6.3)–(6.5) (p. 145: "local Lorentz frames" or "local inertial frames") · Couzens, GR1, §4.4.2 (pp. 89–91: normal coordinates) · rotating chart and Earth numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.2.1: Light Cones of the Weak-Field Metric
> *Setup.* The static weak-field metric $ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi)\,dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)\,\delta_{ij}dx^idx^j$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]) at an event $p$ with $\Phi = \Phi(\vec x)$. Only $1 + 2\Phi > 0$ and $1 - 2\Phi > 0$ are used, which hold throughout the domain of the model. Write $k = k^t\partial_t + k^i\partial_i \in T_pM$ and $|\vec k|^2 = \delta_{ij}k^ik^j$.
>
> *(a) The cone in coordinates.* $k$ is null ([[#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]) when
>
> $$
> g(k, k) = -(1 + 2\Phi)(k^t)^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)|\vec k|^2 = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{|\vec k|}{|k^t|} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 2\Phi}{1 - 2\Phi}} = 1 + 2\Phi + O(\Phi^2) .
> $$
>
> This is the coordinate speed of light of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]], part 1. Drawn in the coordinates $(t, x^i)$ the cones narrow where $\Phi < 0$, as in the figure after that theorem.
>
> *(b) The cone in an orthonormal basis.* The metric is diagonal, so $e_0 = (1 + 2\Phi)^{-1/2}\partial_t$, $e_i = (1 - 2\Phi)^{-1/2}\partial_i$ is orthonormal ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]): $g(e_0, e_0) = -(1 + 2\Phi)/(1 + 2\Phi) = -1$, $g(e_i, e_j) = (1 - 2\Phi)\delta_{ij}/(1 - 2\Phi) = \delta_{ij}$, $g(e_0, e_i) = 0$. The components of $k$ in this basis are $\hat k^0 = \sqrt{1 + 2\Phi}\,k^t$ and $\hat k^i = \sqrt{1 - 2\Phi}\,k^i$, and the null condition becomes
>
> $$
> -(\hat k^0)^2 + |\hat{\vec k}|^2 = 0 ,
> $$
>
> the $45°$ cone of [[#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]. This is [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]], part 2 (a static observer measures the speed of light $1$), read as a statement about tangent spaces: in an orthonormal basis every light cone is Minkowski's.
>
> *(c) Time orientation.* $T = \partial_t$ has $g(\partial_t, \partial_t) = -(1 + 2\Phi) < 0$ everywhere, so it is a time orientation ([[#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]). A causal $k$ is future-directed when $g(\partial_t, k) = g_{tt}k^t = -(1 + 2\Phi)k^t < 0$, that is, when $k^t > 0$. "$t$ increases along it" in [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]] is this time orientation.
>
> *(d) Worldlines.* A body with coordinate velocity $\vec v = d\vec x/dt$ has velocity $\partial_t + v^i\partial_i$, which is timelike when $-(1 + 2\Phi) + (1 - 2\Phi)v^2 < 0$, i.e. $v^2 < (1 + 2\Phi)/(1 - 2\Phi)$: slower than the coordinate speed of light at its own position ([[#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]], physical reading). A static clock, $\vec v = 0$, has $\tau = \int\sqrt{1 + 2\Phi}\,dt$, [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-1|Theorem §C1.5.1]], part 1.
>
> *Source: Hartle §7.5, eqs. (7.18)–(7.19) and Example 7.3 (pp. 142–143; light cones and a timelike world line in a two-dimensional metric) · Couzens, GR1, §4.1.2 (p. 76) · the weak-field metric as in [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]] · orthonormality checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.2.2: Local Inertial Coordinates for the Weak-Field Metric to First Order
> *Question* (Schutz, Exercise 6.36; Schutz writes $\phi$ for the potential $\Phi$ of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]). The line element $ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi)\,dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2)$ has $|\Phi(t, x, y, z)| \ll 1$ everywhere ($\Phi$ may depend on $t$). At an event $P$ with coordinates $x_P^\mu$, find a coordinate change to a local inertial frame ([[#^def-c3-2-4|Def. §C3.2.4]]), to first order in $\Phi$. At what rate does this frame accelerate relative to the original coordinates?
>
> *Setup.* Write $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}$ with $h_{00} = -2\Phi$, $h_{ij} = -2\Phi\,\delta_{ij}$, $h_{0i} = 0$. Work to first order in $\Phi$ and abbreviate $\Phi_P = \Phi(P)$, $\Phi_t = \partial_t\Phi(P)$, $\Phi_i = \partial_i\Phi(P)$. Seek new coordinates $y^\mu$ with
>
> $$
> x^\mu = x_P^\mu + y^\mu + \xi^\mu(y), \qquad \xi = O(\Phi), \qquad \xi_\nu \equiv \eta_{\nu\alpha}\xi^\alpha .
> $$
>
> *Step 1 (the new metric to first order).* With $\partial x^\alpha/\partial y^\mu = \delta^\alpha_\mu + \partial_\mu\xi^\alpha$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 3),
>
> $$
> g'_{\mu\nu}(y) = (\delta^\alpha_\mu + \partial_\mu\xi^\alpha)(\delta^\beta_\nu + \partial_\nu\xi^\beta)\bigl(\eta_{\alpha\beta} + h_{\alpha\beta}(x(y))\bigr) = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}(x_P + y) + \partial_\mu\xi_\nu + \partial_\nu\xi_\mu + O(\Phi^2) .
> $$
>
> The dropped terms are $\partial\xi\,\partial\xi$, $\partial\xi\,h$ and $h(x(y)) - h(x_P + y) = O(\xi\,\partial h)$, all of order $\Phi^2$. So $h'_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu\xi_\nu + \partial_\nu\xi_\mu$.
>
> *Step 2 (order 0).* With $\xi_\nu = a_{\nu\mu}y^\mu$, $a$ symmetric, $h'_{\mu\nu}(P) = h_{\mu\nu}(P) + 2a_{\mu\nu} = 0$ needs $a_{00} = -\tfrac12h_{00}(P) = \Phi_P$, $a_{ij} = -\tfrac12h_{ij}(P) = \Phi_P\delta_{ij}$, $a_{0i} = 0$. So $\xi_0 = \Phi_Py^0$ and $\xi_i = \Phi_Py^i$, i.e. $\xi^0 = -\Phi_Py^0$ and $\xi^i = \Phi_Py^i$. To first order this is the rescaling $t = (1 - \Phi_P)y^0$, $x^i = (1 + \Phi_P)y^i$, which is $\hat t = \sqrt{1 + 2\Phi_P}\,t$, $\hat x^i = \sqrt{1 - 2\Phi_P}\,x^i$ of the opening expanded to first order.
>
> *Step 3 (order 1).* Add $\tfrac12b_{\nu\mu\lambda}y^\mu y^\lambda$ to $\xi_\nu$. The condition $\partial_\lambda h'_{\mu\nu}(P) = 0$ is $b_{\nu\mu\lambda} + b_{\mu\nu\lambda} = -\partial_\lambda h_{\mu\nu}(P)$, the system of [[#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]], Step 3, with $G_{\mu\nu\lambda} = \partial_\lambda h_{\mu\nu}(P)$. Its unique solution (Steps 4–5 there) is $b_{\sigma\mu\lambda} = -\tfrac12(\partial_\mu h_{\sigma\lambda} + \partial_\lambda h_{\sigma\mu} - \partial_\sigma h_{\mu\lambda})(P)$. Component by component, with $\partial_t h_{00} = -2\Phi_t$, $\partial_i h_{00} = -2\Phi_i$, $\partial_\lambda h_{jk} = -2\partial_\lambda\Phi\,\delta_{jk}$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> b_{000} &= -\tfrac12\,\partial_th_{00} = \Phi_t, & b_{00i} = b_{0i0} &= -\tfrac12\,\partial_ih_{00} = \Phi_i, & b_{0ij} &= \tfrac12\,\partial_th_{ij} = -\Phi_t\,\delta_{ij},\\
> b_{i00} &= \tfrac12\,\partial_ih_{00} = -\Phi_i, & b_{i0j} = b_{ij0} &= -\tfrac12\,\partial_th_{ij} = \Phi_t\,\delta_{ij}, & b_{ijk} &= \Phi_j\delta_{ik} + \Phi_k\delta_{ij} - \Phi_i\delta_{jk} .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> *Step 4 (the coordinate change).* Collecting $\xi_0 = \Phi_Py^0 + \tfrac12\bigl[b_{000}(y^0)^2 + 2b_{00i}y^0y^i + b_{0ij}y^iy^j\bigr]$ and the same for $\xi_i$, and raising with $\eta$ ($\xi^0 = -\xi_0$, $\xi^i = \xi_i$):
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> t &= t_P + (1 - \Phi_P)\,y^0 - \tfrac12\bigl[\Phi_t\,(y^0)^2 + 2\Phi_iy^i\,y^0 - \Phi_t\,|\vec y|^2\bigr],\\
> x^i &= x_P^i + (1 + \Phi_P)\,y^i + \tfrac12\bigl[-\Phi_i\,(y^0)^2 + 2\Phi_t\,y^0y^i + 2(\Phi_jy^j)\,y^i - \Phi_i\,|\vec y|^2\bigr].
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> In the coordinates $y^\mu$, $g'_{\mu\nu}(P) = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ and $\partial_\lambda g'_{\mu\nu}(P) = 0$ to first order in $\Phi$ (checked by script for a general $\Phi$).
>
> *Step 5 (the acceleration of the frame).* The frame's observer is $\vec y = 0$, $y^0 = \tau$. Substituting into Step 4,
>
> $$
> t(\tau) = t_P + (1 - \Phi_P)\,\tau - \tfrac12\Phi_t\,\tau^2, \qquad x^i(\tau) = x_P^i - \tfrac12\Phi_i\,\tau^2 .
> $$
>
> Hence $dx^i/dt = -\Phi_i\tau/(1 - \Phi_P - \Phi_t\tau)$ and, to first order in $\Phi$, $d^2x^i/dt^2 = -\partial_i\Phi(P)$. *Answer:* the local inertial frame falls with the Newtonian acceleration $-\nabla\Phi$ relative to the original coordinates ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]], part 3). Its clock runs at $d\tau/dt = 1 + \Phi_P$ at $P$, the clock-rate law of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-1|Theorem §C1.5.1]].
>
> *Step 6 (what is left).* $\xi$ is quadratic, so $\partial\xi$ is linear in $y$ and has no second-order part. By Steps 2–3 the terms of $h_{\mu\nu}(x_P + y)$ of order $0$ and $1$ in $y$ are cancelled. What remains is the second-order Taylor term:
>
> $$
> h'_{\mu\nu}(y) = \tfrac12\,\partial_\kappa\partial_\lambda h_{\mu\nu}(P)\,y^\kappa y^\lambda + O(y^3): \qquad h'_{00} = -\partial_\kappa\partial_\lambda\Phi\,y^\kappa y^\lambda, \quad h'_{ij} = -\partial_\kappa\partial_\lambda\Phi\,y^\kappa y^\lambda\,\delta_{ij}, \quad h'_{0i} = 0 .
> $$
>
> For a static $\Phi$ the time–time part is $g'_{00} = -\bigl(1 + 2\cdot\tfrac12E_{ij}y^iy^j\bigr)$ with $E_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi(P)$, the tidal tensor ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]]). By [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]], a slow body in the falling frame then moves in the potential $\tfrac12E_{ij}y^iy^j$. Its acceleration is $-E_{ij}y^j$, the tidal acceleration of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]], part 1. Cubic terms could still change some of these coefficients ([[#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]], part 3), but not the $20$ invariant combinations. In the weak field these include $R^i{}_{0j0} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi$ ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-2|Theorem §C3.12.2]]).
>
> *Source: Schutz, Exercise 6.36 (p. 168; worked here, no solution in the book) · the order-by-order method of [[#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]] (Couzens, GR1, §4.4.2; Schutz §6.2) · every identity of Steps 1–6 checked by script with a general $\Phi(t, x, y, z)$*

> [!example] Example §C3.2.3: Counting for n = 2 and n = 3; the Sphere at the Pole
> *The counts.* [[#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]] in two, three and four dimensions. For $n = 2$ the unordered index pairs are $11, 12, 22$ and the triples $111, 112, 122, 222$. For $n = 3$ the pairs are $11, 12, 13, 22, 23, 33$ and the triples $111, 112, 113, 122, 123, 133, 222, 223, 233, 333$.
>
> | $n$ | $g(p)$ | $A$ | left: Lorentz | $\partial g(p)$ | $B$ | $\partial\partial g(p)$ | $C$ | left: curvature |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ | $1$ | $2 \times 3 = 6$ | $2 \times 3 = 6$ | $3 \times 3 = 9$ | $2 \times 4 = 8$ | $1$ |
> | $3$ | $6$ | $9$ | $3$ | $3 \times 6 = 18$ | $3 \times 6 = 18$ | $6 \times 6 = 36$ | $3 \times 10 = 30$ | $6$ |
> | $4$ | $10$ | $16$ | $6$ | $4 \times 10 = 40$ | $4 \times 10 = 40$ | $10 \times 10 = 100$ | $4 \times 20 = 80$ | $20$ |
>
> In two dimensions one Lorentz parameter is left at order 0 (one boost, or one rotation angle for a Riemannian metric), and one number survives at order 2. In three dimensions three are left (two boosts and a rotation) and six survive. The last column is $\tfrac1{12}n^2(n^2 - 1)$, the number of independent components of the Riemann tensor ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-4|Theorem §C3.10.4]]).
>
> *The one survivor in two dimensions.* For $n = 2$, in a chart with $g(p) = \delta$ (or $\eta$) and $\partial g(p) = 0$, set
>
> $$
> K_p \equiv \partial_1\partial_2g_{12} - \tfrac12\,\partial_1\partial_1g_{22} - \tfrac12\,\partial_2\partial_2g_{11}\quad\text{at } p ,
> $$
>
> one half of the function $\ell$ of [[#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]], Step 8. That step shows a cubic term changes the three second derivatives by $C_{2112} + C_{1212}$, $2C_{2211}$, $2C_{1122}$ and leaves $K_p$ unchanged. A rotation of the axes leaves it unchanged too (checked by script). So $K_p$ is the surviving number.
>
> *The sphere.* The round sphere of radius $a$, $dS^2 = a^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]), near the north pole in the coordinates $x = a\theta\cos\varphi$, $y = a\theta\sin\varphi$ (Hartle (7.15)). With $\rho = a\theta = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$,
>
> $$
> dS^2 = d\rho^2 + a^2\sin^2(\rho/a)\,d\varphi^2, \qquad dx^2 + dy^2 = d\rho^2 + \rho^2d\varphi^2, \qquad \rho^2\,d\varphi = x\,dy - y\,dx ,
> $$
>
> the last from $\varphi = \arctan(y/x)$. Subtracting the flat metric,
>
> $$
> dS^2 = dx^2 + dy^2 + \bigl(a^2\sin^2(\rho/a) - \rho^2\bigr)\frac{(x\,dy - y\,dx)^2}{\rho^4} .
> $$
>
> By Taylor's theorem, $\sin^2u = u^2 - \tfrac13u^4 + O(u^6)$, so $a^2\sin^2(\rho/a) - \rho^2 = -\rho^4/(3a^2) + O(\rho^6/a^4)$. Expanding $(x\,dy - y\,dx)^2 = y^2dx^2 - 2xy\,dx\,dy + x^2dy^2$,
>
> $$
> g_{xx} = 1 - \frac{y^2}{3a^2}, \qquad g_{xy} = +\frac{xy}{3a^2}, \qquad g_{yy} = 1 - \frac{x^2}{3a^2}, \qquad\text{up to terms of fourth order.}
> $$
>
> All corrections are quadratic, so $g(p) = \delta$ and $\partial g(p) = 0$ at the pole: these coordinates satisfy parts 1–2 of the theorem. The second derivatives are $\partial_x\partial_yg_{xy} = \tfrac1{3a^2}$, $\partial_x\partial_xg_{yy} = -\tfrac2{3a^2}$, $\partial_y\partial_yg_{xx} = -\tfrac2{3a^2}$, and
>
> $$
> K_p = \frac1{3a^2} + \frac12\cdot\frac2{3a^2} + \frac12\cdot\frac2{3a^2} = \frac1{a^2} \neq 0 .
> $$
>
> No chart flattens the sphere to second order at the pole. For the plane, $K_p = 0$. The number $1/a^2$ is the Gaussian curvature of the sphere, $K = R/2$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^ex-c3-11-3|Example §C3.11.3]]; $R = 2/a^2$).
>
> *Source: Hartle, Example 7.2, eqs. (7.14)–(7.17) (p. 141; the second-order coefficients are corrected here, see the caution below) · Couzens, GR1, eq. (4.96) (p. 91; general $m$) · Schutz §6.2 (pp. 148–149; $n = 4$) · counts, ranks, $K_p$ and its invariance checked by script*

> [!caution] Caution: The second-order terms of Hartle's Example 7.2
> Hartle, Example 7.2, eq. (7.17) (p. 141) gives the sphere's metric near the north pole as $g_{AB} = \begin{pmatrix}1 - 2y^2/(3a^2) & 2xy/(3a^2)\\ 2xy/(3a^2) & 1 - 2x^2/(3a^2)\end{pmatrix}$ plus third-order terms. The expansion in [[#^ex-c3-2-3|Example §C3.2.3]] gives half of each correction: $1 - y^2/(3a^2)$, $xy/(3a^2)$, $1 - x^2/(3a^2)$. With the printed coefficients the surviving combination would be $K_p = 2/a^2$, twice the Gaussian curvature of a sphere of radius $a$. The correct coefficients agree with the general second-order form $g_{\mu\nu} = \delta_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac13R_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta}x^\alpha x^\beta$ of normal coordinates ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^thm-c3-8-2|Theorem §C3.8.2]]). The conclusion drawn in the book, $g_{AB} = \delta_{AB}$ and $\partial g_{AB} = 0$ at the pole, is unaffected.
>

> [!example] Example §C3.2.4: A Lorentzian Manifold with No Time Orientation
> *Setup.* The cylinder $M = \mathbf R \times S^1$ with coordinates $(t, \theta)$, $\theta \sim \theta + 2\pi$, and the metric
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -\cos\theta\,dt^2 - 2\sin\theta\,dt\,d\theta + \cos\theta\,d\theta^2, \qquad g_{\mu\nu} = \begin{pmatrix}-\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> The components are smooth and $2\pi$-periodic in $\theta$, so $g$ is a smooth tensor field on $M$. Its determinant is $-\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = -1 < 0$ at every point. A symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrix with negative determinant has one negative and one positive eigenvalue, so $g$ is Lorentzian ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]).
>
> *An orthonormal frame on the strip.* For $\theta \in \mathbf R$ let
>
> $$
> E_0 = \cos\tfrac\theta2\,\partial_t + \sin\tfrac\theta2\,\partial_\theta, \qquad E_1 = -\sin\tfrac\theta2\,\partial_t + \cos\tfrac\theta2\,\partial_\theta .
> $$
>
> With $c = \cos\frac\theta2$, $s = \sin\frac\theta2$, $\cos\theta = c^2 - s^2$ and $\sin\theta = 2sc$,
>
> $$
> g(E_0, E_0) = -c^2\cos\theta - 2cs\sin\theta + s^2\cos\theta = -(c^2 - s^2)^2 - (2sc)^2 = -(c^2 + s^2)^2 = -1 ,
> $$
>
> and in the same way $g(E_1, E_1) = +1$ and $g(E_0, E_1) = 0$. The light cone at each point is spanned by $E_0 \pm E_1$. As $\theta$ runs from $0$ to $2\pi$ the cone turns by half a revolution in the $(t, \theta)$ picture (figure below). At $\theta = 0$, $E_0 = \partial_t$. At $\theta = \pi$, $E_0 = \partial_\theta$, and $\partial_t$ is spacelike there ($g_{tt} = -\cos\pi = 1$). At $\theta = 2\pi$, which is the point $\theta = 0$ again, $E_0 = -\partial_t$. The frame is not a frame on $M$, since $E_a(\theta + 2\pi) = -E_a(\theta)$; the metric is, since it is unchanged by $E_a \to -E_a$.
>
> *No time orientation.* Suppose $T$ were a continuous timelike vector field on $M$ ([[#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]). Along the circle $t = 0$ write $T = \alpha(\theta)E_0 + \beta(\theta)E_1$ for $\theta \in [0, 2\pi]$, with continuous $\alpha = -g(T, E_0)$ and $\beta = g(T, E_1)$. Timelike means $g(T, T) = -\alpha^2 + \beta^2 < 0$, so $\alpha(\theta) \neq 0$ on all of $[0, 2\pi]$, and by the intermediate value theorem $\alpha$ has one sign there. But $\theta = 0$ and $\theta = 2\pi$ are the same point, where $T$ has one value:
>
> $$
> \alpha(0)E_0(0) + \beta(0)E_1(0) = T = \alpha(2\pi)E_0(2\pi) + \beta(2\pi)E_1(2\pi) = -\alpha(2\pi)E_0(0) - \beta(2\pi)E_1(0) ,
> $$
>
> so $\alpha(2\pi) = -\alpha(0)$, of the opposite sign. This contradiction shows that $M$ is not time-orientable. A future chosen at $\theta = 0$ and carried continuously once around the cylinder comes back as the past.
>
> *What it shows.* Every tangent space of $M$ is a copy of two-dimensional Minkowski space ([[#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]), yet there is no global distinction between future and past. Time orientability is a global condition on the metric, which is why it is assumed for spacetimes rather than derived. (The metric is curved, with scalar curvature $\cos\theta$, but curvature plays no role in the argument.)
>
> *Source: written here (no typed source treats time orientability); the metric, frame, antiperiodicity and curvature checked by script*
