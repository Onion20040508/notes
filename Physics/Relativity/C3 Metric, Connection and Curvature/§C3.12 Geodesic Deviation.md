Two free test bodies released side by side near the Earth drift together or apart. In Newtonian language their separation $\boldsymbol\xi$ obeys $\ddot\xi^i = -E_{ij}\xi^j$, with the tidal tensor $E_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi$ ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]], [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]]), and this relative acceleration is the part of gravity that no freely falling frame removes. Chapter C1 promised that in general relativity it becomes curvature. The tools are now in place: free bodies move on geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]), vectors at one point are differentiated along a curve with the Levi-Civita connection ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-3|Def. §C3.5.3]], [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-1|Theorem §C3.6.1]]), and the Riemann tensor measures how parallel transport around a small loop fails to close ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-4|Theorem §C3.9.4]]). This section asks: *how is the relative acceleration of two free bodies written with these tools, and is it the Riemann tensor?*

First, what fails if the Newtonian recipe is copied. The two bodies are at different events, so their separation is not a vector of any one tangent space. To first order it is: the derivative of the worldlines with respect to the label that tells the bodies apart. Its *components*, however, cannot be differentiated naively. Take two parallel straight lines in the flat plane, $y = 0$ and $y = b$, both run through with $\lambda = x$; they are geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^ex-c3-7-1|Example §C3.7.1]]) and stay a distance $b$ apart. In polar coordinates the second line is $r = \sqrt{\lambda^2 + b^2}$, $\theta = \arctan(b/\lambda)$, so the separation vector, to first order in $b$, is $b\,S$ with

$$
S^r = \frac{\partial r}{\partial b}\Big|_{b=0} = \frac{b}{\sqrt{\lambda^2 + b^2}}\Big|_{b=0} = 0, \qquad S^\theta = \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial b}\Big|_{b=0} = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda^2 + b^2}\Big|_{b=0} = \frac1\lambda, \qquad \frac{d^2S^\theta}{d\lambda^2} = \frac{2}{\lambda^3} \neq 0 .
$$

The second derivative of the components says that the lines accelerate relative to each other, which is false: in Cartesian coordinates the separation is $(0, b)$ for all $\lambda$. Differentiating components is not a tensor operation ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]]). The covariant derivative along the line removes the artefact. Along $y = 0$, $r = \lambda$, $\theta = 0$, the tangent is $T = \partial_r$, and with the polar Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta} = 1/r$, $\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta} = -r$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^ex-c3-6-1|Example §C3.6.1]]),

$$
\frac{DS^\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{dS^\theta}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}\,T^rS^\theta = -\frac{1}{\lambda^2} + \frac1\lambda\cdot 1\cdot\frac1\lambda = 0, \qquad \frac{DS^r}{d\lambda} = \frac{dS^r}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}\,T^\theta S^\theta = 0 + (-\lambda)\cdot 0\cdot\frac1\lambda = 0 .
$$

The covariant rate of change of $S$ is zero, so its covariant second derivative is zero too: no relative acceleration, as it should be (checked by script).

The section builds the answer in this order. It defines a one-parameter family of geodesics, its deviation vector and their relative acceleration ([[#^def-c3-12-1|Def. §C3.12.1]]–[[#^def-c3-12-3|Def. §C3.12.3]]). The relative acceleration is the Riemann tensor applied to the velocity and the separation ([[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]]), proved twice: without coordinates, and in a chart from two nearby geodesic equations. In the weak-field limit this is Newton's tidal law, and the tidal tensor $E_{ij}$ is the curvature component $R^i{}_{0j0}$ ([[#^thm-c3-12-2|Theorem §C3.12.2]]). Every family has zero relative acceleration exactly where spacetime is flat ([[#^thm-c3-12-3|Theorem §C3.12.3]]). Finally, a freely falling observer with non-rotating axes measures the tide as a symmetric $3\times3$ matrix ([[#^def-c3-12-4|Def. §C3.12.4]]) whose trace is $R_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu$; for a static source this trace is $4\pi\rho$ ([[#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]]).

**Setup.** A manifold $M$ with a metric $g$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]) of any signature and its Levi-Civita connection ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-1|Theorem §C3.6.1]]): torsion-free and metric-compatible. The sphere examples are Riemannian, spacetime is Lorentzian. A family of geodesics is a smooth two-parameter map whose image is a two-dimensional surface; points where the family degenerates (the poles, for the meridians of a sphere) are excluded. Physically, timelike geodesics with proper time are the worldlines of free test bodies ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]), and the deviation vector times the label difference $\delta s$ is their separation to first order in $\delta s$. Theorems §C3.12.1, §C3.12.3 and parts 1–4 of §C3.12.4 are exact statements of geometry. [[#^thm-c3-12-2|Theorem §C3.12.2]] and part 5 of [[#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]] hold in the static weak-field model ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]) to first order in its small parameter.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; a curvature component has the dimension $(\text{length})^{-2}$); $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}$ with the derivative index first; curvature $R(X, Y)Z = \nabla_X\nabla_YZ - \nabla_Y\nabla_XZ - \nabla_{[X,Y]}Z$ with $R(e_\mu, e_\nu)e_\sigma = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}e_\rho$ ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^def-c3-9-1|Def. §C3.9.1]], [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]]); Ricci $R_{\mu\nu} = R^\rho{}_{\mu\rho\nu}$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^def-c3-11-1|Def. §C3.11.1]]); Greek indices $0$–$3$ (or $1, \dots, n$ in general), Latin $1$–$3$; hatted indices are components in an orthonormal frame ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]). ## Families of geodesics and their separation

> [!definition] Definition §C3.12.1: One-Parameter Family of Geodesics
> *Structure.* A smooth map ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-5|Def. §C2.1.5]]) from a rectangle of $\mathbf R^2$ into the manifold,
>
> $$
> x : I \times J \to M, \qquad (\tau, s) \mapsto x(\tau, s), \qquad I, J \subset \mathbf R \text{ open intervals},
> $$
>
> such that for every fixed $s$ the curve $\tau \mapsto x(\tau, s)$ is a geodesic with affine parameter $\tau$ ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]). The parameter $s$ labels the geodesics. The **tangent vector** of the family at $x(\tau, s)$ is the velocity of the geodesic through that point ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]):
>
> $$
> T = \frac{\partial x}{\partial\tau} \in T_{x(\tau, s)}M, \qquad T^\mu = \frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial\tau}\ \text{in a chart}.
> $$
>
> In these notes the map is injective with injective differential on the region considered, so the family sweeps out a two-dimensional surface, and $T$ is a vector field on that surface.
>
> *Physical reading.* For timelike geodesics with $\tau$ proper time, the family is a one-parameter set of freely falling test bodies ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]): body number $s$ has the worldline $x(\cdot, s)$. A row of particles released from rest along a line, or a ring of particles parametrized by angle, is such a family.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.5, eq. (4.143), Fig. 15 (p. 100) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §10.3, eqs. (10.12)–(10.13) (p. 174) · Hartle, Gravity, §21.1, p. 446*

> [!definition] Definition §C3.12.2: Deviation Vector
> *Structure.* For a family of geodesics ([[#^def-c3-12-1|Def. §C3.12.1]]), the **deviation vector** at $x(\tau, s)$ is the velocity of the transverse curve $s \mapsto x(\tau, s)$, along which $\tau$ is held fixed:
>
> $$
> S = \frac{\partial x}{\partial s} \in T_{x(\tau, s)}M, \qquad S^\mu = \frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial s}\ \text{in a chart}.
> $$
>
> Equivalently, $T$ and $S$ are the push-forwards of the coordinate fields $\partial_\tau$ and $\partial_s$ of the rectangle by the map $x$ ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^def-c2-4-1|Def. §C2.4.1]]). On the surface swept out by the family, $S$ is a vector field.
>
> *Meaning.* In any chart, Taylor's theorem in $s$ gives
>
> $$
> x^\mu(\tau, s + \delta s) - x^\mu(\tau, s) = S^\mu\,\delta s + O(\delta s^2) ,
> $$
>
> so $S\,\delta s$ is the displacement from body $s$ to body $s + \delta s$ at equal values of the parameter, to first order. The coordinate difference on the left depends on the chart at order $\delta s^2$; the vector $S$ does not depend on the chart at all.
>
> *What is not fixed.* Moving the origin of the parameter on each geodesic, $\tau \mapsto \tau + \beta(s)$, gives the same geodesics with $S$ replaced by $S + \beta'(s)\,T$: which event on the neighbouring worldline counts as "simultaneous" is a choice. [[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]], part 4, shows that the choice can be made so that $S$ is orthogonal to $T$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.5, eq. (4.144) and the paragraph after it (p. 100) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §10.3, eq. (10.14) ("connecting vector", p. 174) · Hartle, Gravity, §21.1, eq. (21.3) (separation vector $\chi$, p. 446)*

^def-c3-12-2

> [!definition] Definition §C3.12.3: Relative Velocity and Relative Acceleration
> *Structure.* Fix one geodesic $\gamma(\tau) = x(\tau, s_0)$ of a family. Along it, $S(\tau) \in T_{\gamma(\tau)}M$ is a vector field along a curve, and so is each of its covariant derivatives along $\gamma$ ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-3|Def. §C3.5.3]]). The **relative velocity** and the **relative acceleration** of the family along $\gamma$ are
>
> $$
> \frac{DS}{d\tau} = \nabla_TS, \qquad \frac{D^2S}{d\tau^2} = \nabla_T\nabla_TS .
> $$
>
> *Components.* In a chart, with $V \equiv DS/d\tau$,
>
> $$
> V^\mu = \frac{DS^\mu}{d\tau} = \frac{dS^\mu}{d\tau} + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\lambda}\,T^\nu S^\lambda, \qquad \frac{D^2S^\mu}{d\tau^2} = \frac{dV^\mu}{d\tau} + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\lambda}\,T^\nu V^\lambda .
> $$
>
> Multiplied by $\delta s$, they are the velocity and the acceleration of body $s_0 + \delta s$ relative to body $s_0$, to first order in $\delta s$, with the comparison made by parallel transport along $\gamma$. Unlike $d^2S^\mu/d\tau^2$, which the opening found to depend on the chart, $D^2S/d\tau^2$ is a vector at each point of $\gamma$. In a basis that is parallel-transported along $\gamma$ the covariant derivatives are the ordinary derivatives of the components ([[#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]], part 1).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.5, eq. (4.149) and the sentence after it (p. 101) · Hartle, Gravity, §21.2, eqs. (21.15)–(21.18) (p. 451) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §10.3, before (10.21) (p. 175)*

## The deviation equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.12.1: The Geodesic Deviation Equation
> Let $x(\tau, s)$ be a one-parameter family of geodesics ([[#^def-c3-12-1|Def. §C3.12.1]]) of a manifold with a metric and its Levi-Civita connection ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-1|Theorem §C3.6.1]]), with tangent vector $T$ and deviation vector $S$ ([[#^def-c3-12-2|Def. §C3.12.2]]). Then:
> 1. **$T$ and $S$ commute.** $[T, S] = 0$ (Lie bracket, [[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]]), and therefore $\nabla_TS = \nabla_ST$.
> 2. **The relative acceleration is curvature.** With the curvature operator of [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^def-c3-9-1|Def. §C3.9.1]] and the Riemann components of [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]],
>
> $$
> \frac{D^2S}{d\tau^2} = R(T, S)\,T, \qquad \frac{D^2S^\rho}{d\tau^2} = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}\,T^\sigma T^\mu S^\nu .
> $$
>
> 3. **It depends only on the point.** The right-hand side is a tensor evaluated at the point, linear in $S$ and quadratic in $T$ ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-1|Theorem §C3.9.1]]). The relative acceleration of two neighbouring free bodies is fixed by their common velocity and their separation at that event; it does not depend on the rest of the family or on the chart.
> 4. **Orthogonal deviation.** If $g(T, T)$ has the same value $\kappa$ on every geodesic of the family (for instance $\kappa = -1$ when all are parametrized by proper time), then $g(T, S)$ is constant along each geodesic. If moreover $\kappa \neq 0$, the parameter origins $\tau \mapsto \tau + \beta(s)$ can be chosen so that $g(T, S) = 0$ everywhere; the resulting change $S \mapsto S + \beta'(s)\,T$ leaves both sides of part 2 unchanged.
>
> The solutions $S$ of part 2 along a fixed geodesic are called **Jacobi fields**.
>
> *Model:* none: a theorem about the Levi-Civita connection; *applies:* exactly, for any signature and any family of affinely parametrized geodesics (timelike, null or spacelike). Read as a statement about two test bodies, it gives their relative acceleration to first order in their separation $S\,\delta s$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.5, eqs. (4.145)–(4.149) (pp. 100–101) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §10.3, eqs. (10.15)–(10.21) (pp. 174–175) · Hartle, Gravity, §21.2, eq. (21.19) (p. 451; derivation deferred to the book's website) · Schutz §6.5, eqs. (6.79)–(6.87) (pp. 161–162)*

^thm-c3-12-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation (without coordinates)
> **Step 1** ($[T, S] = 0$). In a chart, $T^\mu = \partial x^\mu/\partial\tau$ and $S^\mu = \partial x^\mu/\partial s$ are functions on the surface. The bracket ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]]) differentiates $S$ along $T$ and $T$ along $S$, both directions tangent to the surface, so by the chain rule along the surface
>
> $$
> T^\nu\partial_\nu S^\mu = \frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial s}\Bigr), \qquad S^\nu\partial_\nu T^\mu = \frac{\partial}{\partial s}\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial\tau}\Bigr), \qquad [T, S]^\mu = \frac{\partial^2x^\mu}{\partial\tau\,\partial s} - \frac{\partial^2x^\mu}{\partial s\,\partial\tau} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> since mixed partial derivatives of the smooth functions $x^\mu(\tau, s)$ commute. (The fields are the push-forwards of $\partial_\tau$ and $\partial_s$, whose bracket vanishes ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-7|Theorem §C2.5.7]]); this is the same fact.)
>
> **Step 2** (symmetry of $\nabla$). The connection is torsion-free ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^def-c3-6-1|Def. §C3.6.1]], [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-1|Theorem §C3.6.1]]): $\nabla_TS - \nabla_ST - [T, S] = 0$. With Step 1, $\nabla_TS = \nabla_ST$. In components ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-5-1|Theorem §C3.5.1]]) the same statement reads
>
> $$
> (\nabla_TS)^\mu = \frac{\partial^2x^\mu}{\partial\tau\,\partial s} + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\lambda}\frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial\tau}\frac{\partial x^\lambda}{\partial s}, \qquad (\nabla_ST)^\mu = \frac{\partial^2x^\mu}{\partial s\,\partial\tau} + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\lambda}\frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial s}\frac{\partial x^\lambda}{\partial\tau} ,
> $$
>
> equal because $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\lambda} = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\lambda\nu}$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (move one derivative across). By [[#^def-c3-12-3|Def. §C3.12.3]] and Step 2,
>
> $$
> \frac{D^2S}{d\tau^2} = \nabla_T\nabla_TS = \nabla_T\nabla_ST .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (commute the two derivatives). The curvature operator ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^def-c3-9-1|Def. §C3.9.1]]) with $X = T$, $Y = S$, $Z = T$ is
>
> $$
> R(T, S)\,T = \nabla_T\nabla_ST - \nabla_S\nabla_TT - \nabla_{[T,S]}T = \nabla_T\nabla_ST - \nabla_S\nabla_TT ,
> $$
>
> the last term being zero by Step 1. Hence $\nabla_T\nabla_ST = \nabla_S\nabla_TT + R(T, S)T$.
>
> **Step 5** (every member is a geodesic). Each curve $\tau \mapsto x(\tau, s)$ is an affinely parametrized geodesic, so $\nabla_TT = 0$ at every point of the surface, for every $s$ ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]). A field that vanishes on the whole surface has zero derivative along $S$, which is tangent to the surface: $\nabla_S\nabla_TT = \nabla_S0 = 0$. (Here it matters that *all* members are geodesics, not only the one followed.)
>
> **Step 6** (combine). Steps 3–5 give $D^2S/d\tau^2 = R(T, S)T$. Expanding in a basis, with the multilinearity of $R$ ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-1|Theorem §C3.9.1]]) and $R(e_\mu, e_\nu)e_\sigma = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}e_\rho$ ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]]),
>
> $$
> R(T, S)\,T = R\bigl(T^\mu e_\mu, S^\nu e_\nu\bigr)\bigl(T^\sigma e_\sigma\bigr) = T^\mu S^\nu T^\sigma\,R(e_\mu, e_\nu)e_\sigma = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}\,T^\sigma T^\mu S^\nu\,e_\rho .
> $$
>
> This is part 2. Part 3 is the multilinearity used in this line: $R(T, S)T$ at a point needs only $T$ and $S$ at that point.
>
> **Step 7** ($g(T, S)$ along a geodesic). With metric compatibility ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-1|Theorem §C3.6.1]]), $\nabla_TT = 0$ and Step 2,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\tau}\,g(T, S) = g(\nabla_TT, S) + g(T, \nabla_TS) = 0 + g(T, \nabla_ST) = \tfrac12\,S\bigl[g(T, T)\bigr] = \tfrac12\,\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\,g(T, T) ,
> $$
>
> using $S[g(T, T)] = 2g(\nabla_ST, T)$ (metric compatibility again). If $g(T, T) = \kappa$ for all $s$, the right-hand side is zero and $g(T, S)$ is constant along each geodesic.
>
> **Step 8** (shifting the parameter). The family $\tilde x(\tau, s) = x(\tau + \beta(s), s)$ has the same geodesics, the same $T$, and, by the chain rule, $\tilde S = \partial\tilde x/\partial s = S + \beta'(s)\,T$. Then $g(T, \tilde S) = g(T, S) + \beta'(s)\,\kappa$. Choose $\beta'(s) = -g(T, S)/\kappa$, evaluated at $\tau = 0$; by Step 7, $g(T, \tilde S) = 0$ along the whole geodesic. Both sides of part 2 are unchanged: $\beta'(s)$ is constant along each geodesic, so $\nabla_T\nabla_T(\beta'T) = \beta'\,\nabla_T\nabla_TT = 0$, and $R(T, \beta'T)T = \beta'\,R(T, T)T = 0$ by the antisymmetry $R(X, Y) = -R(Y, X)$ ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^def-c3-9-1|Def. §C3.9.1]]). This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Two facts carry the proof: the parametrization makes $T$ and $S$ commute, and torsion-freeness turns that into $\nabla_TS = \nabla_ST$. After that the relative acceleration is a commutator of covariant derivatives, which is the curvature. The tangential part of $S$ is a bookkeeping choice of simultaneity and does not accelerate.

> [!derivation]- Second derivation: two nearby geodesic equations in a chart
> This route (Hartle §21.2 outlines it and leaves the algebra to the book's website) starts from the geodesic equations of two neighbours, as the Newtonian [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]] did, and shows where each Riemann term comes from. A dot is $d/d\tau$ along the first geodesic; $\Gamma$ and its derivatives are evaluated on it.
>
> **Step 1** (two geodesics). Let $x^\mu(\tau) = x^\mu(\tau, s)$ and $x^\mu + \chi^\mu$ with $\chi^\mu(\tau) = x^\mu(\tau, s + \delta s) - x^\mu(\tau, s) = S^\mu\delta s + O(\delta s^2)$ ([[#^def-c3-12-2|Def. §C3.12.2]]). Both obey the geodesic equation ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]):
>
> $$
> \ddot x^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(x)\,\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta = 0, \qquad \ddot x^\mu + \ddot\chi^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(x + \chi)\,\bigl(\dot x^\alpha + \dot\chi^\alpha\bigr)\bigl(\dot x^\beta + \dot\chi^\beta\bigr) = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (linearize in $\chi$). Taylor's theorem gives $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(x + \chi) = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta} + \partial_\lambda\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\,\chi^\lambda + O(\chi^2)$, and the product of velocities is $T^\alpha T^\beta + T^\alpha\dot\chi^\beta + \dot\chi^\alpha T^\beta + O(\chi^2)$, with $T^\alpha = \dot x^\alpha$. Subtract the first equation from the second and use $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\beta\alpha}$ to merge the two middle terms:
>
> $$
> \ddot\chi^\mu = -\partial_\lambda\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\,\chi^\lambda T^\alpha T^\beta - 2\,\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\,T^\alpha\dot\chi^\beta + O(\chi^2), \qquad \dot T^\alpha = -\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\kappa\lambda}\,T^\kappa T^\lambda .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the covariant derivatives). By [[#^def-c3-12-3|Def. §C3.12.3]], with $\chi$ in place of $S\,\delta s$,
>
> $$
> V^\mu = \dot\chi^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\,T^\alpha\chi^\beta, \qquad W^\mu \equiv \frac{D^2\chi^\mu}{d\tau^2} = \dot V^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\,T^\alpha V^\beta .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (differentiate $V$). Along the curve $\frac{d}{d\tau}\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta} = T^\lambda\partial_\lambda\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}$, so by the product rule
>
> $$
> \dot V^\mu = \ddot\chi^\mu + T^\lambda\partial_\lambda\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\,T^\alpha\chi^\beta + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\,\dot T^\alpha\chi^\beta + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\,T^\alpha\dot\chi^\beta .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (substitute everything). Insert $\ddot\chi$ and $\dot T$ from Step 2 and $V$ from Step 3 into $W$. All seven terms:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> W^\mu ={}& \underbrace{-\partial_\lambda\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\,\chi^\lambda T^\alpha T^\beta}_{(a)} \underbrace{-\,2\,\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}T^\alpha\dot\chi^\beta}_{(b)} + \underbrace{T^\lambda\partial_\lambda\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\,T^\alpha\chi^\beta}_{(c)} \underbrace{-\,\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\kappa\lambda}\,T^\kappa T^\lambda\chi^\beta}_{(d)} \\
> &+ \underbrace{\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}T^\alpha\dot\chi^\beta}_{(e)} + \underbrace{\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}T^\alpha\dot\chi^\beta}_{(f)} + \underbrace{\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\Gamma^\beta{}_{\kappa\lambda}\,T^\alpha T^\kappa\chi^\lambda}_{(g)} .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> Term (e) comes from $\dot V$, (f) and (g) from $\Gamma T V$.
>
> **Step 6** (the velocity terms cancel). $(b) + (e) + (f) = (-2 + 1 + 1)\,\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}T^\alpha\dot\chi^\beta = 0$. This is why the covariant derivative is the right one: the connection terms in $D/d\tau$ absorb the velocity-dependent part of the linearized geodesic equation.
>
> **Step 7** (rename the dummy indices). Write the remaining terms as $(\dots)^\mu{}_{\sigma\kappa\nu}\,T^\sigma T^\kappa\chi^\nu$:
> - (c): $\lambda \to \kappa$, $\alpha \to \sigma$, $\beta \to \nu$: $\partial_\kappa\Gamma^\mu{}_{\sigma\nu} = \partial_\kappa\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\sigma}$;
> - (a): $\lambda \to \nu$, $\alpha \to \kappa$, $\beta \to \sigma$: $-\partial_\nu\Gamma^\mu{}_{\kappa\sigma}$;
> - (g): $\alpha \to \kappa$, $\beta \to \lambda$, $\kappa \to \sigma$, $\lambda \to \nu$: $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\kappa\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\sigma\nu} = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\kappa\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma}$;
> - (d): $\beta \to \nu$, $\alpha \to \lambda$, $\lambda \to \sigma$: $-\Gamma^\mu{}_{\lambda\nu}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\kappa\sigma} = -\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\kappa\sigma}$.
>
> Together, by [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]],
>
> $$
> W^\mu = \bigl(\partial_\kappa\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\mu{}_{\kappa\sigma} + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\kappa\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\kappa\sigma}\bigr)\,T^\sigma T^\kappa\chi^\nu = R^\mu{}_{\sigma\kappa\nu}\,T^\sigma T^\kappa\chi^\nu .
> $$
>
> Dividing by $\delta s$ and letting $\delta s \to 0$ gives part 2 of the theorem.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The two derivative terms of the Riemann tensor come from two places: $-\partial_\nu\Gamma$ from the Taylor expansion of the force on the neighbour (the analogue of $-\partial_j\partial_i\Phi\,\xi^j$ in [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^der-c1-3-6|the derivation of Theorem §C1.3.6]], Step 2), and $+\partial_\kappa\Gamma$ from differentiating the connection term of $D/d\tau$ along the path. The index order is forced: with the last two indices of $R$ exchanged the identity fails, by an overall sign (checked symbolically on a generic two-dimensional metric and a generic diagonal three-dimensional one).

## The Newtonian limit: tides are curvature

In the static weak-field metric ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]) slow free bodies obey Newton's $\ddot{\mathbf x} = -\nabla\Phi$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]]; from the geodesic equation, [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^ex-c3-7-3|Example §C3.7.3]]). Subtracting two such equations gave the Newtonian tidal law. The deviation equation should therefore reduce to that law, and the curvature on its right-hand side should reduce to $\partial_i\partial_j\Phi$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.12.2: The Newtonian Limit: the Tidal Tensor Is a Curvature Component
> In the static weak-field metric $ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi)\,dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)\,\delta_{ij}dx^idx^j$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]), with $\Phi = O(\varepsilon)$ and $\ell$ the length over which $\Phi$ changes appreciably:
> 1. **Curvature.** Every Riemann component with an odd number of time indices vanishes, exactly. To first order in $\varepsilon$,
>
> $$
> R^i{}_{0j0} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi = E_{ij}, \qquad \text{restored } (x^0 = ct)\text{:}\quad R^i{}_{0j0} = \frac{1}{c^2}\,\partial_i\partial_j\Phi ,
> $$
>
> the tidal tensor of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]].
> 2. **Deviation of slow bodies.** Let a family of free bodies ([[#^def-c3-12-1|Def. §C3.12.1]]) move slowly, $v^2 = O(\varepsilon)$ with $v^i = dx^i/dt$, be parametrized by proper time with $g(T, S) = 0$ ([[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]], part 4), and let their separation change on the orbital time scale, $dS/d\tau = O(\varepsilon^{1/2}|S|/\ell)$. Then $S^0 = v_kS^k\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)]$, and the spatial components of the deviation equation are
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2S^i}{dt^2} = -\partial_i\partial_j\Phi\;S^j\,\bigl[1 + O(\varepsilon)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> The separation at equal coordinate time, $\xi^i = S^i\delta s\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)]$, obeys $\ddot\xi^i = -E_{ij}\xi^j$: part 1 of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]].
>
> So the Newtonian tidal law is the weak-field, slow-motion limit of the geodesic deviation equation, and the tidal tensor, defined in Newton's theory through a potential, is a component of the Riemann tensor, which needs no potential. This is the layer change of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]] and [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]].
>
> *Model:* [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]] with free bodies on timelike geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]); *applies:* static weak field, slow bodies whose separation evolves on the orbital time scale, first order in $\varepsilon$ (the parity statement of part 1 exactly, for the metric as written).
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §10.6, $R^\alpha{}_{0\gamma0} = \partial_\alpha\partial_\gamma\phi$ (p. 181) and §10.5, eqs. (10.37)–(10.39) (p. 178) · Schutz, ex. 7.9 (p. 182; result stated, worked here) · Hartle, Gravity, Table 21.1 (p. 452) · Couzens, GR1, §4.4.5, after eq. (4.149) ("tidal forces", p. 101)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 0** (sizes). $\Phi = O(\varepsilon)$, $\partial_i\Phi = O(\varepsilon/\ell)$, $\partial_i\partial_j\Phi = O(\varepsilon/\ell^2)$, $v^i = O(\varepsilon^{1/2})$. The metric is static, so along a worldline $d/d\tau$ of a function of position is $T^k\partial_k = O(\varepsilon^{1/2}/\ell)$. The accelerations are $dv^i/dt = O(\varepsilon/\ell)$ and $dt/d\tau = 1 + O(\varepsilon)$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]], part 1). By assumption $dS^\mu/d\tau = O(\varepsilon^{1/2}|S|/\ell)$.
>
> **Step 1** (Christoffel symbols). From the Christoffel formula ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]; [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^ex-c3-6-3|Example §C3.6.3]]) for the diagonal, $t$-independent metric, exactly (their first-order forms are the ones of Example §C3.6.3; the exact forms checked by script),
>
> $$
> \Gamma^i{}_{00} = \frac{\partial_i\Phi}{1 - 2\Phi}, \qquad \Gamma^0{}_{0i} = \Gamma^0{}_{i0} = \frac{\partial_i\Phi}{1 + 2\Phi}, \qquad \Gamma^0{}_{00} = \Gamma^0{}_{ij} = \Gamma^i{}_{0j} = \Gamma^i{}_{j0} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> and, to first order, $\Gamma^i{}_{jk} = -\bigl(\delta_{ij}\partial_k\Phi + \delta_{ik}\partial_j\Phi - \delta_{jk}\partial_i\Phi\bigr) + O(\varepsilon^2/\ell)$. All are $O(\varepsilon/\ell)$.
>
> **Step 2** (parity). The nonzero symbols of Step 1 have an even number of time indices among their three (two or none); every symbol with one or three is zero. In the component formula ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]])
>
> $$
> R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\sigma} + \Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\sigma} ,
> $$
>
> a derivative term vanishes if the derivative index is $0$ (static), and otherwise has the time indices of one $\Gamma$, hence an even number. In a product term $\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma}$ the numbers of time indices in $(\rho, \mu, \lambda)$ and $(\lambda, \nu, \sigma)$ are both even, so the number in $(\rho, \mu, \nu, \sigma)$ is even plus even minus twice the count of $\lambda = 0$: even. So $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = 0$ whenever an odd number of its indices are $0$ (all 120 such components checked by script).
>
> **Step 3** ($R^i{}_{0j0}$). Put $\rho = i$, $\sigma = 0$, $\mu = j$, $\nu = 0$ in the formula of Step 2:
>
> $$
> R^i{}_{0j0} = \partial_j\Gamma^i{}_{00} - \partial_0\Gamma^i{}_{j0} + \Gamma^i{}_{j\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{00} - \Gamma^i{}_{0\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{j0} .
> $$
>
> Term by term: $\partial_j\Gamma^i{}_{00} = \partial_j\bigl[\partial_i\Phi\,(1 + 2\Phi + O(\varepsilon^2))\bigr] = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi + O(\varepsilon^2/\ell^2)$; $\partial_0\Gamma^i{}_{j0} = 0$; $\Gamma^i{}_{j\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{00} = \Gamma^i{}_{jk}\Gamma^k{}_{00} = O(\varepsilon^2/\ell^2)$ (the $\lambda = 0$ term has $\Gamma^i{}_{j0} = 0$); $\Gamma^i{}_{0\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{j0} = \Gamma^i{}_{00}\Gamma^0{}_{j0} = \partial_i\Phi\,\partial_j\Phi + O(\varepsilon^3/\ell^2) = O(\varepsilon^2/\ell^2)$ (the $\lambda = k$ terms have $\Gamma^i{}_{0k} = 0$). Hence $R^i{}_{0j0} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi + O(\varepsilon^2/\ell^2)$. This is part 1. Restoring: a curvature component is a second derivative of the metric in the coordinates $x^\mu$, so with $x^0 = ct$ and $\Phi \to \Phi/c^2$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]) it is $\partial_i\partial_j\Phi/c^2$, in $\mathrm m^{-2}$.
>
> **Step 4** (the time component of $S$). Write $T^\mu = \frac{dt}{d\tau}(1, v^i)$. Then
>
> $$
> 0 = g(T, S) = \frac{dt}{d\tau}\Bigl[-(1 + 2\Phi)\,S^0 + (1 - 2\Phi)\,v_kS^k\Bigr] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S^0 = \frac{1 - 2\Phi}{1 + 2\Phi}\,v_kS^k = v_kS^k\,\bigl[1 + O(\varepsilon)\bigr] = O(\varepsilon^{1/2}|S|) .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (the right-hand side, all index combinations). In $R^i{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}T^\sigma T^\mu S^\nu$ each of $\sigma, \mu, \nu$ is $0$ or spatial:
> - $(\sigma, \mu, \nu) = (0, 0, j)$: $R^i{}_{00j}(T^0)^2S^j = -R^i{}_{0j0}S^j\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)]$, of order $\varepsilon|S|/\ell^2$;
> - $(0, 0, 0)$ and $(k, 0, 0)$: $R^i{}_{000} = R^i{}_{k00} = 0$ (antisymmetry in the last pair, [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^def-c3-9-1|Def. §C3.9.1]]);
> - $(0, k, j)$, $(k, 0, j)$, $(k, l, 0)$: one time index, zero by Step 2;
> - $(0, k, 0)$: $R^i{}_{0k0}T^0T^kS^0 = O(\varepsilon/\ell^2)\cdot O(\varepsilon^{1/2})\cdot O(\varepsilon^{1/2}|S|) = O(\varepsilon^2|S|/\ell^2)$;
> - $(k, l, j)$: $R^i{}_{klj}T^kT^lS^j = O(\varepsilon/\ell^2)\cdot O(\varepsilon)\cdot|S| = O(\varepsilon^2|S|/\ell^2)$.
>
> Hence $R^i{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}T^\sigma T^\mu S^\nu = -\partial_i\partial_j\Phi\,S^j + O(\varepsilon^2|S|/\ell^2)$ (checked by script, the terms sorted by powers of the velocity).
>
> **Step 6** (the left-hand side). By [[#^def-c3-12-3|Def. §C3.12.3]] and Step 1 ($\Gamma^i{}_{0k} = 0$, $\Gamma^0{}_{00} = \Gamma^0{}_{kl} = 0$),
>
> $$
> V^i = \frac{dS^i}{d\tau} + \Gamma^i{}_{00}T^0S^0 + \Gamma^i{}_{kl}T^kS^l, \qquad V^0 = \frac{dS^0}{d\tau} + \Gamma^0{}_{0k}\bigl(T^0S^k + T^kS^0\bigr),
> $$
>
> $$
> \frac{D^2S^i}{d\tau^2} = \frac{d^2S^i}{d\tau^2} + \frac{d}{d\tau}\bigl(\Gamma^i{}_{00}T^0S^0 + \Gamma^i{}_{kl}T^kS^l\bigr) + \Gamma^i{}_{00}T^0V^0 + \Gamma^i{}_{kl}T^kV^l .
> $$
>
> The sizes, from Step 0 and Step 4: $V^0 = O(\varepsilon|S|/\ell)$; $\frac{d}{d\tau}(\Gamma^i{}_{00}T^0S^0)$ is $O(\varepsilon^{3/2}/\ell^2)\cdot O(\varepsilon^{1/2}|S|) + O(\varepsilon/\ell)\cdot O(\varepsilon|S|/\ell)$; $\frac{d}{d\tau}(\Gamma^i{}_{kl}T^kS^l)$ has the three pieces $\dot\Gamma TS$, $\Gamma\dot TS$, $\Gamma T\dot S$, each $O(\varepsilon^2|S|/\ell^2)$; $\Gamma^i{}_{00}T^0V^0 = O(\varepsilon/\ell)\cdot O(\varepsilon|S|/\ell)$; $\Gamma^i{}_{kl}T^kV^l = O(\varepsilon/\ell)\cdot O(\varepsilon^{1/2})\cdot O(\varepsilon^{1/2}|S|/\ell)$. All are $O(\varepsilon^2|S|/\ell^2)$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{D^2S^i}{d\tau^2} = \frac{d^2S^i}{d\tau^2} + O(\varepsilon^2|S|/\ell^2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 7** (from $\tau$ to $t$). By the chain rule, $\frac{d^2S^i}{d\tau^2} = \bigl(\frac{dt}{d\tau}\bigr)^2\frac{d^2S^i}{dt^2} + \frac{d^2t}{d\tau^2}\frac{dS^i}{dt}$. The geodesic equation gives $\frac{d^2t}{d\tau^2} = -\Gamma^0{}_{\alpha\beta}T^\alpha T^\beta = -2\,\Gamma^0{}_{0k}T^0T^k = O(\varepsilon^{3/2}/\ell)$, so the second term is $O(\varepsilon^2|S|/\ell^2)$, and $(dt/d\tau)^2 = 1 + O(\varepsilon)$.
>
> **Step 8** (combine). [[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]] with Steps 5–7:
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2S^i}{dt^2}\,\bigl[1 + O(\varepsilon)\bigr] = -\partial_i\partial_j\Phi\,S^j + O(\varepsilon^2|S|/\ell^2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2S^i}{dt^2} = -\partial_i\partial_j\Phi\,S^j\,\bigl[1 + O(\varepsilon)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> **Step 9** (equal-time separation). The neighbour $s + \delta s$ is at $x^i(\tau', s + \delta s)$ with $\tau'$ chosen so that its $t$ equals $t(\tau, s)$. To first order in $\delta s$, $\tau' = \tau - (S^0/T^0)\,\delta s$, so
>
> $$
> \xi^i = \Bigl(S^i - \frac{T^i}{T^0}\,S^0\Bigr)\delta s = \bigl(S^i - v^i\,v_kS^k\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)]\bigr)\delta s = S^i\,\delta s\,\bigl[1 + O(\varepsilon)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> With Step 8, $\ddot\xi^i = -E_{ij}\xi^j\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)]$, $E_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi$ ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]]): part 2.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Only one curvature component survives for slow bodies, $R^i{}_{0j0}$, and it comes from the single term $\partial_j\Gamma^i{}_{00}$: the gradient of the gravitational acceleration $\Gamma^i{}_{00} = \partial_i\Phi$ of [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^ex-c3-7-3|Example §C3.7.3]]. The spatial curvature ($\Gamma^i{}_{jk}$) and the connection terms of $D/d\tau$ enter only at relative order $\varepsilon$.
> - Choosing $S$ orthogonal to $T$ (Step 4) is what makes "equal proper time" agree with "equal coordinate time" to this order (Step 9). Without it, $S$ would contain a multiple of $T$, whose spatial part $\beta' v^i$ changes with the bodies' acceleration although the bodies' separation does not.
> - An end-to-end numerical check integrates two exact geodesics of the weak-field metric of a point mass ($r_0 = 10^4M$, so $\varepsilon = 10^{-4}$; radial infall from rest and a circular-speed start) and compares their separation at equal $t$ with the solution of $\ddot\xi = -E\xi$ along the first: after an eighth of an orbital period they agree to $2.5\times10^{-4}$ and $0.9\times10^{-4}$, relative, which is of order $\varepsilon$.

## No tides anywhere means no curvature

In Newton's theory a field can be removed in a whole region exactly when it is uniform there, which is when $E_{ij} = 0$ ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]], part 3). The geometric statement replaces "uniform field" by "flat".

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.12.3: Zero Relative Acceleration for Every Family Is Equivalent to Zero Curvature
> Let $(M, g)$ carry its Levi-Civita connection, and let $p \in M$.
> 1. If $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = 0$ on an open set $U$, every family of geodesics in $U$ has zero relative acceleration: $D^2S/d\tau^2 = 0$.
> 2. Conversely, if every family of geodesics through $p$ has $D^2S/d\tau^2 = 0$ at $p$, then $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}(p) = 0$.
> 3. On a Lorentzian manifold it is enough in part 2 to use timelike families, that is, families of free test bodies.
>
> Combined with [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-5|Theorem §C3.9.5]]: in a region where no two neighbouring free bodies ever accelerate relative to each other, coordinates with $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ exist locally, and gravity is absent there in every sense. This is the geometric form of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]], part 3; a "uniform field" is flat spacetime in accelerated coordinates ([[#^ex-c3-12-3|Example §C3.12.3]]).
>
> *Model:* none: a theorem about the Levi-Civita connection; *applies:* exactly, at each point.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.5, last sentence (p. 101: "the relative acceleration vanishes for all families of geodesics if and only if the Riemann tensor vanishes"; proof written here) · Schutz §6.5, after (6.87) (p. 162)*

^thm-c3-12-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). If $R = 0$ on $U$, the right-hand side of [[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]], part 2, is zero for every $T$ and $S$ there.
>
> **Step 2** (every pair of vectors is realized by a family). Let $T_p, S_p \in T_pM$. If they are linearly dependent, $S_p = cT_p$ and $R(T_p, S_p)T_p = c\,R(T_p, T_p)T_p = 0$ already. Otherwise choose a curve $c(s)$ with $c(0) = p$ and velocity $c'(0) = S_p$, let $W(s)$ be the parallel transport of $T_p$ along it ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-1|Def. §C3.7.1]]), and set
>
> $$
> x(\tau, s) = \exp_{c(s)}\bigl(\tau\,W(s)\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the geodesic through $c(s)$ with initial velocity $W(s)$ ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^def-c3-8-1|Def. §C3.8.1]]), defined and smooth for small $|\tau|$, $|s|$ by smooth dependence of solutions of the geodesic equation on their initial data. Each $\tau \mapsto x(\tau, s)$ is an affinely parametrized geodesic, so this is a family ([[#^def-c3-12-1|Def. §C3.12.1]]). At $\tau = 0$, $x(0, s) = c(s)$, so $S(0, 0) = c'(0) = S_p$, and $T(0, 0) = W(0) = T_p$. Its differential has rank 2 at $(0, 0)$ because $T_p$, $S_p$ are independent, so near $(0, 0)$ it sweeps out a surface as required.
>
> **Step 3** (the hypothesis gives $R(T, S)T = 0$ for all $T$, $S$). By Step 2 and [[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]], part 2, the hypothesis of part 2 gives $R(T_p, S_p)T_p = 0$ for every pair.
>
> **Step 4** (polarize). Replace $T_p$ by $T + U$ (both arbitrary) and expand by multilinearity ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-1|Theorem §C3.9.1]]):
>
> $$
> 0 = R(T + U, S)(T + U) = R(T, S)T + R(T, S)U + R(U, S)T + R(U, S)U = R(T, S)U + R(U, S)T ,
> $$
>
> the first and last terms being zero by Step 3. In components, $R(T, S)U = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}U^\sigma T^\mu S^\nu$ and $R(U, S)T = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}T^\sigma U^\mu S^\nu = R^\rho{}_{\mu\sigma\nu}U^\sigma T^\mu S^\nu$ (dummy indices $\sigma \leftrightarrow \mu$ renamed). Since $U$, $T$, $S$ are arbitrary,
>
> $$
> R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = -R^\rho{}_{\mu\sigma\nu} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (total antisymmetry). Also $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = -R^\rho{}_{\sigma\nu\mu}$ ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-1|Theorem §C3.10.1]]; it is $R(X, Y) = -R(Y, X)$). The transpositions of positions (2, 3) and (3, 4) generate all permutations of the three lower indices, so $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}$ is totally antisymmetric in $(\sigma, \mu, \nu)$. Cyclic permutations are even:
>
> $$
> R^\rho{}_{\mu\nu\sigma} = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}, \qquad R^\rho{}_{\nu\sigma\mu} = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (first Bianchi identity). For the torsion-free connection $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} + R^\rho{}_{\mu\nu\sigma} + R^\rho{}_{\nu\sigma\mu} = 0$ ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-2|Theorem §C3.10.2]]). By Step 5 the three terms are equal, so $3R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = 0$. This is part 2. (A linear-algebra check confirms it in dimensions 2, 3 and 4: the three conditions of Steps 4–6 have only the zero solution, while the last-pair antisymmetry and Bianchi alone leave 4, 24 and 80 free components.)
>
> **Step 7** (timelike families suffice). Fix $S$. The components of $R(T, S)T$ are homogeneous quadratic polynomials in the components $T^\mu$. Timelike vectors form a nonempty open subset of $T_pM$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]). A polynomial that vanishes on an open set has all its partial derivatives zero at a point inside it, and for a quadratic polynomial these second derivatives are its coefficients; so it vanishes identically. Hence $R(T, S)T = 0$ for all $T$, and Steps 4–6 apply. This is part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The deviation equation probes only the combination $R(T, S)T$, symmetric in the two $T$ slots. That combination already determines the whole Riemann tensor, because the rest is fixed by the antisymmetry in the last pair and the first Bianchi identity. Measuring tides in all directions for all velocities is measuring the full curvature.

## The tide measured by a freely falling observer

[[#^thm-c3-12-2|Theorem §C3.12.2]] recovered the Newtonian law in the coordinates of one particular metric. An observer in free fall carries her own instruments: a clock reading $\tau$ and three rods held in fixed directions by gyroscopes ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]]). The geometric version of her non-rotating axes is a frame parallel-transported along her geodesic. In that frame the deviation equation should take exactly the Newtonian form, with a $3\times3$ matrix in place of $E_{ij}$.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.12.4: Tidal Tensor of an Observer
> *Structure.* Let $u \in T_pM$ be a unit timelike vector at an event $p$, $g(u, u) = -1$: the four-velocity of an observer at $p$. The **tidal tensor** of $u$ is the linear map of the tangent space to itself
>
> $$
> \mathcal E_u : T_pM \to T_pM, \qquad \mathcal E_u(S) \equiv R(S, u)\,u ,
> $$
>
> with the curvature operator of [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^def-c3-9-1|Def. §C3.9.1]]. As a $(1, 1)$ tensor and, with the index lowered ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]), as a $(0, 2)$ tensor,
>
> $$
> \mathcal E^\rho{}_\nu = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\nu\mu}\,u^\sigma u^\mu, \qquad \mathcal E_{\rho\nu} = R_{\rho\sigma\nu\mu}\,u^\sigma u^\mu .
> $$
>
> In an orthonormal frame with $e_{\hat 0} = u$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]) its spatial components are $\mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath} = R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat\jmath\hat0}$. With it the deviation equation of a timelike family with $T = u$ reads $D^2S/d\tau^2 = R(u, S)u = -\mathcal E_u(S)$ ([[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]] and $R(u, S) = -R(S, u)$), the form $\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi} = -E\boldsymbol\xi$ of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]]: $\mathcal E_u$ is the relativistic successor of the Newtonian tidal tensor $E_{ij}$ ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]]). Unlike $E_{ij}$, it depends on the observer: different four-velocities at the same event see different tides.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §10.4, eq. (10.22) ($K^a{}_b = R^a{}_{cbd}V^cV^d$, p. 175); §10.5, eq. (10.39) ($K^\alpha{}_\beta = R^\alpha{}_{0\beta0}$, p. 178) · Hartle, Gravity, §21.2, eq. (21.21) and the paragraph before it (p. 452)*

^def-c3-12-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.12.4: The Tide Measured by a Freely Falling Observer
> Let $\gamma$ be a timelike geodesic with proper time $\tau$ and four-velocity $u = T$, and let $(e_{\hat 0} = u, e_{\hat 1}, e_{\hat 2}, e_{\hat 3})$ be an orthonormal frame at one event of $\gamma$, parallel-transported along it ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-1|Def. §C3.7.1]]): the frame of a freely falling observer with non-rotating axes. Let $S = S^{\hat a}e_{\hat a}$ be the deviation vector of a family of free bodies containing $\gamma$ ([[#^def-c3-12-2|Def. §C3.12.2]]), all parametrized by proper time. Then:
> 1. **The deviation equation in the frame.** $S^{\hat 0} = -g(u, S)$ is constant along $\gamma$ (and zero with the choice of [[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]], part 4), and the spatial components obey
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2S^{\hat\imath}}{d\tau^2} = -\mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath}\,S^{\hat\jmath}, \qquad \mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath} = R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat\jmath\hat0} ,
> $$
>
> with ordinary derivatives of the components: the form of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]], part 1, now exact for the vector $S$.
> 2. **Symmetric and spatial.** $\mathcal E_{\rho\nu} = \mathcal E_{\nu\rho}$ and $\mathcal E_{\rho\nu}u^\nu = 0$ ([[#^def-c3-12-4|Def. §C3.12.4]]). So $\mathcal E_u$ is a symmetric bilinear form on the observer's rest space $u^\perp$: a real symmetric $3\times3$ matrix $\mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath}$, with three real eigenvalues and orthogonal principal axes ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]).
> 3. **Trace.** $\mathcal E^\rho{}_\rho = \mathcal E_{\hat1\hat1} + \mathcal E_{\hat2\hat2} + \mathcal E_{\hat3\hat3} = R_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu$, with the Ricci tensor of [[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^def-c3-11-1|Def. §C3.11.1]].
> 4. **Volume.** A small ball of free bodies released at rest relative to the observer ($dS^{\hat\imath}/d\tau = 0$ at $\tau = 0$) has a volume in her rest frame
>
> $$
> V(\tau) = V(0)\,\bigl[1 - \tfrac12R_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu\,\tau^2 + O(\tau^3)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> 5. **⚑ Newtonian limit.** For an observer momentarily at rest in the static weak-field metric ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]), to first order in $\varepsilon$,
>
> $$
> \mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi = E_{ij}, \qquad R_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = R_{00} = \nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi\rho \qquad (\text{restored: } c^2R_{00} = 4\pi G\rho) .
> $$
>
> Parts 3–4 then are [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-7|Theorem §C1.3.7]]: the trace of the tide is the mass density, and in vacuum a falling ball starts to change shape at constant volume. The matter density sits in one contraction of the curvature, $R_{00}$; this is the form in which the field equations are guessed in [[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations|§C5.1]].
>
> *Model:* parts 1–4 none (geometry of a timelike geodesic); part 5 [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]], whose potential obeys $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi\rho$ ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]); *applies:* parts 1–3 exactly, part 4 to first order in the size of the ball and for times short compared with the time over which $\mathcal E$ changes along $\gamma$, part 5 to first order in $\varepsilon$.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §10.5, eqs. (10.27)–(10.39) (pp. 176–178); §10.4, eqs. (10.24)–(10.26) (the trace, pp. 175–176) · Hartle, Gravity, §21.2, eq. (21.21) (p. 452); Table 21.1 (last row) · Couzens, GR1, §2.2.1 (p. 25)*

^thm-c3-12-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the frame stays adapted). Parallel transport preserves inner products ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-1|Theorem §C3.7.1]]), so $g(e_{\hat a}, e_{\hat b}) = \eta_{\hat a\hat b}$ all along $\gamma$. The four-velocity is itself parallel, $\nabla_uu = 0$ ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]); it agrees with $e_{\hat 0}$ at the initial event, so by uniqueness of parallel transport ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-1|Def. §C3.7.1]]) $e_{\hat 0} = u$ everywhere on $\gamma$.
>
> **Step 2** (components and their derivatives). The frame components are $S^{\hat\imath} = g(e_{\hat\imath}, S)$ and $S^{\hat 0} = -g(u, S)$. Since $De_{\hat a}/d\tau = 0$, the Leibniz rule of the covariant derivative along a curve ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-3|Def. §C3.5.3]]) gives
>
> $$
> \frac{DS}{d\tau} = \frac{dS^{\hat a}}{d\tau}\,e_{\hat a} + S^{\hat a}\,\frac{De_{\hat a}}{d\tau} = \frac{dS^{\hat a}}{d\tau}\,e_{\hat a}, \qquad \frac{D^2S}{d\tau^2} = \frac{d^2S^{\hat a}}{d\tau^2}\,e_{\hat a} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the right-hand side in the frame). By [[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]] and [[#^def-c3-12-4|Def. §C3.12.4]], $D^2S/d\tau^2 = R(u, S)u = -\mathcal E_u(S)$. Its spatial frame components, with $u^{\hat a} = \delta^{\hat a}_{\hat 0}$ and the antisymmetry in the last pair ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-1|Theorem §C3.10.1]]):
>
> $$
> g\bigl(e_{\hat\imath}, R(u, S)u\bigr) = R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat0\hat\nu}\,S^{\hat\nu} = -R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat\nu\hat0}\,S^{\hat\nu} = -R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat\jmath\hat0}\,S^{\hat\jmath} ,
> $$
>
> since the $\hat\nu = \hat 0$ term is $R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat0\hat0} = 0$. Its time component is $-g(u, R(u, S)u) = -R_{\hat0\hat0\hat0\hat\nu}S^{\hat\nu} = 0$ by the antisymmetry in the first pair (same theorem). With Step 2: $d^2S^{\hat\imath}/d\tau^2 = -R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat\jmath\hat0}S^{\hat\jmath}$ and $d^2S^{\hat 0}/d\tau^2 = 0$. Moreover $dS^{\hat 0}/d\tau = -\frac{d}{d\tau}g(u, S) = 0$ by [[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]], part 4 ($g(u, u) = -1$ on every member). This is part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (symmetric). By pair symmetry $R_{\rho\sigma\nu\mu} = R_{\nu\mu\rho\sigma}$ ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-3|Theorem §C3.10.3]]),
>
> $$
> \mathcal E_{\nu\rho} = R_{\nu\sigma\rho\mu}\,u^\sigma u^\mu = R_{\rho\mu\nu\sigma}\,u^\sigma u^\mu = R_{\rho\sigma\nu\mu}\,u^\mu u^\sigma = \mathcal E_{\rho\nu} ,
> $$
>
> renaming $\sigma \leftrightarrow \mu$ in the third expression.
>
> **Step 5** (spatial). $\mathcal E_{\rho\nu}u^\nu = R_{\rho\sigma\nu\mu}u^\sigma u^\nu u^\mu = 0$, because $u^\nu u^\mu$ is symmetric and $R_{\rho\sigma\nu\mu}$ antisymmetric in $(\nu, \mu)$; by Step 4 also $u^\rho\mathcal E_{\rho\nu} = 0$. In the frame, $\mathcal E_{\hat0\hat a} = \mathcal E_{\hat a\hat0} = 0$, so only the symmetric block $\mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath}$ remains. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 6** (trace). With $g^{\rho\nu}$ and $R_{\sigma\mu} = R^\nu{}_{\sigma\nu\mu}$,
>
> $$
> \mathcal E^\rho{}_\rho = g^{\rho\nu}R_{\rho\sigma\nu\mu}\,u^\sigma u^\mu = R^\nu{}_{\sigma\nu\mu}\,u^\sigma u^\mu = R_{\sigma\mu}\,u^\sigma u^\mu .
> $$
>
> In the frame the trace is $\eta^{\hat a\hat b}\mathcal E_{\hat a\hat b} = \sum_i\mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\imath}$, the $\hat 0$ entries being zero (Step 5). This is part 3.
>
> **Step 7** (volume). Label each body of the ball by its initial frame components $S^{\hat\jmath}_0$. Part 1 is linear, so with $dS^{\hat\imath}/d\tau(0) = 0$ its solution is $S^{\hat\imath}(\tau) = J^{\hat\imath}{}_{\hat\jmath}(\tau)\,S^{\hat\jmath}_0$ with $\ddot J = -\mathcal E(\tau)J$, $J(0) = \mathbb 1$, $\dot J(0) = 0$, hence $J(\tau) = \mathbb 1 - \tfrac12\mathcal E(0)\tau^2 + O(\tau^3)$. The ball at $\tau$ is the image of the initial ball under the linear map $J(\tau)$, so its volume is $|\det J(\tau)|\,V(0)$ ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-61|LADR Thm. 9.61]]). Expanding the determinant as in [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^der-c1-3-7|the derivation of Theorem §C1.3.7]], Step 4,
>
> $$
> \det J(\tau) = 1 - \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}\mathcal E(0)\,\tau^2 + O(\tau^3) = 1 - \tfrac12R_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu\,\tau^2 + O(\tau^3)
> $$
>
> by part 3. This is part 4.
>
> **Step 8** (Newtonian limit). An observer momentarily at rest has $u^\mu = (1 + 2\Phi)^{-1/2}(1, 0, 0, 0)$, and an orthonormal frame is $e_{\hat 0} = u$, $e_{\hat\imath} = (1 - 2\Phi)^{-1/2}\partial_i$. Then, with $R_{i0j0} = g_{ik}R^k{}_{0j0} = (1 - 2\Phi)R^i{}_{0j0}$ and [[#^thm-c3-12-2|Theorem §C3.12.2]], part 1,
>
> $$
> \mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath} = \frac{R_{i0j0}}{(1 - 2\Phi)(1 + 2\Phi)} = \frac{R^i{}_{0j0}}{1 + 2\Phi} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi\,\bigl[1 + O(\varepsilon)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> For the trace, $R_{00} = R^\rho{}_{0\rho0} = R^0{}_{000} + R^i{}_{0i0} = 0 + \partial_i\partial_i\Phi + O(\varepsilon^2/\ell^2) = \nabla^2\Phi$, and $R_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = R_{00}/(1 + 2\Phi) = \nabla^2\Phi\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)]$. The potential of the model obeys $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi\rho$ ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]). Restoring, $R_{00}$ is in $\mathrm m^{-2}$ and $\nabla^2\Phi/c^2 = 4\pi G\rho/c^2$. This is part 5 (both checked by script).
>
> **What the derivation shows.** In the observer's freely falling, non-rotating frame the deviation equation is literally Newton's tidal law with $E_{ij}$ replaced by $R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat\jmath\hat0}$; the only difference from §C1.3 is that no potential is needed to define it. The symmetries of the Riemann tensor make the tide a symmetric spatial matrix, and its trace is a component of the Ricci tensor, which in the Newtonian limit is the source of gravity.

> [!remark]- Remark: Focusing and conjugate points
> - On the sphere the meridians from the north pole meet again at the south pole ([[#^ex-c3-12-1|Example §C3.12.1]]). A point where a Jacobi field that vanished at the start vanishes again is called a conjugate point. Positive curvature focuses geodesics, negative curvature spreads them, and this is how a geodesic stops being the shortest (or, in spacetime, the longest) curve between its endpoints ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^cau-c3-7-1|§C3.7, Caution: Extremal, not always maximal]]).
> - Part 4 of [[#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]] is the first term of an evolution equation for the volume of a bundle of free bodies. When matter makes $R_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu \ge 0$ for every observer, every bundle released at rest starts to contract. Stated for whole congruences, this focusing is the engine of the singularity theorems (energy conditions §C4.5 and focusing C10, planned).

^rem-c3-12-1

The deviation equation describes how neighbouring free bodies separate. Some families separate in a special way. On the sphere the deviation vector between meridians is $S = \partial_\varphi$, the field that rotates the sphere about its axis; it moves every meridian onto another meridian without stretching any length, and it satisfies the deviation equation along every geodesic, not only along meridians (d'Inverno & Vickers, ex. 10.5). Vector fields whose flows preserve the metric are symmetries of the geometry, and along geodesics they yield conserved quantities such as the energy of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-5|Theorem §C1.5.5]]. Which vector fields are they, and what do they conserve? [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors|§C3.13]] answers this.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C3.12.1: Meridians on the Sphere Converge
> *Question.* The meridians of a sphere of radius $a$ all start at the north pole and are geodesics. How does the distance between two neighbouring meridians change along them, and where does the curvature appear?
>
> *Setup.* Sphere $ds^2 = a^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]), with $\Gamma^\theta{}_{\varphi\varphi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta$, $\Gamma^\varphi{}_{\theta\varphi} = \Gamma^\varphi{}_{\varphi\theta} = \cot\theta$ and all others zero ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^ex-c3-6-2|Example §C3.6.2]]). The family is $x(\lambda, s) = (\theta = \lambda/a,\ \varphi = s)$ for $0 < \lambda < \pi a$: each curve is a meridian, a geodesic ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^ex-c3-7-2|Example §C3.7.2]]), with arc length $\lambda$ as affine parameter.
>
> **Step 1** (the two vectors). $T = \partial x/\partial\lambda = \frac1a\,\partial_\theta$, $S = \partial x/\partial s = \partial_\varphi$ ([[#^def-c3-12-1|Def. §C3.12.1]], [[#^def-c3-12-2|Def. §C3.12.2]]). Then $g(T, T) = a^2\cdot\frac{1}{a^2} = 1$ for every meridian and $g(T, S) = 0$ (the metric is diagonal), so part 4 of [[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]] holds with no shift.
>
> **Step 2** (relative velocity). With $T^\theta = 1/a$, $T^\varphi = 0$, $S^\theta = 0$, $S^\varphi = 1$ in [[#^def-c3-12-3|Def. §C3.12.3]]:
>
> $$
> \frac{DS^\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{dS^\theta}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta\varphi}\,T^\theta S^\varphi = 0 + 0 = 0, \qquad \frac{DS^\varphi}{d\lambda} = \frac{dS^\varphi}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^\varphi{}_{\theta\varphi}\,T^\theta S^\varphi = 0 + \cot\theta\cdot\frac1a\cdot 1 = \frac{\cot\theta}{a} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (relative acceleration). With $V = DS/d\lambda = (0, \cot\theta/a)$ and $d\theta/d\lambda = 1/a$:
>
> $$
> \frac{D^2S^\varphi}{d\lambda^2} = \frac{d}{d\lambda}\Bigl(\frac{\cot\theta}{a}\Bigr) + \Gamma^\varphi{}_{\theta\varphi}\,T^\theta V^\varphi = -\frac{1}{a^2\sin^2\theta} + \frac{\cot^2\theta}{a^2} = -\frac{1}{a^2}, \qquad \frac{D^2S^\theta}{d\lambda^2} = 0 + \Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta\varphi}\,T^\theta V^\varphi = 0 ,
> $$
>
> using $\cot^2\theta - 1/\sin^2\theta = -1$. So $D^2S/d\lambda^2 = -\frac{1}{a^2}\,\partial_\varphi = -\frac{1}{a^2}\,S$.
>
> **Step 4** (the curvature side). The sphere has $R_{\theta\varphi\theta\varphi} = a^2\sin^2\theta$ ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^ex-c3-9-1|Example §C3.9.1]]). With the antisymmetries ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-1|Theorem §C3.10.1]]) and $g^{\varphi\varphi} = 1/(a^2\sin^2\theta)$,
>
> $$
> R^\varphi{}_{\theta\theta\varphi} = g^{\varphi\varphi}R_{\varphi\theta\theta\varphi} = -g^{\varphi\varphi}R_{\theta\varphi\theta\varphi} = -1, \qquad \bigl(R(T, S)T\bigr)^\varphi = R^\varphi{}_{\theta\theta\varphi}\,T^\theta T^\theta S^\varphi = -\frac{1}{a^2} ,
> $$
>
> and the $\theta$-component is $R^\theta{}_{\theta\theta\varphi}T^\theta T^\theta S^\varphi = g^{\theta\theta}R_{\theta\theta\theta\varphi}/a^2 = 0$. Both sides of [[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]] agree.
>
> **Step 5** (the distance). The unit vector $\hat e_\varphi = \partial_\varphi/(a\sin\theta)$ is parallel along a meridian: $\frac{D\hat e_\varphi^\varphi}{d\lambda} = \frac{d}{d\lambda}\frac{1}{a\sin\theta} + \cot\theta\cdot\frac1a\cdot\frac{1}{a\sin\theta} = -\frac{\cos\theta}{a^2\sin^2\theta} + \frac{\cos\theta}{a^2\sin^2\theta} = 0$. Write $S = \eta(\lambda)\,\hat e_\varphi$ with $\eta = |S| = a\sin\theta = a\sin(\lambda/a)$. Then $D^2S/d\lambda^2 = \eta''\,\hat e_\varphi$, and Step 3 becomes
>
> $$
> \eta'' = -\frac{1}{a^2}\,\eta, \qquad \eta(0) = 0,\ \ \eta'(0) = 1, \qquad \eta(\lambda) = a\sin\frac{\lambda}{a} .
> $$
>
> Two meridians $\delta\varphi$ apart are $a\sin(\lambda/a)\,\delta\varphi$ apart after arc length $\lambda$: they diverge from the pole, stop separating at the equator ($\lambda = \pi a/2$) and meet again at the south pole ($\lambda = \pi a$), a conjugate point.
>
> **Step 6** (any surface). On any two-dimensional surface the Riemann tensor is $R_{abcd} = K(g_{ac}g_{bd} - g_{ad}g_{bc})$ with Gaussian curvature $K = R/2$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^ex-c3-11-3|Example §C3.11.3]]). For a unit-speed geodesic and $S = \eta\,n$ with $n$ the unit normal to the geodesic (parallel along it, being orthogonal to the parallel $T$ in two dimensions), $\bigl(R(T, S)T\bigr)_a = R_{a\sigma\mu\nu}T^\sigma T^\mu S^\nu = K\bigl(T_a\,g(T, S) - S_a\,g(T, T)\bigr) = -K\,\eta\,n_a$, so $\eta'' = -K\eta$. The sphere has $K = 1/a^2$; the plane $K = 0$ gives $\eta = \lambda$, straight lines spreading linearly; a surface of constant $K = -1/a^2$ (the hyperbolic plane of [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-4|Example §C3.1.4]] has $K = -1$) gives $\eta = a\sinh(\lambda/a)$, exponential spreading. See the figure after [[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]].
>
> **Step 7** (what it shows). No force pulls the meridians together. Their convergence is the curvature of the surface, read off from the separation of geodesics alone, by someone who lives on the surface and never leaves it.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.5 (p. 100: initially parallel geodesics need not stay parallel) · d'Inverno & Vickers, ex. 15.7 (distances on a sphere, p. 300) · worked here; all steps checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.12.2: The Ring of Particles Again, Read from Curvature
> *Question.* [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle|§C1.3]] drew a ring of free particles stretched along the line to a mass and squeezed across it, and animated a falling ball that changes shape at constant volume (the figure and animation after [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-7|Theorem §C1.3.7]]). Both were computed from $\Phi$. What do they look like from the curvature, and how large is the curvature at the Earth's surface?
>
> *Setup.* Weak-field metric of a point mass, $\Phi = -M/r$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]), and a freely falling observer momentarily at rest at the point $(r, 0, 0)$, with the centre of the ring on her worldline.
>
> **Step 1** (the curvature components). By [[#^thm-c3-12-2|Theorem §C3.12.2]], part 1, and the second derivatives computed in [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^der-c1-3-6|the derivation of Theorem §C1.3.6]], Step 6,
>
> $$
> R^i{}_{0j0} = \partial_i\partial_j\Bigl(-\frac Mr\Bigr) = \frac{M}{r^3}\bigl(\delta_{ij} - 3n_in_j\bigr), \qquad \text{on the } x\text{-axis: } R^x{}_{0x0} = -\frac{2M}{r^3},\ \ R^y{}_{0y0} = R^z{}_{0z0} = \frac{M}{r^3},
> $$
>
> to first order; all other $R^i{}_{0j0}$ vanish there (checked by script from the metric).
>
> **Step 2** (the observer's tidal tensor). By [[#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]], part 5, $\mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath} = \operatorname{diag}(-2, 1, 1)\,M/r^3$ in her frame, with $\hat x$ pointing away from the mass.
>
> **Step 3** (the ring). Part 1 of the same theorem gives
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2S^{\hat x}}{d\tau^2} = +\frac{2M}{r^3}\,S^{\hat x}, \qquad \frac{d^2S^{\hat y}}{d\tau^2} = -\frac{M}{r^3}\,S^{\hat y}, \qquad \frac{d^2S^{\hat z}}{d\tau^2} = -\frac{M}{r^3}\,S^{\hat z} :
> $$
>
> a ring in the $\hat x\hat y$-plane released at rest stretches along the radial direction and narrows across it, exactly the arrows of the §C1.3 ring figure, which are $-E\boldsymbol\xi$ with $E = \mathcal E$. A passing gravitational wave acts on the same ring through a transverse, traceless tidal tensor that oscillates: proper distances change by $\tfrac12h_{ij}n^in^j$ ([[§C8.3 What a Passing Wave Does#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]]), the ring pulses into ellipses whose $+$ and $\times$ patterns differ by $45°$ ([[§C8.3 What a Passing Wave Does#^thm-c8-3-3|Theorem §C8.3.3]]), and under circular polarisation the ellipse rotates ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^ex-c8-2-2|Example §C8.2.2]]).
>
> **Step 4** (the ball). The trace is $(-2 + 1 + 1)M/r^3 = 0 = R_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu$: vacuum, $R_{00} = 0$ off the mass (checked by script). By part 4 the ball's volume is constant to order $\tau^2$, while its shape changes: the animation's statement, now read from the Ricci tensor.
>
> **Step 5** (numbers at the Earth's surface). Restored, $R^{\hat x}{}_{\hat0\hat x\hat0} = -2GM_\oplus/(c^2R_\oplus^3)$ and the tidal accelerations are $c^2$ times the curvature times the separation. With $GM_\oplus = 3.986\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}$, $R_\oplus = 6.378\times10^6$ m:
>
> $$
> \frac{2GM_\oplus}{R_\oplus^3} = 3.07\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}, \qquad R^{\hat x}{}_{\hat0\hat x\hat0} = -3.42\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}}, \qquad \mathcal R \equiv \bigl|R^{\hat x}{}_{\hat0\hat x\hat0}\bigr|^{-1/2} = 1.71\times10^{11}\ \mathrm m = 1.14\ \mathrm{AU} .
> $$
>
> Across the vertical, $|R^{\hat y}{}_{\hat0\hat y\hat0}|^{-1/2} = 2.42\times10^{11}$ m. Spacetime at the Earth's surface is curved on a length scale comparable to the Earth–Sun distance, or, divided by $c$, on time scales of $570$ s and $807$ s: the times over which tides grow to order one (§C1.3, [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^cau-c1-3-1|Caution: "Equivalence" is local]], quotes $807$ s). In normal coordinates the metric differs from $\eta$ at second order in the distance, with the curvature as coefficient ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates|§C3.8]]): over a laboratory of size $L$ the relative deviation is of order $L^2/\mathcal R^2$, about $10^{-22}$ for $L = 2$ m. This is why a local inertial frame works so well on the Earth.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, Ex. 21.1, eqs. (21.6)–(21.9) (pp. 448–449) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §10.2, eqs. (10.9)–(10.11) (p. 173) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.12.3: A Uniform Field Has No Tides
> *Question.* The metric $ds^2 = -(1 + gx)^2dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$ (Hartle Prob. 21.10; [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^ex-c3-9-3|Example §C3.9.3]]) describes a "uniform gravitational field" $g$: an observer at fixed $x$ is accelerated. Do free bodies in it have relative accelerations?
>
> **Step 1** (Christoffel symbols). Only $g_{tt} = -(1 + gx)^2$ is non-constant. By [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]],
>
> $$
> \Gamma^x{}_{tt} = -\tfrac12\,g^{xx}\,\partial_xg_{tt} = \tfrac12\,\partial_x(1 + gx)^2 = g\,(1 + gx), \qquad \Gamma^t{}_{tx} = \Gamma^t{}_{xt} = \tfrac12\,g^{tt}\,\partial_xg_{tt} = \tfrac12\cdot\frac{-1}{(1 + gx)^2}\cdot\bigl(-2g(1 + gx)\bigr) = \frac{g}{1 + gx} ,
> $$
>
> and all others vanish.
>
> **Step 2** (there is a "field"). A body momentarily at rest at $x = 0$ has $T = (dt/d\tau)\,\partial_t$ with $dt/d\tau = 1$ there, and the geodesic equation ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]) gives $d^2x/d\tau^2 = -\Gamma^x{}_{tt}(dt/d\tau)^2 = -g$: it falls with acceleration $g$ relative to the static observers.
>
> **Step 3** (no curvature). The only candidate components involve $t$ and $x$. From [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]] with $\rho = x$, $\sigma = t$, $\mu = t$, $\nu = x$:
>
> $$
> R^x{}_{ttx} = \partial_t\Gamma^x{}_{xt} - \partial_x\Gamma^x{}_{tt} + \Gamma^x{}_{t\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{xt} - \Gamma^x{}_{x\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{tt} = 0 - g^2 + \Gamma^x{}_{tt}\Gamma^t{}_{xt} - 0 = -g^2 + g(1 + gx)\cdot\frac{g}{1 + gx} = 0 .
> $$
>
> All other components vanish as well (checked by script; [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^ex-c3-9-3|Example §C3.9.3]]).
>
> **Step 4** (no tides). By [[#^thm-c3-12-3|Theorem §C3.12.3]], part 1, every family of free bodies has $D^2S/d\tau^2 = 0$: two balls dropped side by side or one above the other inside the "field" keep their separation in their own freely falling frame ([[#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]], part 1, with $\mathcal E = 0$). This is the relativistic form of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]]: a uniform field is removed everywhere at once by free fall, because it is flat spacetime seen from accelerated coordinates (Rindler).
>
> **Step 5** (contrast). The weak-field metric with the uniform potential $\Phi = gx$ also has $R^i{}_{0j0} = \partial_i\partial_j(gx) = 0$ (checked by script). The field of a real body has $\partial_i\partial_j\Phi \neq 0$ ([[#^ex-c3-12-2|Example §C3.12.2]]), and its tides can be removed by no choice of frame ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]], part 2): it is curved spacetime.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, Prob. 21.10 (worked here) · Couzens, GR1, §2.2.1 (p. 25)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - **Level A and B tides.** Classical Mechanics level A defines the tidal force as the difference of pulls across a body ([[§A8.4 Tidal Forces#^def-a8-4-1|CM Def. §A8.4.1]]) and estimates it ([[§A8.4 Tidal Forces#^thm-a8-4-1|CM Theorem §A8.4.1]]); level B computes it in the Earth's freely falling frame ([[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^thm-b9-2-7|CM Theorem §B9.2.7]]). Both are the radial and transverse entries of $\mathcal E$ in [[#^ex-c3-12-2|Example §C3.12.2]].
> - **Layer changes.** [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]] (tidal tensor from $\Phi$) becomes [[#^def-c3-12-4|Def. §C3.12.4]] (tidal tensor from curvature) through [[#^thm-c3-12-2|Theorem §C3.12.2]]; [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]] becomes [[#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]]; [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-7|Theorem §C1.3.7]] becomes parts 3–5 of [[#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]]. The open row "tidal acceleration … curvature, geodesic deviation" of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^rem-c1-5-1|§C1.5, Remark: What has been assumed]] is closed here.
> - **Two ways to see curvature.** [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-4|Theorem §C3.9.4]] detects $R$ by transporting one vector around a small loop; the deviation equation detects it by watching two geodesics. Both are commutators of covariant derivatives (Step 4 of [[#^der-c3-12-1|the derivation of Theorem §C3.12.1]]), and the second needs only free bodies and clocks.
> - **Mathematics.** The tidal tensor is a real symmetric form on the rest space, diagonalized by [[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]. The deviation vector is a push-forward of a coordinate field ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^def-c2-4-1|Def. §C2.4.1]]), and $[T, S] = 0$ is the commuting-flows statement of [[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-7|Theorem §C2.5.7]]. On a surface, $\eta'' = -K\eta$ ([[#^ex-c3-12-1|Example §C3.12.1]]) is Gauss's way of measuring curvature from inside.
> - **Forward.** Einstein's vacuum equation $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$ guessed from the trace of the tide ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations|§C5.1]]; d'Inverno & Vickers §10.4); the tide of a gravitational wave and the ring of test particles (C8: [[§C8.3 What a Passing Wave Does#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]], [[§C8.3 What a Passing Wave Does#^thm-c8-3-3|Theorem §C8.3.3]], [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^ex-c8-2-2|Example §C8.2.2]]); focusing of free bodies and the singularity theorems (C10, planned).
