[[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative|§C3.4]] built a derivative of tensor fields that needs only a direction at a point: an affine connection $\nabla$ ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]), with coefficients $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}$ in each chart ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-4-2|Def. §C3.4.2]]). It did not say *which* connection. The difference of two connections is a $(1, 2)$ tensor field ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]]), so adding any $(1, 2)$ tensor field to one connection gives another: on spacetime that is $4 \cdot 4 \cdot 4 = 64$ free functions. Meanwhile [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor|§C3.1]] gave the manifold a metric $g$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]), which measures lengths and angles. The question of this section is therefore: *does the metric single out one connection, and what must a connection satisfy to deserve it?*

Two connections on the Euclidean plane, $g = dx^2 + dy^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\theta^2$, show what can go wrong.

*(a) $\Gamma = 0$ in the polar chart.* Declare $\nabla_\mu V^\nu = \partial_\mu V^\nu$ in the chart $(r, \theta)$. The field $\partial_\theta$ has components $(V^r, V^\theta) = (0, 1)$ everywhere, so $\nabla\partial_\theta = 0$: this connection calls it constant. Its length is $\sqrt{g_{\theta\theta}} = r$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-1|Example §C3.1.1]]), so along a ray the "constant" field doubles in length between $r = 1$ and $r = 2$. In components the failure is one number, computed with the formula for the derivative of a $(0, 2)$ tensor ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]]):

$$
\nabla_rg_{\theta\theta} = \partial_rg_{\theta\theta} - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{r\theta}\,g_{\lambda\theta} - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{r\theta}\,g_{\theta\lambda} = \partial_r(r^2) - 0 - 0 = 2r \neq 0 .
$$

The connection does not respect the metric. It is also not the connection with $\Gamma = 0$ in Cartesian coordinates, whose polar coefficients are $\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta} = -r$, $\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta} = \Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r} = 1/r$ ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^ex-c3-4-1|Example §C3.4.1]]): "$\Gamma = 0$ in the chart at hand" is a different rule in every chart.

*(b) A rotating connection.* In Cartesian coordinates set

$$
\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = \kappa\,\delta^x_\mu\,J^\lambda{}_\nu, \qquad J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\ \ (J^x{}_y = -1,\ J^y{}_x = 1), \qquad \kappa > 0 \text{ constant} .
$$

Lowering with $\delta$ gives $J_{\mu\nu} \equiv \delta_{\mu\lambda}J^\lambda{}_\nu$, antisymmetric, so the metric is respected: $\nabla_\rho\delta_{\mu\nu} = 0 - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu}\delta_{\lambda\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\nu}\delta_{\mu\lambda} = -\kappa\,\delta^x_\rho\,(J_{\nu\mu} + J_{\mu\nu}) = 0$.

Yet its self-parallel curves, $\ddot x^\lambda + \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu = 0$ (the geodesic equation of §C3.6, [[§C3.6 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]), are not straight. For a curve with unit speed and heading $\varphi$, $\dot x = (\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$, so $\ddot x = \dot\varphi\,(-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)$ and $J\dot x = (-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)$; the equation reads

$$
\dot\varphi\,(-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi) = -\kappa\,\dot x^x\,J\dot x = -\kappa\cos\varphi\,(-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dot\varphi = -\kappa\cos\varphi ,
$$

and every curve not heading along $\pm\partial_y$ turns, although no length or angle changes (figure). The coefficients are not symmetric: $\Gamma^x{}_{xy} = \kappa J^x{}_y = -\kappa$, while $\Gamma^x{}_{yx} = 0$.

Connection (a) has symmetric coefficients but does not respect the metric; connection (b) respects the metric but has non-symmetric coefficients. Each condition alone leaves a large family. The section shows that the two together leave exactly one. It first turns the asymmetry of $\Gamma$ into a tensor, the torsion ([[#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]]), and the respect for the metric into the condition $\nabla g = 0$ ([[#^def-c3-5-2|Def. §C3.5.2]]). The fundamental theorem of Riemannian geometry then gives a unique connection with both properties, through the Koszul formula ([[#^thm-c3-5-1|Theorem §C3.5.1]]); in a chart its coefficients are the Christoffel symbols ([[#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]]). Two consequences make the metric volume and the connection work together: the contracted symbol is $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\nu\ln\sqrt{|g|}$ ([[#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]]), which turns the covariant divergence into an ordinary one and gives the divergence theorem ([[#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]]), and index raising commutes with $\nabla$ ([[#^thm-c3-5-5|Theorem §C3.5.5]]). Why general relativity uses this connection and no other is [[#^rem-c3-5-1|Remark: Why the connection of general relativity is torsion-free]].

**Setup.** $M$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $n$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]) with a metric $g$ of any signature, Riemannian or Lorentzian ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]); for spacetime $n = 4$ and the signature is $(-, +, +, +)$. Vector fields are smooth ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-4|Def. §C2.2.4]]); $f$ denotes a smooth function and $Xf$ its derivative along $X$. A connection is an affine connection in the sense of [[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]: $\nabla_XY$ is additive in $X$ and $Y$, $\nabla_{fX}Y = f\nabla_XY$, and $\nabla_X(fY) = f\nabla_XY + (Xf)Y$; it acts on functions as $\nabla_Xf = Xf$ and on tensors of every type by the Leibniz rule and commutation with contraction ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]]). The theorems use the nondegeneracy of $g$, never its signature, and are exact; the identities and numbers in the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]); connection coefficients with the derivative index first, $\nabla_{\partial_\mu}\partial_\nu = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}\partial_\lambda$, so $\nabla_\mu V^\lambda = \partial_\mu V^\lambda + \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}V^\nu$ and $\nabla_\mu\omega_\nu = \partial_\mu\omega_\nu - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}\omega_\lambda$ ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]], [[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^cau-c3-4-1|§C3.4, Caution: Index order of Γ in the sources]]); torsion $T^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu}$; $g \equiv \det(g_{\mu\nu})$ when it stands without indices, $|g|$ its absolute value; $\flat$ and $\sharp$ lower and raise indices ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]).

## Torsion

Connection (b) shows that the asymmetry of $\Gamma$ has a geometric effect. But $\Gamma$ is not a tensor ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]]), so "$\Gamma$ is not symmetric" might be an artefact of the chart. It is not: the antisymmetric part of $\Gamma$ is the component array of a tensor built from $\nabla$ alone.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.5.1: Torsion Tensor
> *Structure.* Let $\nabla$ be an affine connection on $M$ ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]). Its **torsion** takes two vector fields to a vector field,
>
> $$
> T(X, Y) = \nabla_XY - \nabla_YX - [X, Y] ,
> $$
>
> where $[X, Y]$ is the Lie bracket ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]]; [[§58 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^def-58-1|591 Def. §58.1]]). Equivalently, $T(\omega; X, Y) = \omega\bigl(T(X, Y)\bigr)$ for one-forms $\omega$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-7|Def. §C2.2.7]]).
> 1. *Type.* $T$ is a $(1, 2)$ tensor field ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]): $T(X, Y)$ at $p$ depends only on $X_p$ and $Y_p$, bilinearly. It is antisymmetric, $T(X, Y) = -T(Y, X)$.
> 2. *Components.* In a chart,
>
> $$
> T^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} \equiv dx^\lambda\bigl(T(\partial_\mu, \partial_\nu)\bigr) = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu} ,
> $$
>
> the antisymmetric part (times $2$) of the connection coefficients ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-4-2|Def. §C3.4.2]]). The array $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}$ is not a tensor ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]]); its antisymmetric part is.
>
> A connection with $T = 0$, that is with $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu}$ in every chart, or $\nabla_XY - \nabla_YX = [X, Y]$ for all $X$, $Y$, is called **torsion-free** (or **symmetric**). Connection (a) of the opening is torsion-free; connection (b) has $T^x{}_{xy} = \Gamma^x{}_{xy} - \Gamma^x{}_{yx} = -\kappa$. What the torsion measures geometrically, the failure of a small parallelogram of transported vectors to close, is [[#^ex-c3-5-4|Example §C3.5.4]]. Sources that put the derivative index of $\Gamma$ last, as d'Inverno & Vickers do, obtain the same tensor with the opposite sign ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^cau-c3-4-1|§C3.4, Caution: Index order of Γ in the sources]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.3, Definition 36, eqs. (4.58)–(4.59) (PDF p. 84, printed p. 83); §4.3.1, eqs. (4.62)–(4.63), Definition 38 (p. 84); Exercise 4.2 · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.3, before eq. (6.28) (p. 91; derivative index last)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation of parts 1 and 2
> **Step 1** (two rules for brackets). For functions $f$, $h$, the bracket $[X, Y]h = X(Yh) - Y(Xh)$ ([[§58 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^def-58-1|591 Def. §58.1]]) and the product rule for the derivations $X$, $Y$ give
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> [fX, Y]h &= fX(Yh) - Y(fXh) = fX(Yh) - (Yf)(Xh) - fY(Xh) = \bigl(f[X, Y] - (Yf)X\bigr)h , \\
> [X, fY]h &= X(fYh) - fY(Xh) = (Xf)(Yh) + fX(Yh) - fY(Xh) = \bigl(f[X, Y] + (Xf)Y\bigr)h .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (antisymmetry and additivity). $[Y, X] = -[X, Y]$, so $T(Y, X) = \nabla_YX - \nabla_XY + [X, Y] = -T(X, Y)$. $\nabla$ is additive in each slot ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]) and the bracket is $\mathbf R$-bilinear, so $T$ is additive in each slot.
>
> **Step 3** ($C^\infty$-linearity). With the axioms of $\nabla$ and Step 1,
>
> $$
> T(fX, Y) = \underbrace{f\nabla_XY}_{\nabla_{fX}Y} - \underbrace{\bigl(f\nabla_YX + (Yf)X\bigr)}_{\nabla_Y(fX)} - \underbrace{\bigl(f[X, Y] - (Yf)X\bigr)}_{[fX,\, Y]} = f\,T(X, Y) ,
> $$
>
> the two terms $(Yf)X$ cancelling. By Step 2, $T(X, fY) = -T(fY, X) = -fT(Y, X) = fT(X, Y)$. Without the bracket in the definition, $\nabla_XY - \nabla_YX$ alone would leave the term $-(Yf)X$: the bracket is what makes $T$ a tensor.
>
> **Step 4** ($C^\infty$-linearity means pointwise). Let $p \in M$, let $(U, x^\mu)$ be a chart around $p$, and let $\psi$ be a bump function at $p$ with support in $U$, equal to $1$ on a neighbourhood of $p$ ([[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-6|591 Lemma §56.6]]). Suppose $X_p = 0$. On $U$ write $X = X^\mu\partial_\mu$ with $X^\mu(p) = 0$. The functions $\psi X^\mu$ and the vector fields $\psi\,\partial_\mu$, extended by zero outside $U$, are smooth on $M$, and $\psi^2X = (\psi X^\mu)(\psi\,\partial_\mu)$. Then, by Step 3, $T(X, Y) = T(\psi^2X, Y) + T((1 - \psi^2)X, Y) = (\psi X^\mu)\,T(\psi\,\partial_\mu, Y) + (1 - \psi^2)\,T(X, Y)$, and at $p$ both coefficients vanish: $\psi X^\mu = X^\mu = 0$ and $1 - \psi^2 = 0$. So $T(X, Y)_p = 0$ whenever $X_p = 0$; by additivity $T(X, Y)_p$ depends on $X$ only through $X_p$, and likewise on $Y$ only through $Y_p$. Hence $T_p$ is a bilinear map $T_pM \times T_pM \to T_pM$, a $(1, 2)$ tensor at $p$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]); its components below are smooth, so $T$ is a tensor field. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 5** (components). On $U$ the coordinate fields have vanishing brackets, $[\partial_\mu, \partial_\nu]f = \partial_\mu\partial_\nu f - \partial_\nu\partial_\mu f = 0$ ([[§58 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^prop-58-2|591 Prop. §58.2]]), and $\nabla_{\partial_\mu}\partial_\nu = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}\partial_\lambda$ ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-4-2|Def. §C3.4.2]]), so $T(\partial_\mu, \partial_\nu) = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}\partial_\lambda - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu}\partial_\lambda - 0$ and $dx^\lambda(T(\partial_\mu, \partial_\nu)) = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu}$. (By Step 4, using the coordinate fields of $U$ in place of global fields changes nothing at points of $U$.) Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 6** (the same, from the transformation law). Under a change of chart $x \to x'$ the coefficients obey ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]])
>
> $$
> \Gamma'^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial x'^\lambda}{\partial x^\alpha}\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\mu}\frac{\partial x^\gamma}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma} + \frac{\partial x'^\lambda}{\partial x^\alpha}\frac{\partial^2x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\mu\,\partial x'^\nu} .
> $$
>
> The second term is symmetric in $\mu\nu$, since second partial derivatives commute. Subtracting the same law with $\mu \leftrightarrow \nu$ removes it:
>
> $$
> \Gamma'^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} - \Gamma'^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu} = \frac{\partial x'^\lambda}{\partial x^\alpha}\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\mu}\frac{\partial x^\gamma}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\bigl(\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma} - \Gamma^\alpha{}_{\gamma\beta}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the law of a $(1, 2)$ tensor ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]]). For the rotating connection carried to polar coordinates the script confirms this term by term.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** A change of chart alters only the *symmetric* part of $\Gamma$ at a point (Step 6). The antisymmetric part is the torsion, a tensor: if it is nonzero at $p$ in one chart, it is nonzero there in every chart, and no choice of coordinates makes all $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}(p)$ vanish. The bump-function argument of Step 4 turns "$C^\infty$-linear" into "pointwise"; it is used again in the proof of [[#^thm-c3-5-1|Theorem §C3.5.1]].

## Metric compatibility

Connection (a) failed because carrying a vector with it changes the vector's length. The metric is a tensor field, so its covariant derivative is defined ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]]); asking it to vanish is the condition that the connection does not change what the metric measures.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.5.2: Metric Compatibility
> *Structure.* An affine connection $\nabla$ on $(M, g)$ is **compatible with the metric** $g$ (a **metric connection**) if the $(0, 3)$ tensor field $\nabla g$ vanishes, $\nabla_Xg = 0$ for every vector field $X$: the metric is covariantly constant. With the extension of $\nabla$ to $(0, 2)$ tensors ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]]) this reads, equivalently,
> 1. *(product rule for inner products)* for all vector fields $X$, $Y$, $Z$,
>
> $$
> X\bigl(g(Y, Z)\bigr) = g(\nabla_XY, Z) + g(Y, \nabla_XZ) ;
> $$
>
> 2. *(components)* in every chart,
>
> $$
> \nabla_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu}\,g_{\lambda\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\nu}\,g_{\mu\lambda} = 0 .
> $$
>
> Part 1 says that the derivative of the number $g(Y, Z)$ along $X$ comes entirely from the derivatives of $Y$ and $Z$; carrying two vectors along a curve without changing them then keeps their inner product, so lengths and angles are preserved ([[§C3.6 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-6-1|Theorem §C3.6.1]]). Connection (a) of the opening is not metric ($\nabla_rg_{\theta\theta} = 2r$); connection (b) is.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.3.4, eqs. (4.67)–(4.69) (PDF p. 86, printed p. 85) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.10, eq. (6.74) (p. 104: "the metric is 'covariantly constant'") · Hartle §20.4, eq. (20.70) and Problem 20.17*

^def-c3-5-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation of the equivalent forms
> **Step 1** (the derivative of $g$ along $X$). By [[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]], $\nabla_X$ obeys the Leibniz rule on $g\otimes Y\otimes Z$ and commutes with the contraction that produces the function $g(Y, Z)$, and on functions $\nabla_X = X$: $X(g(Y, Z)) = (\nabla_Xg)(Y, Z) + g(\nabla_XY, Z) + g(Y, \nabla_XZ)$. So $\nabla_Xg = 0$ for all $X$ if and only if part 1 holds for all $X$, $Y$, $Z$.
>
> **Step 2** (components). Put $X = \partial_\rho$, $Y = \partial_\mu$, $Z = \partial_\nu$ in Step 1 and use $\nabla_{\partial_\rho}\partial_\mu = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu}\partial_\lambda$ ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-4-2|Def. §C3.4.2]]) and $g(\partial_\lambda, \partial_\nu) = g_{\lambda\nu}$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]]):
>
> $$
> (\nabla_\rho g)_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} - g\bigl(\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu}\partial_\lambda, \partial_\nu\bigr) - g\bigl(\partial_\mu, \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\nu}\partial_\lambda\bigr) = \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu}g_{\lambda\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\nu}g_{\mu\lambda} .
> $$
>
> A tensor vanishes if and only if all its components vanish in one chart, and then in every chart ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 4). So $\nabla g = 0$ is equivalent to part 2 in every chart, or in the charts of one atlas.

## The fundamental theorem

A count shows why the two conditions might fix the connection. In a chart a connection has $n^3$ coefficients. Torsion-free leaves the symmetric ones, $n\cdot n(n+1)/2$. Metric compatibility, part 2 of [[#^def-c3-5-2|Def. §C3.5.2]], is one equation for each $\rho$ and each symmetric pair $\mu\nu$: again $n\cdot n(n+1)/2$ equations, linear in $\Gamma$. For $n = 4$ that is $40$ unknowns and $40$ equations. The theorem says that this square system always has exactly one solution, and gives it in a form that does not use coordinates.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.1: Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry
> Let $(M, g)$ be a smooth manifold with a metric of any signature ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]], [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]). There is exactly one affine connection $\nabla$ on $M$ ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]) that is
> 1. torsion-free, $\nabla_XY - \nabla_YX = [X, Y]$ ([[#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]]), and
> 2. compatible with the metric, $\nabla g = 0$ ([[#^def-c3-5-2|Def. §C3.5.2]]).
>
> It is determined by the **Koszul formula**: for all vector fields $X$, $Y$, $Z$,
>
> $$
> 2\,g(\nabla_XY, Z) = X\,g(Y, Z) + Y\,g(Z, X) - Z\,g(X, Y) + g\bigl([X, Y], Z\bigr) - g\bigl([Y, Z], X\bigr) + g\bigl([Z, X], Y\bigr) .
> $$
>
> This connection is the **Levi-Civita connection** of $g$; its coefficients in a chart are the Christoffel symbols ([[#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]]). Only the nondegeneracy of $g$ enters, so the theorem holds for Lorentzian metrics as for Riemannian ones.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a manifold with a metric; *applies:* every smooth manifold with a smooth metric of any signature; exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.3.4, Theorem 5, eqs. (4.67)–(4.74) (PDF pp. 86–87, printed pp. 85–86; GR1 writes the Koszul formula (4.73) for $g(\nabla_YX, Z)$, the same formula with $X \leftrightarrow Y$, and checks one axiom, $\nabla_{fY}X = f\nabla_YX$; all are checked here) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.10, Theorem on p. 104 (for symmetric connections: $\nabla g = 0$ exactly for the metric connection; proof left as an exercise)*

^thm-c3-5-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation (proof)
> Write $K(X, Y, Z)$ for the right-hand side of the Koszul formula. The bracket rules $[fX, Y] = f[X, Y] - (Yf)X$ and $[X, fY] = f[X, Y] + (Xf)Y$ are Step 1 of the derivation after [[#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]].
>
> *Uniqueness.*
>
> **Step 1** (three product rules). Let $\nabla$ be torsion-free and metric. Part 1 of [[#^def-c3-5-2|Def. §C3.5.2]], written for the cyclic orders $(X, Y, Z)$, $(Y, Z, X)$, $(Z, X, Y)$:
>
> $$
> \text{(a)}\ \ X\,g(Y, Z) = g(\nabla_XY, Z) + g(Y, \nabla_XZ), \quad \text{(b)}\ \ Y\,g(Z, X) = g(\nabla_YZ, X) + g(Z, \nabla_YX), \quad \text{(c)}\ \ Z\,g(X, Y) = g(\nabla_ZX, Y) + g(X, \nabla_ZY) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (combine and remove the torsion). Form (a) + (b) − (c) and group the six terms by the vector that appears undifferentiated, using the symmetry of $g$:
>
> $$
> \text{(a)} + \text{(b)} - \text{(c)} = g\bigl(Z, \nabla_XY + \nabla_YX\bigr) + g\bigl(Y, \nabla_XZ - \nabla_ZX\bigr) + g\bigl(X, \nabla_YZ - \nabla_ZY\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Torsion-free means $\nabla_XZ - \nabla_ZX = [X, Z]$, $\nabla_YZ - \nabla_ZY = [Y, Z]$ and $\nabla_YX = \nabla_XY - [X, Y]$. Substituting,
>
> $$
> X\,g(Y, Z) + Y\,g(Z, X) - Z\,g(X, Y) = 2\,g(\nabla_XY, Z) - g\bigl([X, Y], Z\bigr) + g\bigl([X, Z], Y\bigr) + g\bigl([Y, Z], X\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Solving for $2g(\nabla_XY, Z)$ and writing $g([X, Z], Y) = -g([Z, X], Y)$ gives the Koszul formula, $2g(\nabla_XY, Z) = K(X, Y, Z)$.
>
> **Step 3** (a vector field is fixed by its inner products). Let $W$ be a vector field with $g(W, Z) = 0$ for every vector field $Z$. At $p \in M$ take any $v \in T_pM$, a chart around $p$ and a bump function $\psi$ at $p$ supported in it ([[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-6|591 Lemma §56.6]]); then $Z = \psi\,v^\mu\partial_\mu$, extended by zero, is a vector field with $Z_p = v$. So $g_p(W_p, v) = 0$ for all $v$, and $W_p = 0$ because $g_p$ is nondegenerate ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]). If two torsion-free metric connections $\nabla$, $\nabla'$ existed, Step 2 would give $g(\nabla_XY - \nabla'_XY, Z) = \tfrac12K - \tfrac12K = 0$ for all $Z$, hence $\nabla'_XY = \nabla_XY$. Uniqueness is proved.
>
> *Existence.* Define $\nabla$ by the Koszul formula, and check that this is possible and that the result has every property claimed.
>
> **Step 4** ($K$ is $C^\infty$-linear in $Z$). $K$ is additive in each slot, because $g$ is bilinear, the bracket is $\mathbf R$-bilinear and $X(\cdot)$ is additive. Replace $Z$ by $fZ$ and expand each of the six terms with the product rule and the bracket rules:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> X\,g(Y, fZ) &= f\,X\,g(Y, Z) + (Xf)\,g(Y, Z), & g\bigl([X, Y], fZ\bigr) &= f\,g\bigl([X, Y], Z\bigr), \\
> Y\,g(fZ, X) &= f\,Y\,g(Z, X) + (Yf)\,g(Z, X), & -g\bigl([Y, fZ], X\bigr) &= -f\,g\bigl([Y, Z], X\bigr) - (Yf)\,g(Z, X), \\
> -fZ\,g(X, Y) &= -f\,Z\,g(X, Y), & g\bigl([fZ, X], Y\bigr) &= f\,g\bigl([Z, X], Y\bigr) - (Xf)\,g(Z, Y) .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> The extra terms $(Xf)g(Y, Z) - (Xf)g(Z, Y)$ and $(Yf)g(Z, X) - (Yf)g(Z, X)$ cancel: $K(X, Y, fZ) = f\,K(X, Y, Z)$.
>
> **Step 5** (a one-form, then a vector field). For fixed $X$, $Y$ let $\theta(Z) \equiv \tfrac12K(X, Y, Z)$. By Step 4 and the bump-function argument of Step 4 of the derivation after [[#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]], $\theta(Z)(p)$ depends only on $Z_p$, linearly: $\theta_p \in T^{\ast}_pM$. Its components near $p$, $\theta_\rho = \theta(\psi\,\partial_\rho)$ on the set where $\psi = 1$, are smooth, so $\theta$ is a one-form field ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-7|Def. §C2.2.7]]). Define
>
> $$
> \nabla_XY \equiv \theta^\sharp, \qquad (\nabla_XY)^\lambda = g^{\lambda\rho}\,\theta_\rho ,
> $$
>
> the vector field obtained by raising the index ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]). By construction $g(\nabla_XY, Z) = \tfrac12K(X, Y, Z)$ for all $Z$, and by Step 3 this property determines $\nabla_XY$. The connection axioms and the two properties are now checked through $K$: by Step 3, an identity between vector fields holds once it holds after taking $g(\cdot, Z)$ for every $Z$.
>
> **Step 6** (additivity). $K$ is additive in $X$ and in $Y$ (Step 4), so $\nabla_{X + X'}Y = \nabla_XY + \nabla_{X'}Y$ and $\nabla_X(Y + Y') = \nabla_XY + \nabla_XY'$.
>
> **Step 7** ($C^\infty$-linear in $X$). Replace $X$ by $fX$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> fX\,g(Y, Z) &= f\,X\,g(Y, Z), & g\bigl([fX, Y], Z\bigr) &= f\,g\bigl([X, Y], Z\bigr) - (Yf)\,g(X, Z), \\
> Y\,g(Z, fX) &= f\,Y\,g(Z, X) + (Yf)\,g(Z, X), & -g\bigl([Y, Z], fX\bigr) &= -f\,g\bigl([Y, Z], X\bigr), \\
> -Z\,g(fX, Y) &= -f\,Z\,g(X, Y) - (Zf)\,g(X, Y), & g\bigl([Z, fX], Y\bigr) &= f\,g\bigl([Z, X], Y\bigr) + (Zf)\,g(X, Y) .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> The extra terms cancel in pairs, $K(fX, Y, Z) = f\,K(X, Y, Z)$, so $\nabla_{fX}Y = f\,\nabla_XY$.
>
> **Step 8** (Leibniz rule in $Y$). Replace $Y$ by $fY$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> X\,g(fY, Z) &= f\,X\,g(Y, Z) + (Xf)\,g(Y, Z), & g\bigl([X, fY], Z\bigr) &= f\,g\bigl([X, Y], Z\bigr) + (Xf)\,g(Y, Z), \\
> fY\,g(Z, X) &= f\,Y\,g(Z, X), & -g\bigl([fY, Z], X\bigr) &= -f\,g\bigl([Y, Z], X\bigr) + (Zf)\,g(Y, X), \\
> -Z\,g(X, fY) &= -f\,Z\,g(X, Y) - (Zf)\,g(X, Y), & g\bigl([Z, X], fY\bigr) &= f\,g\bigl([Z, X], Y\bigr) .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> The $(Zf)$ terms cancel and the $(Xf)$ terms add: $K(X, fY, Z) = f\,K(X, Y, Z) + 2(Xf)\,g(Y, Z)$. Halving, $g(\nabla_X(fY), Z) = g\bigl(f\nabla_XY + (Xf)Y, Z\bigr)$ for all $Z$, so $\nabla_X(fY) = f\nabla_XY + (Xf)Y$. With Steps 6–7, $\nabla$ is an affine connection.
>
> **Step 9** (torsion-free). Write $K(Y, X, Z)$ out and subtract it from $K(X, Y, Z)$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> K(X, Y, Z) - K(Y, X, Z) ={}& \bigl[X\,g(Y, Z) - X\,g(Z, Y)\bigr] + \bigl[Y\,g(Z, X) - Y\,g(X, Z)\bigr] - \bigl[Z\,g(X, Y) - Z\,g(Y, X)\bigr] \\
> &+ g\bigl([X, Y], Z\bigr) - g\bigl([Y, X], Z\bigr) - g\bigl([Y, Z], X\bigr) - g\bigl([Z, Y], X\bigr) + g\bigl([Z, X], Y\bigr) + g\bigl([X, Z], Y\bigr) \\
> ={}& 0 + 0 - 0 + 2\,g\bigl([X, Y], Z\bigr) + 0 + 0 .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> So $g(\nabla_XY - \nabla_YX, Z) = g([X, Y], Z)$ for all $Z$: $T(X, Y) = 0$.
>
> **Step 10** (metric compatible). Write $K(X, Z, Y)$ out and add it to $K(X, Y, Z)$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> K(X, Y, Z) + K(X, Z, Y) ={}& \bigl[X\,g(Y, Z) + X\,g(Z, Y)\bigr] + \bigl[Y\,g(Z, X) - Y\,g(X, Z)\bigr] + \bigl[-Z\,g(X, Y) + Z\,g(Y, X)\bigr] \\
> &+ g\bigl([X, Y], Z\bigr) + g\bigl([Y, X], Z\bigr) - g\bigl([Y, Z], X\bigr) - g\bigl([Z, Y], X\bigr) + g\bigl([Z, X], Y\bigr) + g\bigl([X, Z], Y\bigr) \\
> ={}& 2\,X\,g(Y, Z) .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> Halving, $g(\nabla_XY, Z) + g(Y, \nabla_XZ) = X\,g(Y, Z)$, which is part 1 of [[#^def-c3-5-2|Def. §C3.5.2]]. Existence is proved. (Steps 4 and 7–10 were also verified symbolically for a general two-dimensional metric and general fields by script.)
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Uniqueness uses the two conditions at different places: metric compatibility gives the three product rules, torsion-freeness turns the six $\nabla$ terms into one. Nondegeneracy (Step 3) turns knowledge of $g(\nabla_XY, Z)$ for all $Z$ into knowledge of $\nabla_XY$. The signature is never used.
> - In the Koszul formula every term is built from $g$, the brackets and derivatives of functions. So the connection is a function of the metric and its first derivatives alone; [[#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]] makes this explicit.
> - The axioms of a connection are not automatic for a formula like Koszul's: the bracket terms are exactly what cancels the derivatives of $f$ in Steps 4, 7 and 8.

In a chart the Koszul formula becomes a formula for the coefficients. The coordinate fields have vanishing brackets, so only the three derivative terms survive.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.2: Christoffel Symbols
> In every chart the coefficients ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-4-2|Def. §C3.4.2]]) of the Levi-Civita connection ([[#^thm-c3-5-1|Theorem §C3.5.1]]) are
> 1. with the first index lowered, $\Gamma_{\rho\mu\nu} \equiv g_{\rho\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}$:
>
> $$
> \Gamma_{\rho\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho} + \partial_\nu g_{\mu\rho} - \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}\bigr) ;
> $$
>
> 2. with the inverse metric $g^{\lambda\rho}$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]]), the **Christoffel symbols**
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\,g^{\lambda\rho}\bigl(\partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho} + \partial_\nu g_{\mu\rho} - \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}\bigr) = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu} ,
> $$
>
> $\tfrac12n^2(n + 1)$ functions ($40$ on spacetime), built from $g_{\mu\nu}$ and its first derivatives only;
> 3. ⚑ **they vanish at a point exactly where the first derivatives of the metric do:** $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$ for all indices if and only if $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$ for all indices, because conversely $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma_{\nu\rho\mu} + \Gamma_{\mu\rho\nu}$. Coordinates with $\partial g(p) = 0$ exist around every point ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]]), so in suitable coordinates the Levi-Civita connection has $\Gamma(p) = 0$: the local inertial frames of the equivalence principle ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]]; normal coordinates, [[§C3.7 Normal Coordinates#^thm-c3-7-1|Theorem §C3.7.1]]).
>
> In particular the Christoffel symbols of any metric with constant components vanish, and multiplying the metric by a constant $c \neq 0$ leaves them unchanged ($g^{\lambda\rho} \to g^{\lambda\rho}/c$, $\partial g \to c\,\partial g$).
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a manifold with a metric; *applies:* every chart, every signature; exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.3.4, eqs. (4.75)–(4.76) (PDF p. 87, printed p. 86) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.10, eq. (6.72) (p. 103; derivative index last, symmetric) · Hartle §8.1, eq. (8.19) (p. 174) · Schutz §5.4, eq. (5.75) (p. 133; from $\nabla g = 0$ in Cartesian coordinates)*

^thm-c3-5-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Koszul on coordinate fields). Fix $p$ in a chart domain $U$, and a bump function $\psi$ at $p$ supported in $U$ ([[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-6|591 Lemma §56.6]]). Apply the Koszul formula of [[#^thm-c3-5-1|Theorem §C3.5.1]] to $X = \psi\partial_\mu$, $Y = \psi\partial_\nu$, $Z = \psi\partial_\rho$ and evaluate on the neighbourhood of $p$ where $\psi = 1$; there these are the coordinate fields, every term of $K$ involves only values and first derivatives at the point, and the brackets vanish, $[\partial_\mu, \partial_\nu] = 0$ ([[§58 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^prop-58-2|591 Prop. §58.2]]). With $g(\partial_\nu, \partial_\rho) = g_{\nu\rho}$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]]) and $\partial_\mu(g_{\nu\rho}) = \partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho}$:
>
> $$
> 2\,g\bigl(\nabla_{\partial_\mu}\partial_\nu, \partial_\rho\bigr) = \partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho} + \partial_\nu g_{\rho\mu} - \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} + 0 - 0 + 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (read off $\Gamma$). By [[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-4-2|Def. §C3.4.2]], $\nabla_{\partial_\mu}\partial_\nu = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}\partial_\lambda$, so the left side is $2\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}g_{\lambda\rho} = 2\Gamma_{\rho\mu\nu}$, using $g_{\lambda\rho} = g_{\rho\lambda}$. With $g_{\rho\mu} = g_{\mu\rho}$ this is part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (raise). Multiply part 1 by $g^{\sigma\rho}$ and sum over $\rho$; $g^{\sigma\rho}g_{\rho\lambda} = \delta^\sigma{}_\lambda$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]], part 1) gives $g^{\sigma\rho}\Gamma_{\rho\mu\nu} = \Gamma^\sigma{}_{\mu\nu}$. Renaming $\sigma \to \lambda$ gives part 2. The bracket in part 2 is unchanged under $\mu \leftrightarrow \nu$, so $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu}$, as torsion-freeness requires ([[#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]]). The independent symbols are $n$ choices of $\lambda$ times $\tfrac12n(n + 1)$ symmetric pairs.
>
> **Step 4** (the same by the cyclic trick, in components). Most textbooks derive part 1 without the Koszul formula. Metric compatibility in components ([[#^def-c3-5-2|Def. §C3.5.2]], part 2), with $\Gamma_{\nu\rho\mu} = g_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu}$, reads $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma_{\nu\rho\mu} + \Gamma_{\mu\rho\nu}$. Write it three times with the indices permuted:
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho} = \Gamma_{\rho\mu\nu} + \Gamma_{\nu\mu\rho}, \qquad \partial_\nu g_{\mu\rho} = \Gamma_{\rho\nu\mu} + \Gamma_{\mu\nu\rho}, \qquad \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma_{\nu\rho\mu} + \Gamma_{\mu\rho\nu} .
> $$
>
> Add the first two and subtract the third. Torsion-freeness, symmetry in the last two indices, gives $\Gamma_{\rho\nu\mu} = \Gamma_{\rho\mu\nu}$, $\Gamma_{\nu\mu\rho} = \Gamma_{\nu\rho\mu}$, $\Gamma_{\mu\nu\rho} = \Gamma_{\mu\rho\nu}$, so
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho} + \partial_\nu g_{\mu\rho} - \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = 2\Gamma_{\rho\mu\nu} + \bigl(\Gamma_{\nu\mu\rho} - \Gamma_{\nu\rho\mu}\bigr) + \bigl(\Gamma_{\mu\nu\rho} - \Gamma_{\mu\rho\nu}\bigr) = 2\Gamma_{\rho\mu\nu} ,
> $$
>
> part 1 again. This route shows uniqueness directly (the linear system of the count before the theorem is solved), and the Koszul route supplies existence.
>
> **Step 5** (part 3). If $\partial g(p) = 0$, part 2 gives $\Gamma(p) = 0$. Conversely the first equation of Step 4, $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma_{\nu\rho\mu} + \Gamma_{\mu\rho\nu}$, gives $\partial g(p) = 0$ when $\Gamma(p) = 0$. For constant $g_{\mu\nu}$, all $\partial g = 0$; under $g \to cg$ the factor $c$ of $\partial g$ cancels the factor $1/c$ of $g^{-1}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The Christoffel symbols are the Koszul formula on coordinate fields, where the brackets drop out. The first derivatives of the metric and the connection coefficients carry the same information at a point (part 3): making one vanish makes the other vanish. This is the reason the equivalence principle, which asks for $\Gamma(p) = 0$ in a freely falling frame, is a statement about first derivatives of $g$ only, while curvature (§C3.8) needs second derivatives. (Checked by script for a general two-dimensional metric and for the examples below.)

> [!remark] Remark: Why the connection of general relativity is torsion-free
> Mathematics allows any connection on spacetime; general relativity uses the Levi-Civita connection, and the reasons are physical.
> - *Metric compatibility* is what makes transported clocks and rulers keep their readings: the inner product of parallel-transported vectors is constant ([[§C3.6 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-6-1|Theorem §C3.6.1]]). A non-metric connection, like connection (a) of the opening, would let the length of a freely carried ruler drift.
> - *Torsion-freeness* is the equivalence principle at a point. In a freely falling frame the laws of special relativity hold at the event, so the connection coefficients vanish there ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]). A change of chart can remove only the symmetric part of $\Gamma$ at a point; the antisymmetric part is the torsion tensor and survives in every chart (derivation after [[#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]], Step 6). So local inertial frames with $\Gamma(p) = 0$ exist only if $T = 0$ ('t Hooft (5.6)). With $T = 0$ and $\nabla g = 0$, they exist at every point (part 3 of [[#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]]).
> - *Two notions of straight line agree.* With the Levi-Civita connection, the self-parallel curves (autoparallels) are the curves of stationary length or proper time ([[§C3.6 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-6-4|Theorem §C3.6.4]]). With connection (b) they are not: its self-parallel curves bend, while the stationary-length curves of the Euclidean metric are straight lines. Free fall, which C1 described by stationary proper time ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]), and free fall as "moving without turning" describe the same curves only for this connection (d'Inverno & Vickers §6.10, Fig. 6.11).
>
> ★ Theories that keep torsion as an independent field, such as the Einstein–Cartan theory, in which the spin density of matter sources torsion, are beyond these notes; no source used here treats them.
>

(5.6) (PDF p. 22, printed p. 21: "In this case there are no local inertial frames …") · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.10, Fig. 6.11 and text (p. 103: affine and metric geodesics coincide for the metric connection) · Couzens, GR1, §4.4.3, "The meaning of torsion" (PDF p. 95, printed p. 94: "Torsion will not play a role in this course")*

## Divergences and the volume form

Chapter C2 met a gap: the divergence $\partial_\mu V^\mu$ of a vector field is not a scalar, while the divergence of a vector *density* of weight $1$ is a scalar density and integrates to a boundary term ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]]). With a metric there are two candidate fixes. The covariant divergence $\nabla_\mu V^\mu$ is a scalar, the contraction of the $(1, 1)$ tensor $\nabla V$ ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]]). And $\sqrt{|g|}\,V^\mu$ is a vector density of weight $1$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], parts 3–4), whose ordinary divergence is a scalar density. The next two theorems show that these are the same object, through one identity for the contracted Christoffel symbol.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.3: The Contracted Christoffel Symbol
> Let $g \equiv \det(g_{\mu\nu})$ in a chart, nonzero by nondegeneracy; $g > 0$ for a Riemannian metric, $g < 0$ for a Lorentzian one, and $|g| = \pm g$ accordingly.
> 1. ⚑ **Jacobi's formula.** The derivative of the determinant is $\partial_\nu g = g\,g^{\mu\rho}\,\partial_\nu g_{\mu\rho}$.
> 2. **The contraction.** For the Levi-Civita connection, contracting the upper index with the derivative index,
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\,g^{\mu\rho}\,\partial_\nu g_{\mu\rho} = \frac{\partial_\nu g}{2g} = \partial_\nu\ln\sqrt{|g|} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\nu\sqrt{|g|} ,
> $$
>
> valid for either sign of $g$. Since $\Gamma$ is symmetric, $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\mu}$ is the same. (Couzens reaches part 2 through matrix logarithms, which need care when $g < 0$: [[#^cau-c3-5-1|Caution: Misprints and gaps in Couzens' §4.3.4]].)
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* every chart, any signature; part 1 for any smooth field of invertible symmetric matrices, part 2 for the Levi-Civita connection; exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.3.4, eqs. (4.77)–(4.79) (PDF p. 87, printed p. 86; via $\operatorname{tr}\log = \log\det$) · d'Inverno & Vickers §7.3, eqs. (7.7)–(7.10) (pp. 118–119; Jacobi's formula by cofactors, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) · Schutz §6.3, eqs. (6.38)–(6.40) (p. 151)*

^thm-c3-5-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (two terms cancel). Contract part 2 of [[#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]] over $\lambda = \mu$: $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\,g^{\mu\rho}(\partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho} + \partial_\nu g_{\mu\rho} - \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu})$. In the third term rename the dummy indices $\mu \leftrightarrow \rho$ and use $g^{\rho\mu} = g^{\mu\rho}$, $g_{\rho\nu} = g_{\nu\rho}$: $g^{\mu\rho}\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = g^{\rho\mu}\partial_\mu g_{\rho\nu} = g^{\mu\rho}\partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho}$, which is the first term. They cancel, leaving $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12g^{\mu\rho}\partial_\nu g_{\mu\rho}$.
>
> **Step 2** (the determinant near the identity). Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix and $\varepsilon$ a number. By the Leibniz formula ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-46|LADR Thm. 9.46]]), $\det(\mathbf 1 + \varepsilon A)$ is a sum over permutations of products of one entry from each column. The identity permutation contributes $\prod_{k=1}^n(1 + \varepsilon A_{kk}) = 1 + \varepsilon\sum_kA_{kk} + O(\varepsilon^2) = 1 + \varepsilon\operatorname{tr}A + O(\varepsilon^2)$. Every other permutation moves at least two indices, so its product contains at least two off-diagonal entries $\varepsilon A_{jk}$ and is $O(\varepsilon^2)$. Hence $\det(\mathbf 1 + \varepsilon A) = 1 + \varepsilon\operatorname{tr}A + O(\varepsilon^2)$.
>
> **Step 3** (Jacobi's formula). Fix the point and write $G = (g_{\mu\rho})$, $H = (\partial_\nu g_{\mu\rho})$ for fixed $\nu$. Since $\det$ is a polynomial in the entries, the chain rule ([[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]) gives $\partial_\nu\det G = \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\det(G + \varepsilon H)\big|_{\varepsilon = 0}$. By multiplicativity ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]) and Step 2 with $A = G^{-1}H$,
>
> $$
> \det(G + \varepsilon H) = \det G\,\det(\mathbf 1 + \varepsilon G^{-1}H) = \det G\,\bigl(1 + \varepsilon\operatorname{tr}(G^{-1}H) + O(\varepsilon^2)\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> so $\partial_\nu g = g\operatorname{tr}(G^{-1}H) = g\,g^{\mu\rho}\partial_\nu g_{\rho\mu} = g\,g^{\mu\rho}\partial_\nu g_{\mu\rho}$. This is part 1. No diagonalization and no logarithm of a matrix is needed.
>
> **Step 4** (logarithms). Insert part 1 into Step 1: $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\nu g/(2g)$. The function $g$ is nonzero, so on a connected chart domain it has one sign and $|g| = \sigma g$ with $\sigma = \pm1$ constant. Then $\partial_\nu|g| = \sigma\,\partial_\nu g$ and
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial_\nu g}{2g} = \frac{\sigma\,\partial_\nu g}{2\sigma g} = \frac{\partial_\nu|g|}{2|g|} = \tfrac12\,\partial_\nu\ln|g| = \partial_\nu\ln\sqrt{|g|} = \frac{\partial_\nu\sqrt{|g|}}{\sqrt{|g|}} ,
> $$
>
> the last step from $\partial_\nu\sqrt{|g|} = \partial_\nu|g|/(2\sqrt{|g|})$. This is part 2. (Checked by script for a general symmetric $3\times3$ matrix field and a general two-dimensional metric.)
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The contracted symbol needs only the determinant of the metric. The sign of $g$ never matters, because only the logarithmic derivative $\partial g/g$ enters; for spacetime one may write $\sqrt{-g}$ throughout.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.4: Divergence Formula and the Divergence Theorem
> Let $V$ be a vector field on $(M, g)$ and $\nabla$ the Levi-Civita connection.
> 1. **Divergence formula.** In every chart,
>
> $$
> \sqrt{|g|}\;\nabla_\mu V^\mu = \partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\,V^\mu\bigr), \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \nabla_\mu V^\mu = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\,V^\mu\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 2. **Divergence theorem.** Let $M$ be oriented ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]]), possibly with boundary, and let $V$ have compact support. Then, with the metric volume form $\sqrt{|g|}\,d^mx$ ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Hodge Star#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]) and $\mathfrak V^\mu = \sqrt{|g|}\,V^\mu$,
>
> $$
> \int_M\nabla_\mu V^\mu\,\sqrt{|g|}\,d^mx = \int_{\partial M}\omega_{\mathfrak V} ,
> $$
>
> with the flux form $\omega_{\mathfrak V}$ of [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]]. If $\partial M = \varnothing$, $\int_M\nabla_\mu V^\mu\,\sqrt{|g|}\,d^mx = 0$.
> 3. **The boundary term with a normal.** On a piece of $\partial M$ where the induced metric $\gamma$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]) is nondegenerate (the piece is not null), in boundary coordinates $y^1, \dots, y^{m-1}$ ordered by the induced orientation ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-5|Def. §C2.7.5]]),
>
> $$
> \int_{\partial M}\omega_{\mathfrak V} = \int\sqrt{|\gamma|}\;n_\mu V^\mu\;d^{m-1}y ,
> $$
>
> where $n$ is the **outward unit normal covector**: $n_\mu t^\mu = 0$ for every $t$ tangent to $\partial M$, $n_\mu N^\mu > 0$ for outward-pointing $N$ ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-4|Def. §C2.7.4]]), and $|g^{\mu\nu}n_\mu n_\nu| = 1$. If instead $N^\mu$ is the outward unit normal *vector* and $N_\mu = g_{\mu\nu}N^\nu$, then $n_\mu = \varepsilon N_\mu$ with $\varepsilon = g(N, N) = \pm1$, and the integrand is $\varepsilon\sqrt{|\gamma|}\,N_\mu V^\mu$: on a Lorentzian manifold $\varepsilon = -1$ on spacelike pieces of the boundary. Null pieces have no unit normal and are not covered. The sign is a common trap ([[#^cau-c3-5-2|Caution: Outward normal vector or outward normal covector]]; Couzens' version, [[#^cau-c3-5-1|Caution: Misprints and gaps in Couzens' §4.3.4]]).
>
> For a Riemannian metric $\varepsilon = 1$ and part 3 is the divergence theorem of vector calculus, $\int\nabla\cdot V\,dV = \oint V\cdot N\,dA$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|EM Theorem §B1.1.3]]); part 1 contains its curvilinear divergence formulas ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|EM Theorem §B1.1.5]]).
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* part 1 in every chart (for any connection with this $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu\nu}$, in particular Levi-Civita); parts 2–3 on an oriented manifold, $V$ of compact support, part 3 on non-null boundary pieces; exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.3.4, eqs. (4.80)–(4.81) (PDF p. 88, printed p. 87; "on a Lorentzian manifold this holds provided that $\partial M$ is either purely spacelike or purely timelike") · d'Inverno & Vickers §7.3, eq. (7.6); §7.4, eq. (7.24) (pp. 118, 121) · Schutz §6.3, eqs. (6.41)–(6.45) (pp. 151–152)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). By [[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]], $\nabla_\mu V^\mu = \partial_\mu V^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu\nu}V^\nu$. Insert [[#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]], part 2, multiply by $\sqrt{|g|}$ and use the product rule: $\sqrt{|g|}\,\nabla_\mu V^\mu = \sqrt{|g|}\,\partial_\mu V^\mu + V^\nu\,\partial_\nu\sqrt{|g|} = \partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}\,V^\mu)$, after renaming $\nu \to \mu$ in the second term.
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). In an oriented atlas $\sqrt{|g|}$ is a scalar density of weight $1$ and $\mathfrak V^\mu = \sqrt{|g|}V^\mu$ a vector density of weight $1$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], parts 3–4), with compact support since $V$ has. [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]], part 2, gives $\int_M\partial_\mu\mathfrak V^\mu\,d^mx = \int_{\partial M}\omega_{\mathfrak V}$, and by Step 1 $\partial_\mu\mathfrak V^\mu = \sqrt{|g|}\,\nabla_\mu V^\mu$.
>
> **Step 3** (the boundary integrand in a boundary chart). By a partition of unity ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-2|Def. §C2.7.2]]) it suffices to take $V$ supported in one positively oriented boundary chart $(x^1, \dots, x^m)$, $x^m \ge 0$, with $\partial M$ at $x^m = 0$ and outward direction $-\partial_m$. There [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]], part 2, gives, with $y = (x^1, \dots, x^{m-1})$, $\int_{\partial M}\omega_{\mathfrak V} = \int(-\mathfrak V^m(y, 0))\,d^{m-1}y = -\int\sqrt{|g|}\,V^m\,d^{m-1}y$.
>
> **Step 4** (a determinant identity). The induced metric on $x^m = 0$ has components $\gamma_{ij} = g_{ij}$, $i, j \le m - 1$ (the pull-back by $y \mapsto (y, 0)$, [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]). Claim: $g^{mm}\,\det g = \det\gamma$. Let $u^\mu = g^{\mu m}$, so $g_{\nu\mu}u^\mu = \delta^m{}_\nu$. The matrix $P$ with columns $e_1, \dots, e_{m-1}, u$ is upper triangular with diagonal $1, \dots, 1, u^m$, so $\det P = g^{mm}$ ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-48|LADR Thm. 9.48]]). The product $(g_{\mu\nu})P$ has columns $g_{\cdot1}, \dots, g_{\cdot(m-1)}, e_m$, and $\det\bigl((g_{\mu\nu})P\bigr) = \det g\,g^{mm}$ ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]). In the Leibniz formula ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-46|LADR Thm. 9.46]]) for a matrix whose last column is $e_m$, only permutations fixing $m$ contribute; they are the permutations of $\{1, \dots, m-1\}$, with the same signs, multiplying entries $g_{ij}$ with $i, j \le m - 1$. So the determinant is $\det\gamma$, and the claim follows. In particular $g^{mm} \neq 0$ exactly where $\gamma$ is nondegenerate.
>
> **Step 5** (the normal covector). The covector $-dx^m$ annihilates the tangent vectors $\partial_1, \dots, \partial_{m-1}$ of the boundary and is positive on the outward vector $-\partial_m$. Its squared norm is $g^{-1}(dx^m, dx^m) = g^{mm} \neq 0$ (Step 4), so $n = -dx^m/\sqrt{|g^{mm}|}$ is the outward unit normal covector, $n_\mu V^\mu = -V^m/\sqrt{|g^{mm}|}$. By Step 4, $\sqrt{|g|}\,\sqrt{|g^{mm}|} = \sqrt{|\gamma|}$, hence $-\sqrt{|g|}\,V^m = \sqrt{|g|}\sqrt{|g^{mm}|}\,\bigl(-V^m/\sqrt{|g^{mm}|}\bigr) = \sqrt{|\gamma|}\,n_\mu V^\mu$. With Step 3 this is part 3 in the covector form.
>
> **Step 6** (the vector form and $\varepsilon$). Let $N = \varepsilon\,n^\sharp$ with $\varepsilon = g^{\mu\nu}n_\mu n_\nu = \pm1$. Then $g(N, N) = \varepsilon^2g^{-1}(n, n) = \varepsilon$, $N$ is orthogonal to the boundary, and $n_\mu N^\mu = \varepsilon\,g^{-1}(n, n) = \varepsilon^2 = 1 > 0$: $N$ is the outward unit normal vector. Lowering, $N_\mu = \varepsilon n_\mu$, so $n_\mu = \varepsilon N_\mu$. On a Lorentzian manifold a spacelike boundary piece has a timelike normal, $\varepsilon = -1$. A 1+1-dimensional check: for $V = (x^2t,\ tx + t^3)$ on the square $0 \le t, x \le 1$ of Minkowski space, both sides of part 2 equal $5/6$ with the covector form, while lowering the outward vector without $\varepsilon$ gives $1/6$ (script).
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The covariant divergence is the ordinary divergence of the density $\sqrt{|g|}V$; the metric enters only through $\sqrt{|g|}$. The divergence theorem is Stokes' theorem ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]]) applied to that density. Writing the boundary term with a unit normal needs the boundary to be non-null, and on a Lorentzian manifold the sign depends on whether the normal is taken as a covector or a vector.

## Raising and lowering under ∇

Index gymnastics with $g_{\mu\nu}$ and $g^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]]) is used inside covariant derivatives constantly. Whether "lower, then differentiate" equals "differentiate, then lower" is a question about $\nabla g$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.5: Raising and Lowering Commute with ∇
> 1. For every affine connection, $\nabla_\rho\delta^\mu{}_\nu = 0$. For the Levi-Civita connection (more generally, any metric connection), also $\nabla_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = 0$ and $\nabla_\rho g^{\mu\nu} = 0$.
> 2. For a metric connection, lowering and raising commute with $\nabla$: for vector fields and one-forms
>
> $$
> \nabla_\rho\bigl(g_{\mu\nu}V^\nu\bigr) = g_{\mu\nu}\,\nabla_\rho V^\nu, \qquad \nabla_\rho\bigl(g^{\mu\nu}\omega_\nu\bigr) = g^{\mu\nu}\,\nabla_\rho\omega_\nu ,
> $$
>
> coordinate-free $\nabla_X(Y^\flat) = (\nabla_XY)^\flat$ and $\nabla_X(\omega^\sharp) = (\nabla_X\omega)^\sharp$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]); and for any tensor an index may be moved before or after differentiating, e.g. $\nabla_\rho T^\mu{}_\nu = g_{\nu\lambda}\nabla_\rho T^{\mu\lambda}$. So $\nabla_\rho V_\mu$ is unambiguous, and $\nabla^\rho \equiv g^{\rho\sigma}\nabla_\sigma$.
> 3. ⚑ **Without metric compatibility this fails.** For connection (a) of the opening ($\Gamma = 0$ in the polar chart) and $V = \partial_\theta$: $\nabla_rV^\theta = \partial_r1 = 0$, but $V_\theta = g_{\theta\theta}V^\theta = r^2$ and $\nabla_rV_\theta = \partial_r(r^2) = 2r \neq g_{\theta\theta}\nabla_rV^\theta = 0$.
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* parts 1–2 for any metric connection, in particular Levi-Civita; exact.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers §6.10, eqs. (6.74)–(6.76) (p. 104) · Couzens, GR1, §4.3.4 (used tacitly from (4.80) on) · Hartle §20.4, eqs. (20.64)–(20.66), (20.70) (Leibniz rule; $\nabla_\alpha v_\beta$ by lowering one index)*

^thm-c3-5-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** ($\nabla\delta = 0$). By the general formula of [[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]] for a $(1, 1)$ tensor, with $\partial_\rho\delta^\mu{}_\nu = 0$: $\nabla_\rho\delta^\mu{}_\nu = 0 + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\rho\lambda}\delta^\lambda{}_\nu - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\nu}\delta^\mu{}_\lambda = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\rho\nu} - \Gamma^\mu{}_{\rho\nu} = 0$.
>
> **Step 2** ($\nabla g^{-1} = 0$). $\nabla_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = 0$ is [[#^def-c3-5-2|Def. §C3.5.2]], part 2. Differentiate $g^{\mu\lambda}g_{\lambda\nu} = \delta^\mu{}_\nu$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]], part 1) with the Leibniz rule and commutation with contraction ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]]):
>
> $$
> (\nabla_\rho g^{\mu\lambda})\,g_{\lambda\nu} + g^{\mu\lambda}\,\nabla_\rho g_{\lambda\nu} = \nabla_\rho\delta^\mu{}_\nu = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (\nabla_\rho g^{\mu\lambda})\,g_{\lambda\nu} = 0 .
> $$
>
> Multiply by $g^{\nu\sigma}$ and sum: $\nabla_\rho g^{\mu\sigma} = 0$. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 3** (part 2). By the Leibniz rule and commutation with contraction, $\nabla_\rho(g_{\mu\nu}V^\nu) = (\nabla_\rho g_{\mu\nu})V^\nu + g_{\mu\nu}\nabla_\rho V^\nu = g_{\mu\nu}\nabla_\rho V^\nu$; the same with $g^{\mu\nu}$ and $\omega_\nu$, and for each index of a general tensor. Contracting with $X^\rho$ gives the coordinate-free forms.
>
> **Step 4** (part 3). With $\Gamma = 0$: $\nabla_rV^\theta = \partial_rV^\theta = 0$ and $\nabla_rV_\theta = \partial_r(g_{\theta\theta}V^\theta) = \partial_r(r^2) = 2r$. The difference is $(\nabla_rg_{\theta\theta})V^\theta = 2r$, the term that Step 3 drops for a metric connection. (Checked by script; part 2 also for a general two-dimensional metric.)

The metric now fixes the connection, and with it a derivative of every tensor field. One thing is still unexplained: what the connection *connects*. A derivative along a direction can be integrated along a curve, which carries vectors from one tangent space to another without a chart. That transport, and the curves that transport their own tangent, are [[§C3.6 Parallel Transport and Geodesics|§C3.6]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C3.5.1: Christoffel Symbols of Polar Coordinates Three Ways
> *Setup.* The Euclidean plane in the polar chart $(r, \theta)$, $r > 0$: $g_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(1, r^2)$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-1|Example §C3.1.1]]), $g^{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(1, 1/r^2)$, $\sqrt{|g|} = r$. The only nonzero derivative of the metric is $\partial_rg_{\theta\theta} = 2r$.
>
> *Way 1: from the derivatives of the basis vectors* ([[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^ex-c3-4-1|Example §C3.4.1]]; Schutz (5.45)). Take the flat connection, $\nabla\partial_x = \nabla\partial_y = 0$. With $\partial_r = \cos\theta\,\partial_x + \sin\theta\,\partial_y$ and $\partial_\theta = -r\sin\theta\,\partial_x + r\cos\theta\,\partial_y$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]]), only the coefficients are differentiated:
>
> $$
> \nabla_{\partial_r}\partial_r = 0, \qquad \nabla_{\partial_\theta}\partial_r = -\sin\theta\,\partial_x + \cos\theta\,\partial_y = \tfrac1r\partial_\theta = \nabla_{\partial_r}\partial_\theta, \qquad \nabla_{\partial_\theta}\partial_\theta = -r\cos\theta\,\partial_x - r\sin\theta\,\partial_y = -r\,\partial_r .
> $$
>
> So $\Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r} = \Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta} = 1/r$, $\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta} = -r$, and all others vanish. The flat connection has $\Gamma = 0$ in Cartesian coordinates, where $\partial g = 0$: it is torsion-free and metric there, hence everywhere (both are tensor conditions), so by [[#^thm-c3-5-1|Theorem §C3.5.1]] it *is* the Levi-Civita connection. Ways 2 and 3 must agree with Way 1.
>
> *Way 2: from the formula* ([[#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]]; Hartle Ex. 8.4). The six independent symbols:
>
> $$
> \Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac12g^{rr}\bigl(2\partial_\theta g_{\theta r} - \partial_rg_{\theta\theta}\bigr) = \tfrac12\cdot1\cdot(0 - 2r) = -r, \qquad \Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta} = \tfrac12g^{\theta\theta}\bigl(\partial_rg_{\theta\theta} + \partial_\theta g_{r\theta} - \partial_\theta g_{r\theta}\bigr) = \tfrac12\cdot\frac{1}{r^2}\cdot2r = \frac1r ,
> $$
>
> and $\Gamma^r{}_{rr} = \tfrac12g^{rr}\partial_rg_{rr} = 0$, $\Gamma^r{}_{r\theta} = \tfrac12g^{rr}(\partial_rg_{\theta r} + \partial_\theta g_{rr} - \partial_rg_{r\theta}) = 0$, $\Gamma^\theta{}_{rr} = \tfrac12g^{\theta\theta}(2\partial_rg_{r\theta} - \partial_\theta g_{rr}) = 0$, $\Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac12g^{\theta\theta}\partial_\theta g_{\theta\theta} = 0$.
>
> *Way 3: from the geodesic Lagrangian* (Hartle Ex. 8.3; the method is [[§C3.6 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-6-6|Theorem §C3.6.6]]). Take $L = \tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu = \tfrac12(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2)$, a dot being $d/ds$. The Euler–Lagrange equations ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|CM Theorem §B5.1.4]]) are
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{ds}\dot r - r\dot\theta^2 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 = 0, \qquad \frac{d}{ds}\bigl(r^2\dot\theta\bigr) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \ddot\theta + \frac2r\,\dot r\dot\theta = 0 .
> $$
>
> The geodesic equation $\ddot x^\lambda + \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu = 0$ ([[§C3.6 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]) has the $r$-component $\ddot r + \Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}\dot\theta^2 + \dots$, so $\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta} = -r$; its $\theta$-component contains the two equal terms $\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}\dot r\dot\theta + \Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r}\dot\theta\dot r = 2\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}\dot r\dot\theta$, so $2\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta} = 2/r$. The factor $2$ in front of a mixed term is shared by the two index orders, a common slip when reading off.
>
> *Checks.* $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu r} = \Gamma^r{}_{rr} + \Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r} = 1/r = \partial_r\ln r$ and $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu\theta} = 0 = \partial_\theta\ln r$ ([[#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]]), so $\nabla_\mu V^\mu = \frac1r\partial_r(rV^r) + \partial_\theta V^\theta$ ([[#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]]), the formula of [[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^ex-c3-4-2|Example §C3.4.2]]. Metric compatibility, against connection (a) of the opening: $\nabla_rg_{\theta\theta} = \partial_r(r^2) - 2\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}g_{\theta\theta} = 2r - 2\cdot\frac1r\cdot r^2 = 0$.
>
> *Source: Schutz §5.3, eq. (5.45) (p. 127) · Hartle §8.1, Examples 8.3–8.4, eqs. (8.17), (8.20) (pp. 173–174; there with $\phi$ for the angle) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.5.2: Christoffel Symbols of the Sphere
> *Setup.* The sphere of radius $a$ in the chart $(\theta, \varphi)$, $0 < \theta < \pi$: $g_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(a^2, a^2\sin^2\theta)$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]), $g^{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(1/a^2, 1/(a^2\sin^2\theta)\bigr)$, $\sqrt{|g|} = a^2\sin\theta$. The only nonzero derivative of the metric is $\partial_\theta g_{\varphi\varphi} = 2a^2\sin\theta\cos\theta$.
>
> *The symbols* ([[#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]]). The two that contain $\partial_\theta g_{\varphi\varphi}$:
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\theta{}_{\varphi\varphi} = \tfrac12g^{\theta\theta}\bigl(-\partial_\theta g_{\varphi\varphi}\bigr) = \tfrac12\cdot\frac{1}{a^2}\cdot(-2a^2\sin\theta\cos\theta) = -\sin\theta\cos\theta, \qquad \Gamma^\varphi{}_{\theta\varphi} = \Gamma^\varphi{}_{\varphi\theta} = \tfrac12g^{\varphi\varphi}\,\partial_\theta g_{\varphi\varphi} = \frac{2a^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{2a^2\sin^2\theta} = \cot\theta .
> $$
>
> The other four contain only $\partial_\theta g_{\theta\theta}$, $\partial_\varphi g_{\mu\nu}$ or $g_{\theta\varphi}$, all zero: $\Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta\theta} = \Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta\varphi} = \Gamma^\varphi{}_{\theta\theta} = \Gamma^\varphi{}_{\varphi\varphi} = 0$. The radius has dropped out, as part 3's corollary on constant multiples of the metric says.
>
> *Checks.* $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu\theta} = 0 + \cot\theta = \partial_\theta\ln(a^2\sin\theta)$ and $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu\varphi} = 0$ ([[#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]]), so $\nabla_\mu V^\mu = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,V^\theta) + \partial_\varphi V^\varphi$, the angular part of the divergence in spherical coordinates ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|EM Theorem §B1.1.5]], in coordinate rather than unit-vector components). One component of $\nabla g = 0$: $\nabla_\varphi g_{\theta\varphi} = 0 - \Gamma^\varphi{}_{\varphi\theta}g_{\varphi\varphi} - \Gamma^\theta{}_{\varphi\varphi}g_{\theta\theta} = -\cot\theta\cdot a^2\sin^2\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta\cdot a^2 = 0$.
>
> *What it previews.* $\Gamma^\theta{}_{\varphi\varphi} = 0$ only at $\theta = \pi/2$: moving along a latitude circle $\theta = \theta_0 \neq \pi/2$ produces an acceleration in $\theta$, so the equator is a geodesic and the other latitude circles are not ([[§C3.6 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^ex-c3-6-2|Example §C3.6.2]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.4, eq. (4.115) (PDF p. 97) · Hartle, Problem 8.2 (p. 183) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.5.3: Christoffel Symbols of the Weak-Field Metric
> *Setup.* The weak-field metric of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]], written $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}$ with $h_{00} = -2\Phi$, $h_{ij} = -2\Phi\,\delta_{ij}$, $h_{0i} = 0$, to first order in $\Phi$. Schutz keeps a time-dependent $\Phi(t, \vec x)$ to see which terms a static field removes; so do we. (One intermediate sign in Schutz's computation is misprinted: [[#^cau-c3-5-3|Caution: A sign in Schutz's (7.22)]].)
>
> *Step 1* (only $\eta^{-1}$ is needed). Every $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\rho h_{\mu\nu}$ is $O(\Phi)$, so in [[#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]] the inverse metric enters at zeroth order, $g^{\lambda\rho} = \eta^{\lambda\rho} + O(\Phi)$:
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\,\eta^{\lambda\rho}\bigl(\partial_\mu h_{\nu\rho} + \partial_\nu h_{\mu\rho} - \partial_\rho h_{\mu\nu}\bigr) + O(\Phi^2) .
> $$
>
> *Step 2* (the six kinds of symbol), with $\eta^{00} = -1$, $\eta^{ij} = \delta^{ij}$, $\partial_\rho h_{00} = -2\partial_\rho\Phi$, $\partial_\rho h_{ij} = -2\partial_\rho\Phi\,\delta_{ij}$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \Gamma^0{}_{00} &= \tfrac12(-1)\,\partial_0h_{00} = \partial_t\Phi, & \Gamma^i{}_{00} &= \tfrac12\bigl(-\partial_ih_{00}\bigr) = \partial_i\Phi, \\
> \Gamma^0{}_{0i} &= \tfrac12(-1)\,\partial_ih_{00} = \partial_i\Phi, & \Gamma^i{}_{0j} &= \tfrac12\,\partial_0h_{ji} = -\partial_t\Phi\,\delta_{ij}, \\
> \Gamma^0{}_{ij} &= \tfrac12(-1)\bigl(-\partial_0h_{ij}\bigr) = -\partial_t\Phi\,\delta_{ij}, & \Gamma^i{}_{jk} &= \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_jh_{ki} + \partial_kh_{ji} - \partial_ih_{jk}\bigr) = \delta_{jk}\partial_i\Phi - \delta_{ik}\partial_j\Phi - \delta_{ij}\partial_k\Phi ,
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> each $+\,O(\Phi^2)$, all $64$ confirmed by script. For a static field the time derivatives vanish, leaving $\Gamma^0{}_{0i} = \Gamma^i{}_{00} = \partial_i\Phi$ and $\Gamma^i{}_{jk}$.
>
> *Step 3* (check against [[#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]]). Summing, $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu0} = \partial_t\Phi - 3\partial_t\Phi = -2\partial_t\Phi$ and $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu j} = \partial_j\Phi + (\partial_j\Phi - 3\partial_j\Phi - \partial_j\Phi) = -2\partial_j\Phi$. On the other side $\sqrt{|g|} = \sqrt{(1 + 2\Phi)(1 - 2\Phi)^3} = 1 - 2\Phi + O(\Phi^2)$, so $\partial_\nu\ln\sqrt{|g|} = -2\partial_\nu\Phi + O(\Phi^2)$. They agree.
>
> *Where Newton's force sits.* For a slow body, $dx^i/d\tau$ is small against $dt/d\tau \approx 1$, and the geodesic equation ([[§C3.6 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]) keeps one term: $d^2x^i/d\tau^2 \approx -\Gamma^i{}_{00} = -\partial_i\Phi$, Newton's law, as derived from proper time in [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]] and from the geodesic equation in [[§C3.6 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^ex-c3-6-3|Example §C3.6.3]]. The gravitational "force" is the symbol $\Gamma^i{}_{00}$, which comes from $g_{00}$ alone. Restored ($x^0 = ct$, $\Phi \to \Phi/c^2$): $\Gamma^i{}_{00} = \partial_i\Phi/c^2$, and $d^2x^i/dt^2 \approx -c^2\Gamma^i{}_{00} = -\partial_i\Phi$.
>
> *Source: Schutz §7.2, eqs. (7.8), (7.13)–(7.24) (pp. 174–176; $\Gamma^0{}_{00}$ and $\Gamma^i{}_{00}$ only) · all symbols checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.5.4: The Parallelogram That Does Not Close
> *Setup.* A connection with coefficients $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}$, a point $p$ with coordinates $x^\mu$, two vectors $X$, $Y$ at $p$ and a small number $\varepsilon$. To carry a vector $w$ a small step $\varepsilon u$ without changing it ([[§C3.6 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-6-1|Def. §C3.6.1]]) means, to first order, that its covariant derivative along $u$ vanishes, $u^\nu(\partial_\nu w^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}w^\rho) = 0$, so its components change by $-\varepsilon\,\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}u^\nu w^\rho$.
>
> *Two routes.* Step by $\varepsilon X$, carry $Y$ along, and step by the carried vector; or the other way round:
>
> $$
> t^\mu = x^\mu + \varepsilon X^\mu + \varepsilon\bigl(Y^\mu - \varepsilon\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}X^\nu Y^\rho\bigr), \qquad q^\mu = x^\mu + \varepsilon Y^\mu + \varepsilon\bigl(X^\mu - \varepsilon\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}Y^\nu X^\rho\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Renaming $\nu \leftrightarrow \rho$ in the last term of $q$,
>
> $$
> q^\mu - t^\mu = \varepsilon^2\bigl(\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho} - \Gamma^\mu{}_{\rho\nu}\bigr)X^\nu Y^\rho = \varepsilon^2\,T^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}X^\nu Y^\rho + O(\varepsilon^3) .
> $$
>
> The gap of the parallelogram at order $\varepsilon^2$ is the torsion ([[#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]]); for the Levi-Civita connection it closes to this order.
>
> *The rotating connection.* At the origin with $X = \partial_x$, $Y = \partial_y$: $\Gamma^\mu{}_{xy} = \kappa J^\mu{}_y = (-\kappa, 0)$ and $\Gamma^\mu{}_{yx} = 0$, so $t = (\varepsilon + \kappa\varepsilon^2, \varepsilon)$ and $q = (\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$, a gap $q - t = (-\kappa\varepsilon^2, 0) = \varepsilon^2(T^x{}_{xy}, T^y{}_{xy})$. Carried along $x$ by $\varepsilon$, the vector $\partial_y$ turns clockwise by the angle $\kappa\varepsilon$ (figure, top of panel b) and the second side leans to the right by $\kappa\varepsilon^2$; carried along $y$, $\partial_x$ does not turn.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.3, "The meaning of torsion", eqs. (4.109)–(4.112), Figure 13 (PDF pp. 95–96, printed pp. 94–95) · checked by script*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - **From C2.** Torsion-freeness reads $\nabla_XY - \nabla_YX = [X, Y] = \mathcal L_XY$ ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]]): the antisymmetrized covariant derivative is the Lie derivative. The divergence theorem is Stokes' theorem ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]]) for the density $\sqrt{|g|}V$, completing [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]].
> - **From C1.** The weak-field symbol $\Gamma^i{}_{00} = \partial_i\Phi$ ([[#^ex-c3-5-3|Example §C3.5.3]]) is the Newtonian field in the language of connections; the extremal-proper-time argument of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]] produced it without naming it. Part 3 of [[#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]] is the reason local inertial frames ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]) can exist.
> - **Forward.** Metric compatibility makes parallel transport an isometry ([[§C3.6 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-6-1|Theorem §C3.6.1]]) and gives the antisymmetry of $R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}$ in its first pair ([[§C3.9 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor; Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^thm-c3-9-1|Theorem §C3.9.1]]); torsion-freeness gives the first Bianchi identity ([[§C3.9 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor; Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]]) and removes the torsion term of the Ricci identity ([[§C3.8 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-8-3|Theorem §C3.8.3]]). The divergence formula gives the Laplacian $\Box f = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu f)$ of [[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Hodge Star#^def-c3-3-4|Def. §C3.3.4]] and Killing's equation in terms of $\nabla$ ([[§C3.11 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-11-1|Theorem §C3.11.1]]); the divergence theorem drops boundary terms in the Einstein–Hilbert variation (§C5.2, planned) and conserves charges $\int\sqrt{|g|}J^0d^3x$ of covariantly conserved currents.
> - **Level B and EM.** In inertial coordinates of Minkowski space $\Gamma = 0$ and $\nabla_\mu = \partial_\mu$, which is why level B never needed a connection (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). The curvilinear divergence formulas of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|EM Theorem §B1.1.5]] are [[#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]], part 1, in unit-vector components.
> - **Mathematics.** Nondegeneracy of $g_p$ ([[§35 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-9|LADR Def. 9.9]]) is the only property of the metric the fundamental theorem uses. Jacobi's formula ([[#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]], part 1) rests on the Leibniz formula and multiplicativity of determinants ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-46|LADR Thm. 9.46]], [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]). MATH 591 has not reached metrics or connections.
> - **Conventions.** Index order of $\Gamma$ and the sign of the torsion in each source: [[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative#^cau-c3-4-1|§C3.4, Caution: Index order of Γ in the sources]] and [[General Relativity Conventions]].
