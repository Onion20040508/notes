[[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor|§C3.1]] gave the manifold a metric, and with it the lengths of vectors and of curves. Two jobs from chapter C2 are still open. The first is volume. A bare manifold integrates top forms, not functions: to integrate a function one has to choose a nowhere-vanishing top form to multiply it with, and nothing selects one ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^rem-c2-7-1|§C2.7, Remark: No canonical volume without a metric]]). Without that choice an action $\int\mathcal L\,d^4x$ cannot be written in general coordinates. The second is duality. On $\mathbf R^3$ the exterior derivative became gradient, curl and divergence only after one-forms and two-forms were both turned into vectors, a step that used the Euclidean metric and the orientation and was left unnamed ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-1|Example §C2.6.1]], "What needs more structure"). The question of this section is: *what does a metric give for areas, volumes and integrals of functions, and how does it turn $p$-forms into $(n - p)$-forms in every chart?*

Two naive rules fail, and their failures show what is needed. **Area.** Take the unit sphere and integrate the function $1$ against the coordinates. In the chart $(\theta, \varphi) \in (0, \pi)\times(0, 2\pi)$,

$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi 1\;d\theta\,d\varphi = 2\pi^2 \approx 19.74 ,
$$

and in the stereographic chart $(u, v)$, which covers the sphere minus one point with $u, v \in \mathbf R$, $\int\!\!\int du\,dv$ diverges. Neither is the area $4\pi \approx 12.57$, and they disagree with each other: a function is not a density ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 2). The metric supplies a density. Its determinant is $\det g = \sin^2\theta$ in the first chart ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]] with $a = 1$), and in the stereographic chart the induced metric is $4(du^2 + dv^2)/(1 + u^2 + v^2)^2$, so $\det g = 16/(1 + u^2 + v^2)^4$. Integrating $\det g$ itself gives $\int\!\!\int\sin^2\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi = \pi^2$, still wrong. Its square root works in both charts (with $\rho^2 = u^2 + v^2$):

$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\;d\theta\,d\varphi = 2\pi\cdot2 = 4\pi, \qquad \int\!\!\int\frac{4\;du\,dv}{(1 + u^2 + v^2)^2} = 2\pi\int_0^\infty\frac{4\rho\;d\rho}{(1 + \rho^2)^2} = 2\pi\Bigl[-\frac{2}{1 + \rho^2}\Bigr]_0^\infty = 4\pi .
$$

**Duality.** On the plane, turning a one-form through a right angle should send $dx$ to $dy$. The naive rule uses the Levi-Civita symbol alone, $J(\omega)_\mu = \omega_\nu\tilde\epsilon_{\nu\mu}$ with $\tilde\epsilon_{xy} = 1$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-7|Def. §C2.3.7]]). In Cartesian coordinates $J(dx)_x = \omega_y\tilde\epsilon_{yx} = 0$ and $J(dx)_y = \omega_x\tilde\epsilon_{xy} = 1$: $J(dx) = dy$. In polar coordinates the same one-form has the components $(dx)_r = \cos\theta$, $(dx)_\theta = -r\sin\theta$, and $dy$ has $(\sin\theta, r\cos\theta)$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]]). The rule gives

$$
J(dx)_r = \omega_\theta\,\tilde\epsilon_{\theta r} = r\sin\theta, \qquad J(dx)_\theta = \omega_r\,\tilde\epsilon_{r\theta} = \cos\theta \qquad\ne\qquad (\sin\theta,\ r\cos\theta) .
$$

The rule is not a tensor operation: it adds two lower indices, and the symbol is a density, not a tensor ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]]). Raising the index of $\omega$ with $g^{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(1, r^{-2})$ and multiplying the symbol by $\sqrt{\det g} = r$ repairs both defects: $r\,(g^{\theta\theta}\omega_\theta)\,\tilde\epsilon_{\theta r} = r\cdot r^{-2}\cdot(-r\sin\theta)\cdot(-1) = \sin\theta$ and $r\,(g^{rr}\omega_r)\,\tilde\epsilon_{r\theta} = r\cos\theta$, the components of $dy$.

Both repairs use the same two ingredients, $\sqrt{|\det g|}$ and the inverse metric. The section builds them in order. The determinant of the metric is a density of weight $2$ whose sign is fixed by the signature ([[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]); its square root makes the volume form ([[#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]), the same in every positively oriented chart and equal to $1$ on orthonormal bases ([[#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]]). Read as a tensor it is the Levi-Civita tensor ([[#^def-c3-3-2|Def. §C3.3.2]]), whose raised form and contractions are computed in [[#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]]; its overall sign on spacetime differs from level B's, which [[#^cau-c3-3-1|Caution: The sign of ε on spacetime, and level B]] reconciles. Contracting it with a raised form gives the Hodge star ([[#^def-c3-3-3|Def. §C3.3.3]]), with $\star\star = \pm1$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]). The star gives an inner product of forms ([[#^def-c3-3-4|Def. §C3.3.4]], [[#^thm-c3-3-5|Theorem §C3.3.5]]), the adjoint of $d$ ([[#^def-c3-3-5|Def. §C3.3.5]], [[#^thm-c3-3-6|Theorem §C3.3.6]]) and the Laplacian ([[#^def-c3-3-6|Def. §C3.3.6]], [[#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]]). The examples compute volumes, recover vector calculus, write Maxwell's equations on any spacetime and derive the spherical Laplacian.

**Setup.** A smooth manifold $M$ of dimension $n$ with a metric $g$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]) of signature $(q, n - q)$, $q$ negative signs ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]); Riemannian means $q = 0$, Lorentzian $q = 1$, and spacetime is the Lorentzian case $n = 4$. $M$ is oriented ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]]), and "chart" means a positively oriented chart unless said otherwise; on spacetime the inertial coordinates $(t, x, y, z)$ of Minkowski spacetime are positively oriented. Forms, $\wedge$ and $d$ are those of [[§C2.6 Differential Forms|§C2.6]], in the determinant convention ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^cau-c2-6-1|§C2.6, Caution: Normalizations of the wedge product]]). Integrals of forms are those of [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem|§C2.7]]; integrals over $M$ are taken over compact supports or where they converge. Every statement is an exact theorem of geometry, in any dimension and signature; no physical model enters except in [[#^ex-c3-3-3|Example §C3.3.3]]. Every sign and number in this section was checked by script, for $n = 2, 3, 4$ and both signatures.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$, $\mu_0$ kept in Maxwell's equations as in [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^ex-c1-2-4|Example §C1.2.4]]. $g \equiv \det(g_{\mu\nu})$ in a chart and $s \equiv \operatorname{sign} g = (-1)^q$; $\sqrt{|g|}$ is $\sqrt g$ for Riemannian and $\sqrt{-g}$ for Lorentzian metrics. $\tilde\epsilon$ is the Levi-Civita symbol ($\tilde\epsilon_{1\cdots n} = \tilde\epsilon^{1\cdots n} = +1$ in every chart, [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-7|Def. §C2.3.7]]); $\epsilon$ without tilde is the Levi-Civita tensor, $\epsilon_{0123} = +\sqrt{-g}$, $\epsilon^{0123} = -1/\sqrt{-g}$ on spacetime. The Hodge star contracts the form with the **first** indices of $\epsilon$ ([[#^cau-c3-3-2|Caution: Index placement in the Hodge star]]). $\Delta = dd^\dagger + d^\dagger d$ is the Laplace–de Rham operator, and $\Box = g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu$ ($= -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2$ in inertial coordinates) the d'Alembertian; on functions $\Delta = -\Box$ ([[#^cau-c3-3-3|Caution: Laplacian, d'Alembertian and their signs]]).

## The volume form

The opening found that $\sqrt{\det g}$, not $\det g$, turns coordinate area into area. The reason is the transformation law of the determinant, and its sign is what needs a modulus on a Lorentzian manifold.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.1: det g Is a Density of Weight 2; Its Sign Is Fixed by the Signature
> Let $g$ be a metric on $M$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]) and $g = \det(g_{\mu\nu})$ its determinant in a chart $x^\mu$.
> 1. **Weight 2.** In a second chart $x'^\mu$, with $J = \det(\partial x^\mu/\partial x'^\nu)$ at the same point,
>
> $$
> g' = J^2\,g :
> $$
>
> $g$ is a scalar density of weight $+2$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-6|Def. §C2.3.6]]), the case $T = g$ of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 2 (and of [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 3).
> 2. **Sign.** $g \ne 0$ at every point, and its sign is $s = (-1)^q$, with $q$ the number of negative signs of the signature ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]): the same in every chart and, on a connected manifold, at every point. For a Riemannian metric $g > 0$; for a Lorentzian metric $g < 0$.
> 3. **Its root.** $\sqrt{|g|}$ is a smooth positive function in each chart and a scalar density of weight $+1$ under changes of chart with $J > 0$: $\sqrt{|g'|} = J\sqrt{|g|}$. Under a change with $J < 0$, $\sqrt{|g'|} = |J|\sqrt{|g|} = -J\sqrt{|g|}$.
>
> *Examples.* The plane in polar coordinates: $g = r^2 = J^2\cdot1$ with $J = \det\partial(x, y)/\partial(r, \theta) = r$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^ex-c2-3-2|Example §C2.3.2]]). Minkowski spacetime in spherical coordinates: $g = -r^4\sin^2\theta = J^2\cdot(-1)$ with $J = \det\partial(t, x, y, z)/\partial(t, r, \theta, \varphi) = r^2\sin\theta$, and $\sqrt{-g} = r^2\sin\theta$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-3|Example §C3.1.3]]).
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a manifold with a metric; *applies:* every chart, every point; part 3 with the sign of $J$ as stated.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.26)–(4.27) (p. 78) · d'Inverno & Vickers §7.3, eq. (7.5) and the text after it (pp. 117–118: "$g$ is a scalar density of weight $+2$", "$\sqrt{-g}$ is a scalar density of weight $+1$") · signs checked by script*

^thm-c3-3-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). The components of $g$ transform as those of a $(0, 2)$ tensor ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 2), $g'_{\mu\nu} = P^\alpha{}_\mu P^\beta{}_\nu\,g_{\alpha\beta}$ with $P^\alpha{}_\mu = \partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\mu$, that is $g' = P^{\mathsf T}gP$ as matrices. The determinant is multiplicative ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]) and $\det P^{\mathsf T} = \det P = J$ ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-56|LADR Thm. 9.56]]):
>
> $$
> \det g' = \det P^{\mathsf T}\,\det g\,\det P = J^2\,\det g .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). At a point $p$ there is a basis $e_a = E^\mu{}_a\,\partial_\mu$ of $T_pM$ with $g(e_a, e_b) = \eta_{ab} = \operatorname{diag}(-1, \dots, -1, +1, \dots, +1)$, $q$ entries $-1$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 4). In matrices $\eta = E^{\mathsf T}gE$, where $E = (E^\mu{}_a)$ is invertible because the $e_a$ form a basis. Taking determinants as in Step 1,
>
> $$
> (-1)^q = \det\eta = (\det E)^2\,g \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad g = \frac{(-1)^q}{(\det E)^2} ,
> $$
>
> so $g \ne 0$ and $\operatorname{sign} g = (-1)^q$. The number $q$ does not depend on the chart or the basis (Sylvester's law, same part 4), and it is the same at every point of a connected manifold ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]).
>
> **Step 3** (part 3). By part 2, $|g| = s\,g$ with the constant $s$, so $|g|$ is a smooth function, nowhere zero, and $\sqrt{|g|}$ is smooth and positive. By part 1, $|g'| = J^2|g|$, so $\sqrt{|g'|} = |J|\sqrt{|g|}$, which is $J\sqrt{|g|}$ when $J > 0$ and $-J\sqrt{|g|}$ when $J < 0$.
>
> **Step 4** (the examples). For polar coordinates the Jacobian matrix $\partial(x, y)/\partial(r, \theta)$ has the rows $(\cos\theta, -r\sin\theta)$ and $(\sin\theta, r\cos\theta)$, determinant $r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r$. For spherical coordinates in space the determinant of $\partial(x, y, z)/\partial(r, \theta, \varphi)$ is $r^2\sin\theta$ (the volume element of [[§22 Properties of the Integral#^thm-22-6|452 Thm. §22.6]]), and $t$ is unchanged, so $J = r^2\sin\theta$ and $J^2\cdot\det\eta = -r^4\sin^2\theta$, which is $\det\operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, r^2, r^2\sin^2\theta)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The sign of $\det g$ is a property of the metric, not of the chart, which is what makes the single rule "take $\sqrt{|g|}$" possible; on a Lorentzian manifold it means $\sqrt{-g}$. Only the *root* has weight $1$, the power that the change-of-variables formula needs.

*Uses:* [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]], [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]], [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-56|LADR Thm. 9.56]], [[§22 Properties of the Integral#^thm-22-6|452 Thm. §22.6]]

A density of weight $1$ is what a top form's coefficient is ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 2). So $\sqrt{|g|}$ can be the coefficient of a top form that is the same in every chart: the canonical choice that §C2.7 could not make.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.3.1: Metric Volume Form
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be an oriented manifold with a metric. The **volume form** of $g$ is the $n$-form $\mathrm{vol} \in \Omega^n(M)$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]]) given in every positively oriented chart by
>
> $$
> \mathrm{vol} = \sqrt{|g|}\;dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n ; \qquad\text{on spacetime}\qquad \mathrm{vol} = \sqrt{-g}\;dx^0\wedge dx^1\wedge dx^2\wedge dx^3 .
> $$
>
> That this is one form, whichever positively oriented chart is used, is [[#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]].
>
> *Components.* An $n$-form has one independent component, and the expansion $\omega = \sum_{\mu_1 < \dots < \mu_n}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{\mu_n}$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]]) has the single term $\mu_k = k$. So
>
> $$
> \mathrm{vol}_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \sqrt{|g|}\;\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}, \qquad \mathrm{vol} = \frac{1}{n!}\sqrt{|g|}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}\,dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{\mu_n} ,
> $$
>
> the totally antisymmetric tensor $v_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}$ of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 4.
>
> *Integrals of functions.* With $\mathrm{vol}$ as the nowhere-vanishing top form of [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-2|Def. §C2.7.2]], part 3, a function $f$ of compact support has the integral
>
> $$
> \int_Mf\,\mathrm{vol} = \int f\,\sqrt{|g|}\;d^nx \qquad(\text{support in one chart}) ,
> $$
>
> and the **volume** of a region $U$ is $\mathrm{Vol}(U) = \int_U\mathrm{vol}$: length for $n = 1$, area for $n = 2$, the four-volume of a region of spacetime for $n = 4$. Physics writes $\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x$ for $\mathrm{vol}$ on spacetime: an action is $S = \int\mathcal L\,\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x$ with a scalar $\mathcal L$.
>
> *Names.* Couzens writes $\mathrm{vol}(M)$ (GR1 (4.22)–(4.23)) and, in GR2, $d\mathrm{vol}$; Hartle writes $dv = \sqrt{-g}\,d^4x$ for four-volume and $dA$, $dV$ for area and three-volume of a diagonal metric.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.22)–(4.23), (4.29) (pp. 77–78) · Couzens, GR2, App. A, eqs. (A.28)–(A.29) (p. 182) · Hartle §7.6, eqs. (7.28)–(7.30) (pp. 146–147) · d'Inverno & Vickers §7.3 (p. 118: "$\sqrt{-g}$ plays an important role in integration")*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.2: The Volume Form Is Chart-Independent
> Let $(M, g)$ be an oriented manifold with a metric.
> 1. **Chart independence.** The formula of [[#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]] gives the same $n$-form in any two positively oriented charts.
> 2. **Value on orthonormal bases.** If $e_1, \dots, e_n$ is an orthonormal basis of $T_pM$, $g(e_a, e_b) = \eta_{ab}$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]), that is positively oriented (in the class that the orientation selects at $p$, [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]], part 3), then
>
> $$
> \mathrm{vol}(e_1, \dots, e_n) = 1 ,
> $$
>
> and $\mathrm{vol}_p$ is the only $n$-form at $p$ with this property. A unit cube spanned by orthonormal vectors has volume $1$, which is the reason $\mathrm{vol}$ deserves its name.
> 3. **Orientation.** In a negatively oriented chart the same formula gives $-\mathrm{vol}$. Reversing the orientation of $M$ replaces $\mathrm{vol}$ by $-\mathrm{vol}$, and the integral $\int_Mf\,\mathrm{vol}$ of a function does not change: volumes do not depend on the orientation chosen.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of an oriented manifold with a metric; *applies:* any signature, any dimension, every point.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.24)–(4.28) ("which is therefore invariant", pp. 77–78) · d'Inverno & Vickers §7.3 (pp. 117–118) · part 2 written here*

^thm-c3-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1, components). The components of $\mathrm{vol}$ are $\sqrt{|g|}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}$ ([[#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]). The symbol is a density of weight $-1$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 1) and $\sqrt{|g|}$ one of weight $+1$ for $J > 0$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]], part 3). Their product has weight $0$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 3), so the arrays in two positively oriented charts are related by the tensor law: they are the components of one $(0, n)$ tensor, the same $n$-form.
>
> **Step 2** (part 1, coefficient). The same in terms of the coefficient: for charts $x$ and $y$ with $J = \det(\partial x^\mu/\partial y^\nu) > 0$, $dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n = J\,dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^n$ ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 1), and $\sqrt{|g_x|} = \sqrt{|g_y|}/J$ (Theorem §C3.3.1, part 3, with the roles of the charts as there: $g_y = J^2g_x$). Hence
>
> $$
> \sqrt{|g_x|}\;dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n = \frac{\sqrt{|g_y|}}{J}\cdot J\;dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^n = \sqrt{|g_y|}\;dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^n .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (part 2, the value). Write $e_a = E^\mu{}_a\partial_\mu$ in a positively oriented chart. Evaluating the $n$-form on the $n$ vectors uses its components ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 1) and the Leibniz formula for the determinant ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-7|Def. §C2.3.7]]):
>
> $$
> \mathrm{vol}(e_1, \dots, e_n) = \sqrt{|g|}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}E^{\mu_1}{}_1\cdots E^{\mu_n}{}_n = \sqrt{|g|}\,\det E .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (part 2, $\det E$). Orthonormality is $E^{\mathsf T}gE = \eta$ in matrices, so $(\det E)^2\,g = \det\eta = (-1)^q$ (Step 2 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]) and $(\det E)^2 = (-1)^q/g = 1/|g|$, because $g$ has the sign $(-1)^q$. So $|\det E| = 1/\sqrt{|g|}$. The basis $(e_a)$ is in the class of the coordinate basis $(\partial_\mu)$ of a positively oriented chart exactly when $\det E > 0$ ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]]). Then $\det E = 1/\sqrt{|g|}$ and Step 3 gives $\mathrm{vol}(e_1, \dots, e_n) = 1$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 2, uniqueness). The $n$-forms at $p$ form a space of dimension $\binom nn = 1$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]], counting), and a nonzero $n$-form is nonzero on every basis (Step 3: its value is a nonzero multiple of a determinant of an invertible matrix). So $\omega = c\,\mathrm{vol}_p$ for some number $c$, and $\omega(e_1, \dots, e_n) = 1$ forces $c = 1$.
>
> **Step 6** (part 3). In a negatively oriented chart $y$ and a positively oriented chart $x$, $J = \det(\partial x/\partial y) < 0$ and $\sqrt{|g_x|} = \sqrt{|g_y|}/|J| = -\sqrt{|g_y|}/J$, so Step 2 becomes $\sqrt{|g_x|}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n = -\sqrt{|g_y|}\,dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^n$. Reversing the orientation of $M$ makes the former negative charts positive, so the volume form of $-M$ is $-\mathrm{vol}$. By [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]], part 3, $\int_{-M}\omega = -\int_M\omega$, so $\int_{-M}f\,(-\mathrm{vol}) = \int_Mf\,\mathrm{vol}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The volume form is fixed by two pieces of structure, the metric (through $|\det E| = 1/\sqrt{|g|}$) and the orientation (through the sign of $\det E$). The integral of a function needs only the first.

## The Levi-Civita tensor

The volume form has a second life as a tensor with $n$ lower indices. Read that way it can be contracted with other tensors, and it is the object that replaces level B's $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ in curved spacetime. Its components with raised indices are not $\tilde\epsilon$ again: the metric that raises them also changes their size and, on a Lorentzian manifold, their sign.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.3.2: The Levi-Civita Tensor
> *Structure.* On an oriented manifold with a metric, the **Levi-Civita tensor** $\epsilon$ is the volume form ([[#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]) regarded as a totally antisymmetric tensor of type $(0, n)$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]). In every positively oriented chart
>
> $$
> \epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \sqrt{|g|}\;\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}, \qquad\text{on spacetime}\qquad \epsilon_{0123} = +\sqrt{-g} ,
> $$
>
> with $\tilde\epsilon$ the Levi-Civita symbol ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-7|Def. §C2.3.7]]). It is a tensor by [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 4 (weights $+1$ and $-1$ cancel); the symbol is not.
>
> *Other heights.* Its indices are raised with the inverse metric like those of any tensor ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]): $\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = g^{\mu_1\nu_1}\cdots g^{\mu_n\nu_n}\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_n}$, and $\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_k}{}_{\mu_{k+1}\dots\mu_n}$ with only the first $k$ raised. The raised components are $s\,\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}/\sqrt{|g|}$, so $\epsilon^{0123} = -1/\sqrt{-g}$ on spacetime ([[#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]], part 1). In the inertial coordinates of Minkowski spacetime, $\epsilon_{0123} = +1$ and $\epsilon^{0123} = -1$.
>
> *Notation.* A tilde marks the symbol, the same numbers in every chart; $\epsilon$ without a tilde is always this tensor, and $\epsilon^{\mu\cdots}$ always means its raised components. Couzens writes $v_{\mu_1\dots\mu_m}$ for the tensor and $\epsilon$ for the symbol at both heights.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.29)–(4.30) (p. 78) · d'Inverno & Vickers §7.2 (pp. 116–117) · Hartle, Box 20.1, item (k) (p. 438: the alternating tensor $g^{-1/2}\epsilon^{ijk}$)*

^def-c3-3-2

Every computation with the Hodge star below reduces to contracting two Levi-Civita tensors over some of their indices. The result is a signed count of permutations, multiplied by one number, the sign of $\det g$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.3: Raised Levi-Civita Tensor and Contraction Identities
> Let $s = \operatorname{sign}\det g = (-1)^q$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]], part 2).
> 1. **Raised components.**
>
> $$
> \epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{|g|}}\;\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} ; \qquad\text{Riemannian: } \epsilon^{1\cdots n} = +\frac{1}{\sqrt g} ; \qquad\text{Lorentzian: } \epsilon^{0123} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}} .
> $$
>
> 2. **Generalized Kronecker delta.** For $m \ge 1$ index lists $(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m)$ and $(\nu_1, \dots, \nu_m)$, let $\delta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_m}_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_m} = \operatorname{sgn}\pi$ if the $\lambda$'s are distinct and $\nu_i = \lambda_{\pi(i)}$ for a permutation $\pi$, and $0$ otherwise; for $m = 0$ it is $1$. Equivalently $\delta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_m}_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_m} = m!\,\delta^{[\nu_1}_{\lambda_1}\cdots\delta^{\nu_m]}_{\lambda_m}$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-5|Def. §C2.3.5]]). The first cases are $\delta^\nu_\lambda$ and $\delta^{\nu_1\nu_2}_{\lambda_1\lambda_2} = \delta^{\nu_1}_{\lambda_1}\delta^{\nu_2}_{\lambda_2} - \delta^{\nu_1}_{\lambda_2}\delta^{\nu_2}_{\lambda_1}$.
> 3. **Contraction over $k$ indices.** For $0 \le k \le n$,
>
> $$
> \epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_k\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{n-k}}\;\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_k\nu_1\dots\nu_{n-k}} = s\;k!\;\delta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_{n-k}}_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{n-k}} .
> $$
>
> In particular the full contraction is $\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = s\,n!$: $6$ on $\mathbf R^3$, $-24$ on spacetime.
> 4. **Cases used below.** On $\mathbf R^3$ with the Euclidean metric, $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon^{ilm} = \delta^l_j\delta^m_k - \delta^l_k\delta^m_j$ and $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon^{ijm} = 2\delta^m_k$. On spacetime, $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta} = -2\bigl(\delta^\alpha_\rho\delta^\beta_\sigma - \delta^\alpha_\sigma\delta^\beta_\rho\bigr)$ and $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\beta} = -6\,\delta^\beta_\sigma$.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of an oriented manifold with a metric; *applies:* every chart and point; any signature.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.30) (p. 78: "$\pm1/\sqrt{|g|}$") · d'Inverno & Vickers §7.2 (pp. 116–117: the contractions $\epsilon_{abcd}\epsilon^{ebcd} = 3!\,\delta^e_a$, $\epsilon_{abcd}\epsilon^{abcd} = 4!$ of the symbols) · all parts checked by script for $n = 2, 3, 4$ in both signatures*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). By [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]], part 1, $g^{\mu\nu}$ is the inverse matrix of $g_{\mu\nu}$, with determinant $1/g$ ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]). A symbol contracted with $n$ copies of a matrix absorbs its determinant ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], Step 1 of the derivation): $M^{\mu_1}{}_{\nu_1}\cdots M^{\mu_n}{}_{\nu_n}\tilde\epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_n} = \det M\,\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}$, used here with $M = (g^{\mu\nu})$ and the numbers $\tilde\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_n} = \tilde\epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_n}$. So
>
> $$
> \epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \sqrt{|g|}\;g^{\mu_1\nu_1}\cdots g^{\mu_n\nu_n}\tilde\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_n} = \sqrt{|g|}\cdot\frac1g\cdot\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} ,
> $$
>
> since $g = s\,|g|$ gives $\sqrt{|g|}/g = s\sqrt{|g|}/|g| = s/\sqrt{|g|}$ (and $1/s = s$).
>
> **Step 2** (part 2, the two descriptions agree). $m!\,\delta^{[\nu_1}_{\lambda_1}\cdots\delta^{\nu_m]}_{\lambda_m} = \sum_\sigma\operatorname{sgn}\sigma\,\delta^{\nu_{\sigma(1)}}_{\lambda_1}\cdots\delta^{\nu_{\sigma(m)}}_{\lambda_m}$ (antisymmetrization over the upper indices with weight $1/m!$). A term is $1$ exactly when $\nu_{\sigma(i)} = \lambda_i$ for all $i$. If the $\lambda$'s are distinct and the $\nu$'s are a rearrangement of them, exactly one $\sigma$ does this, namely $\sigma = \pi^{-1}$ with $\nu_i = \lambda_{\pi(i)}$, and $\operatorname{sgn}\pi^{-1} = \operatorname{sgn}\pi$. If two $\lambda$'s coincide, the terms cancel in pairs (exchange the two corresponding $\sigma$-values); if the $\nu$'s are not a rearrangement of the $\lambda$'s, no term is nonzero. For $m = 2$ the two permutations give the formula in part 2.
>
> **Step 3** (part 3 for symbols). Fix $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{n-k}$ and $\nu_1, \dots, \nu_{n-k}$ and consider $S = \sum_{\mu_1, \dots, \mu_k}\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_k\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{n-k}}\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_k\nu_1\dots\nu_{n-k}}$. A term is nonzero only if $(\mu, \lambda)$ is a list of $n$ distinct values, that is a rearrangement of $(1, \dots, n)$, and the same for $(\mu, \nu)$. Then the $\lambda$'s are distinct, and the sets $\{\lambda_i\}$ and $\{\nu_i\}$ are both the complement of $\{\mu_j\}$, so they are equal. If $\{\nu_i\} \ne \{\lambda_i\}$ or the $\lambda$'s repeat, every term vanishes and $S = 0 = \delta^{\nu\cdots}_{\lambda\cdots}$. Otherwise write $\nu_i = \lambda_{\pi(i)}$; rearranging the last $n - k$ entries by $\pi$ multiplies the sign of a permutation by $\operatorname{sgn}\pi$, so
>
> $$
> \tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_k\nu_1\dots\nu_{n-k}} = \operatorname{sgn}\pi\;\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_k\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{n-k}}, \qquad \tilde\epsilon_{\mu\cdots\lambda\cdots}\,\tilde\epsilon^{\mu\cdots\nu\cdots} = \operatorname{sgn}\pi\,\bigl(\tilde\epsilon_{\mu\cdots\lambda\cdots}\bigr)^2 = \operatorname{sgn}\pi
> $$
>
> for each of the $k!$ lists $(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_k)$ that run through the complement of $\{\lambda_i\}$ in some order. Summing, $S = k!\,\operatorname{sgn}\pi = k!\,\delta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_{n-k}}_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{n-k}}$.
>
> **Step 4** (part 3 for the tensor). By [[#^def-c3-3-2|Def. §C3.3.2]] and part 1, $\epsilon_{\cdots}\epsilon^{\cdots} = \sqrt{|g|}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\cdots}\cdot(s/\sqrt{|g|})\,\tilde\epsilon^{\cdots} = s\,\tilde\epsilon_{\cdots}\tilde\epsilon^{\cdots}$, and Step 3 gives $s\,k!\,\delta$. For $k = n$ the delta has no indices and equals $1$: $s\,n!$, which is $3! = 6$ for $s = 1$, $n = 3$, and $-4! = -24$ for $s = -1$, $n = 4$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 4). On $\mathbf R^3$, $s = 1$: $k = 1$ gives $1!\,\delta^{lm}_{jk}$ and $k = 2$ gives $2!\,\delta^m_k$. On spacetime, $s = -1$: $k = 2$ gives $-2!\,\delta^{\alpha\beta}_{\rho\sigma}$ and $k = 3$ gives $-3!\,\delta^\beta_\sigma$; part 2 writes out $\delta^{\alpha\beta}_{\rho\sigma}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The factors $\sqrt{|g|}$ cancel in every contraction of two $\epsilon$'s, which is therefore the same signed count of permutations as for the symbols, times the sign of $\det g$. That sign is the only trace the signature leaves, and it is the origin of the $\pm$ in every formula of this section.

> [!caution] Caution: The sign of ε on spacetime, and level B
> - **Level C.** $\epsilon_{0123} = +\sqrt{-g}$ in positively oriented charts, and the inertial coordinates $(t, x, y, z)$ are positively oriented. So $\mathrm{vol} = dt\wedge dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$ in an inertial frame, $\epsilon_{0123} = +1$ and $\epsilon^{0123} = -1$ there ([[#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]], part 1). This is Couzens' choice (GR1 (4.29)–(4.30), GR2 (A.29)), and it ties the sign of $\epsilon$ to the orientation: $\epsilon$ is the volume form.
> - **Level B.** $\epsilon^{0123} = +1$ and $\epsilon_{0123} = -1$ ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-2|Def. §B2.2.2]], [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], part 2; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). Lowering all four indices costs $\det\eta = -1$ in either signature, so the two conventions differ by one overall sign at every height: $\epsilon^{\rm C} = -\epsilon^{\rm B}$. Upper components of tensors are the same at both levels ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]), so the difference is a choice of normalization, not of signature.
> - **Converting.** A quantity that contains one $\epsilon$ (or one Hodge star) changes sign under conversion beyond the dictionary of Theorem §C1.2.2: convert every other factor by the dictionary and replace $\epsilon^{\rm B}$ by $-\epsilon^{\rm C}$. Example: level B has $F_{\mu\nu}\tilde F^{\mu\nu} = -4\,\vec E\cdot\vec B/c$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]]); level C has $F_{\mu\nu}(\star F)^{\mu\nu} = +4\,\vec E\cdot\vec B$ ([[#^ex-c3-3-3|Example §C3.3.3]]). Products of two $\epsilon$'s are unchanged: $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = -24$ at both levels.
> - **Pseudotensor.** Under a change of chart that reverses the orientation, $\epsilon$ changes sign relative to the tensor law ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 4); at level B the same statement is its sign change under parity and time reversal ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], part 1). On an oriented manifold, with positively oriented charts only, $\epsilon$ is a tensor.

## The Hodge star

The opening repaired the quarter turn of the plane by raising the index of the form and contracting it with $\sqrt{\det g}\,\tilde\epsilon$, that is with $\epsilon$. The same recipe works for forms of every degree: raise all $p$ indices of a $p$-form and contract them with $p$ of the $n$ indices of $\epsilon$; the $n - p$ indices left over make an $(n - p)$-form.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.3.3: Hodge Star
> *Structure.* On an oriented $n$-manifold with a metric, the **Hodge star** (Hodge dual) is the family of linear maps $\star : \Omega^p(M) \to \Omega^{n-p}(M)$, $p = 0, 1, \dots, n$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]]), given by
>
> $$
> (\star\omega)_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} = \frac{1}{p!}\;\omega^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}\;\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} = \frac{1}{p!}\,\sqrt{|g|}\;\omega^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}\;\tilde\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} ,
> $$
>
> where $\omega^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p} = g^{\nu_1\lambda_1}\cdots g^{\nu_p\lambda_p}\omega_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p}$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]) and $\epsilon$ is the Levi-Civita tensor ([[#^def-c3-3-2|Def. §C3.3.2]]). The form is contracted with the **first** $p$ indices of $\epsilon$. The $\frac1{p!}$ makes the sum run once over each set of $p$ indices.
>
> *Why it is a form.* The right side is a contraction of the tensor product $\omega^\sharp\otimes\epsilon$, so it is a tensor, the same in every chart ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]); it is antisymmetric in $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_{n-p}$ because $\epsilon$ is. $\star$ is linear over functions, $\star(f\omega) = f\star\omega$, so it acts point by point. It uses the metric twice (to raise the indices of $\omega$ and through $\sqrt{|g|}$) and the orientation once (the sign of $\epsilon$).
>
> *Dimensions.* $\Lambda^p$ and $\Lambda^{n-p}$ at a point both have dimension $\binom np = \binom n{n-p}$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]], counting); $\star$ is an isomorphism between them ([[#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]], part 4). On spacetime it maps functions to four-forms, one-forms to three-forms, and two-forms to two-forms.
>
> *First values.* $\star1 = \mathrm{vol}$ and $\star\mathrm{vol} = s$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]). On the plane, $\star dx = dy$ and $\star dy = -dx$: the quarter turn of the opening, which in polar coordinates reads $\star dr = r\,d\theta$, $\star d\theta = -dr/r$. On $\mathbf R^3$, $\star dx = dy\wedge dz$ ([[#^ex-c3-3-2|Example §C3.3.2]]); on spacetime, $\star(dt\wedge dx) = -dy\wedge dz$ ([[#^ex-c3-3-3|Example §C3.3.3]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.31) (p. 78), with the contraction moved to the first indices of $\epsilon$ ([[#^cau-c3-3-2|Caution: Index placement in the Hodge star]]); footnote 15 (p. 62: the metric provides the isomorphism $\Omega^r_p \cong \Omega^{m-r}_p$) · Couzens, GR2, App. A, eq. (A.30) (p. 182) · the values checked by script*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.4: ⋆1 = vol, ⋆vol = s, and ⋆⋆ = s(−1)^{p(n−p)}
> Let $s = \operatorname{sign}\det g$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]) and let $\star$ be the Hodge star ([[#^def-c3-3-3|Def. §C3.3.3]]).
> 1. $\star1 = \mathrm{vol}$ ([[#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]).
> 2. $\star\mathrm{vol} = s$: $+1$ for a Riemannian metric, $-1$ for a Lorentzian one.
> 3. For every $p$-form $\omega$,
>
> $$
> \star\star\omega = s\,(-1)^{p(n-p)}\,\omega .
> $$
>
> Riemannian ($s = +1$): $\star\star = (-1)^{p(n-p)}$, which is $+1$ in every degree when $n$ is odd ($p$ or $n - p$ is even). Lorentzian ($s = -1$): $\star\star = -(-1)^{p(n-p)}$. The tables:
>
> | | $p = 0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $\mathbf R^2$ | $+1$ | $-1$ | $+1$ | | |
> | $\mathbf R^3$ | $+1$ | $+1$ | $+1$ | $+1$ | |
> | $\mathbf R^4$, Riemannian | $+1$ | $-1$ | $+1$ | $-1$ | $+1$ |
> | spacetime | $-1$ | $+1$ | $-1$ | $+1$ | $-1$ |
>
> 4. $\star$ is invertible: on $(n - p)$-forms, $\star^{-1} = s\,(-1)^{p(n-p)}\,\star$.
> 5. ⚑ **Two-forms in four dimensions.** For $n = 4$ and $p = 2$, $\star\star = s$. With a Riemannian metric, $\star^2 = 1$ and every two-form splits into a self-dual and an anti-self-dual part, $\omega = \frac12(\omega + \star\omega) + \frac12(\omega - \star\omega)$, with $\star = \pm1$ on them. On spacetime $\star^2 = -1$ on two-forms, like $i^2 = -1$: no nonzero real two-form is self-dual, and the eigenvalues $\pm i$ need complex forms such as $F \mp i\star F$. The same holds for one-forms on the plane, where $\star$ is the quarter turn and $\star^2 = -1$.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of an oriented manifold with a metric; *applies:* every degree, any dimension and signature.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.32) (p. 79: "with $+$ for a Riemannian metric and $-$ for a Lorentzian"; stated without proof) · Couzens, GR2, App. A, eq. (A.31) (p. 182) · proof written here · checked by script for $n = 2, 3, 4$, all $p$, both signatures*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). For $p = 0$ the form $\omega = 1$ has no indices and the definition reads $(\star1)_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \mathrm{vol}_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}$ ([[#^def-c3-3-2|Def. §C3.3.2]]).
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). For $p = n$ no index is left over: $\star\mathrm{vol} = \frac{1}{n!}\mathrm{vol}^{\nu_1\dots\nu_n}\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_n} = \frac1{n!}\epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_n}\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_n} = \frac{1}{n!}\,s\,n! = s$, by [[#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]], part 3 with $k = n$.
>
> **Step 3** (the star with raised free indices). Raising the free indices of $\star\omega$, and moving the metric factors of the contracted pairs from $\omega$ to $\epsilon$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]], parts 2 and 4),
>
> $$
> (\star\omega)^{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} = \frac{1}{p!}\,\omega_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}\,\epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (apply the star twice). $\star\omega$ has degree $n - p$, so by the definition with $p$ replaced by $n - p$, and Step 3,
>
> $$
> (\star\star\omega)_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} = \frac{1}{(n-p)!}\,(\star\omega)^{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}}\,\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} = \frac{1}{(n-p)!\,p!}\;\omega_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}\;\epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}}\;\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (reorder). In $\epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}}$ move the block $\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}$ to the front: each of its $n - p$ indices passes the $p$ indices $\nu$, one transposition each, so $p(n - p)$ transpositions in all:
>
> $$
> \epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} = (-1)^{p(n-p)}\,\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\nu_1\dots\nu_p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (contract). [[#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]], part 3, with $k = n - p$ contracted indices:
>
> $$
> \epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p}\,\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\nu_1\dots\nu_p} = s\,(n-p)!\;\delta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 7** (the delta against $\omega$). $\delta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p}\omega_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}$ is a sum over the $p!$ rearrangements $\nu_i = \lambda_{\pi(i)}$ of $\operatorname{sgn}\pi\;\omega_{\lambda_{\pi(1)}\dots\lambda_{\pi(p)}} = \operatorname{sgn}\pi\cdot\operatorname{sgn}\pi\;\omega_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p}$ (antisymmetry of $\omega$), so it equals $p!\,\omega_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p}$; if the $\lambda$'s repeat, both sides are $0$. Inserting Steps 5–7 into Step 4:
>
> $$
> (\star\star\omega)_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} = \frac{1}{(n-p)!\,p!}\,(-1)^{p(n-p)}\,s\,(n-p)!\;p!\;\omega_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} = s\,(-1)^{p(n-p)}\,\omega_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 8** (the tables). $p(n - p)$ for $n = 2$: $0, 1, 0$; for $n = 3$: $0, 2, 2, 0$; for $n = 4$: $0, 3, 4, 3, 0$. Multiply $(-1)^{p(n-p)}$ by $s = +1$ or $-1$. If $n$ is odd, one of $p$ and $n - p$ is even, so $p(n-p)$ is even.
>
> **Step 9** (part 4). By part 3 applied to $(n - p)$-forms, $\star\star = s(-1)^{(n-p)p}$ on them, so $\bigl(s(-1)^{p(n-p)}\star\bigr)\star = 1$ on $\Omega^{n-p}$, and in the other order on $\Omega^p$.
>
> **Step 10** (part 5). For $n = 4$, $p = 2$: $p(n - p) = 4$, so $\star\star = s$. If $s = 1$, $\star\bigl(\frac12(\omega \pm \star\omega)\bigr) = \frac12(\star\omega \pm \omega) = \pm\frac12(\omega \pm \star\omega)$. If $s = -1$ and $\star\omega = \lambda\omega$ with real $\lambda$ and $\omega \ne 0$, then $-\omega = \star\star\omega = \lambda^2\omega$, impossible; for $\lambda = \mp i$, $\star(F \mp i\star F) = \star F \mp i(-F) = \pm i(F \mp i\star F)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** $\star\star$ is the product of two signs: $s$, from the one contraction of two $\epsilon$'s, and $(-1)^{p(n-p)}$, from reordering. The first is why Lorentzian signature changes every sign; the second does not depend on the metric at all. Since the reordering occurs once in each application of $\star$, putting the form on the last instead of the first indices of $\epsilon$ changes $\star$ by $(-1)^{p(n-p)}$ but leaves $\star\star$ unchanged.

## Inner product, codifferential and Laplacian

The metric measures vectors; with the Hodge star it also measures forms. Two $p$-forms can be paired point by point by raising the indices of one, and the pairing integrated with $\mathrm{vol}$ gives an inner product on forms of compact support. With an inner product, $d$ has an adjoint, which lowers the degree by one; it is a divergence, and $d$ followed by its adjoint is the Laplacian.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.3.4: Inner Product of Forms
> *Structure.* For $p$-forms $\omega$ and $\eta$ on a manifold with a metric, the **pointwise inner product** is the function
>
> $$
> \langle\omega, \eta\rangle = \frac{1}{p!}\,\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_p}\,\eta^{\mu_1\dots\mu_p} = \sum_{\mu_1 < \dots < \mu_p}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_p}\,\eta^{\mu_1\dots\mu_p} ,
> $$
>
> a full contraction ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]) with indices raised by $g^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]), hence the same in every chart. For $p = 0$ it is the product $fh$; for $p = 1$ it is $g^{\mu\nu}\omega_\mu\eta_\nu$, the inverse metric of §C3.1. It is symmetric and bilinear over functions. On an oriented manifold, for forms of which at least one has compact support, the **inner product** is the number
>
> $$
> (\omega, \eta) = \int_M\langle\omega, \eta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = \int\langle\omega, \eta\rangle\,\sqrt{|g|}\;d^nx \qquad(\text{support in one chart}) ,
> $$
>
> an integral of a function against the volume form ([[#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]).
>
> *Definite or not.* At each point the pairing is nondegenerate: if $\langle\omega, \eta\rangle = 0$ for all $\eta$, then $\omega = 0$ (take $\eta$ with one independent component $\eta_{\mu_1\dots\mu_p} = 1$, $\mu_1 < \dots < \mu_p$; then $\langle\omega, \eta\rangle$ is $\omega^{\mu_1\dots\mu_p}$, and all of them vanish). For a Riemannian metric it is positive definite: in an orthonormal basis ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]) $\langle\omega, \omega\rangle$ is the sum of the squares of the components. For a Lorentzian metric it is indefinite: $\langle dt, dt\rangle = -1$ and $\langle dx, dx\rangle = +1$ in Minkowski spacetime, and for the field strength $\langle F, F\rangle = \frac12F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = B^2 - E^2$ ([[#^ex-c3-3-3|Example §C3.3.3]]). "Inner product" then means a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.33) (p. 79: $\langle\eta, \omega\rangle \equiv \int_M\eta\wedge\star\omega$; equal to the integral here by [[#^thm-c3-3-5|Theorem §C3.3.5]]) · values checked by script*

^def-c3-3-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.5: η ∧ ⋆ω = ⟨η, ω⟩ vol
> For $p$-forms $\eta$ and $\omega$ on an oriented manifold with a metric,
>
> $$
> \eta\wedge\star\omega = \langle\eta, \omega\rangle\;\mathrm{vol} = \omega\wedge\star\eta ,
> $$
>
> with $\star$ of [[#^def-c3-3-3|Def. §C3.3.3]], $\langle\cdot, \cdot\rangle$ of [[#^def-c3-3-4|Def. §C3.3.4]] and the wedge product of [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]]. Consequently $(\eta, \omega) = \int_M\eta\wedge\star\omega$, Couzens' definition (4.33). Two cases used below: $\omega\wedge\star\omega = \langle\omega, \omega\rangle\,\mathrm{vol}$, and for a two-form on spacetime $F\wedge\star F = \frac12F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\,\mathrm{vol}$, so the Maxwell Lagrangian is $-\frac{1}{4\mu_0}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\,\mathrm{vol} = -\frac{1}{2\mu_0}F\wedge\star F$ (GR1 (5.46), with $\mu_0 = 1$ there).
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* every degree, dimension and signature, with the first-index star ([[#^cau-c3-3-2|Caution: Index placement in the Hodge star]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.33) (p. 79); §5.1.5, eq. (5.46) (p. 111) · checked by script for $n = 2, 3, 4$, every $p$, both signatures*

With an inner product on every $\Omega^p$, the exterior derivative $d : \Omega^{p-1} \to \Omega^p$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]]) has an adjoint going the other way. The finite-dimensional picture is the transpose of a matrix; here the transpose is computed by integration by parts.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.3.5: Codifferential
> *Structure.* On an oriented manifold with a metric, the **codifferential** (the adjoint of $d$) is the family of linear maps $d^\dagger : \Omega^p(M) \to \Omega^{p-1}(M)$, $p = 1, \dots, n$, characterized by
>
> $$
> (d\alpha, \beta) = (\alpha, d^\dagger\beta)
> $$
>
> for every $p$-form $\beta$ and every $(p - 1)$-form $\alpha$ with compact support in the interior of $M$, with the inner product of [[#^def-c3-3-4|Def. §C3.3.4]]. On functions $d^\dagger = 0$. Such a map exists, is unique, and is a differential operator with the components ([[#^thm-c3-3-6|Theorem §C3.3.6]])
>
> $$
> (d^\dagger\beta)^{\nu_2\dots\nu_p} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\;\beta^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> For a one-form, $d^\dagger\beta = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\,\beta^\mu\bigr)$ is minus the divergence of the vector $\beta^\sharp$: the density $\sqrt{|g|}\,\beta^\mu$ is differentiated, as in the divergence of [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]] and the polar formula of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^ex-c2-3-3|Example §C2.3.3]].
>
> *Names.* Couzens writes $d^\dagger$ (GR1 (4.34)); many texts write $\delta$. "Adjoint" here means formal adjoint: only $\alpha$ of compact support are tested, so no boundary terms arise.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.34)–(4.35) (p. 79) · component formula derived here*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.6: The Codifferential: Components, Adjointness, d† = ±⋆d⋆
> Let $\beta$ be a $p$-form, $1 \le p \le n$, on an oriented manifold with a metric, $s = \operatorname{sign}\det g$, and define in each chart
>
> $$
> D^{\nu_2\dots\nu_p} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\;\beta^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 1. **A form, built from $\star$ and $d$.** $D$ are the raised components of a $(p - 1)$-form, the same in every chart, and
>
> $$
> D = s\,(-1)^{n(p+1)+1}\,\star d\star\beta .
> $$
>
> 2. **Adjointness and uniqueness.** $(d\alpha, \beta) = (\alpha, D)$ for every $(p - 1)$-form $\alpha$ of compact support in the interior of $M$, and $D$ is the only $(p - 1)$-form with this property. So $d^\dagger\beta = D$ ([[#^def-c3-3-5|Def. §C3.3.5]]).
> 3. **Cases.** On spacetime ($n = 4$, $s = -1$): $d^\dagger = \star d\star$ in every degree. On $\mathbf R^3$: $d^\dagger = (-1)^p\star d\star$. On the Euclidean plane: $d^\dagger = -\star d\star$. On one-forms in any dimension: $d^\dagger\beta = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}\,\beta^\mu)$.
> 4. **$d^\dagger d^\dagger = 0$.**
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* every degree, dimension and signature; part 2 for compactly supported $\alpha$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.34)–(4.35) (p. 79; part 1 is (4.35), stated without proof) · proof written here · parts 1, 2 and 4 checked by script for $n = 2, 3, 4$, every $p$, both signatures, on metrics that depend on the coordinates*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Write $\mathfrak B^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p} = \sqrt{|g|}\,\beta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}$, a totally antisymmetric density of weight $1$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]], part 3; [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 3).
>
> **Step 1** (the first star). By [[#^def-c3-3-3|Def. §C3.3.3]], with $\epsilon = \sqrt{|g|}\,\tilde\epsilon$,
>
> $$
> (\star\beta)_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} = \frac{1}{p!}\,\mathfrak B^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** ($d$). $\star\beta$ has degree $n - p$; by [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1,
>
> $$
> (d\star\beta)_{\mu_0\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-p}(-1)^k\,\partial_{\mu_k}(\star\beta)_{\mu_0\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_{n-p}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the second star, raised). $d\star\beta$ has degree $m = n - p + 1$, and $n - m = p - 1$. By Step 3 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]] and [[#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]], part 1 ($\epsilon^{\cdots} = s\,\tilde\epsilon^{\cdots}/\sqrt{|g|}$),
>
> $$
> (\star d\star\beta)^{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\frac{1}{(n-p+1)!}\,(d\star\beta)_{\mu_0\dots\mu_{n-p}}\,\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_0\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (the $n - p + 1$ terms are equal). In the $k$-th term of Step 2, contracted with $\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_0\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda\cdots}$, move $\mu_k$ to the front of the symbol: $k$ transpositions, $\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_0\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda\cdots} = (-1)^k\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_k\mu_0\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda\cdots}$. The factor $(-1)^k$ cancels the sign in Step 2, and after renaming the summed indices the term equals the $k = 0$ term. So
>
> $$
> (\star d\star\beta)^{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\frac{1}{(n-p)!}\,\partial_{\mu_0}(\star\beta)_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}}\,\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_0\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (insert Step 1). The symbol is constant, so $\partial_{\mu_0}$ acts on $\mathfrak B$ only:
>
> $$
> (\star d\star\beta)^{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\frac{1}{(n-p)!\,p!}\;\tilde\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}}\;\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_0\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}}\;\partial_{\mu_0}\mathfrak B^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (reorder both symbols). Move the block $\nu_1\dots\nu_p$ to the end of the first symbol, past $n - p$ indices: $(-1)^{p(n-p)}$. Move $\mu_0$ past the block $\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}$ in the second: $(-1)^{n-p}$. Then
>
> $$
> \tilde\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}}\;\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_0\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} = (-1)^{p(n-p) + (n-p)}\;\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\nu_1\dots\nu_p}\;\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\mu_0\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 7** (contract). Step 3 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]] (symbols, $k = n - p$ summed indices) gives $(n - p)!\,\delta^{\mu_0\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}}_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}$, and against the antisymmetric $\partial_{\mu_0}\mathfrak B^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}$ the delta gives $p!\,\partial_{\mu_0}\mathfrak B^{\mu_0\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}}$ (Step 7 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]). Collecting,
>
> $$
> (\star d\star\beta)^{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} = s\,(-1)^{(n-p)(p+1)}\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\,\beta^{\mu\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}}\bigr) = s\,(-1)^{(n-p)(p+1)+1}\,D^{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 8** (the exponent; part 1). $(n - p)(p + 1) = n(p + 1) - p(p + 1)$, and $p(p + 1)$ is even, so $(-1)^{(n-p)(p+1)+1} = (-1)^{n(p+1)+1}$. Multiplying by $s(-1)^{n(p+1)+1}$, whose square is $1$: $D = s(-1)^{n(p+1)+1}\star d\star\beta$. The right side is built from $\star$ and $d$, which give forms independent of the chart ([[#^def-c3-3-3|Def. §C3.3.3]]; [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 2), so $D$ is a $(p - 1)$-form. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 9** ($\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle$ in components). By [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1, $(d\alpha)_{\mu_1\dots\mu_p} = \sum_{k=1}^{p}(-1)^{k-1}\partial_{\mu_k}\alpha_{\mu_1\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_p}$. Contracted with the antisymmetric $\beta^{\mu_1\dots\mu_p}$, the $k$-th term equals the first (move $\mu_k$ to the front of $\beta$: $k - 1$ transpositions, cancelling $(-1)^{k-1}$). So, by [[#^def-c3-3-4|Def. §C3.3.4]],
>
> $$
> \langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle = \frac{1}{p!}\cdot p\;\partial_\mu\alpha_{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\,\beta^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p} = \frac{1}{(p-1)!}\;\partial_\mu\alpha_{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\,\beta^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 10** (integrate by parts; part 2). Multiply by $\sqrt{|g|}$ and use the product rule on $\partial_\mu\bigl(\alpha_{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\mathfrak B^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\bigr)$:
>
> $$
> \sqrt{|g|}\,\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle = \frac{1}{(p-1)!}\,\partial_\mu\mathfrak V^\mu + \frac{1}{(p-1)!}\,\alpha_{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\Bigl(-\partial_\mu\mathfrak B^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{(p-1)!}\,\partial_\mu\mathfrak V^\mu + \sqrt{|g|}\,\langle\alpha, D\rangle ,
> $$
>
> with $\mathfrak V^\mu = \alpha_{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\mathfrak B^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p}$, a vector density of weight $1$ (a contraction of a tensor with a density, [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 3) with compact support in the interior, because $\alpha$ has. As top forms, $\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = \langle\alpha, D\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} + \frac{1}{(p-1)!}\,d\omega_{\mathfrak V}$ with the flux form $\omega_{\mathfrak V}$ of [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]], part 1, whose integral vanishes by part 2 of that theorem (no contribution from a boundary that the support does not reach). Integrating, $(d\alpha, \beta) = (\alpha, D)$.
>
> **Step 11** (uniqueness). If $D'$ also satisfies part 2, then $(\alpha, D - D') = 0$ for all $\alpha$ of compact support. In a chart, take $\alpha$ with the single independent component $\alpha_{\nu_2\dots\nu_p} = \varphi$ for one increasing list $\nu_2 < \dots < \nu_p$, $\varphi$ a smooth function of compact support in the chart, the other components zero or fixed by antisymmetry. Then $0 = (\alpha, D - D') = \int\varphi\,(D - D')^{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\sqrt{|g|}\,d^nx$ for every such $\varphi$, so the continuous function $(D - D')^{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\sqrt{|g|}$ vanishes, and with it every component of $D - D'$. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 12** (part 3). $s(-1)^{n(p+1)+1}$: for $n = 4$, $s = -1$, the exponent $4(p+1)+1$ is odd, so the sign is $(-1)(-1) = +1$. For $n = 3$, $s = 1$: $(-1)^{3p+4} = (-1)^p$. For $n = 2$, $s = 1$: $(-1)^{2p+3} = -1$. For $p = 1$ the formula of $D$ has the single free index removed.
>
> **Step 13** (part 4). $d^\dagger d^\dagger\beta = c_1\star d\star\,c_2\star d\star\beta = c_1c_2\,\star d\,(\star\star)\,d\star\beta$ with signs $c_1, c_2 = \pm1$. $\star\star = \pm1$ on the $(n - p + 1)$-form $d\star\beta$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]), so this is $\pm\star\,d\,d\star\beta = 0$ by $d^2 = 0$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 4).
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The adjoint of $d$ is a divergence: $-\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}\,\beta^{\mu\cdots})$ is a tensor although it contains only partial derivatives, because $\beta^{\mu\cdots}$ is antisymmetric and the density $\sqrt{|g|}$ absorbs the Jacobian. No connection is needed. For general tensors this fails; the connection that repairs it is [[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative|§C3.4]], and the same divergence formula for vector fields reappears there with $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\nu\ln\sqrt{|g|}$ ([[§C3.5 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]]).
> - The sign in $d^\dagger = \pm\star d\star$ collects three sources: $s$ from raising $\epsilon$, the reorderings of the symbols, and the convention that the form sits on the first indices of $\epsilon$. Only the component formula is free of conventions.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.3.6: Laplace–de Rham Operator
> *Structure.* On an oriented manifold with a metric, the **Laplace–de Rham operator** is the linear map $\Delta : \Omega^p(M) \to \Omega^p(M)$,
>
> $$
> \Delta = d\,d^\dagger + d^\dagger d = (d + d^\dagger)^2 ,
> $$
>
> with $d$ of [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]] and $d^\dagger$ of [[#^def-c3-3-5|Def. §C3.3.5]]; the second form holds because $d^2 = 0$ and $(d^\dagger)^2 = 0$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-6|Theorem §C3.3.6]], part 4). On functions $d^\dagger f = 0$, so $\Delta f = d^\dagger df$. A form with $\Delta\omega = 0$ is **harmonic**.
>
> *Names and sign.* Couzens writes $\Box$ (GR1 (4.36); footnote 19: also $\triangle$). These notes reserve $\Box$ for the d'Alembertian; on functions $\Delta = -\Box$, and on $\mathbf R^3$, $\Delta = -\nabla^2$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]], [[#^cau-c3-3-3|Caution: Laplacian, d'Alembertian and their signs]]). The sign makes $\Delta$ a positive operator on a Riemannian manifold.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.36) and footnote 19 (p. 79)*

^def-c3-3-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.7: The Laplace–de Rham Operator on Functions
> Let $f$ be a smooth function on an oriented manifold with a metric.
> 1. **Formula.** In every chart
>
> $$
> \Delta f = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\Bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\;g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\nu f\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 2. **Euclidean space.** On $\mathbf R^3$ with Cartesian coordinates, $\Delta f = -(\partial_x^2 + \partial_y^2 + \partial_z^2)f = -\nabla^2f$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^def-b1-1-1|EM Def. §B1.1.1]]): $\Delta$ is minus the Laplacian of vector calculus. In curvilinear coordinates part 1 reproduces its curvilinear forms ([[#^ex-c3-3-4|Example §C3.3.4]]).
> 3. **Minkowski spacetime.** In inertial coordinates, $\Delta f = \partial_t^2f - \nabla^2f = -\Box f$, with $\Box = -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2 = \eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu$ the d'Alembertian of [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-4|Theorem §C1.1.4]]. The wave equation is $\Delta f = 0$.
> 4. ⚑ **Positivity.** For $f$ of compact support, $(f, \Delta f) = (df, df) = \int g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu f\,\partial_\nu f\;\mathrm{vol}$. For a Riemannian metric this is $\ge 0$, and $0$ only for $f = 0$: $\Delta$ is a positive operator. For a Lorentzian metric it has no sign; in Minkowski spacetime the integrand is $-(\partial_tf)^2 + |\nabla f|^2$.
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* any chart, any dimension and signature; part 4 for compactly supported $f$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.37) and the sentence before it (p. 79: "only positive definite on Riemannian manifolds"; stated without proof) · Hartle, Box 20.1, item (j) (p. 437: $\nabla^2f = g^{-1/2}\partial_i(g^{1/2}g^{ij}\partial_jf)$, the same formula with the opposite sign) · proof written here · checked by script (Cartesian, spherical, $S^2$, Minkowski, and $(f, \Delta f) = (df, df)$ for $f = e^{-r^2}$ on $\mathbf R^3$)*

^thm-c3-3-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). $df$ has the components $\partial_\nu f$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1, $r = 0$), raised $(df)^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu f$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]], part 2). [[#^thm-c3-3-6|Theorem §C3.3.6]] with $p = 1$ has no free index left:
>
> $$
> \Delta f = d^\dagger df = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\,(df)^\mu\bigr) = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\,g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu f\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> and $d\,d^\dagger f = 0$ because $d^\dagger f = 0$ ([[#^def-c3-3-5|Def. §C3.3.5]]).
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). $g_{\mu\nu} = \delta_{ij}$: $\sqrt g = 1$, $g^{ij} = \delta^{ij}$, and part 1 gives $-\partial_i\partial_if$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 3). $g = \eta$: $\sqrt{-g} = 1$, $\eta^{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$, and part 1 gives $-\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu f = -(-\partial_t^2f + \nabla^2f) = \partial_t^2f - \nabla^2f$.
>
> **Step 4** (part 4). [[#^thm-c3-3-6|Theorem §C3.3.6]], part 2, with $\alpha = f$ (compact support) and $\beta = df$: $(df, df) = (f, d^\dagger df) = (f, \Delta f)$. By [[#^def-c3-3-4|Def. §C3.3.4]], $\langle df, df\rangle = g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu f\,\partial_\nu f$. For a Riemannian metric this is $\ge 0$ at every point, so $(df, df) \ge 0$; if it is $0$, then $df = 0$ everywhere (a continuous nonnegative integrand with zero integral against the positive density $\sqrt g$ vanishes), so $f$ is constant on each connected component, and compact support makes it $0$ on a noncompact component. (On a compact manifold the constants are harmonic: $\Delta1 = 0$.) For $\eta$, $\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu f\partial_\nu f = -(\partial_tf)^2 + |\nabla f|^2$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The Laplacian of every chart, curvilinear or curved, is one formula, the divergence of the gradient with the density $\sqrt{|g|}$. The overall minus sign is the price of making $d^\dagger$ the adjoint of $d$, and it is what makes $\Delta$ positive.

> [!caution] Caution: Laplacian, d'Alembertian and their signs
> - **Three operators, one formula.** On functions, $\Delta f = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu f)$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]]). The d'Alembertian of these notes is $\Box f = +\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu f) = -\Delta f$, equal to $-\partial_t^2f + \nabla^2f$ in inertial coordinates ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-4|Theorem §C1.1.4]]); in general it is $g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu f$, with the covariant derivative of [[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative|§C3.4]]. On $\mathbf R^3$, $\nabla^2 = -\Delta$.
> - **Couzens' $\Box$.** GR1 (4.36)–(4.37) writes $\Box$ for $\Delta$. A wave equation written $\Box\phi = 0$ is the same equation in either convention, but $\Box\phi = m^2\phi$ is not: here the Klein–Gordon equation is $(\Box - m^2)\phi = 0$, that is $(\Delta + m^2)\phi = 0$.
> - **Level B.** Level B's $\Box = \partial_t^2/c^2 - \nabla^2$ ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^cau-b2-2-3|§B2.2, Caution: The sign of the box]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) equals $\Delta$ of Minkowski spacetime here, and $-\Box$ of level C.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.36)–(4.37), footnote 19 (p. 79) · signs checked by script*

^cau-c3-3-3

The volume form, the star, the codifferential and the Laplacian used only partial derivatives and $\sqrt{|g|}$. They could, because every derivative in them acts on the components of a form or of a density, where the non-tensorial terms of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]] cancel by antisymmetry or are absorbed by the Jacobian. A vector field that is not differentiated in a divergence has no such protection: $\partial_\mu V^\nu$ is still not a tensor, and the Lie derivative needs a whole vector field, not a direction at a point ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors|§C2.5]]). Which extra structure gives a derivative of any tensor along a single direction, and how does the metric pick it? [[§C3.4 The Covariant Derivative|§C3.4]] begins the answer.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C3.3.1: Volume of a Sphere and of a Ball; a Curved Three-Space
> *(a) The sphere of radius $a$.* The round metric $ds^2 = a^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]) has $g = a^4\sin^2\theta$ and, on $0 < \theta < \pi$, $\sqrt g = a^2\sin\theta$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]). With the orientation in which $(\theta, \varphi)$ is positive (outward normal first, [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^ex-c2-7-4|Example §C2.7.4]]), $\mathrm{vol} = a^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi$ ([[#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]). The chart misses a half great circle, a set of measure zero ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]], part 3), so
>
> $$
> \mathrm{Area}(S^2_a) = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi a^2\sin\theta\;d\theta\,d\varphi = a^2\cdot2\pi\cdot\bigl[-\cos\theta\bigr]_0^\pi = 4\pi a^2 .
> $$
>
> In the stereographic chart the induced metric is $4a^2(du^2 + dv^2)/(1 + u^2 + v^2)^2$, so $\sqrt g = 4a^2/(1 + \rho^2)^2$ with $\rho^2 = u^2 + v^2$. That chart is negatively oriented for the outward orientation ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^ex-c2-7-4|Example §C2.7.4]]); by [[#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]], part 3, the area does not depend on the orientation, so it may be computed with the orientation in which the chart is positive. In polar coordinates $(\rho, \phi)$ of the $(u, v)$-plane, $du\,dv = \rho\,d\rho\,d\phi$:
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathbf R^2}\frac{4a^2\;du\,dv}{(1 + u^2 + v^2)^2} = 2\pi\int_0^\infty\frac{4a^2\rho\;d\rho}{(1 + \rho^2)^2} = 2\pi\cdot4a^2\Bigl[-\frac{1}{2(1 + \rho^2)}\Bigr]_0^\infty = 4\pi a^2 .
> $$
>
> *(b) The ball of radius $R$ in $\mathbf R^3$.* In spherical coordinates $g = \operatorname{diag}(1, r^2, r^2\sin^2\theta)$, $\sqrt g = r^2\sin\theta$ (Hartle (7.33)):
>
> $$
> \mathrm{Vol} = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\!\!\int_0^R r^2\sin\theta\;dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi = \frac{R^3}{3}\cdot2\cdot2\pi = \frac{4\pi R^3}{3} .
> $$
>
> The orthonormal frame $e_r = \partial_r$, $e_\theta = \partial_\theta/r$, $e_\varphi = \partial_\varphi/(r\sin\theta)$ has $\det E = 1/(r^2\sin\theta)$, and $\mathrm{vol}(e_r, e_\theta, e_\varphi) = r^2\sin\theta\cdot1/(r^2\sin\theta) = 1$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]], part 2).
>
> *(c) A curved three-space.* Hartle's spatial metric of a homogeneous closed universe (or the interior of a uniform star) is
>
> $$
> dS^2 = \frac{dr^2}{1 - r^2/a^2} + r^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr), \qquad 0 < r < a, \qquad \sqrt g = \frac{r^2\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1 - r^2/a^2}} .
> $$
>
> The sphere $r = R$ has the induced metric $R^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)$, area $4\pi R^2$ by (a). The volume inside it is not $\frac43\pi R^3$. With $r = a\sin\chi$, $dr = a\cos\chi\,d\chi$, $\sqrt{1 - r^2/a^2} = \cos\chi$ ($0 \le \chi \le \pi/2$), and $\chi_R = \arcsin(R/a)$:
>
> $$
> V(R) = 4\pi\int_0^R\frac{r^2\,dr}{\sqrt{1 - r^2/a^2}} = 4\pi a^3\int_0^{\chi_R}\sin^2\chi\;d\chi = 4\pi a^3\Bigl[\frac{\chi}{2} - \frac{\sin2\chi}{4}\Bigr]_0^{\chi_R} = 4\pi a^3\Bigl[\frac12\arcsin\frac Ra - \frac{R}{2a}\sqrt{1 - \frac{R^2}{a^2}}\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> Hartle (7.38), using $\frac14\sin2\chi_R = \frac12\sin\chi_R\cos\chi_R = \frac{R}{2a}\sqrt{1 - R^2/a^2}$. For $R = a/2$: $V = 0.5692\,a^3$, against $\frac43\pi(a/2)^3 = 0.5236\,a^3$, a ratio $1.087$. For small $R$, $V = \frac43\pi R^3\bigl(1 + \frac{3R^2}{10a^2} + \dots\bigr)$: there is more volume inside a sphere of given area than in flat space. At $R = a$, $V = 4\pi a^3\cdot\frac\pi4 = \pi^2a^3$. The substitution turns the metric into $a^2\bigl(d\chi^2 + \sin^2\chi\,(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)\bigr)$, the three-sphere of radius $a$; $r < a$ covers half of it, and the whole three-sphere ($0 < \chi < \pi$) has $\sqrt g = a^3\sin^2\chi\sin\theta$ and volume $a^3\cdot\frac\pi2\cdot2\cdot2\pi = 2\pi^2a^3$.
>
> *(d) Four-volume.* Minkowski spacetime in spherical coordinates has $\sqrt{-g} = r^2\sin\theta$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]), $\mathrm{vol} = r^2\sin\theta\,dt\wedge dr\wedge d\theta\wedge d\varphi$ (GR2 (5.52)), and the region $0 < t < T$, $r < R$ has four-volume $T\cdot\frac43\pi R^3$.
>
> *Source: Hartle §7.6, Examples 7.5–7.6, eqs. (7.31)–(7.38) (pp. 147–148) · Couzens, GR2, §5.4, eq. (5.52) (p. 108) · all values checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.3.2: Cross Product, Curl and Divergence as Hodge Duals in ℝ³
> *Setup.* $\mathbf R^3$ with the Euclidean metric and the orientation $(x, y, z)$. In Cartesian coordinates $\sqrt g = 1$, $g^{ij} = \delta^{ij}$, so raising changes no component and $\epsilon_{ijk} = \tilde\epsilon_{ijk}$ ([[#^def-c3-3-2|Def. §C3.3.2]]); $s = 1$ and $\star\star = 1$ in every degree ([[#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]).
>
> *The star on basis forms.* For $\omega = dx$, $\omega^i = \delta^i_1$ and $(\star dx)_{jk} = \omega^i\epsilon_{ijk} = \epsilon_{1jk}$ ([[#^def-c3-3-3|Def. §C3.3.3]]): the only independent component is $(\star dx)_{23} = 1$, so $\star dx = dy\wedge dz$. Cyclically, $\star dy = dz\wedge dx$, $\star dz = dx\wedge dy$. For $\omega = dx\wedge dy$, $\omega^{12} = -\omega^{21} = 1$ and $(\star\omega)_k = \frac12(\omega^{12}\epsilon_{12k} + \omega^{21}\epsilon_{21k}) = \epsilon_{12k}$, so $\star(dx\wedge dy) = dz$, and cyclically. Finally $\star1 = dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$ and $\star(dx\wedge dy\wedge dz) = 1$.
>
> *Cross product* (GR1, footnote 18). For vectors $\vec a$, $\vec b$ take the one-forms $a^\flat = a_i\,dx^i$, $b^\flat = b_i\,dx^i$. Their wedge has the components $(a^\flat\wedge b^\flat)_{ij} = a_ib_j - a_jb_i$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]]), and
>
> $$
> \bigl(\star(a^\flat\wedge b^\flat)\bigr)_k = \frac12\,(a_ib_j - a_jb_i)\,\epsilon_{ijk} = \epsilon_{ijk}\,a_ib_j = (\vec a\times\vec b)_k ,
> $$
>
> the two halves being equal after renaming $i \leftrightarrow j$. So $\vec a\times\vec b = \bigl(\star(a^\flat\wedge b^\flat)\bigr)^\sharp$: the cross product is the oriented area element $a^\flat\wedge b^\flat$, turned into a one-form by the star and into a vector by the metric. For $\vec a = (1, 2, 0)$, $\vec b = (0, 1, 3)$: $a^\flat\wedge b^\flat$ has the components $(a\wedge b)_{12} = 1\cdot1 - 2\cdot0 = 1$, $(a\wedge b)_{23} = 2\cdot3 - 0\cdot1 = 6$, $(a\wedge b)_{31} = 0\cdot0 - 1\cdot3 = -3$, and the star reads them off as $\vec a\times\vec b = (6, -3, 1)$. The construction needs a metric (to lower and raise) and an orientation (the sign of $\epsilon$): under a reflection $\vec a\times\vec b$ does not transform as a vector, the pseudovector property of [[#^cau-c3-3-1|Caution: The sign of ε on spacetime, and level B]].
>
> *Curl.* For $V^\flat = V_i\,dx^i$, $(dV^\flat)_{ij} = \partial_iV_j - \partial_jV_i$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]]), and by the same computation $(\star dV^\flat)_k = \epsilon_{ijk}\partial_iV_j = (\nabla\times\vec V)_k$: $\nabla\times\vec V = (\star dV^\flat)^\sharp$.
>
> *Divergence.* $\star V^\flat = V_x\,dy\wedge dz + V_y\,dz\wedge dx + V_z\,dx\wedge dy$, the two-form that [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-1|Example §C2.6.1]] identified with $\vec V$; its $d$ is $(\nabla\cdot\vec V)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$, and the star of that is $\nabla\cdot\vec V$. So $\nabla\cdot\vec V = \star d\star V^\flat = -d^\dagger V^\flat$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-6|Theorem §C3.3.6]], part 3, $d^\dagger = (-1)^p\star d\star$ with $p = 1$). With the gradient $(df)^\sharp$, the Laplacian is $\nabla^2f = \star d\star df = -\Delta f$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]]). The dictionary of Example §C2.6.1 is the Hodge star, and $d^2 = 0$ becomes $\nabla\times\nabla f = 0$, $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec V) = 0$.
>
> *Where the metric shows: spherical coordinates.* Take the rotation field $\vec V = -y\,\partial_x + x\,\partial_y$, whose curl is $(0, 0, 2)$. In spherical coordinates it is $\partial_\varphi$, and $V^\flat = g_{\varphi\varphi}\,d\varphi = r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi$. Then
>
> $$
> dV^\flat = 2r\sin^2\theta\;dr\wedge d\varphi + 2r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\;d\theta\wedge d\varphi .
> $$
>
> With $\epsilon_{r\theta\varphi} = r^2\sin\theta$ and $g^{rr} = 1$, $g^{\theta\theta} = r^{-2}$, $g^{\varphi\varphi} = (r\sin\theta)^{-2}$, the components $(\star\omega)_k = \frac12\omega^{ij}\epsilon_{ijk}$ are
>
> $$
> (\star dV^\flat)_r = (dV^\flat)^{\theta\varphi}\epsilon_{\theta\varphi r} = \frac{2r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r^2\cdot r^2\sin^2\theta}\cdot r^2\sin\theta = 2\cos\theta, \qquad (\star dV^\flat)_\theta = (dV^\flat)^{\varphi r}\epsilon_{\varphi r\theta} = -\frac{2r\sin^2\theta}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\cdot r^2\sin\theta = -2r\sin\theta ,
> $$
>
> and $(\star dV^\flat)_\varphi = 0$ (it would need $(dV^\flat)^{r\theta} = 0$). So $\star dV^\flat = 2\cos\theta\,dr - 2r\sin\theta\,d\theta = 2\,dz$, since $z = r\cos\theta$: the curl is $2\hat z$ in these coordinates too. The factors $r^2$, $\sin\theta$ that enter, and that make the curvilinear curl formulas of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|EM Theorem §B1.1.5]] look different from the Cartesian ones, all come from $\sqrt g$ and $g^{ij}$. The same way, $\star dr = r^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi$ and $\star d\star dr = \star\bigl(2r\sin\theta\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge d\varphi\bigr) = 2/r$, the divergence of the unit radial field.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, footnote 18 (p. 79) · Couzens, GR1, §3.4.2, Example 3.9 (pp. 63–64) · Hartle, Box 20.1, items (i)–(k) (pp. 437–438) · all components checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.3.3: Maxwell's Equations dF = 0 and d⋆F = μ₀⋆J
> *Setup.* Spacetime $(M, g)$, oriented, with the electromagnetic potential $A$, a one-form, its field strength $F = dA$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-3|Example §C2.6.3]]) and a current $J^\mu$, written as the one-form $J = J_\mu dx^\mu$. Level C's Maxwell equations in inertial coordinates are $\partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0J^\mu$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^ex-c1-2-4|Example §C1.2.4]]; [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-2|EM Theorem §C1.2.2]], signature $+{-}{-}{-}$ there); in a curved spacetime they read $\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_\nu(\sqrt{-g}F^{\mu\nu}) = \mu_0J^\mu$ (the coupling to curvature is §C4, planned). $G = c = 1$; restored, $x^0 = ct$ and $F_{0i} = -E_i/c$.
>
> *Step 1 (homogeneous pair).* $dF = d\,dA = 0$, with no metric: $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ and Faraday's law ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-3|Example §C2.6.3]]; [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-4|EM Theorem §C1.2.4]]).
>
> *Step 2 ($\star F$ in terms of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$).* In an inertial chart $F_{0i} = -E_i$, $F_{ij} = \epsilon_{ijk}B_k$, so $F^{0i} = E_i$, $F^{ij} = \epsilon_{ijk}B_k$, and with $\epsilon_{0123} = 1$ ([[#^def-c3-3-3|Def. §C3.3.3]]; a pair of indices moves past a pair with no sign, $\epsilon_{\mu\nu0i} = \epsilon_{0i\mu\nu}$),
>
> $$
> (\star F)_{0i} = \tfrac12F^{\mu\nu}\epsilon_{\mu\nu0i} = \tfrac12\epsilon_{jkl}B_l\,\epsilon_{0ijk} = \tfrac12\epsilon_{jkl}\epsilon_{ijk}B_l = B_i, \qquad (\star F)_{ij} = F^{0k}\epsilon_{0kij} = \epsilon_{kij}E_k = \epsilon_{ijk}E_k .
> $$
>
> So, with $\mathcal B = B_i\,dx^i$ and $\mathcal E = E_x\,dy\wedge dz + E_y\,dz\wedge dx + E_z\,dx\wedge dy$,
>
> $$
> F = -dt\wedge(E_i\,dx^i) + \bigl(B_x\,dy\wedge dz + \dots\bigr), \qquad \star F = dt\wedge\mathcal B + \mathcal E :
> $$
>
> the star replaces $(\vec E, \vec B)$ by $(-\vec B, \vec E)$, and twice by $(-\vec E, -\vec B)$, the $\star\star = -1$ of [[#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]], part 5. In particular $\star(dt\wedge dx) = -dy\wedge dz$ and $\star(dy\wedge dz) = dt\wedge dx$.
>
> *Step 3 (inhomogeneous pair as $d^\dagger F = \mu_0J$).* By [[#^thm-c3-3-6|Theorem §C3.3.6]] with $p = 2$, $(d^\dagger F)^\nu = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-g}F^{\mu\nu}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-g}F^{\nu\mu})$, so the Maxwell equation is $d^\dagger F = \mu_0J$. On spacetime $d^\dagger = \star d\star$ (part 3 of that theorem): $\star d\star F = \mu_0J$. Apply $\star$; on the three-form $d\star F$, $\star\star = +1$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]):
>
> $$
> d\star F = \mu_0\star J, \qquad dF = 0 .
> $$
>
> These hold in every chart of every spacetime; the metric enters only through $\star$.
>
> *Step 4 (check in an inertial frame).* $(\star J)_{\lambda\mu\nu} = J^\sigma\epsilon_{\sigma\lambda\mu\nu}$: $(\star J)_{123} = J^0 = \rho$ and $(\star J)_{023} = J^1\epsilon_{1023} = -J_x$, so $\star J = \rho\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz - dt\wedge(J_x\,dy\wedge dz + J_y\,dz\wedge dx + J_z\,dx\wedge dy)$. On the other side, split $d = dt\wedge\partial_t + d_s$ as in Example §C2.6.3: $d(dt\wedge\mathcal B) = -dt\wedge d_s\mathcal B$ and $d\mathcal E = dt\wedge\partial_t\mathcal E + (\nabla\cdot\vec E)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-1|Example §C2.6.1]]), where $d_s\mathcal B$ is the two-form of $\nabla\times\vec B$. Comparing the parts with and without $dt$:
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec E = \mu_0\rho, \qquad \partial_t\vec E - \nabla\times\vec B = -\mu_0\vec J ,
> $$
>
> Gauss's law and the Ampère–Maxwell law ($\mu_0\rho = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ at $c = 1$).
>
> *Step 5 (conservation and charge).* $d\star J = \mu_0^{-1}\,d\,d\star F = 0$, which in components is $\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-g}J^\mu) = 0$ ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]], part 1). On a slice $\Sigma = \{t = \text{const}\}$ oriented by $(x^1, x^2, x^3)$, $(\star J)_{123} = \sqrt{-g}\,J^0$, so $\int_\Sigma\star J = \int\sqrt{-g}\,J^0\,d^3x = Q$, the conserved charge of [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]], part 3. With Stokes' theorem ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]]), for a region $\Sigma_R$ of the slice with boundary sphere $S^2_R$ (outward normal first),
>
> $$
> Q = \int_{\Sigma_R}\star J = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\int_{\Sigma_R}d\star F = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\oint_{S^2_R}\star F :
> $$
>
> Gauss's law in integral form, on any spacetime.
>
> *Step 6 (Coulomb field).* In spherical coordinates of Minkowski spacetime, $A = -\frac{\mu_0Q}{4\pi r}\,dt$ (so $V = \mu_0Q/4\pi r = Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$). Then $F = dA = -\frac{\mu_0Q}{4\pi r^2}\,dt\wedge dr$, $F_{tr} = -E_r$. With $F^{tr} = g^{tt}g^{rr}F_{tr} = \frac{\mu_0Q}{4\pi r^2}$ and $\epsilon_{tr\theta\varphi} = r^2\sin\theta$,
>
> $$
> (\star F)_{\theta\varphi} = F^{tr}\,\epsilon_{tr\theta\varphi} = \frac{\mu_0Q}{4\pi r^2}\,r^2\sin\theta, \qquad \star F = \frac{\mu_0Q}{4\pi}\,\sin\theta\;d\theta\wedge d\varphi, \qquad \frac1{\mu_0}\oint_{S^2_R}\star F = \frac{Q}{4\pi}\cdot4\pi = Q ,
> $$
>
> for every $R$, and $d\star F = 0$ for $r > 0$ (GR2's Example 5.7, with $\mu_0/4\pi \to 1$).
>
> *Step 7 (the action and the invariants).* By [[#^thm-c3-3-5|Theorem §C3.3.5]], $F\wedge\star F = \frac12F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\,\mathrm{vol} = (B^2 - E^2)\,\mathrm{vol}$, and the Maxwell action is $-\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int F\wedge\star F$. The metric-free four-form $F\wedge F = -2\,\vec E\cdot\vec B\;\mathrm{vol}$ in an inertial frame, and $F_{\mu\nu}(\star F)^{\mu\nu} = +4\,\vec E\cdot\vec B$; level B's $F_{\mu\nu}\tilde F^{\mu\nu} = -4\,\vec E\cdot\vec B/c$ has the opposite sign because the two levels' $\epsilon$ differ by a sign ([[#^cau-c3-3-1|Caution: The sign of ε on spacetime, and level B]]).
>

> [!example] Example §C3.3.4: The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates from the Formula
> *Setup.* [[#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]], part 1, $\Delta f = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu f)$, for three diagonal metrics; for a diagonal metric $g^{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(1/g_{\mu\mu})$ and the double sum has only the terms $\mu = \nu$.
>
> *(a) $\mathbf R^3$, spherical coordinates.* $\sqrt g = r^2\sin\theta$, $g^{rr} = 1$, $g^{\theta\theta} = r^{-2}$, $g^{\varphi\varphi} = (r\sin\theta)^{-2}$. The three terms:
>
> $$
> \partial_r\bigl(r^2\sin\theta\,\partial_rf\bigr) = \sin\theta\,\partial_r(r^2\partial_rf), \qquad \partial_\theta\Bigl(r^2\sin\theta\cdot\frac{1}{r^2}\,\partial_\theta f\Bigr) = \partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f), \qquad \partial_\varphi\Bigl(r^2\sin\theta\cdot\frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\,\partial_\varphi f\Bigr) = \frac{\partial_\varphi^2f}{\sin\theta} .
> $$
>
> Dividing by $-r^2\sin\theta$,
>
> $$
> \Delta f = -\Bigl[\frac{1}{r^2}\partial_r\bigl(r^2\partial_rf\bigr) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\partial_\theta\bigl(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f\bigr) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\partial_\varphi^2f\Bigr] = -\nabla^2f ,
> $$
>
> the spherical Laplacian of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|EM Theorem §B1.1.5]], obtained without scale factors or unit vectors. Checks: $\Delta(1/r) = -r^{-2}\partial_r(r^2\cdot(-r^{-2})) = 0$ for $r > 0$; $\Delta r^2 = -r^{-2}\partial_r(2r^3) = -6$.
>
> *(b) The sphere of radius $a$.* $\sqrt g = a^2\sin\theta$, $g^{\theta\theta} = a^{-2}$, $g^{\varphi\varphi} = (a\sin\theta)^{-2}$:
>
> $$
> \Delta f = -\frac{1}{a^2}\Bigl[\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_\theta\bigl(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f\bigr) + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\partial_\varphi^2f\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> For $f = \cos\theta$: $\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta\cos\theta = -\sin^2\theta$, $\partial_\theta(-\sin^2\theta) = -2\sin\theta\cos\theta$, so $\Delta\cos\theta = \frac{2}{a^2}\cos\theta$. For $f = 3\cos^2\theta - 1$: $\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f = -6\sin^2\theta\cos\theta$, $\partial_\theta(-6\sin^2\theta\cos\theta) = -12\sin\theta\cos^2\theta + 6\sin^3\theta = \sin\theta\,(6 - 18\cos^2\theta)$, so $\Delta f = -\frac{1}{a^2}(6 - 18\cos^2\theta) = \frac{6}{a^2}f$. The eigenvalues $\ell(\ell + 1)/a^2$ with $\ell = 1, 2$ are positive, as part 4 of the theorem requires; these are the spherical harmonics of quantum mechanics, where $L^2 = \hbar^2a^2\Delta$ on the sphere of radius $a$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-7|QM Theorem §B5.2.7]]).
>
> *(c) Minkowski spacetime, spherical coordinates.* $\sqrt{-g} = r^2\sin\theta$ and $g^{tt} = -1$; the $t$-term is $-\frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\partial_t\bigl(r^2\sin\theta\cdot(-1)\,\partial_tf\bigr) = \partial_t^2f$, and the spatial terms are those of (a). So $\Delta f = \partial_t^2f - \nabla^2f = -\Box f$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]], part 3), and a spherical wave $f = h(t - r)/r$ solves $\Delta f = 0$, since $\nabla^2(h(t - r)/r) = r^{-1}\partial_r^2h(t - r) = \partial_t^2f$ by the first term of (a).
>
> *(d) Positivity, a number.* On $\mathbf R^3$ with $f = e^{-r^2}$: $\partial_rf = -2re^{-r^2}$, and $(f, \Delta f) = (df, df) = 4\pi\int_0^\infty4r^4e^{-2r^2}\,dr = 16\pi\cdot\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{64} = \frac{3\sqrt2\,\pi^{3/2}}{4} \approx 5.906 > 0$, using $\int_0^\infty r^4e^{-2r^2}\,dr = \frac{3\sqrt\pi}{8\cdot2^{5/2}} = \frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{64}$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.37) (p. 79) · Hartle, Box 20.1, item (j) (p. 437) · all four parts checked by script*
