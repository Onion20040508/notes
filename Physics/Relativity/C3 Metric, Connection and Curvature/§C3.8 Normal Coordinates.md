[[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames|§C3.2]] showed that around every event $p$ there are coordinates in which $g_{\mu\nu}(p) = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ and $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]]), and read this as the Einstein equivalence principle in geometric form ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]]; [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]). The argument counted Taylor coefficients. It shows that such coordinates exist; it does not say how an observer at $p$ would lay them out, and it says nothing definite about the next order. [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics|§C3.7]] supplied what an observer actually has: free test bodies move on geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]). The questions of this section are: *can a freely falling observer build a local inertial frame out of free fall alone, and once she has, what does the metric look like at second order around $p$, that is, which part of gravity is left over?*

First, what goes wrong without a rule that fixes the coordinates. Start in any chart with $p$ at the origin and $g(p) = \eta$. The change $x'^\mu = x^\mu + \tfrac12\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(p)\,x^\alpha x^\beta$ makes $\Gamma'(p) = 0$ by the inhomogeneous transformation law ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]]; d'Inverno & Vickers call the result geodesic coordinates), and then $\partial g'(p) = 0$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]], part 3). But the cubic terms of the change are free, and they move the second derivatives. Take the flat plane with Cartesian coordinates $(x, y)$ and new coordinates $(X, Y)$ with

$$
x = X + \tfrac16 kX^3, \quad y = Y \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad dx = \bigl(1 + \tfrac12kX^2\bigr)dX, \qquad ds^2 = \bigl(1 + \tfrac12kX^2\bigr)^2dX^2 + dY^2 .
$$

So $g_{XX} = 1 + kX^2 + \tfrac14k^2X^4$, $g_{XY} = 0$, $g_{YY} = 1$. At the origin $g = \delta$ and every first derivative vanishes, and the only nonzero Christoffel symbol, $\Gamma^X{}_{XX} = \tfrac12g^{XX}\partial_Xg_{XX} = kX/(1 + \tfrac12kX^2)$, vanishes there: $(X, Y)$ pass every test of a local inertial frame at the origin. Yet

$$
\partial_X\partial_Xg_{XX}(0) = 2k, \qquad \partial_X\Gamma^X{}_{XX}(0) = k ,
$$

for any $k$, on a plane whose curvature is zero. The second derivatives of the metric at $p$ say nothing about the geometry until the cubic terms of the coordinates are fixed; in four dimensions these are the $80$ free coefficients of the counting in [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]]. The defect shows in the geodesics. The straight line $x = \lambda$, $y = 0$ through the origin has $X(\lambda) = \lambda - \tfrac16k\lambda^3 + O(\lambda^5)$: in the coordinates $(X, Y)$ it is a line through the origin, but it is not traversed at constant coordinate speed. (All of this was checked by script.)

That suggests the rule. Lay out the coordinates with the geodesics themselves: a geodesic leaving $p$ with initial velocity $v$ is to be the straight line $y^\mu = \lambda v^\mu$ through the origin, at constant coordinate speed. The map that sends $v$ to the point reached is the exponential map ([[#^def-c3-8-1|Def. §C3.8.1]]); its inverse, read in an orthonormal basis at $p$, gives Riemann normal coordinates ([[#^def-c3-8-2|Def. §C3.8.2]]). They exist around every point and are a local inertial frame there, now constructed rather than counted ([[#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]]). Because they are fixed to all orders, their second-order metric is a geometric statement: $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac13R_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta}\,y^\alpha y^\beta + O(y^3)$, so what free fall leaves of gravity at $p$ is the Riemann tensor ([[#^thm-c3-8-2|Theorem §C3.8.2]]). Normal coordinates belong to a freely falling observer and her choice of axes, and two observers at the same event are related by a Lorentz transformation ([[#^thm-c3-8-3|Theorem §C3.8.3]]). A remark and a caution place this against the equivalence principle of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle|§C1.3]]. The examples are the sphere at its north pole (Hartle's example) and the weak-field metric of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric|§C1.5]] at one event, where the construction reproduces Newton's freely falling frame and leaves exactly the tidal tensor.

**Setup.** A smooth manifold $M$ of dimension $n$ with a metric $g$ of any signature ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]], [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]) and its Levi-Civita connection ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-1|Theorem §C3.6.1]]), which is torsion-free and metric-compatible. Spacetime is the case $n = 4$, Lorentzian. Everything happens in a neighbourhood of one point $p$. All statements are exact theorems of differential geometry; [[#^thm-c3-8-2|Theorem §C3.8.2]] is a Taylor expansion about $p$ with a remainder of third order in the normal coordinates. That theorem uses the Riemann tensor, whose geometric definition comes in [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor|§C3.9]]. Only its component formula ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]], displayed in the conventions line below) and its antisymmetry in the last pair of indices, which is visible in that formula, are needed for the derivation; the final symmetric form also uses the first-pair antisymmetry and the pair symmetry of [[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities|§C3.10]]. The statement is placed here because it is a property of normal coordinates. The examples are exact for the sphere and first order in $\Phi$ for the weak field. Numbers and identities were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); for a Riemannian metric read $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \delta_{\mu\nu}$; $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]). Connection coefficients with the derivative index first, $\nabla_\mu\partial_\nu = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}\partial_\lambda$ ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-2|Def. §C3.5.2]]); Riemann tensor $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\sigma} + \Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\sigma}$, lowered as $R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = g_{\rho\lambda}R^\lambda{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}$ (MTW sign: the sphere has positive curvature). Greek indices are components in a chart or in normal coordinates $y^\mu$, $\mu = 0, \dots, n - 1$ (Riemannian: $1, \dots, n$); Latin $i, j, k, l = 1, 2, 3$ are spatial; $A, B = 1, 2$ on the sphere. $O(y^k)$ means terms of total degree at least $k$ in the $y^\mu$. A dot is $d/d\lambda$.

## The exponential map and normal coordinates

A geodesic is fixed by its starting point and its initial velocity ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]], part 3). So at a fixed $p$ every tangent vector names a geodesic, and following it for one unit of affine parameter names a point. This is the map the opening asked for.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.8.1: Exponential Map
> *Structure.* Let $p \in M$. For each tangent vector $v \in T_pM$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]) let $\gamma_v$ be the affinely parametrized geodesic ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]) with
>
> $$
> \gamma_v(0) = p, \qquad \dot\gamma_v(0) = v ,
> $$
>
> on its maximal interval of definition (unique by [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]], part 3). Let $\mathcal E_p \subset T_pM$ be the set of $v$ for which $\gamma_v$ is defined at least on $[0, 1]$. The **exponential map at $p$** is
>
> $$
> \exp_p : \mathcal E_p \to M, \qquad \exp_p(v) = \gamma_v(1) .
> $$
>
> It sends a vector at $p$, an element of the vector space $T_pM$, to a point of $M$: start at $p$, follow the geodesic with initial velocity $v$, stop after one unit of affine parameter. $\exp_p(0) = p$, because $\gamma_0$ is the constant curve.
>
> *Components.* In a chart $x^\mu$ around $p$, $\exp_p(v)$ is the point with coordinates $x^\mu(1)$, where $x^\mu(\lambda)$ solves the geodesic equation with initial data $x^\mu(p)$ and $v^\mu$:
>
> $$
> \ddot x^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(x)\,\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta = 0, \qquad x^\mu(0) = x^\mu(p), \qquad \dot x^\mu(0) = v^\mu .
> $$
>
> *Name.* On a matrix group the curves $t \mapsto \exp(tA)$ ([[§71 The Exponential Map#^def-71-1|591 Def. §71.1]]) start at the identity with velocity $A$; here $\lambda \mapsto \exp_p(\lambda v)$ is the geodesic that starts at $p$ with velocity $v$ ([[#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]], part 1). The notation $\exp(tX)$ for the flow of a vector field in GR1 is a different use ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^cau-c2-5-1|§C2.5, Caution: Flows need not be global; exp(tX) is notation]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.2, p. 91 (named; "we will not consider this construction here") · Hartle, Gravity, §8.4, eq. (8.44), Fig. 8.1, p. 180 (as the point reached at distance $s$ along a unit direction $n$)*

^def-c3-8-1

> [!definition] Definition §C3.8.2: Riemann Normal Coordinates
> *Structure.* Fix $p \in M$ and an orthonormal basis $(e_0, \dots, e_{n-1})$ of $T_pM$,
>
> $$
> g(e_\mu, e_\nu) = \eta_{\mu\nu}
> $$
>
> (one exists: [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]; [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]). Let $V \subset \mathcal E_p$ be an open set containing $0$ that is **star-shaped** about $0$ ($\lambda v \in V$ whenever $v \in V$ and $0 \le \lambda \le 1$), such that $\exp_p$ ([[#^def-c3-8-1|Def. §C3.8.1]]) restricted to $V$ is a diffeomorphism ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-6|Def. §C2.1.6]]) onto an open set $N \ni p$. Then $N$ is a **normal neighbourhood** of $p$, and the **Riemann normal coordinates** $y^\mu(q)$ of a point $q \in N$ are the components, in the basis $(e_\mu)$, of the vector that $\exp_p$ sends to $q$:
>
> $$
> q = \exp_p\bigl(y^\mu(q)\,e_\mu\bigr), \qquad y^\mu(p) = 0 .
> $$
>
> The chart is $(N, y)$ with $y = L\circ(\exp_p|_V)^{-1}$, where $L : T_pM \to \mathbf R^n$, $v^\mu e_\mu \mapsto (v^\mu)$, is the linear isomorphism given by the basis ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]). Normal neighbourhoods exist around every point ([[#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]], part 3).
>
> *Reading.* $y^\mu(q)$ is the initial velocity, in the observer's basis, of the geodesic that runs from $p$ to $q$ in unit affine parameter inside $N$. For a unit vector $n$ ($g(n, n) = \pm 1$), $\exp_p(sn)$ is the point reached after arc length or proper time $s$ along the geodesic leaving $p$ in the direction $n$ ([[#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]], part 1; [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-7|Theorem §C3.7.7]], part 3), so its normal coordinates are $y^\mu = s\,n^\mu$: Hartle's description. For null $v$ there is no length, and the exponential map covers that case without a separate rule.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, §8.4, eq. (8.44), Fig. 8.1, p. 180 (orthonormal basis of the observer at $P$; "filling in by continuity if it is null") · Couzens, GR1, §4.4.2, p. 91 (geodesics through $p$ label the point $q$)*

The definition presupposes that $\exp_p$ is a diffeomorphism near $0$. The theorem shows it is, and computes what the metric and the connection are at $p$ in the coordinates it produces.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.8.1: Normal Coordinates Exist; at p They Give g = η, Γ = 0, ∂g = 0
> Let $p \in M$ and let $\gamma_v$, $\mathcal E_p$, $\exp_p$ be as in [[#^def-c3-8-1|Def. §C3.8.1]].
> 1. **Rescaled geodesics.** For every constant $c$, $\gamma_{cv}(\lambda) = \gamma_v(c\lambda)$, where either side is defined. Hence $\mathcal E_p$ is star-shaped about $0$, and
>
> $$
> \exp_p(\lambda v) = \gamma_v(\lambda), \qquad 0 \le \lambda \le 1,\ v \in \mathcal E_p :
> $$
>
> the geodesic with initial velocity $v$ is the image of the ray $\lambda \mapsto \lambda v$.
> 2. **The exponential map in a chart.** In any chart $x^\mu$ around $p$,
>
> $$
> x^\mu\bigl(\exp_p v\bigr) = x^\mu(p) + v^\mu - \tfrac12\,\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(p)\,v^\alpha v^\beta + O(|v|^3) .
> $$
>
> 3. **Normal neighbourhoods exist.** $\mathcal E_p$ contains an open neighbourhood of $0$ on which $\exp_p$ is smooth, its derivative at $v = 0$ is the identity of $T_pM$, and there is an open ball $V$ about $0$ (in the components $y^\mu$ of any basis) on which $\exp_p$ is a diffeomorphism onto an open set $N \ni p$. So Riemann normal coordinates ([[#^def-c3-8-2|Def. §C3.8.2]]) exist around every point.
> 4. **Geodesics through $p$ are straight lines.** In normal coordinates the geodesic with initial velocity $v = v^\mu e_\mu \in V$ is
>
> $$
> y^\mu(\lambda) = \lambda\,v^\mu, \qquad 0 \le \lambda \le 1 .
> $$
>
> 5. **Flat to first order at $p$.** In normal coordinates the coordinate basis at $p$ is the chosen orthonormal basis, $\partial/\partial y^\mu|_p = e_\mu$, and
>
> $$
> g_{\mu\nu}(p) = \eta_{\mu\nu}, \qquad \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0, \qquad \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0 .
> $$
>
> Normal coordinates are therefore a local inertial frame at $p$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-4|Def. §C3.2.4]]): an explicit construction of the coordinates whose existence [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]] established by counting. Because every first derivative of the metric can be removed at a point, a gravitational wave has no energy density built from them ([[§C8.4 The Energy of Gravitational Waves#^thm-c8-4-1|Theorem §C8.4.1]]).
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a manifold with a metric and its Levi-Civita connection; *applies:* exactly, in every signature, in a normal neighbourhood of $p$. Parts 1–4 hold for any connection; $\Gamma(p) = 0$ uses zero torsion, and $\partial g(p) = 0$ also metric compatibility.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, §8.4, eqs. (8.44)–(8.47), Fig. 8.1, pp. 180–181 (parts 4–5, argued with unit directions) · Couzens, GR1, §4.4.2, eq. (4.92), pp. 89–90 (part 5 stated; existence by counting, (4.93)–(4.96)) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.6, eqs. (6.42)–(6.43), pp. 95–96 ($\Gamma(P) = 0$ by a quadratic change of coordinates) · parts 1–3 written out here; part 2 and its next term checked by script against numerically integrated geodesics*

^thm-c3-8-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (rescaling, part 1). Fix $v$ and a constant $c$, and put $\sigma(\lambda) = \gamma_v(c\lambda)$. In a chart, with $x^\mu(\lambda)$ the coordinates of $\gamma_v$, the chain rule gives
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma^\mu}{d\lambda} = c\,\dot x^\mu(c\lambda), \qquad \frac{d^2\sigma^\mu}{d\lambda^2} = c^2\,\ddot x^\mu(c\lambda) ,
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2\sigma^\mu}{d\lambda^2} + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(\sigma)\,\frac{d\sigma^\alpha}{d\lambda}\frac{d\sigma^\beta}{d\lambda} = c^2\Bigl[\ddot x^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(x)\,\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta\Bigr](c\lambda) = 0 .
> $$
>
> $\sigma$ is an affinely parametrized geodesic ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]], part 1) with $\sigma(0) = p$ and $\dot\sigma(0) = c\,v$. By uniqueness (same theorem, part 3) it is $\gamma_{cv}$; $\gamma_{cv}$ is defined at $\lambda$ exactly when $\gamma_v$ is defined at $c\lambda$. If $v \in \mathcal E_p$ and $0 \le c \le 1$, then $c\lambda \in [0, 1]$ for $\lambda \in [0, 1]$, so $cv \in \mathcal E_p$. Finally $\exp_p(\lambda v) = \gamma_{\lambda v}(1) = \gamma_v(\lambda)$.
>
> **Step 2** (Taylor series in the affine parameter). For fixed $v$ the coordinates $x^\mu(\lambda)$ of $\gamma_v$ are smooth, with $x^\mu(0) = x^\mu(p)$, $\dot x^\mu(0) = v^\mu$, and, from the geodesic equation at $\lambda = 0$, $\ddot x^\mu(0) = -\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(p)v^\alpha v^\beta$. Taylor's theorem in $\lambda$:
>
> $$
> x^\mu\bigl(\gamma_v(\lambda)\bigr) = x^\mu(p) + \lambda\,v^\mu - \tfrac12\lambda^2\,\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(p)\,v^\alpha v^\beta + O(\lambda^3) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (smoothness in $v$). Write the geodesic equation as a first-order system for $(x^\mu, u^\mu)$ on $U \times \mathbf R^n$, $U$ the image of the chart:
>
> $$
> \frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda} = u^\mu, \qquad \frac{du^\mu}{d\lambda} = -\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(x)\,u^\alpha u^\beta .
> $$
>
> The right-hand side is a smooth vector field on $U \times \mathbf R^n$. Its flow $\Psi$ is defined on an open flow domain in $\mathbf R \times U \times \mathbf R^n$ and is smooth there ([[§69 Maximal Integral Curves and Flows#^thm-69-4|591 Thm. §69.4]], part 2). The constant curve $(x(p), 0)$ is an integral curve for all $\lambda$, so $(1, x(p), 0)$ lies in the flow domain, and so does $(1, x(p), v)$ for all $v$ in an open neighbourhood $W$ of $0$. For $v \in W$, $x(\exp_pv)$ is the $x$-part of $\Psi_1(x(p), v)$: the map $F^\mu(v) \equiv x^\mu(\exp_pv)$ is smooth on $W$.
>
> **Step 4** (the expansion in $v$, part 2). By Step 1, $F(\lambda v) = x(\gamma_v(\lambda))$. Expand $F$ about $0$ to second order with a cubic remainder ([[§11 Taylor's Theorem for Multivariable Functions#^thm-11-2|452 Thm. §11.2]], in $n$ variables) and set the argument to $\lambda v$:
>
> $$
> F^\mu(\lambda v) = F^\mu(0) + \lambda\,\frac{\partial F^\mu}{\partial v^\alpha}(0)\,v^\alpha + \tfrac12\lambda^2\,\frac{\partial^2F^\mu}{\partial v^\alpha\partial v^\beta}(0)\,v^\alpha v^\beta + O(\lambda^3) .
> $$
>
> Compare the coefficients of $\lambda$ and $\lambda^2$ with Step 2, for every $v$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial F^\mu}{\partial v^\alpha}(0)\,v^\alpha = v^\mu, \qquad \frac{\partial^2F^\mu}{\partial v^\alpha\partial v^\beta}(0)\,v^\alpha v^\beta = -\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(p)\,v^\alpha v^\beta .
> $$
>
> Inserting these into the Taylor expansion at $\lambda = 1$ gives part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (local diffeomorphism, part 3). The first relation of Step 4, for all $v$, says $\partial F^\mu/\partial v^\alpha(0) = \delta^\mu_\alpha$: the derivative of $\exp_p$ at $0$, written in the chart, is the identity matrix, in particular invertible. By the inverse function theorem ([[§35 Local Diffeomorphisms#^thm-35-1|591 Thm. §35.1]]) there are open sets $W' \ni 0$ in $T_pM$ and $N' \ni p$ such that $\exp_p : W' \to N'$ is bijective with smooth inverse. In the components $y^\mu$ of a basis, take for $V$ an open ball $\sum_\mu (y^\mu)^2 < r^2$ inside $W'$; a ball is star-shaped about its centre, and $\exp_p$ restricted to $V$ is a diffeomorphism onto the open set $N = \exp_p(V)$.
>
> **Step 6** (straight lines, part 4). Let $v = v^\mu e_\mu \in V$ and $0 \le \lambda \le 1$. Then $\lambda v \in V$ ($V$ is star-shaped) and $\gamma_v(\lambda) = \exp_p(\lambda v)$ (Step 1). By [[#^def-c3-8-2|Def. §C3.8.2]] the normal coordinates of $\exp_p(\lambda v)$ are the components of $\lambda v$:
>
> $$
> y^\mu\bigl(\gamma_v(\lambda)\bigr) = \lambda\,v^\mu .
> $$
>
> **Step 7** (the basis and the metric at $p$). Take $v = c\,e_\nu$ with $c > 0$ small enough that $v \in V$. By Step 6 the curve $\gamma_v$ has $y^\mu(\lambda) = \lambda c\,\delta^\mu_\nu$, so its velocity at $\lambda = 0$ has the components $dy^\mu/d\lambda = c\,\delta^\mu_\nu$ in the coordinate basis ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]): $\dot\gamma_v(0) = c\,\partial/\partial y^\nu|_p$. It is also $\dot\gamma_v(0) = v = c\,e_\nu$ ([[#^def-c3-8-1|Def. §C3.8.1]]). Hence $\partial/\partial y^\nu|_p = e_\nu$, and with the components of the metric ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]])
>
> $$
> g_{\mu\nu}(p) = g\bigl(\partial_\mu|_p, \partial_\nu|_p\bigr) = g(e_\mu, e_\nu) = \eta_{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> **Step 8** ($\Gamma(p) = 0$). For $v \in V$ the curve $y^\mu(\lambda) = \lambda v^\mu$ is a geodesic (Step 6), and $d^2y^\mu/d\lambda^2 = 0$, so the geodesic equation ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]) reduces to
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(\lambda v)\,v^\alpha v^\beta = 0, \qquad 0 \le \lambda \le 1,\ v \in V .
> $$
>
> At $\lambda = 0$: $Q^\mu(v) \equiv \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(p)\,v^\alpha v^\beta = 0$ for all $v \in V$, hence for all $v$, since $Q^\mu(v) = Q^\mu(cv)/c^2$ and $cv \in V$ for small $c > 0$. Differentiating twice with respect to the components,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2Q^\mu}{\partial v^\rho\,\partial v^\sigma} = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\rho\sigma}(p) + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\sigma\rho}(p) = 2\,\Gamma^\mu{}_{\rho\sigma}(p) = 0 ,
> $$
>
> using that the Levi-Civita coefficients are symmetric in their lower indices, zero torsion ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]], part 2; [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^def-c3-6-1|Def. §C3.6.1]]). The same follows from part 2 applied in the normal chart itself, where $y^\mu(\exp_pv) = v^\mu$ exactly, so the quadratic term $-\tfrac12\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(p)v^\alpha v^\beta$ must vanish.
>
> **Step 9** ($\partial g(p) = 0$). Metric compatibility in components, $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma_{\nu\rho\mu} + \Gamma_{\mu\rho\nu}$ with $\Gamma_{\nu\rho\mu} = g_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu}$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]], part 3), gives $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$ by Step 8. This completes part 5.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Two inputs do all the work: the geodesic equation is invariant under $\lambda \to c\lambda$, $v \to cv$ (Step 1), and the derivative of $\exp_p$ at $0$ is the identity (Steps 4–5). The first makes the geodesics through $p$ into the rays of a vector space; the second makes those rays into coordinates. In these coordinates free fall through $p$ has zero coordinate acceleration at $p$ (Step 8), which is the statement that $\Gamma(p) = 0$.

> - Couzens (GR1 §4.4.2) calls any coordinates with $g_{\mu\nu}(p) = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ and $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$ (his (4.92)) "normal coordinates", proves their existence by counting and names the construction by the exponential map without carrying it out. Hartle calls such coordinates a **local inertial frame** at $p$ ((7.13); here [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-4|Def. §C3.2.4]]) and reserves **Riemann normal coordinates** for the construction of [[#^def-c3-8-2|Def. §C3.8.2]]. These notes follow Hartle: "normal coordinates" always means [[#^def-c3-8-2|Def. §C3.8.2]].
> - d'Inverno & Vickers (§6.6) call coordinates with $\Gamma(P) = 0$, obtained by the quadratic change $x' = x + \tfrac12Q\,xx$ with $Q = \Gamma(P)$, **geodesic coordinates**; their chapter has a connection before a metric, so nothing is said about $g(P)$.
> - Hartle (§8.4) calls coordinates in which $\Gamma$ vanishes all along one geodesic a **freely falling frame**; his footnote names the usual terms (Fermi normal coordinates, proper reference frame) and notes that some texts call any local inertial frame "freely falling". These notes do not construct them ([[#^cau-c3-8-2|Caution: Normal coordinates at one event are not a laboratory frame for a finite time]]).
> - What depends on the choice. Arguments that use only $\Gamma(p) = 0$ or $\partial g(p) = 0$ hold in every local inertial frame: the Riemann tensor at $p$ as $\partial\Gamma - \partial\Gamma$ ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]], part 1) and the proof of the second Bianchi identity ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-5|Theorem §C3.10.5]]). Statements about $\partial\Gamma(p)$ or $\partial\partial g(p)$ themselves, such as [[#^thm-c3-8-2|Theorem §C3.8.2]], hold only in Riemann normal coordinates; the coordinates $(X, Y)$ of the opening are a local inertial frame in which they fail.
>

## The metric to second order: what is left is curvature

[[#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]] used only the value of the condition $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(\lambda v)v^\alpha v^\beta = 0$ at $\lambda = 0$. The condition holds along every radial geodesic, so its derivatives at $p$ say more. They fix the first derivatives of $\Gamma$, and with them the second derivatives of the metric, entirely in terms of the Riemann tensor.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.8.2: The Metric to Second Order Is Fixed by the Curvature
> In Riemann normal coordinates $y^\mu$ at $p$ ([[#^def-c3-8-2|Def. §C3.8.2]]), let $R^\lambda{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}$ be the Riemann tensor at $p$ (components by [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]]; the tensor is [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^def-c3-9-1|Def. §C3.9.1]]) and $R_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta} = \eta_{\mu\lambda}R^\lambda{}_{\alpha\nu\beta}$ its lowered components at $p$. Then:
> 1. **the connection to first order:**
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}(y) = -\tfrac13\bigl(R^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu\rho} + R^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu\rho}\bigr)\,y^\rho + O(y^2) ;
> $$
>
> 2. **the metric to second order:**
>
> $$
> g_{\mu\nu}(y) = \eta_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac13\,R_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta}\,y^\alpha y^\beta + O(y^3) ;
> $$
>
> 3. ⚑ **the second derivatives of the metric at $p$ are the curvature:**
>
> $$
> \partial_\alpha\partial_\beta g_{\mu\nu}(p) = -\tfrac13\bigl(R_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta} + R_{\mu\beta\nu\alpha}\bigr), \qquad R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_\mu\partial_\sigma g_{\rho\nu} - \partial_\mu\partial_\rho g_{\sigma\nu} - \partial_\nu\partial_\sigma g_{\rho\mu} + \partial_\nu\partial_\rho g_{\sigma\mu}\bigr)(p) .
> $$
>
> In four dimensions the $100$ second derivatives $\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta g_{\mu\nu}(p)$ are linear in the $20$ independent components of $R(p)$ ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-4|Theorem §C3.10.4]]) and give them back. The $80$ third-order coefficients that the counting of [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]] leaves free are fixed by the geodesics, and what remains at second order is the curvature and nothing else.
>
> The metric is flat to first order at every point in suitable coordinates; at second order the deviation from $\eta$ is the Riemann tensor, and it vanishes for every $p$ exactly when $R = 0$.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a manifold with a metric and its Levi-Civita connection; *applies:* in Riemann normal coordinates at $p$ only (in the coordinates $(X, Y)$ of the opening, part 2 fails), any signature; the remainders are governed by the derivatives of $R$ at $p$.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, §21.3, eq. (21.28), p. 454 (the second formula of part 3, in any local inertial frame; Problem 21.6) · Couzens, GR1, §4.4.2, pp. 90–91 ("one cannot generically make the second derivative of the metric vanish at $p$"; the counting (4.96)) · Schutz §6.2, p. 149 ("the 20 second derivatives … the independent components of a tensor which represents the curvature") · the coefficient $-\tfrac13$ is derived here (none of the sources derives it); checked by script on generic metrics in three dimensions (Riemannian and Lorentzian) and four dimensions (Lorentzian), and on the sphere ([[#^ex-c3-8-1|Example §C3.8.1]])*

^thm-c3-8-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the normal-coordinate condition). By [[#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]], part 4, every $y^\mu(s) = s\,v^\mu$ with $v \in V$ is a geodesic, and $d^2y^\mu/ds^2 = 0$, so the geodesic equation ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]) gives
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}(s\,v)\,v^\mu v^\nu = 0, \qquad 0 \le s \le 1,\ v \in V .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (its first derivative at $p$). Differentiate with respect to $s$ at $s = 0$ (chain rule, $d\Gamma(sv)/ds = \partial_\rho\Gamma\,v^\rho$):
>
> $$
> \partial_\rho\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}(p)\,v^\rho v^\mu v^\nu = 0 \qquad\text{for all } v \in V ,
> $$
>
> hence for all $v$, since the left side is homogeneous of degree $3$ in $v$. Differentiate this cubic form once with respect to each of $v^\rho$, $v^\mu$, $v^\nu$: the result is the sum of the coefficients over the six orderings of $(\rho, \mu, \nu)$ in the three slots (derivative index, first lower, second lower). The coefficients are symmetric in the two lower indices ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]), so the six orderings come in equal pairs:
>
> $$
> 2\bigl(\partial_\rho\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\rho} + \partial_\nu\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu}\bigr)(p) = 0 . \tag{C}
> $$
>
> Only the totally symmetrized derivative of $\Gamma$ vanishes at $p$, not $\partial\Gamma$ itself.
>
> **Step 3** (the Riemann tensor at $p$). In the component formula the products $\Gamma\Gamma$ vanish at $p$ ([[#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]], part 5):
>
> $$
> R^\lambda{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}(p) = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma}(p) - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\sigma}(p) .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (solve for $\partial\Gamma$). Write Step 3 for the index sets $(\sigma, \mu, \nu) = (\mu, \rho, \nu)$ and $(\nu, \rho, \mu)$, everything at $p$:
>
> $$
> R^\lambda{}_{\mu\rho\nu} = \partial_\rho\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu}, \qquad R^\lambda{}_{\nu\rho\mu} = \partial_\rho\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\mu\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\nu} .
> $$
>
> Add them, using $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu} = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}$ and $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\nu} = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\rho}$, then (C) in the form $-\partial_\mu\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\rho} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu} = \partial_\rho\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}$:
>
> $$
> R^\lambda{}_{\mu\rho\nu} + R^\lambda{}_{\nu\rho\mu} = 2\,\partial_\rho\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\mu\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\rho} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu} = 2\,\partial_\rho\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\rho\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = 3\,\partial_\rho\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> The component formula is antisymmetric in its last two indices (exchanging $\mu \leftrightarrow \nu$ in it exchanges the two derivative terms and the two product terms), so $R^\lambda{}_{\mu\rho\nu} = -R^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu\rho}$ and
>
> $$
> \partial_\rho\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}(p) = \tfrac13\bigl(R^\lambda{}_{\mu\rho\nu} + R^\lambda{}_{\nu\rho\mu}\bigr) = -\tfrac13\bigl(R^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu\rho} + R^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu\rho}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (part 1). Taylor's theorem about $p$ with $\Gamma(p) = 0$: $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}(y) = \partial_\rho\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}(p)\,y^\rho + O(y^2)$; insert Step 4.
>
> **Step 6** (metric compatibility, differentiated). In every chart $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = g_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu} + g_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\nu}$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]], part 3). Differentiate with respect to $y^\alpha$ and evaluate at $p$, where $\Gamma = 0$ removes the terms $(\partial_\alpha g)\,\Gamma$ and $g = \eta$:
>
> $$
> \partial_\alpha\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}(p) = \eta_{\nu\lambda}\,\partial_\alpha\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu}(p) + \eta_{\mu\lambda}\,\partial_\alpha\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\nu}(p) .
> $$
>
> **Step 7** (insert Step 4). With the indices renamed, $\partial_\alpha\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu}(p) = -\tfrac13(R^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu\alpha} + R^\lambda{}_{\mu\rho\alpha})$; lowering with $\eta$, $\eta_{\nu\lambda}R^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu\alpha} = R_{\nu\rho\mu\alpha}$:
>
> $$
> \partial_\alpha\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}(p) = -\tfrac13\bigl(R_{\nu\rho\mu\alpha} + R_{\nu\mu\rho\alpha} + R_{\mu\rho\nu\alpha} + R_{\mu\nu\rho\alpha}\bigr) . \tag{D}
> $$
>
> **Step 8** (Taylor expansion of the metric). By [[#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]], part 5, the zeroth-order term is $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ and the first-order term vanishes:
>
> $$
> g_{\mu\nu}(y) = \eta_{\mu\nu} + \tfrac12\,\partial_\alpha\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}(p)\,y^\alpha y^\rho + O(y^3) .
> $$
>
> Insert (D). The second and fourth terms of (D) are antisymmetric in $(\rho, \alpha)$ and are contracted with the symmetric $y^\alpha y^\rho$, so they drop out: $R_{\nu\mu\rho\alpha}y^\alpha y^\rho = -R_{\nu\mu\alpha\rho}y^\alpha y^\rho = -R_{\nu\mu\rho\alpha}y^\rho y^\alpha$ is its own negative. Renaming the summed pair $(\rho, \alpha) \to (\alpha, \beta)$,
>
> $$
> g_{\mu\nu}(y) = \eta_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac16\bigl(R_{\nu\rho\mu\alpha} + R_{\mu\rho\nu\alpha}\bigr)y^\rho y^\alpha + O(y^3) = \eta_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac16\bigl(R_{\nu\alpha\mu\beta} + R_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta}\bigr)y^\alpha y^\beta + O(y^3) .
> $$
>
> This form uses nothing beyond the component formula and is visibly symmetric in $\mu \leftrightarrow \nu$.
>
> **Step 9** (part 2). By pair symmetry ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-3|Theorem §C3.10.3]]) $R_{\nu\alpha\mu\beta} = R_{\mu\beta\nu\alpha}$, and renaming $\alpha \leftrightarrow \beta$ in the symmetric contraction, $R_{\mu\beta\nu\alpha}y^\alpha y^\beta = R_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta}y^\beta y^\alpha$. The two terms of Step 8 are equal, and $-\tfrac16 \cdot 2 = -\tfrac13$.
>
> **Step 10** (part 3, first formula). In (D) the first-pair antisymmetry $R_{\nu\mu\rho\alpha} = -R_{\mu\nu\rho\alpha}$ ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-1|Theorem §C3.10.1]], part 2) cancels the second term against the fourth, and pair symmetry turns $R_{\nu\rho\mu\alpha}$ into $R_{\mu\alpha\nu\rho}$:
>
> $$
> \partial_\alpha\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}(p) = -\tfrac13\bigl(R_{\mu\alpha\nu\rho} + R_{\mu\rho\nu\alpha}\bigr) ;
> $$
>
> rename $\rho \to \beta$.
>
> **Step 11** (part 3, the inverse formula). At $p$, $\partial g = 0$, so $\partial g^{-1} = -g^{-1}(\partial g)g^{-1} = 0$ there, and differentiating $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma} = g^{\lambda\kappa}\Gamma_{\kappa\nu\sigma}$ gives $\eta_{\rho\lambda}\partial_\mu\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma}(p) = \partial_\mu\Gamma_{\rho\nu\sigma}(p)$, with $\Gamma_{\rho\nu\sigma} = \tfrac12(\partial_\nu g_{\sigma\rho} + \partial_\sigma g_{\nu\rho} - \partial_\rho g_{\nu\sigma})$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]], part 1). Lower Step 3:
>
> $$
> R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}(p) = \partial_\mu\Gamma_{\rho\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma_{\rho\mu\sigma} = \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_\mu\partial_\nu g_{\sigma\rho} + \partial_\mu\partial_\sigma g_{\nu\rho} - \partial_\mu\partial_\rho g_{\nu\sigma}\bigr) - \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_\nu\partial_\mu g_{\sigma\rho} + \partial_\nu\partial_\sigma g_{\mu\rho} - \partial_\nu\partial_\rho g_{\mu\sigma}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The terms $\partial_\mu\partial_\nu g_{\sigma\rho}$ cancel, leaving the second formula of part 3 (Hartle's (21.28)). It holds in every local inertial frame, normal or not; in normal coordinates the first formula is its inverse, so the map between $\partial\partial g(p)$ and $R(p)$ is one-to-one on the image.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The geodesics through $p$ impose (C): the totally symmetric part of $\partial\Gamma(p)$ vanishes. The Riemann tensor at $p$ is the antisymmetrized part, $\partial_\mu\Gamma_{\nu} - \partial_\nu\Gamma_{\mu}$. A tensor $\partial_\rho\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}$ symmetric in $\mu\nu$ is fixed by these two pieces, and solving gives the factor $\tfrac13$ (Step 4). The metric inherits it through $\nabla g = 0$ (Steps 6–9). In other local inertial frames (C) fails, and $\partial\Gamma(p)$ contains, besides the curvature, an arbitrary symmetric part, which is the $k$ of the opening.

## Normal coordinates and freely falling observers

On spacetime the construction has a physical reading. The orthonormal basis at $p$ is the laboratory of an observer passing $p$ (Hartle), and the radial geodesics are the worldlines of free bodies released at $p$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.8.3: Normal Coordinates Belong to a Freely Falling Observer and Are Unique up to a Lorentz Transformation
> Let $M$ be a Lorentzian manifold of dimension $4$, time-oriented ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]), $p \in M$, and $(e_\mu)$ an orthonormal basis of $T_pM$ with $e_0$ future-directed timelike.
> 1. **The time axis is a free worldline.** In the normal coordinates built on $(e_\mu)$ ([[#^def-c3-8-2|Def. §C3.8.2]]), the curve $y^\mu(\tau) = (\tau, 0, 0, 0)$ is the timelike geodesic through $p$ with four-velocity $e_0$, and $y^0 = \tau$ is the proper time along it ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]). It is the worldline of the freely falling observer ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]) who passes $p$ with four-velocity $e_0$; $e_1, e_2, e_3$ are her spatial axes at $p$. Restored: $y^0 = c\tau$.
> 2. **Every observer at $p$ has normal coordinates.** For every future-directed timelike $u \in T_pM$ with $g(u, u) = -1$ there are normal coordinates whose time axis is that observer's worldline: take $e_0 = u$.
> 3. **Uniqueness up to a Lorentz transformation.** If $(e'_\mu)$ is another orthonormal basis of $T_pM$ and $e_\nu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,e'_\mu$, then $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta$, and on the common domain the two normal coordinate systems are related by the constant linear map
>
> $$
> y'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,y^\nu .
> $$
>
> Conversely, every Lorentz transformation $\Lambda$ of normal coordinates gives normal coordinates (for the basis $e'_\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu$). The matrices $\Lambda$ are those of [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-1|REL Def. §B1.2.1]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$; the condition $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta$ is unchanged by $\eta \to -\eta$): boosts change the observer, rotations her axes. This is the six-parameter freedom that the counting of [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]] leaves at first order; normal coordinates have no other freedom.
>
> *Model:* a time-oriented Lorentzian manifold; free test bodies on geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]) for the reading in part 1; *applies:* exactly, within a normal neighbourhood of $p$.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, §8.4, p. 180 ("the orthonormal basis vectors of the laboratory of an observer at $P$") · Couzens, GR1, §4.4.2, eq. (4.95), p. 90 (the $\tfrac12m(m - 1)$ unfixed parameters, "precisely the same number of components of … $SO(1, m - 1)$"); p. 91 (freely falling observer and normal coordinates) · Schutz §6.2, p. 149 (six degrees of freedom: boosts and rotations)*

^thm-c3-8-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). For $\tau > 0$ small enough that $\tau e_0 \in V$, [[#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]], part 1, gives $\exp_p(s\,e_0) = \gamma_{e_0}(s)$ for $0 \le s \le \tau$, and by [[#^def-c3-8-2|Def. §C3.8.2]] this point has $y^\mu = s\,\delta^\mu_0$. For negative parameter, the same part with $c = -1$ gives $\gamma_{e_0}(-s) = \gamma_{-e_0}(s) = \exp_p(-s\,e_0)$, with $y^\mu = -s\,\delta^\mu_0$. So the time axis is $\gamma_{e_0}$. Along an affinely parametrized geodesic $g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)$ is constant ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-7|Theorem §C3.7.7]], part 1), here $g(e_0, e_0) = -1$, so the affine parameter is proper time (same theorem, part 3) and $y^0 = \tau$.
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). Complete $e_0 = u$ to an orthonormal basis ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]: Gram–Schmidt starting from $u$) and apply Step 1.
>
> **Step 3** (orthonormality forces Lorentz). Insert $e_\nu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu e'_\mu$ into $g(e_\nu, e_\sigma) = \eta_{\nu\sigma}$ and use $g(e'_\mu, e'_\kappa) = \eta_{\mu\kappa}$:
>
> $$
> \eta_{\nu\sigma} = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\Lambda^\kappa{}_\sigma\,g(e'_\mu, e'_\kappa) = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,\eta_{\mu\kappa}\,\Lambda^\kappa{}_\sigma, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (the coordinates). The exponential map does not involve a basis ([[#^def-c3-8-1|Def. §C3.8.1]]). A point $q$ of the common normal neighbourhood is $q = \exp_p(w)$ for one vector $w$, whose components in the two bases are $y^\nu(q)$ and $y'^\mu(q)$:
>
> $$
> w = y^\nu e_\nu = y^\nu\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,e'_\mu = y'^\mu e'_\mu \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,y^\nu .
> $$
>
> For the converse, given $\Lambda$ with $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta$, the vectors $e'_\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu e_\nu$ are orthonormal (Step 3 read backwards, with $\Lambda^{-1}$ also Lorentz), satisfy $e_\nu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu e'_\mu$, and Step 4 shows that their normal coordinates are $\Lambda y$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Everything in a normal coordinate system except the choice of an orthonormal basis at one point is fixed by the geometry. That choice is an observer's four-velocity and her three axes, and two observers at the same event relate their normal coordinates by the Lorentz transformation between their bases, linearly, with no correction terms at any order.

> [!remark] Remark: Normal coordinates are the freely falling frame of the equivalence principle
> [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle|§C1.3]] stated the Einstein equivalence principle in Newtonian language: in a freely falling, non-rotating laboratory ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]]), small enough in space and time ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]]), every non-gravitational experiment gives the result of special relativity ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]). The three theorems of this section are the geometric version, item by item. The laboratory is the observer of [[#^thm-c3-8-3|Theorem §C3.8.3]], her clock is $y^0$ and her axes are $e_i$. "Gravity is transformed away" is $g(p) = \eta$ and $\Gamma(p) = 0$ ([[#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]]): free bodies passing $p$ have zero coordinate acceleration there, as in [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]], part 1. "Small enough" is [[#^thm-c3-8-2|Theorem §C3.8.2]]: over a region of size $L$ the metric differs from $\eta$ by terms of order $|R|\,L^2$. That is the bound $\tfrac12|E|LT^2 < \delta$ of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]], with the tidal tensor $E$ now a part of the curvature ([[#^ex-c3-8-2|Example §C3.8.2]]; [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-2|Theorem §C3.12.2]]). Couzens puts the limit the same way: the Riemann tensor can be detected at $p$ only by comparing experiments at $p$ with experiments at nearby points, which is a "non-local" observable as far as the principle is concerned. What normal coordinates at one event do not give is a laboratory frame for the whole duration of an experiment; the next caution makes this precise.

^rem-c3-8-1

> [!caution] Caution: Normal coordinates at one event are not a laboratory frame for a finite time
> In normal coordinates at $p$, $\Gamma$ vanishes at $p$ only. On the observer's own time axis $y = (\tau, 0, 0, 0)$, [[#^thm-c3-8-2|Theorem §C3.8.2]], part 1, gives
>
> $$
> \Gamma^i{}_{0j}(\tau, \mathbf 0) = -\tfrac13\bigl(R^i{}_{0j0} + R^i{}_{j00}\bigr)\tau + O(\tau^2) = -\tfrac13R^i{}_{0j0}\,\tau + O(\tau^2) ,
> $$
>
> using $R^i{}_{j00} = 0$ (antisymmetry in the last pair). Consequence: a free particle released at rest next to the observer, at $y = (0, \boldsymbol\xi)$, has the coordinate acceleration
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2y^i}{d\tau^2} = -\Gamma^i{}_{00}(0, \boldsymbol\xi) = \tfrac13\bigl(R^i{}_{00j} + R^i{}_{00j}\bigr)\xi^j = -\tfrac23\,R^i{}_{0j0}\,\xi^j + O(\xi^2) ,
> $$
>
> two thirds of the relative acceleration $-R^i{}_{0j0}\,\xi^j$ that geodesic deviation predicts ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]], part 2, with $T = e_0$: $R^i{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}T^\sigma T^\mu S^\nu = R^i{}_{00j}\xi^j$). The missing third is the time derivative of $\Gamma$ on the axis. The covariant derivative along the axis ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-3|Def. §C3.5.3]]) of a separation $S^\mu$ with $S^0 = 0$ at $p$ is
>
> $$
> \frac{DS^i}{d\tau} = \frac{dS^i}{d\tau} + \Gamma^i{}_{0\nu}S^\nu, \qquad \frac{D^2S^i}{d\tau^2}\Big|_p = \frac{d^2S^i}{d\tau^2} + \partial_0\Gamma^i{}_{0j}(p)\,S^j = -\tfrac23R^i{}_{0j0}\xi^j - \tfrac13R^i{}_{0j0}\xi^j = -R^i{}_{0j0}\,\xi^j ,
> $$
>
> because $\Gamma(p) = 0$ removes every other term. So the grid of normal coordinates is not carried along the observer's worldline as a non-rotating, unstrained laboratory: tides read off coordinate accelerations in it come out at $\tfrac23$ of their value. For the weak field this is $-\tfrac23E_{ij}\xi^j$ against Newton's $-E_{ij}\xi^j$ ([[#^ex-c3-8-2|Example §C3.8.2]], Step 7; [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]]). Coordinates in which $\Gamma = 0$ all along the observer's worldline (Hartle's "freely falling frame", usually called Fermi normal coordinates; d'Inverno & Vickers note that $\Gamma$ can be made to vanish along a curve) have $\partial_0\Gamma = 0$ on it, and there the coordinate acceleration is the full $-R^i{}_{0j0}\xi^j$. For the static weak field, the quadratic coordinates of [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^ex-c3-2-2|Example §C3.2.2]] are such a frame to first order in $\Phi$: their metric deviates from $\eta$ by terms quadratic in the spatial $y^i$ only, so every $\Gamma$ vanishes on the axis $y^i = 0$ (checked by script). The general construction of Fermi normal coordinates carries the axes along the worldline by the transport law of gyroscopes (Hartle §8.4, ch. 14, §20.5) and is not done in these notes. For a weak plane gravitational wave such a parallel-transported frame along a free mass is written down to first order, and in it the separations obey the full geodesic-deviation equation ([[§C8.3 What a Passing Wave Does#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]], part 3).
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, §8.4, pp. 181–182 (freely falling frames: Christoffel symbols vanish along the geodesic, "not at every point labeled by the coordinates") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.6, p. 96 · the factor $\tfrac23$ computed here, checked by script*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C3.8.1: Normal Coordinates at the North Pole of the Sphere
> *Question* (Hartle, Ex. 8.8 and Ex. 7.2). On a sphere of radius $a$, construct normal coordinates at the north pole $p$, find the metric in them, compare it with [[#^thm-c3-8-2|Theorem §C3.8.2]], and say what the second-order term measures.
>
> *Setup.* $g = a^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]) with Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^\theta{}_{\varphi\varphi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta$, $\Gamma^\varphi{}_{\theta\varphi} = \cot\theta$, the rest zero ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^ex-c3-6-2|Example §C3.6.2]]). The chart $(\theta, \varphi)$ fails at the pole itself; the meridians do not. Orthonormal basis at $p$: $e_1$, $e_2$ the unit tangents of the meridians $\varphi = 0$ and $\varphi = \pi/2$ (Hartle). A unit vector at $p$ pointing along the meridian of longitude $\varphi_0$ is $n(\varphi_0) = \cos\varphi_0\,e_1 + \sin\varphi_0\,e_2$.
>
> **Step 1** (the geodesics from $p$). The meridian $\theta(s) = s/a$, $\varphi(s) = \varphi_0$ has $\theta'' = 0$, $\varphi' = \varphi'' = 0$, so
>
> $$
> \theta'' + \Gamma^\theta{}_{\varphi\varphi}\,\varphi'^2 = 0, \qquad \varphi'' + 2\,\Gamma^\varphi{}_{\theta\varphi}\,\theta'\varphi' = 0 :
> $$
>
> it is an affinely parametrized geodesic, part of a great circle ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^ex-c3-7-2|Example §C3.7.2]]). Its speed is $g(\gamma', \gamma') = a^2\theta'^2 = a^2/a^2 = 1$, so $s$ is arc length, and it leaves $p$ in the direction $n(\varphi_0)$.
>
> **Step 2** (the exponential map). By [[#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]], part 1, $\exp_p\bigl(s\,n(\varphi_0)\bigr) = \gamma_{n(\varphi_0)}(s)$, the point $(\theta, \varphi) = (s/a, \varphi_0)$. A vector $v = v^1e_1 + v^2e_2$ has length $|v| = s$ and direction angle $\varphi_0 = \arg v$, so
>
> $$
> \exp_p(v) = \bigl(\theta = |v|/a,\ \varphi = \arg v\bigr), \qquad y^1 = a\theta\cos\varphi, \quad y^2 = a\theta\sin\varphi ,
> $$
>
> the normal coordinates of the point $(\theta, \varphi)$ (Hartle (8.49), (7.15)).
>
> **Step 3** (the normal neighbourhood). For $|v| < \pi a$ the map is one-to-one onto the sphere minus the south pole, with inverse $\theta = |y|/a$, $\varphi = \arg y$. On the circle $|v| = \pi a$ every direction gives $\theta = \pi$: the whole circle is sent to the south pole, so $\exp_p$ is not injective there. Its derivative is singular there too: the circle $|y| = \rho$ goes to the latitude circle $\theta = \rho/a$, of circumference $2\pi a\sin(\rho/a)$, so $\exp_p$ stretches it by the factor $a\sin(\rho/a)/\rho$, which is $0$ at $\rho = \pi a$. The open disk $|y| < \pi a$ is the largest normal neighbourhood here (figure after [[#^def-c3-8-2|Def. §C3.8.2]]).
>
> **Step 4** (the metric in these coordinates). With $\rho = a\theta = |y|$ the metric is $ds^2 = d\rho^2 + a^2\sin^2(\rho/a)\,d\varphi^2$. [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^ex-c3-2-3|Example §C3.2.3]] rewrote it in exactly these coordinates (Hartle's (7.15)), using $\rho^2\,d\varphi = y^1dy^2 - y^2dy^1$ and $dy\cdot dy = d\rho^2 + \rho^2d\varphi^2$:
>
> $$
> ds^2 = dy\cdot dy + \Bigl(\frac{a^2\sin^2(\rho/a)}{\rho^2} - 1\Bigr)\frac{\bigl(y^1dy^2 - y^2dy^1\bigr)^2}{\rho^2}, \qquad \frac{a^2\sin^2(\rho/a)}{\rho^2} = 1 - \frac{\rho^2}{3a^2} + \frac{2\rho^4}{45a^4} - \dots ,
> $$
>
> and found, with $(y^1dy^2 - y^2dy^1)^2 = (y^2)^2(dy^1)^2 - 2y^1y^2\,dy^1dy^2 + (y^1)^2(dy^2)^2$,
>
> $$
> g_{11} = 1 - \frac{(y^2)^2}{3a^2}, \qquad g_{12} = g_{21} = +\frac{y^1y^2}{3a^2}, \qquad g_{22} = 1 - \frac{(y^1)^2}{3a^2}, \qquad\text{each } + O(y^4) .
> $$
>
> (Hartle's printed coefficients are twice these: [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^cau-c3-2-1|§C3.2, Caution: The second-order terms of Hartle's Example 7.2]].) Example §C3.2.3 used these coordinates only as some chart with $g(p) = \delta$, $\partial g(p) = 0$. Step 2 shows more: they are the normal coordinates of the pole, so [[#^thm-c3-8-2|Theorem §C3.8.2]] applies to them. The exact metric differs from the second-order one by $1.26 \times 10^{-6}$ at $|y| = 0.076\,a$ and by $7.9 \times 10^{-8}$ at half that distance, a factor $16$: the remainder is of fourth order, because the expansion is even in $y$.
>
> **Step 5** (compare with the theorem). In two dimensions the Riemann tensor has one independent component ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-4|Theorem §C3.10.4]]). In the chart $(\theta, \varphi)$, $R_{\theta\varphi\theta\varphi} = a^2\sin^2\theta$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^ex-c3-11-1|Example §C3.11.1]]); in an orthonormal basis divide by $g_{\theta\theta}g_{\varphi\varphi} = a^4\sin^2\theta$: $R_{1212} = 1/a^2 = K$, the Gaussian curvature ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^ex-c3-11-3|Example §C3.11.3]]). The nonzero components are $R_{1212} = R_{2121} = 1/a^2$ and $R_{1221} = R_{2112} = -1/a^2$. Then $-\tfrac13R_{A C B D}\,y^Cy^D$ is
>
> $$
> -\tfrac13R_{1212}(y^2)^2 = -\frac{(y^2)^2}{3a^2}, \qquad -\tfrac13R_{1221}\,y^2y^1 = +\frac{y^1y^2}{3a^2}, \qquad -\tfrac13R_{2121}(y^1)^2 = -\frac{(y^1)^2}{3a^2}
> $$
>
> for $AB = 11, 12, 22$: exactly Step 4. (Computing $R(p)$ directly from the quadratic metric of Step 4 gives the same $R_{1212} = 1/a^2$; checked by script, which also confirms $\partial_D\Gamma^A{}_{BC}(p) = -\tfrac13(R^A{}_{BCD} + R^A{}_{CBD})$ and that the radial lines solve the geodesic equation of the exact metric.)
>
> **Step 6** (what the second-order term measures). The circle $|y| = \rho$, parametrized by $\varphi$, has velocity $t = (-y^2, y^1)$. With the metric of Step 4,
>
> $$
> g(t, t) = g_{11}(y^2)^2 - 2g_{12}\,y^1y^2 + g_{22}(y^1)^2 = \rho^2 - \frac{(y^2)^4 + 2(y^1y^2)^2 + (y^1)^4}{3a^2} = \rho^2 - \frac{\rho^4}{3a^2} ,
> $$
>
> so the circumference is
>
> $$
> C(\rho) = \int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{\rho^2 - \frac{\rho^4}{3a^2}}\;d\varphi = 2\pi\rho\Bigl(1 - \frac{\rho^2}{6a^2}\Bigr) + O(\rho^5) = 2\pi\rho\Bigl(1 - \frac{K\rho^2}{6}\Bigr) + O(\rho^5) ,
> $$
>
> in agreement with the exact $C = 2\pi a\sin(\rho/a)$. A surveyor on the sphere who measures a geodesic circle's radius $\rho$ and circumference $C$ finds $K$ from $2\pi\rho - C \approx \pi K\rho^3/3$, without leaving the surface. On the Earth ($a = 6371$ km), a geodesic circle of radius $100$ km has circumference $628.319$ km minus $25.8$ m (exact and second-order values agree to $0.01$ m), a relative deficit $\rho^2/(6a^2) = 4.1 \times 10^{-5}$.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, Ex. 8.8, eqs. (8.48)–(8.49), p. 181; Ex. 7.2, eqs. (7.14)–(7.17), p. 141 (coefficients of (7.17) corrected, see Step 4) · Steps 3, 5 and 6 written out here; numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.8.2: Normal Coordinates for the Weak-Field Metric at an Event
> *Question.* In the static weak-field metric of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric|§C1.5]], what are normal coordinates at an event $p$, and what does the metric look like in them? Which parts of the Newtonian potential survive?
>
> *Setup.* $ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi)\,dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)\,\delta_{ij}dx^idx^j$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]), to first order in $\Phi$ throughout: products of $\Phi$ and its derivatives are dropped. Put $p$ at $t = 0$, $\mathbf x = 0$ and expand the potential about it,
>
> $$
> \Phi(\mathbf x) = \Phi_0 + \Phi_i\,x^i + \tfrac12E_{ij}\,x^ix^j + O(x^3), \qquad \Phi_i = \partial_i\Phi(p) = -g_i, \qquad E_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi(p) ,
> $$
>
> with $\mathbf g = -\nabla\Phi$ the Newtonian field at $p$ ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]) and $E_{ij}$ the tidal tensor ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]]).
>
> **Step 1** (Christoffel symbols at $p$). From $\Gamma_{\lambda\mu\nu} = \tfrac12(\partial_\mu g_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_\nu g_{\mu\lambda} - \partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu})$, raised with $\eta$ at first order ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]; [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^ex-c3-6-3|Example §C3.6.3]]), with $\partial_0g = 0$:
>
> $$
> \Gamma^i{}_{00} = -\tfrac12\partial_ig_{00} = \Phi_i, \qquad \Gamma^0{}_{0i} = \tfrac12\eta^{00}\partial_ig_{00} = \Phi_i, \qquad \Gamma^i{}_{jk} = \tfrac12\bigl(-2\Phi_j\delta_{ki} - 2\Phi_k\delta_{ji} + 2\Phi_i\delta_{jk}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> and $\Gamma^0{}_{00} = \Gamma^0{}_{ij} = \Gamma^i{}_{0j} = 0$.
>
> **Step 2** (the observer's basis). $e_0 = (1 - \Phi_0)\,\partial_t$ and $e_i = (1 + \Phi_0)\,\partial_i$ are orthonormal at $p$ to first order:
>
> $$
> g(e_0, e_0) = -(1 + 2\Phi_0)(1 - \Phi_0)^2 = -1 + O(\Phi^2), \qquad g(e_i, e_j) = (1 - 2\Phi_0)(1 + \Phi_0)^2\,\delta_{ij} = \delta_{ij} + O(\Phi^2) .
> $$
>
> $e_0$ is the four-velocity of the observer momentarily at rest at $p$ ([[#^thm-c3-8-3|Theorem §C3.8.3]], part 1).
>
> **Step 3** (the coordinate change to second order). Apply [[#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]], part 2, to $v = y^\mu e_\mu$, whose chart components are $v^0 = (1 - \Phi_0)y^0$, $v^i = (1 + \Phi_0)y^i$; in the $\Gamma$ term $v$ may be replaced by $y$, because $\Gamma$ is already of first order. The two quadratic terms are
>
> $$
> -\tfrac12\Gamma^0{}_{\alpha\beta}y^\alpha y^\beta = -\Gamma^0{}_{0i}\,y^0y^i = -\Phi_i\,y^0y^i, \qquad -\tfrac12\Gamma^i{}_{\alpha\beta}y^\alpha y^\beta = -\tfrac12\Phi_i(y^0)^2 - \tfrac12\bigl(-2y^i\Phi_ky^k + \Phi_i|\mathbf y|^2\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> so the event with normal coordinates $y$ has
>
> $$
> t = (1 - \Phi_0)\,y^0 - \Phi_i\,y^0y^i + O(y^3), \qquad x^i = (1 + \Phi_0)\,y^i - \tfrac12\Phi_i\,(y^0)^2 + y^i\,\Phi_ky^k - \tfrac12\Phi_i\,|\mathbf y|^2 + O(y^3) .
> $$
>
> On the observer's axis $y^i = 0$: $x^i = -\tfrac12\Phi_i(y^0)^2 = \tfrac12g_i(y^0)^2$ and $t = (1 - \Phi_0)y^0$. The origin of normal coordinates falls with the Newtonian acceleration $\mathbf g$: this is the freely falling frame $\mathbf x' = \mathbf x - \tfrac12\mathbf g t^2$ of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]], part 1 ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]]), now produced by the geodesics instead of being postulated. To this order the change is the one that [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^ex-c3-2-2|Example §C3.2.2]] (Schutz's exercise, static case) found by solving $\partial g'(p) = 0$ order by order; normal coordinates continue it with cubic terms fixed by the geodesics. The observer's clock reads $y^0 = (1 + \Phi_0)t$, the clock-rate law $d\tau = (1 + \Phi)\,dt$ at $p$ ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]]).
>
> **Step 4** (the curvature at $p$). In the component formula the products $\Gamma\Gamma$ are of second order in $\Phi$, and $\partial g^{-1} = O(\Phi)$ multiplies $\Gamma = O(\Phi)$, so to first order the lowered Riemann tensor is given by Step 11 of [[#^der-c3-8-2|the derivation of Theorem §C3.8.2]], $R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = \tfrac12(\partial_\beta\partial_\gamma g_{\alpha\delta} - \partial_\beta\partial_\delta g_{\alpha\gamma} - \partial_\alpha\partial_\gamma g_{\beta\delta} + \partial_\alpha\partial_\delta g_{\beta\gamma})$, even though $\partial g(p) \neq 0$ in these coordinates. With $g_{00} = -1 - 2\Phi$, $g_{0i} = 0$, $g_{ij} = (1 - 2\Phi)\delta_{ij}$ and no time dependence:
>
> $$
> R_{0i0j} = -\tfrac12\partial_i\partial_jg_{00} = E_{ij}, \qquad R_{0ijk} = \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_i\partial_jg_{0k} - \partial_i\partial_kg_{0j}\bigr) = 0 ,
> $$
>
> $$
> R_{ijkl} = \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_j\partial_kg_{il} - \partial_j\partial_lg_{ik} - \partial_i\partial_kg_{jl} + \partial_i\partial_lg_{jk}\bigr) = \delta_{ik}E_{jl} + \delta_{jl}E_{ik} - \delta_{il}E_{jk} - \delta_{jk}E_{il} .
> $$
>
> The first is $R^i{}_{0j0} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi$ of [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-2|Theorem §C3.12.2]]. The rescaling of Step 2 changes these $O(\Phi)$ components only at $O(\Phi^2)$, so they are also the components in the basis $e_\mu$.
>
> **Step 5** (the metric in normal coordinates). Insert into [[#^thm-c3-8-2|Theorem §C3.8.2]], part 2. For $g_{00}$ only spatial $\alpha, \beta$ contribute ($R_{00\nu\beta} = 0$). For $g_{0i}$ only $\alpha = j$, $\beta = 0$ contributes, with $R_{0ji0} = -R_{0j0i} = -E_{ij}$. For $g_{ij}$, $R_{i0j0} = R_{0i0j} = E_{ij}$ ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-3|Theorem §C3.10.3]]), the mixed components vanish, and $R_{ikjl}y^ky^l = \delta_{ij}E_{kl}y^ky^l + |\mathbf y|^2E_{ij} - y_iE_{jk}y^k - y_jE_{ik}y^k$. So
>
> $$
> g_{00} = -1 - \tfrac13E_{ij}\,y^iy^j, \qquad g_{0i} = +\tfrac13E_{ij}\,y^j\,y^0, \qquad g_{ij} = \delta_{ij} - \tfrac13\bigl[E_{ij}(y^0)^2 + \delta_{ij}E_{kl}y^ky^l + |\mathbf y|^2E_{ij} - y_iE_{jk}y^k - y_jE_{ik}y^k\bigr] ,
> $$
>
> each $+ O(y^3)$. The script reaches the same metric independently, by pulling $g$ back through the coordinate change of Step 3 extended by its cubic terms.
>
> **Step 6** (what survived). $\Phi_0$ and $\Phi_i$ are gone. The potential at $p$ went into the units of the observer's clock and rulers (Step 2), the field $\mathbf g$ at $p$ into the free fall of the frame (Step 3). Only the tidal tensor remains, at second order. Restored ($y^0 = c\tau$, $E \to E/c^2$): $g_{00} = -1 - E_{ij}y^iy^j/(3c^2)$. At the Earth's surface the vertical eigenvalue of $E$ has magnitude $2GM_\oplus/R_\oplus^3 = 3.07 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$, so $E/c^2 = 3.42 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}}$, and $g_{00}$ differs from $-1$ by $1.1 \times 10^{-23}$ one metre above or below the observer ($1.1 \times 10^{-17}$ at $1$ km). The terms that normal coordinates removed were far larger: $\Phi_0/c^2 = -6.95 \times 10^{-10}$ and $g/c^2 = 1.09 \times 10^{-16}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$. These numbers give the size of "locally" in [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]].
>
> **Step 7** (a particle released at rest). For a particle at rest at $y = (0, \boldsymbol\xi)$, the geodesic equation gives $d^2y^i/d\tau^2 = -\Gamma^i{}_{00}(0, \boldsymbol\xi)$. At first order, $\Gamma^i{}_{00} = \partial_0g_{0i} - \tfrac12\partial_ig_{00}$, and from Step 5
>
> $$
> \partial_0g_{0i} = \tfrac13E_{ij}\xi^j, \qquad -\tfrac12\partial_ig_{00} = \tfrac13E_{ij}\xi^j, \qquad \frac{d^2y^i}{d\tau^2} = -\tfrac23E_{ij}\,\xi^j .
> $$
>
> Newton's relative acceleration is $-E_{ij}\xi^j$ ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]]). In the coordinates of [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^ex-c3-2-2|Example §C3.2.2]], which stop after the quadratic terms, the same particle has the full $-E_{ij}\xi^j$ (its Step 6, where $g'_{00} = -1 - E_{ij}y^iy^j$ with coefficient $1$, not $\tfrac13$): the second-order coefficients depend on the cubic terms of the coordinates, as the opening showed. In normal coordinates the missing third sits in $\partial_0\Gamma^i{}_{0j}(p) = -\tfrac13E_{ij}$ ([[#^cau-c3-8-2|Caution: Normal coordinates at one event are not a laboratory frame for a finite time]]). One metre above the observer at the Earth's surface the coordinate acceleration is $2.05 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, the tidal acceleration $3.07 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
