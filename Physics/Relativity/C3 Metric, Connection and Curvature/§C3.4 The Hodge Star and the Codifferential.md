[[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor|§C3.3]] gave an oriented manifold with a metric its volume form $\mathrm{vol} = \sqrt{|g|}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n$ ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]), read it as the Levi-Civita tensor $\epsilon$ ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^def-c3-3-2|Def. §C3.3.2]]), and computed every contraction of two $\epsilon$'s ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]]). A second job from chapter C2 is still open: duality. On $\mathbf R^3$ the exterior derivative became gradient, curl and divergence only after one-forms and two-forms were both turned into vectors, a step that used the Euclidean metric and the orientation and was left unnamed ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-1|Example §C2.6.1]], "What needs more structure"). The question of this section is: *how does a metric turn $p$-forms into $(n - p)$-forms in every chart, and what does that make of the exterior derivative?*

A naive rule fails, and its failure shows what is needed. **Duality.** On the plane, turning a one-form through a right angle should send $dx$ to $dy$. The naive rule uses the Levi-Civita symbol alone, $J(\omega)_\mu = \omega_\nu\tilde\epsilon_{\nu\mu}$ with $\tilde\epsilon_{xy} = 1$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-7|Def. §C2.3.7]]). In Cartesian coordinates $J(dx)_x = \omega_y\tilde\epsilon_{yx} = 0$ and $J(dx)_y = \omega_x\tilde\epsilon_{xy} = 1$: $J(dx) = dy$. In polar coordinates the same one-form has the components $(dx)_r = \cos\theta$, $(dx)_\theta = -r\sin\theta$, and $dy$ has $(\sin\theta, r\cos\theta)$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]]). The rule gives

$$
J(dx)_r = \omega_\theta\,\tilde\epsilon_{\theta r} = r\sin\theta, \qquad J(dx)_\theta = \omega_r\,\tilde\epsilon_{r\theta} = \cos\theta \qquad\ne\qquad (\sin\theta,\ r\cos\theta) .
$$

The rule is not a tensor operation: it adds two lower indices, and the symbol is a density, not a tensor ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]]). Raising the index of $\omega$ with $g^{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(1, r^{-2})$ and multiplying the symbol by $\sqrt{\det g} = r$ repairs both defects: $r\,(g^{\theta\theta}\omega_\theta)\,\tilde\epsilon_{\theta r} = r\cdot r^{-2}\cdot(-r\sin\theta)\cdot(-1) = \sin\theta$ and $r\,(g^{rr}\omega_r)\,\tilde\epsilon_{r\theta} = r\cos\theta$, the components of $dy$.

The repair uses the two ingredients of §C3.3 and §C3.1, $\sqrt{|\det g|}$ and the inverse metric: $r\,\tilde\epsilon$ is the Levi-Civita tensor of the plane in polar coordinates, so the repaired rule is a contraction of the raised form with $\epsilon$. The section builds it in order. Contracting $\epsilon$ with a raised form gives the Hodge star ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]), with $\star\star = \pm1$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]]); which indices of $\epsilon$ the form occupies is a convention that the sources do not share ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^cau-c3-4-1|Caution: Index placement in the Hodge star]]). The star gives an inner product of forms ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-2|Def. §C3.4.2]], [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]]), the adjoint of $d$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-3|Def. §C3.4.3]], [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]]) and the Laplacian ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-4|Def. §C3.4.4]], [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]]), whose sign is compared with the d'Alembertian's in [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^cau-c3-4-2|Caution: Laplacian, d'Alembertian and their signs]]. The examples recover vector calculus, write Maxwell's equations on any spacetime and derive the spherical Laplacian.

**Setup.** As in [[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor|§C3.3]]: a smooth manifold $M$ of dimension $n$ with a metric $g$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]) of signature $(q, n - q)$, $q$ negative signs ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]); Riemannian means $q = 0$, Lorentzian $q = 1$, and spacetime is the Lorentzian case $n = 4$. $M$ is oriented ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]]), and "chart" means a positively oriented chart unless said otherwise; on spacetime the inertial coordinates $(t, x, y, z)$ of Minkowski spacetime are positively oriented. The volume form $\mathrm{vol}$, the Levi-Civita tensor $\epsilon$ and the sign $s$ are those of §C3.3; indices of forms are raised with the inverse metric ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]). Forms, $\wedge$ and $d$ are those of [[§C2.6 Differential Forms|§C2.6]], in the determinant convention ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^cau-c2-6-1|§C2.6, Caution: Normalizations of the wedge product]]). Integrals of forms are those of [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem|§C2.7]]; integrals over $M$ are taken over compact supports or where they converge. Every statement is an exact theorem of geometry, in any dimension and signature; no physical model enters except in [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^ex-c3-4-2|Example §C3.4.2]]. Every sign and number in this section was checked by script, for $n = 2, 3, 4$ and both signatures.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$, $\mu_0$ kept in Maxwell's equations as in [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^ex-c1-2-4|Example §C1.2.4]]. $g \equiv \det(g_{\mu\nu})$ in a chart and $s \equiv \operatorname{sign} g = (-1)^q$; $\sqrt{|g|}$ is $\sqrt g$ for Riemannian and $\sqrt{-g}$ for Lorentzian metrics. $\tilde\epsilon$ is the Levi-Civita symbol ($\tilde\epsilon_{1\cdots n} = \tilde\epsilon^{1\cdots n} = +1$ in every chart, [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-7|Def. §C2.3.7]]); $\epsilon$ without tilde is the Levi-Civita tensor, $\epsilon_{0123} = +\sqrt{-g}$, $\epsilon^{0123} = -1/\sqrt{-g}$ on spacetime. The Hodge star contracts the form with the **first** indices of $\epsilon$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^cau-c3-4-1|Caution: Index placement in the Hodge star]]). $\Delta = dd^\dagger + d^\dagger d$ is the Laplace–de Rham operator, and $\Box = g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu$ ($= -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2$ in inertial coordinates) the d'Alembertian; on functions $\Delta = -\Box$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^cau-c3-4-2|Caution: Laplacian, d'Alembertian and their signs]]).

## The Hodge star

The opening repaired the quarter turn of the plane by raising the index of the form and contracting it with $\sqrt{\det g}\,\tilde\epsilon$, that is with $\epsilon$. The same recipe works for forms of every degree: raise all $p$ indices of a $p$-form and contract them with $p$ of the $n$ indices of $\epsilon$; the $n - p$ indices left over make an $(n - p)$-form.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.4.1: Hodge Star
> *Structure.* On an oriented $n$-manifold with a metric, the **Hodge star** (Hodge dual) is the family of linear maps $\star : \Omega^p(M) \to \Omega^{n-p}(M)$, $p = 0, 1, \dots, n$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]]), given by
>
> $$
> (\star\omega)_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} = \frac{1}{p!}\;\omega^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}\;\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} = \frac{1}{p!}\,\sqrt{|g|}\;\omega^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}\;\tilde\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} ,
> $$
>
> where $\omega^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p} = g^{\nu_1\lambda_1}\cdots g^{\nu_p\lambda_p}\omega_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p}$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]) and $\epsilon$ is the Levi-Civita tensor ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^def-c3-3-2|Def. §C3.3.2]]). The form is contracted with the **first** $p$ indices of $\epsilon$. The $\frac1{p!}$ makes the sum run once over each set of $p$ indices.
>
> *Why it is a form.* The right side is a contraction of the tensor product $\omega^\sharp\otimes\epsilon$, so it is a tensor, the same in every chart ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]); it is antisymmetric in $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_{n-p}$ because $\epsilon$ is. $\star$ is linear over functions, $\star(f\omega) = f\star\omega$, so it acts point by point. It uses the metric twice (to raise the indices of $\omega$ and through $\sqrt{|g|}$) and the orientation once (the sign of $\epsilon$).
>
> *Dimensions.* $\Lambda^p$ and $\Lambda^{n-p}$ at a point both have dimension $\binom np = \binom n{n-p}$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]], counting); $\star$ is an isomorphism between them ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]], part 4). On spacetime it maps functions to four-forms, one-forms to three-forms, and two-forms to two-forms.
>
> *First values.* $\star1 = \mathrm{vol}$ and $\star\mathrm{vol} = s$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]]). On the plane, $\star dx = dy$ and $\star dy = -dx$: the quarter turn of the opening, which in polar coordinates reads $\star dr = r\,d\theta$, $\star d\theta = -dr/r$. On $\mathbf R^3$, $\star dx = dy\wedge dz$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^ex-c3-4-1|Example §C3.4.1]]); on spacetime, $\star(dt\wedge dx) = -dy\wedge dz$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^ex-c3-4-2|Example §C3.4.2]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.31) (p. 78), with the contraction moved to the first indices of $\epsilon$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^cau-c3-4-1|Caution: Index placement in the Hodge star]]); footnote 15 (p. 62: the metric provides the isomorphism $\Omega^r_p \cong \Omega^{m-r}_p$) · Couzens, GR2, App. A, eq. (A.30) (p. 182) · the values checked by script*

^def-c3-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.1: ⋆1 = vol, ⋆vol = s, and ⋆⋆ = s(−1)^{p(n−p)}
> Let $s = \operatorname{sign}\det g$ ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]) and let $\star$ be the Hodge star ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]).
> 1. $\star1 = \mathrm{vol}$ ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]).
> 2. $\star\mathrm{vol} = s$: $+1$ for a Riemannian metric, $-1$ for a Lorentzian one.
> 3. For every $p$-form $\omega$,
>
> $$
> \star\star\omega = s\,(-1)^{p(n-p)}\,\omega .
> $$
>
> Riemannian ($s = +1$): $\star\star = (-1)^{p(n-p)}$, which is $+1$ in every degree when $n$ is odd ($p$ or $n - p$ is even). Lorentzian ($s = -1$): $\star\star = -(-1)^{p(n-p)}$. The tables:
>
> | | $p = 0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $\mathbf R^2$ | $+1$ | $-1$ | $+1$ | | |
> | $\mathbf R^3$ | $+1$ | $+1$ | $+1$ | $+1$ | |
> | $\mathbf R^4$, Riemannian | $+1$ | $-1$ | $+1$ | $-1$ | $+1$ |
> | spacetime | $-1$ | $+1$ | $-1$ | $+1$ | $-1$ |
>
> 4. $\star$ is invertible: on $(n - p)$-forms, $\star^{-1} = s\,(-1)^{p(n-p)}\,\star$.
> 5. ⚑ **Two-forms in four dimensions.** For $n = 4$ and $p = 2$, $\star\star = s$. With a Riemannian metric, $\star^2 = 1$ and every two-form splits into a self-dual and an anti-self-dual part, $\omega = \frac12(\omega + \star\omega) + \frac12(\omega - \star\omega)$, with $\star = \pm1$ on them. On spacetime $\star^2 = -1$ on two-forms, like $i^2 = -1$: no nonzero real two-form is self-dual, and the eigenvalues $\pm i$ need complex forms such as $F \mp i\star F$. The same holds for one-forms on the plane, where $\star$ is the quarter turn and $\star^2 = -1$. In electromagnetism, rotating $F$ in the plane of $F$ and $\star F$ is duality, a symmetry of the source-free Einstein–Maxwell equations ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^thm-c11-1-4|Theorem §C11.1.4]]).
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of an oriented manifold with a metric; *applies:* every degree, any dimension and signature.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.32) (p. 79: "with $+$ for a Riemannian metric and $-$ for a Lorentzian"; stated without proof) · Couzens, GR2, App. A, eq. (A.31) (p. 182) · proof written here · checked by script for $n = 2, 3, 4$, all $p$, both signatures*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). For $p = 0$ the form $\omega = 1$ has no indices and the definition reads $(\star1)_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \mathrm{vol}_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}$ ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^def-c3-3-2|Def. §C3.3.2]]).
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). For $p = n$ no index is left over: $\star\mathrm{vol} = \frac{1}{n!}\mathrm{vol}^{\nu_1\dots\nu_n}\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_n} = \frac1{n!}\epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_n}\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_n} = \frac{1}{n!}\,s\,n! = s$, by [[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]], part 3 with $k = n$.
>
> **Step 3** (the star with raised free indices). Raising the free indices of $\star\omega$, and moving the metric factors of the contracted pairs from $\omega$ to $\epsilon$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]], parts 2 and 4),
>
> $$
> (\star\omega)^{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} = \frac{1}{p!}\,\omega_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}\,\epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (apply the star twice). $\star\omega$ has degree $n - p$, so by the definition with $p$ replaced by $n - p$, and Step 3,
>
> $$
> (\star\star\omega)_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} = \frac{1}{(n-p)!}\,(\star\omega)^{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}}\,\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} = \frac{1}{(n-p)!\,p!}\;\omega_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}\;\epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}}\;\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (reorder). In $\epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}}$ move the block $\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}$ to the front: each of its $n - p$ indices passes the $p$ indices $\nu$, one transposition each, so $p(n - p)$ transpositions in all:
>
> $$
> \epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} = (-1)^{p(n-p)}\,\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\nu_1\dots\nu_p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (contract). [[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]], part 3, with $k = n - p$ contracted indices:
>
> $$
> \epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p}\,\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\nu_1\dots\nu_p} = s\,(n-p)!\;\delta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 7** (the delta against $\omega$). $\delta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p}\omega_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}$ is a sum over the $p!$ rearrangements $\nu_i = \lambda_{\pi(i)}$ of $\operatorname{sgn}\pi\;\omega_{\lambda_{\pi(1)}\dots\lambda_{\pi(p)}} = \operatorname{sgn}\pi\cdot\operatorname{sgn}\pi\;\omega_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p}$ (antisymmetry of $\omega$), so it equals $p!\,\omega_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p}$; if the $\lambda$'s repeat, both sides are $0$. Inserting Steps 5–7 into Step 4:
>
> $$
> (\star\star\omega)_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} = \frac{1}{(n-p)!\,p!}\,(-1)^{p(n-p)}\,s\,(n-p)!\;p!\;\omega_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} = s\,(-1)^{p(n-p)}\,\omega_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 8** (the tables). $p(n - p)$ for $n = 2$: $0, 1, 0$; for $n = 3$: $0, 2, 2, 0$; for $n = 4$: $0, 3, 4, 3, 0$. Multiply $(-1)^{p(n-p)}$ by $s = +1$ or $-1$. If $n$ is odd, one of $p$ and $n - p$ is even, so $p(n-p)$ is even.
>
> **Step 9** (part 4). By part 3 applied to $(n - p)$-forms, $\star\star = s(-1)^{(n-p)p}$ on them, so $\bigl(s(-1)^{p(n-p)}\star\bigr)\star = 1$ on $\Omega^{n-p}$, and in the other order on $\Omega^p$.
>
> **Step 10** (part 5). For $n = 4$, $p = 2$: $p(n - p) = 4$, so $\star\star = s$. If $s = 1$, $\star\bigl(\frac12(\omega \pm \star\omega)\bigr) = \frac12(\star\omega \pm \omega) = \pm\frac12(\omega \pm \star\omega)$. If $s = -1$ and $\star\omega = \lambda\omega$ with real $\lambda$ and $\omega \ne 0$, then $-\omega = \star\star\omega = \lambda^2\omega$, impossible; for $\lambda = \mp i$, $\star(F \mp i\star F) = \star F \mp i(-F) = \pm i(F \mp i\star F)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** $\star\star$ is the product of two signs: $s$, from the one contraction of two $\epsilon$'s, and $(-1)^{p(n-p)}$, from reordering. The first is why Lorentzian signature changes every sign; the second does not depend on the metric at all. Since the reordering occurs once in each application of $\star$, putting the form on the last instead of the first indices of $\epsilon$ changes $\star$ by $(-1)^{p(n-p)}$ but leaves $\star\star$ unchanged.

The two agree on $\mathbf R^3$ and on two-forms in four dimensions, and differ by a sign for odd $p$ in even $n$: one-forms of the plane, one-forms and three-forms of spacetime. $\star\star$ is the same for both, so GR1 (4.32) holds either way.
> - **What depends on it.** With the placement here, $\eta\wedge\star\omega = \langle\eta, \omega\rangle\,\mathrm{vol}$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]]); with the last-index placement, $\eta\wedge\star_{\rm last}\,\omega = (-1)^{p(n-p)}\langle\eta, \omega\rangle\,\mathrm{vol}$ (checked by script for $n = 2, 3, 4$, every $p$, both signatures). On the Euclidean plane, for instance, $dx\wedge\star_{\rm last}\,dx = dx\wedge(-dy) = -\mathrm{vol}$. GR1 then defines the inner product as $\int\eta\wedge\star\omega$ (4.33), its adjoint $d^\dagger$ (4.34)–(4.35) and the Laplacian (4.36)–(4.37). Read with the last-index star, (4.33) is negative definite on one-forms of the plane, and (4.33)–(4.35) give $d^\dagger = +\operatorname{div}$ on them and a Laplacian of the opposite sign to (4.37). Read with the first-index star, every one of (4.32)–(4.37) holds as printed, in every dimension and signature (Theorems §C3.4.2–§C3.4.4, checked by script). This note therefore uses the first-index placement, and its formulas agree with GR1's (4.32)–(4.37) and with GR2's (2.16).

## Inner product, codifferential and Laplacian

The metric measures vectors; with the Hodge star it also measures forms. Two $p$-forms can be paired point by point by raising the indices of one, and the pairing integrated with $\mathrm{vol}$ gives an inner product on forms of compact support. With an inner product, $d$ has an adjoint, which lowers the degree by one; it is a divergence, and $d$ followed by its adjoint is the Laplacian.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.4.2: Inner Product of Forms
> *Structure.* For $p$-forms $\omega$ and $\eta$ on a manifold with a metric, the **pointwise inner product** is the function
>
> $$
> \langle\omega, \eta\rangle = \frac{1}{p!}\,\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_p}\,\eta^{\mu_1\dots\mu_p} = \sum_{\mu_1 < \dots < \mu_p}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_p}\,\eta^{\mu_1\dots\mu_p} ,
> $$
>
> a full contraction ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]) with indices raised by $g^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]), hence the same in every chart. For $p = 0$ it is the product $fh$; for $p = 1$ it is $g^{\mu\nu}\omega_\mu\eta_\nu$, the inverse metric of §C3.1. It is symmetric and bilinear over functions. On an oriented manifold, for forms of which at least one has compact support, the **inner product** is the number
>
> $$
> (\omega, \eta) = \int_M\langle\omega, \eta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = \int\langle\omega, \eta\rangle\,\sqrt{|g|}\;d^nx \qquad(\text{support in one chart}) ,
> $$
>
> an integral of a function against the volume form ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]).
>
> *Definite or not.* At each point the pairing is nondegenerate: if $\langle\omega, \eta\rangle = 0$ for all $\eta$, then $\omega = 0$ (take $\eta$ with one independent component $\eta_{\mu_1\dots\mu_p} = 1$, $\mu_1 < \dots < \mu_p$; then $\langle\omega, \eta\rangle$ is $\omega^{\mu_1\dots\mu_p}$, and all of them vanish). For a Riemannian metric it is positive definite: in an orthonormal basis ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]) $\langle\omega, \omega\rangle$ is the sum of the squares of the components. For a Lorentzian metric it is indefinite: $\langle dt, dt\rangle = -1$ and $\langle dx, dx\rangle = +1$ in Minkowski spacetime, and for the field strength $\langle F, F\rangle = \frac12F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} = B^2 - E^2$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^ex-c3-4-2|Example §C3.4.2]]). "Inner product" then means a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.33) (p. 79: $\langle\eta, \omega\rangle \equiv \int_M\eta\wedge\star\omega$; equal to the integral here by [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]]) · values checked by script*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.2: η ∧ ⋆ω = ⟨η, ω⟩ vol
> For $p$-forms $\eta$ and $\omega$ on an oriented manifold with a metric,
>
> $$
> \eta\wedge\star\omega = \langle\eta, \omega\rangle\;\mathrm{vol} = \omega\wedge\star\eta ,
> $$
>
> with $\star$ of [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]], $\langle\cdot, \cdot\rangle$ of [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-2|Def. §C3.4.2]] and the wedge product of [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]]. Consequently $(\eta, \omega) = \int_M\eta\wedge\star\omega$, Couzens' definition (4.33). Two cases used below: $\omega\wedge\star\omega = \langle\omega, \omega\rangle\,\mathrm{vol}$, and for a two-form on spacetime $F\wedge\star F = \frac12F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\,\mathrm{vol}$, so the Maxwell Lagrangian is $-\frac{1}{4\mu_0}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\,\mathrm{vol} = -\frac{1}{2\mu_0}F\wedge\star F$ (GR1 (5.46), with $\mu_0 = 1$ there).
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* every degree, dimension and signature, with the first-index star ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^cau-c3-4-1|Caution: Index placement in the Hodge star]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.33) (p. 79); §5.1.5, eq. (5.46) (p. 111) · checked by script for $n = 2, 3, 4$, every $p$, both signatures*

With an inner product on every $\Omega^p$, the exterior derivative $d : \Omega^{p-1} \to \Omega^p$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]]) has an adjoint going the other way. > [!definition] Definition §C3.4.3: Codifferential
> *Structure.* On an oriented manifold with a metric, the **codifferential** (the adjoint of $d$) is the family of linear maps $d^\dagger : \Omega^p(M) \to \Omega^{p-1}(M)$, $p = 1, \dots, n$, characterized by
>
> $$
> (d\alpha, \beta) = (\alpha, d^\dagger\beta)
> $$
>
> for every $p$-form $\beta$ and every $(p - 1)$-form $\alpha$ with compact support in the interior of $M$, with the inner product of [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-2|Def. §C3.4.2]]. On functions $d^\dagger = 0$. Such a map exists, is unique, and is a differential operator with the components ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]])
>
> $$
> (d^\dagger\beta)^{\nu_2\dots\nu_p} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\;\beta^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> For a one-form, $d^\dagger\beta = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\,\beta^\mu\bigr)$ is minus the divergence of the vector $\beta^\sharp$: the density $\sqrt{|g|}\,\beta^\mu$ is differentiated, as in the divergence of [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]] and the polar formula of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^ex-c2-3-3|Example §C2.3.3]].
>
> *Names.* Couzens writes $d^\dagger$ (GR1 (4.34)); many texts write $\delta$. "Adjoint" here means formal adjoint: only $\alpha$ of compact support are tested, so no boundary terms arise.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.34)–(4.35) (p. 79) · component formula derived here*

^def-c3-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.3: The Codifferential: Components, Adjointness, d† = ±⋆d⋆
> Let $\beta$ be a $p$-form, $1 \le p \le n$, on an oriented manifold with a metric, $s = \operatorname{sign}\det g$, and define in each chart
>
> $$
> D^{\nu_2\dots\nu_p} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\;\beta^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 1. **A form, built from $\star$ and $d$.** $D$ are the raised components of a $(p - 1)$-form, the same in every chart, and
>
> $$
> D = s\,(-1)^{n(p+1)+1}\,\star d\star\beta .
> $$
>
> 2. **Adjointness and uniqueness.** $(d\alpha, \beta) = (\alpha, D)$ for every $(p - 1)$-form $\alpha$ of compact support in the interior of $M$, and $D$ is the only $(p - 1)$-form with this property. So $d^\dagger\beta = D$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-3|Def. §C3.4.3]]).
> 3. **Cases.** On spacetime ($n = 4$, $s = -1$): $d^\dagger = \star d\star$ in every degree. On $\mathbf R^3$: $d^\dagger = (-1)^p\star d\star$. On the Euclidean plane: $d^\dagger = -\star d\star$. On one-forms in any dimension: $d^\dagger\beta = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}\,\beta^\mu)$.
> 4. **$d^\dagger d^\dagger = 0$.**
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* every degree, dimension and signature; part 2 for compactly supported $\alpha$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.34)–(4.35) (p. 79; part 1 is (4.35), stated without proof) · proof written here · parts 1, 2 and 4 checked by script for $n = 2, 3, 4$, every $p$, both signatures, on metrics that depend on the coordinates*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Write $\mathfrak B^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p} = \sqrt{|g|}\,\beta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}$, a totally antisymmetric density of weight $1$ ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]], part 3; [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 3).
>
> **Step 1** (the first star). By [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]], with $\epsilon = \sqrt{|g|}\,\tilde\epsilon$,
>
> $$
> (\star\beta)_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} = \frac{1}{p!}\,\mathfrak B^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** ($d$). $\star\beta$ has degree $n - p$; by [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1,
>
> $$
> (d\star\beta)_{\mu_0\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-p}(-1)^k\,\partial_{\mu_k}(\star\beta)_{\mu_0\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_{n-p}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the second star, raised). $d\star\beta$ has degree $m = n - p + 1$, and $n - m = p - 1$. By Step 3 of the derivation of [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]] and [[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]], part 1 ($\epsilon^{\cdots} = s\,\tilde\epsilon^{\cdots}/\sqrt{|g|}$),
>
> $$
> (\star d\star\beta)^{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\frac{1}{(n-p+1)!}\,(d\star\beta)_{\mu_0\dots\mu_{n-p}}\,\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_0\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (the $n - p + 1$ terms are equal). In the $k$-th term of Step 2, contracted with $\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_0\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda\cdots}$, move $\mu_k$ to the front of the symbol: $k$ transpositions, $\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_0\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda\cdots} = (-1)^k\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_k\mu_0\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda\cdots}$. The factor $(-1)^k$ cancels the sign in Step 2, and after renaming the summed indices the term equals the $k = 0$ term. So
>
> $$
> (\star d\star\beta)^{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\frac{1}{(n-p)!}\,\partial_{\mu_0}(\star\beta)_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}}\,\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_0\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (insert Step 1). The symbol is constant, so $\partial_{\mu_0}$ acts on $\mathfrak B$ only:
>
> $$
> (\star d\star\beta)^{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\frac{1}{(n-p)!\,p!}\;\tilde\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}}\;\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_0\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}}\;\partial_{\mu_0}\mathfrak B^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (reorder both symbols). Move the block $\nu_1\dots\nu_p$ to the end of the first symbol, past $n - p$ indices: $(-1)^{p(n-p)}$. Move $\mu_0$ past the block $\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}$ in the second: $(-1)^{n-p}$. Then
>
> $$
> \tilde\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}}\;\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_0\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} = (-1)^{p(n-p) + (n-p)}\;\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\nu_1\dots\nu_p}\;\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_{n-p}\mu_0\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 7** (contract). Step 3 of the derivation of [[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]] (symbols, $k = n - p$ summed indices) gives $(n - p)!\,\delta^{\mu_0\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}}_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}$, and against the antisymmetric $\partial_{\mu_0}\mathfrak B^{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}$ the delta gives $p!\,\partial_{\mu_0}\mathfrak B^{\mu_0\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}}$ (Step 7 of the derivation of [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]]). Collecting,
>
> $$
> (\star d\star\beta)^{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} = s\,(-1)^{(n-p)(p+1)}\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\,\beta^{\mu\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}}\bigr) = s\,(-1)^{(n-p)(p+1)+1}\,D^{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{p-1}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 8** (the exponent; part 1). $(n - p)(p + 1) = n(p + 1) - p(p + 1)$, and $p(p + 1)$ is even, so $(-1)^{(n-p)(p+1)+1} = (-1)^{n(p+1)+1}$. Multiplying by $s(-1)^{n(p+1)+1}$, whose square is $1$: $D = s(-1)^{n(p+1)+1}\star d\star\beta$. The right side is built from $\star$ and $d$, which give forms independent of the chart ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]; [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 2), so $D$ is a $(p - 1)$-form. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 9** ($\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle$ in components). By [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1, $(d\alpha)_{\mu_1\dots\mu_p} = \sum_{k=1}^{p}(-1)^{k-1}\partial_{\mu_k}\alpha_{\mu_1\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_p}$. Contracted with the antisymmetric $\beta^{\mu_1\dots\mu_p}$, the $k$-th term equals the first (move $\mu_k$ to the front of $\beta$: $k - 1$ transpositions, cancelling $(-1)^{k-1}$). So, by [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-2|Def. §C3.4.2]],
>
> $$
> \langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle = \frac{1}{p!}\cdot p\;\partial_\mu\alpha_{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\,\beta^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p} = \frac{1}{(p-1)!}\;\partial_\mu\alpha_{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\,\beta^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p} .
> $$
>
> **Step 10** (integrate by parts; part 2). Multiply by $\sqrt{|g|}$ and use the product rule on $\partial_\mu\bigl(\alpha_{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\mathfrak B^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\bigr)$:
>
> $$
> \sqrt{|g|}\,\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle = \frac{1}{(p-1)!}\,\partial_\mu\mathfrak V^\mu + \frac{1}{(p-1)!}\,\alpha_{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\Bigl(-\partial_\mu\mathfrak B^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{(p-1)!}\,\partial_\mu\mathfrak V^\mu + \sqrt{|g|}\,\langle\alpha, D\rangle ,
> $$
>
> with $\mathfrak V^\mu = \alpha_{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\mathfrak B^{\mu\nu_2\dots\nu_p}$, a vector density of weight $1$ (a contraction of a tensor with a density, [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 3) with compact support in the interior, because $\alpha$ has. As top forms, $\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = \langle\alpha, D\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} + \frac{1}{(p-1)!}\,d\omega_{\mathfrak V}$ with the flux form $\omega_{\mathfrak V}$ of [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]], part 1, whose integral vanishes by part 2 of that theorem (no contribution from a boundary that the support does not reach). Integrating, $(d\alpha, \beta) = (\alpha, D)$.
>
> **Step 11** (uniqueness). If $D'$ also satisfies part 2, then $(\alpha, D - D') = 0$ for all $\alpha$ of compact support. In a chart, take $\alpha$ with the single independent component $\alpha_{\nu_2\dots\nu_p} = \varphi$ for one increasing list $\nu_2 < \dots < \nu_p$, $\varphi$ a smooth function of compact support in the chart, the other components zero or fixed by antisymmetry. Then $0 = (\alpha, D - D') = \int\varphi\,(D - D')^{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\sqrt{|g|}\,d^nx$ for every such $\varphi$, so the continuous function $(D - D')^{\nu_2\dots\nu_p}\sqrt{|g|}$ vanishes, and with it every component of $D - D'$. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 12** (part 3). $s(-1)^{n(p+1)+1}$: for $n = 4$, $s = -1$, the exponent $4(p+1)+1$ is odd, so the sign is $(-1)(-1) = +1$. For $n = 3$, $s = 1$: $(-1)^{3p+4} = (-1)^p$. For $n = 2$, $s = 1$: $(-1)^{2p+3} = -1$. For $p = 1$ the formula of $D$ has the single free index removed.
>
> **Step 13** (part 4). $d^\dagger d^\dagger\beta = c_1\star d\star\,c_2\star d\star\beta = c_1c_2\,\star d\,(\star\star)\,d\star\beta$ with signs $c_1, c_2 = \pm1$. $\star\star = \pm1$ on the $(n - p + 1)$-form $d\star\beta$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]]), so this is $\pm\star\,d\,d\star\beta = 0$ by $d^2 = 0$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 4).
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The adjoint of $d$ is a divergence: $-\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}\,\beta^{\mu\cdots})$ is a tensor although it contains only partial derivatives, because $\beta^{\mu\cdots}$ is antisymmetric and the density $\sqrt{|g|}$ absorbs the Jacobian. No connection is needed. For general tensors this fails; the connection that repairs it is [[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative|§C3.5]], and the same divergence formula for vector fields reappears there with $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\nu\ln\sqrt{|g|}$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-3|Theorem §C3.6.3]]).
> - The sign in $d^\dagger = \pm\star d\star$ collects three sources: $s$ from raising $\epsilon$, the reorderings of the symbols, and the convention that the form sits on the first indices of $\epsilon$. Only the component formula is free of conventions.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.4.4: Laplace–de Rham Operator
> *Structure.* On an oriented manifold with a metric, the **Laplace–de Rham operator** is the linear map $\Delta : \Omega^p(M) \to \Omega^p(M)$,
>
> $$
> \Delta = d\,d^\dagger + d^\dagger d = (d + d^\dagger)^2 ,
> $$
>
> with $d$ of [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]] and $d^\dagger$ of [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-3|Def. §C3.4.3]]; the second form holds because $d^2 = 0$ and $(d^\dagger)^2 = 0$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]], part 4). On functions $d^\dagger f = 0$, so $\Delta f = d^\dagger df$. A form with $\Delta\omega = 0$ is **harmonic**.
>
> *Names and sign.* Couzens writes $\Box$ (GR1 (4.36); footnote 19: also $\triangle$). These notes reserve $\Box$ for the d'Alembertian; on functions $\Delta = -\Box$, and on $\mathbf R^3$, $\Delta = -\nabla^2$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]], [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^cau-c3-4-2|Caution: Laplacian, d'Alembertian and their signs]]). The sign makes $\Delta$ a positive operator on a Riemannian manifold.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.36) and footnote 19 (p. 79)*

^def-c3-4-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.4.4: The Laplace–de Rham Operator on Functions
> Let $f$ be a smooth function on an oriented manifold with a metric.
> 1. **Formula.** In every chart
>
> $$
> \Delta f = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\Bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\;g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\nu f\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 2. **Euclidean space.** On $\mathbf R^3$ with Cartesian coordinates, $\Delta f = -(\partial_x^2 + \partial_y^2 + \partial_z^2)f = -\nabla^2f$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^def-b1-1-1|EM Def. §B1.1.1]]): $\Delta$ is minus the Laplacian of vector calculus. In curvilinear coordinates part 1 reproduces its curvilinear forms ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^ex-c3-4-3|Example §C3.4.3]]).
> 3. **Minkowski spacetime.** In inertial coordinates, $\Delta f = \partial_t^2f - \nabla^2f = -\Box f$, with $\Box = -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2 = \eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu$ the d'Alembertian of [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-4|Theorem §C1.1.4]]. The wave equation is $\Delta f = 0$.
> 4. ⚑ **Positivity.** For $f$ of compact support, $(f, \Delta f) = (df, df) = \int g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu f\,\partial_\nu f\;\mathrm{vol}$. For a Riemannian metric this is $\ge 0$, and $0$ only for $f = 0$: $\Delta$ is a positive operator. For a Lorentzian metric it has no sign; in Minkowski spacetime the integrand is $-(\partial_tf)^2 + |\nabla f|^2$.
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* any chart, any dimension and signature; part 4 for compactly supported $f$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.37) and the sentence before it (p. 79: "only positive definite on Riemannian manifolds"; stated without proof) · Hartle, Box 20.1, item (j) (p. 437: $\nabla^2f = g^{-1/2}\partial_i(g^{1/2}g^{ij}\partial_jf)$, the same formula with the opposite sign) · proof written here · checked by script (Cartesian, spherical, $S^2$, Minkowski, and $(f, \Delta f) = (df, df)$ for $f = e^{-r^2}$ on $\mathbf R^3$)*

^thm-c3-4-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). $df$ has the components $\partial_\nu f$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1, $r = 0$), raised $(df)^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu f$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]], part 2). [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]] with $p = 1$ has no free index left:
>
> $$
> \Delta f = d^\dagger df = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\,(df)^\mu\bigr) = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{|g|}\,g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu f\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> and $d\,d^\dagger f = 0$ because $d^\dagger f = 0$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-3|Def. §C3.4.3]]).
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). $g_{\mu\nu} = \delta_{ij}$: $\sqrt g = 1$, $g^{ij} = \delta^{ij}$, and part 1 gives $-\partial_i\partial_if$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 3). $g = \eta$: $\sqrt{-g} = 1$, $\eta^{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$, and part 1 gives $-\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu f = -(-\partial_t^2f + \nabla^2f) = \partial_t^2f - \nabla^2f$.
>
> **Step 4** (part 4). [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]], part 2, with $\alpha = f$ (compact support) and $\beta = df$: $(df, df) = (f, d^\dagger df) = (f, \Delta f)$. By [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-2|Def. §C3.4.2]], $\langle df, df\rangle = g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu f\,\partial_\nu f$. For a Riemannian metric this is $\ge 0$ at every point, so $(df, df) \ge 0$; if it is $0$, then $df = 0$ everywhere (a continuous nonnegative integrand with zero integral against the positive density $\sqrt g$ vanishes), so $f$ is constant on each connected component, and compact support makes it $0$ on a noncompact component. (On a compact manifold the constants are harmonic: $\Delta1 = 0$.) For $\eta$, $\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu f\partial_\nu f = -(\partial_tf)^2 + |\nabla f|^2$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The Laplacian of every chart, curvilinear or curved, is one formula, the divergence of the gradient with the density $\sqrt{|g|}$. The overall minus sign is the price of making $d^\dagger$ the adjoint of $d$, and it is what makes $\Delta$ positive.

> [!caution] Caution: Laplacian, d'Alembertian and their signs
> - **Three operators, one formula.** On functions, $\Delta f = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu f)$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]]). The d'Alembertian of these notes is $\Box f = +\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu f) = -\Delta f$, equal to $-\partial_t^2f + \nabla^2f$ in inertial coordinates ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-4|Theorem §C1.1.4]]); in general it is $g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu f$, with the covariant derivative of [[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative|§C3.5]]. On $\mathbf R^3$, $\nabla^2 = -\Delta$.
> - **Couzens' $\Box$.** GR1 (4.36)–(4.37) writes $\Box$ for $\Delta$. A wave equation written $\Box\phi = 0$ is the same equation in either convention, but $\Box\phi = m^2\phi$ is not: here the Klein–Gordon equation is $(\Box - m^2)\phi = 0$, that is $(\Delta + m^2)\phi = 0$.
> - **Level B.** Level B's $\Box = \partial_t^2/c^2 - \nabla^2$ ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^cau-b2-2-3|§B2.2, Caution: The sign of the box]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) equals $\Delta$ of Minkowski spacetime here, and $-\Box$ of level C.
>

^cau-c3-4-2

The volume form, the star, the codifferential and the Laplacian used only partial derivatives and $\sqrt{|g|}$. They could, because every derivative in them acts on the components of a form or of a density, where the non-tensorial terms of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]] cancel by antisymmetry or are absorbed by the Jacobian. A vector field that is not differentiated in a divergence has no such protection: $\partial_\mu V^\nu$ is still not a tensor, and the Lie derivative needs a whole vector field, not a direction at a point ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors|§C2.5]]). Which extra structure gives a derivative of any tensor along a single direction, and how does the metric pick it? [[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative|§C3.5]] begins the answer.

> [!example] Example §C3.4.1: Cross Product, Curl and Divergence as Hodge Duals in ℝ³
> *Setup.* $\mathbf R^3$ with the Euclidean metric and the orientation $(x, y, z)$. In Cartesian coordinates $\sqrt g = 1$, $g^{ij} = \delta^{ij}$, so raising changes no component and $\epsilon_{ijk} = \tilde\epsilon_{ijk}$ ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^def-c3-3-2|Def. §C3.3.2]]); $s = 1$ and $\star\star = 1$ in every degree ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]]).
>
> *The star on basis forms.* For $\omega = dx$, $\omega^i = \delta^i_1$ and $(\star dx)_{jk} = \omega^i\epsilon_{ijk} = \epsilon_{1jk}$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]): the only independent component is $(\star dx)_{23} = 1$, so $\star dx = dy\wedge dz$. Cyclically, $\star dy = dz\wedge dx$, $\star dz = dx\wedge dy$. For $\omega = dx\wedge dy$, $\omega^{12} = -\omega^{21} = 1$ and $(\star\omega)_k = \frac12(\omega^{12}\epsilon_{12k} + \omega^{21}\epsilon_{21k}) = \epsilon_{12k}$, so $\star(dx\wedge dy) = dz$, and cyclically. Finally $\star1 = dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$ and $\star(dx\wedge dy\wedge dz) = 1$.
>
> *Cross product* (GR1, footnote 18). For vectors $\vec a$, $\vec b$ take the one-forms $a^\flat = a_i\,dx^i$, $b^\flat = b_i\,dx^i$. Their wedge has the components $(a^\flat\wedge b^\flat)_{ij} = a_ib_j - a_jb_i$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]]), and
>
> $$
> \bigl(\star(a^\flat\wedge b^\flat)\bigr)_k = \frac12\,(a_ib_j - a_jb_i)\,\epsilon_{ijk} = \epsilon_{ijk}\,a_ib_j = (\vec a\times\vec b)_k ,
> $$
>
> the two halves being equal after renaming $i \leftrightarrow j$. So $\vec a\times\vec b = \bigl(\star(a^\flat\wedge b^\flat)\bigr)^\sharp$: the cross product is the oriented area element $a^\flat\wedge b^\flat$, turned into a one-form by the star and into a vector by the metric. For $\vec a = (1, 2, 0)$, $\vec b = (0, 1, 3)$: $a^\flat\wedge b^\flat$ has the components $(a\wedge b)_{12} = 1\cdot1 - 2\cdot0 = 1$, $(a\wedge b)_{23} = 2\cdot3 - 0\cdot1 = 6$, $(a\wedge b)_{31} = 0\cdot0 - 1\cdot3 = -3$, and the star reads them off as $\vec a\times\vec b = (6, -3, 1)$. The construction needs a metric (to lower and raise) and an orientation (the sign of $\epsilon$): under a reflection $\vec a\times\vec b$ does not transform as a vector, the pseudovector property of [[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^cau-c3-3-1|Caution: The sign of ε on spacetime, and level B]].
>
> *Curl.* For $V^\flat = V_i\,dx^i$, $(dV^\flat)_{ij} = \partial_iV_j - \partial_jV_i$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]]), and by the same computation $(\star dV^\flat)_k = \epsilon_{ijk}\partial_iV_j = (\nabla\times\vec V)_k$: $\nabla\times\vec V = (\star dV^\flat)^\sharp$.
>
> *Divergence.* $\star V^\flat = V_x\,dy\wedge dz + V_y\,dz\wedge dx + V_z\,dx\wedge dy$, the two-form that [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-1|Example §C2.6.1]] identified with $\vec V$; its $d$ is $(\nabla\cdot\vec V)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$, and the star of that is $\nabla\cdot\vec V$. So $\nabla\cdot\vec V = \star d\star V^\flat = -d^\dagger V^\flat$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]], part 3, $d^\dagger = (-1)^p\star d\star$ with $p = 1$). With the gradient $(df)^\sharp$, the Laplacian is $\nabla^2f = \star d\star df = -\Delta f$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]]). The dictionary of Example §C2.6.1 is the Hodge star, and $d^2 = 0$ becomes $\nabla\times\nabla f = 0$, $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec V) = 0$.
>
> *Where the metric shows: spherical coordinates.* Take the rotation field $\vec V = -y\,\partial_x + x\,\partial_y$, whose curl is $(0, 0, 2)$. In spherical coordinates it is $\partial_\varphi$, and $V^\flat = g_{\varphi\varphi}\,d\varphi = r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi$. Then
>
> $$
> dV^\flat = 2r\sin^2\theta\;dr\wedge d\varphi + 2r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\;d\theta\wedge d\varphi .
> $$
>
> With $\epsilon_{r\theta\varphi} = r^2\sin\theta$ and $g^{rr} = 1$, $g^{\theta\theta} = r^{-2}$, $g^{\varphi\varphi} = (r\sin\theta)^{-2}$, the components $(\star\omega)_k = \frac12\omega^{ij}\epsilon_{ijk}$ are
>
> $$
> (\star dV^\flat)_r = (dV^\flat)^{\theta\varphi}\epsilon_{\theta\varphi r} = \frac{2r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r^2\cdot r^2\sin^2\theta}\cdot r^2\sin\theta = 2\cos\theta, \qquad (\star dV^\flat)_\theta = (dV^\flat)^{\varphi r}\epsilon_{\varphi r\theta} = -\frac{2r\sin^2\theta}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\cdot r^2\sin\theta = -2r\sin\theta ,
> $$
>
> and $(\star dV^\flat)_\varphi = 0$ (it would need $(dV^\flat)^{r\theta} = 0$). So $\star dV^\flat = 2\cos\theta\,dr - 2r\sin\theta\,d\theta = 2\,dz$, since $z = r\cos\theta$: the curl is $2\hat z$ in these coordinates too. The factors $r^2$, $\sin\theta$ that enter, and that make the curvilinear curl formulas of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|EM Theorem §B1.1.5]] look different from the Cartesian ones, all come from $\sqrt g$ and $g^{ij}$. The same way, $\star dr = r^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi$ and $\star d\star dr = \star\bigl(2r\sin\theta\,dr\wedge d\theta\wedge d\varphi\bigr) = 2/r$, the divergence of the unit radial field.
>

> [!example] Example §C3.4.2: Maxwell's Equations dF = 0 and d⋆F = μ₀⋆J
> *Setup.* Spacetime $(M, g)$, oriented, with the electromagnetic potential $A$, a one-form, its field strength $F = dA$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-3|Example §C2.6.3]]) and a current $J^\mu$, written as the one-form $J = J_\mu dx^\mu$. Level C's Maxwell equations in inertial coordinates are $\partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0J^\mu$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^ex-c1-2-4|Example §C1.2.4]]; [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-2|EM Theorem §C1.2.2]], signature $+{-}{-}{-}$ there); in a curved spacetime they read $\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_\nu(\sqrt{-g}F^{\mu\nu}) = \mu_0J^\mu$ (the coupling to curvature is [[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]]). $G = c = 1$; restored, $x^0 = ct$ and $F_{0i} = -E_i/c$.
>
> *Step 1 (homogeneous pair).* $dF = d\,dA = 0$, with no metric: $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ and Faraday's law ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-3|Example §C2.6.3]]; [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-4|EM Theorem §C1.2.4]]).
>
> *Step 2 ($\star F$ in terms of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$).* In an inertial chart $F_{0i} = -E_i$, $F_{ij} = \epsilon_{ijk}B_k$, so $F^{0i} = E_i$, $F^{ij} = \epsilon_{ijk}B_k$, and with $\epsilon_{0123} = 1$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]]; a pair of indices moves past a pair with no sign, $\epsilon_{\mu\nu0i} = \epsilon_{0i\mu\nu}$),
>
> $$
> (\star F)_{0i} = \tfrac12F^{\mu\nu}\epsilon_{\mu\nu0i} = \tfrac12\epsilon_{jkl}B_l\,\epsilon_{0ijk} = \tfrac12\epsilon_{jkl}\epsilon_{ijk}B_l = B_i, \qquad (\star F)_{ij} = F^{0k}\epsilon_{0kij} = \epsilon_{kij}E_k = \epsilon_{ijk}E_k .
> $$
>
> So, with $\mathcal B = B_i\,dx^i$ and $\mathcal E = E_x\,dy\wedge dz + E_y\,dz\wedge dx + E_z\,dx\wedge dy$,
>
> $$
> F = -dt\wedge(E_i\,dx^i) + \bigl(B_x\,dy\wedge dz + \dots\bigr), \qquad \star F = dt\wedge\mathcal B + \mathcal E :
> $$
>
> the star replaces $(\vec E, \vec B)$ by $(-\vec B, \vec E)$, and twice by $(-\vec E, -\vec B)$, the $\star\star = -1$ of [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]], part 5. In particular $\star(dt\wedge dx) = -dy\wedge dz$ and $\star(dy\wedge dz) = dt\wedge dx$.
>
> *Step 3 (inhomogeneous pair as $d^\dagger F = \mu_0J$).* By [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]] with $p = 2$, $(d^\dagger F)^\nu = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-g}F^{\mu\nu}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-g}F^{\nu\mu})$, so the Maxwell equation is $d^\dagger F = \mu_0J$. On spacetime $d^\dagger = \star d\star$ (part 3 of that theorem): $\star d\star F = \mu_0J$. Apply $\star$; on the three-form $d\star F$, $\star\star = +1$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]]):
>
> $$
> d\star F = \mu_0\star J, \qquad dF = 0 .
> $$
>
> These hold in every chart of every spacetime; the metric enters only through $\star$.
>
> *Step 4 (check in an inertial frame).* $(\star J)_{\lambda\mu\nu} = J^\sigma\epsilon_{\sigma\lambda\mu\nu}$: $(\star J)_{123} = J^0 = \rho$ and $(\star J)_{023} = J^1\epsilon_{1023} = -J_x$, so $\star J = \rho\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz - dt\wedge(J_x\,dy\wedge dz + J_y\,dz\wedge dx + J_z\,dx\wedge dy)$. On the other side, split $d = dt\wedge\partial_t + d_s$ as in Example §C2.6.3: $d(dt\wedge\mathcal B) = -dt\wedge d_s\mathcal B$ and $d\mathcal E = dt\wedge\partial_t\mathcal E + (\nabla\cdot\vec E)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-1|Example §C2.6.1]]), where $d_s\mathcal B$ is the two-form of $\nabla\times\vec B$. Comparing the parts with and without $dt$:
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec E = \mu_0\rho, \qquad \partial_t\vec E - \nabla\times\vec B = -\mu_0\vec J ,
> $$
>
> Gauss's law and the Ampère–Maxwell law ($\mu_0\rho = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ at $c = 1$).
>
> *Step 5 (conservation and charge).* $d\star J = \mu_0^{-1}\,d\,d\star F = 0$, which in components is $\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-g}J^\mu) = 0$ ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]], part 1). On a slice $\Sigma = \{t = \text{const}\}$ oriented by $(x^1, x^2, x^3)$, $(\star J)_{123} = \sqrt{-g}\,J^0$, so $\int_\Sigma\star J = \int\sqrt{-g}\,J^0\,d^3x = Q$, the conserved charge of [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]], part 3. With Stokes' theorem ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]]), for a region $\Sigma_R$ of the slice with boundary sphere $S^2_R$ (outward normal first),
>
> $$
> Q = \int_{\Sigma_R}\star J = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\int_{\Sigma_R}d\star F = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\oint_{S^2_R}\star F :
> $$
>
> Gauss's law in integral form, on any spacetime.
>
> *Step 6 (Coulomb field).* In spherical coordinates of Minkowski spacetime, $A = -\frac{\mu_0Q}{4\pi r}\,dt$ (so $V = \mu_0Q/4\pi r = Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$). Then $F = dA = -\frac{\mu_0Q}{4\pi r^2}\,dt\wedge dr$, $F_{tr} = -E_r$. With $F^{tr} = g^{tt}g^{rr}F_{tr} = \frac{\mu_0Q}{4\pi r^2}$ and $\epsilon_{tr\theta\varphi} = r^2\sin\theta$,
>
> $$
> (\star F)_{\theta\varphi} = F^{tr}\,\epsilon_{tr\theta\varphi} = \frac{\mu_0Q}{4\pi r^2}\,r^2\sin\theta, \qquad \star F = \frac{\mu_0Q}{4\pi}\,\sin\theta\;d\theta\wedge d\varphi, \qquad \frac1{\mu_0}\oint_{S^2_R}\star F = \frac{Q}{4\pi}\cdot4\pi = Q ,
> $$
>
> for every $R$, and $d\star F = 0$ for $r > 0$ (GR2's Example 5.7, with $\mu_0/4\pi \to 1$).
>
> *Step 7 (the action and the invariants).* By [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-2|Theorem §C3.4.2]], $F\wedge\star F = \frac12F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\,\mathrm{vol} = (B^2 - E^2)\,\mathrm{vol}$, and the Maxwell action is $-\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int F\wedge\star F$. The metric-free four-form $F\wedge F = -2\,\vec E\cdot\vec B\;\mathrm{vol}$ in an inertial frame, and $F_{\mu\nu}(\star F)^{\mu\nu} = +4\,\vec E\cdot\vec B$; level B's $F_{\mu\nu}\tilde F^{\mu\nu} = -4\,\vec E\cdot\vec B/c$ has the opposite sign because the two levels' $\epsilon$ differ by a sign ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor#^cau-c3-3-1|Caution: The sign of ε on spacetime, and level B]]).
>
> Chapter C11 builds on Steps 2 and 5. The exchange of $(\vec E, \vec B)$ for $(-\vec B, \vec E)$ by the star is electromagnetic duality, which maps solutions of the source-free Einstein–Maxwell equations to solutions with the same metric ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^thm-c11-1-4|Theorem §C11.1.4]]). The surface integral of $\star F$ becomes the definition of electric charge ([[§C11.5 Charges in Curved Spacetime#^def-c11-5-1|Def. §C11.5.1]]), conserved and independent of the surface wherever no current flows between ([[§C11.5 Charges in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c11-5-3|Theorem §C11.5.3]]).

> [!example] Example §C3.4.3: The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates from the Formula
> *Setup.* [[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]], part 1, $\Delta f = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu f)$, for three diagonal metrics; for a diagonal metric $g^{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(1/g_{\mu\mu})$ and the double sum has only the terms $\mu = \nu$.
>
> *(a) $\mathbf R^3$, spherical coordinates.* $\sqrt g = r^2\sin\theta$, $g^{rr} = 1$, $g^{\theta\theta} = r^{-2}$, $g^{\varphi\varphi} = (r\sin\theta)^{-2}$. The three terms:
>
> $$
> \partial_r\bigl(r^2\sin\theta\,\partial_rf\bigr) = \sin\theta\,\partial_r(r^2\partial_rf), \qquad \partial_\theta\Bigl(r^2\sin\theta\cdot\frac{1}{r^2}\,\partial_\theta f\Bigr) = \partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f), \qquad \partial_\varphi\Bigl(r^2\sin\theta\cdot\frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\,\partial_\varphi f\Bigr) = \frac{\partial_\varphi^2f}{\sin\theta} .
> $$
>
> Dividing by $-r^2\sin\theta$,
>
> $$
> \Delta f = -\Bigl[\frac{1}{r^2}\partial_r\bigl(r^2\partial_rf\bigr) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\partial_\theta\bigl(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f\bigr) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\partial_\varphi^2f\Bigr] = -\nabla^2f ,
> $$
>
> the spherical Laplacian of [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|EM Theorem §B1.1.5]], obtained without scale factors or unit vectors. Checks: $\Delta(1/r) = -r^{-2}\partial_r(r^2\cdot(-r^{-2})) = 0$ for $r > 0$; $\Delta r^2 = -r^{-2}\partial_r(2r^3) = -6$.
>
> *(b) The sphere of radius $a$.* $\sqrt g = a^2\sin\theta$, $g^{\theta\theta} = a^{-2}$, $g^{\varphi\varphi} = (a\sin\theta)^{-2}$:
>
> $$
> \Delta f = -\frac{1}{a^2}\Bigl[\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_\theta\bigl(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f\bigr) + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\partial_\varphi^2f\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> For $f = \cos\theta$: $\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta\cos\theta = -\sin^2\theta$, $\partial_\theta(-\sin^2\theta) = -2\sin\theta\cos\theta$, so $\Delta\cos\theta = \frac{2}{a^2}\cos\theta$. For $f = 3\cos^2\theta - 1$: $\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f = -6\sin^2\theta\cos\theta$, $\partial_\theta(-6\sin^2\theta\cos\theta) = -12\sin\theta\cos^2\theta + 6\sin^3\theta = \sin\theta\,(6 - 18\cos^2\theta)$, so $\Delta f = -\frac{1}{a^2}(6 - 18\cos^2\theta) = \frac{6}{a^2}f$. The eigenvalues $\ell(\ell + 1)/a^2$ with $\ell = 1, 2$ are positive, as part 4 of the theorem requires; these are the spherical harmonics of quantum mechanics, where $L^2 = \hbar^2a^2\Delta$ on the sphere of radius $a$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-7|QM Theorem §B5.2.7]]).
>
> *(c) Minkowski spacetime, spherical coordinates.* $\sqrt{-g} = r^2\sin\theta$ and $g^{tt} = -1$; the $t$-term is $-\frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\partial_t\bigl(r^2\sin\theta\cdot(-1)\,\partial_tf\bigr) = \partial_t^2f$, and the spatial terms are those of (a). So $\Delta f = \partial_t^2f - \nabla^2f = -\Box f$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^thm-c3-4-4|Theorem §C3.4.4]], part 3), and a spherical wave $f = h(t - r)/r$ solves $\Delta f = 0$, since $\nabla^2(h(t - r)/r) = r^{-1}\partial_r^2h(t - r) = \partial_t^2f$ by the first term of (a).
>
> *(d) Positivity, a number.* On $\mathbf R^3$ with $f = e^{-r^2}$: $\partial_rf = -2re^{-r^2}$, and $(f, \Delta f) = (df, df) = 4\pi\int_0^\infty4r^4e^{-2r^2}\,dr = 16\pi\cdot\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{64} = \frac{3\sqrt2\,\pi^{3/2}}{4} \approx 5.906 > 0$, using $\int_0^\infty r^4e^{-2r^2}\,dr = \frac{3\sqrt\pi}{8\cdot2^{5/2}} = \frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{64}$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.37) (p. 79) · Hartle, Box 20.1, item (j) (p. 437) · all four parts checked by script*
