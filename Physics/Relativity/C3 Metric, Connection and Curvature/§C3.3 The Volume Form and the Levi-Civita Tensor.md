[[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor|§C3.1]] gave the manifold a metric, and with it the lengths of vectors and of curves. One job from chapter C2 is still open: volume. A bare manifold integrates top forms, not functions: to integrate a function one has to choose a nowhere-vanishing top form to multiply it with, and nothing selects one ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^rem-c2-7-1|§C2.7, Remark: No canonical volume without a metric]]). Without that choice an action $\int\mathcal L\,d^4x$ cannot be written in general coordinates. The question of this section is: *what does a metric give for areas, volumes and integrals of functions, in every chart?*

A naive rule fails, and its failure shows what is needed. **Area.** Take the unit sphere and integrate the function $1$ against the coordinates. In the chart $(\theta, \varphi) \in (0, \pi)\times(0, 2\pi)$,

$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi 1\;d\theta\,d\varphi = 2\pi^2 \approx 19.74 ,
$$

and in the stereographic chart $(u, v)$, which covers the sphere minus one point with $u, v \in \mathbf R$, $\int\!\!\int du\,dv$ diverges. Neither is the area $4\pi \approx 12.57$, and they disagree with each other: a function is not a density ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 2). The metric supplies a density. Its determinant is $\det g = \sin^2\theta$ in the first chart ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]] with $a = 1$), and in the stereographic chart the induced metric is $4(du^2 + dv^2)/(1 + u^2 + v^2)^2$, so $\det g = 16/(1 + u^2 + v^2)^4$. Integrating $\det g$ itself gives $\int\!\!\int\sin^2\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi = \pi^2$, still wrong. Its square root works in both charts (with $\rho^2 = u^2 + v^2$):

$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\;d\theta\,d\varphi = 2\pi\cdot2 = 4\pi, \qquad \int\!\!\int\frac{4\;du\,dv}{(1 + u^2 + v^2)^2} = 2\pi\int_0^\infty\frac{4\rho\;d\rho}{(1 + \rho^2)^2} = 2\pi\Bigl[-\frac{2}{1 + \rho^2}\Bigr]_0^\infty = 4\pi .
$$

So the density that turns coordinate area into area is $\sqrt{\det g}$, not $\det g$, and in Lorentzian signature, where $\det g < 0$, even the root needs a modulus. The section explains both facts and builds the object that $\sqrt{|\det g|}$ is the coefficient of. The determinant of the metric is a density of weight $2$ whose sign is fixed by the signature ([[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]); its square root makes the volume form ([[#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]), the same in every positively oriented chart and equal to $1$ on orthonormal bases ([[#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]]). Read as a tensor it is the Levi-Civita tensor ([[#^def-c3-3-2|Def. §C3.3.2]]), whose raised form and contractions are computed in [[#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]]; its overall sign on spacetime differs from level B's, which [[#^cau-c3-3-1|Caution: The sign of ε on spacetime, and level B]] reconciles. The example computes the area of a sphere in two charts, the volume of a ball, the volume inside a sphere of a curved three-space, and a four-volume of spacetime. The contraction identities are the tool of the next section, where $\epsilon$ turns $p$-forms into $(n - p)$-forms.

**Setup.** A smooth manifold $M$ of dimension $n$ with a metric $g$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]) of signature $(q, n - q)$, $q$ negative signs ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]); Riemannian means $q = 0$, Lorentzian $q = 1$, and spacetime is the Lorentzian case $n = 4$. $M$ is oriented ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]]), and "chart" means a positively oriented chart unless said otherwise; on spacetime the inertial coordinates $(t, x, y, z)$ of Minkowski spacetime are positively oriented. Tensors and densities are those of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields|§C2.3]]; top forms and $\wedge$ are those of [[§C2.6 Differential Forms|§C2.6]], in the determinant convention ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^cau-c2-6-1|§C2.6, Caution: Normalizations of the wedge product]]). Integrals of forms are those of [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem|§C2.7]]; integrals over $M$ are taken over compact supports or where they converge. Every statement is an exact theorem of geometry, in any dimension and signature; no physical model enters. Every sign and number in this section was checked by script, for $n = 2, 3, 4$ and both signatures.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$. $g \equiv \det(g_{\mu\nu})$ in a chart and $s \equiv \operatorname{sign} g = (-1)^q$; $\sqrt{|g|}$ is $\sqrt g$ for Riemannian and $\sqrt{-g}$ for Lorentzian metrics. $\tilde\epsilon$ is the Levi-Civita symbol ($\tilde\epsilon_{1\cdots n} = \tilde\epsilon^{1\cdots n} = +1$ in every chart, [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-7|Def. §C2.3.7]]); $\epsilon$ without tilde is the Levi-Civita tensor, $\epsilon_{0123} = +\sqrt{-g}$, $\epsilon^{0123} = -1/\sqrt{-g}$ on spacetime, opposite in sign to level B's ([[#^cau-c3-3-1|Caution: The sign of ε on spacetime, and level B]]).

## The volume form

The opening found that $\sqrt{\det g}$, not $\det g$, turns coordinate area into area. The reason is the transformation law of the determinant, and its sign is what needs a modulus on a Lorentzian manifold.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.1: det g Is a Density of Weight 2; Its Sign Is Fixed by the Signature
> Let $g$ be a metric on $M$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]) and $g = \det(g_{\mu\nu})$ its determinant in a chart $x^\mu$.
> 1. **Weight 2.** In a second chart $x'^\mu$, with $J = \det(\partial x^\mu/\partial x'^\nu)$ at the same point,
>
> $$
> g' = J^2\,g :
> $$
>
> $g$ is a scalar density of weight $+2$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-6|Def. §C2.3.6]]), the case $T = g$ of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 2 (and of [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 3).
> 2. **Sign.** $g \ne 0$ at every point, and its sign is $s = (-1)^q$, with $q$ the number of negative signs of the signature ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]): the same in every chart and, on a connected manifold, at every point. For a Riemannian metric $g > 0$; for a Lorentzian metric $g < 0$.
> 3. **Its root.** $\sqrt{|g|}$ is a smooth positive function in each chart and a scalar density of weight $+1$ under changes of chart with $J > 0$: $\sqrt{|g'|} = J\sqrt{|g|}$. Under a change with $J < 0$, $\sqrt{|g'|} = |J|\sqrt{|g|} = -J\sqrt{|g|}$.
>
> *Examples.* The plane in polar coordinates: $g = r^2 = J^2\cdot1$ with $J = \det\partial(x, y)/\partial(r, \theta) = r$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^ex-c2-3-2|Example §C2.3.2]]). Minkowski spacetime in spherical coordinates: $g = -r^4\sin^2\theta = J^2\cdot(-1)$ with $J = \det\partial(t, x, y, z)/\partial(t, r, \theta, \varphi) = r^2\sin\theta$, and $\sqrt{-g} = r^2\sin\theta$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-3|Example §C3.1.3]]).
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a manifold with a metric; *applies:* every chart, every point; part 3 with the sign of $J$ as stated.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.26)–(4.27) (p. 78) · d'Inverno & Vickers §7.3, eq. (7.5) and the text after it (pp. 117–118: "$g$ is a scalar density of weight $+2$", "$\sqrt{-g}$ is a scalar density of weight $+1$") · signs checked by script*

^thm-c3-3-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). The components of $g$ transform as those of a $(0, 2)$ tensor ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 2), $g'_{\mu\nu} = P^\alpha{}_\mu P^\beta{}_\nu\,g_{\alpha\beta}$ with $P^\alpha{}_\mu = \partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\mu$, that is $g' = P^{\mathsf T}gP$ as matrices. The determinant is multiplicative ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]) and $\det P^{\mathsf T} = \det P = J$ ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-56|LADR Thm. 9.56]]):
>
> $$
> \det g' = \det P^{\mathsf T}\,\det g\,\det P = J^2\,\det g .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). At a point $p$ there is a basis $e_a = E^\mu{}_a\,\partial_\mu$ of $T_pM$ with $g(e_a, e_b) = \eta_{ab} = \operatorname{diag}(-1, \dots, -1, +1, \dots, +1)$, $q$ entries $-1$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 4). In matrices $\eta = E^{\mathsf T}gE$, where $E = (E^\mu{}_a)$ is invertible because the $e_a$ form a basis. Taking determinants as in Step 1,
>
> $$
> (-1)^q = \det\eta = (\det E)^2\,g \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad g = \frac{(-1)^q}{(\det E)^2} ,
> $$
>
> so $g \ne 0$ and $\operatorname{sign} g = (-1)^q$. The number $q$ does not depend on the chart or the basis (Sylvester's law, same part 4), and it is the same at every point of a connected manifold ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]).
>
> **Step 3** (part 3). By part 2, $|g| = s\,g$ with the constant $s$, so $|g|$ is a smooth function, nowhere zero, and $\sqrt{|g|}$ is smooth and positive. By part 1, $|g'| = J^2|g|$, so $\sqrt{|g'|} = |J|\sqrt{|g|}$, which is $J\sqrt{|g|}$ when $J > 0$ and $-J\sqrt{|g|}$ when $J < 0$.
>
> **Step 4** (the examples). For polar coordinates the Jacobian matrix $\partial(x, y)/\partial(r, \theta)$ has the rows $(\cos\theta, -r\sin\theta)$ and $(\sin\theta, r\cos\theta)$, determinant $r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r$. For spherical coordinates in space the determinant of $\partial(x, y, z)/\partial(r, \theta, \varphi)$ is $r^2\sin\theta$ (the volume element of [[§22 Properties of the Integral#^thm-22-6|452 Thm. §22.6]]), and $t$ is unchanged, so $J = r^2\sin\theta$ and $J^2\cdot\det\eta = -r^4\sin^2\theta$, which is $\det\operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, r^2, r^2\sin^2\theta)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The sign of $\det g$ is a property of the metric, not of the chart, which is what makes the single rule "take $\sqrt{|g|}$" possible; on a Lorentzian manifold it means $\sqrt{-g}$. Only the *root* has weight $1$, the power that the change-of-variables formula needs.

A density of weight $1$ is what a top form's coefficient is ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 2). So $\sqrt{|g|}$ can be the coefficient of a top form that is the same in every chart: the canonical choice that §C2.7 could not make.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.3.1: Metric Volume Form
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be an oriented manifold with a metric. The **volume form** of $g$ is the $n$-form $\mathrm{vol} \in \Omega^n(M)$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]]) given in every positively oriented chart by
>
> $$
> \mathrm{vol} = \sqrt{|g|}\;dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n ; \qquad\text{on spacetime}\qquad \mathrm{vol} = \sqrt{-g}\;dx^0\wedge dx^1\wedge dx^2\wedge dx^3 .
> $$
>
> That this is one form, whichever positively oriented chart is used, is [[#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]].
>
> *Components.* An $n$-form has one independent component, and the expansion $\omega = \sum_{\mu_1 < \dots < \mu_n}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{\mu_n}$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]]) has the single term $\mu_k = k$. So
>
> $$
> \mathrm{vol}_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \sqrt{|g|}\;\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}, \qquad \mathrm{vol} = \frac{1}{n!}\sqrt{|g|}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}\,dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{\mu_n} ,
> $$
>
> the totally antisymmetric tensor $v_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}$ of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 4.
>
> *Integrals of functions.* With $\mathrm{vol}$ as the nowhere-vanishing top form of [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-2|Def. §C2.7.2]], part 3, a function $f$ of compact support has the integral
>
> $$
> \int_Mf\,\mathrm{vol} = \int f\,\sqrt{|g|}\;d^nx \qquad(\text{support in one chart}) ,
> $$
>
> and the **volume** of a region $U$ is $\mathrm{Vol}(U) = \int_U\mathrm{vol}$: length for $n = 1$, area for $n = 2$, the four-volume of a region of spacetime for $n = 4$. Physics writes $\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x$ for $\mathrm{vol}$ on spacetime: an action is $S = \int\mathcal L\,\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x$ with a scalar $\mathcal L$.
>
> *Names.* Couzens writes $\mathrm{vol}(M)$ (GR1 (4.22)–(4.23)) and, in GR2, $d\mathrm{vol}$; Hartle writes $dv = \sqrt{-g}\,d^4x$ for four-volume and $dA$, $dV$ for area and three-volume of a diagonal metric.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.22)–(4.23), (4.29) (pp. 77–78) · Couzens, GR2, App. A, eqs. (A.28)–(A.29) (p. 182) · Hartle §7.6, eqs. (7.28)–(7.30) (pp. 146–147) · d'Inverno & Vickers §7.3 (p. 118: "$\sqrt{-g}$ plays an important role in integration")*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.2: The Volume Form Is Chart-Independent
> Let $(M, g)$ be an oriented manifold with a metric.
> 1. **Chart independence.** The formula of [[#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]] gives the same $n$-form in any two positively oriented charts.
> 2. **Value on orthonormal bases.** If $e_1, \dots, e_n$ is an orthonormal basis of $T_pM$, $g(e_a, e_b) = \eta_{ab}$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]), that is positively oriented (in the class that the orientation selects at $p$, [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]], part 3), then
>
> $$
> \mathrm{vol}(e_1, \dots, e_n) = 1 ,
> $$
>
> and $\mathrm{vol}_p$ is the only $n$-form at $p$ with this property. A unit cube spanned by orthonormal vectors has volume $1$, which is the reason $\mathrm{vol}$ deserves its name.
> 3. **Orientation.** In a negatively oriented chart the same formula gives $-\mathrm{vol}$. Reversing the orientation of $M$ replaces $\mathrm{vol}$ by $-\mathrm{vol}$, and the integral $\int_Mf\,\mathrm{vol}$ of a function does not change: volumes do not depend on the orientation chosen.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of an oriented manifold with a metric; *applies:* any signature, any dimension, every point.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.24)–(4.28) ("which is therefore invariant", pp. 77–78) · d'Inverno & Vickers §7.3 (pp. 117–118) · part 2 written here*

^thm-c3-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1, components). The components of $\mathrm{vol}$ are $\sqrt{|g|}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}$ ([[#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]). The symbol is a density of weight $-1$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 1) and $\sqrt{|g|}$ one of weight $+1$ for $J > 0$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]], part 3). Their product has weight $0$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 3), so the arrays in two positively oriented charts are related by the tensor law: they are the components of one $(0, n)$ tensor, the same $n$-form.
>
> **Step 2** (part 1, coefficient). The same in terms of the coefficient: for charts $x$ and $y$ with $J = \det(\partial x^\mu/\partial y^\nu) > 0$, $dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n = J\,dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^n$ ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 1), and $\sqrt{|g_x|} = \sqrt{|g_y|}/J$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]], part 3, with the roles of the charts as there: $g_y = J^2g_x$). Hence
>
> $$
> \sqrt{|g_x|}\;dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n = \frac{\sqrt{|g_y|}}{J}\cdot J\;dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^n = \sqrt{|g_y|}\;dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^n .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (part 2, the value). Write $e_a = E^\mu{}_a\partial_\mu$ in a positively oriented chart. Evaluating the $n$-form on the $n$ vectors uses its components ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 1) and the Leibniz formula for the determinant ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-7|Def. §C2.3.7]]):
>
> $$
> \mathrm{vol}(e_1, \dots, e_n) = \sqrt{|g|}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}E^{\mu_1}{}_1\cdots E^{\mu_n}{}_n = \sqrt{|g|}\,\det E .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (part 2, $\det E$). Orthonormality is $E^{\mathsf T}gE = \eta$ in matrices, so $(\det E)^2\,g = \det\eta = (-1)^q$ (Step 2 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]) and $(\det E)^2 = (-1)^q/g = 1/|g|$, because $g$ has the sign $(-1)^q$. So $|\det E| = 1/\sqrt{|g|}$. The basis $(e_a)$ is in the class of the coordinate basis $(\partial_\mu)$ of a positively oriented chart exactly when $\det E > 0$ ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]]). Then $\det E = 1/\sqrt{|g|}$ and Step 3 gives $\mathrm{vol}(e_1, \dots, e_n) = 1$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 2, uniqueness). The $n$-forms at $p$ form a space of dimension $\binom nn = 1$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]], counting), and a nonzero $n$-form is nonzero on every basis (Step 3: its value is a nonzero multiple of a determinant of an invertible matrix). So $\omega = c\,\mathrm{vol}_p$ for some number $c$, and $\omega(e_1, \dots, e_n) = 1$ forces $c = 1$.
>
> **Step 6** (part 3). In a negatively oriented chart $y$ and a positively oriented chart $x$, $J = \det(\partial x/\partial y) < 0$ and $\sqrt{|g_x|} = \sqrt{|g_y|}/|J| = -\sqrt{|g_y|}/J$, so Step 2 becomes $\sqrt{|g_x|}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n = -\sqrt{|g_y|}\,dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^n$. Reversing the orientation of $M$ makes the former negative charts positive, so the volume form of $-M$ is $-\mathrm{vol}$. By [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]], part 3, $\int_{-M}\omega = -\int_M\omega$, so $\int_{-M}f\,(-\mathrm{vol}) = \int_Mf\,\mathrm{vol}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The volume form is fixed by two pieces of structure, the metric (through $|\det E| = 1/\sqrt{|g|}$) and the orientation (through the sign of $\det E$). The integral of a function needs only the first.

## The Levi-Civita tensor

The volume form has a second life as a tensor with $n$ lower indices. Read that way it can be contracted with other tensors, and it is the object that replaces level B's $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ in curved spacetime. Its components with raised indices are not $\tilde\epsilon$ again: the metric that raises them also changes their size and, on a Lorentzian manifold, their sign.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.3.2: The Levi-Civita Tensor
> *Structure.* On an oriented manifold with a metric, the **Levi-Civita tensor** $\epsilon$ is the volume form ([[#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]) regarded as a totally antisymmetric tensor of type $(0, n)$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]). In every positively oriented chart
>
> $$
> \epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \sqrt{|g|}\;\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}, \qquad\text{on spacetime}\qquad \epsilon_{0123} = +\sqrt{-g} ,
> $$
>
> with $\tilde\epsilon$ the Levi-Civita symbol ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-7|Def. §C2.3.7]]). It is a tensor by [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 4 (weights $+1$ and $-1$ cancel); the symbol is not.
>
> *Other heights.* Its indices are raised with the inverse metric like those of any tensor ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]): $\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = g^{\mu_1\nu_1}\cdots g^{\mu_n\nu_n}\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_n}$, and $\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_k}{}_{\mu_{k+1}\dots\mu_n}$ with only the first $k$ raised. The raised components are $s\,\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}/\sqrt{|g|}$, so $\epsilon^{0123} = -1/\sqrt{-g}$ on spacetime ([[#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]], part 1). In the inertial coordinates of Minkowski spacetime, $\epsilon_{0123} = +1$ and $\epsilon^{0123} = -1$.
>
> *Notation.* A tilde marks the symbol, the same numbers in every chart; $\epsilon$ without a tilde is always this tensor, and $\epsilon^{\mu\cdots}$ always means its raised components. Couzens writes $v_{\mu_1\dots\mu_m}$ for the tensor and $\epsilon$ for the symbol at both heights.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.29)–(4.30) (p. 78) · d'Inverno & Vickers §7.2 (pp. 116–117) · Hartle, Box 20.1, item (k) (p. 438: the alternating tensor $g^{-1/2}\epsilon^{ijk}$)*

^def-c3-3-2

Every computation with the Hodge star below reduces to contracting two Levi-Civita tensors over some of their indices. The result is a signed count of permutations, multiplied by one number, the sign of $\det g$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.3: Raised Levi-Civita Tensor and Contraction Identities
> Let $s = \operatorname{sign}\det g = (-1)^q$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]], part 2).
> 1. **Raised components.**
>
> $$
> \epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{|g|}}\;\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} ; \qquad\text{Riemannian: } \epsilon^{1\cdots n} = +\frac{1}{\sqrt g} ; \qquad\text{Lorentzian: } \epsilon^{0123} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}} .
> $$
>
> 2. **Generalized Kronecker delta.** For $m \ge 1$ index lists $(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m)$ and $(\nu_1, \dots, \nu_m)$, let $\delta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_m}_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_m} = \operatorname{sgn}\pi$ if the $\lambda$'s are distinct and $\nu_i = \lambda_{\pi(i)}$ for a permutation $\pi$, and $0$ otherwise; for $m = 0$ it is $1$. Equivalently $\delta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_m}_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_m} = m!\,\delta^{[\nu_1}_{\lambda_1}\cdots\delta^{\nu_m]}_{\lambda_m}$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-5|Def. §C2.3.5]]). The first cases are $\delta^\nu_\lambda$ and $\delta^{\nu_1\nu_2}_{\lambda_1\lambda_2} = \delta^{\nu_1}_{\lambda_1}\delta^{\nu_2}_{\lambda_2} - \delta^{\nu_1}_{\lambda_2}\delta^{\nu_2}_{\lambda_1}$.
> 3. **Contraction over $k$ indices.** For $0 \le k \le n$,
>
> $$
> \epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_k\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{n-k}}\;\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_k\nu_1\dots\nu_{n-k}} = s\;k!\;\delta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_{n-k}}_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{n-k}} .
> $$
>
> In particular the full contraction is $\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = s\,n!$: $6$ on $\mathbf R^3$, $-24$ on spacetime.
> 4. **Cases used below.** On $\mathbf R^3$ with the Euclidean metric, $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon^{ilm} = \delta^l_j\delta^m_k - \delta^l_k\delta^m_j$ and $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon^{ijm} = 2\delta^m_k$. On spacetime, $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta} = -2\bigl(\delta^\alpha_\rho\delta^\beta_\sigma - \delta^\alpha_\sigma\delta^\beta_\rho\bigr)$ and $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\beta} = -6\,\delta^\beta_\sigma$.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of an oriented manifold with a metric; *applies:* every chart and point; any signature.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eq. (4.30) (p. 78: "$\pm1/\sqrt{|g|}$") · d'Inverno & Vickers §7.2 (pp. 116–117: the contractions $\epsilon_{abcd}\epsilon^{ebcd} = 3!\,\delta^e_a$, $\epsilon_{abcd}\epsilon^{abcd} = 4!$ of the symbols) · all parts checked by script for $n = 2, 3, 4$ in both signatures*

^thm-c3-3-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). By [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]], part 1, $g^{\mu\nu}$ is the inverse matrix of $g_{\mu\nu}$, with determinant $1/g$ ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]). A symbol contracted with $n$ copies of a matrix absorbs its determinant ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], Step 1 of the derivation): $M^{\mu_1}{}_{\nu_1}\cdots M^{\mu_n}{}_{\nu_n}\tilde\epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_n} = \det M\,\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n}$, used here with $M = (g^{\mu\nu})$ and the numbers $\tilde\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_n} = \tilde\epsilon^{\nu_1\dots\nu_n}$. So
>
> $$
> \epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \sqrt{|g|}\;g^{\mu_1\nu_1}\cdots g^{\mu_n\nu_n}\tilde\epsilon_{\nu_1\dots\nu_n} = \sqrt{|g|}\cdot\frac1g\cdot\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_n} ,
> $$
>
> since $g = s\,|g|$ gives $\sqrt{|g|}/g = s\sqrt{|g|}/|g| = s/\sqrt{|g|}$ (and $1/s = s$).
>
> **Step 2** (part 2, the two descriptions agree). $m!\,\delta^{[\nu_1}_{\lambda_1}\cdots\delta^{\nu_m]}_{\lambda_m} = \sum_\sigma\operatorname{sgn}\sigma\,\delta^{\nu_{\sigma(1)}}_{\lambda_1}\cdots\delta^{\nu_{\sigma(m)}}_{\lambda_m}$ (antisymmetrization over the upper indices with weight $1/m!$). A term is $1$ exactly when $\nu_{\sigma(i)} = \lambda_i$ for all $i$. If the $\lambda$'s are distinct and the $\nu$'s are a rearrangement of them, exactly one $\sigma$ does this, namely $\sigma = \pi^{-1}$ with $\nu_i = \lambda_{\pi(i)}$, and $\operatorname{sgn}\pi^{-1} = \operatorname{sgn}\pi$. If two $\lambda$'s coincide, the terms cancel in pairs (exchange the two corresponding $\sigma$-values); if the $\nu$'s are not a rearrangement of the $\lambda$'s, no term is nonzero. For $m = 2$ the two permutations give the formula in part 2.
>
> **Step 3** (part 3 for symbols). Fix $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{n-k}$ and $\nu_1, \dots, \nu_{n-k}$ and consider $S = \sum_{\mu_1, \dots, \mu_k}\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\dots\mu_k\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{n-k}}\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_k\nu_1\dots\nu_{n-k}}$. A term is nonzero only if $(\mu, \lambda)$ is a list of $n$ distinct values, that is a rearrangement of $(1, \dots, n)$, and the same for $(\mu, \nu)$. Then the $\lambda$'s are distinct, and the sets $\{\lambda_i\}$ and $\{\nu_i\}$ are both the complement of $\{\mu_j\}$, so they are equal. If $\{\nu_i\} \ne \{\lambda_i\}$ or the $\lambda$'s repeat, every term vanishes and $S = 0 = \delta^{\nu\cdots}_{\lambda\cdots}$. Otherwise write $\nu_i = \lambda_{\pi(i)}$; rearranging the last $n - k$ entries by $\pi$ multiplies the sign of a permutation by $\operatorname{sgn}\pi$, so
>
> $$
> \tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_k\nu_1\dots\nu_{n-k}} = \operatorname{sgn}\pi\;\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_k\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{n-k}}, \qquad \tilde\epsilon_{\mu\cdots\lambda\cdots}\,\tilde\epsilon^{\mu\cdots\nu\cdots} = \operatorname{sgn}\pi\,\bigl(\tilde\epsilon_{\mu\cdots\lambda\cdots}\bigr)^2 = \operatorname{sgn}\pi
> $$
>
> for each of the $k!$ lists $(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_k)$ that run through the complement of $\{\lambda_i\}$ in some order. Summing, $S = k!\,\operatorname{sgn}\pi = k!\,\delta^{\nu_1\dots\nu_{n-k}}_{\lambda_1\dots\lambda_{n-k}}$.
>
> **Step 4** (part 3 for the tensor). By [[#^def-c3-3-2|Def. §C3.3.2]] and part 1, $\epsilon_{\cdots}\epsilon^{\cdots} = \sqrt{|g|}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\cdots}\cdot(s/\sqrt{|g|})\,\tilde\epsilon^{\cdots} = s\,\tilde\epsilon_{\cdots}\tilde\epsilon^{\cdots}$, and Step 3 gives $s\,k!\,\delta$. For $k = n$ the delta has no indices and equals $1$: $s\,n!$, which is $3! = 6$ for $s = 1$, $n = 3$, and $-4! = -24$ for $s = -1$, $n = 4$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 4). On $\mathbf R^3$, $s = 1$: $k = 1$ gives $1!\,\delta^{lm}_{jk}$ and $k = 2$ gives $2!\,\delta^m_k$. On spacetime, $s = -1$: $k = 2$ gives $-2!\,\delta^{\alpha\beta}_{\rho\sigma}$ and $k = 3$ gives $-3!\,\delta^\beta_\sigma$; part 2 writes out $\delta^{\alpha\beta}_{\rho\sigma}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The factors $\sqrt{|g|}$ cancel in every contraction of two $\epsilon$'s, which is therefore the same signed count of permutations as for the symbols, times the sign of $\det g$. That sign is the only trace the signature leaves, and it is the origin of the $\pm$ in every formula of this section.

> [!caution] Caution: The sign of ε on spacetime, and level B
> - **Level C.** $\epsilon_{0123} = +\sqrt{-g}$ in positively oriented charts, and the inertial coordinates $(t, x, y, z)$ are positively oriented. So $\mathrm{vol} = dt\wedge dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$ in an inertial frame, $\epsilon_{0123} = +1$ and $\epsilon^{0123} = -1$ there ([[#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]], part 1). This is Couzens' choice (GR1 (4.29)–(4.30), GR2 (A.29)), and it ties the sign of $\epsilon$ to the orientation: $\epsilon$ is the volume form.
> - **Level B.** $\epsilon^{0123} = +1$ and $\epsilon_{0123} = -1$ ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-2|Def. §B2.2.2]], [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], part 2; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). Lowering all four indices costs $\det\eta = -1$ in either signature, so the two conventions differ by one overall sign at every height: $\epsilon^{\rm C} = -\epsilon^{\rm B}$. Upper components of tensors are the same at both levels ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]), so the difference is a choice of normalization, not of signature.
> - **Converting.** A quantity that contains one $\epsilon$ (or one Hodge star) changes sign under conversion beyond the dictionary of [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]: convert every other factor by the dictionary and replace $\epsilon^{\rm B}$ by $-\epsilon^{\rm C}$. Example: level B has $F_{\mu\nu}\tilde F^{\mu\nu} = -4\,\vec E\cdot\vec B/c$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]]); level C has $F_{\mu\nu}(\star F)^{\mu\nu} = +4\,\vec E\cdot\vec B$ ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential#^ex-c3-4-2|Example §C3.4.2]]). Products of two $\epsilon$'s are unchanged: $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = -24$ at both levels.
> - **Pseudotensor.** Under a change of chart that reverses the orientation, $\epsilon$ changes sign relative to the tensor law ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 4); at level B the same statement is its sign change under parity and time reversal ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], part 1). On an oriented manifold, with positively oriented charts only, $\epsilon$ is a tensor.
> - **Symbol versus tensor in the sources.** Couzens writes $\epsilon^{\mu_1\dots\mu_m}$ for the symbol with $\epsilon^{1\dots m} = 1$ ("we do not raise the indices on $\epsilon$ with the metric") and $v$ for the tensor; here the symbol carries a tilde and $\epsilon^{\mu\cdots}$ is always the raised tensor. [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^cau-c2-3-1|§C2.3, Caution: Conventions for densities and the Levi-Civita symbol]] announced this choice of sign.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.29)–(4.30) (p. 78) · Couzens, GR2, App. A, eqs. (A.29)–(A.30) (p. 182) · signs checked by script*

The volume form and the Levi-Civita tensor are now in hand, together with the rule for contracting two of them. ## Examples

> [!example] Example §C3.3.1: Volume of a Sphere and of a Ball; a Curved Three-Space
> *(a) The sphere of radius $a$.* The round metric $ds^2 = a^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]) has $g = a^4\sin^2\theta$ and, on $0 < \theta < \pi$, $\sqrt g = a^2\sin\theta$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]). With the orientation in which $(\theta, \varphi)$ is positive (outward normal first, [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^ex-c2-7-4|Example §C2.7.4]]), $\mathrm{vol} = a^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi$ ([[#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]). The chart misses a half great circle, a set of measure zero ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]], part 3), so
>
> $$
> \mathrm{Area}(S^2_a) = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi a^2\sin\theta\;d\theta\,d\varphi = a^2\cdot2\pi\cdot\bigl[-\cos\theta\bigr]_0^\pi = 4\pi a^2 .
> $$
>
> In the stereographic chart the induced metric is $4a^2(du^2 + dv^2)/(1 + u^2 + v^2)^2$, so $\sqrt g = 4a^2/(1 + \rho^2)^2$ with $\rho^2 = u^2 + v^2$. That chart is negatively oriented for the outward orientation ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^ex-c2-7-4|Example §C2.7.4]]); by [[#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]], part 3, the area does not depend on the orientation, so it may be computed with the orientation in which the chart is positive. In polar coordinates $(\rho, \phi)$ of the $(u, v)$-plane, $du\,dv = \rho\,d\rho\,d\phi$:
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathbf R^2}\frac{4a^2\;du\,dv}{(1 + u^2 + v^2)^2} = 2\pi\int_0^\infty\frac{4a^2\rho\;d\rho}{(1 + \rho^2)^2} = 2\pi\cdot4a^2\Bigl[-\frac{1}{2(1 + \rho^2)}\Bigr]_0^\infty = 4\pi a^2 .
> $$
>
> *(b) The ball of radius $R$ in $\mathbf R^3$.* In spherical coordinates $g = \operatorname{diag}(1, r^2, r^2\sin^2\theta)$, $\sqrt g = r^2\sin\theta$ (Hartle (7.33)):
>
> $$
> \mathrm{Vol} = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\!\!\int_0^R r^2\sin\theta\;dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi = \frac{R^3}{3}\cdot2\cdot2\pi = \frac{4\pi R^3}{3} .
> $$
>
> The orthonormal frame $e_r = \partial_r$, $e_\theta = \partial_\theta/r$, $e_\varphi = \partial_\varphi/(r\sin\theta)$ has $\det E = 1/(r^2\sin\theta)$, and $\mathrm{vol}(e_r, e_\theta, e_\varphi) = r^2\sin\theta\cdot1/(r^2\sin\theta) = 1$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]], part 2).
>
> *(c) A curved three-space.* Hartle's spatial metric of a homogeneous closed universe (or the interior of a uniform star) is
>
> $$
> dS^2 = \frac{dr^2}{1 - r^2/a^2} + r^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr), \qquad 0 < r < a, \qquad \sqrt g = \frac{r^2\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1 - r^2/a^2}} .
> $$
>
> The sphere $r = R$ has the induced metric $R^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)$, area $4\pi R^2$ by (a). The volume inside it is not $\frac43\pi R^3$. With $r = a\sin\chi$, $dr = a\cos\chi\,d\chi$, $\sqrt{1 - r^2/a^2} = \cos\chi$ ($0 \le \chi \le \pi/2$), and $\chi_R = \arcsin(R/a)$:
>
> $$
> V(R) = 4\pi\int_0^R\frac{r^2\,dr}{\sqrt{1 - r^2/a^2}} = 4\pi a^3\int_0^{\chi_R}\sin^2\chi\;d\chi = 4\pi a^3\Bigl[\frac{\chi}{2} - \frac{\sin2\chi}{4}\Bigr]_0^{\chi_R} = 4\pi a^3\Bigl[\frac12\arcsin\frac Ra - \frac{R}{2a}\sqrt{1 - \frac{R^2}{a^2}}\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> Hartle (7.38), using $\frac14\sin2\chi_R = \frac12\sin\chi_R\cos\chi_R = \frac{R}{2a}\sqrt{1 - R^2/a^2}$. For $R = a/2$: $V = 0.5692\,a^3$, against $\frac43\pi(a/2)^3 = 0.5236\,a^3$, a ratio $1.087$. For small $R$, $V = \frac43\pi R^3\bigl(1 + \frac{3R^2}{10a^2} + \dots\bigr)$: there is more volume inside a sphere of given area than in flat space. At $R = a$, $V = 4\pi a^3\cdot\frac\pi4 = \pi^2a^3$. The substitution turns the metric into $a^2\bigl(d\chi^2 + \sin^2\chi\,(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)\bigr)$, the three-sphere of radius $a$; $r < a$ covers half of it, and the whole three-sphere ($0 < \chi < \pi$) has $\sqrt g = a^3\sin^2\chi\sin\theta$ and volume $a^3\cdot\frac\pi2\cdot2\cdot2\pi = 2\pi^2a^3$.
>
> *(d) Four-volume.* Minkowski spacetime in spherical coordinates has $\sqrt{-g} = r^2\sin\theta$ ([[#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]), $\mathrm{vol} = r^2\sin\theta\,dt\wedge dr\wedge d\theta\wedge d\varphi$ (GR2 (5.52)), and the region $0 < t < T$, $r < R$ has four-volume $T\cdot\frac43\pi R^3$.
>
> *Source: Hartle §7.6, Examples 7.5–7.6, eqs. (7.31)–(7.38) (pp. 147–148) · Couzens, GR2, §5.4, eq. (5.52) (p. 108) · all values checked by script*
