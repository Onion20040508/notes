Chapter C1 ended with gravity described by a metric. Free fall makes proper time stationary in a metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]), and the part of gravity that no choice of frame removes is the tidal acceleration ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]]). Chapter C2 built the arena: a manifold with tangent vectors, tensors of every type, Lie derivatives and integrals of forms, all without a metric. Three things are still missing, and this chapter supplies them in order. *How are lengths, angles and times measured on a manifold? How is a vector at one point compared with a vector at another? And how is curvature detected by someone who lives inside the space?* This section answers the first question, as far as it can be answered at a single point and along a curve.

First, what fails without new structure. On the plane with polar coordinates, [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms|§C2.2]] built the basis vector $\partial_\theta$, but gave it no length ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^cau-c2-2-2|§C2.2, Caution: ∂θ is not the unit vector θ̂]]). The obvious guess, the square root of the sum of the squared components, depends on the chart. At the point $r = 2$, $\theta = \pi/2$ the vector $\partial_\theta$ has polar components $(0, 1)$ and Cartesian components $(-r\sin\theta, r\cos\theta) = (-2, 0)$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]]):

$$
\sqrt{0^2 + 1^2} = 1, \qquad \sqrt{(-2)^2 + 0^2} = 2 .
$$

The field $\partial_x$ fails the same way: $\sqrt{1^2 + 0^2} = 1$ in Cartesian components, $\sqrt{\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta/r^2} = \tfrac12$ in polar ones at that point ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^cau-c2-2-1|§C2.2, Caution: Index position is the type, and nothing raises it yet]]). A sum of squares of components is not a function of the vector, so it cannot be a length. The same gap appeared in chapter C1 with physical consequences. In the coordinates of the weak-field metric light moves at a coordinate speed below $1$, while a static observer measures exactly $1$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]]). A speed is a ratio of a length to a time, and the coordinates alone supply neither.

What does supply them? A length must be a number attached to the vector itself, the same in every chart, so it must come from a tensor at the point ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 4). Special relativity shows which tensor. The interval is the quadratic form of the Minkowski metric $\eta$, a symmetric bilinear form, and the bilinear form is recovered from its squares by polarization ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], part 4; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). So the extra structure is a symmetric $(0, 2)$ tensor field, one bilinear form on each tangent space, and nondegenerate as $\eta$ is. That is the metric tensor ([[#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]). The section then derives its components and their transformation law ([[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]]). The chart-independent content of the matrix $g_{\mu\nu}$ turns out to be the signs of its eigenvalues, which sort metrics into Riemannian and Lorentzian ones ([[#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]). With a metric come norms, angles and lengths of curves ([[#^def-c3-1-3|Def. §C3.1.3]]), the identification of vectors with one-forms that chapter C2 lacked ([[#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]], [[#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]]), the metric a surface inherits from its surroundings ([[#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]), and orthonormal frames, in which components are the numbers that rulers and clocks read ([[#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]). Four examples measure circles in the plane, on the sphere and on the hyperbolic plane, and Minkowski spacetime in spherical coordinates.

**Setup.** A smooth manifold $M$ of dimension $n$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]), with tangent spaces $T_pM$, cotangent spaces $T^{\ast}_pM$ and tensor fields as in chapter C2 ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]], [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-5|Def. §C2.2.5]], [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]). The metric is one more field on $M$, chosen in addition to the manifold structure ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]], part 4). Spacetime is the case $n = 4$ with a Lorentzian metric; the definitions hold in any dimension and signature, and the examples use $n = 2, 4$. Everything in this section happens at a point or along a curve: no derivative of the metric appears. All statements are exact theorems of linear algebra and calculus. The identities and numbers in the opening and in the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; no measurable result in this section needs restoring); Greek indices are coordinate indices of a chart, $\mu = 0, \dots, 3$ on spacetime and $1, \dots, n$ in general; Latin indices $a, b$ from the start of the alphabet label the vectors of a frame (orthonormal components carry a hat, $X^{\hat a}$); a repeated upper–lower pair is summed. ## The metric and its components

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.1: Metric Tensor
> *Structure.* A **metric** on $M$ is a tensor field $g$ of type $(0, 2)$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]) such that at every point $p$ the bilinear map $g_p : T_pM \times T_pM \to \mathbf R$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]], case 5) is
> 1. **symmetric**: $g_p(X, Y) = g_p(Y, X)$ for all $X, Y \in T_pM$ ([[§35 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-9|LADR Def. 9.9]]);
> 2. **nondegenerate**: if $g_p(X, Y) = 0$ for every $Y \in T_pM$, then $X = 0$.
>
> A manifold with a metric, $(M, g)$, is a **pseudo-Riemannian manifold**. The number $g_p(X, Y)$ is the **scalar product** of $X$ and $Y$ at $p$; $X$ and $Y$ are **orthogonal** if it is $0$. Smoothness of $g$ is part of the definition: its components are smooth in every chart ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]).
>
> *What it adds.* Nothing in the manifold structure singles out a metric: the same $M$ carries many, and each gives it a different geometry (the plane carries the Euclidean metric and also the metric of the hyperbolic plane, [[#^ex-c3-1-4|Example §C3.1.4]]). In general relativity the metric is the gravitational field. Its components in a chart are the ten functions $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$ of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]], and their transformation law is [[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]].
>
> *Layer change.* At one point a metric is the same kind of object as the Minkowski metric $\eta$ of special relativity, a symmetric nondegenerate bilinear form ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-3|Def. §B1.1.3]] and [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], parts 1–2, level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$; [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]] in the signature used here). There it was one form on the single vector space of displacements. Here there is one form on each tangent space, and it varies smoothly from point to point.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1, Definition 34 (p. 73; "pseudo-Riemannian metric": symmetric and non-degenerate) · Schutz §6.2 (p. 143: picking a metric "adds structure to the manifold") · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.8, eq. (6.51) (p. 100)*

Nondegeneracy is a condition on every tangent space, while a chart gives one matrix at each point. The next theorem translates the definition into that matrix and finds which of its properties do not depend on the chart. Linear algebra says that a symmetric matrix can be brought to diagonal form, but its eigenvalues change with the basis. What survives is a count of signs.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.1: Components g_μν = g(∂_μ, ∂_ν) and Their Transformation
> Let $g$ be a metric on $M$ ([[#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]), and let $x^\mu$ and $x'^\mu$ be charts at $p$.
> 1. **Components.** With the coordinate basis $\partial_\mu$ and its dual basis $dx^\mu$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]], [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]),
>
> $$
> g_{\mu\nu} = g(\partial_\mu, \partial_\nu) = g_{\nu\mu}, \qquad g = g_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu \otimes dx^\nu, \qquad g(X, Y) = g_{\mu\nu}X^\mu Y^\nu ,
> $$
>
> and nondegeneracy is equivalent to $\det(g_{\mu\nu}) \neq 0$ at every point of the chart.
> 2. **Line element.** Because $g_{\mu\nu}$ is symmetric, $g = g_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu dx^\nu$ with the symmetric product $dx^\mu dx^\nu \equiv \tfrac12(dx^\mu \otimes dx^\nu + dx^\nu \otimes dx^\mu)$. This is the **line element**, written
>
> $$
> ds^2 = g_{\mu\nu}(x)\,dx^\mu dx^\nu ;
> $$
>
> on a displacement $X = dx^\mu\,\partial_\mu$ it gives $g(X, X)$. A metric in $n$ dimensions has $\tfrac12n(n + 1)$ independent components: $3$ for $n = 2$, $10$ for $n = 4$.
> 3. **Transformation law.** With the Jacobian matrix $J^\alpha{}_\mu = \partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\mu$ at $p$,
>
> $$
> g'_{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\mu}\,\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,g_{\alpha\beta}, \qquad\text{in matrices}\qquad g' = J^{\mathsf T}g\,J, \qquad \det g' = (\det J)^2\det g .
> $$
>
> In practice: substitute $dx^\alpha = (\partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\mu)\,dx'^\mu$ into $ds^2$ and collect terms ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 4).
> 4. ⚑ **The signs of the eigenvalues are invariant (Sylvester's law of inertia).** At $p$ the symmetric matrix $(g_{\mu\nu}(p))$ has no zero eigenvalue, $q$ negative and $n - q$ positive ones, and $q$ is the same in every chart, although the eigenvalues themselves are not. $q$ equals the largest dimension of a subspace of $T_pM$ on which $g_p$ is negative definite. Every basis of $T_pM$ in which $g_p$ is diagonal has exactly $q$ negative diagonal entries. Such bases exist, and rescaling one gives a basis $e_a$ with
>
> $$
> g_p(e_a, e_b) = \operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{-1, \dots, -1}_{q}, \underbrace{+1, \dots, +1}_{n - q})_{ab} ,
> $$
>
> an **orthonormal basis** at $p$.
> 5. ⚑ **The count is the same at every point of a connected manifold.** If $M$ is connected, $q$ does not depend on $p$. A signature can change only where $\det g = 0$, which a metric excludes.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a manifold with a metric; *applies:* any two charts at $p$ (parts 1–4); part 5 on each connected component of $M$; exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1, eqs. (4.1)–(4.3) (p. 73); p. 74 (the matrix is diagonalisable; Sylvester's law of inertia quoted); §4.1.3, eq. (4.26) (p. 78) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.8 (p. 100: "the eigenvalues themselves depend on the choice of coordinates the signature does not (why?)"); §6.11 (p. 104: the signature is invariant because the metric is continuous with nonzero determinant) · Schutz §6.2, eq. (6.2) (p. 144) · Hartle §7.2, eqs. (7.8)–(7.9) (p. 138: 10 independent components) · [[§35 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-7|LADR Thm. 9.7]]*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (components). Expand $X = X^\mu\partial_\mu$ and $Y = Y^\nu\partial_\nu$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]]). Bilinearity pulls out both sets of components:
>
> $$
> g(X, Y) = g\bigl(X^\mu\partial_\mu, Y^\nu\partial_\nu\bigr) = X^\mu Y^\nu\,g(\partial_\mu, \partial_\nu) = g_{\mu\nu}X^\mu Y^\nu .
> $$
>
> Since $dx^\mu \otimes dx^\nu$ evaluated on $(X, Y)$ is $X^\mu Y^\nu$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]]), this says $g = g_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu \otimes dx^\nu$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 1). Symmetry of $g$ with $X = \partial_\mu$, $Y = \partial_\nu$ gives $g_{\mu\nu} = g_{\nu\mu}$.
>
> **Step 2** (nondegeneracy as a determinant). $g(X, Y) = 0$ for all $Y$ holds exactly when it holds for $Y = \partial_\nu$, $\nu = 1, \dots, n$, i.e. when $g_{\nu\mu}X^\mu = 0$ for every $\nu$. This is the linear system $GX = 0$ for the matrix $G = (g_{\mu\nu}(p))$ and the column $X = (X^\mu)$. It has only the solution $X = 0$ if and only if $G$ is injective, if and only if $G$ is invertible ([[§10 Invertibility and Isomorphisms#^ladr-3-65|LADR Thm. 3.65]]), if and only if $\det G \neq 0$. So condition 2 of [[#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]] at $p$ is $\det(g_{\mu\nu}(p)) \neq 0$.
>
> **Step 3** (line element). Split the sum into its two halves and rename the summation indices $\mu \leftrightarrow \nu$ in the second half, then use $g_{\nu\mu} = g_{\mu\nu}$:
>
> $$
> g_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu \otimes dx^\nu = \tfrac12 g_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu \otimes dx^\nu + \tfrac12 g_{\nu\mu}\,dx^\nu \otimes dx^\mu = g_{\mu\nu}\,\tfrac12\bigl(dx^\mu \otimes dx^\nu + dx^\nu \otimes dx^\mu\bigr) = g_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu dx^\nu .
> $$
>
> On $(X, X)$ with $X = dx^\mu\partial_\mu$ both orderings give $dx^\mu dx^\nu$, so the value is $g_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu = g(X, X)$. A symmetric $n \times n$ matrix is fixed by its entries with $\mu \le \nu$: $n$ diagonal and $\tfrac12n(n - 1)$ above the diagonal, $\tfrac12n(n + 1)$ in all.
>
> **Step 4** (transformation law). $g$ is a $(0, 2)$ tensor, so the general law ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 2, with $r = 0$, $s = 2$) gives $g'_{\mu\nu} = (\partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\mu)(\partial x^\beta/\partial x'^\nu)\,g_{\alpha\beta}$. As a product of matrices this is $g'_{\mu\nu} = (J^{\mathsf T})_\mu{}^\alpha g_{\alpha\beta}J^\beta{}_\nu$, i.e. $g' = J^{\mathsf T}gJ$, the change-of-basis formula for bilinear forms ([[§35 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-7|LADR Thm. 9.7]]). Taking determinants, $\det g' = \det J^{\mathsf T}\,\det g\,\det J = (\det J)^2\det g$, the density law of weight $2$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 2). The substitution rule is the same law read from $ds^2$: inserting $dx^\alpha = J^\alpha{}_\mu dx'^\mu$ into $g_{\alpha\beta}dx^\alpha dx^\beta$ gives $g_{\alpha\beta}J^\alpha{}_\mu J^\beta{}_\nu\,dx'^\mu dx'^\nu$.
>
> **Step 5** (a diagonalizing basis and the count $q'$). Symmetric bilinear forms have a diagonal matrix in some basis ([[§35 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-12|LADR Thm. 9.12]]). Let $e_1, \dots, e_n$ be any basis of $T_pM$ with $g_p(e_a, e_b) = d_a\delta_{ab}$ (no sum). No $d_a$ is $0$: if $d_a = 0$, then $g_p(e_a, e_b) = 0$ for every $b$, so $g_p(e_a, Y) = 0$ for every $Y$, and nondegeneracy forces $e_a = 0$, which a basis vector is not. Let $q'$ be the number of negative $d_a$; order the basis so that $d_1, \dots, d_{q'} < 0 < d_{q'+1}, \dots, d_n$.
>
> **Step 6** ($q'$ is basis-free). Let $q$ be the largest dimension of a subspace of $T_pM$ on which $g_p$ is negative definite ($g_p(v, v) < 0$ for every nonzero $v$ in it). On $N_0 = \operatorname{span}(e_1, \dots, e_{q'})$, a vector $v = \sum_{a \le q'}c^ae_a \neq 0$ has
>
> $$
> g_p(v, v) = \sum_{a, b \le q'}c^ac^b\,d_a\delta_{ab} = \sum_{a \le q'}d_a\,(c^a)^2 < 0 ,
> $$
>
> so $q \ge q'$. On $P_0 = \operatorname{span}(e_{q'+1}, \dots, e_n)$ the same computation gives $g_p(v, v) = \sum_{a > q'}d_a(c^a)^2 \ge 0$. Let $W$ be any subspace on which $g_p$ is negative definite. A nonzero $v \in W \cap P_0$ would have $g_p(v, v) < 0$ and $\ge 0$ at once, so $W \cap P_0 = \{0\}$, and the dimension of the sum ([[§6 Dimension#^ladr-2-43|LADR Thm. 2.43]]) gives
>
> $$
> \dim W + (n - q') = \dim(W + P_0) \le n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dim W \le q' .
> $$
>
> Hence $q = q'$. Since $q$ is defined without a basis, every diagonalizing basis has $q$ negative entries, and (exchanging the roles of the signs) $n - q$ positive ones.
>
> **Step 7** (eigenvalues). In a chart, the matrix $G = (g_{\mu\nu}(p))$ is real and symmetric, so $\mathbf R^n$ has a basis of eigenvectors $u_1, \dots, u_n$ of $G$, orthonormal for the dot product of $\mathbf R^n$, $Gu_a = \lambda_au_a$ ([[§35 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-13|LADR Thm. 9.13]]). The tangent vectors $e_a = u_a{}^\mu\,\partial_\mu$ then satisfy
>
> $$
> g_p(e_a, e_b) = u_a{}^{\mathsf T}G\,u_b = \lambda_b\,u_a{}^{\mathsf T}u_b = \lambda_b\,\delta_{ab} ,
> $$
>
> a diagonalizing basis with $d_a = \lambda_a$. By Step 6 the number of negative eigenvalues is $q$, in every chart. The eigenvalues themselves change: for the Euclidean plane they are $(1, 1)$ in Cartesian and $(1, r^2)$ in polar coordinates ([[#^ex-c3-1-1|Example §C3.1.1]]).
>
> **Step 8** (orthonormal basis). Replace $e_a$ of Step 5 by $e_a/\sqrt{|d_a|}$. Bilinearity gives $g_p(e_a, e_b)/\sqrt{|d_a||d_b|} = d_a\delta_{ab}/|d_a| = \operatorname{sgn}(d_a)\,\delta_{ab}$: $q$ entries $-1$ followed by $n - q$ entries $+1$. This is part 4.
>
> **Step 9** ($q$ cannot drop near a point). Take a chart around $p$ and the negative-definite space $N_0 \subset T_pM$ of Step 6, with basis $e_a = c_a{}^\mu\partial_\mu|_p$, $a \le q(p)$. Extend these to the vector fields $E_a = c_a{}^\mu\partial_\mu$ on the chart domain, with the same constant components. The function
>
> $$
> F(x, s) = g_{\mu\nu}(x)\,V^\mu V^\nu, \qquad V^\mu = \sum_{a \le q(p)}s^a c_a{}^\mu, \qquad s \in S = \Bigl\{s \in \mathbf R^{q(p)} : \sum_a (s^a)^2 = 1\Bigr\} ,
> $$
>
> is continuous, because the $g_{\mu\nu}$ are, and $F(p, s) < 0$ for every $s \in S$ (Step 6). $S$ is compact, so the open set $\{(x, s) : F(x, s) < 0\}$, which contains $\{p\} \times S$, contains $U \times S$ for some neighbourhood $U$ of $p$ (the tube lemma). For $x \in U$ every unit combination of the $E_a(x)$ has $g_x < 0$. In particular no such combination is zero, so the $E_a(x)$ are linearly independent and span a $q(p)$-dimensional subspace on which $g_x$ is negative definite. By Step 6 at $x$, $q(x) \ge q(p)$. The same argument with the positive-definite space $P_0$ gives $n - q(x) \ge n - q(p)$ on a neighbourhood of $p$. On the intersection of the two neighbourhoods $q(x) = q(p)$.
>
> **Step 10** (connectedness). By Step 9 each set $M_k = \{p \in M : q(p) = k\}$, $k = 0, \dots, n$, is open. They are disjoint and cover $M$. If two were nonempty, $M$ would be the union of two disjoint nonempty open sets (one of them and the union of the others), which a connected $M$ is not. So exactly one $M_k$ is nonempty: part 5. Nondegeneracy was used in Steps 5 and 9 (no $d_a = 0$). A symmetric field with $\det g = 0$ somewhere can change its count there: $\operatorname{diag}(x, 1)$ on $\mathbf R^2$ has $q = 1$ for $x < 0$ and $q = 0$ for $x > 0$, and $\det = x$ vanishes on the line $x = 0$ between.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The matrix $g_{\mu\nu}$ changes by $g \mapsto J^{\mathsf T}gJ$, a congruence, not a similarity $J^{-1}gJ$. Congruences change eigenvalues, so the eigenvalues of $g_{\mu\nu}$ carry no geometric meaning. Their signs do, because the number of negative signs is the dimension of the largest subspace of "negative-square" vectors, a notion that needs no basis.
> - At every point there is an orthonormal basis (Step 8). In spacetime this is the algebraic core of the statement that every event has a local Minkowski frame: the chart version is [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]], and frames on open sets are [[#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]].

The count $q$ is the one number the matrix $g_{\mu\nu}$ carries independently of the chart, and it decides what kind of geometry the metric describes.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.2: Riemannian and Lorentzian Metrics; Signature
> Let $g$ be a metric on $M$ ([[#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]), and let $q$ and $n - q$ be the numbers of negative and positive entries in a diagonal matrix of $g_p$, the same for every diagonalizing basis ([[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 4).
> 1. The **signature** of $g$ at $p$ is the list of these signs, written with the negative ones first, e.g. $(-, +, +, +)$; equivalently the pair $(q, n - q)$. On a connected manifold it is the same at every point ([[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 5), and one speaks of the signature of $g$.
> 2. $g$ is **Riemannian** if $q = 0$: $g_p(X, X) > 0$ for every $X \neq 0$ (positive definite), signature $(+, \dots, +)$. $(M, g)$ is then a **Riemannian manifold**.
> 3. $g$ is **Lorentzian** if $q = 1$ and $n \ge 2$: signature $(-, +, \dots, +)$. $(M, g)$ is then a **Lorentzian manifold**. Spacetime is a four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold, signature $(-, +, +, +)$.
>
> Minkowski spacetime is $\mathbf R^4$ with the metric $\eta = -dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$ in inertial coordinates ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]). Each tangent space of a Lorentzian manifold has an orthonormal basis in which $g_p$ has the components $\eta_{ab}$ ([[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 4), so at each point it is a copy of Minkowski space. What sets general relativity apart is that no chart need make $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ on a whole open set: the first derivatives of $g_{\mu\nu}$ can be removed at a point, the second ones in general cannot ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]]), and an open set with $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ exists exactly where the curvature vanishes ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-5|Theorem §C3.9.5]]).
>
> *Test.* The signature can be read off any one chart at any one point: diagonalize the matrix there, or count the signs of its eigenvalues. A diagonal $g_{\mu\nu}$ shows it directly. Level B's $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1)$ has signature $(+, -, -, -)$, with three negative entries; it describes the same geometry because $g^{\rm B} = -g^{\rm C}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]], part 1).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1, Definition 34 and p. 74 (index $(i, j)$, Lorentz metric for $j = 1$) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.8 (p. 100: Riemannian = all eigenvalues positive; Lorentzian) · Schutz §6.2, eq. (6.2) (p. 144)*

> [!caution] Caution: Names for the signature in the sources
> The same metric is described by different numbers in the sources. Here the signature of spacetime is the list $(-, +, +, +)$ ([[#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]).
> - **Couzens** (GR1, p. 74) calls the pair $(i, j)$ of positive and negative eigenvalue counts the *index* of the metric and the number $j$ of negative entries the *signature*. A Lorentz metric has signature $1$ there, a Euclidean one signature $0$. Other mathematical texts use "index" for $j$ alone.
> - **d'Inverno & Vickers** (§6.8, p. 100) define the signature as the number of positive minus the number of negative eigenvalues. Their spacetime metric has one positive and three negative eigenvalues, "signature $-2$". In that convention the metric of these notes has signature $+2$.
> - **Schutz** (§6.2, p. 144) defines the signature as the sum of the diagonal entries of the form $\operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$: $+2$.
> - **Eigenvalues are not the diagonal entries.** All sources speak of eigenvalues of $g_{\mu\nu}$, but only their signs are invariant ([[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 4). The diagonal entries $\pm1$ are those of an orthonormal basis, not eigenvalues of the matrix in a given chart.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1 (p. 74) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.8 (p. 100) · Schutz §6.2 (p. 144)*

## Lengths and angles

The opening asked for the length of $\partial_\theta$. A metric answers at once: the length is computed from $g(X, X)$, a number that [[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]] makes the same in every chart. Lengths of curves follow by adding up the lengths of their velocities.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.3: Norm, Angle and Length of a Curve
> Let $(M, g)$ be a manifold with a metric ([[#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]), $p \in M$ and $X, Y \in T_pM$.
> 1. **Norm.** The **norm** of $X$ is $|X| = \sqrt{|g(X, X)|}$. For a Riemannian metric ([[#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]) $|X| > 0$ whenever $X \neq 0$. For a Lorentzian one, nonzero vectors with $g(X, X) = 0$ exist and have norm $0$; the sign of $g(X, X)$ classifies vectors as timelike, null or spacelike ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]). A **unit vector** has $g(X, X) = \pm1$.
> 2. **Angle.** For a Riemannian metric and $X, Y \neq 0$, the **angle** $\vartheta \in [0, \pi]$ between $X$ and $Y$ is defined by
>
> $$
> \cos\vartheta = \frac{g(X, Y)}{|X|\,|Y|} .
> $$
>
> The right side lies in $[-1, 1]$ by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the inner product $g_p$ ([[§20 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-14|LADR Thm. 6.14]]). For a Lorentzian metric the same formula gives an angle when $X$ and $Y$ span a plane on which $g_p$ is positive definite. It fails for two timelike vectors. In two-dimensional Minkowski space the unit vectors $u = \partial_t$ and $v = \cosh\beta\,\partial_t + \sinh\beta\,\partial_x$ have $g(u, v) = -\cosh\beta$ and $|u| = |v| = 1$, so the ratio is $-\cosh\beta$, of modulus greater than $1$ for every $\beta \neq 0$ ($\cosh 1 = 1.5431$). The number $\beta$ is the rapidity between the two four-velocities ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-3|Def. §B1.2.3]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where $u\cdot v = +\cosh\beta$), a hyperbolic angle. **Orthogonality**, $g(X, Y) = 0$, is defined in every signature.
> 3. **Length of a curve.** Let $\gamma : [\lambda_A, \lambda_B] \to M$ be a smooth curve with velocity $\dot\gamma$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]), components $\dot x^\mu = dx^\mu/d\lambda$ in a chart, and $g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma) \ge 0$ along it: any curve for a Riemannian metric, a spacelike or null curve for a Lorentzian one. Its **length** is
>
> $$
> L[\gamma] = \int_{\lambda_A}^{\lambda_B}\sqrt{g\bigl(\dot\gamma(\lambda), \dot\gamma(\lambda)\bigr)}\;d\lambda = \int_{\lambda_A}^{\lambda_B}\sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}\bigl(x(\lambda)\bigr)\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}\;d\lambda .
> $$
>
> A null curve has length $0$. For a timelike curve the corresponding integral of $\sqrt{-g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)}$ is its proper time ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]; in one chart, [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]]).
>
> *Well defined.* The integrand is the norm of $\dot\gamma$, a chart-independent number, so $L$ does not depend on the chart. It does not depend on the parametrization either. Let $\lambda = f(\sigma)$ with $f : [\sigma_A, \sigma_B] \to [\lambda_A, \lambda_B]$ a $C^1$ bijection, $f' \neq 0$, and $\tilde\gamma = \gamma \circ f$. By the chain rule $d\tilde x^\mu/d\sigma = f'(\sigma)\,\dot x^\mu\bigl(f(\sigma)\bigr)$, so
>
> $$
> L[\tilde\gamma] = \int_{\sigma_A}^{\sigma_B}\sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}\,\Big|_{f(\sigma)}\,\bigl|f'(\sigma)\bigr|\;d\sigma = \int_{\lambda_A}^{\lambda_B}\sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}\;d\lambda = L[\gamma] ,
> $$
>
> pulling $(f')^2$ out of the square root as $|f'|$ and changing variables in one dimension ([[§24 The Change of Variables Formula#^lem-24-1|452 Lemma §24.1]]). Length belongs to the image of the curve traversed once, not to its parametrization.
>
> *Length is not yet distance.* Couzens writes $d(p, q)$ for the length of a curve from $p$ to $q$ (GR1 (4.7)). Different curves between the same points have different lengths. The distance between two points of a Riemannian manifold is the greatest lower bound of the lengths of all curves joining them; which curves come closest is the subject of geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-4|Theorem §C3.7.4]]). The examples below find the shortest curve directly, from a lower bound on the integrand.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.1, eqs. (4.5)–(4.8) (p. 74: reparametrization invariance stated); §2.1.2, eq. (2.15) and Exercise 2.3 (the flat case) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.8, eq. (6.53) and the angle formula (pp. 100–101; written there with $|g(X, X)|^{1/2}$ for any metric) · Hartle §2.6, eqs. (2.22)–(2.23)*

## Raising and lowering indices

The second gap of chapter C2 was the missing identification of vectors with covectors. Without a metric, the constant field $\partial_x$ and the one-form $dx$ have equal components in Cartesian coordinates and different ones in polar coordinates, so "equal components" is a coincidence of one chart ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^cau-c2-2-1|§C2.2, Caution: Index position is the type, and nothing raises it yet]]). A metric turns each vector into a covector by filling one of its two slots.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.4: Inverse Metric; Raising and Lowering
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be a manifold with a metric ([[#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]) and $p \in M$.
> 1. **Lowering** is the linear map $\flat : T_pM \to T^{\ast}_pM$, $X \mapsto X^\flat \equiv g(X, \cdot\,)$, the covector $Y \mapsto g(X, Y)$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-5|Def. §C2.2.5]]). Its kernel is $\{0\}$ by nondegeneracy, and $T_pM$ and $T^{\ast}_pM$ have the same finite dimension, so $\flat$ is an isomorphism ([[§10 Invertibility and Isomorphisms#^ladr-3-65|LADR Thm. 3.65]]), the **musical isomorphism** defined by $g$.
> 2. **Raising** is its inverse, $\sharp : T^{\ast}_pM \to T_pM$, $\omega \mapsto \omega^\sharp$: the unique vector with $g(\omega^\sharp, Y) = \omega(Y)$ for all $Y$.
> 3. The **inverse metric** is the $(2, 0)$ tensor ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]) $g^{-1}(\omega, \eta) \equiv g(\omega^\sharp, \eta^\sharp)$, with components $g^{\mu\nu} \equiv g^{-1}(dx^\mu, dx^\nu)$. It is symmetric, because $g$ is.
> 4. **Index notation.** The components of $X^\flat$ are written $X_\mu \equiv (X^\flat)_\mu$, those of $\omega^\sharp$ are written $\omega^\mu \equiv (\omega^\sharp)^\mu$. For a tensor of any type, lowering or raising one index means applying $\flat$ or $\sharp$ in that slot and keeping the order of the slots: from $T^{\mu\nu}$ one gets $T_\mu{}^\nu$ (first slot lowered) and $T^\mu{}_\nu$ (second slot lowered), which are different arrays unless $T$ is symmetric.
>
> Each of these is done point by point; applied to fields, $\flat$ and $\sharp$ take smooth fields to smooth fields, because their components are polynomials in $g_{\mu\nu}$ and the entries of its inverse matrix ([[#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]]).
>
> *Layer change.* At level B the same operations used the constant matrix $\eta$ ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-4|Def. §B1.1.4]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where lowering flips the spatial components, $a_\mu = (a^0, -\vec a)$; here, with $\eta$, it flips the time component, $a_\mu = (-a^0, \vec a)$, [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]). Here the matrix is $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$, different at each point, and the identification depends on which metric is chosen.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.19)–(4.21) (p. 77: "the metric provides an isomorphism between a vector space and its dual") · Schutz §6.2 (pp. 143–144) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.8 (p. 100)*

^def-c3-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.2: Index Gymnastics on a Manifold
> Let $(M, g)$ be a manifold with a metric, with components $g_{\mu\nu}$ in a chart ([[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]]), and $\flat$, $\sharp$, $g^{-1}$ as in [[#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]].
> 1. **The inverse metric is the inverse matrix.** $g^{\mu\lambda}g_{\lambda\nu} = \delta^\mu{}_\nu$ and $g^{\mu\nu} = g^{\nu\mu}$. Its components transform as those of a $(2, 0)$ tensor, $g'^{\mu\nu} = (\partial x'^\mu/\partial x^\alpha)(\partial x'^\nu/\partial x^\beta)\,g^{\alpha\beta}$.
> 2. **Lowering and raising in components.**
>
> $$
> X_\mu = g_{\mu\nu}X^\nu, \qquad \omega^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}\omega_\nu, \qquad g^{\mu\nu}(g_{\nu\lambda}X^\lambda) = X^\mu, \qquad g_{\mu\nu}(g^{\nu\lambda}\omega_\lambda) = \omega_\mu ;
> $$
>
> raising undoes lowering, and the metric with one index raised is the identity, $g^\mu{}_\nu = g^{\mu\lambda}g_{\lambda\nu} = \delta^\mu{}_\nu$.
> 3. **Scalar products as contractions.**
>
> $$
> g(X, Y) = g_{\mu\nu}X^\mu Y^\nu = X^\mu Y_\mu = X_\mu Y^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}X_\mu Y_\nu, \qquad g^{-1}(\omega, \eta) = g^{\mu\nu}\omega_\mu\eta_\nu = \omega_\mu\eta^\mu .
> $$
>
> 4. **Tensors.** Lowering and raising indices of a tensor give tensors, e.g. $T_{\mu\nu} = g_{\mu\alpha}g_{\nu\beta}T^{\alpha\beta}$, and they commute with each other and with contraction: $T^\mu{}_\mu = T_\mu{}^\mu = g_{\mu\nu}T^{\mu\nu}$ and $g^{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu} = T^\mu{}_\mu$.
> 5. ⚑ **Traces with the metric are invariant.** $\delta^\mu{}_\mu = n$ and $g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\nu} = n$. For a $(0, 2)$ tensor $T$, the chart-independent replacement of the sum $\sum_\mu T_{\mu\mu}$ of two lower indices, which is not invariant ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]], part 3), is $g^{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu}$.
> 6. **Level B.** For $g = \eta$ in inertial coordinates, parts 1–5 are [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$: there $\eta^{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$, here $\operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$; in both, $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ is numerically equal to $\eta_{\mu\nu}$).
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a manifold with a metric; *applies:* at every point, any chart; for tensor fields pointwise; exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.19)–(4.21) (p. 77) · Schutz §6.2 (p. 144) · Hartle §7.3 (index placement rules, pp. 138–139)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (lowering). By [[#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]], the components of the covector $X^\flat$ are its values on the basis vectors ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]):
>
> $$
> X_\mu = X^\flat(\partial_\mu) = g(X, \partial_\mu) = g\bigl(X^\nu\partial_\nu, \partial_\mu\bigr) = X^\nu g_{\nu\mu} = g_{\mu\nu}X^\nu .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (raising). Let $G^{\mu\nu}$ be the entries of the inverse of the matrix $(g_{\mu\nu})$, which exists because $\det g \neq 0$ ([[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 1); the inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric, $G^{\mu\nu} = G^{\nu\mu}$. The vector $\omega^\sharp$ satisfies $(\omega^\sharp)^\flat = \omega$, so by Step 1 its components solve $g_{\mu\nu}(\omega^\sharp)^\nu = \omega_\mu$. Multiply by $G^{\lambda\mu}$ and sum over $\mu$:
>
> $$
> G^{\lambda\mu}g_{\mu\nu}(\omega^\sharp)^\nu = \delta^\lambda{}_\nu(\omega^\sharp)^\nu = (\omega^\sharp)^\lambda = G^{\lambda\mu}\omega_\mu .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the inverse metric). The basis covector $dx^\mu$ has components $(dx^\mu)_\beta = \delta^\mu{}_\beta$, so by Step 2 $(dx^\mu)^\sharp$ has components $G^{\alpha\beta}\delta^\mu{}_\beta = G^{\alpha\mu}$. Then, with Step 1 of [[#^der-c3-1-1|the derivation of Theorem §C3.1.1]] and the symmetry of $G$,
>
> $$
> g^{\mu\nu} = g\bigl((dx^\mu)^\sharp, (dx^\nu)^\sharp\bigr) = g_{\alpha\beta}\,G^{\alpha\mu}G^{\beta\nu} = \bigl(G^{\mu\alpha}g_{\alpha\beta}\bigr)G^{\beta\nu} = \delta^\mu{}_\beta\,G^{\beta\nu} = G^{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> So $g^{\mu\nu}$ is the inverse matrix: part 1, and Step 2 reads $\omega^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}\omega_\nu$. $g^{-1}$ is a $(2, 0)$ tensor by construction (it is bilinear in $(\omega, \eta)$, since $\sharp$ is linear and $g$ bilinear), so its components obey the law of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 2, with two upper indices.
>
> **Step 4** (part 2). $g^{\mu\nu}g_{\nu\lambda}X^\lambda = \delta^\mu{}_\lambda X^\lambda = X^\mu$ by part 1, and likewise $g_{\mu\nu}g^{\nu\lambda}\omega_\lambda = \delta_\mu{}^\lambda\omega_\lambda = \omega_\mu$, where $g_{\mu\nu}g^{\nu\lambda} = \delta_\mu{}^\lambda$ because a left inverse of a square matrix is also a right inverse. Raising one index of $g_{\lambda\nu}$ is $g^{\mu\lambda}g_{\lambda\nu} = \delta^\mu{}_\nu$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 3). $X^\mu Y_\mu = X^\mu g_{\mu\nu}Y^\nu = g(X, Y)$ by Step 1 and [[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 1. $X_\mu Y^\mu = g_{\mu\nu}X^\nu Y^\mu = g(Y, X) = g(X, Y)$ by symmetry. $g^{\mu\nu}X_\mu Y_\nu = X^\nu Y_\nu$ by part 2. For covectors, $g^{-1}(\omega, \eta) = g^{-1}(\omega_\mu dx^\mu, \eta_\nu dx^\nu) = g^{\mu\nu}\omega_\mu\eta_\nu = \omega_\mu\eta^\mu$.
>
> **Step 6** (part 4). Lowering the first slot of a $(2, 0)$ tensor $T$ is the map $(X, \eta) \mapsto T(X^\flat, \eta)$. It is bilinear, because $\flat$ is linear and $T$ bilinear, so it is a tensor of type $(1, 1)$ with vector slot first. Its components are
>
> $$
> T\bigl((\partial_\mu)^\flat, dx^\nu\bigr) = T\bigl(g_{\mu\alpha}dx^\alpha, dx^\nu\bigr) = g_{\mu\alpha}T^{\alpha\nu} = T_\mu{}^\nu ,
> $$
>
> using $(\partial_\mu)^\flat = g_{\mu\alpha}dx^\alpha$ (Step 1 with $X = \partial_\mu$). Lowering both slots in turn gives $T_{\mu\nu} = g_{\mu\alpha}g_{\nu\beta}T^{\alpha\beta}$, and the result is the same in either order, since the two factors act on different indices. Contractions:
>
> $$
> T^\mu{}_\mu = g_{\mu\beta}T^{\mu\beta}, \qquad T_\mu{}^\mu = g_{\mu\alpha}T^{\alpha\mu} = g_{\alpha\mu}T^{\alpha\mu} ,
> $$
>
> equal after renaming $\alpha \to \mu$, $\mu \to \beta$ in the second and using $g_{\beta\mu} = g_{\mu\beta}$. Raising both indices of $T_{\mu\nu}$ and contracting gives back the same number: $g^{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu} = g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\alpha}g_{\nu\beta}T^{\alpha\beta} = \delta^\nu{}_\alpha g_{\nu\beta}T^{\alpha\beta} = g_{\alpha\beta}T^{\alpha\beta}$.
>
> **Step 7** (part 5). $\delta^\mu{}_\mu = \sum_{\mu = 1}^n 1 = n$, and $g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\nu} = \delta^\nu{}_\nu = n$ by part 1. The scalar $g^{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu}$ is a full contraction of the tensor $g^{-1} \otimes T$, so it is chart-independent ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]). For the Euclidean metric of the plane in polar coordinates, $g = \operatorname{diag}(1, r^2)$ and $g^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(1, r^{-2})$: $g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\nu} = 1\cdot1 + r^{-2}\cdot r^2 = 2$, as in Cartesian coordinates, while $\sum_\mu g_{\mu\mu} = 1 + r^2$ ([[#^ex-c3-1-1|Example §C3.1.1]]).
>
> **Step 8** (part 6). With $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$, each diagonal entry is its own reciprocal, so the inverse matrix is $\eta$ again, and parts 1–5 become the rules of [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]] with $\eta^{\rm C} = -\eta^{\rm B}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows.** All index rules come from one fact: $g$ is an invertible symmetric matrix at each point. Nothing uses that $g_{\mu\nu}$ is constant, so the rules of level B hold unchanged on any manifold with a metric, point by point. What is lost is only the derivative: $\partial_\lambda(g_{\mu\nu}X^\nu) \neq g_{\mu\nu}\partial_\lambda X^\nu$ when $g$ varies, which is one reason a new derivative is needed ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-5|Theorem §C3.6.5]]).

## Induced metrics and orthonormal frames

Two more constructions complete the toolkit. Surfaces inside a space inherit its metric: the sphere's metric was found in chapter C2 by pulling back the Euclidean one ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^ex-c2-4-2|Example §C2.4.2]]). And the components that instruments read are not the coordinate components: a ruler laid along $\partial_\theta$ reads $r\,X^\theta$, not $X^\theta$.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.5: Induced Metric on a Submanifold
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be a manifold with a metric ([[#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]), $S$ a manifold of dimension $k$, and $\iota : S \to M$ an immersion: a smooth map whose push-forward $\iota_{\ast}$ ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^def-c2-4-1|Def. §C2.4.1]]) is injective at every point ([[§36 Submersions#^def-36-2|591 Def. §36.2]]), e.g. the inclusion of a surface or a hypersurface. The **induced metric** on $S$ is the pull-back ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]])
>
> $$
> h = \iota^{\ast}g, \qquad h(V, W) = g\bigl(\iota_{\ast}V, \iota_{\ast}W\bigr), \qquad h_{ab} = g_{\mu\nu}\bigl(x(y)\bigr)\,\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial y^a}\frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial y^b} ,
> $$
>
> in charts $y^a$ on $S$ and $x^\mu$ on $M$ ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 3). In practice one substitutes $x^\mu = x^\mu(y)$ and $dx^\mu = (\partial x^\mu/\partial y^a)\,dy^a$ into $ds^2$ (part 4 there).
>
> *When it is a metric.* $h$ is always a symmetric $(0, 2)$ tensor field; nondegeneracy depends on $g$ and on how $S$ sits in $M$.
> - $g$ Riemannian: $h$ is Riemannian ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-5|Theorem §C2.4.5]], part 3).
> - $g$ Lorentzian: $S$ is **spacelike** if $h$ is Riemannian, **timelike** if $h$ is Lorentzian, and **null** if $h$ is degenerate (then $h$ is not a metric). All three occur ([[#^ex-c3-1-3|Example §C3.1.3]]).
>
> *Lengths agree.* A curve $\gamma$ in $S$ has, in $(S, h)$, the same length as $\iota \circ \gamma$ in $(M, g)$: the velocity of $\iota \circ \gamma$ is $\iota_{\ast}\dot\gamma$ ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^ex-c2-4-1|Example §C2.4.1]]), so $h(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma) = g(\iota_{\ast}\dot\gamma, \iota_{\ast}\dot\gamma)$ and the integrands of [[#^def-c3-1-3|Def. §C3.1.3]] are equal. Measuring inside $S$ with $h$ is measuring in $M$ along $S$.
>
> *Names.* d'Inverno & Vickers call the induced metric the first fundamental form. MATH 591 pulls back alternating forms ([[§80 Differential Forms on Manifolds#^def-80-9|591 Def. §80.9]]), not symmetric ones; the physics form is [[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.1, Exercise 4.1 (p. 76: the metric on $S^2$ as a pull-back); §3.4.4, before eq. (3.145) (p. 72: submanifolds as images of maps with injective push-forward) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.8 (p. 100: "first fundamental form")*

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.6: Orthonormal Frame; Coordinate vs Orthonormal Components
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be a manifold with a metric of signature $(q, n - q)$ ([[#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]), and $U \subset M$ open. An **orthonormal frame** on $U$ is a list of $n$ smooth vector fields $e_1, \dots, e_n$ on $U$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-4|Def. §C2.2.4]]; on spacetime labelled $e_0, \dots, e_3$) such that at every point of $U$
>
> $$
> g(e_a, e_b) = \eta_{ab}, \qquad \eta_{ab} = \operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{-1, \dots, -1}_{q}, \underbrace{+1, \dots, +1}_{n - q}) ,
> $$
>
> the Minkowski matrix for a Lorentzian $g$, the identity $\delta_{ab}$ for a Riemannian one. Its **dual coframe** is the list of one-forms $\theta^a$ with $\theta^a(e_b) = \delta^a{}_b$ ([[§12 Duality#^ladr-3-112|LADR Def. 3.112]], at each point).
> - **Orthonormal components.** $X^{\hat a} \equiv \theta^a(X)$, so $X = X^{\hat a}e_a$, and $g(X, Y) = \eta_{ab}X^{\hat a}Y^{\hat b}$: in orthonormal components every scalar product has its special-relativistic form, at every point. These are the components measured by an observer whose rulers and clock are aligned with the frame.
> - **Relation to coordinate components.** Write $e_a = e_a{}^\mu\partial_\mu$. Then $g_{\mu\nu}e_a{}^\mu e_b{}^\nu = \eta_{ab}$ and $X^\mu = e_a{}^\mu X^{\hat a}$. For a **diagonal** metric the frame $e_\mu = |g_{\mu\mu}|^{-1/2}\,\partial_\mu$ (no sum) is orthonormal, and
>
> $$
> X^{\hat\mu} = \sqrt{|g_{\mu\mu}|}\;X^\mu \qquad (\text{no sum}) .
> $$
>
> For the plane in polar coordinates, $\hat r = \partial_r$ and $\hat\theta = \frac1r\partial_\theta$, so $X^{\hat\theta} = r\,X^\theta$ ([[#^ex-c3-1-1|Example §C3.1.1]]). The factor $r$ that [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^cau-c2-2-2|§C2.2, Caution: ∂θ is not the unit vector θ̂]] had to take from the Cartesian dot product is now the norm $|\partial_\theta| = \sqrt{g_{\theta\theta}} = r$ given by the metric.
> - **Not a coordinate basis.** In general no chart has the $e_a$ as its coordinate fields. Coordinate fields have vanishing brackets, while orthonormal frames usually do not: on the plane $[\partial_r, \frac1r\partial_\theta] = -\frac1{r^2}\partial_\theta$ (computed in §C2.2), on the sphere $[e_{\hat\theta}, e_{\hat\varphi}] = -\frac{\cot\theta}{a}\,e_{\hat\varphi}$ ([[#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]).
> - **Freedom.** If $e_a$ is an orthonormal frame on $U$, so is $e'_a = e_b\,\Lambda^b{}_a(x)$ for any smooth matrix-valued function with $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta$ at each point, because $g(e'_a, e'_b) = \Lambda^c{}_a\Lambda^d{}_b\,\eta_{cd} = (\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda)_{ab}$. For a Lorentzian metric these are Lorentz transformations ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, the same condition with $\eta^{\rm B}$), now chosen independently at each point.
>
> *Existence near every point.* At one point an orthonormal basis exists ([[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 4). Write it $e_a(p) = c_a{}^\mu\partial_\mu|_p$ and extend to the fields $E_a = c_a{}^\mu\partial_\mu$ on a chart domain. The functions $g(E_a, E_b)$ are continuous and equal $\eta_{ab}$ at $p$. Apply Gram–Schmidt with signs ([[§21 Orthonormal Bases#^ladr-6-32|LADR Thm. 6.32]] is the positive-definite case): $e_1 = E_1/\sqrt{|g(E_1, E_1)|}$, and for $k \ge 2$
>
> $$
> \tilde E_k = E_k - \sum_{j < k}\eta_{jj}\,g(E_k, e_j)\,e_j, \qquad e_k = \frac{\tilde E_k}{\sqrt{|g(\tilde E_k, \tilde E_k)|}} .
> $$
>
> Then $g(\tilde E_k, e_j) = g(E_k, e_j) - \eta_{jj}\,g(E_k, e_j)\,\eta_{jj} = 0$ for $j < k$. At $p$ the subtracted terms vanish and $g(\tilde E_k, \tilde E_k) = \eta_{kk} = \pm1$. By continuity $g(\tilde E_k, \tilde E_k)$ keeps that sign, and stays nonzero, on a neighbourhood of $p$. There all steps are smooth and give an orthonormal frame. For diagonal metrics the frame above needs no construction. A frame on all of $M$ need not exist (on $S^2$ every vector field vanishes somewhere, the hairy-ball theorem, so there is none); frames are used on open sets.
>
> *Names.* An orthonormal frame on a spacetime is a **tetrad** or **vierbein**; in $n$ dimensions a **vielbein**. Couzens mentions vielbeins and defers them to the problem sheets (GR1, §3.2.2). Schutz calls such a basis a noncoordinate basis; the curvilinear unit vectors of vector calculus ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^def-b1-1-2|EM Def. §B1.1.2]]) are the orthonormal frames of the Euclidean metric.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.2, eq. (4.17) (p. 76: an orthonormal basis of $T_pM$ with $g_{\mu\nu}|_p = \eta_{\mu\nu}$) · Schutz §6.2 (p. 144: the matrix that produces $\eta$ "may not be a coordinate transformation"); §5.5 (noncoordinate bases) · Hartle §7.8, eq. (7.50), Fig. 7.7 (orthonormal bases; $|e_\phi| = r$ in polar coordinates; the frame along orthogonal coordinate directions)*

At each point a manifold with a metric now has lengths, angles, an identification of vectors with covectors, and orthonormal bases; along curves it has lengths. For a Lorentzian metric the sign of $g(X, X)$ also sorts the tangent vectors at each point into timelike, null and spacelike ones: a light cone in every tangent space, tilting from point to point. *What do these light cones, and the proper time along timelike curves, mean for the causal structure of a curved spacetime, and how close to Minkowski can a chart make the metric near an event?* That is [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames|§C3.2]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C3.1.1: The Plane in Polar Coordinates
> *Setup.* $M = \mathbf R^2$ with the Euclidean metric $g = dx^2 + dy^2$, Cartesian components $\delta_{ij}$, and the polar chart $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ on the plane minus a half-line ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]]). The metric is Riemannian: both eigenvalues of $\delta_{ij}$ are $+1$.
>
> *(a) Components.* With $J = \partial(x, y)/\partial(r, \theta) = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\cos\theta & -r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, [[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 3, gives
>
> $$
> g' = J^{\mathsf T}J = \begin{pmatrix}\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta & -r\sin\theta\cos\theta + r\sin\theta\cos\theta\\ -r\sin\theta\cos\theta + r\sin\theta\cos\theta & r^2\sin^2\theta + r^2\cos^2\theta\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & r^2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\theta^2
> $$
>
> (also [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^ex-c2-3-2|Example §C2.3.2]]). The eigenvalues are $1$ and $r^2$, not $1$ and $1$; both are positive, as part 4 requires. $\det g' = r^2 = (\det J)^2$ with $\det J = r$. The inverse metric is $g^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(1, r^{-2})$ ([[#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]], part 1).
>
> *(b) The opening, answered.* By [[#^def-c3-1-3|Def. §C3.1.3]], $|\partial_r| = \sqrt{g_{rr}} = 1$ and $|\partial_\theta| = \sqrt{g_{\theta\theta}} = r$, which is $2$ at $r = 2$. The Cartesian sum of squares gave the right value because Cartesian components are orthonormal components ([[#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]); the polar sum did not. For $\partial_x$, with polar components $X = (\cos\theta, -\sin\theta/r)$,
>
> $$
> g(\partial_x, \partial_x) = g_{rr}(X^r)^2 + g_{\theta\theta}(X^\theta)^2 = 1\cdot\cos^2\theta + r^2\cdot\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2} = 1 ,
> $$
>
> at every point, while the sum of squares $(X^r)^2 + (X^\theta)^2 = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta/r^2$ is $\tfrac14$ at $(r, \theta) = (2, \pi/2)$.
>
> *(c) Angles.* $g(\partial_r, \partial_\theta) = g_{r\theta} = 0$: the coordinate lines cross at right angles. The angle $\vartheta$ between $\partial_r$ and $\partial_x$:
>
> $$
> \cos\vartheta = \frac{g(\partial_r, \partial_x)}{|\partial_r|\,|\partial_x|} = \frac{g_{rr}\cdot1\cdot\cos\theta + g_{\theta\theta}\cdot0\cdot(-\sin\theta/r)}{1\cdot1} = \cos\theta ,
> $$
>
> so $\vartheta = \theta$ for $0 \le \theta \le \pi$: the radial direction makes the polar angle with the $x$-axis, as it should.
>
> *(d) Lowering: the identification chapter C2 lacked.* By [[#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]], part 2,
>
> $$
> (\partial_x)^\flat_r = g_{rr}X^r = \cos\theta, \qquad (\partial_x)^\flat_\theta = g_{\theta\theta}X^\theta = r^2\cdot\Bigl(-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Bigr) = -r\sin\theta ,
> $$
>
> the polar components of $dx$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]], (c)). So $(\partial_x)^\flat = dx$, in every chart. Raising goes back: $(d\theta)^\sharp = g^{\theta\theta}\partial_\theta = \frac1{r^2}\partial_\theta$, and for a function $f$, $(df)^\sharp = \partial_rf\,\partial_r + \frac1{r^2}\partial_\theta f\,\partial_\theta$. In the frame of (f) this is $\partial_rf\,\hat r + \frac1r\partial_\theta f\,\hat\theta$, the gradient of vector calculus in polar coordinates ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|EM Theorem §B1.1.5]]). The vector "gradient" is the raised differential.
>
> *(e) Lengths of curves.* The circle $r = R$, parametrized by $\lambda = \theta \in [0, 2\pi]$, has $\dot x = (\dot r, \dot\theta) = (0, 1)$ and
>
> $$
> L = \int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{g_{rr}\cdot0^2 + g_{\theta\theta}\cdot1^2}\;d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}R\,d\theta = 2\pi R .
> $$
>
> The segment $x = 1$, $0 \le y \le 1$ in polar coordinates is $r = \sec\theta$, $0 \le \theta \le \pi/4$. With $\lambda = \theta$: $\dot r = \sec\theta\tan\theta$, $\dot\theta = 1$, and
>
> $$
> \dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2 = \sec^2\theta\tan^2\theta + \sec^2\theta = \sec^2\theta\,(\tan^2\theta + 1) = \sec^4\theta, \qquad L = \int_0^{\pi/4}\sec^2\theta\,d\theta = \tan\frac\pi4 - \tan0 = 1 ,
> $$
>
> the Cartesian length $\int_0^1dy = 1$. With the parametrization $y = \lambda^3$, $\lambda \in [0, 1]$, the Cartesian integral is $\int_0^1 3\lambda^2\,d\lambda = 1$ as well ([[#^def-c3-1-3|Def. §C3.1.3]], well defined).
>
> *(f) Orthonormal components.* The frame $\hat r = \partial_r$, $\hat\theta = \frac1r\partial_\theta$ is orthonormal ([[#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]), and $\partial_x$ has the components $X^{\hat r} = X^r = \cos\theta$, $X^{\hat\theta} = r\,X^\theta = -\sin\theta$, whose squares add to $1$, as orthonormal components of a unit vector must.
>
> *(g) Traces.* $g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\nu} = 1 + r^{-2}r^2 = 2$ in polar as in Cartesian coordinates, while $\sum_\mu g_{\mu\mu} = 1 + r^2$ ([[#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]], part 5).
>
> *Source: Schutz §5.2, eqs. (5.28)–(5.33) (pp. 122–123) · Hartle §7.1, eq. (7.6) (p. 136); §7.8, Fig. 7.7 · Couzens, GR1, eq. (4.4) · all numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.1.2: The Round Sphere
> *Setup.* The sphere of radius $a$, $S^2_a \subset \mathbf R^3$, with the chart $(\theta, \varphi) \in (0, \pi) \times (0, 2\pi)$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-3|Example §C2.1.3]]). Its metric is induced from the Euclidean metric of $\mathbf R^3$ ([[#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]); the pull-back was computed in [[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^ex-c2-4-2|Example §C2.4.2]] (GR1 Exercise 4.1), with the embedding $(a\sin\theta\cos\varphi, a\sin\theta\sin\varphi, a\cos\theta)$:
>
> $$
> ds^2 = a^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr), \qquad g_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(a^2,\ a^2\sin^2\theta), \qquad \det g = a^4\sin^2\theta .
> $$
>
> On the chart $\det g > 0$ and both entries are positive: a Riemannian metric. At the poles $\det g \to 0$; this is the chart failing, not the metric, which is positive definite in the stereographic chart through the poles ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^ex-c2-4-2|Example §C2.4.2]]).
>
> *(a) A latitude circle.* The curve $\lambda \mapsto (\theta_0, \lambda)$, $\lambda \in [0, 2\pi]$, has $\dot x = (0, 1)$ and $g(\dot x, \dot x) = g_{\varphi\varphi} = a^2\sin^2\theta_0$. Its length ([[#^def-c3-1-3|Def. §C3.1.3]]) is
>
> $$
> C(\theta_0) = \int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{a^2\sin^2\theta_0}\;d\lambda = 2\pi a\sin\theta_0 \qquad (0 < \theta_0 < \pi) ,
> $$
>
> GR1 (4.15) with $a = 1$. Equivalently, the circle is the submanifold $\iota(\varphi) = (\theta_0, \varphi)$ with induced metric $h = a^2\sin^2\theta_0\,d\varphi^2$ ([[#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]), since $d\theta$ pulls back to $0$.
>
> *(b) Its radius, measured on the sphere.* The meridian $\lambda \mapsto (\lambda, \varphi_0)$, $0 < \lambda \le \theta_0$, has $g(\dot x, \dot x) = a^2$, so its length from $\theta = \varepsilon$ to $\theta_0$ is $a(\theta_0 - \varepsilon)$, which tends to $a\theta_0$ as the starting point approaches the north pole. No curve from the pole to the circle is shorter. For any curve $\lambda \mapsto (\theta(\lambda), \varphi(\lambda))$ in the chart running from $\theta = \varepsilon$ to $\theta = \theta_0$,
>
> $$
> g(\dot x, \dot x) = a^2\bigl(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2\bigr) \ge a^2\dot\theta^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad L \ge a\int|\dot\theta|\,d\lambda \ge a\Bigl|\int\dot\theta\,d\lambda\Bigr| = a(\theta_0 - \varepsilon) .
> $$
>
> So the radius of the circle, as an inhabitant of the sphere measures it, is $\rho = a\theta_0$, and
>
> $$
> C = 2\pi a\sin\frac{\rho}{a} < 2\pi\rho \qquad (\rho > 0) .
> $$
>
> *(c) Numbers.* For $a = 1$, $\theta_0 = 1$: $C = 2\pi\sin1 = 5.287$, against $2\pi\rho = 6.283$; the ratio $C/2\pi\rho = \sin 1 = 0.8415$. For the equator, $\rho = \pi a/2$ and $C = 2\pi a$, so $C/\rho = 4$ instead of $2\pi$. The distance from pole to pole along a meridian is $\pi a$ (Hartle (2.23)).
>
> *(d) ⚑ Curvature is measurable from inside.* Parts (a)–(c) use only the metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ of the surface, not the embedding: someone confined to the sphere, with a measuring tape, finds circles that are too short for their radii. Expanding, $C = 2\pi a\bigl(\rho/a - \rho^3/6a^3 + \dots\bigr) = 2\pi\rho\bigl(1 - \rho^2/6a^2 + \dots\bigr)$. The coefficient $1/a^2$ reappears as the Gaussian curvature of the sphere ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^ex-c3-11-3|Example §C3.11.3]]).
>
> *(e) An orthonormal frame.* $e_{\hat\theta} = \frac1a\partial_\theta$, $e_{\hat\varphi} = \frac{1}{a\sin\theta}\partial_\varphi$ ([[#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]], diagonal case): $g(e_{\hat\theta}, e_{\hat\theta}) = a^2/a^2 = 1$, $g(e_{\hat\varphi}, e_{\hat\varphi}) = a^2\sin^2\theta/(a^2\sin^2\theta) = 1$, $g(e_{\hat\theta}, e_{\hat\varphi}) = 0$. It is not a coordinate basis. Acting on a function $f$,
>
> $$
> [e_{\hat\theta}, e_{\hat\varphi}]f = \frac1a\partial_\theta\Bigl(\frac{1}{a\sin\theta}\partial_\varphi f\Bigr) - \frac{1}{a\sin\theta}\partial_\varphi\Bigl(\frac1a\partial_\theta f\Bigr) = \frac1{a^2}\Bigl(-\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin^2\theta}\Bigr)\partial_\varphi f = -\frac{\cot\theta}{a}\,e_{\hat\varphi}f ,
> $$
>
> the mixed second derivatives cancelling; a coordinate basis would give $0$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.1, Example 4.1, eqs. (4.9)–(4.15) (p. 75); Exercise 4.1 (p. 76), solution App. C.3.1 · Hartle §2.6, eqs. (2.21)–(2.23) (pp. 26–27) · Schutz, Exercise 6.38 (circumference of a latitude circle) · d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 15.7 (distances on a sphere of radius $a$) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.1.3: Minkowski Spacetime in Spherical Coordinates
> *Setup.* Minkowski spacetime, $ds^2 = -dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$ in inertial coordinates ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]), and the chart $(t, r, \theta, \varphi)$ with $x = r\sin\theta\cos\varphi$, $y = r\sin\theta\sin\varphi$, $z = r\cos\theta$, $r > 0$, $0 < \theta < \pi$, $0 < \varphi < 2\pi$.
>
> *(a) Substitute* ([[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 3). The differentials are
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> dx &= \sin\theta\cos\varphi\,dr + r\cos\theta\cos\varphi\,d\theta - r\sin\theta\sin\varphi\,d\varphi, \\
> dy &= \sin\theta\sin\varphi\,dr + r\cos\theta\sin\varphi\,d\theta + r\sin\theta\cos\varphi\,d\varphi, \\
> dz &= \cos\theta\,dr - r\sin\theta\,d\theta .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> In $dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$ the coefficients are
> - $dr^2$: $\sin^2\theta\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\theta\sin^2\varphi + \cos^2\theta = 1$;
> - $d\theta^2$: $r^2\cos^2\theta\cos^2\varphi + r^2\cos^2\theta\sin^2\varphi + r^2\sin^2\theta = r^2$;
> - $d\varphi^2$: $r^2\sin^2\theta\sin^2\varphi + r^2\sin^2\theta\cos^2\varphi = r^2\sin^2\theta$;
> - $2\,dr\,d\theta$: $r\sin\theta\cos\theta\cos^2\varphi + r\sin\theta\cos\theta\sin^2\varphi - r\sin\theta\cos\theta = 0$;
> - $2\,dr\,d\varphi$: $-r\sin^2\theta\sin\varphi\cos\varphi + r\sin^2\theta\sin\varphi\cos\varphi = 0$;
> - $2\,d\theta\,d\varphi$: $-r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\sin\varphi\cos\varphi + r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\sin\varphi\cos\varphi = 0$.
>
> So
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -dt^2 + dr^2 + r^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr), \qquad g_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(-1,\ 1,\ r^2,\ r^2\sin^2\theta\bigr) .
> $$
>
> *(b) Signature and inverse.* $\det g = -r^4\sin^2\theta < 0$ on the chart; the diagonal entries have one negative sign, so the signature is $(-, +, +, +)$ at every point of the chart, as [[#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 5, requires. The inverse is $g^{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, r^{-2}, r^{-2}\sin^{-2}\theta)$. The components vary from point to point, yet the spacetime is the flat one; whether a metric is curved cannot be read off its components ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^ex-c3-9-3|Example §C3.9.3]]).
>
> *(c) Names of coordinates mean nothing.* Hartle's (7.5), $ds^2 = -dx^2 + dy^2 + y^2dz^2 + y^2\sin^2z\,dt^2$, is the same metric with the coordinates renamed $(t, r, \theta, \varphi) \to (x, y, z, t)$. There $\partial_x$ is timelike, $g(\partial_x, \partial_x) = -1$, and "$t$" is an angle. Causal character is read from $g$, not from the letters.
>
> *(d) Three induced metrics* ([[#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]).
> 1. *The slice $t = t_0$*, $\iota(r, \theta, \varphi) = (t_0, r, \theta, \varphi)$: $dt$ pulls back to $0$, so $h = dr^2 + r^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)$, Euclidean space in spherical coordinates. Riemannian: a **spacelike** hypersurface (also [[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-5|Theorem §C2.4.5]], part 4).
> 2. *The tube $r = R$*, $\iota(t, \theta, \varphi) = (t, R, \theta, \varphi)$: $dr$ pulls back to $0$, so $h = -dt^2 + R^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)$, signature $(-, +, +)$. Lorentzian: a **timelike** hypersurface, swept out by a sphere of static observers.
> 3. *The light cone $t = r$*, $\iota(r, \theta, \varphi) = (r, r, \theta, \varphi)$: $dt$ pulls back to $dr$, so
>
> $$
> h = -dr^2 + dr^2 + r^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr) = 0\cdot dr^2 + r^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr), \qquad \det h = 0 .
> $$
>
> The tangent vector $\iota_{\ast}\partial_r = \partial_t + \partial_r$ is null, $g = -1 + 1 = 0$, and orthogonal to the whole cone: $h(\partial_r, V) = 0$ for every $V$. A **null** hypersurface; $h$ is degenerate and not a metric.
>
> *Source: Hartle §7.1, eqs. (7.1)–(7.5) (pp. 135–136); §7.2, eq. (7.9) (p. 138) · Couzens, GR1, §4.1.2, eq. (4.16) (p. 76) · d'Inverno & Vickers §8.1 (Minkowski spacetime in curvilinear coordinates; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) · checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.1.4: The Hyperbolic Plane
> *Setup* (Hartle Prob. 8.12). The upper half-plane $H = \{(x, y) : y > 0\}$ with
>
> $$
> ds^2 = \frac{dx^2 + dy^2}{y^2}, \qquad g_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(y^{-2},\ y^{-2}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> a Riemannian metric ([[#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]): both entries are positive. Hartle writes the domain as $y \ge 0$; the metric is not defined at $y = 0$, and part (a) shows that the $x$-axis lies at infinite distance, so the manifold is $y > 0$. As a manifold $H$ is the same as an open half of the Euclidean plane; only the metric differs.
>
> *(a) The boundary is infinitely far away* (Hartle 8.12(a)). Let a curve run from $(x_0, y_0)$ to a point with $y = \varepsilon < y_0$. Its length ([[#^def-c3-1-3|Def. §C3.1.3]]) obeys
>
> $$
> L = \int\frac{\sqrt{\dot x^2 + \dot y^2}}{y}\,d\lambda \ \ge\ \int\frac{|\dot y|}{y}\,d\lambda \ \ge\ \Bigl|\int\frac{\dot y}{y}\,d\lambda\Bigr| = \Bigl|\ln\frac{\varepsilon}{y_0}\Bigr| = \ln\frac{y_0}{\varepsilon} ,
> $$
>
> with equality for the vertical segment $x = x_0$. As $\varepsilon \to 0$ the bound grows without limit: every point of the $x$-axis is at infinite distance. The vertical segment from $(0, 1)$ to $(0, e)$ has length $\int_1^e dy/y = 1$.
>
> *(b) The same geometry as a surface in Minkowski space.* Take three-dimensional Minkowski space with $ds^2 = -dT^2 + dX^2 + dY^2$ and the upper sheet $S$ of the hyperboloid $-T^2 + X^2 + Y^2 = -1$, $T > 0$, with the chart
>
> $$
> \iota(\rho, \varphi) = \bigl(\cosh\rho,\ \sinh\rho\cos\varphi,\ \sinh\rho\sin\varphi\bigr), \qquad \rho > 0,\ 0 < \varphi < 2\pi ,
> $$
>
> which lies on $S$ because $-\cosh^2\rho + \sinh^2\rho\,(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi) = -1$. The differentials are $dT = \sinh\rho\,d\rho$, $dX = \cosh\rho\cos\varphi\,d\rho - \sinh\rho\sin\varphi\,d\varphi$, $dY = \cosh\rho\sin\varphi\,d\rho + \sinh\rho\cos\varphi\,d\varphi$. In $dX^2 + dY^2$ the cross terms $\mp2\cosh\rho\sinh\rho\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi$ cancel, leaving $\cosh^2\rho\,d\rho^2 + \sinh^2\rho\,d\varphi^2$. The induced metric ([[#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]) is
>
> $$
> h = -\sinh^2\rho\,d\rho^2 + \cosh^2\rho\,d\rho^2 + \sinh^2\rho\,d\varphi^2 = d\rho^2 + \sinh^2\rho\,d\varphi^2 ,
> $$
>
> using $\cosh^2\rho - \sinh^2\rho = 1$. It is Riemannian although the surrounding metric is Lorentzian: $S$ is a spacelike surface, all its tangent vectors are spacelike. ($S$ is the set of unit future-pointing four-velocities of a $2 + 1$-dimensional special relativity, and $\rho$ is the rapidity relative to $(1, 0, 0)$.)
>
> *(c) The two descriptions agree.* The map
>
> $$
> F(x, y) = \Bigl(\frac{x^2 + y^2 + 1}{2y},\ \frac{x}{y},\ \frac{x^2 + y^2 - 1}{2y}\Bigr)
> $$
>
> sends $H$ into $S$: with $T - Y = 1/y$ and $T + Y = (x^2 + y^2)/y$, $-T^2 + Y^2 = -(T - Y)(T + Y) = -(x^2 + y^2)/y^2$, and adding $X^2 = x^2/y^2$ gives $-1$; and $T - 1 = \bigl(x^2 + (y - 1)^2\bigr)/2y \ge 0$, so $T > 0$. Pull back the Minkowski metric ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 4), writing $-dT^2 + dY^2 = -d(T - Y)\,d(T + Y)$:
>
> $$
> d(T - Y) = -\frac{dy}{y^2}, \qquad d(T + Y) = \frac{2x\,dx}{y} + \frac{(y^2 - x^2)\,dy}{y^2}, \qquad -d(T - Y)\,d(T + Y) = \frac{2x\,dx\,dy}{y^3} + \frac{(y^2 - x^2)\,dy^2}{y^4} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> dX = \frac{dx}{y} - \frac{x\,dy}{y^2}, \qquad dX^2 = \frac{dx^2}{y^2} - \frac{2x\,dx\,dy}{y^3} + \frac{x^2\,dy^2}{y^4} .
> $$
>
> The sum is $dx^2/y^2 + (y^2 - x^2 + x^2)\,dy^2/y^4 = (dx^2 + dy^2)/y^2$: $F^{\ast}(-dT^2 + dX^2 + dY^2)$ is Hartle's metric. The point $(0, 1)$ goes to $(1, 0, 0)$, the centre $\rho = 0$ of the chart of (b). Since $\cosh\rho = T$, the circle $\rho = \rho_0$ is, in the half-plane, $x^2 + y^2 + 1 = 2y\cosh\rho_0$, i.e.
>
> $$
> x^2 + (y - \cosh\rho_0)^2 = \sinh^2\rho_0 ,
> $$
>
> a Euclidean circle with Euclidean centre $(0, \cosh\rho_0)$, not $(0, 1)$, and Euclidean radius $\sinh\rho_0$. It meets the $y$-axis at $y = \cosh\rho_0 \pm \sinh\rho_0 = e^{\pm\rho_0}$, at distance $|\ln e^{\pm\rho_0}| = \rho_0$ from $(0, 1)$ along the axis, by (a).
>
> *(d) Circles are too long.* In the chart of (b), the circle $\rho = \rho_0$ ($\lambda = \varphi$) has length $\int_0^{2\pi}\sinh\rho_0\,d\varphi = 2\pi\sinh\rho_0$. Its radius is $\rho_0$: every curve from the centre to the circle has $h(\dot x, \dot x) = \dot\rho^2 + \sinh^2\rho\,\dot\varphi^2 \ge \dot\rho^2$, so its length is at least $\int|\dot\rho|\,d\lambda \ge \rho_0$, with equality along $\varphi = \text{const}$. So
>
> $$
> C = 2\pi\sinh\rho > 2\pi\rho \qquad (\rho > 0) .
> $$
>
> For $\rho = 1$: $C = 2\pi\sinh1 = 7.384$ against $2\pi = 6.283$, ratio $\sinh 1 = 1.1752$. The same number in the half-plane: parametrize the circle of (c) by $x = \sinh\rho_0\cos u$, $y = \cosh\rho_0 + \sinh\rho_0\sin u$; then $\dot x^2 + \dot y^2 = \sinh^2\rho_0$ and
>
> $$
> C = \int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\sinh\rho_0\,du}{\cosh\rho_0 + \sinh\rho_0\sin u} = \frac{2\pi\sinh\rho_0}{\sqrt{\cosh^2\rho_0 - \sinh^2\rho_0}} = 2\pi\sinh\rho_0 ,
> $$
>
> by $\int_0^{2\pi}du/(A + B\sin u) = 2\pi/\sqrt{A^2 - B^2}$ for $A > |B|$ (checked numerically for $\rho_0 = 0.5, 1, 2$). Two charts, one geometry, one length.
>
> *(e) Three geometries compared.* With $\rho$ the radius measured in the surface, a circle has $C/2\pi = \rho$ in the plane ([[#^ex-c3-1-1|Example §C3.1.1]]), $\sin\rho$ on the unit sphere ([[#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]) and $\sinh\rho$ on the hyperbolic plane (figure below). All three agree to first order in $\rho$, as every metric does near a point (§C3.2); they differ at order $\rho^3$, where curvature enters: positive for the sphere, negative for the hyperbolic plane (Hartle, remark to Prob. 8.12; curvature in §C3.11).
>
> *Source: Hartle, Problem 8.12 (a) (PDF 185, p. 184; worked here, no solution in the book; the domain is printed as $y \ge 0$) · Hartle §2.6, eq. (2.22) (circumference from the line element) · the hyperboloid chart and the map $F$ are written here · all identities and numbers checked by script*
