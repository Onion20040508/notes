Chapter C1 ended on a principle stated in one chart: a freely falling body moves on a worldline of stationary proper time ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]), and for slow bodies in a weak field this reproduces Newton ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]]). In special relativity the same worldlines are the straight ones ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). This section asks two questions that the connection of [[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative|§C3.5]] and [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection|§C3.6]] can now answer. *How is a vector at one point compared with a vector at another?* And *what is a straight line on a manifold, and is it the same thing as a worldline of stationary proper time?*

First, what fails without the connection. A natural guess for "straight" is "constant coordinate velocity", $d^2x^\mu/ds^2 = 0$. Try it on the flat plane in polar coordinates $(r, \varphi)$, with metric $ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\varphi^2$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-1|Example §C3.1.1]]). The curve $r = 1 + s$, $\varphi = s$ has $d^2r/ds^2 = 0$ and $d^2\varphi/ds^2 = 0$, yet it is an Archimedean spiral. The straight line $x = 1$, written $r = \sqrt{1 + s^2}$, $\varphi = \arctan s$, has

$$
\frac{d^2r}{ds^2} = \frac{d}{ds}\frac{s}{\sqrt{1 + s^2}} = \frac{1}{(1 + s^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{1}{r^3} \neq 0 .
$$

So "constant components" picks out different curves in different charts, for the same reason that $\partial_\mu V^\nu$ is not a tensor ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]]; the constant field $\partial_x$ has non-constant polar components, [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^ex-c2-3-3|Example §C2.3.3]]). On the sphere there is no chart in which great circles have constant coordinate velocity everywhere, and in a curved spacetime no chart is inertial everywhere ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-7|Theorem §C1.4.7]]). The fix is to measure the change of a vector along a curve with the covariant derivative along the curve ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-3|Def. §C3.5.3]]), which corrects the coordinate derivative by the connection coefficients. A vector whose covariant derivative along the curve vanishes is "carried without turning": that is parallel transport ([[#^def-c3-7-1|Def. §C3.7.1]]). For the Levi-Civita connection it preserves lengths and angles ([[#^thm-c3-7-1|Theorem §C3.7.1]]). A curve that carries its own velocity this way is a geodesic ([[#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]), the "straightest" curve. In components this is the geodesic equation ([[#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]), and it fixes the parametrization up to $\lambda \mapsto a\lambda + b$ ([[#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]]). The centrepiece answers the second question: the timelike geodesics are exactly the worldlines of stationary proper time ([[#^thm-c3-7-4|Theorem §C3.7.4]]). With it the free-fall principle of C1 takes its final form, the geodesic postulate ([[#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]). Two working tools follow: the quadratic geodesic Lagrangian, the fastest way to the Christoffel symbols and to conserved quantities ([[#^thm-c3-7-6|Theorem §C3.7.6]]), and the constancy of $g(\dot x, \dot x)$ along a geodesic ([[#^thm-c3-7-7|Theorem §C3.7.7]]). A caution records that stationary does not mean maximal ([[#^cau-c3-7-1|Caution: Extremal, not always maximal]]).

**Setup.** A smooth manifold $M$ of dimension $n$ with an affine connection $\nabla$ ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-1|Def. §C3.5.1]]) and, from [[#^thm-c3-7-1|Theorem §C3.7.1]], part 2, on, a metric $g$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]) with its Levi-Civita connection ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-1|Theorem §C3.6.1]]). The metric is Riemannian (the sphere, the plane) or Lorentzian (spacetime) ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]); each statement says which. Curves are smooth maps $\gamma : J \to M$ from an interval $J \subset \mathbf R$ with velocity $\dot\gamma$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]); in the variational statements they are $C^2$ on a closed interval with fixed endpoints, as in [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^def-b5-1-1|CM Def. §B5.1.1]]. All statements are exact within this setup. The physical content enters in one place only, [[#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]. The numbers in the examples, the transported vectors of the figure and the animation were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; $G = c = 1$; Greek indices $0$–$3$ (or $1$–$n$ on a general manifold), Latin $1$–$3$. Connection coefficients with the derivative index first, $\nabla_\mu e_\nu = \Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}e_\lambda$, so $\nabla_\mu V^\lambda = \partial_\mu V^\lambda + \Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}V^\nu$ ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-2|Def. §C3.5.2]]; [[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^cau-c3-5-1|§C3.5, Caution: Index order of Γ in the sources]]). A dot is $d/d\lambda$ for the curve's parameter $\lambda$. Proper time $d\tau^2 = -ds^2$ and $u\cdot u = -1$; in Relativity B (signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) $u\cdot u = +c^2$, and proper time is the same positive number ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]).

## Comparing vectors along a curve

On a manifold the tangent spaces $T_pM$ and $T_qM$ at two points are different vector spaces ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]), and nothing so far identifies them. The Lie derivative compares them by dragging along a flow, but it needs a whole vector field near the curve ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-8|Theorem §C2.5.8]]). The covariant derivative along a curve needs only the curve. Setting it to zero turns it into a rule that carries a vector from $p$ to $q$.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.7.1: Parallel Transport along a Curve
> *Structure.* Let $\gamma : J \to M$ be a smooth curve. A **vector field along $\gamma$** assigns to each $\lambda \in J$ a tangent vector $Y(\lambda) \in T_{\gamma(\lambda)}M$, smoothly in $\lambda$ (its components in any chart are smooth functions of $\lambda$). It need not extend to a vector field on $M$: if $\gamma$ crosses itself, $Y$ may take two values at the same point. The connection $\nabla$ gives its **covariant derivative along $\gamma$**, $DY/d\lambda$, again a vector field along $\gamma$ ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-3|Def. §C3.5.3]]). In a chart $x^\mu$, with $x^\mu(\lambda) = x^\mu(\gamma(\lambda))$,
>
> $$
> \frac{DY^\mu}{d\lambda} = \frac{dY^\mu}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\bigl(x(\lambda)\bigr)\,\frac{dx^\nu}{d\lambda}\,Y^\rho .
> $$
>
> $Y$ is **parallel along $\gamma$** (is **parallel-transported** along $\gamma$) if
>
> $$
> \frac{DY}{d\lambda} = 0, \qquad\text{in a chart}\qquad \frac{dY^\mu}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\,\dot x^\nu\,Y^\rho = 0 \quad (\mu = 1, \dots, n) .
> $$
>
> *Transport map.* For $\lambda_0, \lambda_1 \in J$, $p = \gamma(\lambda_0)$, $q = \gamma(\lambda_1)$, the **parallel transport** along $\gamma$ is the map
>
> $$
> P^\gamma_{\lambda_1\lambda_0} : T_pM \to T_qM, \qquad Y_0 \mapsto Y(\lambda_1), \quad\text{where } Y \text{ is parallel along } \gamma \text{ with } Y(\lambda_0) = Y_0 .
> $$
>
> It is well defined because the parallel field with given initial value exists and is unique ([[#^thm-c3-7-1|Theorem §C3.7.1]], part 1). It depends on the curve, not only on $p$ and $q$ ([[#^ex-c3-7-4|Example §C3.7.4]]).
>
> If $\gamma$ is an integral curve of a vector field $X$ and $Y$ is a vector field on $M$, the condition reads $\nabla_XY = 0$ along $\gamma$ (GR1 (4.83)). Tensors of any type are transported in the same way, $DT/d\lambda = 0$, with the covariant derivative of [[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]] contracted with $\dot x$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4, eqs. (4.82)–(4.85) (p. 87); §4.4.4, eq. (4.124) (p. 97; derivative index of $\Gamma$ written last there, see [[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^cau-c3-5-1|§C3.5, Caution: Index order of Γ in the sources]]) · Schutz §6.4, eqs. (6.46)–(6.48) (p. 154) · Hartle §20.4 (parallel transport)*

^def-c3-7-1

The transport equation is a system of $n$ linear first-order ordinary differential equations for the components $Y^\mu(\lambda)$, with coefficients fixed by the curve. Linear equations have solutions on the whole interval and depend linearly on the initial value. That gives the transport map its basic properties. For a metric-compatible connection it also preserves the metric.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.7.1: Parallel Transport Is Linear and Preserves Inner Products
> Let $\gamma : J \to M$ be a smooth curve, $\lambda_0, \lambda_1 \in J$, $p = \gamma(\lambda_0)$, $q = \gamma(\lambda_1)$.
> 1. **Existence, uniqueness, linearity.** For every $Y_0 \in T_pM$ there is exactly one vector field $Y$ along $\gamma$, defined on all of $J$, that is parallel along $\gamma$ ([[#^def-c3-7-1|Def. §C3.7.1]]) and has $Y(\lambda_0) = Y_0$. The transport map $P^\gamma_{\lambda_1\lambda_0} : T_pM \to T_qM$ is a linear isomorphism, with inverse $P^\gamma_{\lambda_0\lambda_1}$.
> 2. **Inner products.** If $\nabla$ is compatible with a metric $g$, $\nabla g = 0$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^def-c3-6-2|Def. §C3.6.2]]), in particular for the Levi-Civita connection, then for any two fields $Y, Z$ parallel along $\gamma$
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\lambda}\,g\bigl(Y(\lambda), Z(\lambda)\bigr) = 0 .
> $$
>
> So $P^\gamma_{\lambda_1\lambda_0}$ is a **linear isometry**, $g_q(PY_0, PZ_0) = g_p(Y_0, Z_0)$: parallel transport preserves norms, angles (Riemannian case) and, on a Lorentzian manifold, the causal character of a vector ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]).
>
> *Model:* a manifold with a connection; part 2 with a metric and a metric-compatible connection; *applies:* exactly, for every smooth curve. Torsion plays no role.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4, after (4.85) (p. 87: "unique vector field at each point along the curve"); §4.4.1, eq. (4.91) (p. 89) · Schutz §6.4 (p. 154: "keeping it parallel and of the same length") · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.10, eq. (6.74) (p. 104; $\nabla_cg_{ab} = 0$ for the metric connection, the input of part 2)*

^thm-c3-7-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (one chart: a linear system). Let $[s, s'] \subset J$ be a closed interval with $\gamma([s, s'])$ inside the domain of one chart. With $A^\mu{}_\rho(\lambda) \equiv \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\bigl(x(\lambda)\bigr)\,\dot x^\nu(\lambda)$, the transport equation of [[#^def-c3-7-1|Def. §C3.7.1]] reads
>
> $$
> \frac{dY^\mu}{d\lambda} = -A^\mu{}_\rho(\lambda)\,Y^\rho, \qquad \mu = 1, \dots, n ,
> $$
>
> a homogeneous linear system whose coefficients $A^\mu{}_\rho$ are continuous (the $\Gamma$'s are smooth and $\dot x$ is smooth). By [[§33 Introduction to Systems of First-Order Linear Equations#^thm-33-3|ODE Theorem §33.3]], for every initial value $Y^\mu(s)$ there is exactly one solution, and it exists on the whole of $[s, s']$ (for a linear system there is no blow-up before the end of the interval).
>
> **Step 2** (charts agree). The condition $DY/d\lambda = 0$ is chart-independent: $DY/d\lambda$ is a vector along $\gamma$ ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-3|Def. §C3.5.3]]), and a vector vanishes in one chart exactly when it vanishes in every chart ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]). So where two charts overlap, their solutions with the same initial vector both solve the same coordinate-free condition, and by the uniqueness of Step 1 (applied in either chart) they agree.
>
> **Step 3** (the whole curve). Take $\lambda_1 > \lambda_0$ (the other case is the same with the interval reversed). The image $\gamma([\lambda_0, \lambda_1])$ is compact, so finitely many chart domains cover it, and there are $\lambda_0 = s_0 < s_1 < \dots < s_N = \lambda_1$ with each $\gamma([s_{k-1}, s_k])$ inside one chart. Solve on $[s_0, s_1]$ with $Y(s_0) = Y_0$, then on $[s_1, s_2]$ with initial value $Y(s_1)$, and so on. By Steps 1–2 each piece exists and is unique, so $Y$ exists and is unique on $[\lambda_0, \lambda_1]$. Every $\lambda_1 \in J$ is reached this way, so $Y$ is defined on all of $J$.
>
> **Step 4** (linearity). Let $Y, Z$ be parallel along $\gamma$ and $a, b \in \mathbf R$. The covariant derivative along $\gamma$ is linear in the field (its component formula is linear in $Y^\mu$), so
>
> $$
> \frac{D(aY + bZ)^\mu}{d\lambda} = a\Bigl(\frac{dY^\mu}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\dot x^\nu Y^\rho\Bigr) + b\Bigl(\frac{dZ^\mu}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\dot x^\nu Z^\rho\Bigr) = a\cdot 0 + b\cdot 0 = 0 .
> $$
>
> So $aY + bZ$ is the parallel field with initial value $aY_0 + bZ_0$, and by uniqueness $P(aY_0 + bZ_0) = aPY_0 + bPZ_0$.
>
> **Step 5** (inverse). The parallel field $Y$ with $Y(\lambda_0) = Y_0$ is also the parallel field with initial value $Y(\lambda_1)$ at $\lambda_1$ (same equation, uniqueness). Hence $P^\gamma_{\lambda_0\lambda_1}P^\gamma_{\lambda_1\lambda_0}Y_0 = Y_0$, and likewise in the other order. So $P^\gamma_{\lambda_1\lambda_0}$ is bijective, a linear isomorphism. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 6** (differentiate the inner product). In a chart, $g(Y, Z) = g_{\mu\nu}(x(\lambda))\,Y^\mu Z^\nu$. By the product and chain rules,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\lambda}\bigl(g_{\mu\nu}Y^\mu Z^\nu\bigr) = \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^\rho\,Y^\mu Z^\nu + g_{\mu\nu}\,\frac{dY^\mu}{d\lambda}\,Z^\nu + g_{\mu\nu}\,Y^\mu\,\frac{dZ^\nu}{d\lambda} .
> $$
>
> **Step 7** (insert the transport equations). With $dY^\mu/d\lambda = -\Gamma^\mu_{\rho\sigma}\dot x^\rho Y^\sigma$ and $dZ^\nu/d\lambda = -\Gamma^\nu_{\rho\sigma}\dot x^\rho Z^\sigma$, the last two terms are
>
> $$
> -g_{\mu\nu}\Gamma^\mu_{\rho\sigma}\dot x^\rho Y^\sigma Z^\nu - g_{\mu\nu}\Gamma^\nu_{\rho\sigma}\dot x^\rho Y^\mu Z^\sigma = -g_{\sigma\nu}\Gamma^\sigma_{\rho\mu}\dot x^\rho Y^\mu Z^\nu - g_{\mu\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma_{\rho\nu}\dot x^\rho Y^\mu Z^\nu ,
> $$
>
> where in the first term the dummy indices $\mu$ and $\sigma$ were renamed into each other, and in the second $\nu$ and $\sigma$. All three terms now carry $\dot x^\rho Y^\mu Z^\nu$:
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\lambda}\,g(Y, Z) = \dot x^\rho\,Y^\mu Z^\nu\,\bigl(\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} - \Gamma^\sigma_{\rho\mu}\,g_{\sigma\nu} - \Gamma^\sigma_{\rho\nu}\,g_{\mu\sigma}\bigr) = \dot x^\rho\,Y^\mu Z^\nu\,\nabla_\rho g_{\mu\nu} ,
> $$
>
> the bracket being the covariant derivative of the $(0, 2)$ tensor $g$ ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]], one minus-$\Gamma$ term per lower index, derivative index first).
>
> **Step 8** (metric compatibility). $\nabla_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^def-c3-6-2|Def. §C3.6.2]]), so $d\,g(Y, Z)/d\lambda = 0$. Taking $\lambda = \lambda_1$, $g_q(PY_0, PZ_0) = g_p(Y_0, Z_0)$. With $Z = Y$ the norm $g(Y, Y)$ is constant, so its sign, which decides timelike, null or spacelike, cannot change; angles are ratios of inner products and norms and are preserved too. This is part 2.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Linearity and invertibility come from the linearity of the transport equation alone; any connection gives them. Preservation of inner products needs exactly $\nabla g = 0$ along the curve. The torsion-free condition was not used, so a metric connection with torsion would also transport isometrically; it is the geodesics that see the torsion ([[#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]], part 2).

## Geodesics: curves that carry their own velocity

In the plane a straight line is the curve whose direction never changes: its velocity at one point is parallel to its velocity at the next (Schutz §6.4). Euclid's "extend a line" means exactly this, not "keep a constant distance". With parallel transport the same idea makes sense on any manifold with a connection.

> [!definition] Definition §C3.7.2: Geodesic; Affine Parameter
> *Structure.* A smooth curve $\gamma : J \to M$ is an **affinely parametrized geodesic** if its velocity $\dot\gamma$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]), a vector field along $\gamma$, is parallel along $\gamma$ ([[#^def-c3-7-1|Def. §C3.7.1]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{D\dot\gamma}{d\lambda} = 0, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma = 0 .
> $$
>
> Its parameter $\lambda$ is then called an **affine parameter**. A curve that becomes an affinely parametrized geodesic after a change of parameter $\lambda = \lambda(\sigma)$ with $d\lambda/d\sigma > 0$ is a **geodesic** (as a set of points with a direction of travel, the parametrization aside); in another parameter it is a non-affinely parametrized geodesic.
>
> *On a Lorentzian manifold* a geodesic is **timelike, null or spacelike** according to the type of its velocity ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]); for the Levi-Civita connection the type is the same at every point ([[#^thm-c3-7-7|Theorem §C3.7.7]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.1, Definition 39, eq. (4.86) (p. 88) · Schutz §6.4, eq. (6.49) (p. 155) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.4 (affine geodesics), §6.9 (metric geodesics), p. 103 (the two coincide for the metric connection, (6.71)–(6.72)) · Hartle §8.1, §8.3*

^def-c3-7-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.7.2: Geodesic Equation
> Let $\nabla$ be a connection on $M$ and $\gamma$ a curve with coordinates $x^\mu(\lambda)$ in a chart.
> 1. **The equation.** $\gamma$ is an affinely parametrized geodesic ([[#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]) exactly when
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2x^\mu}{d\lambda^2} + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}(x)\,\frac{dx^\nu}{d\lambda}\,\frac{dx^\rho}{d\lambda} = 0, \qquad \mu = 1, \dots, n .
> $$
>
> For the Levi-Civita connection the coefficients are the Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho} = \tfrac12 g^{\mu\sigma}(\partial_\nu g_{\sigma\rho} + \partial_\rho g_{\sigma\nu} - \partial_\sigma g_{\nu\rho})$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]).
> 2. **Only the symmetric part enters.** The equation contains $\Gamma^\mu_{(\nu\rho)} = \tfrac12(\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho} + \Gamma^\mu_{\rho\nu})$ only. Two connections with the same symmetric part, that is differing by torsion ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^def-c3-6-1|Def. §C3.6.1]]), have the same geodesics.
> 3. **Initial-value problem.** For every $p \in M$ and $v \in T_pM$ there is an open interval $J \ni 0$ and an affinely parametrized geodesic $\gamma : J \to M$ with $\gamma(0) = p$, $\dot\gamma(0) = v$; two such geodesics agree on the intersection of their intervals. Unlike parallel transport, the equation is nonlinear in $x^\mu$, and the solution need not extend to all $\lambda \in \mathbf R$.
>
> *Model:* a manifold with a connection; *applies:* exactly, in any chart.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.1, eq. (4.87) (p. 88) · Schutz §6.4, eqs. (6.50)–(6.51) (p. 155; unique solution for given $x_0^\alpha$, $U_0^\alpha$) · Hartle §8.1, eqs. (8.14)–(8.16) (p. 173) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.9, eq. (6.67) (p. 102)*

^thm-c3-7-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the velocity as a field along $\gamma$). By [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]] the velocity has components $\dot\gamma^\mu = dx^\mu/d\lambda$. Put $Y^\rho = dx^\rho/d\lambda$ into the formula of [[#^def-c3-7-1|Def. §C3.7.1]]:
>
> $$
> \frac{D\dot\gamma^\mu}{d\lambda} = \frac{d}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\,\frac{dx^\nu}{d\lambda}\,\frac{dx^\rho}{d\lambda} = \frac{d^2x^\mu}{d\lambda^2} + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\,\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho .
> $$
>
> Setting it to zero is part 1. For the Levi-Civita connection insert [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]].
>
> **Step 2** (symmetric part). Split $\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho} = \Gamma^\mu_{(\nu\rho)} + \Gamma^\mu_{[\nu\rho]}$ with $\Gamma^\mu_{[\nu\rho]} = \tfrac12(\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho} - \Gamma^\mu_{\rho\nu})$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-5|Def. §C2.3.5]]). Renaming $\nu \leftrightarrow \rho$ in the antisymmetric term,
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\mu_{[\nu\rho]}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho = \Gamma^\mu_{[\rho\nu]}\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu = -\Gamma^\mu_{[\nu\rho]}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Gamma^\mu_{[\nu\rho]}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho = 0 .
> $$
>
> The antisymmetric part is $\tfrac12T^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}$, half the torsion. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 3** (first-order system). Introduce $u^\mu = dx^\mu/d\lambda$. Part 1 is equivalent to the $2n$ first-order equations
>
> $$
> \frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda} = u^\mu, \qquad \frac{du^\mu}{d\lambda} = -\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}(x)\,u^\nu u^\rho ,
> $$
>
> whose right-hand sides are smooth functions of $(x, u)$ on (chart domain) $\times\,\mathbf R^n$, with continuous partial derivatives. By [[§33 Introduction to Systems of First-Order Linear Equations#^thm-33-2|ODE Theorem §33.2]] the initial values $x^\mu(0) = x^\mu(p)$, $u^\mu(0) = v^\mu$ have a unique solution on some interval $|\lambda| < h$. (On the manifold this is the integral curve of a vector field on the tangent bundle, [[§68 Local Existence and Uniqueness#^thm-68-2|591 Thm. §68.2]].) Uniqueness on overlaps of intervals and of charts follows as in Steps 2–3 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c3-7-1|Theorem §C3.7.1]], since the condition $D\dot\gamma/d\lambda = 0$ is chart-independent.
>
> **Step 4** (no global existence in general). The system is quadratic in $u$, so the theorem for linear systems ([[§33 Introduction to Systems of First-Order Linear Equations#^thm-33-3|ODE Theorem §33.3]]) does not apply, and $h$ may be finite. The open half-plane $y > 0$ of $\mathbf R^2$ with the flat metric is an example: the geodesic $x = 0$, $y = 1 - \lambda$ leaves the manifold at $\lambda = 1$. This is part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The geodesic equation is the transport equation with the curve's own velocity as the transported vector, so it is second order and nonlinear. A point and a velocity determine a geodesic: in physics, an event and a four-velocity determine a free worldline ([[#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]).

The definition singles out a parameter: a geodesic run at a non-constant speed is no longer "affinely parametrized", although it traces the same points. The next theorem says how the equation changes under a change of parameter, and how much freedom is left.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.7.3: Reparametrization of Geodesics
> Let $\gamma(\lambda)$ be a curve and $\lambda = \lambda(\sigma)$ a change of parameter with $f \equiv d\lambda/d\sigma > 0$; write $\tilde\gamma(\sigma) = \gamma(\lambda(\sigma))$, with velocity $\tilde\gamma' = d\tilde\gamma/d\sigma$.
> 1. **Transformation.** $\tilde\gamma' = f\,\dot\gamma$ and
>
> $$
> \frac{D\tilde\gamma'}{d\sigma} = f^2\,\frac{D\dot\gamma}{d\lambda} + \frac{d^2\lambda}{d\sigma^2}\,\dot\gamma .
> $$
>
> If $\lambda$ is affine, then $D\tilde\gamma'/d\sigma = h\,\tilde\gamma'$ with $h = \dfrac{d^2\lambda/d\sigma^2}{d\lambda/d\sigma}$; in the vector-field notation of GR1 ($X = \dot\gamma$, $Y = fX$), $\nabla_YY = X[f]\,Y$. The curve is the same geodesic, non-affinely parametrized.
> 2. **Every non-affine geodesic can be made affine.** If a curve with parameter $\lambda$ obeys $D\dot\gamma/d\lambda = h(\lambda)\,\dot\gamma$ for a continuous function $h$, then
>
> $$
> \sigma(\lambda) = \int_{\lambda_0}^{\lambda}\exp\Bigl(\int_{\lambda_0}^{\lambda'}h(\lambda'')\,d\lambda''\Bigr)d\lambda'
> $$
>
> is an affine parameter for it.
> 3. **Two-parameter freedom.** The affine parameters of an affinely parametrized geodesic are exactly $\sigma = a\lambda + b$ with constants $a > 0$, $b$ ($a < 0$ if the direction of travel may be reversed).
>
> *Where non-affine geodesics arise.* The integral curves of the normal of a null hypersurface are null geodesics, in general non-affinely parametrised; part 2 makes them affine ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^thm-c10-5-3|Theorem §C10.5.3]]).
>
> *Model:* a manifold with a connection; *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.1, eqs. (4.88)–(4.89) and the two paragraphs after (p. 88) · Schutz §6.4, eq. (6.52) (p. 155) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.9, eqs. (6.64), (6.66) (p. 102); §7.6, eqs. (7.42), (7.44)–(7.45) (pp. 126–127)*

^thm-c3-7-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (velocity). By the chain rule, $dx^\mu/d\sigma = (d\lambda/d\sigma)\,dx^\mu/d\lambda = f\,\dot x^\mu$.
>
> **Step 2** (second derivative). With the product rule and $d/d\sigma = f\,d/d\lambda$,
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2x^\mu}{d\sigma^2} = \frac{df}{d\sigma}\,\dot x^\mu + f\,\frac{d\dot x^\mu}{d\sigma} = \frac{d^2\lambda}{d\sigma^2}\,\dot x^\mu + f^2\,\ddot x^\mu .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (covariant acceleration). Adding $\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\,(dx^\nu/d\sigma)(dx^\rho/d\sigma) = f^2\,\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho$,
>
> $$
> \frac{D\tilde\gamma'^\mu}{d\sigma} = f^2\bigl(\ddot x^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho\bigr) + \frac{d^2\lambda}{d\sigma^2}\,\dot x^\mu = f^2\,\frac{D\dot\gamma^\mu}{d\lambda} + \frac{d^2\lambda}{d\sigma^2}\,\dot x^\mu .
> $$
>
> If $D\dot\gamma/d\lambda = 0$, then, with $\dot x^\mu = \tilde\gamma'^\mu/f$, $D\tilde\gamma'/d\sigma = (d^2\lambda/d\sigma^2)/f\cdot\tilde\gamma'$. In GR1's form: $d^2\lambda/d\sigma^2 = df/d\sigma = f\,df/d\lambda = f\,X[f]$, so the factor is $X[f]$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (making it affine). Now let $D\dot\gamma/d\lambda = h\,\dot\gamma$ and look for a new parameter $\sigma$, with $\lambda = \lambda(\sigma)$. Step 3 with the roles as stated gives
>
> $$
> \frac{D\tilde\gamma'}{d\sigma} = \Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{d\lambda}{d\sigma}\Bigr)^2h + \frac{d^2\lambda}{d\sigma^2}\Bigr]\dot\gamma ,
> $$
>
> which vanishes exactly when $d^2\lambda/d\sigma^2 = -h\,(d\lambda/d\sigma)^2$. Write $w(\lambda) = d\sigma/d\lambda > 0$, so $d\lambda/d\sigma = 1/w$ and
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2\lambda}{d\sigma^2} = \frac{d}{d\sigma}\frac1w = \frac1w\,\frac{d}{d\lambda}\frac1w = -\frac{w'}{w^3}, \qquad w' = \frac{dw}{d\lambda} .
> $$
>
> The condition becomes $-w'/w^3 = -h/w^2$, that is $w' = h\,w$, solved by $w = \exp\bigl(\int_{\lambda_0}^{\lambda}h\bigr) > 0$. Integrating $d\sigma/d\lambda = w$ gives the $\sigma$ of part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (the remaining freedom). If $\lambda$ is already affine ($h = 0$), Step 4 requires $w' = 0$: $w = a$ constant and $\sigma = a\lambda + b$. Conversely, for $\sigma = a\lambda + b$, $d^2\lambda/d\sigma^2 = 0$ and Step 3 keeps the equation homogeneous. This is part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** A change of parameter adds to the covariant acceleration a term along the velocity, never one across it. Whether a curve is a geodesic as a set of points is decided by the part of $D\dot\gamma/d\lambda$ transverse to $\dot\gamma$; the part along $\dot\gamma$ only records how the parameter runs.

## Geodesics and proper time

Geodesics were defined as the straightest curves, through the connection. C1 described free fall by a different property, stationary proper time, which uses only the metric. For the Levi-Civita connection the two descriptions select the same curves. This is GR1's Exercise 4.3, which has no written solution there, worked out in full.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.7.4: Geodesics Are the Stationary Curves of Proper Time
> Let $(M, g)$ be a Lorentzian manifold with its Levi-Civita connection, and fix two events $p, q$. Compare $C^2$ timelike curves $x^\mu(\lambda)$, $\lambda \in [\lambda_A, \lambda_B]$, from $x(\lambda_A) = p$ to $x(\lambda_B) = q$, through their proper time ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]; [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]] in one chart)
>
> $$
> \tau[x] = \int_{\lambda_A}^{\lambda_B}F\,d\lambda, \qquad F(x, \dot x) = \sqrt{-g_{\mu\nu}(x)\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu} = \frac{d\tau}{d\lambda} > 0 .
> $$
>
> 1. **Euler–Lagrange equations.** A timelike curve is stationary for $\tau$ under all variations with fixed endpoints ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^def-b5-1-2|CM Def. §B5.1.2]]) exactly when, in any chart,
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2x^\mu}{d\lambda^2} + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\,\frac{dx^\nu}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\rho}{d\lambda} = \frac{\dot F}{F}\,\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}, \qquad \frac{\dot F}{F} = \frac{d^2\tau/d\lambda^2}{d\tau/d\lambda} ,
> $$
>
> with the Christoffel symbols of $g$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]). That is: **the stationary curves of proper time are exactly the timelike geodesics** ([[#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]), in an arbitrary parametrization ([[#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 1).
> 2. **Proper time is an affine parameter.** Parametrized by its proper time, a stationary curve obeys the geodesic equation $d^2x^\mu/d\tau^2 + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}u^\nu u^\rho = 0$ with $u^\mu = dx^\mu/d\tau$, $u\cdot u = -1$. The affine parameters of a timelike geodesic are $a\tau + b$, $a > 0$ (the answer to GR1 Exercise 4.3).
> 3. **Length.** On a Riemannian manifold, and for spacelike curves of a Lorentzian manifold, the same holds for the length $\int\sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}\,d\lambda$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-3|Def. §C3.1.3]]), with arc length as the affine parameter.
> 4. **Layer change.** [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]] ("free fall makes proper time stationary") is therefore equivalent to "free bodies move on timelike geodesics", the form taken as the principle from now on ([[#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]). In flat spacetime it is the uniform straight motion of [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]].
>
> "Stationary" is not "maximal" ([[#^cau-c3-7-1|Caution: Extremal, not always maximal]]). Null curves are excluded: on them $F = 0$, and the square root is not differentiable there ([[#^thm-c3-7-6|Theorem §C3.7.6]] covers them).
>
> *Model:* Lorentzian (part 3: also Riemannian) manifold, Levi-Civita connection; $C^2$ curves of one causal type, fixed endpoints; *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.1, Exercise 4.3, eq. (4.90) (pp. 88–89; Appendix C.3.3 refers to a problem sheet, no written solution; derived here) · Hartle §8.1, eqs. (8.7)–(8.14), Table 8.1 (pp. 170–173) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.9, eqs. (6.61)–(6.67) (p. 102); §7.6, eqs. (7.37)–(7.43) (pp. 125–127; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, $L = (g_{ab}\dot x^a\dot x^b)^{1/2}$ there) · Schutz §6.4, p. 155 (Exercise: geodesics are curves of extremal length)*

^thm-c3-7-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Write $\varepsilon = -1$ for timelike curves and $\varepsilon = +1$ for the length of spacelike or Riemannian curves, so that in both cases $F = \sqrt{\varepsilon\,g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu} > 0$ and the functional is $\int F\,d\lambda$.
>
> **Step 0** (variations stay admissible). Let $\eta^\mu(\lambda)$ be $C^2$ with $\eta(\lambda_A) = \eta(\lambda_B) = 0$. The function $(s, \lambda) \mapsto \varepsilon\,g_{\mu\nu}(x + s\eta)(\dot x + s\dot\eta)^\mu(\dot x + s\dot\eta)^\nu$ is continuous and positive at $s = 0$ on the compact interval $[\lambda_A, \lambda_B]$, hence bounded below by a positive constant there, and still positive for $|s|$ small. So nearby curves are of the same type, $F$ is differentiable along the family, and the Euler–Lagrange equations for several functions apply ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|CM Theorem §B5.1.4]]; independent variable $\lambda$, functions $x^1, \dots, x^n$).
>
> **Step 1** (partial derivatives). Treat $x^\alpha$ and $\dot x^\alpha$ as independent. Using $g_{\mu\nu} = g_{\nu\mu}$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial F}{\partial\dot x^\alpha} = \frac{\varepsilon\,(g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\nu + g_{\mu\alpha}\dot x^\mu)}{2F} = \frac{\varepsilon\,g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\nu}{F}, \qquad \frac{\partial F}{\partial x^\alpha} = \frac{\varepsilon\,\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}{2F} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (Euler–Lagrange). $\dfrac{d}{d\lambda}\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial\dot x^\alpha} - \dfrac{\partial F}{\partial x^\alpha} = 0$ reads
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\lambda}\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon\,g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\nu}{F}\Bigr) - \frac{\varepsilon\,\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}{2F} = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (clear the square root). Multiply by $\varepsilon F \neq 0$ and use $F\,\frac{d}{d\lambda}\bigl(\frac{A}{F}\bigr) = \frac{dA}{d\lambda} - \frac{\dot F}{F}A$:
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\lambda}\bigl(g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\nu\bigr) - \frac{\dot F}{F}\,g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\nu - \tfrac12\,\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu = 0 .
> $$
>
> The sign $\varepsilon$ has dropped out: timelike and spacelike curves give the same equation.
>
> **Step 4** (expand the derivative). By the product and chain rules, $\frac{d}{d\lambda}(g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\nu) = g_{\alpha\nu}\ddot x^\nu + \partial_\rho g_{\alpha\nu}\,\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu$. So
>
> $$
> g_{\alpha\nu}\ddot x^\nu + \partial_\rho g_{\alpha\nu}\,\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu - \tfrac12\,\partial_\alpha g_{\rho\nu}\,\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu = \frac{\dot F}{F}\,g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\nu ,
> $$
>
> where the dummy pair $\mu\nu$ of the last term on the left was renamed $\rho\nu$.
>
> **Step 5** (symmetrize). $\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu$ is symmetric in $\rho\nu$, so only the symmetric part of the coefficient counts: $\partial_\rho g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu = \tfrac12(\partial_\rho g_{\alpha\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\alpha\rho})\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu$ (rename $\rho \leftrightarrow \nu$ in one half). Hence
>
> $$
> g_{\alpha\nu}\ddot x^\nu + \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_\rho g_{\alpha\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\alpha\rho} - \partial_\alpha g_{\rho\nu}\bigr)\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu = \frac{\dot F}{F}\,g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\nu .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (raise the index). Multiply by the inverse metric $g^{\mu\alpha}$ and sum over $\alpha$; $g^{\mu\alpha}g_{\alpha\nu} = \delta^\mu_\nu$. The coefficient becomes $\tfrac12 g^{\mu\alpha}(\partial_\rho g_{\alpha\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\alpha\rho} - \partial_\alpha g_{\rho\nu}) = \Gamma^\mu_{\rho\nu}$, the Christoffel symbol of [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]:
>
> $$
> \ddot x^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\rho\nu}\,\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu = \frac{\dot F}{F}\,\dot x^\mu, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{D\dot x}{d\lambda} = \frac{\dot F}{F}\,\dot x .
> $$
>
> Every step can be reversed ($\varepsilon F \neq 0$, $g^{\mu\alpha}$ invertible), so this equation is equivalent to the Euler–Lagrange equations: the equation of part 1.
>
> **Step 7** (stationary $\Rightarrow$ geodesic). The equation of Step 6 has the form $D\dot x/d\lambda = h\,\dot x$ with $h = \dot F/F$ continuous. By [[#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 2, the parameter with $d\sigma/d\lambda = w = \exp\int h\,d\lambda = \exp(\ln F + \text{const}) = F/F(\lambda_A)$ is affine. Since $F = d\tau/d\lambda$, this is $\sigma = (\tau - \tau_A)/F(\lambda_A)$: up to a constant factor and shift, proper time. So the curve is a timelike geodesic.
>
> **Step 8** (geodesic $\Rightarrow$ stationary). Let $x(\lambda) = \gamma(\mu(\lambda))$ with $\gamma$ an affinely parametrized geodesic and $\mu' > 0$. By [[#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 1, $D\dot x/d\lambda = (\mu''/\mu')\,\dot x$. Also $\dot x = \mu'\,\gamma'$, so $F = \mu'\sqrt{\varepsilon\,g(\gamma', \gamma')}$, and $g(\gamma', \gamma')$ is constant along $\gamma$ by [[#^thm-c3-7-1|Theorem §C3.7.1]], part 2, applied to $Y = Z = \gamma'$, which is parallel along $\gamma$. Hence $F = c\,\mu'$ with $c > 0$ constant, $\dot F/F = \mu''/\mu'$, and the equation of Step 6 holds: the curve is stationary. Parts 1 and 3 are proved.
>
> **Step 9** (proper time as parameter). Choose $\lambda = \tau$. Then $F = d\tau/d\tau = 1$, $\dot F = 0$, and Step 6 is the geodesic equation of [[#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]] with $u^\mu = dx^\mu/d\tau$; $F = 1$ is $-g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = 1$, i.e. $u\cdot u = -1$. By [[#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 3, the other affine parameters are $a\tau + b$. This is part 2. For $\varepsilon = +1$ the same step gives arc length.
>
> **Step 10** (the flat case). For $g = \eta$ in inertial coordinates all $\Gamma = 0$, and part 2 reads $d^2x^\mu/d\tau^2 = 0$: uniform straight motion, as in [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). Part 4 then follows from parts 1–2 and [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The Christoffel combination $\tfrac12(\partial g + \partial g - \partial g)$ is not put in by hand: it is what the variation of $\sqrt{-g\dot x\dot x}$ produces. The connection that makes "straightest" equal to "stationary proper time" is forced to be the Levi-Civita one (d'Inverno & Vickers, (6.71)–(6.72)).
> - The right-hand side $(\dot F/F)\,\dot x$ is pure parametrization: the square-root action is reparametrization invariant ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^pr-b3-1-2|Principle §B3.1.2]]), so it cannot fix a parameter, and its Euler–Lagrange equations are the non-affine geodesic equation.
> - ⚑ The parameter that removes the right-hand side is proper time itself (Step 7): **the clock carried by a free body runs at an affine rate**. This is part 2 of the statement.

With the equivalence in hand, the free-fall principle of C1 can be stated without any chart, and extended to light, for which proper time is zero and no variational principle of the C1 kind exists.

> [!principle] Principle §C3.7.5: Free Test Bodies Follow Timelike Geodesics, Light Follows Null Geodesics
> In a spacetime $(M, g)$ with its Levi-Civita connection:
> 1. a test body on which no non-gravitational force acts moves on a **timelike geodesic** ([[#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]). Its proper time is an affine parameter, and its four-velocity $u = dx/d\tau$ obeys
>
> $$
> \frac{Du^\mu}{d\tau} = \frac{du^\mu}{d\tau} + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}u^\nu u^\rho = 0, \qquad g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = -1 ;
> $$
>
> 2. a light ray moves on a **null geodesic**: with an affine parameter $\lambda$ and tangent $k^\mu = dx^\mu/d\lambda$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dk^\mu}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}k^\nu k^\rho = 0, \qquad g_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Domain:* test bodies, as in [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]: negligible self-gravity, size small compared with the scale on which the curvature changes, no spin, no charge in an electromagnetic field (forces are added in C4). Light in the geometric-optics regime: wavelength small compared with the curvature scale and with the scale on which the amplitude changes.
>
> *Layer change.* For massive bodies this is [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]] in its equivalent geodesic form ([[#^thm-c3-7-4|Theorem §C3.7.4]], part 4), now stated on any spacetime. In coordinates with $\Gamma(p) = 0$ at an event, the local inertial frames of [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]], the equation at $p$ is $d^2x^\mu/d\tau^2 = 0$, the free particle of special relativity: the Einstein equivalence principle ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]) asks for exactly this, and a chart-independent equation that does it is the geodesic equation. For light, part 2 is that same extension of the special-relativistic $d^2x^\mu/d\lambda^2 = 0$; it does not follow from proper time, which vanishes on null curves. Both parts are derived later from the field equations of matter: part 1 from $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ for dust ([[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c4-4-5|Theorem §C4.4.5]]), part 2 from Maxwell's equations in the short-wavelength limit ([[§C4.6 Geometric Optics꞉ Light Follows Null Geodesics#^thm-c4-6-3|Theorem §C4.6.3]]). No mass or composition appears, so the weak equivalence principle ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-3|Principle §C1.3.3]]) is built in.
>
> *Model:* test bodies and light rays in a given metric; *applies:* any spacetime; in the static weak field it reproduces Newtonian free fall ([[#^ex-c3-7-3|Example §C3.7.3]]).
>

(7.9)–(7.10) (p. 174) · Couzens, GR1, §4.4.1 (p. 88: geodesics as free-fall curves) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.9, eqs. (6.69)–(6.70) (p. 103: null geodesics)*

^pr-c3-7-5

The square root in $F$ is awkward in practice and fails on null curves. Dropping it gives a Lagrangian that is quadratic in the velocities, works for every causal type, and selects the affine parametrization by itself.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.7.6: The Geodesic Lagrangian
> On a manifold with metric $g$ and its Levi-Civita connection, let
>
> $$
> L(x, \dot x) = g_{\mu\nu}(x)\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu, \qquad S_2[x] = \int_{\lambda_A}^{\lambda_B}L\,d\lambda ,
> $$
>
> varied with fixed endpoints and a fixed parameter interval.
> 1. **Equations.** The Euler–Lagrange equations of $S_2$ are $2g_{\alpha\nu}\bigl(\ddot x^\nu + \Gamma^\nu_{\rho\sigma}\dot x^\rho\dot x^\sigma\bigr) = 0$: exactly the affine geodesic equation ([[#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]), for timelike, null and spacelike curves alike.
> 2. **First integral.** Along every solution, $L = g(\dot x, \dot x)$ is constant.
> 3. **Same curves as proper time.** A timelike solution, rescaled to $L = -1$, is a stationary curve of proper time parametrized by $\tau$ ([[#^thm-c3-7-4|Theorem §C3.7.4]]); conversely every stationary curve of proper time, parametrized by $\tau$, solves the equations of $S_2$. The square-root functional fixes the curve only; $S_2$ fixes the curve and an affine parameter.
> 4. **Method for the Christoffel symbols.** Write the Euler–Lagrange equations of $L$ for a given metric, solve each for $\ddot x^\mu$, and compare with $\ddot x^\mu = -\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho$: the coefficient of $\dot x^\nu\dot x^\nu$ (no sum) is $-\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\nu}$, the coefficient of $\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho$ with $\nu \neq \rho$ is $-2\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}$.
> 5. **⚑ Conserved momenta.** If no component $g_{\mu\nu}$ depends on the coordinate $x^\alpha$, then
>
> $$
> p_\alpha \equiv g_{\alpha\nu}\,\dot x^\nu = \dot x_\alpha
> $$
>
> (the lowered velocity component, [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]) is constant along every affinely parametrized geodesic. The energy $E = -g_{00}\,dt/d\tau$ of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-5|Theorem §C1.5.5]] is $-p_0$ for the static weak field; the coordinate-free form is the Killing-vector theorem [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-2|Theorem §C3.13.2]].
>
> *Model:* metric with Levi-Civita connection; *applies:* exactly; parts 1, 2, 4, 5 for every causal type.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.4, eqs. (4.116)–(4.118) (pp. 96–97); §6.2, eqs. (6.30)–(6.33) (pp. 119–120; Schwarzschild: the square-root Lagrangian replaced by its square for an affine parameter, $g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu = \epsilon$, conserved $E$ and $l$) · Hartle §8.1, Example 8.3, eq. (8.17) (p. 173), Example 8.4, eq. (8.20) (p. 174) · d'Inverno & Vickers §7.6, eqs. (7.40)–(7.45) (pp. 126–127; "work where possible with $L^2$ rather than $L$")*

^thm-c3-7-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (partial derivatives). $\partial L/\partial\dot x^\alpha = g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\nu + g_{\mu\alpha}\dot x^\mu = 2g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\nu$ and $\partial L/\partial x^\alpha = \partial_\alpha g_{\rho\nu}\,\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu$.
>
> **Step 2** (Euler–Lagrange, [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|CM Theorem §B5.1.4]]). With the product rule,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\lambda}\bigl(2g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\nu\bigr) - \partial_\alpha g_{\rho\nu}\,\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu = 2g_{\alpha\nu}\ddot x^\nu + 2\,\partial_\rho g_{\alpha\nu}\,\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu - \partial_\alpha g_{\rho\nu}\,\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu .
> $$
>
> Symmetrizing $\partial_\rho g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu$ as in Step 5 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c3-7-4|Theorem §C3.7.4]] and factoring out $2$,
>
> $$
> = 2\Bigl[g_{\alpha\nu}\ddot x^\nu + \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_\rho g_{\alpha\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\alpha\rho} - \partial_\alpha g_{\rho\nu}\bigr)\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu\Bigr] = 2g_{\alpha\mu}\bigl(\ddot x^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\rho\nu}\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> using $\tfrac12(\dots) = g_{\alpha\mu}\Gamma^\mu_{\rho\nu}$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]], lowered with $g_{\alpha\mu}$). Since $g_{\alpha\mu}$ is invertible, the Euler–Lagrange equations hold exactly when the geodesic equation holds. Nothing was divided by $L$, so $L = 0$ (null curves) is allowed. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (constancy of $L$). A solution is an affinely parametrized geodesic (Step 2), so $\dot x$ is parallel along it ([[#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]). [[#^thm-c3-7-1|Theorem §C3.7.1]], part 2, with $Y = Z = \dot x$ gives $\frac{d}{d\lambda}g(\dot x, \dot x) = 0$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (relation to proper time). Let $x$ solve part 1 and be timelike: $L = -c^2$ with $c > 0$ constant (Step 3). Rescaling $\lambda \to c\lambda$ ([[#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 3) makes $L = -1$, so $F = \sqrt{-L} = 1$, $\lambda = \tau$ up to a constant, $\dot F = 0$, and the equation of [[#^thm-c3-7-4|Theorem §C3.7.4]], part 1, holds: the curve is stationary for $\tau$. Conversely, a stationary curve of $\tau$ parametrized by $\tau$ obeys the affine geodesic equation (Theorem §C3.7.4, part 2), hence solves part 1. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (reading off $\Gamma$). In $\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho$ a pair $\nu \neq \rho$ appears twice, as $\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho$ and $\Gamma^\mu_{\rho\nu}\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu$, which are equal because $\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho} = \Gamma^\mu_{\rho\nu}$ (Levi-Civita); a pair $\nu = \rho$ appears once. Matching coefficients of the monomials $\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho$ gives part 4.
>
> **Step 6** (cyclic coordinates). If $\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\nu} = 0$, then $\partial L/\partial x^\alpha = 0$ and, by [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-5|CM Theorem §B5.1.5]], $\partial L/\partial\dot x^\alpha = 2g_{\alpha\nu}\dot x^\nu$ is constant along solutions. Dividing by $2$ gives part 5. For the static weak field ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]) $t$ is cyclic and $p_0 = g_{00}\,dt/d\tau = -(1 + 2\Phi)\,dt/d\tau = -E$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** $L$ and $F = \sqrt{\mp L}$ have the same stationary curves because along a geodesic $L$ is constant, so differentiating $\sqrt{\mp L}$ only multiplies the equations by the constant $1/(2F)$. In practice $L$ is the quickest route to both the $\Gamma$'s and the conserved quantities, and it is the only one of the two that works for light.

Step 3 above used a fact that deserves its own statement, because it is what makes the causal labels of [[#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]] and the normalization $u\cdot u = -1$ consistent.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.7.7: g(ẋ, ẋ) Is Conserved; Causal Character Is Preserved
> Let $\gamma$ be an affinely parametrized geodesic of the Levi-Civita connection (any metric-compatible connection suffices).
> 1. $g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)$ is constant along $\gamma$.
> 2. On a Lorentzian manifold a geodesic is timelike everywhere, null everywhere or spacelike everywhere; the type does not depend on the parametrization.
> 3. For a timelike geodesic the normalization $g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma) = -1$ can be imposed at one point and then holds everywhere; with it $\lambda = \tau + \text{const}$. For a spacelike or Riemannian geodesic, $g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma) = 1$ makes $\lambda$ the arc length.
> 4. If $Y$ is parallel along $\gamma$, then $g(\dot\gamma, Y)$ is constant: a parallel-transported vector keeps a fixed angle with the tangent of a geodesic.
>
> *Model:* metric with a metric-compatible connection; *applies:* exactly. For a connection that is not metric-compatible, $g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)$ can change along a geodesic and a geodesic can change its causal type (GR1 p. 89).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.1, eq. (4.91) and the paragraph after it (p. 89) · Hartle §8.1, (8.15) with $u\cdot u = -1$ · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.9, eqs. (6.68), (6.70) (p. 103)*

^thm-c3-7-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1.** $\dot\gamma$ is parallel along $\gamma$ ([[#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]). [[#^thm-c3-7-1|Theorem §C3.7.1]], part 2, with $Y = Z = \dot\gamma$ gives $\frac{d}{d\lambda}g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma) = 0$: part 1. With $Y$ parallel and $Z = \dot\gamma$ it gives part 4.
>
> **Step 2.** A constant cannot change sign, so the type ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]) is the same at every point. Under $\lambda = \lambda(\sigma)$ with $f = d\lambda/d\sigma > 0$ the velocity becomes $f\dot\gamma$ ([[#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 1) and $g(f\dot\gamma, f\dot\gamma) = f^2\,g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)$ has the same sign: part 2.
>
> **Step 3.** If $g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma) = -c^2 < 0$, the affine parameter $\sigma = c\lambda$ ([[#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 3) has $g(\gamma', \gamma') = -1$ by part 1 at every point. Then $d\tau/d\sigma = \sqrt{-g(\gamma', \gamma')} = 1$, so $\sigma = \tau + \text{const}$. The spacelike and Riemannian cases are the same with $+1$ and arc length. This is part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The norm of the velocity is conserved for the same reason as any transported inner product; nothing special about geodesics enters except that the velocity is itself transported. The physical normalization $u\cdot u = -1$ of a free body is therefore a single initial condition, not an extra equation.

> [!caution] Caution: Extremal, not always maximal
> [[#^thm-c3-7-4|Theorem §C3.7.4]] says that geodesics make proper time or length **stationary**. Whether the stationary value is a maximum, a minimum or neither is a separate question, with different answers in different cases (d'Inverno & Vickers §6.9, p. 102: "the interval may be a maximum, a minimum, or a saddle point").
> - **Timelike, flat.** In Minkowski spacetime the straight worldline is the longest between its endpoints ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-5|Theorem §B1.3.5]]).
> - **Timelike, curved.** No timelike curve can be a minimum: jagged null curves approximate it arbitrarily closely with zero proper time (GR1, p. 89, Figure 11). A sufficiently short timelike geodesic is a local maximum among nearby timelike curves. Globally this fails: in the Earth's field the circular orbit and a vertical throw are both geodesics between the same two events, and the throw ages more ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^ex-c1-5-2|Example §C1.5.2]]). The precise condition (no conjugate points) belongs to the theory of geodesic deviation ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^rem-c3-12-1|§C3.12, Remark: Focusing and conjugate points]]) and is not proved here.
> - **Riemannian.** Short geodesics **minimize** length. On the sphere the short great-circle arc between two points is the shortest curve, while the long arc is a **saddle**: one variation shortens it, another lengthens it ([[#^ex-c3-7-2|Example §C3.7.2]], part (d)).
> - **The source.** GR1 (p. 89) says of the short and the long arc that "both maximise locally the length functional". Neither does: the short arc is a local (and global) minimum, the long arc is neither a maximum nor a minimum. The surrounding statement for timelike geodesics, "maximises the proper time locally", is the correct one in Lorentzian signature; the sign of the comparison is reversed between proper time and Riemannian length.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.1, p. 89 · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.9, p. 102 · Hartle §8.1 ("extremizes") · numbers in Example §C3.7.2 checked by script*

The geodesic equation and its Lagrangian are the working tools of the rest of the course: the orbits of planets and light in C6, the infall into black holes in C7. Before the examples, one more question remains open. Parallel transport around a closed loop on the sphere does not return a vector to itself ([[#^ex-c3-7-4|Example §C3.7.4]]). Which tensor measures this failure is the question of [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor|§C3.9]]. First, [[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates|§C3.8]] uses the geodesics leaving one point as coordinate lines.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C3.7.1: Straight Lines in Polar Coordinates
> *Setup.* The Euclidean plane in polar coordinates, $ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\varphi^2$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-1|Example §C3.1.1]]), Riemannian; curves parametrized by arc length $s$, a dot is $d/ds$. The geodesics must be the straight lines; the point is to see the equations produce them in a chart where straight lines do not have constant coordinate velocity.
>
> *(a) The equations, by the geodesic Lagrangian* ([[#^thm-c3-7-6|Theorem §C3.7.6]]). $L = \dot r^2 + r^2\dot\varphi^2$, so $\partial L/\partial\dot r = 2\dot r$, $\partial L/\partial r = 2r\dot\varphi^2$, $\partial L/\partial\dot\varphi = 2r^2\dot\varphi$, $\partial L/\partial\varphi = 0$. The Euler–Lagrange equations are
>
> $$
> 2\ddot r - 2r\dot\varphi^2 = 0, \qquad \frac{d}{ds}\bigl(2r^2\dot\varphi\bigr) = 2r^2\ddot\varphi + 4r\dot r\dot\varphi = 0 ,
> $$
>
> that is
>
> $$
> \ddot r - r\dot\varphi^2 = 0, \qquad \ddot\varphi + \frac2r\,\dot r\dot\varphi = 0 .
> $$
>
> By the reading rule of [[#^thm-c3-7-6|Theorem §C3.7.6]], part 4: $\Gamma^r_{\varphi\varphi} = -r$ (diagonal pair, coefficient $-r$), $2\Gamma^\varphi_{r\varphi} = 2/r$, so $\Gamma^\varphi_{r\varphi} = \Gamma^\varphi_{\varphi r} = 1/r$; all others vanish. These are the symbols of [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^ex-c3-6-1|Example §C3.6.1]].
>
> *(b) Two first integrals.* $\varphi$ is cyclic, so $r^2\dot\varphi = \ell$ is constant ([[#^thm-c3-7-6|Theorem §C3.7.6]], part 5). Arc length means $L = \dot r^2 + r^2\dot\varphi^2 = 1$ ([[#^thm-c3-7-7|Theorem §C3.7.7]], part 3). Eliminating $\dot\varphi = \ell/r^2$,
>
> $$
> \dot r^2 + \frac{\ell^2}{r^2} = 1 .
> $$
>
> *(c) Solve.* For $\ell = 0$, $\dot\varphi = 0$ and $\dot r = \pm 1$: the radial lines. For $\ell = b > 0$ shift $s$ so that $\dot r(0) = 0$; then $r(0) = b$, and $r(s) = \sqrt{b^2 + s^2}$ solves the energy equation, since $\dot r = s/\sqrt{b^2 + s^2}$ gives $\dot r^2 = s^2/(b^2 + s^2) = 1 - b^2/r^2$. Then
>
> $$
> \dot\varphi = \frac{b}{r^2} = \frac{b}{b^2 + s^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \varphi(s) = \varphi_0 + \arctan\frac sb .
> $$
>
> *(d) It is a straight line.* With $\cos(\arctan u) = 1/\sqrt{1 + u^2}$,
>
> $$
> r\cos(\varphi - \varphi_0) = \sqrt{b^2 + s^2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + s^2/b^2}} = \sqrt{b^2 + s^2}\cdot\frac{b}{\sqrt{b^2 + s^2}} = b ,
> $$
>
> the line at distance $b$ from the origin, perpendicular to the direction $\varphi_0$. Its coordinate acceleration $\ddot r = \frac{d}{ds}\frac{s}{r} = \frac1r - \frac{s^2}{r^3} = \frac{b^2}{r^3}$ is not zero, and it equals $r\dot\varphi^2 = r\,b^2/r^4$: the first equation of (a) holds. For $b = 1$ this is the curve of the opening.
>
> *(e) The spiral fails.* For $r = 1 + s$, $\varphi = s$ (zero coordinate acceleration), the covariant acceleration ([[#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]) is
>
> $$
> \frac{D\dot x^r}{ds} = 0 - (1 + s)\cdot 1 = -(1 + s), \qquad \frac{D\dot x^\varphi}{ds} = 0 + \frac{2}{1 + s}\cdot 1\cdot 1 = \frac{2}{1 + s} ,
> $$
>
> nonzero: the spiral turns, and the connection terms say by how much.
>
> *Source: Hartle §8.1, Examples 8.1 and 8.3, eqs. (8.2)–(8.6), (8.17) (pp. 170–173) · Couzens, GR1, §4.4.4, (4.116)–(4.118) (the same method on the sphere) · solutions checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.7.2: Great Circles on the Sphere
> *Setup.* The round sphere of radius $a$, $ds^2 = a^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-2|Example §C3.1.2]]), away from the poles; arc length $s$.
>
> *(a) Christoffel symbols from the Lagrangian.* $L = a^2(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2)$. The $\theta$ equation: $\frac{d}{ds}(2a^2\dot\theta) - 2a^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\varphi^2 = 0$. The $\varphi$ equation: $\frac{d}{ds}(2a^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi) = 2a^2(\sin^2\theta\,\ddot\varphi + 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\dot\varphi) = 0$. Dividing,
>
> $$
> \ddot\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\varphi^2 = 0, \qquad \ddot\varphi + 2\cot\theta\,\dot\theta\dot\varphi = 0 ,
> $$
>
> so $\Gamma^\theta_{\varphi\varphi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta$, $\Gamma^\varphi_{\theta\varphi} = \Gamma^\varphi_{\varphi\theta} = \cot\theta$, the rest zero, as in [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^ex-c3-6-2|Example §C3.6.2]] and GR1 (4.115). The constant $a^2$ drops out.
>
> *(b) The equator is a geodesic, and so is every great circle.* For $\theta = \pi/2$, $\varphi = s/a$: $\ddot\theta = 0$ and $\sin\frac\pi2\cos\frac\pi2 = 0$, so the first equation holds; $\ddot\varphi = 0$ and $\dot\theta = 0$, so the second holds. A rotation $R$ of $\mathbf R^3$ maps the sphere to itself and pulls the Euclidean metric back to itself, so in the rotated chart $(\theta\circ R^{-1}, \varphi\circ R^{-1})$ the metric components, the Christoffel symbols and the geodesic equations are the same functions. The image of the equator under $R$ is therefore a geodesic, and every great circle is such an image. Conversely, for any point $p$ and unit vector $v$ the great circle through $p$ in the direction $v$ is a geodesic with these initial data, so by uniqueness ([[#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]], part 3) **every geodesic of the sphere is a great circle** (Hartle Prob. 8.2; checked numerically for a generic initial condition).
>
> *(c) A latitude is not.* For $\theta = \theta_0 \in (0, \pi/2)$, $\varphi = s/(a\sin\theta_0)$ (unit speed):
>
> $$
> \frac{D\dot x^\theta}{ds} = 0 + \Gamma^\theta_{\varphi\varphi}\,\dot\varphi^2 = -\sin\theta_0\cos\theta_0\cdot\frac{1}{a^2\sin^2\theta_0} = -\frac{\cot\theta_0}{a^2}, \qquad \frac{D\dot x^\varphi}{ds} = 0 + 2\cot\theta_0\cdot 0\cdot\dot\varphi = 0 .
> $$
>
> Its length is $\sqrt{g_{\theta\theta}}\,|D\dot x^\theta/ds| = a\cdot\cot\theta_0/a^2 = \cot\theta_0/a$, pointing toward smaller $\theta$, toward the nearer pole: walking east along a northern latitude one must keep turning left (north). For $\theta_0 = \pi/3$ this is $0.577/a$; at the equator it is $0$.
>
> *(d) Short arcs are minima, long arcs are saddles* (unit sphere, $a = 1$). Take two points on the equator a quarter turn apart. The short arc has length $L = \pi/2$, the long arc the other way round $L = 3\pi/2$; both are geodesics by (b). Vary either, with the base arc written $\theta = \pi/2$, $\varphi = s$ (or $-s$), $s \in [0, L]$, by
>
> $$
> \theta = \tfrac\pi2 + \eta(s), \qquad \eta(s) = \epsilon\sin\frac{k\pi s}{L}, \qquad k = 1, 2, \dots ,
> $$
>
> which keeps the endpoints. The length is $\int_0^L\sqrt{\eta'^2 + \sin^2(\frac\pi2 + \eta)}\,ds = \int_0^L\sqrt{\eta'^2 + \cos^2\eta}\,ds$. With $\cos^2\eta = 1 - \eta^2 + O(\eta^4)$ and $\sqrt{1 + u} = 1 + \tfrac12u + O(u^2)$,
>
> $$
> \text{length} = L + \tfrac12\int_0^L\bigl(\eta'^2 - \eta^2\bigr)ds + O(\epsilon^4) .
> $$
>
> The two integrals are $\int_0^L\eta'^2\,ds = \epsilon^2\bigl(\frac{k\pi}{L}\bigr)^2\int_0^L\cos^2\frac{k\pi s}{L}\,ds = \epsilon^2\bigl(\frac{k\pi}{L}\bigr)^2\frac L2$ and $\int_0^L\eta^2\,ds = \epsilon^2\,\frac L2$, so
>
> $$
> \Delta L = \frac{\epsilon^2L}{4}\Bigl(\frac{k^2\pi^2}{L^2} - 1\Bigr) + O(\epsilon^4) .
> $$
>
> For $L = \pi/2$ the bracket is $4k^2 - 1 > 0$ for every $k$: every such variation lengthens the short arc. For $L = 3\pi/2$ and $k = 1$ the bracket is $\frac49 - 1 < 0$: the long arc can be **shortened**; for $k = 3$ it is $4 - 1 > 0$: it can be lengthened. With $\epsilon = 0.05$:
>
> | arc | $k$ | $\Delta L$, formula | $\Delta L$, exact integral |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | short, $L = \pi/2$ | $1$ | $+2.945\times10^{-3}$ | $+2.939\times10^{-3}$ |
> | long, $L = 3\pi/2$ | $1$ | $-1.636\times10^{-3}$ | $-1.636\times10^{-3}$ |
> | long, $L = 3\pi/2$ | $3$ | $+8.836\times10^{-3}$ | $+8.818\times10^{-3}$ |
>
> The long arc is stationary ([[#^thm-c3-7-4|Theorem §C3.7.4]], part 3) but neither a minimum nor a maximum. The $k = 1$ bracket changes sign at $L = \pi$, the length at which the arc reaches the antipode of its starting point: arcs longer than half a great circle stop being minima. The $-\eta^2$ term, which does it, comes from $\sin^2\theta$ in the metric; in the plane it is absent and every straight segment is a minimum ([[#^cau-c3-7-1|Caution: Extremal, not always maximal]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.4, eqs. (4.113)–(4.118) (pp. 96–97); §4.4.1, p. 89 (short and long arcs) · Hartle, Problem 8.2 · Schutz §6.4, Fig. 6.1 (p. 153) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.7.3: Geodesics of the Weak-Field Metric Reproduce Newton
> *Setup.* The static weak-field metric $ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi)dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)\delta_{ij}dx^idx^j$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]), to first order in $\Phi$; any affinely parametrized geodesic, a dot is $d/d\lambda$; $v^i = dx^i/dt$, $a^i = d^2x^i/dt^2$, $v\cdot\nabla\Phi = v^j\partial_j\Phi$. [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]] obtained Newton's law by varying proper time; here the same result comes from the geodesic equation, as in Schutz §7.2.
>
> *Christoffel symbols* ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^ex-c3-6-3|Example §C3.6.3]]; recomputed by script). The metric is diagonal and independent of $t$. For instance $\Gamma^i_{00} = \tfrac12 g^{ii}(\partial_0g_{i0} + \partial_0g_{i0} - \partial_ig_{00}) = \tfrac12(1 - 2\Phi)^{-1}\,\partial_i(1 + 2\Phi) = \partial_i\Phi/(1 - 2\Phi)$. In all,
>
> $$
> \Gamma^0_{0i} = \Gamma^0_{i0} = \frac{\partial_i\Phi}{1 + 2\Phi}, \qquad \Gamma^i_{00} = \partial_i\Phi + O(\Phi^2), \qquad \Gamma^i_{jk} = -\bigl(\delta_{ik}\partial_j\Phi + \delta_{ij}\partial_k\Phi - \delta_{jk}\partial_i\Phi\bigr) + O(\Phi^2) ,
> $$
>
> and $\Gamma^0_{00} = \Gamma^0_{ij} = \Gamma^i_{0j} = 0$.
>
> *(a) Time component: energy.* The $\mu = 0$ equation is $\ddot t + 2\Gamma^0_{0i}\,\dot t\,\dot x^i = 0$, i.e. $\ddot t = -2\dot t\,\dot x^i\partial_i\Phi/(1 + 2\Phi)$. Then, exactly,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\lambda}\bigl[(1 + 2\Phi)\,\dot t\bigr] = 2\,\dot x^i\partial_i\Phi\;\dot t + (1 + 2\Phi)\,\ddot t = 2\dot t\,\dot x^i\partial_i\Phi - 2\dot t\,\dot x^i\partial_i\Phi = 0 :
> $$
>
> $E = (1 + 2\Phi)\,dt/d\tau$ is conserved, [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-5|Theorem §C1.5.5]], here as $-p_0$ of [[#^thm-c3-7-6|Theorem §C3.7.6]], part 5.
>
> *(b) Space components: coordinate acceleration.* Change from $\lambda$ to $t$: $\dot x^i = \dot t\,v^i$ and $\ddot x^i = \ddot t\,v^i + \dot t^2a^i$. Write $u^\mu = (1, v^i)$, so $\dot x^\mu = \dot t\,u^\mu$. The geodesic equations give $\ddot x^i = -\dot t^2\,\Gamma^i_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu$ and $\ddot t = -\dot t^2\,\Gamma^0_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu$, hence
>
> $$
> a^i = -\Gamma^i_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu + \bigl(\Gamma^0_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu\bigr)v^i .
> $$
>
> The two contractions, term by term:
>
> $$
> \Gamma^i_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = \Gamma^i_{00} + 2\Gamma^i_{0j}v^j + \Gamma^i_{jk}v^jv^k = \partial_i\Phi + 0 - \bigl(v^i\,v\cdot\nabla\Phi + v^i\,v\cdot\nabla\Phi - v^2\partial_i\Phi\bigr), \qquad \Gamma^0_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = 2\Gamma^0_{0j}v^j = 2\,v\cdot\nabla\Phi .
> $$
>
> So, to first order in $\Phi$ and for any speed,
>
> $$
> a^i = -(1 + v^2)\,\partial_i\Phi + 4\,(v\cdot\nabla\Phi)\,v^i .
> $$
>
> *(c) Slow bodies.* With $v^2 = O(\varepsilon)$, $a^i = -\partial_i\Phi\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)]$: Newton's law, part 3 of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]]. At leading order only $\Gamma^i_{00} = \partial_i\Phi$, which comes from $g_{00}$ alone, contributes (Schutz (7.18)–(7.24)). Restored: $\ddot{\vec x} = -\nabla\Phi$.
>
> *(d) Light.* For a ray moving along $x$ ($v = (1, 0, 0)$, $v^2 = 1$), the transverse components are $a^y = -2\,\partial_y\Phi$, $a^z = -2\,\partial_z\Phi$, since $v^y = v^z = 0$ removes the last term: twice the pull on a slow body. This is the factor $2$ of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]], part 3, half from $g_{00}$ and half from $g_{ij}$, behind the light deflection of [[§C6.5 The Bending of Light#^thm-c6-5-3|Theorem §C6.5.3]]. Like every coordinate acceleration, $a^i$ refers to the coordinates of the model ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^cau-c1-5-2|§C1.5, Caution: Which parts of the metric are coordinate statements]]).
>
> *Source: Schutz §7.2, eqs. (7.9)–(7.24) (pp. 174–176) · Hartle §8.1, Table 8.1 (p. 170) · formulas checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C3.7.4: Parallel Transport around a Latitude
> *Setup.* The sphere of radius $a$ as in [[#^ex-c3-7-2|Example §C3.7.2]]; the latitude $\theta = \theta_0$, parametrized by $\varphi \in [0, 2\pi]$, so $\dot x^\theta = 0$, $\dot x^\varphi = 1$. It is not a geodesic (Example §C3.7.2 (c)), but any curve can carry a vector.
>
> *(a) Transport equations* ([[#^def-c3-7-1|Def. §C3.7.1]]). With the symbols of Example §C3.7.2 (a) and $\dot x = (0, 1)$:
>
> $$
> \frac{dY^\theta}{d\varphi} + \Gamma^\theta_{\varphi\varphi}Y^\varphi = \frac{dY^\theta}{d\varphi} - \sin\theta_0\cos\theta_0\,Y^\varphi = 0, \qquad \frac{dY^\varphi}{d\varphi} + \Gamma^\varphi_{\varphi\theta}Y^\theta = \frac{dY^\varphi}{d\varphi} + \cot\theta_0\,Y^\theta = 0 .
> $$
>
> *(b) Orthonormal components.* In the unit vectors $\hat\theta = \partial_\theta/a$, $\hat\varphi = \partial_\varphi/(a\sin\theta_0)$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]) the components are $Y^{\hat\theta} = aY^\theta$, $Y^{\hat\varphi} = a\sin\theta_0\,Y^\varphi$. Multiplying the two equations by $a$ and by $a\sin\theta_0$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dY^{\hat\theta}}{d\varphi} = \cos\theta_0\,Y^{\hat\varphi}, \qquad \frac{dY^{\hat\varphi}}{d\varphi} = -\cos\theta_0\,Y^{\hat\theta} .
> $$
>
> *(c) Solution.* Differentiating the first and inserting the second, $d^2Y^{\hat\theta}/d\varphi^2 = -\cos^2\theta_0\,Y^{\hat\theta}$. With initial values $(Y^{\hat\theta}_0, Y^{\hat\varphi}_0)$,
>
> $$
> Y^{\hat\theta}(\varphi) = Y^{\hat\theta}_0\cos(\varphi\cos\theta_0) + Y^{\hat\varphi}_0\sin(\varphi\cos\theta_0), \qquad Y^{\hat\varphi}(\varphi) = -Y^{\hat\theta}_0\sin(\varphi\cos\theta_0) + Y^{\hat\varphi}_0\cos(\varphi\cos\theta_0)
> $$
>
> (check: $dY^{\hat\theta}/d\varphi = \cos\theta_0\,[-Y^{\hat\theta}_0\sin + Y^{\hat\varphi}_0\cos] = \cos\theta_0\,Y^{\hat\varphi}$). Measured from $\hat\theta$ toward $\hat\varphi$ (counterclockwise seen from outside, since $\hat\theta\times\hat\varphi$ points outward), $Y$ turns by the angle $\psi = -\varphi\cos\theta_0$ relative to the frame $(\hat\theta, \hat\varphi)$, and its length $\bigl(Y^{\hat\theta}\bigr)^2 + \bigl(Y^{\hat\varphi}\bigr)^2$ is constant ([[#^thm-c3-7-1|Theorem §C3.7.1]], part 2). This is GR1 (4.136).
>
> *(d) Once around.* At $\varphi = 2\pi$ the frame $(\hat\theta, \hat\varphi)$ is back to itself, and $Y$ has turned by $-2\pi\cos\theta_0$, the same as $2\pi(1 - \cos\theta_0)$ counterclockwise. For $\theta_0 = \pi/3$: $-\pi$, the vector comes back reversed ($\hat\theta \mapsto -\hat\theta$, figure below). For $\theta_0 = \pi/4$: $-254.56^\circ \equiv +105.44^\circ$. For $\theta_0 = \pi/6$: $-311.77^\circ \equiv +48.23^\circ$. For the equator, $0$: the equator is a geodesic. In each case the counterclockwise angle $2\pi(1 - \cos\theta_0)$ equals the area of the cap $\theta < \theta_0$ divided by $a^2$ ($\pi$, $1.8403$ and $0.8418$ rad); the general statement is [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^ex-c3-9-2|Example §C3.9.2]]. Parallel transport around a closed loop is path-dependent: the transport map of the closed curve is a rotation, not the identity.
>
> *(e) A geodesic triangle, for comparison.* Let $A$, $B$ be points on the equator a quarter turn apart and $N$ the north pole, and go $A \to B \to N \to A$ along great circles (one octant, area $4\pi a^2/8$). Along each leg the angle between $Y$ and the leg's tangent is constant ([[#^thm-c3-7-7|Theorem §C3.7.7]], part 4). Start at $A$ with $Y$ pointing east, along the first leg: on $A \to B$ it stays along the tangent; at $B$ the second leg heads north, so $Y$ (pointing west) is perpendicular to it and stays so up to $N$; there the third leg heads back toward $A$ in the direction opposite to $Y$, and $Y$ stays antiparallel to the tangent down to $A$, where the tangent points south. So $Y$ returns pointing **north**: turned by $+\pi/2$, the octant's area divided by $a^2$ (Schutz Fig. 6.3; the animation integrates the transport equation and finds the same).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.4.4, eqs. (4.124)–(4.136), (4.142) and p. 100 (the triangle limit $\theta_0 = \pi/2$, $\omega = \pi/2$, $\theta_1 = 0$) · Schutz §6.4, Figs. 6.1–6.3 (pp. 153–154) · numbers checked by script*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - **Layer change of the free-fall law.** In flat spacetime the free worldline is straight and of longest proper time ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-5|Theorem §B1.3.5]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). In C1 it became the principle of stationary proper time in one chart ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]). Here [[#^thm-c3-7-4|Theorem §C3.7.4]] identifies those worldlines with the timelike geodesics, and the principle is restated as the geodesic postulate ([[#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]), derived from $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ for dust in [[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c4-4-5|Theorem §C4.4.5]] (and, for light, from Maxwell's equations in [[§C4.6 Geometric Optics꞉ Light Follows Null Geodesics#^thm-c4-6-3|Theorem §C4.6.3]]).
> - **Newtonian limit twice.** [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]] varies proper time; [[#^ex-c3-7-3|Example §C3.7.3]] solves the geodesic equation; both give $\ddot{\vec x} = -\nabla\Phi$, and the second also gives the factor $2$ for light.
> - **Variational tools.** The derivations use the Euler–Lagrange equations for several functions and cyclic variables ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|CM Theorem §B5.1.4]], [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-5|CM Theorem §B5.1.5]]). The square-root action is the reparametrization-invariant action of [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]] with $\eta$ replaced by $g$.
> - **Mathematics.** Parallel transport is a linear ODE along the curve ([[§33 Introduction to Systems of First-Order Linear Equations#^thm-33-3|ODE Theorem §33.3]]); the geodesic equation is a nonlinear one, an integral curve on the tangent bundle ([[§68 Local Existence and Uniqueness#^thm-68-2|591 Thm. §68.2]]).
> - **Forward.** Geodesics from one point give normal coordinates ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates|§C3.8]]); the rotation of [[#^ex-c3-7-4|Example §C3.7.4]] is holonomy, measured by the Riemann tensor ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor|§C3.9]]); neighbouring geodesics and tides are [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation|§C3.12]]; the conserved momenta of [[#^thm-c3-7-6|Theorem §C3.7.6]], part 5, become Killing-vector charges ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-2|Theorem §C3.13.2]]). A conformal rescaling of the metric keeps the null geodesics as unparametrised curves but not the timelike ones: [[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^thm-c10-1-2|Theorem §C10.1.2]].
> - **Conventions.** Index order of $\Gamma$ in the transport equation: [[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^cau-c3-5-1|§C3.5, Caution: Index order of Γ in the sources]]; register: [[General Relativity Conventions]], row "Connection".
