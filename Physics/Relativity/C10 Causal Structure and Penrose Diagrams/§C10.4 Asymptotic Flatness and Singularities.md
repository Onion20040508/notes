The Penrose diagrams of [[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space|§C10.1]]–[[§C10.3 Penrose Diagrams of Kruskal and of Collapse|§C10.3]] have edges of two kinds. One kind is infinity: the null lines $\mathscr I^\pm$ and the points $i^\pm$, $i^0$ ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-3|Def. §C10.1.3]]), where light rays and observers arrive after infinite affine parameter. The other is the jagged line $r = 0$ of the Kruskal diagram ([[§C10.3 Penrose Diagrams of Kruskal and of Collapse#^thm-c10-3-2|Theorem §C10.3.2]]), where infalling observers arrive after finite proper time ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]]). Neither edge is a set of points of the spacetime: the diagram adds them. The black hole of [[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons|§C10.7]] will be defined as the region from which no signal reaches $\mathscr I^+$, and a naked singularity as one that can be seen from there. So the section asks: **what does it mean, without coordinates, that a spacetime looks like Minkowski space far away, and that it has a singularity?**

First the naive answers, computed. *Infinity.* "Asymptotically flat" might mean "$g_{\mu\nu} \to \eta_{\mu\nu}$ as $r \to \infty$". Write Minkowski space in a rotating chart, $\varphi = \varphi_r + \omega t$ in cylindrical coordinates $(t, \rho, \varphi, z)$:

$$
ds^2 = -dt^2 + d\rho^2 + \rho^2\bigl(d\varphi_r + \omega\,dt\bigr)^2 + dz^2 = -\bigl(1 - \omega^2\rho^2\bigr)dt^2 + 2\omega\rho^2\,dt\,d\varphi_r + d\rho^2 + \rho^2d\varphi_r^2 + dz^2 .
$$

Its Riemann tensor vanishes (checked by script), yet $g_{tt} = -(1 - \omega^2\rho^2) \to +\infty$ as $\rho \to \infty$. The test fails for flat space itself in an admissible chart, so it has to say "in suitable coordinates", and then which coordinates and which rates of approach become the whole problem. *Singularities.* "A singularity is where a curvature invariant blows up" ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]], part 2) misses the apex of a cone, where every invariant is zero ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^ex-c7-1-3|Example §C7.1.3]]). The opposite test, "a singularity is where a freely falling worldline ends", fires on Minkowski space with one point removed: a flat spacetime in which the clock at rest at $x = 0$ stops at $t = 0$ ([[#^ex-c10-4-2|Example §C10.4.2]]). Neither test alone is right.

The way out has two halves. Infinity is replaced by a boundary attached at finite distance after a conformal rescaling ([[#^def-c10-4-1|Def. §C10.4.1]]), and "looks like Minkowski far away" becomes conditions on that boundary ([[#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]], [[#^def-c10-4-3|Def. §C10.4.3]]); Schwarzschild passes them ([[#^ex-c10-4-3|Example §C10.4.3]]). For singularities, the chart-based classification ([[#^def-c10-4-4|Def. §C10.4.4]]) is replaced by curves that end: endpoints ([[#^def-c10-4-5|Def. §C10.4.5]]), complete geodesics ([[#^def-c10-4-6|Def. §C10.4.6]]) and extensions ([[#^def-c10-4-7|Def. §C10.4.7]]) combine into the singular spacetime ([[#^def-c10-4-8|Def. §C10.4.8]]). Kruskal is singular ([[#^thm-c10-4-1|Theorem §C10.4.1]]); Rindler is not ([[#^thm-c10-4-2|Theorem §C10.4.2]]); and every extendible spacetime has an incomplete geodesic ([[#^thm-c10-4-3|Theorem §C10.4.3]]), which is why both conditions are needed.

**Setup.** A **spacetime** is a connected ([[§15 Connected Spaces#^def-15-2|590 Def. §15.2]]), time-oriented ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]) four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold $(M, g)$ with a smooth metric ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]); the manifold is Hausdorff ([[§9 Hausdorff Spaces#^def-9-1|590 Def. §9.1]]), as every manifold of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]. A singular point is therefore never a point of $M$: where the metric fails, $M$ has been cut off. Geodesics are those of the Levi-Civita connection; "affine parameter" is that of [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]], proper time for timelike geodesics. The examples are Minkowski space, the cone, Rindler space (§C10.2), and the Schwarzschild exterior and Kruskal spacetime of C7. The definitions are Couzens'; the precise wording of condition 3 of [[#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]] repairs a garbled line of the source ([[#^cau-c10-4-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens §4.2–§4.3]]). The singularity theorems are named, not proved.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]); $f(r) = 1 - 2M/r$; $d\Omega^2 = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2$; $u = t - r_\ast$, $v = t + r_\ast$ with $r_\ast$ the tortoise coordinate ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]); $\tilde g = \Omega^2g$ is the "unphysical" metric; $\mathcal K$ is the Kruskal spacetime and $K = R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}R^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}$ the Kretschmann scalar. d'Inverno writes $+{-}{-}{-}$.

## Infinity as a boundary

[[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-2|Def. §C10.1.2]] rescaled the metric by a conformal factor that vanishes at infinity and drew the result. The rescaled metric is a perfectly good metric on a larger manifold, and the points that were at infinity form a hypersurface of it. That hypersurface, not a chart, carries the notion of infinity.

> [!definition] Definition §C10.4.1: Conformal Boundary
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be a spacetime. A **conformal boundary** of $(M, g)$ is given by three things:
> - a spacetime $(\tilde M, \tilde g)$ and a smooth function $\Omega : \tilde M \to \mathbf R$;
> - an inclusion of $M$ as an open subset of $\tilde M$ on which $\Omega > 0$ and $\tilde g = \Omega^2g$: a conformal transformation of $g$ ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-1|Def. §C10.1.1]]) followed by an extension of the result, in the sense of [[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]], part 1;
> - a subset $\partial M \subset \tilde M$, disjoint from $M$, such that $M \cup \partial M$ with the charts of $\tilde M$ is a manifold with boundary ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-7|Def. §C2.1.7]]) with interior $M$ and boundary $\partial M$, and $\Omega = 0$ on $\partial M$.
>
> $\tilde g$ is the **unphysical metric**; it is smooth and Lorentzian on $\partial M$, where the physical metric $g = \Omega^{-2}\tilde g$ is undefined.
>
> *Why $\Omega = 0$ means "at infinity".* A curve that reaches $\partial M$ has finite length or affine parameter for $\tilde g$; for $g = \Omega^{-2}\tilde g$ every length element is multiplied by $\Omega^{-1} \to \infty$ near $\partial M$. *Why the light cones survive.* Since $\Omega > 0$ on $M$, $\tilde g$ and $g$ have the same timelike, null and spacelike vectors ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^thm-c10-1-1|Theorem §C10.1.1]]); causal statements about $M$ can be made with $\tilde g$, and extended by continuity to $\partial M$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.2, Definition 3, conditions 1–3 and the paragraph before it (manifold with boundary), pp. 73–74 (PDF 74–75)*

^def-c10-4-1

The boundary of Minkowski space found in §C10.1 has two null pieces $\mathscr I^\pm$, each swept out by the outgoing (incoming) light rays, one for every retarded (advanced) time and direction, and three points $i^\pm$, $i^0$. Couzens drops the three points: for Kruskal $i^\pm$ are singular points of the conformal metric and $i^0$ is not smooth ([[§C10.3 Penrose Diagrams of Kruskal and of Collapse#^cau-c10-3-1|§C10.3, Caution on the conformal factor at the points at infinity]]), and a definition meant to include black holes must not ask for them.

> [!definition] Definition §C10.4.2: Asymptotically Flat at Null Infinity
> *Structure.* A spacetime $(M, g)$ is **asymptotically flat at null infinity** if it has a conformal boundary $(\tilde M, \tilde g, \Omega, \partial M)$ ([[#^def-c10-4-1|Def. §C10.4.1]]) with:
> 1. $d\Omega \neq 0$ at every point of $\partial M$;
> 2. $\partial M$ is the disjoint union of two pieces $\mathscr I^+$ and $\mathscr I^-$, each diffeomorphic ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-6|Def. §C2.1.6]]) to $\mathbf R \times S^2$;
> 3. no past-directed causal curve ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]) of $\tilde M$ that starts in $M$ meets $\mathscr I^+$, and no future-directed one that starts in $M$ meets $\mathscr I^-$;
> 4. $\mathscr I^+$ and $\mathscr I^-$ are complete ([[#^def-c10-4-3|Def. §C10.4.3]]).
>
> *Why each condition.*
> - Condition 1 fixes the rate. The spheres of radius $r$ have area $4\pi r^2$; for $\tilde g$ to stay nondegenerate on $\partial M$, $\Omega^2r^2$ must have a finite nonzero limit, so $\Omega \sim 1/r$, which vanishes to first order in $x = 1/r$: $d\Omega \neq 0$. $\Omega \sim r^{-p}$ with $p \neq 1$ makes the spheres collapse or blow up ([[#^ex-c10-4-3|Example §C10.4.3]], Step 4). Couzens: this makes the metric approach Minkowski "at the appropriate rate".
> - Condition 2 asks for the shape of Minkowski's null infinity ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-3|Def. §C10.1.3]]): one outgoing light ray reaches $\mathscr I^+$ for each retarded time $u \in \mathbf R$ and each direction in $S^2$. That the two pieces are disjoint keeps $i^0$ (and $i^\pm$) out of $\partial M$.
> - Condition 3 places $\mathscr I^+$ in the future of $M$ and $\mathscr I^-$ in its past: light reaches $\mathscr I^+$ only by travelling forward in time.
> - Condition 4 forbids a $\mathscr I^+$ that is cut short, as it would be if part of it were missing from $\partial M$.
>
> *What is not asked.* $\partial M$ need not contain every asymptotic region: a spacetime with several ends, like Kruskal, is asymptotically flat with one chosen end attached ([[#^ex-c10-4-3|Example §C10.4.3]], Step 7).
>
> *In coordinates.* Couzens states (eq. (4.32)) that near $\mathscr I^+$ the metric is that of Minkowski space in retarded coordinates, $-du^2 - 2\,du\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2$, plus terms suppressed by powers of $1/r$; for Schwarzschild the only extra term is $(2M/r)\,du^2$ ([[#^ex-c10-4-3|Example §C10.4.3]], Step 1). The general statement is quoted, not derived.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.2, Definition 3 and the paragraph after it, eq. (4.32), pp. 73–74 (PDF 74–75); condition 3 as repaired here ([[#^cau-c10-4-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens §4.2–§4.3]])*

^def-c10-4-2

> [!definition] Definition §C10.4.3: Completeness of ℐ⁺
> *Structure.* Let $\mathscr I^+$ be as in [[#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]], and let it be a null hypersurface of $(\tilde M, \tilde g)$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]]), as it is in every example of these notes. The vector field $n^\mu = \tilde g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu\Omega$ is tangent to $\mathscr I^+$, because $n^\mu\partial_\mu\Omega = \tilde g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\Omega\,\partial_\nu\Omega = 0$ there; its integral curves are null geodesics of $\tilde g$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^thm-c10-5-3|Theorem §C10.5.3]]), the **generators** of $\mathscr I^+$. $\mathscr I^+$ is **complete** if along every generator an affine parameter of $\tilde g$ ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]) takes all values in $\mathbf R$. The same for $\mathscr I^-$.
>
> *For Minkowski and Schwarzschild* with $\Omega = 1/r$ the generators are the lines of constant $(\theta, \varphi)$ on $\mathscr I^+$, affinely parametrized by the retarded time $u \in \mathbf R$ ([[#^ex-c10-4-3|Example §C10.4.3]], Step 6): each runs from $i^0$ ($u \to -\infty$) to $i^+$ ($u \to +\infty$).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.2, Definition 4, p. 74 (PDF 75)*

^def-c10-4-3

The conditions are checked for Schwarzschild in [[#^ex-c10-4-3|Example §C10.4.3]], whose figure shows $\mathscr I^+$ in a chart. With $\mathscr I^+$ defined, "escaping to infinity" has a coordinate-free meaning, the one [[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^def-c10-7-2|Def. §C10.7.2]] needs. The other edge of the diagrams is harder, because it lies at finite distance.

## Singularities as places: the chart-based classification

> [!definition] Definition §C10.4.4: Physical Singularity (Scalar-Curvature or Non-Scalar)
> *Structure.* Let $S$ be a singularity of the metric in a chart $x$ ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]]): coordinate values at the edge of $x(U)$ at which a component of $g_{\mu\nu}$ or $g^{\mu\nu}$ diverges or is undefined, or $\det g_{\mu\nu} \to 0$. $S$ is a **physical singularity** if it is not a coordinate singularity: no extension of $(U, g)$ makes the points that the chart approaches at $S$ regular ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]], part 1). A physical singularity is of one of two kinds:
> - a **scalar-curvature singularity**, if some curvature invariant ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-1|Def. §C7.1.1]]) is unbounded along a curve of finite proper time or affine parameter that runs into $S$ (the curvature singularity of [[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]], part 2); since the invariant is a scalar, it diverges in every chart;
> - a **non-scalar singularity**, if it is physical but no curvature invariant diverges. The **conical singularity** of [[#^ex-c10-4-1|Example §C10.4.1]] is one, with every invariant zero.
>
> *Examples.* Schwarzschild: $r = 2M$ coordinate, $r = 0$ scalar-curvature ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]]). Rindler: $\xi = 0$ coordinate ([[#^thm-c10-4-2|Theorem §C10.4.2]]). The cone: non-scalar.
>
> *What the definition cannot do.* It is phrased in a chart, about coordinate values that are not points of $M$; and to show that $S$ is physical one must exclude every extension, of which there is no list. A finite invariant proves nothing ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]]), and the cone shows that a non-removable defect can have none at all.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.3.1, p. 75 (PDF 76) ("metric singularity", "coordinate singularity", "physical singularity", "scalar curvature singularity"; "Not all physical singularities are curvature singularities")*

^def-c10-4-4

What all physical singularities in these examples share is visible without a chart: some geodesics run into them and stop after finite affine parameter, the cone's radial lines at the apex, Kruskal's infalling observers at $r = 0$. Couzens: singularities "are not places", but they show up as curves that end. The next four definitions make "end" precise.

## Singularities as ends of curves

> [!definition] Definition §C10.4.5: Endpoints and Inextendible Curves
> *Structure.* Let $\gamma : (a, b) \to M$ be a curve in a manifold $M$, $-\infty \le a < b \le +\infty$; the case of interest is a future-directed causal curve ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]).
> - A point $p \in M$ is a **future endpoint** of $\gamma$ if for every neighbourhood $O$ of $p$ there is $t_0 < b$ with $\gamma(t) \in O$ for all $t > t_0$: $\gamma(t) \to p$ as $t \to b$. By the Hausdorff property a curve has at most one future endpoint ([[§9 Hausdorff Spaces#^thm-9-3|590 Thm. §9.3]]).
> - $\gamma$ is **future-inextendible** if it has no future endpoint in $M$. **Past endpoint** and **past-inextendible** are defined with $t \to a$. $\gamma$ is **inextendible** if it is both.
>
> The name: a curve with a future endpoint $p$ can be continued through $p$, so it is part of a longer curve. Whether $\gamma$ has an endpoint depends on $M$, not only on the points of $\gamma$: removing $p$ from $M$ makes $\gamma$ inextendible ([[#^ex-c10-4-2|Example §C10.4.2]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.3.1, Definition 5 and Example 4.1, p. 76 (PDF 77)*

^def-c10-4-5

> [!definition] Definition §C10.4.6: Complete Geodesic; Geodesically Complete Spacetime
> *Structure.* An inextendible ([[#^def-c10-4-5|Def. §C10.4.5]]) affinely parametrized geodesic $\gamma : (a, b) \to M$ ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]) is **complete** if $a = -\infty$ and $b = +\infty$: its affine parameter extends to $\pm\infty$. The notion does not depend on which affine parameter is used, since any two are related by $\sigma = \alpha\lambda + \beta$, $\alpha \neq 0$ ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 3). It is **future-complete** if $b = +\infty$, **past-complete** if $a = -\infty$.
>
> A spacetime is **geodesically complete** if every inextendible geodesic is complete, and **causally geodesically complete** if every inextendible timelike or null geodesic is complete; **timelike** (**null**) **geodesically complete** if every inextendible timelike (null) geodesic is. Otherwise it is (causally, timelike, null) geodesically **incomplete**; null or timelike incompleteness implies causal incompleteness.
>
> *Physical reading.* Along an affinely parametrized geodesic $g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)$ is constant ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-7|Theorem §C3.7.7]]), so for a timelike geodesic an affine parameter can be normalised to proper time, the clock of a freely falling observer ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]): a future-incomplete timelike geodesic is a freely falling observer whose clock stops after a finite reading, not because anything happens to the clock but because there is no more spacetime. A future-incomplete null geodesic is a light ray with the same fate.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.3.1, Definition 6, p. 76 (PDF 77) (Couzens' "geodesically complete" is the causal version) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.13, pp. 391–392 (PDF 406–407) (the world-line "comes to an end after a finite time (or affine parameter for a photon)")*

^def-c10-4-6

Incompleteness cannot be the whole definition. The Schwarzschild exterior $r > 2M$ is incomplete, because the infalling observer leaves it at $r = 2M$ ([[#^thm-c10-4-1|Theorem §C10.4.1]], part 1), and so is Minkowski space with a point removed ([[#^ex-c10-4-2|Example §C10.4.2]]). In both, the missing piece of spacetime exists and can be put back. The next definition names that possibility.

> [!definition] Definition §C10.4.7: Extension; Extendible Spacetime
> *Structure.* An **extension** of a spacetime $(M, g)$ is a spacetime $(M', g')$ (connected) together with a map $\iota : M \to M'$ that is a diffeomorphism ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-6|Def. §C2.1.6]]) of $M$ onto an open subset $\iota(M) \neq M'$ and pulls the metric back to the metric ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]]):
>
> $$
> \iota^{\ast}g' = g, \qquad g'_{\iota(p)}(\iota_{\ast}V, \iota_{\ast}W) = g_p(V, W) \quad (p \in M,\ V, W \in T_pM) ,
> $$
>
> an isometry ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-1|Def. §C3.13.1]], here between two manifolds) onto a proper open part of $M'$. $(M, g)$ is **extendible** if it has an extension and **inextendible** otherwise.
>
> *Examples.* Region I of Schwarzschild has the extensions $N$ (ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein, [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]], part 3) and $\mathcal K$ (Kruskal, [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]); the Rindler wedge has Minkowski space ([[#^thm-c10-4-2|Theorem §C10.4.2]]). The "maximal extension" of [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]] is an extension that is itself inextendible.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.3.1, Example 4.2 ("A spacetime which is extendible … is also incomplete … consider the maximal extension"), pp. 76–77 (PDF 77–78) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.13, p. 392 (PDF 407) ("cannot be extended")*

^def-c10-4-7

> [!definition] Definition §C10.4.8: Singular Spacetime
> *Structure.* A spacetime $(M, g)$ is **singular** if it is causally geodesically incomplete ([[#^def-c10-4-6|Def. §C10.4.6]]) and inextendible ([[#^def-c10-4-7|Def. §C10.4.7]]).
>
> In words: some freely falling observer or light ray ends after finite proper time or affine parameter, and no larger spacetime continues it. The singularity is not a set of points; it is the missing end of the incomplete geodesics.
>
> *What the definition does and does not say.* It says neither where the singularity is nor that the curvature diverges there. The cone ([[#^ex-c10-4-1|Example §C10.4.1]]) is singular with zero curvature; Kruskal ([[#^thm-c10-4-1|Theorem §C10.4.1]]) is singular with $K \to \infty$. Minkowski space with a point removed is not singular ([[#^ex-c10-4-2|Example §C10.4.2]]), nor is Rindler space ([[#^thm-c10-4-2|Theorem §C10.4.2]]). Schutz: of the singularities inside horizons "it is not known whether [they] will always have infinite curvature; all that is known is that infalling geodesics are incomplete". The definition does not require a horizon either: the charged black hole with more charge than mass is a singular spacetime whose timelike singularity is visible from infinity ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-6|Theorem §C11.3.6]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.3.1, Definition 7, p. 77 (PDF 78) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.13, pp. 391–392 (PDF 406–407) · Schutz, §11.3, item (7), p. 327 (PDF 345)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.4.1: Schwarzschild Is Geodesically Incomplete and Kruskal Is a Singular Spacetime
> Let $M > 0$, let $\mathrm I = \{r > 2M\}$ carry the Schwarzschild metric ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]) and $\mathcal K$ be the Kruskal spacetime ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]). An ingoing radial light ray at radius $r_0$ with energy $E = f\dot t > 0$ is the curve $v = v_0$, $\theta, \varphi$ fixed, with affine parameter $\lambda$ and
>
> $$
> r(\lambda) = r_0 - E\lambda .
> $$
>
> 1. **Region I is incomplete but extendible.** In $\mathrm I$ the ray is future-inextendible and its affine parameter is bounded, $\lambda < (r_0 - 2M)/E$; likewise a radial infall reaches $r \to 2M$ after finite proper time ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]], part 2). $\mathrm I$ is extendible, so it is not singular.
> 2. **$\mathcal K$ is incomplete.** In $\mathcal K$ the ray is future-inextendible with $\lambda < r_0/E$, and $K = 48M^2/r^6 \to \infty$ along it. A radial infall reaches $r \to 0$ after finite proper time too ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]], part 4).
> 3. **$\mathcal K$ is inextendible.** Every extension would contain a geodesic of $\mathcal K$ along which $K$ stays bounded and which is inextendible after finite affine parameter; in $\mathcal K$ every such geodesic runs into $r = 0$, where $K \to \infty$.
> 4. Hence $\mathcal K$ is a singular spacetime ([[#^def-c10-4-8|Def. §C10.4.8]]).
>
> Restored: $K = 48G^2M^2/(c^4r^6)$.
>
> *Model:* the vacuum Schwarzschild solution and its maximal extension, $M > 0$; *applies:* exactly. Part 3 uses Kruskal's result that every geodesic of $\mathcal K$ either has unbounded affine parameter or ends at $r = 0$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], part 5), proved in these notes for radial null geodesics and quoted for the rest.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.3.1, Example 4.2 and the sentence after Definition 7, pp. 76–77 (PDF 77–78) ("Kruskal spacetime … is geodesically incomplete because some geodesics reach $r = 0$ in finite affine parameter"; Schwarzschild coordinates "also incomplete" because extendible; Kruskal singular, stated) · the proof of part 3 is written here*

^thm-c10-4-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the ray is a geodesic with affine parameter $r$). In ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates the metric is $-f\,dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2$ on $N = \mathbf R_v \times (0, \infty)_r \times S^2$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]). Along $v = v_0$, $\theta, \varphi$ fixed, the tangent is $k = \dot r\,\partial_r$. With $g_{vr} = 1$, $g_{rr} = 0$, $g_{\theta r} = g_{\varphi r} = 0$, all constant,
>
> $$
> \Gamma_{\mu rr} = \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_rg_{\mu r} + \partial_rg_{\mu r} - \partial_\mu g_{rr}\bigr) = \partial_rg_{\mu r} - \tfrac12\partial_\mu g_{rr} = 0 \qquad (\mu = v, r, \theta, \varphi) ,
> $$
>
> so $\Gamma^\mu{}_{rr} = 0$ and the geodesic equation $\ddot x^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta = 0$ reduces to $\ddot r = 0$: $r = r_0 - E\lambda$ is an affinely parametrized geodesic for every constant $E > 0$ (the sign makes it ingoing), and $g(k, k) = g_{rr}\dot r^2 = 0$: null. Its energy is $-g(\partial_v, k) = -g_{vr}\dot r = E$; in region I, $\partial_v = \partial_t$ and $-g(\partial_t, k) = f\dot t$.
>
> **Step 2** (region I). The ray lies in $\mathrm I$ for $\lambda < (r_0 - 2M)/E$. If it had a future endpoint $p \in \mathrm I$ ([[#^def-c10-4-5|Def. §C10.4.5]]), continuity of the function $r$ would give $r(p) = \lim r(\lambda) = 2M$, but $r > 2M$ on $\mathrm I$. So it is future-inextendible with bounded affine parameter: $\mathrm I$ is causally geodesically incomplete ([[#^def-c10-4-6|Def. §C10.4.6]]). The map $(t, r, \theta, \varphi) \mapsto (v = t + r_\ast, r, \theta, \varphi)$ is an isometry of $\mathrm I$ onto $\{r > 2M\} \subsetneq N$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]], part 3), an extension ([[#^def-c10-4-7|Def. §C10.4.7]]). Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 3** ($\mathcal K$ is incomplete). $N$ sits in $\mathcal K$ as regions I ∪ II and the horizon $U = 0$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], part 2), so the ray is a geodesic of $\mathcal K$ for $0 \le \lambda < r_0/E$. As $\lambda \to r_0/E$, $r \to 0$ and
>
> $$
> K\bigl(\gamma(\lambda)\bigr) = \frac{48M^2}{(r_0 - E\lambda)^6} \to \infty .
> $$
>
> A future endpoint $p \in \mathcal K$ would have $r(p) = 0$, but $r > 0$ on $\mathcal K$; so the ray is future-inextendible in $\mathcal K$ with $\lambda < r_0/E$. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 4** (an extension has a boundary point). Suppose $\iota : \mathcal K \to M'$ is an extension. $\iota(\mathcal K)$ is open, nonempty and $\neq M'$. If it were also closed, $\iota(\mathcal K)$ and $M' \setminus \iota(\mathcal K)$ would separate the connected space $M'$ ([[§15 Connected Spaces#^def-15-2|590 Def. §15.2]]). So some $p \in M'$ lies in the closure of $\iota(\mathcal K)$ but not in $\iota(\mathcal K)$.
>
> **Step 5** (a geodesic from inside to $p$). By [[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]] the exponential map $\exp_p$ ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^def-c3-8-1|Def. §C3.8.1]]) maps a neighbourhood of $0 \in T_pM'$ diffeomorphically onto a neighbourhood $\mathcal N$ of $p$. Since $p$ is in the closure of $\iota(\mathcal K)$, there is $q \in \mathcal N \cap \iota(\mathcal K)$; with $w = \exp_p^{-1}(q)$ the curve
>
> $$
> \sigma(s) = \exp_p\bigl((1 - s)w\bigr), \qquad 0 \le s \le 1 ,
> $$
>
> is an affinely parametrized geodesic of $M'$ from $\sigma(0) = q$ to $\sigma(1) = p$ (part 1 of that theorem, read backward).
>
> **Step 6** (where it leaves $\iota(\mathcal K)$). Let $s_\ast = \sup\{s \in [0, 1] : \sigma([0, s]) \subset \iota(\mathcal K)\}$. Since $\iota(\mathcal K)$ is open and contains $q$, $s_\ast > 0$. The point $\sigma(s_\ast)$ is not in $\iota(\mathcal K)$: if it were, openness would put $\sigma([s_\ast, s_\ast + \delta))$ in $\iota(\mathcal K)$ for some $\delta > 0$, contradicting the supremum when $s_\ast < 1$, and $\sigma(1) = p \notin \iota(\mathcal K)$ when $s_\ast = 1$. So $\sigma([0, s_\ast)) \subset \iota(\mathcal K)$ and $\sigma(s_\ast) \notin \iota(\mathcal K)$.
>
> **Step 7** (pull back). $\gamma = \iota^{-1} \circ \sigma$ on $[0, s_\ast)$ is a geodesic of $\mathcal K$ with affine parameter $s$: the geodesic equation is built from the metric and its derivatives alone, and $\iota$ carries $g$ to $g'$. $\gamma$ is future-inextendible in $\mathcal K$: if $x \in \mathcal K$ were its endpoint, continuity of $\iota$ would give $\sigma(s) \to \iota(x)$ as $s \to s_\ast$, while $\sigma(s) \to \sigma(s_\ast)$; limits are unique in the Hausdorff space $M'$ ([[§9 Hausdorff Spaces#^thm-9-3|590 Thm. §9.3]]), so $\sigma(s_\ast) = \iota(x) \in \iota(\mathcal K)$, contradicting Step 6. So $\gamma$ is inextendible in that direction after the finite affine parameter $s_\ast$.
>
> **Step 8** (curvature along it). By [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], part 5, a geodesic of $\mathcal K$ that is inextendible after finite affine parameter ends at $UV = 1$, $r \to 0$, so $K(\gamma(s)) = 48M^2/r^6 \to \infty$ as $s \to s_\ast$. On the other side, the Kretschmann scalar $K'$ of $g'$ is a continuous function on $M'$, and $K'(\iota(x)) = K(x)$ because $K$ is built from the metric alone ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-1|Def. §C7.1.1]]) and $\iota$ is an isometry onto $\iota(\mathcal K)$. $K' \circ \sigma$ is continuous on the closed interval $[0, s_\ast]$, hence bounded, and equals $K \circ \gamma$ on $[0, s_\ast)$. A bounded function cannot tend to $\infty$. So no extension exists. Part 3 is proved; with part 2 and [[#^def-c10-4-8|Def. §C10.4.8]], part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Incompleteness is shown by one explicit light ray. Inextendibility needs a statement about every way out: Steps 4–7 turn any extension into an incomplete geodesic that sees only bounded curvature, and Step 8 rules that out because $\mathcal K$'s incomplete geodesics all meet diverging curvature.
> - Steps 4–7 use nothing about Kruskal. They are the general [[#^thm-c10-4-3|Theorem §C10.4.3]].

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.4.2: Rindler Space Is Incomplete but Extendible
> Let Rindler space be $R = \{(\eta, \xi, y, z) : \xi > 0\}$ with $ds^2 = -\xi^2d\eta^2 + d\xi^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]], [[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]]). For geodesics in the $(\eta, \xi)$ plane, $E = \xi^2\dot\eta$ is constant.
> 1. **Null geodesics.** $\xi^2 = \xi_0^2 \pm 2E\lambda$. With the lower sign the ray reaches $\xi \to 0$, $\eta \to +\infty$, at the finite affine parameter $\lambda = \xi_0^2/2E$.
> 2. **Timelike geodesics.** $\xi = \sqrt{E^2 - \tau^2}$, $\eta = \operatorname{artanh}(\tau/E)$, for $-E < \tau < E$: the geodesic exists for the proper time $2E$ only, ending at $\xi \to 0$ in both directions.
> 3. **Extendible.** $(\eta, \xi, y, z) \mapsto (t, x, y, z) = (\xi\sinh\eta, \xi\cosh\eta, y, z)$ is an isometry of $R$ onto the wedge $\{x > |t|\}$ of Minkowski space, an extension. The geodesics of parts 1–2 are straight lines of Minkowski space that leave the wedge through its horizons $t = \pm x$; the timelike one of part 2 is $x = E$.
>
> So Rindler space is causally geodesically incomplete but not singular, and $\xi = 0$ is a coordinate singularity ([[#^def-c10-4-4|Def. §C10.4.4]]) in the strong sense of [[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]], part 1: an extension makes it regular.
>
> Restored: the timelike geodesic $x = E$ crosses the wedge in proper time $2E/c$.
>
> *Model:* flat spacetime in the Rindler chart; *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.3.1, Example 4.3, eqs. (4.36)–(4.40), p. 77 (PDF 78) (null geodesics; "We may extend Rindler space to obtain Minkowski space") · timelike geodesics and the map worked here · checked by script*

^thm-c10-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Lagrangian and the conserved quantity). For geodesics in the $(\eta, \xi)$ plane use $\mathcal L = -\xi^2\dot\eta^2 + \dot\xi^2$ ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-6|Theorem §C3.7.6]]). $\eta$ does not appear, so
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\lambda}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\eta} = \frac{d}{d\lambda}\bigl(-2\xi^2\dot\eta\bigr) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E \equiv \xi^2\dot\eta = \text{const} .
> $$
>
> The $\xi$ equation is $\frac{d}{d\lambda}(2\dot\xi) = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\xi = -2\xi\dot\eta^2$, i.e. $\ddot\xi = -\xi\dot\eta^2$ (equivalently $\Gamma^\xi{}_{\eta\eta} = \xi$, $\Gamma^\eta{}_{\eta\xi} = 1/\xi$, checked by script).
>
> **Step 2** (null). $\mathcal L = 0$ gives $\dot\xi^2 = \xi^2\dot\eta^2 = E^2/\xi^2$, so $\xi\dot\xi = \pm E$ and
>
> $$
> \frac{d(\xi^2)}{d\lambda} = 2\xi\dot\xi = \pm 2E \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \xi^2 = \xi_0^2 \pm 2E\lambda .
> $$
>
> Check of the $\xi$ equation, lower sign: $\dot\xi = -E/\xi$, $\ddot\xi = E\dot\xi/\xi^2 = -E^2/\xi^3$, and $-\xi\dot\eta^2 = -\xi E^2/\xi^4 = -E^2/\xi^3$. With the lower sign $\xi \to 0$ as $\lambda \to \xi_0^2/2E$, and
>
> $$
> \eta(\lambda) = \int\frac{E\,d\lambda}{\xi_0^2 - 2E\lambda} = -\tfrac12\ln\bigl(\xi_0^2 - 2E\lambda\bigr) + \text{const} \to +\infty .
> $$
>
> A future endpoint would have $\xi = 0$, which no point of $R$ has: the ray is future-inextendible with bounded affine parameter. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 3** (timelike). The normalisation $\mathcal L = -1$ gives $\dot\xi^2 = E^2/\xi^2 - 1$, so $(\xi\dot\xi)^2 = E^2 - \xi^2$. With $w = \xi^2$, $\dot w = 2\xi\dot\xi$ and $\dot w^2/4 = E^2 - w$; $w = E^2 - \tau^2$ solves it ($\dot w = -2\tau$, $\dot w^2/4 = \tau^2$), with $\tau = 0$ at the largest $\xi = E$. Then $\dot\eta = E/\xi^2 = E/(E^2 - \tau^2)$, whose integral with $\eta(0) = 0$ is $\eta = \operatorname{artanh}(\tau/E)$. $\xi$ is real only for $|\tau| < E$, and $\xi \to 0$ as $\tau \to \pm E$; the same endpoint argument as in Step 2 shows the geodesic is inextendible both ways. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 4** (the map is an isometry). From $t = \xi\sinh\eta$, $x = \xi\cosh\eta$: $dt = \sinh\eta\,d\xi + \xi\cosh\eta\,d\eta$, $dx = \cosh\eta\,d\xi + \xi\sinh\eta\,d\eta$, and
>
> $$
> -dt^2 + dx^2 = (\cosh^2\eta - \sinh^2\eta)\,d\xi^2 + 2\xi(\cosh\eta\sinh\eta - \sinh\eta\cosh\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta + \xi^2(\sinh^2\eta - \cosh^2\eta)\,d\eta^2 = d\xi^2 - \xi^2d\eta^2 .
> $$
>
> The image satisfies $x = \xi\cosh\eta > \xi|\sinh\eta| = |t|$; conversely every $(t, x)$ with $x > |t|$ comes from exactly one $(\eta, \xi) = (\operatorname{artanh}(t/x), \sqrt{x^2 - t^2})$, smooth in $(t, x)$. So the map is a diffeomorphism onto the open wedge, which is not all of $\mathbf R^4$, and it carries the Rindler metric to $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]]).
>
> **Step 5** (the curves in Minkowski space). Part 2: $x = \xi\cosh\eta = \sqrt{E^2 - \tau^2}/\sqrt{1 - \tau^2/E^2} = E$ and $t = \xi\sinh\eta = E\tanh\eta = \tau$: the inertial worldline $x = E$, which lies in the wedge only for $|t| < E$. Part 1, lower sign: as $\lambda \to \xi_0^2/2E$, $\xi \to 0$ and $\eta \to +\infty$, so $(t, x) \to$ a point of the future horizon $t = x > 0$; in Minkowski space the null line continues across it. Part 3 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** In the Rindler chart, light and inertial observers run into $\xi = 0$ after finite affine parameter, exactly as infalling observers run into $r = 2M$ in Schwarzschild coordinates. The incompleteness belongs to the chart's domain, not to spacetime: in the extension the same curves are straight lines that cross the horizon. This is [[#^thm-c10-4-3|Theorem §C10.4.3]] at work.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.4.3: ⚑ Every Extendible Spacetime Is Geodesically Incomplete
> Let $\iota : M \to M'$ be an extension of $(M, g)$ ([[#^def-c10-4-7|Def. §C10.4.7]]). Then $M$ contains a geodesic $\gamma : [0, s_\ast) \to M$, affinely parametrized, that is future-inextendible in $M$ after the finite affine parameter $s_\ast$, and for which:
> 1. $\iota \circ \gamma$ has a future endpoint in $M'$ ([[#^def-c10-4-5|Def. §C10.4.5]]), a point of the boundary of $\iota(M)$;
> 2. every curvature invariant ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-1|Def. §C7.1.1]]) of $g$ is bounded along $\gamma$.
>
> So an extendible spacetime is geodesically incomplete ([[#^def-c10-4-6|Def. §C10.4.6]]), with incomplete geodesics that meet no curvature divergence. Incompleteness alone cannot distinguish a singularity from a missing piece of spacetime; inextendibility is what [[#^def-c10-4-8|Def. §C10.4.8]] adds. The causal type of $\gamma$ is not controlled by the argument; in the examples (Schwarzschild exterior, Rindler, Minkowski minus a point) causal incomplete geodesics were found directly.
>
> *Model:* none (a statement about Lorentzian manifolds); *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.3.1, Example 4.2 ("A spacetime which is extendible … is also incomplete. The incompleteness arises because we are not considering the full spacetime") and Example 4.3, pp. 76–77 (PDF 77–78) · statement and proof written here*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the geodesic). Steps 4–7 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c10-4-1|Theorem §C10.4.1]] used only that $\iota$ is an extension. Repeated with $M$ in place of $\mathcal K$: a point $p$ in the closure of $\iota(M)$ but not in it; a radial geodesic $\sigma(s) = \exp_p((1 - s)w)$ from $q \in \iota(M)$ to $p$; the first exit parameter $s_\ast$ with $\sigma([0, s_\ast)) \subset \iota(M)$, $\sigma(s_\ast) \notin \iota(M)$; and $\gamma = \iota^{-1} \circ \sigma$ on $[0, s_\ast)$, a geodesic of $M$, future-inextendible in $M$, with affine parameter bounded by $s_\ast \le 1$. $\sigma(s_\ast)$ is the future endpoint of $\iota \circ \gamma = \sigma|_{[0, s_\ast)}$ in $M'$, and it lies in the closure of $\iota(M)$ but not in $\iota(M)$. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 2** (bounded invariants). Let $I$ be a curvature invariant and $I'$ the same expression built from $g'$, a continuous function on $M'$. Since $\iota^{\ast}g' = g$, $I = I' \circ \iota$ on $M$. Along $\gamma$, $I(\gamma(s)) = I'(\sigma(s))$, and $I' \circ \sigma$ is continuous on the closed interval $[0, s_\ast]$, hence bounded. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The boundary of $\iota(M)$ in $M'$ is reached by geodesics of $M$ after finite affine parameter, and nothing singular happens there in $M'$. Incompleteness is the symptom both of a missing piece and of a true singularity; only the impossibility of an extension tells them apart.

> [!remark] Remark: What the singularity theorems prove
> Spherical collapse ends at the $r = 0$ singularity of the Schwarzschild geometry ([[§C9.3 White Dwarfs, Neutron Stars and Collapse#^thm-c9-3-6|Theorem §C9.3.6]], [[§C10.3 Penrose Diagrams of Kruskal and of Collapse#^thm-c10-3-3|Theorem §C10.3.3]]). Couzens asks whether this is an artefact of the symmetry, since in Newtonian gravity a slightly perturbed collapsing ball bounces instead. Penrose (1965) showed it is not. d'Inverno describes the common pattern of the theorems that followed: a spacetime with (i) an initial or boundary condition, such as a trapped surface ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^def-c10-6-5|Def. §C10.6.5]]), (ii) a condition on the curvature, obtained from an energy condition ([[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^def-c4-5-1|Def. §C4.5.1]], [[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^def-c4-5-3|Def. §C4.5.3]]) through Einstein's equations, and (iii) a causality condition, such as no closed timelike curves, contains incomplete causal geodesics. The conclusion is incompleteness, not divergent curvature; this is why [[#^def-c10-4-8|Def. §C10.4.8]] is phrased by incompleteness. Penrose's theorem is stated with its hypotheses in [[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^rem-c10-6-1|§C10.6, Remark: Penrose's singularity theorem]]. Couzens adds that general relativity thereby "predicts its own downfall": it predicts singularities it cannot describe.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.3, pp. 74–75 (PDF 75–76) and footnote 15 · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.13, pp. 391–393 (PDF 406–408) ("pattern singularity theorem"; "none of the theorems leads directly to the existence of curvature singularities")*

> [!caution] Caution: Slips in Couzens §4.2–§4.3
> Read on the rendered pages (PDF 74–78, printed 73–77).
> - p. 73: charts of a manifold with boundary map to "$\mathbf R^n/\mathbf Z_2 = \{x^1 \ge 0\}$". The target is the closed half-space; the quotient of $\mathbf R^n$ by $x^1 \mapsto -x^1$ is homeomorphic to it, but charts go into the half-space itself ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-7|Def. §C2.1.7]], which uses $x^n \ge 0$).
> - p. 74, Definition 3, condition 5: "No past (future) directed causal curve starting in $M$ $\mathscr I^+$ ($\mathscr I^-$)" lacks its verb. Read: "… starting in $M$ meets $\mathscr I^+$ ($\mathscr I^-$)", condition 3 of [[#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]].
> - p. 74: "$\Omega$ has a first order singularity on $\partial M$" means a first-order zero. "Condition 4 says that $i^0$ should exist" is unclear: the condition makes $\mathscr I^+$ and $\mathscr I^-$ disjoint, which keeps $i^0$ out of $\partial M$.
> - p. 74, eq. (4.32): $g = -2\,du\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2 + \dots$, with "the leading terms are simply the Minkowski metric". Minkowski space in retarded coordinates is $-du^2 - 2\,du\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2$; without $-du^2$ the metric is not flat (its Ricci tensor has $R_{\theta\theta} = 1$; checked by script). Also "an asymptotically metric" for "an asymptotically flat metric".

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C10.4.1: A Conical Singularity
> *Question.* Is there a spacetime that is singular in the sense of [[#^def-c10-4-8|Def. §C10.4.8]] although every curvature invariant vanishes everywhere?
>
> *Setup.* $C = \mathbf R^2 \setminus \{0\}$ with polar coordinates $(r, \varphi)$, $\varphi \sim \varphi + 2\pi$, and the Riemannian metric
>
> $$
> g = dr^2 + \lambda^2r^2\,d\varphi^2, \qquad 0 < \lambda < 1 ,
> $$
>
> the cone of [[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^ex-c7-1-3|Example §C7.1.3]] (there $\alpha$): a flat sheet with a wedge of angle $2\pi(1 - \lambda)$ removed and the edges glued (figure below). The spacetime is $\mathbf R_t \times \mathbf R_z \times C$ with $ds^2 = -dt^2 + dz^2 + dr^2 + \lambda^2r^2d\varphi^2$.
>
> **Step 1** (flat). On any range of $\varphi$ shorter than $2\pi$, $\psi = \lambda\varphi$ turns $g$ into $dr^2 + r^2d\psi^2$, the Euclidean plane in polar coordinates. So $C$ is locally isometric to the plane, its Riemann tensor vanishes, and so does that of the 4D product (both checked by script). Every curvature invariant is zero.
>
> **Step 2** (circles). The circle $r = \varepsilon$ has length $\int_0^{2\pi}\lambda\varepsilon\,d\varphi = 2\pi\lambda\varepsilon$, so circumference/radius $= 2\pi\lambda$ for every $\varepsilon$. For $\lambda = 3/4$: $1.5\pi = 4.712$ against $2\pi = 6.283$; the **deficit angle** is $2\pi(1 - \lambda) = \pi/2$, i.e. $90°$.
>
> **Step 3** (incomplete). In the unrolled plane, geodesics are straight lines, $r(s) = \sqrt{(s - s_0)^2 + b^2}$ with unit speed $s$ and $r^2\,d\psi/ds = b$ (checked by script). For $b > 0$ both ends go to $r \to \infty$: complete. For $b = 0$ the line is radial, $r = s_0 - s$, and reaches $r \to 0$ at $s = s_0$; no point of $C$ has $r = 0$, so it is inextendible ([[#^def-c10-4-5|Def. §C10.4.5]]) with bounded parameter. In the spacetime, the null and timelike geodesics with $\dot r < 0$ and $\dot\varphi = 0$ do the same: incomplete ([[#^def-c10-4-6|Def. §C10.4.6]]).
>
> **Step 4** ($C$ is inextendible). Suppose $\iota : C \to C'$ is an extension (a connected 2D Riemannian manifold, same definition). By Steps 1–2 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c10-4-3|Theorem §C10.4.3]] there is an incomplete geodesic of $C$ whose image has an endpoint in $C'$; by Step 3 of this example it is a radial line, ending at some $p' \in C'$ as $r \to 0$.
> - *All radial lines end at $p'$.* The points $(\varepsilon, \varphi_0)$ and $(\varepsilon, \varphi_1)$ are joined by an arc of length at most $2\pi\lambda\varepsilon$, so their images are at most that far apart in $C'$; as $\varepsilon \to 0$ the first tends to $p'$, so the second does too.
> - *The circle $r = \varepsilon$ is a geodesic circle about $p'$.* Each radial line is a geodesic of $C'$ ending at $p'$ at distance $\varepsilon$ from $(\varepsilon, \varphi)$, so for small $\varepsilon$, $\iota(\varepsilon, \varphi) = \exp_{p'}(\varepsilon\,w(\varphi))$ with $w(\varphi)$ a unit vector at $p'$ ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^def-c3-8-1|Def. §C3.8.1]]). $\varphi \mapsto w(\varphi)$ is continuous and one-to-one (two radial lines with the same direction at $p'$ coincide, by uniqueness of geodesics), so it is a continuous injective map of a circle into the unit circle of $T_{p'}C'$, hence onto (its image is compact and homeomorphic to a circle, and a proper closed piece of a circle is an arc, which is not): $\iota$ maps the circle $r = \varepsilon$ onto the whole geodesic circle of radius $\varepsilon$ about $p'$.
> - *Its length.* In normal coordinates $g'_{ij} = \delta_{ij} + O(\varepsilon^2)$ ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^thm-c3-8-2|Theorem §C3.8.2]]), so the geodesic circle has length $2\pi\varepsilon\,(1 + O(\varepsilon^2))$, as for the sphere, $2\pi a\sin(\varepsilon/a) = 2\pi\varepsilon(1 - \varepsilon^2/6a^2 + \dots)$ ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^ex-c3-8-1|Example §C3.8.1]]; checked by script). Isometries preserve length, so $2\pi\lambda\varepsilon = 2\pi\varepsilon(1 + O(\varepsilon^2))$, and $\varepsilon \to 0$ gives $\lambda = 1$. Contradiction.
>
> **Step 5** (verdict). $C$ is incomplete and inextendible: singular, with zero curvature. Its singularity is non-scalar ([[#^def-c10-4-4|Def. §C10.4.4]]). For the 4D product, Step 3 gives incomplete causal geodesics and Step 4, in each plane $t, z = \text{const}$, shows that the axis $r = 0$ cannot be added with a smooth metric; a proof that no other extension exists would need the 4D version of Step 4, which is not written out here. Metrics of this product form are the usual model of the field outside an idealised straight **cosmic string**, a proposed early-universe object that Schutz names as a possible gravitational-wave source; how $\lambda$ depends on the string's mass per length is not derived in these notes.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.3.1, eqs. (4.33)–(4.35), pp. 75–76 (PDF 76–77) (with (4.35) corrected; [[#^cau-c10-4-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens §4.2–§4.3]]) · Schutz, §12.2, p. 369 (PDF 387) (cosmic strings named) · inextendibility argument written here · checked by script (`verify_c10.py`, `verify_c10_4.py`)*

> [!example] Example §C10.4.2: Minkowski Space with a Point Removed
> *Question.* Couzens' Example 4.1: is Minkowski space with the origin removed singular?
>
> *Setup.* $M = \mathbf R^4 \setminus \{0\}$ with $\eta_{\mu\nu}$, and the worldline at rest $\gamma(t) = (t, 0, 0, 0)$, $t \in (-\infty, 0)$, a timelike geodesic with proper time $t$ as affine parameter.
>
> **Step 1** (in full Minkowski space it has an endpoint). Let $O$ be a neighbourhood of the origin in $\mathbf R^4$; it contains a Euclidean ball of some radius $\delta > 0$ about $0$. For $-\delta < t < 0$, $|\gamma(t)| = |t| < \delta$, so $\gamma(t) \in O$: the origin is a future endpoint ([[#^def-c10-4-5|Def. §C10.4.5]]).
>
> **Step 2** (in $M$ it has none). Suppose $p \in M$ were a future endpoint. $p \neq 0$, so there are disjoint neighbourhoods $O_p$ of $p$ and $O_0$ of $0$ in $\mathbf R^4$ ([[§9 Hausdorff Spaces#^def-9-1|590 Def. §9.1]]). By Step 1, $\gamma(t) \in O_0$ for all $t$ close to $0$, so not in $O_p$: $p$ is not an endpoint. $\gamma$ is future-inextendible in $M$.
>
> **Step 3** (incomplete). Its affine parameter is bounded above by $0$. A clock released at rest at the spatial origin at $t_0 < 0$ has run for the proper time $|t_0|$ when its worldline ends. So $M$ is causally geodesically incomplete ([[#^def-c10-4-6|Def. §C10.4.6]]). Light rays through the origin are incomplete the same way.
>
> **Step 4** (no curvature, no chart singularity). $M$ is flat: every curvature invariant is $0$. In the Cartesian chart the metric is $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ up to the missing point, with nothing to diverge; [[#^def-c10-4-4|Def. §C10.4.4]] finds no singularity of the metric at all.
>
> **Step 5** (extendible). The inclusion $M \to \mathbf R^{1,3}$ is an isometry onto the open set $\mathbf R^4 \setminus \{0\} \neq \mathbf R^4$: an extension ([[#^def-c10-4-7|Def. §C10.4.7]]).
>
> *Result.* $M$ is incomplete but extendible, so it is **not** singular ([[#^def-c10-4-8|Def. §C10.4.8]]); the incomplete geodesic is the one [[#^thm-c10-4-3|Theorem §C10.4.3]] predicts, with bounded curvature. The comparison with [[#^ex-c10-4-1|Example §C10.4.1]] is the point of the definition: both spacetimes are flat and incomplete, and they differ only in whether the missing point can be put back. The figure after [[#^def-c10-4-8|Def. §C10.4.8]], panel (a), draws it.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.3.1, Example 4.1, p. 76 (PDF 77) · Steps 2–5 worked here*

> [!example] Example §C10.4.3: Schwarzschild Is Asymptotically Flat
> *Question.* Does the Schwarzschild exterior satisfy [[#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]], and with which conformal factor? Couzens states the definition with no example worked.
>
> *Setup.* Region I, $r > 2M$, in outgoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates $(u, r, \theta, \varphi)$, $u = t - r_\ast$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-4|Def. §C7.2.4]]); $M = 0$ is Minkowski space. Conformal factor $\Omega = 1/r$, and $x \equiv 1/r$.
>
> **Step 1** (the metric near $\mathscr I^+$). With $dt = du + dr/f$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-4|Theorem §C7.2.4]]),
>
> $$
> -f\,dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{f} = -f\Bigl(du^2 + \frac{2\,du\,dr}{f} + \frac{dr^2}{f^2}\Bigr) + \frac{dr^2}{f} = -f\,du^2 - 2\,du\,dr ,
> $$
>
> $$
> g = -f\,du^2 - 2\,du\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2 = \underbrace{\bigl(-du^2 - 2\,du\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2\bigr)}_{\text{Minkowski, } u = t - r} + \frac{2M}{r}\,du^2 .
> $$
>
> The bracket is flat (the case $M = 0$, checked by script); the only correction is $2M/r$, suppressed by $1/r$. This is the correct form of Couzens' (4.32).
>
> **Step 2** (rescale). $dr = -dx/x^2$, so $2\,du\,dr = -2\,du\,dx/x^2$ and $r^2d\Omega^2 = d\Omega^2/x^2$; with $f = 1 - 2Mx$,
>
> $$
> g = -(1 - 2Mx)\,du^2 + \frac{2\,du\,dx}{x^2} + \frac{d\Omega^2}{x^2}, \qquad \tilde g = \Omega^2g = x^2g = -x^2(1 - 2Mx)\,du^2 + 2\,du\,dx + d\Omega^2 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (attach $\mathscr I^+$). The components of $\tilde g$ are polynomials in $x$, so the same formula defines a smooth metric for $x \in (-1, 1/2M)$, $u \in \mathbf R$; $\tilde M$ is region I with this strip glued on. In $(u, x)$ the block $\begin{pmatrix} -x^2(1 - 2Mx) & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $-1$, so $\det\tilde g = -\sin^2\theta \neq 0$ (away from the usual poles of $(\theta, \varphi)$): $\tilde g$ is Lorentzian on $x = 0$. $M \cup \partial M$ is $\{x \ge 0\}$, a manifold with boundary whose charts use $x$ as the coordinate $\ge 0$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-7|Def. §C2.1.7]]), with $\partial M = \{x = 0\}$ and $\Omega = x = 0$ there: a conformal boundary ([[#^def-c10-4-1|Def. §C10.4.1]]).
>
> **Step 4** (condition 1). $d\Omega = dx \neq 0$. Other powers fail: with $\Omega = x^p$ the sphere part of $\Omega^2g$ is $x^{2p}\cdot x^{-2}d\Omega^2 = x^{2p - 2}d\Omega^2$, which tends to $0$ for $p > 1$ (spheres collapse, $\tilde g$ degenerate) and to $\infty$ for $p < 1$; only $p = 1$ works.
>
> **Step 5** (conditions 2 and 3). $\mathscr I^+ = \{x = 0\}$ has coordinates $(u, \theta, \varphi) \in \mathbf R \times S^2$. The inverse of the $(u, x)$ block is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & x^2(1 - 2Mx) \end{pmatrix}$, so $\tilde g^{xx} = x^2(1 - 2Mx)$ vanishes at $x = 0$: $d\Omega$ is null there and $\mathscr I^+$ is a null hypersurface. The radial null vectors are $k_1 = -\partial_x$ ($u$ constant: outgoing light, toward $\mathscr I^+$) and $k_2 = \partial_u + \tfrac12x^2(1 - 2Mx)\partial_x$ (ingoing, $dr/du = -f/2$). Both are future-directed: $\partial_u = \partial_t$ is future-directed timelike for $0 < x < 1/2M$, and $\tilde g(\partial_u, k_1) = -1 < 0$, $\tilde g(\partial_u, k_2) = -\tfrac12x^2(1 - 2Mx) < 0$ (checked by script). For a past-directed causal vector $W$, pairing with the future null $k_1$ gives $\tilde g(W, k_1) = -W^u \ge 0$, so $W^u \le 0$; and $\tilde g(W, W) \le 0$ reads
>
> $$
> 2W^uW^x \le x^2(1 - 2Mx)(W^u)^2 - (\text{angular part}) \le x^2(W^u)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad W^x \ge \tfrac12x^2\,W^u \quad (W^u < 0),
> $$
>
> while $W^u = 0$ forces $W = W^x\partial_x$ with $W^x > 0$. Along a past-directed causal curve with parameter $\sigma$ this gives $d(1/x)/d\sigma = -W^x/x^2 \le -\tfrac12W^u = -\tfrac12\,du/d\sigma$, so
>
> $$
> r(\sigma) = \frac{1}{x(\sigma)} \le r(0) + \tfrac12\bigl(u(0) - u(\sigma)\bigr) .
> $$
>
> A curve meeting $\mathscr I^+$ at finite $u$ would need $r \to \infty$ at finite $u$: impossible. Going to the past, $r$ grows at most like $|u|/2$, and reaches infinity only as $u \to -\infty$, i.e. on $\mathscr I^-$ (figure below). $\mathscr I^-$ is attached the same way with $v$: $\Omega^2(-f\,dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2) = -x^2(1 - 2Mx)\,dv^2 - 2\,dv\,dx + d\Omega^2$ (checked by script), the time reverse $u \to -v$; the two pieces lie in different charts and do not meet.
>
> **Step 6** (condition 4). The normal is $n^\mu = \tilde g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu x$: $n = \partial_u + x^2(1 - 2Mx)\partial_x$, which is $\partial_u$ on $\mathscr I^+$. Since $\tilde g$ does not depend on $u$, $\tilde\Gamma_{\nu uu} = \partial_u\tilde g_{\nu u} - \tfrac12\partial_\nu\tilde g_{uu} = -\tfrac12\partial_\nu\tilde g_{uu}$, nonzero only for $\nu = x$:
>
> $$
> \tilde\Gamma_{xuu} = -\tfrac12\,\partial_x\bigl(-x^2 + 2Mx^3\bigr) = x - 3Mx^2 \;\xrightarrow{\ x = 0\ }\; 0 ,
> $$
>
> and $n^\alpha\partial_\alpha n^\mu = 0$ at $x = 0$ (there $n^x = 0$ and $\partial_u n = 0$). So $\tilde\nabla_nn = 0$ on $\mathscr I^+$ (checked by script): the generators $u \mapsto (u, 0, \theta_0, \varphi_0)$ are affinely parametrized null geodesics of $\tilde g$ with $u \in \mathbf R$. $\mathscr I^+$ is complete ([[#^def-c10-4-3|Def. §C10.4.3]]), and so is $\mathscr I^-$ with $v$.
>
> **Step 7** (Kruskal). $\Omega = 1/r$ is smooth and positive on all of $\mathcal K$, where $r > 0$. Attaching the $\mathscr I^\pm$ of region I (Steps 3–6) satisfies [[#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]]; region III, a second exterior ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], part 2), is simply not given a boundary. Attaching both ends would make $\partial M$ four pieces, against condition 2.
>
> *Result.* The Schwarzschild exterior, Kruskal (with one end chosen) and, for $M = 0$, Minkowski space are asymptotically flat at null infinity with $\Omega = 1/r$; $\mathscr I^+$ is the null surface $x = 0$, ruled by complete generators labelled by retarded time.
>
> *Source: definition from Couzens, GR2, §4.2, Definitions 3–4, eq. (4.32), pp. 73–74 (PDF 74–75); the verification is worked here · checked by script (`verify_c10_4.py`)*
