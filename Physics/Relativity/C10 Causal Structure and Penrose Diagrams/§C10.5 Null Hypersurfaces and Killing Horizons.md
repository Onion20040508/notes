The chapter asks what a black hole is when no coordinates are allowed. Two surfaces met so far are candidates for "horizon". The Schwarzschild surface $r = 2M$ has a null normal $dr$, the raised normal $\partial_v$ lies in the surface, and its integral curves are light rays that keep their radius ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]], parts 1–2). The Rindler horizon of flat spacetime has the same look: a null plane on which the boost field becomes null ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-3|Theorem §C10.2.3]]), and near $r = 2M$ Schwarzschild is Rindler space times a sphere with $\kappa = 1/4M$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-4|Theorem §C10.2.4]]). Both surfaces are null hypersurfaces on which a Killing vector turns null. This section turns these observations into definitions. Three naive descriptions fail, and the computations show why.

1. *"A horizon is where $g_{tt}$ vanishes."* In Kruskal coordinates the metric is $F(r)(-dT^2 + dX^2) + r^2d\Omega^2$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]]), and at $r = 2M$ the coefficient is $g_{TT} = -F(2M) = -16M^2/e = -5.886\,M^2$. No component of $g_{\mu\nu}$ vanishes on the horizon $U = 0$. What vanishes is the norm of its normal one-form, $g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu U\,\partial_\nu U = g^{UU} = 0$. Being null is a property of the surface's normal, not of a metric component.
2. *"The gravity of a hole is the acceleration needed to hover near it."* A static observer at radius $r$ needs the proper acceleration $|a| = M/(r^2\sqrt{1 - 2M/r})$ ([[#^thm-c10-5-4|Theorem §C10.5.4]], part 3). In units $1/M$ this is $0.19$ at $r = 3M$, $1.04$ at $2.1M$, $3.51$ at $2.01M$, and it diverges at the horizon. The divergence describes a hovering rocket, not the hole.
3. *"The generators are light rays, so the Killing field along them obeys the geodesic equation."* On $r = 2M$ the Killing vector $\xi = \partial_v$ is tangent to the light rays of the horizon, yet $\nabla_\xi\xi = \xi/4M \neq 0$ ([[#^thm-c10-5-4|Theorem §C10.5.4]]). This looks like a contradiction with [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]].

The section builds the tools in order. Hypersurfaces and their normal one-forms come first ([[#^def-c10-5-1|Def. §C10.5.1]]); the causal type of a hypersurface is read off the normal ([[#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]]); Frobenius' theorem says which one-forms are normal to hypersurfaces at all ([[#^thm-c10-5-1|Theorem §C10.5.1]], stated). On a null hypersurface the normal is tangent ([[#^thm-c10-5-2|Theorem §C10.5.2]]), and its integral curves are null geodesics in a parametrization that need not be affine ([[#^thm-c10-5-3|Theorem §C10.5.3]]): this resolves attempt 3. A null hypersurface normal to a Killing vector is a Killing horizon ([[#^def-c10-5-3|Def. §C10.5.3]]). The failure of the Killing parameter to be affine there is a number, the surface gravity ([[#^def-c10-5-4|Def. §C10.5.4]]). For Schwarzschild it is $1/4M$ and equals the hovering acceleration of attempt 2 multiplied by the redshift factor ([[#^thm-c10-5-4|Theorem §C10.5.4]]). In Kruskal coordinates the affine parameter along the horizon is $V = e^{\kappa v}$ ([[#^thm-c10-5-5|Theorem §C10.5.5]]).

**Setup.** A spacetime $(M, g)$: a time-oriented Lorentzian manifold of dimension $n$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]), $n = 4$ in the examples, with its Levi-Civita connection ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-1|Theorem §C3.6.1]]). Hypersurfaces are smooth embedded submanifolds of dimension $n - 1$. All statements are local, near a point of the hypersurface, except where a whole spacetime is named. The Schwarzschild examples use the ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein chart, regular for all $r > 0$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]), and the Kruskal chart ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]]). Results are exact; the numbers were checked by script.

## Hypersurfaces and their normals

A surface in spacetime is specified most simply by one equation, $f = c$. Differentiating the equation along the surface gives the object that describes the surface's orientation: a one-form, not a vector. No metric is needed for it.

> [!definition] Definition §C10.5.1: Hypersurface; Normal One-Form
> *Structure.* A **hypersurface** of the $n$-dimensional manifold $M$ is an embedded submanifold $\Sigma \subset M$ of dimension $n - 1$ (a regular submanifold of codimension $1$, MATH 591 §37). Near each of its points it is a **regular level set** (in an adapted chart, $x^n = 0$): there is a smooth function $f$ on an open set $O$ with
>
> $$
> \Sigma \cap O = \{p \in O : f(p) = c\}, \qquad df_p \neq 0 \ \text{ for } p \in \Sigma \cap O .
> $$
>
> Conversely, every such level set is a hypersurface, by the regular value theorem ([[§38 The Regular Value Theorem for Manifolds#^thm-38-2|591 Thm. §38.2]]).
>
> *Normal one-form.* At $p \in \Sigma$, a **normal one-form** is a nonzero covector $n_p \in T_p^{\ast}M$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-5|Def. §C2.2.5]]) that vanishes on every tangent vector of $\Sigma$:
>
> $$
> n_p(X) = n_\mu X^\mu = 0 \qquad \text{for all } X \in T_p\Sigma .
> $$
>
> The covectors vanishing on $T_p\Sigma$ form a one-dimensional space spanned by $df_p$ ([[§39 Transversality and Lagrange Multipliers#^prop-39-7|591 Prop. §39.7]], with $k = 1$), so the normal one-forms at $p$ are exactly the multiples $h\,df_p$, $h \neq 0$. That $df$ vanishes on tangent vectors is seen with a curve $\gamma$ in $\Sigma$: $f(\gamma(\lambda)) = c$ for all $\lambda$, so $0 = \frac{d}{d\lambda}f(\gamma(\lambda)) = \dot\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu f = df(\dot\gamma)$ (the differential, [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]]). A **normal one-form field** on $\Sigma \cap O$ is $n = h\,df$ with $h$ smooth and nowhere zero on $\Sigma$.
>
> *Normal vector.* With the metric, $n^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}n_\nu$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]) is a **normal vector**: $g(n, X) = n_\mu X^\mu = 0$ for every tangent vector $X$. Couzens calls the raised $df$ hypersurface-orthogonal; for vector fields this is [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-3|Def. §C3.13.3]], part 2.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.4, Definitions 8–9, eq. (4.41) and footnote 16, pp. 77–78 (PDF 78–79) · Hartle, Gravity, §7.9, eqs. (7.66)–(7.68), pp. 158–159 (PDF 159–160) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §5.6, eq. (5.22), p. 73 (PDF 88) (normal co-vector); §15.2, eqs. (15.3)–(15.6), p. 270 (PDF 285)*

In Euclidean space a normal vector is perpendicular to the surface and points out of it. In a Lorentzian metric "perpendicular" includes the vector itself when it is null. The causal type of the normal therefore carries the information about the surface.

> [!definition] Definition §C10.5.2: Spacelike, Timelike and Null Hypersurfaces
> *Structure.* Let $\Sigma$ be a hypersurface of a Lorentzian manifold with normal vector $n_p$ at $p$ ([[#^def-c10-5-1|Def. §C10.5.1]]). $\Sigma$ is, at $p$,
> - **spacelike** if $n_p$ is timelike, $g^{\mu\nu}n_\mu n_\nu < 0$;
> - **timelike** if $n_p$ is spacelike, $g^{\mu\nu}n_\mu n_\nu > 0$;
> - **null** if $n_p$ is null, $g^{\mu\nu}n_\mu n_\nu = 0$
>
> (vector types: [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]). The type does not depend on the normal chosen, since $g(hn, hn) = h^2g(n, n)$ with $h^2 > 0$. $\Sigma$ is a spacelike, timelike or null hypersurface if it has that type at every point. For a regular level set, the test is the sign of $g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu f\,\partial_\nu f$ on $\Sigma$.
>
> The names are those of the tangent vectors, not of the normal: on a spacelike hypersurface every tangent vector is spacelike, on a timelike one some are timelike, and on a null one the induced metric is degenerate. So this definition agrees with the one by the induced metric in [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]] ([[#^thm-c10-5-2|Theorem §C10.5.2]], part 3). In Minkowski spacetime: $t = \text{const}$ is spacelike (normal $dt$, norm $-1$), $x = \text{const}$ is timelike (normal $dx$, norm $+1$), and the future light cone $t = r$ is null (normal $dt - dr$, norm $-1 + 1 = 0$; [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-3|Example §C3.1.3]], part (d)).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.4, Definition 10, p. 78 (PDF 79) · Hartle, Gravity, §7.9, Examples 7.11–7.13, eqs. (7.69)–(7.81), pp. 159–163 (PDF 160–164) (spacelike surfaces have timelike normals; a normal to a null surface is a null vector lying in it)*

^def-c10-5-2

Not every one-form field is the normal of a family of hypersurfaces. A one-form gives at each point a hyperplane, its kernel, and these hyperplanes may twist so that no surface is tangent to all of them ([[#^ex-c10-5-5|Example §C10.5.5]] computes such a field). The test is a single equation between differential forms.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.5.1: Frobenius (Stated): n ∧ dn = 0 ⇔ n = g df Locally
> Let $n$ be a smooth one-form on an open set $W \subseteq M$, nonzero at every point. The following are equivalent.
> 1. $n \wedge dn = 0$ on $W$: the three-form built from the wedge product ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]]) and the exterior derivative ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]]) vanishes.
> 2. Every point of $W$ has a neighbourhood $O$ with smooth functions $f$ and $g$ on $O$ (here $g$ is a function, not the metric), $g \neq 0$ and $df \neq 0$, such that
>
> $$
> n = g\,df \qquad \text{on } O .
> $$
>
>    Then the kernel of $n$ at each point is the tangent space of the level set $f = \text{const}$ through it. $n$ is a normal one-form of each of these hypersurfaces ([[#^def-c10-5-1|Def. §C10.5.1]]), and the raised $n^\mu$ is hypersurface-orthogonal ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-3|Def. §C3.13.3]], part 2).
>
> In a chart, $n \wedge dn = 0$ is the vanishing of the components $3\,n_{[\mu}(dn)_{\nu\rho]}$ (by [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]] with $q = 1$, $r = 2$), with $(dn)_{\nu\rho} = \partial_\nu n_\rho - \partial_\rho n_\nu$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1). For a vector field $X$, d'Inverno & Vickers state the condition as $X_{[a}\nabla_bX_{c]} = 0$.
>
> *Model:* none (smooth manifolds; no metric needed for parts 1–2); *applies:* locally, near each point of $W$. The functions $f$, $g$ need not exist on all of $W$.
>
> *Proof:* not given here; to be filled after the MATH 591 homework. The theorem is the one-form version of the Frobenius theorem (Lee, *Introduction to Smooth Manifolds*, ch. 19). MATH 591 proves only the necessary condition on brackets for integral submanifolds ([[§59 Related Vector Fields#^cor-59-9|591 Cor. §59.9]]) and names the Frobenius theorem as beyond the course.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.4, Aside, eq. (4.42), and Theorem 2, p. 78 (PDF 79) (stated) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §15.2, eqs. (15.7)–(15.9), p. 271 (PDF 286) (the condition in components)*

## Null hypersurfaces

On a null hypersurface the normal is orthogonal to itself, so it is one of its own tangent vectors. This is the fact the Schwarzschild computation showed ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]], part 1). The next theorem shows that it holds on every null hypersurface and describes all tangent vectors there.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.5.2: On a Null Hypersurface the Normal Is Tangent, and Every Other Tangent Vector Is Spacelike
> Let $\Sigma$ be a hypersurface of a Lorentzian manifold of dimension $n$ and $n_p$ a normal vector at $p \in \Sigma$ ([[#^def-c10-5-1|Def. §C10.5.1]]).
> 1. **Tangent space.** $T_p\Sigma = n_p^{\perp} \equiv \{X \in T_pM : g(n_p, X) = 0\}$.
> 2. **Null case.** If $\Sigma$ is null at $p$ ([[#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]]), then $n_p \in T_p\Sigma$. Every $X \in T_p\Sigma$ that is not a multiple of $n_p$ is spacelike. The null tangent vectors are exactly the nonzero multiples of $n_p$.
> 3. **Induced metric.** The induced metric $h = \iota^{\ast}g$ on $T_p\Sigma$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]) is positive definite if $n_p$ is timelike, Lorentzian if $n_p$ is spacelike, and degenerate if $n_p$ is null. In the null case it is positive semidefinite with the kernel spanned by $n_p$. So the types of [[#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]] and of [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]] agree.
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* at every point of every hypersurface, exactly.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.4, p. 80 (PDF 81) ("either $X^\mu$ is spacelike or $X^\mu$ is parallel to $n^\mu$") · Hartle, Gravity, §7.9, eq. (7.79), p. 162 (PDF 163) · Carroll, §6.3, p. 244 (PDF 257) ("a null surface cannot have two linearly independent null tangent vectors")*

^thm-c10-5-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (tangent space). $T_p\Sigma$ has dimension $n - 1$ and lies in the kernel of $n_\mu$ ([[#^def-c10-5-1|Def. §C10.5.1]]). The kernel of a nonzero covector on the $n$-dimensional $T_pM$ has dimension $n - 1$. A subspace of the same finite dimension is the whole space, so $T_p\Sigma = \{X : n_\mu X^\mu = 0\}$. Since $n_\mu X^\mu = g_{\mu\nu}n^\mu X^\nu = g(n_p, X)$, this is part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (null normal is tangent). If $n_p$ is null, $g(n_p, n_p) = 0$, so $n_p \in n_p^{\perp} = T_p\Sigma$ by Step 1.
>
> **Step 3** (an adapted orthonormal basis). Take an orthonormal basis $e_0, \dots, e_{n-1}$ of $T_pM$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]) and write $n_p = a\,e_0 + \vec b$, with $\vec b$ in the span of $e_1, \dots, e_{n-1}$. Null means $-a^2 + |\vec b|^2 = 0$, so $|\vec b| = |a| \neq 0$. Rotate the spatial vectors $e_1, \dots, e_{n-1}$ among themselves, which keeps the basis orthonormal, so that $\vec b = b\,e_{n-1}$ with $b > 0$. Then $b = |a|$, and after replacing $n_p$ by $n_p/a$ (allowed by [[#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]]) we may take $n_p = e_0 + \epsilon\,e_{n-1}$ with $\epsilon = \pm1$.
>
> **Step 4** (tangent vectors in the null case). A vector $X = X^0e_0 + \dots + X^{n-1}e_{n-1}$ is tangent iff
>
> $$
> 0 = g(n_p, X) = -X^0 + \epsilon X^{n-1} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad X^{n-1} = \epsilon X^0 .
> $$
>
> So $X = X^0(e_0 + \epsilon e_{n-1}) + \sum_{i=1}^{n-2}X^ie_i = X^0n_p + Y$, with $Y$ in the span of $e_1, \dots, e_{n-2}$. Its norm is
>
> $$
> g(X, X) = (X^0)^2g(n_p, n_p) + 2X^0g(n_p, Y) + g(Y, Y) = 0 + 0 + \sum_{i=1}^{n-2}(X^i)^2 \ \ge 0 ,
> $$
>
> because $g(n_p, e_i) = 0$ for $1 \le i \le n - 2$. The norm is zero only if $Y = 0$, that is, only if $X$ is a multiple of $n_p$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (induced metric, null case). In the basis $n_p, e_1, \dots, e_{n-2}$ of $T_p\Sigma$, Step 4 gives $h = \operatorname{diag}(0, 1, \dots, 1)$: positive semidefinite, with the kernel spanned by $n_p$, and $\det h = 0$.
>
> **Step 6** (induced metric, non-null cases). If $g(n_p, n_p) \neq 0$, then $n_p \notin n_p^{\perp}$, so $T_pM = \operatorname{span}(n_p) \oplus T_p\Sigma$, and the two summands are $g$-orthogonal. In a basis made of $n_p$ and a basis of $T_p\Sigma$, the matrix of $g$ is block diagonal, $\operatorname{diag}\bigl(g(n_p, n_p), h\bigr)$. By Sylvester's law of inertia the numbers of negative and positive eigenvalues add up block by block ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], part 4). The total is one negative and $n - 1$ positive. If $g(n_p, n_p) < 0$, the negative one is used up and $h$ is positive definite; if $g(n_p, n_p) > 0$, $h$ has one negative and $n - 2$ positive eigenvalues, so it is Lorentzian. This is part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** A null hypersurface is foliated, at each point, by one distinguished direction: the normal, which is null and tangent. All other directions in it are spacelike. A null hypersurface is therefore "made of" its null direction and a spacelike $(n - 2)$-dimensional cross-section, a sphere for $r = 2M$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]], part 3).

*Uses:* [[#^def-c10-5-1|Def. §C10.5.1]], [[#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]], [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]], [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]], [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]

The normal field of a null hypersurface is tangent to it, so its integral curves run inside the surface. These curves are null; they turn out to be geodesics, although in general not affinely parametrized. Attempt 3 of the opening found exactly this: $\nabla_\xi\xi \propto \xi$, not $0$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.5.3: The Integral Curves of the Normal Are Null Geodesics
> Let $\mathcal N$ be a null hypersurface ([[#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]]) and $n$ a normal vector field on $\mathcal N$, smooth and nowhere zero ([[#^def-c10-5-1|Def. §C10.5.1]]).
> 1. **Pre-geodesic equation.** There is a function $\kappa_n$ on $\mathcal N$ with
>
> $$
> n^\nu\nabla_\nu n^\mu = \kappa_n\,n^\mu \qquad \text{on } \mathcal N .
> $$
>
>    It depends on the choice of normal: for $n' = h\,n$, $\kappa_{n'} = h\,\kappa_n + n(h)$, where $n(h) = n^\nu\partial_\nu h$.
> 2. **Generators.** The integral curves of $n$ that start on $\mathcal N$ stay in $\mathcal N$. They are null geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]), parametrized non-affinely when $\kappa_n \neq 0$, and an affine parameter is obtained from [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 2. These null geodesics are the **generators** of $\mathcal N$; through each point of $\mathcal N$ passes exactly one.
> 3. **Affine case.** If $\mathcal N$ is a level set $f = c$ of a function whose gradient $N = df$ is null on a whole open neighbourhood of $\mathcal N$, so that the nearby level sets are null too, then $N^\nu\nabla_\nu N^\mu = 0$: the integral curves of $N$ are affinely parametrized. The wavefronts of a short-wavelength light wave are such level sets, since the eikonal equation makes the gradient of the phase null on an open set; their generators are the light rays, affinely parametrized null geodesics ([[§C4.6 Geometric Optics꞉ Light Follows Null Geodesics#^thm-c4-6-3|Theorem §C4.6.3]]).
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* every null hypersurface of a Lorentzian manifold with its Levi-Civita connection, exactly. Part 2 uses only the values of $n$ on $\mathcal N$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.4, Proposition and proof, eqs. (4.55)–(4.56), footnote 17, pp. 80–81 (PDF 81–82) · Hartle, Gravity, Problems 20.15 and 20.19, pp. 443–444 (PDF 442–443) (see [[#^cau-c10-5-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens' §4.4, and the sign of κ in other books]]) · symmetry of the Hessian and the identity of Step 4 checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (a gradient normal). Near a point of $\mathcal N$ write $\mathcal N = \{f = c\}$ with $df \neq 0$ ([[#^def-c10-5-1|Def. §C10.5.1]]) and set $N_\mu = \partial_\mu f$, $N^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}N_\nu$. On $\mathcal N$ every normal is a multiple, $n = h\,N$ with $h \neq 0$.
>
> **Step 2** (the norm of $N$ has a normal gradient). $\Phi \equiv g^{\mu\nu}N_\mu N_\nu$ is a smooth function near $\mathcal N$ and vanishes on $\mathcal N$, which is null. So $\Phi$ is constant ($= 0$) along every curve in $\mathcal N$, and, as for $df$ in [[#^def-c10-5-1|Def. §C10.5.1]], $d\Phi_p$ vanishes on $T_p\mathcal N$. A covector vanishing on $T_p\mathcal N$ is a multiple of $N_p$, possibly zero. Hence there is a function $\alpha$ on $\mathcal N$ with
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu\Phi = 2\alpha\,N_\mu \qquad \text{on } \mathcal N .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (differentiate the norm). The connection is metric ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^def-c3-6-2|Def. §C3.6.2]]), so raising commutes with $\nabla$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-5|Theorem §C3.6.5]]) and, $\Phi$ being a scalar,
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu\Phi = \nabla_\mu(N^\nu N_\nu) = (\nabla_\mu N^\nu)N_\nu + N^\nu\nabla_\mu N_\nu = 2N^\nu\nabla_\mu N_\nu .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (the Hessian is symmetric). With the covariant derivative of a one-form ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]]),
>
> $$
> \nabla_\mu N_\nu = \nabla_\mu\partial_\nu f = \partial_\mu\partial_\nu f - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\lambda f .
> $$
>
> Partial derivatives commute, and $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu}$ because the Levi-Civita connection is torsion-free ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^def-c3-6-1|Def. §C3.6.1]]). So $\nabla_\mu N_\nu = \nabla_\nu N_\mu$, and Steps 2–3 give
>
> $$
> N^\nu\nabla_\nu N_\mu = N^\nu\nabla_\mu N_\nu = \tfrac12\,\partial_\mu\Phi = \alpha\,N_\mu \qquad \text{on } \mathcal N .
> $$
>
> Raising the free index: $N^\nu\nabla_\nu N^\mu = \alpha\,N^\mu$. This is part 1 for $n = N$, with $\kappa_N = \alpha$.
>
> **Step 5** (only values on $\mathcal N$ enter). For vector fields $X$, $Y$, $(\nabla_XY)^\mu = X^\nu\partial_\nu Y^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\lambda}X^\nu Y^\lambda$. At $p \in \mathcal N$ with $X_p$ tangent to $\mathcal N$, $X^\nu\partial_\nu Y^\mu$ is the derivative of $Y^\mu$ along a curve in $\mathcal N$ with velocity $X_p$, so only the values of $Y$ on $\mathcal N$ matter. By [[#^thm-c10-5-2|Theorem §C10.5.2]], part 2, $n$ and $N$ are tangent to $\mathcal N$, so $n^\nu\nabla_\nu n^\mu$ on $\mathcal N$ is determined by $n|_{\mathcal N} = h\,N|_{\mathcal N}$.
>
> **Step 6** (any normal). With $n = hN$ on $\mathcal N$ and the Leibniz rule,
>
> $$
> n^\nu\nabla_\nu n^\mu = h\,N^\nu\nabla_\nu(h\,N^\mu) = h\,(N^\nu\partial_\nu h)\,N^\mu + h^2\alpha\,N^\mu = \bigl(N(h) + h\,\alpha\bigr)\,n^\mu .
> $$
>
> So part 1 holds with $\kappa_n = N(h) + h\alpha$. The same computation with $n' = h\,n$ in place of $n = h\,N$ gives $n'^\nu\nabla_\nu n'^\mu = \bigl(n(h) + h\kappa_n\bigr)\,n'^\mu$, the transformation rule.
>
> **Step 7** (integral curves). Since $n$ is tangent to $\mathcal N$, its restriction is a smooth vector field on the submanifold $\mathcal N$ ([[§59 Related Vector Fields#^prop-59-6|591 Prop. §59.6]]). Its integral curve in $\mathcal N$ through $p$ exists ([[§68 Local Existence and Uniqueness#^thm-68-2|591 Thm. §68.2]]) and is also an integral curve of $n$ in $M$; by uniqueness, the integral curve of $n$ through $p$ in $M$ stays in $\mathcal N$. Along such a curve $\gamma(s)$ with $\dot\gamma = n$, part 1 reads $D\dot\gamma/ds = \kappa_n\,\dot\gamma$. This is the equation of a non-affinely parametrized geodesic, and [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 2, gives the affine parameter
>
> $$
> \lambda(s) = \int_{s_0}^{s}\exp\Bigl(\int_{s_0}^{s'}\kappa_n\bigl(\gamma(s'')\bigr)\,ds''\Bigr)ds' .
> $$
>
> The curve is null because $n$ is null on $\mathcal N$. One generator passes through each point because integral curves are unique. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 8** (affine case). If $\Phi = 0$ on an open neighbourhood, then $\partial_\mu\Phi = 0$ there, and Step 4 gives $N^\nu\nabla_\nu N_\mu = \tfrac12\partial_\mu\Phi = 0$. This is part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The only inputs are that $\Phi = N\cdot N$ vanishes on the surface and that second covariant derivatives of a function are symmetric. Attempt 3 of the opening is resolved: the light rays of a null hypersurface are geodesics, and a normal field moves along them with a speed that is in general not constant in any affine parameter. The factor $\kappa_n$ depends on how the normal is scaled ([[#^ex-c10-5-4|Example §C10.5.4]]).

## Killing horizons and surface gravity

The normal of a null hypersurface is fixed only up to a factor, and $\kappa_n$ changes with it. A symmetry removes the freedom. If a Killing vector ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-2|Def. §C3.13.2]]) happens to be normal to the surface, it is a preferred normal, determined by the geometry up to one constant factor.

> [!definition] Definition §C10.5.3: Killing Horizon
> *Structure.* Let $\xi$ be a Killing vector field on $(M, g)$ ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-2|Def. §C3.13.2]]) and $\mathcal N$ a null hypersurface ([[#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]]). $\mathcal N$ is a **Killing horizon** of $\xi$ if, at every point of $\mathcal N$, $\xi$ is nonzero and normal to $\mathcal N$ ([[#^def-c10-5-1|Def. §C10.5.1]]): $\xi_\mu = h\,n_\mu$ for a normal one-form $n$ and a function $h \neq 0$ on $\mathcal N$.
>
> Equivalently, $\xi$ is nonzero, null and tangent to $\mathcal N$: a normal of a null hypersurface is null and tangent, and a null tangent vector is a multiple of the normal ([[#^thm-c10-5-2|Theorem §C10.5.2]], part 2). The integral curves of $\xi$ on $\mathcal N$ are then the generators ([[#^thm-c10-5-3|Theorem §C10.5.3]], part 2), with the Killing parameter as their parameter. Points where $\xi = 0$, such as the bifurcation sphere of Kruskal ([[#^thm-c10-5-5|Theorem §C10.5.5]], part 4), are not part of the Killing horizon.
>
> A Killing horizon is a local, geometric notion. It need not bound a black hole: the Killing horizons of the boost field in Minkowski spacetime are the null planes $x = \pm t$ ([[#^ex-c10-5-2|Example §C10.5.2]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.4, Definition 11, p. 79 (PDF 80) · Carroll, §6.3, eqs. (6.5)–(6.7), pp. 244–245 (PDF 257–258)*

^def-c10-5-3

> [!definition] Definition §C10.5.4: Surface Gravity
> *Structure.* Let $\mathcal N$ be a Killing horizon of $\xi$ ([[#^def-c10-5-3|Def. §C10.5.3]]). Since $\xi$ is a normal vector field on $\mathcal N$, [[#^thm-c10-5-3|Theorem §C10.5.3]], part 1, gives a function $\kappa$ on $\mathcal N$, the **surface gravity**, with
>
> $$
> \xi^\mu\nabla_\mu\xi^\nu = \kappa\,\xi^\nu \qquad \text{on } \mathcal N .
> $$
>
> *Normalization.* For a constant $c \neq 0$ the field $c\,\xi$ is also Killing, with the same horizon, and its surface gravity is $c\kappa$ (transformation rule of [[#^thm-c10-5-3|Theorem §C10.5.3]], part 1, with $h = c$, $n(h) = 0$). A number needs a normalization. In an asymptotically flat spacetime ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]]) whose Killing vector is timelike near infinity, $\xi$ is normalized by $\xi\cdot\xi \to -1$ at infinity ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-3|Def. §C3.13.3]], part 1): its parameter is then the proper time of the static observers far away. Without such a region, as for Rindler, $\kappa$ is fixed only up to the constant factor ([[#^ex-c10-5-2|Example §C10.5.2]]). In Kerr the horizon generator is spacelike far away, and its normalization is fixed by its $\partial_t$ part ([[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-3|Theorem §C12.2.3]], part 1); with that normalization $\kappa$ is the coefficient of the area in the first law for Kerr ([[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^thm-c12-5-5|Theorem §C12.5.5]]).
>
> *Failure of affine parametrization.* Along a generator, with the Killing parameter $v$ ($dx^\mu/dv = \xi^\mu$) and $\kappa$ constant on it ([[#^rem-c10-5-1|Remark: Constancy of κ]]), the affine parameter of [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 2, is
>
> $$
> \lambda = \int_{0}^{v}e^{\kappa v'}\,dv' = \frac{e^{\kappa v} - 1}{\kappa} \quad (\kappa \neq 0), \qquad \lambda = v \quad (\kappa = 0) .
> $$
>
> So $\kappa$ measures how far the Killing parameter is from affine. For $\kappa > 0$ the whole range $v \in \mathbf R$ covers only $\lambda > -1/\kappa$. Where the spacetime extends, the generator continues beyond $\lambda = -1/\kappa$; in Kruskal it reaches the bifurcation sphere, where $\xi = 0$ ([[#^thm-c10-5-5|Theorem §C10.5.5]]). A Killing horizon with $\kappa = 0$ is called **degenerate**.
>
> *Sign.* $\kappa$ changes sign with $\xi$. With $\xi$ future-directed on the future horizon of a black hole, $\kappa > 0$; on a past horizon the same $\xi$ gives $\kappa < 0$ ([[#^thm-c10-5-5|Theorem §C10.5.5]], part 4).
>
> *Name.* For a static black hole $\kappa$ is the acceleration needed to hover just outside the horizon, multiplied by the redshift factor to infinity ([[#^thm-c10-5-4|Theorem §C10.5.4]], part 3).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.4, eq. (4.44), p. 79 (PDF 80) · Carroll, §6.3, eqs. (6.8), (6.10), pp. 245–246 (PDF 258–259) (with $-\kappa$) · Hartle, Gravity, Problem 20.16, p. 443 (PDF 442) (with $-\kappa$)*

Now the Schwarzschild horizon, computed in the chart that covers it. The computation is Couzens' Example 4.6, carried out away from the horizon as well. Off the horizon the same quantity $\nabla_\xi\xi$ is the hovering acceleration of attempt 2, so one formula answers both attempts.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.5.4: The Schwarzschild Horizon Is a Killing Horizon of ∂_t with κ = 1/4M
> In ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]), let $\xi = \partial_v$, which is the Schwarzschild $\partial_t$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-2|Def. §C7.2.2]]), and $n = g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu r\,\partial_\mu = \partial_v + f\,\partial_r$.
> 1. **Killing horizon.** $\xi$ is a Killing vector with $\xi\cdot\xi = -f \to -1$ at infinity. The hypersurface $H = \{r = 2M\}$ is null, and on it $\xi = n$: $H$ is a Killing horizon of $\xi$ ([[#^def-c10-5-3|Def. §C10.5.3]]).
> 2. **Surface gravity.** At every $r > 0$,
>
> $$
> \nabla_\xi\xi = \frac{M}{r^2}\bigl(\partial_v + f\,\partial_r\bigr) = \frac{M}{r^2}\,n ,
> $$
>
>    so on $H$, $\nabla_\xi\xi = \xi/4M$ and
>
> $$
> \kappa = \frac{1}{4M} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \kappa = \frac{c^4}{4GM}\ \text{as an acceleration}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
>    the same at every point of $H$. It agrees with the Rindler factor of the near-horizon metric ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-4|Theorem §C10.2.4]]).
> 3. **⚑ Surface gravity as redshifted hovering acceleration.** A static observer at $r > 2M$ ([[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay#^def-c6-6-1|Def. §C6.6.1]]) has the proper acceleration
>
> $$
> a = \frac{M}{r^2f}\,n, \qquad |a| = \frac{M}{r^2\sqrt{1 - 2M/r}} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } |a| = \frac{GM}{r^2\sqrt{1 - 2GM/c^2r}}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
>    which diverges at $r = 2M$, while the product with the redshift factor $\sqrt{-\xi\cdot\xi} = \sqrt f$ stays finite:
>
> $$
> \sqrt{-\xi\cdot\xi}\;|a| = \frac{M}{r^2} \;\xrightarrow{\;r \to 2M\;}\; \frac{1}{4M} = \kappa .
> $$
>
>    The same limit was found from the near-horizon Rindler form ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-4|Theorem §C10.2.4]], part 4); here it is exact for every $r > 2M$ before the limit.
>
> *Model:* the vacuum Schwarzschild metric, $M > 0$, in the Eddington–Finkelstein extension; *applies:* exactly; part 3 for $r > 2M$. For a black hole formed by collapse, on the part of the horizon outside the matter.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.4, Example 4.6, eqs. (4.45)–(4.54), pp. 79–80 (PDF 80–81) · Carroll, §6.3, eqs. (6.11)–(6.24), pp. 246–248 (PDF 259–261) (the redshifted acceleration) · Hartle, Gravity, Problem 20.16, p. 443 (PDF 442) · every Christoffel symbol, $\nabla_\xi\xi$, $|a|$ and the limit checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Killing vector and normalization). No component of the Eddington–Finkelstein metric $-f\,dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2$ depends on $v$, so $\partial_v$ is a Killing vector ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-1|Theorem §C3.13.1]], part 3). Its norm is $g_{vv} = -f$, and $-f = -1 + 2M/r \to -1$ as $r \to \infty$.
>
> **Step 2** (the horizon is null, and $\xi$ is its normal). $H$ is the level set $r = 2M$ of $r$, with $dr \neq 0$. The inverse metric has $g^{vv} = 0$, $g^{vr} = 1$, $g^{rr} = f$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]], part 2), so
>
> $$
> g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu r\,\partial_\nu r = g^{rr} = f, \qquad n^\mu = g^{\mu r} = (1, f, 0, 0) .
> $$
>
> At $r = 2M$, $f = 0$: $H$ is null ([[#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]]) and $n = \partial_v = \xi \neq 0$ there. Part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^\nu{}_{vv}$). From [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]],
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\nu{}_{vv} = \tfrac12 g^{\nu\sigma}\bigl(\partial_vg_{\sigma v} + \partial_vg_{\sigma v} - \partial_\sigma g_{vv}\bigr) = -\tfrac12 g^{\nu\sigma}\partial_\sigma g_{vv} = -\tfrac12 g^{\nu r}\partial_r(-f) = \tfrac12 g^{\nu r}f' ,
> $$
>
> since nothing depends on $v$ and $g_{vv} = -f$ depends only on $r$. With $f' = 2M/r^2$:
>
> $$
> \Gamma^v{}_{vv} = \tfrac12\cdot 1\cdot\frac{2M}{r^2} = +\frac{M}{r^2}, \qquad \Gamma^r{}_{vv} = \tfrac12 f\cdot\frac{2M}{r^2} = \frac{fM}{r^2}, \qquad \Gamma^\theta{}_{vv} = \Gamma^\varphi{}_{vv} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> the last because $g^{\theta r} = g^{\varphi r} = 0$.
>
> **Step 4** ($\nabla_\xi\xi$). The components $\xi^\mu = \delta^\mu_v$ are constant, so
>
> $$
> (\nabla_\xi\xi)^\nu = \xi^\mu\partial_\mu\xi^\nu + \Gamma^\nu{}_{\mu\lambda}\xi^\mu\xi^\lambda = 0 + \Gamma^\nu{}_{vv} ,
> $$
>
> which is $\frac{M}{r^2}(1, f, 0, 0) = \frac{M}{r^2}\,n^\nu$ by Steps 2–3. At $r = 2M$: $n = \xi$ and $M/r^2 = 1/4M$, so $\nabla_\xi\xi = \xi/4M$ and $\kappa = 1/4M$ ([[#^def-c10-5-4|Def. §C10.5.4]]), with the normalization of Step 1. Restored, $M \to GM/c^2$ turns $1/4M$ into $c^2/(4GM)$ per length, i.e. the acceleration $c^4/(4GM)$. Part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (static observer). For $r > 2M$ the static observer has four-velocity $u = \xi/\sqrt f$, a unit timelike vector ($u\cdot u = -f/f = -1$). Its acceleration is
>
> $$
> a = \nabla_uu = \frac{1}{\sqrt f}\,\nabla_\xi\Bigl(\frac{\xi}{\sqrt f}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{f}\,\nabla_\xi\xi + \frac{1}{\sqrt f}\,\xi\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt f}\Bigr)\xi = \frac{1}{f}\,\nabla_\xi\xi = \frac{M}{r^2f}\,n ,
> $$
>
> because $\xi(f) = \partial_vf = 0$. Its norm, with $n\cdot n = g^{rr} = f$:
>
> $$
> |a|^2 = \Bigl(\frac{M}{r^2f}\Bigr)^2f = \frac{M^2}{r^4f}, \qquad |a| = \frac{M}{r^2\sqrt f} .
> $$
>
> Multiplying by $\sqrt{-\xi\cdot\xi} = \sqrt f$ gives $M/r^2$, which tends to $M/(2M)^2 = 1/4M$. Part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** One vector, $\nabla_\xi\xi = (M/r^2)\,n$, does all the work. Outside the horizon it is the hovering acceleration times $f$; on the horizon $n$ and $\xi$ coincide and the same vector becomes $\kappa\,\xi$. The divergent hovering acceleration of attempt 2 and the non-affine generators of attempt 3 are the same number, $1/4M$, read in two ways. The value is Couzens' (4.52)–(4.54), with the sign of $\Gamma^v{}_{vv}$ corrected ([[#^cau-c10-5-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens' §4.4, and the sign of κ in other books]]).

The Eddington–Finkelstein chart sees only the future horizon. The Kruskal chart sees both horizons and their crossing, and in it the horizon has an affinely parametrized normal. The Killing parameter and the affine parameter can therefore be compared directly.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.5.5: On the Kruskal Horizon U = 0, V Is an Affine Parameter of the Generators
> In the Kruskal spacetime ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]), with $\xi = \partial_t = \frac{1}{4M}(-U\partial_U + V\partial_V)$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], part 4):
> 1. **Null foliation.** $N = dU$ is null everywhere, so every hypersurface $U = \text{const}$ is null, with
>
> $$
> N^\mu\partial_\mu = -\frac{r\,e^{r/2M}}{16M^3}\,\partial_V, \qquad N^\nu\nabla_\nu N^\mu = 0 .
> $$
>
>    Its integral curves ($U$, $\theta$, $\varphi$ fixed) are affinely parametrized null geodesics.
> 2. **Affine parameter on the horizon.** On $U = 0$ ($r = 2M$), $N = -(e/8M^2)\,\partial_V$ with a constant factor, so $V$ is an affine parameter of the generators of $U = 0$. Likewise $U$ is an affine parameter of the generators of $V = 0$.
> 3. **⚑ Killing parameter versus affine parameter.** On $U = 0$, $V > 0$, $\xi = (V/4M)\,\partial_V$. Along a generator, the Killing parameter is the advanced time $v$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-2|Def. §C7.2.2]]), and
>
> $$
> V = e^{v/4M} = e^{\kappa v}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{1}{4M} ,
> $$
>
>    so $\nabla_\xi\xi = \xi/4M$ there, as in [[#^thm-c10-5-4|Theorem §C10.5.4]]. Equal steps of $v$ are exponentially growing steps of $V$; all $v \in \mathbf R$ cover only $V > 0$ ([[#^ex-c10-5-3|Example §C10.5.3]] gives the time scale $1/\kappa$ in seconds).
> 4. **Both horizons, and where $\xi$ vanishes.** On the past horizon $V = 0$, $U < 0$, the same $\xi$ is $-(U/4M)\,\partial_U$ and $\nabla_\xi\xi = -\xi/4M$. At the bifurcation sphere $U = V = 0$, $\xi = 0$. The Killing horizon of $\xi$ ([[#^def-c10-5-3|Def. §C10.5.3]]) is $\{UV = 0\}$ minus the bifurcation sphere: four null half-surfaces, with $\kappa = +1/4M$ on $U = 0$, $V > 0$ and $\kappa = -1/4M$ on $V = 0$, $U < 0$.
>
> *Model:* the Kruskal metric, $M > 0$; *applies:* exactly, wherever $UV < 1$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.4, Example 4.7, eq. (4.57), p. 81 (PDF 82) (parts 1–2) · Carroll, §6.3, p. 245 (PDF 258) ("κ can change sign at the bifurcation two-sphere") · parts 3–4 worked here · $\nabla_NN = 0$, the Killing property of $\xi$ and both values of $\nabla_\xi\xi$ checked by script with $r(U, V)$ implicit*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (inverse metric). The $(U, V)$ block of the Kruskal metric $-F\,dU\,dV + r^2d\Omega^2$ is $g_{UU} = g_{VV} = 0$, $g_{UV} = g_{VU} = -F/2$, with $F > 0$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], part 4). Its inverse is
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -F/2 \\ -F/2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2/F \\ -2/F & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad g^{UU} = g^{VV} = 0, \quad g^{UV} = -\frac2F = -\frac{r\,e^{r/2M}}{16M^3} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** ($dU$ is null; part 1). $N_\mu = \partial_\mu U = \delta^U_\mu$, so $N\cdot N = g^{UU} = 0$ at every point, and $N^\mu = g^{\mu U}$ has the single component $N^V = g^{VU} = -2/F$. Every level set $U = c$ is a null hypersurface ([[#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]]), and $N\cdot N = 0$ on an open set. [[#^thm-c10-5-3|Theorem §C10.5.3]], part 3, gives $N^\nu\nabla_\nu N^\mu = 0$ (also checked directly by script). The integral curves of $N \propto \partial_V$ have $U$, $\theta$, $\varphi$ fixed.
>
> **Step 3** (part 2). On $U = 0$, $UV = 0 = (1 - r/2M)e^{r/2M}$ forces $r = 2M$, so $F = 16M^2/e$ and $N = -(2/F)\,\partial_V = -(e/8M^2)\,\partial_V$, a constant multiple of $\partial_V$ along the whole surface. An integral curve of $N$ has $dV/d\lambda = -e/8M^2$ with $\lambda$ affine, so $V = V_0 - (e/8M^2)\lambda$: an affine function of $\lambda$, hence itself an affine parameter ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 3, reversed in direction). The roles of $U$ and $V$ are symmetric in the metric, so $dV$ is null too and $U$ is affine on $V = 0$. As a by-product, $N = c\,\partial_V$ with $c$ constant gives $\nabla_{\partial_V}\partial_V = c^{-2}\nabla_NN = 0$ on $U = 0$, and likewise $\nabla_{\partial_U}\partial_U = 0$ on $V = 0$.
>
> **Step 4** (Killing parameter; part 3). At $U = 0$, $\xi = \frac{1}{4M}(-0\cdot\partial_U + V\partial_V) = (V/4M)\,\partial_V$. Its integral curve satisfies
>
> $$
> \frac{dV}{dv} = \frac{V}{4M} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad V(v) = V(0)\,e^{v/4M} .
> $$
>
> The Kruskal coordinate is defined as $V = e^{v/4M}$ with $v$ the advanced time ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^def-c7-3-1|Def. §C7.3.1]]), and $\xi = \partial_v$ in Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates, so the Killing parameter is $v$ itself and $V(0) = 1$. In the derivative along the generator, with $\phi(V) = V/4M$ and $\nabla_{\partial_V}\partial_V = 0$ from Step 3:
>
> $$
> \nabla_\xi\xi = \phi\,\nabla_{\partial_V}\bigl(\phi\,\partial_V\bigr) = \phi\,\frac{d\phi}{dV}\,\partial_V + \phi^2\,\nabla_{\partial_V}\partial_V = \frac{V}{4M}\cdot\frac{1}{4M}\,\partial_V = \frac{1}{4M}\,\xi .
> $$
>
> This is $\kappa = 1/4M$ again, now computed from the affine parameter, independently of [[#^thm-c10-5-4|Theorem §C10.5.4]]. With $\kappa = 1/4M$ the relation reads $V = e^{\kappa v}$, the formula $\lambda \propto e^{\kappa v}$ of [[#^def-c10-5-4|Def. §C10.5.4]] up to the affine change $\lambda = (V - 1)/\kappa$.
>
> **Step 5** (past horizon and the bifurcation sphere; part 4). At $V = 0$, $\xi = \frac{1}{4M}(-U\partial_U + 0) = \psi(U)\,\partial_U$ with $\psi = -U/4M$, and $\nabla_{\partial_U}\partial_U = 0$ there (Step 3):
>
> $$
> \nabla_\xi\xi = \psi\,\frac{d\psi}{dU}\,\partial_U = \Bigl(-\frac{U}{4M}\Bigr)\Bigl(-\frac{1}{4M}\Bigr)\partial_U = -\frac{1}{4M}\Bigl(-\frac{U}{4M}\,\partial_U\Bigr) = -\frac{1}{4M}\,\xi .
> $$
>
> At $U = V = 0$ both components of $\xi$ vanish. On $V = 0$, $U < 0$, $\xi = (|U|/4M)\,\partial_U$ points to increasing $U$, the future ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], part 5), yet shrinks to zero as the generator approaches the bifurcation sphere: a future-directed field that decreases along its own flow has $\kappa < 0$. The remaining half-surfaces $U = 0$, $V < 0$ and $V = 0$, $U > 0$ (between regions III, IV and II, III) are covered by the same formulas with $V < 0$, respectively $U > 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Along the future horizon the symmetry's clock $v$ and the geometry's affine clock $V$ are related exponentially, with rate $\kappa$. The Killing parameter runs to $-\infty$ at a finite affine distance, at the bifurcation sphere, where the symmetry has a fixed point. This is the same structure as the boost in flat spacetime, which fixes the origin ([[#^ex-c10-5-2|Example §C10.5.2]], [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^ex-c7-3-2|Example §C7.3.2]]). The exponential is the one seen in the redshift of an infalling source, whose rate $1/4M$ ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^ex-c7-1-2|Example §C7.1.2]]) is $\kappa$, and in the fading of a lamp carried across the Rindler horizon ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^ex-c10-2-3|Example §C10.2.3]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Constancy of κ
> In every example of this section $\kappa$ is the same at every point of the horizon. Along a single generator this is a consequence of the symmetry: the flow of $\xi$ maps the generator to itself and preserves $g$ and $\xi$, hence also the function $\kappa$ defined from them. Constancy across generators is a different matter. Couzens states it in §4.4 without proof. It is the **zeroth law of black-hole mechanics** (Bardeen, Carter and Hawking): $\kappa$ is constant on the event horizon of a stationary black hole if Einstein's equations hold with matter obeying the dominant energy condition ([[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^def-c4-5-4|Def. §C4.5.4]]), and, independently of field equations, on any bifurcate Killing horizon. The proof is in Couzens' GR2 §7.2 and belongs to C13 (planned). A temperature $\hbar\kappa/2\pi ck_B$ makes sense only because $\kappa$ is one number for the whole horizon.

> - **p. 80 (PDF 81)**: "κ … will later be understood as the temperature of the black hole." The Hawking temperature is $\hbar\kappa/2\pi$ in Couzens' units ($\hbar\kappa/2\pi ck_B$ restored, his (7.2)), not $\kappa$.
> - **Carroll (6.8) and Hartle Problem 20.16** write $\xi^\mu\nabla_\mu\xi^\nu = -\kappa\xi^\nu$. For the future-directed $\partial_t$ on the future Schwarzschild horizon, $\nabla_\xi\xi = +\xi/4M$, so this sign gives $\kappa = -1/4M$. Carroll's own result (6.24) is $\kappa = +1/4GM$, obtained from $\kappa = \sqrt{-\xi\cdot\xi}\,|a|$ (6.17), which is positive by construction. The minus sign fits a past horizon ([[#^thm-c10-5-5|Theorem §C10.5.5]], part 4). These notes use $+\kappa$ (Couzens (4.44)).
> - **Hartle Problem 20.19** asks to show that the normal $\ell_\alpha = \partial_\alpha f$ of a null surface $f = 0$ satisfies $\nabla_\ell\ell = 0$. This holds when every nearby level set of $f$ is null as well ([[#^thm-c10-5-3|Theorem §C10.5.3]], part 3). In general only $\nabla_\ell\ell = \alpha\ell$ holds: for $f = r - 2M$ in Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates, $\nabla_\ell\ell = \ell/4M$ on the horizon ([[#^thm-c10-5-4|Theorem §C10.5.4]], Step 4, where $\ell = n$), and $f = t^2 - x^2$ gives $\nabla_\ell\ell = -2\ell$ on the light cone ([[#^ex-c10-5-4|Example §C10.5.4]]).

> [!example] Example §C10.5.1: The Surfaces r = const of Schwarzschild
> *Question.* Classify the hypersurfaces $r = r_0$ of the Schwarzschild spacetime ($r$ the areal radius, [[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^def-c6-1-2|Def. §C6.1.2]]) by [[#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]], in a chart that covers $r = 2M$, and see the answer in the Kruskal diagram.
>
> **Step 1** (normal and its norm). In ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates the normal one-form is $dr$, with $g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu r\,\partial_\nu r = g^{rr} = f = 1 - 2M/r_0$ ([[#^thm-c10-5-4|Theorem §C10.5.4]], Step 2):
>
> | $r_0$ | $3M$ | $2M$ | $M$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $g^{rr} = 1 - 2M/r_0$ | $1/3 > 0$ | $0$ | $-1 < 0$ |
> | normal | spacelike | null | timelike |
> | surface | timelike | null | spacelike |
>
> **Step 2** (check with the induced metric). Parametrize $r = r_0$ by $(v, \theta, \varphi)$. Since $dr$ pulls back to $0$, $h = -f\,dv^2 + r_0^2\,d\Omega^2$, with $\det h = -f\,r_0^4\sin^2\theta$: Lorentzian for $f > 0$, degenerate for $f = 0$, Riemannian for $f < 0$, as [[#^thm-c10-5-2|Theorem §C10.5.2]], part 3, predicts. Outside, $r = r_0$ is the history of a sphere of static observers. Inside, it is an instant of time, because there $r$ is a time coordinate ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]).
>
> **Step 3** (Kruskal picture). In Kruskal coordinates $r = r_0$ is $UV = G(r_0)$, or $X^2 - T^2 = (r_0/2M - 1)e^{r_0/2M}$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], part 2). With $UV = T^2 - X^2$, the normal one-form $d(UV) = 2T\,dT - 2X\,dX$, raised with $g^{TT} = -1/F$, $g^{XX} = 1/F$ (metric $F(-dT^2 + dX^2) + r^2d\Omega^2$), is
>
> $$
> \nabla(UV) = -\frac{2}{F}\bigl(T\,\partial_T + X\,\partial_X\bigr), \qquad g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu(UV)\,\partial_\nu(UV) = \frac{-4T^2 + 4X^2}{F} = -\frac{4UV}{F} .
> $$
>
> This is positive in regions I and III ($UV < 0$), zero on the horizons, negative in II and IV. In the diagram the normal points along the ray from the origin, $(X, T)$, which is "perpendicular" to the hyperbola in the Minkowski sense. It is spacelike where the hyperbola opens sideways and timelike where it opens up. On $U = 0$ it lies along the line itself.
>
> **Step 4** (the failed criterion). Throughout, $g_{TT} = -F(r)$, with $F(2M) = 16M^2/e$: no metric component of the Kruskal chart signals the horizon. The criterion is the norm of the normal, $-4UV/F$, which vanishes there.
>
> *What it shows.* The same family of surfaces changes causal type at $r = 2M$, and the change is read off the normal in any chart. The Schwarzschild chart's $g_{tt} = 0$ at $r = 2M$ was a coincidence of that chart.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.4, Example 4.5, eq. (4.43), pp. 78–79 (PDF 79–80) · worked here (Steps 2–4); values checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C10.5.2: The Rindler Horizon Is a Killing Horizon of the Boost Field, κ = 1/ξ₀
> *Question.* In Minkowski spacetime, show that the null plane $x = t$ is a Killing horizon of the boost field and find its surface gravity when the field is normalized to the observer of proper acceleration $1/\xi_0$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^mod-c10-2-1|Model §C10.2.1]]; Couzens writes $\alpha$ for $\xi_0$, [[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^cau-c10-2-1|§C10.2, Caution: Couzens' α]]).
>
> **Step 1** (the Killing field). The boost along $x$, $\chi = x\,\partial_t + t\,\partial_x$, is a Killing vector ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^ex-c3-13-1|Example §C3.13.1]], where it is $B_x$), with
>
> $$
> \chi\cdot\chi = -x^2 + t^2 ,
> $$
>
> zero exactly on the planes $x = \pm t$. In Rindler coordinates $t = \xi\sinh\eta$, $x = \xi\cosh\eta$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]]), $\partial_\eta = \xi\cosh\eta\,\partial_t + \xi\sinh\eta\,\partial_x = x\,\partial_t + t\,\partial_x = \chi$: the Rindler time translation.
>
> **Step 2** (Killing horizon). $\mathcal N = \{x - t = 0\}$ has the normal one-form $d(x - t) = dx - dt$, raised $n = \partial_t + \partial_x$, with $n\cdot n = -1 + 1 = 0$: null. On $\mathcal N$, $\chi = t\,\partial_t + t\,\partial_x = t\,n$, normal and nonzero for $t \neq 0$. So $x = t$, $t > 0$ (the future horizon of the wedge $x > |t|$) and $x = t$, $t < 0$ are Killing horizons of $\chi$ ([[#^def-c10-5-3|Def. §C10.5.3]]), and in the same way the two halves of $x = -t$, among them the past horizon $x = -t$, $t < 0$, of the wedge. At the origin $\chi = 0$.
>
> **Step 3** ($\nabla_\chi\chi$). In inertial coordinates $\Gamma = 0$, so $(\nabla_\chi\chi)^\mu = \chi^\nu\partial_\nu\chi^\mu$:
>
> $$
> (\nabla_\chi\chi)^t = (x\partial_t + t\partial_x)\,x = t, \qquad (\nabla_\chi\chi)^x = (x\partial_t + t\partial_x)\,t = x, \qquad \nabla_\chi\chi = t\,\partial_t + x\,\partial_x .
> $$
>
> On $x = t$ this is $t\,\partial_t + t\,\partial_x = \chi$: $\kappa_\chi = 1$. On the past horizon $x = -t$, $t < 0$: $\nabla_\chi\chi = t\,\partial_t - t\,\partial_x = -\chi$, so $\kappa_\chi = -1$, the sign pattern of [[#^thm-c10-5-5|Theorem §C10.5.5]], part 4.
>
> **Step 4** (normalization). No region has $\chi\cdot\chi \to -1$, so the normalization of [[#^def-c10-5-4|Def. §C10.5.4]] is unavailable. The natural choice is a reference observer $\xi = \xi_0$, whose proper acceleration is $1/\xi_0$: $\chi_0 \equiv \chi/\xi_0$ has $\chi_0\cdot\chi_0 = -\xi^2/\xi_0^2 = -1$ there, and its parameter is that observer's proper time $\tau = \xi_0\eta$. By the scaling rule of [[#^def-c10-5-4|Def. §C10.5.4]], $\kappa = \kappa_\chi/\xi_0$:
>
> $$
> \kappa = \frac{1}{\xi_0} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \kappa = \frac{c^2}{\xi_0}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the proper acceleration of the reference observer.
>
> **Step 5** (redshifted acceleration). The observer at fixed $\xi$ has proper acceleration $1/\xi$ ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, the same hyperbola) and redshift factor $\sqrt{-\chi_0\cdot\chi_0} = \xi/\xi_0$ relative to the reference observer. The product is $(\xi/\xi_0)(1/\xi) = 1/\xi_0$ for every $\xi$, not only in the limit. This is the Rindler form of [[#^thm-c10-5-4|Theorem §C10.5.4]], part 3, and the reason the near-horizon Schwarzschild metric, Rindler with $\kappa = 1/4M$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-4|Theorem §C10.2.4]]), reproduces the surface gravity.
>
> *What it shows.* A Killing horizon with nonzero surface gravity exists in flat spacetime. It is a horizon only for the accelerated observers whose time translation $\chi$ is ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^rem-c10-2-1|§C10.2, Remark: Horizons are relative to observers; black-hole horizons are not]]). Killing horizons are local; which of them bound a black hole is decided globally (C10.7).
>
> *Source: Carroll, §6.3, eqs. (6.5)–(6.7), p. 245 (PDF 258) (boost horizon) · Couzens, GR2, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.19)–(4.23), p. 70 (PDF 71) (Rindler coordinates) · worked here; values checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C10.5.4: The Light Cone with Two Normals: Affine and Not
> *Question.* In two-dimensional Minkowski spacetime, the null line $\mathcal N = \{t = x\}$ is the level set of $f_1 = t - x$ and also of $f_2 = t^2 - x^2$. Compute $\kappa_n$ of [[#^thm-c10-5-3|Theorem §C10.5.3]] for both normals. (In four dimensions the same holds for the light cone $t = r$ of Hartle's Example 7.13 with $t - r$ and $t^2 - r^2$.)
>
> **Step 1** ($f_1$). $N_1 = dt - dx$, raised $N_1 = -\partial_t - \partial_x$ (constant components), with $N_1\cdot N_1 = -1 + 1 = 0$ everywhere. All level sets $t - x = c$ are null, and $\nabla_{N_1}N_1 = 0$ ($\Gamma = 0$, constant components): $\kappa_{N_1} = 0$, as part 3 of the theorem predicts.
>
> **Step 2** ($f_2$). $N_2 = 2t\,dt - 2x\,dx$, raised $N_2 = -2t\,\partial_t - 2x\,\partial_x$, with $N_2\cdot N_2 = -4t^2 + 4x^2$, zero only on the cone. Then
>
> $$
> \nabla_{N_2}N_2 = (-2t\,\partial_t - 2x\,\partial_x)(-2t)\,\partial_t + (-2t\,\partial_t - 2x\,\partial_x)(-2x)\,\partial_x = 4t\,\partial_t + 4x\,\partial_x = -2\,N_2 ,
> $$
>
> so $\kappa_{N_2} = -2$. Check with the transformation rule: on $\mathcal N$, $N_2 = 2t\,(dt - dx) = h\,N_1$ with $h = 2t$, and $\kappa_{N_2} = h\,\kappa_{N_1} + N_1(h) = 0 + (-\partial_t - \partial_x)(2t) = -2$.
>
> **Step 3** (same curves, different parameter). The integral curve of $N_2$ through $(t_0, t_0)$ solves $dt/ds = -2t$, $dx/ds = -2x$: $t = x = t_0e^{-2s}$, the same straight null line as for $N_1$. By [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 2, with $h = -2$, an affine parameter is $\lambda = \int_0^se^{-2s'}ds' = (1 - e^{-2s})/2$, and indeed $dt/d\lambda = (-2t_0e^{-2s})/e^{-2s} = -2t_0$ is constant.
>
> *What it shows.* The generators are a property of the surface; $\kappa_n$ is a property of the normal chosen. A number like the surface gravity needs a preferred normal, which a Killing vector with a normalization supplies ([[#^def-c10-5-4|Def. §C10.5.4]]).
>

> [!example] Example §C10.5.5: Hypersurface-Orthogonal and Twisting One-Forms
> *Question.* Test [[#^thm-c10-5-1|Theorem §C10.5.1]] on three one-forms, computing $n \wedge dn$ with the coordinate formula for $d$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]]) and the rules of the wedge product ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]]: bilinear, graded commutative, $dx\wedge dx = 0$).
>
> **(a) The static Killing field.** For Schwarzschild $\xi = \partial_t$, $\xi_\mu = g_{\mu t} = -f\,\delta^t_\mu$, so $\xi_\flat = -f\,dt$ with $f = f(r)$. Then $d\xi_\flat = -df\wedge dt = -f'\,dr\wedge dt$ and
>
> $$
> \xi_\flat\wedge d\xi_\flat = (-f\,dt)\wedge(-f'\,dr\wedge dt) = f f'\,dt\wedge dr\wedge dt = -f f'\,dr\wedge dt\wedge dt = 0 .
> $$
>
> Consistently, $\xi_\flat = g\,dt$ with $g = -f$: $\partial_t$ is orthogonal to the slices $t = \text{const}$, the static case of [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-3|Def. §C3.13.3]].
>
> **(b) A twisting field of planes.** On $\mathbf R^3$ (or on $\mathbf R^3$ times a time axis) let $n = dz - y\,dx$. Then $dn = -dy\wedge dx = dx\wedge dy$ and
>
> $$
> n\wedge dn = dz\wedge dx\wedge dy - y\,dx\wedge dx\wedge dy = dz\wedge dx\wedge dy = dx\wedge dy\wedge dz \neq 0 ,
> $$
>
> using two transpositions for the last step. By the theorem, no family of surfaces has the planes $\ker n$ as tangent planes. Here this can be seen directly. If $n = g\,dF$ near a point, comparing components gives $g\,\partial_xF = -y$, $g\,\partial_yF = 0$, $g\,\partial_zF = 1$. Then $\partial_yF = 0$, so $F = F(x, z)$ and $\partial_xF/\partial_zF = -y$; the left side does not depend on $y$ and the right side does, a contradiction.
>
> **(c) Kerr.** For the Kerr metric in Boyer–Lindquist form the lowered $\partial_t$ is $g_{tt}\,dt + g_{t\varphi}\,d\varphi$ with $g_{t\varphi} \neq 0$, and the $(t, r, \varphi)$ component of $n\wedge dn$ is nonzero (checked by script). The rotating black hole is stationary but not static, as [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-3|Def. §C3.13.3]] states; the Killing vectors of Kerr and this failure of hypersurface-orthogonality are derived in [[§C12.1 The Kerr Metric#^thm-c12-1-4|Theorem §C12.1.4]].
>
> *What it shows.* $n \wedge dn$ detects twist pointwise. The same three-form appears for congruences of light rays as their twist, which vanishes for the generators of a null hypersurface ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^thm-c10-6-3|Theorem §C10.6.3]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.4, Theorem 2, p. 78 (PDF 79) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §15.2, eq. (15.9), p. 271 (PDF 286) · worked here; all components checked by script*
