What is a black hole, defined without coordinates? C7 gave two answers that work for one spacetime only. An event belongs to the Schwarzschild black hole if no future-directed causal curve from it reaches arbitrarily large values of the coordinate $r$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]]), and $r = 0$ is singular because a curvature invariant blows up there, a test that can fail ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]], [[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^ex-c7-1-3|Example §C7.1.3]]). Collapse produces such an object ([[§C9.3 White Dwarfs, Neutron Stars and Collapse#^thm-c9-3-6|Theorem §C9.3.6]]). Both definitions speak about the ends of spacetime, where light goes when it "escapes to infinity" and where worldlines stop, in the language of a chart. This chapter builds a chart-free language for the ends of spacetime and defines black holes and event horizons with it (§C10.7).

The first step is to see all of a spacetime at once, its infinity included, in a picture where causal relations can be read by eye: light rays at $45°$, so that "can $p$ signal to $q$?" becomes "is $q$ above both light rays through $p$?". Minkowski spacetime, whose infinity is best understood, is the test case.

*The naive attempt, computed.* Squeeze each coordinate of two-dimensional Minkowski space separately, $T_n = \arctan t$, $X_n = \arctan x$, which maps the plane onto the square $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)^2$. A light ray $x = t + c$ becomes the curve $X_n = \arctan(\tan T_n + c)$, of slope

$$
\frac{dX_n}{dT_n} = \frac{dx/(1 + x^2)}{dt/(1 + t^2)} = \frac{1 + t^2}{1 + x^2} ,
$$

which is $1$ only where $|t| = |x|$. At $(t, x) = (2, 0)$ it is $5$: in the picture this light ray rises $1/5$ per unit across, at $\arctan(1/5) = 11.3°$ to the horizontal instead of $45°$, and the tilt changes from point to point. Worse, as $t \to \infty$ both $\arctan t$ and $\arctan(t + c)$ tend to $\pi/2$: every right-moving light ray ends at the same corner $(\pi/2, \pi/2)$, whatever $c$ (figure below, left; checked by script). The picture keeps the points and loses exactly what it was drawn for: the cones, and where different light rays go.

The cure is to squeeze along the light rays themselves. In the null coordinates $u = t - x$, $v = t + x$ the light rays are the lines $u = \text{const}$ and $v = \text{const}$, and $\arctan$ applied to $u$ and to $v$ keeps them lines of constant coordinate. The metric is then multiplied by a positive function, nothing else ([[#^thm-c10-1-3|Theorem §C10.1.3]]), and a light cone does not notice such a factor ([[#^thm-c10-1-1|Theorem §C10.1.1]]). The section defines this operation, a conformal transformation ([[#^def-c10-1-1|Def. §C10.1.1]]); shows what it keeps (causal character, null geodesics) and what it loses (timelike geodesics, lengths) ([[#^thm-c10-1-1|Theorems §C10.1.1]] and [[#^thm-c10-1-2|§C10.1.2]]); turns it into a recipe, conformal compactification and the Penrose diagram ([[#^def-c10-1-2|Def. §C10.1.2]]); carries it out for two-dimensional Minkowski space ([[#^thm-c10-1-3|Theorem §C10.1.3]]), names the pieces of infinity ([[#^def-c10-1-3|Def. §C10.1.3]]), and finds four-dimensional Minkowski space inside the Einstein static universe ([[#^def-c10-1-4|Def. §C10.1.4]], [[#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]]).

**Setup.** A spacetime is a connected, time-oriented Lorentzian manifold $(M, g)$ of dimension $n \ge 2$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]], [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]). Conformal factors are smooth and positive. The examples are Minkowski spacetime in two dimensions, $g = -dt^2 + dx^2$, and in four, $g = -dt^2 + dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$ in spherical coordinates ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-3|Example §C3.1.3]]), the axis $r = 0$ included as usual. No field equation is used: every statement is about metrics only, and exact.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $c = 1$ (no mass enters, so $G$ does not appear); $d\Omega^2 = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2$; null coordinates $u = t - x$, $v = t + x$ (in four dimensions $u = t - r$, $v = t + r$); compactified null coordinates $\tilde u = \arctan u$, $\tilde v = \arctan v$; diagram coordinates $T = \tilde v + \tilde u$ and $X = \tilde v - \tilde u$ (two dimensions) or $\chi = \tilde v - \tilde u$ (four), as in Couzens. d'Inverno's $(p, q, t', r')$ are our $(\tilde v, \tilde u, T, \chi)$, in signature $+{-}{-}{-}$; Hartle's $(v', u')$ are our $(\tilde v, \tilde u)$ but his $(t', r') = (T/2, \chi/2)$. $\mathscr I$ is read "scri".

## Conformal transformations

Light cones are defined by $g(X, X) = 0$, an equation that does not change when $g$ is multiplied by a positive number. Multiplying by a positive function changes the metric everywhere, but not its cones.

> [!definition] Definition §C10.1.1: Conformal Transformation
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be a Lorentzian manifold and $\Omega : M \to \mathbf R$ a smooth function with $\Omega > 0$ everywhere. The **conformally rescaled metric** is the symmetric $(0, 2)$ tensor field
>
> $$
> \tilde g = \Omega^2 g, \qquad \tilde g_p(X, Y) = \Omega(p)^2\,g_p(X, Y)\ \ (X, Y \in T_pM), \qquad \tilde g_{\mu\nu}(x) = \Omega(x)^2\,g_{\mu\nu}(x) .
> $$
>
> The passage $g \to \tilde g$ is a **conformal transformation**, $\Omega$ the **conformal factor**; two metrics on $M$ related in this way are **conformally related**, and a metric conformally related to a flat one is **conformally flat**. The manifold, its points and its tangent spaces stay the same; only the metric ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]) changes. Writing $\Omega = e^\omega$, with $\omega = \ln\Omega$ smooth, is often convenient.
>
> *Well defined.* $\tilde g$ is again a Lorentzian metric ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]): if $e_0, \dots, e_{n-1}$ is an orthonormal basis of $g$ at $p$, then $e_a/\Omega(p)$ is one of $\tilde g$, with the same signs. The inverse is $\tilde g^{\mu\nu} = \Omega^{-2}g^{\mu\nu}$, since $\tilde g_{\mu\nu}\tilde g^{\nu\rho} = \Omega^2\Omega^{-2}\delta_\mu^\rho$.
>
> *Through a map.* A smooth map $\psi : M \to \tilde M$ into a second Lorentzian manifold $(\tilde M, \tilde g)$ that is an embedding ([[§41 Embeddings#^def-41-1|591 Def. §41.1]]) and pulls $\tilde g$ back to $\Omega^2g$, in coordinates (transformation law, [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^thm-c3-1-1|Theorem §C3.1.1]])
>
> $$
> \tilde g_{\alpha\beta}\bigl(\psi(x)\bigr)\,\frac{\partial\psi^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}\,\frac{\partial\psi^\beta}{\partial x^\nu} = \Omega(x)^2\,g_{\mu\nu}(x) ,
> $$
>
> is a **conformal embedding**. It identifies $(M, \Omega^2g)$ with the open set $\psi(M) \subset \tilde M$ carrying $\tilde g$: this is how a conformal transformation is used to place a spacetime inside a larger one.
>
> *Analogy.* An analytic function $w = f(z)$ with $f' \neq 0$ is a conformal mapping of the plane ([[§112★ Preservation of Angles and Scale Factors#^def-112-2|342 Def. §112.2]]): from $dw = f'(z)\,dz$, $|dw|^2 = |f'(z)|^2|dz|^2$, so it pulls the flat metric back to $|f'|^2$ times itself, and preserves angles. In Lorentzian signature what is preserved is the light cone ([[#^thm-c10-1-1|Theorem §C10.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1, Definition 2, eq. (4.1) (p. 64, PDF 65; "$\Omega(x) \neq 0$") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.4, eq. (18.12) (p. 348, PDF 363; the "physical" metric $g$ and the "unphysical" metric $\bar g = \Omega^2g$)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.1.1: Conformal Maps Preserve the Causal Character of Vectors and Curves
> Let $\tilde g = \Omega^2g$ on $M$ ([[#^def-c10-1-1|Def. §C10.1.1]]).
> 1. **Vectors.** For $X \in T_pM$, $\tilde g(X, X) = \Omega(p)^2\,g(X, X)$. Hence $X$ is timelike, null or spacelike for $\tilde g$ exactly when it is for $g$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]): the light cones of $g$ and $\tilde g$ at every point are the same set.
> 2. **Time orientation.** A time orientation $T$ of $g$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]) is one of $\tilde g$, and a causal vector is future-directed for $\tilde g$ exactly when it is for $g$.
> 3. **Curves.** A curve is timelike, null, spacelike, causal or future-directed for $\tilde g$ exactly when it is for $g$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]). So an event $q$ can be reached from $p$ by a future-directed causal (or timelike) curve in $(M, g)$ exactly when it can in $(M, \tilde g)$: the two metrics have the same causal relations (the causal future and past of [[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^def-c10-7-1|Def. §C10.7.1]]).
> 4. **Lengths change.** A future-directed timelike curve has $\tilde g$-proper time $\tilde\tau = \int\Omega\sqrt{-g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)}\,d\lambda = \int\Omega\,d\tau$. A worldline of infinite proper time $\tau$ has finite $\tilde\tau$ when $\Omega$ falls off fast enough along it ([[#^thm-c10-1-3|Theorem §C10.1.3]], part 5).
>
> *Model:* any Lorentzian manifold, $\Omega$ smooth and positive; *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Layer change.* A Lorentz transformation preserves causal character because it preserves the metric ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). A conformal transformation changes the metric and still preserves causal character: causal structure needs only the metric up to a positive factor.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1, eq. (4.2) and the paragraph after (pp. 64–65, PDF 65–66) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.4 (p. 348, PDF 363: "the null geodesics determine the light cones, which in turn define the causal structure")*

^thm-c10-1-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (vectors). By [[#^def-c10-1-1|Def. §C10.1.1]], $\tilde g(X, X) = \Omega(p)^2g(X, X)$ with $\Omega(p)^2 > 0$, so
>
> $$
> g(X, X) < 0 \iff \tilde g(X, X) < 0, \qquad g(X, X) = 0 \iff \tilde g(X, X) = 0, \qquad g(X, X) > 0 \iff \tilde g(X, X) > 0 .
> $$
>
> This is part 1, Couzens' (4.2).
>
> **Step 2** (time orientation). $T$ is continuous and $g(T, T) < 0$; by Step 1 $\tilde g(T, T) < 0$, so $T$ is a time orientation of $\tilde g$. For a causal $X$, $\tilde g(T_p, X) = \Omega(p)^2g(T_p, X)$ has the sign of $g(T_p, X)$: future-directed ($< 0$) for one metric exactly when for the other. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 3** (curves). The conditions of [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]] are conditions on the velocity $\dot\gamma(\lambda)$ at each $\lambda$; by Steps 1–2 each holds for $\tilde g$ exactly when it holds for $g$. "There is a future-directed causal curve from $p$ to $q$" is therefore the same statement for both metrics. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 4** (proper time). $\sqrt{-\tilde g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)} = \sqrt{\Omega^2}\sqrt{-g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)} = \Omega\sqrt{-g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)}$, since $\Omega > 0$; integrate along the curve, with $d\tau = \sqrt{-g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)}\,d\lambda$. This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Everything causal is decided by signs of $g(X, X)$ and $g(T, X)$, and a positive factor does not change signs. Everything metric (proper time, length, curvature) is multiplied or changed.

^der-c10-1-1

*Uses:* [[#^def-c10-1-1|Def. §C10.1.1]], [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]], [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]], [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]

Causal curves survive the rescaling. Free fall is a different matter: geodesics are defined through the connection ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]), which contains derivatives of the metric, and the derivatives of $\Omega$ enter. The question is which geodesics survive.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.1.2: Null Geodesics Are Conformally Invariant as Unparametrised Curves; Timelike Geodesics Are Not
> Let $\tilde g = e^{2\omega}g$ ([[#^def-c10-1-1|Def. §C10.1.1]], $\Omega = e^\omega$), with Levi-Civita connections $\nabla$ and $\tilde\nabla$, and write $\nabla^\lambda\omega = g^{\lambda\sigma}\partial_\sigma\omega$.
> 1. **The connections.** $\tilde\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu} + C^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}$ with the tensor ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]])
>
> $$
> C^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = \delta^\lambda_\mu\,\partial_\nu\omega + \delta^\lambda_\nu\,\partial_\mu\omega - g_{\mu\nu}\,\nabla^\lambda\omega .
> $$
>
> 2. **Along a curve** with velocity $k^\mu = dx^\mu/d\lambda$ and covariant derivatives along it $D/d\lambda$, $\tilde D/d\lambda$ ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-3|Def. §C3.5.3]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{\tilde Dk^\lambda}{d\lambda} = \frac{Dk^\lambda}{d\lambda} + 2\,\frac{d\omega}{d\lambda}\,k^\lambda - g(k, k)\,\nabla^\lambda\omega, \qquad \frac{d\omega}{d\lambda} = k^\mu\partial_\mu\omega .
> $$
>
> 3. **Null geodesics.** An affinely parametrised null geodesic of $g$ is a null geodesic of $\tilde g$, with an affine parameter $\tilde\lambda$ given by
>
> $$
> \frac{d\tilde\lambda}{d\lambda} = \Omega^2 \qquad (\text{up to } \tilde\lambda \to a\tilde\lambda + b) .
> $$
>
> Since $g = e^{-2\omega}\tilde g$, the converse holds too: $g$ and $\tilde g$ have the same null geodesics as unparametrised curves.
> 4. **Timelike geodesics.** A timelike geodesic of $g$ is a geodesic of $\tilde g$, in some parametrisation, exactly when $\nabla\omega$ is parallel to $k$ at each of its points. In two-dimensional Minkowski space with $\omega = a\,x$, $a \neq 0$, the inertial worldline $x = 0$ is not a geodesic of $\tilde g$. The same holds for spacelike geodesics.
>
> *Model:* any Lorentzian manifold, $\Omega$ smooth and positive; *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1, p. 65 (PDF 66), Exercise 3 (stated; proof left to his problem sheet 1) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.4, p. 348 (PDF 363), referring to their Exercise 6.29 · formula of part 1 checked by script for an arbitrary $\omega$ on flat and on curved two-dimensional metrics*

^thm-c10-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Christoffel symbols of $\tilde g$). By [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]], $\tilde\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\tilde g^{\lambda\sigma}(\partial_\mu\tilde g_{\sigma\nu} + \partial_\nu\tilde g_{\sigma\mu} - \partial_\sigma\tilde g_{\mu\nu})$. With $\tilde g_{\sigma\nu} = e^{2\omega}g_{\sigma\nu}$ the product rule gives
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu\tilde g_{\sigma\nu} = e^{2\omega}\bigl(\partial_\mu g_{\sigma\nu} + 2\,\partial_\mu\omega\,g_{\sigma\nu}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> and $\tilde g^{\lambda\sigma} = e^{-2\omega}g^{\lambda\sigma}$. The factors $e^{\pm2\omega}$ cancel:
>
> $$
> \tilde\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12g^{\lambda\sigma}(\partial_\mu g_{\sigma\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\sigma\mu} - \partial_\sigma g_{\mu\nu}) + g^{\lambda\sigma}\bigl(\partial_\mu\omega\,g_{\sigma\nu} + \partial_\nu\omega\,g_{\sigma\mu} - \partial_\sigma\omega\,g_{\mu\nu}\bigr) = \Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu} + \delta^\lambda_\nu\,\partial_\mu\omega + \delta^\lambda_\mu\,\partial_\nu\omega - g_{\mu\nu}\,\nabla^\lambda\omega ,
> $$
>
> using $g^{\lambda\sigma}g_{\sigma\nu} = \delta^\lambda_\nu$. The difference of two connections is a tensor ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]]), and indeed $C$ is built from $\delta$, $g$ and $d\omega$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (along a curve). $Dk^\lambda/d\lambda = dk^\lambda/d\lambda + \Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu$ ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]], Step 1), and the same with tildes. Subtracting and contracting $C$ with $k^\mu k^\nu$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\tilde Dk^\lambda}{d\lambda} - \frac{Dk^\lambda}{d\lambda} = C^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu = k^\lambda(k^\nu\partial_\nu\omega) + k^\lambda(k^\mu\partial_\mu\omega) - (g_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu)\,\nabla^\lambda\omega = 2\,\frac{d\omega}{d\lambda}\,k^\lambda - g(k, k)\,\nabla^\lambda\omega ,
> $$
>
> with $k^\mu\partial_\mu\omega = d\omega\bigl(x(\lambda)\bigr)/d\lambda$ by the chain rule. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 3** (null geodesics). Let $Dk/d\lambda = 0$ and $g(k, k) = 0$. Part 2 leaves
>
> $$
> \frac{\tilde Dk}{d\lambda} = h(\lambda)\,k, \qquad h = 2\,\frac{d\omega}{d\lambda} ,
> $$
>
> a non-affinely parametrised geodesic of $\tilde g$ ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 1), null for $\tilde g$ by [[#^thm-c10-1-1|Theorem §C10.1.1]], part 1. By part 2 of that theorem, $\sigma$ with $d\sigma/d\lambda = \exp\bigl(\int_{\lambda_0}^\lambda h\bigr) = e^{2\omega(\lambda) - 2\omega(\lambda_0)} = \Omega^2/\Omega(\lambda_0)^2$ is affine; dropping the constant factor ($\tilde\lambda = a\sigma + b$, part 3 there) gives $d\tilde\lambda/d\lambda = \Omega^2$. Applying the same argument to $g = e^{2(-\omega)}\tilde g$ gives the converse. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 4** (timelike geodesics). Let $Dk/d\lambda = 0$ with proper time as parameter, $g(k, k) = -1$. Part 2 gives $\tilde Dk/d\lambda = 2(d\omega/d\lambda)\,k + \nabla\omega$. Split $\nabla\omega$ into its part along $k$ and the part orthogonal to it, $\nabla\omega = -g(k, \nabla\omega)\,k + (\nabla\omega)_\perp$, with $g\bigl(k, (\nabla\omega)_\perp\bigr) = -g(k, \nabla\omega) + g(k, \nabla\omega) = 0$. A change of parameter adds to the covariant acceleration only a multiple of the velocity ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]], part 1). So the curve is a $\tilde g$-geodesic in some parametrisation exactly when $(\nabla\omega)_\perp = 0$ along it, that is, when $\nabla\omega$ is parallel to $k$.
>
> **Step 5** (the counterexample). Take $g = -dt^2 + dx^2$, $\omega = a\,x$, the worldline $x = 0$ with $k = \partial_t$. Then $\nabla\omega = g^{xx}\partial_x\omega\,\partial_x = a\,\partial_x$, orthogonal to $k$ and nonzero. By part 1, $\tilde\Gamma^x_{tt} = -g_{tt}\nabla^x\omega = a$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{\tilde Dk^x}{d\tau} = \frac{d^2x}{d\tau^2} + \tilde\Gamma^x_{tt}\Bigl(\frac{dt}{d\tau}\Bigr)^2 = 0 + a \neq 0 ,
> $$
>
> a covariant acceleration across $k$: in $\tilde g = e^{2ax}(-dt^2 + dx^2)$ the observer at rest at $x = 0$ is not in free fall (checked by script). For a spacelike geodesic the same split, with $g(k, k) = +1$, gives the same condition. This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The extra term in the covariant acceleration has two pieces. The piece along $k$ only reparametrises. The piece $-g(k, k)\nabla\omega$ points across $k$ in general, and it is multiplied by $g(k, k)$: it vanishes for null curves and for no others. Light rays survive the rescaling as curves; free particles do not.

Light rays and light cones, the whole of the causal structure, can therefore be studied in any conformally related metric. If the factor $\Omega$ goes to zero at infinity, the rescaled metric can place infinity at a finite distance. The definition makes this precise.

## Conformal compactification

> [!definition] Definition §C10.1.2: Conformal Compactification; Penrose Diagram
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be a spacetime.
> 1. A **conformal compactification** of $(M, g)$ is a spacetime $(\tilde M, \tilde g)$ together with a conformal embedding $\psi : M \to \tilde M$, $\psi^\ast\tilde g = \Omega^2g$ with $\Omega > 0$ smooth on $M$ ([[#^def-c10-1-1|Def. §C10.1.1]]), such that the closure $\overline{\psi(M)}$ in $\tilde M$ is compact. The **conformal boundary** is $\partial\psi(M) = \overline{\psi(M)} \setminus \psi(M)$: the points of $\tilde M$ that $\psi(M)$ approaches without containing them. The points that $M$ sends "to infinity" lie there, at finite $\tilde g$-distance, because $\Omega \to 0$ along the way.
> 2. A **Penrose diagram** (conformal diagram) of a spherically symmetric spacetime is the picture of $\overline{\psi(M)}$ in the plane of the two coordinates of $\tilde M$ other than the angles, drawn so that radial null curves of $\tilde g$ are lines at $\pm45°$. Each interior point stands for a round two-sphere (a point of the axis $r = 0$ for a single event), and the pieces of the boundary are labelled ([[#^def-c10-1-3|Def. §C10.1.3]]).
>
> *Recipe* (Couzens' three steps). (i) Choose coordinates with finite ranges; the standard choice is $\tilde u = \arctan u$, $\tilde v = \arctan v$ for the null coordinates of radial light rays. The components of $g$ then diverge at the edges of the range. (ii) Choose $\Omega$ to cancel the divergence, so that $\tilde g = \Omega^2g$ extends smoothly beyond the edges, and recognise the extended $(\tilde M, \tilde g)$. (iii) Adjoin the edges: pass to the closure of $\psi(M)$. Couzens adjoins the boundary to $M$ itself, making a manifold with boundary ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-1|Def. §C10.4.1]]); for Minkowski space the boundary has corners at $i^\pm$ and $i^0$, so the closure inside $\tilde M$ is the cleaner statement.
>
> *What a Penrose diagram shows.* By [[#^thm-c10-1-1|Theorem §C10.1.1]] and [[#^thm-c10-1-2|Theorem §C10.1.2]]: which events are timelike, null or spacelike separated; whether a causal curve can get from one region to another; radial light rays as $45°$ lines; which piece of the boundary a curve begins and ends on; which pieces are points and which are null surfaces.
>
> *What it does not show.* Distances and proper times ([[#^thm-c10-1-1|Theorem §C10.1.1]], part 4); whether a timelike curve is free fall ([[#^thm-c10-1-2|Theorem §C10.1.2]], part 4); the curvature of $g$, since $\tilde g$ has its own (for Minkowski space it is not flat, [[#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]], part 5) and is not a solution of Einstein's equations for the matter of $g$; the precise shape of curves, which changes with the choice of $\Omega$ and coordinates, only the $45°$ of radial light being fixed. Non-radial light rays are not $45°$ lines ([[#^ex-c10-1-1|Example §C10.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1, p. 65 (PDF 66; the three-step procedure and its limitations: $\tilde R_{\mu\nu} \neq R_{\mu\nu}$, $\tilde g$ "unphysical") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.4, pp. 348–350 (PDF 363–365; "compactified Minkowski space-time"; a Penrose diagram is "a space-time diagram of a conformally compactified space-time")*

## Two-dimensional Minkowski space

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.1.3: The Penrose Diagram of Two-Dimensional Minkowski Space
> Let $g = -dt^2 + dx^2$ on $M = \mathbf R^2$.
> 1. **Null coordinates.** With $u = t - x$, $v = t + x$ ($u, v \in \mathbf R$), $g = -du\,dv$; right-moving light rays are $u = \text{const}$, left-moving ones $v = \text{const}$.
> 2. **Compactified null coordinates.** With $u = \tan\tilde u$, $v = \tan\tilde v$, $\tilde u, \tilde v \in (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$,
>
> $$
> g = -\frac{d\tilde u\,d\tilde v}{\cos^2\tilde u\,\cos^2\tilde v} ,
> $$
>
> which diverges at the edges $\tilde u, \tilde v = \pm\pi/2$.
> 3. **Conformal factor.** With $\Omega = \cos\tilde u\cos\tilde v$,
>
> $$
> \tilde g = \Omega^2g = -d\tilde u\,d\tilde v = \tfrac14\bigl(-dT^2 + dX^2\bigr), \qquad T = \tilde v + \tilde u,\ \ X = \tilde v - \tilde u, \qquad \Omega = \tfrac12(\cos T + \cos X) .
> $$
>
> $\tilde g$ is flat and smooth on all of $\mathbf R^2$. Minkowski space is conformally embedded onto the open diamond $D = \{|T| + |X| < \pi\}$ of this flat plane; the closure of $D$ is compact, $\Omega > 0$ on $D$ and $\Omega = 0$ on its edges. So $(\mathbf R^2, \tilde g)$ with $\psi(t, x) = (T, X)$ is a conformal compactification ([[#^def-c10-1-2|Def. §C10.1.2]]).
> 4. **The map back.** $t = \dfrac{\sin T}{\cos T + \cos X}$, $x = \dfrac{\sin X}{\cos T + \cos X}$. The curves $t = t_0$ are $\sin T = t_0(\cos T + \cos X)$ and the curves $x = x_0$ are $\sin X = x_0(\cos T + \cos X)$.
> 5. ⚑ **Infinity at finite distance.** Light rays are the lines $T - X = \text{const}$ and $T + X = \text{const}$, at $\pm45°$. The worldline $x = 0$, of infinite proper time, has $\tilde g$-proper time $\pi$ over its whole history; the light ray $x = t$ from $t = 0$ reaches the edge of $D$ at $\tilde g$-affine parameter $\pi/2$.
>
> *Model:* two-dimensional Minkowski spacetime; *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.1, eqs. (4.3)–(4.7), Fig. 8 (pp. 66–67, PDF 67–68) · d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 18.8 and its answer (pp. 353, 588; PDF 368, 603; the curves $t, r = \text{const}$ in the form $\tan\tfrac12(t' + r') \pm \tan\tfrac12(t' - r') = 2t_0,\ 2r_0$) · all parts checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (null coordinates). $t = \tfrac12(u + v)$, $x = \tfrac12(v - u)$, so $dt = \tfrac12(du + dv)$, $dx = \tfrac12(dv - du)$ and
>
> $$
> -dt^2 + dx^2 = -\tfrac14\bigl[(du + dv)^2 - (dv - du)^2\bigr] = -\tfrac14\cdot4\,du\,dv = -du\,dv .
> $$
>
> On $u = \text{const}$ (or $v = \text{const}$) $ds^2 = 0$: these are the light rays $x = t - u$ and $x = v - t$. Part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (compactify). $\arctan$ maps $\mathbf R$ onto $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ bijectively and smoothly with smooth inverse $\tan$. With $du = d\tilde u/\cos^2\tilde u$ and $dv = d\tilde v/\cos^2\tilde v$, $-du\,dv = -d\tilde u\,d\tilde v/(\cos^2\tilde u\cos^2\tilde v)$, Couzens' (4.6). Part 2.
>
> **Step 3** (conformal factor and range). Multiplying by $\Omega^2 = \cos^2\tilde u\cos^2\tilde v$ leaves $-d\tilde u\,d\tilde v$, (4.7). With $\tilde u = \tfrac12(T - X)$, $\tilde v = \tfrac12(T + X)$, $d\tilde u\,d\tilde v = \tfrac14(dT - dX)(dT + dX) = \tfrac14(dT^2 - dX^2)$, so $\tilde g = \tfrac14(-dT^2 + dX^2)$: flat, with constant components. The ranges $\tilde u, \tilde v \in (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ say $T - X = 2\tilde u \in (-\pi, \pi)$ and $T + X = 2\tilde v \in (-\pi, \pi)$, which together are $|T| + |X| < \pi$. The product-to-sum formula gives
>
> $$
> \Omega = \cos\tilde u\cos\tilde v = \tfrac12\bigl[\cos(\tilde v - \tilde u) + \cos(\tilde v + \tilde u)\bigr] = \tfrac12(\cos X + \cos T) ,
> $$
>
> positive on $D$ (both $|\tilde u|, |\tilde v| < \pi/2$) and zero on the edges, where $|\tilde u|$ or $|\tilde v|$ equals $\pi/2$. The closed diamond is closed and bounded in $\mathbf R^2$, hence compact. Part 3.
>
> **Step 4** (inverse). With $\tan a + \tan b = \sin(a + b)/(\cos a\cos b)$ and $\tan b - \tan a = \sin(b - a)/(\cos a\cos b)$,
>
> $$
> t = \tfrac12(\tan\tilde u + \tan\tilde v) = \frac{\sin(\tilde u + \tilde v)}{2\cos\tilde u\cos\tilde v} = \frac{\sin T}{\cos T + \cos X}, \qquad x = \tfrac12(\tan\tilde v - \tan\tilde u) = \frac{\sin X}{\cos T + \cos X} .
> $$
>
> Setting $t = t_0$ or $x = x_0$ and multiplying by the positive denominator gives the curves. As $|x| \to \infty$ at fixed $t_0$ the denominator tends to $0$, so $t = t_0$ runs into the left and right corners $(T, X) = (0, \pm\pi)$; likewise $x = x_0$ runs into the top and bottom corners $(\pm\pi, 0)$. Part 4.
>
> **Step 5** (finite distances). $\tilde u = \text{const}$ means $T - X = \text{const}$, and $\tilde v = \text{const}$ means $T + X = \text{const}$. On $x = 0$, $\tilde u = \tilde v = \arctan t$, so $d\tilde u = d\tilde v$ and $\tilde g$ gives $d\tilde\tau^2 = d\tilde u\,d\tilde v = d\tilde u^2$:
>
> $$
> \tilde\tau = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}d\tilde u = \pi, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \tilde\tau = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Omega\,dt = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{1 + t^2} = \pi ,
> $$
>
> since $\Omega = \cos^2(\arctan t) = 1/(1 + t^2)$ there ([[#^thm-c10-1-1|Theorem §C10.1.1]], part 4). In the coordinates $(\tilde u, \tilde v)$ all Christoffel symbols of $\tilde g$ vanish, so the coordinates are affine along its straight null lines. On $x = t$ ($\tilde u = 0$) the affine parameter is $\tilde\lambda = \tilde v = \arctan t$, running from $0$ to $\pi/2$; this agrees with $d\tilde\lambda/dt = \Omega^2 = \cos^2\tilde v = 1/(1 + t^2)$ of [[#^thm-c10-1-2|Theorem §C10.1.2]], part 3. Part 5.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The squeeze along light rays multiplies the metric by a factor and does nothing else; two-dimensional Minkowski space is conformal to a bounded diamond of itself. Its infinity, infinitely far in $g$, is a finite distance away in $\tilde g$.

*Uses:* [[#^def-c10-1-1|Def. §C10.1.1]], [[#^def-c10-1-2|Def. §C10.1.2]], [[#^thm-c10-1-1|Theorem §C10.1.1]], [[#^thm-c10-1-2|Theorem §C10.1.2]]

The edges of the diamond are of three kinds: corners where timelike curves end, corners where spacelike curves end, and null edges where light rays end. They get names.

> [!definition] Definition §C10.1.3: Timelike Infinities i±, Spatial Infinity i⁰, Null Infinities ℐ±
> *Structure.* In the conformal compactification of Minkowski spacetime ([[#^thm-c10-1-3|Theorem §C10.1.3]] for $n = 2$, [[#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]] for $n = 4$), the conformal boundary ([[#^def-c10-1-2|Def. §C10.1.2]]) is the union of the following pieces.
> - **Future timelike infinity** $i^+$: the point $T = \pi$ ($X = 0$, resp. $\chi = 0$), that is $(\tilde u, \tilde v) = (\pi/2, \pi/2)$. **Past timelike infinity** $i^-$: $T = -\pi$, $(\tilde u, \tilde v) = (-\pi/2, -\pi/2)$.
> - **Spatial infinity** $i^0$: in two dimensions the two points $(T, X) = (0, \pm\pi)$, one for each end $x \to \pm\infty$, at $(\tilde u, \tilde v) = (\mp\pi/2, \pm\pi/2)$; in four dimensions the single point $(T, \chi) = (0, \pi)$, $(\tilde u, \tilde v) = (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$.
> - **Future null infinity** $\mathscr I^+$: the null edges $T + |X| = \pi$, $0 < |X| < \pi$ (two segments, $\tilde v = \pi/2$ on the right, $\tilde u = \pi/2$ on the left); in four dimensions $T + \chi = \pi$, $0 < \chi < \pi$, that is $\tilde v = \pi/2$, $|\tilde u| < \pi/2$. **Past null infinity** $\mathscr I^-$: $T - |X| = -\pi$, resp. $T - \chi = -\pi$ ($\tilde u = -\pi/2$ in four dimensions).
>
> *Reading the names.* The names say which curves end there ([[#^ex-c10-1-1|Example §C10.1.1]]): inertial worldlines begin at $i^-$ and end at $i^+$, light rays begin on $\mathscr I^-$ and end on $\mathscr I^+$, spacelike geodesics begin and end at $i^0$. "Timelike infinity" is not where every timelike curve ends: a worldline that accelerates forever ends on $\mathscr I^+$ (same example).
>
> *Layer change.* "Signals that reach arbitrarily large $r$" in the concrete black-hole definition ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]]) becomes "causal curves that reach $\mathscr I^+$" in the general one ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^def-c10-7-2|Def. §C10.7.2]]); null infinity of a general asymptotically flat spacetime is defined in [[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.1, p. 66 (PDF 67); eq. (4.18), p. 69 (PDF 70) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.4, p. 350 (PDF 365) ("scri"; the endpoints of geodesics) · Hartle, Box 7.1, p. 137 (PDF 138)*

## Four-dimensional Minkowski space and the Einstein static universe

In four dimensions the same squeeze works on the radial null coordinates, but the result is not a piece of flat space: the spheres $r = \text{const}$ come along, and the rescaled metric is that of a closed static universe.

> [!definition] Definition §C10.1.4: Einstein Static Universe
> *Structure.* The **Einstein static universe** is the manifold $\mathbf R \times S^3$ with the metric
>
> $$
> \hat g = -dT^2 + d\chi^2 + \sin^2\chi\,\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr), \qquad T \in \mathbf R,\ \ \chi \in [0, \pi],\ \ \theta \in [0, \pi],\ \ \varphi \in [0, 2\pi) .
> $$
>
> The spatial part is the round metric of the unit three-sphere: it is the induced metric ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]) of $x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 + x_4^2 = 1$ in Euclidean $\mathbf R^4$ with $x_4 = \cos\chi$, $(x_1, x_2, x_3) = \sin\chi\,\vec n(\theta, \varphi)$, $\vec n$ the unit radial vector of the angles, because
>
> $$
> dx_4^2 + |d(\sin\chi\,\vec n)|^2 = \sin^2\chi\,d\chi^2 + \cos^2\chi\,d\chi^2 + \sin^2\chi\,|d\vec n|^2 = d\chi^2 + \sin^2\chi\,d\Omega^2 ,
> $$
>
> using $\vec n\cdot d\vec n = 0$ and $|d\vec n|^2 = d\Omega^2$ (checked by script). $\chi = 0$ and $\chi = \pi$ are the two poles $x_4 = \pm1$; at each $\chi$ the angles cover a two-sphere of radius $\sin\chi$, which shrinks to a point at the poles (a coordinate singularity of the polar kind, like the axis of spherical coordinates).
>
> *Properties.* $\partial_T$ is a timelike Killing vector orthogonal to the slices $T = \text{const}$: the metric is static ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-3|Def. §C3.13.3]]). It is curved: the three-sphere has constant curvature $K = 1$, so its Ricci tensor is $2\hat g_{ij}$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^ex-c3-11-2|Example §C3.11.2]] with $n = 3$), and the full Ricci tensor ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^def-c3-11-1|Def. §C3.11.1]]) is $\operatorname{diag}(0, 2, 2\sin^2\chi, 2\sin^2\chi\sin^2\theta)$, with scalar curvature $6$ (checked by script).
>
> *Name.* It is Einstein's 1917 static model of a closed universe, a solution of his equations with a cosmological constant and dust (cosmology, planned). Here it serves only as the larger spacetime $\tilde M$ into which Minkowski space is conformally embedded.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.2, eq. (4.17) and the paragraph after (p. 68, PDF 69) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.4, eqs. (18.27)–(18.28) and the embedding as the cylinder $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + w^2 = 1$ (pp. 349–350, PDF 364–365; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, $(t', r') = (T, \chi)$)*

^def-c10-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.1.4: Four-Dimensional Minkowski Space Is Conformal to Part of the Einstein Static Universe
> Let $g = -dt^2 + dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$, $t \in \mathbf R$, $r \ge 0$.
> 1. **Null coordinates.** With $u = t - r$, $v = t + r$ (so $u \le v$), $g = -du\,dv + \tfrac14(v - u)^2\,d\Omega^2$.
> 2. **Compactified.** With $u = \tan\tilde u$, $v = \tan\tilde v$, $-\pi/2 < \tilde u \le \tilde v < \pi/2$,
>
> $$
> g = \frac{1}{4\cos^2\tilde u\,\cos^2\tilde v}\Bigl[-4\,d\tilde u\,d\tilde v + \sin^2(\tilde v - \tilde u)\,d\Omega^2\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> 3. **The embedding.** With $T = \tilde v + \tilde u$, $\chi = \tilde v - \tilde u$ and $\Omega = 2\cos\tilde u\cos\tilde v = \cos T + \cos\chi$,
>
> $$
> \Omega^2g = -dT^2 + d\chi^2 + \sin^2\chi\,d\Omega^2 ,
> $$
>
> the Einstein static universe ([[#^def-c10-1-4|Def. §C10.1.4]]). The map $\psi(t, r, \theta, \varphi) = (T, \chi, \theta, \varphi)$ is a conformal embedding of Minkowski space onto the region $\{0 \le \chi < \pi,\ |T| + \chi < \pi\}$, with inverse
>
> $$
> t = \frac{\sin T}{\cos T + \cos\chi}, \qquad r = \frac{\sin\chi}{\cos T + \cos\chi} .
> $$
>
> 4. **The boundary.** The closure $\{0 \le \chi \le \pi,\ |T| + \chi \le \pi\}$ is compact, so this is a conformal compactification ([[#^def-c10-1-2|Def. §C10.1.2]]). Its boundary consists of $i^\pm$, $i^0$ and $\mathscr I^\pm$ ([[#^def-c10-1-3|Def. §C10.1.3]]): $i^+$, $i^-$ and $i^0$ are single points of the Einstein static universe (poles of $S^3$), and $\mathscr I^+$, $\mathscr I^-$ are null hypersurfaces of it, each a cone of two-spheres of radius $\sin\chi$ with the topology $\mathbf R \times S^2$.
> 5. **The rescaled metric is curved.** $\Omega^2g$ has Ricci tensor $\operatorname{diag}(0, 2, 2\sin^2\chi, 2\sin^2\chi\sin^2\theta)$, while $g$ has zero curvature: the compactified metric is not a vacuum solution.
>
> The interior of a collapsing ball of dust is conformal, with a time-dependent factor, to a strip $0 \le \chi \le \chi_0$ of the same Einstein static universe ([[§C10.3 Penrose Diagrams of Kruskal and of Collapse#^thm-c10-3-3|Theorem §C10.3.3]]).
>
> *Model:* four-dimensional Minkowski spacetime; *applies:* exactly; in $d$ dimensions the same steps give $\mathbf R \times S^{d-1}$ (Couzens, (4.8)–(4.16)).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.2, eqs. (4.8)–(4.18), Fig. 9 (pp. 67–69, PDF 68–70) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.4, eqs. (18.13)–(18.28), Figs. 18.6–18.9 (pp. 348–351, PDF 363–366) · Hartle, Box 7.1, eqs. (a)–(c) and Problem 7.6 (pp. 137, 164; PDF 138, 165) · parts 2, 3 and 5 checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (null coordinates). As in [[#^thm-c10-1-3|Theorem §C10.1.3]], Step 1, with $x$ replaced by $r$: $t = \tfrac12(u + v)$, $r = \tfrac12(v - u)$, $-dt^2 + dr^2 = -du\,dv$, and $r^2 = \tfrac14(v - u)^2$. The condition $r \ge 0$ is $v \ge u$. Part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (compactify). $-du\,dv = -d\tilde u\,d\tilde v/(\cos^2\tilde u\cos^2\tilde v) = -4\,d\tilde u\,d\tilde v/(4\cos^2\tilde u\cos^2\tilde v)$, and
>
> $$
> \tfrac14(v - u)^2 = \tfrac14(\tan\tilde v - \tan\tilde u)^2 = \frac14\,\frac{\sin^2(\tilde v - \tilde u)}{\cos^2\tilde u\cos^2\tilde v} .
> $$
>
> Both terms carry the factor $1/(4\cos^2\tilde u\cos^2\tilde v)$, with a plus sign in front. Since $\arctan$ is increasing, $v \ge u$ becomes $\tilde v \ge \tilde u$. Part 2.
>
> **Step 3** (rescale and rotate). Multiplying by $\Omega^2 = 4\cos^2\tilde u\cos^2\tilde v$ leaves $-4\,d\tilde u\,d\tilde v + \sin^2(\tilde v - \tilde u)\,d\Omega^2$. With $\tilde u = \tfrac12(T - \chi)$, $\tilde v = \tfrac12(T + \chi)$,
>
> $$
> -4\,d\tilde u\,d\tilde v = -(dT - d\chi)(dT + d\chi) = -dT^2 + d\chi^2, \qquad \sin^2(\tilde v - \tilde u) = \sin^2\chi ,
> $$
>
> and $2\cos\tilde u\cos\tilde v = \cos(\tilde v - \tilde u) + \cos(\tilde v + \tilde u) = \cos\chi + \cos T$. The inverse follows as in [[#^thm-c10-1-3|Theorem §C10.1.3]], Step 4: $t = \sin(\tilde u + \tilde v)/(2\cos\tilde u\cos\tilde v) = \sin T/\Omega$ and $r = \sin(\tilde v - \tilde u)/(2\cos\tilde u\cos\tilde v) = \sin\chi/\Omega$.
>
> **Step 4** (the region). $\tilde v \ge \tilde u$ is $\chi \ge 0$. $\tilde u > -\pi/2$ is $T - \chi > -\pi$, and $\tilde v < \pi/2$ is $T + \chi < \pi$; the other two bounds, $\tilde u < \pi/2$ and $\tilde v > -\pi/2$, follow from $\tilde u \le \tilde v$. For $T \ge 0$ the binding condition is $T + \chi < \pi$, for $T < 0$ it is $-T + \chi < \pi$: together $|T| + \chi < \pi$, which forces $\chi < \pi$. The map is a bijection onto this region with smooth inverse (Step 3), and the angles are untouched. On the region $\Omega = 2\cos\tfrac12(T + \chi)\cos\tfrac12(T - \chi) > 0$, since $|T \pm \chi| < \pi$. Part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (the boundary). The closure $\{0 \le \chi \le \pi,\ |T| + \chi \le \pi\}$ is a closed and bounded set of $(T, \chi)$ times the compact sphere of angles, hence compact. Its points not in the region are: $T = \pi$, $\chi = 0$ and $T = -\pi$, $\chi = 0$, where the two-sphere has radius $\sin 0 = 0$, so each is one point (the pole $x_4 = 1$ of $S^3$ at times $\pm\pi$): $i^\pm$; $T = 0$, $\chi = \pi$, the other pole, one point: $i^0$; and $T + \chi = \pi$ or $T - \chi = -\pi$ with $0 < \chi < \pi$, a one-parameter family ($\chi$) of two-spheres: $\mathscr I^\pm \cong \mathbf R \times S^2$. On $T + \chi = \pi$ the radial tangent direction $\partial_T - \partial_\chi$ has $\hat g$-norm $-1 + 1 = 0$, while the sphere directions are spacelike and orthogonal to it: the surface contains a null direction orthogonal to all its tangents, a null hypersurface (defined in §C10.5, [[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]]). Likewise for $\mathscr I^-$. Part 4.
>
> **Step 6** (curvature). Part 5 is [[#^def-c10-1-4|Def. §C10.1.4]], *Properties*; Minkowski space has $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The four-dimensional computation is the two-dimensional one plus the spheres, and $r \ge 0$ halves the diamond. The new feature is topological: the spheres shrink to points at $\chi = 0$ and $\chi = \pi$, so $i^0$ is one point in four dimensions, where two-dimensional space had two ends.

> [!caution] Caution: Slips in the sources (as verified)
> - Couzens, GR2, eq. (4.15) (p. 68, PDF 69) prints the compactified metric with an overall minus sign in front of the bracket $[-4d\tilde u\,d\tilde v + \sin^2(\tilde v - \tilde u)d\Omega^2]$. The bracket already carries the sign: the factor is $+1/(4\cos^2\tilde u\cos^2\tilde v)$, as (4.16) requires ([[#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]], part 2).
> - Couzens, p. 69 (PDF 70): each point of the diagram is a two-sphere "with radius $\sin^2\chi$"; the radius is $\sin\chi$ (the metric coefficient is $\sin^2\chi$). The caption of Fig. 8 (p. 67, PDF 68) calls the dashed curves "$r = \text{const}$"; in two dimensions they are $x = \text{const}$.
> - Couzens, p. 66 (PDF 67): "All past and future directed timelike curves end up at $i^\mp$". This holds for timelike geodesics, not for all timelike curves: the uniformly accelerated worldline ends on $\mathscr I^+$ ([[#^ex-c10-1-1|Example §C10.1.1]], part (d)). d'Inverno states the rule for geodesics and adds that non-geodesic curves need not obey it (p. 350, PDF 365); Hartle, Box 7.1 (p. 137, PDF 138), speaks of "particle trajectories" in the same sense.
> - Hartle, Box 7.1 (p. 137, PDF 138): his $(t', r')$ are half of $(T, \chi)$, so his diagram is the half-diamond $|t'| + r' < \pi/2$; $i^\mp$ ("$I_\mp$") are the points $t' = \mp\pi/2$, $r' = 0$, where the box prints "$t' = -\infty$" and "$t' = +\infty$" (those are the values of $t$). In his coordinates the metric of Problem 7.6 is $ds^2 = \bigl[-dt'^2 + dr'^2 + \tfrac14\sin^2(2r')\,d\Omega^2\bigr]/\bigl[\cos^2(t' + r')\cos^2(t' - r')\bigr]$ (checked by script).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C10.1.1: Where Geodesics Begin and End
> *Setup.* Four-dimensional Minkowski space in inertial coordinates $(t, \vec x)$, $r = |\vec x|$, compactified by [[#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]]; a curve's place in the diagram is $\tilde u = \arctan(t - r)$, $\tilde v = \arctan(t + r)$, $T = \tilde v + \tilde u$, $\chi = \tilde v - \tilde u$. Geodesics of flat space are straight lines ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^ex-c3-7-1|Example §C3.7.1]]). The question: where on the boundary ([[#^def-c10-1-3|Def. §C10.1.3]]) does each kind begin and end?
>
> *(a) Inertial worldline,* $\vec x = \vec x_0 + \vec w\,t$, $|\vec w| < 1$. By the triangle inequality $r \le |\vec x_0| + |\vec w|\,|t|$. As $t \to +\infty$, $u = t - r \ge (1 - |\vec w|)\,t - |\vec x_0| \to \infty$ and $v \ge u$, so $(\tilde u, \tilde v) \to (\pi/2, \pi/2)$, that is $(T, \chi) \to (\pi, 0) = i^+$. As $t \to -\infty$, $v = t + r \le -(1 - |\vec w|)\,|t| + |\vec x_0| \to -\infty$ and $u \le v$: $(T, \chi) \to (-\pi, 0) = i^-$. For $\vec x_0 = (1, 0, 0)$, $\vec w = (0.5, 0.3, 0)$ the script finds $(T, \chi) = (\pm3.141593, 1.8\times10^{-9})$ at $t = \pm10^9$.
>
> *(b) Light ray,* $\vec x = \vec x_0 + \hat n\,t$, $|\hat n| = 1$. Expanding $r = \sqrt{t^2 + 2t\,\hat n\cdot\vec x_0 + |\vec x_0|^2}$ for large $|t|$,
>
> $$
> r = |t| + \frac{t}{|t|}\,\hat n\cdot\vec x_0 + O(1/|t|) .
> $$
>
> As $t \to +\infty$: $u = t - r \to -\hat n\cdot\vec x_0$, finite, and $v \to \infty$, so $(\tilde u, \tilde v) \to \bigl(\arctan(-\hat n\cdot\vec x_0), \pi/2\bigr)$, a point with $T + \chi = \pi$, $0 < \chi < \pi$: on $\mathscr I^+$, in the direction $\hat n$ on the sphere. As $t \to -\infty$: $v \to -\hat n\cdot\vec x_0$ and $u \to -\infty$: on $\mathscr I^-$, in the direction $-\hat n$. A point of $\mathscr I^+$ is thus labelled by the limiting value of the retarded time $u$ and a direction, which is $\mathbf R \times S^2$ again. For $\vec x_0 = (-2, 1, 0)$, $\hat n = (1, 0, 0)$: $\hat n\cdot\vec x_0 = -2$, the ray arrives at $u = 2$, $(T, \chi) = (\pi/2 + \arctan 2, \pi/2 - \arctan 2) = (2.6779, 0.4636)$, and came from $v = 2$, $(T, \chi) = (-0.4636, 2.6779)$. This ray passes the origin at distance $1$; it is not radial, and in the diagram it is a curve, not a $45°$ line (figure below).
>
> *(c) Spacelike geodesic,* $\vec x = \vec x_0 + \hat m\,s$, $t = t_0 + w\,s$, $|\hat m| = 1$, $|w| < 1$. As $s \to \pm\infty$, $r = |s| + O(1)$ and $t = ws + t_0$, so $u = t - r \le -(1 - |w|)|s| + \text{const} \to -\infty$ and $v = t + r \ge (1 - |w|)|s| - \text{const} \to +\infty$: $(\tilde u, \tilde v) \to (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$, $(T, \chi) \to (0, \pi) = i^0$, at both ends. For $t = 0.6\,s$, $\vec x = (s, 2, 0)$ the script confirms $(0, \pi)$ at both ends.
>
> *(d) Uniformly accelerated worldline* (not a geodesic). The hyperbola of [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$; here $c = 1$ and the origin moved to the centre of the hyperbola) is $x = \sqrt{a^{-2} + t^2}$, $y = z = 0$, with proper acceleration $a$; here $r = x$. As $t \to +\infty$,
>
> $$
> u = t - \sqrt{a^{-2} + t^2} = -\frac{a^{-2}}{t + \sqrt{a^{-2} + t^2}} \to 0^-, \qquad v \to \infty ,
> $$
>
> so $(\tilde u, \tilde v) \to (0, \pi/2)$, $(T, \chi) \to (\pi/2, \pi/2)$: the middle of $\mathscr I^+$, not $i^+$. As $t \to -\infty$, $v \to 0^+$, $u \to -\infty$: $(T, \chi) \to (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ on $\mathscr I^-$. For $a = 1$ the whole worldline is a vertical line: $uv = t^2 - r^2 = -1$ gives $\tan\tilde u\tan\tilde v = -1$, and then $\cos\chi = \cos(\tilde v - \tilde u) = \cos\tilde u\cos\tilde v\,(1 + \tan\tilde u\tan\tilde v) = 0$, $\chi = \pi/2$. Its proper time $\tau = \operatorname{arsinh}(at)/a$ is unbounded, yet it ends on null infinity. Events above the null line $u = 0$ ($t > x$) can never send it a signal, since every point of its worldline has $u < 0$: a horizon in flat space ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^rem-b3-3-4|§B3.3, Remark: A horizon, and where this leads]]), the subject of [[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons|§C10.2]].
>
> *What it shows.* The three corners and two null edges are sorted by geodesics: timelike ones from $i^-$ to $i^+$, null ones from $\mathscr I^-$ to $\mathscr I^+$, spacelike ones from $i^0$ to $i^0$. Accelerated observers break the rule, which is why the names refer to geodesics.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.4, Fig. 18.8, p. 350–351 (PDF 365–366) · Couzens, GR2, p. 66, p. 69 (PDF 67, 70) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C10.1.2: A Light Ray through the Origin in the Diagram
> *Setup.* Four-dimensional Minkowski space and its diagram ([[#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]]). Observer A is at rest at $r = 1$ in the direction $\hat n$, observer B at rest at $r = 1$ in the direction $-\hat n$. At $t = 0$ A sends a light pulse radially inward. Where is it in the diagram, and how is the diagram read? (Hartle's Problem 7.4 asks for the curves $r = \text{const}$ and $t = \text{const}$ that this uses.)
>
> *Step 1* (the grid). By the inverse map of part 3, $r = r_0$ is the curve $\sin\chi = r_0(\cos T + \cos\chi)$, and $t = t_0$ is $\sin T = t_0(\cos T + \cos\chi)$. A static observer is an inertial worldline with $\vec w = 0$, so $r = 1$ runs from $i^-$ to $i^+$ ([[#^ex-c10-1-1|Example §C10.1.1]], (a)) and crosses $T = 0$ where $\sin\chi = 1 + \cos\chi$, at $\chi = \pi/2$. A slice $t = t_0$ meets the axis $\chi = 0$ at $\sin T = t_0(\cos T + 1)$, $\tan(T/2) = t_0$, $T = 2\arctan t_0$, and runs to $i^0$ as $r \to \infty$.
>
> *Step 2* (the ingoing leg). The pulse has $v = t + r = 1$, so $\tilde v = \pi/4$: in the diagram the line $T + \chi = 2\tilde v = \pi/2$, at $-45°$. The emission event E, $u = -1$, $\tilde u = -\pi/4$, is $(T, \chi) = (0, \pi/2)$. Followed back, $u \to -\infty$, $\tilde u \to -\pi/2$: the line starts on $\mathscr I^-$ at $(T, \chi) = (-\pi/4, 3\pi/4)$, and a pulse that had been travelling inward forever would have come from there. It reaches the axis when $\tilde u = \tilde v = \pi/4$: $T = \pi/2$, $\chi = 0$, the event O at $t = 1$, $r = 0$.
>
> *Step 3* (through the centre). After O the pulse moves outward in the direction $-\hat n$, with $u = t - r = 1$, $\tilde u = \pi/4$: the line $T - \chi = \pi/2$, at $+45°$. In the diagram it bounces off the axis; in space it goes straight through the centre, because a point of the diagram is a whole sphere, and the outgoing pulse is at the antipodal point $(\theta, \varphi) \to (\pi - \theta, \varphi + \pi)$ of each sphere. On the Einstein static universe it runs along a great circle of $S^3$ through the pole $\chi = 0$ (Couzens' Fig. 9).
>
> *Step 4* (reception and beyond). B receives it at $t = 2$ ($u = 1$, $v = 3$): $(T, \chi) = (\pi/4 + \arctan 3, \arctan 3 - \pi/4) = (2.0344, 0.4636)$, the event R. It continues to $\mathscr I^+$, reached as $v \to \infty$ at $(T, \chi) = (3\pi/4, \pi/4)$.
>
> *Step 5* (reading the diagram). Which events can send a signal to some point of the sphere of R ($t = 2$, $r = 1$)? Those within distance $2 - t'$ of the unit sphere at time $t'$: $|r' - 1| \le 2 - t'$, that is $t' + r' \le 3$ and $t' - r' \le 1$, that is $\tilde v' \le \arctan 3$ and $\tilde u' \le \pi/4$. In the diagram this is the region below both $45°$ lines through R, Hartle's shaded region in Box 7.1. E lies strictly inside it ($\tilde u = -\pi/4 < \pi/4$, $\tilde v = \pi/4 < \arctan 3$): a slower-than-light signal from E reaches the points of R's sphere near the direction $\hat n$. The event R itself, on the far side, is reached from E only by light through the centre, since $|\vec x_R - \vec x_E| = 2 = t_R - t_E$: E and R are null separated although the point E lies inside the region. A point of the diagram is a sphere, and the diagram answers questions about spheres. What it does not give is the elapsed time $\Delta t = 2$ ([[#^def-c10-1-2|Def. §C10.1.2]]); that comes from the inverse map.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Box 7.1 and Problem 7.4 (pp. 137–138, 164; PDF 138–139, 165) · Couzens, GR2, Fig. 9 and its caption (p. 69, PDF 70) · d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 18.8 and its answer (pp. 353, 588; PDF 368, 603) · numbers checked by script*
