Two pictures of black holes are on the table. The Kruskal diagram ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]) shows the whole vacuum solution, but not its infinity: a light ray that escapes runs off the edge of the page, so "which events can send a signal to a distant observer?", the question that defines a black hole ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]]), cannot be read off it. The collapsing star of [[§C9.3 White Dwarfs, Neutron Stars and Collapse#^thm-c9-3-6|Theorem §C9.3.6]] is the physical case, and its interior has not been drawn at all. This section asks: *what do the Kruskal spacetime and a collapsing star look like with infinity brought to a finite place, and where, in the collapse, does the horizon begin?*

*The naive attempts, computed.* (1) Squeeze the Kruskal plane the obvious way, coordinate by coordinate: $T = \tan T'$, $X = \tan X'$. Then $-dT^2 + dX^2 = -\sec^4T'\,dT'^2 + \sec^4X'\,dX'^2$, and a radial light ray has $dT'/dX' = \pm\cos^2T'/\cos^2X'$. At $(T', X') = (0, 1.2)$ the slope is $\pm1/\cos^2 1.2 = \pm7.6$, at $(1.2, 0)$ it is $\pm0.13$: the light cones stand up in one place and lie down in another, and the picture can no longer be read by eye. The recipe of [[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-2|Def. §C10.1.2]] squeezes the two *null* coordinates instead, which keeps light at $45°$. (2) "The horizon of a collapsing star forms when its surface crosses $r = 2M$": d'Inverno & Vickers' Figs. 17.13–17.14 draw it so. For a dust star released from rest at $4M$, the event horizon in fact appears at the centre of the star after $4.22M$ of the star's proper time, while the surface reaches $2M$ only at $7.27M$ ([[#^thm-c10-3-4|Theorem §C10.3.4]]).

The section builds the answer in order. The Kruskal metric is rewritten in compactified null coordinates ([[#^thm-c10-3-1|Theorem §C10.3.1]]), and the resulting diagram is read: four regions, two copies of each piece of infinity, the singularity as two horizontal lines ([[#^thm-c10-3-2|Theorem §C10.3.2]]), with a caution about what the conformal factor does at the corners ([[#^cau-c10-3-1|Caution: Conformal factor not smooth at i± and i⁰ in Kruskal]]). Then a star is put in: the Oppenheimer–Snyder spacetime ([[#^def-c10-3-1|Def. §C10.3.1]]) solves Einstein's equations with dust inside and vacuum outside, and its diagram keeps only the exterior and the black hole ([[#^thm-c10-3-3|Theorem §C10.3.3]]). Finally the event horizon is located inside the star and compared with the first trapped sphere ([[#^thm-c10-3-4|Theorem §C10.3.4]]).

**Setup.** The Kruskal spacetime $\mathcal K$ with $M > 0$, coordinates and regions I–IV as in [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^def-c7-3-1|Def. §C7.3.1]] and [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]] (I exterior, II black hole, III second exterior, IV white hole). The collapsing star is spherical, pressure-free and uniform (dust), released from rest at areal radius $R_0 > 2M$; before release it is held static by a star whose interior is not modelled. Spherical symmetry is kept throughout: every point of a diagram stands for a round 2-sphere ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-2|Def. §C10.1.2]]), and "trapped" is tested on these round spheres only. The results are exact for these models; the figures are computed from the formulas, and every number was checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here $M \to GM/c^2$, $t, \tau \to ct, c\tau$, while $U$, $V$, $\tilde U$, $\tilde V$, $\eta$, $\chi$ are dimensionless); Kruskal $U = -e^{-u/4M}$, $V = e^{v/4M}$, $u = t - r_\ast$, $v = t + r_\ast$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^def-c7-3-1|Def. §C7.3.1]]); compactified $\tilde U = \arctan U$, $\tilde V = \arctan V$, drawn with $\tilde T = \tfrac12(\tilde U + \tilde V)$ up and $\tilde X = \tfrac12(\tilde V - \tilde U)$ to the right (Couzens' $T$, $X$; the tildes keep them apart from the Kruskal $T$, $X$ of §C7.3); $G(r) \equiv (1 - r/2M)e^{r/2M}$; $dU\,dV$ is the symmetrized product. Region labels of the other books: [[#^cau-c10-3-2|Caution: Slips in the sources on these diagrams]]. Level B and d'Inverno & Vickers use $+{-}{-}{-}$.

## Compactifying the Kruskal plane

The Kruskal metric already has the form "positive factor times $-dU\,dV$" in the radial plane. That is the starting point of the Minkowski recipe ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^thm-c10-1-3|Theorem §C10.1.3]]), so the same $\arctan$ applies.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.3.1: The Compactified Kruskal Metric
> On the Kruskal spacetime $\mathcal K$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]) put $U = \tan\tilde U$, $V = \tan\tilde V$ with $\tilde U, \tilde V \in (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$.
> 1. **Chart and range.** $(\tilde U, \tilde V, \theta, \varphi)$ is a chart on all of $\mathcal K$. Its range is
>
> $$
> |\tilde U| < \frac\pi2, \qquad |\tilde V| < \frac\pi2, \qquad |\tilde U + \tilde V| < \frac\pi2 ,
> $$
>
>    and the singularity $UV = 1$ ($r = 0$) is $\tilde U + \tilde V = \pm\pi/2$, i.e. $\tilde T = \pm\pi/4$: the sign $+$ for the future branch ($U, V > 0$), $-$ for the past one.
> 2. **The metric.** With $r = r(U, V)$ of [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], part 3,
>
> $$
> ds^2 = \frac{1}{4\cos^2\tilde U\cos^2\tilde V}\Bigl[-\frac{128M^3}{r}\,e^{-r/2M}\,d\tilde U\,d\tilde V + 4r^2\cos^2\tilde U\cos^2\tilde V\,d\Omega^2\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> 3. **The rescaled metric.** With $\Omega = 2\cos\tilde U\cos\tilde V > 0$, a conformal transformation ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-1|Def. §C10.1.1]]),
>
> $$
> \tilde g = \Omega^2g = -\frac{128M^3}{r}\,e^{-r/2M}\,d\tilde U\,d\tilde V + 4r^2\cos^2\tilde U\cos^2\tilde V\,d\Omega^2 = \frac{128M^3}{r}\,e^{-r/2M}\bigl(-d\tilde T^2 + d\tilde X^2\bigr) + 4r^2\cos^2\tilde U\cos^2\tilde V\,d\Omega^2 .
> $$
>
>    Radial null curves are $\tilde U = \text{const}$ and $\tilde V = \text{const}$, lines at $\pm45°$ in the $(\tilde X, \tilde T)$ plane, and the causal character of every vector is that of $g$ ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^thm-c10-1-1|Theorem §C10.1.1]]).
>
> *Model:* the Kruskal spacetime, $M > 0$; *applies:* exactly, on all of $\mathcal K$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.4, eqs. (4.27)–(4.31), p. 72 (PDF 73) (printed without the factor $4$ in the sphere term: [[#^cau-c10-3-2|Caution: Slips in the sources on these diagrams]]) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.5, p. 351 (PDF 366) ($v'' = \tan^{-1}[v'/(2m)^{1/2}]$, $w''$ likewise, which are $\tilde V$, $\tilde U$) · Hartle, Box 12.5, p. 274 (PDF 275) · 't Hooft, ch. 11, eq. (11.49), p. 51 (PDF 52) · every step checked by script*

^thm-c10-3-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the chart). $\arctan : \mathbf R \to (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ is smooth and strictly increasing, with smooth inverse $\tan$. Changing $U$ and $V$ separately by it is therefore a change of chart ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]). $\tilde U$ is constant exactly where $U$ is, so the radial null curves keep their labels ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], part 1).
>
> **Step 2** (the range). $\mathcal K$ is $UV < 1$. Expand with $\tan = \sin/\cos$:
>
> $$
> (1 - \tan\tilde U\tan\tilde V)\cos\tilde U\cos\tilde V = \cos\tilde U\cos\tilde V - \sin\tilde U\sin\tilde V = \cos(\tilde U + \tilde V) .
> $$
>
> Since $\cos\tilde U\cos\tilde V > 0$ on the open square, $UV < 1$ holds exactly where $\cos(\tilde U + \tilde V) > 0$. On the square $|\tilde U + \tilde V| < \pi$, and there $\cos(\tilde U + \tilde V) > 0$ means $|\tilde U + \tilde V| < \pi/2$. Likewise $UV = 1$ means $\cos(\tilde U + \tilde V) = 0$, $\tilde U + \tilde V = \pm\pi/2$; on the future branch $U, V > 0$, so $\tilde U, \tilde V > 0$ and the sign is $+$. With $\tilde T = \tfrac12(\tilde U + \tilde V)$ this is $\tilde T = \pm\pi/4$. Part 1 (Couzens' (4.31)).
>
> **Step 3** (differentials). $dU = d\tan\tilde U = \sec^2\tilde U\,d\tilde U$ and $dV = \sec^2\tilde V\,d\tilde V$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]]).
>
> **Step 4** (substitute into the Kruskal metric). From [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], part 2,
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -\frac{32M^3}{r}e^{-r/2M}\,\frac{d\tilde U\,d\tilde V}{\cos^2\tilde U\cos^2\tilde V} + r^2d\Omega^2 = \frac{1}{4\cos^2\tilde U\cos^2\tilde V}\Bigl[-\frac{4\cdot32M^3}{r}e^{-r/2M}\,d\tilde U\,d\tilde V + 4r^2\cos^2\tilde U\cos^2\tilde V\,d\Omega^2\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> pulling out the common factor $1/(4\cos^2\tilde U\cos^2\tilde V)$: the first term is multiplied by $4$ inside the bracket, the second by $4\cos^2\tilde U\cos^2\tilde V$. With $4\cdot32 = 128$ this is part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (rescale). $\Omega = 2\cos\tilde U\cos\tilde V$ is smooth and positive on the open square, and $\Omega^2 = 4\cos^2\tilde U\cos^2\tilde V$ cancels the prefactor of part 2: $\tilde g = \Omega^2g$ is the bracket.
>
> **Step 6** (the drawing coordinates). With $\tilde U = \tilde T - \tilde X$, $\tilde V = \tilde T + \tilde X$, $d\tilde U\,d\tilde V = (d\tilde T - d\tilde X)(d\tilde T + d\tilde X) = d\tilde T^2 - d\tilde X^2$ (symmetrized product), which gives the second form of part 3. A radial displacement is null exactly when $d\tilde U\,d\tilde V = 0$, because the factor $128M^3e^{-r/2M}/r$ is positive: $d\tilde U = 0$ or $d\tilde V = 0$, the lines $\tilde T = \pm\tilde X + \text{const}$. Part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The Minkowski squeeze works unchanged because the Kruskal radial metric is already conformal to $-dU\,dV$; the whole difference from Minkowski space is the region $|\tilde U + \tilde V| < \pi/2$ cut out of the square by the singularity. The two radial terms of the bracket carry different powers of $\cos\tilde U\cos\tilde V$ before rescaling ($0$ and $2$), which is where a factor $4$ is easily lost.

## The Penrose diagram of the Kruskal spacetime

Now the diagram can be drawn and its boundary named. Each piece of the boundary is identified by the family of curves that ends on it, as for Minkowski space ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-3|Def. §C10.1.3]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.3.2: The Penrose Diagram of the Kruskal Spacetime
> In the $(\tilde X, \tilde T)$ plane of [[#^thm-c10-3-1|Theorem §C10.3.1]]:
> 1. **Shape and regions.** $\mathcal K$ is the open hexagon with vertices $(\tilde X, \tilde T) = (\pm\pi/2, 0)$ and $(\pm\pi/4, \pm\pi/4)$. Region I ($\tilde U < 0 < \tilde V$) is the open square with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(\pi/4, \pi/4)$, $(\pi/2, 0)$, $(\pi/4, -\pi/4)$; region III is its mirror image under $\tilde X \mapsto -\tilde X$. Region II ($\tilde U, \tilde V > 0$) is the triangle $(0, 0)$, $(\pi/4, \pi/4)$, $(-\pi/4, \pi/4)$; region IV is its mirror image under $\tilde T \mapsto -\tilde T$.
> 2. **Horizons and singularity.** The horizons $U = 0$ and $V = 0$ are the diagonals $\tilde X = \pm\tilde T$ through the bifurcation sphere at the centre. The singularity $r = 0$ is the two horizontal segments $\tilde T = \pm\pi/4$, $|\tilde X| < \pi/4$, both spacelike.
> 3. **Infinity of region I** (names of [[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-3|Def. §C10.1.3]]):
>    - $\mathscr I^+$: the null edge $\tilde V = \pi/2$, $-\pi/2 < \tilde U < 0$. Every outgoing radial light ray of region I ends there, with $r \to \infty$ and unbounded affine parameter.
>    - $\mathscr I^-$: the null edge $\tilde U = -\pi/2$, $0 < \tilde V < \pi/2$, where every ingoing radial light ray of region I begins.
>    - $i^+ = (\tilde U, \tilde V) = (0, \pi/2)$ and $i^- = (-\pi/2, 0)$: every static worldline $r = \text{const} > 2M$ begins at $i^-$ ($t \to -\infty$) and ends at $i^+$ ($t \to +\infty$).
>    - $i^0 = (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$: every slice $t = \text{const}$ of region I runs from the bifurcation sphere to $i^0$ ($r \to \infty$).
>
>    Region III has its own $\mathscr I^\pm$, $i^\pm$, $i^0$, the mirror images. The diagram has two of each.
> 4. ⚑ **All curves $r = \text{const}$ meet at $i^\pm$.** A curve $r = \text{const} < 2M$ in region II runs from $i^+$ of region III to $i^+$ of region I, and so does the singularity $r = 0$: its two ends are the two points $i^+$. In region IV the same holds with $i^-$.
>
> *Model:* the Kruskal spacetime; *applies:* exactly; the identification of the boundary uses radial curves only.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.4, Fig. 11 (left), pp. 72–73 (PDF 73–74) ("Lines of constant $r$ meet at $i^\pm$ and this includes the curvature singularity") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.5, Fig. 18.10, pp. 351–352 (PDF 366–367) (regions I, II, I′, II′; "Both regions I and I′ have their own future, past, and null infinities") · Hartle, Box 12.5, p. 274 (PDF 275) · 't Hooft, ch. 11, Fig. 4a, p. 51 (PDF 52) · limits checked by script*

^thm-c10-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (regions). By [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], part 2, the regions are fixed by the signs of $U$ and $V$, and $\arctan$ keeps signs. On region I, $\tilde U \in (-\pi/2, 0)$ and $\tilde V \in (0, \pi/2)$, so $\tilde U + \tilde V \in (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ automatically: the whole open square belongs to $\mathcal K$. Its corners map by $(\tilde X, \tilde T) = (\tfrac12(\tilde V - \tilde U), \tfrac12(\tilde U + \tilde V))$:
>
> $$
> (\tilde U, \tilde V) = (0, 0) \mapsto (0, 0), \quad (0, \tfrac\pi2) \mapsto (\tfrac\pi4, \tfrac\pi4), \quad (-\tfrac\pi2, \tfrac\pi2) \mapsto (\tfrac\pi2, 0), \quad (-\tfrac\pi2, 0) \mapsto (\tfrac\pi4, -\tfrac\pi4) .
> $$
>
> On region II, $\tilde U, \tilde V \in (0, \pi/2)$ with $\tilde U + \tilde V < \pi/2$: the triangle with corners $(0, 0)$, $(0, \pi/2) \mapsto (\pi/4, \pi/4)$, $(\pi/2, 0) \mapsto (-\pi/4, \pi/4)$. The isometry $(U, V) \mapsto (V, U)$ of [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]] is $\tilde X \mapsto -\tilde X$ and carries I to III. The map $(U, V) \mapsto (-V, -U)$ leaves $UV$ and $dU\,dV$ unchanged, so it is also an isometry ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-1|Def. §C3.13.1]]); it is $\tilde T \mapsto -\tilde T$ and carries II to IV. Part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (horizons and singularity). $U = 0$ is $\tilde U = 0$, i.e. $\tilde T = \tilde X$; $V = 0$ is $\tilde T = -\tilde X$. The singularity is $\tilde T = \pm\pi/4$ by [[#^thm-c10-3-1|Theorem §C10.3.1]], part 1; inside the square $|\tilde X| = \tfrac12|\tilde V - \tilde U| < \pi/4$ there. Along it $d\tilde T = 0$, so $\tilde g$ restricted to its tangent is $(128M^3e^{-r/2M}/r)\,d\tilde X^2 > 0$: spacelike, as [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], part 4 found in the uncompactified chart. Part 2.
>
> **Step 3** ($\mathscr I^\pm$). An outgoing radial light ray of region I is $U = U_0 < 0$, $V \in (0, \infty)$. In the diagram it is the $45°$ segment $\tilde U = \tilde U_0$, $\tilde V \in (0, \pi/2)$. Along it $UV = U_0V \to -\infty$ as $V \to \infty$; since $G$ decreases strictly from $1$ to $-\infty$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], part 3), $r = G^{-1}(UV) \to \infty$. On a radial light ray $r$ is an affine parameter (Step 8 of the derivation of [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]), so the affine parameter is unbounded: the ray does not stop in spacetime, it reaches infinity. Its endpoint $(\tilde U_0, \pi/2)$ runs over the whole open segment $\tilde V = \pi/2$, $\tilde U \in (-\pi/2, 0)$ as $U_0$ runs over $(-\infty, 0)$. An ingoing ray $V = V_0 > 0$ traced to the past has $U \to -\infty$, $UV \to -\infty$, $r \to \infty$, and starts at $(-\pi/2, \tilde V_0)$ on $\tilde U = -\pi/2$.
>
> **Step 4** ($i^\pm$). A static observer at $r_1 > 2M$ has $U = -e^{-(t - r_{\ast1})/4M}$, $V = e^{(t + r_{\ast1})/4M}$, $r_{\ast1} = r_\ast(r_1)$. As $t \to +\infty$, $U \to 0^-$ and $V \to \infty$, so $(\tilde U, \tilde V) \to (0, \pi/2)$; as $t \to -\infty$, $U \to -\infty$ and $V \to 0^+$, so $(\tilde U, \tilde V) \to (-\pi/2, 0)$. Neither limit depends on $r_1$, and the observer's proper time $\sqrt{1 - 2M/r_1}\,t$ is unbounded both ways.
>
> **Step 5** ($i^0$). On the slice $t = t_1$ of region I, $U/V = -k$ with $k = e^{-t_1/2M}$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], part 1), so $UV = -kV^2$. As $r \to \infty$, $UV = G(r) \to -\infty$, hence $V \to \infty$ and $U = -kV \to -\infty$: $(\tilde U, \tilde V) \to (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ for every $t_1$. As $r \to 2M^+$, $UV \to 0$ with $U/V$ fixed, so $U, V \to 0$: the slice starts at the bifurcation sphere.
>
> **Step 6** ($r = \text{const}$ inside). In region II, $r = r_2 < 2M$ is $UV = c$ with $c = G(r_2) \in (0, 1)$. As $V \to \infty$, $U = c/V \to 0^+$ and $(\tilde U, \tilde V) \to (0, \pi/2)$, the point $i^+$ of region I. As $V \to 0^+$, $U \to \infty$ and $(\tilde U, \tilde V) \to (\pi/2, 0)$, which is $(\tilde X, \tilde T) = (-\pi/4, \pi/4)$, the point $i^+$ of region III. For $c = 1$ (the singularity) the limits are the same. Applying $\tilde T \mapsto -\tilde T$ gives the statement for region IV. Part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The boundary is assembled from limits of curve families, never from $\tilde g$ itself: $\mathscr I^\pm$ is where light escapes, $i^\pm$ where every static observer goes, $i^0$ where every slice ends. The one new feature compared with Minkowski space is part 4: the singularity, at which the curvature is unbounded, touches $i^+$, where static observers with bounded curvature end. This is the root of the caution that follows.

> [!caution] Caution: Conformal factor not smooth at i± and i⁰ in Kruskal
> The rescaled metric $\tilde g$ of [[#^thm-c10-3-1|Theorem §C10.3.1]] is good for drawing, but it does not make the boundary of the diagram a smooth part of a spacetime, as it did for Minkowski space ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]]). Computed:
> - **On $\mathscr I^+$ this $\tilde g$ degenerates.** Along an outgoing ray $U = U_0 < 0$, $(r/2M - 1)e^{r/2M} = |U_0|V$, so the radial coefficient is $(128M^3/r)e^{-r/2M} = 64M^2(1 - 2M/r)/(|U_0|V) \to 0$, and the sphere coefficient $4r^2\cos^2\tilde U\cos^2\tilde V \to 0$ as well. For $U_0 = -1$ and $V = 10^2$, $10^4$, $10^6$ the two coefficients are $(0.46, 0.021)$, $(5.5\times10^{-3}, 8.7\times10^{-6})$, $(5.8\times10^{-5}, 2.1\times10^{-9})$. The Kruskal $V = e^{v/4M}$ grows exponentially in advanced time, so $\Omega = 2\cos\tilde U\cos\tilde V$ vanishes exponentially, not like $1/r$. A conformal factor that is smooth on $\mathscr I^\pm$ is $\Omega = 1/r$ in the coordinates $(u, r)$ ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^ex-c10-4-3|Example §C10.4.3]]).
> - **No conformal factor helps at $i^\pm$.** The Weyl tensor with one index up is conformally invariant, $\tilde C^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma\delta} = C^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma\delta}$ (d'Inverno & Vickers (6.92); [[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^rem-c3-11-1|§C3.11, ★ Remark: The other ten components (Weyl tensor)]]). Lowering one index with $\tilde g = \Omega^2g$ and raising three with $\tilde g^{-1} = \Omega^{-2}g^{-1}$ gives $\tilde C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\tilde C^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = \Omega^2\cdot\Omega^{-6}\,C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}C^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = \Omega^{-4}C^2$. In vacuum $C = $ Riemann, so $C^2 = 48M^2/r^6$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-7|Theorem §C6.2.7]]), a fixed number along each static worldline. If $\Omega \to 0$ at $i^+$, as a point at infinity requires, then $\tilde C^2 \to \infty$ along every such worldline, and $\tilde g$ cannot be continuous at $i^+$. For this $\Omega$ along $r = 3M$: $\Omega = 0.11$, $9.0\times10^{-3}$, $6.1\times10^{-5}$ at $t = 10M$, $20M$, $40M$, and $\tilde C^2 = 4.7\times10^2$, $1.0\times10^7$, $4.9\times10^{15}\,M^{-4}$. In Minkowski space $C = 0$ and nothing obstructs. Couzens: "the conformally related metric is singular at $i^\pm$" (p. 72).
> - **At $i^0$** Couzens states that no $\Omega$ makes $\tilde g$ smooth (p. 72, fn. 14), without proof. For this $\Omega$, along $t = 0$, $\Omega = 2/(1 + (r/2M - 1)e^{r/2M}) \approx 4Me^{-r/2M}/(r - 2M)$, and $\Omega^{-4}\cdot48M^2/r^6$ is $3.8\times10^5$ at $r = 10M$ and $7.2\times10^{13}$ at $20M$ (units $M^{-4}$).
>
> This is why asymptotic flatness is defined with $\mathscr I^\pm$ alone, leaving out $i^\pm$ and $i^0$ ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]]). The diagram is unaffected: it uses only the chart $(\tilde U, \tilde V)$, in which radial light runs at $45°$, and limits of curves.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.4, p. 72 (PDF 73); §4.2, fn. 14, p. 73 (PDF 74) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.13, eq. (6.92), p. 108 (PDF 123) · numbers checked by script*

## Collapse: a star cuts the diagram in half

The Kruskal diagram is the vacuum solution continued as far as it goes. By Birkhoff's theorem ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-5|Theorem §C6.2.5]]) the outside of a collapsing spherical star is a piece of it, cut off by the surface ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^rem-c7-3-1|§C7.3, Remark: Which parts are physical]]). To draw the inside as well, and to find where the horizon is inside the matter, a solution for the interior is needed. The simplest one is a ball of uniform dust. Its interior is a piece of a closed, homogeneous universe of dust whose radius shrinks along a cycloid, the same cycloid the surface follows outside ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]]).

> [!definition] Definition §C10.3.1: The Oppenheimer–Snyder Spacetime
> *Parameters.* A mass $M > 0$ and an initial areal radius $R_0 > 2M$. Define $\chi_0 \in (0, \pi/2)$ and $a_m > 0$ by
>
> $$
> \sin^2\chi_0 = \frac{2M}{R_0}, \qquad a_m = \frac{R_0}{\sin\chi_0}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad M = \frac{a_m}{2}\sin^3\chi_0 .
> $$
>
> *Interior (the star).* The manifold $[0, \pi)_\eta \times [0, \chi_0]_\chi \times S^2$ with the metric
>
> $$
> ds^2 = a(\eta)^2\bigl(-d\eta^2 + d\chi^2 + \sin^2\chi\,d\Omega^2\bigr), \qquad a(\eta) = \frac{a_m}{2}(1 + \cos\eta) ,
> $$
>
> a piece of a closed homogeneous universe; $\chi = 0$ is the centre, the areal radius of the sphere $(\eta, \chi)$ is $a(\eta)\sin\chi$, and $\eta$ is a conformal time. The matter is dust ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^mod-c4-2-3|Model §C4.2.3]]) at rest in these coordinates, four-velocity $u = a^{-1}\partial_\eta$, energy density $\rho(\eta) = 3a_m/(8\pi a^3)$. Its proper time, the same for every dust particle, is $\tau = \tfrac12a_m(\eta + \sin\eta)$.
>
> *Exterior.* The part of the Kruskal spacetime $\mathcal K$ to the right of the surface worldline $\Sigma$: the radial geodesic released from rest at $r = R_0$, $t = 0$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]]), $r = \tfrac12R_0(1 + \cos\eta)$, $\tau = \sqrt{R_0^3/8M}\,(\eta + \sin\eta)$, $0 \le \eta \le \pi$, continued into the past by the static worldline $r = R_0$, $t < 0$.
>
> *Gluing.* The interior sphere $(\eta, \chi_0)$ is identified with the exterior sphere of $\Sigma$ at the same $\eta$, angle by angle. Before release ($t < 0$) the surface is held at $R_0$ by a static star whose interior is not specified.
>
> *Model:* spherical symmetry; pressure-free uniform matter; released from rest; the pre-release star idealised; *applies:* as the exact solution of Oppenheimer and Snyder (1939) after release ([[#^thm-c10-3-3|Theorem §C10.3.3]], part 1). Real collapse has pressure, rotation and non-uniform density; the singularity theorems make the qualitative picture robust ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^rem-c10-6-1|§C10.6, Remark: Penrose's singularity theorem]]).
>
> *Source: not derived in the typed sources ([[§C9.3 White Dwarfs, Neutron Stars and Collapse#^rem-c9-3-1|§C9.3, Remark: Collapse to a black hole]]); Hartle Problem 24.7(b), p. 538 (PDF 537), notes that the inside of a uniform star has the spatial geometry of a closed universe · worked here; every relation checked by script*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.3.3: The Penrose Diagram of Spherical Collapse
> Let $(M_{\rm OS}, g)$ be the Oppenheimer–Snyder spacetime of [[#^def-c10-3-1|Def. §C10.3.1]].
> 1. **It solves Einstein's equations.** Inside, $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$ with $T = \rho\,u \otimes u$ ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^pr-c5-1-4|Principle §C5.1.4]]); outside, $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$. On $\Sigma$ the metric induced from both sides is $-d\tau^2 + R(\tau)^2d\Omega^2$ with the same $R(\tau)$, and the extrinsic curvature from both sides is $K_{\tau\tau} = 0$, $K_{\theta\theta} = R\cos\chi_0$: no surface layer. The mass is $M = \tfrac43\pi\rho R^3$ at every instant, $R = a\sin\chi_0$ the areal radius of the surface.
> 2. **A chart for the diagram.** Let $\tilde U_\Sigma(\eta)$, $\tilde V_\Sigma(\eta)$ be the compactified Kruskal coordinates of the surface ([[#^thm-c10-3-1|Theorem §C10.3.1]]). Inside, put
>
> $$
> \tilde U = \phi(\eta - \chi), \quad \tilde V = \psi(\eta + \chi), \qquad \phi(s) = \tilde U_\Sigma(s + \chi_0), \quad \psi(s) = \tilde V_\Sigma(s - \chi_0) ,
> $$
>
>    with $\phi$ continued by any increasing function where $s + \chi_0 > \pi$. Then $(\tilde U, \tilde V)$ is continuous across $\Sigma$, radial light rays are $45°$ lines everywhere, and the centre $\chi = 0$ is a timelike curve.
> 3. **The diagram** (figure below). Its boundary consists of the centre (from $i^-$ to the end of the star), one $\mathscr I^-$, one $i^0$, one $\mathscr I^+$, one $i^+$, and the singularity: a single spacelike curve from the end of the centre to $i^+$, made of the final crunch of the star ($\eta = \pi$) and the exterior $r = 0$ ($\tilde U + \tilde V = \pi/2$).
> 4. **What is gone.** The exterior lies in $V > 0$ and contains only parts of regions I and II of $\mathcal K$. There is no white hole, no past horizon, no second exterior and no Einstein–Rosen bridge: everything to the left of $\Sigma$ in [[#^thm-c10-3-2|Theorem §C10.3.2]] is replaced by the star.
>
> *Model:* [[#^def-c10-3-1|Def. §C10.3.1]]; *applies:* exactly after release; the part of the diagram before release depends on the static star and is drawn schematically. That the junction conditions of part 1 make the glued metric a solution across $\Sigma$ (with $G_{\mu\nu}$ taken as a distribution) is the Darmois–Israel theorem, quoted, not proved here.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.4, Fig. 11 (right), p. 73 (PDF 74) ("only the two regions 1 and 3 of Kruskal spacetime, there is no white hole region anymore") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.5, Fig. 18.12, p. 352 (PDF 367); Exercise 18.10 and its answer, pp. 353, 589 (PDF 368, 604) ("The introduction of a source suppresses regions I′ and II′") · 't Hooft, ch. 11, Fig. 4b and p. 52 (PDF 52–53) · junction conditions: Schutz, §10.8, p. 291 (PDF 309) (pointer to Misner–Thorne–Wheeler and Wald) · Einstein tensor, matching and diagram checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Christoffel symbols inside). Write the interior metric with proper time, $ds^2 = -d\tau^2 + a^2\gamma_{ij}dx^idx^j$, where $\gamma$ is the metric of the unit 3-sphere in $(\chi, \theta, \varphi)$ and $\dot{} = d/d\tau$. From $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12g^{\lambda\sigma}(\partial_\mu g_{\sigma\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\sigma\mu} - \partial_\sigma g_{\mu\nu})$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection|§C3.6]]), with $g_{\tau\tau} = -1$ and $g_{ij} = a^2\gamma_{ij}$, the only non-zero symbols are
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\tau{}_{ij} = -\tfrac12g^{\tau\tau}\partial_\tau(a^2\gamma_{ij}) = a\dot a\,\gamma_{ij}, \qquad \Gamma^i{}_{\tau j} = \tfrac12a^{-2}\gamma^{ik}\partial_\tau(a^2\gamma_{kj}) = \frac{\dot a}{a}\,\delta^i{}_j, \qquad \Gamma^i{}_{jk} = {}^{(\gamma)}\Gamma^i{}_{jk} .
> $$
>
> In particular $\Gamma^\chi{}_{\tau\tau} = 0$: the dust worldlines $\chi, \theta, \varphi = \text{const}$ are geodesics, as dust requires ([[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c4-4-5|Theorem §C4.4.5]]).
>
> **Step 2** (Ricci tensor and Einstein tensor). From $R_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\lambda\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\lambda} + \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\lambda\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\mu\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\mu\lambda}$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^def-c3-11-1|Def. §C3.11.1]]):
>
> $$
> R_{\tau\tau} = 0 - \partial_\tau\Bigl(3\frac{\dot a}{a}\Bigr) + 0 - 3\Bigl(\frac{\dot a}{a}\Bigr)^2 = -3\frac{\ddot a}{a} ,
> $$
>
> using $\partial_\tau(\dot a/a) = \ddot a/a - \dot a^2/a^2$. For the spatial components the terms with $\gamma$ alone give the Ricci tensor of the unit 3-sphere, $2\gamma_{ij}$ (the Einstein static universe, [[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]]); the time-dependent terms give $\partial_\tau(a\dot a)\gamma_{ij} + 3(\dot a/a)a\dot a\gamma_{ij} - 2(\dot a/a)a\dot a\gamma_{ij} = (a\ddot a + \dot a^2 + \dot a^2)\gamma_{ij}$, so
>
> $$
> R_{ij} = \bigl(a\ddot a + 2\dot a^2 + 2\bigr)\gamma_{ij}, \qquad R = g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu} = 3\frac{\ddot a}{a} + \frac{3(a\ddot a + 2\dot a^2 + 2)}{a^2} = 6\Bigl(\frac{\ddot a}{a} + \frac{\dot a^2}{a^2} + \frac{1}{a^2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> With $G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12Rg_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^def-c3-11-3|Def. §C3.11.3]]):
>
> $$
> G_{\tau\tau} = -3\frac{\ddot a}{a} + 3\Bigl(\frac{\ddot a}{a} + \frac{\dot a^2 + 1}{a^2}\Bigr) = \frac{3(\dot a^2 + 1)}{a^2}, \qquad G_{ij} = \bigl(a\ddot a + 2\dot a^2 + 2 - 3a\ddot a - 3\dot a^2 - 3\bigr)\gamma_{ij} = -\bigl(2a\ddot a + \dot a^2 + 1\bigr)\gamma_{ij} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the cycloid solves them). Dust at rest has $T_{\tau\tau} = \rho$, $T_{ij} = 0$, so Einstein's equations are $3(\dot a^2 + 1)/a^2 = 8\pi\rho$ and $2a\ddot a + \dot a^2 + 1 = 0$. With $a = \tfrac12a_m(1 + \cos\eta) = a_m\cos^2(\eta/2)$ and $\tau = \tfrac12a_m(\eta + \sin\eta)$: $d\tau/d\eta = \tfrac12a_m(1 + \cos\eta) = a$, so $\eta$ is conformal time ($d\tau = a\,d\eta$), and
>
> $$
> \dot a = \frac{da/d\eta}{d\tau/d\eta} = \frac{-\tfrac12a_m\sin\eta}{\tfrac12a_m(1 + \cos\eta)} = -\tan\frac\eta2, \qquad \dot a^2 + 1 = \frac{1}{\cos^2(\eta/2)} = \frac{a_m}{a}, \qquad \ddot a = -\frac{1}{2\cos^2(\eta/2)}\,\frac{d\eta}{d\tau} = -\frac{a_m}{2a^2} .
> $$
>
> So $2a\ddot a + \dot a^2 + 1 = -a_m/a + a_m/a = 0$, and $8\pi\rho = 3(a_m/a)/a^2$, i.e. $\rho = 3a_m/(8\pi a^3)$: $\rho a^3$ is constant, the dust is conserved.
>
> **Step 4** (first junction condition). The surface $\chi = \chi_0$ is a dust worldtube with proper time $\tau$ and areal radius $R = a\sin\chi_0 = \tfrac12a_m\sin\chi_0\,(1 + \cos\eta)$, $\tau = \tfrac12a_m(\eta + \sin\eta)$. The exterior geodesic has $r = \tfrac12R_0(1 + \cos\eta)$, $\tau = \sqrt{R_0^3/8M}\,(\eta + \sin\eta)$. They coincide for all $\eta$ exactly when $R_0 = a_m\sin\chi_0$ and $\sqrt{R_0^3/8M} = a_m/2$. The first is the definition of $a_m$; the second, squared, is $R_0^3 = 2Ma_m^2$, i.e. $M = R_0^3/(2a_m^2) = \tfrac12a_m\sin^3\chi_0$. Then $2M/R_0 = \sin^2\chi_0$, as in [[#^def-c10-3-1|Def. §C10.3.1]]. On both sides the induced metric ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]) of the worldtube is $-d\tau^2 + R(\tau)^2d\Omega^2$.
>
> **Step 5** (mass). $\tfrac43\pi\rho R^3 = \tfrac43\pi\,\frac{3a_m}{8\pi a^3}\,a^3\sin^3\chi_0 = \tfrac12a_m\sin^3\chi_0 = M$: the Schwarzschild mass outside equals the density times the flat-space volume formula with the areal radius, at every instant.
>
> **Step 6** (second junction condition). The extrinsic curvature of the worldtube is $K_{ab} = e_a{}^\mu e_b{}^\nu\nabla_\mu n_\nu$, $n$ its outward unit normal. For $\tau\tau$: $K_{\tau\tau} = u^\mu u^\nu\nabla_\mu n_\nu = -n_\nu u^\mu\nabla_\mu u^\nu = 0$ on both sides, because $n\cdot u = 0$ and the surface moves on a geodesic on both sides (Step 1 inside, [[#^def-c10-3-1|Def. §C10.3.1]] outside). For the sphere: $K_{\theta\theta} = -\Gamma^\mu{}_{\theta\theta}n_\mu = \tfrac12n^\mu\partial_\mu(g_{\theta\theta}) = R\,n^\mu\partial_\mu R$, the rate of change of areal radius along $n$. Inside $n = a^{-1}\partial_\chi$ and the areal radius is $a\sin\chi$, so $K_{\theta\theta} = a\sin\chi_0\cdot a^{-1}a\cos\chi_0 = R\cos\chi_0$. Outside, in Schwarzschild coordinates the surface has $u = (\dot t, \dot r)$ with $f\dot t = E$ and $\dot r^2 = E^2 - f$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]], [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]). The one-form $n_\mu = (-\dot r, \dot t, 0, 0)$ has $n_\mu u^\mu = -\dot r\dot t + \dot t\dot r = 0$ and $n_\mu n^\mu = -\dot r^2/f + f\dot t^2 = (E^2 - \dot r^2)/f = 1$, and $n^r = f\dot t = E$. So $K_{\theta\theta} = r\,n^r = RE$ with $E^2 = 1 - 2M/R_0 = 1 - \sin^2\chi_0 = \cos^2\chi_0$, $E = \cos\chi_0$. The two sides agree. Part 1.
>
> **Step 7** (the chart). Inside, the radial metric is $a^2(-d\eta^2 + d\chi^2) = -a^2\,d(\eta - \chi)\,d(\eta + \chi)$, conformal to $-d\tilde U\,d\tilde V$ for any $\tilde U = \phi(\eta - \chi)$, $\tilde V = \psi(\eta + \chi)$ with $\phi$, $\psi$ increasing: $-a^2\,d(\eta - \chi)\,d(\eta + \chi) = -\frac{a^2}{\phi'\psi'}\,d\tilde U\,d\tilde V$. On $\chi = \chi_0$, $\phi(\eta - \chi_0) = \tilde U_\Sigma(\eta)$ and $\psi(\eta + \chi_0) = \tilde V_\Sigma(\eta)$ by construction: the two charts agree on $\Sigma$. Both $\tilde U_\Sigma$ and $\tilde V_\Sigma$ increase along the surface (future-directed timelike curve, [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-4|Theorem §C7.3.4]], part 1), so $\phi$, $\psi$ are increasing. The arguments needed are $\eta - \chi \in [-\chi_0, \pi]$ and $\eta + \chi \in [0, \pi + \chi_0]$; the surface supplies $\phi$ on $[-\chi_0, \pi - \chi_0]$ and $\psi$ on $[\chi_0, \pi + \chi_0]$, the pre-release history supplies $\psi$ below $\chi_0$, and the outgoing rays with $\eta - \chi > \pi - \chi_0$ never reach the surface (they end in the crunch), so there $\phi$ is free. The centre $(\phi(\eta), \psi(\eta))$ has both coordinates increasing: timelike. Part 2.
>
> **Step 8** (the boundary). The pre-release surface $r = R_0$ is a static worldline: it begins at $i^-$ ([[#^thm-c10-3-2|Theorem §C10.3.2]], Step 4). After release it crosses $U = 0$ and ends on $UV = 1$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^ex-c7-3-1|Example §C7.3.1]]). To its right the exterior contains all outgoing rays from points with $U < 0$, so all of $\mathscr I^+$; all ingoing rays to $\mathscr I^-$ come in from $\mathscr I^-$; and the slices $t = \text{const}$ reach $i^0$. The crunch $\eta = \pi$, $0 \le \chi \le \chi_0$, has $\phi(\pi - \chi)$ decreasing and $\psi(\pi + \chi)$ increasing as $\chi$ grows: spacelike; it ends at the surface's last point on $UV = 1$, where the exterior singularity $\tilde U + \tilde V = \pi/2$ continues to $i^+ = (0, \pi/2)$. Part 3.
>
> **Step 9** (what is gone). The static worldline $r = R_0$ has $V = e^{(t + r_\ast(R_0))/4M} > 0$, and $V$ increases along the fall, so every exterior point, lying to the right of $\Sigma$, has $V > 0$: no point of regions III or IV, nor of the past horizon $V = 0$. The exterior meets region II in the part $U > 0$ to the right of $\Sigma$. Part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The interior is conformally a strip of the Einstein static universe ($\eta$, $\chi$ are its $T$, $\chi$): the same structure that compactified Minkowski space ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]]) now carries matter.
> - The matching needs nothing beyond what the surface already does outside: Steps 4–6 use only the cycloid of [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]] and its energy $E = \cos\chi_0$.
> - The interior chart is not unique: any increasing $\phi$, $\psi$ that match on $\Sigma$ give a valid diagram. Only the $45°$ rule and the order of events along light rays are fixed.

## Where the horizon begins

In the figure the event horizon and the surface do not meet where one might expect. A signal from the centre of the star sent late enough cannot get out, although at that moment the star is well outside $2M$. The next theorem computes when this begins and compares it with the local test for a black hole, the trapped sphere ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^def-c10-6-5|Def. §C10.6.5]]): a round sphere whose areal radius decreases along both of its future radial light directions.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.3.4: The Horizon Forms before the Surface Reaches r = 0 and Begins inside the Star
> In the Oppenheimer–Snyder spacetime ([[#^def-c10-3-1|Def. §C10.3.1]]) with $R_0 > 8M/3$, i.e. $\chi_0 < \pi/3$, put $\eta_0 = \pi - 3\chi_0$ and $\eta_H = \pi - 2\chi_0$.
> 1. **The event horizon.** The set of events from which some future causal curve reaches $\mathscr I^+$ is $\{\eta - \chi < \eta_0\}$ inside the star and $\{U < 0\}$ outside. Its boundary, the event horizon ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^def-c10-7-3|Def. §C10.7.3]]; for Schwarzschild [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]]), is the outgoing null hypersurface $\eta - \chi = \eta_0$ inside and $U = 0$ ($r = 2M$) outside: in the diagram, the $45°$ line $\tilde U = 0$ from the centre to $i^+$.
> 2. **It begins at the centre.** The horizon starts at $\chi = 0$ at conformal time $\eta_0$, with zero area. Inside the star its areal radius is $r_{\rm EH}(\eta) = a(\eta)\sin(\eta - \eta_0)$; it reaches $2M$ exactly when it reaches the surface, at $\eta_H$, the moment the surface crosses $r = 2M$.
> 3. **Trapped spheres.** A round sphere inside the star is trapped exactly when $\chi + \eta/2 > \pi/2$; outside, exactly when $r < 2M$. The first trapped sphere is the surface itself at $\eta_H$. Inside the star the boundary of the trapped region (the apparent horizon) is $\chi = \pi/2 - \eta/2$, a timelike curve with areal radius $a_m\cos^3(\eta/2)$, twice the mass inside it, which moves from the surface at $\eta_H$ to the centre at the crunch.
> 4. ⚑ **The horizon comes first.** On the dust clocks, the horizon is born at $\tau_0 = \tfrac12a_m(\eta_0 + \sin\eta_0)$ and the first trapped sphere appears at $\tau_H = \tfrac12a_m(\eta_H + \sin\eta_H) > \tau_0$. Every trapped round sphere lies inside the black-hole region, and none lies in the causal past of any event of the horizon. Numbers:
>    - $R_0 = 4M$: $\tau_0 = 4.22M$, $\tau_H = 7.27M$, end of the star $\tau = 8.89M$;
>    - $R_0 \gg M$: $\tau_H - \tau_0 \to 19M/6 = 3.17M$, while the surface needs only $4M/3$ more to reach $r = 0$;
>    - a $1.4\,M_\odot$ core falling from the size of the Earth ([[§C9.3 White Dwarfs, Neutron Stars and Collapse#^ex-c9-3-2|Example §C9.3.2]]): the horizon is born $21.8\ \mu\mathrm s$ before the surface crosses $2M$ (restored: $\tau_H - \tau_0 \approx 19GM/6c^3$).
>
> For $R_0 \le 8M/3$, $\eta_0 \le 0$: the horizon already exists inside the star at release.
>
> *Model:* [[#^def-c10-3-1|Def. §C10.3.1]]; "trapped" tested on round spheres; *applies:* exactly. Trapped surfaces that are not round can reach further out, so the apparent horizon depends on the slicing; the event horizon does not.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.4, Fig. 11 (right), p. 73 (PDF 74) (the horizon dashed from inside the star; "appears in spacetime once the star has collapsed sufficiently") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.5, Fig. 18.12, p. 352 (PDF 367) (event horizon and a trapped surface marked) · 't Hooft, ch. 11, Fig. 4b, p. 51 (PDF 52) · worked here; all numbers checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (causal curves only go forward in $\eta - \chi$ and in $U$). Along a future-directed causal curve inside the star, $a^2(-d\eta^2 + d\chi^2) + a^2\sin^2\chi\,d\Omega^2 \le 0$ gives $d\chi^2 \le d\eta^2$, and $d\eta > 0$, so $d(\eta - \chi) \ge 0$. Outside, $-F\,dU\,dV + r^2d\Omega^2 \le 0$ gives $dU\,dV \ge 0$, and future-directed means $dU, dV \ge 0$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], part 5). Define $\mathcal U = \tilde U$ outside and $\mathcal U = \phi(\eta - \chi)$ inside ([[#^thm-c10-3-3|Theorem §C10.3.3]], part 2). It is continuous across $\Sigma$, and never decreases along a future causal curve.
>
> **Step 2** (the region that sees $\mathscr I^+$). $\mathscr I^+$ lies at $\tilde U < 0$ ([[#^thm-c10-3-2|Theorem §C10.3.2]], part 3). By Step 1 no future causal curve from a point with $\mathcal U \ge 0$ reaches it. Conversely, from a point with $\mathcal U < 0$ follow the outgoing radial light ray, along which $\mathcal U$ is constant. Inside, it is $\eta - \chi = s$ with $\phi(s) < 0$; it reaches the surface at $\eta = s + \chi_0$, where $\tilde U_\Sigma(s + \chi_0) = \phi(s) < 0$, and continues outside as $U = \text{const} < 0$ to $\mathscr I^+$. So the set of part 1 is $\{\mathcal U < 0\}$ and its boundary is $\{\mathcal U = 0\}$.
>
> **Step 3** ($\eta_H$). The surface is at $r = 2M$ when $\tfrac12R_0(1 + \cos\eta) = 2M$, i.e. $1 + \cos\eta = 4M/R_0 = 2\sin^2\chi_0$, $\cos\eta = 2\sin^2\chi_0 - 1 = -\cos2\chi_0$, so $\eta_H = \pi - 2\chi_0$. There $U = 0$, $\tilde U_\Sigma(\eta_H) = 0$.
>
> **Step 4** (the horizon inside). $\phi(s) = 0$ means $\tilde U_\Sigma(s + \chi_0) = 0$, $s + \chi_0 = \eta_H$, $s = \pi - 3\chi_0 = \eta_0$. So the horizon inside is $\eta - \chi = \eta_0$, and outside $U = 0$. Part 1. Since $\chi \ge 0$, it exists inside only for $\eta \ge \eta_0$ and starts at $\chi = 0$, $\eta = \eta_0$, a single point of zero area; $\eta_0 > 0$ exactly when $\chi_0 < \pi/3$, i.e. $2M/R_0 < 3/4$. Its areal radius is $a(\eta)\sin\chi = a(\eta)\sin(\eta - \eta_0)$. At $\eta_H$: $\chi = \eta_H - \eta_0 = \chi_0$, and $a(\eta_H)\sin\chi_0 = \tfrac12a_m(1 - \cos2\chi_0)\sin\chi_0 = a_m\sin^3\chi_0 = 2M$. Part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (trapped spheres inside). Along the outgoing radial light direction $d\chi = d\eta$, and $a'/a = -\sin\eta/(1 + \cos\eta) = -\tan(\eta/2)$ with $' = d/d\eta$:
>
> $$
> \frac{d(a\sin\chi)}{d\eta}\Big|_{\rm out} = a'\sin\chi + a\cos\chi = a\Bigl(\cos\chi - \tan\frac\eta2\sin\chi\Bigr) = \frac{a\cos(\chi + \eta/2)}{\cos(\eta/2)} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \frac{d(a\sin\chi)}{d\eta}\Big|_{\rm in} = a'\sin\chi - a\cos\chi = -\frac{a\cos(\chi - \eta/2)}{\cos(\eta/2)} < 0 ,
> $$
>
> the second negative because $|\chi - \eta/2| < \pi/2$ for $0 \le \chi \le \chi_0 < \pi/2$, $0 \le \eta < \pi$. The ingoing areal radius always decreases, the outgoing one exactly when $\chi + \eta/2 > \pi/2$.
>
> **Step 6** (trapped spheres outside). In ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates the ingoing radial light rays have $v = \text{const}$ and $r$ decreasing, the outgoing ones $dr/dv = \tfrac12(1 - 2M/r)$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-2|Theorem §C7.2.2]]). Both decrease exactly when $r < 2M$. Part 3, first sentence.
>
> **Step 7** (the apparent horizon). The boundary $\chi = \pi/2 - \eta/2$ has $|d\chi/d\eta| = \tfrac12 < 1$: timelike. It meets the surface where $\chi_0 = \pi/2 - \eta/2$, i.e. at $\eta = \pi - 2\chi_0 = \eta_H$, and the centre at $\eta = \pi$. Its areal radius is $a\sin(\pi/2 - \eta/2) = a_m\cos^2(\eta/2)\cos(\eta/2) = a_m\cos^3(\eta/2)$. The mass inside the sphere $\chi$, by Step 5 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c10-3-3|Theorem §C10.3.3]] with $\chi$ for $\chi_0$, is $m(\chi) = \tfrac12a_m\sin^3\chi$, and $2m(\pi/2 - \eta/2) = a_m\cos^3(\eta/2)$: each dust shell becomes marginally trapped when its areal radius equals twice the mass inside it, the interior analogue of $r = 2M$. Inside the star, a trapped sphere needs $\eta > \pi - 2\chi \ge \pi - 2\chi_0 = \eta_H$; outside, $r < 2M$ to the right of $\Sigma$ exists only after $\Sigma$ has crossed $U = 0$ at $\eta_H$. Part 3.
>
> **Step 8** (the horizon comes first). Inside, a trapped sphere has $\eta > \pi - 2\chi$, so $\eta - \chi > \pi - 3\chi \ge \pi - 3\chi_0 = \eta_0$, i.e. $\mathcal U > 0$; outside, $r < 2M$ means $U > 0$. So every trapped round sphere has $\mathcal U > 0$ and lies in the black-hole region. If $q$ is trapped and $p$ is on the horizon, $\mathcal U(q) > 0 = \mathcal U(p)$, and by Step 1 $q$ cannot lie in the causal past of $p$. In dust proper time, $\eta_0 < \eta_H$ and $\tau(\eta) = \tfrac12a_m(\eta + \sin\eta)$ increases, so $\tau_0 < \tau_H$.
>
> **Step 9** (numbers). For $R_0 = 4M$: $\sin^2\chi_0 = 1/2$, $\chi_0 = \pi/4$, $a_m = 4\sqrt2M$, $\eta_0 = \pi/4$, $\eta_H = \pi/2$. Then
>
> $$
> \tau_0 = 2\sqrt2M\Bigl(\frac\pi4 + \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\Bigr) = \Bigl(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt2} + 2\Bigr)M = 4.22M, \qquad \tau_H = 2\sqrt2M\Bigl(\frac\pi2 + 1\Bigr) = 7.27M, \qquad \tau(\pi) = 2\sqrt2\pi M = 8.89M ,
> $$
>
> and $\tau_H$ agrees with the crossing time of [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^ex-c7-3-1|Example §C7.3.1]] for $r_0 = 4M$. For small $\chi_0$ (large $R_0$), expand $\sin2\chi_0 - \sin3\chi_0 = (2\chi_0 - \tfrac86\chi_0^3) - (3\chi_0 - \tfrac{27}{6}\chi_0^3) + O(\chi_0^5) = -\chi_0 + \tfrac{19}{6}\chi_0^3 + O(\chi_0^5)$, so
>
> $$
> \tau_H - \tau_0 = \tfrac12a_m\bigl[(\eta_H - \eta_0) + \sin\eta_H - \sin\eta_0\bigr] = \tfrac12a_m\bigl[\chi_0 + \sin2\chi_0 - \sin3\chi_0\bigr] = \tfrac12a_m\Bigl[\tfrac{19}{6}\chi_0^3 + O(\chi_0^5)\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> using $\sin(\pi - x) = \sin x$. With $a_m\chi_0^3 \approx a_m\sin^3\chi_0 = 2M$ this tends to $19M/6$. For the Earth-sized $1.4\,M_\odot$ core ($M = 2.067\ \mathrm{km}$, $R_0 = 6371\ \mathrm{km}$, $\chi_0 = 0.0255$) the script gives $\tau_H - \tau_0 = 3.167M = 6.55\ \mathrm{km}/c = 21.8\ \mu\mathrm s$. Part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The event horizon is found by tracing the last escaping light ray backwards from the end of the collapse ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]]); trapped spheres are found by a local computation at one instant. The two answers differ: the horizon is there before anything local announces it.
> - The horizon grows from a point to the area $16\pi M^2$ and then stays; it never shrinks.
> - The proof that trapped spheres are inside the black hole used only Step 1, the monotonicity of $\mathcal U$; it is the special case of a general theorem for asymptotically flat spacetimes that obey the null energy condition (C10.6–C10.7).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C10.3.1: An Observer Falling in, Drawn in the Penrose Diagram
> *Question.* Observer B is released from rest at $r_0 = 4M$, $t = 0$, in region I and sends a radial light signal outward every $M$ of her proper time, starting at $\tau = 0$. Which signals reach $\mathscr I^+$, at what retarded time $u$, and where do they land in the diagram? (B also is the surface of the star of [[#^thm-c10-3-3|Theorem §C10.3.3]] with $R_0 = 4M$; her signals are the star's last light.)
>
> **Step 1** (the worldline). By [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^ex-c7-3-1|Example §C7.3.1]] with $b = \sqrt{r_0/2M - 1} = 1$: $r = 2M(1 + \cos\eta)$, $\tau = 2\sqrt2M(\eta + \sin\eta)$,
>
> $$
> V(\eta) = \Bigl(\cos\frac\eta2 + \sin\frac\eta2\Bigr)\exp\Bigl[\frac\eta2 + \frac{\eta + \sin\eta}{2} + \frac{1 + \cos\eta}{2}\Bigr], \qquad U(\eta) = \frac{G(r)}{V(\eta)} ,
> $$
>
> and in the diagram $(\tilde U, \tilde V) = (\arctan U, \arctan V)$. She crosses $U = 0$ at $\eta = \pi/2$, $\tau_H = 2\sqrt2(\pi/2 + 1)M = 7.27M$, and reaches $r = 0$ at $\tau = 2\sqrt2\pi M = 8.89M$.
>
> **Step 2** (where a signal goes). An outgoing radial signal keeps $U$, hence $\tilde U$, fixed: a $45°$ line up and to the right. If it is sent at $U_e < 0$ it reaches $\mathscr I^+$ at $(\tilde U_e, \pi/2)$ ([[#^thm-c10-3-2|Theorem §C10.3.2]], part 3). Since $U = -e^{-u/4M}$, its retarded time there is $u = -4M\ln(-U_e)$, which is the time $t - r_\ast$ at which a distant observer at fixed $r$ receives it, up to the constant $r_\ast$ of the observer. If $U_e \ge 0$ the signal stays at $\tilde U_e \ge 0$ and ends on the singularity at $\tilde V = \pi/2 - \tilde U_e$.
>
> **Step 3** (the signals). Solving $\tau(\eta) = \tau_e$ for $\eta$ and inserting:
>
> | $\tau_e/M$ | $r/M$ | $U_e$ | $\tilde U_e$ | $u/M$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | $0$ | $4.000$ | $-2.718$ | $-1.218$ | $-4.000$ |
> | $1$ | $3.969$ | $-1.877$ | $-1.081$ | $-2.519$ |
> | $2$ | $3.874$ | $-1.247$ | $-0.895$ | $-0.884$ |
> | $3$ | $3.712$ | $-0.788$ | $-0.667$ | $0.953$ |
> | $4$ | $3.477$ | $-0.464$ | $-0.434$ | $3.075$ |
> | $5$ | $3.159$ | $-0.243$ | $-0.238$ | $5.657$ |
> | $6$ | $2.739$ | $-0.101$ | $-0.100$ | $9.182$ |
> | $7$ | $2.183$ | $-0.0155$ | $-0.0155$ | $16.669$ |
> | $8$ | $1.403$ | $+0.0289$ | $+0.0289$ | never |
>
> The first row checks: at release $U = -e^{-(0 - r_\ast(4M))/4M} = -e^{1} = -2.718$ ($r_\ast(4M) = 4M + 2M\ln1 = 4M$) and $u = 0 - r_\ast = -4M$.
>
> **Step 4** (the spacing). Equal intervals of $\tau$ arrive at intervals $\Delta u = 1.48$, $1.64$, $1.84$, $2.12$, $2.58$, $3.53$, $7.49\,M$. Near the crossing $U_e$ vanishes linearly in $\tau_H - \tau$, so $u = -4M\ln(-U_e) \approx -4M\ln(\tau_H - \tau) + \text{const}$: each factor $10$ closer to $\tau_H$ adds $4M\ln10 = 9.2M$ to the arrival time (script: slope $4.0006M$ per $\ln$-unit). The signal at $\tau_H$ itself would arrive at $u = \infty$, i.e. at $i^+$.
>
> *What it shows.* In the diagram the arrival points $\tilde U_e$ crowd toward $\tilde U = 0$, the corner $i^+$, and the infinite wait of the distant observer is the short last stretch of $\mathscr I^+$ below $i^+$ ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^ex-c7-1-2|Example §C7.1.2]] computed the redshift; here it is geometry). Eight signals escape, the ninth, sent $0.73M$ after the crossing, cannot: $\mathscr I^+$ lies entirely at $\tilde U < 0$, and the event horizon is the line $\tilde U = 0$ that separates the two.
>

> [!example] Example §C10.3.2: Hartle Problem 12.26: the Boundaries of the Kruskal Diagram
> *Question* (Hartle, Problem 12.26). Show that the boundaries of the Penrose diagram of the Kruskal extension are as in Hartle's Box 12.5: the singularity at $V' = \pm\pi/4$ for $|U'| \le \pi/4$, the horizon as a $45°$ line, and $\mathscr I^\pm$, $i^\pm$, $i^0$ at the corners and edges shown there.
>
> **Step 1** (Hartle's coordinates). Hartle writes $U$ for the Kruskal space coordinate and $V$ for the time coordinate ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^cau-c7-3-2|§C7.3, Caution: Names for the Kruskal coordinates]]); his null $u = V - U$ and $v = V + U$ are our $U$ and $V$. His $u' = \tan^{-1}u$, $v' = \tan^{-1}v$ are our $\tilde U$, $\tilde V$, and his $V' = \tfrac12(u' + v')$, $U' = \tfrac12(v' - u')$ are our $\tilde T$, $\tilde X$.
>
> **Step 2** (the singularity). $r = 0$ is $uv = 1$, i.e. $u' + v' = \pm\pi/2$ by Step 2 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c10-3-1|Theorem §C10.3.1]], i.e. $V' = \pm\pi/4$. On the future branch $u', v' \in (0, \pi/2)$, so $U' = \tfrac12(v' - u') \in (-\pi/4, \pi/4)$. On the past branch the same with $u', v' \in (-\pi/2, 0)$. This is Hartle's statement for both branches (checked on points of $T^2 - X^2 = 1$ by script).
>
> **Step 3** (the horizons). $u = 0$ is $u' = 0$, i.e. $V' = U'$; $v = 0$ is $V' = -U'$: the two $45°$ diagonals through the origin.
>
> **Step 4** (the edges and corners, by following curves). For region I ($u < 0 < v$):
> - a static observer at $r = 3M$ sits at $(U', V') = (0.982, 0)$ at $t = 0$, $(0.917, \pm0.413)$ at $t = \pm4M$, $(0.806, \pm0.732)$ at $t = \pm12M$ and $(0.785, 0.785)$ at $t = 40M$: it runs from $i^- = (\pi/4, -\pi/4)$ to $i^+ = (\pi/4, \pi/4)$;
> - the slice $t = 0$ ($V' = 0$) passes $U' = 0.982$, $1.414$, $1.569$ at $r = 3M$, $6M$, $20M$, approaching $i^0 = (\pi/2, 0)$;
> - an outgoing light ray $u = u_0 < 0$ has $v' \to \pi/2$, so it ends on the edge $V' + U' = \pi/2$ between $i^0$ and $i^+$, where $r \to \infty$ ([[#^thm-c10-3-2|Theorem §C10.3.2]], Step 3): this edge is $\mathscr I^+$, and its mirror $V' - U' = -\pi/2$ is $\mathscr I^-$.
>
> The check of the first entry: at $t = 0$, $u = -v$ and $uv = -v^2 = G(3M) = -\tfrac12e^{3/2}$, so $v = 1.497$ and $U' = \tfrac12(v' - u') = \arctan1.497 = 0.982$. Region III is the mirror image $U' \mapsto -U'$.
>
> *Result.* The diagram is the hexagon of [[#^thm-c10-3-2|Theorem §C10.3.2]]: singularities at $V' = \pm\pi/4$, $|U'| < \pi/4$; corners $i^\pm = (\pm\pi/4, \pm\pi/4)$-type points and $i^0 = (\pm\pi/2, 0)$; null edges $\mathscr I^\pm$ between them. Hartle's sentence "the horizon is the boundary of the region of spacetime that can be connected to future null infinity by a light ray" is the line $u' = 0$ of Step 3, which separates $u' < 0$ (rays reach $v' = \pi/2$, i.e. $\mathscr I^+$) from $u' \ge 0$ (rays reach $u' + v' = \pi/2$, the singularity).
>
> *Source: Hartle, Box 12.5, p. 274 (PDF 275); Problem 12.26, p. 280 (PDF 281) (the question; no solution in the book) · worked here; numbers checked by script*
