The chapter asked for a definition of a black hole that does not depend on coordinates. [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]] gave one for Schwarzschild alone: the events from which no causal curve reaches large $r$. Its weak point is the coordinate $r$. Two local substitutes come to mind, and both fail on examples this chapter has already computed.

*Attempt 1: a horizon is where $g_{tt}$ vanishes, or more invariantly where a Killing field turns null.* Rindler spacetime is flat Minkowski space, and its metric $-\xi^2d\eta^2 + d\xi^2$ has $g_{\eta\eta} = 0$ at $\xi = 0$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]]). The boost field $\partial_\eta$ is null there, and $\xi = 0$ is even a Killing horizon with surface gravity $1/\xi_0$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^ex-c10-5-2|Example §C10.5.2]]). Yet Minkowski space contains no black hole: every event sends light to infinity. The test finds a horizon where there is no black hole.

*Attempt 2: a black hole begins where something local happens: strong curvature, or a trapped surface.* Let a thin spherical shell of light with total energy $M$ fall inwards through flat space, crossing the centre at time $v_0$ ([[#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]]). Consider an observer at rest at radius $M$ at the time $t = v_0 - 2M$. The shell is still at radius $2M$, outside her. Inside the shell the metric is exactly $-dt^2 + dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$: the curvature is zero, $g_{tt} = -1$, and every round sphere has positive outgoing expansion $\theta_{\text{out}} = 1/r$, so none is trapped ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^def-c10-6-5|Def. §C10.6.5]]). Nonetheless no signal she sends from then on ever reaches a distant observer: she is already inside the black hole. The test misses a black hole where there is one.

What decides the question is where light *ends up*, and "ends up" needs a place for infinity. That place is future null infinity $\mathscr I^+$ of the conformal compactification ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-3|Def. §C10.1.3]]), made precise for curved spacetimes by asymptotic flatness ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]]). This section names the sets of events reachable by causal curves ([[#^def-c10-7-1|Def. §C10.7.1]]) and then defines the black hole as the events that cannot reach $\mathscr I^+$ ([[#^def-c10-7-2|Def. §C10.7.2]]), its boundary as the event horizon ([[#^def-c10-7-3|Def. §C10.7.3]]) and the white hole by time reversal ([[#^def-c10-7-4|Def. §C10.7.4]]). The definition is computed for Kruskal, where it reproduces C7 ([[#^thm-c10-7-1|Theorem §C10.7.1]]). It then shows what kind of surface every event horizon is ([[#^thm-c10-7-2|Theorem §C10.7.2]]), and why the observer above is inside: the horizon depends on the whole future ([[#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]]). For stationary holes the horizon is a Killing horizon after all ([[#^thm-c10-7-4|Theorem §C10.7.4]]), which explains why attempt 1 nearly works. The section ends with singularities that are not hidden ([[#^def-c10-7-5|Def. §C10.7.5]]) and the conjecture that collapse never makes them ([[#^rem-c10-7-1|Remark: Weak cosmic censorship]]).

**Setup.** A spacetime $(M, g)$: a connected, time-oriented Lorentzian manifold ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]). From [[#^def-c10-7-2|Def. §C10.7.2]] on it is asymptotically flat at null infinity ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]]): it sits inside an unphysical spacetime $(\tilde M, \tilde g)$, $\tilde g = \Omega^2g$ on $M$ ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-1|Def. §C10.1.1]]), whose boundary contains $\mathscr I^+$ and $\mathscr I^-$. The definitions are exact. The computations are exact for the spacetimes named: Kruskal, Minkowski with a Rindler observer, the null-shell spacetime, negative-mass Schwarzschild. General facts about horizons that need more causal theory than these notes build (achronal boundaries, rigidity) are quoted and marked as such. Numbers come from `tools/verify_c10_7.py`.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here $M \to GM/c^2$ for lengths, $M \to GM/c^3$ for times); $f = 1 - 2M/r$; tortoise coordinate $r_\ast$ and $u = t - r_\ast$, $v = t + r_\ast$ as in [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]; Kruskal $U = -e^{-u/4M}$, $V = e^{v/4M}$ as in [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^def-c7-3-1|Def. §C7.3.1]], compactified by $U = \tan\tilde U$, $V = \tan\tilde V$ ([[§C10.3 Penrose Diagrams of Kruskal and of Collapse#^thm-c10-3-1|Theorem §C10.3.1]]); Couzens writes $(\bar M, \bar g)$ for the unphysical spacetime, these notes $(\tilde M, \tilde g)$. Null infinity is $\mathscr I^\pm$ ("scri"). Closure $\bar S$, boundary $\partial S$ and interior are taken in $M$ unless $\tilde M$ is said.

## Who can influence whom

> [!definition] Definition §C10.7.1: Causal and Chronological Future and Past
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be a time-oriented Lorentzian manifold and $S \subset M$ any subset. Curves are the piecewise smooth curves of [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]].
> 1. The **causal future** $J^+(S)$ is $S$ together with every event $q$ for which a future-directed causal curve runs from some $p \in S$ to $q$.
> 2. The **chronological future** $I^+(S)$ is the set of events $q$ for which a future-directed *timelike* curve runs from some $p \in S$ to $q$.
> 3. The **causal past** $J^-(S)$ and the **chronological past** $I^-(S)$ are the same with past-directed curves.
>
> For one event, $J^\pm(p) \equiv J^\pm(\{p\})$. By the convention in part 1, $p \in J^+(p)$ always. On the other hand $p \in I^+(p)$ only if a closed timelike curve passes through $p$, which happens in none of the spacetimes of these notes.
>
> *Two properties used below.* Both follow from joining curves end to end, which keeps a curve future-directed causal:
> - **Transitivity:** $q \in J^+(p)$ and $x \in J^+(q)$ imply $x \in J^+(p)$.
> - **$J^-(S)$ is a past set:** if $x \in J^-(S)$ and $q \in J^-(x)$, then $q \in J^-(S)$. Taking pasts never leads out of it.
>
> *Minkowski space.* By [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where timelike means $a^2 > 0$), $J^+(p)$ is the closed solid future cone $\{q : (q - p)^2 \le 0,\ q^0 \ge p^0\}$, written in $-{+}{+}{+}$. $I^+(p)$ is its open interior, and its boundary is the future light cone of $p$. In curved spacetime the same three sets are no longer cones, but they keep these roles.
>
> *Physical reading.* $J^+(p)$ is everything an event can influence, by signals at most as fast as light; $J^-(p)$ is everything that can influence it.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.5, Definitions 12–13, p. 82 (PDF 83) (with two slips, [[#^cau-c10-7-2|Caution: Slips and loose terms in the sources]])*

## The black hole as the region that cannot signal to infinity

> [!definition] Definition §C10.7.2: Black-Hole Region
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be asymptotically flat at null infinity ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]]), with unphysical spacetime $(\tilde M, \tilde g)$ and future null infinity $\mathscr I^+ \subset \partial M$ ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-3|Def. §C10.1.3]]). Compute the causal past $J^-(\mathscr I^+)$ in $(\tilde M, \tilde g)$ ([[#^def-c10-7-1|Def. §C10.7.1]]). The **black-hole region** is
>
> $$
> \mathcal B \equiv M \setminus \bigl[M \cap J^-(\mathscr I^+)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> $(M, g)$ **contains a black hole** if $\mathcal B \neq \emptyset$.
>
> *Why $\tilde M$.* $\mathscr I^+$ is not part of $M$, so $J^-(\mathscr I^+)$ makes sense only in a spacetime that contains it. On $M$ the metric $\tilde g$ is $\Omega^2g$ with $\Omega > 0$. It has the same timelike, null and spacelike vectors and the same time orientation as $g$ ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^thm-c10-1-1|Theorem §C10.1.1]]). So an event $p \in M$ lies in $J^-(\mathscr I^+)$ exactly when some future-directed causal curve of the physical metric runs from $p$ and, drawn in $\tilde M$, ends on $\mathscr I^+$.
>
> *Physical reading.* $\mathscr I^+$ is where outgoing light ends up ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^ex-c10-1-1|Example §C10.1.1]]). An event outside $\mathcal B$ can send a signal to observers arbitrarily far away, however long it takes to arrive. An event in $\mathcal B$ cannot, by any means.
>
> *Model:* asymptotically flat spacetimes; *applies:* to isolated systems. Without an $\mathscr I^+$ (a closed universe, for instance) the definition says nothing.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.5, Definition 14, eq. (4.58), p. 82 (PDF 83) · Hartle, Box 12.5 (p. 274, PDF 275: the horizon "is the boundary of the region of spacetime that can be connected to future null infinity by a light ray") · Schutz §11.3, p. 322 (PDF 340: "the boundary in spacetime between events that can communicate with distant observers and events that cannot")*

^def-c10-7-2

> [!definition] Definition §C10.7.3: Future Event Horizon
> In the setting of [[#^def-c10-7-2|Def. §C10.7.2]], the **future event horizon** is the boundary of the black-hole region in $M$,
>
> $$
> \mathcal H^+ \equiv \partial\mathcal B .
> $$
>
> It consists of the events every neighbourhood of which contains events of $\mathcal B$ and events that can signal to $\mathscr I^+$. Since $M \setminus \mathcal B = M \cap J^-(\mathscr I^+)$, also $\mathcal H^+ = M \cap \partial J^-(\mathscr I^+)$.
>
> *Layer change.* [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]] is the same definition with "reaches $\mathscr I^+$" replaced by "reaches arbitrarily large $r$", in one chart of one spacetime. For Schwarzschild the two agree ([[#^thm-c10-7-1|Theorem §C10.7.1]], part 3). The definition here needs no coordinate. It applies to spacetimes with no symmetry, and it is not tied to an observer: $\mathscr I^+$ belongs to the spacetime, not to anyone's worldline (contrast [[#^ex-c10-7-1|Example §C10.7.1]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.5, Definition 14, p. 82 (PDF 83) · Schutz §11.3, p. 322 (PDF 340: "a three-dimensional surface that separates the events of spacetime into two regions")*

^def-c10-7-3

> [!definition] Definition §C10.7.4: White-Hole Region; Past Event Horizon
> In the setting of [[#^def-c10-7-2|Def. §C10.7.2]], with past null infinity $\mathscr I^-$ and $J^+(\mathscr I^-)$ computed in $(\tilde M, \tilde g)$, the **white-hole region** and the **past event horizon** are
>
> $$
> \mathcal W \equiv M \setminus \bigl[M \cap J^+(\mathscr I^-)\bigr], \qquad \mathcal H^- \equiv \partial\mathcal W .
> $$
>
> $\mathcal W$ is the set of events that no signal from past null infinity can reach. It is the black-hole region of the time-reversed spacetime, with the time orientation $-T$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]), since reversing the orientation swaps $J^+$ with $J^-$ and $\mathscr I^+$ with $\mathscr I^-$. It generalizes [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-5|Def. §C7.2.5]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.5, Definition 15, eq. (4.59), p. 82 (PDF 83)*

^def-c10-7-4

The definition can now be tested where the answer is known. In Kruskal the future-directed causal vectors are simple to describe, and so is everything that follows from them.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.7.1: In Kruskal, B Is Region II with Its Boundary and H⁺ Is the Pair of Future Horizons
> Let $\mathcal K$ be the Kruskal spacetime ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]), time-oriented by $\partial_T$, with its two future null infinities $\mathscr I^+_{\rm I}$ (of region I) and $\mathscr I^+_{\rm III}$ (of region III), and $\mathscr I^+ = \mathscr I^+_{\rm I} \cup \mathscr I^+_{\rm III}$ ([[§C10.3 Penrose Diagrams of Kruskal and of Collapse#^thm-c10-3-2|Theorem §C10.3.2]]).
> 1. **Causal curves.** Along every future-directed causal curve, $U$ and $V$ never decrease.
> 2. **Black hole and future horizon.**
>
> $$
> M \cap J^-(\mathscr I^+) = \{U < 0\} \cup \{V < 0\}, \qquad \mathcal B = \{U \ge 0,\ V \ge 0\}, \qquad \mathcal H^+ = \{U = 0,\ V \ge 0\} \cup \{V = 0,\ U \ge 0\} .
> $$
>
>    $\mathcal B$ is region II together with its boundary. $\mathcal H^+$ consists of the future horizon of region I and that of region III, which meet at the bifurcation sphere $U = V = 0$.
> 3. **Agreement with C7.** In the ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein spacetime $N = \{V > 0\}$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], part 2), $\mathcal B \cap N = \{0 < r \le 2M\}$ and $\mathcal H^+ \cap N = \{r = 2M\}$, the sets of [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]].
> 4. **White hole.** $M \cap J^+(\mathscr I^-) = \{U > 0\} \cup \{V > 0\}$, $\mathcal W = \{U \le 0,\ V \le 0\}$ (region IV with its boundary), $\mathcal H^- = \{U = 0,\ V \le 0\} \cup \{V = 0,\ U \le 0\}$.
>
> *Model:* the Kruskal spacetime; *applies:* exactly. Kruskal has two asymptotic regions, so condition 4 of [[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]] (one $\mathscr I^+$ and one $\mathscr I^-$) holds region by region, and $\mathscr I^+$ is taken as the union of both.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.5, Definitions 14–15 (applied, not computed there) and §4.1.4 (Kruskal diagram) · Hartle, Box 12.5 (p. 274, PDF 275) · sets checked by script*

^thm-c10-7-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (future-directed causal vectors). By [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]] the metric is $g = -F\,dU\,dV + r^2d\Omega^2$ with $F > 0$. Since $U = T - X$ and $V = T + X$, the chain rule gives $\partial_T = \partial_U + \partial_V$. For a vector $Y = Y^U\partial_U + Y^V\partial_V + Y_\Omega$, with $Y_\Omega$ tangent to the sphere and $|Y_\Omega|^2 \ge 0$ its squared length under $r^2d\Omega^2$:
>
> $$
> g(Y, Y) = -F\,Y^UY^V + |Y_\Omega|^2, \qquad g(\partial_T, Y) = -\tfrac12F\,(Y^U + Y^V) .
> $$
>
> $Y$ causal means $g(Y, Y) \le 0$, so $Y^UY^V \ge |Y_\Omega|^2/F \ge 0$: $Y^U$ and $Y^V$ do not have opposite signs. $Y$ future-directed means $g(\partial_T, Y) < 0$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]), so $Y^U + Y^V > 0$. Together, $Y^U \ge 0$ and $Y^V \ge 0$. Along a future-directed causal curve $dU/d\lambda = \dot x^U \ge 0$ and $dV/d\lambda \ge 0$. Part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (where the two $\mathscr I^+$ lie). By [[§C10.3 Penrose Diagrams of Kruskal and of Collapse#^thm-c10-3-2|Theorem §C10.3.2]], in $\tilde U = \arctan U$, $\tilde V = \arctan V$ the null infinity $\mathscr I^+_{\rm I}$ is $\{\tilde V = \pi/2,\ -\pi/2 < \tilde U < 0\}$ and $\mathscr I^+_{\rm III}$ is $\{\tilde U = \pi/2,\ -\pi/2 < \tilde V < 0\}$, the mirror image under $U \leftrightarrow V$. Every point of $\mathscr I^+_{\rm I}$ has $U = \tan\tilde U < 0$, and every point of $\mathscr I^+_{\rm III}$ has $V < 0$.
>
> **Step 3** ($\{U < 0\}$ reaches $\mathscr I^+$). Let $p$ have $U = U_0 < 0$. The outgoing radial null geodesic through $p$ is $U = U_0$, $\theta, \varphi$ fixed, with $V$ increasing. Along it $UV = U_0V \to -\infty$ as $V \to \infty$, and since $r = G^{-1}(UV)$ with $G$ a decreasing bijection of $(0, \infty)$ onto $(-\infty, 1)$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], part 3), $r \to \infty$ (script: $r = 2.20M, 3.21M, 5.62M, 12.7M$ at $V = 1, 10, 10^2, 10^4$ for $U_0 = -0.3$). In the compactification the ray ends at $\tilde V = \pi/2$, $\tilde U = \arctan U_0 \in (-\pi/2, 0)$, a point of $\mathscr I^+_{\rm I}$. So $p \in J^-(\mathscr I^+)$. By the symmetry $U \leftrightarrow V$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], part 2), every $p$ with $V < 0$ reaches $\mathscr I^+_{\rm III}$.
>
> **Step 4** ($\{U \ge 0, V \ge 0\}$ does not). Let $p$ have $U \ge 0$, $V \ge 0$. By Step 1, along every future-directed causal curve from $p$, $U \ge 0$ and $V \ge 0$, and so do the limits $\tilde U \ge 0$, $\tilde V \ge 0$ at any endpoint in $\tilde M$. Points of $\mathscr I^+_{\rm I}$ have $\tilde U < 0$ and points of $\mathscr I^+_{\rm III}$ have $\tilde V < 0$ (Step 2), so the curve ends on neither. Hence $p \notin J^-(\mathscr I^+)$. Steps 3–4 give the first two sets of part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (the boundary). $\mathcal B = \{U \ge 0, V \ge 0\}$ is closed in $\mathcal K$. Its interior is $\{U > 0, V > 0\}$, region II. So $\partial\mathcal B = \mathcal B \setminus \text{interior} = \{U = 0, V \ge 0\} \cup \{V = 0, U \ge 0\}$. By [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], part 3, the first piece is the future horizon between I and II. The second piece is its mirror image, between III and II. Part 2.
>
> **Step 6** (agreement). On $N = \{V > 0\}$, $\mathcal B \cap N = \{U \ge 0, V > 0\} = \{UV \ge 0\}$. Since $UV = G(r)$ and $G(r) \ge 0 \Leftrightarrow r \le 2M$, this is $\{0 < r \le 2M\}$, and $\mathcal H^+ \cap N = \{U = 0, V > 0\} = \{r = 2M\}$. These are the sets of [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]], part 4. Part 3.
>
> **Step 7** (white hole). Reverse the time orientation: $-\partial_T$ makes $U$ and $V$ non-increasing along future-directed causal curves, and $\mathscr I^-_{\rm I} = \{\tilde U = -\pi/2,\ 0 < \tilde V < \pi/2\}$ has $V > 0$, $\mathscr I^-_{\rm III}$ has $U > 0$. Steps 3–5 with all inequalities reversed give part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - In Kruskal the global definition gives back the C7 horizon. It also gives a little more: the bifurcation sphere and the second future horizon belong to $\mathcal H^+$, and the horizons are included in $\mathcal B$, which is closed. [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-5|Def. §C7.2.5]] wrote the white hole as the open set $\{0 < r < 2M\}$. Its own criterion ("no future-directed causal curve from $r > 2M$ reaches it") also holds on the past horizon $r = 2M$ of $N'$, which part 4 puts into $\mathcal W$.
> - ⚑ The white-hole singularity (the past branch of $UV = 1$) lies in $\{U < 0, V < 0\} \subset J^-(\mathscr I^+)$: signals from arbitrarily close to it reach infinity. This is used in [[#^def-c10-7-5|Def. §C10.7.5]].

## What kind of surface an event horizon is

[[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]] found the Schwarzschild horizon to be a null surface crossed only inward. Both properties follow from the definition alone, for any asymptotically flat spacetime.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.7.2: The Event Horizon Is a Null Hypersurface Generated by Null Geodesics
> Let $(M, g)$ be asymptotically flat at null infinity, with $\mathcal B$ and $\mathcal H^+$ as in [[#^def-c10-7-2|Def. §C10.7.2]] and [[#^def-c10-7-3|Def. §C10.7.3]].
> 1. **Nothing leaves $\mathcal B$** (proved). If $p \in \mathcal B$, then $J^+(p) \subset \mathcal B$.
> 2. **Smooth pieces are null** (proved). Suppose that near some $p \in \mathcal H^+$ the horizon is a smooth hypersurface $\{f = 0\}$ with $df \neq 0$ and $\mathcal B = \{f \ge 0\}$ near $p$. Then the normal $n^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu f$ is null at $p$, so $\mathcal H^+$ is a null hypersurface there ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]]). Its generators, the integral curves of $n$, are null geodesics lying in $\mathcal H^+$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^thm-c10-5-3|Theorem §C10.5.3]]).
> 3. **In general** (quoted, not proved here). $\mathcal H^+$ is always a closed, achronal set (no two of its points are joined by a timelike curve), and it is a three-dimensional hypersurface that is continuous but need not be smooth. Through every point of it passes a null geodesic segment lying in $\mathcal H^+$. Followed to the future, a generator stays in $\mathcal H^+$ for ever, so it has no future endpoint. Followed to the past it may leave $\mathcal H^+$, and where generators leave (enter, read forwards) the horizon has kinks.
> 4. **The computed cases** (proved). In Kruskal and in the null-shell spacetime of [[#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]], $\mathcal H^+$ is generated by outgoing radial null geodesics. In the shell spacetime all generators start at one event, the centre $r = 0$ at $t = v_0 - 4M$, the tip of a light cone where $\mathcal H^+$ is not smooth.
>
> *Model:* asymptotically flat spacetimes; *applies:* parts 1, 2, 4 exactly, part 3 as quoted. Part 3 is Penrose's theorem on boundaries of causal pasts (achronal boundaries) applied to $\partial J^-(\mathscr I^+)$, with Hawking's result that horizon generators have no future endpoints; the proofs (Hawking and Ellis 1973, §6.3 and §9.2) need more causal theory than these notes develop.
>
> *Source: Schutz §11.3, p. 323 (PDF 341: "the horizon itself is composed of null world lines … these marginal null rays stay on the horizon forever") · Couzens, GR2, §4.4, p. 81 (PDF 82) (horizons as null hypersurfaces ruled by null geodesics) · Hartle §12.1 (the Schwarzschild case) · the step in Step 2 checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation (parts 1, 2 and 4)
> **Step 1** (part 1). Let $p \in \mathcal B$ and $q \in J^+(p)$, and suppose $q \in J^-(\mathscr I^+)$. A future-directed causal curve runs from $p$ to $q$ and another from $q$ to $\mathscr I^+$. Joined, they make one from $p$ to $\mathscr I^+$ (transitivity, [[#^def-c10-7-1|Def. §C10.7.1]]), so $p \in J^-(\mathscr I^+)$, against $p \in \mathcal B$. Hence $q \in \mathcal B$.
>
> **Step 2** (the normal is causal). Near $p$, $\mathcal B = \{f \ge 0\}$, so $p \in \mathcal B$. Let $X \in T_pM$ be future-directed timelike and $\gamma$ the geodesic with $\gamma(0) = p$, $\dot\gamma(0) = X$; it is future-directed timelike for $s \ge 0$ ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-7|Theorem §C3.7.7]]). By part 1, $\gamma(s) \in \mathcal B$ for $s > 0$, i.e. $f(\gamma(s)) \ge 0 = f(p)$. Hence
>
> $$
> g(n, X) = \partial_\mu f\,X^\mu = \frac{d}{ds}f\bigl(\gamma(s)\bigr)\Big|_{s = 0} \ge 0 \qquad \text{for every future-directed timelike } X .
> $$
>
> Suppose $n$ were spacelike. Take an orthonormal basis $e_0, \ldots, e_3$ at $p$ with $e_0$ future-directed ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]) and write $n = n^0e_0 + \vec n$, with $|\vec n| > |n^0|$. Choose $s$ with $|n^0|/|\vec n| < s < 1$ and put $X = e_0 - s\,\vec n/|\vec n|$. Then $g(X, X) = -1 + s^2 < 0$, $g(e_0, X) = -1 < 0$ (future-directed), and
>
> $$
> g(n, X) = -n^0 - s\,|\vec n| \le |n^0| - s\,|\vec n| < 0 ,
> $$
>
> a contradiction. So $n$ is causal, and $n \neq 0$ because $df \neq 0$.
>
> **Step 3** (the normal is not timelike). Suppose $n$ were timelike at $p$, i.e. the hypersurface spacelike. By continuity $n$ is timelike on a neighbourhood of $p$, and by Step 2 it lies in the past cone, so $g(n, Y) > 0$ for every future-directed causal $Y$ there ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]: two causal vectors in opposite cones have positive product). Take a chart $x^\mu$ on a closed coordinate ball $W$ of radius $\delta$ about $p$ with $g_{\mu\nu}(p) = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]). Shrinking $\delta$, every future-directed causal $Y$ in $W$ has $Y^0 > 0$ and $|\vec Y| \le 2Y^0$, and by compactness there is a $c > 0$ with
>
> $$
> df(Y) = g(n, Y) \ge c\,Y^0 \qquad \text{for all future-directed causal } Y \text{ at points of } W .
> $$
>
> Since $p \in \partial\mathcal B$, there are events $q \notin \mathcal B$, i.e. $f(q) = -\varepsilon < 0$, as close to $p$ as we like. Take one within coordinate distance $\delta/2$ of $p$ and with $3\varepsilon/c < \delta/2$. Because $q \in J^-(\mathscr I^+)$, some future-directed causal curve runs from $q$ to $\mathscr I^+$. Parametrize its first part by $x^0$, which increases along it. While it stays in $W$,
>
> $$
> \frac{df}{dx^0} \ge c, \qquad \Bigl|\frac{d\vec x}{dx^0}\Bigr| \le 2 ,
> $$
>
> so $f$ reaches $0$ after a coordinate time at most $\varepsilon/c$, during which the curve moves less than $3\varepsilon/c < \delta/2$ in coordinates and stays in $W$. There $f = 0$ puts it in $\mathcal B$, and by part 1 its continuation cannot reach $\mathscr I^+$, a contradiction. So $n$ is not timelike.
>
> **Step 4** (part 2). By Steps 2–3, $n$ is null at $p$: the hypersurface is null near $p$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]]), $n$ is tangent to it ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^thm-c10-5-2|Theorem §C10.5.2]]), and its integral curves are null geodesics ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^thm-c10-5-3|Theorem §C10.5.3]]).
>
> **Step 5** (part 4). Kruskal: $\mathcal H^+$ is $U = 0$ or $V = 0$ ([[#^thm-c10-7-1|Theorem §C10.7.1]]), each ruled by radial null geodesics ($V$ or $U$ an affine parameter, [[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^thm-c10-5-5|Theorem §C10.5.5]]). Shell: $\mathcal H^+$ is the future light cone $r = (v - v_0 + 4M)/2$ of the event $r = 0$, $t = v_0 - 4M$, continued by $r = 2M$ ([[#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]], part 2). Each generator is a radial light ray from that event, and at the event the cone has a vertex.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The one-way property (part 1) is pure logic: the definition is made of "can reach". The null character then follows because a spacelike horizon would be crossed by every causal curve from just outside, trapping events that were assumed to escape. A timelike one would let causal curves from inside leave. Only the borderline case, a surface of light rays, is consistent.

## The horizon depends on the whole future

Whether an event lies in $\mathcal B$ is decided by the causal curves that leave it, and they run to the end of time. The simplest spacetime that shows the consequence is flat space with one shell of light falling in. Its metric is the ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein form of Schwarzschild ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-2|Def. §C7.2.2]]) with the mass allowed to depend on advanced time $v$ (Vaidya's metric):

$$
ds^2 = -\Bigl(1 - \frac{2m(v)}{r}\Bigr)dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2, \qquad m(v) = M\,\Theta(v - v_0) .
$$

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.7.3: ⚑ The Event Horizon Is Teleological: It Depends on the Whole Future
> 1. **Decided by the future.** Whether $p \in \mathcal B$ depends only on the future-directed causal curves from $p$, i.e. on the spacetime in $J^+(p)$. No measurement near $p$ and no data on a time slice through $p$ decides it. Two spacetimes can be isometric on the whole region before some time and still have different black-hole regions there.
> 2. **A shell of light into flat space.** The metric above, with $M > 0$, solves Einstein's equations ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^pr-c5-1-4|Principle §C5.1.4]]) with the source of an infalling null shell, $T_{vv} = M\,\delta(v - v_0)/(4\pi r^2)$, all other components zero. This source satisfies the dominant energy condition ([[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^def-c4-5-4|Def. §C4.5.4]]). For $v < v_0$ the spacetime is flat, $v = t + r$; for $v > v_0$ it is Schwarzschild of mass $M$. Its event horizon is
>
> $$
> \mathcal H^+ :\quad r = \tfrac12(v - v_0 + 4M) \ \ (v_0 - 4M \le v \le v_0), \qquad r = 2M \ \ (v \ge v_0) ,
> $$
>
>    and $\mathcal B = \{t - r \ge v_0 - 4M,\ v \le v_0\} \cup \{r \le 2M,\ v \ge v_0\}$. The horizon is born at the centre at $t = v_0 - 4M$, while the shell is still at radius $4M$. It grows at the speed of light and meets the shell at $r = 2M$, $t = v_0 - 2M$.
> 3. **No trapped surface is needed.** For $v < v_0$ every round sphere has expansions $\theta_{\text{out}} = 1/r > 0$, $\theta_{\text{in}} = -2/r$ and is not trapped ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^def-c10-6-5|Def. §C10.6.5]]). This includes the spheres of $\mathcal H^+$ and of $\mathcal B$ there. Trapped spheres exist only for $v > v_0$, $r < 2M$, where $\theta_{\text{out}} = (1 - 2M/r)/r < 0$. The boundary of the trapped region jumps to $r = 2M$ at $v = v_0$, while $\mathcal H^+$ has grown continuously from $r = 0$.
> 4. **A shell onto an existing hole.** If instead a shell raises the mass of a Schwarzschild hole from $M_0$ to $M_1$ at $v = v_0$, then for $v < v_0$ the event horizon is the outgoing light ray with $v - 2r_\ast(r) = v_0 - 2r_\ast(2M_1)$ ($r_\ast$ of mass $M_0$). It lies strictly outside $r = 2M_0$ and approaches it as $r - 2M_0 \propto e^{(v - v_0)/4M_0}$ in the past. The surface $r = 2M_0$, a static null surface before the shell, lies inside $\mathcal B$ but is not part of $\mathcal H^+$. For $M_1 = 1.5M_0$: $r = 2.478M_0$ at $v_0 - 4M_0$, $2.202M_0$ at $v_0 - 8M_0$, $2.011M_0$ at $v_0 - 20M_0$.
>
> *Model:* the Vaidya metric with a step in $m$; a thin null shell is the idealization of a short, spherical burst of radiation or of any matter moving at nearly the speed of light; *applies:* exactly within the model. For a collapsing dust star the same happens inside the matter ([[§C10.3 Penrose Diagrams of Kruskal and of Collapse#^thm-c10-3-4|Theorem §C10.3.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schutz §11.3, Fig. 11.13, pp. 323–324 (PDF 341–342) (the horizon growing from zero radius; the shell falling onto a hole of mass $M_0$, "photons inside it fall towards the singularity", "the only way to find the true horizon is to know the entire future evolution") · Couzens, GR2, §4.5 (the definition) · the shell spacetime, its Einstein tensor and all numbers by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). By [[#^def-c10-7-2|Def. §C10.7.2]], $p \notin \mathcal B$ means that some future-directed causal curve from $p$ ends on $\mathscr I^+$. Every such curve lies in $J^+(p)$. Part 2 exhibits two spacetimes, Minkowski ($M = 0$) and the shell spacetime, that are identical on $\{v < v_0\}$. The event $t = v_0 - 2M$, $r = M$ lies in that region and is in $\mathcal B$ for the shell spacetime but not for Minkowski, where $\mathcal B = \emptyset$ ([[#^ex-c10-7-1|Example §C10.7.1]]).
>
> **Step 2** (Christoffel symbols). With $x^\mu = (v, r, \theta, \varphi)$, $g_{vv} = -(1 - 2m/r)$, $g_{vr} = 1$, $g_{rr} = 0$, inverse $g^{vv} = 0$, $g^{vr} = 1$, $g^{rr} = 1 - 2m/r$, the nonzero symbols (up to symmetry; script) are
>
> $$
> \Gamma^v_{vv} = \frac{m}{r^2}, \quad \Gamma^r_{vv} = \frac{m'}{r} + \frac{m}{r^2}\Bigl(1 - \frac{2m}{r}\Bigr), \quad \Gamma^r_{vr} = -\frac{m}{r^2}, \quad \Gamma^v_{\theta\theta} = -r, \quad \Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -(r - 2m) ,
> $$
>
> with $\Gamma^v_{\varphi\varphi} = \sin^2\theta\,\Gamma^v_{\theta\theta}$, $\Gamma^r_{\varphi\varphi} = \sin^2\theta\,\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}$, $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = \Gamma^\varphi_{r\varphi} = 1/r$, $\Gamma^\theta_{\varphi\varphi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta$, $\Gamma^\varphi_{\theta\varphi} = \cot\theta$, and $m' = dm/dv$. The derivative $m'$ appears only in $\Gamma^r_{vv}$.
>
> **Step 3** (Ricci tensor). With $R_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\rho\Gamma^\rho_{\nu\mu} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho_{\rho\mu} + \Gamma^\rho_{\rho\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\mu} - \Gamma^\rho_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\rho\mu}$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^def-c3-11-1|Def. §C3.11.1]]) and $\Gamma^\rho_{\rho\lambda} = \partial_\lambda\ln\sqrt{-g} = \partial_\lambda\ln(r^2\sin\theta)$, so $\Gamma^\rho_{\rho v} = 0$, $\Gamma^\rho_{\rho r} = 2/r$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \partial_\rho\Gamma^\rho_{vv} &= \partial_v\frac{m}{r^2} + \partial_r\Bigl(\frac{m'}{r} + \frac{m}{r^2} - \frac{2m^2}{r^3}\Bigr) = \frac{m'}{r^2} - \frac{m'}{r^2} - \frac{2m}{r^3} + \frac{6m^2}{r^4}, \\
> -\partial_v\Gamma^\rho_{\rho v} &= 0, \qquad \Gamma^\rho_{\rho\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{vv} = \frac2r\,\Gamma^r_{vv} = \frac{2m'}{r^2} + \frac{2m}{r^3} - \frac{4m^2}{r^4}, \\
> -\Gamma^\rho_{v\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\rho v} &= -(\Gamma^v_{vv})^2 - (\Gamma^r_{vr})^2 = -\frac{2m^2}{r^4} \qquad (\Gamma^v_{vr} = 0) .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> Adding, $R_{vv} = 2m'/r^2$. In every other component, $m'$ could enter only through $\Gamma^r_{vv}$ or through a $v$-derivative of a $\Gamma^v_{\mu\nu}$ or of $\Gamma^\rho_{\rho\mu}$. For $\mu\nu \ne vv$ none of these occurs: every product containing $\Gamma^r_{vv}$ also contains a $\Gamma^v_{r\mu}$ or $\Gamma^v_{\mu r}$, all zero, and $\Gamma^v_{\theta\theta}$, $\Gamma^v_{\varphi\varphi}$, $\Gamma^\rho_{\rho\mu}$ do not contain $m$. The remaining terms depend on $m$ algebraically, exactly as for constant mass. So these components equal those of the Schwarzschild metric with constant mass $m(v)$, which are zero ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]], [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]). Hence $R = g^{vv}R_{vv} = 0$ and $G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu}$: the only nonzero component is $G_{vv} = 2m'/r^2$ (script: full Ricci tensor).
>
> **Step 4** (the source). $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$ gives $T_{vv} = m'/(4\pi r^2)$. With $m = M\Theta(v - v_0)$, $m' = M\delta(v - v_0)$. $R_{vv}$ is linear in $m'$ and contains no product of $m'$ with the jump of $m$, so the equation holds as an equation between distributions in $v$. The energy crossing a sphere is $\int T_{vv}\,4\pi r^2\,dv = M$. Write $T_{\mu\nu} = \mu\,k_\mu k_\nu$ with $k_\mu = -\partial_\mu v$, $\mu = m'/(4\pi r^2) \ge 0$. Raising, $k^\mu = -g^{\mu v}$, so $k^v = 0$, $k^r = -1$: $k = -\partial_r$, the future-directed ingoing null vector of [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-2|Theorem §C7.2.2]]. For a future-directed timelike $u$, $g(k, u) < 0$, and the current of [[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^def-c4-5-4|Def. §C4.5.4]] is
>
> $$
> j(u)^\mu = -T^\mu{}_\nu u^\nu = -\mu\,k^\mu\,g(k, u) = \mu\,|g(k, u)|\,k^\mu ,
> $$
>
> future-directed null or zero: the DEC holds.
>
> **Step 5** (the two regions). For $v < v_0$, $m = 0$, and with $v = t + r$, $dv = dt + dr$:
>
> $$
> -dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr = -(dt + dr)^2 + 2(dt + dr)\,dr = -dt^2 + dr^2 ,
> $$
>
> flat space. For $v > v_0$ the metric is Schwarzschild in ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein form ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]). $\mathscr I^+$ lies at $v \to \infty$, in the Schwarzschild part, where the spacetime is asymptotically flat ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^ex-c10-4-3|Example §C10.4.3]]).
>
> **Step 6** (horizon). In the flat part put $u = t - r = v - 2r$. For a future-directed causal vector, $dt \ge |d\vec x| \ge |dr|$, so $du = dt - dr \ge 0$: $u$ never decreases. A causal curve reaching $\mathscr I^+$ must cross $v = v_0$, where $r = (v_0 - u)/2$ ($r$ is continuous across the shell). Write $v_h = v_0 - 4M$.
> - If $u_p < v_h$, the outgoing radial light ray from $p$ keeps $u = u_p$ and meets the shell at $r = (v_0 - u_p)/2 > 2M$. Beyond the shell it is an outgoing ray of Schwarzschild at $r > 2M$, which reaches arbitrarily large $r$ and $\mathscr I^+$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]], Step 7 of its derivation). So $p \notin \mathcal B$.
> - If $u_p \ge v_h$, every future-directed causal curve from $p$ has $u \ge v_h$ when it meets the shell, hence $r \le 2M$ there. By [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]], part 4, it stays at $r \le 2M$, so it never reaches $\mathscr I^+$, and $p \in \mathcal B$.
>
> For $v \ge v_0$, [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]] gives $\mathcal B = \{r \le 2M\}$. The boundary is $u = v_h$, i.e. $r = (v - v_h)/2$, for $v \le v_0$, then $r = 2M$. At $v = v_h$ this ray is at $r = 0$, $t = v_h$, while the shell, at $r = v_0 - t$ for $t < v_0$, is at $r = 4M$. The ray reaches $r = 2M$ at $v = v_0$, $t = v_0 - 2M$. Part 2 (script also integrates rays leaving the centre at $v_c = -5M, -4.2M, -4.02M$: escape; $-3.98M, -3.8M, -3M$: fall to $r = 0$; $M = 1$, $v_0 = 0$).
>
> **Step 7** (expansions). The outgoing null vector $\ell = \partial_v + \tfrac12(1 - 2m/r)\partial_r$ is null and satisfies $\ell^\nu\nabla_\nu\ell^\mu = (m/r^2)\,\ell^\mu$ (script), so it is tangent to null geodesics, and the round spheres of radius $r$ have area $4\pi r^2$. The expansion is the fractional rate of change of area along $\ell$ ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^def-c10-6-2|Def. §C10.6.2]]; computed for Schwarzschild in [[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^thm-c10-6-5|Theorem §C10.6.5]]):
>
> $$
> \theta_{\text{out}} = \frac{\ell(4\pi r^2)}{4\pi r^2} = \frac{2\,\ell^r}{r} = \frac1r\Bigl(1 - \frac{2m(v)}{r}\Bigr), \qquad \theta_{\text{in}} = \frac{2\,(-\partial_r)^r}{r} = -\frac2r .
> $$
>
> For $v < v_0$, $\theta_{\text{out}} = 1/r > 0$; for $v > v_0$ it is negative exactly when $r < 2M$. Part 3.
>
> **Step 8** (part 4). Replace $m$ by $M_0$ for $v < v_0$ and $M_1$ for $v > v_0$. Steps 2–4 are unchanged, with $m' = (M_1 - M_0)\delta(v - v_0)$, so the shell carries energy $M_1 - M_0$. For $v > v_0$, $\mathcal H^+$ is $r = 2M_1$. For $v < v_0$ it is the outgoing ray of the mass-$M_0$ metric that arrives at $r = 2M_1$ at $v_0$. Outgoing rays obey $dr/dv = \tfrac12(1 - 2M_0/r)$, and since $dr_\ast/dr = (1 - 2M_0/r)^{-1}$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]),
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dv}\bigl[v - 2r_\ast(r)\bigr] = 1 - 2\Bigl(1 - \frac{2M_0}{r}\Bigr)^{-1}\cdot\frac12\Bigl(1 - \frac{2M_0}{r}\Bigr) = 0 .
> $$
>
> So the ray is $v - 2r_\ast(r) = v_0 - 2r_\ast(2M_1)$. As $v \to -\infty$, $r_\ast = r + 2M_0\ln(r/2M_0 - 1) \to -\infty$, so $r \to 2M_0^+$, with $\ln(r/2M_0 - 1) \approx (v - C')/4M_0$, i.e. $r - 2M_0 \propto e^{(v - v_0)/4M_0}$ (script: the ratio of $r - 2M_0$ at $v_0 - 20M_0$ and $v_0 - 40M_0$ is $147.6$, against $e^5 = 148.4$). A ray at $r = 2M_0$ for $v < v_0$ stays there until $v_0$, then lies inside $r = 2M_1$ and falls in. It is in $\mathcal B$ but not on its boundary.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** At the event $(t, r) = (v_0 - 2M, M)$ every local quantity is that of empty flat space. What puts it in $\mathcal B$ is Step 6: the light it emits will meet the shell inside $r = 2M$, an event in its future. The event horizon is defined by where light ends up, so locating it means solving the field equations to the end. Schutz: "The only way to find the true horizon is to know the entire future evolution".

## Stationary black holes

The Rindler and shell examples show that local tests and event horizons can disagree. For a black hole that has settled down, they agree again, and this is a theorem.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.7.4: Hawking's Rigidity Theorem (Stated): a Stationary Black Hole's Horizon Is a Killing Horizon
> Let $(M, g)$ be stationary ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-3|Def. §C3.13.3]]), asymptotically flat at null infinity ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]]), analytic, and a solution of the vacuum equations $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$, and let it contain a black hole ([[#^def-c10-7-2|Def. §C10.7.2]]). Then the future event horizon $\mathcal H^+$ is a Killing horizon ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-3|Def. §C10.5.3]]) of some Killing vector field $\xi$ ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-2|Def. §C3.13.2]]).
>
> *What follows.*
> - If the stationary Killing field $k$ is itself null on $\mathcal H^+$, then $\xi = k$. Schwarzschild is this case: $\mathcal H^+ = \{U = 0\}$ is the Killing horizon of $\partial_t$ with $\kappa = 1/4M$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^thm-c10-5-4|Theorem §C10.5.4]]).
> - Otherwise the hole rotates. The theorem then also gives a second, axial Killing field $m$, and $\xi = k + \Omega_Hm$ with a constant $\Omega_H$, the angular velocity of the horizon (Kerr, C12.2 and C12.5, planned).
> - Every stationary event horizon therefore has a surface gravity ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-4|Def. §C10.5.4]]), constant along it ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^rem-c10-5-1|§C10.5, Remark: Constancy of κ]]): the starting point of black-hole thermodynamics (C13.1, planned).
> - The converse fails: a Killing horizon need not be an event horizon ([[#^ex-c10-7-1|Example §C10.7.1]]).
>
> *Not proved here.* The proof (Hawking 1972; Hawking and Ellis 1973, §9.3; research literature, not in the typed sources) first shows, with Raychaudhuri's equation ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^thm-c10-6-2|Theorem §C10.6.2]]), that the generators of a stationary horizon neither expand nor shear, and then uses analyticity to extend a Killing field from the horizon to the whole spacetime. Analyticity is an assumption about the solution, not a physical requirement. Versions without it are known only under extra conditions. With charge (Einstein–Maxwell) the same statement holds.
>
> *Model:* stationary, analytic, asymptotically flat vacuum spacetimes; *applies:* to black holes that have settled down, not during collapse or merger. In the shell spacetime of [[#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]], $\mathcal H^+$ for $v < v_0$ is not a Killing horizon: the spacetime is not stationary there.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.5, Theorem 3 ("Hawking 1972"), p. 82 (PDF 83) · Hartle §15.1, pp. 310–311 (PDF 311–312) (stationary holes described by $M$ and $J$)*

## Singularities that can be seen

The singularity theorems guarantee that collapse produces a singularity once a trapped surface forms ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^rem-c10-6-1|§C10.6, Remark: Penrose's singularity theorem]]). Whether a black hole hides it is a separate question, and the definition of $\mathcal B$ makes it precise.

> [!definition] Definition §C10.7.5: Naked Singularity
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be asymptotically flat at null infinity and singular ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-8|Def. §C10.4.8]]): causally geodesically incomplete ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-6|Def. §C10.4.6]]) and inextendible ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-7|Def. §C10.4.7]]). $(M, g)$ has a **naked singularity** if some causal geodesic that is past-inextendible ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-5|Def. §C10.4.5]]) and incomplete towards the past (its affine parameter is bounded below) lies in $J^-(\mathscr I^+)$ ([[#^def-c10-7-2|Def. §C10.7.2]]).
>
> *Reading.* The geodesic comes out of the singularity: followed backwards, it ends after finite affine parameter and cannot be continued. Since $J^-(\mathscr I^+)$ is a past set ([[#^def-c10-7-1|Def. §C10.7.1]]), it is enough that one event of the geodesic can signal to $\mathscr I^+$; then every earlier event can too. So signals from arbitrarily close to where it ends reach distant observers. A singularity whose incomplete geodesics all end inside $\mathcal B$ is **hidden behind the event horizon**.
>
> *Examples.* The future singularity of Kruskal and of collapse lies in $\mathcal B$: hidden. The white-hole singularity of Kruskal lies in $\{U < 0, V < 0\} \subset J^-(\mathscr I^+)$ ([[#^thm-c10-7-1|Theorem §C10.7.1]], derivation): naked in this sense. Kruskal is not formed from regular initial data, so this does not contradict [[#^rem-c10-7-1|Remark: Weak cosmic censorship]]. Schwarzschild with $M < 0$ has a naked singularity ([[#^ex-c10-7-3|Example §C10.7.3]]). So does the charged black hole with more charge than mass, whose singularity is timelike and visible from $\mathscr I^+$ ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-6|Theorem §C11.3.6]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.5, Definition 16, p. 83 (PDF 84) ("a singularity from which signals can reach ℐ⁺, i.e. one that is not hidden behind an event horizon"; made precise here with incomplete geodesics) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.10, p. 384 (PDF 399) (Kerr with $a^2 > m^2$: "the singularity is visible, in all its nakedness, to the outside world") · Schutz §11.3, p. 327 (PDF 345) ("singularities outside horizons")*

^def-c10-7-5

> [!remark] Remark: Weak cosmic censorship
> *The conjecture* (as Couzens states it, p. 83, PDF 84): naked singularities ([[#^def-c10-7-5|Def. §C10.7.5]]) do not form in gravitational collapse from generic, initially non-singular states in an asymptotically flat spacetime ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]]) whose matter obeys the dominant energy condition ([[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^def-c4-5-4|Def. §C4.5.4]]). A sharper modern form says that $\mathscr I^+$ is complete ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-3|Def. §C10.4.3]]) for the evolution of generic regular data: distant observers live for ever, and no singularity cuts their future short.
>
> *Why each hypothesis is there.*
> - *Energy condition.* Without one, any metric solves Einstein's equations with $T_{\mu\nu} \equiv G_{\mu\nu}/8\pi$. Negative-mass Schwarzschild is a naked singularity ([[#^ex-c10-7-3|Example §C10.7.3]]), and the positive energy theorem excludes it once the DEC holds (d'Inverno & Vickers §20.12, p. 391, PDF 406).
> - *Generic.* For finely tuned initial data naked singularities do form: spherical collapse of a scalar field (Christodoulou, *Ann. of Math.* 140 (1994) 607–653), and the critical solutions of numerical relativity (Choptuik, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 70 (1993) 9). They disappear under small perturbations of the data (Christodoulou, *Ann. of Math.* 149 (1999) 183–217, arXiv:math/9901147). These are research results, not in the typed sources; references checked 2026-11-20.
> - *Non-singular start.* Kruskal's white-hole singularity is naked, but it is there from the start ([[#^thm-c10-7-1|Theorem §C10.7.1]]).
>
> *What it buys.* If it holds, the singularities that collapse must produce ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^rem-c10-6-1|§C10.6, Remark: Penrose's singularity theorem]]) stay inside $\mathcal B$. The breakdown of the theory there never reaches distant observers. The outside settles to a stationary black hole, a Killing horizon by [[#^thm-c10-7-4|Theorem §C10.7.4]], and by the uniqueness theorems a Kerr black hole described by $M$ and $J$ (Hartle §15.1: "the final Kerr black hole … is characterized by just two parameters").
>
> *Status.* Unproved, and even its precise formulation is debated (Hartle §12.4, p. 275; Schutz p. 327). The evidence is the absence of generic counterexamples and failed attempts to make one, such as Hartle's Example 15.2: a particle thrown into an extremal Kerr hole to spin it beyond $a = M$ is not captured.

^rem-c10-7-1

> [!caution] Caution: Strong vs weak cosmic censorship
> Couzens (§4.5, p. 83, PDF 84) labels the conjecture above "Strong cosmic conjecture". The statement printed is the **weak** cosmic censorship conjecture: no singularity visible from $\mathscr I^+$. The **strong** conjecture is a different claim. For generic initial data, the region the data determine (the maximal globally hyperbolic development) cannot be extended. In other words, there are no Cauchy horizons, beyond which the future stops being determined by the data, even for observers who fall into the black hole. It concerns the inside of black holes, such as the inner horizons of Reissner–Nordström and Kerr ([[§C11.4 Cauchy Surfaces and Cauchy Horizons#^thm-c11-4-2|Theorem §C11.4.2]]), where weak censorship says nothing. Reissner–Nordström is the test case of the strong conjecture ([[§C11.4 Cauchy Surfaces and Cauchy Horizons#^rem-c11-4-2|§C11.4, Remark: Strong cosmic censorship]]). Despite the names, neither conjecture implies the other (a standard remark of the research literature, not in the typed sources). Couzens uses "strong" in its correct sense in §5.3. Schutz (p. 327, PDF 345) states the weak conjecture and then cites Dafermos and Luk (2017) as "a disproof of one important form of the conjecture … a naked but weak null singularity". That work (Dafermos and Luk, arXiv:1710.01722; *Ann. of Math.* 202 (2025)) concerns the strong conjecture in its continuous-metric form: it shows that the Cauchy horizon inside a perturbed rotating black hole survives. The singularity it describes lies inside $\mathcal B$, and it is not visible from $\mathscr I^+$.

> Read on the rendered pages:
> - *Couzens, §4.5, p. 82 (PDF 83).* "A point $p$ will always be in its causal future $J^+(S)$" should read $J^+(p)$. "The causal past $J^-$ … along a past-directed timelike paths" should say *causal* paths: with timelike paths one gets $I^-$ ([[#^def-c10-7-1|Def. §C10.7.1]]).

Schutz means "the boundary rays between escaping and trapped".

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C10.7.1: Rindler Has a Horizon but No Black Hole
> *Question.* In Minkowski space an observer moves with constant proper acceleration $1/\xi_0$ on $x^2 - t^2 = \xi_0^2$, $x > 0$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^mod-c10-2-1|Model §C10.2.1]]; Couzens writes $\alpha$ for $\xi_0$). She has a horizon. Does Minkowski space contain a black hole?
>
> **Step 1** (no black hole). Let $p$ be any event and $k$ any future-directed null vector at $p$. The null geodesic $p + \lambda k$, $\lambda \to \infty$, ends on $\mathscr I^+$ ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^ex-c10-1-1|Example §C10.1.1]]). So $J^-(\mathscr I^+) \supset M$, and $\mathcal B = \emptyset$, $\mathcal H^+ = \emptyset$ ([[#^def-c10-7-2|Def. §C10.7.2]]).
>
> **Step 2** (her horizon). A light ray moving in the $+x$ direction from $p = (t_p, x_p, y_p, z_p)$ keeps $x - t = d \equiv x_p - t_p$. On the observer's worldline $(x - t)(x + t) = \xi_0^2$, so the ray meets her (at $x + t = \xi_0^2/d$) iff $d > 0$. Conversely, along any future-directed causal curve $dx \le |d\vec x| \le dt$, so $x - t$ never increases, and from an event with $x_p - t_p \le 0$ no causal curve reaches her worldline, where $x - t > 0$. So the events that can signal to her form $J^-(\text{worldline}) = \{t < x\}$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-3|Theorem §C10.2.3]]). Its boundary $t = x$ is the Rindler horizon. Its normal $d(t - x)$ has norm $\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu(t - x)\partial_\nu(t - x) = -1 + 1 = 0$: a null hypersurface ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]]). It is even a Killing horizon of the boost field with $\kappa = 1/\xi_0$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^ex-c10-5-2|Example §C10.5.2]]).
>
> **Step 3** (comparison). The Rindler horizon shares every local property of the Schwarzschild horizon: it is null, ruled by null geodesics, a Killing horizon with nonzero surface gravity, and $g_{\eta\eta} = 0$ there. What it lacks is the global property: it is $\partial J^-$ of one worldline, not $\partial J^-(\mathscr I^+)$. An inertial observer has no horizon at all, because in four dimensions the past of a whole inertial worldline is all of $M$. Events behind her horizon are hidden from her, not from the universe.
>
> *Moral.* Killing horizons, vanishing $g_{tt}$ and nonzero surface gravity do not define a black hole (attempt 1 of the opening). [[#^thm-c10-7-4|Theorem §C10.7.4]] goes one way only: stationary event horizons are Killing horizons, not the reverse. The observer-dependence was announced in [[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^rem-c10-2-1|§C10.2, Remark: Horizons are relative to observers]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.3 (Rindler) and §4.5 (definition) · Schutz Exercise 5.21 (Rindler horizons resembling black-hole horizons) · 't Hooft, ch. 3, Fig. 1 · checked by script*

^ex-c10-7-1

> [!example] Example §C10.7.2: A Shell of Light Falling into Flat Space
> *Question.* A spherical shell of radiation with energy $Mc^2$ converges on the centre of an otherwise empty region and reaches it at $t = v_0$ ([[#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]]). When and where does the black hole begin? What happens to an observer at rest at $r = M$, and how long is she inside $\mathcal B$? Numbers for $M = 10\,M_\odot$ and for $M = 4.3\times10^6\,M_\odot$ (the mass of Sgr A$^\ast$).
>
> **Step 1** (birth of the horizon). By [[#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]], part 2, $\mathcal H^+$ leaves the centre at $t_h = v_0 - 4M$ (restored $v_0 - 4GM/c^3$), when the shell is at $r = 4M$ ($4GM/c^2$). With $GM_\odot/c^3 = 4.925\ \mu\mathrm s$ and $GM_\odot/c^2 = 1.477\ \mathrm{km}$:
>
> | $M$ | $4GM/c^3$ (horizon before the shell reaches the centre) | $4GM/c^2$ (shell radius then) | $2GM/c^3$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $10\,M_\odot$ | $197\ \mu\mathrm s$ | $59.1\ \mathrm{km}$ | $98.5\ \mu\mathrm s$ |
> | $4.3\times10^6\,M_\odot$ | $84.7\ \mathrm s$ | $2.54\times10^{10}\ \mathrm m = 0.17\ \mathrm{AU}$ | $42.4\ \mathrm s$ |
>
> **Step 2** (when the observer is caught). She sits at $r_1 = M$ in flat space. The outgoing ray from her event $(t, r_1)$ has $u = t - r_1$, and by Step 6 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]] she is in $\mathcal B$ iff $u \ge v_0 - 4M$, i.e. from
>
> $$
> t = v_0 - 4M + r_1 = v_0 - 3M .
> $$
>
> The shell reaches her at $v = t + r_1 = v_0$, $t = v_0 - M$. For $2M$ of her proper time she is at rest in flat space inside the black-hole region. She still receives light from outside and feels no tidal force; nothing she can measure tells her. For $10\,M_\odot$ this lasts $98.5\ \mu\mathrm s$.
>
> **Step 3** (the shell passes). Her velocity before is $u^v = dv/d\tau = 1$, $u^r = 0$. In the geodesic equation ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]) only $\Gamma^r_{vv}$ contains the delta function, through $m'/r$, so with $dv = u^v d\lambda$
>
> $$
> \Delta u^r = -\int\frac{m'(v)}{r}\,u^v\,dv = -\frac{M}{r_1}\,u^v = -1, \qquad u^v = 1 \ \text{unchanged}.
> $$
>
> Check: $g(u, u) = -(1 - 2M/M)(1)^2 + 2(1)(-1) = 1 - 2 = -1$. After the shell she is in Schwarzschild at $r = M < 2M$ with $E = -u_v = -(g_{vv}u^v + g_{vr}u^r) = -(1 - 1) = 0$, so $(dr/d\tau)^2 = E^2 - f = 2M/r - 1$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]).
>
> **Step 4** (the end). With $r = 2M\sin^2\psi$, $dr = 4M\sin\psi\cos\psi\,d\psi$ and $\sqrt{2M/r - 1} = \cos\psi/\sin\psi$, so
>
> $$
> \tau = \int_0^M\frac{dr}{\sqrt{2M/r - 1}} = \int_0^{\pi/4}4M\sin^2\psi\,d\psi = 2M\Bigl[\psi - \sin\psi\cos\psi\Bigr]_0^{\pi/4} = 2M\Bigl(\frac\pi4 - \frac12\Bigr) = \Bigl(\frac\pi2 - 1\Bigr)M = 0.571M ,
> $$
>
> below the bound $\pi M$ of [[§C7.4 Falling into a Black Hole#^thm-c7-4-4|Theorem §C7.4.4]]. Her worldline is $dv/dr = u^v/u^r = 1/f$, i.e. $v = v_0 + r - M + 2M\ln(2 - r/M)$, which reaches $r = 0$ at $v = v_0 + (2\ln 2 - 1)M = v_0 + 0.386M$ (green in the figure above). Total time inside $\mathcal B$: $2M + 0.571M = 2.571M$, for $10\,M_\odot$: $98.5 + 28.1 = 126.6\ \mu\mathrm s$.
>
> *What it shows.* The opening's observer is this one at $t = v_0 - 2M$: inside $\mathcal B$ for $M$ already, in flat space, with no trapped surface anywhere ([[#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]], part 3). Only the future, the arrival of the shell, puts her there.
>
> *Source: Schutz §11.3, pp. 323–324 (PDF 341–342) (the idea) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C10.7.3: Negative-Mass Schwarzschild Has a Naked Singularity
> *Question.* The Schwarzschild metric with $M < 0$ solves the vacuum equations for $r > 0$, since the computation of [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]] never used the sign of $M$. Is there a horizon, and is $r = 0$ hidden?
>
> **Step 1** (no horizon). $f = 1 - 2M/r = 1 + 2|M|/r \ge 1$ for all $r > 0$. Radial outgoing light obeys $dr/dt = f \ge 1$ ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-3|Theorem §C7.1.3]]), so $r(t) \ge r_0 + (t - t_0) \to \infty$: from every event a light ray reaches $\mathscr I^+$, $J^-(\mathscr I^+) \supset M$, $\mathcal B = \emptyset$.
>
> **Step 2** (singular). For radial null geodesics ($L = 0$) the radial equation is $(dr/d\lambda)^2 = E^2$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]), so $r$ is an affine parameter up to the factor $E$. An ingoing ray from $r_0$ reaches $r = 0$ at $\lambda = r_0/E$, finite. There $K = 48M^2/r^6 \to \infty$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-7|Theorem §C6.2.7]]), so no extension ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-7|Def. §C10.4.7]]) can continue it, and the spacetime is singular ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-8|Def. §C10.4.8]]).
>
> **Step 3** (naked). Run the same geodesic backwards: the outgoing radial null geodesic $r = E(\lambda - \lambda_0)$, $\lambda > \lambda_0$, is past-inextendible and past-incomplete, and by Step 1 it lies in $J^-(\mathscr I^+)$. By [[#^def-c10-7-5|Def. §C10.7.5]] the singularity is naked. Moreover the normal $dr$ of $r = \text{const}$ has $g^{rr} = f > 0$, so these surfaces are timelike ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-2|Def. §C10.5.2]]): the singularity is a place that exists at all times, not a moment as in Kruskal.
>
> **Step 4** (why it does not refute censorship). Far away the metric is that of a mass $M < 0$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-4|Theorem §C6.2.4]]). By the positive energy theorem quoted by d'Inverno & Vickers (§20.12, p. 391, PDF 406), an asymptotically flat spacetime whose matter satisfies the dominant energy condition has non-negative mass. So no collapse of ordinary matter produces this spacetime, and [[#^rem-c10-7-1|Remark: Weak cosmic censorship]] assumes the DEC for this reason.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.12, p. 391 (PDF 406) (positive energy theorem) · Couzens, GR2, §4.5, Definition 16 · checked by script (f > 0, escape of outgoing rays)*

> [!remark]- Connections

The chapter's question has its answer. A black hole is the set of events that cannot send a signal to future null infinity, and its event horizon, the boundary of that set, is a surface of light rays that nothing crosses outward. It is fixed by the whole future of the spacetime, not by anything measured locally. The Schwarzschild hole, the only one met so far, is uncharged and non-rotating. What changes when the hole carries charge, which gives it a second horizon inside the first and a singularity that is timelike, is [[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström|§C11.3]].
