The chapter asks what a black hole is, defined without coordinates. C7 gave a working answer for one spacetime: the event horizon of Schwarzschild is the boundary of the region from which no signal reaches large $r$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]]), and it sits where $g_{tt} = -(1 - 2M/r)$ vanishes ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-1|Theorem §C7.1.1]]). The quickest candidate for a general definition is therefore: *a black hole is bounded by the surface where $g_{tt} = 0$.*

The candidate fails in flat spacetime. Take the $(t, x)$ plane of Minkowski spacetime, $ds^2 = -dt^2 + dx^2$, restrict to the wedge $x > |t|$, and change to the coordinates $t = \xi\sinh\eta$, $x = \xi\cosh\eta$ (the chart already met in [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^ex-c2-2-3|Example §C2.2.3]]). Then $dt = \sinh\eta\,d\xi + \xi\cosh\eta\,d\eta$, $dx = \cosh\eta\,d\xi + \xi\sinh\eta\,d\eta$, and

$$
-dt^2 + dx^2 = -\xi^2\,d\eta^2 + d\xi^2, \qquad g_{\eta\eta} = -\xi^2 \xrightarrow{\ \xi \to 0\ } 0 .
$$

The "time" component vanishes at $\xi = 0$, as $g_{tt}$ does at $r = 2M$, and the metric degenerates there, $\det g = -\xi^2 \to 0$, as the Schwarzschild components degenerate at $r = 2M$. Yet the Riemann tensor is zero, there is no mass anywhere, and in the inertial chart nothing vanishes at all. The test "$g_{tt} = 0$" is a property of a chart, not of spacetime. What is invariant is that $g_{\eta\eta} = K\cdot K$ for the vector field $K = \partial_\eta$, which turns out to be the boost Killing field: the surface is where one chosen symmetry becomes null. Minkowski spacetime has such a surface for its boosts and none for its time translations.

The failure is instructive, because the surface $\xi = 0$ really is a horizon, for the observers who sit at constant $\xi$: light from beyond it never reaches them. This section studies that horizon, the simplest one there is. It sets up the family of uniformly accelerated observers ([[#^mod-c10-2-1|Model §C10.2.1]]) and the chart adapted to them ([[#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]]), shows that the metric in it is flat spacetime and what each of its features means ([[#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]]), proves that the boundary of the wedge is a horizon, and one that belongs to the observers rather than to spacetime ([[#^thm-c10-2-3|Theorem §C10.2.3]]), and finds the same geometry, to leading order, next to every Schwarzschild horizon ([[#^thm-c10-2-4|Theorem §C10.2.4]]).

**Setup.** Minkowski spacetime in an inertial chart $(t, x, y, z)$, time-oriented by $\partial_t$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]). The accelerated motion is along $x$; the transverse coordinates $y$, $z$ are carried along unchanged, so most statements are made in the $(t, x)$ plane and hold in four dimensions with $+\,dy^2 + dz^2$ added. Observers are test observers that accelerate forever: their own gravity is neglected, and "forever" is part of the idealisation (what changes when it is dropped is [[#^thm-c10-2-3|Theorem §C10.2.3]], part 5). A signal from $p$ to $q$ is a future-directed causal curve from $p$ to $q$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]). [[#^thm-c10-2-4|Theorem §C10.2.4]] concerns the exterior $r > 2M$ of the Schwarzschild metric ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]) close to $r = 2M$; it is exact as an identity and leading-order as an approximation, with the error stated.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here a proper acceleration $a$ is $a/c^2$, so $1/a \to c^2/a$, a proper time $\tau \to c\tau$, and $M \to GM/c^2$); light-cone coordinates $u = t - x$, $v = t + x$ as Couzens (4.4); $\xi$ is the Rindler coordinate and $\xi_0$ the value labelling one observer; $d\Omega^2 = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2$. ## Uniformly accelerated observers and their chart

Level B followed one particle that accelerates uniformly from rest. A horizon is a statement about which events can reach an observer, and the natural object is a whole family of such observers that together fill a region of spacetime, as the points of a rigid rocket do.

> [!model] Model §C10.2.1: Uniformly Accelerated Observers
> *Arena.* Two-dimensional Minkowski spacetime, $\mathbf R^2$ with $ds^2 = -dt^2 + dx^2$ in inertial coordinates $(t, x)$, time-oriented by $\partial_t$; in four dimensions each observer also keeps fixed $y$, $z$.
>
> *One observer.* For each $\xi_0 > 0$ the worldline
>
> $$
> \gamma_{\xi_0}(\tau) = \bigl(t, x\bigr) = \Bigl(\xi_0\sinh\frac{\tau}{\xi_0},\ \xi_0\cosh\frac{\tau}{\xi_0}\Bigr), \qquad \tau \in \mathbf R .
> $$
>
> Its velocity is $u = (\cosh(\tau/\xi_0), \sinh(\tau/\xi_0))$ with $u\cdot u = -\cosh^2 + \sinh^2 = -1$, so $\tau$ is proper time. Its four-acceleration $a = du/d\tau = \xi_0^{-1}(\sinh(\tau/\xi_0), \cosh(\tau/\xi_0))$ has $u\cdot a = 0$ and
>
> $$
> a\cdot a = \frac{1}{\xi_0^2}\bigl(-\sinh^2 + \cosh^2\bigr) = \frac{1}{\xi_0^2} :
> $$
>
> the **proper acceleration** is constant, $1/\xi_0$ (restored $c^2/\xi_0$), directed toward larger $x$ in every momentary rest frame. The worldline is the branch $x = +\sqrt{\xi_0^2 + t^2}$ of the hyperbola $x^2 - t^2 = \xi_0^2$, asymptotic to the null lines $x = -t$ in the past and $x = t$ in the future. The number $\xi_0$ is the observer's distance from the **pivot** $t = x = 0$, measured in its momentary rest frame ([[#^ex-c10-2-1|Example §C10.2.1]], part (a)).
>
> *The family.* All $\xi_0 \in (0, \infty)$ together: nested hyperbolae with common asymptotes, observers farther out accelerating less. They fill the wedge $x > |t|$, one through each event ([[#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]]). They keep constant proper distances from one another: the family is the rigid rocket of [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^mod-c1-4-1|Model §C1.4.1]], extended to all heights (the floor of a rocket with acceleration $g$ is $\xi_0 = 1/g$, the point at height $h$ is $\xi_0 = 1/g + h$; rigidity is Step 2 of the derivation of [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]]). As $\xi_0 \to 0$ the acceleration needed diverges: no rigid rocket extends more than $1/g$ below a floor accelerating at $g$.
>
> *Layer change.* [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^mod-b3-3-4|Model §B3.3.4]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, $c$ explicit) is one particle starting from rest at $x = 0$, with $(x + c^2/\alpha)^2 - (ct)^2 = (c^2/\alpha)^2$ ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]); moving the origin to the pivot, $x \to x - c^2/\alpha$, gives $\gamma_{\xi_0}$ with $\xi_0 = c^2/\alpha$. New here: a family that fills a region, all of whose members are orbits of one vector field, the boost Killing field $x\,\partial_t + t\,\partial_x$ ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^ex-c3-13-1|Example §C3.13.1]]), with the common flow parameter $\eta = \tau/\xi_0$. That shared parameter is what makes the family a chart.
>
> - *Assumes:* flat spacetime; test observers; each observer driven along its hyperbola for all $\tau \in \mathbf R$.
> - *Couzens' letter.* GR2 (4.19) writes this worldline with $\alpha$ in place of $\xi_0$; its proper acceleration is $\alpha^{-1}$, as his sentence before (4.19) says, not $\alpha$ as (4.20) prints ([[#^cau-c10-2-1|Caution: Couzens' α]]).
> - *Fails:* for any real rocket, which accelerates for a finite time; the horizon of [[#^thm-c10-2-3|Theorem §C10.2.3]] then disappears (part 5 there).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.19)–(4.21), p. 70 (PDF 71) · 't Hooft, ch. 3, eq. (3.12), p. 12 (PDF 13) · Schutz, Exercise 5.21, eq. (5.96), p. 140 (PDF 158) · acceleration checked by script*

> [!definition] Definition §C10.2.1: Rindler Coordinates and the Rindler Wedge
> *Structure.* The light cone of the origin cuts the $(t, x)$ plane of Minkowski spacetime into four open wedges,
>
> $$
> R = \{x > |t|\}, \qquad F = \{t > |x|\}, \qquad L = \{x < -|t|\}, \qquad P = \{t < -|x|\} .
> $$
>
> The **Rindler wedge** is $R$ (in four dimensions $R\times\mathbf R^2$, all $y$, $z$). **Rindler coordinates** are the chart ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]])
>
> $$
> (\eta, \xi) : R \to \mathbf R\times(0, \infty), \qquad \xi = \sqrt{x^2 - t^2}, \quad \eta = \operatorname{artanh}\frac tx, \qquad\text{inverse}\quad t = \xi\sinh\eta, \quad x = \xi\cosh\eta .
> $$
>
> On $R$, $x^2 - t^2 > 0$ and $|t/x| < 1$, so both functions are defined and smooth; conversely, for $\xi > 0$ the inverse gives $x = \xi\cosh\eta > \xi|\sinh\eta| = |t|$, a point of $R$. The two maps are smooth and inverse to each other, so $(\eta, \xi)$ is a chart covering all of $R$ and nothing else. In light-cone coordinates
>
> $$
> u = t - x = -\xi e^{-\eta}, \qquad v = t + x = \xi e^{\eta}, \qquad R = \{u < 0 < v\}, \qquad uv = -\xi^2, \quad \frac uv = -e^{-2\eta} .
> $$
>
> The curves $\xi = \xi_0$ are the observers of [[#^mod-c10-2-1|Model §C10.2.1]], with $\eta = \tau/\xi_0$; the curves $\eta = \text{const}$ are the half-lines $t = x\tanh\eta$ from the origin.
>
> The boundary of $R$ consists of two null half-lines and their common point: the **future Rindler horizon** $H^+ = \{u = 0,\ v > 0\}$ (the half-line $t = x > 0$), the **past Rindler horizon** $H^- = \{v = 0,\ u < 0\}$ (the half-line $t = -x < 0$), and the **bifurcation point** $t = x = 0$ (in four dimensions: null half-hyperplanes meeting in the plane $t = x = 0$). The name "horizon" is earned in [[#^thm-c10-2-3|Theorem §C10.2.3]].
>
> *Other wedges.* The same formulas with $\xi < 0$ cover $L$ (Schutz's remark that the chart "really covers two disjoint quadrants"); $F$ is covered by $t = \xi\cosh\eta$, $x = \xi\sinh\eta$, $\xi > 0$ ([[#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]], part 5).
>
> For a quantum field the boost time $\eta$ defines particles differently from inertial time: the vacuum that all inertial observers share ([[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^thm-c14-1-6|Theorem §C14.1.6]]) entangles the field in $R$ with that in $L$, and restricted to $R$ it is thermal ([[§C14.2 The Unruh Effect#^thm-c14-2-2|Theorem §C14.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.22)–(4.23) and Fig. 10, pp. 70–71 (PDF 71–72) · Schutz, Exercise 5.21(b), p. 140 (PDF 158) · 't Hooft, ch. 3, Fig. 1 and item (iii), p. 12 (PDF 13) · checked by script*

## The Rindler metric is flat spacetime

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.2.2: The Rindler Metric ds² = −ξ²dη² + dξ² Is Flat Spacetime
> On the Rindler wedge, in the chart of [[#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]]:
> 1. **Metric.** The Minkowski metric is
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -\xi^2\,d\eta^2 + d\xi^2\ \ \bigl(+\,dy^2 + dz^2\bigr) ,
> $$
>
>    diagonal: the lines $\eta = \text{const}$ are orthogonal to the observers $\xi = \text{const}$ wherever they cross.
> 2. **Connection and curvature.** The only nonzero Christoffel symbols are $\Gamma^\eta{}_{\eta\xi} = \Gamma^\eta{}_{\xi\eta} = 1/\xi$ and $\Gamma^\xi{}_{\eta\eta} = \xi$, and the Riemann tensor vanishes. So the metric $-\xi^2d\eta^2 + d\xi^2$ on $\mathbf R\times(0, \infty)$, given by itself, is flat ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-5|Theorem §C3.9.5]]).
> 3. **The observers.** On $\xi = \xi_0$, proper time is $\tau = \xi_0\eta$ and the four-acceleration, computed in the chart, is $a = \xi_0^{-1}\,\partial_\xi$, of magnitude $1/\xi_0$: the curves $\xi = \text{const}$ are the observers of [[#^mod-c10-2-1|Model §C10.2.1]]. The line $\eta = \eta_0$ is their common rest space at the instant $\eta_0$, and the proper distance along it between the observers $\xi_1 < \xi_2$ is $\xi_2 - \xi_1$, the same at every $\eta_0$.
> 4. **The boost as Rindler time.** $\partial_\eta = x\,\partial_t + t\,\partial_x$, the boost Killing field of [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^ex-c3-13-1|Example §C3.13.1]]: translating $\eta$ by $\Delta\eta$ is the boost with rapidity $\Delta\eta$. It is timelike on $R$, with $\partial_\eta\cdot\partial_\eta = g_{\eta\eta} = -\xi^2$, and hypersurface-orthogonal, so $R$ is static with respect to it ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-5|Def. §C3.13.5]]). Clocks at rest in $R$ run at rates proportional to $\xi$: $d\tau = \xi\,d\eta$.
> 5. **⚑ Beyond the horizon, $\xi$ is time.** On $F$, the chart $t = \xi\cosh\eta$, $x = \xi\sinh\eta$ ($\xi > 0$) gives $ds^2 = -d\xi^2 + \xi^2d\eta^2$: there $\xi$ is a time coordinate and $\eta$ a space coordinate, and $\partial_\eta$ is spacelike, as $r$ and $\partial_t$ swap roles inside $r = 2M$ ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]).
> 6. **⚑ The degeneracy at $\xi = 0$ is a coordinate singularity of three different places.** $g_{\eta\eta} \to 0$ and $\det g = -\xi^2 \to 0$ as $\xi \to 0$: a singularity of the metric in the chart, removable in the sense of [[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]], part 1, since the inertial chart extends across it. Approached with $v = \xi e^{\eta}$ fixed ($\eta \to +\infty$) it is a point of $H^+$; with $u = -\xi e^{-\eta}$ fixed ($\eta \to -\infty$) a point of $H^-$; at fixed $\eta$, the bifurcation point. The single "surface" $\xi = 0$ of the chart is the two horizons and their crossing, as $r = 2M$ of the Schwarzschild chart is the two horizons and the bifurcation sphere of the Kruskal extension ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]).
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c10-2-1|Model §C10.2.1]]; *applies:* exactly, on $R$ (parts 1–4, 6) and $F$ (part 5).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.3, eq. (4.23) and the proper time $\tau = \xi\eta$, p. 70 (PDF 71) · Schutz, Exercise 5.21(a)–(c), p. 140 (PDF 158) (orthogonality, the metric and its Christoffel symbols) · 't Hooft, ch. 3, eq. > [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (metric). From $t = \xi\sinh\eta$, $x = \xi\cosh\eta$: $dt = \sinh\eta\,d\xi + \xi\cosh\eta\,d\eta$ and $dx = \cosh\eta\,d\xi + \xi\sinh\eta\,d\eta$. Squaring,
>
> $$
> -dt^2 + dx^2 = (\cosh^2\eta - \sinh^2\eta)\,d\xi^2 - \xi^2(\cosh^2\eta - \sinh^2\eta)\,d\eta^2 + 2\xi(\cosh\eta\sinh\eta - \sinh\eta\cosh\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta = d\xi^2 - \xi^2d\eta^2 .
> $$
>
> The cross term cancels, so $g_{\eta\xi} = 0$: the tangent vectors $\partial_\eta$ and $\partial_\xi$ are orthogonal (Schutz 5.21(a)). This is part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (Christoffel symbols). Only $g_{\eta\eta} = -\xi^2$ varies, with $\partial_\xi g_{\eta\eta} = -2\xi$. By the Christoffel formula ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]) with the diagonal inverse $g^{\eta\eta} = -\xi^{-2}$, $g^{\xi\xi} = 1$:
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\xi{}_{\eta\eta} = -\tfrac12 g^{\xi\xi}\partial_\xi g_{\eta\eta} = \xi, \qquad \Gamma^\eta{}_{\eta\xi} = \tfrac12 g^{\eta\eta}\partial_\xi g_{\eta\eta} = \tfrac12\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\xi^2}\Bigr)(-2\xi) = \frac1\xi .
> $$
>
> The other symbols, $\Gamma^\eta{}_{\eta\eta}$, $\Gamma^\eta{}_{\xi\xi}$, $\Gamma^\xi{}_{\xi\xi}$, $\Gamma^\xi{}_{\eta\xi}$, contain only $\partial_\eta g$ or $\partial_\xi g_{\xi\xi}$ and vanish.
>
> **Step 3** (curvature). In two dimensions the Riemann tensor has one independent component. With [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]], keeping only nonzero products ($\Gamma^\xi{}_{\xi\lambda} = 0$ for both $\lambda$):
>
> $$
> R^\xi{}_{\eta\xi\eta} = \partial_\xi\Gamma^\xi{}_{\eta\eta} - \partial_\eta\Gamma^\xi{}_{\xi\eta} + \Gamma^\xi{}_{\xi\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\eta\eta} - \Gamma^\xi{}_{\eta\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\xi\eta} = 1 - 0 + 0 - \Gamma^\xi{}_{\eta\eta}\Gamma^\eta{}_{\xi\eta} = 1 - \xi\cdot\frac1\xi = 0 .
> $$
>
> Adding $dy^2 + dz^2$ adds no Christoffel symbols, so all components vanish in four dimensions as well (script: all 256). This is part 2; it is the computation of [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^ex-c3-9-3|Example §C3.9.3]], part (b), in the shifted variables $\xi = 1/g + x_R$, $\eta = gt_R$.
>
> **Step 4** (observers). On $\xi = \xi_0$, $ds^2 = -\xi_0^2d\eta^2$, so $d\tau = \xi_0\,d\eta$ and $\tau = \xi_0\eta$ (choosing $\tau = 0$ at $\eta = 0$). The four-velocity is $u = \xi_0^{-1}\partial_\eta$, with $g(u, u) = -\xi_0^2/\xi_0^2 = -1$. Its covariant derivative along the curve ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-3|Def. §C3.5.3]]), with constant components:
>
> $$
> a^\xi = \frac{du^\xi}{d\tau} + \Gamma^\xi{}_{\eta\eta}(u^\eta)^2 = 0 + \xi_0\cdot\frac{1}{\xi_0^2} = \frac{1}{\xi_0}, \qquad a^\eta = \frac{du^\eta}{d\tau} + 2\Gamma^\eta{}_{\eta\xi}u^\eta u^\xi = 0 ,
> $$
>
> and $a\cdot a = g_{\xi\xi}(a^\xi)^2 = 1/\xi_0^2$. Along $\eta = \eta_0$, $ds^2 = d\xi^2$, so the proper distance from $\xi_1$ to $\xi_2$ is $\int_{\xi_1}^{\xi_2}d\xi = \xi_2 - \xi_1$; by Step 1 this line is orthogonal to every observer it meets, i.e. it is their simultaneity line. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (Killing field). The components of $\partial_\eta$ in the inertial chart are $(\partial t/\partial\eta, \partial x/\partial\eta) = (\xi\cosh\eta, \xi\sinh\eta) = (x, t)$, so $\partial_\eta = x\,\partial_t + t\,\partial_x$. No metric component depends on $\eta$ and there is no cross term, with $g_{\eta\eta} = -\xi^2 < 0$ on $R$: by [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-6|Theorem §C3.13.6]], part 2 (converse), $\partial_\eta$ is a timelike, hypersurface-orthogonal Killing vector, orthogonal to the slices $\eta = \text{const}$. Its flow shifts $\eta$, which in the inertial chart is the boost of [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^ex-c3-13-1|Example §C3.13.1]] with rapidity $\Delta\eta$. This is part 4.
>
> **Step 6** (region $F$). With $t = \xi\cosh\eta$, $x = \xi\sinh\eta$: $dt = \cosh\eta\,d\xi + \xi\sinh\eta\,d\eta$, $dx = \sinh\eta\,d\xi + \xi\cosh\eta\,d\eta$, and the computation of Step 1 with $\cosh$ and $\sinh$ exchanged gives $-dt^2 + dx^2 = -d\xi^2 + \xi^2d\eta^2$. For $\xi > 0$ these cover $t > |x|$, since $t = \xi\cosh\eta > \xi|\sinh\eta| = |x|$. This is part 5.
>
> **Step 7** (the three limits). From $u = -\xi e^{-\eta}$, $v = \xi e^{\eta}$: at fixed $v > 0$, $\xi = ve^{-\eta} \to 0$ forces $\eta \to +\infty$ and $u = -\xi^2/v \to 0$, a point $(u, v) = (0, v)$ of $H^+$; at fixed $u < 0$ similarly a point $(u, 0)$ of $H^-$; at fixed $\eta$, $u, v \to 0$. The determinant of $\operatorname{diag}(-\xi^2, 1)$ is $-\xi^2$. This is part 6.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Every feature of the metric that looks like gravity, the vanishing $g_{\eta\eta}$, the nonzero $\Gamma$, the clock rates proportional to $\xi$, comes from the choice of observers; the curvature is zero. The one invariant object is the Killing field $\partial_\eta$, and $g_{\eta\eta}$ is its norm.

## The Rindler horizon

The observers of $R$ cannot see everything. Which events can send them a signal? In flat spacetime the answer reduces to the light-cone coordinates, because along any causal curve $u$ and $v$ can only grow.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.2.3: The Rindler Horizon: No Signal from t ≥ x Reaches the Accelerated Observer
> Let $\gamma = \gamma_{\xi_0}$ be any observer of [[#^mod-c10-2-1|Model §C10.2.1]], at fixed $y = z = 0$, in four-dimensional Minkowski spacetime. Write $J^-(\gamma)$ for the set of events that can send a signal to some event of $\gamma$, and $J^+(\gamma)$ for the set of events that some event of $\gamma$ can send a signal to.
> 1. **What the observer can see.** $J^-(\gamma) = \{t < x\}$: an event can signal to $\gamma$ if and only if $u = t - x < 0$. The set does not depend on $\xi_0$, nor on $y$, $z$ of the event.
> 2. **What the observer can influence.** $J^+(\gamma) = \{t > -x\}$, i.e. $v > 0$.
> 3. **Horizons.** The future Rindler horizon $H^+$ lies on the null hyperplane $t = x$, the boundary of $J^-(\gamma)$; the past horizon $H^-$ on $t = -x$, the boundary of $J^+(\gamma)$. The wedge is exactly the region of two-way contact, $R = J^-(\gamma)\cap J^+(\gamma)$. Events of $F$ receive the observer's signals but cannot answer; events of $P$ can signal but never hear back; $L$ has no contact either way.
> 4. **Arrival times diverge at the horizon.** A signal from an event $p$ with $u_p < 0$ reaches $\gamma$ no earlier than the proper time
>
> $$
> \tau_{\text{arr}} = \xi_0\ln\frac{\xi_0}{|u_p|} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \frac{c}{a}\ln\frac{c^2/a}{|ct_p - x_p|}\Bigr),
> $$
>
>    with $x_p$ measured from the pivot, attained by the light ray toward $+x$ when it arrives first. As $p$ approaches the horizon ($u_p \to 0^-$), $\tau_{\text{arr}} \to \infty$.
> 5. **⚑ The horizon belongs to the observer.** For every inertial observer (a timelike geodesic), and for an observer that stops accelerating at any finite proper time, the set of events that can signal to it is all of Minkowski spacetime: no horizon. Which events are hidden depends on the whole future of the worldline.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c10-2-1|Model §C10.2.1]]; *applies:* exactly, for observers that accelerate for all $\tau$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.3, p. 70 (PDF 71) ("The region $x \le t$ is forever hidden … a horizon to these observers"; "observer independent" vs "associated with a special family of observers") and Fig. 10, p. 71 (PDF 72) · 't Hooft, ch. 3, Fig. 1 and item (iii), p. 12 (PDF 13) (past and future horizons; a signal from a point beyond them is never received) · Couzens, GR2, §9.4, Fig. 32, p. 163 (PDF 164) · proved here; the equivalence in part 1 sampled by script*

^thm-c10-2-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (monotone light-cone coordinates). Let $c(\lambda)$ be a future-directed causal curve with tangent $(\dot t, \dot x, \dot y, \dot z)$: $-\dot t^2 + \dot x^2 + \dot y^2 + \dot z^2 \le 0$ and $\dot t > 0$. Then $\dot t \ge \sqrt{\dot x^2 + \dot y^2 + \dot z^2} \ge |\dot x|$, so
>
> $$
> \dot u = \dot t - \dot x \ge 0, \qquad \dot v = \dot t + \dot x \ge 0 .
> $$
>
> Along every signal $u$ and $v$ are non-decreasing.
>
> **Step 2** (when a straight signal exists). Let $p$, $q$ be events with $\Delta u = u_q - u_p$, $\Delta v = v_q - v_p$, $\rho_\perp^2 = (y_q - y_p)^2 + (z_q - z_p)^2$. Since $-\Delta t^2 + \Delta x^2 = -\Delta u\,\Delta v$, the straight segment from $p$ to $q$ is a future-directed causal curve whenever
>
> $$
> \Delta u \ge 0, \qquad \Delta v \ge 0, \qquad \Delta u\,\Delta v \ge \rho_\perp^2, \qquad \Delta u + \Delta v > 0 ,
> $$
>
> because then $-\Delta t^2 + \Delta x^2 + \rho_\perp^2 = -\Delta u\,\Delta v + \rho_\perp^2 \le 0$ and $\Delta t = \frac12(\Delta u + \Delta v) > 0$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 1). On $\gamma$, $u = -\xi_0e^{-\tau/\xi_0} < 0$ and $v = \xi_0e^{\tau/\xi_0}$ ([[#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]]). *Necessity:* if a signal runs from $p$ to a point $q$ of $\gamma$, Step 1 gives $u_p \le u_q < 0$. *Sufficiency:* let $u_p < 0$. As $\tau \to +\infty$, $\Delta u = -\xi_0e^{-\tau/\xi_0} - u_p \to |u_p| > 0$ and $\Delta v = \xi_0e^{\tau/\xi_0} - v_p \to \infty$, so for large $\tau$ all conditions of Step 2 hold, whatever $\rho_\perp$. So $J^-(\gamma) = \{u < 0\}$, independent of $\xi_0$, $y$, $z$.
>
> **Step 4** (part 2). The same argument with the roles of $u$ and $v$ exchanged and $\tau \to -\infty$: a signal from $\gamma$ to $p$ needs $v_p \ge v_q > 0$; conversely, for $v_p > 0$ and $\tau \to -\infty$, $v_p - v_q \to v_p > 0$ and $u_p - u_q \to \infty$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 3). $J^-(\gamma) = \{u < 0\}$ is an open half-space with boundary $\{u = 0\} = \{t = x\}$, which contains $H^+$; likewise $\{v = 0\}$ contains $H^-$. The intersection $\{u < 0 < v\}$ is $R$ ([[#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]]). $F = \{u > 0, v > 0\}$ lies in $J^+(\gamma)$ only, $P = \{u < 0, v < 0\}$ in $J^-(\gamma)$ only, and $L = \{u > 0 > v\}$ in neither.
>
> **Step 6** (part 4). By Step 1, a signal from $p$ reaches $\gamma$ only at an event with $u_\gamma(\tau) \ge u_p$, i.e. $-\xi_0e^{-\tau/\xi_0} \ge u_p = -|u_p|$, i.e. $e^{-\tau/\xi_0} \le |u_p|/\xi_0$, i.e. $\tau \ge \xi_0\ln(\xi_0/|u_p|)$. The light ray from $p$ toward $+x$ keeps $u = u_p$ and meets $\gamma$ exactly at this $\tau$, provided $v$ there, $\xi_0^2/|u_p|$, is at least $v_p$ (otherwise the first arrival is later, constrained by $v$). Restoring units, $\xi_0 = c^2/a$, the time $\tau \to c\tau$ and $u_p = ct_p - x_p$ measured from the pivot.
>
> **Step 7** (part 5). An inertial observer moves on $x = x_1 + \beta(t - t_1)$ with $|\beta| < 1$, so along it $u = (1 - \beta)(t - t_1) + u_1 \to +\infty$ and $v = (1 + \beta)(t - t_1) + v_1 \to +\infty$ as $t \to \infty$. For any $p$, Step 2 then holds for a late enough point of the worldline: every event can signal to it. An observer that follows $\gamma$ up to proper time $\tau_1$ and then moves inertially with the velocity it had, $\beta = \tanh(\tau_1/\xi_0) < 1$, is of this kind after $\tau_1$, and its late points are what Step 3 needed. So the hidden region $\{u \ge 0\}$ exists only because $\gamma$ accelerates for all future time.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The horizon is the boundary of the past of one worldline. Every Rindler observer has the same one, because all their worldlines approach the same null line $u = 0$; an observer whose worldline does not approach a null line has none.

## Near every Schwarzschild horizon: Rindler

[[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^ex-c7-3-2|Example §C7.3.2]] showed that spacetime is locally flat at the bifurcation sphere and that the static observers there look like hyperbolae. The statement below makes this quantitative along the whole horizon region of the exterior: in the right variables the Schwarzschild metric near $r = 2M$ is Rindler times a sphere, with an error that is a power series in one small number.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.2.4: ⚑ Near the Schwarzschild Horizon the Metric Is Rindler × S² with κ = 1/4M
> In the exterior $r > 2M$ of the Schwarzschild metric ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]), $f = 1 - 2M/r$, set
>
> $$
> r = 2M + \frac{\rho^2}{8M}\quad(\rho > 0), \qquad \kappa = \frac{1}{4M}\ \ \Bigl(\text{restored: } \kappa = \frac{c^4}{4GM}\Bigr), \qquad \epsilon = (\kappa\rho)^2 = \frac{\rho^2}{16M^2} .
> $$
>
> 1. **Exact form.** The metric is
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -\frac{\kappa^2\rho^2}{1 + \epsilon}\,dt^2 + (1 + \epsilon)\,d\rho^2 + 4M^2(1 + \epsilon)^2\,d\Omega^2 ,
> $$
>
>    so where $\rho \ll 4M$ ($\epsilon \ll 1$),
>
> $$
> ds^2 = \bigl[-\kappa^2\rho^2\,dt^2 + d\rho^2 + 4M^2\,d\Omega^2\bigr]\bigl(1 + O(\epsilon)\bigr) ,
> $$
>
>    term by term: with $\eta = \kappa t$ and $\xi = \rho$, the $(t, \rho)$ part is the Rindler metric ([[#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]]) and the angular part a fixed round sphere of radius $2M$.
> 2. **$\rho$ is proper distance.** The proper radial distance from the horizon to $r$ is $\int_0^\rho\sqrt{1 + \epsilon'}\,d\rho' = \rho\,(1 + \epsilon/6 + \dots)$.
> 3. **Static observers are Rindler observers.** The static observer at $r$ ([[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay#^def-c6-6-1|Def. §C6.6.1]]) has proper acceleration, exactly,
>
> $$
> a = \frac{M}{r^2\sqrt f} = \frac{1}{\rho}\,(1 + \epsilon)^{-3/2} ,
> $$
>
>    the Rindler value $1/\xi_0$ for $\xi_0 = \rho$, up to $O(\epsilon)$; it diverges at the horizon.
> 4. **⚑ The redshifted acceleration tends to $\kappa$.** $\sqrt f\,a = M/r^2 \to 1/4M = \kappa$ as $r \to 2M$: the static observer's acceleration times its clock-rate factor $\sqrt f$ ([[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay#^thm-c6-6-1|Theorem §C6.6.1]]) is finite at the horizon and equals $\kappa$. This is why $\kappa$ is called the **surface gravity**; its definition from the Killing field is [[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-4|Def. §C10.5.4]].
> 5. **The Rindler horizons are the Kruskal horizons.** The inertial coordinates of the Rindler part, $\hat X = \rho\cosh\kappa t$, $\hat T = \rho\sinh\kappa t$, agree to leading order with the rescaled Kruskal coordinates $\hat X = (4M/\sqrt e)\,X$, $\hat T = (4M/\sqrt e)\,T$ of [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^ex-c7-3-2|Example §C7.3.2]]: exactly, $\hat T/\hat X = \tanh\kappa t$ and $\hat X^2 - \hat T^2 = \rho^2e^{\epsilon}$. So $H^+$, $H^-$ and the bifurcation point of the Rindler part are the horizons $U = 0$, $V = 0$ and the bifurcation sphere, and Schwarzschild time translation by $\Delta t$ is the boost by rapidity $\kappa\Delta t$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], part 4).
>
> The Rindler form needs $\kappa \neq 0$. At the degenerate horizon of an extremal charged black hole, where $\kappa = 0$, the same zoom gives instead a two-dimensional anti-de Sitter space times a sphere, curved in the radial plane ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^ex-c11-3-2|Example §C11.3.2]]).
>
> *Model:* the Schwarzschild exterior; *applies:* parts 1 (first form), 3, 4 and the identities of part 5 exactly; the Rindler reading for $\rho \ll 4M$, with relative errors $O(\epsilon)$; the four-dimensional flat (Rindler $\times\,\mathbf R^2$) reading only in a patch of the sphere small compared with $2M$, where its curvature, of order $1/M^2$, is negligible.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.3, Exercise 4, eqs. (4.24)–(4.26), p. 71 (PDF 72) (the same substitution with $r_h = 2M$: $r = r_h + \rho^2/4r_h$) · Couzens, GR2, §8.1, eqs. (8.1)–(8.6), pp. 148–149 (PDF 149–150) (the same limit with $4r_s\delta = \rho^2$ and $X = \rho\cosh(t/2r_s)$, $T = \rho\sinh(t/2r_s)$) · 't Hooft, ch. 11, p. 50 (PDF 51) (the Rindler comparison) · exact forms and the limit checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** ($f$ in terms of $\rho$). With $r - 2M = \rho^2/8M$ and $r = (16M^2 + \rho^2)/8M$,
>
> $$
> f = \frac{r - 2M}{r} = \frac{\rho^2/8M}{(16M^2 + \rho^2)/8M} = \frac{\rho^2}{16M^2 + \rho^2} = \frac{\rho^2}{16M^2}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + \rho^2/16M^2} = \frac{\kappa^2\rho^2}{1 + \epsilon} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the radial term). $dr = (\rho/4M)\,d\rho$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{dr^2}{f} = \frac{\rho^2}{16M^2}\,d\rho^2\cdot\frac{16M^2 + \rho^2}{\rho^2} = \Bigl(1 + \frac{\rho^2}{16M^2}\Bigr)d\rho^2 = (1 + \epsilon)\,d\rho^2 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the sphere). $r = 2M + \rho^2/8M = 2M(1 + \rho^2/16M^2) = 2M(1 + \epsilon)$, so $r^2 = 4M^2(1 + \epsilon)^2$. Substituting Steps 1–3 into $-f\,dt^2 + dr^2/f + r^2d\Omega^2$ gives part 1; each coefficient is its $\epsilon = 0$ value times $(1 + \epsilon)^{-1}$, $(1 + \epsilon)$ or $(1 + \epsilon)^2$, i.e. $1 + O(\epsilon)$.
>
> **Step 4** (part 2). The proper length of the radial line from $r = 2M$ ($\rho = 0$) at fixed $t$ is $\int\sqrt{g_{\rho\rho}}\,d\rho = \int_0^\rho(1 + \rho'^2/16M^2)^{1/2}d\rho'$; expanding the root, $\int_0^\rho(1 + \rho'^2/32M^2 + \dots)\,d\rho' = \rho + \rho^3/96M^2 + \dots = \rho(1 + \epsilon/6 + \dots)$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 3). The static observer has $u = f^{-1/2}\partial_t$ with constant components. By [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-1|Theorem §C6.2.1]] with $e^{2A} = f$, $e^{2B} = 1/f$: $\Gamma^r{}_{tt} = e^{2(A - B)}A' = f^2\cdot f'/2f = \frac12ff'$, and $f' = 2M/r^2$. Then ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-3|Def. §C3.5.3]])
>
> $$
> a^r = \Gamma^r{}_{tt}(u^t)^2 = \tfrac12ff'\cdot\frac1f = \frac{M}{r^2}, \qquad a = \sqrt{g_{rr}}\,a^r = \frac{1}{\sqrt f}\,\frac{M}{r^2} ,
> $$
>
> the other components vanishing ($\Gamma^t{}_{tt} = \Gamma^\theta{}_{tt} = \Gamma^\varphi{}_{tt} = 0$). In terms of $\rho$, with $M/r^2 = 1/(4M(1 + \epsilon)^2)$ (Step 3) and $1/\sqrt f = \sqrt{1 + \epsilon}/(\kappa\rho) = 4M\sqrt{1 + \epsilon}/\rho$ (Step 1),
>
> $$
> a = \frac{1}{4M(1 + \epsilon)^2}\cdot\frac{4M\sqrt{1 + \epsilon}}{\rho} = \frac1\rho\,(1 + \epsilon)^{-3/2} .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (part 4). $\sqrt f\,a = M/r^2$ by Step 5, a smooth function of $r$, equal to $M/4M^2 = 1/4M$ at $r = 2M$.
>
> **Step 7** (part 5). The Kruskal coordinates of region I satisfy $X^2 - T^2 = (r/2M - 1)e^{r/2M}$ and $T/X = \tanh(t/4M)$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], parts 2–3). With $r/2M - 1 = \rho^2/16M^2 = \epsilon$ and $e^{r/2M} = e^{1 + \epsilon}$,
>
> $$
> \hat X^2 - \hat T^2 = \frac{16M^2}{e}\cdot\frac{\rho^2}{16M^2}\cdot e\,e^{\epsilon} = \rho^2e^{\epsilon}, \qquad \frac{\hat T}{\hat X} = \tanh\frac{t}{4M} = \tanh\kappa t .
> $$
>
> So $\hat X = \rho\,e^{\epsilon/2}\cosh\kappa t$, $\hat T = \rho\,e^{\epsilon/2}\sinh\kappa t$: the Rindler map of [[#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]] with $\xi = \rho e^{\epsilon/2} = \rho(1 + O(\epsilon))$ and $\eta = \kappa t$. The horizons $\hat T = \pm\hat X$ of the Rindler part are $\rho \to 0$ with $t \to \pm\infty$, i.e. $U = 0$ and $V = 0$, and $\rho \to 0$ at fixed $t$ is $U = V = 0$ (as in [[#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]], part 6). A shift $t \to t + \Delta t$ is $\eta \to \eta + \kappa\Delta t$, a boost of $(\hat T, \hat X)$ with rapidity $\kappa\Delta t$ ([[#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]], part 4).
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Nothing was expanded except at the end: the near-horizon geometry is Rindler $\times\,S^2$ up to factors $(1 + \epsilon)^n$, with $\epsilon = (\rho/4M)^2$. The number $1/4M$ enters three times, as the coefficient that turns $t$ into Rindler time, as the limit of the redshifted acceleration, and as the boost rate of Schwarzschild time in the Kruskal plane; that it is one number is the content of the name "surface gravity".

## What the Rindler horizon teaches

> [!remark] Remark: Horizons are relative to observers; black-hole horizons are not
> The Rindler horizon is the boundary of the region that can signal to one family of worldlines ([[#^thm-c10-2-3|Theorem §C10.2.3]]). Move the pivot and the horizon moves; take an inertial observer, or let the rocket's engine stop, and it is gone (part 5 there). Minkowski spacetime itself has no horizon: every event can send light to arbitrarily large distances.
>
> The Schwarzschild horizon was defined differently ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]]): as the boundary of the events that cannot signal to large $r$ at all, by any route. That set is fixed by the spacetime, and every observer who stays outside agrees on it. The coordinate-free form of "large $r$" is future null infinity ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-3|Def. §C10.1.3]]), and the black-hole region is the complement of the past of $\mathscr I^+$ ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^def-c10-7-2|Def. §C10.7.2]]); for Rindler that past is all of Minkowski spacetime, so there is a horizon but no black hole ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^ex-c10-7-1|Example §C10.7.1]]).
>
> Locally, however, the two cannot be told apart. [[#^thm-c10-2-4|Theorem §C10.2.4]] shows that near $r = 2M$ the static observers are Rindler observers and the black-hole horizon is their Rindler horizon. No experiment in a laboratory small compared with $M$ near the horizon distinguishes "hovering above a black hole" from "accelerating in empty space", apart from tidal effects of relative size $\epsilon$ and curvature of order $1/M^2$, which vanish in Rindler spacetime ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^ex-c7-3-2|Example §C7.3.2]], Step 4); the difference lies in the whole future of the spacetime, which is why the definition of a black hole in [[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons|§C10.7]] refers to $\mathscr I^+$. What the two horizons do share is local: each is a null surface on which a Killing field ($\partial_\eta$, $\partial_t$) becomes null, with $g_{\eta\eta} = 0$ and $g_{tt} = 0$ as the chart-dependent shadow of that fact. This shared structure is the Killing horizon of [[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-3|Def. §C10.5.3]], and its surface gravity is $1/\xi_0$ for the Rindler horizon when $\partial_\eta$ is normalised to unit length on the observer $\xi_0$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^ex-c10-5-2|Example §C10.5.2]]), $1/4M$ for Schwarzschild.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C10.2.1: How Far behind the Accelerated Rocket Is the Horizon
> **(a) The distance.** At the observer's proper time $\tau_0$ its rest space is the line $\eta = \eta_0 = \tau_0/\xi_0$ ([[#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]], part 3), which meets the horizon $t = x$ only at the bifurcation point $\xi = 0$, at proper distance $\int_0^{\xi_0}d\xi = \xi_0$. In the momentary rest frame, obtained by the boost with rapidity $\eta_0$, which maps the null line $u = 0$ to itself ($u \mapsto e^{-\eta_0}u$, [[#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]]), the horizon is the light ray $t' = x'$ through a point at distance $\xi_0$ behind the observer. So a rocket with proper acceleration $a$ always has its horizon
>
> $$
> d = \frac1a \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } d = \frac{c^2}{a}\Bigr)
> $$
>
> behind it, receding at the speed of light: every light ray that starts farther back than $d$ chases it forever.
>
> **(b) One g.** For $a = g = 9.80665\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$: $c^2/g = 9.165\times10^{15}\ \mathrm m = 0.9687$ light-years, and the matching time is $c/g = 0.9687$ years $= 353.8$ days. The rocket of [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^ex-b3-3-2|Example §B3.3.2]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) starts from rest at the Earth, $x = 0$, so its pivot is at $x = -c^2/g$ and its horizon is $ct = x + c^2/g$ ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^rem-b3-3-4|§B3.3, Remark: A horizon, and where this leads]]). At the Earth that line is reached at $t = c/g$: a radio message sent from the Earth $353.8$ days or more after launch never reaches a rocket that keeps accelerating. Earlier messages do arrive, late: with $u_p = ct_e - c^2/g$ measured from the pivot, [[#^thm-c10-2-3|Theorem §C10.2.3]], part 4 (the light ray toward the rocket arrives first here, as $\xi_0^2/|u_p| \ge v_p$ holds), gives the rocket's clock reading at reception
>
> $$
> \tau = \frac cg\ln\frac{1}{1 - gt_e/c} : \qquad t_e = 0.5\ \mathrm{yr} \;\Rightarrow\; \tau = 0.703\ \mathrm{yr}, \qquad t_e = 0.9\ \mathrm{yr} \;\Rightarrow\; \tau = 2.563\ \mathrm{yr} .
> $$
>
> **(c) Where the horizon is close.** A horizon $1\ \mathrm m$ behind needs $a = c^2/(1\ \mathrm m) = 8.988\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} = 9.165\times10^{15}\,g$. That is the acceleration of a static observer $1\ \mathrm m$ (proper distance) above the horizon of a $10\,M_\odot$ black hole: there $4GM/c^2 = 59.07\ \mathrm{km}$, $\epsilon = (\rho/4M)^2 = 2.87\times10^{-10}$, and [[#^thm-c10-2-4|Theorem §C10.2.4]], part 3, gives $a = (c^2/\rho)(1 + \epsilon)^{-3/2}$, equal to $c^2/\rho$ within $4.3\times10^{-10}$. The static observer's horizon is $1\ \mathrm m$ behind it, as for a rocket in empty space.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.3 (the horizon of the accelerated observer), p. 70 (PDF 71) · worked here; numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C10.2.2: The Rindler Wedge in the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space
> *Setup.* Two-dimensional Minkowski spacetime compactified with $u = \tan\tilde u$, $v = \tan\tilde v$, $|\tilde u|, |\tilde v| < \pi/2$ (Couzens (4.5); [[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^thm-c10-1-3|Theorem §C10.1.3]]), drawn with $\tilde v - \tilde u$ across and $\tilde v + \tilde u$ up, so that the diagram is a diamond with $i^\pm$ at the top and bottom, $i^0$ at the sides and $\mathscr I^\pm$ as its four edges ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-3|Def. §C10.1.3]]).
>
> **Step 1** (the wedge). $R = \{u < 0 < v\}$ ([[#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]]) becomes $-\pi/2 < \tilde u < 0 < \tilde v < \pi/2$: a square whose corners are the bifurcation point $(\tilde u, \tilde v) = (0, 0)$, the right spatial infinity $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$, the point $(0, \pi/2)$ where $H^+$ meets $\mathscr I^+$, and the point $(-\pi/2, 0)$ where $H^-$ meets $\mathscr I^-$. Two of its edges, $H^\pm$, lie inside the diagram; the other two are halves of the right $\mathscr I^+$ and $\mathscr I^-$.
>
> **Step 2** (the observers). $uv = -\xi_0^2$ becomes $\tan\tilde u\,\tan\tilde v = -\xi_0^2$. Along the observer, $u = -\xi_0e^{-\eta} \to 0^-$ and $v = \xi_0e^{\eta} \to +\infty$ as $\eta \to +\infty$, so $(\tilde u, \tilde v) \to (0, \pi/2)$; as $\eta \to -\infty$, $(\tilde u, \tilde v) \to (-\pi/2, 0)$. Every Rindler observer, whatever its acceleration, begins at the same point of $\mathscr I^-$ and ends at the same point of $\mathscr I^+$, Couzens' "from past null infinity to future null infinity". An inertial observer instead runs from $i^-$ to $i^+$ ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^ex-c10-1-1|Example §C10.1.1]]); that the Rindler worldlines end on $\mathscr I^+$, at a point that is also an end of $u = 0$, is the picture of [[#^thm-c10-2-3|Theorem §C10.2.3]], part 1. (For $\xi_0 = 1$, $\tan\tilde u\tan\tilde v = -1$ means $\tilde v = \tilde u + \pi/2$, a vertical segment; this depends on the length unit fixed by the tangent map and has no invariant meaning.)
>
> **Step 3** (the simultaneity lines). $u/v = -e^{-2\eta}$ becomes $\tan\tilde u = -e^{-2\eta}\tan\tilde v$: curves from the bifurcation point ($\xi \to 0$) to the right $i^0$ ($\xi \to \infty$).
>
> **Step 4** (the wedge on its own is a whole diamond). Put $\xi = e^\zeta$, $\zeta \in \mathbf R$. Then, by [[#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]], part 1, with $d\xi = e^\zeta d\zeta$,
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -e^{2\zeta}d\eta^2 + e^{2\zeta}d\zeta^2 = e^{2\zeta}\bigl(-d\eta^2 + d\zeta^2\bigr), \qquad (\eta, \zeta) \in \mathbf R^2 ,
> $$
>
> conformal ([[§C10.1 Conformal Compactification and the Penrose Diagram of Minkowski Space#^def-c10-1-1|Def. §C10.1.1]]) to all of two-dimensional Minkowski spacetime (Couzens' (9.66), (9.70) with $a = 1$). The coordinate $\zeta = \ln\xi$ plays the role of the tortoise coordinate $r_\ast$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]): it runs to $-\infty$ at the horizon. With $u_R = \eta - \zeta$, $v_R = \eta + \zeta$, the relations $u = -\xi e^{-\eta} = -e^{-u_R}$ and $v = e^{v_R}$ show what the wedge, drawn by itself, calls infinity. Its "left future null infinity" $u_R \to +\infty$ is $u \to 0^-$, the horizon $H^+$; its "left past null infinity" $v_R \to -\infty$ is $H^-$; its "left spatial infinity" $\zeta \to -\infty$ is the bifurcation point. Light moving toward the horizon reaches what the wedge calls infinity after a finite affine parameter of Minkowski spacetime. This is the reason Rindler spacetime is geodesically incomplete but extendible ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^thm-c10-4-2|Theorem §C10.4.2]]).
>
> *Result.* Embedded in Minkowski's diagram the wedge is the right-hand square; on its own it is a full diamond whose left edges are at a finite place. A Penrose diagram shows where a spacetime's infinities are only when the spacetime is inextendible.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §4.1.3, pp. 70–71 (PDF 71–72) ("the Penrose diagram is just a piece of figure 8"; the observers travel "from past null infinity to future null infinity"); §9.4, eqs. (9.66)–(9.70), pp. 162–163 (PDF 163–164) · worked here; limits and the conformal form checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C10.2.3: A Lamp That Crosses the Horizon Fades Exponentially
> *Question.* The observer $\xi_0$ releases a lamp at $\tau = 0$; the lamp stays at rest at $x = \xi_0$ (it is inertial) and emits light of proper frequency $\nu_E$ toward the observer. What does the observer receive?
>
> **Step 1** (emission and reception). The lamp's proper time is $t_e$. Its light toward $+x$ keeps $u = t_e - \xi_0 < 0$ and reaches the observer at proper time ([[#^thm-c10-2-3|Theorem §C10.2.3]], part 4)
>
> $$
> \tau = \xi_0\ln\frac{\xi_0}{\xi_0 - t_e}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad t_e = \xi_0\bigl(1 - e^{-\tau/\xi_0}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The ray does arrive first: at reception $v = \xi_0^2/(\xi_0 - t_e)$, and $\xi_0^2/(\xi_0 - t_e) - (\xi_0 + t_e) = t_e^2/(\xi_0 - t_e) \ge 0$, so the condition of part 4 holds.
>
> **Step 2** (frequency). Successive crests leave at $t_e$ and $t_e + dt_e$ and arrive at $\tau$ and $\tau + d\tau$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_R}{\nu_E} = \frac{dt_e}{d\tau} = e^{-\tau/\xi_0} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } e^{-a\tau/c}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> The light is redshifted by a factor $e$ in every proper time $\xi_0 = 1/a$, and photons arrive at the same falling rate.
>
> **Step 3** (the crossing is never seen). The lamp reaches $H^+$ ($t = x = \xi_0$) at its own proper time $t_e = \xi_0$, and $\tau \to \infty$ as $t_e \to \xi_0$. Light emitted after that has $u \ge 0$ and never arrives ([[#^thm-c10-2-3|Theorem §C10.2.3]], part 1). The observer sees the lamp freeze and fade just in front of the horizon, while the lamp itself passes through after a finite time and finds nothing there.
>
> **Step 4** (numbers). Dropped from a rocket at $1\,g$: the lamp crosses after $c/g = 0.9687$ years of its own time; after $10$ years on the rocket's clock its light arrives with $\nu_R/\nu_E = e^{-10/0.9687} = e^{-10.32} = 3.3\times10^{-5}$.
>
> *Result.* This is the fading of a source falling into a black hole seen from far away ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^ex-c7-1-2|Example §C7.1.2]]), where the frequency drops by $e$ every $4M$ of Schwarzschild time. The two agree: with Rindler time $\eta = \tau/\xi_0$ the factor here is $e^{-\eta}$, and near a Schwarzschild horizon $\eta = \kappa t = t/4M$ ([[#^thm-c10-2-4|Theorem §C10.2.4]]), so $e^{-\eta} = e^{-t/4M}$. The exponential fading is a property of the horizon, flat or not; the rate is the surface gravity. Read backwards, the same factor is a blueshift: a wave that reaches a distant observer late from near a black-hole horizon left the collapsing star at exponentially high frequency, the starting point of Hawking's derivation ([[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^thm-c14-3-3|Theorem §C14.3.3]], part 3).
>
> *Source: worked here, after Couzens, GR2, §4.1.3 (events beyond $x = t$ are hidden), p. 70 (PDF 71) and Fig. 10 · 't Hooft, ch. 3, Fig. 1, p. 12 (PDF 13) · numbers checked by script*
