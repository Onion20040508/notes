The postulates force observers in relative motion to disagree about simultaneity ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^thm-a1-3-4|Theorem §A1.3.4]]). The first measurable consequence is that they also disagree about how much time passes between two events: a clock that moves relative to an inertial observer is found to run slow. This section derives the effect from a light clock and from the Lorentz transformation, and checks it against cosmic-ray muons. Frames are in the standard configuration ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^def-a1-4-1|Def. §A1.4.1]]): $S'$ moves with velocity $v$ along the $+x$ axis of $S$, and $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^def-a1-4-2|Def. §A1.4.2]]).

## Proper time

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.1: Proper Time Interval
> The **proper time interval** $\Delta\tau$ between two events is the time between them measured in the inertial frame in which both occur at the same place. It is read off a single clock that is present at both events; the frame of that clock is the **rest frame** of the process.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.3 · Tipler §1-4*

^def-a2-1-1

> [!model] Model §A2.1.1: Light Clock
> A **light clock** is a pulse of light bouncing between two parallel mirrors a fixed distance $D$ apart; one tick is a round trip. Assumptions:
> - the clock is at rest in an inertial frame, with its mirrors perpendicular to the $x$ axis (the light travels transverse to the relative motion);
> - the pulse travels at $c$ in every inertial frame (postulate 2, [[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-2|Principle §A1.3.2]]);
> - mirrors and pulse are ideal: no delay at reflection, no spreading of the beam.
>
> In its rest frame one tick lasts $\Delta\tau = 2D/c$, a proper time interval: emission and return happen at the same mirror.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.3 · Tipler §1-4 · Morin §1.3.2*

^mod-a2-1-1

## Time dilation

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.1.2: Time Dilation
> Let two events be separated by the proper time interval $\Delta\tau$ (they occur at the same place in $S'$). In an inertial frame $S$ relative to which $S'$ moves with speed $v$, the time between the same two events is
>
> $$
> \Delta t = \frac{\Delta\tau}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = \gamma\,\Delta\tau \;\ge\; \Delta\tau ,
> $$
>
> with equality only for $v = 0$. A clock moving relative to an inertial observer runs slow by the factor $1/\gamma$; the proper time is the shortest time between the two events measured in any inertial frame.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.3, eqs. (5.1)–(5.2) · Tipler §1-4, eq. (1-26)*

^thm-a2-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> **Light clock.** In $S'$ a tick lasts $\Delta\tau = 2D/c$ ([[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^mod-a2-1-1|Model §A2.1.1]]). In $S$ the clock moves a distance $v\Delta t$ during the tick, so the pulse runs along two slanted legs. By postulate 2 it still travels at $c$, so each leg has length $c\Delta t/2$; the mirror separation, transverse to the motion, is still $D$ ([[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]]). Pythagoras on one leg:
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{c\,\Delta t}{2}\Bigr)^2 = D^2 + \Bigl(\frac{v\,\Delta t}{2}\Bigr)^2
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (c^2 - v^2)\,\Delta t^2 = (2D)^2 = c^2\,\Delta\tau^2
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta t = \frac{\Delta\tau}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} .
> $$
>
> **Lorentz transformation.** The time transformation of [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]], applied to differences, is $\Delta t = \gamma\,(\Delta t' + v\,\Delta x'/c^2)$. For two events at the same place in $S'$, $\Delta x' = 0$ and $\Delta t' = \Delta\tau$, so $\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta\tau$.
>
> **Interval.** The quantity $\Delta\tau^2 = \Delta t^2 - \Delta x^2/c^2$ ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]) is the same in every frame ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]). In $S'$ it is $\Delta\tau^2$ itself ($\Delta x' = 0$); in $S$ the events are $\Delta x = v\,\Delta t$ apart, so $\Delta\tau^2 = \Delta t^2\,(1 - v^2/c^2)$.
>
> Since $0 \le v < c$, $\gamma \ge 1$, with $\gamma = 1$ only at $v = 0$.

> [!remark] Remark: Every clock, not only light clocks
> The light clock is only a convenient way to compute. Put any other clock, a quartz watch, a decaying particle, a beating heart, at rest beside the light clock in $S'$. If it kept a different rate from the light clock, an observer in $S'$ could tell from the mismatch that $S'$ moves, against the principle of relativity ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-1|Principle §A1.3.1]]). So every process at rest in $S'$ is slowed by the same factor in $S$: time dilation is a property of time, not of clocks, and it is not an error of measurement.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.3 · Morin §1.3.2*

^rem-a2-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Each finds the other's clocks slow
> Time dilation is reciprocal: $S$ finds the clocks of $S'$ slow, and $S'$ finds the clocks of $S$ slow by the same factor, since $\gamma$ depends only on $v^2$. This is no contradiction, because the two statements concern different pairs of events. "$\Delta t = \gamma\Delta\tau$ for ticks of an $S'$ clock" uses events at one place in $S'$; the reverse statement uses ticks of an $S$ clock, which are at one place in $S$ and at different places in $S'$. To compare one $S'$ clock with $S$ time, $S$ needs two of its own clocks, at the two places where the $S'$ clock was, synchronised in $S$, and $S'$ does not regard them as synchronised. The asymmetry is in which frame the events share a location; it disappears only when the same two clocks are brought together twice, and then the answer is unambiguous ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes]]).
>
> *Source: Morin §1.3.2 · UP3 §5.3*

^rem-a2-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Elapsed time on one clock
> - Time dilation relates *elapsed* times, read on a *single* moving clock, to the times of the observer's frame. Taking the reading of one moving clock, then later the reading of a different clock of the same moving frame, and multiplying the difference by $\gamma$ is wrong: those clocks are not synchronised in the observer's frame.
> - Decide first which time is proper (the one measured where both events happen at the same place), then put $\gamma > 1$ on the side that must be larger. The dilated time is always the longer one.
> - "Measured in $S$" means after correcting for the travel time of light to the observer. What an observer *sees* includes that travel time and is described by the Doppler effect ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light]]), which can make an approaching clock look fast.
>
> *Source: Morin §1.3.2 · UP3 §5.3*

^cau-a2-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Small speeds
> For $v \ll c$ the binomial expansion ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) gives
>
> $$
> \gamma = \Bigl(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\Bigr)^{-1/2} \approx 1 + \frac{v^2}{2c^2}, \qquad \Delta t - \Delta\tau \approx \frac{v^2}{2c^2}\,\Delta\tau .
> $$
>
> The effect is second order in $v/c$. It is invisible in daily life but measurable with atomic clocks: in 1971 Hafele and Keating flew cesium clocks around the world on airliners, and in 1975 Alley flew clocks for 15 hours at about $140\ \mathrm{m/s}$ over Chesapeake Bay; after removing the gravitational effect of general relativity (Relativity level C, Chapter C2, planned), the flown clocks lost $5.6\ \mathrm{ns}$ to motion, against a predicted $5.7\ \mathrm{ns}$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.3 · Tipler §1-4*

^rem-a2-1-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.1.1: When Does Time Dilation Matter?
> (a) An experimental hypersonic vehicle flies at $v = 5830\ \mathrm{m/s}$ ($21\,000\ \mathrm{km/h}$). Its clock ticks off $\Delta\tau = 1\ \mathrm s$. With $v/c = 1.94 \times 10^{-5}$,
>
> $$
> \Delta t - \Delta\tau \approx \frac{v^2}{2c^2}\,\Delta\tau = \tfrac12\,(1.94 \times 10^{-5})^2 (1\ \mathrm s) = 1.89 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm s .
> $$
>
> (b) For a 1 % effect, $\Delta\tau/\Delta t = 1/1.01$, so $v/c = \sqrt{1 - (\Delta\tau/\Delta t)^2} = \sqrt{1 - (1/1.01)^2} = 0.14$.
>
> Even the fastest vehicles are far from relativistic; time dilation becomes a percent effect only near $0.1c$, and dominant only close to $c$ (at $v = 0.950c$, $\gamma = 3.20$).
>
> *Source: UP3 Examples 5.1–5.3*

^ex-a2-1-1

> [!example] Example §A2.1.2: Cosmic-Ray Muons Reach the Ground
> Muons made by cosmic rays high in the atmosphere decay with mean lifetime $\tau = 2.20\ \mu\mathrm s$ in their rest frame: of $N_0$ muons, $N = N_0\,e^{-t/\tau}$ survive a proper time $t$ (half-life $\tau\ln 2 = 1.52\ \mu\mathrm s$). Take muons moving straight down at $v = 0.998c$ through $9.0\ \mathrm{km}$ of atmosphere.
>
> In the Earth frame the trip takes $\Delta t = (9.0 \times 10^3\ \mathrm m)/(0.998 \times 3.00 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}) = 30.1\ \mu\mathrm s$. With $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.998^2} = 15.8$, the muon's own clock advances only
>
> $$
> \Delta\tau = \frac{\Delta t}{\gamma} = \frac{30.1\ \mu\mathrm s}{15.8} = 1.90\ \mu\mathrm s, \qquad \frac{N}{N_0} = e^{-1.90/2.20} = 0.42 .
> $$
>
> Without time dilation the survivors would be $e^{-30.1/2.20} = e^{-13.7} \approx 1 \times 10^{-6}$ of those produced. About 42 % reach the ground, as observed: the decay is a clock carried by the muon. In the muon's frame the same result comes from length contraction of the atmosphere ([[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^ex-a2-2-1|Example §A2.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-4 (muon decay), numbers recomputed · UP3 §5.3 and Example 5.3*

^ex-a2-1-2

> [!example] Example §A2.1.3: A Birth Announced by Radio
> A pregnant elephant (gestation $21$ months) is launched toward a distant planet at $v = 0.75c$, and the ship radios home when the calf is born. When does Earth hear the news?
>
> The $21$ months are a proper time of the ship. With $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.75^2} = 1.51$, the birth happens at $\Delta t_1 = \gamma(21\ \text{months}) = 31.7$ months in the Earth frame, when the ship is $v\,\Delta t_1 = 0.75 \times 31.7 = 23.8$ light-months away. The signal needs $23.8$ months more, so it arrives
>
> $$
> \Delta t = 31.7 + 23.8 = 55.5\ \text{months}
> $$
>
> after launch. The $31.7$ months is what time dilation predicts; the $55.5$ months is what Earth *hears*, delay of the signal included. Their ratio to the $21$ months, $55.5/21 = 2.65 = \sqrt{1.75/0.25}$, is the Doppler factor of [[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]].
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 1-8*
