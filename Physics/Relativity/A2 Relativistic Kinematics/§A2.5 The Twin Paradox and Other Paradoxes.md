Time dilation and length contraction are reciprocal: each observer finds the other's clocks slow and rods short. Paradoxes arise when this symmetry is applied where it does not hold. Two are classic: the twins, where one twin returns younger although each seems to see the other's clock run slow, and the pole and the barn, where a pole fits in a short barn in one frame but not in another. Both are resolved by asking which observers stay inertial and by the relativity of simultaneity ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity]]).

## Clocks on arbitrary paths

> [!model] Model §A2.5.1: Ideal Clock (Clock Hypothesis)
> An **ideal clock** moving with velocity $\vec u(t)$ relative to an inertial frame $S$ ticks, at each instant, at the rate of an inertial clock momentarily moving with it; its acceleration has no further effect:
>
> $$
> \frac{d\tau}{dt} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{u(t)^2}{c^2}}, \qquad \tau = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sqrt{1 - \frac{u(t)^2}{c^2}}\;dt .
> $$
>
> The integral is the **proper time** along the clock's worldline between the two times. Assumptions: $S$ is inertial; the clock's mechanism is not disturbed by the acceleration (a real pendulum clock or a fragile watch may be; atomic clocks and decaying particles have passed this test). For a clock at constant speed $\tau$ is the proper time interval of [[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^def-a2-1-1|Def. §A2.1.1]].
>
> *Source: Morin §1.3.2 (remark 5) · UP3 §5.5 (proper time along a world line)*

## The twin paradox

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.5.2: The Stay-at-Home Twin Ages More
> Twin A stays at rest in an inertial frame $S$. Twin B leaves A and later returns, moving with speed $u(t)$, not zero throughout. Between parting and reunion, A ages $\Delta t$ and B ages
>
> $$
> \tau_B = \int\sqrt{1 - \frac{u(t)^2}{c^2}}\;dt \;<\; \Delta t ;
> $$
>
> for a trip at constant speed $u$ out and back, $\tau_B = \Delta t/\gamma_u$. The comparison is made with the twins side by side, so every inertial observer agrees on it.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.3, §5.5 (the twin paradox seen in space-time) · Tipler §1-6 · Morin §1.3.2*

^thm-a2-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A is at rest in $S$, so A's clock simply shows $S$ time: A ages $\Delta t$. B's age is the reading of an ideal clock carried along, the proper time of [[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^mod-a2-5-1|Model §A2.5.1]]. Since $0 \le \sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2} \le 1$, monotonicity of the integral ([[§33 Properties of the Riemann Integral#^thm-33-3|451 Thm. §33.3]]) gives $\tau_B \le \int dt = \Delta t$. Equality would make the continuous non-negative function $1 - \sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}$ have zero integral, hence vanish everywhere ([[§33 Properties of the Riemann Integral#^thm-33-7|451 Thm. §33.7]]), i.e. $u \equiv 0$, contrary to assumption. For constant speed the integrand is the constant $1/\gamma_u$ ([[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^thm-a2-1-2|Theorem §A2.1.2]]).
>
> Both ages are readings of two clocks at one event, the reunion, so they are frame-independent facts. The reversed argument ("in B's frame A moves, so A is younger") is invalid: B is not at rest in one inertial frame during the trip, and the time-dilation formula holds only for an inertial observer. Only A can apply it throughout.

^der-a2-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^mod-a2-5-1|Model §A2.5.1]], [[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^thm-a2-1-2|Theorem §A2.1.2]], [[§33 Properties of the Riemann Integral#^thm-33-3|451 Thm. §33.3]], [[§33 Properties of the Riemann Integral#^thm-33-7|451 Thm. §33.7]]

> [!remark] Remark: Where the symmetry breaks, and what acceleration has to do with it
> - **The asymmetry is real.** B feels the turnaround; A feels nothing. Uniform motion cannot be detected, acceleration can. On a spacetime diagram of $S$, A's worldline is straight and B's is bent; the bent one, though longer on paper, has less proper time ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval]]).
> - **Simultaneity does the work.** During each leg B does see A's clock run slow. But B's lines of simultaneity swing when B changes frame, and in B's bookkeeping A's clock jumps ahead at the turnaround ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^ex-a2-5-1|Example §A2.5.1]]). The jump, not the acceleration itself, accounts for the difference: the turnaround can be made as short as one likes without changing the ages, and twins with identical accelerations can still age differently ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^ex-a2-5-3|Example §A2.5.3]]).
> - **No general relativity needed.** Special relativity handles accelerated objects, provided the analysis is done in an inertial frame. Describing the trip in B's own accelerated frame is possible with the equivalence principle, where A's clock runs fast at the turnaround because A is "higher" in the apparent gravitational field; the answer is the same (Chapter B4, planned).
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-6 · Morin §1.3.2, Appendix C · UP3 §5.3*

^rem-a2-5-1

> [!example] Example §A2.5.1: Ulysses and Homer
> A star is $L = 4.0$ ly from Earth, both at rest in $S$. Ulysses flies there at $0.80c$ ($\gamma = 5/3$), turns around quickly and returns at $0.80c$; Homer stays home.
>
> *Ages.* Each leg takes $L/v = 5.0$ y in $S$, so Homer ages $10.0$ y. Ulysses ages $5.0\ \mathrm y/\gamma = 3.0$ y per leg ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^thm-a2-5-2|Theorem §A2.5.2]]), $6.0$ y in all, and returns $4.0$ y younger.
>
> *Ulysses' bookkeeping.* On the way out he sees Homer's clock slow by $\gamma$: at the turnaround, after his $3.0$ y, he says Homer's clock reads $3.0\ \mathrm y/\gamma = 1.8$ y. Check with the outbound frame's line of simultaneity through the turnaround event ($x = 4.0$ ly, $ct = 5.0$ ly), $ct - 0.8x = \text{const}$: it meets Homer's worldline at $ct = 5.0 - 3.2 = 1.8$ ly. In the return frame the line is $ct + 0.8x = 8.2$ ly, which meets Homer at $8.2$ y. Changing frames thus moves "Homer's now" forward by
>
> $$
> 8.2\ \mathrm y - 1.8\ \mathrm y = 6.4\ \mathrm y = \frac{2Lv}{c^2} .
> $$
>
> On the way back Homer ages another $1.8$ y in Ulysses' view, and $1.8 + 6.4 + 1.8 = 10.0$ y, in agreement with Homer.
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-6 · Morin Appendix C (resolutions 1 and 3)*

^ex-a2-5-1

> [!example] Example §A2.5.2: The Twins Exchange Yearly Signals
> In the trip of [[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^ex-a2-5-1|Example §A2.5.1]], each twin sends a light signal on every birthday of his own clock. While they separate, signals arrive at $\sqrt{(1 - 0.8)/(1 + 0.8)} = 1/3$ of the sending rate; while they approach, at $3$ times the rate ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]]).
> - **Ulysses receives** at $1/3$ per year for his $3$ outbound years ($1$ signal) and at $3$ per year for his $3$ return years ($9$ signals): $10$ signals, Homer's $10$ birthdays.
> - **Homer receives** Ulysses' outbound signals until the light from the turnaround arrives, at $5.0 + 4.0 = 9.0$ y: $3$ signals in $9$ years. In the final year he receives the $3$ return-leg signals: $6$ in all, Ulysses' $6$ birthdays.
>
> Both counts agree. The asymmetry is visible in the light itself: Ulysses switches from slow to fast reception halfway through his trip, at the turnaround, but Homer only after $9$ of his $10$ years.
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 1-14 · Morin Appendix C (resolution 5)*

> [!example] Example §A2.5.3: Identically Accelerated Twins
> Twins Dick and Jane sit in identical rockets on the $x$ axis of $S$, Jane a distance $x_0$ ahead (to the $+x$ side), clocks synchronised in $S$. At the same $S$ time they fire identical engines and reach the same speed $v$ after the same proper time, so their arrivals in the coasting frame $S'$ happen at the same $S$ time and both clocks then read the same. The Lorentz transformation of [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation]] gives, for these two arrival events ($\Delta t = 0$, $\Delta x = x_J - x_D = x_0$),
>
> $$
> t_J' - t_D' = -\frac{\gamma v x_0}{c^2}, \qquad x_J' - x_D' = \gamma x_0 .
> $$
>
> In $S'$ Jane arrived first, by $\gamma v x_0/c^2$, with the same clock reading as Dick, and has aged that much more since: comparing clocks in their common rest frame, Jane is now older. For $v = 0.60c$ ($\gamma = 1.25$) and $x_0 = 1.0$ ly, she is older by $0.75$ y, and they are $1.25$ ly apart. Their accelerations were identical; the difference comes entirely from simultaneity, since clocks synchronised in $S$ are not synchronised in $S'$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Tipler supplement "The case of the identically accelerated twins" (More Chapter 1)*

## The pole and the barn

> [!example] Example §A2.5.4: The Pole and the Barn
> A runner carries a pole of proper length $10\ \mathrm m$ toward a barn of proper length $5\ \mathrm m$ with open doors at both ends, at $v = 0.866c$ ($\gamma = 2$).
>
> *Barn frame $S$.* The pole is contracted to $10\ \mathrm m/2 = 5\ \mathrm m$ ([[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]) and fits exactly. Put the front door at $x = 0$, the rear door at $x = 5\ \mathrm m$, and let the front of the pole pass the front door at $t = 0$. Event 1, front of pole at rear door: $x = 5\ \mathrm m$, $ct = 5\ \mathrm m/0.866 = 5.77\ \mathrm m$. Event 2, back of pole at front door: $x = 0$, $ct = 5.77\ \mathrm m$. They are simultaneous in $S$; a farmer can close both doors at that instant with the pole inside.
>
> *Runner's frame $S'$.* The barn is contracted to $2.5\ \mathrm m$ and the pole is $10\ \mathrm m$: it cannot fit. The Lorentz transformation $ct' = \gamma(ct - \beta x)$ gives
>
> $$
> ct_1' = 2\,(5.77 - 0.866 \times 5)\ \mathrm m = 2.9\ \mathrm m, \qquad ct_2' = 2\,(5.77 - 0)\ \mathrm m = 11.5\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> In $S'$ the front of the pole reaches the rear door well before the back enters the barn. Both frames agree on every event (each door closes and reopens at definite events, and the pole is never struck), but "the pole is entirely inside at one instant" is true in $S$ and false in $S'$, because it is a statement about simultaneity.
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-6 · Morin §1.7 (Problem: pole in barn)*

^ex-a2-5-4

> [!caution] Caution: No rigid bodies
> If the rear of the barn is a solid wall, the farmer still sees the whole pole inside at one instant, and so must the runner, who sees a $2.5\ \mathrm m$ barn. When the wall stops the front of the pole, the back end cannot learn of it before a signal travelling at most at $c$ crosses the pole, $10\ \mathrm m/c = 3.3 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm s$ in the runner's frame; meanwhile the front door, $7.5\ \mathrm m$ away, reaches the back of the pole in $7.5\ \mathrm m/(0.866c) = 2.9 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm s$. The pole is compressed (or breaks) and is briefly inside the barn for both. A perfectly rigid pole would transmit the stop instantly, faster than light; relativity allows only the ideal measuring rods of [[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^mod-a2-2-1|Model §A2.2.1]], not rigid bodies.
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-6*
