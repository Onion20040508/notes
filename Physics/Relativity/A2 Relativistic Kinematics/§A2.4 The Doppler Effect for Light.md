A receding source delivers fewer wave crests per second than it emits, an approaching one more: this is the Doppler effect, familiar for sound ([[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves|WO §A3.5]]). For sound the shift depends on who moves through the air. Light has no medium, so by the principle of relativity its shift can depend only on the relative velocity of source and observer. The exact formula combines the classical crowding or stretching of crests with the time dilation of the source ([[§A2.1 Time Dilation]]).

## The relativistic Doppler effect

> [!definition] Definition §A2.4.1: Source Frequency; Redshift and Blueshift
> - The **source frequency** $f_s$ (proper frequency) and wavelength $\lambda_s = c/f_s$ are those measured in the rest frame of the source; $f_{\text{obs}}$ and $\lambda_{\text{obs}}$ are measured by an observer moving relative to it.
> - The light is **redshifted** if $\lambda_{\text{obs}} > \lambda_s$ (lower frequency) and **blueshifted** if $\lambda_{\text{obs}} < \lambda_s$, whatever the colour of the light.
> - Astronomers measure the shift by the **redshift** $z = (\lambda_{\text{obs}} - \lambda_s)/\lambda_s = (f_s - f_{\text{obs}})/f_{\text{obs}}$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.7 · Tipler §1-5*

^def-a2-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.4.1: Relativistic Doppler Effect
> Let a source and an observer move apart along the line joining them with relative speed $v$, and $\beta = v/c$. Then
>
> $$
> f_{\text{obs}} = f_s\sqrt{\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}}, \qquad \lambda_{\text{obs}} = \lambda_s\sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}} .
> $$
>
> For source and observer approaching each other, replace $\beta$ by $-\beta$ (a blueshift). More generally, any time interval between two signals at emission, measured on the source's clock, arrives stretched by the factor $\sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)}$ on the observer's clock (compressed when approaching). The result depends only on the relative velocity.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.7 · Tipler §1-5, eqs. (1-36)–(1-37)*

^thm-a2-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> **In the observer's frame $S$.** Successive crests (or flashes) leave the source a proper time $T_s = 1/f_s$ apart. Both emissions happen at the source, so in $S$ they are $\gamma T_s$ apart ([[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^thm-a2-1-2|Theorem §A2.1.2]]). Meanwhile the receding source moves $v\gamma T_s$ farther away, so the second crest has that much farther to go at speed $c$ (postulate 2). The crests therefore arrive
>
> $$
> T_{\text{obs}} = \gamma T_s + \frac{v\,\gamma T_s}{c} = \gamma\,(1 + \beta)\,T_s = \frac{1 + \beta}{\sqrt{(1 - \beta)(1 + \beta)}}\,T_s = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}}\;T_s
> $$
>
> apart. Then $f_{\text{obs}} = 1/T_{\text{obs}}$ and, since the light travels at $c$ in $S$, $\lambda_{\text{obs}} = cT_{\text{obs}}$, giving both formulas. For an approaching source the extra distance is $-v\gamma T_s$, which replaces $\beta$ by $-\beta$. Nothing in the argument used the nature of the signal, so any interval between received signals scales the same way.
>
> **Check in the source's frame.** Here the crests are $cT_s$ apart and the observer recedes at $v$, so they reach the observer every $cT_s/(c - v) = T_s/(1 - \beta)$ of source-frame time. The observer's clock runs slow in this frame, so it records $T_{\text{obs}} = T_s/\bigl(\gamma(1 - \beta)\bigr) = T_s\sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)}$, the same. For sound the two calculations would differ (the medium picks out a frame); for light they must agree, by the principle of relativity.

> [!remark] Remark: Light versus sound
> For sound in still air, a source approaching at speed $\beta$ (in units of the sound speed) gives $f_s/(1 - \beta)$, an observer approaching gives $f_s(1 + \beta)$ ([[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^thm-a3-5-3|WO Theorem §A3.5.3]]): two different answers, because the air defines a rest frame. For light there is one answer,
>
> $$
> f_{\text{obs}} = f_s\sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}} = \sqrt{\frac{f_s}{1 - \beta}\cdot f_s(1 + \beta)}
> $$
>
> for approach, the geometric mean of the two classical ones. All three agree to first order, $f_{\text{obs}} \approx f_s(1 + \beta)$, so $|\Delta f|/f_s \approx \beta$ for $v \ll c$; they differ at order $\beta^2$, which is where time dilation enters.
>
> *Source: Morin §2.5.1 · Tipler §1-5*

^rem-a2-4-1

> [!caution] Caution: Seen versus measured
> Time dilation ([[§A2.1 Time Dilation]]) refers to what an observer *measures* after correcting for the travel time of light; the Doppler factor describes what arrives, travel times included. An approaching clock is dilated, yet it is *seen* to run fast, by $\sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)} > 1$, because each tick has a shorter distance to travel than the previous one. Only for a receding clock do both effects slow it down.
>
> *Source: Morin §2.5.1*

^cau-a2-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Transverse Doppler effect
> If, in the observer's frame, the light that reaches the observer leaves the source at angle $\theta$ to the source's velocity, the frequency is $f_{\text{obs}} = f_s/\bigl(\gamma(1 - \beta\cos\theta)\bigr)$, which gives the approaching and receding cases at $\theta = 0$ and $180^\circ$. At $\theta = 90^\circ$ there is still a shift, $f_{\text{obs}} = f_s/\gamma$, a redshift with no classical counterpart: pure time dilation of the source. Kündig (1962) measured it with $14.4\ \mathrm{keV}$ gamma rays from iron nuclei on a centrifuge, using the Mössbauer effect to detect tiny frequency shifts, and found agreement to about 1 % at rim speeds up to about $400\ \mathrm{m/s}$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-5 (Exploring: transverse Doppler effect)*

^rem-a2-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Redshifts of galaxies
> Light from distant galaxies is redshifted: they recede. Solving $1 + z = \sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)}$ for the speed,
>
> $$
> \beta = \frac{(1 + z)^2 - 1}{(1 + z)^2 + 1} ,
> $$
>
> so the exact formula, not $z \approx \beta$, must be used for large $z$; a quasar with $z = 3.78$ recedes at $0.91c$. In cosmology the redshift of distant galaxies is really due to the expansion of space, and the relation of $z$ to distance (Hubble's law) is taken up in [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe|QM §A8.4]].
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-5 (Doppler effect of starlight), eq. (1-38) · UP3 §5.7*

^rem-a2-4-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.4.1: A Receding Quasar
> When it emitted the light now reaching us, a quasar was moving away at $0.80c$. A spectral line emitted at $\lambda_s = 120\ \mathrm{nm}$ (far ultraviolet) arrives at
>
> $$
> \lambda_{\text{obs}} = (120\ \mathrm{nm})\sqrt{\frac{1.80}{0.20}} = (120\ \mathrm{nm})(3.0) = 360\ \mathrm{nm},
> $$
>
> near the visible. If the quasar shines for $10^6$ years of its own time, the first and last light arrive $3.0 \times 10^6$ years apart: the whole history is stretched by the Doppler factor, not by $\gamma = 1.67$. Both numbers carry the same factor $3.0$ because a wave period and a lifetime are both intervals between signals.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Midterm 1 practice*

^ex-a2-4-1

> [!example] Example §A2.4.2: A Radar Speed Gun
> A radar gun sends microwaves of $f_0 = 30\ \mathrm{GHz}$ at a fast vehicle approaching at $\beta = 0.02$ and measures the reflected frequency. In the vehicle's frame the radiation arrives at $f_0\sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)}$, and the vehicle re-emits it at that frequency as a source approaching the gun, so the shift is applied twice:
>
> $$
> f = f_0\,\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta} \approx f_0(1 + 2\beta) .
> $$
>
> Here the shift is $2\beta f_0 = 1.2\ \mathrm{GHz}$. To measure $\beta$ to 1 % ($\delta\beta = 2 \times 10^{-4}$), the gun must resolve $\delta f \approx 2 f_0\,\delta\beta = 2(30\ \mathrm{GHz})(2 \times 10^{-4}) = 12\ \mathrm{MHz}$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 3 solutions*

^ex-a2-4-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The approximation $\sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)} \approx 1 + \beta$ is first-order Taylor expansion ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]).
> - The Doppler factor resolves the twin paradox from the point of view of the light signals the twins exchange ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^ex-a2-5-2|Example §A2.5.2]]).
> - In the photon picture ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect|QM §A1.2]]) the Doppler shift is the change of photon energy $E = hf$ between frames, a special case of the transformation of energy and momentum ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]): for a photon along $+x$, $E' = \gamma(E - vE/c) = E\sqrt{(1 - \beta)/(1 + \beta)}$.
