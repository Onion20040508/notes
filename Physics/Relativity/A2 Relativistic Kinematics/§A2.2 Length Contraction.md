To measure the length of a moving object one must locate both ends at the same instant, and observers in relative motion disagree about which instants are simultaneous. As a result a moving object is shorter along its direction of motion than at rest, by the same factor $\gamma$ that dilates time ([[§A2.1 Time Dilation]]); lengths across the motion are unchanged. Frames are in the standard configuration of [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation]].

## Proper length

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.1: Proper Length; Length of a Moving Object
> - The **proper length** $L_0$ of an object (or the proper distance between two points) is its length measured in the frame in which it (or both points) is at rest.
> - In a frame in which the object moves along its length, its **length** $L$ is the distance between the positions of its two ends *at the same time* in that frame.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.4 · Tipler §1-4*

^def-a2-2-1

> [!model] Model §A2.2.1: Ideal Measuring Rod
> An **ideal measuring rod** has a proper length that is a property of the rod alone: whenever it is at rest in some inertial frame, after any history of motion, it has the same length there. Lengths are measured by laying such rods, at rest in the frame in question, along the object. The model does *not* assume a perfectly rigid body: when one end of a real rod is pushed, the other end cannot respond before a signal (at most at speed $c$) has crossed the rod ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^ex-a2-5-4|Example §A2.5.4]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-4, §1-6 (pole and barn) · Morin §1.3.3*

^mod-a2-2-1

## Length contraction

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.2: Length Contraction
> An object of proper length $L_0$ that moves with speed $v$ along its length has, in the frame in which it moves, the length
>
> $$
> L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma} = L_0\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}} \;\le\; L_0 .
> $$
>
> The same holds for the distance between two points at rest relative to each other: it is greatest (equal to $L_0$) in their common rest frame.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.4, eqs. (5.3)–(5.4) · Tipler §1-4, eq. (1-28)*

^thm-a2-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> **From time dilation.** Let a rod of proper length $L_0$ lie at rest in $S$ along $x$, and let an observer $O'$ ride with $S'$ past it at speed $v$. Both agree on the relative speed $v$. In $S$, $O'$ takes the time $\Delta t = L_0/v$ to pass from one end to the other. These two events (passing the ends) both happen at $O'$, so $O'$'s own clock records the proper time $\Delta\tau = \Delta t/\gamma$ ([[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^thm-a2-1-2|Theorem §A2.1.2]]). In $S'$ the rod moves past $O'$ at speed $v$ during $\Delta\tau$, so its length there is $L = v\,\Delta\tau = v\,\Delta t/\gamma = L_0/\gamma$.
>
> **From the Lorentz transformation.** Now let the rod be at rest in $S'$, ends at $x_1'$ and $x_2'$, $L_0 = x_2' - x_1'$. In $S$, locate the ends at the same time $t_1 = t_2 = t$. The transformation $x' = \gamma(x - vt)$ of [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation]] gives $x_2' - x_1' = \gamma\,(x_2 - x_1)$, because the $vt$ terms cancel. So $L = x_2 - x_1 = L_0/\gamma$.
>
> The second route shows where simultaneity enters: the instants used in $S$ are not simultaneous in $S'$, so $S'$ does not regard the measurement as a valid length measurement at all.

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.3: No Contraction Across the Motion
> Lengths perpendicular to the relative velocity are the same in both frames: $\Delta y = \Delta y'$ and $\Delta z = \Delta z'$ for an object at rest in $S'$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.4 · Tipler §1-4*

^thm-a2-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Give the observers in $S$ and $S'$ identical metre sticks, held along $y$ and $y'$, with their lower ends sliding past each other on the common $x$ axis. A brush on top of the $S'$ stick marks the $S$ stick as they pass. Suppose a moving stick were shorter. Then $S$ would see the brush mark his stick below its top. By the principle of relativity the situation is symmetric, so $S'$ would see the $S$ stick shorter and the brush pass above its top, leaving no mark. Whether there is a paint mark on a stick is one fact that all observers agree on, so the assumption leads to a contradiction; the same argument excludes a longer moving stick. Hence transverse lengths agree, as in the Lorentz transformation $y' = y$, $z' = z$.

^der-a2-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity]], [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation]]

> [!remark] Remark: What contraction is and is not
> - **Reciprocal.** A rod at rest in $S$ is contracted for $S'$, and a rod at rest in $S'$ for $S$. Each measures the other's rod with an end-point pair that the other calls non-simultaneous.
> - **Not a squeeze.** Nothing pushes on the moving rod and it feels no stress; the contraction is a statement about which events count as "the ends now". Before Einstein, Lorentz and FitzGerald had proposed a real physical shrinking of bodies moving through the ether to explain the null result of Michelson and Morley ([[§A1.2 The Michelson–Morley Experiment]]); it gives the same factor, which is why the effect is also called the Lorentz–FitzGerald contraction.
> - **Not what a camera shows.** A photograph records light that left different parts of the object at different times, so a fast object looks distorted in ways that also depend on the light travel times, not only on contraction.
> - **Shapes change.** Only the component along the motion shrinks, so angles and shapes of two-dimensional objects are not preserved ([[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^ex-a2-2-3|Example §A2.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-4 · UP3 §5.4 · Morin §1.3.3*

^rem-a2-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Which length is proper
> The proper length belongs to the frame in which the two end points are at rest, not to the frame of whoever "is moving". For a muon crossing the atmosphere, the distance from production point to ground is proper in the Earth frame; the muon's own length is proper in the muon frame. Mixing the two, or dividing by $\gamma$ where one should multiply, is the usual error; check that the contracted length is the shorter one.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.4*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.2.1: The Muon's View of the Atmosphere
> Return to the muons of [[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^ex-a2-1-2|Example §A2.1.2]], moving down at $0.998c$ ($\gamma = 15.8$) through $9.0\ \mathrm{km}$ of atmosphere. In the muon's rest frame its clock runs normally, lifetime $\tau = 2.20\ \mu\mathrm s$, and it is the atmosphere that rushes up at $0.998c$. The thickness $9.0\ \mathrm{km}$ is a proper length of the Earth frame, so for the muon it is
>
> $$
> L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma} = \frac{9.0\ \mathrm{km}}{15.8} = 0.57\ \mathrm{km}, \qquad \text{crossed in} \quad \frac{0.57 \times 10^3\ \mathrm m}{0.998 \times 3.00 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}} = 1.90\ \mu\mathrm s .
> $$
>
> This is the same proper time as before, so the same 42 % survive. The two frames tell different stories (slow clock in one, short atmosphere in the other) about the same event count.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.4 · Tipler §1-4 (muon decay)*

^ex-a2-2-1

> [!example] Example §A2.2.2: An Electron Bunch in a Linear Collider
> In the Stanford Linear Collider, bunches of electrons with energy $40\ \mathrm{GeV}$ are about $1\ \mathrm{cm}$ long and $10\ \mu\mathrm m$ wide in the laboratory. The electron rest energy is $0.511\ \mathrm{MeV}$, so $\gamma = 1 + K/mc^2 = 1 + (40 \times 10^3)/0.511 = 7.8 \times 10^4$ ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]). In its own rest frame the bunch has its proper length
>
> $$
> L_0 = \gamma L = (7.8 \times 10^4)(1.0 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm m) \approx 780\ \mathrm m ,
> $$
>
> but still $10\ \mu\mathrm m$ width (Theorem §A2.2.3). Conversely, for the whole bunch to fit inside the accelerator at one instant of the bunch's frame, the accelerator, contracted by $\gamma$ in that frame, would need a proper length of at least $\gamma L_0 \approx 6 \times 10^7\ \mathrm m$; the real one is under $1\ \mathrm{km}$, so in the bunch's frame it never contains the bunch.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 4 solutions*

^ex-a2-2-2

> [!example] Example §A2.2.3: A Moving Square
> A square at rest in $S'$ has one side $B'$ at $30^\circ$ to the $x'$ axis and the other side $A'$ at $60^\circ$ on the other side. Seen from $S$, with $v = 0.5c$ ($\gamma = 1.155$), only the $x$-components shrink by $1/\gamma$:
>
> $$
> B = B'\sqrt{\sin^2 30^\circ + \cos^2 30^\circ/\gamma^2} = 0.901\,B', \qquad A = A'\sqrt{\cos^2 30^\circ + \sin^2 30^\circ/\gamma^2} = 0.968\,A' ,
> $$
>
> and the sides now make angles $\arctan(\gamma\tan 30^\circ) = 33.7^\circ$ and $\arctan(\gamma\cot 30^\circ) = 63.4^\circ$ with the $x$ axis. The interior angle is $180^\circ - 33.7^\circ - 63.4^\circ = 82.9^\circ$: in $S$ the object is a parallelogram with unequal sides, not a square.
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 1-10*

^ex-a2-2-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The time-dilation and contraction factors are the same $\gamma$ because both come from the same Lorentz transformation; the invariant interval of [[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval]] gives the proper length of a spacelike pair of events directly.
> - Length contraction is used again in the pole-and-barn paradox ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^ex-a2-5-4|Example §A2.5.4]]), and in Morin's derivation of velocity addition ([[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation]]).
