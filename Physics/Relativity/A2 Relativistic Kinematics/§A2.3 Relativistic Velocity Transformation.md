A light pulse sent forward from a moving car cannot travel at $c + v$: the second postulate says it moves at $c$ in every frame. So velocities cannot simply add as in classical mechanics. Since displacements and time intervals both change between frames, the velocity of a particle changes in a new way, which follows from the Lorentz transformation and reduces to vector addition at low speeds. Notation as in [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^def-a1-4-1|Def. §A1.4.1]]: $S'$ moves with velocity $v$ along $+x$ relative to $S$, $\vec u$ is a particle's velocity in $S$ and $\vec u\,'$ in $S'$, and $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ always refers to the frame speed $v$.

## The transformation

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.3.1: Relativistic Velocity Transformation
> A particle with velocity $\vec u\,' = (u_x', u_y', u_z')$ in $S'$ has, in $S$, the velocity
>
> $$
> u_x = \frac{u_x' + v}{1 + v u_x'/c^2}, \qquad u_y = \frac{u_y'}{\gamma\,(1 + v u_x'/c^2)}, \qquad u_z = \frac{u_z'}{\gamma\,(1 + v u_x'/c^2)} ,
> $$
>
> and conversely
>
> $$
> u_x' = \frac{u_x - v}{1 - v u_x/c^2}, \qquad u_y' = \frac{u_y}{\gamma\,(1 - v u_x/c^2)}, \qquad u_z' = \frac{u_z}{\gamma\,(1 - v u_x/c^2)} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.6 · Tipler §1-3, eqs. (1-22)–(1-23)*

^thm-a2-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Follow the particle for a short time. Its displacement and time increments in the two frames are related by the Lorentz transformation ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]]), which is linear, so it holds for differences and differentials:
>
> $$
> dx = \gamma\,(dx' + v\,dt'), \qquad dy = dy', \qquad dz = dz', \qquad dt = \gamma\,\Bigl(dt' + \frac{v}{c^2}\,dx'\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Divide each spatial increment by $dt$, then divide numerator and denominator by $dt'$ and write $u_x' = dx'/dt'$, etc.:
>
> $$
> u_x = \frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{\gamma\,(dx' + v\,dt')}{\gamma\,(dt' + v\,dx'/c^2)} = \frac{u_x' + v}{1 + v u_x'/c^2}, \qquad
> u_y = \frac{dy'}{\gamma\,(dt' + v\,dx'/c^2)} = \frac{u_y'}{\gamma\,(1 + v u_x'/c^2)} ,
> $$
>
> and the same for $u_z$. The factor $\gamma$ cancels in $u_x$ but not in the transverse components, because only $dx$, not $dy$, is transformed. The inverse follows by exchanging the roles of the frames: $S$ moves with velocity $-v$ relative to $S'$, so replace $v$ by $-v$ and swap primed and unprimed quantities.

^der-a2-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The Galilean limit
> If $v \ll c$ and $u_x' \ll c$, then $v u_x'/c^2 \ll 1$ and $\gamma \approx 1$, and the transformation becomes $u_x = u_x' + v$, $u_y = u_y'$, $u_z = u_z'$: the vector addition of velocities of classical mechanics ([[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|CM Theorem §A2.8.1]]), which follows from the Galilean transformation ([[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]]). Everyday velocity addition is therefore not exact, only accurate to order $v u_x'/c^2$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.6*

^rem-a2-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Transverse velocities change too
> Although $y = y'$, the transverse velocity is not the same in both frames: $u_y = dy/dt$ and $u_y' = dy'/dt'$ divide the same displacement by different time intervals. In particular a velocity that is purely along $y'$ in $S'$ has, in $S$, the smaller $y$-component $u_y'/\gamma$ (since $u_x' = 0$) and an $x$-component $v$. Directions of motion therefore differ between frames as well as speeds.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.6 · Tipler §1-3*

^cau-a2-3-1

## Composing speeds below $c$

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.3.2: Speeds Compose to Less than $c$
> For $|v| < c$ the speeds $u = |\vec u|$ and $u' = |\vec u\,'|$ satisfy
>
> $$
> 1 - \frac{u^2}{c^2} = \frac{\bigl(1 - u'^2/c^2\bigr)\bigl(1 - v^2/c^2\bigr)}{\bigl(1 + v u_x'/c^2\bigr)^2} ,
> \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
> \gamma_u = \gamma\,\gamma_{u'}\Bigl(1 + \frac{v u_x'}{c^2}\Bigr) \;\;(u' < c),
> $$
>
> where $\gamma_u = 1/\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}$ and $\gamma_{u'} = 1/\sqrt{1 - u'^2/c^2}$; in the other direction $\gamma_{u'} = \gamma\,\gamma_u\,(1 - v u_x/c^2)$. Consequently:
> - if $u' < c$, then $u < c$: composing speeds below $c$ never gives $c$ or more;
> - if $u' = c$, then $u = c$: anything that moves at $c$ in one inertial frame moves at $c$, in some direction, in every inertial frame.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.6 · Morin §1.5, §2.2 (identity worked out here from the transformation)*

^thm-a2-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $D = 1 + v u_x'/c^2$, which is positive because $|v u_x'| < c^2$ when $|v| < c$ and $|u_x'| \le c$. By [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]], $D^2 u^2 = D^2(u_x^2 + u_y^2 + u_z^2) = (u_x' + v)^2 + (u_y'^2 + u_z'^2)(1 - v^2/c^2)$, using $1/\gamma^2 = 1 - v^2/c^2$. Hence
>
> $$
> c^2 D^2\Bigl(1 - \frac{u^2}{c^2}\Bigr) = c^2 + 2vu_x' + \frac{v^2u_x'^2}{c^2} - (u_x' + v)^2 - (u_y'^2 + u_z'^2)\Bigl(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> The first four terms give $c^2 - v^2 - u_x'^2 + v^2u_x'^2/c^2 = (c^2 - u_x'^2)(1 - v^2/c^2)$, and adding the last term gives $(c^2 - u'^2)(1 - v^2/c^2)$. Dividing by $c^2D^2$ is the identity; taking the reciprocal square root (both sides positive when $u' < c$) is the form with $\gamma$'s, and the inverse form follows by exchanging frames and $v \to -v$. If $u' < c$ the right side is positive, so $u < c$; if $u' = c$ it is zero, so $u = c$.

> [!remark] Remark: Light from a moving source, and other derivations
> - A ship approaching Earth at $0.500c$ sends a laser signal ahead at $c$ relative to the ship. Then $u = (c + 0.500c)/(1 + 0.500) = c$: the signal reaches Earth at $c$, as the second postulate requires.
> - The longitudinal formula can also be derived without the Lorentz transformation, from the three fundamental effects (time dilation, length contraction and the lag $Lv/c^2$ of a front clock, [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-2|Theorem §A1.4.2]]): time a ball thrown along a moving train in two ways in the ground frame, and equate.
> - The composition is symmetric in $u_x'$ and $v$, and is not the same as subtracting velocities measured in one frame (next caution).
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 5.9 · Morin §1.5*

^rem-a2-3-2

> [!caution] Caution: Closing speed is not a relative velocity
> In Earth's frame, two cosmic-ray protons with velocities $+0.6c$ and $-0.8c$ approach each other: the gap between them shrinks at $1.4c$. That is a rate of change of a distance in one frame, not the velocity of any object, and may exceed $c$. The velocity of proton 2 in the rest frame of proton 1 comes from the transformation with $v = 0.6c$: $u_2' = (-0.8c - 0.6c)/(1 + 0.48) = -0.946c$.
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 1-4 · Morin §1.5*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.3.1: Fastest and Slowest Pion from a Moving Kaon
> A $K^0$ meson at rest decays into a $\pi^+$ and a $\pi^-$, each with speed $0.85c$. Now the kaon moves through the lab at $0.90c$. Take its rest frame as $S'$ ($v = 0.90c$). By the identity of [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]], $\gamma_u$ in the lab grows with $u_x'$, so the extreme lab speeds belong to pions emitted forward and backward along the line of motion, $u_x' = \pm 0.85c$:
>
> $$
> u_{\max} = \frac{0.85c + 0.90c}{1 + (0.85)(0.90)} = \frac{1.75c}{1.765} = 0.991c, \qquad
> u_{\min} = \frac{-0.85c + 0.90c}{1 - (0.85)(0.90)} = \frac{0.05c}{0.235} = 0.213c .
> $$
>
> Even the "backward" pion moves forward in the lab, and the forward one stays below $c$, where Galilean addition would give $1.75c$ and $0.05c$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Midterm 1 practice*

^ex-a2-3-1

> [!example] Example §A2.3.2: A Cosmic-Ray Proton Seen from a Moving Frame
> A proton crosses the lab at $0.85c$ at $50^\circ$ to the $+x$ axis in the $xy$ plane. Find its velocity in $S'$ moving at $v = 0.72c$ along $+x$ ($\gamma = 1.441$).
>
> In the lab, $u_x = 0.85c\cos 50^\circ = 0.546c$ and $u_y = 0.85c\sin 50^\circ = 0.651c$, and $1 - vu_x/c^2 = 1 - (0.72)(0.546) = 0.607$. By [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]],
>
> $$
> u_x' = \frac{0.546c - 0.72c}{0.607} = -0.286c, \qquad u_y' = \frac{0.651c}{(1.441)(0.607)} = 0.745c ,
> $$
>
> so $u' = \sqrt{0.286^2 + 0.745^2}\,c = 0.798c$ at $\theta' = 180^\circ - \arctan(0.745/0.286) = 111^\circ$ from the $+x'$ axis. The $y$-component grew ($0.745c > 0.651c$), although $y' = y$. Check with Theorem §A2.3.2: $1 - 0.798^2 = 0.363$ and $(1 - 0.85^2)(1 - 0.72^2)/0.607^2 = 0.363$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 3 solutions*
