A black hole formed by collapse sends a thermal flux to ℐ⁺ at the Hawking temperature $T_H = \kappa/2\pi$ ([[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^thm-c14-3-5|Theorem §C14.3.5]]), which for Schwarzschild is $1/8\pi M$. That derivation held the geometry fixed. This section asks what the radiation does to the hole, and what it does to the quantum state.

The naive answer keeps $M$ fixed. Then the hole radiates at a constant luminosity $L$, computed below as $L = 1/(15360\pi M^2)$, and after a time $t$ it has emitted the energy $Lt$. At $t = 15360\pi M^3$ the emitted energy equals $M$; for one solar mass that is $6.29\times10^{67}$ years. Later the hole would have given away more energy than it has. Energy is conserved: the mass measured from far away must decrease by what the flux carries off (Hartle; Carroll, p. 417), and because $L \propto 1/M^2$ the decrease accelerates. A second shortcut, dimensional analysis, fails differently. With $G = c = 1$ the only scales are $M$ and $\hbar = l_{\rm P}^2$, so the lifetime must be $\tau \sim M^3/\hbar = (M/m_{\rm P})^3t_{\rm P}$ (Carroll (9.178)). For one solar mass this gives $4.1\times10^{70}$ s, while the black-body estimate of [[#^thm-c14-4-1|Theorem §C14.4.1]] gives $6.6\times10^{74}$ s: dimensions do not fix the factor $5120\pi = 1.6\times10^4$.

The section first integrates the mass loss ([[#^thm-c14-4-1|Theorem §C14.4.1]]): the lifetime is $5120\pi M_0^3$, the hole gets hotter as it shrinks, and the radiation carries away $4/3$ of the entropy the hole loses. It then states when the fixed-background input may be used one instant at a time ([[#^thm-c14-4-2|Theorem §C14.4.2]]): as long as $M \gg m_{\rm P}$. Last it computes the state outside. Each emitted mode is entangled with a partner behind the horizon, and the state outside, obtained by a partial trace, is exactly thermal and mixed ([[#^thm-c14-4-3|Theorem §C14.4.3]]). If the hole disappears, nothing is left to purify it. That is the information paradox ([[#^rem-c14-4-1|Remark: The information paradox]]).

**Setup.** A Schwarzschild hole formed by collapse ([[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^mod-c14-3-2|Model §C14.3.2]]), far from other matter, observed from ℐ⁺ with retarded time $u$, which for a distant static observer is the time $t$. Its emission is described quasi-statically: at each $u$ the hole is the Schwarzschild hole of the current mass $M(u)$ and radiates at $T_H(M(u))$. The rate is a **black-body estimate**: photons only (two polarisations), the horizon area as the emitting area, no greybody factor. Its scaling with $M$ is fixed by $T_H$, while its coefficient is not (Theorem §C14.4.1, part 5). [[#^thm-c14-4-3|Theorem §C14.4.3]] is exact within the free-field model of §C14.2–§C14.3. Numbers were checked by script.

## How fast a hole evaporates

> [!theorem] Theorem §C14.4.1: The Evaporation Rate dM/dt ∝ −1/M² and the Lifetime ∝ M³
> A Schwarzschild hole of mass $M$ radiates photons as a black body of area $A = 16\pi M^2$ at $T_H = 1/8\pi M$ ([[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^thm-c14-3-5|Theorem §C14.3.5]]); the energy carried to ℐ⁺ is lost from $M$.
> 1. **Luminosity.** With $\sigma_{\rm SB} = \pi^2/60$,
>
> $$
> L = \sigma_{\rm SB}AT_H^4 = \frac{1}{15360\pi M^2} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } L = \frac{\hbar c^6}{15360\pi G^2M^2} = 9.0\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm W\,\Bigl(\frac{M_\odot}M\Bigr)^2\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 2. **Mass loss and lifetime.** $\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt = -1/(15360\pi M^2)$. A hole of initial mass $M_0$ has
>
> $$
> M(t) = M_0\Bigl(1 - \frac t\tau\Bigr)^{1/3}, \qquad T_H(t) = T_0\Bigl(1 - \frac t\tau\Bigr)^{-1/3}, \qquad \tau = 5120\pi M_0^3 \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \tau = \frac{5120\pi G^2M_0^3}{\hbar c^4} = 8.41\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm s\,\Bigl(\frac{M_0}{1\ \mathrm{kg}}\Bigr)^3\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 3. ⚑ **The end is a burst.** The time left is set by the present mass alone, $\tau - t = 5120\pi M(t)^3$. The first half of the mass takes $\frac78$ of the lifetime. In the last interval $\Delta t$ the hole releases the mass $M = (\Delta t/5120\pi)^{1/3}$, and $T_H$ and $L$ diverge as $t \to \tau$.
> 4. ⚑ **Entropy.** The radiation carries entropy at $\frac43$ times the rate at which the hole's entropy $S_{\rm BH} = 4\pi M^2$ falls:
>
> $$
> \frac{\mathrm dS_{\rm rad}}{\mathrm dt} = \frac43\,\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm dS_{\rm BH}}{\mathrm dt}\Bigr| = \frac{4L}{3T_H}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\bigl(S_{\rm BH} + S_{\rm rad}\bigr) = \frac{L}{3T_H} > 0 .
> $$
>
>    The generalised second law ([[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^pr-c13-4-3|Principle §C13.4.3]]) holds throughout, and complete evaporation leaves radiation of thermal entropy $\frac43S_{\rm BH}(M_0)$.
> 5. **What fixes the coefficient.** The area is the weak input. In geometric optics a hole absorbs isotropic radiation through its capture cross-section $27\pi M^2$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-5|Theorem §C6.3.5]], part 5), so by Kirchhoff's law it emits like a black sphere of area $108\pi M^2 = \frac{27}4A$. But at the thermal peak the wavelength exceeds the hole (the product $\omega b_c$ of peak frequency and critical impact parameter is $0.58$), so wave effects reduce the emission through the greybody factor ([[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^def-c14-3-1|Def. §C14.3.1]]). The laws $L \propto M^{-2}$ and $\tau \propto M_0^3$ are exact consequences of $T_H \propto 1/M$; the numbers $15360\pi$ and $5120\pi$ are estimates.
>
> *Model:* quasi-static Schwarzschild evaporation ([[#^thm-c14-4-2|Theorem §C14.4.2]]); photons only, horizon area, no greybody factor; *applies:* as an estimate for $M \gg m_{\rm P}$. Other species add to $L$: nearly massless neutrinos and gravitons at every mass, and a species of mass $m$ once $T_H \gtrsim m$ (electrons below $2.07\times10^{13}$ kg).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §9.6, eqs. (9.114)–(9.116) (p. 172, PDF 173) · Hartle, §13.3, eqs. (13.11), (13.15), (13.16), Fig. 13.7 (pp. 291–294, PDF 292–295; $\nu = 1/15360\pi$) · Schutz, §11.5, eqs. > [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the black-body flux). A black surface at temperature $T$ emits, per unit area, $\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4$ with $\sigma_{\rm SB} = \pi^2k^4/60\hbar^3c^2$ ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-6|TH Theorem §B11.1.6]]; the law at level A is [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-7|TH Law §A1.5.7]]). With $k = \hbar = c = 1$, $\sigma_{\rm SB} = \pi^2/60$. It counts the two photon polarisations.
>
> **Step 2** (part 1). Insert $A = 16\pi M^2$ and $T_H = 1/8\pi M$, with $(8\pi M)^4 = 4096\pi^4M^4$:
>
> $$
> L = \frac{\pi^2}{60}\cdot16\pi M^2\cdot\frac1{4096\pi^4M^4} = \frac{16\pi^3}{245760\,\pi^4}\,\frac1{M^2} = \frac1{15360\pi M^2} .
> $$
>
> Restored: $L$ is a power, so $L = \hbar c^6/(15360\pi G^2M^2)$ (the only combination with $\hbar^1$ and $M^{-2}$ that is a power). For $M_\odot = 1.98847\times10^{30}$ kg this is $9.0\times10^{-29}$ W.
>
> **Step 3** (energy balance). The energy reaching ℐ⁺ per unit retarded time is $L$, and the mass seen from infinity decreases by as much (Hartle; Carroll, p. 417; the partner quanta carry negative Killing energy into the hole, [[§C13.3 The Area Theorem#^rem-c13-3-1|§C13.3, Remark: Hawking radiation violates the area theorem]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{\mathrm dM}{\mathrm dt} = -L = -\frac1{15360\pi M^2} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (integration, part 2). Separate the variables and integrate from $(0, M_0)$ to $(t, M)$:
>
> $$
> \int_{M_0}^{M}M'^2\,\mathrm dM' = -\int_0^t\frac{\mathrm dt'}{15360\pi} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{M^3 - M_0^3}3 = -\frac t{15360\pi} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M^3 = M_0^3 - \frac t{5120\pi} .
> $$
>
> $M$ reaches zero at $t = \tau = 5120\pi M_0^3$, and $M^3 = M_0^3(1 - t/\tau)$ gives $M(t)$. Then $T_H = 1/8\pi M = T_0(1 - t/\tau)^{-1/3}$, and $L \propto M^{-2}$ gives $L = L_0(1 - t/\tau)^{-2/3}$. Restored, $\tau = 5120\pi G^2M_0^3/(\hbar c^4)$, and $5120\pi G^2/(\hbar c^4) = 8.41\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm{s\,kg^{-3}}$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 3). From Step 4, $\tau - t = 5120\pi(M_0^3 - t/5120\pi) = 5120\pi M(t)^3$. $M = M_0/2$ means $1 - t/\tau = \frac18$, so $t = \frac78\tau$. Setting $\tau - t = \Delta t$ gives the mass left, $M = (\Delta t/5120\pi)^{1/3}$, all of which is radiated in $\Delta t$.
>
> **Step 6** (entropy flux, part 4). Black-body radiation has entropy density $s = \frac43aT^3$ with $a = 4\sigma_{\rm SB}$ ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-5|TH Theorem §B11.1.5]]: $S = \frac43aVT^3$). It streams out of a black surface with the same factor $c/4 = \frac14$ as the energy (TH Theorem §B11.1.6, part 1), so the entropy flux is $\frac14\cdot\frac43\cdot4\sigma_{\rm SB}T^3 = \frac43\sigma_{\rm SB}T^3$ per unit area, and
>
> $$
> \frac{\mathrm dS_{\rm rad}}{\mathrm dt} = \frac43\sigma_{\rm SB}AT_H^3 = \frac43\,\frac{L}{T_H} .
> $$
>
> The hole loses entropy at $\mathrm dS_{\rm BH}/\mathrm dt = (\mathrm dS_{\rm BH}/\mathrm dM)\,\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt = (1/T_H)(-L)$ ([[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^thm-c13-4-4|Theorem §C13.4.4]], part 2). The sum is $(\frac43 - 1)L/T_H = L/3T_H > 0$. Since $\mathrm dS_{\rm rad} = \frac43|\mathrm dS_{\rm BH}|$ at every instant, integrating to $M = 0$ gives $S_{\rm rad} = \frac43\cdot4\pi M_0^2$.
>
> **Step 7** (part 5). Isotropic radiation of energy density $u$ has intensity $u/4\pi$ per unit solid angle. A body that absorbs every ray within the cross-section $\sigma_{\rm capt}$, the same from every direction, absorbs $\int\mathrm d\Omega\,(u/4\pi)\,\sigma_{\rm capt} = u\,\sigma_{\rm capt} = 4\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4\sigma_{\rm capt}$. For a black sphere of radius $R$, $\sigma_{\rm capt} = \pi R^2$ and this is $\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4\cdot4\pi R^2$, so the emitting area equals $4\sigma_{\rm capt}$. In equilibrium a body emits what it absorbs (Kirchhoff, TH Theorem §B11.1.6, part 2). With $\sigma_{\rm capt} = 27\pi M^2$ the area is $108\pi M^2 = \frac{27}4\cdot16\pi M^2$. Geometric optics needs $\omega b_c \gg 1$ with $b_c = 3\sqrt3M$. At the Planck peak $\omega = 2.821\,T_H$ ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-4|TH Theorem §B11.1.4]]), $\omega b_c = 2.821\cdot3\sqrt3/8\pi = 0.58$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** $L \propto M^{-2}$ is $A \propto M^2$ times $T^4 \propto M^{-4}$, and the hole runs away because its heat capacity is negative ([[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^thm-c13-4-4|Theorem §C13.4.4]], part 3). The coefficient depends on an emitting area that neither the horizon nor geometric optics gives correctly at the wavelengths that matter.

## When the hole may be treated as static

[[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^thm-c14-3-3|Theorem §C14.3.3]] obtained the thermal spectrum from the exponential relation $U = -e^{-\kappa u}$ between the affine parameter on the horizon and retarded time, with $\kappa$ constant. An evaporating hole has $\kappa(u) = 1/4M(u)$. The thermal reading survives if $\kappa$ hardly changes during the time over which one thermal quantum is assembled, a few times $1/\kappa$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C14.4.2: When the Quasi-Static Description Holds
> Define the **adiabaticity parameter** of an evaporating hole as the fractional change of $\kappa$ per e-folding time $1/\kappa$, $\varepsilon = |\mathrm d\kappa/\mathrm du|/\kappa^2$. For Schwarzschild evaporation ([[#^thm-c14-4-1|Theorem §C14.4.1]]):
> 1. $\varepsilon = |\dot M|/(\kappa M) = 4|\dot M| = \dfrac1{3840\pi M^2}$. The temperature changes by $|\dot T_H|/T_H^2 = 2\pi\varepsilon = 1/(1920M^2)$ of itself per thermal time $1/T_H$.
> 2. The mean energy of an emitted photon, $2.701\,T_H$, is the fraction $2.701/(8\pi M^2) = 0.107/M^2$ of the hole's mass.
> 3. Both are small exactly when $M \gg m_{\rm P}$. Restored,
>
> $$
> \varepsilon = \frac{4GL}{c^5} = \frac{1}{3840\pi}\Bigl(\frac{m_{\rm P}}M\Bigr)^2 :
> $$
>
>    the condition $|\dot M|/M \ll \kappa$ says the luminosity is far below $c^5/4G = 9.07\times10^{51}$ W. For $1\,M_\odot$, $\varepsilon = 9.9\times10^{-81}$; for the hole that lives $13.8$ Gyr, $1.3\times10^{-42}$.
> 4. $\varepsilon$ reaches $1$ only at $M = 0.009\,m_{\rm P}$, while a single quantum carries the whole mass already at $M \approx 0.33\,m_{\rm P}$. Part 2 therefore fails first: the semiclassical description ends at the Planck mass.
>
> *Model:* Thm §C14.3.3 with $\kappa$ allowed to vary slowly, $U = -\exp(-\int^u\kappa\,\mathrm du')$; *applies:* as a necessary condition for reading the flux at retarded time $u$ as thermal at $T_H(M(u))$. That the condition is also sufficient is not proved in the typed sources.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §9.6, after (9.116) (p. 172, PDF 173; condition corrected, see [[#^cau-c14-4-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens §8.2, §9.6 and in the textbooks]]) · Schutz, §11.5, before (11.111) (p. 349, PDF 367; $E \ll M$) · derived here · checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (why $\varepsilon$). Along ℐ⁺ the relation of Theorem §C14.3.3 between $U$ and $u$ reads $\mathrm dU/\mathrm du = -\kappa U$. For constant $\kappa$, a mode $e^{-i\omega u}$ becomes $(-U)^{i\omega/\kappa}$ and its Bogoliubov ratio is $e^{-\pi\omega/\kappa}$ ([[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^thm-c14-3-4|Theorem §C14.3.4]]). The Fourier transform that produces it samples the mode over a few periods $2\pi/\omega \sim 1/T_H = 2\pi/\kappa$. If $\kappa$ varies, over the window $\Delta u = 2\pi n/\kappa$ it changes by $|\mathrm d\kappa/\mathrm du|\,\Delta u = 2\pi n\,\varepsilon\kappa$. The constant-$\kappa$ result is used with a relative error of order $\varepsilon$.
>
> **Step 2** (part 1). With $\kappa = 1/4M$, $\mathrm d\kappa/\mathrm du = -\dot M/4M^2$, so
>
> $$
> \varepsilon = \frac{|\dot M|}{4M^2}\cdot16M^2 = 4|\dot M| = \frac{|\dot M|}{\kappa M}, \qquad 4|\dot M| = \frac{4}{15360\pi M^2} = \frac1{3840\pi M^2} .
> $$
>
> For the temperature, $T_H = \kappa/2\pi$ gives $|\dot T_H|/T_H^2 = (|\dot\kappa|/2\pi)(2\pi/\kappa)^2 = 2\pi\varepsilon = 2\pi/(3840\pi M^2) = 1/(1920M^2)$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 2). The mean photon energy of a Planck spectrum is $\frac{\pi^4}{30\zeta(3)}T = 2.701\,T$ ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-5|TH Theorem §B11.1.5]]). Divided by $M$: $2.701/(8\pi M\cdot M) = 0.1075/M^2$. Schutz's condition $E \ll M$ is this ratio being small: one quantum must not change the background it is computed on.
>
> **Step 4** (parts 3–4). With $G = c = \hbar = 1$, $M$ is measured in Planck masses, so $M^2 \to (M/m_{\rm P})^2$. $\dot M$ is dimensionless; restored it is $G\,\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt/c^3 = GL/c^5$, so $\varepsilon = 4GL/c^5$ and $c^5/4G = 9.07\times10^{51}$ W. Solving $\varepsilon = 1$ gives $M = (3840\pi)^{-1/2} = 0.0091$; solving $0.1075/M^2 = 1$ gives $M = 0.33$. For $M_\odot = 9.14\times10^{37}\,m_{\rm P}$: $\varepsilon = 1/(3840\pi\cdot8.36\times10^{75}) = 9.9\times10^{-81}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The rate that must be small is $\dot\kappa/\kappa^2$, a dimensionless number. Couzens' "$\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt \ll M$" compares quantities of different dimensions; the correct comparison is between the evaporation rate $|\dot M|/M$ and $\kappa$. Back-reaction on the spectrum is negligible until the hole is within a factor of a few of the Planck mass.

## The state outside the horizon

A thermal flux is what one expects from a hot body, and a hot body can be in a pure state of body plus radiation. What differs here is where the rest of the state is. In the vacuum of §C14.3 each outgoing mode reaching ℐ⁺ comes with a partner mode that ends behind the horizon, and the two are created together ([[§C14.2 The Unruh Effect#^thm-c14-2-2|Theorem §C14.2.2]], the thermofield double; Couzens (8.16)). Once a horizon exists, the Hilbert space of the field splits as $\mathcal H = \mathcal H_{\rm in}\otimes\mathcal H_{\rm out}$ ([[§C1.5 Composite Systems and Tensor Products#^pr-c1-5-1|QM Principle §C1.5.1]]): partner modes inside, emitted modes outside. An observer outside measures only operators on $\mathcal H_{\rm out}$. By [[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^thm-c11-3-1|QM Theorem §C11.3.1]], everything such an observer sees is fixed by the reduced density operator $\rho_{\rm out} = \operatorname{tr}_{\rm in}\rho$ ([[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^def-c11-3-1|QM Def. §C11.3.1]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C14.4.3: The Exterior State Is Mixed: the Reduced Density Matrix Is Thermal
> Fix one outgoing mode of frequency $\omega$ (at infinity), with annihilation operator $b$ on $\mathcal H_{\rm out}$, and its partner $\tilde b$ on $\mathcal H_{\rm in}$. Put $x = e^{-\beta\omega}$, $\beta = 1/T_H$, and let $|n, \tilde n\rangle$ be the number states ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-5|QM Theorem §B4.1.5]]).
> 1. **The pair state.** The in-vacuum is annihilated by $b - x^{1/2}\tilde b^\dagger$ and $\tilde b - x^{1/2}b^\dagger$, where $x^{1/2} = e^{-\pi\omega/\kappa}$ is the ratio of the Bogoliubov coefficients ([[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^thm-c14-3-4|Theorem §C14.3.4]]; the Rindler analogue is [[§C14.2 The Unruh Effect#^thm-c14-2-1|Theorem §C14.2.1]]). The unique normalised state with these two properties is, up to a phase,
>
> $$
> |\Psi_\omega\rangle = \sqrt{1 - x}\,\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^{n/2}\,|n\rangle_{\rm out}|n\rangle_{\rm in} = \sqrt{1 - x}\;e^{x^{1/2}b^\dagger\tilde b^\dagger}\,|0\rangle_{\rm out}|0\rangle_{\rm in} ,
> $$
>
>    a two-mode squeezed state: the numbers of quanta inside and outside are perfectly correlated.
> 2. **Partial trace.** The reduced state outside is the canonical density operator of the mode ([[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-5|QM Theorem §C11.2.5]]),
>
> $$
> \rho_{\rm out} = \operatorname{tr}_{\rm in}|\Psi_\omega\rangle\langle\Psi_\omega| = (1 - x)\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n\,|n\rangle\langle n| = \frac{e^{-\beta\omega b^\dagger b}}{Z}, \qquad Z = \frac1{1 - x},
> $$
>
>    with mean occupation $\langle b^\dagger b\rangle = 1/(e^{\beta\omega} - 1)$, the Bose–Einstein (Planck) value ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-3|TH Theorem §B10.1.3]]).
> 3. **Mixed, though the whole is pure.** $|\Psi_\omega\rangle$ is a pure state of $\mathcal H_{\rm in}\otimes\mathcal H_{\rm out}$, but $\operatorname{tr}\rho_{\rm out}^2 = \tanh(\beta\omega/2) < 1$ ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-3|QM Theorem §C11.1.3]]). Its entanglement entropy ([[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^def-c11-3-2|QM Def. §C11.3.2]]) is the entropy of one Bose mode,
>
> $$
> S_\omega = -\operatorname{tr}\rho_{\rm out}\ln\rho_{\rm out} = \frac{\beta\omega}{e^{\beta\omega} - 1} - \ln\bigl(1 - e^{-\beta\omega}\bigr) > 0 .
> $$
>
> 4. **All modes.** For the independent modes $(\omega, l, m)$ the state is the product of the pair states, and the trace factorises:
>
> $$
> \rho_{\rm out} = \bigotimes_{\omega lm}\Bigl[(1 - e^{-\beta\omega})\sum_n e^{-\beta\omega n}\,|n\rangle\langle n|_{\omega lm}\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
>    Couzens' (8.20). Its entropy is the sum of the $S_\omega$, the entropy of thermal radiation.
> 5. ⚑ **The radiation's entropy overtakes the hole's.** In the black-body model of [[#^thm-c14-4-1|Theorem §C14.4.1]] the thermal entropy emitted by the time the mass is $M$ is $\frac43(S_{\rm BH}(M_0) - S_{\rm BH}(M))$. It equals $S_{\rm BH}(M)$ at $M = \sqrt{4/7}\,M_0 = 0.756\,M_0$, that is, at $t = \bigl(1 - (4/7)^{3/2}\bigr)\tau = 0.568\,\tau$, and exceeds it afterwards.
>
> *Model:* free massless field on the collapse background in the in-vacuum (§C14.2–§C14.3), one mode at a time; greybody scattering ignored (with it, the flux at ℐ⁺ is still a diagonal thermal state per mode, with occupation reduced by the transmission probability, [[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^def-c14-3-1|Def. §C14.3.1]]); part 5 also uses Theorem §C14.4.1; *applies:* exact within the model for parts 1–4.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §8.1, eq. (8.16) (p. 151, PDF 152); §8.2, eq. (8.20) (p. 152, PDF 153); §9.6 (pp. 172–173, PDF 173–174; $\mathcal H = \mathcal H_{\rm in}\otimes\mathcal H_{\rm out}$, $\rho_{\rm out} = \operatorname{tr}_{\rm in}\rho$) · parts 3 and 5 derived here · checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the conditions fix the state, part 1). Expand $|\Psi\rangle = \sum_{m,k}c_{mk}|m\rangle|k\rangle$. With $b|m\rangle = \sqrt m|m-1\rangle$ and $\tilde b^\dagger|k\rangle = \sqrt{k+1}|k+1\rangle$ (QM Theorem §B4.1.5), the condition $(b - q\tilde b^\dagger)|\Psi\rangle = 0$, $q = x^{1/2}$, is, coefficient of $|m\rangle|k\rangle$,
>
> $$
> \sqrt{m+1}\,c_{m+1,k} = q\sqrt k\,c_{m,k-1} .
> $$
>
> (the left side from $b$ lowering $m + 1$ to $m$, the right side from $\tilde b^\dagger$ raising $k - 1$ to $k$; for $k = 0$ the right side is $0$). The second condition, $(\tilde b - qb^\dagger)|\Psi\rangle = 0$, gives the same relation with the two indices exchanged, $\sqrt{k+1}\,c_{m,k+1} = q\sqrt m\,c_{m-1,k}$.
>
> - At $k = 0$ the first relation gives $c_{m+1,0} = 0$ for all $m \ge 0$; at $m = 0$ the second gives $c_{0,k+1} = 0$ for all $k \ge 0$. So the first row and column vanish except $c_{00}$.
> - Induction on $k$: if $c_{m,k-1} = 0$ for $m \ne k - 1$, the first relation gives $c_{m+1,k} = 0$ for $m + 1 \ne k$ ($m \ge 0$), and $c_{0,k} = 0$ is already known. On the diagonal, $m + 1 = k$: $\sqrt k\,c_{kk} = q\sqrt k\,c_{k-1,k-1}$, so $c_{kk} = q\,c_{k-1,k-1}$.
>
> Hence $c_{mk} = q^mc_{00}\,\delta_{mk}$. Normalisation: $\sum_n|c_{nn}|^2 = |c_{00}|^2\sum_nx^n = |c_{00}|^2/(1 - x) = 1$, so $c_{00} = \sqrt{1 - x}$ up to a phase.
>
> **Step 2** (exponential form). $(b^\dagger\tilde b^\dagger)^n|0\rangle|0\rangle = (b^\dagger)^n|0\rangle\,(\tilde b^\dagger)^n|0\rangle = \sqrt{n!}\sqrt{n!}\,|n\rangle|n\rangle$, since $b^\dagger$ and $\tilde b^\dagger$ act on different factors and commute. Hence
>
> $$
> e^{qb^\dagger\tilde b^\dagger}|0\rangle|0\rangle = \sum_n\frac{q^n}{n!}\,n!\,|n\rangle|n\rangle = \sum_nq^n|n\rangle|n\rangle ,
> $$
>
> which is Couzens' (8.16) for one mode, with $q = e^{-\omega/2T_H}$.
>
> **Step 3** (partial trace, part 2). By QM Def. §C11.3.1, $\langle n|\rho_{\rm out}|n'\rangle = \sum_k\langle n|\langle k|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|n'\rangle|k\rangle = \sum_kc_{nk}c_{n'k}^\ast$. With $c_{nk} = \sqrt{1 - x}\,q^n\delta_{nk}$,
>
> $$
> \langle n|\rho_{\rm out}|n'\rangle = (1 - x)\,q^{2n}\,\delta_{nn'} = (1 - x)\,x^n\delta_{nn'} .
> $$
>
> Since $b^\dagger b|n\rangle = n|n\rangle$, $e^{-\beta\omega b^\dagger b}|n\rangle = x^n|n\rangle$ and $\operatorname{tr}e^{-\beta\omega b^\dagger b} = \sum x^n = 1/(1 - x) = Z$. The occupation is $\sum_nn(1 - x)x^n = (1 - x)\,x\,\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\frac1{1 - x} = \frac x{1 - x} = \frac1{e^{\beta\omega} - 1}$.
>
> **Step 4** (part 3). The whole state is a unit vector, so $\rho = |\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|$ has $\rho^2 = \rho$. For the part: $\operatorname{tr}\rho_{\rm out}^2 = (1 - x)^2\sum x^{2n} = (1 - x)^2/(1 - x^2) = (1 - x)/(1 + x)$, and $(1 - e^{-\beta\omega})/(1 + e^{-\beta\omega}) = \tanh(\beta\omega/2)$. The eigenvalues are $p_n = (1 - x)x^n$, with $\ln p_n = \ln(1 - x) - n\beta\omega$, so
>
> $$
> S_\omega = -\sum_np_n\ln p_n = -\ln(1 - x) + \beta\omega\sum_nnp_n = -\ln\bigl(1 - e^{-\beta\omega}\bigr) + \frac{\beta\omega}{e^{\beta\omega} - 1} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (part 4). For $|\Psi\rangle = \bigotimes_j|\Psi_j\rangle$, with $\mathcal H_{\rm in} = \bigotimes_j\mathcal H_{{\rm in},j}$, the partial trace of a product operator is the product of the partial traces, and $\ln$ of a product state is a sum ([[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^thm-c11-3-3|QM Theorem §C11.3.3]], part 4).
>
> **Step 6** (part 5). By Theorem §C14.4.1, part 4, $\mathrm dS_{\rm rad} = \frac43|\mathrm dS_{\rm BH}|$, so $S_{\rm rad}(M) = \frac43\cdot4\pi(M_0^2 - M^2)$. Setting this equal to $S_{\rm BH}(M) = 4\pi M^2$: $\frac43(M_0^2 - M^2) = M^2$, $M^2 = \frac47M_0^2$. With $M^3 = M_0^3(1 - t/\tau)$: $t/\tau = 1 - (4/7)^{3/2} = 1 - 0.432 = 0.568$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Thermality outside is entanglement across the horizon: each emitted quantum has a partner inside, and the outside state is mixed because the partners are traced out, not because information was destroyed. The global state is pure. Part 5 measures how much entanglement the radiation records by the time the hole has lost a quarter of its mass.

> [!remark] Remark: The information paradox
> Theorem §C14.4.3 is not yet a paradox. While the hole exists, $\rho_{\rm out}$ is mixed because part of a pure state is out of reach, as for one spin of an entangled pair ([[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^rem-c11-3-1|QM §C11.3, Remark: Ignorance and subsystem density matrices]]). Couzens puts it so: the reduced density matrix parametrises the outside observer's ignorance of the interior.
>
> The paradox comes with the end of [[#^thm-c14-4-1|Theorem §C14.4.1]]. If the hole evaporates completely, $\mathcal H_{\rm in}$ is gone, and the radiation's mixed state is the whole state: a pure state (the collapsing star, prepared in $|\psi\rangle$) has become mixed. Unitary evolution cannot do this ([[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-3|QM Theorem §C11.2.3]]). Couzens and Carroll contrast burning a book. The radiation is thermal too, but the early radiation is entangled with the hot ashes, and later emission from the ashes can carry the correlations out, so the process is unitary in principle. In the hole the partners sit behind the horizon, which by causality cannot affect the later outgoing radiation. Carroll's version is an evaporating Penrose diagram (his Fig. 9.3, hypothetical): a surface from $i^0$ to $r = 0$ before the singularity is a Cauchy surface ([[§C11.4 Cauchy Surfaces and Cauchy Horizons#^def-c11-4-5|Def. §C11.4.5]]); one after it is not, so the past cannot be retrodicted from the future.
>
> Part 5 sharpens the clash. If the hole's states are counted by $S_{\rm BH}$ ([[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^def-c13-4-1|Def. §C13.4.1]]), then from $t = 0.568\,\tau$ on, the radiation is entangled with a system too small to purify it. Couzens' version throws qubits in at the rate the radiation carries energy out: the area stays constant while the number of states the hole would have to hold grows without bound.
>
> Couzens lists three possible ends, once the hole reaches the Planck size:
> - **Remnant.** Evaporation stops and a Planck-size object stays, holding the partners. Purity requires its entanglement entropy to be at least that of the radiation, about $\frac43S_{\rm BH}(M_0) = 1.4\times10^{77}$ for a solar-mass hole. Its own Bekenstein–Hawking value would be $4\pi$. This contradicts the state-counting reading of $S_{\rm BH}$.
> - **Information loss.** The hole disappears and the final state is mixed, with entropy of the order of the initial $S_{\rm BH}$. The last burst has too little energy to purify the earlier radiation. Hawking argued for this (S. W. Hawking, *Phys. Rev. D* 14 (1976) 2460; not in the typed sources). Couzens adds that it would also violate energy conservation. Neither source gives the argument; it is due to T. Banks, M. E. Peskin and L. Susskind, *Nucl. Phys. B* 244 (1984) 125 (not in the typed sources).
> - **Information escapes** in the radiation. The outgoing quanta would have to carry the infalling state, which within the low-energy theory looks like superluminal transport from the interior. So the effective field theory would have to fail where the curvature at the horizon is small.
>
> The typed sources present the question as open (Couzens: "still an open and active area of research"; Carroll: "keep an open mind"). Carroll reports evidence from string theory against information loss: the state count of certain supersymmetric holes reproduces $A/4$ (Strominger and Vafa 1996, cited there). The following research is not in the typed sources. If evaporation is unitary, the fine-grained entropy of the radiation must rise and then fall to zero, following the smaller of the radiation's thermal entropy and $S_{\rm BH}$ (the **Page curve**: D. N. Page, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 71 (1993) 3743, arXiv:hep-th/9306083). Part 5 is the crude version of its turnover point. Semiclassical gravitational path-integral calculations have since produced a Page curve in specific models, mostly two-dimensional gravity coupled to a bath, through "islands" and "replica wormholes": G. Penington, arXiv:1905.08255 (*JHEP* 09 (2020) 002); A. Almheiri, N. Engelhardt, D. Marolf, H. Maxfield, arXiv:1905.08762 (*JHEP* 12 (2019) 063); A. Almheiri, T. Hartman, J. Maldacena, E. Shaghoulian, A. Tajdini, arXiv:1911.12333 (*JHEP* 05 (2020) 013); G. Penington, S. Shenker, D. Stanford, Z. Yang, arXiv:1911.11977; review: Almheiri et al., *Rev. Mod. Phys.* 93 (2021) 035002, arXiv:2006.06872. How far these computations carry over to an evaporating four-dimensional, asymptotically flat hole, and what mechanism they imply, is a research question.

> [!remark] Remark: Back-reaction and the end point
> Everything above is semiclassical: a quantum field on a classical geometry, with the geometry updated only through $M(u)$. [[#^thm-c14-4-2|Theorem §C14.4.2]] says this is self-consistent while $M \gg m_{\rm P}$, which covers almost the whole history: a solar-mass hole is $9\times10^{37}$ Planck masses. It fails in the last instants. At $M = m_{\rm P} = 2.18\times10^{-8}$ kg the estimate gives $T_H = 5.6\times10^{30}$ K, a radius $r_s = 3.2\times10^{-35}$ m $= 2\,l_{\rm P}$, a remaining lifetime $5120\pi\,t_{\rm P} = 8.7\times10^{-40}$ s and $S_{\rm BH} = 4\pi$: a handful of states ([[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^def-c13-4-1|Def. §C13.4.1]]). Curvature at the horizon is then Planckian, and a quantum theory of gravity is needed (Couzens; Carroll; Schutz before (11.111)). What happens there, a final burst to nothing, a remnant, or something else, is outside the theory of these notes; the three options of [[#^rem-c14-4-1|Remark: The information paradox]] are statements about this end point.

> [!example] Example §C14.4.1: Lifetimes: Solar-Mass, Primordial 10¹¹ kg and Planck-Mass Holes
> *Question.* How long do holes of different masses live in the estimate of [[#^thm-c14-4-1|Theorem §C14.4.1]], and how far can the numbers be trusted?
>
> **Step 1** (the coefficient). $5120\pi G^2/(\hbar c^4) = 5120\pi\times(6.6743\times10^{-11})^2/(1.05457\times10^{-34}\times(2.99792\times10^8)^4) = 8.41\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm{s\,kg^{-3}}$, so $\tau = 8.41\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm s\,(M_0/\mathrm{kg})^3$. In grams this is Hartle's (13.16), $8.4\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm s\,(M_0/\mathrm g)^3$.
>
> **Step 2** (one solar mass). $M_0^3 = (1.98847\times10^{30})^3 = 7.862\times10^{90}\ \mathrm{kg^3}$, so $\tau = 6.61\times10^{74}$ s $= 2.10\times10^{67}$ yr, with $T_H = 6.17\times10^{-8}$ K and $L = 9.0\times10^{-29}$ W. d'Inverno & Vickers quote "about $10^{67}$ years". Dimensional analysis, $(M_\odot/m_{\rm P})^3t_{\rm P} = 4.1\times10^{70}$ s (Carroll (9.178)), is smaller by the factor $5120\pi = 1.6\times10^4$.
>
> **Step 3** (the primordial range). For $10^{11}$ kg: $\tau = 8.41\times10^{-17}\times10^{33}$ s $= 8.41\times10^{16}$ s $= 2.66\times10^9$ yr, with $T_H = 1.23\times10^{12}$ K and $r_s = 1.49\times10^{-16}$ m, a sixth of a proton radius. A hole that ends today was born with $M_0 = (13.8\ \mathrm{Gyr}/8.41\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm s)^{1/3} = 1.73\times10^{11}$ kg, about the mass of a mountain (Hartle: $10^{14}$ g), with $T_H = 7.1\times10^{11}$ K and $L = 1.2\times10^{10}$ W. Only holes formed in the early universe can be this light. At $7.1\times10^{11}$ K the hole also emits electrons (threshold $5.9\times10^9$ K) and heavier particles, so the photon estimate undercounts. With all species and greybody factors, Page found that holes now ending began with $(5 \pm 1)\times10^{11}$ kg (D. N. Page, *Phys. Rev. D* 13 (1976) 198; not in the typed sources), three times the photon estimate.
>
> **Step 4** (the last second). Part 3 of the theorem with $\Delta t = 1$ s: $M = (1/8.41\times10^{-17})^{1/3}$ kg $= 2.28\times10^5$ kg, released as $Mc^2 = 2.05\times10^{22}$ J in that second, at $T_H \ge 5.4\times10^{17}$ K: a gamma-ray burst of about $10^{22}$ W. Schutz's $10^6$ kg uses his normalisation (11.109).
>
> **Step 5** (the Planck mass). $\tau = 5120\pi\,t_{\rm P} = 5120\pi\times5.39\times10^{-44}$ s $= 8.67\times10^{-40}$ s, with $T_H = 5.6\times10^{30}$ K and $L = 7.5\times10^{47}$ W. These numbers only show the scale at which the model ends: by [[#^thm-c14-4-2|Theorem §C14.4.2]], part 2, one quantum then carries a tenth of the mass.

> [!example] Example §C14.4.2: Today's Astrophysical Holes Grow: Above About 4.5 × 10²² kg a Hole Absorbs More from the CMB than It Emits
> *Question.* A hole bathed in the cosmic microwave background at $T_{\rm CMB} = 2.7255$ K both emits and absorbs. Which wins?
>
> **Step 1** (the inequality). Per mode, the hole absorbs from the CMB in proportion to $\Gamma(\omega)\,n(\omega, T_{\rm CMB})$ and emits in proportion to $\Gamma(\omega)\,n(\omega, T_H)$, with the same transmission probability $\Gamma$ ([[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^def-c14-3-1|Def. §C14.3.1]]) and $n = 1/(e^{\omega/T} - 1)$, which increases with $T$. So the net gain in every mode has the sign of $T_{\rm CMB} - T_H$. Whatever the greybody factors, the hole grows exactly when $T_H < T_{\rm CMB}$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\hbar c^3}{8\pi Gk_BM} < T_{\rm CMB} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad M > \frac{\hbar c^3}{8\pi Gk_BT_{\rm CMB}} = \frac{1.227\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{K\,kg}}{2.7255\ \mathrm K} = 4.50\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{kg} ,
> $$
>
> the balance mass of [[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^ex-c13-4-2|Example §C13.4.2]] ($0.61$ lunar masses). Every hole formed from a star lies far above it.
>
> **Step 2** (the size of the effect, black-body estimate). Net rate $\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt = \sigma_{\rm SB}A(T_{\rm CMB}^4 - T_H^4)/c^2$ with $A = 4\pi r_s^2 = 1.096\times10^8\ \mathrm{m^2}$ for $1\,M_\odot$: $\sigma_{\rm SB}T_{\rm CMB}^4 = 5.670\times10^{-8}\times55.18 = 3.13\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$, so the hole absorbs $343$ W $= 3.8\times10^{-15}$ kg/s. That is $(T_{\rm CMB}/T_H)^4 = (4.42\times10^7)^4 = 3.8\times10^{30}$ times what it emits. Over $13.8$ Gyr it gains $1.7\times10^3$ kg, so both processes are negligible for the hole (Hartle).
>
> **Step 3** (stability). Near the balance mass, $\mathrm d(\mathrm dM/\mathrm dt)/\mathrm dM > 0$: a hole slightly heavier is colder, absorbs more and grows; one slightly lighter is hotter, emits more and shrinks. The balance is unstable, as [[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^thm-c13-4-4|Theorem §C13.4.4]], part 4, requires for any body of negative heat capacity in contact with a large bath.
>
> **Step 4** (the future). As the universe expands, $T_{\rm CMB}$ falls (C15, planned). A solar-mass hole starts to lose mass once the background has cooled by the factor $2.7255/6.17\times10^{-8} = 4.42\times10^7$. Only then does the evaporation of [[#^thm-c14-4-1|Theorem §C14.4.1]] begin for it, and its lifetime $2.1\times10^{67}$ yr counts from then.
>

> [!remark]- Connections
> - **C13 becomes physics.** The first law with $T = \kappa/2\pi$ gave $S = A/4$ ([[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^def-c13-4-1|Def. §C13.4.1]]). Here that entropy is radiated away at $\frac43$ of its rate of loss, so the generalised second law ([[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^pr-c13-4-3|Principle §C13.4.3]]) holds during evaporation, while the area theorem fails ([[§C13.3 The Area Theorem#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]]; [[§C13.3 The Area Theorem#^rem-c13-3-1|§C13.3, Remark: Hawking radiation violates the area theorem]]). Negative heat capacity ([[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^thm-c13-4-4|Theorem §C13.4.4]]) is what makes the evaporation run away.
