Does a black hole radiate once quantum fields are included? Chapter C13 found that the first law of black-hole mechanics contains $(\kappa/8\pi)\,dA$ where thermodynamics has $T\,dS$, and it took the entropy $S = A/4$ ([[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^def-c13-4-1|Def. §C13.4.1]]) on the strength of a temperature $T = \kappa/2\pi$ that was stated, not derived. Classical general relativity cannot supply that temperature: it contains no $\hbar$. A temperature means radiation, and radiation of quanta needs a quantum field on the black-hole spacetime. This chapter sets up that field theory (here), finds that an accelerated observer in flat space sees the vacuum as hot ([[§C14.2 The Unruh Effect#^thm-c14-2-2|§C14.2]]), carries the result to black holes ([[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^thm-c14-3-5|§C14.3]]) and follows the consequences ([[§C14.4 Evaporation and the Information Paradox#^thm-c14-4-1|§C14.4]]).

Two ingredients are in place. The classical Klein–Gordon field on any spacetime obeys $\Box\phi = m^2\phi$ ([[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]). In flat spacetime the quantum field was built in Quantum Field Theory (signature $+{-}{-}{-}$ and $\hbar = c = 1$ there): it is expanded in plane waves, the coefficients of the positive-frequency waves $e^{-iEt}$ become annihilation operators, and the vacuum is the state they annihilate ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-2|QFT Theorem §C2a.2.2]], [[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^pr-c2a-3-2|QFT Principle §C2a.3.2]]). The naive plan is to repeat this on a curved background. It fails at the first step. Take the expanding two-dimensional spacetime $ds^2 = C(\eta)(-d\eta^2 + dx^2)$ of [[#^ex-c14-1-1|Example §C14.1.1]], where a field mode $e^{ikx}\chi(\eta)$ obeys $\chi'' + \omega(\eta)^2\chi = 0$ with $\omega(\eta)^2 = k^2 + m^2C(\eta)$. The flat-space guess, a wave of positive frequency at every instant, is $\chi = e^{-i\Theta(\eta)}$ with $\Theta' = \omega$. Then $\chi' = -i\omega\chi$ and $\chi'' = (-i\omega' - \omega^2)\chi$, so
$$
\chi'' + \omega^2\chi = -i\,\omega'(\eta)\,\chi \neq 0 \qquad\text{wherever } C'(\eta) \neq 0 .
$$
No solution keeps one sign of frequency while the universe expands. A solution that is a pure positive-frequency wave before the expansion is a mixture of $e^{-i\omega\eta}$ and $e^{+i\omega\eta}$ after it, so an early and a late observer disagree about which state contains no particles. In Minkowski space this question never arose, because all inertial observers agree on what positive frequency means ([[#^thm-c14-1-6|Theorem §C14.1.6]]); the proof uses Lorentz transformations, which a curved spacetime does not have.

The section builds the replacement in order: the classical field on a globally hyperbolic spacetime ([[#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]]); the Klein–Gordon inner product on a Cauchy surface ([[#^def-c14-1-1|Def. §C14.1.1]]) and its independence of the surface ([[#^thm-c14-1-2|Theorem §C14.1.2]]); positive frequency with respect to a timelike Killing vector ([[#^def-c14-1-2|Def. §C14.1.2]]), which quantizes the field in a stationary region ([[#^thm-c14-1-3|Theorem §C14.1.3]]); the Bogoliubov transformation between two such quantizations ([[#^def-c14-1-3|Def. §C14.1.3]]), its identities ([[#^thm-c14-1-4|Theorem §C14.1.4]]) and the particles it creates ([[#^thm-c14-1-5|Theorem §C14.1.5]]); and the flat-space uniqueness of the vacuum, seen as the special case without mixing ([[#^thm-c14-1-6|Theorem §C14.1.6]]).

**Setup.** A real free scalar field of mass $m \ge 0$, a test field on a fixed spacetime: its energy does not change the metric (semiclassical approximation; when it fails: §C14.4, [[§C14.4 Evaporation and the Information Paradox#^rem-c14-4-2|Remark: Back-reaction and the end point]]). The spacetime is globally hyperbolic. Quantization uses a region in which a timelike Killing vector exists; the theorems on positive frequency are proved for static regions, which covers every application in this chapter (Minkowski space, the Rindler wedge, the Schwarzschild exterior, the asymptotic regions of the examples). Statements are exact within the model; the examples' numbers were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; $\hbar = c = 1$ (for $G$ and $c$: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; for $\hbar$: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^rem-c1-2-1|§C1.2, Remark: Why not ħ as well]] and [[§C1a.2 Natural Units and Dimensional Analysis#^thm-c1a-2-1|QFT Theorem §C1a.2.1]]). The Klein–Gordon product is antilinear in its first slot, as in Quantum Field Theory; Carroll's product is linear in the first slot, so his $(\phi_1, \phi_2)$ is $(\phi_2, \phi_1)$ here. Bogoliubov coefficients $A_{ij}$, $B_{ij}$ are Couzens' (9.57), Carroll's $\alpha_{ij}$, $\beta_{ij}$. Couzens normalizes plane waves to $\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$, Quantum Field Theory to $(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-3|QFT Theorem §C2a.2.3]]).

## The classical field and its conserved product

> [!model] Model §C14.1.1: A Free Scalar Field on a Globally Hyperbolic Spacetime
> *Arena.* A spacetime $(M, g)$, oriented and time-oriented ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]) and **globally hyperbolic** ([[§C11.4 Cauchy Surfaces and Cauchy Horizons#^def-c11-4-6|Def. §C11.4.6]]): it has a Cauchy surface ([[§C11.4 Cauchy Surfaces and Cauchy Horizons#^def-c11-4-5|Def. §C11.4.5]]), and the surfaces used below are smooth and spacelike. The metric is given; it does not respond to the field.
>
> *Field.* A real scalar field $\phi : M \to \mathbf R$ with the minimally coupled action and field equation of [[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]],
> $$
> S[\phi] = \int\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x\,\Bigl[-\tfrac12g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial_\nu\phi - \tfrac12m^2\phi^2\Bigr], \qquad \Box\phi \equiv g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu\phi = m^2\phi .
> $$
>
> *Solution space.* $\mathcal S$ is the real vector space of solutions whose support meets every Cauchy surface in a compact set (data of compact support; on a globally hyperbolic spacetime such data determine a unique solution, [[§C11.4 Cauchy Surfaces and Cauchy Horizons#^def-c11-4-6|Def. §C11.4.6]], item 3, quoted there). Its complexification $\mathcal S_{\mathbf C}$ carries complex conjugation $f \mapsto f^{\ast}$; the equation has real coefficients, so $f^{\ast}$ solves it whenever $f$ does.
>
> *Canonical momentum.* In a chart $(t, x^i)$ whose slices $t = \text{const}$ are spacelike Cauchy surfaces, $\pi = \partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_t\phi) = -\sqrt{-g}\,g^{t\nu}\partial_\nu\phi$, a density on the slice ([[§C1b.4 Hamiltonian Field Theory#^def-c1b-4-1|QFT Def. §C1b.4.1]], signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where flat space gives $\pi = \dot\phi$). For any $f \in \mathcal S_{\mathbf C}$ write $\pi_f \equiv -\sqrt{-g}\,g^{t\nu}\partial_\nu f$.
>
> *Quantization.* The field and its momentum become Hermitian operator-valued distributions with the equal-time relations
> $$
> [\hat\phi(t, \mathbf x), \hat\pi(t, \mathbf y)] = i\,\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y), \qquad [\hat\phi(t, \mathbf x), \hat\phi(t, \mathbf y)] = [\hat\pi(t, \mathbf x), \hat\pi(t, \mathbf y)] = 0
> $$
> ([[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^pr-c2a-1-2|QFT Principle §C2a.1.2]]; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$ there). They are identities after smearing with test functions in $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf y$ ([[§C2a.1 Canonical Quantization of Fields#^thm-c2a-1-3|QFT Theorem §C2a.1.3]]; the delta function: [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]).
>
> - *Assumes:* a test field (no back-reaction); free and minimally coupled (a coupling $\xi R\phi^2$, Carroll (9.87), is allowed by local physics: [[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^ex-c4-1-2|Example §C4.1.2]]); global hyperbolicity, so that data on one surface determine the field everywhere.
> - *Fails:* when the field's energy density is large enough to change the geometry, and at curvatures near the Planck scale, where the metric itself would have to be quantized.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §9.2, eqs. (9.29)–(9.33), (9.41); §9.3, eq. (9.49) (pp. 157–158, 160) · Carroll, §9.4, eqs. (9.87), (9.90)–(9.93) (pp. 394–395)*

In flat space the coefficients $\hat a_{\mathbf p}$ were extracted from the field by a product of solutions that does not depend on the time slice ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-5|QFT Theorem §C2a.2.5]]). On a curved spacetime there are no preferred time slices; the replacement integrates over an arbitrary Cauchy surface.

> [!definition] Definition §C14.1.1: Klein–Gordon Inner Product on a Cauchy Surface
> *Structure.* A sesquilinear form on the complexified solution space $\mathcal S_{\mathbf C}$ of [[#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]], antilinear in the first slot. For a smooth spacelike Cauchy surface $\Sigma$ with coordinates $y^i$, induced metric $\gamma_{ij}$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]), $\gamma = \det\gamma_{ij}$, and future-directed unit normal vector $N^\mu$ ($N\cdot N = -1$),
> $$
> (f, g)_\Sigma = i\int_\Sigma\sqrt\gamma\;N^\mu\bigl(f^{\ast}\,\partial_\mu g - g\,\partial_\mu f^{\ast}\bigr)\,d^3y .
> $$
>
> *On a slice of a chart.* If $\Sigma$ is $t = \text{const}$, then $\sqrt\gamma\,N^\mu = -\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\mu t}$, and the product is the pairing of canonical data,
> $$
> (f, g) = i\int d^3x\,\bigl(f^{\ast}\,\pi_g - g\,\pi_{f^{\ast}}\bigr) .
> $$
> *Why.* The normal covector to $t = \text{const}$ is $N_\mu = -\lambda\,\partial_\mu t$ with $\lambda > 0$ (minus sign: $N$ future-directed), so $N^\mu = -\lambda g^{\mu t}$, and $N\cdot N = \lambda^2g^{tt} = -1$ gives $\lambda = (-g^{tt})^{-1/2}$. By Cramer's rule $g^{tt}$ is the cofactor of $g_{tt}$, which is $\det g_{ij} = \gamma$, divided by $g = \det g_{\mu\nu}$: $-g^{tt} = \gamma/(-g)$. So $\lambda = \sqrt{-g}/\sqrt\gamma$ and $\sqrt\gamma\,N^\mu = -\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\mu t}$.
>
> *Flat space.* For $\Sigma = \{t = \text{const}\}$ in Minkowski space this is $(f, g) = i\int d^3x\,(f^{\ast}\partial_tg - g\,\partial_tf^{\ast})$, the product of [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^def-c2a-2-1|QFT Def. §C2a.2.1]] (written there in signature $+{-}{-}{-}$; the form with $\partial_t$ is the same in both signatures).
>
> *Properties* (from the formula): $(g, f) = (f, g)^{\ast}$, so $(f, f)$ is real; $(f^{\ast}, g^{\ast}) = -(f, g)^{\ast}$; the form is indefinite: $(f^{\ast}, f^{\ast}) = -(f, f)$. It is not a Hilbert-space inner product ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^rem-c2a-2-3|QFT §C2a.2, Remark: Why this inner product, and why it is indefinite]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §9.2, eq. (9.37); §9.3, eq. (9.50) (pp. 158, 160; second term of (9.50) corrected: [[#^cau-c14-1-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens §9.1–§9.3 and Carroll §9.4]]) · Carroll, §9.4, eq. (9.94) (p. 396; linear in the first slot)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C14.1.2: The Inner Product Does Not Depend on the Cauchy Surface
> Let $f, g \in \mathcal S_{\mathbf C}$ ([[#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]]).
> 1. **Conserved current.** The vector field
> $$
> j^\mu(f, g) = -i\,g^{\mu\nu}\bigl(f^{\ast}\,\partial_\nu g - g\,\partial_\nu f^{\ast}\bigr)
> $$
>    satisfies $\nabla_\mu j^\mu = 0$, and $(f, g)_\Sigma = \int_\Sigma\sqrt\gamma\,(-N_\mu j^\mu)\,d^3y$ ([[#^def-c14-1-1|Def. §C14.1.1]]).
> 2. **Independence.** Let $\Sigma_1$, $\Sigma_2$ be smooth spacelike Cauchy surfaces with $\Sigma_2$ to the future of $\Sigma_1$, $\Sigma_2 \subset I^+(\Sigma_1)$, and let the supports of $f$ and $g$ meet the region $V$ between them in a compact set. Then
> $$
> (f, g)_{\Sigma_2} = (f, g)_{\Sigma_1} .
> $$
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]]; *applies:* exactly, for solutions of the free equation (the conservation uses $\Box f = m^2f$). Plane-wave-like modes of infinite norm are covered after smearing into wave packets, as in flat space ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-4|QFT Theorem §C2a.2.4]]). Two Cauchy surfaces that cross are not nested; the same argument applied to the parts of the region above and below each surface gives the same result, but the corners where they meet need an approximation argument not written out here. Every use in this chapter compares nested surfaces.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §9.2, after eq. (9.37) ("by using Stoke's theorem and the equation of motion"; not carried out) (p. 158) · Carroll, §9.4, after eq. (9.94) (p. 396; stated) · proof written here*

^thm-c14-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (divergence). By the product rule, with $\nabla^\nu = g^{\nu\lambda}\nabla_\lambda$ commuting with raising ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-5|Theorem §C3.6.5]]),
> $$
> \nabla_\mu j^\mu = -i\bigl(\nabla_\mu f^{\ast}\,\nabla^\mu g + f^{\ast}\,\Box g - \nabla_\mu g\,\nabla^\mu f^{\ast} - g\,\Box f^{\ast}\bigr) = -i\bigl(f^{\ast}\,m^2g - g\,m^2f^{\ast}\bigr) = 0 ,
> $$
> since the first and third terms cancel ($g^{\mu\nu}$ is symmetric) and $\Box g = m^2g$, $\Box f^{\ast} = m^2f^{\ast}$ ([[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]).
>
> **Step 2** (flux through $\Sigma$). Contracting with $-N_\mu$: $-N_\mu j^\mu = i\bigl(f^{\ast}N^\nu\partial_\nu g - g\,N^\nu\partial_\nu f^{\ast}\bigr)$, the integrand of [[#^def-c14-1-1|Def. §C14.1.1]] apart from $\sqrt\gamma$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (divergence theorem). $V = J^+(\Sigma_1) \cap J^-(\Sigma_2)$ is a manifold with boundary $\partial V = \Sigma_1 \cup \Sigma_2$, two disjoint spacelike pieces. Apply [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-4|Theorem §C3.6.4]], part 3, to $j$ (compact support in $V$ by hypothesis):
> $$
> 0 = \int_V\nabla_\mu j^\mu\,\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x = \int_{\Sigma_2}\sqrt\gamma\;n_\mu j^\mu\,d^3y + \int_{\Sigma_1}\sqrt\gamma\;n_\mu j^\mu\,d^3y ,
> $$
> with $n_\mu$ the outward unit normal covector: $n_\mu M^\mu > 0$ for outward-pointing $M$.
>
> **Step 4** (signs). On $\Sigma_2$ outward is to the future, $M = N$; $n_\mu = -N_\mu$ gives $n_\mu N^\mu = -N\cdot N = 1 > 0$. On $\Sigma_1$ outward is to the past, $M = -N$; $n_\mu = +N_\mu$ gives $n_\mu(-N^\mu) = 1 > 0$. With Step 2,
> $$
> 0 = \int_{\Sigma_2}\sqrt\gamma\,(-N_\mu j^\mu)\,d^3y - \int_{\Sigma_1}\sqrt\gamma\,(-N_\mu j^\mu)\,d^3y = (f, g)_{\Sigma_2} - (f, g)_{\Sigma_1} .
> $$
> This is part 2.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The product is a conserved charge, like every Noether charge ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^rem-c2a-2-3|QFT §C2a.2, Remark: Why this inner product, and why it is indefinite]]); only the field equation and the divergence theorem were used, never a time coordinate.
> - Hence a product computed in one region of spacetime, where the geometry is simple, holds on every later surface. The examples evaluate products in the far past or far future for this reason.

## Positive frequency and the vacuum of a stationary region

The product is defined on all solutions, but quantization needs more: a split of $\mathcal S_{\mathbf C}$ into a part whose coefficients become annihilators and its complex conjugate, whose coefficients become creators. In flat space the split is by the sign of the frequency with respect to $t$. A time translation is a symmetry of the metric there, and on a curved spacetime the only candidate with the same property is a timelike Killing vector.

> [!definition] Definition §C14.1.2: Positive Frequency with Respect to a Timelike Killing Vector
> *Structure.* Let $U \subseteq M$ be a region, mapped into itself by the flow of a Killing vector $K$ ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-2|Def. §C3.13.2]]) that is timelike and future-directed on $U$ (a stationary region, [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-3|Def. §C3.13.3]]). A solution $f \in \mathcal S_{\mathbf C}$ is a **positive-frequency mode of frequency $\omega > 0$** with respect to $K$ if, on $U$,
> $$
> \mathcal L_Kf = K^\mu\partial_\mu f = -i\omega f .
> $$
> Its conjugate satisfies $K^\mu\partial_\mu f^{\ast} = +i\omega f^{\ast}$: a **negative-frequency** mode. The closed span of the positive-frequency modes is the **positive-frequency subspace** $\mathcal S^+_K \subset \mathcal S_{\mathbf C}$, and $\mathcal S^-_K = (\mathcal S^+_K)^{\ast}$.
>
> *Components.* In a chart adapted to $K$, $K = \partial_t$ ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-6|Theorem §C3.13.6]]), the condition reads $\partial_tf = -i\omega f$, so $f = e^{-i\omega t}u(\mathbf x)$. In Minkowski space with $K = \partial_t$ these are the plane waves $e^{ip_\mu x^\mu}$ with $p^0 = \sqrt{\mathbf p^2 + m^2} > 0$ (Couzens (9.34)–(9.36)).
>
> *Reading.* $\omega$ is measured with the parameter of $K$. A static observer, moving along $K$, measures the frequency $\omega/\sqrt{-K\cdot K}$ with her own clock; the factor is the gravitational redshift ([[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay#^thm-c6-6-1|Theorem §C6.6.1]]). The modes are defined by their behaviour on $U$ and continued to all of $M$ by the field equation.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §9.3, eqs. (9.51)–(9.52) (p. 160; written $iK^\mu\partial_\mu f = \omega f$) · Carroll, §9.4, eqs. (9.115)–(9.119) (p. 400)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C14.1.3: Mode Expansion, Canonical Commutators and the Vacuum of a Stationary Region
> In [[#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]], let $K$ be a timelike Killing vector that is hypersurface-orthogonal on a region $U$ (static, [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-3|Def. §C3.13.3]]), with static chart $ds^2 = g_{tt}\,dt^2 + g_{ij}\,dx^idx^j$, $K = \partial_t$, whose slices $t = \text{const}$ are Cauchy surfaces.
> 1. **The Killing vector commutes with the wave operator.** If $f$ solves $\Box f = m^2f$, so does $K^\mu\partial_\mu f$.
> 2. **Orthogonality and sign.** For positive-frequency modes $f = e^{-i\omega t}u$ and $h = e^{-i\omega' t}v$ ([[#^def-c14-1-2|Def. §C14.1.2]]):
>    - $(f, h) = 0$ if $\omega \neq \omega'$;
>    - $(f, h^{\ast}) = 0$ always;
>    - $(f, f) = 2\omega\int\sqrt{-g}\,(-g^{tt})\,|u|^2\,d^3x > 0$.
>
>    So the product is positive definite on $\mathcal S^+_K$, negative definite on $\mathcal S^-_K$, and the two are orthogonal.
> 3. **Mode expansion.** Let $\{f_i\}$ be a complete set of positive-frequency modes, orthonormal: $(f_i, f_j) = \delta_{ij}$. Then $(f_i^{\ast}, f_j^{\ast}) = -\delta_{ij}$, $(f_i, f_j^{\ast}) = 0$, and every real solution is
> $$
> \phi = \sum_i\bigl(a_i\,f_i + a_i^{\ast}\,f_i^{\ast}\bigr), \qquad a_i = (f_i, \phi) .
> $$
> 4. **Canonical commutators.** For $\hat\phi = \sum_i(\hat a_if_i + \hat a_i^\dagger f_i^{\ast})$ with $\hat a_i = (f_i, \hat\phi)$, the equal-time relations of [[#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]] hold if and only if
> $$
> [\hat a_i, \hat a_j^\dagger] = \delta_{ij}, \qquad [\hat a_i, \hat a_j] = [\hat a_i^\dagger, \hat a_j^\dagger] = 0 .
> $$
> 5. **The vacuum.** The **vacuum of the region**, $|0_K\rangle$, is the state with $\hat a_i|0_K\rangle = 0$ for all $i$; the Fock space is built on it as in flat space ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^pr-c2a-3-2|QFT Principle §C2a.3.2]]). $|0_K\rangle$ depends only on the subspace $\mathcal S^+_K$, not on the orthonormal basis chosen in it.
>
> For continuous labels, $\delta_{ij}$ is a delta function of the labels, and the relations hold after smearing in the labels ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-4|QFT Theorem §C2a.2.4]]); sums become integrals.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]]; *applies:* exactly, in a static region containing a Cauchy surface (Minkowski space with any inertial $t$; the asymptotic regions of a sandwich spacetime; for the Rindler wedge and the Schwarzschild exterior the region is not all of spacetime, and §C14.2 and §C14.3 say what that changes). Completeness of the modes is assumed, as in the sources. For a stationary region that is not static, part 1 holds as proved ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-6|Theorem §C3.13.6]], part 1) and parts 3–5 follow once part 2 is known; part 2 then needs an argument not given here.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §9.3, eqs. (9.51)–(9.54) (p. 160; part 2 asserted as "a complete set of common eigenfunctions") · Carroll, §9.4, eqs. (9.95)–(9.99), (9.113)–(9.119) (pp. 396, 399–400) · proof written here*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). In the adapted chart no metric component depends on $t$ ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-6|Theorem §C3.13.6]], part 1), so the coefficients of $\Box = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu\,\cdot\bigr)$ ([[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]], part 2) do not depend on $t$, and $\partial_t\Box f = \Box\,\partial_tf$. Hence $\Box(\partial_tf) = \partial_t(m^2f) = m^2\,\partial_tf$.
>
> **Step 2** (the product in the static chart). With $g_{ti} = 0$, $g^{ti} = 0$ and $\pi_f = \sqrt{-g}\,(-g^{tt})\,\partial_tf$, where $-g^{tt} = -1/g_{tt} > 0$ because $K = \partial_t$ is timelike. Write $\mu(\mathbf x) = \sqrt{-g}\,(-g^{tt}) > 0$. For $f = e^{-i\omega t}u$, $h = e^{-i\omega't}v$, [[#^def-c14-1-1|Def. §C14.1.1]] on the slice $t$ gives
> $$
> (f, h) = i\int\mu\bigl(f^{\ast}\partial_th - h\,\partial_tf^{\ast}\bigr)d^3x = i\int\mu\,e^{i(\omega - \omega')t}u^{\ast}v\,(-i\omega' - i\omega)\,d^3x = (\omega + \omega')\,e^{i(\omega - \omega')t}\int\mu\,u^{\ast}v\,d^3x .
> $$
> By [[#^thm-c14-1-2|Theorem §C14.1.2]] the left side is the same on every slice. If $\omega \neq \omega'$, the right side changes with $t$ unless the integral vanishes; so $(f, h) = 0$. For $h = f$: $(f, f) = 2\omega\int\mu|u|^2\,d^3x > 0$.
>
> **Step 3** (positive against negative). With $h^{\ast} = e^{i\omega't}v^{\ast}$,
> $$
> (f, h^{\ast}) = i\int\mu\,e^{i(\omega + \omega')t}u^{\ast}v^{\ast}\,(i\omega' - i\omega)\,d^3x = (\omega - \omega')\,e^{i(\omega + \omega')t}\int\mu\,u^{\ast}v^{\ast}\,d^3x .
> $$
> Since $\omega + \omega' > 0$, constancy in $t$ forces the integral to vanish, unless $\omega = \omega'$, where the prefactor vanishes. Either way $(f, h^{\ast}) = 0$. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 4** (part 3). $(f_i^{\ast}, f_j^{\ast}) = -(f_i, f_j)^{\ast} = -\delta_{ij}$ and $(f_i, f_j^{\ast}) = 0$ by Step 3. Completeness means every $F \in \mathcal S_{\mathbf C}$ is $F = \sum_j(c_jf_j + d_jf_j^{\ast})$. Taking the product with $f_i$ and with $f_i^{\ast}$ and using the relations: $(f_i, F) = c_i$, $(f_i^{\ast}, F) = -d_i$, so
> $$
> F = \sum_i\bigl[(f_i, F)\,f_i - (f_i^{\ast}, F)\,f_i^{\ast}\bigr] .
> $$
> For real $\phi$: $(f_i^{\ast}, \phi) = (f_i^{\ast}, \phi^{\ast}) = -(f_i, \phi)^{\ast} = -a_i^{\ast}$, which gives the expansion.
>
> **Step 5** (commutators ⇒ mode algebra). By [[#^def-c14-1-1|Def. §C14.1.1]], $\hat a_i = i\int d^3x\,(f_i^{\ast}\hat\pi - \hat\phi\,\pi_{f_i^{\ast}})$ and, $\hat\phi$, $\hat\pi$ being Hermitian, $\hat a_j^\dagger = -i\int d^3y\,(f_j\hat\pi - \hat\phi\,\pi_{f_j})$. Only the mixed commutators survive, $[\hat\pi(\mathbf x), \hat\phi(\mathbf y)] = -i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$ and $[\hat\phi(\mathbf x), \hat\pi(\mathbf y)] = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$:
> $$
> [\hat a_i, \hat a_j^\dagger] = \int\!\!\int d^3x\,d^3y\,\Bigl[-f_i^{\ast}(\mathbf x)\,\pi_{f_j}(\mathbf y)\,(-i\delta^3) - \pi_{f_i^{\ast}}(\mathbf x)\,f_j(\mathbf y)\,(i\delta^3)\Bigr] = i\int d^3x\,\bigl(f_i^{\ast}\pi_{f_j} - f_j\,\pi_{f_i^{\ast}}\bigr) = (f_i, f_j) = \delta_{ij} .
> $$
> The same computation with $\hat a_j = i\int(f_j^{\ast}\hat\pi - \hat\phi\,\pi_{f_j^{\ast}})$ gives $[\hat a_i, \hat a_j] = -i\int(f_i^{\ast}\pi_{f_j^{\ast}} - f_j^{\ast}\pi_{f_i^{\ast}}) = -(f_i, f_j^{\ast}) = 0$. The $\delta^3$ is integrated against the smooth mode functions, which is its definition as a distribution ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-4|QFT Def. §CA.2.4]]).
>
> **Step 6** (mode algebra ⇒ commutators). Write the expansion of Step 4 for data $(F, \pi_F)$ on one slice, with $(f_i, F) = i\int d^3y\,(f_i^{\ast}\pi_F - F\pi_{f_i^{\ast}})$ and $(f_i^{\ast}, F) = i\int d^3y\,(f_i\pi_F - F\pi_{f_i})$:
> $$
> F(\mathbf x) = i\int d^3y\Bigl[\sum_i\bigl(f_i(\mathbf x)f_i^{\ast}(\mathbf y) - f_i^{\ast}(\mathbf x)f_i(\mathbf y)\bigr)\pi_F(\mathbf y) - \sum_i\bigl(f_i(\mathbf x)\pi_{f_i^{\ast}}(\mathbf y) - f_i^{\ast}(\mathbf x)\pi_{f_i}(\mathbf y)\bigr)F(\mathbf y)\Bigr] .
> $$
> The data $F$ and $\pi_F$ on a Cauchy surface can be chosen independently, so the kernels are fixed: $\sum_i(f_i(\mathbf x)f_i^{\ast}(\mathbf y) - f_i^{\ast}(\mathbf x)f_i(\mathbf y)) = 0$ and $\sum_i(f_i(\mathbf x)\pi_{f_i^{\ast}}(\mathbf y) - f_i^{\ast}(\mathbf x)\pi_{f_i}(\mathbf y)) = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y)$. The same steps for $\pi_F$ give $\sum_i(\pi_{f_i}(\mathbf x)\pi_{f_i^{\ast}}(\mathbf y) - \pi_{f_i^{\ast}}(\mathbf x)\pi_{f_i}(\mathbf y)) = 0$. With the mode algebra,
> $$
> [\hat\phi(\mathbf x), \hat\pi(\mathbf y)] = \sum_i\bigl(f_i(\mathbf x)\pi_{f_i^{\ast}}(\mathbf y)[\hat a_i, \hat a_i^\dagger] + f_i^{\ast}(\mathbf x)\pi_{f_i}(\mathbf y)[\hat a_i^\dagger, \hat a_i]\bigr) = i\delta^3(\mathbf x - \mathbf y) ,
> $$
> and the other two commutators are the two vanishing kernels. Part 4 is proved; the identities hold as distributions in $(\mathbf x, \mathbf y)$.
>
> **Step 7** (part 5). Another orthonormal basis of $\mathcal S^+_K$ is $f'_i = \sum_jU_{ij}f_j$ with $U$ unitary. Then $\hat a'_i = (f'_i, \hat\phi) = \sum_jU_{ij}^{\ast}\hat a_j$, a combination of annihilators only, so $\hat a'_i|0_K\rangle = 0$: the same vacuum.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The only input beyond the field equation is the conservation of the product: the sign of the frequency is meaningful because $(f, h^{\ast})$ must not depend on the slice.
> - The commutator $[\hat\phi, \hat\pi] = i\delta^3$ is the same statement as the orthonormality of the modes, read through the completeness relation (checked on a lattice by script, for every conformal factor in [[#^ex-c14-1-1|Example §C14.1.1]]).

## Two regions, two vacua: the Bogoliubov transformation

Couzens' **sandwich spacetime** has a stationary region $B$ at the bottom with Killing vector $K_B$, a dynamical region $C$ in the centre, and a stationary region $T$ at the top with Killing vector $K_T$. Region $B$ gives orthonormal positive-frequency modes $f_i$ ("in"), region $T$ gives modes $g_i$ ("out"); each set is continued through all of spacetime by the field equation, so both expand the same field, $\hat\phi = \sum_i(\hat a_if_i + \hat a_i^\dagger f_i^{\ast}) = \sum_i(\hat b_ig_i + \hat b_i^\dagger g_i^{\ast})$ (Couzens (9.55)–(9.56)). Each set is a basis of $\mathcal S_{\mathbf C}$ together with its conjugates, so each $g_i$ is a combination of the $f_j$ and $f_j^{\ast}$.

> [!definition] Definition §C14.1.3: Bogoliubov Transformation
> *Structure.* Let $\{f_i\}$ and $\{g_i\}$ be two complete orthonormal sets of positive-frequency modes ([[#^thm-c14-1-3|Theorem §C14.1.3]], part 3), for two splittings $\mathcal S_{\mathbf C} = \mathcal S^+_{\rm in} \oplus \mathcal S^-_{\rm in} = \mathcal S^+_{\rm out} \oplus \mathcal S^-_{\rm out}$. The **Bogoliubov transformation** is the change of basis
> $$
> g_i = \sum_j\bigl(A_{ij}\,f_j + B_{ij}\,f_j^{\ast}\bigr), \qquad A_{ij} = (f_j, g_i), \qquad B_{ij} = -(f_j^{\ast}, g_i) ,
> $$
> and $A_{ij}$, $B_{ij}$ are the **Bogoliubov coefficients**. On the pairs $(f, f^{\ast})$ it acts by the block matrix $\begin{pmatrix}A & B\\ B^{\ast} & A^{\ast}\end{pmatrix}$ (the conjugate $g_i^{\ast} = \sum_j(B_{ij}^{\ast}f_j + A_{ij}^{\ast}f_j^{\ast})$ gives the second row): a linear map of $\mathcal S_{\mathbf C}$ that commutes with complex conjugation and preserves the Klein–Gordon product ([[#^def-c14-1-1|Def. §C14.1.1]]).
>
> *Why the coefficients are these products.* Take the product of the expansion with $f_k$ and with $f_k^{\ast}$, using $(f_k, f_j) = \delta_{kj}$, $(f_k, f_j^{\ast}) = 0$, $(f_k^{\ast}, f_j^{\ast}) = -\delta_{kj}$: $(f_k, g_i) = A_{ik}$ and $(f_k^{\ast}, g_i) = -B_{ik}$.
>
> *Meaning.* $B$ measures how much negative in-frequency the out-modes contain. $B = 0$ exactly when $\mathcal S^+_{\rm out} = \mathcal S^+_{\rm in}$: the two regions agree on positive frequency, and $A$ is then unitary (a change of basis as in [[#^thm-c14-1-3|Theorem §C14.1.3]], part 5).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §9.3, eq. (9.57) (p. 161; summation index corrected) · Carroll, §9.4, eqs. (9.106)–(9.107) (p. 398; $\alpha_{ij} = A_{ij}$, $\beta_{ij} = B_{ij}$)*

^def-c14-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C14.1.4: The Bogoliubov Identities AA† − BB† = 1, AB^T = BA^T
> For the Bogoliubov coefficients of [[#^def-c14-1-3|Def. §C14.1.3]]:
> 1. **Orthonormality of the out-modes.**
> $$
> AA^\dagger - BB^\dagger = \mathbb 1, \qquad AB^T = BA^T, \qquad\text{i.e.}\quad \sum_k\bigl(A_{ik}A_{jk}^{\ast} - B_{ik}B_{jk}^{\ast}\bigr) = \delta_{ij}, \quad \sum_k\bigl(A_{ik}B_{jk} - B_{ik}A_{jk}\bigr) = 0 .
> $$
>    For one mode: $|A|^2 - |B|^2 = 1$.
> 2. **The inverse.** $f_i = \sum_j\bigl(A_{ji}^{\ast}\,g_j - B_{ji}\,g_j^{\ast}\bigr)$, and hence also $A^\dagger A - B^TB^{\ast} = \mathbb 1$ and $A^\dagger B = B^TA^{\ast}$.
> 3. **The operators.** The out-annihilators are combinations of in-annihilators and in-creators,
> $$
> \hat b_i = \sum_j\bigl(A_{ij}^{\ast}\,\hat a_j - B_{ij}^{\ast}\,\hat a_j^\dagger\bigr), \qquad \hat a_i = \sum_j\bigl(A_{ji}\,\hat b_j + B_{ji}^{\ast}\,\hat b_j^\dagger\bigr),
> $$
>    and by part 1 they satisfy the mode algebra $[\hat b_i, \hat b_j^\dagger] = \delta_{ij}$, $[\hat b_i, \hat b_j] = 0$.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]]; *applies:* exactly, for complete orthonormal mode sets; for continuous labels as distributions in the labels.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §9.3, eqs. (9.58)–(9.60) (p. 161; stated) · Carroll, §9.4, eqs. (9.106), (9.108)–(9.109) (p. 398) · proof written here; identities checked by script on a random composite transformation*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (products of out-modes). Insert the expansions; the product is antilinear in the first slot, and only $(f_k, f_l) = \delta_{kl}$ and $(f_k^{\ast}, f_l^{\ast}) = -\delta_{kl}$ survive ([[#^thm-c14-1-3|Theorem §C14.1.3]], part 3):
> $$
> (g_i, g_j) = \sum_{k,l}\bigl[A_{ik}^{\ast}A_{jl}(f_k, f_l) + B_{ik}^{\ast}B_{jl}(f_k^{\ast}, f_l^{\ast})\bigr] = \sum_k\bigl(A_{ik}^{\ast}A_{jk} - B_{ik}^{\ast}B_{jk}\bigr) .
> $$
> Setting this equal to $\delta_{ij}$ and taking the complex conjugate gives $\sum_k(A_{ik}A_{jk}^{\ast} - B_{ik}B_{jk}^{\ast}) = \delta_{ij}$. With $g_j^{\ast} = \sum_l(B_{jl}^{\ast}f_l + A_{jl}^{\ast}f_l^{\ast})$,
> $$
> (g_i, g_j^{\ast}) = \sum_{k,l}\bigl[A_{ik}^{\ast}B_{jl}^{\ast}(f_k, f_l) + B_{ik}^{\ast}A_{jl}^{\ast}(f_k^{\ast}, f_l^{\ast})\bigr] = \sum_k\bigl(A_{ik}^{\ast}B_{jk}^{\ast} - B_{ik}^{\ast}A_{jk}^{\ast}\bigr) = 0 ,
> $$
> whose conjugate is $(AB^T - BA^T)_{ij} = 0$. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 2** (inverse). Expand $f_i$ in the out-basis by Step 4 of [[#^der-c14-1-3|the derivation of Theorem §C14.1.3]]: $f_i = \sum_j[(g_j, f_i)g_j - (g_j^{\ast}, f_i)g_j^{\ast}]$. Here $(g_j, f_i) = \sum_k[A_{jk}^{\ast}(f_k, f_i) + B_{jk}^{\ast}(f_k^{\ast}, f_i)] = A_{ji}^{\ast}$ and $(g_j^{\ast}, f_i) = \sum_k[B_{jk}(f_k, f_i) + A_{jk}(f_k^{\ast}, f_i)] = B_{ji}$. So $f_i = \sum_j(A_{ji}^{\ast}g_j - B_{ji}g_j^{\ast})$. Repeating Step 1 for this expansion, with the roles of $f$ and $g$ exchanged and $(A, B)$ replaced by $(A^\dagger, -B^T)$, gives $A^\dagger A - B^TB^{\ast} = \mathbb 1$ and $A^\dagger B = B^TA^{\ast}$. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 3** (operators). $\hat b_i = (g_i, \hat\phi)$ ([[#^thm-c14-1-3|Theorem §C14.1.3]], part 4). With $\hat\phi = \sum_j(\hat a_jf_j + \hat a_j^\dagger f_j^{\ast})$, and $(g_i, f_j) = A_{ij}^{\ast}$, $(g_i, f_j^{\ast}) = \sum_k B_{ik}^{\ast}(f_k^{\ast}, f_j^{\ast}) = -B_{ij}^{\ast}$:
> $$
> \hat b_i = \sum_j\bigl(A_{ij}^{\ast}\hat a_j - B_{ij}^{\ast}\hat a_j^\dagger\bigr) .
> $$
> Likewise $\hat a_i = (f_i, \hat\phi) = \sum_j[\hat b_j(f_i, g_j) + \hat b_j^\dagger(f_i, g_j^{\ast})]$ with $(f_i, g_j) = A_{ji}$ and $(f_i, g_j^{\ast}) = \sum_kB_{jk}^{\ast}(f_i, f_k) = B_{ji}^{\ast}$.
>
> **Step 4** (algebra of the $\hat b$'s). From $[\hat a_k, \hat a_l^\dagger] = \delta_{kl}$,
> $$
> [\hat b_i, \hat b_j^\dagger] = \sum_{k,l}\bigl[A_{ik}^{\ast}\hat a_k - B_{ik}^{\ast}\hat a_k^\dagger,\ A_{jl}\hat a_l^\dagger - B_{jl}\hat a_l\bigr] = \sum_k\bigl(A_{ik}^{\ast}A_{jk} - B_{ik}^{\ast}B_{jk}\bigr) = \delta_{ij}
> $$
> by Step 1, and $[\hat b_i, \hat b_j] = \sum_k(-A_{ik}^{\ast}B_{jk}^{\ast} + B_{ik}^{\ast}A_{jk}^{\ast}) = -(AB^T - BA^T)_{ij}^{\ast} = 0$. Part 3 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The identities are the orthonormality of the out-modes, nothing more; their operator form is the statement that the $\hat b$'s are again annihilators and creators of a Fock space. A transformation with $B \neq 0$ mixes annihilators with creators: that is the whole difference from a change of basis.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C14.1.5: Particle Creation: ⟨N_i⟩ = Σ_j|B_ij|²
> Let the field be in the in-vacuum $|0_{\rm in}\rangle$ ($\hat a_i|0_{\rm in}\rangle = 0$ for all $i$), and let $\hat N_i = \hat b_i^\dagger\hat b_i$ count out-particles in mode $g_i$ ([[#^def-c14-1-3|Def. §C14.1.3]]).
> 1. **Particle number.**
> $$
> \langle0_{\rm in}|\hat N_i|0_{\rm in}\rangle = \sum_j|B_{ij}|^2 .
> $$
> 2. **No mixing, no particles.** $|0_{\rm in}\rangle$ is the out-vacuum (up to a phase) if and only if $B = 0$.
>
> So an observer at rest in region $T$, who counts with the out-modes, finds particles in a state that region $B$ calls empty, whenever the geometry between them mixes positive with negative frequency. A changing spacetime creates particles.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]]; *applies:* exactly, free field, no back-reaction (the energy of the created particles is not taken from the geometry). The number is in general finite mode by mode; whether the total $\sum_{i,j}|B_{ij}|^2$ is finite decides whether $|0_{\rm in}\rangle$ is a state of the out-Fock space at all ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^rem-c2a-2-4|QFT §C2a.2, ★ Remark: Other mode functions, Bogoliubov transformations and the vacuum]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §9.3, eq. (9.61) (p. 161) · Carroll, §9.4, eqs. (9.110)–(9.111) (pp. 398–399) · checked by script in a two-mode Fock space*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (act with $\hat b_i$). By [[#^thm-c14-1-4|Theorem §C14.1.4]], part 3, and $\hat a_j|0_{\rm in}\rangle = 0$,
> $$
> \hat b_i|0_{\rm in}\rangle = \sum_j\bigl(A_{ij}^{\ast}\hat a_j - B_{ij}^{\ast}\hat a_j^\dagger\bigr)|0_{\rm in}\rangle = -\sum_jB_{ij}^{\ast}\,\hat a_j^\dagger|0_{\rm in}\rangle .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (norm). $\langle0_{\rm in}|\hat N_i|0_{\rm in}\rangle = \|\hat b_i|0_{\rm in}\rangle\|^2$, and with $\hat a_j\hat a_k^\dagger = \delta_{jk} + \hat a_k^\dagger\hat a_j$,
> $$
> \|\hat b_i|0_{\rm in}\rangle\|^2 = \sum_{j,k}B_{ij}B_{ik}^{\ast}\langle0_{\rm in}|\hat a_j\hat a_k^\dagger|0_{\rm in}\rangle = \sum_{j,k}B_{ij}B_{ik}^{\ast}\,\delta_{jk} = \sum_j|B_{ij}|^2 .
> $$
> Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 3** (part 2). If $B = 0$, Step 1 gives $\hat b_i|0_{\rm in}\rangle = 0$ for every $i$, and the vacuum of a Fock space is unique up to a phase ([[§C2a.3 Energy, Momentum and the Zero-Point Energy#^pr-c2a-3-2|QFT Principle §C2a.3.2]]). Conversely, if $|0_{\rm in}\rangle$ is the out-vacuum, every $\langle\hat N_i\rangle$ vanishes, so $\sum_j|B_{ij}|^2 = 0$ for each $i$ and $B = 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Only $B$ enters: the in-vacuum looks occupied to the out-observer because her annihilator contains in-creators. The created quanta come in pairs, since the in-vacuum is reached from the out-vacuum by operators quadratic in $\hat b^\dagger$ ([[#^ex-c14-1-2|Example §C14.1.2]]).

## Why flat space hid the problem

The opening promised the flat-space reason that the question "which vacuum?" never came up in Quantum Field Theory. It is the case $B = 0$ of the last theorem, for two inertial frames.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C14.1.6: ⚑ In Minkowski Space the Vacuum Is the Same for All Inertial Observers
> In Minkowski spacetime let $\tilde x^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu + a^\mu$ with $\Lambda$ an orthochronous Lorentz transformation, $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$. Let $\psi_{\mathbf p}(x) = N_{\mathbf p}e^{ip_\mu x^\mu}$, $N_{\mathbf p} = (2p^0)^{-1/2}(2\pi)^{-3/2}$, $p^0 = \sqrt{\mathbf p^2 + m^2}$, be the positive-frequency modes of the frame $x$ (normalized to $\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$), and $\tilde\psi_{\mathbf p}(x) = N_{\mathbf p}e^{ip_\mu\tilde x^\mu}$ those of the frame $\tilde x$.
> 1. **Positive frequency is frame-independent.** $\tilde\psi_{\mathbf p} = e^{ip_\mu a^\mu}\bigl(\tilde p^0/p^0\bigr)^{1/2}\psi_{\tilde{\mathbf p}}$ with $\tilde p_\nu = p_\mu\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$, and $\tilde p^0 > 0$. So $\mathcal S^+_{\partial_{\tilde t}} = \mathcal S^+_{\partial_t}$.
> 2. **No mixing.** The Bogoliubov coefficients between the two frames have $B = 0$, and $\tilde a_{\mathbf p} = e^{-ip_\mu a^\mu}(\tilde p^0/p^0)^{1/2}\,a_{\tilde{\mathbf p}}$.
> 3. **One vacuum.** $a_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle = 0$ for all $\mathbf p$ implies $\tilde a_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle = 0$ for all $\mathbf p$, and conversely ([[#^thm-c14-1-5|Theorem §C14.1.5]], part 2).
> 4. **Only orthochronous maps.** A time-reversing $\Lambda$ ($\Lambda^0{}_0 \le -1$) gives $\tilde p^0 < 0$: it exchanges positive- and negative-frequency modes.
>
> The frequency of a given mode does depend on the frame: under a boost with velocity $v$ along $x$, $\omega' = \gamma(\omega - vk)$, which stays positive (Carroll (9.77)). What every inertial observer agrees on is its sign. A curved spacetime has no Lorentz transformations, and even Minkowski space has timelike Killing vectors on parts of it that are not inertial time translations; the boost Killing vector of the Rindler wedge ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]]) is the one taken up in §C14.2 ([[§C14.2 The Unruh Effect#^def-c14-2-1|Def. §C14.2.1]]).
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]] on Minkowski spacetime; *applies:* exactly, $m \ge 0$ (for $m = 0$ the mode $\mathbf p = 0$ is excluded); proper or improper orthochronous $\Lambda$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §9.2, eqs. (9.38)–(9.48) (pp. 158–159) · Carroll, §9.3, eqs. (9.74)–(9.77) (pp. 390–391) · checked by script on 2000 random orthochronous transformations*

^thm-c14-1-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the phase). $p_\mu\tilde x^\mu = p_\mu\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu + p_\mu a^\mu = \tilde p_\nu x^\nu + p_\mu a^\mu$ with $\tilde p_\nu \equiv p_\mu\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$. As a vector, $\tilde p^\nu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu\,p^\mu$: lowering with $\eta$ and $\eta\Lambda^T\eta = \Lambda^{-1}$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, the relation is the same in either signature).
>
> **Step 2** (mass shell and sign). $\tilde p\cdot\tilde p = p\cdot p = -m^2$, so $\tilde p$ lies on the mass shell, and $\Lambda^{-1}$ is orthochronous with $\Lambda$ (the orthochronous transformations form a group, [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]], part 3). An orthochronous transformation maps future-pointing timelike or null vectors to future-pointing ones ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]], part 2; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), and $p$ is future-pointing, so $\tilde p^0 > 0$ and $\tilde p^0 = \sqrt{\tilde{\mathbf p}^2 + m^2}$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 1). By Step 1, $\tilde\psi_{\mathbf p} = N_{\mathbf p}e^{ip\cdot a}e^{i\tilde p_\nu x^\nu} = e^{ip\cdot a}(N_{\mathbf p}/N_{\tilde{\mathbf p}})\,\psi_{\tilde{\mathbf p}}$ and $N_{\mathbf p}/N_{\tilde{\mathbf p}} = (\tilde p^0/p^0)^{1/2}$. Each new mode is a multiple of one old positive-frequency mode; the map $\mathbf p \mapsto \tilde{\mathbf p}$ is onto (apply the argument to $\Lambda^{-1}$), so the two positive-frequency subspaces coincide.
>
> **Step 4** (part 2). With $c = e^{ip\cdot a}(\tilde p^0/p^0)^{1/2}$ and $\hat\phi = \int d^3q\,(a_{\mathbf q}\psi_{\mathbf q} + a_{\mathbf q}^\dagger\psi_{\mathbf q}^{\ast})$ (Couzens (9.39)), the products $(\psi_{\tilde{\mathbf p}}, \psi_{\mathbf q}) = \delta^3(\tilde{\mathbf p} - \mathbf q)$ and $(\psi_{\tilde{\mathbf p}}, \psi_{\mathbf q}^{\ast}) = 0$, identities in $\mathcal S'$ in $\mathbf q$ ([[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-3|QFT Theorem §C2a.2.3]], with the $(2\pi)^3$ absorbed into $N_{\mathbf p}$; [[§CA.4 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-4-3|QFT Theorem §CA.4.3]]), give
> $$
> \tilde a_{\mathbf p} = (\tilde\psi_{\mathbf p}, \hat\phi) = c^{\ast}\int d^3q\,\bigl[a_{\mathbf q}\,\delta^3(\tilde{\mathbf p} - \mathbf q) + a_{\mathbf q}^\dagger\cdot0\bigr] = e^{-ip\cdot a}\bigl(\tilde p^0/p^0\bigr)^{1/2}a_{\tilde{\mathbf p}} .
> $$
> No $a^\dagger$ appears: $B = 0$. As a check of normalization, $[\tilde a_{\mathbf p}, \tilde a_{\mathbf q}^\dagger] = (\tilde p^0/p^0)\,\delta^3(\tilde{\mathbf p} - \tilde{\mathbf q}) = \delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$, because $p^0\delta^3(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)$ is Lorentz invariant ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^thm-c2a-4-4|QFT Theorem §C2a.4.4]]).
>
> **Step 5** (parts 3–4). Part 3 follows from part 2 and [[#^thm-c14-1-5|Theorem §C14.1.5]], part 2. For part 4, the same Step 2 with $\Lambda^0{}_0 \le -1$ maps future-pointing $p$ to past-pointing $\tilde p$ ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]], part 2), so $e^{i\tilde p_\nu x^\nu}$ has $\partial_t = +i|\tilde p^0|$: negative frequency.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Uniqueness of the flat-space vacuum rests on a group of isometries that maps one observer's time translation into another's while preserving the future light cone. Positive frequency is a property of the subspace $\mathcal S^+$, and the Poincaré group preserves that subspace.

> [!example] Example §C14.1.1: A Sandwich Spacetime: Particle Creation by an Expanding Universe
> *Setup.* Two-dimensional spacetime $M = \mathbf R \times S^1$, $x \sim x + L$, with metric $ds^2 = C(\eta)\,(-d\eta^2 + dx^2)$, conformal factor $C(\eta) = A + B\tanh\rho\eta$, $A > B > 0$, $\rho > 0$: a closed one-dimensional universe whose size $\sqrt C\,L$ grows from $\sqrt{A - B}\,L$ to $\sqrt{A + B}\,L$ during a conformal time of about $1/\rho$. Each slice $\eta = \text{const}$ is a Cauchy surface: a causal curve has $|dx/d\eta| \le 1$, so $\eta$ increases strictly along it and an inextendible one takes every value of $\eta$ once. For $\eta \to \mp\infty$ the metric tends to the flat metrics $(A \mp B)(-d\eta^2 + dx^2)$ with Killing vector $\partial_\eta$: the in- and out-regions $B$ and $T$. A scalar field of mass $m$ ([[#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]]).
>
> **Step 1** (mode equation). $\sqrt{-g} = C$, $g^{\eta\eta} = -1/C$, $g^{xx} = 1/C$, so $\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1)$ and ([[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]])
> $$
> \Box\phi = \frac1C\bigl(-\partial_\eta^2\phi + \partial_x^2\phi\bigr) = m^2\phi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \partial_\eta^2\phi - \partial_x^2\phi + m^2C(\eta)\,\phi = 0 .
> $$
> With $\phi = e^{ikx}\chi(\eta)/\sqrt L$, $k = 2\pi n/L$, $n \in \mathbf Z$:
> $$
> \chi'' + \omega_k(\eta)^2\chi = 0, \qquad \omega_k(\eta)^2 = k^2 + m^2C(\eta) :
> $$
> one oscillator per $k$, with a frequency that changes in time.
>
> **Step 2** (normalization). $\pi_f = -\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\eta\eta}\partial_\eta f = \partial_\eta f$, so for $f_k = e^{ikx}\chi_k/\sqrt L$ ([[#^def-c14-1-1|Def. §C14.1.1]])
> $$
> (f_k, f_{k'}) = \frac iL\int_0^Ldx\,e^{i(k' - k)x}\bigl(\chi_k^{\ast}\chi_{k'}' - \chi_{k'}\chi_k^{\ast\prime}\bigr) = \delta_{kk'}\,W[\chi_k], \qquad W[\chi] = i\bigl(\chi^{\ast}\chi' - \chi\chi^{\ast\prime}\bigr) .
> $$
> $W$ is constant: $W' = i(\chi^{\ast}\chi'' - \chi\chi^{\ast\prime\prime}) = i(-\omega^2 + \omega^2)|\chi|^2 = 0$ ([[#^thm-c14-1-2|Theorem §C14.1.2]] for this case). A unit-norm positive-frequency mode in a flat region of frequency $\omega$ is $\chi = e^{-i\omega\eta}/\sqrt{2\omega}$: $W = i(-i\omega - i\omega)/(2\omega) = 1$.
>
> **Step 3** (in, out and the coefficients). $\omega_{\rm in} = \sqrt{k^2 + m^2(A - B)}$, $\omega_{\rm out} = \sqrt{k^2 + m^2(A + B)}$. The in-mode is the solution with $\chi^{\rm in}_k \to e^{-i\omega_{\rm in}\eta}/\sqrt{2\omega_{\rm in}}$ as $\eta \to -\infty$; the out-mode has $\chi^{\rm out}_k \to e^{-i\omega_{\rm out}\eta}/\sqrt{2\omega_{\rm out}}$ as $\eta \to +\infty$. The equation is linear and of second order, so $\chi^{\rm in}_k = \alpha_k\chi^{\rm out}_k + \beta_k\chi^{\rm out\,\ast}_k$. Since $e^{ikx}\chi^{\rm out\,\ast}_k = (e^{-ikx}\chi^{\rm out}_k)^{\ast}$ and $\omega$ depends on $|k|$ only,
> $$
> f^{\rm in}_k = \alpha_k\,g_k + \beta_k\,g_{-k}^{\ast} .
> $$
> Compared with the inverse of [[#^thm-c14-1-4|Theorem §C14.1.4]], part 2, $f_k = \sum_j(A_{jk}^{\ast}g_j - B_{jk}g_j^{\ast})$: $A_{kk} = \alpha_k^{\ast}$, $B_{-k,k} = -\beta_k$, all other entries zero. Constancy of $W$ gives $1 = W[\chi^{\rm in}] = |\alpha_k|^2W[\chi^{\rm out}] - |\beta_k|^2W[\chi^{\rm out}] = |\alpha_k|^2 - |\beta_k|^2$ (the cross terms cancel), the identity of Theorem §C14.1.4, part 1. By [[#^thm-c14-1-5|Theorem §C14.1.5]] the out-observer counts
> $$
> \langle N_k\rangle = |\beta_k|^2
> $$
> in mode $k$, and as many in mode $-k$: the particles are created in pairs of opposite momentum, and the total momentum stays zero.
>
> **Step 4** (sudden limit, $\rho \to \infty$). $C$ jumps from $A - B$ to $A + B$ at $\eta = 0$. Integrating the mode equation over $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$ gives $\chi'(\varepsilon) - \chi'(-\varepsilon) = -\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}\omega^2\chi\,d\eta \to 0$, so $\chi$ and $\chi'$ are continuous. With $\chi = e^{-i\omega_{\rm in}\eta}/\sqrt{2\omega_{\rm in}}$ for $\eta < 0$ and $(\alpha e^{-i\omega_{\rm out}\eta} + \beta e^{i\omega_{\rm out}\eta})/\sqrt{2\omega_{\rm out}}$ for $\eta > 0$, matching at $\eta = 0$:
> $$
> \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\rm in}}} = \frac{\alpha + \beta}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\rm out}}}, \qquad \frac{-i\omega_{\rm in}}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\rm in}}} = \frac{-i\omega_{\rm out}(\alpha - \beta)}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\rm out}}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha + \beta = \sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\rm out}}{\omega_{\rm in}}}, \quad \alpha - \beta = \sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\rm in}}{\omega_{\rm out}}} ,
> $$
> $$
> \alpha = \frac{\omega_{\rm out} + \omega_{\rm in}}{2\sqrt{\omega_{\rm in}\omega_{\rm out}}}, \qquad \beta = \frac{\omega_{\rm out} - \omega_{\rm in}}{2\sqrt{\omega_{\rm in}\omega_{\rm out}}}, \qquad |\beta|^2 = \frac{(\omega_{\rm out} - \omega_{\rm in})^2}{4\,\omega_{\rm in}\omega_{\rm out}} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (smooth expansion). For the tanh profile the mode equation is solved exactly by hypergeometric functions (Bernard and Duncan, *Ann. Phys.* 107 (1977) 201; Birrell and Davies, *Quantum Fields in Curved Space* (1982), §3.4; not in the typed sources), with
> $$
> |\beta_k|^2 = \frac{\sinh^2(\pi\omega_-/\rho)}{\sinh(\pi\omega_{\rm in}/\rho)\,\sinh(\pi\omega_{\rm out}/\rho)}, \qquad \omega_\pm = \tfrac12(\omega_{\rm out} \pm \omega_{\rm in}) ;
> $$
> the formula was checked here by integrating the mode equation numerically. For $\rho \to \infty$, $\sinh z \approx z$ gives $\omega_-^2/(\omega_{\rm in}\omega_{\rm out})$, the sudden result of Step 4. For $\rho \to 0$, $\sinh z \approx e^z/2$ gives $|\beta_k|^2 \approx e^{2\pi\omega_-/\rho - \pi(\omega_{\rm in} + \omega_{\rm out})/\rho} = e^{-2\pi\omega_{\rm in}/\rho}$.
>
> **Step 6** (numbers). $A = 2$, $B = 1$, $m = k = 1$: $\omega_{\rm in} = \sqrt2 = 1.4142$, $\omega_{\rm out} = 2$.
>
> | $\rho$ | $0.25$ | $0.5$ | $1$ | $2$ | $5$ | $20$ | $\infty$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $\lvert\beta_k\rvert^2$ | $3.66\times10^{-16}$ | $1.82\times10^{-8}$ | $9.79\times10^{-5}$ | $4.31\times10^{-3}$ | $0.0210$ | $0.0296$ | $0.0303$ |
>
> The sudden value is $(2 - 1.4142)^2/(4 \times 1.4142 \times 2) = 0.3431/11.314 = 0.0303$.
>
> ![[ph-rel-c14-1-1.svg]]
> *(a) The conformal factor for a slow and a fast expansion. (b) The modulus of the in-mode, computed from the mode equation. Before the expansion both modes are pure positive frequency, $|\chi| = 1/\sqrt{2\omega_{\rm in}}$. After a slow expansion the modulus settles at $1/\sqrt{2\omega_{\rm out}}$: still pure positive frequency, no particles. After a fast one, $|\chi|^2 = (|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 + 2|\alpha\beta|\cos(2\omega_{\rm out}\eta + \theta))/2\omega_{\rm out}$ beats: the $e^{+i\omega_{\rm out}\eta}$ admixture is visible. (Not from the sources; figure added, vault only.)*
>
> *Reading.* The expansion creates particles when it is fast on the scale of the mode period, $\rho \gtrsim \omega$; a slow expansion leaves the vacuum empty up to $e^{-2\pi\omega_{\rm in}/\rho}$, exponentially small (an adiabatic invariance). A sudden jump creates particles in every mode, with a number that falls only as a power of the frequency. An exponential $e^{-2\pi\omega/(\text{rate})}$ of the same form reappears in the Hawking spectrum, with the rate set by the surface gravity ([[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^thm-c14-3-4|§C14.3]]).
>

> [!example] Example §C14.1.2: The Harmonic Oscillator with a Sudden Frequency Change
> *Setup.* One degree of freedom with $H(t) = \frac12\hat p^2 + \frac12\omega(t)^2\hat x^2$, $[\hat x, \hat p] = i$, $\omega = \omega_1$ for $t < 0$ and $\omega_2$ for $t > 0$; the system starts in the ground state of $H_1$. Heisenberg picture. This is one mode of [[#^ex-c14-1-1|Example §C14.1.1]] in the sudden limit, with $\eta \to t$ and the spatial factor dropped.
>
> **Step 1** (mode functions). The Heisenberg equations $\dot{\hat x} = \hat p$, $\dot{\hat p} = -\omega(t)^2\hat x$ give $\ddot{\hat x} + \omega(t)^2\hat x = 0$, the mode equation. For $t < 0$, $\hat x(t) = \hat a\,\chi_1(t) + \hat a^\dagger\chi_1^{\ast}(t)$ with $\chi_1 = e^{-i\omega_1t}/\sqrt{2\omega_1}$ and the ladder operators of $H_1$ ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-2|QM Theorem §B4.1.2]]); for $t > 0$, $\hat x(t) = \hat b\,\chi_2(t) + \hat b^\dagger\chi_2^{\ast}(t)$ with the ladder operators of $H_2$. $W[\chi_1] = W[\chi_2] = 1$ (Example §C14.1.1, Step 2) is $[\hat a, \hat a^\dagger] = [\hat b, \hat b^\dagger] = 1$.
>
> **Step 2** (matching). $\hat x$ and $\hat p = \dot{\hat x}$ are continuous at $t = 0$, since $\dot{\hat p}$ is bounded. The classical solution $\chi_1$ continues as $\alpha\chi_2 + \beta\chi_2^{\ast}$ with the real $\alpha$, $\beta$ of Example §C14.1.1, Step 4 ($\omega_{\rm in} \to \omega_1$, $\omega_{\rm out} \to \omega_2$). So for $t > 0$
> $$
> \hat x(t) = \hat a(\alpha\chi_2 + \beta\chi_2^{\ast}) + \hat a^\dagger(\alpha\chi_2^{\ast} + \beta\chi_2) = (\alpha\hat a + \beta\hat a^\dagger)\chi_2 + (\alpha\hat a^\dagger + \beta\hat a)\chi_2^{\ast} ,
> $$
> and $\hat b = \alpha\hat a + \beta\hat a^\dagger$. In the form of [[#^thm-c14-1-4|Theorem §C14.1.4]], part 3, $\hat b = A^{\ast}\hat a - B^{\ast}\hat a^\dagger$: $A = \alpha$, $B = -\beta$, and $\alpha^2 - \beta^2 = 1$ is part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (particles and energy). [[#^thm-c14-1-5|Theorem §C14.1.5]]: $\langle0_{\rm in}|\hat b^\dagger\hat b|0_{\rm in}\rangle = \beta^2 = (\omega_2 - \omega_1)^2/(4\omega_1\omega_2)$. Directly: in the ground state of $H_1$, $\langle\hat x^2\rangle = 1/(2\omega_1)$ and $\langle\hat p^2\rangle = \omega_1/2$, so
> $$
> \langle H_2\rangle = \frac{\omega_1}{4} + \frac{\omega_2^2}{4\omega_1} = \frac{\omega_1^2 + \omega_2^2}{4\omega_1} = \omega_2\Bigl(\beta^2 + \frac12\Bigr) ,
> $$
> the energy of $\beta^2$ quanta above the new ground state. It is supplied by whatever changes the spring constant, as the expanding geometry supplies it in Example §C14.1.1.
>
> **Step 4** (the state: a squeezed vacuum). Inverting with $\alpha^2 - \beta^2 = 1$: $\hat a = \alpha\hat b - \beta\hat b^\dagger$ (check: $\alpha(\alpha\hat a + \beta\hat a^\dagger) - \beta(\alpha\hat a^\dagger + \beta\hat a) = \hat a$). Try $|0_{\rm in}\rangle = \mathcal N e^{\lambda\hat b^{\dagger2}/2}|0_{\rm out}\rangle$. From $[\hat b, \hat b^{\dagger2}] = 2\hat b^\dagger$, $\hat b\,e^{\lambda\hat b^{\dagger2}/2}|0_{\rm out}\rangle = \lambda\hat b^\dagger e^{\lambda\hat b^{\dagger2}/2}|0_{\rm out}\rangle$, so
> $$
> \hat a|0_{\rm in}\rangle = (\alpha\lambda - \beta)\,\hat b^\dagger|0_{\rm in}\rangle = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lambda = \beta/\alpha .
> $$
> Expanding the exponential with $(\hat b^\dagger)^{2n}|0\rangle = \sqrt{(2n)!}\,|2n\rangle$,
> $$
> |0_{\rm in}\rangle = \mathcal N\sum_{n=0}^\infty\Bigl(\frac\lambda2\Bigr)^n\frac{\sqrt{(2n)!}}{n!}\,|2n\rangle_{\rm out}, \qquad |\mathcal N|^{-2} = \sum_n\binom{2n}{n}\Bigl(\frac{\lambda^2}{4}\Bigr)^n = (1 - \lambda^2)^{-1/2} = \alpha ,
> $$
> using the binomial series $(1 - z)^{-1/2} = \sum_n\binom{2n}{n}(z/4)^n$ and $1 - \beta^2/\alpha^2 = 1/\alpha^2$. Only even numbers occur: the quanta come in pairs, made by the operator $\hat b^{\dagger2}$.
>
> **Step 5** (numbers). $\omega_1 = 1 \to \omega_2 = 3$: $\alpha = 4/(2\sqrt3) = 1.1547$, $\beta = 2/(2\sqrt3) = 0.5774$, $\beta^2 = 1/3$, $\lambda = 1/2$. $P(0) = 1/\alpha = 0.866$, $P(2) = (1/\alpha)\cdot2\cdot(1/16) = 0.108$, $P(4) = (1/\alpha)\cdot6\cdot(1/256) = 0.020$. $\langle H_2\rangle = 10/4 = 2.5$, against the new ground energy $1.5$: one extra unit $= \omega_2\beta^2$.
>
> *Reading.* A force that pushes the oscillator, a term linear in $\hat x$, produces a coherent state, with every number of quanta and Poisson statistics ([[§C2b.9 Particle Production by a Classical Source#^thm-c2b-9-3|QFT Theorem §C2b.9.3]]; [[§C2a.6 Coherent States and the Classical Field#^thm-c2a-6-6|QFT Theorem §C2a.6.6]]). A change of the frequency, a term quadratic in $\hat x$, produces pairs. A curved background enters the field equation only through such quadratic terms, so gravity creates particles in pairs.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §9.1, eqs. (9.14)–(9.22) (pp. 155–156; the oscillator, with (9.16) and (9.21) corrected) · Carroll, §9.2, eqs. (9.43)–(9.45) (p. 385; the transient perturbation and in/out states, qualitatively) · numbers checked by script (`verify_c14_1.py`)*
