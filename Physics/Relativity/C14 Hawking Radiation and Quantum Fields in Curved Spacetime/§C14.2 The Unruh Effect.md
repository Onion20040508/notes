[[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background|§C14.1]] defined particles by positive frequency with respect to a timelike Killing vector ([[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^def-c14-1-2|Def. §C14.1.2]]) and showed that two such definitions related by a Bogoliubov transformation disagree about the vacuum, by $\langle N_i\rangle = \sum_j|B_{ij}|^2$ ([[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^thm-c14-1-5|Theorem §C14.1.5]]). In Minkowski spacetime all inertial observers agree ([[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^thm-c14-1-6|Theorem §C14.1.6]]). Minkowski spacetime has one more family of observers that move along the orbits of a Killing vector: the uniformly accelerated observers of [[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^mod-c10-2-1|Model §C10.2.1]], whose clocks run along the boost field. The question of this section: what does such an observer find in the Minkowski vacuum?

The naive answer is "nothing". At each instant the observer is at rest in some inertial frame, and every inertial frame sees $|0_M\rangle$ empty. Test this on a single wave. Along the observer of proper acceleration $a = 1/\xi_0$, $u = t - x = -\xi_0e^{-\tau/\xi_0}$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]]), so a right-moving positive-frequency Minkowski wave $e^{-i\omega u}$ ($\omega > 0$) reads, as a function of the observer's proper time,

$$
\varphi(\tau) = \exp\bigl(i\omega\xi_0e^{-\tau/\xi_0}\bigr), \qquad \Omega_{\text{inst}}(\tau) = \omega\,\frac{du}{d\tau} = \omega e^{-\tau/\xi_0} > 0 .
$$

Its instantaneous frequency is positive at every moment, only exponentially redshifted, as the light of the lamp in [[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^ex-c10-2-3|Example §C10.2.3]]. But a detector responds to the Fourier components of the whole history, $\varphi(\tau) = \int d\Omega\,c(\Omega)\,e^{-i\Omega\tau}$. With $s = \omega\xi_0e^{-\tau/\xi_0}$, $d\tau = -\xi_0\,ds/s$ and $e^{i\Omega\tau} = (s/\omega\xi_0)^{-i\Omega\xi_0}$,

$$
c(\Omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\tau\,e^{i\Omega\tau}\varphi(\tau) = \frac{\xi_0}{2\pi}(\omega\xi_0)^{i\Omega\xi_0}\int_0^\infty ds\,s^{-i\Omega\xi_0 - 1}e^{is} = \frac{\xi_0}{2\pi}(\omega\xi_0)^{i\Omega\xi_0}\,\Gamma(-i\Omega\xi_0)\,e^{\pi\Omega\xi_0/2},
$$

using $\int_0^\infty s^{z - 1}e^{is}\,ds = \Gamma(z)e^{i\pi z/2}$ (the Gamma function of [[§CA.6 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^def-ca-6-1|QFT Def. §CA.6.1]] with the contour rotated to the imaginary axis; at $\operatorname{Re}z = 0$, $z \neq 0$, as an Abel limit, checked by quadrature). For $-\Omega$ the same steps give $\Gamma(i\Omega\xi_0)e^{-\pi\Omega\xi_0/2}$, and $|\Gamma(iy)| = |\Gamma(-iy)|$, so

$$
\frac{|c(-\Omega)|^2}{|c(\Omega)|^2} = e^{-2\pi\Omega\xi_0} = e^{-2\pi\Omega/a} .
$$

The wave that every inertial observer calls positive-frequency contains, in the accelerated observer's time, negative frequencies with exactly a Boltzmann weight at temperature $a/2\pi$. The naive argument fails because "positive frequency" is fixed by a clock over all time, and the accelerated observer's clock is the boost parameter, not $t$ of any one inertial frame. This section turns the computation into a theorem. It sets up the modes that are positive-frequency for the boost field in the two wedges where it is timelike ([[#^def-c14-2-1|Def. §C14.2.1]]) and their vacuum ([[#^def-c14-2-2|Def. §C14.2.2]]), finds by analytic continuation the combinations of them that are positive-frequency for Minkowski time ([[#^thm-c14-2-1|Theorem §C14.2.1]]), reads off the occupation numbers and the full state, a thermofield double whose restriction to one wedge is thermal ([[#^thm-c14-2-2|Theorem §C14.2.2]]), and finds the temperature measured at each height ([[#^thm-c14-2-3|Theorem §C14.2.3]]).

**Setup.** A free massless real scalar field in two-dimensional Minkowski spacetime, $\Box\phi = 0$ ([[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]] with $m = 0$ on $\mathbf R^2$, which is globally hyperbolic with Cauchy surface $\Sigma = \{t = 0\}$: [[§C11.4 Cauchy Surfaces and Cauchy Horizons#^thm-c11-4-1|Theorem §C11.4.1]]), in its Minkowski vacuum $|0_M\rangle$. Two dimensions and $m = 0$ make the field equation conformally invariant, so in the Rindler chart it is the flat wave equation again; the four-dimensional statement is cited, not derived ([[#^ex-c14-2-2|Example §C14.2.2]]). Observers are the test observers of [[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^mod-c10-2-1|Model §C10.2.1]], accelerated forever; their detectors are idealised as reading the particle number of the Rindler modes. Mode labels $k$ are continuous with $\delta$-normalisation ([[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^thm-c14-1-3|Theorem §C14.1.3]]); statements about states and occupation per mode use discrete labels (a periodic box of length $L_\zeta$ in the coordinate $\zeta$ below, or wave packets, [[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^def-c2a-4-3|QFT Def. §C2a.4.3]]), where $\delta(k - k') \to (L_\zeta/2\pi)\,\delta_{nn'}$ ([[§CA.4 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-4-10|QFT Theorem §CA.4.10]]). Couzens' letters: his $a$ is $1/\xi_0$ here, his $(\eta, \xi)$ are $(\xi_0\eta, \xi_0\zeta)$, his $\omega/a$ is $\nu$, his $\alpha$ (§9.4) is $1/\xi$, his regions I and IV are $R$ and $L$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^cau-c10-2-1|§C10.2, Caution: Couzens' α]]).

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $\hbar = c = k_B = 1$ in derivations, restored in every measurable result ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^rem-c1-2-1|§C1.2, Remark: Why not ħ as well]]; the exponent rule of [[§C1a.2 Natural Units and Dimensional Analysis#^thm-c1a-2-1|QFT Theorem §C1a.2.1]]; here $T = a/2\pi \to \hbar a/2\pi ck_B$); light-cone coordinates $u = t - x$, $v = t + x$; Rindler chart $(\eta, \xi)$ and observer label $\xi_0$ as in [[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]]; the Klein–Gordon inner product $(f, g)$ is antilinear in $f$ ([[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^def-c14-1-1|Def. §C14.1.1]], Couzens (9.37)). Links into Quantum Field Theory: signature $+{-}{-}{-}$ and $\hbar = c = 1$ there; mode functions, inner products and commutators carry over unchanged in form (the mode algebra used from there does not depend on the signature), and the math chapter CA is signature-free.

## The Rindler modes and the Rindler vacuum

The boost field $K = x\,\partial_t + t\,\partial_x$ is the Rindler time translation $\partial_\eta$ on the right wedge ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]], part 4). Positive frequency needs a future-directed timelike Killing vector, and $K$ is one only on $R$; on the left wedge it is timelike but points to the past, and on $F$, $P$ it is spacelike (Figure (a) below). The Cauchy surface $t = 0$ consists of the two half-lines $\Sigma_R = \{t = 0, x > 0\}$ and $\Sigma_L = \{t = 0, x < 0\}$ and the bifurcation point, so modes on the two wedges together carry all Cauchy data.

> [!definition] Definition §C14.2.1: Rindler Modes in the Right and Left Wedges
> *Structure.* $\mathcal S$ is the complex vector space of solutions of $\Box\phi = 0$ on two-dimensional Minkowski spacetime with the Klein–Gordon form $(f, g)$ on $\Sigma = \{t = 0\}$ ([[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^def-c14-1-1|Def. §C14.1.1]]). The boost field $K = x\,\partial_t + t\,\partial_x$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^ex-c10-5-2|Example §C10.5.2]]) is a Killing vector that is future-timelike on $R = \{x > |t|\}$, past-timelike on $L = \{x < -|t|\}$, spacelike on $F$ and $P$, and zero at the origin. The **Rindler modes** are the elements of $\mathcal S$ that are positive-frequency ([[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^def-c14-1-2|Def. §C14.1.2]]) with respect to the future-directed field, $K$ on $R$ and $-K$ on $L$, and vanish on the other half of $\Sigma$.
>
> *Charts.* On $R$, $t = \xi\sinh\eta$, $x = \xi\cosh\eta$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^def-c10-2-1|Def. §C10.2.1]]); on $L$, $t = -\xi\sinh\eta$, $x = -\xi\cosh\eta$ with $\xi > 0$ (§C10.2's "same formulas with $\xi < 0$"). On both, $K = \partial_\eta$ (its components $(\partial t/\partial\eta, \partial x/\partial\eta)$ are $(x, t)$ on each). With a reference observer $\xi_0$ and $\zeta = \ln(\xi/\xi_0) \in \mathbf R$,
>
> $$
> ds^2 = \xi_0^2e^{2\zeta}\bigl(-d\eta^2 + d\zeta^2\bigr), \qquad \Box\phi = \frac{1}{\xi_0^2e^{2\zeta}}\bigl(-\partial_\eta^2 + \partial_\zeta^2\bigr)\phi
> $$
>
> on each wedge ([[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]] with $\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1)$), and the light-cone coordinates are
>
> $$
> R:\ u = -\xi_0e^{\zeta - \eta},\ \ v = \xi_0e^{\zeta + \eta}; \qquad L:\ u = \xi_0e^{\zeta - \eta},\ \ v = -\xi_0e^{\zeta + \eta} .
> $$
>
> *The modes.* For $k \in \mathbf R\setminus\{0\}$ and $\nu = |k|$, the frequency per unit $\eta$,
>
> $$
> g^R_k = \begin{cases}\dfrac{e^{-i\nu\eta + ik\zeta}}{\sqrt{4\pi\nu}} & \text{on } R\\[4pt] 0 & \text{on } L\end{cases}, \qquad g^L_k = \begin{cases}0 & \text{on } R\\[2pt] \dfrac{e^{+i\nu\eta + ik\zeta}}{\sqrt{4\pi\nu}} & \text{on } L\end{cases},
> $$
>
> so that $i(-K)g^L_k = \nu\,g^L_k$ on $L$ as $iK g^R_k = \nu\,g^R_k$ on $R$. In light-cone form, for $k > 0$,
>
> $$
> \sqrt{4\pi\nu}\,g^R_k = \Bigl(\frac{-u}{\xi_0}\Bigr)^{i\nu}, \quad \sqrt{4\pi\nu}\,g^R_{-k} = \Bigl(\frac{v}{\xi_0}\Bigr)^{-i\nu}, \quad \sqrt{4\pi\nu}\,g^L_k = \Bigl(\frac{-v}{\xi_0}\Bigr)^{i\nu}, \quad \sqrt{4\pi\nu}\,g^L_{-k} = \Bigl(\frac{u}{\xi_0}\Bigr)^{-i\nu},
> $$
>
> each on the half-plane where its base is positive and zero on the other half. As solutions on all of spacetime, $g^R_k$ ($k > 0$) is a function of $u$ alone and lives on $u < 0$, i.e. on $R$ and $P$; its phase fronts $u = -\xi_0e^{-2\pi n/\nu}$ pile up at the horizon $u = 0$ (Figure (b)).
>
> *Normalisation.* On $\eta = \text{const}$ the future unit normal times the length element is $\partial_\eta\,d\zeta$ on $R$ and $-\partial_\eta\,d\zeta$ on $L$ (the conformal factors cancel), so $(f, g) = i\int d\zeta\,(f^\ast\partial_\eta g - g\,\partial_\eta f^\ast)$ on $\Sigma_R$, the same with $-\partial_\eta$ on $\Sigma_L$. With $\int d\zeta\,e^{i(k' - k)\zeta} = 2\pi\delta(k - k')$ ([[§CA.4 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-4-3|QFT Theorem §CA.4.3]]),
>
> $$
> (g^R_k, g^R_{k'}) = i\,\frac{-i\nu' - i\nu}{4\pi\sqrt{\nu\nu'}}\,2\pi\delta(k - k') = \delta(k - k'), \qquad (g^I_k, g^J_{k'}) = \delta^{IJ}\delta(k - k'), \quad (g^{I\ast}_k, g^{J\ast}_{k'}) = -\delta^{IJ}\delta(k - k'), \quad (g^I_k, g^{J\ast}_{k'}) = 0,
> $$
>
> for $I, J \in \{R, L\}$; different wedges are orthogonal because their data live on disjoint halves of $\Sigma$. Together with their conjugates the modes span the data on $\Sigma$ minus the bifurcation point, a set of measure zero.
>

> [!definition] Definition §C14.2.2: Rindler Vacuum
> *Structure.* The Rindler modes and their conjugates form a complete set of the kind required by [[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^thm-c14-1-3|Theorem §C14.1.3]], so they define annihilation operators and a Fock vacuum. Expand
>
> $$
> \hat\phi = \int dk\,\bigl(\hat b^R_kg^R_k + \hat b^{R\dagger}_kg^{R\ast}_k + \hat b^L_kg^L_k + \hat b^{L\dagger}_kg^{L\ast}_k\bigr), \qquad \hat b^I_k = (g^I_k, \hat\phi), \qquad [\hat b^I_k, \hat b^{J\dagger}_{k'}] = \delta^{IJ}\delta(k - k') .
> $$
>
> The **Rindler vacuum** $|0_R\rangle$ is the state with $\hat b^R_k|0_R\rangle = \hat b^L_k|0_R\rangle = 0$ for all $k$. The **Rindler number operator** of the mode $k$ in $R$ is $\hat N^R_k = \hat b^{R\dagger}_k\hat b^R_k$; it is what an observer of $R$, whose clock is $\eta$, calls the number of quanta of frequency $\nu$ per unit $\eta$.
>
> *For comparison.* The Minkowski modes $f_p = e^{-i|p|t + ipx}/\sqrt{4\pi|p|}$ are positive-frequency for $\partial_t$; with $\hat a_p = (f_p, \hat\phi)$ the Minkowski vacuum has $\hat a_p|0_M\rangle = 0$ ([[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^thm-c14-1-3|Theorem §C14.1.3]]; [[§C2a.2 Mode Expansion and the Mode Algebra#^thm-c2a-2-6|QFT Theorem §C2a.2.6]] in four dimensions). Both expansions describe the same field operator. Whether the two vacua agree is decided by whether $g^R_k$ is a combination of the $f_p$ alone ([[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^def-c14-1-3|Def. §C14.1.3]]); [[#^thm-c14-2-1|Theorem §C14.2.1]], part 5, shows it is not.
>

## Unruh's analytic continuation

Computing the Bogoliubov coefficients $(g^R_k, f_p)$ directly is possible but long. Unruh's shortcut: find combinations of Rindler modes that are positive-frequency Minkowski modes; they annihilate $|0_M\rangle$, and inverting them gives $\hat b^R_k$ in terms of operators that annihilate $|0_M\rangle$. The criterion is analyticity. A superposition of right-moving positive-frequency waves $e^{-i\omega u}$, $\omega > 0$, stays bounded when $u$ moves into the lower half of the complex plane, since $|e^{-i\omega(u - iy)}| = e^{-\omega y} < 1$; conversely, a function analytic and bounded there has no negative-frequency part. The mode $(-u)^{i\nu}$ is analytic in $u$ away from $u = 0$, and the continuation decides what it must become on $u > 0$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C14.2.1: Unruh's Combinations Are Positive-Frequency Minkowski Modes
> For $k \neq 0$, $\nu = |k|$, define the solutions
>
> $$
> h^R_k = \frac{e^{\pi\nu/2}g^R_k + e^{-\pi\nu/2}g^{L\ast}_{-k}}{\sqrt{2\sinh\pi\nu}}, \qquad h^L_k = \frac{e^{\pi\nu/2}g^L_k + e^{-\pi\nu/2}g^{R\ast}_{-k}}{\sqrt{2\sinh\pi\nu}} .
> $$
>
> 1. **Analytic form.** For $k > 0$, $\sqrt{4\pi\nu}\,(g^R_k + e^{-\pi\nu}g^{L\ast}_{-k})$ is the boundary value $F(u - i0)$ of $F(z) = (-z/\xi_0)^{i\nu}$ (principal branch), which is analytic on $\operatorname{Im}z < 0$ with $e^{-\pi\nu} \le |F| \le 1$ there: it equals $(-u/\xi_0)^{i\nu}$ for $u < 0$ and $e^{-\pi\nu}(u/\xi_0)^{i\nu}$ for $u > 0$. For $k < 0$ the same holds with $v$ in place of $u$ and $F(z) = (z/\xi_0)^{-i\nu}$; for $h^L_k$ with the roles of $u$ and $v$ exchanged.
> 2. **Positive frequency.** Each $h^I_k$ is a superposition of positive-frequency Minkowski modes alone. For $k > 0$, explicitly,
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\,e^{i\omega u}F(u - i0) = \begin{cases}-2i\,e^{-\pi\nu/2}\sinh(\pi\nu)\,\Gamma(1 + i\nu)\,\xi_0^{-i\nu}\,\omega^{-1 - i\nu} & \omega > 0\\ 0 & \omega < 0\end{cases}
> $$
>
>    (as a distribution, Abel limit), so $h^R_k$ is a combination of the right-moving $f_p \propto e^{-ipu}$, $p > 0$.
> 3. **Orthonormality.** $(h^I_k, h^J_{k'}) = \delta^{IJ}\delta(k - k')$, $(h^I_k, h^{J\ast}_{k'}) = 0$.
> 4. **⚑ They share the Minkowski vacuum.** With $\hat c^I_k = (h^I_k, \hat\phi)$: $\hat\phi = \int dk\sum_I(\hat c^I_kh^I_k + \hat c^{I\dagger}_kh^{I\ast}_k)$, $[\hat c^I_k, \hat c^{J\dagger}_{k'}] = \delta^{IJ}\delta(k - k')$, and $\hat c^I_k|0_M\rangle = 0$ for all $I$, $k$.
> 5. **⚑ The Rindler vacuum is not the Minkowski vacuum.** No nonzero combination of the $g^R_k$ alone is positive-frequency for $\partial_t$; so each $\hat b^R_k$ contains Minkowski creation operators and $|0_R\rangle \neq |0_M\rangle$.
> 6. **The side of the continuation is forced.** Continuing through $\operatorname{Im}z > 0$ gives the factor $e^{+\pi\nu}$ instead, and $g^R_k + e^{\pi\nu}g^{L\ast}_{-k}$ is purely negative-frequency.
>
> *Model:* [[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]], $m = 0$, two dimensions; *applies:* exactly; parts 2–4 as identities of distributions in the mode labels.
>

(9.151)–(9.162), pp. 408–410 (PDF 421–423) (the analyticity criterion, "branch cut in the upper-half complex $(t - x)$ plane") · the spectrum of part 2 derived here; parts 2 and 6 checked numerically by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (light-cone form). On $R$, $u = -\xi_0e^{\zeta - \eta}$ gives $e^{-(\eta - \zeta)} = -u/\xi_0$, so for $k = \nu > 0$: $e^{-i\nu\eta + i\nu\zeta} = (e^{-(\eta - \zeta)})^{i\nu} = (-u/\xi_0)^{i\nu}$. On $L$, $u = \xi_0e^{\zeta - \eta}$ gives $e^{\eta - \zeta} = \xi_0/u$, so $g^L_{-k} \propto e^{i\nu\eta - i\nu\zeta} = (\xi_0/u)^{i\nu} = (u/\xi_0)^{-i\nu}$ and
>
> $$
> \sqrt{4\pi\nu}\,g^{L\ast}_{-k} = \Bigl(\frac{u}{\xi_0}\Bigr)^{+i\nu} \quad (u > 0) .
> $$
>
> The other two forms of [[#^def-c14-2-1|Def. §C14.2.1]] follow in the same way from $v = \pm\xi_0e^{\zeta + \eta}$.
>
> **Step 2** (positive frequency is analyticity below the axis). A right-moving positive-frequency superposition is $G(u) = \int_0^\infty d\omega\,\tilde G(\omega)e^{-i\omega u}$. For $z = u - iy$, $y > 0$, each term has $|e^{-i\omega z}| = e^{-\omega y} \le 1$, so $G$ extends to a function analytic on $\operatorname{Im}z < 0$ whose boundary value is $G$ ([[§CA.7 Boundary Values, iε Limits and Fundamental Solutions#^thm-ca-7-1|QFT Theorem §CA.7.1]]). Conversely, if $G$ is the boundary value of $F$ analytic and polynomially bounded on $\operatorname{Im}z < 0$, then for $\omega < 0$ the factor $e^{i\omega z}$ decays as $\operatorname{Im}z \to -\infty$, the contour of $\int du\,e^{i\omega u}F(u - i0)$ can be closed below ([[§CA.5 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-5-2|QFT Theorem §CA.5.2]], [[§CA.5 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-5-6|QFT Theorem §CA.5.6]]) and the integral vanishes: no negative frequencies.
>
> **Step 3** (the continuation, part 1). $\log(-z/\xi_0)$ with the principal branch is analytic except where $-z/\xi_0 \le 0$, i.e. on $z \in [0, \infty)$; so $F(z) = \exp(i\nu\log(-z/\xi_0))$ is analytic on $\operatorname{Im}z < 0$. There $-z$ lies in the upper half-plane, $\arg(-z) \in (0, \pi)$, and $|F| = e^{-\nu\arg(-z)} \in (e^{-\pi\nu}, 1)$. The boundary value from below, by $\log(x + i0) = \ln|x| + i\pi\theta(-x)$ ([[§CA.7 Boundary Values, iε Limits and Fundamental Solutions#^thm-ca-7-3|QFT Theorem §CA.7.3]]) with $x = -u/\xi_0$:
>
> $$
> F(u - i0) = \begin{cases}(-u/\xi_0)^{i\nu} & u < 0\\ e^{i\nu(\ln(u/\xi_0) + i\pi)} = e^{-\pi\nu}(u/\xi_0)^{i\nu} & u > 0\end{cases} \;=\; \sqrt{4\pi\nu}\,\bigl(g^R_k + e^{-\pi\nu}g^{L\ast}_{-k}\bigr)
> $$
>
> by Step 1. For $k < 0$: $\sqrt{4\pi\nu}g^R_k = (v/\xi_0)^{-i\nu}$ on $v > 0$; $z^{-i\nu}$ continued through $\operatorname{Im}z < 0$ to $v < 0$ has $\arg z \to -\pi$, giving $|v/\xi_0|^{-i\nu}e^{-i\nu(-i\pi)} = e^{-\pi\nu}(-v/\xi_0)^{-i\nu} = e^{-\pi\nu}\sqrt{4\pi\nu}\,g^{L\ast}_{-k}$, the same combination. Exchanging $u \leftrightarrow v$ and $R \leftrightarrow L$ gives $h^L_k$.
>
> **Step 4** (the spectrum, part 2). With an Abel factor $e^{-\varepsilon|u|}$ and $\int_0^\infty w^{z - 1}e^{-pw}dw = \Gamma(z)p^{-z}$ for $\operatorname{Re}p > 0$ ([[§CA.6 Gaussian Integrals, Stationary Phase and Bessel Functions#^def-ca-6-1|QFT Def. §CA.6.1]] for $p > 0$; both sides are analytic in $p$, so equal for $\operatorname{Re}p > 0$ by [[§CA.5 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-5-3|QFT Theorem §CA.5.3]]), the half-lines give, with $w = -u$ on the left,
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{0}du\,e^{i\omega u + \varepsilon u}\Bigl(\frac{-u}{\xi_0}\Bigr)^{i\nu} = \frac{\Gamma(1 + i\nu)}{\xi_0^{i\nu}(\varepsilon + i\omega)^{1 + i\nu}}, \qquad e^{-\pi\nu}\int_0^\infty du\,e^{i\omega u - \varepsilon u}\Bigl(\frac{u}{\xi_0}\Bigr)^{i\nu} = \frac{e^{-\pi\nu}\,\Gamma(1 + i\nu)}{\xi_0^{i\nu}(\varepsilon - i\omega)^{1 + i\nu}} .
> $$
>
> As $\varepsilon \to 0^+$, for $\omega > 0$: $\varepsilon \pm i\omega \to \omega e^{\pm i\pi/2}$ and $(\omega e^{\pm i\pi/2})^{-1 - i\nu} = \omega^{-1 - i\nu}(\mp i)e^{\pm\pi\nu/2}$, so the sum is
>
> $$
> \frac{\Gamma(1 + i\nu)}{\xi_0^{i\nu}\omega^{1 + i\nu}}\bigl(-ie^{\pi\nu/2} + ie^{-\pi\nu}e^{-\pi\nu/2}\bigr) = -2i\,e^{-\pi\nu/2}\sinh(\pi\nu)\,\frac{\Gamma(1 + i\nu)}{\xi_0^{i\nu}\omega^{1 + i\nu}} .
> $$
>
> For $\omega = -|\omega| < 0$: $\varepsilon + i\omega \to |\omega|e^{-i\pi/2}$, $\varepsilon - i\omega \to |\omega|e^{+i\pi/2}$, and the bracket becomes $ie^{-\pi\nu/2} + e^{-\pi\nu}(-i)e^{\pi\nu/2} = 0$. (Script: quadrature at $\varepsilon = 0.3$ matches these closed forms; at $\omega = -1$ the transform is $4.7\times10^{-3}$, $4.7\times10^{-4}$, $4.7\times10^{-5}$ for $\varepsilon = 10^{-2}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-4}$.) So $F(u - i0) = \int_0^\infty\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}(\ldots)e^{-i\omega u}$, a superposition of $e^{-i\omega u} \propto f_{p = \omega}$ with $p > 0$ only.
>
> **Step 5** (part 3). By the inner products of [[#^def-c14-2-1|Def. §C14.2.1]], with $N = \sqrt{2\sinh\pi\nu}$,
>
> $$
> (h^R_k, h^R_{k'}) = \frac{e^{\pi\nu}(g^R_k, g^R_{k'}) + e^{-\pi\nu}(g^{L\ast}_{-k}, g^{L\ast}_{-k'})}{N^2} = \frac{e^{\pi\nu} - e^{-\pi\nu}}{2\sinh\pi\nu}\,\delta(k - k') = \delta(k - k'),
> $$
>
> the cross terms vanishing because $R$ and $L$ data are disjoint. With $h^{L\ast}_{k'} = (e^{\pi\nu'/2}g^{L\ast}_{k'} + e^{-\pi\nu'/2}g^R_{-k'})/N'$,
>
> $$
> (h^R_k, h^{L\ast}_{k'}) = \frac{e^{\pi(\nu - \nu')/2}(g^R_k, g^R_{-k'}) + e^{-\pi(\nu - \nu')/2}(g^{L\ast}_{-k}, g^{L\ast}_{k'})}{NN'} = \frac{\delta(k + k') - \delta(k + k')}{N^2} = 0
> $$
>
> (the delta sets $\nu' = \nu$). The other cases are the same computation.
>
> **Step 6** (part 4). By parts 2–3 the $h^I_k$ with their conjugates form a complete orthonormal set (they are invertible combinations of the $g^I_k$, $g^{I\ast}_k$: [[#^der-c14-2-2|derivation of Theorem §C14.2.2]], Step 1), so [[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^thm-c14-1-3|Theorem §C14.1.3]] gives the expansion and the commutators. Each $h^I_k$ is $\int dp\,\alpha_{kp}f_p$ with no $f^\ast_p$ (part 2), so $(h^I_k, f^\ast_p) = 0$ and $\hat c^I_k = (h^I_k, \hat\phi) = \int dp\,\alpha^\ast_{kp}\hat a_p$ contains no $\hat a^\dagger_p$: it annihilates $|0_M\rangle$.
>
> **Step 7** (part 5). Suppose $G = \int dk\,\beta(k)g^R_k$ were positive-frequency. Its right- and left-moving parts are functions of $u$ and of $v$ that vanish for $u > 0$ and for $v < 0$ respectively, on whole half-lines. By Step 2 the right-moving part is the boundary value of a function analytic below the axis that vanishes on the interval $u > 0$; by the reflection principle it continues across that interval, and by the identity theorem ([[§CA.5 Contour Integration#^thm-ca-5-3|QFT Theorem §CA.5.3]]) it vanishes everywhere; likewise the left-moving part. So $G = 0$. Hence $\hat b^R_k = (g^R_k, \hat\phi)$ involves $(g^R_k, f^\ast_p) \neq 0$ for some $p$, i.e. Minkowski creation operators, and $\hat b^R_k|0_M\rangle \neq 0$.
>
> **Step 8** (part 6). Through $\operatorname{Im}z > 0$, $\arg(-z) \to -\pi$ on $u > 0$ and $F(u + i0) = e^{+\pi\nu}(u/\xi_0)^{i\nu}$ there. Step 4 with $\varepsilon \pm i\omega$ replaced accordingly gives a transform that vanishes for $\omega > 0$ instead (script: $|\cdot| < 10^{-3}$ at $\omega = +1$, $27.6$ at $\omega = -1$).
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The Rindler mode oscillates infinitely often as $u \to 0^-$ (Figure (b)), so it has no continuation across the horizon by smoothness; there are two continuations by analyticity, around $u = 0$ below or above, and the time orientation picks the one below. The price of going around the branch point is the factor $e^{-\pi\nu}$ on the far side, and that number, squared, will be the Boltzmann factor.

## The Minkowski vacuum seen from one wedge

Inverting Unruh's combinations expresses the Rindler annihilation operators through operators that annihilate $|0_M\rangle$. The occupation numbers follow from [[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^thm-c14-1-5|Theorem §C14.1.5]]; the same inversion fixes the whole state.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C14.2.2: The Unruh Effect: the Minkowski Vacuum Is Thermal for Rindler Observers at T = a/2π
> In the Minkowski vacuum, for every $k \neq 0$, $\nu = |k|$, $N = \sqrt{2\sinh\pi\nu}$:
> 1. **Bogoliubov relation.** $g^R_k = (e^{\pi\nu/2}h^R_k - e^{-\pi\nu/2}h^{L\ast}_{-k})/N$: coefficients $A = e^{\pi\nu/2}/N$ and $B = -e^{-\pi\nu/2}/N$ ([[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^def-c14-1-3|Def. §C14.1.3]]), with $|A|^2 - |B|^2 = 1$ and $|B/A| = e^{-\pi\nu}$; and
>
> $$
> \hat b^R_k = \frac{e^{\pi\nu/2}\hat c^R_k + e^{-\pi\nu/2}\hat c^{L\dagger}_{-k}}{\sqrt{2\sinh\pi\nu}} .
> $$
>
> 2. **Occupation numbers.** $\langle 0_M|\hat b^{R\dagger}_k\hat b^R_{k'}|0_M\rangle = \delta(k - k')/(e^{2\pi\nu} - 1)$; with discrete labels $\langle\hat N^R_k\rangle = 1/(e^{2\pi\nu} - 1)$. For the reference observer $\xi_0$, of proper acceleration $a = 1/\xi_0$ and proper time $\tau = \xi_0\eta$, the mode has frequency $\Omega = \nu/\xi_0$ and
>
> $$
> \langle\hat N^R_k\rangle = \frac{1}{e^{\Omega/T} - 1}, \qquad T = \frac{a}{2\pi}\qquad\Bigl(\text{restored: } T = \frac{\hbar a}{2\pi ck_B}\Bigr),
> $$
>
>    the Bose–Einstein occupation at zero chemical potential ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-3|TH Theorem §B10.1.3]]).
> 3. **⚑ The full state is a thermofield double.** With discrete labels, pairing the mode $k$ of $R$ with the mode $-k$ of $L$,
>
> $$
> |0_M\rangle = \prod_k\sqrt{1 - e^{-2\pi\nu_k}}\,\sum_{n = 0}^\infty e^{-\pi\nu_kn}\,|n\rangle^R_k\otimes|n\rangle^L_{-k} .
> $$
>
> 4. **⚑ Its restriction to one wedge is thermal.** The reduced density operator ([[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^def-c11-3-1|QM Def. §C11.3.1]]) is
>
> $$
> \hat\rho_R = \operatorname{Tr}_L|0_M\rangle\langle 0_M| = \frac{e^{-2\pi\hat H_R}}{Z}, \qquad \hat H_R = \sum_k\nu_k\hat N^R_k, \qquad Z = \prod_k\bigl(1 - e^{-2\pi\nu_k}\bigr)^{-1},
> $$
>
>    where $\hat H_R$ generates translations of $\eta$ on $R$: the canonical state ([[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-5|QM Theorem §C11.2.5]]) at inverse temperature $2\pi$ in Rindler time, i.e. at $T = a/2\pi$ in the reference observer's proper time. Every measurement confined to $R$ gives thermal results, not only the mean occupation, and the entanglement entropy of $R$ with $L$ ([[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^def-c11-3-2|QM Def. §C11.3.2]]) is the thermal entropy.
> 5. **⚑ No common Fock space.** $\langle 0_R|0_M\rangle = \prod_k\sqrt{1 - e^{-2\pi\nu_k}}$, and for a box of length $L_\zeta$, $\ln\langle 0_R|0_M\rangle \approx -L_\zeta/24 \to -\infty$: in the continuum $|0_M\rangle$ is not a vector of the Rindler Fock space. Parts 2–4 are statements about each mode, or each finite set of modes.
>
> *Model:* [[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^mod-c14-1-1|Model §C14.1.1]], $m = 0$, two dimensions, in $|0_M\rangle$; observers of [[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^mod-c10-2-1|Model §C10.2.1]]; *applies:* parts 1–2 exactly (in the continuum as distributions); parts 3–5 with discrete labels (box or wave packets).

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (inverting the combinations). Conjugating $h^L_{-k} = (e^{\pi\nu/2}g^L_{-k} + e^{-\pi\nu/2}g^{R\ast}_k)/N$ gives $h^{L\ast}_{-k} = (e^{\pi\nu/2}g^{L\ast}_{-k} + e^{-\pi\nu/2}g^R_k)/N$. Then
>
> $$
> e^{\pi\nu/2}h^R_k - e^{-\pi\nu/2}h^{L\ast}_{-k} = \frac{(e^{\pi\nu} - e^{-\pi\nu})\,g^R_k + (1 - 1)\,g^{L\ast}_{-k}}{N} = \frac{2\sinh\pi\nu}{N}\,g^R_k = N\,g^R_k ,
> $$
>
> which is part 1's relation; $g^L_k$ is the same with $R \leftrightarrow L$. So the $g$'s and the $h$'s are invertible combinations of each other. In the form $g = Ah + Bh^\ast$ of [[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^def-c14-1-3|Def. §C14.1.3]], $A = e^{\pi\nu/2}/N$, $B = -e^{-\pi\nu/2}/N$, and
>
> $$
> |A|^2 - |B|^2 = \frac{e^{\pi\nu} - e^{-\pi\nu}}{2\sinh\pi\nu} = 1, \qquad |B|^2 = \frac{e^{-\pi\nu}}{e^{\pi\nu} - e^{-\pi\nu}} = \frac{1}{e^{2\pi\nu} - 1}, \qquad \Bigl|\frac BA\Bigr| = e^{-\pi\nu},
> $$
>
> the identity of [[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^thm-c14-1-4|Theorem §C14.1.4]] for this one pair.
>
> **Step 2** (the operator). $\hat b^R_k = (g^R_k, \hat\phi)$ and the form is antilinear in its first slot, with $A$, $B$ real: $\hat b^R_k = A(h^R_k, \hat\phi) + B(h^{L\ast}_{-k}, \hat\phi)$. From the expansion of [[#^thm-c14-2-1|Theorem §C14.2.1]], part 4, $(h^R_k, \hat\phi) = \hat c^R_k$ and $(h^{L\ast}_{-k}, \hat\phi) = \int dk'\,\hat c^{L\dagger}_{k'}(h^{L\ast}_{-k}, h^{L\ast}_{k'}) = -\hat c^{L\dagger}_{-k}$. So $\hat b^R_k = A\hat c^R_k - B\hat c^{L\dagger}_{-k}$, part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (part 2). $\hat c^R_{k'}|0_M\rangle = 0$, so $\hat b^R_{k'}|0_M\rangle = N'^{-1}e^{-\pi\nu'/2}\hat c^{L\dagger}_{-k'}|0_M\rangle$, and its adjoint gives $\langle 0_M|\hat b^{R\dagger}_k = N^{-1}e^{-\pi\nu/2}\langle 0_M|\hat c^L_{-k}$. With $[\hat c^L_{-k}, \hat c^{L\dagger}_{-k'}] = \delta(k - k')$ and $\hat c^L|0_M\rangle = 0$,
>
> $$
> \langle 0_M|\hat b^{R\dagger}_k\hat b^R_{k'}|0_M\rangle = \frac{e^{-\pi\nu}}{2\sinh\pi\nu}\,\delta(k - k') = \frac{\delta(k - k')}{e^{2\pi\nu} - 1},
> $$
>
> which is $\sum_j|B_{ij}|^2$ of [[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^thm-c14-1-5|Theorem §C14.1.5]] with the single nonzero $B$ of Step 1. In a box, $\delta(k - k') \to (L_\zeta/2\pi)\delta_{nn'}$, and with the discrete operators normalised to $[\hat b_n, \hat b^\dagger_n] = 1$ the factor $L_\zeta/2\pi$ (Couzens' $\delta(0)$, the number of modes per unit $k$) drops out: $\langle\hat N^R_n\rangle = 1/(e^{2\pi\nu_n} - 1)$ ([[§C2a.4 Particles and Relativistic Normalization#^cau-c2a-4-1|QFT §C2a.4, Caution: Plane-wave states are not normalizable]]).
>
> **Step 4** (physical temperature). On $\xi = \xi_0$, $\eta = \tau/\xi_0$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]], part 3), so $e^{-i\nu\eta} = e^{-i(\nu/\xi_0)\tau}$: the frequency is $\Omega = \nu/\xi_0 = a\nu$ and $2\pi\nu = 2\pi\Omega/a = \Omega/T$ with $T = a/2\pi$. Restoring units, $\xi_0 = c^2/a$ is a length and $\nu = \Omega\xi_0/c = \Omega c/a$; the exponent $2\pi\Omega c/a$ must be $\hbar\Omega/k_BT$, so $T = \hbar a/2\pi ck_B$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 3). Applying $(h^R_k, \cdot)$ and $(h^L_{-k}, \cdot)$ to the Rindler expansion of $\hat\phi$, as in Step 2,
>
> $$
> \hat c^R_k = \frac{e^{\pi\nu/2}\hat b^R_k - e^{-\pi\nu/2}\hat b^{L\dagger}_{-k}}{N}, \qquad \hat c^L_{-k} = \frac{e^{\pi\nu/2}\hat b^L_{-k} - e^{-\pi\nu/2}\hat b^{R\dagger}_k}{N} .
> $$
>
> With $q = e^{-\pi\nu}$, $\hat c|0_M\rangle = 0$ becomes $(\hat b^R_k - q\,\hat b^{L\dagger}_{-k})|0_M\rangle = 0$ and $(\hat b^L_{-k} - q\,\hat b^{R\dagger}_k)|0_M\rangle = 0$. These conditions involve only the pair $(R, k)$, $(L, -k)$, so the state is a product over pairs. For one pair write $|\psi\rangle = \sum_{m,n}C_{mn}|m\rangle^R|n\rangle^L$. Then
>
> $$
> \hat b^R|\psi\rangle = \sum_{m,n}C_{mn}\sqrt m\,|m - 1, n\rangle, \qquad q\,\hat b^{L\dagger}|\psi\rangle = q\sum_{m,n}C_{mn}\sqrt{n + 1}\,|m, n + 1\rangle,
> $$
>
> and equating the coefficients of $|m, n\rangle$: $\sqrt{m + 1}\,C_{m+1,n} = q\sqrt n\,C_{m,n-1}$. At $n = 0$ the right side is zero, so $C_{m,0} = 0$ for $m \ge 1$, and by induction on $n$, $C_{mn} = 0$ unless $m = n$, with $\sqrt{n + 1}\,C_{n+1,n+1} = q\sqrt{n + 1}\,C_{nn}$, i.e. $C_{nn} = q^nC_{00}$. The second condition gives the same. Normalising, $|C_{00}|^2\sum_nq^{2n} = |C_{00}|^2/(1 - q^2) = 1$, so $C_{00} = \sqrt{1 - e^{-2\pi\nu}}$ (phase chosen real). This is part 3.
>
> **Step 6** (part 4). For one pair, the partial trace over $L$ of $\sum_{n,n'}(1 - q^2)q^{n + n'}|n\rangle\langle n'|^R\otimes|n\rangle\langle n'|^L$ keeps $n = n'$:
>
> $$
> \hat\rho_R = (1 - e^{-2\pi\nu})\sum_ne^{-2\pi\nu n}|n\rangle\langle n| = (1 - e^{-2\pi\nu})\,e^{-2\pi\nu\hat N^R},
> $$
>
> and the product over pairs is $e^{-2\pi\hat H_R}/Z$. Since $[\hat H_R, \hat b^R_k] = -\nu\hat b^R_k$, $e^{i\hat H_R\Delta\eta}\hat b^R_ke^{-i\hat H_R\Delta\eta} = e^{-i\nu\Delta\eta}\hat b^R_k$, which shifts $\eta \to \eta + \Delta\eta$ in the right-wedge part of $\hat\phi$: $\hat H_R$ is the Rindler Hamiltonian, normal-ordered with respect to $|0_R\rangle$. By [[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^thm-c11-3-1|QM Theorem §C11.3.1]] every observable on $R$ has its canonical expectation value. The pure state of Step 5 is a purification of this thermal state ([[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^thm-c11-3-4|QM Theorem §C11.3.4]]); its entanglement entropy $-\operatorname{Tr}\hat\rho_R\ln\hat\rho_R = 2\pi\langle\hat H_R\rangle + \ln Z$ is the thermal entropy (script, one pair: $\nu = 0.15$ gives $\langle\hat N\rangle = 0.6384$, $S = 1.0955$).
>
> **Step 7** (part 5). $|0_R\rangle$ is $|0\rangle^R|0\rangle^L$ in every pair, so $\langle 0_R|0_M\rangle = \prod C_{00}$. With $k_n = 2\pi n/L_\zeta$, $n \neq 0$, the two signs of $n$ give the same factor, and
>
> $$
> \ln\langle 0_R|0_M\rangle = \sum_{n \ge 1}\ln\bigl(1 - e^{-4\pi^2n/L_\zeta}\bigr) \approx \frac{L_\zeta}{4\pi^2}\int_0^\infty\ln(1 - e^{-y})\,dy = -\frac{L_\zeta}{4\pi^2}\cdot\frac{\pi^2}{6} = -\frac{L_\zeta}{24}
> $$
>
> (script: $-6.59$, $-80.45$, $-829.3$ for $L_\zeta = 200$, $2000$, $20000$, against $-8.33$, $-83.3$, $-833.3$). The infinitely many soft modes ($\nu \to 0$, each with overlap $\sqrt{1 - e^{-2\pi\nu}} \to 0$) drive the overlap to zero.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The Boltzmann factor is $|B/A|^2 = e^{-2\pi\nu}$, the square of the price of continuing around the horizon. Thermality is entanglement: $|0_M\rangle$ correlates each right-wedge mode with its mirror mode behind the horizon, and an observer who can never receive signals from $L$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-3|Theorem §C10.2.3]]) must describe $R$ by the partial trace.

## Every height has its own temperature

The temperature $a/2\pi$ belongs to the reference observer, but nothing singled out $\xi_0$: the state is canonical in the Rindler time $\eta$, which every observer of $R$ shares, while their clocks run at different rates.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C14.2.3: Local Temperature a/2π along ξ = const: the Tolman Relation
> Let an observer sit at fixed $\xi$ in $R$, with proper acceleration $a(\xi) = 1/\xi$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]], part 3), in the Minkowski vacuum.
> 1. **Local frequencies.** The mode $g^R_k$ has frequency $\Omega_\xi = \nu/\xi$ in the observer's proper time (the redshift reading of [[§C14.1 Quantum Fields on a Curved Background#^def-c14-1-2|Def. §C14.1.2]]); a quantum of conserved boost energy $E = -p\cdot K$ ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-2|Theorem §C3.13.2]]) is measured to have energy $E/\sqrt{-K\cdot K} = E/\xi$.
> 2. **Local temperature.** The state $\hat\rho_R$ is canonical in the observer's energies at
>
> $$
> T(\xi) = \frac{1}{2\pi\xi} = \frac{a(\xi)}{2\pi} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } T = \frac{\hbar a(\xi)}{2\pi ck_B}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 3. **Tolman relation.** $T(\xi)\sqrt{-K\cdot K} = T(\xi)\,\xi = 1/2\pi$, the same for all observers: $T(\xi_1)/T(\xi_2) = \xi_2/\xi_1$, the ratio of the clock rates, so a thermal spectrum at $T(\xi_1)$ sent up or down is received as thermal at $T(\xi_2)$.
> 4. **Limits.** $T \to \infty$ at the horizon ($\xi \to 0$) and $T \to 0$ far out ($\xi \to \infty$), where the observers' acceleration vanishes.
> 5. **Couzens' form.** In his chart $\xi = e^{a\xi_C}/a$, so $a(\xi) = ae^{-a\xi_C} = \alpha$, and $T = \alpha/2\pi$ is his (9.93).
>
> *Model:* as [[#^thm-c14-2-2|Theorem §C14.2.2]]; *applies:* exactly, for every observer of $R$.

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). On $\xi = \text{const}$, $d\tau = \xi\,d\eta$ and the four-velocity is $u = \xi^{-1}\partial_\eta = K/\xi$, with $K\cdot K = g_{\eta\eta} = -\xi^2$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]], parts 3–4). Along the worldline $e^{-i\nu\eta} = e^{-i(\nu/\xi)\tau}$, so $\Omega_\xi = \nu/\xi$. For a quantum with momentum $p$ the measured energy is $-p\cdot u = -p\cdot K/\xi = E/\xi$.
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). $\hat H_R = \sum\nu_k\hat N^R_k$ with $\nu_k = \xi\,\Omega_{\xi,k}$, so $e^{-2\pi\hat H_R} = \exp\bigl(-2\pi\xi\sum_k\Omega_{\xi,k}\hat N^R_k\bigr)$: the canonical state for the observer's energies at $\beta = 2\pi\xi$, i.e. $T(\xi) = 1/2\pi\xi$. Units are restored as in Step 4 of [[#^der-c14-2-2|the derivation of Theorem §C14.2.2]], with $\xi_0 \to \xi$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 3). $\sqrt{-K\cdot K} = \xi$, so $T(\xi)\sqrt{-K\cdot K} = 1/2\pi$. A quantum keeps $E$ along its geodesic, so energies measured at $\xi_1$ and $\xi_2$ are in the ratio $\xi_2/\xi_1$ (Step 1), the same ratio as $T(\xi_1)/T(\xi_2)$: the Boltzmann factor $e^{-\Omega/T}$ is the same number at both heights. This is the redshift between static observers ([[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay#^thm-c6-6-1|Theorem §C6.6.1]] in Schwarzschild, with $\sqrt f$ in place of $\xi$).
>
> **Step 4** (parts 4–5). Part 4 follows from $T = 1/2\pi\xi$. Couzens' chart (9.66), $x = a^{-1}e^{a\xi_C}\cosh(a\eta_C)$, is $x = \xi\cosh\eta$ with $\xi = e^{a\xi_C}/a$, $\eta = a\eta_C$; then $1/\xi = ae^{-a\xi_C}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** One state, many temperatures: the temperature is the inverse of $2\pi$ times the length of the Killing vector, and $2\pi$ is the inverse temperature in the Killing time itself. In a static gravitational field the same rule says that equilibrium is hotter lower down; it is known as the Tolman–Ehrenfest relation (R. C. Tolman and P. Ehrenfest, Phys. Rev. 36, 1791 (1930); not in the typed sources). Here it comes out of the quantum state, with no thermodynamic argument.

## What the effect does and does not say

> [!remark] Remark: What a detector sees, and where the energy comes from
> The inertial observer finds no energy in $|0_M\rangle$: with Minkowski normal ordering $\langle 0_M|\hat T_{\mu\nu}|0_M\rangle = 0$. The thermal energy of [[#^ex-c14-2-2|Example §C14.2.2]] is counted with Rindler normal ordering (relative to $|0_R\rangle$); for an operator quadratic in $\hat\phi$ the two orderings differ by a multiple of the identity, so both statements hold at once. An accelerated detector nevertheless clicks. In Couzens' account (GR2, p. 168, and Carroll p. 412) the energy comes from the agent that keeps the detector accelerating: seen inertially, a detector that gets excited emits a Minkowski quantum and feels a radiation-reaction force, and the work against it pays for the excitation.
>
> The standard model of such a detector, the Unruh–DeWitt detector (W. G. Unruh, Phys. Rev. D 14, 870 (1976); B. S. DeWitt, in *General Relativity: An Einstein Centenary Survey*, eds. S. W. Hawking and W. Israel, Cambridge University Press (1979); not in the typed sources), is a two-level system with gap $\Omega$ coupled to $\hat\phi$ along its worldline. To lowest order its excitation rate is the Fourier transform at $\Omega$ of the Wightman function ([[§C2b.2 The Wightman Function#^def-c2b-2-1|QFT Def. §C2b.2.1]], signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) along the worldline. For the massless field in four dimensions that function is $-1/4\pi^2[(\Delta t - i\varepsilon)^2 - \Delta x^2]$ ([[§C2b.3 Explicit Forms of the Wightman Function#^thm-c2b-3-5|QFT Theorem §C2b.3.5]]), and on the hyperbola $(\Delta t)^2 - (\Delta x)^2 = 4\xi_0^2\sinh^2(\Delta\tau/2\xi_0)$ (script), so it becomes $-1/[16\pi^2\xi_0^2\sinh^2((\Delta\tau - i\varepsilon)/2\xi_0)]$. This is periodic under $\Delta\tau \to \Delta\tau + 2\pi i\xi_0 = \Delta\tau + i/T$; that periodicity in imaginary time is the mark of a thermal state, and it makes the ratio of excitation to de-excitation rates $e^{-\Omega/T}$ (same references). The detector picture also covers four dimensions, where this note's mode argument was not carried out.

The Unruh effect is a statement about flat spacetime and one family of observers. Its ingredients are a Killing horizon, a Killing time that is a boost near the horizon, and a state that is regular across the horizon. [[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-4|Theorem §C10.2.4]] showed that near a Schwarzschild horizon the static observers are Rindler observers with $\eta = \kappa t$, and [[§C13.1 Surface Gravity and the Zeroth Law#^thm-c13-1-2|Theorem §C13.1.2]] that their acceleration times the redshift factor tends to $\kappa$. Combined with the Tolman relation, a black hole surrounded by a state that freely falling observers call empty should look thermal at $\kappa/2\pi$ from far away. Whether this happens for a real hole, formed by collapse with no left wedge to entangle with, is the question of [[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature|§C14.3]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C14.2.1: The Unruh Temperature at 1 g and in a Particle Accelerator
> *Question.* How hot is the Minkowski vacuum for accelerations that occur in practice?
>
> **Step 1** (the conversion). By [[#^thm-c14-2-2|Theorem §C14.2.2]], part 2, $T = \hbar a/2\pi ck_B$, and $\hbar/2\pi ck_B = 4.055\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{K\,s^2/m}$.
>
> **Step 2** (one g). For $a = g = 9.80665\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, $T = 3.98\times10^{-20}$ K, twenty orders of magnitude below the 2.725 K of the microwave background. Conversely, $T = 1$ K needs $a = 2.47\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} = 2.5\times10^{19}\,g$.
>
> **Step 3** (the wavelength is the horizon scale). With the Planck spectrum of [[#^ex-c14-2-2|Example §C14.2.2]](b), the energy spectrum per unit $\omega$ peaks at $\hbar\omega = 2.821\,k_BT$ ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-4|TH Theorem §B11.1.4]]), so
>
> $$
> \lambda_{\text{peak}} = \frac{2\pi c}{\omega_{\text{peak}}} = \frac{2\pi c\hbar}{2.821\,k_BT} = \frac{4\pi^2}{2.821}\,\frac{c^2}{a} = 13.99\,\frac{c^2}{a} .
> $$
>
> The distance to the horizon is $c^2/a$ ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^ex-c10-2-1|Example §C10.2.1]]): the typical Unruh quantum is fourteen times longer than the distance to the horizon. At $1\,g$, $c^2/g = 0.9687$ light-years and $\lambda_{\text{peak}} = 13.6$ light-years.
>
> **Step 4** (a linear accelerator). An electron in an accelerating field $E$ along its motion feels the force $eE$ in its momentary rest frame (a field along the boost is unchanged), so its proper acceleration is $eE/m_e$, the uniformly accelerated particle of [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^mod-b3-3-4|Model §B3.3.4]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). For $E = 100$ MV/m: $a = 1.76\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $T = 0.071$ K; for $E = 100$ GV/m, $T = 71$ K.
>
> **Step 5** (a storage ring). On a circle at constant speed $\gamma$ is constant and $u = \gamma(1, \vec v)$, so $du/d\tau = \gamma^2(0, d\vec v/dt)$ with $|d\vec v/dt| = v^2/R$: the proper acceleration is $\gamma^2v^2/R$. For $\gamma = 10^5$ (electrons of 51 GeV) and $R = 1$ km: $a = 8.99\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $\hbar a/2\pi ck_B = 3.6\times10^3$ K. Circular motion is not the uniform linear acceleration of [[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^mod-c10-2-1|Model §C10.2.1]], so this number gives only the scale of the effect, not a Planck spectrum.
>
> *Result.* Ordinary accelerations give absurdly low temperatures; only particle accelerations reach kelvins, and there the trajectory is not the one the theorem covers.
>
> *Source: worked here from Couzens, GR2, §9.4, eq. (9.91) ($T = a/2\pi$), p. 167 (PDF 168) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C14.2.2: The Planck Spectrum Recovered
> *Question.* [[#^thm-c14-2-2|Theorem §C14.2.2]] gives an occupation per mode. What energy spectrum does the reference observer measure, and how does it compare with Couzens' Planck law (9.92)?
>
> **(a) Two dimensions.** In a box of length $L_\zeta$ the right-moving modes with frequency between $\nu$ and $\nu + d\nu$ number $(L_\zeta/2\pi)\,d\nu$ ([[§CA.4 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-4-10|QFT Theorem §CA.4.10]]), each holding $1/(e^{2\pi\nu} - 1)$ quanta. Near $\xi_0$ the metric is $\xi_0^2(-d\eta^2 + d\zeta^2)$, so the box has proper length $\xi_0L_\zeta$ there and a quantum has energy $\hbar\Omega$ with $\nu = \xi_0\Omega$. The right-moving energy per proper length, which for $c = 1$ is also the energy flux, is
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{\xi_0L_\zeta}\cdot\frac{L_\zeta}{2\pi}\,\xi_0\,d\Omega\cdot\frac{\hbar\Omega}{e^{\hbar\Omega/k_BT} - 1} = \frac{\hbar\Omega}{2\pi}\,\frac{d\Omega}{e^{\hbar\Omega/k_BT} - 1}, \qquad \int_0^\infty\frac{\hbar\Omega}{2\pi}\frac{d\Omega}{e^{\hbar\Omega/k_BT} - 1} = \frac{\hbar}{2\pi}\Bigl(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar}\Bigr)^2\frac{\pi^2}{6} = \frac{\pi k_B^2T^2}{12\hbar} ,
> $$
>
> with $y = \hbar\Omega/k_BT$ and $\int_0^\infty y\,dy/(e^y - 1) = \pi^2/6$. This is the black-body law of a one-dimensional world: one Bose–Einstein factor per mode, one mode per $d\Omega/2\pi$, flux $\propto T^2$.
>
> **(b) Four dimensions, photons.** In four dimensions the occupation of the Rindler modes of a free field in $|0_M\rangle$ is the same $1/(e^{2\pi\Omega/a} - 1)$ (W. G. Unruh, Phys. Rev. D 14, 870 (1976); L. C. B. Crispino, A. Higuchi and G. E. A. Matsas, Rev. Mod. Phys. 80, 787 (2008), §II; not in the typed sources). Counting modes as in a cavity with two polarisations, the energy density per unit $\omega$ is $u(\omega) = \hbar\omega^3/\pi^2c^3$ times the Bose factor ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-4|TH Theorem §B11.1.4]]); isotropic radiation carries $c\,u/4\pi$ per steradian, so the spectral radiance per unit angular frequency is
>
> $$
> B_\omega = \frac{c}{4\pi}u(\omega) = \frac{\hbar\omega^3}{4\pi^3c^2}\,\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT} - 1} .
> $$
>
> **(c) Checking the factor.** A black surface emits $\pi\int B_\omega\,d\omega$ per unit area ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-6|TH Theorem §B11.1.6]]). With $\omega = yk_BT/\hbar$ and $\int_0^\infty y^3dy/(e^y - 1) = \pi^4/15$,
>
> $$
> \pi\int_0^\infty B_\omega\,d\omega = \frac{\hbar}{4\pi^2c^2}\Bigl(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar}\Bigr)^4\frac{\pi^4}{15} = \frac{\pi^2k_B^4}{60\hbar^3c^2}\,T^4 = \sigma T^4 ,
> $$
>
> the Stefan–Boltzmann law ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-7|TH Law §A1.5.7]]). Couzens' (9.92), with $4\pi^2c^2$ in the denominator, would give $\pi\sigma T^4$; the factor is $4\pi^3$ ([[#^cau-c14-2-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens §9.4]]).
>
> *Result.* What the derivation fixes is the Bose–Einstein factor per mode; the prefactor ($\hbar\Omega/2\pi$ in two dimensions, $\hbar\omega^3/4\pi^3c^2$ per steradian in four) is mode counting. In the accelerated frame the temperature varies with height ([[#^thm-c14-2-3|Theorem §C14.2.3]]), so these are local spectra at the observer, not those of one uniform cavity.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §9.4, eqs. (9.91)–(9.92), p. 167 (PDF 168) · worked here; integrals and the factor checked by script*
