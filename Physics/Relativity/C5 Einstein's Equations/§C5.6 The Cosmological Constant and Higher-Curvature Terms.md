The chapter has reached Einstein's equations twice. The uniqueness theorem allowed exactly the combinations $aG_{\mu\nu} + bg_{\mu\nu}$ on the curvature side ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^thm-c5-1-3|Theorem §C5.1.3]]); the action principle produced $G_{\mu\nu}$ from the Einstein–Hilbert action ([[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action#^thm-c5-2-5|Theorem §C5.2.5]]); and the Newtonian limit fixed the coupling to $8\pi$ ([[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^thm-c5-5-2|Theorem §C5.5.2]]). Two choices were left open. The constant $b$ survives as the cosmological constant $\Lambda$ of [[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^pr-c5-1-4|Principle §C5.1.4]]. And the action used the simplest curvature scalar, $R$, without showing that nothing else may be added ([[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action#^rem-c5-2-1|§C5.2, Remark: Why R and not another scalar]]). This section asks: *what does the term $\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ do, how large is it, and why does the action stop at $R - 2\Lambda$ instead of continuing with $R^2$, $R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}$ and $R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$?*

Two quick answers fail when they are computed. The first says that $\Lambda$ must vanish because empty flat spacetime has to solve the vacuum equations. With $\Lambda$ the vacuum equations become $R_{\mu\nu} = \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$; Minkowski spacetime has $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$, so it solves them only if $0 = \Lambda\eta_{\mu\nu}$, i.e. $\Lambda = 0$ ([[#^thm-c5-6-1|Theorem §C5.6.1]], part 3). But nothing in the derivation of the equations required flat spacetime to be a solution; that is a question for observation, and observation finds $\Lambda > 0$ ([[#^law-c5-6-3|Law §C5.6.3]]). The second says that adding $R^2$ to the action is a harmless refinement. Its variation contains $\Box R$, four derivatives of the metric: the field equation becomes fourth order, and the scalar curvature, fixed algebraically by $T$ in general relativity, acquires a wave equation of its own ([[#^thm-c5-6-6|Theorem §C5.6.6]]).

The section builds, in order: the cosmological term in the action and in the field equations ([[#^thm-c5-6-1|Theorem §C5.6.1]]); its reading as the energy of empty space, with a negative pressure ([[#^thm-c5-6-2|Theorem §C5.6.2]]); its measured value ([[#^law-c5-6-3|Law §C5.6.3]]); the vacuum solutions with $\Lambda$, Einstein manifolds ([[#^def-c5-6-1|Def. §C5.6.1]]), with de Sitter space as the basic example ([[#^thm-c5-6-4|Theorem §C5.6.4]]); the Newtonian limit with $\Lambda$ ([[#^thm-c5-6-5|Theorem §C5.6.5]]), which lets the planets bound $\Lambda$ ([[#^ex-c5-6-1|Example §C5.6.1]]). Then the four-derivative terms: $R^2$ gives fourth-order equations ([[#^thm-c5-6-6|Theorem §C5.6.6]]), while the Gauss–Bonnet combination drops out of the field equations in four dimensions and integrates to the Euler characteristic ([[#^thm-c5-6-7|★ Theorem §C5.6.7]], [[#^ex-c5-6-2|Example §C5.6.2]]).

**Setup.** A four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold $(M, g)$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]) with its Levi-Civita connection ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-1|Theorem §C3.6.1]]); the action $S = \frac{1}{16\pi}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,(R - 2\Lambda) + S_M$ with a real constant $\Lambda$ of dimension length$^{-2}$ and a matter action $S_M$ whose Hilbert tensor is $T_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C5.3 Coupling to Matter꞉ the Hilbert Energy–Momentum Tensor#^def-c5-3-1|Def. §C5.3.1]]); variations $\delta g^{\mu\nu}$ of compact support, as in [[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action|§C5.2]], so that boundary terms vanish ([[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action#^cau-c5-2-1|§C5.2, Caution: The boundary term]]). The statements about $\Lambda$ are exact consequences of the action, classical, for either sign of $\Lambda$. The Newtonian limit adds the weak-field model [[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^mod-c5-5-1|Model §C5.5.1]] with $|\Lambda| r^2 \ll 1$ over the region considered. The statements about curvature-squared terms hold in the dimension $n$ stated in each; the Chern–Gauss–Bonnet theorem is about compact Riemannian 4-manifolds. The measured value of $\Lambda$ is a dated result that rests on cosmological models, which these notes have not built (cosmology, planned): only the number and the definitions needed to read it are used. Numbers were checked by script.

## The cosmological term

Couzens' starting point: the Einstein–Hilbert density $\sqrt{-g}\,R$ contains two derivatives of the metric, but there is a simpler scalar density still, the volume element $\sqrt{-g}$ itself, which contains none. Adding a constant multiple of it is the only change to the action that does not add derivatives, because a scalar built from $g_{\mu\nu}$ alone is a constant: at every point there are coordinates in which $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]), so such a scalar takes the same value everywhere.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.6.1: The Λ Term from √−g(R − 2Λ)
> Let the total action be the Einstein–Hilbert action ([[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action#^def-c5-2-1|Def. §C5.2.1]]) with a constant $\Lambda$ subtracted from $R$, plus a matter action with Hilbert tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C5.3 Coupling to Matter꞉ the Hilbert Energy–Momentum Tensor#^def-c5-3-1|Def. §C5.3.1]]):
>
> $$
> S[g, \psi] = \frac{1}{16\pi}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\bigl(R - 2\Lambda\bigr) + S_M[g, \psi] .
> $$
>
> 1. **Variation.** For variations $\delta g^{\mu\nu}$ of compact support,
>
> $$
> \delta S = \frac{1}{16\pi}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\bigl(G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} - 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}\bigr)\,\delta g^{\mu\nu} ,
> $$
>
> and $\delta S = 0$ for all $\delta g^{\mu\nu}$ gives Einstein's equations with cosmological constant ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^pr-c5-1-4|Principle §C5.1.4]]; [[§C5.3 Coupling to Matter꞉ the Hilbert Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c5-3-1|Theorem §C5.3.1]]):
>
> $$
> G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu},\ \ \Lambda \text{ in } \mathrm m^{-2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> The term $-2\Lambda\sqrt{-g}$ contributes $\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ and nothing else.
> 2. **Trace and trace reversal.** $R = 4\Lambda - 8\pi T$ with $T = g^{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu}$, and
>
> $$
> R_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi\bigl(T_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12T\,g_{\mu\nu}\bigr) + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}
> $$
>
> (for $\Lambda = 0$, [[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^thm-c5-1-5|Theorem §C5.1.5]]).
> 3. **Vacuum.** Where $T_{\mu\nu} = 0$: $R_{\mu\nu} = \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ and $R = 4\Lambda$. Minkowski spacetime is a solution only if $\Lambda = 0$; for $\Lambda = 0$ the vacuum equations are Ricci-flatness ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^thm-c5-1-6|Theorem §C5.1.6]]).
> 4. **Consistency.** $\nabla^\mu(\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}) = 0$, so the equations still imply $\nabla^\mu T_{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^thm-c5-1-7|Theorem §C5.1.7]]).
>
> *Model:* none beyond the action; *applies:* exact, classical, any $\Lambda \in \mathbf R$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §5.1.2, eqs. (5.23)–(5.25) (PDF p. 108, printed p. 107); §5.1.5, eqs. (5.48)–(5.51) (pp. 111–112) · d'Inverno & Vickers §13.3, eqs. (13.5)–(13.7) (p. 220; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), Exercises 13.1, 13.4 (p. 235) · Schutz §8.1, eqs. (8.4)–(8.8) (p. 184)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (split the action). $S = S_{\mathrm{EH}} + S_\Lambda + S_M$ with
>
> $$
> S_{\mathrm{EH}} = \frac{1}{16\pi}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,R, \qquad S_\Lambda = -\frac{2\Lambda}{16\pi}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g} = -\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (Einstein–Hilbert part). By [[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action#^thm-c5-2-5|Theorem §C5.2.5]], $\delta S_{\mathrm{EH}} = \frac{1}{16\pi}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,G_{\mu\nu}\,\delta g^{\mu\nu}$.
>
> **Step 3** (the $\Lambda$ part). $\Lambda$ is a constant, so only $\sqrt{-g}$ varies; by [[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]], $\delta\sqrt{-g} = -\tfrac12\sqrt{-g}\,g_{\mu\nu}\,\delta g^{\mu\nu}$. Hence
>
> $$
> \delta S_\Lambda = -\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi}\int d^4x\,\Bigl(-\tfrac12\sqrt{-g}\,g_{\mu\nu}\,\delta g^{\mu\nu}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{16\pi}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}\,\delta g^{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (matter part). [[§C5.3 Coupling to Matter꞉ the Hilbert Energy–Momentum Tensor#^def-c5-3-1|Def. §C5.3.1]] says $\delta S_M/\delta g^{\mu\nu} = -\tfrac12\sqrt{-g}\,T_{\mu\nu}$, so
>
> $$
> \delta S_M = \int d^4x\,\frac{\delta S_M}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}\,\delta g^{\mu\nu} = -\frac12\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,T_{\mu\nu}\,\delta g^{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{16\pi}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\bigl(-8\pi T_{\mu\nu}\bigr)\,\delta g^{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (sum; the field equation). Adding Steps 2–4 gives the formula of part 1. Write $E_{\mu\nu} = G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} - 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$, a continuous symmetric tensor. Suppose $E_{\alpha\beta}(p) > 0$ for some chart component at a point $p$ (the case $< 0$ is the same with a sign). Take $\delta g^{\mu\nu} = \varphi\,(\delta^\mu_\alpha\delta^\nu_\beta + \delta^\mu_\beta\delta^\nu_\alpha)$ with a smooth $\varphi \ge 0$, $\varphi(p) > 0$, supported in the chart where $E_{\alpha\beta} > 0$. Then
>
> $$
> \delta S = \frac{1}{16\pi}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\varphi\,\bigl(E_{\alpha\beta} + E_{\beta\alpha}\bigr) = \frac{1}{8\pi}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\varphi\,E_{\alpha\beta} > 0 ,
> $$
>
> contradicting $\delta S = 0$. So $E_{\mu\nu} = 0$ everywhere: $G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$. Restoring: the coupling $8\pi$ becomes $8\pi G/c^4$ ([[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^thm-c5-5-2|Theorem §C5.5.2]]); $G_{\mu\nu}$ and $\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ are curvatures, both in $\mathrm m^{-2}$.
>
> **Step 6** (trace). Contract with $g^{\mu\nu}$. In four dimensions $g^{\mu\nu}G_{\mu\nu} = -R$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^def-c3-11-3|Def. §C3.11.3]]) and $g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\nu} = \delta^\mu{}_\mu = 4$: $-R + 4\Lambda = 8\pi T$, hence $R = 4\Lambda - 8\pi T$.
>
> **Step 7** (trace reversal). Solve the field equation for $R_{\mu\nu}$ and insert Step 6:
>
> $$
> R_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu} - \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} + \tfrac12R\,g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu} - \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} + \tfrac12\bigl(4\Lambda - 8\pi T\bigr)g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi\bigl(T_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12T\,g_{\mu\nu}\bigr) + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> **Step 8** (vacuum). With $T_{\mu\nu} = 0$, also $T = 0$, and Steps 6–7 give $R = 4\Lambda$, $R_{\mu\nu} = \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$. Minkowski spacetime has zero Riemann tensor ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-5|Theorem §C3.9.5]]), hence $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$, and the vacuum equation reads $0 = \Lambda\eta_{\mu\nu}$; its $00$ component is $0 = -\Lambda$.
>
> **Step 9** (divergence). Metric compatibility $\nabla_\lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^def-c3-6-2|Def. §C3.6.2]]) and the constancy of $\Lambda$ give $\nabla^\mu(\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}) = \Lambda\,g^{\mu\lambda}\nabla_\lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 0$. With $\nabla^\mu G_{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^thm-c3-11-2|Theorem §C3.11.2]], part 3), the divergence of the field equation is $0 + 0 = 8\pi\nabla^\mu T_{\mu\nu}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - $\Lambda$ enters only through the variation of the volume element $\sqrt{-g}$; the cosmological term is the field equation's response to "how much spacetime volume there is", with no derivatives of $g$ (Couzens' "even simpler" term).
> - The term is consistent with the contracted Bianchi identity for the same reason $g_{\mu\nu}$ was allowed in the uniqueness theorem: it is covariantly constant.
> - The same action term gives $\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ in every dimension (checked by script on conformally flat metrics in three and four dimensions); only the trace in Step 6 uses $n = 4$.

## The energy of empty space

The term $\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ was put on the curvature side, but it contains no curvature. Moved to the right-hand side it looks like a source, and the question is what kind of matter it describes. Couzens reads the sign through $L = T - V$: in $R - 2\Lambda$, $\Lambda$ plays the role of a potential energy. The computation below makes this precise.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.6.2: Λ as Vacuum Energy
> In the setting of [[#^thm-c5-6-1|Theorem §C5.6.1]]:
> 1. The field equations are equivalent to
>
> $$
> G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi\bigl(T_{\mu\nu} + T^{(\Lambda)}_{\mu\nu}\bigr), \qquad T^{(\Lambda)}_{\mu\nu} \equiv -\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi}\,g_{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> 2. $T^{(\Lambda)}$ is the energy–momentum tensor of a perfect fluid ([[§C4.3 Perfect Fluids#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]], [[§C4.3 Perfect Fluids#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]]) with
>
> $$
> \rho_\Lambda = \frac{\Lambda}{8\pi}, \qquad p_\Lambda = -\rho_\Lambda \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: energy density } \rho_\Lambda c^2 = \frac{\Lambda c^4}{8\pi G},\ \ p_\Lambda = -\rho_\Lambda c^2\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> for every choice of the four-velocity $U$: the vacuum energy of [[§C4.3 Perfect Fluids#^ex-c4-3-2|Example §C4.3.2]], which every observer measures as the same energy density $\rho_\Lambda$, no momentum and the pressure $-\rho_\Lambda$. A positive $\Lambda$ is a positive energy density with a tension.
> 3. $T^{(\Lambda)}$ is conserved by itself, its trace is $-4\rho_\Lambda$, and its active gravitational mass density is
>
> $$
> \rho_\Lambda + 3p_\Lambda = -2\rho_\Lambda = -\frac{\Lambda}{4\pi}
> $$
>
> ([[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^thm-c5-5-4|Theorem §C5.5.4]]): for $\Lambda > 0$ it repels, and it violates the strong energy condition ([[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^def-c4-5-3|Def. §C4.5.3]]; [[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^ex-c4-5-2|Example §C4.5.2]]).
> 4. ⚑ **Only the sum is measured.** If the matter contains a constant vacuum energy density $\rho_{\mathrm{vac}}$, $T_{\mu\nu} = T'_{\mu\nu} - \rho_{\mathrm{vac}}\,g_{\mu\nu}$, the field equations depend on $\Lambda$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{vac}}$ only through
>
> $$
> \Lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} = \Lambda + 8\pi\rho_{\mathrm{vac}} \qquad \bigl(\text{restored: } \Lambda + 8\pi G\rho_{\mathrm{vac}}/c^2,\ \rho_{\mathrm{vac}} \text{ a mass density}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> No gravitational measurement separates a cosmological constant in the action from a constant energy density of the vacuum.
>
> *Model:* the action of [[#^thm-c5-6-1|Theorem §C5.6.1]]; in part 4 a vacuum energy of the Lorentz-invariant form $-\rho_{\mathrm{vac}}g_{\mu\nu}$ (Hartle: the only energy–momentum tensor built from a constant alone); *applies:* exact.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, Example 22.6, eq. (22.38) (p. 480); §18.3, eqs. (18.27)–(18.28) (p. 376) · d'Inverno & Vickers §13.3 (p. 221: $+\Lambda g_{ab}$ "as a source term … of so-called dark-energy") · Couzens, GR1, §5.1.2 (p. 107: $\Lambda$ "playing the role of the potential energy") · Schutz, Exercise 8.18(b) (p. 199)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (move the term). Subtract $\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ from both sides of $G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$: $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu} - \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi\Bigl(T_{\mu\nu} - \frac{\Lambda}{8\pi}g_{\mu\nu}\Bigr)$.
>
> **Step 2** (fluid form). A perfect fluid has $T_{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)U_\mu U_\nu + p\,g_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C4.3 Perfect Fluids#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]]). With $p = -\rho$ the first term drops for every $U$: $T_{\mu\nu} = 0\cdot U_\mu U_\nu - \rho\,g_{\mu\nu} = -\rho\,g_{\mu\nu}$. Comparing with $-(\Lambda/8\pi)g_{\mu\nu}$ gives $\rho = \Lambda/8\pi$.
>
> **Step 3** (what an observer measures). For a unit timelike $u$, $g(u, u) = -1$, and a unit spatial $e$ with $g(u, e) = 0$ ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^def-c4-2-2|Def. §C4.2.2]]):
>
> $$
> T^{(\Lambda)}(u, u) = -\rho_\Lambda\,g(u, u) = \rho_\Lambda, \qquad -T^{(\Lambda)}(u, e) = \rho_\Lambda\,g(u, e) = 0, \qquad T^{(\Lambda)}(e, e) = -\rho_\Lambda .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (restoring units). In geometric units $\rho_\Lambda$ is a length$^{-2}$. An energy density $\varepsilon$ in $\mathrm{J/m^3}$ becomes a length$^{-2}$ on multiplication by $G/c^4$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]), so $G\rho_\Lambda c^2/c^4 = \Lambda/8\pi$ for a mass density $\rho_\Lambda$, i.e. $\rho_\Lambda = \Lambda c^2/(8\pi G)$ and $\rho_\Lambda c^2 = \Lambda c^4/(8\pi G)$.
>
> **Step 5** (conservation, trace). $\nabla^\mu T^{(\Lambda)}_{\mu\nu} = -(\Lambda/8\pi)\nabla^\mu g_{\mu\nu} = 0$ by [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^def-c3-6-2|Def. §C3.6.2]]. Trace: $g^{\mu\nu}T^{(\Lambda)}_{\mu\nu} = -\rho_\Lambda\cdot4$.
>
> **Step 6** (active mass). $\rho_\Lambda + 3p_\Lambda = \rho_\Lambda - 3\rho_\Lambda = -2\rho_\Lambda = -\Lambda/4\pi$. Directly from the trace-reversed source for an observer at rest in a local inertial frame ($g_{00} = -1$):
>
> $$
> 8\pi\Bigl(T^{(\Lambda)}_{00} - \tfrac12T^{(\Lambda)}g_{00}\Bigr) = 8\pi\Bigl(\rho_\Lambda - \tfrac12(-4\rho_\Lambda)(-1)\Bigr) = 8\pi(\rho_\Lambda - 2\rho_\Lambda) = -8\pi\rho_\Lambda = 4\pi(\rho_\Lambda + 3p_\Lambda) ,
> $$
>
> the combination that [[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^thm-c5-5-4|Theorem §C5.5.4]] identifies as the source of $R_{00}$. The strong energy condition requires $\rho + 3p \ge 0$ for a perfect fluid ([[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^thm-c4-5-1|Theorem §C4.5.1]]); here it is $-2\rho_\Lambda < 0$ for $\Lambda > 0$.
>
> **Step 7** (only the sum). Insert $T_{\mu\nu} = T'_{\mu\nu} - \rho_{\mathrm{vac}}g_{\mu\nu}$:
>
> $$
> G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T'_{\mu\nu} - 8\pi\rho_{\mathrm{vac}}g_{\mu\nu} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad G_{\mu\nu} + \bigl(\Lambda + 8\pi\rho_{\mathrm{vac}}\bigr)g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T'_{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> Every solution, and hence every measurement made with it, depends on the pair only through $\Lambda + 8\pi\rho_{\mathrm{vac}}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - "Cosmological constant" and "vacuum energy" are two names for one term; which side of the equation it is written on is bookkeeping.
> - The negative pressure is forced by Lorentz invariance ($T \propto g$), not chosen; and it outweighs the energy density in the source of gravity, $\rho + 3p = -2\rho$.

## Vacuum with Λ: Einstein manifolds

Without $\Lambda$, empty spacetime means $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$. With it, [[#^thm-c5-6-1|Theorem §C5.6.1]], part 3, gives $R_{\mu\nu} = \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$: the Ricci tensor is not zero but proportional to the metric, with the same constant everywhere. The condition makes sense on any manifold with a metric, and geometers studied it under its own name.

> [!definition] Definition §C5.6.1: Einstein Manifold
> *Structure.* A manifold $M$ of dimension $n$ with a metric $g$ of any signature ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]) is an **Einstein manifold** (its metric an **Einstein metric**) if its Ricci tensor ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^def-c3-11-1|Def. §C3.11.1]]), a symmetric $(0, 2)$ tensor field, is a constant multiple of the metric:
>
> $$
> R_{\mu\nu} = \lambda\,g_{\mu\nu}, \qquad \lambda \in \mathbf R \text{ constant} .
> $$
>
> Contracting with $g^{\mu\nu}$ gives the scalar curvature ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^def-c3-11-2|Def. §C3.11.2]]) $R = n\lambda$, a constant. For $\lambda = 0$ the manifold is **Ricci-flat**.
>
> *In general relativity* ($n = 4$, Lorentzian): a spacetime solves the vacuum equations with cosmological constant $\Lambda$ exactly when it is an Einstein manifold with $\lambda = \Lambda$ ([[#^thm-c5-6-1|Theorem §C5.6.1]], part 3); for $\Lambda = 0$, when it is Ricci-flat ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^thm-c5-1-6|Theorem §C5.1.6]]).
>
> *Why "constant".* Allowing a function, $R_{\mu\nu} = f\,g_{\mu\nu}$, adds nothing for $n \ge 3$: then $R = nf$, and the twice-contracted Bianchi identity $\nabla^\mu R_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\partial_\nu R$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^thm-c3-11-2|Theorem §C3.11.2]], part 2) with $\nabla g = 0$ reads $\partial_\nu f = \tfrac n2\partial_\nu f$, so $(\tfrac n2 - 1)\partial_\nu f = 0$ and $f$ is constant on a connected $M$. For $n = 2$ every metric has $R_{\mu\nu} = K g_{\mu\nu}$ with a function $K$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^ex-c3-11-3|Example §C3.11.3]]), and "Einstein" with a constant $\lambda$ is a real restriction.
>
> *Examples.* Every space of constant curvature, $R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = K(g_{\rho\mu}g_{\sigma\nu} - g_{\rho\nu}g_{\sigma\mu})$, is Einstein with $\lambda = (n - 1)K$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^ex-c3-11-2|Example §C3.11.2]]): the round spheres, hyperbolic space, de Sitter and anti-de Sitter space ([[#^thm-c5-6-4|Theorem §C5.6.4]]). The converse fails: Schwarzschild–de Sitter space is Einstein without constant curvature ([[#^thm-c5-6-4|Theorem §C5.6.4]], part 4).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §5.1.2, after eq. (5.25) (p. 107: "Metrics satisfying this property are known as Einstein metrics") · the "why constant" argument written here; checked by script*

^def-c5-6-1

The simplest Einstein spacetimes are those that look the same at every point and in every direction: spaces of constant curvature. The flat slicing of de Sitter space was met as an example of this kind in [[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^ex-c3-11-2|Example §C3.11.2]], Step 3. The static form below is the one that will be compared with Schwarzschild's metric (C6, planned).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.6.4: de Sitter Space (Static Patch) Is an Einstein Manifold
> For a static, spherically symmetric metric of the form
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -f(r)\,dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{f(r)} + r^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr)
> $$
>
> on the region where $f > 0$:
> 1. **de Sitter** ($\Lambda > 0$). With $f = 1 - r^2/\ell^2$, $\ell = \sqrt{3/\Lambda}$, on $0 < r < \ell$, the Riemann tensor has the constant-curvature form
>
> $$
> R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{\ell^2}\bigl(g_{\rho\mu}g_{\sigma\nu} - g_{\rho\nu}g_{\sigma\mu}\bigr), \qquad K = \frac{1}{\ell^2} = \frac{\Lambda}{3} ,
> $$
>
> hence $R_{\mu\nu} = \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$, $R = 4\Lambda$, $G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 0$: a vacuum solution with cosmological constant $\Lambda$, Einstein with $\lambda = \Lambda$ ([[#^def-c5-6-1|Def. §C5.6.1]]). Its Kretschmann scalar is $R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}R^{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = 24/\ell^4 = 8\Lambda^2/3$.
> 2. **The edge of the chart.** At $r = \ell$, $g_{tt} = 0$ and $g_{rr}$ diverges while every curvature component stays $\pm1/\ell^2$ in an orthonormal frame: the chart ends at a horizon of the observer at $r = 0$, not at a singularity (the analogue for Schwarzschild is C7, planned). For the observed $\Lambda$, $\ell = 1.66\times10^{26}\ \mathrm m$ ([[#^law-c5-6-3|Law §C5.6.3]]).
> 3. **Anti-de Sitter** ($\Lambda < 0$). With $f = 1 + r^2/\ell^2$, $\ell = \sqrt{-3/\Lambda}$, for all $r > 0$: the same form with $K = -1/\ell^2 = \Lambda/3$, and $R_{\mu\nu} = \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$.
> 4. **Schwarzschild–de Sitter.** With $f = 1 - 2M/r - \Lambda r^2/3$: $R_{\mu\nu} = \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$, an Einstein manifold, but not of constant curvature: $R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}R^{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = 48M^2/r^6 + 8\Lambda^2/3$ depends on $r$. For $\Lambda = 0$ it is the Schwarzschild metric, Ricci-flat ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^ex-c5-1-2|Example §C5.1.2]]; derived in C6, planned).
>
> *Model:* vacuum with cosmological constant; *applies:* exact, on the stated range of $r$ (outside $r = 0$ for part 4).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §5.1.2, eq. (5.25) (p. 107; the vacuum condition) · d'Inverno & Vickers §13.3 (p. 221: de Sitter's vacuum solution with the cosmological term) · the static form and the curvature computed here; checked by script*

^thm-c5-6-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Christoffel symbols). For $g = \operatorname{diag}(-f, 1/f, r^2, r^2\sin^2\theta)$ with $f = f(r)$, the Christoffel formula ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]) gives, with $f' = df/dr$, the non-zero symbols
>
> $$
> \Gamma^t_{tr} = \frac{f'}{2f}, \quad \Gamma^r_{tt} = \tfrac12ff', \quad \Gamma^r_{rr} = -\frac{f'}{2f}, \quad \Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -rf, \quad \Gamma^r_{\varphi\varphi} = -rf\sin^2\theta ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = \Gamma^\varphi_{r\varphi} = \frac1r, \quad \Gamma^\theta_{\varphi\varphi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta, \quad \Gamma^\varphi_{\theta\varphi} = \cot\theta
> $$
>
> (and those related by symmetry in the lower indices). For instance $\Gamma^r_{tt} = -\tfrac12g^{rr}\partial_rg_{tt} = -\tfrac12f\,\partial_r(-f) = \tfrac12ff'$ and $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -\tfrac12g^{rr}\partial_rg_{\theta\theta} = -\tfrac12f\cdot2r$.
>
> **Step 2** (Riemann components). With $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma} + \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\rho_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\sigma}$ ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]]) and nothing depending on $t$:
>
> $$
> R^t{}_{rtr} = -\partial_r\Gamma^t_{tr} + \Gamma^t_{tr}\Gamma^r_{rr} - \Gamma^t_{rt}\Gamma^t_{tr} = -\Bigl(\frac{f''}{2f} - \frac{f'^2}{2f^2}\Bigr) - \frac{f'^2}{4f^2} - \frac{f'^2}{4f^2} = -\frac{f''}{2f} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> R^t{}_{\theta t\theta} = \Gamma^t_{tr}\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = \frac{f'}{2f}(-rf) = -\frac{rf'}{2} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> R^r{}_{\theta r\theta} = \partial_r\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} + \Gamma^r_{rr}\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} - \Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = (-f - rf') + \frac{rf'}{2} + f = -\frac{rf'}{2} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> R^\theta{}_{\varphi\theta\varphi} = \partial_\theta\Gamma^\theta_{\varphi\varphi} + \Gamma^\theta_{\theta r}\Gamma^r_{\varphi\varphi} - \Gamma^\theta_{\varphi\varphi}\Gamma^\varphi_{\theta\varphi} = -(\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta) - f\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = (1 - f)\sin^2\theta ,
> $$
>
> and $R^t{}_{\varphi t\varphi} = \sin^2\theta\,R^t{}_{\theta t\theta}$, $R^r{}_{\varphi r\varphi} = \sin^2\theta\,R^r{}_{\theta r\theta}$ by the same computation with the extra factor of $g_{\varphi\varphi}$. Components not related to these by the symmetries of [[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-1|Theorem §C3.10.1]] vanish (script).
>
> **Step 3** (orthonormal frame). Lower with $g$ and divide by the norms of the two coordinate pairs, $|g_{tt}g_{rr}| = 1$, $|g_{tt}g_{\theta\theta}| = fr^2$, $|g_{rr}g_{\theta\theta}| = r^2/f$, $g_{\theta\theta}g_{\varphi\varphi} = r^4\sin^2\theta$:
>
> $$
> R_{\hat t\hat r\hat t\hat r} = \frac{f''}{2}, \qquad R_{\hat t\hat\theta\hat t\hat\theta} = R_{\hat t\hat\varphi\hat t\hat\varphi} = \frac{f'}{2r}, \qquad R_{\hat r\hat\theta\hat r\hat\theta} = R_{\hat r\hat\varphi\hat r\hat\varphi} = -\frac{f'}{2r}, \qquad R_{\hat\theta\hat\varphi\hat\theta\hat\varphi} = \frac{1 - f}{r^2} ;
> $$
>
> e.g. $R_{trtr} = g_{tt}R^t{}_{rtr} = (-f)(-f''/2f) = f''/2$ and $R_{\theta\varphi\theta\varphi} = r^2(1 - f)\sin^2\theta$, divided by $r^4\sin^2\theta$.
>
> **Step 4** (Ricci tensor). $R_{\mu\nu} = g^{\rho\lambda}R_{\lambda\mu\rho\nu}$ for the diagonal metric, with Step 2:
>
> $$
> R_{tt} = f\cdot\frac{f''}{2} + \frac{1}{r^2}\cdot\frac{rff'}{2} + \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\cdot\frac{rff'\sin^2\theta}{2} = f\Bigl(\frac{f''}{2} + \frac{f'}{r}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> $$
> R_{rr} = -\frac1f\Bigl(\frac{f''}{2} + \frac{f'}{r}\Bigr), \qquad R_{\theta\theta} = -\frac{rf'}{2} - \frac{rf'}{2} + (1 - f) = 1 - f - rf', \qquad R_{\varphi\varphi} = \sin^2\theta\,R_{\theta\theta} .
> $$
>
> So $R_{\mu\nu} = \lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ exactly when $\tfrac12f'' + f'/r = -\lambda$ (from $tt$ and $rr$, since $g_{tt} = -f$, $g_{rr} = 1/f$) and $1 - f - rf' = \lambda r^2$ (from $\theta\theta$).
>
> **Step 5** (de Sitter). $f = 1 - r^2/\ell^2$: $f' = -2r/\ell^2$, $f'' = -2/\ell^2$. Then $\tfrac12f'' + f'/r = -1/\ell^2 - 2/\ell^2 = -3/\ell^2$ and $1 - f - rf' = r^2/\ell^2 + 2r^2/\ell^2 = 3r^2/\ell^2$: both conditions hold with $\lambda = 3/\ell^2 = \Lambda$. Step 3 gives the frame components $-1/\ell^2$ (pairs with $\hat t$) and $+1/\ell^2$ (spatial pairs), which are those of $K(\eta_{\hat a\hat c}\eta_{\hat b\hat d} - \eta_{\hat a\hat d}\eta_{\hat b\hat c})$ with $K = 1/\ell^2$: $\eta_{\hat t\hat t}\eta_{\hat r\hat r} = -1$, $\eta_{\hat r\hat r}\eta_{\hat\theta\hat\theta} = +1$. Both tensors have the Riemann symmetries and agree on all components, so they are equal; $R = 4\Lambda$ and $G_{\mu\nu} = \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} - 2\Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = -\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$. Kretschmann: each of the six pairs contributes four equal squares, $4\times6\times\ell^{-4} = 24/\ell^4$.
>
> **Step 6** (horizon). $f(\ell) = 0$, so $g_{tt} = -f \to 0$ and $g_{rr} = 1/f \to \infty$ as $r \to \ell$, while the frame components of Step 5 are the constants $\pm1/\ell^2$.
>
> **Step 7** (anti-de Sitter). $f = 1 + r^2/\ell^2$: $\tfrac12f'' + f'/r = 1/\ell^2 + 2/\ell^2 = 3/\ell^2$, $1 - f - rf' = -3r^2/\ell^2$, so $\lambda = -3/\ell^2 = \Lambda$; the frame components are those of Step 5 with the opposite sign, $K = -1/\ell^2$.
>
> **Step 8** (Schwarzschild–de Sitter). $f = 1 - 2M/r - \Lambda r^2/3$: $f' = 2M/r^2 - 2\Lambda r/3$, $f'' = -4M/r^3 - 2\Lambda/3$. Then
>
> $$
> \tfrac12f'' + \frac{f'}{r} = -\frac{2M}{r^3} - \frac\Lambda3 + \frac{2M}{r^3} - \frac{2\Lambda}{3} = -\Lambda, \qquad 1 - f - rf' = \frac{2M}{r} + \frac{\Lambda r^2}{3} - \frac{2M}{r} + \frac{2\Lambda r^2}{3} = \Lambda r^2 :
> $$
>
> Einstein with $\lambda = \Lambda$. The frame components of Step 3 are $f''/2 = -2M/r^3 - \Lambda/3$, $\pm f'/(2r) = \pm(M/r^3 - \Lambda/3)$ (two pairs each), $(1 - f)/r^2 = 2M/r^3 + \Lambda/3$; the Kretschmann scalar is four times the sum of their squares,
>
> $$
> 4\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{2M}{r^3} + \frac\Lambda3\Bigr)^2 + 4\Bigl(\frac{M}{r^3} - \frac\Lambda3\Bigr)^2 + \Bigl(\frac{2M}{r^3} + \frac\Lambda3\Bigr)^2\Bigr] = 4\Bigl[\frac{12M^2}{r^6} + \frac{2\Lambda^2}{3}\Bigr] = \frac{48M^2}{r^6} + \frac{8\Lambda^2}{3} ,
> $$
>
> the cross terms $\tfrac{4M\Lambda}{3r^3}(1 - 2 + 1)$ cancelling. It varies with $r$, so the curvature is not constant.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - For this family of metrics the vacuum equations with $\Lambda$ reduce to two ordinary equations for $f$; $1$, $-2M/r$ and $-\Lambda r^2/3$ are the pieces of their solution, and Step 8 shows they superpose.
> - The term $-\Lambda r^2/3$ in $f$ is the relativistic form of the potential $-\Lambda r^2/6$ of [[#^thm-c5-6-5|Theorem §C5.6.5]]: $f = -g_{tt} = 1 + 2\Phi$.

## The Newtonian limit with Λ

Since $\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ sources curvature even in empty space, it must show up in the motion of planets. The Newtonian limit of [[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling|§C5.5]] can be redone with the extra term.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.6.5: The Newtonian Limit with Λ
> In the weak, static, slow-source model ([[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^mod-c5-5-1|Model §C5.5.1]]), $g_{tt} = -(1 + 2\Phi)$, with matter of density $\rho$ and negligible pressure, and with $|\Lambda|r^2 \ll 1$ over the region considered:
> 1. The $00$ component of the field equations of [[#^thm-c5-6-1|Theorem §C5.6.1]] becomes
>
> $$
> \nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi\rho - \Lambda = 4\pi\bigl(\rho - 2\rho_\Lambda\bigr) \qquad \bigl(\text{restored: } \nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho - \Lambda c^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 2. ⚑ For a point mass $M$, the spherically symmetric solution regular away from $r = 0$ is
>
> $$
> \Phi = -\frac Mr - \frac{\Lambda r^2}{6}, \qquad \frac{d^2\vec x}{dt^2} = -\nabla\Phi = \Bigl(-\frac{M}{r^2} + \frac{\Lambda r}{3}\Bigr)\hat r \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } -\frac{GM}{r^2} + \frac{\Lambda c^2r}{3}\Bigr) :
> $$
>
> for $\Lambda > 0$ a repulsion that grows linearly with distance and is the same for every body. $-(1 + 2\Phi)$ is $g_{tt}$ of Schwarzschild–de Sitter ([[#^thm-c5-6-4|Theorem §C5.6.4]], part 4).
> 3. Circular orbits of radius $r$ have angular velocity $\omega^2 = M/r^3 - \Lambda/3$, so $\Lambda$ changes Kepler's third law by the fraction $\delta\omega/\omega = -\Lambda r^3/(6M)$ at first order.
>
> *Model:* [[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^mod-c5-5-1|Model §C5.5.1]] with $|\Lambda| r^2 \ll 1$; *applies:* to first order in $\Phi$, $\Lambda r^2$ and $v^2$.
>
> *Source: Schutz, Exercise 8.18(a) (p. 199: "Find the Newtonian limit") · d'Inverno & Vickers §13.3 (p. 221: "cosmic repulsion" for $\Lambda > 0$ in the Newtonian model) · worked here; checked by script*

^thm-c5-6-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the curvature side). For a static weak field with $g_{tt} = -(1 + 2\Phi)$, $R_{00} = \nabla^2\Phi$ to first order ([[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^thm-c5-5-2|Theorem §C5.5.2]]; the script confirms that static perturbations of the spatial metric do not enter $R_{00}$ at this order).
>
> **Step 2** (the source side). Trace-reversed form ([[#^thm-c5-6-1|Theorem §C5.6.1]], part 2) with $T_{00} = \rho$, $T = -\rho$ (pressure neglected), $g_{00} = -(1 + 2\Phi)$:
>
> $$
> R_{00} = 8\pi\Bigl(\rho - \tfrac12(-\rho)\bigl(-(1 + 2\Phi)\bigr)\Bigr) - \Lambda(1 + 2\Phi) = 4\pi\rho - \Lambda + O(\rho\Phi, \Lambda\Phi) .
> $$
>
> The dropped terms are products of two small quantities. With $\Lambda = 8\pi\rho_\Lambda$ ([[#^thm-c5-6-2|Theorem §C5.6.2]]), $4\pi\rho - \Lambda = 4\pi(\rho - 2\rho_\Lambda)$. Equating with Step 1 gives part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (point mass). Split $\Phi = \Phi_1 + \Phi_2$ with $\nabla^2\Phi_1 = 4\pi M\delta^3(\vec x)$ and $\nabla^2\Phi_2 = -\Lambda$. The first is $\Phi_1 = -M/r$ ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]], part 2). For the second, $\nabla^2 r^2 = r^{-2}(r^2\cdot2r)' = r^{-2}\cdot6r^2 = 6$, so $\Phi_2 = -\Lambda r^2/6$. Any other spherically symmetric solution adds a solution of $\nabla^2u = 0$ for $r > 0$, $u = A + B/r$: $B$ would change the mass and is fixed by the source, and $A$ is a constant that does not change $\nabla\Phi$. The condition $\Phi \to 0$ at infinity of the Newtonian theory cannot be imposed: $\Phi_2$ grows, and the approximation holds only where $|\Lambda|r^2 \ll 1$.
>
> **Step 4** (acceleration). Slow free fall obeys $d^2\vec x/dt^2 = -\nabla\Phi$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]]); with $\Phi = \Phi(r)$, $\nabla\Phi = \Phi'(r)\hat r$ and $-\Phi'(r) = -\frac{d}{dr}\Bigl(-\frac Mr - \frac{\Lambda r^2}{6}\Bigr) = -\frac{M}{r^2} + \frac{\Lambda r}{3}$.
>
> Restoring: $\Phi \to \Phi/c^2$, $M \to GM/c^2$, $\Lambda$ unchanged ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]), so $-\Phi'$ in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$ is $-GM/r^2 + \Lambda c^2 r/3$. Comparison: $-(1 + 2\Phi) = -(1 - 2M/r - \Lambda r^2/3)$, the $g_{tt}$ of [[#^thm-c5-6-4|Theorem §C5.6.4]], part 4.
>
> **Step 5** (circular orbits). The centripetal acceleration $\omega^2r$ equals the inward pull: $\omega^2r = M/r^2 - \Lambda r/3$, so $\omega^2 = M/r^3 - \Lambda/3$. Then
>
> $$
> \omega = \sqrt{\frac{M}{r^3}}\Bigl(1 - \frac{\Lambda r^3}{3M}\Bigr)^{1/2} = \sqrt{\frac{M}{r^3}}\Bigl(1 - \frac{\Lambda r^3}{6M} + O\bigl(\Lambda^2\bigr)\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - In the Newtonian limit, $\Lambda$ is a uniform negative mass density $-2\rho_\Lambda$ filling space: the factor $2$ is the pressure's contribution $3p_\Lambda$ to the active mass.
> - Its effect relative to that of a mass $M$ grows like $\Lambda r^3/M$: negligible near any star, decisive only where the enclosed mass is small compared with $\Lambda r^3$ ([[#^ex-c5-6-1|Example §C5.6.1]]).

## Why the action stops at R − 2Λ

$\sqrt{-g}$ has no derivatives of the metric and $\sqrt{-g}\,R$ has two. The next scalars in the count are quadratic in curvature and have four: Couzens lists $R^2$, $R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}$ and $R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ (5.26). Nothing in the principle of an action forbids adding them. The test is what they do to the field equations, and the simplest of them shows it.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.6.6: The R² Term Gives Fourth-Order Field Equations
> Let $S = \frac{1}{16\pi}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\bigl(R + \alpha R^2\bigr) + S_M$ with a constant $\alpha$ (dimension length$^2$).
> 1. For variations of compact support, $\delta\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,R^2 = \int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,E_{\mu\nu}\,\delta g^{\mu\nu}$ with
>
> $$
> E_{\mu\nu} = 2R\,R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12R^2g_{\mu\nu} + 2g_{\mu\nu}\Box R - 2\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu R ,
> $$
>
> so the field equations are $G_{\mu\nu} + \alpha E_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$.
> 2. They are of **fourth** order in derivatives of the metric: $R$ contains second derivatives of $g$, and $\Box R$, $\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu R$ contain fourth. Initial data must now include more than $g$ and its first time derivative.
> 3. ⚑ **A new degree of freedom.** In four dimensions $g^{\mu\nu}E_{\mu\nu} = 6\,\Box R$, and the trace of the field equations is
>
> $$
> -R + 6\alpha\,\Box R = 8\pi T \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \Bigl(\Box - \frac{1}{6\alpha}\Bigr)R = \frac{4\pi}{3\alpha}\,T .
> $$
>
> In general relativity ($\alpha = 0$) the scalar curvature is fixed by the matter at the same point, $R = -8\pi T$. With $\alpha > 0$ it obeys a Klein–Gordon equation with mass parameter $m^2 = 1/(6\alpha)$ ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where the equation reads $(\Box_B + m^2)\varphi = 0$ with $\Box_B = -\Box$): it propagates in empty space, a degree of freedom beyond the two of [[§C5.4 Diffeomorphism Invariance#^thm-c5-4-5|Theorem §C5.4.5]].
> 4. **Size.** Relative to $G_{\mu\nu}$, the term $\alpha E_{\mu\nu}$ is of order $\alpha\times$ (curvature). Near a body of mass $M$ and radius $R_\ast$ the curvature is of order $M/R_\ast^3$; with $\alpha$ expressed in units of the Planck area $l_{\rm P}^2$ ([[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^def-c5-5-1|Def. §C5.5.1]]), the ratio is $1.1\times10^{-93}\,(\alpha/l_{\rm P}^2)$ at the Sun's surface and $3\times10^{-79}\,(\alpha/l_{\rm P}^2)$ at the surface of a neutron star.
>
> *Model:* the action above, classical; *applies:* exact for parts 1–3 (part 3 in four dimensions); part 4 an order-of-magnitude estimate.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §5.1.3, eq. (5.26) (p. 107: such terms "will introduce higher derivative terms"; "only become relevant for fast varying fields") · the variation worked here; trace and order checked by script*

^thm-c5-6-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (product rule). $\delta\bigl(\sqrt{-g}\,R^2\bigr) = (\delta\sqrt{-g})\,R^2 + \sqrt{-g}\,2R\,\delta R$, and $\delta\sqrt{-g} = -\tfrac12\sqrt{-g}\,g_{\mu\nu}\delta g^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]]).
>
> **Step 2** (variation of $R$). $\delta R = \delta(g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}) = R_{\mu\nu}\,\delta g^{\mu\nu} + g^{\mu\nu}\delta R_{\mu\nu}$.
>
> **Step 3** ($g^{\mu\nu}\delta R_{\mu\nu}$ in terms of $\delta g$). With $h_{\mu\nu} = \delta g_{\mu\nu}$ and $h = g^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}$, [[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action#^thm-c5-2-4|Theorem §C5.2.4]], part 2, gives $g^{\mu\nu}\delta R_{\mu\nu} = \nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu h^{\mu\nu} - \Box h$ (from $X^\mu = g^{\rho\nu}\delta\Gamma^\mu{}_{\rho\nu} - g^{\mu\nu}\delta\Gamma^\rho{}_{\rho\nu}$ with the covariant form of $\delta\Gamma$, [[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action#^thm-c5-2-2|Theorem §C5.2.2]]). Since $\delta g^{\mu\nu} = -h^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]]) and $h = -g_{\mu\nu}\delta g^{\mu\nu}$,
>
> $$
> g^{\mu\nu}\delta R_{\mu\nu} = -\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu\delta g^{\mu\nu} + g_{\mu\nu}\Box\,\delta g^{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (collect). Steps 1–3:
>
> $$
> \delta\bigl(\sqrt{-g}R^2\bigr) = \sqrt{-g}\Bigl[\bigl(2RR_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12R^2g_{\mu\nu}\bigr)\delta g^{\mu\nu} + 2R\,g_{\mu\nu}\Box\,\delta g^{\mu\nu} - 2R\,\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu\delta g^{\mu\nu}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (move the derivatives onto $R$). For a scalar $u$ and a vector $V$ of compact support, $\nabla_\mu(uV^\mu) = (\nabla_\mu u)V^\mu + u\nabla_\mu V^\mu$ and $\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\nabla_\mu(uV^\mu) = 0$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-4|Theorem §C3.6.4]], part 2), so $\int\sqrt{-g}\,u\nabla_\mu V^\mu = -\int\sqrt{-g}\,(\nabla_\mu u)V^\mu$. Applied twice, first with $V^\mu = \nabla_\nu\delta g^{\mu\nu}$, then with $V^\nu = \nabla_\mu R\,\delta g^{\mu\nu}$:
>
> $$
> \int\sqrt{-g}\,2R\,\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu\delta g^{\mu\nu} = -\int\sqrt{-g}\,2(\nabla_\mu R)\nabla_\nu\delta g^{\mu\nu} = \int\sqrt{-g}\,2(\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu R)\,\delta g^{\mu\nu} ,
> $$
>
> and in the same way, with $\Box = g^{\alpha\beta}\nabla_\alpha\nabla_\beta$ and $\nabla g = 0$,
>
> $$
> \int\sqrt{-g}\,2R\,g_{\mu\nu}\Box\,\delta g^{\mu\nu} = \int\sqrt{-g}\,2\,g_{\mu\nu}(\Box R)\,\delta g^{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> On a scalar $\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu R = \nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu R$ (torsion-free). Together with the first bracket of Step 4 this is $E_{\mu\nu}$ of part 1. The field equations follow as in [[#^thm-c5-6-1|Theorem §C5.6.1]], Step 5, with $\alpha E_{\mu\nu}$ added to $G_{\mu\nu}$.
>
> **Step 6** (order). $R = g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}$ contains $\partial^2g$ ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]]); each further $\nabla$ adds one derivative, so $\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu R$ and $\Box R$ contain $\partial^4g$. On the conformally flat metrics $g = e^{2\sigma}\eta$ the script finds $\partial_t^4\sigma$ in the Euler–Lagrange equation of $\sqrt{-g}R^2$ and no derivative beyond the second in that of $\sqrt{-g}R$.
>
> **Step 7** (trace). With $g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\nu} = 4$, $g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu} = R$, $g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu R = \Box R$: $g^{\mu\nu}E_{\mu\nu} = 2R^2 - 2R^2 + 8\Box R - 2\Box R = 6\,\Box R$, and $g^{\mu\nu}G_{\mu\nu} = -R$, so the trace is $-R + 6\alpha\Box R = 8\pi T$. Divide by $6\alpha$: $\Box R - R/(6\alpha) = 8\pi T/(6\alpha) = (4\pi/3\alpha)T$. (The script checks the trace in three and four dimensions, which fixes both coefficients $2$ and $-2$ of the $\Box R$ and $\nabla\nabla R$ terms.)
>
> **Step 8** (size). Curvature components near a mass $M$ at distance $R_\ast$ are of order $M/R_\ast^3$ ([[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling|§C5.5]]: tidal field $\partial_i\partial_j\Phi \sim M/R_\ast^3$). With $l_{\rm P}^2 = 2.61\times10^{-70}\ \mathrm m^2$: at the Sun's surface $M/R_\ast^3 = 1476.7\ \mathrm m/(6.957\times10^8\ \mathrm m)^3 = 4.39\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm m^{-2}$, product $1.1\times10^{-93}$; at a neutron star ($M = 2067\ \mathrm m$, $R_\ast = 12\ \mathrm{km}$) $1.20\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm m^{-2}$, product $3\times10^{-79}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The fourth derivatives come from integrating by parts the second derivatives hidden in $\delta R$. For $\sqrt{-g}R$ the same terms are a total divergence and drop out ([[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action#^thm-c5-2-4|Theorem §C5.2.4]]); multiplied by $R$ they are not.
> - What makes $R$ dynamical is the trace: it turns into a wave equation, so $R^2$ gravity carries an extra scalar field.

The other two terms of (5.26) also produce fourth-order equations in general (Couzens). One combination of the three is special: its variation vanishes identically in four dimensions.

> [!theorem] ★ Theorem §C5.6.7: The Gauss–Bonnet Combination in Four Dimensions
> Let $\mathcal G \equiv R^2 - 4R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu} + R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$, the **Gauss–Bonnet scalar**, on a manifold of dimension $n$ with a metric of any signature.
> 1. **(Lanczos.)** For variations of compact support, $\delta\int d^nx\,\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathcal G = \int d^nx\,\sqrt{|g|}\,H_{\mu\nu}\,\delta g^{\mu\nu}$ with
>
> $$
> H_{\mu\nu} = 2\bigl(R\,R_{\mu\nu} - 2R_{\mu\alpha}R^\alpha{}_\nu - 2R_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta}R^{\alpha\beta} + R_\mu{}^{\alpha\beta\gamma}R_{\nu\alpha\beta\gamma}\bigr) - \tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}\,\mathcal G ,
> $$
>
> an expression without derivatives of curvature, whose trace is $(2 - \tfrac n2)\,\mathcal G$. In $n = 4$, $H_{\mu\nu} = 0$ identically: adding $c\,\sqrt{-g}\,\mathcal G$ to the action changes no field equation. For $n \ge 5$, $H_{\mu\nu} \ne 0$ in general.
> 2. **Two independent terms.** In four dimensions the action (5.26) is equivalent to one with two couplings:
>
> $$
> c_1R^2 + c_2R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu} + c_3R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = (c_1 - c_3)R^2 + (c_2 + 4c_3)R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu} + c_3\,\mathcal G .
> $$
>
> 3. **(Chern–Gauss–Bonnet.)** On a compact oriented Riemannian 4-manifold without boundary,
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{32\pi^2}\int_M d^4x\,\sqrt g\,\mathcal G = \chi(M) ,
> $$
>
> the Euler characteristic of $M$, an integer fixed by the topology (the four-dimensional version of $V - E + F$, [[§25 Surfaces and the Euler Characteristic#^def-25-2|250 Def. §25.2]]: an alternating count of cells of dimensions $0$ to $4$). For $S^4$, $\chi = 2$; for $S^2\times S^2$, $\chi = 4$ ([[#^ex-c5-6-2|Example §C5.6.2]]).
>
> Couzens (5.27) prints the prefactor $1/8\pi^2$ and attributes the statement to Lovelock's theorem ([[#^cau-c5-6-1|Caution: Couzens' normalization of Gauss–Bonnet]]).
>
> *Model:* none (geometry); *applies:* part 1 identically in $n = 4$, every metric; part 3 compact Riemannian manifolds only (for a spacetime, part 1 is the statement that matters).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §5.1.3, eqs. (5.26)–(5.27) (PDF p. 109, printed p. 108) · part 1: C. Lanczos, Ann. Math. 39, 842 (1938), and D. Lovelock, J. Math. Phys. 12, 498 (1971); part 3: S.-S. Chern, Ann. Math. 45, 747 (1944) (cited, not proved here) · checks by script*

^thm-c5-6-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation (part 2; checks of parts 1 and 3)
> **Part 2.** Solve the definition of $\mathcal G$ for the Riemann square, $R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = \mathcal G - R^2 + 4R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}$, and substitute:
>
> $$
> c_1R^2 + c_2R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu} + c_3\bigl(\mathcal G - R^2 + 4R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}\bigr) = (c_1 - c_3)R^2 + (c_2 + 4c_3)R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu} + c_3\mathcal G .
> $$
>
> By part 1 the last term contributes nothing to the field equations in four dimensions.
>
> **Part 1, trace.** Contracting $H_{\mu\nu}$ with $g^{\mu\nu}$: $2\bigl(R^2 - 2R_{\mu\alpha}R^{\mu\alpha} - 2R_{\alpha\beta}R^{\alpha\beta} + R_{\mu\alpha\beta\gamma}R^{\mu\alpha\beta\gamma}\bigr) - \tfrac n2\mathcal G = 2\mathcal G - \tfrac n2\mathcal G$, using $g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta} = R_{\alpha\beta}$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^def-c3-11-1|Def. §C3.11.1]], [[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-3|Theorem §C3.10.3]]). In $n = 4$ the trace vanishes for every metric; that the whole tensor vanishes is Lanczos' identity, not proved here.
>
> **Part 1, check.** The script builds random tensors with the symmetries of the Riemann tensor (sums of products $S_{\rho\mu}T_{\sigma\nu} - S_{\rho\nu}T_{\sigma\mu} + \dots$ of random symmetric matrices), checks the antisymmetries, pair symmetry and the first Bianchi identity ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-2|Theorem §C3.10.2]]), and evaluates $H_{\mu\nu}$: zero to rounding in four dimensions, of order one in five. The identity is algebraic: it holds at each point for any tensor in the space of [[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-4|Theorem §C3.10.4]].
>
> **Part 3, check.** [[#^ex-c5-6-2|Example §C5.6.2]] evaluates the integral on $S^4$ and on $S^2\times S^2$ of arbitrary radii.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** In four dimensions the curvature-squared corrections to general relativity have two free couplings, not three; the third combination only measures topology.

> [!remark] Remark: Higher-curvature terms
> Couzens' reason for stopping at $R - 2\Lambda$ is the derivative count: $\sqrt{-g}$ and $\sqrt{-g}R$ are the only scalar densities with at most two derivatives of the metric, as $\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ and $G_{\mu\nu}$ are the only divergence-free tensors with at most second derivatives (Lovelock's theorem, [[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^thm-c5-1-3|Theorem §C5.1.3]]). The two statements are the action and the field-equation side of the same restriction. Every four-derivative term either raises the order of the equations ([[#^thm-c5-6-6|Theorem §C5.6.6]]) or, in four dimensions, drops out of them ([[#^thm-c5-6-7|Theorem §C5.6.7]]). In more than four dimensions the Gauss–Bonnet term is the first of a sequence of curvature polynomials (Lovelock's densities) that change the equations while keeping them second order; four dimensions is the case in which general relativity with $\Lambda$ is the whole list.
>
> General relativity does not forbid the higher terms; it predicts that, if present with coefficients set by any length far below laboratory scales, they are invisible wherever curvature is weak ([[#^thm-c5-6-6|Theorem §C5.6.6]], part 4), which is Couzens' remark that they "only become relevant for fast varying fields". In the view of general relativity as the low-energy limit of a deeper theory, such terms are expected as corrections, and the theory of these notes is the leading term. The two-derivative action is used from here on.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §5.1.3 (pp. 107–108)*

^rem-c5-6-1

> [!caution] Caution: Couzens' normalization of Gauss–Bonnet
> - **Prefactor.** GR1 (5.27) (PDF p. 109, printed p. 108) prints $\frac{1}{8\pi^2}\int_M d^4x\,\sqrt g\,(R^2 - 4R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu} + R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}) = \chi(M)$. The correct prefactor is $\frac{1}{32\pi^2}$: on the unit $S^4$ the integral is $64\pi^2$ and $\chi(S^4) = 2$; with $1/8\pi^2$ it would give $8$ ([[#^ex-c5-6-2|Example §C5.6.2]]).
> - **Attribution.** GR1 introduces (5.27) as "Lovelock's theorem". The formula is the Chern–Gauss–Bonnet theorem ([[#^thm-c5-6-7|Theorem §C5.6.7]], part 3). Lovelock's theorem is the uniqueness statement of [[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^thm-c5-1-3|Theorem §C5.1.3]] (with its generalization to higher dimensions); it is related, because it explains why the Gauss–Bonnet term leaves the four-dimensional field equations unchanged (part 1), but it is not the integral formula.
> - **Wording.** The sentence before (5.27), "Generic choices of the constants will not give rise to higher derivative equations of motion with a well-defined initial value problem", is meant as: generic choices give higher-derivative (fourth-order) equations, whose initial value problem needs more data than $g$ and $\partial_tg$ ([[#^thm-c5-6-6|Theorem §C5.6.6]], part 2).
> - The statement in (5.27) uses $\sqrt g$ and a compact $M$: it is about Riemannian manifolds. For a spacetime, what carries over is the local statement that the variation of the integral vanishes.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §5.1.3 (rendered PDF pages 108–109 read); checked by script*

The field equations are now complete: $G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$, with the coupling fixed by Newton, $\Lambda$ fixed by observation and negligible below cosmological scales, and no further terms at the order that matters. The next question is what they say about the simplest source, a single spherical body: *what is the exact field outside it, and how does it differ from Newton's?* (C6, planned).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C5.6.1: Bounding Λ with Planetary Orbits
> *Setup.* Schutz's Exercise 8.18: the field equations with $\Lambda$ and $8\pi$, applied to the solar system with [[#^thm-c5-6-5|Theorem §C5.6.5]]. The Sun's mass in metres is $M = GM_\odot/c^2 = 1476.7\ \mathrm m$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^ex-c1-2-1|Example §C1.2.1]]); Pluto's orbital radius $r = 5.9\times10^{12}\ \mathrm m$.
>
> *Step 1* (what $\Lambda$ does to an orbit). By Theorem §C5.6.5, part 2, the radial acceleration is $-M/r^2 + \Lambda r/3$. The ratio of the $\Lambda$ term to the Sun's pull is $\frac{\Lambda r/3}{M/r^2} = \frac{\Lambda r^3}{3M}$, and by part 3 Kepler's third law changes by the fraction $\Lambda r^3/(6M)$. The planets obey Newton's law with the Sun's mass, so this ratio must be small at the outermost well-observed orbit.
>
> *Step 2* (the bound, Schutz (a)). Requiring $|\Lambda| r^3/(3M) \ll 1$ at Pluto:
>
> $$
> |\Lambda| \ll \frac{3M}{r^3} = \frac{3\times1476.7\ \mathrm m}{(5.9\times10^{12}\ \mathrm m)^3} = \frac{4430\ \mathrm m}{2.05\times10^{38}\ \mathrm m^3} = 2.16\times10^{-35}\ \mathrm m^{-2} .
> $$
>
> As a vacuum mass density ([[#^thm-c5-6-2|Theorem §C5.6.2]]) this is $|\rho_\Lambda| \ll \Lambda c^2/(8\pi G) = 1.16\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$. If orbits are known to agree with Newton to a fraction $\eta$, the bound sharpens to $|\Lambda| \lesssim 3\eta M/r^3$.
>
> *Step 3* (cosmological relevance, Schutz (b)). A density of $10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, the mean density of matter near our Galaxy quoted by Schutz, corresponds to $\Lambda = \frac{8\pi G\rho}{c^2} = \frac{8\pi\times6.674\times10^{-11}\times10^{-27}}{(2.998\times10^{8})^2}\ \mathrm m^{-2} = 1.87\times10^{-53}\ \mathrm m^{-2}$.
>
> The solar-system bound is $1.2\times10^{18}$ times larger. A $\Lambda$ near that bound would mean a vacuum energy $10^{18}$ times the density of matter and would dominate the dynamics of the universe; a $\Lambda$ comparable with the matter density is invisible in the solar system. The observed value ([[#^law-c5-6-3|Law §C5.6.3]]), $\rho_\Lambda = 5.85\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, is of the second kind.
>
> *Step 4* (the observed $\Lambda$ in the solar system). With $\Lambda = 1.09\times10^{-52}\ \mathrm m^{-2}$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\Lambda r^3}{3M}\Big|_{\text{Pluto}} = \frac{1.09\times10^{-52}\times2.05\times10^{38}}{4430} = 5.0\times10^{-18}, \qquad \frac{\Lambda r^3}{3M}\Big|_{1\ \mathrm{AU}} = 8.2\times10^{-23} ,
> $$
>
> and $\Lambda r^2 = 3.8\times10^{-27}$ at Pluto, so the weak-field expansion of Theorem §C5.6.5 is safe. There is no need to include $\Lambda$ in solar-system dynamics.
>
> *Step 5* (where $\Lambda$ wins). The two accelerations are equal at $r_\Lambda = (3M/\Lambda)^{1/3}$. For the Sun, $r_\Lambda = \Bigl(\frac{3\times1476.7\ \mathrm m}{1.09\times10^{-52}\ \mathrm m^{-2}}\Bigr)^{1/3} = (4.06\times10^{55}\ \mathrm m^3)^{1/3} = 3.44\times10^{18}\ \mathrm m = 111\ \mathrm{pc}$, about $360$ light years: an isolated Sun could not hold a test body beyond this radius. (In the Galaxy the enclosed mass grows with radius and the comparison is with the galactic mass.)
>
> *Check.* The potential $-M/r - \Lambda r^2/6$ is $g_{tt} = -(1 - 2M/r - \Lambda r^2/3)$ of Schwarzschild–de Sitter, an exact vacuum solution with $\Lambda$ ([[#^thm-c5-6-4|Theorem §C5.6.4]], part 4).
>
> *Source: Schutz, Exercise 8.18 (pp. 199–200) · worked here; numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C5.6.2: The Gauss–Bonnet Integrand on the Unit S⁴
> *Setup.* The unit 4-sphere, $ds^2 = d\chi_1^2 + \sin^2\chi_1\bigl(d\chi_2^2 + \sin^2\chi_2(d\chi_3^2 + \sin^2\chi_3\,d\varphi^2)\bigr)$, a space of constant curvature $K = 1$; then $S^2\times S^2$ with radii $a$ and $b$. Test of [[#^thm-c5-6-7|Theorem §C5.6.7]], part 3, and of the prefactor in [[#^cau-c5-6-1|Caution: Couzens' normalization of Gauss–Bonnet]].
>
> *Step 1* (curvature of $S^4$). Constant curvature: $R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = g_{\rho\mu}g_{\sigma\nu} - g_{\rho\nu}g_{\sigma\mu}$, so with $n = 4$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^ex-c3-11-2|Example §C3.11.2]], Step 2) $R_{\mu\nu} = (n - 1)g_{\mu\nu} = 3g_{\mu\nu}$ and $R = n(n - 1) = 12$.
>
> *Step 2* (the three squares). $R^2 = 144$, $R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu} = 9\,g_{\mu\nu}g^{\mu\nu} = 9\times4 = 36$, and
>
> $$
> R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}R^{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = (g_{\rho\mu}g_{\sigma\nu} - g_{\rho\nu}g_{\sigma\mu})(g^{\rho\mu}g^{\sigma\nu} - g^{\rho\nu}g^{\sigma\mu}) = n^2 - n - n + n^2 = 2n(n - 1) = 24 ,
> $$
>
> using $g_{\rho\mu}g^{\rho\mu} = n$ and $g_{\rho\mu}g_{\sigma\nu}g^{\rho\nu}g^{\sigma\mu} = \delta_\mu{}^\nu\delta_\nu{}^\mu = n$. Hence $\mathcal G = 144 - 4\times36 + 24 = 24$.
>
> *Step 3* (volume). $\sqrt g = \sin^3\chi_1\sin^2\chi_2\sin\chi_3$, so with $\int_0^\pi\sin^3 = \tfrac43$, $\int_0^\pi\sin^2 = \tfrac\pi2$, $\int_0^\pi\sin = 2$, $\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi = 2\pi$: $\mathrm{vol}(S^4) = \tfrac43\times\tfrac\pi2\times2\times2\pi = \frac{8\pi^2}{3}$.
>
> *Step 4* (the integral). $\mathcal G$ is constant, so $\int\sqrt g\,\mathcal G\,d^4x = 24\times\frac{8\pi^2}{3} = 64\pi^2$, and $\frac{1}{32\pi^2}\times64\pi^2 = 2 = \chi(S^4)$, while $\frac{1}{8\pi^2}\times64\pi^2 = 8$. Here $\chi(S^4) = 2$: $S^4$ is built from one point and one 4-cell, $1 + 1 = 2$ (as $\chi(S^2) = 2$ for surfaces, [[§25 Surfaces and the Euler Characteristic#^ex-25-1|250 Ex. §25.1]]).
>
> *Step 5* ($S^2\times S^2$: radius-independence). For $g = a^2(d\theta_1^2 + \sin^2\theta_1d\varphi_1^2) + b^2(d\theta_2^2 + \sin^2\theta_2d\varphi_2^2)$ the Riemann tensor is that of each factor ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^ex-c3-11-1|Example §C3.11.1]]: $R_{\theta\varphi\theta\varphi}$ with $K = 1/a^2$ and $1/b^2$), with no mixed components. Then $R = 2/a^2 + 2/b^2$, $R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu} = 2/a^4 + 2/b^4$ and $R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = 4/a^4 + 4/b^4$ (each factor's Ricci is $g/a^2$ on a 2-dimensional block, and its Riemann square is $2n(n-1)K^2 = 4/a^4$), so
>
> $$
> \mathcal G = \frac{4}{a^4} + \frac{8}{a^2b^2} + \frac{4}{b^4} - \frac{8}{a^4} - \frac{8}{b^4} + \frac{4}{a^4} + \frac{4}{b^4} = \frac{8}{a^2b^2} .
> $$
>
> The volume is $4\pi a^2\cdot4\pi b^2 = 16\pi^2a^2b^2$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{32\pi^2}\int\sqrt g\,\mathcal G = \frac{1}{32\pi^2}\times\frac{8}{a^2b^2}\times16\pi^2a^2b^2 = 4 = \chi(S^2)\,\chi(S^2) .
> $$
>
> The radii cancel: the integral sees only the topology. A flat torus $T^4$ has $\mathcal G = 0$ and $\chi = 0$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, eq. (5.27) (the statement tested) · worked here; curvature, volume and integrals checked by script*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - **Uniqueness and action.** The freedom $aG_{\mu\nu} + bg_{\mu\nu}$ of [[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^thm-c5-1-3|Theorem §C5.1.3]] is the freedom $R - 2\Lambda$ in the action ([[#^thm-c5-6-1|Theorem §C5.6.1]]); Lovelock's theorem and the derivative count of [[#^rem-c5-6-1|Remark: Higher-curvature terms]] are its two faces.
> - **Matter side.** The cosmological term is the vacuum-energy fluid of [[§C4.3 Perfect Fluids#^ex-c4-3-2|Example §C4.3.2]] and the standard violator of the strong energy condition ([[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^ex-c4-5-2|Example §C4.5.2]]), which makes a ball of test bodies expand ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]]). A slowly rolling scalar field behaves like a temporary $\Lambda$ when its potential dominates ([[§C5.3 Coupling to Matter꞉ the Hilbert Energy–Momentum Tensor#^ex-c5-3-2|Example §C5.3.2]]).
> - **Geometry.** Einstein manifolds include all spaces of constant curvature ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^ex-c3-11-2|Example §C3.11.2]]); in two dimensions the Einstein–Hilbert action is topological ([[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action#^ex-c5-2-1|Example §C5.2.1]]), as the Gauss–Bonnet action is in four; the two-dimensional Gauss–Bonnet theorem was met on the torus ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^ex-c3-11-3|Example §C3.11.3]]).
