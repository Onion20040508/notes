Chapters C10–C12 met the area of a black hole three times, and each time it went up. A thin shell of light falling onto a hole raised the horizon radius from $2M_0$ to $2M_1$ ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]], part 4). A particle that dropped negative energy into a Kerr hole lowered its mass and still raised its area: in [[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^ex-c12-5-1|Example §C12.5.1]] the hole lost $0.126\,m_0$ of mass and gained $8.48\,m_0M$ of area, and [[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^thm-c12-5-4|Theorem §C12.5.4]] showed this for every such decay. The question of this section is whether the increase is a law: does the area of a black hole never decrease, whatever happens to it, as the entropy of an isolated system never decreases ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|TH Principle §A4.1.2]], [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|TH Theorem §B2.2.3]])?

The two computations do not answer it. Each used a special structure: spherical symmetry for the shell, a test particle on a fixed Kerr background for the Penrose process. Neither covers two holes that collide, where nothing is small and nothing is symmetric. Mass, the obvious candidate for a monotone quantity, already fails: the Penrose process lowers it. And the area is not monotone for kinematic reasons either. Replace the shell of light by a shell of *negative* energy, $M_0 \to 0.7M_0$: the same Vaidya computation gives an event horizon that shrinks from $r = 2M_0$ to $r = 1.4M_0$, and its area falls to $0.49$ of its value ([[#^ex-c13-3-3|Example §C13.3.3]]). Whatever makes the area grow must therefore be a condition on the energy of matter, and it must act on the horizon as a whole, not through a formula for a stationary hole.

The tool is already built. The horizon is a null hypersurface ruled by light rays ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^thm-c10-7-2|Theorem §C10.7.2]]); the area of a bundle of rays changes at the rate $\theta$ ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^thm-c10-6-1|Theorem §C10.6.1]], part 5); and under the null energy condition a converging bundle must reach a caustic ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^thm-c10-6-4|Theorem §C10.6.4]]). Hawking's theorem joins these with one global fact: horizon rays never run into a caustic. Hence they never converge, and the area never shrinks ([[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]]). The section then shows how the area grows: new rays join, none leave, and the growth anticipates the matter that causes it ([[#^thm-c13-3-2|Theorem §C13.3.2]]). Two consequences for holes that meet follow: a hole cannot split ([[#^thm-c13-3-3|Theorem §C13.3.3]]), and a merger can radiate at most a computable fraction of its mass ([[#^thm-c13-3-4|Theorem §C13.3.4]]). It ends with what gravitational-wave data say ([[#^law-c13-3-5|Law §C13.3.5]]) and with the one process that breaks the theorem, Hawking radiation ([[#^rem-c13-3-1|Remark: Hawking radiation violates the area theorem]]).

**Setup.** A spacetime $(M, g)$ asymptotically flat at null infinity ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]]) containing a black hole, with black-hole region $\mathcal B$ and future event horizon $\mathcal H^+$ ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^def-c10-7-2|Defs. §C10.7.2]], [[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^def-c10-7-3|§C10.7.3]]). It obeys Einstein's equations with matter satisfying the null energy condition and a precise form of cosmic censorship, both stated in [[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]]. Areas are those of cross-sections of $\mathcal H^+$ by Cauchy surfaces ([[§C11.4 Cauchy Surfaces and Cauchy Horizons#^def-c11-4-5|Def. §C11.4.5]]). The derivations use the horizon where it is a smooth hypersurface. Four facts about boundaries of causal pasts ("achronal boundaries") are quoted from the research literature, not proved; they are listed in [[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]] and marked (Q1)–(Q4) wherever they are used. Mergers are treated as transitions between holes that are far apart and close to Kerr before, and Kerr after; the energy radiated is the difference of masses measured at infinity. The theorems are exact under these hypotheses; the computed spacetimes (Vaidya shells) are exact solutions; all numbers come from `tools/verify_c13_3.py` (31 checks).

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here an area $A \to A$ in $\mathrm m^2$ with $M \to GM/c^2$, so $A_{\text{Schw}} = 16\pi G^2M^2/c^4$, and an energy $E \to Ec^2$); $\lambda$ is an affine parameter along a generator; $\theta$ is the expansion of [[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^def-c10-6-2|Def. §C10.6.2]]; Kerr horizon area $A(M, J) = 8\pi\bigl(M^2 + \sqrt{M^4 - J^2}\bigr) = 8\pi M^2\bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 - \chi^2}\bigr)$ with $\chi = a/M = J/M^2$ ([[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-3|Theorem §C12.2.3]], part 4; $J = aM$ by [[§C12.4 Komar Integrals#^thm-c12-4-3|Theorem §C12.4.3]]); Vaidya metric $ds^2 = -(1 - 2m(v)/r)\,dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2$ as in §C10.7, $t^{\ast} = v - r$.

## Hawking's theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.3.1: Hawking's Area Theorem: under the NEC and Cosmic Censorship, the Area of the Event Horizon Never Decreases
> *Hypotheses.*
> - **(H1) Energy.** Einstein's equations hold with any cosmological constant ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^pr-c5-1-4|Principle §C5.1.4]]), and matter obeys the null energy condition $T_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu \ge 0$ for null $k$ ([[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^def-c4-5-1|Def. §C4.5.1]]). Only the consequence $R_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu \ge 0$ is used.
> - **(H2) Cosmic censorship, in the form of strong asymptotic predictability.** The exterior $J^-(\mathscr I^+)$ together with $\mathcal H^+$ lies in a globally hyperbolic region ([[§C11.4 Cauchy Surfaces and Cauchy Horizons#^def-c11-4-6|Def. §C11.4.6]]) of the unphysical spacetime, so that the exterior and the horizon are foliated by Cauchy surfaces $\Sigma_\tau$, $\tau$ increasing to the future, and $\mathscr I^+$ is complete ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-3|Def. §C10.4.3]]). In words: no singularity is visible from $\mathscr I^+$ or from the horizon ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^rem-c10-7-1|§C10.7, Remark: Weak cosmic censorship]]).
>
> *Cross-sections.* $S_\tau = \mathcal H^+ \cap \Sigma_\tau$ is a spacelike 2-surface (a sphere, or several spheres if there are several holes), with area $A(\tau)$.
>
> *Conclusions.*
> 1. **Generators never converge.** At every point of $\mathcal H^+$ where it is smooth, the expansion of its generators ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^thm-c10-7-2|Theorem §C10.7.2]]), parametrized to the future, satisfies $\theta \ge 0$.
> 2. **Area law along the generators.** If a cross-section $S$ is carried along the generators by the affine parameter $\lambda$, its area obeys
>
> $$
> \frac{dA}{d\lambda} = \int_{S_\lambda}\theta\,dA \ \ge 0 ,
> $$
>
>    the integral of the law $d\,\delta A/d\lambda = \theta\,\delta A$ for each area element ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^thm-c10-6-1|Theorem §C10.6.1]], part 5). Couzens' $dA_H/d\lambda = \theta A_H$, eq. (7.52), is the case of an expansion that is the same at every point of the cross-section.
> 3. **The area theorem.** If $\tau_2 > \tau_1$, then $A(\tau_2) \ge A(\tau_1)$ (restored: the same inequality for areas in $\mathrm m^2$).
>
> *Quoted, not proved here (achronal-boundary facts).*
> - **(Q1)** $\mathcal H^+$ is achronal ([[§C11.4 Cauchy Surfaces and Cauchy Horizons#^def-c11-4-1|Def. §C11.4.1]]): no two of its points are joined by a timelike curve ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^thm-c10-7-2|Theorem §C10.7.2]], part 3).
> - **(Q2)** Every generator of $\mathcal H^+$, followed to the future, stays in $\mathcal H^+$: it has no future endpoint (Theorem §C10.7.2, part 3).
> - **(Q3)** Two ways to make a causal curve timelike. A causal curve from $x$ to $y$ that is not one unbroken null geodesic (it has a corner, or contains a timelike piece) can be deformed into a timelike curve from $x$ to $y$. A null geodesic leaving a spacelike 2-surface $S$ orthogonally contains, beyond a point where the expansion of the light front from $S$ reaches $-\infty$ (a point *focal* to $S$), only points that some timelike curve from $S$ reaches.
> - **(Q4)** Under (H2), the argument of part 1 does not need the generator through a converging point to be future-complete. Hawking pushes the cross-section slightly outward, into $J^-(\mathscr I^+)$. There the light front still converges, and the rays of it that reach the complete $\mathscr I^+$ would have to focus.
>
> (Q1)–(Q3) are proved in Hawking and Ellis (1973), §4.5 and §6.3, and Wald, *General Relativity* (1984), §8.1 and §9.3; (Q4) and the theorem are Hawking, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 26 (1971) 1344; Hawking and Ellis, §9.2; Wald, §12.2 (research literature, not in the typed sources). The extension to horizons that are not smooth anywhere is due to Chruściel, Delay, Galloway and Howard, *Ann. Henri Poincaré* 2 (2001) 109, arXiv:gr-qc/0001003. The two papers were checked 2026-11-21; the section numbers in Hawking and Ellis and in Wald are not checked against the books.
>
> *Model:* general relativity with matter obeying the NEC, asymptotically flat, strongly asymptotically predictable; *applies:* to every classical process, however violent and asymmetric, including mergers. It fails when the NEC fails ([[#^ex-c13-3-3|Example §C13.3.3]]), as it does for quantum fields ([[#^rem-c13-3-1|Remark: Hawking radiation violates the area theorem]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §7.4, eq. (7.52), Fig. 31, p. 145 (PDF 146) (proof sketch; hypotheses as printed are garbled, [[#^cau-c13-3-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens §7.4]]) · Schutz §11.3, item (5), p. 326 (PDF 344) (statement; "a full proof is outlined in Misner et al. 1973, Exercise 34.4") · Hartle §12.4, p. 276 (PDF 277) ("area increase theorem", stated) · area law checked by script on the Vaidya horizon*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the generators are twist-free and orthogonal to every cross-section). Near a smooth point the horizon is $\{f = 0\}$, and its generators are the integral curves of the null normal $n^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu f$ ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^thm-c10-7-2|Theorem §C10.7.2]], part 2; [[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^thm-c10-5-3|Theorem §C10.5.3]]). Rescale $n$ along each generator to the tangent $k = d/d\lambda$ of an affine parameter; $k$ is still normal to the horizon. To have a congruence on an open set, as §C10.6 requires, take a cross-section $S$ and a one-parameter family of nearby spacelike 2-surfaces $S_s$, $S_0 = S$, pushed slightly inward; the outgoing null geodesics orthogonal to $S_s$ sweep out null hypersurfaces $\{F = s\}$ that fill a neighbourhood of $S$, with $\{F = 0\}$ the horizon near $S$. Their tangents form a null geodesic congruence with $k_\mu = A\,\partial_\mu F$, $A \neq 0$, which on the horizon is the generator field. It is hypersurface-orthogonal, so its twist vanishes, $\omega_{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^thm-c10-6-3|Theorem §C10.6.3]], part 1). A cross-section $S$ lies in $\mathcal H^+$, so every vector $V$ tangent to $S$ is tangent to $\mathcal H^+$ and $g(k, V) = A\,V^\mu\partial_\mu F = 0$: the generators meet $S$ orthogonally. With the second null normal $l$ of $S$, $g(k, l) = -1$, $T_pS$ is the transverse space of $k$ and $\theta$ is the outgoing null expansion $\theta_k$ of $S$ ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^def-c10-6-5|Def. §C10.6.5]]).
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). Fix a cross-section $S$ and choose the affine parameter on each generator with $\lambda = 0$ on $S$. Moving each point of $S$ the parameter distance $\lambda$ along its generator gives a surface $S_\lambda$. Two independent deviation vectors tangent to $S$, transported along the generators, span the area element $\delta A(\lambda)$ of $S_\lambda$, and by [[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^thm-c10-6-1|Theorem §C10.6.1]], part 5,
>
> $$
> \frac{d\,\delta A}{d\lambda} = \theta\,\delta A \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \delta A(\lambda) = \delta A(0)\exp\int_0^\lambda\theta\,d\lambda' .
> $$
>
> Adding the elements, $A(\lambda) = \int_S\exp\bigl(\int_0^\lambda\theta\,d\lambda'\bigr)\,dA_0$, and differentiating under the integral,
>
> $$
> \frac{dA}{d\lambda} = \int_S\theta(\lambda)\exp\Bigl(\int_0^\lambda\theta\,d\lambda'\Bigr)dA_0 = \int_{S_\lambda}\theta\,dA .
> $$
>
> If $\theta$ takes one value on $S_\lambda$, this is $\theta A$. A different affine parameter on a generator, $\lambda \to c\lambda$ with a constant $c > 0$, changes $\theta$ to $\theta/c$ and leaves its sign alone; part 1, once proved, makes the right side $\ge 0$.
>
> **Step 3** (focusing). By Step 1 and (H1), [[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^thm-c10-6-4|Theorem §C10.6.4]] applies along each generator. Its Step 1 gives $R_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu \ge 0$ (the trace term and $\Lambda$ drop out because $g(k, k) = 0$), and Raychaudhuri's equation ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^thm-c10-6-2|Theorem §C10.6.2]]) with $\omega = 0$ becomes
>
> $$
> \frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = -\tfrac12\theta^2 - \sigma_{\mu\nu}\sigma^{\mu\nu} - 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu \le -\tfrac12\theta^2 .
> $$
>
> So if $\theta = \theta_0 < 0$ at a point $p$, then $1/\theta \ge 1/\theta_0 + \lambda/2$ and $\theta \to -\infty$ at some $\lambda_\ast \le 2/|\theta_0|$, provided the generator extends that far (Theorem §C10.6.4, part 3).
>
> **Step 4** (part 1: a converging generator contradicts achronality). Suppose $\theta(p) = \theta_0 < 0$ at a point $p$ of a cross-section $S$, and let $\gamma$ be the generator through $p$. By (Q2) $\gamma$ stays in $\mathcal H^+$ to the future. Granting for the moment that it extends to $\lambda = 2/|\theta_0|$, Step 3 gives a point $q = \gamma(\lambda_\ast)$ where the expansion of the light front from $S$ is $-\infty$: $q$ is focal to $S$ (Step 1: the generators are that light front). Take a point $y = \gamma(\lambda_\ast + \varepsilon)$ beyond $q$. By (Q3), some timelike curve runs from a point $z \in S$ to $y$. Both $z$ and $y$ lie in $\mathcal H^+$, against (Q1). Hence $\theta \ge 0$ at $p$; $p$ was arbitrary. The assumption on the length of $\gamma$ is what (Q4) removes, using (H2). Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 5** (part 3: comparing two cross-sections). Let $\tau_2 > \tau_1$ and $S_i = \mathcal H^+ \cap \Sigma_{\tau_i}$. Follow each generator from $S_1$ to the future. By (Q2) it stays in $\mathcal H^+$ and is future-inextendible there; as a causal curve it meets the Cauchy surface $\Sigma_{\tau_2}$ ([[§C11.4 Cauchy Surfaces and Cauchy Horizons#^def-c11-4-5|Def. §C11.4.5]], equivalent form), at one point. This defines a map $\phi : S_1 \to S_2$. It is injective: if two generators met at a point $x$, then a point $y$ after $x$ on one of them would be reached from a point $z$ before $x$ on the other by the broken null curve $z \to x \to y$, which (Q3) deforms into a timelike curve, against (Q1). By Step 2 and part 1, along each generator the area element does not decrease, $\delta A(\lambda) = \delta A(0)\exp\int\theta \ge \delta A(0)$, so (with the time $\tau$ along each generator replacing $\lambda$, a reparametrization that keeps the sign of $\theta$)
>
> $$
> A\bigl(\phi(S_1)\bigr) = \int_{S_1}\exp\Bigl(\int\theta\Bigr)dA_0 \ \ge\ A(S_1) .
> $$
>
> Finally $\phi(S_1) \subset S_2$, so $A(S_2) \ge A(\phi(S_1)) \ge A(S_1)$. The part of $S_2$ not in $\phi(S_1)$ consists of generators that joined the horizon between $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$ ([[#^thm-c13-3-2|Theorem §C13.3.2]]). Part 3 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The only physics in the proof is (H1), and it enters once, through focusing (Step 3). Everything else is causal structure: an event horizon is a wavefront that can never pass a caustic, because past a caustic its rays would be timelike-separated from its earlier points.
> - Einstein's equations are used only to turn the NEC on $T_{\mu\nu}$ into $R_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu \ge 0$. Any metric theory with that inequality for null $k$ has the theorem.
> - The area of every later cross-section bounds the earlier one from above, not only the image of the earlier one: new generators can only add area.

## How the horizon grows

The proof compares a cross-section with its image along the generators, and leaves room for more: generators that were not on the horizon before. Where they come from, and when the area grows, the null-shell spacetimes of §C10.7 show exactly.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.3.2: New Generators Can Enter, None Can Leave; the Horizon Grows before the Matter Arrives
> Under the hypotheses of [[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]]:
> 1. **None leave.** A generator of $\mathcal H^+$, followed to the future, stays on $\mathcal H^+$ for ever (Q2). An area element, once on the horizon, is never lost and never shrinks.
> 2. **New ones enter.** Followed to the past, a generator may leave $\mathcal H^+$: it has a past endpoint. There the horizon is not smooth. Generators join it along such points, and the area of a cross-section grows in two ways: through $\theta > 0$ on the generators already present, and through generators that join. In the shell into flat space all generators start at one event, the vertex $r = 0$, $t = v_0 - 4M$ ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^thm-c10-7-2|Theorem §C10.7.2]], part 4). When two holes merge they join along the seam where the two horizons meet, which numerical simulations show as two distorted horizons fusing into one (Schutz, Fig. 11.14, reproduced there from Lovelace et al. 2010).
> 3. **⚑ The expansion now is set by the future.** Along a generator, with affine parameter $\lambda$, suppose the hole settles down, so that $\theta \to 0$ as $\lambda \to \infty$, as on a stationary horizon ([[§C13.1 Surface Gravity and the Zeroth Law#^thm-c13-1-4|Theorem §C13.1.4]]). Then
>
> $$
> \theta(\lambda) = \int_\lambda^\infty\Bigl[\tfrac12\theta^2 + \sigma_{\mu\nu}\sigma^{\mu\nu} + 8\pi\,T_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu\Bigr]d\lambda' \ \ge\ 0 .
> $$
>
>    The horizon is expanding now if and only if energy crosses it, or it is sheared, at a later parameter. This is the teleology of [[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]] in a formula.
> 4. **Computed: a shell onto a hole** ($M_0 \to M_1 = 1.5M_0$ at $v = v_0$). The horizon radius $r_H(v)$ increases for all $v < v_0$, from $2M_0$ in the far past to $2M_1$ at $v_0$, and is constant after: $r_H = 2.011$, $2.202$, $2.478\,M_0$ at $v_0 - 20$, $-8$, $-4\,M_0$. At $v_0 - 4M_0$ the area has already made $42.8\%$ of its total increase $16\pi(M_1^2 - M_0^2)$. Just before the shell the affine expansion is $\theta = F(M_1 - M_0)/2M_1^2$ ($F$ the normalization of the affine tangent at that point), exactly the drop $8\pi\int T_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu\,d\lambda$ caused by the shell, after which $\theta = 0$. The boundary of the trapped spheres, $r = 2m(v)$, jumps from $2M_0$ to $2M_1$ at $v_0$; the event horizon moves continuously.
> 5. **Computed: a shell into flat space** (mass $M$). The cross-sections $v = \text{const}$ of $\mathcal H^+$ have area $A(v) = \pi(v - v_0 + 4M)^2$ for $v_0 - 4M \le v \le v_0$, growing from $0$ to $16\pi M^2$, then constant; on the generators $\theta = F/r > 0$ before the shell and $\theta = 0$ after.
>
> *Model:* parts 1–3 as [[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]], part 3 assuming late-time stationarity; parts 4–5 the Vaidya metric with a step in $m(v)$; *applies:* exactly within each.
>
> *Source: Schutz §11.3, item (5), p. 326 (PDF 344) ("the area is actually increasing during the period before the infalling gas reaches the hole"); Fig. 11.14 (PDF 344) · Hartle §12.2, p. 268 (PDF 269) (the shell onto a hole: "inside the shell, but outside the star, the horizon … is expanding through larger radii, its area always increasing") · Couzens, GR2, §7.4 (p. 145) · numbers by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation (parts 3–5)
> **Step 1** (part 3). Along a generator, $\omega = 0$ and $R_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu$ ([[#^der-c13-3-1|derivation of Theorem §C13.3.1]], Steps 1 and 3), so Raychaudhuri's equation reads $d\theta/d\lambda = -\bigl[\tfrac12\theta^2 + \sigma^2 + 8\pi T_{kk}\bigr]$, with $\sigma^2 = \sigma_{\mu\nu}\sigma^{\mu\nu}$ and $T_{kk} = T_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu$. Integrate from $\lambda$ to $\Lambda$:
>
> $$
> \theta(\Lambda) - \theta(\lambda) = -\int_\lambda^\Lambda\Bigl[\tfrac12\theta^2 + \sigma^2 + 8\pi T_{kk}\Bigr]d\lambda' .
> $$
>
> Let $\Lambda \to \infty$ with $\theta(\Lambda) \to 0$; the integrand is $\ge 0$ by (H1), so the integral has a limit, and $\theta(\lambda)$ equals it. Each term is non-negative, so $\theta(\lambda) = 0$ forces $\theta = \sigma = T_{kk} = 0$ on the rest of the generator.
>
> **Step 2** (the horizon before the shell; part 4). For $v < v_0$ the metric is Schwarzschild of mass $M_0$. Outgoing radial light obeys $dr/dv = \tfrac12(1 - 2M_0/r)$ and the ray that arrives at $r = 2M_1$ at $v_0$ is $v - 2r_\ast(r) = v_0 - 2r_\ast(2M_1)$, $r_\ast = r + 2M_0\ln|r/2M_0 - 1|$ ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]], part 4 and Step 8 of its derivation; [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]). Since $2M_1 > 2M_0$ and $dr/dv > 0$ for $r > 2M_0$, $r_H(v)$ increases on the whole ray. Solving the ray equation for $r$ at $v = v_0 - 4M_0, v_0 - 8M_0, v_0 - 20M_0$ ($M_0 = 1$, $M_1 = 1.5$) gives $2.478$, $2.202$, $2.011$. The area fraction at $v_0 - 4M_0$ is
>
> $$
> \frac{r_H^2 - 4M_0^2}{4M_1^2 - 4M_0^2} = \frac{2.478^2 - 4}{9 - 4} = 0.428 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the expansion on the generators). The vector $\ell = \partial_v + \tfrac12(1 - 2m/r)\partial_r$ is null and tangent to outgoing radial geodesics with $\ell^\nu\nabla_\nu\ell^\mu = (m/r^2)\,\ell^\mu$ (script; [[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^der-c10-7-3|derivation of Theorem §C10.7.3]], Step 7). The affine tangent is $k = F\ell$ with $\ell(F) = -(m/r^2)F$, $F > 0$; then $k^\nu\nabla_\nu k^\mu = F\bigl[\ell(F) + (m/r^2)F\bigr]\ell^\mu = 0$, and by [[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^thm-c10-6-1|Theorem §C10.6.1]], part 4,
>
> $$
> \theta = \nabla_\mu k^\mu = F\,\nabla_\mu\ell^\mu + \ell(F) = F\Bigl(\nabla_\mu\ell^\mu - \frac{m}{r^2}\Bigr) = \frac{F}{r}\Bigl(1 - \frac{2m(v)}{r}\Bigr)
> $$
>
> (the divergence computed by script). The same number follows from the area of the round spheres: $\delta A \propto r^2$, $k(\ln r^2) = F\,\ell^r\cdot 2/r = (F/r)(1 - 2m/r)$, which is the area law $d\,\delta A/d\lambda = \theta\,\delta A$ checked on this horizon. Before the shell, on $r_H > 2M_0$, $\theta > 0$; after it, on $r = 2M_1$, $\theta = 0$.
>
> **Step 4** (the jump at the shell). The shell has $T_{vv} = m'(v)/(4\pi r^2)$ with $m' = (M_1 - M_0)\,\delta(v - v_0)$ (Theorem §C10.7.3, part 2). With $k^v = F$, $T_{kk} = F^2T_{vv}$, and along the ray $d\lambda = dv/F$, so
>
> $$
> 8\pi\int T_{kk}\,d\lambda = 8\pi\int F^2\frac{m'}{4\pi r^2}\frac{dv}{F} = \frac{2F(M_1 - M_0)}{r^2}\bigg|_{r = 2M_1} = \frac{F(M_1 - M_0)}{2M_1^2} .
> $$
>
> The terms $\tfrac12\theta^2$ and $\sigma^2$ ($= 0$ by spherical symmetry) are bounded and contribute nothing across the zero width of the shell. Just before, Step 3 gives $\theta = (F/2M_1)(1 - M_0/M_1) = F(M_1 - M_0)/2M_1^2$: the same number, as part 3 requires.
>
> **Step 5** (part 5). For $v < v_0$ the shell interior is flat, $\mathcal H^+$ is $r = \tfrac12(v - v_0 + 4M)$ (Theorem §C10.7.3, part 2), so $A = 4\pi r^2 = \pi(v - v_0 + 4M)^2$, which increases from $0$ at $v = v_0 - 4M$ to $16\pi M^2$ at $v_0$; after the shell $r = 2M$. Step 3 with $m = 0$ gives $\theta = F/r > 0$, and with $m = M$, $r = 2M$ gives $\theta = 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The horizon spends its whole growth in advance: the expansion it has at any moment is exactly what the matter still to come will cancel. A shell that will arrive later already makes the horizon grow now, and the growth stops at the moment the shell crosses.

## Black holes that meet

Two holes far apart have a horizon made of two spheres, whose areas add. The area theorem compares this sum with the area of whatever the holes become, and the Kerr area formula turns the comparison into statements about masses and spins.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.3.3: Black Holes Cannot Bifurcate
> Under the hypotheses of [[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]], with initial and final holes far apart and close to Kerr:
> 1. **A Schwarzschild hole cannot split.** A Schwarzschild hole of mass $M$ cannot become two holes of masses $m_1, m_2$ with any spins, whatever radiation is emitted, as long as energy is conserved, $m_1 + m_2 \le M$:
>
> $$
> A(m_1, J_1) + A(m_2, J_2) \le 16\pi(m_1^2 + m_2^2) < 16\pi(m_1 + m_2)^2 \le 16\pi M^2 .
> $$
>
> 2. **A Kerr hole cannot split conserving $M$ and $J$** (Schutz, Exercise 11.29). For any Kerr holes $(m_1, J_1)$, $(m_2, J_2)$, $|J_i| \le m_i^2$,
>
> $$
> A(m_1, J_1) + A(m_2, J_2) < A(m_1 + m_2, J_1 + J_2) :
> $$
>
>    the two have less area than one hole with their total mass and angular momentum. Read backwards in time, two holes can always merge into one.
> 3. **What the area alone does not exclude.** If radiation carries off angular momentum, the area argument can fail for a rapidly spinning parent. An extremal Kerr hole, $J = M^2$, has $A = 8\pi M^2$, while two Schwarzschild holes of $0.6M$ and $0.4M$ have $16\pi(0.36 + 0.16)M^2 = 8.32\pi M^2$. Splitting is excluded in general by a separate causal argument (Hawking and Ellis 1973, §9.2; research literature, not in the typed sources).
>
> *Model:* as [[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]]; the holes are Kerr before and after (area formula of [[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-3|Theorem §C12.2.3]]); *applies:* exactly under these idealizations.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §7.4, Example 7.2, p. 145 (PDF 146) (Schwarzschild into two holes with $M = m_1 + m_2$; with a slip in the area, [[#^cau-c13-3-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens §7.4]]) · Hartle, Problem 12.27, p. 280 (PDF 281) · Schutz §11.3, item (5), p. 326 (PDF 344); Exercise 11.29, p. 358 (PDF 376) · checked by script ($2\times10^5$ random Kerr pairs; the steps of the proof)*

^thm-c13-3-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Kerr area bounded by Schwarzschild). $A(m, J) = 8\pi\bigl(m^2 + \sqrt{m^4 - J^2}\bigr) \le 8\pi(m^2 + m^2) = 16\pi m^2$, with equality only at $J = 0$.
>
> **Step 2** (part 1). By Step 1 for each product, then $2m_1m_2 > 0$, then $m_1 + m_2 \le M$:
>
> $$
> A_1 + A_2 \le 16\pi(m_1^2 + m_2^2) < 16\pi(m_1^2 + m_2^2 + 2m_1m_2) = 16\pi(m_1 + m_2)^2 \le 16\pi M^2 = A_{\text{parent}} .
> $$
>
> The final area would be smaller than the initial one, against [[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]], part 3.
>
> **Step 3** (a reverse triangle inequality). For vectors $u = (u_0, u_1)$, $w = (w_0, w_1)$ with $u_0 \ge |u_1|$, $w_0 \ge |w_1|$ (future-directed causal vectors of two-dimensional Minkowski space), write $|u| = \sqrt{u_0^2 - u_1^2}$. Then $|u| + |w| \le |u + w|$. Both sides are $\ge 0$; square them:
>
> $$
> (|u| + |w|)^2 = |u|^2 + |w|^2 + 2|u||w|, \qquad |u + w|^2 = |u|^2 + |w|^2 + 2(u_0w_0 - u_1w_1) .
> $$
>
> So the claim is $|u||w| \le u_0w_0 - u_1w_1$. The right side is $\ge u_0w_0 - |u_1||w_1| \ge 0$, so it suffices to compare squares:
>
> $$
> (u_0w_0 - u_1w_1)^2 - (u_0^2 - u_1^2)(w_0^2 - w_1^2) = u_0^2w_1^2 - 2u_0w_0u_1w_1 + u_1^2w_0^2 = (u_0w_1 - u_1w_0)^2 \ge 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (part 2). Apply Step 3 to $u = (m_1^2, J_1)$, $w = (m_2^2, J_2)$, allowed since $|J_i| \le m_i^2$. With $J = J_1 + J_2$:
>
> $$
> \sqrt{m_1^4 - J_1^2} + \sqrt{m_2^4 - J_2^2} \le \sqrt{(m_1^2 + m_2^2)^2 - J^2} \le \sqrt{(m_1 + m_2)^4 - J^2} ,
> $$
>
> the second step because $(m_1 + m_2)^2 > m_1^2 + m_2^2$ (and $|J| \le m_1^2 + m_2^2$, so both roots are real). Hence
>
> $$
> \frac{A(m_1 + m_2, J)}{8\pi} = (m_1 + m_2)^2 + \sqrt{(m_1 + m_2)^4 - J^2} \ \ge\ m_1^2 + m_2^2 + 2m_1m_2 + \sqrt{m_1^4 - J_1^2} + \sqrt{m_2^4 - J_2^2} \ >\ \frac{A(m_1, J_1) + A(m_2, J_2)}{8\pi} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (part 3). Extremal Kerr: $\sqrt{M^4 - J^2} = 0$, $A = 8\pi M^2$. The products: $16\pi(0.6^2 + 0.4^2)M^2 = 16\pi\cdot0.52M^2 = 8.32\pi M^2 > 8\pi M^2$. Energy is conserved ($0.6M + 0.4M = M$) only if the radiation carries all of $J = M^2$ and no energy; the area theorem alone does not say whether such radiation exists.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The area of a Kerr hole, divided by $8\pi$, is $m^2$ plus the Minkowski length of the vector $(m^2, J)$. Joining two holes adds these vectors, and Minkowski lengths of causal vectors are superadditive; the cross term $2m_1m_2$ makes the inequality strict.

*Uses:* [[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]], [[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-3|Theorem §C12.2.3]], [[§C12.4 Komar Integrals#^thm-c12-4-3|Theorem §C12.4.3]]

Read forward, the same inequality bounds how much of the mass a merger can turn into gravitational waves: the final hole must have at least the initial area, and so at least a minimum mass.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.3.4: Bound on the Energy Radiated in a Merger
> Two holes far apart, Kerr with masses $m_1 \ge m_2$ and spins $\chi_1, \chi_2$ ($\chi = a/m$), merge into one Kerr hole of mass $M_f$ and spin $\chi_f$, radiating the energy $E = m_1 + m_2 - M_f$ (masses measured at infinity; the binding energy of the initial pair is neglected, as in Couzens' (7.53)). Under the hypotheses of [[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]]:
> 1. **General bound.**
>
> $$
> \frac{E}{m_1 + m_2} \le \eta \equiv 1 - \frac{1}{m_1 + m_2}\sqrt{\frac{m_1^2\bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 - \chi_1^2}\bigr) + m_2^2\bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 - \chi_2^2}\bigr)}{1 + \sqrt{1 - \chi_f^2}}} \qquad \bigl(\text{restored: } E \le \eta\,(m_1 + m_2)c^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 2. **Non-spinning holes, non-spinning remnant.** With $q = m_2/m_1 \le 1$,
>
> $$
> \eta = 1 - \frac{\sqrt{m_1^2 + m_2^2}}{m_1 + m_2} = 1 - \frac{\sqrt{1 + q^2}}{1 + q} ,
> $$
>
>    increasing in $q$ and largest for equal masses: $1 - 1/\sqrt2 = 29.29\%$. For $q = 0.1$, $0.5$, $0.8$: $8.64\%$, $25.46\%$, $28.85\%$.
> 3. **A small hole falling in.** The energy radiated is less than the smaller mass, $E < m_2$, so the remnant is heavier than the larger hole (Hartle's Example 12.3). As $q \to 0$ the bound allows $E/m_2 \to 1$: all of the small mass (Schutz, Exercise 11.37(b)).
> 4. **Spins.** Spin of the initial holes loosens the bound, spin of the remnant tightens it. Two extremal holes of equal mass with opposite spins merging into a Schwarzschild hole: $\eta = 1/2$ (Hawking 1971). Equal non-spinning holes and a remnant of spin $\chi_f = 0.5$, $0.68$, $0.9$, $1$: $\eta = 26.79\%$, $24.04\%$, $16.55\%$, $0$.
> 5. **⚑ Irreducible masses.** Equivalently, the irreducible masses $M_{\rm irr} = \sqrt{A/16\pi}$ ([[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^def-c12-5-3|Def. §C12.5.3]]) obey $M_{\rm irr, f}^2 \ge M_{\rm irr,1}^2 + M_{\rm irr,2}^2$, and $M_f \ge M_{\rm irr, f}$ ([[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^thm-c12-5-4|Theorem §C12.5.4]], part 3). Whatever the final spin, $E \le m_1 + m_2 - \sqrt{M_{\rm irr,1}^2 + M_{\rm irr,2}^2}$; for non-spinning holes $M_{\rm irr} = m$ and this is part 2.
>
> *Model:* initial holes widely separated and Kerr, final hole Kerr, energy balance at infinity; *applies:* as an upper bound for every classical merger. Numerical relativity and observation give radiated fractions far below it ([[#^ex-c13-3-1|Example §C13.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §7.4, Example 7.3, eqs. (7.53)–(7.59), pp. 145–147 (PDF 146–148) · Schutz, Exercises 11.37 (a)–(b), 11.29, p. 358 (PDF 376) · Hartle §12.4, Example 12.3, p. 276 (PDF 277) · Hawking, Phys. Rev. Lett. 26 (1971) 1344 (the $29\%$ and $50\%$ bounds; research literature) · numbers by script*

^thm-c13-3-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (areas). Initially the horizon has two components far apart, with total area $A_i = A_1 + A_2$, $A_j = 8\pi m_j^2\bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 - \chi_j^2}\bigr)$ ([[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-3|Theorem §C12.2.3]], part 4, with $a = \chi m$). Finally $A_f = 8\pi M_f^2\bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 - \chi_f^2}\bigr)$. [[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]], part 3, gives $A_f \ge A_i$:
>
> $$
> M_f^2\bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 - \chi_f^2}\bigr) \ge m_1^2\bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 - \chi_1^2}\bigr) + m_2^2\bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 - \chi_2^2}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (part 1). Divide by $1 + \sqrt{1 - \chi_f^2} > 0$, take the square root, and insert in $E/(m_1 + m_2) = 1 - M_f/(m_1 + m_2)$: a lower bound on $M_f$ is an upper bound on $E$, which is $\eta$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 2). With $\chi_1 = \chi_2 = \chi_f = 0$ every bracket is $2$, so $M_f^2 \ge m_1^2 + m_2^2$ (Couzens' (7.54)) and $\eta = 1 - \sqrt{m_1^2 + m_2^2}/(m_1 + m_2)$; divide numerator and denominator by $m_1$. Differentiate the ratio:
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dq}\frac{\sqrt{1 + q^2}}{1 + q} = \frac{\dfrac{q(1 + q)}{\sqrt{1 + q^2}} - \sqrt{1 + q^2}}{(1 + q)^2} = \frac{q + q^2 - 1 - q^2}{\sqrt{1 + q^2}\,(1 + q)^2} = \frac{q - 1}{\sqrt{1 + q^2}\,(1 + q)^2} < 0 \quad (q < 1) ,
> $$
>
> so the ratio decreases and $\eta$ increases on $0 < q < 1$. At $q = 1$: $\sqrt2/2$, $\eta = 1 - 1/\sqrt2 = 0.29289$. At $q = 0.5$: $1 - \sqrt{1.25}/1.5 = 1 - 0.74536 = 0.25464$ (others by script).
>
> **Step 4** (part 3). From Step 3, $M_f^2 \ge m_1^2 + m_2^2 > m_1^2$, so $M_f > m_1$ and $E = m_1 + m_2 - M_f < m_2$. The bound itself is $E_{\max} = m_1 + m_2 - \sqrt{m_1^2 + m_2^2}$; for $m_2 \ll m_1$, $\sqrt{m_1^2 + m_2^2} = m_1 + m_2^2/2m_1 + O(m_2^4/m_1^3)$, so $E_{\max} = m_2 - m_2^2/2m_1 + \dots$ and $E_{\max}/m_2 \to 1$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 4). $\chi_1 = \chi_2 = 1$, $m_1 = m_2 = m$, $\chi_f = 0$: $M_f^2\cdot2 \ge m^2 + m^2$, $M_f \ge m$, $\eta = 1 - m/2m = 1/2$. Opposite spins make the total $J$ zero, so a non-spinning remnant is consistent. For equal non-spinning holes and remnant spin $\chi_f$: $M_f \ge 2m/\sqrt{1 + \sqrt{1 - \chi_f^2}}$, so $\eta = 1 - 1/\sqrt{1 + \sqrt{1 - \chi_f^2}}$; at $\chi_f = 0.68$, $\sqrt{1 - 0.4624} = 0.73321$, $\eta = 1 - 1/\sqrt{1.73321} = 1 - 0.75958 = 0.2404$. At $\chi_f = 1$, $\eta = 0$: an extremal remnant would need all the initial mass.
>
> **Step 6** (part 5). $A = 16\pi M_{\rm irr}^2$ turns $A_f \ge A_1 + A_2$ into $M_{\rm irr,f}^2 \ge M_{\rm irr,1}^2 + M_{\rm irr,2}^2$, and the mass formula $M^2 = M_{\rm irr}^2 + J^2/4M_{\rm irr}^2$ gives $M_f \ge M_{\rm irr,f} \ge \sqrt{M_{\rm irr,1}^2 + M_{\rm irr,2}^2}$; subtract from $m_1 + m_2$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The area theorem allows gravity to turn up to $29\%$ of the rest energy of two equal non-spinning holes into radiation, and with suitably spinning holes up to $50\%$. The bound uses nothing about the merger except its initial and final states.

## What has been observed

> [!law] Law §C13.3.5: Tests of the Area Theorem with Gravitational Waves
> 1. **The quoted parameters of GW150914 satisfy the theorem.** Schutz's Table 12.1 lists component masses $36$ and $29\,M_\odot$ and final mass $62\,M_\odot$; d'Inverno & Vickers give $35 + 30 \to 62\,M_\odot$, a remnant spin $a/M \approx 0.68$, and $3\,M_\odot$ radiated ([[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral#^law-c8-6-7|Law §C8.6.7]]). With these numbers the final area exceeds the largest possible initial area by a factor of at least $1.56$, and the radiated $4.6\%$ is far below the bounds of [[#^thm-c13-3-4|Theorem §C13.3.4]] ([[#^ex-c13-3-1|Example §C13.3.1]]).
> 2. **This is not yet a test.** The final mass in the table is "inferred from numerical simulations" (Schutz, Table 12.1 note): it comes from fitting general-relativistic waveforms, which obey the theorem by construction. A test needs the initial areas and the final area measured independently: the masses and spins of the two holes from the inspiral part of the signal, and the mass and spin of the remnant from the ringdown, the damped oscillations of the final hole ([[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral#^rem-c8-6-2|§C8.6, Remark: Sources beyond the inspiral]]). Schutz (§12.4, pp. 397–398, PDF 415–416) calls observing these ringdown modes "a good proof that the final remnant is a black hole"; the collaboration had not claimed such a direct test of the remnant, and Schutz cites Isi et al. (2019) for the suggestion that it is possible.
> 3. **A published test** (research literature, not in the typed sources; checked 2026-11-21). Isi, Farr, Giesler, Scheel and Teukolsky, "Testing the black-hole area law with GW150914", *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 127 (2021) 011103, arXiv:2012.04486, analysed the inspiral and the ringdown of GW150914 separately and found agreement with the area theorem at $97\%$ probability when the ringdown model includes overtones of the quadrupole mode ($95\%$ without them).
>
> *Valid when:* the inspiral and ringdown estimates are independent, and the ringdown is modelled by Kerr quasinormal modes; the probabilities are those of the cited analysis of one event.
>
> *Model:* binary black-hole merger in full general relativity, remnant Kerr; *applies:* as of the sources' dates (Schutz, d'Inverno & Vickers: 2022; Isi et al.: 2021).
>
> *Source: Schutz §12.4, Table 12.1 and its note, pp. 397–398 (PDF 415–416) · d'Inverno & Vickers §21.14, p. 442 (PDF 457) ("a spin parameter a of about 0.68": the dimensionless $a/M$) · Isi et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 127 (2021) 011103 (research literature)*

> [!remark] Remark: Hawking radiation violates the area theorem
> Schutz (§11.3, item (6), p. 327, PDF 345): an isolated black hole radiates away mass, and with it area, by the Hawking effect, "converting its entropy into the even larger entropy of the escaping radiation". The theorem fails because its hypothesis (H1) fails. Quantum fields can have negative energy density at a point, so the NEC does not hold for them ([[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^rem-c4-5-1|§C4.5, Remark: Where the conditions are used]], last item). In the picture of Hawking emission as pair creation near the horizon, the partner that falls in carries negative energy across the horizon. That is the situation of [[#^ex-c13-3-3|Example §C13.3.3]], with a classical negative-energy shell standing in for the quantum flux. Its horizon shrinks, and the generators converge before the flux arrives. The law that survives is not the area theorem but the generalised second law: the black hole's entropy $A/4$ (in units with $\hbar = k_B = 1$) plus the entropy outside never decreases ([[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^def-c13-4-1|Def. §C13.4.1]], [[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^pr-c13-4-3|Principle §C13.4.3]]). The radiation itself is derived in [[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^thm-c14-3-5|Theorem §C14.3.5]]. How fast the mass, and with it the area, then falls, and how much entropy the radiation carries off, is [[§C14.4 Evaporation and the Information Paradox#^thm-c14-4-1|Theorem §C14.4.1]].

^rem-c13-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Slips in Couzens §7.4
> Read on the rendered pages (PDF 146–148):
> - *Hypotheses (p. 145).* The theorem is said to be proved "using just the Einstein equation, the weak energy condition and cosmic censorship". The sketch then uses the null energy condition, and ends with "the proof assumes Einstein's equations, they are not used in an essential way". The consistent statement is (H1)–(H2) of [[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]]: the NEC suffices ([[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^def-c4-5-2|Def. §C4.5.2]]: the WEC implies it, so the printed hypothesis is correct but stronger than needed). Einstein's equations are used once, to turn the NEC into $R_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu \ge 0$. Schutz (p. 326) likewise states the hypothesis as positive local energy density.
> - *The proof sketch.* Reaching $\theta \to -\infty$ is not yet a contradiction, since caustics happen in flat space ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^cau-c10-6-1|§C10.6, Caution: A caustic is not a singularity]]). What makes it one is the focal-point fact (Q3) together with achronality (Q1). The sketch also assumes that the converging generator is long enough to reach its caustic; Hawking's proof removes this with (H2), (Q4).
> - *Eq. (7.52).* $dA_H/d\lambda = \theta A_H$ holds for each area element; for a whole cross-section it is $\int\theta\,dA$ ([[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]], part 2).
> - *Example 7.2 (p. 145).* "The surface area of a Schwarzschild black hole is $A = 4\pi M^2$" should read $16\pi M^2$; the comparison $A_i > A_f$ is unaffected, since only the ratio of the two areas enters. The areas are called "the entropy of the final state" and "of the initial state", and (7.57) says the bound is reached "when the entropy does not change". The area is not an entropy in this classical setting; it becomes one only through $S = A/4$ with $\hbar$ ([[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^def-c13-4-1|Def. §C13.4.1]]).
> - *Example 7.3.* The remnant is assumed to be a Schwarzschild hole. Real remnants spin, and a spinning remnant only tightens the bound ([[#^thm-c13-3-4|Theorem §C13.3.4]], part 4), so (7.58) remains a valid upper bound.

^cau-c13-3-1

The area of a black hole behaves like entropy: it never decreases in any classical process, it adds when holes are far apart, and it is maximal, at fixed mass, for the hole with no spin ([[#^ex-c13-3-2|Example §C13.3.2]]), as entropy is maximal at equilibrium ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-5|TH Theorem §B2.2.5]]). An area is not an entropy, though: it has the wrong units, and the hole is classically at zero temperature. Of the four laws side by side ([[§C13.1 Surface Gravity and the Zeroth Law#^law-c13-1-6|Law §C13.1.6]]), the zeroth, first and second now have proofs; the third has not been addressed. Whether the analogy is more than formal, and what constant turns an area into an entropy, is the question of [[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy|§C13.4]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C13.3.1: GW150914 against the Area Bound
> *Question.* GW150914 merged holes of $36$ and $29\,M_\odot$ into one of $62\,M_\odot$ (Schutz, Table 12.1), with remnant spin $a/M \approx 0.68$ (d'Inverno & Vickers). How does the radiated energy compare with what [[#^thm-c13-3-4|Theorem §C13.3.4]] allows, and what does the remnant's spin change?
>
> **Step 1** (what was radiated). $E = 36 + 29 - 62 = 3\,M_\odot$, a fraction $3/65 = 4.62\%$; restored, $E = 3\times1.98847\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}\times(2.998\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s})^2 = 5.36\times10^{47}\ \mathrm J$.
>
> **Step 2** (bound for non-spinning holes and remnant). Part 2 with $q = 29/36$:
>
> $$
> \eta = 1 - \frac{\sqrt{36^2 + 29^2}}{65} = 1 - \frac{\sqrt{1296 + 841}}{65} = 1 - \frac{46.228}{65} = 0.2888 ,
> $$
>
> i.e. up to $0.2888\times65 = 18.8\,M_\odot$ could have been radiated. The observed $3\,M_\odot$ is $16\%$ of what is allowed.
>
> **Step 3** (with the remnant's spin). Part 1 with $\chi_1 = \chi_2 = 0$, $\chi_f = 0.68$, $1 + \sqrt{1 - 0.68^2} = 1.73321$:
>
> $$
> M_f \ge \sqrt{\frac{2\,(36^2 + 29^2)}{1.73321}} = \sqrt{\frac{4274}{1.73321}} = \sqrt{2466.0} = 49.66\,M_\odot, \qquad \eta = 1 - \frac{49.66}{65} = 0.2360 .
> $$
>
> The spin of the remnant stores area-free energy: a spinning remnant of given area is heavier ([[#^ex-c13-3-2|Example §C13.3.2]]), so less can be radiated. If the initial holes spun, their area was smaller and the bound looser: for two extremal initial holes the same computation gives $46.0\%$.
>
> **Step 4** (the areas themselves). The largest possible initial area belongs to non-spinning holes, $A_i \le 16\pi(36^2 + 29^2)M_\odot^2 = 16\pi\cdot2137\,M_\odot^2$. The remnant has $A_f = 8\pi\cdot62^2\cdot1.73321\,M_\odot^2 = 16\pi\cdot3331\,M_\odot^2$. So $A_f/A_i \ge 1.559$: the merger increased the horizon area by at least $56\%$, whatever the initial spins were. Restored with $GM_\odot/c^2 = 1.4767\ \mathrm{km}$: $A_i \le 2.34\times10^5\ \mathrm{km^2}$, $A_f = 3.65\times10^5\ \mathrm{km^2}$. d'Inverno & Vickers' $35 + 30 \to 62$ give the same picture: $\eta = 29.1\%$, or $23.8\%$ with $\chi_f = 0.68$.
>
> *What it shows.* Real mergers radiate a few per cent, a factor of $5$ to $6$ below the area bound ($6.3$ without, $5.1$ with the remnant's spin); the bound is reached only by an idealized merger that wastes no area. The check uses parameters fitted with general relativity, so it is a consistency check, not a test ([[#^law-c13-3-5|Law §C13.3.5]], part 2).
>
> *Source: Schutz §12.4, Table 12.1, p. 398 (PDF 416) · d'Inverno & Vickers §21.14, p. 442 (PDF 457) · Couzens, GR2, Example 7.3 (method) · numbers by script*

> [!example] Example §C13.3.2: At Fixed Mass the Area Is Largest at a = 0
> *Question* (Schutz, Exercise 11.39). (a) Among Kerr holes of mass $M$, which has the largest horizon area? (b) Among holes of area $A$, which has the smallest mass? What does this mean for spinning a hole up?
>
> **Step 1** (fixed $M$). $A(a) = 8\pi M\bigl(M + \sqrt{M^2 - a^2}\bigr)$ ([[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-3|Theorem §C12.2.3]], part 4). Differentiate:
>
> $$
> \frac{dA}{da} = 8\pi M\cdot\frac{-2a}{2\sqrt{M^2 - a^2}} = -\frac{8\pi Ma}{\sqrt{M^2 - a^2}} < 0 \qquad (0 < a < M) ,
> $$
>
> so $A$ falls monotonically from $16\pi M^2$ at $a = 0$ to $8\pi M^2$ at $a = M$. In terms of $\chi = a/M$, $A/16\pi M^2 = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 - \chi^2}\bigr)$: $0.9330$, $0.7179$, $0.5705$, $0.5000$ at $\chi = 0.5$, $0.9$, $0.99$, $1$.
>
> **Step 2** (fixed $A$). From [[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^thm-c12-5-4|Theorem §C12.5.4]], part 3, with $M_{\rm irr}^2 = A/16\pi$:
>
> $$
> M^2 = \frac{A}{16\pi} + \frac{4\pi J^2}{A} \ \ge\ \frac{A}{16\pi} ,
> $$
>
> with equality only at $J = 0$: the Schwarzschild hole is the lightest hole of a given area (Christodoulou's formula; with charge, [[§C13.2 Smarr's Formula and the First Law#^thm-c13-2-6|Theorem §C13.2.6]]).
>
> **Step 3** (spinning up). Any process that adds angular momentum $\delta J > 0$ to a hole changes its area by $\delta A = (8\pi/\kappa)(\delta M - \Omega_H\delta J)$ (the first law, [[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^thm-c12-5-5|Theorem §C12.5.5]]; with charge [[§C13.2 Smarr's Formula and the First Law#^thm-c13-2-4|Theorem §C13.2.4]]). By [[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]], $\delta A \ge 0$, so $\delta M \ge \Omega_H\delta J$: a hole cannot be spun up at fixed mass (that would lower its area, by Step 1); each unit of angular momentum must come with at least $\Omega_H$ units of energy. At $a = 0.9M$, $\Omega_H = a/(2Mr_+) = 0.9/(2\times1.4359)\,M^{-1} = 0.3134/M$.
>
> *What it shows.* At fixed mass, spin costs area. Read in the language of §C13.4, the non-rotating hole is the state of maximal entropy for its energy.
>
> *Source: Schutz, Exercise 11.39, p. 358 (PDF 376) · Hartle §15.5 (irreducible mass) · checked by script (symbolic derivative; the mass formula)*

> [!example] Example §C13.3.3: A Negative-Energy Shell Shrinks the Horizon
> *Question* (Hartle, Problem 12.19). A hole of mass $M_0$ absorbs at $v = v_0$ a thin shell of *negative* energy, so its mass drops to $M_1 = 0.7M_0$. Is the area theorem violated, and which hypothesis fails?
>
> **Step 1** (the spacetime and its energy). The Vaidya metric with $m = M_0$ for $v < v_0$ and $M_1$ after solves Einstein's equations with $T_{vv} = m'/(4\pi r^2) = -0.3M_0\,\delta(v - v_0)/(4\pi r^2)$, all other components zero ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]], part 2, with the opposite sign of the jump). For the null vector $k = \partial_v + \tfrac12(1 - 2m/r)\partial_r$, $T_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu = T_{vv} < 0$ on the shell: the NEC fails ([[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^def-c4-5-1|Def. §C4.5.1]]; such matter is the kind wormholes need, [[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^ex-c4-5-4|Example §C4.5.4]]).
>
> **Step 2** (the horizon). After the shell, $\mathcal H^+$ is $r = 2M_1 = 1.4M_0$. Before it, $\mathcal H^+$ is the outgoing ray of the mass-$M_0$ metric that reaches $r = 1.4M_0$ at $v_0$, i.e. $v - 2r_\ast(r) = v_0 - 2r_\ast(1.4M_0)$. Inside $r = 2M_0$, outgoing rays move inward, $dr/dv = \tfrac12(1 - 2M_0/r) < 0$, so towards the past the ray climbs towards $2M_0$: $r_H = 1.997M_0$ at $v_0 - 20M_0$ and $1.821M_0$ at $v_0 - 4M_0$. The area falls monotonically from $16\pi M_0^2$ to $16\pi(0.7M_0)^2 = 0.49\times16\pi M_0^2$ (green curve in the figure after [[#^thm-c13-3-2|Theorem §C13.3.2]]).
>
> **Step 3** (the expansion). On the generators $\theta = (F/r)(1 - 2M_0/r) < 0$ before the shell ([[#^der-c13-3-2|derivation of Theorem §C13.3.2]], Step 3). The rays converge, yet reach no caustic: at $v_0$ the shell's term $-8\pi\int T_{kk}\,d\lambda$ in Raychaudhuri's equation is positive and brings $\theta$ up to $0$. This is exactly the step that (H1) forbids.
>
> **Step 4** (which hypothesis). Everything in [[#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]] holds here except (H1): the horizon is achronal, its generators never leave it, and it is null. The theorem is not violated; its energy hypothesis is. The answer to Hartle's question, "would the area of the horizon always increase?", is therefore no, for a negative-mass shell.
>
> *What it shows.* The area theorem is a theorem about energy, not about the geometry of horizons alone. A flux of negative energy into a hole shrinks it, and the shrinking, like the growth in [[#^thm-c13-3-2|Theorem §C13.3.2]], begins before the flux arrives.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Problem 12.19, p. 279 (PDF 280) · worked here; numbers by script*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - **Second law of thermodynamics.** The entropy of an isolated system never decreases ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|TH Principle §A4.1.2]], Clausius; [[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^pr-a4-3-1|TH Principle §A4.3.1]], Kelvin–Planck; formally [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|TH Theorem §B2.2.3]]). The area theorem is the black-hole counterpart, the second of the four laws ([[§C13.1 Surface Gravity and the Zeroth Law#^law-c13-1-6|Law §C13.1.6]]). Unlike the thermodynamic law, which is a principle extracted from experience, it is a theorem of general relativity plus an energy condition. The two are joined into one law, the generalised second law, in [[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^pr-c13-4-3|Principle §C13.4.3]].
> - **Penrose process and first law.** [[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^thm-c12-5-4|Theorem §C12.5.4]] is the test-particle case: there $\delta A > 0$ followed from the bound $\delta J < \delta M/\Omega_H$ on a single absorbed particle. The first law ([[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^thm-c12-5-5|Theorem §C12.5.5]], [[§C13.2 Smarr's Formula and the First Law#^thm-c13-2-4|Theorem §C13.2.4]]) compares neighbouring Kerr states; the area theorem compares any earlier and later state, stationary or not.
> - **Focusing and singularities.** The same pair, focusing plus "a boundary ray cannot pass a caustic", drives Penrose's singularity theorem ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^rem-c10-6-1|§C10.6, Remark: Penrose's singularity theorem]]); there the rays leave a trapped surface, here they rule the horizon.
> - **Teleology.** [[#^thm-c13-3-2|Theorem §C13.3.2]], part 3, is [[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^thm-c10-7-3|Theorem §C10.7.3]] in a formula: the horizon grows in anticipation of what will fall in. The trapped-surface boundary, a local object, jumps instead ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^def-c10-6-5|Def. §C10.6.5]]).
> - **Collapse.** In the Oppenheimer–Snyder collapse the horizon forms inside the star and grows to $r = 2M$ ([[§C10.3 Penrose Diagrams of Kruskal and of Collapse#^thm-c10-3-4|Theorem §C10.3.4]]): another case of part 5 of Theorem §C13.3.2, with dust instead of light.
> - **Gravitational waves.** The merger bound limits the luminosity budget of the sources in [[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral#^law-c8-6-7|Law §C8.6.7]]; the chirp mass of GW150914 is read off the signal in [[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral#^ex-c8-6-2|Example §C8.6.2]].
