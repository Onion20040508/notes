*Sources: Couzens, GR2, §7.1, Table 1, eqs. (7.1)–(7.3); §7.5; §7.6 · Schutz, A First Course in General Relativity, §11.5, eqs. (11.111)–(11.114) · Hartle, Gravity, §13.3, eqs. (13.12)–(13.14), (13.17)–(13.18) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.14, eq. (20.98).*

Three of the four laws side by side ([[§C13.1 Surface Gravity and the Zeroth Law#^law-c13-1-6|Law §C13.1.6]]) are now theorems: $\kappa$ is constant on a stationary horizon ([[§C13.1 Surface Gravity and the Zeroth Law#^thm-c13-1-5|Theorem §C13.1.5]]), $\delta M = (\kappa/8\pi)\delta A + \Omega_H\delta J + \Phi_H\delta Q$ ([[§C13.2 Smarr's Formula and the First Law#^thm-c13-2-4|Theorem §C13.2.4]]), and $\delta A \ge 0$ ([[§C13.3 The Area Theorem#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]]). Two questions remain open. The first is whether the analogue of absolute zero, a hole with $\kappa = 0$, can be reached. The second is whether the analogy is more than a formal match, that is, whether $A$ really is an entropy.

The naive attempt at the first question is to charge a Reissner–Nordström hole up to extremality, $Q = M$, where $\kappa = 0$ ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-5|Theorem §C11.3.5]]). Take $M = 1$, $Q = \sqrt{1 - 0.01^2} = 0.99995$. The gap is $M - Q = 5.0\times10^{-5}$. Dropping in a particle of charge $q$ and energy $E < q$ closes the gap by $q - E$. It fails: a particle reaches the horizon only if $E > qQ/r_+ = 0.99005\,q$, because the hole repels it electrically. The allowed charge-to-energy ratios lie in a window $1 < q/E < 1.0101$, and the window closes as $\kappa \to 0$ ([[#^thm-c13-4-2|Theorem §C13.4.2]]). This section states the third law with its status ([[#^law-c13-4-1|Law §C13.4.1]]) and proves the test-particle form of it ([[#^thm-c13-4-2|Theorem §C13.4.2]]). It then turns to entropy. Without one, a cup of tea thrown into a hole lowers the entropy of the universe ([[#^rem-c13-4-1|Remark: Bekenstein's argument]]). Once Hawking's temperature is accepted, the first law fixes the entropy at $A/4$ ([[#^def-c13-4-1|Def. §C13.4.1]]), and the second law becomes the generalised second law ([[#^pr-c13-4-3|Principle §C13.4.3]]). The Schwarzschild case is worked out in full ([[#^thm-c13-4-4|Theorem §C13.4.4]]). Its heat capacity is negative, so a hole can be in stable equilibrium only inside a small box ([[#^ex-c13-4-2|Example §C13.4.2]]).

**Setup.** Stationary, asymptotically flat black holes of the Kerr–Newman family with parameters $(M, J, Q)$, for $Q \neq 0$ in Gaussian units ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^cau-c11-1-1|§C11.1, Caution: Units and normalisations in the sources]]). Changes are quasi-static: the hole absorbs test bodies and settles to a nearby member of the family. [[#^law-c13-4-1|Law §C13.4.1]] and [[#^thm-c13-4-2|Theorem §C13.4.2]] are classical. From [[#^def-c13-4-1|Def. §C13.4.1]] on, the section is semiclassical: it uses the Hawking temperature, quoted here and derived in [[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^thm-c14-3-5|Theorem §C14.3.5]], and holds for $M \gg m_{\rm P}$. All numbers were checked by script.

This is the one section of C13 where $\hbar$ and $k_B$ appear ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^rem-c1-2-1|§C1.2, Remark: Why not ħ as well]]). In formulas, $k_B = 1$ and $\hbar$ is kept as a length squared, $\hbar = l_{\rm P}^2 = 2.612\times10^{-70}\ \mathrm{m^2}$. Every number is also given in SI. ## Can a hole be cooled to κ = 0?

> [!law] Law §C13.4.1: The Third Law of Black-Hole Mechanics
> *Statement* (Bardeen–Carter–Hawking; Couzens, Table 1). The surface gravity $\kappa$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-4|Def. §C10.5.4]]) of a black hole cannot be reduced to zero by any finite sequence of operations.
> 1. **Extremal means κ = 0.** For Kerr–Newman, $\kappa = 0$ exactly when $M^2 = a^2 + Q^2$ (with a magnetic charge, $+P^2$). For Reissner–Nordström this is [[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-5|Theorem §C11.3.5]], and for Kerr $\kappa_+ = 0$ at $a = M$ ([[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-3|Theorem §C12.2.3]]).
> 2. **Thermal analogue.** With $\kappa \leftrightarrow T$ it is the unattainability of absolute zero ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-3|TH Principle §A4.6.3]]), the form proved equivalent to Nernst's for ordinary systems in [[§B2.3 The Third Law#^thm-b2-3-4|TH Theorem §B2.3.4]].
> 3. ⚑ **Only the unattainability form carries over.** At $\kappa = 0$ the area is not zero: $A = 4\pi M^2$ for extremal Reissner–Nordström and $A = 8\pi M^2$ for extremal Kerr. With $S = A/4\hbar$ ([[#^def-c13-4-1|Def. §C13.4.1]]), extremal holes have $S \neq 0$. The Nernst–Planck form "$S \to$ const, the same for all parameters, as $T \to 0$" ([[§B2.3 The Third Law#^rem-b2-3-1|TH §B2.3, Remark: The third law, stated precisely]]) fails: the limit $\pi M^2/\hbar$ depends on $M$. For black holes the two forms are not equivalent.
>
> *Valid when:* unsettled, and of the four laws the one on the least firm ground (Couzens §7.5). The typed sources give only evidence: near extremality it gets harder and harder for infalling matter to bring the hole closer to $\kappa = 0$ ([[#^thm-c13-4-2|Theorem §C13.4.2]]). Research literature, not in the typed sources:
> - Israel gave a precise formulation: a sub-extremal hole cannot become extremal in finite advanced time through a continuous process in which the accreted matter obeys the weak energy condition, with a proof under regularity assumptions (W. Israel, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 57 (1986) 397).
> - Kehle and Unger constructed gravitational collapse of a charged scalar field, from regular one-ended data, whose event horizon becomes exactly extremal Reissner–Nordström after finite advanced time. Earlier, the apparent horizon coincides with a Schwarzschild one. They present this as a disproof of the third law in Israel's sense (C. Kehle and R. Unger, arXiv:2211.15742).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §7.1, Table 1 (p. 137, PDF 138); §7.5 (p. 147, PDF 148) · Bardeen, Carter and Hawking, Commun. Math. Phys. 31 (1973) 161 (Couzens' Fig. 30) · areas checked by script*

^law-c13-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.4.2: Test Particles Cannot Push a Hole Past Extremality, and Approach It Ever More Slowly
> *Setup.* A Reissner–Nordström hole with $0 < Q \le M$, $r_\pm = M \pm \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon = \sqrt{M^2 - Q^2}$, $\kappa = \varepsilon/r_+^2$ ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-2|Theorem §C11.3.2]]). A test particle of mass $m$ and charge $q$ moves under the Lorentz force ([[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]]) and falls through the future horizon $r = r_+$. Its conserved energy is $E = -mu_t + qQ/r$. The hole then settles to $M' = M + E$, $Q' = Q + q$.
> 1. **Coulomb barrier.** The particle crosses $r_+$ only if
>
> $$
> E > q\,\Phi_H, \qquad \Phi_H = \frac{Q}{r_+}
> $$
>
>    ($\Phi_H$ is the horizon potential, [[§C13.2 Smarr's Formula and the First Law#^def-c13-2-1|Def. §C13.2.1]]). Equivalently, by the first law ([[§C13.2 Smarr's Formula and the First Law#^thm-c13-2-4|Theorem §C13.2.4]]), $\delta A = (8\pi/\kappa)(E - \Phi_Hq) > 0$.
> 2. **No over-charging.** If the hole is extremal ($\varepsilon = 0$, $\Phi_H = 1$), then $\delta(M - Q) = E - q > 0$ for either sign of $q$: to first order it becomes sub-extremal, never super-extremal.
> 3. **A closing window.** A sub-extremal hole moves toward extremality ($\delta(M - Q) < 0$) only if $E < q$. With part 1, this needs
>
> $$
> 1 < \frac qE < \frac{r_+}{Q}, \qquad \frac{r_+}{Q} - 1 = \frac\varepsilon M + \frac{\varepsilon^2}{2M^2} + O(\varepsilon^3) = \kappa M + O(\varepsilon^2) .
> $$
>
>    The window closes as $\kappa \to 0$. At $\varepsilon = 0.1M,\ 0.01M,\ 0.001M$ its width is $0.106$, $0.0101$, $0.00100$.
> 4. ⚑ **First order cannot decide the last step.** Reaching $M' = Q'$ in one absorption needs at least
>
> $$
> q_{\min} = \frac{M - Q}{1 - Q/r_+} = \frac\varepsilon2 + O(\varepsilon^2), \qquad q_{\min}^2 \approx \tfrac14\,(M^2 - Q^2) .
> $$
>
>    The neglected second-order terms in $q$ are therefore as large as the quantity $M^2 - Q^2$ being driven to zero. The exact change at the bound, $\delta(M^2 - Q^2) = -2qQ\varepsilon/r_+ - q^2(r_+^2 - Q^2)/r_+^2$, already contains one such term, and the particle's own field adds others. Test-particle reasoning at first order gives no answer near extremality.
> 5. **Spin.** For Kerr, every absorbed test particle obeys $\delta J \le \delta M/\Omega_H$, and an extremal hole cannot be over-spun to first order ([[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^thm-c12-5-3|Theorem §C12.5.3]], parts 4–5). Moving toward $J = M^2$ needs $\delta J > 2M\,\delta M$, so
>
> $$
> 2M < \frac{\delta J}{\delta M} \le \frac1{\Omega_H} = \frac{2Mr_+}a, \qquad \frac1{\Omega_H} - 2M = \frac{2M(r_+ - a)}a \xrightarrow{a \to M} 0 ,
> $$
>
>    a window of width $1.19M$, $0.305M$, $0.0915M$ at $a = 0.9M,\ 0.99M,\ 0.999M$.
>
> *Model:* test bodies (charge, energy and angular momentum small compared with $Q$, $M$, $J$), their self-field and radiation neglected; the end state is again Reissner–Nordström (or Kerr) with the totals measured far away ([[§C11.5 Charges in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c11-5-3|Theorem §C11.5.3]], [[§C12.4 Komar Integrals#^thm-c12-4-3|Theorem §C12.4.3]]); *applies:* to first order in $q/M$, $E/M$; part 4 marks where that order stops being enough.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §7.5 (p. 147, PDF 148) (statement only, "it gets harder and harder") · Hartle, Example 15.2 (p. 319, PDF 320) (the Kerr threshold) · part 4 as research literature, not in the typed sources: Hubeny, Phys. Rev. D 59 (1999) 064013, arXiv:gr-qc/9808043, found over-charging of near-extremal Reissner–Nordström at this order with back-reaction neglected; Sorce and Wald, Phys. Rev. D 96 (2017) 104014, arXiv:1707.05862, include the second-order self-force effects and find that Kerr–Newman holes cannot be over-charged or over-spun · all expansions and numbers checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the conserved energy). The particle's action is $S = \int\bigl[-m\sqrt{-g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu} + qA_\mu\dot x^\mu\bigr]d\lambda$ ([[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]], part 1). Neither $g_{\mu\nu}$ nor $A = -(Q/r)\,dt$ depends on $t$, so the Euler–Lagrange equation for $t$ says $\partial L/\partial\dot t$ is constant. With $\lambda = \tau$, the square root is $1$ on the solution and
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot t} = \frac{m\,g_{t\nu}\dot x^\nu}{\sqrt{-g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}} + qA_t = m\,u_t + qA_t = m\,u_t - \frac{qQ}r .
> $$
>
> Define $E = -\partial L/\partial\dot t = -m\,u_t + qQ/r$. Far away ($f \to 1$, $r \to \infty$) $E = m\,u^t = \gamma m$, the energy at infinity. With $u_t = g_{tt}u^t = -f\,\dot t$, $E - qQ/r = mf\dot t$: kinetic energy plus the Coulomb energy $qQ/r$ (restored $qQ/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$).
>
> **Step 2** (part 1). $u_t = g(\xi, u)$ with $\xi = \partial_t$. At $r_+$, $\xi$ is null, nonzero and future-directed, since $r_+$ is a Killing horizon of $\partial_t$ ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-2|Theorem §C11.3.2]]). $u$ is future-directed timelike, so $g(\xi, u) < 0$ (Step 1 of [[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^der-c12-5-2|the derivation of Theorem §C12.5.2]]). The scalar does not depend on the chart, so it may be evaluated in the ingoing coordinates that cover $r_+$. At the crossing event,
>
> $$
> E - \frac{qQ}{r_+} = -m\,g(\xi, u)\big|_{r_+} > 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E > q\,\frac Q{r_+} = q\,\Phi_H .
> $$
>
> Since $\delta M = E$ and $\delta Q = q$, the first law $\delta M = (\kappa/8\pi)\delta A + \Phi_H\delta Q$ gives $\delta A = (8\pi/\kappa)(E - \Phi_Hq) > 0$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 2). At $\varepsilon = 0$: $r_+ = M = Q$ and $\Phi_H = 1$, so Step 2 gives $E > q$ and $\delta(M - Q) = E - q > 0$. For $q < 0$, $Q' < Q \le M < M'$ in any case.
>
> **Step 4** (part 3). $\delta(M - Q) = E - q < 0$ needs $q > E$, so $q > 0$ and $q/E > 1$. Step 2 gives $q/E < r_+/Q$. Expand with $Q = \sqrt{M^2 - \varepsilon^2}$ and $e = \varepsilon/M$:
>
> $$
> \frac{r_+}Q = \frac{1 + e}{\sqrt{1 - e^2}} = (1 + e)\Bigl(1 + \frac{e^2}2 + O(e^4)\Bigr) = 1 + e + \frac{e^2}2 + O(e^3) .
> $$
>
> With $\kappa = \varepsilon/r_+^2 = \varepsilon/(M + \varepsilon)^2$, $\kappa M = e(1 + e)^{-2} = e + O(e^2)$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 4). By Step 2, $\delta(M - Q) = E - q > q\Phi_H - q = -q(1 - Q/r_+)$. Reaching $M' - Q' \le 0$ needs $M - Q \le q(1 - Q/r_+)$, i.e. $q \ge q_{\min}$. Now $M - Q = (M^2 - Q^2)/(M + Q) = \varepsilon^2/(M + Q)$, and
>
> $$
> 1 - \frac Q{r_+} = \frac{r_+ - Q}{r_+} = \frac{\varepsilon + (M - Q)}{M + \varepsilon} = \frac\varepsilon M\bigl(1 + O(e)\bigr), \qquad q_{\min} = \frac{\varepsilon^2}{2M}\bigl(1 + O(e)\bigr)\cdot\frac M\varepsilon\bigl(1 + O(e)\bigr) = \frac\varepsilon2 + O(\varepsilon^2) .
> $$
>
> For the exact change at the bound, insert $M' = M + qQ/r_+$ and $Q' = Q + q$:
>
> $$
> M'^2 - Q'^2 - (M^2 - Q^2) = 2qQ\Bigl(\frac M{r_+} - 1\Bigr) + q^2\Bigl(\frac{Q^2}{r_+^2} - 1\Bigr) = -\frac{2qQ\varepsilon}{r_+} - \frac{q^2(r_+^2 - Q^2)}{r_+^2},
> $$
>
> using $M - r_+ = -\varepsilon$. With $q \sim \varepsilon$ the two terms are both of order $\varepsilon^2$.
>
> **Step 6** (part 5). Part 4 of [[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^thm-c12-5-3|Theorem §C12.5.3]] gives $\delta J \le \delta M/\Omega_H$. $\delta(M^2 - J) = 2M\delta M - \delta J < 0$ needs $\delta J > 2M\delta M$. With $\Omega_H = a/(r_+^2 + a^2)$ ([[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^def-c12-2-2|Def. §C12.2.2]]) and $r_+^2 + a^2 = 2Mr_+$ (from $r_+^2 - 2Mr_+ + a^2 = 0$), $1/\Omega_H = 2Mr_+/a$ and $1/\Omega_H - 2M = 2M(r_+ - a)/a$, which vanishes at $a = M = r_+$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The barrier that keeps charge out is the same Killing-horizon inequality that bounds the Penrose process. Its strength, measured by the window, is set by $\kappa$, so the approach to $\kappa = 0$ slows as $\kappa$ falls. That is the third law's mechanism, but only to first order.

## Why black holes must carry entropy

> [!remark] Remark: Bekenstein's argument
> Wheeler asked Bekenstein what happens to the entropy of a cup of tea thrown into a black hole (Couzens, footnote 25). Outside, the entropy has decreased. Inside, nothing can be measured, and by the uniqueness theorems the hole that settles down carries only $M$, $J$, $Q$ ([[§C12.1 The Kerr Metric#^rem-c12-1-1|§C12.1, Remark: Uniqueness and "no hair"]]). Either the second law ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|TH Principle §A4.1.2]]; entropy form [[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-5|TH Theorem §A4.5.5]]) fails, or the hole itself has an entropy that grows by at least as much. Among its parameters, only the area never decreases ([[§C13.3 The Area Theorem#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]]), so Bekenstein proposed $S \propto A$ (J. D. Bekenstein, *Phys. Rev. D* 7 (1973) 2333; not in the typed sources). The counting reading appears in Couzens §7.6 and in d'Inverno §20.14. A hole of given $(M, J, Q)$ can form from very many different collapses. If the number $\Omega$ of these is finite, which the uncertainty principle suggests, then $k_B\ln\Omega$ is an entropy in the sense of [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-5|TH Def. §B6.1.5]]. Classically the analogy fails at one point. A hole absorbs but never emits, so as a thermodynamic body it would be at zero temperature, and an entropy could not enter its first law as $T\,dS$. Quantum theory supplies the missing temperature.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §7.1 (p. 137, PDF 138); §7.6 and footnote 25 (p. 147, PDF 148) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.14 (p. 394, PDF 409) · Schutz, §11.5 (p. 350, PDF 368)*

^rem-c13-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §C13.4.1: Bekenstein–Hawking Entropy
> *Structure.* A function on the family of stationary black holes, $(M, J, Q) \mapsto S_{\rm BH}$, through the area $A$ of a cross-section of the event horizon (for Kerr–Newman $A = 4\pi(r_+^2 + a^2)$, [[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-3|Theorem §C12.2.3]]). The **Bekenstein–Hawking entropy** is
>
> $$
> S_{\rm BH} = \frac{A}{4\hbar} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } S_{\rm BH} = \frac{k_Bc^3A}{4G\hbar} = k_B\,\frac{A}{4l_{\rm P}^2}, \quad l_{\rm P} = \sqrt{\hbar G/c^3} = 1.616\times10^{-35}\ \mathrm m\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Input, stated (derived in [[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^thm-c14-3-5|Theorem §C14.3.5]]).* A black hole radiates like a black body at the **Hawking temperature**
>
> $$
> T_H = \frac{\hbar\kappa}{2\pi} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } T_H = \frac{\hbar\kappa}{2\pi ck_B},\ \kappa \text{ in } \mathrm{m/s^2}; \text{ Schwarzschild: } T_H = \frac{\hbar c^3}{8\pi Gk_BM} = 6.17\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm K\,\frac{M_\odot}M\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Why $1/4$.* The first law ([[§C13.2 Smarr's Formula and the First Law#^thm-c13-2-4|Theorem §C13.2.4]]) has the term $(\kappa/8\pi)\,dA$. Read as $T\,dS$ with $T = T_H$, it gives
>
> $$
> dS = \frac{\kappa\,dA/8\pi}{\hbar\kappa/2\pi} = \frac{dA}{4\hbar} .
> $$
>
> This fixes $S$ up to a constant. The constant is set to zero, so that a hole of zero mass has zero entropy (Hartle). The entropy is an area in Planck units: one $k_B$ per $4l_{\rm P}^2$ of horizon. For one solar mass, $A = 1.096\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ and $S_{\rm BH} = 1.05\times10^{77}\,k_B$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §7.1, eqs. (7.2)–(7.3) (p. 137, PDF 138) · Hartle, §13.3, eqs. (13.12)–(13.14), (13.17)–(13.18) (pp. 292–294, PDF 293–295) · Schutz, §11.5, eq. (11.114) (p. 350, PDF 368) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.14, eq. (20.98) (p. 394, PDF 409) · numbers checked by script*

^def-c13-4-1

> [!principle] Principle §C13.4.3: The Generalised Second Law
> In every process, the entropy outside black holes plus the Bekenstein–Hawking entropies of all holes does not decrease:
>
> $$
> d\bigl(S_{\rm outside} + S_{\rm BH}\bigr) \ge 0 .
> $$
>
> 1. **Classical processes.** $S_{\rm outside}$ may fall when matter is swallowed, and $S_{\rm BH}$ never falls ([[§C13.3 The Area Theorem#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]]). The principle asks that the growth of $A/4\hbar$ always cover the entropy lost from view ([[#^ex-c13-4-3|Example §C13.4.3]]).
> 2. **Hawking radiation.** The area decreases ([[§C13.3 The Area Theorem#^rem-c13-3-1|§C13.3, Remark: Hawking radiation violates the area theorem]]), and the thermal radiation emitted carries outside entropy that more than compensates. For a Schwarzschild hole radiating into empty space the radiation carries four thirds of the entropy the hole loses ([[§C14.4 Evaporation and the Information Paradox#^thm-c14-4-1|Theorem §C14.4.1]], part 4).
>
> It is the second law of thermodynamics ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|TH Principle §A4.1.2]], [[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^pr-a4-3-1|TH Principle §A4.3.1]]; entropy statement [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|TH Theorem §B2.2.3]]) applied to a universe whose inventory of entropy includes horizons. The area theorem alone is its classical restriction.
>
> *Domain:* all processes involving black holes, classical and semiclassical; with $S_{\rm BH}$ of [[#^def-c13-4-1|Def. §C13.4.1]]. Its general proof is not in the typed sources; they state it (Couzens) or argue it qualitatively (Hartle, Schutz).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §7.1, eq. (7.1) (p. 137, PDF 138) · Hartle, §13.3 (p. 294, PDF 295) · Schutz, §11.5 (p. 350, PDF 368)*

## Thermodynamics of a Schwarzschild hole

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.4.4: Schwarzschild Thermodynamics: dM = T dS, Negative Heat Capacity
> Take $T_H = \hbar\kappa/2\pi$ and $S_{\rm BH} = A/4\hbar$ ([[#^def-c13-4-1|Def. §C13.4.1]]).
> 1. **First law in thermodynamic form.** For Kerr–Newman, $dM = T_H\,dS_{\rm BH} + \Omega_H\,dJ + \Phi_H\,dQ$: $M$ is the internal energy, and $\Omega_H\,dJ + \Phi_H\,dQ$ is the work done on the hole ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|TH Principle §A3.3.1]]).
> 2. **Schwarzschild.** With $\kappa = 1/4M$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^thm-c10-5-4|Theorem §C10.5.4]]) and $A = 16\pi M^2$,
>
> $$
> T_H = \frac\hbar{8\pi M}, \qquad S_{\rm BH} = \frac{4\pi M^2}\hbar, \qquad dM = T_H\,dS_{\rm BH}, \qquad \frac{dS_{\rm BH}}{dM} = \frac1{T_H},
> $$
>
>    and $1/T = dS/dE$ is the statistical definition of temperature ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|TH Def. §B6.2.1]]).
> 3. **Negative heat capacity.** The hole heats up as it loses mass:
>
> $$
> C = \frac{dM}{dT_H} = -\frac{8\pi M^2}\hbar = -\frac M{T_H} < 0 \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } C = -\frac{8\pi Gk_BM^2}{\hbar c} = -2.90\times10^{54}\ \mathrm{J/K} \text{ at } 1\,M_\odot\Bigr) .
> $$
>
>    $S_{\rm BH}(M)$ is convex, $d^2S/dM^2 = 8\pi/\hbar > 0$, where an ordinary system's $S(U)$ is concave.
> 4. ⚑ **No stable equilibrium with a heat bath.** In contact with a reservoir at $T_{\rm bath}$, a hole with $T_H > T_{\rm bath}$ loses energy, grows hotter and loses energy faster. A hole with $T_H < T_{\rm bath}$ absorbs, cools and absorbs more. The equilibrium at $T_H = T_{\rm bath}$ is unstable, as for every system with $C < 0$ ([[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^rem-b2-1-4|TH §B2.1, Remark: Heat capacities can be negative]]). In empty space the same instability makes an isolated hole evaporate ever faster, with a lifetime proportional to the cube of its initial mass ([[§C14.4 Evaporation and the Information Paradox#^thm-c14-4-1|Theorem §C14.4.1]]).
>
> *Model:* semiclassical black holes, $T_H$ quoted from [[§C14.3 The Hawking Temperature#^thm-c14-3-5|Theorem §C14.3.5]]; quasi-static changes between stationary holes; *applies:* for $M \gg m_{\rm P} = \sqrt{\hbar c/G} = 2.18\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{kg}$, where the fixed background approximation behind $T_H$ holds (Schutz (11.111)); part 1 for Kerr–Newman, parts 2–4 for Schwarzschild.
>
> *Source: Schutz, §11.5, eqs. (11.111)–(11.114) (pp. 349–350, PDF 367–368) · Hartle, §13.3, eqs. (13.17)–(13.18) (p. 294, PDF 295) · Couzens, GR2, §9.5, p. 172 (PDF 173) ("the black hole heats up") · checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). Insert $\kappa/8\pi\,dA = T_H\,dS_{\rm BH}$ (Def. §C13.4.1, *Why 1/4*) into the first law $dM = (\kappa/8\pi)\,dA + \Omega_H\,dJ + \Phi_H\,dQ$ ([[§C13.2 Smarr's Formula and the First Law#^thm-c13-2-4|Theorem §C13.2.4]]).
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). $T_H = \hbar\kappa/2\pi = \hbar/(2\pi\cdot4M) = \hbar/8\pi M$; $S_{\rm BH} = 16\pi M^2/4\hbar = 4\pi M^2/\hbar$; $dS_{\rm BH} = (8\pi M/\hbar)\,dM$, so $T_H\,dS_{\rm BH} = (\hbar/8\pi M)(8\pi M/\hbar)\,dM = dM$. This is Schutz's (11.113): $dM = dA/32\pi M = (\hbar/8\pi M)\,d(A/4\hbar)$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 3). $dT_H/dM = -\hbar/8\pi M^2$, so $C = (dT_H/dM)^{-1} = -8\pi M^2/\hbar$, and $-M/T_H = -M\cdot8\pi M/\hbar$ is the same. Restored, $E = Mc^2$ and $T_H = \hbar c^3/(8\pi Gk_BM)$: $dT_H/dM = -T_H/M$ and $C = c^2\,dM/dT_H = -Mc^2/T_H = -8\pi Gk_BM^2/(\hbar c)$. For $M_\odot$: $-1.98847\times10^{30}\cdot(2.998\times10^8)^2/6.170\times10^{-8} = -2.90\times10^{54}\ \mathrm{J/K}$. Convexity: $d^2S/dM^2 = d(8\pi M/\hbar)/dM = 8\pi/\hbar$.
>
> **Step 4** (part 4). Energy flows from hot to cold ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-1|TH Theorem §B6.2.1]]). If $T_H > T_{\rm bath}$, then $dM < 0$, and $dT_H = dM/C > 0$ because $C < 0$: the difference $T_H - T_{\rm bath}$ grows. If $T_H < T_{\rm bath}$, then $dM > 0$ and $dT_H < 0$: the difference grows again.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** $S \propto M^2$ is convex because the area grows faster than the mass. Convexity is negative heat capacity, and it is why a hole and a large heat bath cannot coexist in stable equilibrium.

> [!caution] Caution: Slips in the sources
> - **Couzens (7.3)** prints $S_{\rm BH} = Ac^3/4G_N\hbar$ without $k_B$, while (7.2) keeps $k_B$ in $T_H = \hbar\kappa/2\pi ck_B$. With both kept, $S_{\rm BH} = k_Bc^3A/4G\hbar$. Then $T_H\,dS_{\rm BH} = (\kappa c^2/8\pi G)\,dA$, the restored first-law term. (7.2) itself is right with $\kappa$ in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$.
> - **d'Inverno & Vickers, p. 395 (PDF 410)**, after (20.98): the temperature is given as "about $(M/M_\odot)\times6\times10^{-8}$ K". (20.98) gives $T \propto 1/M$, i.e. $6.17\times10^{-8}\,(M_\odot/M)$ K. A $10\,M_\odot$ hole has $6.2\times10^{-9}$ K, not $6\times10^{-7}$ K.
> - **Schutz (11.111)** writes the Planck mass as $m_{\rm P} = h^{1/2} = 1.6\times10^{-35}\ \mathrm m = 2.2\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{kg}$ (and "$M \approx h/M$" before it). The numbers are those of $\hbar^{1/2}$: $\sqrt{\hbar G/c^3} = 1.616\times10^{-35}$ m and $\sqrt{\hbar c/G} = 2.18\times10^{-8}$ kg, whereas $h^{1/2}$ would give $4.05\times10^{-35}$ m and $5.46\times10^{-8}$ kg.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, p. 137 (PDF 138) · d'Inverno & Vickers, p. 395 (PDF 410) · Schutz, p. 349 (PDF 367) · all rendered and checked by script*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C13.4.1: Entropy of a Solar-Mass Hole and of the Sun
> *Question.* How much entropy does the Sun gain if it collapses to a black hole of the same mass?
>
> **Step 1** (the hole). $r_s = 2GM_\odot/c^2 = 2953.3\ \mathrm m$ and $A = 4\pi r_s^2 = 1.0961\times10^8\ \mathrm{m^2}$. With $l_{\rm P}^2 = \hbar G/c^3 = 2.612\times10^{-70}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ ([[#^def-c13-4-1|Def. §C13.4.1]]),
>
> $$
> \frac{S_{\rm BH}}{k_B} = \frac{A}{4l_{\rm P}^2} = \frac{1.0961\times10^{8}}{4\times2.612\times10^{-70}} = 1.05\times10^{77} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (a model of the Sun). Treat the Sun as a uniform sphere of fully ionised hydrogen: $N = M_\odot/m_p = 1.19\times10^{57}$ protons and as many electrons, in $V = \frac43\pi R_\odot^3 = 1.41\times10^{27}\ \mathrm{m^3}$, so $n = N/V = 8.43\times10^{29}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$ for each species. Its potential energy is
>
> $$
> U = -\int_0^R\frac{G\,M(r)}r\,dm = -\int_0^R\frac{G}{r}\,\frac{Mr^3}{R^3}\,\frac{3Mr^2}{R^3}\,dr = -\frac{3GM^2}{R^6}\int_0^Rr^4\,dr = -\frac{3GM^2}{5R} .
> $$
>
> The virial theorem $2K = -U$ ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-6|CM Theorem §B3.1.6]]) with $K = \frac32(2N)k_BT$ gives
>
> $$
> T = \frac{GM_\odot m_p}{10\,k_BR_\odot} = 2.31\times10^{6}\ \mathrm K .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (its entropy). Both gases are non-degenerate: $n\lambda^3 = 0.099$ for electrons and $1.3\times10^{-6}$ for protons, with $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$. The Sackur–Tetrode equation ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-5|TH Theorem §B8.1.5]]), with $\ln2$ for the two spin states, gives per particle $S/Nk_B = \ln(1/n\lambda^3) + \frac52 + \ln2$: $5.50$ for an electron and $16.78$ for a proton. So
>
> $$
> \frac{S_\odot}{k_B} = 1.19\times10^{57}\times(16.78 + 5.50) = 2.6\times10^{58} .
> $$
>
> The photons add $\frac43aT^3V/k_B = 1.3\times10^{54}$ ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-5|TH Theorem §B11.1.5]]), which is negligible. The model is crude (the real Sun is centrally concentrated), but entropies per particle are logarithmic, so the order $10^{58}$ is robust.
>
> **Step 4** (the comparison). $S_{\rm BH}/S_\odot = 1.05\times10^{77}/2.6\times10^{58} = 4\times10^{18}$. Collapse multiplies the entropy by about $10^{19}$. In the counting reading ([[#^rem-c13-4-1|Remark: Bekenstein's argument]]), the hole corresponds to $e^{10^{77}}$ microstates against $e^{10^{58}}$ for the star. Entropically, collapse is overwhelmingly favoured.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, eq. (7.3) (with $k_B$) · Schutz, eq. (11.114) · solar model and Sackur–Tetrode estimate added here; CODATA constants · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C13.4.2: A Black Hole in a Box: Stable Equilibrium Needs a Small Box
> *Question.* By [[#^thm-c13-4-4|Theorem §C13.4.4]], part 4, a hole cannot sit stably in a heat bath. Can it be in stable equilibrium with its own radiation in a closed box of volume $V$?
>
> *Setup.* An isolated, perfectly reflecting box with total energy $E = M + E_r$, shared between a Schwarzschild hole and black-body photons ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-5|TH Theorem §B11.1.5]]): $E_r = \sigma VT^4$ and $S_r = \frac43\sigma VT^3$, with $\sigma = \pi^2/15$ in $G = c = \hbar = k_B = 1$. Gravitons and the hole's effect on the box geometry are neglected. Equilibrium of an isolated system is a maximum of total entropy at fixed $E$ ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-5|TH Theorem §B2.2.5]]).
>
> **Step 1** (total entropy). Eliminate $T = (E_r/\sigma V)^{1/4}$: $S_r = \frac43(\sigma V)^{1/4}E_r^{3/4}$. Then
>
> $$
> S(M) = 4\pi M^2 + \tfrac43(\sigma V)^{1/4}(E - M)^{3/4} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (stationary point). $dS/dM = 8\pi M - (\sigma V)^{1/4}(E - M)^{-1/4} = 1/T_H - 1/T_r = 0$: equal temperatures.
>
> **Step 3** (maximum?). $d^2S/dM^2 = 8\pi - \frac14(\sigma V)^{1/4}(E - M)^{-5/4}$. At the stationary point, $(\sigma V)^{1/4} = 8\pi M(E - M)^{1/4}$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2S}{dM^2} = 8\pi - \frac14\cdot\frac{8\pi M}{E - M} = 8\pi\Bigl(1 - \frac M{4E_r}\Bigr) < 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E_r < \frac M4 .
> $$
>
> The equilibrium is stable only if the radiation holds less than a quarter of the hole's energy. The hole's negative heat capacity must be outweighed by a radiation bath too small to act as a reservoir.
>
> **Step 4** (in terms of the box). At $E_r = M/4$ and $T = T_H = 1/8\pi M$: $V_c = (M/4)/(\sigma T_H^4) = 1024\pi^4M^5/\sigma = 15360\pi^2M^5$. Stable holes need $V < V_c$, a bound that grows as $M^5$. In the scaled form of the figure, $x = M/E$ and $w = (\sigma V)^{1/4}E^{-5/4}$. Then $S/E^2 = 4\pi x^2 + \frac43w(1 - x)^{3/4}$ and the stationarity condition is $w = 8\pi x(1 - x)^{1/4}$. Its right side peaks at $x = 4/5$ (where $E_r = M/4$) with value $13.45$. For $w > 13.45$ the box is too large for any equilibrium with a hole. For $w < 9.56$ the hole state is the global entropy maximum. In between it is only a local one.
>
> **Step 5** (numbers). For $M = M_\odot$, $T_H = 6.17\times10^{-8}$ K. Then $aT_H^4 = 1.10\times10^{-44}\ \mathrm{J/m^3}$ ($a = 7.566\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}K^{-4}}$), and
>
> $$
> V_c = \frac{M_\odot c^2}{4aT_H^4} = \frac{1.787\times10^{47}\ \mathrm J}{4\times1.097\times10^{-44}\ \mathrm{J/m^3}} = 4.1\times10^{90}\ \mathrm{m^3},
> $$
>
> a sphere of radius $9.9\times10^{29}\ \mathrm m = 1.0\times10^{14}$ light-years. "Small" is relative. In the actual universe, a hole is in equilibrium with the cosmic background at $2.7255$ K when $M = 1.227\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{K\,kg}/2.7255\ \mathrm K = 4.50\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $0.61$ lunar masses. The background is an effectively infinite bath, so by part 4 that equilibrium is unstable. Which side of this mass a hole lies on decides whether it grows or evaporates today, and at what rate: [[§C14.4 Evaporation and the Information Paradox#^ex-c14-4-2|Example §C14.4.2]].
>
> *Source: Hawking analysed this equilibrium (S. W. Hawking, *Phys. Rev. D* 13 (1976) 191; not in the typed sources); the inequality is derived here from [[#^thm-c13-4-4|Theorem §C13.4.4]] and the photon gas · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C13.4.3: Throwing a Cup of Tea into a Hole
> *Question.* Wheeler's cup: $m = 0.25\ \mathrm{kg}$ of tea (water) at $298.15$ K falls into a solar-mass hole. Does the generalised second law ([[#^pr-c13-4-3|Principle §C13.4.3]]) hold, and by how much?
>
> **Step 1** (entropy lost from view). The standard molar entropy of liquid water at $298.15$ K is $69.95\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}mol^{-1}}$ (CODATA Key Values for Thermodynamics, Cox, Wagman and Medvedev 1989; not in the typed sources). It is a third-law entropy ([[§B2.3 The Third Law#^ex-b2-3-2|TH Example §B2.3.2]] shows how such values are measured). With $0.25/0.018015 = 13.88$ mol,
>
> $$
> S_{\rm tea} = 13.88 \times 69.95\ \mathrm{J/K} = 970.7\ \mathrm{J/K} = 7.03\times10^{25}\,k_B .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (entropy gained by the hole). The tea brings energy $\delta M = m$ (dropped from rest far away). With $S_{\rm BH} = 4\pi GM^2k_B/\hbar c$ (restored from Theorem §C13.4.4),
>
> $$
> \frac{\delta S_{\rm BH}}{k_B} = \frac{8\pi GM_\odot m}{\hbar c} = \frac{8\pi\times6.674\times10^{-11}\times1.988\times10^{30}\times0.25}{1.0546\times10^{-34}\times2.998\times10^{8}} = 2.64\times10^{46} .
> $$
>
> The linear form is exact up to a relative correction $m/2M_\odot = 6\times10^{-32}$.
>
> **Step 3** (the balance). $d(S_{\rm outside} + S_{\rm BH}) = (2.64\times10^{46} - 7.03\times10^{25})\,k_B > 0$. The hole gains $3.8\times10^{20}$ times what the outside lost.
>
> **Step 4** (can a hole be small enough to lose?). The break-even hole, with $\delta S_{\rm BH} = S_{\rm tea}$, has $M_\ast = S_{\rm tea}\hbar c/(8\pi Gmk_B) = 5.30\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{kg}$. Its radius is $r_s = 7.9\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm m$ and its temperature $T_H = 2.3\times10^{13}$ K. It cannot swallow the cup whole, and it is a fierce radiator, so the classical bookkeeping of Steps 1–3 does not apply to it. A hole that can swallow the cup in one piece, $r_s \ge R_{\rm cup} = 4$ cm, has $M \ge c^2R_{\rm cup}/2G = 2.69\times10^{25}$ kg. It gains at least
>
> $$
> \frac{\delta S_{\rm BH}}{k_B} \ge \frac{8\pi G}{\hbar c}\cdot\frac{c^2R_{\rm cup}}{2G}\cdot m = \frac{4\pi mcR_{\rm cup}}{\hbar} = 3.57\times10^{41} \gg 7.03\times10^{25} .
> $$
>
> The size of the object, and not only its energy, protects the generalised second law.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §7.6, footnote 25 (p. 147, PDF 148) (the question) · CODATA key value for water · numbers checked by script*
