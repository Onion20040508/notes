Every black hole of chapters C6–C11 was spherically symmetric. Real collapsing stars are not: they rotate, and their angular momentum cannot be radiated away as easily as their bumps ([[#^ex-c12-1-2|Example §C12.1.2]]: collapsed whole, the Sun would carry more angular momentum than any hole of its mass can hold). The question of chapter C12 is **what a spinning black hole is**. Charge, the subject of C11, is cancelled in bulk matter; spin is not (Schutz §11.3, item (2)). Two results are the starting point: the uniqueness of Schwarzschild among spherical vacua ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-5|Theorem §C6.2.5]]), which says that a spinning hole needs a new metric, and the far field of a slowly rotating body ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^ex-c8-1-3|Example §C8.1.3]]), which says what that metric must look like far away: a cross term $g_{t\varphi} = -2J\sin^2\theta/r$, falling off like $1/r$.

*Naive attempt: rotate Schwarzschild.* Take the Schwarzschild metric ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]) and let the angle turn, $\varphi = \varphi' + \Omega t$ with a constant $\Omega$. Then $d\varphi = d\varphi' + \Omega\,dt$ and

$$
ds^2 = -\Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r} - \Omega^2r^2\sin^2\theta\Bigr)dt^2 + 2\Omega r^2\sin^2\theta\,dt\,d\varphi' + \frac{dr^2}{1 - 2M/r} + r^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi'^2\bigr) .
$$

A cross term $g_{t\varphi'}$ has appeared, and the metric still solves the vacuum equations, since only the chart changed. But it is the wrong cross term. It *grows* like $r^2$ instead of falling like $1/r$, and $g_{tt}$ changes sign at $r\sin\theta \approx 1/\Omega$, where an observer at fixed $(r, \theta, \varphi')$ would have to move faster than light. Every coordinate-free quantity is still Schwarzschild's, and the angular momentum is zero ([[#^ex-c12-1-1|Example §C12.1.1]] works this out). Rotation of the *source* has to show up in the geometry itself, as the falling cross term of Example §C8.1.3 does. Couzens' remark explains why a new metric is needed: run time backwards and a rotating body rotates the other way, so its metric cannot be invariant under $t \to -t$. It is stationary but not static, and spherical symmetry is lost as well, with only the rotations about the spin axis surviving.

This section first names that symmetry class ([[#^def-c12-1-1|Def. §C12.1.1]]). It then states the exact vacuum solution with the required far field, Kerr's metric, in two charts, the Boyer–Lindquist chart, which generalises Schwarzschild's coordinates, and the Kerr–Schild chart, a flat metric plus the square of a null one-form ([[#^thm-c12-1-1|Theorem §C12.1.1]]). Its limits show what the coordinates are: at $a = 0$ Schwarzschild's, at $M = 0$ flat space in oblate spheroidal coordinates, in which $r = 0$ is a disc ([[#^thm-c12-1-2|Theorem §C12.1.2]]). The far field identifies $M$ as the mass and $J = aM$ as the angular momentum ([[#^thm-c12-1-3|Theorem §C12.1.3]]). The Killing vectors prove that Kerr is stationary but not static ([[#^thm-c12-1-4|Theorem §C12.1.4]]). The charged generalisation, Kerr–Newman, closes the family ([[#^thm-c12-1-5|Theorem §C12.1.5]]), and its far field adds a magnetic dipole ([[#^thm-c12-1-6|Theorem §C12.1.6]]). Uniqueness says this family is the whole story ([[#^rem-c12-1-1|Remark: Uniqueness and "no hair"]]).

**Setup.** Vacuum solutions of Einstein's equations, $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^thm-c5-1-6|Theorem §C5.1.6]]), and, for Kerr–Newman, electrovacuum solutions ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^mod-c11-1-2|Model §C11.1.2]]), stationary and axisymmetric. Two parameters for Kerr, $M > 0$ and $a$ (any real number; $a > 0$ means rotation in the sense of increasing $\varphi$); $Q$ and $P$ for Kerr–Newman. Statements about the metric hold on any region where the chart is regular ($\Delta \neq 0$, $\rho \neq 0$, $\theta \neq 0, \pi$); which regions are black holes, and what happens at $\Delta = 0$ and $\rho = 0$, is §C12.2. The identification of $J$ uses the weak-field metric far away; its coordinate-free form is the Komar integral of §C12.4. Algebraic identities are derived in full; Ricci-flatness and the Einstein–Maxwell equations are checked by script (`tools/verify_c12_1.py`, 51 checks; Boyer–Lindquist Ricci-flatness also in `tools/verify_c12.py`).

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here $M \to GM/c^2$ and $a \to J/(Mc)$, both lengths); Gaussian electromagnetism with $\mu_0 = 4\pi$ as in C11 ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^thm-c11-1-1|Theorem §C11.1.1]]); $\rho^2 = r^2 + a^2\cos^2\theta$ and $\Delta = r^2 - 2Mr + a^2$ throughout; d'Inverno & Vickers ($+{-}{-}{-}$) converted.

## Stationary, axisymmetric spacetimes

A spinning isolated body looks the same at every time and after any rotation about its spin axis. In the language of [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors|§C3.13]] that is two Killing vectors, one timelike far away and one whose flow is a rotation.

> [!definition] Definition §C12.1.1: Stationary and Axisymmetric Spacetime
> *Structure.* Let $(\mathcal M, g)$ be a time-oriented Lorentzian manifold that is asymptotically flat ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-2|Def. §C10.4.2]]), with two Killing vector fields $K$ and $R$ ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-2|Def. §C3.13.2]]). $(\mathcal M, g)$ is **stationary and axisymmetric** if
> 1. $K$ is timelike **near infinity** (outside some compact set of each spatial slice) and normalised by $g(K, K) \to -1$ there: the spacetime is stationary on that region in the sense of [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-3|Def. §C3.13.3]];
> 2. the flow of $R$ is a rotation: its orbits are closed curves of parameter length $2\pi$, $R$ is spacelike where it is nonzero, and the set where $R = 0$, the **axis**, is not empty;
> 3. $K$ and $R$ commute, $[K, R] = 0$ ([[§72 Lie Derivatives#^def-72-3|591 Def. §72.3]]), so the time translations and the rotations can be applied in either order.
>
> $K$ need not be timelike everywhere: between the two horizons of a black hole it is spacelike, and for a rotating hole it is spacelike in a region outside the horizon as well, the ergosphere ([[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^def-c12-5-2|Def. §C12.5.2]]). This is why "everywhere timelike" in Couzens' Definition 21 is replaced by "near infinity" ([[#^cau-c12-1-1|Caution: Slips in the sources]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §6 introduction, Defs. 21–22, p. 111 (PDF 112) (definition corrected) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.4, p. 372 (PDF 387) (axial symmetry: an invariantly defined axis, orbits of $\partial_\phi$ circles) · Hartle §15.2, eqs. (15.4)–(15.5), p. 312 (PDF 313)*

## The Kerr metric

Kerr found the solution in 1963 by searching among metrics of the form "flat metric plus the square of a null one-form" (d'Inverno & Vickers §20.3); The chart named after Boyer and Lindquist is the one in which it most resembles Schwarzschild. Both charts are needed: the second for the physics, the first because it is regular across the horizons and is the form the user's Kerr ray tracer integrates.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C12.1.1: The Kerr Metric in Boyer–Lindquist Coordinates Solves the Vacuum Equations
> Let $M > 0$ and $a \in \mathbf R$, and write $\rho^2 = r^2 + a^2\cos^2\theta$, $\Delta = r^2 - 2Mr + a^2$.
> 1. **Boyer–Lindquist form.** The **Kerr metric** is
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -\Bigl(1 - \frac{2Mr}{\rho^2}\Bigr)dt^2 - \frac{4Mar\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}\,dt\,d\varphi + \frac{\rho^2}{\Delta}\,dr^2 + \rho^2\,d\theta^2 + \Bigl(r^2 + a^2 + \frac{2Ma^2r\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}\Bigr)\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2 ,
> $$
>
>    on the region of $(t, r, \theta, \varphi)$ where $\Delta \neq 0$, $\rho \neq 0$, $0 < \theta < \pi$, $\varphi \sim \varphi + 2\pi$; $r$ may take negative values ([[#^thm-c12-1-2|Theorem §C12.1.2]], part 3). It satisfies $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$, the vacuum equations ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^thm-c5-1-6|Theorem §C5.1.6]]), on that whole region: outside, between and inside the zeros of $\Delta$ (checked by script).
> 2. **Split form.** Equivalently, with the one-form $k = dt - a\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi$,
>
> $$
> ds^2 = \underbrace{-dt^2 + \frac{\rho^2}{r^2 + a^2}\,dr^2 + \rho^2\,d\theta^2 + (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2}_{\text{flat (}M = 0\text{)}} + \frac{2Mr}{\rho^2}\,k^2 + \Bigl(\frac{\rho^2}{\Delta} - \frac{\rho^2}{r^2 + a^2}\Bigr)dr^2 ,
> $$
>
>    in 't Hooft's arrangement $-dt^2 + (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2 + \frac{2Mr}{\rho^2}k^2 + \rho^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + dr^2/\Delta\bigr)$.
> 3. **Kerr–Schild form.** Define $v$, $\bar\varphi$ by $dv = dt + \frac{r^2 + a^2}{\Delta}dr$, $d\bar\varphi = d\varphi + \frac{a}{\Delta}dr$, then $\bar t = v - r$ and Cartesian-type coordinates
>
> $$
> x + iy = (r + ia)\sin\theta\,e^{i\bar\varphi}, \qquad z = r\cos\theta .
> $$
>
>    In the chart $(\bar t, x, y, z)$ the metric is
>
> $$
> g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + 2H\,l_\mu l_\nu, \qquad H = \frac{Mr^3}{r^4 + a^2z^2}, \qquad l_\mu dx^\mu = d\bar t + \frac{r(x\,dx + y\,dy) + a(y\,dx - x\,dy)}{r^2 + a^2} + \frac{z\,dz}{r} ,
> $$
>
>    where $r(x, y, z)$ is the root of $r^4 - (x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - a^2)r^2 - a^2z^2 = 0$ that is positive in the region $r > 0$. The one-form $l$ is null for $\eta$ and for $g$, the inverse metric is $g^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\nu} - 2H\,l^\mu l^\nu$ (indices of $l$ raised with $\eta$), and $\det g_{\mu\nu} = -1$. This is the form, with $M = 1$, used in the user's Kerr ray tracer.
>
> *Model:* vacuum, $\Lambda = 0$; *applies:* exactly, on each region where the chart is regular. It is the exterior of a rotating *black hole*, not of a rotating star ([[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^rem-c12-2-1|the remark on collapse in §C12.2]]); the horizons and the ring $\rho = 0$ are [[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-1|Theorem §C12.2.1]] and [[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-4|Theorem §C12.2.4]].
>

(11.71)–(11.72), p. 328 (PDF 346) · 't Hooft, ch. 13b, eqs. (13.21)–(13.22), p. 58 (PDF 59) ("requires patience but is not difficult") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.3, eqs. > [!derivation]- Derivation
> The vacuum equations are not solved here by hand: the Ricci tensor of part 1 and of part 3 is computed by script at points outside, between and inside the zeros of $\Delta$ (largest component $2\times10^{-29}$ at 30 digits). What is derived is that the three forms are one metric, and the algebra that makes the Kerr–Schild form simple.
>
> **Step 1** (part 1 ⇒ part 2). Expand $\frac{2Mr}{\rho^2}k^2 = \frac{2Mr}{\rho^2}\bigl(dt^2 - 2a\sin^2\theta\,dt\,d\varphi + a^2\sin^4\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr)$ and add $-dt^2 + (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2$:
>
> $$
> -\Bigl(1 - \frac{2Mr}{\rho^2}\Bigr)dt^2 - \frac{4Mar\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}\,dt\,d\varphi + \Bigl(r^2 + a^2 + \frac{2Ma^2r\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}\Bigr)\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2 ,
> $$
>
> the $(t, \varphi)$ block of part 1; the $dr^2$ and $d\theta^2$ terms are $\rho^2dr^2/\Delta$ and $\rho^2d\theta^2$ in both. At $M = 0$, $\Delta = r^2 + a^2$, and only the underbraced flat part remains.
>
> **Step 2** (the null one-form in Boyer–Lindquist coordinates). With the definitions of part 3, $dt = dv - \frac{r^2 + a^2}{\Delta}dr$ and $d\varphi = d\bar\varphi - \frac{a}{\Delta}dr$, so
>
> $$
> k = dv - a\sin^2\theta\,d\bar\varphi - \frac{(r^2 + a^2) - a^2\sin^2\theta}{\Delta}\,dr = dv - a\sin^2\theta\,d\bar\varphi - \frac{\rho^2}{\Delta}\,dr .
> $$
>
> Define $l \equiv dv - a\sin^2\theta\,d\bar\varphi = k + \frac{\rho^2}{\Delta}dr$.
>
> **Step 3** (subtract the square of $l$). Using part 2, $g = -dt^2 + (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2 + \rho^2d\theta^2 + \frac{\rho^2}{\Delta}dr^2 + \frac{2Mr}{\rho^2}\bigl(l - \frac{\rho^2}{\Delta}dr\bigr)^2$, and
>
> $$
> g - \frac{2Mr}{\rho^2}\,l^2 = -dt^2 + (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2 + \rho^2d\theta^2 + \frac{\rho^2}{\Delta}\Bigl(1 + \frac{2Mr}{\Delta}\Bigr)dr^2 - \frac{4Mr}{\Delta}\,l\,dr .
> $$
>
> Insert $dt$, $d\varphi$ from Step 2 and $l = dv - a\sin^2\theta\,d\bar\varphi$:
>
> $$
> -dt^2 = -dv^2 + \frac{2(r^2 + a^2)}{\Delta}\,dv\,dr - \frac{(r^2 + a^2)^2}{\Delta^2}\,dr^2, \qquad (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2 = (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta\Bigl(d\bar\varphi^2 - \frac{2a}{\Delta}\,d\bar\varphi\,dr + \frac{a^2}{\Delta^2}\,dr^2\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Collect, using $r^2 + a^2 - 2Mr = \Delta$, $\Delta + 2Mr = r^2 + a^2$ and $r^2 + a^2 - a^2\sin^2\theta = \rho^2$:
>
> $$
> dv\,dr:\ \frac{2(r^2 + a^2) - 4Mr}{\Delta} = 2, \qquad d\bar\varphi\,dr:\ \frac{-2a(r^2 + a^2) + 4Mar}{\Delta}\sin^2\theta = -2a\sin^2\theta ,
> $$
>
> $$
> dr^2:\ \frac{-(r^2 + a^2)^2 + a^2(r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta + \rho^2(\Delta + 2Mr)}{\Delta^2} = \frac{-(r^2 + a^2)\rho^2 + \rho^2(r^2 + a^2)}{\Delta^2} = 0 .
> $$
>
> So, with $v = \bar t + r$, $-dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr = -d\bar t^2 + dr^2$, and
>
> $$
> g - \frac{2Mr}{\rho^2}\,l^2 = -d\bar t^2 + dr^2 - 2a\sin^2\theta\,dr\,d\bar\varphi + (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta\,d\bar\varphi^2 + \rho^2d\theta^2 .
> $$
>
> Every $1/\Delta$ has cancelled: this part does not see the horizons.
>
> **Step 4** (it is flat). Write $w = x + iy = (r + ia)\sin\theta\,e^{i\bar\varphi}$. Then $dw = e^{i\bar\varphi}(R_1 + iI_1)$ with
>
> $$
> R_1 = \sin\theta\,dr + r\cos\theta\,d\theta - a\sin\theta\,d\bar\varphi, \qquad I_1 = a\cos\theta\,d\theta + r\sin\theta\,d\bar\varphi ,
> $$
>
> and $dx^2 + dy^2 = |dw|^2 = R_1^2 + I_1^2$; with $dz = \cos\theta\,dr - r\sin\theta\,d\theta$,
>
> $$
> R_1^2 + I_1^2 + dz^2 = dr^2 + (r^2 + a^2\cos^2\theta)\,d\theta^2 + (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta\,d\bar\varphi^2 - 2a\sin^2\theta\,dr\,d\bar\varphi ,
> $$
>
> since the $d\theta\,d\bar\varphi$ terms $\mp2ar\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and the $dr\,d\theta$ terms $\pm2r\sin\theta\cos\theta$ cancel, and $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ in $dr^2$. This is Step 3's spatial part: $g - \frac{2Mr}{\rho^2}l^2 = -d\bar t^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2 = \eta$.
>
> **Step 5** ($l$ in Cartesian form, and $H$). From $x^2 + y^2 = |w|^2 = (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta$ and $z = r\cos\theta$: $(x^2 + y^2)/(r^2 + a^2) + z^2/r^2 = 1$, which multiplied by $r^2(r^2 + a^2)$ is the quartic of part 3. Then $x\,dx + y\,dy = \frac12d(x^2 + y^2) = r\sin^2\theta\,dr + (r^2 + a^2)\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta$, $z\,dz = r\cos^2\theta\,dr - r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta$, and $x\,dy - y\,dx = \operatorname{Im}(\bar w\,dw) = -a\sin^2\theta\,dr + (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta\,d\bar\varphi$. So
>
> $$
> \frac{r(x\,dx + y\,dy) + a(y\,dx - x\,dy)}{r^2 + a^2} + \frac{z\,dz}{r} = \Bigl(\frac{r^2\sin^2\theta + a^2\sin^2\theta}{r^2 + a^2} + \cos^2\theta\Bigr)dr - a\sin^2\theta\,d\bar\varphi = dr - a\sin^2\theta\,d\bar\varphi ,
> $$
>
> the $d\theta$ terms cancelling. Adding $d\bar t$ gives $d\bar t + dr - a\sin^2\theta\,d\bar\varphi = dv - a\sin^2\theta\,d\bar\varphi = l$. And $r^4 + a^2z^2 = r^2(r^2 + a^2\cos^2\theta) = r^2\rho^2$, so $2H = 2Mr^3/(r^2\rho^2) = 2Mr/\rho^2$. With Step 4, $g = \eta + 2H\,l\otimes l$: part 3.
>
> **Step 6** (null, inverse, determinant). $\eta^{\mu\nu}l_\mu l_\nu = -1 + \frac{(rx + ay)^2 + (ry - ax)^2}{(r^2 + a^2)^2} + \frac{z^2}{r^2} = -1 + \frac{x^2 + y^2}{r^2 + a^2} + \frac{z^2}{r^2} = 0$ by the quartic. Then, raising with $\eta$,
>
> $$
> (\eta_{\mu\nu} + 2Hl_\mu l_\nu)(\eta^{\nu\rho} - 2Hl^\nu l^\rho) = \delta_\mu^\rho - 2Hl_\mu l^\rho + 2Hl_\mu l^\rho - 4H^2l_\mu(l_\nu l^\nu)l^\rho = \delta_\mu^\rho ,
> $$
>
> and $g^{\mu\nu}l_\mu l_\nu = (\eta^{\mu\nu} - 2Hl^\mu l^\nu)l_\mu l_\nu = 0$. For the determinant write $g = \eta(1 + N)$ with $N^\mu{}_\nu = 2Hl^\mu l_\nu$; $N^2 = 4H^2l^\mu(l_\lambda l^\lambda)l_\nu = 0$, so $N$ is nilpotent, all eigenvalues of $1 + N$ equal $1$, and $\det g = \det\eta\cdot\det(1 + N) = -1$.
>
> **Step 7** (the ray tracer). The ray tracer's `metric_Hl` computes exactly $r$ (the positive root of the quartic), $H = r^3/(r^4 + a^2z^2)$ and $(l_x, l_y, l_z) = \bigl((rx + ay)/(r^2 + a^2),\ (ry - ax)/(r^2 + a^2),\ z/r\bigr)$ with $l_{\bar t} = 1$, in units $M = 1$; its geodesic Hamiltonian uses $g^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\nu} - 2Hl^\mu l^\nu$, Step 6.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Kerr is flat space plus a single term, $2H\,l\otimes l$, built from a null one-form: the same structure as Schwarzschild, for which $l = d\bar t + dr$ and $2H = 2M/r$ (d'Inverno & Vickers (20.34)–(20.35)).
> - The flat part, written in Kerr's coordinates, is flat space in the oblate spheroidal coordinates of [[#^thm-c12-1-2|Theorem §C12.1.2]]: $M$ enters only through $H$.
> - All $1/\Delta$ singularities sit in the change of chart $(t, \varphi) \to (v, \bar\varphi)$, not in the geometry; that the zeros of $\Delta$ are horizons is §C12.2.
> - Because $l$ is null, the inverse metric is exact at first order in $H$ and $\sqrt{-g} = 1$: this is why the ray tracer can integrate geodesics through the horizon without a coordinate singularity.

*Uses:* [[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^thm-c5-1-6|Theorem §C5.1.6]], [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]], [[#^thm-c12-1-2|Theorem §C12.1.2]]

What do the coordinates mean? Two limits answer this, and the second shows that $r$ is not the distance from a centre.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C12.1.2: Limits: a → 0 Is Schwarzschild; M → 0 Is Flat Space in Oblate Spheroidal Coordinates
> 1. **No rotation.** At $a = 0$ the Kerr metric of [[#^thm-c12-1-1|Theorem §C12.1.1]] is the Schwarzschild metric ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]) in Schwarzschild coordinates.
> 2. **No mass.** At $M = 0$ it is
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -dt^2 + \frac{\rho^2}{r^2 + a^2}\,dr^2 + \rho^2\,d\theta^2 + (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2 ,
> $$
>
>    Minkowski space ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]) with the spatial part written in **oblate spheroidal coordinates**:
>
> $$
> x = \sqrt{r^2 + a^2}\,\sin\theta\cos\varphi, \qquad y = \sqrt{r^2 + a^2}\,\sin\theta\sin\varphi, \qquad z = r\cos\theta .
> $$
>
> 3. **The surfaces.** With $\varpi = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, the surfaces $r = \text{const} > 0$ are the oblate spheroids $\varpi^2/(r^2 + a^2) + z^2/r^2 = 1$ (equatorial radius $\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}$, polar radius $r$), and the surfaces $\theta = \text{const}$ ($\neq 0, \frac\pi2, \pi$) are the hyperboloids of one sheet $\varpi^2/(a^2\sin^2\theta) - z^2/(a^2\cos^2\theta) = 1$; both families share the focal circle $\varpi = a$, $z = 0$. The surface $r = 0$ is the **disc** $\varpi \le a$, $z = 0$, of radius $a$. With $r \ge 0$ and $0 \le \theta \le \pi$ the coordinates cover all of space once, except that each interior point of the disc has two labels, $\theta$ (its upper face) and $\pi - \theta$ (its lower face). The labels $(r, \theta)$ and $(-r, \pi - \theta)$ name the same point, so continuing $r$ to negative values carries a curve through the disc; for $M \neq 0$ this continuation leads to a new region (§C12.2, [[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-4|Theorem §C12.2.4]]).
> 4. **The two ends.** For $r \to +\infty$, $g_{tt} = -1 + 2M/r + O(r^{-3})$: Schwarzschild with mass $M$ far away. For $r \to -\infty$, $g_{tt} = -1 - 2M/|r| + O(|r|^{-3})$: the far field of a *negative* mass $-M$ (compare [[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^ex-c10-7-3|Example §C10.7.3]]).
>
> *Model:* the Kerr family; *applies:* exactly; part 3 is a statement about flat space and becomes a statement about Kerr's coordinates, not its geometry, when $M \neq 0$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §6.2, Remark with eqs. (6.5)–(6.6), Fig. 27, pp. 112–113 (PDF 113–114) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.4, eqs. (20.40)–(20.42), p. 373 (PDF 388) · Hartle §15.2 ("Schwarzschild when not rotating"), p. 312 (PDF 313) · checked by script (limits, pull-back of $dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$, both families of surfaces)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). At $a = 0$: $\rho^2 = r^2$, $\Delta = r^2 - 2Mr = r^2(1 - 2M/r)$, so $g_{tt} = -(1 - 2M/r)$, $g_{t\varphi} = 0$, $g_{rr} = r^2/\Delta = (1 - 2M/r)^{-1}$, $g_{\theta\theta} = r^2$, $g_{\varphi\varphi} = r^2\sin^2\theta$.
>
> **Step 2** (part 2, the metric). At $M = 0$: $g_{tt} = -1$, $g_{t\varphi} = 0$, $g_{\varphi\varphi} = (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta$, $\Delta = r^2 + a^2$, so $g_{rr} = \rho^2/(r^2 + a^2)$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 2, it is flat). With $\varpi = \sqrt{r^2 + a^2}\sin\theta$, $dx^2 + dy^2 = d\varpi^2 + \varpi^2d\varphi^2$ (polar coordinates in the plane) and
>
> $$
> d\varpi = \frac{r\sin\theta}{\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}}\,dr + \sqrt{r^2 + a^2}\cos\theta\,d\theta, \qquad dz = \cos\theta\,dr - r\sin\theta\,d\theta .
> $$
>
> Squaring and adding, the $dr\,d\theta$ terms are $2r\sin\theta\cos\theta - 2r\sin\theta\cos\theta = 0$, and
>
> $$
> d\varpi^2 + dz^2 = \frac{r^2\sin^2\theta + (r^2 + a^2)\cos^2\theta}{r^2 + a^2}\,dr^2 + \bigl((r^2 + a^2)\cos^2\theta + r^2\sin^2\theta\bigr)d\theta^2 = \frac{\rho^2}{r^2 + a^2}\,dr^2 + \rho^2d\theta^2 .
> $$
>
> With $\varpi^2d\varphi^2 = (r^2 + a^2)\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2$ this is the spatial part of Step 2.
>
> **Step 4** (part 3, the surfaces). From $\varpi = \sqrt{r^2 + a^2}\sin\theta$, $z = r\cos\theta$: $\varpi^2/(r^2 + a^2) + z^2/r^2 = \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$, and $\varpi^2/\sin^2\theta - z^2/\cos^2\theta = (r^2 + a^2) - r^2 = a^2$. An ellipse with semi-axes $\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}$ and $r$ has foci at distance $\sqrt{(r^2 + a^2) - r^2} = a$ from the centre; a hyperbola with semi-axes $a\sin\theta$, $a\cos\theta$ has foci at $\sqrt{a^2\sin^2\theta + a^2\cos^2\theta} = a$: confocal. At $r = 0$: $z = 0$ and $\varpi = a\sin\theta \in [0, a]$; $\theta$ and $\pi - \theta$ give the same $\varpi$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 3, the fold). The map $(r, \theta) \mapsto (\varpi, z)$ has Jacobian determinant $\partial_r\varpi\,\partial_\theta z - \partial_\theta\varpi\,\partial_rz = -\frac{r^2\sin^2\theta}{\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}} - \sqrt{r^2 + a^2}\cos^2\theta = -\frac{\rho^2}{\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}}$, nonzero except on the rim $\rho = 0$ ($r = 0$, $\theta = \frac\pi2$). So near an interior point of the disc it is a local diffeomorphism on a neighbourhood containing $r < 0$, and $z = r\cos\theta$ changes sign with $r$ at fixed $\theta \neq \frac\pi2$: crossing the disc is $r$ passing through $0$. Finally $\varpi(-r, \pi - \theta) = \varpi(r, \theta)$ and $z(-r, \pi - \theta) = (-r)(-\cos\theta) = z(r, \theta)$.
>
> **Step 6** (part 4). $g_{tt} = -1 + 2Mr/\rho^2$ and $r/\rho^2 = \frac1r\bigl(1 + a^2\cos^2\theta/r^2\bigr)^{-1} = \frac1r - \frac{a^2\cos^2\theta}{r^3} + \dots$ For $r < 0$, $\frac1r = -\frac1{|r|}$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The Boyer–Lindquist $r$ labels spheroids, not spheres: near the centre it is the "distance" to a disc, and only far away, where $r \gg a$, is it the ordinary radius ($R^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = r^2 + a^2\sin^2\theta$). The rim of the disc, $\rho = 0$, is where the coordinates fail even in flat space; in Kerr it is where the curvature diverges.

The coordinates are now understood. Which numbers in the metric are physical? Far away the metric must look like the field of a slowly rotating body.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C12.1.3: The Far Field: g_tφ ≈ −2Ma sin²θ/r Identifies J = aM
> 1. **Expansion.** For $r \to \infty$ at fixed $\theta$,
>
> $$
> g_{tt} = -1 + \frac{2M}{r} + O(r^{-3}), \quad g_{rr} = 1 + \frac{2M}{r} + O(r^{-2}), \quad g_{t\varphi} = -\frac{2Ma\sin^2\theta}{r} + O(r^{-3}), \quad g_{\varphi\varphi} = r^2\sin^2\theta\bigl(1 + O(r^{-2})\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 2. **Kerr–Schild form.** In the chart of [[#^thm-c12-1-1|Theorem §C12.1.1]], part 3, with $R^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$, the rotational part of $g_{\bar ti}$ is $2Ma\,(y, -x, 0)/R^3$, i.e. the cross term
>
> $$
> -\frac{4Ma}{R^3}\,(x\,dy - y\,dx)\,d\bar t \qquad \bigl(\text{plus a radial part } 4M\,x_i\,dx^i\,d\bar t/R^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 3. **Identification.** Comparing with the weak field of a body of mass $M$ and angular momentum $\vec J$ ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^ex-c8-1-3|Example §C8.1.3]]: $g_{tt} = -1 + 2M/r$, cross term $-\frac{4J\sin^2\theta}{r}dt\,d\varphi$), $M$ is the total mass (as in [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-4|Theorem §C6.2.4]]) and
>
> $$
> \vec J = aM\,\hat z, \qquad a = \frac JM \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } a = \frac{J}{Mc}\ \text{(a length)},\ \ g_{t\varphi} \approx -\frac{2GJ\sin^2\theta}{c^2r}\ \text{with } dt \text{ in seconds}\Bigr) :
> $$
>
>    $a$ is the angular momentum per unit mass, and $a > 0$ is rotation in the sense of increasing $\varphi$. The coordinate-free statement, $J = aM$ as a Komar integral, is [[§C12.4 Komar Integrals#^thm-c12-4-3|Theorem §C12.4.3]].
>
> *Model:* Kerr ([[#^thm-c12-1-1|Theorem §C12.1.1]]) read in the weak-field regime $r \gg M$, $r \gg |a|$; *applies:* the identification is exact (it fixes the meaning of the parameters); the expansions are asymptotic.
>
> *Source: Hartle §15.2, eq. (15.3), p. 312 (PDF 313) (comparison with (14.22)) · Couzens, GR2, eq. (6.3), p. 112 (PDF 113) (result quoted, Komar proof in §6.4.4) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.4, eq. (20.43), p. 374 (PDF 389) (sign and a misprint corrected, [[#^cau-c12-1-1|Caution: Slips in the sources]]) · Schutz §11.3, eq. (11.70), p. 328 (PDF 346); Exercise 8.19 · series checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Boyer–Lindquist series). With $u = 1/r$, $\rho^2 = r^2(1 + a^2\cos^2\theta\,u^2)$, so $\frac{2Mr}{\rho^2} = 2Mu(1 - a^2\cos^2\theta\,u^2 + \dots)$ and
>
> $$
> g_{t\varphi} = -\frac{2Mar\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2} = -2Ma\sin^2\theta\,u + 2Ma^3\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta\,u^3 + \dots, \qquad g_{tt} = -1 + 2Mu - 2Ma^2\cos^2\theta\,u^3 + \dots
> $$
>
> Similarly $\rho^2/\Delta = (1 + a^2\cos^2\theta\,u^2)(1 - 2Mu + a^2u^2)^{-1} = 1 + 2Mu + (4M^2 - a^2\sin^2\theta)u^2 + \dots$ and $g_{\varphi\varphi} = r^2\sin^2\theta\,(1 + a^2u^2 + 2Ma^2\sin^2\theta\,u^3)$ (all checked by script).
>
> **Step 2** (the same chart as Example §C8.1.3, to the order needed). Example §C8.1.3 uses isotropic coordinates $(t, R_{\rm i}, \Theta, \varphi)$ with $g = -(1 - 2M/R_{\rm i})dt^2 + (1 + 2M/R_{\rm i})(dR_{\rm i}^2 + R_{\rm i}^2d\Omega^2) - \frac{4J\sin^2\Theta}{R_{\rm i}}dt\,d\varphi$ to first order. Put $r = R_{\rm i} + M$: then $r^2d\Omega^2 = R_{\rm i}^2(1 + 2M/R_{\rm i} + M^2/R_{\rm i}^2)d\Omega^2$ and $(1 + 2M/r)dr^2 = (1 + 2M/R_{\rm i} + O(R_{\rm i}^{-2}))dR_{\rm i}^2$, so the Schwarzschild-type terms of Step 1 agree to first order. The angles differ by $\cos\Theta = z/R = r\cos\theta/\sqrt{r^2 + a^2\sin^2\theta} = \cos\theta\,(1 + O(a^2/r^2))$. Both changes alter $g_{t\varphi}$ only by relative corrections $O(M/r)$ and $O(a^2/r^2)$, so the leading terms $-2Ma\sin^2\theta/r$ and $-2J\sin^2\Theta/R_{\rm i}$ must agree:
>
> $$
> -2Ma\sin^2\theta = -2J\sin^2\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad J = aM .
> $$
>
> The direction: in Example §C8.1.3, $h_{0x} = 2Jy/r^3$, $h_{0y} = -2Jx/r^3$ for $\vec J$ along $+\hat z$.
>
> **Step 3** (Kerr–Schild series, part 2). From $g_{\bar ti} = 2H\,l_{\bar t}l_i = 2H\,l_i$ with $2H = 2Mr/\rho^2 = 2M/R + O(R^{-3})$ and $r = R - \frac{a^2\sin^2\theta}{2R} + \dots$,
>
> $$
> g_{\bar tx} = \frac{2M}{R}\cdot\frac{rx + ay}{r^2 + a^2} = \frac{2Mx}{R^2} + \frac{2May}{R^3} + O(R^{-3}), \qquad g_{\bar ty} = \frac{2My}{R^2} - \frac{2Max}{R^3} + O(R^{-3})
> $$
>
> (checked by script). The cross term $2g_{\bar tx}dx\,d\bar t + 2g_{\bar ty}dy\,d\bar t$ is $\frac{4M}{R^2}(x\,dx + y\,dy)d\bar t - \frac{4Ma}{R^3}(x\,dy - y\,dx)d\bar t$, and with $x\,dy - y\,dx = \varpi^2d\varphi \approx R^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi$ the second term is $-\frac{4Ma\sin^2\theta}{R}d\bar t\,d\varphi$: part 1 again, now in a chart that is Cartesian at infinity. The radial term comes from $\bar t \neq t$ and carries no rotation.
>
> **Step 4** (restoring units). $M \to GM/c^2$, $a \to J/(Mc)$, $x^0 = ct$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]): $Ma \to GJ/c^3$, and $-\frac{4Ma\sin^2\theta}{r}\,c\,dt\,d\varphi \to -\frac{4GJ\sin^2\theta}{c^2r}dt\,d\varphi$, as in Example §C8.1.3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The two parameters of the exact solution are read off the far field, as Newton's mass is read off $g_{tt}$: $M$ from the $1/r$ term of $g_{tt}$, $J = aM$ from the $1/r$ term of $g_{t\varphi}$. The falling cross term is what the rotated Schwarzschild metric of the opening lacked.

## Symmetries: stationary but not static

The rotated Schwarzschild metric also had a $dt\,d\varphi$ term, yet it was static: a different time coordinate removes the cross term. For Kerr no choice of Killing time does, and this is the coordinate-free sense in which the hole rotates.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C12.1.4: Killing Vectors ∂_t and ∂_φ; ∂_t Is Not Hypersurface-Orthogonal, so Kerr Is Stationary but Not Static
> For the Kerr metric of [[#^thm-c12-1-1|Theorem §C12.1.1]]:
> 1. **Killing vectors.** $K = \partial_t$ and $R = \partial_\varphi$ are Killing vectors and commute. $R$ has closed orbits of parameter length $2\pi$ and $g(R, R) = g_{\varphi\varphi} \to 0$ on the axis $\theta \to 0, \pi$. $K$ is timelike wherever $r > 2M$ (at every $\theta$), with $g(K, K) \to -1$ at infinity. So the region $r > 2M$ is stationary and axisymmetric ([[#^def-c12-1-1|Def. §C12.1.1]]).
> 2. **Discrete symmetries.** The metric is invariant under $(t, \varphi) \mapsto (-t, -\varphi)$ and under $\theta \mapsto \pi - \theta$. The reversal $t \mapsto -t$ alone maps the Kerr metric with parameter $a$ to the one with $-a$: reversing time reverses the spin.
> 3. **No hypersurface-orthogonal Killing vector.** Let $a \neq 0$. For no constants $(\alpha, \beta)$ with $\alpha \neq 0$ is $\alpha\partial_t + \beta\partial_\varphi$ hypersurface-orthogonal ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-3|Def. §C3.13.3]], part 2) near any point of the region where $\Delta \neq 0$, $\rho \neq 0$, $0 < \theta < \pi$. In particular $\partial_t$ is not.
> 4. **Not static.** Of the combinations $\partial_t + c\,\partial_\varphi$, only $c = 0$ is timelike near infinity. Since $\partial_t$ and $\partial_\varphi$ span all Killing vectors of Kerr (d'Inverno & Vickers, stated), Kerr with $a \neq 0$ is stationary but has no timelike, hypersurface-orthogonal Killing vector near infinity: it is **not static**.
>
> *Model:* Kerr, $a \neq 0$ in parts 3–4; *applies:* exactly. The region where $K$ is timelike extends inside $r = 2M$ away from the equator; its exact boundary, the stationary limit $g_{tt} = 0$, is §C12.5, [[§C12.5 The Ergosphere and the Penrose Process#^def-c12-5-1|Def. §C12.5.1]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §6.2, p. 113 (PDF 114) ($K = \partial_t$, $R = \partial_\phi$; "$K$ is not orthogonal to $t = \text{constant}$ hypersurfaces", stated) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.4, eq. (20.36), p. 372 (PDF 387) ("the only continuous symmetries"; $t \to -t$ with $\phi \to -\phi$, and with $a \to -a$) · Hartle §15.2, eqs. (15.4)–(15.5), p. 312 (PDF 313); Problem 15.2 · checked by script*

^thm-c12-1-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). No component of the metric depends on $t$ or $\varphi$, so $\partial_t$ and $\partial_\varphi$ are Killing ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-1|Theorem §C3.13.1]], part 3). Coordinate fields commute, $[\partial_t, \partial_\varphi] = 0$. The flow of $\partial_\varphi$ is $\varphi \mapsto \varphi + s$, periodic with period $2\pi$; $g_{\varphi\varphi} = \bigl(r^2 + a^2 + \frac{2Ma^2r\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}\bigr)\sin^2\theta \to 0$ as $\sin\theta \to 0$. For $K$: $g(K, K) = g_{tt} = -1 + 2Mr/\rho^2$, and $\rho^2 \ge r^2$ gives $2Mr/\rho^2 \le 2M/r < 1$ for $r > 2M$, so $g_{tt} < 0$ there; $g_{tt} \to -1$ by [[#^thm-c12-1-3|Theorem §C12.1.3]].
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). Under $(t, \varphi) \mapsto (-t, -\varphi)$ the products $dt^2$, $d\varphi^2$ and $dt\,d\varphi$ are unchanged. Under $t \mapsto -t$ alone only $dt\,d\varphi$ changes sign, and its coefficient $-4Mar\sin^2\theta/\rho^2$ is odd in $a$, while all others are even in $a$. Under $\theta \mapsto \pi - \theta$, $\sin^2\theta$, $\cos^2\theta$ and $d\theta^2$ are unchanged.
>
> **Step 3** (lowering $K$). Let $K = \alpha\partial_t + \beta\partial_\varphi$, $\alpha \neq 0$. Its lowered components are $K_\mu = g_{\mu\nu}K^\nu$:
>
> $$
> K_t = \alpha g_{tt} + \beta g_{t\varphi} \equiv A(r, \theta), \qquad K_\varphi = \alpha g_{t\varphi} + \beta g_{\varphi\varphi} \equiv B(r, \theta), \qquad K_r = K_\theta = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (what orthogonality would require). Suppose $K_\mu = \lambda\,\partial_\mu f$ on an open set $U$, with $\lambda \neq 0$. The $r$ and $\theta$ components give $\partial_rf = \partial_\theta f = 0$, so $f = f(t, \varphi)$ on $U$ (shrinking $U$ to a coordinate box). Where $A \neq 0$ also $\partial_tf = A/\lambda \neq 0$ and
>
> $$
> \frac{B(r, \theta)}{A(r, \theta)} = \frac{\partial_\varphi f(t, \varphi)}{\partial_tf(t, \varphi)} .
> $$
>
> The left side depends only on $(r, \theta)$, the right side only on $(t, \varphi)$; differentiating with respect to $r$, $\theta$, $t$, $\varphi$ shows that both are a constant $\kappa_0$ on $U$. So $B - \kappa_0A = 0$ on an open set of $(r, \theta)$.
>
> **Step 5** (it is not). Multiply by $\rho^2$, divide by $\alpha$, put $c = \beta/\alpha$ and $u = \cos\theta$ (a local coordinate for $0 < \theta < \pi$), $\sin^2\theta = 1 - u^2$, $\rho^2 = r^2 + a^2u^2$:
>
> $$
> \rho^2(B - \kappa_0A)/\alpha = (1 - u^2)\bigl[-2Mar + c\bigl((r^2 + a^2)(r^2 + a^2u^2) + 2Ma^2r(1 - u^2)\bigr)\bigr] + \kappa_0\bigl[r^2 + a^2u^2 - 2Mr + 2cMar(1 - u^2)\bigr] ,
> $$
>
> a polynomial in $(r, u)$. A polynomial that vanishes on an open set is the zero polynomial, so every coefficient vanishes. The only terms of degree $4$ in $r$ come from $c(1 - u^2)(r^2 + a^2)(r^2 + a^2u^2)$ and are $c(1 - u^2)r^4$; the coefficient of $r^4u^0$ is $c$, so $c = 0$. Then the only term in $r^2$ is $\kappa_0r^2$, so $\kappa_0 = 0$. What remains is $-2Mar(1 - u^2)$, whose coefficient $-2Ma$ is nonzero for $M > 0$, $a \neq 0$. Contradiction: $K$ is not hypersurface-orthogonal on any open set. The polynomial $\rho^2A/\alpha = -(r^2 + a^2u^2 - 2Mr) - 2cMar(1 - u^2)$ is not zero, so every open set contains points where $A \neq 0$, as used in Step 4. Part 3 is proved.
>
> **Step 6** (part 4). $g(\partial_t + c\partial_\varphi, \partial_t + c\partial_\varphi) = g_{tt} + 2c\,g_{t\varphi} + c^2g_{\varphi\varphi} = -1 + O(1/r) + c^2r^2\sin^2\theta\,(1 + O(r^{-2}))$, which is positive for large $r$ off the axis unless $c = 0$: the "light cylinder" of the opening. A timelike Killing vector near infinity is therefore $\alpha\partial_t$, which by part 3 is not hypersurface-orthogonal.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Hypersurface-orthogonality would make the ratio $K_\varphi/K_t$ constant, and for Kerr it varies with $\theta$: it is $0$ on the axis and nonzero at the equator, at every radius. In the language of forms this is the twist $K^\flat \wedge dK^\flat \neq 0$ of Frobenius' condition ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^thm-c10-5-1|Theorem §C10.5.1]]), computed for Kerr's $\partial_t$ in [[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^ex-c10-5-5|Example §C10.5.5]], part (c); the argument here needs only the definition.

The two Killing vectors give two conserved quantities along every geodesic ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-2|Theorem §C3.13.2]]), energy and axial angular momentum; their use for orbits and frame dragging is §C12.3 ([[§C12.3 Geodesics and Frame Dragging#^thm-c12-3-1|Theorem §C12.3.1]]).

## Adding charge: Kerr–Newman

Chapter C11 added charge to Schwarzschild. Adding it to Kerr completes the family of stationary black holes of Einstein–Maxwell theory.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C12.1.5: The Kerr–Newman Metric
> Let $e^2 = Q^2 + P^2$ and $\Delta_e = r^2 - 2Mr + a^2 + e^2$. The **Kerr–Newman** metric and potential are
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -\frac{\Delta_e}{\rho^2}\bigl(dt - a\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi\bigr)^2 + \frac{\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}\bigl(a\,dt - (r^2 + a^2)\,d\varphi\bigr)^2 + \frac{\rho^2}{\Delta_e}\,dr^2 + \rho^2\,d\theta^2 ,
> $$
>
> $$
> A = -\frac{Qr}{\rho^2}\bigl(dt - a\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi\bigr) + \frac{P\cos\theta}{\rho^2}\bigl(a\,dt - (r^2 + a^2)\,d\varphi\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 1. **It solves Einstein–Maxwell.** With $F = dA$, the pair satisfies $G_{\mu\nu} = 2\bigl(F_{\mu\rho}F_\nu{}^\rho - \frac14g_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}F^{\rho\sigma}\bigr)$ and $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^thm-c11-1-1|Theorem §C11.1.1]] with $J = 0$) wherever $\Delta_e \neq 0$, $\rho \neq 0$ (checked by script).
> 2. **Boyer–Lindquist components.** It is the Kerr metric with $2Mr$ replaced by $2Mr - e^2$:
>
> $$
> g_{tt} = -\Bigl(1 - \frac{2Mr - e^2}{\rho^2}\Bigr), \quad g_{t\varphi} = -\frac{a(2Mr - e^2)\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}, \quad g_{\varphi\varphi} = \Bigl(r^2 + a^2 + \frac{a^2(2Mr - e^2)\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}\Bigr)\sin^2\theta, \quad g_{rr} = \frac{\rho^2}{\Delta_e} .
> $$
>
> 3. **Limits.** At $a = 0$: the Reissner–Nordström metric and potential $A = -\frac Qr\,dt - P\cos\theta\,d\varphi$ of [[§C11.2 The Reissner–Nordström Solution#^thm-c11-2-2|Theorem §C11.2.2]]. At $Q = P = 0$: Kerr ([[#^thm-c12-1-1|Theorem §C12.1.1]]). At $M = 0$, $e \neq 0$ it is not flat.
> 4. **Field strength** (for $P = 0$; the $P$ terms follow by duality, [[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^thm-c11-1-4|Theorem §C11.1.4]]):
>
> $$
> F_{tr} = -\frac{Q(r^2 - a^2\cos^2\theta)}{\rho^4}, \quad F_{t\theta} = \frac{2Qa^2r\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\rho^4}, \quad F_{r\varphi} = -\frac{Qa\sin^2\theta\,(r^2 - a^2\cos^2\theta)}{\rho^4}, \quad F_{\theta\varphi} = \frac{2Qar(r^2 + a^2)\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\rho^4} .
> $$
>
> 5. **Symmetries and horizons.** $\partial_t$, $\partial_\varphi$ are Killing and leave $A$ invariant (no component depends on $t$, $\varphi$). $\Delta_e = 0$ at $r_\pm = M \pm \sqrt{M^2 - a^2 - e^2}$, which exist for $a^2 + e^2 \le M^2$; their meaning is as for Kerr (§C12.2).
>
> Restored: $e^2 \to G(Q^2 + P^2)/(4\pi\varepsilon_0c^4)$ as in [[§C11.2 The Reissner–Nordström Solution#^thm-c11-2-2|Theorem §C11.2.2]].
>
> *Model:* electrovacuum ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^mod-c11-1-2|Model §C11.1.2]]), $\Lambda = 0$, stationary and axisymmetric; *applies:* exactly, where the chart is regular. Astrophysical holes have $e^2 \ll M^2$ ([[§C11.2 The Reissner–Nordström Solution#^ex-c11-2-1|Example §C11.2.1]]).

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> The field equations are checked by script at three points with $Q$ and $P$ both nonzero, outside, between and inside the zeros of $\Delta_e$ (largest residual $9\times10^{-29}$ in $G_{\mu\nu} - 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$, $3\times10^{-29}$ in $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu}$). The algebra is written out.
>
> **Step 1** (part 2). Expand the two squares. $dt^2$: $\frac{-\Delta_e + a^2\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2} = -\frac{\rho^2 - (2Mr - e^2)}{\rho^2}$, since $\Delta_e - a^2\sin^2\theta = r^2 + a^2\cos^2\theta - 2Mr + e^2 = \rho^2 - (2Mr - e^2)$. $dt\,d\varphi$ (half the coefficient): $\frac{a\sin^2\theta\,\Delta_e - a\sin^2\theta\,(r^2 + a^2)}{\rho^2} = -\frac{a(2Mr - e^2)\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}$, since $r^2 + a^2 - \Delta_e = 2Mr - e^2$. $d\varphi^2$: $\frac{\sin^2\theta\,[(r^2 + a^2)^2 - a^2\sin^2\theta\,\Delta_e]}{\rho^2}$, and $(r^2 + a^2)^2 - a^2\sin^2\theta\,(r^2 + a^2) = (r^2 + a^2)\rho^2$, so this is $\sin^2\theta\,\bigl[r^2 + a^2 + a^2\sin^2\theta\,(2Mr - e^2)/\rho^2\bigr]$. At $e = 0$ these are the components of Theorem §C12.1.1.
>
> **Step 2** (part 3). At $a = 0$: $\rho^2 = r^2$, $\Delta_e = r^2f$ with $f = 1 - 2M/r + e^2/r^2$, so $ds^2 = -f\,dt^2 + \frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}r^4d\varphi^2 + f^{-1}dr^2 + r^2d\theta^2$; and $A = -\frac Qr\,dt + \frac{P\cos\theta}{r^2}(-r^2d\varphi) = -\frac Qr\,dt - P\cos\theta\,d\varphi$. At $Q = P = 0$, $\Delta_e = \Delta$ and $A = 0$. At $M = 0$, $e \neq 0$, the field $F$ is nonzero, so $T_{\mu\nu} \neq 0$ and, by part 1, $G_{\mu\nu} \neq 0$: not flat.
>
> **Step 3** (part 4). $A_t = -Qr/\rho^2$, $A_\varphi = Qar\sin^2\theta/\rho^2$, with $\partial_r(r/\rho^2) = (\rho^2 - 2r^2)/\rho^4 = -(r^2 - a^2\cos^2\theta)/\rho^4$ and $\partial_\theta(1/\rho^2) = 2a^2\sin\theta\cos\theta/\rho^4$:
>
> $$
> F_{tr} = -\partial_rA_t = Q\,\partial_r\frac{r}{\rho^2} = -\frac{Q(r^2 - a^2\cos^2\theta)}{\rho^4}, \qquad F_{t\theta} = -\partial_\theta A_t = Qr\,\frac{2a^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\rho^4} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> F_{r\varphi} = \partial_rA_\varphi = Qa\sin^2\theta\,\partial_r\frac r{\rho^2}, \qquad F_{\theta\varphi} = \partial_\theta A_\varphi = Qar\,\frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\rho^2 + 2a^2\sin^3\theta\cos\theta}{\rho^4} = \frac{2Qar(r^2 + a^2)\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\rho^4} .
> $$
>
> At $a = 0$, $F_{tr} = -Q/r^2$, the Reissner–Nordström field.
>
> **Step 4** (part 5). The Lie derivative of $A$ along a coordinate field is the derivative of its components ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-3|Theorem §C2.5.3]]), here zero. $\Delta_e = (r - M)^2 - (M^2 - a^2 - e^2)$ vanishes at $r_\pm$, real when $M^2 \ge a^2 + e^2$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Charge enters the geometry only through $e^2$, and only by $2Mr \to 2Mr - e^2$: the field's energy reduces the effective mass at small $r$ exactly as the mass function of [[§C11.2 The Reissner–Nordström Solution#^thm-c11-2-7|Theorem §C11.2.7]] does. The rotating charge also produces magnetic field components ($F_{r\varphi}$, $F_{\theta\varphi}$) proportional to $Qa$: the next theorem reads them off.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C12.1.6: ⚑ The Kerr–Newman Field far Away: Charge Q and a Magnetic Dipole μ = Qa, Gyromagnetic Ratio 2
> For the Kerr–Newman potential of [[#^thm-c12-1-5|Theorem §C12.1.5]], as $r \to \infty$,
>
> $$
> A_t = -\frac Qr + \frac{Pa\cos\theta}{r^2} + O(r^{-3}), \qquad A_\varphi = -P\cos\theta + \frac{Qa\sin^2\theta}{r} + O(r^{-2}) .
> $$
>
> 1. The hole carries electric charge $Q$ and magnetic charge $P$ (the $1/r$ term of $A_t$ and the monopole term of $A_\varphi$, as for Reissner–Nordström, [[§C11.5 Charges in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c11-5-4|Theorem §C11.5.4]]).
> 2. It has a **magnetic dipole moment** $\vec\mu = Qa\,\hat z$, along the spin, since a flat-space dipole $\vec A = \vec\mu\times\vec x/R^3$ has $A_\varphi = \mu\sin^2\theta/R$ (Gaussian units). For $P \neq 0$ it also has an electric dipole moment $-Pa\,\hat z$.
> 3. Its **gyromagnetic ratio** $g$, defined by $\vec\mu = g\,\frac{Q}{2M}\vec J$, is
>
> $$
> g = \frac{2M\mu}{QJ} = \frac{2M\cdot Qa}{Q\cdot Ma} = 2 \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \mu = \frac{QJ}{M}\ \text{in A m}^2\text{, } Q \text{ in coulombs}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
>    twice the value $g = 1$ of a classical body whose charge and mass are distributed alike.
>
> *Model:* Kerr–Newman far from the hole; *applies:* exactly as an asymptotic statement about the solution.
>
> *Source: not stated in the sources; derived here from the potential of Couzens' (6.1) (sign of $P$ as in C11) · series and the flat-space dipole checked by script*

^thm-c12-1-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (series). With $\frac{r}{\rho^2} = \frac1r - \frac{a^2\cos^2\theta}{r^3} + \dots$ and $\frac1{\rho^2} = \frac1{r^2} + O(r^{-4})$:
>
> $$
> A_t = -\frac{Qr}{\rho^2} + \frac{Pa\cos\theta}{\rho^2} = -\frac Qr + \frac{Pa\cos\theta}{r^2} + O(r^{-3}), \qquad A_\varphi = \frac{Qar\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2} - \frac{P\cos\theta\,(r^2 + a^2)}{\rho^2} = \frac{Qa\sin^2\theta}{r} - P\cos\theta + O(r^{-2}) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (flat-space dipole). For $\vec A = \mu\,\hat z\times\vec x/R^3 = \mu(-y, x, 0)/R^3$ the covariant $\varphi$ component is $A_\varphi = \vec A\cdot\partial_\varphi\vec x = \mu(-y, x, 0)\cdot(-y, x, 0)/R^3 = \mu(x^2 + y^2)/R^3 = \mu\sin^2\theta/R$. Matching with Step 1 at large $r$ ($r \approx R$, [[#^thm-c12-1-3|Theorem §C12.1.3]], Step 2): $\mu = Qa$. Likewise $A_t = -V$ with $V = \vec p\cdot\vec x/R^3 = p\cos\theta/R^2$ for an electric dipole gives $p = -Pa$.
>
> **Step 3** (classical $g$). For charge density $\rho_e$ and mass density $\rho_m$ moving with velocity $\vec v$, $\vec\mu = \frac12\int\vec x\times\rho_e\vec v\,d^3x$ and $\vec J = \int\vec x\times\rho_m\vec v\,d^3x$. If $\rho_e/\rho_m = Q/M$ everywhere, $\vec\mu = \frac{Q}{2M}\vec J$: $g = 1$. Kerr–Newman has $\mu = Qa = \frac QM J$: $g = 2$.
>
> **Step 4** (units). In SI the definition reads $\vec\mu = g\frac{Q}{2M}\vec J$ with $Q$ in C, $M$ in kg, $\vec J$ in kg m²/s; with $g = 2$, $\mu = QJ/M$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** A spinning charged hole is a magnetic dipole whose strength is fixed by its charge and spin alone, with no freedom left for how the charge is distributed: another face of uniqueness.

> [!remark] Remark: Uniqueness and "no hair"
> The Kerr–Newman family is all there is. Couzens states (Theorem 5, Carter 1971 and Robinson 1975) that a stationary, axisymmetric, asymptotically flat vacuum spacetime that is suitably regular on and outside a connected event horizon is a member of the Kerr family, fixed by $M$ and $J$, and that the statement extends to Kerr–Newman with $M$, $J$, $Q$, $P$ in Einstein–Maxwell theory. Axial symmetry need not be assumed separately for a rotating hole: Hawking's rigidity theorem makes the horizon of a stationary black hole a Killing horizon and supplies the second Killing vector ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^thm-c10-7-4|Theorem §C10.7.4]]; Schutz names Hawking, Carter and Robinson). Wheeler's phrase "a black hole has no hair" (d'Inverno & Vickers, Fig. 20.10) summarises the consequence: whatever collapses, once the hole has settled only $M$, $J$ and the charges remain, everything else being radiated away or swallowed (Schutz §11.3, items (3)–(4); numerical mergers settle to Kerr). Since astrophysical holes are nearly neutral, Kerr itself is the end state of collapse (Hartle §15.1). Two qualifications: the theorems hold for the stationary end state, not during collapse, and only for vacuum or electrovacuum outside the hole (Schutz, item (2): disks, other fields and binary companions distort the metric).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §6.2.1, Theorem 5, pp. 117–118 (PDF 118–119) · Schutz §11.3, items (1)–(4), pp. 325–326 (PDF 343–344) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §20.11, pp. 386–387 (PDF 401–402) · Hartle §15.1, p. 311 (PDF 312)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C12.1.1: Rotating Schwarzschild Is Not Kerr
> *Question.* The opening rotated the Schwarzschild coordinates, $\varphi = \varphi' + \Omega t$. Is the result a rotating black hole? Test it against the coordinate-free criteria of this section.
>
> *Step 1* (the metric). With $d\varphi = d\varphi' + \Omega\,dt$ in $r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2$, $r^2\sin^2\theta\,(d\varphi'^2 + 2\Omega\,dt\,d\varphi' + \Omega^2dt^2)$, so
>
> $$
> g_{t't'} = -\Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr) + \Omega^2r^2\sin^2\theta, \qquad g_{t'\varphi'} = \Omega r^2\sin^2\theta, \qquad g_{\varphi'\varphi'} = r^2\sin^2\theta ,
> $$
>
> with $g_{rr}$ and $g_{\theta\theta}$ unchanged ($t' = t$; checked by script).
>
> *Step 2* (still vacuum). The Ricci tensor is a tensor; it vanishes in the old chart ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]), so it vanishes in the new one (script: $|R_{\mu\nu}| < 10^{-31}$ at a sample point).
>
> *Step 3* (the light cylinder). On the equator $g_{t't'} = 0$ where $\Omega^2r^2 = 1 - 2M/r$. For $\Omega = 0.05/M$ the root is $r = 18.91M$; for $\Omega = 0.01/M$ it is $r = 98.98M$, close to $1/\Omega - M$ (by script). Beyond it the Killing vector $\partial_{t'} = \partial_t + \Omega\,\partial_\varphi$ is spacelike: an observer at fixed $(r, \theta, \varphi')$ would move, roughly, with speed $\Omega r\sin\theta > 1$ relative to the static frame. This is a property of the chart, not of spacetime: the static Killing vector $\partial_t = \partial_{t'} - \Omega\,\partial_{\varphi'}$ is still there.
>
> *Step 4* (the far field). $g_{t'\varphi'} = \Omega r^2\sin^2\theta$ grows; the cross term of a rotating source falls, $g_{t\varphi} = -2J\sin^2\theta/r$ ([[#^thm-c12-1-3|Theorem §C12.1.3]]). No value of $J$ matches. And the spacetime is static: $\partial_t$ (old chart) is a timelike, hypersurface-orthogonal Killing vector for $r > 2M$, the case [[#^thm-c12-1-4|Theorem §C12.1.4]] excludes for Kerr.
>
> *Step 5* (invariants). The Kretschmann scalar is Schwarzschild's, $48M^2/r^6$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-7|Theorem §C6.2.7]]), independent of $\theta$ (script: $3.072\times10^{-3}/M^4$ at $r = 5M$ for $\theta = 0.3$ and $1.4$). For Kerr with $a = 0.9M$ at $r = 5M$ it is $3.072\times10^{-3}/M^4$ on the equator but $1.344\times10^{-3}/M^4$ near the axis ($\theta = 10^{-3}$), in agreement with Couzens' formula (6.13), [[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-4|Theorem §C12.2.4]]: Kerr's curvature knows about the axis, the rotated metric's does not.
>
> *Step 6* (angular momentum). The axial Killing vector of the new chart is $\partial_{\varphi'} = \partial_\varphi$ (the Jacobian leaves it unchanged), so any angular momentum built from it, such as the Komar integral ([[§C12.4 Komar Integrals#^def-c12-4-4|Def. §C12.4.4]]), is the Schwarzschild value, zero.
>
> ![[ph-rel-c12-1-2.svg]]
> *Equatorial plane, $M = 1$. Left: $-g_{tt}$ for Schwarzschild in coordinates rotating with $\Omega = 0.05/M$ (red), which changes sign again at the light cylinder $r = 18.91M$, and for Kerr (blue), whose equatorial $g_{tt} = -(1 - 2M/r)$ does not depend on $a$. Right, log–log: $|g_{t\varphi}|$, growing as $\Omega r^2$ for the rotated chart and falling as $2Ma/r$ for Kerr with $a = 0.9M$, which merges with the weak field $2J/r$ of Example §C8.1.3 for $J = aM$. Computed from the formulas of the example. (Not from the sources; figure added, vault only.)*
>
> *Answer.* No. Rotating the chart produces a cross term that grows, a static Killing vector, Schwarzschild's invariants and zero angular momentum. A rotating source is detected by a cross term that falls like $1/r$, by the absence of any hypersurface-orthogonal Killing time, and by curvature that depends on the angle from the axis. Kerr has all three.
>

(20.37)–(20.39), Fig. 20.2, p. 373 (PDF 388) (flat space in a rotating frame, "merely suggestive of rotation") · Couzens, GR2, §6 introduction, p. 111 (PDF 112) (time reversal reverses rotation) · worked here; metric, Ricci tensor, light-cylinder radii, Killing fields and Kretschmann values checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C12.1.2: How Fast Do the Sun, a Pulsar and Real Black Holes Spin?
> *Question.* A Kerr black hole needs $|a| \le M$, i.e. $|J| \le M^2$, to have a horizon (§C12.2, [[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-1|Theorem §C12.2.1]]). Compare $a/M = J/M^2$ ($= cJ/GM^2$) for ordinary rotating bodies (Hartle, Problem 15.1; Schutz, Exercise 11.38).
>
> *Method.* Model each body as a uniform ball, $J = \frac25MR^2\Omega$; then $a = J/(Mc) = \frac25R^2\Omega/c$ and $M_{\rm geo} = GM/c^2$. Real stars are centrally condensed, which lowers $I$ below $\frac25MR^2$; the numbers are upper estimates.
>
> *The Sun* (Schutz's data: period $25\ \mathrm d$, $M = 2\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $R = 7\times10^8\ \mathrm m$). $\Omega = 2\pi/(25\times86400\ \mathrm s) = 2.91\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $J = \frac25(2\times10^{30})(7\times10^8)^2(2.91\times10^{-6}) = 1.14\times10^{42}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$, $a = J/(Mc) = 1.90\ \mathrm{km} = 1.90\times10^5\ \mathrm{cm}$, $M_{\rm geo} = 1.485\ \mathrm{km}$:
>
> $$
> a_\odot/M_\odot = 1.28 .
> $$
>
> *The Earth* (uniform ball, $R = 6.3781\times10^6\ \mathrm m$, sidereal day $86164\ \mathrm s$). $J = 7.09\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$, $a = 3.96\ \mathrm m$ against $M_{\rm geo} = 4.435\ \mathrm{mm}$: $a/M = 892$.
>
> *A millisecond pulsar.* The fastest known spins at $716\ \mathrm{Hz}$ (PSR J1748−2446ad, Schutz §10.7). For $1.4M_\odot$ ($M_{\rm geo} = 2067\ \mathrm m$) and $R = 10\ \mathrm{km}$: $a = \frac25(10^4)^2(2\pi\cdot716)/c = 600\ \mathrm m$, $a/M = 0.29$; for $R = 12\ \mathrm{km}$, $a/M = 0.42$. At the Newtonian break-up spin $\Omega^2R = M/R^2$ a uniform ball has $a/M = \frac25\sqrt{R/M}$: $1.03$ for $M/R = 0.15$, $0.89$ for $M/R = 0.2$.
>
> *The electron* ($J = \hbar/2$, mass $m_e$): $a = \hbar/(2m_ec) = 1.93\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm m$, $M_{\rm geo} = Gm_e/c^2 = 6.77\times10^{-58}\ \mathrm m$, $a/M = 2.9\times10^{44}$ (Schutz 11.38(d); charge neglected).
>
> *Black holes.* The typed sources give no measured spin of a particular hole. Hartle (2003) states that accretion spins a hole up towards $a = M$ and that the accretion of hot radiating matter limits it to about $a = 0.998M$; his Fig. 11.8 (an X-ray line from MCG-6-30-15, 1994) could not yet tell a rotating from a non-rotating hole. Schutz (2022) reports that black-hole mergers observed in gravitational waves are consistent with a single Kerr remnant, without quoting spins.
>
> *Answer.* Ordinary stars and planets have $a > M$: the Sun at $1.3$, the Earth at about $900$. They are not black holes and need no horizon, but a star that collapsed whole would have too much angular momentum to form one; it must first shed angular momentum (Schutz). Neutron stars, compact and spinning near break-up, sit at $a/M \approx 0.3$–$1$, the range of Kerr holes, and accretion pushes holes towards $a/M \approx 1$. So the generic astrophysical black hole is a rapidly rotating Kerr hole, not Schwarzschild.
