What action does a free particle have in Minkowski spacetime, and what follows from it? Level A defined the relativistic momentum $\gamma_u m\vec u$ so that it would be conserved in collisions ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^rem-a3-1-2|Remark: A definition built to be conserved]]), obtained the energy $\gamma_u mc^2$ from the work–energy theorem ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]) and found the relation $E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$ by direct computation ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]]). Here the Lagrangian mechanics of Classical Mechanics runs on the geometry of Chapters B1–B2: the requirements that the action be a Lorentz scalar, depend only on the worldline and reduce to Newton at low speed fix it as $S = -mc\int d\tau$. Momentum and energy then come out as the Noether charges of spacetime translations, the four of them forming the four-momentum ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^def-b2-1-3|Def. §B2.1.3]]), and $E = \sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}$ becomes the Hamiltonian. Notation as level A: $\vec u = d\vec x/dt$ is the particle's three-velocity with components $u_x, u_y, u_z$ and $\gamma_u = (1 - u^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$; $u^\mu = dx^\mu/d\tau = \gamma_u(c, \vec u)$ is its four-velocity, metric $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1)$ ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-3|Def. §B1.1.3]]).

## The action of a free particle

> [!model] Model §B3.1.1: Relativistic Point Particle
> A **point particle** of mass $m > 0$ is described by a worldline $x^\mu(\lambda)$, a curve in spacetime with an arbitrary increasing parameter $\lambda$, whose tangent $\dot x^\mu = dx^\mu/d\lambda$ is timelike and future-pointing, $\dot x\cdot\dot x = \eta_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu > 0$, $\dot x^0 > 0$. The particle has no size, no internal structure and no spin; its mass is a Lorentz scalar. It carries an ideal clock ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^mod-a2-5-1|Model §A2.5.1]]) that reads the proper time
>
> $$
> c\,d\tau = \sqrt{dx\cdot dx} = \sqrt{\dot x\cdot\dot x}\;d\lambda, \qquad d\tau = \frac{dt}{\gamma_u}
> $$
>
> along the worldline ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^def-b1-3-2|Def. §B1.3.2]]). **Free** means: no fields act on it and no event or direction of spacetime is singled out.
>
> *Assumptions: classical (no quantum effects), structureless, speed below $c$; inertial coordinates of special relativity.*
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.1, §3.2 · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Four-Momentum" (Correct Definition) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.2.1*

^mod-b3-1-1

> [!principle] Principle §B3.1.2: Relativistic Action Principle
> Between two fixed events $x_A$ and $x_B$, a particle moves along a worldline that makes its action stationary,
>
> $$
> \delta S = 0 \qquad \text{for all variations } \delta x^\mu(\lambda) \text{ with } \delta x^\mu = 0 \text{ at } x_A, x_B ,
> $$
>
> where $S[x]$ is a **Lorentz scalar** that depends on the worldline as a set of events, not on how it is parametrized (**reparametrization invariance**: replacing $\lambda$ by any increasing function of a new parameter leaves $S$ unchanged). In an inertial frame, parametrizing by the coordinate time $t$ writes the action as $S = \int L\,dt$, and the principle becomes Hamilton's principle ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^pr-b6-2-1|CM Principle §B6.2.1]]) with Lagrangian $L$.
>
> *Domain: classical particles in special relativity, in inertial frames; the action is local, built from the worldline, its first derivative and the fields at the particle. A scalar action gives equations of motion of the same form in every inertial frame, so the principle implements the covariance principle ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^pr-b2-2-9|Principle §B2.2.9]]) and with it the first postulate ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-1|Principle §A1.3.1]]). The layer is that of Hamilton's principle in Classical Mechanics; what is new is the requirement that $S$ be a scalar.*
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.1 · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Derivation from Hamilton's Principle" · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §1.1.2, §7.4.1*

^pr-b3-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.3: The Free-Particle Action
> Let the action of a free particle be $S = \int F(x, \dot x)\,d\lambda$, depending on the worldline and its first derivative only, and require (i) that $S$ be a Lorentz scalar, unchanged also by spacetime translations (nothing is singled out), (ii) that it be reparametrization invariant, and (iii) that for $u \ll c$ the Lagrangian reduce, up to an additive constant, to the Newtonian kinetic energy $\tfrac12 mu^2$. Then
>
> $$
> S = -mc\int d\tau = -mc\int\sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}\;d\lambda, \qquad L(\vec u) = -mc^2\sqrt{1 - \frac{u^2}{c^2}} = -\frac{mc^2}{\gamma_u} .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.1, eqs. (particle action), (L expansion) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.4.3*

^thm-b3-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Translations and Lorentz transformations.* Invariance under translations $x \to x + a$ forbids explicit dependence on $x$, so $F = F(\dot x)$, and Lorentz invariance requires $F(\Lambda\dot x) = F(\dot x)$ for every Lorentz transformation $\Lambda$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]). Any future timelike $\dot x$ is carried by a boost ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]) with velocity $c\,\dot{\vec x}/\dot x^0$ (of speed below $c$, since $\dot x$ is timelike) to $(\sqrt{\dot x\cdot\dot x}, \vec 0)$. So two future timelike vectors of equal norm are related by a Lorentz transformation, and $F(\dot x) = f(\dot x\cdot\dot x)$ for a function $f$ of one variable.
>
> *Reparametrization.* Let $\lambda = \lambda(\sigma)$ be increasing and $\tilde x(\sigma) = x(\lambda(\sigma))$. Then $d\tilde x/d\sigma = \lambda'(\sigma)\,\dot x$, and with $d\sigma = d\lambda/\lambda'$,
>
> $$
> \int f\Bigl(\frac{d\tilde x}{d\sigma}\cdot\frac{d\tilde x}{d\sigma}\Bigr)d\sigma = \int f\bigl(\lambda'^2\,\dot x\cdot\dot x\bigr)\,\frac{d\lambda}{\lambda'} .
> $$
>
> This must equal $\int f(\dot x\cdot\dot x)\,d\lambda$ for every worldline and every reparametrization. Take the linear reparametrization $\lambda = a\sigma$ ($\lambda' = a > 0$ constant) of a straight worldline with $\dot x\cdot\dot x = s$ constant: over the same stretch $\Delta\lambda$ the two integrals are $f(a^2s)\,\Delta\lambda/a$ and $f(s)\,\Delta\lambda$, so $f(a^2s) = a\,f(s)$ for all $a > 0$, $s > 0$; with $s = 1$ and $a = \sqrt{s'}$, $f(s') = f(1)\sqrt{s'}$. Hence $S = k\int\sqrt{\dot x\cdot\dot x}\,d\lambda = kc\int d\tau$ for a constant $k$.
>
> *Normalization.* Parametrize by $t$: $\dot x^\mu = (c, \vec u)$, $\sqrt{\dot x\cdot\dot x} = c\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}$, and Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) gives
>
> $$
> L = kc\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2} = kc - \frac{k}{2c}\,u^2 - \frac{k}{8c^3}\,u^4 - \cdots .
> $$
>
> The velocity-dependent leading term is $\tfrac12 mu^2$ exactly when $k = -mc$. The minus sign is needed because $\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}$ decreases with speed. With $k$ fixed nothing is left free; in particular the constant term is $-mc^2$.

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^mod-b3-1-1|Model §B3.1.1]], [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^pr-b3-1-2|Principle §B3.1.2]], [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]], [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why the action is forced, and what was assumed
> - The worldline alone offers only one scalar built from first derivatives that survives reparametrization: its Minkowski arc length, which is the proper time. A free particle cannot couple to anything else, so its action must be proper time times a constant, and the Newtonian limit fixes the constant. Phys505 reaches the same Lagrangian from the other end: since $S = \int L\,dt = \int\gamma_u L\,d\tau$ and $d\tau$ is a scalar, $\gamma_u L$ must be a scalar, and the only scalars a free point particle offers are constants ($m$ and $u\cdot u = c^2$); so $L = -\alpha/\gamma_u$, and matching $\partial L/\partial\vec u$ with the known momentum gives $\alpha = mc^2$ (Phys505 §7.4.3).
> - *First derivatives only* is an assumption, the standing one of Lagrangian mechanics ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-4|CM Def. §B6.1.4]]). Scalars with higher derivatives exist, such as the magnitude of the four-acceleration ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^def-b2-1-2|Def. §B2.1.2]]), but an action containing them gives equations of motion of higher than second order (the series states this without proof).
> - At $m = 0$ the action vanishes identically, since $d\tau = 0$ along a light-like worldline. Massless particles are described by the mass-shell relation $E = pc$ alone ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]); a proper action for them belongs to quantum field theory.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.1, §4.3 (mass shell, massless limit) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.4.1, §7.4.3*

^rem-b3-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Stationary action is maximal proper time
> Since $S = -mc^2\,\Delta\tau$, the action is stationary exactly when the proper time between the two events is. Among all timelike worldlines between two events, the straight one has the **largest** proper time ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^thm-a2-5-2|Theorem §A2.5.2]]; [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]]), so the action of the free particle is not just stationary but minimal on the true path. A free particle moves so that its own clock runs the longest: inertial motion is the stay-at-home twin's path ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^rem-a1-5-5|§A1.5, Remark: Proper time and the twins, from the interval]]).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.1 (Remark: Stationary action is stationary proper time)*

^rem-b3-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: The Lagrangian is not a scalar
> $S$ and $d\tau$ are Lorentz scalars; $L$ and $dt$ are not. Under a boost $dt$ changes, so $L = -mc^2/\gamma_u$ takes different values in different frames for the same instant of the same motion; the invariant combination is $\gamma_u L = -mc^2$ (Phys505 §7.4.1). The Lagrangian formalism of Classical Mechanics works in each inertial frame separately, and covariance is a property of the action, not of $L$. Further, $L$ contains the constant $-mc^2$, which has no effect on the equations of motion but, as shown below, does matter for the energy.
>
> *Source: PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.4.1*

## Equations of motion

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.4: Free Motion Is Uniform
> The action of [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]] gives, in an inertial frame, the canonical momentum and equation of motion
>
> $$
> \vec p = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec u} = \gamma_u m\,\vec u, \qquad \frac{d\vec p}{dt} = \vec 0 ,
> $$
>
> so $\vec u$ is constant; and, covariantly, without choosing a frame,
>
> $$
> \frac{du^\mu}{d\tau} = 0, \qquad x^\mu(\tau) = x^\mu(0) + u^\mu\tau :
> $$
>
> the worldline is straight.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.2, eqs. (canonical momentum), (covariant EOM) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.4.3*

^thm-b3-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Lab coordinates.* $L$ depends on $\vec u$ only. With $\partial\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}/\partial u_x = -(u_x/c^2)/\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial L}{\partial u_x} = \frac{m\,u_x}{\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}} = \gamma_u m\,u_x ,
> $$
>
> and likewise for $y$, $z$. Since $\partial L/\partial\vec x = \vec 0$, Lagrange's equations ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|CM Theorem §B6.1.4]]) give $d\vec p/dt = \vec 0$. The map $\vec u \mapsto \gamma_u m\vec u$ keeps directions and $\gamma_u mu$ increases with $u$, so it is one-to-one, and $\vec p$ constant means $\vec u$ constant.
>
> *Covariant variation.* Write $S = -mc\int\sqrt{\dot x\cdot\dot x}\,d\lambda$ with an arbitrary parameter and vary $x^\mu \to x^\mu + \delta x^\mu$ with fixed end points. Since $\eta$ is symmetric, $\delta(\dot x\cdot\dot x) = 2\dot x_\nu\,\delta\dot x^\nu$, so, integrating by parts and dropping the boundary terms,
>
> $$
> \delta S = -mc\int\frac{\dot x_\nu\,\delta\dot x^\nu}{\sqrt{\dot x\cdot\dot x}}\,d\lambda = mc\int\frac{d}{d\lambda}\Bigl(\frac{\dot x_\nu}{\sqrt{\dot x\cdot\dot x}}\Bigr)\delta x^\nu\,d\lambda = m\int\frac{du_\nu}{d\lambda}\,\delta x^\nu\,d\lambda ,
> $$
>
> because $\sqrt{\dot x\cdot\dot x}\,d\lambda = c\,d\tau$ turns $\dot x_\nu/\sqrt{\dot x\cdot\dot x}$ into $(dx_\nu/d\tau)/c = u_\nu/c$. The $\delta x^\nu$ are independent and arbitrary, so the fundamental lemma ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|CM Lemma §B5.1.2]], applied as in [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|CM Theorem §B5.1.4]]) gives $du_\nu/d\lambda = 0$. As $d\tau/d\lambda > 0$, $du^\mu/d\tau = 0$, and integrating once gives the straight line.

^der-b3-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|CM Theorem §B6.1.4]], [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|CM Lemma §B5.1.2]], [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|CM Theorem §B5.1.4]], [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-4|Def. §B1.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Four equations, three laws
> $du^\mu/d\tau = 0$ looks like four equations but carries three. Contracting with $u_\mu$ gives $u_\mu\,du^\mu/d\tau = \tfrac12\,d(u\cdot u)/d\tau = 0$, which holds identically because $u\cdot u = c^2$ on every worldline ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]]). The empty component reflects the redundancy built into the description: the parameter $\lambda$ was arbitrary, and the action does not see it. A redundancy of the same kind, gauge freedom, appears when the particle couples to the electromagnetic potential ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]]). Free particles moving uniformly is also what the linearity of the Lorentz transformation needs: inertial frames map straight worldlines to straight worldlines ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group|§B1.2]]).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.2 (Remark: An equation with a built-in redundancy)*

## Energy and momentum from symmetry

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.5: Energy and Momentum as Noether Charges
> The free-particle Lagrangian has no explicit dependence on $t$ or $\vec x$. The corresponding Noether charges, conserved along every motion, are the energy function and the canonical momentum,
>
> $$
> E \equiv \vec p\cdot\vec u - L = \gamma_u mc^2, \qquad \vec p = \gamma_u m\,\vec u ,
> $$
>
> which are the total energy and the momentum of level A ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]], [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]]). Together they are the components of a four-vector, the four-momentum,
>
> $$
> p^\mu = \Bigl(\frac{E}{c}, \vec p\Bigr) = m\,u^\mu, \qquad \frac{dp^\mu}{d\tau} = 0, \qquad p\cdot p = \frac{E^2}{c^2} - p^2 = m^2c^2 ,
> $$
>
> so that $E = \sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}$ (the mass shell; [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]]) and $E$, $\vec p$ transform under boosts as $ct$, $\vec x$ do ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.3, eqs. (energy), (four-momentum), (mass shell) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Four-Momentum", §"Conservation Laws and the Hamiltonian" · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.2.3, §7.4.3*

^thm-b3-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Time translation.* $L$ does not depend on $t$, so the energy function $h = \sum_i p_i\dot q_i - L$ is conserved ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-3|CM Theorem §B6.4.3]]); it is the Noether charge of $t \to t + \varepsilon$. With $\vec p = \gamma_u m\vec u$ from [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]],
>
> $$
> E = \gamma_u m u^2 + \frac{mc^2}{\gamma_u} = \gamma_u m\Bigl[u^2 + c^2\Bigl(1 - \frac{u^2}{c^2}\Bigr)\Bigr] = \gamma_u mc^2 .
> $$
>
> *Space translations.* $x$, $y$, $z$ are cyclic, so $p_x$, $p_y$, $p_z$ are conserved ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-1|CM Theorem §B6.4.1]]); they are the Noether charges of $\vec x \to \vec x + \vec\varepsilon$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-2|CM Theorem §B6.4.2]] with $\eta = \hat\varepsilon$, $K = 0$).
>
> *Assembly.* $(E/c, \vec p) = \gamma_u m(c, \vec u) = m\,u^\mu$, a scalar times the four-velocity, hence a four-vector ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^def-b2-1-3|Def. §B2.1.3]]), and $dp^\mu/d\tau = m\,du^\mu/d\tau = 0$ by Theorem §B3.1.4. Its norm is $p\cdot p = m^2\,u\cdot u = m^2c^2$, which is $E^2/c^2 - p^2 = m^2c^2$. A four-vector transforms by the boost matrix, which for $(E/c, \vec p)$ is Theorem §A3.3.3.

^der-b3-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-1|CM Theorem §B6.4.1]], [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-2|CM Theorem §B6.4.2]], [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-3|CM Theorem §B6.4.3]], [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^def-b2-1-3|Def. §B2.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: What level B adds: derived, not defined
> - **Momentum.** Level A chose $\gamma_u m\vec u$ because it is the quantity that a glancing collision conserves in every frame ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^rem-a3-1-2|§A3.1, Remark: A definition built to be conserved]]). Here it is the canonical momentum of the only possible free action, and its conservation is the homogeneity of space. The layer of conservation changes accordingly: level A states it as a principle ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|Principle §A3.2.3]]); for a free particle it is now a theorem. For collisions and decays, [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions|§B2.1]] keeps conservation of four-momentum as a principle ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^pr-b2-1-5|Principle §B2.1.5]]). For interacting systems it follows from translation invariance of the total action; with fields this becomes Noether's theorem for fields, in Quantum Field Theory C1 — [[§C1b.5 Noether's Theorem#^thm-c1b-5-4|QFT Theorem §C1b.5.4]], [[§C1b.7 Spacetime Symmetries꞉ Energy and Momentum#^thm-c1b-7-5|QFT Theorem §C1b.7.5]] (also planned for Classical Mechanics level C1).
> - **The rest energy is not a convention.** The constant $-mc^2$ in $L$ does not affect the motion, but it enters $E = \vec p\cdot\vec u - L$. Adding a constant $C$ to $L$ would shift $E$ by $-C$, and $(E/c, \vec p)$ would no longer be a four-vector: under a boost the shifted energy would not mix with $\vec p$ as $ct$ mixes with $\vec x$. Covariance therefore fixes the zero of energy that Newtonian mechanics leaves free, and with it $E = mc^2$ at rest. Phys505 makes the same point from experiment: dynamics alone gives $E = \gamma_u mc^2 + \text{const}$, and the observation that mass can be converted into other energy in decays and reactions ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^thm-a3-2-4|Theorem §A3.2.4]]) is what makes the vacuum, not the particle at rest, the natural zero (Phys505 §6.2.3).
> - **Energy is a time component.** That $E$ and $\vec p$ mix under boosts exactly as $ct$ and $\vec x$ do, which level A computed component by component, is here one statement: the four Noether charges of the four translations form one four-vector.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.3 · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.2.3, eqs. (6.133)–(6.137)*

^rem-b3-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: The other six charges
> The Poincaré group has ten parameters ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-8|Theorem §B1.2.8]]; [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^rem-b1-2-5|§B1.2, Remark: Ten parameters, ten conservation laws]]), and translations supplied four conserved quantities. Rotations conserve the angular momentum $\vec L = \vec x\times\vec p$, as in Newtonian mechanics ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-5|CM Theorem §B6.4.5]]). Boosts conserve
>
> $$
> \vec K = \frac{E}{c^2}\,\vec x - t\,\vec p, \qquad \frac{d\vec K}{dt} = \frac{E}{c^2}\,\vec u - \vec p - t\,\frac{d\vec p}{dt} = \gamma_u m\vec u - \gamma_u m\vec u - \vec 0 = \vec 0 .
> $$
>
> Solved for $\vec x$, $\vec K = \text{const}$ says that the point $\vec x = c^2(\vec K + t\vec p)/E$, the **centre of energy**, moves uniformly: boost invariance is the relativistic form of the centre-of-mass theorem, and for $u \ll c$, where $E/c^2 \to m$, $\vec K$ becomes the Newtonian $m\vec x - \vec p\,t$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-5|CM Theorem §B6.4.5]], item 4). For a single free particle this is just uniform motion again; for systems and fields it has content.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.3 (Remark: The other six charges)*

## The Hamiltonian

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.6: Relativistic Hamiltonian
> The Legendre transform of $L = -mc^2/\gamma_u$ is well defined, the velocity in terms of the momentum is
>
> $$
> \vec u = \frac{c^2\,\vec p}{\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}}, \qquad |\vec u| < c \ \text{ for every } \vec p ,
> $$
>
> and the Hamiltonian is the mass-shell function
>
> $$
> H(\vec p) = \sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}, \qquad \dot{\vec x} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial\vec p} = \vec u, \qquad \dot{\vec p} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial\vec x} = \vec 0 .
> $$
>
> $H$ is conserved and equals the energy $E$ of [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]].
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.4, eq. (relativistic Hamiltonian) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Hamiltonian Mechanics" · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.4.3–7.4.4, eqs. (7.64), (7.72)*

^thm-b3-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Invertibility.* With $\partial\gamma_u/\partial u_j = \gamma_u^3u_j/c^2$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2L}{\partial u_i\,\partial u_j} = \frac{\partial p_i}{\partial u_j} = \gamma_u m\Bigl(\delta_{ij} + \frac{\gamma_u^2u_iu_j}{c^2}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> whose quadratic form $\gamma_u m\bigl(|\vec w|^2 + \gamma_u^2(\vec u\cdot\vec w)^2/c^2\bigr)$ is positive for $\vec w \ne \vec 0$. So the Hessian is positive definite, as [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-2|CM Def. §B7.1.2]] requires.
>
> *Inversion.* Squaring $\vec p = \gamma_u m\vec u$ gives $p^2(1 - u^2/c^2) = m^2u^2$, so $u^2 = p^2c^2/(p^2 + m^2c^2)$, which is below $c^2$; since $\vec u \parallel \vec p$, $\vec u = c\,\vec p/\sqrt{p^2 + m^2c^2} = c^2\vec p/\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}$. Then $1 - u^2/c^2 = m^2c^2/(p^2 + m^2c^2)$.
>
> *Legendre transform.*
>
> $$
> H = \vec p\cdot\vec u - L = \frac{p^2c^2}{\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}} + mc^2\sqrt{1 - \frac{u^2}{c^2}} = \frac{p^2c^2}{\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}} + \frac{m^2c^4}{\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}} = \sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4} .
> $$
>
> Hamilton's equations ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-2|CM Theorem §B7.1.2]]) are then $\dot{\vec x} = c^2\vec p/H = \vec u$ and $\dot{\vec p} = \vec 0$. $H$ has no explicit $t$, so it is conserved, and it equals $h = E$ ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-4|CM Theorem §B7.1.4]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Is the Hamiltonian covariant?
> The covariant route gave $du^\mu/d\tau = 0$, an equation visibly valid in all frames. The Hamiltonian route cannot: the Legendre transform differentiates with respect to one observer's time, and $H = cp^0$ is the time component of a four-vector, not a scalar, so different inertial observers use different Hamiltonians, related by boosts. Covariance becomes a property to be proved: the ten conserved quantities must form, under Poisson brackets, the algebra of the Poincaré group. A trace of this is already visible. $\vec K = E\vec x/c^2 - t\vec p$ depends on $t$ explicitly, and its conservation is a cancellation ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|CM Theorem §B8.1.3]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{dK_i}{dt} = \frac{\partial K_i}{\partial t} + \{K_i, H\} = -p_i + \frac{E}{c^2}\,\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} = -p_i + p_i = 0 ,
> $$
>
> with $\{K_i, H\} = p_i \neq 0$: the boost charges do not commute with the Hamiltonian, which in quantum theory becomes $[\hat K_i, \hat H] \neq 0$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^rem-b8-1-5|CM Remark: From brackets to commutators]]). A manifestly covariant Hamiltonian form exists if the worldline parameter itself is used as the time; then $H$ vanishes identically and the dynamics sits in the constraint $p\cdot p = m^2c^2$, the Hamiltonian face of reparametrization invariance (stated in the series; constrained dynamics is not developed here).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.4 (Remark: Is H covariant? No, and instructively so)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.7: Non-Relativistic Limit
> For $u \ll c$, equivalently $p \ll mc$,
>
> $$
> L = -mc^2 + \tfrac12 mu^2 + \frac{mu^4}{8c^2} + \cdots, \qquad E = mc^2 + \tfrac12 mu^2 + \frac{3mu^4}{8c^2} + \cdots, \qquad H = mc^2 + \frac{p^2}{2m} - \frac{p^4}{8m^3c^2} + \cdots .
> $$
>
> Up to the constant rest energy, the leading terms are the Newtonian free Lagrangian $L = T$ and Hamiltonian $p^2/2m$ of Classical Mechanics ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-4|CM Def. §B6.1.4]], [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-2|CM Def. §B7.1.2]]); the next terms are the first relativistic corrections.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.1, §4.3, eqs. (L expansion), (energy expansion) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Non-Relativistic Limit"*

^thm-b3-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) gives $\sqrt{1 - x} = 1 - \tfrac12 x - \tfrac18 x^2 + O(x^3)$, $(1 - x)^{-1/2} = 1 + \tfrac12 x + \tfrac38 x^2 + O(x^3)$ and $\sqrt{1 + y} = 1 + \tfrac12 y - \tfrac18 y^2 + O(y^3)$. Insert $x = u^2/c^2$ into $L = -mc^2\sqrt{1 - x}$ and $E = mc^2(1 - x)^{-1/2}$, and $y = p^2/m^2c^2$ into $H = mc^2\sqrt{1 + y}$ ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]]).

> [!remark] Remark: The square-root problem
> $H = \sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}$ is awkward to turn into a quantum operator: with $\vec p \to -i\hbar\nabla$ the square root of a differential operator is non-local. The expansion of [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]] avoids it order by order, which is how the $p^4$ term enters the fine structure of hydrogen ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-2|QM Theorem §B8.3.2]]). The historical way out was to square the relation instead, demanding $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4$ of a wave; that gives not a Schrödinger-type equation but a field equation, the Klein–Gordon equation of [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]], whose dispersion relation is the mass shell with $(E, \vec p) \to (\hbar\omega, \hbar\vec k)$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.4 (Remark: The square-root problem)*

> [!caution] Caution: One mass, not a relativistic mass
> Phys505 writes $m_0$ for the rest mass and sets $m = \gamma_u m_0$ ("faster objects have heavier mass"), so that $\vec p = m\vec u$ and $E = mc^2$ keep their Newtonian look (Phys505 §6.2.3). These notes follow level A ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^cau-a3-1-1|§A3.1, Caution: There is one mass]]): $m$ is the invariant mass, the scalar that appears in the action, and the speed dependence sits in $\gamma_u$. The two are the same physics, but only the invariant $m$ fits the four-vector $p^\mu = mu^\mu$ and the scalar action $-mc\int d\tau$.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.1.1: An LHC Proton
> A proton in the Large Hadron Collider has energy $E = 6.8\ \mathrm{TeV}$; its rest energy is $mc^2 = 0.938\ \mathrm{GeV}$. How fast is it, and what do the Newtonian formulas give?
>
> By Theorem §B3.1.5, $\gamma_u = E/mc^2 = 6800/0.938 = 7.25\times10^3$. Hamilton's equation ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]]) gives $u/c = pc/E = \sqrt{1 - 1/\gamma_u^2}$, so
>
> $$
> 1 - \frac{u}{c} = \frac{1/\gamma_u^2}{1 + u/c} \approx \frac{1}{2\gamma_u^2} = 9.5\times10^{-9}, \qquad c - u \approx 2.9\ \mathrm{m/s} :
> $$
>
> the proton trails a light pulse at walking pace. It sits far out on the mass shell, where $pc$ and $E$ agree to one part in $10^8$ ($pc = E\sqrt{1 - 1/\gamma_u^2} \approx E(1 - 1/2\gamma_u^2)$). The non-relativistic expansion of [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]] is useless here, since its parameter $p/mc \approx 7\times10^3$ is not small: $p^2/2m$ would give a kinetic energy of $(6800\ \mathrm{GeV})^2/(2 \times 0.938\ \mathrm{GeV}) = 2.5\times10^7\ \mathrm{GeV}$, more than three thousand times the true $E - mc^2 = 6.8\ \mathrm{TeV}$; and $u = p/m$ would exceed $c$ by the factor $\gamma_u$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §4.3, Example (LHC protons: the worked number)*
