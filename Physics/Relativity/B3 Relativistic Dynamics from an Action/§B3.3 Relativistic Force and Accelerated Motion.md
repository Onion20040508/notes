How should force be defined in relativity, and what motion does a constant force produce? Level A defined the force as the rate of change of relativistic momentum ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-2|Def. §A3.1.2]]) and warned that it is not $\gamma m\vec a$: in linear motion $F = \gamma^3ma$, and in general force and acceleration are not parallel ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^cau-a3-1-2|§A3.1, Caution: Not F = γma]]). [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle|§B3.1]] and [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle|§B3.2]] obtain equations of motion from an action, without forces. This section gives the force-based view on top of the four-vectors of [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions|§B2.1]]: the ordinary three-force against the Minkowski four-force, the exact relation between force and acceleration, how forces transform between frames, motion at constant proper acceleration (hyperbolic motion), cyclotron motion at any speed, and the photon rocket.

Notation as in [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions|§B2.1]]: $\vec u$ is the particle's velocity, $\gamma = \gamma_u$ unless a frame velocity $v$ is involved, $\vec a = d\vec u/dt$ the ordinary acceleration, $u^\mu$ and $a^\mu$ the four-velocity and four-acceleration, $\alpha$ the proper acceleration ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^def-b2-1-2|Def. §B2.1.2]]), and $\varphi$ the rapidity ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-3|Def. §B1.2.3]]). The Minkowski force is written $K^\mu$, so as not to clash with the field tensor $F^{\mu\nu}$.

## Three-force and Minkowski force

> [!definition] Definition §B3.3.1: Three-Force; Minkowski Force
> The **(ordinary) three-force** on a particle is $\vec F = d\vec p/dt$, with $\vec p = \gamma m\vec u$ ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-2|Def. §A3.1.2]]); the rate $dE/dt$ is the **power** delivered to it. The **Minkowski force** (four-force) is the proper-time derivative of the four-momentum,
>
> $$
> K^\mu = \frac{dp^\mu}{d\tau} ,
> $$
>
> a four-vector, because $p^\mu$ is a four-vector and $d\tau$ an invariant.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §12.2.4, eqs. (12.59), (12.68)–(12.70) · Morin §12.5.1, eq. (12.52); §13.2, eq. (13.9) · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §6.2.2–6.2.3*

^def-b3-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.1: Minkowski Force in Terms of the Three-Force
> For a particle of constant mass $m$:
> 1. **Work–energy theorem:** $dE/dt = \vec F\cdot\vec u$, so the work $W = \int\vec F\cdot d\vec l$ equals $E_{\rm final} - E_{\rm initial}$.
> 2. $K^\mu = \gamma\,\bigl(\vec F\cdot\vec u/c,\ \vec F\bigr) = \gamma\,(P/c,\ \vec F)$, with $P$ the power; in the MCRF $K^\mu = (0, \vec F_0)$.
> 3. $K^\mu = ma^\mu$ and $K\cdot u = 0$. If the rest mass changes (heating, a rocket), $K\cdot u = c^2\,dm/d\tau$.
> 4. $K\cdot K = -\gamma^2F^2\bigl(1 - (u^2/c^2)\cos^2\theta\bigr)$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec u$ and $\vec F$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §12.2.4, eqs. (12.62)–(12.64), (12.69)–(12.70); Problem 12.40 (converted to the signature $+---$) · Morin §12.5.1, eqs. (12.55)–(12.57); §13.5.1, eqs. (13.19)–(13.22)*

^thm-b3-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* With $d\gamma/dt = \gamma^3(\vec u\cdot\vec a)/c^2$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]),
>
> $$
> \frac{dE}{dt} = mc^2\frac{d\gamma}{dt} = \gamma^3m\,\vec u\cdot\vec a, \qquad \vec F\cdot\vec u = \Bigl(\gamma m\vec a + \frac{\gamma^3m(\vec u\cdot\vec a)}{c^2}\vec u\Bigr)\cdot\vec u = \gamma m(\vec u\cdot\vec a)\Bigl(1 + \frac{\gamma^2u^2}{c^2}\Bigr) = \gamma^3m\,\vec u\cdot\vec a ,
> $$
>
> using $1 + \gamma^2u^2/c^2 = \gamma^2$. Integrating over time, $\int\vec F\cdot\vec u\,dt = \int\vec F\cdot d\vec l = \Delta E$.
>
> *2.* $dt/d\tau = \gamma$, so $K^0 = \frac1c\frac{dE}{d\tau} = \frac{\gamma}{c}\frac{dE}{dt} = \gamma\vec F\cdot\vec u/c$ and $\vec K = \gamma\,d\vec p/dt = \gamma\vec F$. In the MCRF $\vec u = 0$, $\gamma = 1$.
>
> *3.* $K = d(mu)/d\tau = \frac{dm}{d\tau}u + ma$, and $u\cdot u = c^2$, $a\cdot u = 0$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]) give $K\cdot u = c^2\,dm/d\tau$, which vanishes for constant mass.
>
> *4.* From item 2, $K\cdot K = \gamma^2\bigl[(\vec F\cdot\vec u)^2/c^2 - F^2\bigr] = -\gamma^2F^2\bigl[1 - (u^2/c^2)\cos^2\theta\bigr]$. (Griffiths, with the opposite signature, has the same expression with a plus sign.)

^der-b3-3-1

*Uses:* [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]], [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]], [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^def-b2-1-3|Def. §B2.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Which force is the force?
> - Both descriptions are exact. The Minkowski force is a four-vector and fits covariant laws ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^pr-b2-2-9|Principle §B2.2.9]]); the three-force is what moves the particle in the time $t$ of the frame in which it is observed, so it is often the more practical one (Griffiths §12.2.4).
> - A classical force law does not say which of the two it is. Griffiths asks whether the Lorentz force is $\vec F = q(\vec E + \vec u\times\vec B)$ or $\vec K = q(\vec E + \vec u\times\vec B)$. The answer is the former: the action of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle|§B3.2]] gives $d(\gamma m\vec u)/dt = q(\vec E + \vec u\times\vec B)$, and covariantly $dp^\mu/d\tau = qF^\mu{}_\nu u^\nu$.
> - The PHY 505 instructor writes the four-force as $\gamma(P/c, \vec F)$ and recovers Newton's form $\vec F = d(m\vec u)/dt$ with a velocity-dependent mass $m = \gamma m_0$ (§6.2.3). These notes keep one mass ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^cau-a3-1-1|§A3.1, Caution: There is one mass]]) and put $\gamma$ in the momentum; the physics is the same.

^rem-b3-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.2: Force and Acceleration
> For a particle of constant mass,
>
> $$
> \vec F = \gamma m\,\vec a + \frac{\gamma^3m\,(\vec u\cdot\vec a)}{c^2}\,\vec u, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \vec a = \frac{1}{\gamma m}\Bigl[\vec F - \frac{(\vec F\cdot\vec u)}{c^2}\,\vec u\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Splitting along and across the velocity, $F_\parallel = \gamma^3ma_\parallel$ and $\vec F_\perp = \gamma m\,\vec a_\perp$. The force is parallel to the acceleration only if $\vec a$ is parallel or perpendicular to $\vec u$; otherwise the acceleration is turned away from $\vec u$, since a particle is $\gamma^2$ times harder to accelerate along its motion than across it.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 12.37, eq. (12.74); Problem 12.41 · Morin §12.5.1–12.5.2, eqs. (12.54), (12.60); §13.2, eq. (13.11)*

^thm-b3-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate $\vec p = \gamma m\vec u$ with $d\gamma/dt = \gamma^3(\vec u\cdot\vec a)/c^2$ to get the first form. Dot it with $\vec u$: $\vec F\cdot\vec u = \gamma^3m(\vec u\cdot\vec a)$ ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]]), so $\vec F - (\vec F\cdot\vec u)\vec u/c^2 = \gamma m\vec a$, the second form. For the split, write $\vec a = a_\parallel\hat u + \vec a_\perp$: the component of the first form along $\hat u$ is $\gamma ma_\parallel(1 + \gamma^2u^2/c^2) = \gamma^3ma_\parallel$, and across $\hat u$ only $\gamma m\vec a_\perp$ remains.

^der-b3-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]], [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: From level A, and for a charge
> Level A's $F = \gamma^3ma$ is the case $\vec a \parallel \vec u$; its warning that $\vec F$ and $\vec a$ need not be parallel is made quantitative here. For a charge in electromagnetic fields, $\vec F\cdot\vec u = q\vec E\cdot\vec u$ (the magnetic force does no work, [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-2|EM Theorem §B6.1.2]]), and the second form gives the acceleration
>
> $$
> \vec a = \frac{q}{\gamma m}\Bigl[\vec E + \vec u\times\vec B - \frac{1}{c^2}\,\vec u\,(\vec u\cdot\vec E)\Bigr]
> $$
>
> (Griffiths Problem 12.41), which reduces to $q(\vec E + \vec u\times\vec B)/m$ of [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|EM Principle §B6.1.1]] for $u \ll c$.

^rem-b3-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.3: Transformation of the Three-Force
> Let $\bar S$ move with velocity $v$ along $x$ relative to $S$, and let a particle of constant mass move with velocity $\vec u$ in $S$, where it feels the force $\vec F$. In $\bar S$
>
> $$
> \bar F_x = \frac{F_x - \beta\,(\vec u\cdot\vec F)/c}{1 - \beta u_x/c}, \qquad \bar F_y = \frac{F_y}{\gamma\,(1 - \beta u_x/c)}, \qquad \bar F_z = \frac{F_z}{\gamma\,(1 - \beta u_x/c)} .
> $$
>
> If the particle is momentarily at rest in $S$, then $\bar F_\parallel = F_\parallel$ and $\bar F_\perp = F_\perp/\gamma$: the force along the relative motion is the same in both frames, the transverse force is largest in the particle's rest frame.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §12.2.4, eqs. (12.65)–(12.67) · Morin §12.5.3, eq. (12.65); §13.5.1, eq. (13.22)*

^thm-b3-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $K^\mu = \gamma_u(\vec F\cdot\vec u/c, \vec F)$ is a four-vector ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]]), so $\bar K^1 = \gamma(K^1 - \beta K^0) = \gamma\gamma_u\bigl(F_x - \beta\,\vec u\cdot\vec F/c\bigr)$ and $\bar K^{2,3} = K^{2,3} = \gamma_uF_{y,z}$. The particle's Lorentz factor in $\bar S$ is $\gamma_{\bar u} = \gamma\gamma_u(1 - \beta u_x/c)$, from the time component of the transformation of $u^\mu$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^rem-b2-1-1|§B2.1, Remark: Velocity addition, read off a four-vector]]). Dividing, $\bar F = \bar{\vec K}/\gamma_{\bar u}$ gives the formulas. For $\vec u = 0$: $\bar F_x = F_x$, $\bar F_{y,z} = F_{y,z}/\gamma$.

*Uses:* [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]], [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]], [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why F = ma cannot be a relativistic law
> - Forces and accelerations transform differently. From the rest frame to a frame where the particle moves along $x$, forces go as $F_\parallel \to F_\parallel$, $F_\perp \to F_\perp/\gamma$ (Theorem §B3.3.3), accelerations as $a_\parallel \to a_\parallel/\gamma^3$, $a_\perp \to a_\perp/\gamma^2$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^rem-b2-1-2|§B2.1, Remark: What the orthogonality means]]). An equation $\vec F = m\vec a$ true in one frame is false in the next; its covariant replacement is $K^\mu = ma^\mu$ (Morin §13.5–13.6; [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-8|Theorem §B2.2.8]]).
> - The relation is not symmetric between the frames: forces on a given particle single out its own rest frame, because the events that matter, two points of the particle's worldline, are at one place in that frame only (Morin §12.5.3, Remark 1).
> - Newton's third law fails for bodies apart: "equal and opposite at the same instant" in one frame becomes "at different instants" in another, by the relativity of simultaneity ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^thm-a1-3-4|Theorem §A1.3.4]]). Only contact forces keep it; for separated bodies momentum is conserved only with the momentum of the field between them (Griffiths §12.2.4; [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^pr-b2-1-5|Principle §B2.1.5]]).

## Hyperbolic motion

> [!model] Model §B3.3.4: Uniformly Accelerated Particle
> A point particle moves on a straight line of an inertial frame with constant proper acceleration $\alpha$ along that line: in each of its momentarily comoving frames it has acceleration of the same magnitude $\alpha$, in the same direction. Realizations: a rocket whose accelerometer (or a scale under the astronaut) shows a constant reading, a body pushed by a constant three-force along its motion, a charge in a uniform static electric field. The particle carries an ideal clock ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^mod-a2-5-1|Model §A2.5.1]]) measuring $\tau$.
>
> *Source: Schutz Exercise 2.19 · Morin §11.9.2 · Griffiths Example 12.10 · the user's series, Part III, §5.3, Example (Constant field: hyperbolic motion)*

^mod-b3-3-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.5: Hyperbolic Motion
> Within [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^mod-b3-3-4|Model §B3.3.4]], let the particle start from rest at $x = 0$ at $t = \tau = 0$ and accelerate along $+x$. Then
> 1. its rapidity grows linearly with proper time, $\varphi = \alpha\tau/c$, so
>
> $$
> u^\mu = c\,(\cosh\varphi, \sinh\varphi, 0, 0), \qquad a^\mu = \alpha\,(\sinh\varphi, \cosh\varphi, 0, 0) ;
> $$
>
> 2. its worldline is
>
> $$
> ct = \frac{c^2}{\alpha}\sinh\frac{\alpha\tau}{c}, \qquad x = \frac{c^2}{\alpha}\Bigl(\cosh\frac{\alpha\tau}{c} - 1\Bigr), \qquad \Bigl(x + \frac{c^2}{\alpha}\Bigr)^2 - (ct)^2 = \Bigl(\frac{c^2}{\alpha}\Bigr)^2 ,
> $$
>
> a hyperbola; in coordinate time,
>
> $$
> u(t) = \frac{\alpha t}{\sqrt{1 + (\alpha t/c)^2}}, \qquad x(t) = \frac{c^2}{\alpha}\Bigl[\sqrt{1 + (\alpha t/c)^2} - 1\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> so $u < c$ always, and $x \approx \frac12\alpha t^2$ while $\alpha t \ll c$;
> 3. the same motion results from a constant three-force $F$ along $x$ in the lab, with $\alpha = F/m$; for a charge $q$ in a uniform field $E$, $\alpha = qE/m$.
>
> *Source: Morin §11.9.2, eqs. (11.62)–(11.66); Problem 13.4 · Griffiths Example 12.10, eqs. (12.60)–(12.61); Problem 12.28 · Schutz Exercises 2.19, 2.21 · the user's series, Part III, §5.3, Example (Constant field: hyperbolic motion)*

^thm-b3-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* The motion is in the $(t, x)$ plane, and $u\cdot u = c^2$ with $u^0 > 0$, so $u^\mu = c(\cosh\varphi, \sinh\varphi, 0, 0)$ for some function $\varphi(\tau)$, the rapidity ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-3|Def. §B1.2.3]]). Then $a^\mu = c\dot\varphi\,(\sinh\varphi, \cosh\varphi, 0, 0)$, with the dot $d/d\tau$, and $a\cdot a = -c^2\dot\varphi^2$. Constant proper acceleration means $a\cdot a = -\alpha^2$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]), and acceleration along $+x$ means $\dot\varphi > 0$; so $\dot\varphi = \alpha/c$ and $\varphi = \alpha\tau/c$. (Morin's route: in the MCRF the velocity grows by $\alpha\,d\tau$ in proper time $d\tau$, and collinear velocities compose by adding rapidities ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-7|Theorem §B1.2.7]]), so $d\varphi = \alpha\,d\tau/c$.)
>
> *2.* Integrate $dx^\mu/d\tau = u^\mu$ from the origin: $ct = (c^2/\alpha)\sinh\varphi$, $x = (c^2/\alpha)(\cosh\varphi - 1)$, and $\cosh^2\varphi - \sinh^2\varphi = 1$ gives the hyperbola. With $\sinh\varphi = \alpha t/c$, $u = c\tanh\varphi = c\sinh\varphi/\sqrt{1 + \sinh^2\varphi}$ and $x = (c^2/\alpha)\bigl(\sqrt{1 + \sinh^2\varphi} - 1\bigr)$. For small $\alpha t/c$, $\sqrt{1 + s^2} - 1 \approx s^2/2$.
>
> *3.* With $dp/dt = F$ constant and $p(0) = 0$, $\gamma mu = Ft$, which solved for $u$ is the formula of item 2 with $\alpha = F/m$ (Griffiths' route). Consistently, for linear motion $F = F_\parallel = \gamma^3ma = m\alpha$ ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]], [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]), and the longitudinal force is the same in the MCRF ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]]), where it equals $m$ times the felt acceleration. For a charge the force is $qE$; the covariant derivation from $dp^\mu/d\tau = qF^\mu{}_\nu u^\nu$ is in the user's series and belongs with [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle|§B3.2]].

> [!remark] Remark: A horizon, and where this leads
> Every event on or above the asymptote $ct = x + c^2/\alpha$ is invisible to the uniformly accelerated particle: light from it never catches up. A runner with a constant force and a head start of more than $c^2/\alpha$ outruns a light ray (Griffiths Problem 12.38). This boundary is a horizon of the accelerated observer. The coordinates adapted to a family of such observers (Rindler coordinates), the horizon's properties, and the link to gravity through the equivalence principle belong to Relativity level C (planned). Within special relativity the accelerated particle is described from an inertial frame, as for the turnaround of the travelling twin ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^rem-a2-5-1|§A2.5, Remark: Where the symmetry breaks]]); identically accelerated twins ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^ex-a2-5-3|Example §A2.5.3]]) are two such hyperbolae shifted along $x$.

## Cyclotron motion and the photon rocket

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.6: Relativistic Cyclotron Motion
> A particle of charge $q$ and mass $m$ moves in a uniform static magnetic field $\vec B$ with no electric field, under the Lorentz force $\vec F = q\vec u\times\vec B$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|EM Principle §B6.1.1]]). Then its speed, $\gamma$ and energy are constant, $\vec a = (q/\gamma m)\,\vec u\times\vec B$, and the motion is a helix about $\vec B$ whose projection perpendicular to $\vec B$ is a circle of radius and angular frequency
>
> $$
> R = \frac{p_\perp}{|q|B} = \frac{\gamma mu_\perp}{|q|B}, \qquad \omega = \frac{|q|B}{\gamma m} = \frac{|q|Bc^2}{E} .
> $$
>
> The radius formula is the nonrelativistic one with the relativistic momentum; the frequency is the nonrelativistic one divided by $\gamma$. Per unit proper time the particle turns at the nonrelativistic rate $|q|B/m$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 12.11, eq. (12.71) · the user's series, Part III, §5.3, Example (The LHC proton, bent)*

^thm-b3-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\vec F\cdot\vec u = 0$, so $dE/dt = 0$ ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]]): $E$, $\gamma$ and the speed are constant. The second form of [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]] then gives $\vec a = \vec F/(\gamma m) = (q/\gamma m)\,\vec u\times\vec B$. This is the nonrelativistic equation of motion with $m$ replaced by the constant $\gamma m$, so its solutions are those of [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|EM Theorem §A7.2.2]] and [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-3|EM Theorem §A7.2.3]] with that replacement: $R = \gamma mu_\perp/|q|B$ and $\omega = u_\perp/R = |q|B/\gamma m$. Griffiths' version: $\vec p$ keeps its magnitude and turns, $|d\vec p| = p\,d\theta$, so $F = p\,\omega = p\,u/R$, and $|q|uB = pu/R$ gives $p = |q|BR$. Finally $d\theta/d\tau = \gamma\,d\theta/dt = |q|B/m$.

^der-b3-3-6

*Uses:* [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]], [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]], [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|EM Principle §B6.1.1]], [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|EM Theorem §A7.2.2]], [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-3|EM Theorem §A7.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Consequences for accelerators and detectors
> - **Momentum from a radius.** $p = |q|BR$ holds at every speed, which is how momenta are measured from tracks in a magnetic field ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^ex-a3-3-3|Example §A3.3.3]]; [[§A8.3 Particle Accelerators and Detectors#^thm-a8-3-1|QM Theorem §A8.3.1]]).
> - **The cyclotron fails.** A cyclotron drives particles at a fixed frequency $|q|B/m$ ([[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^thm-a7-5-5|EM Theorem §A7.5.5]]). As $\gamma$ grows the orbit frequency $|q|B/\gamma m$ falls behind, and the particles drift out of phase; synchrotrons ramp $B$ with the energy instead ([[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^rem-a7-5-2|EM §A7.5, Remark: Beyond the cyclotron]]). For a $6.8\ \mathrm{TeV}$ proton in the LHC dipoles ($B = 8.1\ \mathrm T$, $\gamma = 6800/0.938 = 7.2\times10^3$; the user's series computes the bending radius $R \approx 2.80\ \mathrm{km}$), $\omega = c/R \approx 1.07\times10^5\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, compared with $|q|B/m = 7.8\times10^8\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ for a slow proton.

^rem-b3-3-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.7: The Photon Rocket
> A rocket of initial mass $M$, at rest, propels itself by converting its rest mass into photons emitted straight backward. When it moves at speed $v = \beta c$ (rapidity $\varphi$) relative to its initial rest frame, its mass and energy are
>
> $$
> m = M\sqrt{\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}} = Me^{-\varphi}, \qquad E = \gamma mc^2 = \frac{Mc^2}{1 + \beta} ,
> $$
>
> independent of the rate of conversion and of the photons' frequency. Equivalently $\varphi = \ln(M/m)$. As $\beta \to 1$, $E \to \frac12Mc^2$: half of the initial energy stays with the rocket. If the rocket keeps a constant proper acceleration $\alpha$, its mass falls as $m(\tau) = Me^{-\alpha\tau/c}$.
>
> *Source: Morin §12.6, Example (Relativistic rocket), eqs. (12.66)–(12.67), (12.76), (12.80)–(12.81)*

^thm-b3-3-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Use the light-cone component $P^+ \equiv P^0 + P^1$ of four-momenta, with $x$ along the motion. A photon moving along $-x$ has $p_\gamma^1 = -p_\gamma^0$, so $p_\gamma^+ = 0$. Each emission conserves the total four-momentum ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^pr-b2-1-5|Principle §B2.1.5]]), so the rocket's own $P^+$ never changes. The rocket's four-momentum is $mc(\cosh\varphi, \sinh\varphi, 0, 0)$, with $P^+ = mce^{\varphi}$; initially $Mc$. Hence $me^\varphi = M$, and $e^{-\varphi} = \sqrt{(1 - \beta)/(1 + \beta)}$ because $\tanh\varphi = \beta$. The energy is $Mc^2\cosh\varphi\,e^{-\varphi} = \frac12Mc^2(1 + e^{-2\varphi}) = Mc^2/(1 + \beta)$. With constant proper acceleration, $\varphi = \alpha\tau/c$ ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]). Morin's first solution reaches the same result with ordinary components: momentum conservation in the ground frame, with the photons' momentum Doppler-shifted by $\gamma(1 - \beta)$, gives $dm/m + d\varphi = 0$; the equivalent statement $P^0 + P^1 = {\rm const}$ is his third solution.

^der-b3-3-7

> [!remark] Remark: Rapidity is the rocket's velocity
> The classical rocket gains $\Delta v = u_{\rm ex}\ln(m_i/m_f)$ for exhaust speed $u_{\rm ex}$ ([[§A5.5 Rocket Propulsion#^thm-a5-5-2|CM Theorem §A5.5.2]]). The photon rocket, exhaust speed $c$, gains $\Delta\varphi = \ln(M/m)$: the same logarithm, with rapidity in place of velocity divided by $c$. Rapidity adds for successive burns as velocity does classically, which is why the logarithm survives. Morin also notes that the emitted photons are redshifted in the ground frame by $\sqrt{(1 - \beta)/(1 + \beta)}$, the same factor as the mass ratio. A rocket with deceleration or a turnaround must shed its rapidity again, which multiplies the required mass ratio.

## Examples

> [!example]- ★ Example §B3.3.1: A Bead on a Rod
> A rod is fixed in frame $S'$ at $\theta' = 45^\circ$ to the $x'$ axis. A stretched spring, attached to the rod's end, pulls a bead that can slide along the rod; at $t' = 0$ the bead is released from rest. Describe the rod, the force and the acceleration just after release in a frame $S$ in which the rod moves along $+x$ with $\gamma = 2$.
>
> - *Rod.* Its $x$-extent is contracted by $\gamma$ and its $y$-extent is not, so $\tan\theta = \gamma\tan\theta' = 2$: $\theta = 63.4^\circ$.
> - *Force.* In $S'$ the bead is at rest and the force points along the rod. By [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]] the longitudinal component is unchanged and the transverse one divided by $\gamma$: $\tan\psi = \tan\theta'/\gamma = \tfrac12$, $\psi = 26.6^\circ$.
> - *Acceleration.* The bead moves with velocity $v\hat x$, so by [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]] $a_x = F_x/\gamma^3m$, $a_y = F_y/\gamma m$, and $a_y/a_x = \gamma^2F_y/F_x = \gamma\tan\theta' = \tan\theta$: the acceleration points along the rod, at $63.4^\circ$.
>
> The bead must stay on the moving rod, so its acceleration has to lie along the rod, and it does. The force points elsewhere, at $26.6^\circ$, and no constraint force is needed to make up the difference: the bead need not touch the rod in $S'$, so it need not in $S$. Force and acceleration differ in direction by $37^\circ$.
>
> *Source: Morin §12.5.3, Example (Bead on a rod)*

^ex-b3-3-1

> [!example] Example §B3.3.2: A Rocket at One g
> A rocket accelerates from rest with constant proper acceleration $\alpha = g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]). The natural scales are $c/g = 0.968\ \mathrm{yr}$ and $c^2/g = 0.968\ \mathrm{ly}$: Earth's gravity happens to take one year to approach $c$.
>
> *One year on board.* $\varphi = g\tau/c = 1.03$, so $\beta = \tanh\varphi = 0.775$ and $\gamma = 1.58$; the Earth's clocks show $t = (c/g)\sinh\varphi = 1.19\ \mathrm{yr}$, and the rocket has covered $x = (c^2/g)(\cosh\varphi - 1) = 0.56\ \mathrm{ly}$.
>
> *To the centre of the Galaxy*, $d = 2\times10^{20}\ \mathrm m = 21\,140\ \mathrm{ly}$, accelerating all the way: $\cosh\varphi = 1 + gd/c^2 = 21\,830$, so $\varphi = 10.68$ and the crew ages $\tau = (c/g)\varphi = 10.3\ \mathrm{yr}$, while $t = (c/g)\sinh\varphi = 21\,141\ \mathrm{yr}$ pass on Earth, one year more than light needs. To stop there instead, accelerate for half the distance and decelerate for the other half: each half has $\cosh\varphi_{1/2} = 1 + g(d/2)/c^2$, $\varphi_{1/2} = 9.99$, and the crew ages $19.4\ \mathrm{yr}$.
>
> *The fuel bill.* A perfect photon rocket needs $M/m = e^{\varphi}$ ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-7|Theorem §B3.3.7]]): $e^{10.68} = 4.4\times10^4$ for the fly-by, and $e^{2\times9.99} = 4.8\times10^8$ to arrive at rest. Time dilation makes the trip short for the crew; the logarithm in the rocket equation makes it expensive.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.3, Example (Constant field: hyperbolic motion) (the one-year figures) · Schutz Exercise 2.19 (the trip; solved here) · Morin §12.6*
