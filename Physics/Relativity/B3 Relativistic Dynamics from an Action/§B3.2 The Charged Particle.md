*Sources: the user's PHY 505 notes · the user's series, Part III, §5, §12.4 · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), ch. 6, 7 · Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (4th ed.), §12.2.4, §12.3.3–12.3.4.*

How does a charged particle couple to the electromagnetic field, and why does it feel exactly the Lorentz force? Level A defined force as $d\vec p/dt$ with the relativistic momentum ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-2|Def. §A3.1.2]]), and Electromagnetism took the Lorentz force as a principle, valid at all speeds if read that way ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|EM Principle §B6.1.1]]); Classical Mechanics derived its Newtonian form from the Lagrangian $\tfrac12 mv^2 - qV + q\vec v\cdot\vec A$ ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^rem-b6-2-6|CM §B6.2, Remark: Velocity-dependent potentials]]). Here the free action of [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle|§B3.1]] gains the one coupling to a four-vector field that covariance permits, $-q\int A_\mu\,dx^\mu$. Its redundancy is gauge freedom, its equation of motion is the Lorentz force at all speeds, in three-vector and in covariant form, and the variation itself assembles the field tensor $F_{\mu\nu}$ of [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor|§B4.2]]. The momentum splits into a mechanical part, on which the force acts, and a canonical part, which enters the Hamiltonian. Notation: $V$ and $\vec A$ are the scalar and vector potentials, $A^\mu = (V/c, \vec A)$, $A_\mu = (V/c, -\vec A)$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-1|Def. §B4.2.1]]); $\vec u$, $\gamma_u$, $u^\mu$ as in §B3.1.

## The coupling

> [!principle] Principle §B3.2.1: Action of a Charge in an External Field
> A point particle of mass $m$ and charge $q$ in a prescribed electromagnetic four-potential $A_\mu(x)$ has the action
>
> $$
> S = -mc\int d\tau - q\int A_\mu(x)\,dx^\mu = \int\Bigl[-mc\sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu} - q\,A_\mu\bigl(x(\lambda)\bigr)\,\dot x^\mu\Bigr]d\lambda ,
> $$
>
> and its worldline makes $S$ stationary ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^pr-b3-1-2|Principle §B3.1.2]]). The coupling constant $q$ is the charge, a Lorentz scalar.
>
> *Domain: a classical point charge ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^mod-b3-1-1|Model §B3.1.1]]) in an external field that the charge itself does not change: the particle's own field and radiation reaction are neglected (radiation: [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge|EM §B11.4★]]). The field's own dynamics, Maxwell's equations from an action, is in Quantum Field Theory C1 — [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^rem-c1a-7-2|QFT Remark: Electrodynamics as the template for field theory]] (in SI units, with the action as the principle, Electromagnetism C1 — [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^pr-c1-1-4|EM Principle §C1.1.4]], [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-2|EM Theorem §C1.2.2]]). Layer: the action is the principle; the Lorentz force becomes a theorem ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]]).*
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.1, eq. (charged action) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.4.2–7.4.4*

^pr-b3-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why this coupling
> - *Forced, given linearity.* A term local on the worldline and linear in a four-vector field has the form $\int A_\mu(x)\,f^\mu(\dot x)\,d\lambda$, with $f^\mu$ built from the worldline. For a scalar, $f^\mu$ must be a four-vector, and the only one available is $g(\dot x\cdot\dot x)\,\dot x^\mu$. Under a reparametrization with $\dot x \to a\dot x$, $d\lambda \to d\lambda/a$ ($a > 0$), the term becomes $\int A_\mu\,g(a^2\dot x\cdot\dot x)\,\dot x^\mu\,d\lambda$, so invariance requires $g$ constant. What is left is $-q\int A_\mu\,dx^\mu$: it needs no metric and no clock, and its sign is a convention that fixes the sign of $q$. That the field which couples this way is the electromagnetic potential is then a statement about nature, checked by the force law it produces.
> - *Not a scalar Lagrangian, a scalar action.* In lab time the coupling is $L_{\text{int}} = -qV + q\vec u\cdot\vec A$. Phys505 builds it from the field-side coupling density $-(\rho V - \vec J\cdot\vec A)$ for a point charge, $\rho = q\,\delta^3(\vec x - \vec x(t))$, $\vec J = q\vec u\,\delta^3(\vec x - \vec x(t))$, and checks that $\gamma_u L_{\text{int}} = -q\,u^\mu A_\mu$ is a scalar (Phys505 §7.4.1–7.4.2).
> - *One coupling, two faces.* With the four-current of a point charge on its worldline, $J^\mu(x) = qc\int\delta^4\bigl(x - x(\lambda)\bigr)\,\dot x^\mu\,d\lambda$, whose time component integrates to $J^0 = cq\,\delta^3(\vec x - \vec x(t))$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-4|Def. §B4.2.4]]), the field-side term $-\frac{1}{c}\int J^\mu A_\mu\,d^4x$ collapses to $-q\int A_\mu\,dx^\mu$. One term of the total action, varied with respect to the worldline, gives the Lorentz force; varied with respect to $A_\mu$, it gives the source of Maxwell's equations (Quantum Field Theory C1 — [[§C1a.7 Relativistic Electrodynamics in Index Form#^rem-c1a-7-2|QFT Remark: Electrodynamics as the template for field theory]]; in SI units, Electromagnetism C1 — [[§C1.1 Building the Maxwell Action#^thm-c1-1-6|EM Theorem §C1.1.6]] for the current and its coupling, [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-2|EM Theorem §C1.2.2]] for the source, [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^ex-c1-2-2|EM Example §C1.2.2]] for the worldline variation).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.1, §12.4 (One coupling, two faces) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.4.1–7.4.2, eqs. (7.54)–(7.60)*

^rem-b3-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.2: Lagrangian of a Charge
> In an inertial frame, with the worldline parametrized by $t$, the action of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^pr-b3-2-1|Principle §B3.2.1]] is $S = \int L\,dt$ with
>
> $$
> L(\vec x, \vec u, t) = -mc^2\sqrt{1 - \frac{u^2}{c^2}} - qV(\vec x, t) + q\,\vec u\cdot\vec A(\vec x, t) .
> $$
>
> For $u \ll c$ it reduces, up to the constant $-mc^2$, to the Newtonian Lagrangian $\tfrac12 mu^2 - qV + q\vec u\cdot\vec A$ of Classical Mechanics ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^rem-b6-2-6|CM §B6.2, Remark: Velocity-dependent potentials]]).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.1, eq. (charged Lagrangian) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.4.4, eq. (7.65) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Canonical vs. Mechanical Momentum" (Newtonian form)*

^thm-b3-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $x^\mu = (ct, \vec x)$, $dx^\mu = (c\,dt, \vec u\,dt)$, and $A_\mu = (V/c, -\vec A)$ ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-4|Def. §B1.1.4]]),
>
> $$
> A_\mu\,dx^\mu = \frac{V}{c}\,c\,dt - \vec A\cdot\vec u\,dt = (V - \vec u\cdot\vec A)\,dt ,
> $$
>
> so $-q\int A_\mu\,dx^\mu = \int(-qV + q\vec u\cdot\vec A)\,dt$. The free term is [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]], and its expansion [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]] gives the limit.

*Uses:* [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^pr-b3-2-1|Principle §B3.2.1]], [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]], [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]], [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-4|Def. §B1.1.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.3: Gauge Invariance of the Coupling
> Under a gauge transformation of the potential by an arbitrary smooth function $\chi(x)$,
>
> $$
> A_\mu \to A_\mu + \partial_\mu\chi, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad V \to V + \frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}, \quad \vec A \to \vec A - \nabla\chi ,
> $$
>
> the action changes only by an end-point term, $S \to S - q\bigl[\chi(x_B) - \chi(x_A)\bigr]$, and the Lagrangian by a total derivative, $L \to L - q\,d\chi/dt$. The equations of motion are unchanged.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.2, eq. (gauge transformation) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The Four-Potential"*

^thm-b3-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The change of the coupling is $-q\int\partial_\mu\chi\,dx^\mu = -q\int d\chi = -q[\chi(x_B) - \chi(x_A)]$, by the chain rule along the worldline and the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]). The end points are fixed in the variation, so $\delta S$ and with it the stationary worldlines are the same. In lab time, $-q\,\Delta V + q\vec u\cdot\Delta\vec A = -q(\partial_t\chi + \vec u\cdot\nabla\chi) = -q\,d\chi/dt$, the case of equivalent Lagrangians ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^thm-b6-2-4|CM Theorem §B6.2.4]]). The components follow from $\partial_\mu = (\partial_t/c, \nabla)$: $V/c \to V/c + \partial_t\chi/c$ and $-\vec A \to -\vec A + \nabla\chi$.

## The Lorentz force

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.4: The Lorentz Force from the Action
> The Lagrangian of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]] gives the canonical momentum and equation of motion
>
> $$
> \vec P = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec u} = \gamma_u m\,\vec u + q\vec A, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\gamma_u m\,\vec u\bigr) = q\bigl(\vec E + \vec u\times\vec B\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> where the two combinations of the potentials
>
> $$
> \vec E = -\nabla V - \frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t}, \qquad \vec B = \nabla\times\vec A
> $$
>
> are gauge invariant. The energy of the particle changes as
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\gamma_u mc^2\bigr) = q\,\vec E\cdot\vec u ,
> $$
>
> so the magnetic force does no work ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-2|EM Theorem §B6.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.3, eqs. (canonical vs mechanical), (force raw), (E, B definitions), (Lorentz force) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.4.6–7.4.7, eqs. (7.80)–(7.98)*

^thm-b3-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Momentum.* The free part gives $\gamma_u m\vec u$ ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]) and $q\vec u\cdot\vec A$ gives $q\vec A$.
>
> *Force side.* $\vec u$ is an independent argument of $L$, so the gradient acts on the fields only: $\partial L/\partial\vec x = -q\nabla V + q\nabla(\vec u\cdot\vec A)$.
>
> *Time derivative along the path.* $\vec A$ is evaluated at the moving particle, so by the chain rule $d\vec A/dt = \partial_t\vec A + (\vec u\cdot\nabla)\vec A$, the convective derivative. Lagrange's equation $d\vec P/dt = \partial L/\partial\vec x$ ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|CM Theorem §B6.1.4]]) becomes
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}(\gamma_u m\vec u) = -q\Bigl(\nabla V + \frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t}\Bigr) + q\bigl[\nabla(\vec u\cdot\vec A) - (\vec u\cdot\nabla)\vec A\bigr] .
> $$
>
> For $\vec u$ held fixed, the product rule $\nabla(\vec u\cdot\vec A) = \vec u\times(\nabla\times\vec A) + (\vec u\cdot\nabla)\vec A$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|EM Theorem §B1.1.1]] (ii)) turns the bracket into $\vec u\times(\nabla\times\vec A)$, which gives the stated law.
>
> *Gauge invariance.* Under [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]], $\vec E \to \vec E - \nabla\partial_t\chi + \partial_t\nabla\chi = \vec E$ and $\vec B \to \vec B - \nabla\times\nabla\chi = \vec B$, by equality of mixed partials ([[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]; [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|EM Theorem §B1.1.2]]).
>
> *Energy.* $\gamma_u mc^2 = \sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}$ with $\vec p = \gamma_u m\vec u$, and $\partial\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}/\partial\vec p = \vec u$ ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]]). So $d(\gamma_u mc^2)/dt = \vec u\cdot d\vec p/dt = q\,\vec u\cdot\vec E + q\,\vec u\cdot(\vec u\times\vec B) = q\,\vec E\cdot\vec u$.

^der-b3-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]], [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]], [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|CM Theorem §B6.1.4]], [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-1|EM Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from Electromagnetism and level A
> - **Layer.** In Electromagnetism the Lorentz force is a principle ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|EM Principle §B6.1.1]], [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|EM Principle §B8.4.2]]), and its validity at all speeds was part of its stated domain. Here it is a theorem of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^pr-b3-2-1|Principle §B3.2.1]], and the relativistic reading, force as $d(\gamma_u m\vec u)/dt$ ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-2|Def. §A3.1.2]]), comes out rather than being imposed: the free term of the action fixes what the force acts on.
> - **The fields are outputs.** $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ were not put in. The variational principle grouped the derivatives of the potentials into exactly these two gauge-invariant combinations, as it must, since a gauge transformation does not change the equations of motion. The same relations $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A$, $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$ are where Electromagnetism introduces the potentials ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-1|EM Theorem §B11.1.1]]); Electromagnetism also rewrites the Newtonian force law with the canonical momentum ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^rem-b11-1-3|EM Remark: The Lorentz force in terms of potentials]]), and the relativistic version is the Lagrange equation above, $d\vec P/dt = -\nabla[q(V - \vec u\cdot\vec A)]$ with $\vec u$ held fixed under the gradient.
> - **Velocity-dependent potentials.** The vector potential enters $L$ through $q\vec u\cdot\vec A$, a generalized potential; Classical Mechanics met it at low speed ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^rem-b6-4-4|CM §B6.4, Remark: Velocity-dependent potentials]]), where it drops out of the energy function for the same reason the magnetic force does no work here.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.3 · Griffiths §12.2.4, p. 545*

^rem-b3-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.5: The Covariant Lorentz Force
> Varying the worldline in [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^pr-b3-2-1|Principle §B3.2.1]] without choosing a frame gives
>
> $$
> \frac{dp^\mu}{d\tau} = m\,\frac{du^\mu}{d\tau} = q\,F^{\mu\nu}u_\nu = q\,F^\mu{}_\nu\,u^\nu, \qquad F_{\mu\nu} \equiv \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu ,
> $$
>
> with $F$ the electromagnetic field tensor ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-2|Def. §B4.2.2]]), whose components are $F^{0i} = -E^i/c$, $F^{ij} = -\epsilon_{ijk}B^k$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]]). In components, with $p^0 = \gamma_u mc$ and $d/d\tau = \gamma_u\,d/dt$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dp^0}{d\tau} = \frac{q\gamma_u}{c}\,\vec E\cdot\vec u, \qquad \frac{d\vec p}{d\tau} = q\gamma_u\bigl(\vec E + \vec u\times\vec B\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> which are $\gamma_u$ times the energy equation and the force law of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]]. The equation is consistent with $u\cdot u = c^2$: $u_\mu\,dp^\mu/d\tau = qF^{\mu\nu}u_\mu u_\nu = 0$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.3 (Route 2), eq. (covariant Lorentz force) · Griffiths §12.3.4, eqs. (12.128)–(12.129) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.2.2, eq. (6.119)*

^thm-b3-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Variation of the coupling.* With $S_{\text{int}} = -q\int A_\mu(x)\,\dot x^\mu\,d\lambda$ and $\delta A_\mu = \partial_\nu A_\mu\,\delta x^\nu$,
>
> $$
> \delta S_{\text{int}} = -q\int\bigl[\partial_\nu A_\mu\,\dot x^\mu\,\delta x^\nu + A_\nu\,\delta\dot x^\nu\bigr]d\lambda = -q\int\Bigl[\partial_\nu A_\mu\,\dot x^\mu - \frac{dA_\nu}{d\lambda}\Bigr]\delta x^\nu\,d\lambda = -q\int F_{\nu\mu}\,\dot x^\mu\,\delta x^\nu\,d\lambda ,
> $$
>
> integrating the second term by parts (end points fixed) and using the chain rule $dA_\nu/d\lambda = \partial_\mu A_\nu\,\dot x^\mu$, so that the bracket is $(\partial_\nu A_\mu - \partial_\mu A_\nu)\dot x^\mu = F_{\nu\mu}\dot x^\mu$.
>
> *Free part.* From the derivation of [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], $\delta S_{\text{free}} = m\int(du_\nu/d\lambda)\,\delta x^\nu\,d\lambda$.
>
> *Stationarity.* $\delta S = \int\bigl[m\,du_\nu/d\lambda - qF_{\nu\mu}\dot x^\mu\bigr]\delta x^\nu\,d\lambda = 0$ for all $\delta x^\nu$, so the bracket vanishes ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|CM Lemma §B5.1.2]]). Multiplying by $d\lambda/d\tau$ gives $m\,du_\nu/d\tau = qF_{\nu\mu}u^\mu$, and raising $\nu$ ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]) gives $m\,du^\nu/d\tau = qF^\nu{}_\mu u^\mu = qF^{\nu\mu}u_\mu$.
>
> *Components.* $u_\nu = \gamma_u(c, -u_x, -u_y, -u_z)$. For $\mu = 0$: $F^{0i}u_i = \sum_i(-E_i/c)(-\gamma_uu_i) = \gamma_u\vec E\cdot\vec u/c$, writing $E_i$, $u_i$ for the Cartesian components of $\vec E$, $\vec u$. For $\mu = 1$, with $F^{10} = E_x/c$, $F^{12} = -B_z$, $F^{13} = B_y$: $F^{1\nu}u_\nu = (E_x/c)(\gamma_uc) + (-B_z)(-\gamma_uu_y) + B_y(-\gamma_uu_z) = \gamma_u(E_x + u_yB_z - u_zB_y) = \gamma_u[\vec E + \vec u\times\vec B]_x$, and cyclically for $\mu = 2, 3$. With $dp^0/d\tau = \gamma_u\,d(\gamma_u mc)/dt$ and $d\vec p/d\tau = \gamma_u\,d\vec p/dt$ these are $\gamma_u$ times the equations of Theorem §B3.2.4.
>
> *Consistency.* $F^{\mu\nu}$ is antisymmetric and $u_\mu u_\nu$ symmetric, so their full contraction vanishes ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]]); and $m\,u_\mu\,du^\mu/d\tau = \tfrac12 m\,d(u\cdot u)/d\tau = 0$.

*Uses:* [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^pr-b3-2-1|Principle §B3.2.1]], [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]], [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|CM Lemma §B5.1.2]], [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]], [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: The variation manufactures the field tensor
> Only the antisymmetric part of $\partial_\nu A_\mu$ survives the variation; the symmetric part cancelled between the two terms. $F_{\mu\nu}$ is gauge invariant, since $\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\chi - \partial_\nu\partial_\mu\chi = 0$, as [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]] demanded. Its tensor character comes free: for every four-velocity the force law turns the four-vector $u^\nu$ linearly into the four-vector $m\,du^\mu/d\tau$, so by the quotient rule ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]) $F^\mu{}_\nu$ is a tensor of rank $(1, 1)$. Antisymmetry leaves six independent components, and they are the six numbers $\vec E$, $\vec B$ of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]]: the electric and magnetic fields are one tensor seen in a $3 + 1$ split. The tensor itself, its transformation and its invariants are developed in [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor|§B4.2]].
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.3 (Route 2)*

^rem-b3-2-3

> [!caution] Caution: Index placement and conventions in the force law
> - $F^{\mu\nu}u_\nu$ and $F^\mu{}_\nu u^\nu$ are the same; $F^{\nu\mu}u_\nu$ is minus it. The sign of $F^{0i} = -E^i/c$ follows from $A^\mu = (V/c, \vec A)$, the mostly-minus metric and $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^cau-b4-2-1|§B4.2, Caution: Sign conventions for the field tensor]]).
> - Griffiths writes the same law as $K^\mu = q\,\eta_\nu F^{\mu\nu}$ (eq. (12.128)), with $\eta^\nu$ the proper velocity, the metric $(-, +, +, +)$ and $F^{01} = +E_x/c$ (eq. (12.119)). Both his lowered velocity and his $F^{\mu\nu}$ are the negatives of the ones here, so the product, and the law, agree ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^cau-b1-1-2|§B1.1, Caution: Sign conventions for the metric and the interval]]).
> - The user's series sets $c = 1$ and writes $A^\mu = (\varphi, \vec A)$, the user's PHY 505 notes $A^\mu = (\phi/c, \vec A)$; here the scalar potential is $V$, as in Electromagnetism, and $c$ is explicit.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §12.3.3–12.3.4, eqs. (12.119), (12.128) · the user's series, Part III, §5.1 · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The Four-Potential"*

^cau-b3-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Ordinary force or Minkowski force?
> When a Newtonian force law is generalized, should it give $d\vec p/dt$ or the **Minkowski force** $K^\mu = dp^\mu/d\tau$? Griffiths notes that classical physics cannot decide, since $t$ and $\tau$ agree at low speed, and that the Lorentz force turns out to be an ordinary force (Griffiths §12.2.4, p. 545). The action decides it: [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]] gives $q(\vec E + \vec u\times\vec B)$ as $d\vec p/dt$, and the Minkowski force is $\gamma_u$ times it, $K^\mu = qF^{\mu\nu}u_\nu$ ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-5|Theorem §B3.2.5]]). Phys505 reaches the same four-vector from the force density, $f^\mu = F^{\mu\nu}J_\nu$, whose spatial part is $\rho\vec E + \vec J\times\vec B$ and whose time part is the power density $\vec E\cdot\vec J$ over $c$ (Phys505 §6.2.2). The general relation between three-force and Minkowski force, and how forces transform: [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion|§B3.3]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §12.2.4, eqs. (12.68)–(12.70), p. 545 · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.2.2, eqs. (6.112)–(6.122)*

## Canonical momentum and the Hamiltonian

> [!definition] Definition §B3.2.1: Mechanical and Canonical Momentum
> For a charge in a field, the **mechanical** (kinetic) momentum is $\vec p = \gamma_u m\vec u$, and the **canonical** momentum is the momentum conjugate to $\vec x$,
>
> $$
> \vec P = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\vec u} = \vec p + q\vec A .
> $$
>
> Their four-dimensional versions are $p^\mu = mu^\mu$ and $P^\mu = p^\mu + qA^\mu$, both four-vectors (at the particle's position).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.3, §5.4 · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Canonical vs. Mechanical Momentum" · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.4.4–7.4.5, eqs. (7.66)–(7.68), (7.75)*

^def-b3-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.6: Hamiltonian of a Charge; Minimal Coupling
> The Hamiltonian of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]] is
>
> $$
> H(\vec x, \vec P, t) = \sqrt{\bigl|\vec P - q\vec A(\vec x, t)\bigr|^2c^2 + m^2c^4} + qV(\vec x, t) ,
> $$
>
> whose value is $\gamma_u mc^2 + qV$, and Hamilton's equations are equivalent to the Lorentz force law. With $P^\mu = (H/c, \vec P)$ the relation reads
>
> $$
> (P - qA)\cdot(P - qA) = m^2c^2 :
> $$
>
> the free mass shell ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]]) with the **minimal substitution** $p^\mu \to P^\mu - qA^\mu$, that is, $\vec p \to \vec P - q\vec A$ and $H \to H - qV$. For $|\vec P - q\vec A| \ll mc$, $H \approx mc^2 + |\vec P - q\vec A|^2/2m + qV$, the Hamiltonian of Classical Mechanics ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^rem-b7-1-5|CM Remark: The charged particle]]).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.4, eqs. (minimal coupling), (minimal substitution) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.4.4–7.4.5, eqs. (7.69)–(7.77)*

^thm-b3-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Inversion.* $\vec P - q\vec A = \gamma_u m\vec u$ is the free momentum, so the inversion of [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]] applies with $\vec p = \vec P - q\vec A$ (the Hessian $\partial^2L/\partial u_i\partial u_j$ is the free one, positive definite), giving $\vec u = c^2(\vec P - q\vec A)/\sqrt{|\vec P - q\vec A|^2c^2 + m^2c^4}$.
>
> *Legendre transform* ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-2|CM Def. §B7.1.2]]):
>
> $$
> H = \vec P\cdot\vec u - L = (\gamma_u m\vec u + q\vec A)\cdot\vec u + \frac{mc^2}{\gamma_u} + qV - q\vec u\cdot\vec A = \gamma_u mc^2 + qV ,
> $$
>
> the $q\vec u\cdot\vec A$ terms cancelling, and $\gamma_u mc^2 = \sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}$ with $\vec p = \vec P - q\vec A$. Hamilton's equations are equivalent to Lagrange's ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-2|CM Theorem §B7.1.2]]), hence to [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]].
>
> *Covariant form.* $P^\mu - qA^\mu = \bigl((\gamma_u mc^2 + qV)/c - qV/c,\ \gamma_u m\vec u\bigr) = (\gamma_u mc, \gamma_u m\vec u) = p^\mu$, and $p\cdot p = m^2c^2$ ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]]). The non-relativistic form is [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]] with $p \to |\vec P - q\vec A|$.

> [!remark] Remark: Canonical versus mechanical momentum
> The two momenta divide the work:
>
> | | mechanical $\vec p = \gamma_u m\vec u$ | canonical $\vec P = \vec p + q\vec A$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | under a gauge transformation | invariant | shifts by $-q\nabla\chi$ |
> | equation of motion | $d\vec p/dt = q(\vec E + \vec u\times\vec B)$ | $d\vec P/dt = -q\nabla V + q\nabla(\vec u\cdot\vec A)$ |
> | conserved | — | $P_i$, if $V$ and $\vec A$ do not depend on $x_i$ in some gauge ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-1\|CM Theorem §B6.4.1]]) |
> | role | measured (e.g. by the radius $p/\lvert q\rvert B$ in a magnetic field) | the phase-space variable of $H$ |
>
> Neither does the other's job: the measurable, gauge-invariant $\vec p$ is not conserved even when the fields are symmetric, and the conserved $\vec P$ is gauge dependent. The user's PHY 505 notes say that the difference $q\vec A$ "represents the momentum stored in the electromagnetic field". That holds under specific conditions: for static fields of localized sources, with $\vec A$ in the Coulomb gauge, $q\vec A$ is the charge's share of the field momentum ([[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^thm-b9-2-4|EM Theorem §B9.2.4]], [[§B9.2 Momentum and Angular Momentum of Fields#^rem-b9-2-4|EM Remark: Canonical momentum, and Feynman's disk paradox]]). In general $q\vec A$ is gauge dependent, while the field momentum $\varepsilon_0\int\vec E\times\vec B\,d\tau$ is not, so the identification is not gauge independent. The substitution $\vec p \to \vec P - q\vec A$ is the rule quantum mechanics inherits for a charge in a field ($\hat{\vec p} \to \hat{\vec p} - q\vec A$; Quantum Mechanics level C — [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^pr-c4-3-2|QM Principle §C4.3.2]], [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-5|QM Theorem §C4.3.5]]; for a relativistic spinless particle, [[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^pr-c13-1-3|QM Principle §C13.1.3]]), and quantum field theory derives it from gauge symmetry.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.4 (table) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Canonical vs. Mechanical Momentum" · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §7.4.4, note after eq. (7.77)*

> [!caution] Caution: Three ways to write a gauge transformation
> These notes write $A_\mu \to A_\mu + \partial_\mu\chi$, with the user's series: $V \to V + \partial_t\chi$, $\vec A \to \vec A - \nabla\chi$. Electromagnetism follows Griffiths, $\vec A \to \vec A + \nabla\lambda$, $V \to V - \partial_t\lambda$ ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-2|EM Theorem §B11.1.2]]), so $\lambda = -\chi$. The user's PHY 505 notes write $A^\mu \to A^\mu + \partial^\mu\Lambda$, which in components is again $V \to V + \partial_t\Lambda$, $\vec A \to \vec A - \nabla\Lambda$, so $\Lambda = \chi$. All three are the same freedom.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.2 · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The Four-Potential" · Griffiths §10.1.3 (via EM §B11.1★)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.2.1: A Charge in a Uniform Magnetic Field, Gauge by Gauge
> A charge $q > 0$ moves in the uniform field $\vec B = B\hat z$ ($B > 0$, no electric field). Use the canonical momentum to find the motion, and see what a change of gauge does.
>
> *Landau gauge.* $V = 0$, $\vec A = Bx\,\hat y$ gives $\nabla\times\vec A = B\hat z$. Then $L = -mc^2/\gamma_u + qBx\,u_y$ ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]) does not contain $y$, $z$ or $t$, so ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-1|CM Theorem §B6.4.1]], [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-6|Theorem §B3.2.6]])
>
> $$
> P_y = \gamma_u m u_y + qBx, \qquad P_z = \gamma_u m u_z, \qquad H = \gamma_u mc^2
> $$
>
> are conserved. The last says that $\gamma_u$, hence the speed, is constant. Define $x_c = P_y/qB$; then $\gamma_u m\,u_y = -qB(x - x_c)$. The orbit is the helix of [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-6|Theorem §B3.3.6]], whose projection on the $xy$ plane is a circle of radius $p_\perp/qB$ traversed at $\omega = qB/\gamma_u m$; on such a circle $u_y$ vanishes exactly at the two points whose abscissa is that of the centre, and here $u_y = 0$ exactly at $x = x_c$. The conserved canonical momentum $P_y$ is therefore the abscissa of the guiding centre, in units of $qB$.
>
> *Another gauge.* $\chi = -Bay$ ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]]) gives $\vec A' = \vec A - \nabla\chi = B(x + a)\hat y$, the same $\vec B$. Now $P_y' = \gamma_u mu_y + qB(x + a) = P_y + qBa$: the conserved number has changed, as the table of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^rem-b3-2-5|Remark: Canonical versus mechanical momentum]] says it must. The physics has not: $u_y = 0$ now at $x = P_y'/qB - a = x_c$, the same centre. In the symmetric gauge $\vec A = \tfrac12 B(-y, x, 0)$ neither $x$ nor $y$ is cyclic, but $L$ is invariant under rotations about $\hat z$, and Noether's theorem ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-2|CM Theorem §B6.4.2]]) conserves the canonical angular momentum $(\vec x\times\vec P)_z = (\vec x\times\vec p)_z + \tfrac12 qB(x^2 + y^2)$ instead. Which components of $\vec P$ are conserved depends on the gauge; the orbit does not. Why the fall of $\omega = qB/\gamma_u m$ with energy makes a fixed-frequency cyclotron fail: [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^rem-b3-3-5|§B3.3, Remark: Consequences for accelerators and detectors]].
>
> *Source: worked here, from the user's series, Part III, §5.4 (table: canonical momentum as Noether charge, its gauge behaviour) and §5.3, Example (the LHC proton, bent: $r = p/qB$)*

> [!example] Example §B3.2.2: A Uniform Electric Field, Solved Covariantly
> A charge $q$ starts from rest in the uniform static field $\vec E = E\hat x$, $\vec B = \vec 0$. Solve [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-5|Theorem §B3.2.5]] directly.
>
> Take $V = -Ex$, $\vec A = \vec 0$, so $A_0 = -Ex/c$ and the only nonzero components are $F_{01} = -F_{10} = \partial_0A_1 - \partial_1A_0 = E/c$. Raising one index, $F^0{}_1 = \eta^{00}F_{01} = E/c$ and $F^1{}_0 = \eta^{11}F_{10} = E/c$. With $\alpha = qE/m$, an acceleration,
>
> $$
> \frac{du^0}{d\tau} = \frac{\alpha}{c}\,u^1, \qquad \frac{du^1}{d\tau} = \frac{\alpha}{c}\,u^0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{d}{d\tau}\bigl(u^0 \pm u^1\bigr) = \pm\frac{\alpha}{c}\,\bigl(u^0 \pm u^1\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The null combinations $u^0 \pm u^1$, which diagonalize a boost ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-7|Theorem §B1.2.7]]), decouple the equations. From $u^\mu(0) = (c, 0)$, $u^0 \pm u^1 = c\,e^{\pm\alpha\tau/c}$, so
>
> $$
> u^0 = c\cosh\frac{\alpha\tau}{c}, \qquad u^1 = c\sinh\frac{\alpha\tau}{c}, \qquad \frac{u}{c} = \tanh\frac{\alpha\tau}{c} :
> $$
>
> the rapidity ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-3|Def. §B1.2.3]]) grows linearly with proper time, $\varphi = \alpha\tau/c = qE\tau/mc$, and $u/c = \tanh\varphi$ approaches $1$ without reaching it. The four-acceleration $a^\mu = du^\mu/d\tau = \alpha(\sinh\varphi, \cosh\varphi)$ is orthogonal to $u^\mu$ and has constant magnitude $\sqrt{-a\cdot a} = \alpha = qE/m$: the charge has constant proper acceleration ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^def-b2-1-2|Def. §B2.1.2]]). *Check against the three-vector law:* $ct = \int u^0\,d\tau = (c^2/\alpha)\sinh\varphi$, so $p_x = mu^1 = mc\sinh\varphi = qEt$, which is $dp_x/dt = qE$ of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]]. This is the uniformly accelerated particle of [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^mod-b3-3-4|Model §B3.3.4]]; its hyperbolic worldline $(x + c^2/\alpha)^2 - (ct)^2 = (c^2/\alpha)^2$, with $c^2/\alpha = mc^2/qE$, and its horizon are [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]] and [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^rem-b3-3-4|§B3.3, Remark: A horizon, and where this leads]].
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §5.3, Example (Constant field: hyperbolic motion)*
