Relativity starts from an old question: two observers move uniformly relative to each other and watch the same events. How are their descriptions related, and do they find the same laws of physics? Newtonian mechanics gives a clean answer, the Galilean transformation together with the principle that mechanics is the same in every inertial frame. This section sets up the language of events, frames and transformations, and shows where the classical answer fails: electromagnetism.

## Events and frames

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.1: Event; Transformation
> An **event** is something that happens at a definite point of space and at a definite instant: a flash of light, a collision, a hand clap. It is idealised as point-like and instantaneous. A reference frame $S$ (a set of axes with an origin, [[§A2.1 Position, Displacement, Velocity#^def-a2-1-1|CM Def. §A2.1.1]], together with clocks that time what happens) labels the event by four **coordinates** $(x, y, z, t)$; another frame $S'$ labels the same event by $(x', y', z', t')$. A **transformation** between the frames is the rule that gives $(x', y', z', t')$ in terms of $(x, y, z, t)$ for every event.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.5 · Tipler §1-2 · Morin §1.1.1*

^def-a1-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Events belong to no frame
> - The event happens, independently of any frame; only its coordinates are frame-dependent. "The explosion of the star" is one event, whose coordinates two observers may report differently.
> - A train at one instant is not an event (it has no single position), and neither is a pebble lying on the ground for a minute (no single time). A point of the train at an instant, or the pebble at an instant, is.
> - The coordinates of a single event depend on the arbitrary choice of origin, so the physically meaningful statements are about coordinate *differences* $\Delta x, \Delta t$ between two events.
> - Special relativity deals only with **inertial** frames ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-2|CM Def. §A3.2.2]]): that is what "special" means. Accelerated frames and gravity are the subject of general relativity (level C, planned).
>
> *Source: Morin §1.1.1 · UP3 §5.1*

^rem-a1-1-1

## The Galilean transformation

> [!model] Model §A1.1.1: Newtonian Absolute Time and Space
> Classical mechanics assumes, without usually saying so:
> - **Absolute time.** Clocks in all frames, once set to agree, agree forever: $t' = t$. Hence all observers agree on which events are simultaneous and on the time interval between any two events.
> - **Absolute lengths.** The distance between two points measured at the same instant (the two ends of a rod, say) is the same in all frames.
>
> Together with Euclidean geometry this makes positions, and so velocities, add as vectors ([[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|CM Theorem §A2.8.1]]).
>
> *Valid when: all speeds are much smaller than $c$; it is the low-speed limit of the Lorentz transformation ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation|§A1.4]]). It fails at speeds comparable to $c$, where neither simultaneity nor length is absolute ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity|§A1.3]]).*
> *Source: UP3 §5.5 · Tipler §1-1 · PHY 284, class 2 slides*

^mod-a1-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.1.2: Galilean Transformation
> Let $S'$ move with constant velocity $v$ along the $+x$ axis of $S$, with parallel axes and origins that coincide at $t = 0$ (the standard configuration, [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^def-a1-4-1|Def. §A1.4.1]]). In the model of absolute time and space, an event with coordinates $(x, y, z, t)$ in $S$ has, in $S'$,
>
> $$
> x' = x - vt, \qquad y' = y, \qquad z' = z, \qquad t' = t ,
> $$
>
> and conversely $x = x' + vt'$, $y = y'$, $z = z'$, $t = t'$. For two events, $\Delta x' = \Delta x - v\,\Delta t$ and $\Delta t' = \Delta t$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.5 · Tipler §1-1, eq. (1-2)*

^thm-a1-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By absolute time both frames use the same $t$. At time $t$ the origin $O'$ of $S'$ is at $x = vt$ in $S$. The position of the event relative to $O$ is the position of $O'$ relative to $O$ plus the position of the event relative to $O'$ ([[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|CM Theorem §A2.8.1]]); lengths being absolute, the last one is the $x'$ that $S'$ measures. So $x = vt + x'$. The frames do not move relative to each other along $y$ and $z$, and their axes are parallel, so $y' = y$ and $z' = z$. Seen from $S'$, the frame $S$ moves with velocity $-v$, which gives the inverse by replacing $v$ with $-v$. Subtracting the relations for two events gives the differences.

^der-a1-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.1.3: Galilean Velocity and Acceleration Transformation
> In the setting of [[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]], a particle with velocity $\vec u$ in $S$ has velocity $\vec u\,'$ in $S'$ with
>
> $$
> u'_x = u_x - v, \qquad u'_y = u_y, \qquad u'_z = u_z \qquad \bigl(\vec u\,' = \vec u - \vec v\bigr),
> $$
>
> and the same acceleration in both frames, $\vec a\,' = \vec a$. (Throughout Relativity, $u$ is a particle's velocity and $v$ the velocity of one frame relative to another.)
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.5 · Tipler §1-1, eq. (1-3)*

^thm-a1-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate the transformation along the particle's path. Because $t' = t$, the time increments are the same, $dt' = dt$, so $u'_x = dx'/dt' = d(x - vt)/dt = u_x - v$, and $u'_y = u_y$, $u'_z = u_z$. Differentiating once more, $v$ being constant, gives $a'_x = a_x$, $a'_y = a_y$, $a'_z = a_z$. This is the classical addition of relative velocities, [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|CM Theorem §A2.8.1]], with $\vec v_{S'S} = v\,\hat\imath$, and the equality of accelerations of [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-2|CM Theorem §A2.8.2]].

^der-a1-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]], [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|CM Theorem §A2.8.1]], [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-2|CM Theorem §A2.8.2]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]] (derivative of a sum)

> [!remark] Remark: The Galilean transformation as a shear
> Plot the history of things in a frame as lines in the $x$–$t$ plane (the spacetime diagrams of [[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval|§A1.5]]). Passing from $S$ to $S'$ slides every event sideways by $-vt$, more the later it is, and leaves its time alone: the plane is sheared, not rotated. What survives the shear is what classical physics treats as absolute: the time interval between any two events, the distance between simultaneous events, velocity *differences* between bodies, and accelerations. Relativity will keep the idea of a linear change of coordinates but replace the shear ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^rem-a1-5-2|Remark in §A1.5]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 2 slides*

## Newtonian relativity

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.1.4: Newtonian Principle of Relativity
> Newton's laws of motion have the same form in all inertial frames related by Galilean transformations: if $\sum\vec F = m\vec a$ holds in $S$, then $\sum\vec F\,' = m\vec a\,'$ holds in $S'$, with $\vec F\,' = \vec F$ and $\vec a\,' = \vec a$. Any frame moving uniformly relative to an inertial frame is inertial, and no experiment in mechanics done inside a frame can reveal the frame's uniform motion.
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-1 · PHY 284, Tipler supplement "Classical relativity" · UP3 §5.5*

^thm-a1-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]] both frames measure the same acceleration for every body. Masses are the same in both frames (in Newtonian mechanics mass is a property of the body). Forces are interactions between bodies and depend on their separations and relative velocities; at equal times separations are the same in both frames ($\Delta x' = \Delta x$ when $\Delta t = 0$) and relative velocities are too ($\vec u_1' - \vec u_2' = \vec u_1 - \vec u_2$), so $\vec F\,' = \vec F$. Then $\sum\vec F\,' = \sum\vec F = m\vec a = m\vec a\,'$. With zero net force this is the first law in both frames, which is [[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^thm-a3-2-2|CM Theorem §A3.2.2]].

^der-a1-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^thm-a3-2-2|CM Theorem §A3.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: How fast are we moving?
> A lecture room moves with Earth's rotation (about $460\ \mathrm{m/s}$ at the equator, less at higher latitude), with Earth's orbit ($30\ \mathrm{km/s}$), with the Sun around the galactic centre ($230\ \mathrm{km/s}$), and with the Local Group of galaxies relative to the cosmic background radiation ($627\ \mathrm{km/s}$). None of this is felt, and juggling works the same on a smoothly moving train as on the platform: only *changes* of velocity are noticed. A mass hanging from the roof of a boxcar hangs vertically whether the car is at rest or moving uniformly, but tilts while the car speeds up; the passenger then has to invent a pseudoforce, the mark of a non-inertial frame ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^def-a3-6-3|CM Def. §A3.6.3]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 1 slides · Tipler §1-1, Fig. 1-2*

^rem-a1-1-3

> [!caution] Caution: Invariant laws, not invariant numbers
> Relativity of motion does not mean that observers agree on everything. A sailboat moving at $1.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$ toward the northeast relative to a dock moves, relative to a motorboat going east at $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$, at $2.2\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in a direction $61^\circ$ west of north. Velocities, momenta and kinetic energies differ between frames; both values are correct. What is the same in every inertial frame is the *form* of the laws.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Tipler supplement "Classical relativity", Example CR-1*

## Where electromagnetism breaks it

> [!remark] Remark: Maxwell's equations are not Galilean invariant
> - Maxwell's equations ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|EM Principle §A12.1.3]]) predict electromagnetic waves in vacuum travelling at $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 3.00\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$ ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|EM Theorem §A12.2.4]]), with no mention of a frame. By [[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]] a pulse moving at $c$ along $+x$ in $S$ moves at $c - v$ in $S'$. So if the Galilean transformation is right, Maxwell's equations can hold in at most one frame.
> - An observer who could ride along with a light wave at speed $c$ would see electric and magnetic fields that vary in space but not in time. Maxwell's equations allow no such field in empty space. Einstein concluded that the equations are right and that no material observer can move at $c$.
> - A line of charge at rest in $S$ exerts only an electric force on a charge at rest beside it. In a frame $S'$ moving along the line, both move, and Maxwell's theory adds a magnetic force: the forces in the two frames differ, although by [[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]] the accelerations should agree. What one observer calls magnetic, another calls electric ([[§A9.3 Motional EMF#^rem-a9-3-3|EM Remark in §A9.3]], [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^rem-a12-1-7|EM Remark in §A12.1]]).
>
> Three ways out were open: Maxwell's equations are wrong; they hold only in one preferred frame, the frame of a medium for light ([[§A1.2 The Michelson–Morley Experiment|§A1.2]]); or the Galilean transformation is wrong ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity|§A1.3]]). Nineteenth-century physics chose the second; Einstein chose the third.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.1 · Tipler §1-1, Fig. 1-4 · Morin §1.1.1 · PHY 284, class 2 slides*

^rem-a1-1-4

## Example

> [!example] Example §A1.1.1: A Probe Faster than Light?
> A spaceship moving toward a planet at $2.3\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$ launches a probe toward the planet at $2.1\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$ relative to the ship. With [[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]], the probe's speed relative to the planet is
>
> $$
> u = 2.3\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s} + 2.1\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s} = 4.4\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx 1.5\,c .
> $$
>
> If light moves at $c$ relative to the planet, the probe outruns the light it reflects, and astronomers on the planet see it only after it has landed. If instead the light from the probe moved at $c + u$ (Galilean addition applied to light), Maxwell's equations would fail in the planet's frame. Either way, Galilean addition and a universal speed of light cannot both hold. The relativistic rule ([[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation|§A2.3]]) gives $0.95c$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 1 (instructor's solution checked)*

^ex-a1-1-1

Allowing a shift of origin makes it affine. The Lorentz transformation of [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation|§A1.4]] is again linear, but no longer a shear.
