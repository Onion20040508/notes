Faced with the conflict between Galilean mechanics and electromagnetism ([[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^rem-a1-1-4|Remark in §A1.1]]) and with the missing ether wind ([[§A1.2 The Michelson–Morley Experiment#^law-a1-2-4|Law §A1.2.4]]), Einstein in 1905 kept the relativity principle, extended it to all of physics, added that light has the same speed for every inertial observer, and gave up absolute time instead. Special relativity is built on these two postulates. Their first casualty is the notion that two distant events happen "at the same time" for everyone.

## The two postulates

> [!principle] Principle §A1.3.1: The Principle of Relativity (First Postulate)
> The laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames of reference. No experiment of any kind (mechanical, electromagnetic, optical, …) done inside an inertial frame can reveal a state of absolute rest or absolute uniform motion; only motion relative to other bodies or frames has a meaning.
>
> *Domain: inertial frames ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-2|CM Def. §A3.2.2]]) and all laws of physics. Gravity and accelerated frames are outside special relativity (general relativity, level C, planned).*
> *Source: UP3 §5.1 · Tipler §1-2 · Morin §1.2*

^pr-a1-3-1

> [!principle] Principle §A1.3.2: Constancy of the Speed of Light (Second Postulate)
> Light travels in vacuum with the same speed $c$, in every direction, in all inertial frames, whatever the motion of its source:
>
> $$
> c = 2.99792458\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx 3.00\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> *Domain: light (and every electromagnetic wave) in vacuum, measured in inertial frames.*
> *Source: UP3 §5.1 · Tipler §1-2 · Morin §1.2 · value: [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^rem-a4-1-1|WO Remark in §A4.1]]*

^pr-a1-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: What the postulates say, and what they do not
> - **The first postulate** is the Newtonian relativity of [[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^thm-a1-1-4|Theorem §A1.1.4]] extended from mechanics to all of physics, Maxwell's equations included. It does not say that the Galilean transformation holds: that is exactly what has to go. It also implies that space is homogeneous and isotropic (no point and no direction is special), and that $S$ sees $S'$ exactly as $S'$ sees $S$ with the direction of motion reversed. Laws that fail this test are not laws: momentum and energy will have to be redefined to pass it ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum|§A3.1]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy|§A3.2]]).
> - **The second postulate** in Einstein's form says only that the speed of light does not depend on the motion of the source, as for every wave in a medium. Combined with the first it gives the form above: an observer $R_2$ moving toward a source can, by the first postulate, regard herself as at rest and the source as moving, and then by the second she measures $c$, just as an observer $R_1$ at rest relative to the source does. The new element is that light needs no medium.
> - Taken alone, the second postulate is not implied by the first: light could have behaved like a thrown ball. What the theory really needs is that *some* speed is the same in all frames; it happens to be the speed of light, and the theory would be the same if nothing actually travelled at it.
> - **Evidence.** The null result of Michelson and Morley ([[§A1.2 The Michelson–Morley Experiment#^law-a1-2-4|Law §A1.2.4]]); the absence of ghost images of binary stars; source independence verified to one part in $10^{20}$ with gamma-ray bursts; and every particle accelerator, whose design relies on the consequences of both postulates.
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-2 · Morin §1.2 · UP3 §5.1*

^rem-a1-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Einstein's route
> Einstein did not set out to explain the Michelson–Morley experiment. He started from electromagnetism: Maxwell's equations do not allow a light wave at rest ([[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^rem-a1-1-4|Remark in §A1.1]]), and what is electric for one observer is magnetic for another, so the equations should hold in every frame and fix one speed $c$ for all. His 1905 paper mentions the ether experiments only in passing, and he later could not recall whether he had known the details of Michelson's result.
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-2 · UP3 §5.1*

^rem-a1-3-2

> [!caution] Caution: Closing speeds can exceed $c$
> The postulate concerns the speed of light measured by each inertial observer. Within one frame, the rate at which the gap between a light pulse and a moving receiver closes is $c + v$ or $c - v$; this is no one's speed and contradicts nothing. Two bodies moving apart at $0.9c$ each, as seen from the ground, separate at $1.8c$ in the ground frame. What no observer ever measures is a body (or signal) passing *him* faster than $c$.
>
> *Source: Morin §1.3.1*

^cau-a1-3-1

## Clocks, observers and simultaneity

> [!model] Model §A1.3.3: An Inertial Observer as a Lattice of Rods and Clocks
> An inertial frame is realised by:
> - a cubic lattice of identical measuring rods (metre sticks, or microns, or kilometres, as the problem requires), at rest in the frame; they are ideal rods in the sense of [[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^mod-a2-2-1|Model §A2.2.1]];
> - an identical clock at every lattice point, all synchronized ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^def-a1-3-1|Def. §A1.3.1]]); they are ideal clocks in the sense of [[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^mod-a2-5-1|Model §A2.5.1]];
> - every event is recorded by the clock at (or nearest to) its location, so that its coordinates $(x, y, z, t)$ involve no light-travel delay.
>
> The **observer** of special relativity is this whole recording system, not a person at one point who looks at distant events.
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-2, Fig. 1-13*

^mod-a1-3-3

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.1: Synchronized Clocks; Simultaneous Events
> Clocks at rest in one inertial frame are **synchronized** by light: a reference clock at the origin starts at $t = 0$ and emits a flash at that instant; a clock at distance $d$ is set to read $d/c$ when the flash arrives. Two events are **simultaneous in that frame** if they have the same time coordinate on such clocks; equivalently, if light signals from them reach the point midway between them at the same time.
>
> *Source: Tipler §1-2*

^def-a1-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: When it happens is not when it is seen
> An event's time coordinate is the reading of the local clock *at* the event. A person who sees a distant flash at time $t_{\text{seen}}$ infers that it happened at $t_{\text{seen}} - d/c$. All the statements about simultaneity below are about when events happen, after this correction, never about the different delays with which light reaches different observers.
>
> *Source: Morin §1.3.1 · Tipler §1-2*

^cau-a1-3-2

## Relativity of simultaneity

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.3.4: Relativity of Simultaneity
> Two events at different places on the $x$ axis that are simultaneous in an inertial frame $S$ are not simultaneous in a frame $S'$ moving relative to $S$ along $x$: in $S'$ the event that lies ahead, in the direction of $S'$'s motion, happens first. Equivalently, clocks synchronized in one frame are not synchronized in the other. Events at the same place and the same time (coincidences) are simultaneous in every frame.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.2 · Tipler §1-2 · Morin §1.3.1*

^thm-a1-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Einstein's train. A train ($S'$) moves with speed $v$ along $+x$ past a platform ($S$). Lightning strikes the front and the rear end of the train, leaving marks on train and platform, at the same time in $S$, at the instant when the midpoint $C'$ of the train passes a platform observer $C$. In $S$, let $\ell$ be the distance from $C$ to each strike.
> - **In $S$.** Each flash travels at $c$ (second postulate). Both reach $C$, who is at rest midway, at the same time $\ell/c$. Meanwhile $C'$ moves forward: the front flash meets it when $\ell - ct = vt$, at $t_F = \ell/(c + v)$, and the rear flash when $-\ell + ct = vt$, at $t_R = \ell/(c - v) > t_F$.
> - **Coincidences are absolute.** "The front flash reaches $C'$" and "the rear flash reaches $C'$" are events at one place, and their order is a fact all observers agree on (a clock at $C'$ could be stopped by the first flash). So the front flash reaches $C'$ first in $S'$ too.
> - **In $S'$.** $C'$ is at rest midway between the marks on the train. (It is the midpoint in $S$ as well: front and rear half move alike, and nothing distinguishes them.) By the second postulate both flashes travel at $c$ in $S'$ and cover equal distances. Since the front flash arrives first, it was emitted first: the strikes are not simultaneous in $S'$, and the front one, ahead in the direction of $S'$'s motion, comes first.
>
> If the two events coincide in space ($\ell = 0$), $t_F = t_R$ and the argument gives no difference. Clocks at the two ends of the train, synchronized in $S'$, therefore cannot read alike at one instant of $S$: they are not synchronized in $S$.

> [!remark] Remark: Why this never shows with thrown balls
> Replace the flashes by two balls thrown from the middle of the car toward the ends, each at speed $u$ relative to the train. On the ground they move at $u + v$ and $u - v$; the gaps to the receding front and approaching rear close at $(u + v) - v = u$ and $(u - v) + v = u$, so both balls arrive together in either frame. With light the ground speeds are both $c$, the closing speeds are $c - v$ and $c + v$, and the arrivals separate. The relativity of simultaneity is a direct consequence of the second postulate.
>
> *Source: Morin §1.3.1*

^rem-a1-3-3

> [!remark] Remark: Both are right
> The platform observer says the strikes were simultaneous; the passenger says the front one came first. Neither is mistaken and neither frame is preferred (first postulate): "simultaneous" for distant events is a statement relative to a frame, like "at rest". How large the effect is follows from the Lorentz transformation ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-2|Theorem §A1.4.2]]): it is of order $v\,\Delta x/c^2$, invisible in daily life. It does not threaten cause and effect, because events that can influence each other keep their order in every frame ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]). Most apparent paradoxes of relativity dissolve once simultaneity is handled correctly ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes|§A2.5]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.2 · Tipler §1-2*

^rem-a1-3-4

## Example

> [!example] Example §A1.3.1: A Flash in the Middle of a Car
> Points $A'$, $B'$, $C'$ are at rest in a car ($S'$) that moves at $v$ along $+x$ past the ground ($S$); $B'$ is midway between the rear $A'$ and the front $C'$. A flash goes off at $B'$.
> - In $S'$ the light covers equal distances at $c$: it reaches $A'$ and $C'$ simultaneously.
> - In $S$ let $\ell$ be the distance from $B'$ to each end, as measured on the ground. The rear $A'$ approaches the light (closing speed $c + v$), the front recedes ($c - v$), so the light reaches $A'$ first, earlier by
>
> $$
> \Delta t = \frac{\ell}{c - v} - \frac{\ell}{c + v} = \frac{2\ell v}{c^2 - v^2} .
> $$
>
> For a car with $\ell = 13\ \mathrm m$ moving at $v = c/2$: $\Delta t = (4/3)\,\ell/c = 5.8\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm s$.
>
> This is the theorem read backwards: events simultaneous in the car are not simultaneous on the ground, and there the *rear* one comes first. With length contraction, $\ell = L/\gamma$ in terms of the half-length $L$ in the car ([[§A2.2 Length Contraction|§A2.2]]), the result is $\Delta t = 2\gamma Lv/c^2$, exactly what the Lorentz transformation gives ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-2|Theorem §A1.4.2]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 2 (instructor's solution checked; numbers chosen here)*
