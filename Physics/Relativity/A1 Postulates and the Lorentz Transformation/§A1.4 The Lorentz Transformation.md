The Galilean transformation ([[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]) contradicts the second postulate: it turns a light pulse of speed $c$ into one of speed $c - v$. The Lorentz transformation is its replacement, the rule that converts the coordinates of an event from one inertial frame to another consistently with both postulates. It contains everything in relativistic kinematics: the relativity of simultaneity, time dilation, length contraction and the velocity transformation all follow from it.

## Setting and notation

> [!definition] Definition §A1.4.1: Standard Configuration
> Two inertial frames $S$ and $S'$ are in **standard configuration** if $S'$ moves relative to $S$ with constant velocity $v$ along the common $x$, $x'$ axis (toward $+x$ when $v > 0$), the $y'$ and $z'$ axes are parallel to $y$ and $z$, and the origins coincide at $t = t' = 0$, when both origin clocks read zero. Then $S$ moves relative to $S'$ with velocity $-v$. These notes always use this configuration, with $v$ for the velocity of $S'$ relative to $S$ and $u$ (components $u_x, u_y, u_z$) for the velocity of a particle.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.5 · Tipler §1-3, Fig. 1-17*

^def-a1-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.4.2: Speed Parameter and Lorentz Factor
> For a relative speed $v$ with $|v| < c$, the **speed parameter** and the **Lorentz factor** are
>
> $$
> \beta = \frac{v}{c}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}} .
> $$
>
> $\gamma \ge 1$, with $\gamma = 1$ only for $v = 0$; it depends only on $|v|$, and $\gamma \to \infty$ as $|v| \to c$. For $|\beta| \ll 1$, $\gamma \approx 1 + \tfrac12\beta^2$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.3, §5.5 · Tipler §1-3, eq. (1-16)*

^def-a1-4-2

## The transformation

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.1: Lorentz Transformation
> For frames in standard configuration, an event with coordinates $(x, y, z, t)$ in $S$ has, in $S'$,
>
> $$
> x' = \gamma\,(x - vt), \qquad y' = y, \qquad z' = z, \qquad t' = \gamma\Bigl(t - \frac{v x}{c^2}\Bigr),
> $$
>
> and conversely (replace $v$ by $-v$ and exchange primed and unprimed)
>
> $$
> x = \gamma\,(x' + vt'), \qquad y = y', \qquad z = z', \qquad t = \gamma\Bigl(t' + \frac{v x'}{c^2}\Bigr).
> $$
>
> With $ct$ as the time coordinate the transformation is symmetric in space and time,
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix} ct' \\ x' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \gamma & -\beta\gamma \\ -\beta\gamma & \gamma \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} ct \\ x \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> The same relations hold for coordinate differences between two events: $\Delta x' = \gamma(\Delta x - v\,\Delta t)$, $\Delta t' = \gamma(\Delta t - v\,\Delta x/c^2)$, and inversely.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.5 · Tipler §1-3, eqs. (1-18)–(1-19) · Morin §2.1*

^thm-a1-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Besides the two postulates, the derivation uses two symmetry properties of empty space. Both follow from the first postulate, since no point, instant or direction is special in any inertial frame.
> 1. **Linearity.** Equal coordinate differences in $S$ must correspond to equal differences in $S'$, wherever and whenever the pair of events occurs (homogeneity); then the transformation is linear, and with coinciding origins it has no constant terms. Equivalently, uniform straight-line motion in one frame must be uniform straight-line motion in the other.
> 2. **No change across the motion.** $y' = y$ and $z' = z$. Give $S$ and $S'$ identical metre sticks, each at rest in its frame and held across the motion (along $y$ and $y'$), with their lower ends sliding past each other on the common $x$ axis; a brush on top of the $S'$ stick paints a mark on the $S$ stick if it touches it as they pass. Suppose a moving stick were shorter. Then $S$ finds the brush below the top of his stick, which gets marked. By the first postulate the situation is symmetric, so $S'$ finds the stick of $S$ shorter, and the brush passes above its top, leaving no mark. Whether the stick carries a mark is one fact that every observer agrees on, so the supposition is contradictory; the same argument excludes a longer moving stick. Only equal lengths remain, along $y$ and likewise along $z$. (This is [[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]], no contraction across the motion.)
>
> Now the $x$ and $t$ coordinates.
> - The origin of $S'$, $x' = 0$, is at $x = vt$ in $S$. With linearity, $x'$ must be proportional to $x - vt$: $x' = \gamma\,(x - vt)$, with a factor $\gamma$ that can depend only on $v$.
> - By the first postulate, $S'$ describes $S$ (velocity $-v$) by the same rule: $x = \gamma\,(x' + vt')$. The factor is the same, since reversing the direction of the $x$ axes turns one situation into the other.
> - By the second postulate, a flash sent along $+x$ from the common origin at $t = t' = 0$ has $x = ct$ in $S$ and $x' = ct'$ in $S'$. Substituting into the two relations: $ct' = \gamma(c - v)\,t$ and $ct = \gamma(c + v)\,t'$. Multiplying them, $c^2 tt' = \gamma^2(c^2 - v^2)\,tt'$, so $\gamma^2 = 1/(1 - v^2/c^2)$. The positive root is taken, because the transformation must reduce to the identity at $v = 0$.
> - Insert $x' = \gamma(x - vt)$ into $x = \gamma(x' + vt')$: $\gamma v t' = x - \gamma^2 x + \gamma^2 v t$. Since $1 - \gamma^2 = -\gamma^2 v^2/c^2$, dividing by $\gamma v$ gives $t' = \gamma(t - vx/c^2)$.
>
> The inverse relations follow in the same way with $-v$. For two events, subtract the relations for each; the result holds whatever the origins, by linearity. That a flash sent in any direction, not only along $x$, also has speed $c$ in $S'$ is checked in [[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]].

> [!remark] Remark: The Galilean limit, and what it misses
> For $v \ll c$, $\gamma \approx 1$ and the transformation becomes $x' = x - vt$ and $t' = t - vx/c^2$. In everyday situations $vx/c^2$ is negligible next to $t$ (for $v = 30\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $x = 1\ \mathrm{km}$ it is $3\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm s$), and the Galilean transformation is recovered. That is why no one noticed the correction before 1905. But the term $vx/c^2$ is the relativity of simultaneity: it is small only when $x$ is not too large, and over astronomical distances it can be large even at walking speeds ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^ex-a1-4-2|Example §A1.4.2]]).
>
> *Source: Morin §2.1 · UP3 §5.5*

^rem-a1-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: No frame moves at the speed of light
> $\gamma$ is real only for $|v| < c$, and $\gamma \to \infty$ as $|v| \to c$. Two inertial frames can therefore never move relative to each other at $c$ or faster, and no material observer can keep pace with a light wave, the conclusion Einstein drew from Maxwell's equations ([[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^rem-a1-1-4|Remark in §A1.1]]). Why no body or signal outruns light is taken up with causality ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^rem-a1-5-4|Remark in §A1.5]]) and with energy ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy|§A3.2]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.1 · Tipler §1-3*

^rem-a1-4-2

> [!caution] Caution: Signs and frames
> - The transformation relates the coordinates of *one* event in two frames, or the differences between *two* events. It says nothing about "a time" or "a length" until one specifies which two events are meant.
> - To get a sign right, look at a point at rest in the frame on the right-hand side. In $x = \gamma(x' + vt')$, a point with $x' = 0$ has $x = \gamma v t'$: it moves toward $+x$, as $S'$ does seen from $S$. If the frame on the right moves toward $-x$, the sign is minus.
> - Neither frame is "the moving one": the two sets of equations differ only by the sign of $v$.
>
> *Source: Morin §2.1*

^cau-a1-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why Lorentz's name
> H. A. Lorentz wrote down these equations before Einstein, as the transformation that leaves Maxwell's equations unchanged, but justified them by hypotheses about the ether and about the contraction of matter moving through it ([[§A1.2 The Michelson–Morley Experiment#^rem-a1-2-3|Remark in §A1.2]]). Einstein derived them from the two postulates and saw that they concern space and time themselves, for all of physics.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.5 · Morin §1.1.1*

## Simultaneity and synchronization, quantitatively

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.2: Relative Time of Simultaneous Events; Rear Clock Ahead
> **(a)** Two events simultaneous in $S$ ($\Delta t = 0$) and separated by $\Delta x$ along the motion are separated in $S'$ by
>
> $$
> \Delta t' = -\,\gamma\,\frac{v\,\Delta x}{c^2} ;
> $$
>
> for $v > 0$ the event at larger $x$, ahead in the direction of $S'$'s motion, comes first in $S'$.
>
> **(b) Rear clock ahead.** Two clocks at rest in $S'$, synchronized in $S'$ and a distance $L$ apart along $x'$ (measured in $S'$), do not read alike at an instant of $S$: the rear clock reads more than the front clock by
>
> $$
> \frac{Lv}{c^2} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.5, Example 5.8 · Tipler §1-3, eq. (1-20) · Morin §1.3.1, eq. (1.8)*

^thm-a1-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> **(a)** Put $\Delta t = 0$ in $\Delta t' = \gamma(\Delta t - v\,\Delta x/c^2)$ ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]]). If $v\,\Delta x > 0$, then $\Delta t' = t'_2 - t'_1 < 0$: event 2, the one at larger $x$, happens earlier in $S'$. This is [[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^thm-a1-3-4|Theorem §A1.3.4]] with a number attached.
>
> **(b)** Solve the inverse relation $t = \gamma(t' + vx'/c^2)$ for the reading of a clock of $S'$ at position $x'$: $t' = t/\gamma - vx'/c^2$. At one instant $t$ of $S$, the rear clock at $x'_1$ and the front clock at $x'_2 = x'_1 + L$ read $t'_1 - t'_2 = v(x'_2 - x'_1)/c^2 = Lv/c^2$.

^der-a1-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]], [[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^thm-a1-3-4|Theorem §A1.3.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Time dilation and length contraction at a glance
> Two other special cases of [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]] are the subject of the next chapter, where they are derived from it.
> - Two events at the same place in $S'$ ($\Delta x' = 0$), for instance two ticks of a clock at rest in $S'$, are farther apart in time in $S$ by the factor $\gamma$: **time dilation**, [[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^thm-a2-1-2|Theorem §A2.1.2]].
> - The two ends of a rod at rest in $S'$, located by $S$ at the same instant ($\Delta t = 0$), are closer together in $S$ by the factor $1/\gamma$: **length contraction**, [[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]].
>
> In each case the condition ($\Delta x' = 0$, or $\Delta t = 0$) decides which formula applies; applying one where its condition fails is the most common error. Differentiating the transformation gives the relativistic velocity transformation, [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation|§A2.3]].
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.5, Examples 5.6–5.7 · Tipler §1-3, §1-4*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.4.1: Transforming an Event
> An event has coordinates $x = 75\ \mathrm m$, $y = 18\ \mathrm m$, $z = 4.0\ \mathrm m$, $t = 2.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm s$ in $S$; $S'$ moves at $v = 0.85c$ in standard configuration. Here $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.7225} = 1.898$, and $vt = (0.85)(3.00\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s})(2.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm s) = 5100\ \mathrm m$. By [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]],
>
> $$
> x' = \gamma(x - vt) = 1.898\,(75 - 5100)\ \mathrm m = -9.54\times10^{3}\ \mathrm m, \qquad y' = 18\ \mathrm m, \qquad z' = 4.0\ \mathrm m,
> $$
>
> $$
> t' = \gamma\Bigl(t - \frac{vx}{c^2}\Bigr) = 1.898\Bigl(2.0\times10^{-5} - \frac{(0.85)(75)}{3.00\times10^8}\Bigr)\ \mathrm s = 3.76\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm s .
> $$
>
> The event is far behind the origin of $S'$, which has moved $5.1$ km past it, and it is later on the clocks of $S'$. *Check* with the inverse: $x = \gamma(x' + vt') = 1.8983\,(-9539.0 + 9578.5)\ \mathrm m = 75.0\ \mathrm m$ and $t = \gamma(t' + vx'/c^2) = 2.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm s$. The inverse subtracts two nearly equal numbers, so five digits must be carried through: with $x'$ and $t'$ rounded to four figures the recovered $x$ is off by a few metres.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 2 (instructor's solution checked)*

^ex-a1-4-1

> [!example] Example §A1.4.2: Two Novae Seen from an Airliner
> Astronomers on Earth find that two novae, in opposite directions and each $2.5\times10^3$ light years away, flared up simultaneously (after correcting for the light travel time). An airliner flies at $v = 1000\ \mathrm{km/h} = 278\ \mathrm{m/s}$ along the line from one (in Orion) toward the other (in Lyra). Take Earth as $S$ with $+x$ toward Lyra, so $\Delta x = x_{\text{Lyra}} - x_{\text{Orion}} = 5.0\times10^3$ ly and $\Delta t = 0$. Here $\gamma - 1 \approx 4\times10^{-13}$, so $\gamma = 1$, and by [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-2|Theorem §A1.4.2]]
>
> $$
> \Delta t' = -\frac{v}{c}\,\frac{\Delta x}{c} = -\frac{278}{3.00\times10^8}\,(5.0\times10^3\ \mathrm y)(3.15\times10^7\ \mathrm{s/y}) = -1.46\times10^5\ \mathrm s = -40.5\ \mathrm h .
> $$
>
> In the frame of the airliner the nova in Lyra, toward which it flies, happened 40 hours before the one in Orion. A speed of $10^{-6}c$ produces a disagreement of almost two days, because $\Delta x/c$ is 5000 years: relativity of simultaneity is not only a high-speed effect.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 2 (instructor's solution checked)*

^ex-a1-4-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The transformation of $(ct, x)$ is a linear map ([[§7 Vector Space of Linear Maps#^ladr-3-1|LADR Def. 3.1]]) with the matrix above ([[§9 Matrices#^ladr-3-31|LADR Def. 3.31]]); its determinant is $\gamma^2 - \beta^2\gamma^2 = 1$, and its inverse ([[§10 Invertibility and Isomorphisms#^ladr-3-59|LADR Def. 3.59]]) is the same matrix with $\beta \to -\beta$.
> - Two successive transformations along $x$ compose by matrix multiplication ([[§9 Matrices#^ladr-3-43|LADR Thm. 3.43]]); the product is again a Lorentz transformation, whose velocity is given by the relativistic velocity addition of [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation|§A2.3]].
> - $\gamma \approx 1 + \beta^2/2$ is the first-order Taylor polynomial of $(1 - \varepsilon)^{-1/2}$ in $\varepsilon = \beta^2$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]).
