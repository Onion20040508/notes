[[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor|§C4.2]] built the energy–momentum tensor of dust, $T^{\mu\nu} = \rho U^\mu U^\nu$ ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]), by counting particles that all move together. The matter of a star, the air of an atmosphere or the radiation of the early universe is not like that. Its particles move at random relative to one another, or push on one another, and this pushes on any surface placed in the material: the force per unit area is the pressure ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-3|CM Def. §A9.1.3]]). This section asks two questions. *What is the energy–momentum tensor of matter with pressure?* And *what equations of motion does local conservation, $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, impose on it?* The answers are the relativistic forms of the two equations of fluid mechanics, the continuity equation and Euler's equation, in any spacetime, and they contain one result that Newtonian fluid mechanics cannot see: pressure has inertia.

First, what fails. Keep the dust tensor and add the pressure where Newtonian mechanics puts it, as an isotropic stress $p\,\delta^{ij}$ in the spatial components ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^rem-a9-1-2|CM §A9.1, Remark: Pressure has no direction, its force does]]), in the inertial frame of a laboratory in which the fluid moves with speed $v$ along $x$, $U^\mu = \gamma(1, v, 0, 0)$: $T^{\mu\nu}_{\text{naive}} = \rho\,U^\mu U^\nu + \operatorname{diag}(0, p, p, p)$.

Transform it to the rest frame of the fluid. The boost has $\Lambda^0{}_0 = \Lambda^1{}_1 = \gamma$, $\Lambda^0{}_1 = \Lambda^1{}_0 = -\gamma v$, $\Lambda^2{}_2 = \Lambda^3{}_3 = 1$, and a $(2, 0)$ tensor transforms as $T'^{\mu\nu} = \Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta T^{\alpha\beta}$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]]). The dust part becomes $\rho\,\delta^\mu_0\delta^\nu_0$, since $U'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu U^\nu = (1, 0, 0, 0)$. The added stress has only the components $T^{11} = T^{22} = T^{33} = p$, and it contributes

$$
\Lambda^0{}_1\Lambda^0{}_1\,p = \gamma^2v^2p, \qquad \Lambda^0{}_1\Lambda^1{}_1\,p = -\gamma^2vp, \qquad \Lambda^1{}_1\Lambda^1{}_1\,p = \gamma^2p, \qquad \Lambda^2{}_2\Lambda^2{}_2\,p = p .
$$

So in the fluid's own rest frame

$$
T'^{00} = \rho + \gamma^2v^2p, \qquad T'^{01} = -\gamma^2vp, \qquad T'^{11} = \gamma^2p, \qquad T'^{22} = T'^{33} = p ;
$$

at $v = 3/5$ ($\gamma = 5/4$) these are $\rho + \tfrac{9}{16}p$, $-\tfrac{15}{16}p$, $\tfrac{25}{16}p$ and $p$ (checked by script). The fluid at rest would have an energy density other than $\rho$, would carry energy through itself (a heat flow, $T'^{01} \neq 0$) although nothing in it conducts heat, and would push harder along $x$ than across. All three defects depend on the speed of the laboratory in which the pressure was added: "add $p\,\delta^{ij}$" is a rule tied to one frame, not a tensor equation, the defect found for the Newtonian source in [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-4|Theorem §C1.1.4]], part 3. Pressure is a property of the fluid, so it has to be stated where the fluid singles out a frame, its rest frame, and then carried to every other frame as the components of one tensor.

The section builds this in order. The perfect fluid is the matter that is isotropic in its rest frame ([[#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]]); isotropy and covariance fix its tensor, $T^{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)U^\mu U^\nu + p\,g^{\mu\nu}$, and d'Inverno's route through the Newtonian limit leads to the same tensor ([[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]]). An equation of state closes the system ([[#^def-c4-3-1|Def. §C4.3.1]]). The projector onto the rest space ([[#^def-c4-3-2|Def. §C4.3.2]]) splits $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ into an energy equation along $U$ and a relativistic Euler equation across it ([[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]]). The Euler equation shows that the inertial mass density is $\rho + p$ ([[#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]]). In the weak field of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]] the two equations become Newton's continuity and Euler equations with gravity ([[#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]]), and in a static spacetime they give the exact law of hydrostatic equilibrium, $p' = -(\rho + p)\Phi'$ ([[#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]]).

**Setup.** A fluid in a spacetime $(M, g)$: a continuum whose state at each event is given by a few fields, averaged over a fluid element that is small compared with the lengths over which the fields change but holds many particles. The metric is given. The fluid may be a test fluid or the source of the metric, but the field equations ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^pr-c5-1-4|Principle §C5.1.4]]) are not used. Every fluid element has a four-velocity $U$. Its rest frame at an event is the local inertial frame ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-4|Def. §C3.2.4]]) whose time axis is $U$, Schutz's "momentarily comoving reference frame". The energy–momentum tensor is read as in [[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^def-c4-2-2|Def. §C4.2.2]]: $T^{00}$ energy density, $T^{0i}$ energy flux and momentum density, $T^{ij}$ stress. From [[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]] on, local conservation $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ is assumed ([[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime#^pr-c4-4-1|Principle §C4.4.1]], the minimally coupled form of $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, [[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^pr-c4-1-1|Principle §C4.1.1]]); conservation of particle number, $\nabla_\mu(nU^\mu) = 0$ ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c4-2-2|Theorem §C4.2.2]]), only where stated. Theorems §C4.3.2–§C4.3.4 and §C4.3.6 are exact; Theorem §C4.3.5 holds to first order in the small parameter $\varepsilon$ of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]. The numbers in the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $U\cdot U = -1$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$, with restored forms beside the results (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here $\rho$ is an energy density in $\mathrm{J/m^3}$ and $\rho/c^2$ a mass density, $p$ in $\mathrm{Pa} = \mathrm{J/m^3}$, $\Phi \to \Phi/c^2$, $U \to U/c$); $T^{00}$ is the energy density; Greek indices $0$–$3$, Latin $1$–$3$; hatted indices $\hat a$ are components in an orthonormal frame ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]). Level B and its links use $+{-}{-}{-}$; d'Inverno & Vickers too ([[#^ex-c4-3-4|Example §C4.3.4]]).

## The perfect-fluid model

What does "the pressure is the same in every direction" mean for a tensor? At an event, the observer who moves with the fluid has a rest space, the three directions orthogonal to $U$. Isotropy means that turning these three directions into one another changes nothing.

> [!model] Model §C4.3.1: Perfect Fluid
> *Structure.* On a spacetime $(M, g)$, a **perfect fluid** is described by three fields:
> - a **four-velocity** field $U$, future-directed and timelike with $g(U, U) = -1$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]], [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]): at each event the four-velocity of the fluid element there ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]);
> - the **energy density** $\rho$, a scalar field: all energy per unit volume in the rest frame of the element, rest energy included ([[#^cau-c4-3-1|Caution: ρ is the energy density, not the rest-mass density]]);
> - the **pressure** $p$, a scalar field.
>
> At each event $q$ a **rest frame** is an orthonormal basis $e_{\hat 0} = U_q$, $e_{\hat 1}$, $e_{\hat 2}$, $e_{\hat 3}$ of $T_qM$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]), the axes of a local inertial frame at $q$ moving with the element. The $e_{\hat i}$ span the **rest space** $U_q^\perp = \{V \in T_qM : g(U_q, V) = 0\}$. Two rest frames at $q$ differ by a rotation $R \in SO(3)$ of the rest space, $e_{\hat i} \mapsto e_{\hat j}R^j{}_i$: a Lorentz transformation of $T_qM$ that fixes $U_q$.
>
> *Defining property (isotropy in the rest frame).* The energy–momentum tensor $T_q$ ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^def-c4-2-2|Def. §C4.2.2]]), a symmetric $(2, 0)$ tensor ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c4-2-5|Theorem §C4.2.5]]), has the same components in every rest frame at $q$. Its energy density there is $T^{\hat 0\hat 0} = T_{\mu\nu}U^\mu U^\nu = \rho$, and the stress across a surface element of the rest space is normal to the element with magnitude $p$ ([[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]], part 1, shows that isotropy allows nothing else).
>
> *Physical reading.* In the rest frame, $T^{\hat 0\hat i} = 0$: energy flows only with the fluid, there is no heat conduction. $T^{\hat i\hat j} = p\,\delta^{ij}$: the force across any surface element is normal to it and the same for every orientation, so there is no shear stress and no viscosity, as for a fluid at rest in Newtonian hydrostatics ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-3|CM Def. §A9.1.3]]).
>
> - *Assumes:* local thermodynamic equilibrium in each element; no viscosity; no heat conduction; no other anisotropic stress (no elastic solid, no magnetic field); a continuum description (each element holds many particles; for a collisionless gas, an isotropic distribution of velocities in the rest frame, which also has pressure: Schutz §4.7).
> - *Special case:* $p = 0$ is dust ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^mod-c4-2-3|Model §C4.2.3]]); radiation, stiff matter and vacuum energy are other choices of $p(\rho)$ ([[#^def-c4-3-1|Def. §C4.3.1]]).
> - *Fails:* viscous shear flows, heat flow (imperfect fluids, not treated in these notes), anisotropic stress, and radiation streaming in one direction (a beam, [[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^ex-c4-2-2|Example §C4.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Schutz §4.7, p. 100 ("no viscosity and no heat conduction in the MCRF") · Hartle §22.1, Example 22.3, eq. (22.36), pp. 476, 479 · Couzens, GR1, §2.1, eq. (2.53), p. 23 ("isotropic … in the rest frame") · d'Inverno & Vickers §12.4, p. 207*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.2: The Energy–Momentum Tensor of a Perfect Fluid
> In [[#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]], at every event:
> 1. **Rest-frame components.** Isotropy forces $T^{\hat 0\hat i} = 0$ and $T^{\hat i\hat j} = p\,\delta^{ij}$; in a rest frame
>
> $$
> \bigl(T^{\hat a\hat b}\bigr) = \operatorname{diag}(\rho, p, p, p) .
> $$
>
> 2. **Covariant form.** As a tensor $T = (\rho + p)\,U \otimes U + p\,g^{-1}$, with $g^{-1}$ the inverse metric ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]); in every chart
>
> $$
> T^{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)\,U^\mu U^\nu + p\,g^{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> 3. **Lowered and mixed components; trace.**
>
> $$
> T_{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)\,U_\mu U_\nu + p\,g_{\mu\nu}, \qquad T^\mu{}_\nu = (\rho + p)\,U^\mu U_\nu + p\,\delta^\mu{}_\nu, \qquad T^\mu{}_\mu = -\rho + 3p .
> $$
>
> 4. **Uniqueness.** Among the symmetric tensors built from $U$ and $g$ without derivatives, $A\,U \otimes U + B\,g^{-1}$, only $A = \rho + p$, $B = p$ has the rest-frame components of part 1.
> 5. **Seen from a moving frame.** In a local inertial frame in which the fluid moves with velocity $\vec v$, $U^\mu = \gamma(1, \vec v)$:
>
> $$
> T^{00} = \gamma^2(\rho + v^2p), \qquad T^{0i} = \gamma^2(\rho + p)\,v^i, \qquad T^{ij} = \gamma^2(\rho + p)\,v^iv^j + p\,\delta^{ij} .
> $$
>
>    For $p = 0$ this is boosted dust ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^ex-c4-2-1|Example §C4.2.1]]). The pressure enters the momentum density $T^{0i}$, not only the stress ([[#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]]).
>
> Restored, with $\rho$ the energy density and $U\cdot U = -c^2$: $T^{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)\,U^\mu U^\nu/c^2 + p\,g^{\mu\nu}$; with the mass density $\rho_m = \rho/c^2$, $T^{\mu\nu} = (\rho_m + p/c^2)\,U^\mu U^\nu + p\,g^{\mu\nu}$.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]]; *applies:* exact, any metric, event by event; no equation of motion is used.
>
> *Source: Schutz §4.7, eqs. (4.36)–(4.38), p. 100; §7.1, eq. (7.7), p. 173 · Hartle §22.1, eqs. (22.36)–(22.37), p. 479; §22.2, eq. (22.39), p. 480 · Couzens, GR1, §2.1, eqs. (2.53)–(2.54), p. 23 · d'Inverno & Vickers §12.4, eqs. (12.20)–(12.22), p. 207 (signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) · components checked by script*

^thm-c4-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *Route 1: isotropy and covariance.* Fix an event $q$ and a rest frame there. A rotation $R \in SO(3)$ of the rest space gives the new basis $e'_{\hat 0} = U$, $e'_{\hat i} = e_{\hat j}R^j{}_i$; the components of $T$ in it are $T'^{\hat 0\hat i} = (R^{-1})^i{}_j\,T^{\hat 0\hat j}$ and $T'^{\hat i\hat j} = (R^{-1})^i{}_k(R^{-1})^j{}_l\,T^{\hat k\hat l}$ (the transformation law of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]] for this change of basis). Isotropy says these equal the old components for every $R$.
>
> **Step 1** (no energy flux). Let $w^i = T^{\hat 0\hat i}$. Isotropy gives $R^{-1}w = w$ for every rotation, hence $Rw = w$ for every rotation. Take the rotations by $\pi$ about $e_{\hat 1}$ and about $e_{\hat 3}$:
>
> $$
> \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1)\,w = w \;\Rightarrow\; w^2 = w^3 = 0, \qquad \operatorname{diag}(-1, -1, 1)\,w = w \;\Rightarrow\; w^1 = 0 .
> $$
>
> So $T^{\hat 0\hat i} = 0$.
>
> **Step 2** (isotropic stress). Let $S = (T^{\hat i\hat j})$, a real symmetric $3\times3$ matrix ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c4-2-5|Theorem §C4.2.5]]). Isotropy gives $R^{-1}S(R^{-1})^{\mathsf T} = S$, i.e. $RSR^{\mathsf T} = S$, for every rotation $R$ ($R^{-1} = R^{\mathsf T}$). By the real spectral theorem ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]) $S$ has a unit eigenvector $e$, $Se = \lambda e$. For every rotation $R$, $S(Re) = RSR^{\mathsf T}Re = RSe = \lambda\,Re$, and every unit vector of $\mathbf R^3$ is $Re$ for some rotation $R$. So every vector is an eigenvector with the same eigenvalue $\lambda$, and $S = \lambda\,\mathbf 1$. The force across a surface element with unit normal $\hat n$ in the rest space is $S\hat n\,dA = \lambda\,\hat n\,dA$ (Hartle (22.21)): normal, the same for every $\hat n$. So $\lambda$ is the pressure, $\lambda = p$. With $T^{\hat 0\hat 0} = \rho$ (the model), part 1 is proved. (Both facts were also checked by script: the vectors fixed by the two half-turns and the symmetric matrices commuting with the three rotation generators.)
>
> **Step 3** (the candidate's rest-frame components). Let $\tilde T = (\rho + p)\,U \otimes U + p\,g^{-1}$. In the rest frame $U^{\hat a} = \delta^a_0$ and $g^{\hat a\hat b} = \eta^{ab}$ (orthonormal frame, [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]), so
>
> $$
> \tilde T^{\hat 0\hat 0} = (\rho + p)\cdot 1\cdot 1 + p\,\eta^{00} = \rho + p - p = \rho, \qquad \tilde T^{\hat 0\hat i} = 0 + p\,\eta^{0i} = 0, \qquad \tilde T^{\hat i\hat j} = 0 + p\,\eta^{ij} = p\,\delta^{ij} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (equal components, equal tensors). A tensor at $q$ is determined by its components in one basis ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]). By Steps 1–3, $T_q$ and $\tilde T_q$ have the same components in the rest frame, so $T_q = \tilde T_q$; $q$ was arbitrary. In a chart the components of $U \otimes U$ are $U^\mu U^\nu$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]]) and those of $g^{-1}$ are $g^{\mu\nu}$, which is part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (lowering). Apply $g_{\alpha\mu}g_{\beta\nu}$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]), with $g_{\alpha\mu}U^\mu = U_\alpha$ and $g_{\alpha\mu}g_{\beta\nu}g^{\mu\nu} = g_{\alpha\mu}\delta^\mu{}_\beta = g_{\alpha\beta}$: $T_{\alpha\beta} = (\rho + p)\,U_\alpha U_\beta + p\,g_{\alpha\beta}$.
>
> Lowering only the second index uses $g_{\beta\nu}g^{\mu\nu} = \delta^\mu{}_\beta$: $T^\mu{}_\beta = (\rho + p)\,U^\mu U_\beta + p\,\delta^\mu{}_\beta$. The trace, with $U^\mu U_\mu = -1$ and $\delta^\mu{}_\mu = 4$: $T^\mu{}_\mu = (\rho + p)(-1) + 4p = -\rho + 3p$.
>
> This is part 3.
>
> **Step 6** (uniqueness). For $A\,U \otimes U + B\,g^{-1}$ the computation of Step 3 gives the rest-frame components $A - B$ (for $\hat 0\hat 0$), $0$ (for $\hat 0\hat i$) and $B\,\delta^{ij}$. Matching $\operatorname{diag}(\rho, p, p, p)$: $B = p$ and $A - B = \rho$, so $A = \rho + p$. This is part 4.
>
> **Step 7** (a moving frame). With $g^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\nu}$, $U^0 = \gamma$, $U^i = \gamma v^i$ and $1/\gamma^2 = 1 - v^2$:
>
> $$
> T^{00} = (\rho + p)\gamma^2 - p = \gamma^2\bigl[\rho + p - p(1 - v^2)\bigr] = \gamma^2(\rho + v^2p), \qquad T^{0i} = (\rho + p)\gamma^2v^i + p\,\eta^{0i} = \gamma^2(\rho + p)\,v^i ,
> $$
>
> $$
> T^{ij} = (\rho + p)\gamma^2v^iv^j + p\,\eta^{ij} = \gamma^2(\rho + p)\,v^iv^j + p\,\delta^{ij} .
> $$
>
> Boosting these back by $-v$ returns $\operatorname{diag}(\rho, p, p, p)$ (checked by script). This is part 5.
>
> *Route 2: the Newtonian limit (d'Inverno & Vickers).* d'Inverno & Vickers reach the same tensor differently: they guess its form and fix the coefficients by demanding Newtonian fluid mechanics in the limit. In the signature of these notes:
>
> **Step 8** (ansatz). Dust plus pressure times a symmetric tensor built from the fluid: $T = \rho\,U \otimes U + p\,S$, $S = \lambda\,U \otimes U + \mu\,g^{-1}$, with constants $\lambda$, $\mu$ (their (12.20)–(12.21)). In the rest frame, as in Step 3,
>
> $$
> T^{\hat 0\hat 0} = \rho + (\lambda - \mu)\,p, \qquad T^{\hat 0\hat i} = 0, \qquad T^{\hat i\hat j} = \mu\,p\,\delta^{ij} .
> $$
>
> **Step 9** (the Newtonian limit fixes $\mu$). In an inertial frame of Minkowski spacetime let $v^2 = O(\varepsilon)$, $p = O(\varepsilon\rho)$, and let the flow change over a length $L$ and a time $L/v$. The components are
>
> $$
> T^{00} = (\rho + \lambda p)\gamma^2 - \mu p = \rho\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)], \qquad T^{0i} = (\rho + \lambda p)\gamma^2v^i = \rho v^i\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)], \qquad T^{ij} = \rho v^iv^j\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)] + \mu\,p\,\delta^{ij} .
> $$
>
> The $\nu = 0$ component of $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ has leading terms of order $\rho v/L$: $\partial_tT^{00} + \partial_iT^{i0} = \partial_t\rho + \partial_i(\rho v^i) + O(\varepsilon\rho v/L) = 0$, the continuity equation, whatever $\lambda$ and $\mu$. The $\nu = j$ components have leading terms of order $\varepsilon\rho/L$ (each of $\rho\,\partial_tv^j$, $\rho v^i\partial_iv^j$ and $\partial_jp$):
>
> $$
> \partial_tT^{0j} + \partial_iT^{ij} = \partial_t(\rho v^j) + \partial_i(\rho v^iv^j) + \mu\,\partial_jp + O(\varepsilon^2\rho/L) = \rho\bigl(\partial_t + v^i\partial_i\bigr)v^j + \mu\,\partial_jp + O(\varepsilon^2\rho/L) = 0 ,
> $$
>
> where the middle step subtracts $v^j$ times the continuity equation. Euler's equation of fluid mechanics without external forces (d'Inverno & Vickers (12.10)) has $+\partial_jp$, so $\mu = 1$. The constant $\lambda$ appears only in the $O(\varepsilon)$ corrections: the Newtonian limit does not determine it (checked by script with $\lambda$, $\mu$ kept general).
>
> **Step 10** (the rest-frame energy fixes $\lambda$). For $\rho$ to be the energy density in the rest frame, as the model requires, Step 8 needs $\lambda - \mu = 0$, so $\lambda = 1$, and $T = (\rho + p)\,U \otimes U + p\,g^{-1}$ again. (In d'Inverno's signature the same pair reads $\lambda = 1$, $\mu = -1$: [[#^ex-c4-3-4|Example §C4.3.4]]; on what their text claims for the limit, [[#^cau-c4-3-2|Caution: The fluid tensor in the sources]].)
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Isotropy in one frame plus covariance fixes the whole tensor: the two numbers $\rho$ and $p$ are all a perfect fluid has, and the tensor law carries them to every frame. The naive guess of the opening fails exactly because it imposed isotropy in the wrong frame.
> - The pressure enters $T^{\mu\nu}$ twice: in the stress ($p\,g^{\mu\nu}$) and next to the energy density in $(\rho + p)U^\mu U^\nu$. The second appearance is invisible in the Newtonian limit (Step 9). It is the inertia of pressure, [[#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]].

The tensor contains two scalar functions and the four-velocity. What the matter is made of enters only through the relation between $\rho$ and $p$.

> [!definition] Definition §C4.3.1: Equation of State
> *Structure.* For a perfect fluid ([[#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]]) an **equation of state** is a relation between $p$, $\rho$ and possibly further thermodynamic variables of the fluid element (temperature, entropy per particle; [[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^def-a3-1-2|TH Def. §A3.1.2]]), fixed by the microphysics of the matter and not by gravity. It is **barotropic** if $p$ is a function of $\rho$ alone, $p = p(\rho)$, the same function at every event.
>
> *Why it is needed.* Local conservation $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ gives four equations ([[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]]: one along $U$, three across it) for five unknown functions: $\rho$, $p$ and the three independent components of $U$ (the fourth is fixed by $U\cdot U = -1$). A barotropic equation of state is the fifth equation. Otherwise more variables enter, with more equations, such as particle conservation ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c4-2-2|Theorem §C4.2.2]]).
>
> *Linear equations of state,* $p = w\rho$ with a constant $w$:
>
> | Matter | $w$ | $p(\rho)$ | Where it is used |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | dust | $0$ | $p = 0$ | pressureless matter ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^mod-c4-2-3\|Model §C4.2.3]]); galaxies as a gas on cosmic scales |
> | radiation | $\tfrac13$ | $p = \rho/3$ | a photon gas, any isotropic gas of massless particles ([[#^ex-c4-3-1\|Example §C4.3.1]]; [[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-3\|TH Theorem §B11.1.3]]) |
> | stiff matter | $1$ | $p = \rho$ | the largest pressure the dominant energy condition $\rho \ge \lvert p\rvert$ allows ([[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^def-c4-5-4\|Def. §C4.5.4]]) |
> | vacuum energy | $-1$ | $p = -\rho$ | the cosmological constant ([[#^ex-c4-3-2\|Example §C4.3.2]]) |
>
> A nonrelativistic ideal gas has $p = nk_BT$ ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|TH Law §A2.1.1]], with $n$ the number density) and $\rho = n(m + \tfrac32k_BT)$ for a monatomic gas, so $p/\rho \approx k_BT/m \ll 1$: nearly dust ([[#^ex-c4-3-3|Example §C4.3.3]]). ## Splitting the conservation law along and across the flow

Four equations $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ are one vector equation. A fluid element has its own split of every vector: the part along its four-velocity, which its clock measures, and the part in its rest space, which its rulers measure. Projecting the conservation law in the two ways separates its energy content from its force content.

> [!definition] Definition §C4.3.2: Projector onto the Rest Space
> *Structure.* Let $U$ be a vector field with $g(U, U) = -1$. At each event $q$ the tangent space splits as a direct sum $T_qM = \operatorname{span}(U_q) \oplus U_q^\perp$ of the time line of the observer $U$ and its rest space ([[#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]]); the sum is direct because $g(U, U) \neq 0$. The **projector onto the rest space** of $U$ is the $(1, 1)$ tensor field ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]])
>
> $$
> h^\mu{}_\nu = \delta^\mu{}_\nu + U^\mu U_\nu, \qquad V^\mu \mapsto h^\mu{}_\nu V^\nu = V^\mu + (U\cdot V)\,U^\mu .
> $$
>
> Every vector splits as $V = -(U\cdot V)\,U + hV$, a part along $U$ and a part in $U^\perp$. With both indices down or both up (by [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]), $h_{\mu\nu} = g_{\mu\nu} + U_\mu U_\nu$ and $h^{\mu\nu} = g^{\mu\nu} + U^\mu U^\nu$, the **spatial metric** of the observer $U$.
>
> *Properties* (each by one line of algebra with $U^\mu U_\mu = -1$):
> 1. $h^\mu{}_\nu U^\nu = U^\mu + U^\mu(U_\nu U^\nu) = 0$ and $U_\mu h^\mu{}_\nu = U_\nu + (U_\mu U^\mu)U_\nu = 0$: $h$ removes the time direction;
> 2. if $U\cdot V = 0$, then $h^\mu{}_\nu V^\nu = V^\mu$: $h$ is the identity on the rest space;
> 3. $h^\mu{}_\rho h^\rho{}_\nu = \delta^\mu{}_\nu + 2U^\mu U_\nu + U^\mu(U_\rho U^\rho)U_\nu = h^\mu{}_\nu$: $h$ is a projector;
> 4. $h^\mu{}_\mu = 4 + U^\mu U_\mu = 3$, the dimension of the rest space;
> 5. in a rest frame ($e_{\hat 0} = U$), $\bigl(h^{\hat a}{}_{\hat b}\bigr) = \bigl(h_{\hat a\hat b}\bigr) = \operatorname{diag}(0, 1, 1, 1)$: on the rest space $h$ is the positive-definite metric that the observer's rulers measure.
>
> In these terms the perfect fluid of [[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]] is $T^{\mu\nu} = \rho\,U^\mu U^\nu + p\,h^{\mu\nu}$: energy density along the time line, isotropic pressure on the rest space.
>
> *Source: Schutz, Exercise 3.31, p. 82 ($P_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + U_\mu U_\nu$) · d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 10.4, p. 185; §14.1, eqs. (14.2)–(14.6), p. 240 (signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where the projector is $\delta^a{}_b - V^aV_b$) · properties checked by script*

The four-acceleration of the flow appears in the projected equation. For the worldlines of the fluid it is $a^\mu = U^\nu\nabla_\nu U^\mu$, the covariant derivative of $U$ along itself ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-3|Def. §C3.5.3]] with [[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-5-5|Theorem §C3.5.5]], part 2); it vanishes exactly when the flow lines are geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.3: Energy Equation and Relativistic Euler Equation
> Let $T^{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)U^\mu U^\nu + p\,g^{\mu\nu}$ ([[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]]) satisfy $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime#^pr-c4-4-1|Principle §C4.4.1]]). Write $h$ for the projector of [[#^def-c4-3-2|Def. §C4.3.2]], $a^\mu = U^\nu\nabla_\nu U^\mu$ for the four-acceleration of the flow, $\theta = \nabla_\mu U^\mu$ for the **expansion** of the flow, and $d/d\tau = U^\mu\partial_\mu$ for the rate of change along a flow line. Then:
> 1. **Split.** $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ holds if and only if both $U_\nu\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ (one equation) and $h^\lambda{}_\nu\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ (three independent equations, since $U_\lambda h^\lambda{}_\nu = 0$).
> 2. **Energy equation** (along $U$):
>
> $$
> \frac{d\rho}{d\tau} + (\rho + p)\,\theta = 0 .
> $$
>
> 3. **Relativistic Euler equation** (across $U$):
>
> $$
> (\rho + p)\,a^\lambda = -h^{\lambda\mu}\,\partial_\mu p .
> $$
>
>    In a rest frame at an event, $(\rho + p)\,a^{\hat i} = -\partial_{\hat i}\,p$.
> 4. **The first law along the flow.** If in addition particles are conserved, $\nabla_\mu(nU^\mu) = 0$ ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c4-2-2|Theorem §C4.2.2]]), then $\theta = -\frac1n\frac{dn}{d\tau}$ and the energy equation becomes
>
> $$
> \frac{d\rho}{d\tau} = \frac{\rho + p}{n}\,\frac{dn}{d\tau} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{d(\rho\,\mathcal V)}{d\tau} = -p\,\frac{d\mathcal V}{d\tau}, \qquad \mathcal V = \frac1n ,
> $$
>
>    the first law of thermodynamics ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|TH Principle §A3.3.1]]) for the energy $\rho\mathcal V$ in the volume $\mathcal V$ that holds one particle, with no heat exchanged: the flow of a perfect fluid is adiabatic. Then $\theta = \frac{1}{\mathcal V}\frac{d\mathcal V}{d\tau}$ is the fractional rate at which a fluid element's volume grows.
>
> Restored ($\rho$ energy density, $x^0 = ct$, $a$ in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$): $d\rho/d\tau + (\rho + p)\,\theta = 0$ with $\theta = \nabla_\mu U^\mu$ in $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$; $\frac{\rho + p}{c^2}\,a^\lambda = -h^{\lambda\mu}\partial_\mu p$.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]] with local conservation, in any metric; *applies:* exact; part 4 needs particle conservation.
>
> *Source: Schutz §4.7, eqs. (4.39)–(4.54), pp. 101–103 (in special relativity, with the comoving frame instead of $h$) · Hartle §22.2, eqs. (22.31)–(22.33), (22.40), pp. 478–480 · d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 12.3, p. 215; Exercise 13.5, p. 235 · split checked by script for a general flow in a general two-dimensional metric*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (expand the divergence). The covariant derivative obeys the Leibniz rule ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]], (iii)), is $\partial$ on functions, and kills the inverse metric, $\nabla_\mu g^{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-5|Theorem §C3.6.5]], part 1). So
>
> $$
> \nabla_\mu\bigl[(\rho + p)\,U^\mu U^\nu\bigr] = U^\mu\partial_\mu(\rho + p)\,U^\nu + (\rho + p)\,(\nabla_\mu U^\mu)\,U^\nu + (\rho + p)\,U^\mu\nabla_\mu U^\nu, \qquad \nabla_\mu\bigl(p\,g^{\mu\nu}\bigr) = g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\mu p ,
> $$
>
> and with the abbreviations of the statement
>
> $$
> \nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = \Bigl[\frac{d\rho}{d\tau} + \frac{dp}{d\tau} + (\rho + p)\,\theta\Bigr]U^\nu + (\rho + p)\,a^\nu + g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\mu p .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the acceleration is orthogonal to $U$). Differentiate $g_{\nu\lambda}U^\nu U^\lambda = -1$ along $U$, using $\nabla g = 0$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^def-c3-6-2|Def. §C3.6.2]]) and the symmetry of $g$:
>
> $$
> 0 = U^\mu\nabla_\mu\bigl(g_{\nu\lambda}U^\nu U^\lambda\bigr) = g_{\nu\lambda}\,a^\nu U^\lambda + g_{\nu\lambda}\,U^\nu a^\lambda = 2\,U_\nu a^\nu .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (along $U$). Contract Step 1 with $U_\nu$, using $U_\nu U^\nu = -1$, Step 2, and $U_\nu g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu p = U^\mu\partial_\mu p = dp/d\tau$:
>
> $$
> U_\nu\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = -\frac{d\rho}{d\tau} - \frac{dp}{d\tau} - (\rho + p)\,\theta + 0 + \frac{dp}{d\tau} = -\Bigl[\frac{d\rho}{d\tau} + (\rho + p)\,\theta\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> The two $dp/d\tau$ cancel. Setting this to zero gives part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (across $U$). Apply $h^\lambda{}_\nu$ to Step 1. By [[#^def-c4-3-2|Def. §C4.3.2]], property 1, $h^\lambda{}_\nu U^\nu = 0$; by Step 2 and property 2, $h^\lambda{}_\nu a^\nu = a^\lambda$; and $h^\lambda{}_\nu g^{\mu\nu} = g^{\lambda\mu} + U^\lambda U^\mu = h^{\lambda\mu}$. So
>
> $$
> h^\lambda{}_\nu\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0 + (\rho + p)\,a^\lambda + h^{\lambda\mu}\,\partial_\mu p ,
> $$
>
> and setting it to zero gives part 3. In a rest frame, $a^{\hat 0} = -U_{\hat a}a^{\hat a}$ vanishes by Step 2, and $h^{\hat i\hat\mu}\partial_{\hat\mu}p = \delta^{ij}\partial_{\hat j}p$ by property 5, which gives the rest-frame form.
>
> **Step 5** (the split is complete). Write $\delta^\lambda{}_\nu = h^\lambda{}_\nu - U^\lambda U_\nu$ and let $X^\nu = \nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}$. Then $X^\lambda = h^\lambda{}_\nu X^\nu - U^\lambda\,(U_\nu X^\nu)$, so $X = 0$ if both projections vanish; conversely both projections of $X = 0$ vanish. The projection $h^\lambda{}_\nu X^\nu$ satisfies $U_\lambda h^\lambda{}_\nu X^\nu = 0$, so it has three independent components. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 6** (particle conservation). Expand $\nabla_\mu(nU^\mu) = U^\mu\partial_\mu n + n\,\nabla_\mu U^\mu = \frac{dn}{d\tau} + n\theta = 0$, so $\theta = -\frac1n\frac{dn}{d\tau}$. Inserting it into part 2: $\frac{d\rho}{d\tau} - \frac{\rho + p}{n}\,\frac{dn}{d\tau} = 0$.
>
> With $\mathcal V = 1/n$, $\frac{d\mathcal V}{d\tau} = -\frac{1}{n^2}\frac{dn}{d\tau}$, and so $\theta = \frac{1}{\mathcal V}\frac{d\mathcal V}{d\tau}$. The product rule and the last display give
>
> $$
> \frac{d(\rho\mathcal V)}{d\tau} = \frac1n\,\frac{d\rho}{d\tau} - \frac{\rho}{n^2}\,\frac{dn}{d\tau} = \frac{\rho + p}{n^2}\,\frac{dn}{d\tau} - \frac{\rho}{n^2}\,\frac{dn}{d\tau} = \frac{p}{n^2}\,\frac{dn}{d\tau} = -p\,\frac{d\mathcal V}{d\tau} .
> $$
>
> In the first law $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q - W$ with $W = \int p\,d\mathcal V$ for a slow expansion, this is $Q = 0$. This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The energy equation contains no pressure gradient and the Euler equation contains no $d\rho/d\tau$: the projection separates the two physical contents of $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ exactly. The pressure enters the energy equation only as the work $p\,\theta$ done when an element expands.
> - The pressure gradient pushes only across the flow: $h$ removes its time part, so a pressure that changes along a flow line, $dp/d\tau \neq 0$, exerts no force.
> - The coefficient of the acceleration is $\rho + p$, not $\rho$: [[#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]].

## Pressure has inertia

In Newton's mechanics of fluids the mass per unit volume decides how hard an element is to accelerate, and pressure only pushes. The relativistic Euler equation has $\rho + p$ in that place. The difference has a direct physical meaning.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.4: Pressure Has Inertia
> For a perfect fluid with $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]]):
> 1. **⚑ Inertial mass density.** In the rest frame of a fluid element the net pressure force per unit volume is $f^{\hat i} = -\partial_{\hat i}\,p$, and the Euler equation reads $(\rho + p)\,a^{\hat i} = f^{\hat i}$: the mass per unit volume that resists acceleration is $\rho + p$, not $\rho$. Restored: $(\rho + p)/c^2$ with $\rho$ the energy density; for radiation ($p = \rho/3$) it is $\tfrac43\rho/c^2$.
> 2. **⚑ Momentum density.** A fluid moving with velocity $\vec v$ in a local inertial frame carries the momentum density $T^{0i} = \gamma^2(\rho + p)\,v^i = (\rho + p)\,v^i\,\bigl[1 + O(v^2)\bigr]$: the same $\rho + p$ multiplies the velocity, so pressure carries momentum.
> 3. **Support.** A fluid held on worldlines with a given four-acceleration $a$ (for example at rest in a static gravitational field, [[#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]]) needs the pressure gradient $h^{\lambda\mu}\,\partial_\mu p = -(\rho + p)\,a^\lambda$, larger by the factor $1 + p/\rho$ than the Newtonian $\rho\,|a|$. Schutz writes this $-a^i = p_{,i}/(\rho + p)$, with $a$ the free-fall acceleration that the pressure must cancel.
> 4. **Size.** In Newtonian fluid mechanics $p/\rho$ is of the order of $v^2$ or $\lvert\Phi\rvert$ and the term is invisible ([[#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]]); for air at sea level $p/\rho = 9.20\times10^{-13}$ ([[#^ex-c4-3-3|Example §C4.3.3]]). It is of order one for radiation ($p/\rho = \tfrac13$) and in the very dense matter of neutron stars (Schutz §4.8).
>
> In general relativity the pressure also acts as a source of gravity ([[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^thm-c5-5-4|Theorem §C5.5.4]]). In a compact star the two effects compound: a stronger field needs a larger pressure gradient, a larger pressure weighs more and adds to the field. This is the reason relativistic stars have a maximum mass (Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff equation, [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-4|Theorem §C9.1.4]]). The opening of [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations|§C9.1]] estimates how the two effects cap the core a central pressure can hold up, and [[§C9.2 The Uniform-Density Star and Buchdahl's Limit#^thm-c9-2-5|Theorem §C9.2.5]] turns this into an exact bound on $m(r)/r$ that tightens as the pressure rises.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]] with local conservation; *applies:* exact; part 4 is an order-of-magnitude statement.
>
> *Source: Schutz §4.7, eqs. (4.54)–(4.55), p. 103 ("$\rho + p$ plays the role of the 'inertial mass density'"); §4.8, pp. 103–104 · Hartle §22.2, Example 22.5, eq. (22.35), p. 479 · Couzens, GR2, §3.1, eqs. (3.7), (3.9), p. 55*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the force on a cube). In the rest frame at an event take local inertial coordinates and a small cube of side $L$ with faces at $x$ and $x + L$. The fluid outside pushes on each face along its inward normal with force $p\,L^2$ (Hartle, Example 22.5). In the $x$ direction: $F^x = p(x)\,L^2 - p(x + L)\,L^2 = -L^3\,\partial_xp + O(L^4)$, by Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]); the faces normal to $y$ and $z$ give no $x$ component. So the force per unit volume is $f^x = F^x/L^3 \to -\partial_xp$, and likewise $f^y$, $f^z$. In terms of the stress, $f^i = -\partial_jT^{ij}$ with $T^{ij} = p\,\delta^{ij}$ (Hartle (22.33)).
>
> **Step 2** (Euler in the rest frame). By [[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]], part 3, in the rest frame $(\rho + p)\,a^{\hat i} = -\partial_{\hat i}\,p = f^{\hat i}$. Newton's second law for the matter in a unit volume, (mass) $\times$ $a$ $=$ force, identifies the mass with $\rho + p$. Restoring units, $a$ in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $f$ in $\mathrm{N/m^3}$ require $(\rho + p)/c^2$ in $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. For $p = \rho/3$: $\rho + \rho/3 = \tfrac43\rho$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (momentum density). [[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]], part 5, gives $T^{0i} = \gamma^2(\rho + p)v^i$, and $\gamma^2 = (1 - v^2)^{-1} = 1 + v^2 + O(v^4)$. A Newtonian momentum density is (mass density) $\times$ velocity, again with the mass density $\rho + p$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (support). If the worldlines of the fluid are prescribed, so is $a^\lambda = U^\nu\nabla_\nu U^\lambda$; [[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]], part 3, then fixes the projected pressure gradient as $-(\rho + p)\,a^\lambda$. A freely falling element would have the acceleration $-a$ relative to the supported one; in Schutz's notation that is his $a^i$, hence his sign. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (size). For an ideal gas $p/\rho = nk_BT/[n(m + e)] \approx k_BT/m$; the number for air is computed in [[#^ex-c4-3-3|Example §C4.3.3]]. This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Part 1 and part 2 are one fact seen twice: the energy current of moving matter is $(\rho + p)\vec v$, because a moving fluid element carries its energy $\rho$ and also transports energy through the work $p\vec v$ its pressure does on the fluid ahead. (Energy flux and momentum density are the same component $T^{0i}$, [[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^rem-c4-2-1|§C4.2, Remark: Why momentum density equals energy flux]].) In Newtonian mechanics this work is negligible against the rest energy, which is why pressure there has no inertia.

## The Newtonian limit and hydrostatic equilibrium

Do the relativistic equations contain the fluid mechanics that has always worked? Put the fluid into the weak field of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]] and let it move slowly.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.5: The Newtonian Limit: Continuity and Euler with Gravity
> Let a perfect fluid with $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ move in the static weak-field metric $ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi)\,dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)\,\delta_{ij}\,dx^idx^j$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]), with coordinate velocity $v^i = dx^i/dt$, and let $\Phi$, $v^2$ and $p/\rho$ be of order $\varepsilon \ll 1$, with the flow changing over a length $L$ and a time $L/v$. Then, up to relative corrections of order $\varepsilon$:
> 1. **Continuity** (from the energy equation, [[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]], part 2):
>
> $$
> \partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot(\rho\vec v) = 0 .
> $$
>
> 2. **Euler's equation with gravity** (from the relativistic Euler equation, part 3):
>
> $$
> \rho\Bigl(\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} + (\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v\Bigr) = -\nabla p - \rho\,\nabla\Phi .
> $$
>
> 3. **Hydrostatic equilibrium.** For a fluid at rest, $\vec v = 0$: $\nabla p = -\rho\,\nabla\Phi$; in a uniform field $\Phi = gy$ this is $dp/dy = -\rho g$, the hydrostatic law of level A ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-3|CM Theorem §A9.1.3]]).
>
> Restored, with the mass density $\rho_m = \rho/c^2$ and $\Phi$ in $\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$: $\partial_t\rho_m + \nabla\cdot(\rho_m\vec v) = 0$ and $\rho_m(\partial_t + \vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v = -\nabla p - \rho_m\nabla\Phi$. The first is the local form of the equation of continuity ([[§A9.4 Flow and Continuity#^thm-a9-4-2|CM Theorem §A9.4.2]] is its integral for steady flow in a pipe).
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]] in [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]: a static external field, the fluid's own field included in $\Phi$ only if it changes slowly; *applies:* first order in $\varepsilon$. With a slowly changing $\Phi(t, \vec x)$ the time-dependent Christoffel symbols of [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^ex-c3-6-3|Example §C3.6.3]] add terms of relative order $\varepsilon$ only.
>
> *Source: Schutz, Exercise 7.5, eqs. (7.38)–(7.39), p. 180 · d'Inverno & Vickers §12.2, eqs. (12.8)–(12.10), pp. 204–205 (special relativity, no gravity) · leading orders of both components checked by script, with an explicit bookkeeping parameter*

^thm-c4-3-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 0** (orders). $\Phi = O(\varepsilon)$, $v^2 = O(\varepsilon)$, $p = O(\varepsilon\rho)$. Spatial derivatives bring a factor $1/L$; time derivatives of the fluid fields a factor $v/L$, so $\partial_tv = O(\varepsilon/L)$; $\partial_t\Phi = 0$. Each step keeps the leading terms and displays the order of the dropped ones.
>
> **Step 1** (the four-velocity). From $g_{\mu\nu}U^\mu U^\nu = -1$ with $U^\mu = U^0(1, v^i)$: $(U^0)^2\bigl[(1 + 2\Phi) - (1 - 2\Phi)v^2\bigr] = 1$, so, inverting $d\tau/dt = 1 + \Phi - \tfrac12v^2 + O(\varepsilon^2)$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]], part 1),
>
> $$
> U^0 = \frac{dt}{d\tau} = 1 - \Phi + \tfrac12v^2 + O(\varepsilon^2), \qquad U^i = U^0v^i = v^i\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)] .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the volume factor). $\sqrt{-g} = \sqrt{(1 + 2\Phi)(1 - 2\Phi)^3} = 1 - 2\Phi + O(\varepsilon^2)$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^ex-c3-6-3|Example §C3.6.3]], Step 3).
>
> **Step 3** (the energy equation). Along the flow, $\frac{d\rho}{d\tau} = U^0(\partial_t\rho + v^i\partial_i\rho) = (\partial_t\rho + \vec v\cdot\nabla\rho)\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)]$. For the expansion use the divergence formula ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-4|Theorem §C3.6.4]], part 1), $\theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-g}\,U^\mu)$, with Steps 1–2:
>
> $$
> \sqrt{-g}\,U^0 = (1 - 2\Phi)\bigl(1 - \Phi + \tfrac12v^2\bigr) = 1 - 3\Phi + \tfrac12v^2 + O(\varepsilon^2), \qquad \sqrt{-g}\,U^i = v^i\bigl(1 - 3\Phi + \tfrac12v^2\bigr) + O(\varepsilon^2v) .
> $$
>
> Since $\partial_t\Phi = 0$, $\partial_t(\sqrt{-g}\,U^0) = v^j\partial_tv^j + \dots = O(\varepsilon v/L)$, and $\partial_i(\sqrt{-g}\,U^i) = \partial_iv^i + O(\varepsilon v/L)$; with $1/\sqrt{-g} = 1 + O(\varepsilon)$, $\theta = \nabla\cdot\vec v + O(\varepsilon v/L)$.
>
> The energy equation, with $\rho + p = \rho\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)]$, becomes $\partial_t\rho + \vec v\cdot\nabla\rho + \rho\,\nabla\cdot\vec v = O(\varepsilon\rho v/L)$, and the left side is $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot(\rho\vec v)$, of order $\rho v/L$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (the acceleration). $a^i = U^\nu\partial_\nu U^i + \Gamma^i{}_{\mu\nu}U^\mu U^\nu$ ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^thm-c3-5-1|Theorem §C3.5.1]]). The first term, by the product rule,
>
> $$
> U^\nu\partial_\nu(U^0v^i) = (U^0)^2\,(\partial_t + \vec v\cdot\nabla)\,v^i + U^0\,v^i\,(\partial_t + \vec v\cdot\nabla)\,U^0 = (\partial_t + \vec v\cdot\nabla)\,v^i\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)] + O(\varepsilon^2/L) ,
> $$
>
> since $(\partial_t + \vec v\cdot\nabla)U^0 = -\vec v\cdot\nabla\Phi + \vec v\cdot(\partial_t + \vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v + \dots = O(\varepsilon v/L)$ and it is multiplied by $v^i$. The Christoffel symbols of the static weak-field metric ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^ex-c3-6-3|Example §C3.6.3]]) are $\Gamma^i{}_{00} = \partial_i\Phi$, $\Gamma^i{}_{0j} = 0$ and $\Gamma^i{}_{jk} = \delta_{jk}\partial_i\Phi - \delta_{ik}\partial_j\Phi - \delta_{ij}\partial_k\Phi$, so
>
> $$
> \Gamma^i{}_{\mu\nu}U^\mu U^\nu = \partial_i\Phi\,(U^0)^2 + 0 + (U^0)^2\bigl(v^2\,\partial_i\Phi - 2v^i\,\vec v\cdot\nabla\Phi\bigr) = \partial_i\Phi + O(\varepsilon^2/L) .
> $$
>
> Altogether $a^i = \partial_tv^i + (\vec v\cdot\nabla)v^i + \partial_i\Phi + O(\varepsilon^2/L)$, each kept term of order $\varepsilon/L$. Gravity enters through $\Gamma^i{}_{00}$, that is through $g_{00}$ alone, as for a single particle.
>
> **Step 5** (the pressure term). With $g^{ij} = \delta^{ij}/(1 - 2\Phi)$ and $g^{i0} = 0$,
>
> $$
> h^{i\mu}\partial_\mu p = g^{ij}\partial_jp + U^iU^\mu\partial_\mu p = \partial_ip\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)] + v^i\,(\partial_t + \vec v\cdot\nabla)\,p\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)] = \partial_ip + O(\varepsilon^2\rho/L) ,
> $$
>
> because $\partial_ip = O(\varepsilon\rho/L)$ while $v^i(\partial_t + \vec v\cdot\nabla)p = O(v\cdot vp/L) = O(\varepsilon^2\rho/L)$.
>
> **Step 6** (Euler). Insert Steps 4–5 into $(\rho + p)\,a^i = -h^{i\mu}\partial_\mu p$ ([[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]], part 3) with $\rho + p = \rho\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)]$:
>
> $$
> \rho\bigl[\partial_tv^i + (\vec v\cdot\nabla)v^i + \partial_i\Phi\bigr] = -\partial_ip + O(\varepsilon^2\rho/L) ,
> $$
>
> where all kept terms are of order $\varepsilon\rho/L$. This is part 2. For $\vec v = 0$ it reduces to $\partial_ip = -\rho\,\partial_i\Phi$; with $\Phi = gy$ near the Earth's surface ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^mod-a8-1-5|CM Model §A8.1.5]], restored $\Phi/c^2 = gy/c^2$), $dp/dy = -\rho g$. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 7** (check). The script repeats the computation with a bookkeeping parameter: coordinates $(T, x, y, z)$ with $T = \sqrt\varepsilon\,t$, $\Phi \to \varepsilon\Phi$, $v \to \sqrt\varepsilon\,v$, $p \to \varepsilon p$, and the exact covariant divergence expanded in $\varepsilon$. The $\nu = T$ component starts at order $\varepsilon$ with the continuity equation, the $\nu = x$ component at order $\varepsilon$ with $\partial_T(\rho v_x) + \partial_j(\rho v_xv_j) + \partial_xp + \rho\,\partial_x\Phi$, which is $v_x$ times the continuity equation plus Euler's equation.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Newtonian hydrodynamics is the leading order of $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ in a weak static field: the energy equation becomes the conservation of mass, because rest energy dominates $\rho$, and the Euler equation acquires gravity through the single Christoffel symbol $\Gamma^i{}_{00} = \partial_i\Phi$.
> - Everything that distinguishes $\rho$ from the rest-mass density, and $\rho + p$ from $\rho$, is of relative order $\varepsilon$ and drops out: the Newtonian limit cannot see the inertia of pressure, as Step 9 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]] found for $\lambda$.

In a strong field the weak-field expansion is not available. For a fluid at rest in a static spacetime, the case of an atmosphere or a star, the Euler equation can be solved exactly.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.6: Hydrostatic Equilibrium in a Static Metric
> Let the metric be static ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-5|Def. §C3.13.5]]) and written in adapted coordinates ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-6|Theorem §C3.13.6]], part 2), with the function $\Phi(x^k)$ defined by $g_{00} = -e^{2\Phi}$: $ds^2 = -e^{2\Phi(x^k)}\,dt^2 + g_{ij}(x^k)\,dx^idx^j$.
>
> Let a perfect fluid be at rest, $U = e^{-\Phi}\,\partial_t$ (the static observers), with $\rho$ and $p$ independent of $t$, and let $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$. Then:
> 1. **The static observers accelerate.** $a^0 = 0$ and $a_i = \partial_i\Phi$: to stay at rest a fluid element must be pushed "up", along $\nabla\Phi$.
> 2. **Hydrostatic equilibrium.** The energy equation holds identically, and the Euler equation is equivalent to
>
> $$
> \partial_ip = -(\rho + p)\,\partial_i\Phi \qquad (i = 1, 2, 3) .
> $$
>
> 3. **A first integral.** For a barotropic equation of state $p(\rho)$ ([[#^def-c4-3-1|Def. §C4.3.1]]), with $F(p) = \int^p dp'/\bigl(\rho(p') + p'\bigr)$, $F(p) + \Phi = \text{const}$ on every connected region of fluid: the surfaces of constant pressure are the surfaces of constant $\Phi$, the surfaces on which static clocks run at the same rate.
> 4. **Spherical symmetry and the Newtonian limit.** For $ds^2 = -e^{2\Phi(r)}dt^2 + e^{2\Lambda(r)}dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$, part 2 reads $dp/dr = -(\rho + p)\,d\Phi/dr$. In the weak field $e^{2\Phi} = 1 + 2\Phi + O(\Phi^2)$, so $\Phi$ is the Newtonian potential of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]] to first order; with $p \ll \rho$ and $d\Phi/dr = M(r)/r^2$ ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]], part 3) it becomes Newtonian hydrostatic equilibrium $dp/dr = -\rho M(r)/r^2$ ([[#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]], part 3). In general relativity the field equations give $d\Phi/dr = \bigl(m(r) + 4\pi r^3p\bigr)/\bigl(r(r - 2m(r))\bigr)$, and part 2 becomes the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff equation ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-4|Theorem §C9.1.4]]).
>
> Restored: $\partial_ip = -(\rho + p)\,\partial_i\Phi/c^2$ with $\rho$ the energy density and $\Phi$ in $\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$; with the mass density, $\partial_ip = -(\rho_m + p/c^2)\,\partial_i\Phi$.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]] at rest in a static metric; *applies:* exact, any strength of the field; part 4's Newtonian form to first order.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §3.1, eqs. (3.1)–(3.3), (3.7), (3.9)–(3.10), pp. 54–55 · Schutz §4.8, p. 104; Exercise 7.5(c), eq. (7.39), p. 180 · d'Inverno & Vickers §15.8, p. 282 (static fluid) · checked by script (general static diagonal metric; the spherical case also in the chapter checks)*

^thm-c4-3-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (normalization). $g(U, U) = g_{00}(U^0)^2 = -e^{2\Phi}e^{-2\Phi} = -1$, and $U^i = 0$, $U_0 = g_{00}U^0 = -e^{\Phi}$, $U_i = 0$.
>
> **Step 2** (the Christoffel symbols needed). With $g_{0i} = 0$, $g^{00} = -e^{-2\Phi}$, $g^{i0} = 0$ and $\partial_0g_{\mu\nu} = 0$, [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]] gives
>
> $$
> \Gamma^0{}_{00} = \tfrac12g^{00}\bigl(2\partial_0g_{00} - \partial_0g_{00}\bigr) = 0, \qquad \Gamma^i{}_{00} = \tfrac12g^{ij}\bigl(\partial_0g_{0j} + \partial_0g_{0j} - \partial_jg_{00}\bigr) = \tfrac12g^{ij}\,\partial_je^{2\Phi} = e^{2\Phi}g^{ij}\,\partial_j\Phi .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (acceleration). $a^\mu = U^\nu\partial_\nu U^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\lambda}U^\nu U^\lambda$, and only $U^0 = e^{-\Phi}$ is nonzero, which does not depend on $t$, so $U^\nu\partial_\nu U^\mu = e^{-\Phi}\partial_tU^\mu = 0$. Hence
>
> $$
> a^0 = \Gamma^0{}_{00}(U^0)^2 = 0, \qquad a^i = \Gamma^i{}_{00}(U^0)^2 = e^{2\Phi}g^{ij}\partial_j\Phi\;e^{-2\Phi} = g^{ij}\,\partial_j\Phi, \qquad a_k = g_{ki}a^i = \partial_k\Phi .
> $$
>
> This is part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (energy equation). $d\rho/d\tau = U^0\partial_t\rho = 0$. By the divergence formula ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-4|Theorem §C3.6.4]], part 1), $\theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-g}\,U^\mu) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_t\bigl(\sqrt{-g}\,e^{-\Phi}\bigr) = 0$, since nothing depends on $t$. So [[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]], part 2, reads $0 + (\rho + p)\cdot 0 = 0$.
>
> **Step 5** (Euler). $h^{i\mu}\partial_\mu p = (g^{i\mu} + U^iU^\mu)\,\partial_\mu p = g^{ij}\partial_jp$, since $g^{i0} = 0$ and $U^i = 0$; $h^{0\mu}\partial_\mu p = (g^{00} + U^0U^0)\,\partial_tp = 0$. [[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]], part 3, is therefore $(\rho + p)\,g^{ij}\partial_j\Phi = -g^{ij}\partial_jp$. Multiplying by $g_{ki}$ and summing over $i$ ($g_{ki}g^{ij} = \delta_k{}^j$): $\partial_kp = -(\rho + p)\,\partial_k\Phi$.
>
> This is part 2.
>
> **Step 6** (first integral). With $p = p(\rho)$ invertible, $\rho + p$ is a function of $p$ alone, and $\partial_kF(p) = F'(p)\,\partial_kp = \partial_kp/(\rho + p) = -\partial_k\Phi$ by Step 5. So $\partial_k(F + \Phi) = 0$ for $k = 1, 2, 3$, and $F + \Phi$ is constant on each connected region. On a surface $\Phi = \text{const}$ therefore $F(p)$, hence $p$, is constant; and $\Phi$ fixes the clock rate $d\tau/dt = e^{\Phi}$ of static observers ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-1|Theorem §C1.5.1]] in weak fields). This is part 3.
>
> **Step 7** (spherical case and weak field). For the spherical metric only $\partial_r\Phi \neq 0$, and Step 5 with $k = r$ gives $dp/dr = -(\rho + p)\,\Phi'$; the components $k = \theta, \varphi$ give $\partial_\theta p = \partial_\varphi p = 0$. In the weak field $-e^{2\Phi} = -(1 + 2\Phi + 2\Phi^2 + \dots)$ agrees with $g_{00} = -(1 + 2\Phi_{\mathrm N})$ of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]] when $\Phi = \Phi_{\mathrm N} + O(\Phi_{\mathrm N}^2)$. Then $(\rho + p)\Phi' = \rho\,\Phi_{\mathrm N}'\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)]$, and $\Phi_{\mathrm N}' = M(r)/r^2$ gives the Newtonian form. Inserting Couzens' $d\Phi/dr$ (GR2 (3.7)) into $dp/dr = -(\rho + p)\,d\Phi/dr$ gives his (3.9). This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - In a static spacetime "at rest" is not free fall: the static observers have the four-acceleration $\nabla\Phi$ of part 1, and the pressure gradient supplies it, with the inertial factor $\rho + p$ of [[#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]].
> - Only $g_{00}$ enters, through $\Phi$; the spatial metric $g_{ij}$ only raises and lowers the index and drops out of part 2. The same was true of Newton's force in the weak field ([[#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]], Step 4).

The fluid equations of this section were derived by assuming $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$. In flat spacetime its integral says that energy and momentum are conserved. In a curved spacetime, what, if anything, does it conserve? That is the question of [[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime|§C4.4]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C4.3.1: Radiation p = ρ/3 Is Traceless
> *Setup.* Isotropic radiation: photons, or any massless particles, moving in all directions alike, as in a cavity in equilibrium ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^mod-b11-1-1|TH Model §B11.1.1]]), seen in a local inertial frame in which no direction is preferred.
>
> *Step 1* (one beam). Let $n$ massless particles per unit volume all move along the unit vector $\hat n$, each with energy $E$, so momentum $E\hat n$ and speed $1$. As for the beam of [[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^ex-c4-2-2|Example §C4.2.2]]: the energy density is $nE$; the energy crossing a unit area normal to $x^i$ per unit time is $n\,n^i\,E$; the $j$-momentum crossing it is $n\,n^i\,E\,n^j$. With $k^\mu = (1, \hat n)$,
>
> $$
> T^{\mu\nu}_{\text{beam}} = nE\;k^\mu k^\nu, \qquad \eta_{\mu\nu}T^{\mu\nu}_{\text{beam}} = nE\,(-1 + \hat n\cdot\hat n) = 0 .
> $$
>
> Every beam of massless particles is traceless, because $k$ is null.
>
> *Step 2* (average over directions). Let beams of total energy density $\rho$ point uniformly in all directions. Then $T^{\mu\nu} = \rho\,\langle k^\mu k^\nu\rangle$, with $\langle\cdot\rangle$ the average over the unit sphere: $\langle1\rangle = 1$, $\langle n^i\rangle = 0$ (antipodal directions cancel), and, in polar angles about the $z$ axis,
>
> $$
> \langle n_z^2\rangle = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!d\varphi\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\,\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{1}{4\pi}\cdot2\pi\cdot\frac23 = \frac13 ;
> $$
>
> by the same isotropy argument as in Step 2 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]], $\langle n^in^j\rangle = c\,\delta^{ij}$, and its trace $\langle\hat n\cdot\hat n\rangle = 1$ gives $c = \tfrac13$. Hence
>
> $$
> T^{00} = \rho, \qquad T^{0i} = \rho\,\langle n^i\rangle = 0, \qquad T^{ij} = \rho\,\langle n^in^j\rangle = \frac\rho3\,\delta^{ij} .
> $$
>
> This is a perfect fluid at rest ([[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]], part 1) with $p = \rho/3$, the radiation pressure of thermodynamics ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-3|TH Theorem §B11.1.3]]).
>
> *Step 3* (trace). By [[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]], part 3, $T^\mu{}_\mu = -\rho + 3p = -\rho + \rho = 0$, as the average of traceless beams must be. The trace is a scalar, so it vanishes in every frame and, by minimal coupling, in every spacetime. The electromagnetic $T^{\mu\nu}$ is traceless for every field configuration ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^ex-c4-2-3|Example §C4.2.3]]); isotropic radiation is its average.
>
> *Massive particles.* If the particles have mass $m$, speed $u < 1$ and energy $E = m/\sqrt{1 - u^2}$, the beam has $T^{\mu\nu} = nE\,w^\mu w^\nu$ with $w^\mu = (1, u\hat n)$ (the momentum crossing the area is now $n\,u\,n^i\,Eu\,n^j$), and its trace is $nE(-1 + u^2) < 0$. Averaging over directions gives $\rho = nE$ and $p = \tfrac13nEu^2 < \tfrac13\rho$. A gas of particles therefore has $T^\mu{}_\mu = -\rho + 3p \le 0$, with equality only in the massless limit; dust ($u = 0$) is the other extreme, $T^\mu{}_\mu = -\rho$.
>
> *Source: Hartle §22.1, p. 479 (the background radiation as a fluid with $p = \rho/3$); Problem 22.5, p. 487 (a box of gas with an isotropic momentum distribution) · Schutz §4.7, p. 101 ("pressure arises in the random velocities of the particles") · averages and traces checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C4.3.2: Vacuum Energy p = −ρ Looks the Same to Every Observer
> *Setup.* Suppose empty space has an energy density $\rho$, the same at every event, and is a perfect fluid with $p = -\rho$ (Hartle's vacuum energy; [[#^def-c4-3-1|Def. §C4.3.1]]).
>
> *Step 1* (the tensor). In [[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]], $\rho + p = 0$, so the term with $U$ drops out: $T^{\mu\nu} = (\rho - \rho)\,U^\mu U^\nu - \rho\,g^{\mu\nu} = -\rho\,g^{\mu\nu}$.
>
> *Step 2* (every observer sees the same). Let any observer have four-velocity $u$ ($u\cdot u = -1$) and orthonormal spatial axes $e_i$ ($e_i\cdot u = 0$, $e_i\cdot e_j = \delta_{ij}$). With the readings of [[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^def-c4-2-2|Def. §C4.2.2]] and $T_{\mu\nu} = -\rho\,g_{\mu\nu}$:
>
> $$
> T_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = -\rho\,(u\cdot u) = \rho, \qquad -T_{\mu\nu}u^\mu e_i^\nu = \rho\,(u\cdot e_i) = 0, \qquad T_{\mu\nu}e_i^\mu e_j^\nu = -\rho\,\delta_{ij} .
> $$
>
> Every observer, whatever its velocity, measures the energy density $\rho$, no momentum and the isotropic pressure $-\rho$, a tension. Vacuum energy has no rest frame: in a local inertial frame $T^{\mu\nu} = -\rho\,\eta^{\mu\nu}$, and $\Lambda\eta\Lambda^{\mathsf T} = \eta$ for every Lorentz transformation ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]). (Checked by script for an arbitrary $u$.) Hartle notes that $-\rho\,g^{\mu\nu}$ is the only energy–momentum tensor built from a constant alone.
>
> *Step 3* (why $p = -\rho$). Enclose a volume $\mathcal V$ of vacuum and enlarge it by $d\mathcal V$, for instance with a piston. The energy inside grows by $\rho\,d\mathcal V$, since the density stays $\rho$. With no heat exchanged, the first law ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|TH Principle §A3.3.1]], in the form of [[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]], part 4) requires $d(\rho\mathcal V) = -p\,d\mathcal V$, so $\rho\,d\mathcal V = -p\,d\mathcal V$ and $p = -\rho$: to pull the piston out one has to do work against a tension.
>
> *Step 4* (conservation). Since $\nabla_\mu g^{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-5|Theorem §C3.6.5]]), $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = -g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\mu\rho$, which vanishes if and only if $\rho$ is constant. In [[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]] the energy equation reads $d\rho/d\tau + 0\cdot\theta = 0$ and the Euler equation $0\cdot a^\lambda = -h^{\lambda\mu}\partial_\mu\rho$: the vacuum has zero inertia ([[#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]]), so no "flow" $U$ can be attributed to it.
>
> *Preview.* With $\rho = \Lambda/8\pi$ (restored $\rho = \Lambda c^4/(8\pi G)$), $T^{\mu\nu} = -(\Lambda/8\pi)\,g^{\mu\nu}$ is the cosmological constant written as a source (Hartle (22.38); [[§C5.6 The Cosmological Constant and Higher-Curvature Terms#^thm-c5-6-2|Theorem §C5.6.2]]). It violates the strong energy condition ([[§C4.5 Energy Conditions#^ex-c4-5-2|Example §C4.5.2]]).
>
> *Source: Hartle §22.1, Example 22.6, eq. (22.38), p. 480; Problems 22.8–22.9, p. 488*

> [!example] Example §C4.3.3: The Law of Atmospheres from the Euler Equation
> *Setup* (Hartle, Problem 22.3). An atmosphere of molecules of mass $m$ at a constant temperature $T$, an ideal gas, $p = nk_BT$ with $n$ the number density ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|TH Law §A2.1.1]]), at rest in the Earth's field, at heights $z$ small compared with the Earth's radius $R$; the Earth's gravity is the only force. Numbers: air, $m = 28.96\ \mathrm u = 4.809\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $T = 288.15\ \mathrm K$, $g = 9.80665\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, $R = 6.371\times10^6\ \mathrm m$.
>
> *Newtonian solution.* By [[#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]], part 3, with the mass density $nm$ and $\Phi = gz$: $dp/dz = -nmg$. Replace $n = p/(k_BT)$:
>
> $$
> \frac{dp}{dz} = -\frac{mg}{k_BT}\,p \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p(z) = p_{\text{sea}}\,e^{-z/H}, \qquad H = \frac{k_BT}{mg} ,
> $$
>
> the isothermal atmosphere of level A ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-6|CM Theorem §A9.1.6]]). With $k_BT = 3.978\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm J$, $H = 8436\ \mathrm m$. At $z = 1\ \mathrm{km}$, $z/H = 0.1185$ and $p/p_{\text{sea}} = 0.888$; at the summit of Everest, $z = 8849\ \mathrm m$, $z/H = 1.049$ and $p/p_{\text{sea}} = 0.350$.
>
> *Exact relativistic solution.* By [[#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]], part 3. The energy density counts the rest energy and the internal energy, $\rho = n(m + e)$, with $e = \tfrac52k_BT$ for diatomic molecules at room temperature (equipartition, [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|TH Theorem §A2.3.2]]). So
>
> $$
> \rho + p = n\,h, \qquad h = m + \tfrac72k_BT \qquad (\text{restored } h = mc^2 + \tfrac72k_BT) ,
> $$
>
> the enthalpy per molecule, constant at fixed $T$. With $n = p/(k_BT)$:
>
> $$
> F(p) = \int\frac{dp}{\rho + p} = \int\frac{k_BT\,dp}{h\,p} = \frac{k_BT}{h}\,\ln p, \qquad \frac{k_BT}{h}\ln\frac{p}{p_{\text{sea}}} = -\bigl(\Phi - \Phi_{\text{sea}}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> $$
> p(z) = p_{\text{sea}}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{h}{k_BT}\bigl(\Phi(z) - \Phi_{\text{sea}}\bigr)\Bigr] \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \exp\Bigl[-\frac{h}{c^2}\,\frac{\Phi(z) - \Phi_{\text{sea}}}{k_BT}\Bigr]\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *What differs from Newton.* Three corrections to the exponent $mgz/(k_BT)$:
> 1. **Heat and pressure weigh.** The mass $m$ is replaced by $h/c^2 = m\bigl(1 + \tfrac72k_BT/mc^2\bigr)$. For air $k_BT/mc^2 = 3.978\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm J/4.322\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm J = 9.20\times10^{-13}$, so the correction is $3.22\times10^{-12}$. The same number $p/\rho \approx k_BT/mc^2 = 9.20\times10^{-13}$ is the size of the inertia of pressure at sea level ([[#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]], part 4).
> 2. **The field is not uniform.** With the Newtonian potential $-GM/r$, $\Phi(R + z) - \Phi(R) = GM/R - GM/(R + z) = gz/(1 + z/R)$, $g = GM/R^2$; at the summit $z/R = 1.39\times10^{-3}$. This Newtonian correction is about $4\times10^8$ times the relativistic one: the exponent at the summit is $1.04896$ for a uniform field and $1.04751$ with both corrections.
> 3. **$e^{2\Phi}$ against $1 + 2\Phi$.** The exact $\Phi$ of the theorem differs from the Newtonian one by $O(\Phi^2)$, about $(6.95\times10^{-10})^2 = 5\times10^{-19}$ at the Earth's surface ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]]).
>
> An atmosphere cannot reveal the inertia of pressure; a neutron star, with $p$ comparable with $\rho$, cannot be described without it ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^ex-c9-1-1|Example §C9.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Hartle, Problem 22.3, p. 487 · Couzens, GR2, §3.1, eq. (3.10), p. 55 (the Newtonian limit of hydrostatic equilibrium) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C4.3.4: The Fluid in d'Inverno's Signature
> *Setup.* d'Inverno & Vickers use the signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, with $u^au_a = +1$, and write the perfect fluid as (12.22) $T^{\mathrm{dI}}_{ab} = (\rho + p)\,u_au_b - p\,g^{\mathrm{dI}}_{ab}$.
>
> Convert with the dictionary of [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]] (stated for level B, the same rule for any metric): $g^{\mathrm{dI}}_{ab} = -g_{ab}$, the upper components $u^a$ are shared, and a lowered component flips, $u^{\mathrm{dI}}_a = -u_a$.
>
> *Step 1* (lower indices: equal). $T^{\mathrm{dI}}_{ab} = (\rho + p)(-u_a)(-u_b) - p\,(-g_{ab}) = (\rho + p)\,u_au_b + p\,g_{ab} = T_{ab}$.
>
> *Step 2* (upper indices: equal). Raising both indices uses $g_{\mathrm{dI}}^{ab} = -g^{ab}$ twice, and $(-1)^2 = 1$: $T^{ab}_{\mathrm{dI}} = T^{ab}$; in particular $T^{00}$ is the energy density in both.
>
> *Step 3* (mixed components and trace: flipped). One inverse metric: $(T^{\mathrm{dI}})^a{}_b = g_{\mathrm{dI}}^{ac}T^{\mathrm{dI}}_{cb} = -g^{ac}T_{cb} = -T^a{}_b$. In the rest frame d'Inverno has $T^0{}_0 = +\rho$, $T^i{}_j = -p\,\delta^i_j$ (his (15.66)–(15.67)); here $T^0{}_0 = -\rho$, $T^i{}_j = +p\,\delta^i_j$. The trace is $\rho - 3p$ there and $-\rho + 3p$ here.
>
> *Step 4* (his constants). His $S_{ab} = \lambda u_au_b + \mu g_{ab}$ with $(\lambda, \mu) = (1, -1)$ becomes, by Step 1's rule, $\lambda u_au_b - \mu g_{ab}$ here, i.e. $(\lambda, \mu) = (1, 1)$ of Step 10 in the derivation of [[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]].
>
> So a formula with both indices down or both up can be copied from d'Inverno & Vickers unchanged; one with mixed indices, or a trace, changes sign. (All four statements checked by script.)
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers §12.4, eqs. (12.20)–(12.22), p. 207; §15.8, eqs. (15.65)–(15.67), p. 282*
