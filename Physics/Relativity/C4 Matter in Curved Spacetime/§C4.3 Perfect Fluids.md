[[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor|§C4.2]] built the energy–momentum tensor of dust, $T^{\mu\nu} = \rho U^\mu U^\nu$ ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]), by counting particles that all move together. The matter of a star, the air of an atmosphere or the radiation of the early universe is not like that. Its particles move at random relative to one another, or push on one another, and this pushes on any surface placed in the material: the force per unit area is the pressure ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-3|CM Def. §A9.1.3]]). This section asks two questions. *What is the energy–momentum tensor of matter with pressure?* And *what equations of motion does local conservation, $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, impose on it?* The answers are the relativistic forms of the two equations of fluid mechanics, the continuity equation and Euler's equation, in any spacetime, and they contain one result that Newtonian fluid mechanics cannot see: pressure has inertia.

First, what fails. Keep the dust tensor and add the pressure where Newtonian mechanics puts it, as an isotropic stress $p\,\delta^{ij}$ in the spatial components ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^rem-a9-1-2|CM §A9.1, Remark: Pressure has no direction, its force does]]), in the inertial frame of a laboratory in which the fluid moves with speed $v$ along $x$, $U^\mu = \gamma(1, v, 0, 0)$:

$$
T^{\mu\nu}_{\text{naive}} = \rho\,U^\mu U^\nu + \operatorname{diag}(0, p, p, p) .
$$

Transform it to the rest frame of the fluid. The boost has $\Lambda^0{}_0 = \Lambda^1{}_1 = \gamma$, $\Lambda^0{}_1 = \Lambda^1{}_0 = -\gamma v$, $\Lambda^2{}_2 = \Lambda^3{}_3 = 1$, and a $(2, 0)$ tensor transforms as $T'^{\mu\nu} = \Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta T^{\alpha\beta}$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]]). The dust part becomes $\rho\,\delta^\mu_0\delta^\nu_0$, since $U'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu U^\nu = (1, 0, 0, 0)$. The added stress has only the components $T^{11} = T^{22} = T^{33} = p$, and it contributes

$$
\Lambda^0{}_1\Lambda^0{}_1\,p = \gamma^2v^2p, \qquad \Lambda^0{}_1\Lambda^1{}_1\,p = -\gamma^2vp, \qquad \Lambda^1{}_1\Lambda^1{}_1\,p = \gamma^2p, \qquad \Lambda^2{}_2\Lambda^2{}_2\,p = p .
$$

So in the fluid's own rest frame

$$
T'^{00} = \rho + \gamma^2v^2p, \qquad T'^{01} = -\gamma^2vp, \qquad T'^{11} = \gamma^2p, \qquad T'^{22} = T'^{33} = p ;
$$

at $v = 3/5$ ($\gamma = 5/4$) these are $\rho + \tfrac{9}{16}p$, $-\tfrac{15}{16}p$, $\tfrac{25}{16}p$ and $p$ (checked by script). The fluid at rest would have an energy density other than $\rho$, would carry energy through itself (a heat flow, $T'^{01} \neq 0$) although nothing in it conducts heat, and would push harder along $x$ than across. All three defects depend on the speed of the laboratory in which the pressure was added: "add $p\,\delta^{ij}$" is a rule tied to one frame, not a tensor equation, the defect found for the Newtonian source in [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-4|Theorem §C1.1.4]], part 3. Pressure is a property of the fluid, so it has to be stated where the fluid singles out a frame, its rest frame, and then carried to every other frame as the components of one tensor.

The section builds this in order. The perfect fluid is the matter that is isotropic in its rest frame ([[#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]]); isotropy and covariance fix its tensor, $T^{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)U^\mu U^\nu + p\,g^{\mu\nu}$, and d'Inverno's route through the Newtonian limit leads to the same tensor ([[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]]). An equation of state closes the system ([[#^def-c4-3-1|Def. §C4.3.1]]). The projector onto the rest space ([[#^def-c4-3-2|Def. §C4.3.2]]) splits $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ into an energy equation along $U$ and a relativistic Euler equation across it ([[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]]). The Euler equation shows that the inertial mass density is $\rho + p$ ([[#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]]). In the weak field of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric|§C1.5]] the two equations become Newton's continuity and Euler equations with gravity ([[#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]]), and in a static spacetime they give the exact law of hydrostatic equilibrium, $p' = -(\rho + p)\Phi'$ ([[#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]]).

**Setup.** A fluid in a spacetime $(M, g)$: a continuum whose state at each event is given by a few fields, averaged over a fluid element that is small compared with the lengths over which the fields change but holds many particles. The metric is given. The fluid may be a test fluid or the source of the metric, but the field equations (C5, planned) are not used. Every fluid element has a four-velocity $U$. Its rest frame at an event is the local inertial frame ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-4|Def. §C3.2.4]]) whose time axis is $U$, Schutz's "momentarily comoving reference frame". The energy–momentum tensor is read as in [[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^def-c4-2-2|Def. §C4.2.2]]: $T^{00}$ energy density, $T^{0i}$ energy flux and momentum density, $T^{ij}$ stress. From [[#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]] on, local conservation $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ is assumed ([[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime#^pr-c4-4-1|Principle §C4.4.1]], the minimally coupled form of $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, [[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^pr-c4-1-1|Principle §C4.1.1]]); conservation of particle number, $\nabla_\mu(nU^\mu) = 0$ ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c4-2-2|Theorem §C4.2.2]]), only where stated. Theorems §C4.3.2–§C4.3.4 and §C4.3.6 are exact; Theorem §C4.3.5 holds to first order in the small parameter $\varepsilon$ of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric|§C1.5]]. The numbers in the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $U\cdot U = -1$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$, with restored forms beside the results (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here $\rho$ is an energy density in $\mathrm{J/m^3}$ and $\rho/c^2$ a mass density, $p$ in $\mathrm{Pa} = \mathrm{J/m^3}$, $\Phi \to \Phi/c^2$, $U \to U/c$); $T^{00}$ is the energy density; Greek indices $0$–$3$, Latin $1$–$3$; hatted indices $\hat a$ are components in an orthonormal frame ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]). Level B and its links use $+{-}{-}{-}$; d'Inverno & Vickers too ([[#^ex-c4-3-4|Example §C4.3.4]]).

## The perfect-fluid model

What does "the pressure is the same in every direction" mean for a tensor? At an event, the observer who moves with the fluid has a rest space, the three directions orthogonal to $U$. Isotropy means that turning these three directions into one another changes nothing.

> [!model] Model §C4.3.1: Perfect Fluid
> *Structure.* On a spacetime $(M, g)$, a **perfect fluid** is described by three fields:
> - a **four-velocity** field $U$, future-directed and timelike with $g(U, U) = -1$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]], [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]): at each event the four-velocity of the fluid element there ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]);
> - the **energy density** $\rho$, a scalar field: all energy per unit volume in the rest frame of the element, rest energy included ([[#^cau-c4-3-1|Caution: ρ is the energy density, not the rest-mass density]]);
> - the **pressure** $p$, a scalar field.
>
> At each event $q$ a **rest frame** is an orthonormal basis $e_{\hat 0} = U_q$, $e_{\hat 1}$, $e_{\hat 2}$, $e_{\hat 3}$ of $T_qM$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]), the axes of a local inertial frame at $q$ moving with the element. The $e_{\hat i}$ span the **rest space** $U_q^\perp = \{V \in T_qM : g(U_q, V) = 0\}$. Two rest frames at $q$ differ by a rotation $R \in SO(3)$ of the rest space, $e_{\hat i} \mapsto e_{\hat j}R^j{}_i$: a Lorentz transformation of $T_qM$ that fixes $U_q$.
>
> *Defining property (isotropy in the rest frame).* The energy–momentum tensor $T_q$ ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^def-c4-2-2|Def. §C4.2.2]]), a symmetric $(2, 0)$ tensor ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c4-2-5|Theorem §C4.2.5]]), has the same components in every rest frame at $q$. Its energy density there is $T^{\hat 0\hat 0} = T_{\mu\nu}U^\mu U^\nu = \rho$, and the stress across a surface element of the rest space is normal to the element with magnitude $p$ ([[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]], part 1, shows that isotropy allows nothing else).
>
> *Physical reading.* In the rest frame, $T^{\hat 0\hat i} = 0$: energy flows only with the fluid, there is no heat conduction. $T^{\hat i\hat j} = p\,\delta^{ij}$: the force across any surface element is normal to it and the same for every orientation, so there is no shear stress and no viscosity, as for a fluid at rest in Newtonian hydrostatics ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-3|CM Def. §A9.1.3]]).
>
> - *Assumes:* local thermodynamic equilibrium in each element; no viscosity; no heat conduction; no other anisotropic stress (no elastic solid, no magnetic field); a continuum description (each element holds many particles; for a collisionless gas, an isotropic distribution of velocities in the rest frame, which also has pressure: Schutz §4.7).
> - *Special case:* $p = 0$ is dust ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^mod-c4-2-3|Model §C4.2.3]]); radiation, stiff matter and vacuum energy are other choices of $p(\rho)$ ([[#^def-c4-3-1|Def. §C4.3.1]]).
> - *Fails:* viscous shear flows, heat flow (imperfect fluids, not treated in these notes), anisotropic stress, and radiation streaming in one direction (a beam, [[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^ex-c4-2-2|Example §C4.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Schutz §4.7, p. 100 ("no viscosity and no heat conduction in the MCRF") · Hartle §22.1, Example 22.3, eq. (22.36), pp. 476, 479 · Couzens, GR1, §2.1, eq. (2.53), p. 23 ("isotropic … in the rest frame") · d'Inverno & Vickers §12.4, p. 207*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.2: The Energy–Momentum Tensor of a Perfect Fluid
> In [[#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]], at every event:
> 1. **Rest-frame components.** Isotropy forces $T^{\hat 0\hat i} = 0$ and $T^{\hat i\hat j} = p\,\delta^{ij}$; in a rest frame
>
> $$
> \bigl(T^{\hat a\hat b}\bigr) = \operatorname{diag}(\rho, p, p, p) .
> $$
>
> 2. **Covariant form.** As a tensor $T = (\rho + p)\,U \otimes U + p\,g^{-1}$, with $g^{-1}$ the inverse metric ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]); in every chart
>
> $$
> T^{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)\,U^\mu U^\nu + p\,g^{\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
> 3. **Lowered and mixed components; trace.**
>
> $$
> T_{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)\,U_\mu U_\nu + p\,g_{\mu\nu}, \qquad T^\mu{}_\nu = (\rho + p)\,U^\mu U_\nu + p\,\delta^\mu{}_\nu, \qquad T^\mu{}_\mu = -\rho + 3p .
> $$
>
> 4. **Uniqueness.** Among the symmetric tensors built from $U$ and $g$ without derivatives, $A\,U \otimes U + B\,g^{-1}$, only $A = \rho + p$, $B = p$ has the rest-frame components of part 1.
> 5. **Seen from a moving frame.** In a local inertial frame in which the fluid moves with velocity $\vec v$, $U^\mu = \gamma(1, \vec v)$:
>
> $$
> T^{00} = \gamma^2(\rho + v^2p), \qquad T^{0i} = \gamma^2(\rho + p)\,v^i, \qquad T^{ij} = \gamma^2(\rho + p)\,v^iv^j + p\,\delta^{ij} .
> $$
>
>    For $p = 0$ this is boosted dust ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^ex-c4-2-1|Example §C4.2.1]]). The pressure enters the momentum density $T^{0i}$, not only the stress ([[#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]]).
>
> Restored, with $\rho$ the energy density and $U\cdot U = -c^2$: $T^{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)\,U^\mu U^\nu/c^2 + p\,g^{\mu\nu}$; with the mass density $\rho_m = \rho/c^2$, $T^{\mu\nu} = (\rho_m + p/c^2)\,U^\mu U^\nu + p\,g^{\mu\nu}$.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c4-3-1|Model §C4.3.1]]; *applies:* exact, any metric, event by event; no equation of motion is used.
>
> *Source: Schutz §4.7, eqs. (4.36)–(4.38), p. 100; §7.1, eq. (7.7), p. 173 · Hartle §22.1, eqs. (22.36)–(22.37), p. 479; §22.2, eq. (22.39), p. 480 · Couzens, GR1, §2.1, eqs. (2.53)–(2.54), p. 23 · d'Inverno & Vickers §12.4, eqs. (12.20)–(12.22), p. 207 (signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) · components checked by script*

^thm-c4-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *Route 1: isotropy and covariance.* Fix an event $q$ and a rest frame there. A rotation $R \in SO(3)$ of the rest space gives the new basis $e'_{\hat 0} = U$, $e'_{\hat i} = e_{\hat j}R^j{}_i$; the components of $T$ in it are $T'^{\hat 0\hat i} = (R^{-1})^i{}_j\,T^{\hat 0\hat j}$ and $T'^{\hat i\hat j} = (R^{-1})^i{}_k(R^{-1})^j{}_l\,T^{\hat k\hat l}$ (the transformation law of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]] for this change of basis). Isotropy says these equal the old components for every $R$.
>
> **Step 1** (no energy flux). Let $w^i = T^{\hat 0\hat i}$. Isotropy gives $R^{-1}w = w$ for every rotation, hence $Rw = w$ for every rotation. Take the rotations by $\pi$ about $e_{\hat 1}$ and about $e_{\hat 3}$:
>
> $$
> \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1)\,w = w \;\Rightarrow\; w^2 = w^3 = 0, \qquad \operatorname{diag}(-1, -1, 1)\,w = w \;\Rightarrow\; w^1 = 0 .
> $$
>
> So $T^{\hat 0\hat i} = 0$.
>
> **Step 2** (isotropic stress). Let $S = (T^{\hat i\hat j})$, a real symmetric $3\times3$ matrix ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c4-2-5|Theorem §C4.2.5]]). Isotropy gives $R^{-1}S(R^{-1})^{\mathsf T} = S$, i.e. $RSR^{\mathsf T} = S$, for every rotation $R$ ($R^{-1} = R^{\mathsf T}$). By the real spectral theorem ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]) $S$ has a unit eigenvector $e$, $Se = \lambda e$. For every rotation $R$,
>
> $$
> S(Re) = RSR^{\mathsf T}Re = RSe = \lambda\,Re ,
> $$
>
> and every unit vector of $\mathbf R^3$ is $Re$ for some rotation $R$. So every vector is an eigenvector with the same eigenvalue $\lambda$, and $S = \lambda\,\mathbf 1$. The force across a surface element with unit normal $\hat n$ in the rest space is $S\hat n\,dA = \lambda\,\hat n\,dA$ (Hartle (22.21)): normal, the same for every $\hat n$. So $\lambda$ is the pressure, $\lambda = p$. With $T^{\hat 0\hat 0} = \rho$ (the model), part 1 is proved. (Both facts were also checked by script: the vectors fixed by the two half-turns and the symmetric matrices commuting with the three rotation generators.)
>
> **Step 3** (the candidate's rest-frame components). Let $\tilde T = (\rho + p)\,U \otimes U + p\,g^{-1}$. In the rest frame $U^{\hat a} = \delta^a_0$ and $g^{\hat a\hat b} = \eta^{ab}$ (orthonormal frame, [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]), so
>
> $$
> \tilde T^{\hat 0\hat 0} = (\rho + p)\cdot 1\cdot 1 + p\,\eta^{00} = \rho + p - p = \rho, \qquad \tilde T^{\hat 0\hat i} = 0 + p\,\eta^{0i} = 0, \qquad \tilde T^{\hat i\hat j} = 0 + p\,\eta^{ij} = p\,\delta^{ij} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (equal components, equal tensors). A tensor at $q$ is determined by its components in one basis ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]). By Steps 1–3, $T_q$ and $\tilde T_q$ have the same components in the rest frame, so $T_q = \tilde T_q$; $q$ was arbitrary. In a chart the components of $U \otimes U$ are $U^\mu U^\nu$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]]) and those of $g^{-1}$ are $g^{\mu\nu}$, which is part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (lowering). Apply $g_{\alpha\mu}g_{\beta\nu}$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]), with $g_{\alpha\mu}U^\mu = U_\alpha$ and $g_{\alpha\mu}g_{\beta\nu}g^{\mu\nu} = g_{\alpha\mu}\delta^\mu{}_\beta = g_{\alpha\beta}$:
>
> $$
> T_{\alpha\beta} = (\rho + p)\,U_\alpha U_\beta + p\,g_{\alpha\beta} .
> $$
>
> Lowering only the second index uses $g_{\beta\nu}g^{\mu\nu} = \delta^\mu{}_\beta$: $T^\mu{}_\beta = (\rho + p)\,U^\mu U_\beta + p\,\delta^\mu{}_\beta$. The trace, with $U^\mu U_\mu = -1$ and $\delta^\mu{}_\mu = 4$:
>
> $$
> T^\mu{}_\mu = (\rho + p)(-1) + 4p = -\rho + 3p .
> $$
>
> This is part 3.
>
> **Step 6** (uniqueness). For $A\,U \otimes U + B\,g^{-1}$ the computation of Step 3 gives the rest-frame components $A - B$ (for $\hat 0\hat 0$), $0$ (for $\hat 0\hat i$) and $B\,\delta^{ij}$. Matching $\operatorname{diag}(\rho, p, p, p)$: $B = p$ and $A - B = \rho$, so $A = \rho + p$. This is part 4.
>
> **Step 7** (a moving frame). With $g^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\nu}$, $U^0 = \gamma$, $U^i = \gamma v^i$ and $1/\gamma^2 = 1 - v^2$:
>
> $$
> T^{00} = (\rho + p)\gamma^2 - p = \gamma^2\bigl[\rho + p - p(1 - v^2)\bigr] = \gamma^2(\rho + v^2p), \qquad T^{0i} = (\rho + p)\gamma^2v^i + p\,\eta^{0i} = \gamma^2(\rho + p)\,v^i ,
> $$
>
> $$
> T^{ij} = (\rho + p)\gamma^2v^iv^j + p\,\eta^{ij} = \gamma^2(\rho + p)\,v^iv^j + p\,\delta^{ij} .
> $$
>
> Boosting these back by $-v$ returns $\operatorname{diag}(\rho, p, p, p)$ (checked by script). This is part 5.
>
> *Route 2: the Newtonian limit (d'Inverno & Vickers).* d'Inverno & Vickers reach the same tensor differently: they guess its form and fix the coefficients by demanding Newtonian fluid mechanics in the limit. In the signature of these notes:
>
> **Step 8** (ansatz). Dust plus pressure times a symmetric tensor built from the fluid: $T = \rho\,U \otimes U + p\,S$, $S = \lambda\,U \otimes U + \mu\,g^{-1}$, with constants $\lambda$, $\mu$ (their (12.20)–(12.21)). In the rest frame, as in Step 3,
>
> $$
> T^{\hat 0\hat 0} = \rho + (\lambda - \mu)\,p, \qquad T^{\hat 0\hat i} = 0, \qquad T^{\hat i\hat j} = \mu\,p\,\delta^{ij} .
> $$
>
> **Step 9** (the Newtonian limit fixes $\mu$). In an inertial frame of Minkowski spacetime let $v^2 = O(\varepsilon)$, $p = O(\varepsilon\rho)$, and let the flow change over a length $L$ and a time $L/v$. The components are
>
> $$
> T^{00} = (\rho + \lambda p)\gamma^2 - \mu p = \rho\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)], \qquad T^{0i} = (\rho + \lambda p)\gamma^2v^i = \rho v^i\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)], \qquad T^{ij} = \rho v^iv^j\,[1 + O(\varepsilon)] + \mu\,p\,\delta^{ij} .
> $$
>
> The $\nu = 0$ component of $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ has leading terms of order $\rho v/L$:
>
> $$
> \partial_tT^{00} + \partial_iT^{i0} = \partial_t\rho + \partial_i(\rho v^i) + O(\varepsilon\rho v/L) = 0 ,
> $$
>
> the continuity equation, whatever $\lambda$ and $\mu$. The $\nu = j$ components have leading terms of order $\varepsilon\rho/L$ (each of $\rho\,\partial_tv^j$, $\rho v^i\partial_iv^j$ and $\partial_jp$):
>
> $$
> \partial_tT^{0j} + \partial_iT^{ij} = \partial_t(\rho v^j) + \partial_i(\rho v^iv^j) + \mu\,\partial_jp + O(\varepsilon^2\rho/L) = \rho\bigl(\partial_t + v^i\partial_i\bigr)v^j + \mu\,\partial_jp + O(\varepsilon^2\rho/L) = 0 ,
> $$
>
> where the middle step subtracts $v^j$ times the continuity equation. Euler's equation of fluid mechanics without external forces (d'Inverno & Vickers (12.10)) has $+\partial_jp$, so $\mu = 1$. The constant $\lambda$ appears only in the $O(\varepsilon)$ corrections: the Newtonian limit does not determine it (checked by script with $\lambda$, $\mu$ kept general).
>
> **Step 10** (the rest-frame energy fixes $\lambda$). For $\rho$ to be the energy density in the rest frame, as the model requires, Step 8 needs $\lambda - \mu = 0$, so $\lambda = 1$, and $T = (\rho + p)\,U \otimes U + p\,g^{-1}$ again. (In d'Inverno's signature the same pair reads $\lambda = 1$, $\mu = -1$: [[#^ex-c4-3-4|Example §C4.3.4]]; on what their text claims for the limit, [[#^cau-c4-3-2|Caution: The fluid tensor in the sources]].)
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Isotropy in one frame plus covariance fixes the whole tensor: the two numbers $\rho$ and $p$ are all a perfect fluid has, and the tensor law carries them to every frame. The naive guess of the opening fails exactly because it imposed isotropy in the wrong frame.
> - The pressure enters $T^{\mu\nu}$ twice: in the stress ($p\,g^{\mu\nu}$) and next to the energy density in $(\rho + p)U^\mu U^\nu$. The second appearance is invisible in the Newtonian limit (Step 9). It is the inertia of pressure, [[#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]].

> [!caution] Caution: ρ is the energy density, not the rest-mass density
> In these notes, as in Hartle, Schutz and Couzens, $\rho$ is the total energy density in the rest frame: the rest energy of the particles plus their kinetic energy of random motion plus the energy of their interactions. For particles of mass $m$ with number density $n$ in the rest frame and internal energy $e$ per particle (Schutz's $\Pi$), $\rho = n(m + e)$. The rest-mass density $nm$ (Schutz's $\rho_0$, Hartle's $\mu$, d'Inverno's "proper density" $\rho_0$ for dust) is smaller by $ne$, which for a gas is of the order of $p$ ($e = \tfrac32k_BT$ and $p = nk_BT$ for a monatomic ideal gas, [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-3|TH Theorem §A2.2.3]]). The two densities agree for dust and differ by a relative amount of order $p/\rho$, so the distinction matters exactly where pressure matters. Hartle reserves $\rho$ for the rest-frame value and writes $\epsilon$ for the energy density in an arbitrary frame (his (22.36)); in a frame where the fluid moves, the energy density is $T^{00} = \gamma^2(\rho + v^2p)$, not $\rho$ ([[#^thm-c4-3-2|Theorem §C4.3.2]], part 5).
>
> *Source: Hartle §22.1, after eq. (22.36), p. 479 · Schutz §4.5, p. 94 (table: $\rho = n(m + \Pi)$, "rest mass, random kinetic, chemical, …"; $\rho_0 = mn$) · d'Inverno & Vickers §12.4, p. 207*
