Gravity is by far the weakest of the known interactions: between two protons the gravitational attraction is $8.1\times10^{-37}$ of the electric repulsion. Yet it shapes everything large, because it only attracts, while electric charges of both signs cancel in bulk matter (Couzens, GR1, §1). The theory of it that worked for two centuries is Newton's ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]]). Special relativity, completed at level B, ends with two demands on any law of physics: no influence may reach an event outside the future light cone of its cause ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), and laws are equations between tensors of the same type ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^pr-b2-2-9|Principle §B2.2.9]]). Electromagnetism passed both tests once its fields were assembled into a tensor ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-7|Theorem §B4.2.7]]). The question of this chapter is whether gravity can be treated the same way, and, when it cannot, what replaces it.

This section answers the first half. It recasts Newton's law of force as a field theory for a potential $\Phi$ ([[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]), the only form in which it can be compared with electrodynamics, and solves its field equation ([[#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]]). It then computes where the theory collides with level B: the potential responds instantaneously to its source ([[#^thm-c1-1-3|Theorem §C1.1.3]]), and the field equation changes its form under a Lorentz boost, also after the obvious repair of replacing the Laplacian by the d'Alembertian ([[#^thm-c1-1-4|Theorem §C1.1.4]]). The failure of the repair says what the new theory needs: a source that is a tensor with two indices, and hence a gravitational potential with two indices. Finally the section measures how large relativistic corrections are wherever gravity acts ([[#^def-c1-1-1|Def. §C1.1.1]], [[#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]]) and records the observation Newton's theory could not explain ([[#^law-c1-1-6|Law §C1.1.6]]).

**Setup.** Newtonian gravity in an inertial frame of Newtonian mechanics: an absolute time $t$ and, at each time, Euclidean space $\mathbf R^3$ with Cartesian coordinates. Matter is described by a given mass density $\rho(t, \vec x) \ge 0$, bounded and zero outside a bounded region at each time (a point mass is the limit of such densities); the bodies whose motion is studied are test bodies, whose own field is neglected. The theorems about the model are exact statements within it. Their comparison with special relativity uses the inertial frames of Minkowski spacetime (Relativity B, written here in the signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ of [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]). [[#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]] and the examples are order-of-magnitude statements; their numbers were checked by script. [[#^law-c1-1-6|Law §C1.1.6]] is an observation.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$ in every formula, with the restored form beside it (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here $\Phi \to \Phi/c^2$, $\rho \to G\rho/c^2$, $M \to GM/c^2$, $v \to v/c$, $t \to ct$); $\Phi < 0$ near masses and $\Phi \to 0$ at infinity; Greek indices $0$–$3$, Latin $1$–$3$, $x^0 = t$.

## Newtonian gravity as a field theory

Newton's law gives the force between two point masses at the same instant. A field theory splits this into two steps: the source produces a field everywhere, and a body responds to the field where it is. The split is what makes a comparison with the electromagnetic field possible.

> [!model] Model §C1.1.1: Newtonian Gravity as a Field Theory
> *Arena.* Newtonian spacetime: an absolute time $t \in \mathbf R$ and, at each time, Euclidean space $\mathbf R^3$ with the metric $\delta_{ij}$. Inertial frames are related by rotations, translations and Galilean boosts.
>
> *Field and source.* The **Newtonian potential** is a scalar field, one real number at each time and point, $\Phi : \mathbf R \times \mathbf R^3 \to \mathbf R$. Its source is the **mass density** $\rho(t, \vec x) \ge 0$, a given scalar field. They obey the **Poisson equation** with a boundary condition at infinity,
>
> $$
> \nabla^2\Phi(t, \vec x) = 4\pi\rho(t, \vec x), \qquad \Phi(t, \vec x) \to 0 \ \ (|\vec x| \to \infty) \qquad \bigl(\text{restored: } \nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The equation contains no time derivative: $t$ enters only as a label of the slice on which it is solved.
>
> *Response of a test body.* A test body of inertial mass $m_I$ ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]]) and gravitational mass $m_G$ ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^def-a8-1-1|CM Def. §A8.1.1]]) at the position $\vec x(t)$ obeys $m_I\ddot{\vec x} = -m_G\nabla\Phi(t, \vec x(t))$. Since $m_G = m_I$ for every body ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^law-a8-1-3|CM Law §A8.1.3]]),
>
> $$
> \ddot{\vec x}(t) = -\nabla\Phi\bigl(t, \vec x(t)\bigr) = \vec g\bigl(t, \vec x(t)\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec g = -\nabla\Phi$ the gravitational field ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^def-a8-1-2|CM Def. §A8.1.2]]); in SI units $\Phi$ is measured in $\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$, and $\Phi/c^2$ is the dimensionless number written $\Phi$ here.
>
> *Layer.* Newton's law of gravitation, a principle at level A ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]]), is a theorem of this model: the point-source case of [[#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]].
>
> - *Assumes:* test bodies (their own field and their effect on $\rho$ neglected); $\rho$ given, bounded, zero outside a bounded region; one mass in both laws.
> - *Analogy:* electrostatics has the same equation with the opposite sign, $\nabla^2V = -\rho_e/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-2|EM Theorem §B2.2.2]]), and the force $-q\nabla V$: mass plays the role of charge, but like masses attract (Hartle, Table 3.1).
> - *Fails:* for sources that change faster than light could cross them ([[#^thm-c1-1-3|Theorem §C1.1.3]]), in other inertial frames of special relativity ([[#^thm-c1-1-4|Theorem §C1.1.4]]), and wherever the compactness $M/r$ is not small ([[#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §2.2, eqs. (2.55)–(2.57), (2.61) · Hartle, Gravity, §3.3, eqs. (3.12)–(3.18), Table 3.1 (pp. 39–40); §3.4, eqs. (3.25)–(3.30) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §4.1, eqs. (4.4)–(4.6) (p. 51)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.1.2: Solution of the Poisson Equation
> In [[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]], let $\rho(t, \cdot)$ be bounded and zero outside the ball $|\vec x\,'| \le R_0$, at each time $t$.
> 1. The Poisson equation with $\Phi \to 0$ at infinity has exactly one solution,
>
> $$
> \Phi(t, \vec x) = -\int_{\mathbf R^3}\frac{\rho(t, \vec x\,')}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|}\,d^3x' \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \Phi = -G\int\frac{\rho(t, \vec x\,')}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|}\,d^3x'\Bigr) :
> $$
>
> the superposition of the potentials of the mass elements $\rho\,d^3x'$, with the **Green's function** $-1/|\vec x - \vec x\,'|$, which solves $\nabla^2\bigl(-1/|\vec x - \vec x\,'|\bigr) = 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec x - \vec x\,')$ as a distribution ([[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^def-c7-3-1|EM Def. §C7.3.1]]).
> 2. For a point mass $M$ at the origin, $\rho = M\delta^3(\vec x)$: $\Phi = -M/r$, and the force on a test mass $m$ is
>
> $$
> \vec F = -m\nabla\Phi = -\frac{mM}{r^2}\,\hat r \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \vec F = -\frac{GmM}{r^2}\,\hat r\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> Newton's law of gravitation ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]]).
> 3. If $\rho$ is spherically symmetric, with mass $M(r)$ inside radius $r$, then $\Phi$ depends only on $r$ and $\Phi'(r) = M(r)/r^2$; outside all the mass, $\Phi = -M/r$ (Newton's theorem; [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-7|CM Theorem §A8.1.7]]). The uniform sphere, inside and out, is [[#^ex-c1-1-1|Example §C1.1.1]].
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]; *applies:* on each time slice separately, exact within the model; for densities that are only piecewise continuous (a body with a sharp surface) the equation holds as an equation between distributions.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §2.2, eqs. (2.58)–(2.60), Exercise 2.7 · Hartle, Gravity, §3.3, eqs. (3.13)–(3.15), Example 3.1, eqs. (3.19)–(3.22) (pp. 38–40)*

^thm-c1-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Green's function). As distributions in $\vec x$, at fixed $\vec x\,'$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|EM Theorem §B1.2.2]]),
>
> $$
> \nabla^2\frac{1}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|} = -4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec x - \vec x\,') \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \nabla^2\Bigl(-\frac{1}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|}\Bigr) = 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec x - \vec x\,') .
> $$
>
> Tested against a smooth function $f$ of bounded support, this reads $\int d^3x\,\bigl(-1/|\vec x - \vec x\,'|\bigr)\nabla^2f(\vec x) = 4\pi f(\vec x\,')$.
>
> **Step 2** (the integral solves the equation). Let $f$ be a smooth function of bounded support. Moving $\nabla^2$ onto $f$ (the definition of the distributional Laplacian) and inserting the integral for $\Phi$,
>
> $$
> \int d^3x\,\Phi(t, \vec x)\,\nabla^2f(\vec x) = \int d^3x\,\nabla^2f(\vec x)\Bigl(-\int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(t, \vec x\,')}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|}\Bigr) = \int d^3x'\,\rho(t, \vec x\,')\Bigl[\int d^3x\,\Bigl(-\frac{1}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|}\Bigr)\nabla^2f(\vec x)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> The order of integration may be exchanged because the double integral converges absolutely: $\rho$ and $\nabla^2f$ are bounded with bounded supports, and $1/|\vec x - \vec x\,'|$ is integrable over bounded regions ($\int_{|\vec y| < a}d^3y/|\vec y| = 2\pi a^2$). By Step 1 the bracket is $4\pi f(\vec x\,')$, so
>
> $$
> \int d^3x\,\Phi\,\nabla^2f = \int d^3x'\,4\pi\rho(t, \vec x\,')\,f(\vec x\,') ,
> $$
>
> which is the statement $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi\rho$ as distributions. Where $\rho$ is smooth, $\Phi$ is smooth and the equation holds point by point.
>
> **Step 3** (boundary condition). For $|\vec x| > R_0$ and $|\vec x\,'| \le R_0$, the triangle inequality gives $|\vec x - \vec x\,'| \ge |\vec x| - |\vec x\,'| \ge |\vec x| - R_0 > 0$, so
>
> $$
> |\Phi(t, \vec x)| \le \int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(t, \vec x\,')}{|\vec x| - R_0} = \frac{M(t)}{|\vec x| - R_0} \xrightarrow{\ |\vec x| \to \infty\ } 0, \qquad M(t) = \int\rho(t, \vec x\,')\,d^3x' .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (uniqueness). If $\Phi_1$, $\Phi_2$ both solve the problem, $u = \Phi_1 - \Phi_2$ satisfies $\nabla^2u = 4\pi\rho - 4\pi\rho = 0$ on all of $\mathbf R^3$ and $u \to 0$ at infinity. A harmonic function has no local maximum or minimum inside a region ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-2|EM Theorem §B3.1.2]]), so on a closed ball of radius $R$ its largest and smallest values lie on the bounding sphere. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $R$ so large that $|u| < \varepsilon$ on that sphere; then $|u| < \varepsilon$ in the whole ball. Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary and $R$ can be taken larger still, $u = 0$ everywhere. This is the uniqueness theorem of electrostatics with the outer boundary at infinity ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|EM Theorem §B3.1.3]]). Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 5** (point mass). Substitute $\rho(t, \vec x\,') = M\delta^3(\vec x\,')$ into the integral of part 1:
>
> $$
> \Phi(\vec x) = -M\int d^3x'\,\frac{\delta^3(\vec x\,')}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|} = -\frac{M}{|\vec x|} = -\frac{M}{r} .
> $$
>
> With $\nabla(1/r) = -\hat r/r^2$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|EM Theorem §B1.2.2]]), $\nabla\Phi = -M\nabla(1/r) = M\hat r/r^2$, and
>
> $$
> \vec F = -m\nabla\Phi = -\frac{mM}{r^2}\,\hat r .
> $$
>
> In [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]] the force on $m_1$ points along $\hat r_{12}$, from $m_1$ toward $m_2$; with $m_1 = m$ at $\vec x$ and $m_2 = M$ at the origin, $\hat r_{12} = -\hat r$, and the two agree. Restoring $G$ (multiply $\Phi$ and $\vec F$ by $G$) gives the SI forms. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 6** (spherical symmetry). A rotation $R$ maps a solution $\Phi(\vec x)$ to $\Phi(R^{-1}\vec x)$, a solution for the density $\rho(R^{-1}\vec x) = \rho(\vec x)$; by uniqueness (Step 4) the two coincide, so $\Phi = \Phi(r)$. Integrate the Poisson equation over the ball $B_r$ of radius $r$ and apply the divergence theorem ([[§32 Flux Integrals and the Divergence Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-32-1|452 Thm. §32.1]]), with $\nabla\Phi = \Phi'(r)\hat r$ on the sphere of area $4\pi r^2$:
>
> $$
> \int_{B_r}\nabla^2\Phi\,d^3x = \oint_{\partial B_r}\nabla\Phi\cdot\hat r\,dA = 4\pi r^2\,\Phi'(r), \qquad \int_{B_r}4\pi\rho\,d^3x = 4\pi M(r) ,
> $$
>
> so $\Phi'(r) = M(r)/r^2$. Outside all the mass $M(r) = M$, and integrating inward from infinity, where $\Phi = 0$,
>
> $$
> \Phi(r) = -\int_r^\infty\Phi'(s)\,ds = -\int_r^\infty\frac{M}{s^2}\,ds = -\frac{M}{r} .
> $$
>
> Part 3 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The field equation and the inverse-square law carry the same content: the law is the field equation's response to a point source, and every other source is a superposition of point sources.
> - The integral in part 1 contains the density at the single time $t$ and no other. This is the instantaneous action of [[#^thm-c1-1-3|Theorem §C1.1.3]].
> - The delta function is used as a distribution (Steps 1–2): the potential of a point mass is singular, but its pairing with smooth test functions is finite.

## Where Newton's theory collides with special relativity

The potential of [[#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]] was found slice by slice. Nothing in it says how a change in the source reaches a distant point, and nothing says how the equation looks to an observer who moves. Both questions have definite answers within the model, and both answers conflict with level B.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.1.3: Newtonian Gravity Acts Instantaneously
> In [[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]:
> 1. *(One slice.)* $\Phi(t, \vec x)$ is determined by $\rho(t, \cdot)$ alone. Two mass distributions that agree at the time $t$ produce the same potential, and the same force on every test body, everywhere at that time, whatever their past.
> 2. *(⚑ Unbounded signal speed.)* Let a mass $M$, at rest at the origin until $t_0$, move along a path $\vec d(t)$ inside a bounded region $K$ from $t_0$ on. Then at every point $\vec x$ outside $K$, however far away, the potential changes from $t_0$ on: $\Phi(t, \vec x) = -M/|\vec x - \vec d(t)|$ for $t \ge t_0$, and at distance $r = |\vec x| \gg |\vec d|$
>
> $$
> \Delta\Phi(t, \vec x) = -\frac{M}{|\vec x - \vec d(t)|} + \frac{M}{r} = -\frac{M\,\vec d(t)\cdot\hat x}{r^2} + O\Bigl(\frac{M d^2}{r^3}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> nonzero wherever $\vec d\cdot\hat x \neq 0$. A test body there changes its acceleration at the same instant as the source moves: the influence crosses any distance in zero time.
> 3. *(Conflict with special relativity.)* The cause, event $A = (t_0, \vec 0)$, and its effect at distance $r$, event $B = (t_0, r\hat x)$, are spacelike separated ($\Delta s^2 = r^2 > 0$ in the signature of [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]). In the inertial frame moving with speed $v$ along $\hat x$,
>
> $$
> t'_B - t'_A = -\gamma v r < 0 \qquad \bigl(\text{restored: } -\gamma vr/c^2\bigr) :
> $$
>
> the effect happens before its cause. Special relativity admits influences only into the causal future, a statement that holds in every inertial frame ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]; [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^rem-b1-3-2|§B1.3, Remark: Causality as a statement about cones]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where spacelike means $\Delta s^2 < 0$). The Newtonian potential therefore cannot be a field of special relativity.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]], compared with the inertial frames of special relativity; *applies:* exact within the model; the source in part 2 is any bounded motion of a fixed mass.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §1 (the force "depends on the location of the other mass at the same time"); §2.3 ("effects are instantaneous in Newtonian gravity") · Hartle, Gravity, ch. 6 introduction, eq. (6.1) (p. 107)*

^thm-c1-1-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (one slice). By [[#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]], part 1, the unique solution is $\Phi(t, \vec x) = -\int\rho(t, \vec x\,')\,d^3x'/|\vec x - \vec x\,'|$; the integrand contains $\rho$ at the time $t$ only. Equal densities at $t$ give equal integrals, hence equal $\Phi(t, \cdot)$ and equal $\vec g = -\nabla\Phi$.
>
> **Step 2** (moving source). The density of the displaced mass is $\rho(t, \vec x\,') = M\delta^3(\vec x\,' - \vec d(t))$. The integral gives
>
> $$
> \Phi(t, \vec x) = -M\int d^3x'\,\frac{\delta^3(\vec x\,' - \vec d(t))}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|} = -\frac{M}{|\vec x - \vec d(t)|} \qquad (t \ge t_0) ,
> $$
>
> and $-M/r$ for $t < t_0$. Expand for $d = |\vec d| \ll r$, with $\vec d\cdot\vec x = rd\cos\theta$:
>
> $$
> |\vec x - \vec d|^2 = r^2 - 2rd\cos\theta + d^2, \qquad \frac{1}{|\vec x - \vec d|} = \frac1r\Bigl(1 - \frac{2d\cos\theta}{r} + \frac{d^2}{r^2}\Bigr)^{-1/2} = \frac1r + \frac{d\cos\theta}{r^2} + O\Bigl(\frac{d^2}{r^3}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> using $(1 + w)^{-1/2} = 1 - \frac12w + O(w^2)$ with $w = -2d\cos\theta/r + d^2/r^2$. Hence
>
> $$
> \Delta\Phi = -M\Bigl(\frac1r + \frac{d\cos\theta}{r^2}\Bigr) + \frac{M}{r} + O\Bigl(\frac{Md^2}{r^3}\Bigr) = -\frac{M\,\vec d\cdot\hat x}{r^2} + O\Bigl(\frac{Md^2}{r^3}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> The expression holds at every $t > t_0$ and every $\vec x$, with no delay depending on $r$.
>
> **Step 3** (the two events in a moving frame). Take the $x$-axis along $\hat x$. The Lorentz transformation to the frame moving with speed $v$ along $+x$ is $t' = \gamma(t - vx)$, $x' = \gamma(x - vt)$ ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]], with $c = 1$). For $A = (t_0, 0)$ and $B = (t_0, r)$:
>
> $$
> t'_A = \gamma(t_0 - v\cdot 0) = \gamma t_0, \qquad t'_B = \gamma(t_0 - vr), \qquad t'_B - t'_A = -\gamma vr .
> $$
>
> For every $v > 0$ this is negative. The interval is $\Delta s^2 = -(t_B - t_A)^2 + r^2 = r^2 > 0$, spacelike in the signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; the time order of spacelike separated events is not invariant ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]), and Step 3 exhibits the frames that reverse it.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The instantaneity is not a feature of a particular solution: it is the absence of $\partial_t$ from the field equation. Any equation that determines $\Phi$ on one slice from $\rho$ on the same slice has it.
> - Unlike the scalar potential of electrostatics in the Coulomb gauge, which is also instantaneous but cannot be measured on its own ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^rem-b11-1-2|EM §B11.1★, Remark: The instantaneous potential of the Coulomb gauge]]), the Newtonian $\Phi$ fixes the force on a test body directly, so here the instantaneous change is observable.

*Uses:* [[#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]], [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]], [[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]], [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]], [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]

The second failure concerns the form of the law, not the speed of its effects. Level B's test for a law is that it be an equation between tensors of the same type; then it holds in every inertial frame once it holds in one ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-8|Theorem §B2.2.8]]). Apply the test to the Poisson equation, regarded as an equation for a scalar field on Minkowski spacetime, $\Phi'(x') = \Phi(x)$ under $x' = \Lambda x$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.1.4: The Poisson Equation Is Not Lorentz Covariant
> Let $S'$ move with speed $v$ along the $x$-axis of $S$, $t' = \gamma(t - vx)$, $x' = \gamma(x - vt)$, $y' = y$, $z' = z$, $\gamma = (1 - v^2)^{-1/2}$.
> 1. *(The operator.)* The d'Alembertian $\Box = -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2$ is the same in both frames, $\Box = \Box'$ ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where $\Box_B = \partial_t^2 - \nabla^2 = -\Box$, [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^cau-b2-2-3|Caution: The sign of the box]]). The Laplacian is not:
>
> $$
> \nabla^2 = \Box + \partial_t^2 = \nabla'^2 - \partial_{t'}^2 + \gamma^2\bigl(\partial_{t'} - v\,\partial_{x'}\bigr)^2 = \nabla'^2 + \gamma^2v^2\bigl(\partial_{t'}^2 + \partial_{x'}^2\bigr) - 2\gamma^2v\,\partial_{t'}\partial_{x'} .
> $$
>
> A Poisson equation that holds in $S$ has a different form in $S'$; the theory singles out one inertial frame.
> 2. *(A computed disagreement.)* The potential $\Phi = -M/r$ of a point mass at rest in $S$ is, in $S'$, where the mass moves with velocity $-v$ along $x'$,
>
> $$
> \Phi'(t', \vec x\,') = -\frac{M}{s}, \qquad s^2 = \gamma^2(x' + vt')^2 + y'^2 + z'^2 ,
> $$
>
> and away from the mass
>
> $$
> \nabla'^2\Phi' = \partial_{t'}^2\Phi' = \frac{M\gamma^2v^2\bigl[y'^2 + z'^2 - 2\gamma^2(x' + vt')^2\bigr]}{s^5} \neq 0 .
> $$
>
> The Poisson equation in $S'$ would require $\nabla'^2\Phi' = 0$ wherever $\rho' = 0$. The transformed field instead obeys the wave equation $\Box'\Phi' = 0$.
> 3. *(The naive repair fails.)* Replacing $\nabla^2$ by the invariant $\Box$, i.e. $\Box\Phi = 4\pi\rho$, does not give a covariant law, because the mass-energy density is not a scalar. For dust of rest density $\rho_0$ moving with speed $v$, an observer measures
>
> $$
> \rho = \gamma^2\rho_0 \qquad \bigl(\text{restored: energy density } \gamma^2\rho_0c^2\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> one factor $\gamma$ from the energy of each particle and one from the contraction of the volume. It is the $00$-component of the rank-2 tensor $T^{\mu\nu} = \rho_0u^\mu u^\nu$, the energy–momentum tensor of dust ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]]). An equation $\Box\Phi = 4\pi T^{00}$ sets a scalar equal to one component of a rank-2 tensor and holds in at most one frame ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-8|Theorem §B2.2.8]]).
> 4. *(What a covariant equation needs.)* Both sides must be tensors of the same type. For an equation linear in the source there are two possibilities: the scalar $T^\mu{}_\mu$ ($= -\rho_0$ for dust) as the source of a scalar potential, or the whole $T^{\mu\nu}$ as the source of a symmetric potential with two indices, $h_{\mu\nu}$, the type of a metric. Charge density needs one index less: it is the time component of the four-current $J^\mu = \rho_{e,0}u^\mu$, with one factor $\gamma$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]), and Maxwell's equations take a four-vector potential ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-7|Theorem §B4.2.7]]). Which possibility gravity takes, and why, is the subject of the field equations ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^pr-c5-1-4|Principle §C5.1.4]]); the geometric reading of $h_{\mu\nu}$ begins in [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]].
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]], with $\Phi$ read as a scalar field on Minkowski spacetime and dust as the source in parts 3–4; *applies:* exact.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, ch. 6 introduction (the Newtonian law "could be true in only one frame", p. 107); §6.6 (Newtonian gravity remains inconsistent with special relativity even in geometric form, p. 131) · Couzens, GR1, §2.2 ("not Lorentz invariant"); §2.1, eqs. (2.50)–(2.52) (dust: $\rho$ is the $00$-component of $\rho U^\mu U^\nu$) · Schutz, §4.4, eq. (4.13) (energy density of moving dust, two factors $\gamma$; "impossible … to represent the energy density as some component of a vector", pp. 91–92)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (chain rule). From $t' = \gamma(t - vx)$, $x' = \gamma(x - vt)$:
>
> $$
> \partial_t = \frac{\partial t'}{\partial t}\partial_{t'} + \frac{\partial x'}{\partial t}\partial_{x'} = \gamma\,\partial_{t'} - \gamma v\,\partial_{x'}, \qquad \partial_x = \frac{\partial t'}{\partial x}\partial_{t'} + \frac{\partial x'}{\partial x}\partial_{x'} = -\gamma v\,\partial_{t'} + \gamma\,\partial_{x'} ,
> $$
>
> and $\partial_y = \partial_{y'}$, $\partial_z = \partial_{z'}$.
>
> **Step 2** (squares). The coefficients are constants, so
>
> $$
> \partial_t^2 = \gamma^2\bigl(\partial_{t'}^2 - 2v\,\partial_{t'}\partial_{x'} + v^2\partial_{x'}^2\bigr), \qquad \partial_x^2 = \gamma^2\bigl(\partial_{x'}^2 - 2v\,\partial_{t'}\partial_{x'} + v^2\partial_{t'}^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** ($\Box$ is invariant). Subtract:
>
> $$
> -\partial_t^2 + \partial_x^2 = \gamma^2\bigl[(1 - v^2)\partial_{x'}^2 - (1 - v^2)\partial_{t'}^2\bigr] + \gamma^2(2v - 2v)\,\partial_{t'}\partial_{x'} = -\partial_{t'}^2 + \partial_{x'}^2 ,
> $$
>
> since $\gamma^2(1 - v^2) = 1$. Adding $\partial_y^2 + \partial_z^2 = \partial_{y'}^2 + \partial_{z'}^2$ gives $\Box = \Box'$.
>
> **Step 4** ($\nabla^2$ is not). Write $\nabla^2 = \Box + \partial_t^2$, use Step 3 for $\Box$ and Step 1 for $\partial_t$, and $\Box' = -\partial_{t'}^2 + \nabla'^2$:
>
> $$
> \nabla^2 = \nabla'^2 - \partial_{t'}^2 + \gamma^2\bigl(\partial_{t'} - v\,\partial_{x'}\bigr)^2 = \nabla'^2 + (\gamma^2 - 1)\partial_{t'}^2 - 2\gamma^2v\,\partial_{t'}\partial_{x'} + \gamma^2v^2\partial_{x'}^2 ,
> $$
>
> and $\gamma^2 - 1 = \gamma^2v^2$ gives the form in part 1. The extra terms do not vanish for $v \neq 0$: applied to the function $x'^2$ they give $2\gamma^2v^2$. (The identity was checked symbolically.)
>
> **Step 5** (the point mass seen from $S'$). The inverse boost is $x = \gamma(x' + vt')$, $y = y'$, $z = z'$, so the scalar field $\Phi'(t', \vec x\,') = \Phi(t, \vec x) = -M/\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$ is $-M/s$ with $s^2 = \gamma^2X^2 + y'^2 + z'^2$, $X \equiv x' + vt'$. Its derivatives, with $\partial_{x'}s = \gamma^2X/s$, $\partial_{y'}s = y'/s$, $\partial_{t'}s = \gamma^2vX/s$:
>
> $$
> \partial_{x'}^2\frac1s = -\frac{\gamma^2}{s^3} + \frac{3\gamma^4X^2}{s^5}, \qquad \partial_{y'}^2\frac1s = -\frac{1}{s^3} + \frac{3y'^2}{s^5}, \qquad \partial_{z'}^2\frac1s = -\frac{1}{s^3} + \frac{3z'^2}{s^5} .
> $$
>
> Their sum, over the common denominator $s^5$ and with $s^2 = \gamma^2X^2 + y'^2 + z'^2$:
>
> $$
> \nabla'^2\frac1s = \frac{-(\gamma^2 + 2)\bigl(\gamma^2X^2 + y'^2 + z'^2\bigr) + 3\gamma^4X^2 + 3y'^2 + 3z'^2}{s^5} = \frac{(\gamma^2 - 1)\bigl[2\gamma^2X^2 - y'^2 - z'^2\bigr]}{s^5} .
> $$
>
> Multiplying by $-M$ and using $\gamma^2 - 1 = \gamma^2v^2$ gives the formula of part 2. For the time derivative,
>
> $$
> \partial_{t'}^2\frac1s = v^2\Bigl(-\frac{\gamma^2}{s^3} + \frac{3\gamma^4X^2}{s^5}\Bigr) = \frac{\gamma^2v^2\bigl(3\gamma^2X^2 - s^2\bigr)}{s^5} = \frac{\gamma^2v^2\bigl[2\gamma^2X^2 - y'^2 - z'^2\bigr]}{s^5} ,
> $$
>
> the same as $\nabla'^2(1/s)$; hence $\nabla'^2\Phi' = \partial_{t'}^2\Phi'$ and $\Box'\Phi' = 0$, as Step 3 requires of a field with $\Box\Phi = 0$ off the source in $S$. At $X = 0$, $y' = 1$, $z' = 0$, $M = 1$, $v = 3/5$ ($\gamma = 5/4$): $\nabla'^2\Phi' = \gamma^2v^2 = 9/16$.
>
> **Step 6** (moving dust). In its rest frame the dust has $n_0$ particles of mass $m$ per unit volume, $\rho_0 = mn_0$. In a frame where it moves with speed $v$, a box that holds $N$ particles has rest volume $V_0 = N/n_0$, contracted along the motion to $V = V_0/\gamma$ ([[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]), so $n = N/V = \gamma n_0$; each particle carries energy $E = \gamma m$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^def-b2-1-3|Def. §B2.1.3]], $c = 1$). The energy per unit volume is
>
> $$
> \rho = E\,n = (\gamma m)(\gamma n_0) = \gamma^2\rho_0 .
> $$
>
> With the four-velocity $u^\mu = \gamma(1, v, 0, 0)$ (upper components are the same in both signatures; [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]), $T^{00} = \rho_0u^0u^0 = \rho_0\gamma^2$: the same number.
>
> **Step 7** (the repaired equation in two frames). Suppose $\Box\Phi = 4\pi T^{00}$ holds in the rest frame $S$ of a dust cloud, where $T^{00} = \rho_0$: $\Box\Phi = 4\pi\rho_0$. In $S'$, $\Box'\Phi'$ at the image point equals $\Box\Phi$ (Step 3, $\Phi$ a scalar), so $\Box'\Phi' = 4\pi\rho_0$; the law in $S'$ would require $\Box'\Phi' = 4\pi T'^{00} = 4\pi\gamma^2\rho_0$ (Step 6). The two agree only for $\gamma = 1$.
>
> **Step 8** (the scalar that is available). Contract with the metric: $T^\mu{}_\mu = \eta_{\mu\nu}T^{\mu\nu} = \rho_0\,\eta_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = \rho_0\,(u\cdot u) = -\rho_0$, since $u\cdot u = -1$ in the signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]). For charge: the charge in a box is the same in every frame ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^law-b4-2-5|Law §B4.2.5]]) and only the volume contracts, so $\rho_e = \gamma\rho_{e,0}$, the time component of $\rho_{e,0}u^\mu$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Newtonian gravity fails the test of level B twice: in its operator ($\nabla^2$, Steps 1–5) and in its source ($\rho$, Steps 6–7). Repairing the operator alone does not help.
> - The count of factors $\gamma$ in the source fixes the number of indices of the potential: one for charge (a four-vector potential), two for mass-energy (a potential with two indices, $h_{\mu\nu}$, of the same type as the metric $\eta_{\mu\nu}$).
> - The static field of Step 5, seen from a moving frame, already obeys the covariant wave equation. A covariant theory has the Newtonian potential as its static limit; it is the slice-by-slice Poisson equation that has to go.

## How large are relativistic corrections?

Newton's theory is wrong in principle, yet it navigates every spacecraft ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton#^law-a8-5-1|CM Law §A8.5.1]]). The two statements are reconciled by a size estimate: in a system held together by gravity, the speeds and the potential are tied to one dimensionless number, and that number is small almost everywhere.

> [!definition] Definition §C1.1.1: Compactness (Relativistic Parameter)
> For a mass $M$ (a body, or the central mass of an orbit) and a distance $r$ from its centre, the **compactness** at $r$ is the dimensionless number
>
> $$
> \epsilon \equiv \frac{M}{r} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \epsilon = \frac{GM}{rc^2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> In geometric units a mass is a length ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-1|Def. §C1.2.1]]: $M_\odot = 1476.7\ \mathrm m$, $M_\oplus = 4.435\ \mathrm{mm}$), so $\epsilon$ is a ratio of two lengths. Outside a spherical body $\epsilon = |\Phi(r)|$ ([[#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]], part 3), and $\epsilon = r_s/(2r)$ with the Schwarzschild radius $r_s = 2M$ ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton#^def-a8-5-1|CM Def. §A8.5.1]], its Newtonian home; in general relativity [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^def-c6-2-1|Def. §C6.2.1]]). The compactness *of a body* of radius $R$ is $\epsilon$ at its surface, $M/R$. It also caps the gravitational-wave power a self-gravitating source can radiate, at order $(M/R)^5$ times $c^5/G$ ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^ex-c8-5-3|Example §C8.5.3]]). Couzens, GR2, §3.2, eq. (3.16), uses the name for $C = 2M/R = 2\epsilon$ (Buchdahl's bound, [[§C9.2 The Uniform-Density Star and Buchdahl's Limit#^thm-c9-2-4|Theorem §C9.2.4]]); these notes write $\epsilon$ for $M/r$ and $C$ for $2M/R$ ([[§C9.2 The Uniform-Density Star and Buchdahl's Limit#^def-c9-2-1|Def. §C9.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §2.3, eqs. (2.79)–(2.80) ("the dimensionless parameter which governs corrections to Newtonian gravity") · Couzens, GR2, §3.2, eq. (3.16)*

^def-c1-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.1.5: Size of Relativistic Corrections
> Let a test body move on a bound orbit about a spherical mass $M$ ([[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]).
> 1. On a circular orbit of radius $r$ the speed satisfies
>
> $$
> v^2 = \frac{M}{r} = \epsilon \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \frac{v^2}{c^2} = \frac{GM}{rc^2}\Bigr)
> $$
>
> ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-5|CM Theorem §A8.2.5]]).
> 2. On any bound orbit the long-time averages satisfy $\langle v^2\rangle = \langle M/r\rangle$, the virial theorem for the inverse-square force ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-6|CM Theorem §B3.1.6]]).
> 3. Hence the two small numbers in which special relativity and gravity would correct Newton's theory are of the same order: $v^2$, by which $\gamma = 1 + \frac12v^2 + \dots$ departs from $1$, and $|\Phi| = \epsilon$, the potential energy per unit rest energy. Newtonian gravity is the leading term of an expansion in $\epsilon$, and the first corrections are of relative order $\epsilon$; they are of order one where $r$ approaches $r_s = 2M$, $\epsilon \to \frac12$.
> 4. Light moves with $v = 1$ in every field, so for light there is no slow-motion expansion: its first corrections are of relative order $\epsilon$ alone (the deflection of light, [[§C6.5 The Bending of Light#^thm-c6-5-2|Theorem §C6.5.2]]).
>
> For reference (computations in [[#^ex-c1-1-3|Example §C1.1.3]]):
>
> | Where | $\epsilon$ |
> |---|---|
> | GPS orbit, $26\,562\ \mathrm{km}$ from Earth's centre | $1.67\times10^{-10}$ |
> | Earth's surface | $6.95\times10^{-10}$ |
> | Earth's orbit, $1\ \mathrm{AU}$ | $9.87\times10^{-9}$ |
> | Mercury's orbit, $0.387\ \mathrm{AU}$ | $2.55\times10^{-8}$ |
> | Sun's surface | $2.12\times10^{-6}$ |
> | white dwarf, $0.6\,M_\odot$, $7000\ \mathrm{km}$ | $1.27\times10^{-4}$ |
> | neutron star, $1.4\,M_\odot$, $12\ \mathrm{km}$ | $0.17$ |
> | black-hole horizon, $r = 2M$ | $0.5$ |
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]] with a test body on a bound orbit about a spherical mass; *applies:* an order-of-magnitude statement; the number that multiplies $\epsilon$ in each effect is a result of general relativity ([[· C6 The Schwarzschild Solution and the Classical Tests|C6]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §2.3, eqs. (2.78)–(2.80) (pp. 29–30) · Hartle, Gravity, §3.3, Example 3.2, eq. (3.23)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (circular orbit). The acceleration on a circle of radius $r$ at speed $v$ is $v^2/r$ toward the centre ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-5|CM Theorem §A8.2.5]]); the gravitational acceleration there is $|\nabla\Phi| = M/r^2$ ([[#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]], part 3). Equating,
>
> $$
> \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{M}{r^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v^2 = \frac{M}{r} = \epsilon .
> $$
>
> With $G$ and $c$: $v^2/r = GM/r^2$, so $v^2 = GM/r$ and $v^2/c^2 = GM/(rc^2)$.
>
> **Step 2** (any bound orbit). The potential energy $U = -mM/r$ is homogeneous of degree $k = -1$ in the position, so the virial theorem ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-6|CM Theorem §B3.1.6]]) gives $2\langle T\rangle = -\langle U\rangle$, i.e.
>
> $$
> 2\Bigl\langle\tfrac12mv^2\Bigr\rangle = \Bigl\langle\frac{mM}{r}\Bigr\rangle \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle v^2\rangle = \Bigl\langle\frac{M}{r}\Bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the two expansion parameters). The kinetic energy of special relativity expands as
>
> $$
> (\gamma - 1)m = m\Bigl[(1 - v^2)^{-1/2} - 1\Bigr] = m\Bigl(\tfrac12v^2 + \tfrac38v^4 + \dots\Bigr) = \tfrac12mv^2\Bigl(1 + \tfrac34v^2 + \dots\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> so the first special-relativistic correction to Newtonian mechanics has relative size $\frac34v^2 \sim \epsilon$ by Steps 1–2. The potential energy of the body, $m\Phi$, compared with its rest energy $m$ is $|\Phi| = M/r = \epsilon$ at the orbit (Theorem §C1.1.2, part 3). Both are of order $\epsilon$.
>
> **Step 4** (where corrections are of order one). $\epsilon = M/r = r_s/(2r)$, so $\epsilon = \frac12$ at $r = r_s$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - For bodies bound by gravity, slow motion and weak field are one condition, not two: either small number controls the other.
> - Light has $v = 1$ whatever the field, so the expansion in $v^2$ is not available for it: part 4.

> [!law] Law §C1.1.6: Mercury's Anomalous Perihelion Advance
> The perihelion of Mercury advances. Observed from the Earth the advance is $5599.74'' \pm 0.41''$ per century. Of this, $5025.64'' \pm 0.50''$ is the precession of the equinoxes, a property of the Earth-based reference frame, and about $531''$ is the Newtonian effect of the other planets (GR1: $532''$). The remainder,
>
> $$
> \delta\varphi_{\text{anom}} = 42.98'' \pm 0.04''\ \text{per century} ,
> $$
>
> is not explained by Newtonian gravity. Per orbit ($415.2$ orbits per century) it is $0.1035'' = 5.02\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{rad}$, about $20\,\epsilon$ with $\epsilon = 2.55\times10^{-8}$ for Mercury's orbit ([[#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]]): the size of a first relativistic correction.
>
> For comparison, Newton's theory gives a single planet about a spherical Sun a closed ellipse whose perihelion does not move (Couzens, GR1, §2.2.2; [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-4|CM Theorem §B2.3.4]]), and a small extra term in the potential makes the perihelion advance ([[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-5|CM Theorem §B2.3.5]]). General relativity accounts for it ([[§C6.4 Perihelion Precession#^law-c6-4-3|Law §C6.4.3]]).

## What the replacement has to do

> [!remark] Remark: Universality of free fall points to geometry
> The equation of motion of [[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]], $\ddot{\vec x} = -\nabla\Phi$, contains no property of the falling body: its mass and its composition have cancelled, because $m_G = m_I$ ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^law-a8-1-3|CM Law §A8.1.3]]). A charge in an electric field obeys $\ddot{\vec x} = (q/m)\vec E$ instead, and bodies with different $q/m$ part company. In a gravitational field every test body released at the same event with the same velocity follows the same worldline. Couzens draws the conclusion that guides everything that follows: a path shared by all bodies is more naturally a property of spacetime itself than of a force acting on each body, and gravity is to be the curvature of spacetime, with free bodies moving on its straightest lines. He quotes Wheeler's summary: "Mass tells space how to curve, while curved space tells matter how to move."
>
> Newton's theory states the equality $m_G = m_I$ without explaining it ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton#^rem-a8-5-1|CM §A8.5, Remark: Two unexplained assumptions]]). The theorem that free fall is universal is [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-1|Theorem §C1.3.1]]; Einstein's step is to make the universality the founding principle ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-3|Principle §C1.3.3]]). The straightest lines of a curved spacetime, in their first form, are the worldlines of extremal proper time ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §1 (p. 7); §2.2, after eq. (2.61) (p. 24)*

^rem-c1-1-1

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Mach's principle
> Newton's laws hold only in inertial frames, and Newtonian gravity, like the rest of Newtonian mechanics, takes these frames as given. What singles them out? Newton answered with absolute space and the bucket experiment: water in a bucket that spins on a twisted rope stays flat while only the bucket turns, curves once friction has set the water rotating, and stays curved after the bucket has stopped; the curvature follows the water's rotation relative to absolute space, not relative to the bucket. Mach (1893, after Berkeley) held instead that only relative motion has meaning and that the inertial frames are fixed by the distant matter of the universe, the "fixed stars". In support, a pendulum swinging at the North Pole turns relative to the Earth once in the time the Earth turns once relative to the fixed stars, within the accuracy of measurement; for Newton this is a coincidence. d'Inverno & Vickers stress that Mach's principle has no agreed quantitative form (they list three of the many versions, from "the matter distribution determines the geometry" to "a body in an otherwise empty universe has no inertia"), that it mattered to Einstein, and that general relativity incorporates it only in part. The question it asks returns in these notes in local form: in general relativity the freely falling frames take over the role of the inertial frames ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §9.1–9.2, Figs. 9.2–9.4 (pp. 153–159)*

^rem-c1-1-2

The three failures of this section set the tasks. The theory that replaces Newton's must let influences travel no faster than light, must be built from tensors, with the energy–momentum tensor as its source, and must reduce to [[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]] where $\epsilon \ll 1$; the universality of free fall suggests that it be a theory of geometry. Before building it, [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B|§C1.2]] fixes the signature and the units in which all of this is written, and [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle|§C1.3]] asks what the equality of the two masses, a coincidence for Newton, says about free fall when it is taken as a principle.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C1.1.1: Field of a Uniform Sphere from the Poisson Equation
> A sphere of radius $R$ and uniform density $\rho_0$, total mass $M = \frac43\pi\rho_0R^3$. By [[#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]], part 3, $\Phi = \Phi(r)$, and the Laplacian of a function of $r$ is $\nabla^2\Phi = r^{-2}(r^2\Phi')'$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|EM Theorem §B1.1.5]]).
>
> *Inside* ($r < R$): $(r^2\Phi')' = 4\pi\rho_0r^2$. Integrate once:
>
> $$
> r^2\Phi' = \tfrac43\pi\rho_0r^3 + C_1 = \frac{M}{R^3}\,r^3 + C_1, \qquad \Phi' = \frac{M}{R^3}\,r + \frac{C_1}{r^2} .
> $$
>
> The field $-\Phi'\hat r$ must stay finite at the centre, so $C_1 = 0$. Integrate again: $\Phi = \frac{M}{2R^3}\,r^2 + C_2$.
>
> *Outside* ($r > R$): $(r^2\Phi')' = 0$, so $r^2\Phi' = C_3$, $\Phi = -C_3/r + C_4$, and $\Phi \to 0$ at infinity gives $C_4 = 0$.
>
> *Matching at $r = R$.* Integrate the Poisson equation, multiplied by $r^2$, across a thin shell $R - \delta < r < R + \delta$:
>
> $$
> \bigl[r^2\Phi'\bigr]_{R - \delta}^{R + \delta} = 4\pi\int_{R - \delta}^{R + \delta}\rho\,r^2\,dr \le 4\pi\rho_0(R + \delta)^2\,2\delta \xrightarrow{\ \delta \to 0\ } 0 ,
> $$
>
> so $\Phi'$ is continuous (no surface layer of mass), and $\Phi$, the integral of a bounded $\Phi'$, is continuous too. Continuity of $\Phi'$: $M/R^2 = C_3/R^2$, so $C_3 = M$. Continuity of $\Phi$: $\frac{M}{2R} + C_2 = -\frac{M}{R}$, so $C_2 = -\frac{3M}{2R}$. Altogether
>
> $$
> \Phi(r) = \begin{cases} -\dfrac{M\,(3R^2 - r^2)}{2R^3} , & r \le R , \\[2mm] -\dfrac{M}{r} , & r \ge R , \end{cases} \qquad \bigl(\text{restored: } M \to GM\ \text{for } \Phi \text{ in } \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Checks.* Inside, $|\vec g| = \Phi' = Mr/R^3 = \frac43\pi\rho_0r$, growing linearly from zero at the centre: [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-8|CM Theorem §A8.1.8]] (with $G$). Outside, $|\vec g| = M/r^2$: Newton's theorem ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-7|CM Theorem §A8.1.7]]). The potential is deepest at the centre, $\Phi(0) = -\frac32M/R$, one and a half times its surface value; for the Earth taken as uniform, $|\Phi(0)| = 1.04\times10^{-9}$ against $6.95\times10^{-10}$ at the surface. (The interior solution and the matching were checked symbolically.) In general relativity the lapse of a uniform-density star reduces to this potential to first order in $M/R$ ([[§C9.2 The Uniform-Density Star and Buchdahl's Limit#^thm-c9-2-2|Theorem §C9.2.2]], part 3).
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, Problem 3.4 (p. 46) · Couzens, GR1, Exercise 2.7 (Newton's theorem via Gauss's theorem) · worked here*

> [!example] Example §C1.1.2: What the Earth Would Notice if the Sun Vanished
> Couzens' thought experiment: suppose the Sun disappeared at the time $t_0$. In Newton's theory the potential at the Earth jumps from $-M_\odot/r$ to $0$ at $t_0$ itself ([[#^thm-c1-1-3|Theorem §C1.1.3]], part 1), the centripetal pull vanishes, and the Earth leaves its orbit along the tangent at once. If no influence travels faster than light, the Earth cannot learn of the change before the light from the Sun's last moments arrives, after
>
> $$
> t_{\text{light}} = \frac{1\ \mathrm{AU}}{c} = \frac{1.496\times10^{11}\ \mathrm m}{2.998\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}} = 499\ \mathrm s = 8.3\ \mathrm{min} .
> $$
>
> In that time the Earth keeps moving on its circle at $v = \sqrt{GM_\odot/1\ \mathrm{AU}} = 29.8\ \mathrm{km/s}$ ([[#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]], part 1), so it covers
>
> $$
> s = v\,t_{\text{light}} = 29.8\ \mathrm{km/s}\times499\ \mathrm s = 1.49\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{km} = 2.33\,R_\oplus ,
> $$
>
> an arc of $s/(1\ \mathrm{AU}) = 9.9\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad} = 20.5''$ of its orbit, the same as $2\pi\times(499\ \mathrm s)/(365.25\ \mathrm d)$. The two theories disagree about where the Earth leaves its orbit by this arc, and about the time at which it leaves by $8.3$ minutes.
>
> The thought experiment isolates the signal speed; it is not a possible process in either theory, since mass is conserved in Newtonian mechanics and energy–momentum in relativity. A physical version is the moving source of Theorem §C1.1.3, part 2: had the Sun been displaced by $d$, the Newtonian potential at the Earth would change at once by a fraction $d/(1\ \mathrm{AU})$ of itself (for $d = R_\odot$, $4.65\times10^{-3}$). What general relativity says about the arrival of such a change, and with what speed, is the subject of gravitational waves ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-5|Theorem §C8.1.5]]; speed of light: [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-1|Theorem §C8.2.1]]); the statement here is only that Newton's answer, "at once", is excluded by special relativity.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §1 (p. 7) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C1.1.3: Where Newtonian Gravity Suffices
> The compactness $\epsilon = M/r$ ([[#^def-c1-1-1|Def. §C1.1.1]]) with $M$ in metres, $M = GM_{\text{kg}}/c^2$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-1|Def. §C1.2.1]]): $M_\oplus = 4.435\ \mathrm{mm}$, $M_\odot = 1476.7\ \mathrm m$, $0.6\,M_\odot = 886\ \mathrm m$, $1.4\,M_\odot = 2067\ \mathrm m$.
>
> | System | $M$ | $r$ | $\epsilon = M/r$ | What it means |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | GPS satellite | $4.435\ \mathrm{mm}$ | $2.6562\times10^{7}\ \mathrm m$ | $1.67\times10^{-10}$ | orbit Newtonian; clock rates differ from the ground by parts in $10^{10}$, which matters for navigation ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^law-c1-4-8\|Law §C1.4.8]]) |
> | Earth's surface | $4.435\ \mathrm{mm}$ | $6.3781\times10^{6}\ \mathrm m$ | $6.95\times10^{-10}$ | all of mechanics Newtonian; seen in the frequency of light climbing a $22.5\ \mathrm m$ tower ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^law-c1-4-5\|Law §C1.4.5]]) |
> | Earth's orbit | $1476.7\ \mathrm m$ | $1.496\times10^{11}\ \mathrm m$ | $9.87\times10^{-9}$ | $v^2 = \epsilon$: $v = 29.8\ \mathrm{km/s}$ |
> | Mercury's orbit | $1476.7\ \mathrm m$ | $5.791\times10^{10}\ \mathrm m$ | $2.55\times10^{-8}$ | $v = 47.9\ \mathrm{km/s}$; the anomaly of [[#^law-c1-1-6\|Law §C1.1.6]], about $20\,\epsilon$ per orbit, adds up to $43''$ per century |
> | Sun's surface | $1476.7\ \mathrm m$ | $6.957\times10^{8}\ \mathrm m$ | $2.12\times10^{-6}$ | redshift of sunlight; deflection of starlight grazing the Sun ([[§C6.5 The Bending of Light#^ex-c6-5-1\|Example §C6.5.1]]) |
> | white dwarf | $886\ \mathrm m$ | $7.0\times10^{6}\ \mathrm m$ | $1.27\times10^{-4}$ | redshift of its spectral lines measurable ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^ex-c1-4-2\|Example §C1.4.2]]) |
> | neutron star | $2067\ \mathrm m$ | $1.2\times10^{4}\ \mathrm m$ | $0.17$ | no Newtonian description of its structure ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-4\|Theorem §C9.1.4]]; size of the corrections: [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^ex-c9-1-1\|Example §C9.1.1]]) |
> | black hole | $M$ | $2M$ | $0.5$ | the horizon ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3\|Def. §C7.2.3]]) |
>
> Each entry is one division; for instance at the Earth's surface $\epsilon = 4.435\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm m/6.3781\times10^{6}\ \mathrm m = 6.95\times10^{-10}$, and for the white dwarf $886\ \mathrm m/7.0\times10^{6}\ \mathrm m = 1.27\times10^{-4}$. Across the solar system $\epsilon$ never exceeds $2.12\times10^{-6}$, its value at the Sun's surface: Newtonian gravity there is correct to a few parts in a million, and every relativistic effect in it is a small correction that accumulates (Mercury) or is read off a clock (GPS). Only the compact remnants of stars reach $\epsilon$ near one.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §2.3, eqs. (2.79)–(2.80) (pp. 29–30) · numbers checked by script*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Electrodynamics faced the same instantaneity and escaped it. Its static potential obeys the same Poisson equation ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-2|EM Theorem §B2.2.2]]), but the full theory determines the potentials by wave equations whose solutions are retarded ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|EM Theorem §B11.1.4]]), because charge density is part of a four-vector. The gravitational analogue, retarded solutions of a wave equation for a two-index potential, is linearised gravity ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-5|Theorem §C8.1.5]], [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-2|Theorem §C8.5.2]]).
> - A covariant scalar field with a point source has the $1/r$ potential as its static solution ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^rem-b4-1-4|§B4.1, Remark: Static solutions and the Yukawa potential]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, $m = 0$). Theorem §C1.1.4, part 2, shows the same thing from the other side: the static Newtonian potential, viewed from a moving frame, solves the wave equation.
> - The level-A home of these questions is [[§A8.5 Beyond Newton|CM §A8.5]]: its remarks on instantaneous action and on the two masses ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton#^rem-a8-5-1|CM §A8.5, Remark: Two unexplained assumptions]]) are made quantitative here.
> - The Newtonian orbits that Law §C1.1.6 compares with are worked out in [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits|CM §B2.2]]; why only the $1/r$ and $r^2$ potentials give closed orbits is [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-4|CM Theorem §B2.3.4]].
> - The Green's function of the Poisson equation on bounded regions, with boundary conditions, is [[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^def-c7-3-1|EM Def. §C7.3.1]].
