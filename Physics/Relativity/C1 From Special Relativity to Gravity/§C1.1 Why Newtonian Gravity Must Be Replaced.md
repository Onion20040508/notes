Gravity is by far the weakest of the known interactions: between two protons the gravitational attraction is $8.1\times10^{-37}$ of the electric repulsion. Yet it shapes everything large, because it only attracts, while electric charges of both signs cancel in bulk matter (Couzens, GR1, §1). The theory of it that worked for two centuries is Newton's ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]]). Special relativity, completed at level B, ends with two demands on any law of physics: no influence may reach an event outside the future light cone of its cause ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), and laws are equations between tensors of the same type ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^pr-b2-2-9|Principle §B2.2.9]]). Electromagnetism passed both tests once its fields were assembled into a tensor ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-7|Theorem §B4.2.7]]). The question of this chapter is whether gravity can be treated the same way, and, when it cannot, what replaces it.

This section answers the first half. It recasts Newton's law of force as a field theory for a potential $\Phi$ ([[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]), the only form in which it can be compared with electrodynamics, and solves its field equation ([[#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]]). It then computes where the theory collides with level B: the potential responds instantaneously to its source ([[#^thm-c1-1-3|Theorem §C1.1.3]]), and the field equation changes its form under a Lorentz boost, also after the obvious repair of replacing the Laplacian by the d'Alembertian ([[#^thm-c1-1-4|Theorem §C1.1.4]]). The failure of the repair says what the new theory needs: a source that is a tensor with two indices, and hence a gravitational potential with two indices. Finally the section measures how large relativistic corrections are wherever gravity acts ([[#^def-c1-1-1|Def. §C1.1.1]], [[#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]]) and records the observation Newton's theory could not explain ([[#^law-c1-1-6|Law §C1.1.6]]).

**Setup.** Newtonian gravity in an inertial frame of Newtonian mechanics: an absolute time $t$ and, at each time, Euclidean space $\mathbf R^3$ with Cartesian coordinates. Matter is described by a given mass density $\rho(t, \vec x) \ge 0$, bounded and zero outside a bounded region at each time (a point mass is the limit of such densities); the bodies whose motion is studied are test bodies, whose own field is neglected. The theorems about the model are exact statements within it. Their comparison with special relativity uses the inertial frames of Minkowski spacetime (Relativity B, written here in the signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ of [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]). [[#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]] and the examples are order-of-magnitude statements; their numbers come from the check scripts `verify_c1.py` and `verify_c1_1.py`. [[#^law-c1-1-6|Law §C1.1.6]] is an observation.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$ in every formula, with the restored form beside it (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here $\Phi \to \Phi/c^2$, $\rho \to G\rho/c^2$, $M \to GM/c^2$, $v \to v/c$, $t \to ct$); $\Phi < 0$ near masses and $\Phi \to 0$ at infinity; Greek indices $0$–$3$, Latin $1$–$3$, $x^0 = t$.

## Newtonian gravity as a field theory

Newton's law gives the force between two point masses at the same instant. A field theory splits this into two steps: the source produces a field everywhere, and a body responds to the field where it is. The split is what makes a comparison with the electromagnetic field possible.

> [!model] Model §C1.1.1: Newtonian Gravity as a Field Theory
> *Arena.* Newtonian spacetime: an absolute time $t \in \mathbf R$ and, at each time, Euclidean space $\mathbf R^3$ with the metric $\delta_{ij}$. Inertial frames are related by rotations, translations and Galilean boosts.
>
> *Field and source.* The **Newtonian potential** is a scalar field, one real number at each time and point, $\Phi : \mathbf R \times \mathbf R^3 \to \mathbf R$. Its source is the **mass density** $\rho(t, \vec x) \ge 0$, a given scalar field. They obey the **Poisson equation** with a boundary condition at infinity,
>
> $$
> \nabla^2\Phi(t, \vec x) = 4\pi\rho(t, \vec x), \qquad \Phi(t, \vec x) \to 0 \ \ (|\vec x| \to \infty) \qquad \bigl(\text{restored: } \nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The equation contains no time derivative: $t$ enters only as a label of the slice on which it is solved.
>
> *Response of a test body.* A test body of inertial mass $m_I$ ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]]) and gravitational mass $m_G$ ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^def-a8-1-1|CM Def. §A8.1.1]]) at the position $\vec x(t)$ obeys $m_I\ddot{\vec x} = -m_G\nabla\Phi(t, \vec x(t))$. Since $m_G = m_I$ for every body ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^law-a8-1-3|CM Law §A8.1.3]]),
>
> $$
> \ddot{\vec x}(t) = -\nabla\Phi\bigl(t, \vec x(t)\bigr) = \vec g\bigl(t, \vec x(t)\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec g = -\nabla\Phi$ the gravitational field ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^def-a8-1-2|CM Def. §A8.1.2]]); in SI units $\Phi$ is measured in $\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$, and $\Phi/c^2$ is the dimensionless number written $\Phi$ here.
>
> *Layer.* Newton's law of gravitation, a principle at level A ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]]), is a theorem of this model: the point-source case of [[#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]].
>
> - *Assumes:* test bodies (their own field and their effect on $\rho$ neglected); $\rho$ given, bounded, zero outside a bounded region; one mass in both laws.
> - *Analogy:* electrostatics has the same equation with the opposite sign, $\nabla^2V = -\rho_e/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§B2.2 The Potential and the Poisson and Laplace Equations#^thm-b2-2-2|EM Theorem §B2.2.2]]), and the force $-q\nabla V$: mass plays the role of charge, but like masses attract (Hartle, Table 3.1).
> - *Fails:* for sources that change faster than light could cross them ([[#^thm-c1-1-3|Theorem §C1.1.3]]), in other inertial frames of special relativity ([[#^thm-c1-1-4|Theorem §C1.1.4]]), and wherever the compactness $M/r$ is not small ([[#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §2.2, eqs. (2.55)–(2.57), (2.61) · Hartle, Gravity, §3.3, eqs. (3.12)–(3.18), Table 3.1 (pp. 39–40); §3.4, eqs. (3.25)–(3.30) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §4.1, eqs. (4.4)–(4.6) (p. 51)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.1.2: Solution of the Poisson Equation
> In [[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]], let $\rho(t, \cdot)$ be bounded and zero outside the ball $|\vec x\,'| \le R_0$, at each time $t$.
> 1. The Poisson equation with $\Phi \to 0$ at infinity has exactly one solution,
>
> $$
> \Phi(t, \vec x) = -\int_{\mathbf R^3}\frac{\rho(t, \vec x\,')}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|}\,d^3x' \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \Phi = -G\int\frac{\rho(t, \vec x\,')}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|}\,d^3x'\Bigr) :
> $$
>
> the superposition of the potentials of the mass elements $\rho\,d^3x'$, with the **Green's function** $-1/|\vec x - \vec x\,'|$, which solves $\nabla^2\bigl(-1/|\vec x - \vec x\,'|\bigr) = 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec x - \vec x\,')$ as a distribution ([[§C7.3 Green Functions for Poisson’s Equation#^def-c7-3-1|EM Def. §C7.3.1]]).
> 2. For a point mass $M$ at the origin, $\rho = M\delta^3(\vec x)$: $\Phi = -M/r$, and the force on a test mass $m$ is
>
> $$
> \vec F = -m\nabla\Phi = -\frac{mM}{r^2}\,\hat r \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \vec F = -\frac{GmM}{r^2}\,\hat r\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> Newton's law of gravitation ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]]).
> 3. If $\rho$ is spherically symmetric, with mass $M(r)$ inside radius $r$, then $\Phi$ depends only on $r$ and $\Phi'(r) = M(r)/r^2$; outside all the mass, $\Phi = -M/r$ (Newton's theorem; [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-7|CM Theorem §A8.1.7]]). The uniform sphere, inside and out, is [[#^ex-c1-1-1|Example §C1.1.1]].
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]; *applies:* on each time slice separately, exact within the model; for densities that are only piecewise continuous (a body with a sharp surface) the equation holds as an equation between distributions.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §2.2, eqs. (2.58)–(2.60), Exercise 2.7 · Hartle, Gravity, §3.3, eqs. (3.13)–(3.15), Example 3.1, eqs. (3.19)–(3.22) (pp. 38–40)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Green's function). As distributions in $\vec x$, at fixed $\vec x\,'$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|EM Theorem §B1.2.2]]),
>
> $$
> \nabla^2\frac{1}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|} = -4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec x - \vec x\,') \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \nabla^2\Bigl(-\frac{1}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|}\Bigr) = 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec x - \vec x\,') .
> $$
>
> Tested against a smooth function $f$ of bounded support, this reads $\int d^3x\,\bigl(-1/|\vec x - \vec x\,'|\bigr)\nabla^2f(\vec x) = 4\pi f(\vec x\,')$.
>
> **Step 2** (the integral solves the equation). Let $f$ be a smooth function of bounded support. Moving $\nabla^2$ onto $f$ (the definition of the distributional Laplacian) and inserting the integral for $\Phi$,
>
> $$
> \int d^3x\,\Phi(t, \vec x)\,\nabla^2f(\vec x) = \int d^3x\,\nabla^2f(\vec x)\Bigl(-\int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(t, \vec x\,')}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|}\Bigr) = \int d^3x'\,\rho(t, \vec x\,')\Bigl[\int d^3x\,\Bigl(-\frac{1}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|}\Bigr)\nabla^2f(\vec x)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> The order of integration may be exchanged because the double integral converges absolutely: $\rho$ and $\nabla^2f$ are bounded with bounded supports, and $1/|\vec x - \vec x\,'|$ is integrable over bounded regions ($\int_{|\vec y| < a}d^3y/|\vec y| = 2\pi a^2$). By Step 1 the bracket is $4\pi f(\vec x\,')$, so
>
> $$
> \int d^3x\,\Phi\,\nabla^2f = \int d^3x'\,4\pi\rho(t, \vec x\,')\,f(\vec x\,') ,
> $$
>
> which is the statement $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi\rho$ as distributions. Where $\rho$ is smooth, $\Phi$ is smooth and the equation holds point by point.
>
> **Step 3** (boundary condition). For $|\vec x| > R_0$ and $|\vec x\,'| \le R_0$, the triangle inequality gives $|\vec x - \vec x\,'| \ge |\vec x| - |\vec x\,'| \ge |\vec x| - R_0 > 0$, so
>
> $$
> |\Phi(t, \vec x)| \le \int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(t, \vec x\,')}{|\vec x| - R_0} = \frac{M(t)}{|\vec x| - R_0} \xrightarrow{\ |\vec x| \to \infty\ } 0, \qquad M(t) = \int\rho(t, \vec x\,')\,d^3x' .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (uniqueness). If $\Phi_1$, $\Phi_2$ both solve the problem, $u = \Phi_1 - \Phi_2$ satisfies $\nabla^2u = 4\pi\rho - 4\pi\rho = 0$ on all of $\mathbf R^3$ and $u \to 0$ at infinity. A harmonic function has no local maximum or minimum inside a region ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-2|EM Theorem §B3.1.2]]), so on a closed ball of radius $R$ its largest and smallest values lie on the bounding sphere. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $R$ so large that $|u| < \varepsilon$ on that sphere; then $|u| < \varepsilon$ in the whole ball. Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary and $R$ can be taken larger still, $u = 0$ everywhere. This is the uniqueness theorem of electrostatics with the outer boundary at infinity ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^thm-b3-1-3|EM Theorem §B3.1.3]]). Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 5** (point mass). Substitute $\rho(t, \vec x\,') = M\delta^3(\vec x\,')$ into the integral of part 1:
>
> $$
> \Phi(\vec x) = -M\int d^3x'\,\frac{\delta^3(\vec x\,')}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|} = -\frac{M}{|\vec x|} = -\frac{M}{r} .
> $$
>
> With $\nabla(1/r) = -\hat r/r^2$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|EM Theorem §B1.2.2]]), $\nabla\Phi = -M\nabla(1/r) = M\hat r/r^2$, and
>
> $$
> \vec F = -m\nabla\Phi = -\frac{mM}{r^2}\,\hat r .
> $$
>
> In [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]] the force on $m_1$ points along $\hat r_{12}$, from $m_1$ toward $m_2$; with $m_1 = m$ at $\vec x$ and $m_2 = M$ at the origin, $\hat r_{12} = -\hat r$, and the two agree. Restoring $G$ (multiply $\Phi$ and $\vec F$ by $G$) gives the SI forms. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 6** (spherical symmetry). A rotation $R$ maps a solution $\Phi(\vec x)$ to $\Phi(R^{-1}\vec x)$, a solution for the density $\rho(R^{-1}\vec x) = \rho(\vec x)$; by uniqueness (Step 4) the two coincide, so $\Phi = \Phi(r)$. Integrate the Poisson equation over the ball $B_r$ of radius $r$ and apply the divergence theorem ([[§32 Flux Integrals and the Divergence Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-32-1|452 Thm. §32.1]]), with $\nabla\Phi = \Phi'(r)\hat r$ on the sphere of area $4\pi r^2$:
>
> $$
> \int_{B_r}\nabla^2\Phi\,d^3x = \oint_{\partial B_r}\nabla\Phi\cdot\hat r\,dA = 4\pi r^2\,\Phi'(r), \qquad \int_{B_r}4\pi\rho\,d^3x = 4\pi M(r) ,
> $$
>
> so $\Phi'(r) = M(r)/r^2$. Outside all the mass $M(r) = M$, and integrating inward from infinity, where $\Phi = 0$,
>
> $$
> \Phi(r) = -\int_r^\infty\Phi'(s)\,ds = -\int_r^\infty\frac{M}{s^2}\,ds = -\frac{M}{r} .
> $$
>
> Part 3 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The field equation and the inverse-square law carry the same content: the law is the field equation's response to a point source, and every other source is a superposition of point sources.
> - The integral in part 1 contains the density at the single time $t$ and no other. This is the instantaneous action of [[#^thm-c1-1-3|Theorem §C1.1.3]].
> - The delta function is used as a distribution (Steps 1–2): the potential of a point mass is singular, but its pairing with smooth test functions is finite.

## Where Newton's theory collides with special relativity

The potential of [[#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]] was found slice by slice. Nothing in it says how a change in the source reaches a distant point, and nothing says how the equation looks to an observer who moves. Both questions have definite answers within the model, and both answers conflict with level B.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.1.3: Newtonian Gravity Acts Instantaneously
> In [[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]:
> 1. *(One slice.)* $\Phi(t, \vec x)$ is determined by $\rho(t, \cdot)$ alone. Two mass distributions that agree at the time $t$ produce the same potential, and the same force on every test body, everywhere at that time, whatever their past.
> 2. *(⚑ Unbounded signal speed.)* Let a mass $M$, at rest at the origin until $t_0$, move along a path $\vec d(t)$ inside a bounded region $K$ from $t_0$ on. Then at every point $\vec x$ outside $K$, however far away, the potential changes from $t_0$ on: $\Phi(t, \vec x) = -M/|\vec x - \vec d(t)|$ for $t \ge t_0$, and at distance $r = |\vec x| \gg |\vec d|$
>
> $$
> \Delta\Phi(t, \vec x) = -\frac{M}{|\vec x - \vec d(t)|} + \frac{M}{r} = -\frac{M\,\vec d(t)\cdot\hat x}{r^2} + O\Bigl(\frac{M d^2}{r^3}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> nonzero wherever $\vec d\cdot\hat x \neq 0$. A test body there changes its acceleration at the same instant as the source moves: the influence crosses any distance in zero time.
> 3. *(Conflict with special relativity.)* The cause, event $A = (t_0, \vec 0)$, and its effect at distance $r$, event $B = (t_0, r\hat x)$, are spacelike separated ($\Delta s^2 = r^2 > 0$ in the signature of [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]). In the inertial frame moving with speed $v$ along $\hat x$,
>
> $$
> t'_B - t'_A = -\gamma v r < 0 \qquad \bigl(\text{restored: } -\gamma vr/c^2\bigr) :
> $$
>
> the effect happens before its cause. Special relativity admits influences only into the causal future, a statement that holds in every inertial frame ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]; [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^rem-b1-3-2|§B1.3, Remark: Causality as a statement about cones]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where spacelike means $\Delta s^2 < 0$). The Newtonian potential therefore cannot be a field of special relativity.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]], compared with the inertial frames of special relativity; *applies:* exact within the model; the source in part 2 is any bounded motion of a fixed mass.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §1 (the force "depends on the location of the other mass at the same time"); §2.3 ("effects are instantaneous in Newtonian gravity") · Hartle, Gravity, ch. 6 introduction, eq. (6.1) (p. 107)*

^thm-c1-1-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (one slice). By [[#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]], part 1, the unique solution is $\Phi(t, \vec x) = -\int\rho(t, \vec x\,')\,d^3x'/|\vec x - \vec x\,'|$; the integrand contains $\rho$ at the time $t$ only. Equal densities at $t$ give equal integrals, hence equal $\Phi(t, \cdot)$ and equal $\vec g = -\nabla\Phi$.
>
> **Step 2** (moving source). The density of the displaced mass is $\rho(t, \vec x\,') = M\delta^3(\vec x\,' - \vec d(t))$. The integral gives
>
> $$
> \Phi(t, \vec x) = -M\int d^3x'\,\frac{\delta^3(\vec x\,' - \vec d(t))}{|\vec x - \vec x\,'|} = -\frac{M}{|\vec x - \vec d(t)|} \qquad (t \ge t_0) ,
> $$
>
> and $-M/r$ for $t < t_0$. Expand for $d = |\vec d| \ll r$, with $\vec d\cdot\vec x = rd\cos\theta$:
>
> $$
> |\vec x - \vec d|^2 = r^2 - 2rd\cos\theta + d^2, \qquad \frac{1}{|\vec x - \vec d|} = \frac1r\Bigl(1 - \frac{2d\cos\theta}{r} + \frac{d^2}{r^2}\Bigr)^{-1/2} = \frac1r + \frac{d\cos\theta}{r^2} + O\Bigl(\frac{d^2}{r^3}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> using $(1 + w)^{-1/2} = 1 - \frac12w + O(w^2)$ with $w = -2d\cos\theta/r + d^2/r^2$. Hence
>
> $$
> \Delta\Phi = -M\Bigl(\frac1r + \frac{d\cos\theta}{r^2}\Bigr) + \frac{M}{r} + O\Bigl(\frac{Md^2}{r^3}\Bigr) = -\frac{M\,\vec d\cdot\hat x}{r^2} + O\Bigl(\frac{Md^2}{r^3}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> The expression holds at every $t > t_0$ and every $\vec x$, with no delay depending on $r$.
>
> **Step 3** (the two events in a moving frame). Take the $x$-axis along $\hat x$. The Lorentz transformation to the frame moving with speed $v$ along $+x$ is $t' = \gamma(t - vx)$, $x' = \gamma(x - vt)$ ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|REL Theorem §A1.4.1]], with $c = 1$). For $A = (t_0, 0)$ and $B = (t_0, r)$:
>
> $$
> t'_A = \gamma(t_0 - v\cdot 0) = \gamma t_0, \qquad t'_B = \gamma(t_0 - vr), \qquad t'_B - t'_A = -\gamma vr .
> $$
>
> For every $v > 0$ this is negative. The interval is $\Delta s^2 = -(t_B - t_A)^2 + r^2 = r^2 > 0$, spacelike in the signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; the time order of spacelike separated events is not invariant ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-3|REL Theorem §A1.5.3]]), and Step 3 exhibits the frames that reverse it.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The instantaneity is not a feature of a particular solution: it is the absence of $\partial_t$ from the field equation. Any equation that determines $\Phi$ on one slice from $\rho$ on the same slice has it.
> - Unlike the scalar potential of electrostatics in the Coulomb gauge, which is also instantaneous but cannot be measured on its own ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^rem-b11-1-2|EM §B11.1★, Remark: The instantaneous potential of the Coulomb gauge]]), the Newtonian $\Phi$ fixes the force on a test body directly, so here the instantaneous change is observable.

The second failure concerns the form of the law, not the speed of its effects. Level B's test for a law is that it be an equation between tensors of the same type; then it holds in every inertial frame once it holds in one ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-8|Theorem §B2.2.8]]). Apply the test to the Poisson equation, regarded as an equation for a scalar field on Minkowski spacetime, $\Phi'(x') = \Phi(x)$ under $x' = \Lambda x$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.1.4: The Poisson Equation Is Not Lorentz Covariant
> Let $S'$ move with speed $v$ along the $x$-axis of $S$, $t' = \gamma(t - vx)$, $x' = \gamma(x - vt)$, $y' = y$, $z' = z$, $\gamma = (1 - v^2)^{-1/2}$.
> 1. *(The operator.)* The d'Alembertian $\Box = -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2$ is the same in both frames, $\Box = \Box'$ ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where $\Box_B = \partial_t^2 - \nabla^2 = -\Box$, [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^cau-b2-2-3|Caution: The sign of the box]]). The Laplacian is not:
>
> $$
> \nabla^2 = \Box + \partial_t^2 = \nabla'^2 - \partial_{t'}^2 + \gamma^2\bigl(\partial_{t'} - v\,\partial_{x'}\bigr)^2 = \nabla'^2 + \gamma^2v^2\bigl(\partial_{t'}^2 + \partial_{x'}^2\bigr) - 2\gamma^2v\,\partial_{t'}\partial_{x'} .
> $$
>
> A Poisson equation that holds in $S$ has a different form in $S'$; the theory singles out one inertial frame.
> 2. *(A computed disagreement.)* The potential $\Phi = -M/r$ of a point mass at rest in $S$ is, in $S'$, where the mass moves with velocity $-v$ along $x'$,
>
> $$
> \Phi'(t', \vec x\,') = -\frac{M}{s}, \qquad s^2 = \gamma^2(x' + vt')^2 + y'^2 + z'^2 ,
> $$
>
> and away from the mass
>
> $$
> \nabla'^2\Phi' = \partial_{t'}^2\Phi' = \frac{M\gamma^2v^2\bigl[y'^2 + z'^2 - 2\gamma^2(x' + vt')^2\bigr]}{s^5} \neq 0 .
> $$
>
> The Poisson equation in $S'$ would require $\nabla'^2\Phi' = 0$ wherever $\rho' = 0$. The transformed field instead obeys the wave equation $\Box'\Phi' = 0$.
> 3. *(The naive repair fails.)* Replacing $\nabla^2$ by the invariant $\Box$, i.e. $\Box\Phi = 4\pi\rho$, does not give a covariant law, because the mass-energy density is not a scalar. For dust of rest density $\rho_0$ moving with speed $v$, an observer measures
>
> $$
> \rho = \gamma^2\rho_0 \qquad \bigl(\text{restored: energy density } \gamma^2\rho_0c^2\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> one factor $\gamma$ from the energy of each particle and one from the contraction of the volume. It is the $00$-component of the rank-2 tensor $T^{\mu\nu} = \rho_0u^\mu u^\nu$, the energy–momentum tensor of dust (§C4.2, planned). An equation $\Box\Phi = 4\pi T^{00}$ sets a scalar equal to one component of a rank-2 tensor and holds in at most one frame ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-8|Theorem §B2.2.8]]).
> 4. *(What a covariant equation needs.)* Both sides must be tensors of the same type. With this source there are two possibilities: the scalar $T^\mu{}_\mu$ ($= -\rho_0$ for dust) as the source of a scalar potential, or the whole $T^{\mu\nu}$ as the source of a symmetric potential with two indices, $h_{\mu\nu}$, the type of a metric. Charge density needs one index less: it is the time component of the four-current $J^\mu = \rho_{e,0}u^\mu$, with one factor $\gamma$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]), and Maxwell's equations take a four-vector potential ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-7|Theorem §B4.2.7]]). Which possibility gravity takes, and why, is the subject of the field equations (§C5.1, planned); the geometric reading of $h_{\mu\nu}$ begins in [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]].
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]], with $\Phi$ read as a scalar field on Minkowski spacetime and dust as the source in parts 3–4; *applies:* exact.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, ch. 6 introduction (the Newtonian law "could be true in only one frame", p. 107); §6.6 (Newtonian gravity remains inconsistent with special relativity even in geometric form, p. 131) · Couzens, GR1, §2.2 ("not Lorentz invariant"); §2.1, eqs. (2.50)–(2.52) (dust: $\rho$ is the $00$-component of $\rho U^\mu U^\nu$) · Schutz, §4.4, eq. (4.13) (energy density of moving dust, two factors $\gamma$; "impossible … to represent the energy density as some component of a vector", pp. 91–92)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (chain rule). From $t' = \gamma(t - vx)$, $x' = \gamma(x - vt)$:
>
> $$
> \partial_t = \frac{\partial t'}{\partial t}\partial_{t'} + \frac{\partial x'}{\partial t}\partial_{x'} = \gamma\,\partial_{t'} - \gamma v\,\partial_{x'}, \qquad \partial_x = \frac{\partial t'}{\partial x}\partial_{t'} + \frac{\partial x'}{\partial x}\partial_{x'} = -\gamma v\,\partial_{t'} + \gamma\,\partial_{x'} ,
> $$
>
> and $\partial_y = \partial_{y'}$, $\partial_z = \partial_{z'}$.
>
> **Step 2** (squares). The coefficients are constants, so
>
> $$
> \partial_t^2 = \gamma^2\bigl(\partial_{t'}^2 - 2v\,\partial_{t'}\partial_{x'} + v^2\partial_{x'}^2\bigr), \qquad \partial_x^2 = \gamma^2\bigl(\partial_{x'}^2 - 2v\,\partial_{t'}\partial_{x'} + v^2\partial_{t'}^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** ($\Box$ is invariant). Subtract:
>
> $$
> -\partial_t^2 + \partial_x^2 = \gamma^2\bigl[(1 - v^2)\partial_{x'}^2 - (1 - v^2)\partial_{t'}^2\bigr] + \gamma^2(2v - 2v)\,\partial_{t'}\partial_{x'} = -\partial_{t'}^2 + \partial_{x'}^2 ,
> $$
>
> since $\gamma^2(1 - v^2) = 1$. Adding $\partial_y^2 + \partial_z^2 = \partial_{y'}^2 + \partial_{z'}^2$ gives $\Box = \Box'$.
>
> **Step 4** ($\nabla^2$ is not). Write $\nabla^2 = \Box + \partial_t^2$, use Step 3 for $\Box$ and Step 1 for $\partial_t$, and $\Box' = -\partial_{t'}^2 + \nabla'^2$:
>
> $$
> \nabla^2 = \nabla'^2 - \partial_{t'}^2 + \gamma^2\bigl(\partial_{t'} - v\,\partial_{x'}\bigr)^2 = \nabla'^2 + (\gamma^2 - 1)\partial_{t'}^2 - 2\gamma^2v\,\partial_{t'}\partial_{x'} + \gamma^2v^2\partial_{x'}^2 ,
> $$
>
> and $\gamma^2 - 1 = \gamma^2v^2$ gives the form in part 1. The extra terms do not vanish for $v \neq 0$: applied to the function $x'^2$ they give $2\gamma^2v^2$. (The identity is checked symbolically in `verify_c1_1.py`.)
>
> **Step 5** (the point mass seen from $S'$). The inverse boost is $x = \gamma(x' + vt')$, $y = y'$, $z = z'$, so the scalar field $\Phi'(t', \vec x\,') = \Phi(t, \vec x) = -M/\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$ is $-M/s$ with $s^2 = \gamma^2X^2 + y'^2 + z'^2$, $X \equiv x' + vt'$. Its derivatives, with $\partial_{x'}s = \gamma^2X/s$, $\partial_{y'}s = y'/s$, $\partial_{t'}s = \gamma^2vX/s$:
>
> $$
> \partial_{x'}^2\frac1s = -\frac{\gamma^2}{s^3} + \frac{3\gamma^4X^2}{s^5}, \qquad \partial_{y'}^2\frac1s = -\frac{1}{s^3} + \frac{3y'^2}{s^5}, \qquad \partial_{z'}^2\frac1s = -\frac{1}{s^3} + \frac{3z'^2}{s^5} .
> $$
>
> Their sum, over the common denominator $s^5$ and with $s^2 = \gamma^2X^2 + y'^2 + z'^2$:
>
> $$
> \nabla'^2\frac1s = \frac{-(\gamma^2 + 2)\bigl(\gamma^2X^2 + y'^2 + z'^2\bigr) + 3\gamma^4X^2 + 3y'^2 + 3z'^2}{s^5} = \frac{(\gamma^2 - 1)\bigl[2\gamma^2X^2 - y'^2 - z'^2\bigr]}{s^5} .
> $$
>
> Multiplying by $-M$ and using $\gamma^2 - 1 = \gamma^2v^2$ gives the formula of part 2. For the time derivative,
>
> $$
> \partial_{t'}^2\frac1s = v^2\Bigl(-\frac{\gamma^2}{s^3} + \frac{3\gamma^4X^2}{s^5}\Bigr) = \frac{\gamma^2v^2\bigl(3\gamma^2X^2 - s^2\bigr)}{s^5} = \frac{\gamma^2v^2\bigl[2\gamma^2X^2 - y'^2 - z'^2\bigr]}{s^5} ,
> $$
>
> the same as $\nabla'^2(1/s)$; hence $\nabla'^2\Phi' = \partial_{t'}^2\Phi'$ and $\Box'\Phi' = 0$, as Step 3 requires of a field with $\Box\Phi = 0$ off the source in $S$. At $X = 0$, $y' = 1$, $z' = 0$, $M = 1$, $v = 3/5$ ($\gamma = 5/4$): $\nabla'^2\Phi' = \gamma^2v^2 = 9/16$ (`verify_c1_1.py`).
>
> **Step 6** (moving dust). In its rest frame the dust has $n_0$ particles of mass $m$ per unit volume, $\rho_0 = mn_0$. In a frame where it moves with speed $v$, a box that holds $N$ particles has rest volume $V_0 = N/n_0$, contracted along the motion to $V = V_0/\gamma$ ([[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^thm-a2-2-2|REL Theorem §A2.2.2]]), so $n = N/V = \gamma n_0$; each particle carries energy $E = \gamma m$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^def-b2-1-3|Def. §B2.1.3]], $c = 1$). The energy per unit volume is
>
> $$
> \rho = E\,n = (\gamma m)(\gamma n_0) = \gamma^2\rho_0 .
> $$
>
> With the four-velocity $u^\mu = \gamma(1, v, 0, 0)$ (upper components are the same in both signatures; [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]), $T^{00} = \rho_0u^0u^0 = \rho_0\gamma^2$: the same number.
>
> **Step 7** (the repaired equation in two frames). Suppose $\Box\Phi = 4\pi T^{00}$ holds in the rest frame $S$ of a dust cloud, where $T^{00} = \rho_0$: $\Box\Phi = 4\pi\rho_0$. In $S'$, $\Box'\Phi'$ at the image point equals $\Box\Phi$ (Step 3, $\Phi$ a scalar), so $\Box'\Phi' = 4\pi\rho_0$; the law in $S'$ would require $\Box'\Phi' = 4\pi T'^{00} = 4\pi\gamma^2\rho_0$ (Step 6). The two agree only for $\gamma = 1$.
>
> **Step 8** (the scalar that is available). Contract with the metric: $T^\mu{}_\mu = \eta_{\mu\nu}T^{\mu\nu} = \rho_0\,\eta_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = \rho_0\,(u\cdot u) = -\rho_0$, since $u\cdot u = -1$ in the signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]). For charge: the charge in a box is the same in every frame ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^law-b4-2-5|Law §B4.2.5]]) and only the volume contracts, so $\rho_e = \gamma\rho_{e,0}$, the time component of $\rho_{e,0}u^\mu$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]).
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Newtonian gravity fails the test of level B twice: in its operator ($\nabla^2$, Steps 1–5) and in its source ($\rho$, Steps 6–7). Repairing the operator alone does not help.
> - The count of factors $\gamma$ in the source fixes the number of indices of the potential: one for charge (a four-vector potential), two for mass-energy (a potential with two indices, $h_{\mu\nu}$, of the same type as the metric $\eta_{\mu\nu}$).
> - The static field of Step 5, seen from a moving frame, already obeys the covariant wave equation. A covariant theory has the Newtonian potential as its static limit; it is the slice-by-slice Poisson equation that has to go.
