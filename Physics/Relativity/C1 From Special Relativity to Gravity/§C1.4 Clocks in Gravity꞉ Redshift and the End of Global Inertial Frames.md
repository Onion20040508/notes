What does the equivalence principle say about clocks? [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle|§C1.3]] made two statements: a uniform gravitational field cannot be told apart from uniform acceleration ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]]), and in a freely falling frame all non-gravitational physics is that of special relativity ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]). Both were statements about falling bodies and local laboratories. This section draws a prediction from them about time, before any geometry is introduced, and finds that it breaks the picture with which level C started.

That picture is the naive one: keep the inertial frames of special relativity and add gravity as one more force ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]). Then the laboratory at rest on the Earth is an inertial frame. Two identical clocks at rest in it, one at the bottom of a tower and one at the top, both measure the time of that frame, and light sent from the bottom to the top arrives with the frequency it left with: source and receiver are at rest relative to each other, and the Doppler formula ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]]) with $\beta = 0$ gives $\nu_{\text{top}} = \nu_{\text{bottom}}$. The equivalence principle predicts something else. Replace the tower by a rocket accelerating at $g$ in empty space: while the light climbs the height $h$, the receiver picks up the speed $gh$ away from it, and the light arrives redshifted by the fraction $gh$. If gravity is equivalent to acceleration, light climbing a tower must lose the same fraction, and Pound and Rebka measured exactly this loss, to about 1%. The naive picture fails at its first quantitative test.

In order, the section builds: the rocket model ([[#^mod-c1-4-1|Model §C1.4.1]]) and the frequency shift in it, first to first order and then exactly ([[#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]]); the transfer to a static gravitational field by the equivalence principle, $\Delta\nu/\nu = -\Delta\Phi$ ([[#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]]); an independent derivation from energy conservation ([[#^thm-c1-4-4|Theorem §C1.4.4]]); the experiment ([[#^law-c1-4-5|Law §C1.4.5]]); the same fact read as a law for clock rates, $d\tau = (1 + \Phi)\,dt$ ([[#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]]); Schild's argument that the frame at rest on the Earth is not inertial, and the conclusion that near a mass no frame is inertial except locally ([[#^thm-c1-4-7|Theorem §C1.4.7]]); and two working tests of the clock-rate law, the Global Positioning System and the Hafele–Keating flights ([[#^law-c1-4-8|Law §C1.4.8]], [[#^law-c1-4-9|Law §C1.4.9]]).

**Setup.** Two systems, linked only by the equivalence principle. (i) A rocket in flat spacetime, described from an inertial frame of special relativity: exact kinematics, no gravity. (ii) Clocks and light sources at rest in a *static*, *weak* gravitational field: the Newtonian potential $\Phi(\vec x)$ ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]) does not depend on time, $|\Phi| \ll 1$, and a *static clock* is one held at a fixed position relative to the source of the field (by a tower, a floor, a rocket engine). Every result for (ii) is first order in $\Phi$ (equivalently in $gh$); the exact relations need the metric ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric|§C1.5]] and C6 (planned)). Clocks are ideal: their rate depends only on their position and instantaneous velocity, not on their acceleration ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^mod-a2-5-1|Model §A2.5.1]]). A frequency is always measured by a clock at rest with the instrument: $\nu = 1/\Delta\tau$ for the proper time $\Delta\tau$ between successive crests. The *coordinate time* $t$ of a static field is defined in [[#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]]: the reading of one static reference clock, at $\Phi = 0$, carried to every static point by light signals.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $ds^2 = -dt^2 + d\vec x^{\,2}$ in an inertial frame and proper time $d\tau^2 = -ds^2$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$, each measurable result also restored (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here $\Phi \to \Phi/c^2$, $gh \to gh/c^2$, $M \to GM/c^2$, $v \to v/c$); $\Phi < 0$ near masses, $\Phi \to 0$ at infinity unless another reference level is named; "redshift" means a lower received frequency ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^def-a2-4-1|Def. §A2.4.1]]). Links back to Relativity B carry its signature $+{-}{-}{-}$ with $c$ explicit; the hyperbolic-motion formulas used from there do not depend on the signature. Planck's constant is written $2\pi\hbar$, since $h$ is a height here.

## The accelerated rocket

> [!model] Model §C1.4.1: Emitter and Receiver in a Uniformly Accelerated Rocket
> In an inertial frame of special relativity, with coordinates $(t, x)$ ($y$, $z$ play no role), a rocket moves along $+x$, starting from rest at $t = 0$. Its floor carries an **emitter** $E$: a light source of proper frequency $\nu_E$ with its own clock. A **receiver** $R$, a clock with a frequency meter, sits at height $h$ above the floor, measured in the rocket's momentary rest frame.
> - The floor moves with constant proper acceleration $g$: it is the uniformly accelerated particle of [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^mod-b3-3-4|Model §B3.3.4]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, $c$ explicit; here $c = 1$), with the worldline of [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]].
> - The rocket is **rigid**: the proper distance between floor and receiver stays $h$. With the pivot $X \equiv x + 1/g$, the point at height $\xi$ moves on the hyperbola
>
> $$
> X = \Bigl(\frac1g + \xi\Bigr)\cosh g\eta, \qquad t = \Bigl(\frac1g + \xi\Bigr)\sinh g\eta ,
> $$
>
> all points sharing the parameter $\eta$ (the floor's proper time). The point at height $\xi$ then has proper acceleration $g/(1 + g\xi)$: the receiver accelerates slightly less than the floor, $g/(1 + gh) = g(1 - gh + \dots)$. That this motion is rigid, and these accelerations, are Steps 1–2 of the Derivation of [[#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]].
>
> *Assumptions: flat spacetime, the rocket a test system (its own gravity neglected); each part of the rocket driven so as to follow its hyperbola (rigid motion, not a rigid body that transmits forces instantly, which relativity excludes: [[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^cau-a2-5-1|§A2.5, Caution: No rigid bodies]]); ideal clocks ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^mod-a2-5-1|Model §A2.5.1]]); light in vacuum along the $x$ axis; the motion lasts as long as the experiment.*
> *Source: Hartle §6.3, eqs. (6.5)–(6.6), p. 116 (PDF 117), fn. 4; Prob. 6.6–6.7, p. 132 (PDF 133) · 't Hooft ch. 3, eq. (3.12), p. 12 (PDF 13) · Schutz §5.1, p. 114 (PDF 132)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.2: Frequency Shift in an Accelerated Rocket
> Within [[#^mod-c1-4-1|Model §C1.4.1]], light of proper frequency $\nu_E$ sent up from the floor is received at height $h$ with the frequency $\nu_R$ given by:
> 1. to first order in $gh$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_R}{\nu_E} = 1 - gh + O(g^2h^2), \qquad\text{restored}\quad \frac{\nu_R}{\nu_E} = 1 - \frac{gh}{c^2} :
> $$
>
>    a redshift for light travelling along the acceleration; light sent down from $R$ to $E$ arrives blueshifted, $\nu_{E,\text{rec}}/\nu_{R,\text{em}} = 1 + gh$;
> 2. exactly, for the rigid rocket, at every time of emission,
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_R}{\nu_E} = \frac{1}{1 + gh}, \qquad \Delta\tau_R = (1 + gh)\,\Delta\tau_E ,
> $$
>
>    where $\Delta\tau_E$ is the floor clock's interval between two emitted crests and $\Delta\tau_R$ the receiver clock's interval between their arrivals;
> 3. the rocket is **stationary**: the boost with rapidity $g\,\Delta\eta$ about the pivot maps every rocket worldline onto itself and shifts its parameter by $\Delta\eta$, so an experiment repeated later in the rocket gives the same result.
>
> No gravity acts. The shift is a Doppler shift: the receiver gains speed while the light is in flight.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c1-4-1|Model §C1.4.1]]; *applies:* flat spacetime; part 2 exact for the rigid rocket; part 1 needs $gh \ll 1$, and it is the only part the comparison with gravity uses.
>
> *Source: Hartle §6.3, eqs. (6.5)–(6.10), Fig. 6.7, pp. 116–117 (PDF 117–118); Prob. 6.6(c), p. 132 (PDF 133) · 't Hooft ch. 3, eqs. (3.12)–(3.13), p. 12 (PDF 13) · Schutz §5.1, eq. (5.2), p. 115 (PDF 133)*

^thm-c1-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1 (proper time and proper acceleration along a rocket worldline).** Write $\rho = 1/g + \xi$. Along the worldline of [[#^mod-c1-4-1|Model §C1.4.1]], $dt = \rho g\cosh g\eta\,d\eta$ and $dX = dx = \rho g\sinh g\eta\,d\eta$, so
>
> $$
> d\tau^2 = dt^2 - dx^2 = \rho^2g^2\bigl(\cosh^2 g\eta - \sinh^2 g\eta\bigr)\,d\eta^2 = \rho^2g^2\,d\eta^2, \qquad d\tau = g\rho\,d\eta = (1 + g\xi)\,d\eta .
> $$
>
> On the floor ($\xi = 0$) $\tau_E = \eta$; on the receiver ($\xi = h$) $d\tau_R = (1 + gh)\,d\eta$. In terms of its own proper time $\tau$ the worldline reads $X = \rho\cosh(\tau/\rho)$, $t = \rho\sinh(\tau/\rho)$, which is [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]] (with $c = 1$, origin shifted to the pivot) for the proper acceleration $\alpha = 1/\rho$. So the floor has $1/\rho = g$ and the receiver $1/(1/g + h) = g/(1 + gh)$.
>
> **Step 2 (rigidity).** Fix $\eta$. The floor's four-velocity is $u_E = dx^\mu/d\tau_E = (\cosh g\eta, \sinh g\eta)$ in $(t, x)$ components. The receiver event with the same $\eta$ is displaced from the floor event by
>
> $$
> \Delta x^\mu = h\,(\sinh g\eta, \cosh g\eta), \qquad u_E\cdot\Delta x = -h\cosh g\eta\sinh g\eta + h\sinh g\eta\cosh g\eta = 0 ,
> $$
>
> so the two events are simultaneous in the floor's momentary rest frame (the line of constant $\eta$ passes through the pivot), and their separation there is
>
> $$
> \Delta x\cdot\Delta x = h^2\bigl(-\sinh^2 g\eta + \cosh^2 g\eta\bigr) = h^2 .
> $$
>
> The proper height is $h$ at every $\eta$: the motion is rigid. (Two rockets with the *same* proper acceleration at both ends would drift apart in their rest frame; rigidity forces the top to accelerate less.)
>
> **Step 3 (a light ray from floor to receiver).** Light moving up obeys $X - t = \text{const}$. On a rocket worldline $X - t = \rho(\cosh g\eta - \sinh g\eta) = \rho\,e^{-g\eta}$. A crest leaving the floor at $\eta_e$ and reaching the receiver at $\eta_r$ therefore satisfies
>
> $$
> \frac1g\,e^{-g\eta_e} = \Bigl(\frac1g + h\Bigr)e^{-g\eta_r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \eta_r = \eta_e + \frac1g\ln(1 + gh) .
> $$
>
> The delay in $\eta$ is the same for every crest.
>
> **Step 4 (two crests: part 2).** Crests leaving at $\eta_e$ and $\eta_e + \Delta\eta$ arrive at $\eta_r$ and $\eta_r + \Delta\eta$. By Step 1,
>
> $$
> \Delta\tau_E = \Delta\eta, \qquad \Delta\tau_R = (1 + gh)\,\Delta\eta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\nu_R}{\nu_E} = \frac{1/\Delta\tau_R}{1/\Delta\tau_E} = \frac{1}{1 + gh} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5 (light sent down).** Light moving down obeys $X + t = \text{const}$, and $X + t = \rho\,e^{g\eta}$ on a rocket worldline. A crest from the receiver at $\eta_e$ reaches the floor at $\eta_r$ with $(1/g + h)e^{g\eta_e} = (1/g)e^{g\eta_r}$, again $\eta_r = \eta_e + \frac1g\ln(1 + gh)$. Now the emitter's interval is $(1 + gh)\Delta\eta$ and the receiver's $\Delta\eta$, so the floor receives $1 + gh$ times the frequency emitted at the top: a blueshift.
>
> **Step 6 (part 3).** The boost with rapidity $\theta$ about the pivot acts as $X \mapsto X\cosh\theta + t\sinh\theta$, $t \mapsto X\sinh\theta + t\cosh\theta$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-3|Def. §B1.2.3]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). Applied to $X = \rho\cosh g\eta$, $t = \rho\sinh g\eta$, the addition formulas give
>
> $$
> X \mapsto \rho\cosh(g\eta + \theta), \qquad t \mapsto \rho\sinh(g\eta + \theta) :
> $$
>
> the same worldline, with $\eta$ shifted by $\theta/g$. A Lorentz transformation maps light rays to light rays and preserves proper times, so every experiment in the rocket is mapped to the same experiment performed $\theta/g$ later.
>
> **Step 7 (part 1 by Hartle's route, to first order).** Independently of Steps 1–5, expand everything to first order in $g$. At $t = 0$ the rocket is at rest, the floor at $x = 0$ and the receiver at $x = h$. To the order needed both move as $\frac12gt^2$ ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]], item 2: $x \approx \frac12\alpha t^2$ for $\alpha t \ll 1$), so $x_E(t) = \frac12gt^2$, $x_R(t) = h + \frac12gt^2$. A crest emitted at $t = 0$ from $x = 0$ reaches the receiver when
>
> $$
> t_1 = x_R(t_1) - 0 = h + \tfrac12gt_1^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_1 = h + \tfrac12gh^2 + O(g^2) .
> $$
>
> At emission the emitter is at rest, so in the inertial frame the light has frequency $\nu_E$, and it keeps that frequency along its path (a plane wave in an inertial frame). At reception the receiver moves away from the light's origin with speed $v = gt_1 = gh + O(g^2h^2)$. The Doppler formula for a receiver receding at speed $v$ ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]]) gives
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_R}{\nu_E} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - v}{1 + v}} = 1 - v + O(v^2) = 1 - gh + O(g^2h^2) ,
> $$
>
> in agreement with part 2 expanded: $1/(1 + gh) = 1 - gh + g^2h^2 - \dots$ (both expansions checked by script).
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The redshift in the rocket is kinematics: the light's frequency in the inertial frame never changes; the receiver moves.
> - The exact ratio is the ratio of the two clocks' rates along lines of constant $\eta$: $d\tau_R/d\tau_E = 1 + gh$. Clocks higher up in the rocket run fast relative to the floor ('t Hooft's local clock speed $1 + g\xi$, eq. (3.13)); this is the rocket form of [[#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]].
> - Stationarity (part 3) is what makes the shift the same at every emission, although the rocket's speed grows without bound. It plays the role of the time independence of a static field in [[#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]] and [[#^thm-c1-4-7|Theorem §C1.4.7]].

## From the rocket to gravity

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.3: Gravitational Redshift
> Let light pass from an emitter at rest at potential $\Phi_E$ to a receiver at rest at potential $\Phi_R$ in a static weak field ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]). To first order in the potentials,
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_R}{\nu_E} = 1 - (\Phi_R - \Phi_E), \qquad \frac{\Delta\nu}{\nu} \equiv \frac{\nu_R - \nu_E}{\nu_E} = -\Delta\Phi ; \qquad\text{restored}\quad \frac{\Delta\nu}{\nu} = -\frac{\Delta\Phi}{c^2} .
> $$
>
> Light climbing to higher potential is redshifted, light falling to lower potential blueshifted. In particular:
> 1. in a uniform field $g$ ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^mod-a8-1-5|CM Model §A8.1.5]]), with the receiver a height $h$ above the emitter, $\Delta\Phi = gh$ and $\nu_R/\nu_E = 1 - gh$ (restored $1 - gh/c^2$), the rocket result of [[#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]], part 1;
> 2. outside a spherical mass $M$, with $\Phi = -M/r$ ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]]), between radii $r_E$ and $r_R$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\Delta\nu}{\nu} = -M\Bigl(\frac{1}{r_E} - \frac{1}{r_R}\Bigr), \qquad\text{for a distant receiver}\quad \frac{\Delta\nu}{\nu} = -\frac{M}{r_E} \quad\Bigl(\text{restored } -\frac{GM}{r_Ec^2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> The shift depends only on the potentials at the two clocks: not on the path of the light, and not on what kind of signal or clock is used.
>
> *Model:* static weak field, emitter and receiver static; *applies:* first order in $\Phi$; derived from the equivalence principle alone, without field equations. The exact form for a static metric, $\nu_R/\nu_E = \sqrt{g_{00}(\vec x_E)/g_{00}(\vec x_R)}$ (d'Inverno (16.5); the ratio is the same in both signatures), needs the metric of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric|§C1.5]], and its Schwarzschild form is in C6 (planned).
>
> *Source: Hartle §6.3, eqs. (6.11)–(6.12), (6.14), Ex. 6.2, pp. 117–119 (PDF 118–120) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.2, eqs. (16.5)–(16.9), pp. 291–292 (PDF 306–307) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2.3, eq. (6.81), p. 129 (preview) · Schutz §5.1, eq. (5.1)*

^thm-c1-4-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1 (a uniform field, seen from free fall).** Take a tower of height $h$ in a uniform field $g$, small enough that the field does not change across it while the light is in flight. Let $F$ be the frame that falls freely from rest at the moment of emission ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]]). By the Einstein equivalence principle ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]), $F$ is an inertial frame of special relativity for everything that happens in the tower: emission, propagation and reception of light, and the ticking of the clocks. Relative to $F$, every static point of the tower accelerates upward with $g$, rigidly (the tower keeps its height). So, seen from $F$, the tower is the rocket of [[#^mod-c1-4-1|Model §C1.4.1]] ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]] is the Newtonian form of this identification).
>
> **Step 2 (the rocket result).** [[#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]], part 1, then gives $\nu_R/\nu_E = 1 - gh$ for light from the bottom to the top. The potential of a uniform field is $\Phi = gz$ up to a constant (potential energy $mgz$ per unit mass, [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-1|CM Theorem §A4.4.1]]), so $gh = \Phi_R - \Phi_E$: part 1, and the general formula for this case.
>
> **Step 3 (a non-uniform field, piece by piece).** Join $E$ to $R$ by a chain of static points $\vec x_0 = \vec x_E, \vec x_1, \dots, \vec x_N = \vec x_R$, so close that the field is uniform across each step $\Delta\vec x_k = \vec x_{k+1} - \vec x_k$ (length $L_k$, direction $\hat n_k$). Send the light from static point to static point; each static observer measures the frequency with a local clock. On step $k$, Steps 1–2 apply with the local field $\vec g_k = -\nabla\Phi(\vec x_k)$: in the freely falling frame the static points accelerate with $-\vec g_k = \nabla\Phi$, and during the flight time $L_k$ the receiving point gains the velocity $L_k\nabla\Phi$. Only its component along the light, $v_k = L_k\,\hat n_k\cdot\nabla\Phi = \Phi(\vec x_{k+1}) - \Phi(\vec x_k) + O(L_k^2)$, gives a first-order Doppler shift; a transverse velocity shifts the frequency only at second order ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-10|Theorem §B2.1.10]]; [[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^law-a2-4-3|Law §A2.4.3]]). So
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_{k+1}}{\nu_k} = 1 - \bigl[\Phi(\vec x_{k+1}) - \Phi(\vec x_k)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> **Step 4 (multiply the steps).** The frequencies measured at the intermediate points cancel in the product:
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_R}{\nu_E} = \prod_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\nu_{k+1}}{\nu_k} = \prod_{k=0}^{N-1}\Bigl(1 - \bigl[\Phi(\vec x_{k+1}) - \Phi(\vec x_k)\bigr]\Bigr) = 1 - \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\bigl[\Phi(\vec x_{k+1}) - \Phi(\vec x_k)\bigr] + O(\Phi^2) = 1 - (\Phi_R - \Phi_E) + O(\Phi^2) ,
> $$
>
> since the sum telescopes. The result is independent of the chain, hence of the path of the light.
>
> **Step 5 (part 2).** Insert $\Phi(r) = -M/r$: $\Phi_R - \Phi_E = -M/r_R + M/r_E = M(1/r_E - 1/r_R)$. For $r_R \to \infty$ this is $M/r_E$. Restoring with $M \to GM/c^2$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]) gives $-GM/(r_Ec^2)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - In the freely falling frame the light's frequency does not change; the static observers are the ones who move. The gravitational redshift is the Doppler shift of static observers relative to free fall, added up along the path ([[#^thm-c1-4-7|Theorem §C1.4.7]], part 2, states this as a fact about frames).
> - Nothing in Steps 1–4 refers to light as such: any periodic signal between static points is shifted by the same factor. This is why the redshift is a statement about clocks ([[#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]]).
> - The equivalence principle is used only locally, step by step, which is all it can deliver ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^cau-c1-3-1|§C1.3, Caution: "Equivalence" is local]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.4: Redshift from Energy Conservation
> In a uniform field $g$, drop a body of rest mass $m$ from the top of a tower of height $h$, convert its whole energy at the bottom into one photon, send the photon up, and convert it back into a body of rest mass $m'$ at the top. If energy is conserved, so that no cycle of this kind can gain energy, then $m' = m$, and the photon's frequencies at the bottom, $\nu$, and at the top, $\nu'$, obey
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu'}{\nu} = \frac{1}{1 + gh} + O(g^2h^2) = 1 - gh + O(g^2h^2) \qquad\Bigl(\text{restored } 1 - \frac{gh}{c^2}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the shift of [[#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]], part 1, obtained without the equivalence principle.
>
> *Model:* Newtonian free fall in a uniform field ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^mod-a8-1-5|CM Model §A8.1.5]]); a photon of frequency $\nu$ carries the energy $2\pi\hbar\nu$ ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|QM Principle §A1.2.3]]); the conversions are ideal (all energy into one photon and back; the Earth takes up momentum but no energy); *applies:* first order in $gh$. It is a consistency argument: any theory in which energy is conserved and a photon's energy is proportional to its frequency must have this shift.
>
> *Source: Schutz §5.1, eq. (5.1), Fig. 5.1, pp. 111–112 (PDF 129–130) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.2 (the same perpetual-motion argument with buckets of excited atoms, Fig. 16.1), pp. 290–291 (PDF 305–306) · Hartle Prob. 6.8 (the "photon mass" version)*

^thm-c1-4-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1 (energy of the body at the bottom).** Falling from rest through $h$, the body reaches the speed $v$ with $\frac12v^2 = gh$ (energy conservation in the uniform field, [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-1|CM Theorem §A4.4.1]]). Its total energy, measured at the bottom, is rest energy plus kinetic energy ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]], with $c = 1$):
>
> $$
> E = m + \tfrac12mv^2 + O(mv^4) = m + mgh + O(mg^2h^2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2 (the photon).** All of $E$ becomes one photon: $2\pi\hbar\nu = E$. At the top the photon has the energy $E' = 2\pi\hbar\nu'$, which becomes a body of rest mass $m' = E'$.
>
> **Step 3 (no perpetual motion).** If $m' > m$, the body at the top can be dropped again, and each cycle gains $m' - m$; if $m' < m$, the cycle run backwards gains $m - m'$. Energy conservation forbids both, so $m' = m$.
>
> **Step 4 (the ratio).**
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu'}{\nu} = \frac{E'}{E} = \frac{m}{m + mgh + O(mg^2h^2)} = \frac{1}{1 + gh} + O(g^2h^2) = 1 - gh + O(g^2h^2) .
> $$
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - A photon climbing through $h$ loses the same fraction $gh$ of its energy as a body of the same energy would lose in kinetic energy: light "weighs" $E$. The equivalence principle and energy conservation give the same answer at this order.
> - The agreement of $1/(1 + gh)$ with the exact rocket ratio of [[#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]], part 2, is only to first order: Step 1 used Newtonian free fall and dropped $O(g^2h^2)$.

> [!law] Law §C1.4.5: The Pound–Rebka–Snider Experiment
> Pound and Rebka (1960), and Pound and Snider (1964–65) with improved accuracy, sent the $14.4\ \mathrm{keV}$ gamma rays of ${}^{57}\mathrm{Fe}$ through the $22.5\ \mathrm m$ height of the tower of the Jefferson Physical Laboratory at Harvard, between a source and an absorber containing the same nuclei. In the Mössbauer effect the nuclei are bound in a crystal, so emission and absorption are free of recoil and the line is sharp enough to show a shift of order $10^{-15}$ (the line width was still about $1000$ times the shift; the signal was extracted by modulating the source's speed). A gamma ray shifted away from the absorber's frequency is absorbed less; moving the source at a small measured speed adds a Doppler shift that restores resonant absorption, and that speed measures the gravitational shift.
> - *Predicted* ([[#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]], part 1): $gh/c^2 = 2.46\times10^{-15}$ per pass, a redshift going up and a blueshift going down; the difference between the two directions is $2gh/c^2 = 4.91\times10^{-15}$. The compensating source speed is $gh/c = 0.74\ \mu\mathrm m/\mathrm s$; the energy shift of the line is $14.4\ \mathrm{keV}\times2.46\times10^{-15} = 3.5\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{eV}$.
> - *Measured:* d'Inverno & Vickers quote $0.997 \pm 0.009$ times the predicted $4.92\times10^{-15}$; Hartle and Schutz state agreement to about 1% for Pound–Rebka and Pound–Snider together.
> - *Later:* in 1976 a hydrogen-maser clock carried by a Scout rocket to about $10\,000\ \mathrm{km}$ was compared with one on the ground; the predicted gravitational shift there is about $4.2\times10^{-10}$, and theory and measurement agreed to within $2\times10^{-4}$ of it (d'Inverno & Vickers; Hartle's chapter 10, the home of solar-system tests in C6 (planned)).
>
> The sources describe the geometry differently: d'Inverno & Vickers have the gamma rays rise from the bottom, Hartle has them fall from the top. The quoted $4.92\times10^{-15}$ is twice the one-way shift over $22.5\ \mathrm m$, the size of the difference between the two directions.
>
> *Valid when:* a weak, nearly uniform field over $22.5\ \mathrm m$ near the Earth's surface; it tests the first-order shift to about 1%, and the rocket experiment tests the $1/r$ potential over $10^4\ \mathrm{km}$ to $2\times10^{-4}$. An experimental input: it decides between the naive picture of the opening (no shift) and the equivalence principle (shift $gh$).
>
> *Source: Hartle Box 6.1, p. 118 (PDF 119) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.2, pp. 292–293 (PDF 307–308) · Schutz §5.1, p. 112 (PDF 130) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2.3, p. 129 · numbers checked by script*

## Clocks at different heights

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.6: Static Clocks Run at Different Rates
> In a static weak field, let the **coordinate time** $t$ be the time of a static reference clock at $\Phi = 0$, carried to every static point by light signals (each static point subtracts the fixed travel time of the signals from the reference). To first order:
> 1. a clock at rest at potential $\Phi$ measures the proper time
>
> $$
> d\tau = (1 + \Phi)\,dt \qquad\Bigl(\text{restored } d\tau = \Bigl(1 + \frac{\Phi}{c^2}\Bigr)dt\Bigr) :
> $$
>
>    clocks lower down, at more negative $\Phi$, run slow;
> 2. two static clocks at $\Phi_A$ and $\Phi_B$ run at the relative rate $d\tau_A/d\tau_B = 1 + \Phi_A - \Phi_B$. This is [[#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]] read as a statement about clocks instead of light: every process at $A$ (atomic oscillations, heartbeats, decays) runs at this rate as compared from $B$;
> 3. a clock moving slowly, with speed $v \ll 1$ relative to the static clocks at its position, measures
>
> $$
> d\tau = \Bigl(1 + \Phi - \tfrac12v^2\Bigr)dt \qquad\Bigl(\text{restored } d\tau = \Bigl(1 + \frac{\Phi}{c^2} - \frac{v^2}{2c^2}\Bigr)dt\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
>    to first order in $\Phi$ and $v^2$: gravitational rate and time dilation add.
>
> Part 1 is the input from which [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-1|Theorem §C1.5.1]] builds $g_{00}$; part 3 is rederived from the weak-field metric in [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]].
>
> *Model:* static weak field ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]), ideal clocks ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^mod-a2-5-1|Model §A2.5.1]]); *applies:* first order in $\Phi$ (and in $v^2$ for part 3), with $\Phi = 0$ at the reference clock; another reference level adds a constant to $\Phi$ and rescales $t$.
>
> *Source: Hartle §6.5, p. 125 (PDF 126) ("clocks run at a rate … $(1 + \Phi/c^2)$"); Box 6.2, p. 130 (PDF 131) · 't Hooft ch. 3, eqs. (3.13), (3.15), pp. 12–13 (PDF 13–14) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2.3, eq. (6.82) (preview) · Schutz §5.1, Fig. 5.2*

^thm-c1-4-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1 (successive crests take equal coordinate times).** In a static field nothing changes under $t \mapsto t + T$. A crest leaving a static clock $A$ at coordinate time $t_1$ arrives at a static clock $B$ at $t_1 + T_{AB}$. The next crest, leaving at $t_1 + \Delta t_A$, travels along the first crest's path shifted by $\Delta t_A$ in $t$, whatever gravity does to light, so it arrives at $t_1 + \Delta t_A + T_{AB}$. Hence
>
> $$
> \Delta t_B = \Delta t_A \equiv \Delta t .
> $$
>
> **Step 2 (rate of a static clock).** By the equivalence principle every static clock is locally a standard clock, so its rate relative to $t$ depends only on where it is: $\Delta\tau = f(\vec x)\,\Delta t$ with $f(\vec x_0) = 1$ at the reference clock. The two clocks' intervals between the same two crests are $\Delta\tau_A = f(\vec x_A)\Delta t$ and $\Delta\tau_B = f(\vec x_B)\Delta t$, and the measured frequencies are their reciprocals. [[#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]] gives
>
> $$
> \frac{f(\vec x_B)}{f(\vec x_A)} = \frac{\Delta\tau_B}{\Delta\tau_A} = \frac{\nu_A}{\nu_B} = \frac{1}{1 - (\Phi_B - \Phi_A)} = 1 + \Phi_B - \Phi_A + O(\Phi^2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3 (part 1).** Take $A$ to be the reference clock: $f(\vec x_A) = 1$, $\Phi_A = 0$. Then $f(\vec x_B) = 1 + \Phi_B$ for every static point $B$: $d\tau = (1 + \Phi)\,dt$. Check of consistency for two arbitrary clocks: $(1 + \Phi_B)/(1 + \Phi_A) = 1 + \Phi_B - \Phi_A + O(\Phi^2)$, as Step 2 requires.
>
> **Step 4 (part 2).** $d\tau_A/d\tau_B = (1 + \Phi_A)/(1 + \Phi_B) = 1 + \Phi_A - \Phi_B + O(\Phi^2)$. Since Steps 1–2 never used what the periodic signal was, the same ratio holds for any process at $A$ watched from $B$.
>
> **Step 5 (part 3).** At the moving clock's position, take the freely falling frame momentarily at rest relative to the static clocks there. By [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]] it is a local inertial frame, in which a static clock and the moving clock are two clocks of special relativity with relative speed $v$. Time dilation ([[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^thm-a2-1-2|Theorem §A2.1.2]]) and the ideal-clock assumption (the rate depends only on the instantaneous speed) give $d\tau = \sqrt{1 - v^2}\,d\tau_{\text{static}}$. With part 1,
>
> $$
> d\tau = \sqrt{1 - v^2}\,(1 + \Phi)\,dt = \bigl(1 - \tfrac12v^2 + O(v^4)\bigr)(1 + \Phi)\,dt = \bigl(1 + \Phi - \tfrac12v^2\bigr)dt + O(\Phi v^2, v^4)\,dt .
> $$
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The coordinate time $t$ is a label built with the help of stationarity; it is the reading of only one clock. Static clocks elsewhere tick at $1 + \Phi$ times its rate.
> - The redshift and the clock-rate law are the same fact: Step 1 makes the coordinate intervals equal at both ends, so a frequency ratio can only come from the clocks.

## No global inertial frame

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.7: No Global Inertial Frame at Rest on Earth (Schild's Argument)
> 1. **The static frame is not inertial.** The frame at rest on the Earth, in which the redshift of [[#^law-c1-4-5|Law §C1.4.5]] is observed, is not an inertial frame of special relativity. If it were, the clocks at rest at the bottom and the top of the tower would both measure its time $t$; successive crests climbing the tower follow congruent paths, so $\Delta t_{\text{top}} = \Delta t_{\text{bottom}}$, hence $\Delta\tau_{\text{top}} = \Delta\tau_{\text{bottom}}$ and there would be no shift.
> 2. **The freely falling frame is.** In the frame that falls freely from rest at the moment of emission, the light reaches the top with the frequency it was emitted with, to first order in $gh$. The shift measured at the top is entirely the Doppler shift of the top receiver, which moves at $gh$ relative to that frame. For this experiment the freely falling frame behaves as an inertial frame ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]]).
> 3. **No frame covers the region.** Around a body whose field is not uniform, freely falling frames at different places accelerate relative to each other ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]]). So no single freely falling frame is inertial over a region in which the tidal acceleration can be detected, and by part 1 no static frame is inertial either. Near a mass, inertial frames exist only locally.
>
> Part 1 alone does not exclude flat spacetime. In the rocket of [[#^mod-c1-4-1|Model §C1.4.1]] the rocket's own frame shows the same redshift ([[#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]]), and there spacetime is flat: one inertial frame covers everything, and the rocket frame is simply not it. What a gravitating body adds is part 3. That spacetime near a mass is not Minkowski spacetime in any coordinates is made precise by curvature: the Riemann tensor, [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor|§C3.9]], and geodesic deviation, [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation|§C3.12]].
>
> *Model:* static weak field: a uniform field over the tower for parts 1–2, the field of a body ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]) for part 3; *applies:* part 2 to first order in $gh$; parts 1 and 3 hold for any measured redshift and any non-uniform field.
>
> *Source: Schutz §5.1, "Nonexistence of a Lorentz frame at rest on Earth" (after Schild 1967), Fig. 5.2, eq. (5.2), Fig. 5.3, pp. 113–116 (PDF 131–134) · Hartle §6.5, pp. 125–126 (PDF 126–127) · Couzens, GR1, §2.2.1 (tidal forces tell a field from an accelerated frame), p. 25 (PDF 26)*

^thm-c1-4-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Part 1.**
> *Step 1 (the hypothesis).* Suppose the frame at rest on the Earth were inertial, with time $t$ and the Minkowski interval $ds^2 = -dt^2 + dz^2$ in the vertical plane ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]). A clock at rest ($dz = 0$) then measures $d\tau^2 = -ds^2 = dt^2$, i.e. $d\tau = dt$ ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^def-b1-3-2|Def. §B1.3.2]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where $d\tau^2 = ds^2/c^2$), at the bottom and at the top alike.
>
> *Step 2 (congruent crests).* Draw two successive crests of the light on a spacetime diagram of this frame. Gravity may bend their paths in any way, but the field does not change in time, so the second path is the first shifted by $\Delta t_{\text{bottom}}$ along $t$. Shifted paths start and end $\Delta t_{\text{bottom}}$ apart, so $\Delta t_{\text{top}} = \Delta t_{\text{bottom}}$ (the argument of Step 1 of [[#^der-c1-4-6|the Derivation of Theorem §C1.4.6]]).
>
> *Step 3 (no shift).* By Step 1, $\Delta\tau_{\text{top}} = \Delta t_{\text{top}} = \Delta t_{\text{bottom}} = \Delta\tau_{\text{bottom}}$, so $\nu_{\text{top}} = 1/\Delta\tau_{\text{top}} = \nu_{\text{bottom}}$.
>
> *Step 4 (contradiction).* [[#^law-c1-4-5|Law §C1.4.5]] measures $\nu_{\text{top}}/\nu_{\text{bottom}} = 1 - gh \neq 1$, i.e. $\Delta\tau_{\text{top}} > \Delta\tau_{\text{bottom}}$. Step 2 used only the time independence of the field, so the hypothesis of Step 1 is what fails.
>
> **Part 2.** Let $F$ fall freely from rest at the moment of emission. At emission the source is at rest in $F$, so in $F$ the light has the frequency $\nu_E$, and since $F$ is inertial for the experiment ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]) it keeps $\nu_E$ along its path. The light needs the time $t_1 = h + O(gh^2)$ to reach the top; meanwhile $F$ has fallen, so relative to $F$ the top moves upward, away from the light's origin, at $v = gt_1 = gh + O(g^2h^2)$. The receiver therefore measures
>
> $$
> \nu_{\text{top}} = \nu_E\sqrt{\frac{1 - v}{1 + v}} = \nu_E\,(1 - gh) + O(g^2h^2) ,
> $$
>
> the observed shift. Equivalently, the frequency the light has in $F$ when it arrives, compared with what the top measures, is $\nu_E/\nu_{\text{top}} = (1 + gh)/\sqrt{1 - g^2h^2} = 1 + gh + O(g^2h^2)$ (Schutz (5.2); expansion checked by script), so the frequency in $F$ at arrival equals the frequency emitted: no shift in the freely falling frame.
>
> **Part 3.** A frame in which every free particle of a region moves uniformly must fall freely at every point of the region with the local free-fall acceleration. Two free particles a small distance $\vec\xi$ apart accelerate relative to each other by $\ddot\xi^i = -(\partial_i\partial_j\Phi)\,\xi^j$ ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]]), which is nonzero wherever the field is not uniform (for the Earth, $+2M\xi/r^3$ radially, $-M\xi/r^3$ transversally). No single frame moves with both; frames falling freely on opposite sides of the Earth fall toward each other (Schutz Fig. 5.3). Over a region and a time in which this relative motion is below the precision of the experiment, the freely falling frame is inertial; beyond it, no frame is.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The redshift is evidence about frames, not about light: Steps 1–4 hold for any time-independent effect of gravity on the light.
> - The equivalence principle survives in its local form only. Gravity can be removed at a point (part 2) but not over a region (part 3).
> - The question left for [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric|§C1.5]]: if static clocks do not measure a Minkowski time, which interval do they measure? [[#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]] says $d\tau = (1 + \Phi)\,dt$, and a metric that contains this is the next step.

> [!remark] Remark: Two readings of the clock-rate law
> Hartle (§6.5) asks how the different rates of static clocks should be explained, and contrasts two answers. In the first, spacetime stays flat, with an inertial time $t$, and gravity is a field that slows every clock by the same factor $1 + \Phi$. Since every clock, ruler and process is affected in exactly the same way, the flat geometry underneath can never be measured. Hartle compares this with a flat-Earth theory in which rulers lengthen toward the poles so that Paris–Montreal measures shorter than Lagos–Bogotá: consistent, but the "true" flat distances are unobservable by construction. In the second, clocks measure the geometry correctly, and the geometry is not flat. General relativity takes the second reading, as simpler and more economical. Part 3 of Theorem §C1.4.7 adds a reason that is not about economy: the inertial frames that a flat background would supply do not exist near a mass.
>

## Clocks in orbit and in aircraft

> [!law] Law §C1.4.8: Clock Rates in the Global Positioning System
> The Global Positioning System uses 24 satellites in 12-hour orbits in six planes; each carries atomic clocks that keep their proper time to a few parts in $10^{13}$, and broadcasts the time and place of emission. A receiver finds its position and time from the arrival times of signals from four satellites, multiplying time differences by $c$ (a nanosecond is $30\ \mathrm{cm}$; $2\ \mathrm m$ accuracy needs about $6\ \mathrm{ns}$). For a circular equatorial orbit with half a sidereal day as period, $a = 26\,562\ \mathrm{km} = 4.16\,R_\oplus$ and $v = 3.87\ \mathrm{km/s}$ ([[#^ex-c1-4-3|Example §C1.4.3]]), and [[#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]], parts 2–3, predicts that a satellite clock, compared with a clock on the ground, runs
>
> | effect | fractional rate | per day |
> |---|---|---|
> | gravitational, $\frac{GM_\oplus}{c^2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{R_\oplus} - \frac1a\Bigr)$ | $+5.28\times10^{-10}$ | $+45.7\ \mu\mathrm s$ |
> | time dilation, $-\frac{v^2}{2c^2}$ | $-0.83\times10^{-10}$ | $-7.2\ \mu\mathrm s$ |
> | net | $+4.45\times10^{-10}$ | $+38.4\ \mu\mathrm s$ ($11.5\ \mathrm{km}$ of light travel) |
>
> The system works only because these rates are taken into account: the rates of the satellite clocks are corrected downward to keep the time of the ground-based frame (Hartle). Uncorrected, the net rate would pass the $6\ \mathrm{ns}$ budget in about $13\ \mathrm s$ (Hartle: "less than a minute"). The successful operation of the GPS is itself an accurate check of the redshift (Schutz, citing Ashby 2003).
>
> *Valid when:* the numbers are the leading $1/c^2$ terms for the idealized orbit; the real system adds smaller corrections (Hartle lists the ground clocks' rotation with the Earth, here $0.10\ \mu\mathrm s$ per day, the Earth's oblateness, the relativity of simultaneity, the ionosphere's refractive index, clock errors). Both terms are confirmed in daily operation; the sources give no separate precision figure.
>

> [!caution] Caution: Which clock the GPS numbers are relative to
> - **Hartle's numbers refer to a distant observer.** Hartle's (6.18)–(6.19) give the corrections that make a satellite clock keep the time of an inertial observer far from the Earth: $\frac12(v/c)^2 \approx 0.84\times10^{-10}$ for time dilation and $GM_\oplus/(R_sc^2) \approx 1.6\times10^{-10}$ for the potential, both making the satellite clock slow, $2.5\times10^{-10}$ in total. Relative to a clock on the ground, which is itself deeper in the potential, the gravitational term is $\frac{GM_\oplus}{c^2}\bigl(\frac{1}{R_\oplus} - \frac1a\bigr) = 6.95\times10^{-10} - 1.67\times10^{-10} = 5.28\times10^{-10}$, and it makes the satellite clock *fast*, opposite to time dilation. Hartle says this himself on the next page (the actual system uses a frame rotating with the Earth, whose time is kept by surface clocks); his cross-reference there points to "Problem 14", whereas the problem that treats the satellite-to-ground rate is Problem 6.9. Not an error, but the two sets of numbers must not be mixed.
> - **d'Inverno & Vickers' GPS paragraph is not used.** Its numbers are about a factor 100 too large: it writes "$\gamma \simeq 8\times10^{-9}$" for $\gamma - 1 \approx 8\times10^{-11}$, and $GM_e/(c^2R_S) \simeq 16\times10^{-9}$ for $1.6\times10^{-10}$; it calls the orbital speed "radial"; and it states that both effects make the signals arrive at a slower rate, although for a receiver on the ground the gravitational effect makes them arrive faster.
>
> *Source: Hartle §6.4, pp. 124–125 (PDF 125–126) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.2, p. 293 (PDF 308) · numbers checked by script*

> [!law] Law §C1.4.9: The Hafele–Keating Experiment
> In 1971 J. C. Hafele and R. E. Keating carried caesium-beam atomic clocks around the world on scheduled commercial flights, once eastward and once westward, and compared them on return with a reference clock at rest on the Earth's surface. The flying clocks sit higher in the potential, which makes them gain, and move relative to the ground, which makes them gain or lose depending on the direction. Predictions (from the flight logs of altitude and ground speed) and observations, positive meaning the flying clock gained:
>
> | flight | flying time | predicted | observed |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | eastward | $41\ \mathrm h$ | $-40 \pm 23\ \mathrm{ns}$ | $-59 \pm 10\ \mathrm{ns}$ |
> | westward | $49\ \mathrm h$ | $+275 \pm 21\ \mathrm{ns}$ | $+273 \pm 7\ \mathrm{ns}$ |
>
> The prediction is [[#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]], part 3, applied in the inertial frame of the Earth's centre to both clocks. For flights along the equator at altitude $h(t)$ and ground speed $V_g(t)$ (positive eastward), with the surface speed $V_\oplus = 2\pi R_\oplus/(24\ \mathrm h) = 464\ \mathrm{m/s}$, the flying clock moves at $V_\oplus + V_g$ and the ground clock at $V_\oplus$, and
>
> $$
> \Delta\tau_{\text{flying}} - \Delta\tau_{\text{ground}} = \int dt\,\Bigl[gh - \tfrac12\bigl((V_\oplus + V_g)^2 - V_\oplus^2\bigr)\Bigr] = \int dt\,\Bigl[gh - \tfrac12V_g\,(V_g + 2V_\oplus)\Bigr]
> $$
>
> (restored: divide the bracket by $c^2$). Eastward, $V_g > 0$, the motional term $-\frac12V_g(V_g + 2V_\oplus)$ is negative and outweighs the altitude term $gh$; westward, $-2V_\oplus < V_g < 0$, the motional term is positive and both terms make the flying clock gain.
>
> *Valid when:* 1971, timing accuracies of a few parts in $10^{13}$ over the flights; both observations agree with the first-order prediction within the stated errors. It tests the gravitational rate and time dilation together, with real clocks carried along different worldlines between the same two events: a twin experiment in the Earth's field.
>
> *Source: Hartle Box 6.2, p. 130 (PDF 131); Prob. 6.15, p. 134 (PDF 135)*

> [!example] Example §C1.4.1: How Much Faster the Top Floor Ages
> *Question.* Theorists work on the top floor of a physics building, $h = 30\ \mathrm m$ above the experimentalists on the ground floor (Hartle's example). By what factor do their clocks, hearts included, run fast, and how much older are they after a year, and after a century? And for $h = 100\ \mathrm m$?
>
> *Setup.* Two static clocks in the Earth's field, which is uniform over $100\ \mathrm m$ to $3\times10^{-5}$ ($2h/R_\oplus$, [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^mod-a8-1-5|CM Model §A8.1.5]]), so $\Phi_{\text{top}} - \Phi_{\text{ground}} = gh$. [[#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]], part 2, restored:
>
> $$
> \frac{d\tau_{\text{top}}}{d\tau_{\text{ground}}} = 1 + \frac{\Phi_{\text{top}} - \Phi_{\text{ground}}}{c^2} = 1 + \frac{gh}{c^2} .
> $$
>
> *30 m.* With $g = 9.807\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $c^2 = 8.988\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}$,
>
> $$
> \frac{gh}{c^2} = \frac{9.807\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\times30\ \mathrm m}{8.988\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}} = \frac{294.2}{8.988\times10^{16}} = 3.27\times10^{-15} .
> $$
>
> In one year, $3.156\times10^7\ \mathrm s$ on the ground clock, the top-floor clock gains $3.27\times10^{-15}\times3.156\times10^7\ \mathrm s = 1.03\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm s = 103\ \mathrm{ns}$; in a century, $10.3\ \mu\mathrm s$.
>
> *100 m.* The shift is proportional to $h$: $gh/c^2 = 1.09\times10^{-14}$, $344\ \mathrm{ns}$ per year.
>
> *Comparison.* The Pound–Rebka tower, $22.5\ \mathrm m$, gives $2.46\times10^{-15}$ ([[#^law-c1-4-5|Law §C1.4.5]]): the 30 m office building differs from it only by the ratio of heights, $30/22.5$. Hartle's text describes $3.27\times10^{-15}$ as "a few parts in $10^{14}$" and the century's gain as "a few microseconds"; the numbers are $3.3$ parts in $10^{15}$ and $10\ \mu\mathrm s$ (checked by script).
>
> *Source: Hartle Ex. 6.1, eq. (6.13), pp. 118–119 (PDF 119–120) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C1.4.2: Redshift of Light from the Sun's Surface and from a White Dwarf
> *Question.* Light leaves the surface of a star of mass $M$ and radius $R$ and is received far away (or at the Earth). By what fraction is its frequency lowered, by how much is the H$\alpha$ line ($6563\ \text{Å}$) shifted, and what Doppler speed would mimic the shift?
>
> *Setup.* Emitter static on the surface, receiver static far away: [[#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]], part 2, restored,
>
> $$
> z \equiv \frac{\lambda_R - \lambda_E}{\lambda_E} = \frac{\nu_E - \nu_R}{\nu_R} = \frac{GM}{Rc^2} + O\Bigl(\frac{GM}{Rc^2}\Bigr)^2, \qquad \Delta\lambda = z\,\lambda_E, \qquad v_{\text{mimic}} = zc ,
> $$
>
> with the redshift $z$ of [[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^def-a2-4-1|Def. §A2.4.1]] and $v_{\text{mimic}}$ the recession speed with the same first-order Doppler shift. The first-order formula is enough because $GM/Rc^2$ is the compactness of [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^def-c1-1-1|Def. §C1.1.1]], small for both stars.
>
> *The Sun.* $GM_\odot/c^2 = 1476.7\ \mathrm m$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-1|Def. §C1.2.1]]), $R_\odot = 6.957\times10^8\ \mathrm m$:
>
> $$
> z_\odot = \frac{1476.7\ \mathrm m}{6.957\times10^8\ \mathrm m} = 2.12\times10^{-6}, \qquad \Delta\lambda = 2.12\times10^{-6}\times6563\ \text{Å} = 0.0139\ \text{Å}, \qquad v_{\text{mimic}} = 0.64\ \mathrm{km/s} .
> $$
>
> A receiver at the Earth sits at $\Phi = -GM_\odot/(1\ \mathrm{AU}) = -9.87\times10^{-9}c^2$, not at $0$; with this term the shift is $(2.123 - 0.010)\times10^{-6} = 2.113\times10^{-6}$ (d'Inverno & Vickers' (16.11) quotes $2.12\times10^{-6}$ for this case). The convective motions of the solar surface produce Doppler shifts of the same size, which makes the measurement hard (d'Inverno & Vickers).
>
> *A white dwarf* of $0.6\,M_\odot$ and radius $7000\ \mathrm{km}$, about the size of the Earth (Schutz):
>
> $$
> z = \frac{0.6\times1476.7\ \mathrm m}{7.0\times10^6\ \mathrm m} = \frac{886.0\ \mathrm m}{7.0\times10^6\ \mathrm m} = 1.27\times10^{-4}, \qquad \Delta\lambda = 0.83\ \text{Å}, \qquad v_{\text{mimic}} = 38\ \mathrm{km/s} ,
> $$
>
> sixty times the solar value. For 40 Eridani B, with $\Phi = -6\times10^{-5}$ at the surface (Schutz Exercise 11.7(b)), $\Delta\lambda = 6\times10^{-5}\times6563\ \text{Å} = 0.39\ \text{Å}$ ($v_{\text{mimic}} = 18\ \mathrm{km/s}$). Hartle's estimate "$\sim10^{-3}$" for white dwarfs assumes $R \sim 10^3\ \mathrm{km}$; Earth-sized white dwarfs give $10^{-4}$.
>
> *What it shows.* The shift is a property of the light's start and end potentials, and it mimics a recession speed that must be separated from the star's real motion. That is why the laboratory test of [[#^law-c1-4-5|Law §C1.4.5]] mattered.
>
> *Source: Hartle Ex. 6.2, eq. (6.14), p. 119 (PDF 120) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.2, eq. (16.11), p. 292 (PDF 307) · Schutz §10.7 (white dwarfs about the size of the Earth), p. 281 (PDF 299); Exercise 11.7(b), p. 352 (PDF 370) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C1.4.3: The GPS Budget Step by Step
> *Question.* How fast does a GPS satellite clock run compared with a clock on the ground, and what does the ground receive (Hartle Prob. 6.9, PHY 360 HW 4)?
>
> *Setup.* Circular equatorial orbit of period $T = \frac12$ sidereal day $= 43\,082\ \mathrm s$; static clock on the ground, its rotation with the Earth neglected at first; inertial frame of the Earth's centre; the clock-rate law of [[#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]], part 3, for both clocks, restored. Constants: $GM_\oplus = 3.986\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}$, $GM_\oplus/c^2 = 4.435\ \mathrm{mm}$, $R_\oplus = 6378\ \mathrm{km}$.
>
> *Step 1 (orbit radius).* Kepler's third law ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-4|CM Theorem §A8.3.4]]; for a circle, [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-5|CM Theorem §A8.2.5]]):
>
> $$
> a = \Bigl(\frac{GM_\oplus T^2}{4\pi^2}\Bigr)^{1/3} = \Bigl(\frac{3.986\times10^{14}\times(43\,082)^2}{39.48}\Bigr)^{1/3}\ \mathrm m = 2.6562\times10^7\ \mathrm m = 4.165\,R_\oplus .
> $$
>
> *Step 2 (speed).* $v = \sqrt{GM_\oplus/a} = \sqrt{3.986\times10^{14}/2.6562\times10^7}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 3874\ \mathrm{m/s}$, $v/c = 1.29\times10^{-5}$.
>
> *Step 3 (gravitational term).* The satellite at $\Phi_a = -GM_\oplus/a$, the ground clock at $\Phi_\oplus = -GM_\oplus/R_\oplus$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\Phi_a - \Phi_\oplus}{c^2} = \frac{GM_\oplus}{c^2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{R_\oplus} - \frac1a\Bigr) = 4.435\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm m\times\Bigl(\frac{1}{6.378\times10^6\ \mathrm m} - \frac{1}{2.656\times10^7\ \mathrm m}\Bigr) = 6.954\times10^{-10} - 1.670\times10^{-10} = 5.284\times10^{-10} .
> $$
>
> *Step 4 (velocity term).* $-\dfrac{v^2}{2c^2} = -\dfrac{(1.292\times10^{-5})^2}{2} = -8.35\times10^{-11}$.
>
> *Step 5 (net rate, per day, as a distance).* Dividing the satellite clock's rate, $1 + \Phi_a/c^2 - v^2/2c^2$, by the ground clock's, $1 + \Phi_\oplus/c^2$, and keeping first-order terms:
>
> $$
> \frac{d\tau_{\text{sat}}}{d\tau_{\text{ground}}} - 1 = 5.284\times10^{-10} - 0.835\times10^{-10} = 4.449\times10^{-10} ;
> $$
>
> times $86\,400\ \mathrm s$: $+45.65\ \mu\mathrm s - 7.21\ \mu\mathrm s = +38.4\ \mu\mathrm s$ per day, which light crosses in $38.4\ \mu\mathrm s\times c = 11.5\ \mathrm{km}$.
>
> *Step 6 (what the ground receives).* A signal of proper frequency $\nu_{\text{sat}}$ arrives with the gravitational blueshift $1 + 5.284\times10^{-10}$ ([[#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]]) and, for propagation at $90°$ to the satellite's velocity in the receiver's frame (Hartle's assumption), only the transverse Doppler factor $1 - v^2/2c^2$ ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^law-a2-4-3|Law §A2.4.3]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_{\text{ground}}}{\nu_{\text{sat}}} = (1 + 5.284\times10^{-10})(1 - 0.835\times10^{-10}) = 1 + 4.45\times10^{-10} :
> $$
>
> the ground receives the satellite's signals faster than they were emitted, by the net rate of Step 5.
>
> *Step 7 (what was neglected, and the other reference).* The ground clock moves at $\omega R_\oplus = 465\ \mathrm{m/s}$, which slows it by $v^2/2c^2 = 1.2\times10^{-12}$, $0.10\ \mu\mathrm s$ per day: small against $38.4$. Relative to a distant observer instead of the ground, both terms slow the satellite clock: $1.670\times10^{-10} + 0.835\times10^{-10} = 2.50\times10^{-10}$, Hartle's (6.18)–(6.19) ([[#^cau-c1-4-1|Caution: Which clock the GPS numbers are relative to]]).
>
> *Source: Hartle §6.4, eqs. (6.16)–(6.19), p. 124 (PDF 125); Prob. 6.9, p. 133 (PDF 134) · PHY 360 (audit), HW 4 (Hartle 6.9; no solutions file) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C1.4.4: A Rocket Trip to the Galactic Centre at 1 g
> *Question* (PHY 360, HW 3, Problem 3). You leave the Earth in a rocket with constant proper acceleration $g = 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ and fly to the centre of the Galaxy, $D = 30\,000$ light years away. How much older are you on arrival? (Variant added here: how much if you decelerate at $g$ for the second half and arrive at rest?)
>
> *Setup.* The floor of the rocket of [[#^mod-c1-4-1|Model §C1.4.1]], i.e. hyperbolic motion from rest ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]], item 2, with $\alpha = g$, $c$ restored): $x = \frac{c^2}{g}\bigl(\cosh\frac{g\tau}{c} - 1\bigr)$, $ct = \frac{c^2}{g}\sinh\frac{g\tau}{c}$. By [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]], the crew feels the weight they had at home throughout.
>
> *Step 1 (solve for the proper time).* At $x = D$: $\cosh(g\tau/c) = 1 + gD/c^2$, so
>
> $$
> \tau = \frac cg\,\operatorname{arccosh}\Bigl(1 + \frac{gD}{c^2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Step 2 (numbers).* $D = 3.0\times10^4\times9.461\times10^{15}\ \mathrm m = 2.838\times10^{20}\ \mathrm m$; $c/g = 2.998\times10^8/9.8\ \mathrm s = 3.059\times10^7\ \mathrm s = 0.969\ \mathrm{yr}$; $gD/c^2 = 9.8\times2.838\times10^{20}/8.988\times10^{16} = 30\,948$. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{g\tau}{c} = \operatorname{arccosh}(30\,949) = \ln\bigl(30\,949 + \sqrt{30\,949^2 - 1}\bigr) = 11.033, \qquad \tau = 0.969\ \mathrm{yr}\times11.033 = 10.7\ \mathrm{yr}\ (3.375\times10^8\ \mathrm s) .
> $$
>
> On the Earth, $t = \frac cg\sinh(11.033) = 30\,001\ \mathrm{yr}$ pass: about one year ($c/g$) more than light needs. The top speed has $\gamma = \cosh(11.033) = 30\,949$. (The course solution writes $\operatorname{arccosh}(gD/c^2)$, without the $1$, and uses $c = 3\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$; it finds $3.38\times10^8\ \mathrm s \approx 11$ years. The dropped $1$ changes $g\tau/c$ by $3\times10^{-5}$.)
>
> *Step 3 (arriving at rest).* Accelerate over $D/2$ and decelerate over $D/2$; by symmetry each half takes the proper time of Step 1 with $D/2$:
>
> $$
> \tau = 2\,\frac cg\,\operatorname{arccosh}\Bigl(1 + \frac{gD}{2c^2}\Bigr) = 2\times0.969\ \mathrm{yr}\times\operatorname{arccosh}(15\,475) = 2\times0.969\ \mathrm{yr}\times10.340 = 20.0\ \mathrm{yr} .
> $$
>
> *Step 4 (a clock in the nose).* A clock $h = 100\ \mathrm m$ above the floor runs fast by $1 + gh/c^2$ ([[#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]], part 2): over the $10.7$-year trip it gains $1.09\times10^{-14}\times3.375\times10^8\ \mathrm s = 3.7\ \mu\mathrm s$, the same rate as the top of a 100 m tower on the Earth ([[#^ex-c1-4-1|Example §C1.4.1]]), as the equivalence principle requires.
>
> [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^ex-b3-3-2|Example §B3.3.2]] (level B) makes the same trip with $D = 2\times10^{20}\ \mathrm m$ and finds $10.3$ and $19.4$ years; the times grow only logarithmically with the distance.
>
> *Source: PHY 360 (audit), HW 3, Problem 3, and its typed solution (Hartle Prob. 5.6 for the hyperbolic worldline) · Hartle Prob. 6.6(c) for Step 4 · numbers checked by script*
