[[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames|§C1.4]] ended with two facts about clocks at rest in a gravitational field. A static clock at potential $\Phi$ ticks at the rate $d\tau = (1 + \Phi)\,dt$ relative to a common coordinate time $t$ ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]]), and no single inertial frame can cover a tower on the Earth: the geometry of spacetime in a gravitational field is not that of Minkowski ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-7|Theorem §C1.4.7]]). This section asks the next two questions. *Which geometry encodes these clock rates?* And *does the law of free motion of special relativity still describe falling bodies once proper time is measured with that geometry?* In flat spacetime that law is geometric: between two events the worldline of a free particle has the longest proper time ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-5|Theorem §B1.3.5]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), equivalently it makes the action $S = -m\int d\tau$ stationary ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]).

First, what fails if the geometry stays flat. Ask a ball thrown upward to make its proper time stationary in Minkowski spacetime. With $t$ as parameter and $\vec v = d\vec x/dt$, $d\tau/dt = \sqrt{1 - v^2}$ ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]], part 3, with $c = 1$), and the Euler–Lagrange equations ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|CM Theorem §B5.1.4]]) for $F = \sqrt{1 - v^2}$ read

$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial x^i} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial F}{\partial v^i} = -\frac{v^i}{\sqrt{1 - v^2}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\,\frac{v^i}{\sqrt{1 - v^2}} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \vec v = \text{const} .
$$

The extremal worldlines of the flat metric are straight. The ball's worldline in the coordinates of the ground (inertial ones, if spacetime were flat and gravity were not part of its geometry), $z = v_0t - \tfrac12 gt^2$, is not. In flat spacetime gravity would have to be added as a force from a field, which is the route that [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced|§C1.1]] found inconsistent with special relativity ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-4|Theorem §C1.1.4]]). One could still keep a flat geometry and declare that a universal field slows every clock by the factor $1 + \Phi$. Hartle compares this with a flat-earth theory in which a field lengthens every ruler at high latitudes: consistent, but the underlying flat geometry can never be measured, since every instrument is affected alike. It is simpler to take the clocks at their word. They measure the geometry, and that geometry is not flat (Hartle §6.5).

The section builds the answer in order. It first defines a metric that varies from event to event and the proper time it assigns to a worldline ([[#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]], [[#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]]). The clock-rate law then fixes the time–time component, $g_{00} = -(1 + 2\Phi)$ ([[#^thm-c1-5-1|Theorem §C1.5.1]]). Adding the spatial part that general relativity predicts gives the static weak-field metric ([[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]). With it the free-fall law of special relativity is carried over as a principle ([[#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]), and for slow bodies it reproduces Newton's equation of motion $\ddot{\vec x} = -\nabla\Phi$, with the Newtonian action appearing as the first correction to proper time ([[#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]]). Two consequences follow: a conserved energy that contains the Newtonian potential energy ([[#^thm-c1-5-5|Theorem §C1.5.5]]), and a coordinate speed of light below $1$ near masses ([[#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]]). So the answer to the second question is yes: gravity can be described as the geometry of spacetime, with free bodies moving on worldlines of extremal proper time. That is the core idea of general relativity. What the argument takes for granted is listed at the end ([[#^rem-c1-5-1|Remark: What has been assumed]]); chapters C2–C5 supply it.

**Setup.** A static, weak gravitational field. Static means that the source does not change in time and that coordinates $(t, x^i)$ exist in which the metric components do not depend on $t$ and $g_{0i} = 0$ (the formal definition is [[§C3.11 Isometries and Killing Vectors|§C3.11]]). Weak means that the Newtonian potential $\Phi$ of the source ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]), normalized by $\Phi \to 0$ at infinity, satisfies $|\Phi| \ll 1$ wherever the metric is used. The bodies studied are test bodies (their own field is neglected) and, except for light, slow: $v^2 \ll 1$. For bound motion the two small numbers are of the same size, $v^2 \sim |\Phi|$ ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]]); both are counted as order $\varepsilon$, and every expansion below is to first order in $\varepsilon$, the dropped terms displayed. The theorems are exact consequences of the model and the principle at that order. The metric itself, beyond $g_{00}$, is an input taken from general relativity and justified in §C5.5 (planned). The numbers in the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$ in every formula, with the restored form beside it (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here $\Phi \to \Phi/c^2$, $t \to ct$, $v \to v/c$, $M \to GM/c^2$); $\Phi < 0$ near masses; Greek indices $0$–$3$, Latin $1$–$3$, $x^0 = t$; $\vec v = d\vec x/dt$, $v^2 = \delta_{ij}v^iv^j$; a dot is $d/d\lambda$ for a general parameter $\lambda$. Level B and its links use $+{-}{-}{-}$ with $c$ kept; proper time is the same positive number in both conventions ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]).

## A metric for the clocks

In special relativity one bilinear form $\eta$ serves every event. The clocks of §C1.4 tick at different rates at different places, so the form that turns coordinate displacements into proper times must depend on the event. The first two boxes say what such an object is; the formal home, on a manifold, is [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor|§C3.1]].

> [!definition] Definition §C1.5.1: Spacetime Metric in a Coordinate Patch
> *Structure.* At each event $p$, the **metric** $g_p$ is a symmetric, nondegenerate bilinear form ([[§35 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-9|LADR Def. 9.9]]) of signature $(-, +, +, +)$ on the four-dimensional space of infinitesimal displacements at $p$, the tangent space at $p$ ([[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^def-28-2|591 Def. §28.2]]; physics form in [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]). It is the same kind of object as the Minkowski metric $\eta$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]), but it may differ from event to event.
>
> *Components.* In coordinates $x^\mu$ covering a region of spacetime the metric is ten functions $g_{\mu\nu}(x) = g_{\nu\mu}(x)$, and the squared interval between the events $x$ and $x + dx$ is the **line element**
>
> $$
> ds^2 = g_{\mu\nu}(x)\,dx^\mu dx^\nu .
> $$
>
> A displacement is timelike, null or spacelike as $ds^2 < 0$, $= 0$ or $> 0$, as in [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]. Minkowski spacetime in inertial coordinates is the case $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ at every event.
>
> *On a manifold.* $g_p$ is a tensor of type $(0, 2)$ at $p$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]], case 5), and requiring $g_{\mu\nu} = g_{\nu\mu}$ loses nothing: a line element determines only the symmetric part of its coefficients ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]], part 2).
>
> *Source: Hartle §6.6, eq. (6.20) and Table 6.1 · Couzens, GR1, §4.1 (the metric on a manifold) · Schutz §7.2, eq. (7.8)*

^def-c1-5-1

> [!definition] Definition §C1.5.2: Proper Time along a Timelike Worldline
> Let $\lambda \mapsto x^\mu(\lambda)$, $\lambda \in [\lambda_A, \lambda_B]$, be a worldline whose tangent $\dot x^\mu = dx^\mu/d\lambda$ is timelike at every point, $g_{\mu\nu}(x)\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu < 0$, and future-pointing (here: $t$ increases along it; time orientation in general is [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames|§C3.2]]). Its **proper time** from $A$ to $B$ is
>
> $$
> \tau_{AB}[x] = \int_{\lambda_A}^{\lambda_B}\sqrt{-g_{\mu\nu}\bigl(x(\lambda)\bigr)\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}\;d\lambda, \qquad\text{briefly}\qquad d\tau^2 = -ds^2 .
> $$
>
> It does not depend on the parametrization, by the change-of-variables argument of [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]], part 1, which uses nothing about the metric. An ideal clock carried along the worldline reads $\tau$ ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^mod-a2-5-1|Model §A2.5.1]]; [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^rem-b1-3-3|§B1.3, Remark: Geometry, and the clock hypothesis]]). For $g = \eta$ this is the proper time of special relativity ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^def-b1-3-2|Def. §B1.3.2]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where $c\,d\tau = \sqrt{ds^2}$ with no minus sign, because $ds^2_{\mathrm B} = -ds^2$ here).
>
> In the language of maps, $\dot x$ is the push-forward of $d/d\lambda$ by the worldline $\gamma : \lambda \mapsto x(\lambda)$, and the integrand is $\sqrt{-\gamma^{\ast}g}$, the square root of minus the metric pulled back to the parameter interval ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^ex-c2-4-1|Example §C2.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Hartle §6.6, eq. (6.24) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2.3, eq. (6.83) · d'Inverno & Vickers §10.6 (definition of $d\tau$ before (10.43), signature $+{-}{-}{-}$)*

^def-c1-5-2

The clock-rate law now determines one component of the metric. The proper time of a clock at rest is read off the metric, and the law says what it must be.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.5.1: The Clock-Rate Law Fixes g₀₀
> Let the metric be static in coordinates $(t, x^i)$: $g_{\mu\nu}$ independent of $t$ and $g_{0i} = 0$. Then:
> 1. a clock at rest at the position $\vec x$ ticks at the rate
>
> $$
> \frac{d\tau}{dt} = \sqrt{-g_{00}(\vec x)}\,;
> $$
>
> 2. if static clocks obey the clock-rate law $d\tau = \bigl(1 + \Phi(\vec x)\bigr)dt$ ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]]) with $\Phi \to 0$ at infinity, then
>
> $$
> g_{00} = -(1 + 2\Phi) + O(\Phi^2), \qquad\text{restored:}\qquad g_{00} = -\Bigl(1 + \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\Bigr),
> $$
>
> and $t$ is the proper time of static clocks far from the source.
>
> The clock rates fix nothing else: $g_{ij}$ and the terms of order $\Phi^2$ in $g_{00}$ are left open.
>
> *Model:* static metric ([[#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]]), static clocks reading proper time ([[#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]]); *applies:* part 1 exactly for any static metric; part 2 to first order in $\Phi$, the order to which the clock-rate law holds.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.1, eq. (6.3); §6.2.3, eqs. (6.83)–(6.84) · Hartle §6.6, eqs. (6.21)–(6.23) · d'Inverno & Vickers §10.6, eq. (10.49) (signature $+{-}{-}{-}$: $g_{00} = 1 + 2\phi/c^2$ there)*

^thm-c1-5-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the static clock's worldline). A clock at rest at $\vec x_0$ has the worldline $x^\mu(t) = (t, \vec x_0)$; take $\lambda = t$. Its tangent is
>
> $$
> \dot x^\mu = \frac{dx^\mu}{dt} = (1, 0, 0, 0), \qquad g_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu = g_{00}(\vec x_0)\cdot 1\cdot 1 = g_{00}(\vec x_0) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (its proper time). By [[#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]],
>
> $$
> \tau = \int_{t_A}^{t_B}\sqrt{-g_{00}(\vec x_0)}\;dt = \sqrt{-g_{00}(\vec x_0)}\;(t_B - t_A) ,
> $$
>
> since the integrand does not depend on $t$ (the metric is static). So $d\tau/dt = \sqrt{-g_{00}(\vec x_0)}$: part 1. The worldline is timelike exactly where $g_{00} < 0$.
>
> **Step 3** (equate with the clock-rate law). [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]] gives $d\tau/dt = 1 + \Phi + O(\Phi^2)$, so
>
> $$
> \sqrt{-g_{00}} = 1 + \Phi + O(\Phi^2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (square). Both sides are positive; squaring,
>
> $$
> -g_{00} = (1 + \Phi)^2 + O(\Phi^2) = 1 + 2\Phi + \Phi^2 + O(\Phi^2) = 1 + 2\Phi + O(\Phi^2) ,
> $$
>
> where $\Phi^2$ is absorbed into the error term, because the clock-rate law itself is known only to first order. Hence $g_{00} = -(1 + 2\Phi) + O(\Phi^2)$.
>
> **Step 5** (the normalization of $t$). Where $\Phi \to 0$, $g_{00} \to -1$ and Step 2 gives $d\tau = dt$: the coordinate $t$ is the proper time of static clocks far from the source, the reference time of [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]].
>
> **Step 6** (restore units). By [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]], $\Phi \to \Phi/c^2$ and $t \to ct$: $c\,d\tau = \sqrt{1 + 2\Phi/c^2}\;c\,dt$, so the component multiplying $(c\,dt)^2$ is $g_{00} = -(1 + 2\Phi/c^2)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Only the worldline $(t, \vec x_0)$ entered, and its tangent has a time component only. A static clock therefore samples $g_{00}$ at its own position and nothing else. Clocks alone fix the time–time part of the geometry to first order. The spatial part needs another input ([[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]) and matters for light ([[#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]]).

^cau-c1-5-1

Theorem §C1.5.1 leaves the spatial components open. General relativity fills them in. For a static weak source its field equations give a spatial factor $1 - 2\Phi$ (§C5.5, planned). The resulting metric is the model for the rest of this section and the first curved spacetime in these notes.

> [!model] Model §C1.5.2: The Static Weak-Field Metric
> *Structure.* A metric ([[#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]]) on $\mathbf R \times U$, $U \subset \mathbf R^3$ the region outside or around a static source, built from the source's Newtonian potential $\Phi(\vec x)$ ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]):
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi)\,dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)\,\delta_{ij}\,dx^idx^j, \qquad g_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(-(1 + 2\Phi),\ 1 - 2\Phi,\ 1 - 2\Phi,\ 1 - 2\Phi\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Restored:
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -\Bigl(1 + \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\Bigr)c^2dt^2 + \Bigl(1 - \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\Bigr)(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2) .
> $$
>
> *Content.* The time–time part is forced by the clocks ([[#^thm-c1-5-1|Theorem §C1.5.1]]). The spatial part is taken from general relativity and justified in §C5.5 (planned). For a point mass outside the body, $\Phi = -M/r$. **For slow bodies only $g_{00}$ matters.** Along a worldline with velocity $\vec v$,
>
> $$
> -g_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{dt}\frac{dx^\nu}{dt} = (1 + 2\Phi) - (1 - 2\Phi)v^2 = 1 \;\underbrace{+\,2\Phi}_{g_{00},\ O(\varepsilon)}\;\underbrace{-\,v^2}_{\delta_{ij},\ O(\varepsilon)}\;\underbrace{+\,2\Phi v^2}_{g_{ij} - \delta_{ij},\ O(\varepsilon^2)} ,
> $$
>
> so the deviation of $g_{ij}$ from $\delta_{ij}$ enters only at second order. For light $v^2 = 1$, and the same term $2\Phi v^2 = 2\Phi$ is of first order: the spatial part matters for light.
>
> *Assumptions:* static source; $|\Phi| \ll 1$, $\Phi \to 0$ at infinity; the metric is correct to first order in $\Phi$ only (terms of order $\Phi^2$ are not included); the coordinates are the static, isotropic ones in which the metric takes this form ([[#^cau-c1-5-2|Caution: Which parts of the metric are coordinate statements]]); test bodies do not change $\Phi$.
>
> *Source: Hartle §6.6, eq. (6.20) ("static weak field metric"); p. 131 (the spatial factor plays no role at leading order) · Schutz §7.2, eq. (7.8) · Couzens, GR1, §6.1, eqs. (6.3)–(6.4) · d'Inverno & Vickers §10.6, eq. (10.49)*

^mod-c1-5-2

> [!caution] Caution: Which parts of the metric are coordinate statements
> - **$g_{00}$ is fixed by measurements.** Static clocks and the redshift between them measure it ([[#^thm-c1-5-1|Theorem §C1.5.1]]). d'Inverno & Vickers (§10.6, eq. (10.50)) show that among coordinate changes $x^\mu \to x^\mu + \varepsilon X^\mu(x)$ compatible with weak fields and slow motion, $g_{00}$ is the only component left unchanged to first order.
> - **The form $(1 - 2\Phi)\delta_{ij}$ is a choice of spatial coordinates.** A change $x^i \to x^i + \xi^i(\vec x)$ with small $\xi^i$ changes $g_{ij}$ by $-(\partial_i\xi_j + \partial_j\xi_i)$ to first order. The isotropic form of Model §C1.5.2 is the one in which the spatial part is a multiple of $\delta_{ij}$. Hartle (p. 131) notes that any spatial factor equal to $1$ at leading order reproduces Newton for slow bodies; the factor $1 - 2\Phi$ is the prediction of general relativity and is tested by light (§C6.5, planned).
> - **Quantities built from coordinates inherit this.** The coordinate speed of light ([[#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]]) is one. A quantity read by one clock or one ruler, such as a proper time ([[#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]]) or a locally measured speed, is not.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers §10.6, eq. (10.50) · Hartle §6.6, p. 131*

## Free fall as extremal proper time

In special relativity a free particle's worldline is the one of longest proper time between its endpoints, a theorem about the flat metric ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-5|Theorem §B1.3.5]]). Equivalently, it is the stationary point of the action $-m\int d\tau$, which special relativity forces on a free particle ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, $S = -mc\int d\tau$ there, with the same positive $d\tau$). The equivalence principle says why the same law should hold with gravity. In a local inertial frame a freely falling body moves as a free particle of special relativity ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]), so each short piece of its worldline is locally straight. Stating this for the whole worldline, with the metric of the field in place of $\eta$, is the step taken now.

> [!principle] Principle §C1.5.3: Free Fall Extremizes Proper Time
> A freely falling test body (one on which no force other than gravity acts) moves between two timelike-separated events $A$ and $B$ along a timelike worldline that makes its proper time ([[#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]])
>
> $$
> \tau_{AB}[x] = \int_{\lambda_A}^{\lambda_B}\sqrt{-g_{\mu\nu}(x)\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}\;d\lambda
> $$
>
> stationary, $\delta\tau_{AB} = 0$, under all variations of the worldline that keep $A$ and $B$ fixed. Equivalently, the action $S = -m\,\tau_{AB}$ is stationary.
>
> *Domain: test bodies: negligible self-gravity, size small compared with the distance over which the field varies, no non-gravitational force, no spin. Extremal, not maximal: in flat spacetime the stationary worldline is the longest ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-5|Theorem §B1.3.5]]), but in a gravitational field several stationary worldlines can join the same two events, and one of them need not be the longest ([[#^ex-c1-5-2|Example §C1.5.2]]). Massive bodies only; light moves on null worldlines ([[#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]]). Layer: for $g = \eta$ this is a theorem of special relativity ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-5|Theorem §B1.3.5]]) or, read as an action principle, the free case of [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^pr-b3-1-2|Principle §B3.1.2]]. With gravity it is a principle here. It is restated as the geodesic equation in [[§C3.6 Parallel Transport and Geodesics|§C3.6]] and derived from the coupling of matter to the metric in §C4.1 (planned). No mass or composition appears in $\tau_{AB}$, so the weak equivalence principle ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-3|Principle §C1.3.3]]) is built in.*
>
> *Model:* test body in a given metric ([[#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]]); *applies:* any metric; used here with [[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]].
>
> *Source: Hartle §6.6, eq. (6.24), p. 131 and footnote 9 · Couzens, GR1, §2.2, after eq. (2.61) (same initial conditions, same curve, "a property of the geometry") · Schutz §7.2, eqs. (7.9)–(7.10) (geodesic form)*

^pr-c1-5-3

The centrepiece of the section follows: the principle, applied to the weak-field metric, gives back Newton.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.5.4: The Newtonian Limit of Free Fall
> In the static weak-field metric ([[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]), for a worldline $\vec x(t)$ with $v^2 = O(\varepsilon)$ and $\Phi = O(\varepsilon)$:
> 1. **Rate of proper time.**
>
> $$
> \frac{d\tau}{dt} = 1 + \Phi - \tfrac12 v^2 + O(\varepsilon^2) .
> $$
>
> 2. **⚑ The Newtonian action appears as the first correction to proper time.** Between events $A = (t_A, \vec x_A)$ and $B = (t_B, \vec x_B)$,
>
> $$
> \tau_{AB}[\vec x] = (t_B - t_A) - \int_{t_A}^{t_B}\Bigl(\tfrac12 v^2 - \Phi\Bigr)dt + O(\varepsilon^2), \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad -m\,\tau_{AB} = -m\,(t_B - t_A) + S_{\mathrm N}[\vec x] + O(\varepsilon^2) ,
> $$
>
> where $S_{\mathrm N} = \int L_{\mathrm N}\,dt$ with $L_{\mathrm N} = \tfrac12 mv^2 - m\Phi$ is the action of a Newtonian particle in the potential $\Phi$ ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^pr-b6-2-1|CM Principle §B6.2.1]]).
>
> 3. **Equation of motion.** The worldlines that make $\tau_{AB}$ stationary ([[#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]) obey
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2\vec x}{dt^2} = -\nabla\Phi\,\bigl[1 + O(\varepsilon)\bigr] ,
> $$
>
> Newton's law of motion in a gravitational field ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^def-a8-1-2|CM Def. §A8.1.2]]; [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]).
>
> 4. **No mass appears.** The equation contains neither the mass nor the composition of the body. The equality of inertial and gravitational mass ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^law-a8-1-3|CM Law §A8.1.3]]), an input in Newton's theory, is here a consequence of describing gravity by the metric.
>
> Restored: $d\tau/dt = 1 + \Phi/c^2 - v^2/2c^2$, $\;\tau_{AB} = (t_B - t_A) - \frac{1}{mc^2}S_{\mathrm N} + \dots$, $\;\ddot{\vec x} = -\nabla\Phi$.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]] with [[#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]; *applies:* static weak field, slow test bodies, first order in $\varepsilon$. The corrections of relative order $\varepsilon$ (post-Newtonian) also need the $\Phi^2$ terms of $g_{00}$, which the model does not contain (§C6.4, planned).
>
> *Source: Hartle §6.6, eqs. (6.24)–(6.29), Table 6.1 · Schutz §7.2, eqs. (7.16)–(7.24) (the same limit from the geodesic equation) · d'Inverno & Vickers §10.6, eqs. (10.42)–(10.48) · Couzens, GR1, §2.2, eq. (2.61)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 0** (order counting). $\Phi = O(\varepsilon)$ and $v^2 = O(\varepsilon)$ (Setup; [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]]). Let $\ell$ be the length over which $\Phi$ changes appreciably, so $\partial_i\Phi = O(\varepsilon/\ell)$. The metric is static, so along a worldline $d\Phi/dt = v^j\partial_j\Phi$.
>
> **Step 1** (parametrize by $t$). The worldline is timelike and future-pointing, so $t$ increases along it and can serve as the parameter ([[#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]]; parametrization independence). With $\lambda = t$ the tangent is $dx^\mu/dt = (1, v^1, v^2, v^3)$, and with the metric of [[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]
>
> $$
> -g_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{dt}\frac{dx^\nu}{dt} = (1 + 2\Phi)\cdot 1 - (1 - 2\Phi)\,\delta_{ij}v^iv^j = 1 + 2\Phi - v^2 + 2\Phi v^2 .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (proper time as an integral over $t$).
>
> $$
> \tau_{AB} = \int_{t_A}^{t_B}\sqrt{1 + u}\;dt, \qquad u \equiv 2\Phi - v^2 + 2\Phi v^2 = O(\varepsilon) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (expand the square root). Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) gives $\sqrt{1 + u} = 1 + \tfrac12 u - \tfrac18 u^2 + O(u^3)$. The two pieces are
>
> $$
> \tfrac12 u = \Phi - \tfrac12 v^2 + \Phi v^2, \qquad \tfrac18 u^2 = \tfrac18(2\Phi - v^2)^2 + O(\varepsilon^3) = \tfrac12\Phi^2 - \tfrac12\Phi v^2 + \tfrac18 v^4 + O(\varepsilon^3) ,
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> \frac{d\tau}{dt} = 1 + \underbrace{\Phi - \tfrac12 v^2}_{O(\varepsilon)} + \underbrace{\tfrac32\Phi v^2 - \tfrac12\Phi^2 - \tfrac18 v^4}_{O(\varepsilon^2)} + O(\varepsilon^3) .
> $$
>
> The second-order bracket is dropped. It is not reliable anyway, since the metric itself omits terms of order $\Phi^2$ ([[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]], assumptions). This is part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (integrate). Inserting part 1 into Step 2,
>
> $$
> \tau_{AB} = \int_{t_A}^{t_B}\Bigl(1 + \Phi - \tfrac12 v^2\Bigr)dt + O(\varepsilon^2) = (t_B - t_A) - \int_{t_A}^{t_B}\Bigl(\tfrac12 v^2 - \Phi\Bigr)dt + O(\varepsilon^2) .
> $$
>
> Multiplying by $-m$ gives $-m\tau_{AB} = -m(t_B - t_A) + \int(\tfrac12 mv^2 - m\Phi)\,dt + O(\varepsilon^2)$: part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (what is varied). [[#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]] compares worldlines with the same endpoints $A$ and $B$. In the parametrization by $t$, every competitor runs over the same interval $[t_A, t_B]$ with $\vec x(t_A) = \vec x_A$ and $\vec x(t_B) = \vec x_B$, and the variations are $\vec x \to \vec x + \vec\eta$ with $\vec\eta(t_A) = \vec\eta(t_B) = 0$. The term $t_B - t_A$ is the same for all of them. So, to first order,
>
> $$
> \delta\tau_{AB} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \delta\int_{t_A}^{t_B}\Bigl(\tfrac12 v^2 - \Phi\Bigr)dt = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (Euler–Lagrange). Apply [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|CM Theorem §B5.1.4]] with independent variable $t$, the three functions $x, y, z$, and integrand $F = \tfrac12(\dot x^2 + \dot y^2 + \dot z^2) - \Phi(x, y, z)$ (here the dot is $d/dt$):
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial F}{\partial \dot x} = \dot x,\quad \frac{\partial F}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dt}\dot x - \Bigl(-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}\Bigr) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot x = -\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x} ,
> $$
>
> and in the same way $\ddot y = -\partial\Phi/\partial y$, $\ddot z = -\partial\Phi/\partial z$. Together, $\ddot{\vec x} = -\nabla\Phi$.
>
> **Step 7** (the truncation is legitimate). Steps 5–6 varied the truncated integrand. To check that the dropped terms change the equation only at relative order $\varepsilon$, take the exact integrand $F = \sqrt{A}$, $A = 1 + 2\Phi - (1 - 2\Phi)v^2$, and write $a^i = dv^i/dt$. Its partial derivatives are
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial A}{\partial v^i} = -2(1 - 2\Phi)v^i, \quad \frac{\partial F}{\partial v^i} = -\frac{(1 - 2\Phi)v^i}{F}; \qquad \frac{\partial A}{\partial x^i} = 2(1 + v^2)\,\partial_i\Phi, \quad \frac{\partial F}{\partial x^i} = \frac{(1 + v^2)\,\partial_i\Phi}{F} .
> $$
>
> The Euler–Lagrange equation $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial F}{\partial v^i} - \frac{\partial F}{\partial x^i} = 0$, with the product rule $\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[(1 - 2\Phi)v^i/F\bigr] = \bigl[(1 - 2\Phi)a^i - 2(v^j\partial_j\Phi)v^i\bigr]/F - (1 - 2\Phi)v^i\dot F/F^2$ and $\dot F/F = \dot A/(2A)$, becomes after multiplication by $-F$
>
> $$
> (1 - 2\Phi)\,a^i = -(1 + v^2)\,\partial_i\Phi + 2(v^j\partial_j\Phi)\,v^i + (1 - 2\Phi)\,v^i\,\frac{\dot A}{2A}, \qquad \dot A = 2v^j\partial_j\Phi\,(1 + v^2) - 2(1 - 2\Phi)\,v^ja^j .
> $$
>
> Now count orders. $\partial_i\Phi = O(\varepsilon/\ell)$, and the equation itself makes $a^i = O(\varepsilon/\ell)$. Then $v^2\partial_i\Phi$, $(v^j\partial_j\Phi)v^i$ and $\Phi a^i$ are $O(\varepsilon^2/\ell)$, while $\dot A = O(v\,\varepsilon/\ell)$, so $v^i\dot A/(2A) = O(\varepsilon^2/\ell)$. Keeping the terms of order $\varepsilon/\ell$,
>
> $$
> a^i = -\partial_i\Phi + O(\varepsilon^2/\ell) = -\partial_i\Phi\,\bigl[1 + O(\varepsilon)\bigr] :
> $$
>
> the exact extremals obey Newton's equation up to relative corrections of order $\varepsilon$. This is part 3. (In one dimension a numerical check solves the exact equation for $a$ and finds $a = -\Phi'\,[1 + 2\Phi - 3v^2] + O(\varepsilon^3/\ell)$. The bracket is the correction this truncated metric would give; it is incomplete without the $\Phi^2$ terms of $g_{00}$.)
>
> **Step 8** (the mass). The mass $m$ multiplies $\tau_{AB}$ as a whole and drops out of $\delta\tau_{AB} = 0$; Steps 1–7 never used it. This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The gravitational term comes from $g_{00}$ alone: $\Phi$ in part 1 is the $2\Phi$ of $-g_{00} = 1 + 2\Phi$ halved by the square root. The kinetic term is special-relativistic time dilation, the $-\tfrac12 v^2$ of $\sqrt{1 - v^2}$ ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]]). The spatial factor $1 - 2\Phi$ enters only at $O(\varepsilon^2)$ (Step 3).
> - Newton's equation is the stationarity condition of proper time. Newton's Lagrangian $\tfrac12 v^2 - \Phi$ is the first correction to $d\tau/dt$ with the sign reversed. This is why Hamilton's principle with $L = T - U$ works for gravity: it is the slow, weak-field form of "clocks in free fall read stationary time".
> - The sign: stationary $\tau$ means stationary $\int(\tfrac12 v^2 - \Phi)\,dt$. Clocks moving fast lose time ($-\tfrac12 v^2$), clocks higher up gain time ($+\Phi$), and a falling body balances the two.

The weak-field metric does not depend on $t$, so the proper-time integrand has a cyclic variable. In Lagrangian mechanics a cyclic coordinate gives a conserved momentum. Here the momentum conjugate to $t$ turns out to be an energy, and it contains the Newtonian potential energy.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.5.5: The Conserved Energy in a Static Field
> Along every freely falling worldline in the static weak-field metric ([[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]; [[#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]) the quantity
>
> $$
> E \equiv (1 + 2\Phi)\,\frac{dt}{d\tau} = -g_{00}\,\frac{dt}{d\tau}
> $$
>
> is constant: the energy per unit mass. For slow bodies
>
> $$
> E = 1 + \Phi + \tfrac12 v^2 + O(\varepsilon^2) ,
> $$
>
> rest energy plus potential energy plus kinetic energy per unit mass. Its constancy contains Newtonian energy conservation, $\tfrac12 v^2 + \Phi = \text{const}$ ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-3|CM Theorem §A8.2.3]]). With $\Phi = 0$, $E = dt/d\tau = \gamma$, the energy per unit mass of special relativity.
>
> Restored: the energy $\mathcal E = mc^2(1 + 2\Phi/c^2)\,dt/d\tau = mc^2 + m\Phi + \tfrac12 mv^2 + \dots$ is conserved.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]] with [[#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]; *applies:* conservation of $E$ for any static metric of the form of Def. §C1.5.1 with $g_{0i} = 0$ (only $\partial_tg_{\mu\nu} = 0$ is used); the expansion to first order in $\varepsilon$.
>
> *Source: Schutz §7.4, eqs. (7.29)–(7.34) ($-p_0 \approx m + m\phi + p^2/2m$) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2.3 (the conserved $E$ of time translations, used for the redshift (6.80))*

^thm-c1-5-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (general parameter). Write $\tau_{AB} = \int F\,d\lambda$ with $\lambda$ arbitrary and, by [[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]],
>
> $$
> F(t, \vec x, \dot t, \dot{\vec x}) = \sqrt{(1 + 2\Phi(\vec x))\,\dot t^2 - (1 - 2\Phi(\vec x))\,\delta_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j}, \qquad \dot{} = \frac{d}{d\lambda} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** ($t$ is cyclic). $F$ contains $\dot t$ but not $t$, because the metric is static: $\partial F/\partial t = 0$. By [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-5|CM Theorem §B5.1.5]] (independent variable $\lambda$, functions $t, x^i$), $\partial F/\partial\dot t$ is constant along every stationary worldline.
>
> **Step 3** (the conjugate momentum).
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial F}{\partial \dot t} = \frac{1}{2F}\cdot 2(1 + 2\Phi)\,\dot t = \frac{(1 + 2\Phi)\,\dot t}{F} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (choose $\lambda = \tau$ after varying). The stationary worldline may now be parametrized by its proper time. Then $F\,d\lambda = d\tau$ gives $F = 1$ and $\dot t = dt/d\tau$, so the constant of Step 2 is $E = (1 + 2\Phi)\,dt/d\tau$. Since $-g_{00} = 1 + 2\Phi$, $E = -g_{00}\,dt/d\tau$.
>
> **Step 5** (invert the rate). From [[#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]], part 1, $d\tau/dt = 1 + q$ with $q = \Phi - \tfrac12 v^2 + O(\varepsilon^2)$. Using $1/(1 + q) = 1 - q + O(q^2)$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dt}{d\tau} = 1 - \Phi + \tfrac12 v^2 + O(\varepsilon^2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (multiply out).
>
> $$
> E = (1 + 2\Phi)\Bigl(1 - \Phi + \tfrac12 v^2\Bigr) + O(\varepsilon^2) = 1 - \Phi + \tfrac12 v^2 + 2\Phi + \underbrace{(-2\Phi^2 + \Phi v^2)}_{O(\varepsilon^2)} + O(\varepsilon^2) = 1 + \Phi + \tfrac12 v^2 + O(\varepsilon^2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 7** (Newtonian energy). $E$ is constant, so $\tfrac12 v^2 + \Phi = E - 1 + O(\varepsilon^2)$ is constant to first order.
>
> **Step 8** (special relativity and units). For $\Phi = 0$, $E = dt/d\tau = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2} = \gamma$. Restoring by [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]] ($\Phi \to \Phi/c^2$, $v \to v/c$) and multiplying by $mc^2$ gives $\mathcal E = mc^2 + m\Phi + \tfrac12 mv^2 + \dots$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The conservation law comes from the time-independence of the metric alone, through the cyclic variable $t$. It is the geometric form of the energy function of a time-translation symmetry ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-3|CM Theorem §B6.4.3]]). The potential energy is not added by hand: it is the $2\Phi$ of $g_{00}$ combined with the $-\Phi$ of time dilation in a gravitational potential. For a general static metric the same steps give $E = -g_{00}\,dt/d\tau$; the Killing-vector form is [[§C3.11 Isometries and Killing Vectors|§C3.11]].

## Light in the weak field

Slow bodies feel only $g_{00}$. Light is the opposite case, because for it the time and space parts of the line element are equally large. The question asked in PHY 360 (HW 4, Problem 3) is how fast light moves in the coordinates of the weak-field metric, and why the answer need not be $1$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.5.6: The Coordinate Speed of Light in a Weak Field
> In the static weak-field metric ([[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]) light moves on null worldlines, $ds^2 = 0$. Then:
> 1. **Coordinate speed.** In every direction,
>
> $$
> \Bigl|\frac{d\vec x}{dt}\Bigr| = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 2\Phi}{1 - 2\Phi}} = 1 + 2\Phi + O(\Phi^2) ,
> $$
>
> less than $1$ wherever $\Phi < 0$, and equal to $1$ only where $\Phi = 0$ (far from the source). The light cones drawn in the coordinates $(t, x^i)$ narrow toward the mass.
> 2. **Local speed.** A static observer at any point, measuring with her own clock and rulers, finds the speed of light exactly $1$.
> 3. **⚑ Half from time, half from space.** Of the deficit $2\Phi$, one $\Phi$ comes from $g_{00}$ and one from $g_{ij}$. With a flat spatial part ($g_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$) the coordinate speed would be $1 + \Phi$. The factor $2$ is the seed of the light deflection, which is twice the equivalence-principle estimate (§C6.5, planned), and of the radar time delay (§C6.6, planned).
>
> Restored: $|d\vec x/dt| = c\sqrt{(1 + 2\Phi/c^2)/(1 - 2\Phi/c^2)} \approx c + 2\Phi/c$.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]; light as null worldlines (in a local inertial frame light moves as in special relativity, [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]], and $ds^2 = 0$ is a statement independent of coordinates); *applies:* part 1 is a statement about the coordinates of the model ([[#^cau-c1-5-2|Caution: Which parts of the metric are coordinate statements]]), exact for the metric as written, first order in $\Phi$ as physics; part 2 is coordinate-independent.
>
> *Source: PHY 360 (audit), HW 4, Problem 3 (no solution file; derived here) · Hartle §6.6, eq. (6.20), p. 131 (the spatial factor matters for light) · Schutz §7.2, eq. (7.10) (photons follow the same geodesic law)*

^thm-c1-5-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (null condition). For light $ds^2 = 0$. With the metric of [[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]] and $|d\vec x|^2 = \delta_{ij}dx^idx^j$,
>
> $$
> 0 = -(1 + 2\Phi)\,dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)\,|d\vec x|^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Bigl|\frac{d\vec x}{dt}\Bigr|^2 = \frac{1 + 2\Phi}{1 - 2\Phi} .
> $$
>
> Only $|d\vec x|$ enters, so the result is the same in every direction: the metric is isotropic.
>
> **Step 2** (expand). With $\sqrt{1 + 2\Phi} = 1 + \Phi - \tfrac12\Phi^2 + O(\Phi^3)$ and $(1 - 2\Phi)^{-1/2} = 1 + \Phi + \tfrac32\Phi^2 + O(\Phi^3)$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]),
>
> $$
> \Bigl|\frac{d\vec x}{dt}\Bigr| = \Bigl(1 + \Phi - \tfrac12\Phi^2\Bigr)\Bigl(1 + \Phi + \tfrac32\Phi^2\Bigr) + O(\Phi^3) = 1 + 2\Phi + 2\Phi^2 + O(\Phi^3) .
> $$
>
> Only $1 + 2\Phi$ is meaningful, since the metric is correct to first order only. For $\Phi = -M/r$ the speed is $1$ only as $r \to \infty$. This is part 1. (PHY 360 parts (c)–(d): the coordinate time $t$ is kept by clocks far away, so only there do coordinate time and local time agree.)
>
> **Step 3** (local coordinates of a static observer). Fix a point $\vec x_0$ with $\Phi_0 = \Phi(\vec x_0)$, and rescale the coordinates there by constants,
>
> $$
> \hat t = \sqrt{1 + 2\Phi_0}\;t, \qquad \hat x^i = \sqrt{1 - 2\Phi_0}\;x^i .
> $$
>
> At $\vec x_0$ the line element becomes $ds^2 = -d\hat t^2 + \delta_{ij}\,d\hat x^id\hat x^j$, the Minkowski form ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]). A static clock at $\vec x_0$ reads $\hat t$, because $d\tau = \sqrt{1 + 2\Phi_0}\,dt$ ([[#^thm-c1-5-1|Theorem §C1.5.1]], part 1). A short rod at rest there has length $\sqrt{ds^2} = \sqrt{1 - 2\Phi_0}\,|d\vec x|$, which is its length in $\hat x$. So $(\hat t, \hat x^i)$ are the time and length of the static observer's own instruments near $\vec x_0$.
>
> **Step 4** (local speed). In these coordinates, at $\vec x_0$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl|\frac{d\hat{\vec x}}{d\hat t}\Bigr| = \frac{\sqrt{1 - 2\Phi_0}}{\sqrt{1 + 2\Phi_0}}\;\Bigl|\frac{d\vec x}{dt}\Bigr| = \frac{\sqrt{1 - 2\Phi_0}}{\sqrt{1 + 2\Phi_0}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{1 + 2\Phi_0}}{\sqrt{1 - 2\Phi_0}} = 1 ,
> $$
>
> exactly, at every point: part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (factor 2). Write the result of Step 1 as $\sqrt{1 + 2\Phi}\cdot(1 - 2\Phi)^{-1/2}$. The first factor comes from $g_{00}$ and gives $1 + \Phi$. The second comes from $g_{ij}$ and gives another factor $1 + \Phi$. If $g_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$, Step 1 reads $|d\vec x/dt|^2 = 1 + 2\Phi$ and $|d\vec x/dt| = 1 + \Phi + O(\Phi^2)$: part 3.
>
> **Step 6** (restore units). $\Phi \to \Phi/c^2$, $t \to ct$: $|d\vec x|/(c\,dt) = 1 + 2\Phi/c^2$, so $|d\vec x/dt| = c + 2\Phi/c$, the form asked for in PHY 360, HW 4, Problem 3(b).
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The coordinate speed of light differs from $1$ because the coordinates are not the local measuring instruments of any observer, except far away. Locally, light still moves at $1$, as the equivalence principle requires. For light, unlike for slow bodies, both parts of the metric contribute equally, so light tests the spatial part of the metric that slow bodies cannot see.

Gravity is now the geometry of spacetime, written in one particular set of coordinates. Theorem §C1.4.7 showed that no global inertial frame exists, and the coordinates $(t, x^i)$ of the weak-field metric are not the measuring instruments of any observer except far away ([[#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]], part 2). Without a preferred frame, coordinates are arbitrary labels. The next chapter asks how calculus is done when no chart is preferred (C2 Manifolds and Tensor Fields, planned).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C1.5.1: Three Clocks: Held, Thrown Up, Carried
> *Question* (Hartle Prob. 6.13). Three observers stand together on the ground with synchronized clocks. At $t = 0$ the first throws his clock straight up, and it returns at $t = T$. The second holds hers. The third carries his up to the thrown clock's highest point and back down, at constant speed on each leg, also returning at $T$. Which clock reads most when they meet again?
>
> *Setup.* Near the ground the field is uniform ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^mod-a8-1-5|CM Model §A8.1.5]]): $\Phi(z) = \Phi_0 + gz$, $|\Phi_0| \ll 1$, height $z$. All three worldlines run from $(t, z) = (0, 0)$ to $(T, 0)$. By [[#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]], part 1, each clock reads
>
> $$
> \tau = \int_0^T\Bigl(1 + \Phi_0 + gz - \tfrac12\dot z^2\Bigr)dt = (1 + \Phi_0)\,T + \Delta\tau, \qquad \Delta\tau \equiv \int_0^T\Bigl(gz - \tfrac12\dot z^2\Bigr)dt ,
> $$
>
> with $\dot z = dz/dt$. The common part $(1 + \Phi_0)T$ is the held clock's reading, so $\Delta\tau$ is each clock's lead over the held one.
>
> *Held.* $z \equiv 0$, so $\Delta\tau_{\text{held}} = 0$.
>
> *Thrown* (free fall, $\ddot z = -g$, returning at $T$). $z(t) = \tfrac12 g\,t(T - t)$ and $\dot z = g(\tfrac12 T - t)$. The launch speed is $\tfrac12 gT$ and the highest point is $h = z(T/2) = gT^2/8$. The two integrals are
>
> $$
> \int_0^T gz\,dt = \tfrac12 g^2\int_0^T(tT - t^2)\,dt = \tfrac12 g^2\Bigl(\frac{T^3}{2} - \frac{T^3}{3}\Bigr) = \frac{g^2T^3}{12},
> $$
>
> $$
> \int_0^T\tfrac12\dot z^2\,dt = \tfrac12 g^2\int_0^T\Bigl(\tfrac12 T - t\Bigr)^2dt = \tfrac12 g^2\Bigl[-\tfrac13\Bigl(\tfrac12 T - t\Bigr)^3\Bigr]_0^T = \tfrac12 g^2\cdot\frac{T^3}{12} = \frac{g^2T^3}{24},
> $$
>
> so $\Delta\tau_{\text{thrown}} = g^2T^3/12 - g^2T^3/24 = g^2T^3/24$.
>
> *Carried* (to height $h$ in time $T/2$ at the constant speed $u = h/(T/2) = gT/4$, then down). $z = ut$ for $0 \le t \le T/2$ and $z = u(T - t)$ afterwards, with $\dot z^2 = u^2$ throughout. The two integrals are
>
> $$
> \int_0^T gz\,dt = 2\,g u\int_0^{T/2}t\,dt = g u\,\frac{T^2}{4} = \frac{g^2T^3}{16}, \qquad \int_0^T\tfrac12 u^2\,dt = \tfrac12\Bigl(\frac{gT}{4}\Bigr)^2T = \frac{g^2T^3}{32},
> $$
>
> so $\Delta\tau_{\text{carried}} = g^2T^3/16 - g^2T^3/32 = g^2T^3/32$.
>
> *Result.* $\Delta\tau_{\text{thrown}} = \tfrac{1}{24}g^2T^3 > \Delta\tau_{\text{carried}} = \tfrac{1}{32}g^2T^3 > \Delta\tau_{\text{held}} = 0$; restored, divide by $c^2$. The thrown clock, the only one in free fall, reads most. For $T = 10\ \mathrm s$ ($h = 122.6\ \mathrm m$): thrown $+4.46\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm s$, carried $+3.34\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm s$, a difference of $g^2T^3/96c^2 = 1.11\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm s$.
>
> *Why free fall wins.* Compare all parabolas $z_k(t) = k\,t(T - t)$ with the same endpoints. With $\dot z_k = k(T - 2t)$ and $\int_0^T(T - 2t)^2dt = T^3/3$,
>
> $$
> \Delta\tau(k) = gk\,\frac{T^3}{6} - \tfrac12 k^2\,\frac{T^3}{3} = \frac{T^3}{6}\bigl(gk - k^2\bigr), \qquad \frac{d\,\Delta\tau}{dk} = \frac{T^3}{6}(g - 2k) = 0 \iff k = \frac g2 .
> $$
>
> The maximum of this family is the free-fall parabola, as [[#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]] requires. Going higher gains time through $gz$; moving faster loses it through $-\tfrac12\dot z^2$. Free fall strikes the balance (figure below). If the same motions were carried out horizontally, $gz$ would be the same for all three, $\Delta\tau = -\int\tfrac12 v^2\,dt \le 0$, and the held clock would read most. That is time dilation of special relativity alone.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Problem 6.13 (worked here; no solution in the book) · Hartle §6.6, eq. (6.26), Box 6.2 (the same integrand for Hafele–Keating)*

> [!example] Example §C1.5.2: An Orbit Is Extremal but Not Maximal
> *Question* (Hartle Prob. 6.14). A satellite is on a circular orbit of radius $R$ around the Earth. Let $A$ and $B$ be two events at the same point of the orbit, one orbital period $P$ apart in $t$. The satellite's worldline from $A$ to $B$ is freely falling, so its proper time is stationary ([[#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]). Is it the longest?
>
> *Setup.* Weak field $\Phi = -M/r$ outside the Earth ([[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]). Take an orbit at $400\ \mathrm{km}$ altitude, the height of the International Space Station: $R = R_\oplus + 400\ \mathrm{km} = 6778\ \mathrm{km}$. The circular-orbit speed and period are $v^2 = M/R$ and $P = 2\pi\sqrt{R^3/M}$ ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-5|CM Theorem §A8.2.5]]), so $P = 5554\ \mathrm s$, and $M/R$ (restored $GM_\oplus/Rc^2$) $= 6.54\times10^{-10}$. Each proper time follows from $\tau = \int(1 + \Phi - \tfrac12 v^2)\,dt$ ([[#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]], part 1); restored, $\Phi$ and $v^2$ are divided by $c^2$.
>
> *(a) The orbit.* $\Phi = -M/R$ and $\tfrac12 v^2 = M/2R$ are constant along it:
>
> $$
> \tau_a = P\Bigl(1 - \frac MR - \frac{M}{2R}\Bigr) = P\Bigl(1 - \frac{3M}{2R}\Bigr) = P - 5.45\ \mu\mathrm s .
> $$
>
> *(b) A clock held at rest at the orbit's point* (not in free fall: it needs a rocket). $v = 0$:
>
> $$
> \tau_b = P\Bigl(1 - \frac MR\Bigr) = P - 3.63\ \mu\mathrm s, \qquad \tau_b - \tau_a = \frac{M}{2R}\,P = 1.82\ \mu\mathrm s > 0 .
> $$
>
> *(c) A light signal* sent outward from $A$ and reflected back to arrive at $B$: its worldline is null, so $\tau_c = 0$. There is no shortest worldline, as in flat spacetime ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-5|Theorem §B1.3.5]]).
>
> *(d) Another freely falling worldline from $A$ to $B$.* Throw the clock straight up from the orbit's point, so that it falls back at time $P$. Radial Kepler motion with semimajor axis $a$ is $r = a(1 - \cos\eta)$, $t = \sqrt{a^3/M}\,(\eta - \sin\eta)$, with energy $\tfrac12 v^2 - M/r = -M/2a$. Leaving $r = R$ at $\eta_0$ ($\cos\eta_0 = 1 - R/a$) and returning at $2\pi - \eta_0$ takes
>
> $$
> \sqrt{a^3/M}\,\bigl(2\pi - 2\eta_0 + 2\sin\eta_0\bigr) = P \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = 7557\ \mathrm{km},\ \ \eta_0 = 1.468 ,
> $$
>
> solved numerically. The launch speed is $8.05\ \mathrm{km/s}$, and the highest point is $2a = 15\,114\ \mathrm{km}$ from the centre. Along it, $\Phi - \tfrac12 v^2 = 2\Phi + M/2a$ by the energy relation, and $dt/r = \sqrt{a/M}\,d\eta$. Therefore
>
> $$
> \tau_d - P = \frac{M}{2a}P + 2\int\Phi\,dt = \frac{M}{2a}P - 2M\sqrt{\frac aM}\,(2\pi - 2\eta_0) = 1.63\ \mu\mathrm s - 4.09\ \mu\mathrm s = -2.46\ \mu\mathrm s ,
> $$
>
> confirmed by direct numerical integration in the script.
>
> *Result.* $\tau_d = P - 2.46\ \mu\mathrm s\ >\ \tau_b = P - 3.63\ \mu\mathrm s\ >\ \tau_a = P - 5.45\ \mu\mathrm s\ >\ \tau_c = 0$. The orbit (a) is stationary but not the longest. A clock held at rest (b), which is not freely falling, ages more. Another freely falling worldline (d) between the same events ages more still. In flat spacetime only one stationary worldline joins two timelike-separated events, and it is the longest ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-5|Theorem §B1.3.5]]). In a gravitational field several do: the orbit, the vertical throw, orbits in other planes through the same point. The principle selects the stationary ones, not the longest (Hartle p. 131, footnote 9).
>
> *Source: Hartle, Problem 6.14 (a)–(d) (worked here; no solution in the book); §6.6, p. 131 and footnote 9 · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C1.5.3: The Coordinate Speed of Light near the Sun
> *Setup.* The Sun's field outside its surface is $\Phi = -M_\odot/r$. At the surface (the solar limb) $|\Phi| = M_\odot/R_\odot = 2.12\times10^{-6}$ (restored $GM_\odot/R_\odot c^2$; the Sun's entry in the compactness table of [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]]).
>
> *Coordinate speed* ([[#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]], part 1). $2\Phi = -4.25\times10^{-6}$, so $|d\vec x/dt| = c\,(1 - 4.25\times10^{-6}) = c - 1.27\ \mathrm{km/s}$ at the limb, in every direction.
>
> *Local speed* (part 2). An observer hovering at the limb measures exactly $c$ with her own clock and rulers.
>
> *What the coordinate speed adds up to.* A radial light signal from the surface $r = R_\odot$ out to $D = 1\ \mathrm{AU}$ takes the coordinate time
>
> $$
> \Delta t = \int_{R_\odot}^{D}\Bigl|\frac{d\vec x}{dt}\Bigr|^{-1}dr = \int_{R_\odot}^{D}\Bigl(1 + \frac{2M_\odot}{r}\Bigr)dr + O(\Phi^2) = (D - R_\odot) + 2M_\odot\ln\frac{D}{R_\odot} ,
> $$
>
> using $|d\vec x/dt|^{-1} = 1 - 2\Phi + O(\Phi^2)$. With $\ln(D/R_\odot) = 5.371$ and $M_\odot$ (restored $GM_\odot/c^3$) $= 4.93\ \mu\mathrm s$, the excess over the coordinate distance is $2M_\odot\ln(D/R_\odot) = 52.9\ \mu\mathrm s$.
>
> *⚑ Coordinate dependence.* Both numbers, $1.27\ \mathrm{km/s}$ and $52.9\ \mu\mathrm s$, are statements about the coordinates $(t, x^i)$ of [[#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]. Another choice of radial coordinate changes them ([[#^cau-c1-5-2|Caution: Which parts of the metric are coordinate statements]]), because $D - R_\odot$ is a coordinate difference, not a measured length. A measurable delay must compare the round trip of a radar signal, timed by one clock, with a distance determined independently. That is the Shapiro experiment, §C6.6 (planned). This example shows only where the effect comes from.
>
> *Source: PHY 360 (audit), HW 4, Problem 3 (c)–(d), applied to the Sun · numbers checked by script*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - **Layer change of the free-fall law.** In flat spacetime the longest-proper-time property is a theorem ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-5|Theorem §B1.3.5]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), and stationary action means maximal proper time ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^rem-b3-1-2|§B3.1, Remark: Stationary action is maximal proper time]]). With gravity it becomes [[#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]], stationary but not always maximal ([[#^ex-c1-5-2|Example §C1.5.2]]).
> - **Newton's Lagrangian from proper time.** [[#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]], part 2, gives Hamilton's principle with $L = T - U$ for gravity ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^pr-b6-2-1|CM Principle §B6.2.1]]) as the weak-field, slow-motion form of stationary proper time. The kinetic term is the expansion of the free relativistic Lagrangian ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]]).
> - **Energy.** The conserved $E$ of [[#^thm-c1-5-5|Theorem §C1.5.5]] is the energy function of the time-translation symmetry ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-3|CM Theorem §B6.4.3]]) and reduces to Newtonian energy conservation in a gravitational field ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-3|CM Theorem §A8.2.3]]).
> - **Mass equality explained.** CM records the equality of inertial and gravitational mass as an experimental law ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^law-a8-1-3|CM Law §A8.1.3]]); level C makes it the weak equivalence principle ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-3|Principle §C1.3.3]]); here it follows because the mass multiplies the proper time as a whole ([[#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]], part 4).
