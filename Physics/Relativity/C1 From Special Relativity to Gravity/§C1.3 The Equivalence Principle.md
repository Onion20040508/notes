Why does every body fall with the same acceleration, and what does that say about gravity? In Newton's theory, recast as a field theory in [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]], the answer is a coincidence. The mass that resists acceleration in Newton's second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]]) and the mass that feels gravity in the law of gravitation ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]]) are measured to be equal ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^law-a8-1-3|CM Law §A8.1.3]]), and the theory gives no reason why ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton#^rem-a8-5-1|CM Remark: Two unexplained assumptions]]). [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced|§C1.1]] closed by noting that this universality points toward geometry ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^rem-c1-1-1|§C1.1, Remark: Universality of free fall points to geometry]]). Einstein took the coincidence as the foundation of a new theory, and this section retraces that step.

It goes in four stages. First the coincidence and its consequence: the path of a falling body does not depend on what the body is made of ([[#^thm-c1-3-1|Theorem §C1.3.1]]); this is measured to about one part in $10^{13}$ ([[#^law-c1-3-2|Law §C1.3.2]]) and is stated as a principle ([[#^pr-c1-3-3|Principle §C1.3.3]]). Second, what the coincidence allows: an observer who falls freely removes a uniform gravitational field from every equation of motion, and an observer who accelerates creates one ([[#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]]). No other force can be switched off by a choice of frame, so gravity starts to look like a property of frames rather than a force. Third, the limit of that picture, computed rather than asserted: a real field can be removed only near one point, because two freely falling bodies accelerate relative to each other through the second derivatives of $\Phi$ ([[#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]]). This fixes what a *local* inertial frame is ([[#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]]), and Einstein's principle then says that inside such a frame all of physics is special relativity ([[#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]). Fourth, a first consequence beyond mechanics: light falls ([[#^thm-c1-3-8|Theorem §C1.3.8]]). Levels A and B of Classical Mechanics stated the equivalence principle in words and pointed here ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton#^rem-a8-5-2|CM Remark: The principle of equivalence]], [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^rem-b9-1-3|CM Remark: A uniformly accelerating frame imitates gravity]]); this section is its home.

**Setup.** Newtonian gravity in the form of [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]: a potential $\Phi(t, \mathbf x)$ on space at each time, sourced by the mass density through $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi\rho$, seen from an inertial frame of Newtonian mechanics, with speeds $\ll 1$. Inertial and gravitational mass are kept apart in [[#^thm-c1-3-1|Theorem §C1.3.1]] and set equal from then on. The bodies are *test bodies*: their own gravity is negligible, and they are small compared with the distance over which $\nabla\Phi$ changes. Frames are non-rotating, related to an inertial frame by $\mathbf x' = \mathbf x - \mathbf R(t)$, $t' = t$. Theorems §C1.3.1, §C1.3.4, §C1.3.6 and §C1.3.7 are exact statements of Newtonian mechanics (§C1.3.6–§C1.3.7 to first order in the separation of the bodies). [[#^law-c1-3-2|Law §C1.3.2]] is experimental. Principles §C1.3.3 and §C1.3.5 are assumptions of the new theory: they say more than Newtonian gravity implies. [[#^thm-c1-3-8|Theorem §C1.3.8]] uses special relativity inside a freely falling frame, to first order in $gL$.

## Universality of free fall

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.3.1: Free Fall Is Independent of Composition
> A test body has an inertial mass $m_I$ ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-1|CM Def. §A3.2.1]]) and a gravitational mass $m_G$ ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^def-a8-1-1|CM Def. §A8.1.1]]). It moves under gravity alone in the potential $\Phi$ of [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]], seen from an inertial frame. Then:
> 1. its acceleration is
>
> $$
> \ddot{\mathbf x} = -\frac{m_G}{m_I}\,\nabla\Phi(t, \mathbf x) ;
> $$
>
> 2. its trajectory is fixed by its initial position, its initial velocity and the single number $m_G/m_I$; all bodies started with the same position and velocity follow the same trajectory if and only if $m_G/m_I$ has the same value for all of them;
> 3. if $m_G = \lambda\,m_I$ for every body, with one constant $\lambda$, then redefining every gravitational mass as $m_G/\lambda$ and Newton's constant as $\lambda^2G$ changes no prediction, and afterwards $m_G = m_I$ and $\ddot{\mathbf x} = -\nabla\Phi$. (Part 3 is written with $G$ kept, since it is a statement about $G$.)
>
> *Model:* Newtonian gravity ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]) with the two masses kept distinct; *applies:* test bodies on which gravity is the only force, any smooth $\Phi$, in an inertial frame.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §2.2, eq. (2.61) · Hartle, Gravity, §3.4, eqs. (3.25)–(3.32), pp. 41–42 · 't Hooft, ch. 2, eq. (2.1)*

^thm-c1-3-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the force). The gravitational force on the body is its gravitational mass times the field $\mathbf g = -\nabla\Phi$ ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^def-a8-1-2|CM Def. §A8.1.2]], [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]):
>
> $$
> \mathbf F = -m_G\,\nabla\Phi(t, \mathbf x) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (second law). In an inertial frame $m_I\ddot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf F$ ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]]). Divide by $m_I$:
>
> $$
> m_I\,\ddot{\mathbf x} = -m_G\,\nabla\Phi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot{\mathbf x} = -\kappa\,\nabla\Phi(t, \mathbf x), \qquad \kappa \equiv \frac{m_G}{m_I} .
> $$
>
> This is part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (the initial-value problem). Write the equation as a first-order system for the position and the velocity $\mathbf v$:
>
> $$
> \dot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf v, \qquad \dot{\mathbf v} = -\kappa\,\nabla\Phi(t, \mathbf x) .
> $$
>
> The right-hand side is a smooth function of $(t, \mathbf x, \mathbf v)$ when $\Phi$ is smooth, so the solution through given $(\mathbf x_0, \mathbf v_0)$ exists and is unique ([[§68 Local Existence and Uniqueness#^thm-68-1|591 Thm. §68.1]], applied in $(\mathbf x, \mathbf v)$-space with $t$ adjoined as a coordinate). The only property of the body that enters is $\kappa$. If two bodies have $\kappa_1 = \kappa_2$, they solve the same system with the same initial data, hence follow the same trajectory. If $\kappa_1 \neq \kappa_2$ and $\nabla\Phi(0, \mathbf x_0) \neq 0$, their accelerations already differ at $t = 0$,
>
> $$
> \ddot{\mathbf x}_1(0) - \ddot{\mathbf x}_2(0) = -(\kappa_1 - \kappa_2)\,\nabla\Phi(0, \mathbf x_0) \neq 0 ,
> $$
>
> so their trajectories separate. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (rescaling). Suppose $m_G = \lambda m_I$ for every body. The force between two bodies at distance $r$ ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]], $G$ kept) is
>
> $$
> F = \frac{G\,m_{G1}m_{G2}}{r^2} = \frac{G\,(\lambda m_{I1})(\lambda m_{I2})}{r^2} = \frac{(\lambda^2G)\,\tilde m_{G1}\tilde m_{G2}}{r^2}, \qquad \tilde m_G \equiv \frac{m_G}{\lambda} = m_I .
> $$
>
> So every force is unchanged when $(G, m_G)$ is replaced by $(\tilde G, \tilde m_G) = (\lambda^2G, m_G/\lambda)$. The potential changes with it: from [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-2|Theorem §C1.1.2]] with the density $\rho = \lambda\tilde\rho$ of gravitational mass,
>
> $$
> \Phi = -G\int\frac{\rho(t, \mathbf x')}{|\mathbf x - \mathbf x'|}\,d^3x' = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\,\tilde G\int\frac{\tilde\rho(t, \mathbf x')}{|\mathbf x - \mathbf x'|}\,d^3x' = \frac{\tilde\Phi}{\lambda} ,
> $$
>
> and part 1 becomes $\ddot{\mathbf x} = -\lambda\nabla\Phi = -\nabla\tilde\Phi$. This is part 3: a common factor is a choice of units for gravitational mass (Hartle defines the two masses to agree for one standard body, eq. (3.32)).
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The motion of a body in a gravitational field depends on the body through one number, $m_G/m_I$. Newtonian mechanics allows that number to differ from body to body; only experiment ([[#^law-c1-3-2|Law §C1.3.2]]) says that it does not.

> [!law] Law §C1.3.2: Universality of Free Fall Tested
> Bodies of different composition, in the same gravitational field, have the same acceleration. A test compares two bodies $A$, $B$ and bounds the **Eötvös parameter**, the difference of their accelerations as a fraction of the mean,
>
> $$
> \eta \equiv \frac{a_A - a_B}{\tfrac12(a_A + a_B)} = \frac{\kappa_A - \kappa_B}{\tfrac12(\kappa_A + \kappa_B)}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{m_G}{m_I}
> $$
>
> (the second form by [[#^thm-c1-3-1|Theorem §C1.3.1]], part 1). Results, as the sources report them:
> - Eötvös, torsion balance in the Earth's field, around 1900: $|\eta| \lesssim 10^{-9}$ ('t Hooft; d'Inverno & Vickers: $|\eta| < 5 \times 10^{-9}$);
> - Roll, Krotkov and Dicke (Princeton, 1964), the Sun's field, modulated by the Earth's rotation: $|\eta| < 10^{-11}$ (d'Inverno & Vickers; 't Hooft: "one part in $10^{11}$");
> - Braginsky and Panov (Moscow, 1971), also the Sun's field: $|\eta| < 10^{-12}$ (d'Inverno & Vickers);
> - Su et al. (1994), rotating torsion pendulum with beryllium and copper: $\eta = (-0.2 \pm 2.8) \times 10^{-12}$;
> - lunar laser ranging, the Earth and the Moon falling toward the Sun: accelerations equal to within $1.5 \times 10^{-13}$ (Williams et al. 1996, Anderson and Williams 2001, as cited by Hartle).
>
> The later sources quote the same level: Couzens (2024) an accuracy of $10^{-13}$, Schutz (2022) "a few parts in $10^{13}$".
>
> *Valid when:* laboratory and solar-system bodies of every composition tested (metals such as beryllium and copper; the Earth, with an iron core, and the Moon, mostly rock); test bodies in the fields of the Earth, the Sun and the Galaxy. Layer: [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^law-a8-1-3|CM Law §A8.1.3]] states the equality $m_G = m_I$, and [[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^law-a2-3-2|CM Law §A2.3.2]] the observation near the Earth's surface; this box records how precisely it holds. The numbers are as of the sources' dates: Hartle's are from 2003.
>
> *Model:* Newtonian gravity with the two masses kept distinct ([[#^thm-c1-3-1|Theorem §C1.3.1]]); *applies:* test bodies, gravity the only force along the measured direction; an experimental input.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, §6.1, eqs. (6.2)–(6.4), pp. 107–109; Box 2.1, p. 14 · 't Hooft, ch. 2, p. 9 · d'Inverno & Vickers, §16.3, p. 294 · Couzens, GR1, §2.2, after eq. (2.61) · Schutz §7.1, p. 172*

> [!principle] Principle §C1.3.3: The Weak Equivalence Principle
> The worldline of a freely falling test body depends only on its initial position and its initial velocity, and not on its mass, composition or internal structure.
>
> In Newtonian language: $m_G/m_I$ is the same for every body, and with the units of [[#^thm-c1-3-1|Theorem §C1.3.1]], part 3, every test body obeys $\ddot{\mathbf x} = -\nabla\Phi(t, \mathbf x)$.
>
> *Domain: test bodies: their own gravity is negligible, they are small compared with the distance over which the field changes appreciably ([[#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]] measures this), and no force other than gravity acts on them. Bodies whose gravitational binding energy is not negligible are outside it ([[#^rem-c1-3-1|Remark: The strong equivalence principle]]). Layer: Newton's theory has the equality as an experimental fact ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^law-a8-1-3|CM Law §A8.1.3]], [[#^law-c1-3-2|Law §C1.3.2]]); here it is a principle, which general relativity builds in and which the theory cannot survive without. In relativity the statement is about worldlines in spacetime; it becomes "free test bodies follow timelike geodesics" in [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]].*
>
> *Model:* Newtonian gravity ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]) for the Newtonian form; *applies:* test bodies as in the Domain line.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §2.2.1 (boxed statement, p. 25) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §9.4, P1, p. 162; §16.4, point 1, p. 294 · Schutz §5.1, p. 115; §7.1, Postulate IV and its footnote, p. 171*

## A uniform field can be transformed away

What does the universality of free fall buy? Look first at what an accelerated observer sees without gravity. A particle moving along $x$ with $x(t) = kt^3$ in an inertial frame has acceleration $6kt$, so the net force on it is $F_{\text{net}} = 6mkt$. An observer moving at constant velocity measures the same acceleration $6kt$. An observer who starts from rest with constant acceleration $a_N$ measures $6kt - a_N$, and for her $m \times (\text{acceleration}) = F_{\text{net}} - ma_N$: Newton's second law fails by a term $-ma_N$ ([[#^ex-c1-3-3|Example §C1.3.3]], computed there step by step). That term is the translational fictitious force of an accelerated frame ([[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|CM Theorem §B9.1.5]]), and it is proportional to the inertial mass. Gravity is the one real force proportional to the same mass ([[#^thm-c1-3-1|Theorem §C1.3.1]], [[#^pr-c1-3-3|Principle §C1.3.3]]). So the two can cancel exactly, for every body at once.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.3.4: A Uniform Gravitational Field Is Equivalent to Uniform Acceleration
> Let particles $a = 1, \dots, N$ with masses $m_a$ ($m_G = m_I$, [[#^pr-c1-3-3|Principle §C1.3.3]]) move under mutual forces $\mathbf F_a$ that depend only on time, on the relative positions $\mathbf x_a - \mathbf x_b$ and on the relative velocities $\dot{\mathbf x}_a - \dot{\mathbf x}_b$.
> 1. **Free fall removes a uniform field.** In an inertial frame $S$ with a uniform field $\mathbf g$ (constant in space and time; $\Phi = -\mathbf g\cdot\mathbf x$), pass to the frame $S'$ that falls with acceleration $\mathbf g$ and does not rotate:
>
> $$
> \mathbf x' = \mathbf x - \tfrac12\,\mathbf g\,t^2, \qquad t' = t .
> $$
>
> In $S'$ the equations of motion are $m_a\ddot{\mathbf x}'_a = \mathbf F_a$, with no gravitational term: those of the same particles in an inertial frame without gravity.
> 2. **Acceleration creates a uniform field.** Without gravity, in a non-rotating frame $S'$ whose origin has constant acceleration $\mathbf A$ relative to an inertial frame, the equations of motion are $m_a\ddot{\mathbf x}'_a = \mathbf F_a - m_a\mathbf A$: those of the same particles at rest in an inertial frame with the uniform field $\mathbf g = -\mathbf A$.
> 3. **No mechanical experiment tells them apart.** Same equations and same initial data give the same motions, so no experiment with these particles distinguishes the freely falling frame of part 1 from an inertial frame without gravity, or the accelerated frame of part 2 from a frame at rest in the field $-\mathbf A$.
> 4. **The equality of masses is necessary.** If some particle had $m_{G,a} \neq m_{I,a}$, it would keep the force $(m_{G,a} - m_{I,a})\,\mathbf g$ in $S'$, and no single frame would remove the field for every particle.
>
> *Model:* Newtonian mechanics of $N$ particles with Galilean-invariant mutual forces, in a uniform gravitational field; *applies:* exactly, for uniform $\mathbf g$; for a real field only approximately, over a region where $\mathbf g$ does not vary measurably ([[#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]]).
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, §6.2, Fig. 6.4, pp. 111–113 · d'Inverno & Vickers, §9.4, P3–P4 and the lift experiments, pp. 163–164 · Schutz §5.1, pp. 114–115 · Couzens, GR1, §2.2.1, p. 25*

^thm-c1-3-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (equations in $S$). In the inertial frame $S$, with the gravitational force $m_{G,a}\mathbf g$ of the uniform field ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^def-a8-1-2|CM Def. §A8.1.2]]) and Newton's second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]]),
>
> $$
> m_{I,a}\,\ddot{\mathbf x}_a = \mathbf F_a + m_{G,a}\,\mathbf g .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (change of frame). Substitute $\mathbf x_a = \mathbf x'_a + \tfrac12\mathbf g t^2$ for every particle. Differentiating twice,
>
> $$
> \dot{\mathbf x}_a = \dot{\mathbf x}'_a + \mathbf g\,t, \qquad \ddot{\mathbf x}_a = \ddot{\mathbf x}'_a + \mathbf g .
> $$
>
> The shift $\tfrac12\mathbf g t^2$ and the velocity shift $\mathbf g t$ are the same for every particle, so they cancel in differences:
>
> $$
> \mathbf x_a - \mathbf x_b = \mathbf x'_a - \mathbf x'_b, \qquad \dot{\mathbf x}_a - \dot{\mathbf x}_b = \dot{\mathbf x}'_a - \dot{\mathbf x}'_b ,
> $$
>
> and $\mathbf F_a$, which depends only on these, takes the same values in both frames.
>
> **Step 3** (the gravitational term cancels). Insert Step 2 into Step 1:
>
> $$
> m_{I,a}\bigl(\ddot{\mathbf x}'_a + \mathbf g\bigr) = \mathbf F_a + m_{G,a}\,\mathbf g \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_{I,a}\,\ddot{\mathbf x}'_a = \mathbf F_a + \bigl(m_{G,a} - m_{I,a}\bigr)\,\mathbf g .
> $$
>
> With $m_{G,a} = m_{I,a}$ the last term vanishes for every $a$: part 1. If it is nonzero for some particle, that particle keeps the force $(m_{G,a} - m_{I,a})\mathbf g$ while the others feel none: part 4.
>
> **Step 4** (an accelerated frame without gravity). Now $S$ has no field: $m_a\ddot{\mathbf x}_a = \mathbf F_a$. The origin of $S'$ is at $\mathbf R(t) = \mathbf R_0 + \mathbf V_0t + \tfrac12\mathbf A t^2$, so $\mathbf x_a = \mathbf x'_a + \mathbf R(t)$ and $\ddot{\mathbf x}_a = \ddot{\mathbf x}'_a + \mathbf A$. As in Step 2, $\mathbf F_a$ is unchanged, and
>
> $$
> m_a\bigl(\ddot{\mathbf x}'_a + \mathbf A\bigr) = \mathbf F_a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_a\,\ddot{\mathbf x}'_a = \mathbf F_a - m_a\mathbf A = \mathbf F_a + m_a\,(-\mathbf A) .
> $$
>
> The extra term is the translational fictitious force $-m\ddot{\mathbf R}$ of [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|CM Theorem §B9.1.5]] (with $\boldsymbol\omega = 0$). It has the form $m_a\mathbf g$ of Step 1 with $\mathbf g = -\mathbf A$ and $m_G = m_I = m_a$: part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (indistinguishable). In each pair of situations the particles obey the same second-order equations; with the same initial positions and velocities the solutions coincide ([[§68 Local Existence and Uniqueness#^thm-68-1|591 Thm. §68.1]], as in [[#^der-c1-3-1|the derivation of Theorem §C1.3.1]], Step 3). Every mechanical observation is a function of these motions: part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The transformation $\mathbf x' = \mathbf x - \frac12\mathbf g t^2$ is not a symmetry of Newtonian mechanics without gravity (Step 4 produces a new term), but with a uniform field it maps the theory onto itself with the field switched off. That works only because the fictitious force and the gravitational force are both proportional to the same mass (Step 3).

> [!definition] Definition §C1.3.1: Freely Falling Frame
> A reference frame of Newtonian mechanics is an origin moving on a path $t \mapsto \mathbf R(t)$ in space, a Cartesian basis of space, and the absolute time $t$. A **freely falling frame** is a frame such that
> 1. its origin moves as a free test body: $\ddot{\mathbf R}(t) = -\nabla\Phi\bigl(t, \mathbf R(t)\bigr)$;
> 2. it does not rotate: its basis vectors are those of an inertial frame, constant in time.
>
> Its coordinates are $\mathbf x' = \mathbf x - \mathbf R(t)$, $t' = t$, where $\mathbf x$ are the coordinates of an inertial frame. In a uniform field $\mathbf R(t) = \mathbf R_0 + \mathbf V_0t + \tfrac12\mathbf g t^2$, which is the frame $S'$ of [[#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]], part 1. Physically: a closed laboratory with gravity as the only force on it (a lift in an evacuated shaft, a spacecraft in orbit), whose axes are held fixed by gyroscopes. The relativistic version, an observer on a free worldline with non-rotating axes carried along it, is set up in [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]].
>
> *Model:* Newtonian gravity ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]); *applies:* any potential $\Phi$; the frame is a coordinate system on all of space, but only near its origin do free bodies look unaccelerated in it ([[#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §9.4 (non-rotating free fall, P3), p. 163; §10.5, eq. (10.30) (gyroscopes) · Hartle, Gravity, §6.2, p. 111 · Schutz §5.1, pp. 114–116*

> [!definition] Definition §C1.3.2: Local Inertial Frame
> A **local inertial frame** at an event $(t_0, \mathbf x_0)$ is a freely falling frame ([[#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]]) whose origin passes through $\mathbf x_0$ at $t_0$, used only in a region of size $L$ about its origin and during a time interval $T$ about $t_0$ so small that the non-uniformity of the field is undetectable there: free test bodies released inside it at rest relative to each other stay at rest relative to each other, within the resolution $\delta$ of the experiments performed. Within these bounds free bodies move on straight lines at constant velocity, and the frame plays the part of an inertial frame of special relativity.
>
> How small $L$ and $T$ must be is fixed by the tidal tensor $E_{ij}$ ([[#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]]) at the origin: free bodies a distance $L$ apart drift by about $\tfrac12|E|\,LT^2$ in a time $T$ ([[#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]]), with $|E|$ the largest magnitude of an eigenvalue of $E_{ij}$, so the condition is $\tfrac12|E|\,LT^2 < \delta$. Near the Earth's surface $|E| = 2GM_\oplus/R_\oplus^3 = 3.07 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$ ([[#^cau-c1-3-1|Caution: "Equivalence" is local]]). The precise mathematical form, coordinates in which the metric at a point is $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ and its first derivatives vanish, is [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-4|Def. §C3.2.4]] (existence: [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]]; normal coordinates: [[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]]).
>
> *Model:* Newtonian gravity ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]); *applies:* any field; the bounds depend on the field and on the experiment.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §9.4 ("locally" = a region over which the variation of the field is unobservably small), p. 163 · Schutz §5.1, Fig. 5.3, p. 116 · Hartle, Gravity, §6.3 (statement and footnote 5), p. 119 · Couzens, GR1, §2.2.1, p. 25*

> [!principle] Principle §C1.3.5: The Einstein Equivalence Principle
> 1. The weak equivalence principle holds ([[#^pr-c1-3-3|Principle §C1.3.3]]).
> 2. In a local inertial frame ([[#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]]) the result of every non-gravitational experiment is the same as the result of the same experiment in an inertial frame of special relativity, in Minkowski spacetime ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]).
>
> Part 2 contains two independences (d'Inverno & Vickers): the result does not depend on the velocity of the freely falling frame (**local Lorentz invariance**), and it does not depend on where and when in the universe the experiment is done (**local position invariance**). In words (Hartle): experiments in a sufficiently small freely falling laboratory, over a sufficiently short time, give the results of the same experiments in an inertial frame in empty space.
>
> *Domain: experiments that do not involve gravity: mechanics with non-gravitational forces, electromagnetism and light, atomic and nuclear physics, clocks; performed within the bounds of a local inertial frame, with fields that do not reach outside it (Schutz). Experiments in which gravity itself is measured, such as the attraction between masses inside the lab or the fall of bodies held together by their own gravity, are outside it ([[#^rem-c1-3-1|Remark: The strong equivalence principle]]). Layer: in special relativity the principle of relativity ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-1|Principle §A1.3.1]]) and the principle of covariance ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^pr-b2-2-9|Principle §B2.2.9]], signature $+{-}{-}{-}$ there) hold in global inertial frames. In a gravitational field only local ones exist ([[#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]]; no global one at rest on the Earth: [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-7|Theorem §C1.4.7]]), and this principle carries special relativity into each of them. Tested part by part: part 1 by [[#^law-c1-3-2|Law §C1.3.2]]; local Lorentz invariance by the tests of special relativity; local position invariance by clocks compared at different heights ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^law-c1-4-5|Law §C1.4.5]]).*
>
> *Model:* any non-gravitational physics of special relativity (Relativity level B, Electromagnetism, Quantum Field Theory) in a local inertial frame; *applies:* as in the Domain line.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §2.2.1 (boxed statement, p. 25) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §16.4, points 1–3, pp. 294–295 · Hartle, Gravity, §6.2, p. 113; §6.3, p. 119 · Schutz §5.1, p. 115; §7.1, Postulate IV′, p. 172*

^pr-c1-3-5

[[#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]] was proved for particles; the Einstein principle asserts it for all of physics. Couzens notes that part of this extension is already tested: ordinary test bodies are held together by electromagnetic and nuclear forces, and their universal fall ([[#^law-c1-3-2|Law §C1.3.2]]) shows that those forces too are unaffected by free fall.

## Tidal accelerations: what no frame removes

Can the Earth's field be removed in the same way? Take a freely falling frame ([[#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]]) with origin at $\mathbf R(t)$. A free body at $\mathbf x = \mathbf R + \mathbf x'$ has, in that frame,

$$
\ddot{\mathbf x}' = \ddot{\mathbf x} - \ddot{\mathbf R} = -\nabla\Phi\bigl(t, \mathbf R + \mathbf x'\bigr) + \nabla\Phi\bigl(t, \mathbf R\bigr) .
$$

For every $\mathbf x'$ this vanishes only if $\nabla\Phi$ is the same at all points, which is the uniform field of [[#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]]. For the Earth, $\Phi = -M_\oplus/r$, it does not vanish. Two lifts falling down shafts on opposite sides of the Earth each accelerate toward the centre, in opposite directions; their relative acceleration is $2g = 19.6\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, and no single frame makes both unaccelerated. Even two bodies 1 m apart near the surface accelerate relative to each other: by $1.54 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ toward each other if they are side by side, by $3.07 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ apart if one is above the other (numbers from Theorem §C1.3.6 below). This residue is what is left of gravity after free fall, and it is governed by the second derivatives of $\Phi$.

> [!definition] Definition §C1.3.3: Tidal Tensor
> At each time $t$ and point $\mathbf x$ of space, the **tidal tensor** is the symmetric bilinear form on displacement vectors given by the second derivatives (the Hessian) of the gravitational potential:
>
> $$
> E_{ij}(t, \mathbf x) \equiv \partial_i\partial_j\Phi(t, \mathbf x), \qquad i, j = 1, 2, 3 .
> $$
>
> In a Cartesian basis it is a symmetric $3 \times 3$ matrix, $E_{ij} = E_{ji}$; under a rotation $O$ of the axes it changes as $E \mapsto OEO^{\mathsf T}$, the law of a $(0, 2)$ tensor of Euclidean space, so it has three real eigenvalues and orthogonal principal axes. It has the dimension of $(\text{time})^{-2}$: restored, $\Phi$ is in $\mathrm{J/kg}$ and $E_{ij}$ in $\mathrm s^{-2}$; with $G = c = 1$, in $(\text{length})^{-2}$.
>
> *Model:* Newtonian gravity ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]); *applies:* wherever $\Phi$ is twice differentiable. Its relativistic replacement is the curvature component $R^i{}_{0j0}$ of geodesic deviation, [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-2|Theorem §C3.12.2]] (observer form: [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^def-c3-12-4|Def. §C3.12.4]]).
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §10.2, eq. (10.9), p. 173 (written $K^\alpha{}_\beta$ there) · Couzens, GR1, §2.2.1, p. 25 (tidal forces, made precise in its §4.4.5)*

^def-c1-3-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.3.6: Tidal Accelerations Cannot Be Transformed Away
> Let two free test bodies move in the potential $\Phi$, at $\mathbf x(t)$ and $\mathbf x(t) + \boldsymbol\xi(t)$, with $|\boldsymbol\xi|$ small compared with the distance over which $E_{ij}$ ([[#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]]) changes.
> 1. Their separation obeys
>
> $$
> \ddot\xi^i = -E_{ij}\bigl(t, \mathbf x(t)\bigr)\,\xi^j + O\bigl(|\boldsymbol\xi|^2\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> with $E$ evaluated on the worldline of the first body, at each time.
> 2. The same equation holds in every non-rotating frame $\mathbf x' = \mathbf x - \mathbf R(t)$, whatever $\mathbf R(t)$: the separation and its acceleration are frame-independent. So if $E_{ij} \neq 0$ at a body's position, no such frame, freely falling or not, makes both bodies unaccelerated.
> 3. $E_{ij} = 0$ throughout a connected region if and only if the field $\mathbf g = -\nabla\Phi$ is uniform there. Only then does [[#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]] remove the field in the whole region.
> 4. For a point mass $M$ (or outside a spherical body, [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-7|CM Theorem §A8.1.7]]) at distance $r$, with $\mathbf n$ the unit vector away from the mass,
>
> $$
> E_{ij} = \frac{M}{r^3}\bigl(\delta_{ij} - 3n_in_j\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> so a separation along $\mathbf n$ grows and one across $\mathbf n$ shrinks:
>
> $$
> \ddot\xi_\parallel = +\frac{2M}{r^3}\,\xi_\parallel , \qquad \ddot\xi_\perp = -\frac{M}{r^3}\,\xi_\perp ; \qquad\text{restored:}\quad \ddot\xi_\parallel = +\frac{2GM}{r^3}\,\xi_\parallel ,\quad \ddot\xi_\perp = -\frac{GM}{r^3}\,\xi_\perp .
> $$
>
> *Model:* Newtonian gravity ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]), two test bodies; *applies:* to first order in $|\boldsymbol\xi|$, for all times provided $E$ is taken along the first body's worldline; with $E$ frozen at its initial value only for times short compared with the time over which $E$ changes along the path ([[#^ex-c1-3-2|Example §C1.3.2]]). The bodies' mutual attraction is neglected.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §10.1–10.2, eqs. (10.4)–(10.10), pp. 171–173 · Couzens, GR1, §2.2.1, p. 25 · Schutz §5.1, pp. 116–117 · Hartle, Gravity, Ex. 6.3, p. 120*

^thm-c1-3-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the two equations of motion). Each body is free ([[#^pr-c1-3-3|Principle §C1.3.3]]):
>
> $$
> \ddot x^i = -\partial_i\Phi\bigl(t, \mathbf x\bigr), \qquad \ddot x^i + \ddot\xi^i = -\partial_i\Phi\bigl(t, \mathbf x + \boldsymbol\xi\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (Taylor expansion of the second force about $\mathbf x$). To first order in $\boldsymbol\xi$, with a remainder quadratic in $\boldsymbol\xi$ ([[§11 Taylor's Theorem for Multivariable Functions#^thm-11-2|452 Thm. §11.2]], stated there in two variables; the same expansion holds in three):
>
> $$
> \partial_i\Phi\bigl(t, \mathbf x + \boldsymbol\xi\bigr) = \partial_i\Phi(t, \mathbf x) + \partial_j\partial_i\Phi(t, \mathbf x)\,\xi^j + O\bigl(|\boldsymbol\xi|^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (subtract). Subtract the first equation of Step 1 from the second and insert Step 2:
>
> $$
> \ddot\xi^i = -\partial_i\Phi(t, \mathbf x) - \partial_j\partial_i\Phi(t, \mathbf x)\,\xi^j + \partial_i\Phi(t, \mathbf x) + O\bigl(|\boldsymbol\xi|^2\bigr) = -E_{ij}(t, \mathbf x)\,\xi^j + O\bigl(|\boldsymbol\xi|^2\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> using $\partial_j\partial_i\Phi = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi = E_{ij}$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (any non-rotating frame). In $\mathbf x' = \mathbf x - \mathbf R(t)$ the two bodies are at $\mathbf x - \mathbf R$ and $\mathbf x + \boldsymbol\xi - \mathbf R$, so the separation is
>
> $$
> \boldsymbol\xi' = (\mathbf x + \boldsymbol\xi - \mathbf R) - (\mathbf x - \mathbf R) = \boldsymbol\xi , \qquad \ddot{\boldsymbol\xi}' = \ddot{\boldsymbol\xi} .
> $$
>
> The accelerations of the two bodies in the frame are $\ddot{\mathbf x}'$ and $\ddot{\mathbf x}' + \ddot{\boldsymbol\xi}$. Both vanish only if $\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi} = 0$. If $E \neq 0$, the symmetric matrix $E$ has a nonzero eigenvalue $e$ with eigenvector $\mathbf u$; for $\boldsymbol\xi$ along $\mathbf u$, Step 3 gives $\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi} = -e\,\boldsymbol\xi + O(|\boldsymbol\xi|^2) \neq 0$ for small $\boldsymbol\xi$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (uniform field). $E_{ij} = \partial_j(\partial_i\Phi) = -\partial_jg_i$. If all these vanish on a connected region, each $g_i$ has zero gradient there and is constant in $\mathbf x$ (mean-value theorem along polygonal paths in the region); conversely a uniform $\mathbf g$ has $\partial_jg_i = 0$. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 6** (point mass). With $\Phi = -M/r$, $r = (x_kx_k)^{1/2}$ and $\partial_jr = x_j/r$:
>
> $$
> \partial_i\Phi = \frac{M x_i}{r^3}, \qquad \partial_j\partial_i\Phi = M\Bigl(\frac{\delta_{ij}}{r^3} - \frac{3x_ix_j}{r^5}\Bigr) = \frac{M}{r^3}\bigl(\delta_{ij} - 3n_in_j\bigr), \qquad n_i = \frac{x_i}{r} .
> $$
>
> For $\boldsymbol\xi = \xi_\parallel\mathbf n$: $E_{ij}\xi^j = \frac{M}{r^3}(1 - 3)\,\xi_\parallel n_i = -\frac{2M}{r^3}\xi_\parallel n_i$, so $\ddot\xi_\parallel = +2M\xi_\parallel/r^3$. For $\boldsymbol\xi \perp \mathbf n$: $n_j\xi^j = 0$, $E_{ij}\xi^j = \frac{M}{r^3}\xi^i$, so $\ddot\xi_\perp = -M\xi_\perp/r^3$. Restoring $G$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]: $M \to GM$) gives the last display of part 4. A symbolic check confirms the matrix and the expansion of Step 2 symbolically.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Passing to an accelerated frame adds the same vector to every acceleration and so removes $\nabla\Phi$ at one point; it cannot touch the differences between accelerations, which are governed by $E_{ij}$. The frame-independent content of a gravitational field is its tidal tensor. Level A met the same quantity as the difference of pulls across a body ([[§A8.4 Tidal Forces#^def-a8-4-1|CM Def. §A8.4.1]]; [[§A8.4 Tidal Forces#^thm-a8-4-1|CM Theorem §A8.4.1]]: near minus far side $= 4GMR/r^3$ per unit mass, which is $\ddot\xi_\parallel$ of part 4 with $\xi_\parallel = 2R$), and level B as a force in the Earth's freely falling frame ([[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^thm-b9-2-7|CM Theorem §B9.2.7]], the same matrix as Step 6). New here: the general $\Phi$, and the fact that no frame removes it.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.3.7: In Vacuum the Tidal Tensor Is Traceless
> 1. The trace of the tidal tensor ([[#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]]) is the mass density:
>
> $$
> E_{ii} = \nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi\rho \qquad (\text{restored: } 4\pi G\rho),
> $$
>
> so in vacuum ($\rho = 0$ at the point) $E_{ii} = 0$: the eigenvalues add to zero, and stretching along one axis is paid for by squeezing along the others. For a point mass they are $(-2, 1, 1)\,M/r^3$.
> 2. A small ball of free test particles released at rest relative to its centre has volume
>
> $$
> V(t) = V(0)\,\bigl[1 - 2\pi\rho\,t^2 + O(t^3)\bigr] ,
> $$
>
> with $\rho$ the density at the centre at release. In vacuum the volume is constant to this order: the ball begins to turn into an ellipsoid of the same volume. The statement is about the start of the deformation; once the particles move relative to the centre, terms quadratic in their relative velocities make even a vacuum ball shrink (computed in the animation below).
>
> *Model:* Newtonian gravity ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]]), free test particles; *applies:* to first order in the size of the ball, for times short compared with the time over which $E$ changes along the centre's path; the particles' mutual attraction is neglected.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §10.2, eq. (10.11), p. 173 (Laplace's equation as $K^\alpha{}_\alpha = 0$) · d'Inverno & Vickers, ex. 9.6 (sphere of free particles falling toward the Earth becomes an ellipsoid), p. 168 · part 2 written out here*

^thm-c1-3-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (trace). By [[#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]] and the field equation of [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]],
>
> $$
> E_{ii} = \partial_1\partial_1\Phi + \partial_2\partial_2\Phi + \partial_3\partial_3\Phi = \nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi\rho .
> $$
>
> For a point mass the matrix of [[#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]], part 4, has eigenvalue $\frac{M}{r^3}(1 - 3) = -\frac{2M}{r^3}$ on $\mathbf n$ and $\frac{M}{r^3}$ on the plane $\perp\mathbf n$; the sum is $(-2 + 1 + 1)M/r^3 = 0$, consistent with $\rho = 0$ off the mass. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (the ball as a linear image). Label each particle by its initial displacement $\boldsymbol\xi_0$ from the centre. By [[#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]], part 1, $\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi} = -E(t)\boldsymbol\xi$ to first order: a linear equation, so its solution is linear in the initial data. With $\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}(0) = 0$ (released at rest relative to the centre),
>
> $$
> \boldsymbol\xi(t) = J(t)\,\boldsymbol\xi_0, \qquad \ddot J = -E(t)\,J, \qquad J(0) = \mathbb 1, \qquad \dot J(0) = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (Taylor expansion of $J$). From the initial data, $\ddot J(0) = -E(0)$, so
>
> $$
> J(t) = \mathbb 1 - \tfrac12E(0)\,t^2 + O(t^3) .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (volume). The ball at time $t$ is the image of the initial ball under the linear map $J(t)$, so its volume is $|\det J(t)|\,V(0)$ ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-61|LADR Thm. 9.61]]). For a matrix $A$ of size $O(t^2)$, expanding the determinant (product of the diagonal entries plus terms with at least two off-diagonal entries) gives $\det(\mathbb 1 + A) = 1 + \operatorname{tr}A + O(|A|^2)$. With $A = -\frac12E(0)t^2 + O(t^3)$,
>
> $$
> \det J(t) = 1 - \tfrac12E_{ii}(0)\,t^2 + O(t^3) = 1 - 2\pi\rho\,t^2 + O(t^3) ,
> $$
>
> by Step 1. This is part 2. (For a point mass with $E$ held fixed, the axes of the ball are $\cosh(\sqrt2\,\omega t)$ along $\mathbf n$ and $\cos(\omega t)$ twice across, $\omega^2 = M/r^3$; their product is $1 - \frac12\omega^4t^4 + \dots$, constant through order $t^2$ as stated. A plane section through $\mathbf n$ has area factor $\cosh(\sqrt2\,\omega t)\cos(\omega t) = 1 + \frac12\omega^2t^2 + \dots$: it is the volume, not the area of a section, that is kept. Both series were checked symbolically.)
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The vacuum field equation $\nabla^2\Phi = 0$ is a statement about tides: it says the tidal tensor has zero trace, so a falling ball keeps its volume. Inside matter the ball shrinks at the rate set by the density. This is the form in which the field equation of general relativity will be guessed: vacuum is where a certain trace of the curvature vanishes ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^thm-c5-1-6|Theorem §C5.1.6]]).

> [!caution] Caution: "Equivalence" is local
> - [[#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]] removes a *uniform* field everywhere at once. A real field can be removed only near one point, along one worldline ([[#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]]). The Earth's field is radial: it points toward the centre from every side, and lifts falling on opposite sides of the Earth accelerate toward each other at $2g = 19.6\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
> - Near the surface, for two bodies 1 m apart: $E$ has eigenvalue magnitudes $2GM_\oplus/R_\oplus^3 = 3.07 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$ (vertical) and $GM_\oplus/R_\oplus^3 = 1.54 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$ (horizontal). After a 1 s fall a vertical pair has separated by $1.5 \times 10^{-6}$ m. The time scale on which tides become order one is $(GM_\oplus/R_\oplus^3)^{-1/2} = 807$ s.
> - Two bodies falling side by side converge because they fall toward the same centre along different lines, not because they attract each other; their mutual gravity is a separate, here neglected, effect (Couzens).
> - A gravitational field is therefore not "equivalent to acceleration" without qualification. What is equivalent to acceleration is the field at one point; its variation is real and frame-independent, and in general relativity it becomes the curvature of spacetime ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-2|Theorem §C3.12.2]]).
>

## Light falls

Newtonian mechanics has no equation of motion for light in a gravitational field: there is no $\mathbf F = m_I\mathbf a$ to apply to a photon. The Einstein principle supplies the answer without one.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.3.8: Light Falls in a Gravitational Field
> A laboratory is at rest in a uniform field of strength $g$, pointing down (or, equivalently by [[#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]], is accelerated upward at $g$ far from all masses). A light ray enters horizontally through one wall at height $z_0$ and crosses the width $L$, with $gL \ll 1$. Then:
> 1. relative to the laboratory the ray follows the parabola
>
> $$
> z(x) = z_0 - \tfrac12\,g\,x^2 ,
> $$
>
> the path of a body launched horizontally with speed $1$: it falls with the same acceleration $g$ as matter, reaches the far wall lower by $d = \tfrac12gL^2$ and leaves at an angle $gL$ below the horizontal. Restored: $d = gL^2/(2c^2)$, angle $gL/c^2$;
> 2. on the Earth's surface, for $L = 2$ m: $d = 2.18 \times 10^{-16}$ m and the angle is $2.18 \times 10^{-16}$ rad. A drop of 1 mm would need $L = 4.28 \times 10^6$ m $= 0.67\,R_\oplus$, over which the field is far from uniform, so the effect cannot be shown in a laboratory on the Earth.
>
> *Model:* a uniform gravitational field ([[#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]]) and special relativity in the freely falling frame ([[#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]); *applies:* to first order in $gL$ (restored $gL/c^2$), across a laboratory small enough to count as a local inertial frame ([[#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]]). The bending of light along a whole path past a star is not obtained from this alone ([[#^cau-c1-3-2|Caution: The equivalence principle gives only part of the bending by the Sun]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §2.2.1 ("Implications"), pp. 25–26 · Hartle, Gravity, §6.2, Fig. 6.5, p. 113; Prob. 6.3, p. 132 · d'Inverno & Vickers, ex. 9.8, p. 168*

^thm-c1-3-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (coordinates). Lab coordinates $(x, z)$, $x$ across the lab, $z$ up; the field is $\mathbf g = -g\,\hat{\mathbf z}$. Let $S'$ be the freely falling frame ([[#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]]) that coincides with the lab at $t = 0$ and is at rest relative to it at that moment: its origin is at $\mathbf R(t) = -\tfrac12gt^2\,\hat{\mathbf z}$, so
>
> $$
> x' = x, \qquad z' = z - R_z(t) = z + \tfrac12gt^2 .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the ray in the freely falling frame). $S'$ is a local inertial frame for the crossing ([[#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]]); by [[#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]], light in it behaves as in special relativity, moving on a straight line at speed $1$ ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-2|Principle §A1.3.2]]). The ray enters horizontally at $t = 0$, when $S'$ and the lab agree:
>
> $$
> x'(t) = t, \qquad z'(t) = z_0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (back to the lab). Invert Step 1 and insert Step 2:
>
> $$
> x(t) = t, \qquad z(t) = z'(t) - \tfrac12gt^2 = z_0 - \tfrac12gt^2 .
> $$
>
> Eliminate $t = x$: $z(x) = z_0 - \tfrac12gx^2$. At the far wall, $x = L$, the drop is $d = \tfrac12gL^2$, and the slope is $dz/dx = -gx = -gL$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (what was neglected). The relation of Step 1 is the Galilean one. During the crossing $S'$ moves relative to the lab with speed $gt \le gL$; the Lorentz transformation differs from the Galilean one by terms of relative order $(gL)^2$, and points of a rigid accelerated lab at different heights have proper accelerations differing at relative order $gL$ (Hartle, Prob. 6.7; [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^mod-c1-4-1|Model §C1.4.1]]). Both are beyond first order in $gL$.
>
> **Step 5** (the accelerated rocket). For a lab accelerated upward at $g$ in empty space, take $S'$ to be the inertial frame in which the lab is momentarily at rest at $t = 0$; Steps 1–3 are unchanged. The two cases agree, as [[#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]] (for the lab's mechanics) and [[#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]] (for light) require.
>
> **Step 6** (numbers). Restored, $d = gL^2/(2c^2)$. With $g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $L = 2$ m: $d = 9.81 \times 4/(2 \times 8.99 \times 10^{16})\ \mathrm m = 2.18 \times 10^{-16}$ m. Solving $gL^2/(2c^2) = 10^{-3}$ m for $L$: $L = c\sqrt{2 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm m/g} = 4.28 \times 10^6$ m $= 0.67\,R_\oplus$ (checked by script).
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The path of light is frame-dependent in the same way as the path of a thrown ball: straight in the freely falling frame, a parabola in the frame at rest in the field. The Einstein principle fixes the behaviour of light in a gravitational field without any force law for light.

> [!caution] Caution: The equivalence principle gives only part of the bending by the Sun
> It is tempting to treat light passing the Sun as a body moving at speed $1$ that falls with the local acceleration $-\nabla\Phi$ all along its path, as [[#^thm-c1-3-8|Theorem §C1.3.8]] does across one lab. That gives a deflection $2M/b$ for a ray passing at distance $b$: Cavendish (1784), von Soldner (1801) and Einstein (1911; Schutz gives 1908) obtained it so (Schutz; the 1911 paper: A. Einstein, *Ann. Phys.* 35 (1911) 898–908, not in the typed sources). General relativity gives twice that, $4M/b$, which is $1.75''$ at the Sun's limb (Hartle; checked by script), derived in [[§C6.5 The Bending of Light#^thm-c6-5-2|Theorem §C6.5.2]]. The other half comes from the spatial part of the metric, which matters for a ray because its spatial steps are as large as its time steps (Schutz). In these notes the factor 2 first appears in the coordinate speed of light of the weak-field metric ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]]). The equivalence principle describes light in each local inertial frame; joining the frames along a whole path takes the geometry of [[· C3 Metric, Connection and Curvature|C3]].
>

## The strong form, and the names

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The strong equivalence principle
> The weak principle ([[#^pr-c1-3-3|Principle §C1.3.3]]) is about test bodies, whose own gravity is negligible, and the Einstein principle ([[#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]) about experiments that do not involve gravity. Neither says how a body held together by its own gravity falls, or what a gravitational experiment inside a freely falling lab finds. The extension to these cases is usually called the strong equivalence principle; d'Inverno & Vickers describe the Einstein principle as lying "between the weak and strong versions" (§16.4). Lunar laser ranging bears on it. The gravitational binding energy of a uniform sphere is a fraction $3GM/(5Rc^2)$ of its rest energy: about $4.2 \times 10^{-10}$ for the Earth and $1.9 \times 10^{-11}$ for the Moon (uniform-density estimates, checked by script; Hartle, Prob. 3.6, asks for the Moon's). Both are far above the accuracy $1.5 \times 10^{-13}$ with which the Earth and the Moon are seen to fall alike toward the Sun ([[#^law-c1-3-2|Law §C1.3.2]]). So the test also shows that gravitational binding energy falls like other mass-energy: a body whose binding energy fell differently by a fraction $\delta$ would change its acceleration by $\delta$ times its binding fraction, and for the Earth this bounds $\delta$ at roughly $1.5 \times 10^{-13}/4.2 \times 10^{-10} \approx 4 \times 10^{-4}$ (an estimate made here, not in the sources).
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §16.4, p. 294 · Hartle, Gravity, Box 2.1, p. 14; Prob. 3.6, p. 46*

^rem-c1-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Names of the equivalence principles
> - d'Inverno & Vickers (§9.4) call the composition-independence of free fall (their P1) the **strong** form and "the gravitational field is coupled to everything" (their P2) the **weak** form. Their own §16.4, Couzens, Hartle and Schutz call the content of P1 the **weak** equivalence principle. These notes follow the majority: [[#^pr-c1-3-3|Principle §C1.3.3]] is the weak principle, and "strong" is reserved for [[#^rem-c1-3-1|Remark: The strong equivalence principle]].
> - d'Inverno & Vickers' P3 (no local experiment distinguishes non-rotating free fall from uniform motion far from masses) and P4 (a frame linearly accelerated in special relativity is locally identical to a frame at rest in a gravitational field) are parts 1 and 2 of [[#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]] extended from mechanics to all experiments, which is [[#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]].
> - Hartle uses "equivalence principle" for the Einstein form (§6.2–6.3). Schutz states the weak principle geometrically, "freely falling particles move on timelike geodesics" (Postulate IV), and notes in a footnote that it is more commonly stated as universality of free fall, as here.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §9.4, pp. 162–163; §16.4, p. 294 · Couzens, GR1, §2.2.1, p. 25 · Hartle, Gravity, pp. 113, 119 · Schutz §7.1, p. 171*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C1.3.1: The Eötvös Torsion Balance
> *Question.* A balance at rest in a laboratory feels the Earth's gravity and the centrifugal effect of the Earth's rotation. How does this let it detect a difference of $m_I/m_G$ between two materials, and how large is the signal?
>
> *Setup.* Laboratory at latitude $\theta$, at rest on the Earth. Sidereal rotation rate $\omega = 2\pi/(86\,164\ \mathrm s) = 7.29 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, radius $R = 6.378 \times 10^6$ m, $GM_\oplus = 3.986 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}$. In the Earth's rotating frame a body $i$ feels gravity, proportional to $m_G$, and the centrifugal force, proportional to $m_I$ ([[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|CM Theorem §B9.1.5]]). Write $q_i = m_{I,i}/m_{G,i}$ ('t Hooft's ratio).
>
> **Step 1** (the force on body $i$). With $\hat{\mathbf r}$ the local vertical and $\mathbf r_\perp$ the vector from the rotation axis to the body, $|\mathbf r_\perp| = R\cos\theta$,
>
> $$
> \mathbf F^{(i)} = m_{G,i}\Bigl[-\frac{GM_\oplus}{R^2}\,\hat{\mathbf r} + q_i\,\omega^2\,\mathbf r_\perp\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (vertical and horizontal parts of the centrifugal term). Take axes with $\hat{\mathbf z}$ along the rotation axis and the body in the $xz$-plane: $\hat{\mathbf r} = (\cos\theta, 0, \sin\theta)$, the horizontal unit vector pointing north is $\hat{\mathbf e}_N = (-\sin\theta, 0, \cos\theta)$, and $\mathbf r_\perp = (R\cos\theta, 0, 0)$. Then
>
> $$
> \cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf r} - \sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf e}_N = (\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta,\ 0,\ \cos\theta\sin\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta) = (1, 0, 0), \qquad \mathbf r_\perp = R\cos\theta\,\bigl(\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf r} - \sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf e}_N\bigr) .
> $$
>
> So the centrifugal acceleration has a vertical part $\omega^2R\cos^2\theta$ (up) and a horizontal part of size $\omega^2R\cos\theta\sin\theta$, pointing toward the equator.
>
> **Step 3** (direction of the force). The force points at an angle $\delta_i$ from straight down, with
>
> $$
> \tan\delta_i = \frac{q_i\,\omega^2R\cos\theta\sin\theta}{GM_\oplus/R^2 - q_i\,\omega^2R\cos^2\theta} = q_i\,\frac{\omega^2R^3}{GM_\oplus}\cos\theta\sin\theta\,\Bigl[1 + O\Bigl(\frac{\omega^2R^3}{GM_\oplus}\Bigr)\Bigr], \qquad \frac{GM_\oplus}{\omega^2R^3} = 289
> $$
>
> ('t Hooft: "$\approx 300$").
>
> **Step 4** (misalignment of two materials). Two bodies hang along directions that differ by
>
> $$
> \alpha = |\delta_1 - \delta_2| = |q_1 - q_2|\,\frac{\cos\theta\sin\theta}{289} .
> $$
>
> With $q = 1/\kappa$ and $\kappa \approx 1$, $q_1 - q_2 = (\kappa_2 - \kappa_1)/(\kappa_1\kappa_2) = -(\kappa_1 - \kappa_2)[1 + O(\eta)]$, so $|q_1 - q_2| = |\eta|$ to first order, with $\eta$ the Eötvös parameter of [[#^law-c1-3-2|Law §C1.3.2]].
>
> **Step 5** (numbers at $\theta = 47^\circ$, the latitude of Seattle, where Su et al. worked; Hartle, Prob. 6.1). $\cos\theta\sin\theta = 0.4988$. The fibre itself hangs off the line to the Earth's centre by $\delta = 0.4988/289 = 1.73 \times 10^{-3}$ rad $= 0.099^\circ$; the horizontal acceleration it compensates is $\omega^2R\cos\theta\sin\theta = 0.0169\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. The misalignment of two materials is $\alpha = 1.73 \times 10^{-3}\,|\eta|$: $1.7 \times 10^{-12}$ rad for $|\eta| = 10^{-9}$ (Eötvös), $1.7 \times 10^{-15}$ rad for $|\eta| = 10^{-12}$ (Su et al.).
>
> **Step 6** (the twist). Hang the two bodies, of equal mass $m$, on a rod of length $l$ pointing east–west. Relative to the fibre, which hangs along their mean force, their north–south force components are $\pm\frac12mg\alpha$, a couple of torque $N = \tfrac12mg\,l\,\alpha$ about the fibre. With Hartle's data (Prob. 6.2: $m = 10$ g, $l = 4$ cm, torsion constant $2 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{N\,m/rad}$) and $|\eta| = 10^{-12}$: $N = 3.4 \times 10^{-18}\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ and a twist of $N/(2 \times 10^{-8}) = 1.7 \times 10^{-10}$ rad. Turning the balance through $180^\circ$ about the fibre reverses the couple, which separates it from a constant offset; modern balances rotate continuously and look for a twist at the rotation frequency (Hartle §6.1; d'Inverno & Vickers §16.3). All numbers checked by script.
>
> *Source: 't Hooft, ch. 2, eqs. (2.2)–(2.7), pp. 8–9 · Hartle, Gravity, §6.1, Fig. 6.1, pp. 107–109; Probs. 6.1–6.2, pp. 131–132 · d'Inverno & Vickers, §16.3, Fig. 16.4, pp. 293–294*

> [!example] Example §C1.3.2: Ping-Pong Balls in an Orbiting Spacecraft
> *Question* (Hartle, Ex. 6.3). Astronauts in a spacecraft in a circular orbit release two ping-pong balls at rest relative to each other, $s = 1$ m apart along the line to the Earth's centre. How soon does their separation reveal the Earth's field, and what happens over a whole orbit?
>
> *Setup.* Orbit radius $r = R_\oplus + 400$ km $= 6778$ km, the height of [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^ex-a8-1-2|CM Example §A8.1.2]]. Then $\omega = (GM_\oplus/r^3)^{1/2} = 1.131 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, period $P = 2\pi/\omega = 5554$ s $= 92.6$ min, and $g(r) = GM_\oplus/r^2 = 8.68\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. The inner ball is on the circular orbit; both are released with the same velocity in the Earth-centred inertial frame. Air, electrostatic forces and the balls' mutual attraction are ignored.
>
> **Step 1** (initial relative acceleration). The separation is radial, so by [[#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]], part 4 (restored),
>
> $$
> \ddot s = +\frac{2GM_\oplus}{r^3}\,s = 2\omega^2s = \frac{2g\,s}{r} = 2.56 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (short times). With $E$ frozen at its initial value, $\ddot s = 2\omega^2s$, $s(0) = 1$ m, $\dot s(0) = 0$:
>
> $$
> s(t) = s\cosh(\sqrt2\,\omega t) = s\bigl(1 + \omega^2t^2 + \tfrac16\omega^4t^4 + \dots\bigr), \qquad \frac{\delta s}{s} \approx (\omega t)^2 = \Bigl(\frac{2\pi t}{P}\Bigr)^2 .
> $$
>
> This is Hartle's estimate (6.15), here with coefficient exactly 1. The separation has changed by 1 mm when $(\omega t)^2 = 10^{-3}$, after $t = 0.0316/\omega = 28.0$ s ($27.9$ s from the $\cosh$).
>
> **Step 3** (compare with the exact orbits). Integrating both Kepler orbits numerically gives the separations below, next to three approximations: Theorem §C1.3.6 with $E$ frozen ($\cosh$ law), Hartle's quadratic law, and Theorem §C1.3.6 with $E(t)$ taken along the inner ball's orbit, as the theorem states.
>
> | $t$ | exact | $E(t)$ along the orbit | $E$ frozen, $\cosh$ | quadratic $1 + (\omega t)^2$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 28 s | 1.001003 m | 1.001003 m | 1.001004 m | 1.001004 m |
> | 300 s | 1.1142 m | 1.1142 m | 1.1174 m | 1.1152 m |
> | $P/4$ = 1388 s | 3.370 m | 3.370 m | 4.665 m | 3.467 m |
> | $P/2$ = 2777 s | 9.891 m | 9.891 m | 42.5 m | 10.87 m |
> | $P$ = 5554 s | 18.876 m | 18.876 m | 3614 m | 40.5 m |
>
> **Step 4** (reading the table). With $E$ evaluated along the orbit, the linear law of Theorem §C1.3.6 matches the exact orbits to the millimetre for the whole orbit: the theorem needs $s$ small, not $t$. Freezing $E$ fails after a fraction of an orbit, because in the non-rotating frame the stretching axis $\mathbf n$ turns with the radial direction, once per orbit. The one-orbit result also follows from the orbits directly: the outer ball, at $r + s$ with the circular speed of radius $r$, is at the periapsis of an ellipse with semi-major axis $r + 2s$ ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-3|CM Theorem §A8.3.3]]) and apoapsis $r + 3s$; its period is longer by the fraction ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-4|CM Theorem §A8.3.4]], $P \propto a^{3/2}$) $\frac32\cdot\frac{2s}{r} = \frac{3s}{r} = 4.4 \times 10^{-7}$, so after one orbit it trails by $2\pi r \cdot 3s/r = 6\pi s = 18.8$ m along the orbit, one metre above the inner ball: $\sqrt{18.85^2 + 1^2} = 18.88$ m.
>
> **Step 5** (what it shows). In a laboratory as large as a spacecraft, gravity is detectable at the millimetre level within half a minute. A local inertial frame ([[#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]]) is small in time as well as in space.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, Ex. 6.3, eq. (6.15), Fig. 6.8, pp. 120–121; Prob. 6.5 (the outer ball's ellipse), p. 132 · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C1.3.3: Newton's Second Law in Moving Frames
> *Question* (PHY 360, HW 1, Problem 2). I am at rest in an inertial frame and see a particle of mass $m$ move with $x(t) = kt^3$. Will moves past me with constant velocity $v_W$ along $x$; Nalini starts from rest with constant acceleration $a_N$ along $x$. In which frames does the particle obey Newton's second law?
>
> **Step 1** (units). $x = kt^3$ is a length, so $k$ has units $\mathrm{m/s^3}$; then $kt$ is an acceleration and $kt^2$ a velocity.
>
> **Step 2** (inertial frame). $v = dx/dt = 3kt^2$, $a = d^2x/dt^2 = 6kt$, so by [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]] the net force is $F_{\text{net}} = ma = 6mkt$.
>
> **Step 3** (Will's frame). Will's position is $v_Wt$, so the particle is at $x_W(t) = kt^3 - v_Wt$:
>
> $$
> v_W' = 3kt^2 - v_W, \qquad a_W' = 6kt, \qquad m\,a_W' = 6mkt = F_{\text{net}} .
> $$
>
> The second law holds.
>
> **Step 4** (Nalini's frame). Nalini's position is $\tfrac12a_Nt^2$, so the particle is at $x_N(t) = kt^3 - \tfrac12a_Nt^2$:
>
> $$
> v_N' = 3kt^2 - a_Nt, \qquad a_N' = 6kt - a_N, \qquad m\,a_N' = F_{\text{net}} - m\,a_N \neq F_{\text{net}} .
> $$
>
> The second law fails, by the translational fictitious force $-ma_N$ ([[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|CM Theorem §B9.1.5]]).
>
> **Step 5** (summary). The particle obeys Newton's second law in the inertial frame and in every frame moving uniformly relative to it, and not in the accelerated frame. The defect is proportional to the particle's inertial mass. If the force on the particle were gravity in a uniform field $\mathbf g$ with $m_G = m_I$, the defect would be cancelled by the frame with $a_N = g$: that is [[#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]], part 1.
>
> *Source: PHY 360 (audit), HW 1, Problem 2 (statement and typed solution)*

^ex-c1-3-3

The equivalence principle has so far fixed how bodies and light move. It also constrains clocks. If light is sent from the floor to the ceiling of the accelerating rocket of [[#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]], part 2, the ceiling is moving away from the floor's earlier position by the time the light arrives. Does the light arrive with a different frequency, and must a clock at rest in a gravitational field therefore run at a different rate? [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames|§C1.4]] answers this, and draws the conclusion that no global inertial frame exists in a gravitational field.
