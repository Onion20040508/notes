From here on these notes write every formula with the metric $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ and in units with $G = c = 1$. Level B did neither: it used $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1)$ and kept $c$ in every formula ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-3|Def. §B1.1.3]] and [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^cau-b1-1-1|§B1.1, Caution: The speed of light stays explicit]], both in signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), and Quantum Field Theory uses the same signature with $\hbar = c = 1$ ([[§C1a.2 Natural Units and Dimensional Analysis#^def-c1a-2-1|QFT Def. §C1a.2.1]], signature $+{-}{-}{-}$ there). Why change, and how does a formula cross between the levels without a wrong sign or a wrong power of $c$? Two naive transfers show what goes wrong.

- *Units.* [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced|§C1.1]] found that the size of relativistic effects is $GM/(rc^2)$. In units with $G = c = 1$ the Newtonian potential at the Sun's surface reads $\Phi = -M/R$. Inserting the familiar numbers $M = 1.988\times10^{30}$ kg and $R = 6.957\times10^{8}$ m gives $\Phi = -2.858\times10^{21}$ kg/m, a number with a unit and no meaning. The formula is right; the numbers are in the wrong units. With $M$ in metres ($M_\odot = 1477$ m, Example §C1.2.1) it gives $-2.12\times10^{-6}$, the dimensionless compactness of the Sun.
- *Signature.* Level B normalizes the four-velocity by $u\cdot u = c^2$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]], signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). Copied with $c = 1$ into a calculation with the metric above, $u\cdot u = 1$ with $u^\mu = \gamma(1, v, 0, 0)$ would demand $-\gamma^2 + \gamma^2v^2 = \gamma^2(v^2 - 1) = 1$. For $v = 0.6$ the left side is $1.5625\times(-0.64) = -1$, and for every $|v| < 1$ it is $-1$: the imported equation has no solution with a speed below that of light.

This section fixes the two conventions once. It defines geometric units (Def. §C1.2.1) and proves the rule that restores $G$ and $c$ (Theorem §C1.2.1); it defines the metric in the signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ (Def. §C1.2.2) and derives the dictionary between level B and level C (Theorem §C1.2.2), in which every sign change has one cause, the number of metric factors in an expression. Two cautions list the conventions of the sources and the earlier sign changes of levels A and B; four examples convert concrete quantities. The physics resumes in [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle|§C1.3]].

**Setup.** No physical system: units, the overall sign of the metric, and the translation of formulas between conventions. Every box is a convention or exact algebra. The dictionary is stated for flat spacetime in inertial coordinates, where level B lives; the same rule with a general metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ in place of $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ converts curved-spacetime formulas (used for d'Inverno & Vickers, Caution: Conventions of the sources). Only classical quantities built from mass, length and time are covered: $\hbar$ (Remark: Why not ħ as well) and electric charge (Charged Black Holes, chapter C11 (planned)) each need one more convention.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ (Def. §C1.2.2); $G = c = 1$ (restore: Theorem §C1.2.1); $\Phi < 0$ near masses; Greek indices $0$–$3$, Latin $1$–$3$; $x^0 = t$. A superscript or subscript B or C on a symbol says at which level it is meant ($\eta^{\rm B}$, $(u\cdot u)_{\rm C}$).

## Geometric units

Why $G$ and $c$ in particular? In every formula of gravitation they occur only in a few combinations. The speed of light converts times into lengths, $x^0 = ct$, as at level B. Newton's constant enters through $GM$, and in a relativistic formula through $GM/c^2$, which is a length: the Schwarzschild radius is $2GM/c^2$ ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton#^def-a8-5-1|CM Def. §A8.5.1]]), and the relativistic parameter of [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced|§C1.1]] is $GM/(rc^2)$, a ratio of two lengths. Declaring $G/c^2$ and $c$ to be conversion factors, as $c$ already was for times, makes every mass a length and every such ratio visible at a glance. A second reason is precision: planetary and satellite orbits measure $GM$, not $M$, so the geometric mass $GM/c^2$ is known to many more digits than the mass in kilograms (Schutz §8.1).

> [!definition] Definition §C1.2.1: Geometric Units
> **Geometric units** (also geometrized units, $G = c = 1$ units) measure every quantity in powers of the metre by declaring the two conversion factors
>
> $$
> c = 2.998\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = 1, \qquad \frac{G}{c^2} = 7.426\times10^{-28}\ \mathrm{m\,kg^{-1}} = 1
> $$
>
> to be the pure number $1$, so that $G = 1$ too ($G = 6.674\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$, the measured value of [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^law-a8-1-2|CM Law §A8.1.2]]). In the language of [[§C1a.2 Natural Units and Dimensional Analysis#^def-c1a-2-3|QFT Def. §C1a.2.3]], the dimensions $M^\alpha L^\beta T^\gamma$ form the group $\mathbb Z^3$ of exponent triples, and setting $G = c = 1$ identifies all dimensions that differ by powers of $[G] = M^{-1}L^3T^{-2}$ and $[c] = LT^{-1}$: the quotient is $\mathbb Z$, generated by the length. A quantity of SI dimension $M^\alpha L^\beta T^\gamma$ becomes a number times $\mathrm m^{k}$ with $k = \alpha + \beta + \gamma$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]). Conversions:
>
> | Quantity | SI unit | Geometric value | Power of length |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | time | 1 s | $2.998\times10^{8}$ m | $1$ |
> | mass | 1 kg | $7.426\times10^{-28}$ m | $1$ |
> | energy | 1 J | $8.263\times10^{-45}$ m | $1$ |
> | velocity | 1 m/s | $3.336\times10^{-9}$ | $0$ |
> | acceleration | 1 m/s² | $1.113\times10^{-17}$ m⁻¹ | $-1$ |
> | mass density | 1 kg/m³ | $7.426\times10^{-28}$ m⁻² | $-2$ |
> | pressure, energy density | 1 Pa | $8.263\times10^{-45}$ m⁻² | $-2$ |
> | force | 1 N | $8.263\times10^{-45}$ | $0$ |
> | power | 1 W | $2.756\times10^{-53}$ | $0$ |
> | angular momentum, action | $\hbar = 1.055\times10^{-34}$ J s | $2.612\times10^{-70}$ m² | $2$ |
>
> Two masses used throughout: $M_\odot = 1.477$ km and $M_\oplus = 4.435$ mm. Force and power are pure numbers: $c^4/G = 1.210\times10^{44}$ N and $c^5/G = 3.628\times10^{52}$ W are the geometric units of force and power.
>
> *Source: Schutz §8.1, eq. (8.9), Table 8.1, pp. 185–186; §1.3, pp. 4–5 (time in metres) · Hartle §9.1, eq. (9.8), p. 188 · Couzens, GR1, Conventions (p. 5: $c = 1$), §5.1.1 (setting $G_N = 1$)*

^def-c1-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.2.1: Restoring G and c
> Let a quantity $X$ have SI dimension $M^\alpha L^\beta T^\gamma$.
> 1. Its **geometric value** is
>
> $$
> \hat X = G^{\alpha}\,c^{\gamma - 2\alpha}\,X ,
> $$
>
> a number times $\mathrm m^{\alpha + \beta + \gamma}$, and no other powers $G^ac^b$ turn $X$ into a pure power of length.
> 2. A formula written in geometric units holds with every symbol read as its geometric value. Replacing each symbol $X$, the one solved for included, by $G^\alpha c^{\gamma - 2\alpha}X$ and solving gives the SI formula; when every term of the geometric formula carries the same power of length, the result is dimensionally consistent.
> 3. The standard replacements:
>
> | Symbol | mass $M$ | time $t$ | length $r$ | energy $E$ | potential $\Phi$ | velocity $v$ | acceleration $a$ | density $\rho$ | pressure $p$ | angular momentum $J$ | power $P$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | replace by | $GM/c^2$ | $ct$ | $r$ | $GE/c^4$ | $\Phi/c^2$ | $v/c$ | $a/c^2$ | $G\rho/c^2$ | $Gp/c^4$ | $GJ/c^3$ | $GP/c^5$ |
>
> The information needed is the SI dimension of each symbol, and nothing else. This is the gravitational counterpart of the exponent rule that restores $\hbar$ and $c$ in Quantum Field Theory ([[§C1a.2 Natural Units and Dimensional Analysis#^thm-c1a-2-1|QFT Theorem §C1a.2.1]], procedure [[P4 Restoring ħ and c|QFT P4 Restoring ħ and c]]; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$ there, which does not affect units).
>
> *Model:* none, a statement about units; *applies:* every formula whose symbols have dimensions built from mass, length and time; formulas containing $\hbar$ or electric charge need the conventions of those constants as well.
>
> *Source: Hartle §9.1, after eq. (9.8), p. 188 ("replacing … M by $GM/c^2$, τ by cτ"); §4.6, Example 4.6, p. 73 · Schutz §1.3, Exercises 1.1–1.2, pp. 27–28 · Couzens, GR1, Conventions (p. 5: "reintroduce c … by dimensional analysis"), §5.1.1, eqs. (5.20)–(5.21)*

^thm-c1-2-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the dimension of $G^ac^b$). $[G] = M^{-1}L^3T^{-2}$ and $[c] = LT^{-1}$, so
>
> $$
> [G^ac^bX] = M^{-a}L^{3a}T^{-2a}\cdot L^{b}T^{-b}\cdot M^\alpha L^\beta T^\gamma = M^{\alpha - a}\,L^{\beta + 3a + b}\,T^{\gamma - 2a - b} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (pure length). $G^ac^bX$ is a pure power $L^k$ exactly when the mass and time counters vanish:
>
> $$
> M:\ \alpha - a = 0, \qquad T:\ \gamma - 2a - b = 0, \qquad L:\ k = \beta + 3a + b .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (solve). The $M$ equation gives $a = \alpha$. The $T$ equation gives $b = \gamma - 2a = \gamma - 2\alpha$. The $L$ equation gives $k = \beta + 3\alpha + \gamma - 2\alpha = \alpha + \beta + \gamma$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (uniqueness). As equations for $(a, b)$, Step 2 reads $-a = -\alpha$, $-2a - b = -\gamma$, with coefficient matrix $\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\-2&-1\end{pmatrix}$ of determinant $(-1)(-1) - 0 = 1 \neq 0$; the solution of Step 3 is the only one. Equivalently, the exponent vectors of $L = (0, 1, 0)$, $[G] = (-1, 3, -2)$ and $[c] = (0, 1, -1)$ have
>
> $$
> \det\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\-1&3&-2\\0&1&-1\end{pmatrix} = 0 - 1\cdot\bigl((-1)(-1) - (-2)\cdot0\bigr) + 0 = -1 ,
> $$
>
> so they form a basis of $\mathbb Z^3$: every dimension is uniquely $L^kG^ac^b$, and setting $G = c = 1$ forgets exactly the two integers $a$, $b$ (as for $\hbar$ and $c$: [[§C1a.2 Natural Units and Dimensional Analysis#^thm-c1a-2-2|QFT Theorem §C1a.2.2]], part 1).
>
> **Step 5** (formulas). For a product of symbols the factors multiply: if $X_1$ has exponents $(\alpha_1, \beta_1, \gamma_1)$ and $X_2$ has $(\alpha_2, \beta_2, \gamma_2)$, then
>
> $$
> \hat X_1\hat X_2 = G^{\alpha_1}c^{\gamma_1 - 2\alpha_1}X_1\cdot G^{\alpha_2}c^{\gamma_2 - 2\alpha_2}X_2 = G^{\alpha_1 + \alpha_2}c^{(\gamma_1 + \gamma_2) - 2(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)}\,X_1X_2 = \widehat{X_1X_2} ,
> $$
>
> and the same for quotients and powers (exponents add or scale). So replacing every symbol of a term by its geometric value replaces the term by the geometric value of the SI product, a quantity of dimension $L^{k}$ by Step 3. If all terms of the geometric formula have the same $k$, all substituted terms are SI quantities of dimension $L^k$, and the substituted equation is consistent in SI; solving it for the target gives the SI formula. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 6** (the table and three checks). Part 1 with the dimensions of the table: mass $(1, 0, 0) \to G^1c^{-2}$; time $(0, 0, 1) \to c^{1}$; energy $(1, 2, -2) \to G^1c^{-4}$; potential, a velocity squared, $(0, 2, -2) \to c^{-2}$; velocity $(0, 1, -1) \to c^{-1}$; acceleration $(0, 1, -2) \to c^{-2}$; density $(1, -3, 0) \to G^1c^{-2}$; pressure $(1, -1, -2) \to G^1c^{-4}$; angular momentum $(1, 2, -1) \to G^1c^{-3}$; power $(1, 2, -3) \to G^1c^{-5}$. Checks:
>
> $$
> E = M:\quad \frac{GE}{c^4} = \frac{GM}{c^2} \;\Rightarrow\; E = Mc^2 ; \qquad r_s = 2M:\quad r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2} ;
> $$
>
> $$
> d\tau = (1 + \Phi)\,dt:\quad c\,d\tau = \Bigl(1 + \frac{\Phi}{c^2}\Bigr)c\,dt \;\Rightarrow\; d\tau = \Bigl(1 + \frac{\Phi}{c^2}\Bigr)dt .
> $$
>
> In the last one both terms in the bracket have $k = 0$, as part 2 requires; the clock-rate law is [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Setting $G = c = 1$ loses two integers per quantity, and the SI dimension returns them uniquely (Steps 2–4).
> - The target must be replaced too: in $E = M$ the left side is an energy, and forgetting its factor $G/c^4$ gives the wrong $E = M$ in kilograms.
> - Counting in length is a choice of the surviving unit, as counting in mass is in natural units ([[§C1a.2 Natural Units and Dimensional Analysis#^thm-c1a-2-2|QFT Theorem §C1a.2.2]], part 3); time ($t$ in seconds, $M$ in $GM/c^3$) would serve equally.
> - The numbers of Def. §C1.2.1 are part 1 evaluated on the SI units; checks in `rel-C/tools/verify_c1_2.py`.

> [!remark] Remark: Why not ħ as well
> Classical general relativity contains no $\hbar$, so geometric units lose nothing in it. Couzens (GR1 §5.1.1) notes the trade-off: for gravity alone $G_N = 1$ is the sensible choice, while a theory that couples gravity to the other forces is better written with $\hbar = c = 1$ (the natural units of [[§C1a.2 Natural Units and Dimensional Analysis#^def-c1a-2-1|QFT Def. §C1a.2.1]]), where $G$ is an inverse mass squared and defines the Planck mass $M_{\rm pl}^2 = \hbar c/8\pi G_N$ (GR1 (5.22)), about $2.4\times10^{18}$ GeV. Setting all three constants to $1$ leaves no unit at all: in geometric units $\hbar = 2.612\times10^{-70}$ m² is the square of the Planck length $1.616\times10^{-35}$ m. The Planck scale is homed in §C5.5 (planned); Couzens' GR2 sets $\hbar = k_B = 1$ in its black-hole thermodynamics, which these notes restore when they reach it (C13–C14, planned).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §5.1.1, eq. (5.22) · Schutz §8.1, Table 8.1*
