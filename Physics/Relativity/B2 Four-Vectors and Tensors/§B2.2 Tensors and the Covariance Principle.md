Which objects may appear in a relativistic law, and why does writing a law with them guarantee the first postulate? Level A checked frame independence case by case: the interval by direct substitution ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]), the conservation laws by an explicit transformation ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]]); and it noted that Maxwell's equations, and with them the wave equation for light, are not Galilean invariant ([[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^rem-a1-1-4|§A1.1, Remark: Maxwell's equations are not Galilean invariant]]). [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions|§B2.1]] used four-vectors and their dot products. This section completes the hierarchy: tensors of every rank, defined by how their components change between inertial frames; the operations that build new tensors from old; the tensors whose components never change ($\eta$, $\delta$, $\epsilon$); the gradient $\partial_\mu$ and the wave operator $\Box$; and the principle of covariance, under which every law is an equation between tensors and holds in every inertial frame automatically.

Notation as in [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation|§B1.1]]–[[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group|§B1.2]]: $x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu$ with $\eta_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta = \eta_{\alpha\beta}$, and $\Lambda_\mu{}^\nu \equiv \eta_{\mu\alpha}\Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta\eta^{\beta\nu} = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu$, the inverse written by moving indices ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]), so that $\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho\Lambda_\mu{}^\sigma = \delta_\rho{}^\sigma$. Repeated indices, one up and one down, are summed ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]]); raising and lowering are as in [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]].

## Tensors

> [!definition] Definition §B2.2.1: Lorentz Tensor of Type (m, n); Tensor Field
> A **(Lorentz) tensor of type $(m, n)$**, or rank $m + n$, assigns to each inertial frame $4^{m+n}$ components $T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_m}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_n}$, such that the components in two frames related by $x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu$ satisfy
>
> $$
> T'^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_m}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_n} = \Lambda^{\mu_1}{}_{\alpha_1}\cdots\Lambda^{\mu_m}{}_{\alpha_m}\;\Lambda_{\nu_1}{}^{\beta_1}\cdots\Lambda_{\nu_n}{}^{\beta_n}\;T^{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_m}{}_{\beta_1\cdots\beta_n} :
> $$
>
> each upper index transforms like the displacement $dx^\mu$ (with $\Lambda$), each lower index like the gradient $\partial_\mu$ (with $\Lambda^{-1}$, [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]). Special cases: a **scalar** (type $(0, 0)$, $s' = s$), a **four-vector** or contravariant vector (type $(1, 0)$), a **covector**, covariant vector or one-form (type $(0, 1)$).
>
> A **tensor field** assigns a tensor to every event; components at the same event are related by the law above. For a scalar field, $\phi'(x') = \phi(x)$ whenever $x' = \Lambda x$: the moved observer, evaluating her function at her coordinates of the same event, reads the same number.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Lorentz Tensors and Invariants" · the user's series, Part III, §3.6, Definition (Lorentz scalars, vectors, tensors), eqs. (tensor-law), (scalar-field) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.3.2, §5.3.4, §5.3.9 · Schutz §3.2–3.3*

> [!remark] Remark: Why two kinds of index
> Why lower components co-vary with the basis, why the distinction is invisible for rotations, and why only up–down pairs may be summed is explained for vectors in [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^rem-b1-1-1|§B1.1, Remark: Why two index positions]] and [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]. Definition §B2.2.1 applies the same rule slot by slot. For a boost with velocity $v$ along $x$ the two laws read
>
> $$
> A'^0 = \gamma(A^0 - \beta A^1), \quad A'^1 = \gamma(A^1 - \beta A^0) \qquad\text{versus}\qquad A'_0 = \gamma(A_0 + \beta A_1), \quad A'_1 = \gamma(A_1 + \beta A_0) :
> $$
>
> lower components see the inverse boost, and a tensor $T^\mu{}_\nu$ mixes one factor of each kind.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.1, §3.6 · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.3.4*

^rem-b2-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Tensors without coordinates
> Schutz defines a tensor of type $(0, N)$ as a function of $N$ four-vectors into the real numbers, linear in each argument; its components in a frame are its values on that frame's basis vectors, $T_{\alpha\beta} = T(e_\alpha, e_\beta)$. The metric is the first example: $g(A, B) = A\cdot B$, with components $g(e_\alpha, e_\beta) = \eta_{\alpha\beta}$ in every inertial frame. A covector is a linear functional on four-vectors, an element of the dual space, and a type $(m, n)$ tensor is a multilinear function of $m$ covectors and $n$ vectors. The transformation law of Definition §B2.2.1 is then a consequence: changing the basis changes the values on basis vectors by one factor of $\Lambda$ or $\Lambda^{-1}$ per slot. Linear algebra has all the pieces: linear functionals and the dual space ([[§12 Duality#^ladr-3-108|LADR Def. 3.108]], [[§12 Duality#^ladr-3-110|LADR Def. 3.110]]), multilinear functionals ([[§38 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-85|LADR Def. 9.85]]), tensor products ([[§38 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-88|LADR Def. 9.88]]), and the change-of-basis formula for bilinear forms ([[§35 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-7|LADR Thm. 9.7]]). Physicists use the component definition because it tells at once how to test a candidate.
>
> *Source: Schutz §3.2–3.3*

^rem-b2-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: The grammar of indices, for higher rank
> The rules for free and dummy indices ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]]), the ban on summing two upper indices ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^ex-b1-1-1|Example §B1.1.1]]) and the trace $\delta^\mu{}_\mu = 4$ ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]) carry over unchanged. Two traps are new with two or more indices (the user's series, §3.7):
> - **Slot order matters.** $T^\mu{}_\nu = \eta^{\mu\rho}T_{\rho\nu}$ and $T_\nu{}^\mu = T_{\nu\rho}\eta^{\rho\mu}$ differ unless $T$ is symmetric: if only $T_{01} = 1$, then $T^0{}_1 = 1$ but $T_1{}^0 = 0$. $\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$ and $\Lambda_\nu{}^\mu$ are inverse transposes of each other ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^cau-b1-2-1|§B1.2, Caution: Transpose, not inverse]]); never stack the indices of a non-symmetric tensor vertically.
> - **Rename before differentiating.** $\partial(a_\mu a^\mu)/\partial a_\nu$ needs a fresh dummy: $\partial(\eta^{\rho\sigma}a_\rho a_\sigma)/\partial a_\nu = 2a^\nu$, both slots contributing.

^cau-b2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.1: Algebra of Tensors
> 1. **Linear combinations.** A linear combination of tensors of the same type, with scalar coefficients, is a tensor of that type.
> 2. **Outer products.** If $S$ has type $(m, n)$ and $T$ type $(p, q)$, the products of their components form a tensor of type $(m + p, n + q)$; for instance $a^\mu b^\nu$ is of type $(2, 0)$.
> 3. **Contraction.** Setting one upper index equal to one lower index and summing gives a tensor of type $(m - 1, n - 1)$. A full contraction, with no free index left, is a scalar. Examples: $a^\mu b_\mu$, $T^\mu{}_\mu$ and $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ are scalars; $X_\mu F^{\mu\nu}$ is a four-vector.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Lorentz Tensors and Invariants" (Key Properties) · the user's series, Part III, §3.6, Proposition (Contraction) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.3.11–5.3.14 · Morin §13.3*

^thm-b2-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* The transformation law is linear in the components and has the same coefficients for all tensors of one type.
>
> *2.* Multiply the laws of $S$ and $T$: every index of the product carries its own factor.
>
> *3.* Follow one contracted pair; the other indices carry their factors along. For a type $(1, 1)$ tensor,
>
> $$
> T'^\mu{}_\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\rho\,\Lambda_\mu{}^\sigma\,T^\rho{}_\sigma = \delta_\rho{}^\sigma\,T^\rho{}_\sigma = T^\rho{}_\rho ,
> $$
>
> since $\Lambda_\mu{}^\sigma = (\Lambda^{-1})^\sigma{}_\mu$. The factor of an upper index cancels the factor of a lower one, which is why only up–down pairs may be contracted. For $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ this gives $(\Lambda_\mu{}^\alpha\Lambda^\mu{}_\gamma)(\Lambda_\nu{}^\beta\Lambda^\nu{}_\delta)F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\gamma\delta} = \delta^\alpha{}_\gamma\delta^\beta{}_\delta F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\gamma\delta} = F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}$, the proof in the user's PHY 505 notes.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.2: Invariant Tensors; Raising and Lowering Indices
> 1. The metric defines a tensor $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ of type $(0, 2)$ whose components are $\mathrm{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$ in every inertial frame; likewise $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ (type $(2, 0)$) and the Kronecker delta $\delta^\mu{}_\nu$ (type $(1, 1)$). Lorentz transformations are exactly the linear maps that leave the components of $\eta$ unchanged.
> 2. Lowering an index of a type $(m, n)$ tensor with $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ gives a tensor of type $(m - 1, n + 1)$; raising with $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ gives one of type $(m + 1, n - 1)$. Lowering and transforming commute. In particular $a\cdot b = a^\mu b_\mu$ is the contraction of $a$ with the lowered $b$. For four-vectors these are [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]] and [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]; the point here is every rank.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.1, eq. (lowered-transform); §3.6 (The invariant tensors; Index gymnastics is frame-proof) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Relation Between Λ and Λ⁻¹" · Schutz §3.1, §3.8*

^thm-b2-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Apply the law of a $(0, 2)$ tensor to $\eta$: $\eta'_{\rho\sigma} = \Lambda_\rho{}^\mu\Lambda_\sigma{}^\nu\eta_{\mu\nu} = (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\rho(\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\sigma\eta_{\mu\nu}$. The inverse of a Lorentz transformation is a Lorentz transformation ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]), so this equals $\eta_{\rho\sigma}$; conversely, a linear map with $\Lambda^{-1}$ leaving $\eta$'s components fixed satisfies the defining condition. For $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ the law of a $(2, 0)$ tensor asks for $\Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta\eta^{\alpha\beta} = \eta^{\mu\nu}$, which is $\Lambda\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda^{\mathsf T} = \boldsymbol\eta$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]], part 3). For the delta, $\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho\Lambda_\nu{}^\sigma\delta^\rho{}_\sigma = \Lambda^\mu{}_\rho(\Lambda^{-1})^\rho{}_\nu = \delta^\mu{}_\nu$.
>
> *2.* Lowering is an outer product with $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ followed by a contraction, both covered by [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]; since $\eta$'s components are the same in every frame, lowering before or after transforming gives the same numbers (for a vector, [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]], part 4).

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]], [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.3: Quotient Rule
> Let an array $T_{\mu\nu}$ be given in every inertial frame, and suppose that for every four-vector $a^\nu$ the contraction $c_\mu = T_{\mu\nu}a^\nu$ is a covector. Then $T_{\mu\nu}$ is a tensor of type $(0, 2)$. The same holds for every index structure: an array that turns every tensor of one type, linearly, into a tensor of another type is itself a tensor.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.6, Proposition (Quotient rule)*

^thm-b2-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the primed frame $T'_{\mu\nu}a'^\nu = c'_\mu$. Express both sides through unprimed quantities with the known laws of $a$ and $c$:
>
> $$
> T'_{\mu\nu}\,\Lambda^\nu{}_\sigma\,a^\sigma = \Lambda_\mu{}^\rho\,c_\rho = \Lambda_\mu{}^\rho\,T_{\rho\sigma}\,a^\sigma .
> $$
>
> This holds for every $a^\sigma$, so the coefficients agree: $T'_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^\nu{}_\sigma = \Lambda_\mu{}^\rho T_{\rho\sigma}$. Multiply by $\Lambda_\kappa{}^\sigma$ and use $\Lambda^\nu{}_\sigma\Lambda_\kappa{}^\sigma = \delta^\nu{}_\kappa$: $T'_{\mu\kappa} = \Lambda_\mu{}^\rho\Lambda_\kappa{}^\sigma T_{\rho\sigma}$, the law of a $(0, 2)$ tensor.

^der-b2-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]], [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: How tensors are detected
> One rarely checks sixteen transformation equations. Tensors arise as outer products and contractions of known ones ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]), as derivatives of tensor fields ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]), and as coefficients of linear laws (Theorem §B2.2.3). The last is how the field tensor is certified: the force on a charge is linear in its four-velocity, $dp^\mu/d\tau = qF^\mu{}_\nu u^\nu$, so $F^\mu{}_\nu$ is a tensor, and the transformation of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ follows from the tensor law ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-5|Theorem §B3.2.5]], [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.6, §5.3*

^rem-b2-2-3

> [!definition] Definition §B2.2.2: Levi-Civita Symbol
> The **Levi-Civita symbol** $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ is totally antisymmetric in its four indices and normalized by
>
> $$
> \epsilon^{0123} = +1 :
> $$
>
> $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ is $+1$ or $-1$ when $(\mu\nu\rho\sigma)$ is an even or odd permutation of $(0123)$, and $0$ when two indices coincide. Its lowered form is $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = \eta_{\mu\alpha}\eta_{\nu\beta}\eta_{\rho\gamma}\eta_{\sigma\delta}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}$. With this normalization $\epsilon^{0ijk} = \epsilon_{ijk}$, the three-dimensional symbol with $\epsilon_{123} = 1$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"C: Index Notation Reference" · the user's series, Part III, §11.3*

^def-b2-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.4: The Levi-Civita Symbol Is an Invariant Pseudotensor
> 1. For every Lorentz transformation,
>
> $$
> \Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta\Lambda^\rho{}_\gamma\Lambda^\sigma{}_\delta\,\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = (\det\Lambda)\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} .
> $$
>
> So $\epsilon$ has the same components in all frames related by transformations with $\det\Lambda = +1$ (boosts, rotations, and also $PT$), and changes sign under parity $P$ and time reversal $T$ separately: it is a **pseudotensor**. A full contraction containing one $\epsilon$, such as $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}$, is a pseudoscalar.
> 2. $\epsilon_{0123} = -1$; in general $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = -\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ as numbers.
> 3. $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = -24$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §11.3 · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"C: Index Notation Reference"*

^thm-b2-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Call the left side $L^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$. Exchanging two of its free indices, say $\mu \leftrightarrow \nu$, and renaming the dummies $\alpha \leftrightarrow \beta$ returns $L$ with $\epsilon^{\beta\alpha\gamma\delta} = -\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}$, so $L$ is totally antisymmetric. A totally antisymmetric array with four indices taking four values is fixed by its $0123$ component, so $L^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = L^{0123}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$. And
>
> $$
> L^{0123} = \sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\,\Lambda^0{}_\alpha\Lambda^1{}_\beta\Lambda^2{}_\gamma\Lambda^3{}_\delta = \det\Lambda ,
> $$
>
> the Leibniz formula for the determinant ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-46|LADR Thm. 9.46]], with [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-56|LADR Thm. 9.56]] for rows versus columns). Every Lorentz transformation has $\det\Lambda = \pm1$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]; $P$ and $T$ as in [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-2|Def. §B1.2.2]]): $+1$ for boosts, rotations and $PT$ ($x^\mu \to -x^\mu$), $-1$ for $P$ and $T$.
>
> *2.* A nonzero component has four different indices, one $0$ and three spatial, so lowering multiplies it by $\eta_{00}\eta_{11}\eta_{22}\eta_{33} = (+1)(-1)^3 = -1$.
>
> *3.* There are $4! = 24$ nonzero terms, each $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = (\pm1)(\mp1) = -1$.

> [!caution] Caution: Sign conventions for epsilon
> These notes follow the user's notes, $\epsilon^{0123} = +1$, hence $\epsilon_{0123} = -1$. Some texts fix $\epsilon_{0123} = +1$ instead, which flips the sign of every expression with one $\epsilon$, for instance the dual field tensor and the invariant $\vec E\cdot\vec B$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-3|Def. §B4.2.3]], [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]]). In either signature, lowering all four indices costs a factor $\det\eta = -1$. The user's series (§3.6 and §11.3) describes $\epsilon$ as invariant under "the proper orthochronous transformations only"; by item 1 it is invariant under every transformation with $\det\Lambda = +1$, which includes the non-orthochronous $PT$, and it changes sign under $P$ and $T$ separately, as the series also states.

## Symmetric and antisymmetric tensors

> [!definition] Definition §B2.2.3: Symmetric and Antisymmetric Tensors
> A tensor with two indices at the same height is **symmetric** if $T^{\mu\nu} = T^{\nu\mu}$ and **antisymmetric** if $T^{\mu\nu} = -T^{\nu\mu}$ (likewise for two lower indices). Its **symmetric** and **antisymmetric parts** are
>
> $$
> T^{(\mu\nu)} = \tfrac12\bigl(T^{\mu\nu} + T^{\nu\mu}\bigr), \qquad T^{[\mu\nu]} = \tfrac12\bigl(T^{\mu\nu} - T^{\nu\mu}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schutz §3.5 (Symmetries) · the user's series, Part III, §3.7 (Rule 5) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §3.1.4*

^def-b2-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.5: Symmetric and Antisymmetric Parts
> 1. Every tensor $T^{\mu\nu}$ splits uniquely as $T^{\mu\nu} = T^{(\mu\nu)} + T^{[\mu\nu]}$; both parts are tensors, and a tensor that is symmetric (antisymmetric) in one inertial frame is so in every frame. The symmetric part has $10$ independent components, the antisymmetric part $6$.
> 2. If $S^{\mu\nu}$ is symmetric and $A_{\mu\nu}$ antisymmetric, $S^{\mu\nu}A_{\mu\nu} = 0$. In particular $A_{\mu\nu}v^\mu v^\nu = 0$ for every vector $v$, and $A^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\phi = 0$ for every smooth field $\phi$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.7, Proposition (Rule 5) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §3.1.4 · Schutz §3.5*

^thm-b2-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* The transposed array $\tilde T^{\mu\nu} = T^{\nu\mu}$ is a tensor: $\tilde T'^{\mu\nu} = T'^{\nu\mu} = \Lambda^\nu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\mu{}_\beta T^{\alpha\beta} = \Lambda^\mu{}_\beta\Lambda^\nu{}_\alpha\tilde T^{\beta\alpha}$. So $T^{(\mu\nu)}$ and $T^{[\mu\nu]}$ are tensors ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]), and if $T = \pm\tilde T$ in one frame, the difference $T \mp \tilde T$ is a tensor vanishing in that frame, hence in all. Uniqueness: if $T = S + A$ with $S$ symmetric and $A$ antisymmetric, then $\tilde T = S - A$, which determines $S$ and $A$ (the decomposition $V^{(2)} = V^{(2)}_{\rm sym}\oplus V^{(2)}_{\rm alt}$, [[§35 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-17|LADR Thm. 9.17]]). A symmetric $4\times4$ array is fixed by its $4$ diagonal and $6$ upper entries, an antisymmetric one by its $6$ upper entries.
>
> *2.* Rename the dummies, then use both symmetries: $S^{\mu\nu}A_{\mu\nu} = S^{\nu\mu}A_{\nu\mu} = (S^{\mu\nu})(-A_{\mu\nu})$, a number equal to its own negative. Apply it to $S^{\mu\nu} = v^\mu v^\nu$ and, by equality of mixed partials, to $S_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\partial_\nu\phi$.

> [!remark] Remark: Where the two parts reappear
> - Six is the number of coordinate planes of spacetime, three rotations and three boosts, the dimension of the Lorentz group ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-6|Theorem §B1.2.6]]); it is also the number of components of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ together, which form one antisymmetric tensor $F_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]]).
> - Item 2 is why the Lorentz force $dp^\mu/d\tau = qF^\mu{}_\nu u^\nu$ preserves $u\cdot u = c^2$: $u_\mu\,du^\mu/d\tau = (q/m)F_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = 0$ ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-5|Theorem §B3.2.5]]). With $\partial_\mu\partial_\nu$ in place of $v^\mu v^\nu$ it is behind gauge invariance and charge conservation ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]], [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]], [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-7|Theorem §B4.2.7]]).
> - Symmetry is a frame-independent property only for two indices at the same height. For a mixed tensor $T^\mu{}_\nu$, "symmetric as a matrix" changes under boosts, because the two indices transform with $\Lambda$ and $\Lambda^{-1}$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.7, §3.8*

## The gradient and the wave operator

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.6: The Gradient Is a Covector
> 1. The derivatives $\partial_\mu \equiv \partial/\partial x^\mu = \bigl(\tfrac1c\partial_t, \nabla\bigr)$ transform as the components of a covector,
>
> $$
> \partial'_\mu = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu\,\partial_\nu , \qquad\text{and}\qquad \partial^\mu = \eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu = \Bigl(\frac1c\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\ -\nabla\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 2. If $\phi$ is a scalar field, $\partial_\mu\phi$ is a covector field; if $T$ is a tensor field of type $(m, n)$, $\partial_\lambda T^{\cdots}{}_{\cdots}$ is a tensor field of type $(m, n + 1)$. In particular the four-divergence of a vector field, $\partial_\mu A^\mu = \frac1c\partial_tA^0 + \nabla\cdot\vec A$, is a scalar field.
> 3. $\partial_\mu x^\nu = \delta_\mu{}^\nu$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Transformation of Derivatives" · the user's series, Part III, §3.5, eq. (gradient-transform); §3.6 (Fields) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.3.3–5.3.4 · Schutz §3.4 · Griffiths Problem 12.56*

^thm-b2-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Invert $x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu$: $x^\nu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu x'^\mu = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu x'^\mu$, so $\partial x^\nu/\partial x'^\mu = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu$ (constant). By the chain rule ([[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]),
>
> $$
> \partial'_\mu = \frac{\partial}{\partial x'^\mu} = \frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial x'^\mu}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\nu} = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu\,\partial_\nu ,
> $$
>
> the law of a lower index ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]]). Raising with $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ flips the sign of the spatial components.
>
> *2.* For a scalar field, $\phi'(x') = \phi(x)$, so $\partial'_\mu\phi'(x') = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu\partial_\nu\phi(x)$. For a tensor field the factors $\Lambda$ in its law are constants and pass through the derivative, which adds one lower index with its factor $\Lambda_\lambda{}^\kappa$. The four-divergence is a contraction ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]).
>
> *3.* The coordinates are independent variables.

> [!remark] Remark: Why the gradient carries a lower index
> The change of a scalar field between neighbouring events, $d\phi = \partial_\mu\phi\,dx^\mu$, is a number every observer agrees on, and $dx^\mu$ is a four-vector; so $\partial_\mu\phi$ must be what pairs with a four-vector to give a scalar, a covector (Schutz §3.4). In the language of manifolds $d\phi$ is the differential, a linear functional on tangent vectors ([[§32 The Cotangent Space#^def-32-1|591 Def. §32.1]], [[§32 The Cotangent Space#^rem-32-2|591 Remark: Differential — Not Gradient]]). The wave four-vector is an example: $k_\mu = \partial_\mu(k_\nu x^\nu)$ is the gradient of the phase ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-9|Theorem §B2.1.9]]). Another is the continuity equation, $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$ ([[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^thm-b6-1-5|EM Theorem §B6.1.5]]), which is the vanishing four-divergence $\partial_\mu J^\mu = 0$ of $J^\mu = (c\rho, \vec J)$ and so holds in every frame once $J^\mu$ is known to be a four-vector ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]; PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §6.1.1; Griffiths Problem 12.56). The PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering) (§5.3.4) call $\partial_\mu$ the "covariant derivative"; that name usually belongs to other operators: the gauge-covariant $D_\mu = \partial_\mu - ieA_\mu$ of the user's PHY 505 notes, and in curved spacetime the derivative with a connection (Relativity level C, planned).

^rem-b2-2-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.7: The d'Alembertian Is Invariant
> The **d'Alembertian**
>
> $$
> \Box \equiv \partial_\mu\partial^\mu = \eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu = \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2
> $$
>
> is the same operator in every inertial frame, $\Box' = \Box$, and maps scalar fields to scalar fields. Hence the wave equation $\Box\phi = 0$, waves of speed $c$, has the same form in every inertial frame; a plane wave $\phi = \cos(k_\mu x^\mu)$ solves it exactly when $k\cdot k = 0$, that is $\omega = c|\vec k|$, in every frame.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The d'Alembertian Operator" · the user's series, Part III, §3.5, eq. (dalembertian); §2.7 · Griffiths §12.3.4, eq. (12.138)*

^thm-b2-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]] and the invariance of $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]),
>
> $$
> \Box' = \eta^{\mu\nu}\partial'_\mu\partial'_\nu = \eta^{\mu\nu}\Lambda_\mu{}^\alpha\Lambda_\nu{}^\beta\,\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta = \eta^{\alpha\beta}\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta = \Box ,
> $$
>
> a full contraction. If $\phi$ is a scalar field, $\Box\phi$ is one, so $\Box\phi = 0$ in one frame implies it in all. For the plane wave, $\partial_\mu\cos(k\cdot x) = -k_\mu\sin(k\cdot x)$ and $\Box\cos(k\cdot x) = -k_\mu k^\mu\cos(k\cdot x)$, which vanishes identically iff $k\cdot k = \omega^2/c^2 - |\vec k|^2 = 0$, a scalar condition.

^der-b2-2-7

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]], [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]], [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-9|Theorem §B2.1.9]]

> [!caution] Caution: The sign of the box
> With the signature $(-,+,+,+)$ of Griffiths and Schutz, $\partial_\mu\partial^\mu = \nabla^2 - \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2$, the negative of the operator above; Griffiths writes it $\Box^2$ (eq. (12.138)). The wave equation $\Box\phi = 0$ reads the same in both conventions, but source terms change sign: Griffiths' $\Box^2A^\mu = -\mu_0J^\mu$ (eq. (12.137)) becomes $\Box A^\mu = \mu_0J^\mu$ here ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-7|Theorem §B4.2.7]]). The Klein–Gordon operator is $\Box + (mc/\hbar)^2$ in this signature ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]]).

## The covariance principle

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.8: Tensor Equations Hold in Every Inertial Frame
> If an equation $A = B$ between two tensors (or tensor fields) of the same type holds in one inertial frame, it holds in every inertial frame. An equation between objects of different types does not keep its form: if a law said $X = Y^1$ with $X$ a scalar and $Y$ a four-vector, in another frame it would read $X' = (\Lambda^{-1})^1{}_\nu Y'^\nu$, a different law.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.6, Remark (The covariance principle) · Morin §13.6, eq. (13.32) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.3.1, eqs. (5.44)–(5.45)*

^thm-b2-2-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $D = A - B$ is a tensor of the same type ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]); its transformation law is linear and homogeneous, so components that vanish in one frame vanish in all. For the mixed equation, $Y^\nu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu Y'^\mu$ while $X = X'$; the coefficients $(\Lambda^{-1})^1{}_\nu$ depend on the frame, so the law would single out the frame in which they are $\delta^1{}_\nu$.

^der-b2-2-8

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]

> [!principle] Principle §B2.2.9: Principle of Covariance
> The laws of physics in special relativity are equations between Lorentz tensors (or tensor fields) of the same type. They are built from the physical quantities by the operations of [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]] and by differentiation with $\partial_\mu$, with the invariant tensors $\eta_{\mu\nu}$, $\eta^{\mu\nu}$, $\delta^\mu{}_\nu$ and $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ as the only fixed ingredients.
>
> *Domain: inertial frames of flat (Minkowski) spacetime, related by Lorentz transformations connected to the identity (proper orthochronous); together with translations, the Poincaré group ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]], [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-4|Def. §B1.2.4]]). Parity and time reversal are not symmetries of every law (the weak interaction violates both), so laws need not be covariant under them, and laws containing one $\epsilon$ are covariant only under $\det\Lambda = +1$. Gravity and non-inertial coordinates are outside it: general relativity extends covariance to arbitrary coordinates on curved spacetime (Relativity level C, planned).*
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.6, Remark (The covariance principle); §3.8 · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.3; §6.2.3 · Morin §13.6*

^pr-b2-2-9

> [!remark] Remark: What covariance adds to the first postulate
> - **Layer.** The principle of relativity remains the postulate ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-1|Principle §A1.3.1]]). Covariance is how level B implements it: write each law as a tensor equation and its frame independence is a theorem ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-8|Theorem §B2.2.8]]) instead of a calculation. The PHY 505 instructor states it as "physics laws must take the form scalar = scalar, vector = vector, tensor = tensor", first for rotations in three dimensions (§3.1.2), then for boosts (§5.3).
> - **A filter, not a source of laws.** Covariance admits $\Delta P^\mu = 0$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^pr-b2-1-5|Principle §B2.1.5]]) but also $K^\mu = p^\mu$ or $2p^\mu = 3p^\mu$, which are covariant and false; experiment chooses (Morin §13.6). What it excludes is any law whose two sides transform differently, such as $\vec F = m\vec a$ with the relativistic force and acceleration: under boosts the components of $\vec F$ and of $\vec a$ change by different factors ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]], [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]]).
> - **No background objects.** The clause on fixed ingredients has content. Sound obeys a wave equation of the same form with $c_s$ in place of $c$, yet the medium's rest frame is detectable (the user's series, §2.7): a covariant description of sound needs the medium's four-velocity as an extra fixed vector, and that vector is a preferred frame. Covariance with $\eta$, $\delta$ and $\epsilon$ alone says that empty spacetime has none.
> - **Laws met in covariant form.** Conservation of four-momentum and the mass shell ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions|§B2.1]]), the wave equation for light (Theorem §B2.2.7), the free particle's equation of motion ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle|§B3.1]]), the Lorentz force ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle|§B3.2]]), the Klein–Gordon equation ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation|§B4.1]]), the continuity and Maxwell equations ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor|§B4.2]]).

^rem-b2-2-6

> [!caution] Caution: Two meanings of "covariant"
> A *covariant index* is a lower index, one that transforms like the gradient. A *covariant equation* is one written between tensors of the same type, hence form-invariant. A covariant equation may well contain only upper indices ($\Delta P^\mu = 0$). Both usages are standard (the user's series, §3.6).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.2.1: Not Every Quadruple Is a Four-Vector
> Is $A = (c\,dt, 2\,dx, dy, dz)$, built from the displacement between two events, a four-vector? Let $S'$ move with velocity $v$ along $x$ relative to $S$. If $A$ were a four-vector, its components would obey the inverse boost, $A^0 = \gamma(A'^0 + \beta A'^1)$:
>
> $$
> c\,dt = \gamma\,\bigl(c\,dt' + 2\beta\,dx'\bigr) .
> $$
>
> But each component is also determined by how $dt$ and $dx$ themselves transform, $c\,dt = \gamma(c\,dt' + \beta\,dx')$. The two agree only when $dx' = 0$, so $A$ is not a four-vector. The quadruple $(c\,dt, dx, 2\,dy, dz)$ passes every boost along $x$, because $y$ is untouched by them, but fails a boost along $y$. A four-vector must transform correctly under all Lorentz transformations, boosts in every direction and rotations; its spatial part must in particular be a vector under rotations. That is why the correspondence between $(E, \vec pc)$ and $(ct, \vec x)$ pairs $E$ with $ct$ and $\vec pc$ with $\vec x$, although the boost formulas alone are symmetric in the two slots.
>
> *Source: Morin §13.1–13.2, eqs. (13.2)–(13.3); §12.2, footnote 7*

^ex-b2-2-1

> [!example] Example §B2.2.2: The Wave Equation under Galilean and Lorentz Boosts
> Take waves of speed $c$ along $x$, $\frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2\phi - \partial_x^2\phi = 0$, and change frames by a boost with velocity $v$.
>
> *Galilean boost.* With $\bar x = x - vt$, $\bar t = t$, the chain rule gives $\partial_x = \partial_{\bar x}$ and $\partial_t = \partial_{\bar t} - v\partial_{\bar x}$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2 - \partial_x^2 = \frac{1}{c^2}\Bigl(\partial_{\bar t}^2 - 2v\,\partial_{\bar t}\partial_{\bar x} + v^2\partial_{\bar x}^2\Bigr) - \partial_{\bar x}^2 .
> $$
>
> A cross term appears and the coefficient of $\partial_{\bar x}^2$ changes: the moving observer finds waves of speed $c - v$ downstream and $c + v$ upstream. For sound this is right, since the equation holds in the rest frame of the air. For light it was the puzzle of [[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^rem-a1-1-4|§A1.1, Remark: Maxwell's equations are not Galilean invariant]].
>
> *Lorentz boost.* With $x' = \gamma(x - vt)$, $t' = \gamma(t - vx/c^2)$ ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]]), $\partial_t = \gamma(\partial_{t'} - v\partial_{x'})$ and $\partial_x = \gamma(\partial_{x'} - \frac{v}{c^2}\partial_{t'})$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_t^2 - \partial_x^2 = \gamma^2\Bigl[\frac{1}{c^2}\partial_{t'}^2 - \frac{2v}{c^2}\partial_{t'}\partial_{x'} + \frac{v^2}{c^2}\partial_{x'}^2 - \partial_{x'}^2 + \frac{2v}{c^2}\partial_{x'}\partial_{t'} - \frac{v^2}{c^4}\partial_{t'}^2\Bigr] = \frac{1}{c^2}\partial_{t'}^2 - \partial_{x'}^2 ,
> $$
>
> the cross terms cancelling and $\gamma^2(1 - v^2/c^2) = 1$. This is [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]] done by hand in $1 + 1$ dimensions; the index computation does it in one line for every Lorentz transformation.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §2.1, eq. (wave-not-invariant); §2.7 · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The d'Alembertian Operator"*
