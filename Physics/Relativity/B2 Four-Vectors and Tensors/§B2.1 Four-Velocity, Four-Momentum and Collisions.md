How do the energy, momentum and frequency of level A look once spacetime is treated as one four-dimensional space? Level A worked frame by frame. It defined $\vec p = \gamma_u m\vec u$ and $E = \gamma_u mc^2$ ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]), found the invariant $E^2 - (pc)^2 = (mc^2)^2$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]]), checked by algebra that $(E, \vec p)$ transforms like $(ct, \vec r)$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]) and that conservation in one frame gives conservation in all ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]]), and derived the Doppler shift from time dilation and light travel times ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]]). Level B packs each of these into a four-vector: the four-velocity and four-acceleration of a worldline, the four-momentum $p^\mu = mu^\mu$, and the wave four-vector $k^\mu$ of a plane wave. Invariants become norms and dot products, to be evaluated in whichever frame is easiest; transformation formulas become one Lorentz transformation; the two conservation laws become one four-vector equation. Threshold energies, Compton scattering and the Doppler effect at any angle then take a line or two each.

Notation: the metric is $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1)$, the dot product $a\cdot b = \eta_{\mu\nu}a^\mu b^\nu = a^0b^0 - \vec a\cdot\vec b$, and $a^2 \equiv a\cdot a$ ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation|§B1.1]]); a Lorentz transformation acts as $x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu$ and preserves every dot product ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group|§B1.2]]); $\tau$ is proper time ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time|§B1.3]]). As at level A, $\vec u$ is a particle's velocity with $\gamma_u = (1 - u^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$, while $v$, $\beta = v/c$ and $\gamma$ belong to the relative velocity of two frames. The rapidity is $\phi$, with $\beta = \tanh\phi$.

## Four-velocity and four-acceleration

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.1: Four-Velocity
> Let the worldline of a massive particle be parametrized by its proper time, $x^\mu(\tau)$. Its **four-velocity** is the tangent vector
>
> $$
> u^\mu = \frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau} .
> $$
>
> In an inertial frame in which the particle has velocity $\vec u$, $dt = \gamma_u\,d\tau$ gives the components
>
> $$
> u^\mu = \gamma_u\,\frac{dx^\mu}{dt} = \gamma_u\,(c, \vec u) .
> $$
>
> The inertial frame in which the particle is at rest at a given event is its **momentarily comoving reference frame** (MCRF); there $u^\mu = (c, \vec 0)$, so $u^\mu/c$ is the time basis vector of the MCRF.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The Correct Definition" · the user's series, Part III, §3.3 · Schutz §2.3, §2.6, eq. (2.31) · Morin §13.2, eq. (13.4) · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §6.2.1*

^def-b2-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Names and normalizations differ between the sources
> Griffiths calls $u^\mu$ the **proper velocity** and writes it $\eta^\mu$ (§12.2.1), a letter these notes reserve for the metric. Schutz writes $\vec U$ with $c = 1$ and the signature $(-,+,+,+)$, so his $\vec U\cdot\vec U = -1$ (eq. (2.28)); Morin writes $V$ with $c = 1$ and our signature, $V\cdot V = 1$. Here $u\cdot u = +c^2$. Changing signature flips the sign of every dot product ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation|§B1.1]]).

^cau-b2-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.1: The Four-Velocity Is a Timelike Four-Vector of Norm c
> 1. $u^\mu$ is a four-vector: $u'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu u^\nu$.
> 2. $u\cdot u = c^2$ in every frame, and $u^0 = \gamma_uc \ge c > 0$: the four-velocity is timelike and future-pointing.
> 3. Conversely, every $w^\mu$ with $w\cdot w = c^2$ and $w^0 > 0$ is the four-velocity of a particle with velocity $\vec u = c\,\vec w/w^0$, $|\vec u| < c$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Four-Velocity Normalization" · the user's series, Part III, §3.3, eq. (u-norm) · Schutz §2.5, Exercise 2.17 · Morin §13.2*

^thm-b2-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* The displacement $dx^\mu$ between two neighbouring events on the worldline transforms by the linear map $\Lambda$, and $d\tau$, defined by $c^2\,d\tau^2 = dx\cdot dx$, is the same number in every frame ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time|§B1.3]]). Dividing a four-vector by an invariant leaves its transformation law unchanged, because $\Lambda$ is linear; the limit $d\tau \to 0$ preserves it too.
>
> *2.* $u\cdot u = dx\cdot dx/d\tau^2 = c^2$ by the definition of proper time; in components, $\gamma_u^2(c^2 - u^2) = c^2$. Also $u^0 = \gamma_u c \ge c$.
>
> *3.* From $(w^0)^2 = c^2 + |\vec w|^2$ we get $w^0 > |\vec w|$, so $\vec u = c\vec w/w^0$ has $|\vec u| < c$, and $1 - u^2/c^2 = \bigl((w^0)^2 - |\vec w|^2\bigr)/(w^0)^2 = c^2/(w^0)^2$. Hence $\gamma_u = w^0/c$ and $\gamma_u(c, \vec u) = (w^0, \vec w)$.

> [!remark] Remark: Velocity addition, read off a four-vector
> Level A derived the velocity transformation ([[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]) and the composition of Lorentz factors ([[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]) separately. Both are one Lorentz transformation of $u^\mu$. For a boost with velocity $v$ along $x$,
>
> $$
> u'^0 = \gamma\,(u^0 - \beta u^1), \qquad u'^1 = \gamma\,(u^1 - \beta u^0), \qquad u'^2 = u^2, \qquad u'^3 = u^3 .
> $$
>
> With $u^\mu = \gamma_u(c, \vec u)$ the first equation is $\gamma_{u'} = \gamma\gamma_u(1 - vu_x/c^2)$, and the ratios $u'_x = cu'^1/u'^0$, $u'_y = cu'^2/u'^0$ give $u'_x = (u_x - v)/(1 - vu_x/c^2)$ and $u'_y = u_y/\bigl(\gamma(1 - vu_x/c^2)\bigr)$. The three-velocity is a ratio of components of a four-vector, which is why it transforms nonlinearly and why it cannot itself appear in a covariant law ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle|§B2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 13.1 · Schutz Exercise 2.16 · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §6.2.1*

^rem-b2-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.2: Four-Acceleration; Proper Acceleration
> The **four-acceleration** of a worldline is
>
> $$
> a^\mu = \frac{du^\mu}{d\tau} = \frac{d^2x^\mu}{d\tau^2} ,
> $$
>
> a four-vector by the argument of [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]] (a difference of four-vectors divided by an invariant). The **proper acceleration** $\alpha \ge 0$ is defined by $a\cdot a = -\alpha^2$; by the next theorem it is the magnitude of the ordinary acceleration in the MCRF, the reading of an accelerometer carried along (Morin: the astronaut standing on a scale reads $m\alpha$).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.3 · Schutz §2.6, eq. (2.32) · Morin §13.2, eqs. (13.6)–(13.8); §11.9.2 · Griffiths Problem 12.39*

^def-b2-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.2: Properties of the Four-Acceleration
> Let a particle have velocity $\vec u$ and ordinary acceleration $\vec a = d\vec u/dt$ in some inertial frame, and write $\gamma = \gamma_u$.
> 1. **Components:**
>
> $$
> a^\mu = \Bigl(\frac{\gamma^4\,(\vec u\cdot\vec a)}{c},\; \gamma^2\vec a + \frac{\gamma^4\,(\vec u\cdot\vec a)}{c^2}\,\vec u\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 2. **Orthogonality:** $a\cdot u = 0$.
> 3. **Rest frame and norm:** in the MCRF, $a^\mu = (0, \vec a_0)$ with $\vec a_0$ the acceleration measured there. So $a^\mu$ is spacelike or zero, $\alpha = |\vec a_0|$, and in any frame
>
> $$
> \alpha^2 = -a\cdot a = \gamma^4\Bigl[a^2 + \frac{\gamma^2(\vec u\cdot\vec a)^2}{c^2}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> 4. **Special cases:** for $\vec a \parallel \vec u$ (linear motion) $\alpha = \gamma^3 a$; for $\vec a \perp \vec u$ (e.g. circular motion at constant speed) $\alpha = \gamma^2 a$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.3, eq. (a-perp-u) · Schutz §2.6 · Morin §13.2, eqs. (13.7)–(13.8); §13.5.2, Example (Acceleration for circular motion) · Griffiths Problem 12.39*

^thm-b2-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Differentiating $\gamma = (1 - \vec u\cdot\vec u/c^2)^{-1/2}$ gives $d\gamma/dt = \gamma^3(\vec u\cdot\vec a)/c^2$. With $d/d\tau = \gamma\,d/dt$,
>
> $$
> a^\mu = \gamma\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\gamma c,\ \gamma\vec u\bigr) = \gamma\Bigl(\frac{\gamma^3(\vec u\cdot\vec a)}{c},\ \gamma\vec a + \frac{\gamma^3(\vec u\cdot\vec a)}{c^2}\vec u\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *2.* The metric is constant, so the product rule gives $\frac{d}{d\tau}(u\cdot u) = 2\,a\cdot u$; and $u\cdot u = c^2$ is constant.
>
> *3.* In the MCRF $u^\mu = (c, \vec 0)$, so $0 = a\cdot u = c\,a^0$, and item 1 with $\vec u = 0$, $\gamma = 1$ gives $\vec a_0$ for the spatial part. Hence $a\cdot a = -|\vec a_0|^2 \le 0$, and since $a\cdot a$ is invariant, $\alpha = |\vec a_0|$. In a general frame, with $s = \vec u\cdot\vec a$, item 1 gives $(a^0)^2 = \gamma^8s^2/c^2$ and $|\vec a^{\,\rm sp}|^2 = \gamma^4a^2 + 2\gamma^6s^2/c^2 + \gamma^8s^2u^2/c^4$, so
>
> $$
> a\cdot a = \frac{\gamma^8s^2}{c^2}\Bigl(1 - \frac{u^2}{c^2}\Bigr) - \gamma^4a^2 - \frac{2\gamma^6s^2}{c^2} = -\gamma^4a^2 - \frac{\gamma^6s^2}{c^2} .
> $$
>
> *4.* For $\vec a \parallel \vec u$, $s^2 = u^2a^2$ and $\alpha^2 = \gamma^4a^2(1 + \gamma^2u^2/c^2) = \gamma^6a^2$, since $1 + \gamma^2\beta_u^2 = \gamma^2$. For $\vec a \perp \vec u$, $s = 0$.

> [!remark] Remark: What the orthogonality means
> - The four-velocity is confined to the hyperboloid $u\cdot u = c^2$; acceleration can only turn it along that surface, never stretch it, so $a$ is tangent to the hyperboloid and Minkowski-orthogonal to $u$ (the user's series, §3.3). Orthogonal does not mean perpendicular on a spacetime diagram: two vectors are orthogonal when they make equal angles with a light ray ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-1|Theorem §A1.5.1]] shows this for the axes of a moving frame; Schutz §2.5).
> - Since $a$ is a four-vector equal to $(0, \vec a_0)$ in the MCRF, a boost to a frame in which the particle moves along $x$ gives $a_x = a_{0x}/\gamma^3$ and $a_{y,z} = a_{0y,z}/\gamma^2$ (Morin eq. (13.26)): the lab sees smaller accelerations than the particle feels, more so along the motion. A constant $\alpha$ defines uniform acceleration ([[§B3.3★ Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion|§B3.3★]]).

## Four-momentum and the mass shell

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.3: Four-Momentum
> The **four-momentum** of a particle of mass $m > 0$ is
>
> $$
> p^\mu = m\,u^\mu = \Bigl(\frac{E}{c},\ \vec p\Bigr), \qquad E = \gamma_umc^2, \quad \vec p = \gamma_um\vec u ,
> $$
>
> so its components are level A's energy and momentum ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]). A massless particle has $p^\mu = (E/c, \vec p)$ with $|\vec p| = E/c$ and $\vec p$ along its motion; for a photon $E = \hbar\omega$, $\vec p = \hbar\vec k$ ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|QM Principle §A1.2.3]]). The four-momentum of a system is the sum $P^\mu = \sum_i p_i^\mu$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The Correct Definition" · the user's series, Part III, §3.3 · Schutz §2.4, §2.7 · Morin §12.3, eq. (12.32); §13.2, eq. (13.5) · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §6.2.1*

^def-b2-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.3: Mass Shell; Lorentz Transformation of Energy and Momentum
> 1. $p^\mu$ is a four-vector. For a boost with velocity $v$ along $x$,
>
> $$
> \frac{E'}{c} = \gamma\Bigl(\frac Ec - \beta p_x\Bigr), \qquad p'_x = \gamma\Bigl(p_x - \beta\frac Ec\Bigr), \qquad p'_y = p_y, \qquad p'_z = p_z ,
> $$
>
> which is [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]].
> 2. **Mass shell:** $p\cdot p = E^2/c^2 - |\vec p|^2 = m^2c^2$ and $p^0 > 0$. The four-momenta of particles of mass $m > 0$ fill the upper sheet of the hyperboloid $p\cdot p = m^2c^2$; those of massless particles lie on the future light cone, $p\cdot p = 0$.
> 3. The velocity is $\vec u = c^2\vec p/E$: below $c$ for $m > 0$, equal to $c$ for $m = 0$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The Mass-Shell Condition" · the user's series, Part III, §3.3, §3.7 (Rule 2) · Schutz §2.6, eqs. (2.33)–(2.34); §2.7, eq. (2.40) · Morin §13.4 · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §6.2.3*

^thm-b2-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* The mass $m$ is a property of the particle, measured in its rest frame, hence a scalar; $p^\mu = mu^\mu$ is a scalar times a four-vector ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]]). The displayed formulas are the boost matrix of [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group|§B1.2]] applied to $(E/c, p_x, p_y, p_z)$. For a massless particle $p^\mu$ is defined directly; it transforms as a four-vector as the limit $m \to 0$ at fixed $E$ and $\vec p$ of the massive case, and for a photon because $p^\mu = \hbar k^\mu$ with $k^\mu$ a four-vector ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-9|Theorem §B2.1.9]]).
>
> *2.* $p\cdot p = m^2\,u\cdot u = m^2c^2$, most simply evaluated in the rest frame, $p^\mu = (mc, \vec 0)$; and $p^0 = \gamma_umc > 0$. Written out, $E^2/c^2 - |\vec p|^2 = m^2c^2$ is [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]]. For $m = 0$, $|\vec p| = E/c$ by definition.
>
> *3.* $\vec p/p^0 = \gamma_um\vec u/(\gamma_umc) = \vec u/c$, so $\vec u = c\vec p/p^0 = c^2\vec p/E$; on the light cone $|\vec p| = p^0$.

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level A
> - **One fact instead of two.** Level A proved the energy–momentum relation by substituting $\gamma_u$ and the transformation of $E$ and $\vec p$ by composing velocities. At level B both say that $p^\mu$ is a four-vector of norm $mc$; the layer is unchanged (theorems), the derivations shrink to a line.
> - **A working rule.** Never solve for velocities if they are not asked for; write the four-momenta, isolate the unwanted one, square it, and use $p\cdot p = m^2c^2$ (Morin §12.3 calls $E^2 - p^2c^2 = m^2c^4$ the "Very Important Relation"). Every dot product may be evaluated in whichever frame makes it simplest.
> - **Geometry.** The mass shell is the same hyperboloid as the events at fixed timelike interval from the origin ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]; [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time|§B1.3]]). A boost slides $p$ along it; in terms of rapidity, a particle moving along $\hat n$ has $p^\mu = mc(\cosh\phi, \sinh\phi\,\hat n)$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group|§B1.2]]).

^rem-b2-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.4: What an Observer Measures
> Let an observer have four-velocity $U^\mu$ ($U\cdot U = c^2$).
> 1. A particle of four-momentum $p$ has, in the observer's rest frame, the energy and momentum
>
> $$
> E_{\rm obs} = p\cdot U, \qquad |\vec p_{\rm obs}|^2 = \frac{(p\cdot U)^2}{c^2} - p\cdot p .
> $$
>
> 2. For two massive particles with four-velocities $u_1$, $u_2$,
>
> $$
> u_1\cdot u_2 = \gamma_{\rm rel}\,c^2, \qquad v_{\rm rel} = c\sqrt{1 - \frac{c^4}{(u_1\cdot u_2)^2}} ,
> $$
>
> where $v_{\rm rel}$ is the speed of either particle in the rest frame of the other and $\gamma_{\rm rel}$ its Lorentz factor.
>
> *Source: Schutz §2.6, eq. (2.35); Exercise 2.30 · Morin Problems 13.2–13.3*

^thm-b2-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* The right-hand side is a scalar, so it may be computed in the observer's rest frame, where $U^\mu = (c, \vec 0)$ and $p^\mu = (E_{\rm obs}/c, \vec p_{\rm obs})$: $p\cdot U = E_{\rm obs}$. Then $p\cdot p = E_{\rm obs}^2/c^2 - |\vec p_{\rm obs}|^2$ gives the momentum.
>
> *2.* Take $p = mu_1$ and $U = u_2$. In the rest frame of particle 2, particle 1 has energy $\gamma_{\rm rel}mc^2$, so by item 1 $m\,u_1\cdot u_2 = \gamma_{\rm rel}mc^2$. The expression is symmetric in 1 and 2, so the relative speed is the same seen from either particle; solving $\gamma_{\rm rel} = (1 - v_{\rm rel}^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$ for $v_{\rm rel}$ gives the formula.

## Conservation of four-momentum

> [!principle] Principle §B2.1.5: Conservation of Four-Momentum
> In every interaction within an isolated system of particles (collisions, decays, reactions, creation and annihilation of particles) the total four-momentum
>
> $$
> P^\mu = \sum_i p_i^\mu
> $$
>
> is the same before and after. Its time component is the conservation of total energy, rest energies included; its space components are the conservation of total relativistic momentum.
>
> *Domain: isolated systems of particles, massive or massless, that interact only at contact (collisions, decays, reactions), in inertial frames. When parts of a system interact at a distance through a field, the four-momentum of the particles alone is not conserved; the field's energy and momentum must be counted ([[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^thm-b10-1-4|EM Theorem §B10.1.4]] for a plane wave; in general the field's energy–momentum tensor: Electromagnetism level C3, planned). For $u \ll c$ it reduces to the separate Newtonian conservation of mass, momentum and energy.*
> *Source: Schutz §2.4, eq. (2.22) · Morin §12.3, eq. (12.34) · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §6.3 · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Four-Momentum"*

^pr-b2-1-5

> [!remark] Remark: One principle instead of two
> - **Layer.** Level A took the conservation of relativistic momentum and of total energy as two principles ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|Principle §A3.2.3]]) and then proved them consistent between frames ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]]). Level B states one principle about one four-vector. Schutz calls it an extra postulate: it is one of many relativistic laws with the right Newtonian limit, and experiment selects it; one of its new predictions is that rest mass can be turned into kinetic energy and heat, as in every nuclear reactor. The PHY 505 instructor likewise assumes it in ch. 6 (§6.3) and returns to it with Noether's theorem in ch. 7.
> - **Before and after.** Two collisions at spacelike separation may occur in different orders for different observers, so the particles one observer adds up "at time $t$" differ from another's. Each collision conserves $P$, so every observer obtains the same four-vector on each of its surfaces of constant time (Schutz §2.4).
> - **Status at level B.** For a free particle, and for any system described by an action that does not change under translations in space and time, conservation of four-momentum becomes a theorem by Noether's theorem ([[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle|§B3.1]]).

^rem-b2-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.6: Conservation in Every Frame; System Mass
> 1. If $\Delta P^\mu = P^\mu_{\rm after} - P^\mu_{\rm before}$ vanishes in one inertial frame, it vanishes in every inertial frame.
> 2. If one component of a four-vector vanishes in every inertial frame, the four-vector is zero. Hence if energy is conserved in every frame, so is momentum; and if one momentum component is conserved in every frame, so are energy and the whole momentum.
> 3. The invariant mass $M$ of a system ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]]) is the norm of its four-momentum, $M^2c^2 = P\cdot P$: invariant, and conserved in every interaction. For two particles,
>
> $$
> M^2c^2 = m_1^2c^2 + m_2^2c^2 + 2\,p_1\cdot p_2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Morin §13.3 (the theorem on vanishing components), §13.4 · Schutz §2.4 · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Lorentz Tensors and Invariants"*

^thm-b2-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\Delta P$ is a sum and difference of four-vectors, hence a four-vector, and $\Lambda$ maps the zero vector to zero.
>
> *2.* Suppose $A^0 = 0$ in every frame but $\vec A \ne 0$ in one. Boost with speed $v$ along $\vec A$: $A'^0 = \gamma(A^0 - \beta|\vec A|) = -\gamma\beta|\vec A| \ne 0$, a contradiction. Suppose instead $A^1 = 0$ in every frame. Rotations are Lorentz transformations ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group|§B1.2]]) and can turn any spatial direction into $x$, so all spatial components vanish in every frame; then a boost along $x$ gives $A'^1 = \gamma(A^1 - \beta A^0) = -\gamma\beta A^0$, which must vanish, so $A^0 = 0$. The same argument works for $A^2$, $A^3$. Apply this to $A = \Delta P$.
>
> *3.* $P$ is a four-vector, so $P\cdot P$ is invariant; in the zero-momentum frame it is $(E_{\rm tot}/c)^2 = M^2c^2$. By [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^pr-b2-1-5|Principle §B2.1.5]] $P$, hence $P\cdot P$, is conserved. For two particles expand $(p_1 + p_2)\cdot(p_1 + p_2)$ by bilinearity and use the mass shell.

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^pr-b2-1-5|Principle §B2.1.5]], [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group|§B1.2]], [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Level A's frame argument, generalized
> [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]] proved item 1 with the explicit transformation for boosts along $x$, and showed that momentum conservation in all frames forces energy conservation. Item 2 is the general form: once $E$ and $\vec p$ are the components of one four-vector, conserving any one component in every frame conserves all four (Morin §13.4.2). The invariant mass of item 3 and its consequences ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-5|Theorem §A3.3.5]]) need no new proof; for two particles it is the collision energy $W = Mc^2$ of [[§A8.3 Particle Accelerators and Detectors#^thm-a8-3-2|QM Theorem §A8.3.2]].

## Collisions and thresholds

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.7: Threshold Energy on a Fixed Target
> Let a particle $a$ of energy $E_a$ hit a particle $b$ at rest, producing particles of total mass $M_f = \sum_i m_{c_i} > m_a + m_b$. The reaction $a + b \to c_1 + \dots + c_n$ is allowed by four-momentum conservation exactly when
>
> $$
> E_a \ge E_{\rm th} = \frac{M_f^2 - m_a^2 - m_b^2}{2m_b}\,c^2, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad K_a \ge K_{\rm th} = \frac{M_f^2 - (m_a + m_b)^2}{2m_b}\,c^2 .
> $$
>
> At threshold all products are at rest in the centre-of-mass frame and move together in the lab with velocity $\vec u_{\rm cm} = c^2\vec p_a/(E_a + m_bc^2)$. For identical particles of mass $m$ producing $N$ particles of mass $m$, $E_{\rm th} = \bigl(\tfrac12N^2 - 1\bigr)mc^2$.
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 12.5 and its solution, eqs. (12.105)–(12.107) · Griffiths Problem 12.35*

^thm-b2-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Before the collision $p_b = (m_bc, \vec 0)$, so $p_a\cdot p_b = (E_a/c)(m_bc) = E_am_b$ and
>
> $$
> P\cdot P = (p_a + p_b)^2 = m_a^2c^2 + m_b^2c^2 + 2E_am_b .
> $$
>
> After the collision evaluate $P\cdot P$ in the centre-of-mass frame, where $P = \bigl(\sum_iE_i^*/c, \vec 0\bigr)$ with each $E_i^* \ge m_{c_i}c^2$: so $P\cdot P \ge M_f^2c^2$, with equality exactly when every product is at rest in that frame. $P\cdot P$ is conserved and invariant ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-6|Theorem §B2.1.6]]), so the reaction needs $m_a^2c^2 + m_b^2c^2 + 2E_am_b \ge M_f^2c^2$, which is the stated bound; conversely, at or above it the products can be given momenta in the centre-of-mass frame that add to zero and share the energy, and the reaction is kinematically allowed. Products at rest in the centre-of-mass frame move with it, at $\vec u_{\rm cm} = c^2\vec P/E_{\rm tot}$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-5|Theorem §A3.3.5]]). For $m_a = m_b = m$ and $M_f = Nm$, $E_{\rm th} = (N^2 - 2)mc^2/2$.

^der-b2-1-7

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^pr-b2-1-5|Principle §B2.1.5]], [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-6|Theorem §B2.1.6]], [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-5|Theorem §A3.3.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why the threshold exceeds the mass difference
> The naive answer $K = (M_f - m_a - m_b)c^2$ ignores momentum: the products must carry the beam's momentum, and their motion in the lab costs energy that is not available for making mass. The waste grows with $M_f$: $E_{\rm th}$ grows like $M_f^2$, which is why fixed targets lose to colliders, where $W = 2E_{\rm beam}$ ([[§A8.3 Particle Accelerators and Detectors#^thm-a8-3-2|QM Theorem §A8.3.2]]; Griffiths Problem 12.35: the energy of one beam particle in the rest frame of the other is $2E^2/mc^2 - mc^2$). For $p + p \to p + p + p + \bar p$, $N = 4$ gives $E_{\rm th} = 7m_pc^2$, the antiproton threshold of [[§A8.3 Particle Accelerators and Detectors#^ex-a8-3-2|QM Example §A8.3.2]]; the figure shows the same computation in momentum space.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.8: Compton Scattering from Four-Vectors
> A photon scatters elastically off a particle of mass $m$: photon $p_\gamma \to p'_\gamma$, particle $p \to p'$. In every frame
>
> $$
> p\cdot(p_\gamma - p'_\gamma) = p_\gamma\cdot p'_\gamma .
> $$
>
> In the rest frame of the target before the collision, with the photon scattered through the angle $\theta$, this becomes
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{E'} - \frac 1E = \frac{1 - \cos\theta}{mc^2}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{mc}\,(1 - \cos\theta) ,
> $$
>
> the Compton shift of [[§A1.3 The Compton Effect#^thm-a1-3-2|QM Theorem §A1.3.2]].
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 12.7 and its solution, eqs. (12.113)–(12.114) · Schutz Exercise 2.32 · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §6.3.2*

^thm-b2-1-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Conservation ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^pr-b2-1-5|Principle §B2.1.5]]) gives $p' = p + p_\gamma - p'_\gamma$; the recoil momentum, which is not asked for, now stands alone. Square both sides and use the mass shells $p\cdot p = p'\cdot p' = m^2c^2$, $p_\gamma\cdot p_\gamma = p'_\gamma\cdot p'_\gamma = 0$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]):
>
> $$
> m^2c^2 = m^2c^2 + 2\,p\cdot p_\gamma - 2\,p\cdot p'_\gamma - 2\,p_\gamma\cdot p'_\gamma ,
> $$
>
> which is the invariant relation. In the target's rest frame $p = (mc, \vec 0)$, $p_\gamma = (E/c)(1, \hat n)$, $p'_\gamma = (E'/c)(1, \hat n')$ with $\hat n\cdot\hat n' = \cos\theta$, so $p\cdot(p_\gamma - p'_\gamma) = m(E - E')$ and $p_\gamma\cdot p'_\gamma = (EE'/c^2)(1 - \cos\theta)$. Dividing $m(E - E') = (EE'/c^2)(1 - \cos\theta)$ by $mEE'$ gives the first form, and $E = hc/\lambda$ the second.

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^pr-b2-1-5|Principle §B2.1.5]], [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|QM Principle §A1.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Squaring away what is not asked for
> Quantum Mechanics level A derives the Compton shift by eliminating the electron's recoil momentum from three component equations ([[§A1.3 The Compton Effect#^der-a1-3-2|QM §A1.3, Derivation]]); the four-vector route never introduces it. The same move settles two impossibility results at once.
> - *A free particle cannot absorb a photon* ([[§A1.3 The Compton Effect#^ex-a1-3-2|QM Example §A1.3.2]]): $p + p_\gamma = p'$ squared gives $p\cdot p_\gamma = 0$, but in the particle's rest frame $p\cdot p_\gamma = mE > 0$.
> - *An electron and a positron cannot annihilate into one photon* (Schutz Exercise 2.24): the pair has $P\cdot P = M^2c^2 \ge (2m_ec)^2 > 0$, a single photon $p_\gamma\cdot p_\gamma = 0$. Two photons can share it, back to back in the zero-momentum frame ([[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^thm-a8-1-2|QM Theorem §A8.1.2]]).
>
> Both use one geometric fact: the dot product of two future-pointing vectors, timelike or null and not both null and parallel, is positive.

## The wave four-vector and the Doppler effect

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.4: Wave Four-Vector
> A plane wave $\psi = A\cos(\omega t - \vec k\cdot\vec x)$ in an inertial frame has the phase
>
> $$
> \theta = \omega t - \vec k\cdot\vec x = k_\mu x^\mu, \qquad k^\mu = \Bigl(\frac\omega c,\ \vec k\Bigr), \quad k_\mu = \Bigl(\frac\omega c,\ -\vec k\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> $k^\mu$ is the **wave four-vector**: angular frequency and wave vector ([[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-b7-1-2|WO Def. §B7.1.2]]) as one object.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Relativistic Doppler Effect" · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §5.3.4 · Schutz §2.7*

^def-b2-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.9: The Wave Four-Vector Is a Four-Vector
> 1. $k'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu k^\nu$: frequency and wave vector transform together like $(ct, \vec x)$.
> 2. For light in vacuum, $k\cdot k = \omega^2/c^2 - |\vec k|^2 = 0$ in every frame: $k$ is null and future-pointing.
> 3. A photon's four-momentum is $p^\mu = \hbar k^\mu$, a null four-vector.
> 4. An observer with four-velocity $U$ measures the frequency $\omega_{\rm obs} = k\cdot U$.
>
> *Source: Phys505 (Wells) notes, §5.3.4 · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Relativistic Doppler Effect" · Schutz §2.7, eqs. (2.36)–(2.39)*

^thm-b2-1-9

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* The phase counts crests: whether an event lies on the $n$-th crest of the wave is a fact about the event, not about the frame describing it. Choose the common origin of the two frames on a crest; then the phase is the same number at each event in both frames, $k'_\mu x'^\mu = k_\nu x^\nu$. Insert $x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu$:
>
> $$
> \bigl(k'_\mu\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu - k_\nu\bigr)\,x^\nu = 0 \quad\text{for every event } x \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k'_\mu\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu = k_\nu .
> $$
>
> Multiplying by the inverse matrix $(\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\rho = \Lambda_\rho{}^\nu$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group|§B1.2]]) gives $k'_\rho = \Lambda_\rho{}^\nu k_\nu$, the law of lower-index components of a four-vector; raising the index ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation|§B1.1]]) gives $k'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu k^\nu$. Equivalently, $k_\mu = \partial_\mu\theta$ is the gradient of a scalar field, a covector ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]); this is the Phys505 route.
>
> *2.* Light travels at $c$ in every frame ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-2|Principle §A1.3.2]]), so $\omega = c|\vec k|$ and $k\cdot k = 0$ in every frame, as invariance of $k\cdot k$ requires. Orthochronous transformations keep $k^0 > 0$.
>
> *3.* $E = \hbar\omega$ and $\vec p = \hbar\vec k$ ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|QM Principle §A1.2.3]]); $\hbar$ is a constant, so $p^\mu = \hbar k^\mu$ is a four-vector, null by item 2, consistent with $m = 0$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]).
>
> *4.* In the observer's rest frame $U = (c, \vec 0)$ and $k\cdot U = (\omega_{\rm obs}/c)\,c$; both sides are scalars.

^der-b2-1-9

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^def-b2-1-4|Def. §B2.1.4]], [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation|§B1.1]], [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group|§B1.2]], [[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-2|Principle §A1.3.2]], [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|QM Principle §A1.2.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.10: Doppler Effect and Aberration
> A source emits light of angular frequency $\omega_s$ in its rest frame. In the rest frame of an observer, let the source move with velocity $\vec v$ ($\beta$, $\gamma$), and let the light that reaches the observer travel at the angle $\theta$ to $\vec v$; let $\theta'$ be the angle between the same light and $\vec v$ in the source's frame. Then
>
> $$
> \omega_{\rm obs} = \frac{\omega_s}{\gamma\,(1 - \beta\cos\theta)} = \gamma\,(1 + \beta\cos\theta')\,\omega_s, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{\cos\theta' + \beta}{1 + \beta\cos\theta'} ,
> $$
>
> the last relation being the **aberration** of light. Invariantly, $\omega_{\rm obs}/\omega_s = (k\cdot U_{\rm obs})/(k\cdot U_s)$. Special cases:
> - $\theta = 0$ (source approaching): $\omega_{\rm obs} = \omega_s\sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)}$; $\theta = \pi$ (receding): $\omega_s\sqrt{(1 - \beta)/(1 + \beta)}$, as in [[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]];
> - $\theta = \pi/2$ (perpendicular in the observer's frame): $\omega_{\rm obs} = \omega_s/\gamma$, a redshift;
> - $\theta' = \pi/2$ (perpendicular in the source's frame): $\omega_{\rm obs} = \gamma\omega_s$, a blueshift.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Relativistic Doppler Effect" · the user's series, Part III, §3.4 · Schutz §2.7, eq. (2.39); Exercise 2.25*

^thm-b2-1-10

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-9|Theorem §B2.1.9]] (item 4), $\omega_s = k\cdot U_s$ and $\omega_{\rm obs} = k\cdot U_{\rm obs}$, which is the invariant form. Evaluate $k\cdot U_s$ in the observer's frame, where $k = (\omega_{\rm obs}/c)(1, \hat n)$ with $\hat n\cdot\vec v = v\cos\theta$, and $U_s = \gamma(c, \vec v)$:
>
> $$
> \omega_s = k\cdot U_s = \frac{\omega_{\rm obs}}{c}\,\gamma\,(c - v\cos\theta) = \gamma\,(1 - \beta\cos\theta)\,\omega_{\rm obs} .
> $$
>
> Evaluate $k\cdot U_{\rm obs}$ in the source's frame instead, where $k = (\omega_s/c)(1, \hat n')$ and the observer moves with $-\vec v$: $\omega_{\rm obs} = (\omega_s/c)\gamma(c + v\cos\theta') = \gamma(1 + \beta\cos\theta')\omega_s$. For aberration take $\vec v$ along $x$ and boost $k$ from the source's frame to the observer's: $k^0 = \gamma(k'^0 + \beta k'^1)$, $k^1 = \gamma(k'^1 + \beta k'^0)$, so $\cos\theta = k^1/|\vec k| = k^1/k^0 = (\cos\theta' + \beta)/(1 + \beta\cos\theta')$. (The two Doppler forms agree: by the aberration formula $1 - \beta\cos\theta = (1 - \beta^2)/(1 + \beta\cos\theta')$.) The special cases follow by substitution, with $\gamma(1 \mp \beta) = \sqrt{(1 \mp \beta)/(1 \pm \beta)}$.

^der-b2-1-10

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-9|Theorem §B2.1.9]], [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]], [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group|§B1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Transverse in whose frame?
> "Light emitted at right angles to the source's motion" names two different rays. Perpendicular in the observer's frame ($\theta = \pi/2$, e.g. light emitted at closest approach) is redshifted by $1/\gamma$: this is level A's transverse Doppler effect ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^rem-a2-4-2|§A2.4, Remark: Transverse Doppler effect]]), time dilation of the source seen directly. Perpendicular in the source's frame ($\theta' = \pi/2$) is blueshifted by $\gamma$; aberration tilts that ray forward, to $\cos\theta = \beta$ in the observer's frame. The user's PHY 505 notes (§"Relativistic Doppler Effect", Case 3) take $k_0^z = 0$ in the source frame and box $\omega = \omega_0/\gamma$; with the boost formula used there, $\omega = \gamma(\omega_0 + \beta ck_0^z)$, the value $k_0^z = 0$ gives $\omega = \gamma\omega_0$, and the $1/\gamma$ redshift belongs to $90^\circ$ in the observer's frame.

^cau-b2-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: What level B adds to the Doppler effect
> - Level A obtained only the collinear shift, from time dilation and travel times, and quoted the transverse one. Here one invariant, $k\cdot U$, gives every angle, together with aberration, which A did not treat. Aberration concentrates the light of a fast source forward: rays emitted at $\theta' = \pi/2$, half of an isotropic emission, end up within $\cos\theta \ge \beta$, a cone of half-angle about $1/\gamma$ for $\gamma \gg 1$.
> - Along the line of motion the Doppler factor is $e^{\mp\phi}$, with $\phi$ the rapidity: a boost along the light's direction multiplies $k^0 + k^1$ by $e^{\phi}$ or $e^{-\phi}$, the eigenvalues of the boost ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group|§B1.2]]). Shifts from successive collinear relative motions therefore multiply, and rapidities add (the user's series, §3.4).
> - For sound the shift depends on the motion relative to the air ([[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^thm-a3-5-3|WO Theorem §A3.5.3]]): the medium gives a second four-velocity, and $\omega_{\rm obs}$ is not a function of $k\cdot U_{\rm obs}$ and $k\cdot U_s$ alone.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.1.1: Energy of a Cosmic Ray Seen from a Rocket
> In a frame $O$ a rocket has four-velocity $U = c\,(2, 1, 1, 1)$ (check: $U\cdot U = c^2(4 - 3) = c^2$; it moves at $\tfrac{\sqrt3}2c$ with $\gamma = 2$ along the diagonal) and meets a cosmic-ray nucleus with $p = (300, 299, 0, 0)\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}\cdot c$. What energy do the passengers measure?
>
> Transforming $p$ to the rocket's frame would need a boost along $(1, 1, 1)/\sqrt3$, a full $4\times4$ matrix. [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]] needs one line:
>
> $$
> E_{\rm obs} = p\cdot U = (300\cdot2 - 299\cdot1)\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}\,c^2 = 301\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}\,c^2 = 2.71\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm J \approx 1.7\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{eV} .
> $$
>
> In $O$ the energy is $300\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}\,c^2$, almost the same, although the rocket moves at $0.87c$: its motion is mostly across the ray's. The ray's mass is $m = \sqrt{300^2 - 299^2}\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg} = 2.45\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$ (about $15$ proton masses), so relative to the rocket $\gamma_{\rm rel} = E_{\rm obs}/mc^2 = 12.3$ and $v_{\rm rel} = 0.9967c$.
>
> *Source: Schutz Exercise 2.30 (solved here)*

^ex-b2-1-1

> [!example] Example §B2.1.2: Relativistic Billiards
> A particle of mass $m$ and energy $E$ hits an identical particle at rest; they collide elastically (no mass changes) and leave symmetrically, each at the angle $\theta$ to the incident direction. Find $\theta$.
>
> With $p = \sqrt{E^2 - m^2c^4}/c$, the four-momenta before are $(E/c, p, 0, 0)$ and $(mc, 0, 0, 0)$; after, by symmetry, $(E'/c, p'\cos\theta, \pm p'\sin\theta, 0)$. Energy conservation gives $E' = (E + mc^2)/2$, conservation of $p_x$ gives $p'\cos\theta = p/2$, so each outgoing four-momentum is
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{E + mc^2}{2c},\ \frac p2,\ \pm\frac p2\tan\theta,\ 0\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Its square must be $m^2c^2$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]): $4m^2c^4 = (E + mc^2)^2 - p^2c^2/\cos^2\theta$, and with $p^2c^2 = (E - mc^2)(E + mc^2)$,
>
> $$
> \cos^2\theta = \frac{(E - mc^2)(E + mc^2)}{(E + mc^2)^2 - 4m^2c^4} = \frac{E + mc^2}{E + 3mc^2} .
> $$
>
> - Nonrelativistic limit $E \to mc^2$ (not $E \to 0$): $\cos^2\theta \to \tfrac12$, $\theta = 45^\circ$, and the particles separate at $90^\circ$, the classical rule for equal masses ([[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^thm-b4-1-4|CM Theorem §B4.1.4]]).
> - For $E = 3mc^2$: $\cos^2\theta = \tfrac23$, $\theta = 35.3^\circ$, opening angle $70.5^\circ$.
> - Ultrarelativistic, $E \gg mc^2$: $\theta \to 0$; both go forward. In the centre-of-mass frame the particles leave at right angles to the beam; the boost back to the lab keeps their transverse momenta and multiplies their longitudinal ones by a large factor.
>
> No velocity was computed, and none was needed.
>
> *Source: Morin §12.3, Example (Relativistic billiards), eqs. (12.37)–(12.40)*

^ex-b2-1-2

> [!example] Example §B2.1.3: A Cosmic-Ray Proton Boosts a Microwave Photon
> A proton of energy $10^9m_pc^2$ ($\gamma = 10^9$, about $10^{18}\ \mathrm{eV}$) meets a photon of the cosmic microwave background, $E = 2\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV}$, in the Sun's frame. What is the largest energy the photon can have after Compton-scattering off the proton?
>
> *Into the proton's rest frame.* Let the proton move along $+x$ and the photon meet it head-on (along $-x$), which maximizes the result. By [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]] with $p_x = -E/c$,
>
> $$
> E^* = \gamma\,(E + \beta E) = \gamma(1 + \beta)E \approx 2\gamma E = 4.0\times10^5\ \mathrm{eV} = 0.40\ \mathrm{MeV} .
> $$
>
> *Compton scattering there.* By [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-8|Theorem §B2.1.8]] with $m = m_p$ ($m_pc^2 = 938\ \mathrm{MeV}$), backscattering ($\theta = \pi$) gives
>
> $$
> E^{*\prime} = \frac{E^*}{1 + 2E^*/m_pc^2} = \frac{0.40\ \mathrm{MeV}}{1 + 8.5\times10^{-4}} = 0.3997\ \mathrm{MeV} :
> $$
>
> the heavy proton barely recoils, and in its frame the photon simply bounces back.
>
> *Back to the Sun's frame.* The backscattered photon moves along $+x$, so $E' = \gamma(1 + \beta)E^{*\prime} \approx 2\gamma E^{*\prime} = 8.0\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{eV}$, a gamma ray of nearly $1\ \mathrm{PeV}$: the photon's energy has grown by $4\gamma^2 \approx 4\times10^{18}$. Backscattering is the maximum: for other angles the final Doppler factor $\gamma(1 + \beta\cos\theta_{\rm out})$ falls from $2\gamma$ towards $1/(2\gamma)$, while $E^{*\prime}$ changes by less than $0.1\,\%$. The proton gives up $8\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{eV}$ of its $10^{18}\ \mathrm{eV}$. The two boosts carry all the physics; the collision itself is a nonrelativistic bounce.
>
