Conservation of momentum is the main tool for collisions ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|CM Theorem §A5.2.2]]). The principle of relativity demands that it hold in every inertial frame, but with velocities transforming as in [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation]], the classical momentum $m\vec u$ cannot be conserved in all frames at once. This section finds the quantity that can, $\gamma m\vec u$, and takes its conservation as a principle. From here on $\gamma_u = 1/\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}$ refers to the speed $u$ of a particle; when a frame speed $v$ also appears, its factor is written $\gamma$ or $\gamma_v$.

## Why $m\vec u$ fails

> [!remark] Remark: A glancing collision seen from two frames
> Two identical balls of mass $m$: ball A is thrown along $+y$ with speed $u_0$ by an observer in $S$, ball B along $-y'$ with speed $u_0$ by an observer in $S'$, which moves at $v$ along $x$. They meet in a glancing elastic collision, and by symmetry each ball's $y$-velocity is reversed in its own thrower's frame. In $S$, ball B has $u_{xB} = v$ and, by the velocity transformation with $u_x' = 0$ ([[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]), $u_{yB} = -u_0/\gamma$. So the total classical $y$-momentum in $S$ is $mu_0 - mu_0/\gamma \neq 0$ before the collision and its negative after: $\sum m u_y$ is not conserved in $S$, nor (by symmetry) in $S'$. The failure is not in the velocities, which come from the Lorentz transformation, but in the expression for momentum.
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-1 · PHY 284, Relativistic momentum and energy slides*

## Relativistic momentum

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.1: Relativistic Momentum
> A particle of mass $m$ moving with velocity $\vec u$ relative to an inertial frame has **relativistic momentum**
>
> $$
> \vec p = \gamma_u m\vec u = \frac{m\vec u}{\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}}, \qquad u = |\vec u| .
> $$
>
> Here $m$ is the **mass** (rest mass): the same in every frame, measured with the particle at rest. The factor $\gamma_u$ involves the particle's whole speed, the same factor for every component. Equivalently $\vec p = m\,d\vec r/d\tau$, the displacement per unit of the particle's proper time.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.8, eqs. (5.6)–(5.7) · Tipler §2-1, eq. (2-6)*

^def-a3-1-1

> [!principle] Principle §A3.1.1: Conservation of Relativistic Momentum
> If the net external force on a system is zero, its total relativistic momentum
>
> $$
> \vec p_{\text{tot}} = \sum_i \gamma_{u_i} m_i \vec u_i
> $$
>
> is constant in time, and this holds in every inertial frame. In collisions and decays, the total momentum after equals the total momentum before.
>
> *Domain: inertial frames; isolated systems (collisions, decays, reactions); all speeds below $c$, and massless particles with momentum $E/c$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]). Reduces to classical momentum conservation for $u \ll c$.*
> *Source: UP3 §5.8 · Tipler §2-1*

^pr-a3-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: A definition built to be conserved
> Tipler and UP define relativistic momentum by two requirements: it must be conserved in collisions (in every inertial frame), and it must approach $m\vec u$ when $u \ll c$. The glancing collision fixes the form $\gamma_u m\vec u$ ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]]); symmetry demands that $\vec p$ point along $\vec u$, and any other power of $\gamma_u$ fails the test. That $\gamma_u m\vec u$ is then conserved in *every* interaction cannot be derived; it is a principle, as conservation of $m\vec u$ was for Newton (through his third law), and it is tested continually in particle accelerators. Its consistency between frames, given energy conservation as well, is proved in [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]].
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-1 · Morin §3.1.1 (and its remarks 1–2)*

^rem-a3-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.1.2: The Glancing Collision Conserves $\gamma m\vec u$
> In the glancing collision of [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^rem-a3-1-1|Remark: A glancing collision seen from two frames]], the relativistic momenta of the two balls satisfy $p_{yB} = -p_{yA}$ in $S$, at any speeds $u_0$ and $v$ below $c$. Hence total relativistic momentum is conserved in $S$, and by symmetry in $S'$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-1 · PHY 284, Relativistic momentum and energy slides*

^thm-a3-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Ball A has speed $u_0$ in $S$, so $p_{yA} = \gamma_{u_0} m u_0$. Ball B has, in $S'$, speed $u_0$ with $u_x' = 0$; by [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]] its factor in $S$ is $\gamma_{u_B} = \gamma\,\gamma_{u_0}(1 + v\cdot 0/c^2) = \gamma\,\gamma_{u_0}$. Its $y$-velocity in $S$ is $-u_0/\gamma$, so
>
> $$
> p_{yB} = \gamma_{u_B} m\,u_{yB} = \gamma\,\gamma_{u_0} m\Bigl(-\frac{u_0}{\gamma}\Bigr) = -\gamma_{u_0} m u_0 = -p_{yA} .
> $$
>
> The total $p_y$ is zero before and after the collision (the collision reverses both $y$-velocities and changes no speed). The $x$-momentum is carried by B alone, $\gamma_{u_B} m v$, and is unchanged. The factor $\gamma_{u_B}$ grows exactly as much as $|u_{yB}|$ shrinks; for $m\vec u$ the factor is missing.

> [!caution] Caution: There is one mass
> Older books write $\vec p = m_{\text{rel}}\vec u$ with a "relativistic mass" $m_{\text{rel}} = \gamma_u m$ that grows with speed. This text, like UP, Tipler and Einstein himself, does not: $m$ always means the rest mass, a frame-independent property of the particle, and $\gamma_u$ belongs to the momentum. A moving body's mass could only be inferred from its momentum anyway, so a separate velocity-dependent mass adds nothing, and it is not the inertia in $\vec F = m\vec a$ (next section).
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.8 · Tipler §2-1*

## Low and high speeds

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.1.3: Momentum at Low and High Speeds
> - For $u \ll c$, $\vec p = m\vec u\,\bigl(1 + u^2/2c^2 + \dots\bigr) \approx m\vec u$, the classical momentum ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^def-a3-3-2|CM Def. §A3.3.2]]).
> - As $u \to c$, $p \to \infty$. Conversely, the speed of a particle of momentum $p$ is
>
> $$
> u = \frac{p/m}{\sqrt{1 + (p/mc)^2}} \;<\; c ,
> $$
>
> so no finite momentum brings a massive particle to $c$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.8 · Tipler §2-1*

^thm-a3-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The binomial expansion $\gamma_u = (1 - u^2/c^2)^{-1/2} = 1 + u^2/2c^2 + \dots$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) gives the first statement; $\gamma_u \to \infty$ as $u \to c$ gives the second. For the speed, square $p = mu/\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}$: $p^2(1 - u^2/c^2) = m^2u^2$, so $u^2\,(m^2 + p^2/c^2) = p^2$ and $u^2 = (p/m)^2/\bigl(1 + (p/mc)^2\bigr)$. The denominator exceeds $(p/mc)^2$, so $u^2 < c^2$.

## Force

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.2: Force in Relativity
> The force on a particle is the rate of change of its relativistic momentum,
>
> $$
> \vec F = \frac{d\vec p}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\gamma_u m\vec u\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the momentum form of Newton's second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-3|CM Principle §A3.3.3]]) with the relativistic momentum.
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-2, eq. (2-8) · UP3 §5.9*

^def-a3-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Not $\vec F = \gamma m\vec a$
> Because $\gamma_u$ changes with speed, $d(\gamma_u m\vec u)/dt$ is not $\gamma_u m\,d\vec u/dt$. For motion along a line, $d(\gamma_u u)/du = \gamma_u^3$, so $F = \gamma_u^3 m a$: the same force produces less and less acceleration as $u \to c$. In general $\vec F$ and $\vec a$ are not even parallel, and, unlike in Galilean relativity, the force on a particle differs between inertial frames except for motion along the line of the frames' relative velocity. Energy, momentum and mass are the more useful quantities for relativistic collisions.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Relativistic momentum and energy slides · Tipler §2-2*

^cau-a3-1-2

## Example

> [!example] Example §A3.1.1: Slowing a Fast Probe
> A probe of mass $m = 5.0 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{kg}$ travels at $0.80c$ ($\gamma_u = 5/3$). Its momentum is
>
> $$
> p = \gamma_u m u = \tfrac53\,(5.0 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{kg})(0.80)(3.00 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}) = 2.0 \times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s},
> $$
>
> against the classical $mu = 1.2 \times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$. Slowed to $0.40c$ ($\gamma_u = 1.091$), it has
>
> $$
> \frac{p_{0.4c}}{p_{0.8c}} = \frac{1.091 \times 0.40}{1.667 \times 0.80} = 0.33 :
> $$
>
> halving the speed cuts the momentum to a third. Near $c$, most of a change in momentum shows up in $\gamma_u$, little in the speed.
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 2-2*

^ex-a3-1-1

> [!remark]- Connections
> - With the relativistic momentum, the impulse–momentum theorem ([[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^thm-a5-1-1|CM Theorem §A5.1.1]]) and the rest of CM chapter A5 carry over unchanged in form; only $\vec p = m\vec u$ is replaced.
> - The radius of a charged particle's circle in a magnetic field, $r = p/(|q|B)$ ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|EM Theorem §A7.2.2]]), stays valid at any speed with the relativistic $p$, since the magnetic force does no work and $\gamma_u$ stays constant; this is how momentum is measured in [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^ex-a3-3-3|Example §A3.3.3]].
> - Momentum together with energy forms a four-vector, which transforms like $(ct, x, y, z)$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]); the four-vector formalism is level B (planned).
