Energy and momentum both depend on the frame, but one combination of them does not: $E^2 - (pc)^2 = (mc^2)^2$. This relation is the most used formula of relativistic dynamics. It gives the energy of massless particles such as the photon, it shows that $E$ and $\vec p$ transform between frames exactly as $t$ and $\vec r$ do, and, applied to the totals of a system, it defines the system's mass, which is invariant and conserved but not the sum of the masses of its parts. Notation: $\gamma_u$ for a particle of speed $u$; $S'$ moves with velocity $v$ along $+x$ relative to $S$, $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^def-a1-4-1|Def. §A1.4.1]], [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^def-a1-4-2|Def. §A1.4.2]]).

## Energy and momentum of one particle

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.1: Energy–Momentum Relation
> The total energy $E$ and momentum $\vec p$ of a particle of mass $m$ satisfy
>
> $$
> E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2, \qquad \vec u = \frac{c^2\,\vec p}{E} .
> $$
>
> Hence its kinetic energy in terms of momentum is $K = \sqrt{(pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2} - mc^2$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.9, eq. (5.11) · Tipler §2-4, eqs. (2-31), (2-34) · Morin §3.1.2*

^thm-a3-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $E = \gamma_u mc^2$ ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]) and $\vec p = \gamma_u m\vec u$ ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]]),
>
> $$
> E^2 - (pc)^2 = \gamma_u^2 m^2c^4 - \gamma_u^2 m^2u^2c^2 = \gamma_u^2 m^2c^4\Bigl(1 - \frac{u^2}{c^2}\Bigr) = m^2c^4 ,
> $$
>
> and $c^2\vec p/E = c^2\gamma_u m\vec u/(\gamma_u mc^2) = \vec u$. Finally $K = E - mc^2$.

^der-a3-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: A right triangle, and its two limits
> The relation is Pythagoras for a right triangle with legs $pc$ and $mc^2$ and hypotenuse $E$. The leg $mc^2$ is the same in every frame; motion lengthens the other leg.
> - **Slow particles**, $pc \ll mc^2$: $K = mc^2\bigl(\sqrt{1 + (pc/mc^2)^2} - 1\bigr) \approx p^2/2m$, the classical relation.
> - **Ultrarelativistic particles**, $pc \gg mc^2$: $E \approx pc$ and $K \approx E$; the rest energy is negligible. A $40\ \mathrm{GeV}$ electron ($mc^2 = 0.511\ \mathrm{MeV}$) has $pc$ and $E$ equal to one part in $10^{10}$. Given $E$ and $p$, the speed follows from $u/c = pc/E$ without computing $\gamma_u$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.9 · Tipler §2-4 · PHY 286, Experiment 4 manual*

^rem-a3-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.2: Massless Particles
> A particle of zero mass has
>
> $$
> E = pc
> $$
>
> and moves at speed $c$ in every inertial frame. Conversely, a particle moving at $c$ with finite energy has zero mass.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.9 · Tipler §2-4, eq. (2-35)*

^thm-a3-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $m = 0$, [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]] gives $E = pc$, and then $u = c^2p/E = c$; by [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]] the speed is $c$ in every frame. Conversely, $E = \gamma_u mc^2$ with $\gamma_u \to \infty$ as $u \to c$ stays finite only if $m = 0$. The formulas $E = \gamma_u mc^2$ and $\vec p = \gamma_u m\vec u$ themselves become $0 \cdot \infty$ at $m = 0$, $u = c$; for massless particles $E$ and $\vec p$ are defined directly (for the photon by $E = hf$, $p = h/\lambda$), and the relation $E = pc$, the $m \to 0$ limit of Theorem §A3.3.1, is what survives.

^der-a3-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]], [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Photons and other massless particles
> - The photon, the quantum of light ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|QM Principle §A1.2.3]]), is massless, with $E = hf$ and $p = E/c = h/\lambda$. Classical electromagnetism already gives a wave that delivers energy $U$ the momentum $U/c$ ([[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure#^thm-a12-4-1|EM Theorem §A12.4.1]]); radiation pressure is this momentum. The gluon is massless too. Neutrinos were long thought massless, but they have small nonzero masses.
> - "Rest mass" has no operational meaning for a single photon, since no frame exists in which it is at rest. A *system* of photons can nevertheless have mass ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]]): two photons of $5\ \mathrm{MeV}$ and $2\ \mathrm{MeV}$ moving toward each other have $E = 7\ \mathrm{MeV}$, $pc = 3\ \mathrm{MeV}$ and $Mc^2 = \sqrt{7^2 - 3^2}\ \mathrm{MeV} = 6.3\ \mathrm{MeV}$. Conversely an electron and positron annihilating at rest ($Mc^2 = 1.022\ \mathrm{MeV}$, $p = 0$) must produce at least two photons, here back to back with $0.511\ \mathrm{MeV}$ each ([[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^thm-a8-1-2|QM Theorem §A8.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-4 (massless particles; creation and annihilation), Example 2-12*

## Changing frames

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.3: Transformation of Energy and Momentum
> If a particle has energy $E$ and momentum $\vec p$ in $S$, then in $S'$
>
> $$
> p_x' = \gamma\Bigl(p_x - \frac{vE}{c^2}\Bigr), \qquad p_y' = p_y, \qquad p_z' = p_z, \qquad E' = \gamma\,(E - v p_x) ,
> $$
>
> and inversely with $v \to -v$. In the form $p_x'c = \gamma(p_xc - \beta E)$, $E' = \gamma(E - \beta\,p_xc)$ with $\beta = v/c$, the pair $(p_xc, E)$ transforms exactly like $(x, ct)$ in the Lorentz transformation ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-2, eqs. (2-16)–(2-17) · Morin §3.2 · PHY 284, Relativistic momentum and energy slides*

^thm-a3-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let the particle have velocity $\vec u$ in $S$ and $\vec u\,'$ in $S'$. By [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]] in the direction $S \to S'$, $\gamma_{u'} = \gamma\,\gamma_u\,(1 - vu_x/c^2)$. Then, with [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]],
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> E' &= \gamma_{u'}mc^2 = \gamma\,\bigl(\gamma_u mc^2 - v\,\gamma_u m u_x\bigr) = \gamma\,(E - vp_x),\\
> p_x' &= \gamma_{u'}m u_x' = \gamma\,\gamma_u\Bigl(1 - \frac{vu_x}{c^2}\Bigr)m\,\frac{u_x - v}{1 - vu_x/c^2} = \gamma\,\bigl(\gamma_u m u_x - v\,\gamma_u m\bigr) = \gamma\Bigl(p_x - \frac{vE}{c^2}\Bigr),\\
> p_y' &= \gamma_{u'}m u_y' = \gamma\,\gamma_u\Bigl(1 - \frac{vu_x}{c^2}\Bigr)m\,\frac{u_y}{\gamma\,(1 - vu_x/c^2)} = \gamma_u m u_y = p_y ,
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> and the same for $p_z$. For a massless particle the formulas follow as the limit $m \to 0$ at fixed $E$ and $\vec p$, or directly from the Doppler effect ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]]) for photons.

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.4: The Conservation Laws Hold in Every Frame
> If the total energy and total momentum of an isolated system are both conserved in one inertial frame, they are conserved in every inertial frame. Momentum conservation in all frames in fact requires energy conservation.
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-2 · Morin §3.2*

^thm-a3-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The transformation of [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]] is linear in $(E, \vec p)$ with coefficients that depend only on $v$, the same for every particle ([[§7 Vector Space of Linear Maps#^ladr-3-1|LADR Def. 3.1]]). Summing over particles, the totals $E_{\text{tot}}, \vec p_{\text{tot}}$ transform by the same formulas, and so do their changes $\Delta E$, $\Delta\vec p$ between before and after an interaction:
>
> $$
> \Delta p_x' = \gamma\Bigl(\Delta p_x - \frac{v\,\Delta E}{c^2}\Bigr), \qquad \Delta p_y' = \Delta p_y, \qquad \Delta p_z' = \Delta p_z, \qquad \Delta E' = \gamma\,(\Delta E - v\,\Delta p_x) .
> $$
>
> If $\Delta E = 0$ and $\Delta\vec p = 0$ in $S$, all four vanish in $S'$. If only $\Delta\vec p = 0$ in $S$, then $\Delta p_x' = -\gamma v\,\Delta E/c^2$, which vanishes for all $v$ only if $\Delta E = 0$.

## The mass of a system

> [!definition] Definition §A3.3.1: Invariant Mass of a System; Zero-Momentum Frame
> For a system of particles with total energy $E_{\text{tot}} = \sum_i E_i$ and total momentum $\vec p_{\text{tot}} = \sum_i \vec p_i$, the **invariant mass** (system mass) $M$ is defined by
>
> $$
> (Mc^2)^2 = E_{\text{tot}}^2 - (p_{\text{tot}}\,c)^2 .
> $$
>
> A frame in which $\vec p_{\text{tot}} = 0$ is a **zero-momentum frame** (centre-of-mass frame); in it $E_{\text{tot}} = Mc^2$: the system's mass is its total energy in its rest frame, divided by $c^2$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-4 · PHY 284, Relativistic momentum and energy slides*

^def-a3-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.5: Invariance and Conservation of System Mass
> Let a system have invariant mass $M > 0$.
> - $M$ has the same value in every inertial frame.
> - If the system is isolated, $M$ does not change in collisions, decays or reactions inside it.
> - The system moves like one particle of mass $M$ with velocity $\vec u_{\text{cm}} = c^2\vec p_{\text{tot}}/E_{\text{tot}}$: $E_{\text{tot}} = \gamma_{u_{\text{cm}}}Mc^2$, $\vec p_{\text{tot}} = \gamma_{u_{\text{cm}}}M\vec u_{\text{cm}}$, and the zero-momentum frame moves at $\vec u_{\text{cm}}$.
> - For free (non-interacting) particles of masses $m_i$, $M \ge \sum_i m_i$, with equality only if all move with the same velocity.
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-4 · Morin §3.2*

^thm-a3-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Invariance.* As in the derivation of [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]], the totals transform by [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]. Then
>
> $$
> E'^2 - c^2p_x'^2 = \gamma^2\bigl[(E - vp_x)^2 - (cp_x - vE/c)^2\bigr] = \gamma^2\Bigl(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\Bigr)(E^2 - c^2p_x^2) = E^2 - c^2p_x^2 ,
> $$
>
> the cross terms $\mp 2\gamma^2 vEp_x$ cancelling; adding $-c^2(p_y'^2 + p_z'^2) = -c^2(p_y^2 + p_z^2)$ gives $E'^2 - c^2p'^2 = E^2 - c^2p^2$.
>
> *Conservation.* $E_{\text{tot}}$ and $\vec p_{\text{tot}}$ are conserved ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|Principle §A3.2.3]]), hence so is $M$.
>
> *One particle.* Put $\vec u_{\text{cm}} = c^2\vec p_{\text{tot}}/E_{\text{tot}}$. Then $1 - u_{\text{cm}}^2/c^2 = (E_{\text{tot}}^2 - c^2p_{\text{tot}}^2)/E_{\text{tot}}^2 = (Mc^2/E_{\text{tot}})^2 > 0$, so $u_{\text{cm}} < c$ and $\gamma_{u_{\text{cm}}} = E_{\text{tot}}/Mc^2$; this gives $E_{\text{tot}} = \gamma_{u_{\text{cm}}}Mc^2$ and $\gamma_{u_{\text{cm}}}M\vec u_{\text{cm}} = \vec p_{\text{tot}}$. Taking $v = u_{\text{cm}}$ along $\vec p_{\text{tot}}$ in Theorem §A3.3.3 gives $p'_{\text{tot}} = \gamma(p_{\text{tot}} - u_{\text{cm}}E_{\text{tot}}/c^2) = 0$.
>
> *Free particles.* In the zero-momentum frame, $Mc^2 = \sum_i \gamma_{u_i} m_i c^2 \ge \sum_i m_i c^2$, with equality only if every $u_i = 0$ there.

^der-a3-3-5

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]], [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]], [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|Principle §A3.2.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Mass is not additive
> The mass of a system is not the sum of the masses of its parts. It includes the kinetic energy of their relative motion (free particles moving apart or together: $M > \sum m_i$) and their interaction energy (bound systems: $M < \sum m_i$, [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^thm-a3-2-5|Theorem §A3.2.5]]). The extra or missing mass is not located in any of the parts; it belongs to the system. Distinguish too: total energy is conserved but frame-dependent; system mass is conserved *and* invariant; the sum of the parts' masses is invariant but not conserved.
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-4*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.3.1: Two Particles Make 10 kg
> Two particles of $m = 4.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ approach each other along $x$ at $\pm 0.60c$ in $S$. Each has $\gamma_u = 1.25$ and $E = 1.25 \times 4.0\ \mathrm{kg}\,c^2 = 5.0\ \mathrm{kg}\,c^2$; their momenta, $\pm 3.0\ \mathrm{kg}\,c$, cancel. So $S$ is the zero-momentum frame and
>
> $$
> M = \frac{E_{\text{tot}}}{c^2} = 10\ \mathrm{kg} > 8.0\ \mathrm{kg} .
> $$
>
> Check in the rest frame $S'$ of the right-hand particle ($v = -0.60c$; the other particle then moves at $(0.60c + 0.60c)/1.36 = 0.882c$, $\gamma_u = 2.125$). By [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]] the totals become $E' = \gamma(E_{\text{tot}} - v\cdot 0) = 1.25 \times 10\ \mathrm{kg}\,c^2 = 12.5\ \mathrm{kg}\,c^2$ and $p_x' = \gamma(0 - vE_{\text{tot}}/c^2) = 7.5\ \mathrm{kg}\,c$, the same as adding $4.0 + 2.125 \times 4.0 = 12.5\ \mathrm{kg}\,c^2$ and $2.125 \times 4.0 \times 0.882 = 7.5\ \mathrm{kg}\,c$ particle by particle. Then $Mc^2 = \sqrt{12.5^2 - 7.5^2}\ \mathrm{kg}\,c^2 = 10\ \mathrm{kg}\,c^2$ again. If the particles stick together, the result is a body of $10\ \mathrm{kg}$ at rest in $S$ ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^thm-a3-2-4|Theorem §A3.2.4]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 2-11 · PHY 284, Relativistic momentum and energy slides*

^ex-a3-3-1

> [!example] Example §A3.3.2: A Kaon Decays into Two Pions
> A neutral kaon at rest ($498\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$) decays as $K^0 \to \pi^0 + \pi^0$ ($135\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$ each). Energy conservation: the kinetic energy released is
>
> $$
> Q = 498\ \mathrm{MeV} - 2(135\ \mathrm{MeV}) = 228\ \mathrm{MeV} .
> $$
>
> The total momentum is zero, so the pions fly apart in opposite directions with equal momenta, hence (Theorem §A3.3.1) equal energies: $E = 498/2 = 249\ \mathrm{MeV}$ each, $K = 114\ \mathrm{MeV}$ each, and
>
> $$
> pc = \sqrt{E^2 - (mc^2)^2} = \sqrt{249^2 - 135^2}\ \mathrm{MeV} = 209\ \mathrm{MeV}, \qquad \frac{u}{c} = \frac{pc}{E} = 0.840 .
> $$
>
> The invariant mass of the two pions is $498\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$, the kaon's, in agreement with [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-5|Theorem §A3.3.5]]; this is how unstable particles are identified from their decay products.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 4 solutions*

^ex-a3-3-2

> [!example] Example §A3.3.3: Measuring K Against p for Fast Electrons
> In the relativistic-energy experiment, beta-decay electrons from $^{90}\mathrm{Sr}$ enter a magnet, run a semicircle of radius $r$ and are detected at the distance $2r$, which selects the momentum $p = eBr$ ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|EM Theorem §A7.2.2]]). This holds at any speed with the relativistic momentum: the magnetic force does no work ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-1|EM Theorem §A7.2.1]]), so $\gamma_u$ is constant and $d(\gamma_u m\vec u)/dt = \gamma_u m\,d\vec u/dt$. A scintillation detector and pulse-height analyser measure the kinetic energy $K$, calibrated with the monoenergetic $0.975\ \mathrm{MeV}$ electrons emitted by $^{207}\mathrm{Bi}$.
>
> *Field.* By Theorem §A3.3.1 the calibration electrons have $E = 0.975 + 0.511 = 1.486\ \mathrm{MeV}$ and $pc = \sqrt{1.486^2 - 0.511^2}\ \mathrm{MeV} = 1.395\ \mathrm{MeV}$; they were found at $r = 3.75\ \mathrm{cm}$, so $B = p/(er) = 0.124 \pm 0.004\ \mathrm T$, and $p = (37.2\ \mathrm{MeV}/c\ \text{per m})\,r$.
>
> *Data* (momentum uncertainty $\pm 0.037\ \mathrm{MeV}/c$ from $\pm 1\ \mathrm{mm}$ in $r$; the third row is the calibration position):
>
> | $r$ (cm) | $p$ (MeV$/c$) | $K$ measured (MeV) | $\sqrt{(pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2} - mc^2$ | $p^2/2m$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 2.85 | 1.060 | 0.627 ± 0.016 | 0.666 | 1.10 |
> | 3.25 | 1.209 | 0.794 ± 0.023 | 0.802 | 1.43 |
> | 3.75 | 1.395 | 0.985 ± 0.020 | 0.975 | 1.90 |
> | 4.50 | 1.674 | 1.261 ± 0.029 | 1.239 | 2.74 |
> | 5.25 | 1.953 | 1.551 ± 0.027 | 1.508 | 3.73 |
>
> Including the momentum uncertainty (it shifts $K$ by $\beta \times 0.037\ \mathrm{MeV}$), every point agrees with the relativistic relation to within about one standard deviation, while the Newtonian $p^2/2m$ is too large by factors of $1.8$ to $2.4$ and is ruled out. The fastest electrons have $u/c = pc/E = 1.953/2.019 = 0.97$.
>
> *Source: PHY 286, Relativistic energy lab (the user's analysis) · PHY 286, Experiment 4 manual*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $(E/c, \vec p)$ is a four-vector: it transforms by the same linear map as $(ct, \vec r)$ ([[§7 Vector Space of Linear Maps#^ladr-3-1|LADR Def. 3.1]]), and $(mc^2)^2 = E^2 - (pc)^2$ is its invariant "length", the analogue of $c^2\Delta\tau^2 = -\Delta s^2$ for the interval ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]], [[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]). The four-vector formalism is level B (planned).
> - The Compton shift ([[§A1.3 The Compton Effect#^thm-a1-3-2|QM Theorem §A1.3.2]]) applies energy and momentum conservation with $E = pc$ for the photon and Theorem §A3.3.1 for the electron. The energy available in a collision is the invariant mass of the colliding pair ([[§A8.3 Particle Accelerators and Detectors#^thm-a8-3-2|QM Theorem §A8.3.2]]); particle decays and conservation laws: [[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws|QM §A8.1]].
> - Measuring momentum by the radius in a magnetic field: [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|EM Theorem §A7.2.2]]; for particle tracks, [[§A8.3 Particle Accelerators and Detectors#^thm-a8-3-1|QM Theorem §A8.3.1]].
> - Theorem §A3.3.1 for every electron of a degenerate gas gives its pressure at any density, from nonrelativistic to ultrarelativistic, as in a white dwarf: [[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^thm-b13-3-2|TH Theorem §B13.3.2]].
> - Quantum Mechanics level B: expanding Theorem §A3.3.1 to order $p^4$ gives the relativistic correction to the energy levels of hydrogen — [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-2|QM Theorem §B8.3.2]].
