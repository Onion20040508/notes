With the relativistic momentum of [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum]], the work needed to bring a particle up to speed is no longer $\tfrac12 mu^2$: it grows without limit as $u \to c$. Rearranging that work into "total minus rest" energy uncovers the rest energy $mc^2$, and with it the equivalence of mass and energy: any energy stored in a body adds to its mass, and the mass of a bound system falls short of the mass of its parts by the binding energy. As before, $\gamma_u = 1/\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}$ for a particle of speed $u$.

## Kinetic energy

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.1: Kinetic Energy
> The **kinetic energy** $K$ of a particle moving at speed $u$ is the work the net force must do to accelerate it from rest to that speed, with the force of [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-2|Def. §A3.1.2]] ($\vec F = d\vec p/dt$) and work as in [[§A4.1 Work#^def-a4-1-1|CM Def. §A4.1.1]]. This keeps the classical work–energy theorem ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^thm-a4-2-1|CM Theorem §A4.2.1]]) true by construction.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.9 · Tipler §2-2*

^def-a3-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.1: Relativistic Kinetic Energy
> A particle of mass $m$ and speed $u$ has kinetic energy
>
> $$
> K = (\gamma_u - 1)\,mc^2 = \frac{mc^2}{\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}} - mc^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.9, eq. (5.8) · Tipler §2-2, eq. (2-9)*

^thm-a3-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the motion along $x$ (a force perpendicular to the velocity does no work). With $dx = u\,dt$,
>
> $$
> K = \int F\,dx = \int \frac{dp}{dt}\,u\,dt = \int_0^{u} u'\,\frac{dp}{du'}\,du' .
> $$
>
> Differentiating $p = mu'(1 - u'^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$ by the product and chain rules ([[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]),
>
> $$
> \frac{dp}{du'} = m\Bigl(1 - \frac{u'^2}{c^2}\Bigr)^{-1/2} + m\,\frac{u'^2}{c^2}\Bigl(1 - \frac{u'^2}{c^2}\Bigr)^{-3/2} = m\Bigl(1 - \frac{u'^2}{c^2}\Bigr)^{-3/2} .
> $$
>
> The integrand $mu'(1 - u'^2/c^2)^{-3/2}$ is the derivative of $mc^2(1 - u'^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$, so by the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]])
>
> $$
> K = \Bigl[\frac{mc^2}{\sqrt{1 - u'^2/c^2}}\Bigr]_0^{u} = \gamma_u mc^2 - mc^2 .
> $$
>
> (UP reaches the same result by integrating by parts.)

^der-a3-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-2|Def. §A3.1.2]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.2: Classical Limit and the Speed Limit
> - For $u \ll c$, $K = \tfrac12 mu^2 + \tfrac38 mu^4/c^2 + \dots \approx \tfrac12 mu^2$, the classical kinetic energy ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|CM Def. §A4.2.1]]).
> - As $u \to c$, $K \to \infty$: no finite amount of work brings a particle with mass to the speed of light.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.9 · Tipler §2-2 · PHY 284, HW 4 solutions*

^thm-a3-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The binomial series ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) gives $\gamma_u = (1 - u^2/c^2)^{-1/2} = 1 + \tfrac12 u^2/c^2 + \tfrac38 u^4/c^4 + \dots$, so $(\gamma_u - 1)mc^2 = \tfrac12 mu^2 + \tfrac38 mu^4/c^2 + \dots$; the correction is a fraction $\tfrac34 u^2/c^2$ of the classical value. As $u \to c$, $\gamma_u \to \infty$.

^der-a3-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

## Rest energy and total energy

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.2: Rest Energy; Total Energy
> A particle of mass $m$ has **rest energy** $E_0 = mc^2$ and, moving at speed $u$, **total energy**
>
> $$
> E = \gamma_u mc^2 = K + mc^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.9, eqs. (5.9)–(5.10) · Tipler §2-2, eq. (2-10)*

^def-a3-2-2

> [!principle] Principle §A3.2.3: Conservation of Total Energy
> In an isolated system the total energy $\sum_i E_i$, rest energies included, is the same before and after any interaction (collision, decay, reaction), and this holds in every inertial frame. Neither the total kinetic energy nor the total mass is conserved separately.
>
> *Domain: inertial frames; isolated systems. The relativistic form of the conservation of energy ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]); for slow particles with conserved masses it reduces to it, since the rest energies then cancel.*
> *Source: UP3 §5.9 · Tipler §2-2*

^pr-a3-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Conserved is not invariant
> - **Built to be conserved.** As with momentum, the relativistic energy is defined to satisfy two conditions: it is conserved for isolated systems in every inertial frame, and it reduces to the classical expression (up to the constant $mc^2$, harmless because the zero of energy is arbitrary while masses do not change) when $u \ll c$. Conservation itself is a principle confirmed by experiment; Tipler checks it in a perfectly inelastic collision in two frames, and [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]] shows that conservation in one frame implies it in all.
> - **Two different properties.** A *conserved* quantity keeps its value through an interaction, in a given frame; an *invariant* quantity has the same value in all frames. Energy is conserved but not invariant ($E = \gamma_u mc^2$ depends on the frame); a particle's mass is invariant but the total mass of a system is not conserved.
> - **Simpler bookkeeping.** In relativity heat and other internal energy need no separate account: they are part of the rest energy of the body that holds them.
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-2 · Morin §3.1.2 (remark 3)*

^rem-a3-2-1

## Mass–energy equivalence

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.4: Mass–Energy Equivalence
> (a) If a body at rest gains internal energy $\Delta E$ (heat, stored chemical or elastic energy, kinetic energy of its parts) without gaining momentum, its mass increases by
>
> $$
> \Delta m = \frac{\Delta E}{c^2} .
> $$
>
> (b) In particular, two particles of mass $m$ that collide head-on with equal speeds $u$ and stick form a body of mass
>
> $$
> M = 2\gamma_u m = 2m + \frac{K}{c^2} > 2m ,
> $$
>
> where $K = 2(\gamma_u - 1)mc^2$ is the kinetic energy that disappears in the collision; momentum conservation in a second frame alone forces this value.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.9 · Tipler §2-3, Example 2-7 · Morin §3.1.2*

^thm-a3-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (a) Treat the body as one particle of mass $M$. It absorbs $\Delta E$ with no net momentum, so by [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]] it stays at rest, and by [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|Principle §A3.2.3]] its energy becomes $Mc^2 + \Delta E$. A body at rest has total energy equal to its rest energy ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]), so its new mass $M'$ satisfies $M'c^2 = Mc^2 + \Delta E$. (That a composite body behaves as a particle of mass $\sqrt{E^2 - p^2c^2}/c^2$ is [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-5|Theorem §A3.3.5]].)
>
> (b) In the zero-momentum frame $S$ the composite body is at rest. Go to the frame $S'$ moving with the right-hand particle (velocity $u$ relative to $S$ along the line of motion). There the right particle is at rest, the composite body moves at $u$, and the left particle moves at $u' = 2u/(1 + u^2/c^2)$ ([[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]), with
>
> $$
> 1 - \frac{u'^2}{c^2} = \frac{(1 - u^2/c^2)^2}{(1 + u^2/c^2)^2}, \qquad \gamma_{u'} = \gamma_u^2\Bigl(1 + \frac{u^2}{c^2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Momentum conservation in $S'$: $\gamma_{u'} m u' = \gamma_u M u$, i.e. $\gamma_u^2(1 + u^2/c^2)\,m\cdot 2u/(1 + u^2/c^2) = \gamma_u M u$, so $M = 2\gamma_u m$. Then $Mc^2 = 2mc^2 + 2(\gamma_u - 1)mc^2 = 2mc^2 + K$. Energy is then conserved in both frames. In $S$: $2\gamma_u mc^2 = Mc^2$. In $S'$, with $\beta = u/c$ and $\gamma_u^2\beta^2 = \gamma_u^2 - 1$: before, $\gamma_{u'}mc^2 + mc^2 = \bigl(\gamma_u^2 + \gamma_u^2\beta^2 + 1\bigr)mc^2 = 2\gamma_u^2 mc^2$; after, $\gamma_u Mc^2 = 2\gamma_u^2 mc^2$.

^der-a3-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|Principle §A3.2.3]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]], [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]], [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-5|Theorem §A3.3.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why mass seemed conserved
> Nineteenth-century chemistry confirmed the conservation of mass with great precision, yet relativity says that heating, charging or compressing a body changes its mass. The two agree because $c^2$ is enormous: ordinary energy changes alter masses by parts in $10^{10}$ or less ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^ex-a3-2-2|Example §A3.2.2]]). In nuclear reactions the fraction is about $10^{-3}$ and is measured routinely; the Sun, radiating $3.85 \times 10^{26}\ \mathrm W$, loses $4.3 \times 10^9\ \mathrm{kg}$ of mass every second. Einstein proposed in 1905 that radium salts might show the effect, before the nucleus was known.
>
> *Source: UP3 §5.9 · Tipler §2-3, Example 2-6*

^rem-a3-2-2

## Binding energy

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.3: Binding Energy
> The **binding energy** $E_b$ of a bound system (an atom, a nucleus, a molecule) is the minimum energy that must be supplied to separate it into its constituents, at rest and far apart.
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-3*

^def-a3-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.5: Mass of a Bound System
> A bound system of constituents with masses $m_i$ and binding energy $E_b$ has mass
>
> $$
> M = \sum_i m_i - \frac{E_b}{c^2} :
> $$
>
> it is lighter than its separated parts by the **mass defect** $E_b/c^2$. Conversely, forming the system from its parts at rest releases the energy $E_b$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-3*

^thm-a3-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Start with the system at rest: total energy $Mc^2$ ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]). Supply exactly $E_b$, ending with the constituents at rest and far apart, where they no longer interact: total energy $\sum m_i c^2$. Energy is conserved ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|Principle §A3.2.3]]), so $Mc^2 + E_b = \sum m_i c^2$. Running the process backwards releases $E_b$.

^der-a3-2-5

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-3|Def. §A3.2.3]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|Principle §A3.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Units for particles and nuclei
> Energies are given in electron-volts ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-2|EM Def. §A3.2.2]]), $1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm J$, with $\mathrm{keV}$, $\mathrm{MeV}$, $\mathrm{GeV}$ for $10^3$, $10^6$, $10^9\ \mathrm{eV}$. Masses are then quoted as rest energies divided by $c^2$, in $\mathrm{MeV}/c^2$, and momenta in $\mathrm{MeV}/c$: electron $0.511\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$, proton $938.272\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$, neutron $939.565\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$, muon $105.7\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$. The atomic mass unit is $1\ \mathrm u = 1.66054 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg} = 931.494\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$. With these units most factors of $c$ drop out of calculations.
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-2, §2-3 (Table 2-1)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.2.1: How Large Is the Relativistic Correction?
> (a) An electron ($mc^2 = 0.511\ \mathrm{MeV}$) moves at $0.990c$, so $\gamma_u = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.990^2} = 7.09$ and
>
> $$
> K = (\gamma_u - 1)mc^2 = (6.09)(0.511\ \mathrm{MeV}) = 3.11\ \mathrm{MeV}, \qquad \tfrac12 mu^2 = \tfrac12(0.511\ \mathrm{MeV})(0.990)^2 = 0.250\ \mathrm{MeV} .
> $$
>
> The classical formula is low by a factor of $12$: reaching $0.990c$ takes far more energy than Newtonian mechanics predicts.
>
> (b) The electron in a hydrogen atom moves at about $2.3 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{m/s}$, $\beta = 7.7 \times 10^{-3}$. By [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]] the kinetic energies differ by a fraction $\tfrac34\beta^2 = 4.4 \times 10^{-5}$, and the momenta by $\gamma_u - 1 \approx \tfrac12\beta^2 = 2.9 \times 10^{-5}$: negligible for most atomic physics.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 5.12 · PHY 284, HW 4 solutions*

^ex-a3-2-1

> [!example] Example §A3.2.2: The Mass of a Charged Battery
> A car battery stores the energy to move $600\ \mathrm{A\,h}$ of charge through $12.0\ \mathrm V$:
>
> $$
> \Delta E = qV = (600\ \mathrm A)(3600\ \mathrm s)(12.0\ \mathrm V) = 2.59 \times 10^7\ \mathrm J, \qquad \Delta m = \frac{\Delta E}{c^2} = \frac{2.59 \times 10^7\ \mathrm J}{(3.00 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s})^2} = 2.88 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm{kg} .
> $$
>
> Fully charging a $20.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ battery (no reactants entering or leaving) raises its mass by $1.4 \times 10^{-11}$ of itself, far below any weighing precision.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 5.14*

^ex-a3-2-2

> [!example] Example §A3.2.3: Binding Energies of a Nucleus and an Atom
> The deuteron (one proton, one neutron) has rest energy $1875.613\ \mathrm{MeV}$, while $m_pc^2 + m_nc^2 = 938.272\ \mathrm{MeV} + 939.565\ \mathrm{MeV} = 1877.837\ \mathrm{MeV}$. By [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^thm-a3-2-5|Theorem §A3.2.5]], $E_b = 2.224\ \mathrm{MeV}$, a mass defect of about $1.2 \times 10^{-3}$ of the total: at least $2.224\ \mathrm{MeV}$ (from a gamma ray, say) is needed to split it, and that much is released when a proton captures a neutron.
>
> For the hydrogen atom $E_b = 13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$, so the atom is lighter than a free proton and electron by $13.6\ \mathrm{eV}/c^2 = 1.46 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm u$, about $1.5 \times 10^{-8}$ of its mass: atomic binding is a million times weaker than nuclear binding and its mass defect is usually neglected.
>
> *Source: Tipler §2-3, Example 2-8*
