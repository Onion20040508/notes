Which transformations relate the coordinates of inertial observers? Level A derived the boost between frames in standard configuration from the two postulates ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]]), checked that it leaves the interval unchanged ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]) and composed collinear velocities ([[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]). Level B turns the logic round. It first shows that the postulates force the interval to be invariant, then *defines* a Lorentz transformation as any linear map that preserves the Minkowski metric of [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation|§B1.1]], $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda = \boldsymbol\eta$, and studies the set of all of them: it is a group with four disconnected pieces, every element of the physical piece is a rotation followed by a boost, boosts along one axis are hyperbolic rotations whose rapidities add, and adding translations gives the ten-parameter Poincaré group.

## From the postulates to the invariant

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.1: The Postulates Force the Invariance of the Interval
> Let two inertial frames $S$ and $S'$ have parallel axes, relative velocity $\vec v$ and a common origin event, and let their coordinates be related by a map $x' = L_{\vec v}\,x$ that is continuous and depends continuously on $\vec v$. If space and time are homogeneous and space is isotropic, so that $L_{\vec v}$ depends on nothing but $\vec v$ (the same map for every such pair of frames), light travels with speed $c$ in every direction in both frames ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-2|Principle §A1.3.2]]), and the principle of relativity holds ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-1|Principle §A1.3.1]]), then $L_{\vec v}$ is linear and
>
> $$
> \Delta s'^2 = \Delta s^2 \quad\text{for every pair of events}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad L_{\vec v}^{\mathsf T}\,\boldsymbol\eta\,L_{\vec v} = \boldsymbol\eta .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §2.2–2.3 (Lemma: Linearity; Theorem: Invariance of the interval), Appendix A · Schutz §1.6, eqs. (1.2)–(1.7), Exercise 1.8*

^thm-b1-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Step 0: linearity.* Homogeneity means that the transformed displacement between two events depends only on their displacement, not on where they sit: $L(x + a) - L(x) = g(a)$ for all $x$. Then $g(a_1 + a_2) = g(a_1) + g(a_2)$ (insert the intermediate event $x + a_1$), so $g(qa) = q\,g(a)$ for rational $q$, and by continuity for real $q$: $g$ is linear. Setting $x = 0$, $L(a) = g(a) + L(0) = g(a)$, since the origins coincide (series, Appendix A).
>
> *Step 1: the light cone fixes the form up to a factor.* By Step 0, $\Delta s'^2 = \eta_{\mu\nu}(L\Delta x)^\mu(L\Delta x)^\nu = M_{\rho\sigma}\Delta x^\rho\Delta x^\sigma$ with the symmetric matrix $M = L^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta L$. Two events joined by a light signal have $\Delta s^2 = 0$ in $S$, and by the second postulate also $\Delta s'^2 = 0$. Take any $\Delta\vec x$ and $\Delta x^0 = r = |\Delta\vec x|$:
>
> $$
> 0 = M_{00}\,r^2 + 2r\,M_{0i}\Delta x^i + M_{ij}\Delta x^i\Delta x^j .
> $$
>
> Replacing $\Delta\vec x$ by $-\Delta\vec x$ (also a light signal) and subtracting gives $M_{0i}\Delta x^i = 0$ for every $\Delta\vec x$, so $M_{0i} = 0$. What remains says that the quadratic forms $M_{ij}\Delta x^i\Delta x^j$ and $-M_{00}\,\delta_{ij}\Delta x^i\Delta x^j$ agree on all of $\mathbf R^3$; a quadratic form determines its symmetric matrix ([[§35 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-21|LADR Thm. 9.21]]), so $M_{ij} = -M_{00}\,\delta_{ij}$. Hence $M = \lambda\,\boldsymbol\eta$ with $\lambda(\vec v) = M_{00}$:
>
> $$
> \Delta s'^2 = \lambda(\vec v)\,\Delta s^2 .
> $$
>
> *Step 2: isotropy.* Turn the spatial axes of both frames through a half-turn about an axis perpendicular to $\vec v$: $x \mapsto \hat Rx$ with $\hat R = \operatorname{diag}(1, R)$, $R \in SO(3)$ and $R\vec v = -\vec v$. The new pair of frames again has parallel axes, now with relative velocity $-\vec v$, and is related by $\hat R\,L_{\vec v}\,\hat R^{-1}$; since space has no preferred direction, this is the map for that velocity, $L_{-\vec v} = \hat R\,L_{\vec v}\,\hat R^{-1}$. A rotation does not change $\Delta s^2$, so $\lambda(-\vec v) = \lambda(\vec v)$. (The series makes the same step in one space dimension with the reflection $x \mapsto -x$; in three dimensions the half-turn does it without assuming mirror symmetry.)
>
> *Step 3: reciprocity.* By the principle of relativity, $S$ moves with velocity $-\vec v$ relative to $S'$, and the transformation back is the one belonging to that velocity: $L_{\vec v}^{-1} = L_{-\vec v}$. Applying Step 1 there and back, $\Delta s^2 = \lambda(-\vec v)\lambda(\vec v)\,\Delta s^2$, so $\lambda(\vec v)^2 = 1$ by Step 2.
>
> *Step 4: continuity.* $\lambda$ is a continuous function on the ball $|\vec v| < c$, with values in $\{+1, -1\}$ and $\lambda(\vec 0) = 1$ (at $\vec v = 0$ the frames coincide). A continuous image of the connected ball is connected ([[§15 Connected Spaces#^thm-15-3|590 Thm. §15.3]]), so $\lambda \equiv 1$. Thus $M = L^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta L = \boldsymbol\eta$ (Step 1), that is, $\Delta s'^2 = \Delta s^2$ for all displacements.

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from level A
> - **Order of the argument.** Level A derived the boost from the postulates by following one light flash ([[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^der-a1-4-1|§A1.4]]) and then checked the invariance of the interval ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]). Theorem §B1.2.1 gets the invariant first, using light signals in *every* direction, and needs no formula for the transformation. The second postulate alone only protects the light cone, $\Delta s^2 = 0$; the principle of relativity, through isotropy and reciprocity, fixes the scale. Everything else, including the boost formula, is then algebra ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-7|Theorem §B1.2.7]]).
> - **Layers.** The two postulates stay principles. The invariance of the interval was a theorem at level A and is a theorem here, but from now on it serves as the *definition* of a Lorentz transformation (below), and the Lorentz group, not a particular formula, is the object of study.
> - **What linearity rests on.** The user's series derives linearity from homogeneity with Cauchy's functional equation (Step 0); it also mentions a second route, from the fact that free particles move on straight worldlines in every inertial frame, which needs the harder fundamental theorem of affine geometry.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §2.3 (Remark: What just happened), Appendix A (Remark: An alternative route)*

## Lorentz transformations

> [!definition] Definition §B1.2.1: Lorentz Transformation; Lorentz Group; Four-Vector
> A **Lorentz transformation** is a real $4\times4$ matrix $\Lambda = [\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu]$ (row $\mu$, column $\nu$) that preserves the metric,
>
> $$
> \eta_{\mu\nu}\,\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho\,\Lambda^\nu{}_\sigma = \eta_{\rho\sigma}, \qquad\text{in matrix form}\qquad \Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\,\Lambda = \boldsymbol\eta ,
> $$
>
> acting on coordinates by $x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,x^\nu$. The set of all Lorentz transformations is the **Lorentz group** $O(1,3)$. A **four-vector** is a quantity with four components $a^\mu$ in every inertial frame such that, whenever two frames are related by $\Lambda$, its components are related by $a'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,a^\nu$, as the coordinate differences $\Delta x^\mu$ are.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Lorentz Transformations: Definition" · the user's series, Part III, §3.1, eqs. for the defining condition and its matrix form · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.2.3, eq. (5.34), §5.3.2 · Schutz §2.1, eq. (2.7)*

^def-b1-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.2: Equivalent Characterizations
> For a real $4\times4$ matrix $\Lambda$ the following are equivalent:
> 1. $\Lambda$ preserves the interval: $(\Lambda a)^2 = a^2$ for every $a$;
> 2. $\Lambda$ preserves all scalar products: $(\Lambda a)\cdot(\Lambda b) = a\cdot b$ for all $a, b$;
> 3. $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\,\Lambda = \boldsymbol\eta$, i.e. $\Lambda$ is a Lorentz transformation.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Lorentz Transformations: Definition" · the user's series, Part III, §3.1 (polarization argument) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.2.3, eqs. (5.29)–(5.34) · Schutz §2.5*

^thm-b1-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1 ⟹ 2* is polarization ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], part 4). *2 ⟹ 3:* apply 2 to the basis vectors $a = e_{(\rho)}$, $b = e_{(\sigma)}$, whose images are the columns of $\Lambda$, $(\Lambda e_{(\rho)})^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\rho$: $\eta_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho\Lambda^\nu{}_\sigma = e_{(\rho)}\cdot e_{(\sigma)} = \eta_{\rho\sigma}$. *3 ⟹ 1:* $(\Lambda a)^2 = (\Lambda a)^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta(\Lambda a) = a^{\mathsf T}(\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda)a = a^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\,a = a^2$. In words: the columns of $\Lambda$ are a basis that is orthonormal for $\eta$, as the coordinate basis is ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^ex-b1-1-2|Example §B1.1.2]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.3: The Lorentz Group; Inverse; Lowered Components
> 1. $O(1,3)$ is a group under matrix multiplication, a subgroup of $GL(4, \mathbf R)$.
> 2. The inverse needs no inversion: $\Lambda^{-1} = \boldsymbol\eta^{-1}\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta$, in index form
>
> $$
> (\Lambda^{-1})^\mu{}_\nu = \Lambda_\nu{}^\mu \equiv \eta_{\nu\rho}\,\Lambda^\rho{}_\sigma\,\eta^{\sigma\mu} ,
> $$
>
> the transpose with both indices moved by the metric.
> 3. $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}$ is also a Lorentz transformation: $\Lambda\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda^{\mathsf T} = \boldsymbol\eta$.
> 4. The lowered components of a four-vector transform with the inverse transpose, $a'_\mu = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu\,a_\nu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu\,a_\nu$, and the contraction $a^\mu b_\mu$ is the same number in every frame.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Relation Between Λ and Λ⁻¹" · the user's series, Part III, §3.1 (consequences (ii) and (iii)), §3.8 · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.3.4–5.3.6, eqs. (5.69)–(5.84) · Schutz §2.2, eq. (2.18)*

^thm-b1-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* If $\Lambda_1, \Lambda_2 \in O(1,3)$, then $(\Lambda_1\Lambda_2)^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta(\Lambda_1\Lambda_2) = \Lambda_2^{\mathsf T}(\Lambda_1^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda_1)\Lambda_2 = \Lambda_2^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda_2 = \boldsymbol\eta$, and the identity is in $O(1,3)$. Taking determinants of $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda = \boldsymbol\eta$ gives $(\det\Lambda)^2 = 1$, so every element is invertible; by the subgroup criterion ([[§4 Subgroups#^prop-4-2|493 Prop. §4.2]]) it remains to see that the inverse lies in $O(1,3)$, which is part 2.
>
> *2.* Multiply $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda = \boldsymbol\eta$ on the left by $\boldsymbol\eta^{-1}$: $(\boldsymbol\eta^{-1}\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta)\Lambda = \mathbb 1$, so $\Lambda^{-1} = \boldsymbol\eta^{-1}\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta$. Its entries are $\eta^{\mu\sigma}(\Lambda^{\mathsf T})_\sigma{}^\rho\eta_{\rho\nu} = \eta^{\mu\sigma}\Lambda^\rho{}_\sigma\eta_{\rho\nu} = \Lambda_\nu{}^\mu$. Multiplying $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda = \boldsymbol\eta$ by $(\Lambda^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$ on the left and $\Lambda^{-1}$ on the right gives $\boldsymbol\eta = (\Lambda^{-1})^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda^{-1}$: the inverse is a Lorentz transformation.
>
> *3.* Invert both sides of $(\Lambda^{-1})^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda^{-1} = \boldsymbol\eta$: $\Lambda\boldsymbol\eta^{-1}\Lambda^{\mathsf T} = \boldsymbol\eta^{-1}$, and $\boldsymbol\eta^{-1} = \boldsymbol\eta$ ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]).
>
> *4.* $a'_\mu = \eta_{\mu\nu}a'^\nu = \eta_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^\nu{}_\rho\,a^\rho = \eta_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^\nu{}_\rho\,\eta^{\rho\sigma}a_\sigma = \Lambda_\mu{}^\sigma a_\sigma$, which by part 2 is $(\Lambda^{-1})^\sigma{}_\mu\,a_\sigma$; as a matrix acting on the column $(a_\sigma)$ this is $(\Lambda^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$. Then $a'^\mu b'_\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\rho a^\rho\,(\Lambda^{-1})^\sigma{}_\mu b_\sigma = (\Lambda^{-1}\Lambda)^\sigma{}_\rho\,a^\rho b_\sigma = a^\sigma b_\sigma$. Lowering then transforming agrees with transforming then lowering; both say $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda = \boldsymbol\eta$ once more.

> [!caution] Caution: Transpose, not inverse; and the order of the indices
> - The defining condition has the **transpose** on the left: $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda = \boldsymbol\eta$. The look-alike $\Lambda^{-1}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda = \boldsymbol\eta$ would say that $\boldsymbol\eta$ commutes with $\Lambda$, which is false already for a single boost: $\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda$ flips the signs of the spatial rows of $\Lambda$, $\Lambda\boldsymbol\eta$ those of its spatial columns. For rotations, where the metric is the identity, transpose and inverse coincide, which is why the distinction never arose in three dimensions (Phys505, §5.3.4).
> - In $\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$ and $\Lambda_\nu{}^\mu$ the *horizontal* order of the indices matters: the second is the inverse of the first, not the same array with indices moved. Writing the indices stacked vertically loses this information.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.1 (consequences (i)–(ii)) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.3.4*

## The four components

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.4: Determinant, Time Component and the Four Pieces of the Lorentz Group
> For every $\Lambda \in O(1,3)$:
> 1. $\det\Lambda = \pm1$;
> 2. $(\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 = 1 + \sum_i(\Lambda^i{}_0)^2 = 1 + \sum_i(\Lambda^0{}_i)^2$, so either $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$ or $\Lambda^0{}_0 \le -1$;
> 3. the transformations with $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$ form a subgroup, and so do those with $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$ and $\det\Lambda = +1$, the **proper orthochronous Lorentz group** $SO^+(1,3)$;
> 4. $O(1,3)$ is the disjoint union of four pieces, labelled by $(\det\Lambda, \operatorname{sgn}\Lambda^0{}_0)$:
>
> | piece | $\det\Lambda$ | $\operatorname{sgn}\Lambda^0{}_0$ | elements | contains |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | proper orthochronous | $+1$ | $+$ | $SO^+(1,3)$ | identity, rotations, boosts |
> | improper orthochronous | $-1$ | $+$ | $P\,SO^+(1,3)$ | parity $P$ |
> | improper non-orthochronous | $-1$ | $-$ | $T\,SO^+(1,3)$ | time reversal $T$ |
> | proper non-orthochronous | $+1$ | $-$ | $PT\,SO^+(1,3)$ | $PT = -\mathbb 1$ |
>
> with $P$, $T$ as in [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-2|Def. §B1.2.2]]. No continuous family of Lorentz transformations leads from one piece to another; in particular, no sequence of boosts and rotations ever reflects space or reverses time.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Lorentz Group Structure", §"Discrete Transformations" · the user's series, Part III, §3.8 (two discrete invariants; table of components) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.2.3, eqs. (5.35)–(5.37) and the four cases (++), (−+), (−−), (+−)*

^thm-b1-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\det(\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda) = (\det\Lambda)^2\det\boldsymbol\eta$ must equal $\det\boldsymbol\eta = -1$, so $(\det\Lambda)^2 = 1$.
>
> *2.* The $00$ entry of $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda = \boldsymbol\eta$ is $\eta_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^\mu{}_0\Lambda^\nu{}_0 = (\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 - \sum_i(\Lambda^i{}_0)^2 = 1$: the first column of $\Lambda$ is a unit timelike vector. The $00$ entry of $\Lambda\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda^{\mathsf T} = \boldsymbol\eta$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]], part 3) gives the same for the first row. Hence $(\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 \ge 1$.
>
> *3.* Let $\Lambda, \Lambda'$ have $\Lambda^0{}_0, \Lambda'^0{}_0 \ge 1$. Then $(\Lambda'\Lambda)^0{}_0 = \Lambda'^0{}_0\Lambda^0{}_0 + \sum_i\Lambda'^0{}_i\Lambda^i{}_0$, and by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in $\mathbf R^3$ ([[§20 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-14|LADR Thm. 6.14]]) and part 2,
>
> $$
> \Bigl|\sum_i\Lambda'^0{}_i\Lambda^i{}_0\Bigr| \le \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_i(\Lambda'^0{}_i)^2}\,\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_i(\Lambda^i{}_0)^2} = \sqrt{(\Lambda'^0{}_0)^2 - 1}\,\sqrt{(\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 - 1} < \Lambda'^0{}_0\,\Lambda^0{}_0 ,
> $$
>
> so $(\Lambda'\Lambda)^0{}_0 > 0$, hence $\ge 1$ by part 2. The inverse $\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta$ has the same $00$ entry as $\Lambda$, and $\det$ is multiplicative; so both sets are subgroups.
>
> *4.* $P = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$ has $(\det, \operatorname{sgn}) = (-1, +)$, $T = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ has $(-1, -)$, $PT = -\mathbb 1$ has $(+1, -)$, and all three square to $\mathbb 1$. If $\Lambda$ has $(-1, +)$, then $P\Lambda$ has determinant $+1$ and $(P\Lambda)^0{}_0 = \Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$, so $\Lambda = P(P\Lambda) \in P\,SO^+(1,3)$; likewise $T\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$ if $\Lambda$ has $(-1, -)$, since $(T\Lambda)^0{}_0 = -\Lambda^0{}_0$, and $(PT)\Lambda = -\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$ if $\Lambda$ has $(+1, -)$. The labels differ, so the pieces are disjoint. Along a continuous path $t \mapsto \Lambda(t)$ the functions $\det\Lambda(t)$ and $\Lambda^0{}_0(t)$ are continuous (polynomials in the entries, [[§11 Topological Groups and Classical Matrix Groups#^prop-11-3|591 Prop. §11.3]]) and take values in $\{\pm1\}$ and $\mathbf R \setminus (-1, 1)$; a continuous image of an interval is connected ([[§15 Connected Spaces#^thm-15-3|590 Thm. §15.3]]), so both labels stay constant. (Each piece is moreover connected; for $SO^+(1,3)$ this follows from [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-6|Theorem §B1.2.6]], by shrinking the boost velocity and the rotation angle to zero.)

> [!definition] Definition §B1.2.2: Proper, Orthochronous; Parity and Time Reversal
> A Lorentz transformation is **proper** if $\det\Lambda = +1$ and **orthochronous** if $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$; the proper orthochronous ones form $SO^+(1,3)$. The **parity** (space inversion) and **time reversal** transformations are
>
> $$
> P = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1): \ (ct, \vec x) \mapsto (ct, -\vec x), \qquad T = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1): \ (ct, \vec x) \mapsto (-ct, \vec x) .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Lorentz Group Structure", §"Discrete Transformations" · the user's series, Part III, §3.8 · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.2.4, eqs. (5.42)–(5.43)*

> [!caution] Caution: Names of the group
> - The user's series writes $O(1,3)$ and $SO^+(1,3)$, listing the signs of $\eta$ in the order $+$ then $-$; the user's PHY 505 notes write $O(3,1)$, $SO(3,1)$ and $SO^{\uparrow}(3,1)$; other common symbols are $\mathcal L$ for the full group and $\mathcal L^\uparrow_+$ for the proper orthochronous one. They are the same groups: a matrix preserves $\boldsymbol\eta$ exactly when it preserves $-\boldsymbol\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$, which has three plus signs and one minus, so $O(1,3)$ and $O(3,1)$ differ only by the order in which the coordinates are listed.
> - $SO(1,3)$, the group with $\det\Lambda = +1$ alone, is not the physical group: it contains $PT = -\mathbb 1$, which reverses time. The PHY 505 notes describe $SO(3,1)$ as the group that "excludes parity"; it excludes $P$ and $T$ separately, but contains their product.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Lorentz Group Structure" · the user's series, Part III, §3.1 (Remark: Rotations and Lorentz transformations: one template), §3.8*

^cau-b1-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Parity and time reversal are questions for dynamics
> Theorem §B1.2.4 is a statement about geometry: $P$ and $T$ preserve the metric but cannot be reached continuously from the identity. Inertial observers set up with right-handed axes and clocks running forward are related by $SO^+(1,3)$ (together with translations), and that is the group the postulates require the laws to respect; the PHY 505 notes accordingly state the fundamental axiom with *proper* transformations. Whether a law is *also* unchanged under $P$ or $T$ is a separate, experimental question about the interaction it describes. Electromagnetism respects both; the weak interaction violates parity, and time reversal as well. A non-orthochronous transformation also reverses the order of events that could be causally connected ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]), so it never relates two physical observers.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.8 · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Postulates of Special Relativity" (Fundamental Axiom of SR)*

## Rotations and boosts

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.5: Rotations and Boosts
> 1. **Rotations.** For a $3\times3$ matrix $R$, the matrix $\hat R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & R \end{pmatrix}$, acting as $(ct, \vec x) \mapsto (ct, R\vec x)$, is a Lorentz transformation if and only if $R^{\mathsf T}R = \mathbb 1$, $R \in O(3)$; it lies in $SO^+(1,3)$ if and only if $R \in SO(3)$.
> 2. **Boosts.** For a velocity $\vec v = c\vec\beta$ with $|\vec\beta| < 1$ and $\gamma = (1 - \beta^2)^{-1/2}$, the **pure boost** $B(\vec\beta)$ to a frame with parallel axes moving with velocity $\vec v$ is
>
> $$
> ct' = \gamma\bigl(ct - \vec\beta\cdot\vec x\bigr), \qquad \vec x\,' = \vec x + \Bigl[\frac{(\gamma - 1)\,\vec\beta\cdot\vec x}{\beta^2} - \gamma\,ct\Bigr]\vec\beta ,
> $$
>
> with matrix entries
>
> $$
> B^0{}_0 = \gamma, \qquad B^0{}_i = B^i{}_0 = -\gamma\beta^i, \qquad B^i{}_j = \delta^i{}_j + (\gamma - 1)\frac{\beta^i\beta^j}{\beta^2} .
> $$
>
> It is symmetric, lies in $SO^+(1,3)$, and has inverse $B(-\vec\beta)$. For $\vec\beta = \beta\hat x$ it is the standard boost of [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]]; for $\vec\beta = \beta\hat z$ it is the boost written out in the PHY 505 notes, $ct' = \gamma(ct - \beta z)$, $z' = \gamma(z - \beta ct)$.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Components of the Lorentz Group" · the user's series, Part III, §3.8 · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.2.4, eqs. (5.40)–(5.41), §5.3.2, eqs. (5.48)–(5.49) · Schutz §2.2 (boost matrices are symmetric, in any direction)*

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\hat R^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\hat R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -R^{\mathsf T}R \end{pmatrix}$, which equals $\boldsymbol\eta$ exactly when $R^{\mathsf T}R = \mathbb 1$ ([[§3 Basic Examples of Groups#^def-3-8|493 Def. §3.8]]). Also $\det\hat R = \det R$ and $\hat R^0{}_0 = 1$.
>
> *2.* Split $\vec x = x_\parallel\hat\beta + \vec x_\perp$ with $x_\parallel = \hat\beta\cdot\vec x$. The boost acts on $(ct, x_\parallel)$ as the standard boost and leaves $\vec x_\perp$ alone (no change across the motion, [[§A2.2 Length Contraction#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]]):
>
> $$
> ct' = \gamma(ct - \beta x_\parallel), \qquad x'_\parallel = \gamma(x_\parallel - \beta ct), \qquad \vec x\,'_\perp = \vec x_\perp .
> $$
>
> Then $\vec x\,' = \vec x + (x'_\parallel - x_\parallel)\hat\beta = \vec x + [(\gamma - 1)x_\parallel - \gamma\beta ct]\hat\beta$, and $x_\parallel\hat\beta = (\vec\beta\cdot\vec x)\vec\beta/\beta^2$, $\beta\hat\beta = \vec\beta$ give the stated formula; reading off coefficients gives the matrix, which is visibly symmetric. The interval: $c^2t'^2 - x_\parallel'^2 = \gamma^2(1 - \beta^2)(c^2t^2 - x_\parallel^2) = c^2t^2 - x_\parallel^2$ (the computation of [[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^der-a1-5-2|§A1.5]]) and $|\vec x\,'_\perp| = |\vec x_\perp|$, so $(Bx)^2 = x^2$ and $B \in O(1,3)$ by [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]. In an orthonormal spatial basis with first vector $\hat\beta$, $B$ is block diagonal with the $2\times2$ block of determinant $\gamma^2 - \beta^2\gamma^2 = 1$ and an identity block, so $\det B = 1$; and $B^0{}_0 = \gamma \ge 1$. Replacing $\vec\beta$ by $-\vec\beta$ inverts the $2\times2$ block and leaves the rest, so $B(-\vec\beta) = B(\vec\beta)^{-1}$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.6: Every Proper Orthochronous Transformation Is a Boost Times a Rotation
> Every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$ can be written uniquely as
>
> $$
> \Lambda = B(\vec\beta)\,\hat R, \qquad \vec\beta = -\frac{(\Lambda^1{}_0, \Lambda^2{}_0, \Lambda^3{}_0)}{\Lambda^0{}_0}, \qquad R \in SO(3) ,
> $$
>
> a rotation of the spatial axes followed by a pure boost. Consequently $SO^+(1,3)$ has six continuous parameters, three rotation angles and three boost velocities (or rapidities), and any two inertial frames with a common origin event are related by a rotation of axes and one boost in standard configuration.
>

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The boost.* The first column of $\Lambda$ satisfies $(\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 - \sum_i(\Lambda^i{}_0)^2 = 1$ with $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]). So $\vec\beta$ as defined has $\beta^2 = 1 - 1/(\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 < 1$ and $\gamma(\vec\beta) = \Lambda^0{}_0$, and by [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]] the first column of $B(\vec\beta)$ is $(\gamma, -\gamma\vec\beta) = (\Lambda^0{}_0, \Lambda^i{}_0)$: $B(\vec\beta)$ and $\Lambda$ send the time basis vector $e_{(0)}$ to the same vector.
>
> *The rotation.* $\hat R \equiv B(\vec\beta)^{-1}\Lambda = B(-\vec\beta)\Lambda$ is in $SO^+(1,3)$ (a subgroup) and fixes $e_{(0)}$: its first column is $(1, 0, 0, 0)$. The $(0, i)$ entry of $\hat R^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\hat R = \boldsymbol\eta$ reads $\eta_{\mu\nu}\hat R^\mu{}_0\hat R^\nu{}_i = \eta_{00}\hat R^0{}_i = 0$, so the first row is $(1, 0, 0, 0)$ as well, and $\hat R$ has the block form of Theorem §B1.2.5, part 1, with $R \in O(3)$; $\det R = \det\Lambda/\det B = 1$, so $R \in SO(3)$.
>
> *Uniqueness and counting.* If $B(\vec\beta)\hat R = B(\vec\beta\,')\hat R'$, compare first columns: $\hat R e_{(0)} = e_{(0)}$, so $B(\vec\beta)e_{(0)} = B(\vec\beta\,')e_{(0)}$, which forces $\vec\beta = \vec\beta\,'$ and then $\hat R = \hat R'$. The pair $(\vec\beta, R)$ has $3 + 3$ parameters ($\dim SO(3) = 3$, [[§23 The Orthogonal and Unitary Groups as Smooth Manifolds#^ex-23-1|591 Ex. §23.1]]). The count agrees with the constraints: $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda = \boldsymbol\eta$ is a symmetric matrix equation, $10$ conditions on $16$ entries. Since $\hat R B(\vec b)\hat R^{-1} = B(R\vec b)$ (rotate the vector that defines the boost), also $\Lambda = \hat R\,B(R^{-1}\vec\beta)$: a boost followed by a rotation.

## Boosts along one axis: rapidity

> [!definition] Definition §B1.2.3: Rapidity
> The **rapidity** of a boost with velocity $v = \beta c$, $|\beta| < 1$, along a fixed axis is
>
> $$
> \varphi = \operatorname{artanh}\beta = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}, \qquad\text{so that}\qquad \tanh\varphi = \beta, \quad \cosh\varphi = \gamma, \quad \sinh\varphi = \beta\gamma .
> $$
>
> $\varphi$ ranges over all of $\mathbf R$ as $\beta$ ranges over $(-1, 1)$, and $\varphi \approx \beta$ for $|\beta| \ll 1$. In terms of it the boost along $x$ reads
>
> $$
> \Lambda(\varphi) = \begin{pmatrix} \cosh\varphi & -\sinh\varphi \\ -\sinh\varphi & \cosh\varphi \end{pmatrix} \quad\text{on } (ct, x), \qquad\text{identity on } (y, z) .
> $$
>
> *Notation:* these notes write the rapidity $\varphi$, following the user's notes and series; the Phys505 notes write $\zeta$ and Schutz $u$ ("velocity parameter"). The letter $\eta$, used for rapidity in many texts, is reserved here for the metric. The scalar field of [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation|§B4.1]] is also written $\phi$; context separates them, as in the series.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Rapidity: Alternative Parametrization" · the user's series, Part III, §2.4, eq. for the rapidity · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.2.2, eq. (5.23) · Schutz Exercises 1.18–1.19*

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.7: Boosts Are Hyperbolic Rotations; Rapidities Add
> Consider Lorentz transformations that act only on $(x^0, x^1) = (ct, x)$.
> 1. Those with $\det = +1$ and $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$ are exactly the $\Lambda(\varphi)$, $\varphi \in \mathbf R$, and $\Lambda(\varphi)$ is the boost to a frame moving with velocity $c\tanh\varphi$ along $+x$.
> 2. In the light-cone coordinates $u = ct - x$ and $w = ct + x$ (for which $c^2t^2 - x^2 = uw$), the boost is diagonal:
>
> $$
> u' = e^{\varphi}\,u, \qquad w' = e^{-\varphi}\,w ;
> $$
>
> the two light-ray directions are its eigenvectors, with eigenvalues $e^{\pm\varphi} = \gamma(1 \pm \beta) = \sqrt{(1 \pm \beta)/(1 \mp \beta)}$, real, positive and reciprocal.
> 3. **Rapidities add**: $\Lambda(\varphi_2)\Lambda(\varphi_1) = \Lambda(\varphi_1 + \varphi_2)$ and $\Lambda(\varphi)^{-1} = \Lambda(-\varphi)$.
> 4. **Collinear velocities compose** as
>
> $$
> \beta_{12} = \tanh(\varphi_1 + \varphi_2) = \frac{\beta_1 + \beta_2}{1 + \beta_1\beta_2}, \qquad |\beta_{12}| < 1 \ \text{ whenever } |\beta_1|, |\beta_2| < 1 ,
> $$
>
> where $S'$ moves with $\beta_1 c$ relative to $S$, $S''$ with $\beta_2 c$ relative to $S'$, and $S''$ with $\beta_{12}c$ relative to $S$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §2.4 (Route 1: null coordinates), §2.5 (Composition; Corollary: Velocity composition; Remark: The light cone is an eigendirection) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Rapidity: Alternative Parametrization" (rapidity additivity; velocity addition from rapidity) · Schutz Exercises 1.18(a), 1.19*

^thm-b1-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1 and 2.* A transformation preserving $uw$ maps the two null lines $u = 0$ and $w = 0$ onto null lines. Write it as $u' = pu + qw$, $w' = ru + sw$; then $u'w' = pr\,u^2 + (ps + qr)\,uw + qs\,w^2$ equals $uw$ for all $u, w$ only if $pr = 0$, $qs = 0$, $ps + qr = 1$. Invertibility rules out $p = q = 0$ and $r = s = 0$, which leaves either $q = r = 0$, $ps = 1$ (each null line mapped to itself; determinant $ps = 1$) or $p = s = 0$, $qr = 1$ (the null lines exchanged; determinant $-qr = -1$, a reflection $x \mapsto -x$ composed with the first kind). So the proper ones are $u' = pu$, $w' = w/p$. Back in $(ct, x)$, with $ct = (u + w)/2$, $x = (w - u)/2$,
>
> $$
> ct' = \tfrac12\bigl(p + p^{-1}\bigr)ct - \tfrac12\bigl(p - p^{-1}\bigr)x, \qquad x' = -\tfrac12\bigl(p - p^{-1}\bigr)ct + \tfrac12\bigl(p + p^{-1}\bigr)x .
> $$
>
> Here $\Lambda^0{}_0 = (p + p^{-1})/2$, which is $\ge 1$ for $p > 0$ and $\le -1$ for $p < 0$. Orthochronous therefore means $p > 0$, and $p = e^{\varphi}$ for exactly one real $\varphi$; then $(p \pm p^{-1})/2 = \cosh\varphi, \sinh\varphi$, and the map is $\Lambda(\varphi)$. Its origin $x' = 0$ moves along $x = ct\tanh\varphi$, with velocity $c\tanh\varphi$; by [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-3|Def. §B1.2.3]], $\cosh\varphi = \gamma$ and $\sinh\varphi = \beta\gamma$, and $\Lambda(\varphi)$ is the standard boost of [[§A1.4 The Lorentz Transformation#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]]. In the light-cone coordinates $\Lambda(\varphi)$ multiplies $u$ by $e^{\varphi}$ and $w$ by $e^{-\varphi}$: the null directions $(1, -1)$ (the line $w = 0$) and $(1, 1)$ (the line $u = 0$) are eigenvectors, with $e^{\pm\varphi} = \cosh\varphi \pm \sinh\varphi = \gamma(1 \pm \beta)$, and $\gamma^2(1 \pm \beta)^2 = (1 \pm \beta)/(1 \mp \beta)$.
>
> *3.* Two diagonal maps multiply entrywise: $e^{\varphi_2}e^{\varphi_1} = e^{\varphi_1 + \varphi_2}$ on $u$ and $e^{-\varphi_2}e^{-\varphi_1} = e^{-(\varphi_1 + \varphi_2)}$ on $w$. (Equivalently, multiply the matrices and use $\cosh(\varphi_1 + \varphi_2) = \cosh\varphi_1\cosh\varphi_2 + \sinh\varphi_1\sinh\varphi_2$ and the analogous formula for $\sinh$.) With $\varphi_2 = -\varphi_1$ the product is the identity.
>
> *4.* $x'' = \Lambda(\varphi_2)x' = \Lambda(\varphi_2)\Lambda(\varphi_1)x = \Lambda(\varphi_1 + \varphi_2)x$, so $S''$ moves relative to $S$ with $\tanh(\varphi_1 + \varphi_2)$, and the addition formula $\tanh(a + b) = (\tanh a + \tanh b)/(1 + \tanh a\tanh b)$ gives $\beta_{12}$. Since $\tanh$ maps $\mathbf R$ into $(-1, 1)$, $|\beta_{12}| < 1$. This is the collinear case of [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]] (with $u'_x = \beta_2 c$, $v = \beta_1 c$) and of [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]].

> [!remark] Remark: A rotation with a hyperbola instead of a circle
> - **The comparison.** A rotation preserves $x^2 + y^2$ and moves points along circles; a boost preserves $c^2t^2 - x^2$ and moves events along the hyperbolas $c^2t^2 - x^2 = \text{const}$ (the invariant hyperbolas of [[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-1|Theorem §A1.5.1]]). Angles add, and so do rapidities; $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ parametrizes the circle, $(\cosh\varphi, \sinh\varphi)$ the hyperbola, and $\cosh^2\varphi - \sinh^2\varphi = 1$ replaces $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$.
> - **Rapidity as area.** The sector between the time axis, the ray to $(\sinh\varphi, \cosh\varphi)$ and the unit hyperbola has area $\frac12\int_0^{\varphi}\bigl(\cosh^2s - \sinh^2s\bigr)\,ds = \varphi/2$ (the area formula $\frac12\oint(x\,dy - y\,dx)$ of Green's theorem, [[§27 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^thm-27-1|452 Thm. §27.1]], applied along the hyperbola; the two rays contribute nothing), exactly as a circular sector of angle $\theta$ has area $\theta/2$. Panel (a) of the figure shows two equal sectors.
> - **What the sign changes.** Rotation angles live on a circle, rapidities on the whole line: there is no largest boost, and the Lorentz group, unlike $O(4)$, is not compact. Rotations have the complex eigenvalues $e^{\pm i\theta}$; boosts have real ones, $e^{\pm\varphi}$, with the light rays as eigenvectors. That every boost along $x$ maps each light ray to itself is the second postulate in the language of linear algebra, and level A met the diagonal form as a squeeze along the light lines ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^rem-a1-5-2|Remark: A squeeze along the light lines]]).
> - **The eigenvalue is the Doppler factor.** $e^{\varphi} = \sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)}$ is the factor by which a receding observer finds light periods stretched ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]]); its four-vector derivation, at every angle, is [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-10|Theorem §B2.1.10]].
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §2.4 (Remark: Why hyperbolic functions were inevitable), §2.5 (Remark: The light cone is an eigendirection), §3.1 (Remark: one template) · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.2.2*

## The Poincaré group

> [!definition] Definition §B1.2.4: Poincaré Transformation; Poincaré Group
> A **Poincaré transformation** (inhomogeneous Lorentz transformation) is a map
>
> $$
> x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,x^\nu + a^\mu, \qquad \Lambda \in O(1,3), \ a \in \mathbf R^4 ,
> $$
>
> written $(\Lambda, a)$: a Lorentz transformation followed by a translation of the origin in space and time. The set of all of them is the **Poincaré group**.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The Poincaré Group" · the user's series, Part III, §3.8 · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.2.3–5.2.4, eqs. (5.25)–(5.27), (5.38)–(5.40)*

^def-b1-2-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.8: Structure of the Poincaré Group
> 1. Poincaré transformations compose as $(\Lambda_2, a_2)(\Lambda_1, a_1) = (\Lambda_2\Lambda_1, \Lambda_2 a_1 + a_2)$; with identity $(\mathbb 1, 0)$ and inverse $(\Lambda, a)^{-1} = (\Lambda^{-1}, -\Lambda^{-1}a)$ they form a group.
> 2. They preserve the interval between every pair of events: $\Delta x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\Delta x^\nu$, so $\Delta s'^2 = \Delta s^2$. Conversely, every affine map of spacetime that preserves the interval between every pair of events is a Poincaré transformation.
> 3. The group has $6 + 4 = 10$ continuous parameters: three rotation angles, three boost velocities and four translations.
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"The Poincaré Group" (10 generators) · the user's series, Part III, §3.8 · PHY 505 typed notes (earlier offering), §5.2.3–5.2.4*

^thm-b1-2-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $x \mapsto \Lambda_1x + a_1 \mapsto \Lambda_2(\Lambda_1x + a_1) + a_2 = (\Lambda_2\Lambda_1)x + (\Lambda_2a_1 + a_2)$, with $\Lambda_2\Lambda_1 \in O(1,3)$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]). Solving $x' = \Lambda x + a$ for $x$ gives $x = \Lambda^{-1}x' - \Lambda^{-1}a$. Composition of maps is associative.
>
> *2.* The translation cancels in differences: $x'_B - x'_A = \Lambda(x_B - x_A)$, and $\Lambda$ preserves squares ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]). Conversely, an affine map $x \mapsto Lx + a$ sends the displacement $\Delta x$ to $L\Delta x$; every four-vector is a displacement between two events, so preserving all intervals means $(L\Delta x)^2 = (\Delta x)^2$ for all $\Delta x$, and $L \in O(1,3)$ by Theorem §B1.2.2.
>
> *3.* By [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-6|Theorem §B1.2.6]] the identity piece $SO^+(1,3)$ is parametrized by $(\vec\beta, R)$, six numbers, and $a$ adds four.

> [!remark] Remark: Ten parameters, ten conservation laws
> By Noether's theorem ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-2|CM Theorem §B6.4.2]]) each continuous symmetry parameter of a system's dynamics gives a conserved quantity. For the Poincaré group: time translation gives the energy, the three space translations the momentum, the three rotations the angular momentum, and the three boosts a "boost vector" $\vec K$ whose conservation says that the centre of energy moves uniformly. The Galilean group of Newtonian mechanics has the same ten parameters and the same ten conserved quantities, the boost giving uniform motion of the centre of mass ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-5|CM Theorem §B6.4.5]]). The relativistic particle's version, with energy and momentum combined into the four-momentum, is [[§B3.1 The Free Relativistic Particle#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]].
>

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Infinitesimal Lorentz transformations
> Differentiate a smooth family $\Lambda(t) \in SO^+(1,3)$ with $\Lambda(0) = \mathbb 1$ at $t = 0$, and write $\omega = \Lambda'(0)$. From $\Lambda(t)^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta\Lambda(t) = \boldsymbol\eta$: $\omega^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\eta + \boldsymbol\eta\,\omega = 0$, i.e. the lowered array $\omega_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\rho}\,\omega^\rho{}_\nu$ is antisymmetric, $\omega_{\mu\nu} = -\omega_{\nu\mu}$. An antisymmetric $4\times4$ array has six independent entries: $\omega_{ij}$ are infinitesimal rotations, $\omega_{0i}$ infinitesimal boosts, the six planes again. This is the Lorentz analogue of the fact that the tangent space of $O(n)$ at the identity consists of the antisymmetric matrices ([[§25 The Geometric Tangent Space#^thm-25-5|591 Thm. §25.5]]). The commutators of these generators, in particular that two boost generators commute to a rotation generator (the infinitesimal form of Example §B1.2.2, behind Thomas precession), are the Lorentz algebra; the series defers them to quantum field theory ([[§CB.4 The Lorentz Algebra꞉ Generators and Commutation Relations#^thm-cb-4-2|QFT Theorem §CB.4.2]]).
>

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.2.1: A Chain of Receding Stars
> A star measures a second star receding at $0.9c$; the second measures a third receding in the same direction at $0.9c$, and so on. How fast does the $N$th star recede from the first?
>
> Each step has rapidity $\varphi_1 = \operatorname{artanh}0.9 = \frac12\ln\frac{1.9}{0.1} = \frac12\ln 19 = 1.472$, and the steps are collinear, so by [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-7|Theorem §B1.2.7]] the rapidities add: star $N$ has rapidity $(N - 1)\varphi_1$ relative to star 1. With $e^{2\varphi_1} = 19$,
>
> $$
> \frac{v_N}{c} = \tanh\bigl((N - 1)\varphi_1\bigr) = \frac{19^{N-1} - 1}{19^{N-1} + 1} \approx 1 - 2\cdot19^{-(N-1)} \quad (N \gg 1) .
> $$
>
> For $N = 3$ this is $360/362 = 0.99448$, as two applications of the velocity-addition formula give, $1.8/1.81$. For $N = 11$ (ten steps) $1 - v/c \approx 2/19^{10} = 3.3\times10^{-13}$. The Galilean answer, $0.9(N - 1)c$, passes $c$ already at the second step; the true velocity never reaches $c$, while the rapidity grows without bound in equal steps. Rapidity, not velocity, is the quantity that accumulates.
>
> *Source: Schutz Exercise 1.18(b) (solved here)*

> [!example] Example §B1.2.2: Two Perpendicular Boosts Are Not a Boost
> Frame $\bar S$ moves at $0.6c$ along the $x$ axis of $S$; frame $S''$ moves at $0.8c$ along the $\bar y$ axis of $\bar S$; at each step the axes are parallel, so each step is a pure boost ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]), with $\gamma_1 = 5/4$ and $\gamma_2 = 5/3$. The product, on $(ct, x, y)$ ($z$ untouched), is
>
> $$
> \Lambda = B(0.8\,\hat y)\,B(0.6\,\hat x) = \begin{pmatrix} 5/3 & 0 & -4/3 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -4/3 & 0 & 5/3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 5/4 & -3/4 & 0 \\ -3/4 & 5/4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 25/12 & -5/4 & -4/3 \\ -3/4 & 5/4 & 0 \\ -5/3 & 1 & 5/3 \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> *It is a Lorentz transformation.* Its columns are $\eta$-orthonormal: e.g. $(25/12)^2 - (3/4)^2 - (5/3)^2 = (625 - 81 - 400)/144 = 1$, and the first two columns give $(25/12)(-5/4) - (-3/4)(5/4) - (-5/3)(1) = (-125 + 45 + 80)/48 = 0$; the others check the same way.
>
> *It is not a pure boost:* it is not symmetric ($\Lambda^0{}_1 = -5/4$ but $\Lambda^1{}_0 = -3/4$), while every pure boost is (Theorem §B1.2.5). By [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-6|Theorem §B1.2.6]], $\Lambda = B(\vec\beta)\hat R$ with
>
> $$
> \vec\beta = \frac{(3/4,\ 5/3)}{25/12} = (0.36,\ 0.80), \qquad \gamma = \frac{25}{12} = \gamma_1\gamma_2, \qquad R = \begin{pmatrix} 35/37 & -12/37 \\ 12/37 & 35/37 \end{pmatrix} ,
> $$
>
> a rotation by $\theta = \arctan(12/35) = 18.9^\circ$ about the $z$ axis. Physically: the velocity of $S''$ relative to $S$, from the velocity transformation ([[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]) applied to the origin of $S''$, is $(0.6,\ 0.8/\gamma_1)c = (0.60,\ 0.64)c$, of speed $0.877c$; but $S''$ finds $S$ moving at $-(0.36,\ 0.80)c$, of the same speed and a different direction. The two velocity vectors are not opposite because the spatial axes of $S''$ are rotated by $18.9^\circ$ relative to those of $S$, although no step involved a rotation. This is the **Wigner rotation**; for a spinning particle accelerated along a curved path it accumulates into the Thomas precession. The rapidity of the boost part is $\operatorname{arcosh}(25/12) = 1.36$, less than $\operatorname{artanh}0.6 + \operatorname{artanh}0.8 = 0.69 + 1.10 = 1.79$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^cau-b1-2-3|Caution: Rapidities add only along one axis]]). In the opposite order the product is the transpose $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}$ (both factors are symmetric), a different transformation: $S''$ then moves relative to $S$ at $(0.36,\ 0.80)c$. Boosts in different directions do not commute.
>
> *Source: Schutz Exercise 2.13(c)–(f) (solved here) · Griffiths Problem 12.18(c) · the user's series, Part III, §3.8 (Remark: Boosts alone do not close)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - **Matrix groups.** $O(1,3)$ is the group of matrices preserving the bilinear form $\boldsymbol\eta$, as $O(n)$ ([[§3 Basic Examples of Groups#^def-3-8|493 Def. §3.8]], [[§11 Topological Groups and Classical Matrix Groups#^def-11-7|591 Def. §11.7]]) is the group preserving the identity form; the template $M^{\mathsf T}GM = G$ gives $O(n)$ for $G = \mathbb 1$, the Lorentz group for $G = \boldsymbol\eta$, and the symplectic group, behind canonical transformations, for an antisymmetric $G$ (series §3.1). The transpose in it is the change-of-basis rule for bilinear forms ([[§35 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-7|LADR Thm. 9.7]]); for $G = \mathbb 1$ it is the characterization of isometries ([[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-49|LADR Thm. 7.49]]).
