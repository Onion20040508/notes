What structure of spacetime do all inertial observers agree on, and how long is a worldline? Level A sorted pairs of events into timelike, lightlike and spacelike ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^def-a1-5-3|Def. §A1.5.3]]), drew the light cone ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^def-a1-5-4|Def. §A1.5.4]]), showed for frames in standard configuration that the classification and the order of causally connectable events are frame-independent ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]), and resolved the twin paradox with the proper time of an ideal clock ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^mod-a2-5-1|Model §A2.5.1]], [[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^thm-a2-5-2|Theorem §A2.5.2]]). Level B proves the invariance for the whole Lorentz group of [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group|§B1.2]], identifies which transformations preserve the direction of time, derives the inequalities of Minkowski geometry (in which the triangle inequality is reversed), and defines proper time as the Lorentz-invariant length of a worldline. The twin paradox then becomes a theorem of geometry: between two events, the straight worldline is the longest.

## Causal character

> [!definition] Definition §B1.3.1: Timelike, Null, Spacelike; Future and Past; Light Cone
> A nonzero four-vector $a$ is **timelike** if $a^2 > 0$, **null** (lightlike) if $a^2 = 0$, and **spacelike** if $a^2 < 0$. A timelike or null vector is **future-pointing** if $a^0 > 0$ and **past-pointing** if $a^0 < 0$ (it cannot have $a^0 = 0$, since $(a^0)^2 \ge |\vec a|^2 > 0$). For timelike $a$ write $\|a\| = \sqrt{a^2}$. Two events are timelike, null or spacelike separated according to their displacement $\Delta x$. Relative to an event $E$:
> - the **light cone** of $E$ is the set of events null separated from it; its future and past halves have $\Delta x^0 > 0$ and $< 0$;
> - the **chronological future** (past) of $E$ is the set of events with $\Delta x$ timelike and future- (past-) pointing, the interior of the future (past) cone;
> - the **causal future** (past) adds the future (past) light cone; the events spacelike separated from $E$ form its **elsewhere**.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.2 (table of classes) · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Classification of Intervals", §"Causality and the Light Cone" · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §4.4 · Schutz §1.6, §2.5*

> With $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1)$, timelike means $a^2 > 0$. Level A (University Physics) and Schutz use the opposite sign for the interval, so there timelike means $\Delta s^2 < 0$ ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^def-a1-5-3|Def. §A1.5.3]]; [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^cau-b1-1-2|§B1.1, Caution: Sign conventions]]). The names, and the physics, are the same.

^cau-b1-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.1: Causal Structure Is Invariant
> 1. Every Lorentz transformation maps timelike, null and spacelike vectors to vectors of the same kind.
> 2. An orthochronous Lorentz transformation ($\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$) maps future-pointing timelike or null vectors to future-pointing ones; a non-orthochronous one maps them to past-pointing ones.
> 3. Consequently a Poincaré transformation with orthochronous $\Lambda$ maps the light cone, the chronological and causal future and past, and the elsewhere of each event onto those of its image. All inertial observers agree whether two events are timelike, null or spacelike separated, and, for timelike or null separation, which of the two is earlier. For spacelike separation the order is not invariant ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.2 (classes invariant because the interval is), §3.8 (orthochronous) · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §4.4 (relations 1–4; "Lorentz transformation doesn't change causality") · Schutz §1.6*

^thm-b1-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $(\Lambda a)^2 = a^2$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]).
>
> *2.* Let $a$ be timelike or null with $a^0 > 0$, so $|\vec a| \le a^0$. Then $(\Lambda a)^0 = \Lambda^0{}_0a^0 + \sum_i\Lambda^0{}_ia^i$. The first row of $\Lambda$ satisfies $\sum_i(\Lambda^0{}_i)^2 = (\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 - 1$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]), so by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in $\mathbf R^3$ ([[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-14|LADR Thm. 6.14]])
>
> $$
> \Bigl|\sum_i\Lambda^0{}_ia^i\Bigr| \le \sqrt{(\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 - 1}\;|\vec a| \le \sqrt{(\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 - 1}\;a^0 < |\Lambda^0{}_0|\,a^0 .
> $$
>
> Hence $(\Lambda a)^0$ has the sign of $\Lambda^0{}_0$. For past-pointing $a$ apply this to $-a$.
>
> *3.* A Poincaré transformation maps displacements by $\Delta x \mapsto \Lambda\Delta x$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-8|Theorem §B1.2.8]]); each set in [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^def-b1-3-1|Def. §B1.3.1]] is defined by the kind and time orientation of $\Delta x$, which parts 1 and 2 preserve. Inertial observers are related by orthochronous transformations ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^rem-b1-2-2|§B1.2, Remark: Parity and time reversal]]). For a spacelike $\Delta x$, part 2 says nothing, and level A exhibited frames with either order.

> [!remark] Remark: Normal forms, and the orbits of the Lorentz group
> - **Rest frame of a timelike vector.** If $a$ is future-pointing timelike, the boost $B(\vec\beta)$ with $\vec\beta = \vec a/a^0$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]; $|\vec\beta| < 1$ because $a$ is timelike) gives $B(\vec\beta)\,a = (\|a\|, \vec 0)$: indeed $\gamma = a^0/\|a\|$, the time component is $\gamma(a^0 - \vec\beta\cdot\vec a) = \gamma\,a^2/a^0 = \|a\|$, and the spatial part is $\vec a + [(\gamma - 1)a^0 - \gamma a^0]\vec\beta = \vec 0$. For a displacement this is the frame in which both events happen at the same place, $\Delta t = \|\Delta x\|/c$ apart; for a vector built from a particle's motion it will be the particle's rest frame ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions|§B2.1]]).
> - **Spacelike vectors** can be brought to $(0, \sqrt{-a^2}, 0, 0)$: rotate $\vec a$ onto the $x$ axis, then boost along $x$ with $\beta = a^0/|\vec a|$, which is less than $1$ in size; for a displacement, this is the frame in which the events are simultaneous. **Null vectors** can be brought to $(\kappa, \kappa, 0, 0)$, but a boost along $x$ rescales $\kappa$ by $e^{-\varphi}$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-7|Theorem §B1.2.7]]) and no transformation brings it to rest: light has no rest frame. These are level A's normal forms ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]), now for vectors in any direction.
> - **Orbits.** So the orbits of $SO^+(1,3)$ on four-vectors are: each sheet of a two-sheeted hyperboloid $a^2 = m^2 > 0$ (future and past), each one-sheeted hyperboloid $a^2 = -d^2 < 0$, the future and the past null cone (without the origin), and the origin. In Euclidean space the rotation group has the spheres as orbits ([[§30 Examples꞉ Linear Groups and the Cube#^prop-30-2|493 Prop. §30.2]]); here the "spheres" are hyperboloids, and the hyperbolas of [[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^thm-a1-5-1|the §A1.5 figure]] are their sections.
> - **Tangents and simultaneity.** The tangent to the hyperbola $c^2t^2 - x^2 = \text{const}$ at an event $P$ is orthogonal to the position vector of $P$ (differentiate $x\cdot x = \text{const}$), so by Theorem §B1.3.2 below it is spacelike: it is the line of simultaneity of the inertial observer whose time axis runs from the origin through $P$.
>
> *Source: Schutz §1.7 (tangents to the invariant hyperbolae), Exercise 2.17(b) · the user's series, Part III, §3.2 (two normal forms) · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §4.4 (relations 2–3)*

^rem-b1-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.2: The Reversed Inequalities of Minkowski Geometry
> Let $a$, $b$ be nonzero four-vectors.
> 1. If $a$ is timelike and $a\cdot b = 0$, then $b$ is spacelike. In particular a timelike vector is never orthogonal to a null vector.
> 2. Two null vectors are orthogonal if and only if they are parallel.
> 3. **Reversed Cauchy–Schwarz.** If $a$ and $b$ are timelike, $(a\cdot b)^2 \ge a^2\,b^2$, with equality only for $b \parallel a$. If both are future-pointing, $a\cdot b \ge \|a\|\,\|b\| > 0$.
> 4. **Reversed triangle inequality.** If $a$ and $b$ are future-pointing timelike, so is $a + b$, and
>
> $$
> \|a + b\| \ge \|a\| + \|b\| ,
> $$
>
> with equality only for $b \parallel a$. By induction, $\|a_1 + \dots + a_n\| \ge \|a_1\| + \dots + \|a_n\|$ for future-pointing timelike $a_k$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.2 (Remark: The twin "paradox": reversed triangle inequality) · Schutz §2.5 (a null vector is orthogonal to itself), Exercise 2.18(b)*

^thm-b1-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Scalar products and the time orientation are the same in every frame reached by an orthochronous transformation ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]), so each statement may be checked in a convenient frame.
>
> *1 and 3.* Replacing $a$ by $-a$ if needed, $a$ is future-pointing; go to its rest frame ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^rem-b1-3-1|Remark: Normal forms]]), where $a = (\|a\|, \vec 0)$. Then $a\cdot b = \|a\|\,b^0$ and $b^2 = (b^0)^2 - |\vec b|^2$. If $a\cdot b = 0$, then $b^0 = 0$ and $b^2 = -|\vec b|^2 < 0$ since $b \neq 0$: part 1. For part 3, $a^2b^2 = \|a\|^2\bigl((b^0)^2 - |\vec b|^2\bigr) \le \|a\|^2(b^0)^2 = (a\cdot b)^2$, with equality exactly when $\vec b = \vec 0$ in this frame, i.e. $b \parallel a$. If $b$ is also future-pointing, $b^0 > 0$ in this frame, and $b^0 \ge \sqrt{(b^0)^2 - |\vec b|^2} = \|b\|$ gives $a\cdot b = \|a\|\,b^0 \ge \|a\|\,\|b\|$.
>
> *2.* Write $k^0 = s\,|\vec k|$ and $l^0 = s'\,|\vec l|$ with signs $s, s'$. Then $k\cdot l = ss'\,|\vec k|\,|\vec l| - \vec k\cdot\vec l$, and by Cauchy–Schwarz in $\mathbf R^3$ ([[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-14|LADR Thm. 6.14]]) this vanishes only if $\vec l = \lambda\vec k$ with $\operatorname{sgn}\lambda = ss'$; then $l^0 = s'|\lambda|\,|\vec k| = \lambda\,s|\vec k| = \lambda k^0$, so $l = \lambda k$. Conversely $k\cdot(\lambda k) = \lambda k^2 = 0$.
>
> *4.* $(a + b)^0 = a^0 + b^0 > 0$, and by part 3
>
> $$
> (a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2\,a\cdot b + b^2 \ge \|a\|^2 + 2\|a\|\,\|b\| + \|b\|^2 = (\|a\| + \|b\|)^2 > 0 ,
> $$
>
> with equality exactly when $a\cdot b = \|a\|\,\|b\|$, i.e. $b \parallel a$. Induction on $n$ gives the sum version.

> [!remark] Remark: Causality as a statement about cones
> - The PHY 505 notes take as an axiom that an event $B$ can never be causally affected by an event $A$ if $B$ lies outside the future light cone of $A$; more precisely, influences reach only the causal future. By Theorem §B1.3.1 this axiom means the same thing to every inertial observer, and every observer agrees on the time order of any cause and effect. A signal faster than light would connect spacelike separated events, whose order some observers reverse; this is level A's argument that nothing outruns light ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^rem-a1-5-4|Remark: Causality, and why nothing outruns light]]).
> - The cone structure, not a universal "now", is what spacetime offers in place of Newton's division into past and future: every event has its own past, future and elsewhere, the past and future of two events overlap without coinciding, and the elsewhere is a four-dimensional region in which time order depends on the observer (Schutz, Figure 1.9).
> - Part 1 of Theorem §B1.3.2 says that everything orthogonal to a timelike vector is spacelike. It will give, for example, that a particle's four-acceleration is spacelike, being orthogonal to its four-velocity ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions|§B2.1]]).
>
> *Source: the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Causality and the Light Cone" (Causality Axiom) · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §4.4 · Schutz §1.6, Figure 1.9 · the user's series, Part III, §3.2*

## Proper time

> [!definition] Definition §B1.3.2: Worldline; Proper Time
> A **worldline** of a massive particle is a curve $\lambda \mapsto x^\mu(\lambda)$, $\lambda \in [\lambda_1, \lambda_2]$, continuous and piecewise continuously differentiable, whose tangent $dx^\mu/d\lambda$ is future-pointing timelike wherever it is defined (finitely many kinks are allowed). Its **proper time** is the Minkowski length of the curve divided by $c$:
>
> $$
> \tau = \frac1c\int_{\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2}\sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\,\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}\,\frac{dx^\nu}{d\lambda}}\;d\lambda, \qquad\text{briefly}\qquad c\,d\tau = \sqrt{ds^2} = \sqrt{dx^\mu dx_\mu} .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.2, eq. for the proper time · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Spacetime Intervals" (proper time interval), §"Proper Time Along a Worldline" · Schutz §2.6, eq. (2.29)*

^def-b1-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.3: Proper Time Is a Lorentz-Invariant Length
> 1. The proper time of a worldline does not depend on its parametrization.
> 2. It is the same in every inertial frame: invariant under all Poincaré transformations.
> 3. In an inertial frame in which the worldline is $\vec x(t)$ with velocity $\vec u(t)$,
>
> $$
> \tau = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sqrt{1 - \frac{u(t)^2}{c^2}}\;dt = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\frac{dt}{\gamma(t)} ,
> $$
>
> and for a straight worldline (constant velocity) between events with displacement $\Delta x$, $\tau = \|\Delta x\|/c = \sqrt{\Delta t^2 - |\Delta\vec x|^2/c^2}$, the proper time interval of [[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^def-a2-1-1|Def. §A2.1.1]].
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.2, boxed eq. for $\tau$ ("Proper time is a Lorentz scalar") · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Proper Time Along a Worldline" · Phys505 (Wells) notes, §5.2.1 (proper time as the invariant "distance" divided by $c$)*

^thm-b1-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Let $\lambda = \lambda(\sigma)$ be continuously differentiable with $d\lambda/d\sigma > 0$. By the chain rule $dx^\mu/d\sigma = (dx^\mu/d\lambda)(d\lambda/d\sigma)$, so the integrand in $\sigma$ is the integrand in $\lambda$ times $d\lambda/d\sigma$ (the square root of a positive factor squared), and the one-dimensional change of variables ([[§15 Multivariable Integration#^lem-15-13|452 Lem. §15.13]]) turns one integral into the other, piece by piece between kinks.
>
> *2.* Under $x \mapsto \Lambda x + a$ the tangent becomes $\Lambda\,dx/d\lambda$, and the integrand is unchanged because $\Lambda$ preserves squares ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]); an orthochronous $\Lambda$ also keeps the tangent future-pointing ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]).
>
> *3.* The tangent is future-pointing, so $dx^0/d\lambda = c\,dt/d\lambda > 0$: $t$ increases along the worldline and is an admissible parameter (part 1). With $\lambda = t$, $dx^\mu/dt = (c, \vec u)$ and $\eta_{\mu\nu}(dx^\mu/dt)(dx^\nu/dt) = c^2 - u^2$, which gives the formula. For constant $\vec u$ the integrand is constant: $\tau = \Delta t\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2} = \sqrt{c^2\Delta t^2 - |\Delta\vec x|^2}/c$.

> [!remark] Remark: Geometry, and the clock hypothesis
> - **Layer.** Theorem §B1.3.3 is geometry: every timelike curve has a length. That a real clock carried along the curve *reads* this length is a physical assumption, the ideal clock of level A ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^mod-a2-5-1|Model §A2.5.1]]), which stays a model at level B: the rate of a clock depends on its instantaneous speed only, not on its acceleration. Special relativity relates inertial frames; the assumption is the bridge to accelerated clocks. It is well tested: muons circulating in a storage ring at $\gamma \approx 29$, with centripetal accelerations of order $10^{18}\,g$, decay with lifetimes dilated by exactly $\gamma$.
> - **What level A had.** Level A's formula $\tau = \int\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}\,dt$ was the definition of the model in one frame, and its frame-independence was argued from the clock being a physical object. Here the formula is part 3 of a theorem whose part 2 *proves* that all frames give the same number. The time-dilation formula $\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta\tau$ ([[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^thm-a2-1-2|Theorem §A2.1.2]]) is the case of constant velocity.
> - **The natural parameter.** Proper time is the parameter that every observer agrees on; parametrized by $\tau$, a worldline has a tangent of constant square $c^2$, the four-velocity of [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions|§B2.1]].
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.2 (Remark: The clock hypothesis)*

^rem-b1-3-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.4: The Inertial Worldline Has the Longest Proper Time
> Let event $B$ lie in the chronological future of event $A$. Among all worldlines from $A$ to $B$, the straight one, the worldline of the inertial observer present at both events, has the largest proper time,
>
> $$
> \tau_{\max} = \frac{\|x_B - x_A\|}{c} = \frac{\sqrt{\Delta s^2_{AB}}}{c} ,
> $$
>
> and every other worldline from $A$ to $B$ has strictly less. There is no shortest worldline: proper times arbitrarily close to $0$ occur.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part III, §3.2 (Remark: The twin "paradox") · the user's PHY 505 notes, §"Proper Time Along a Worldline" (Key Point: straight worldlines maximize proper time) · PHY 408 (audit), HW 1, Problem 2*

^thm-b1-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Proper time is invariant ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]]), so compute in the rest frame of $x_B - x_A$ ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^rem-b1-3-1|Remark: Normal forms]]), with the origin at $A$: there $A = (0, \vec 0)$ and $B = (cT, \vec 0)$ with $cT = \|x_B - x_A\|$, and the straight worldline is $\vec x(t) \equiv \vec 0$, of proper time $T$. Any worldline from $A$ to $B$ can be parametrized by $t \in [0, T]$ (Theorem §B1.3.3, part 3), and
>
> $$
> \tau = \int_0^T\sqrt{1 - \frac{u(t)^2}{c^2}}\;dt \le \int_0^T dt = T ,
> $$
>
> by monotonicity of the integral ([[§33 Properties of the Riemann Integral#^thm-33-3|451 Thm. §33.3]]). If equality holds, the non-negative function $1 - \sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}$, continuous between kinks, has integral zero, so it vanishes ([[§33 Properties of the Riemann Integral#^thm-33-7|451 Thm. §33.7]]): $\vec u \equiv \vec 0$, and since $\vec x(0) = \vec 0$, the worldline is the straight one. Because the statement involves only invariant quantities (proper times and straightness, which a linear map preserves), it holds in every frame. For the last claim, go out at speed $\beta c$ for time $T/2$ and back: $\tau = T\sqrt{1 - \beta^2} \to 0$ as $\beta \to 1$.
>
> *For a broken worldline* $A \to E_1 \to \dots \to E_{n-1} \to B$ of straight pieces, no frame is needed: the proper time is $\sum_k\|x_{E_k} - x_{E_{k-1}}\|/c$, and the reversed triangle inequality ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]], part 4) bounds the sum by $\|x_B - x_A\|/c$, with equality only if all pieces are parallel.

> [!remark] Remark: The twin paradox as geometry
> - **What changes from level A.** [[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^thm-a2-5-2|Theorem §A2.5.2]] put the stay-at-home twin at rest in a chosen frame and integrated there. Theorem §B1.3.4 says the same thing without a preferred frame: the ages at reunion are the Minkowski lengths of two worldlines between the same two events, and the straight one is longer. Straightness is frame-independent, so there is nothing symmetric about the situation and no paradox: one worldline is straight, the other is not.
> - **Ulysses and Homer in one line.** In [[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^ex-a2-5-1|Example §A2.5.1]] each leg of Ulysses' trip has displacement $(c\Delta t, \Delta x) = (5, \pm4)$ ly, of Minkowski length $\sqrt{25 - 16} = 3$ ly, so he ages $3 + 3 = 6$ y, while Homer's straight worldline $(10, 0)$ ly gives $10$ y. No frame change, no lines of simultaneity, no analysis of the turnaround: only the reversed triangle inequality $3 + 3 < 10$. Level A's bookkeeping in Ulysses' frames ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^rem-a2-5-1|Remark: Where the symmetry breaks]]) remains the answer to the question "what does each twin say about the other *during* the trip"; Schutz's version of the story (Diana and Artemis at $0.96c$) makes the same point: switching frames in mid-journey without covering all events is like measuring a curve with a ruler that is rotated halfway.
> - **Acceleration.** The PHY 505 notes put the asymmetry in "the acceleration at the turnaround point". In the geometric statement acceleration enters only as the reason the worldline bends; how long the turnaround takes does not matter, and what is compared are lengths. Level A's examples make the same point ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^ex-a2-5-3|Example §A2.5.3]]).
> - **Euclid reversed.** In the plane the straight line is the *shortest* path between two points (the triangle inequality, [[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-17|LADR Thm. 6.17]]); among timelike curves in spacetime it is the *longest*. The minus sign in $\eta$ reverses the inequality. The exchange of yearly signals between the twins, the first problem of the PHY 408 homework, is [[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^ex-a2-5-2|Example §A2.5.2]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.3.1: Three Events Seen in Opposite Orders
> Observer 1 sees three events in the order $A, B, C$; observer 2 sees them in the order $C, B, A$. (a) What can be said about their separations? (b) Can a third observer see them in the order $A, C, B$?
>
> *(a)* Every pair is seen in opposite orders by the two observers. Observers are related by orthochronous transformations, which preserve the time order of timelike and null separated pairs ([[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]); so **all three pairs are spacelike separated**. In particular, no one of the events can have influenced another.
>
> *(b)* It can happen, but it need not. Take events on the $x$ axis, in units with $ct$ and $x$ in the same length unit:
>
> $$
> A = (ct, x) = (0, 0), \qquad B = (1, 2), \qquad C = (2, 5) ;
> $$
>
> the separations $(1, 2)$, $(2, 5)$, $(1, 3)$ are all spacelike. An observer moving with velocity $\beta c$ along $x$ assigns $ct' = \gamma\,(ct - \beta x)$, so the order is that of $f(\beta) = ct - \beta x$, which is $0$, $1 - 2\beta$ and $2 - 5\beta$ for $A$, $B$, $C$. These straight lines cross pairwise at $\beta = 1/3$ ($B$ and $C$ simultaneous), $2/5$ ($A$ and $C$) and $1/2$ ($A$ and $B$). Hence observer 1 at $\beta = 0$ sees $A, B, C$; observer 2 at $\beta = 0.6$ sees $C\,(-1) < B\,(-0.2) < A\,(0)$; and an observer at $\beta = 0.35$ has $f = 0, 0.30, 0.25$ and sees $A, C, B$. Since two straight lines cross at most once, the order changes by swapping one adjacent pair at each crossing. Going from $ABC$ to $CBA$ along a line of velocities therefore passes either through $ACB$ and $CAB$ or through $BAC$ and $BCA$; which route occurs depends on the events, and for events on one line, which can be compared only through $\beta_x$, the other route is then impossible.
>
> *Source: PHY 408 (audit), HW 1, Problem 3 (solved here)*

> [!example] Example §B1.3.2: The Proper Time of an Oscillating Clock
> A clock leaves event $A$ at the origin and moves along $x(t) = a\sin(\pi ct/L)$, returning to $x = 0$ at $ct = L$ (event $B$); its peak speed is $\beta_{\max}c$ with $\beta_{\max} = \pi a/L < 1$. By [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]], with $u = \beta_{\max}c\cos(\pi ct/L)$ and the substitution $\theta = \pi ct/L$,
>
> $$
> c\tau = \int_0^L\sqrt{1 - \beta_{\max}^2\cos^2\frac{\pi s}{L}}\;ds = \frac{2L}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2}\sqrt{1 - \beta_{\max}^2\sin^2\theta}\;d\theta ,
> $$
>
> a complete elliptic integral of the second kind, evaluated numerically. For $L = 10$ and $a = 3$ (any length unit), $\beta_{\max} = 0.942$ and $c\tau = 7.09$: less than the $10$ of the clock that stays at $x = 0$, as [[§B1.3 Causal Structure and Proper Time#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]] requires, and less than the $8$ of a clock that goes to the same turning point $x = 3$ at the constant speed $0.6c$ and comes straight back, whose average speed is the same but whose peak speed is lower. For small amplitude, expanding the root, $c\tau \approx L\bigl(1 - \beta_{\max}^2/4\bigr) = L\bigl(1 - \langle u^2\rangle/2c^2\bigr)$: the clock loses time in proportion to its mean squared speed, as a sequence of short inertial pieces with the low-speed time dilation of [[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^thm-a2-1-2|Theorem §A2.1.2]] would suggest.
>
> *Source: PHY 408 (audit), HW 1, Problem 2 (curved worldlines; worked here) · the user's series, Part III, §3.2, eq. for $\tau$*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The proof of the reversed inequalities uses the ordinary Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in $\mathbf R^3$ ([[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-14|LADR Thm. 6.14]]); the conclusions reverse those of an inner product space ([[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-17|LADR Thm. 6.17]]) because the Minkowski form is indefinite. Restricted to the vectors orthogonal to a fixed timelike vector, which are all spacelike (Theorem §B1.3.2, part 1), $-\eta$ *is* positive definite: the rest space of an observer is an ordinary Euclidean space.
> - The orbits of $SO^+(1,3)$ (hyperboloids and cones) play the role of the spheres that are the orbits of $O(3)$ ([[§30 Examples꞉ Linear Groups and the Cube#^prop-30-2|493 Prop. §30.2]]); by the orbit–stabilizer idea, the stabilizer of a timelike vector is the rotation group $SO(3)$ of its rest frame ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-6|§B1.2, Theorem §B1.2.6]]).
> - Proper time is the arc length of a curve for an indefinite metric; independence of parametrization is the change-of-variables lemma ([[§15 Multivariable Integration#^lem-15-13|452 Lem. §15.13]]). In general relativity (Relativity level C, planned) the same definition with a curved metric makes freely falling worldlines the ones of extremal proper time, and the gravitational time dilation that level A mentions alongside the airborne-clock tests belongs there.
