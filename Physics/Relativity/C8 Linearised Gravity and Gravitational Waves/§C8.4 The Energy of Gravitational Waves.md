A passing gravitational wave moves free test masses back and forth ([[§C8.3 What a Passing Wave Does#^thm-c8-3-3|Theorem §C8.3.3]]). Thread two of them as beads on a rod with a little friction (Feynman's "sticky bead", in Schutz §9.5): the beads rub, and the rod warms up. The heat has to come from somewhere, and the only candidate is the wave. Electrodynamics answers the analogous question with a local energy density quadratic in the field strengths, $u \propto E^2 + B^2$, whose flux is the Poynting vector ([[§C1.5 The Electromagnetic Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c1-5-3|EM Theorem §C1.5.3]]; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$ there). The question of this section is what plays this role for gravity, and the answer has to get around a theorem that forbids the obvious one ([[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c4-4-6|Theorem §C4.4.6]]).

The obvious candidate fails twice, and both failures can be computed. *First*, the gravitational "field strength" is the first derivative of the metric, and a quadratic expression in it such as $\tfrac{1}{32\pi}\dot h_{ij}\dot h_{ij}$ (the normalisation will be justified later) is not a property of the wave: in a chart adapted to an event $p$ it is zero at $p$, while in the transverse-traceless chart it is $\tfrac{\omega^2}{16\pi}(h_+^2 + h_\times^2)\sin^2\omega t$ there ([[#^thm-c8-4-1|Theorem §C8.4.1]], Figure ph-rel-c8-4-2). *Second*, the linearised theory of [[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom|§C8.1]] has no room for the wave's energy at all: the background is $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ by assumption, the field equation is linear in $h$, and an energy, quadratic in $h$, appears nowhere in it. Going to second order does produce a quadratic term, $R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}$, which acts on the background like matter. But read point by point it is no energy density either. For a single $+$ wave of amplitude $A$ along $z$, the would-be density $-\tfrac{1}{8\pi}\bigl(R^{(2)}_{00} - \tfrac12\eta_{00}R^{(2)}\bigr)$ works out to

$$
\frac{\omega^2A^2}{8\pi}\Bigl(\cos^2\varphi - \tfrac12\sin^2\varphi\Bigr), \qquad \varphi = \omega(t - z) ,
$$

which is negative wherever $\tan^2\varphi > 2$, down to $-\omega^2A^2/16\pi$ (computed from the second-order Ricci tensor of [[#^thm-c8-4-2|Theorem §C8.4.2]]; checked by script). Its average over a period, $\omega^2A^2/32\pi$, is positive, and it is the answer.

The section builds that answer in order. It first states what the equivalence principle rules out for a wave ([[#^thm-c8-4-1|Theorem §C8.4.1]]). It then lets the background respond: the metric is split into a slowly varying background and a short-wavelength ripple ([[#^def-c8-4-1|Def. §C8.4.1]]), quantities are averaged over a region containing many wavelengths ([[#^def-c8-4-2|Def. §C8.4.2]]), and Einstein's equations at second order split into a slow equation for the background and a fast one for the ripple ([[#^thm-c8-4-2|Theorem §C8.4.2]]). The quadratic term in the slow equation is the waves' effective energy–momentum tensor ([[#^def-c8-4-3|Def. §C8.4.3]]). Far from the source it reduces to $\tfrac{1}{32\pi}\langle\partial_\mu h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\partial_\nu h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\rangle$, which the sources state and this section derives, integration by parts by integration by parts ([[#^thm-c8-4-3|Theorem §C8.4.3]]). Its conservation law gives the energy and momentum carried through a distant sphere ([[#^thm-c8-4-4|Theorem §C8.4.4]]).

**Setup.** Vacuum regions far from any source, where spacetime is flat up to a gravitational wave: in a chart, $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}$ with $|h_{\mu\nu}| \ll 1$ ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^mod-c8-1-1|Model §C8.1.1]]), $h$ a solution of the linearised vacuum equations, oscillating with reduced wavelength $\bar\lambda = \lambda/2\pi$. The general split into background and ripple ([[#^def-c8-4-1|Def. §C8.4.1]]) allows a curved background; the explicit formulas are derived for a flat one. Quantities are expanded to second order in the amplitude $\epsilon \sim |h|$; averages are taken over a scale $l$ with $\bar\lambda \ll l$, and corrections of relative order $\bar\lambda/l$ are dropped. The identities were checked symbolically by script: the second-order Ricci tensor against the full curvature code, the averaged identities exactly for superpositions of plane waves in several directions; the numbers of the examples were checked by script too.

## Why there is no local energy density

[[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c4-4-6|Theorem §C4.4.6]] proved, for every spacetime, that no tensor built from $g$ and $\partial g$ at a point can be a gravitational energy density. For a wave the argument can be carried out in a chart one can write down, and it says more: it also rules out the curvature scalars.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.4.1: Gravity Has No Local Energy Density
> Let $g = \eta + h$ be the plane wave $h_{11} = -h_{22} = h_+\cos\omega(t - z)$, $h_{12} = h_\times\cos\omega(t - z)$ in TT gauge ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-3|Theorem §C8.2.3]]), to first order in $h$, and $p$ any event.
> 1. *(The field strength can be removed at $p$.)* The new chart $x'^\mu = x^\mu - \xi^\mu(x)$ with
>
> $$
> \xi_\nu(x) = -\tfrac12h_{\nu\mu}(p)\,(x - p)^\mu - \tfrac12\Gamma^{(1)}_{\nu\mu\lambda}(p)\,(x - p)^\mu(x - p)^\lambda, \qquad \Gamma^{(1)}_{\nu\mu\lambda} = \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_\mu h_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_\lambda h_{\nu\mu} - \partial_\nu h_{\mu\lambda}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> gives $h'_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$ and $\partial_\lambda h'_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$: it is the first-order form of normal coordinates at $p$ ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]], part 5). Every expression built from $g$ and $\partial g$ at $p$ takes its Minkowski value there. In particular the candidate $\tfrac{1}{32\pi}\dot h_{ij}\dot h_{ij}$ is $\tfrac{\omega^2}{16\pi}(h_+^2 + h_\times^2)\sin^2\omega t_p$ in the TT chart and $0$ in the new one.
> 2. *(Curvature survives, but is linear.)* The linearised Riemann tensor at $p$ is the same in both charts ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-3|Theorem §C8.1.3]]) and is not zero, e.g. $R^{(1)}_{x0x0} = -\tfrac12\ddot h_{11}$. It is linear in $h$, so it oscillates about zero and has no definite sign.
> 3. *(Quadratic curvature scalars vanish.)* For plane waves along $z$ with arbitrary profiles $f_+(t - z)$, $f_\times(t - z)$, the quadratic scalars of the linearised curvature vanish identically:
>
> $$
> R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{(1)\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = 0, \qquad R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\,({}^\star R^{(1)})^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = 0 \qquad \text{at order } h^2 ,
> $$
>
>    with ${}^\star R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = \tfrac12R_{\mu\nu}{}^{\alpha\beta}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\rho\sigma}$.
> 4. *(What this rules out.)* No function of the metric and its derivatives at one event, first derivatives (part 1), curvature (part 2) or quadratic curvature scalars (part 3), can serve as the energy density of a plane gravitational wave. An energy can only be assigned to a region containing many wavelengths, with the background allowed to respond: [[#^def-c8-4-1|Def. §C8.4.1]] onwards.
>
> *Model:* [[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^mod-c8-1-1|Model §C8.1.1]], vacuum plane waves; *applies:* exactly at first order in $h$ (parts 1–2) and at second order (part 3); the general statement for every spacetime is [[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c4-4-6|Theorem §C4.4.6]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.4, first bullet ("we can always introduce normal coordinates so that the metric at that point is the Minkowski metric", p. 25, PDF 26) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §21.8 (geodesic coordinates, "one cannot construct a tensorial pointwise energy density", p. 420, PDF 435) · Hartle §16.5 ("the first derivatives of the metric all vanish in a local inertial frame", p. 342, PDF 343) · parts 1–3 worked here, checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (how the chart change acts). Under $x'^\mu = x^\mu - \xi^\mu(x)$ with $\xi$ of first order, $h'_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu\xi_\nu + \partial_\nu\xi_\mu$, all evaluated at the same coordinate values ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-3|Theorem §C8.1.3]]; [[§C5.4 Diffeomorphism Invariance#^thm-c5-4-1|Theorem §C5.4.1]], part 2).
>
> **Step 2** (value at $p$). Put $p$ at the origin of $y^\mu = (x - p)^\mu$. The quadratic term of $\xi$ has vanishing first derivatives at $y = 0$, and $\partial_\mu\bigl(-\tfrac12h_{\nu\alpha}(p)y^\alpha\bigr) = -\tfrac12h_{\nu\mu}(p)$, so
>
> $$
> h'_{\mu\nu}(p) = h_{\mu\nu}(p) - \tfrac12h_{\nu\mu}(p) - \tfrac12h_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (first derivatives at $p$). The linear term of $\xi$ has constant first derivatives, so only the quadratic term contributes to $\partial_\lambda\partial_\mu\xi_\nu$; since $\Gamma^{(1)}_{\nu\mu\lambda}$ is symmetric in $\mu\lambda$, $\partial_\lambda\partial_\mu\bigl(-\tfrac12\Gamma^{(1)}_{\nu\alpha\beta}(p)y^\alpha y^\beta\bigr) = -\Gamma^{(1)}_{\nu\mu\lambda}(p)$. Hence
>
> $$
> \partial_\lambda h'_{\mu\nu}(p) = \partial_\lambda h_{\mu\nu}(p) - \Gamma^{(1)}_{\nu\mu\lambda}(p) - \Gamma^{(1)}_{\mu\nu\lambda}(p) ,
> $$
>
> and the sum of the two Christoffel symbols is
>
> $$
> \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_\mu h_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_\lambda h_{\nu\mu} - \partial_\nu h_{\mu\lambda}\bigr) + \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_\nu h_{\mu\lambda} + \partial_\lambda h_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\mu h_{\nu\lambda}\bigr) = \partial_\lambda h_{\mu\nu} ,
> $$
>
> so $\partial_\lambda h'_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$. This is the linearised form of $x'^\mu = x^\mu + \tfrac12\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(p)\,y^\alpha y^\beta$, the second-order term of the exponential map ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]], part 2).
>
> **Step 4** (the candidate density). In the TT chart $\dot h_{11} = -\dot h_{22} = -\omega h_+\sin\omega(t - z)$ and $\dot h_{12} = -\omega h_\times\sin\omega(t - z)$, so at $p$ (take $z_p = 0$)
>
> $$
> \dot h_{ij}\dot h_{ij} = \dot h_{11}^2 + \dot h_{22}^2 + 2\dot h_{12}^2 = 2\omega^2\bigl(h_+^2 + h_\times^2\bigr)\sin^2\omega t_p ,
> $$
>
> giving $\tfrac{\omega^2}{16\pi}(h_+^2 + h_\times^2)\sin^2\omega t_p$; in the new chart all $\partial h'$ vanish at $p$ (Step 3), and the candidate is $0$. On the worldline $x = y = z = 0$ the new components are $h'_{ij}(t) = h_{ij}(t) - h_{ij}(p) - \dot h_{ij}(p)(t - t_p)$ for $i, j \in \{x, y\}$, the Taylor remainder after the linear term (Figure ph-rel-c8-4-2).
>
> **Step 5** (curvature). The linearised Riemann tensor is a combination of second derivatives, $R^{(1)}_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = \tfrac12(\partial_\sigma\partial_\mu h_{\rho\nu} + \partial_\rho\partial_\nu h_{\sigma\mu} - \partial_\sigma\partial_\nu h_{\rho\mu} - \partial_\rho\partial_\mu h_{\sigma\nu})$ ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-2|Theorem §C8.1.2]]); the pure-gauge part $\partial_\mu\xi_\nu + \partial_\nu\xi_\mu$ drops out of it ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-3|Theorem §C8.1.3]]). For $(\rho, \sigma, \mu, \nu) = (x, 0, x, 0)$ only the third term survives ($h_{0\mu} = 0$): $R^{(1)}_{x0x0} = -\tfrac12\partial_0\partial_0h_{xx} = -\tfrac12\ddot h_{11}$, nonzero wherever $\cos\omega(t - z) \neq 0$.
>
> **Step 6** (quadratic scalars). With $u = t - z$ and $k_\mu = \partial_\mu u = (1, 0, 0, -1)$, a null covector ($\eta^{\mu\nu}k_\mu k_\nu = -1 + 1 = 0$), every second derivative is $\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta h_{\rho\nu} = k_\alpha k_\beta\,h''_{\rho\nu}$, so
>
> $$
> R^{(1)}_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\bigl(k_\sigma k_\mu h''_{\rho\nu} + k_\rho k_\nu h''_{\sigma\mu} - k_\sigma k_\nu h''_{\rho\mu} - k_\rho k_\mu h''_{\sigma\nu}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> In the full contraction $R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}R^{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}$ each of the sixteen products contains either $k_\alpha k^\alpha = 0$ or $k^\alpha h''_{\alpha\beta} = 0$ (transversality: $h_{0\mu} = h_{3\mu} = 0$). For instance $(k_\sigma k_\mu h''_{\rho\nu})(k^\sigma k^\mu h''^{\rho\nu}) = (k\cdot k)^2\,h''_{\rho\nu}h''^{\rho\nu} = 0$ and $(k_\sigma k_\mu h''_{\rho\nu})(k^\rho k^\nu h''^{\sigma\mu}) = (k^\rho h''_{\rho\nu}k^\nu)(k_\sigma h''^{\sigma\mu}k_\mu) = 0$. The parity-odd contraction vanishes for the same reason. (Both checked by script for arbitrary $f_+$, $f_\times$.)
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The equivalence principle in its first-derivative form (Step 3) removes the "field strength" of the wave at any one event; what remains at $p$ is the tide $R^{(1)}$, the quantity that moved the test masses of [[§C8.3 What a Passing Wave Does#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]].
> - A plane wave is a null field: like a plane electromagnetic wave, whose invariants $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ and $F_{\mu\nu}{}^\star F^{\mu\nu}$ vanish, it carries energy although every local scalar built from its field is zero. The energy is frame-dependent (it is the $00$ component of a tensor, as for light), and for gravity, in addition, not localisable.

## Background, ripple and averaging

The way out is the one Couzens takes. If the wave's energy has any gravitational effect, it must curve spacetime, so the background must be allowed to be curved and to respond. That requires saying which part of the metric is "background" and which is "wave", and the answer is a separation of scales.

> [!definition] Definition §C8.4.1: Background and Ripple; the Short-Wave Condition
> *Structure.* On a spacetime $(M, g)$ a **background–ripple split** is a decomposition of the metric into two symmetric tensor fields of type $(0, 2)$,
>
> $$
> g_{\mu\nu} = \bar g_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu} ,
> $$
>
> with $\bar g$ a Lorentzian metric (the **background**) and $h$ small (the **ripple**), such that in some chart the two vary on separated scales:
> - the background varies on a length $L_B$: $\bar g_{\mu\nu} = O(1)$, $\partial\bar g = O(1/L_B)$, $\partial\partial\bar g = O(1/L_B^2)$;
> - the ripple has amplitude $\epsilon \ll 1$ and reduced wavelength $\bar\lambda = \lambda/2\pi$: $h_{\mu\nu} = O(\epsilon)$, $\partial h = O(\epsilon/\bar\lambda)$, $\partial\partial h = O(\epsilon/\bar\lambda^2)$.
>
> The **short-wave condition** is
>
> $$
> \bar\lambda \ll L_B .
> $$
>
> In Fourier terms, $h$ is built from wave vectors with $|k| \sim 1/\bar\lambda$ and $\bar g$ from $|k| \lesssim 1/L_B$. The alternative **high-frequency condition** separates frequencies instead: $\bar g$ contains frequencies up to $f_B$, $h$ is peaked at $f \gg f_B$. Either condition suffices; they are independent, because $L_B$ and $f_B$ are not tied to each other the way $\lambda f = 1$ ties $\lambda$ and $f$ for the wave.
>
> The split is not defined by $g$ alone: modes with wavelengths between $\bar\lambda$ and $L_B$ could be booked to either side, and the split is meaningful only up to corrections of order $\bar\lambda/L_B$. About flat spacetime ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^mod-c8-1-1|Model §C8.1.1]]), $\bar g = \eta$ is fixed by its vanishing curvature and $L_B = \infty$.
>
> *Model:* a spacetime with two separated scales; *applies:* to radiation whose wavelength is short compared with the curvature radius of the region it crosses, e.g. far from an isolated source, where $L_B$ is the distance $r$ to it.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.4, eqs. (2.75)–(2.77), pp. 25–26 (PDF 26–27) · Hartle §16.5 ($\lambda$ much shorter than the curvature scale $\mathcal R$, p. 343, PDF 344)*

^def-c8-4-1

Ground-based detectors are an instance of the high-frequency condition rather than the short-wave one. At $f = 100\ \mathrm{Hz}$ the reduced wavelength is $\bar\lambda = c/2\pi f = 477\ \mathrm{km}$, at $1\ \mathrm{kHz}$ it is $47.7\ \mathrm{km}$ (Couzens' "$\bar\lambda \sim 50$–$500\ \mathrm{km}$", p. 26), far larger than a $4\ \mathrm{km}$ arm: a detector samples the wave in time at one place.

The ripple oscillates; the background does not. An average over many wavelengths keeps the second and removes the first, and this is what turns the split into equations.

> [!definition] Definition §C8.4.2: Averaging over Several Wavelengths
> *Structure.* In a region where the background can be taken as $\eta$ in a chart (flat, or curved on a scale $L_B \gg l$), fix a **weight** $W$: a smooth, even function on $\mathbf R^4$ with $W \ge 0$, $W(-y) = W(y)$, $\int W\,d^4y = 1$, vanishing outside a box of side $l$ about the origin; for instance $W(y) = l^{-4}w(y/l)$ with a fixed profile $w$, a test function in the sense of [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-1|QFT Def. §CA.2.1]]. The **average** of a tensor field $Q$ over the scale $l$ is the tensor field with components
>
> $$
> \langle Q_{\mu\nu\dots}\rangle(x) = \int_{\mathbf R^4}W(x - y)\,Q_{\mu\nu\dots}(y)\,d^4y ,
> $$
>
> a moving average (convolution) taken component by component in that chart. The scale is chosen between the two scales of [[#^def-c8-4-1|Def. §C8.4.1]],
>
> $$
> \bar\lambda \ll l \ll L_B .
> $$
>
> Variants: an average over space only (a box of side $l$ at fixed $t$), or over time only (an interval $T$ with $1/f \ll T \ll 1/f_B$, the case of the high-frequency condition), e.g. $\langle F\rangle = T^{-1}\int_0^TF(t)\,dt$. The box average of the sources is the limit of a weight with sharp edges; it gives the same results with the error $O(\bar\lambda/l)$ in place of a faster decay.
>
> *Example.* $\langle\cos\omega t\rangle = 0$ and $\langle\cos^2\omega t\rangle = \tfrac12$ over many periods ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|WO Theorem §B1.1.6]]): a term linear in the ripple averages away, a quadratic one does not (Figure ph-rel-c8-4-1).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.4.1, eq. (2.84), Example 2.2, eq. (2.85), pp. 28 (PDF 29) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §21.8, eqs. (21.119)–(21.120), p. 422 (PDF 437) · Hartle §16.5 (an average "over spacetime volumes whose dimensions are larger than $\lambda$ but much smaller than $\mathcal R$", p. 343, PDF 344)*

## Einstein's equations at second order

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.4.2: The Second-Order Equations and the Coarse-Grained Einstein Equation
> Let $g = \bar g + h$ be a background–ripple split ([[#^def-c8-4-1|Def. §C8.4.1]]) satisfying Einstein's equations without cosmological constant in trace-reversed form, $R_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi(T_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}T)$ ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^thm-c5-1-5|Theorem §C5.1.5]]), and let $\langle\cdot\rangle$ be an average over $\bar\lambda \ll l \ll L_B$ ([[#^def-c8-4-2|Def. §C8.4.2]]).
> 1. *(Averaging rules.)* For a background quantity $B$, $\langle B\rangle = B + O(l^2/L_B^2)$. For a term linear in the ripple, $\langle h\rangle = O\bigl((\bar\lambda/l)^N\bigr)$ for every $N$ (smooth weight). For any field $V$, $\langle\partial_\mu V\rangle = \partial_\mu\langle V\rangle$ and $|\langle\partial_\mu V\rangle| \le C_w\sup|V|/l$, so for products of ripple fields
>
> $$
> \langle A\,\partial_\mu B\rangle = -\langle(\partial_\mu A)\,B\rangle \qquad \text{up to relative order } \bar\lambda/l
> $$
>
>    (integration by parts under the average). For a time-only average this holds for $\partial_t$; for waves of known direction, spatial derivatives can be traded for time derivatives first.
> 2. *(Expansion.)* $R_{\mu\nu}[\bar g + h] = \bar R_{\mu\nu} + R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}[h] + R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}[h] + O(\epsilon^3/\bar\lambda^2)$, with sizes $\bar R \sim 1/L_B^2$, $R^{(1)} \sim \epsilon/\bar\lambda^2$, $R^{(2)} \sim \epsilon^2/\bar\lambda^2$. On a flat background $R^{(1)}$ is the linearised Ricci tensor of [[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-2|Theorem §C8.1.2]], and the second-order Ricci tensor is
>
> $$
> R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\rho\Gamma^{(2)\rho}{}_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^{(2)\rho}{}_{\rho\mu} + \Gamma^{(1)\rho}{}_{\rho\lambda}\Gamma^{(1)\lambda}{}_{\nu\mu} - \Gamma^{(1)\rho}{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^{(1)\lambda}{}_{\rho\mu} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \Gamma^{(1)\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\lambda\sigma}\Gamma^{(1)}_{\sigma\mu\nu}, \qquad \Gamma^{(1)}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_\mu h_{\sigma\nu} + \partial_\nu h_{\sigma\mu} - \partial_\sigma h_{\mu\nu}\bigr), \qquad \Gamma^{(2)\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu} = -h^{\lambda\sigma}\Gamma^{(1)}_{\sigma\mu\nu} .
> $$
>
>    Written out,
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned} R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\Bigl[&\tfrac12\partial_\mu h_{\alpha\beta}\partial_\nu h^{\alpha\beta} + h^{\alpha\beta}\bigl(\partial_\mu\partial_\nu h_{\alpha\beta} + \partial_\alpha\partial_\beta h_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\alpha\partial_\mu h_{\nu\beta} - \partial_\alpha\partial_\nu h_{\mu\beta}\bigr) \\ &+ \partial^\beta h^\alpha{}_\nu\bigl(\partial_\beta h_{\alpha\mu} - \partial_\alpha h_{\beta\mu}\bigr) - \bigl(\partial_\beta h^{\alpha\beta} - \tfrac12\partial^\alpha h\bigr)\bigl(\partial_\mu h_{\nu\alpha} + \partial_\nu h_{\mu\alpha} - \partial_\alpha h_{\mu\nu}\bigr)\Bigr] . \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> 3. *(Slow and fast equations.)* $\bar R_{\mu\nu}$ contains only long wavelengths, $R^{(1)}$ only short ones, $R^{(2)}$ and $T$ both. Averaging separates
>
> $$
> \bar R_{\mu\nu} = -\langle R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}\rangle + 8\pi\bigl\langle T_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}T\bigr\rangle, \qquad R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu} = -\bigl(R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu} - \langle R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}\rangle\bigr) + 8\pi\bigl(T_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}T\bigr)^{\mathrm{High}} .
> $$
>
>    In vacuum about a flat background the fast equation is, at leading order, $R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}[h] = 0$: the linearised vacuum equation of [[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-5|Theorem §C8.1.5]].
> 4. *(Coarse-grained Einstein equation.)* Define the **smoothed matter tensor** $\bar T_{\mu\nu}$ by $\langle T_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}T\rangle = \bar T_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\bar g_{\mu\nu}\bar T$, $\bar T = \bar g^{\mu\nu}\bar T_{\mu\nu}$, and $R^{(2)} = \bar g^{\mu\nu}R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}$. Then the Einstein tensor of the background obeys
>
> $$
> \bar G_{\mu\nu} \equiv \bar R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\bar g_{\mu\nu}\bar R = 8\pi\bar T_{\mu\nu} - \Bigl(\bigl\langle R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}\bigr\rangle - \tfrac12\bar g_{\mu\nu}\bigl\langle R^{(2)}\bigr\rangle\Bigr) :
> $$
>
>    the averaged ripple acts on the background exactly as an extra source would.
> 5. *(⚑ How strong the ripple can be.)* Where the waves are the only source of the background curvature, $1/L_B^2 \sim \epsilon^2/\bar\lambda^2$, i.e. $\epsilon \sim \bar\lambda/L_B$; with other sources present, $\epsilon \lesssim \bar\lambda/L_B$. The two small parameters of the expansion are therefore not independent.
>
> *Model:* [[#^def-c8-4-1|Def. §C8.4.1]] with $\epsilon \ll 1$ and $\bar\lambda \ll l \ll L_B$; *applies:* to second order in $\epsilon$ and leading order in $\bar\lambda/l$, $l/L_B$. Part 2 is exact algebra; on a curved background the same structure holds with $\partial \to \bar\nabla$ (Couzens (2.82)–(2.83), stated there and not derived here), and the fast equation becomes the wave equation $\bar\nabla^\rho\bar\nabla_\rho\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0$ in the gauge $\bar\nabla^\nu\bar h_{\nu\mu} = 0$ (stated by Couzens, with a reference).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.4.1, eqs. (2.78)–(2.84), (2.86), (2.89), pp. 27–29 (PDF 28–30; on (2.83) see [[#^cau-c8-4-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens' §2.4]]) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §21.8, eqs. (21.114)–(21.117), pp. 421–422 (PDF 436–437; flat background) · part 1 and the derivation of part 2 worked here*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (averaging rules). *Background:* expand $B(y) = B(x) + (y - x)^\alpha\partial_\alpha B(x) + O(|y - x|^2/L_B^2)\,B$ inside the integral; the zeroth term gives $B(x)\int W = B(x)$, the first is $O(l/L_B)$ and vanishes for a weight symmetric about $0$, so $\langle B\rangle = B + O(l^2/L_B^2)$. *Linear in the ripple:* for one Fourier mode $h = \mathrm{Re}\,(A\,e^{ik\cdot y})$,
>
> $$
> \langle h\rangle(x) = \mathrm{Re}\Bigl(A\,e^{ik\cdot x}\int W(s)\,e^{-ik\cdot s}\,d^4s\Bigr) = \mathrm{Re}\bigl(A\,e^{ik\cdot x}\,\hat w(lk)\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> substituting $y = x - s$ and $W(s) = l^{-4}w(s/l)$. The Fourier transform $\hat w$ of a smooth function of bounded support decays faster than any power: it lies in $\mathcal S$ ([[§CA.4 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-4-1|QFT Theorem §CA.4.1]], part 1), and $l|k| \sim l/\bar\lambda \gg 1$. *Derivatives:* differentiating under the integral and moving the derivative onto $W$,
>
> $$
> \langle\partial_\mu V\rangle(x) = \int W(x - y)\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial y^\mu}\,d^4y = \int\frac{\partial W(x - y)}{\partial x^\mu}\,V(y)\,d^4y = \partial_\mu\langle V\rangle ,
> $$
>
> with no boundary term since $W$ vanishes outside the box (the divergence theorem, [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]]); and $|\partial_\mu W| \le l^{-5}|\partial w|$ gives $|\langle\partial_\mu V\rangle| \le \sup|V|\,\int|\partial_\mu w|\,d^4s/l = C_w\sup|V|/l$. For $V = AB$ quadratic in the ripple, $\sup|V| \sim \epsilon^2/\bar\lambda^{n}$ while the terms $(\partial A)B$ and $A\,\partial B$ are of size $\epsilon^2/\bar\lambda^{n+1}$; so $\langle A\,\partial B\rangle + \langle(\partial A)B\rangle = \langle\partial(AB)\rangle$ is smaller by $\bar\lambda/l$. For a time average only $\partial_t$ moves; for a wave $f(t - z)$, $\partial_zf = -\partial_tf$ turns a $z$-derivative into a $t$-derivative first (Couzens' footnote 5, p. 30).
>
> **Step 2** (inverse metric and Christoffel symbols on a flat background). With $\bar g = \eta$, $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}$, the inverse is $g^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\nu} - h^{\mu\nu} + h^{\mu\alpha}h_\alpha{}^\nu + O(h^3)$; check: $(\eta_{\mu\alpha} + h_{\mu\alpha})(\eta^{\alpha\nu} - h^{\alpha\nu} + h^{\alpha\beta}h_\beta{}^\nu) = \delta_\mu^\nu - h_\mu{}^\nu + h_\mu{}^\beta h_\beta{}^\nu + h_\mu{}^\nu - h_{\mu\alpha}h^{\alpha\nu} + O(h^3) = \delta_\mu^\nu + O(h^3)$. Since $\partial\eta = 0$, the lowered Christoffel symbols are exactly linear in $h$,
>
> $$
> \Gamma_{\sigma\mu\nu} \equiv g_{\sigma\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_\mu g_{\sigma\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\sigma\mu} - \partial_\sigma g_{\mu\nu}\bigr) = \Gamma^{(1)}_{\sigma\mu\nu} ,
> $$
>
> and raising with the inverse metric,
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = g^{\lambda\sigma}\Gamma^{(1)}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \underbrace{\eta^{\lambda\sigma}\Gamma^{(1)}_{\sigma\mu\nu}}_{\Gamma^{(1)\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}} \;\underbrace{-\,h^{\lambda\sigma}\Gamma^{(1)}_{\sigma\mu\nu}}_{\Gamma^{(2)\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu}} + O(h^3) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (Ricci tensor to second order). In the conventions above $R_{\mu\nu} = R^\rho{}_{\mu\rho\nu} = \partial_\rho\Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\mu} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\rho\mu} + \Gamma^\rho{}_{\rho\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\mu} - \Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\rho\mu}$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^def-c3-11-1|Def. §C3.11.1]]). Insert $\Gamma = \Gamma^{(1)} + \Gamma^{(2)}$: the derivative terms are linear in $\Gamma$, so their order-$h^2$ part is $\partial_\rho\Gamma^{(2)\rho}{}_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^{(2)\rho}{}_{\rho\mu}$; the products are quadratic, so their order-$h^2$ part has $\Gamma^{(1)}$ in both factors. This is the formula of part 2; the order-$h$ part, $\partial_\rho\Gamma^{(1)\rho}{}_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^{(1)\rho}{}_{\rho\mu}$, is $R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-2|Theorem §C8.1.2]]). The compact formula agrees with $\tfrac12\,d^2/d\varepsilon^2$ of the full Ricci tensor of $\eta + \varepsilon h$ at $\varepsilon = 0$ (checked by script, full curvature code).
>
> **Step 4** (written out). Insert $\Gamma^{(1)}$, $\Gamma^{(2)}$ and collect. The second-derivative terms come only from the first two terms: $\partial_\rho\Gamma^{(2)\rho}{}_{\mu\nu} = -(\partial_\rho h^{\rho\sigma})\,\Gamma^{(1)}_{\sigma\mu\nu} - h^{\rho\sigma}\partial_\rho\Gamma^{(1)}_{\sigma\mu\nu}$ gives $-\tfrac12h^{\alpha\beta}(\partial_\alpha\partial_\mu h_{\beta\nu} + \partial_\alpha\partial_\nu h_{\beta\mu} - \partial_\alpha\partial_\beta h_{\mu\nu})$, and $-\partial_\nu\Gamma^{(2)\rho}{}_{\rho\mu} = \tfrac12\partial_\nu(h^{\alpha\beta}\partial_\mu h_{\alpha\beta})$ (by the identity of Theorem §C8.4.3, Step 2, which holds in every gauge) gives $\tfrac12h^{\alpha\beta}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu h_{\alpha\beta}$: together the $h^{\alpha\beta}(\dots)$ bracket of the written-out form. The first-derivative products (from $\partial h^{\rho\sigma}$ in these two terms and from the two $\Gamma^{(1)}\Gamma^{(1)}$ products) are many; their sum was not collected by hand but checked by script: the written-out form of part 2 equals the compact one for ten arbitrary functions $h_{\mu\nu}(t, x, y, z)$, all ten components. So does d'Inverno & Vickers' (21.114), read unchanged in $-{+}{+}{+}$. Couzens' (2.83) on a flat background agrees only if its second term is doubled ([[#^cau-c8-4-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens' §2.4]]).
>
> **Step 5** (slow and fast equations). Insert the expansion of part 2 into $R_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi(T_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}T)$ and average. By Step 1, $\langle\bar R_{\mu\nu}\rangle = \bar R_{\mu\nu}$ and $\langle R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}\rangle = 0$, because $R^{(1)}$ is linear in $h$ (on a curved background it contains $\bar g$ and its derivatives as slowly varying coefficients, which leave it linear in $h$); $R^{(2)}$ keeps its average (products such as $\cos^2 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos 2kx$). This gives the slow equation; subtracting it from the full equation gives the fast one. In vacuum about $\eta$, $\bar R_{\mu\nu} = 0$ at order $\epsilon$, and the fast equation at order $\epsilon/\bar\lambda^2$ is $R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu} = 0$, since $R^{(2)} - \langle R^{(2)}\rangle$ is smaller by the factor $\epsilon$.
>
> **Step 6** (coarse-grained form). Call $S_{\mu\nu} = \langle T_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}T\rangle$ and $S = \bar g^{\mu\nu}S_{\mu\nu}$. In four dimensions trace reversal is its own inverse: with $\bar T_{\mu\nu} := S_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\bar g_{\mu\nu}S$ one has $\bar T = S - 2S = -S$ and
>
> $$
> \bar T_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\bar g_{\mu\nu}\bar T = S_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\bar g_{\mu\nu}S + \tfrac12\bar g_{\mu\nu}S = S_{\mu\nu} ,
> $$
>
> so $\bar T$ as defined in part 4 exists and is unique. Trace the slow equation with $\bar g^{\mu\nu}$: $\bar R = -\langle R^{(2)}\rangle + 8\pi S$. Then
>
> $$
> \bar R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\bar g_{\mu\nu}\bar R = -\bigl(\langle R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}\rangle - \tfrac12\bar g_{\mu\nu}\langle R^{(2)}\rangle\bigr) + 8\pi\bigl(S_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\bar g_{\mu\nu}S\bigr) = -\bigl(\langle R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}\rangle - \tfrac12\bar g_{\mu\nu}\langle R^{(2)}\rangle\bigr) + 8\pi\bar T_{\mu\nu} ,
> $$
>
> using $\langle\bar g_{\mu\nu}X\rangle = \bar g_{\mu\nu}\langle X\rangle$ to this order (Step 1).
>
> **Step 7** (sizes). From Definition §C8.4.1: $\bar R \sim \partial\partial\bar g \sim 1/L_B^2$; $R^{(1)} \sim \partial\partial h \sim \epsilon/\bar\lambda^2$; $R^{(2)} \sim h\,\partial\partial h, (\partial h)^2 \sim \epsilon^2/\bar\lambda^2$. If $\bar T = 0$, the slow equation balances $\bar R \sim 1/L_B^2$ against $\langle R^{(2)}\rangle \sim \epsilon^2/\bar\lambda^2$, so $\epsilon \sim \bar\lambda/L_B$; a matter source can only add curvature, $1/L_B^2 \gtrsim \epsilon^2/\bar\lambda^2$. This is part 5.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The waves enter the background's field equation through the average of a term quadratic in $h$: the energy of the waves is a second-order effect, invisible in the linear theory, and it appears only after averaging, as the shape of the slow equation shows.
> - The second-order Ricci tensor on a flat background has a three-line compact form; the long expanded forms in the sources are its expansion, and one of them (Couzens (2.83)) carries a factor-2 slip that the compact form exposes.
> - ⚑ A wave can curve its own background only weakly: $\epsilon \sim \bar\lambda/L_B$ is small precisely because the short-wave condition holds.

The extra source term has the place of $8\pi\times$ an energy–momentum tensor on the right-hand side of the background's equation. It is given that name.

> [!definition] Definition §C8.4.3: Effective (Isaacson) Energy–Momentum Tensor of Gravitational Waves
> *Structure.* For a background–ripple split with an average $\langle\cdot\rangle$ ([[#^def-c8-4-1|Def. §C8.4.1]], [[#^def-c8-4-2|Def. §C8.4.2]]), the **effective energy–momentum tensor of the waves** is the symmetric $(0, 2)$ tensor field on the background
>
> $$
> t_{\mu\nu} = -\frac{1}{8\pi}\Bigl\langle R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\bar g_{\mu\nu}R^{(2)}\Bigr\rangle \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } t_{\mu\nu} = -\frac{c^4}{8\pi G}\bigl\langle\dots\bigr\rangle\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> with $R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}$ the second-order Ricci tensor of the ripple ([[#^thm-c8-4-2|Theorem §C8.4.2]], part 2), $R^{(2)} = \bar g^{\mu\nu}R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}$, and trace $t = \bar g^{\mu\nu}t_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac{1}{8\pi}\langle R^{(2)}\rangle$. It is quadratic in $h$, varies only on the background scale, and contains no matter. With this name, [[#^thm-c8-4-2|Theorem §C8.4.2]], part 4, reads
>
> $$
> \bar G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi\bigl(\bar T_{\mu\nu} + t_{\mu\nu}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the **coarse-grained Einstein equation**: for the slowly varying metric, the waves gravitate like matter with energy–momentum tensor $t$. Its energy density for an observer with four-velocity $u$ is $t_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu$ ([[§C4.2 Dust and the Energy–Momentum Tensor#^def-c4-2-2|Def. §C4.2.2]]).
>
> It is not a local quantity: it is an average over a region of size $l \gg \bar\lambda$, and so it escapes [[#^thm-c8-4-1|Theorem §C8.4.1]] and [[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c4-4-6|Theorem §C4.4.6]], which concern values at one event.
>
> *Model:* [[#^def-c8-4-1|Def. §C8.4.1]]; *applies:* to second order in the amplitude, for $\bar\lambda \ll l \ll L_B$.
>

## The tensor far from the source

Far from the source the background is flat, $\bar T = 0$, and the ripple is a vacuum wave. There $t_{\mu\nu}$ can be computed. The sources do the last step with "one finds"; it takes four short computations.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.4.3: In Vacuum, Far from the Source, t_μν = (1/32π)⟨∂_μh^TT_ij ∂_νh^TT_ij⟩
> On a flat background, let $h$ solve the linearised vacuum equations, $R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}[h] = 0$, and average over $l \gg \bar\lambda$ ([[#^def-c8-4-2|Def. §C8.4.2]]).
> 1. *(TT gauge.)* If $h$ is in TT gauge ($h_{0\mu} = 0$, $h = 0$, $\partial^\alpha h_{\alpha\beta} = 0$, hence $\Box h_{\mu\nu} = 0$; [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-1|Def. §C8.2.1]]), then
>
> $$
> \bigl\langle R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}\bigr\rangle = -\tfrac14\bigl\langle\partial_\mu h_{\alpha\beta}\,\partial_\nu h^{\alpha\beta}\bigr\rangle = -\tfrac14\bigl\langle\partial_\mu h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\,\partial_\nu h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
>    For a plane wave in one direction (any profile) this holds before averaging up to a total derivative: $R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu} = -\tfrac14\partial_\mu h_{\alpha\beta}\partial_\nu h^{\alpha\beta} + \tfrac14\partial_\mu\partial_\nu(h_{\alpha\beta}h^{\alpha\beta})$.
> 2. *(⚑ Traceless.)* $\langle R^{(2)}\rangle = \eta^{\mu\nu}\langle R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}\rangle = 0$, so $t^\mu{}_\mu = 0$: like electromagnetic radiation, gravitational radiation has a traceless energy–momentum tensor.
> 3. *(Gauge invariance under the average.)* For every vector field $\xi$ that is bounded with bounded derivatives (oscillating on the scale $\bar\lambda$),
>
> $$
> \bigl\langle R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}[h + \partial\xi + \partial\xi]\bigr\rangle = \bigl\langle R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}[h]\bigr\rangle, \qquad (\partial\xi + \partial\xi)_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\xi_\nu + \partial_\nu\xi_\mu ,
> $$
>
>    so $t_{\mu\nu}$ is the same in every gauge, although $R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}$ point by point is not.
> 4. *(The Isaacson tensor.)* Hence, in every gauge, with $h^{\mathrm{TT}}$ the TT form of the wave ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-2|Theorem §C8.2.2]]),
>
> $$
> t_{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{32\pi}\bigl\langle\partial_\mu h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\,\partial_\nu h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\bigr\rangle \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } t_{\mu\nu} = \frac{c^4}{32\pi G}\bigl\langle\partial_\mu h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\,\partial_\nu h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\bigr\rangle\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
>    with energy density
>
> $$
> t^{00} = t_{00} = \frac{1}{32\pi}\bigl\langle\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\bigr\rangle \ge 0 \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } u = \frac{c^2}{32\pi G}\bigl\langle\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\bigr\rangle\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Model:* [[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^mod-c8-1-1|Model §C8.1.1]] in vacuum, with the averaging of [[#^def-c8-4-2|Def. §C8.4.2]]; *applies:* to second order in $h$ and leading order in $\bar\lambda/l$; part 1 needs TT gauge, parts 3–4 any gauge. The TT form exists only in vacuum ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-2|Theorem §C8.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.4.2, eqs. (2.90)–(2.92), footnote 5, pp. 30–31 (PDF 31–32; (2.90) stated "one finds") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §21.8, eqs. (21.121)–(21.125), p. 423 (PDF 438; "a straightforward calculation (exercise)"; on (21.121) see [[#^cau-c8-4-2|Caution: The short formula needs TT gauge]]) · derivation worked here; part 1 checked by script exactly for superpositions of TT waves along three different directions with different frequencies, and pointwise for one direction with arbitrary profiles; part 3 checked for gauge vectors with null and non-null wave vectors*

^thm-c8-4-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Throughout, $\langle A\,\partial B\rangle = -\langle(\partial A)B\rangle$ ([[#^thm-c8-4-2|Theorem §C8.4.2]], part 1), and in TT gauge
>
> $$
> h \equiv \eta^{\alpha\beta}h_{\alpha\beta} = 0, \qquad \partial^\alpha h_{\alpha\beta} = 0, \qquad \Box h_{\alpha\beta} = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 1** (the four pieces). By [[#^thm-c8-4-2|Theorem §C8.4.2]], part 2, $R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu} = A_{\mu\nu} + B_{\mu\nu} + C_{\mu\nu} + D_{\mu\nu}$ with
>
> $$
> A_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\rho\Gamma^{(2)\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}, \qquad B_{\mu\nu} = -\partial_\nu\Gamma^{(2)\rho}{}_{\rho\mu}, \qquad C_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma^{(1)\rho}{}_{\rho\lambda}\Gamma^{(1)\lambda}{}_{\nu\mu}, \qquad D_{\mu\nu} = -\Gamma^{(1)\rho}{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^{(1)\lambda}{}_{\rho\mu} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** ($A$ and $B$ are total derivatives). $A_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\rho V^\rho{}_{\mu\nu}$ with $V^\rho{}_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma^{(2)\rho}{}_{\mu\nu}$, quadratic in the ripple; so $\langle A_{\mu\nu}\rangle = \partial_\rho\langle V^\rho{}_{\mu\nu}\rangle$, of relative order $\bar\lambda/l$: it drops. The same holds for $B$. Explicitly (in every gauge),
>
> $$
> \Gamma^{(2)\rho}{}_{\rho\mu} = -h^{\rho\sigma}\cdot\tfrac12\bigl(\partial_\rho h_{\sigma\mu} + \partial_\mu h_{\sigma\rho} - \partial_\sigma h_{\rho\mu}\bigr) = -\tfrac12h^{\rho\sigma}\partial_\mu h_{\rho\sigma} = -\tfrac14\partial_\mu\bigl(h^{\rho\sigma}h_{\rho\sigma}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> because $h^{\rho\sigma}\partial_\rho h_{\sigma\mu} - h^{\rho\sigma}\partial_\sigma h_{\rho\mu} = 0$ (exchange the dummy indices $\rho \leftrightarrow \sigma$ in the second term and use $h^{\sigma\rho} = h^{\rho\sigma}$); hence $B_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac14\partial_\mu\partial_\nu(h_{\alpha\beta}h^{\alpha\beta})$.
>
> **Step 3** ($C = 0$). The contracted symbol is
>
> $$
> \Gamma^{(1)\rho}{}_{\rho\lambda} = \tfrac12\eta^{\rho\sigma}\bigl(\partial_\rho h_{\sigma\lambda} + \partial_\lambda h_{\sigma\rho} - \partial_\sigma h_{\rho\lambda}\bigr) = \tfrac12\partial_\lambda h ,
> $$
>
> the first and third terms cancelling by the symmetry of $\eta^{\rho\sigma}$. In TT gauge $h = 0$, so $C_{\mu\nu} = 0$.
>
> **Step 4** ($D$, nine products). Raise and lower with $\eta$:
>
> $$
> D_{\mu\nu} = -\tfrac14\bigl(\partial_\nu h^\rho{}_\lambda + \partial_\lambda h^\rho{}_\nu - \partial^\rho h_{\nu\lambda}\bigr)\bigl(\partial_\rho h^\lambda{}_\mu + \partial_\mu h^\lambda{}_\rho - \partial^\lambda h_{\rho\mu}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Multiply out and average each product, moving one derivative across:
>
> | | product | average |
> |---|---|---|
> | (1) | $\partial_\nu h^\rho{}_\lambda\,\partial_\rho h^\lambda{}_\mu$ | $-\langle\partial_\nu(\partial_\rho h^\rho{}_\lambda)\,h^\lambda{}_\mu\rangle = 0$ |
> | (2) | $\partial_\nu h^\rho{}_\lambda\,\partial_\mu h^\lambda{}_\rho$ | $\langle\partial_\mu h_{\alpha\beta}\,\partial_\nu h^{\alpha\beta}\rangle$ |
> | (3) | $-\partial_\nu h^\rho{}_\lambda\,\partial^\lambda h_{\rho\mu}$ | $\langle\partial_\nu(\partial^\lambda h^\rho{}_\lambda)\,h_{\rho\mu}\rangle = 0$ |
> | (4) | $\partial_\lambda h^\rho{}_\nu\,\partial_\rho h^\lambda{}_\mu$ | $-\langle h^\rho{}_\nu\,\partial_\rho(\partial_\lambda h^\lambda{}_\mu)\rangle = 0$ |
> | (5) | $\partial_\lambda h^\rho{}_\nu\,\partial_\mu h^\lambda{}_\rho$ | $-\langle h^\rho{}_\nu\,\partial_\mu(\partial_\lambda h^\lambda{}_\rho)\rangle = 0$ |
> | (6) | $-\partial_\lambda h^\rho{}_\nu\,\partial^\lambda h_{\rho\mu}$ | $\langle h^\rho{}_\nu\,\Box h_{\rho\mu}\rangle = 0$ |
> | (7) | $-\partial^\rho h_{\nu\lambda}\,\partial_\rho h^\lambda{}_\mu$ | $\langle h_{\nu\lambda}\,\Box h^\lambda{}_\mu\rangle = 0$ |
> | (8) | $-\partial^\rho h_{\nu\lambda}\,\partial_\mu h^\lambda{}_\rho$ | $\langle h_{\nu\lambda}\,\partial_\mu(\partial^\rho h^\lambda{}_\rho)\rangle = 0$ |
> | (9) | $\partial^\rho h_{\nu\lambda}\,\partial^\lambda h_{\rho\mu}$ | $-\langle h_{\nu\lambda}\,\partial^\lambda(\partial^\rho h_{\rho\mu})\rangle = 0$ |
>
> In (2), $\partial_\nu h^\rho{}_\lambda\,\partial_\mu h^\lambda{}_\rho = \partial_\nu h^{\rho\lambda}\,\partial_\mu h_{\lambda\rho} = \partial_\mu h_{\alpha\beta}\,\partial_\nu h^{\alpha\beta}$. In (1), (3)–(5), (8), (9) the derivative that was moved meets a divergence $\partial^\alpha h_{\alpha\beta} = 0$, after the two derivatives are commuted; in (6), (7) it makes $\Box h = 0$. Therefore
>
> $$
> \langle D_{\mu\nu}\rangle = -\tfrac14\bigl\langle\partial_\mu h_{\alpha\beta}\,\partial_\nu h^{\alpha\beta}\bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (part 1). Adding Steps 2–4: $\langle R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}\rangle = 0 + 0 + 0 - \tfrac14\langle\partial_\mu h_{\alpha\beta}\partial_\nu h^{\alpha\beta}\rangle$. In TT gauge only spatial components survive and $h^{ij} = h_{ij}$, so $\partial_\mu h_{\alpha\beta}\partial_\nu h^{\alpha\beta} = \partial_\mu h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\partial_\nu h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}$. *One direction, before averaging:* if $h$ depends on $x$ only through $u = k\cdot x$ with $k$ null and $k^\alpha h_{\alpha\beta} = 0$, each of $A$, $C$ and the products (1), (3)–(9) contains $k^\alpha k_\alpha = 0$ or $k^\alpha h_{\alpha\beta} = 0$ and vanishes identically, e.g. (1) $= k_\nu k_\rho\,h'^\rho{}_\lambda h'^\lambda{}_\mu = 0$; what remains is $B + D$, the pointwise identity of part 1 (checked by script for arbitrary $f_+(t - z)$, $f_\times(t - z)$). The total derivative $\tfrac14\partial_\mu\partial_\nu(h_{\alpha\beta}h^{\alpha\beta})$ is what made the opening's pointwise density change sign; it averages to zero.
>
> **Step 6** (part 2). Contract part 1 with $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ and move the derivative:
>
> $$
> \langle R^{(2)}\rangle = -\tfrac14\bigl\langle\partial^\mu h_{\alpha\beta}\,\partial_\mu h^{\alpha\beta}\bigr\rangle = +\tfrac14\bigl\langle h_{\alpha\beta}\,\Box h^{\alpha\beta}\bigr\rangle = 0 .
> $$
>
> Then $t^\mu{}_\mu = \tfrac{1}{8\pi}\langle R^{(2)}\rangle = 0$ ([[#^def-c8-4-3|Def. §C8.4.3]]).
>
> **Step 7** (part 3, by diffeomorphism invariance). Let $\sigma_\varepsilon$ be the flow of $\xi^\mu = \eta^{\mu\nu}\xi_\nu$ ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^def-c2-5-2|Def. §C2.5.2]]) and $g_\varepsilon = \eta + \varepsilon h$. From $\sigma_{\varepsilon + s} = \sigma_\varepsilon\circ\sigma_s$ ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-1|Theorem §C2.5.1]]) and the definition of the Lie derivative ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]]), $\tfrac{d}{d\varepsilon}\sigma_\varepsilon^{\ast}T = \tfrac{d}{ds}\big|_0\sigma_\varepsilon^{\ast}\sigma_s^{\ast}T = \sigma_\varepsilon^{\ast}\mathcal L_\xi T$ for any fixed tensor $T$, and once more $\tfrac{d^2}{d\varepsilon^2}\sigma_\varepsilon^{\ast}T = \sigma_\varepsilon^{\ast}\mathcal L_\xi\mathcal L_\xi T$. Taylor's theorem then gives $\sigma_\varepsilon^{\ast}T = T + \varepsilon\mathcal L_\xi T + \tfrac12\varepsilon^2\mathcal L_\xi^2T + O(\varepsilon^3)$, and applied to $g_\varepsilon$,
>
> $$
> \sigma_\varepsilon^{\ast}g_\varepsilon = \eta + \varepsilon\,\bigl(h + \mathcal L_\xi\eta\bigr) + \varepsilon^2\bigl(\mathcal L_\xi h + \tfrac12\mathcal L_\xi^2\eta\bigr) + O(\varepsilon^3), \qquad (\mathcal L_\xi\eta)_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\xi_\nu + \partial_\nu\xi_\mu
> $$
>
> ([[§C5.4 Diffeomorphism Invariance#^thm-c5-4-1|Theorem §C5.4.1]], part 3, with $\nabla = \partial$ for $\eta$ in Cartesian coordinates). The Ricci tensor is built from the metric by the same formula in every chart, so the Ricci tensor of a pulled-back metric is the pulled-back Ricci tensor, $\mathrm{Ric}[\sigma_\varepsilon^{\ast}g_\varepsilon] = \sigma_\varepsilon^{\ast}\mathrm{Ric}[g_\varepsilon]$. Expand both sides to order $\varepsilon^2$, using $\mathrm{Ric}[\eta + \varepsilon k_1 + \varepsilon^2k_2] = \varepsilon R^{(1)}[k_1] + \varepsilon^2\bigl(R^{(1)}[k_2] + R^{(2)}[k_1]\bigr) + O(\varepsilon^3)$ on the left and $\mathrm{Ric}[g_\varepsilon] = \varepsilon R^{(1)}[h] + \varepsilon^2R^{(2)}[h] + O(\varepsilon^3)$ on the right:
>
> $$
> R^{(2)}[h + \mathcal L_\xi\eta] + R^{(1)}\bigl[\mathcal L_\xi h + \tfrac12\mathcal L_\xi^2\eta\bigr] = R^{(2)}[h] + \mathcal L_\xi R^{(1)}[h] .
> $$
>
> The wave solves the vacuum equation, $R^{(1)}[h] = 0$, so the last term is zero. The linearised Ricci tensor is a sum of second derivatives, $R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}[k] = \tfrac12\partial_\sigma\bigl(\partial_\nu k^\sigma{}_\mu + \partial_\mu k^\sigma{}_\nu - \partial^\sigma k_{\mu\nu} - \delta^\sigma_\mu\partial_\nu k\bigr)$ ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-2|Theorem §C8.1.2]]), a total derivative of a bounded quantity, so its average drops (Step 2's argument). Averaging gives part 3.
>
> **Step 8** (part 4). By Steps 6–7, $t_{\mu\nu} = -\tfrac{1}{8\pi}\langle R^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}\rangle$ takes the same value in every gauge; evaluate it in TT gauge with Step 5: $t_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac{1}{32\pi}\langle\partial_\mu h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\partial_\nu h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\rangle$. For $\mu = \nu = 0$, $t_{00} = \tfrac{1}{32\pi}\langle\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\rangle$, a sum of averaged squares, hence $\ge 0$; and $t^{00} = \eta^{0\alpha}\eta^{0\beta}t_{\alpha\beta} = (-1)^2t_{00}$. Restoring units: $\partial_0 = c^{-1}\partial_t$ and $t = \tfrac{c^4}{G}\times$ (curvature) ([[§C5.1 From Poisson's Equation to the Field Equations#^pr-c5-1-4|Principle §C5.1.4]], $8\pi \to 8\pi G/c^4$) give $u = \tfrac{c^4}{32\pi G}\cdot\tfrac{1}{c^2}\langle\dot h\dot h\rangle$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The average is not a convenience but the content: point by point $R^{(2)}$ differs from $-\tfrac14\partial h\,\partial h$ by total derivatives, sign-indefinite and gauge-dependent; the average removes exactly these.
> - Every integration by parts ends on one of the three TT conditions, $h = 0$, $\partial^\alpha h_{\alpha\beta} = 0$, $\Box h = 0$. Outside TT gauge the short formula is wrong ([[#^cau-c8-4-2|Caution: The short formula needs TT gauge]]); the gauge-invariant object is $\langle R^{(2)}\rangle$ itself (Step 7).
> - The energy density of the waves is positive, as it must be for gravity to attract the energy: through the coarse-grained equation the waves' energy curves the background in the same sense as matter does.

## Energy and momentum carried off

A tensor that obeys a conservation law gives a balance of energy in a volume against a flux through its boundary, as $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ did for matter in special relativity ([[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime#^pr-c4-4-1|Principle §C4.4.1]]) and as Poynting's theorem does for the electromagnetic field ([[§B9.1 Charge, Energy and Poynting's Theorem#^thm-b9-1-2|EM Theorem §B9.1.2]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.4.4: Energy and Momentum Flux; the Radiated Power
> In a vacuum region far from the source, with flat background and $t_{\mu\nu}$ as in [[#^thm-c8-4-3|Theorem §C8.4.3]]:
> 1. *(Conservation.)* $\partial_\mu t^{\mu\nu} = 0$.
> 2. *(Balance.)* For a fixed volume $V$ with boundary $S$ and outward normal $n$, the wave energy $E_V = \int_Vt^{00}\,d^3x$ and momentum $P^k_V = \int_Vt^{0k}\,d^3x$ obey
>
> $$
> \frac{dE_V}{dt} = -\oint_St^{0i}n_i\,dA, \qquad \frac{dP^k_V}{dt} = -\oint_St^{ik}n_i\,dA .
> $$
>
> 3. *(Outgoing waves.)* For an outgoing wave $h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij} = f_{ij}(t - r)/r$ at large $r$, with $t$ averaged over many periods,
>
> $$
> t^{0r} = t^{00} = \frac{1}{32\pi}\bigl\langle\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\bigr\rangle, \qquad t^{rr} = t^{00} + O(r^{-3}) :
> $$
>
>    the energy flows outward at the speed of light, and the energy flux equals the energy density.
> 4. *(Radiated power.)* The energy per unit time and area through a large sphere is $dE/dA\,dt = t^{00}$, and the total power is
>
> $$
> \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{r^2}{32\pi}\oint\bigl\langle\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\bigr\rangle\,d\Omega \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{c^3r^2}{32\pi G}\oint\bigl\langle\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\bigr\rangle\,d\Omega\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 5. *(Momentum carried off.)*
>
> $$
> \frac{dP^k}{dt} = -\frac{r^2}{32\pi}\oint\bigl\langle\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\,\partial_kh^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\bigr\rangle\,d\Omega = \frac{r^2}{32\pi}\oint n^k\bigl\langle\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\dot h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}\bigr\rangle\,d\Omega \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \times\frac{c^2}{G}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
>    nonzero when the radiation is stronger in one direction than in the opposite one: the source recoils.
>
> *Model:* [[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^mod-c8-1-1|Model §C8.1.1]] in the far zone of an isolated source, with [[#^thm-c8-4-3|Theorem §C8.4.3]]; *applies:* to leading order in $1/r$ and in the amplitude; the outgoing form $f_{ij}(t - r)/r$ is derived for a source in [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-5|Theorem §C8.5.5]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.4.2, eqs. (2.97)–(2.111), pp. 32–34 (PDF 33–35; see [[#^cau-c8-4-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens' §2.4]] on index placement) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §21.8, eqs. (21.112)–(21.113), (21.131), pp. 421, 424 (PDF 436, 439) · Hartle §16.5, eq. (16.22) (flux = energy density, $c = 1$), p. 343 (PDF 344)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (conservation, two ways). *From the background's equation:* the contracted Bianchi identity of the background, $\bar\nabla^\mu\bar G_{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^thm-c3-11-2|Theorem §C3.11.2]]), and the coarse-grained equation ([[#^def-c8-4-3|Def. §C8.4.3]]) give $\bar\nabla^\mu(\bar T_{\mu\nu} + t_{\mu\nu}) = 0$; far from the source $\bar T = 0$ and $\bar\nabla = \partial$, so $\partial^\mu t_{\mu\nu} = 0$, and raising both indices with the constant $\eta$ gives part 1. *Directly:* with $\Box h = 0$,
>
> $$
> \partial^\mu\bigl(\partial_\mu h_{ij}\,\partial_\nu h_{ij}\bigr) = \Box h_{ij}\,\partial_\nu h_{ij} + \partial^\mu h_{ij}\,\partial_\mu\partial_\nu h_{ij} = 0 + \tfrac12\partial_\nu\bigl(\partial^\mu h_{ij}\,\partial_\mu h_{ij}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> a total derivative, whose average is of relative order $\bar\lambda/l$ (checked by script on a superposition of three waves).
>
> **Step 2** (balance). The $\nu = 0$ component of part 1 is $\partial_0t^{00} + \partial_it^{i0} = 0$. Integrate over the fixed volume $V$ and use the divergence theorem ([[§32 Flux Integrals and the Divergence Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-32-1|452 Thm. §32.1]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{dE_V}{dt} = \int_V\partial_0t^{00}\,d^3x = -\int_V\partial_it^{i0}\,d^3x = -\oint_St^{i0}n_i\,dA ,
> $$
>
> and $t^{i0} = t^{0i}$. The component $\nu = k$ gives the momentum balance the same way.
>
> **Step 3** (outgoing wave). With $r = |\vec x|$, $n_i = x_i/r$ and $n_i\partial_i = \partial_r$, for $h_{ij} = f_{ij}(t - r)/r$:
>
> $$
> \partial_0h_{ij} = \frac{f'_{ij}}{r}, \qquad \partial_rh_{ij} = -\frac{f'_{ij}}{r} - \frac{f_{ij}}{r^2}, \qquad \partial_kh_{ij} = n_k\,\partial_rh_{ij} + O(r^{-2}) .
> $$
>
> The radial flux is $t^{0r} = n_it^{0i} = -n_it_{0i}$ (one time index raised), so by [[#^thm-c8-4-3|Theorem §C8.4.3]], part 4,
>
> $$
> t^{0r} = -\frac{1}{32\pi}\bigl\langle\partial_0h_{ij}\,\partial_rh_{ij}\bigr\rangle = \frac{1}{32\pi}\Bigl(\frac{\langle f'_{ij}f'_{ij}\rangle}{r^2} + \frac{\langle f_{ij}f'_{ij}\rangle}{r^3}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{32\pi}\,\frac{\langle f'_{ij}f'_{ij}\rangle}{r^2} ,
> $$
>
> since $f_{ij}f'_{ij} = \tfrac12\,\tfrac{d}{dt}(f_{ij}f_{ij})$ averages to zero for a bounded $f$ ([[#^thm-c8-4-2|Theorem §C8.4.2]], part 1). And $t^{00} = \tfrac{1}{32\pi}\langle\partial_0h_{ij}\partial_0h_{ij}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{32\pi}\langle f'_{ij}f'_{ij}\rangle/r^2$: the same. Likewise $t^{rr} = \tfrac{1}{32\pi}\langle(\partial_rh_{ij})^2\rangle = t^{00} + O(r^{-3})$.
>
> **Step 4** (power). For a sphere of radius $r$, $dA = r^2d\Omega$ and $n = \hat r$; by Steps 2–3 the energy inside decreases at the rate $\oint t^{0r}r^2\,d\Omega$, and this is the power carried off, $dE/dt = \tfrac{r^2}{32\pi}\oint\langle\dot h_{ij}\dot h_{ij}\rangle d\Omega$; $r^2\langle\dot h\dot h\rangle = \langle f'f'\rangle$ is independent of $r$, so every large sphere sees the same power. Restored, the flux is $c\,u = \tfrac{c^3}{32\pi G}\langle\dot h\dot h\rangle$.
>
> **Step 5** (momentum). By Step 2 the momentum inside decreases at the rate $\oint n_it^{ik}r^2\,d\Omega$, so this is the momentum carried off. With $t^{ik} = t_{ik} = \tfrac{1}{32\pi}\langle\partial_ih_{jl}\partial_kh_{jl}\rangle$ and $n_i\partial_ih = \partial_rh = -\dot h + O(r^{-2})$ (Step 3),
>
> $$
> n_it^{ik} = -\frac{1}{32\pi}\bigl\langle\dot h_{jl}\,\partial_kh_{jl}\bigr\rangle = \frac{n_k}{32\pi}\bigl\langle\dot h_{jl}\dot h_{jl}\bigr\rangle + O(r^{-3}) ,
> $$
>
> which gives part 5. Restored, momentum flux $=$ energy flux$/c$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - $t^{00} = t^{0r} = t^{rr}$: far from the source, gravitational radiation is a stream of energy moving radially at the speed of light, the same structure as a beam of light or of massless particles, with momentum flux equal to energy flux.
> - The power is a flux integral at large $r$, where the background is flat and the expansion in $h$ is accurate; this is why the formula is reliable even for sources, like merging black holes, that are themselves far from weak-field.

> [!caution] Caution: The short formula needs TT gauge
> The formula $t_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac{1}{32\pi}\langle\partial_\mu h_{\alpha\beta}\partial_\nu h^{\alpha\beta}\rangle$ (Couzens (2.91), d'Inverno & Vickers (21.125)) uses the TT conditions; with a general $h$ it is false. Couzens' sentence "one can check that this is gauge invariant" (p. 31) refers to the averaged $R^{(2)}$, i.e. to the full $t_{\mu\nu}$, and the short formula is its value in TT gauge.
>
> d'Inverno & Vickers give a general-gauge form, (21.121): $\langle t_{ab}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{32\pi}\langle h_{cd,a}h^{cd}{}_{,b} - \tfrac12h_{,a}h_{,b} - h^{cd}{}_{,c}h_{ad,b} - h^{cd}{}_{,c}h_{bd,a}\rangle$. As printed, with the metric perturbation $h$, it is not gauge invariant: for a TT wave plus a pure-gauge wave with null wave vector and $\partial\cdot\xi \neq 0$ (de Donder gauge kept, trace $h = 2\partial\cdot\xi \neq 0$) it differs from $-\tfrac{1}{8\pi}\langle R^{(2)} - \tfrac12\eta R^{(2)}\rangle$. Their (21.131) writes the flux along $z$ of a wave travelling in $+z$ as $\bar T_{0z} = +\tfrac{1}{32\pi}(h_\times^2 + h_+^2)\omega^2$; with lower indices $\bar T_{0z} = -\bar T_{00}$, and the positive flux is $\bar T^{0z}$ ([[#^ex-c8-4-1|Example §C8.4.1]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C8.4.1: Energy Density and Flux of a Plane Wave
> *Question.* What are $t^{\mu\nu}$, the energy density and the energy flux of the plane wave $h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{11} = -h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{22} = h_+\cos\varphi$, $h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{12} = h_\times\cos\varphi$, $\varphi = \omega(t - z)$ (Couzens (2.93))? How large is the flux for $h_+ = 10^{-21}$, $h_\times = 0$ at $f = 100\ \mathrm{Hz}$?
>
> *Derivatives.* $\partial_0h_{ij} = -\omega\sin\varphi\,H_{ij}$ and $\partial_zh_{ij} = +\omega\sin\varphi\,H_{ij}$, with $H_{11} = -H_{22} = h_+$, $H_{12} = H_{21} = h_\times$; $\partial_xh = \partial_yh = 0$. So
>
> $$
> \partial_0h_{ij}\,\partial_0h_{ij} = \omega^2\sin^2\varphi\,\bigl(H_{11}^2 + H_{22}^2 + 2H_{12}^2\bigr) = 2\omega^2\bigl(h_+^2 + h_\times^2\bigr)\sin^2\varphi ,
> $$
>
> and $\partial_0h_{ij}\partial_zh_{ij} = -2\omega^2(h_+^2 + h_\times^2)\sin^2\varphi$, $\partial_zh_{ij}\partial_zh_{ij} = +2\omega^2(h_+^2 + h_\times^2)\sin^2\varphi$.
>
> *Average.* $\langle\sin^2\varphi\rangle = \tfrac12$ over many periods ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|WO Theorem §B1.1.6]]). By [[#^thm-c8-4-3|Theorem §C8.4.3]], part 4, with $\rho \equiv \omega^2(h_+^2 + h_\times^2)/32\pi$:
>
> $$
> t_{00} = \rho, \qquad t_{0z} = -\rho, \qquad t_{zz} = \rho ; \qquad t^{00} = t^{0z} = t^{zz} = \rho = \frac{\omega^2\bigl(h_+^2 + h_\times^2\bigr)}{32\pi} ,
> $$
>
> all other components zero, i.e. $t^{\mu\nu} = \rho\,\hat k^\mu\hat k^\nu$ with $\hat k = (1, 0, 0, 1)$: null dust moving along $+z$, traceless since $\hat k\cdot\hat k = 0$. The flux through a surface $z = \text{const}$ is $t^{0z} = \rho$, the energy density times the speed of light (Hartle (16.21)–(16.22) for one polarisation; d'Inverno & Vickers (21.113), (21.130)).
>
> *Two waves.* Averaging kills cross terms between different wave vectors. Two waves along $+z$ with $\omega_1 \neq \omega_2$ (and $l$ longer than the beat length $2\pi/|\omega_1 - \omega_2|$) give $t^{00} = (\omega_1^2 + \omega_2^2)(h_+^2 + h_\times^2)/32\pi$: energies add. Two equal waves along $+z$ and $-z$ (a standing wave) give $t^{00} = t^{zz} = 2\rho$ but $t^{0z} = 0$: energy without net flux, as for a standing light wave (all checked by script).
>
> *Numbers.* Restored, the flux is $F = \tfrac{c^3}{32\pi G}\,\omega^2h_+^2$, with $\tfrac{c^3}{32\pi G} = 4.016\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{W\,s^2\,m^{-2}}$. For $f = 100\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $\omega = 2\pi f = 628.3\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\omega^2 = 3.948\times10^5\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$:
>
> $$
> F = 4.016\times10^{33}\times3.948\times10^5\times\bigl(10^{-21}\bigr)^2\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}} = 1.59\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}} ,
> $$
>
> an energy density $F/c = 5.29\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$. A strain of $10^{-21}$, a thousandth of a proton radius over a $4\ \mathrm{km}$ arm ([[§C8.3 What a Passing Wave Does#^ex-c8-3-1|Example §C8.3.1]]), carries milliwatts per square metre: the factor $c^3/G$ makes spacetime very stiff, so a small strain stores a large energy.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, Example 2.3, eqs. (2.93)–(2.96), pp. 31–32 (PDF 32–33) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §21.8, eqs. (21.111)–(21.113), (21.126)–(21.131), pp. 420–424 (PDF 435–439) · Hartle §16.5, eqs. (16.21)–(16.22), p. 343 (PDF 344) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C8.4.2: The Flux at Earth from GW150914
> *Question.* How much energy per unit area and time reached the Earth at the peak of the first detected signal (14 September 2015; [[§C8.3 What a Passing Wave Does#^law-c8-3-5|Law §C8.3.5]])? Two routes are open, one through the source's luminosity and one through the measured strain.
>
> *Data (typed sources).* Peak gravitational-wave power $L = 3.6\times10^{49}\ \mathrm W$, distance $1.4 \pm 0.6$ billion light years, orbital frequency $75\ \mathrm{Hz}$ at merger (half the wave frequency), three solar masses radiated (d'Inverno & Vickers, p. 442, PDF 457); luminosity distance $D_L = 410\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ (Schutz Table 12.1, p. 398, PDF 416); observed peak amplitude $10^{-21}$ (Schutz p. 231, PDF 249).
>
> *(a) From the luminosity.* The power crosses a sphere of radius $D$; with $D = 410\ \mathrm{Mpc} = 410\times3.0857\times10^{22}\ \mathrm m = 1.265\times10^{25}\ \mathrm m$,
>
> $$
> F = \frac{L}{4\pi D^2} = \frac{3.6\times10^{49}\ \mathrm W}{4\pi\,\bigl(1.265\times10^{25}\ \mathrm m\bigr)^2} = 1.79\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}} ;
> $$
>
> with d'Inverno & Vickers' $1.4\times10^9\ \mathrm{ly} = 1.325\times10^{25}\ \mathrm m$, $F = 1.63\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. (Isotropic emission is assumed; the real pattern is not isotropic, [[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral#^thm-c8-6-2|Theorem §C8.6.2]].)
>
> *(b) From the strain.* At the peak $f = 2\times75\ \mathrm{Hz} = 150\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $\omega = 942.5\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. A wave of amplitude $h = 10^{-21}$ in one polarisation carries, by [[#^ex-c8-4-1|Example §C8.4.1]],
>
> $$
> F = 4.016\times10^{33}\times\bigl(942.5\bigr)^2\times10^{-42}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}} = 3.57\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}} .
> $$
>
> *(c) Comparison.* Route (a) is $5.0$ times route (b); in amplitude the two agree within a factor $\sqrt{5.0} = 2.2$: route (a) needs $\sqrt{h_+^2 + h_\times^2} = 2.24\times10^{-21}$. The direction of the difference is expected. A single interferometer records $D^{ij}h_{ij} = F_+h_+ + F_\times h_\times$ ([[§C8.3 What a Passing Wave Does#^def-c8-3-2|Def. §C8.3.2]]), and since $F_+^2 + F_\times^2 \le 1$ for its detector tensor (equality only for a wave arriving along the normal of the detector plane; checked by script), the recorded strain is at most $\sqrt{h_+^2 + h_\times^2}$; a peak read off a noisy record to one significant figure does the rest. At the order of magnitude, the measured strain and the inferred luminosity are consistent through $t^{00}$.
>
> *(d) Against sunlight.* With the Sun's luminosity $3.9\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{erg/s} = 3.9\times10^{26}\ \mathrm W$ (Hartle p. 508, PDF 507) and $1\ \mathrm{AU} = 1.496\times10^{11}\ \mathrm m$, sunlight at the Earth carries $3.9\times10^{26}/\bigl(4\pi(1.496\times10^{11})^2\bigr) = 1.39\times10^3\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. The peak gravitational flux of (a) is $1.29\times10^{-5}$ of that, for a fraction of a second. The whole event delivered $3M_\odot c^2/4\pi D^2 = 5.36\times10^{47}\ \mathrm J/2.01\times10^{51}\ \mathrm{m^2} = 2.7\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}$, which sunlight delivers in $0.19\ \mu\mathrm s$. A source that for an instant outshone, in gravitational waves, all the starlight of the observable universe (d'Inverno & Vickers' comparison) arrives as a whisper because it is $1.3\times10^{25}\ \mathrm m$ away, and it is detectable at all only because of the stiffness factor of [[#^ex-c8-4-1|Example §C8.4.1]] working in reverse: a small energy flux means a strain of $10^{-21}$, not of $10^{-40}$.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §21.14, p. 442 (PDF 457) · Schutz §9.4, p. 231 (PDF 249); Table 12.1, p. 398 (PDF 416) · Hartle §23.6, p. 508 (PDF 507) · numbers checked by script*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Schutz's route: the energy a detector removes from the wave
> Schutz obtains the same flux without second-order perturbation theory, from energy conservation applied to detectors. A plane wave $\bar h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{xx} = -\bar h^{\mathrm{TT}}_{yy} = A\cos\Omega(z - t)$ falls on a sheet $z = 0$ of identical damped oscillators (two masses on a spring with friction), $\sigma$ per unit area. Each oscillator is driven into steady motion by the wave's tidal force and dissipates the power $\tfrac12m\gamma\Omega^2R^2$ in friction, so the flux behind the sheet must be lower by $\delta F = -\tfrac12\sigma m\gamma\Omega^2R^2$. Independently, each moving oscillator radiates (by the quadrupole formula, [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-5|Theorem §C8.5.5]]); the waves from the whole sheet add up to a plane wave that interferes destructively with the incident one downstream and lowers its amplitude by $\delta A = -2\pi\sigma m\Omega l_0R\sin\phi$. Dividing the two, with $R$ and the phase $\phi$ of the driven oscillator inserted, the detector parameters cancel and leave $\delta F/\delta A = \Omega^2A/16\pi$, a property of the wave alone. Integrating from $A = 0$ gives $F = \Omega^2A^2/32\pi$ (Schutz (9.122)), the $h_\times = 0$ case of [[#^ex-c8-4-1|Example §C8.4.1]].
>
> The two routes rest on different assumptions. Schutz assumes that energy is conserved when gravitational waves exchange it with matter and shows that only this flux is consistent with it, to lowest order; the route of this section derives the flux from Einstein's equations by asking how the waves curve the background, and conservation of $T + t$ is then a consequence (Theorem §C8.4.4, Step 1). The agreement of the two is a consistency check of general relativity: the energy that the waves deposit in a detector is the energy with which they gravitate.
>
> *Source: Schutz §9.5, eqs. (9.108)–(9.123), Figs. 9.5–9.7, pp. 239–244 (PDF 257–262)*

The energy flux of [[#^thm-c8-4-4|Theorem §C8.4.4]] is written in terms of the wave far away. To learn what a given source radiates, $h^{\mathrm{TT}}$ far away must be computed from the motion of the source, and the energy books then balance with the source's own energy: [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula|§C8.5]] finds the field of a slowly moving source and, inserted here, its luminosity.
