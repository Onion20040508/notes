[[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum|§C8.2]] reduced a gravitational wave far from its source to two functions. A plane wave moving along $z$ has, in the transverse-traceless gauge ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-1|Def. §C8.2.1]]), the metric ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-3|Theorem §C8.2.3]])

$$
ds^2 = -dt^2 + \bigl(1 + h_+(t - z)\bigr)dx^2 + \bigl(1 - h_+(t - z)\bigr)dy^2 + 2h_\times(t - z)\,dx\,dy + dz^2 ,
$$

with the plus and cross polarisations $h_+$, $h_\times$ of [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-2|Def. §C8.2.2]]. This section asks: *what does such a wave do that an instrument can record?*

The naive answer fails, and it is worth computing why. Put a free test mass at rest at the origin before the wave arrives and follow its coordinates. Its acceleration is given by the geodesic equation ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]); at rest, $dx^\mu/d\tau = (1, 0, 0, 0)$, so

$$
\frac{d^2x^i}{d\tau^2} = -\Gamma^i{}_{00} = -\tfrac12 g^{ij}\bigl(2\partial_0g_{j0} - \partial_jg_{00}\bigr) = -\tfrac12 g^{ij}\bigl(2\partial_0 0 - \partial_j(-1)\bigr) = 0 .
$$

Nothing moves: every free mass stays at the same coordinates, and the coordinate distance between any two of them never changes. Read literally, the wave has no effect. Yet the wave is curved spacetime: its Riemann tensor is $R^x{}_{0x0} = -\tfrac12\ddot h_+$, not zero ([[#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]]), and nonzero curvature means that some family of free bodies accelerates relative to itself ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-3|Theorem §C3.12.3]]). The contradiction is only apparent. The transverse-traceless coordinates are tied to the masses by construction, so their coordinate positions cannot show anything; what changes is the proper distance between them, a quantity that does not depend on the chart.

The section builds the answer in this order. Free masses keep their transverse-traceless coordinates ([[#^thm-c8-3-1|Theorem §C8.3.1]]). The proper distance between two of them oscillates with half the wave amplitude, by two routes: from the metric, and from geodesic deviation with the curvature of the wave ([[#^def-c8-3-1|Def. §C8.3.1]], [[#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]]). A ring of masses shows the two polarisations as two ellipse patterns, turned by 45° against each other ([[#^thm-c8-3-3|Theorem §C8.3.3]]). Light sent along two perpendicular arms turns the oscillation into a measurable change of return times ([[#^mod-c8-3-4|Model §C8.3.4]], [[#^def-c8-3-2|Def. §C8.3.2]]), and on 14 September 2015 two such instruments recorded it ([[#^law-c8-3-5|Law §C8.3.5]]).

**Setup.** Linearised gravity about Minkowski spacetime ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^mod-c8-1-1|Model §C8.1.1]]), in vacuum, with a wave in the transverse-traceless gauge: $h_{0\mu} = 0$, $h^i{}_i = 0$, $\partial_ih_{ij} = 0$ ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-1|Def. §C8.2.1]]). The plane wave along $z$ above is the main case; [[#^thm-c8-3-1|Theorem §C8.3.1]] holds for any wave in this gauge. The transverse-traceless gauge is set up in the frame in which the test masses are at rest before the wave arrives. Test masses are point bodies on timelike geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]) whose own field is neglected; light follows null geodesics (same principle). All statements are to first order in $h$; waves reaching the Earth have $h \sim 10^{-21}$ ([[#^law-c8-3-5|Law §C8.3.5]]), so the second order is far below any measurement. A monochromatic wave is written with real amplitudes and a choice of time origin, $h_+(t - z) = h_+\cos\omega(t - z)$; Couzens' $\mathrm{Re}[h_+e^{-i\omega(t - z)}]$ with complex $h_+$ differs by a phase.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; $h$ is dimensionless and unchanged, $t \to ct$, a curvature component has the dimension $(\text{length})^{-2}$); MTW curvature with $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\sigma} + \Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\sigma}$ ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]]); Greek indices $0$–$3$, Latin $1$–$3$, $x^0 = t$; hatted indices are components in an orthonormal frame ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]); $\dot{} = \partial_t$. d'Inverno & Vickers use $+{-}{-}{-}$ and let their waves travel along $x$ ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^cau-c8-2-1|§C8.2, Caution: d'Inverno & Vickers' waves travel along x]]); their formulas are converted where quoted.

## Free masses and the transverse-traceless frame

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.3.1: Free Test Masses Keep Their TT Coordinates
> Let $g = \eta + h$ with $h$ in the transverse-traceless gauge ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-1|Def. §C8.2.1]]): $g_{00} = -1$, $g_{0i} = 0$, $g_{ij} = \delta_{ij} + h_{ij}(t, \vec x)$.
> 1. **Christoffel symbols.** Exactly, $\Gamma^\mu{}_{00} = 0$ and $\Gamma^0{}_{0i} = 0$. To first order in $h$,
>
> $$
> \Gamma^i{}_{0j} = \Gamma^0{}_{ij} = \tfrac12\,\dot h_{ij}, \qquad \Gamma^i{}_{jk} = \tfrac12\bigl(\partial_jh_{ik} + \partial_kh_{ij} - \partial_ih_{jk}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 2. **Masses at rest stay at rest.** For every fixed $\vec x_0$ the worldline $t = \tau$, $\vec x = \vec x_0$ is a timelike geodesic ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]) with proper time $\tau = t$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]). A free test mass at rest before the wave arrives follows it: it keeps its coordinates $\vec x_0$ while the wave passes.
> 3. **Meaning of the gauge.** The transverse-traceless chart is comoving with free masses that were at rest, and its time coordinate is the proper time of each of them. The coordinate distance $|\vec x_1 - \vec x_2|$ between two such masses is constant and measures nothing about the wave.
>
> *Model:* [[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^mod-c8-1-1|Model §C8.1.1]] in vacuum, transverse-traceless gauge set up in the initial rest frame of the masses; *applies:* parts 1 (exact equalities) and 2 hold exactly for the metric as written, which solves Einstein's equation to first order in $h$; so the statement about general relativity is a first-order statement (Couzens: "strictly speaking … to linear order").
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.3.4, eqs. (2.64)–(2.65) (PDF 22) · Schutz §9.2, eqs. (9.22)–(9.23) (PDF 224) · d'Inverno & Vickers §21.11, eqs. (21.149)–(21.152) (PDF 445) · Hartle §16.2, eqs. (16.3)–(16.8) (PDF 334–335)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the inverse metric). $g_{00} = -1$ and $g_{0i} = 0$ exactly, so the inverse is block diagonal: $g^{00} = -1$, $g^{0i} = 0$, and $g^{ij}$ is the inverse of $\delta_{ij} + h_{ij}$, equal to $\delta_{ij} - h_{ij} + O(h^2)$ ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^mod-c8-1-1|Model §C8.1.1]]).
>
> **Step 2** (the Christoffel symbols with two time indices). By the Christoffel formula ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection#^thm-c3-6-2|Theorem §C3.6.2]]),
>
> $$
> \Gamma^i{}_{00} = \tfrac12 g^{ij}\bigl(\partial_0g_{j0} + \partial_0g_{j0} - \partial_jg_{00}\bigr) + \tfrac12 g^{i0}(\dots) = \tfrac12 g^{ij}\bigl(0 + 0 - \partial_j(-1)\bigr) + 0 = 0 ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \Gamma^0{}_{00} = \tfrac12 g^{00}\,\partial_0g_{00} = 0, \qquad \Gamma^0{}_{0i} = \tfrac12 g^{00}\bigl(\partial_0g_{0i} + \partial_ig_{00} - \partial_0g_{0i}\bigr) = \tfrac12(-1)\bigl(0 + 0 - 0\bigr) = 0 .
> $$
>
> No approximation was used.
>
> **Step 3** (the mixed and spatial symbols). With Step 1 and $\partial_0g_{ij} = \dot h_{ij}$,
>
> $$
> \Gamma^i{}_{0j} = \tfrac12 g^{ik}\bigl(\partial_0g_{kj} + \partial_jg_{k0} - \partial_kg_{0j}\bigr) = \tfrac12(\delta_{ik} - h_{ik})\,\dot h_{kj} = \tfrac12\dot h_{ij} + O(h^2) ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \Gamma^0{}_{ij} = \tfrac12 g^{00}\bigl(\partial_ig_{0j} + \partial_jg_{0i} - \partial_0g_{ij}\bigr) = \tfrac12(-1)(0 + 0 - \dot h_{ij}) = \tfrac12\dot h_{ij} \quad (\text{exact}),
> $$
>
> and $\Gamma^i{}_{jk} = \tfrac12(\delta_{il} - h_{il})(\partial_jh_{lk} + \partial_kh_{lj} - \partial_lh_{jk}) = \tfrac12(\partial_jh_{ik} + \partial_kh_{ij} - \partial_ih_{jk}) + O(h^2)$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (the worldline is a geodesic). For $x^\mu(\tau) = (\tau, \vec x_0)$, $\dot x^\mu = (1, 0, 0, 0)$ and $\ddot x^\mu = 0$. The geodesic equation ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]) reads
>
> $$
> \ddot x^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\lambda}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\lambda = 0 + \Gamma^\mu{}_{00}\cdot 1\cdot 1 = 0
> $$
>
> by Step 2. The tangent has $g(\dot x, \dot x) = g_{00} = -1$, so $\tau$ is proper time and the curve is timelike.
>
> **Step 5** (the mass follows it). Before the wave arrives $h = 0$, the chart is an inertial frame, and the mass is at rest at $\vec x_0$: its geodesic has initial position $\vec x_0$ and initial velocity $(1, \vec 0)$. A geodesic is fixed by its initial point and velocity (uniqueness of solutions of the second-order geodesic equation, [[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]), and the curve of Step 4 has the same data, so the two coincide for all $\tau$. This is part 2; part 3 restates it.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Nothing in the argument used the field equations or the form of $h_{ij}$; only $h_{0\mu} = 0$. The transverse-traceless gauge is the gauge in which the wave has no $h_{00}$ to push masses and no $h_{0i}$ to drag them. The whole effect of the wave sits in $h_{ij}$, which changes the *metric* between masses that stay put. That is why the next theorem must look at lengths.

## What changes: proper distance and tidal acceleration

The masses keep their coordinates, and their clocks all read $t$. What a ruler or a light signal measures between two of them at one instant $t$ is the length of the segment joining them in the slice $t = \text{const}$, which is orthogonal to every mass's worldline because $g_{0i} = 0$.

> [!definition] Definition §C8.3.1: Strain of a Pair of Test Masses
> *Structure.* Let two free test masses be at rest in the transverse-traceless chart of [[#^thm-c8-3-1|Theorem §C8.3.1]], at $\vec x_A$ and $\vec x_B = \vec x_A + L_0\,\vec n$, with $\vec n$ a unit vector of $\mathbf R^3$ and $L_0 > 0$ their separation before the wave arrives. Their **proper separation** $L(t)$ at time $t$ is the length ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-3|Def. §C3.1.3]]) of the straight coordinate segment $s \mapsto (t, \vec x_A + s\vec n)$, $0 \le s \le L_0$, in the metric $g_{ij}(t, \vec x)$ of that slice. The **strain** is the dimensionless function
>
> $$
> \frac{\delta L(t)}{L_0} \equiv \frac{L(t) - L_0}{L_0} .
> $$
>
> *Why this segment.* Any other curve between the same two events of the slice that differs from the straight segment by $O(h)$ has the same length to first order ([[#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]], part 2): the straight line is stationary for the flat length. So $L(t)$ is, to first order, the shortest distance between the masses at time $t$, and it is what radar ranging measures when $L_0$ is small compared with the wavelength ([[#^mod-c8-3-4|Model §C8.3.4]], part 3). Hartle calls $\delta L/L_0$ the "fractional strain", borrowing the word from elasticity.
>
> *Source: Hartle §16.2, Ex. 16.1, eqs. (16.9)–(16.12) (PDF 335–336) · Schutz §9.2, eq. (9.24) (PDF 224) · d'Inverno & Vickers §21.11, eq. (21.153) (PDF 446)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.3.2: The Proper Distance Oscillates: δL/L = ½h₊ cos ωt along x
> Let the plane wave $h_{ij}(t - z)$ along $z$ of [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-3|Theorem §C8.2.3]] pass free masses at rest in its transverse-traceless chart.
> 1. **The curvature of the wave.** To first order the Riemann tensor with two time indices is
>
> $$
> R^i{}_{0j0} = R_{i0j0} = -\tfrac12\,\ddot h_{ij}, \qquad R^i{}_{00j} = +\tfrac12\,\ddot h_{ij} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } R^i{}_{0j0} = -\frac{1}{2c^2}\frac{\partial^2h_{ij}}{\partial t^2}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> nonzero only for $i, j \in \{1, 2\}$; these are the components of [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-3|Theorem §C8.2.3]], part 2, read with an upper index and rederived below from the Christoffel symbols of the masses' chart. The tidal tensor of the masses' rest frame, $\mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath} = R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat\jmath\hat0}$ ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^def-c3-12-4|Def. §C3.12.4]]), is therefore $-\tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}$: transverse (no entry along $z$) and traceless (vacuum, [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]], part 3). The other independent components are $R^i{}_{3j3} = -\tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}$ and $R^i{}_{0j3} = +\tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}$.
> 2. **From the metric.** For two masses a coordinate distance $L_0$ apart along a unit vector $\vec n$ in the plane $z = 0$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\delta L(t)}{L_0} = \tfrac12\,h_{ij}(t, 0)\,n^in^j \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\delta L}{L_0} = \tfrac12 h_+\cos\omega t \quad (\vec n = \hat x,\ h_+(u) = h_+\cos\omega u) ,
> $$
>
> for any $L_0$; along $\hat y$ the strain is $-\tfrac12 h_+\cos\omega t$, and along $z$ it is zero. If $\vec n$ has a $z$-component, the formula holds for $L_0$ small compared with the reduced wavelength $1/\omega$. Every $O(h)$ deformation of the segment leaves $L$ unchanged to first order.
> 3. **From geodesic deviation.** Along the worldline of a mass $A$ at rest, the frame
>
> $$
> e_{\hat 0} = \partial_t, \qquad e_{\hat\imath} = \partial_i - \tfrac12 h_{ij}\,\partial_j
> $$
>
> is orthonormal and parallel-transported ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-1|Def. §C3.7.1]]) to first order: it is the frame of a freely falling observer with non-rotating axes. In it the deviation vector $S$ ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^def-c3-12-2|Def. §C3.12.2]]) to a neighbouring mass obeys ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]], part 1)
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2S^{\hat\imath}}{dt^2} = -R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat\jmath\hat0}\,S^{\hat\jmath} = \tfrac12\,\ddot h_{ij}\,S^{\hat\jmath}, \qquad \text{solved by} \qquad S^{\hat\imath}(t) = \bigl(\delta_{ij} + \tfrac12 h_{ij}(t)\bigr)S^{\hat\jmath}_0 ,
> $$
>
> where $S_0$ is the separation before the wave. The same vector has constant transverse-traceless components $S^j = S^j_0$ ([[#^thm-c8-3-1|Theorem §C8.3.1]]), and $S^{\hat\imath} = (\delta_{ij} + \tfrac12 h_{ij})S^j$: the two descriptions agree, and both give part 2.
>
> In words: the wave produces a tidal acceleration $\tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}\xi^j$ in the transverse plane, and free masses respond by changing their proper separations by the fraction $\tfrac12 h_{ij}n^in^j$, following the wave's own waveform.
>
> *Model:* [[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^mod-c8-1-1|Model §C8.1.1]] in vacuum with a plane wave in transverse-traceless gauge, free masses at rest before it arrives; *applies:* first order in $h$; part 3 for separations small compared with the wavelength (geodesic deviation is a first-order statement in the separation), part 2 in the transverse plane for any separation.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.3.4, eqs. (2.66)–(2.67) and the text before them (PDF 22–23); eq. (2.4) (PDF 11) · Schutz §9.2, eqs. (9.24)–(9.29) (PDF 224–227) · d'Inverno & Vickers §21.11, eqs. (21.153)–(21.157) (PDF 446–447; waves along $x$ there) · Hartle §16.2, eqs. (16.9)–(16.12) (PDF 335–336)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation 1 (from the metric)
> **Step 1** (the length integral). On the segment $x^i(s) = x_A^i + sn^i$ in the slice $t$, $dx^i/ds = n^i$ and, by [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-3|Def. §C3.1.3]],
>
> $$
> L(t) = \int_0^{L_0}\sqrt{g_{ij}\,n^in^j}\,ds = \int_0^{L_0}\sqrt{1 + h_{ij}(t - z(s))\,n^in^j}\,ds ,
> $$
>
> using $\delta_{ij}n^in^j = 1$.
>
> **Step 2** (expand). $\sqrt{1 + w} = 1 + \tfrac12 w + O(w^2)$ with $w = h_{ij}n^in^j = O(h)$:
>
> $$
> L(t) = L_0 + \tfrac12\,n^in^j\int_0^{L_0}h_{ij}\bigl(t - z(s)\bigr)\,ds + O(h^2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (transverse separation). If $\vec n$ lies in the plane $z = 0$ and $z_A = 0$, then $z(s) = 0$ along the whole segment, the integrand is the constant $h_{ij}(t)$, and $\delta L/L_0 = \tfrac12 h_{ij}(t)n^in^j$ for every $L_0$. For $\vec n = \hat x$: $h_{ij}n^in^j = h_{11} = h_+(t) = h_+\cos\omega t$, so $\delta L/L_0 = \tfrac12 h_+\cos\omega t$. For $\vec n = \hat y$: $h_{22} = -h_+$. For $\vec n = \hat z$: $h_{33} = 0$ (transversality, [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-1|Def. §C8.2.1]]).
>
> **Step 4** (separation with a $z$-component). Then $z(s) = sn^3$ and $h_{ij}(t - sn^3) = h_{ij}(t) - sn^3\dot h_{ij}(t) + \dots$; the correction to Step 3 is of relative size $L_0\omega$, small when $L_0 \ll 1/\omega$.
>
> **Step 5** (other curves give the same length). Deform the segment to $x^i(s) = x^i_A + sn^i + \delta x^i(s)$ with $\delta x = O(h)$ and $\delta x(0) = \delta x(L_0) = 0$ (same endpoints). Then $dx^i/ds = n^i + \delta x'^i$ and
>
> $$
> g_{ij}\frac{dx^i}{ds}\frac{dx^j}{ds} = (\delta_{ij} + h_{ij})(n^i + \delta x'^i)(n^j + \delta x'^j) = 1 + 2n_i\,\delta x'^i + h_{ij}n^in^j + O(h^2) ,
> $$
>
> so the integrand is $1 + n_i\delta x'^i + \tfrac12 h_{ij}n^in^j + O(h^2)$. The new term integrates to
>
> $$
> \int_0^{L_0}n_i\,\delta x'^i\,ds = n_i\bigl[\delta x^i(L_0) - \delta x^i(0)\bigr] = 0 :
> $$
>
> the straight line is stationary for the flat length, and the first-order length is that of Step 2 (checked by script).
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The strain is the component of $\tfrac12 h_{ij}$ along the line joining the masses. It is a property of the metric at time $t$, and the masses need not move in any chart for it to change.

> [!derivation]- Derivation 2 (from geodesic deviation)
> **Step 1** (the curvature components). Use the component formula of [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^thm-c3-9-2|Theorem §C3.9.2]] with $\rho = i$, $\sigma = 0$, $\mu = j$, $\nu = 0$:
>
> $$
> R^i{}_{0j0} = \partial_j\Gamma^i{}_{00} - \partial_0\Gamma^i{}_{j0} + \Gamma^i{}_{j\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{00} - \Gamma^i{}_{0\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{j0} .
> $$
>
> By [[#^thm-c8-3-1|Theorem §C8.3.1]], part 1: $\Gamma^i{}_{00} = 0$ and $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{00} = 0$ exactly, $\Gamma^i{}_{j0} = \tfrac12\dot h_{ij} + O(h^2)$, and the last product is $O(h^2)$. Hence
>
> $$
> R^i{}_{0j0} = 0 - \partial_0\bigl(\tfrac12\dot h_{ij}\bigr) + 0 - O(h^2) = -\tfrac12\ddot h_{ij} + O(h^2) .
> $$
>
> The antisymmetry in the last pair ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-1|Theorem §C3.10.1]]) gives $R^i{}_{00j} = -R^i{}_{0j0} = +\tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}$, and lowering with $g_{ik} = \delta_{ik} + O(h)$ changes nothing at first order: $R_{i0j0} = -\tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}$. The same result follows from the general linearised Riemann tensor of [[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-2|Theorem §C8.1.2]] with $h_{0\mu} = 0$ (Couzens (2.4)), and it is gauge invariant ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-3|Theorem §C8.1.3]]). Since $h_{i3} = 0$, every component with $i$ or $j$ equal to $3$ vanishes, and $\sum_i R^i{}_{0i0} = -\tfrac12\ddot h_{ii} = 0$. The sign and the $z$-components were checked with the full curvature code, by script.
>
> **Step 2** (the frame is orthonormal). With $e_{\hat\imath}{}^k = \delta_{ik} - \tfrac12 h_{ik}$,
>
> $$
> g(e_{\hat\imath}, e_{\hat\jmath}) = (\delta_{kl} + h_{kl})\bigl(\delta_{ik} - \tfrac12 h_{ik}\bigr)\bigl(\delta_{jl} - \tfrac12 h_{jl}\bigr) = \delta_{ij} + h_{ij} - \tfrac12 h_{ij} - \tfrac12 h_{ji} + O(h^2) = \delta_{ij} + O(h^2) ,
> $$
>
> $g(e_{\hat 0}, e_{\hat\imath}) = g_{0k}e_{\hat\imath}{}^k = 0$ and $g(e_{\hat 0}, e_{\hat 0}) = -1$.
>
> **Step 3** (the frame is parallel). Along the worldline of $A$ the tangent is $T = \partial_t$, and the covariant derivative of a vector field $V$ along it is $DV^k/dt = \dot V^k + \Gamma^k{}_{0l}V^l$ ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative#^def-c3-5-3|Def. §C3.5.3]]). For $V = e_{\hat\imath}$:
>
> $$
> \frac{De_{\hat\imath}{}^k}{dt} = -\tfrac12\dot h_{ik} + \tfrac12\dot h_{kl}\bigl(\delta_{il} - \tfrac12 h_{il}\bigr) = -\tfrac12\dot h_{ik} + \tfrac12\dot h_{ki} + O(h^2) = O(h^2) ,
> $$
>
> and $De_{\hat\imath}{}^0/dt = \Gamma^0{}_{0l}e_{\hat\imath}{}^l = 0$. Also $De_{\hat 0}/dt = \Gamma^\mu{}_{00} = 0$: $e_{\hat 0}$ is the geodesic's own tangent.
>
> **Step 4** (the deviation equation in the frame). By [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]], part 1, with $\tau = t$, $d^2S^{\hat\imath}/dt^2 = -R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat\jmath\hat0}S^{\hat\jmath}$. The frame components of the curvature differ from the coordinate components by terms $h\cdot R = O(h^2)$, so $R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat\jmath\hat0} = -\tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}$ and
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2S^{\hat\imath}}{dt^2} = \tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}\,S^{\hat\jmath} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (solve to first order). Write $S^{\hat\imath} = S^{\hat\imath}_0 + \delta S^{\hat\imath}$ with $S_0$ constant and $\delta S = O(h)$. On the right $S^{\hat\jmath}$ may be replaced by $S^{\hat\jmath}_0$ (the error is $O(h^2)$):
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2\delta S^{\hat\imath}}{dt^2} = \tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}(t)\,S^{\hat\jmath}_0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \delta S^{\hat\imath} = \tfrac12 h_{ij}(t)\,S^{\hat\jmath}_0 + a^i + b^it .
> $$
>
> Before the wave, $h = 0$, $\delta S = 0$ and $d\delta S/dt = 0$, so $a = b = 0$. Hence $S^{\hat\imath}(t) = (\delta_{ij} + \tfrac12 h_{ij}(t))S^{\hat\jmath}_0$ (checked by script for arbitrary $h_+(t)$, $h_\times(t)$).
>
> **Step 6** (agreement with the transverse-traceless components). The masses sit at fixed coordinates, so the deviation vector $S = \partial x/\partial\sigma$ at fixed $\tau = t$ has constant components $S^j$. Its frame components are $S^{\hat\imath} = e^{\hat\imath}{}_kS^k$ with the dual frame $e^{\hat\imath}{}_k = \delta_{ik} + \tfrac12 h_{ik}$ (the inverse of $e_{\hat\imath}{}^k$ to first order: $(\delta_{ik} - \tfrac12 h_{ik})(\delta_{kj} + \tfrac12 h_{kj}) = \delta_{ij} + O(h^2)$). So $S^{\hat\imath} = S^i + \tfrac12 h_{ik}S^k$, Step 5 with $S_0^{\hat\jmath} = S^j$. Its length is $|S| = \bigl(\delta_{ij}S^{\hat\imath}S^{\hat\jmath}\bigr)^{1/2} = |S_0|\bigl(1 + \tfrac12 h_{ij}n^in^j\bigr) + O(h^2)$ with $\vec n = \vec S_0/|S_0|$: Derivation 1, Step 3.
>
> **Step 7** (the covariant derivative in transverse-traceless components). As a check that the equation is covariant, compute $D^2S/dt^2$ from the constant components $S^j$ in the transverse-traceless chart ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^def-c3-12-3|Def. §C3.12.3]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{DS^i}{dt} = 0 + \Gamma^i{}_{0j}S^j = \tfrac12\dot h_{ij}S^j, \qquad \frac{D^2S^i}{dt^2} = \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\tfrac12\dot h_{ij}S^j\bigr) + \Gamma^i{}_{0k}\,\tfrac12\dot h_{kj}S^j = \tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}S^j + O(h^2) ,
> $$
>
> which is $R^i{}_{00j}S^j$, the right-hand side of [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-1|Theorem §C3.12.1]] with $T = \partial_t$. The ordinary second derivative $d^2S^i/dt^2 = 0$ is not: in this chart the connection term carries the whole effect.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The tidal field of a gravitational wave is $\mathcal E_{ij} = -\tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}$, transverse and traceless; it is the only gauge-invariant content of the wave at a point.
> - Two pictures describe the same physics: in transverse-traceless coordinates the masses stand still and the metric between them changes; in the freely falling frame of one mass the metric is flat to first order and the other masses move under a tidal force. The frame components $S^{\hat\imath}$ are the measurable ones (rulers, light); the coordinate components are not. This is the content of [[#^cau-c8-3-1|Caution: "Space stretches"]].

> [!caution] Caution: "Space stretches"
> The phrase is often used, and it invites three mistakes.
> - *Coordinates are not measurements.* In transverse-traceless coordinates nothing moves ([[#^thm-c8-3-1|Theorem §C8.3.1]]); in the freely falling frame of one mass the others move ([[#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]], part 3). Neither statement alone is the physics. What is measured, a proper distance or the return time of light, is the same number in both descriptions, $L_0(1 + \tfrac12 h_{ij}n^in^j)$. Schutz: "The physical effects of gravitational fields always show up in measurables."
> - *A ruler does not stretch with it.* A ruler is not a set of free masses: its atoms are held at their spacing by electric forces. In the freely falling frame the wave acts on each atom as a tidal force $\tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}\xi^j$ per unit mass, and the atoms respond to it together with their binding forces, $\ddot\xi^i = -R^i{}_{0j0}\xi^j + F^i/m$ (Schutz (9.30)–(9.31)). Electric binding is so much stronger that the ruler keeps its length, and it can therefore record the change in the distance to a free mass ([[#^ex-c8-3-2|Example §C8.3.2]]).
> - *The stretched wavelength of the light does not cancel the signal.* The naive objection: if the wave stretches an arm and, with it, the light inside, the number of wavelengths in the arm is unchanged and an interferometer sees nothing. An interferometer does not count wavelengths in the arm. It compares, at the beam splitter, the phases $\omega_L(t - T_x)$ and $\omega_L(t - T_y)$ of light that left the laser at the earlier times $t - T_x$ and $t - T_y$; only the return times $T_x$, $T_y$ along null worldlines enter ([[#^mod-c8-3-4|Model §C8.3.4]], part 4), whatever happens to the wavelength on the way. The wavelength of light in the arm is in any case observer-dependent and has no invariant meaning (Schutz's footnote 2).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.3.4, "The Physics behind the TT frame" (PDF 22–23) · Schutz §9.2, "Measuring the stretching of space", eqs. (9.29)–(9.31) (PDF 225–228); §9.3 (PDF 242) and its footnote 2*

## A ring of test masses

[[#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]] gives the separation of one pair. A ring of pairs, all sharing a central mass, shows at once how the strain depends on direction, and it is the picture by which the two polarisations of [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-2|Def. §C8.2.2]] are recognised.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.3.3: A Ring of Test Particles under + and ×
> Free test masses lie on a circle of radius $r_0$ in the plane $z = 0$ around a central mass, at the angles $\varphi$: $S_0 = r_0(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$ in the central mass's frame before the wave. A monochromatic wave along $z$ ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-3|Theorem §C8.2.3]]) passes. To first order, with $S^{\hat\imath}(t) = (\delta_{ij} + \tfrac12 h_{ij}(t))S_0^j$ ([[#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]], part 3):
> 1. **Plus.** For $h_+(t) = h_+\cos\omega t$, $h_\times = 0$:
>
> $$
> S^{\hat x} = r_0\cos\varphi\,\bigl(1 + \tfrac12 h_+\cos\omega t\bigr), \qquad S^{\hat y} = r_0\sin\varphi\,\bigl(1 - \tfrac12 h_+\cos\omega t\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The ring is an ellipse with semi-axes $r_0(1 \pm \tfrac12 h_+\cos\omega t)$ along $\hat x$ and $\hat y$: stretched along $x$ and squeezed along $y$ for half a period, the reverse for the other half.
> 2. **Cross.** For $h_\times(t) = h_\times\cos\omega t$, $h_+ = 0$:
>
> $$
> S^{\hat x} = r_0\bigl(\cos\varphi + \tfrac12 h_\times\cos\omega t\,\sin\varphi\bigr), \qquad S^{\hat y} = r_0\bigl(\sin\varphi + \tfrac12 h_\times\cos\omega t\,\cos\varphi\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> an ellipse with semi-axes $r_0(1 \pm \tfrac12 h_\times\cos\omega t)$ along the diagonals $(\hat x \pm \hat y)/\sqrt2$.
> 3. **45°, not 90°.** In axes turned by $45°$ about $z$, a pure cross wave of amplitude $h_\times$ is a pure plus wave of amplitude $h_\times$: the two patterns differ by a rotation of $45°$. In general a rotation by $\theta$ turns $(h_+, h_\times)$ by the angle $2\theta$ (helicity $2$, [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-5|Theorem §C8.2.5]], parts 1 and 4); the two polarisations of light differ by $90°$ ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-3|EM Theorem §C1.4.3]]), helicity $1$.
> 4. **How each mass moves.** The displacement of the mass at $\varphi$ is $\tfrac12 r_0h_+\cos\omega t\,(\cos\varphi, -\sin\varphi)$ under plus and $\tfrac12 r_0h_\times\cos\omega t\,(\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)$ under cross: each mass oscillates on a straight segment, which is radial only on the axes of the ellipse. The radial part is $\delta r/r_0 = \tfrac12(h_+\cos2\varphi + h_\times\sin2\varphi)\cos\omega t$, a quadrupole pattern.
> 5. **Area and the third direction.** The area enclosed is $\pi r_0^2$ to first order at all times (the displacement field $\tfrac12 h_{ij}\xi^j$ is divergence-free because $h_{ii} = 0$), and masses displaced along $z$ do not move (transversality).
>
> See the animations below.
>
> *Model:* as [[#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]]; *applies:* first order in $h$, ring small compared with the wavelength if it is not in a plane $z = \text{const}$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.3.4, eqs. (2.68)–(2.71), Figs. 2–3 (PDF 23–24) · Schutz §9.2, eqs. (9.28a)–(9.28b), Fig. 9.1 (PDF 226–228) · Hartle §16.3, eqs. (16.13)–(16.15), Fig. 16.2 (PDF 337–338)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (plus). With $h_{11} = -h_{22} = h_+\cos\omega t \equiv 2\epsilon(t)$, $h_{12} = 0$:
>
> $$
> S^{\hat x} = \bigl(1 + \tfrac12 h_{11}\bigr)S_0^x = r_0\cos\varphi\,(1 + \epsilon), \qquad S^{\hat y} = \bigl(1 + \tfrac12 h_{22}\bigr)S_0^y = r_0\sin\varphi\,(1 - \epsilon) .
> $$
>
> These points satisfy
>
> $$
> \frac{(S^{\hat x})^2}{r_0^2(1 + \epsilon)^2} + \frac{(S^{\hat y})^2}{r_0^2(1 - \epsilon)^2} = \cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi = 1 ,
> $$
>
> an ellipse with semi-axes $r_0(1 + \epsilon)$ and $r_0(1 - \epsilon)$, $\epsilon = \tfrac12 h_+\cos\omega t$; its area is $\pi r_0^2(1 + \epsilon)(1 - \epsilon) = \pi r_0^2(1 - \epsilon^2) = \pi r_0^2 + O(h^2)$.
>
> **Step 2** (cross). With $h_{12} = h_{21} = h_\times\cos\omega t \equiv 2\epsilon$, $h_{11} = h_{22} = 0$, the map is $S = (\mathbb 1 + \epsilon\sigma)S_0$ with $\sigma = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, which gives the formulas of part 2. The eigenvectors of $\sigma$ are $(1, 1)/\sqrt2$ with eigenvalue $+1$ and $(1, -1)/\sqrt2$ with eigenvalue $-1$, so $\mathbb 1 + \epsilon\sigma$ stretches by $1 + \epsilon$ along one diagonal and by $1 - \epsilon$ along the other; it maps the circle to an ellipse with these principal axes (spectral theorem, [[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]), of area $\pi r_0^2|\det(\mathbb 1 + \epsilon\sigma)| = \pi r_0^2(1 - \epsilon^2)$.
>
> **Step 3** (45°). Under a rotation by $\theta$ about $z$ the components transform as $H'_{11} = \cos2\theta\,H_{11} + \sin2\theta\,H_{12}$, $H'_{12} = -\sin2\theta\,H_{11} + \cos2\theta\,H_{12}$ ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-5|Theorem §C8.2.5]]; Couzens (2.53)). For $H_{11} = 0$, $H_{12} = h_\times$, $\theta = 45°$: $H'_{11} = \sin90°\,h_\times = h_\times$, $H'_{12} = \cos90°\,h_\times = 0$. A pure cross wave is a pure plus wave in the turned axes, so its ring pattern is the plus pattern turned by $45°$. The angle is half the $90°$ that maps $h_+ \to -h_+$ (the same pattern half a period later).
>
> **Step 4** (tracks). Subtract $S_0$: under plus, $S - S_0 = \epsilon\,r_0(\cos\varphi, -\sin\varphi)$; under cross, $S - S_0 = \epsilon\,r_0(\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)$. The direction is fixed and the magnitude $\propto\cos\omega t$, so each mass moves back and forth on a segment. The radial components are $\epsilon\,r_0(\cos^2\varphi - \sin^2\varphi) = \epsilon\,r_0\cos2\varphi$ and $\epsilon\,r_0\,2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi = \epsilon\,r_0\sin2\varphi$.
>
> **Step 5** (area, any shape). The displacement field $\xi^i \mapsto \tfrac12 h_{ij}\xi^j$ has divergence $\tfrac12 h_{ii} = 0$ (traceless), so to first order it moves every region of the plane without changing its area ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]], part 4, with $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$). All formulas were checked by script.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Polarisation is visible as a shape: a gravitational wave deforms a circle into an ellipse at constant area, with axes fixed by the polarisation and the deformation oscillating at the wave frequency. The angle of $45°$ between the two patterns is the spin-2 character of the field in pictures.

## The laser interferometer

The strain is about $10^{-21}$. A ruler-and-mass measurement at that level is hopeless; light is not. The idea is radar: send light from one free mass to another and back, and time it with the clock of the first. [[#^def-c8-3-1|Def. §C8.3.1]] already noted that radar distance equals proper distance for short arms; the model below derives this and the rest.

> [!definition] Definition §C8.3.2: Antenna Pattern Functions
> *Structure.* An L-shaped detector has two arms along orthonormal unit vectors $\vec a$, $\vec b$ of $\mathbf R^3$ (its plane has normal $\hat e_z = \vec a\times\vec b$). Its **detector tensor** is the symmetric, traceless $3\times3$ matrix $D^{ij} = \tfrac12(a^ia^j - b^ib^j)$. A plane wave arrives along the direction $\hat N(\vartheta, \varphi)$, with $(\vartheta, \varphi)$ spherical angles about $\hat e_z$ measured from $\vec a$. In the plane of the sky (orthogonal to $\hat N$) take the orthonormal pair $\vec p$, $\vec q$ obtained by turning $(\hat e_\vartheta, \hat e_\varphi)$ through the **polarisation angle** $\psi$, and the polarisation tensors $e^+_{ij} = p_ip_j - q_iq_j$, $e^\times_{ij} = p_iq_j + q_ip_j$, so that the transverse-traceless wave is $h_{ij} = h_+e^+_{ij} + h_\times e^\times_{ij}$ ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-2|Def. §C8.2.2]]; [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-4|Theorem §C8.2.4]] for the transverse projection). The **antenna pattern functions** are
>
> $$
> F_+(\vartheta, \varphi, \psi) = D^{ij}e^+_{ij}, \qquad F_\times(\vartheta, \varphi, \psi) = D^{ij}e^\times_{ij} ,
> $$
>
> and $F_+^2 + F_\times^2$ is the **antenna power pattern**. They depend only on the geometry, not on the wave's amplitudes.
>
> *Source: Schutz §12.3, eqs. (12.6)–(12.10), Figs. 12.3–12.4 (PDF 392–394; his detector tensor is twice this one, [[#^cau-c8-3-3|Caution: Slips in the sources on detection]])*

^def-c8-3-2

> [!model] Model §C8.3.4: The Laser Interferometer
> *Arena.* The plane-wave spacetime of [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-3|Theorem §C8.2.3]] in its transverse-traceless chart, to first order in $h$.
>
> *Instrument.* A Michelson interferometer ([[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^def-a6-4-1|WO Def. §A6.4.1]]) built of three free test masses: the beam splitter at the origin and two end mirrors at the transverse-traceless positions $(L, 0, 0)$ and $(0, L, 0)$. By [[#^thm-c8-3-1|Theorem §C8.3.1]] all three keep these coordinates, and the clock at the beam splitter reads $t$. A laser of angular frequency $\omega_L$ (wavelength $\lambda_L = 2\pi/\omega_L$) feeds the splitter, which sends half the light along each arm; the returning beams recombine at the splitter, and one output, the **dark port**, goes to a photodetector. Light follows null geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]).
>
> *Results* (derived below; first order in $h$, plus wave along $z$ unless stated).
> 1. **Round trip.** Light leaving the splitter at $t_s$ along the $x$-arm returns after
>
> $$
> T_x(t_s) = 2L + \tfrac12\int_0^L h_+(t_s + x)\,dx + \tfrac12\int_0^L h_+(t_s + L + x)\,dx ,
> $$
>
> and $T_y$ is the same with $h_+ \to -h_+$, for any $L$.
> 2. **Rate of the return time.** $\dfrac{d(t_s + T_x)}{dt_s} = 1 + \tfrac12\bigl[h_+(t_s + 2L) - h_+(t_s)\bigr]$: only the wave at emission and at return enters. Crests emitted $dt_s$ apart return $d(t_s + T_x)$ apart, so the returning light is shifted in frequency by $\nu_{\text{return}}/\nu_{\text{start}} = dt_s/d(t_s + T_x)$.
> 3. **Short arms.** If $L$ is small compared with the gravitational wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/\omega$, then $T_x = 2L\bigl(1 + \tfrac12 h_+(t_s)\bigr) + O(L^2\dot h)$: the round trip is twice the proper length of the arm ([[#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]], part 2), and $T_x - T_y = 2L\,h_+(t_s)$.
> 4. **The dark port.** With a lossless splitter, the power at the dark port is
>
> $$
> P_D = P_{\text{in}}\,\sin^2\frac{\Delta\phi}{2}, \qquad \Delta\phi = \omega_L(T_x - T_y) = \frac{4\pi L\,h_+}{\lambda_L}\ \ (\text{short arms}) ,
> $$
>
> and the rest, $P_{\text{in}}\cos^2(\Delta\phi/2)$, returns toward the laser. Without a wave and with equal arms the dark port is dark. At exactly zero static phase the response is quadratic in $h$; with a small static offset $\phi_0$, $P_D = P_{\text{in}}\bigl[\sin^2\frac{\phi_0}{2} + \tfrac12\sin\phi_0\,\delta\phi\bigr] + O(\delta\phi^2)$ is linear in the wave's phase $\delta\phi$.
> 5. **Response to any wave.** For short arms along $\vec a$, $\vec b$ and any transverse-traceless wave,
>
> $$
> h(t) \equiv \frac{T_a - T_b}{2L} = \frac{\delta L_a - \delta L_b}{L} = D^{ij}h_{ij} = F_+h_+(t) + F_\times h_\times(t) ,
> $$
>
> with the antenna pattern functions of [[#^def-c8-3-2|Def. §C8.3.2]]. For a wave along $z$ with arms along $x$ and $y$: $h(t) = h_+(t)$, and a pure cross wave gives no signal.
> 6. **Antenna pattern.** For $\psi = 0$,
>
> $$
> F_+ = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + \cos^2\vartheta\bigr)\cos2\varphi, \qquad F_\times = -\cos\vartheta\,\sin2\varphi ,
> $$
>
> and a polarisation angle $\psi$ turns $(F_+, F_\times)$ through $2\psi$. The power pattern $F_+^2 + F_\times^2$ is $1$ for waves from the zenith, $\tfrac14$ for waves in the detector's plane along an arm, $0$ in the plane along the bisector of the arms, and $\tfrac25$ on average over the sky: one interferometer hears almost the whole sky, with little sense of direction.
>
> - *Assumes:* free (suspended) masses, hung so that they can swing horizontally (Hartle §16.4; d'Inverno & Vickers §21.11); monochromatic laser; a simple Michelson. Real detectors fold the light many times in Fabry–Pérot arm cavities ([[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^def-b10-4-2|WO Def. §B10.4.2]]) and recycle the bright-port light (Schutz Fig. 9.3, PDF 243); both multiply the phase of part 4 without changing parts 1–3, 5–6.
> - *Fails:* when noise (seismic, thermal, photon shot noise, Schutz PDF 242–243) exceeds the signal; part 3 fails for arms comparable with $\lambda$ (space detectors, pulsar timing), where part 1 must be used (d'Inverno & Vickers, PDF 450, 453).
>
> *Model:* [[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^mod-c8-1-1|Model §C8.1.1]] in vacuum, plane wave in transverse-traceless gauge; *applies:* first order in $h$; parts 3, 5, 6 for $L \ll \lambda$, which holds for 4 km arms and the band of [[#^law-c8-3-5|Law §C8.3.5]] ($\lambda \ge 1199$ km).

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the light path). The $x$-axis is $y = z = 0$. Light moving along it has $dy = dz = 0$, and $ds^2 = 0$ in the metric of [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-3|Theorem §C8.2.3]] gives
>
> $$
> 0 = -dt^2 + \bigl(1 + h_+(t)\bigr)dx^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dt}{dx} = \pm\bigl(1 + h_+(t)\bigr)^{1/2} ,
> $$
>
> $+$ outward, $-$ on the way back (with $x$ decreasing). The true null geodesic leaves the axis by $O(h)$, because $\Gamma^z{}_{xx} = \tfrac12\dot h_+ \neq 0$. For a null curve in this metric $dt = (g_{ij}\,dx^idx^j)^{1/2}$ ($g_{00} = -1$, $g_{0i} = 0$), so its time of flight is a length in the sense of [[#^der-c8-3-2|Derivation 1 of Theorem §C8.3.2]], Step 5; an $O(h)$ deformation of the straight path with fixed ends changes it only at $O(h^2)$ (d'Inverno & Vickers PDF 450; Hartle PDF 337). The straight path may be used.
>
> **Step 2** (outward). Integrate from $x = 0$ at $t_s$:
>
> $$
> t(x) = t_s + \int_0^x\bigl(1 + h_+(t(x'))\bigr)^{1/2}dx' = t_s + x + \tfrac12\int_0^x h_+\bigl(t(x')\bigr)\,dx' + O(h^2) .
> $$
>
> Inside $h_+$ the flat value $t(x') = t_s + x' + O(h)$ may be used, the error being $O(h^2)$:
>
> $$
> t(x) = t_s + x + \tfrac12\int_0^x h_+(t_s + x')\,dx', \qquad t_{\text{far}} = t(L) = t_s + L + \tfrac12\int_0^L h_+(t_s + x)\,dx .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (back). Let $l = L - x$ be the distance travelled back from the mirror (fixed at $x = L$, [[#^thm-c8-3-1|Theorem §C8.3.1]]). Then $dt/dl = (1 + h_+(t))^{1/2}$ and, as in Step 2 with $t_{\text{far}} = t_s + L + O(h)$ inside $h_+$,
>
> $$
> t_{\text{return}} = t_{\text{far}} + L + \tfrac12\int_0^L h_+(t_s + L + l)\,dl .
> $$
>
> Substituting $t_{\text{far}}$ gives part 1 for $T_x = t_{\text{return}} - t_s$, which is a proper time of the splitter's clock ($\tau = t$). For the $y$-arm $g_{yy} = 1 - h_+$: replace $h_+ \to -h_+$. The outward and return solutions were checked against the null condition symbolically, and against a numerical integration of $dt/dx = (1 + h_+(t))^{1/2}$ (differences of order $h^2$), by script.
>
> **Step 4** (rate). $t_s$ enters the integrals only through the argument of $h_+$, so by the fundamental theorem of calculus
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt_s}\int_0^L h_+(t_s + x)\,dx = \int_0^L h_+'(t_s + x)\,dx = h_+(t_s + L) - h_+(t_s), \qquad \frac{d}{dt_s}\int_0^L h_+(t_s + L + x)\,dx = h_+(t_s + 2L) - h_+(t_s + L) .
> $$
>
> Adding, with the factor $\tfrac12$, and adding $d(t_s + 2L)/dt_s = 1$: $d(t_s + T_x)/dt_s = 1 + \tfrac12[h_+(t_s + 2L) - h_+(t_s)]$; the value at $t_s + L$ cancels. A crest emitted at $t_s$ and the next at $t_s + dt_s$ return at times $d(t_s + T_x)$ apart; frequency is the inverse of the time between crests, which gives the ratio in part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (short arms). Taylor-expand $h_+(t_s + x) = h_+(t_s) + x\,\dot h_+(t_s) + \dots$ in both integrals, $0 \le x \le 2L$:
>
> $$
> T_x = 2L + \tfrac12\,L\,h_+(t_s) + \tfrac12\,L\,h_+(t_s) + O(L^2\dot h_+) = 2L\bigl(1 + \tfrac12 h_+(t_s)\bigr) + O(L^2\dot h_+) .
> $$
>
> The correction is smaller by the factor $L\omega$ ($\dot h_+ \sim \omega h_+$). With $h_+ \to -h_+$ for $T_y$: $T_x - T_y = 2Lh_+(t_s)$. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 6** (interference). Let the field entering the splitter be $E_0e^{-i\omega_Lt}$, and let the splitter transmit with amplitude $\mathsf t$ and reflect with $\mathsf r$ from the laser's side and $\mathsf r'$ from the other side, $|\mathsf t| = |\mathsf r| = |\mathsf r'| = 1/\sqrt2$. Light reaching the dark port at time $t$ left the splitter at $t - T_x$ along the $x$-arm (transmitted out, reflected by $\mathsf r'$ on return) or at $t - T_y$ along the $y$-arm (reflected out, transmitted on return):
>
> $$
> E_D(t) = E_0\bigl[\mathsf t\,\mathsf r'\,e^{-i\omega_L(t - T_x)} + \mathsf r\,\mathsf t\,e^{-i\omega_L(t - T_y)}\bigr] .
> $$
>
> For a lossless splitter $\mathsf r' = -\mathsf r$: the two outputs must carry complementary interference terms, as energy conservation requires ([[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^rem-b10-1-5|WO §B10.1, Remark: The Mach–Zehnder interferometer, and where the light goes at a dark fringe]]); Couzens: the phase "is inverted". Then, with $|\mathsf t\,\mathsf r|^2 = \tfrac14$,
>
> $$
> |E_D|^2 = \tfrac14|E_0|^2\,\bigl|e^{i\omega_LT_y} - e^{i\omega_LT_x}\bigr|^2 = \tfrac14|E_0|^2\bigl(2 - 2\cos\Delta\phi\bigr) = |E_0|^2\sin^2\frac{\Delta\phi}{2} ,
> $$
>
> $\Delta\phi = \omega_L(T_x - T_y)$, the two-beam formula of [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^thm-b10-1-1|WO Theorem §B10.1.1]] with a relative phase $\pi$. The other output, toward the laser, has $\mathsf t\,\mathsf t\,e^{i\omega_LT_x} + \mathsf r\,\mathsf r\,e^{i\omega_LT_y}$ up to the common factor and power $|E_0|^2\cos^2(\Delta\phi/2)$; the two add to $|E_0|^2$. With part 3, $\Delta\phi = \omega_L\cdot 2Lh_+ = 4\pi Lh_+/\lambda_L$: each arm is crossed twice, as in the fringe count $\Delta d = m\lambda_0/2$ of [[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^thm-a6-4-1|WO Theorem §A6.4.1]].
>
> **Step 7** (offset). With a static phase $\phi_0$ plus the wave's $\delta\phi$, $\sin^2\frac{\phi_0 + \delta\phi}{2} = \sin^2\frac{\phi_0}{2} + 2\sin\frac{\phi_0}{2}\cos\frac{\phi_0}{2}\cdot\frac{\delta\phi}{2} + O(\delta\phi^2) = \sin^2\frac{\phi_0}{2} + \tfrac12\sin\phi_0\,\delta\phi + O(\delta\phi^2)$. At $\phi_0 = 0$ the linear term vanishes. This is part 4.
>
> **Step 8** (any wave, short arms). An arm of length $L$ along a unit vector $\vec a$ has proper length $L(1 + \tfrac12 h_{ij}a^ia^j)$ ([[#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]], part 2, $L \ll \lambda$), and by Step 5 its round trip is twice that. For any wave in transverse-traceless form, also one not along $z$, this holds with $h_{ij}$ at the detector ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-4|Theorem §C8.2.4]]: $h^{\text{TT}} = \Lambda h$). Hence
>
> $$
> \frac{T_a - T_b}{2L} = \tfrac12\bigl(h_{ij}a^ia^j - h_{ij}b^ib^j\bigr) = D^{ij}h_{ij} = h_+D^{ij}e^+_{ij} + h_\times D^{ij}e^\times_{ij} = F_+h_+ + F_\times h_\times .
> $$
>
> For the wave along $z$: $D^{ij}h_{ij} = \tfrac12(h_{11} - h_{22}) = \tfrac12(h_+ + h_+) = h_+$, while $h_{12}$ never enters $D^{ij}h_{ij}$ ($D^{12} = 0$). This is part 5.
>
> **Step 9** (antenna pattern). With $\vec a = \hat x$, $\vec b = \hat y$, $\hat N = (\sin\vartheta\cos\varphi, \sin\vartheta\sin\varphi, \cos\vartheta)$, $\psi = 0$: $\vec p = \hat e_\vartheta = (\cos\vartheta\cos\varphi, \cos\vartheta\sin\varphi, -\sin\vartheta)$, $\vec q = \hat e_\varphi = (-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi, 0)$. Then $D^{ij}p_ip_j = \tfrac12(p_x^2 - p_y^2)$ and so on:
>
> $$
> F_+ = \tfrac12\bigl[(p_x^2 - p_y^2) - (q_x^2 - q_y^2)\bigr] = \tfrac12\bigl[\cos^2\vartheta(\cos^2\varphi - \sin^2\varphi) - (\sin^2\varphi - \cos^2\varphi)\bigr] = \tfrac12(1 + \cos^2\vartheta)\cos2\varphi ,
> $$
>
> $$
> F_\times = \tfrac12\bigl[2p_xq_x - 2p_yq_y\bigr] = -\cos\vartheta\cos\varphi\sin\varphi - \cos\vartheta\sin\varphi\cos\varphi = -\cos\vartheta\sin2\varphi .
> $$
>
> Turning $(\vec p, \vec q)$ by $\psi$ turns $(e^+, e^\times)$ by $2\psi$ (as in [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-5|Theorem §C8.2.5]]), hence $(F_+, F_\times)$. Values: zenith $\vartheta = 0$, $\varphi = 0$: $F_+ = 1$, $F_\times = 0$. In the plane along an arm, $\vartheta = \tfrac\pi2$, $\varphi = 0$: $F_+ = \tfrac12$, $F_\times = 0$. Along the bisector, $\varphi = \tfrac\pi4$: both zero. The sky average is
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{4\pi}\int\bigl(F_+^2 + F_\times^2\bigr)d\Omega = \tfrac14\cdot\tfrac{28}{15}\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac{7}{30} + \tfrac{5}{30} = \tfrac25 ,
> $$
>
> using $\langle(1 + \cos^2\vartheta)^2\rangle = 1 + \tfrac23 + \tfrac15 = \tfrac{28}{15}$, $\langle\cos^2\vartheta\rangle = \tfrac13$, $\langle\cos^22\varphi\rangle = \langle\sin^22\varphi\rangle = \tfrac12$ (all checked by script). This is part 6.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Light measures the wave through times of flight along null worldlines, read on the clock of a free mass; no wavelength or ruler enters.
> - For arms short compared with the wavelength, the interferometer reads $D^{ij}h_{ij}$, the strain of [[#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]] projected on its two arms; the doubling in the round trip and the factor $\tfrac12$ in the strain give $\Delta\phi = 4\pi Lh/\lambda_L$.

In (21.162) and (21.166) the $x$-arm shortens when $h_{22} > 0$, while (21.153) and (21.155) shorten the $y$-direction: the sign of $\Delta L$ in (21.163) depends on which is meant; its magnitude $\mathcal AL$ is right. ## The first detection

> [!law] Law §C8.3.5: Direct Detection: GW150914
> On **14 September 2015** the two Advanced LIGO interferometers, at Hanford (Washington) and Livingston (Louisiana), each with 4 km arms, recorded the same signal, GW150914, during an engineering run before the first observing run (announced 11 February 2016):
> 1. **The waveform.** A "chirp": an oscillation whose frequency rose from 35 Hz to 250 Hz in about 0.2 s, reaching a peak strain of about $10^{-21}$ (d'Inverno & Vickers PDF 456, Fig. 21.12; Schutz PDF 249, 396).
> 2. **Two sites.** The signals at the two sites agreed in shape and arrived 7 ms apart, consistent with the light travel time between the sites (d'Inverno & Vickers PDF 456): the wave moves at the speed of light within this resolution. The statistical significance was at least $5.1\sigma$.
> 3. **The source, from matching waveforms.** Two black holes of $36$ and $29\,M_\odot$ merged into one of $62\,M_\odot$ at a luminosity distance of 410 Mpc, signal-to-noise ratio 24 (Schutz Table 12.1). d'Inverno & Vickers give about $35$ and $30\,M_\odot$, a final spin parameter $a \approx 0.68$, a distance of $1.4 \pm 0.6$ billion light years ($410$ Mpc $= 1.34$ Gly), about $3\,M_\odot$ radiated as gravitational waves ($3\,M_\odot c^2 = 5.4\times10^{47}$ J), a peak power of about $3.6\times10^{49}$ W, and an orbital frequency of 75 Hz (half the wave frequency) at a separation of 350 km at merger. Couzens rounds the masses to $30 + 35 \to 62\,M_\odot$ and the distance to about 1.3 billion light years.
>
> What the detectors measured is the strain of [[#^mod-c8-3-4|Model §C8.3.4]], part 5: $h(t) = F_+h_+ + F_\times h_\times$, a relative arm-length difference of about $10^{-21}$ ([[#^ex-c8-3-1|Example §C8.3.1]]). The arm (4 km) is far shorter than the wavelength ($c/250\ \mathrm{Hz} = 1199$ km), so the short-arm response applies. The masses, the distance and the radiated energy are inferences from the inspiral and merger waveform (§C8.6); a Newtonian check of the last figures: Kepler's third law ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-4|CM Theorem §B2.2.4]]) for $65\,M_\odot$ at an orbital frequency of 75 Hz gives a separation of 339 km, close to the quoted 350 km.
>
> *Valid when:* as reported in Schutz (third edition, text of mid-2021; Table 12.1 lists best-fit values without uncertainties) and d'Inverno & Vickers (2022 edition); the parameter values are those of the collaboration's analyses quoted there.
>
> *Model:* the detector model [[#^mod-c8-3-4|Model §C8.3.4]] for the measurement; the source parameters rest on general-relativistic waveforms of binary black holes ([[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral#^law-c8-6-7|Law §C8.6.7]] for the inspiral; numerical relativity for the merger); *applies:* an observation.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers §21.14, pp. 441–443 (PDF 456–458) · Schutz §9.3 (PDF 242); §9.4 (PDF 249); §12.3 (PDF 396); §12.4, Table 12.1 (PDF 414–416; announcement date PDF 415) · Couzens, GR2, §2.3 and footnote 3 (PDF 15–16) · conversions checked by script*

The wave did work on the detector's masses, and the source lost $3\,M_\odot$ of rest energy to waves that moved them. A wave that moves masses carries energy. But by the equivalence principle no gravitational field has an energy density at a point: in normal coordinates ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]]) every first derivative of the metric vanishes there. In what sense, then, does a gravitational wave carry energy, and how much? [[§C8.4 The Energy of Gravitational Waves|§C8.4]] answers this.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C8.3.1: The Size of the Effect: a 4 km Arm and h = 10⁻²¹
> *Question.* A plus wave of amplitude $h_+ = 10^{-21}$ arrives along the normal of a detector with 4 km arms. By how much do the arms change, what phase does it produce, and is the short-arm formula valid?
>
> **Step 1** (one arm). By [[#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]], part 2 (Hartle Ex. 16.1, (16.10)), the $x$-arm changes by
>
> $$
> \delta L_x = \tfrac12 h_+L = \tfrac12\times10^{-21}\times4\times10^{3}\ \mathrm m = 2.0\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm m ,
> $$
>
> and the $y$-arm by $-2.0\times10^{-18}$ m at the same moment.
>
> **Step 2** (the difference). $\Delta L = \delta L_x - \delta L_y = h_+L = 4.0\times10^{-18}$ m. The proton's charge radius is $0.84$ fm (CODATA 2018), so $\Delta L$ is $4.8\times10^{-3}$ of the proton radius, $2.4\times10^{-3}$ of its diameter, and one arm's change $\delta L_x$ is $2.4\times10^{-3}$ of the radius. Couzens' "as little as 1/1000th of the width of a proton" is this order.
>
> **Step 3** (times and phase). By [[#^mod-c8-3-4|Model §C8.3.4]], parts 3–4, restored ($T \to T/c$),
>
> $$
> T_x - T_y = \frac{2\Delta L}{c} = \frac{8.0\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm m}{2.998\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}} = 2.67\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm s, \qquad \Delta\phi = \frac{4\pi\Delta L}{\lambda_L} = \frac{4\pi\times4.0\times10^{-18}}{1.0\times10^{-6}} = 5.0\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{rad}
> $$
>
> for an infrared laser of wavelength $1.0\ \mu$m (d'Inverno & Vickers PDF 449), a fraction $2\Delta L/\lambda_L = 8\times10^{-12}$ of a fringe. A simple Michelson cannot resolve this; folding the light in Fabry–Pérot cavities multiplies the phase by the number of round trips ([[#^mod-c8-3-4|Model §C8.3.4]], *Assumes*).
>
> **Step 4** (short arms?). The round trip takes $2L/c = 26.7\ \mu$s. A wave at 100 Hz has wavelength $c/f = 2998$ km and period 10 ms, so $L/\lambda = 1.3\times10^{-3}$ and $(2L/c)f = 2.7\times10^{-3}$: the wave hardly changes during one round trip, and the short-arm formula holds. At 250 Hz, the top of the band of [[#^law-c8-3-5|Law §C8.3.5]], $\lambda = 1199$ km and $(2L/c)f = 6.7\times10^{-3}$.

> [!example] Example §C8.3.2: Does a Bowl of Soup Feel the Wave?
> *Question* (Schutz ex. 9.8–9.9). A bowl of soup, much smaller than the wavelength, falls freely (say in an orbiting spacecraft) while a wave passes. Does the soup slosh, and can an observer at the bowl's centre see anything?
>
> **Step 1** (what free fall removes). In the freely falling frame of the bowl's centre a uniform acceleration is absent: neither the gravity that keeps the spacecraft in orbit nor the wave makes the soup climb one side of the bowl, as an accelerating train would (Schutz §1.1). What remains is the tide ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]], part 1).
>
> **Step 2** (the tidal acceleration). A drop of soup at displacement $\xi$ from the centre, in the plane transverse to the wave, has relative acceleration ([[#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]], part 3)
>
> $$
> a^i = -R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat\jmath\hat0}\,\xi^j = \tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}\,\xi^j = -\tfrac12\omega^2h_{ij}\,\xi^j \qquad (h_{ij} \propto \cos\omega t) .
> $$
>
> It vanishes at the centre, grows linearly with $\xi$, points along $\xi$ in one transverse direction and against it in the other, and has zero divergence ($\partial_ia^i = -\tfrac12\omega^2h_{ii} = 0$). The soup is not compressed; it is driven in the quadrupole pattern of the ring ([[#^thm-c8-3-3|Theorem §C8.3.3]]). So yes, it feels the wave, as a tidal force and not as a push.
>
> **Step 3** (how much). For $h = 10^{-21}$ at $f = 100$ Hz ($\omega = 628\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$) and $\xi = 0.1$ m, restored,
>
> $$
> |a| = \tfrac12\omega^2h\,\xi = \tfrac12\times(628.3\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})^2\times10^{-21}\times0.1\ \mathrm m = 2.0\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} .
> $$
>
> If the drops were free, they would move by $\tfrac12 h\xi = 5\times10^{-23}$ m. The soup is not free: pressure and surface tension act too, $\ddot\xi^i = -R^i{}_{0j0}\xi^j + F^i/m$ (Schutz (9.30)), and a liquid responds to a 100 Hz drive only in proportion to how close the frequency is to one of its sloshing modes, as a driven oscillator (d'Inverno & Vickers (21.159)–(21.161)). The sloshing is real and immeasurably small.
>
> **Step 4** (seeing it). The observer at the centre can send light to a drop at distance $\xi$ and time its return. By [[#^mod-c8-3-4|Model §C8.3.4]], part 3, the round trip is $2\xi(1 + \tfrac12 h_{ij}n^in^j)$ on the observer's clock: monitoring it, the observer sees the drop's proper distance oscillate (Schutz ex. 9.9). This is the interferometer of [[#^mod-c8-3-4|Model §C8.3.4]] in miniature.
>
> *Source: Schutz ex. 9.8–9.9 (PDF 274; worked here); §1.1 (the bowl of soup in a train and in orbit, PDF 20); eqs. (9.29)–(9.31) (PDF 227) · d'Inverno & Vickers §21.11, eqs. (21.158)–(21.161) (PDF 447–448) · numbers checked by script*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - **Tides, then and now.** The ring of [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^ex-c3-12-2|Example §C3.12.2]] (and of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-7|Theorem §C1.3.7]]) is deformed by a static mass: stretched along the line to the mass, squeezed across it, $\mathcal E = \operatorname{diag}(-2, 1, 1)M/r^3$, with a component along every axis. The wave's tide $\mathcal E_{ij} = -\tfrac12\ddot h_{ij}$ is transverse, traceless and oscillating. Both are the tidal tensor of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-3|Def. §C1.3.3]] and [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^def-c3-12-4|Def. §C3.12.4]]; the level-A tidal force is [[§A8.4 Tidal Forces#^def-a8-4-1|CM Def. §A8.4.1]].
> - **Coordinates of free fall.** The parallel frame of [[#^thm-c8-3-2|Theorem §C8.3.2]], part 3, is the frame of a freely falling observer ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]]); along one worldline it extends the normal coordinates of [[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates|§C3.8]], in which the metric is flat at the point and the wave appears as a tidal force.
> - **Gauge.** That free masses sit still in transverse-traceless coordinates is a statement about a gauge choice ([[§C5.4 Diffeomorphism Invariance#^pr-c5-4-4|Principle §C5.4.4]]); the measured strain and the curvature are gauge invariant ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-3|Theorem §C8.1.3]]).
> - **Optics.** The Michelson interferometer and its fringe count are [[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer|WO §A6.4]]; two-beam interference [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer|WO §B10.1]]; the arm cavities are Fabry–Pérot interferometers, [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer|WO §B10.4]]. The Michelson–Morley experiment used the same instrument to look for a change of the speed of light with direction ([[§A1.2 The Michelson–Morley Experiment|REL §A1.2]]).
> - **Polarisation.** Light has two polarisations at $90°$ ([[§C1.4 Gauge Fixing and the Two Polarizations#^thm-c1-4-3|EM Theorem §C1.4.3]]); gravitational waves have two at $45°$ ([[#^thm-c8-3-3|Theorem §C8.3.3]]), the helicity $\pm2$ of [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-5|Theorem §C8.2.5]].
> - **Forward.** The energy carried by the wave: [[§C8.4 The Energy of Gravitational Waves|§C8.4]]. Where waves of $10^{-21}$ come from, and the inspiral of GW150914's black holes: [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula|§C8.5]], [[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral|§C8.6]].
