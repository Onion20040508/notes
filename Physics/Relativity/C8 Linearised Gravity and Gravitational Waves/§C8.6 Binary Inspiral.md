The quadrupole formula of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula|§C8.5]] turns any slowly moving source into a wave field ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-5|Theorem §C8.5.5]]) and a radiated power ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-7|Theorem §C8.5.7]]). The source nature actually provides, and the only one observed so far, is a pair of compact stars or black holes in orbit about each other. This section asks two questions about it: what wave does a binary emit, and what does the emission do to the binary?

The second question has no answer within Newton's theory. There a bound two-body orbit conserves its energy $E$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-2|CM Theorem §B2.1.2]]), so its semi-major axis $a = \mu m/(2\lvert E\rvert)$ ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-3|CM Theorem §B2.2.3]]) and its period ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-4|CM Theorem §B2.2.4]]) never change: $\dot P_b = 0$. Apply the quadrupole luminosity to the Hulse–Taylor binary pulsar, treated as a circle (computed in [[#^thm-c8-6-3|Theorem §C8.6.3]] and [[#^ex-c8-6-3|Example §C8.6.3]]): it radiates $L = 1.75\times10^{-29}$ in geometric units, $6.3\times10^{23}\ \mathrm W$, from an orbit whose binding energy is $1.33\times10^{41}\ \mathrm J$. A fixed orbit radiating at this rate would emit its whole binding energy in $\lvert E\rvert/L = 6.7\times10^{9}$ years and keep radiating after that. The waves carry energy away through every large sphere ([[§C8.4 The Energy of Gravitational Waves#^thm-c8-4-4|Theorem §C8.4.4]]), so the orbit cannot stay fixed: $E$ must fall, the stars must approach, and $P_b$ must shrink. The same failure, with Larmor radiation in place of gravitational radiation, is the collapse of the classical atom ([[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^ex-b11-4-2|EM Example §B11.4.2]]).

The section builds the answer in order: the Newtonian circular binary as the source ([[#^mod-c8-6-1|Model §C8.6.1]]); its wave, at twice the orbital frequency ([[#^thm-c8-6-2|Theorem §C8.6.2]]); its power ([[#^thm-c8-6-3|Theorem §C8.6.3]]); the one combination of the masses that the radiation depends on ([[#^def-c8-6-1|Def. §C8.6.1]]); the slow inspiral that energy balance forces, with the frequency and amplitude rising together, the chirp ([[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]], [[#^thm-c8-6-5|Theorem §C8.6.5]]); and the two observations that confirm it, the decay of the Hulse–Taylor orbit and the detected inspirals ([[#^law-c8-6-6|Law §C8.6.6]], [[#^law-c8-6-7|Law §C8.6.7]]).

**Setup.** Two bodies of masses $m_1, m_2$ in a bound orbit, far from all other matter, observed from a distance $r$ much larger than the orbit and than the wavelength. The bodies are point masses (no tides, no spin), moving under Newtonian gravity on a circle of radius $R$ with speed $v \ll 1$; the field they produce is weak and the space between source and observer is flat. The wave field is the quadrupole field of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^mod-c8-5-3|Model §C8.5.3]], computed from the Newtonian energy density, which for a self-gravitating source rests on the argument of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^cau-c8-5-2|§C8.5, Caution: Self-gravitating sources]]. Radiation reaction enters only through the energy balance between the orbit and the waves, and only while the orbit changes slowly; the condition is stated and checked in [[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]]. The theorems are exact statements within this model; [[#^law-c8-6-6|Law §C8.6.6]] and [[#^law-c8-6-7|Law §C8.6.7]] are observations, quoted with their dates from the typed sources. All numbers were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; $G = c = 1$, so masses are lengths or times ($M_\odot = 1476.67\ \mathrm m = 4.9256\ \mu\mathrm s$; restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]); $m = m_1 + m_2$, $\mu = m_1m_2/m$, $\eta = \mu/m \le \frac14$; $R$ the separation, $\omega$ the orbital angular frequency, $\omega_{\text{gw}} = 2\omega$ and $f = \omega_{\text{gw}}/2\pi = \omega/\pi$ those of the wave; $\iota$ the inclination; $t_r = t - r$ the retarded time; $\tau = t_c - t$ the time left to coalescence.

## The source and its wave

> [!model] Model §C8.6.1: A Newtonian Circular Binary
> *Arena.* Euclidean space $\mathbf R^3$ with Cartesian coordinates centred on the centre of mass, and Newtonian time $t$, embedded in Minkowski spacetime as the near zone of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^mod-c8-5-3|Model §C8.5.3]].
>
> *Source.* Two point masses $m_1, m_2$ at $\vec y_1 = -\frac{m_2}{m}\vec x(t)$, $\vec y_2 = +\frac{m_1}{m}\vec x(t)$, where the relative position $\vec x = \vec y_2 - \vec y_1$ obeys the one-body equation $\mu\ddot{\vec x} = -\mu m\,\vec x/\lvert\vec x\rvert^3$ of the reduced mass $\mu$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-2|CM Def. §B2.1.2]], [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-2|CM Theorem §B2.1.2]]). The orbit is the circle of radius $R$ in the $xy$-plane, traversed counter-clockwise (orbital angular momentum along $+z$):
>
> $$
> \vec x(t) = \bigl(-R\sin\omega t,\ R\cos\omega t,\ 0\bigr), \qquad \omega^2 = \frac{m}{R^3} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \omega^2 = \frac{Gm}{R^3}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> Kepler's third law for two bodies with $a = R$ ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-4|CM Theorem §B2.2.4]]). The speed of each body relative to the other is $v = \omega R = (m\omega)^{1/3}$, and $v^2 = m/R$ is the compactness of the orbit ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^def-c1-1-1|Def. §C1.1.1]]). The energy density is $T^{00} = m_1\delta^3(\vec y - \vec y_1) + m_2\delta^3(\vec y - \vec y_2)$, and the orbital energy is $E = -\mu m/(2R)$ ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-3|CM Theorem §B2.2.3]] with $\alpha = m_1m_2 = \mu m$).
>
> - *Assumes:* $v^2 = m/R \ll 1$ (Newtonian orbit, [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-5|Theorem §C1.1.5]]); point masses; a circular orbit (eccentric orbits: [[#^rem-c8-6-1|★ Remark: Eccentric orbits]]); the back-reaction of the waves neglected over one orbit.
> - *Fails:* when $R$ approaches a few $m$: there the orbit is not Newtonian, and below $R = 6m$ a test body has no stable circular orbit at all ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]], part 4, for a test body about the total mass). For neutron stars also when $R$ approaches twice their radius (about $10\ \mathrm{km}$, Couzens).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5.1, eqs. (2.155)–(2.159), pp. 41–42 (PDF 42–43) · Schutz §9.6, p. 247 (PDF 265) (unequal masses)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.6.2: The Waveform of a Circular Binary
> In [[#^mod-c8-6-1|Model §C8.6.1]], let the observer be at distance $r$ in the direction $\vec n = (\sin\iota\cos\varphi, \sin\iota\sin\varphi, \cos\iota)$, so that $\iota$ is the angle between the orbital angular momentum and the line of sight. Take the polarisations ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-2|Def. §C8.2.2]]) with respect to the transverse axes $\vec e_\theta = (\cos\iota\cos\varphi, \cos\iota\sin\varphi, -\sin\iota)$ and $\vec e_\varphi = (-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi, 0)$, which with $\vec n$ form a right-handed triad, as $x, y, z$ do for a wave along $z$. Then
>
> $$
> h_+ = \frac{4\mu\omega^2R^2}{r}\,\frac{1 + \cos^2\iota}{2}\,\cos\bigl(2\omega t_r - 2\varphi\bigr), \qquad h_\times = \frac{4\mu\omega^2R^2}{r}\,\cos\iota\,\sin\bigl(2\omega t_r - 2\varphi\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 1. *(⚑ Twice the orbital frequency.)* The wave has the single frequency $\omega_{\text{gw}} = 2\omega$: the mass distribution returns to itself after half an orbit.
> 2. *(Polarisation by direction.)* Face-on ($\iota = 0$) the two polarisations have equal amplitude and are a quarter period apart: circular polarisation. Edge-on ($\iota = \pi/2$) $h_\times = 0$: linear polarisation, along the orbital plane.
> 3. *(Azimuth = time.)* $\varphi$ enters only through $2\omega t_r - 2\varphi$: turning the observer about the orbital axis by $\Delta\varphi$ is the same as waiting $\Delta t = \Delta\varphi/\omega$.
> 4. *(Size.)* By Kepler, $4\mu\omega^2R^2/r = 4\mu m/(Rr)$; for two bodies of mass $M$ at separation $R$, $\lvert h\rvert \sim M^2/(Rr)$.
>
> Restored: amplitude $4G\mu\omega^2R^2/(c^4r)$.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c8-6-1|Model §C8.6.1]] with the quadrupole field of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-5|Theorem §C8.5.5]]; *applies:* far zone, $r \gg \lambda = \pi/\omega$; the overall sign depends on the origin of time and the orientation of the transverse axes (Couzens' (2.164) has $+2\phi$ with his azimuth).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5.1, eqs. (2.160)–(2.165), pp. 42–43 (PDF 43–44) · Schutz §9.4, eqs. (9.98)–(9.100), p. 236 (PDF 254); §9.6, eqs. (9.149)–(9.152), pp. 247–248 (PDF 265–266)*

^thm-c8-6-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (second mass moment). By [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-1|Def. §C8.5.1]], $M^{ij} = \int y^iy^jT^{00}\,d^3y$. The delta functions of [[#^mod-c8-6-1|Model §C8.6.1]] give
>
> $$
> M^{ij} = m_1y_1^iy_1^j + m_2y_2^iy_2^j = \Bigl(m_1\frac{m_2^2}{m^2} + m_2\frac{m_1^2}{m^2}\Bigr)x^ix^j = \frac{m_1m_2(m_2 + m_1)}{m^2}\,x^ix^j = \mu\,x^ix^j :
> $$
>
> the binary has the second moment of a single mass $\mu$ on the relative orbit (Couzens writes $T^{00} = \mu\,\delta^3(\vec y - \vec x)$ at once).
>
> **Step 2** (components). With $x^1 = -R\sin\omega t$, $x^2 = R\cos\omega t$, $x^3 = 0$ and $\sin^2 = \frac12(1 - \cos2\omega t)$, $\cos^2 = \frac12(1 + \cos2\omega t)$, $\sin\cos = \frac12\sin2\omega t$:
>
> $$
> M^{11} = \frac{\mu R^2}{2}(1 - \cos2\omega t), \qquad M^{22} = \frac{\mu R^2}{2}(1 + \cos2\omega t), \qquad M^{12} = -\frac{\mu R^2}{2}\sin2\omega t ,
> $$
>
> all others zero. Differentiating twice, with $\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\cos2\omega t = -4\omega^2\cos2\omega t$ and likewise for $\sin$,
>
> $$
> \ddot M^{11} = -\ddot M^{22} = B\cos2\omega t, \qquad \ddot M^{12} = B\sin2\omega t, \qquad B \equiv 2\mu R^2\omega^2 .
> $$
>
> Only $\cos2\omega t$ and $\sin2\omega t$ occur: part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (projection). By [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-5|Theorem §C8.5.5]], $h^{TT}_{ij} = \frac2r\Lambda_{ij,kl}(\vec n)\ddot M^{kl}(t_r)$; the trace part of $M$ is removed by $\Lambda$, so $M$ may stand for the reduced quadrupole $Q$ ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-2|Def. §C8.5.2]]). $\Lambda$ keeps the part of a symmetric tensor transverse to $\vec n$ and removes its trace ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-3|Def. §C8.2.3]], [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-4|Theorem §C8.2.4]]). In the transverse basis $(\vec e_\theta, \vec e_\varphi)$, with $\ddot M_{ab} \equiv e_a^i e_b^j\ddot M_{ij}$,
>
> $$
> h^{TT}_{ab} = \frac2r\Bigl(\ddot M_{ab} - \frac12\delta_{ab}\bigl(\ddot M_{\theta\theta} + \ddot M_{\varphi\varphi}\bigr)\Bigr), \qquad h_+ = \frac12\bigl(h_{\theta\theta} - h_{\varphi\varphi}\bigr) = \frac1r\bigl(\ddot M_{\theta\theta} - \ddot M_{\varphi\varphi}\bigr), \qquad h_\times = h_{\theta\varphi} = \frac2r\ddot M_{\theta\varphi} ,
> $$
>
> the form of [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-2|Def. §C8.2.2]] with $(x, y, z) \to (\vec e_\theta, \vec e_\varphi, \vec n)$.
>
> **Step 4** (the three contractions). Only $\ddot M_{11}, \ddot M_{22}, \ddot M_{12}$ are nonzero, and $e_\theta^3$ does not enter. Using $\cos^2\varphi - \sin^2\varphi = \cos2\varphi$, $2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi = \sin2\varphi$ and $\cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B = \cos(A - B)$:
>
> $$
> \ddot M_{\theta\theta} = \cos^2\iota\bigl(\cos^2\varphi\,\ddot M_{11} + \sin^2\varphi\,\ddot M_{22} + 2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,\ddot M_{12}\bigr) = B\cos^2\iota\bigl(\cos2\varphi\cos2\omega t + \sin2\varphi\sin2\omega t\bigr) = B\cos^2\iota\,\cos(2\omega t - 2\varphi) ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \ddot M_{\varphi\varphi} = \sin^2\varphi\,\ddot M_{11} + \cos^2\varphi\,\ddot M_{22} - 2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,\ddot M_{12} = -B\cos(2\omega t - 2\varphi) ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \ddot M_{\theta\varphi} = \cos\iota\bigl(-\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,\ddot M_{11} + \sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,\ddot M_{22} + \cos2\varphi\,\ddot M_{12}\bigr) = B\cos\iota\bigl(-\sin2\varphi\cos2\omega t + \cos2\varphi\sin2\omega t\bigr) = B\cos\iota\,\sin(2\omega t - 2\varphi) .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (polarisations). Insert into Step 3 at the retarded time, $B = 2\mu R^2\omega^2$:
>
> $$
> h_+ = \frac Br\bigl(\cos^2\iota + 1\bigr)\cos(2\omega t_r - 2\varphi) = \frac{4\mu\omega^2R^2}{r}\,\frac{1 + \cos^2\iota}{2}\cos(2\omega t_r - 2\varphi), \qquad h_\times = \frac{2B}{r}\cos\iota\,\sin(2\omega t_r - 2\varphi) .
> $$
>
> At $\iota = 0$ both amplitudes are $4\mu\omega^2R^2/r$ and $\cos$, $\sin$ are a quarter period apart; at $\iota = \pi/2$, $\cos\iota = 0$: part 2. Part 3 is read off the argument. Part 4: $\omega^2R^2 = m/R$ by Kepler, and with $\mu \sim m \sim M$ the amplitude is of order $M^2/(Rr)$. (The projection and the final forms were checked numerically at random points.)
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The factor 2 in the frequency is geometric: the second moment $x^ix^j$ is even under $\vec x \to -\vec x$, half an orbit.
> - The same pattern, $1 + \cos^2\iota$ face-on versus edge-on, circular on the axis and linear in the plane, is that of a rotating electric dipole ([[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^ex-b11-3-2|EM Example §B11.3.2]]); there the radiation is at the rotation frequency, because a dipole is odd under $\vec x \to -\vec x$, and gravity has no dipole radiation ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-6|Theorem §C8.5.6]]).

*Uses:* [[#^mod-c8-6-1|Model §C8.6.1]], [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-1|Def. §C8.5.1]], [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-5|Theorem §C8.5.5]], [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-2|Def. §C8.2.2]], [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-3|Def. §C8.2.3]], [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-4|Theorem §C8.2.4]], [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-4|CM Theorem §B2.2.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.6.3: The Power Radiated by a Circular Binary
> In [[#^mod-c8-6-1|Model §C8.6.1]]:
> 1. the radiated power is constant in time and
>
> $$
> P = \frac{32}{5}\,\mu^2R^4\omega^6 = \frac{32}{5}\,\frac{\mu^2m^3}{R^5} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } P = \frac{32}{5}\,\frac{G}{c^5}\,\mu^2R^4\omega^6 = \frac{32}{5}\,\frac{G^4\mu^2m^3}{c^5R^5}\Bigr) ;
> $$
>
> 2. its angular distribution is
>
> $$
> \frac{dP}{d\Omega} = \frac{2\mu^2R^4\omega^6}{\pi}\,g(\iota), \qquad g(\iota) = \Bigl(\frac{1 + \cos^2\iota}{2}\Bigr)^2 + \cos^2\iota ,
> $$
>
> with $g(0) = 2$ and $g(\pi/2) = \frac14$: the flux along the orbital axis is $8$ times that in the orbital plane.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c8-6-1|Model §C8.6.1]], quadrupole order; *applies:* to the circular orbit held fixed; for an orbit that shrinks slowly, at each instant ([[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5.1, eqs. (2.167)–(2.172), pp. 43–44 (PDF 44–45) (see [[#^cau-c8-6-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens' §2.5.1]]) · Schutz §9.5, eq. (9.139), p. 246 (PDF 264); §9.6, eq. (9.153), p. 248 (PDF 266)*

^thm-c8-6-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> *First route: the luminosity formula.*
>
> **Step 1** (the trace is constant). From Step 2 of [[#^der-c8-6-2|the derivation of Theorem §C8.6.2]], $M^{kk} = M^{11} + M^{22} = \mu R^2$, constant. The reduced quadrupole $Q^{ij} = M^{ij} - \frac13\delta^{ij}M^{kk}$ ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-2|Def. §C8.5.2]]) therefore has the same time derivatives as $M^{ij}$.
>
> **Step 2** (third derivatives). Differentiate $\ddot M$ once more:
>
> $$
> \dddot Q^{11} = -\dddot Q^{22} = -2\omega B\sin2\omega t, \qquad \dddot Q^{12} = \dddot Q^{21} = 2\omega B\cos2\omega t, \qquad B = 2\mu R^2\omega^2 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (sum of squares). The four nonzero entries give
>
> $$
> \dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij} = 4\omega^2B^2\bigl(\sin^2 + \sin^2 + \cos^2 + \cos^2\bigr) = 8\omega^2B^2 = 32\mu^2R^4\omega^6 ,
> $$
>
> the same at every instant, so the average of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-7|Theorem §C8.5.7]] is not needed: $P = \frac15\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij} = \frac{32}{5}\mu^2R^4\omega^6$. With $\omega^2 = m/R^3$, $R^4\omega^6 = R^4m^3/R^9 = m^3/R^5$. Part 1 is proved.
>
> *Second route: the flux through a sphere, which also gives the pattern.*
>
> **Step 4** (flux in terms of the polarisations). In the transverse basis $h^{TT}$ is the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} h_+ & h_\times \\ h_\times & -h_+\end{pmatrix}$, so $h^{TT}_{ij}h^{TT}_{ij} = 2(h_+^2 + h_\times^2)$, and [[§C8.4 The Energy of Gravitational Waves#^thm-c8-4-4|Theorem §C8.4.4]] gives per unit solid angle
>
> $$
> \frac{dP}{d\Omega} = \frac{r^2}{32\pi}\bigl\langle\dot h^{TT}_{ij}\dot h^{TT}_{ij}\bigr\rangle = \frac{r^2}{16\pi}\bigl\langle\dot h_+^2 + \dot h_\times^2\bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (insert the waveform). With $A = 4\mu\omega^2R^2/r$ and $\psi = 2\omega t_r - 2\varphi$, [[#^thm-c8-6-2|Theorem §C8.6.2]] gives $\dot h_+ = -2\omega A\frac{1 + \cos^2\iota}{2}\sin\psi$, $\dot h_\times = 2\omega A\cos\iota\cos\psi$. The averages over a period are $\langle\sin^2\psi\rangle = \langle\cos^2\psi\rangle = \frac12$ ([[§C8.4 The Energy of Gravitational Waves#^def-c8-4-2|Def. §C8.4.2]]), so
>
> $$
> \frac{dP}{d\Omega} = \frac{r^2}{16\pi}\cdot4\omega^2A^2\cdot\frac12\,g(\iota) = \frac{r^2\omega^2}{8\pi}\cdot\frac{16\mu^2\omega^4R^4}{r^2}\,g(\iota) = \frac{2\mu^2R^4\omega^6}{\pi}\,g(\iota) .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (integrate over the sphere). With $c = \cos\iota$, $d\Omega = d\varphi\,dc$:
>
> $$
> \int_{-1}^1\frac{(1 + c^2)^2}{4}\,dc = \frac14\Bigl(2 + \frac43 + \frac25\Bigr) = \frac{14}{15}, \qquad \int_{-1}^1c^2\,dc = \frac23 = \frac{10}{15}, \qquad \oint g\,d\Omega = 2\pi\cdot\frac{24}{15} = \frac{16\pi}{5} .
> $$
>
> Hence $P = \frac{2\mu^2R^4\omega^6}{\pi}\cdot\frac{16\pi}{5} = \frac{32}{5}\mu^2R^4\omega^6$, as in Step 3. Part 2 and the values $g(0) = 1 + 1 = 2$, $g(\pi/2) = \frac14 + 0$ follow. (Both routes checked symbolically.)
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Two independent routes, one through the multipole moments and one through the field at infinity, agree: the luminosity formula and the energy flux of the waves are one statement.
> - The power is constant because $\lvert\dddot Q\rvert$ is constant on a circle; for an ellipse it is not, and the average then matters ([[#^rem-c8-6-1|★ Remark: Eccentric orbits]]).

^der-c8-6-3

*Uses:* [[#^thm-c8-6-2|Theorem §C8.6.2]], [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-2|Def. §C8.5.2]], [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-7|Theorem §C8.5.7]], [[§C8.4 The Energy of Gravitational Waves#^thm-c8-4-4|Theorem §C8.4.4]], [[§C8.4 The Energy of Gravitational Waves#^def-c8-4-2|Def. §C8.4.2]], [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-4|CM Theorem §B2.2.4]]

The amplitude $4\mu\omega^2R^2/r$ and the power $\frac{32}{5}\mu^2R^4\omega^6$ contain $\mu$, $m$ and $R$. A detector measures neither $R$ nor the masses directly; it measures the frequency $\omega_{\text{gw}}$ and how it changes. Eliminating $R$ with Kepler's law, $R = (m/\omega^2)^{1/3}$, turns $\mu^2R^4\omega^6$ into $\mu^2m^{4/3}\omega^{10/3} = (\mu^{3/5}m^{2/5}\omega)^{10/3}$, and $\mu\omega^2R^2$ into $(\mu^{3/5}m^{2/5})^{5/3}\omega^{2/3}$: at a given frequency the masses enter only in one combination.

> [!definition] Definition §C8.6.1: Chirp Mass
> For a binary with masses $m_1, m_2 > 0$, total mass $m$ and reduced mass $\mu$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-2|CM Def. §B2.1.2]]), the **chirp mass** is the function $\mathcal M : (0, \infty)^2 \to (0, \infty)$,
>
> $$
> \mathcal M = \mu^{3/5}m^{2/5} = \frac{(m_1m_2)^{3/5}}{(m_1 + m_2)^{1/5}} = \eta^{3/5}m \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } G\mathcal M/c^3 \text{ is a time, } G\mathcal M/c^2 \text{ a length}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> It is symmetric in $m_1, m_2$ and homogeneous of degree one, and $\mathcal M^{5/3} = \mu m^{2/3}$. Since $\eta \le \frac14$, with equality for equal masses, $\mathcal M \le 4^{-3/5}m = 0.435\,m$. The name refers to the chirp of [[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]], whose rate it fixes.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5.1, eq. (2.173), p. 44 (PDF 45) · Schutz §9.6, eq. (9.151), p. 248 (PDF 266)*

## The inspiral

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.6.4: The Inspiral
> In [[#^mod-c8-6-1|Model §C8.6.1]], let the orbital energy decrease at the rate at which the waves carry energy away, $dE/dt = -P$, and let the orbit pass slowly through circular orbits. Then:
> 1. *(Energy and power through $\mathcal M$.)* $E = -\bigl(\mathcal M^5\omega_{\text{gw}}^2/32\bigr)^{1/3}$ and $P = \frac{32}{5}\bigl(\mathcal M\omega_{\text{gw}}/2\bigr)^{10/3}$.
> 2. *(Chirp.)* The frequency rises:
>
> $$
> \dot\omega_{\text{gw}} = \frac{12}{5}\,2^{1/3}\,\mathcal M^{5/3}\omega_{\text{gw}}^{11/3}, \qquad \dot f = \frac{96}{5}\,\pi^{8/3}\mathcal M^{5/3}f^{11/3} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \dot f = \frac{96}{5}\pi^{8/3}\Bigl(\frac{G\mathcal M}{c^3}\Bigr)^{5/3}f^{11/3}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 3. *(Frequency and time to coalescence.)* The frequency diverges at a finite time $t_c$, and with $\tau = t_c - t$
>
> $$
> f(\tau) = \frac1\pi\Bigl(\frac{5}{256\,\tau}\Bigr)^{3/8}\mathcal M^{-5/8}, \qquad \tau(f) = \frac{5}{256}\,\mathcal M^{-5/3}(\pi f)^{-8/3} \qquad \bigl(\text{restored: } \mathcal M \to G\mathcal M/c^3\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 4. *(Shrinking orbit.)* $\dot R/R = -1/(4\tau)$, so $R(\tau) = R_0(\tau/\tau_0)^{1/4}$, and an orbit of radius $R_0$ coalesces after
>
> $$
> \tau_0 = \frac{5}{256}\,\frac{R_0^4}{m^2\mu} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \tau_0 = \frac{5}{256}\,\frac{c^5R_0^4}{G^3m^2\mu}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 5. *(Phase.)* The wave phase is $\Phi(\tau) = \Phi_c - 2(5\mathcal M)^{-5/8}\tau^{5/8}$, and the number of wave cycles left at frequency $f$ is $N = (\Phi_c - \Phi)/2\pi = \frac{1}{32\pi}(\pi\mathcal Mf)^{-5/3}$.
> 6. *(⚑ The quasi-circular condition.)* With $v = (m\omega)^{1/3}$ and $\eta = \mu/m$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\dot\omega}{\omega^2} = \frac{96}{5}\,\eta\,v^5, \qquad \frac{\lvert\dot R\rvert}{v} = \frac{64}{5}\,\eta\,v^5 :
> $$
>
> the orbit changes little in one orbit as long as $v \ll 1$. Even at $R = 6m$ ($v^2 = \frac16$, equal masses) $\dot\omega/\omega^2 = 0.054$.
> 7. *(Where it ends.)* The Newtonian point-mass description is stopped near $R = 6m$, the innermost stable circular orbit of a test body about the total mass ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]], part 4), at $f_{\text{ISCO}} = 1/(6^{3/2}\pi m)$, restored $c^3/(6^{3/2}\pi Gm) = 4.40\ \mathrm{kHz}\,(M_\odot/m)$. The divergence at $t_c$ is a property of the model, not of the binary.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c8-6-1|Model §C8.6.1]] with adiabatic energy loss; *applies:* while $v^2 = m/R \ll 1$ (part 6 measures how well); exact within the model.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5.1, eqs. (2.174)–(2.182), (2.184), (2.186), Example 2.4, pp. 44–46 (PDF 45–47) · Schutz §9.6, eqs. (9.153)–(9.160), pp. 248–250 (PDF 266–268)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (energy in terms of $\omega$). From $E = -\mu m/(2R)$ ([[#^mod-c8-6-1|Model §C8.6.1]]) and Kepler, $R = m^{1/3}\omega^{-2/3}$:
>
> $$
> E = -\frac{\mu m}{2}\,m^{-1/3}\omega^{2/3} = -\frac12\,\mu m^{2/3}\omega^{2/3} = -\frac12\,\mathcal M^{5/3}\omega^{2/3} ,
> $$
>
> using $\mathcal M^{5/3} = \mu m^{2/3}$ ([[#^def-c8-6-1|Def. §C8.6.1]]). With $\omega = \omega_{\text{gw}}/2$: $E = -\frac12\mathcal M^{5/3}2^{-2/3}\omega_{\text{gw}}^{2/3} = -\bigl(\frac18\cdot\frac14\,\mathcal M^5\omega_{\text{gw}}^2\bigr)^{1/3} = -\bigl(\mathcal M^5\omega_{\text{gw}}^2/32\bigr)^{1/3}$.
>
> **Step 2** (power in terms of $\omega$). From [[#^thm-c8-6-3|Theorem §C8.6.3]] and $R^4 = m^{4/3}\omega^{-8/3}$:
>
> $$
> P = \frac{32}{5}\mu^2m^{4/3}\omega^{-8/3}\omega^6 = \frac{32}{5}\bigl(\mu m^{2/3}\bigr)^2\omega^{10/3} = \frac{32}{5}\bigl(\mathcal M\omega\bigr)^{10/3} = \frac{32}{5}\Bigl(\frac{\mathcal M\omega_{\text{gw}}}{2}\Bigr)^{10/3} .
> $$
>
> Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 3** (energy balance). The energy carried through a large sphere is $P$ ([[§C8.4 The Energy of Gravitational Waves#^thm-c8-4-4|Theorem §C8.4.4]]); the only reservoir is the orbit (point masses have no internal energy; Couzens' footnote 9). Along a sequence of circular orbits $E$ is a function of $\omega$, so $\frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{dE}{d\omega}\dot\omega$ with $\frac{dE}{d\omega} = -\frac13\mathcal M^{5/3}\omega^{-1/3}$, and $dE/dt = -P$ gives
>
> $$
> \frac13\mathcal M^{5/3}\omega^{-1/3}\,\dot\omega = \frac{32}{5}\mathcal M^{10/3}\omega^{10/3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot\omega = \frac{96}{5}\,\mathcal M^{5/3}\omega^{11/3} .
> $$
>
> For $\omega_{\text{gw}} = 2\omega$: $\dot\omega_{\text{gw}} = 2\dot\omega = \frac{192}{5}\mathcal M^{5/3}2^{-11/3}\omega_{\text{gw}}^{11/3}$, and $192\cdot2^{-11/3} = 12\cdot2^4\cdot2^{-11/3} = 12\cdot2^{1/3}$. For $f = \omega/\pi$: $\pi\dot f = \frac{96}{5}\mathcal M^{5/3}\pi^{11/3}f^{11/3}$, so $\dot f = \frac{96}{5}\pi^{8/3}\mathcal M^{5/3}f^{11/3}$. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 4** (integration). Separate variables in $\dot\omega = \frac{96}{5}\mathcal M^{5/3}\omega^{11/3}$ and integrate from $t$ to the time $t_c$ at which $\omega \to \infty$:
>
> $$
> \int_{\omega(t)}^{\infty}\omega'^{-11/3}\,d\omega' = \frac38\,\omega(t)^{-8/3} = \frac{96}{5}\mathcal M^{5/3}(t_c - t) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega^{-8/3} = \frac{256}{5}\,\mathcal M^{5/3}\tau .
> $$
>
> The integral converges at the upper end, so $t_c$ is finite. Solving, $\omega = (5/256\tau)^{3/8}\mathcal M^{-5/8}$ and $f = \omega/\pi$; solving instead for $\tau$ gives $\tau = \frac{5}{256}\mathcal M^{-5/3}\omega^{-8/3}$ with $\omega = \pi f$. Part 3 is proved.
>
> **Step 5** (radius). Kepler gives $R = m^{1/3}\omega^{-2/3}$, so $\dot R/R = -\frac23\dot\omega/\omega$. From $\omega \propto \tau^{-3/8}$ and $d\tau/dt = -1$, $\dot\omega/\omega = \frac38\cdot\frac1\tau$, hence $\dot R/R = -\frac{1}{4\tau}$. Integrating, $\ln R = \frac14\ln\tau + \text{const}$, i.e. $R = R_0(\tau/\tau_0)^{1/4}$. At $R = R_0$: $\omega^{-8/3} = (R_0^3/m)^{4/3} = R_0^4m^{-4/3}$, and with $\mathcal M^{-5/3} = \mu^{-1}m^{-2/3}$ the formula of Step 4 gives $\tau_0 = \frac{5}{256}R_0^4\mu^{-1}m^{-2/3}m^{-4/3} = \frac{5}{256}\frac{R_0^4}{m^2\mu}$. Part 4 is proved.
>
> **Step 6** (phase and cycles). The wave phase advances at $\dot\Phi = \omega_{\text{gw}} = 2\omega$, so
>
> $$
> \Phi_c - \Phi(t) = \int_t^{t_c}2\omega\,dt' = 2\int_0^\tau\Bigl(\frac{5}{256\tau'}\Bigr)^{3/8}\mathcal M^{-5/8}d\tau' = 2\Bigl(\frac{5}{256}\Bigr)^{3/8}\mathcal M^{-5/8}\,\frac85\,\tau^{5/8} = 2(5\mathcal M)^{-5/8}\tau^{5/8} ,
> $$
>
> since $256^{3/8} = 8$ and $(5/256)^{3/8}\cdot\frac85 = 5^{3/8}\cdot\frac{8}{8\cdot5} = 5^{-5/8}$. Substituting $\tau^{5/8} = (5/256)^{5/8}\mathcal M^{-25/24}\omega^{-5/3}$ from Step 4 and $256^{5/8} = 32$: $\Phi_c - \Phi = 2\cdot5^{-5/8}\mathcal M^{-5/8}\cdot5^{5/8}\cdot\frac{1}{32}\mathcal M^{-25/24}\omega^{-5/3} = \frac{1}{16}(\mathcal M\omega)^{-5/3}$, and $N = \frac{1}{32\pi}(\pi\mathcal Mf)^{-5/3}$. Part 5 is proved.
>
> **Step 7** (quasi-circular condition). From Step 3, $\dot\omega/\omega^2 = \frac{96}{5}\mathcal M^{5/3}\omega^{5/3} = \frac{96}{5}\mu m^{2/3}\omega^{5/3} = \frac{96}{5}\frac\mu m(m\omega)^{5/3} = \frac{96}{5}\eta v^5$, since $v = \omega R = \omega m^{1/3}\omega^{-2/3} = (m\omega)^{1/3}$. From Step 5, $\lvert\dot R\rvert = \frac23R\dot\omega/\omega$, so $\lvert\dot R\rvert/v = \frac23\dot\omega/\omega^2 = \frac{64}{5}\eta v^5$. At $R = 6m$, $v^5 = 6^{-5/2} = 0.0113$, and with $\eta = \frac14$: $\frac{96}{5}\cdot\frac14\cdot0.0113 = 0.054$. Part 6 is proved.
>
> **Step 8** (ISCO frequency). At $R = 6m$ Kepler gives $\omega = (m/216m^3)^{1/2} = 6^{-3/2}m^{-1}$ and $f = \omega/\pi$. With $GM_\odot/c^3 = 4.9256\ \mu\mathrm s$: $f_{\text{ISCO}} = 1/(14.70\pi\times4.9256\ \mu\mathrm s) = 4.40\ \mathrm{kHz}$ for $m = M_\odot$. (Steps 1–8 checked symbolically; the numbers by script.)
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Every observable of the inspiral, $E$, $P$, $\dot f$, $\tau$, $N$, depends on the masses only through $\mathcal M$; gravity has no intrinsic scale, so $\mathcal M$ and $f$ enter only as $\mathcal Mf$ (Schutz's scale-free argument).
> - The time left falls as $f^{-8/3}$: most of the time, and most of the cycles, are spent at low frequency, while most of the change happens at the end.
> - The adiabatic approximation is good almost to the end; what fails first is the Newtonian orbit, not the slowness.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.6.5: The Chirp Waveform; the Distance Can Be Read from the Signal
> In the setting of [[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]]:
> 1. *(Waveform.)* To leading order in $\dot\omega/\omega^2$,
>
> $$
> h_+ = \frac4r\,\mathcal M^{5/3}\bigl(\pi f(t_r)\bigr)^{2/3}\,\frac{1 + \cos^2\iota}{2}\,\cos\Phi(t_r), \qquad h_\times = \frac4r\,\mathcal M^{5/3}\bigl(\pi f(t_r)\bigr)^{2/3}\cos\iota\,\sin\Phi(t_r) ,
> $$
>
> with $f$ and $\Phi$ from [[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]] (the constant $-2\varphi$ absorbed into $\Phi_c$). In terms of $\tau$ the amplitude is $\frac1r\mathcal M^{5/4}(5/\tau)^{1/4}$. Frequency and amplitude rise together, as $\tau^{-3/8}$ and $\tau^{-1/4}$: the **chirp**.
> 2. *(Chirp mass from the frequency track.)* A measurement of $f$ and $\dot f$ at one time gives
>
> $$
> \mathcal M = \Bigl[\frac{5}{96}\,\pi^{-8/3}\,\dot f\,f^{-11/3}\Bigr]^{3/5} .
> $$
>
> 3. *(⚑ Standard siren.)* If in addition the face-on amplitude $h_0 = \frac4r\mathcal M^{5/3}(\pi f)^{2/3}$ is known, the inclination having been found from the ratio of the two polarisation amplitudes, $2\cos\iota/(1 + \cos^2\iota)$, then the distance is
>
> $$
> r = \frac{5\,\dot f}{24\pi^2f^3h_0} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } r = \frac{5c\,\dot f}{24\pi^2f^3h_0}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> with no further knowledge of the source.
>
> Restored amplitude: $\frac4r\bigl(\frac{G\mathcal M}{c^2}\bigr)^{5/3}\bigl(\frac{\pi f}{c}\bigr)^{2/3}$.
>
> *Model:* [[#^mod-c8-6-1|Model §C8.6.1]] with adiabatic inspiral; *applies:* while $\dot\omega/\omega^2 \ll 1$; the distance is the luminosity distance once the expansion of the universe is included, and the masses are then redshifted (Schutz, Table 12.1 note; not derived here); a single detector does not measure $\iota$, a network does (Schutz §9.6).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5.1, eqs. (2.183)–(2.187), Fig. 5, pp. 46–47 (PDF 47–48) · Schutz §9.6, eqs. (9.152), (9.156), (9.161)–(9.162), pp. 248–251 (PDF 266–269)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (slowly varying orbit). Write the orbit as $\vec x = (-R(t)\sin\psi(t), R(t)\cos\psi(t), 0)$ with $\dot\psi = \omega(t)$; then $M^{11} = \mu R^2\sin^2\psi$ and, for example,
>
> $$
> \ddot M^{11} = \mu\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\Bigl[\frac{R^2}{2}(1 - \cos2\psi)\Bigr] = 2\mu R^2\omega^2\cos2\psi + \mu R^2\dot\omega\sin2\psi + O\bigl(R\dot R\omega,\ \dot R^2,\ R\ddot R\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Relative to the first term the others are of order $\dot\omega/\omega^2$ and $\lvert\dot R\rvert/(\omega R)$, which are $O(\eta v^5)$ by [[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]], part 6. Dropping them, the derivation of [[#^thm-c8-6-2|Theorem §C8.6.2]] goes through with $2\omega t \to 2\psi(t) = \Phi(t)$ in the trigonometric functions and $\omega \to \omega(t)$, $R \to R(t)$ in the amplitude, all at $t_r$.
>
> **Step 2** (amplitude in $\mathcal M$ and $f$). With $R^2 = m^{2/3}\omega^{-4/3}$: $4\mu\omega^2R^2 = 4\mu m^{2/3}\omega^{2/3} = 4\mathcal M^{5/3}\omega^{2/3}$, and $\omega = \pi f$. Part 1, first form.
>
> **Step 3** (amplitude in $\tau$). Insert $\pi f = (5/256\tau)^{3/8}\mathcal M^{-5/8}$: $(\pi f)^{2/3} = (5/256\tau)^{1/4}\mathcal M^{-5/12}$, so $4\mathcal M^{5/3}(\pi f)^{2/3} = 4\cdot256^{-1/4}\,\mathcal M^{5/3 - 5/12}(5/\tau)^{1/4} = \mathcal M^{5/4}(5/\tau)^{1/4}$, since $256^{1/4} = 4$ and $\frac53 - \frac5{12} = \frac54$.
>
> **Step 4** (invert the chirp). From $\dot f = \frac{96}{5}\pi^{8/3}\mathcal M^{5/3}f^{11/3}$ ([[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]], part 2), $\mathcal M^{5/3} = \frac{5}{96}\pi^{-8/3}\dot ff^{-11/3}$; raising to the power $\frac35$ gives part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (distance). Insert $\mathcal M^{5/3}$ from Step 4 into $h_0$:
>
> $$
> h_0 = \frac4r\cdot\frac{5}{96}\pi^{-8/3}\dot ff^{-11/3}\cdot\pi^{2/3}f^{2/3} = \frac{5}{24\pi^2}\,\frac{\dot f}{f^3\,r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{5\dot f}{24\pi^2f^3h_0} .
> $$
>
> Restoring units: $\dot f/f^3$ has dimension of time, multiplied by $c$ it is a length. The inclination: by part 1 the ratio of the $h_\times$ and $h_+$ amplitudes is $\cos\iota/\frac12(1 + \cos^2\iota)$, a one-to-one function of $\cos\iota \in [0, 1]$; knowing $\iota$, the measured $h_+$ amplitude divided by $\frac12(1 + \cos^2\iota)$ is $h_0$. (Steps 2–5 checked symbolically; the closed formula returns $410\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ from the $f$, $\dot f$, $h_0$ of an $\mathcal M = 28\,M_\odot$ source placed there.)
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - A binary announces its own luminosity: $\dot f$ and $f$ fix $\mathcal M$, hence the power ([[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]], part 1), and the received amplitude then fixes the distance. Astronomy calls such a source a standard candle; Schutz's name for the gravitational version is standard siren.
> - What cannot be read from the Newtonian chirp is $m_1$ and $m_2$ separately: they enter only through $\mathcal M$ at this order (Schutz: post-Newtonian corrections break the degeneracy).

## What has been observed

> [!law] Law §C8.6.6: The Hulse–Taylor Pulsar: Orbital Decay
> In 1974 Hulse and Taylor found a pulsar, PSR B1913+16, with pulse period $59\ \mathrm{ms}$, in a binary with another neutron star (Couzens; d'Inverno & Vickers). Its orbital period is $7\ \mathrm h\,45\ \mathrm{min}\,7\ \mathrm s = 27\,907\ \mathrm s$, eccentricity $e = 0.617$, both masses about $1.4\,M_\odot$, distance about $8\ \mathrm{kpc}$ (Schutz, 2022); the orbit is about $3$ light-seconds across (d'Inverno & Vickers). The pulse arrival times, a precise clock, give the orbital elements, the periastron advance and the Einstein delay, from which the two masses follow ([[§C6.4 Perihelion Precession#^ex-c6-4-2|Example §C6.4.2]]). The orbital period decreases:
>
> | | $\dot P_b$ | per year |
> |---|---|---|
> | GR prediction for the measured masses (Schutz 2022, Weisberg & Huang 2016) | $-2.40263\times10^{-12}$ | $75.8\ \mu\mathrm s$ |
> | observed (Schutz 2022, Weisberg & Huang 2016) | $-(2.398 \pm 0.004)\times10^{-12}$ | $75.7\ \mu\mathrm s$ |
> | observed (Hartle 2003, Taylor & Weisberg) | $-(2.422 \pm 0.006)\times10^{-12}$ | $76.4\ \mu\mathrm s$ |
>
> Prediction and observation agree within $0.17\%$ (Schutz) or better than $\frac13\%$ (Hartle; Couzens: $0.3\%$). The two observed values differ by more than their errors; the typed sources do not say why (different data spans and analyses), and each agrees with the prediction it is compared with. The cumulative shift of the time of periastron, about $-40\ \mathrm s$ by 2005, follows the general-relativistic curve point by point (data figures in Couzens Fig. 6, Hartle Fig. 23.3, d'Inverno & Vickers Fig. 21.5). The radiation itself, at $2/P_b = 72\ \mu\mathrm{Hz}$, is not observed. Hulse and Taylor received the Nobel Prize in 1993.
>
> *Valid when:* the observation is indirect: it tests the energy loss of [[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]] (with the eccentricity factor of [[#^rem-c8-6-1|★ Remark: Eccentric orbits]]), after the masses are fixed by other relativistic timing effects.
>
> *Model:* point-mass binary with quadrupole energy loss; *applies:* a weakly relativistic orbit ($v^2 \sim 10^{-6}$), where the model is excellent.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5, p. 41 (PDF 42); §2.6, pp. 49–50 (PDF 50–51) · Schutz §9.4, p. 236 (PDF 254); §9.5, eqs. (9.146)–(9.148), p. 247 (PDF 265) · Hartle §23.7, eq. (23.60), Fig. 23.3, pp. 508–509 (PDF 507–508) · d'Inverno & Vickers §21.10, p. 428, Fig. 21.5 (PDF 443–444); per-year conversions by script*

> [!law] Law §C8.6.7: Observed Inspirals and What They Measured
> Ground-based interferometers ([[§C8.3 What a Passing Wave Does#^mod-c8-3-4|Model §C8.3.4]]) have recorded the last stage of binary inspirals directly. As stated in the typed sources, with their dates:
> 1. **GW150914** (14 September 2015, the first detection, [[§C8.3 What a Passing Wave Does#^law-c8-3-5|Law §C8.3.5]]): two black holes, $m_1 = 36$, $m_2 = 29$, $\mathcal M = 28$, final mass $62\,M_\odot$, luminosity distance $410\ \mathrm{Mpc}$, signal-to-noise ratio $24$ (Schutz, Table 12.1, 2022). d'Inverno & Vickers (2022): $35 + 30 \to 62\,M_\odot$, final spin parameter about $0.68$, $3\,M_\odot$ radiated with peak power $3.6\times10^{49}\ \mathrm W$, distance $1.4 \pm 0.6$ billion light-years ($430 \pm 180\ \mathrm{Mpc}$), signal from $35$ to $250\ \mathrm{Hz}$ lasting about $0.2\ \mathrm s$, orbital frequency $75\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and separation $350\ \mathrm{km}$ at merger. Couzens rounds to $30 + 35 \to 62$.
> 2. **GW170817** (17 August 2017, 12:41:04.4 UTC; Schutz 2022): the first binary neutron star, $m_1 = 1.44$, $m_2 = 1.28$, $\mathcal M = 1.186\,M_\odot$, distance $40\ \mathrm{Mpc}$ ($40 \pm 8$ from the standard siren), signal-to-noise ratio $32$, in the band for about $100\ \mathrm s$. The Fermi satellite recorded the gamma-ray burst GRB170817A $1.7\ \mathrm s$ after the merger, and about $11$ hours later six teams found the kilonova in the galaxy NGC 4993. The redshift of NGC 4993 and the standard-siren distance ([[#^thm-c8-6-5|Theorem §C8.6.5]], part 3) gave the Hubble–Lemaître constant: a posterior peaked near $H_0 = 70\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$, then $70.3 \pm 5\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ once radio observations fixed the jet angle, and so the inclination.
> 3. Schutz's table lists further events (GW170814, the first seen by three detectors; GW190521, $85 + 65 \to 142\,M_\odot$ at $5.3\ \mathrm{Gpc}$; GW190425; GW190814), and d'Inverno & Vickers report ten binary black holes and one binary neutron star from the first two observing runs.
>
> *Valid when:* the parameters are those of the collaborations' fits with full general-relativistic waveforms (post-Newtonian inspiral, numerical-relativity merger), not of the Newtonian formulas here; the chirp mass is the best-determined mass, the component masses have larger errors (Schutz). Masses are in the source frame, corrected for cosmological redshift (Schutz, Table 12.1 note).
>
> *Model:* binary inspiral and merger in full general relativity; *applies:* as of the sources' dates (Schutz, d'Inverno & Vickers: 2022).
>
> *Source: Schutz §12.4, Table 12.1, pp. 398–402, Fig. 12.6 (PDF 416–420) · d'Inverno & Vickers §21.14, pp. 441–444 (PDF 456–459) · Couzens, GR2, §2.3, p. 15 (PDF 16)*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Eccentric orbits
> For a Kepler ellipse of semi-major axis $a$ and eccentricity $e$, the quadrupole power averaged over an orbit is the circular result multiplied by the Peters–Mathews factor (Couzens (2.188), stated, citing Peters and Mathews 1963 and Maggiore; Schutz Exercise 9.56):
>
> $$
> \langle P\rangle = \frac{32}{5}\,\frac{\mu^2m^3}{a^5}\,f(e), \qquad f(e) = \frac{1 + \frac{73}{24}e^2 + \frac{37}{96}e^4}{(1 - e^2)^{7/2}} ,
> $$
>
> and the period decays as $\dot P_b/P_b = -\frac{96}{5}\mu m^{2/3}(2\pi/P_b)^{8/3}f(e)$ (Couzens (2.189), whose $f(e)$ is this factor). At $e = 0$, $f = 1$ and $a = R$, and both reduce to [[#^thm-c8-6-3|Theorem §C8.6.3]] and [[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]] ($\dot P_b/P_b = -\dot\omega/\omega$). The factor grows fast: $f(0.617) = 11.8$, because the emission scales as $R^{-5}$ and the stars spend part of each orbit at periastron, $1 - e = 0.383$ of $a$. The emission also removes angular momentum ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-8|Theorem §C8.5.8]]) at a rate that makes $e$ decrease: an isolated binary circularises long before it merges, which is why the circular model describes the inspirals that interferometers see (Couzens, citing Peters 1964). These results are stated in the sources, not derived here; deriving them needs the time average of $\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij}$ over a Kepler ellipse ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-6|CM Theorem §B2.2.6]] for the time dependence).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5.1, eqs. (2.188)–(2.189), p. 48 (PDF 49) · Schutz §9.5, p. 246 (PDF 264) ("some 12 times"); $f(0.617)$ by script*

^rem-c8-6-1

> [!remark] Remark: Sources beyond the inspiral
> Couzens lists four classes of sources: binaries, spinning neutron stars, gravitational collapse and the early universe, and discusses two. For binaries the inspiral of this section is followed by the merger, which needs numerical solutions of the full Einstein equations, and by the **ringdown**: the remnant black hole radiates away its deformations and settles to a Kerr black hole (C12, planned), with a distinctive signal that would distinguish a black hole from other remnants. A spinning neutron star radiates only if it is not symmetric about its spin axis, because a time-dependent quadrupole moment is needed ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-6|Theorem §C8.5.6]]). The early universe may have left a stochastic background of waves, isotropic like the cosmic microwave background, whose strength is unknown.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.6, pp. 48–50 (PDF 49–51) · d'Inverno & Vickers §21.14, p. 442 (PDF 457)*

^rem-c8-6-2

The inspiral ends where the Newtonian orbit fails: at a separation of a few $m$, where the bodies' own fields are strong and the stars either touch or fall into each other. What two neutron stars are made of, how massive such a star can be, and when a star must collapse to a black hole, are the questions of C9 (planned). The rotating black hole that the merger leaves behind is the subject of C12 (planned).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C8.6.1: Two 1.4 Solar-Mass Neutron Stars
> *Setup.* $m_1 = m_2 = 1.4\,M_\odot$: $m = 2.8\,M_\odot$, $\mu = 0.7\,M_\odot$, $\eta = \frac14$, and $\mathcal M = 0.7^{3/5}\times2.8^{2/5}\,M_\odot = 0.8073\times1.5096\,M_\odot = 1.2188\,M_\odot$ ([[#^def-c8-6-1|Def. §C8.6.1]]), so $G\mathcal M/c^3 = 1.2188\times4.9256\ \mu\mathrm s = 6.003\ \mu\mathrm s$ (Couzens rounds to $1.21\,M_\odot$).
>
> *Time to coalescence.* [[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]], part 3, restored: $\tau = \frac{5}{256}(G\mathcal M/c^3)^{-5/3}(\pi f)^{-8/3}$. At $f = 100\ \mathrm{Hz}$: $(6.003\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm s)^{-5/3} = 5.043\times10^{8}\ \mathrm s^{-5/3}$, $(314.16\ \mathrm s^{-1})^{-8/3} = 2.192\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm s^{8/3}$, and $\tau = \frac{5}{256}\times5.043\times10^{8}\times2.192\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm s = 2.16\ \mathrm s$. Since $\tau \propto f^{-8/3}$ and $10^{8/3} = 464$:
>
> | $f$ | $\tau$ | $R = (Gm/\pi^2f^2)^{1/3}$ | wave cycles left | $v/c$ | $\dot\omega/\omega^2$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $10\ \mathrm{Hz}$ | $1002\ \mathrm s = 16.7\ \mathrm{min}$ | $722\ \mathrm{km}$ | $1.60\times10^{4}$ | $0.076$ | $1.2\times10^{-5}$ |
> | $100\ \mathrm{Hz}$ | $2.16\ \mathrm s$ | $156\ \mathrm{km}$ | $346$ | | |
> | $1\ \mathrm{kHz}$ | $4.65\ \mathrm{ms}$ | $33.5\ \mathrm{km}$ | $7.4$ | $0.35$ | $0.026$ |
>
> Couzens' Example 2.4: $17$ min, $2$ s, a few ms, $R \simeq 33$ km. The cycles are $N = \frac{1}{32\pi}(\pi G\mathcal Mf/c^3)^{-5/3}$ (part 5); the last column is part 6 with $v = (\pi Gmf)^{1/3}/c$.
>
> *Where the model ends.* The ISCO of $2.8\,M_\odot$ lies at $6Gm/c^2 = 24.8\ \mathrm{km}$, reached at $f_{\text{ISCO}} = 4.40\ \mathrm{kHz}/2.8 = 1570\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $1.4\ \mathrm{ms}$ before the formal $t_c$. Neutron stars have radii of about $10\ \mathrm{km}$ (Couzens), so at $1\ \mathrm{kHz}$, $R = 33.5\ \mathrm{km}$, the stars are close to touching: the point-mass model is "inaccurate, though not meaningless" there (Couzens).
>
> *Amplitude.* At GW170817's distance, $40\ \mathrm{Mpc}$, face-on: $h_0 = \frac4r(G\mathcal M/c^2)^{5/3}(\pi f/c)^{2/3} = 1.9\times10^{-23}$ at $10\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $8.9\times10^{-23}$ at $100\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $4.1\times10^{-22}$ at $1\ \mathrm{kHz}$ ([[#^thm-c8-6-5|Theorem §C8.6.5]]).
>
> *Result.* A detector that reaches down to $10\ \mathrm{Hz}$ follows such a binary for a quarter of an hour and sixteen thousand cycles; nearly all of the change in frequency happens in the last seconds. Tracking that many cycles is what lets matched filtering pull the signal out of noise (Schutz §12.3).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, Example 2.4, pp. 45–46 (PDF 46–47) · Schutz §12.1, eq. (12.3), p. 364 (PDF 382) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C8.6.2: Reading the Chirp Mass of GW150914 off the Signal
> *From the masses.* With Schutz's $m_1 = 36$, $m_2 = 29\,M_\odot$: $\mathcal M = (36\times29)^{3/5}/65^{1/5}\,M_\odot = 28.10\,M_\odot$, the tabulated $28$ (d'Inverno's $35 + 30$ give $28.19$).
>
> *From the signal alone.* d'Inverno & Vickers: the signal entered the band at $35\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and lasted about $0.2\ \mathrm s$. Treat $0.2\ \mathrm s$ as $\tau$ at $f = 35\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and invert [[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]], part 3:
>
> $$
> \frac{G\mathcal M}{c^3} = \Bigl[\frac{5}{256\,\tau}\,(\pi f)^{-8/3}\Bigr]^{3/5} = 1.342\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm s, \qquad \mathcal M = \frac{1.342\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm s}{4.9256\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm s}\,M_\odot = 27.3\,M_\odot ,
> $$
>
> within $3\%$ of the fitted value, from two numbers read off the record. Conversely, $\mathcal M = 28\,M_\odot$ gives $\tau(35\ \mathrm{Hz}) = 0.19\ \mathrm s$ and $\dot f = 69\ \mathrm{Hz/s}$ there (Schutz's form of part 2, $\dot f = 12\,(\mathcal M/M_\odot)^{5/3}(f/100\ \mathrm{Hz})^{11/3}\ \mathrm s^{-2}$, Exercise 12.10, has the coefficient $12.5$ by the formula).
>
> *How Newtonian is it?* The ISCO of $m = 65\,M_\odot$ lies at $f = 4.40\ \mathrm{kHz}/65 = 67.6\ \mathrm{Hz}$, only $7$ wave cycles after $35\ \mathrm{Hz}$. The signal kept rising to about $250\ \mathrm{Hz}$: that part is merger and ringdown, beyond this section. At the orbital frequency of $75\ \mathrm{Hz}$ at merger (d'Inverno & Vickers), Kepler with $m = 65\,M_\odot$ gives $R = (Gm/\omega^2)^{1/3} = 339\ \mathrm{km}$ (d'Inverno & Vickers: $350\ \mathrm{km}$), only $3.5\,Gm/c^2$: deep in the strong field.
>
> *Amplitude.* At $410\ \mathrm{Mpc}$, face-on, [[#^thm-c8-6-5|Theorem §C8.6.5]] gives $h_0 = 8.0\times10^{-22}$ at $35\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $2.1\times10^{-21}$ at $150\ \mathrm{Hz}$; the observed peak strain was about $10^{-21}$ ([[§C8.3 What a Passing Wave Does#^law-c8-3-5|Law §C8.3.5]]), the same order once the detector's antenna pattern and the inclination are included.
>
> *Result.* The Newtonian chirp, read from the first tenth of a second, already gives the chirp mass within a few per cent. The masses of black holes this heavy were the surprise of the event (Schutz: the heaviest known before were about $20\,M_\odot$).
>
> *Source: Schutz §12.4, Table 12.1, p. 398 (PDF 416); Exercise 12.10, eq. (12.46), p. 413 (PDF 431) · d'Inverno & Vickers §21.14, pp. 441–442 (PDF 456–457) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C8.6.3: The Hulse–Taylor Decay Predicted
> *Setup.* Schutz's numbers: $P_b = 27\,907\ \mathrm s$, $e = 0.617$, $m_1 = m_2 = 1.4\,M_\odot$, so $\mathcal M = 1.2188\,M_\odot$ and $G\mathcal M/c^3 = 6.003\ \mu\mathrm s$ ([[#^ex-c8-6-1|Example §C8.6.1]]).
>
> *Circular formula.* For a period, $\dot P_b/P_b = -\dot\omega/\omega = -\frac{96}{5}\mathcal M^{5/3}\omega^{8/3}$ ([[#^thm-c8-6-4|Theorem §C8.6.4]], Step 3 of the derivation), and with $\omega = 2\pi/P_b$:
>
> $$
> \dot P_b = -\frac{192\pi}{5}\Bigl(\frac{2\pi G\mathcal M/c^3}{P_b}\Bigr)^{5/3}, \qquad \frac{2\pi\times6.003\ \mu\mathrm s}{27\,907\ \mathrm s} = 1.352\times10^{-9} ,
> $$
>
> $(1.352\times10^{-9})^{5/3} = 1.65\times10^{-15}$, and $\dot P_b = -120.6\times1.65\times10^{-15} = -1.99\times10^{-13}$, i.e. $6.3\ \mu\mathrm s$ per year. This is Schutz's estimate (9.144) and Hartle's (23.60), $-3.4\times10^{-12}(1.4/7.75)^{5/3} = -1.96\times10^{-13}$, $6.2\ \mu\mathrm s$ per year. The same circle radiates $L = \frac{32}{5}(\mathcal M\omega)^{10/3} = 1.75\times10^{-29}$, $6.3\times10^{23}\ \mathrm W$ (Schutz (9.139): $1.71\times10^{-29}$), the number used in the opening.
>
> *Eccentricity.* $f(0.617) = (1 + 3.0417\times0.3807 + 0.3854\times0.1449)/(0.6193)^{7/2} = 2.2138/0.1869 = 11.84$ ([[#^rem-c8-6-1|★ Remark: Eccentric orbits]]), so $\dot P_b = -1.99\times10^{-13}\times11.84 = -2.36\times10^{-12}$, $74.5\ \mu\mathrm s$ per year.
>
> *Comparison.* The full prediction with the measured masses is $-2.40263\times10^{-12}$ ([[#^law-c8-6-6|Law §C8.6.6]]); the remaining $1.7\%$ comes from rounding both masses to $1.4\,M_\odot$, which enter as $\mathcal M^{5/3}$. The circular formula is low by the factor $11.8$: on the ellipse the stars come within $1 - e = 0.383$ of $a$ at periastron, and the emission scales as $R^{-5}$ ($0.383^{-5} = 121$ on a circle of that radius), so the short passage near periastron dominates the average.
>
> *Result.* An order-of-magnitude estimate misses by a factor $12$, not because the quadrupole formula fails but because the orbit is not a circle. Couzens' "about $7\,\mu$s per year" is the circular estimate; the measurement is $75.7\ \mu$s per year.
>
> *Source: Schutz §9.4, p. 236 (PDF 254); §9.5, eqs. (9.139)–(9.148), pp. 246–247 (PDF 264–265) · Hartle §23.7, eqs. (23.59)–(23.60), p. 508 (PDF 507) · numbers checked by script*
