Sections §C8.2–§C8.4 studied waves that were already there: plane waves in vacuum, what they do to test masses, and the energy they carry. The chapter's question was how the field outside a moving source finds out that the source moved. The linearised field equation $\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} = -16\pi T_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-5|Theorem §C8.1.5]]) is a wave equation with a source, four copies of the one that electrodynamics solves with retarded potentials ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|EM Theorem §B11.1.4]]). This section solves it and reads off which property of the source produces the wave.

The obvious guess is that gravity radiates like electromagnetism, whose leading radiation comes from the second time derivative of the electric dipole moment, $P = \mu_0|\ddot{\vec p}|^2/6\pi c$ ([[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-7|EM Theorem §B11.3.7]]). The gravitational analogue of $\vec p = \sum_a q_a\vec x_a$ is $\vec p_G = \sum_a m_a\vec x_a$. For any isolated system of masses, Newton's third law gives

$$
\ddot{\vec p}_G = \sum_a m_a\ddot{\vec x}_a = \sum_a\vec F_a = \vec 0 ,
$$

because the internal forces cancel in pairs. For the Earth and the Sun, $\vec p_G = (M_\odot + M_\oplus)\vec X_{\rm cm}$, and the centre of mass moves uniformly. The dipole guess predicts no radiation at all, from any system. The reason is that all masses have the same sign and momentum is conserved; the monopole fails for the same reason with mass in place of momentum. So the first radiating term must come from a higher moment of the mass distribution. To find it, the section builds the retarded Green's function of $\Box$ ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-1|Theorem §C8.5.1]]) and the retarded solution for $\bar h_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-2|Theorem §C8.5.2]]). It then expands that solution far from a slowly moving source ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^mod-c8-5-3|Model §C8.5.3]]). Conservation of $T^{\mu\nu}$ turns the stresses into the second time derivative of the mass distribution ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-4|Theorem §C8.5.4]]), and the result is the quadrupole field ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-5|Theorem §C8.5.5]]). Conservation also proves the failure just seen ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-6|Theorem §C8.5.6]]). The energy and angular momentum carried away follow in [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-7|Theorem §C8.5.7]] and [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-8|Theorem §C8.5.8]].

**Setup.** Linearised gravity about Minkowski spacetime in an inertial chart ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^mod-c8-1-1|Model §C8.1.1]]) in de Donder gauge ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^def-c8-1-2|Def. §C8.1.2]]). The source is a given symmetric $T^{\mu\nu}$, smooth, conserved in the flat sense $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, and confined for all times to a ball $|\vec y| \le d$ about the origin (a world tube). Theorems §C8.5.1–§C8.5.2 and §C8.5.4, §C8.5.6 are exact within linearised theory. From [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^mod-c8-5-3|Model §C8.5.3]] on, results are leading order in $d/r$ and in the source's speed $v$. Sources held together by their own gravity are discussed in [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^cau-c8-5-2|Caution: Self-gravitating sources]]. The examples are a rotating rod and the Earth–Sun system, an oscillator on a spring, and the luminosity scale $c^5/G$; their numbers were checked by script.

Dots are $d/dt$; $\langle\cdot\rangle$ is the average over several periods ([[§C8.4 The Energy of Gravitational Waves#^def-c8-4-2|Def. §C8.4.2]]). ## The retarded Green's function

A point source that flashes once, at one event, is the building block of every source. The wave equation with that source has more than one solution, and the physical one is fixed by a condition that is not in the equation.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.5.1: The Retarded Green's Function of the d'Alembertian
> On $\mathbb R^4$ with inertial coordinates, write $\tau = t - t'$, $\vec\xi = \vec x - \vec x\,'$, $R = |\vec\xi|$.
> 1. The **retarded Green's function**, the distribution ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-2|QFT Def. §CA.2.2]])
>
> $$
> G_R(\tau, \vec\xi) = \frac{\delta(\tau - R)}{R}, \qquad G_R[\varphi] \equiv \int d^3\xi\,\frac{\varphi(R, \vec\xi)}{R} \quad (\varphi \in \mathcal D(\mathbb R^4)) ,
> $$
>
> solves $\Box G_R = -4\pi\,\delta^4$, with the derivative taken in the sense of distributions ([[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^def-ca-2-7|QFT Def. §CA.2.7]]).
> 2. $G_R$ vanishes for $\tau < 0$ and is supported on the future light cone $\tau = R$ itself, not inside it: a flash at $(t', \vec x\,')$ is felt at $\vec x$ exactly at $t = t' + |\vec x - \vec x\,'|$, the retarded time of [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^def-b11-1-2|EM Def. §B11.1.2]].
> 3. It is the only tempered solution of $\Box G = -4\pi\delta^4$ that vanishes for $\tau < 0$.
> 4. The **advanced Green's function** $G_A = \delta(\tau + R)/R$ also solves $\Box G_A = -4\pi\delta^4$ and is supported on the past cone. $G_R - G_A$ solves the homogeneous equation.
>
> Restored ($x^0 = ct$): $G_R = \delta\bigl(c(t - t') - R\bigr)/R = \delta(t - t' - R/c)/(cR)$.
>
> *Model:* Minkowski spacetime; *applies:* exact, a statement about the operator $\Box$ alone.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5, eqs. (2.113)–(2.123) (pp. 34–35) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §21.5, eqs. (21.63)–(21.74) (pp. 411–414)*

^thm-c8-5-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Fourier transform in time). Expand in time at fixed $\vec\xi$, and write the time delta function the same way ([[§CA.4 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-4-3|QFT Theorem §CA.4.3]], $n = 1$, as distributions in $\tau$):
>
> $$
> G(\tau, \vec\xi) = \int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,\tilde G(\omega, \vec\xi)\,e^{-i\omega\tau}, \qquad \delta(\tau) = \int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,e^{-i\omega\tau} .
> $$
>
> Since $-\partial_\tau^2e^{-i\omega\tau} = \omega^2e^{-i\omega\tau}$, the operator $\Box = -\partial_\tau^2 + \nabla^2$ acts on each mode as $\nabla^2 + \omega^2$ ([[§CA.4 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-4-4|QFT Theorem §CA.4.4]], 2). Comparing the two sides of $\Box G = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec\xi)\delta(\tau)$ mode by mode gives the **Helmholtz equation**
>
> $$
> (\nabla^2 + \omega^2)\,\tilde G(\omega, \vec\xi) = -4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec\xi) \qquad \text{for each } \omega .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (spherical waves away from the origin). Try $f_\pm = e^{\pm i\omega R}/R$. For a radial function $\nabla^2f = \frac1R\partial_R^2(Rf)$, and $Rf_\pm = e^{\pm i\omega R}$, so for $R > 0$
>
> $$
> \nabla^2f_\pm = \frac1R\,(\pm i\omega)^2e^{\pm i\omega R} = -\omega^2f_\pm , \qquad (\nabla^2 + \omega^2)f_\pm = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (the delta function at the origin). Let $\varphi \in \mathcal D(\mathbb R^3)$. Since $f_\pm$ is integrable near $0$, $\int f_\pm\nabla^2\varphi = \lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\int_{R > \varepsilon}f_\pm\nabla^2\varphi$. Green's second identity on $R > \varepsilon$, where the outward normal of the inner sphere is $-\hat\xi$, gives
>
> $$
> \int_{R > \varepsilon}\bigl(f_\pm\nabla^2\varphi - \varphi\nabla^2f_\pm\bigr)d^3\xi = -\oint_{R = \varepsilon}\bigl(f_\pm\,\partial_R\varphi - \varphi\,\partial_Rf_\pm\bigr)\varepsilon^2\,d\Omega .
> $$
>
> The first surface term is at most $4\pi\varepsilon\sup|\nabla\varphi| \to 0$. In the second, $\partial_Rf_\pm = (\pm i\omega/R - 1/R^2)e^{\pm i\omega R}$, so $\varepsilon^2\partial_Rf_\pm|_{R = \varepsilon} = (\pm i\omega\varepsilon - 1)e^{\pm i\omega\varepsilon} \to -1$, and $\oint\varphi\,\varepsilon^2\partial_Rf_\pm\,d\Omega \to -4\pi\varphi(0)$. With Step 2 on the left, $\int_{R > \varepsilon}\varphi\nabla^2f_\pm = -\omega^2\int_{R > \varepsilon}\varphi f_\pm$, and in the limit
>
> $$
> \int f_\pm\,\nabla^2\varphi\,d^3\xi = -\omega^2\int f_\pm\varphi\,d^3\xi - 4\pi\varphi(0) , \qquad\text{that is}\qquad (\nabla^2 + \omega^2)f_\pm = -4\pi\,\delta^3 \ \text{ in } \mathcal D'(\mathbb R^3) .
> $$
>
> For $\omega = 0$ this is $\nabla^2(1/R) = -4\pi\delta^3$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|EM Theorem §B1.2.2]]).
>
> **Step 4** (transform back; the choice). Insert $\tilde G = f_\pm$ into Step 1. At fixed $R > 0$, by [[§CA.4 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-4-3|QFT Theorem §CA.4.3]] shifted by $R$,
>
> $$
> G_\pm(\tau, \vec\xi) = \frac1R\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,e^{-i\omega\tau}e^{\pm i\omega R} = \frac1R\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,e^{-i\omega(\tau \mp R)} = \frac{\delta(\tau \mp R)}{R} .
> $$
>
> $G_+ = G_R$ vanishes for $\tau < 0$; $G_- = G_A$ lives at $\tau = -R \le 0$. The Helmholtz equation does not decide between them: $f_+ - f_- = 2i\sin(\omega R)/R$ is a regular solution of the homogeneous equation, so any combination $a(\omega)f_+ + (1 - a(\omega))f_-$ solves Step 1. The choice is a boundary condition in time, as the choice of contour is in [[§C2b.6 Green's Functions and Contours#^thm-c2b-6-3|QFT Theorem §C2b.6.3]]. Outgoing waves $e^{+i\omega R}$ (with $e^{-i\omega\tau}$) are the retarded ones.
>
> **Step 5** (direct check of part 1, no exchange of integrals). By definition of the distributional derivative ($\Box$ has even order), $(\Box G_R)[\varphi] = G_R[\Box\varphi] = \int d^3\xi\,(\Box\varphi)(R, \vec\xi)/R$. Put $\Phi(\vec\xi) = \varphi(R, \vec\xi)$ and $\psi(\vec\xi) = \partial_\tau\varphi(R, \vec\xi)$, the values on the cone. With $\nabla R = \hat\xi$, $\nabla\cdot\hat\xi = 2/R$ and the chain rule,
>
> $$
> \nabla\Phi = \bigl(\nabla\varphi + \hat\xi\,\partial_\tau\varphi\bigr)\big|_{\tau = R}, \qquad \nabla^2\Phi = \bigl(\nabla^2\varphi + 2\hat\xi\cdot\nabla\partial_\tau\varphi + \partial_\tau^2\varphi\bigr)\big|_{\tau = R} + \frac2R\,\psi ,
> $$
>
> and $\partial_R\psi = \hat\xi\cdot\nabla\psi = \bigl(\hat\xi\cdot\nabla\partial_\tau\varphi + \partial_\tau^2\varphi\bigr)|_{\tau = R}$. Eliminating the mixed derivative,
>
> $$
> (\Box\varphi)\big|_{\tau = R} = \bigl(-\partial_\tau^2\varphi + \nabla^2\varphi\bigr)\big|_{\tau = R} = \nabla^2\Phi - 2\partial_R\psi - \frac2R\psi = \nabla^2\Phi - \frac2R\,\partial_R(R\psi) .
> $$
>
> Hence, in spherical coordinates ($d^3\xi = R^2dR\,d\Omega$),
>
> $$
> G_R[\Box\varphi] = \int d^3\xi\,\frac{\nabla^2\Phi}{R} - 2\int d\Omega\int_0^\infty dR\,\partial_R(R\psi) = \int d^3\xi\,\frac{\nabla^2\Phi}{R} - 2\int d\Omega\,\bigl[R\psi\bigr]_0^\infty = \int d^3\xi\,\frac{\nabla^2\Phi}{R} ,
> $$
>
> since $R\psi$ vanishes at $R = 0$ and for large $R$. $\Phi$ is continuous with bounded first derivatives and $\nabla^2\Phi = O(1/R)$ near $0$, so the surface-term argument of Step 3 with $\omega = 0$ applies unchanged and gives $\int d^3\xi\,\nabla^2\Phi/R = -4\pi\Phi(0) = -4\pi\varphi(0, \vec 0)$. So $(\Box G_R)[\varphi] = -4\pi\varphi(0)$: part 1. (The identity of this step was checked by script at random points for an explicit $\varphi$.)
>
> **Step 6** (parts 2–4). Part 2 is read off the formula: $\delta(\tau - R)$ vanishes off $\tau = R \ge 0$. For part 3, in the QFT notes (signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, $\partial^2 = -\Box$, normalization $\partial^2D_R = -i\delta^4$) the massless retarded function is $D_R = -\frac{i}{4\pi R}\delta(\tau - R)$ ([[§C2b.6 Green's Functions and Contours#^thm-c2b-6-6|QFT Theorem §C2b.6.6]], 3). So $G_R = 4\pi i\,D_R$, and $\Box G_R = 4\pi i\,(-\partial^2D_R) = 4\pi i\cdot i\delta^4 = -4\pi\delta^4$, as in part 1. That theorem proves that $D_R$ is the only tempered fundamental solution vanishing for $\tau < 0$, and the factor $4\pi i$ carries this over. Part 4: $\Box$ contains $\tau$ only through $\partial_\tau^2$, so Step 5 with $\tau \to -\tau$ gives $\Box G_A = -4\pi\delta^4$; the difference of two fundamental solutions is homogeneous ([[§CA.7 Boundary Values, iε Limits and Fundamental Solutions#^thm-ca-7-7|QFT Theorem §CA.7.7]], 1).
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Fourier transforming in time turns the wave equation into one Helmholtz equation per frequency. The outgoing spherical wave $e^{i\omega R}/R$ is the retarded choice, exactly as in the radiation of a point source ([[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^thm-b7-1-5|WO Theorem §B7.1.5]]).
> - Every step with $\delta$ is a statement about pairings with test functions; the $1/R$ singularity is harmless because $d^3\xi$ supplies $R^2$.
> - Causality is an extra input: the equation is symmetric in time, and choosing retarded over advanced chooses the arrow of time ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^rem-b11-1-4|EM §B11.1, Remark: Looking back along the light cone]]).

## The retarded solution

A general source is a superposition of flashes, and the field equation is linear: the field is the superposition of their retarded Green's functions.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.5.2: The Retarded Solution of the Linearised Field Equation
> Let $T_{\mu\nu}$ be as in the Setup: smooth, symmetric, $\partial^\mu T_{\mu\nu} = 0$, zero outside the world tube $|\vec y| \le d$.
> 1. The field
>
> $$
> \bar h_{\mu\nu}(t, \vec x) = 4\int d^4y\;G_R(x - y)\,T_{\mu\nu}(y) = 4\int d^3y\;\frac{T_{\mu\nu}\bigl(t - |\vec x - \vec y|, \vec y\bigr)}{|\vec x - \vec y|}
> $$
>
> solves the linearised field equation $\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} = -16\pi T_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^thm-c8-1-5|Theorem §C8.1.5]]). Its value at $(t, \vec x)$ depends only on $T$ on the past light cone of that event.
> 2. It satisfies the de Donder condition $\partial^\mu\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^def-c8-1-2|Def. §C8.1.2]]) because $T$ is conserved: the gauge assumed in deriving the field equation is reproduced by its retarded solution.
> 3. Any other solution differs from it by a solution of $\Box\bar h = 0$, a wave coming in from outside. The retarded solution is the one with no incoming radiation.
>
> Restored: $\bar h_{\mu\nu}(t, \vec x) = \dfrac{4G}{c^4}\displaystyle\int d^3y\,\frac{T_{\mu\nu}(t - |\vec x - \vec y|/c, \vec y)}{|\vec x - \vec y|}$, with $T_{\mu\nu}$ an energy density (J m⁻³).
>
> *Model:* [[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^mod-c8-1-1|Model §C8.1.1]]; *applies:* exact in linearised theory, inside and outside the source; electrodynamics has the same formula for $A_\mu$ ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-4|EM Theorem §B11.1.4]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5, eq. (2.124) (p. 36) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §21.5, eqs. (21.75)–(21.77) (p. 414), signature $+{-}{-}{-}$: his $\Phi_{ab}$, with $\Box\Phi_{ab} = -2\kappa T_{ab}$ for his $\Box = \partial_t^2 - \nabla^2$, equals minus our $\bar h_{\mu\nu}$*

^thm-c8-5-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the integral is finite). Fix $x = (t, \vec x)$. As a function of $y$, $G_R(x - y)$ lives on the past cone $y^0 = t - |\vec x - \vec y|$. On the world tube $|\vec y| \le d$, the triangle inequality gives $|\vec x| - d \le |\vec x - \vec y| \le |\vec x| + d$, so the cone meets the tube in the compact set $\{|\vec y| \le d,\ t - |\vec x| - d \le y^0 \le t - |\vec x| + d\}$. The integrand has compact support.
>
> **Step 2** (the delta function does the time integral). $\delta(t - y^0 - R) = \delta\bigl(y^0 - (t - R)\bigr)$ (the delta function is even, [[§CA.2 Generalized Functions#^thm-ca-2-6|QFT Theorem §CA.2.6]], 1, with $\lambda = -1$), so with $R = |\vec x - \vec y|$
>
> $$
> 4\int d^3y\int dy^0\,\frac{\delta\bigl(y^0 - (t - R)\bigr)}{R}\,T_{\mu\nu}(y^0, \vec y) = 4\int d^3y\,\frac{T_{\mu\nu}(t - R, \vec y)}{R} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (it solves the equation). Derivatives in $x$ act on $G_R(x - y)$ only, and $\Box G_R = -4\pi\delta^4$ ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-1|Theorem §C8.5.1]]):
>
> $$
> \Box_x\bar h_{\mu\nu}(x) = 4\int d^4y\,(\Box G_R)(x - y)\,T_{\mu\nu}(y) = 4\int d^4y\,\bigl(-4\pi\,\delta^4(x - y)\bigr)T_{\mu\nu}(y) = -16\pi\,T_{\mu\nu}(x) .
> $$
>
> This is the convolution theorem [[§CA.7 Boundary Values, iε Limits and Fundamental Solutions#^thm-ca-7-8|QFT Theorem §CA.7.8]] (there for sources of compact support; here Step 1 supplies the compactness, and the QFT normalization $P(\partial)G = -i\delta$ is replaced by $\Box G_R = -4\pi\delta^4$).
>
> **Step 4** (de Donder gauge). $G_R(x - y)$ depends on $x - y$, so $\partial^\mu_xG_R(x - y) = -\partial^\mu_yG_R(x - y)$. Moving the derivative onto $T$ (no boundary terms, by Step 1):
>
> $$
> \partial_x^\mu\bar h_{\mu\nu}(x) = 4\int d^4y\,\partial^\mu_xG_R(x - y)\,T_{\mu\nu}(y) = -4\int d^4y\,\partial^\mu_yG_R(x - y)\,T_{\mu\nu}(y) = 4\int d^4y\,G_R(x - y)\,\partial^\mu T_{\mu\nu}(y) = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (other solutions). If $\bar h'$ also solves $\Box\bar h' = -16\pi T$, then $\Box(\bar h' - \bar h) = -16\pi T + 16\pi T = 0$ by linearity ([[§CA.7 Boundary Values, iε Limits and Fundamental Solutions#^thm-ca-7-7|QFT Theorem §CA.7.7]], 1).
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Each component of $\bar h_{\mu\nu}$ is a retarded potential with source $4T_{\mu\nu}$: a disturbance of the source reaches $\vec x$ after the light travel time, which answers the chapter's question.
> - Conservation of $T$ (Step 4) and the gauge condition are the same statement; without it the retarded solution would solve the field equation only in a gauge that the field does not satisfy.

## Far from a slow source

The retarded integral is exact but says little until the source is specified. Most sources of interest are small compared with the distance to the detector and slow compared with light. Expanding in these two small numbers reduces the integral to a few moments of the source.

> [!model] Model §C8.5.3: Far Zone and Slow Source
> *Source.* $T^{\mu\nu}$ as in [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-2|Theorem §C8.5.2]], inside $|\vec y| \le d$, with typical internal speed $v$. It changes on the time scale $t_s \sim d/v$, so it emits at reduced wavelength $\bar\lambda \sim t_s$. The field point is $\vec x = r\vec n$, $|\vec n| = 1$.
>
> *Assumptions, and what each one drops.*
> 1. **Far zone**, $r \gg d$: $1/|\vec x - \vec y| = 1/r + O(d/r^2)$, so in the amplitude the whole source is at distance $r$.
> 2. **Slow source**, $v \ll 1$, equivalently $d \ll \bar\lambda$: the source is small compared with the wavelength it emits. In the retarded time $t - |\vec x - \vec y| = t - r + \vec n\cdot\vec y + O(d^2/r)$, the shift $\vec n\cdot\vec y \le d$ is small compared with $t_s$, so $T(t - r + \vec n\cdot\vec y) = T(t - r) + (\vec n\cdot\vec y)\,\partial_tT + \dots$ with each term smaller than the previous by $d/\bar\lambda \sim v$. Keeping the first term drops the higher multipoles (mass octupole, current quadrupole).
> 3. **Wave zone**, $r \gg \bar\lambda$: in $\partial_i\bigl[f(t - r)/r\bigr] = -n_i\dot f/r - n_if/r^2$ the second term is smaller by $\bar\lambda/r$. Only the $1/r$ part, the radiation, is kept.
> 4. **Weak field and Newtonian matter**: $|h| \ll 1$ everywhere, including inside the source; $T^{00} = \rho\,(1 + O(v^2))$ is the mass density, and the stresses $T^{ij} \sim \rho v^iv^j + p\,\delta^{ij}$ are smaller by $v^2$.
>
> - *Assumes:* $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ in the flat sense, which holds when the forces holding the source together are not gravitational ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^cau-c8-5-2|Caution: Self-gravitating sources]]).
> - *Analogy:* the same three zones and the slow-source condition $d \ll \lambda$ define electric dipole radiation ([[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-7|EM Theorem §B11.3.7]], its two conditions on $\ell$).
> - *Fails:* for sources moving near the speed of light or with $M/d$ near one (merging black holes, core collapse), where it gives only orders of magnitude ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^ex-c8-5-3|Example §C8.5.3]]); inside the near zone $r \lesssim \bar\lambda$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5, eqs. (2.126)–(2.128) and footnote 6 (p. 36) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §21.5, eqs. (21.80)–(21.86) (pp. 414–415) · Schutz, §9.4 (p. 233) · Hartle, §23.8 (p. 510)*

In the far zone the integrand of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-2|Theorem §C8.5.2]] becomes $\frac4r\int T_{kl}(t - r, \vec y)\,d^3y$: the field is the integrated stress of the source. > [!theorem] Theorem §C8.5.4: The Tensor Virial Identity
> Let $T^{\mu\nu}$ be smooth, symmetric, $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, and zero outside $|\vec y| \le d$. Then at every time
>
> $$
> \int T^{0i}\,d^3y = \frac{d}{dt}\int y^iT^{00}\,d^3y, \qquad \int\bigl(y^iT^{0j} + y^jT^{0i}\bigr)d^3y = \frac{d}{dt}\int y^iy^jT^{00}\,d^3y, \qquad \int T^{ij}\,d^3y = \frac12\,\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\int y^iy^jT^{00}\,d^3y .
> $$
>
> The integrated stress equals half the second time derivative of the second moment of the energy density.
>
> *Model:* the Setup's source; *applies:* exact for flat-space conservation; for self-gravitating sources see [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^cau-c8-5-2|Caution: Self-gravitating sources]].
>
> *Source: 't Hooft, ch. 15, eq. (15.31) (p. 68) · Couzens, GR2, §2.5, eqs. (2.129)–(2.132) (p. 37) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §21.6, eqs. (21.89)–(21.93) (p. 416) · Schutz, §9.4, eq. (9.81) (p. 233) · Hartle, §23.4, eqs. (23.32)–(23.33) (p. 499)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> The conservation law $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ has a time and a space part, $\partial_tT^{00} + \partial_kT^{k0} = 0$ and $\partial_tT^{0j} + \partial_kT^{kj} = 0$. Every integration by parts below has no surface term, because $T$ vanishes outside $|\vec y| \le d$ (divergence theorem, [[§32 Flux Integrals and the Divergence Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-32-1|452 Thm. §32.1]]). Write $\partial_ky^i = \delta^i_k$.
>
> **Step 1** (stress to momentum flux). Insert $\delta^i_k$, integrate by parts, use the space part of conservation:
>
> $$
> \int T^{ij}d^3y = \int T^{kj}\,\partial_ky^i\,d^3y = -\int y^i\,\partial_kT^{kj}\,d^3y = \int y^i\,\partial_tT^{0j}\,d^3y = \frac{d}{dt}\int y^iT^{0j}\,d^3y .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (symmetrize). $T^{ij} = T^{ji}$, so the same holds with $i \leftrightarrow j$; averaging the two,
>
> $$
> \int T^{ij}d^3y = \frac12\,\frac{d}{dt}\int\bigl(y^iT^{0j} + y^jT^{0i}\bigr)d^3y .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (momentum to energy). Since $\partial_k(y^iy^j) = \delta^i_ky^j + y^i\delta^j_k$, and with the time part of conservation,
>
> $$
> \int\bigl(y^iT^{0j} + y^jT^{0i}\bigr)d^3y = \int T^{0k}\,\partial_k(y^iy^j)\,d^3y = -\int y^iy^j\,\partial_kT^{k0}\,d^3y = \int y^iy^j\,\partial_tT^{00}\,d^3y = \frac{d}{dt}\int y^iy^jT^{00}\,d^3y .
> $$
>
> This is the second identity. The first is Step 3 with $y^iy^j$ replaced by $y^i$: $\int T^{0i} = \int T^{0k}\partial_ky^i = -\int y^i\partial_kT^{k0} = \frac{d}{dt}\int y^iT^{00}$.
>
> ⚑ By-product: the first identity says that the mass dipole changes at the rate of the total momentum, which is conserved. This kills dipole radiation → [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-6|Theorem §C8.5.6]].
>
> **Step 4** (combine). Insert Step 3 into Step 2:
>
> $$
> \int T^{ij}d^3y = \frac12\,\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\int y^iy^jT^{00}\,d^3y .
> $$
>
> **Second route** (Couzens, d'Inverno: a pointwise identity first). Conservation twice gives $\partial_t^2T^{00} = -\partial_t\partial_kT^{k0} = -\partial_k\partial_tT^{0k} = \partial_k\partial_lT^{kl}$. Multiplying by $y^iy^j$ and moving the derivatives out with the product rule,
>
> $$
> \partial_t^2\bigl(y^iy^jT^{00}\bigr) = \partial_m\partial_n\bigl(y^iy^jT^{mn}\bigr) - 2\,\partial_m\bigl(y^jT^{im} + y^iT^{jm}\bigr) + 2T^{ij} ,
> $$
>
> an identity checked by script for arbitrary symmetric $T^{mn}$. Integrated over space the two divergences drop out, which is Step 4 again.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The stresses of a conserved source are fixed by how its energy density moves. Whatever holds the source together (a spring, a rod's elasticity, gravity) is in $T^{ij}$, and the identity says its integral equals $\frac12\ddot M^{ij}$, which can be read off the motion alone.
> - The identity was also checked by script on an explicit conserved, time-dependent source built from a superpotential.

The integral on the right is a property of the mass distribution alone. It and its relatives get names.

> [!definition] Definition §C8.5.1: Mass Moments of a Source
> Fix a Cartesian frame of $\mathbb R^3$ with origin $O$. The **moments** of the energy density $T^{00}(t, \vec y)$ of a source are
>
> $$
> M(t) = \int T^{00}\,d^3y, \qquad M^i(t) = \int y^i\,T^{00}\,d^3y, \qquad M^{ij}(t) = \int y^iy^j\,T^{00}\,d^3y :
> $$
>
> the zeroth moment (total mass-energy), the first moment (mass dipole) and the **second mass moment**. Under a rotation $\vec y \to A\vec y$ of the frame ($A \in SO(3)$) they transform as a scalar, a vector and a symmetric 2-tensor on $\mathbb R^3$; under a shift of the origin $\vec y \to \vec y + \vec a$, $M^i \to M^i + a^iM$ and $M^{ij} \to M^{ij} + a^iM^j + a^jM^i + a^ia^jM$. In a slow source $T^{00} = \rho$, the mass density. The inertia tensor about $O$ ([[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^def-b10-1-1|CM Def. §B10.1.1]]) is $I_{ij} = \delta_{ij}M^{kk} - M^{ij}$.
>
> Names in the sources: Couzens $M^{ij}$, "second moment of the energy density"; d'Inverno $I^{ij}$, "second mass-moment"; Schutz and Hartle $I^{ij}$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5, Definition 1, eqs. (2.133)–(2.134) (p. 37) · d'Inverno & Vickers, eq. (21.95) (p. 417) · Schutz, eq. (9.82a) (p. 233) · Hartle, §23.4, eq. (23.34) and footnote 2 (p. 499)*

> [!definition] Definition §C8.5.2: Reduced Quadrupole Moment
> The **reduced (trace-free) quadrupole moment** of a source is the traceless part of its second mass moment ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-1|Def. §C8.5.1]]),
>
> $$
> Q^{ij}(t) = M^{ij} - \frac13\,\delta^{ij}M^{kk} = \int T^{00}\Bigl(y^iy^j - \frac13|\vec y|^2\delta^{ij}\Bigr)d^3y .
> $$
>
> It is a symmetric traceless 2-tensor on $\mathbb R^3$, five components. $M^{ij} = Q^{ij} + \frac13\delta^{ij}M^{kk}$ splits the symmetric tensor into its two parts that do not mix under rotations: the trace, a scalar, and the traceless part ($\ell = 2$). A spherically symmetric mass distribution has $Q = 0$.
>
> Conventions differ by constant factors. Schutz's and Hartle's $I\!\!\!-^{ij}$ and Couzens' $Q^{ij}$ are this one. The electrostatic quadrupole of the EM notes is $\frac12\int(3y_iy_j - |\vec y|^2\delta_{ij})\rho_e$, that is $\frac32$ times this with charge density ([[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^rem-b4-1-3|EM §B4.1, Remark: The quadrupole moment tensor]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5, eqs. (2.144)–(2.145) (p. 39) · Schutz, eq. (9.87) (p. 234) · Hartle, eq. (23.50) (p. 506) · 't Hooft, eqs. (15.36), (15.39) (p. 69)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.5.5: The Quadrupole Field
> In [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^mod-c8-5-3|Model §C8.5.3]], at $\vec x = r\vec n$, to leading order in $1/r$ and in $v$, with all moments ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-1|Def. §C8.5.1]], [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-2|Def. §C8.5.2]]) evaluated at the retarded time $t - r$:
> 1. The spatial components are
>
> $$
> \bar h_{ij}(t, \vec x) = \frac2r\,\ddot M_{ij}(t - r) ,
> $$
>
> and the time components follow from the de Donder condition: $\bar h_{0j} = -n_i\bar h_{ij}$ and $\bar h_{00} = n_in_j\bar h_{ij}$, up to time-independent terms.
> 2. The transverse-traceless part ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-1|Def. §C8.2.1]]), with the projector $\Lambda_{ij,kl}(\vec n)$ of [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-3|Def. §C8.2.3]], is
>
> $$
> h^{\rm TT}_{ij}(t, \vec x) = \frac2r\,\Lambda_{ij,kl}(\vec n)\,\ddot Q_{kl}(t - r) \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } h^{\rm TT}_{ij} = \frac{2G}{c^4r}\,\Lambda_{ij,kl}\,\ddot Q_{kl}(t - r/c)\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 3. For $\vec n = \vec e_z$ the polarisations ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-2|Def. §C8.2.2]]) are
>
> $$
> h_+ = \frac1r\bigl(\ddot M_{11} - \ddot M_{22}\bigr), \qquad h_\times = \frac2r\,\ddot M_{12} .
> $$
>
> 4. For $\vec n = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi, \sin\theta\sin\varphi, \cos\theta)$ take the polarisation axes of [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-2|Def. §C8.2.2]] to be $\vec u = \hat\theta$, $\vec v = \hat\varphi$ (so that $\vec u\times\vec v = \vec n$); then $h_+ = \frac12(u_iu_j - v_iv_j)h^{\rm TT}_{ij}$ and $h_\times = u_iv_jh^{\rm TT}_{ij}$. Then $h_+ = \frac1r(\ddot M_{uu} - \ddot M_{vv})$ and $h_\times = \frac2r\ddot M_{uv}$, where $\ddot M_{uv} = u_i\ddot M_{ij}v_j$. Explicitly,
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> r\,h_+ &= \ddot M_{11}(\cos^2\theta\cos^2\varphi - \sin^2\varphi) + \ddot M_{22}(\cos^2\theta\sin^2\varphi - \cos^2\varphi) + \ddot M_{33}\sin^2\theta \\
> &\quad + \ddot M_{12}\sin2\varphi\,(1 + \cos^2\theta) - \ddot M_{13}\sin2\theta\cos\varphi - \ddot M_{23}\sin2\theta\sin\varphi , \\
> r\,h_\times &= -(\ddot M_{11} - \ddot M_{22})\cos\theta\sin2\varphi + 2\ddot M_{12}\cos\theta\cos2\varphi + 2\ddot M_{13}\sin\theta\sin\varphi - 2\ddot M_{23}\sin\theta\cos\varphi .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> *Model:* [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^mod-c8-5-3|Model §C8.5.3]]; *applies:* far and wave zone, slow source, leading order. The time components of $\bar h$ carry no TT part.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5, eqs. (2.125)–(2.148) (pp. 36–39) · d'Inverno & Vickers, eqs. (21.94)–(21.96) (p. 417) · Schutz, eqs. (9.83)–(9.86) (pp. 233–234) · Hartle, eq. (23.35) (p. 499)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (far-zone distance). With $\vec x = r\vec n$, $|\vec x - \vec y|^2 = r^2 - 2r\,\vec n\cdot\vec y + |\vec y|^2$, so
>
> $$
> |\vec x - \vec y| = r\Bigl(1 - \frac{2\vec n\cdot\vec y}{r} + \frac{|\vec y|^2}{r^2}\Bigr)^{1/2} = r - \vec n\cdot\vec y + \frac{|\vec y|^2 - (\vec n\cdot\vec y)^2}{2r} + O\Bigl(\frac{d^3}{r^2}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> from $(1 + \epsilon)^{1/2} = 1 + \frac\epsilon2 - \frac{\epsilon^2}8 + \dots$ with $\epsilon = -2\vec n\cdot\vec y/r + |\vec y|^2/r^2$ (checked by script).
>
> **Step 2** (insert into the retarded solution). In [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-2|Theorem §C8.5.2]] the amplitude is $1/|\vec x - \vec y| = 1/r + O(d/r^2)$ (assumption 1), and the time argument is $t - r + \vec n\cdot\vec y + O(d^2/r)$. Taylor expanding in time,
>
> $$
> T_{kl}\bigl(t - r + \vec n\cdot\vec y, \vec y\bigr) = T_{kl}(t - r, \vec y) + (\vec n\cdot\vec y)\,\partial_tT_{kl}(t - r, \vec y) + \dots ,
> $$
>
> and the second term is smaller by $d/\bar\lambda \sim v$ (assumption 2). Keeping the leading term,
>
> $$
> \bar h_{kl}(t, \vec x) = \frac4r\int T_{kl}(t - r, \vec y)\,d^3y .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (virial identity). By [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-4|Theorem §C8.5.4]], $\int T^{kl}d^3y = \frac12\ddot M^{kl}$, and spatial indices are lowered with $\delta$ ($T_{kl} = T^{kl}$). Hence $\bar h_{kl} = \frac4r\cdot\frac12\ddot M_{kl}(t - r) = \frac2r\ddot M_{kl}(t - r)$: part 1, spatial components.
>
> **Step 4** (time components). By [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-2|Theorem §C8.5.2]], 2, $\partial^\mu\bar h_{\mu\nu} = -\partial_t\bar h_{0\nu} + \partial_i\bar h_{i\nu} = 0$. For $\bar h_{i\nu} = f_{i\nu}(t - r)/r$ the wave zone (assumption 3) gives $\partial_i\bar h_{i\nu} = -n_i\dot f_{i\nu}/r$, so $\partial_t(\bar h_{0\nu} + n_i\bar h_{i\nu}) = 0$. With $\nu = j$: $\bar h_{0j} = -n_i\bar h_{ij}$ up to a constant. With $\nu = 0$: $\bar h_{00} = -n_i\bar h_{i0} = n_in_j\bar h_{ij}$, again up to constants. The constants are the static terms of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-6|Theorem §C8.5.6]]. Part 1.
>
> **Step 5** (TT projection; $M$ may be replaced by $Q$). To leading order in $1/r$, near any far point the field is a function of $t - r$ alone, i.e. locally a plane wave along $\vec n$ (Step 4 used exactly this). Outside the source the TT gauge exists ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-2|Theorem §C8.2.2]]), and for a plane wave along $\vec n$ its components are $h^{\rm TT}_{ij} = \Lambda_{ij,kl}(\vec n)\,h_{kl}$ ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-4|Theorem §C8.2.4]]). $\Lambda$ annihilates $\delta_{kl}$: with $P_{ij} = \delta_{ij} - n_in_j$ and $P_{kk} = 2$,
>
> $$
> \Lambda_{ij,kl}\,\delta_{kl} = P_{ik}P_{jk} - \tfrac12P_{ij}P_{kk} = P_{ij} - P_{ij} = 0 .
> $$
>
> So the trace terms in $h_{kl} = \bar h_{kl} - \frac12\delta_{kl}\bar h$ ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^def-c8-1-1|Def. §C8.1.1]]) and in $M_{kl} = Q_{kl} + \frac13\delta_{kl}M_{mm}$ drop out:
>
> $$
> h^{\rm TT}_{ij} = \Lambda_{ij,kl}\bar h_{kl} = \frac2r\,\Lambda_{ij,kl}\ddot M_{kl} = \frac2r\,\Lambda_{ij,kl}\ddot Q_{kl} .
> $$
>
> Part 2. Restoring units multiplies $\bar h$ by $G/c^4$ (Theorem §C8.5.2) and replaces $t - r$ by $t - r/c$.
>
> **Step 6** ($\vec n = \vec e_z$). Here $P = \operatorname{diag}(1, 1, 0)$, and for a symmetric $A$, $(\Lambda A)_{ij} = (PAP)_{ij} - \frac12P_{ij}\operatorname{tr}(PA)$ with $\operatorname{tr}(PA) = A_{11} + A_{22}$:
>
> $$
> (\Lambda A)_{11} = A_{11} - \tfrac12(A_{11} + A_{22}) = \tfrac12(A_{11} - A_{22}), \quad (\Lambda A)_{22} = -\tfrac12(A_{11} - A_{22}), \quad (\Lambda A)_{12} = (\Lambda A)_{21} = A_{12} ,
> $$
>
> and all components with an index $3$ vanish. With $A = \ddot M$ and $h_+ = h^{\rm TT}_{11}$, $h_\times = h^{\rm TT}_{12}$: $h_+ = \frac2r\cdot\frac12(\ddot M_{11} - \ddot M_{22})$, $h_\times = \frac2r\ddot M_{12}$. Part 3.
>
> **Step 7** (general direction). For a unit vector $\vec u \perp \vec n$, $P\vec u = \vec u$ and $\vec u\cdot P\vec u = 1$, so
>
> $$
> u_iu_j\Lambda_{ij,kl} = (P\vec u)_k(P\vec u)_l - \tfrac12(\vec u\cdot P\vec u)P_{kl} = u_ku_l - \tfrac12P_{kl}, \qquad u_iv_j\Lambda_{ij,kl} = u_kv_l - \tfrac12(\vec u\cdot P\vec v)P_{kl} = u_kv_l ,
> $$
>
> the last because $\vec u\cdot\vec v = 0$. Hence
>
> $$
> h_+ = \frac12\cdot\frac2r\Bigl[\bigl(u_ku_l - \tfrac12P_{kl}\bigr) - \bigl(v_kv_l - \tfrac12P_{kl}\bigr)\Bigr]\ddot M_{kl} = \frac1r\bigl(\ddot M_{uu} - \ddot M_{vv}\bigr), \qquad h_\times = \frac2r\,u_kv_l\ddot M_{kl} = \frac2r\,\ddot M_{uv} .
> $$
>
> With $\hat\theta = (\cos\theta\cos\varphi, \cos\theta\sin\varphi, -\sin\theta)$ and $\hat\varphi = (-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi, 0)$, expanding $\ddot M_{\hat\theta\hat\theta} - \ddot M_{\hat\varphi\hat\varphi}$ and $2\ddot M_{\hat\theta\hat\varphi}$ term by term gives the explicit formulas of part 4; for instance the $\ddot M_{11}$ coefficient of $rh_+$ is $\hat\theta_1^2 - \hat\varphi_1^2 = \cos^2\theta\cos^2\varphi - \sin^2\varphi$. All coefficients were checked by script, and at $\theta = \varphi = 0$ the formulas reduce to part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Only the second time derivative of the mass distribution radiates at leading order; the stresses entered only through the virial identity.
> - The trace of $M^{ij}$ (spherical "breathing") drops out of the TT field: what radiates is the change of shape, $Q^{ij}$.
> - In each direction only the two components of $\ddot Q$ transverse to $\vec n$ are seen; the other three of its five components radiate in other directions.

*Uses:* [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^mod-c8-5-3|Model §C8.5.3]], [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-2|Theorem §C8.5.2]], [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-4|Theorem §C8.5.4]], [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-1|Def. §C8.5.1]], [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-2|Def. §C8.5.2]], [[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom#^def-c8-1-1|Def. §C8.1.1]], [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-2|Theorem §C8.2.2]], [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-3|Def. §C8.2.3]], [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-4|Theorem §C8.2.4]], [[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^def-c8-2-2|Def. §C8.2.2]]

The opening guess, radiation from the mass dipole, can now be settled from conservation alone.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.5.6: No Monopole or Dipole Gravitational Radiation
> Let $T^{\mu\nu}$ be smooth, symmetric, $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, zero outside $|\vec y| \le d$, with the moments of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-1|Def. §C8.5.1]].
> 1. The mass is constant: $\dot M = 0$.
> 2. The mass dipole moves uniformly: $\dot M^i = P^i \equiv \int T^{0i}d^3y$, the total momentum, and $\dot P^i = 0$, so $\ddot M^i = 0$.
> 3. The angular momentum $J^{ij} = \int(y^iT^{0j} - y^jT^{0i})\,d^3y$ is constant.
> 4. Hence in the far field of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-2|Theorem §C8.5.2]] the monopole and dipole terms at order $1/r$ are time-independent, $\bar h_{00} \supset 4M/r + 4\,\vec n\cdot\vec P/r$ and $\bar h_{0i} \supset -4P^i/r$: the stationary field of a mass moving with momentum $\vec P$. Every time-dependent term at order $1/r$ contains $\ddot M^{ij}$ or higher derivatives: the lowest radiating multipole is the quadrupole.
> 5. A source that stays spherically symmetric has $Q^{ij} = 0$ and emits no quadrupole radiation, in agreement with the exact statement of [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-6|Theorem §C6.2.6]].
>
> In electrodynamics the dipole $\vec p = \int\vec y\rho_e$ can accelerate, because charges of both signs move against each other, and electric dipole radiation is the leading term ([[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-4|EM Theorem §B11.3.4]]). Charge conservation forbids only monopole radiation ([[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^rem-b11-3-4|EM §B11.3, Remark: Monopoles, multipoles and selection]]). In gravity momentum conservation forbids the dipole as well, and angular-momentum conservation forbids the analogue of magnetic dipole radiation.
>
> *Model:* flat-space conservation, the Setup's source; *applies:* parts 1–3 exact; part 4 at the order of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^mod-c8-5-3|Model §C8.5.3]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5, eq. (2.134) (p. 37) (see [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^cau-c8-5-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens' §2.5]]) · Schutz, §9.4, eqs. (9.77)–(9.80) (p. 233) · Hartle, Problem 23.6*

^thm-c8-5-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> As in Derivation §C8.5.4, integrals of divergences vanish.
>
> **Step 1** (part 1). $\dot M = \int\partial_tT^{00}d^3y = -\int\partial_kT^{k0}d^3y = 0$.
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). $\dot M^i = \int y^i\partial_tT^{00} = -\int y^i\partial_kT^{k0} = \int T^{k0}\partial_ky^i = \int T^{i0} = P^i$, and $\dot P^i = \int\partial_tT^{0i} = -\int\partial_kT^{ki} = 0$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 3). By Step 1 of Derivation §C8.5.4, $\frac{d}{dt}\int y^iT^{0j} = \int T^{ij}$, which is symmetric in $i, j$. So $\dot J^{ij} = \int T^{ij} - \int T^{ji} = 0$.
>
> **Step 4** (part 4). Expand $\bar h_{00}$ of Theorem §C8.5.2 to order $1/r$, keeping the first correction in the time argument and in the amplitude, $1/|\vec x - \vec y| = 1/r + \vec n\cdot\vec y/r^2 + \dots$:
>
> $$
> \bar h_{00} = \frac4r\int\bigl[T^{00}(t - r) + (\vec n\cdot\vec y)\,\partial_tT^{00}(t - r)\bigr]d^3y + \frac{4}{r^2}\int(\vec n\cdot\vec y)\,T^{00}\,d^3y + \dots = \frac{4M}r + \frac{4\,n_i\dot M^i}r + \frac{4\,n_iM^i}{r^2} + \dots .
> $$
>
> By Steps 1–2, $M$ and $\dot M^i = P^i$ are constant: the $1/r$ terms are $4M/r + 4\vec n\cdot\vec P/r$, static. For $\bar h_{0i}$, $T_{0i} = -T^{0i}$ gives the leading term $\frac4r\int T_{0i} = -4P^i/r$, constant; the next term, $-\frac4rn_k\frac{d}{dt}\int y^kT^{0i}$, is by Derivation §C8.5.4, Step 1, $-\frac4rn_k\int T^{ki} = -\frac2rn_k\ddot M^{ki}$, the quadrupole term of Theorem §C8.5.5, part 1. The spatial components start at $\frac2r\ddot M_{ij}$. So no term at order $1/r$ varies in time unless it contains $\ddot M^{ij}$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 5). If $T^{00}(t, \vec y) = \rho(t, |\vec y|)$, the angular integral of $y^iy^j$ gives $\frac13\delta^{ij}|\vec y|^2$ ([[§CA.4 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-4-8|QFT Theorem §CA.4.8]], 2), so $M^{ij} = \frac13\delta^{ij}M^{kk}$ and $Q^{ij} = 0$ for all $t$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The opening computation $\ddot{\vec p}_G = 0$ was Step 2 for point masses: momentum conservation is Newton's third law.
> - The field's static $1/r$ terms are the linearised far field of a moving mass; they carry the conserved charges $M$, $\vec P$, $J$ to infinity and do not change in time.

## The power and angular momentum carried off

The energy a wave carries through a large sphere per unit time is $\frac{r^2}{32\pi}\oint d\Omega\,\langle\dot h^{\rm TT}_{ij}\dot h^{\rm TT}_{ij}\rangle$ ([[§C8.4 The Energy of Gravitational Waves#^thm-c8-4-4|Theorem §C8.4.4]]). Inserting the quadrupole field leaves an integral of $\Lambda$ over directions.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.5.7: The Quadrupole Formula for the Radiated Power
> In [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^mod-c8-5-3|Model §C8.5.3]], with $Q$ ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-2|Def. §C8.5.2]]) at the retarded time and $\langle\cdot\rangle$ the average over several periods:
> 1. The power radiated per unit solid angle in the direction $\vec n$ is
>
> $$
> \frac{dP}{d\Omega} = \frac{1}{8\pi}\,\Lambda_{ij,kl}(\vec n)\,\bigl\langle\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{kl}\bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> 2. The total radiated power (gravitational luminosity) is
>
> $$
> P = \frac15\,\bigl\langle\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij}\bigr\rangle = \frac15\,\Bigl\langle\dddot M_{ij}\dddot M_{ij} - \frac13\bigl(\dddot M_{kk}\bigr)^2\Bigr\rangle \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } P = \frac{G}{5c^5}\bigl\langle\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij}\bigr\rangle\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 3. The angular integral behind part 2 is $\displaystyle\int d\Omega\,\Lambda_{ij,kl}(\vec n) = \frac{2\pi}{15}\bigl(11\,\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} - 4\,\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\bigr)$.
>
> *Model:* [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^mod-c8-5-3|Model §C8.5.3]]; *applies:* slow, weakly gravitating sources, leading order; an order-of-magnitude guide for strong sources ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^ex-c8-5-3|Example §C8.5.3]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5, eqs. (2.149)–(2.153) (pp. 39–40) · Schutz, §9.5, eq. (9.133) (p. 245) · Hartle, §23.6, eqs. (23.51)–(23.52) (p. 507) · 't Hooft, eq. (15.38) (p. 69)*

^thm-c8-5-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (time derivative of the field). Differentiating part 2 of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-5|Theorem §C8.5.5]] at fixed $\vec x$: $\dot h^{\rm TT}_{ij} = \frac2r\Lambda_{ij,kl}\dddot Q_{kl}(t - r)$.
>
> **Step 2** (square). $\Lambda$ is a projector with pair symmetry on symmetric tensors, $\Lambda_{ij,ab}\Lambda_{ab,kl} = \Lambda_{ij,kl}$ and $\Lambda_{ij,kl} = \Lambda_{kl,ij}$ ([[§C8.2 Gravitational Waves in Vacuum#^thm-c8-2-4|Theorem §C8.2.4]]; checked by script). So
>
> $$
> \dot h^{\rm TT}_{ij}\dot h^{\rm TT}_{ij} = \frac4{r^2}\,\Lambda_{ij,kl}\Lambda_{ij,mn}\,\dddot Q_{kl}\dddot Q_{mn} = \frac4{r^2}\,\Lambda_{kl,ij}\Lambda_{ij,mn}\,\dddot Q_{kl}\dddot Q_{mn} = \frac4{r^2}\,\Lambda_{kl,mn}\,\dddot Q_{kl}\dddot Q_{mn} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (power per solid angle). By [[§C8.4 The Energy of Gravitational Waves#^thm-c8-4-4|Theorem §C8.4.4]], $dP/d\Omega = \frac{r^2}{32\pi}\langle\dot h^{\rm TT}_{ij}\dot h^{\rm TT}_{ij}\rangle = \frac{r^2}{32\pi}\cdot\frac4{r^2}\Lambda\langle\dddot Q\dddot Q\rangle = \frac1{8\pi}\Lambda_{ij,kl}\langle\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{kl}\rangle$. The $r^2$ cancels: the energy flux falls as $1/r^2$ and the same power crosses every large sphere. Part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (moments of the direction). $I_{ij} = \int n_in_j\,d\Omega$ and $I_{ijkl} = \int n_in_jn_kn_l\,d\Omega$ are fully symmetric and unchanged by rotations. Contract with an arbitrary vector $\vec a$: $I_{ijkl}a^ia^ja^ka^l = \int(\vec n\cdot\vec a)^4d\Omega$ depends only on $|\vec a|$ and is of degree 4, so it equals $c\,|\vec a|^4 = c\,(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}a^ia^ja^ka^l)$. A fully symmetric tensor is fixed by its quartic form, and the symmetric tensor with quartic form $|\vec a|^4$ is $\frac13(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} + \delta_{il}\delta_{jk})$. Taking $\vec a = \vec e_z$, $c = \int\cos^4\theta\,d\Omega = 2\pi\int_{-1}^1u^4du = 4\pi/5$. The same argument at degree 2 gives $c' = \int\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega = 4\pi/3$. So
>
> $$
> \int d\Omega = 4\pi, \qquad \int n_in_j\,d\Omega = \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\delta_{ij}, \qquad \int n_in_jn_kn_l\,d\Omega = \frac{4\pi}{15}\bigl(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} + \delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\bigr)
> $$
>
> (the second also in [[§CA.4 Fourier Transforms and Fourier Tricks#^thm-ca-4-8|QFT Theorem §CA.4.8]], 2; all checked by script).
>
> **Step 5** (integrate $\Lambda$). With $P_{ij} = \delta_{ij} - n_in_j$,
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> P_{ik}P_{jl} &= \delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} - \delta_{ik}n_jn_l - \delta_{jl}n_in_k + n_in_jn_kn_l, \qquad P_{ij}P_{kl} = \delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} - \delta_{ij}n_kn_l - \delta_{kl}n_in_j + n_in_jn_kn_l , \\
> \Lambda_{ij,kl} &= \delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} - \tfrac12\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} - \delta_{ik}n_jn_l - \delta_{jl}n_in_k + \tfrac12\delta_{ij}n_kn_l + \tfrac12\delta_{kl}n_in_j + \tfrac12n_in_jn_kn_l .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> Integrating term by term with Step 4,
>
> $$
> \int d\Omega\,\Lambda_{ij,kl} = 4\pi\bigl(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} - \tfrac12\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\bigr) - \frac{4\pi}3\cdot2\,\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} + \frac{4\pi}3\,\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \frac{2\pi}{15}\bigl(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} + \delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Collecting: $\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}$ has $4\pi - \frac{8\pi}3 + \frac{2\pi}{15} = \frac{22\pi}{15}$; $\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}$ has $-2\pi + \frac{4\pi}3 + \frac{2\pi}{15} = -\frac{8\pi}{15}$; $\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}$ has $\frac{2\pi}{15}$. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 6** (contract). For $Q$ symmetric and traceless, $\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{kl} = \dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij}$, $\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{kl} = (\dddot Q_{ii})^2 = 0$, $\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{kl} = \dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ji} = \dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij}$. So
>
> $$
> P = \int d\Omega\,\frac{dP}{d\Omega} = \frac1{8\pi}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{15}(11 + 1)\,\langle\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij}\rangle = \frac1{8\pi}\cdot\frac{8\pi}5\,\langle\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij}\rangle = \frac15\,\langle\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij}\rangle .
> $$
>
> **Step 7** (in terms of $M$). With $Q = M - \frac13\delta\operatorname{tr}M$: $Q_{ij}Q_{ij} = M_{ij}M_{ij} - \frac23(\operatorname{tr}M)^2 + \frac19\cdot3(\operatorname{tr}M)^2 = M_{ij}M_{ij} - \frac13(\operatorname{tr}M)^2$, the same for third derivatives. Restoring: $P$ is a power only after multiplication by $G/c^5$ (dimension check: $[\dddot Q^2] = \mathrm{kg^2\,m^4\,s^{-6}}$, $[G/c^5] = \mathrm{kg^{-1}\,m^{-2}\,s^3}$, product $\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-3}} = \mathrm W$).
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Five components of $\dddot Q$ radiate, two in each direction. Averaged over directions, the weight $\frac{1}{8\pi}\cdot\frac{8\pi}{5}$ is the fraction $\frac25$ that 't Hooft obtains by counting ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^rem-c8-5-1|Remark: 't Hooft's Lagrangian route]]).
> - Three time derivatives: power grows as $\omega^6$ at fixed size and mass, so fast compact sources dominate.

A rotating source loses angular momentum along with energy; for a binary this is what makes the orbit shrink while staying circular (§C8.6).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.5.8: The Angular Momentum Carried Off
> 1. In [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^mod-c8-5-3|Model §C8.5.3]] the gravitational waves carry off angular momentum at the rate
>
> $$
> \frac{dJ^i}{dt} = \frac25\,\epsilon^{ikl}\bigl\langle\ddot Q_{km}\dddot Q_{lm}\bigr\rangle \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \frac{dJ^i}{dt} = \frac{2G}{5c^5}\,\epsilon^{ikl}\bigl\langle\ddot Q_{km}\dddot Q_{lm}\bigr\rangle\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> and the source's angular momentum decreases by the same amount. This part is stated in the sources and not derived here.
> 2. For a quadrupole that rotates rigidly about the $z$-axis with angular velocity $\Omega$,
>
> $$
> Q_{11} = -Q_{22} = A\cos2\Omega t + \text{const}, \qquad Q_{12} = A\sin2\Omega t, \qquad Q_{13} = Q_{23} = 0, \quad Q_{33} = \text{const}
> $$
>
> (any body rotating about a principal axis, e.g. a rod or a circular binary), the power and torque are constant:
>
> $$
> P = \frac{128}{5}A^2\Omega^6, \qquad \frac{dJ_z}{dt} = \frac{128}{5}A^2\Omega^5 = \frac{P}{\Omega} .
> $$
>
> Energy and angular momentum leave in the ratio $\Omega$, the ratio $dE/dJ$ of a rigid rotator or a circular Kepler orbit, so a circular binary stays circular as it loses both.
>
> *Model:* [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^mod-c8-5-3|Model §C8.5.3]]; *applies:* leading order, slow source; part 2 exact within the quadrupole formula.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §2.5, eq. (2.154) (p. 40) · Hartle, Problem 23.13*

^thm-c8-5-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Part 1 (what a derivation needs).** Energy flux uses only $\partial h$ (Theorem §C8.4.4). The flux of angular momentum through a large sphere involves $h^{\rm TT}$ itself as well, in an orbital term ($\vec x\times\nabla$ acting on $h^{\rm TT}$) and a spin term (the rotation of the indices of $h^{\rm TT}_{ij}$). Neither is derived in §C8.4. Inserting Theorem §C8.5.5 into that flux leaves angular integrals of products of up to six components of $\vec n$, done as in Step 4 of Derivation §C8.5.7. The sources state the result (Couzens (2.154)); part 2 checks its normalization against part 2 of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-7|Theorem §C8.5.7]].
>
> **Step 1** (derivatives). Write $\nu = 2\Omega$, $c = \cos\nu t$, $s = \sin\nu t$. In the $12$ block (the rest is constant and drops out of every derivative),
>
> $$
> \ddot Q = -\nu^2A\begin{pmatrix}c & s\\ s & -c\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \dddot Q = \nu^3A\begin{pmatrix}s & -c\\ -c & -s\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (power). $\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij} = \nu^6A^2(s^2 + c^2 + c^2 + s^2) = 2\nu^6A^2$, constant, so $P = \frac25\nu^6A^2 = \frac25\cdot64\,\Omega^6A^2 = \frac{128}5A^2\Omega^6$.
>
> **Step 3** (torque). $\epsilon^{zkl}\ddot Q_{km}\dddot Q_{lm} = \ddot Q_{1m}\dddot Q_{2m} - \ddot Q_{2m}\dddot Q_{1m}$, with
>
> $$
> \ddot Q_{1m}\dddot Q_{2m} = -\nu^5A^2\bigl[c(-c) + s(-s)\bigr] = \nu^5A^2, \qquad \ddot Q_{2m}\dddot Q_{1m} = -\nu^5A^2\bigl[s\,s + (-c)(-c)\bigr] = -\nu^5A^2 .
> $$
>
> So $dJ_z/dt = \frac25\cdot2\nu^5A^2 = \frac45\cdot32\,\Omega^5A^2 = \frac{128}5A^2\Omega^5 = P/\Omega$. The sign is positive for counterclockwise rotation ($J_z > 0$): the waves carry off angular momentum of the source's sign. (Checked by script for the rod and the binary.)
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - For a rigid rotator $E = \frac12I\Omega^2$, $J = I\Omega$, so $dE/dJ = \Omega$; for a circular Kepler orbit $E = -m\mu/2a$, $J = \mu\sqrt{ma}$ give $dE/dJ = (m\mu/2a^2)/(\frac12\mu\sqrt{m/a}) = \sqrt{m/a^3} = \Omega$ ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-4|CM Theorem §B2.2.4]]). The waves remove $E$ and $J$ in the ratio that keeps a circular orbit circular.

> [!caution] Caution: Self-gravitating sources
> The derivation assumed $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, conservation in flat space. For a star or a binary held together by its own gravity, matter obeys $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ ([[§C4.4 Conservation in Curved Spacetime#^pr-c4-4-1|Principle §C4.4.1]]), and $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = -\Gamma T$ terms are the gravitational forces. These terms are not small corrections: in a bound system they are as large as the kinetic stresses. For a circular binary ($\mu$, separation $a$, $\vec x = a(\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t, 0)$, $\omega^2 = m/a^3$), $M_{11} = \mu a^2\cos^2\omega t$ and $\frac12\ddot M_{11} = -\mu a^2\omega^2\cos2\omega t$, while the kinetic stress alone is $\int\rho v^1v^1 = \mu a^2\omega^2\sin^2\omega t = \frac12\mu a^2\omega^2(1 - \cos2\omega t)$. The two differ; the difference is the gravitational force term $x^1F^1 = -\mu\omega^2a^2\cos^2\omega t$ (checked by script). The virial identity ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-4|Theorem §C8.5.4]]) holds only for the total conserved stress, gravity's included. 't Hooft states the caution himself: the formula is expected to hold for binaries, but "this does not follow from the above derivation". Schutz notes that the final formula uses only the motion, not the forces (p. 235). Why the result survives: the quadrupole field needs $\ddot M^{ij}$, a property of the motion, and the energy density of the Newtonian field changes $M^{ij}$ only at relative order $v^2$. Including the gravitational field's own stress-energy in the source (the relaxed Einstein equations of post-Newtonian theory, not developed here) restores a flat conservation law and gives the same leading formula. Hartle makes the same point (p. 499). The binary-pulsar test (§C8.6) confirms it to better than a percent.

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: 't Hooft's Lagrangian route
> 't Hooft reaches the same formula without Green's functions. He expands $\sqrt{-g}\,(R + \mathcal L_{\rm matter})$ to second order in $h$, with $16\pi G = 1$ (his (15.3)), and fixes the radiation gauge $\partial_ih_{ij} = \partial_ih_{i0} = 0$, the analogue of the Coulomb gauge. In momentum space the Lagrangian splits into three parts. $\mathcal L_1$ contains the two transverse traceless components for each wave vector, each a harmonic oscillator driven by the TT part of $T_{ij}$: this is the radiation. $\mathcal L_2$ and $\mathcal L_3$ have no time derivatives of the fields and produce forces between the sources that look instantaneous, among them the Newtonian potential (his (15.30)). The instantaneity is an artefact of the gauge, as for the Coulomb-gauge scalar potential in electrodynamics ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^rem-b11-1-2|EM §B11.1, Remark: The instantaneous potential of the Coulomb gauge]]); only $\mathcal L_1$ radiates. For a small source the virial identity (his (15.31), [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-4|Theorem §C8.5.4]]) replaces $\int T_{ij}$ by $\frac12\ddot M_{ij}$. The power then follows from the scalar-field result $P = \frac{k^2}{4\pi}|\int J|^2$ for a periodic source, applied to the five components of $\hat T_{ij}$, of which two act in each direction: averaged over directions, the fraction $\frac25$. The result, his (15.38), is $P = \frac{G}{5}(\dddot Q_{ij})^2$, Theorem §C8.5.7. The route makes visible what Derivation §C8.5.7 computes: only the TT part of the source radiates, two components at a time.
>
> *Source: 't Hooft, ch. 15, eqs. (15.3)–(15.38) (pp. 65–69)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C8.5.1: A Rotating Rod, and the Earth–Sun System
> *Rod.* A thin uniform rod of mass $M$ and length $L$ rotates in the $xy$-plane about its midpoint with angular velocity $\Omega$, slowly ($\Omega L \ll 1$). The element at distance $\xi \in [-L/2, L/2]$ from the centre sits at $\xi(\cos\Omega t, \sin\Omega t, 0)$ with mass $(M/L)\,d\xi$. Using $\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}\xi^2\,d\xi = L^3/12$, the second moments ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-1|Def. §C8.5.1]]) are
>
> $$
> M_{11} = \frac{ML^2}{12}\cos^2\Omega t = \frac{ML^2}{24}(1 + \cos2\Omega t), \qquad M_{22} = \frac{ML^2}{24}(1 - \cos2\Omega t), \qquad M_{12} = \frac{ML^2}{24}\sin2\Omega t ,
> $$
>
> with $M_{i3} = 0$ and $M_{kk} = ML^2/12$, constant. Subtracting $\frac13\delta_{ij}M_{kk} = \frac{ML^2}{36}\delta_{ij}$ gives $Q$ ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^def-c8-5-2|Def. §C8.5.2]]):
>
> $$
> Q_{11} = ML^2\Bigl(\frac1{72} + \frac1{24}\cos2\Omega t\Bigr), \quad Q_{22} = ML^2\Bigl(\frac1{72} - \frac1{24}\cos2\Omega t\Bigr), \quad Q_{12} = \frac{ML^2}{24}\sin2\Omega t, \quad Q_{33} = -\frac{ML^2}{36} .
> $$
>
> At $t = 0$ this is $Q_{11} = ML^2/18$, $Q_{22} = Q_{33} = -ML^2/36$, 't Hooft's values. The rod looks the same after half a turn, so everything varies at $2\Omega$: the waves have twice the rotation frequency.
>
> *Field.* The second derivatives are $\ddot M_{11} = -\ddot M_{22} = -\frac{ML^2\Omega^2}6\cos2\Omega t$ and $\ddot M_{12} = -\frac{ML^2\Omega^2}6\sin2\Omega t$. In [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-5|Theorem §C8.5.5]], part 4, the $\ddot M_{11}$ and $\ddot M_{22}$ coefficients of $rh_+$ combine to $(\cos^2\theta\cos^2\varphi - \sin^2\varphi) - (\cos^2\theta\sin^2\varphi - \cos^2\varphi) = (1 + \cos^2\theta)\cos2\varphi$, and the $\ddot M_{12}$ term carries $(1 + \cos^2\theta)\sin2\varphi$. With $\cos2\Omega t\cos2\varphi + \sin2\Omega t\sin2\varphi = \cos(2\Omega t - 2\varphi)$,
>
> $$
> h_+ = -\frac{ML^2\Omega^2}{3r}\cdot\frac{1 + \cos^2\theta}2\,\cos\bigl(2\Omega(t - r) - 2\varphi\bigr), \qquad h_\times = -\frac{ML^2\Omega^2}{3r}\cos\theta\,\sin\bigl(2\Omega(t - r) - 2\varphi\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Along the axis ($\theta = 0$) the two polarisations have equal amplitude and are $90°$ out of phase, so the wave is circularly polarised. In the plane of rotation ($\theta = \pi/2$) only $h_+$ survives, with half the amplitude: linear polarisation.
>
> *Power.* With $\dddot Q_{11} = -\dddot Q_{22} = \frac{ML^2}{24}(2\Omega)^3\sin2\Omega t$ and $\dddot Q_{12} = -\frac{ML^2}{24}(2\Omega)^3\cos2\Omega t$,
>
> $$
> \dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij} = \Bigl(\frac{8ML^2\Omega^3}{24}\Bigr)^2\bigl(\sin^2 + \sin^2 + 2\cos^2\bigr) = 2\cdot\frac{64}{576}M^2L^4\Omega^6 = \frac29M^2L^4\Omega^6 ,
> $$
>
> constant in time, so no averaging is needed, and [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-7|Theorem §C8.5.7]] gives
>
> $$
> P = \frac15\cdot\frac29M^2L^4\Omega^6 = \frac2{45}M^2L^4\Omega^6 \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } P = \frac{2G}{45c^5}M^2L^4\Omega^6\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> In the form of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-8|Theorem §C8.5.8]], 2, $A = ML^2/24$ and $\frac{128}5A^2 = \frac{2}{45}M^2L^4$; the rod loses angular momentum at $P/\Omega$. The angular distribution, from $dP/d\Omega = \frac{r^2}{32\pi}\langle\dot h^{\rm TT}_{ij}\dot h^{\rm TT}_{ij}\rangle$ ([[§C8.4 The Energy of Gravitational Waves#^thm-c8-4-4|Theorem §C8.4.4]]) with $h^{\rm TT}_{ij}h^{\rm TT}_{ij} = 2(h_+^2 + h_\times^2)$, i.e. $dP/d\Omega = \frac{r^2}{16\pi}\langle\dot h_+^2 + \dot h_\times^2\rangle$, and $\langle\cos^2\rangle = \langle\sin^2\rangle = \frac12$, is
>
> $$
> \Bigl\langle\frac{dP}{d\Omega}\Bigr\rangle = \frac{M^2L^4\Omega^6}{72\pi}\,g(\theta), \qquad g(\theta) = \frac14(1 + \cos^2\theta)^2 + \cos^2\theta, \qquad \int g\,d\Omega = \frac{16\pi}5 ,
> $$
>
> and $\frac{1}{72\pi}\cdot\frac{16\pi}{5} = \frac{2}{45}$ reproduces the total. Eight times more power goes out along the axis ($g = 2$) than in the plane ($g = \frac14$).
>
> A steel beam of $M = 10^4$ kg and $L = 10$ m spun at $\Omega = 10$ s⁻¹ (ends at $50$ m/s) radiates $P = 1.2\times10^{-36}$ W.
>
> *Earth and Sun.* For two point masses in a circular orbit about their centre of mass, with relative position $\vec x$ of length $a$ and reduced mass $\mu$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-2|CM Def. §B2.1.2]]), the positions $\vec x_1 = -\frac{m_2}m\vec x$ and $\vec x_2 = \frac{m_1}m\vec x$ give
>
> $$
> M^{ij} = m_1x_1^ix_1^j + m_2x_2^ix_2^j = \frac{m_1m_2(m_2 + m_1)}{m^2}\,x^ix^j = \mu\,x^ix^j .
> $$
>
> This is the rod's $M^{ij}$ with $ML^2/12 \to \mu a^2$, the moment of inertia about the axis in both cases, so all of the above carries over. In particular $P = \frac{2}{45}(12\mu a^2)^2\omega^6 = \frac{32}{5}\mu^2a^4\omega^6$, the circular-binary formula of §C8.6 ([[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral#^thm-c8-6-3|Theorem §C8.6.3]]). For the Earth and the Sun, $\mu = 5.972\times10^{24}$ kg (the Earth's mass to 3 parts in $10^6$), $a = 1$ AU $= 1.496\times10^{11}$ m, $\omega = 2\pi/(1\ \text{sidereal yr}) = 1.991\times10^{-7}$ s⁻¹:
>
> $$
> P = \frac{32}5\,\frac{G}{c^5}\,\mu^2a^4\omega^6 = 196\ \mathrm W .
> $$
>
> Why so little: with Kepler's law $\omega^2 = m/a^3$ ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-4|CM Theorem §B2.2.4]]), $P = \frac{32}5(\mu/m)^2(m/a)^5$ in units of $c^5/G$. The Earth's orbit has compactness $m/a = 9.87\times10^{-9}$ ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^ex-c1-1-3|Example §C1.1.3]]), whose fifth power is $9.4\times10^{-41}$, and $(\mu/m)^2 = 9.0\times10^{-12}$; so $P = 5.4\times10^{-51}\,c^5/G$. In $4.6\times10^9$ years the orbit loses $2.8\times10^{19}$ J, against an orbital binding energy $m\mu/2a = 2.6\times10^{33}$ J.
>
> *Source: 't Hooft, ch. 15, eqs. (15.39)–(15.46) (pp. 69–70) · Hartle, Problem 23.16 (p. 513) · numbers checked by script (Earth–Sun computed here, not from the sources)*

> [!example] Example §C8.5.2: Two Masses Oscillating on a Spring
> Two equal masses $m$ on the $x$-axis, joined by a spring of rest length $l_0$, oscillate symmetrically about the origin: $x_1 = -\frac12l_0 - A\cos\omega t$, $x_2 = \frac12l_0 + A\cos\omega t$ (Schutz's oscillator, (9.88)). The spring's tension is part of $T^{ij}$; with it the source is conserved in flat space and [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-4|Theorem §C8.5.4]] applies. The spring's own mass is neglected.
>
> *Moments.* Only $M_{11}$ is nonzero:
>
> $$
> M_{11} = m(x_1^2 + x_2^2) = 2m\Bigl(\frac{l_0}2 + A\cos\omega t\Bigr)^2 = \text{const} + 2ml_0A\cos\omega t + mA^2\cos2\omega t ,
> $$
>
> using $2A^2\cos^2\omega t = A^2 + A^2\cos2\omega t$. The $\omega$ part dominates when $A \ll l_0$: in the field the two amplitudes are $2ml_0A\omega^2$ and $4mA^2\omega^2$, ratio $2A/l_0$. Keep the $\omega$ part: $Q_{11} = \frac43ml_0A\cos\omega t$, $Q_{22} = Q_{33} = -\frac23ml_0A\cos\omega t$.
>
> *Field.* Along $z$ ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-5|Theorem §C8.5.5]], part 3), $h_\times = 0$ and
>
> $$
> h_+ = \frac{\ddot M_{11}}{r} = -\frac{2ml_0A\omega^2}r\cos\omega(t - r) - \frac{4mA^2\omega^2}r\cos2\omega(t - r) ,
> $$
>
> Schutz's (9.93). Along $x$ (part 4 with $\theta = \pi/2$, $\varphi = 0$) the $\ddot M_{11}$ coefficients $\cos^2\theta\cos^2\varphi - \sin^2\varphi$ and $-\cos\theta\sin2\varphi$ both vanish: no radiation along the line of motion (Schutz (9.91)). In general only $\ddot M$ along the oscillation axis is nonzero. Renaming that axis $z$, part 4 gives $rh_+ = \ddot M_{33}\sin^2\Theta$ and $h_\times = 0$, with $\Theta$ the angle from the axis. The radiation is linearly polarised, with power pattern $\propto\sin^4\Theta$, against $\sin^2\Theta$ for an oscillating electric dipole ([[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-4|EM Theorem §B11.3.4]]).
>
> *Power* ($\omega$ part). $\dddot Q_{11} = \frac43ml_0A\omega^3\sin\omega t$, $\dddot Q_{22} = \dddot Q_{33} = -\frac23ml_0A\omega^3\sin\omega t$, so $\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij} = \bigl(\frac{16}9 + \frac49 + \frac49\bigr)m^2l_0^2A^2\omega^6\sin^2\omega t = \frac83m^2l_0^2A^2\omega^6\sin^2\omega t$. With $\langle\sin^2\rangle = \frac12$,
>
> $$
> P = \frac15\cdot\frac43\,m^2l_0^2A^2\omega^6 = \frac4{15}\,m^2l_0^2A^2\omega^6 \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } P = \frac{4G}{15c^5}\,m^2l_0^2A^2\omega^6\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Check through the pattern: $dP/d\Omega = \frac{r^2}{16\pi}\langle\dot h_+^2\rangle = \frac1{16\pi}\langle\dddot M_{33}^2\rangle\sin^4\Theta$ with $\langle\dddot M_{33}^2\rangle = \frac12(2ml_0A\omega^3)^2$, and $\int\sin^4\Theta\,d\Omega = \frac{32\pi}{15}$, so $P = \frac{2m^2l_0^2A^2\omega^6}{16\pi}\cdot\frac{32\pi}{15} = \frac4{15}m^2l_0^2A^2\omega^6$.
>
> *Numbers* (Schutz's values: $m = 10^3$ kg, $l_0 = 1$ m, $A = 10^{-4}$ m, $\omega = 10^4$ s⁻¹). In geometric units $m = Gm/c^2 = 7.43\times10^{-25}$ m and $\omega/c = 3.34\times10^{-5}$ m⁻¹. The amplitude is $2ml_0A\omega^2 = 1.65\times10^{-37}$ m, so $h \approx 1.7\times10^{-37}/(r/1\,\mathrm m)$; the $2\omega$ term is $3.3\times10^{-41}$ m. Schutz's text says $10^{-34}/r$ ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^cau-c8-5-3|Caution: Slips in d'Inverno and Schutz]]). The wave zone begins only at $r \gg \bar\lambda = c/\omega = 30$ km, where $h \lesssim 10^{-41}$. The power is $P = 7.4\times10^{-32}$ W.
>
> *Source: Schutz, §9.4, eqs. (9.88)–(9.93) (pp. 234–235) · Hartle, Problem 23.10 · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C8.5.3: The Luminosity Scale c⁵/G
> In geometric units the power $P$ of [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^thm-c8-5-7|Theorem §C8.5.7]] is dimensionless: $Q$ has dimension length³ (mass × length²), and three time derivatives remove three lengths. The SI power is $P$ times
>
> $$
> \frac{c^5}{G} = 3.63\times10^{52}\ \mathrm W .
> $$
>
> *Estimate.* A source of mass $M$, size $R$ and internal speed $v$ changes on the time $R/v$, so $Q \sim MR^2$ and $\dddot Q \sim MR^2(v/R)^3 = Mv^3/R$. Hence $P \sim (M/R)^2v^6$ (Schutz's $L \sim \phi_0^2v^6$). If gravity binds the source, $v^2 \sim M/R$ (virial), and
>
> $$
> P \lesssim \Bigl(\frac MR\Bigr)^5 \cdot\frac{c^5}{G} .
> $$
>
> Compactness $M/R$ cannot exceed order one without a black hole forming ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^def-c1-1-1|Def. §C1.1.1]]), so $c^5/G$ is the natural ceiling. Schutz argues it for any radiation (Hartle poses the same argument as Problem 23.18): a source of size $R$ and luminosity $L$ contains radiation energy $\gtrsim LR$, whose own Newtonian potential $\gtrsim L$ must stay below one.
>
> *Comparisons* (numbers from the script):
>
> | Source | Power | Fraction of $c^5/G$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | Earth–Sun ([[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^ex-c8-5-1\|Example §C8.5.1]]) | $196$ W | $5.4\times10^{-51}$ |
> | Sun, in light (Hartle: $\sim10^{33}$ erg/s) | $3.8\times10^{26}$ W | $1.1\times10^{-26}$ |
> | GW150914 at its peak (detected 14 Sept 2015) | $3.6\times10^{49}$ W | $9.9\times10^{-4}$ |
>
> The peak of GW150914, the merger of two black holes of about 35 and 30 solar masses as reported by d'Inverno (2022), exceeded the light of the Sun $10^{23}$ times; d'Inverno quotes about 50 times the light of all stars in the observable universe. It is $10^{-3}$ of the ceiling, as Schutz says numerical simulations give for black-hole mergers. Hartle (2003, before any detection) estimated $\epsilon\,c^5/G$ with $\epsilon \sim 10^{-2}$. Such sources violate [[§C8.5 The Quadrupole Formula#^mod-c8-5-3|Model §C8.5.3]] ($v$ near $c$, $M/R$ near one): the quadrupole formula gives the scale, numerical relativity the number.
>
> *Source: Schutz, §9.5, eqs. (9.134)–(9.135) (p. 245) · Hartle, §23.8, Example 23.4, eq. (23.61) (p. 510); Problem 23.18 (p. 513) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §21.14 (p. 442) · numbers checked by script*
