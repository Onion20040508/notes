[[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-4|Theorem §C11.3.4]] left an observer who has fallen through $r_+$ and then $r_-$ with a choice that no earlier spacetime offered: the singularity $r = 0$ is timelike, so she may steer towards it or away from it, out through a white hole into another universe. Couzens puts the worry plainly: nothing on the way in decides in advance which of these happens (§5.3, p. 98). In Newtonian mechanics and in special relativity the state on one instant fixes everything later. The question of this section is how far the state on one instant fixes the Reissner–Nordström spacetime, and where it stops doing so.

*The naive attempt.* In Minkowski space the slice $t = 0$ does the job: every observer, followed far enough into the past, crosses it once ([[#^thm-c11-4-1|Theorem §C11.4.1]], part 1). The Reissner–Nordström analogue is the Einstein–Rosen slice $\Sigma$: $t = 0$ in the two exteriors I and III, joined through the bifurcation sphere of $r_+$ ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-3|Theorem §C11.3.3]]). Take $M = 1$, $e = 0.8$, so $r_+ = 1.6$, $r_- = 0.4$, and the observer at fixed $r_0 = 0.2$ and fixed angles in region VI, beyond $r_-$. There
$$
f(r_0) = \frac{(r_0 - r_+)(r_0 - r_-)}{r_0^2} = \frac{(-1.4)(-0.2)}{0.04} = 7 > 0 ,
$$
so $\partial_t$ is timelike and the curve $t \mapsto (t, r_0)$ is a timelike worldline with proper time $\sqrt7\,|\Delta t|$, unbounded in both directions. In the Kruskal-type chart at $r_-$ it is the hyperbola $U_-V_- = F_-(0.2) = -0.945$, which lies in region VI for all $t$ (the numbers are in `tools/verify_c11_4.py`). It never meets $\Sigma$, which lies at $r \ge r_+$. A whole observer history, infinitely long, is untouched by anything on $\Sigma$. Worse, region VI borders the timelike singularity: a signal may come out of $r = 0$ and reach her, and no data anywhere describe what comes out.

So "a slice at one instant" is not enough, and the section builds what replaces it. It names the sets that never contain two events one of which can influence the other ([[#^def-c11-4-1|Def. §C11.4.1]]), the events that a set determines ([[#^def-c11-4-2|Def. §C11.4.2]], [[#^def-c11-4-4|Def. §C11.4.4]]) and the boundary where determination ends ([[#^def-c11-4-3|Def. §C11.4.3]]). A set that determines everything is a Cauchy surface ([[#^def-c11-4-5|Def. §C11.4.5]]), and a spacetime that has one is globally hyperbolic ([[#^def-c11-4-6|Def. §C11.4.6]]). Minkowski space and Kruskal are ([[#^thm-c11-4-1|Theorem §C11.4.1]]); maximally extended Reissner–Nordström is not, and its inner horizon $r = r_-$ is exactly where determination ends ([[#^thm-c11-4-2|Theorem §C11.4.2]]). Why physicists do not believe in the region beyond is the blue shift at $r_-$ ([[#^ex-c11-4-4|Example §C11.4.4]], [[#^rem-c11-4-1|Remark: Blue-shift instability of the inner horizon]]), and the conjecture that generic collapse never produces such a horizon is strong cosmic censorship ([[#^rem-c11-4-2|Remark: Strong cosmic censorship]]).

**Setup.** A spacetime $(M, g)$: a connected, time-oriented Lorentzian manifold ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]). Curves are the piecewise smooth causal curves of [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]; endpoints and inextendibility are those of [[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-5|Def. §C10.4.5]], and the futures and pasts $I^\pm$, $J^\pm$ those of [[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^def-c10-7-1|Def. §C10.7.1]]. Closed sets and closures are taken in $M$ ([[§7 Closed Sets and Limit Points#^def-7-1|590 Def. §7.1]], [[§8 Interior and Closure#^def-8-2|590 Def. §8.2]]). What is proved here: the domains of the examples, and [[#^thm-c11-4-1|Theorems §C11.4.1]]–[[#^thm-c11-4-2|§C11.4.2]], by reducing to radial curves in a Penrose diagram. What is quoted, and marked as such: the general structure of Cauchy horizons, the topology of globally hyperbolic spacetimes and the well-posedness of field equations on them (Wald §8.3, §10.1–10.2; research results not in the typed sources). Reissner–Nordström enters through the maximal analytic extension of [[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-3|Theorem §C11.3.3]] with $0 < e^2 < M^2$; its region labels are those of Kruskal ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]): exteriors I (right) and III (left), black hole II, white hole IV; beyond $r_-$, the regions $0 < r < r_-$ are V (left) and VI (right), the next white hole is IV′ and the next universe has I′, II′, III′, following §C11.3. Couzens (Figs. 13–16, 25) calls the second exterior IV and the white hole III, so his III′ is IV′ here. Numbers come from `tools/verify_c11_4.py`.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here $M \to GM/c^2$ for lengths and $GM/c^3$ for times); $f = 1 - 2M/r + e^2/r^2 = (r - r_+)(r - r_-)/r^2$ with $e^2 = Q^2 + P^2$ ([[§C11.2 The Reissner–Nordström Solution#^thm-c11-2-5|Theorem §C11.2.5]]); $\kappa_\pm = (r_+ - r_-)/(2r_\pm^2) > 0$ ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-2|Theorem §C11.3.2]]); tortoise coordinate $r_\ast = r + \frac{1}{2\kappa_+}\ln\frac{|r - r_+|}{r_+} - \frac{1}{2\kappa_-}\ln\frac{|r - r_-|}{r_-}$, normalised so that $r_\ast(0) = 0$; $u = t - r_\ast$, $v = t + r_\ast$ ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-1|Theorem §C11.3.1]]). *Penrose-diagram coordinates.* In each Kruskal-type chart $(U, V)$ of [[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^def-c11-3-1|Def. §C11.3.1]] put $\tilde u = \arctan U$, $\tilde v = \arctan V$ and draw $x = \tilde v - \tilde u$ across, $y = \tilde u + \tilde v$ up; the chart at $r_+$ ("+ chart": I, II, III, IV) is the diamond $|x| + |y| < \pi$, the chart at $r_-$ ("− chart": II, V, VI, IV′) is shifted up by $\pi$, the next + chart by $2\pi$ (as in [[§C10.3 Penrose Diagrams of Kruskal and of Collapse#^thm-c10-3-2|Theorem §C10.3.2]] for Kruskal).

## Which events does a set determine?

In Minkowski space a field obeying a wave equation is fixed at an event $p$ by its values on the part of $t = 0$ inside the past light cone of $p$: nothing travels faster than light. Two things in that sentence need names in a curved spacetime: the "instant" (a set none of whose points can signal to another) and the "past light cone reaching back to it" (every causal curve from $p$ into the past reaches it).

> [!definition] Definition §C11.4.1: Achronal Set
> *Structure.* Let $(M, g)$ be a time-oriented spacetime and $S \subset M$ a subset. $S$ is **achronal** if no two of its points are joined by a future-directed timelike curve:
> $$
> I^+(S) \cap S = \emptyset ,
> $$
> with $I^+$ the chronological future of [[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^def-c10-7-1|Def. §C10.7.1]]. Equivalently, every timelike curve ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]) meets $S$ at most once.
>
> *Reading.* No event of $S$ can send a massive signal to another event of $S$: $S$ is a candidate for "one instant".
>
> *Examples and a non-example.* In Minkowski space every slice $t = c$ is achronal, since $t$ increases strictly along every future-directed timelike curve. A null line $\{t = x\}$ of two-dimensional Minkowski space is achronal too: two of its points have $(\Delta t)^2 - (\Delta x)^2 = 0$, never $> 0$. A spacelike curve need not be achronal, because "spacelike" is a condition at each point and "achronal" a condition on pairs of points. The helix $\gamma(s) = (t, x, y) = (s/2, \cos s, \sin s)$ in three-dimensional Minkowski space has tangent $(\tfrac12, -\sin s, \cos s)$ with $\eta(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma) = -\tfrac14 + 1 = \tfrac34 > 0$, spacelike everywhere; yet $\gamma(s)$ and $\gamma(s + 2\pi)$ differ by $(\pi, 0, 0)$, a timelike separation, so the straight segment between them is a timelike curve with both ends on $\gamma$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.3, Definition 17, p. 99 (PDF 100) ("no two points in $S$ are connected by a time-like curve") · helix worked here*

^def-c11-4-1

The second name is the region whose past always runs back into $S$. The word that matters is *inextendible*: a curve that simply stops before reaching $S$ proves nothing, but a curve that has no past endpoint, because it comes in from infinity, from a singularity or from a hole in spacetime, carries influences that $S$ does not record.

> [!definition] Definition §C11.4.2: Future and Past Domains of Dependence
> *Structure.* Let $S \subset M$ be closed and achronal ([[#^def-c11-4-1|Def. §C11.4.1]]).
> 1. The **future domain of dependence** $D^+(S)$ is the set of events $p \in M$ such that every past-inextendible causal curve through $p$ meets $S$.
> 2. The **past domain of dependence** $D^-(S)$ is the set of events $p$ such that every future-inextendible causal curve through $p$ meets $S$.
>
> Past- and future-inextendible mean: without a past (future) endpoint in $M$ ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-5|Def. §C10.4.5]]). This is not the same as complete ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-6|Def. §C10.4.6]]): a curve that runs into a singularity or a removed point after finite proper time is inextendible, and it is exactly such curves that shrink the domains ([[#^ex-c11-4-3|Example §C11.4.3]], [[#^thm-c11-4-2|Theorem §C11.4.2]]).
>
> *Immediate facts.* $S \subset D^\pm(S)$: a curve through $p \in S$ meets $S$ at $p$. By time reversal everything said about $D^+$ holds for $D^-$.
>
> *Physical reading.* If no signal is faster than light, any influence on $p$ travels along a causal curve ending at $p$. Followed into the past, that curve meets $S$. So data on $S$, together with field equations whose signals travel inside the light cones, should fix the fields at every $p \in D^+(S)$. For the wave equation and for Maxwell's equations on a fixed spacetime this is a theorem (Wald §10.1, quoted); for the metric itself it is the content of the Einstein initial-value problem ([[#^def-c11-4-6|Def. §C11.4.6]], quoted).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.3, Definition 17 and Fig. 18, pp. 99–100 (PDF 100–101); the Remark after it (PDF 101, with a slip, [[#^cau-c11-4-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens §5.3]])*

^def-c11-4-2

For a bounded segment $S$ of the line $t = 0$, $D^+(S)$ is the triangle above $S$ whose sides are the light rays from its two ends ([[#^ex-c11-4-1|Example §C11.4.1]], figure after [[#^ex-c11-4-3|Example §C11.4.3]], panel (a)). Its upper sides are where determination ends: an event just beyond them receives light that passed outside the ends of $S$. These sides get a name; the boundary of $D^+(S)$ would also contain $S$ itself, so the definition removes the part that can still be pushed into the future.

> [!definition] Definition §C11.4.3: Cauchy Horizons
> *Structure.* For $S$ closed and achronal, the **future Cauchy horizon** of $S$ is
> $$
> H^+(S) = \overline{D^+(S)} \setminus I^-\bigl(D^+(S)\bigr) ,
> $$
> the points of the closure of $D^+(S)$ ([[§8 Interior and Closure#^def-8-2|590 Def. §8.2]]) from which no future-directed timelike curve enters $D^+(S)$ ([[#^def-c11-4-2|Def. §C11.4.2]], [[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^def-c10-7-1|Def. §C10.7.1]]). The **past Cauchy horizon** is $H^-(S) = \overline{D^-(S)} \setminus I^+\bigl(D^-(S)\bigr)$.
>
> *Reading.* $H^+(S)$ is the future boundary of what $S$ determines. A point of $S$ in the middle of the segment of [[#^ex-c11-4-1|Example §C11.4.1]] lies on the boundary of $D^+(S)$ (just below it there are no points of $D^+$) but not on $H^+(S)$: a short timelike curve from it enters $D^+(S)$. So $\partial D^+(S) = H^+(S) \cup S$ there, and $H^+(S)$ is the pair of null sides only.
>
> *Quoted structure* (Wald §8.3; Hawking & Ellis §6.5; not in the typed sources). $H^+(S)$ is closed and achronal, and it is generated by null geodesics. Followed into the past, each generator either never ends in $M$ or ends on the **edge** of $S$: the points of $S$ near which timelike curves can pass from just below $S$ to just above it without meeting $S$, such as the two endpoints of a segment. When $S$ has no edge, as a slice $t = 0$ of Minkowski space or the hyperboloid of [[#^ex-c11-4-2|Example §C11.4.2]], the generators of $H^+(S)$ have no past endpoint at all. All examples below show this.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.3, Definition 18 and Fig. 19, p. 100 (PDF 101) (Couzens defines $H^+(S)$ as the boundary of $D^+(S)$: [[#^cau-c11-4-1|Caution: Slips in Couzens §5.3]]) · standard definition: Wald §8.3*

^def-c11-4-3

> [!definition] Definition §C11.4.4: Domain of Dependence
> *Structure.* For $S$ closed and achronal, the **domain of dependence** of $S$ is
> $$
> D(S) = D^+(S) \cup D^-(S) ,
> $$
> with $D^\pm(S)$ of [[#^def-c11-4-2|Def. §C11.4.2]].
>
> *Reading.* All events about which data on $S$ decide, to the future and to the past. For a bounded segment it is a compact diamond ([[#^ex-c11-4-1|Example §C11.4.1]]); for an unbounded edgeless curve it can still be far from everything ([[#^ex-c11-4-2|Example §C11.4.2]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.3, Definition 19, p. 101 (PDF 102)*

## Cauchy surfaces and global hyperbolicity

> [!definition] Definition §C11.4.5: Cauchy Surface
> *Structure.* A **Cauchy surface** of $(M, g)$ is a closed achronal set $\Sigma \subset M$ ([[#^def-c11-4-1|Def. §C11.4.1]]) whose domain of dependence is all of spacetime:
> $$
> D(\Sigma) = M
> $$
> ([[#^def-c11-4-4|Def. §C11.4.4]]).
>
> *Equivalent form.* $D(\Sigma) = M$ if and only if every inextendible causal curve meets $\Sigma$; by achronality a timelike one meets it exactly once. *Why.* Suppose every inextendible causal curve meets $\Sigma$, and let $p \in M$ lie neither in $D^+(\Sigma)$ nor in $D^-(\Sigma)$. Then some past-inextendible causal curve $\gamma_-$ from $p$ and some future-inextendible causal curve $\gamma_+$ from $p$ miss $\Sigma$ (and $p \notin \Sigma$, since $\Sigma \subset D^\pm(\Sigma)$). Joined at $p$ they form one inextendible causal curve that misses $\Sigma$: a contradiction. Conversely, let $D(\Sigma) = M$ and let $\gamma$ be inextendible and causal; pick $p$ on $\gamma$. If $p \in D^+(\Sigma)$, the part of $\gamma$ before $p$ is past-inextendible and meets $\Sigma$; if $p \in D^-(\Sigma)$, the part after $p$ does.
>
> *Reading.* Data on $\Sigma$ decide everything, past and future: $\Sigma$ is "an instant" in the full sense. Every observer, followed for her whole life, passes it once.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.3, Definition 20, p. 101 (PDF 102) ("a closed achronal surface Σ … if the domain of dependence D(Σ) is the entire manifold") · equivalent form worked here*

^def-c11-4-5

> [!definition] Definition §C11.4.6: Globally Hyperbolic Spacetime
> *Structure.* A spacetime $(M, g)$ is **globally hyperbolic** if it has a Cauchy surface ([[#^def-c11-4-5|Def. §C11.4.5]]).
>
> *Why the name.* A hyperbolic equation, such as the wave equation, has a well-posed initial-value problem; "global" says the initial data on one surface reach the whole spacetime.
>
> *What it implies* (quoted; Wald §8.3 and §10.1–10.2, where these are theorems of Geroch and of Choquet-Bruhat and Geroch; not in the typed sources):
> 1. $M$ is homeomorphic to $\mathbf R \times \Sigma$, and there is a time function $\tau$ on $M$, increasing along every future-directed causal curve, whose level sets are Cauchy surfaces. Different Cauchy surfaces of one spacetime are homeomorphic.
> 2. Equivalently, $(M, g)$ has no almost-closed causal curves (strong causality) and every causal diamond $J^+(p) \cap J^-(q)$ is compact.
> 3. On a globally hyperbolic spacetime the Klein–Gordon and Maxwell equations have unique solutions for given data on a spacelike Cauchy surface. For Einstein's equations, data on a three-manifold satisfying the constraints have a unique maximal globally hyperbolic development: the largest spacetime, up to isometry, in which the data sit as a Cauchy surface.
>
> *Physical reading.* Global hyperbolicity is classical determinism in general relativity. Item 3 is what makes the strong cosmic censorship conjecture precise ([[#^rem-c11-4-2|Remark: Strong cosmic censorship]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.3, Remark after Definition 20, p. 101 (PDF 102) ("If a spacetime has a Cauchy surface (it need not) it is said to be globally hyperbolic") · Wald §8.3, §10.1–10.2 (quoted)*

^def-c11-4-6

Two spacetimes met earlier pass the test: flat space, and the full Kruskal spacetime with both its singularities. The proof is the same in both: along a causal curve a time coordinate increases strictly, and an inextendible curve cannot stop before it has run through every value of that time.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.4.1: Minkowski and Kruskal Are Globally Hyperbolic
> 1. **Minkowski.** In Minkowski space $\mathbf R^{1,n}$ ($n \ge 1$) every slice $\Sigma_c = \{t = c\}$ is a Cauchy surface ([[#^def-c11-4-5|Def. §C11.4.5]]).
> 2. **Kruskal.** In the Kruskal spacetime $\mathcal K$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]), with $T = \frac12(U + V)$, $X = \frac12(V - U)$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^def-c7-3-1|Def. §C7.3.1]]), every slice $\Sigma_c = \{T = c\}$ with $|c| < 1$ is a Cauchy surface.
> 3. **Infinitely many.** The time translations $t \mapsto t + t_0$ of Schwarzschild act on $\mathcal K$ as $(U, V) \mapsto (e^{-t_0/4M}U, e^{t_0/4M}V)$. They map $\Sigma_0$ to the slices $\{t = t_0\}$ of regions I and III joined through the bifurcation sphere, which are Cauchy surfaces as well.
>
> So both spacetimes are globally hyperbolic ([[#^def-c11-4-6|Def. §C11.4.6]]).
>
> *Model:* Minkowski space; the maximally extended Schwarzschild solution; *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.3, text before Example 5.5 and Example 5.5 with Fig. 24, p. 105 (PDF 106) (stated: "Kruskal spacetime … also admits an infinite family of Cauchy surfaces") · proof worked here*

^thm-c11-4-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Minkowski: $t$ along a causal curve). Time-orient by $\partial_t$. A future-directed causal velocity $\dot x$ has $-g(\partial_t, \dot x) = \dot t > 0$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]) and $-\dot t^2 + |\dot{\vec x}|^2 \le 0$. So $t$ is a parameter along the curve, and
> $$
> \Bigl|\frac{d\vec x}{dt}\Bigr| = \frac{|\dot{\vec x}|}{\dot t} \le 1 .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (an inextendible causal curve runs through every $t$). Let $\gamma$ be future-directed causal, parametrized by $t$ on an interval, and suppose $t$ is bounded above along it, $t_1 = \sup t < \infty$. By Step 1, $|\vec x(t) - \vec x(t')| \le |t - t'|$, so $\vec x(t)$ is Cauchy as $t \to t_1$ and converges to some $\vec x_1$. Then $\gamma(t) \to (t_1, \vec x_1) \in M$, a future endpoint ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-5|Def. §C10.4.5]]). Hence along a future-inextendible causal curve $t \to +\infty$, and by the same argument along a past-inextendible one $t \to -\infty$.
>
> **Step 3** (Minkowski: Cauchy). Along an inextendible causal curve $t$ is continuous, strictly increasing and unbounded in both directions, so it takes the value $c$ exactly once (intermediate value theorem). $\Sigma_c$ is the preimage of the closed set $\{c\}$ under the continuous function $t$, hence closed ([[§7 Closed Sets and Limit Points#^def-7-1|590 Def. §7.1]]), and achronal because $t$ increases along timelike curves ([[#^def-c11-4-1|Def. §C11.4.1]]). By the equivalent form in [[#^def-c11-4-5|Def. §C11.4.5]], $\Sigma_c$ is a Cauchy surface. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 4** (Kruskal: causal curves in the $(X, T)$ plane). By [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], part 2,
> $$
> ds^2 = \frac{32M^3}{r}\,e^{-r/2M}\bigl(-dT^2 + dX^2\bigr) + r^2d\Omega^2 , \qquad \mathcal K = \{T^2 - X^2 < 1\} \times S^2 ,
> $$
> time-oriented by $\partial_T$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]). Write $A(r) = (32M^3/r)e^{-r/2M} > 0$. For a future-directed causal velocity, $-g(\partial_T, \dot x) = A\dot T > 0$ and $A(-\dot T^2 + \dot X^2) + r^2|\dot\Omega|^2 \le 0$, so with $T$ as parameter
> $$
> \Bigl|\frac{dX}{dT}\Bigr| \le 1 , \qquad \Bigl|\frac{d\Omega}{dT}\Bigr|^2 \le \frac{A(r)}{r^2} ,
> $$
> where $|d\Omega|^2 = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2$.
>
> **Step 5** (the singularities can be approached only from the right side). The past singularity is $h_- \equiv T + \sqrt{1 + X^2} = 0$, the future one $h_+ \equiv T - \sqrt{1 + X^2} = 0$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], part 4). Along a future-directed causal curve, with $X' = dX/dT$ and Step 4,
> $$
> \frac{dh_\pm}{dT} = 1 \mp \frac{X X'}{\sqrt{1 + X^2}} \ \ge\ 1 - \frac{|X|}{\sqrt{1 + X^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + X^2}\,\bigl(\sqrt{1 + X^2} + |X|\bigr)} > 0 .
> $$
> Both increase. Since $h_- > 0 > h_+$ on $\mathcal K$, a future-directed curve moves away from the past singularity and towards the future one.
>
> **Step 6** (Kruskal: an inextendible causal curve crosses $T = c$). Let $\gamma$ be future-inextendible from a point with $T < c$, and suppose $T \le T_1$ along it with $T_1 \le c < 1$ (it never gets above $c$). Then $h_+ \le T_1 - 1 < 0$ and $h_- \ge h_-(\text{start}) > 0$ along $\gamma$, so $\gamma$ stays in a closed region at positive distance from both branches of $T^2 - X^2 = 1$. As in Step 2, $X(T)$ converges as $T \to T_1$, to some $X_1$ with $T_1^2 - X_1^2 < 1$; near that point $r$ is bounded away from $0$, so $|d\Omega/dT|$ is bounded and the angles converge too. That is a future endpoint in $\mathcal K$, a contradiction. So $\gamma$ reaches $T = c$. The past direction is the same with the roles of $h_+$ and $h_-$ exchanged, using $-\sqrt{1 + X^2} \le -1 < c$. As in Step 3, $T$ is strictly increasing, so an inextendible causal curve meets $\Sigma_c$ exactly once; $\Sigma_c$ lies inside $\mathcal K$ because $1 + X^2 - c^2 \ge 1 - c^2 > 0$, it is closed and achronal. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 7** (more Cauchy surfaces). The map $\phi_{t_0}(U, V) = (e^{-t_0/4M}U, e^{t_0/4M}V)$ keeps $UV$, hence $r$, and $dU\,dV$, so it is an isometry of $\mathcal K$; it keeps the signs of $dU$ and $dV$, hence the time orientation. On region I, $U = -e^{-u/4M}$ and $V = e^{v/4M}$ become $-e^{-(u + t_0)/4M}$ and $e^{(v + t_0)/4M}$: this is $t \mapsto t + t_0$. An isometry preserving time orientation maps inextendible causal curves to inextendible causal curves and achronal closed sets to achronal closed sets, so it maps Cauchy surfaces to Cauchy surfaces. The image of $\Sigma_0 = \{U + V = 0\}$ is $\{V = -e^{t_0/2M}U\}$. In region I, $V/(-U) = e^{t/2M}$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], part 1), so this is the slice $t = t_0$, continued through $U = V = 0$ into region III. Part 3 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - A Cauchy surface needs a time function along which no causal curve can stop early. In Minkowski space nothing can stop at all; in Kruskal, curves do stop at $r = 0$, but only at the future or past end of time, after (or before) crossing every $\Sigma_c$.
> - Global hyperbolicity is not the absence of singularities. Kruskal has two, and both are spacelike: they are moments, never a source of signals sideways.

## Reissner–Nordström: the inner horizon is a Cauchy horizon

The same test applied to Reissner–Nordström fails, and the Penrose diagram shows where. Two facts about the diagram coordinates carry the proof. In each chart the metric is $-G\,dU\,dV + r^2d\Omega^2$ with $G > 0$ and finite across the chart's horizon (Couzens (5.32), (5.34); [[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^def-c11-3-1|Def. §C11.3.1]]; at $M = 1$, $e = 0.8$ the script finds $G_+(r_+) = 6.908$, $G_-(r_-) = 0.0430$). With $dU = \sec^2\tilde u\,d\tilde u$, $dV = \sec^2\tilde v\,d\tilde v$ and $d\tilde u\,d\tilde v = \frac14(dy^2 - dx^2)$,
$$
ds^2 = -\frac{G}{4}\sec^2\tilde u\,\sec^2\tilde v\,\bigl(dy^2 - dx^2\bigr) + r^2d\Omega^2 .
$$
Both Kruskal-type coordinates increase to the future (in region I, $\partial_t = \kappa_+(-U_+\partial_{U_+} + V_+\partial_{V_+})$ has two positive components). Hence, as in Step 4 of [[#^der-c11-4-1|the derivation above]]: along a future-directed causal curve $y$ increases strictly and $|dx/dy| \le 1$, and every radial curve with $|dx/dy| < 1$ is timelike. Second, the singularity: $U_-V_- = F_-(r) = \operatorname{sgn}(r - r_-)\,e^{-2\kappa_-r_\ast}$ is $-1$ at $r = 0$ (Couzens (5.35), p. 92), and $\tan\tilde u\,\tan\tilde v = -1$ is $\cos(\tilde v - \tilde u) = 0$: $r = 0$ is the pair of vertical lines $x = \pm\pi/2$. Region VI is the triangle $0 < x < \pi/2$, $|y - \pi| < x$, region V its mirror image.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.4.2: Maximally Extended Reissner–Nordström Has No Cauchy Surface; r = r₋ Is a Cauchy Horizon
> Let $(M, g)$ be the maximal analytic extension of sub-extremal Reissner–Nordström ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-3|Theorem §C11.3.3]]), and $\Sigma$ the Einstein–Rosen slice: $t = 0$ in regions I and III together with the bifurcation sphere of $r_+$, the line $y = 0$ of the + chart.
> 1. $\Sigma$ is closed and achronal ([[#^def-c11-4-1|Def. §C11.4.1]]).
> 2. $D^+(\Sigma)$ is the part $y \ge 0$ of the + chart: the points of I, III, II and of the horizons $r = r_+$ between them on or above $\Sigma$. $D^-(\Sigma)$ is the part $y \le 0$, with IV. So $D(\Sigma)$ is the + chart, regions I–IV ([[#^def-c11-4-2|Def. §C11.4.2]], [[#^def-c11-4-4|Def. §C11.4.4]]).
> 3. $H^+(\Sigma)$ is the pair of null hypersurfaces $r = r_-$ that bound II to the future, together with their meeting sphere $B_-$ (the bifurcation sphere of $r_-$); $H^-(\Sigma)$ is the pair bounding IV to the past ([[#^def-c11-4-3|Def. §C11.4.3]]). Nothing in V, VI or beyond is determined by data on $\Sigma$.
> 4. $(M, g)$ has no Cauchy surface at all ([[#^def-c11-4-5|Def. §C11.4.5]]): it is not globally hyperbolic ([[#^def-c11-4-6|Def. §C11.4.6]]).
>
> *Model:* the eternal, maximally extended sub-extremal Reissner–Nordström spacetime, $0 < e^2 < M^2$; *applies:* exactly to that spacetime. A black hole formed by collapse has no region III, no white hole and no past copies, and its inner horizon is unstable ([[#^rem-c11-4-1|Remark: Blue-shift instability of the inner horizon]]). Parts 2–3 hold, with the same proof, for every closed achronal set that is a spacelike curve $y = h(x)$ from one $i^0$ to the other inside the + chart, such as the bent curve $\Sigma'$ through II in the figure: all candidate surfaces have the same Cauchy horizons (Couzens, Fig. 25).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.3, Example 5.6 and Fig. 25, pp. 105–106 (PDF 106–107) (stated: "does not admit a Cauchy surface and has Cauchy horizons at the inner horizon") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §19.5, Fig. 19.6 (PDF 378) (the chain of universes) · proof worked here*

^thm-c11-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the tower is ordered). In the + chart a future-directed causal curve has $y$ increasing (diagram facts above). Its future boundary inside $M$ consists of the two $r = r_-$ sheets of II and $B_-$; $\mathscr I^+$ and $i^+$ are not points of $M$. In the − chart, II is the quadrant $U_- < 0$, $V_- < 0$, and $U_-$, $V_-$ only increase to the future. So a future-directed causal curve that has left the closure of II through an $r_-$ sheet has $U_- > 0$ or $V_- > 0$ from then on, and never returns to II or to anything below it. The same holds at each later horizon of the tower.
>
> **Step 2** (part 1). Let $\gamma$ be a future-directed timelike curve starting on $\Sigma$. While in the + chart it has $y > 0$; after leaving through an $r_-$ sheet it never returns (Step 1). So it does not meet $\Sigma = \{y = 0\}$ again: $\Sigma$ is achronal. $\Sigma$ is the zero set of the continuous function $y$ on the open + chart, and its only limit points outside the chart would be $i^0$, which is not in $M$; so $\Sigma$ is closed in $M$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 2, $\supseteq$). Let $p = (x_p, y_p)$ be in the + chart with $y_p \ge 0$, and $\gamma$ a past-inextendible causal curve from $p$. Going to the past, $y$ decreases and $|x - x_p| \le y_p - y$, so as long as $y \ge 0$ the curve stays in the closed triangle
> $$
> K = \bigl\{(x, y) : 0 \le y \le y_p,\ |x - x_p| \le y_p - y\bigr\} ,
> $$
> whose bottom corners $x_p \pm y_p$ lie strictly inside $(-\pi, \pi)$ because $|x_p| + y_p < \pi$. So $K \times S^2$ is a compact subset of the chart. Suppose $\gamma$ never reaches $y = 0$. Then $y$ decreases to some $y^\ast \ge 0$, $x(y)$ converges ($|dx/dy| \le 1$), and the angles converge because $|d\Omega/dy|^2 \le G\sec^2\tilde u\sec^2\tilde v/(4r^2)$ is bounded on $K$. The limit is a past endpoint in $M$: a contradiction. So $\gamma$ meets $y = 0$, i.e. $\Sigma$, and $p \in D^+(\Sigma)$. The reflection $y \mapsto -y$ gives $D^-(\Sigma) \supseteq \{y \le 0\}$.
>
> **Step 4** (part 2, nothing more: V and VI). Let $p = (x_p, y_p) \in$ VI, so $0 < x_p < \pi/2$ and $x_p + y_p > \pi$. The radial curve from $p$ with $dy/dx = -1/(1 - \varepsilon)$, run towards larger $x$, is past-directed and timelike. It reaches $x = \pi/2$ at the height
> $$
> y_p - \frac{\pi/2 - x_p}{1 - \varepsilon} > \frac{\pi}{2} \quad\text{for small } \varepsilon > 0, \qquad\text{since } y_p - \frac\pi2 > \frac\pi2 - x_p ,
> $$
> so it stays in VI and runs into $r = 0$, which is not part of $M$. It has no past endpoint: it is past-inextendible, and it never meets $\Sigma$. So $p \notin D^+(\Sigma)$. Region V is the mirror image. For $p \in$ IV′ the same straight line in the − chart works: $y_p > \pi + |x_p|$ gives $x_p + y_p > \pi$, and the line passes from IV′ into VI before reaching $r = 0$. Points of later regions have past-directed timelike curves into IV′ or a later copy of it, so they are excluded the same way.
>
> **Step 5** (part 2, nothing more: the sheets). Let $p$ lie on the $r_-$ sheet $x + y = \pi$, $0 < x < \pi/2$, that bounds II towards VI. Its null generator through $p$, followed to the past, runs down the sheet to the corner $(\pi/2, \pi/2)$, which is $i^+$ of region I, not a point of $M$. It is a past-inextendible causal curve that never meets $\Sigma$. So the sheet, its mirror image, and $B_-$ (whose generators run along both sheets) are not in $D^+(\Sigma)$. With Steps 3–4: $D^+(\Sigma) = \{y \ge 0\}$ in the + chart, exactly.
>
> **Step 6** (part 3). The closure of $D^+(\Sigma)$ adds to $\{y \ge 0\}$ the two $r_-$ sheets of II and $B_-$, the limits of points of II that lie outside the chart. A point $a$ of the chart with $y_a \ge 0$ is in $I^-(D^+(\Sigma))$: the point a little above it, at the same $x$, is in $D^+(\Sigma)$ and is reached by a vertical, hence timelike, segment. A point on a sheet is not: its chronological future lies beyond the sheet (Step 1), outside $D^+(\Sigma)$. So $H^+(\Sigma) = \overline{D^+(\Sigma)} \setminus I^-(D^+(\Sigma))$ is the two sheets with $B_-$. The generators found in Step 5 have no past endpoint in $M$, as the quoted structure in [[#^def-c11-4-3|Def. §C11.4.3]] requires for an edgeless $\Sigma$. The reflection $y \mapsto -y$ gives $H^-(\Sigma)$.
>
> **Step 7** (part 4, two inextendible observers). Fix $r_0 > r_+$. The static observer at $r = r_0$ and fixed angles in region I is timelike ($f(r_0) > 0$). As $t \to +\infty$, $V_+ = e^{\kappa_+(t + r_\ast(r_0))} \to \infty$ and $U_+ \to 0^-$; as $t \to -\infty$, $V_+ \to 0^+$ and $U_+ \to -\infty$. It runs from the corner $i^-$ to the corner $i^+$ of region I, neither of which is a point of $M$, so it is inextendible. The same holds for the static observer at $r_0$ in the next exterior I′.
>
> **Step 8** (part 4, every point of I′ is in the chronological future of every point of I). Let $a = (x_a, y_a) \in$ I, so $x_a > |y_a|$ and $x_a + y_a < \pi$, and let $b \in$ I′ have coordinates $(x_b, y'_b)$ in the next + chart, $x_b > |y'_b|$, $x_b - y'_b < \pi$.
> - *From $a$ into II.* Pick $q = (0, \pi - \delta)$ with $0 < \delta < \pi - x_a - y_a$. Then $\Delta y = \pi - \delta - y_a > x_a = |\Delta x|$: the straight radial segment from $a$ to $q$ is timelike, and it lies in the convex diamond of the + chart.
> - *Through $B_-$.* The points with $x = 0$ in II are $U_+ = V_+$, i.e. $t = 0$, since $V_+/U_+ = e^{2\kappa_+t}$ there. The curve $t = 0$, $r$ decreasing from $r(q)$ to $r_-$, then through $B_-$ (the origin of the − chart, where it is the line $U_- = V_-$), then $r$ increasing from $r_-$ into IV′, is timelike: off $B_-$ it has $ds^2 = dr^2/f < 0$ because $f < 0$ in II and in IV′.
> - *From IV′ into I′.* In the next + chart IV′ is the bottom quadrant and its $t = 0$ line is $x = 0$. Take $s = (0, -\varepsilon)$ there with $x_b - y'_b < \varepsilon < \pi$. Then $\Delta y = y'_b + \varepsilon > x_b = |\Delta x|$: the segment from $s$ to $b$ is timelike.
>
> Joined, these pieces form a future-directed timelike curve from $a$ to $b$: $b \in I^+(a)$.
>
> **Step 9** (part 4, conclusion). Suppose $\hat\Sigma$ were a Cauchy surface. By the equivalent form of [[#^def-c11-4-5|Def. §C11.4.5]] it meets the inextendible observer of Step 7 in I at some $a$, and the one in I′ at some $b$. By Step 8, $b \in I^+(a)$: two points of $\hat\Sigma$ joined by a timelike curve. This contradicts achronality. Hence no Cauchy surface exists.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Determination fails for two reasons, both new compared with Kruskal. The singularity is timelike, so past-inextendible curves can come out of it sideways (Step 4). And the future of one universe contains the next universe whole (Step 8), so no single instant can cut through all of them.
> - The Cauchy horizon is the boundary of what $\Sigma$ determines, and in Reissner–Nordström it coincides with the Killing horizon $r = r_-$ of [[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-2|Theorem §C11.3.2]]. The two notions are different: a Killing horizon is defined by a symmetry, a Cauchy horizon by a set of initial data.
> - Its generators come from $i^+$ of region I (Step 5). An observer crossing the sheet next to VI sees, in a finite time, everything that falls in from region I during its infinite future ([[#^ex-c11-4-4|Example §C11.4.4]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Blue-shift instability of the inner horizon
> [[#^ex-c11-4-4|Example §C11.4.4]] computes what an observer crossing the Cauchy horizon next to region VI receives from region I: the whole infinite future of region I, compressed into a finite proper time, with a frequency that grows like $e^{\kappa_-v}$ in the advanced time $v$ of the emission. Penrose's argument (Couzens p. 106, his Alice and Bob) turns this into an instability. Any small perturbation falling into the hole, such as the tail of radiation from its formation, is blue-shifted without bound at $r_-$, and its energy density measured there diverges. A linear field on the fixed background already shows this (Simpson and Penrose, *Int. J. Theor. Phys.* 7 (1973) 183). Once the field's energy is allowed to curve spacetime, it inflates the mass function near $r_-$ without bound (Poisson and Israel, *Phys. Rev. D* 41 (1990) 1796, "mass inflation"). Both are research results, quoted, not in the typed sources.
>
> *Consequences.* The smooth region beyond $r_-$, with its timelike singularity, its white hole and its further universes, is not expected to survive any perturbation. The model's caveat on [[#^thm-c11-4-2|Theorem §C11.4.2]] is therefore not a technicality: what is robust is the exterior and the region II up to near $r_-$; what lies past $r_-$ is a property of the exact, eternal solution only. Couzens adds that a collapsing star never produces the maximal extension anyway (p. 106: an "eternal black hole"). He reports the expectation that a spacelike singularity forms. The research literature he alludes to ("still a very active topic") finds, for spherical charged collapse, a weak null singularity along the Cauchy horizon, through which the metric stays continuous while the mass function blows up (Dafermos, *Comm. Pure Appl. Math.* 58 (2005), arXiv:gr-qc/0307013; research literature, not in the typed sources; references checked 2026-11-20); see [[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^cau-c10-7-1|§C10.7, Caution: Strong vs weak cosmic censorship]] for the corresponding Kerr result of Dafermos and Luk. The same argument discards the region beyond the inner horizon of Kerr ([[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^rem-c12-2-1|§C12.2, Remark: What the extension describes]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Strong cosmic censorship
> *The conjecture* (Penrose; stated loosely by Couzens, p. 106, as "Penrose's strong cosmic censorship hypothesis"). For generic asymptotically flat initial data for Einstein's equations with reasonable matter, the maximal globally hyperbolic development ([[#^def-c11-4-6|Def. §C11.4.6]], item 3) is inextendible ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-7|Def. §C10.4.7]]). In words: the future is determined by the data everywhere it exists, and there are no Cauchy horizons ([[#^def-c11-4-3|Def. §C11.4.3]]) to step through.
>
> *Reissner–Nordström as the test case.* The data on the Einstein–Rosen slice $\Sigma$ have the development $D(\Sigma)$, regions I–IV ([[#^thm-c11-4-2|Theorem §C11.4.2]]). This development can be extended, smoothly and even analytically, across $r = r_-$: the conclusion of the conjecture fails. The conjecture survives only because Reissner–Nordström data are not generic, and the blue-shift instability ([[#^rem-c11-4-1|Remark: Blue-shift instability of the inner horizon]]) is the evidence that generic data close the door at $r_-$. Kerr has the same inner horizon ([[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-6|Theorem §C12.2.6]], part 7). There too the region beyond it is believed not to form in a collapse ([[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^rem-c12-2-1|§C12.2, Remark: What the extension describes]]).
>
> *Strong is not weak.* Weak cosmic censorship ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^rem-c10-7-1|§C10.7, Remark: Weak cosmic censorship]]) concerns what distant observers can see. Strong censorship concerns observers who fall in: the Cauchy horizon of Reissner–Nordström lies inside the black hole, invisible from $\mathscr I^+$. Couzens names the weak conjecture "strong" in §4.5 and uses the word correctly here ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^cau-c10-7-1|§C10.7, Caution: Strong vs weak cosmic censorship]], where the status of the conjecture, after Dafermos and Luk, is recorded: false in the form that demands inextendibility as a continuous metric, open in weaker-regularity forms).

Read as "goes on forever", the definition would ignore them and put V and VI inside $D^+(\Sigma)$. ## Examples

> [!example] Example §C11.4.1: A Bounded Segment in 2D Minkowski Space
> *Question.* In two-dimensional Minkowski space, $ds^2 = -dt^2 + dx^2$, let $S = \{t = 0,\ |x| \le a\}$, $a > 0$ (Couzens' Example 5.1, with a straight segment). Find $D^\pm(S)$, $D(S)$ and $H^\pm(S)$.
>
> **Step 1** ($S$ is closed and achronal). $S$ is a closed interval of the line $t = 0$, achronal because $t$ increases along timelike curves.
>
> **Step 2** (points inside the triangle). Let $p = (t_0, x_0)$ with $t_0 \ge 0$ and $t_0 + |x_0| \le a$, and $\gamma$ a past-inextendible causal curve from $p$. By Steps 1–2 of [[#^der-c11-4-1|the derivation of Theorem §C11.4.1]], $t \to -\infty$ along $\gamma$ and $|x - x_0| \le t_0 - t$. At the moment $t = 0$, $|x| \le |x_0| + t_0 \le a$: $\gamma$ meets $S$. So $p \in D^+(S)$.
>
> **Step 3** (points outside). Let $t_0 \ge 0$ and $t_0 + |x_0| > a$, say $x_0 \ge 0$. The past-directed null ray $(t_0 - s, x_0 + s)$, $s \ge 0$, is inextendible and crosses $t = 0$ at $x = x_0 + t_0 > a$, outside $S$; it never returns to $t = 0$. So $p \notin D^+(S)$. Points with $t_0 < 0$ are not in $D^+(S)$ either, since the vertical past ray from them never reaches $t = 0$. Hence
> $$
> D^+(S) = \{0 \le t \le a - |x|\}, \qquad D^-(S) = \{-(a - |x|) \le t \le 0\}, \qquad D(S) = \{|t| + |x| \le a\} .
> $$
> $D(S)$ is a closed and bounded square, compact, of area $2a^2$.
>
> **Step 4** ($H^+$). $D^+(S)$ is closed, so $\overline{D^+(S)} = D^+(S)$. A point with $t_0 + |x_0| < a$ has points of $D^+(S)$ directly above it: it lies in $I^-(D^+(S))$. A point with $t_0 = a - |x_0|$ has chronological future $\{t - t_0 > |x - x_0|\}$, which lies entirely in $t + |x| > a$, outside $D^+(S)$. So
> $$
> H^+(S) = \{t = a - |x|,\ |x| \le a\} ,
> $$
> the two null segments from the ends $(0, \pm a)$ of $S$ to the tip $(a, 0)$. Their generators end in the past at the edge of $S$, its two endpoints, as [[#^def-c11-4-3|Def. §C11.4.3]] says they must. The boundary of $D^+(S)$ in the plane is $H^+(S)$ together with $S$.
>
> *Result.* A bounded piece of an instant determines a bounded diamond, and the edges of the diamond are null: determination spreads inwards from the ends of $S$ at the speed of light. Figure below, panel (a).
>

> [!example] Example §C11.4.2: The Hyperboloid t = −√(1 + x²)
> *Question.* Couzens' Example 5.2: in two-dimensional Minkowski space let $S = \{t = -\sqrt{1 + x^2}\}$, eq. (5.46). It is unbounded, has no edge, and looks like an instant. Is it a Cauchy surface?
>
> **Step 1** ($S$ is spacelike, closed and achronal). Its slope is
> $$
> \frac{dt}{dx} = -\frac{x}{\sqrt{1 + x^2}}, \qquad \Bigl|\frac{dt}{dx}\Bigr| < 1 ,
> $$
> so it is spacelike, and for two of its points the mean value theorem gives $|\Delta t| < |\Delta x|$: never timelike separated. It is closed, the graph of a continuous function. As $|x| \to \infty$, $-\sqrt{1 + x^2} = -|x| - \frac{1}{2|x|} + \dots$: $S$ approaches the past light cone $t = -|x|$ of the origin from below.
>
> **Step 2** (inside the past cone of the origin, on or above $S$). Let $p = (t_0, x_0)$ with $-\sqrt{1 + x_0^2} \le t_0 < -|x_0|$, so $t_0 + |x_0| = -\delta$ with $\delta > 0$. Let $\gamma$ be past-inextendible and causal from $p$; then $t \to -\infty$ and $|x - x_0| \le t_0 - t$, so along $\gamma$
> $$
> t + |x| \le t_0 + |x_0| = -\delta , \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad |x| \le T - \delta \quad\text{with } T = -t .
> $$
> Then $\sqrt{1 + x^2} \le \sqrt{1 + (T - \delta)^2}$, and $1 + (T - \delta)^2 - T^2 = 1 + \delta^2 - 2T\delta < 0$ as soon as $T > (1 + \delta^2)/(2\delta)$. From then on $t + \sqrt{1 + x^2} < 0$: $\gamma$ is below $S$. At $p$ the same function is $\ge 0$. By continuity $\gamma$ meets $S$, and $p \in D^+(S)$.
>
> **Step 3** (on or above the cone). Let $t_0 \ge -|x_0|$, say $x_0 \ge 0$. Along the past null ray $(t_0 - s, x_0 + s)$, $t + x = t_0 + x_0 \ge 0$, so $t \ge -x > -\sqrt{1 + x^2}$: the ray stays above $S$ for ever and is past-inextendible. So $p \notin D^+(S)$. This includes the cone $t = -|x|$ itself: a light ray on it runs parallel to the asymptote of $S$ and never meets $S$.
>
> **Step 4** (below $S$). Let $t_0 \le -\sqrt{1 + x_0^2}$, and $\gamma$ future-inextendible and causal from $p$; along it $t \to +\infty$. The function $h = t + \sqrt{1 + x^2}$ is $\le 0$ at $p$, and $dh/dt = 1 + xx'/\sqrt{1 + x^2} \ge 1 - |x|/\sqrt{1 + x^2} > 0$ (Step 5 of [[#^der-c11-4-1|the derivation of Theorem §C11.4.1]]), so $h > 0$ once $t > -1$. Hence $\gamma$ meets $S$, and every point on or below $S$ lies in $D^-(S)$; no point above $S$ does, since the vertical future ray from it never meets $S$.
>
> **Step 5** (result). With Steps 2–4,
> $$
> D^+(S) = \bigl\{-\sqrt{1 + x^2} \le t < -|x|\bigr\}, \qquad D^-(S) = \bigl\{t \le -\sqrt{1 + x^2}\bigr\} ,
> $$
> Couzens' (5.47). So $D(S) = \{t < -|x|\}$, the open chronological past of the origin, not the whole plane: $S$ is not a Cauchy surface. $D^+(S)$ is not closed: its closure adds the cone $t = -|x|$, from which no timelike curve re-enters $D^+(S)$, so $H^+(S) = \{t = -|x|\}$. Its generators have no past endpoint: followed into the past they run off to infinity, as for an edgeless $S$ they must ([[#^def-c11-4-3|Def. §C11.4.3]]).
>
> *Result.* Closed, achronal, spacelike and edgeless is not enough. Light rays that come in from infinity parallel to the asymptotes of $S$ slip past it, and everything to the future of the origin's light cone is beyond the reach of the data. The difference from the slice $t = c$ of [[#^thm-c11-4-1|Theorem §C11.4.1]] is at infinity, not anywhere in the picture. Figure below, panel (b).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.3, Example 5.2, eqs. (5.46)–(5.47), Fig. 21, pp. 102–103 (PDF 103–104) · derivation worked here; (5.47) checked by script (null-ray test on 1499 random points, 300 random causal curves)*

> [!example] Example §C11.4.3: Removing a Point from S or from Spacetime
> *Question.* Couzens' Examples 5.3–5.4: how do $D^+$ and $H^+$ change when one point is missing, (a) from $S$, (b) from spacetime?
>
> **(a) From $S$.** In two-dimensional Minkowski space let $S = \{t = 0\} \setminus \{o\}$, $o$ the origin. $S$ is achronal but not closed; the defining condition of [[#^def-c11-4-2|Def. §C11.4.2]] still makes sense for it, and Couzens uses it this way.
> - *Step 1.* If $p \in J^+(o)$ ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^def-c10-7-1|Def. §C10.7.1]]), take a causal curve from $p$ to $o$ and continue it as the vertical line $x = 0$ into the past. It is past-inextendible, and it crosses $t = 0$ only at $o \notin S$. So $p \notin D^+(S)$.
> - *Step 2.* If $t_0 \ge 0$ and $p \notin J^+(o)$, every past-inextendible causal curve from $p$ reaches $t = 0$ (as in [[#^ex-c11-4-1|Example §C11.4.1]], Step 2) at a point $\ne o$, because $o \notin J^-(p)$. So $p \in D^+(S)$.
> - *Result.* $D^+(S) = \{t \ge 0\} \setminus J^+(o)$, and by the argument of [[#^ex-c11-4-1|Example §C11.4.1]], Step 4, $H^+(S) = \{t = |x|\}$: the future light cone of the missing point. One missing point removes its whole causal future.
>
> **(b) From spacetime.** Let $M = \mathbf R^2 \setminus \{q\}$ with $q = (t_q, 0)$, $t_q > 0$, and $S = \{t = 0\}$, closed and achronal in $M$.
> - *Step 1.* If $p \in J^+(q)$ (computed in $\mathbf R^2$) and $p \ne q$, a causal curve from $p$ towards $q$ has no past endpoint in $M$: its endpoint would be $q$, which is not there ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^ex-c10-4-2|Example §C10.4.2]], Step 2). It is past-inextendible and never reaches $S$. So $p \notin D^+(S)$; every other point with $t \ge 0$ is in $D^+(S)$, as in (a). Hence $D^+(S) = \{t \ge 0\} \setminus J^+(q)$, $D^-(S) = \{t \le 0\}$, and $H^+(S)$ is the light cone of $q$ without $q$.
> - *Step 2* (no Cauchy surface at all). Suppose $\hat\Sigma$ were one. The worldline $x = 0$ splits at the hole into two inextendible timelike curves, $t < t_q$ and $t > t_q$; $\hat\Sigma$ meets the first at some $a = (t_a, 0)$ and the second at some $b = (t_b, 0)$. The polyline $a \to (t_q, \varepsilon) \to b$ with $0 < \varepsilon < \min(t_q - t_a, t_b - t_q)$ has both legs timelike ($\Delta t > |\Delta x| = \varepsilon$) and misses $q$. So $b \in I^+(a)$, contradicting achronality. $M$ is not globally hyperbolic.
>
> *Result.* A hole in spacetime is felt like a hole in the data: it removes its future light cone from $D^+$. Part (b), Step 2, is the argument of [[#^thm-c11-4-2|Theorem §C11.4.2]], part 4, in miniature, with the hole in place of the timelike singularity and the detour in place of the passage through $B_-$. Figure below, panels (c)–(d).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.3, Examples 5.3–5.4, Figs. 22–23, pp. 103–105 (PDF 104–106) · worked here; regions checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C11.4.4: The Blue Shift at the Inner Horizon
> *Question.* Alice stays at large $r$ in region I and sends radial signals of fixed frequency $\omega_A$ into the hole. Bob is in region II on a radial geodesic and crosses $r = r_-$. What frequency $\omega_B$ does he measure? Take $M = 1$, $e = 0.8$: $r_+ = 1.6$, $r_- = 0.4$, $\kappa_- = (r_+ - r_-)/(2r_-^2) = 3.75$.
>
> **Step 1** (Bob's geodesic). $\partial_t$ is a Killing field, so $E = -g(\partial_t, u) = f\dot t$ is constant along Bob's geodesic (overdot: $d/d\tau$). With $-f\dot t^2 + \dot r^2/f = -1$ and $r$ decreasing towards the future in II,
> $$
> \dot r = -\sqrt{E^2 - f} .
> $$
> In II, $f < 0$, so $E$ can have either sign: $\partial_t$ is spacelike there.
>
> **Step 2** (the signals). Alice's signals travel on ingoing radial null rays $v = \text{const}$, and the advanced time $v = t + r_\ast$ continues from I into II along them ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-1|Theorem §C11.3.1]]). On Bob's worldline
> $$
> \dot v = \dot t + \frac{\dot r}{f} = \frac{E - \sqrt{E^2 - f}}{f} .
> $$
> Alice, static at $r_A$, emits two crests on the rays $v$ and $v + dv$ a proper time $d\tau_A = \sqrt{f(r_A)}\,dv$ apart; Bob receives them $d\tau_B = dv/\dot v$ apart. So
> $$
> \frac{\omega_B}{\omega_A} = \frac{d\tau_A}{d\tau_B} = \sqrt{f(r_A)}\;\dot v \ \longrightarrow\ \dot v \qquad (r_A \to \infty) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (near $r_-$: three cases). As $r \to r_-$ from above, $f \to 0^-$. Expanding $\sqrt{E^2 - f} = |E| - f/(2|E|) + O(f^2)$:
> - $E > 0$: $E - \sqrt{E^2 - f} = f/(2E) + O(f^2)$, so $\dot v \to 1/(2E)$, finite. Bob crosses the sheet of $r_-$ that bounds II towards V at a finite $v$, where the ingoing coordinates $(v, r)$ are regular, and receives only signals sent before that $v$. Every geodesic coming from region I has $E > 0$ (there $\partial_t$ is future-directed timelike). A fall from rest at infinity, $E = 1$, ends with $\omega_B/\omega_A \to \frac12$: a red shift.
> - $E = 0$: $\dot v = 1/\sqrt{-f} \to \infty$ and $t$ is constant: the curve runs into $B_-$.
> - $E < 0$ (Bob came from region III, or fired a rocket inside II): $E - \sqrt{E^2 - f} \to 2E$, so $\dot v \approx 2E/f \to +\infty$. Bob crosses the sheet towards VI, where $v = \infty$: the Cauchy horizon whose generators come from $i^+$ of region I ([[#^thm-c11-4-2|Theorem §C11.4.2]], Step 5).
>
> **Step 4** (the exponential, $E < 0$). Near $r_-$, $f \approx f'(r_-)(r - r_-) = -2\kappa_-(r - r_-)$ ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-2|Theorem §C11.3.2]]). Then
> $$
> \frac{dv}{dr} = \frac{\dot v}{\dot r} \approx \frac{2E/f}{-|E|} = \frac{2}{f} \approx -\frac{1}{\kappa_-(r - r_-)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v \approx -\frac{1}{\kappa_-}\ln(r - r_-) + \text{const}, \qquad r - r_- \propto e^{-\kappa_-v} ,
> $$
> and
> $$
> \frac{\omega_B}{\omega_A} \approx \frac{2E}{f} = \frac{|E|}{\kappa_-(r - r_-)} \ \propto\ e^{\kappa_-v} .
> $$
> Two signals that Alice sends an advanced time $\Delta v$ apart arrive with blue shifts in the ratio $e^{\kappa_-\Delta v}$. Bob's proper time stays finite: $d\tau = dr/\dot r$ and $1/\sqrt{E^2 - f} \to 1/|E|$, so $\tau$ to $r_-$ is finite while $v \to \infty$. All of Alice's future reaches Bob before he crosses.
>
> **Step 5** (numbers, $E = -1$, from the script). From $r = 1.0$ to $r_-$ Bob needs $\tau = 0.499\,M$. Measuring $v$ from its value at $r = 1$:
>
> | $r$ | $v$ | $\tau$ | $\omega_B/\omega_A$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 0.9 | 0.468 | 0.085 | 5.08 |
> | 0.41 | 2.612 | 0.489 | 28.7 |
> | 0.401 | 3.236 | 0.498 | 269 |
> | 0.4001 | 3.851 | 0.4988 | 2669 |
> | 0.40001 | 4.465 | 0.4988 | 26669 |
>
> Each further $\Delta v = 0.614$ multiplies the frequency by $10 = e^{3.75 \times 0.614}$; the fitted slope of $\ln(\omega_B/\omega_A)$ against $v$ is $3.748$, against $\kappa_- = 3.75$. One unit of Alice's time, $\Delta v = 1\,M$, gives the factor $e^{3.75} = 42.5$; $\Delta v = 10\,M$ gives $1.9\times10^{16}$. Restored: for $M = 10\,M_\odot$, $GM/c^3 = 49.3\ \mu\mathrm s$, so one millisecond of Alice's time is $\Delta v = 20.3\,M$ and the factor is about $10^{33}$.
>
> *Result.* At the part of the inner horizon where $v = \infty$, the infinite future of the outside is received in a finite proper time and blue-shifted as $e^{\kappa_-v}$. It is the mirror image of the red shift $e^{-\kappa u}$ of a lamp that falls through an event horizon ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^ex-c10-2-3|Example §C10.2.3]]), with the surface gravity $\kappa_-$ of $r_-$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-4|Def. §C10.5.4]]) in place of $\kappa$. Geodesic infallers from our universe meet the other sheet and see nothing infinite in exact Reissner–Nordström; in a black hole formed by collapse there is no region III, and the sheet at $v = \infty$ is the Cauchy horizon that remains. This is the input to [[#^rem-c11-4-1|Remark: Blue-shift instability of the inner horizon]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.2.2, p. 94 (PDF 95) ("They see this infinitely long history in a finite proper time thus any signal that gets to them as they approach r = r₋ is infinitely blue-shifted"); §5.3, p. 106 (PDF 107) (Alice and Bob) · computation worked here; limits and numbers checked by script*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - **Horizons of three kinds.** The event horizon is defined by $\mathscr I^+$ ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^def-c10-7-3|Def. §C10.7.3]]), a Killing horizon by a symmetry ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-3|Def. §C10.5.3]]), a Cauchy horizon by a set of data ([[#^def-c11-4-3|Def. §C11.4.3]]). In Reissner–Nordström, $r_+$ is the first two and $r_-$ the last two. The Rindler horizon of [[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-3|Theorem §C10.2.3]] is a Killing horizon and none of the others in Minkowski space; it is the Cauchy horizon of the Rindler wedge's own slice $\eta = 0$ if the wedge is regarded as the whole spacetime.
> - **Singularity theorems.** Penrose's theorem assumes a non-compact Cauchy surface ([[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^rem-c10-6-1|§C10.6, Remark: Penrose's singularity theorem]]) and the cosmological theorem a compact one ([[§C10.8 Timelike Congruences and the Strong Energy Condition|§C10.8]]); [[#^def-c11-4-5|Def. §C11.4.5]] and [[#^def-c11-4-6|Def. §C11.4.6]] are those assumptions.
> - **Initial-value problem.** The count of degrees of freedom in [[§C5.4 Diffeomorphism Invariance#^thm-c5-4-5|Theorem §C5.4.5]] presupposes data on a spacelike slice; [[#^def-c11-4-6|Def. §C11.4.6]], item 3, is the global version (the 3 + 1 formulation, planned).
> - **Level B.** The Klein–Gordon field of [[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation|§B4.1]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) is fixed by its value and time derivative on $t = 0$, a Cauchy surface of Minkowski space by [[#^thm-c11-4-1|Theorem §C11.4.1]].
> - **Forward.** The charges of the two exteriors joined by $\Sigma$: [[§C11.5 Charges in Curved Spacetime|§C11.5]]. The inner horizon of Kerr and its Cauchy horizon: [[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-6|Theorem §C12.2.6]], part 7; [[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^rem-c12-2-1|§C12.2, Remark: What the extension describes]].
