The chapter asks what changes when a black hole carries electric charge. [[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory|§C11.1]] set up the equations: outside all charges the Maxwell field obeys the source-free Maxwell equations, and its energy–momentum tensor is the source of curvature, $R_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$ with $R = 0$ ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^thm-c11-1-1|Theorem §C11.1.1]], [[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^mod-c11-1-2|Model §C11.1.2]], [[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^thm-c11-1-3|Theorem §C11.1.3]]). This section solves them with spherical symmetry, as [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem|§C6.2]] solved the vacuum equations.

Start with the naive repair of Schwarzschild. A charge $Q$ at rest has a Coulomb field $E = Q/r^2$ (Gaussian units), and that field carries energy. Count the energy as extra mass at the centre, $f = 1 - 2(M_0 + U)/r$. The energy density of the field is $E^2/8\pi$, the Gaussian form of $\varepsilon_0E^2/2$ ([[§B2.3 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#^def-b2-3-1|EM Def. §B2.3.1]], SI there). Outside a radius $a$ it adds up to

$$
U(a) = \int_a^\infty\frac{Q^2}{8\pi s^4}\,4\pi s^2\,ds = \frac{Q^2}{2}\int_a^\infty\frac{ds}{s^2} = \frac{Q^2}{2a} \xrightarrow{\ a \to 0\ } \infty .
$$

For a point charge the extra mass is infinite: the classical self-energy of a point charge ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^thm-c2-3-7|EM Theorem §C2.3.7]], part 2). A charge spread over a ball of radius $a$ has a finite total, but the repair still fails outside the ball. Any metric $f = 1 - 2M_{\rm tot}/r$ with a constant $M_{\rm tot}$ has $R_{\theta\theta} = 1 - (rf)' = 1 - 1 = 0$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]], combination 2), while the field equation demands $R_{\theta\theta} = 8\pi T_{\theta\theta} = Q^2/r^2$ ([[#^thm-c11-2-2|Theorem §C11.2.2]], Step 4 of its derivation). The field's energy is not at the centre: an amount $Q^2/2r$ of it lies outside every sphere of radius $r$.

That suggests the working idea. A distant astronomer weighs the whole system, field included, and finds a mass $M$. The mass "inside radius $r$" should then be $M$ minus the field energy outside $r$, $m(r) = M - Q^2/2r$. Put it into the form that the metric of a static star takes with its mass function, $g^{rr} = 1 - 2m(r)/r$ ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^def-c9-1-1|Def. §C9.1.1]]):

$$
f(r) = 1 - \frac{2}{r}\Bigl(M - \frac{Q^2}{2r}\Bigr) = 1 - \frac{2M}{r} + \frac{Q^2}{r^2} .
$$

This is the Reissner–Nordström metric. The argument is a heuristic: it uses a flat-space energy density, the flat volume element $4\pi s^2ds$ and Newtonian bookkeeping of mass. The section derives the same $f$ from the field equations and then explains why the heuristic works.

The steps are these. Spherical symmetry and Maxwell's equations fix the field up to two constants, an electric $Q$ and a magnetic $P$ ([[#^thm-c11-2-1|Theorem §C11.2.1]]). The field equations, solved with the curvature already computed in §C6.2, give the metric ([[#^thm-c11-2-2|Theorem §C11.2.2]]), and Birkhoff's argument carries over ([[#^thm-c11-2-3|Theorem §C11.2.3]]). The constants are the mass and the charges measured far away ([[#^thm-c11-2-4|Theorem §C11.2.4]]). The sign of $M^2 - Q^2 - P^2$ sorts the solutions into three cases ([[#^def-c11-2-1|Def. §C11.2.1]]), which differ in the zeros of $f$: two horizons, one, or none ([[#^thm-c11-2-5|Theorem §C11.2.5]]). A curvature invariant locates the one true singularity at $r = 0$ ([[#^thm-c11-2-6|Theorem §C11.2.6]]), and the mass function makes the opening heuristic exact ([[#^thm-c11-2-7|Theorem §C11.2.7]]).

**Setup.** A region of spacetime free of charges and currents, with $\Lambda = 0$: the electrovacuum of [[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^mod-c11-1-2|Model §C11.1.2]]. Outside a charged star, or around a charged black hole. The metric is spherically symmetric ([[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]]) and so is the Maxwell field: it is unchanged by the rotations. The coordinates are those of [[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]], part 4, with $r$ the areal radius ([[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^def-c6-1-2|Def. §C6.1.2]]); they exist where the gradient of $r$ is spacelike. The results are exact statements about the Einstein–Maxwell equations. The examples treat test bodies, and their numbers were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$ and, in this chapter, **Gaussian electromagnetism**, $\mu_0 = 4\pi$, $\varepsilon_0 = 1/4\pi$ ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^thm-c11-1-1|Theorem §C11.1.1]]): $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = -4\pi J^\nu$, $T_{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{4\pi}\bigl(F_{\mu\rho}F_\nu{}^\rho - \frac14g_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}F^{\rho\sigma}\bigr)$ ([[§C5.3 Coupling to Matter꞉ the Hilbert Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c5-3-3|Theorem §C5.3.3]] with $\mu_0 = 4\pi$). In an orthonormal frame $F_{\hat 0\hat\imath} = -E_{\hat\imath}$, $F^{\hat 0\hat\imath} = E_{\hat\imath}$, $F_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath} = \epsilon_{ijk}B_{\hat k}$ ([[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]], part 6). Restoring SI ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]): $M \to GM/c^2$, $Q^2 \to GQ^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0c^4)$, both lengths. Coordinates $(t, r, \theta, \varphi)$, $d\Omega^2 = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2$, a prime is $\partial_r$, a dot $\partial_t$ (in the examples $d/d\tau$). The letter $e$ stands for $\sqrt{Q^2 + P^2} \ge 0$, Couzens' notation, not for the elementary charge. d'Inverno & Vickers write $\varepsilon$, $m$ in signature $+{-}{-}{-}$; 't Hooft's $Q^2/4\pi$ is the same quantity in Heaviside–Lorentz units ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^cau-c11-1-1|§C11.1, Caution: Units and normalisations in the sources]]).

## The field: spherical symmetry leaves two numbers

In the vacuum problem only the metric was unknown. Now the Maxwell field is unknown too, and the first task is to find what spherical symmetry and Maxwell's equations allow for it.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.2.1: Spherically Symmetric Electrovacuum: the Field Is Radial
> Let the metric be spherically symmetric, in the form of [[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]], part 4,
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -e^{2A(t, r)}dt^2 + e^{2B(t, r)}dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2 ,
> $$
>
> on a connected region where the gradient of $r$ is spacelike. Let $F$ be a Maxwell field ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^def-c11-1-1|Def. §C11.1.1]]) on it that is invariant under the rotation group $G$, $\phi^\ast F = F$ for every $\phi \in G$, and obeys the source-free Maxwell equations.
> 1. **Form.** No component mixes the sphere with the $(t, r)$ directions:
>
> $$
> F = F_{tr}(t, r)\,dt\wedge dr + P(t, r)\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi .
> $$
>
> 2. **Magnetic part.** $dF = 0$ forces $P$ to be a constant.
> 3. **Electric part.** $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0$ forces $e^{-A - B}r^2F_{tr}$ to be a constant, written $-Q$:
>
> $$
> F = -\frac{Q\,e^{A + B}}{r^2}\,dt\wedge dr + P\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi .
> $$
>
> 4. **What static observers measure.** In the orthonormal frame $e_{\hat t} = e^{-A}\partial_t$, $e_{\hat r} = e^{-B}\partial_r$, $e_{\hat\theta} = r^{-1}\partial_\theta$, $e_{\hat\varphi} = (r\sin\theta)^{-1}\partial_\varphi$, the only non-zero fields are
>
> $$
> E_{\hat r} = \frac{Q}{r^2}, \qquad B_{\hat r} = \frac{P}{r^2} ,
> $$
>
> at every $r$, whatever $A$ and $B$ are. In the areal radius the field is exactly a Coulomb field and a monopole field.
> 5. **Potential.** Where $A + B = 0$, $F = dA$ with Couzens' potential $A = -(Q/r)\,dt - P\cos\theta\,d\varphi$. Its magnetic term is singular on both halves of the axis. The potentials $P(1 - \cos\theta)\,d\varphi$ (for $\theta \neq \pi$) and $-P(1 + \cos\theta)\,d\varphi$ (for $\theta \neq 0$) are regular where defined and differ by $2P\,d\varphi$. For $P \neq 0$ no potential exists on a whole sphere: $\oint_{S^2}F = 4\pi P$, while $\oint_{S^2}dA = 0$.
>
> $Q$ and $P$ enter as constants of integration; that they are the electric and magnetic charges is [[#^thm-c11-2-4|Theorem §C11.2.4]].
>
> *Model:* [[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^mod-c11-1-2|Model §C11.1.2]] with a rotation-invariant field; *applies:* exactly, on any region where $\nabla r$ is spacelike, with or without time dependence; no Einstein equation is used.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §19.1, eqs. (19.7)–(19.10), Fig. 19.1, p. 356 (PDF 371) (electric case, static, "the Maxwell tensor must take on the form"; Exercises 19.2–19.3) · 't Hooft, ch. 13a, eqs. (13.2)–(13.8), p. 57 (PDF 58) (electric case, $Q/4\pi$ normalisation) · Couzens, GR2, eq. (5.19), p. 87 (PDF 88) (the potential) · every component checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (invariance at one point). Fix a point $p$ off the centres. The rotations of $G$ that fix $p$ form a one-parameter subgroup $R_\alpha$, the rotations about the axis through $p$. Each $R_\alpha$ maps the orbit of $p$ into itself and acts on its tangent plane $T_pO_p$ as the rotation by the angle $\alpha$ ([[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^def-c6-1-1|Def. §C6.1.1]], part 2). In the coordinates of [[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]], part 3, the rotations act on the angles alone, so $dR_\alpha(\partial_t) = \partial_t$ and $dR_\alpha(\partial_r) = \partial_r$. Invariance at $p$ reads $F_p(dR_\alpha v, dR_\alpha w) = F_p(v, w)$ for all $v, w \in T_pM$.
>
> **Step 2** (no mixed components). Let $e_1, e_2$ be an orthonormal basis of $T_pO_p$ and $n \in \{\partial_t, \partial_r\}$. The rotation by $\alpha = \pi$ sends $e_1 \mapsto -e_1$ and fixes $n$, so
>
> $$
> F_p(n, e_1) = F_p(dR_\pi n, dR_\pi e_1) = F_p(n, -e_1) = -F_p(n, e_1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_p(n, e_1) = 0 ,
> $$
>
> and likewise $F_p(n, e_2) = 0$. Since $\partial_\theta$, $\partial_\varphi$ span $T_pO_p$: $F_{t\theta} = F_{t\varphi} = F_{r\theta} = F_{r\varphi} = 0$.
>
> **Step 3** (the two remaining components are functions of $t$ and $r$). On the sphere, with $e_1' = \cos\alpha\,e_1 + \sin\alpha\,e_2$ and $e_2' = -\sin\alpha\,e_1 + \cos\alpha\,e_2$, antisymmetry gives $F(e_1', e_2') = (\cos^2\alpha + \sin^2\alpha)F(e_1, e_2) = F(e_1, e_2)$: $F$ takes one value $b(p)$ on every oriented orthonormal pair of $T_pO_p$. For any $\phi \in G$,
>
> $$
> F_{tr}(p) = (\phi^\ast F)_p(\partial_t, \partial_r) = F_{\phi(p)}(d\phi\,\partial_t, d\phi\,\partial_r) = F_{tr}(\phi(p)) ,
> $$
>
> and $\phi$ maps the oriented orthonormal pair $(e_{\hat\theta}, e_{\hat\varphi})$ at $p$ to an oriented orthonormal pair at $\phi(p)$, so $b(p) = b(\phi(p))$. Both are constant on each orbit, i.e. functions of $(t, r)$. With $F_{\theta\varphi} = F(\partial_\theta, \partial_\varphi) = r^2\sin\theta\,F(e_{\hat\theta}, e_{\hat\varphi}) = r^2b\,\sin\theta$, put $P(t, r) = r^2b(t, r)$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 4** ($dF = 0$). In components $(dF)_{\lambda\mu\nu} = \partial_\lambda F_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu F_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_\nu F_{\lambda\mu}$ ([[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]], part 2). The four index triples give
>
> $$
> (dF)_{t\theta\varphi} = \partial_tF_{\theta\varphi} = \sin\theta\,\partial_tP, \qquad (dF)_{r\theta\varphi} = \sin\theta\,\partial_rP, \qquad (dF)_{tr\theta} = \partial_\theta F_{tr} = 0, \qquad (dF)_{tr\varphi} = \partial_\varphi F_{tr} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> the other terms containing a mixed component. So $\partial_tP = \partial_rP = 0$: part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (Maxwell). Use $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = (-g)^{-1/2}\partial_\mu\bigl(\sqrt{-g}\,F^{\mu\nu}\bigr)$ ([[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]], part 3) with $\sqrt{-g} = e^{A + B}r^2\sin\theta$. The metric is diagonal, so
>
> $$
> F^{tr} = g^{tt}g^{rr}F_{tr} = -e^{-2A - 2B}F_{tr}, \qquad F^{\theta\varphi} = g^{\theta\theta}g^{\varphi\varphi}F_{\theta\varphi} = \frac{P}{r^4\sin\theta} .
> $$
>
> Component by component, with $W \equiv e^{-A - B}r^2F_{tr}$:
>
> $$
> \nu = t:\ \partial_r\bigl(\sqrt{-g}\,F^{rt}\bigr) = \sin\theta\,\partial_rW, \qquad \nu = r:\ \partial_t\bigl(\sqrt{-g}\,F^{tr}\bigr) = -\sin\theta\,\partial_tW ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \nu = \varphi:\ \partial_\theta\bigl(\sqrt{-g}\,F^{\theta\varphi}\bigr) = \partial_\theta\Bigl(\frac{e^{A + B}P}{r^2}\Bigr) = 0, \qquad \nu = \theta:\ \partial_\varphi\bigl(\sqrt{-g}\,F^{\varphi\theta}\bigr) = 0 .
> $$
>
> The last two hold identically. The first two say $\partial_rW = \partial_tW = 0$, so $W$ is constant on the connected region; call it $-Q$. Then $F_{tr} = -Qe^{A + B}/r^2$: part 3.
>
> **Step 6** (frame components). $F_{\hat t\hat r} = F(e_{\hat t}, e_{\hat r}) = e^{-A}e^{-B}F_{tr} = -Q/r^2$, so $E_{\hat r} = -F_{\hat t\hat r} = Q/r^2$. $F_{\hat\theta\hat\varphi} = F_{\theta\varphi}/(r^2\sin\theta) = P/r^2 = B_{\hat r}$, since $(\hat r, \hat\theta, \hat\varphi)$ is right-handed. All other frame components vanish by Step 2. Part 4.
>
> **Step 7** (potential). With $A + B = 0$, $F_{tr} = -Q/r^2$. For $A_t = -Q/r$: $F_{tr} = \partial_tA_r - \partial_rA_t = -\partial_r(-Q/r) = -Q/r^2$. For $A_\varphi = -P\cos\theta$: $F_{\theta\varphi} = \partial_\theta A_\varphi - \partial_\varphi A_\theta = P\sin\theta$. The norm of the magnetic term, $g^{\varphi\varphi}A_\varphi^2 = P^2\cos^2\theta/(r^2\sin^2\theta)$, diverges at $\theta = 0$ and at $\theta = \pi$. For $A_\varphi = P(1 - \cos\theta)$ the ratio $A_\varphi/\sin\theta = P\tan(\theta/2)$ tends to $0$ at $\theta = 0$, and for $-P(1 + \cos\theta)$ the same happens at $\theta = \pi$; both have $\partial_\theta A_\varphi = P\sin\theta$. Finally,
>
> $$
> \oint_{S^2}F = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi P\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi = 4\pi P ,
> $$
>
> whereas an $F = dA$ with $A$ smooth on the whole sphere would give $\oint_{S^2}dA = 0$ by Stokes' theorem on a manifold without boundary ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]]). Part 5.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Symmetry does most of the work: a rotation by $\pi$ about the radial direction kills every component that would point along the sphere. What is left is one radial electric and one radial magnetic field.
> - Maxwell's equations then make each a conserved flux. The factor $e^{A + B}$ is exactly what makes the frame field $Q/r^2$: Gauss's law with the sphere's area $4\pi r^2$, whatever the metric does in the $(t, r)$ directions.

## Solving the Einstein–Maxwell equations

With the field known, its energy–momentum tensor is known, and the Einstein equations become equations for $A$ and $B$ alone. The Ricci components of the static ansatz were computed once for all in [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]]; only the right-hand side is new.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.2.2: The Reissner–Nordström Metric
> Let a static, spherically symmetric metric $ds^2 = -e^{2A(r)}dt^2 + e^{2B(r)}dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$ ([[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^thm-c6-1-2|Theorem §C6.1.2]]) and a rotation-invariant Maxwell field ([[#^thm-c11-2-1|Theorem §C11.2.1]]) solve the Einstein–Maxwell equations with $J = 0$ ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^thm-c11-1-1|Theorem §C11.1.1]]) on a region where $\nabla r$ is spacelike. Write $e^2 = Q^2 + P^2$.
> 1. **The source.** In mixed components
>
> $$
> T^\mu{}_\nu = \frac{e^2}{8\pi r^4}\operatorname{diag}(-1, -1, 1, 1) :
> $$
>
>    energy density $\rho = e^2/8\pi r^4$ for static observers, a radial tension equal to it ($T^r{}_r = -\rho$), and a pressure $+\rho$ along the sphere. The same holds for the time-dependent ansatz.
> 2. **$g_{tt}g_{rr} = -1$ again.** $A + B$ is constant, and after a constant rescaling of $t$, $A = -B$ ('t Hooft's "$B = 1/A$").
> 3. **One first-order equation.** With $f \equiv e^{2A}$ the remaining content of the equations is $(rf)' = 1 - e^2/r^2$.
> 4. **The solution.** With one constant of integration, written $-2M$,
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -f\,dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{f} + r^2d\Omega^2, \qquad f(r) = 1 - \frac{2M}{r} + \frac{Q^2 + P^2}{r^2}, \qquad F = -\frac{Q}{r^2}\,dt\wedge dr + P\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi ,
> $$
>
>    the **Reissner–Nordström solution**. All Einstein–Maxwell equations hold. Restored (for $P = 0$; a magnetic charge enters the same way), $f = 1 - \dfrac{2GM}{c^2r} + \dfrac{GQ^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^4r^2}$, with $-f\,c^2dt^2$ in place of $-f\,dt^2$.
> 5. **Domain.** The form needs $f > 0$; where that holds is [[#^thm-c11-2-5|Theorem §C11.2.5]]. For $Q = P = 0$ it is the Schwarzschild metric ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]).
>
> *Model:* electrovacuum ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^mod-c11-1-2|Model §C11.1.2]]), $\Lambda = 0$, static and spherically symmetric; *applies:* exactly, on any such region (outside a charged star, around a charged black hole); staticity is removed in [[#^thm-c11-2-3|Theorem §C11.2.3]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.2, eq. (5.19), p. 87 (PDF 88) (stated) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §19.1, eqs. (19.11)–(19.14), pp. 356–357 (PDF 371–372); Exercise 19.4, p. 364 (PDF 379) · 't Hooft, ch. 13a, eqs. (13.9)–(13.19), pp. 57–58 (PDF 58–59) · all components checked by script*

^thm-c11-2-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the source in the frame). By [[#^thm-c11-2-1|Theorem §C11.2.1]], part 4, the only non-zero frame components are $F_{\hat t\hat r} = -F_{\hat r\hat t} = -Q/r^2$ and $F_{\hat\theta\hat\varphi} = -F_{\hat\varphi\hat\theta} = P/r^2$. Frame indices are raised with $\eta$, so raising $\hat t$ flips a sign. The invariant:
>
> $$
> F_{\hat a\hat b}F^{\hat a\hat b} = 2F_{\hat t\hat r}F^{\hat t\hat r} + 2F_{\hat\theta\hat\varphi}F^{\hat\theta\hat\varphi} = -\frac{2Q^2}{r^4} + \frac{2P^2}{r^4} .
> $$
>
> The products $F_{\hat a\hat c}F_{\hat b}{}^{\hat c}$, summed over $\hat c$:
>
> $$
> \hat t\hat t:\ F_{\hat t\hat r}F_{\hat t}{}^{\hat r} = \frac{Q^2}{r^4}, \qquad \hat r\hat r:\ F_{\hat r\hat t}F_{\hat r}{}^{\hat t} = -\frac{Q^2}{r^4}, \qquad \hat\theta\hat\theta,\ \hat\varphi\hat\varphi:\ \frac{P^2}{r^4} .
> $$
>
> For $\hat a \neq \hat b$ the product needs one $\hat c$ with $F_{\hat a\hat c} \neq 0$ and $F_{\hat b\hat c} \neq 0$; the non-zero components pair $\hat t$ only with $\hat r$ and $\hat\theta$ only with $\hat\varphi$, so the off-diagonal products vanish. Insert into $T_{\hat a\hat b} = \frac{1}{4\pi}\bigl(F_{\hat a\hat c}F_{\hat b}{}^{\hat c} - \frac14\eta_{\hat a\hat b}F^2\bigr)$:
>
> $$
> T_{\hat t\hat t} = \frac{1}{4\pi r^4}\Bigl(Q^2 + \frac{P^2 - Q^2}{2}\Bigr) = \frac{e^2}{8\pi r^4}, \qquad T_{\hat r\hat r} = \frac{1}{4\pi r^4}\Bigl(-Q^2 - \frac{P^2 - Q^2}{2}\Bigr) = -\frac{e^2}{8\pi r^4} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> T_{\hat\theta\hat\theta} = T_{\hat\varphi\hat\varphi} = \frac{1}{4\pi r^4}\Bigl(P^2 - \frac{P^2 - Q^2}{2}\Bigr) = \frac{e^2}{8\pi r^4} .
> $$
>
> Raising the first index flips only $T_{\hat t\hat t}$, and for a diagonal metric the mixed coordinate components equal the mixed frame components. This is part 1; the computation is algebra at one point and holds with time dependence too. The trace vanishes, as [[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^thm-c11-1-3|Theorem §C11.1.3]] says.
>
> **Step 2** (the equations to solve). Since $R = 0$, the field equations are $R_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^thm-c11-1-3|Theorem §C11.1.3]]). Lower the index with $g_{tt} = -e^{2A}$, $g_{rr} = e^{2B}$, and use $8\pi\rho = e^2/r^4$:
>
> $$
> 8\pi T_{tt} = e^{2A}\frac{e^2}{r^4}, \qquad 8\pi T_{rr} = -e^{2B}\frac{e^2}{r^4}, \qquad 8\pi T_{\theta\theta} = \frac{e^2}{r^2}, \qquad 8\pi T_{\varphi\varphi} = \frac{e^2\sin^2\theta}{r^2} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** ($A + B$ constant). Combination 1 of [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]]:
>
> $$
> \frac2r\,(A' + B') = e^{2(B - A)}R_{tt} + R_{rr} = 8\pi\bigl(e^{2(B - A)}T_{tt} + T_{rr}\bigr) = \frac{e^2}{r^4}\bigl(e^{2B} - e^{2B}\bigr) = 0 .
> $$
>
> So $A + B = c$, and the rescaling $\tilde t = e^ct$ of Step 3 of [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^der-c6-2-3|the derivation of Theorem §C6.2.3]] gives $A = -B$. Part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (the $\theta\theta$ equation). With $B = -A$, combination 2 gives $R_{\theta\theta} = 1 - (re^{2A})' = 1 - (rf)'$, and
>
> $$
> 1 - (rf)' = 8\pi T_{\theta\theta} = \frac{e^2}{r^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (rf)' = 1 - \frac{e^2}{r^2} .
> $$
>
> Part 3. (For constant-mass $f = 1 - 2M/r$ the left side is $0$: the failure of the opening.)
>
> **Step 5** (integrate). $rf = r + e^2/r + C$; with $C = -2M$, $f = 1 - 2M/r + e^2/r^2$.
>
> **Step 6** (the other equations hold). From Step 6 of [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^der-c6-2-3|the derivation of Theorem §C6.2.3]], for $B = -A$ and $f = e^{2A}$: $R_{tt} = f\bigl(\tfrac12f'' + f'/r\bigr)$ and $R_{rr} = -f^{-1}\bigl(\tfrac12f'' + f'/r\bigr)$. Here
>
> $$
> f' = \frac{2M}{r^2} - \frac{2e^2}{r^3}, \qquad f'' = -\frac{4M}{r^3} + \frac{6e^2}{r^4}, \qquad \frac{f''}{2} + \frac{f'}{r} = -\frac{2M}{r^3} + \frac{3e^2}{r^4} + \frac{2M}{r^3} - \frac{2e^2}{r^4} = \frac{e^2}{r^4} ,
> $$
>
> so $R_{tt} = fe^2/r^4 = 8\pi T_{tt}$ and $R_{rr} = -f^{-1}e^2/r^4 = 8\pi T_{rr}$ (Step 2 with $e^{2A} = f$, $e^{2B} = 1/f$). $R_{\varphi\varphi} = \sin^2\theta\,R_{\theta\theta} = 8\pi T_{\varphi\varphi}$, and the off-diagonal components vanish on both sides. As in vacuum this is no accident: $\frac{d}{dr}\bigl[1 - (rf)'\bigr] = -2r\bigl(\tfrac12f'' + f'/r\bigr)$ and $\frac{d}{dr}(e^2/r^2) = -2r\cdot e^2/r^4$, the radial component of the contracted Bianchi identity.
>
> **Step 7** (the field). With $A + B = 0$, [[#^thm-c11-2-1|Theorem §C11.2.1]], part 3, gives $F_{tr} = -Q/r^2$; Maxwell's equations hold by that theorem. Part 4. Restoring: $2M/r \to 2GM/(c^2r)$, and $e^2/r^2$ must be dimensionless, so $Q^2$ (in Gaussian units an energy times a length) becomes $Q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ times $G/c^4$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The field's stress has $T^t{}_t = T^r{}_r$: in the $(t, r)$ directions it is proportional to the metric, the same for every observer moving radially. That is why the vacuum relation $g_{tt}g_{rr} = -1$ survives. A perfect-fluid star has $T^t{}_t = -\rho \neq p = T^r{}_r$, and there it fails ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-2|Theorem §C9.1.2]]).
> - All that changes from vacuum is the source $e^2/r^2$ of the $\theta\theta$ equation, the field's pressure along the sphere. Integrated once, it produces the $e^2/r^2$ in $f$.

*Uses:* [[#^thm-c11-2-1|Theorem §C11.2.1]], [[§C5.3 Coupling to Matter꞉ the Hilbert Energy–Momentum Tensor#^thm-c5-3-3|Theorem §C5.3.3]], [[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^thm-c11-1-3|Theorem §C11.1.3]], [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]], [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]

The static assumption can be dropped, exactly as in vacuum. The time-dependent ansatz adds one Ricci component, $R_{tr}$, and the field supplies no matching source.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.2.3: Birkhoff's Theorem for Einstein–Maxwell
> Let the metric be $ds^2 = -e^{2A(t, r)}dt^2 + e^{2B(t, r)}dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$ on a connected region where $\nabla r$ is spacelike, with a rotation-invariant Maxwell field, and let the Einstein–Maxwell equations with $J = 0$ hold.
> 1. **The field.** $Q$ and $P$ are constants and $T^\mu{}_\nu$ has the form of [[#^thm-c11-2-2|Theorem §C11.2.2]], part 1; in particular $T_{tr} = 0$.
> 2. **The metric is Reissner–Nordström.** $\dot B = 0$ and $A = a(r) + h(t)$. With $t' = \int e^{h(t)}dt$ the metric is static, and it is the Reissner–Nordström metric with one set of constants $M$, $Q$, $P$ throughout the region. ⚑ Staticity and asymptotic flatness are consequences, not assumptions.
> 3. **⚑ Constant areal radius.** Unlike vacuum ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-5|Theorem §C6.2.5]], part 5), a metric $h_{ab}(y)\,dy^ady^b + r_0^2d\Omega^2$ with constant $r_0$ can solve the equations, exactly when $r_0^2 = e^2 \neq 0$. The two-dimensional metric $h$ then has constant curvature $R^{(2)} = -2/e^2$: the product of a two-dimensional anti-de Sitter space and a sphere, both of radius $|e|$ (the Bertotti–Robinson solution, named in [[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^ex-c11-1-1|Example §C11.1.1]]; locally, a two-dimensional Lorentzian metric of constant negative curvature is AdS$_2$, a standard fact not proved here). It is the near-horizon geometry of extremal Reissner–Nordström ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^ex-c11-3-2|Example §C11.3.2]]).
>
> *What is proved, and what the sources state.* Couzens' Theorem 4 states, without proof, that the unique spherically symmetric Einstein–Maxwell solution with non-constant area radius is Reissner–Nordström, and his aside mentions AdS$_2\times S^2$ for constant radius. d'Inverno & Vickers state that "a spherically symmetric exterior solution of the Einstein–Maxwell field equations is necessarily static" and omit the calculation. The proof here is local, on regions where $\nabla r$ is spacelike, and assumes that the field itself, not only its stress tensor, is rotation-invariant. Where $\nabla r$ is timelike ($r_- < r < r_+$, [[#^thm-c11-2-5|Theorem §C11.2.5]]) the same steps with the roles of $t$ and $r$ exchanged give the interior region of Reissner–Nordström, spherically symmetric but not static, as for Schwarzschild ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]).
>
> *Model:* electrovacuum, $\Lambda = 0$, spherical symmetry of metric and field; *applies:* exactly and locally, in particular outside every spherical charged body whose surface lies where $f > 0$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.2, Theorem 4 and Aside, p. 87 (PDF 88) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §19.1, Theorem, p. 357 (PDF 372) · Bertotti–Robinson check and the components checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the field with time dependence). [[#^thm-c11-2-1|Theorem §C11.2.1]] allowed $A(t, r)$, $B(t, r)$: $Q$ and $P$ are constants. Step 1 of [[#^der-c11-2-2|the derivation of Theorem §C11.2.2]] is algebra in the orthonormal frame at one point and gives $T^\mu{}_\nu = \frac{e^2}{8\pi r^4}\operatorname{diag}(-1, -1, 1, 1)$ again, so $T_{tr} = 0$. Part 1.
>
> **Step 2** ($B$ is static). By [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-5|Theorem §C6.2.5]], part 2, $R_{tr} = 2\dot B/r$. The $tr$ equation $R_{tr} = 8\pi T_{tr} = 0$ gives $\dot B = 0$.
>
> **Step 3** ($A$ separates). By the same theorem, $R_{\theta\theta} = e^{-2B}\bigl[r(B' - A') - 1\bigr] + 1$ contains no time derivatives. The $\theta\theta$ equation sets it equal to $8\pi T_{\theta\theta} = e^2/r^2$, which does not depend on $t$. Differentiate with respect to $t$, using $\dot B = 0$:
>
> $$
> 0 = \partial_t\Bigl(R_{\theta\theta} - \frac{e^2}{r^2}\Bigr) = -e^{-2B}\,r\,\partial_t\partial_rA \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A(t, r) = a(r) + h(t) .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (new time). Steps 6–7 of [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^der-c6-2-5|the derivation of Theorem §C6.2.5]] apply unchanged: $t' = \int^te^{h(s)}ds$ turns the metric into the static ansatz, and [[#^thm-c11-2-2|Theorem §C11.2.2]] gives Reissner–Nordström. $e^{-2B(r)} = f(r)$ fixes $M$ once for the region, and $Q$, $P$ are constants by Step 1. As $r \to \infty$, $f \to 1$. Part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (constant radius). Let $g = h_{ab}(y)dy^ady^b + r_0^2d\Omega^2$ with coordinates $y^0, y^1$ on the two-dimensional part. Steps 1–3 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c11-2-1|Theorem §C11.2.1]] did not use $r$ as a coordinate: $F = E(y)\,\epsilon_h + b(y)\,r_0^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi$, with $\epsilon_h$ the area form of $h$, $(\epsilon_h)_{ab} = \sqrt{-h}\,[ab]$, $[01] = -[10] = 1$. $dF = 0$ gives $r_0^2\,db\wedge\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi = 0$, so $b$ is constant ($dE\wedge\epsilon_h = 0$ holds automatically). Raising both indices of $\epsilon_h$ multiplies by $\det h^{-1} = 1/h$, so $F^{ab} = -E\,[ab]/\sqrt{-h}$, and with $\sqrt{-g} = \sqrt{-h}\,r_0^2\sin\theta$,
>
> $$
> \partial_a\bigl(\sqrt{-g}\,F^{ab}\bigr) = -r_0^2\sin\theta\,[ab]\,\partial_aE = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \partial_0E = \partial_1E = 0 ;
> $$
>
> the angular components hold identically, as in Step 5 there. So $E = -Q/r_0^2$ and $b = P/r_0^2$ are constants, and Step 1 gives $T^\mu{}_\nu = \frac{e^2}{8\pi r_0^4}\operatorname{diag}(-1, -1, 1, 1)$. The metric is a product, so the Christoffel symbols mixing $y^a$ with the angles vanish ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^der-c6-2-5|derivation of Theorem §C6.2.5]], Step 8), and the Ricci tensor splits: $R_{\theta\theta} = 1$, the round sphere, and $R_{ab}$ is the Ricci tensor of $h$. The equations become
>
> $$
> 1 = R_{\theta\theta} = 8\pi T_{\theta\theta} = \frac{e^2}{r_0^2}, \qquad R_{ab} = 8\pi T_{ab} = -\frac{e^2}{r_0^4}\,h_{ab} = -\frac{1}{e^2}\,h_{ab} .
> $$
>
> The first gives $r_0^2 = e^2$. In two dimensions $R_{ab} = \tfrac12R^{(2)}h_{ab}$ for every metric, so the second says $R^{(2)} = -2/e^2$, constant. For $e = 0$ the first equation reads $1 = 0$: the vacuum statement. Part 3. The explicit metric $-(\rho^2/e^2)\,dt^2 + (e^2/\rho^2)\,d\rho^2 + e^2d\Omega^2$ with $F_{\hat t\hat\rho} = -Q/e^2$, $F_{\hat\theta\hat\varphi} = P/e^2$ solves all equations (checked by script).
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The vacuum proof needed only that $T_{tr} = 0$ and that the $\theta\theta$ source is static. The radial field supplies both, so the spherical electrovacuum, like the vacuum, has no dynamics of its own. The field's tension along the sphere is what allows a cylinder-like throat of constant area, $r_0 = |e|$, which vacuum forbids.

The three constants entered as constants of integration. Their meaning is read off where the field is weak, as for Schwarzschild.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.2.4: M and Q Are the Mass and the Charge
> For the Reissner–Nordström solution ([[#^thm-c11-2-2|Theorem §C11.2.2]]):
> 1. **Mass.** $-g_{tt} = f = 1 + 2\Phi$ with $\Phi = -M/r + e^2/2r^2$. For $r \gg e^2/M$ the second term is negligible, and slow test bodies obey Kepler's third law $\Omega^2 = M/r^3$ ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-4|CM Theorem §A8.3.4]]): $M$ is the mass a distant astronomer measures, as in [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-4|Theorem §C6.2.4]]. It includes the energy of the field ([[#^thm-c11-2-7|Theorem §C11.2.7]]).
> 2. **Electric charge.** The flux of $E_{\hat r} = Q/r^2$ through the sphere of area $4\pi r^2$ is $4\pi Q$ for every $r$. Far away, where spacetime is flat to order $M/r$, this is Gauss's law $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec A = 4\pi Q_{\rm enc}$ (Gaussian; $Q_{\rm enc}/\varepsilon_0$ in SI, [[§C2.2 Gauss's Law and the Solid Angle#^thm-c2-2-2|EM Theorem §C2.2.2]]): $Q$ is the charge. Its coordinate-free form, $Q = \frac{1}{4\pi}\oint\star F$, is [[§C11.5 Charges in Curved Spacetime#^def-c11-5-1|Def. §C11.5.1]] and [[§C11.5 Charges in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c11-5-4|Theorem §C11.5.4]].
> 3. **Magnetic charge.** $B_{\hat r} = P/r^2$ has flux $4\pi P$: $P$ is a magnetic monopole charge ([[§C11.5 Charges in Curved Spacetime#^def-c11-5-2|Def. §C11.5.2]]). No magnetic monopole has been observed; the solution with $P \neq 0$ is kept because it costs nothing (Couzens).
> 4. **Only $e^2$ enters the metric.** The sign of $Q$ does not matter, and the rotation $(Q, P) \mapsto (Q\cos\alpha - P\sin\alpha, Q\sin\alpha + P\cos\alpha)$ leaves $e^2$ fixed: the duality of [[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^thm-c11-1-4|Theorem §C11.1.4]].
>
> Restored: $E_{\hat r} = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$, $\Phi = -GM/r + GQ^2/(8\pi\varepsilon_0c^2r^2)$ in $\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$.
>
> *Model:* Reissner–Nordström with the weak-field model at large $r$ ([[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^mod-c5-5-1|Model §C5.5.1]]); *applies:* parts 1–2 compare at $r \gg M$, $r \gg e^2/M$; the identifications are then exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.2, p. 87 (PDF 88) ("we will show later that these are the mass, magnetic charge and electric charge") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §19.1, eqs. (19.9)–(19.10), p. 356 (PDF 371) ("we therefore interpret ε as the charge"); p. 357 (PDF 372) ("geometric mass") · 't Hooft, ch. 13a, eq. (13.7), p. 57 (PDF 58) · Schutz, §11.3, item (2), p. 325 (PDF 343) (both charges "measured by Gauss' law integrals")*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the potential). $f = 1 - 2M/r + e^2/r^2 = 1 + 2\bigl(-M/r + e^2/2r^2\bigr)$. The ratio of the two terms in $\Phi$ is $(e^2/2r^2)/(M/r) = e^2/2Mr \to 0$ as $r \to \infty$.
>
> **Step 2** (Kepler). Slow bodies in $g_{00} = -(1 + 2\Phi)$ obey $\ddot{\vec x} = -\nabla\Phi$ to first order ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]]). On a circle, $\Omega^2r = \Phi'(r) = M/r^2 - e^2/r^3$, so
>
> $$
> \Omega^2 = \frac{M}{r^3}\Bigl(1 - \frac{e^2}{Mr}\Bigr) \xrightarrow{\ r \gg e^2/M\ } \frac{M}{r^3} .
> $$
>
> The areal radius differs from the isotropic radius of the weak-field model by $O(M)$, which changes $\Omega^2$ only at relative order $M/r$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^der-c6-2-4|derivation of Theorem §C6.2.4]], Step 2). Part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (flux). [[#^thm-c11-2-1|Theorem §C11.2.1]], part 4, gives $E_{\hat r} = Q/r^2$ at every $r$, and the sphere has proper area $4\pi r^2$ ([[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^def-c6-1-2|Def. §C6.1.2]]): flux $(Q/r^2)\cdot4\pi r^2 = 4\pi Q$. For $r \gg M$ the metric is Minkowski's up to $O(M/r)$, the static observers are inertial, and Gauss's law of flat space in Gaussian units ($\varepsilon_0 = 1/4\pi$ in $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec A = Q_{\rm enc}/\varepsilon_0$) identifies $Q_{\rm enc} = Q$. The same with $B_{\hat r} = P/r^2$ gives part 3.
>
> **Step 4** (duality). $e^2 = Q^2 + P^2$ is unchanged by $Q \to -Q$ and by the rotation, since $(Q\cos\alpha - P\sin\alpha)^2 + (Q\sin\alpha + P\cos\alpha)^2 = Q^2 + P^2$. Part 4.
>
> **Step 5** (restore). Gaussian $Q/r^2$ is SI $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$. $\Phi$ in $\mathrm{m^2/s^2}$ is $c^2$ times the dimensionless $\Phi$: $c^2\cdot GQ^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0c^4)/(2r^2) = GQ^2/(8\pi\varepsilon_0c^2r^2)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The mass is read off the clocks and orbits far away, the charges off the fluxes far away. The $e^2/r^2$ term is invisible at large $r$: the far field of a charged hole is that of a neutral one of the same total mass.

## Horizons: three cases

The Schwarzschild metric misbehaves where $f = 1 - 2M/r$ vanishes. Here $f$ is a quadratic in $1/r$, and the number of its zeros depends on how $e$ compares with $M$.

> [!definition] Definition §C11.2.1: Sub-Extremal, Extremal and Super-Extremal
> *Structure.* A Reissner–Nordström solution ([[#^thm-c11-2-2|Theorem §C11.2.2]]) with $M > 0$ and $e = \sqrt{Q^2 + P^2}$ is
> - **sub-extremal** if $e < M$,
> - **extremal** if $e = M$,
> - **super-extremal** if $e > M$.
>
> Restored, the dividing line is $|Q| = M\sqrt{4\pi\varepsilon_0G}$ for $P = 0$. At this charge-to-mass ratio two bodies with charges of the same sign repel electrically exactly as strongly as they attract gravitationally, $Q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2) = GM^2/r^2$. The extremal case is the largest charge for which $f$ has a zero ([[#^thm-c11-2-5|Theorem §C11.2.5]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, §5.2.1–5.2.3 (headings), pp. 88–96 (PDF 89–97) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §19.2, p. 357 (PDF 372) (cases $\varepsilon^2 < m^2$, $= m^2$, $> m^2$)*

^def-c11-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.2.5: Horizons r± = M ± √(M² − e²), and Where r and t Change Character
> Let $f = 1 - 2M/r + e^2/r^2$ with $M > 0$ ([[#^thm-c11-2-2|Theorem §C11.2.2]]), and $\Delta(r) \equiv r^2f = r^2 - 2Mr + e^2$.
> 1. **Zeros.** For $e \le M$,
>
> $$
> \Delta = (r - r_+)(r - r_-), \qquad r_\pm = M \pm \sqrt{M^2 - e^2}, \qquad r_+ + r_- = 2M, \qquad r_+r_- = e^2 .
> $$
>
>    For $0 < e < M$: $0 < r_- < M < r_+ < 2M$; the outer zero lies inside the Schwarzschild radius of the same mass.
> 2. **Sub-extremal: three regions.** $f > 0$ on region I, $r > r_+$, and on the inner region $0 < r < r_-$ (regions V and VI of the extension, [[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-3|Theorem §C11.3.3]]; d'Inverno calls it region III); $f < 0$ on region II, $r_- < r < r_+$. On I and on the inner region V, VI the Killing vector $\partial_t$ is timelike and $\nabla r$ spacelike: the regions are static ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-5|Def. §C3.13.5]]). On II, $\partial_t$ is spacelike and $\nabla r$ timelike: $r$ is a time coordinate, strictly monotonic along every causal curve, as inside a Schwarzschild hole ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]).
> 3. **The surfaces $r = r_\pm$.** There $g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu r\,\partial_\nu r = f = 0$: the normal $dr$ is null, and in the chart $(t, r)$ the metric degenerates. That these are coordinate singularities, null Killing horizons of $\partial_t$, is [[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-1|Theorem §C11.3.1]] and [[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-2|Theorem §C11.3.2]].
> 4. **Extremal.** $f = (1 - M/r)^2 \ge 0$ with a double zero at $r = M$. There is no region in which $r$ is a time coordinate ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-5|Theorem §C11.3.5]]).
> 5. **Super-extremal.** $f > 0$ for every $r > 0$: no zero, no horizon, static all the way down to $r = 0$ ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-6|Theorem §C11.3.6]]).
> 6. **The minimum.** For $e \neq 0$, $f$ has its only minimum at $r = e^2/M$, with value $1 - M^2/e^2$; as $r \to 0$, $f \to +\infty$ for $e \neq 0$. For $0 < e < M$, $r_- < e^2/M < r_+$.
>
> Restored: $r_\pm = GM/c^2 \pm \sqrt{(GM/c^2)^2 - GQ^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0c^4)}$ for $P = 0$.
>
> *Model:* Reissner–Nordström in the static chart; *applies:* exactly; part 3 states only what the chart shows.
>

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (factor). $\Delta = r^2 - 2Mr + e^2$ is a quadratic in $r$ with roots $r = M \pm \sqrt{M^2 - e^2}$, real for $e \le M$. Vieta: $r_+ + r_- = 2M$, $r_+r_- = e^2$. For $0 < e < M$: $\sqrt{M^2 - e^2}$ lies strictly between $0$ and $M$, so $0 < r_- < M < r_+ < 2M$.
>
> **Step 2** (signs). $f = \Delta/r^2$ has the sign of $(r - r_+)(r - r_-)$: positive when both factors have the same sign ($r > r_+$ or $r < r_-$), negative between the roots.
>
> **Step 3** (character of $t$ and $r$). $g(\partial_t, \partial_t) = g_{tt} = -f$ and $g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu r\,\partial_\nu r = g^{rr} = f$. On I and on V, VI, $f > 0$: $\partial_t$ timelike, $\nabla r$ spacelike, and $\partial_t$ is a hypersurface-orthogonal Killing vector, since no component depends on $t$ and there is no $dt\,dr$ term ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-6|Theorem §C3.13.6]], part 2). On II, $f < 0$ and both signs flip. If $\nabla r$ is timelike, then along any causal curve $x(\lambda)$, $dr/d\lambda = g(\nabla r, \dot x)$ is the product of a timelike vector with a non-zero causal vector, which is never zero, so it never changes sign ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^der-c7-1-4|derivation of Theorem §C7.1.4]]). Part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (extremal). For $e = M$, $\Delta = r^2 - 2Mr + M^2 = (r - M)^2$, so $f = (1 - M/r)^2$.
>
> **Step 5** (super-extremal, minimum). $f'(r) = 2M/r^2 - 2e^2/r^3 = (2/r^3)(Mr - e^2)$ vanishes only at $r = e^2/M$, is negative before and positive after: a minimum, with $f(e^2/M) = 1 - 2M^2/e^2 + M^2/e^2 = 1 - M^2/e^2$. For $e > M$ this minimum is positive, so $f > 0$ everywhere. For $0 < e < M$, $r_+r_- = e^2$ and $r_+ > M$ give $e^2/M = r_-\,(r_+/M) > r_-$, and $r_+ + r_- = 2M$ with $r_- > 0$ gives $e^2/M = r_+\,(r_-/M) < r_+$. Parts 4–6.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The charge adds a positive term that dominates at small $r$ and turns $f$ back up. A horizon survives only while the minimum of $f$ is not positive, $e \le M$; the inner zero is created by the same term that makes $f \to +\infty$ at the centre.

## Curvature and the mass inside a sphere

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.2.6: Curvature: Kretschmann Scalar and the Singularity at r = 0
> For the Reissner–Nordström metric:
> 1. $R = 0$ and $R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu} = 4e^4/r^8$: unlike Schwarzschild, the Ricci tensor does not vanish; it is the field's energy, $R_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$.
> 2. The Kretschmann scalar ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-1|Def. §C7.1.1]]) is
>
> $$
> K = R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}R^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = \frac{8\bigl(6M^2r^2 - 12Me^2r + 7e^4\bigr)}{r^8} = \frac{48M^2}{r^6} - \frac{96Me^2}{r^7} + \frac{56e^4}{r^8} .
> $$
>
> 3. $K > 0$ for all $r > 0$, finite at $r = r_\pm$ and everywhere on $r > 0$. As $r \to 0$, $K \to \infty$ like $56e^4/r^8$, faster than Schwarzschild's $48M^2/r^6$: $r = 0$ is a curvature singularity ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]]). Finite curvature at $r_\pm$ only suggests that those are coordinate singularities; the proof is the regular chart of [[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-1|Theorem §C11.3.1]].
>
> Restored: $K$ in $\mathrm{m^{-4}}$ with $M \to GM/c^2$ and $e^2 \to GQ^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0c^4)$.
>
> *Model:* Reissner–Nordström; *applies:* exactly, at every $r > 0$ in every region.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR2, eq. (5.22), p. 87 (PDF 88) (printed with $7e^3$; [[#^cau-c11-2-1|Caution: Slips in the sources]]) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §19.2, p. 357 (PDF 372) ("an intrinsic singularity at r = 0 – as can be shown by calculating the Riemann invariant") · checked by script from all Riemann components*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (frame components). For $ds^2 = -f\,dt^2 + f^{-1}dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$ with any $f(r)$, the independent non-zero orthonormal-frame components of the Riemann tensor are ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^der-c6-2-7|derivation of Theorem §C6.2.7]], Step 1)
>
> $$
> R_{\hat t\hat r\hat t\hat r} = \frac{f''}{2}, \qquad R_{\hat t\hat\theta\hat t\hat\theta} = R_{\hat t\hat\varphi\hat t\hat\varphi} = \frac{f'}{2r}, \qquad R_{\hat r\hat\theta\hat r\hat\theta} = R_{\hat r\hat\varphi\hat r\hat\varphi} = -\frac{f'}{2r}, \qquad R_{\hat\theta\hat\varphi\hat\theta\hat\varphi} = \frac{1 - f}{r^2} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (contract). As in Step 2 there, each pair $\hat a < \hat b$ contributes four times its square:
>
> $$
> K = 4\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{f''}{2}\Bigr)^2 + 2\Bigl(\frac{f'}{2r}\Bigr)^2 + 2\Bigl(\frac{f'}{2r}\Bigr)^2 + \Bigl(\frac{1 - f}{r^2}\Bigr)^2\Bigr] = f''^2 + \frac{4f'^2}{r^2} + \frac{4(1 - f)^2}{r^4} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (insert). With $f' = 2M/r^2 - 2e^2/r^3$, $f'' = -4M/r^3 + 6e^2/r^4$, $1 - f = 2M/r - e^2/r^2$:
>
> $$
> f''^2 = \frac{16M^2r^2 - 48Me^2r + 36e^4}{r^8}, \qquad \frac{4f'^2}{r^2} = \frac{16M^2r^2 - 32Me^2r + 16e^4}{r^8}, \qquad \frac{4(1 - f)^2}{r^4} = \frac{16M^2r^2 - 16Me^2r + 4e^4}{r^8} .
> $$
>
> The sum is $(48M^2r^2 - 96Me^2r + 56e^4)/r^8$: part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (Ricci). $R_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$ and $R = 0$ ([[#^thm-c11-2-2|Theorem §C11.2.2]]). In the frame $8\pi T_{\hat a\hat b} = (e^2/r^4)\operatorname{diag}(1, -1, 1, 1)$, and raising both indices does not change the squares: $R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu} = 4\cdot(e^2/r^4)^2 = 4e^4/r^8$. Part 1.
>
> **Step 5** (sign and limits). The quadratic $6M^2x^2 - 12Me^2x + 7e^4$ in $x = r$ has discriminant $144M^2e^4 - 168M^2e^4 = -24M^2e^4 < 0$ (for $Me \neq 0$) and positive leading coefficient, so it is positive. $K$ is a rational function of $r$ with denominator $r^8$: finite at every $r > 0$, in particular at $r_\pm$, and dominated by $56e^4/r^8 \to \infty$ as $r \to 0$. A chart in which the metric were smooth at a point of $r = 0$ would give a finite $K$ there. Part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Near the centre the field's own energy density, $e^2/8\pi r^4$, dominates the curvature; it diverges faster than the vacuum tidal field. Nothing special happens to any frame component at $r_\pm$.

The opening guessed $f$ from a mass inside each sphere. The static star of C9 already had an exact notion of that mass, defined from the geometry alone. Evaluated on Reissner–Nordström, it turns the heuristic into a theorem.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.2.7: ⚑ The Mass Function m(r) = M − e²/2r: the Field Energy outside r
> For the Reissner–Nordström solution, with $\rho = e^2/8\pi r^4$ the field's energy density ([[#^thm-c11-2-2|Theorem §C11.2.2]], part 1):
> 1. **The mass function.** The scalar $m(r) = \frac r2\bigl(1 - g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu r\,\partial_\nu r\bigr)$ of [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^def-c9-1-1|Def. §C9.1.1]] is
>
> $$
> m(r) = \frac r2\,(1 - f) = M - \frac{e^2}{2r} .
> $$
>
> 2. **It is the total mass minus the field energy outside $r$.**
>
> $$
> M - m(r) = \int_r^\infty4\pi s^2\rho(s)\,ds = \frac{e^2}{2r}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad m'(r) = 4\pi r^2\rho ,
> $$
>
>    the $tt$ equation of a static spherical source ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-2|Theorem §C9.1.2]], part 3). The volume element is $4\pi s^2ds$, not the proper $4\pi s^2e^{B}ds$; the difference is gravitational binding energy ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^cau-c9-1-1|§C9.1, Caution: M is not the sum of the energies of the fluid elements]]).
> 3. **Negative mass inside.** $m(r) < 0$ for $r < e^2/2M$.
> 4. **⚑ Where gravity turns repulsive.** A radial timelike geodesic with energy $E = -g(\partial_t, u)$ obeys $\dot r^2 = E^2 - f(r)$ and
>
> $$
> \ddot r = -\frac{f'(r)}{2} = -\frac{m(r) + 4\pi r^3p_r}{r^2} = -\frac{M}{r^2} + \frac{e^2}{r^3}, \qquad p_r = T^r{}_r = -\rho ,
> $$
>
>    with $\tau$ the proper time. The combination $m + 4\pi r^3p_r = M - e^2/r$ is the source of radial acceleration, the same as in the $rr$ equation of a static star with $p$ replaced by the radial pressure ([[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-2|Theorem §C9.1.2]], part 4). The field's radial tension counts against the mass. Gravity attracts for $r > e^2/M$ and repels for $r < e^2/M$, the radius of the minimum of $f$.
>
> The opening heuristic is part 1 read backwards: $1 - 2m(r)/r$ with $m = M$ minus the outside field energy is exactly $f$.
>
> Restored: $m(r) = M - Q^2/(8\pi\varepsilon_0c^2r)$ in kg, the field energy outside $r$ being $Q^2/(8\pi\varepsilon_0r)$ in joules.
>

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). $g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu r\,\partial_\nu r = g^{rr} = f$, so $m = \frac r2(1 - f) = \frac r2\bigl(\frac{2M}{r} - \frac{e^2}{r^2}\bigr) = M - \frac{e^2}{2r}$.
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). $4\pi s^2\rho(s) = 4\pi s^2\cdot e^2/(8\pi s^4) = e^2/(2s^2)$, and $\int_r^\infty e^2/(2s^2)\,ds = e^2/2r = M - m(r)$. Differentiating: $m' = e^2/2r^2 = 4\pi r^2\rho$. The $tt$ equation of [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-2|Theorem §C9.1.2]], part 3, was derived from $G_{tt} = 8\pi T_{tt}$ with $T^t{}_t = -\rho$ and nothing else about the source, so it applies here.
>
> **Step 3** (part 3). $m < 0 \iff M < e^2/2r \iff r < e^2/2M$.
>
> **Step 4** (the radial equation). The metric has the Killing vector $\partial_t$, so $E = -g(\partial_t, u) = f\,\dot t$ is constant along the geodesic ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-2|Theorem §C3.13.2]]); this is [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]] with $1 - 2M/r$ replaced by $f$. With $\dot\theta = \dot\varphi = 0$ the normalization $g(u, u) = -1$ reads
>
> $$
> -f\,\dot t^2 + \frac{\dot r^2}{f} = -1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -\frac{E^2}{f} + \frac{\dot r^2}{f} = -1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot r^2 = E^2 - f ,
> $$
>
> the radial equation of [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]] with $L = 0$ and $V = f$. The division by $f$ fails at $r_\pm$. In the ingoing chart $v = t + r_\ast$ of [[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-1|Theorem §C11.3.1]], where $ds^2 = -f\,dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2$ is regular, $E = -g(\partial_v, u) = f\dot v - \dot r$, and the normalization $-f\dot v^2 + 2\dot v\dot r = -1$ becomes, after inserting $\dot v = (E + \dot r)/f$ and multiplying by $f$, $-(E + \dot r)^2 + 2\dot r(E + \dot r) = -f$, i.e. $\dot r^2 - E^2 = -f$: the same equation, now with no division, valid across $r_\pm$.
>
> **Step 5** (acceleration). Differentiate $\dot r^2 = E^2 - f(r)$ along the geodesic: $2\dot r\ddot r = -f'(r)\dot r$, so $\ddot r = -f'/2$ wherever $\dot r \neq 0$, and by continuity at turning points. With $f' = 2M/r^2 - 2e^2/r^3$: $\ddot r = -M/r^2 + e^2/r^3$. And with $p_r = -\rho$, $4\pi r^3p_r = -e^2/2r$, so $m + 4\pi r^3p_r = M - e^2/2r - e^2/2r = M - e^2/r$, and $-(M - e^2/r)/r^2 = -M/r^2 + e^2/r^3$. The $rr$ equation of [[§C9.1 Static Spherical Stars꞉ the TOV Equations#^thm-c9-1-2|Theorem §C9.1.2]], part 4, came from $G_{rr} = 8\pi T_{rr}$ with $T_{rr} = p\,e^{2\Psi}$ alone, so it holds here with $p \to p_r$; with $e^{2\Phi} = f$ it reads $f'/2f = (m + 4\pi r^3p_r)/(r(r - 2m))$, and $r - 2m = rf$ gives the same $f'/2$. Part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The heuristic of the opening worked because the $tt$ equation for any static spherical source is $m' = 4\pi r^2\rho$: integrating the field energy inward from infinity is solving that equation with $m(\infty) = M$.
> - Two different radii appear. The mass inside vanishes at $e^2/2M$, but the attraction already stops at $e^2/M$, because the field's radial tension acts as a negative pressure and pressure gravitates.

The metric is now known, with its field, its constants and its curvature. Two questions remain open. In the static chart the metric degenerates at $r_\pm$ while every invariant stays finite there; and inside $r_-$ the region is static again, around a singularity at $r = 0$ that is timelike rather than spacelike. ## Examples

> [!example] Example §C11.2.1: How Much Charge Could an Astrophysical Hole Hold?
> *Question.* What charge makes a solar-mass hole extremal, and how much charge can a real hole keep?
>
> **Step 1** (the extremal charge). By [[#^def-c11-2-1|Def. §C11.2.1]], restored, $Q_{\rm ext} = M\sqrt{4\pi\varepsilon_0G}$. For $M = M_\odot = 1.98847\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}$,
>
> $$
> Q_{\rm ext} = 1.98847\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}\times\sqrt{4\pi\cdot8.854\times10^{-12}\cdot6.674\times10^{-11}}\ \mathrm{C/kg} = 1.71\times10^{20}\ \mathrm C = 1.07\times10^{39}\ \text{elementary charges} .
> $$
>
> Check: $\sqrt{GQ_{\rm ext}^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0c^4)} = GM_\odot/c^2 = 1476.7\ \mathrm m$, as $e = M$ requires.
>
> **Step 2** (charge-to-mass ratios of particles). A particle of charge $q$ and mass $m$ has, in the same geometric units, the ratio $q/(m\sqrt{4\pi\varepsilon_0G})$. With the elementary charge $q = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm C$: $1.11\times10^{18}$ for the proton and $2.04\times10^{21}$ for the electron. Matter is made of particles that are super-extremal by 18 to 21 orders of magnitude.
>
> **Step 3** (selective accretion). Far from the hole the electric and gravitational forces on a particle are $qQ/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$ and $GmM/r^2$ (Coulomb, [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^law-b2-1-2|EM Law §B2.1.2]]; Newton), with ratio
>
> $$
> \frac{F_{\rm el}}{F_{\rm grav}} = \frac{q}{m\sqrt{4\pi\varepsilon_0G}}\cdot\frac{Q}{M\sqrt{4\pi\varepsilon_0G}} .
> $$
>
> A positively charged hole repels protons more than it attracts them once $Q/Q_{\rm ext} > 1/(1.11\times10^{18}) = 9.0\times10^{-19}$, i.e. $Q > 154\ \mathrm C$ per solar mass, while it still attracts electrons. In ionized surroundings it then swallows electrons preferentially and its charge falls. A negative hole repels electrons once $|Q| > Q_{\rm ext}/(2.04\times10^{21}) = 0.084\ \mathrm C$ per solar mass and accretes protons. So $|Q|/Q_{\rm ext} \lesssim 10^{-18}$, a net excess of $154\ \mathrm C/q = 9.6\times10^{20}$ protons among the $1.19\times10^{57}$ of a solar mass, a fraction $8.1\times10^{-37} = (1.11\times10^{18})^{-2}$. Close to the horizon the repulsion becomes a sharp threshold: a particle charged like the hole falls in only if its energy exceeds its charge times the horizon potential, which is also what keeps test particles from charging a hole past extremality ([[§C13.4 The Third Law and Black-Hole Entropy#^thm-c13-4-2|Theorem §C13.4.2]]).
>
> **Step 4** (effect on the metric). With $e/M \lesssim 9\times10^{-19}$, $r_+ = M + \sqrt{M^2 - e^2} = 2M - e^2/2M + \dots$ differs from $2M$ by a fraction $e^2/4M^2 \approx 2\times10^{-37}$. Astrophysical holes are Schwarzschild (or Kerr) holes for every practical purpose (Couzens, §5 introduction; Schutz, §11.3: collapse "is unlikely to lead to a significant residual charge").
>
> **Step 5** (an electron as a Reissner–Nordström solution). For an electron $e/M = 2.04\times10^{21}$: super-extremal, no horizon ([[#^thm-c11-2-5|Theorem §C11.2.5]], part 5). Its mass function ([[#^thm-c11-2-7|Theorem §C11.2.7]]) vanishes at $r = e^2/2M$, restored $q^2/(8\pi\varepsilon_0m_ec^2) = r_e/2 = 1.41\ \mathrm{fm}$, half the classical electron radius $r_e = 2.82\ \mathrm{fm}$: the radius inside which the classical field energy exceeds the rest mass ([[§C2.3 Electrostatic Energy and Self-Energy#^rem-c2-3-2|EM §C2.3, Remark: The classical electron radius, and what quantum electrodynamics changes]]). General relativity reproduces the classical self-energy problem; it does not cure it, and at that scale quantum electrodynamics takes over.
>
> *Why study charged holes then.* Reissner–Nordström is the simplest spacetime with an inner horizon and a timelike singularity, and the template for the rotating Kerr hole ([[§C12.2 Horizons, the Ring Singularity and the Maximal Extension#^thm-c12-2-6|Theorem §C12.2.6]]), whose inner structure is the same in outline (d'Inverno & Vickers, §19.1).

> [!example] Example §C11.2.2: Radial Motion: the Gravitational Repulsion near r = 0
> *Question.* A neutral test body falls radially into a sub-extremal hole. Must it hit $r = 0$, as in Schwarzschild ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]])? Take $M = 1$, $e = 0.8$, so $r_+ = 1.6$, $r_- = 0.4$ and $e^2/M = 0.64$ ([[#^thm-c11-2-5|Theorem §C11.2.5]]).
>
> **Step 1** (the potential). By [[#^thm-c11-2-7|Theorem §C11.2.7]], part 4, $\dot r^2 + V(r) = E^2$ with $V = f$, the effective potential of [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^def-c6-3-1|Def. §C6.3.1]] with $L = 0$ and $1 - 2M/r$ replaced by $f$. Motion is confined to $f(r) \le E^2$. Since $f \to +\infty$ as $r \to 0$ ([[#^thm-c11-2-5|Theorem §C11.2.5]], part 6), for every $E$ there is a radius below which $f > E^2$, a region the body cannot enter: no radial timelike geodesic reaches $r = 0$. (Angular momentum only adds $fL^2/r^2$ to $V$, which also diverges.)
>
> **Step 2** (turning points). $E^2 = f$ means $(1 - E^2)r^2 - 2Mr + e^2 = 0$.
> - Bound, $E^2 = 0.5$: $0.5r^2 - 2r + 0.64 = 0$, $r = 2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 1.28} = 2 \pm 1.6492$. So $r_P = 3.649$ in region I and $r_Q = 0.351$ in the inner region, below $r_- = 0.4$.
> - Unbound, $E^2 = 1.5$: $-0.5r^2 - 2r + 0.64 = 0$, $r = -2 + \sqrt{4 + 1.28} = 0.298 = r_R$, the only positive root, again in the inner region.
>
> The body falls through $r_+$ and $r_-$ and turns around at $r_Q$ (or $r_R$). There $\ddot r = -f'/2 = -1/r^2 + 0.64/r^3 = +6.70$ at $r_Q$: outward. At $r_P$ it is $-0.062$, inward.
>
> **Step 3** (proper time, exactly). $E^2 - f = \bigl[(E^2 - 1)r^2 + 2Mr - e^2\bigr]/r^2 = (1 - E^2)(r_P - r)(r - r_Q)/r^2$, so
>
> $$
> d\tau = \frac{dr}{\sqrt{E^2 - f}} = \frac{r\,dr}{\sqrt{1 - E^2}\,\sqrt{(r_P - r)(r - r_Q)}} .
> $$
>
> Put $r = c - a\cos u$ with $c = (r_P + r_Q)/2$, $a = (r_P - r_Q)/2$. Then $r_P - r = a(1 + \cos u)$, $r - r_Q = a(1 - \cos u)$, the root is $a\sin u$, $dr = a\sin u\,du$, and
>
> $$
> d\tau = \frac{(c - a\cos u)\,du}{\sqrt{1 - E^2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tau(u) = \frac{cu - a\sin u}{\sqrt{1 - E^2}} + \text{const} ,
> $$
>
> a cycloid, as for Schwarzschild infall ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]]). From $r_P$ ($u = \pi$) to $r_Q$ ($u = 2\pi$): $\Delta\tau = \pi c/\sqrt{1 - E^2}$. Vieta gives $c = M/(1 - E^2)$, so
>
> $$
> \Delta\tau = \frac{\pi M}{(1 - E^2)^{3/2}} = \pi\cdot2^{3/2} = 8.886 \qquad (M = 1,\ E^2 = 0.5) .
> $$
>
> It does not depend on $e$. It equals the Schwarzschild time from rest at $r_{\max} = 2M/(1 - E^2)$ to $r = 0$, $\pi\sqrt{r_{\max}^3/8M}$: the charged body takes as long to reach its bounce as the neutral one takes to reach the singularity. Along the way it crosses $r_+$ after $7.399$ and $r_-$ after $8.759$; the last stretch, $r_- \to r_Q$, takes $0.127$.
>
> **Step 4** (after the bounce). $r(\tau)$ is periodic, between $r_Q$ and $r_P$. Since $r$ is a time coordinate in region II and decreases there on the way in, the body cannot re-enter the same region II with $r$ increasing; after the bounce it crosses $r_-$ outward into a different region. Where that is, and why $r$ alone cannot tell, needs the extended spacetime: [[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-4|Theorem §C11.3.4]].
>
> *Result.* The repulsion inside $r = e^2/M$ ([[#^thm-c11-2-7|Theorem §C11.2.7]], part 4) turns every radial timelike geodesic around before $r = 0$. Radial light rays ($V \equiv 0$) and accelerated observers can still reach $r = 0$ (Couzens, §5.2.1).
>
