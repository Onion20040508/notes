Chapter C1 ended with a metric written in one coordinate system. The static weak-field metric ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]) uses coordinates $(t, x^i)$ adapted to a static source, and no inertial frame covers the region around such a source ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-7|Theorem §C1.4.7]]). Without global inertial frames no class of coordinates is preferred. In special relativity the inertial frames simplify every law because flat spacetime has the Lorentz symmetries; a curved spacetime in general has no symmetry that singles out a class of coordinates, and each problem has its own convenient ones (Hartle §7.1). So general relativity must be written so that it holds in *any* coordinates. It must also handle spacetimes that no single coordinate system covers: the Schwarzschild coordinates, for instance, break down at $r = 2M$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]). The question of this chapter is therefore: **how is calculus done when coordinates are arbitrary labels, each valid only on part of spacetime?** This section answers the first part of it: what spacetime is, if it is not $\mathbf R^4$ with a preferred set of coordinates.

Coordinates fail in two ways, and both can be seen in two dimensions. *First, a coordinate system can break down where nothing is wrong with the space.* Plane polar coordinates $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ have the Jacobian

$$
\frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(r, \theta)} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r ,
$$

which vanishes at the origin. There every $\theta$ labels the same point, and the inverse matrix

$$
\frac{\partial(r, \theta)}{\partial(x, y)} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta/r & \cos\theta/r \end{pmatrix}
$$

has entries that grow without bound as $r \to 0$. The angle also fails away from the origin. With the range $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$, the points $(1, \varepsilon)$ and $(1, -\varepsilon)$ are close, but $\theta(1, \varepsilon) \to 0$ while $\theta(1, -\varepsilon) \to 2\pi$ as $\varepsilon \to 0^+$: the label jumps by $2\pi$ across the positive $x$-axis. Restricting $\theta$ to the open interval $(0, 2\pi)$ removes the jump and also removes the half-line $\{y = 0,\ x \ge 0\}$ from the region labelled. Polar coordinates therefore label the plane minus a half-line, and the Cartesian coordinates cover what they miss (d'Inverno & Vickers §5.2, Fig. 5.1). *Second, some spaces cannot be covered by one coordinate system at all.* The angles $(\theta, \varphi)$ on the unit sphere miss a closed half great circle through both poles, and at each pole every $\varphi$ labels the same point ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-3|Example §C2.1.3]]). Rotating the angles moves the missing half-circle elsewhere but does not remove it. [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-2|Theorem §C2.1.2]] shows that no choice of coordinates can work on the whole sphere, because the sphere is compact.

Both failures lead to the same construction. Cover the space with several coordinate patches, each a faithful labelling of an open piece, and require the relabelling between overlapping patches to be smooth. The section builds this in order: a chart ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]), an atlas of charts ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-2|Def. §C2.1.2]]), the transition functions between them ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]) and the smooth manifold ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]). The physical statement that spacetime is such a manifold follows ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]]). The sphere then shows both sides of the construction: one chart is impossible ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-2|Theorem §C2.1.2]]), two suffice ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]]). Maps between manifolds come next: smooth maps and functions ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-5|Def. §C2.1.5]]) and diffeomorphisms ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-6|Def. §C2.1.6]]), the notion of "the same manifold" and, from §C2.4 on, of symmetries and gauge transformations. Last come manifolds with boundary ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-7|Def. §C2.1.7]], [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-4|Theorem §C2.1.4]]), which Stokes' theorem needs (§C2.7). A remark separates failures of a chart from failures of the manifold.

**Setup.** A set of points with a topology and nothing more: no metric, no connection, no notion of length, angle or straight line. These are added in [[· C3 Metric, Connection and Curvature|C3]]. The dimension $n$ is general; for spacetime $n = 4$. The topological notions used (open set, continuity, homeomorphism, Hausdorff, second countable, compact, connected) are those of the Topology notes, linked where they are used. *Smooth* means $C^\infty$: all partial derivatives of all orders exist and are continuous. The definitions are those of the MATH 591 notes, written in the form general relativity uses them: coordinates $x^\mu$ with upper indices, transition functions read as coordinate changes $x^\mu(y)$. The theorems are exact statements of mathematics; [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]] is the physical assumption. The formulas of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]] and of the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): no metric is used in this section, so the signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]) and $G = c = 1$ do not enter. On an $n$-manifold coordinate indices run over $\mu = 1, \dots, n$; on spacetime over $\mu = 0, \dots, 3$ with $x^0 = t$. Charts are written $(U_i, \varphi_i)$, and the transition function $\psi_{ij} = \varphi_i \circ \varphi_j^{-1}$ takes the $j$-coordinates to the $i$-coordinates ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^cau-c2-1-1|Caution: Which way a transition function goes]]).

## Charts, atlases and smooth manifolds

A coordinate system is a way of assigning $n$ numbers to each point of some region, one tuple per point, so that nearby points get nearby numbers and conversely. The polar angle with range $[0, 2\pi)$ fails the second condition, and the polar origin fails the first. The first definition states the conditions as they are used.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.1.1: Chart
> *Structure.* Let $M$ be a topological space ([[§1 Topological Spaces#^def-1-1|590 Def. §1.1]]): in practice a set of events with a notion of neighbourhood. A **chart** (coordinate system, coordinate patch) on $M$ is a pair $(U, \varphi)$ consisting of
> 1. an open set $U \subset M$, the **chart domain** (coordinate neighbourhood), and
> 2. a homeomorphism ([[§10 Continuous Functions#^def-10-2|590 Def. §10.2]]) $\varphi : U \to \varphi(U)$ onto an open subset $\varphi(U) \subset \mathbf R^n$: a bijection onto its image, continuous, with continuous inverse.
>
> *Components.* $\varphi$ is a list of $n$ real functions on $U$,
>
> $$
> \varphi(p) = \bigl(x^1(p), \dots, x^n(p)\bigr), \qquad p \in U ,
> $$
>
> the **coordinates** (coordinate functions) of the chart; on spacetime $\varphi(p) = \bigl(x^0(p), x^1(p), x^2(p), x^3(p)\bigr)$. The point $p$ exists independently of these numbers. Writing "the point $x$" is shorthand for $\varphi^{-1}(x)$, and it refers to a chart.
>
> *What the conditions exclude.* Injectivity gives each point of $U$ one label; polar coordinates violate it at $r = 0$. Continuity of $\varphi$ and of $\varphi^{-1}$ means that nearby points have nearby labels and conversely; the angle with range $[0, 2\pi)$ violates it ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-2|Example §C2.1.2]]). Openness of $\varphi(U)$ lets limits be taken from all sides, so that derivatives exist at every point of the chart.
>
> *Equivalence.* This is [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^def-17-1|591 Def. §17.1]] (= [[§2 Topological Manifolds#^def-2-3|591 Def. §2.3]]). There $M$ is already known to be a topological manifold; here charts are what will make $M$ one ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.1, Definition 13, items 2–4, and the paragraph after it (chart, coordinate neighbourhood, coordinate function; "a point p exists independently of its coordinates") (pp. 38–39) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §5.2 (coordinate patch; non-degenerate coordinate system) (p. 66) · Hartle, Gravity, §7.1 ("a unique set of labels for each point in the region they cover") (p. 135)*

^def-c2-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §C2.1.2: Atlas
> An **atlas** on $M$ is a family of charts $\mathcal A = \{(U_i, \varphi_i)\}_{i \in I}$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]), all with values in the same $\mathbf R^n$, whose domains cover $M$:
>
> $$
> \bigcup_{i \in I} U_i = M .
> $$
>
> Every point then has at least one chart around it, and a point in an overlap $U_i \cap U_j$ has several coordinate tuples. An atlas is **smooth** when its charts are pairwise smoothly compatible ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]); the atlases of these notes are smooth from [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]] on.
>
> *Equivalence.* [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^def-17-8|591 Def. §17.8]] builds compatibility into the word "atlas"; this box calls a covering family of charts an atlas, as Couzens and d'Inverno & Vickers do, and adds "smooth" for the compatible ones.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.1, Definition 13, item 3, and p. 39 ("the collection of charts is called an atlas") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §5.2 ("a set of coordinate patches which covers the whole manifold is called an atlas"), Fig. 5.3 (p. 66)*

^def-c2-1-2

On an overlap the same point carries two coordinate tuples. The map that converts one into the other is the coordinate transformation of physics, and its smoothness is the condition that makes calculus independent of the chart.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.1.3: Transition Function
> *Structure.* Let $(U_i, \varphi_i)$ and $(U_j, \varphi_j)$ be charts ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]) with $U_i \cap U_j \neq \emptyset$. The **transition function** from the $j$-chart to the $i$-chart is
>
> $$
> \psi_{ij} = \varphi_i \circ \varphi_j^{-1} : \varphi_j(U_i \cap U_j) \longrightarrow \varphi_i(U_i \cap U_j) ,
> $$
>
> a map between open subsets of $\mathbf R^n$ (open because $U_i \cap U_j$ is open and $\varphi_j$, $\varphi_i$ are homeomorphisms). It is a homeomorphism with inverse $\psi_{ji} = \varphi_j \circ \varphi_i^{-1}$.
>
> *Components.* Write $x = \varphi_i(p)$ and $y = \varphi_j(p)$ for the two coordinate tuples of a point $p$ of the overlap. Then $x = \psi_{ij}(y)$, i.e. $n$ functions of $n$ variables,
>
> $$
> x^\mu = x^\mu(y^1, \dots, y^n), \qquad \mu = 1, \dots, n ,
> $$
>
> the **coordinate transformation** of physics (d'Inverno & Vickers write $x'^a = x'^a(x)$).
>
> *Smooth compatibility.* The two charts are **smoothly compatible** if $\psi_{ij}$ and $\psi_{ji}$ are both $C^\infty$. Charts with disjoint domains count as compatible. For compatible charts the Jacobian matrices $\partial x^\mu/\partial y^\nu$ and $\partial y^\nu/\partial x^\mu$ are inverse to each other at every point of the overlap, by the chain rule applied to $\psi_{ij} \circ \psi_{ji} = \mathrm{id}$ ([[§17 Differentiable Structures#^lem-17-1|591 Lemma §17.1]]), so
>
> $$
> \det\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial y^\nu}\Bigr) \neq 0 \quad\text{on the overlap} .
> $$
>
> Schutz (5.6) and d'Inverno & Vickers (5.8) take a nonzero Jacobian as the criterion for good coordinates; here it is a consequence of compatibility.
>
> *Equivalence.* This is [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^def-17-4|591 Def. §17.4]], which writes the transition from $(U, \varphi)$ to $(V, \psi)$ as $\psi \circ \varphi^{-1}$, with the target chart on the left as here.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.1, Definition 13, item 5, and p. 39 ("analogous to labelling a point by Euclidean coordinates and polar coordinates") (pp. 38–39) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §5.4, eqs. (5.5)–(5.8) (pp. 68–69) · Schutz, §5.2, eq. (5.6) (p. 118)*

^def-c2-1-3

> [!caution] Caution: Which way a transition function goes
> Couzens, GR1, Definition 13 (p. 38) defines $\psi_{ij} = \varphi_i \circ \varphi_j^{-1}$ as a map from $\varphi_j(U_i \cap U_j)$ to $\varphi_i(U_i \cap U_j)$: $j$-coordinates in, $i$-coordinates out, as in [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]. On the next page, with $\varphi_i$ giving the coordinates $x$ and $\varphi_j$ giving $y$, the text calls the transformation $x^\mu = x^\mu(y)$ "the explicit form of the map $\psi_{ji} = \varphi_j \circ \varphi_i^{-1}$". By Definition 13 the map $y \mapsto x(y)$ is $\varphi_i \circ \varphi_j^{-1} = \psi_{ij}$; the map $\psi_{ji}$ gives $y(x)$. GR1's own circle example uses the definition's order: (3.10) computes $\varphi_2(\varphi_1^{-1}(\theta))$, which takes the coordinate of chart 1 to that of chart 2. These notes follow Definition 13. Read the composition right to left: the chart applied first is the one whose coordinates go in.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.1, Definition 13, item 5 (p. 38); p. 39; Example 3.5, eq. (3.10) (p. 41); checked against the definition*

^cau-c2-1-1

A single atlas is a choice, and many atlases describe the same space: the circle has an atlas of two angle charts and one of four projection charts ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-2|Example §C2.1.2]]). What is to count as the space is the collection of all charts compatible with a given atlas, so that every reasonable coordinate system is admitted and none is singled out.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.1.4: Smooth Manifold
> *Structure.* An $n$-dimensional **smooth manifold** is a set $M$ with
> 1. a topology in which $M$ is Hausdorff ([[§9 Hausdorff Spaces#^def-9-1|590 Def. §9.1]]) and second countable ([[§22 Countability Axioms#^def-22-3|590 Def. §22.3]]);
> 2. a smooth atlas: charts onto open subsets of $\mathbf R^n$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]) covering $M$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-2|Def. §C2.1.2]]), any two of them smoothly compatible ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]);
> 3. maximality: the atlas contains every chart that is smoothly compatible with all of its charts.
>
> The maximal atlas is the **smooth structure**; its charts are the **admissible coordinates**. Every smooth atlas lies in exactly one maximal atlas, the set of all charts compatible with it ([[§17 Differentiable Structures#^thm-17-6|591 Thm. §17.6]]). So a manifold is specified in practice by any one smooth atlas, and two smooth atlases specify the same manifold exactly when their union is again a smooth atlas ([[§17 Differentiable Structures#^def-17-9|591 Def. §17.9]]).
>
> *What each condition does.*
> - Hausdorff: two distinct points have disjoint neighbourhoods, so limits are unique.
> - Second countable: it guarantees partitions of unity ([[§65 Partitions of Unity#^thm-65-7|591 Thm. §65.7]]), which glue chart-by-chart constructions together (integration, §C2.7).
> - Compatibility: whether a function or a map is smooth is decided in one chart and then holds in every chart ([[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-1|591 Prop. §19.1]]).
> - Maximality: every admissible coordinate system belongs to the structure on an equal footing. This is the mathematical form of "coordinates are arbitrary labels".
>
> *Equivalence.* MATH 591 builds the same object in two steps: a topological manifold (second countable, Hausdorff, locally Euclidean, [[§2 Topological Manifolds#^def-2-2|591 Def. §2.2]]) and a smooth structure on it (a maximal atlas, [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^def-17-11|591 Def. §17.11]]). "Locally Euclidean" is condition 2 without smoothness. Couzens' Definition 13 lists the Hausdorff condition and the smooth atlas but not second countability, which GR1 does not use; d'Inverno & Vickers and Schutz describe a manifold informally as a set whose points can be labelled locally by $n$ numbers.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.1, Definition 13 (p. 38); p. 39 (compatible atlases, differentiable structure); Definition 6 and the remark that Hausdorff is assumed throughout (pp. 33–34) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §5.2 (p. 66) · Schutz, §6.1 (pp. 141–142)*

## Spacetime is a manifold

Level B took spacetime to be $\mathbf R^4$ with a distinguished family of global coordinates, the inertial frames, related by Poincaré transformations. Chapter C1 removed the distinguished family: near a mass no frame is inertial over a region in which tidal effects can be detected ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-7|Theorem §C1.4.7]]), and only local inertial frames remain ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]]). What survives is that each event has a neighbourhood that can be labelled by four numbers. A smooth manifold is exactly a space with this local property and no preferred labels.

> [!principle] Principle §C2.1.1: Spacetime Is a Four-Dimensional Manifold
> The events of a spacetime form a connected four-dimensional smooth manifold $M$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]).
> 1. **Events are points.** An event is a point $p \in M$; it exists independently of the numbers assigned to it.
> 2. **Coordinates are labels on a chart domain.** Coordinates $x^\mu = (t, x, y, z)$ are the functions of a chart $(U, \varphi)$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]), defined on $U \subset M$ and in general not on all of $M$. Any chart of the maximal atlas may be used and none is preferred. A physical statement must therefore have the same content in every chart; how quantities change from chart to chart is the subject of §C2.2–§C2.3.
> 3. **Local physics fixes the local structure only.** By the Einstein equivalence principle each event has a neighbourhood in which physics is that of special relativity ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]), labelled by four numbers; this is where the dimension $4$ and the local coordinates come from. It does not fix the global topology of $M$: $\mathbf R^4$, $\mathbf R \times S^3$ and $\mathbf R^2 \times S^2$ are all four-manifolds. The global structure is part of a solution of the field equations ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]; C10, planned).
> 4. **Physical fields live on $M$.** Scalar fields are smooth functions on $M$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-5|Def. §C2.1.5]]); vectors and tensors follow in §C2.2–§C2.3. The metric is one more field on $M$ ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]), not part of the manifold.
>
> *Domain: classical general relativity, with special relativity as the case $M = \mathbf R^4$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-1|Example §C2.1.1]]). Hausdorff, second countability and connectedness are assumptions about spacetime (Couzens assumes the Hausdorff property throughout, GR1 p. 34; a component that no signal can reach is dropped). Points at which curvature grows without bound are not points of $M$ (C10, planned). The description by a smooth continuum is expected to fail at the Planck length $\sqrt{\hbar G/c^3} = 1.6\times10^{-35}\ \mathrm m$, where quantum gravity would be needed; the principle is assumed at all larger scales. Layer: at level B, spacetime is $\mathbf R^4$ with global inertial coordinates ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, $x^0 = ct$), related by Poincaré transformations ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-4|Def. §B1.2.4]]); here the inertial charts are one compatible family among all charts.*
>
> *Model:* the spacetime of classical general relativity; *applies:* every spacetime in these notes, Minkowski spacetime included.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, §7.1 ("Coordinates are a systematic way of labeling the points of spacetime. The choice of coordinates is arbitrary"; no class of coordinates simplifies the general laws in curved spacetime) (p. 135) · Couzens, GR1, §3, introduction (p. 31); §4.1.2 ("For General Relativity we need to consider Lorentzian manifolds") (p. 76); §3.1, remark after Example 3.2 (Hausdorff assumed) (p. 34) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §5.2 (locally like $\mathbf R^n$; compactness of $S^2$ is a global property; topology "will surface" in solutions of Einstein's equations) (p. 66) · Schutz, §6.1 ("the large-scale topology of a manifold may be very different from Euclidean space") (p. 141) · Planck length checked by script*

^pr-c2-1-1

The principle allows spacetimes, and parts of spacetimes, that no single chart covers. The simplest such space is the sphere, which appears in every spherically symmetric spacetime as the orbit of the rotations ($r$ and $t$ fixed). The next two theorems settle how many charts it needs.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.1.2: The Sphere Is Not One Chart
> Let $S^2 = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbf R^3 : x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\}$ carry the subspace topology ([[§5 Subspace Topology#^def-5-1|590 Def. §5.1]]).
> 1. There is no chart $(U, \varphi)$ of $S^2$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]) with $U = S^2$: the sphere is not homeomorphic to any open subset of $\mathbf R^2$.
> 2. More generally, a nonempty compact space ([[§18 Compact Spaces#^def-18-2|590 Def. §18.2]]) is not the domain of a chart with values in $\mathbf R^n$, $n \ge 1$. Every atlas of a compact manifold of dimension $n \ge 1$ has at least two charts.
>
> So every coordinate system on $S^2$ either misses points or fails to be a homeomorphism somewhere. The defects of $(\theta, \varphi)$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-3|Example §C2.1.3]]) are not a poor choice: some defect is forced. Two charts suffice ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]]).
>
> *Model:* none (a statement of topology); *applies:* every compact manifold of positive dimension: the circle ([[§17 Differentiable Structures#^prop-17-4|591 Prop. §17.4]], the analogue for $S^1$), the torus, the spatial three-sphere of a closed universe.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §5.2 ("there is no single coordinate system which covers the whole of a 2-sphere without degeneracy. The smallest number needed is two"; stated without proof), Fig. 5.2 (pp. 66–67) · Couzens, GR1, §3.1 ("to describe n-dimensional spheres we need more than one chart", p. 43); Theorem 1 (Heine–Borel, p. 36) · proof written here*

^thm-c2-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** ($S^2$ is compact). The function $f(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ is continuous on $\mathbf R^3$ and $S^2 = f^{-1}(\{1\})$ is the preimage of a closed set, hence closed. Every point of $S^2$ has $|(x, y, z)| = 1$, so $S^2$ is bounded. By the Heine–Borel theorem ([[§18 Compact Spaces#^thm-18-12|590 Thm. §18.12]]) $S^2$ is compact.
>
> **Step 2** (the image of a global chart would be compact). Suppose $\varphi : S^2 \to A$ were a homeomorphism onto an open set $A \subset \mathbf R^2$. Then $A = \varphi(S^2)$ is the continuous image of a compact space, hence compact ([[§18 Compact Spaces#^thm-18-3|590 Thm. §18.3]]), hence closed and bounded in $\mathbf R^2$ by Heine–Borel again.
>
> **Step 3** ($A$ would be all of $\mathbf R^2$). $A$ is nonempty, open (Step 2's assumption) and closed (Step 2). The line $\mathbf R$ is connected ([[§16 Connected Subspaces of ℝ#^thm-16-1|590 Thm. §16.1]]), so $\mathbf R^2 = \mathbf R \times \mathbf R$ is connected ([[§15 Connected Spaces#^thm-15-6|590 Thm. §15.6]]), and in a connected space the only subsets that are both open and closed are $\emptyset$ and the whole space ([[§15 Connected Spaces#^lem-15-1|590 Lemma §15.1]]). Hence $A = \mathbf R^2$.
>
> **Step 4** (contradiction). $\mathbf R^2$ is not bounded: it contains $(k, 0)$ for every integer $k$. But $A$ is bounded (Step 2). So no such $\varphi$ exists. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 5** (any compact space). Replace $S^2$ by a nonempty compact space $K$ and $\mathbf R^2$ by $\mathbf R^n$, $n \ge 1$. Steps 2–4 use only the compactness of the domain and the connectedness and unboundedness of $\mathbf R^n$ ($\mathbf R^n$ is connected by the same product theorem). For $n = 0$ the argument fails, correctly: $\mathbf R^0$ is a point, which is bounded, and a one-point space is a chart domain. This is part 2.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The obstruction is global. Every small piece of the sphere is a chart domain ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-3|Example §C2.1.3]]); what no chart can do is map a compact space onto an open subset of $\mathbf R^n$, because such subsets are never compact. Local measurements, which only see chart domains, cannot detect the obstruction; it is a property of the whole space.

^der-c2-1-2

*Uses:* [[§5 Subspace Topology#^def-5-1|590 Def. §5.1]], [[§18 Compact Spaces#^thm-18-12|590 Thm. §18.12]], [[§18 Compact Spaces#^thm-18-3|590 Thm. §18.3]], [[§16 Connected Subspaces of ℝ#^thm-16-1|590 Thm. §16.1]], [[§15 Connected Spaces#^thm-15-6|590 Thm. §15.6]], [[§15 Connected Spaces#^lem-15-1|590 Lemma §15.1]], [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]

Two charts are enough if each omits a single point. Projecting from a pole onto the equatorial plane does this.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.1.3: The Stereographic Atlas of S²
> On $S^2$ let $N = (0, 0, 1)$, $S = (0, 0, -1)$, and define the **stereographic projections**
>
> $$
> \varphi_N : S^2 \setminus \{N\} \to \mathbf R^2, \quad \varphi_N(x, y, z) = \frac{(x, y)}{1 - z}; \qquad \varphi_S : S^2 \setminus \{S\} \to \mathbf R^2, \quad \varphi_S(x, y, z) = \frac{(x, y)}{1 + z} .
> $$
>
> 1. **Charts.** $\varphi_N(p)$ is the point where the line from $N$ through $p$ meets the plane $z = 0$. $\varphi_N$ is a homeomorphism onto all of $\mathbf R^2$, with inverse
>
> $$
> \varphi_N^{-1}(u) = \frac{\bigl(2u^1,\ 2u^2,\ |u|^2 - 1\bigr)}{|u|^2 + 1}, \qquad |u|^2 = (u^1)^2 + (u^2)^2 ,
> $$
>
> and likewise $\varphi_S^{-1}(w) = \bigl(2w^1, 2w^2, 1 - |w|^2\bigr)/(1 + |w|^2)$. Both are charts ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]).
> 2. **Transition.** The overlap is $S^2 \setminus \{N, S\}$, with $\varphi_N(S^2 \setminus \{N, S\}) = \mathbf R^2 \setminus \{0\}$, and the transition function ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]) is the inversion in the unit circle,
>
> $$
> \psi_{SN} = \varphi_S \circ \varphi_N^{-1} : \mathbf R^2 \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbf R^2 \setminus \{0\}, \qquad w = \frac{u}{|u|^2} ,
> $$
>
> which is its own inverse, $\psi_{NS} = \psi_{SN}$. It is $C^\infty$, with Jacobian determinant
>
> $$
> \det\Bigl(\frac{\partial w^a}{\partial u^b}\Bigr) = -\frac{1}{|u|^4} \neq 0 .
> $$
>
> 3. **⚑ Orientation.** The determinant is negative everywhere on the overlap. Following $\varphi_S$ by the reflection $(w^1, w^2) \mapsto (w^1, -w^2)$ gives a chart whose transition from $\varphi_N$ is $u \mapsto (u^1, -u^2)/|u|^2$, with determinant $+1/|u|^4 > 0$. The sign of transition determinants is what an orientation controls ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]]).
> 4. **Atlas.** $\{(S^2 \setminus \{N\}, \varphi_N), (S^2 \setminus \{S\}, \varphi_S)\}$ is a smooth atlas ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-2|Def. §C2.1.2]]). With it, $S^2$ is a smooth two-dimensional manifold ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]); by [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-2|Theorem §C2.1.2]] two charts is the minimum.
> 5. **One structure.** This atlas is compatible with the six hemisphere charts of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-3|Example §C2.1.3]]; for instance, with $\varphi_{z+}(x, y, z) = (x, y)$ on $\{z > 0\}$,
>
> $$
> \varphi_{z+} \circ \varphi_N^{-1}(u) = \frac{2u}{1 + |u|^2}, \qquad |u| > 1 .
> $$
>
> Both atlases therefore define the same smooth manifold $S^2$.
>
> *Model:* none (a construction); *applies:* exact. The same formulas with $n$ components give a two-chart atlas of $S^n$, with transition $u \mapsto u/|u|^2$ on $\mathbf R^n \setminus \{0\}$.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §5.2, Fig. 5.2 (two non-degenerate coordinate systems covering $S^2$, overlapping at the equator; schematic) (p. 67) · Couzens, GR1, §3.1, Example 3.5 (the hemisphere atlas, eqs. (3.15)–(3.19)) (pp. 42–43) · the stereographic formulas are worked here ([[§17 Differentiable Structures#^ex-17-3|591 Ex. §17.3]] is the version for the circle); checked by script*

^thm-c2-1-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the projection from $N$). Let $p = (x, y, z) \in S^2$, $p \neq N$, so $z < 1$. The line through $N$ and $p$ is $N + s\,(p - N) = \bigl(sx,\ sy,\ 1 + s(z - 1)\bigr)$, $s \in \mathbf R$. It meets the plane $z = 0$ where $1 + s(z - 1) = 0$, i.e. $s = 1/(1 - z)$, at the point
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{x}{1 - z},\ \frac{y}{1 - z},\ 0\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Dropping the third component gives $\varphi_N(p) = (x, y)/(1 - z)$. It is continuous on $S^2 \setminus \{N\}$, a quotient of continuous functions with denominator $1 - z > 0$.
>
> **Step 2** (the inverse). Put $u = \varphi_N(p)$. Using $x^2 + y^2 = 1 - z^2$ on the sphere,
>
> $$
> |u|^2 = \frac{x^2 + y^2}{(1 - z)^2} = \frac{1 - z^2}{(1 - z)^2} = \frac{(1 - z)(1 + z)}{(1 - z)^2} = \frac{1 + z}{1 - z} .
> $$
>
> Solve for $z$: $|u|^2(1 - z) = 1 + z$, so $z = (|u|^2 - 1)/(|u|^2 + 1)$, and then $1 - z = 2/(|u|^2 + 1)$, so $(x, y) = (1 - z)\,u = 2u/(|u|^2 + 1)$. This is the formula for $\varphi_N^{-1}$. Conversely, for every $u \in \mathbf R^2$ that formula gives a point of the sphere,
>
> $$
> \frac{4(u^1)^2 + 4(u^2)^2 + (|u|^2 - 1)^2}{(|u|^2 + 1)^2} = \frac{|u|^4 + 2|u|^2 + 1}{(|u|^2 + 1)^2} = 1 ,
> $$
>
> with third component $(|u|^2 - 1)/(|u|^2 + 1) < 1$, so the point is not $N$; and applying $\varphi_N$ to it returns $u$ (multiply its first two components by $1/(1 - z) = (|u|^2 + 1)/2$). So $\varphi_N$ is a bijection of $S^2 \setminus \{N\}$ onto $\mathbf R^2$, and its inverse, a rational map with positive denominator, is continuous. Hence $\varphi_N$ is a homeomorphism onto the open set $\mathbf R^2$. Replacing $z$ by $-z$ throughout gives the statements for $\varphi_S$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (the overlap). $(S^2 \setminus \{N\}) \cap (S^2 \setminus \{S\}) = S^2 \setminus \{N, S\}$. Since $\varphi_N(S) = (0, 0)/(1 - (-1)) = 0$ and $\varphi_N$ is a bijection onto $\mathbf R^2$, $\varphi_N(S^2 \setminus \{N, S\}) = \mathbf R^2 \setminus \{0\}$.
>
> **Step 4** (the transition). For $u \neq 0$ let $p = \varphi_N^{-1}(u) = (x, y, z)$. Then $u = (x, y)/(1 - z)$ and $w = \varphi_S(p) = (x, y)/(1 + z)$, so by Step 2
>
> $$
> w = \frac{1 - z}{1 + z}\,u = \frac{u}{|u|^2} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (smoothness, inverse, Jacobian). The components $w^a = u^a/|u|^2$ are rational functions whose denominator does not vanish on $\mathbf R^2 \setminus \{0\}$, so they are $C^\infty$ there. Applying the map twice,
>
> $$
> \frac{u/|u|^2}{\bigl|u/|u|^2\bigr|^2} = \frac{u/|u|^2}{1/|u|^2} = u ,
> $$
>
> so it is its own inverse, and $\psi_{NS} = \psi_{SN}$ is $C^\infty$ too. Differentiating,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial w^a}{\partial u^b} = \frac{1}{|u|^2}\,\frac{\partial u^a}{\partial u^b} + u^a\,\frac{\partial}{\partial u^b}\frac{1}{|u|^2} = \frac{\delta_{ab}}{|u|^2} - \frac{2u^au^b}{|u|^4}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{\partial w}{\partial u} = \frac{1}{|u|^2}\bigl(I - 2\,n\,n^{\mathsf T}\bigr), \quad n = \frac{u}{|u|} ,
> $$
>
> with $\partial|u|^{-2}/\partial u^b = -2u^b/|u|^4$ and $\delta_{ab}$ the entries of the unit matrix $I$ (these are entries of a matrix of numbers; no metric is involved). The matrix $I - 2nn^{\mathsf T}$ sends $n$ to $n - 2n(n^{\mathsf T}n) = -n$ and fixes every vector perpendicular to $n$: it is a reflection, with determinant $-1$. A $2 \times 2$ matrix multiplied by $1/|u|^2$ has its determinant multiplied by $1/|u|^4$, so $\det(\partial w/\partial u) = -1/|u|^4$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 6** (the reflected chart). Composing with $R(w^1, w^2) = (w^1, -w^2)$, $\det R = -1$, the new transition $R \circ \psi_{SN}$ has determinant $(-1)\cdot(-1/|u|^4) = 1/|u|^4$ by the chain rule. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 7** (atlas). The two domains cover $S^2$, both charts are homeomorphisms onto open sets (Step 2), and the transitions in both directions are $C^\infty$ (Step 5). $S^2$ is Hausdorff and second countable as a subspace of $\mathbf R^3$: two distinct points of $S^2$ are separated by the traces on $S^2$ of disjoint balls in $\mathbf R^3$, and subspaces of second countable spaces are second countable ([[§22 Countability Axioms#^thm-22-3|590 Thm. §22.3]]). The maximal atlas containing the two charts ([[§17 Differentiable Structures#^thm-17-6|591 Thm. §17.6]]) makes $S^2$ a smooth manifold. This is part 4.
>
> **Step 8** (compatibility with the hemisphere charts). $\varphi_{z+}$ keeps the first two components, so $\varphi_{z+} \circ \varphi_N^{-1}(u) = 2u/(1 + |u|^2)$, defined where the third component $(|u|^2 - 1)/(|u|^2 + 1)$ is positive, i.e. $|u| > 1$; it is $C^\infty$. Its inverse is $\varphi_N \circ \varphi_{z+}^{-1}(x, y) = (x, y)/\bigl(1 - \sqrt{1 - x^2 - y^2}\bigr)$ on $0 < x^2 + y^2 < 1$, where the denominator is positive; it is $C^\infty$ too. The same holds for every pair of charts, for one reason: every chart of either atlas is the restriction to $S^2$ of a $C^\infty$ map defined on an open subset of $\mathbf R^3$ ($\varphi_N$ of $(x, y, z) \mapsto (x, y)/(1 - z)$ on $\{z \neq 1\}$; $\varphi_{x+}$ of the linear map $(x, y, z) \mapsto (y, z)$), and every inverse chart is a $C^\infty$ map from an open subset of $\mathbf R^2$ into $\mathbf R^3$ with values on $S^2$. A transition is then a composite of $C^\infty$ maps between open subsets of Euclidean spaces, hence $C^\infty$ by the chain rule. This is part 5.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The transition is smooth precisely because its domain excludes $u = 0$, the image of the other chart's missing pole: the singular point of $u \mapsto u/|u|^2$ is the point the overlap leaves out. All formulas above were checked symbolically.

## Maps between manifolds

Fields on spacetime, worldlines, embeddings of a sphere into space and coordinate changes are all maps involving manifolds. Each is smooth or not, and on a manifold this can only be decided in charts. The compatibility condition of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]] is what makes the answer independent of the charts chosen.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.1.5: Smooth Map; Smooth Function
> *Structure.* Let $M$ and $N$ be smooth manifolds ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]) of dimensions $m$ and $n$. A map $F : M \to N$ is **smooth** if it is continuous and for every $p \in M$ there are admissible charts $(U, \varphi)$ at $p$ and $(V, \psi)$ at $F(p)$ with $F(U) \subset V$ such that the **coordinate representation**
>
> $$
> \hat F = \psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1} : \varphi(U) \longrightarrow \psi(V) \subset \mathbf R^n
> $$
>
> is $C^\infty$. A **smooth function** is a smooth map $f : M \to \mathbf R$, with $\mathbf R$ carrying its identity chart: $f \circ \varphi^{-1}$ is $C^\infty$ for every chart. The smooth functions on $M$ form the set $C^\infty(M)$ (Couzens' $\mathcal F(M)$).
>
> *Components.* With $\varphi = (x^\mu)$ and $\psi = (y^\alpha)$, $\hat F$ is $n$ functions of $m$ variables,
>
> $$
> y^\alpha = F^\alpha(x^1, \dots, x^m), \qquad \alpha = 1, \dots, n ,
> $$
>
> and a smooth function is written $f(x^1, \dots, x^m)$. "A function of the coordinates" always means a coordinate representation $f \circ \varphi^{-1}$ in some chart.
>
> *Independence of the charts.* If $(U', \varphi')$ and $(V', \psi')$ are other admissible charts, then on the common domain
>
> $$
> \psi' \circ F \circ \varphi'^{-1} = \bigl(\psi' \circ \psi^{-1}\bigr) \circ \bigl(\psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1}\bigr) \circ \bigl(\varphi \circ \varphi'^{-1}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> a composite of two transition functions ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]) and $\hat F$, all $C^\infty$. So a map smooth in one pair of charts is smooth in every pair ([[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-2|591 Prop. §19.2]]).
>
> *In physics.* A scalar field on spacetime, such as the Klein–Gordon field of level B ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation|§B4.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) or a temperature, is a smooth function on $M$. A worldline is a smooth map from an interval of $\mathbf R$ into $M$ (its velocity is §C2.2). Each chart $\varphi : U \to \varphi(U)$ is itself a smooth map, with coordinate representation the identity.
>
> *Equivalence.* [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^def-19-3|591 Def. §19.3]] (smooth map) and [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^def-19-2|591 Def. §19.2]] (smooth function), which agree for $N = \mathbf R$ ([[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^prop-19-2|591 Prop. §19.2]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.1, eq. (3.20), Definitions 14–15 ("This is actually independent of the coordinate system") (p. 44)*

^def-c2-1-5

> [!definition] Definition §C2.1.6: Diffeomorphism
> *Structure.* A **diffeomorphism** is a smooth map $F : M \to N$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-5|Def. §C2.1.5]]) that is a bijection and whose inverse $F^{-1} : N \to M$ is also smooth. $M$ and $N$ are **diffeomorphic** if a diffeomorphism between them exists; for every purpose of these notes they are then the same manifold.
>
> *Components.* In charts, $\hat F = \psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1}$ is a $C^\infty$ bijection between open subsets of $\mathbf R^m$ and $\mathbf R^n$ whose inverse $\varphi \circ F^{-1} \circ \psi^{-1}$ is $C^\infty$. By the chain rule the two Jacobian matrices are inverse to each other, so $m = n$ and $\det\bigl(\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu\bigr) \neq 0$.
>
> *Examples within this section.* A chart is a diffeomorphism of its domain onto its image ([[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^prop-28-9|591 Prop. §28.9]]); a transition function is a diffeomorphism between open subsets of $\mathbf R^n$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]). A diffeomorphism of $M$ onto itself moves points while keeping the smooth structure. It carries tensor fields of every type along ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]]), and in general relativity it becomes the gauge freedom of the theory ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^rem-c2-4-1|§C2.4, Remark: Active and passive readings of a diffeomorphism]]; [[§C5.4 Diffeomorphism Invariance#^pr-c5-4-4|Principle §C5.4.4]]).
>
> *Homeomorphic is weaker.* A homeomorphism need not be a diffeomorphism, and one topological manifold can carry smooth structures that are not diffeomorphic: Milnor found exotic seven-spheres (28 of them), and $\mathbf R^4$ has uncountably many; this happens only in dimension $4$ and higher. These notes, like Couzens, use the standard smooth structure of each manifold. A simpler phenomenon, two different but diffeomorphic structures on $\mathbf R$, is part (c) of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-1|Example §C2.1.1]].
>
> *Equivalence.* [[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^def-19-4|591 Def. §19.4]]. Couzens' Definition 16 starts from a homeomorphism and asks that its coordinate representation and the inverse representation be $C^\infty$, which is the same condition.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.1, Definition 16 and the following paragraphs (exotic structures; a unique differentiable structure assumed) (pp. 44–45)*

^def-c2-1-6

A region of spacetime between two instants, or a solid ball, is not a manifold in the sense of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]: near its edge it looks like a half-space, not like $\mathbf R^n$. Such regions are needed whenever an integral over a region is related to its boundary, as in boundary terms of an action and in Stokes' theorem (§C2.7).

> [!definition] Definition §C2.1.7: Manifold with Boundary
> *Structure.* Let $\mathbf H^n = \{(x^1, \dots, x^n) \in \mathbf R^n : x^n \ge 0\}$ be the closed half-space, with the subspace topology ([[§5 Subspace Topology#^def-5-1|590 Def. §5.1]]). A map defined on an open subset $A$ of $\mathbf H^n$ is **smooth** if it is the restriction of a $C^\infty$ map defined on an open subset of $\mathbf R^n$ that contains $A$.
>
> An $n$-dimensional **smooth manifold with boundary** is defined as in [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]], with two changes:
> 1. a chart is a homeomorphism $\varphi : U \to \varphi(U)$ onto an open subset of $\mathbf H^n$ (open in $\mathbf H^n$; it may contain points with $x^n = 0$);
> 2. smooth compatibility means that the transition functions are smooth in the sense just defined.
>
> A point mapped to $x^n > 0$ by a chart is an **interior point**; a point mapped to $x^n = 0$ is a **boundary point**. The set of boundary points is the **boundary** $\partial M$, with coordinates $(x^1, \dots, x^{n-1}, 0)$. Both notions are independent of the chart, and $\partial M$ is an $(n-1)$-dimensional manifold ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-4|Theorem §C2.1.4]]). A manifold in the sense of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]] is the case $\partial M = \emptyset$.
>
> *In physics.* The region $[t_1, t_2] \times \mathbf R^3$ of Minkowski spacetime between two instants has the two instants as its boundary ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-4|Example §C2.1.4]]). Stokes' theorem ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]]) relates an integral over such a region to one over its boundary.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.1, manifolds with a boundary, Fig. 4 (pp. 39–40)*

^def-c2-1-7

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.1.4: The Boundary Is Well Defined and Is a Manifold
> Let $M$ be an $n$-dimensional smooth manifold with boundary ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-7|Def. §C2.1.7]]), $n \ge 1$.
> 1. If one chart maps $p$ to a point with $x^n > 0$, then every chart at $p$ does. So the split of $M$ into interior points and boundary points does not depend on the charts.
> 2. The boundary $\partial M$, with the charts $\tilde\varphi = (x^1, \dots, x^{n-1})$ obtained by restricting boundary charts to $U \cap \partial M$, is an $(n-1)$-dimensional smooth manifold ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]) without boundary.
> 3. The set of interior points is an $n$-dimensional smooth manifold without boundary.
>
> *Model:* none (a statement about manifolds with boundary); *applies:* exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.1 (the boundary $\partial M$ and its coordinates $(x^1, \dots, x^{n-1}, 0)$; stated) (p. 39) · proof written here*

^thm-c2-1-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (setting up a contradiction). Let $(U, \varphi)$ and $(V, \psi)$ be charts at $p$ with $\varphi(p)^n > 0$, and suppose $\psi(p)^n = 0$. Write $F = \psi \circ \varphi^{-1}$ on $\varphi(U \cap V)$ and $G = \varphi \circ \psi^{-1}$ on $\psi(U \cap V)$; they are inverse to each other.
>
> **Step 2** ($F$ near $\varphi(p)$). $\varphi(U \cap V)$ is open in $\mathbf H^n$ and contains $\varphi(p)$, whose last coordinate is positive; so it contains a ball $B \subset \{x^n > 0\}$ of $\mathbf R^n$ around $\varphi(p)$, open in $\mathbf R^n$. On $B$, $F$ is $C^\infty$ (it is the restriction of a $C^\infty$ map, Def. §C2.1.7).
>
> **Step 3** ($DF$ is invertible at $\varphi(p)$). $G$ is the restriction of a $C^\infty$ map $\tilde G$ on an open set of $\mathbf R^n$ containing $\psi(U \cap V)$. On $B$, $\tilde G \circ F = G \circ F = \mathrm{id}$, and the chain rule at $q = \varphi(p)$ gives
>
> $$
> D\tilde G\bigl(F(q)\bigr)\,DF(q) = I ,
> $$
>
> so the $n \times n$ matrix $DF(q)$ is invertible.
>
> **Step 4** (inverse function theorem). By [[§35 Local Diffeomorphisms#^thm-35-1|591 Thm. §35.1]] there is an open $B_0 \subset B$ around $q$ such that $F(B_0)$ is open in $\mathbf R^n$. It contains $F(q) = \psi(p)$, whose last coordinate is $0$, so it contains the points $\psi(p) - \varepsilon e_n$ for small $\varepsilon > 0$, whose last coordinate is $-\varepsilon < 0$. But $F$ takes values in $\psi(V) \subset \mathbf H^n$, where $x^n \ge 0$. Contradiction: $\psi(p)^n > 0$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 5** (charts of $\partial M$). By part 1 a boundary chart $(U, \varphi)$ maps $U \cap \partial M$ onto $\varphi(U) \cap \{x^n = 0\}$, which is open in the hyperplane $\{x^n = 0\} \cong \mathbf R^{n-1}$. Dropping the last coordinate, $\tilde\varphi = (x^1, \dots, x^{n-1})$ is a homeomorphism of $U \cap \partial M$ onto an open subset of $\mathbf R^{n-1}$.
>
> **Step 6** (their transitions). For two boundary charts, part 1 says $\psi \circ \varphi^{-1}$ maps points with $x^n = 0$ to points with $x^n = 0$, and
>
> $$
> \tilde\psi \circ \tilde\varphi^{-1}(x^1, \dots, x^{n-1}) = \text{first } n - 1 \text{ components of } \psi \circ \varphi^{-1}(x^1, \dots, x^{n-1}, 0) ,
> $$
>
> the restriction of a $C^\infty$ map to the hyperplane $x^n = 0$, hence $C^\infty$. The subspace $\partial M$ is Hausdorff (traces of disjoint neighbourhoods are disjoint) and second countable ([[§22 Countability Axioms#^thm-22-3|590 Thm. §22.3]]). This is part 2.
>
> **Step 7** (the interior). Restricting each chart to the points it maps to $x^n > 0$ gives charts onto open subsets of $\mathbf R^n$, with transitions the restrictions of the old ones. This is part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Being on the boundary is a property of the point, not of the chart, because a smooth change of coordinates with invertible Jacobian maps open sets of $\mathbf R^n$ to open sets and so cannot push an interior point onto the edge of the half-space. The boundary of a region of spacetime is therefore a definite hypersurface, on which boundary data and boundary integrals can be given.

## Coordinate singularities

> [!remark] Remark: Coordinate singularities are failures of a chart, not of the manifold
> A **coordinate singularity** is a place where a coordinate system degenerates although the manifold is perfectly regular there. Two have been computed in this section. At the origin of polar coordinates the Jacobian determinant $r$ vanishes and the inverse Jacobian blows up (opening of this section); the Cartesian chart covers the origin without trouble. At the poles of $(\theta, \varphi)$ every $\varphi$ labels the same point and the Jacobian determinant against the hemisphere chart, $\sin\theta\cos\theta$, tends to zero ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-3|Example §C2.1.3]]); the hemisphere chart covers the poles. In each case another chart of the same atlas covers the bad points, so the defect belongs to the chart.
>
> The same diagnosis applies to metrics written in coordinates. Hartle relabels the flat plane, $ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\varphi^2$, by $r = a^2/r'$ and obtains $ds^2 = (a^4/r'^4)(dr'^2 + r'^2d\varphi^2)$, which blows up at $r' = 0$, although the plane is flat everywhere: the new coordinate has sent the points at infinity to $r' = 0$. A component of a metric that diverges in some coordinates is therefore not by itself a property of spacetime. The Schwarzschild metric in its standard coordinates diverges at $r = 2M$, and other coordinates continue it smoothly across that surface ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]], [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]). A true singularity is one that no chart removes. It is detected by quantities that do not depend on the chart, such as curvature invariants ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-1|Def. §C7.1.1]]), and by freely falling worldlines that end after finite proper time (C10, planned).
>

(7.6)–(7.7) ("nothing physically interesting happens there") (p. 136) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §5.2, Fig. 5.1 (polar coordinates degenerate at the origin) (p. 66) · Couzens, GR1, §3.1, end ("we will care a lot about changing coordinates and the limitations of the coordinate systems") (p. 43)*

With charts, a function on a manifold can be differentiated: in a chart it is an ordinary function of $n$ variables, and smoothness does not depend on the chart. What cannot yet be written is a displacement or a velocity. In $\mathbf R^n$ a vector is the difference of two points; on the sphere the difference of two of its points is not on the sphere, and in a chart the coordinate differences $\Delta x^\mu$ of two events change nonlinearly when the chart changes. What object, attached to a single event, does the work of a displacement? [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms|§C2.2]] answers this.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C2.1.1: ℝⁿ and Minkowski Spacetime
> *(a) $\mathbf R^n$.* The single chart $(\mathbf R^n, \mathrm{id})$ is an atlas ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-2|Def. §C2.1.2]]). $\mathbf R^n$ is Hausdorff (distinct points $x \neq x'$ lie in the disjoint balls of radius $|x - x'|/2$ around them) and second countable (balls with rational centres and rational radii form a countable basis). A chart $(U, \varphi)$ is compatible with the identity exactly when $\varphi \circ \mathrm{id}^{-1} = \varphi$ and $\mathrm{id} \circ \varphi^{-1} = \varphi^{-1}$ are $C^\infty$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]). So the maximal atlas of $\mathbf R^n$ consists of all diffeomorphisms between open subsets of $\mathbf R^n$ in the sense of calculus ([[§17 Differentiable Structures#^ex-17-1|591 Ex. §17.1]]). Polar coordinates on the slit plane are one of them: $(r, \theta) \mapsto (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ on $(0, \infty) \times (0, 2\pi)$ is a $C^\infty$ bijection onto $\mathbf R^2 \setminus \{y = 0,\ x \ge 0\}$ with Jacobian determinant $r > 0$, so its inverse is $C^\infty$ ([[§35 Local Diffeomorphisms#^thm-35-1|591 Thm. §35.1]]). It is an admissible chart of $\mathbf R^2$ that does not cover $\mathbf R^2$.
>
> *(b) Minkowski spacetime.* The events of special relativity form $M = \mathbf R^4$ with this standard structure. An inertial frame is a global chart $x^\mu = (t, x, y, z)$. A second inertial frame is related to it by a Poincaré transformation $x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu + a^\mu$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-4|Def. §B1.2.4]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, $x^0 = ct$). This transition function is affine, hence $C^\infty$; its inverse $x^\nu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu\,(x'^\mu - a^\mu)$ is affine too; its Jacobian $\partial x'^\mu/\partial x^\nu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$ is constant with $\det\Lambda = \pm 1$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]). So all inertial charts are compatible and lie in one maximal atlas, the standard one. That atlas contains far more than inertial charts. Spherical coordinates $(t, r, \theta, \varphi)$, with $x = r\sin\theta\cos\varphi$, $y = r\sin\theta\sin\varphi$, $z = r\cos\theta$ on $r > 0$, $\theta \in (0, \pi)$, $\varphi \in (0, 2\pi)$, have
>
> $$
> \det\frac{\partial(x, y, z)}{\partial(r, \theta, \varphi)} = r^2\sin\theta > 0
> $$
>
> on that domain: an admissible chart, which misses the half-plane $\{y = 0,\ x \ge 0\}$ at every $t$. In it the Minkowski line element reads $-dt^2 + dr^2 + r^2d\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2$ (Hartle (7.4)): the same spacetime, labelled differently. What distinguishes the inertial charts is the metric, which takes the form $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ in them ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-4|Def. §C3.2.4]]), not the manifold.
>
> *(c) A homeomorphism that is not an admissible chart.* $\varphi(x) = x^3$ is a homeomorphism $\mathbf R \to \mathbf R$ (its inverse $s \mapsto s^{1/3}$ is continuous), so $(\mathbf R, \varphi)$ is a chart in the sense of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]. Its transition to the identity chart is
>
> $$
> \mathrm{id} \circ \varphi^{-1}(s) = s^{1/3}, \qquad \frac{d}{ds}\,s^{1/3} = \frac13\,s^{-2/3} \xrightarrow{\ s \to 0\ } \infty ,
> $$
>
> not $C^\infty$ at $s = 0$. So $\varphi$ is not an admissible chart of $\mathbf R$. On its own, the atlas $\{(\mathbf R, \varphi)\}$ defines a second smooth structure $\tilde{\mathbf R}$ on the same set. In it a function $f$ is smooth when $f(s^{1/3})$ is $C^\infty$ in $s$: $f(x) = x^3$ is smooth on $\tilde{\mathbf R}$, while $f(x) = x$ is not (its representation is $s^{1/3}$). The two structures are nevertheless diffeomorphic ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-6|Def. §C2.1.6]]): $F(x) = x^3$ from $\tilde{\mathbf R}$ to $\mathbf R$ has coordinate representation $\mathrm{id} \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1}(s) = (s^{1/3})^3 = s$, and its inverse has representation $\varphi \circ F^{-1}(y) = (y^{1/3})^3 = y$. The identity map $\tilde{\mathbf R} \to \mathbf R$, with representation $s^{1/3}$, is not a diffeomorphism. Being a homeomorphism does not make a coordinate admissible; admissibility is relative to the smooth structure. ([[§19 Smooth Functions and Smooth Maps#^ex-19-1|591 Ex. §19.1]] treats the same pair with the cube root as the chart.)
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.1, Example 3.5, first item ($\mathbf R^n$ with one chart) (p. 40) · Hartle, Gravity, §7.1, eqs. (7.1)–(7.4) (pp. 135–136) · worked here; determinants and the derivative checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.1.2: The Circle with Two Angle Charts
> *Setting.* $S^1 = \{(x, y) \in \mathbf R^2 : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$ with the subspace topology.
>
> *(a) Two angle charts.* Let
>
> $$
> \varphi_1^{-1} : (0, 2\pi) \to S^1 \setminus \{(1, 0)\}, \qquad \varphi_2^{-1} : (-\pi, \pi) \to S^1 \setminus \{(-1, 0)\}, \qquad \theta \mapsto (\cos\theta, \sin\theta) .
> $$
>
> Each is a continuous bijection onto its image. The charts themselves are continuous: from the half-angle identities $\cot(\theta/2) = \sin\theta/(1 - \cos\theta)$ and $\tan(\theta/2) = \sin\theta/(1 + \cos\theta)$,
>
> $$
> \varphi_1(x, y) = 2\operatorname{arccot}\frac{y}{1 - x}\ \ (x \neq 1), \qquad \varphi_2(x, y) = 2\arctan\frac{y}{1 + x}\ \ (x \neq -1) ,
> $$
>
> with $\operatorname{arccot} : \mathbf R \to (0, \pi)$ and $\arctan : \mathbf R \to (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ continuous. So both are homeomorphisms onto open intervals: charts ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]). Their domains cover $S^1$.
>
> *(b) The transition.* The overlap is $S^1 \setminus \{(1, 0), (-1, 0)\}$, the two open semicircles, and $\varphi_1(\text{overlap}) = (0, \pi) \cup (\pi, 2\pi)$. A point with $\varphi_1$-coordinate $\theta \in (0, \pi)$ lies on the upper semicircle, and its $\varphi_2$-coordinate is the same $\theta$. A point with $\theta \in (\pi, 2\pi)$ lies on the lower semicircle; its $\varphi_2$-coordinate must lie in $(-\pi, 0)$ and have the same cosine and sine, so it is $\theta - 2\pi$. Hence
>
> $$
> \psi_{21}(\theta) = \varphi_2 \circ \varphi_1^{-1}(\theta) = \begin{cases} \theta , & \theta \in (0, \pi) , \\ \theta - 2\pi , & \theta \in (\pi, 2\pi) , \end{cases} \qquad \psi_{12}(\theta) = \begin{cases} \theta , & \theta \in (0, \pi) , \\ \theta + 2\pi , & \theta \in (-\pi, 0) , \end{cases}
> $$
>
> each a translation on each of two intervals, with derivative $1$: $C^\infty$ on its domain ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]). A transition function need not be one formula; its domain here has two components. The two charts form a smooth atlas, and $S^1$ is a smooth 1-manifold.
>
> *(c) Why $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ is not a chart.* First, $[0, 2\pi)$ is not open in $\mathbf R$: at $\theta = 0$ only one-sided limits exist, and derivatives at $(1, 0)$ cannot be formed (Couzens' footnote). Second, the labelling is not continuous at $(1, 0)$. The points $(\cos\varepsilon, -\sin\varepsilon)$ tend to $(1, 0)$ as $\varepsilon \to 0^+$, but their labels $2\pi - \varepsilon$ tend to $2\pi$, not to the label $0$ of $(1, 0)$: nearby points get distant labels. No chart at all can have the whole circle as its domain ([[§17 Differentiable Structures#^prop-17-4|591 Prop. §17.4]]; [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-2|Theorem §C2.1.2]], part 2).
>
> *(d) A second atlas with the same structure.* Couzens also covers $S^1$ by four projection charts: $\chi_1(x, y) = y$ on $\{x > 0\}$, $\chi_2(x, y) = y$ on $\{x < 0\}$, $\chi_3(x, y) = x$ on $\{y > 0\}$, $\chi_4(x, y) = x$ on $\{y < 0\}$, with for instance $\chi_3 \circ \chi_1^{-1}(y) = \sqrt{1 - y^2}$ on $0 < y < 1$ (GR1 (3.14)). Compare with the angle charts: on the upper semicircle,
>
> $$
> \chi_3 \circ \varphi_1^{-1}(\theta) = \cos\theta, \quad \frac{d}{d\theta}\cos\theta = -\sin\theta < 0 \ \text{on}\ (0, \pi); \qquad \varphi_1 \circ \chi_3^{-1}(s) = \arccos s, \quad \frac{d}{ds}\arccos s = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - s^2}} \ \text{on}\ (-1, 1) ,
> $$
>
> both $C^\infty$; the other overlaps go the same way. So the angle atlas and the projection atlas are compatible, and they define one smooth manifold $S^1$ ([[§17 Differentiable Structures#^rem-17-9|591 §17, Remark: Three Atlases and One Structure]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.1, Example 3.5, eqs. (3.8)–(3.14), Figs. 5–6 and footnote a (pp. 40–43) · [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^ex-17-2|591 Ex. §17.2]], [[§17 Differentiable Structures#^ex-17-4|591 Ex. §17.4]] · chart formulas and derivatives checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.1.3: The Sphere: Hemisphere Charts and (θ, φ)
> *(a) Hemisphere charts.* Let $U_{x\pm} = \{p \in S^2 : \pm x > 0\}$, and likewise $U_{y\pm}$, $U_{z\pm}$: six open sets, which cover $S^2$ because every point of the sphere has a nonzero coordinate. The chart $\varphi_{x+}(x, y, z) = (y, z)$ maps $U_{x+}$ bijectively onto the open unit disk $D = \{(y, z) : y^2 + z^2 < 1\}$, with continuous inverse $(y, z) \mapsto \bigl(\sqrt{1 - y^2 - z^2},\ y,\ z\bigr)$; the other five drop the coordinate whose sign is fixed and restore it in the inverse as $+\sqrt{\cdot}$ or $-\sqrt{\cdot}$ according to that sign. The transition from $\varphi_{x+}$ to $\varphi_{y-}$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]) is defined on $\varphi_{x+}(U_{x+} \cap U_{y-}) = \{(y, z) \in D : y < 0\}$:
>
> $$
> \psi_{(y-)(x+)}(y, z) = \varphi_{y-}\bigl(\sqrt{1 - y^2 - z^2},\ y,\ z\bigr) = \bigl(\sqrt{1 - y^2 - z^2},\ z\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> $C^\infty$ because $1 - y^2 - z^2 > 0$ on $D$. Its Jacobian matrix and determinant are
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix} -y/\sqrt{1 - y^2 - z^2} & -z/\sqrt{1 - y^2 - z^2} \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det = -\frac{y}{\sqrt{1 - y^2 - z^2}} > 0 \quad (y < 0) ,
> $$
>
> and the inverse transition $(x, z) \mapsto \bigl(-\sqrt{1 - x^2 - z^2},\ z\bigr)$ on $\{(x, z) \in D : x > 0\}$ is $C^\infty$ as well. So the six charts form a smooth atlas, compatible with the two stereographic charts ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]], part 5).
>
> *(b) The angles $(\theta, \varphi)$.* Couzens' polar chart is the inverse of
>
> $$
> (\theta, \varphi) \mapsto (\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ \cos\theta), \qquad (\theta, \varphi) \in (0, \pi) \times (0, 2\pi) .
> $$
>
> *What it misses.* On this domain $\sin\theta > 0$, so $y = \sin\theta\sin\varphi = 0$ only for $\varphi = \pi$, where $x = -\sin\theta < 0$. No point with $y = 0$ and $x \ge 0$ is reached: the chart misses the closed half great circle $C = \{y = 0,\ x \ge 0\}$, which contains both poles (figure after [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^rem-c2-1-1|the remark on coordinate singularities]]). Every other point is reached exactly once ($\theta = \arccos z$, then $\varphi$ from $(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi) = (x, y)/\sin\theta$).
>
> *Same structure.* On the upper part, $\theta < \pi/2$, compare with the hemisphere chart $\varphi_{z+}(x, y, z) = (x, y)$:
>
> $$
> \varphi_{z+} \circ (\theta, \varphi)^{-1} : (\theta, \varphi) \mapsto (\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi), \qquad \det\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta\cos\varphi & -\sin\theta\sin\varphi \\ \cos\theta\sin\varphi & \sin\theta\cos\varphi \end{pmatrix} = \sin\theta\cos\theta\,(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi) = \sin\theta\cos\theta .
> $$
>
> For $0 < \theta < \pi/2$ this is positive, and the map is injective, so its inverse is $C^\infty$ ([[§35 Local Diffeomorphisms#^thm-35-1|591 Thm. §35.1]]): $(\theta, \varphi)$ is an admissible chart of the same smooth structure.
>
> *What happens at a pole.* As $\theta \to 0$ the determinant $\sin\theta\cos\theta \to 0$, and changing $\varphi$ at fixed $\theta$ moves the point with speed $\bigl|\partial(x, y, z)/\partial\varphi\bigr| = \bigl|(-\sin\theta\sin\varphi, \sin\theta\cos\varphi, 0)\bigr| = \sin\theta \to 0$: at $\theta = 0$ every $\varphi$ gives $N$. The chart cannot be extended to $N$. The hemisphere chart $\varphi_{z+}$ covers $N$ with the coordinates $(0, 0)$, so the defect belongs to the chart ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^rem-c2-1-1|Remark: Coordinate singularities are failures of a chart, not of the manifold]]).
>
> *(c) Two angle charts cover the sphere.* Couzens' second polar chart, $x = -\sin\theta'\cos\varphi'$, $y = \cos\theta'$, $z = \sin\theta'\sin\varphi'$ on $(0, \pi) \times (0, 2\pi)$, misses $C' = \{z = 0,\ x \le 0\}$ by the same argument ($z = 0$ only at $\varphi' = \pi$, where $x = \sin\theta' > 0$). The two missed sets do not meet:
>
> $$
> C \cap C' \subset \{y = 0,\ z = 0,\ x \ge 0,\ x \le 0\} = \{(0, 0, 0)\}, \qquad (0, 0, 0) \notin S^2 .
> $$
>
> So the two charts together cover $S^2$: for instance $(1, 0, 0) \in C$ is reached by $(\theta', \varphi') = (\pi/2, \pi)$, and $(-1, 0, 0) \in C'$ by $(\theta, \varphi) = (\pi/2, \pi)$. In each chart the round metric of the sphere takes the familiar form $d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2$ (Couzens (4.9), (4.13); metrics are [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.1, Example 3.5, eqs. (3.15)–(3.19) (pp. 42–43); §4.1.1, Example 4.1, eqs. (4.9)–(4.13) (p. 75) · Hartle, Gravity, §7.1 (points on the polar axis are "labeled by more than one set of coordinate values") (p. 136) · worked here; transitions, determinants and the covering checked by script*

> [!caution] Caution: Embeddings and angle ranges in Couzens' GR1
> - In the sphere example, GR1 (§3.1, p. 42) notes that the embedding in $\mathbf R^{n+1}$ is only a convenience and adds that "some n-dimensional spaces cannot be embedded in $\mathbf R^{n+1}$, for example hyperbolic space". As a smooth manifold, hyperbolic space is diffeomorphic to $\mathbf R^n$ (its upper half-space model is the open set $\{x^n > 0\} \subset \mathbf R^n$), so it embeds in $\mathbf R^{n+1}$ as a hyperplane. What fails is an embedding that preserves the metric of hyperbolic space (for $n = 2$ this is Hilbert's theorem), a statement about metrics ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^ex-c3-1-4|Example §C3.1.4]]). The general point stands: [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]] refers only to charts, and every $n$-manifold can be embedded in $\mathbf R^{2n+1}$ anyway ([[§66 The Whitney Embedding Theorem#^thm-66-11|591 Thm. §66.11]]).
> - GR1 (4.9) gives the polar angle the range $\varphi \in [0, 2\pi)$, and (4.11) the open range $(0, 2\pi)$. Only the open range gives a chart ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-2|Example §C2.1.2]], part (c)); the text that follows (4.11) uses the open range.

> [!example] Example §C2.1.4: The Closed Disk and a Slab of Spacetime
> *(a) The closed disk* $\bar D = \{(x, y) : x^2 + y^2 \le 1\}$, a manifold with boundary ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-7|Def. §C2.1.7]]) of dimension $2$.
> - *Interior chart:* $(x, y) \mapsto (x, y + 2)$ on the open disk $x^2 + y^2 < 1$. Its image lies in $\{y > 0\}$, so it is an open subset of $\mathbf H^2$ without boundary points.
> - *Edge charts:* with $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ and $\theta$ the angle of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-2|Example §C2.1.2]], let $\varphi_1(p) = (\theta, 1 - r)$, $\theta \in (0, 2\pi)$, on the set $\{\tfrac14 < r^2 \le 1\}$ minus the segment $\{y = 0,\ x > 0\}$. Its image $(0, 2\pi) \times [0, \tfrac12)$ is open in $\mathbf H^2$; the second coordinate $s = 1 - r$ is $\ge 0$. A second edge chart $\varphi_2$ uses $\theta \in (-\pi, \pi)$ and omits the segment $\{y = 0,\ x < 0\}$.
> - *Transitions:* $\varphi_2 \circ \varphi_1^{-1}(\theta, s) = (\theta, s)$ or $(\theta - 2\pi, s)$, as in Example §C2.1.2, part (b); from $\varphi_1$ to the interior chart, $(\theta, s) \mapsto \bigl((1 - s)\cos\theta,\ (1 - s)\sin\theta + 2\bigr)$ for $0 < s < \tfrac12$. All are restrictions of $C^\infty$ maps on open subsets of $\mathbf R^2$.
> - *Boundary:* $s = 0$ exactly when $r = 1$, so $\partial\bar D = S^1$. Restricting $\varphi_1$, $\varphi_2$ to it and dropping $s$ gives the two angle charts of Example §C2.1.2: the atlas of the boundary predicted by [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-4|Theorem §C2.1.4]], part 2.
>
> *(b) A slab of Minkowski spacetime*, $M = [t_1, t_2] \times \mathbf R^3 = \{(t, x, y, z) : t_1 \le t \le t_2\}$, $t_1 < t_2$.
> - *Charts:* $\varphi_-(t, x, y, z) = (x, y, z, t - t_1)$ on $\{t < t_2\}$ and $\varphi_+(t, x, y, z) = (x, y, z, t_2 - t)$ on $\{t > t_1\}$. Both have image $\mathbf R^3 \times [0, t_2 - t_1)$, open in $\mathbf H^4$.
> - *Transition:* on the overlap $t_1 < t < t_2$, $\varphi_+ \circ \varphi_-^{-1}(x, y, z, s) = \bigl(x, y, z, (t_2 - t_1) - s\bigr)$, affine, hence the restriction of a $C^\infty$ map on $\mathbf R^4$.
> - *Boundary:* the last coordinate vanishes at $t = t_1$ in $\varphi_-$ and at $t = t_2$ in $\varphi_+$, so $\partial M = \bigl(\{t_1\} \times \mathbf R^3\bigr) \cup \bigl(\{t_2\} \times \mathbf R^3\bigr)$, two disjoint copies of $\mathbf R^3$. These are the initial and final instants on which a variational principle fixes the field, and over which the integral of a divergence across the slab is evaluated (Stokes' theorem, [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.1, manifolds with a boundary, Fig. 4 (pp. 39–40) · worked here*
