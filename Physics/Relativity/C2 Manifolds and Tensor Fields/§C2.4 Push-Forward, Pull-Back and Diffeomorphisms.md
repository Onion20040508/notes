[[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms|§C2.2]] and [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields|§C2.3]] built vectors, one-forms and tensors at a point of one manifold, and compared their components in two charts on the same region ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]]). General relativity also has to carry these objects *from one manifold to another* and *from one point to another*. A worldline is a map from an interval of the real line into spacetime; its velocity at an instant is the image of the unit vector $d/d\lambda$ of the interval. The round metric of a sphere is inherited from the Euclidean space it sits in. The coupling of a charge to the electromagnetic potential integrates a one-form of spacetime along a curve. The Lie derivative of [[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors|§C2.5]] compares a tensor at one point with a tensor at a nearby point by carrying it along a flow. And a smooth relabelling of the points of spacetime, applied to all fields at once, will turn out to change nothing physical (§C5.4, planned). The question of this section: *given a smooth map $f : M \to N$, which tensors can be carried along it, in which direction, and how do their components change?*

First, what fails. Let $M$ be the open unit disk with Cartesian coordinates $(x^1, x^2)$, $N = \mathbf R^3$ with coordinates $(y^1, y^2, y^3)$, and let $f$ map the disk onto the upper unit hemisphere (Couzens, GR1, Example 3.6):

$$
f(x^1, x^2) = \Bigl(x^1,\ x^2,\ \sqrt{1 - (x^1)^2 - (x^2)^2}\Bigr) .
$$

Take the vector $V = \partial_1$ at $p = (\tfrac35, 0)$, so $f(p) = (\tfrac35, 0, \tfrac45)$. The naive transfer copies the components: $V$ "is" $(1, 0, 0)$ in $\mathbf R^3$, i.e. $\partial/\partial y^1$. Is that tangent to the sphere? The sphere is the level set $F = 1$ of $F(y) = (y^1)^2 + (y^2)^2 + (y^3)^2$, and $F$ is constant along every curve in the sphere, so the velocity $W$ of such a curve has $W[F] = 0$. The copied vector gives

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial y^1}\Bigl[(y^1)^2 + (y^2)^2 + (y^3)^2\Bigr]_{f(p)} = 2y^1 = \tfrac65 \neq 0 :
$$

it points out of the sphere. Moving along $x^1$ on the disk also changes the height $y^3 = \sqrt{1 - r^2}$, and the copy ignores that. A vector has to be carried through the derivatives of *all* the component functions of the map. A second failure concerns direction. Let $\pi : \mathbf R^2 \to \mathbf R$, $\pi(x, y) = x$, and try to carry the vector field $V = (1 + y^2)\,\partial_x$ to $\mathbf R$. The points $(x_0, 0)$ and $(x_0, 1)$ both map to $x_0$, and their vectors $\partial_x$ and $2\,\partial_x$ map to $d/dx$ and $2\,d/dx$ there: the "field on $\mathbf R$" would have two values at $x_0$. The one-form $dx$ on $\mathbf R$, in the other direction, gives the single one-form $dx$ on $\mathbf R^2$.

The section builds the answer in order. Vectors at a point are carried forward along any smooth map ([[#^def-c2-4-1|Def. §C2.4.1]]), with the Jacobian as their matrix ([[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]]). One-forms and all tensors with only lower indices are carried backward ([[#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]], [[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]]), by the transpose of the same matrix, and the two operations fit together in the pairing ([[#^thm-c2-4-3|Theorem §C2.4.3]]). Tensors with only upper indices go forward at a point ([[#^def-c2-4-3|Def. §C2.4.3]]). A diffeomorphism carries tensors of every type, as fields, and its action on components is the transformation law of §C2.3 read the other way round ([[#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]]; [[#^rem-c2-4-1|Remark: Active and passive readings]]). Last, what survives along maps that are not diffeomorphisms, such as worldlines and embedded surfaces ([[#^thm-c2-4-5|Theorem §C2.4.5]]).

**Setup.** Two smooth manifolds $M$ and $N$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]) of dimensions $m$ and $n$, which may differ, and a smooth map $f : M \to N$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-5|Def. §C2.1.5]]). At $p \in M$ choose charts ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]) $(U, x^\mu)$ at $p$ and $(U', y^\alpha)$ at $f(p)$ with $f(U) \subset U'$. The coordinates of the image point are then smooth functions of those of the point, written $y^\alpha(x)$ (short for the coordinate representation $y \circ f \circ x^{-1}$), and $\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu$ is the $n \times m$ Jacobian matrix, row $\alpha$, column $\mu$. Everything in the section is exact and uses no metric. Where a metric appears (the Euclidean $\delta$ of $\mathbf R^3$, the Minkowski $\eta$), it is a given $(0, 2)$ tensor field, pulled back like any other; nothing is raised or lowered. The algebra of the examples and their numbers were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$, $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]), used in Examples §C2.4.1 and §C2.4.3; $G = c = 1$; Greek indices from the middle of the alphabet ($\mu, \nu, \rho$) label coordinates of $M$, from the start ($\alpha, \beta, \gamma$) coordinates of $N$, summed when repeated; for spacetime they run over $0$–$3$. The push-forward carries a lower star, $f_{\ast}$, the pull-back an upper star, $f^{\ast}$. Level B and its links use $+{-}{-}{-}$ with $c$ kept.

## Vectors are pushed forward

A tangent vector is a derivation: it differentiates functions ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]]). A function on $N$ becomes a function on $M$ by composition with $f$, and a vector at $p$ can differentiate that. This gives a derivation at $f(p)$, with no choice made and no chart used.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.4.1: Push-Forward of a Vector
> *Structure.* Let $f : M \to N$ be a smooth map ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-5|Def. §C2.1.5]]) and $p \in M$. A tangent vector $V \in T_pM$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]) is a derivation of smooth functions at $p$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]]). A smooth function $g$ on $N$, defined near $f(p)$, gives the smooth function $g \circ f$ on $M$ near $p$, so $V[g \circ f]$ is a number. The **push-forward** of $V$ is the tangent vector at $f(p)$
>
> $$
> f_{\ast}V \in T_{f(p)}N, \qquad (f_{\ast}V)[g] \equiv V[g \circ f] .
> $$
>
> It is a derivation at $f(p)$: for two functions $g$, $h$, the Leibniz rule of $V$ gives $(f_{\ast}V)[gh] = V[(g \circ f)(h \circ f)] = g(f(p))\,(f_{\ast}V)[h] + h(f(p))\,(f_{\ast}V)[g]$. It depends linearly on $V$, and the linear map
>
> $$
> f_{\ast p} : T_pM \longrightarrow T_{f(p)}N, \qquad V \longmapsto f_{\ast}V ,
> $$
>
> is the **differential** of $f$ at $p$ (also called the push-forward map or tangent map; written $df_p$ as well).
>
> *Curves.* If $V$ is the velocity at $p$ of a curve $c$ with $c(0) = p$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]), then $f_{\ast}V$ is the velocity of the image curve $f \circ c$ at $f(p)$:
>
> $$
> (f_{\ast}V)[g] = V[g \circ f] = \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(g \circ f \circ c\bigr)(0) = \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(g \circ (f \circ c)\bigr)(0) .
> $$
>
> A vector is carried by carrying a curve it is the velocity of (figure below).
>
> *Mathematics.* This is 591's pushforward ([[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^def-28-6|591 Def. §28.6]], linear by [[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^prop-28-5|591 Prop. §28.5]]). There $V$ acts on germs of functions at $p$; here, as in Couzens, on smooth functions. The two spaces of derivations are identified by [[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^prop-28-3|591 Prop. §28.3]], and the defining formula is the same. The curve form is [[§31 Tangent Vectors as Velocities of Curves#^cor-31-5|591 Cor. §31.5]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.6, Def. 20, eqs. (3.53)–(3.54), Fig. 7 (pp. 51–52)*

^def-c2-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.4.1: Push-Forward in Components
> Let $f : M \to N$ be smooth, with charts $(U, x^\mu)$ at $p$ and $(U', y^\alpha)$ at $f(p)$, $f(U) \subset U'$.
> 1. *Basis vectors.* The coordinate basis vectors ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]]) are carried to
>
> $$
> f_{\ast}\Bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\Big|_p\Bigr) = \frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}(p)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y^\alpha}\Big|_{f(p)} .
> $$
>
> 2. *Components.* For $V = V^\mu\,\partial_\mu|_p$, the push-forward is $f_{\ast}V = W^\alpha\,\partial/\partial y^\alpha|_{f(p)}$ with
>
> $$
> W^\alpha = \frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}(p)\,V^\mu = V\bigl[y^\alpha \circ f\bigr] :
> $$
>
> the $n \times m$ Jacobian times the column of components. The $\alpha$-th component is the rate of change of the $\alpha$-th coordinate of the image point along $V$.
> 3. *Chain rule.* For a second smooth map $h : N \to P$, with a chart $z^a$ near $h(f(p))$,
>
> $$
> (h \circ f)_{\ast} = h_{\ast} \circ f_{\ast}, \qquad \frac{\partial z^a}{\partial x^\mu} = \frac{\partial z^a}{\partial y^\alpha}\,\frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu} ,
> $$
>
> and $(\mathrm{id}_M)_{\ast} = \mathrm{id}$: Jacobians multiply.
> 4. *A change of chart is a special case.* For $M = N$, $f = \mathrm{id}_M$ and two charts $x$, $x'$ at $p$ (take $y = x'$), part 2 reads $V'^\nu = (\partial x'^\nu/\partial x^\mu)\,V^\mu$, the transformation law of vector components ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]). A change of chart is the push-forward by the identity map, read in two charts.
>
> In 591 these are the matrix of the differential ([[§30 The Differential in Coordinates#^thm-30-2|591 Thm. §30.2]]), the chain rule ([[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^thm-28-6|591 Thm. §28.6]]) and the change of coordinates ([[§30 The Differential in Coordinates#^cor-30-4|591 Cor. §30.4]]).
>
> *Model:* none, a statement about smooth maps; *applies:* every smooth map and every pair of charts with $f(U) \subset U'$; exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.6, eqs. (3.55)–(3.56) (p. 52; "nothing but the Jacobian of the map")*

^thm-c2-4-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (a vector at $f(p)$ is fixed by its values on the coordinate functions). By [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]], every $W \in T_{f(p)}N$ is
>
> $$
> W = W[y^\alpha]\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y^\alpha}\Big|_{f(p)} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (apply the push-forward of a basis vector to $y^\alpha$). By [[#^def-c2-4-1|Def. §C2.4.1]] with $g = y^\alpha$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(f_{\ast}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\Big|_p\Bigr)[y^\alpha] = \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\Big|_p\bigl(y^\alpha \circ f\bigr) = \frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}(p) ,
> $$
>
> the partial derivative of the $\alpha$-th coordinate of the image point with respect to $x^\mu$. Inserted into Step 1 with $W = f_{\ast}\partial_\mu$, this is part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (linearity). With $V = V^\mu\partial_\mu$ and the linearity of $f_{\ast p}$,
>
> $$
> f_{\ast}V = V^\mu\,f_{\ast}\partial_\mu = V^\mu\,\frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y^\alpha}, \qquad W^\alpha = (f_{\ast}V)[y^\alpha] = V[y^\alpha \circ f] .
> $$
>
> This is part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (chain rule). Let $k$ be a smooth function near $h(f(p))$. Applying the definition twice,
>
> $$
> \bigl((h \circ f)_{\ast}V\bigr)[k] = V[k \circ h \circ f] = V\bigl[(k \circ h) \circ f\bigr] = (f_{\ast}V)[k \circ h] = \bigl(h_{\ast}(f_{\ast}V)\bigr)[k] .
> $$
>
> Two derivations at the same point that agree on every function are equal, so $(h \circ f)_{\ast}V = h_{\ast}(f_{\ast}V)$. In components, part 2 for $h \circ f$ gives $\bigl((h \circ f)_{\ast}V\bigr)^a = (\partial z^a/\partial x^\mu)V^\mu$, and part 2 for $h$ applied to $W = f_{\ast}V$ gives $(\partial z^a/\partial y^\alpha)W^\alpha = (\partial z^a/\partial y^\alpha)(\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu)V^\mu$. These agree for every $V$, which gives the product of Jacobians. For the identity, $((\mathrm{id})_{\ast}V)[g] = V[g \circ \mathrm{id}] = V[g]$. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (the identity map in two charts). With $f = \mathrm{id}_M$, $y^\alpha = x'^\alpha$, the coordinate representation $x' \circ \mathrm{id} \circ x^{-1}$ is the transition function of the two charts, and part 2 becomes $V'^\nu = (\partial x'^\nu/\partial x^\mu)V^\mu$. This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The components of a pushed-forward vector are derivatives of the image coordinates along the vector (Step 3). The third component in the opening failure is exactly the rate of change of the height $y^3$, which copying the components dropped.
> - Nothing beyond the chain rule enters. The Jacobian of an arbitrary smooth map has taken the place of level B's Lorentz matrix $\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$, the Jacobian of the linear map $x \mapsto \Lambda x$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where $\Lambda$ has the same entries).

## Covectors and covariant tensors are pulled back

A one-form at $f(p)$ eats vectors at $f(p)$, and a vector at $p$ can be pushed there and fed to it. That defines a one-form at $p$, so one-forms travel against the map. The same works for any number of vector slots. It is the route by which the metric of $\mathbf R^3$ becomes the metric of a sphere ([[#^ex-c2-4-2|Example §C2.4.2]]) and by which a line integral of a one-form becomes an ordinary integral over a parameter ([[#^ex-c2-4-3|Example §C2.4.3]]).

> [!definition] Definition §C2.4.2: Pull-Back of One-Forms and of (0, s) Tensors
> *Structure.* Let $f : M \to N$ be smooth, $p \in M$, $s \ge 1$, and let $T$ be a tensor of type $(0, s)$ at $f(p)$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]), a multilinear map $(T_{f(p)}N)^s \to \mathbf R$. The **pull-back** of $T$ is the $(0, s)$ tensor at $p$
>
> $$
> (f^{\ast}T)(V_1, \dots, V_s) \equiv T\bigl(f_{\ast}V_1, \dots, f_{\ast}V_s\bigr), \qquad V_1, \dots, V_s \in T_pM ,
> $$
>
> with the push-forward of [[#^def-c2-4-1|Def. §C2.4.1]] in each slot.
> - *One-forms* ($s = 1$). For a one-form $\omega \in T^{\ast}_{f(p)}N$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-5|Def. §C2.2.5]]), $\langle f^{\ast}\omega, V\rangle = \langle\omega, f_{\ast}V\rangle$. The map $f^{\ast}_p : T^{\ast}_{f(p)}N \to T^{\ast}_pM$ is the dual map of $f_{\ast p}$ ([[§12 Duality#^ladr-3-118|LADR Def. 3.118]]).
> - *Functions* ($s = 0$). $f^{\ast}g \equiv g \circ f$.
> - *Fields.* For a $(0, s)$ tensor field $T$ on $N$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]), $f^{\ast}T$ is the $(0, s)$ tensor field on $M$ with $(f^{\ast}T)_p \equiv f^{\ast}\bigl(T_{f(p)}\bigr)$. Every $p$ has an image point $f(p)$, so the definition asks nothing of $f$ beyond smoothness.
>
> The arrow is reversed: $f$ goes from $M$ to $N$, $f^{\ast}$ from tensors on $N$ to tensors on $M$.
>
> *Mathematics.* 591 defines the pull-back of covectors at a point ([[§34 The Cotangent Space from Germs#^def-34-3|591 Def. §34.3]]), of one-form fields ([[§53 One-Forms#^def-53-8|591 Def. §53.8]]), of functions ([[§53 One-Forms#^def-53-7|591 Def. §53.7]]) and of alternating forms ([[§79 Pullbacks of Linear Forms#^def-79-1|591 Def. §79.1]], [[§80 Differential Forms on Manifolds#^def-80-9|591 Def. §80.9]]), by the same formula. Symmetric $(0, s)$ tensors, a metric among them, are not covered there; this box is their home.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.6, Def. 21, eqs. (3.59)–(3.60) and the sentence after (3.61) ("can be extended to tensors of type (0, r)", p. 53)*

^def-c2-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.4.2: Pull-Back in Components
> Let $f : M \to N$ be smooth, with charts $(U, x^\mu)$ at $p$ and $(U', y^\alpha)$ at $f(p)$, $f(U) \subset U'$.
> 1. *Basis one-forms and differentials.* With the differential of a function ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]]),
>
> $$
> f^{\ast}(dy^\alpha) = \frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}\,dx^\mu = d\bigl(y^\alpha \circ f\bigr), \qquad\text{and more generally}\qquad f^{\ast}(dg) = d(g \circ f) .
> $$
>
> 2. *One-forms.* For $\omega = \omega_\alpha\,dy^\alpha$,
>
> $$
> (f^{\ast}\omega)_\mu(p) = \frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}(p)\,\omega_\alpha\bigl(f(p)\bigr) :
> $$
>
> the transpose of the Jacobian acts on the column of components.
> 3. *$(0, s)$ tensors.*
>
> $$
> (f^{\ast}T)_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_s}(p) = \frac{\partial y^{\alpha_1}}{\partial x^{\mu_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial y^{\alpha_s}}{\partial x^{\mu_s}}\;T_{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_s}\bigl(f(p)\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 4. *Substitution rule.* $f^{\ast}$ respects tensor products and multiplication by functions, $f^{\ast}(S \otimes S') = f^{\ast}S \otimes f^{\ast}S'$ and $f^{\ast}(hS) = (h \circ f)\,f^{\ast}S$. So a tensor written in coordinates is pulled back by substituting the coordinates of the image point and their differentials:
>
> $$
> f^{\ast}\bigl(T_{\alpha\beta}(y)\,dy^\alpha \otimes dy^\beta\bigr) = T_{\alpha\beta}\bigl(y(x)\bigr)\,\frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}\frac{\partial y^\beta}{\partial x^\nu}\,dx^\mu \otimes dx^\nu ,
> $$
>
> and in the same way for a line element $g_{\alpha\beta}\,dy^\alpha dy^\beta$.
> 5. *A change of chart is a special case.* For $M = N$, $f = \mathrm{id}$ and two charts $x$, $x'$, part 2 reads $\omega_\mu = (\partial x'^\alpha/\partial x^\mu)\,\omega'_\alpha$, equivalent to the covariant law $\omega'_\nu = (\partial x^\mu/\partial x'^\nu)\,\omega_\mu$ of [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]] after multiplication by the inverse Jacobian; part 3 likewise gives the lower-index part of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]].
>
> In 591: [[§34 The Cotangent Space from Germs#^prop-34-6|591 Prop. §34.6]] and [[§53 One-Forms#^prop-53-6|591 Prop. §53.6]] (one-forms), [[§53 One-Forms#^cor-53-5|591 Cor. §53.5]] ($f^{\ast}dg = d(g \circ f)$), [[§80 Differential Forms on Manifolds#^prop-80-5|591 Prop. §80.5]] (alternating forms).
>
> *Model:* none, a statement about smooth maps; *applies:* every smooth map and every pair of charts with $f(U) \subset U'$; exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.6, eq. (3.61) (p. 53)*

^thm-c2-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (components are values on basis vectors). A $(0, s)$ tensor at $p$ is fixed by its values on the coordinate basis vectors, and these are its components: $S = S(\partial_{\mu_1}, \dots, \partial_{\mu_s})\,dx^{\mu_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes dx^{\mu_s}$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]] for $s = 1$, [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]] in general).
>
> **Step 2** (basis one-forms). By [[#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]] and [[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]], part 1,
>
> $$
> (f^{\ast}dy^\alpha)(\partial_\mu) = dy^\alpha\bigl(f_{\ast}\partial_\mu\bigr) = dy^\alpha\Bigl(\frac{\partial y^\beta}{\partial x^\mu}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y^\beta}\Bigr) = \frac{\partial y^\beta}{\partial x^\mu}\,\delta^\alpha_\beta = \frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu} ,
> $$
>
> so $f^{\ast}dy^\alpha = (\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu)\,dx^\mu$ by Step 1. The function $y^\alpha \circ f$ has the differential $d(y^\alpha \circ f) = \partial_\mu(y^\alpha \circ f)\,dx^\mu$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]]), the same. For any $g$: $(f^{\ast}dg)(\partial_\mu) = dg(f_{\ast}\partial_\mu) = (f_{\ast}\partial_\mu)[g] = \partial_\mu(g \circ f) = d(g \circ f)(\partial_\mu)$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (one-forms). With $\omega = \omega_\alpha dy^\alpha$ and the linearity of $\omega$,
>
> $$
> (f^{\ast}\omega)_\mu = (f^{\ast}\omega)(\partial_\mu) = \omega\bigl(f_{\ast}\partial_\mu\bigr) = \omega_\alpha\,dy^\alpha\Bigl(\frac{\partial y^\beta}{\partial x^\mu}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y^\beta}\Bigr) = \frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}\,\omega_\alpha .
> $$
>
> This is part 2.
>
> **Step 4** ($(0, s)$ tensors). Insert $f_{\ast}\partial_{\mu_i} = (\partial y^{\alpha_i}/\partial x^{\mu_i})\,\partial_{\alpha_i}$ into each slot and use multilinearity:
>
> $$
> (f^{\ast}T)_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_s} = T\bigl(f_{\ast}\partial_{\mu_1}, \dots, f_{\ast}\partial_{\mu_s}\bigr) = \frac{\partial y^{\alpha_1}}{\partial x^{\mu_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial y^{\alpha_s}}{\partial x^{\mu_s}}\,T(\partial_{\alpha_1}, \dots, \partial_{\alpha_s}) = \frac{\partial y^{\alpha_1}}{\partial x^{\mu_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial y^{\alpha_s}}{\partial x^{\mu_s}}\,T_{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_s} .
> $$
>
> This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (products and the substitution rule). For a $(0, s)$ tensor $S$ and a $(0, s')$ tensor $S'$ at $f(p)$, the tensor product ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]]) evaluated on pushed-forward vectors factorizes:
>
> $$
> \bigl(f^{\ast}(S \otimes S')\bigr)(V_1, \dots, V_{s + s'}) = S(f_{\ast}V_1, \dots, f_{\ast}V_s)\,S'(f_{\ast}V_{s+1}, \dots, f_{\ast}V_{s + s'}) = \bigl(f^{\ast}S \otimes f^{\ast}S'\bigr)(V_1, \dots, V_{s + s'}) ,
> $$
>
> and $(f^{\ast}(hS))_p = h(f(p))\,(f^{\ast}S)_p$. Hence $f^{\ast}(T_{\alpha\beta}\,dy^\alpha \otimes dy^\beta) = (T_{\alpha\beta} \circ f)\,f^{\ast}dy^\alpha \otimes f^{\ast}dy^\beta$, and part 1 turns each $f^{\ast}dy^\alpha$ into $(\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu)\,dx^\mu$. This is part 4.
>
> **Step 6** (the identity map in two charts). With $f = \mathrm{id}$ and $y = x'$, part 2 gives $\omega_\mu = (\partial x'^\alpha/\partial x^\mu)\,\omega'_\alpha$. Multiplying by $\partial x^\mu/\partial x'^\nu$ and using $(\partial x'^\alpha/\partial x^\mu)(\partial x^\mu/\partial x'^\nu) = \delta^\alpha_\nu$ ([[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]], part 3, for $\mathrm{id} = \mathrm{id} \circ \mathrm{id}$ in the charts $x' \to x \to x'$) gives $\omega'_\nu = (\partial x^\mu/\partial x'^\nu)\,\omega_\mu$. This is part 5.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The pull-back uses the same Jacobian as the push-forward, summed over its other index; no inverse of the Jacobian appears anywhere. That is why it works for every smooth map, also when $m \neq n$ ([[#^thm-c2-4-5|Theorem §C2.4.5]], part 1).
> - The substitution rule (part 4) is how line elements are converted in practice: the familiar recipe "insert the parametrization into $ds^2$" (Couzens, GR1, App. C.3.1, eq. (C.59)) is the pull-back of a $(0, 2)$ tensor.

The push-forward and the pull-back were built from one another. The next statement records how they fit, and what follows from that fit for matrices and for compositions.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.4.3: Push-Forward and Pull-Back Respect the Pairing
> Let $f : M \to N$ be smooth and $p \in M$.
> 1. *Pairing.* For every one-form $\omega$ at $f(p)$ and every vector $V$ at $p$,
>
> $$
> \langle f^{\ast}\omega, V\rangle = \langle\omega, f_{\ast}V\rangle, \qquad\text{in components}\qquad \Bigl(\frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}\,\omega_\alpha\Bigr)V^\mu = \omega_\alpha\Bigl(\frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}\,V^\mu\Bigr) :
> $$
>
> one double sum, grouped in two ways. For a $(0, s)$ tensor, $(f^{\ast}T)(V_1, \dots, V_s) = T(f_{\ast}V_1, \dots, f_{\ast}V_s)$: a full contraction can be carried out on either manifold.
> 2. *⚑ One matrix, two directions.* In coordinate bases the matrix of $f_{\ast p}$ is the Jacobian $J = [\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu]$ ($n \times m$, row $\alpha$) and the matrix of $f^{\ast}_p$ on one-forms is its transpose ($m \times n$, row $\mu$) ([[§12 Duality#^ladr-3-132|LADR Thm. 3.132]]). And $f^{\ast}_p$ is the only linear map $T^{\ast}_{f(p)}N \to T^{\ast}_pM$ with the property of part 1.
> 3. *The order of composition reverses.* For a second smooth map $h : N \to P$, $(h \circ f)^{\ast} = f^{\ast} \circ h^{\ast}$ on $(0, s)$ tensors, the counterpart of the chain rule $(h \circ f)_{\ast} = h_{\ast} \circ f_{\ast}$ ([[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]], part 3). In matrices, $(J_hJ_f)^{\mathsf T} = J_f^{\mathsf T}J_h^{\mathsf T}$.
> 4. *Chart independence of the pairing as a special case.* For $f = \mathrm{id}$ in two charts, part 1 is the statement that $\omega_\mu V^\mu$ has the same value in every chart ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]]).
>
> *Model:* none, a statement about smooth maps; *applies:* every smooth map; exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.6, eq. (3.60) (taken there as the definition) and the remark after (3.61) (the pull-back "induces a map in the opposite direction", p. 53)*

^thm-c2-4-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (pairing). Part 1 is [[#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]] with $s = 1$ and $V_1 = V$, and for general $s$ the definition itself. In components, [[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 2, and [[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]], part 2, give the two groupings of $\sum_{\alpha, \mu}\omega_\alpha\,(\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu)\,V^\mu$, which are equal because a finite double sum can be summed in either order.
>
> **Step 2** (matrices). The $\mu$-th column of the matrix of $f_{\ast p}$ holds the components of $f_{\ast}\partial_\mu = (\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu)\,\partial_\alpha$: entry $(\alpha, \mu)$ is $\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu$. The $\alpha$-th column of the matrix of $f^{\ast}_p$ in the dual bases holds the components of $f^{\ast}dy^\alpha = (\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu)\,dx^\mu$: entry $(\mu, \alpha)$ is $\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu$. The second matrix is the transpose of the first.
>
> **Step 3** (uniqueness). If a linear map $L$ satisfies $\langle L\omega, V\rangle = \langle\omega, f_{\ast}V\rangle$ for all $V$, then $\langle L\omega - f^{\ast}\omega, V\rangle = 0$ for all $V \in T_pM$, and a covector that vanishes on every vector is zero. So $L = f^{\ast}_p$. This completes part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (composition). For vectors $V_i$ at $p$ and a $(0, s)$ tensor $T$ at $h(f(p))$, by [[#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]] three times and the chain rule,
>
> $$
> \bigl((h \circ f)^{\ast}T\bigr)(V_1, \dots) = T\bigl((h \circ f)_{\ast}V_1, \dots\bigr) = T\bigl(h_{\ast}f_{\ast}V_1, \dots\bigr) = (h^{\ast}T)\bigl(f_{\ast}V_1, \dots\bigr) = \bigl(f^{\ast}(h^{\ast}T)\bigr)(V_1, \dots) .
> $$
>
> In matrices, the Jacobian of $h \circ f$ is $J_hJ_f$ (Theorem §C2.4.1, part 3), and the transpose of a product is the product of the transposes in reverse order (checked by script for a pair of maps $\mathbf R^2 \to \mathbf R^3 \to \mathbf R^2$). This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (the identity map). With $f = \mathrm{id}$ in the charts $x$ and $x'$, part 1 reads $\omega_\mu V^\mu = \omega'_\nu V'^\nu$. This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The whole of this section's linear algebra is one matrix: the Jacobian moves vectors forward and, transposed, covectors backward, and the pairing does not notice on which side it is evaluated.

With covectors pulled back, tensors with upper indices only can be pushed forward: feed them pulled-back covectors.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.4.3: Push-Forward of an (r, 0) Tensor at a Point
> Let $f : M \to N$ be smooth, $p \in M$, $r \ge 1$, and let $T$ be a tensor of type $(r, 0)$ at $p$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]), a multilinear map $(T^{\ast}_pM)^r \to \mathbf R$. Its **push-forward** is the $(r, 0)$ tensor at $f(p)$
>
> $$
> (f_{\ast}T)(\omega^1, \dots, \omega^r) \equiv T\bigl(f^{\ast}\omega^1, \dots, f^{\ast}\omega^r\bigr), \qquad \omega^1, \dots, \omega^r \in T^{\ast}_{f(p)}N ,
> $$
>
> with the pull-back of [[#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]] in each slot. For $r = 1$, a vector $V$ read as the $(1, 0)$ tensor $\omega \mapsto \langle\omega, V\rangle$, this is the push-forward of [[#^def-c2-4-1|Def. §C2.4.1]], by [[#^thm-c2-4-3|Theorem §C2.4.3]], part 1. Evaluating on $dy^{\alpha_1}, \dots, dy^{\alpha_r}$ with $f^{\ast}dy^\alpha = (\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu)\,dx^\mu$ ([[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 1) gives the components
>
> $$
> (f_{\ast}T)^{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_r} = \frac{\partial y^{\alpha_1}}{\partial x^{\mu_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial y^{\alpha_r}}{\partial x^{\mu_r}}\;T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r} ,
> $$
>
> and in particular $f_{\ast}(V \otimes W) = f_{\ast}V \otimes f_{\ast}W$. The definition is at a point; a field version needs more of $f$ ([[#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]], part 2; [[#^thm-c2-4-5|Theorem §C2.4.5]], part 2).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.6, after eq. (3.56) ("extended to tensors of type (q, 0) without additional thought", p. 52)*

## Diffeomorphisms carry every tensor

A tensor of mixed type at $p$, say of type $(1, 1)$, has a covector slot and a vector slot. To define its image at $f(p)$, covectors at $f(p)$ must be brought back to $p$, which the pull-back does for any $f$, and vectors at $f(p)$ must also be brought back to $p$, which needs the inverse of $f_{\ast p}$. To carry a *field*, each point of $N$ must moreover come from exactly one point of $M$. Both hold when $f$ is a diffeomorphism ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-6|Def. §C2.1.6]]): its differential at every point is invertible, with inverse $(f^{-1})_{\ast}$ ([[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^cor-28-7|591 Cor. §28.7]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.4.4: A Diffeomorphism Transports Tensors of Every Type
> Let $\phi : M \to N$ be a diffeomorphism ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-6|Def. §C2.1.6]]); then $\dim M = \dim N$ and $\phi_{\ast p}$ is invertible at every $p$, with inverse $(\phi^{-1})_{\ast\phi(p)}$.
> 1. *Transport at a point.* For a tensor $T$ of type $(r, s)$ at $p$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]),
>
> $$
> (\phi_{\ast}T)\bigl(\omega^1, \dots, \omega^r, W_1, \dots, W_s\bigr) \equiv T\bigl(\phi^{\ast}\omega^1, \dots, \phi^{\ast}\omega^r, (\phi^{-1})_{\ast}W_1, \dots, (\phi^{-1})_{\ast}W_s\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> for $\omega^i \in T^{\ast}_{\phi(p)}N$ and $W_j \in T_{\phi(p)}N$, defines a tensor of type $(r, s)$ at $\phi(p)$. On $(r, 0)$ tensors it is the push-forward of [[#^def-c2-4-3|Def. §C2.4.3]]; on $(0, s)$ tensors it is the pull-back by the inverse, $\phi_{\ast} = (\phi^{-1})^{\ast}$. The pull-back by $\phi$ is extended to all types by $\phi^{\ast} \equiv (\phi^{-1})_{\ast}$.
> 2. *Transport of fields.* For a tensor field $T$ on $M$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]), $(\phi_{\ast}T)_q \equiv \phi_{\ast}\bigl(T_{\phi^{-1}(q)}\bigr)$, $q \in N$, is a tensor field of the same type on $N$, smooth when $T$ is. For vector fields this is [[§59 Related Vector Fields#^def-59-1|591 Def. §59.1]], [[§59 Related Vector Fields#^prop-59-2|591 Prop. §59.2]].
> 3. *Components.* In charts $x^\mu$ at $p$ and $y^\alpha$ at $\phi(p)$,
>
> $$
> (\phi_{\ast}T)^{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_r}{}_{\beta_1\cdots\beta_s}\bigl(\phi(p)\bigr) = \frac{\partial y^{\alpha_1}}{\partial x^{\mu_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial y^{\alpha_r}}{\partial x^{\mu_r}}\;\frac{\partial x^{\nu_1}}{\partial y^{\beta_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial x^{\nu_s}}{\partial y^{\beta_s}}\;T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_s}(p) ,
> $$
>
> where $\partial x^\nu/\partial y^\beta$, the Jacobian of $\phi^{-1}$ at $\phi(p)$, is the inverse matrix of $\partial y^\beta/\partial x^\nu$ at $p$.
> 4. *Algebra.* $\phi_{\ast}$ is linear, $\phi_{\ast}(T \otimes S) = \phi_{\ast}T \otimes \phi_{\ast}S$, it commutes with every contraction ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-4|Def. §C2.3.4]]), $(\psi \circ \phi)_{\ast} = \psi_{\ast} \circ \phi_{\ast}$ for a second diffeomorphism $\psi$, and $(\phi^{-1})_{\ast} = (\phi_{\ast})^{-1}$. Hence an equation between tensor fields on $M$ built from sums, tensor products and contractions holds for their images on $N$.
> 5. *⚑ Active equals passive.* The components of $\phi_{\ast}T$ in the chart $y$ at $\phi(p)$ equal the components of $T$ at $p$ in the chart $x' \equiv y \circ \phi$ of $M$. Moving the tensor with the chart held fixed, and keeping the tensor while relabelling the points of $M$ by $x' = y \circ \phi$, produce the same numbers.
>
> *Model:* none, a statement about smooth maps; *applies:* every diffeomorphism, and every diffeomorphism between open subsets on its domain; exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.6, p. 53 (with a diffeomorphism one "can transport any object from M to N and back"; asserted) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.2, eqs. (6.3)–(6.5) (pp. 87–88; the transformation law read actively, as a "point transformation" that drags a tensor) · Couzens, GR1, §5.1.4, after eq. (5.28) (p. 108; the active and the passive view "lead to the same conclusion")*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the definition makes sense). $\phi^{\ast}\omega^i \in T^{\ast}_pM$ by [[#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]], and $(\phi^{-1})_{\ast}W_j \in T_pM$ by [[#^def-c2-4-1|Def. §C2.4.1]] applied to $\phi^{-1} : N \to M$ at $\phi(p)$, since $\phi^{-1}(\phi(p)) = p$. Both maps are linear and $T$ is multilinear, so $\phi_{\ast}T$ is multilinear: a tensor of type $(r, s)$ at $\phi(p)$. For $s = 0$ the formula is Def. §C2.4.3. For $r = 0$ it reads $(\phi_{\ast}T)(W_1, \dots) = T((\phi^{-1})_{\ast}W_1, \dots) = ((\phi^{-1})^{\ast}T)(W_1, \dots)$, Def. §C2.4.2 for the map $\phi^{-1}$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (the inverse Jacobian). The chain rule ([[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]], part 3) for $\phi^{-1} \circ \phi = \mathrm{id}_M$ and $\phi \circ \phi^{-1} = \mathrm{id}_N$ gives
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial y^\beta}\bigl(\phi(p)\bigr)\,\frac{\partial y^\beta}{\partial x^\mu}(p) = \delta^\nu_\mu, \qquad \frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\nu}(p)\,\frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial y^\beta}\bigl(\phi(p)\bigr) = \delta^\alpha_\beta .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (components). The components of a tensor are its values on the dual-basis covectors and the basis vectors ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]]). By [[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 1, $\phi^{\ast}dy^\alpha = (\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu)\,dx^\mu$, and by [[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]], part 1, for $\phi^{-1}$, $(\phi^{-1})_{\ast}\,\partial/\partial y^\beta = (\partial x^\nu/\partial y^\beta)\,\partial/\partial x^\nu$. For a $(1, 1)$ tensor, by multilinearity,
>
> $$
> (\phi_{\ast}T)^\alpha{}_\beta = (\phi_{\ast}T)\Bigl(dy^\alpha, \frac{\partial}{\partial y^\beta}\Bigr) = T\Bigl(\frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}\,dx^\mu,\ \frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial y^\beta}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\nu}\Bigr) = \frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}\,\frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial y^\beta}\,T^\mu{}_\nu ,
> $$
>
> and each further slot contributes one more factor of the same kind. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 4** (fields). Every $q \in N$ has exactly one preimage $\phi^{-1}(q)$, so $(\phi_{\ast}T)_q$ is defined at every point of $N$ and only once. Its components at $q$ (part 3) are products of $\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu$ evaluated at $\phi^{-1}(q)$, of $\partial x^\nu/\partial y^\beta$ at $q$ and of the components of $T$ at $\phi^{-1}(q)$: smooth functions of $q$ when $T$ is smooth, because $\phi^{-1}$ is smooth. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (algebra). Linearity: $T \mapsto T(\phi^{\ast}\omega, \dots, (\phi^{-1})_{\ast}W, \dots)$ is linear in $T$. Tensor products: evaluated on its arguments, $\phi_{\ast}(T \otimes S)$ gives $T(\phi^{\ast}\cdots)\,S(\phi^{\ast}\cdots)$, the value of $\phi_{\ast}T \otimes \phi_{\ast}S$. Contraction: in part 3, contract an upper index $\alpha$ with a lower index $\beta = \alpha$; Step 2 gives
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}\,\frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial y^\alpha} = \delta^\nu_\mu ,
> $$
>
> which contracts $\mu$ with $\nu$ in $T$: the image of the contraction. Composition: for $\omega$ and $W$ at $\psi(\phi(p))$, with the reversed order of [[#^thm-c2-4-3|Theorem §C2.4.3]], part 3, and the chain rule for $(\psi \circ \phi)^{-1} = \phi^{-1} \circ \psi^{-1}$,
>
> $$
> \bigl((\psi \circ \phi)_{\ast}T\bigr)(\omega, W) = T\bigl(\phi^{\ast}\psi^{\ast}\omega,\ (\phi^{-1})_{\ast}(\psi^{-1})_{\ast}W\bigr) = (\phi_{\ast}T)\bigl(\psi^{\ast}\omega,\ (\psi^{-1})_{\ast}W\bigr) = \bigl(\psi_{\ast}(\phi_{\ast}T)\bigr)(\omega, W) ,
> $$
>
> shown for one slot of each kind; more slots repeat it. With $\psi = \phi^{-1}$, $(\phi^{-1})_{\ast}\phi_{\ast} = (\mathrm{id})_{\ast} = \mathrm{id}$. An equation $E = 0$ built from sums, products and contractions is mapped to $\phi_{\ast}E = 0$, the same equation for the images. This is part 4.
>
> **Step 6** (active equals passive). On $\phi^{-1}(U')$, $x' = y \circ \phi$ is a chart of $M$: a homeomorphism onto the open set $y(U')$, smoothly compatible with the atlas because $\phi$ and $\phi^{-1}$ are smooth. The coordinate representation of $\phi$ from the chart $x'$ to the chart $y$ is
>
> $$
> y \circ \phi \circ (x')^{-1} = y \circ \phi \circ \phi^{-1} \circ y^{-1} = \mathrm{id}_{y(U')}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{\partial y^\alpha}{\partial x'^\mu} = \delta^\alpha_\mu, \quad \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial y^\beta} = \delta^\nu_\beta .
> $$
>
> Part 3 with the chart $x'$ at $p$ therefore gives $(\phi_{\ast}T)^{\alpha\cdots}{}_{\beta\cdots}(\phi(p)) = T'^{\alpha\cdots}{}_{\beta\cdots}(p)$, the components of $T$ in the chart $x'$. This is part 5. The script checks it for a $(1, 1)$ field under $\phi(x, y) = (x + y^2, y)$, computing the passive side independently by the transformation law of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Invertibility is used twice, for different reasons: $\phi_{\ast p}^{-1}$ to bring vectors back into the lower slots (Steps 1, 3), and $\phi^{-1}$ as a map to make the field single-valued and defined everywhere on $N$ (Step 4).
> - The component formula of part 3 has the shape of the transformation law of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], one Jacobian per upper index and one inverse Jacobian per lower index. Part 5 says why: a change of chart and a diffeomorphism are the same computation read in two ways.

> [!remark] Remark: Active and passive readings of a diffeomorphism
> - *Passive.* The tensor and the point stay; the chart changes. The numbers change by the Jacobian of the transition function ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]]). Level B read Lorentz transformations this way: one event, two inertial frames ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). Schutz calls it the passive transformation: "the coordinates change, but the vector does not".
> - *Active.* The chart stays; the tensor is moved to another point by $\phi$, and its components there are given by [[#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]], part 3. d'Inverno & Vickers read the coordinate change $\tilde x^a = x^a + \delta u\,X^a(x)$ this way, as a "point transformation" that drags a tensor along a vector field; that is the route to the Lie derivative ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]]).
> - *Why both readings matter.* Part 5 of the theorem says they give the same numbers. A statement about components in arbitrary charts is therefore also a statement about all fields moved together by a diffeomorphism.
> - *Forward to physics.* In special relativity the metric is fixed. An active Lorentz transformation preserves it, $\phi^{\ast}\eta = \eta$, so moving the matter fields alone produces another physically possible configuration: a symmetry. In general relativity the metric is a field like the others. Applying one diffeomorphism to the metric and to every matter field together produces a configuration that no measurement can tell from the first; Couzens (GR1, §5.1.4) compares this with a gauge transformation and counts the redundancy as 4 of the 10 components of $g_{\mu\nu}$ (§C5.4, planned). Moving the matter alone while keeping the metric is in general not a symmetry.
>
> *Source: Schutz, §2.1, footnote 1 (p. 34) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.2, eq. (6.3) (p. 87) · Couzens, GR1, §5.1.4, eqs. (5.28)–(5.30) (pp. 108–109)*

## Beyond diffeomorphisms: worldlines and embedded surfaces

A worldline maps a one-dimensional interval into four-dimensional spacetime, and an embedded sphere maps a two-dimensional manifold into $\mathbf R^3$. Neither is a diffeomorphism, yet both are where pull-backs and push-forwards are used most. What survives without invertibility is the content of the next statement; it is the setting of all four examples.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.4.5: What Survives along Maps That Are Not Diffeomorphisms
> Let $f : M \to N$ be a smooth map, with no further condition.
> 1. *Covariant tensor fields pull back.* For every $(0, s)$ tensor field $T$ on $N$, $f^{\ast}T$ ([[#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]]) is a $(0, s)$ tensor field on $M$, smooth when $T$ is.
> 2. *Vector fields need not push forward.* Each vector pushes forward ([[#^def-c2-4-1|Def. §C2.4.1]]), so a vector field $V$ on $M$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-4|Def. §C2.2.4]]) gives the assignment $p \mapsto f_{\ast}V_p \in T_{f(p)}N$, a *vector field along $f$*: the velocity of a curve is the standard case. It is a vector field on $N$ only if every point of $N$ is the image of some point and points with a common image give equal vectors. For the projection $\pi(x, y) = x$ and $V = (1 + y^2)\,\partial_x$ on $\mathbf R^2$,
>
> $$
> \pi_{\ast}V_{(x_0, 0)} = \frac{d}{dx}\Big|_{x_0}, \qquad \pi_{\ast}V_{(x_0, 1)} = 2\,\frac{d}{dx}\Big|_{x_0} ,
> $$
>
> two different vectors at one point of $\mathbf R$.
> 3. *Pull-back of a positive-definite tensor.* Let $g$ be a symmetric $(0, 2)$ tensor field on $N$, positive definite at $f(p)$ (a Riemannian metric, [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor|§C3.1]]). Then $f^{\ast}g$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite at $p$, and positive definite exactly when $f_{\ast p}$ is injective, i.e. when $f$ is an immersion at $p$ ([[§36 Submersions#^def-36-2|591 Def. §36.2]]). For the polar map $(r, \theta) \mapsto (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$, $f^{\ast}\delta = dr^2 + r^2d\theta^2$, degenerate at $r = 0$, where $f_{\ast}\partial_\theta = 0$.
> 4. *Pull-back of the Minkowski metric.* For the indefinite $\eta$, $f^{\ast}\eta$ can have any sign. Along a worldline $\gamma(\lambda)$ with velocity $u = \gamma_{\ast}(d/d\lambda)$, $\gamma^{\ast}\eta = \eta(u, u)\,d\lambda^2$: negative where $u$ is timelike, zero along a null curve (e.g. $\lambda \mapsto (\lambda, \lambda, 0, 0)$). On the slice $t = t_0$, the inclusion $(x, y, z) \mapsto (t_0, x, y, z)$ pulls $\eta$ back to $dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$.
>
> *Model:* none, a statement about smooth maps; *applies:* every smooth map; exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.6, Aside after Fig. 7 (p. 52: the push-forward of vectors does not need $f$ invertible; invertibility is assumed "from now on") · §3.4.4, before eq. (3.145) (p. 72: submanifolds as images of maps $\sigma$ with $\sigma_{\ast}$ injective) · §4.1.1, Exercise 4.1 (p. 76)*

^thm-c2-4-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (pull-back of fields). At each $p$, $f(p)$ is a point of $N$ and $T_{f(p)}$ is defined, so $(f^{\ast}T)_p$ is defined at every $p$ by [[#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]]. By [[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 3, its components are sums of products of $\partial y^\alpha/\partial x^\mu$, smooth because $f$ is, and of $T_{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_s} \circ f$, smooth as composites of smooth maps. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (the projection). The Jacobian of $\pi(x, y) = x$ is the $1 \times 2$ matrix $(\partial\pi/\partial x, \partial\pi/\partial y) = (1, 0)$. By [[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]], part 2, the vector $V_{(x_0, y)} = (1 + y^2)\,\partial_x$, with components $(1 + y^2, 0)$, goes to
>
> $$
> \pi_{\ast}V_{(x_0, y)} = \bigl(1\cdot(1 + y^2) + 0\cdot 0\bigr)\frac{d}{dx}\Big|_{x_0} = (1 + y^2)\,\frac{d}{dx}\Big|_{x_0} ,
> $$
>
> which is $d/dx$ for $y = 0$ and $2\,d/dx$ for $y = 1$. This is part 2. (For a map that is not onto, such as $\mathbf R \to \mathbf R^2$, $x \mapsto (x, 0)$, points off the image receive no vector at all.)
>
> **Step 3** (positive-definite tensors). Symmetry: $(f^{\ast}g)(V, W) = g(f_{\ast}V, f_{\ast}W) = g(f_{\ast}W, f_{\ast}V) = (f^{\ast}g)(W, V)$. Semidefiniteness: $(f^{\ast}g)(V, V) = g(f_{\ast}V, f_{\ast}V) \ge 0$, with equality exactly when $f_{\ast}V = 0$, since $g$ is positive definite at $f(p)$. So $(f^{\ast}g)(V, V) > 0$ for every $V \neq 0$ if and only if $f_{\ast}V \neq 0$ for every $V \neq 0$, i.e. $f_{\ast p}$ is injective. For the polar map, the substitution rule ([[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 4) with $dx = \cos\theta\,dr - r\sin\theta\,d\theta$, $dy = \sin\theta\,dr + r\cos\theta\,d\theta$ gives
>
> $$
> dx^2 + dy^2 = (\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta)\,dr^2 + 2(-r\sin\theta\cos\theta + r\sin\theta\cos\theta)\,dr\,d\theta + r^2(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta)\,d\theta^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\theta^2 ,
> $$
>
> and at $r = 0$ the column $f_{\ast}\partial_\theta = (-r\sin\theta, r\cos\theta)$ vanishes, so the rank of the Jacobian drops to $1$. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 4** (Minkowski). For a worldline, $\gamma^{\ast}\eta = \eta_{\mu\nu}\,(dx^\mu/d\lambda)(dx^\nu/d\lambda)\,d\lambda^2 = \eta(u, u)\,d\lambda^2$ by [[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 3, with $m = 1$. For $\lambda \mapsto (\lambda, \lambda, 0, 0)$, $u = (1, 1, 0, 0)$ and $\eta(u, u) = -1 + 1 = 0$. For the slice inclusion, $dt$ pulls back to $d(t_0) = 0$ and $dx^i$ to $dx^i$, so $-dt^2 + \delta_{ij}dx^idx^j$ becomes $\delta_{ij}dx^idx^j$. This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Pull-backs of covariant fields need nothing; push-forwards of fields need the map to be a bijection. A metric pulled back by an immersion is again a metric in the Riemannian case, while the Lorentzian case distinguishes timelike, null and spacelike curves and surfaces: the pulled-back metric sees the causal character of the submanifold.

^der-c2-4-5

*Uses:* [[#^def-c2-4-1|Def. §C2.4.1]], [[#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]], [[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]], [[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], [[§36 Submersions#^def-36-2|591 Def. §36.2]]

The tools are now in place to move tensors between points. A vector field $X$ on $M$ generates a family of diffeomorphisms of $M$, its flow, which slides every point along the integral curves of $X$ ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^def-c2-5-2|Def. §C2.5.2]]). [[#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]] lets the flow bring a tensor from a nearby point back to $p$, where it can be subtracted from the tensor already there. The derivative that results is the Lie derivative, the subject of [[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors|§C2.5]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C2.4.1: The Velocity of a Worldline as a Push-Forward
> *Setup.* A worldline is a smooth map $\gamma : I \to M$ from an open interval $I \subset \mathbf R$ into spacetime. The interval is a one-dimensional manifold with the global chart $\lambda$ and the basis vector $d/d\lambda$ at each point. In a chart $x^\mu$ of $M$, $\gamma$ is $\lambda \mapsto x^\mu(\lambda)$.
>
> *Velocity.* [[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]] with $m = 1$: the Jacobian is the single column $dx^\mu/d\lambda$, and
>
> $$
> \gamma_{\ast}\Bigl(\frac{d}{d\lambda}\Big|_\lambda\Bigr) = \frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}(\lambda)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\Big|_{\gamma(\lambda)} .
> $$
>
> The velocity of the curve ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]) is the push-forward of the unit vector of the parameter line. It is a vector field along $\gamma$, not a field on $M$ ([[#^thm-c2-4-5|Theorem §C2.4.5]], part 2).
>
> *A circular orbit, in two charts.* In Minkowski spacetime with Cartesian coordinates $(t, x, y, z)$, let $x^\mu(\lambda) = (\lambda, R\cos\omega\lambda, R\sin\omega\lambda, 0)$ with $0 < R\omega < 1$. Then
>
> $$
> \gamma_{\ast}\frac{d}{d\lambda} = \partial_t - R\omega\sin\omega\lambda\;\partial_x + R\omega\cos\omega\lambda\;\partial_y .
> $$
>
> In cylindrical coordinates $(t, \rho, \varphi, z)$, $x = \rho\cos\varphi$, $y = \rho\sin\varphi$, the same worldline is $(\lambda, R, \omega\lambda, 0)$, and $\gamma_{\ast}(d/d\lambda) = \partial_t + \omega\,\partial_\varphi$. The two are one vector, related by the transformation law ([[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]], part 4). With $\partial\rho/\partial x = x/\rho = \cos\omega\lambda$, $\partial\rho/\partial y = \sin\omega\lambda$, $\partial\varphi/\partial x = -y/\rho^2 = -\sin\omega\lambda/R$, $\partial\varphi/\partial y = x/\rho^2 = \cos\omega\lambda/R$ on the orbit,
>
> $$
> u^\rho = \cos\omega\lambda\,(-R\omega\sin\omega\lambda) + \sin\omega\lambda\,(R\omega\cos\omega\lambda) = 0, \qquad u^\varphi = -\frac{\sin\omega\lambda}{R}\,(-R\omega\sin\omega\lambda) + \frac{\cos\omega\lambda}{R}\,(R\omega\cos\omega\lambda) = \omega .
> $$
>
> *Reparametrization.* For $\lambda = h(\sigma)$ with $h' > 0$, the chain rule ([[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]], part 3) gives $(\gamma \circ h)_{\ast}(d/d\sigma) = \gamma_{\ast}\bigl(h_{\ast}(d/d\sigma)\bigr) = h'(\sigma)\,\gamma_{\ast}(d/d\lambda)$: the velocity is rescaled, its direction kept.
>
> *Proper time is a pull-back.* The metric pulls back to the interval ([[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 4). In the Cartesian chart, with $\eta = -dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$,
>
> $$
> \gamma^{\ast}\eta = \bigl(-1 + R^2\omega^2\sin^2\omega\lambda + R^2\omega^2\cos^2\omega\lambda\bigr)\,d\lambda^2 = -(1 - R^2\omega^2)\,d\lambda^2 .
> $$
>
> In the cylindrical chart $\eta = -dt^2 + d\rho^2 + \rho^2d\varphi^2 + dz^2$ (itself the pull-back of the Cartesian form by $(t, \rho, \varphi, z) \mapsto (t, \rho\cos\varphi, \rho\sin\varphi, z)$), and $\gamma^{\ast}\eta = (-1 + 0 + R^2\omega^2 + 0)\,d\lambda^2$, the same. The proper time of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]] is the integral of $\sqrt{-\gamma^{\ast}g}$ over the parameter line: here $\tau = \sqrt{1 - R^2\omega^2}\;\Delta\lambda$, the time dilation of a clock moving at speed $v = R\omega$ ([[§A2.1 Time Dilation#^thm-a2-1-2|REL Theorem §A2.1.2]]). The negative sign of $\gamma^{\ast}\eta$ is [[#^thm-c2-4-5|Theorem §C2.4.5]], part 4, for a timelike curve.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.6, Fig. 7 (p. 52; the map carries curves and their tangent vectors) · computations written here, checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.4.2: Pulling Back the Euclidean Metric to the Sphere
> *Setup.* The unit sphere $S^2 \subset \mathbf R^3$, $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$, with the inclusion $\iota : S^2 \to \mathbf R^3$. In the chart $(\theta, \varphi) \in (0, \pi) \times (0, 2\pi)$ of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-3|Example §C2.1.3]], which misses one half great circle from pole to pole,
>
> $$
> \iota(\theta, \varphi) = \bigl(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ \cos\theta\bigr)
> $$
>
> (Couzens, GR1, eq. (4.11)). The Euclidean metric of $\mathbf R^3$ is the $(0, 2)$ tensor field $\delta = dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$, where $dx^2$ means $dx \otimes dx$.
>
> *Substitute* ([[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], parts 1 and 4). The differentials of the three component functions are
>
> $$
> \iota^{\ast}dx = \cos\theta\cos\varphi\,d\theta - \sin\theta\sin\varphi\,d\varphi, \qquad \iota^{\ast}dy = \cos\theta\sin\varphi\,d\theta + \sin\theta\cos\varphi\,d\varphi, \qquad \iota^{\ast}dz = -\sin\theta\,d\theta .
> $$
>
> *Expand each square*, writing $2\,d\theta\,d\varphi$ for $d\theta \otimes d\varphi + d\varphi \otimes d\theta$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> (\iota^{\ast}dx)^2 &= \cos^2\theta\cos^2\varphi\,d\theta^2 - 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,d\theta\,d\varphi + \sin^2\theta\sin^2\varphi\,d\varphi^2, \\
> (\iota^{\ast}dy)^2 &= \cos^2\theta\sin^2\varphi\,d\theta^2 + 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,d\theta\,d\varphi + \sin^2\theta\cos^2\varphi\,d\varphi^2, \\
> (\iota^{\ast}dz)^2 &= \sin^2\theta\,d\theta^2 .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> *Collect.* The $d\theta\,d\varphi$ terms cancel; $d\theta^2$ has the coefficient $\cos^2\theta(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi) + \sin^2\theta = 1$; $d\varphi^2$ has $\sin^2\theta(\sin^2\varphi + \cos^2\varphi) = \sin^2\theta$. So
>
> $$
> \iota^{\ast}\delta = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2, \qquad\text{and for the sphere of radius } a:\qquad a^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the round metric of Couzens' eq. (4.9). Its home as a metric is [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor|§C3.1]].
>
> *Where it degenerates, and why that is the chart.* At $\theta \to 0, \pi$ the coefficient $\sin^2\theta$ tends to $0$: the column $\iota_{\ast}\partial_\varphi = (-\sin\theta\sin\varphi, \sin\theta\cos\varphi, 0)$ vanishes there, so the chart $(\theta, \varphi)$ fails at the poles ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^rem-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Remark: Coordinate singularities are failures of a chart, not of the manifold]]). The inclusion itself is an immersion everywhere. In the stereographic chart from the north pole ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]]), $\iota(u, v) = (2u, 2v, u^2 + v^2 - 1)/(1 + u^2 + v^2)$, and the same substitution gives
>
> $$
> \iota^{\ast}\delta = \frac{4\,(du^2 + dv^2)}{(1 + u^2 + v^2)^2} ,
> $$
>
> positive definite on the whole chart, south pole $u = v = 0$ included. By [[#^thm-c2-4-5|Theorem §C2.4.5]], part 3, $\iota^{\ast}\delta$ is a Riemannian metric on all of $S^2$: the immersion condition is the manifold version of the regular parametrized surfaces of 452 ([[§31 Surface Integrals#^def-31-3|452 Def. §31.3]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §4.1.1, Example 4.1, eqs. (4.9)–(4.11) (p. 75); Exercise 4.1 (p. 76), solution App. C.3.1, eqs. (C.58)–(C.59) (p. 160) · stereographic form computed here, checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.4.3: The Charge Coupling as a Pull-Back along a Worldline
> *Setup.* A charge $q$ on a worldline $\gamma : I \to M$ ([[#^ex-c2-4-1|Example §C2.4.1]]) in a prescribed electromagnetic potential, a one-form $A = A_\mu\,dx^\mu$ on spacetime. At level C the shared upper components are $A^\mu = (V, \vec A)$, so $A_\mu = \eta_{\mu\nu}A^\nu = (-V, \vec A)$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]], parts 2–3).
>
> *The line integral is a pull-back.* $\gamma^{\ast}A$ is a one-form on the interval ([[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 2, with $m = 1$):
>
> $$
> \gamma^{\ast}A = A_\mu\bigl(x(\lambda)\bigr)\,\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}\,d\lambda, \qquad \int_\gamma A \equiv \int_I\gamma^{\ast}A = \int A_\mu\bigl(x(\lambda)\bigr)\,\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}\,d\lambda ,
> $$
>
> Couzens' eqs. (3.146)–(3.147); the general rule for $k$-forms over $k$-dimensional submanifolds is [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-3|Def. §C2.7.3]]. For $\lambda = h(\sigma)$ with $h' > 0$, $(\gamma \circ h)^{\ast}A = h^{\ast}(\gamma^{\ast}A) = A_\mu\,(dx^\mu/d\lambda)\big|_{h(\sigma)}\,h'(\sigma)\,d\sigma$ ([[#^thm-c2-4-3|Theorem §C2.4.3]], part 3), and the substitution $\lambda = h(\sigma)$ turns its integral into the one above: the value does not depend on the parametrization (with $h' < 0$ it changes sign). No metric enters.
>
> *The action.* At level C the charge has $S = -m\int d\tau + q\int_\gamma A$. With $\lambda = t$ and $\vec u = d\vec x/dt$,
>
> $$
> A_\mu\,\frac{dx^\mu}{dt} = -V + \vec u\cdot\vec A, \qquad q\int_\gamma A = \int\bigl(-qV + q\,\vec u\cdot\vec A\bigr)\,dt ,
> $$
>
> the interaction Lagrangian of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]. Level B (signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, $c = 1$) writes the same term as $-q\int A_\mu\,dx^\mu$ with $A^{\rm B}_\mu = (V, -\vec A)$ ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^pr-b3-2-1|Principle §B3.2.1]]). The value is the same, because $A_\mu$ contains one metric factor and flips sign between the levels (Theorem §C1.2.2, part 3). That this coupling needs no metric is the point of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^rem-b3-2-1|§B3.2, Remark: Why this coupling]].
>
> *(a) A charge at rest in a static potential.* $\gamma(\lambda) = (\lambda, \vec x_0)$, $dx^\mu/d\lambda = (1, 0, 0, 0)$, so $\gamma^{\ast}A = A_0\,d\lambda = -V(\vec x_0)\,d\lambda$ and $q\int_\gamma A = -q\int V\,dt$: the potential energy enters with the sign of $L = T - U$.
>
> *(b) A circular orbit in a uniform magnetic field.* Take $\vec B = B\hat z$, $\vec A = \tfrac12 B(-y, x, 0)$, $V = 0$, and the orbit of Example §C2.4.1, $x = R\cos\omega\lambda$, $y = R\sin\omega\lambda$. Then
>
> $$
> A_\mu\,\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda} = \tfrac12 B(-R\sin\omega\lambda)(-R\omega\sin\omega\lambda) + \tfrac12 B(R\cos\omega\lambda)(R\omega\cos\omega\lambda) = \tfrac12 BR^2\omega ,
> $$
>
> and over one revolution, $\lambda \in [0, 2\pi/\omega]$,
>
> $$
> \oint_\gamma A = \tfrac12 BR^2\omega\cdot\frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \pi R^2B ,
> $$
>
> the magnetic flux through the orbit. The script repeats the integral in the parametrization $\lambda = (2\pi/\omega)\sigma^2$, $\sigma \in [0, 1]$, with the same result. A line integral of $A$ equal to a surface integral of $\vec B$ is Stokes' theorem ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]]) applied to $F = dA$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-3|Example §C2.6.3]]).
>
> *(c) Gauge invariance.* Under $A \to A + d\chi$, [[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 1, gives $\gamma^{\ast}d\chi = d(\chi \circ \gamma)$, so $\int_\gamma d\chi = \chi(\gamma(\lambda_B)) - \chi(\gamma(\lambda_A))$, an end-point term that does not change the equations of motion: [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]] in the language of pull-backs.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.4, eqs. (3.146)–(3.147) (p. 72; "precisely the way in which a worldline of a particle couples to the electromagnetic field") · cases (a)–(c) computed here, checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.4.4: The Push-Forward to the Embedded Hemisphere
> *Setup* (Couzens, GR1, Example 3.6). $M$ the open unit disk with coordinates $(x^1, x^2)$, $r^2 = (x^1)^2 + (x^2)^2 < 1$; $N = \mathbf R^3$ with $(y^1, y^2, y^3)$; $f(x^1, x^2) = (x^1, x^2, \sqrt{1 - r^2})$, onto the open upper hemisphere. At an image point $y^1 = x^1$, $y^2 = x^2$, $y^3 = \sqrt{1 - r^2}$.
>
> *Jacobian.* The $3 \times 2$ matrix has rows $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ and
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial y^3}{\partial x^\mu} = \frac{-x^\mu}{\sqrt{1 - r^2}} = -\frac{y^\mu}{y^3} \qquad (\mu = 1, 2) .
> $$
>
> *Push-forward* ([[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]], part 2). For $V = a\,\partial_1 + b\,\partial_2$,
>
> $$
> f_{\ast}V = a\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y^1} + b\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y^2} - \Bigl(a\,\frac{y^1}{y^3} + b\,\frac{y^2}{y^3}\Bigr)\frac{\partial}{\partial y^3} ,
> $$
>
> Couzens' eq. (3.58). It is tangent to the sphere, as the opening's copy $(a, b, 0)$ was not: with $F = (y^1)^2 + (y^2)^2 + (y^3)^2$,
>
> $$
> (f_{\ast}V)[F] = 2y^1a + 2y^2b + 2y^3\Bigl(-\frac{ay^1 + by^2}{y^3}\Bigr) = 0 .
> $$
>
> At $p = (\tfrac35, 0)$, $f(p) = (\tfrac35, 0, \tfrac45)$ and $V = \partial_1$: $f_{\ast}V = \partial/\partial y^1 - \tfrac34\,\partial/\partial y^3$.
>
> *Pull-backs and the pairing.* By [[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 1, the height one-form pulls back to $f^{\ast}dy^3 = -(x^1dx^1 + x^2dx^2)/\sqrt{1 - r^2}$, and
>
> $$
> \langle f^{\ast}dy^3, V\rangle = -\frac{ax^1 + bx^2}{\sqrt{1 - r^2}} = \langle dy^3, f_{\ast}V\rangle ,
> $$
>
> the third component above ([[#^thm-c2-4-3|Theorem §C2.4.3]], part 1). The Euclidean metric pulls back by substitution (part 4 of Theorem §C2.4.2):
>
> $$
> f^{\ast}\delta = (dx^1)^2 + (dx^2)^2 + \frac{(x^1dx^1 + x^2dx^2)^2}{1 - r^2}, \qquad (f^{\ast}\delta)_{\mu\nu} = \delta_{\mu\nu} + \frac{x^\mu x^\nu}{1 - r^2} .
> $$
>
> At $p$: $(f^{\ast}\delta)_{11} = 1 + 0.36/0.64 = 25/16$, $(f^{\ast}\delta)_{12} = 0$, $(f^{\ast}\delta)_{22} = 1$. A small step $dx^1$ at $p$ covers the length $\tfrac54\,dx^1$ on the sphere: projecting the hemisphere onto the disk compresses it toward the rim. The determinant is $1/(1 - r^2)$, which grows without bound as $r \to 1$, as does $\partial y^3/\partial x^\mu$: this chart cannot be extended to the equator, a failure of the chart, not of the sphere (compare [[#^ex-c2-4-2|Example §C2.4.2]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.6, Example 3.6, eqs. (3.57)–(3.58) (p. 52) · tangency, pairing and the pulled-back metric computed here, checked by script*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - **Layer change from level B.** A Lorentz transformation $x \mapsto \Lambda x$ is a diffeomorphism of Minkowski spacetime with the constant Jacobian $\Lambda$. Level B's tensor law ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) is [[#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]], part 3, for that map, read passively, and the defining property $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]) is $\phi^{\ast}\eta = \eta$ in the substitution form of [[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 4 (checked by script for a boost). Maps with $\phi^{\ast}g = g$ are the isometries of [[§C3.11 Isometries and Killing Vectors|§C3.11]].
> - **Changes of chart.** The transformation laws of [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]] and [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]] are push-forward and pull-back by the identity map in two charts ([[#^thm-c2-4-1|Theorem §C2.4.1]], part 4; [[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 5), and every diffeomorphism can be read as a change of chart ([[#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]], part 5).
> - **Next uses.** The Lie derivative pulls tensors back along a flow ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]]). Pull-back commutes with the exterior derivative ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]]); [[#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], part 1, is its degree-zero case. Integrals over curves and surfaces are integrals of pulled-back forms ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-3|Def. §C2.7.3]]). The metric of a surface or a spatial slice is the pulled-back spacetime metric ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor|§C3.1]]), and a diffeomorphism applied to every field is the gauge freedom of general relativity (§C5.4, planned).
