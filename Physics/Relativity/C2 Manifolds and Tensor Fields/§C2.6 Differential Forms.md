In [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields|§C2.3]] the partial derivatives of a vector field failed to form a tensor: under a change of chart they pick up a term containing second derivatives of the coordinate change ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]]). [[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors|§C2.5]] found one way past the failure: differentiate along a vector field, dragging the field back with its flow ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]]). This section finds a second way, which needs no vector field at all: antisymmetrize. The objects for which this works are the totally antisymmetric covariant tensors, the differential forms. They are the language in which the homogeneous Maxwell equations hold unchanged in curved spacetime, and they are the objects that can be integrated over a manifold ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem|§C2.7]]).

First, the failure and its cure, computed. Take the one-form $A = dx$ on the plane. In Cartesian coordinates its components $A_x = 1$, $A_y = 0$ are constant, so every $\partial_\mu A_\nu$ is $0$. In polar coordinates the same one-form has the components $A'_r = \cos\theta$, $A'_\theta = -r\sin\theta$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]]), and their partial derivatives are

$$
\partial'_rA'_r = 0, \qquad \partial'_rA'_\theta = -\sin\theta, \qquad \partial'_\theta A'_r = -\sin\theta, \qquad \partial'_\theta A'_\theta = -r\cos\theta .
$$

A tensor that vanishes in one chart vanishes in every chart, because its transformation law is linear and homogeneous ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]]). So the array $\partial_\mu A_\nu$ is not a tensor. Its antisymmetric part behaves differently:

$$
\partial'_rA'_\theta - \partial'_\theta A'_r = -\sin\theta - (-\sin\theta) = 0 ,
$$

as it must if it is a tensor that vanishes in Cartesian coordinates. The reason is general. A one-form transforms as $A'_\nu = (\partial x^\rho/\partial x'^\nu)A_\rho$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]), and the product rule gives

$$
\partial'_\mu A'_\nu = \frac{\partial x^\sigma}{\partial x'^\mu}\frac{\partial x^\rho}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\partial_\sigma A_\rho + \frac{\partial^2x^\rho}{\partial x'^\mu\,\partial x'^\nu}\,A_\rho .
$$

The second term spoils the tensor law. Here it is $\partial^2(r\cos\theta)/\partial x'^\mu\partial x'^\nu$, the matrix with entries $0$, $-\sin\theta$, $-\sin\theta$, $-r\cos\theta$: exactly the derivatives above. It is symmetric in $\mu$ and $\nu$ (mixed partial derivatives commute), so it drops out of $\partial'_\mu A'_\nu - \partial'_\nu A'_\mu$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-5|Theorem §C2.3.5]]). The same happens for a covariant tensor of any rank, provided it is totally antisymmetric and the derivative index is antisymmetrized with all the others. The section turns this observation into a calculus.

It defines $r$-forms and their product ([[#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]], [[#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]]), with a caution on the factors of $r!$ that differ between books ([[#^cau-c2-6-1|Caution: Normalizations of the wedge product]]). The antisymmetrized derivative becomes the exterior derivative $d$ ([[#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]]), with its rules: chart independence, the graded Leibniz rule and $d^2 = 0$ ([[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]]). $d^2 = 0$ raises the question that matters for electromagnetism: when does a form $F$ with $dF = 0$ have a potential, $F = dA$? The answer is the Poincaré lemma ([[#^def-c2-6-4|Def. §C2.6.4]], [[#^def-c2-6-5|Def. §C2.6.5]], [[#^thm-c2-6-2|Theorem §C2.6.2]]). Finally, inserting a vector field into a form ([[#^def-c2-6-6|Def. §C2.6.6]]) combines with $d$ into the Lie derivative of §C2.5: Cartan's formula ([[#^thm-c2-6-3|Theorem §C2.6.3]]). The mathematical home of forms is MATH 591, Chapter 11 (§75–§84). This section gives the physics form (components, index notation, transformation law), links the definitions and proofs there, and proves here what 591 does not contain.

**Setup.** $M$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $n$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]); for spacetime $n = 4$. Charts $(U, x^\mu)$ are smooth, and all forms and vector fields are smooth. There is no metric. Forms, the wedge product, $d$, the interior product and the Lie derivative need none, so nothing is raised or lowered and every index position is a type ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^cau-c2-2-1|§C2.2, Caution: Index position is the type]]). Where vector calculus is recovered ([[#^ex-c2-6-1|Example §C2.6.1]]), the identification of forms with vectors uses the Euclidean metric and orientation of $\mathbf R^3$; on a manifold that step is the Hodge star ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential|§C3.4]]). All statements are exact. The identities used in the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): the signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ enters only [[#^ex-c2-6-3|Example §C2.6.3]] (via $A_\mu = (-V, \vec A)$, [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]], part 4); $G = c = 1$. Greek indices run over the $n$ coordinates of a chart ($0$–$3$ on spacetime), Latin $1$–$3$. Square brackets antisymmetrize with weight $1/r!$, $\omega_{[\mu_1\dots\mu_r]} = \frac{1}{r!}\sum_{\sigma}\operatorname{sgn}\sigma\;\omega_{\mu_{\sigma(1)}\dots\mu_{\sigma(r)}}$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-5|Def. §C2.3.5]]); $S_r$ is the group of permutations of $r$ objects and $\operatorname{sgn}\sigma = \pm1$ the sign; a hat marks an omitted index. Wedge products in the determinant convention ([[#^cau-c2-6-1|Caution: Normalizations of the wedge product]]).

## Forms and the wedge product

A one-form eats one vector. The derivative of the opening, $\partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$, has two indices and changes sign when they are exchanged: it eats two vectors and is antisymmetric in them. Objects of this kind, in any number of slots, are the first definition.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.6.1: r-Form
> *Structure.* Let $p \in M$ and $r \ge 0$. An **$r$-form at $p$** is a tensor of type $(0, r)$ at $p$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]), a multilinear map $\omega_p : T_pM \times \dots \times T_pM \to \mathbf R$ with $r$ arguments, that is **totally antisymmetric**: exchanging any two arguments changes the sign,
>
> $$
> \omega_p(\dots, X, \dots, Y, \dots) = -\,\omega_p(\dots, Y, \dots, X, \dots) .
> $$
>
> The $r$-forms at $p$ make up the vector space $\Lambda^rT_p^{\ast}M$ of linear $r$-forms on $T_pM$ ([[§75 Multilinear Algebra#^def-75-1|591 Def. §75.1]], [[§75 Multilinear Algebra#^def-75-2|591 Def. §75.2]]). An **$r$-form on $M$** (a differential form of degree $r$) is a tensor field ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]) whose value at every point is an $r$-form ([[§80 Differential Forms on Manifolds#^def-80-1|591 Def. §80.1]], [[§80 Differential Forms on Manifolds#^def-80-4|591 Def. §80.4]]). They make up the space $\Omega^r(M)$ ([[§80 Differential Forms on Manifolds#^def-80-6|591 Def. §80.6]]); $\Omega^0(M) = C^\infty(M)$, and $\Omega^1(M)$ are the one-forms of [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-5|Def. §C2.2.5]].
>
> *Components.* In a chart, $\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r} = \omega(\partial_{\mu_1}, \dots, \partial_{\mu_r})$. Antisymmetry of the map is total antisymmetry of the components, $\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r} = \omega_{[\mu_1\dots\mu_r]}$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-5|Def. §C2.3.5]]). Under a change of chart they transform as the components of any $(0, r)$ tensor ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]]),
>
> $$
> \omega'_{\nu_1\dots\nu_r} = \frac{\partial x^{\mu_1}}{\partial x'^{\nu_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial x^{\mu_r}}{\partial x'^{\nu_r}}\;\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r} ,
> $$
>
> and the law keeps them antisymmetric: exchanging $\nu_i$ and $\nu_j$ on the right is undone by renaming the summed $\mu_i$ and $\mu_j$.
>
> *Counting.* A component with a repeated index vanishes, and the components with increasing indices $\mu_1 < \dots < \mu_r$ determine all others. So $\dim\Lambda^rT_p^{\ast}M = \binom{n}{r}$, and there are no nonzero $r$-forms for $r > n$ ([[§75 Multilinear Algebra#^prop-75-6|591 Prop. §75.6]], [[§75 Multilinear Algebra#^cor-75-3|591 Cor. §75.3]]). On spacetime the dimensions for $r = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$ are $1, 4, 6, 4, 1$: a two-form has six independent components, as the field tensor $F_{\mu\nu}$ has, and a **top form** ($r = n$) has one ([[§75 Multilinear Algebra#^cor-75-7|591 Cor. §75.7]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4, Def. 26, eqs. (3.97), (3.101) (printed pp. 61–62) · MATH 591 §75, §80*

A product of forms should produce a form. The tensor product of two forms is a tensor but not antisymmetric: for one-forms, $(\alpha\otimes\beta)(X, X) = \alpha(X)\beta(X)$ need not vanish. Antisymmetrizing it repairs this. The normalization is a choice, and the one made here is the one of Couzens and of MATH 591.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.6.2: Wedge Product
> *Structure.* The **wedge product** (exterior product) of a $q$-form $\omega$ and an $r$-form $\eta$ is the $(q + r)$-form $\omega\wedge\eta$ given at each point by
>
> $$
> (\omega\wedge\eta)(X_1, \dots, X_{q+r}) = \frac{1}{q!\,r!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{q+r}}\operatorname{sgn}\sigma\;\omega\bigl(X_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, X_{\sigma(q)}\bigr)\,\eta\bigl(X_{\sigma(q+1)}, \dots, X_{\sigma(q+r)}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Two terms that differ only by a reordering inside the first $q$ or inside the last $r$ arguments are equal, so each choice of which $q$ arguments go to $\omega$ occurs $q!\,r!$ times. The formula is therefore MATH 591's sum over shuffles, one term per choice ([[§77 The Wedge Product#^def-77-1|591 Def. §77.1]], [[§77 The Wedge Product#^prop-77-2|591 Prop. §77.2]]; on manifolds [[§80 Differential Forms on Manifolds#^def-80-8|591 Def. §80.8]]). For $q = 0$, $f\wedge\eta = f\eta$.
>
> *One-forms.* $dx^\mu\wedge dx^\nu = dx^\mu\otimes dx^\nu - dx^\nu\otimes dx^\mu$, and in general
>
> $$
> dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r} = \sum_{\sigma\in S_r}\operatorname{sgn}\sigma\;dx^{\mu_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\dots\otimes dx^{\mu_{\sigma(r)}} ,
> $$
>
> whose value on $(X_1, \dots, X_r)$ is the determinant $\det\bigl(X_j^{\mu_i}\bigr)$ ([[§78 Properties of the Wedge Product#^lem-78-2|591 Lemma §78.2]]).
>
> *Components.* The sum in the definition is $(q + r)!$ times the antisymmetrization of $\omega\otimes\eta$, so
>
> $$
> (\omega\wedge\eta)_{\mu_1\dots\mu_q\nu_1\dots\nu_r} = \frac{(q + r)!}{q!\,r!}\;\omega_{[\mu_1\dots\mu_q}\eta_{\nu_1\dots\nu_r]}, \qquad\text{e.g.}\quad (\alpha\wedge\beta)_{\mu\nu} = \alpha_\mu\beta_\nu - \alpha_\nu\beta_\mu .
> $$
>
> *Expansion in a chart.*
>
> $$
> \omega = \frac{1}{r!}\,\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}\,dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r} = \sum_{\mu_1 < \dots < \mu_r}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}\,dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r} .
> $$
>
> More generally, for any array $c_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}$, antisymmetric or not, the form $\frac{1}{r!}c_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r}$ has the components $c_{[\mu_1\dots\mu_r]}$: the product $dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r}$ is antisymmetric in its indices (rules below), so in the sum only the antisymmetric part $c_{[\mu_1\dots\mu_r]}$ of the coefficients survives, and the first expansion then identifies it as the component array. The wedge keeps only the antisymmetric part of its coefficients.
>
> *Rules* ([[§78 Properties of the Wedge Product#^prop-78-4|591 Prop. §78.4]], [[§78 Properties of the Wedge Product#^cor-78-5|591 Cor. §78.5]]): bilinear, also over functions; associative; graded commutative, $\omega\wedge\eta = (-1)^{qr}\,\eta\wedge\omega$. Hence $\alpha\wedge\alpha = 0$ for a one-form (in particular $dx^\mu\wedge dx^\mu = 0$, no sum), while a two-form can have $F\wedge F \ne 0$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4, Def. 27, eqs. (3.98)–(3.100); §3.4.1, Def. 28, eqs. (3.102), (3.105) (printed pp. 61–62) · MATH 591 §77–§78, §80 · rules and expansion checked by script*

> [!caution] Caution: Normalizations of the wedge product
> Couzens and MATH 591 use the same convention, the **determinant convention** (Lee's name). Couzens' (3.98) has no $1/r!$ in front of the sum of tensor products, and his (3.102), with $1/(q!\,r!)$ in front of a sum over all permutations, equals 591's shuffle sum by [[§77 The Wedge Product#^prop-77-2|591 Prop. §77.2]], part 1. In both, $(dx\wedge dy)(\partial_x, \partial_y) = 1$. Other books (Kobayashi–Nomizu, for instance) set $\omega\wedge\eta$ equal to the antisymmetrization of $\omega\otimes\eta$ with no factor, the **Alt convention** ([[§77 The Wedge Product#^rem-77-1|591, Remark: Two Conventions for the Wedge Product]]); their product is smaller by $(q + r)!/(q!\,r!)$. Dictionary:
>
> | quantity | determinant convention (here, Couzens, MATH 591) | Alt convention |
> |---|---|---|
> | $dx\wedge dy$ | $dx\otimes dy - dy\otimes dx$ | $\frac12(dx\otimes dy - dy\otimes dx)$ |
> | $(dx^1\wedge\dots\wedge dx^n)(\partial_1, \dots, \partial_n)$ | $1$ | $1/n!$ |
> | an $r$-form in a basis | $\frac{1}{r!}\,\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}\,dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r} = \sum_{\mu_1<\dots<\mu_r}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}\,dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots$ | $\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}\,dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r}$ |
> | MATH 591's coefficient $f_A$, $A = \{a_1 < \dots < a_k\}$ ([[§80 Differential Forms on Manifolds#^def-80-3\|591 Def. §80.3]]) | $f_A = \omega_{a_1\dots a_k}$ | — |
> | components of $d\omega$ ([[#^thm-c2-6-1\|Theorem §C2.6.1]]) | $(r + 1)\,\partial_{[\mu_0}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r]}$ | depends on how $d$ is normalized there |
>
> Two habits of physics texts to watch for. Writing $F = F_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu\wedge dx^\nu$ without the $\frac12$ doubles the form in the determinant convention. Writing the components of $d\omega$ as $\partial_{[\mu_0}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r]}$ drops the factor $r + 1$. On spacetime the second slip turns $(dA)_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ into half of it.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, eqs. (3.97)–(3.102) (printed pp. 61–62) · MATH 591 §77, Remark: Two Conventions for the Wedge Product · the numbers $1$, $\frac12$, $1/4!$ checked by script*

## The exterior derivative

The opening differentiated a one-form and antisymmetrized. For an $r$-form the recipe is the same: differentiate the components, put the derivative index in front, and antisymmetrize over all $r + 1$ indices. In the language of forms the antisymmetrization is done by the wedge product.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.6.3: Exterior Derivative
> *Structure.* The **exterior derivative** is the linear map $d : \Omega^r(M) \to \Omega^{r+1}(M)$, $r = 0, 1, \dots, n$, given in any chart by
>
> $$
> d\omega = \frac{1}{r!}\,\partial_\nu\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}\;dx^\nu\wedge dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r} = \sum_{\mu_1 < \dots < \mu_r} d\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}\wedge dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r} ,
> $$
>
> where $d\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r} = \partial_\nu\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}\,dx^\nu$ is the differential of the component function ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]]). On functions, $df = \partial_\nu f\,dx^\nu$ is the differential itself; on top forms $d\omega = 0$, since there are no nonzero $(n + 1)$-forms ([[#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]]).
>
> The formula uses a chart, but the form it produces does not depend on the chart ([[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 2), so $d$ is defined on all of $M$. MATH 591 builds the same operator chart by chart, by pulling back the Euclidean $d$ ([[§81 The Exterior Derivative#^def-81-2|591 Def. §81.2]], [[§81 The Exterior Derivative#^def-81-3|591 Def. §81.3]]), proves the chart independence ([[§81 The Exterior Derivative#^lem-81-5|591 Lemma §81.5]]) and obtains this coordinate formula ([[§81 The Exterior Derivative#^cor-81-6|591 Cor. §81.6]]). No metric, no connection and no vector field enters.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.2, eqs. (3.107)–(3.108) (printed p. 63) · MATH 591 §81*

^def-c2-6-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.6.1: Components of dω; d² = 0, Graded Leibniz, d Commutes with Pull-Back
> For $\omega \in \Omega^r(M)$ and $\eta \in \Omega^s(M)$:
> 1. **Components.** In every chart
>
> $$
> (d\omega)_{\mu_0\mu_1\dots\mu_r} = (r + 1)\,\partial_{[\mu_0}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r]} = \sum_{k=0}^{r}(-1)^k\,\partial_{\mu_k}\omega_{\mu_0\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_r} .
> $$
>
> For $r = 0$: $(df)_\mu = \partial_\mu f$. For $r = 1$: $(dA)_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$. For $r = 2$: $(dF)_{\lambda\mu\nu} = \partial_\lambda F_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu F_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_\nu F_{\lambda\mu}$.
> 2. **Chart independence; pull-back.** These components transform as a tensor of type $(0, r + 1)$ under every change of chart, so $d\omega$ is the same form whichever chart computes it. More generally, for every smooth map $\varphi : N \to M$, $\varphi^{\ast}(d\omega) = d(\varphi^{\ast}\omega)$ (pull-back: [[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]]; [[§81 The Exterior Derivative#^thm-81-7|591 Thm. §81.7]], part 4).
> 3. **Graded Leibniz rule.** $d(\omega\wedge\eta) = d\omega\wedge\eta + (-1)^r\,\omega\wedge d\eta$.
> 4. **$d^2 = 0$.** $d(d\omega) = 0$.
>
> $d$ is the only family of linear maps $\Omega^r \to \Omega^{r+1}$ that is the differential on functions and has properties 2–4 ([[§81 The Exterior Derivative#^thm-81-7|591 Thm. §81.7]]).
>
> *Model:* none (a statement about smooth manifolds); *applies:* every smooth manifold and every chart, exactly; no metric.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.2, eqs. (3.108), (3.109) (Exercise 3.8), (3.112)–(3.113) (printed pp. 63–64) · MATH 591 §81, Theorem §81.7 · part 1 for $r \le 3$ on $\mathbf R^4$, parts 3–4 checked by script*

^thm-c2-6-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (coefficients are antisymmetrized). By [[#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]] (expansion), a form $\frac{1}{p!}c_{\nu_1\dots\nu_p}\,dx^{\nu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\nu_p}$ has the components $c_{[\nu_1\dots\nu_p]}$, whatever the symmetry of $c$.
>
> **Step 2** (part 1). In [[#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]] write $\frac{1}{r!} = \frac{r + 1}{(r + 1)!}$:
>
> $$
> d\omega = \frac{1}{(r + 1)!}\,\bigl[(r + 1)\,\partial_\nu\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}\bigr]\,dx^\nu\wedge dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r} .
> $$
>
> By Step 1 with $p = r + 1$ and $c_{\nu\mu_1\dots\mu_r} = (r + 1)\,\partial_\nu\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}$, the components are $(d\omega)_{\mu_0\dots\mu_r} = (r + 1)\,\partial_{[\mu_0}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r]}$. Written out, with the indices labelled $0, \dots, r$,
>
> $$
> (r + 1)\,\partial_{[\mu_0}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r]} = \frac{1}{r!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{r+1}}\operatorname{sgn}\sigma\;\partial_{\mu_{\sigma(0)}}\omega_{\mu_{\sigma(1)}\dots\mu_{\sigma(r)}} .
> $$
>
> Group the terms by $k = \sigma(0)$. For fixed $k$ the remaining $r$ positions hold the indices other than $\mu_k$ in some order, reached from the increasing order by a permutation $\rho$; antisymmetry of $\omega$ gives $\omega_{\mu_{\sigma(1)}\dots\mu_{\sigma(r)}} = \operatorname{sgn}\rho\;\omega_{\mu_0\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_r}$, and $\operatorname{sgn}\sigma = (-1)^k\operatorname{sgn}\rho$, since bringing $\mu_k$ to the front passes $k$ indices. So each of the $r!$ terms with $\sigma(0) = k$ equals $(-1)^k\,\partial_{\mu_k}\omega_{\mu_0\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_r}$, and
>
> $$
> (d\omega)_{\mu_0\dots\mu_r} = \sum_{k=0}^{r}(-1)^k\,\partial_{\mu_k}\omega_{\mu_0\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_r} .
> $$
>
> For $r = 2$ with $(\mu_0, \mu_1, \mu_2) = (\lambda, \mu, \nu)$: $\partial_\lambda F_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\mu F_{\lambda\nu} + \partial_\nu F_{\lambda\mu} = \partial_\lambda F_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu F_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_\nu F_{\lambda\mu}$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 2: a change of chart). Write $J^\mu{}_\nu = \partial x^\mu/\partial x'^\nu$, so that $\omega'_{\nu_1\dots\nu_r} = J^{\mu_1}{}_{\nu_1}\cdots J^{\mu_r}{}_{\nu_r}\,\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}$ ([[#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]]) and $\partial'_{\nu_0} = J^{\mu_0}{}_{\nu_0}\,\partial_{\mu_0}$ (chain rule). The product rule gives
>
> $$
> \partial'_{\nu_0}\omega'_{\nu_1\dots\nu_r} = J^{\mu_0}{}_{\nu_0}J^{\mu_1}{}_{\nu_1}\cdots J^{\mu_r}{}_{\nu_r}\,\partial_{\mu_0}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r} + \sum_{k=1}^{r}\frac{\partial^2x^{\mu_k}}{\partial x'^{\nu_0}\,\partial x'^{\nu_k}}\;\prod_{j\ne k}J^{\mu_j}{}_{\nu_j}\;\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r} .
> $$
>
> The $k$-th extra term is symmetric under $\nu_0 \leftrightarrow \nu_k$ (equality of mixed partials, [[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]). Antisymmetrizing over $\nu_0, \dots, \nu_r$ kills it: exchanging $\nu_0$ and $\nu_k$ in the alternating sum leaves the term unchanged and flips the sign, so the sum equals its own negative. What remains is
>
> $$
> (r + 1)\,\partial'_{[\nu_0}\omega'_{\nu_1\dots\nu_r]} = J^{\mu_0}{}_{\nu_0}\cdots J^{\mu_r}{}_{\nu_r}\;(r + 1)\,\partial_{[\mu_0}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r]} ,
> $$
>
> the $(0, r + 1)$ law ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]]). By part 1 both sides are components of $d\omega$, computed in the two charts: they describe one form. For a diffeomorphism $\varphi$ the same computation, with $J$ the Jacobian of $\varphi$ ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]]), gives $\varphi^{\ast}d\omega = d\varphi^{\ast}\omega$; for a general smooth map the proof is [[§81 The Exterior Derivative#^thm-81-7|591 Thm. §81.7]], part 4.
>
> **Step 4** (part 3). Both sides are bilinear, so it suffices to take $\omega = f\,dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r}$ and $\eta = g\,dx^{\nu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\nu_s}$, with indices in any order. For such terms the definition gives $d(h\,dx^{\lambda_1}\wedge\dots) = dh\wedge dx^{\lambda_1}\wedge\dots$ ([[§81 The Exterior Derivative#^lem-81-2|591 Lemma §81.2]]). With $\omega\wedge\eta = fg\,dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r}\wedge dx^{\nu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\nu_s}$ and $d(fg) = g\,df + f\,dg$,
>
> $$
> d(\omega\wedge\eta) = \bigl(df\wedge dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r}\bigr)\wedge\bigl(g\,dx^{\nu_1}\wedge\dots\bigr) + f\,dg\wedge dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r}\wedge dx^{\nu_1}\wedge\dots .
> $$
>
> The first term is $d\omega\wedge\eta$. In the second, moving the one-form $dg$ to the right past the $r$ one-forms $dx^{\mu_i}$ costs $(-1)^r$ (graded commutativity, [[#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]]), giving $(-1)^r\,(f\,dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r})\wedge(dg\wedge dx^{\nu_1}\wedge\dots) = (-1)^r\,\omega\wedge d\eta$.
>
> **Step 5** (part 4). Apply the definition to $d\omega = \frac{1}{r!}\partial_\nu\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}\,dx^\nu\wedge dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots$, again with [[§81 The Exterior Derivative#^lem-81-2|591 Lemma §81.2]]:
>
> $$
> d(d\omega) = \frac{1}{r!}\,\partial_\sigma\partial_\nu\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}\;dx^\sigma\wedge dx^\nu\wedge dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r} .
> $$
>
> The coefficient is symmetric in $\sigma\nu$ ([[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]) and $dx^\sigma\wedge dx^\nu = -dx^\nu\wedge dx^\sigma$. Renaming $\sigma \leftrightarrow \nu$ turns the sum into its negative, so it is $0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Antisymmetrization is exactly what removes the second-derivative term that makes $\partial_\mu V^\nu$ non-tensorial ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]]; Step 3). That is why $d$ needs no connection, no metric and no vector field.
> - $d^2 = 0$ is the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives, written in forms (Step 5).
> - The sign $(-1)^r$ in the Leibniz rule counts the one-forms that $dg$ has to pass (Step 4).

## Closed and exact forms

By $d^2 = 0$, every form of the type $d\eta$ is annihilated by $d$. Physics needs the converse. The field tensor of electromagnetism obeys $dF = 0$ (the homogeneous Maxwell equations, [[#^ex-c2-6-3|Example §C2.6.3]]); is it always of the form $F = dA$, so that a potential exists? Two names make the question precise.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.6.4: Closed Form
> A form $\omega \in \Omega^r(M)$ is **closed** if $d\omega = 0$ ([[§82 De Rham Cohomology#^def-82-1|591 Def. §82.1]]; for one-forms [[§53 One-Forms#^def-53-5|591 Def. §53.5]]). In a chart this is the system $\sum_{k=0}^{r}(-1)^k\,\partial_{\mu_k}\omega_{\mu_0\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_r} = 0$ ([[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1); for a one-form, $\partial_\mu A_\nu = \partial_\nu A_\mu$. Every top form is closed, since there are no nonzero $(n + 1)$-forms.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.2, p. 64 · MATH 591 §82*

^def-c2-6-4

> [!definition] Definition §C2.6.5: Exact Form
> A form $\omega \in \Omega^r(M)$, $r \ge 1$, is **exact** if $\omega = d\eta$ for some $\eta \in \Omega^{r-1}(M)$, a **potential** (primitive) of $\omega$ ([[§82 De Rham Cohomology#^def-82-2|591 Def. §82.2]]; for one-forms [[§53 One-Forms#^def-53-6|591 Def. §53.6]]). The potential is defined on all of $M$; a form that has potentials only on the sets of an open cover is called **locally exact**. By $d^2 = 0$ every exact form is closed ([[§82 De Rham Cohomology#^lem-82-1|591 Lemma §82.1]]). The closed $r$-forms modulo the exact ones form the de Rham cohomology space $H^r(M)$ ([[§82 De Rham Cohomology#^def-82-3|591 Def. §82.3]]), which measures how far the converse fails on $M$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.2, pp. 64–65, eq. (3.115) · MATH 591 §82*

^def-c2-6-5

The converse holds on regions without holes. The proof builds the potential by integrating $\omega$ along the straight rays from a centre, the construction that gave the four-potential of a field obeying the Bianchi identity in Electromagnetism ([[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-5|EM Theorem §C1.2.5]]), now in every degree.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.6.2: Poincaré Lemma
> Let $U \subseteq \mathbf R^n$ be open and **star-shaped** about the origin: $tx \in U$ for every $x \in U$ and $t \in [0, 1]$. Let $r \ge 1$ and let $\omega$ be a closed $r$-form on $U$ ([[#^def-c2-6-4|Def. §C2.6.4]]). Then $\omega = d\eta$ for the $(r - 1)$-form $\eta$ with components
>
> $$
> \eta_{\mu_2\dots\mu_r}(x) = \int_0^1 t^{r-1}\,x^\nu\,\omega_{\nu\mu_2\dots\mu_r}(tx)\;dt
> $$
>
> (for $r = 1$, $\eta(x) = \int_0^1 x^\nu\omega_\nu(tx)\,dt$, the line integral of $\omega$ along the segment from $0$ to $x$). Consequently:
> 1. on $\mathbf R^n$, Minkowski spacetime in particular, every closed form of degree $r \ge 1$ is exact;
> 2. on any manifold every closed form is locally exact: every point lies in a chart whose image is a ball, and there $\omega = d\eta$;
> 3. the potential is not unique: $d\eta' = d\eta$ exactly when $\eta' - \eta$ is closed, and on $U$ that difference is $d\lambda$ for an $(r - 2)$-form $\lambda$ if $r \ge 2$, and a constant if $r = 1$.
>
> Couzens states the lemma, without proof, for chart domains that can be contracted to a point; the star-shaped case proved here covers the uses in these notes. On a domain with a hole a closed form need not be exact ([[#^ex-c2-6-2|Example §C2.6.2]]).
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* star-shaped open subsets of $\mathbf R^n$ (a star-shaped region about another point $x_0$: replace $x$ by $x - x_0$), and chart domains whose image is star-shaped; the general contractible case needs the homotopy invariance of de Rham cohomology, not proved here.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.2, Theorem 3 (printed p. 65; stated) · [[§39 Closed and Exact Forms#^prop-39-7|452 Prop. §39.7]] (stated) · the homotopy formula and its verification written out here; for $r = 1, 2$ in four dimensions [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-5|EM Theorem §C1.2.5]] · checked by script for closed forms of degree 1, 2, 3 on $\mathbf R^4$*

^thm-c2-6-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (differentiating under the integral). The integrand $t^{r-1}x^\nu\omega_{\nu\mu_2\dots\mu_r}(tx)$ is smooth in $(t, x)$ on $[0, 1]\times U$, so $\eta$ is smooth and may be differentiated under the integral sign ([[§CA.1 Exchanging Limits, Derivatives and Integrals#^thm-ca-1-1|QFT Theorem §CA.1.1]]). With $\partial_\mu x^\nu = \delta_\mu^\nu$ and the chain rule $\partial_\mu[\omega(tx)] = t\,(\partial_\mu\omega)(tx)$,
>
> $$
> \partial_{\mu_1}\eta_{\mu_2\dots\mu_r}(x) = \int_0^1\Bigl[t^{r-1}\,\omega_{\mu_1\mu_2\dots\mu_r}(tx) + t^r\,x^\nu\,(\partial_{\mu_1}\omega_{\nu\mu_2\dots\mu_r})(tx)\Bigr]dt .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (components of $d\eta$). [[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1, for the $(r - 1)$-form $\eta$ with indices $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_r$:
>
> $$
> (d\eta)_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r} = \sum_{k=1}^{r}(-1)^{k-1}\,\partial_{\mu_k}\eta_{\mu_1\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_r} = \int_0^1\Bigl[t^{r-1}\sum_{k=1}^{r}(-1)^{k-1}\omega_{\mu_k\mu_1\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_r} + t^r\,x^\nu\sum_{k=1}^{r}(-1)^{k-1}\partial_{\mu_k}\omega_{\nu\mu_1\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_r}\Bigr]_{tx}dt ,
> $$
>
> inserting Step 1 with the derivative index $\mu_k$ and the remaining indices in their order.
>
> **Step 3** (the first sum). Moving $\mu_k$ from the front to the $k$-th place passes $k - 1$ indices, so $\omega_{\mu_k\mu_1\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_r} = (-1)^{k-1}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}$, and
>
> $$
> \sum_{k=1}^{r}(-1)^{k-1}\omega_{\mu_k\mu_1\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_r} = \sum_{k=1}^{r}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r} = r\,\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r} .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (the second sum: closedness). $d\omega = 0$, by [[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1, with the indices $(\nu, \mu_1, \dots, \mu_r)$:
>
> $$
> 0 = (d\omega)_{\nu\mu_1\dots\mu_r} = \partial_\nu\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r} + \sum_{k=1}^{r}(-1)^k\,\partial_{\mu_k}\omega_{\nu\mu_1\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_{k=1}^{r}(-1)^{k-1}\,\partial_{\mu_k}\omega_{\nu\mu_1\dots\hat\mu_k\dots\mu_r} = \partial_\nu\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (a total derivative in $t$). Inserting Steps 3 and 4 into Step 2,
>
> $$
> (d\eta)_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}(x) = \int_0^1\Bigl[r\,t^{r-1}\,\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}(tx) + t^r\,x^\nu(\partial_\nu\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r})(tx)\Bigr]dt = \int_0^1\frac{d}{dt}\Bigl[t^r\,\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}(tx)\Bigr]dt = \omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}(x) ,
> $$
>
> using $\frac{d}{dt}\,\omega(tx) = x^\nu(\partial_\nu\omega)(tx)$ and $t^r = 0$ at $t = 0$ (here $r \ge 1$ is needed). So $d\eta = \omega$.
>
> **Step 6** (the consequences). Part 1: $\mathbf R^n$ is star-shaped. Part 2: around any point take a chart $\varphi : V \to \mathbf R^n$ whose image is a ball $B$ about $0$ (shrink and translate any chart). The form $(\varphi^{-1})^{\ast}\omega$ on $B$ is closed, because $d$ commutes with pull-backs ([[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 2); so $(\varphi^{-1})^{\ast}\omega = d\eta$ on $B$, and $\omega = \varphi^{\ast}d\eta = d(\varphi^{\ast}\eta)$ on $V$. Part 3: $d\eta' - d\eta = d(\eta' - \eta)$ vanishes exactly when $\eta' - \eta$ is closed; on $U$ a closed form of degree $r - 1 \ge 1$ is $d\lambda$ by the lemma, and a closed function has $\partial_\mu f = 0$, so it is constant on the connected set $U$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The potential is built by integrating $\omega$ along the rays from the centre; for $r = 1$ it is the line integral that defines a potential energy. The hypothesis is that every ray stays in $U$. On $\mathbf R^2\setminus\{0\}$ the rays through the hole are cut, and [[#^ex-c2-6-2|Example §C2.6.2]] shows that no other construction can succeed either.
> - Closedness is used once, in Step 4, to turn the derivative of $\omega$ into the radial derivative $x^\nu\partial_\nu\omega$; together with the factor $t^{r-1}$ the integrand becomes a total derivative.
> - For $r = 2$ on spacetime, $\eta_\nu = \int_0^1 t\,x^\mu F_{\mu\nu}(tx)\,dt$ is the four-potential of [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-5|EM Theorem §C1.2.5]]; part 3 is its gauge freedom ([[#^ex-c2-6-3|Example §C2.6.3]]).

## The interior product and Cartan's formula

[[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors|§C2.5]] computed the Lie derivative of a tensor of type $(0, r)$ with a component formula that differentiates both the tensor and the field $X$ ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-4|Theorem §C2.5.4]]). For forms the same derivative can be assembled from two operations that are already available: $d$, and inserting $X$ into the first slot. The second operation is defined first.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.6.6: Interior Product
> *Structure.* For a vector field $X$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-4|Def. §C2.2.4]]) and $\omega \in \Omega^r(M)$, $r \ge 1$, the **interior product** of $X$ with $\omega$ is the $(r - 1)$-form $i_X\omega$ given at each point by
>
> $$
> (i_X\omega)(Y_1, \dots, Y_{r-1}) = \omega(X, Y_1, \dots, Y_{r-1}) :
> $$
>
> $X$ is inserted in the first slot. For a function, $i_Xf = 0$; for a one-form, $i_X\alpha = \alpha(X) = \alpha_\mu X^\mu$, the pairing of [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]]. MATH 591 calls it contraction by $X$ and writes $\iota_X$ ([[§84 Contraction by Vector Fields#^def-84-1|591 Def. §84.1]]); $i_X\omega$ is a smooth form, and $C^\infty(M)$-linear in $X$ ([[§84 Contraction by Vector Fields#^lem-84-1|591 Lemma §84.1]]).
>
> *Components.*
>
> $$
> (i_X\omega)_{\mu_2\dots\mu_r} = X^\nu\,\omega_{\nu\mu_2\dots\mu_r}, \qquad i_X\omega = \frac{1}{(r - 1)!}\,X^\nu\omega_{\nu\mu_2\dots\mu_r}\,dx^{\mu_2}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_r} .
> $$
>
> This is the contraction ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-4|Def. §C2.3.4]]) of $X\otimes\omega$ over the index of $X$ and the first index of $\omega$, so it does not depend on the chart ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]). On $\mathbf R^3$, for example, the components $(i_{\partial_x}\omega)_\mu = \omega_{x\mu}$ give $i_{\partial_x}(dx\wedge dy) = dy$, $i_{\partial_x}(dy\wedge dz) = 0$ and $i_{\partial_x}(dz\wedge dx) = -dz$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.3, Def. 30, eqs. (3.121)–(3.123) (printed p. 66) · MATH 591 §84 · the three values checked by script*

^def-c2-6-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.6.3: Cartan's Formula
> Let $X$ be a vector field and $\omega \in \Omega^r(M)$, $r \ge 0$, and let $\mathcal L_X$ be the Lie derivative ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]]).
> 1. **Cartan's formula.**
>
> $$
> \mathcal L_X\omega = d(i_X\omega) + i_X(d\omega) .
> $$
>
> In components, the left side is the $(0, r)$ formula of [[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-4|Theorem §C2.5.4]], $X^\nu\partial_\nu\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_r} + \sum_{k=1}^{r}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\nu\dots\mu_r}\,\partial_{\mu_k}X^\nu$ ($\nu$ in the $k$-th slot). For $r = 0$ the first term on the right is absent, and the formula reads $\mathcal L_Xf = i_X df = df(X) = Xf$, the Lie derivative of a function ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]]). For a closed form, $\mathcal L_X\omega = d(i_X\omega)$ is exact.
> 2. **$\mathcal L_X$ commutes with $d$.** $\mathcal L_X(d\omega) = d(\mathcal L_X\omega)$.
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* every smooth manifold, every vector field (complete or not: only the flow near $t = 0$ enters); exact.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.3, eq. (3.124) (printed p. 67; stated without proof) · not yet in MATH 591 Chapter 11 · part 1 checked by script on one- and two-forms in $\mathbf R^3$ (and in components for every degree on $\mathbf R^4$), part 2 for $r = 1, 2$*

^thm-c2-6-3

> [!proof]- Proof (to be filled after the MATH 591 homework)
> The proof uses properties of the interior product that are part of an open MATH 591 assignment; it will be written out after the assignment is submitted.

> [!derivation]- Derivation of part 2
> Part 2 is proved from the flow, without part 1.
>
> **Step 1** (the definition). By [[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]], with $\sigma_t$ the flow of $X$ ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^def-c2-5-2|Def. §C2.5.2]]),
>
> $$
> \mathcal L_X(d\omega) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\sigma_t^{\ast}(d\omega) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** ($d$ commutes with the pull-back). Each $\sigma_t$ is a diffeomorphism between open sets, so $\sigma_t^{\ast}(d\omega) = d(\sigma_t^{\ast}\omega)$ ([[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 2).
>
> **Step 3** (exchange $d/dt$ and $d$). In a chart, the components of $\sigma_t^{\ast}\omega$ are smooth functions of $(t, x)$, because the flow depends smoothly on $t$ and on the initial point ([[§69 Maximal Integral Curves and Flows|591 §69]]). $d$ differentiates only in $x$ ([[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1), and $\partial/\partial t$ commutes with $\partial/\partial x^\mu$ on smooth functions ([[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]). Hence
>
> $$
> \mathcal L_X(d\omega) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}d(\sigma_t^{\ast}\omega) = d\Bigl(\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\sigma_t^{\ast}\omega\Bigr) = d(\mathcal L_X\omega) .
> $$
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Part 2 rests only on the naturality of $d$: $d$ commutes with every diffeomorphism, hence with the infinitesimal diffeomorphism that $\mathcal L_X$ is.

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Hamiltonian mechanics on a symplectic manifold
> Couzens closes his section on forms with a box that recasts Hamiltonian mechanics ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations|CM §B7.1]], [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets|CM §B8.1]]) in this language. One degree of freedom is written out; with $N$ degrees of freedom every term is summed over $i$.
> - *Phase space* is a manifold of dimension $2N$ with coordinates $(q^i, p_i)$ ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^def-b7-2-1|CM Def. §B7.2.1]]); the Hamiltonian $H$ is a function on it.
> - *Symplectic form.* $\omega = dp_i\wedge dq^i$ is closed, $d\omega = d(dp_i)\wedge dq^i - dp_i\wedge d(dq^i) = 0$ by the Leibniz rule and $d^2 = 0$ ([[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]]), and nondegenerate: $i_X\omega = 0$ only for $X = 0$. A manifold with a closed nondegenerate two-form is a **symplectic manifold**.
> - *Hamiltonian vector field.* Nondegeneracy lets one solve $i_{X_H}\omega = -dH$ for $X_H$ (Couzens (3.129)). In the chart $(q, p)$ the components of $\omega = dp\wedge dq$ are $\omega_{pq} = 1$, $\omega_{qp} = -1$ ([[#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]], one-forms). For $X_H = a\,\partial_q + b\,\partial_p$ the components of [[#^def-c2-6-6|Def. §C2.6.6]], $(i_{X_H}\omega)_\nu = X_H^\mu\omega_{\mu\nu}$, give $(i_{X_H}\omega)_q = b\,\omega_{pq} = b$ and $(i_{X_H}\omega)_p = a\,\omega_{qp} = -a$, so $i_{X_H}\omega = b\,dq - a\,dp$; equating with $-dH = -\partial_qH\,dq - \partial_pH\,dp$ gives $a = \partial_pH$, $b = -\partial_qH$. The integral curves of $X_H$ obey $\dot q = \partial_pH$, $\dot p = -\partial_qH$: Hamilton's equations ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-2|CM Theorem §B7.1.2]]).
> - *Poisson bracket.* $df(X_g) = X_gf = \partial_qf\,\partial_pg - \partial_pf\,\partial_qg = \{f, g\}$, the bracket with the sign convention of [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-1|CM Def. §B8.1.1]]; so $\dot f = \{f, H\} = X_Hf = \mathcal L_{X_H}f$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|CM Theorem §B8.1.3]]). In terms of $\omega$, $\{f, g\} = -\,\omega(X_f, X_g)$ ([[#^cau-c2-6-3|Caution: A sign in Couzens' Poisson bracket]]).
> - *Liouville.* By Cartan's formula ([[#^thm-c2-6-3|Theorem §C2.6.3]]) and $d\omega = 0$, $\mathcal L_{X_H}\omega = d(i_{X_H}\omega) + i_{X_H}d\omega = -d(dH) + 0 = 0$: the Hamiltonian flow preserves $\omega$, and with it (Leibniz rule, [[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-5|Theorem §C2.5.5]]) the top form $\omega\wedge\dots\wedge\omega$ ($N$ factors), whose integral is the phase-space volume (integration of top forms: [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem|§C2.7]]). This is Liouville's theorem ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-3|CM Theorem §B7.2.3]]). Couzens adds that $d\omega = 0$ is also what makes the bracket obey the Jacobi identity ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-2|CM Theorem §B8.1.2]]); that is not shown here.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.3, box "Hamiltonian mechanics in differential geometry", eqs. (3.126)–(3.136) (printed pp. 67–68) · Hamilton's equations, the bracket and $\mathcal L_{X_H}\omega = 0$ checked by script*

^rem-c2-6-1

> [!caution] Caution: A sign in Couzens' Poisson bracket
> Couzens' (3.134) reads $\{f, g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g)$. With his own definition $i_{X_f}\omega = -df$ (3.129), $\omega(X_f, X_g) = (i_{X_f}\omega)(X_g) = -df(X_g) = -\{f, g\}$. Directly, in one degree of freedom with the components $\omega_{pq} = 1 = -\omega_{qp}$ of $\omega = dp\wedge dq$ and $X_f = \partial_pf\,\partial_q - \partial_qf\,\partial_p$:
>
> $$
> \omega(X_f, X_g) = \omega_{\mu\nu}X_f^\mu X_g^\nu = \omega_{pq}X_f^pX_g^q + \omega_{qp}X_f^qX_g^p = (-\partial_qf)(\partial_pg) - (\partial_pf)(-\partial_qg) = -\{f, g\} .
> $$
>
> The correct form is $\{f, g\} = -\,\omega(X_f, X_g) = \omega(X_g, X_f)$. The next equation, (3.135), $\{f, g\} = -i_{X_f}\omega(X_g) = df(X_g) = X_g(f)$, has the right sign, and (3.136), $\dot f = \{f, H\} = X_H(f)$, follows from it.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.3, eqs. (3.127), (3.129), (3.133)–(3.136) (printed pp. 67–68; rendered pages read) · checked by script*

^cau-c2-6-3

Forms can now be differentiated in any chart, with no structure beyond the manifold. The remaining property that makes them central is that they can be integrated. A top form transforms with the Jacobian determinant of the change of chart, the factor of the change-of-variables formula for integrals, while a function does not. How is $\int_M\omega$ defined for a top form, and what is the integral of $d\omega$ over a region? [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem|§C2.7]] answers both: the second answer is Stokes' theorem.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C2.6.1: Gradient, Curl and Divergence as d on ℝ³
> *Setup.* $\mathbf R^3$ with Cartesian coordinates $(x, y, z)$. The general forms of each degree are (Couzens (3.110))
>
> $$
> \omega_0 = f, \qquad \omega_1 = \omega_x\,dx + \omega_y\,dy + \omega_z\,dz, \qquad \omega_2 = \omega_{xy}\,dx\wedge dy + \omega_{yz}\,dy\wedge dz + \omega_{zx}\,dz\wedge dx, \qquad \omega_3 = \omega_{xyz}\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz .
> $$
>
> Each term has the form $h\,dx^{\lambda_1}\wedge\dots$, and $d(h\,dx^{\lambda_1}\wedge\dots) = dh\wedge dx^{\lambda_1}\wedge\dots$ ([[#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]]; [[§81 The Exterior Derivative#^lem-81-2|591 Lemma §81.2]]), with $dh = \partial_xh\,dx + \partial_yh\,dy + \partial_zh\,dz$.
>
> *Degree 0.* $d\omega_0 = \partial_xf\,dx + \partial_yf\,dy + \partial_zf\,dz$.
>
> *Degree 1.* In $d\omega_x\wedge dx$ the term $\partial_x\omega_x\,dx\wedge dx$ vanishes, and similarly for $y$, $z$:
>
> $$
> d\omega_1 = (\partial_y\omega_x\,dy + \partial_z\omega_x\,dz)\wedge dx + (\partial_x\omega_y\,dx + \partial_z\omega_y\,dz)\wedge dy + (\partial_x\omega_z\,dx + \partial_y\omega_z\,dy)\wedge dz .
> $$
>
> Bring each product to one of the three orders $dx\wedge dy$, $dy\wedge dz$, $dz\wedge dx$, using $dy\wedge dx = -dx\wedge dy$, $dz\wedge dy = -dy\wedge dz$, $dx\wedge dz = -dz\wedge dx$ ([[#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]], rules):
>
> $$
> d\omega_1 = -\partial_y\omega_x\,dx\wedge dy + \partial_z\omega_x\,dz\wedge dx + \partial_x\omega_y\,dx\wedge dy - \partial_z\omega_y\,dy\wedge dz - \partial_x\omega_z\,dz\wedge dx + \partial_y\omega_z\,dy\wedge dz
> $$
>
> $$
> = (\partial_y\omega_z - \partial_z\omega_y)\,dy\wedge dz + (\partial_z\omega_x - \partial_x\omega_z)\,dz\wedge dx + (\partial_x\omega_y - \partial_y\omega_x)\,dx\wedge dy .
> $$
>
> *Degree 2.* Only the derivative along the missing direction survives in each term:
>
> $$
> d\omega_2 = \partial_z\omega_{xy}\,dz\wedge dx\wedge dy + \partial_x\omega_{yz}\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz + \partial_y\omega_{zx}\,dy\wedge dz\wedge dx = (\partial_x\omega_{yz} + \partial_y\omega_{zx} + \partial_z\omega_{xy})\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz ,
> $$
>
> since $dz\wedge dx\wedge dy$ and $dy\wedge dz\wedge dx$ are cyclic, even, reorderings of $dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$.
>
> *Degree 3.* $d\omega_3 = 0$: there are no nonzero four-forms on $\mathbf R^3$.
>
> *Dictionary.* Identify the one-form $\omega_1$ with the vector $\vec v = (\omega_x, \omega_y, \omega_z)$ and the two-form $\omega_2$ with the vector $\vec w = (\omega_{yz}, \omega_{zx}, \omega_{xy})$. Then
>
> $$
> d\omega_0 \leftrightarrow \nabla f, \qquad d\omega_1 \leftrightarrow \nabla\times\vec v, \qquad d\omega_2 \leftrightarrow \nabla\cdot\vec w ,
> $$
>
> the three derivatives of vector calculus ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^def-b1-1-1|EM Def. §B1.1.1]]; [[§38 The Exterior Derivative#^prop-38-1|452 Prop. §38.1]]–[[§38 The Exterior Derivative#^prop-38-3|452 Prop. §38.3]]). Applied twice, $d^2 = 0$ ([[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 4) becomes $\nabla\times(\nabla f) = 0$ and $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec v) = 0$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-2|EM Theorem §B1.1.2]]).
>
> *What needs more structure.* $d$ itself used only the coordinates. The dictionary used the Euclidean metric and the orientation of $\mathbf R^3$: a one-form becomes a vector by raising its index, and a two-form becomes a vector through the Levi-Civita symbol. That is why the three operations look different in vector calculus and why their curvilinear forms carry scale factors ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|EM Theorem §B1.1.5]]), while the components of $d\omega$ are given in every chart by the one formula of [[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1. The metric step is the Hodge star ([[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential|§C3.4]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.2, Example 3.9, eqs. (3.110)–(3.111) (printed pp. 63–64) · the curl and divergence components checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.6.2: A Closed Form That Is Not Exact
> *Setup.* $M = \mathbf R^2\setminus\{0\}$, the plane with the origin removed, and the one-form (Couzens (3.118); [[§39 Closed and Exact Forms#^prop-39-6|452 Prop. §39.6]]; [[§83 The First Cohomology of the Circle#^ex-83-2|591 Ex. §83.2]])
>
> $$
> \omega = \frac{-y\,dx + x\,dy}{x^2 + y^2}, \qquad \omega_x = \frac{-y}{x^2 + y^2}, \quad \omega_y = \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2} .
> $$
>
> *Closed.* By [[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1, $d\omega = (\partial_x\omega_y - \partial_y\omega_x)\,dx\wedge dy$, and
>
> $$
> \partial_x\omega_y = \frac{(x^2 + y^2) - 2x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}, \qquad \partial_y\omega_x = \frac{-(x^2 + y^2) + 2y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} ,
> $$
>
> so $d\omega = 0$.
>
> *Locally exact: $\omega = d\theta$.* On the plane cut along a ray from the origin, polar coordinates form a chart. With $dx = \cos\theta\,dr - r\sin\theta\,d\theta$ and $dy = \sin\theta\,dr + r\cos\theta\,d\theta$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]]),
>
> $$
> -y\,dx + x\,dy = -r\sin\theta(\cos\theta\,dr - r\sin\theta\,d\theta) + r\cos\theta(\sin\theta\,dr + r\cos\theta\,d\theta) = r^2(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta)\,d\theta = r^2\,d\theta ,
> $$
>
> so $\omega = d\theta$ there: the angle is a potential on every cut plane. Other formulas for the angle work on other regions: $\arctan(y/x)$ is a potential on $x \ne 0$, and $-\arctan(x/y)$ is one on $y \ne 0$, smooth across the line $x = 0$. On the first quadrant the two differ by the constant $\pi/2$, on the second by $-\pi/2$: on a connected region two potentials differ by a constant ([[#^thm-c2-6-2|Theorem §C2.6.2]], part 3; [[§83 The First Cohomology of the Circle#^lem-83-1|591 Lemma §83.1]]).
>
> *Not exact.* Suppose $\omega = df$ for a smooth $f$ on all of $M$. Along the unit circle $\gamma(s) = (\cos s, \sin s)$, $s \in [0, 2\pi]$, the pull-back is $\gamma^{\ast}df = \frac{d}{ds}f(\gamma(s))\,ds$ ([[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 2), so
>
> $$
> \int_0^{2\pi}\gamma^{\ast}\omega = f(\gamma(2\pi)) - f(\gamma(0)) = 0 .
> $$
>
> Computed directly, $\gamma^{\ast}\omega = \bigl[-\sin s\,(-\sin s) + \cos s\,\cos s\bigr]ds = ds$, and $\int_0^{2\pi}ds = 2\pi \ne 0$. The contradiction shows that $\omega$ is not exact. The angle grows by $2\pi$ once around the origin, and no single-valued smooth function can do that.
>
> *What it shows.* Closed implies exact on star-shaped regions ([[#^thm-c2-6-2|Theorem §C2.6.2]]), not on a region with a hole: the converse of $d^2 = 0$ depends on the topology, measured by $H^1(\mathbf R^2\setminus\{0\}) \ne 0$ ([[#^def-c2-6-5|Def. §C2.6.5]]; on the circle, [[§83 The First Cohomology of the Circle#^prop-83-4|591 Prop. §83.4]]). In physics this is the field around a line current or a solenoid: curl-free outside, yet with nonzero circulation ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^cau-b1-2-1|EM Caution: Curl-free is not enough on a region with a hole]]). The integral of a one-form along a curve as the integral of its pull-back is made general in [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-3|Def. §C2.7.3]]. One degree up, the field strength of a magnetic monopole is a closed two-form that is not exact on a sphere around the charge, so it needs two potentials related by a gauge transformation ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^ex-c11-1-2|Example §C11.1.2]], Step 4); a nonzero magnetic charge rules out a potential near the surface ([[§C11.5 Charges in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c11-5-3|Theorem §C11.5.3]], part 4).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.2, Example 3.10, eqs. (3.118)–(3.120) (printed pp. 65–66) · MATH 452 §39 · MATH 591 §83 · closedness, $\omega = d\theta$, the two arctangent potentials and $\oint\omega = 2\pi$ checked by script*

> [!caution] Caution: Why the arctangent does not settle exactness
> Couzens concludes that $\omega$ is not exact because its potential $\arctan(y/x)$ "is ill-defined along the line $x = 0$" (after (3.120)). That observation alone does not decide the question: $\arctan(y/x)$ is one potential among many, and $-\arctan(x/y)$ is smooth across $x = 0$ ([[#^ex-c2-6-2|Example §C2.6.2]]). Every formula for the angle fails somewhere, and what forces this is the integral around the origin, $\oint\omega = 2\pi \ne 0$. Couzens' conclusion is correct; the argument is incomplete. His circle example on the previous page, $d\phi$ on $S^1$, rests on the same fact.
>

> [!example] Example §C2.6.3: The Field Strength F = dA and dF = 0
> *Setup.* Spacetime $M$ with any chart $x^\mu$, flat or curved; no metric is used until Step 3. The electromagnetic potential is a one-form $A = A_\mu\,dx^\mu$, and the field strength is the two-form $F = dA$.
>
> *Step 1* (components). By [[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 1, with $r = 1$,
>
> $$
> F = \tfrac12F_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu\wedge dx^\nu, \qquad F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu ,
> $$
>
> the definition of the field tensor at level B ([[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-2|REL Def. §B4.2.2]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$: $A_\mu$ there is $-A_\mu$ here, so every $F_{\mu\nu}$ flips sign, [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]], part 4; the equations below are linear and homogeneous in $F$, so they are unaffected).
>
> *Step 2* ($dF = 0$). $dF = d(dA) = 0$ ([[#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], part 4). In components (part 1 with $r = 2$),
>
> $$
> (dF)_{\lambda\mu\nu} = \partial_\lambda F_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu F_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_\nu F_{\lambda\mu} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> the Bianchi identity, the homogeneous Maxwell equations ([[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-4|EM Theorem §C1.2.4]]; [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^thm-b4-2-7|REL Theorem §B4.2.7]]). A three-form on spacetime has $\binom43 = 4$ independent components: four equations.
>
> *Step 3* (in terms of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$). In an inertial chart of Minkowski spacetime with $A_\mu = (-V, \vec A)$ and $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial_t\vec A$, $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$: $F_{0i} = \partial_tA_i + \partial_iV = -E_i$ and $F_{ij} = \partial_iA_j - \partial_jA_i = \epsilon_{ijk}B_k$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]], part 4). Summing over $\mu < \nu$ ([[#^def-c2-6-2|Def. §C2.6.2]], expansion),
>
> $$
> F = -dt\wedge\mathcal E + \mathcal B, \qquad \mathcal E = E_x\,dx + E_y\,dy + E_z\,dz, \qquad \mathcal B = B_x\,dy\wedge dz + B_y\,dz\wedge dx + B_z\,dx\wedge dy .
> $$
>
> Split $d = dt\wedge\partial_t + d_s$, with $d_s$ the exterior derivative in $x, y, z$ alone. By the Leibniz rule and $d(dt) = 0$, $d(dt\wedge\mathcal E) = -dt\wedge d\mathcal E = -dt\wedge d_s\mathcal E$ (the $dt\wedge dt$ term vanishes), and $d\mathcal B = dt\wedge\partial_t\mathcal B + d_s\mathcal B$. So
>
> $$
> dF = dt\wedge\bigl(d_s\mathcal E + \partial_t\mathcal B\bigr) + d_s\mathcal B .
> $$
>
> By [[#^ex-c2-6-1|Example §C2.6.1]], $d_s\mathcal B = (\nabla\cdot\vec B)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$ and $d_s\mathcal E$ is the two-form of $\nabla\times\vec E$. The two parts of $dF$ contain different basis forms, so $dF = 0$ says
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\vec B = 0, \qquad \nabla\times\vec E + \partial_t\vec B = 0 ,
> $$
>
> Gauss's law for magnetism and Faraday's law. Restored: $x^0 = ct$, $F_{0i} = -E_i/c$.
>
> *Step 4* (gauge invariance). $A \to A + d\lambda$ gives $F \to d(A + d\lambda) = dA + d(d\lambda) = F$ by $d^2 = 0$ (level B: [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-1|REL Def. §B4.2.1]]).
>
> *Step 5* (the converse). On a star-shaped region, every two-form with $dF = 0$ is $dA$, with $A$ unique up to $d\lambda$ ([[#^thm-c2-6-2|Theorem §C2.6.2]], parts 1 and 3; [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-5|EM Theorem §C1.2.5]]). Around a line current or a monopole the region has a hole, and the converse can fail ([[#^ex-c2-6-2|Example §C2.6.2]]).
>
> *Lesson.* Steps 1, 2, 4 and 5 used no metric, no connection and no inertial frame. The homogeneous Maxwell equations $dF = 0$, with partial derivatives, therefore hold in every chart of a curved spacetime exactly as written; only the inhomogeneous pair needs the metric ([[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]]).
>

> [!remark]- Connections
> - **Layer change of the Bianchi identity.** Electromagnetism proves the homogeneous pair by cancelling six terms in pairs ([[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-4|EM Theorem §C1.2.4]]); here it is $d^2 = 0$ for $F = dA$, valid in any chart ([[#^ex-c2-6-3|Example §C2.6.3]]). Its converse, the potential of [[§C1.2 The Field Equations and the Bianchi Identity#^thm-c1-2-5|EM Theorem §C1.2.5]], is the case $r = 2$, $n = 4$ of the Poincaré lemma ([[#^thm-c2-6-2|Theorem §C2.6.2]]).
> - **Vector calculus.** Gradient, curl and divergence are $d$ in degrees $0$, $1$, $2$ on $\mathbf R^3$ ([[#^ex-c2-6-1|Example §C2.6.1]]; [[§38 The Exterior Derivative|452 §38]]); "curl-free implies gradient" is the Poincaré lemma for one-forms ([[§39 Closed and Exact Forms#^prop-39-7|452 Prop. §39.7]], stated there and proved here for star-shaped domains in every degree).
> - **Tensor derivatives.** $d$ is the second tensorial derivative of C2, after the Lie derivative of [[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors|§C2.5]]; Cartan's formula ([[#^thm-c2-6-3|Theorem §C2.6.3]]) relates the two on forms. The third, the covariant derivative, needs a connection ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative|§C3.5]]); for forms, the antisymmetrized covariant derivative equals $d$ ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative|§C3.5]]).
> - **Integration.** Top forms are the integrands of [[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem|§C2.7]], where Stokes' theorem relates $d$ to boundaries; the $2\pi$ of [[#^ex-c2-6-2|Example §C2.6.2]] is the integral of a closed form over a curve that bounds no region of $M$.
> - **Mechanics.** Hamilton's equations, the Poisson bracket and Liouville's theorem in the language of a symplectic form: [[#^rem-c2-6-1|Remark: Hamiltonian mechanics on a symplectic manifold]] ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-3|CM Theorem §B7.2.3]]).
> - **Mathematics.** The home of forms is MATH 591, Chapter 11: linear forms [[§75 Multilinear Algebra|591 §75]], wedge product [[§77 The Wedge Product|591 §77]]–[[§78 Properties of the Wedge Product|591 §78]], pull-backs [[§79 Pullbacks of Linear Forms|591 §79]]–[[§80 Differential Forms on Manifolds|591 §80]], exterior derivative [[§81 The Exterior Derivative|591 §81]], de Rham cohomology [[§82 De Rham Cohomology|591 §82]]–[[§83 The First Cohomology of the Circle|591 §83]], contraction [[§84 Contraction by Vector Fields|591 §84]]. It writes forms as sums over increasing index sets, $\sum_Af_A\,dx^A$, and contraction as $\iota_X$; the wedge convention is the same ([[#^cau-c2-6-1|Caution: Normalizations of the wedge product]]).
> - **Forward.** The volume form $\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x$ and the Hodge star ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor|§C3.3]]–[[§C3.4 The Hodge Star and the Codifferential|§C3.4]]); Maxwell's inhomogeneous equations in curved spacetime ([[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]]); Killing vectors as fields with $\mathcal L_Xg = 0$ ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors|§C3.13]]).
