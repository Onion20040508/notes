Physics is full of integrals over regions of spacetime and over surfaces in it. A field theory is specified by an action $S = \int\mathcal L\,d^4x$ ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), and the action of general relativity will be one ([[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action|§C5.2]]). A charge is the integral of a density over a slice of constant time, and the theorems of Gauss and Stokes trade the integral of a derivative over a region for an integral over its boundary ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|EM Theorem §B1.1.3]]). In the inertial coordinates of Minkowski spacetime these integrals mean what they say. On a manifold coordinates are labels on chart domains ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]), and none is preferred. The question of this section is therefore: *what can be integrated over a manifold so that the answer does not depend on the chart, and what becomes of the integral theorems of vector calculus?*

Try the obvious rule first: integrate a function against the coordinates of whatever chart is at hand. Take the function $f = 1$ on the closed unit disk $D$ of the plane. In the chart $(x, y)$ the rule gives the area,

$$
\int_D 1\;dx\,dy = \int_{-1}^{1}\Bigl(\int_{-\sqrt{1 - x^2}}^{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}dy\Bigr)dx = \int_{-1}^{1}2\sqrt{1 - x^2}\,dx = \pi .
$$

In the polar chart $(r, \theta)$, which covers $D$ except for the ray $\theta = 0$ and the centre, the same function and the same rule give

$$
\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}1\;d\theta\,dr = 2\pi .
$$

The rule assigns two numbers to one function on one region. The change-of-variables theorem says why ([[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^thm-25-6|452 Thm. §25.6]]): an ordinary integral changes by the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant, $\int_D f\,dx\,dy = \int f\,\bigl|\det\partial(x, y)/\partial(r, \theta)\bigr|\,dr\,d\theta = \int f\,r\,dr\,d\theta$, and a function does not carry that factor. A top form does. By the rules of [[§C2.6 Differential Forms|§C2.6]], with $dx = \cos\theta\,dr - r\sin\theta\,d\theta$ and $dy = \sin\theta\,dr + r\cos\theta\,d\theta$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]),

$$
dx\wedge dy = (\cos\theta\,dr - r\sin\theta\,d\theta)\wedge(\sin\theta\,dr + r\cos\theta\,d\theta) = r\cos^2\theta\,dr\wedge d\theta - r\sin^2\theta\,d\theta\wedge dr = r\,dr\wedge d\theta ,
$$

using $dr\wedge dr = d\theta\wedge d\theta = 0$ and $d\theta\wedge dr = -dr\wedge d\theta$. The coefficient of the two-form $dx\wedge dy$ changes from $1$ to $r$, exactly the factor the integral needs. A second computation shows what is still missing. In the chart $(y, x)$, with the coordinates listed in the other order, $dx\wedge dy = -\,dy\wedge dx$: the coefficient is $-1$, and the ordinary integral of $-1$ over $D$ is $-\pi$. Forms change with the determinant, ordinary integrals with its absolute value. The two agree only for charts whose Jacobian determinant is positive, and choosing such charts consistently is an orientation.

The section builds the answer in order. It defines orientations ([[#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]]) and shows that a top form changes by the Jacobian determinant, so that its chart integrals agree for charts of one orientation ([[#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]]). It glues chart integrals with a partition of unity into the integral of a top form over the manifold ([[#^def-c2-7-2|Def. §C2.7.2]]) and proves that the result depends on nothing but the form and the orientation ([[#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]]). It integrates forms over submanifolds by pulling them back ([[#^def-c2-7-3|Def. §C2.7.3]]), orients the boundary of a manifold ([[#^def-c2-7-4|Def. §C2.7.4]], [[#^def-c2-7-5|Def. §C2.7.5]]) and proves Stokes' theorem $\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M}\omega$ ([[#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]]). Its form for densities, the version used in physics to drop total derivatives and to conserve charges, closes the section ([[#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]]). What a bare manifold cannot do, integrate a *function*, is the subject of [[#^rem-c2-7-1|Remark: No canonical volume without a metric]].

**Setup.** $M$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $m \ge 1$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]); for spacetime $m = 4$. Because $M$ is Hausdorff and second countable, partitions of unity subordinate to any open cover exist ([[§65 Partitions of Unity#^thm-65-7|591 Thm. §65.7]]). Manifolds with boundary are as in [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-7|Def. §C2.1.7]]: their charts take values in the closed half-space $H^m = \{x \in \mathbf R^m : x^m \ge 0\}$ (written $\mathbf H^n$ there), the boundary points are those with $x^m = 0$ (GR1's convention, p. 72), and $\partial M$ is a manifold of dimension $m - 1$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-4|Theorem §C2.1.4]]). The forms are smooth $m$-forms, called **top forms**, and $(m-1)$-forms ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]]). Integrals over subsets of $\mathbf R^m$ are ordinary integrals of continuous functions of compact support, which always converge; the tools are the change-of-variables theorem ([[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^thm-25-6|452 Thm. §25.6]]), Fubini's theorem ([[§23 Fubini's Theorem#^thm-23-1|452 Thm. §23.1]], in $m$ variables) and the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]). No metric is used: every statement holds on any manifold, Riemannian or Lorentzian. The theorems are exact; the numbers in the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): no metric and no signature enter this section. Coordinates of a chart are $x^\mu$, $\mu = 1, \dots, m$ (for spacetime $\mu = 0, \dots, 3$ with $x^0 = t$); summation convention. $d^mx = dx^1\cdots dx^m$ is the measure of an ordinary integral over a subset of $\mathbf R^m$; $dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m$ is a top form. A top form is written $\omega = h\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m$ with $h = \omega_{1\dots m}$; the wedge product is normalized so that $(dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m)(\partial_1, \dots, \partial_m) = 1$, as in GR1 (3.102) and in MATH 591 ([[§77 The Wedge Product#^def-77-1|591 Def. §77.1]]; the two normalizations agree for this; [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^cau-c2-6-1|§C2.6, Caution: Normalizations of the wedge product]]).

## Orientation and the transformation law of top forms

The second computation of the opening is a statement about pairs of charts: the sign of the determinant of their transition function. An orientation is a choice of charts for which that sign is always positive.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.7.1: Orientation; Oriented Atlas
> *Structure.* An **orientation of a real vector space** $V$ of dimension $m \ge 1$ is a class of ordered bases of $V$: two bases $(e_\mu)$ and $(\tilde e_\nu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu e_\mu)$ lie in the same class when $\det\Lambda > 0$. Since $\det$ is multiplicative and never zero on a change of basis, there are exactly two classes. An orientation of a zero-dimensional space is a sign, $+1$ or $-1$.
>
> *On a manifold.* Two charts $(U, x^\mu)$ and $(\tilde U, y^\nu)$ of $M$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]) are **orientation-compatible** if the Jacobian determinant of their transition function ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]) is positive on the overlap,
>
> $$
> \det\Bigl(\frac{\partial y^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}\Bigr) > 0 \quad\text{on } U\cap\tilde U .
> $$
>
> The determinant never vanishes there ([[§17 Differentiable Structures#^lem-17-1|591 Lem. §17.1]]). Because the coordinate bases are related by $\partial/\partial y^\nu = (\partial x^\mu/\partial y^\nu)\,\partial/\partial x^\mu$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], part 1), compatible charts give coordinate bases of the same class at every point of the overlap (GR1 (3.137)).
> 1. An **oriented atlas** is an atlas ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-2|Def. §C2.1.2]]) whose charts are pairwise orientation-compatible.
> 2. $M$ is **orientable** if it has an oriented atlas. An **orientation** of $M$ is the set of all charts orientation-compatible with every chart of a given oriented atlas; these charts are pairwise compatible (at a point $p$ of an overlap, $\det(\partial y/\partial z) = \det(\partial y/\partial x)\det(\partial x/\partial z) > 0$ with $x$ a chart of the atlas at $p$). They are the **positively oriented** charts, and $M$ with an orientation is an **oriented manifold**.
> 3. At each point $p$ an orientation of $M$ selects one orientation of $T_pM$: the class of the coordinate bases of positively oriented charts.
> 4. Replacing $x^1$ by $-x^1$ in every positively oriented chart gives the **opposite orientation**; $M$ with it is written $-M$.
>
> The plane with its Cartesian chart is oriented by $(x, y)$; the polar chart is positively oriented for it, since $\det\partial(x, y)/\partial(r, \theta) = r > 0$; the chart $(y, x)$ is not. The Möbius strip is not orientable ([[#^ex-c2-7-5|Example §C2.7.5]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.4, Definitions 31–32, eq. (3.137), Fig. 10 (pp. 69–70)*

^def-c2-7-1

The transformation law of top forms is what makes this the right notion: it puts the determinant itself, sign included, into the coefficient of the form.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.7.1: Top Forms Transform with the Jacobian Determinant
> Let $(U, x^\mu)$ and $(\tilde U, y^\nu)$ be charts of $M$ with overlap $W = U\cap\tilde U \neq \varnothing$, and let $\omega$ be a top form, $\omega = h\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m$ on $U$ and $\omega = \tilde h\,dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^m$ on $\tilde U$.
> 1. *(Transformation law.)* On $W$
>
> $$
> dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m = \det\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial y^\nu}\Bigr)\,dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^m , \qquad \tilde h = \det\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial y^\nu}\Bigr)\,h .
> $$
>
> 2. *(⚑ What a chart integral sees.)* Let $\omega$ have compact support inside $W$. If $\det(\partial x^\mu/\partial y^\nu) > 0$ on $W$, the ordinary integrals of the two coefficients agree,
>
> $$
> \int_{x(W)}h\;d^mx = \int_{y(W)}\tilde h\;d^my ;
> $$
>
> if the determinant is negative on $W$, they differ by a sign. The coefficient of a top form is therefore a scalar density of weight $+1$ in d'Inverno's convention, $\tilde h = J\,h$ with $J = \det(\partial x/\partial y)$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-6|Def. §C2.3.6]]), and its integral is chart-independent exactly under orientation-preserving changes of chart.
> 3. *(Orientations from top forms.)* If $\omega$ vanishes nowhere, the charts in which its coefficient is positive form an oriented atlas. Conversely, every orientable manifold carries a nowhere-vanishing top form (GR1 calls it a *volume form*). Two nowhere-vanishing top forms $\omega$ and $\omega' = f\,\omega$ define the same orientation if and only if $f > 0$.
>
> *Model:* none (a statement about a bare manifold); *applies:* any smooth manifold, with or without boundary, charts of either orientation in part 1.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.4, eqs. (3.138)–(3.139) and text (pp. 69–70) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §7.1, eq. (7.1) (density of weight $W$, p. 115) · [[§80 Differential Forms on Manifolds#^cor-80-6|591 Cor. §80.6]] (part 1) · parts 2–3: proof written here*

^thm-c2-7-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (one-forms). Each $x^\mu$ is a smooth function on $W$, and its differential in the chart $y$ is ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]; [[§33 Covectors in Coordinates#^lem-33-1|591 Lem. §33.1]])
>
> $$
> dx^\mu = \frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial y^\nu}\,dy^\nu .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the wedge of $m$ one-forms). Insert Step 1 into each factor and expand by multilinearity of $\wedge$:
>
> $$
> dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m = \sum_{\nu_1, \dots, \nu_m}\frac{\partial x^1}{\partial y^{\nu_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial x^m}{\partial y^{\nu_m}}\;dy^{\nu_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^{\nu_m} .
> $$
>
> A term with two equal indices contains $dy^\nu\wedge dy^\nu = 0$. In the remaining terms $(\nu_1, \dots, \nu_m) = (\sigma(1), \dots, \sigma(m))$ for a permutation $\sigma$, and reordering the one-forms gives $dy^{\sigma(1)}\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^{\sigma(m)} = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\,dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^m$. Hence
>
> $$
> dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m = \Bigl(\sum_\sigma\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{\mu = 1}^m\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial y^{\sigma(\mu)}}\Bigr)dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^m = \det\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial y^\nu}\Bigr)dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^m ,
> $$
>
> the Leibniz formula for the determinant ([[§78 Properties of the Wedge Product#^lem-78-2|591 Lem. §78.2]]). For $m = 2$, with $a^\mu{}_\nu = \partial x^\mu/\partial y^\nu$: $(a^1{}_1dy^1 + a^1{}_2dy^2)\wedge(a^2{}_1dy^1 + a^2{}_2dy^2) = (a^1{}_1a^2{}_2 - a^1{}_2a^2{}_1)\,dy^1\wedge dy^2$. (The cases $m = 2, 3$ were expanded symbolically.)
>
> **Step 3** (coefficients). Multiply Step 2 by $h$: $\omega = h\det(\partial x/\partial y)\,dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^m$. The coefficient of a top form in a chart is unique (the one-element basis of top forms at each point, [[§75 Multilinear Algebra#^cor-75-7|591 Cor. §75.7]]), so $\tilde h = \det(\partial x/\partial y)\,h$. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 4** (change of variables). The transition map $\Phi = x\circ y^{-1} : y(W) \to x(W)$ is a diffeomorphism with Jacobian matrix $\partial x^\mu/\partial y^\nu$. The function $h$, read in the chart $x$, is continuous with compact support in $x(W)$. The change-of-variables theorem ([[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^thm-25-6|452 Thm. §25.6]]) gives
>
> $$
> \int_{x(W)}h\;d^mx = \int_{y(W)}h\bigl(\Phi(y)\bigr)\,\Bigl|\det\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial y^\nu}\Bigr|\;d^my .
> $$
>
> If the determinant is positive, $|\det| = \det$ and the integrand is $\tilde h$ by Step 3. If it is negative, $|\det| = -\det$ and the integrand is $-\tilde h$. The transformation law $\tilde h = J\,h$ with $J = \det(\partial x/\partial y)$ is d'Inverno's law (7.1) for a scalar density of weight $W = 1$. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 5** (a nowhere-vanishing form orients $M$). Let $\omega$ vanish nowhere and let $(U, x)$ be a chart with $U$ connected (charts can be restricted to connected neighbourhoods of each point). The coefficient $h$ is continuous and never zero on $U$, so it has one sign there. If it is negative, the chart $(-x^1, x^2, \dots, x^m)$ has Jacobian determinant $-1$ relative to $x$, and by Step 3 the coefficient of $\omega$ in it is $-h > 0$. So charts with positive coefficient cover $M$. For two of them, Step 3 gives $\tilde h = \det(\partial x/\partial y)\,h$ with $h, \tilde h > 0$, hence $\det(\partial x/\partial y) > 0$ on the overlap: they form an oriented atlas.
>
> **Step 6** (an orientable manifold has one). Let $\{(U_i, x_i)\}$ be an oriented atlas and $\{\psi_i\}$ a partition of unity subordinate to $\{U_i\}$ ([[§65 Partitions of Unity#^def-65-3|591 Def. §65.3]], [[§65 Partitions of Unity#^thm-65-7|591 Thm. §65.7]]). Each $\psi_i\,dx_i^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_i^m$, extended by zero outside $U_i$, is a smooth top form on $M$ (its support lies in $\operatorname{supp}\psi_i \subset U_i$), and the sum
>
> $$
> \omega = \sum_i\psi_i\,dx_i^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_i^m
> $$
>
> is locally finite, hence smooth ([[§65 Partitions of Unity#^lem-65-2|591 Lem. §65.2]]). At a point $p$, choose a chart $(U_j, x_j)$ of the atlas containing $p$ and express each term by Step 2:
>
> $$
> \omega_p = \Bigl(\sum_i\psi_i(p)\,\det\Bigl(\frac{\partial x_i^\mu}{\partial x_j^\nu}\Bigr)(p)\Bigr)\,dx_j^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_j^m\big|_p .
> $$
>
> A term with $\psi_i(p) > 0$ has $p \in U_i$ and a positive determinant, because the atlas is oriented; every $\psi_i(p) \ge 0$ and $\sum_i\psi_i(p) = 1$, so at least one term is positive and none is negative. The coefficient is positive and $\omega_p \neq 0$.
>
> **Step 7** (same orientation). If $\omega$ and $\omega' = f\omega$ vanish nowhere, then $f = h'/h$ in any chart, a continuous nowhere-zero function. Where $f > 0$ the two forms are positive in the same charts; where $f < 0$ a chart positive for one is negative for the other. Part 3 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The Jacobian factor that spoiled the naive integral of the opening is exactly the factor by which the coefficient of a top form changes (Step 4): the integrand of an ordinary integral should be a top form, or equivalently a density of weight $1$, not a function.
> - Forms carry the determinant, ordinary integrals its absolute value. The mismatch is a sign, and it disappears for charts of one orientation; this is why integration of forms needs an oriented manifold ([[#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]]).
> - Part 3 makes the orientation visible: it is the sign of any nowhere-vanishing top form. A manifold without such a form, the Möbius strip, cannot be oriented ([[#^ex-c2-7-5|Example §C2.7.5]]).

## The integral of a top form

A top form supported in one positively oriented chart now has an unambiguous integral: the ordinary integral of its coefficient. A form spread over several charts is cut into pieces that each fit into one chart, with functions that add up to $1$, a partition of unity ([[§65 Partitions of Unity#^def-65-2|591 Def. §65.2]]).

> [!definition] Definition §C2.7.2: Integral of a Top Form
> *Structure.* The integral is a linear map from the top forms of compact support on an oriented manifold $M$ ([[#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]]) to $\mathbf R$, $\omega \mapsto \int_M\omega$. The **support** of $\omega$ is the closure of the set where $\omega \neq 0$ ([[§56 Vector Fields#^def-56-10|591 Def. §56.10]]); it is assumed [[§18 Compact Spaces#^def-18-2|compact]].
> 1. *(In one chart.)* If $\operatorname{supp}\omega$ lies in the domain of a positively oriented chart $(U, x^\mu)$, where $\omega = h\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m$,
>
> $$
> \int_M\omega \equiv \int_{x(U)}h\;d^mx ,
> $$
>
> with $h$ read as a function of the coordinates ($h\circ x^{-1}$ on the open set $x(U) \subset \mathbf R^m$): an ordinary integral of a continuous function with compact support in $x(U)$ (GR1 (3.140)).
> 2. *(On $M$.)* In general, choose an oriented atlas $\{(U_i, x_i)\}$ of the orientation and a partition of unity $\{\psi_i\}$ subordinate to $\{U_i\}$ ([[§65 Partitions of Unity#^def-65-3|591 Def. §65.3]]; it exists, [[§65 Partitions of Unity#^thm-65-7|591 Thm. §65.7]]), and set
>
> $$
> \int_M\omega \equiv \sum_i\int_M\psi_i\,\omega ,
> $$
>
> each term defined by part 1, since $\operatorname{supp}(\psi_i\omega) \subset \operatorname{supp}\psi_i\cap\operatorname{supp}\omega$ is compact and lies in $U_i$. Only finitely many terms are nonzero (Step 1 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]]). (GR1 (3.141)–(3.142).)
> 3. *(Functions.)* A function $f$ of compact support is integrated against a chosen nowhere-vanishing top form $\Omega$ ([[#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 3) as $\int_Mf\,\Omega$; the choice of $\Omega$ is extra structure ([[#^rem-c2-7-1|Remark: No canonical volume without a metric]]).
> 4. *(Boundary.)* On a manifold with boundary the same definitions apply, with boundary charts whose images are relatively open subsets of $H^m$. For $m \ge 2$ a boundary chart can always be made positively oriented by replacing $x^1$ with $-x^1$, which preserves $H^m$. For $m = 1$ this is impossible at some boundary points (at the right end $b$ of $[a, b]$ every boundary chart, such as $x = b - t$, reverses the orientation); there the chart integral is taken with a minus sign, as [[#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]], part 3, prescribes.
>
> That the result does not depend on the chart in part 1 or on the atlas and partition in part 2 is [[#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.4, eqs. (3.140)–(3.142), Definition 33 (pp. 70–71) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §7.4, eq. (7.15) (integral of a scalar density, p. 120)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.7.2: The Integral Does Not Depend on the Oriented Atlas or the Partition
> Let $M$ be an oriented manifold, possibly with boundary, and $\omega$ a top form with compact support.
> 1. *(Chart.)* In [[#^def-c2-7-2|Def. §C2.7.2]], part 1, two positively oriented charts whose domains both contain $\operatorname{supp}\omega$ give the same integral.
> 2. *(Atlas and partition.)* Part 2 of the definition gives the same number for every oriented atlas of the orientation and every subordinate partition of unity, and it agrees with part 1 when $\operatorname{supp}\omega$ lies in one chart.
> 3. *(Properties.)* $\int_M(\omega + c\,\omega') = \int_M\omega + c\int_M\omega'$; reversing the orientation reverses the sign, $\int_{-M}\omega = -\int_M\omega$; and if a positively oriented chart $(V, y)$ covers $M$ up to a closed set of measure zero ([[§62 Sets of Measure Zero#^def-62-2|591 Def. §62.2]]), then $\int_M\omega = \int_{y(V)}h_y\,d^my$ with $h_y$ the coefficient of $\omega$ in that chart.
> 4. *(⚑ Diffeomorphism invariance.)* If $F : N \to M$ is an orientation-preserving diffeomorphism ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-6|Def. §C2.1.6]]; positively oriented charts of $M$ composed with $F$ are positively oriented on $N$), then $\int_NF^{\ast}\omega = \int_M\omega$, with the pull-back of [[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]].
>
> Part 3 is what makes coordinates with harmless singularities usable: spherical coordinates miss a half great circle of $S^2$, a set of measure zero ([[#^ex-c2-7-4|Example §C2.7.4]]). Part 4 says that an action built from a top form takes the same value on a configuration and on its image under any diffeomorphism, the starting point of the gauge freedom of general relativity ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^rem-c2-4-1|§C2.4, Remark: Active and passive readings of a diffeomorphism]]; [[§C5.4 Diffeomorphism Invariance|§C5.4]]).
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* every oriented manifold, with or without boundary; $\omega$ continuous with compact support suffices.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.4, after eq. (3.142) ("the integral as defined above stays the same", p. 71; stated without proof) · proof written here*

^thm-c2-7-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (finitely many terms). The supports $\{\operatorname{supp}\psi_i\}$ are locally finite ([[§65 Partitions of Unity#^def-65-2|591 Def. §65.2]]): each $p \in \operatorname{supp}\omega$ has an open neighbourhood $O_p$ that meets only finitely many of them. The $O_p$ cover the compact set $\operatorname{supp}\omega$, so finitely many, $O_{p_1}, \dots, O_{p_n}$, cover it, and only the finitely many $\psi_i$ whose supports meet one of these can have $\psi_i\omega \neq 0$. The sum in Def. §C2.7.2, part 2, is a finite sum.
>
> **Step 2** (part 1). Let $(U, x)$ and $(\tilde U, y)$ be positively oriented with $\operatorname{supp}\omega \subset U\cap\tilde U = W$. Then $\det(\partial x/\partial y) > 0$ on $W$, and [[#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 2, gives $\int_{x(W)}h\,d^mx = \int_{y(W)}\tilde h\,d^my$. The integrands vanish outside $x(\operatorname{supp}\omega)$ and $y(\operatorname{supp}\omega)$, so the integrals over $x(U)$ and $y(\tilde U)$ are the same numbers.
>
> **Step 3** (part 2). Let $\{(U_i, x_i), \psi_i\}$ and $\{(V_j, y_j), \chi_j\}$ be two oriented atlases of the same orientation with subordinate partitions of unity. Since $\sum_j\chi_j = 1$, and only finitely many $\chi_j$ are nonzero on the compact set $\operatorname{supp}(\psi_i\omega)$ (Step 1),
>
> $$
> \psi_i\,\omega = \sum_j\chi_j\psi_i\,\omega \qquad\text{(a finite sum)} .
> $$
>
> The integral of part 1 is linear (linearity of ordinary integrals), so $\int_M\psi_i\omega = \sum_j\int_M\chi_j\psi_i\omega$, each term computed in the chart $x_i$. The form $\chi_j\psi_i\omega$ has compact support in $U_i\cap V_j$, so by Step 2 its integral is the same in the chart $y_j$. Exchanging the two finite sums and using $\sum_i\psi_i = 1$:
>
> $$
> \sum_i\int_M\psi_i\,\omega = \sum_i\sum_j\int_M\chi_j\psi_i\,\omega = \sum_j\int_M\chi_j\Bigl(\sum_i\psi_i\Bigr)\omega = \sum_j\int_M\chi_j\,\omega .
> $$
>
> The left side is the integral built from the first atlas, the right side the one built from the second. If $\operatorname{supp}\omega \subset U$ for one positively oriented chart, the same computation with the single chart $U$ in place of the second atlas gives $\sum_i\int_M\psi_i\omega = \int_M(\sum_i\psi_i)\omega = \int_M\omega$ in the sense of part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (part 3: linearity and reversal). Linearity follows from Def. §C2.7.2, part 2, and the linearity of ordinary integrals. For $-M$, the charts $\bar x = (-x^1, x^2, \dots, x^m)$ are positively oriented. By [[#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 1, with $\det(\partial x/\partial\bar x) = -1$, the coefficient in $\bar x$ is $\bar h(\bar x) = -h(x)$, and the reflection $\bar x^1 = -x^1$ has $|\det| = 1$, so
>
> $$
> \int_{\bar x(U)}\bar h\;d^m\bar x = \int_{x(U)}\bigl(-h\bigr)\;d^mx = -\int_{x(U)}h\;d^mx .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (part 3: a chart up to measure zero). Let $C = M\setminus V$, closed and of measure zero, so that $x_i(U_i\cap C)$ has measure zero in $\mathbf R^m$ for every chart ([[§62 Sets of Measure Zero#^prop-62-4|591 Prop. §62.4]]). Then, with $h_i$ the coefficient of $\omega$ in $x_i$,
>
> $$
> \int_M\psi_i\,\omega = \int_{x_i(U_i)}\psi_i h_i\;d^mx = \int_{x_i(U_i\cap V)}\psi_i h_i\;d^mx = \int_{y(U_i\cap V)}\psi_i h_y\;d^my ,
> $$
>
> the second equality because a set of measure zero does not change a (Lebesgue) integral, the third by [[#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 2, on $U_i\cap V$ (the change-of-variables theorem on an open set; the support of $\psi_ih_i$ restricted to the open set $U_i \cap V$ need not be compact there, so the theorem is used in its Lebesgue form, obtained from [[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^thm-25-6|452 Thm. §25.6]] by exhausting the open set with compact sets). Summing over the finitely many $i$ and using $\sum_i\psi_i = 1$ on $V$ gives $\int_M\omega = \int_{y(V)}h_y\,d^my$.
>
> **Step 6** (part 4). Let $\{(U_i, x_i)\}$ be a positively oriented atlas of $M$ with partition $\{\psi_i\}$. Then $\{(F^{-1}(U_i), x_i\circ F)\}$ is a positively oriented atlas of $N$ and $\{\psi_i\circ F\}$ a subordinate partition of unity. Write $\omega = h_i\,dx_i^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_i^m$ on $U_i$. Pull-back commutes with $d$ and $\wedge$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]]; [[§80 Differential Forms on Manifolds#^prop-80-5|591 Prop. §80.5]]), so
>
> $$
> F^{\ast}(\psi_i\,\omega) = (\psi_i\circ F)(h_i\circ F)\;d(x_i^1\circ F)\wedge\cdots\wedge d(x_i^m\circ F) :
> $$
>
> in the chart $x_i\circ F$ the coefficient of $F^{\ast}(\psi_i\omega)$ is the function $\psi_ih_i$ evaluated at the same coordinate values. The two chart integrals are the same ordinary integral over $x_i(U_i)$; summing over $i$ gives $\int_NF^{\ast}\omega = \int_M\omega$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Every independence statement reduces to one fact, the change-of-variables theorem with a positive Jacobian determinant (Step 2); the partitions of unity only organize the bookkeeping, and compactness keeps every sum finite (Step 1).
> - In coordinates the integral is the ordinary integral of the coefficient; part 3 licenses computing it in a single chart that misses a set of measure zero, which is how every integral over a sphere or over spacetime in these notes will be done.

Every manifold in the sense of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]] is paracompact ([[§65 Partitions of Unity#^thm-65-5|591 Thm. §65.5]]), so GR1's standing assumption (footnote 16) is automatically satisfied.

Its result is still right, because the chart integrals of these bounded integrands over bounded parameter intervals converge; [[#^ex-c2-7-1|Example §C2.7.1]] repeats the computation and then redoes it with a partition that is subordinate in the strict sense.

> [!remark] Remark: No canonical volume without a metric
> [[#^def-c2-7-2|Def. §C2.7.2]] integrates top forms, not functions. To integrate a function $f$ one needs a nowhere-vanishing top form $\Omega = h(x)\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m$ to multiply it with, and on a bare manifold nothing selects one: with $\Omega$ every $g\,\Omega$ with $g > 0$ is an equally good choice, and the coefficient $h(x)$ changes from chart to chart with the Jacobian ([[#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]]), so there is no rule like "$h = 1$" that survives a change of chart. GR1 makes the same point (p. 72): the function $h(x)$ is left arbitrary until a metric is given. Level B never met the problem because it used only inertial coordinates, related by Lorentz transformations with $\det\Lambda = \pm1$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), under which $d^4x$ is invariant. The metric of chapter C3 provides the canonical choice $\sqrt{|g|}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m$, whose coefficient $\sqrt{|g|}$ is a density of weight $1$ ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor|§C3.3]]). Until then an action $\int\mathcal L\,d^4x$ with a scalar $\mathcal L$ cannot even be written down in general coordinates.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.4 (p. 72); §4.1.3, eq. (4.23) (the metric volume form)*

## Integrals over submanifolds

A worldline, a surface of constant time, the sphere at infinity: physics also integrates over objects of lower dimension inside spacetime. A $k$-form can be integrated over a $k$-dimensional oriented manifold mapped into $M$ by pulling it back ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]]); the pulled-back form is a top form on the $k$-manifold, and [[#^def-c2-7-2|Def. §C2.7.2]] applies.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.7.3: Integral over an Oriented Submanifold
> *Structure.* Let $\Sigma$ be an oriented manifold of dimension $k$, $1 \le k \le m$, possibly with boundary, and $\sigma : \Sigma \to M$ a smooth map that is injective with injective push-forward $\sigma_{\ast} : T_p\Sigma \to T_{\sigma(p)}M$ at every point (GR1's sub-manifold; an embedding in the cases used here). For a $k$-form $\omega$ on $M$ such that $\sigma^{\ast}\omega$ has compact support on $\Sigma$, the **integral of $\omega$ over $\sigma(\Sigma)$** is
>
> $$
> \int_{\sigma(\Sigma)}\omega \equiv \int_\Sigma\sigma^{\ast}\omega ,
> $$
>
> the integral of the top form $\sigma^{\ast}\omega$ on $\Sigma$ ([[#^def-c2-7-2|Def. §C2.7.2]]).
>
> *Components.* If $u^a$, $a = 1, \dots, k$, is a positively oriented chart of $\Sigma$ and $\sigma$ reads $x^\mu = x^\mu(u)$, then with $\omega = \frac{1}{k!}\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_k}dx^{\mu_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{\mu_k}$ ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]])
>
> $$
> \sigma^{\ast}\omega = \omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_k}\bigl(x(u)\bigr)\,\frac{\partial x^{\mu_1}}{\partial u^1}\cdots\frac{\partial x^{\mu_k}}{\partial u^k}\;du^1\wedge\cdots\wedge du^k ,
> $$
>
> since the $k!$ orderings of $du^{a_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge du^{a_k}$ each contribute the same term by the antisymmetry of $\omega_{\mu_1\dots\mu_k}$. For $k = 1$ and a curve $x^\mu(\tau)$ this is the line integral
>
> $$
> \int_{\sigma(C)}A = \int d\tau\;A_\mu\bigl(x(\tau)\bigr)\,\frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau}
> $$
>
> (GR1 (3.146)–(3.147)), the form in which a worldline couples to an electromagnetic potential ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^ex-c2-4-3|Example §C2.4.3]]; [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^pr-b3-2-1|Principle §B3.2.1]], level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where the lowered components $A_\mu$ have the opposite sign and the integral is formed the same way). Reversing the orientation of $\Sigma$ (running the curve backwards) reverses the sign ([[#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]], part 3). For $k = m$ and $\sigma$ the inclusion of an open subset, the definition reduces to Def. §C2.7.2.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.4, eqs. (3.145)–(3.147) (p. 72) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §7.4, eqs. (7.16)–(7.17) (the same integral written with the parametric volume element $d\tau^{a_1\dots a_m}$, p. 120)*

## The boundary and Stokes' theorem

The fundamental theorem of calculus, Green's theorem, and the theorems of Gauss and Stokes share one shape: the integral of a derivative over a region equals the integral of the undifferentiated quantity over its boundary, with signs fixed by an orientation rule for the boundary ([[§40 The Generalized Stokes' Theorem#^thm-40-1|452 Thm. §40.1]], stated there without proof). On a manifold the derivative is the exterior derivative $d$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]]), which needs no metric, and the region is a manifold with boundary. What remains is the sign rule, which the two definitions below make precise: put the outward direction first.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.7.4: Outward-Pointing Vector
> Let $M$ be a manifold with boundary and $p \in \partial M$. A tangent vector $N \in T_pM$ is **outward-pointing** if in a boundary chart $(U, x^\mu)$ at $p$, with values in $H^m = \{x^m \ge 0\}$, its last component is negative, $N^m < 0$. The condition does not depend on the boundary chart ([[#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]], Step 1 of the derivation). In every boundary chart $N = -\partial_m$ is outward-pointing.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.4 (half-space charts, p. 72) · proof of chart independence written here*

^def-c2-7-4

> [!definition] Definition §C2.7.5: Induced Orientation of the Boundary
> Let $M$ be an oriented manifold with boundary ([[#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]]), $p \in \partial M$, and $N$ outward-pointing at $p$ ([[#^def-c2-7-4|Def. §C2.7.4]]).
> 1. For $m \ge 2$, an ordered basis $(v_1, \dots, v_{m-1})$ of $T_p(\partial M)$ is **positively oriented** if $(N, v_1, \dots, v_{m-1})$ is a positively oriented basis of $T_pM$: *outward first*.
> 2. For $m = 1$, the boundary is a set of points, and $p$ receives the sign $+1$ if $N$ is a positively oriented basis of $T_pM$, and $-1$ otherwise.
>
> This is the **induced** (or Stokes) **orientation** of $\partial M$. It does not depend on the choice of $N$, and it is an orientation of the manifold $\partial M$ ([[#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]], part 1). For a region of the plane with the orientation $(x, y)$ it runs the outer boundary counterclockwise and the boundary of a hole clockwise; for a surface in $\mathbf R^3$ oriented by a unit normal it is the right-hand rule of vector calculus, $\nu\times T = \mathbf n$ ([[§73 Stokes' Theorem in Calculus III#^lem-73-1|591 Lem. §73.1]]); for an interval $[a, b]$ it gives $b$ the sign $+1$ and $a$ the sign $-1$.
>
> *Source: MATH 452 §40 and MATH 591 §73 (the rule in vector calculus) · Couzens, GR1, §3.4.4, Theorem 4 (uses the orientation of $\partial M$ without defining it, p. 72)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.7.3: Stokes' Theorem
> Let $M$ be an oriented manifold of dimension $m \ge 1$ with boundary $\partial M$ (possibly empty), let $\partial M$ carry the induced orientation ([[#^def-c2-7-5|Def. §C2.7.5]]), and let $\omega$ be a smooth $(m-1)$-form on $M$ with **compact support**.
> 1. *(The boundary is oriented.)* $\partial M$ with the induced orientation is an oriented $(m-1)$-manifold. If $(U, x^\mu)$ is a positively oriented boundary chart, the chart $(x^1, \dots, x^{m-1})$ of $\partial M$ obtained by restriction to $x^m = 0$ is positively oriented if $m$ is even and negatively oriented if $m$ is odd: its sign is $(-1)^m$.
> 2. *(Stokes.)* With $\iota : \partial M \to M$ the inclusion,
>
> $$
> \int_Md\omega = \int_{\partial M}\iota^{\ast}\omega \qquad\Bigl(\text{written } \int_Md\omega = \int_{\partial M}\omega\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> For $m = 1$ the right side is the sum of $\pm\,\omega(p)$ over the boundary points with their signs.
> 3. *(⚑ No boundary.)* If $\partial M = \varnothing$, then $\int_Md\omega = 0$. In particular, on a compact manifold without boundary a top form with nonzero integral is not exact ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-5|Def. §C2.6.5]]).
>
> *Model:* none (a theorem about forms on a bare manifold); *applies:* oriented $M$; $\omega$ continuously differentiable with compact support (automatic when $M$ is compact); the boundary carries the induced orientation. Without compact support the formula fails ([[#^ex-c2-7-6|Example §C2.7.6]]); with the wrong orientation of a chart or of the boundary a sign flips ([[#^ex-c2-7-4|Example §C2.7.4]], stereographic chart). No metric enters, so it holds on Lorentzian spacetimes with any kind of boundary.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.4, Theorem 4, eq. (3.148) (p. 72; stated without hypotheses or proof) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §7.4, eq. (7.18) (p. 120; stated without proof) · proof written here (chart computation plus partition of unity)*

^thm-c2-7-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (transition maps at the boundary). Let $x$ and $y$ be boundary charts at $p \in \partial M$. A transition map takes boundary points to boundary points (the boundary is well defined, [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-4|Theorem §C2.1.4]]), so $y^m(x^1, \dots, x^{m-1}, 0) = 0$, and it takes $x^m \ge 0$ to $y^m \ge 0$. Differentiating the first fact along the boundary and the second across it, at boundary points
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial y^m}{\partial x^a} = 0 \quad (a = 1, \dots, m-1), \qquad \frac{\partial y^m}{\partial x^m} \ge 0 .
> $$
>
> The Jacobian matrix therefore has last row $(0, \dots, 0, \partial y^m/\partial x^m)$, and expanding the determinant along it,
>
> $$
> \det\Bigl(\frac{\partial y^\mu}{\partial x^\nu}\Bigr) = \frac{\partial y^m}{\partial x^m}\;\det\Bigl(\frac{\partial y^a}{\partial x^b}\Bigr)_{a, b < m} .
> $$
>
> The left side is nonzero, so $\partial y^m/\partial x^m > 0$. Two consequences. (a) The $y$-component of a vector is $N'^m = (\partial y^m/\partial x^\nu)N^\nu = (\partial y^m/\partial x^m)N^m$, with the same sign as $N^m$: Def. §C2.7.4 does not depend on the chart. (b) If $x$ and $y$ are positively oriented, $\det(\partial y^a/\partial x^b)_{a, b < m} > 0$: the restricted charts of $\partial M$ are orientation-compatible with each other.
>
> **Step 2** (part 1: the sign $(-1)^m$). In a positively oriented boundary chart, $N = -\partial_m$ is outward. The basis $(-\partial_m, \partial_1, \dots, \partial_{m-1})$ is obtained from $(\partial_1, \dots, \partial_m)$ by moving the last vector to the front, a cyclic permutation of $m$ vectors with sign $(-1)^{m-1}$, and by changing the sign of one vector, a factor $-1$:
>
> $$
> \det\bigl(-e_m, e_1, \dots, e_{m-1}\bigr) = -(-1)^{m-1} = (-1)^m .
> $$
>
> So $(\partial_1, \dots, \partial_{m-1})$ is positive on $\partial M$ exactly when $m$ is even; for $m = 1$ the boundary point gets the sign $-1 = (-1)^1$ in a positively oriented boundary chart, and $+1$ in a negatively oriented one (the only kind available at the right end of an interval, [[#^def-c2-7-2|Def. §C2.7.2]], part 4). (Checked for $m = 1, \dots, 5$ by script.) Any other outward $N' = cN + \sum_{a<m}b^a\partial_a$ with $c > 0$ gives the same sign, since adding multiples of the other basis vectors to the first does not change a determinant. By Step 1(b) the restricted charts, with $x^1 \to -x^1$ when $m$ is odd and $m \ge 2$, form an oriented atlas of $\partial M$ that induces the orientation of Def. §C2.7.5. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 3** ($\omega$ and $d\omega$ in a chart). In a chart, write the $(m-1)$-form with a hat for an omitted factor,
>
> $$
> \omega = \sum_{i=1}^m\omega_i\;dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{dx^i}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m .
> $$
>
> By the coordinate formula for $d$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]]) only $\partial_i\omega_i\,dx^i$ survives in front of the $i$-th term, and moving $dx^i$ past the $i-1$ one-forms before the gap costs $(-1)^{i-1}$:
>
> $$
> d\omega = \sum_{i=1}^m\partial_i\omega_i\;dx^i\wedge dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{dx^i}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m = \Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^m(-1)^{i-1}\partial_i\omega_i\Bigr)dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m .
> $$
>
> For $m = 2$: $\omega = \omega_1dx^2 + \omega_2dx^1$ and $d\omega = \partial_1\omega_1\,dx^1\wedge dx^2 + \partial_2\omega_2\,dx^2\wedge dx^1 = (\partial_1\omega_1 - \partial_2\omega_2)\,dx^1\wedge dx^2$. (The case $m = 3$ was checked by script.)
>
> **Step 4** (support in an interior chart). Let $\operatorname{supp}\omega$ lie in a positively oriented chart with image in $\mathbf R^m$ (no boundary points), inside the cube $[-R, R]^m$. By Def. §C2.7.2 and Fubini's theorem, integrate the $i$-th term first over $x^i$; by the fundamental theorem of calculus
>
> $$
> \int_{-R}^{R}\partial_i\omega_i\;dx^i = \omega_i\big|_{x^i = R} - \omega_i\big|_{x^i = -R} = 0 - 0 ,
> $$
>
> because $\omega$ vanishes on the faces of the cube. So $\int_Md\omega = 0$, and $\int_{\partial M}\omega = 0$ as well, since $\omega$ vanishes on $\partial M$ (its support misses the boundary).
>
> **Step 5** (support in a boundary chart). Let $\operatorname{supp}\omega$ lie in a positively oriented boundary chart, inside $[-R, R]^{m-1}\times[0, R]$. For $i < m$ the $x^i$-integral vanishes as in Step 4. For $i = m$ the lower limit is the boundary, where $\omega$ need not vanish:
>
> $$
> \int_0^R\partial_m\omega_m\;dx^m = \omega_m(x', R) - \omega_m(x', 0) = -\,\omega_m(x', 0), \qquad x' = (x^1, \dots, x^{m-1}) .
> $$
>
> With the sign $(-1)^{m-1}$ of Step 3,
>
> $$
> \int_Md\omega = (-1)^{m-1}\int_{\mathbf R^{m-1}}\bigl(-\omega_m(x', 0)\bigr)\,d^{m-1}x' = (-1)^m\int_{\mathbf R^{m-1}}\omega_m(x', 0)\;d^{m-1}x' .
> $$
>
> On the boundary $x^m = 0$, so $\iota^{\ast}dx^m = 0$, and every term of $\omega$ except $i = m$ contains $dx^m$: $\iota^{\ast}\omega = \omega_m(x', 0)\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{m-1}$. The chart $x'$ of $\partial M$ has the sign $(-1)^m$ (part 1), so by [[#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]], part 3,
>
> $$
> \int_{\partial M}\iota^{\ast}\omega = (-1)^m\int_{\mathbf R^{m-1}}\omega_m(x', 0)\;d^{m-1}x' = \int_Md\omega .
> $$
>
> For $m = 1$: $\omega = \omega_1$ is a function, $\int_0^R\omega_1'\,dx = -\omega_1(0)$, and the boundary point has the sign $-1$. In a negatively oriented boundary chart both sides change sign: $\int_Md\omega = +\omega_1(0)$ by the reversal rule, and the boundary point has the sign $+1$. (The half-space computation was checked by script for $m = 2, 3$.)
>
> **Step 6** (glue). Cover $\operatorname{supp}\omega$ by positively oriented interior and boundary charts, and let $\{\psi_i\}$ be a subordinate partition of unity; only finitely many $\psi_i\omega$ are nonzero (Step 1 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]]). Then $\omega = \sum_i\psi_i\omega$, a finite sum of forms each supported in one chart, and $d$ is linear, so by Steps 4–5 for each term
>
> $$
> \int_Md\omega = \sum_i\int_Md(\psi_i\,\omega) = \sum_i\int_{\partial M}\iota^{\ast}(\psi_i\,\omega) = \int_{\partial M}\iota^{\ast}\Bigl(\sum_i\psi_i\,\omega\Bigr) = \int_{\partial M}\iota^{\ast}\omega .
> $$
>
> Part 2 is proved. If $\partial M = \varnothing$, only Step 4 occurs and every term is $0$. If $\beta$ is an $(m-1)$-form on a compact $M$ without boundary, $\int_Md\beta = 0$, so a top form with $\int_M\omega \neq 0$ cannot be $d\beta$. Part 3 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Stokes' theorem is the one-variable fundamental theorem of calculus, applied in one chart; the partition of unity reduces everything to that case. Only the derivative across the boundary, $\partial_m\omega_m$, leaves a boundary term.
> - Compact support is used in Steps 4–5: it makes $\omega$ vanish on the far faces of the cube. Without it the far faces contribute ([[#^ex-c2-7-6|Example §C2.7.6]]). In physics this is the statement that fields fall off.
> - The sign $(-1)^m$ from the half-space is absorbed by the rule "outward first". With any other rule the theorem would carry dimension-dependent signs.

> [!caution] Caution: The hypotheses of Stokes' theorem in the sources
> - GR1's Theorem 4 (p. 72) states $\int_Md\omega = \int_{\partial M}\omega$ "for a manifold $M$ with a boundary, for any $(m-1)$-form $\omega$". Three hypotheses are needed and left implicit: $M$ oriented, $\partial M$ with the induced orientation, and $\omega$ of compact support (or $M$ compact). Without compact support the formula fails already on the half-line $[0, \infty)$ ([[#^ex-c2-7-6|Example §C2.7.6]]). The same page writes the half-space as "$\mathbf R^m = \{x^m \ge 0\}$" and the charts as maps $M \to U_i$; the half-space is meant, and charts go from open subsets of $M$ to $H^m$.
> - d'Inverno & Vickers (§7.4, p. 120) state the theorem for "a simply connected" region bounded by a hypersurface. Simple connectedness is not needed (the annulus of [[#^ex-c2-7-6|Example §C2.7.6]] has two boundary circles and obeys the theorem), while orientation and compactness are left implicit. Their volume elements $d\tau$ and $d\Omega = d^4x$ are signed, which is why $d^4x$ can be a density of weight $-1$ (7.21)–(7.22): as a measure of an ordinary integral, $d^4x$ changes by $|J|$, and the two readings agree only for orientation-preserving changes of chart ([[#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 2).

## Densities: divergences and conserved charges

Physics usually meets Stokes' theorem as a statement about divergences: a total divergence integrates to a boundary term, and a conserved current has a conserved charge. Without a metric the divergence of a vector field is not a chart-independent object; the divergence of a vector *density* is.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.7.4: Gauss's Theorem for Vector Densities; Total Divergences Integrate to Zero
> Let $M$ be an oriented manifold, possibly with boundary, and $\mathfrak V$ a vector density of weight $1$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-6|Def. §C2.3.6]]), whose components in positively oriented charts are related by $\tilde{\mathfrak V}^\nu = \det(\partial x/\partial y)\,(\partial y^\nu/\partial x^\mu)\,\mathfrak V^\mu$ (d'Inverno's convention, weight $W = 1$).
> 1. *(The flux form.)* The $(m-1)$-form, built with the interior product ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-6|Def. §C2.6.6]]),
>
> $$
> \omega_{\mathfrak V} \equiv \mathfrak V^\mu\,i_{\partial_\mu}\bigl(dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m\bigr) = \sum_{\mu=1}^m(-1)^{\mu-1}\,\mathfrak V^\mu\;dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{dx^\mu}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m ,
> $$
>
> is the same in every positively oriented chart, and
>
> $$
> d\omega_{\mathfrak V} = \bigl(\partial_\mu\mathfrak V^\mu\bigr)\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m .
> $$
>
> So $\partial_\mu\mathfrak V^\mu$ is a scalar density of weight $1$: the divergence of a vector density needs neither a metric nor a connection (d'Inverno (7.3)).
> 2. *(Gauss.)* If $\mathfrak V$ has compact support,
>
> $$
> \int_M\partial_\mu\mathfrak V^\mu\;d^mx = \int_{\partial M}\omega_{\mathfrak V} ,
> $$
>
> where the left side is the integral of the top form $d\omega_{\mathfrak V}$. In a positively oriented boundary chart the boundary integrand is the outward flux: for support in that chart, $\int_{\partial M}\omega_{\mathfrak V} = \int_{\mathbf R^{m-1}}\bigl(-\mathfrak V^m(x', 0)\bigr)\,d^{m-1}x'$, and $-\mathfrak V^m$ is the component along the outward direction $-\partial_m$. If $\partial M = \varnothing$, **a total divergence integrates to zero**: $\int_M\partial_\mu\mathfrak V^\mu\,d^mx = 0$.
> 3. *(Conserved charge.)* Let a chart $(x^0 = t, x^1, \dots, x^{m-1})$ cover the slab $t_1 \le t \le t_2$, $\vec x \in \mathbf R^{m-1}$ (a manifold with boundary, [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-4|Example §C2.1.4]]), let the vector density $\mathfrak J$ satisfy $\partial_\mu\mathfrak J^\mu = 0$ there, and let $\mathfrak J$ vanish outside a bounded set of $\vec x$ for all $t \in [t_1, t_2]$. Then
>
> $$
> Q(t) \equiv \int_{\mathbf R^{m-1}}\mathfrak J^0(t, \vec x)\;d^{m-1}x \qquad\text{satisfies}\qquad Q(t_2) = Q(t_1) ,
> $$
>
> and $Q(t)$ is the integral over the slice $t = \text{const}$ of the $(m-1)$-form $\omega_{\mathfrak J}$ ([[#^def-c2-7-3|Def. §C2.7.3]]), so it does not depend on the coordinates chosen on the slice.
>
> *Model:* none for parts 1–2; part 3 is the form taken by every conservation law of a current density; *applies:* any oriented manifold, no metric. With a metric, $\mathfrak V^\mu = \sqrt{|g|}\,V^\mu$ for a vector field $V$ turns part 2 into the covariant divergence theorem, which equates $\int\sqrt{|g|}\,\nabla_\mu V^\mu\,d^mx$ with a boundary term ([[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection|§C3.6]]; GR1 (4.81), d'Inverno (7.24)).
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §7.1, eq. (7.3); §7.4, eqs. (7.19)–(7.24) (pp. 115, 120–121) · Couzens, GR1, §4.3.4, eqs. (4.80)–(4.81) (the metric version) · proof written here*

^thm-c2-7-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the interior product of $dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m$). By [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-6|Def. §C2.6.6]] ([[§84 Contraction by Vector Fields#^def-84-1|591 Def. §84.1]]), $\bigl(i_{\partial_\mu}\Omega\bigr)(v_1, \dots, v_{m-1}) = \Omega(\partial_\mu, v_1, \dots, v_{m-1})$ with $\Omega = dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m$. On the coordinate vectors $\partial_1, \dots, \widehat{\partial_\mu}, \dots, \partial_m$ in increasing order, moving $\partial_\mu$ from the front to position $\mu$ takes $\mu - 1$ transpositions:
>
> $$
> \Omega\bigl(\partial_\mu, \partial_1, \dots, \widehat{\partial_\mu}, \dots, \partial_m\bigr) = (-1)^{\mu-1}\,\Omega(\partial_1, \dots, \partial_m) = (-1)^{\mu-1} ,
> $$
>
> and on any other set of $m-1$ coordinate vectors one of them repeats $\partial_\mu$ or misses another index, giving $0$. The $(m-1)$-form $dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{dx^\mu}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m$ takes the value $1$ on the same vectors and $0$ on the others, so $i_{\partial_\mu}\Omega = (-1)^{\mu-1}dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{dx^\mu}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^m$.
>
> **Step 2** (chart independence). Let $x$, $y$ be positively oriented, $J = \det(\partial x/\partial y) > 0$. By [[#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 1, $\Omega_x = J\,\Omega_y$; by [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], part 1, $\partial/\partial x^\mu = (\partial y^\nu/\partial x^\mu)\,\partial/\partial y^\nu$. The interior product is linear in the vector and in the form, so
>
> $$
> \mathfrak V^\mu\,i_{\partial/\partial x^\mu}\Omega_x = J\,\frac{\partial y^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}\,\mathfrak V^\mu\;i_{\partial/\partial y^\nu}\Omega_y = \tilde{\mathfrak V}^\nu\,i_{\partial/\partial y^\nu}\Omega_y ,
> $$
>
> by the transformation law of $\mathfrak V$. (Checked in polar coordinates by script.)
>
> **Step 3** ($d\omega_{\mathfrak V}$). Step 3 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]] with $\omega_\mu = (-1)^{\mu-1}\mathfrak V^\mu$:
>
> $$
> d\omega_{\mathfrak V} = \sum_\mu(-1)^{\mu-1}\,\partial_\mu\bigl((-1)^{\mu-1}\mathfrak V^\mu\bigr)\,\Omega = \sum_\mu\partial_\mu\mathfrak V^\mu\;\Omega .
> $$
>
> $d\omega_{\mathfrak V}$ is a top form defined independently of the chart (Step 2 and [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]]), so its coefficient $\partial_\mu\mathfrak V^\mu$ transforms with $J$ ([[#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 1). For the density $\mathfrak V = (x^2y,\ y^3 + x)$ in the plane, the script finds $\partial_\nu\tilde{\mathfrak V}^\nu = r\,\partial_\mu\mathfrak V^\mu$ in polar coordinates, with $J = r$. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 4** (part 2). Apply [[#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]] to $\omega_{\mathfrak V}$, which has compact support with $\mathfrak V$. In a boundary chart, Step 5 of that derivation gives $\int_{\partial M}\iota^{\ast}\omega_{\mathfrak V} = (-1)^m\int\omega_m(x', 0)\,d^{m-1}x'$ with $\omega_m = (-1)^{m-1}\mathfrak V^m$:
>
> $$
> \int_{\partial M}\omega_{\mathfrak V} = (-1)^m(-1)^{m-1}\int_{\mathbf R^{m-1}}\mathfrak V^m(x', 0)\,d^{m-1}x' = -\int_{\mathbf R^{m-1}}\mathfrak V^m(x', 0)\,d^{m-1}x' .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (part 3). Integrate $\partial_\mu\mathfrak J^\mu = 0$ over $[t_1, t_2]\times[-R, R]^{m-1}$, with $R$ so large that $\mathfrak J = 0$ for $|x^i| \ge R$. By Fubini's theorem each term may be integrated first over its own variable, and by the fundamental theorem of calculus
>
> $$
> 0 = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\!dt\int d^{m-1}x\;\partial_0\mathfrak J^0 + \sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\!dt\int d^{m-1}x\;\partial_i\mathfrak J^i = \int d^{m-1}x\,\bigl[\mathfrak J^0(t_2, \vec x) - \mathfrak J^0(t_1, \vec x)\bigr] + 0 = Q(t_2) - Q(t_1) ,
> $$
>
> each $x^i$-integral giving $\mathfrak J^i|_{x^i = R} - \mathfrak J^i|_{x^i = -R} = 0$. This is part 2 on the slab, whose two faces carry opposite induced orientations ([[#^def-c2-7-5|Def. §C2.7.5]]: the outward direction is $-\partial_t$ on the lower face and $+\partial_t$ on the upper). On a slice $t = \text{const}$, $\iota^{\ast}dt = 0$ and Step 1 with $\mu = 0$ in first position gives $\iota^{\ast}\omega_{\mathfrak J} = \mathfrak J^0\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{m-1}$, so $Q(t) = \int_{\Sigma_t}\omega_{\mathfrak J}$ with the slice oriented by $(x^1, \dots, x^{m-1})$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The density weight is what makes the divergence chart-independent: the factor $J$ in the law of $\mathfrak V$ cancels the factor $J$ of the volume form (Step 2). For a vector field the divergence $\partial_\mu V^\mu$ is not a scalar; it becomes one only with a metric, through $\sqrt{|g|}$.
> - Dropping total derivatives from an action, as in every variational derivation from [[§C5.2 The Einstein–Hilbert Action|§C5.2]] on, is part 2 with $\partial M = \varnothing$ or with fields of compact support. Conserved charges are part 3. Both are Stokes' theorem.

This completes the calculus of chapter C2: tensors, their Lie derivatives and exterior derivatives, and integrals of forms, all without a metric. Two gaps were met along the way. The integral of a function needs a volume form that a bare manifold does not supply ([[#^rem-c2-7-1|Remark: No canonical volume without a metric]]), and the divergence of a vector field, as opposed to a density, is not defined. Both are filled by the metric: *how are lengths, angles and volumes measured on a manifold, and how are vectors at different points compared?* That is chapter [[· C3 Metric, Connection and Curvature|C3]], beginning with the metric tensor ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor|§C3.1]]) and the volume form $\sqrt{|g|}\,d^4x$ ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor|§C3.3]]).

> [!example] Example §C2.7.1: ∫ cos²θ over the Circle with a Partition of Unity
> *Setup.* The circle $S^1 \subset \mathbf R^2$ with the two angle charts of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-2|Example §C2.1.2]] (GR1 (3.8)–(3.9)): $U_1 = S^1\setminus\{(1, 0)\}$ with $\theta_1 \in (0, 2\pi)$, and $U_2 = S^1\setminus\{(-1, 0)\}$ with $\theta_2 \in (-\pi, \pi)$. On the upper half of the overlap $\theta_2 = \theta_1$, on the lower half $\theta_2 = \theta_1 - 2\pi$; in both, $d\theta_2/d\theta_1 = 1 > 0$. The atlas is oriented ([[#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]]), and $d\theta_1 = d\theta_2$ on the overlap, so the two define one one-form $d\theta$ on $S^1$ (closed but not exact, [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-2|Example §C2.6.2]]). Integrate the top form $\omega = \cos^2\theta\,d\theta$; the coefficient is the function $x^2$ restricted to the circle.
>
> *(a) GR1's partition.* $\epsilon_1 = \sin^2(\theta/2) = \tfrac12(1 - \cos\theta) = \tfrac12(1 - x)$ and $\epsilon_2 = \cos^2(\theta/2) = \tfrac12(1 + x)$: smooth, between $0$ and $1$, with $\epsilon_1 + \epsilon_2 = 1$, and $\epsilon_1$ vanishes only at $(1, 0)$, $\epsilon_2$ only at $(-1, 0)$. With $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \pi$ and $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^3\theta\,d\theta = 0$ (the same over $(-\pi, \pi)$, by periodicity):
>
> $$
> \int_{U_1}\epsilon_1\,\omega = \int_0^{2\pi}\tfrac12(1 - \cos\theta)\cos^2\theta\;d\theta = \tfrac12\pi - \tfrac12\cdot 0 = \frac\pi2 , \qquad \int_{U_2}\epsilon_2\,\omega = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\tfrac12(1 + \cos\theta)\cos^2\theta\;d\theta = \frac\pi2 ,
> $$
>
> and $\int_{S^1}\omega = \pi$ (GR1 (3.144)). The supports of $\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2$ are all of $S^1$, so this partition is not subordinate in the sense of [[#^def-c2-7-2|Def. §C2.7.2]] ([[#^cau-c2-7-1|Caution: Partitions of unity and paracompactness in Couzens' GR1]]); the integrals converge because the integrands are bounded on bounded intervals.
>
> *(b) A subordinate partition.* Let $g(s) = e^{-1/s}$ for $s > 0$ and $g(s) = 0$ for $s \le 0$, a smooth function ([[§56 Vector Fields#^lem-56-5|591 Lem. §56.5]]), and
>
> $$
> G(x) = \frac{g(x + \frac12)}{g(x + \frac12) + g(\frac45 - x)} , \qquad \psi_2 = G(x), \qquad \psi_1 = 1 - G(x) .
> $$
>
> The denominator never vanishes, $G = 0$ for $x \le -\frac12$ and $G = 1$ for $x \ge \frac45$. So $\psi_2$ vanishes on the arc $|\theta - \pi| \le \pi/3$ around $(-1, 0)$ and $\psi_1$ on the arc $|\theta| \le \arccos\frac45$ around $(1, 0)$: $\operatorname{supp}\psi_i \subset U_i$. The two pieces are now different,
>
> $$
> \int_{U_1}\psi_1\,\omega = 1.5937 , \qquad \int_{U_2}\psi_2\,\omega = 1.5479 , \qquad \text{sum } 3.14159265358979 = \pi ,
> $$
>
> computed numerically by script; the sum agrees with $\pi$ to all printed digits. Different partitions cut the form differently, and the total is the same: [[#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]], part 2.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.4, Example 3.12, eqs. (3.143)–(3.144) (p. 71) · part (b) worked here; numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.7.2: Stokes' Theorem on the Unit Disk
> *Setup.* $M = D = \{x^2 + y^2 \le 1\}$, a manifold with boundary $\partial D = S^1$, oriented by $(x, y)$; the one-form $\alpha = -y^3\,dx + x^3\,dy$ (compact support, since $D$ is compact).
>
> *Left side.* $d\alpha = d(x^3)\wedge dy - d(y^3)\wedge dx = 3x^2\,dx\wedge dy - 3y^2\,dy\wedge dx = 3(x^2 + y^2)\,dx\wedge dy$. The interior chart $(x, y)$ covers $D$ up to the circle, a set of measure zero ([[#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]], part 3), and the polar chart, positively oriented ($dx\wedge dy = r\,dr\wedge d\theta$), covers it up to a ray:
>
> $$
> \int_Dd\alpha = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1 3r^2\cdot r\;dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\frac34 = \frac{3\pi}{2} .
> $$
>
> *Orientation of the boundary.* At $(1, 0)$ the outward vector is $N = \partial_x$, and $(\partial_x, \partial_y)$ is positive, so $\partial_y$ is positive on $\partial D$ there ([[#^def-c2-7-5|Def. §C2.7.5]]): the circle runs counterclockwise, parametrized by $t \mapsto (\cos t, \sin t)$, $t \in (0, 2\pi)$.
>
> *Right side.* Pull back with $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$, $dx = -\sin t\,dt$, $dy = \cos t\,dt$ ([[#^def-c2-7-3|Def. §C2.7.3]]):
>
> $$
> \iota^{\ast}\alpha = \bigl(-\sin^3t\,(-\sin t) + \cos^3t\,\cos t\bigr)dt = \bigl(\sin^4t + \cos^4t\bigr)dt , \qquad \int_{\partial D}\alpha = \frac{3\pi}{4} + \frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{2} ,
> $$
>
> using $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^4t\,dt = \int_0^{2\pi}\cos^4t\,dt = \frac{3\pi}{4}$. Both sides are $\frac{3\pi}{2}$, as [[#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]] requires. (Checked by script.)
>
> *Source: worked here · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.7.3: The Theorems of Gauss and Stokes in ℝ³
> *Setup.* $\mathbf R^3$ with the orientation $(x, y, z)$ and a smooth vector field $\vec F$. GR1's Exercise 3.10 asks to recover the classical theorems from [[#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]]. Two dictionaries do it: a vector field becomes the one-form $F^\flat = F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy + F_z\,dz$ or the two-form $\eta_F = F_x\,dy\wedge dz + F_y\,dz\wedge dx + F_z\,dx\wedge dy$; this uses the Cartesian coordinates of Euclidean space (the metric, chapter C3, makes the dictionaries general). By [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-1|Example §C2.6.1]],
>
> $$
> dF^\flat = (\nabla\times\vec F)_x\,dy\wedge dz + (\nabla\times\vec F)_y\,dz\wedge dx + (\nabla\times\vec F)_z\,dx\wedge dy = \eta_{\nabla\times F} , \qquad d\eta_F = (\nabla\cdot\vec F)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz ,
> $$
>
> the second also being [[#^thm-c2-7-4|Theorem §C2.7.4]], part 1, with $\mathfrak V = \vec F$ in Cartesian charts. For a parametrized surface $\vec X(u, v)$, the pull-back of a flux two-form is ([[#^def-c2-7-3|Def. §C2.7.3]]; checked by script, as in [[§40 The Generalized Stokes' Theorem|452 §40]])
>
> $$
> \vec X^{\ast}\eta_F = \vec F\cdot\bigl(\vec X_u\times\vec X_v\bigr)\;du\wedge dv .
> $$
>
> *Divergence theorem.* Let $V$ be a compact region with smooth boundary. A parametrization $\vec X(u, v)$ of $\partial V$ is positively oriented for the induced orientation iff $(N, \vec X_u, \vec X_v)$ is positively oriented, i.e. $\det(N, \vec X_u, \vec X_v) = N\cdot(\vec X_u\times\vec X_v) > 0$: $\vec X_u\times\vec X_v$ points outward, along the outward unit normal $\hat n$. Then $\vec F\cdot(\vec X_u\times\vec X_v)\,du\,dv = \vec F\cdot\hat n\,dA$, and Stokes' theorem for $\eta_F$ reads
>
> $$
> \int_V(\nabla\cdot\vec F)\;dV = \oint_{\partial V}\vec F\cdot\hat n\;dA ,
> $$
>
> Gauss's theorem ([[§32 Flux Integrals and the Divergence Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-32-1|452 Thm. §32.1]]; [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|EM Theorem §B1.1.3]], part 2). For the unit ball and $\vec F = (x, y, z)$: $\nabla\cdot\vec F = 3$ gives $3\cdot\frac43\pi = 4\pi$, and the flux of [[#^ex-c2-7-4|Example §C2.7.4]] is $4\pi$.
>
> *Stokes' theorem in $\mathbf R^3$.* Let $S$ be a compact surface with boundary, oriented by a unit normal $\mathbf n$: $(u, v)$ is positive iff $\vec X_u\times\vec X_v$ points along $\mathbf n$ ([[§73 Stokes' Theorem in Calculus III#^def-73-3|591 Def. §73.3]]). Then $\int_SdF^\flat = \int_S(\nabla\times\vec F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dA$. On $\partial S$, with $\nu$ the outward unit vector tangent to $S$ and $T$ the unit tangent of the curve, the induced orientation asks that $(\nu, T)$ be positive on $S$, i.e. $\nu\times T = \mathbf n$: the right-hand rule ([[§73 Stokes' Theorem in Calculus III#^lem-73-1|591 Lem. §73.1]]). The pull-back of $F^\flat$ along the curve is $\vec F\cdot d\vec r$, and
>
> $$
> \int_S(\nabla\times\vec F)\cdot\mathbf n\;dA = \oint_{\partial S}\vec F\cdot d\vec r ,
> $$
>
> GR1 (C.54) ([[§34 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-34-1|452 Thm. §34.1]]; [[§73 Stokes' Theorem in Calculus III#^thm-73-2|591 Thm. §73.2]]; [[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-3|EM Theorem §B1.1.3]], part 3).
>
> *Lower dimensions.* For $M = [a, b]$ and a function $f$ ($m = 1$): $\partial M = \{b\}$ with sign $+1$ and $\{a\}$ with sign $-1$, so Stokes' theorem is $\int_a^bf'\,dx = f(b) - f(a)$. For a region of the plane ($m = 2$) with $\alpha = P\,dx + Q\,dy$, $d\alpha = (\partial_xQ - \partial_yP)\,dx\wedge dy$, and Stokes' theorem is Green's theorem ([[§27 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^thm-27-1|452 Thm. §27.1]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.4, Exercise 3.10 (p. 72); solution C.2.10, eqs. (C.54)–(C.57) (p. 159) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.7.4: The Integral of sin θ dθ∧dφ over S² Is 4π
> *Setup.* $S^2 = \partial B$, the boundary of the unit ball in $\mathbf R^3$, with the induced orientation (outward normal first). The two-form is the flux form of the position field, $\sigma = \iota^{\ast}\eta_X$ with $\eta_X = x\,dy\wedge dz + y\,dz\wedge dx + z\,dx\wedge dy$ ([[#^ex-c2-7-3|Example §C2.7.3]]).
>
> *In spherical coordinates.* $\vec X(\theta, \varphi) = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi, \sin\theta\sin\varphi, \cos\theta)$, $(\theta, \varphi) \in (0, \pi)\times(0, 2\pi)$, is a chart of $S^2$ minus a closed half great circle (from pole to pole through $(1, 0, 0)$), a set of measure zero ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-3|Example §C2.1.3]]). With $\vec X_\theta\times\vec X_\varphi = \sin\theta\,\vec X$, which points outward for $0 < \theta < \pi$, the chart is positively oriented, and
>
> $$
> \sigma = \vec X\cdot\bigl(\vec X_\theta\times\vec X_\varphi\bigr)\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi = \sin\theta\,\vec X\cdot\vec X\;d\theta\wedge d\varphi = \sin\theta\;d\theta\wedge d\varphi .
> $$
>
> By [[#^thm-c2-7-2|Theorem §C2.7.2]], part 3,
>
> $$
> \int_{S^2}\sigma = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\;d\theta\,d\varphi = 2\pi\cdot\bigl[-\cos\theta\bigr]_0^\pi = 2\pi\cdot 2 = 4\pi .
> $$
>
> *In a stereographic chart.* The chart $(u, v)$ from the north pole ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]]), $\vec X = (2u, 2v, u^2 + v^2 - 1)/(1 + u^2 + v^2)$, covers $S^2$ minus one point. In it $\sigma = -4\,(1 + u^2 + v^2)^{-2}\,du\wedge dv$, and $\det\partial(u, v)/\partial(\theta, \varphi) = -\sin\theta/(1 - \cos\theta)^2 < 0$: the chart is negatively oriented. By part 3 of Theorem §C2.7.2 (reversal),
>
> $$
> \int_{S^2}\sigma = +\int_{\mathbf R^2}\frac{4\;du\,dv}{(1 + u^2 + v^2)^2} = 2\pi\int_0^\infty\frac{4\rho\;d\rho}{(1 + \rho^2)^2} = 2\pi\Bigl[-\frac{2}{1 + \rho^2}\Bigr]_0^\infty = 4\pi .
> $$
>
> Ignoring the orientation would have given $-4\pi$ here.
>
> *What the number means.* Without a metric, $4\pi$ is the integral of a two-form, the flux of the position field through $S^2$; it agrees with $\int_B\nabla\cdot\vec X\,dV = 4\pi$ ([[#^ex-c2-7-3|Example §C2.7.3]]). It is also the area of the unit sphere because $\sin\theta\,d\theta\wedge d\varphi$ is the area form of the round metric $d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2$ ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^ex-c2-4-2|Example §C2.4.2]]; [[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor|§C3.3]]). Since $\partial S^2 = \varnothing$ and $\int_{S^2}\sigma \neq 0$, $\sigma$ is closed but not exact ([[#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]], part 3): the two-dimensional analogue of $d\theta$ on the punctured plane. In physics $Q\sigma$ is the field strength of a magnetic monopole of charge $Q$, which therefore has no potential on the whole sphere ([[§C11.1 Einstein–Maxwell Theory#^ex-c11-1-2|Example §C11.1.2]], Step 4); in general a magnetic charge requires a surface that bounds no compact region ([[§C11.5 Charges in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c11-5-3|Theorem §C11.5.3]], part 4).
>
> *Source: worked here · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.7.5: The Möbius Strip Is Not Orientable
> *Setup.* The Möbius strip is $M = \bigl(\mathbf R\times(-1, 1)\bigr)/\!\sim$ with $(s, t) \sim (s + 1, -t)$: a strip whose ends are glued with a half twist, GR1's two rectangles glued with a twist on one edge (Fig. 10). Let $\pi : \mathbf R\times(-1, 1) \to M$ be the quotient map; its restrictions to the open strips $(a, a + 1)\times(-1, 1)$ are inverses of charts, so $\pi$ is a local diffeomorphism. The map $\tau(s, t) = (s + 1, -t)$ satisfies $\pi\circ\tau = \pi$.
>
> *Claim.* $M$ has no nowhere-vanishing two-form, so by [[#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 3, it is not orientable.
>
> *Proof.* Suppose $\omega$ is a nowhere-vanishing two-form on $M$. Its pull-back is $\pi^{\ast}\omega = h(s, t)\,ds\wedge dt$ with $h$ smooth and nowhere zero, since $\pi$ is a local diffeomorphism ([[#^thm-c2-7-1|Theorem §C2.7.1]], part 1, with nonzero Jacobian). The strip $\mathbf R\times(-1, 1)$ is connected and $h$ is continuous, so $h$ has one sign everywhere. Pull-backs compose ([[§81 The Exterior Derivative#^lem-81-4|591 Lem. §81.4]]), so $\tau^{\ast}\pi^{\ast}\omega = (\pi\circ\tau)^{\ast}\omega = \pi^{\ast}\omega$. Compute the left side:
>
> $$
> \tau^{\ast}\bigl(h\,ds\wedge dt\bigr) = h(s + 1, -t)\;d(s + 1)\wedge d(-t) = -\,h(s + 1, -t)\;ds\wedge dt .
> $$
>
> Hence $h(s, t) = -h(s + 1, -t)$: $h$ takes opposite signs at $(s, t)$ and $(s + 1, -t)$, a contradiction.
>
> *GR1's version.* With two rectangle charts, one overlap has the transition $y^1 = x^1$, $y^2 = x^2$ and the other $y^1 = x^1$, $y^2 = -x^2$, Jacobian determinant $-1$; replacing $y^2$ by $-y^2$ moves the $-1$ to the first overlap. The proof above shows that no atlas whatever avoids a negative determinant. On a non-orientable manifold top forms cannot be integrated in the sense of [[#^def-c2-7-2|Def. §C2.7.2]]; densities with $|J|$ in place of $J$ can, a case not needed for spacetime, which is assumed orientable.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.4.4, Fig. 10 and text after (3.139) (pp. 69–70) · proof written here; $\det\partial\tau = -1$ checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.7.6: Why the Hypotheses of Stokes' Theorem Are Needed
> *(a) Compact support: the half-line.* $M = [0, \infty)$, oriented by $x$, boundary $\{0\}$. The outward vector at $0$ is $-\partial_x$, negatively oriented, so the point $0$ has the sign $-1$ ([[#^def-c2-7-5|Def. §C2.7.5]]). For the function $\omega = 1$, which does not have compact support,
>
> $$
> \int_Md\omega = \int_0^\infty 0\;dx = 0 , \qquad \int_{\partial M}\omega = -\,\omega(0) = -1 .
> $$
>
> The theorem fails. For $\omega = f = e^{-x}$, which falls off at infinity, it holds: $\int_0^\infty f'\,dx = \int_0^\infty(-e^{-x})\,dx = -1 = -f(0)$. In Step 5 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c2-7-3|Theorem §C2.7.3]], the far end $x = R$ contributes $\omega(R) \to 0$ for $f$, but $1$ for $\omega = 1$.
>
> *(b) Compact support: the open disk.* $M = D^\circ = \{x^2 + y^2 < 1\}$, a manifold without boundary, and $\alpha = -y^3\,dx + x^3\,dy$ of [[#^ex-c2-7-2|Example §C2.7.2]]. Then $\int_{D^\circ}d\alpha = \frac{3\pi}{2}$ (the circle has measure zero), while $\partial D^\circ = \varnothing$ would give $0$: $\alpha$ does not vanish near the edge, so its support is not compact in $D^\circ$. The cut-off form $(1 - r^2)^3\alpha$, which vanishes on the circle, gives $\int_Dd\bigl((1 - r^2)^3\alpha\bigr) = 0$ (script), as Stokes' theorem on the closed disk requires.
>
> *(c) A region with a hole.* The annulus $A = \{a \le r \le 1\}$, $0 < a < 1$, is not simply connected; its boundary has two circles, the outer one counterclockwise and the inner one clockwise ([[#^def-c2-7-5|Def. §C2.7.5]]; figure above). With the same $\alpha$, $\oint_{|x| = R}\alpha = \frac{3\pi}{2}R^4$ counterclockwise, and
>
> $$
> \int_Ad\alpha = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_a^1 3r^3\;dr\,d\theta = \frac{3\pi}{2}\bigl(1 - a^4\bigr) = \underbrace{\frac{3\pi}{2}}_{\text{outer}} \;\underbrace{-\;\frac{3\pi}{2}a^4}_{\text{inner, clockwise}} = \int_{\partial A}\alpha .
> $$
>
> The theorem holds without simple connectedness ([[#^cau-c2-7-2|Caution: The hypotheses of Stokes' theorem in the sources]]); what it needs is the induced orientation on every boundary component.
>
> *Source: worked here · numbers checked by script*
