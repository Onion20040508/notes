[[§C2.3 Tensor Fields|§C2.3]] ended with a negative result: the partial derivatives $\partial_\mu V^\nu$ of a vector field are not the components of a tensor ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]]). The reason is geometric. A derivative compares the field at two nearby points $p$ and $q$, and the values $V_p \in T_pM$ and $V_q \in T_qM$ lie in two different vector spaces ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]). Their difference is not defined until something identifies $T_qM$ with $T_pM$. A chart does it silently, by comparing components, and different charts do it differently. This section asks *what structure lets one compare tensors at different points without choosing a chart*, and gives the first answer: a vector field.

First, what fails. Take the plane, the constant field $Y = \partial_x$, and the unit circle $p(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$. The "rate of change of $Y$ along the circle", computed naively as the $t$-derivative of the components of $Y$ at $p(t)$, depends on the chart:

$$
\text{Cartesian: } Y^i\bigl(p(t)\bigr) = (1, 0) \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{d}{dt}Y^i = (0, 0); \qquad \text{polar: } Y'^\mu = \Bigl(\cos\theta, -\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Bigr)\Big|_{(1, t)} = (\cos t, -\sin t) \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{d}{dt}Y'^\mu\Big|_{t=0} = (0, -1) .
$$

The polar components of $\partial_x$ are those of [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]]. At $(r, \theta) = (1, 0)$ the result $(0, -1)$ is the vector $-\partial_\theta = -\partial_y$, not zero. Neither number is "the" derivative of $Y$: each is an artefact of the identification of tangent spaces that its chart supplies. Now use the circles themselves. They are the orbits of the rotation field $R = -y\,\partial_x + x\,\partial_y$, whose flow $\sigma_t$ turns the plane by the angle $t$. Its differential $(\sigma_{-t})_{\ast}$ carries $T_{\sigma_t(p)}$ back to $T_p$ ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^def-c2-4-1|Def. §C2.4.1]]) without any chart. At $p = (1, 0)$ it turns the value $Y = (1, 0)$ at $\sigma_t(p)$ back by $-t$, to $V_t = (\cos t, -\sin t)$ in Cartesian components, a curve in the single vector space $T_pM$. Its velocity at $t = 0$ is $(0, -1) = -\partial_y$, an honest vector, whatever chart is then used to write it. This is the polar answer. The coincidence has a reason: in polar coordinates $R = \partial_\theta$, and along a coordinate field the flow is a translation of one coordinate, so the naive and the dragged derivatives agree ([[#^ex-c2-5-4|Example §C2.5.4]], [[§72 Lie Derivatives#^lem-72-5|591 Lem. §72.5]]). The Cartesian chart is not adapted to $R$, and there the naive derivative misses the Jacobian of the flow.

The section builds this in order. A vector field has integral curves and a flow ([[#^def-c2-5-1|Def. §C2.5.1]], [[#^def-c2-5-2|Def. §C2.5.2]]); the flow maps are diffeomorphisms and to first order shift points by $tX$ ([[#^thm-c2-5-1|Theorem §C2.5.1]]). A diffeomorphism transports tensors of every type ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]]), so the flow pulls any tensor at $\sigma_t(p)$ back to $p$, and the rate of change at $t = 0$ is the Lie derivative ([[#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]]). On functions and vector fields it is the derivative $Xf$ and the bracket $[X, Y]$, known from MATH 591 ([[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]]). On one-forms and on tensors of every type its components are derived here ([[#^thm-c2-5-3|Theorem §C2.5.3]], [[#^thm-c2-5-4|Theorem §C2.5.4]]). It obeys the Leibniz rule and commutes with contraction ([[#^thm-c2-5-5|Theorem §C2.5.5]]); two Lie derivatives commute up to the Lie derivative along the bracket ([[#^thm-c2-5-6|Theorem §C2.5.6]]), and the bracket measures how far two flows fail to commute ([[#^thm-c2-5-7|Theorem §C2.5.7]]). Last, a by-product: the Lie derivative at $p$ depends on $X$ near $p$, not only on the vector $X_p$ ([[#^thm-c2-5-8|Theorem §C2.5.8]]). So it is not yet a "directional derivative", and general relativity will need one more structure.

**Setup.** A smooth manifold $M$ of dimension $n$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]), a smooth vector field $X$ on it ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-4|Def. §C2.2.4]]), and smooth tensor fields of type $(r, s)$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]). No metric is used anywhere: the comparison of tangent spaces comes from the flow of $X$ alone. All statements are exact and local. The Lie derivative at $p$ uses the flow only for small $|t|$ and near $p$, so $X$ need not be complete and all component computations take place in one chart around $p$. The identities and the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): no metric enters, so the signature plays no role; index positions are types, and nothing is raised or lowered ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^cau-c2-2-1|§C2.2, Caution: Index position is the type, and nothing raises it yet]]). Greek indices label the coordinates $x^\mu$ of one chart, summed when repeated; $\partial_\mu = \partial/\partial x^\mu$. The flow of $X$ is written $\sigma$ as in Couzens (MATH 591 writes $\varphi$); a second field $Y$ has the flow $\tau$. Push-forward $f_{\ast}$, pull-back $f^{\ast}$, as in [[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms|§C2.4]].

## Integral curves and flows

A vector field assigns a velocity to every point. Following those velocities moves every point at once, and the motion is the tool for comparing points. The home of this material is MATH 591, §67–§70; here it is stated in the form general relativity uses.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.5.1: Integral Curve
> *Structure.* Let $X$ be a smooth vector field on $M$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-4|Def. §C2.2.4]]). An **integral curve** of $X$ is a smooth curve $\gamma : I \to M$ on an open interval $I \subseteq \mathbf R$ whose velocity ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]) is the value of $X$ at every point it passes:
>
> $$
> \dot\gamma(t) = X_{\gamma(t)} \in T_{\gamma(t)}M \qquad \text{for all } t \in I .
> $$
>
> *Components.* In a chart $(U, x^\mu)$, with $x^\mu(t)$ the coordinates of $\gamma(t)$ and $X = X^\mu\partial_\mu$, this is the system of first-order ordinary differential equations
>
> $$
> \frac{dx^\mu}{dt} = X^\mu\bigl(x(t)\bigr), \qquad \mu = 1, \dots, n .
> $$
>
> Given $\gamma(0) = p$, a solution exists on some interval around $0$ and two solutions agree where both are defined ([[§68 Local Existence and Uniqueness#^thm-68-2|591 Thm. §68.2]], [[§68 Local Existence and Uniqueness#^prop-68-3|591 Prop. §68.3]]; uniqueness uses that $M$ is Hausdorff). The parameter $t$ is a label along the curve, not a time measured by any clock: there is no metric yet.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.3, Def. 22, eq. (3.62) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.2, eq. (6.2) · MATH 591: [[§67 Integral Curves#^def-67-1|591 Def. §67.1]], [[§67 Integral Curves#^prop-67-2|591 Prop. §67.2]]*

^def-c2-5-1

> [!definition] Definition §C2.5.2: Flow; One-Parameter Group of Diffeomorphisms
> *Structure.* For each $p \in M$ let $\sigma^p : (\alpha(p), \beta(p)) \to M$ be the maximal integral curve of $X$ with $\sigma^p(0) = p$ ([[#^def-c2-5-1|Def. §C2.5.1]]; it exists and is unique, [[§69 Maximal Integral Curves and Flows#^thm-69-4|591 Thm. §69.4]]). The **flow** of $X$ is the map
>
> $$
> \sigma : \mathcal D \longrightarrow M, \qquad \sigma(t, p) = \sigma_t(p) = \sigma^p(t), \qquad \mathcal D = \{(t, p) : \alpha(p) < t < \beta(p)\} \subseteq \mathbf R \times M ,
> $$
>
> defined on the **flow domain** $\mathcal D$, an open set containing $\{0\} \times M$, on which $\sigma$ is smooth ([[§69 Maximal Integral Curves and Flows#^def-69-2|591 Def. §69.2]], [[§69 Maximal Integral Curves and Flows#^thm-69-4|591 Thm. §69.4]]). In a chart, $\sigma^\mu(t, x)$ solves the **flow equation** with its initial condition,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\sigma^\mu(t, x) = X^\mu\bigl(\sigma(t, x)\bigr), \qquad \sigma^\mu(0, x) = x^\mu .
> $$
>
> For fixed $t$ the map $\sigma_t$ moves every point a parameter distance $t$ along its integral curve.
>
> *Complete and local.* $X$ is **complete** if $\mathcal D = \mathbf R \times M$ ([[§70 Complete Vector Fields#^def-70-1|591 Def. §70.1]]). Then every $\sigma_t$ is a diffeomorphism of $M$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-6|Def. §C2.1.6]]) and the family $\{\sigma_t\}_{t \in \mathbf R}$ is a **one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms** with **infinitesimal generator** $X$ ([[§70 Complete Vector Fields#^prop-70-7|591 Prop. §70.7]], [[§70 Complete Vector Fields#^def-70-2|591 Def. §70.2]]). If $X$ is not complete, $\sigma_t$ is defined only on the open set $M_t = \{p : (t, p) \in \mathcal D\}$, and the family is a **local** one-parameter group: the group law holds where both sides are defined ([[#^thm-c2-5-1|Theorem §C2.5.1]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.3, Def. 23, eqs. (3.63)–(3.65); §3.3.1 · d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.2 (the congruence of integral curves, Figs. 6.1–6.2) · MATH 591: [[§69 Maximal Integral Curves and Flows#^def-69-2|591 Def. §69.2]], [[§70 Complete Vector Fields#^prop-70-7|591 Prop. §70.7]]*

> [!caution] Caution: Flows need not be global; exp(tX) is notation
> - Couzens (GR1, §3.3, Theorem 2) states that every vector field has a flow defined on all of $\mathbf R \times M$. This holds for complete fields, in particular for every field on a compact manifold ([[§70 Complete Vector Fields#^thm-70-2|591 Thm. §70.2]]), but not in general, as the same notes say a page earlier ("at least locally"). On $M = (-\infty, 0)$ the field $\partial_x$ has the integral curves $x_0 + t$, which leave $M$ at $t = -x_0$ ([[§69 Maximal Integral Curves and Flows#^ex-69-2|591 Ex. §69.2]]); on $\mathbf R$ the field $x^2\partial_x$ has the integral curves $x_0/(1 - x_0t)$, which run to infinity at $t = 1/x_0$ ([[§69 Maximal Integral Curves and Flows#^ex-69-1|591 Ex. §69.1]] is the same equation in two variables). In general relativity incomplete fields are the rule: a spacetime with a black hole is not compact, and its natural vector fields have orbits that end. Nothing in this section is affected, because the Lie derivative uses $\sigma_t$ only for small $|t|$ near one point.
> - GR1 (3.71)–(3.72) writes the flow as $\sigma^\mu(t, x) = \exp(tX)x^\mu$, justified by a Taylor series in $t$. The series converges to the flow only when the flow is real-analytic in $t$; for a smooth field it is a formal expression. Its first terms are correct and are all that is used ([[#^thm-c2-5-1|Theorem §C2.5.1]], part 3).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.3, pp. 53–54 (Def. 22 and Theorem 2), eqs. (3.71)–(3.72) · MATH 591, §69–§70*

^cau-c2-5-1

The flow maps compose like the additive group of time, are invertible, and to first order move each point by $tX$. These three facts are all that the Lie derivative needs.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.1: Flow Properties
> Let $\sigma$ be the flow of a smooth vector field $X$ ([[#^def-c2-5-2|Def. §C2.5.2]]).
> 1. **Group law.** $\sigma_0 = \mathrm{id}$, and $\sigma_t\bigl(\sigma_s(p)\bigr) = \sigma_{t+s}(p)$ whenever $s$ and $t + s$ lie in $(\alpha(p), \beta(p))$.
> 2. **Diffeomorphisms.** Each $\sigma_t$ maps the open set $M_t$ diffeomorphically onto $M_{-t}$, with inverse $\sigma_{-t}$.
> 3. **Infinitesimal form.** In a chart around $p$, for $x$ near $p$ and small $|t|$,
>
> $$
> \sigma^\mu_t(x) = x^\mu + t\,X^\mu(x) + \tfrac12 t^2\,X^\lambda\partial_\lambda X^\mu(x) + O(t^3), \qquad \frac{\partial\sigma^\mu_t}{\partial x^\nu}(x) = \delta^\mu_\nu + t\,\partial_\nu X^\mu(x) + O(t^2) .
> $$
>
> 4. **Complete fields.** If $X$ is complete, $t \mapsto \sigma_t$ is a homomorphism from $(\mathbf R, +)$ into the group of diffeomorphisms of $M$; its image is commutative, and it may be a circle rather than a line (period $T$ with $\sigma_T = \mathrm{id}$).
>
> *Model:* none; geometry of a smooth manifold with a smooth vector field. *applies:* exactly; part 3 is a Taylor expansion with the remainders shown.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.3, eq. (3.65), Theorem 2; §3.3.1, eqs. (3.69)–(3.70); Exercise 3.3, App. C.2.3, eq. (C.28) · MATH 591: [[§69 Maximal Integral Curves and Flows#^prop-69-5|591 Prop. §69.5]], [[§72 Lie Derivatives#^lem-72-2|591 Lem. §72.2]], [[§70 Complete Vector Fields#^prop-70-7|591 Prop. §70.7]]*

^thm-c2-5-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (parts 1, 2, 4: from MATH 591). The group law is [[§69 Maximal Integral Curves and Flows#^prop-69-5|591 Prop. §69.5]]: both $t \mapsto \sigma_t(\sigma_s(p))$ and $t \mapsto \sigma_{t+s}(p)$ are integral curves of $X$ through $\sigma_s(p)$ at $t = 0$, so they agree by uniqueness. Part 2 is [[§72 Lie Derivatives#^lem-72-2|591 Lem. §72.2]]: $\sigma_{-t}\circ\sigma_t = \sigma_0 = \mathrm{id}$ on $M_t$ by part 1, and both maps are smooth because $\sigma$ is smooth on the open set $\mathcal D$. Part 4 is [[§70 Complete Vector Fields#^prop-70-7|591 Prop. §70.7]]: $\sigma_t\circ\sigma_s = \sigma_{t+s} = \sigma_s\circ\sigma_t$ for all $s, t$, which is a homomorphism with commutative image because $t + s = s + t$ (GR1 Exercise 3.3).
>
> **Step 2** (first time derivative). In a chart around $p$ choose a neighbourhood $U'$ of $p$ and $\varepsilon > 0$ with $\sigma_t(U')$ inside the chart for $|t| < \varepsilon$ (a flow box, [[§69 Maximal Integral Curves and Flows#^lem-69-3|591 Lem. §69.3]], shrunk by continuity). The flow equation at $t = 0$ gives
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\sigma^\mu}{\partial t}(0, x) = X^\mu\bigl(\sigma(0, x)\bigr) = X^\mu(x) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (second time derivative). Differentiate the flow equation once more in $t$, by the chain rule:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2\sigma^\mu}{\partial t^2}(t, x) = \frac{\partial X^\mu}{\partial x^\lambda}\bigl(\sigma(t, x)\bigr)\,\frac{\partial\sigma^\lambda}{\partial t}(t, x) = \partial_\lambda X^\mu\bigl(\sigma(t, x)\bigr)\,X^\lambda\bigl(\sigma(t, x)\bigr) \;\xrightarrow{\;t = 0\;}\; X^\lambda\partial_\lambda X^\mu(x) .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (Taylor in $t$). $\sigma^\mu(\cdot, x)$ is smooth, so Taylor's theorem to second order with $\sigma^\mu(0, x) = x^\mu$ and Steps 2–3 gives the first formula of part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (the Jacobian). $\sigma$ is smooth in $(t, x)$ jointly, so its mixed partial derivatives commute. At $t = 0$, $\sigma^\mu(0, x) = x^\mu$ for all $x$, hence $\partial\sigma^\mu/\partial x^\nu(0, x) = \delta^\mu_\nu$. And
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial\sigma^\mu}{\partial x^\nu}(t, x)\Big|_{t=0} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\nu}\frac{\partial\sigma^\mu}{\partial t}(0, x) = \frac{\partial X^\mu}{\partial x^\nu}(x) ,
> $$
>
> by Step 2 differentiated in $x^\nu$ (legitimate because Step 2 holds for all $x$ near $p$). Taylor's theorem to first order in $t$ gives the second formula of part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The first-order data of the flow are the field and its first derivatives: $\sigma_t \approx \mathrm{id} + tX$ on points, $\partial\sigma_t \approx \mathbf 1 + t\,\partial X$ on tangent vectors. Step 5 needs the flow on a neighbourhood of $p$, not only along the one integral curve through $p$: the Jacobian compares neighbouring curves. GR1 (3.70) records the first formula to first order; the second-order term is needed for [[#^thm-c2-5-7|Theorem §C2.5.7]].

## The Lie derivative

The opening computation carried a vector at $\sigma_t(p)$ back to $p$ by $(\sigma_{-t})_{\ast}$. A one-form at $\sigma_t(p)$ is carried back by the pull-back $(\sigma_t)^{\ast}$, and since every $\sigma_t$ is a diffeomorphism near $p$, tensors of every type can be carried back the same way ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]]). After that the two tensors lie in the same space at $p$ and can be subtracted.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.5.3: Lie Derivative of a Tensor Field
> *Structure.* Let $X$ be a smooth vector field with flow $\sigma$ ([[#^def-c2-5-2|Def. §C2.5.2]]), $T$ a smooth tensor field of type $(r, s)$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]), and $p \in M$. For small $|t|$, $\sigma_t$ is a diffeomorphism from a neighbourhood of $p$ onto a neighbourhood of $\sigma_t(p)$ ([[#^thm-c2-5-1|Theorem §C2.5.1]], part 2), so it transports $T$ ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]]). The **pull-back** of $T$ by $\sigma_t$ at $p$ is the tensor of type $(r, s)$ at $p$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]])
>
> $$
> (\sigma_t^{\ast}T)_p(\omega^1, \dots, \omega^r, V_1, \dots, V_s) = T_{\sigma_t(p)}\bigl((\sigma_{-t})^{\ast}\omega^1, \dots, (\sigma_{-t})^{\ast}\omega^r, (\sigma_t)_{\ast}V_1, \dots, (\sigma_t)_{\ast}V_s\bigr)
> $$
>
> for covectors $\omega^i \in T^{\ast}_pM$ and vectors $V_j \in T_pM$: covector arguments are carried forward to $\sigma_t(p)$ by the pull-back along $\sigma_{-t}$ ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^def-c2-4-2|Def. §C2.4.2]]), vector arguments by the push-forward along $\sigma_t$ ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^def-c2-4-1|Def. §C2.4.1]]). The **Lie derivative** of $T$ along $X$ at $p$ is
>
> $$
> (\mathcal L_XT)_p = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}(\sigma_t^{\ast}T)_p = \lim_{t \to 0}\frac1t\Bigl[(\sigma_t^{\ast}T)_p - T_p\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> the velocity of a curve in the one vector space of $(r, s)$ tensors at $p$, hence again a tensor of type $(r, s)$ at $p$. As $p$ varies, $\mathcal L_XT$ is a tensor field of type $(r, s)$ (smooth: see the components below).
>
> *Components.* In a chart around $p$, with the Jacobian $(J_t)^\alpha{}_\nu = \partial\sigma^\alpha_t/\partial x^\nu$ at $p$ and its inverse matrix $J_t^{-1}$,
>
> $$
> (\sigma_t^{\ast}T)^{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\dots\nu_s}(p) = (J_t^{-1})^{\mu_1}{}_{\alpha_1}\cdots(J_t^{-1})^{\mu_r}{}_{\alpha_r}\;T^{\alpha_1\dots\alpha_r}{}_{\beta_1\dots\beta_s}\bigl(\sigma_t(p)\bigr)\;(J_t)^{\beta_1}{}_{\nu_1}\cdots(J_t)^{\beta_s}{}_{\nu_s} .
> $$
>
> In the notation of [[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-4|Theorem §C2.4.4]], $\sigma_t^{\ast} \equiv (\sigma_{-t})_{\ast}$ (part 1), and this is its component formula (part 3) for $\phi = \sigma_{-t}$: $J_t^{-1}$ is the Jacobian of $\sigma_{-t}$ at $\sigma_t(p)$, by the chain rule applied to $\sigma_{-t}\circ\sigma_t = \mathrm{id}$.
>
> *Special cases.*
> - Function $f$: $\sigma_t^{\ast}f = f\circ\sigma_t$ ([[§72 Lie Derivatives#^def-72-1|591 Def. §72.1]]).
> - Vector field $Y$: $(\sigma_t^{\ast}Y)_p = (\sigma_{-t})_{\ast}\,Y_{\sigma_t(p)}$, the vector of the opening ([[§72 Lie Derivatives#^def-72-2|591 Def. §72.2]]; GR1 (3.74)).
> - One-form $\omega$: $(\sigma_t^{\ast}\omega)_p = (\sigma_t)^{\ast}\,\omega_{\sigma_t(p)}$ (GR1 (3.91)).
>
> *What it needs.* Only the flow near $p$ for small $|t|$: $X$ need not be complete. No metric and no further structure enter. The subscript is the field along which one differentiates; $\mathcal L_XT$ depends on $X$ through its flow, hence on $X$ near $p$ ([[#^thm-c2-5-8|Theorem §C2.5.8]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.3.2, Def. 24, eq. (3.74) (vectors); eq. (3.91) (one-forms); eq. (3.94) (functions); Exercise 3.6 (general tensors) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.2, eqs. (6.3)–(6.8) (by dragging) · MATH 591: [[§72 Lie Derivatives#^def-72-1|591 Def. §72.1]], [[§72 Lie Derivatives#^def-72-2|591 Def. §72.2]]*

> [!remark] Remark: Pulling back or dragging forward
> Couzens and MATH 591 pull the tensor at $\sigma_t(p)$ back to $p$ and compare there. d'Inverno & Vickers (§6.2) do the opposite: they drag the tensor at $p$ forward along the curve to $Q = \sigma_{\delta u}(p)$ by the point transformation $\tilde x^a = x^a + \delta u\,X^a$ and compare it with the tensor already at $Q$, $\mathcal L_XT = \lim_{\delta u\to0}[T(\tilde x) - \tilde T(\tilde x)]/\delta u$ (their (6.7)). The two difference quotients differ by the transport of one into the other, which tends to the identity as the parameter tends to zero, so the limits agree; d'Inverno's (6.8) is the $(2, 0)$ case of [[#^thm-c2-5-4|Theorem §C2.5.4]]. The pull-back picture has the advantage that the whole curve $t \mapsto (\sigma_t^{\ast}T)_p$ lies in one vector space, as in the animation.

The definition is coordinate-free; to use it one needs components. The two cases MATH 591 has already done come first.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.2: Lie Derivative of Functions and Vector Fields
> Let $X$, $Y$ be smooth vector fields and $f$ a smooth function on $M$.
> 1. **Functions.** $\mathcal L_Xf = X[f]$, in components $X^\mu\partial_\mu f$: the directional derivative.
> 2. **Vector fields.** $\mathcal L_XY = [X, Y]$, the Lie bracket $[X, Y][f] = X[Y[f]] - Y[X[f]]$ ([[§58 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^def-58-1|591 Def. §58.1]]), with components
>
> $$
> (\mathcal L_XY)^\nu = X^\mu\partial_\mu Y^\nu - Y^\mu\partial_\mu X^\nu .
> $$
>
> In particular $\mathcal L_XY = -\mathcal L_YX$ and $\mathcal L_XX = 0$.
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* exactly, any smooth $X$, $Y$, $f$, at every point (no completeness needed).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.3.2, eqs. (3.75)–(3.80), (3.94) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.2, eqs. (6.14)–(6.15) · MATH 591: [[§72 Lie Derivatives#^prop-72-1|591 Prop. §72.1]], [[§72 Lie Derivatives#^thm-72-6|591 Thm. §72.6]] (coordinate-free proof), [[§58 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^prop-58-2|591 Prop. §58.2]] (the bracket in coordinates)*

^thm-c2-5-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (functions). $(\sigma_t^{\ast}f)(p) = f(\sigma_t(p))$, and $t \mapsto \sigma_t(p)$ is the integral curve through $p$, with velocity $X_p$ at $t = 0$ ([[#^def-c2-5-1|Def. §C2.5.1]]). By the chain rule
>
> $$
> (\mathcal L_Xf)(p) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}f\bigl(\sigma^\mu_t(p)\bigr) = \partial_\mu f(p)\,\frac{\partial\sigma^\mu}{\partial t}(0, p) = X^\mu(p)\,\partial_\mu f(p) = X_p[f] .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (vector fields: the curve in $T_pM$). By [[#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]] with $(r, s) = (1, 0)$,
>
> $$
> V^\nu(t) \equiv (\sigma_t^{\ast}Y)^\nu(p) = (J_t^{-1})^\nu{}_\alpha\,Y^\alpha\bigl(\sigma_t(p)\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (three derivatives at $t = 0$). (a) $J_0 = \mathbf 1$ and $\frac{d}{dt}J_t|_0 = (\partial_\nu X^\alpha)$ ([[#^thm-c2-5-1|Theorem §C2.5.1]], part 3). (b) Differentiating $J_tJ_t^{-1} = \mathbf 1$ gives $\dot J_tJ_t^{-1} + J_t\,\tfrac{d}{dt}J_t^{-1} = 0$, so at $t = 0$
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}(J_t^{-1})^\nu{}_\alpha = -\partial_\alpha X^\nu(p) .
> $$
>
> (c) By Step 1 applied to the function $Y^\alpha$: $\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}Y^\alpha(\sigma_t(p)) = X^\mu\partial_\mu Y^\alpha(p)$.
>
> **Step 4** (product rule). With $J_0^{-1} = \mathbf 1$ and $Y^\alpha(\sigma_0(p)) = Y^\alpha(p)$,
>
> $$
> \dot V^\nu(0) = \bigl(-\partial_\alpha X^\nu\bigr)Y^\alpha + \delta^\nu_\alpha\,X^\mu\partial_\mu Y^\alpha = X^\mu\partial_\mu Y^\nu - Y^\mu\partial_\mu X^\nu ,
> $$
>
> after renaming the dummy index $\alpha \to \mu$ in the first term.
>
> **Step 5** (the bracket). For any function $f$, $X[Y[f]] = X^\mu\partial_\mu(Y^\nu\partial_\nu f) = X^\mu\partial_\mu Y^\nu\,\partial_\nu f + X^\mu Y^\nu\partial_\mu\partial_\nu f$, and the same with $X \leftrightarrow Y$. The second-derivative terms are equal (rename $\mu \leftrightarrow \nu$, partial derivatives commute) and cancel in the difference:
>
> $$
> [X, Y][f] = \bigl(X^\mu\partial_\mu Y^\nu - Y^\mu\partial_\mu X^\nu\bigr)\partial_\nu f ,
> $$
>
> the components of Step 4 ([[§58 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^prop-58-2|591 Prop. §58.2]]). So $\mathcal L_XY = [X, Y]$. Antisymmetry is read off the components.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The bracket has two halves. $X^\mu\partial_\mu Y^\nu$ is the naive derivative of the components along $X$, the term that failed in the opening. $-Y^\mu\partial_\mu X^\nu$ comes from the Jacobian of the flow: how the flow stretches and turns the vector it carries back. Neither half is a vector; their sum is. This proof works in a chart; [[§72 Lie Derivatives#^thm-72-6|591 Thm. §72.6]] proves the same identity without coordinates, through a chart in which $X = \partial_1$.

One-forms are not in MATH 591's chapter on flows. They are carried back by the pull-back, whose matrix is the Jacobian itself rather than its inverse, and that flips the sign of the correction term.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.3: Lie Derivative of a One-Form, in Components
> Let $X$ be a smooth vector field and $\omega = \omega_\mu dx^\mu$ a smooth one-form ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-5|Def. §C2.2.5]]). Then $\mathcal L_X\omega$ is the one-form
>
> $$
> \mathcal L_X\omega = \bigl(X^\nu\partial_\nu\omega_\mu + \omega_\nu\,\partial_\mu X^\nu\bigr)\,dx^\mu .
> $$
>
> In particular, for a function $f$, $\mathcal L_X(df) = d(X[f])$.
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* exactly, any smooth $X$ and $\omega$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.3.2, eqs. (3.91)–(3.93) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.2, eq. (6.16)*

^thm-c2-5-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the curve in $T^{\ast}_pM$). By [[#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]] with $(r, s) = (0, 1)$, or directly from the pull-back in components ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]]) with $\varphi = \sigma_t$,
>
> $$
> W_\mu(t) \equiv (\sigma_t^{\ast}\omega)_\mu(p) = \omega_\beta\bigl(\sigma_t(p)\bigr)\,\frac{\partial\sigma^\beta_t}{\partial x^\mu}(p) = \omega_\beta\bigl(\sigma_t(p)\bigr)\,(J_t)^\beta{}_\mu .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (two derivatives at $t = 0$). By [[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]], part 1, applied to the function $\omega_\beta$: $\frac{d}{dt}|_0\,\omega_\beta(\sigma_t(p)) = X^\nu\partial_\nu\omega_\beta(p)$. By [[#^thm-c2-5-1|Theorem §C2.5.1]], part 3: $(J_0)^\beta{}_\mu = \delta^\beta_\mu$ and $\frac{d}{dt}|_0(J_t)^\beta{}_\mu = \partial_\mu X^\beta(p)$.
>
> **Step 3** (product rule).
>
> $$
> \dot W_\mu(0) = X^\nu\partial_\nu\omega_\beta\,\delta^\beta_\mu + \omega_\beta\,\partial_\mu X^\beta = X^\nu\partial_\nu\omega_\mu + \omega_\nu\,\partial_\mu X^\nu ,
> $$
>
> renaming $\beta \to \nu$ in the second term. The components $\dot W_\mu(0)$ belong to a covector at $p$, the velocity of the curve $t \mapsto (\sigma_t^{\ast}\omega)_p$ in $T^{\ast}_pM$; so the formula is the component form of a one-form, in every chart.
>
> **Step 4** (exact one-forms). For $\omega = df$, $\omega_\mu = \partial_\mu f$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]]):
>
> $$
> (\mathcal L_Xdf)_\mu = X^\nu\partial_\nu\partial_\mu f + \partial_\nu f\,\partial_\mu X^\nu = \partial_\mu\bigl(X^\nu\partial_\nu f\bigr) = \partial_\mu\bigl(X[f]\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> by the product rule read backwards and $\partial_\nu\partial_\mu f = \partial_\mu\partial_\nu f$. So $\mathcal L_X(df) = d(X[f])$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Compared with vectors (Step 4 of [[#^der-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2's derivation]]), the correction term has a plus sign and the derivative index of $X$ is contracted differently: a covector index is transported by $J_t$, a vector index by $J_t^{-1}$, and the two have opposite first-order terms. This is the rule behind every term of the general formula. Step 4 says that $\mathcal L_X$ commutes with $d$ on functions; the general statement, on forms of every degree, comes with the exterior derivative ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-1|Theorem §C2.6.1]], [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-3|Theorem §C2.6.3]]).

*Uses:* [[#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]], [[#^thm-c2-5-1|Theorem §C2.5.1]], [[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]], [[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-2|Theorem §C2.4.2]], [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]]

Every index of a tensor is carried back by one factor $J_t^{-1}$ or $J_t$, and each factor contributes one correction term. Couzens leaves the general formula to an exercise on the Leibniz rule (Exercise 3.6); it follows directly from the transport formula.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.4: Lie Derivative of an (r, s) Tensor, in Components
> Let $X$ be a smooth vector field and $T$ a smooth tensor field of type $(r, s)$. Then $\mathcal L_XT$ is the tensor field of type $(r, s)$ with components
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> (\mathcal L_XT)^{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\dots\nu_s} = {}& X^\lambda\,\partial_\lambda T^{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\dots\nu_s} \\
> &- T^{\lambda\mu_2\dots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\dots\nu_s}\,\partial_\lambda X^{\mu_1} - \dots - T^{\mu_1\dots\mu_{r-1}\lambda}{}_{\nu_1\dots\nu_s}\,\partial_\lambda X^{\mu_r} \\
> &+ T^{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}{}_{\lambda\nu_2\dots\nu_s}\,\partial_{\nu_1}X^\lambda + \dots + T^{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\dots\nu_{s-1}\lambda}\,\partial_{\nu_s}X^\lambda :
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> the derivative of the components along $X$, minus one term for each upper index (that index moved onto $T$ as $\lambda$, and $\partial_\lambda X$ carrying it), plus one term for each lower index (that index moved onto $\partial X$). Special cases:
>
> $$
> (\mathcal L_XT)_{\mu\nu} = X^\lambda\partial_\lambda T_{\mu\nu} + T_{\lambda\nu}\,\partial_\mu X^\lambda + T_{\mu\lambda}\,\partial_\nu X^\lambda, \qquad (\mathcal L_XT)^\mu{}_\nu = X^\lambda\partial_\lambda T^\mu{}_\nu - T^\lambda{}_\nu\,\partial_\lambda X^\mu + T^\mu{}_\lambda\,\partial_\nu X^\lambda ,
> $$
>
> and $(r, s) = (0, 0), (1, 0), (0, 1)$ give back [[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]] and [[#^thm-c2-5-3|Theorem §C2.5.3]].
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* exactly, any smooth $X$ and $T$; the formula holds in every chart (checked by script against the flow for types $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(2,0)$, $(0,2)$, $(1,1)$, $(1,2)$ and two flows).
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.2, eqs. (6.8), (6.17) · Couzens, GR1, §3.3.2, Exercise 3.6, eqs. (3.95)–(3.96) (states the route, not the formula); §5.1.4, eqs. (5.34)–(5.35) (the $(0, 2)$ case for the metric)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the curve). Fix $p$ and a chart around it. By [[#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]] the components of $(\sigma_t^{\ast}T)_p$ are
>
> $$
> C(t) \equiv (J_t^{-1})^{\mu_1}{}_{\alpha_1}\cdots(J_t^{-1})^{\mu_r}{}_{\alpha_r}\;T^{\alpha_1\dots\alpha_r}{}_{\beta_1\dots\beta_s}\bigl(\sigma_t(p)\bigr)\;(J_t)^{\beta_1}{}_{\nu_1}\cdots(J_t)^{\beta_s}{}_{\nu_s} ,
> $$
>
> a product of $r + s + 1$ smooth functions of $t$, summed over the $\alpha$'s and $\beta$'s.
>
> **Step 2** (the factors and their derivatives at $t = 0$). From Steps 3(a)–(c) of [[#^der-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2's derivation]]:
>
> $$
> (J_0^{-1})^{\mu}{}_{\alpha} = \delta^\mu_\alpha,\quad \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_0(J_t^{-1})^\mu{}_\alpha = -\partial_\alpha X^\mu;\qquad (J_0)^\beta{}_\nu = \delta^\beta_\nu,\quad \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_0(J_t)^\beta{}_\nu = \partial_\nu X^\beta;\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_0T^{\alpha\dots}{}_{\beta\dots}\bigl(\sigma_t(p)\bigr) = X^\lambda\partial_\lambda T^{\alpha\dots}{}_{\beta\dots}(p) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (product rule). $\dot C(0)$ is a sum of $r + s + 1$ terms; in each, one factor is differentiated and all others are taken at $t = 0$, where every Jacobian factor is a Kronecker delta that sets its summed index equal to the free one.
> - The differentiated factor is $T(\sigma_t(p))$: all deltas, giving $X^\lambda\partial_\lambda T^{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\dots\nu_s}$.
> - The differentiated factor is $(J_t^{-1})^{\mu_i}{}_{\alpha_i}$: it gives $-\partial_{\alpha_i}X^{\mu_i}$, and the other deltas set the remaining indices, giving $-T^{\mu_1\dots\alpha_i\dots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\dots\nu_s}\,\partial_{\alpha_i}X^{\mu_i}$; rename $\alpha_i \to \lambda$. One such term for each $i = 1, \dots, r$.
> - The differentiated factor is $(J_t)^{\beta_j}{}_{\nu_j}$: it gives $+\partial_{\nu_j}X^{\beta_j}$, hence $+T^{\mu_1\dots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\dots\beta_j\dots\nu_s}\,\partial_{\nu_j}X^{\beta_j}$; rename $\beta_j \to \lambda$. One such term for each $j = 1, \dots, s$.
>
> Adding the $1 + r + s$ terms gives the formula.
>
> **Step 4** (tensor and smooth). $\dot C(0)$ are the components of the velocity of the curve $t \mapsto (\sigma_t^{\ast}T)_p$ in the vector space of $(r, s)$ tensors at $p$, so they transform as a tensor of type $(r, s)$ under a change of chart, although each of the $1 + r + s$ terms alone does not. They are polynomials in the components of $T$, $X$ and their first derivatives, hence smooth in $p$.
>
> **Step 5** (the $(0, 2)$ and $(1, 1)$ cases). For $T_{\mu\nu}$: no upper index, two lower ones, so $X^\lambda\partial_\lambda T_{\mu\nu} + T_{\lambda\nu}\partial_\mu X^\lambda + T_{\mu\lambda}\partial_\nu X^\lambda$. For $T^\mu{}_\nu$: one term of each kind.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The general formula needs no new idea beyond the vector and one-form cases: an upper index is carried back by $J_t^{-1} \approx \mathbf 1 - t\,\partial X$, a lower one by $J_t \approx \mathbf 1 + t\,\partial X$, and the product rule adds their first-order parts. The non-tensorial term $X^\lambda\partial_\lambda T$, the one that failed in [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]], is repaired by exactly one Jacobian correction per index. The Leibniz rule ([[#^thm-c2-5-5|Theorem §C2.5.5]]) gives the same formula from the vector and one-form cases, the route of Couzens' Exercise 3.6.

## Algebraic rules

The pull-back by one diffeomorphism respects every algebraic operation on tensors. Differentiating in $t$ turns this into rules for $\mathcal L_X$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.5: Leibniz Rule; the Lie Derivative Commutes with Contraction
> Let $X$ be a smooth vector field, $S$ and $T$ smooth tensor fields of any types, $f$ a smooth function.
> 1. **Linearity over constants.** $\mathcal L_X(aS + bT) = a\,\mathcal L_XS + b\,\mathcal L_XT$ for constants $a, b$ and $S, T$ of the same type.
> 2. **Leibniz rule.** $\mathcal L_X(S\otimes T) = (\mathcal L_XS)\otimes T + S\otimes(\mathcal L_XT)$ (tensor product, [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]]). With $S = f$: $\mathcal L_X(fT) = X[f]\,T + f\,\mathcal L_XT$.
> 3. **Contraction.** For any contraction $C$ of an upper with a lower index ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-4|Def. §C2.3.4]]), $\mathcal L_X(CT) = C(\mathcal L_XT)$. In particular, for a one-form $\omega$ and a vector field $Y$,
>
> $$
> X\bigl[\omega_\mu Y^\mu\bigr] = (\mathcal L_X\omega)_\mu Y^\mu + \omega_\mu(\mathcal L_XY)^\mu .
> $$
>
> $\mathcal L_X$ is not linear over functions in $T$ (part 2 has the extra term $X[f]\,T$); it is linear over constants only.
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.3.2, Exercise 3.6, eqs. (3.95)–(3.96); App. C.2.6, eqs. (C.32)–(C.35) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.2, eqs. (6.11)–(6.13)*

^thm-c2-5-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (pull-back respects $\otimes$). In the transport formula of [[#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]] each index carries its own Jacobian factor. The components of $S\otimes T$ are products $S^{\dots}{}_{\dots}T^{\dots}{}_{\dots}$, so the factors split between the two:
>
> $$
> \sigma_t^{\ast}(S\otimes T) = (\sigma_t^{\ast}S)\otimes(\sigma_t^{\ast}T) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (Leibniz). Write $A(t) = (\sigma_t^{\ast}S)_p$ and $B(t) = (\sigma_t^{\ast}T)_p$. The tensor product is bilinear, so the product rule holds for it (componentwise it is the product rule for numbers):
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_0 A(t)\otimes B(t) = \dot A(0)\otimes B(0) + A(0)\otimes\dot B(0) = (\mathcal L_XS)_p\otimes T_p + S_p\otimes(\mathcal L_XT)_p .
> $$
>
> Linearity (part 1) is the linearity of $\sigma_t^{\ast}$ and of $d/dt$. For $S = f$, $\mathcal L_Xf = X[f]$ ([[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]]).
>
> **Step 3** (pull-back respects contraction). Contract the upper index $\mu_1$ with the lower index $\nu_1$ in the transport formula. The factors $(J_t^{-1})^{\mu_1}{}_{\alpha_1}(J_t)^{\beta_1}{}_{\mu_1} = \delta^{\beta_1}_{\alpha_1}$ cancel, which leaves the transport formula for the contracted tensor: $C(\sigma_t^{\ast}T) = \sigma_t^{\ast}(CT)$. This is the pairing rule of [[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-3|Theorem §C2.4.3]] at each index pair.
>
> **Step 4** (contraction commutes with $d/dt$). $C$ is a fixed linear map on tensors at $p$, so $\frac{d}{dt}C(\sigma_t^{\ast}T)_p = C\bigl(\frac{d}{dt}(\sigma_t^{\ast}T)_p\bigr)$; at $t = 0$, with Step 3, $\mathcal L_X(CT) = C(\mathcal L_XT)$.
>
> **Step 5** (the pairing). Apply Step 2 to $\omega\otimes Y$ and contract (Step 4): $\mathcal L_X(\omega_\mu Y^\mu) = (\mathcal L_X\omega)_\mu Y^\mu + \omega_\mu(\mathcal L_XY)^\mu$, and the left side is $X[\omega_\mu Y^\mu]$ because $\omega_\mu Y^\mu$ is a function. In components, with [[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]] and [[#^thm-c2-5-3|Theorem §C2.5.3]], the two correction terms $\omega_\nu\partial_\mu X^\nu\,Y^\mu$ and $-\omega_\nu Y^\mu\partial_\mu X^\nu$ cancel, leaving $X^\lambda\partial_\lambda(\omega_\mu Y^\mu)$, as it must.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Everything $\sigma_t^{\ast}$ preserves exactly, $\mathcal L_X$ preserves infinitesimally. The cancellation in Step 5 is the reason for the opposite signs of the vector and one-form corrections: a scalar has no correction at all. Conversely, the Leibniz rule and the cases $f$, $Y$, $\omega$ determine $\mathcal L_X$ on all tensors, since every tensor is locally a sum of functions times tensor products of $\partial_\mu$ and $dx^\nu$; this reproduces [[#^thm-c2-5-4|Theorem §C2.5.4]].

*Uses:* [[#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]], [[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]], [[#^thm-c2-5-3|Theorem §C2.5.3]], [[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^thm-c2-4-3|Theorem §C2.4.3]], [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]], [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-4|Def. §C2.3.4]]

Two vector fields give two Lie derivatives. Applied in the two orders, they differ by the Lie derivative along the bracket: the bracket is the algebraic shadow of the failure of two flows to commute.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.6: The Commutator of Two Lie Derivatives
> For smooth vector fields $X$, $Y$ and every smooth tensor field $T$,
>
> $$
> \mathcal L_X\mathcal L_YT - \mathcal L_Y\mathcal L_XT = \mathcal L_{[X, Y]}T .
> $$
>
> On functions this is the definition of the bracket; on vector fields $Z$ it reads $\mathcal L_X\mathcal L_YZ - \mathcal L_Y\mathcal L_XZ = \mathcal L_{[X,Y]}Z$ (Couzens' Exercise 3.5). The vector fields therefore act on tensors of each type as a representation of the Lie algebra of vector fields ([[§58 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^cor-58-5|591 Cor. §58.5]]).
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* exactly (checked by script on types $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,1)$, $(0,2)$).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.3.2, Exercise 3.5, eq. (3.86); App. C.2.5, eqs. (C.29)–(C.31); §6.1.1, eq. (6.11) (used for Killing vectors) · MATH 591: [[§72 Lie Derivatives#^cor-72-7|591 Cor. §72.7]] (the vector-field case, part 2)*

^thm-c2-5-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Write $D = \mathcal L_X\mathcal L_Y - \mathcal L_Y\mathcal L_X - \mathcal L_{[X,Y]}$, an operator on tensor fields of each type. The claim is $D = 0$.
>
> **Step 1** (functions). By [[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]], part 1, $\mathcal L_X\mathcal L_Yf = X[Y[f]]$, so $Df = X[Y[f]] - Y[X[f]] - [X, Y][f] = 0$ by the definition of the bracket ([[§58 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^def-58-1|591 Def. §58.1]]).
>
> **Step 2** ($D$ obeys the Leibniz rule and commutes with contraction). Apply [[#^thm-c2-5-5|Theorem §C2.5.5]] twice:
>
> $$
> \mathcal L_X\mathcal L_Y(S\otimes T) = (\mathcal L_X\mathcal L_YS)\otimes T + (\mathcal L_YS)\otimes(\mathcal L_XT) + (\mathcal L_XS)\otimes(\mathcal L_YT) + S\otimes(\mathcal L_X\mathcal L_YT) .
> $$
>
> Exchanging $X$ and $Y$ gives the same two middle terms, which cancel in the difference. With the Leibniz rule for $\mathcal L_{[X,Y]}$,
>
> $$
> D(S\otimes T) = (DS)\otimes T + S\otimes(DT) .
> $$
>
> Each of the three operators commutes with contraction, hence so does $D$.
>
> **Step 3** (exact one-forms). By [[#^thm-c2-5-3|Theorem §C2.5.3]], $\mathcal L_X(df) = d(X[f])$. Applying it twice, $\mathcal L_X\mathcal L_Y(df) = d(X[Y[f]])$, so
>
> $$
> D(df) = d\bigl(X[Y[f]] - Y[X[f]] - [X, Y][f]\bigr) = d(Df) = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (all one-forms). $(D\omega)_p$ depends only on $\omega$ near $p$ (the flows near $p$ for small times), so it may be computed in a chart, where $\omega = \omega_\mu\,dx^\mu$ is a sum of functions times the exact forms $dx^\mu = d(x^\mu)$. By Step 2 with $S = \omega_\mu$, then Steps 1 and 3,
>
> $$
> D(\omega_\mu\,dx^\mu) = (D\omega_\mu)\,dx^\mu + \omega_\mu\,D(dx^\mu) = 0 + 0 = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (vector fields). $DZ = 0$ for every vector field $Z$; by [[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]] this is an identity between iterated brackets of $X$, $Y$, $Z$, and the tool is the Jacobi identity ([[§58 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^cor-58-5|591 Cor. §58.5]]).
> *Proof (to be filled after the homework).* The written-out step is MATH 591 Cor. §72.7, part 2, whose proof is held there for a homework set; it is added here when that hold is released.
>
> **Step 6** (all tensors). In a chart $T = T^{\mu\dots}{}_{\nu\dots}\,\partial_\mu\otimes\dots\otimes dx^\nu\otimes\dots$. By Step 2, $DT$ is a sum of terms in each of which $D$ acts on one factor: on the function $T^{\mu\dots}{}_{\nu\dots}$ (zero by Step 1), on a coordinate field $\partial_\mu$ (zero by Step 5), or on $dx^\nu$ (zero by Step 4). So $DT = 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The identity is fixed by its action on functions, one-forms and vector fields, because $D$ is a derivation. On functions it is the definition of the bracket, on exact one-forms it follows because $\mathcal L_X$ commutes with $d$, and on vector fields it is the Jacobi identity in disguise. Its use in general relativity: the vector fields $K$ with $\mathcal L_Kg = 0$ (Killing vectors, [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors|§C3.13]]) are closed under the bracket, since $\mathcal L_{[K_1, K_2]}g = \mathcal L_{K_1}\mathcal L_{K_2}g - \mathcal L_{K_2}\mathcal L_{K_1}g = 0$ (GR1 (6.11)).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.7: The Bracket Measures the Failure of Flows to Commute
> Let $X$, $Y$ be smooth vector fields with flows $\sigma$, $\tau$, and $p \in M$.
> 1. **The gap.** In any chart around $p$, for small $\varepsilon$, $\delta$,
>
> $$
> \tau^\mu_\delta\bigl(\sigma_\varepsilon(p)\bigr) - \sigma^\mu_\varepsilon\bigl(\tau_\delta(p)\bigr) = \varepsilon\delta\,[X, Y]^\mu(p) + O(\varepsilon^2\delta, \varepsilon\delta^2) ,
> $$
>
> and the gap vanishes identically when $\varepsilon = 0$ or $\delta = 0$; equivalently $\partial^2/\partial\varepsilon\,\partial\delta$ of the gap at $(0, 0)$ is $[X, Y]_p$. Moving first along $X$ and then along $Y$ ends at a different point from moving first along $Y$ and then along $X$, and the leading difference is the bracket.
> 2. **Commuting fields.** $[X, Y] = 0$ if and only if the flows commute, $\tau_\delta\circ\sigma_\varepsilon = \sigma_\varepsilon\circ\tau_\delta$ wherever both sides are defined, if and only if $Y$ is invariant under the flow of $X$: $\sigma_t^{\ast}Y = Y$.
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* exactly; part 1 is a Taylor expansion with the remainder shown, part 2 is a statement about the flows on their whole domain.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.3.2, eqs. (3.87)–(3.90), Fig. 9 · MATH 591: [[§72 Lie Derivatives#^prop-72-12|591 Prop. §72.12]], [[§72 Lie Derivatives#^thm-72-9|591 Thm. §72.9]] (full proof of part 2)*

^thm-c2-5-7

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (expand the first route). By [[#^thm-c2-5-1|Theorem §C2.5.1]], part 3, with $x = p$ and all fields evaluated at $p$ unless shown,
>
> $$
> \sigma^\lambda_\varepsilon(p) = p^\lambda + \varepsilon X^\lambda + \tfrac12\varepsilon^2 X^\kappa\partial_\kappa X^\lambda + O(\varepsilon^3) .
> $$
>
> Then flow along $Y$ from $q = \sigma_\varepsilon(p)$: $\tau^\mu_\delta(q) = q^\mu + \delta\,Y^\mu(q) + \tfrac12\delta^2\,Y^\kappa\partial_\kappa Y^\mu(q) + O(\delta^3)$, with the Taylor expansion $Y^\mu(q) = Y^\mu + \varepsilon X^\lambda\partial_\lambda Y^\mu + O(\varepsilon^2)$ and $Y^\kappa\partial_\kappa Y^\mu(q) = Y^\kappa\partial_\kappa Y^\mu + O(\varepsilon)$. Collecting all terms up to second order,
>
> $$
> \tau^\mu_\delta\bigl(\sigma_\varepsilon(p)\bigr) = p^\mu + \varepsilon X^\mu + \delta Y^\mu + \tfrac12\varepsilon^2 X^\kappa\partial_\kappa X^\mu + \tfrac12\delta^2 Y^\kappa\partial_\kappa Y^\mu + \varepsilon\delta\,X^\lambda\partial_\lambda Y^\mu + O(3) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the second route). The same computation with $(X, \sigma, \varepsilon) \leftrightarrow (Y, \tau, \delta)$:
>
> $$
> \sigma^\mu_\varepsilon\bigl(\tau_\delta(p)\bigr) = p^\mu + \delta Y^\mu + \varepsilon X^\mu + \tfrac12\delta^2 Y^\kappa\partial_\kappa Y^\mu + \tfrac12\varepsilon^2 X^\kappa\partial_\kappa X^\mu + \varepsilon\delta\,Y^\lambda\partial_\lambda X^\mu + O(3) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (subtract). The first-order terms and both pure second-order terms cancel; the mixed terms remain:
>
> $$
> \tau^\mu_\delta\bigl(\sigma_\varepsilon(p)\bigr) - \sigma^\mu_\varepsilon\bigl(\tau_\delta(p)\bigr) = \varepsilon\delta\,\bigl(X^\lambda\partial_\lambda Y^\mu - Y^\lambda\partial_\lambda X^\mu\bigr) + O(3) = \varepsilon\delta\,[X, Y]^\mu(p) + O(3)
> $$
>
> by [[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]]. At $\varepsilon = 0$ both routes are $\tau_\delta(p)$, at $\delta = 0$ both are $\sigma_\varepsilon(p)$, so the gap vanishes there exactly; hence the third-order remainder contains only the mixed orders $\varepsilon^2\delta$, $\varepsilon\delta^2$. Couzens (3.87)–(3.89) does the same computation keeping first order in each parameter (the pure $\varepsilon^2$, $\delta^2$ terms dropped there cancel, Step 3); the first line of (3.87) has a misprint in the argument, $\tau^\mu(\delta + x^\nu + \varepsilon X^\nu)$ for $\tau^\mu(\delta, x^\nu + \varepsilon X^\nu)$.
>
> **Step 4** (part 2). If the flows commute, the gap of part 1 is zero for all small $\varepsilon$, $\delta$, so its mixed derivative $[X, Y]_p$ vanishes at every $p$. The converse needs the flows for all times, not only near $0$: if $[X, Y] = 0$ then $\mathcal L_XY = 0$, the curve $t \mapsto (\sigma_t^{\ast}Y)_p$ has zero velocity at every $t$ (its velocity at $t$ is the Lie derivative at $\sigma_t(p)$ carried back, [[§72 Lie Derivatives#^prop-72-8|591 Prop. §72.8]]), so $\sigma_t^{\ast}Y = Y$; then $\sigma_\varepsilon$ maps integral curves of $Y$ to integral curves of $Y$, which is the commutation of the flows. The full proof, with the domains, is [[§72 Lie Derivatives#^thm-72-9|591 Thm. §72.9]].
>
> **What the derivation shows.** First-order motions always commute ($\varepsilon X + \delta Y = \delta Y + \varepsilon X$); the first obstruction is of order $\varepsilon\delta$, and it is the bracket. Coordinate fields commute, $[\partial_\mu, \partial_\nu] = 0$, because their flows are translations of single coordinates. So a set of vector fields can be the coordinate fields of some chart only if their brackets vanish.

## What the Lie derivative is not

A derivative "in the direction of a vector at $p$" should depend only on that vector. The flow of $X$ uses $X$ on a neighbourhood, and the component formula contains $\partial_\mu X^\nu$. The next theorem shows that this dependence is real.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.5.8: The Lie Derivative Depends on X near p, Not Only on Its Value at p
> Let $X$, $Y$ be smooth vector fields, $\omega$ a one-form and $f$, $g$ smooth functions.
> 1. $\mathcal L_{fX}\,g = f\,\mathcal L_Xg$.
> 2. $\mathcal L_{fX}Y = f\,\mathcal L_XY - Y[f]\,X$.
> 3. $\mathcal L_{fX}\,\omega = f\,\mathcal L_X\omega + \omega(X)\,df$.
>
> **⚑** So on functions $(\mathcal L_Xg)_p$ depends only on $X_p$, but on vectors and one-forms (hence on all tensors with indices) $(\mathcal L_XT)_p$ changes when $X$ is changed away from $p$, or only in its derivatives at $p$, with $X_p$ fixed. For example $X = \partial_x$ and $X' = (1 + y)\,\partial_x$ agree at the origin, yet $[X, \partial_y] = 0$ while $[X', \partial_y] = -\partial_x$ there. The Lie derivative is a derivative along a flow, not along a vector. A derivative of tensors that depends only on the direction $X_p$ (linear over functions in $X$) needs one more structure, a connection ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative|§C3.5]]).
>
> *Model:* none; *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.3.2, Exercise 3.4, eq. (3.84) (part 2); §4.2 introduction (why a connection is needed) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.1 (three kinds of derivative)*

^thm-c2-5-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). By [[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]], $\mathcal L_{fX}g = fX^\mu\partial_\mu g = f\,\mathcal L_Xg$.
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). By [[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]] with $fX$ in place of $X$, and the product rule on $\partial_\mu(fX^\nu)$,
>
> $$
> (\mathcal L_{fX}Y)^\nu = fX^\mu\partial_\mu Y^\nu - Y^\mu\partial_\mu(fX^\nu) = f\bigl(X^\mu\partial_\mu Y^\nu - Y^\mu\partial_\mu X^\nu\bigr) - \bigl(Y^\mu\partial_\mu f\bigr)X^\nu = f(\mathcal L_XY)^\nu - Y[f]\,X^\nu .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (part 3). By [[#^thm-c2-5-3|Theorem §C2.5.3]],
>
> $$
> (\mathcal L_{fX}\omega)_\mu = fX^\nu\partial_\nu\omega_\mu + \omega_\nu\,\partial_\mu(fX^\nu) = f(\mathcal L_X\omega)_\mu + \omega_\nu X^\nu\,\partial_\mu f .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (the example). $X' = fX$ with $f = 1 + y$, $X = \partial_x$, $Y = \partial_y$: $[X, Y] = 0$ (constant components), and by part 2 $[X', Y] = f\cdot 0 - \partial_y(1 + y)\,\partial_x = -\partial_x$ at every point, in particular at the origin, where $f = 1$ and $X'_0 = X_0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The extra terms carry $df$, the derivative of the rescaling: a field equal to $X$ at $p$ but with a different derivative there has a different flow near $p$, which carries tensors back with a different Jacobian. Only for functions is no Jacobian involved. This is the precise sense of Couzens' remark (GR1, opening of §4.2) that the Lie derivative also differentiates the direction field.

^der-c2-5-8

*Uses:* [[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]], [[#^thm-c2-5-3|Theorem §C2.5.3]]

The Lie derivative answers the opening question for one purpose: it compares tensors along the flow of a given vector field, with no metric. It says when a tensor is unchanged by a flow ($\mathcal L_XT = 0$, a symmetry), and this is how isometries and Killing vectors ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors|§C3.13]]) and the gauge freedom of general relativity, $\delta g_{\mu\nu} = (\mathcal L_\xi g)_{\mu\nu}$ (§C5.4, planned), will be stated. It does not provide a derivative of tensors along a single direction. One combination of partial derivatives needs neither a vector field nor any other structure: the antisymmetrized one, $\partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-5|Theorem §C2.3.5]]). Which tensors does it act on, and what is its general form? That is the exterior derivative of [[§C2.6 Differential Forms|§C2.6]], where the Lie derivative of forms becomes Cartan's formula ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^thm-c2-6-3|Theorem §C2.6.3]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C2.5.1: The Rotation Field on the Plane
> *Question.* Find the flow of $R = -y\,\partial_x + x\,\partial_y$ on $\mathbf R^2$, its group, and the Lie derivative of the Euclidean metric $\delta = dx\otimes dx + dy\otimes dy$ along it.
>
> *Flow.* The integral curves solve $\dot x = -y$, $\dot y = x$ ([[#^def-c2-5-1|Def. §C2.5.1]]), so $\ddot x = -x$ and, with $(x, y)(0) = (x_0, y_0)$,
>
> $$
> \sigma_t(x_0, y_0) = (x_0\cos t - y_0\sin t,\ x_0\sin t + y_0\cos t), \qquad J_t = \begin{pmatrix}\cos t & -\sin t\\ \sin t & \cos t\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> Check: $\partial_t\sigma_t = (-x_0\sin t - y_0\cos t,\ x_0\cos t - y_0\sin t) = (-y, x)$ at $\sigma_t$. The field is complete; $\sigma_t$ is rotation by $t$, so $\sigma_t\circ\sigma_s = \sigma_{t+s}$ and $\sigma_{2\pi} = \mathrm{id}$: the one-parameter group is $\{J_t\} = SO(2)$, a circle, not a line ([[#^thm-c2-5-1|Theorem §C2.5.1]], part 4). The origin is a zero of $R$ and a fixed point of every $\sigma_t$.
>
> *From the flow.* By [[#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]] (lower indices, $\delta_{ij}$ constant), $(\sigma_t^{\ast}\delta)_{ij} = (J_t)^k{}_i\,\delta_{kl}\,(J_t)^l{}_j = (J_t^{\mathsf T}J_t)_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$, since $\cos^2t + \sin^2t = 1$. The curve is constant, so $\mathcal L_R\delta = 0$.
>
> *From the formula.* $\partial_xR^x = 0$, $\partial_yR^x = -1$, $\partial_xR^y = 1$, $\partial_yR^y = 0$. By [[#^thm-c2-5-4|Theorem §C2.5.4]], with $\partial\delta = 0$: $(\mathcal L_R\delta)_{ij} = \delta_{kj}\partial_iR^k + \delta_{ik}\partial_jR^k = \partial_iR^j + \partial_jR^i$, so $(\mathcal L_R\delta)_{xx} = 0$, $(\mathcal L_R\delta)_{xy} = \partial_xR^y + \partial_yR^x = 1 - 1 = 0$, $(\mathcal L_R\delta)_{yy} = 0$.
>
> *Contrast.* The Euler field $E = x\,\partial_x + y\,\partial_y$ has the flow $\sigma_t(x, y) = e^t(x, y)$, $J_t = e^t\mathbf 1$, so $\sigma_t^{\ast}\delta = e^{2t}\delta$ and $\mathcal L_E\delta = 2\delta$: dilations stretch lengths.
>
> *Result.* Rotations preserve the Euclidean metric, $\mathcal L_R\delta = 0$; dilations do not. A field whose flow preserves a metric is a Killing vector ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors|§C3.13]]); for the plane, translations and rotations are all of them.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.3, Example 3.7, eq. (3.66); §3.3.1, eq. (3.69) · MATH 591: [[§68 Local Existence and Uniqueness#^ex-68-1|591 Ex. §68.1]] · numbers checked by script*

^ex-c2-5-1

> [!example] Example §C2.5.2: Brackets of the Translations and the Rotation of the Plane
> *Question* (d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 5.17 (iv)–(v)). Compute all brackets of $X = \partial_x$, $Y = \partial_y$, $Z = R = -y\,\partial_x + x\,\partial_y$, first in Cartesian and then in polar coordinates $(r, \phi)$.
>
> *Cartesian.* By [[#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]], $[A, B]^\nu = A^\mu\partial_\mu B^\nu - B^\mu\partial_\mu A^\nu$. $X$, $Y$ have constant components, so $[X, Y] = 0$. For $[X, Z]$: $X^\mu\partial_\mu Z^\nu = \partial_x(-y, x) = (0, 1)$ and $Z^\mu\partial_\mu X^\nu = 0$, so $[\partial_x, R] = \partial_y$. For $[Y, Z]$: $\partial_y(-y, x) = (-1, 0)$, so $[\partial_y, R] = -\partial_x$.
>
> *Polar.* $\partial_x = \cos\phi\,\partial_r - (\sin\phi/r)\,\partial_\phi$, $\partial_y = \sin\phi\,\partial_r + (\cos\phi/r)\,\partial_\phi$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]]; d'Inverno's answer to (ii)), and $R = \partial_\phi$, with components $(0, 1)$. Then $X^\mu\partial_\mu(0, 1) = 0$ and $R^\mu\partial_\mu X^\nu = \partial_\phi(\cos\phi, -\sin\phi/r) = (-\sin\phi, -\cos\phi/r)$, so
>
> $$
> [\partial_x, \partial_\phi] = 0 - (-\sin\phi, -\cos\phi/r) = \sin\phi\,\partial_r + \frac{\cos\phi}{r}\,\partial_\phi = \partial_y ,
> $$
>
> the same vector field as in Cartesian coordinates, although the two computations share no term. In the same way $[\partial_y, \partial_\phi] = -\partial_x$.
>
> *Result.* $[\partial_x, \partial_y] = 0$, $[\partial_x, R] = \partial_y$, $[\partial_y, R] = -\partial_x$, in every chart. The three fields span a vector space closed under the bracket: the Lie algebra of the Euclidean group of the plane. Translations commute with each other; a translation and the rotation do not, and by [[#^thm-c2-5-7|Theorem §C2.5.7]] their flows fail to close a parallelogram by $\varepsilon\delta\,\partial_y$ (figure after Theorem §C2.5.7).
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 5.17 (p. 82) and its answer (p. 578) · Couzens, GR1, §3.3.2, Def. 25 · brackets checked by script in both charts*

> [!example] Example §C2.5.3: The Lie Derivative of a (0, 2) Tensor Two Ways
> *Question.* For $X = R = -y\,\partial_x + x\,\partial_y$ and $T = x^2\,dx\otimes dx$, compute $\mathcal L_RT$ from the flow, from the formula, and in polar coordinates.
>
> *From the flow.* With $\sigma_t$ and $J_t$ of [[#^ex-c2-5-1|Example §C2.5.1]], pulling back the one-form $dx$ gives $\sigma_t^{\ast}dx = d(x\cos t - y\sin t) = \cos t\,dx - \sin t\,dy$, and the function $x^2$ becomes $(x\cos t - y\sin t)^2$. By the Leibniz property of the pull-back (Step 1 of [[#^der-c2-5-5|Theorem §C2.5.5's derivation]]),
>
> $$
> \sigma_t^{\ast}T = (x\cos t - y\sin t)^2\,(\cos t\,dx - \sin t\,dy)\otimes(\cos t\,dx - \sin t\,dy) .
> $$
>
> At $t = 0$ the derivative of $(x\cos t - y\sin t)^2$ is $2x\cdot(-y)$ and that of $\cos t\,dx - \sin t\,dy$ is $-dy$. By the product rule,
>
> $$
> \mathcal L_RT = -2xy\,dx\otimes dx + x^2\bigl(-dy\otimes dx - dx\otimes dy\bigr) .
> $$
>
> *From the formula.* By [[#^thm-c2-5-4|Theorem §C2.5.4]], $(\mathcal L_RT)_{ij} = R^k\partial_kT_{ij} + T_{kj}\partial_iR^k + T_{ik}\partial_jR^k$, with only $T_{xx} = x^2 \neq 0$ and the $\partial R$ of Example §C2.5.1:
> - $xx$: $R^x\partial_x(x^2) + 2T_{xx}\partial_xR^x = -y\cdot 2x + 0 = -2xy$;
> - $xy$: $0 + T_{xx}\partial_yR^x = -x^2$; $yx$: $T_{xx}\partial_yR^x = -x^2$; $yy$: $0$.
>
> The same tensor. The naive term $R^k\partial_kT_{ij}$ alone gives only $-2xy\,dx\otimes dx$.
>
> *In polar coordinates.* $R = \partial_\theta$ has constant components, so $\partial X = 0$ and $\mathcal L_RT$ is just $\partial_\theta$ of the polar components. From $dx = \cos\theta\,dr - r\sin\theta\,d\theta$ and $x = r\cos\theta$: $T_{rr} = r^2\cos^4\theta$, $T_{r\theta} = T_{\theta r} = -r^3\cos^3\theta\sin\theta$, $T_{\theta\theta} = r^4\cos^2\theta\sin^2\theta$. Differentiating in $\theta$,
>
> $$
> (\mathcal L_RT)_{rr} = -4r^2\cos^3\theta\sin\theta, \quad (\mathcal L_RT)_{r\theta} = r^3\cos^2\theta\,(3\sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta), \quad (\mathcal L_RT)_{\theta\theta} = 2r^4\cos\theta\sin\theta\,(\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta) .
> $$
>
> Transforming the Cartesian result instead: its $rr$ component is $-2xy\cos^2\theta - 2x^2\cos\theta\sin\theta = -2r^2\cos^3\theta\sin\theta - 2r^2\cos^3\theta\sin\theta = -4r^2\cos^3\theta\sin\theta$, and the other two agree as well.
>
> *Result.* Three computations, one tensor: the Lie derivative is chart-independent although built from partial derivatives. In the chart adapted to $X$ ($X = \partial_\theta$) it is a plain partial derivative of components (d'Inverno (6.10)); in any other chart the Jacobian terms are essential.
>
> *Source: computed here as an instance of Couzens, GR1, §3.3.2, eqs. (3.91)–(3.96) and d'Inverno & Vickers, §6.2, eqs. (6.8)–(6.10), (6.17) · all three computations checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.5.4: The Field ∂φ on the Sphere
> *Question* (Couzens, GR1, Example 3.7). On $S^2$ with the angles $(\theta, \phi)$ of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-3|Example §C2.1.3]], find the flow of $X = \partial_\phi$ and the Lie derivatives of the round metric and of the field $\cos\phi\,\partial_\theta$.
>
> *Flow.* The integral curves solve $d\theta/dt = 0$, $d\phi/dt = 1$, so $\sigma_t(\theta, \phi) = (\theta, \phi + t)$: the circles of constant latitude. In the embedding $S^2 \subset \mathbf R^3$, $(\sin\theta\cos\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\theta)$, the push-forward of $\partial_\phi$ is $-y\,\partial_x + x\,\partial_y$, so $\sigma_t$ is the rotation by $t$ about the $z$-axis. That rotation is defined on the whole sphere, also at the poles and on the meridian the chart misses; the poles are zeros of $X$ and fixed points. $S^2$ is compact, so the field is complete ([[§70 Complete Vector Fields#^thm-70-2|591 Thm. §70.2]]), and $\sigma_{2\pi} = \mathrm{id}$: again the group $SO(2)$.
>
> *Lie derivatives.* In the chart $(\theta, \phi)$, $\partial_\phi$ has constant components, so $\partial X = 0$ and by [[#^thm-c2-5-4|Theorem §C2.5.4]] $\mathcal L_{\partial_\phi}$ differentiates components in $\phi$. The round metric $g = d\theta\otimes d\theta + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi\otimes d\phi$ (the pull-back of the Euclidean metric, [[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms#^ex-c2-4-2|Example §C2.4.2]]) has components independent of $\phi$, so $\mathcal L_{\partial_\phi}g = 0$: rotations about the axis are isometries. The height $z = \cos\theta$ is invariant, $\mathcal L_{\partial_\phi}z = 0$. The field $W = \cos\phi\,\partial_\theta$ is not: $\mathcal L_{\partial_\phi}W = [\partial_\phi, W] = \partial_\phi(\cos\phi)\,\partial_\theta = -\sin\phi\,\partial_\theta$.
>
> *Result.* A chart adapted to the flow makes the Lie derivative a partial derivative, and $\mathcal L_{\partial_\phi}g = 0$ says in coordinate-free form that $g_{\mu\nu}$ does not depend on $\phi$. The same mechanism turns "the metric does not depend on $t$" into a statement about the field $\partial_t$ in the static spacetimes of C6–C7 (planned).
>
