[[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms|§C2.2]] built, at each event $p$, the two spaces of first-order objects: the tangent space $T_pM$ with the coordinate basis $\partial_\mu$, and its dual $T^{\ast}_pM$ with the basis $dx^\mu$. Under a change of chart their components change with the Jacobian of the transition function at $p$ and with its inverse ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]). General relativity needs objects with more indices: the metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]]), the energy–momentum tensor $T^{\mu\nu}$, which [[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^thm-c1-1-4|Theorem §C1.1.4]] found must be the source of gravity, and later the curvature, with four indices. At level B such objects were defined by their components and a transformation law with the constant Lorentz matrix $\Lambda$ ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, which does not enter the law), and an equation between tensors of one type held in every inertial frame once it held in one ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-8|Theorem §B2.2.8]]). The question of this section is *which rules of level B survive when $\Lambda$ is replaced by the Jacobian $\partial x'^\mu/\partial x^\nu$ of an arbitrary change of chart, a matrix that changes from point to point.*

Most of them survive, because they are linear algebra at one point, and at one point the Jacobian is just an invertible matrix. One rule fails. Level B differentiated tensor fields freely: if $T$ is a tensor field, so is $\partial_\lambda T$ ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]], part 2; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). Try this on the simplest field of the plane, $V = \partial_x$. Its Cartesian components $(V^x, V^y) = (1, 0)$ are constant, so $\partial_jV^i = 0$ at every point. In polar coordinates the same field has the components $V^r = \cos\theta$, $V^\theta = -\sin\theta/r$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]]), and

$$
\frac{\partial V^r}{\partial\theta} = -\sin\theta \neq 0 .
$$

An array that vanishes in one chart and not in another is not the array of components of a tensor ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 4). The first derivative of a vector field, the most basic operation of field theory, is not a tensor once charts are arbitrary.

The section builds the answer in order. A tensor at a point is defined as a multilinear map, with no chart at all ([[#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]); tensor fields assign one to every point ([[#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]), and the tensor product multiplies them ([[#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]]). Their components and the general transformation law then follow as a theorem, with level B's law as the special case of constant Jacobians ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]]). Contraction ([[#^def-c2-3-4|Def. §C2.3.4]], [[#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]), symmetrization ([[#^def-c2-3-5|Def. §C2.3.5]]) and the quotient rule ([[#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]]) carry over. Then the failure is derived in full: the extra term in the transformation of $\partial_\mu V^\nu$ ([[#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]]), and the combinations of partial derivatives that are tensors all the same ([[#^thm-c2-3-5|Theorem §C2.3.5]]). Last come objects whose law carries a power of the Jacobian determinant, tensor densities ([[#^def-c2-3-6|Def. §C2.3.6]]), with the Levi-Civita symbol and the determinant as the examples ([[#^def-c2-3-7|Def. §C2.3.7]], [[#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]]); they are what integration needs.

**Setup.** A smooth manifold $M$ of dimension $n$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]); spacetime is the case $n = 4$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]]). At a point $p$ there are two charts ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]) with coordinates $x^\mu$ and $x'^\mu$, related on the overlap of their domains by the smooth transition function $x' = x'(x)$ and its smooth inverse $x = x(x')$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]). Their Jacobian matrices at the same point are inverse to each other ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], part 3):

$$
\frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\,\frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\nu} = \delta^\mu_\nu, \qquad \frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x'^\alpha}\,\frac{\partial x'^\alpha}{\partial x^\nu} = \delta^\mu_\nu .
$$

There is no metric in this section: no index is raised or lowered, and the position of an index is part of the type ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^cau-c2-2-1|Caution: Index position is the type, and nothing raises it yet]]). Every statement is an exact theorem of differential geometry: linear algebra at a point and the chain rule, with no physical model and no approximation. The statements hold in any dimension. The examples use the plane $\mathbf R^2$ with Cartesian coordinates $(x, y)$ and polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$, the latter a chart on $r > 0$ with $\theta$ in an open interval of length $2\pi$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^rem-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Remark: Coordinate singularities are failures of a chart, not of the manifold]]). The identities and numbers of the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): no metric enters, so the signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ of [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]] plays no role; Greek indices label the $n$ coordinates of a chart (on spacetime $0$–$3$, $x^0 = t$); a repeated upper–lower pair is summed; $\partial_\mu = \partial/\partial x^\mu$, $\partial'_\mu = \partial/\partial x'^\mu$; a prime marks components in the second chart, $T'^{\mu\cdots}{}_{\nu\cdots}$, always at the same point $p$ as the unprimed ones; round and square brackets around indices symmetrize and antisymmetrize with weight $1/k!$ ([[#^def-c2-3-5|Def. §C2.3.5]]); densities are weighted with $J = \det(\partial x^\mu/\partial x'^\nu)$ ([[#^def-c2-3-6|Def. §C2.3.6]]).

## Tensors at a point and tensor fields

At level B a tensor was an array of numbers attached to each inertial frame, with a rule for converting between frames. On a manifold there are infinitely many charts, overlapping in complicated ways, and checking a rule chart by chart is not a definition one can work with. The tangent and cotangent spaces at $p$ are defined without charts, so anything built from them by linear algebra is too. That is the definition.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.3.1: Tensor of Type (r, s) at a Point
> *Structure.* Let $p \in M$, with tangent space $T_pM$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]) and cotangent space $T^{\ast}_pM$, its dual ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-5|Def. §C2.2.5]]). A **tensor of type $(r, s)$ at $p$** is a map
>
> $$
> T : \underbrace{T^{\ast}_pM \times \cdots \times T^{\ast}_pM}_{r} \times \underbrace{T_pM \times \cdots \times T_pM}_{s} \longrightarrow \mathbf R
> $$
>
> that is linear in each of its $r + s$ arguments when the others are held fixed: a multilinear functional ([[§38 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-85|LADR Def. 9.85]]). The tensors of type $(r, s)$ at $p$ form a real vector space $\mathcal T^r_s(p)$ under pointwise addition and multiplication by numbers. $T$ has $r$ covector slots and $s$ vector slots; in components these become $r$ upper and $s$ lower indices ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]]). The number $r + s$ is the **rank**.
>
> *First cases.*
> 1. Type $(0, 0)$: a real number.
> 2. Type $(0, 1)$: a linear map $T_pM \to \mathbf R$, that is, a covector ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-5|Def. §C2.2.5]]).
> 3. Type $(1, 0)$: a linear map $T^{\ast}_pM \to \mathbf R$. Each $X \in T_pM$ gives one, $\omega \mapsto \omega(X)$, and every one arises in this way from exactly one $X$, because a finite-dimensional space is canonically the dual of its dual (both have dimension $n$, [[§12 Duality#^ladr-3-111|LADR Thm. 3.111]], and $X \mapsto (\omega \mapsto \omega(X))$ is injective). Vectors are the tensors of type $(1, 0)$.
> 4. Type $(1, 1)$: each linear map $A : T_pM \to T_pM$ gives the tensor $(\omega, X) \mapsto \omega(AX)$; conversely a $(1, 1)$ tensor $T$ gives, for each $X$, the $(1, 0)$ tensor $T(\cdot, X)$, a vector $AX$ by case 3. So $(1, 1)$ tensors are the linear maps of $T_pM$ to itself.
> 5. Type $(0, 2)$: a bilinear form on $T_pM$, such as the metric $g_p$ of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]] (its home on a manifold is [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor|§C3.1]]). The alternating $(0, k)$ tensors are the $k$-forms of MATH 591 ([[§75 Multilinear Algebra#^def-75-1|591 Def. §75.1]]; physics form in [[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]]).
>
> *No metric.* Nothing identifies $T_pM$ with $T^{\ast}_pM$ in this chapter, so the pair $(r, s)$, and with it the height of every index, is part of what the tensor is ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^cau-c2-2-1|Caution: Index position is the type, and nothing raises it yet]]).
>
> *Names in the sources.* Couzens writes type $(q, r)$, d'Inverno & Vickers $(p, q)$, level B $(m, n)$; in each the first number counts upper indices. Level B met this definition as a remark beside its component definition ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^rem-b2-2-2|§B2.2, Remark: Tensors without coordinates]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$); here it is the definition.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.5, Definition 19, eq. (3.41) (p. 49) · Schutz §3.2 (p. 56: a $(0, N)$ tensor is a function of $N$ vectors, linear in each) · d'Inverno & Vickers §5.7 (p. 74, valence $(p, q)$) · [[§38 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-88|LADR Def. 9.88]]*

> [!definition] Definition §C2.3.2: Tensor Field
> A **tensor field of type $(r, s)$** on $M$ (or on an open subset) assigns to each point $p$ a tensor $T_p \in \mathcal T^r_s(p)$ ([[#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]). In a chart $(U, x^\mu)$ it has the $n^{r+s}$ **component functions** $T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_s}(x) = T_p\bigl(dx^{\mu_1}|_p, \dots, \partial_{\nu_s}|_p\bigr)$, $p \in U$, functions of the coordinates. The field is **smooth** if its component functions are smooth in every chart of an atlas ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-2|Def. §C2.1.2]]). This does not depend on the atlas: on an overlap the components of another chart are those of the first multiplied by entries of Jacobian matrices ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 2), which are smooth because transition functions are ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]). "Tensor field" means smooth tensor field from now on. The set of them is $\mathcal T^r_s(M)$.
>
> *First cases.* $\mathcal T^0_0(M)$ is the set of smooth functions, the **scalar fields** ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-5|Def. §C2.1.5]]): one number at each point, the same whichever chart labels the point. In coordinates this is the rule $f'(x') = f(x)$ when $x$ and $x'$ are the coordinates of the same point, as at level B ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). $\mathcal T^1_0(M)$ is the set of vector fields ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-4|Def. §C2.2.4]]) and $\mathcal T^0_1(M)$ that of one-forms ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-7|Def. §C2.2.7]]). Tensor fields of one type are added, and multiplied by smooth functions, point by point.
>
> MATH 591 has vector fields, one-forms and $k$-forms on manifolds ([[§80 Differential Forms on Manifolds#^def-80-1|591 Def. §80.1]]), but not general $(r, s)$ tensor fields; this box is their home.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.5 (p. 50, the set $\mathcal T^{q,r}(M)$) · d'Inverno & Vickers §5.8, eq. (5.30) (p. 75)*

^def-c2-3-2

> [!definition] Definition §C2.3.3: Tensor Product
> For $S \in \mathcal T^r_s(p)$ and $T \in \mathcal T^{r'}_{s'}(p)$ ([[#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]), the **tensor product** $S \otimes T \in \mathcal T^{r+r'}_{s+s'}(p)$ is
>
> $$
> (S \otimes T)(\omega^1, \dots, \omega^r, \eta^1, \dots, \eta^{r'}, X_1, \dots, X_s, Y_1, \dots, Y_{s'}) = S(\omega^1, \dots, \omega^r, X_1, \dots, X_s)\;T(\eta^1, \dots, \eta^{r'}, Y_1, \dots, Y_{s'}) .
> $$
>
> It is multilinear, since each factor is linear in its own arguments and does not contain the others ([[§77 The Wedge Product#^def-77-2|591 Def. §77.2]], the case of $(0, k)$ tensors; [[§38 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-88b|LADR Def. 9.88b]]). The convention, Couzens', puts the covector slots of $S$ before those of $T$, and likewise the vector slots. Evaluating both sides on basis elements gives the components
>
> $$
> (S \otimes T)^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r\nu_1\cdots\nu_{r'}}{}_{\rho_1\cdots\rho_s\sigma_1\cdots\sigma_{s'}} = S^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}_{\rho_1\cdots\rho_s}\;T^{\nu_1\cdots\nu_{r'}}{}_{\sigma_1\cdots\sigma_{s'}} ,
> $$
>
> the outer product of level B ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], part 2; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). For tensor fields the product is taken point by point, and is smooth when both factors are. The product is associative but not commutative: $(dx \otimes dy)(\partial_x, \partial_y) = 1$, while $(dy \otimes dx)(\partial_x, \partial_y) = 0$. With a scalar, $f \otimes T = fT$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.5, eqs. (3.45)–(3.46) (p. 50) · d'Inverno & Vickers §5.9, eq. (5.35) (pp. 76–77)*

^def-c2-3-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.1: Components and the General Transformation Law
> Let $T \in \mathcal T^r_s(p)$ ([[#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]), and let $x^\mu$ and $x'^\mu$ be two charts at $p$.
> 1. **Components.** The components $T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_s} = T(dx^{\mu_1}, \dots, dx^{\mu_r}, \partial_{\nu_1}, \dots, \partial_{\nu_s})$ determine $T$: for covectors $\omega^k = \omega^k{}_\mu dx^\mu$ and vectors $X_l = X_l{}^\nu\partial_\nu$,
>
> $$
> T(\omega^1, \dots, \omega^r, X_1, \dots, X_s) = T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_s}\;\omega^1{}_{\mu_1}\cdots\omega^r{}_{\mu_r}\;X_1{}^{\nu_1}\cdots X_s{}^{\nu_s} .
> $$
>
> The $n^{r+s}$ tensor products ([[#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]]) $\partial_{\mu_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \partial_{\mu_r} \otimes dx^{\nu_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes dx^{\nu_s}$ form a basis of $\mathcal T^r_s(p)$, and
>
> $$
> T = T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_s}\;\partial_{\mu_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \partial_{\mu_r} \otimes dx^{\nu_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes dx^{\nu_s} .
> $$
>
> 2. **Transformation law.** The components in the two charts are related by
>
> $$
> T'^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_s} = \frac{\partial x'^{\mu_1}}{\partial x^{\alpha_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial x'^{\mu_r}}{\partial x^{\alpha_r}}\;\frac{\partial x^{\beta_1}}{\partial x'^{\nu_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial x^{\beta_s}}{\partial x'^{\nu_s}}\;T^{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_r}{}_{\beta_1\cdots\beta_s} ,
> $$
>
> all Jacobians at $p$: one factor $\partial x'/\partial x$ for each upper index, as for a vector ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]), and one factor $\partial x/\partial x'$ for each lower index, as for a covector ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]). For a $(1, 1)$ tensor, $T'^\mu{}_\nu = (\partial x'^\mu/\partial x^\alpha)(\partial x^\beta/\partial x'^\nu)\,T^\alpha{}_\beta$.
> 3. **Converse.** If arrays of $n^{r+s}$ numbers are given in every chart at $p$ and any two of them are related by the law of part 2, they are the components of exactly one tensor of type $(r, s)$ at $p$. The law applied twice, $x \to x' \to x''$, agrees with the law applied once, $x \to x''$.
> 4. ⚑ **Tensor equations hold in every chart.** If all components of $T$ vanish in one chart at $p$, they vanish in every chart at $p$. Hence an equation $A = B$ between tensors (or tensor fields) of the same type that holds in one chart holds in every chart. An array that is zero in one chart and not zero in another is not a tensor.
> 5. **Level B as a special case.** For inertial coordinates of Minkowski spacetime, $x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu + a^\mu$, the Jacobians are the constant matrices $\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$ and $(\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu$, and part 2 is the definition of a Lorentz tensor ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]]); part 4 is [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-8|Theorem §B2.2.8]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$; the entries of $\Lambda$ are the same in both signatures).
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a smooth manifold; *applies:* any two charts whose domains contain $p$; for tensor fields, at every point of the overlap, with the Jacobians evaluated there.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.5, eqs. (3.42)–(3.44) (pp. 49–50; the law is stated as following "simply") · d'Inverno & Vickers §5.7, eqs. (5.28)–(5.29) (p. 74) · 't Hooft ch. 4, eqs. (4.10)–(4.15) (pp. 16–17; the composition exercise)*

^thm-c2-3-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the components determine $T$). Expand each argument in the bases of the chart ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]], [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]): $\omega^k = \omega^k{}_\mu\,dx^\mu$, $X_l = X_l{}^\nu\,\partial_\nu$. Linearity in the first slot pulls out $\omega^1{}_{\mu_1}$, linearity in the second pulls out $\omega^2{}_{\mu_2}$, and so on through all $r + s$ slots:
>
> $$
> T(\omega^1, \dots, X_s) = \omega^1{}_{\mu_1}\cdots\omega^r{}_{\mu_r}\,X_1{}^{\nu_1}\cdots X_s{}^{\nu_s}\;T(dx^{\mu_1}, \dots, dx^{\mu_r}, \partial_{\nu_1}, \dots, \partial_{\nu_s}) = T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_s}\,\omega^1{}_{\mu_1}\cdots X_s{}^{\nu_s} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (basis). Write $E_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}^{\nu_1\cdots\nu_s} = \partial_{\mu_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \partial_{\mu_r} \otimes dx^{\nu_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes dx^{\nu_s}$, where $\partial_\mu$ is read as the $(1, 0)$ tensor $\omega \mapsto \omega(\partial_\mu)$ ([[#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]], case 3). By [[#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]] and the duality $dx^\alpha(\partial_\beta) = \delta^\alpha_\beta$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]], part 1),
>
> $$
> E_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}^{\nu_1\cdots\nu_s}\bigl(dx^{\alpha_1}, \dots, dx^{\alpha_r}, \partial_{\beta_1}, \dots, \partial_{\beta_s}\bigr) = dx^{\alpha_1}(\partial_{\mu_1})\cdots dx^{\alpha_r}(\partial_{\mu_r})\;dx^{\nu_1}(\partial_{\beta_1})\cdots dx^{\nu_s}(\partial_{\beta_s}) = \delta^{\alpha_1}_{\mu_1}\cdots\delta^{\alpha_r}_{\mu_r}\,\delta^{\nu_1}_{\beta_1}\cdots\delta^{\nu_s}_{\beta_s} .
> $$
>
> So the tensor $S = T^{\mu_1\cdots}{}_{\nu_1\cdots}\,E_{\mu_1\cdots}{}^{\nu_1\cdots}$ has the components $S^{\alpha_1\cdots}{}_{\beta_1\cdots} = T^{\alpha_1\cdots}{}_{\beta_1\cdots}$. By Step 1 a tensor is determined by its components, so $S = T$: the $E$'s span. If $c^{\mu_1\cdots}{}_{\nu_1\cdots}E_{\mu_1\cdots}{}^{\nu_1\cdots} = 0$, evaluating on $(dx^{\alpha_1}, \dots, \partial_{\beta_s})$ gives $c^{\alpha_1\cdots}{}_{\beta_1\cdots} = 0$: they are independent. There are $n^{r+s}$ of them, the dimension of a space of multilinear functionals ([[§38 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-87|LADR Thm. 9.87]]). Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 3** (the bases of the second chart). At $p$, $\partial'_\nu = (\partial x^\beta/\partial x'^\nu)\,\partial_\beta$ and $dx'^\mu = (\partial x'^\mu/\partial x^\alpha)\,dx^\alpha$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], part 1; [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]], part 3).
>
> **Step 4** (the law). Insert Step 3 into the definition of the primed components and use linearity in every slot, as in Step 1:
>
> $$
> T'^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_s} = T\bigl(dx'^{\mu_1}, \dots, dx'^{\mu_r}, \partial'_{\nu_1}, \dots, \partial'_{\nu_s}\bigr) = T\Bigl(\frac{\partial x'^{\mu_1}}{\partial x^{\alpha_1}}dx^{\alpha_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial x^{\beta_s}}{\partial x'^{\nu_s}}\partial_{\beta_s}\Bigr) = \frac{\partial x'^{\mu_1}}{\partial x^{\alpha_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial x^{\beta_s}}{\partial x'^{\nu_s}}\;T^{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_r}{}_{\beta_1\cdots\beta_s} .
> $$
>
> Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 5** (converse). Let $A$ be the array given in the chart $x$, and define the tensor $T = A^{\mu_1\cdots}{}_{\nu_1\cdots}\,E_{\mu_1\cdots}{}^{\nu_1\cdots}$ (Step 2); its components in $x$ are $A$. By Step 4 its components in any chart $x'$ are the law-transformed $A$, which by hypothesis is the array $A'$ given in $x'$. So one tensor has all the given arrays as its components; by Step 1 there is only one. For three charts, the chain rule at $p$ ([[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]; [[§30 The Differential in Coordinates#^cor-30-3|591 Cor. §30.3]]) gives $\frac{\partial x''^\mu}{\partial x'^\lambda}\frac{\partial x'^\lambda}{\partial x^\alpha} = \frac{\partial x''^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}$ and $\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\kappa}\frac{\partial x'^\kappa}{\partial x''^\nu} = \frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x''^\nu}$. Applying the law $x \to x'$ and then $x' \to x''$ multiplies each upper index by the product on the left of the first identity and each lower index by the product on the left of the second, which are the factors of the direct law $x \to x''$. Part 3 is proved.
>
> **Step 6** (vanishing in every chart). Each primed component in Step 4 is a sum of terms, each containing one unprimed component as a factor. If every $T^{\alpha_1\cdots}{}_{\beta_1\cdots}$ is zero, every term is zero. For tensors $A$, $B$ of one type, $A - B$ is a tensor of that type ($\mathcal T^r_s(p)$ is a vector space), whose components are $A^{\cdots}{}_{\cdots} - B^{\cdots}{}_{\cdots}$; if they vanish in one chart they vanish in all. Part 4 is proved.
>
> **Step 7** (inertial coordinates). For $x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu + a^\mu$ with constant $\Lambda$ and $a$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), $\partial x'^\mu/\partial x^\nu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$ at every point, and the inverse, $x^\nu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu(x'^\mu - a^\mu)$, gives $\partial x^\nu/\partial x'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]). Substituting in Step 4 gives the law of [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]] factor by factor. Part 5 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - Steps 1–6 use only linear algebra at the single point $p$ and the chain rule. That the Jacobian varies from point to point plays no role in the algebra of tensors at a point.
> - At level B the law was the definition and had to be checked for consistency. Here the multilinear map is the definition and the law is derived from it, so its consistency over any number of charts (Step 5) is automatic.

Part 4 is what makes tensors the language of general relativity. If the laws of physics are equations between tensor fields of the same type, then they hold in every chart once they hold in one, and no chart is preferred. This is level B's covariance with the Lorentz group replaced by all smooth changes of chart; it becomes a principle of the theory in [[§C4.1 Minimal Coupling|§C4.1]]. The examples at the end show part 4 at work on the Kronecker delta and on the Euclidean metric ([[#^ex-c2-3-1|Example §C2.3.1]], [[#^ex-c2-3-2|Example §C2.3.2]]).

## Operations that make tensors from tensors

Linear combinations of tensors of one type and tensor products are tensors by definition. Two more operations of level B carry over: summing an upper against a lower index, and taking the symmetric or antisymmetric part over indices of one height. The quotient rule, level B's practical test, carries over too.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.3.4: Contraction
> Let $T \in \mathcal T^r_s(p)$ with $r, s \ge 1$ ([[#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]), and choose a covector slot $a$ and a vector slot $b$. The **contraction** of $T$ over the slots $(a, b)$ is the tensor $C^a_bT \in \mathcal T^{r-1}_{s-1}(p)$,
>
> $$
> (C^a_bT)(\omega^1, \dots, \widehat{\omega^a}, \dots, \omega^r, X_1, \dots, \widehat{X_b}, \dots, X_s) = \sum_{\mu=1}^n T(\omega^1, \dots, \underbrace{e^\mu}_{a}, \dots, \omega^r, X_1, \dots, \underbrace{e_\mu}_{b}, \dots, X_s) ,
> $$
>
> where $e_\mu$ is any basis of $T_pM$, $e^\mu$ its dual basis ($e^\mu(e_\nu) = \delta^\mu_\nu$, [[§12 Duality#^ladr-3-112|LADR Def. 3.112]]), and a hat marks an omitted argument. The sum does not depend on the basis ([[#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]). In the coordinate basis $e_\mu = \partial_\mu$, $e^\mu = dx^\mu$, the components are those of $T$ with the $a$-th upper and the $b$-th lower index set equal and summed; for $T$ of type $(2, 2)$, for example, $(C^1_2T)^\mu{}_\nu = T^{\sigma\mu}{}_{\nu\sigma}$. Different choices of the slots give in general different tensors. A full contraction, which removes every index, is a scalar.
>
> *Two cases met before.* For a $(1, 1)$ tensor, $C^1_1T = T^\mu{}_\mu$ is the trace of the linear map $T_pM \to T_pM$ that $T$ is ([[#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]], case 4). The pairing $\omega(X) = \omega_\mu X^\mu$ of [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]] is the contraction of $X \otimes \omega$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.5, eqs. (3.47)–(3.48) (pp. 50–51) · d'Inverno & Vickers §5.9, eqs. (5.36)–(5.38) (pp. 77–78)*

^def-c2-3-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.2: Contraction Is Chart-Independent
> 1. The sum in [[#^def-c2-3-4|Def. §C2.3.4]] is the same for every basis $e_\mu$ of $T_pM$; $C^a_bT$ is a tensor of type $(r - 1, s - 1)$.
> 2. In components, the contracted array $T^{\cdots\sigma\cdots}{}_{\cdots\sigma\cdots}$ obeys the law of [[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]] for type $(r - 1, s - 1)$: the two Jacobian factors of the contracted pair cancel, $(\partial x'^\sigma/\partial x^\alpha)(\partial x^\beta/\partial x'^\sigma) = \delta^\beta_\alpha$.
> 3. ⚑ **Only an upper index with a lower one.** Summing two indices at the same height is not chart-independent. For the Euclidean metric $g$ of the plane (Cartesian components $\delta_{ij}$), $\sum_\mu g_{\mu\mu} = 2$ in Cartesian coordinates and $1 + r^2$ in polar coordinates at the same point ([[#^ex-c2-3-2|Example §C2.3.2]]). The factor that replaces $\delta^\beta_\alpha$ is $\sum_\sigma(\partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\sigma)(\partial x^\beta/\partial x'^\sigma)$, which is the identity matrix only when the Jacobian is an orthogonal matrix, the case of rotations at level A.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a smooth manifold; *applies:* any tensor of type $(r, s)$, $r, s \ge 1$, at a point, and pointwise for tensor fields.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.5, eqs. (3.47)–(3.48) (pp. 50–51) · d'Inverno & Vickers §5.9, eqs. (5.36)–(5.37) (p. 77) · 't Hooft ch. 4, eqs. (4.16)–(4.18) and the warning after (4.12) (pp. 16–17)*

^thm-c2-3-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (change of basis). Let $e'_\nu = A^\mu{}_\nu e_\mu$ be a second basis, $A$ an invertible matrix. Its dual basis is $e'^\nu = (A^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu e^\mu$, because $(A^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu e^\mu\bigl(A^\rho{}_\lambda e_\rho\bigr) = (A^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu A^\rho{}_\lambda\,\delta^\mu_\rho = (A^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu A^\mu{}_\lambda = \delta^\nu_\lambda$ and the dual basis is unique ([[§12 Duality#^ladr-3-112|LADR Def. 3.112]]).
>
> **Step 2** (the sum in the new basis). Suppress the other arguments, which are fixed. By linearity in slots $a$ and $b$,
>
> $$
> \sum_\nu T(\dots, e'^\nu, \dots, e'_\nu, \dots) = \sum_\nu (A^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu A^\rho{}_\nu\;T(\dots, e^\mu, \dots, e_\rho, \dots) = \delta^\rho_\mu\,T(\dots, e^\mu, \dots, e_\rho, \dots) = \sum_\mu T(\dots, e^\mu, \dots, e_\mu, \dots) ,
> $$
>
> using $\sum_\nu A^\rho{}_\nu(A^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu = \delta^\rho_\mu$. The result is linear in each remaining argument, since $T$ is. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 3** (components). Take a $(2, 2)$ tensor and contract the first upper with the second lower index; other types differ only in the number of spectator indices. With [[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 2,
>
> $$
> T'^{\sigma\mu}{}_{\nu\sigma} = \frac{\partial x'^\sigma}{\partial x^\alpha}\frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\gamma}\frac{\partial x^\delta}{\partial x'^\nu}\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\sigma}\,T^{\alpha\gamma}{}_{\delta\beta} = \delta^\beta_\alpha\,\frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\gamma}\frac{\partial x^\delta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,T^{\alpha\gamma}{}_{\delta\beta} = \frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\gamma}\frac{\partial x^\delta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,T^{\alpha\gamma}{}_{\delta\alpha} ,
> $$
>
> the law of a $(1, 1)$ tensor for the array $T^{\alpha\gamma}{}_{\delta\alpha}$. The cancellation is the identity of the Setup. Part 2 is proved; it also gives part 1 again, in components.
>
> **Step 4** (two lower indices). For a $(0, 2)$ tensor, $\sum_\sigma T'_{\sigma\sigma} = \sum_\sigma(\partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\sigma)(\partial x^\beta/\partial x'^\sigma)\,T_{\alpha\beta}$. The coefficient is the $(\alpha, \beta)$ entry of $PP^{\mathsf T}$ with $P^\alpha{}_\sigma = \partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\sigma$, which is the identity matrix exactly when $P$ is orthogonal, equivalently when $P^{\mathsf T}P$ is the identity. For polar coordinates $P = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ and $P^{\mathsf T}P = \operatorname{diag}(1, r^2)$, so $P$ is not orthogonal for $r \neq 1$; the number $1 + r^2$ is computed in [[#^ex-c2-3-2|Example §C2.3.2]]. Part 3 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Contraction is the pairing of a covector slot with a vector slot, and a pairing needs one of each. Summing two indices at the same height would need a way to turn a vector into a covector, a metric, which C2 does not have.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.3.5: Symmetric and Antisymmetric Parts
> Let $T$ be a tensor at $p$ ([[#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]), and pick $k \ge 2$ of its slots of the same kind, all vector slots or all covector slots. Write the other arguments as fixed and $T(Y_1, \dots, Y_k)$ for the dependence on the chosen ones. Its **symmetric part** and **antisymmetric part** in these slots are
>
> $$
> \mathcal S[T](Y_1, \dots, Y_k) = \frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\sigma \in S_k}T\bigl(Y_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, Y_{\sigma(k)}\bigr), \qquad \mathcal A[T](Y_1, \dots, Y_k) = \frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\sigma \in S_k}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\,T\bigl(Y_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, Y_{\sigma(k)}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> with $S_k$ the permutations of $k$ objects. Both are tensors of the type of $T$: every permuted map is multilinear, and no chart enters. In components they are written with round and square brackets,
>
> $$
> T_{(\mu_1\cdots\mu_k)} = \frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\sigma \in S_k}T_{\mu_{\sigma(1)}\cdots\mu_{\sigma(k)}}, \qquad T_{[\mu_1\cdots\mu_k]} = \frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\sigma \in S_k}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\,T_{\mu_{\sigma(1)}\cdots\mu_{\sigma(k)}} ,
> $$
>
> and the same with upper indices; an index excluded from a bracket is fenced off, as in $T_{[\mu|\nu|\rho]} = \frac12(T_{\mu\nu\rho} - T_{\rho\nu\mu})$. For $k = 2$ and $k = 3$:
>
> $$
> T_{(\mu\nu)} = \tfrac12\bigl(T_{\mu\nu} + T_{\nu\mu}\bigr), \quad T_{[\mu\nu]} = \tfrac12\bigl(T_{\mu\nu} - T_{\nu\mu}\bigr), \quad T_{[\mu\nu\rho]} = \tfrac16\bigl(T_{\mu\nu\rho} - T_{\mu\rho\nu} + T_{\rho\mu\nu} - T_{\rho\nu\mu} + T_{\nu\rho\mu} - T_{\nu\mu\rho}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> $T$ is **symmetric** (**antisymmetric**) in the chosen slots if $\mathcal S[T] = T$ ($\mathcal A[T] = T$), equivalently if exchanging any two of them leaves $T$ unchanged (changes its sign). Being symmetric or antisymmetric is a property of the multilinear map, so it holds in every chart if it holds in one ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 4).
>
> *The weight $1/k!$* makes $\mathcal S$ and $\mathcal A$ projections: $\mathcal A[\mathcal A[T]] = \mathcal A[T]$, and $\mathcal A[T] = T$ when $T$ is already antisymmetric ([[§77 The Wedge Product#^lem-77-1|591 Lemma §77.1]], for the skew-symmetrizer of [[§77 The Wedge Product#^def-77-3|591 Def. §77.3]], which is $\mathcal A$ on all slots of a $(0, k)$ tensor); likewise for $\mathcal S$. In $n$ dimensions a symmetric pair of indices has $\frac12n(n + 1)$ independent components and an antisymmetric pair $\frac12n(n - 1)$: $10$ and $6$ for $n = 4$.
>
> *Same height only.* Exchanging a vector slot with a covector slot would feed a vector to a covector slot, which is not defined. In components, "$T^\mu{}_\nu = T^\nu{}_\mu$" can hold in one chart and fail in another, since the two indices transform with different matrices ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^rem-b2-2-4|§B2.2, Remark: Where the two parts reappear]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where this was seen for boosts).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.5, eqs. (3.49)–(3.52) (p. 51) · d'Inverno & Vickers §5.9, eqs. (5.32)–(5.34) (p. 76) · [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-3|Def. §B2.2.3]] (level B, $k = 2$)*

^def-c2-3-5

The last tool of level B is a test rather than an operation. One rarely knows a candidate array's law directly; one knows what it does to other tensors.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.3: Quotient Rule
> Let an array $T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_s}$ ($s \ge 1$) be given in every chart at $p$.
> 1. If, for every vector $X \in T_pM$, the contracted arrays $T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_{s-1}\lambda}\,X^\lambda$ (with $X^\lambda$ the components of $X$ in the same chart) are the components of a tensor of type $(r, s - 1)$ ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 3), then $T$ is a tensor of type $(r, s)$. The same holds for any other slot, and for an upper index contracted with every covector. Repeating the argument: an array that turns every tensor of a fixed type, by full contraction over that tensor's indices, into a tensor is itself a tensor.
> 2. ⚑ **"Every" matters.** If only $T_{\mu\nu}X^\mu X^\nu$ is known to be a scalar for every $X$, then the symmetric part $T_{(\mu\nu)}$ ([[#^def-c2-3-5|Def. §C2.3.5]]) is a $(0, 2)$ tensor, and the antisymmetric part $T_{[\mu\nu]}$ is not constrained at all: it drops out of $T_{\mu\nu}X^\mu X^\nu$ in every chart. This is why a line element $ds^2 = g_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu$ fixes only a symmetric metric ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]], where $g_{\mu\nu} = g_{\nu\mu}$ is part of the definition).
>
> This is the general-coordinate form of [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), with $\Lambda$ replaced by the Jacobians at $p$.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a smooth manifold; *applies:* at a point; for fields, pointwise, with "every vector field" in place of "every vector".
>
> *Source: 't Hooft ch. 4, "The quotient theorem" (p. 19) · [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]] (level B, Lorentz version)*

^thm-c2-3-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the hypothesis in two charts). Write $S^{\mu_1\cdots}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_{s-1}} = T^{\mu_1\cdots}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_{s-1}\lambda}X^\lambda$. Since $S$ is a tensor of type $(r, s - 1)$, its primed components follow from the law ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 2); abbreviate by $\mathsf J$ the product of the $r + s - 1$ Jacobian factors for the indices $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_r, \nu_1, \dots, \nu_{s-1}$:
>
> $$
> T'^{\mu_1\cdots}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_{s-1}\kappa}\,X'^\kappa = S'^{\mu_1\cdots}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_{s-1}} = \mathsf J\;S^{\alpha_1\cdots}{}_{\beta_1\cdots\beta_{s-1}} = \mathsf J\;T^{\alpha_1\cdots}{}_{\beta_1\cdots\beta_{s-1}\lambda}\,X^\lambda .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (insert the law of $X$). On the left, $X'^\kappa = (\partial x'^\kappa/\partial x^\lambda)X^\lambda$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]):
>
> $$
> \Bigl[T'^{\mu_1\cdots}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_{s-1}\kappa}\,\frac{\partial x'^\kappa}{\partial x^\lambda} - \mathsf J\;T^{\alpha_1\cdots}{}_{\beta_1\cdots\beta_{s-1}\lambda}\Bigr]X^\lambda = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (every $X$). Any $n$ numbers $X^\lambda$ are the components of a vector, $X = X^\lambda\partial_\lambda$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]]). Choosing $X = \partial_1, \dots, \partial_n$ in turn shows that the bracket vanishes for each $\lambda$.
>
> **Step 4** (solve for $T'$). Multiply the vanishing bracket by $\partial x^\lambda/\partial x'^\nu$ and sum over $\lambda$; by the Setup identity $(\partial x'^\kappa/\partial x^\lambda)(\partial x^\lambda/\partial x'^\nu) = \delta^\kappa_\nu$,
>
> $$
> T'^{\mu_1\cdots}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_{s-1}\nu} = \mathsf J\;\frac{\partial x^\lambda}{\partial x'^\nu}\;T^{\alpha_1\cdots}{}_{\beta_1\cdots\beta_{s-1}\lambda} ,
> $$
>
> the law of a type $(r, s)$ tensor. By [[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 3, $T$ is a tensor. A covector slot works the same way with $\omega'_\kappa = (\partial x^\lambda/\partial x'^\kappa)\omega_\lambda$ and the second identity of the Setup. For a full contraction with a tensor of type $(a, b)$, use as test tensors the products of $a$ vectors and $b$ covectors ([[#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]]) and apply the one-slot argument $a + b$ times. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 5** (only the quadratic form). Let $q(X) = T_{\mu\nu}X^\mu X^\nu$ be a scalar for every $X$. Polarize: for vectors $X$, $Y$, $\tfrac12\bigl[q(X + Y) - q(X) - q(Y)\bigr] = \tfrac12\bigl(T_{\mu\nu} + T_{\nu\mu}\bigr)X^\mu Y^\nu = T_{(\mu\nu)}X^\mu Y^\nu$, a scalar for every $X$ and $Y$, since the left side is. By part 1, applied twice, $T_{(\mu\nu)}$ is a $(0, 2)$ tensor. The antisymmetric part gives $T_{[\mu\nu]}X^\mu X^\nu = \frac12(T_{\mu\nu}X^\mu X^\nu - T_{\nu\mu}X^\mu X^\nu) = 0$ after renaming the dummies in the second term; so any array may be added to $T_{[\mu\nu]}$, chart by chart, without changing $q$. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The quotient rule needs the contraction with *every* vector, so that the coefficients can be compared one by one (Step 3). Knowledge of a restricted family of contractions, such as $q(X)$ with the same vector in both slots, determines only the part of $T$ that the family can see.

## Differentiating tensor fields: what fails, and what survives

Level B formed new tensor fields by differentiation: $\partial_\mu\phi$ from a scalar, $\partial_\mu A^\nu$ and $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}$ from vector and tensor fields ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). The opening showed that this fails in polar coordinates for the constant field $\partial_x$. The next theorem derives the failure for every vector field and every change of chart, and locates it in one term.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.4: Partial Derivatives of a Vector Field Are Not a Tensor
> Let $V$ be a vector field ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-4|Def. §C2.2.4]]) and $\omega$ a one-form ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-7|Def. §C2.2.7]]), with component functions $V^\mu$, $\omega_\mu$ in the chart $x$ and $V'^\mu$, $\omega'_\mu$ in the chart $x'$. Write $\partial_\nu V^\mu$ and $\partial_\nu\omega_\mu$ for the arrays of partial derivatives of the component functions.
> 1. ⚑ **The non-tensorial term.** At every point of the overlap,
>
> $$
> \partial'_\nu V'^\mu = \underbrace{\frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\,\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\partial_\beta V^\alpha}_{\text{law of a } (1,1) \text{ tensor}} + \underbrace{\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\frac{\partial^2x'^\mu}{\partial x^\beta\,\partial x^\alpha}\,V^\alpha}_{\text{non-tensorial}} .
> $$
>
> The second term contains the second derivatives of the transition function. Written with the second derivatives of the inverse change $x(x')$, it is
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\frac{\partial^2x'^\mu}{\partial x^\beta\,\partial x^\alpha}\,V^\alpha = -\,\frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\rho}\,\frac{\partial^2x^\rho}{\partial x'^\nu\,\partial x'^\sigma}\,V'^\sigma .
> $$
>
> 2. **One-forms.** Likewise
>
> $$
> \partial'_\nu\omega'_\mu = \frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\mu}\,\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\partial_\beta\omega_\alpha + \frac{\partial^2x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\nu\,\partial x'^\mu}\,\omega_\alpha ,
> $$
>
> and the non-tensorial term is **symmetric** in $\mu$ and $\nu$.
> 3. **When it vanishes.** The non-tensorial term vanishes at $p$ for every $V$ (for every $\omega$) exactly when all second derivatives of the transition function vanish at $p$. It vanishes on a connected overlap exactly when the change of chart is affine there, $x'^\mu = A^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu + b^\mu$ with constant $A$ and $b$. Inertial coordinates of Minkowski spacetime are related by such changes, which is why level B could differentiate freely ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]], part 2; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$).
> 4. ⚑ **Statements about $\partial_\nu V^\mu$ are statements about a chart.** The array $\partial_\nu V^\mu$ is not the array of components of a tensor field. "$V$ has constant components", $\partial_\nu V^\mu = 0$, holds for $V = \partial_x$ in Cartesian coordinates and fails in polar coordinates; the "divergence" $\partial_\mu V^\mu$ of the same field is $0$ in one chart and $-\cos\theta/r$ in the other ([[#^ex-c2-3-3|Example §C2.3.3]]).
>
> A derivative of tensor fields that is again a tensor needs a structure beyond the manifold and its charts (see after the derivation).
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a smooth manifold; *applies:* any smooth vector field or one-form, any two charts, at every point of their overlap.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers §6.1, eq. (6.1) and the discussion after it (pp. 85–86); Exercise 5.9 (p. 82, second derivatives of a scalar) · 't Hooft ch. 4, eq. (4.20) (p. 17, the covector case) · Schutz §5.3, eqs. (5.39)–(5.42) (pp. 124–126, the field $\vec e_x$ in polar coordinates)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the primed components as functions of $x'$). On the overlap, at the point with coordinates $x'$ and $x = x(x')$, $V'^\mu(x') = \frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\bigl(x(x')\bigr)\,V^\alpha\bigl(x(x')\bigr)$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-4|Def. §C2.2.4]], "On overlaps"). Both factors depend on the point: the Jacobian is the one at the point where $V$ is evaluated.
>
> **Step 2** (the derivative of a function of $x(x')$). For any smooth function $h$ of $x$, the chain rule ([[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]) gives $\partial'_\nu\bigl[h(x(x'))\bigr] = (\partial x^\beta/\partial x'^\nu)\,\partial_\beta h$.
>
> **Step 3** (product rule). Apply Step 2 to the product of Step 1:
>
> $$
> \partial'_\nu V'^\mu = \frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\Bigl[\frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\,\partial_\beta V^\alpha + V^\alpha\,\partial_\beta\Bigl(\frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\Bigr)\Bigr] = \frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\,\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\partial_\beta V^\alpha + \frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\frac{\partial^2x'^\mu}{\partial x^\beta\,\partial x^\alpha}\,V^\alpha .
> $$
>
> The first term is the law of a $(1, 1)$ tensor ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 2) applied to the array $\partial_\beta V^\alpha$. The second is what remains. This is the first formula of part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (the second form). The Jacobians are inverse at every point of the overlap: $\frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}(x(x'))\,\frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\sigma}(x') = \delta^\mu_\sigma$. Differentiate this identity with respect to $x'^\nu$, using Step 2 for the first factor:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\frac{\partial^2x'^\mu}{\partial x^\beta\,\partial x^\alpha}\,\frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\sigma} + \frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\,\frac{\partial^2x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\nu\,\partial x'^\sigma} = 0 .
> $$
>
> Multiply by $V'^\sigma$ and sum over $\sigma$. In the first term $\frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\sigma}V'^\sigma = V^\alpha$ (the inverse law of [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\frac{\partial^2x'^\mu}{\partial x^\beta\,\partial x^\alpha}\,V^\alpha = -\,\frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\,\frac{\partial^2x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\nu\,\partial x'^\sigma}\,V'^\sigma ,
> $$
>
> which is the second formula of part 1, with the dummy $\alpha$ renamed $\rho$. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 5** (one-forms). By [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]], part 3, $\omega'_\mu(x') = \frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\mu}(x')\,\omega_\alpha(x(x'))$. Here the Jacobian is already a function of $x'$, so differentiating it needs no chain rule; the second factor needs Step 2:
>
> $$
> \partial'_\nu\omega'_\mu = \frac{\partial^2x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\nu\,\partial x'^\mu}\,\omega_\alpha + \frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\mu}\,\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\partial_\beta\omega_\alpha .
> $$
>
> The coefficient $\partial^2x^\alpha/\partial x'^\nu\partial x'^\mu$ is symmetric in $\mu$ and $\nu$ by the equality of mixed partial derivatives of the smooth functions $x^\alpha(x')$ ([[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]). Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 6** (when the term vanishes). If all second derivatives of $x'(x)$ vanish at $p$, the term of Step 3 vanishes for every $V$. Conversely, suppose it vanishes at $p$ for every $V$. On the chart domain the coordinate fields $\partial_\gamma$ are vector fields with components $V^\alpha = \delta^\alpha_\gamma$; inserting them gives, for all $\mu$, $\nu$, $\gamma$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\frac{\partial^2x'^\mu}{\partial x^\beta\,\partial x^\gamma} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\delta}\,\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\frac{\partial^2x'^\mu}{\partial x^\beta\,\partial x^\gamma} = \frac{\partial^2x'^\mu}{\partial x^\delta\,\partial x^\gamma} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> multiplying by $\partial x'^\nu/\partial x^\delta$, summing over $\nu$ and using the Setup identity. For one-forms the same argument with $\omega = dx^\gamma$ gives $\partial^2x^\gamma/\partial x'^\nu\partial x'^\mu = 0$, the second derivatives of the inverse change; by Step 4 these vanish exactly when those of $x'(x)$ do. If the second derivatives vanish at every point of a connected overlap, each function $\partial x'^\mu/\partial x^\gamma$ has zero derivative there and is a constant $A^\mu{}_\gamma$ ([[§30 The Differential in Coordinates#^prop-30-11|591 Prop. §30.11]]); then $x'^\mu - A^\mu{}_\gamma x^\gamma$ has zero derivative and is a constant $b^\mu$. Conversely an affine change has zero second derivatives. For the inertial coordinates of level B, $A = \Lambda$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]). Part 3 is proved.
>
> **Step 7** (the field $\partial_x$). In Cartesian coordinates $V^i = (1, 0)$ at every point, so $\partial_jV^i = 0$ and the tensorial term of Step 3 is zero. If $\partial_\nu V^\mu$ were a tensor, its polar components would vanish too ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 4). They do not: [[#^ex-c2-3-3|Example §C2.3.3]] computes them, $\partial'_\theta V'^r = -\sin\theta$ among them, and checks that the non-tensorial term accounts for all four. Part 4 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The extra term comes from differentiating the Jacobian, which depends on the point (Step 3). A derivative compares the components at $p$ with those at a neighbouring point, and the two sets refer to different bases, $\partial_\mu|_p$ and $\partial_\mu|_{p + dx}$; their difference mixes the change of the field with the change of the basis.
> - The term is linear in the field and has the second derivatives of the coordinate change as coefficients. For one-forms it is symmetric in the two lower indices. Both facts decide which combinations of derivatives survive ([[#^thm-c2-3-5|Theorem §C2.3.5]]).

What is the term in a picture? The field $\partial_x$ is the same arrow at every point of the plane, but the polar basis vectors turn and stretch from point to point, so the components of the one arrow change (figure after [[#^ex-c2-3-3|Example §C2.3.3]]). $\partial_\theta V^r = -\sin\theta$ records the turning of $\partial_r$, not a change of the field. In the plane one can see that $\partial_x$ is constant, because $\mathbf R^2$ identifies all its tangent spaces by parallel translation; Schutz uses this identification to differentiate the basis vectors themselves and finds that the two contributions cancel (his eqs. (5.37)–(5.42)). A general manifold has no such identification: $T_pM$ and $T_qM$ are different vector spaces, and a vector at $q$ cannot be subtracted from one at $p$ (the opening of [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms|§C2.2]]). A tensorial derivative therefore needs some structure that carries tensors from $q$ back to $p$. These notes meet three:
1. a vector field $X$, whose flow drags $q$ back to $p$: the Lie derivative $\mathcal L_X$ ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^def-c2-5-3|Def. §C2.5.3]]);
2. no transport at all, for the totally antisymmetric combinations in which the symmetric extra term of part 2 cancels: the exterior derivative ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]]), previewed in the next theorem;
3. a rule for transporting vectors in every direction, a connection with its covariant derivative $\nabla$: [[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative|§C3.5]], where the coefficients $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}$ transform with an inhomogeneous term that cancels the one of part 1.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.5: What Partial Derivatives Can Do Tensorially
> Let $f$ be a scalar field, $\omega$ a $(0, k)$ tensor field ($k \ge 1$) that is totally antisymmetric ([[#^def-c2-3-5|Def. §C2.3.5]]), and $X$, $Y$ vector fields.
> 1. **First derivatives of a scalar.** The $\partial_\mu f$ are the components of the one-form $df$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]], [[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]], part 2), and transform as a covector: $\partial'_\mu f = (\partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\mu)\,\partial_\alpha f$.
> 2. ⚑ **Antisymmetrized derivatives of antisymmetric covariant tensors.** The array $\partial_{[\mu}\omega_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_k]}$ is a totally antisymmetric $(0, k + 1)$ tensor field. For $k = 1$, $\partial_\mu\omega_\nu - \partial_\nu\omega_\mu = 2\,\partial_{[\mu}\omega_{\nu]}$ is a $(0, 2)$ tensor field. With a choice of normalization this is the exterior derivative ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-3|Def. §C2.6.3]]); for the electromagnetic potential it is the field tensor $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^ex-c2-6-3|Example §C2.6.3]]).
> 3. ⚑ **Second derivatives of a scalar.** On the overlap,
>
> $$
> \partial'_\mu\partial'_\nu f = \frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\mu}\,\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta f + \frac{\partial^2x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\mu\,\partial x'^\nu}\,\partial_\alpha f .
> $$
>
> So $\partial_\mu\partial_\nu f$ is not a tensor. At a point $p$ where $df_p = 0$ the extra term vanishes, and $\partial_\mu\partial_\nu f(p)$ are the components of a $(0, 2)$ tensor at $p$, the Hessian of $f$ at its critical point ([[§54 Critical Points and the Hessian#^def-54-2|591 Def. §54.2]], [[§54 Critical Points and the Hessian#^prop-54-2|591 Prop. §54.2]]). Where $df_p \neq 0$, some change of chart makes the extra term nonzero ([[§54 Critical Points and the Hessian#^ex-54-1|591 Example §54.1]]).
> 4. **The bracket of two vector fields.** The array $X^\mu\partial_\mu Y^\nu - Y^\mu\partial_\mu X^\nu$ is the array of components of a vector field, the Lie bracket $[X, Y]$ ([[§58 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^prop-58-2|591 Prop. §58.2]]), whose meaning as a derivative is given in §C2.5 ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^thm-c2-5-2|Theorem §C2.5.2]]).
>
> In parts 2 and 4 the non-tensorial terms of [[#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]], symmetric in a pair of indices, cancel under antisymmetrization; in part 3 the term is multiplied by $\partial_\alpha f$, which vanishes at a critical point.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a smooth manifold; *applies:* any smooth fields and any two charts, at every point of their overlap (part 3's tensor statement: at critical points of $f$).
>
> *Source: 't Hooft ch. 4, eqs. (4.20)–(4.21) and the conclusion of ch. 4 (pp. 17–19: "thus far we have only been able to differentiate antisymmetrically") · d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 5.9 (p. 82) · Couzens, GR1, §3.4.2, eqs. (3.107)–(3.108) (p. 63; the exterior derivative in components)*

^thm-c2-3-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). The scalar field takes one value at each point, $f'(x') = f(x(x'))$ ([[#^def-c2-3-2|Def. §C2.3.2]]). By the chain rule ([[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]), $\partial'_\mu f' = (\partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\mu)\,\partial_\alpha f$, the covector law ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]], part 3). Unlike in [[#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]], no Jacobian sits in front of $f$ to be differentiated.
>
> **Step 2** (part 2, $k = 1$). Write [[#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]], part 2, with the pair $(\mu, \nu)$ and with the pair exchanged, and call the symmetric extra term $E_{\nu\mu} = (\partial^2x^\alpha/\partial x'^\nu\partial x'^\mu)\,\omega_\alpha = E_{\mu\nu}$:
>
> $$
> \partial'_\mu\omega'_\nu = \frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\mu}\,\frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\partial_\beta\omega_\alpha + E_{\mu\nu}, \qquad \partial'_\nu\omega'_\mu = \frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\mu}\,\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\partial_\beta\omega_\alpha + E_{\nu\mu} .
> $$
>
> In the first, rename the dummies $\alpha \leftrightarrow \beta$, so that both carry the factor $(\partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\mu)(\partial x^\beta/\partial x'^\nu)$. Subtract; $E_{\mu\nu} - E_{\nu\mu} = 0$:
>
> $$
> \partial'_\mu\omega'_\nu - \partial'_\nu\omega'_\mu = \frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\mu}\,\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\bigl(\partial_\alpha\omega_\beta - \partial_\beta\omega_\alpha\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the law of a $(0, 2)$ tensor for the array $\partial_\alpha\omega_\beta - \partial_\beta\omega_\alpha$. By [[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 3, it is a tensor; it is antisymmetric by construction.
>
> **Step 3** (part 2, any $k$). Abbreviate $P^\alpha{}_\mu = \partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\mu$. By [[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 2, $\omega'_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_k} = P^{\alpha_1}{}_{\nu_1}\cdots P^{\alpha_k}{}_{\nu_k}\,\omega_{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_k}$. Differentiate with respect to $x'^\mu$ with the product rule and Step 2 of the previous derivation:
>
> $$
> \partial'_\mu\omega'_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_k} = P^\beta{}_\mu P^{\alpha_1}{}_{\nu_1}\cdots P^{\alpha_k}{}_{\nu_k}\,\partial_\beta\omega_{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_k} + \sum_{j=1}^k\frac{\partial^2x^{\alpha_j}}{\partial x'^\mu\,\partial x'^{\nu_j}}\,\Bigl(\prod_{i \neq j}P^{\alpha_i}{}_{\nu_i}\Bigr)\,\omega_{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_k} .
> $$
>
> The $j$-th extra term is symmetric in $\mu$ and $\nu_j$ (equality of mixed partials, [[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]). Antisymmetrize over $(\mu, \nu_1, \dots, \nu_k)$ ([[#^def-c2-3-5|Def. §C2.3.5]]): the permutations pair off as $\sigma$ and $\sigma$ followed by the exchange of $\mu$ and $\nu_j$, with opposite signs and, by the symmetry, equal values, so the $j$-th term drops out. In the first term the antisymmetrization of the free indices $(\mu, \nu_1, \dots)$ can be moved onto the dummies $(\beta, \alpha_1, \dots)$, because a permutation of the factors $P^{\beta}{}_{\mu}P^{\alpha_1}{}_{\nu_1}\cdots$ in their lower indices is undone by renaming the summed upper ones:
>
> $$
> \partial'_{[\mu}\omega'_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_k]} = P^\beta{}_\mu P^{\alpha_1}{}_{\nu_1}\cdots P^{\alpha_k}{}_{\nu_k}\;\partial_{[\beta}\omega_{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_k]} ,
> $$
>
> the law of a $(0, k + 1)$ tensor. For $k = 2$ this was checked by script for a general antisymmetric $F_{\mu\nu}$ in cylindrical coordinates on $\mathbf R^3$. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 4** (part 3). Differentiate Step 1, $\partial'_\nu f' = P^\beta{}_\nu\,\partial_\beta f$, with respect to $x'^\mu$. The factor $P^\beta{}_\nu$ is a function of $x'$; the factor $\partial_\beta f$ needs the chain rule:
>
> $$
> \partial'_\mu\partial'_\nu f' = \frac{\partial^2x^\beta}{\partial x'^\mu\,\partial x'^\nu}\,\partial_\beta f + P^\beta{}_\nu P^\alpha{}_\mu\,\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta f ,
> $$
>
> which is the formula of part 3. If $df_p = 0$, then $\partial_\beta f(p) = 0$ in every chart (part 1 and [[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 4), the first term vanishes at $p$, and what remains is the law of a $(0, 2)$ tensor; this is [[§54 Critical Points and the Hessian#^prop-54-2|591 Prop. §54.2]] at a critical point. If $\partial_\gamma f(p) \neq 0$ for some $\gamma$, define $x(x') = x' + \tfrac12 c\,(x'^1 - x'^1(p))^2$ with the constant column $c^\alpha = \delta^\alpha_\gamma$ (with the same coordinates at $p$). Its Jacobian at $p$ is the identity, so by the inverse function theorem ([[§16 The Inverse Function Theorem#^thm-16-2|452 Thm. §16.2]]) $x'$ is a chart near $p$. Its only nonzero second derivative is $\partial^2x^\alpha/\partial x'^1\partial x'^1 = \delta^\alpha_\gamma$, so the extra term in the $(1, 1)$ entry is $\partial_\gamma f(p) \neq 0$. For $f(t) = t$ on $\mathbf R$ this is [[§54 Critical Points and the Hessian#^ex-54-1|591 Example §54.1]]. Part 3 is proved.
>
> **Step 5** (part 4). By [[#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]], part 1, applied to $Y$, and the inverse law $X'^\nu\,\partial x^\beta/\partial x'^\nu = X^\beta$:
>
> $$
> X'^\nu\partial'_\nu Y'^\mu = X'^\nu\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\Bigl[\frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\,\partial_\beta Y^\alpha + \frac{\partial^2x'^\mu}{\partial x^\beta\,\partial x^\alpha}\,Y^\alpha\Bigr] = \frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\,X^\beta\partial_\beta Y^\alpha + \frac{\partial^2x'^\mu}{\partial x^\beta\,\partial x^\alpha}\,X^\beta Y^\alpha .
> $$
>
> Exchange $X$ and $Y$ and subtract. The second-derivative terms give $\frac{\partial^2x'^\mu}{\partial x^\beta\partial x^\alpha}\bigl(X^\beta Y^\alpha - Y^\beta X^\alpha\bigr) = 0$: a coefficient symmetric in $(\alpha, \beta)$ against an antisymmetric product (rename $\alpha \leftrightarrow \beta$ in the second piece). What remains is
>
> $$
> X'^\nu\partial'_\nu Y'^\mu - Y'^\nu\partial'_\nu X'^\mu = \frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\,\bigl(X^\beta\partial_\beta Y^\alpha - Y^\beta\partial_\beta X^\alpha\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the law of a vector ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]). Part 4 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Every failure of tensoriality in this section is one object: the second derivatives of the coordinate change, symmetric in their two lower indices. A combination of partial derivatives is tensorial when that object cancels (antisymmetry: $d$, the bracket) or meets a factor that vanishes (a critical point). A derivative of an arbitrary tensor in an arbitrary direction admits neither, which is why it needs a connection ([[§C3.5 The Covariant Derivative|§C3.5]]).

## Tensor densities

Some arrays that matter are not tensors in a milder way: under a change of chart they pick up, besides the Jacobian factors of [[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], a power of the Jacobian determinant. Two have already appeared. The area of a region of the plane is $\int dx\,dy = \int r\,dr\,d\theta$ ([[§22 Properties of the Integral#^thm-22-6|452 Thm. §22.6]]): the integrand $1$ of the Cartesian chart becomes $r$ in the polar chart, multiplied by $J = \det\partial(x, y)/\partial(r, \theta) = r$. And the determinant of the Euclidean metric is $1$ in Cartesian and $r^2 = J^2$ in polar coordinates ([[#^ex-c2-3-2|Example §C2.3.2]]). Neither number is a scalar, yet both change by a rule. Such objects are needed wherever something is integrated (§C2.7) and for $\sqrt{-g}$ ([[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor|§C3.3]]).

> [!definition] Definition §C2.3.6: Tensor Density
> Let $W$ be an integer. A **tensor density of type $(r, s)$ and weight $W$** at $p$ assigns to each chart at $p$ an array of $n^{r+s}$ numbers $\mathfrak T^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_s}$, such that the arrays of any two charts are related by
>
> $$
> \mathfrak T'^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_r}{}_{\nu_1\cdots\nu_s} = J^W\;\frac{\partial x'^{\mu_1}}{\partial x^{\alpha_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial x'^{\mu_r}}{\partial x^{\alpha_r}}\;\frac{\partial x^{\beta_1}}{\partial x'^{\nu_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial x^{\beta_s}}{\partial x'^{\nu_s}}\;\mathfrak T^{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_r}{}_{\beta_1\cdots\beta_s}, \qquad J = \det\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x'^\nu}\Bigr)(p) ,
> $$
>
> the determinant of the Jacobian of the old coordinates with respect to the new ones, at $p$. A density of type $(0, 0)$ is a **scalar density**, $\mathfrak f' = J^W\mathfrak f$. A density of weight $0$ is a tensor ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 3). A **density field** assigns a density to each point, with smooth component functions; densities of one type and weight are added point by point.
>
> *Consistency.* The rule composes over three charts like the tensor law: the Jacobian matrices multiply by the chain rule ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 3), and their determinants multiply ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]), $\det(\partial x/\partial x'') = \det(\partial x/\partial x')\,\det(\partial x'/\partial x'')$.
>
> *Non-integer weight.* $J^W$ for non-integer $W$ is defined only when $J > 0$. Such densities (the weight-$1$ density $\sqrt{|\det g|}$ of [[#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]]) are used with atlases in which every change of chart has $J > 0$, the oriented atlases of §C2.7 ([[§C2.7 Integration on Manifolds and Stokes' Theorem#^def-c2-7-1|Def. §C2.7.1]]).
>
> *Sign convention.* The weight is counted with $J = \det(\partial x/\partial x')$, as in d'Inverno & Vickers; texts that count with $\det(\partial x'/\partial x) = 1/J$ assign the opposite weight to every density ([[#^cau-c2-3-1|Caution: Conventions for densities and the Levi-Civita symbol]]).
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers §7.1, eq. (7.1) (p. 115) · Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3 (p. 78, "tensor density", named only)*

^def-c2-3-6

> [!definition] Definition §C2.3.7: Levi-Civita Symbol
> On an $n$-manifold, in every chart, the **Levi-Civita symbols** are the two arrays
>
> $$
> \tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n} = \tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n} = \begin{cases} +1 & (\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n) \text{ an even permutation of } (1, \dots, n) , \\ -1 & \text{an odd permutation} , \\ 0 & \text{two indices equal} . \end{cases}
> $$
>
> On spacetime, with indices $0, \dots, 3$, $\tilde\epsilon^{0123} = \tilde\epsilon_{0123} = +1$. The numbers are the same in every chart by definition; whether these chart-independent arrays obey a transformation law, and which, is [[#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]]. The tilde marks a symbol, an array fixed chart by chart, as opposed to a tensor. There are two symbols, one at each height, because without a metric neither is obtained from the other by lowering indices.
>
> The symbol is the array that computes determinants: $\tilde\epsilon_{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}M^{\alpha_1}{}_1\cdots M^{\alpha_n}{}_n = \det M$ for any $n \times n$ matrix $M^\mu{}_\nu$ (the Leibniz formula, [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-46|LADR Thm. 9.46]]).
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers §7.2, eq. (7.4) (p. 116) · Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.29)–(4.30) (p. 78; $\epsilon_{1\cdots m} = 1$, "we do not raise the indices on $\epsilon$ with the metric") · [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-2|Def. §B2.2.2]] (level B, the Lorentz-tensor version)*

^def-c2-3-7

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.6: The Levi-Civita Symbol and the Determinant Are Densities
> With $J = \det(\partial x^\mu/\partial x'^\nu)$ as in [[#^def-c2-3-6|Def. §C2.3.6]]:
> 1. The Levi-Civita symbols of [[#^def-c2-3-7|Def. §C2.3.7]] are tensor densities: $\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n}$ of type $(n, 0)$ and weight $+1$, $\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n}$ of type $(0, n)$ and weight $-1$.
> 2. For a $(0, 2)$ tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$ ([[#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]), $\det(T_{\mu\nu})$ is a scalar density of weight $+2$: $\det T' = J^2\det T$. For a $(2, 0)$ tensor, $\det(T^{\mu\nu})$ has weight $-2$.
> 3. The product of densities of weights $W_1$ and $W_2$ (component by component, as in [[#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]]) is a density of weight $W_1 + W_2$; a contraction of a density ([[#^def-c2-3-4|Def. §C2.3.4]]) is a density of the same weight.
> 4. ⚑ **The volume tensor.** Let $g_{\mu\nu}$ be a $(0, 2)$ tensor field with $\det g \neq 0$ (the metric, [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor|§C3.1]]), and allow only changes of chart with $J > 0$. Then $\sqrt{|\det g|}$ is a scalar density of weight $+1$, and $v_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n} = \sqrt{|\det g|}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n}$ is a totally antisymmetric $(0, n)$ tensor field, an $n$-form ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-1|Def. §C2.6.1]]). A change of chart with $J < 0$ multiplies it by $-1$ relative to the tensor law: it is a pseudotensor, as $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ was at level B ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). Its role in integration is [[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor|§C3.3]].
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a smooth manifold; *applies:* any two charts at $p$ (part 4: those with $J > 0$).
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers §7.1–7.2, eqs. (7.1), (7.4) and the text after (7.4) (pp. 115–116) · Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.26)–(4.29) (p. 78)*

^thm-c2-3-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the symbol absorbs a determinant). Let $M$ be any $n \times n$ matrix and $L_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n} = \tilde\epsilon_{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}M^{\alpha_1}{}_{\mu_1}\cdots M^{\alpha_n}{}_{\mu_n}$. Exchanging two free indices, say $\mu_1 \leftrightarrow \mu_2$, and renaming the dummies $\alpha_1 \leftrightarrow \alpha_2$ returns $L$ with $\tilde\epsilon_{\alpha_2\alpha_1\cdots} = -\tilde\epsilon_{\alpha_1\alpha_2\cdots}$: $L$ is totally antisymmetric. A totally antisymmetric array with $n$ indices taking $n$ values is fixed by its $(1, \dots, n)$ entry, so $L_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n} = L_{1\cdots n}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n}$, and $L_{1\cdots n} = \det M$ by the Leibniz formula ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-46|LADR Thm. 9.46]]):
>
> $$
> \tilde\epsilon_{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}M^{\alpha_1}{}_{\mu_1}\cdots M^{\alpha_n}{}_{\mu_n} = \det M\;\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n}, \qquad M^{\mu_1}{}_{\alpha_1}\cdots M^{\mu_n}{}_{\alpha_n}\,\tilde\epsilon^{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n} = \det M\;\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n} ,
> $$
>
> the second by the same argument with rows and columns exchanged ($\det M^{\mathsf T} = \det M$, [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-56|LADR Thm. 9.56]]). This is the computation of [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], with $M$ an arbitrary matrix in place of $\Lambda$.
>
> **Step 2** (part 1, lower symbol). Apply the law of a $(0, n)$ density of weight $W$ to the array $\tilde\epsilon_{\cdots}$, with $M^\alpha{}_\mu = \partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\mu$, whose determinant is $J$: by Step 1, $J^W\,\frac{\partial x^{\alpha_1}}{\partial x'^{\mu_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial x^{\alpha_n}}{\partial x'^{\mu_n}}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n} = J^{W+1}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n}$. This equals the primed symbol $\tilde\epsilon'_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n} = \tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n}$ (same numbers in every chart) for every change of chart exactly when $J^{W+1} = 1$ for all $J$, that is $W = -1$.
>
> **Step 3** (part 1, upper symbol). Now $M^\mu{}_\alpha = \partial x'^\mu/\partial x^\alpha$, the inverse matrix, with determinant $1/J$ ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]] applied to $M\,(\partial x/\partial x') = 1$): $J^W\,\frac{\partial x'^{\mu_1}}{\partial x^{\alpha_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial x'^{\mu_n}}{\partial x^{\alpha_n}}\,\tilde\epsilon^{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n} = J^{W-1}\,\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n}$, equal to $\tilde\epsilon^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n}$ for every change exactly when $W = +1$. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 4** (part 2). By [[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 2, $T'_{\mu\nu} = P^\alpha{}_\mu P^\beta{}_\nu T_{\alpha\beta}$ with $P^\alpha{}_\mu = \partial x^\alpha/\partial x'^\mu$, that is $T' = P^{\mathsf T}TP$ as matrices. The determinant is multiplicative and $\det P^{\mathsf T} = \det P = J$, so $\det T' = \det P^{\mathsf T}\,\det T\,\det P = J^2\det T$. For a $(2, 0)$ tensor, $T' = QTQ^{\mathsf T}$ with $Q = P^{-1}$, $\det Q = J^{-1}$, so $\det T' = J^{-2}\det T$. As a check on the weights: d'Inverno's formula $\det T = \frac{1}{n!}\tilde\epsilon^{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_n}\tilde\epsilon^{\beta_1\cdots\beta_n}T_{\alpha_1\beta_1}\cdots T_{\alpha_n\beta_n}$ (his (7.4) for $n = 4$; checked by script for $n = 2$) has two symbols of weight $+1$ and tensors of weight $0$, total $+2$. Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 5** (part 3). Multiply the laws of the two densities component by component: the Jacobian factors combine as for the tensor product ([[#^def-c2-3-3|Def. §C2.3.3]]), and the scalar factors give $J^{W_1}J^{W_2} = J^{W_1 + W_2}$. In a contraction the factors of the contracted pair cancel as in [[#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]], part 2, and $J^W$ is untouched. Part 3 is proved.
>
> **Step 6** (part 4). By part 2, $|\det g'| = J^2|\det g|$, so $\sqrt{|\det g'|} = |J|\sqrt{|\det g|}$, which is $J^{+1}\sqrt{|\det g|}$ when $J > 0$: weight $+1$. By part 3 the product with $\tilde\epsilon_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n}$ (weight $-1$, part 1) has weight $0$ and is a tensor ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 3), totally antisymmetric because $\tilde\epsilon$ is. If $J < 0$, the same computation gives $v' = |J|\,J^{-1}\,(\text{tensor law applied to } v) = -(\text{tensor law applied to } v)$. Part 4 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The symbol keeps its numbers in every chart only because the determinant that the Jacobian factors produce (Step 1) is cancelled by $J^{\pm1}$. A metric supplies, through $\sqrt{|\det g|}$, exactly the opposite power, which is how a chart-independent volume becomes possible once there is a metric, and why there is none without one.

> [!caution] Caution: Conventions for densities and the Levi-Civita symbol
> - **Sign of the weight.** These notes, with d'Inverno & Vickers, weight with $J = \det(\partial x/\partial x')$: $\tilde\epsilon^{\cdots}$ has weight $+1$, $\tilde\epsilon_{\cdots}$ weight $-1$, $\det g_{\mu\nu}$ weight $+2$, $\sqrt{|\det g|}$ weight $+1$. Texts that weight with $\det(\partial x'/\partial x) = 1/J$ reverse every sign ($\tilde\epsilon_{\cdots}$ weight $+1$, $\det g$ weight $-2$). The transformation of the numbers is the same; only the name of the weight differs.
> - **$J^W$, not $|J|^W$.** The symbol keeps its values also under changes of chart that reverse orientation ($J < 0$), which needs the odd power $J^{\pm1}$ with its sign (Steps 2–3 above). $\sqrt{|\det g|}$ instead transforms with $|J|$; the product $v_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_n}$ is therefore a tensor only within an oriented atlas.
> - **Symbol versus level B's tensor.** At level B, $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ is a Lorentz pseudotensor with $\epsilon^{0123} = +1$, and $\epsilon_{0123} = -1$ is obtained by lowering with $\eta$, which costs $\det\eta = -1$ in either signature ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-2|Def. §B2.2.2]], [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$). Here there is no $\eta$, and the two symbols $\tilde\epsilon^{0123} = \tilde\epsilon_{0123} = +1$ are separate objects. Couzens builds the tensor from the lower symbol, $v_{0123} = +\sqrt{|g|}$ (his (4.29)); raising its indices with a Lorentzian metric gives $v^{0123} = -1/\sqrt{|g|}$ (his (4.30)). In inertial coordinates of Minkowski spacetime this tensor has $v^{0123} = -1$, the opposite overall sign to level B's $\epsilon^{0123} = +1$. Level C fixes its sign in [[§C3.3 The Volume Form and the Levi-Civita Tensor|§C3.3]].
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers §7.1–7.2 (pp. 115–116) · Couzens, GR1, §4.1.3, eqs. (4.29)–(4.30) (p. 78)*

^cau-c2-3-1

Tensors now live at points, and a change of chart acts on them through the Jacobian at that point. What is still missing is a way to move them: a smooth map between manifolds carries points to points, and the next question is how it carries vectors, one-forms and tensors of higher type. Vectors are pushed forward along the map and one-forms pulled back, and a diffeomorphism carries tensors of every type ([[§C2.4 Push-Forward, Pull-Back and Diffeomorphisms|§C2.4]]). Applied to the flow of a vector field, the same construction gives the first tensorial derivative, the Lie derivative ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors|§C2.5]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C2.3.1: The Kronecker Delta Is the Same in Every Chart
> *The tensor.* At each point $p$, the map $\delta_p : T^{\ast}_pM \times T_pM \to \mathbf R$, $\delta_p(\omega, X) = \omega(X)$, is linear in each argument: a tensor of type $(1, 1)$ ([[#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]). As a linear map of $T_pM$ to itself (case 4 of that definition) it is the identity. No chart was used to define it.
>
> *Its components.* By [[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 1, and the duality of the coordinate bases ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]], part 1), in any chart $\delta^\mu{}_\nu = \delta(dx^\mu, \partial_\nu) = dx^\mu(\partial_\nu)$, which is $1$ for $\mu = \nu$ and $0$ otherwise: the same array in every chart.
>
> *Check with the law.* Part 2 of the theorem gives $\delta'^\mu{}_\nu = \frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\delta^\alpha{}_\beta = \frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\nu} = \delta^\mu{}_\nu$, the Setup identity. For polar coordinates on the plane the two Jacobian matrices at the point $(r, \theta)$ are
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial(r, \theta)}{\partial(x, y)} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta/r & \cos\theta/r \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(r, \theta)} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix} ,
> $$
>
> and their product has the entries $(r, r)$: $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$; $(r, \theta)$: $-r\sin\theta\cos\theta + r\sin\theta\cos\theta = 0$; $(\theta, r)$: $-\sin\theta\cos\theta/r + \sin\theta\cos\theta/r = 0$; $(\theta, \theta)$: $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$.
>
> *Trace.* The full contraction is $\delta^\mu{}_\mu = n$ in every chart ([[#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]): the trace of the identity map.
>
> *Same height fails.* An array with $1$ on the diagonal and both indices down (or both up) is not chart-independent: the $(0, 2)$ tensor with Cartesian components $\delta_{ij}$ has the polar components $\operatorname{diag}(1, r^2)$ ([[#^ex-c2-3-2|Example §C2.3.2]]). "The identity matrix" is a chart-independent array only in the mixed position. At level B, $\eta_{\mu\nu}$, $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ and $\delta^\mu{}_\nu$ all had the same components in every inertial frame ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), because Lorentz transformations are defined by preserving $\eta$; under general changes of chart only $\delta^\mu{}_\nu$ keeps its components.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 5.8 (p. 82) · 't Hooft ch. 4, after eq. (4.18) (p. 17) · Jacobian product checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.3.2: The Euclidean Metric δᵢⱼ in Polar Coordinates
> *The tensor.* On $\mathbf R^2$ the dot product of tangent vectors is a $(0, 2)$ tensor field $g$ (given here with the plane; a metric in general is [[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor|§C3.1]]), with Cartesian components $g_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$, that is $g = dx \otimes dx + dy \otimes dy$ ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 1).
>
> *Polar components.* Two lower indices take two factors $\partial x^i/\partial x'^\mu$ ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 2): $g'_{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial x^i}{\partial x'^\mu}\frac{\partial x^j}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\delta_{ij} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial x'^\mu}\frac{\partial x}{\partial x'^\nu} + \frac{\partial y}{\partial x'^\mu}\frac{\partial y}{\partial x'^\nu}$. With $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$: $\partial_rx = \cos\theta$, $\partial_ry = \sin\theta$, $\partial_\theta x = -r\sin\theta$, $\partial_\theta y = r\cos\theta$. Hence
>
> $$
> g'_{rr} = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1, \qquad g'_{r\theta} = g'_{\theta r} = -r\sin\theta\cos\theta + r\sin\theta\cos\theta = 0, \qquad g'_{\theta\theta} = r^2\sin^2\theta + r^2\cos^2\theta = r^2 .
> $$
>
> So $g = dr \otimes dr + r^2\,d\theta \otimes d\theta$, and the line element reads $ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\theta^2$ (Schutz (5.32)–(5.33)). The same tensor has different components in the two charts.
>
> *The inverse.* The $(2, 0)$ tensor with Cartesian components $\delta^{ij}$ takes two factors $\partial x'^\mu/\partial x^i$, from the first matrix of [[#^ex-c2-3-1|Example §C2.3.1]]: $g'^{rr} = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$, $g'^{r\theta} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta/r + \sin\theta\cos\theta/r = 0$, $g'^{\theta\theta} = (\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta)/r^2 = 1/r^2$. The contraction $g'_{\mu\rho}g'^{\rho\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(1\cdot1,\ r^2\cdot r^{-2}) = \delta_\mu{}^\nu$, as in Cartesian coordinates ([[#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]).
>
> *What is not invariant.*
> 1. The sum of the diagonal entries, two lower indices summed: $2$ in Cartesian, $1 + r^2$ in polar coordinates ([[#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]], part 3). At $(r, \theta) = (2, \pi/3)$: $g' = \operatorname{diag}(1, 4)$, sum $5$ against $2$.
> 2. The determinant: $1$ in Cartesian, $r^2$ in polar coordinates. With $J = \det\partial(x, y)/\partial(r, \theta) = \cos\theta\cdot r\cos\theta - (-r\sin\theta)\sin\theta = r$, this is $J^2\cdot1$: a scalar density of weight $2$ ([[#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 2).
> 3. Its square root, $\sqrt{\det g'} = r$, is the factor in the area element $r\,dr\,d\theta$ ([[§22 Properties of the Integral#^thm-22-6|452 Thm. §22.6]]); $\sqrt{\det g'}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\mu\nu}$ has the single independent component $v_{r\theta} = r$ ([[#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], part 4), the polar form of $dx \wedge dy = r\,dr \wedge d\theta$ (§C2.7).
>
> *Source: Schutz §5.2, eqs. (5.29)–(5.33) (p. 123) · all entries checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.3.3: The Non-Tensorial Term for ∂ₓ in Polar Coordinates
> *The field.* $V = \partial_x$ on the plane with $r > 0$: Cartesian components $(V^x, V^y) = (1, 0)$ at every point; polar components, from the first matrix of [[#^ex-c2-3-1|Example §C2.3.1]] ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 2), $V'^r = \cos\theta$, $V'^\theta = -\sin\theta/r$ ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]]).
>
> *The partial derivatives in polar coordinates.*
>
> $$
> \partial'_rV'^r = 0, \qquad \partial'_\theta V'^r = -\sin\theta, \qquad \partial'_rV'^\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{r^2}, \qquad \partial'_\theta V'^\theta = -\frac{\cos\theta}{r} .
> $$
>
> In Cartesian coordinates all four are $0$. A tensor that vanishes in one chart vanishes in all ([[#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], part 4), so $\partial_\nu V^\mu$ is not a tensor.
>
> *The non-tensorial term accounts for everything.* The tensorial term of [[#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]], part 1, is zero, since $\partial_jV^i = 0$. So the whole array must equal the non-tensorial term, in its second form $-\frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\rho}\frac{\partial^2x^\rho}{\partial x'^\nu\partial x'^\sigma}V'^\sigma$. The second derivatives of $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ are
>
> $$
> \partial_r^2x = 0,\quad \partial_r\partial_\theta x = -\sin\theta,\quad \partial_\theta^2x = -r\cos\theta; \qquad \partial_r^2y = 0,\quad \partial_r\partial_\theta y = \cos\theta,\quad \partial_\theta^2y = -r\sin\theta .
> $$
>
> Contract each with $V'^\sigma = (\cos\theta, -\sin\theta/r)$, for $\nu = \theta$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2x}{\partial\theta\,\partial x'^\sigma}V'^\sigma = (-\sin\theta)\cos\theta + (-r\cos\theta)\Bigl(-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Bigr) = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial^2y}{\partial\theta\,\partial x'^\sigma}V'^\sigma = \cos\theta\cos\theta + (-r\sin\theta)\Bigl(-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Bigr) = 1 ,
> $$
>
> and for $\nu = r$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2x}{\partial r\,\partial x'^\sigma}V'^\sigma = 0 + (-\sin\theta)\Bigl(-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Bigr) = \frac{\sin^2\theta}{r}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2y}{\partial r\,\partial x'^\sigma}V'^\sigma = 0 + \cos\theta\Bigl(-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Bigr) = -\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r} .
> $$
>
> Multiply by $-\partial x'^\mu/\partial x^\rho$ (rows of the first matrix of [[#^ex-c2-3-1|Example §C2.3.1]]: $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ for $\mu = r$, $(-\sin\theta/r, \cos\theta/r)$ for $\mu = \theta$):
>
> $$
> (r, \theta):\ -\bigl[\cos\theta\cdot0 + \sin\theta\cdot1\bigr] = -\sin\theta, \qquad (\theta, \theta):\ -\Bigl[-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\cdot0 + \frac{\cos\theta}{r}\cdot1\Bigr] = -\frac{\cos\theta}{r}, \qquad
> (r, r):\ -\Bigl[\cos\theta\,\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r} - \sin\theta\,\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r}\Bigr] = 0, \qquad (\theta, r):\ -\Bigl[-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\,\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r} - \frac{\cos\theta}{r}\,\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r}\Bigr] = \frac{\sin\theta}{r^2} ,
> $$
>
> the four entries above, entry $(\mu, \nu)$ being $\partial'_\nu V'^\mu$.
>
> *What the components see.* $\partial'_\theta V'^r = -\sin\theta$ is the turning of the basis vector $\partial_r$ as $\theta$ changes, not a change of the field (figure below). Schutz differentiates the basis vectors themselves, using the plane's identification of its tangent spaces, and finds that the two contributions cancel: $\partial\vec e_x/\partial\theta = 0$ (his (5.42)).
>
> *The divergence.* The naive divergence of the polar components is $\partial'_\mu V'^\mu = 0 + (-\cos\theta/r) = -\cos\theta/r$, while the Cartesian one is $0$: the trace of a non-tensor is not a scalar. The expression that agrees with Cartesian coordinates is
>
> $$
> \frac1r\,\partial_r\bigl(r\,V'^r\bigr) + \partial_\theta V'^\theta = \frac{\cos\theta}{r} - \frac{\cos\theta}{r} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> with the factor $r = \sqrt{\det g'}$ of [[#^ex-c2-3-2|Example §C2.3.2]]: $r\,V'^\mu$ is a vector density of weight $1$ ([[#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], parts 3–4), and the general divergence formula $\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}\,V^\mu)$ is [[§C3.6 Torsion and the Levi-Civita Connection|§C3.6]].
>
> *Source: Schutz §5.3, eqs. (5.39)–(5.42) (pp. 124–126) · d'Inverno & Vickers §6.1, eq. (6.1) (p. 85) · all entries and the divergence checked by script*
