[[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric|§C1.5]] described gravity by a metric acting on "the space of infinitesimal displacements at an event" ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]]), and [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold|§C2.1]] made spacetime a manifold on which coordinates are labels valid on chart domains ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]]). In special relativity a displacement was a four-vector: the coordinate difference $\Delta x^\mu$ between two events transforms with the Lorentz matrix, $\Delta x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\Delta x^\nu$, for any separation, large or small ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, which does not enter this law). The question of this section is *what a vector at an event of a manifold is, and how its components change when the chart changes.*

First, what fails. On a manifold there is no difference of two points: the points of a sphere cannot be subtracted. On a chart domain one can subtract coordinates, $\Delta x^\mu = x^\mu(q) - x^\mu(p)$, but the result depends on the chart in a way no vector does. Take the plane with Cartesian coordinates $(x, y)$ and polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$, the point $p = (0, 2)$ (so $r = 2$, $\theta = \pi/2$), and the points $q_\varepsilon = (\varepsilon, 2)$ on the horizontal line through $p$. In Cartesian coordinates $\Delta(x, y) = (\varepsilon, 0)$, exactly linear in $\varepsilon$. In polar coordinates

$$
\Delta r = \sqrt{\varepsilon^2 + 4} - 2 = \tfrac14\varepsilon^2 + O(\varepsilon^4), \qquad \Delta\theta = \Bigl(\frac\pi2 - \arctan\frac\varepsilon2\Bigr) - \frac\pi2 = -\tfrac12\varepsilon + \tfrac1{24}\varepsilon^3 + O(\varepsilon^5) .
$$

Doubling the Cartesian displacement does not double the polar one:

$$
\Delta(2\varepsilon) - 2\,\Delta(\varepsilon) = \bigl(\tfrac12\varepsilon^2,\ \tfrac14\varepsilon^3\bigr) + \dots \neq 0 .
$$

For finite steps the mismatch is of order one. The step to $(2, 2)$ gives $(\Delta r, \Delta\theta) = (2\sqrt2 - 2, -\pi/4) = (0.828, -0.785)$; the step twice as long, to $(4, 2)$, gives $(2\sqrt5 - 2, \arctan\tfrac12 - \tfrac\pi2) = (2.472, -1.107)$, not $(1.657, -1.571)$. "The displacement from $p$ to $q$" is linear in one chart and not in the other, so displacements between points do not form a vector space in any chart-independent sense. Taylor's theorem shows what does survive. For any change of chart $x \mapsto x'$,

$$
\Delta x'^\nu = \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}\Big|_p\,\Delta x^\mu + \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2 x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu\partial x^\rho}\Big|_p\,\Delta x^\mu\Delta x^\rho + O(\Delta^3) ,
$$

and only the first term is linear in $\Delta x$. In the example both terms are visible: the Jacobian at $p$ is $\partial(r, \theta)/\partial(x, y) = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1/2 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, which sends $(1, 0)$ to $(0, -\tfrac12)$, exactly the first-order coefficients of $(\Delta r, \Delta\theta)$ above; the $\tfrac14\varepsilon^2$ in $\Delta r$ is the second term. Dividing by $\varepsilon$ and letting $\varepsilon \to 0$ keeps the linear part and discards the rest. What remains is the **velocity** of the curve $\varepsilon \mapsto q_\varepsilon$ at $p$. It is attached to the single point $p$, not to a pair of points, and it acts on functions as a derivative: $f \mapsto \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}f(q_\varepsilon)\big|_0$.

The section turns this into definitions. A tangent vector at $p$ is a derivation of functions at $p$ ([[#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]]), and these form the tangent space $T_pM$ ([[#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]). A chart provides a basis $\partial_\mu|_p$ and components $X^\mu$ ([[#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]]). Every tangent vector is the velocity of a curve ([[#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]), and under a change of chart the components change with the Jacobian of the transition map at $p$ ([[#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]). This is the Lorentz matrix of level B, now different at every point. Vector fields assign a tangent vector to every point ([[#^def-c2-2-4|Def. §C2.2.4]]). The dual objects follow: the cotangent space and its covectors ([[#^def-c2-2-5|Def. §C2.2.5]]), the differential $df$ ([[#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]]), one-form fields ([[#^def-c2-2-7|Def. §C2.2.7]]), the dual basis $dx^\mu$ with the covariant transformation law ([[#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]), and the pairing $\omega_\mu X^\mu$, the same number in every chart ([[#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]]). Two cautions record what cannot be done yet, because there is no metric.

**Setup.** A smooth manifold $M$ of dimension $n$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]), with charts $(U, \varphi)$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]) and the algebra $C^\infty(M)$ of smooth real functions ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-5|Def. §C2.1.5]]). Spacetime is the case $n = 4$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]]), but nothing here uses $n = 4$ or a signature. There is **no metric** in this section: no lengths, no angles, no raising or lowering of indices ([[#^cau-c2-2-1|Caution: Index position is the type, and nothing raises it yet]]). Every statement is an exact theorem of differential geometry at a point (linear algebra and the chain rule). Where MATH 591 proves a theorem, its proof is linked rather than repeated, and the physics form is written here. The identities and numbers in the opening and in the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): Greek indices label the coordinates of a chart, $\mu = 1, \dots, n$ (on spacetime $\mu = 0, \dots, 3$ with $x^0 = t$); a repeated upper–lower pair is summed; $\partial_\mu \equiv \partial/\partial x^\mu$ and $\partial'_\nu \equiv \partial/\partial x'^\nu$, the prime marking a second chart; for a function $f$ on $M$, $f(x)$ and $\partial_\mu f$ mean $f\circ\varphi^{-1}$ and its partial derivatives at the coordinates $x$ of a point (Couzens' "abuse of notation", GR1 (3.23)). The signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]) enters only with the metric (C3).

## Tangent vectors as derivations

The velocity of a curve through $p$ acts on a function $f$ by $f \mapsto \frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))\big|_0$. This map is linear in $f$ and obeys the product rule, and it uses nothing but the point $p$ and the behaviour of $f$ near $p$. The definition keeps exactly these two properties and forgets the curve.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.2.1: Tangent Vector at a Point
> *Structure.* Let $M$ be a smooth manifold and $p \in M$. A **tangent vector at $p$** is a map $X_p : C^\infty(M) \to \mathbf R$ from smooth functions ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-5|Def. §C2.1.5]]) to numbers that is
> 1. linear: $X_p(af + bg) = a\,X_p(f) + b\,X_p(g)$ for $a, b \in \mathbf R$ and $f, g \in C^\infty(M)$;
> 2. a derivation at $p$ (Leibniz rule): $X_p(fg) = f(p)\,X_p(g) + g(p)\,X_p(f)$.
>
> $X_p(f)$ is read as "the rate of change of $f$ at $p$ in the direction $X_p$". It is one number, computed at one point.
>
> *Immediate consequences.* A tangent vector kills constants: $X_p(1) = X_p(1\cdot 1) = 2X_p(1)$, so $X_p(1) = 0$, and $X_p(c) = c\,X_p(1) = 0$ for a constant function $c$. Couzens lists additivity, "kills constants" and the Leibniz rule instead of linearity; the two lists are equivalent, since $X_p(cf) = c\,X_p(f) + f(p)\,X_p(c) = c\,X_p(f)$ by the Leibniz rule.
>
> *MATH 591.* There a derivation at $p$ acts on germs of functions at $p$ ([[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^def-28-1|591 Def. §28.1]]) rather than on $C^\infty(M)$. Restricting a germ derivation to global functions is a linear isomorphism onto the derivations of $C^\infty(M)$ at $p$ ([[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^prop-28-3|591 Prop. §28.3]]), so the two definitions give the same vectors. In particular $X_p(f)$ depends only on the values of $f$ near $p$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.2, Definition 17 (p. 45) · Hartle §20.1, eqs. (20.1)–(20.4) (pp. 419–420; vectors as directional derivatives) · d'Inverno & Vickers §5.5, eqs. (5.14)–(5.17) (p. 71; the tangent to a curve as the prototype vector)*

^def-c2-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §C2.2.2: Tangent Space
> The **tangent space** of $M$ at $p$ is the set $T_pM$ of all tangent vectors at $p$ ([[#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]]), a real vector space under
>
> $$
> (X_p + Y_p)(f) = X_p(f) + Y_p(f), \qquad (aX_p)(f) = a\,X_p(f) ,
> $$
>
> since sums and multiples of derivations at $p$ are again derivations at $p$ (both conditions are linear in $X_p$) ([[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^def-28-2|591 Def. §28.2]]).
>
> *What it is attached to.* $T_pM$ belongs to the point $p$. For $p \ne q$, $T_pM$ and $T_qM$ are different vector spaces: a vector at $p$ and a vector at $q$ can be neither added nor compared without further structure, such as a vector field to drag along ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors|§C2.5]]) or a connection (§C3.4, planned). This is the precise form of "there is no difference of two points": displacements exist only infinitesimally, as velocities at one point.
>
> *In spacetime*, $T_pM$ is the space of infinitesimal displacements at the event $p$ on which the metric of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]] acts.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.2 (p. 48: $T_p(M)$, $\dim T_p(M) = \dim M$, $T_p \ne T_q$) · Hartle §20.1 (p. 420: "the linear space of directional derivatives is the tangent space")*

^def-c2-2-2

A chart turns this abstract space into $\mathbf R^n$. The partial derivatives with respect to the coordinates are tangent vectors, and they are a basis.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.1: Coordinate Basis
> Let $(U, \varphi)$ be a chart with $p \in U$ and coordinate functions $x^\mu = r^\mu\circ\varphi$ ([[§29 Coordinate Derivations and the Basis Theorem#^def-29-2|591 Def. §29.2]]), $n = \dim M$. Define the **coordinate derivations**
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu|_p(f) \equiv \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\Big|_p(f) = \frac{\partial(f\circ\varphi^{-1})}{\partial r^\mu}\bigl(\varphi(p)\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the ordinary partial derivative of the coordinate representation of $f$ ([[§29 Coordinate Derivations and the Basis Theorem#^def-29-3|591 Def. §29.3]]). Then:
> 1. each $\partial_\mu|_p$ is a tangent vector at $p$ ([[#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]]), and $\partial_\mu|_p(x^\nu) = \delta^\nu_\mu$;
> 2. $\{\partial_1|_p, \dots, \partial_n|_p\}$ is a basis of $T_pM$ ([[#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]), the **coordinate basis** of the chart; in particular $\dim T_pM = n = \dim M$ at every point;
> 3. every $X_p \in T_pM$ is $X_p = X^\mu\,\partial_\mu|_p$ with the **components** $X^\mu = X_p(x^\mu)$, the values of $X_p$ on the coordinate functions; on a function, $X_p(f) = X^\mu\,\partial_\mu f(p)$;
> 4. ⚑ a tangent vector sees only first-order data: $X_p(uv) = 0$ whenever $u(p) = v(p) = 0$.
>
> The basis depends on the chart; the vector $X_p$ does not. Part 4 is why the second-order terms of the opening computation have no counterpart among vectors: they are products of two functions vanishing at $p$.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a smooth manifold; *applies:* any chart whose domain contains $p$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.2, eqs. (3.25)–(3.27) (p. 46); Exercise 3.2 · [[§29 Coordinate Derivations and the Basis Theorem#^thm-29-8|591 Thm. §29.8]], [[§29 Coordinate Derivations and the Basis Theorem#^thm-29-10|591 Thm. §29.10]] · Hartle §20.1, eq. (20.4) (p. 420)*

^thm-c2-2-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** ($\partial_\mu|_p$ is a tangent vector). Write $F = f\circ\varphi^{-1}$ and $G = g\circ\varphi^{-1}$, functions on $\varphi(U) \subset \mathbf R^n$, and $a = \varphi(p)$. The ordinary partial derivative is linear, and the product rule of calculus gives
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu|_p(fg) = \frac{\partial(FG)}{\partial r^\mu}(a) = F(a)\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial r^\mu}(a) + G(a)\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial r^\mu}(a) = f(p)\,\partial_\mu|_p(g) + g(p)\,\partial_\mu|_p(f) .
> $$
>
> For the coordinate functions, $x^\nu\circ\varphi^{-1} = r^\nu$, so $\partial_\mu|_p(x^\nu) = \partial r^\nu/\partial r^\mu = \delta^\nu_\mu$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (locality). $X_p(f)$ depends only on $f$ near $p$ ([[#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]], via [[§28 Derivations and the Abstract Tangent Space#^prop-28-3|591 Prop. §28.3]]). Functions defined only near $p$, such as the coordinates $x^\mu$ and the functions $g_\mu$ below, can therefore be fed to $X_p$: each agrees near $p$ with a function on all of $M$ ([[§56 Vector Fields#^cor-56-8|591 Cor. §56.8]]).
>
> **Step 3** (Hadamard's lemma). Let $B$ be a ball around $a$ inside $\varphi(U)$. For $r \in B$ the segment from $a$ to $r$ lies in $B$, and the fundamental theorem of calculus along it gives
>
> $$
> F(r) - F(a) = \int_0^1\frac{d}{ds}F\bigl(a + s(r - a)\bigr)\,ds = (r^\mu - a^\mu)\,G_\mu(r), \qquad G_\mu(r) = \int_0^1\frac{\partial F}{\partial r^\mu}\bigl(a + s(r - a)\bigr)\,ds ,
> $$
>
> with $G_\mu$ smooth and $G_\mu(a) = \partial F/\partial r^\mu(a)$ ([[§29 Coordinate Derivations and the Basis Theorem#^lem-29-5|591 Lemma §29.5]]). On $M$, with $g_\mu = G_\mu\circ\varphi$, this reads, near $p$,
>
> $$
> f = f(p) + \bigl(x^\mu - x^\mu(p)\bigr)\,g_\mu, \qquad g_\mu(p) = \partial_\mu|_p(f) .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (apply $X_p$). Using linearity, $X_p(\text{const}) = 0$, and the Leibniz rule on each product $\bigl(x^\mu - x^\mu(p)\bigr)g_\mu$:
>
> $$
> X_p(f) = 0 + \underbrace{X_p\bigl(x^\mu - x^\mu(p)\bigr)}_{=\,X_p(x^\mu)}\,g_\mu(p) + \underbrace{\bigl(x^\mu(p) - x^\mu(p)\bigr)}_{=\,0}\,X_p(g_\mu) = X_p(x^\mu)\,\partial_\mu|_p(f) .
> $$
>
> This holds for every $f$, so $X_p = X_p(x^\mu)\,\partial_\mu|_p$: the $\partial_\mu|_p$ span $T_pM$, with coefficients $X^\mu = X_p(x^\mu)$. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (independence). If $c^\mu\,\partial_\mu|_p = 0$, apply both sides to $x^\nu$ and use Step 1: $0 = c^\mu\delta^\nu_\mu = c^\nu$ for each $\nu$. So the $n$ vectors are linearly independent and form a basis; $\dim T_pM = n$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 6** (first-order data only). If $u(p) = v(p) = 0$, the Leibniz rule gives $X_p(uv) = u(p)\,X_p(v) + v(p)\,X_p(u) = 0 + 0$. This is part 4. In the opening, the polar coordinate $r$ near $p = (0, 2)$ is $r = 2 + y' + \tfrac14 x^2 + \dots$ with $y' = y - 2$; the term $\tfrac14x^2$ is the product $x\cdot x$ of two functions vanishing at $p$, and every tangent vector kills it.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** A tangent vector at $p$ is fixed by $n$ numbers, its values on the coordinate functions, because the Leibniz rule kills every product of two functions that vanish at $p$ (Step 6). In Hadamard's form of $f$, everything except the first-order term is such a product. So a derivation sees exactly the first-order Taylor data of $f$ at $p$, which is the part of a displacement that survived the opening computation.

## Velocities and the transformation law

The opening started from a curve and its velocity. The next box makes that the second, equivalent description of $T_pM$, and the one physics uses for worldlines.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.2.3: Velocity of a Curve; Four-Velocity in Arbitrary Coordinates
> *Structure.* A **smooth curve** is a smooth map $\gamma : J \to M$ from an open interval $J \subset \mathbf R$ ([[§31 Tangent Vectors as Velocities of Curves#^def-31-1|591 Def. §31.1]]). Its **velocity** at $t_0 \in J$ is the tangent vector at $\gamma(t_0)$
>
> $$
> \dot\gamma(t_0)(f) = \frac{d}{dt}f\bigl(\gamma(t)\bigr)\Big|_{t = t_0}, \qquad \dot\gamma(t_0) \in T_{\gamma(t_0)}M ,
> $$
>
> a derivation at $\gamma(t_0)$ ([[#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]]) by the product rule for $d/dt$ ([[§31 Tangent Vectors as Velocities of Curves#^def-31-2|591 Def. §31.2]]).
>
> *Components.* In a chart write $x^\mu(t) \equiv x^\mu\bigl(\gamma(t)\bigr)$. The chain rule gives $\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t)) = \frac{dx^\mu}{dt}\,\partial_\mu f$, so in the coordinate basis ([[#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]])
>
> $$
> \dot\gamma = \frac{dx^\mu}{dt}\,\partial_\mu, \qquad \dot\gamma^\mu = \frac{dx^\mu}{dt} :
> $$
>
> the components of the velocity are the coordinate velocities ([[§31 Tangent Vectors as Velocities of Curves#^prop-31-1|591 Prop. §31.1]]).
>
> *Every tangent vector is a velocity.* For every $X_p \in T_pM$ there is a curve with $\gamma(0) = p$ and $\dot\gamma(0) = X_p$ ([[§31 Tangent Vectors as Velocities of Curves#^thm-31-4|591 Thm. §31.4]]; in a chart, $x^\mu(t) = x^\mu(p) + t\,X^\mu$ for small $|t|$). Two curves through $p$ have the same velocity exactly when their coordinate velocities agree in one chart, and then in every chart ([[§31 Tangent Vectors as Velocities of Curves#^cor-31-6|591 Cor. §31.6]]). So $T_pM$ is also the set of equivalence classes of curves through $p$. Couzens defines tangent vectors this way (Definition 18) and identifies the classes with derivations; the step that every derivation arises from a curve is 591 Thm. §31.4.
>
> *Worldlines.* The worldline of a particle is a curve in spacetime. With any parameter $\lambda$ its velocity has the components $dx^\mu/d\lambda$ in every chart. The **four-velocity** is the velocity with respect to proper time, $u^\mu = dx^\mu/d\tau$: the same definition as level B ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, $u\cdot u = +c^2$ there), now in arbitrary coordinates. Proper time, and with it the normalization $u\cdot u = -1$, needs the metric ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-2|Def. §C1.5.2]] in a coordinate patch; on a manifold §C3.2, planned). In this chapter $\lambda$ is any parameter. A change of parameter $\lambda \mapsto \tau(\lambda)$ with $d\tau/d\lambda > 0$ rescales the velocity, $dx^\mu/d\lambda = (d\tau/d\lambda)\,u^\mu$; the tangent line is a property of the worldline alone.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.2, eqs. (3.21)–(3.26); Definition 18, eq. (3.32) (pp. 45–47) · Hartle §20.1, eqs. (20.1)–(20.3) (p. 419; "$t$ is the four-velocity $u$ if $\sigma$ is the proper time") · Schutz §5.2, eqs. (5.17)–(5.20) (pp. 120–121) · d'Inverno & Vickers §5.5, eqs. (5.14)–(5.17) (p. 71)*

A second chart gives a second basis and second components for the same vector. The relation between them is the chain rule.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.2: Transformation Law of Vector Components
> Let $(U, \varphi)$ and $(U', \varphi')$ be charts with $p \in U \cap U'$, coordinates $x^\mu$ and $x'^\nu$, and transition function $x'(x) = \varphi'\circ\varphi^{-1}$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]). Then at $p$:
> 1. the coordinate bases ([[#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]]) are related by
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu|_p = \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}(p)\;\partial'_\nu|_p, \qquad \partial'_\nu|_p = \frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x'^\nu}(p)\;\partial_\mu|_p ;
> $$
>
> 2. the components of every $X_p \in T_pM$ transform **contravariantly**:
>
> $$
> X'^\nu = \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}(p)\,X^\mu ;
> $$
>
> 3. the two Jacobian matrices are inverse to each other ([[§17 Differentiable Structures#^lem-17-1|591 Lemma §17.1]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}\,\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x'^\rho} = \delta^\nu_\rho, \qquad \frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\rho} = \delta^\mu_\rho .
> $$
>
> *Layer change.* Two inertial coordinate systems of Minkowski spacetime are related by $x'^\nu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu x^\mu + a^\nu$. Their Jacobian is the constant matrix $\Lambda^\nu{}_\mu$, and part 2 is the law of a four-vector ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]], type $(1, 0)$; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$; the entries $\Lambda^\nu{}_\mu$ are the same in both signatures, because $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta$ is unchanged by $\eta \to -\eta$). For a general change of chart the matrix is the Jacobian of the transition function at $p$, a different matrix at every point. The law holds point by point, for vectors at $p$, and not for coordinate differences between two points (opening).
>
> The vector itself does not change: $X_p = X^\mu\partial_\mu|_p = X'^\nu\partial'_\nu|_p$. Basis and components change by inverse matrices, so that their combination stays fixed.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a smooth manifold; *applies:* any two charts whose domains contain $p$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.2, eqs. (3.28)–(3.31) (pp. 46–47) · [[§30 The Differential in Coordinates#^cor-30-4|591 Cor. §30.4]] · Hartle §20.1, Example 20.1, eqs. (20.5)–(20.6) (pp. 420–421) · d'Inverno & Vickers §5.5, eqs. (5.17)–(5.18) (p. 71) · Schutz §5.2, eqs. (5.8)–(5.9), (5.20) (pp. 119–121)*

^thm-c2-2-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (factor the coordinate representation). For $f \in C^\infty(M)$ let $F' = f\circ\varphi'^{-1}$, a function of the primed coordinates. On $\varphi(U \cap U')$,
>
> $$
> f\circ\varphi^{-1} = (f\circ\varphi'^{-1})\circ(\varphi'\circ\varphi^{-1}) = F'\bigl(x'(x)\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (chain rule). By the definition of $\partial_\mu|_p$ ([[#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]]) and the chain rule in $\mathbf R^n$, with $x = \varphi(p)$ and $x' = \varphi'(p)$,
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu|_p(f) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}F'\bigl(x'(x)\bigr) = \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}(x)\,\frac{\partial F'}{\partial x'^\nu}(x') = \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}(p)\;\partial'_\nu|_p(f) .
> $$
>
> This holds for every $f$, which is the first formula of part 1. Exchanging the roles of the two charts gives the second.
>
> **Step 3** (components). Insert part 1 into $X_p = X^\mu\partial_\mu|_p$:
>
> $$
> X_p = X^\mu\,\frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}\;\partial'_\nu|_p .
> $$
>
> The components of $X_p$ in the basis $\partial'_\nu|_p$ are unique ([[#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]], part 2), so $X'^\nu = (\partial x'^\nu/\partial x^\mu)\,X^\mu$. Directly from part 3 of that theorem: $X'^\nu = X_p(x'^\nu) = X^\mu\,\partial_\mu|_p(x'^\nu) = X^\mu\,\partial x'^\nu/\partial x^\mu$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (inverse matrices). Apply part 1 twice:
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu|_p = \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}\,\partial'_\nu|_p = \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}\,\frac{\partial x^\rho}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\partial_\rho|_p .
> $$
>
> By linear independence the coefficient of $\partial_\rho|_p$ is $\delta^\rho_\mu$, which is the second identity of part 3; the first follows in the same way from the primed basis. It is also the chain rule for $x \mapsto x' \mapsto x$, the identity map.
>
> **Step 5** (Lorentz case). For $x'^\nu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu x^\mu + a^\nu$, $\partial x'^\nu/\partial x^\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu$ at every point, and part 2 becomes $X'^\nu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu X^\mu$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The transformation law is the chain rule at a single point. Its matrix is the derivative of the transition function at $p$, the linear term of the Taylor expansion in the opening. The second derivatives $\partial^2x'/\partial x\,\partial x$, which spoiled the coordinate differences, never enter, because a tangent vector sees only first-order data ([[#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]], part 4). They return as soon as vectors at different points are compared ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]]).

Physics needs a vector at every event, not at one: the four-velocity of a fluid at each point, the generator of a symmetry, the coordinate directions $\partial_\mu$ themselves.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.2.4: Vector Field
> A **vector field** $X$ on $M$ assigns to each $p \in M$ a tangent vector $X_p \in T_pM$ ([[#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]) such that, in every chart,
>
> $$
> X = X^\mu(x)\,\partial_\mu, \qquad X^\mu = X(x^\mu) ,
> $$
>
> with smooth component functions $X^\mu$ on the chart domain ([[§56 Vector Fields#^def-56-1|591 Def. §56.1]], [[§56 Vector Fields#^prop-56-1|591 Prop. §56.1]]). The set of vector fields is $\mathfrak X(M)$.
>
> *As an operator.* A vector field maps functions to functions, $X(f)(p) = X_p(f)$, and $X : C^\infty(M) \to C^\infty(M)$ is linear and satisfies $X(fg) = f\,X(g) + g\,X(f)$. Conversely every such derivation of $C^\infty(M)$ is a vector field ([[§56 Vector Fields#^prop-56-10|591 Prop. §56.10]]).
>
> *On overlaps.* Where two charts overlap, the components obey [[#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]] at each point: $X'^\nu\bigl(x'(x)\bigr) = \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}(x)\,X^\mu(x)$.
>
> *Coordinate fields.* On a chart domain $U$ the maps $p \mapsto \partial_\mu|_p$ are vector fields on $U$, but in general not on $M$: $\partial_\varphi$ on the sphere is not defined at the poles, which the chart $(\theta, \varphi)$ misses ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-3|Example §C2.1.3]]). The curves whose velocity is $X$ at each of their points are the integral curves of $X$ ([[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors#^def-c2-5-1|Def. §C2.5.1]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.3, eq. (3.33) (p. 48) · d'Inverno & Vickers §5.10 (index-free vector fields as operators)*

## One-forms

A tangent vector takes a function and returns its rate of change. Turned around, a function determines, at each point, a linear rule that takes a direction and returns the rate of change of the function in that direction. At level B this was the gradient $\partial_\mu\phi$, whose index sat downstairs because it transforms with the inverse Lorentz matrix ([[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]; [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^rem-b2-2-5|§B2.2, Remark: Why the gradient carries a lower index]]). Such linear rules are the second kind of one-index object.

> [!definition] Definition §C2.2.5: Cotangent Space; One-Form
> *Structure.* The **cotangent space** of $M$ at $p$ is the dual vector space of the tangent space ([[#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]),
>
> $$
> T^{\ast}_pM = (T_pM)^{\ast} ,
> $$
>
> the space of linear maps $\omega_p : T_pM \to \mathbf R$ (linear functionals, [[§12 Duality#^ladr-3-108|LADR Def. 3.108]]; dual space, [[§12 Duality#^ladr-3-110|LADR Def. 3.110]]; [[§32 The Cotangent Space#^def-32-4|591 Def. §32.4]]). Its elements are called **covectors**, cotangent vectors, or **one-forms at $p$**. The number $\omega_p(X_p)$, also written $\langle\omega_p, X_p\rangle$, is the **pairing** of $\omega_p$ with $X_p$. Since $T_pM$ has dimension $n$, so has $T^{\ast}_pM$ ([[§12 Duality#^ladr-3-111|LADR Thm. 3.111]]).
>
> *What it is.* A covector is not a vector of $T_pM$ but a linear measuring device on vectors: it assigns a number to every direction at $p$, linearly. Schutz pictures it as a family of parallel hyperplanes in $T_pM$, with $\omega_p(X_p)$ the number of them that $X_p$ crosses.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.4 (p. 48) · Hartle §20.2, eqs. (20.7)–(20.8) (p. 421) · d'Inverno & Vickers §5.6, eqs. (5.22)–(5.25) (p. 73) · Schutz §5.2, eq. (5.10) (p. 119)*

^def-c2-2-5

> [!definition] Definition §C2.2.6: Differential of a Function
> For $f \in C^\infty(M)$ and $p \in M$, the **differential** of $f$ at $p$ is the covector $df_p \in T^{\ast}_pM$ ([[#^def-c2-2-5|Def. §C2.2.5]]) defined by
>
> $$
> df_p(X_p) = X_p(f) \qquad\text{for all } X_p \in T_pM :
> $$
>
> the rate of change of $f$ along $X_p$ ([[#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]]). It is linear in $X_p$ by the definition of the vector operations on $T_pM$. Along a curve, $df(\dot\gamma) = \frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))$ ([[#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]).
>
> *Coordinate differentials.* The coordinate functions of a chart give the covectors $dx^\mu|_p$, and $dx^\mu|_p(X_p) = X_p(x^\mu) = X^\mu$ ([[#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]]): $dx^\mu$ reads off the $\mu$-th component of a vector. These $dx^\mu$ are exact objects, not small numbers. In a line element such as $ds^2 = g_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]]) they can be read either way: as the components of a small displacement, or as covectors whose products build the $(0, 2)$ tensor $g$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]); Schutz warns that the notation invites the confusion.
>
> *MATH 591* builds $df_p$ as the push-forward $f_{\ast p} : T_pM \to T_{f(p)}\mathbf R$ followed by the identification $T_{f(p)}\mathbf R \cong \mathbf R$ ([[§32 The Cotangent Space#^def-32-5|591 Def. §32.5]]) and then shows $df_p(X_p) = X_p(f)$ ([[§32 The Cotangent Space#^prop-32-2|591 Prop. §32.2]]), the definition used here.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.4, eq. (3.34) (p. 48) · Hartle §20.2, Example 20.2, eq. (20.9) (p. 421) · Schutz §5.2, eqs. (5.10)–(5.11), (5.19) (pp. 119–121); eq. (5.33) and the warning after it (p. 123)*

> [!definition] Definition §C2.2.7: One-Form Field
> A **one-form** (one-form field, covector field) $\omega$ on $M$ assigns to each $p \in M$ a covector $\omega_p \in T^{\ast}_pM$ ([[#^def-c2-2-5|Def. §C2.2.5]]) such that, in every chart,
>
> $$
> \omega = \omega_\mu(x)\,dx^\mu, \qquad \omega_\mu = \omega(\partial_\mu) ,
> $$
>
> with smooth component functions $\omega_\mu$ on the chart domain ([[§53 One-Forms#^def-53-1|591 Def. §53.1]], [[§53 One-Forms#^prop-53-1|591 Prop. §53.1]]). Equivalently, $\omega(X)$, $p \mapsto \omega_p(X_p)$, is a smooth function for every vector field $X$ ([[#^def-c2-2-4|Def. §C2.2.4]]). The set of one-forms is $\Omega^1(M)$. The first example is the differential $df : p \mapsto df_p$ of a smooth function ([[#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]]), a one-form for every $f \in C^\infty(M)$ ([[§53 One-Forms#^prop-53-3|591 Prop. §53.3]]). Not every one-form is a differential ([[§C2.6 Differential Forms#^def-c2-6-4|Def. §C2.6.4]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.4 (p. 49: $\Omega^{(1)}(M)$) · Schutz §5.2, eq. (5.10) (p. 119)*

^def-c2-2-7

The coordinate differentials $dx^\mu$ and the coordinate derivations $\partial_\nu$ are made for each other: each $dx^\mu$ reads off one component. That makes them dual bases, and it fixes how covector components transform.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.3: Dual Basis and Covariant Transformation Law
> Let $(U, \varphi)$ be a chart at $p$ with coordinates $x^\mu$.
> 1. **Dual basis.** $dx^\mu|_p(\partial_\nu|_p) = \delta^\mu_\nu$, so $\{dx^1|_p, \dots, dx^n|_p\}$ is the basis of $T^{\ast}_pM$ dual to the coordinate basis ([[§12 Duality#^ladr-3-112|LADR Def. 3.112]]; [[§33 Covectors in Coordinates#^lem-33-1|591 Lemma §33.1]]).
> 2. **Components.** Every $\omega_p \in T^{\ast}_pM$ is $\omega_p = \omega_\mu\,dx^\mu|_p$, with the unique **components** $\omega_\mu = \omega_p(\partial_\mu|_p)$ ([[§33 Covectors in Coordinates#^cor-33-2|591 Cor. §33.2]]). For a differential ([[#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]]), $(df)_\mu = \partial_\mu f(p)$:
>
> $$
> df = \partial_\mu f\,dx^\mu .
> $$
>
> 3. **Covariant law.** For a second chart with coordinates $x'^\nu$ at $p$,
>
> $$
> dx'^\nu|_p = \frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}(p)\,dx^\mu|_p, \qquad \omega'_\nu = \frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x'^\nu}(p)\,\omega_\mu :
> $$
>
> covector components transform with the inverse Jacobian, contracted on its lower index ([[§52 The Cotangent Bundle#^prop-52-1|591 Prop. §52.1]]). In matrix form, if vector components transform as $X' = A\,X$ with $A^\nu{}_\mu = \partial x'^\nu/\partial x^\mu$ ([[#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]), then the column of covector components transforms as $\omega' = (A^{-1})^{\mathsf T}\omega$.
>
> No metric is used: the lower index records that $\omega$ is an element of the dual space, and the inverse transpose is what duality forces.
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a smooth manifold; *applies:* any chart, any two charts whose domains contain $p$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.4, eqs. (3.35)–(3.40) (p. 49) · Schutz §5.2, eqs. (5.11)–(5.14) (p. 119); eqs. (5.15)–(5.16) (the two matrices are inverse, p. 120) · d'Inverno & Vickers §5.6, eqs. (5.22)–(5.25) (p. 73) · Hartle §20.2 (p. 421)*

^thm-c2-2-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (dual basis). By [[#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]] and [[#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]], part 1,
>
> $$
> dx^\mu|_p(\partial_\nu|_p) = \partial_\nu|_p(x^\mu) = \delta^\mu_\nu .
> $$
>
> $n$ covectors with this property are linearly independent (apply $c_\mu dx^\mu = 0$ to $\partial_\nu$: $c_\nu = 0$) and, since $\dim T^{\ast}_pM = n$, a basis. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (components). Given $\omega_p$, put $\omega_\mu = \omega_p(\partial_\mu|_p)$. The covector $\omega_\mu dx^\mu|_p$ takes the value $\omega_\mu\delta^\mu_\nu = \omega_\nu$ on $\partial_\nu|_p$, the same as $\omega_p$. Two linear maps that agree on a basis are equal, so $\omega_p = \omega_\mu dx^\mu|_p$. If also $\omega_p = c_\mu dx^\mu|_p$, evaluating on $\partial_\nu|_p$ gives $c_\nu = \omega_\nu$: the components are unique. For $\omega = df$, $(df)_\mu = df(\partial_\mu) = \partial_\mu f$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 3** (the new basis covectors). $dx'^\nu|_p$ is the differential of the function $x'^\nu$. By part 2 its components in the unprimed chart are $\partial_\mu x'^\nu = \partial x'^\nu/\partial x^\mu$, so $dx'^\nu|_p = (\partial x'^\nu/\partial x^\mu)\,dx^\mu|_p$.
>
> **Step 4** (the new components). By part 2 in the primed chart, then the second formula of [[#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], part 1, then linearity of $\omega_p$:
>
> $$
> \omega'_\nu = \omega_p(\partial'_\nu|_p) = \omega_p\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\partial_\mu|_p\Bigr) = \frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\omega_p(\partial_\mu|_p) = \frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\omega_\mu .
> $$
>
> This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (matrix form). Write $B^\mu{}_\nu = \partial x^\mu/\partial x'^\nu$; by [[#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], part 3, $B = A^{-1}$. Step 4 reads $\omega'_\nu = B^\mu{}_\nu\,\omega_\mu = (B^{\mathsf T})_\nu{}^\mu\,\omega_\mu$, the sum running over the first index of $B$. So $\omega' = B^{\mathsf T}\omega = (A^{-1})^{\mathsf T}\omega$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The covariant law follows from two facts only: a covector is linear, and the basis vectors transform by the inverse Jacobian. The lower index is the type of the object, an element of the dual space; no metric and no signature enter. At level B the same law was written with metrics, $\partial'_\mu = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu\partial_\nu$ with $\Lambda_\mu{}^\nu = \eta_{\mu\alpha}\Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta\eta^{\beta\nu}$; there the metrics only served to write the inverse transpose of a Lorentz matrix ([[#^ex-c2-2-3|Example §C2.2.3]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.4: The Pairing Is Chart-Independent
> For $\omega_p \in T^{\ast}_pM$ ([[#^def-c2-2-5|Def. §C2.2.5]]) and $X_p \in T_pM$ ([[#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]), in any chart at $p$,
>
> $$
> \omega_p(X_p) = \omega_\mu X^\mu ,
> $$
>
> and for every second chart at $p$, $\omega'_\nu X'^\nu = \omega_\mu X^\mu$. For a differential, $df(X) = X^\mu\partial_\mu f = X(f)$.
>
> Summing an upper index against a lower one gives a number every chart agrees on. Sums over two upper indices, or two lower ones, do not ([[#^cau-c2-2-1|Caution: Index position is the type, and nothing raises it yet]]).
>
> *Model:* none, geometry of a smooth manifold; *applies:* any chart at $p$.

> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §3.2.4, eq. (3.38) (p. 49); §2.1.3, eqs. (2.37)–(2.38) (p. 20, the Lorentz case) · Hartle §20.2, eq. (20.8) (p. 421) · Schutz §5.2, eq. (5.19) (p. 121)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (from linearity). With $X_p = X^\mu\partial_\mu|_p$ ([[#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]]) and $\omega_\mu = \omega_p(\partial_\mu|_p)$ ([[#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]),
>
> $$
> \omega_p(X_p) = \omega_p(X^\mu\partial_\mu|_p) = X^\mu\,\omega_p(\partial_\mu|_p) = \omega_\mu X^\mu .
> $$
>
> The left side does not mention a chart, so the right side is the same in every chart.
>
> **Step 2** (directly from the two laws). By [[#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]], part 3, [[#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], part 2, and part 3 of that theorem,
>
> $$
> \omega'_\nu X'^\nu = \frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x'^\nu}\,\omega_\mu\;\frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\rho}\,X^\rho = \omega_\mu\,\Bigl(\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x'^\nu}\frac{\partial x'^\nu}{\partial x^\rho}\Bigr)X^\rho = \omega_\mu\,\delta^\mu_\rho\,X^\rho = \omega_\mu X^\mu .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (differential). For $\omega = df$, $\omega_\mu = \partial_\mu f$, so $df(X) = X^\mu\partial_\mu f$, which is $X(f)$ by [[#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]], part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The inverse matrices of the two transformation laws cancel in the contraction of an upper with a lower index. This is the mechanism behind every invariant built by contraction ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]).

## What cannot be done without a metric

> [!caution] Caution: Index position is the type, and nothing raises it yet
> At level B every four-vector also had lower components, $a_\mu = \eta_{\mu\nu}a^\nu$ ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-4|Def. §B1.1.4]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), so that "upper" and "lower" looked like two ways of writing the same object. That identification used the metric $\eta$. In this chapter there is no metric, and:
> - $X^\mu$ are components of a vector in $T_pM$ ([[#^def-c2-2-2|Def. §C2.2.2]]) and $\omega_\mu$ components of a covector in $T^{\ast}_pM$ ([[#^def-c2-2-5|Def. §C2.2.5]]). The two spaces have the same dimension but no preferred isomorphism. Matching $\partial_\mu \mapsto dx^\mu$ in one chart gives a different matching in another. An expression $X_\mu$ has no meaning yet.
> - **Computed.** On the plane, the constant field $\partial_x$ and the one-form $dx$ have the same components $(1, 0)$ in Cartesian coordinates. In polar coordinates $\partial_x$ has components $(\cos\theta, -\sin\theta/r)$ and $dx$ has $(\cos\theta, -r\sin\theta)$ ([[#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]]). Equal components in one chart are a coincidence of that chart.
> - **Computed.** $\sum_\mu X^\mu X^\mu$ is not a chart-independent number. For $\partial_x$ it is $1$ in Cartesian coordinates and $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta/r^2$ in polar coordinates, e.g. $\tfrac14$ at $r = 2$, $\theta = \pi/2$. Only up–down contractions are invariant ([[#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]]).
>
> A metric supplies the missing identification (§C3.1, planned). With the Euclidean metric of the plane, whose polar components are $\operatorname{diag}(1, r^2)$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^ex-c2-3-2|Example §C2.3.2]]), lowering $(\cos\theta, -\sin\theta/r)$ gives $(\cos\theta, -r^2\sin\theta/r) = (\cos\theta, -r\sin\theta)$, the components of $dx$ in every chart. The identification exists only once a metric is chosen, and depends on which one.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §2.1.3 (p. 20: raising and lowering with $\eta$); §3.2.4 · Schutz §5.2, eqs. (5.35)–(5.36) (p. 124: "vectors generally have different components from their associated one-forms") · numbers checked by script*

> [!caution] Caution: ∂θ is not the unit vector θ̂
> Vector calculus expands vectors in unit vectors $\hat r, \hat\theta$ with scale factors ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^def-b1-1-2|EM Def. §B1.1.2]]). The coordinate basis is a different basis:
> - $\partial_\theta = -r\sin\theta\,\partial_x + r\cos\theta\,\partial_y$ ([[#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]]). With the Euclidean dot product of its Cartesian components it has length $r$, so $\partial_\theta = r\,\hat\theta$, and a vector's coordinate component is $X^\theta = X^{\hat\theta}/r$.
> - For a gradient, the coordinate component of the one-form is $(df)_\theta = \partial_\theta f$, while the $\hat\theta$-component of the vector $\nabla f$ is $\frac1r\partial_\theta f$ ([[§B1.1 Fields, Integral Theorems and Curvilinear Coordinates#^thm-b1-1-5|EM Theorem §B1.1.5]]). Formulas of vector calculus in curvilinear coordinates cannot be read as coordinate-basis formulas.
> - "Unit" needs lengths, hence a metric (§C3.1, planned). In this chapter $\partial_\theta$ has no length.
> - The field $\hat\theta = \frac1r\partial_\theta$ is not the coordinate basis field of any chart. Its dual one-form would be $r\,d\theta = -\sin\theta\,dx + \cos\theta\,dy$. If this were $d\eta$ for a coordinate $\eta$, the mixed partial derivatives of $\eta$ would agree, $\partial_x(\cos\theta) = \partial_y(-\sin\theta)$. Instead $\partial_x(\cos\theta) - \partial_y(-\sin\theta) = 1/r \ne 0$. Equivalently, coordinate fields have vanishing brackets, $[\partial_\mu, \partial_\nu]f = \partial_\mu\partial_\nu f - \partial_\nu\partial_\mu f = 0$ (the bracket: [[§58 Lie Bracket and Lie Algebra#^def-58-1|591 Def. §58.1]]; its role, [[§C2.5 Flows and the Lie Derivative of Tensors|§C2.5]]), while $[\partial_r, \frac1r\partial_\theta] = -\frac1{r^2}\partial_\theta \ne 0$. Schutz calls such a basis a noncoordinate basis.
>
> *Source: Schutz §5.2, eq. (5.28) (p. 122: $|\vec e_\theta| = r$); §5.5, eqs. (5.76)–(5.84) (pp. 135–136) · Couzens, GR1, §3.2.2 (p. 48: non-coordinate bases, vielbeins, deferred) · checked by script*

^cau-c2-2-2

Vectors and one-forms are the objects with one index, and the pairing of one of each is the first invariant. The metric of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]] needs more. It takes two vectors and returns a number, and so does the electromagnetic field strength $F_{\mu\nu}$; the energy–momentum tensor $T^{\mu\nu}$ takes two covectors. What are these objects with several indices, how do their components transform under an arbitrary change of chart, and which operations on them give results independent of the chart? The answer is [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields|§C2.3]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C2.2.1: ∂x and dx in Polar Coordinates
> *Setup.* The plane $M = \mathbf R^2$ with the global Cartesian chart $(x, y)$ and the polar chart $(r, \theta)$ on $U' = \mathbf R^2$ minus the closed half-line $\{y = 0,\ x \le 0\}$, with $r > 0$ and $-\pi < \theta < \pi$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]; why the half-line must go: [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^rem-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Remark: Coordinate singularities are failures of a chart, not of the manifold]]). The transition is $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, with Jacobian determinant $r > 0$ on $U'$. The two Jacobian matrices ([[#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], part 3) are
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(r, \theta)} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \frac{\partial(r, \theta)}{\partial(x, y)} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta\\ -\sin\theta/r & \cos\theta/r\end{pmatrix} ,
> $$
>
> the second from $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, $\partial r/\partial x = x/r = \cos\theta$, $\partial r/\partial y = \sin\theta$, $\partial\theta/\partial x = -y/r^2 = -\sin\theta/r$, $\partial\theta/\partial y = x/r^2 = \cos\theta/r$. Their product is the identity.
>
> *(a) The polar basis vectors.* By [[#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], part 1 (with the polar chart unprimed), each polar basis vector has the columns of the first matrix as Cartesian components:
>
> $$
> \partial_r = \frac{\partial x}{\partial r}\partial_x + \frac{\partial y}{\partial r}\partial_y = \cos\theta\,\partial_x + \sin\theta\,\partial_y, \qquad \partial_\theta = \frac{\partial x}{\partial\theta}\partial_x + \frac{\partial y}{\partial\theta}\partial_y = -r\sin\theta\,\partial_x + r\cos\theta\,\partial_y .
> $$
>
> *(b) The vector $\partial_x$ in polar components.* $\partial_x$ has Cartesian components $(1, 0)$. By [[#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], part 2, its polar components are the first column of the second matrix:
>
> $$
> (\partial_x)^r = \frac{\partial r}{\partial x}\cdot 1 + \frac{\partial r}{\partial y}\cdot 0 = \cos\theta, \qquad (\partial_x)^\theta = \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x} = -\frac{\sin\theta}{r}, \qquad \partial_x = \cos\theta\,\partial_r - \frac{\sin\theta}{r}\,\partial_\theta .
> $$
>
> Check by inserting (a): $\cos\theta(\cos\theta\,\partial_x + \sin\theta\,\partial_y) - \frac{\sin\theta}{r}(-r\sin\theta\,\partial_x + r\cos\theta\,\partial_y) = (\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta)\,\partial_x + (\cos\theta\sin\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta)\,\partial_y = \partial_x$.
>
> *(c) The one-form $dx$ in polar components.* By [[#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]], part 2, the components of $dx = d(r\cos\theta)$ in the polar chart are the partial derivatives of the function $x = r\cos\theta$:
>
> $$
> dx = \frac{\partial x}{\partial r}\,dr + \frac{\partial x}{\partial\theta}\,d\theta = \cos\theta\,dr - r\sin\theta\,d\theta .
> $$
>
> The covariant law gives the same: $dx$ has Cartesian components $(1, 0)$, so $\omega'_r = \frac{\partial x}{\partial r}\cdot 1 + \frac{\partial y}{\partial r}\cdot 0 = \cos\theta$ and $\omega'_\theta = \frac{\partial x}{\partial\theta} = -r\sin\theta$. In the other direction, $dr = \cos\theta\,dx + \sin\theta\,dy$ and $d\theta = -\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\,dx + \frac{\cos\theta}{r}\,dy$ (the rows of the second matrix).
>
> *(d) Pairings.* In polar components, by [[#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]],
>
> $$
> dx(\partial_x) = \cos\theta\cdot\cos\theta + (-r\sin\theta)\Bigl(-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Bigr) = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1 ,
> $$
>
> the value $1\cdot 1 + 0\cdot 0$ of Cartesian components. Likewise $dr(\partial_\theta) = \cos\theta(-r\sin\theta) + \sin\theta(r\cos\theta) = 0$ in Cartesian components, as the dual-basis relation requires.
>
> *(e) Back to the opening.* At $p = (0, 2)$, i.e. $r = 2$, $\theta = \pi/2$, part (b) gives $\partial_x = 0\cdot\partial_r - \tfrac12\,\partial_\theta$. The curve $q_\varepsilon = (\varepsilon, 2)$ of the opening has velocity $\partial_x$ at $p$, with polar components $(0, -\tfrac12)$: the first-order coefficients of $(\Delta r, \Delta\theta)$ computed there.
>
> *(f) What changes from point to point.* The field $\partial_x$ is "the same everywhere" in the plane, yet its polar components $(\cos\theta, -\sin\theta/r)$ vary with the point, because the basis $\partial_r, \partial_\theta$ does. So differentiating components does not differentiate the field. This is the subject of [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]] and [[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^ex-c2-3-3|Example §C2.3.3]].
>
> *Source: Schutz §5.2, eqs. (5.21)–(5.27) (pp. 121–122); §5.3 (p. 124: the components $(\cos\theta, -r^{-1}\sin\theta)$ of $\vec e_x$); Exercises 5.7–5.8 · d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 5.17 · checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.2.2: Parabolic Coordinates
> *Question* (Hartle, Problem 2.7; PHY 360 HW 1, Problem 1). Parabolic coordinates $(\mu, \nu)$ label the points of the plane by
>
> $$
> x = \mu\nu, \qquad y = \tfrac12(\mu^2 - \nu^2) .
> $$
>
> (a) Sketch the curves of constant $\mu$ and constant $\nu$. (b) Transform the line element $dS^2 = dx^2 + dy^2$ to $(\mu, \nu)$. (c) Do the coordinate curves meet at right angles? (d) Find the equation of the circle of radius $R$ about the origin. (e) (extra credit) Compute the ratio of circumference to diameter in these coordinates.
>
> *Setup: where is this a chart?* The map $(\mu, \nu) \mapsto (x, y)$ is two-to-one, since $(\mu, \nu)$ and $(-\mu, -\nu)$ have the same image. Its Jacobian
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(\mu, \nu)} = \begin{pmatrix}\nu & \mu\\ \mu & -\nu\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det = -(\mu^2 + \nu^2) ,
> $$
>
> vanishes only at $\mu = \nu = 0$. Restrict to $\mu > 0$. With $R = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, the inverse is
>
> $$
> \mu = \sqrt{R + y}, \qquad \nu = \frac{x}{\sqrt{R + y}} ,
> $$
>
> because $R = \tfrac12(\mu^2 + \nu^2)$ (part (d)) and so $R + y = \mu^2$. It is smooth wherever $R + y > 0$, i.e. off the half-line $\{x = 0,\ y \le 0\}$. So $(\mu, \nu)$ with $\mu > 0$ is a chart ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]) on the plane cut along the negative $y$-axis, the analogue of the cut of polar coordinates in [[#^ex-c2-2-1|Example §C2.2.1]].
>
> *(a) Coordinate curves.* On $\mu = \mu_0$, $\nu = x/\mu_0$, so $y = \tfrac12(\mu_0^2 - x^2/\mu_0^2)$: a parabola opening downward, with vertex $(0, \mu_0^2/2)$. On $\nu = \nu_0 \ne 0$, $\mu = x/\nu_0$, so $y = \tfrac12(x^2/\nu_0^2 - \nu_0^2)$: a parabola opening upward, with vertex $(0, -\nu_0^2/2)$ (with $\mu > 0$, only the half with $x/\nu_0 > 0$). The curve $\nu = 0$ is the positive $y$-axis. Writing the first as $x^2 = -2\mu_0^2\bigl(y - \tfrac12\mu_0^2\bigr)$, its focal length is $\mu_0^2/2$, so its focus is the origin; the same holds for the second family. Both families are confocal parabolas (figure below).
>
> *(b) Basis vectors, one-forms, components.* By [[#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], part 1, the columns of the Jacobian are the Cartesian components of the parabolic basis vectors, and by [[#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]], part 2, its rows are the parabolic components of $dx$ and $dy$:
>
> $$
> \partial_\mu = \nu\,\partial_x + \mu\,\partial_y, \quad \partial_\nu = \mu\,\partial_x - \nu\,\partial_y; \qquad dx = \nu\,d\mu + \mu\,d\nu, \quad dy = \mu\,d\mu - \nu\,d\nu .
> $$
>
> The inverse Jacobian, $\frac{1}{\mu^2 + \nu^2}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\nu & \mu\\ \mu & -\nu\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ (the matrix is its own inverse up to the factor), gives the other direction:
>
> $$
> \partial_x = \frac{\nu\,\partial_\mu + \mu\,\partial_\nu}{\mu^2 + \nu^2}, \quad \partial_y = \frac{\mu\,\partial_\mu - \nu\,\partial_\nu}{\mu^2 + \nu^2}; \qquad d\mu = \frac{\nu\,dx + \mu\,dy}{\mu^2 + \nu^2}, \quad d\nu = \frac{\mu\,dx - \nu\,dy}{\mu^2 + \nu^2} .
> $$
>
> Check: $\partial_x$ applied to the function $x = \mu\nu$ gives $(\nu\cdot\nu + \mu\cdot\mu)/(\mu^2 + \nu^2) = 1$, and applied to $y$ gives $(\nu\cdot\mu + \mu\cdot(-\nu))/(\mu^2 + \nu^2) = 0$. Dual basis: in Cartesian components $d\mu(\partial_\mu) = (\nu\cdot\nu + \mu\cdot\mu)/(\mu^2 + \nu^2) = 1$ and $d\mu(\partial_\nu) = (\nu\mu - \mu\nu)/(\mu^2 + \nu^2) = 0$.
>
> *Components of a vector field.* For the position field $V = x\,\partial_x + y\,\partial_y$, [[#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], part 2, gives
>
> $$
> V^\mu = \frac{\partial\mu}{\partial x}\,x + \frac{\partial\mu}{\partial y}\,y = \frac{\nu\cdot\mu\nu + \mu\cdot\frac12(\mu^2 - \nu^2)}{\mu^2 + \nu^2} = \frac{\mu\cdot\frac12(\mu^2 + \nu^2)}{\mu^2 + \nu^2} = \frac\mu2, \qquad V^\nu = \frac{\mu\cdot\mu\nu - \nu\cdot\frac12(\mu^2 - \nu^2)}{\mu^2 + \nu^2} = \frac\nu2 ,
> $$
>
> so $V = \tfrac12(\mu\,\partial_\mu + \nu\,\partial_\nu)$. Directly: $V(x) = \tfrac12(\mu\cdot\nu + \nu\cdot\mu) = \mu\nu = x$ and $V(y) = \tfrac12(\mu\cdot\mu + \nu\cdot(-\nu)) = y$. The factor $\tfrac12$ appears because $x$ and $y$ are quadratic in $\mu$ and $\nu$.
>
> *The line element.* $dS^2 = dx^2 + dy^2$ is the Euclidean metric of the plane, a $(0, 2)$ tensor built from the one-forms $dx$, $dy$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-1|Def. §C2.3.1]]; the metric in general: §C3.1, planned). Its components in the new chart follow by substituting the one-forms of (b), the covariant law applied in each slot ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]]):
>
> $$
> dS^2 = (\nu\,d\mu + \mu\,d\nu)^2 + (\mu\,d\mu - \nu\,d\nu)^2 = \nu^2d\mu^2 + 2\mu\nu\,d\mu\,d\nu + \mu^2d\nu^2 + \mu^2d\mu^2 - 2\mu\nu\,d\mu\,d\nu + \nu^2d\nu^2 = (\mu^2 + \nu^2)(d\mu^2 + d\nu^2) .
> $$
>
> *(c) Right angles.* The Euclidean dot product of the Cartesian components of $\partial_\mu = (\nu, \mu)$ and $\partial_\nu = (\mu, -\nu)$ is $\nu\mu + \mu(-\nu) = 0$ at every point except the origin, where both vanish: the coordinate curves cross at right angles. In (b) this is the absence of a $d\mu\,d\nu$ term. The angle uses the metric of the plane, which is given here, not constructed.
>
> *(d) The circle.* $x^2 + y^2 = \mu^2\nu^2 + \tfrac14(\mu^2 - \nu^2)^2 = \tfrac14(\mu^4 + 2\mu^2\nu^2 + \nu^4) = \tfrac14(\mu^2 + \nu^2)^2$, so the circle of radius $R$ is
>
> $$
> \mu^2 + \nu^2 = 2R ,
> $$
>
> a circle of radius $\sqrt{2R}$ in the $(\mu, \nu)$ plane.
>
> *(e) Circumference over diameter.* Parametrize the circle by $\mu = \sqrt{2R}\cos\alpha$, $\nu = \sqrt{2R}\sin\alpha$. Then $d\mu^2 + d\nu^2 = 2R\,d\alpha^2$ and $\mu^2 + \nu^2 = 2R$, so by (b) $dS^2 = 4R^2\,d\alpha^2$ and $dS = 2R\,d\alpha$. On it $x = 2R\cos\alpha\sin\alpha = R\sin2\alpha$ and $y = R(\cos^2\alpha - \sin^2\alpha) = R\cos2\alpha$: the parameters $\alpha$ and $\alpha + \pi$ give the same point, and $\alpha \in [0, \pi)$ runs once around the circle. Hence
>
> $$
> C = \int_0^\pi 2R\,d\alpha = 2\pi R, \qquad \frac{C}{2R} = \pi ,
> $$
>
> the correct value. Integrating over $\alpha \in [0, 2\pi)$ would give $2\pi$, an artefact of the two-to-one map: a computation in coordinates must stay inside one chart, here $\mu > 0$, $\alpha \in (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$, of length $\pi$.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Problem 2.7 (a)–(e) (p. 29) · PHY 360 (audit), HW 1, Problem 1 and extra credit (the instructor's solution is an image; worked here) · checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C2.2.3: The Gradient Is a One-Form
> *Level B.* [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) showed that under $x' = \Lambda x$ the derivatives transform as $\partial'_\mu = \Lambda_\mu{}^\nu\,\partial_\nu$, with $\Lambda_\mu{}^\nu = \eta_{\mu\alpha}\Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta\eta^{\beta\nu} = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu$. The metric appears in that formula, but only as a way to write the inverse transpose of a Lorentz matrix: for $x' = \Lambda x$, $\partial x^\nu/\partial x'^\mu = (\Lambda^{-1})^\nu{}_\mu$ directly, and the level-B law is [[#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]], part 3, for the differential $d\phi$. The identity $\eta\Lambda\eta^{-1} = (\Lambda^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$ holds with either sign of $\eta$ (checked by script for a boost), so the law is the same in both signatures. [[§B2.2 Tensors and the Covariance Principle#^rem-b2-2-5|§B2.2, Remark: Why the gradient carries a lower index]] already read $d\phi$ as a linear functional on vectors.
>
> *Level C.* For a scalar field $\phi$ on spacetime and any chart, $d\phi = \partial_\mu\phi\,dx^\mu$ is a one-form ([[#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]], [[#^def-c2-2-7|Def. §C2.2.7]]), and under any change of chart, nonlinear ones included, $\partial'_\nu\phi = (\partial x^\mu/\partial x'^\nu)\,\partial_\mu\phi$ ([[#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]). Its value on a vector is the rate of change of $\phi$ along it, the same in every chart ([[#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]]).
>
> *Worked case: Rindler coordinates.* In the $(t, x)$ plane of Minkowski spacetime take the wedge $x > |t|$ and on it the chart $(\eta, \rho)$, $\rho > 0$,
>
> $$
> t = \rho\sinh\eta, \qquad x = \rho\cosh\eta ,
> $$
>
> the coordinates adapted to uniformly accelerated observers ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^mod-b3-3-4|Model §B3.3.4]]; [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames|§C1.4]]). Let $\phi = x^2 - t^2$. Its Cartesian components are $(\partial_t\phi, \partial_x\phi) = (-2t, 2x)$. The Jacobian entries are $\partial t/\partial\eta = \rho\cosh\eta$, $\partial x/\partial\eta = \rho\sinh\eta$, $\partial t/\partial\rho = \sinh\eta$, $\partial x/\partial\rho = \cosh\eta$. The covariant law gives
>
> $$
> \partial_\eta\phi = \rho\cosh\eta\,(-2\rho\sinh\eta) + \rho\sinh\eta\,(2\rho\cosh\eta) = 0, \qquad \partial_\rho\phi = \sinh\eta\,(-2\rho\sinh\eta) + \cosh\eta\,(2\rho\cosh\eta) = 2\rho\,(\cosh^2\eta - \sinh^2\eta) = 2\rho ,
> $$
>
> so $d\phi = 2\rho\,d\rho$, as the direct substitution $\phi = \rho^2\cosh^2\eta - \rho^2\sinh^2\eta = \rho^2$ confirms.
>
> *Pairing with a four-velocity.* The worldline $t = g^{-1}\sinh g\tau$, $x = g^{-1}\cosh g\tau$ is hyperbolic motion with proper acceleration $g$ ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]] with $c = 1$, $\alpha = g$, and the origin of $x$ shifted by $1/g$). Its velocity ([[#^def-c2-2-3|Def. §C2.2.3]]) has Cartesian components $(u^t, u^x) = (\cosh g\tau, \sinh g\tau)$, and
>
> $$
> d\phi(u) = -2t\cosh g\tau + 2x\sinh g\tau = -\frac2g\sinh g\tau\cosh g\tau + \frac2g\cosh g\tau\sinh g\tau = 0 .
> $$
>
> In the Rindler chart the same worldline is $\eta = g\tau$, $\rho = 1/g$, with velocity components $(u^\eta, u^\rho) = (g, 0)$, and $d\phi(u) = 0\cdot g + 2\rho\cdot 0 = 0$: the same number, as [[#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]] requires. The value $0$ says that $\phi = 1/g^2$ is constant along the worldline, which is a level curve of $\phi$. Neither computation needed the metric: here $\tau$ is just the parameter of the curve.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §2.1.3, eq. (2.41) (p. 20); §3.2.4, eq. (3.35) (p. 49) · Hartle §20.2, Example 20.2 (p. 421; the gradient of $-t^2 + x^2 + y^2 + z^2$) · Schutz §5.2, eqs. (5.10)–(5.14) (p. 119) · checked by script*
