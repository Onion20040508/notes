Does gravity bend light, and by how much? A star seen close to the Sun's edge during an eclipse appears displaced: by what angle? Chapter C1 gave a first answer. In a laboratory at rest in a uniform field, light falls like a thrown body ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-8|Theorem §C1.3.8]]); but joining such laboratories along a ray that passes the Sun gives only half the bending that general relativity predicts ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^cau-c1-3-2|§C1.3, Caution: The equivalence principle gives only part of the bending by the Sun]]). The missing half was traced to the spatial part of the metric, first in the coordinate speed of light ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]], part 3), then in the weak-field metric derived from Einstein's equations: a ray at impact parameter $b$ turns by $(1 + \gamma)\,2M/b$, with $\gamma = 1$ in general relativity and $\gamma = 0$ for a Euclidean space ([[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^ex-c5-5-1|Example §C5.5.1]]). That calculation used the weak-field metric and the first order in $M/b$. Now the exact exterior metric is known ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]), and so is the shape equation of its null geodesics ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-6|Theorem §C6.3.6]]). This section computes the bending from them, to first order, to second order and exactly.

First the Newtonian expectation, computed. Newton's theory has no law for light; the oldest guess (Cavendish 1784, von Soldner 1801, Einstein 1908) treats light as a body that moves at speed $1$ far from the Sun. Per unit mass its angular momentum is $h = b \cdot 1 = b$ for impact parameter $b$, and the orbit equation of a central force ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-1|CM Theorem §B2.2.1]]), with the force $-M/r^2$ per unit mass and $u = 1/r$, reads

$$
\frac{d^2u}{d\varphi^2} + u = \frac{M}{h^2} = \frac{M}{b^2} .
$$

Let the body come in from $\varphi = \pi$ along the line $u = \sin\varphi/b$, so that $u(\pi) = 0$ and $u'(\pi) = \cos\pi/b = -1/b$. The solution with these values is

$$
u = \frac{\sin\varphi}{b} + \frac{M}{b^2}\,(1 + \cos\varphi) ,
$$

since $u'' + u = M/b^2$ for the constant part, $u(\pi) = 0 + (M/b^2)(1 - 1) = 0$, and $u'(\pi) = \cos\pi/b - (M/b^2)\sin\pi = -1/b$. It leaves to infinity where $u = 0$ again. With $\sin\varphi = 2\sin\tfrac\varphi2\cos\tfrac\varphi2$ and $1 + \cos\varphi = 2\cos^2\tfrac\varphi2$,

$$
0 = 2\cos\frac\varphi2\Bigl(\frac1b\sin\frac\varphi2 + \frac{M}{b^2}\cos\frac\varphi2\Bigr) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tan\frac\varphi2 = -\frac Mb \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \varphi_{\text{out}} = -2\arctan\frac Mb .
$$

The body sweeps $\pi + 2\arctan(M/b)$ instead of $\pi$, so it is deflected by $2\arctan(M/b) = 2M/b + O(M^3/b^3)$, Rutherford's formula for an attractive $1/r$ potential at speed $1$ ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-5|CM Theorem §B4.2.5]], $\cot(\chi/2) = b v_\infty^2/M$). At the solar limb that is $0.876''$ (checked by script). The 1919 eclipse and every radio measurement since find twice that ([[#^law-c6-5-5|Law §C6.5.5]]). The computation is not wrong as Newtonian mechanics; it answers a different question. It will turn out to be exactly the part of the bending produced by the time–time component of the metric ([[#^thm-c6-5-3|Theorem §C6.5.3]]), the only component a slow body feels ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]).

The section builds the relativistic answer in order. It defines the two quantities that describe a scattered ray, the impact parameter and the deflection angle ([[#^def-c6-5-1|Def. §C6.5.1]], [[#^def-c6-5-2|Def. §C6.5.2]]). The null orbit equation and its flat solution come next ([[#^thm-c6-5-1|Theorem §C6.5.1]]). Solving it to first order in $M/b$ gives the deflection $4M/b$, $1.75''$ at the solar limb ([[#^thm-c6-5-2|Theorem §C6.5.2]]). Switching off parts of the metric shows where the factor $2$ comes from ([[#^thm-c6-5-3|Theorem §C6.5.3]]). The exact deflection is an integral, finite down to the photon sphere, whose expansion gives the second-order term $(15\pi/4)(M/b)^2$ ([[#^thm-c6-5-4|Theorem §C6.5.4]]). Then the measurements ([[#^law-c6-5-5|Law §C6.5.5]]), and three examples: the Sun, Jupiter, and rays that wind around a black hole.

**Setup.** A light ray in the exterior Schwarzschild metric ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]) of a spherical body of mass $M$ and radius $R$, in Schwarzschild coordinates; the ray stays in $r > R$ (it grazes the body at most). Light is treated as a null geodesic ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]): no medium, no refraction by the solar corona (the measurements remove it, [[#^law-c6-5-5|Law §C6.5.5]]), wavelength small compared with $M$ and $b$. The ray comes from and returns to $r = \infty$, where the metric is flat; source and observer are taken to be far away compared with $b$. By [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]] the ray lies in the equatorial plane $\theta = \pi/2$ and has the constants $E = (1 - 2M/r)\dot t > 0$ and $L = r^2\dot\varphi \ne 0$. The ray is drawn above the centre, moving in the $+x$ direction, entering at $\varphi = \pi$ and leaving near $\varphi = 0$ (as in Couzens and in the first figure below); along it $\varphi$ decreases, so $L < 0$. Reversing $\varphi$ gives the convention $L > 0$ of [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]], part 2, and changes nothing below: the shape equations contain only $u'^2$ and $u''$. For the Sun $M/R = 2.1\times10^{-6}$, so first order in $M/b$ suffices for every measurement in the solar system; second order and the exact result matter only near compact objects. The numbers in the examples were checked by script.

## A scattered ray and its orbit equation

A ray that passes a star starts and ends far away, where spacetime is flat and the Schwarzschild coordinates $(r, \varphi)$ in the plane $\theta = \pi/2$ become ordinary polar coordinates ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]: $g_{\mu\nu} \to \eta_{\mu\nu}$ as $r \to \infty$). There the ray is a straight line, and the two numbers that describe the scattering are read off as in Newtonian scattering ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^def-b4-2-1|CM Def. §B4.2.1]]).

> [!definition] Definition §C6.5.1: Impact Parameter of a Light Ray
> *Structure.* Let a light ray in the plane $\theta = \pi/2$ of an asymptotically flat, spherically symmetric spacetime come in from $r = \infty$. Far away, with $x = r\cos\varphi$, $y = r\sin\varphi$ as Cartesian coordinates of the flat region, the incoming ray approaches a straight line, its **incoming asymptote**. The **impact parameter** $b \ge 0$ is the distance from the centre $r = 0$ to that line, a length.
>
> For a ray coming in parallel to the $x$ axis from $x = -\infty$, the asymptote is $y = b$, and in polar form $r\sin\varphi = b$ with $\varphi \to \pi$ at the start. The impact parameter equals the ratio $|L|/E$ of the ray's two constants ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]], part 4; proof: [[#^thm-c6-5-1|Theorem §C6.5.1]], part 4). It is the layer change of the Newtonian impact parameter ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^def-b4-2-1|CM Def. §B4.2.1]]); it is not the distance of closest approach $r_0$, which is smaller ([[#^thm-c6-5-4|Theorem §C6.5.4]], part 4).
>
> *Source: Hartle §9.4, eqs. (9.64), (9.67)–(9.69), Fig. 9.7, p. 206 (PDF 207) · Schutz §11.1, eq. (11.48), p. 309 (PDF 327) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2.2, eq. (6.71), p. 126 (PDF 127)*

^def-c6-5-1

> [!definition] Definition §C6.5.2: Deflection Angle
> *Structure.* For a ray that comes in from $r = \infty$ and returns to $r = \infty$, let $\Delta\varphi > 0$ be the total change of the polar angle $\varphi$ along the ray, from the incoming to the outgoing asymptote. The **deflection angle** is
>
> $$
> \alpha = \Delta\varphi - \pi ,
> $$
>
> taken positive when the ray turns toward the centre. For $0 \le \alpha < \pi$ it is the angle between the directions of motion along the outgoing and the incoming asymptote. A straight line has $\Delta\varphi = \pi$ and $\alpha = 0$. If $\Delta\varphi > 2\pi$ the ray has wound around the centre; $\alpha$ is then larger than $\pi$ and is not reduced modulo $2\pi$, since it counts the windings.
>
> *Source: Hartle §9.4, eq. (9.82), Fig. 9.10, pp. 210–212 (PDF 211–213) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.7, Fig. 16.10, eq. (16.59), pp. 304–305 (PDF 319–320) · Schutz §11.1, Fig. 11.5, p. 310 (PDF 328)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.5.1: Null Orbit Equation and Its Flat Solution
> Let a light ray move in the plane $\theta = \pi/2$ of the Schwarzschild metric with constants $E > 0$, $L \ne 0$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]), and put $b = |L|/E$, $u = 1/r$, $u' = du/d\varphi$. Then:
> 1. **First integral.**
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{du}{d\varphi}\Bigr)^2 + u^2\,(1 - 2Mu) = \frac{1}{b^2} .
> $$
>
> 2. **Orbit equation.** Wherever $u' \ne 0$, and by continuity everywhere,
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2u}{d\varphi^2} + u = 3Mu^2 ,
> $$
>
>    the null case of [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-6|Theorem §C6.3.6]]. Only $b$ appears: the shape of a ray depends on $|L|/E$ alone, not on the normalization of $\lambda$.
> 3. **Flat solution.** For $M = 0$ the solutions are
>
> $$
> u = \frac{\sin(\varphi - \varphi_0)}{b}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad r\sin(\varphi - \varphi_0) = b ,
> $$
>
>    the straight line at distance $b$ from the origin, with ends at $\varphi = \varphi_0 + \pi$ and $\varphi = \varphi_0$. The same lines are the geodesics of the flat plane in polar coordinates ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^ex-c3-7-1|Example §C3.7.1]]).
> 4. **b is the impact parameter.** For $M \ne 0$, a ray from $r = \infty$ has $u \to 0$ and $u' \to -1/b$ at the start, so its incoming asymptote is the line of part 3 and $b = |L|/E$ is the impact parameter of [[#^def-c6-5-1|Def. §C6.5.1]].
>
> Restored: $(du/d\varphi)^2 + u^2(1 - 2GMu/c^2) = 1/b^2$ and $u'' + u = 3GMu^2/c^2$, with $b = c|L|/E$ in terms of the restored constants (a length).
>
> *Model:* light ray as a null geodesic of the Schwarzschild metric ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]), $r > 2M$, $L \ne 0$; *applies:* exactly. (Radial rays, $L = 0$, are not deflected.)
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.2.2, eqs. (6.69)–(6.71), Fig. 17, p. 126 (PDF 127) · Hartle §9.4, eqs. (9.61)–(9.69), (9.75), pp. 205–210 (PDF 206–211) · Schutz §11.1, eqs. (11.47)–(11.51), pp. 309–310 (PDF 327–328) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.7, eqs. (16.51)–(16.53), p. 303 (PDF 318)*

^thm-c6-5-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (radial equation). For light, $\varepsilon = 0$ in [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]:
>
> $$
> \dot r^2 = E^2 - \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)\frac{L^2}{r^2} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (change from $\lambda$ to $\varphi$). Since $\dot\varphi = L/r^2 \ne 0$ has a fixed sign, $\varphi$ is monotonic along the ray and can serve as parameter. By the chain rule, $\dot r = (dr/d\varphi)\,\dot\varphi$, and with $u = 1/r$, $dr/d\varphi = -u'/u^2$:
>
> $$
> \dot r = -\frac{u'}{u^2}\cdot Lu^2 = -L\,u' .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (first integral). Insert Step 2 into Step 1 and divide by $L^2$:
>
> $$
> L^2u'^2 = E^2 - (1 - 2Mu)\,L^2u^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u'^2 + u^2(1 - 2Mu) = \frac{E^2}{L^2} = \frac{1}{b^2} .
> $$
>
> This is part 1. Rescaling $\lambda \to a\lambda$ divides $E$ and $L$ by $a$ and leaves $b$ unchanged.
>
> **Step 4** (differentiate). Differentiate part 1 with respect to $\varphi$; the right side is constant:
>
> $$
> 2u'u'' + 2uu' - 6Mu^2u' = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2u'\bigl(u'' + u - 3Mu^2\bigr) = 0 .
> $$
>
> Where $u' \ne 0$, part 2 follows. Points with $u' = 0$ (the turning point of a scattered ray) are isolated on a non-circular ray, and both sides of part 2 are continuous, so it holds there too. The circular orbit at $r = 3M$, where $u' \equiv 0$, satisfies part 2 directly: $u = 1/(3M)$ gives $u = 3Mu^2$.
>
> **Step 5** (flat solution). For $M = 0$, part 2 is $u'' + u = 0$, with general solution $u = A\cos\varphi + B\sin\varphi = C\sin(\varphi - \varphi_0)$. Part 1 with $M = 0$ gives $u'^2 + u^2 = C^2\cos^2(\varphi - \varphi_0) + C^2\sin^2(\varphi - \varphi_0) = C^2$, so $C = 1/b$. Then $r\sin(\varphi - \varphi_0) = b$; in Cartesian coordinates rotated by $\varphi_0$ this is the line $y = b$. Since $u > 0$ needs $0 < \varphi - \varphi_0 < \pi$, the line has its ends at $\varphi_0 + \pi$ ($x = -\infty$) and $\varphi_0$ ($x = +\infty$). Part 3.
>
> **Step 6** (asymptote). For $M \ne 0$ and a ray from infinity, $u \to 0$ at the start; part 1 then gives $u'^2 \to 1/b^2$, and $u' < 0$ because $u$ grows from $0$ as $\varphi$ decreases from the start value $\varphi_0 + \pi$; so $u' \to -1/b$. The terms $2Mu^3$ in part 1 and $3Mu^2$ in part 2 vanish faster than $u$ as $u \to 0$, so the ray approaches the solution of part 3 with the same values of $u$ and $u'$: the line at distance $b$. Part 4 (the same argument as Hartle's (9.67)–(9.69), where $b = r^2\,d\varphi/dt$ is evaluated far away).
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The two constants of the motion reduce a null geodesic to one ordinary differential equation for its shape. Gravity enters through the single term $3Mu^2$, which has no Newtonian counterpart for light; everything about the bending follows from it.

## The deflection to first order

For the Sun, $M/b \le M_\odot/R_\odot = 2.1\times10^{-6}$, so the term $3Mu^2$ is a small correction to the straight line of [[#^thm-c6-5-1|Theorem §C6.5.1]], part 3. The perihelion calculation solved the massive orbit equation by the same expansion, and there a term growing with $\varphi$ forced care over many orbits ([[§C6.4 Perihelion Precession#^thm-c6-4-1|Theorem §C6.4.1]]). A scattered ray sweeps an angle of only about $\pi$, so the plain expansion is valid along the whole ray.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.5.2: Deflection Angle 4M/b
> A light ray passes a spherical mass $M$ with impact parameter $b$ ([[#^def-c6-5-1|Def. §C6.5.1]]), $M/b \ll 1$, entering along $u = \sin\varphi/b$ from $\varphi = \pi$ ([[#^thm-c6-5-1|Theorem §C6.5.1]]). To first order in $M/b$:
> 1. **Orbit.**
>
> $$
> u(\varphi) = \frac{\sin\varphi}{b} + \frac{M}{2b^2}\,\bigl(3 + 4\cos\varphi + \cos2\varphi\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 2. **Deflection.** The ray reaches $r = \infty$ again at $\varphi = -4M/b$, so $\Delta\varphi = \pi + 4M/b$ and the deflection angle ([[#^def-c6-5-2|Def. §C6.5.2]]) is
>
> $$
> \alpha = \frac{4M}{b} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \alpha = \frac{4GM}{c^2b}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
>    toward the mass. It is independent of the frequency of the light. The same angle follows from the light wave itself: in the weak field the phase of a wave passing the mass turns its wavefronts by $4M/b$ ([[§C4.6 Geometric Optics꞉ Light Follows Null Geodesics#^ex-c4-6-2|Example §C4.6.2]], Step 4).
> 3. **Closest approach.** The ray is closest to the centre near $\varphi = \pi/2$ (shifted by $O(M/b)$), at $r_0 = b - M$; to this order $b$ and $r_0$ may be exchanged in part 2.
> 4. **Size.** At the solar limb $\alpha = 8.49\times10^{-6}$ rad $= 1.751''$ ([[#^ex-c6-5-1|Example §C6.5.1]]), twice the Newtonian value of the opening.
>
> *Model:* light ray as a null geodesic of the Schwarzschild metric (exterior of a static spherical body), source and observer at $r \to \infty$; *applies:* to first order in $M/b$, the dropped terms being of relative order $M/b$ ([[#^thm-c6-5-4|Theorem §C6.5.4]] gives the next one); for the Sun the relative correction is $\sim 10^{-6}$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.2.2, eqs. (6.72)–(6.76), p. 127 (PDF 128) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.7, eqs. (16.54)–(16.60), pp. 304–305 (PDF 319–320) · Schutz §11.1, eqs. (11.52)–(11.56), pp. 310–311 (PDF 328–329) · Hartle §9.4, eqs. (9.79)–(9.84), pp. 211–212 (PDF 212–213) · 't Hooft ch. 12, eqs. (12.34)–(12.39), p. 56 (PDF 57) · checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (expansion). Write $u = u_0 + u_1$, with $u_0 = \sin\varphi/b$ the flat solution of the given incoming line and $u_1$ the correction of order $M/b^2$. Insert into $u'' + u = 3Mu^2$ ([[#^thm-c6-5-1|Theorem §C6.5.1]], part 2):
>
> $$
> \bigl(u_0'' + u_0\bigr) + \bigl(u_1'' + u_1\bigr) = 3M\bigl(u_0^2 + 2u_0u_1 + u_1^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> On the left, $u_0'' + u_0 = 0$. On the right, $3Mu_0^2$ is of order $M/b^2$, while $6Mu_0u_1$ and $3Mu_1^2$ are of order $M^2/b^3$ and $M^3/b^4$, smaller by the factors $M/b$ and $(M/b)^2$; they are dropped. The first-order equation is
>
> $$
> u_1'' + u_1 = 3Mu_0^2 = \frac{3M}{b^2}\sin^2\varphi = \frac{3M}{2b^2}\bigl(1 - \cos2\varphi\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> with $\sin^2\varphi = \tfrac12(1 - \cos2\varphi)$.
>
> **Step 2** (particular solution). Try $u_p = C + D\cos2\varphi$. Then $u_p'' = -4D\cos2\varphi$ and
>
> $$
> u_p'' + u_p = C - 3D\cos2\varphi \overset{!}{=} \frac{3M}{2b^2} - \frac{3M}{2b^2}\cos2\varphi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C = \frac{3M}{2b^2}, \quad D = \frac{M}{2b^2} ,
> $$
>
> so $u_p = (M/2b^2)(3 + \cos2\varphi)$.
>
> **Step 3** (homogeneous part and initial conditions). The general solution is $u_1 = A\cos\varphi + B\sin\varphi + u_p$. The incoming ray is the given line: at $\varphi = \pi$ both $u = 0$ (the ray starts at infinity there) and $u' = -1/b$ ([[#^thm-c6-5-1|Theorem §C6.5.1]], part 4). Since $u_0(\pi) = 0$ and $u_0'(\pi) = -1/b$ already, $u_1(\pi) = u_1'(\pi) = 0$. With $\cos\pi = -1$, $\sin\pi = 0$, $\cos2\pi = 1$, $\sin2\pi = 0$:
>
> $$
> u_1(\pi) = -A + \frac{M}{2b^2}(3 + 1) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; A = \frac{2M}{b^2}, \qquad u_1'(\pi) = -A\sin\pi + B\cos\pi - \frac{M}{b^2}\sin2\pi = -B = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; B = 0 .
> $$
>
> Hence $u_1 = (2M/b^2)\cos\varphi + (M/2b^2)(3 + \cos2\varphi) = (M/2b^2)(3 + 4\cos\varphi + \cos2\varphi)$, and $u = u_0 + u_1$ is part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (where the ray leaves). Without gravity the ray leaves at $\varphi = 0$. With it, $u = 0$ at an angle $\varphi = -\delta$ close to $0$. Expand to first order in the small quantities $\delta$ and $M/b$: $\sin(-\delta) = -\delta + O(\delta^3)$, $\cos(-\delta) = 1 + O(\delta^2)$, $\cos(-2\delta) = 1 + O(\delta^2)$, so
>
> $$
> 0 = u(-\delta) = -\frac{\delta}{b} + \frac{M}{2b^2}\,(3 + 4 + 1) + O\Bigl(\frac{\delta^3}{b}, \frac{M\delta^2}{b^2}\Bigr) = -\frac\delta b + \frac{4M}{b^2} + \dots \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \delta = \frac{4M}{b} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (angle swept). The ray runs from $\varphi = \pi$ to $\varphi = -\delta$, so $\Delta\varphi = \pi + 4M/b$ and $\alpha = \Delta\varphi - \pi = 4M/b$ ([[#^def-c6-5-2|Def. §C6.5.2]]). It leaves at the polar angle $-\delta < 0$, below the axis through the centre parallel to the incoming line, on the side of the mass: the ray is bent toward the mass. No frequency appears anywhere (a null geodesic has none): part 2.
>
> **Step 6** (closest approach). The ray is closest where $u$ is largest, $u' = 0$. At $\varphi = \pi/2$, from part 1, $u'(\pi/2) = \cos(\pi/2)/b - (M/2b^2)\bigl(4\sin(\pi/2) + 2\sin\pi\bigr) = -2M/b^2$, and $u''(\pi/2) = -1/b + O(M/b^2)$. So the maximum lies at $\varphi = \pi/2 + \eta$ with $\eta = u'(\pi/2)/(-u''(\pi/2)) + \dots = -2M/b + O(M^2/b^2)$, and its value differs from $u(\pi/2)$ by $u'(\pi/2)\,\eta + \tfrac12u''(\pi/2)\,\eta^2 = O(M^2/b^3)$, beyond first order. Hence, to first order,
>
> $$
> \frac1{r_0} = u\Bigl(\frac\pi2\Bigr) = \frac1b + \frac{M}{2b^2}\,(3 + 0 - 1) = \frac1b + \frac M{b^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_0 = \frac{b}{1 + M/b} = b - M + O\Bigl(\frac{M^2}{b}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Since $4M/r_0 = (4M/b)(1 + M/b + \dots) = 4M/b + O(M^2/b^2)$, the exchange changes $\alpha$ only at second order: part 3. (The exact relation is $b = r_0/\sqrt{1 - 2M/r_0}$, [[#^thm-c6-5-4|Theorem §C6.5.4]].)
>
> **Step 7** (restore units and evaluate). $M \to GM/c^2$ gives $\alpha = 4GM/(c^2b)$. For $b = R_\odot$ the numbers are worked in [[#^ex-c6-5-1|Example §C6.5.1]]; the Newtonian corpuscle of the opening gave $2\arctan(M/b) = 2M/b + O(M^3/b^3)$: part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The bending is the cumulative effect of the source $3Mu_0^2$ along the whole straight path. The source is largest near closest approach and is symmetric about it, which is why the incoming and outgoing halves contribute equally ($2M/b$ each, Schutz's $\pi/2 + 2M/b$ per half). In this calculation the expansion parameter is $M/b$ and the range of $\varphi$ is bounded, so no secular term arises, unlike the perihelion problem.

## Where the factor 2 comes from

The opening found $2M/b$ for a Newtonian corpuscle, and [[#^thm-c6-5-2|Theorem §C6.5.2]] finds twice that. [[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^ex-c5-5-1|Example §C5.5.1]] located the difference in the weak-field metric written in isotropic coordinates: the time part gives $2M/b$, the spatial factor another $2M/b$. In Schwarzschild coordinates the metric looks different (only $g_{rr}$ is changed in space, not all of $g_{ij}$), so the question is whether the split survives. It does, and it shows exactly what the Newtonian computation was.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.5.3: Twice the Equivalence-Principle Value
> Consider static, spherically symmetric metrics $ds^2 = -A(r)\,dt^2 + B(r)\,dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$ and light rays in them as null geodesics, with $b = |L|/E$ and $u = 1/r$ as in [[#^thm-c6-5-1|Theorem §C6.5.1]].
> 1. **General orbit equation.**
>
> $$
> u'^2 = \frac{1}{AB\,b^2} - \frac{u^2}{B}, \qquad u'' = \frac12\,\frac{d}{du}\Bigl(\frac{1}{AB\,b^2} - \frac{u^2}{B}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 2. **Time part only** ($A = 1 - 2M/r$, $B = 1$: Schwarzschild clocks, flat space). The orbit equation is $u'' + u = M/[b^2(1 - 2Mu)^2] = M/b^2 + O(M^2/b^3)$: to first order it is **exactly the Newtonian orbit equation** of a corpuscle with speed $1$ at infinity (the opening). Its deflection is $2M/b$, the value of Cavendish, von Soldner and Einstein (1908), and the value obtained by joining the freely falling laboratories of [[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-8|Theorem §C1.3.8]] along the path ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^cau-c1-3-2|§C1.3, Caution: The equivalence principle gives only part of the bending by the Sun]]).
> 3. **Space part only** ($A = 1$, $B = (1 - 2M/r)^{-1}$). The orbit equation is $u'' + u = -M/b^2 + 3Mu^2$, and the deflection is again $2M/b$.
> 4. **⚑ The halves add.** The sources of parts 2 and 3 add up to the Schwarzschild source, $M/b^2 + (-M/b^2 + 3Mu^2) = 3Mu^2$, and to first order the deflections add: $2M/b + 2M/b = 4M/b$ ([[#^thm-c6-5-2|Theorem §C6.5.2]]). The same split holds in the isotropic weak-field coordinates of [[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^ex-c5-5-1|Example §C5.5.1]], where $\alpha = (1 + \gamma)\,2M/b$: the time part is the same function, $-g_{tt} = 1 - 2M/r + O(M^2/r^2)$, in both radial coordinates, so the spatial remainder must also be the same $2M/b$.
> 5. **⚑ What light measures.** A slow body feels only $g_{tt}$ at first order ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]; [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]]), which is why Newtonian gravity reproduces the first half. The second half is the curvature of space, invisible to planets at this order. The deflection of light therefore measures the spatial metric, through the parameter $\gamma$ of [[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^ex-c5-5-1|Example §C5.5.1]]: $\alpha = \tfrac12(1 + \gamma)\cdot 4M/b$ ([[#^law-c6-5-5|Law §C6.5.5]]). The coordinate speed of light ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]], part 3) showed the same factor $2$ in a different quantity.
>
> *Model:* the metrics of parts 2 and 3 are diagnostic devices, not solutions of Einstein's equations: each keeps one component of the Schwarzschild metric and replaces the other by its flat value; *applies:* parts 2–4 to first order in $M/b$; part 1 exactly for any static spherically symmetric metric.
>
> *Source: Schutz §11.1, pp. 311–312 (PDF 329–330) (the Newtonian value $2M/b$; "whatever deflection is produced by $g_{00}$ … is doubled by including the contribution of $g_{rr}$"; worked out here) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2.2, p. 127 (PDF 128) ("the Newtonian prediction gives only half") · 't Hooft ch. 12, Exercise after eq. (12.39), p. 56 (PDF 57) · Hartle Problem 9.17, p. 217 (PDF 218) · checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (general first integral). The metric does not depend on $t$ or $\varphi$, so as in [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]] (the argument of [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^ex-c3-13-3|Example §C3.13.3]] uses only these two symmetries) $E = A\dot t$ and $L = r^2\dot\varphi$ are constant, and the ray can be put in the plane $\theta = \pi/2$. The null condition is
>
> $$
> -A\dot t^2 + B\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\varphi^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad B\dot r^2 = \frac{E^2}{A} - \frac{L^2}{r^2} .
> $$
>
> With $\dot r = -L\,u'$ (Step 2 of [[#^der-c6-5-1|Derivation §C6.5.1]]) and $u = 1/r$, divide by $BL^2$:
>
> $$
> u'^2 = \frac{E^2}{L^2}\,\frac{1}{AB} - \frac{u^2}{B} = \frac{1}{AB\,b^2} - \frac{u^2}{B} \equiv W(u) .
> $$
>
> Differentiating, $2u'u'' = W'(u)\,u'$, so $u'' = \tfrac12W'(u)$: part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (check: Schwarzschild). $A = 1 - 2Mu$, $B = (1 - 2Mu)^{-1}$, $AB = 1$: $W = 1/b^2 - u^2(1 - 2Mu)$, $\tfrac12W' = -u + 3Mu^2$, so $u'' + u = 3Mu^2$, as in [[#^thm-c6-5-1|Theorem §C6.5.1]].
>
> **Step 3** (time part only). $A = 1 - 2Mu$, $B = 1$: $W = 1/[b^2(1 - 2Mu)] - u^2$ and
>
> $$
> \tfrac12W'(u) = \frac12\cdot\frac{2M}{b^2(1 - 2Mu)^2} - u \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u'' + u = \frac{M}{b^2(1 - 2Mu)^2} = \frac{M}{b^2}\bigl(1 + 4Mu + \dots\bigr) = \frac{M}{b^2} + O\Bigl(\frac{M^2}{b^3}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> using $u \le 1/r_0 \sim 1/b$ for the dropped term. To first order this is the equation of the opening, whose solution with the same incoming line is $u = \sin\varphi/b + (M/b^2)(1 + \cos\varphi)$; it leaves where $\tan(\varphi/2) = -M/b$, at $\varphi = -2M/b + O(M^3/b^3)$. Deflection $2M/b$: part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (space part only). $A = 1$, $B = (1 - 2Mu)^{-1}$: $W = (1 - 2Mu)(1/b^2 - u^2) = 1/b^2 - u^2 - 2Mu/b^2 + 2Mu^3$, and
>
> $$
> \tfrac12W'(u) = -u - \frac{M}{b^2} + 3Mu^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u'' + u = -\frac{M}{b^2} + 3Mu^2 .
> $$
>
> First order, as in [[#^der-c6-5-2|Derivation §C6.5.2]], Step 1: $u_1'' + u_1 = -M/b^2 + 3M\sin^2\varphi/b^2 = (M/b^2)\bigl(\tfrac12 - \tfrac32\cos2\varphi\bigr)$. A particular solution is $(M/b^2)\bigl(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos2\varphi\bigr)$, since $(\cos2\varphi)'' + \cos2\varphi = -3\cos2\varphi$ and $-3\cdot\tfrac12 = -\tfrac32$. Add $A_1\cos\varphi + B_1\sin\varphi$ and impose $u_1(\pi) = u_1'(\pi) = 0$: $(M/b^2)(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12) - A_1 = 0$, so $A_1 = M/b^2$; and $-B_1 = 0$. So
>
> $$
> u_1 = \frac{M}{b^2}\Bigl(\frac12 + \cos\varphi + \frac12\cos2\varphi\Bigr), \qquad u_1(0) = \frac{M}{b^2}\Bigl(\frac12 + 1 + \frac12\Bigr) = \frac{2M}{b^2} .
> $$
>
> As in Step 4 of [[#^der-c6-5-2|Derivation §C6.5.2]], $0 = -\delta/b + 2M/b^2$ gives $\delta = 2M/b$: part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (adding). The first-order equation $u_1'' + u_1 = S(\varphi)$ is linear in its source $S$, and the conditions $u_1(\pi) = u_1'(\pi) = 0$ are homogeneous, so solutions for $S_2 + S_3$ are sums of solutions for $S_2$ and $S_3$. Indeed
>
> $$
> \frac{M}{b^2}(1 + \cos\varphi) + \frac{M}{b^2}\Bigl(\frac12 + \cos\varphi + \frac12\cos2\varphi\Bigr) = \frac{M}{2b^2}\bigl(3 + 4\cos\varphi + \cos2\varphi\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the full first-order solution of [[#^thm-c6-5-2|Theorem §C6.5.2]], with $u_1(0) = 2M/b^2 + 2M/b^2 = 4M/b^2$. The exit angle is linear in $u_1(0)$ (Step 4 of [[#^der-c6-5-2|Derivation §C6.5.2]]), so the deflections add.
>
> **Step 6** (isotropic coordinates). The isotropic radius $\rho$ of the weak field and the Schwarzschild $r$ differ by $r = \rho + O(M)$ (they agree at large distance), so $M/r = M/\rho + O(M^2/\rho^2)$ and $-g_{tt} = 1 - 2M/\rho + O(M^2)$ in both. The time part therefore gives $2M/b$ in both coordinate systems by Step 3, and since the total $4M/b$ is a property of the ray (an angle between asymptotes in the flat region), the spatial remainder is $2M/b$ in both: $\gamma\cdot 2M/b$ with $\gamma = 1$ in [[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^ex-c5-5-1|Example §C5.5.1]]. Part 4.
>
> **Step 7** (slow bodies). For a body with speed $v \ll 1$, $g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu$ contains $g_{rr}\dot r^2 \sim v^2$ against $g_{tt}\dot t^2 \sim 1$, so the correction $2M/r$ in $g_{rr}$ enters multiplied by $v^2$ and is of second order ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]). For light $|d\vec x/dt| = 1$ and it is of first order. Part 5.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The Newtonian calculation is not a rival theory but one half of general relativity: it is what the curvature of time alone does to light. The equivalence principle, which constrains only what happens in a freely falling laboratory, cannot see the other half, which is the geometry of space accumulated along the ray.

## The exact deflection and the second order

The expansion of [[#^thm-c6-5-2|Theorem §C6.5.2]] cannot tell how good it is, nor what happens when the ray passes close to a compact object, where $M/b$ is not small. The first integral of [[#^thm-c6-5-1|Theorem §C6.5.1]] gives the swept angle as one integral, exact for every ray that escapes, and its expansion gives every further order.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.5.4: Exact Deflection Integral and Its Expansion
> Let a ray from infinity have impact parameter $b > 3\sqrt3\,M$ (smaller $b$ are captured: [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-5|Theorem §C6.3.5]]), and let $r_0 = 1/u_0$ be its closest approach: $u_0$ is the smallest positive root of $u^2(1 - 2Mu) = 1/b^2$, so that
>
> $$
> b^2 = \frac{r_0^3}{r_0 - 2M}, \qquad r_0 > 3M .
> $$
>
> 1. **Exact integral.** With $\mu = 2M/r_0$,
>
> $$
> \alpha = 2\int_0^{u_0}\frac{du}{\sqrt{1/b^2 - u^2 + 2Mu^3}} - \pi = 2\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{(1 - x^2) - \mu\,(1 - x^3)}} - \pi .
> $$
>
> 2. **Range.** The integral is finite for every $r_0 > 3M$, i.e. $b > 3\sqrt3\,M$, and $\alpha \to \infty$ as $r_0 \to 3M$, the photon sphere ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-5|Theorem §C6.3.5]]; the divergence is logarithmic, [[#^ex-c6-5-3|Example §C6.5.3]]).
> 3. **Expansion.**
>
> $$
> \alpha = \frac{4M}{r_0} + \Bigl(\frac{15\pi}{4} - 4\Bigr)\frac{M^2}{r_0^2} + O\Bigl(\frac{M^3}{r_0^3}\Bigr) = \frac{4M}{b} + \frac{15\pi}{4}\,\frac{M^2}{b^2} + O\Bigl(\frac{M^3}{b^3}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 4. **⚑ Impact parameter versus closest approach.** $b = r_0/\sqrt{1 - 2M/r_0} = r_0 + M + \tfrac32M^2/r_0 + \dots$. The first-order term is the same in both variables, but the second-order coefficient is not ($15\pi/4 - 4 = 7.78$ in $M/r_0$, $15\pi/4 = 11.78$ in $M/b$): a deflection beyond first order must say which variable it uses. $b$ is fixed by the incoming ray; $r_0$ is where it turns.
>
> Restored: $M \to GM/c^2$ throughout, e.g. $\alpha = 4GM/(c^2b) + (15\pi/4)\,G^2M^2/(c^4b^2)$.
>
> *Model:* light ray as a null geodesic of the Schwarzschild metric, source and observer at infinity; *applies:* part 1 exactly for $r_0 > 3M$ (outside the body, $r_0 > R$); part 3 for $M/r_0 \ll 1$.
>
> *Source: Hartle §9.4, eqs. (9.76)–(9.81), Fig. 9.11, pp. 210–212 (PDF 211–213) (the integral in $w = b/r$, expanded to first order) · Schutz §11.1, eqs. (11.47)–(11.49), p. 309 (PDF 327) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2.2, eq. (6.69), p. 126 (PDF 127) · the second-order coefficient worked here; checked by script*

^thm-c6-5-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (half the ray). The first integral $u'^2 = 1/b^2 - u^2 + 2Mu^3 \equiv W(u)$ ([[#^thm-c6-5-1|Theorem §C6.5.1]], part 1) is unchanged by $\varphi \to 2\varphi_0 - \varphi$, and $u' = 0$ at the turning point $\varphi_0$ where $u = u_0$. A solution reflected about $\varphi_0$ solves the same orbit equation with the same values $u(\varphi_0) = u_0$, $u'(\varphi_0) = 0$, so by uniqueness the ray is symmetric about the line from the centre to the turning point. On each half $u$ is monotonic between $0$ and $u_0$ and $|d\varphi| = du/\sqrt{W(u)}$, so
>
> $$
> \Delta\varphi = 2\int_0^{u_0}\frac{du}{\sqrt{W(u)}}, \qquad \alpha = \Delta\varphi - \pi \quad ([[#^def-c6-5-2|Def. §C6.5.2]]) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the turning point). $W(u_0) = 0$ gives $1/b^2 = u_0^2(1 - 2Mu_0)$, i.e. $b^2 = r_0^3/(r_0 - 2M)$. Then
>
> $$
> W(u) = u_0^2(1 - 2Mu_0) - u^2 + 2Mu^3 = (u_0^2 - u^2) - 2M(u_0^3 - u^3) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (scale out $u_0$). Substitute $u = u_0x$, $du = u_0\,dx$, $x$ from $0$ to $1$:
>
> $$
> W = u_0^2\bigl[(1 - x^2) - 2Mu_0(1 - x^3)\bigr], \qquad \frac{du}{\sqrt W} = \frac{dx}{\sqrt{(1 - x^2) - \mu(1 - x^3)}}, \quad \mu = 2Mu_0 = \frac{2M}{r_0} .
> $$
>
> This is part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (factor the endpoint). With $1 - x^2 = (1 - x)(1 + x)$ and $1 - x^3 = (1 - x)(1 + x + x^2)$,
>
> $$
> (1 - x^2) - \mu(1 - x^3) = (1 - x^2)\bigl[1 - \mu\,g(x)\bigr], \qquad g(x) = \frac{1 + x + x^2}{1 + x} = x + \frac{1}{1 + x} .
> $$
>
> On $[0, 1]$, $g'(x) = 1 - (1 + x)^{-2} \ge 0$, so $g$ increases from $g(0) = 1$ to $g(1) = 3/2$. The bracket is positive on $[0, 1]$ iff $\tfrac32\mu < 1$, i.e. $r_0 > 3M$. Then the integrand is $(1 - x^2)^{-1/2}$ times a bounded function, and the integral converges. As $\mu \to 2/3$ the bracket vanishes at $x = 1$ like $(1 - x)$, the integrand behaves like $(1 - x)^{-1}$ there, and the integral diverges. Part 2. The condition $r_0 > 3M$ is $b > 3\sqrt3\,M$, since $b^2 = r_0^3/(r_0 - 2M)$ increases with $r_0$ for $r_0 > 3M$ ($\tfrac{d}{dr_0}\ln b^2 = 3/r_0 - 1/(r_0 - 2M) > 0 \iff r_0 > 3M$) and equals $27M^2$ at $r_0 = 3M$.
>
> **Step 5** (expand). For small $\mu$, $0 \le \mu g \le \tfrac32\mu$ is small uniformly in $x$, and by Taylor's theorem with remainder ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) applied to $(1 - y)^{-1/2} = 1 + \tfrac12y + \tfrac38y^2 + O(y^3)$,
>
> $$
> \bigl[1 - \mu g(x)\bigr]^{-1/2} = 1 + \frac\mu2\,g(x) + \frac{3\mu^2}8\,g(x)^2 + O(\mu^3) ,
> $$
>
> the $O(\mu^3)$ bounded uniformly on $[0, 1]$. Since $\int_0^1(1 - x^2)^{-1/2}dx$ is finite, the remainder contributes $O(\mu^3)$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\Delta\varphi}{2} = I_0 + \frac\mu2\,I_1 + \frac{3\mu^2}8\,I_2 + O(\mu^3), \qquad I_k = \int_0^1\frac{g(x)^k}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}\,dx .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (the three integrals). Substitute $x = \cos\theta$, $dx = -\sin\theta\,d\theta$, $\sqrt{1 - x^2} = \sin\theta$, $x: 0 \to 1$ as $\theta: \pi/2 \to 0$, so $dx/\sqrt{1 - x^2} \to d\theta$ on $[0, \pi/2]$. Two auxiliary integrals, with $1 + \cos\theta = 2\cos^2(\theta/2)$ and $s = \theta/2$:
>
> $$
> \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{1 + \cos\theta} = \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{2\cos^2(\theta/2)} = \Bigl[\tan\frac\theta2\Bigr]_0^{\pi/2} = 1 ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{(1 + \cos\theta)^2} = \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{4\cos^4(\theta/2)} = \frac12\int_0^{\pi/4}\sec^4s\,ds = \frac12\Bigl[\tan s + \frac{\tan^3s}{3}\Bigr]_0^{\pi/4} = \frac12\cdot\frac43 = \frac23 ,
> $$
>
> using $\sec^4s = (1 + \tan^2s)\sec^2s$. Then:
>
> $$
> I_0 = \int_0^{\pi/2}d\theta = \frac\pi2, \qquad I_1 = \int_0^{\pi/2}\Bigl(\cos\theta + \frac{1}{1 + \cos\theta}\Bigr)d\theta = 1 + 1 = 2 ,
> $$
>
> and with $g^2 = x^2 + \dfrac{2x}{1 + x} + \dfrac{1}{(1 + x)^2} = x^2 + 2 - \dfrac{2}{1 + x} + \dfrac{1}{(1 + x)^2}$ and $\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \pi/4$,
>
> $$
> I_2 = \frac\pi4 + 2\cdot\frac\pi2 - 2\cdot1 + \frac23 = \frac{5\pi}{4} - \frac43 .
> $$
>
> **Step 7** (assemble in $r_0$).
>
> $$
> \frac{\Delta\varphi}{2} = \frac\pi2 + \mu + \frac{3\mu^2}{8}\Bigl(\frac{5\pi}4 - \frac43\Bigr) + O(\mu^3) = \frac\pi2 + \mu + \Bigl(\frac{15\pi}{32} - \frac12\Bigr)\mu^2 + O(\mu^3) ,
> $$
>
> so $\alpha = \Delta\varphi - \pi = 2\mu + (15\pi/16 - 1)\mu^2 + O(\mu^3)$. With $\mu = 2M/r_0$: $2\mu = 4M/r_0$ and $(15\pi/16 - 1)\cdot4M^2/r_0^2 = (15\pi/4 - 4)M^2/r_0^2$. First half of part 3.
>
> **Step 8** (change to $b$). Write $q = M/r_0$ and $p = M/b$. From Step 2, $p = q\sqrt{1 - 2q} = q(1 - q - \tfrac12q^2 + \dots) = q - q^2 + O(q^3)$. Invert by trying $q = p + cp^2$: $p = (p + cp^2) - p^2 + O(p^3)$ requires $c = 1$, so $q = p + p^2 + O(p^3)$. Insert in Step 7:
>
> $$
> \alpha = 4(p + p^2) + \Bigl(\frac{15\pi}4 - 4\Bigr)p^2 + O(p^3) = 4p + \frac{15\pi}{4}\,p^2 + O(p^3) .
> $$
>
> Second half of part 3. Also $b/r_0 = (1 - 2q)^{-1/2} = 1 + q + \tfrac32q^2 + \dots$, i.e. $b = r_0 + M + \tfrac32M^2/r_0 + \dots$: part 4.
>
> **Step 9** (check). For $b = 1000M$ the exact integral gives $\alpha = 4.0118\times10^{-3}$, against $4M/b = 4.0000\times10^{-3}$ and $4M/b + (15\pi/4)(M/b)^2 = 4.0118\times10^{-3}$ (checked by script, which also confirms both forms of part 3 numerically).
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Factoring $(1 - x^2)$ out of the radicand separates the harmless endpoint singularity, present already for a straight line, from the gravitational correction $1 - \mu g$; the correction is then a bounded function and can be expanded term by term. The same factorization shows why the photon sphere is the limit: there the correction itself vanishes at the turning point.

## The measurements

The bending of light was the first effect of general relativity predicted before it was observed (Schutz). The prediction is $1.75''$ at the solar limb, against the Newtonian $0.876''$, so a measurement to well within a factor $2$ decides between them.

> [!law] Law §C6.5.5: Eclipse and Radio Measurements of the Deflection
> The deflection of light and radio waves by the Sun agrees with $\alpha = 4GM_\odot/(c^2b)$, $1.75''$ at the limb ([[#^thm-c6-5-2|Theorem §C6.5.2]]), and excludes the Newtonian value $2GM_\odot/(c^2b)$:
> 1. **Eclipses (optical).** Stars near the eclipsed Sun are photographed and compared with plates of the same field taken months later at night; the images are displaced outward, away from the Sun, by an amount falling off as $1/b$. First measured by the British expeditions of Eddington and Dyson in 1919. The early results scattered: over ten eclipse campaigns gave between $0.7$ and $1.55$ times the prediction (d'Inverno), the best of them agreeing to about $5\%$ (Hartle). The 2017 eclipse measurement agreed with an error below $3\%$ (d'Inverno).
> 2. **Radio interferometry.** Radio sources (quasars) can be observed close to the Sun at any time. Fomalont and Sramek (NRAO, 1974–1975), with a 35 km baseline at 2.7 and 8.1 GHz, found the ratio of measured to predicted deflection $\tfrac12(1 + \gamma)$ with $\gamma = 1.007 \pm 0.009$ (Hartle); very long baseline interferometry (Lebach et al. 1995) improved on this (Hartle, d'Inverno; the sources give no number).
> 3. **Astrometry.** The Hipparcos satellite measured the deflection over the whole sky, and its successor Gaia must correct every star position for it (Schutz).
>
> The measured quantity is $\gamma$, the coefficient of the spatial part of the weak-field metric: $\alpha = \tfrac12(1 + \gamma)\cdot 4GM/(c^2b)$ ([[#^thm-c6-5-3|Theorem §C6.5.3]], part 5; [[§C5.5 The Newtonian Limit and the Value of the Coupling#^ex-c5-5-1|Example §C5.5.1]]). General relativity has $\gamma = 1$; a Euclidean space would have $\gamma = 0$. The radar time delay measures the same $\gamma$, more precisely ([[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay#^law-c6-6-5|Law §C6.6.5]]).
>
> *Valid when:* the refraction of the solar corona, which depends on the frequency, is removed by observing at two frequencies (the gravitational deflection is the same at every frequency, [[#^thm-c6-5-2|Theorem §C6.5.2]], part 2); stars or sources at angular distance from the Sun such that $b$ is known; the Sun's mass and radius as measured. Status as reported in the sources (Hartle 2003, d'Inverno & Vickers 2nd ed. 2022, Schutz 3rd ed. 2022).
>
> *Source: Hartle §10.3, eqs. (10.8), (10.11)–(10.14), Figs. 10.2–10.6, pp. 223–228 (PDF 224–229) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.7, Fig. 16.11, pp. 305–306 (PDF 320–321); §16.9, eq. (16.81), p. 312 (PDF 327) · Schutz §11.1, pp. 309, 311 (PDF 327, 329) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2.2, pp. 127–128 (PDF 128–129)*

> [!caution] Caution: Couzens' radian value at the solar limb
> Couzens (GR1, §6.2.2, p. 127, PDF 128) takes $GM_\odot/c^2 \approx 1.48$ km and $R_\odot \approx 7\times10^5$ km and quotes the deflection as "$8.6\times10^{-5}$ radians or $1.8''$". With these inputs $4\cdot1.48/(7\times10^5) = 8.46\times10^{-6}$ rad $= 1.74''$: the radian figure is too large by a factor $10$, and the seconds of arc are rounded up. With current values, $8.49\times10^{-6}$ rad $= 1.751''$ ([[#^ex-c6-5-1|Example §C6.5.1]]; checked by script).

^cau-c6-5-1

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Gravitational lensing and Einstein rings
> Every mass deflects light passing it, and the deflection is the same for all frequencies, so a mass between an observer and a distant source acts as a lens without chromatic aberration (d'Inverno). Rays passing on opposite sides can reach the observer from two directions, giving two images of one source (Schutz Fig. 11.7); a source exactly behind a spherical lens is imaged as a ring, the Einstein ring. In 1980 two quasar images separated by $6''$, 0957+561 A and B, were found to be one quasar lensed by a foreground galaxy (d'Inverno); Hubble images of galaxy clusters show arcs and multiple images of background galaxies (Couzens Fig. 18, Schutz). The characteristic angle of the images, the Einstein angle, is about $10^{-3}{}''$ for a star lensing a star (microlensing, seen as a temporary brightening) and about $1''$ for a galaxy (Hartle). Because lensing depends only on mass, it weighs dark matter (d'Inverno). The lens equation and the Einstein angle are derived in Hartle §11.1 and are not part of these notes. How a lens focuses and shears a narrow beam, through the Ricci curvature inside matter and the Weyl curvature outside it, is worked out in [[§C10.6 Null Congruences and Raychaudhuri's Equation#^ex-c10-6-2|Example §C10.6.2]].
>
> *Source: Hartle §11.1, pp. 234–243 (PDF 235–244) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.7, Figs. 16.12–16.13, p. 306 (PDF 321) · Schutz §11.1, pp. 312–313 (PDF 330–331) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2.2, Fig. 18, p. 128 (PDF 129)*

^rem-c6-5-1

The deflection of light tests the spatial part of the metric that planets cannot feel. The same spatial factor also lengthens the path of a light signal and slows its coordinate speed ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]]): how much later does a radar echo from a planet behind the Sun return, and how does a clock deep in the field compare with one far away? [[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay|§C6.6]] answers both.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C6.5.1: The Sun's Limb: 1.75″
> *Question.* By how much is the light of a star that grazes the Sun deflected, how fast does the effect fall off away from the limb, and does the second-order term or the choice between $b$ and $r_0$ matter?
>
> *Step 1* (inputs). $M_\odot = GM_\odot/c^2 = 1476.7$ m and $R_\odot = 6.957\times10^8$ m ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^ex-c1-2-1|Example §C1.2.1]]), so $M_\odot/R_\odot = 2.1226\times10^{-6}$.
>
> *Step 2* (first order, $b = R_\odot$). By [[#^thm-c6-5-2|Theorem §C6.5.2]],
>
> $$
> \alpha = \frac{4M_\odot}{R_\odot} = 4\times2.1226\times10^{-6} = 8.490\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{rad} = 8.490\times10^{-6}\times206265'' = 1.751'' .
> $$
>
> The Newtonian corpuscle of the opening gives $2\arctan(M_\odot/R_\odot) = 0.876''$.
>
> *Step 3* (away from the limb). $\alpha \propto 1/b$: for $b = 2R_\odot$, $5R_\odot$, $10R_\odot$ the deflection is $0.876''$, $0.350''$, $0.175''$. The star appears displaced outward, away from the Sun, because the observer sees the light arriving along the outgoing asymptote, which is turned toward the Sun. (For an observer at the Earth's distance and a star near the limb the formula for a distant observer is accurate; far from the Sun on the sky the light reaches the Earth before the full deflection has accumulated: Schutz Fig. 11.6 and Exercise 11.17.)
>
> *Step 4* (second order). $(15\pi/4)(M_\odot/R_\odot)^2 = 11.78\times(2.1226\times10^{-6})^2 = 5.31\times10^{-11}$ rad $= 11\ \mu$as ([[#^thm-c6-5-4|Theorem §C6.5.4]]), a fraction $6\times10^{-6}$ of the first-order term.
>
> *Step 5* ($b$ or $r_0$). $b - r_0 = M_\odot + O(M_\odot^2/R_\odot) = 1.48$ km out of $6.96\times10^5$ km. Exchanging them changes $\alpha$ by $4M_\odot^2/R_\odot^2$, also of order $10^{-11}$ rad ([[#^thm-c6-5-4|Theorem §C6.5.4]], part 4).
>
> *Result.* $1.751''$ at the limb (checked by script; Hartle quotes $1.75''$, Schutz $1.74''$ with rounded constants). For every solar-system measurement, the first order is the whole answer.
>
> *Source: Schutz §11.1, eq. (11.56), p. 311 (PDF 329) · Hartle §10.3, eq. (10.11), p. 223 (PDF 224) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.7, after eq. (16.60), p. 305 (PDF 320) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C6.5.2: Jupiter
> *Question.* What is the deflection of a ray grazing Jupiter, the largest after the Sun's in the solar system?
>
> *Step 1* (inputs). Jupiter's $GM_J = 1.26687\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$ (standard value; $M_J/M_\odot = 9.546\times10^{-4}$), so $M_J = GM_J/c^2 = 1.26687\times10^{17}/8.98755\times10^{16}\ \mathrm m = 1.410$ m $= 1.41\times10^{-3}$ km. Equatorial radius $R_J = 71\,492$ km.
>
> *Step 2* (deflection). By [[#^thm-c6-5-2|Theorem §C6.5.2]] with $b = R_J$:
>
> $$
> \alpha = \frac{4M_J}{R_J} = \frac{4\times1.410\ \mathrm m}{7.1492\times10^7\ \mathrm m} = 7.89\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{rad} = 0.0163'' .
> $$
>
> *Step 3* (compare with the Sun). $(M_J/R_J)/(M_\odot/R_\odot) = 0.0093$: Jupiter is a thousand times lighter than the Sun but ten times smaller, so its limb deflection is about $1/108$ of the Sun's. Second order is negligible ($M_J/R_J = 2\times10^{-8}$).
>
> *Step 4* (Schutz's value). Schutz (11.57) gives $6.42\times10^{-8}$ rad $= 0.013''$ from $M = 1.12\times10^{-3}$ km and $R = 6.98\times10^4$ km. With the correct mass $1.41\times10^{-3}$ km and his radius, $\alpha = 8.08\times10^{-8}$ rad $= 0.0167''$; with the equatorial radius, $0.0163''$ ([[#^cau-c6-5-2|Caution: Schutz's mass of Jupiter]]).
>
> *Result.* About $0.016''$, measurable only by space astrometry ([[#^law-c6-5-5|Law §C6.5.5]], part 3).
>
> *Source: Schutz §11.1, eq. (11.57), p. 311 (PDF 329) (recomputed here) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C6.5.3: Light Near the Photon Sphere
> *Question.* What happens to the deflection when the ray passes a black hole closely, so that $M/b$ is not small? How close must it come to turn back, or to loop around the hole?
>
> *Step 1* (numerical integral). For each $b$, solve $b^2 = r_0^3/(r_0 - 2M)$ for $r_0 > 3M$ and evaluate the exact integral of [[#^thm-c6-5-4|Theorem §C6.5.4]], part 1, numerically (substituting $x = 1 - w^2$ removes the endpoint singularity: the integrand becomes $2/\sqrt{(1 + x) - \mu(1 + x + x^2)}$ in $w$). With $M = 1$ (checked by script):
>
> | $b/M$ | $r_0/M$ | exact $\alpha$ (rad) | $\alpha$ (deg) | $4M/b$ | $4M/b + (15\pi/4)(M/b)^2$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $100$ | $98.985$ | $0.0412$ | $2.36$ | $0.0400$ | $0.0412$ |
> | $20$ | $18.913$ | $0.2361$ | $13.53$ | $0.2000$ | $0.2295$ |
> | $10$ | $8.789$ | $0.5904$ | $33.83$ | $0.4000$ | $0.5178$ |
> | $7$ | $5.617$ | $1.1276$ | $64.61$ | $0.5714$ | $0.8119$ |
> | $6$ | $4.453$ | $1.7194$ | $98.51$ | $0.6667$ | $0.9939$ |
> | $5.5$ | $3.767$ | $2.5530$ | $146.28$ | $0.7273$ | $1.1167$ |
> | $5.3$ | $3.403$ | $3.5579$ | $203.85$ | $0.7547$ | $1.1741$ |
> | $5.2$ | $3.069$ | $6.8104$ | $390.21$ | $0.7692$ | $1.2049$ |
>
> Down to $b \approx 20M$ the second-order formula is good to a few per cent; below $b \approx 10M$ both expansions fail. At $b = 5.2M$, just above $b_c = 3\sqrt3\,M = 5.196M$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-5|Theorem §C6.3.5]]), the ray winds once around the hole before leaving ($\alpha > 2\pi$).
>
> *Step 2* (landmarks, by root finding on the same integral). $\alpha = \pi/2$ (the ray leaves at right angles) at $b = 6.168M$, $r_0 = 4.660M$; $\alpha = \pi$ (the light is sent back toward its source) at $b = 5.357M$, $r_0 = 3.521M$; $\alpha = 2\pi$ (one full loop) at $b = 5.2027M$, $r_0 = 3.090M$; $\alpha = 4\pi$ at $b = 5.19616M$, $r_0 = 3.0037M$.
>
> *Step 3* (why the angle diverges like a logarithm). Write $F(u) = u^2(1 - 2Mu)$, so $u'^2 = 1/b^2 - F(u)$. At $u_c = 1/(3M)$, the photon sphere,
>
> $$
> F(u_c) = \frac{1}{9M^2}\Bigl(1 - \frac23\Bigr) = \frac{1}{27M^2} = \frac{1}{b_c^2}, \qquad F'(u_c) = 2u_c - 6Mu_c^2 = \frac{2}{3M} - \frac{2}{3M} = 0, \qquad F''(u_c) = 2 - 12Mu_c = -2 .
> $$
>
> With $v = u - u_c$, Taylor's theorem gives $F = 1/b_c^2 - v^2 + O(v^3)$, and with $\kappa^2 = 1/b_c^2 - 1/b^2 > 0$ for $b > b_c$,
>
> $$
> v'^2 = v^2 - \kappa^2 + O(v^3) .
> $$
>
> The solution through the turning point $v = -\kappa$ (just outside the photon sphere) is $v = -\kappa\cosh(\varphi - \varphi_0)$, since $v'^2 = \kappa^2\sinh^2 = \kappa^2(\cosh^2 - 1) = v^2 - \kappa^2$. It stays within a fixed small band $|v| \le v_1$ for the angle $2\operatorname{arccosh}(v_1/\kappa) = 2\ln(2v_1/\kappa) + O(\kappa^2/v_1^2)$, while the parts of the ray outside the band sweep an angle that tends to a finite limit as $b \to b_c$. With $\kappa^2 = (b^2 - b_c^2)/(b^2b_c^2) = (2/b_c^2)(b/b_c - 1) + O\bigl((b/b_c - 1)^2\bigr)$, $2\ln(1/\kappa) = -\ln(b/b_c - 1) + \text{const}$, so
>
> $$
> \alpha = -\ln\Bigl(\frac{b}{b_c} - 1\Bigr) + C + o(1), \qquad C = -0.4002 \ \ (\text{numerically}) .
> $$
>
> The numerical integral confirms the coefficient $1$: $\alpha + \ln(b/b_c - 1) = -0.3999$, $-0.4002$, $-0.4002$ for $b/b_c - 1 = 10^{-4}$, $10^{-6}$, $10^{-8}$ (checked by script).
>
> *Step 4* (loops). Each further loop adds $2\pi$ to $\alpha$, so by Step 3 it needs $b/b_c - 1$ smaller by $e^{2\pi} = 535$. From Step 2: $1.256\times10^{-3}$ for one loop, $2.34\times10^{-6}$ for two, a ratio $537$. Rays that circle the hole are a very thin ring of impact parameters just outside $b_c$; each loop number gives a fainter image of the sky, squeezed toward the edge of the dark disc of captured rays.
>
> *Result.* The deflection grows without bound as $b \to 3\sqrt3\,M$; Hartle's Fig. 9.11 plots the same curve. Only a black hole, or a body more compact than $r = 3M$, can produce these large deflections: for any other body the ray hits the surface first.
>
> *Source: Hartle §9.4, eq. (9.78), Fig. 9.11, p. 211 (PDF 212) (the curve plotted qualitatively) · the numbers, the log law and its coefficient worked here; checked by script*
