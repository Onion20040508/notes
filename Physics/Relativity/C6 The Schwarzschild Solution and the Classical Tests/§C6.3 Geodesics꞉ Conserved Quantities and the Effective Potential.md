The exterior of a spherical body is the Schwarzschild metric ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]). How do planets, infalling gas and light move in it? Newton's answer is a chapter of classical mechanics. With the conserved angular momentum the radial motion becomes one-dimensional motion in an effective potential ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-3|CM Def. §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|CM Theorem §B2.1.4]]); every bound orbit is a closed ellipse ([[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-4|CM Theorem §B2.3.4]]); and the centrifugal barrier $L^2/2r^2$ keeps every particle with $L \neq 0$ away from the centre. General relativity replaces the force by geodesics of the metric ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]).

First, what fails when the Newtonian picture is carried over. Drop a particle from rest far away (energy per unit mass $E_{\mathrm N} = 0$) with angular momentum per unit mass $L = 3.9M$. Newton's energy equation $\tfrac12\dot r^2 - M/r + L^2/2r^2 = 0$ has its turning point where $\dot r = 0$:

$$
-\frac Mr + \frac{L^2}{2r^2} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad r_{\text{turn}} = \frac{L^2}{2M} = \frac{(3.9)^2}{2}\,M = 7.605\,M ,
$$

and the particle swings back out on a parabola. In the Schwarzschild metric the radial component of the geodesic equation ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]) in the plane $\theta = \pi/2$, with the Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^r_{tt} = (1 - 2M/r)M/r^2$, $\Gamma^r_{rr} = -M/[r^2(1 - 2M/r)]$, $\Gamma^r_{\varphi\varphi} = -r(1 - 2M/r)$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-1|Theorem §C6.2.1]] with $e^{2A} = e^{-2B} = 1 - 2M/r$), reads

$$
\ddot r = -\Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)\frac{M}{r^2}\,\dot t^2 + \frac{M}{r^2(1 - 2M/r)}\,\dot r^2 + r\Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)\dot\varphi^2 .
$$

It is coupled to the equations for $t$ and $\varphi$, and nothing in it looks like Newton's barrier. Integrated numerically for the same particle, it shows no turning point at all: the particle reaches $r = 2M$. The Newtonian barrier has failed. Two things are needed: a way to reduce four coupled second-order equations to one, and a potential that shows at a glance what the orbits do.

The section builds both. The two Killing vectors $\partial_t$ and $\partial_\varphi$ give a conserved energy and angular momentum ([[#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]). With them and the normalization of the four-velocity the radial motion is one-dimensional motion in an effective potential ([[#^def-c6-3-1|Def. §C6.3.1]], [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]). The potential differs from Newton's by one term, $-2ML^2/r^3$, which is what captured the particle above ([[#^thm-c6-3-3|Theorem §C6.3.3]]). Its extrema give the circular orbits: an innermost stable one at $r = 6M$, a marginally bound one at $4M$, and a binding energy of $5.72\%$ of the rest energy at the innermost one ([[#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]]). For light the same construction gives the photon sphere at $3M$ and the capture of every ray with impact parameter below $3\sqrt3\,M$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-5|Theorem §C6.3.5]]). Last, the shape of an orbit obeys Newton's orbit equation plus a term $3Mu^2$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-6|Theorem §C6.3.6]]), the starting point of the perihelion precession and the bending of light.

**Setup.** A test body (negligible self-gravity and size, no spin, no non-gravitational force) or a light ray in the exterior Schwarzschild metric ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]),

$$
ds^2 = -\Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)dt^2 + \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)^{-1}dr^2 + r^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr) ,
$$

in Schwarzschild coordinates, $r$ the areal radius ([[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^def-c6-1-2|Def. §C6.1.2]]). The region is $r > 2M$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^def-c6-2-1|Def. §C6.2.1]]), and outside the surface $r > R$ if the source is a star. What happens at and inside $r = 2M$ is C7 (planned). Test bodies follow timelike geodesics, light null geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]), affinely parametrized: by proper time $\tau$ for a body, by an affine parameter $\lambda$, fixed up to $\lambda \to a\lambda + b$, for light. All results are exact consequences of the metric; no weak-field expansion is made unless stated. The numbers in the examples were checked by script.

## Two constants of the motion

The Schwarzschild metric has the Killing vectors $\partial_t$ and the three rotations ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^rem-c6-2-1|§C6.2, Remark: The Killing vectors of the Schwarzschild metric]]). Each Killing vector gives a quantity conserved along every geodesic ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-2|Theorem §C3.13.2]]). [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^ex-c3-13-3|Example §C3.13.3]] previewed the result for a general static, spherically symmetric metric; here it is stated for Schwarzschild, with its physical meaning.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.3.1: Conserved Energy and Angular Momentum
> Let $\lambda \mapsto x^\mu(\lambda)$ be an affinely parametrized geodesic of the Schwarzschild metric in $r > 2M$, timelike or null.
> 1. **Two constants.** The Killing vectors ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-2|Def. §C3.13.2]]) $\partial_t$ and $\partial_\varphi$ give the constants
>
> $$
> E \equiv -g(\partial_t, \dot x) = \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)\dot t, \qquad L \equiv g(\partial_\varphi, \dot x) = r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi .
> $$
>
> 2. **A plane.** The coordinates can be rotated so that the geodesic lies in the equatorial plane, $\theta \equiv \pi/2$; then $L = r^2\dot\varphi$. This choice is made from now on, with $L \ge 0$ (reverse $\varphi$ otherwise).
> 3. **Meaning for a body** ($\lambda = \tau$, four-momentum $p^\mu = m\dot x^\mu$). $mE = -p_t$ is the energy and $mL = p_\varphi$ the angular momentum, both per unit rest mass as measured at infinity. $E > 0$. Far away and for slow motion, $E = 1 - M/r + \tfrac12 v^2 + \dots$, the conserved energy of the weak field ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-5|Theorem §C1.5.5]]), and $L$ becomes Newton's angular momentum per unit mass $r^2\,d\varphi/dt$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-3|CM Theorem §B2.1.3]]).
> 4. **Meaning for light.** $E$ and $L$ are the photon's energy and angular momentum at infinity for the normalization $p^\mu = \dot x^\mu$. Rescaling $\lambda \to a\lambda$ divides both by $a$, so only their ratio $b \equiv L/E$ is a property of the ray.
>
> Restored: $\mathcal E = mc^2(1 - 2GM/rc^2)\,dt/d\tau$ and $\mathcal L = mr^2\,d\varphi/d\tau$ are conserved.
>
> *Model:* test body or light ray on a geodesic ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]) of the Schwarzschild metric; *applies:* exactly, for every geodesic in $r > 2M$; part 3's expansion for $M/r \ll 1$, $v \ll 1$.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.2, eqs. (6.32)–(6.34) and footnote 26, pp. 119–120 (PDF 120–121) · Hartle §9.3, eqs. (9.21)–(9.22), p. 193 (PDF 194) · Schutz §11.1, eqs. (11.5)–(11.6), p. 298 (PDF 316) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.6, eqs. (16.34)–(16.35), p. 299 (PDF 314) (signature $+{-}{-}{-}$; the same $h$ and $k$)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Killing vectors). No component of the metric depends on $t$ or $\varphi$, so $\partial_t$ and $\partial_\varphi$ are Killing vectors ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-1|Theorem §C3.13.1]], part 3).
>
> **Step 2** (lowered components). The metric is diagonal, so
>
> $$
> (\partial_t)_\mu = g_{\mu\nu}\delta^\nu_t = g_{\mu t} = -\Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)\delta^t_\mu, \qquad (\partial_\varphi)_\mu = g_{\mu\varphi} = r^2\sin^2\theta\,\delta^\varphi_\mu .
> $$
>
> Contracting with $\dot x^\mu$: $g(\partial_t, \dot x) = -(1 - 2M/r)\dot t$ and $g(\partial_\varphi, \dot x) = r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi$. Both are constant along an affinely parametrized geodesic by [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-2|Theorem §C3.13.2]], part 1. The minus sign in $E$ makes it positive (Step 5). This is part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (the equatorial plane is invariant). The geodesic Lagrangian ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-6|Theorem §C3.7.6]]) is
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = -\Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)\dot t^2 + \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)^{-1}\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2 .
> $$
>
> Its Euler–Lagrange equation for $\theta$ ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|CM Theorem §B5.1.4]]) is
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\lambda}\bigl(2r^2\dot\theta\bigr) - 2r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\varphi^2 = 0 .
> $$
>
> If $\theta = \pi/2$ and $\dot\theta = 0$ at one value of $\lambda$, then $\theta \equiv \pi/2$ solves this equation for all $\lambda$, because $\sin\theta\cos\theta = 0$ there and the first term vanishes. By uniqueness of solutions of the geodesic equation ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]) it is the solution.
>
> **Step 4** (every geodesic can be put there). At one event of the geodesic, its angular position and the angular part of its velocity, $(\dot\theta, \dot\varphi)$, determine a great circle of the orbit sphere (the one through that point in that direction; any great circle through the point if the angular velocity vanishes). A rotation, an isometry generated by the three rotational Killing vectors ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^ex-c3-13-2|Example §C3.13.2]]), carries this great circle to the equator. It maps geodesics to geodesics and leaves the form of the metric unchanged. After it, $\theta = \pi/2$ and $\dot\theta = 0$ at that event, and Step 3 applies. With $\sin\theta = 1$, $L = r^2\dot\varphi$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (meaning for a body). With $\lambda = \tau$ and $p_\mu = mg_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\nu$, $p_t = -m(1 - 2M/r)\dot t = -mE$ and $p_\varphi = mr^2\dot\varphi = mL$. Where $r \to \infty$ the metric becomes Minkowski and $-p_t$ is the energy measured by observers at rest there. For a future-pointing worldline $\dot t > 0$ (in $r > 2M$, $\partial_t$ is timelike and future-pointing, [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]), and $1 - 2M/r > 0$, so $E > 0$. Exactly, $1 - 2M/r = 1 + 2\Phi$ with $\Phi = -M/r$, so $E = (1 + 2\Phi)\,dt/d\tau$ is the expression of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-5|Theorem §C1.5.5]], which expands to $1 + \Phi + \tfrac12 v^2$ to first order in $M/r$ and $v^2$. For $L$: $d\varphi/d\tau = (d\varphi/dt)(dt/d\tau)$ and $dt/d\tau = 1 + O(M/r, v^2)$. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 6** (light). For light $p^\mu = \dot x^\mu$ with a chosen affine $\lambda$, so $E = -p_t$, $L = p_\varphi$. Under $\lambda = a\lambda'$, $dx^\mu/d\lambda' = a\,\dot x^\mu$ and $\lambda'$ is again affine ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-3|Theorem §C3.7.3]]); $E$ and $L$ computed with $\lambda'$ are both multiplied by $a$, and $L/E$ is unchanged. This is part 4.
>
> **Step 7** (restore units). With $t \to ct$, $\tau \to c\tau$, $M \to GM/c^2$: $E = (1 - 2GM/rc^2)\,dt/d\tau$ stays dimensionless, and $mc^2E$ is an energy; $L = r^2\,d\varphi/d(c\tau)$, and $mcL = mr^2\,d\varphi/d\tau$ is an angular momentum.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The two constants are the Noether charges of time translation and of rotation about the axis, read off as momenta conjugate to the cyclic coordinates $t$ and $\varphi$. The other two rotations give no new number to track, but they force the orbit into a plane, as in Newtonian mechanics.

## The effective potential

A geodesic has three first integrals now: $E$, $L$ and the norm $g(\dot x, \dot x) = -\varepsilon$ ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-7|Theorem §C3.7.7]]). The first two fix $\dot t$ and $\dot\varphi$ as functions of $r$. Inserted into the third, they leave an equation for $r$ alone, in which one function of $r$ plays the part of Newton's effective potential. That function is named first; the equation it enters is the theorem after it.

> [!definition] Definition §C6.3.1: Effective Potential
> *Structure.* For a geodesic of the Schwarzschild metric in the plane $\theta = \pi/2$ with angular momentum $L$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]) and causal type $\varepsilon$ ($\varepsilon = 1$ timelike, $\varepsilon = 0$ null), the **effective potential** is the function on the half-line $r > 2M$
>
> $$
> V(r) = \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)\Bigl(\varepsilon + \frac{L^2}{r^2}\Bigr), \qquad\text{for a body:}\qquad V(r) = 1 - \frac{2M}{r} + \frac{L^2}{r^2} - \frac{2ML^2}{r^3} .
> $$
>
> It is fixed by the normalization chosen here: the radial motion obeys $\dot r^2 + V(r) = E^2$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]), so $V$ plays the role of the potential energy and $E^2$ that of the total energy. Each value of $L$ has its own curve $V$. Both are dimensionless; $V(2M) = 0$ for every $L$, and $V \to \varepsilon$ as $r \to \infty$.
>
> *Layer change.* It is the relativistic counterpart of the Newtonian effective potential $U_{\text{eff}} = U + L^2/2\mu r^2$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-3|CM Def. §B2.1.3]]): per unit mass, with $U = -M/r$, $V = 1 + 2U_{\text{eff}} - 2ML^2/r^3$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-3|Theorem §C6.3.3]]).
>
> The sources normalize differently (Schutz as here; Couzens and d'Inverno use half of $V$; Hartle uses $(V - 1)/2$): [[#^cau-c6-3-1|Caution: Normalizations of the effective potential]].
>
> *Source: Schutz §11.1, eqs. (11.11)–(11.14), p. 299 (PDF 317) (the same normalization, $\tilde V^2$) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2, eq. (6.37), p. 120 (PDF 121) · Hartle §9.3, eq. (9.28), p. 194 (PDF 195) · d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 16.12, p. 317 (PDF 332)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.3.2: The Radial Equation
> Let a geodesic lie in the plane $\theta = \pi/2$, with constants $E$, $L$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]) and $g(\dot x, \dot x) = -\varepsilon$ (for a body $\varepsilon = 1$ and $\lambda = \tau$).
> 1. **First integral.**
>
> $$
> \dot r^2 + V(r) = E^2, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \Bigl(\frac{dr}{d\lambda}\Bigr)^2 = E^2 - \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)\Bigl(\varepsilon + \frac{L^2}{r^2}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
>    with $V$ of [[#^def-c6-3-1|Def. §C6.3.1]]. The motion is confined to $V(r) \le E^2$, and $\dot r = 0$ exactly at the **turning points** $V(r) = E^2$.
> 2. **Second-order form.** Along every such geodesic, circular ones included,
>
> $$
> \ddot r = -\tfrac12\,V'(r) = -\frac{\varepsilon M}{r^2} + \frac{L^2}{r^3} - \frac{3ML^2}{r^4} .
> $$
>
> 3. **The rest by quadrature.** Once $r(\lambda)$ is known, $\dot t = E/(1 - 2M/r)$ and $\dot\varphi = L/r^2$ give $t(\lambda)$ and $\varphi(\lambda)$.
> 4. **Newtonian form** (body). Subtracting $1$ and halving,
>
> $$
> \tfrac12\Bigl(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\Bigr)^2 + \Bigl(-\frac Mr + \frac{L^2}{2r^2} - \frac{ML^2}{r^3}\Bigr) = \frac{E^2 - 1}{2} :
> $$
>
>    the energy equation of one-dimensional motion ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|CM Theorem §B2.1.4]], per unit mass), with $\tau$ in place of $t$, energy $(E^2 - 1)/2$ and potential $(V - 1)/2$. So the turning-point and orbit classification of [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-5|CM Theorem §B2.1.5]] carries over, with $V$ in place of $U_{\text{eff}}$.
>
> Restored (body; $\mathcal E/m = c^2(1 - 2GM/rc^2)\,dt/d\tau$, $\mathcal L/m = r^2\,d\varphi/d\tau$):
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\Bigr)^2 = \frac{(\mathcal E/m)^2}{c^2} - \Bigl(1 - \frac{2GM}{c^2r}\Bigr)\Bigl(c^2 + \frac{(\mathcal L/m)^2}{r^2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Model:* test body or light ray on a geodesic of the Schwarzschild metric, $r > 2M$; *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.2, eqs. (6.35)–(6.36), (6.39)–(6.40), p. 120 (PDF 121) · Schutz §11.1, eqs. (11.9)–(11.12), (11.15)–(11.16), pp. 299–301 (PDF 317–319) · Hartle §9.3, eqs. (9.23)–(9.29), pp. 193–194 (PDF 194–195) · d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 16.12 (i), (iii), p. 317 (PDF 332)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Write $f \equiv 1 - 2M/r$, so $f' = df/dr = 2M/r^2$.
>
> **Step 1** (the norm in the plane). With $\theta = \pi/2$, $\dot\theta = 0$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]], part 2) the norm of the tangent is
>
> $$
> g(\dot x, \dot x) = -f\,\dot t^2 + f^{-1}\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\varphi^2 = -\varepsilon ,
> $$
>
> constant along the geodesic ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-7|Theorem §C3.7.7]]); $\varepsilon = 1$ is the normalization $\lambda = \tau$ (part 3 there), $\varepsilon = 0$ the null case.
>
> **Step 2** (insert the constants). From [[#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]], $\dot t = E/f$ and $\dot\varphi = L/r^2$:
>
> $$
> -f\,\frac{E^2}{f^2} + \frac{\dot r^2}{f} + r^2\,\frac{L^2}{r^4} = -\varepsilon \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad -\frac{E^2}{f} + \frac{\dot r^2}{f} + \frac{L^2}{r^2} = -\varepsilon .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (multiply by $f > 0$).
>
> $$
> -E^2 + \dot r^2 + f\,\frac{L^2}{r^2} = -\varepsilon f \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dot r^2 = E^2 - f\Bigl(\varepsilon + \frac{L^2}{r^2}\Bigr) = E^2 - V(r) .
> $$
>
> Since $\dot r^2 \ge 0$, $V(r) \le E^2$ along the motion, with equality exactly where $\dot r = 0$. This is part 1. Part 3 is Step 2 read backwards.
>
> **Step 4** (the radial Euler–Lagrange equation). The geodesic Lagrangian in the plane is $\mathcal L = -f\dot t^2 + f^{-1}\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\varphi^2$, whose Euler–Lagrange equations are the geodesic equations ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-6|Theorem §C3.7.6]], part 1). Its partial derivatives in $r$ and $\dot r$ are
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot r} = \frac{2\dot r}{f}, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial r} = -f'\dot t^2 - \frac{f'}{f^2}\,\dot r^2 + 2r\dot\varphi^2 = -\frac{2M}{r^2}\,\dot t^2 - \frac{2M}{r^2f^2}\,\dot r^2 + 2r\dot\varphi^2 ,
> $$
>
> and, by the product and chain rules, $\frac{d}{d\lambda}\frac{2\dot r}{f} = \frac{2\ddot r}{f} - \frac{2\dot r}{f^2}f'\dot r = \frac{2\ddot r}{f} - \frac{4M}{r^2f^2}\dot r^2$. The equation $\frac{d}{d\lambda}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot r} - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial r} = 0$ is therefore
>
> $$
> \frac{2\ddot r}{f} - \frac{4M}{r^2f^2}\,\dot r^2 + \frac{2M}{r^2}\,\dot t^2 + \frac{2M}{r^2f^2}\,\dot r^2 - 2r\dot\varphi^2 = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ddot r = \frac{M}{r^2f}\,\dot r^2 - \frac{fM}{r^2}\,\dot t^2 + fr\,\dot\varphi^2 ,
> $$
>
> after multiplying by $f/2$: the equation of the opening, written with the Christoffel symbols.
>
> **Step 5** (insert the constants and the first integral). With $\dot t^2 = E^2/f^2$ and $\dot\varphi^2 = L^2/r^4$,
>
> $$
> \ddot r = \frac{M}{r^2f}\bigl(\dot r^2 - E^2\bigr) + \frac{fL^2}{r^3} .
> $$
>
> Part 1 gives $\dot r^2 - E^2 = -V = -f(\varepsilon + L^2/r^2)$, an identity along the motion that involves no division by $\dot r$:
>
> $$
> \ddot r = -\frac{M}{r^2}\Bigl(\varepsilon + \frac{L^2}{r^2}\Bigr) + \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)\frac{L^2}{r^3} = -\frac{\varepsilon M}{r^2} - \frac{ML^2}{r^4} + \frac{L^2}{r^3} - \frac{2ML^2}{r^4} = -\frac{\varepsilon M}{r^2} + \frac{L^2}{r^3} - \frac{3ML^2}{r^4} .
> $$
>
> **Step 6** (the same as $-\tfrac12V'$). Differentiating $V = \varepsilon - 2\varepsilon M/r + L^2/r^2 - 2ML^2/r^3$ term by term,
>
> $$
> V'(r) = \frac{2\varepsilon M}{r^2} - \frac{2L^2}{r^3} + \frac{6ML^2}{r^4}, \qquad -\tfrac12V'(r) = -\frac{\varepsilon M}{r^2} + \frac{L^2}{r^3} - \frac{3ML^2}{r^4} ,
> $$
>
> which is Step 5. This is part 2. (Differentiating part 1 in $\lambda$ gives $2\dot r\ddot r = -V'\dot r$, the same equation only where $\dot r \neq 0$; Steps 4–5 avoid that division, so circular orbits are covered.)
>
> **Step 7** (Newtonian form). For $\varepsilon = 1$ subtract $1$ from both sides of part 1 and divide by $2$: $\tfrac12\dot r^2 + \tfrac12(V - 1) = \tfrac12(E^2 - 1)$, with $\tfrac12(V - 1) = -M/r + L^2/2r^2 - ML^2/r^3$. This is part 4. The proof of [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-5|CM Theorem §B2.1.5]] used only such an energy equation and the uniqueness of its solutions, so it carries over.
>
> **Step 8** (restore units). Geometric $E$ is $\mathcal E/(mc^2)$, geometric $L$ is $\mathcal L/(mc)$, $\dot r = dr/d(c\tau)$, $M \to GM/c^2$. Part 1 becomes $c^{-2}(dr/d\tau)^2 = (\mathcal E/mc^2)^2 - (1 - 2GM/c^2r)\bigl(1 + (\mathcal L/m)^2/(c^2r^2)\bigr)$; multiplying by $c^2$ gives the restored form.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** No equation of motion had to be solved: two Killing constants and the norm already fix $\dot t$, $\dot\varphi$ and $\dot r^2$ as functions of $r$. The radial geodesic equation is then not new information but the derivative of the first integral; written as $\ddot r = -\tfrac12V'$ it is "acceleration equals minus the gradient of the potential", which is why the potential picture is exact here and not an analogy.

Theorem §C6.3.2, part 4, puts the relativistic radial equation into Newton's form with a modified potential. The modification is one term, and that term decides everything that is new in this chapter.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.3.3: ⚑ The Relativistic Correction −2ML²/r³
> For a body ($\varepsilon = 1$), with the Newtonian effective potential per unit mass $U_{\text{eff}} = -M/r + L^2/2r^2$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-3|CM Def. §B2.1.3]]),
>
> $$
> V(r) = \underbrace{1}_{\text{rest energy}}\ \underbrace{-\ \frac{2M}{r}}_{\text{Newtonian potential}}\ \underbrace{+\ \frac{L^2}{r^2}}_{\text{centrifugal}}\ \underbrace{-\ \frac{2ML^2}{r^3}}_{\text{new}} = 1 + 2U_{\text{eff}}(r) - \frac{2ML^2}{r^3} .
> $$
>
> 1. **Origin.** The new term is the product of the $-2M/r$ in $g_{tt}$ (through $f = 1 - 2M/r$) with the centrifugal term. It is attractive and exists only for $L \neq 0$: a moving body is pulled harder than a resting one.
> 2. **In the equation of motion.** $\ddot r = -M/r^2 + L^2/r^3 - 3ML^2/r^4$: Newton's radial equation ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|CM Theorem §B2.1.4]]) plus an attraction $-3ML^2/r^4$.
> 3. **Size.** Relative to the centrifugal term the new term is $2M/r$ in $V$ and $3M/r$ in $\ddot r$; relative to the Newtonian term it is $L^2/r^2$ in $V$. For Mercury $2M/r = 5.1\times10^{-8}$. At $r = 3M$ the new attraction cancels the centrifugal repulsion exactly for every $L$; inside $3M$ it wins.
> 4. **The barrier is finite.** Newton's barrier $L^2/r^2$ grows without bound as $r \to 0$; with the new term $V(2M) = 0$ for every $L$. A body that comes close enough is captured whatever its angular momentum.
>
> Two further differences are not in $V$: $\dot r$ is $dr/d\tau$, not $dr/dt$, and $r$ is the areal radius, not a distance from the centre ([[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^cau-c6-1-1|§C6.1, Caution: Areal radius is not proper distance from the centre]]).
>
> Restored: $V = c^2 - 2GM/r + L^2/r^2 - 2GML^2/(c^2r^3)$ (with $L$ per unit mass, in $\mathrm{m^2/s}$); the ratio of the new to the centrifugal term is $2GM/(c^2r) = r_S/r$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^def-c6-2-1|Def. §C6.2.1]]).
>
> *Model:* as in [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]; *applies:* exactly; the comparison with Newton term by term.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.2, eqs. (6.37)–(6.38) and the paragraph after, p. 120 (PDF 121) · Hartle §9.3, eqs. (9.28), (9.30), (9.32), Fig. 9.2, pp. 194–195 (PDF 195–196) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.6, p. 300 (PDF 315) (ratio of the two terms of the orbit equation)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (expand). $V = (1 - 2M/r)(1 + L^2/r^2) = 1 + L^2/r^2 - 2M/r - 2ML^2/r^3$, four products of the two binomials.
>
> **Step 2** (compare). $1 + 2U_{\text{eff}} = 1 - 2M/r + L^2/r^2$ reproduces the first three terms; the fourth, $-2M/r \times L^2/r^2$, is left over. This is the displayed identity and part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (equation of motion). By [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], part 2, with $\varepsilon = 1$: $\ddot r = -M/r^2 + L^2/r^3 - 3ML^2/r^4$. The first two terms are $-U_{\text{eff}}'$, the Newtonian radial acceleration; the third is $-\tfrac12 \frac{d}{dr}(-2ML^2/r^3) = -3ML^2/r^4$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (ratios). In $V$: $(2ML^2/r^3)/(L^2/r^2) = 2M/r$ and $(2ML^2/r^3)/(2M/r) = L^2/r^2$. In $\ddot r$: $(3ML^2/r^4)/(L^2/r^3) = 3M/r$, which equals $1$ at $r = 3M$ and exceeds $1$ for $r < 3M$, independently of $L$. For Mercury, $r \approx a = 5.79\times10^{10}\ \mathrm m$ and $GM_\odot/c^2 = 1477\ \mathrm m$, so $2GM_\odot/(c^2a) = 5.1\times10^{-8}$ (script). This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (finite barrier). At $r = 2M$ the factor $1 - 2M/r$ vanishes, so $V(2M) = 0 \le E^2$ for every $L$ and every $E$; no turning point can sit at $r = 2M$. For large $r$ the centrifugal term dominates the new one (Step 4), so $V$ rises from $0$, and if it has a maximum, the maximum is finite. This is part 4; the heights are computed in [[#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]].
>
> **Step 6** (restore). $V$ multiplies $c^2$ in the restored radial equation of [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]: $c^2(1 - 2GM/c^2r)(1 + L^2/c^2r^2)$ expands to the four restored terms.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The whole difference from Newton in the radial motion is one product term. Far out it is a tiny correction, $r_S/r$ of the centrifugal term; near $r = 3M$ it is as large as the centrifugal term. This is why the classical tests in the solar system are small effects (§C6.4–§C6.6), while near a black hole the orbits are qualitatively different.

## Circular orbits and the innermost stable orbit

In Newton's theory every $L > 0$ has exactly one circular orbit, at the minimum of $U_{\text{eff}}$, and it is stable ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-5|CM Theorem §B2.1.5]], part 3; [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-1|CM Theorem §B2.3.1]]). The new term turns the infinite barrier into a hump, so $V$ can have a maximum as well as a minimum, or neither.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.3.4: Circular Orbits; the ISCO at 6M; the Marginally Bound Orbit at 4M; Binding Energy 5.72%
> For a body ($\varepsilon = 1$) in the Schwarzschild metric:
> 1. **Condition.** A circular orbit $r \equiv r_c$ exists exactly when $V'(r_c) = 0$, with $E^2 = V(r_c)$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]). For given $L$ the candidates are
>
> $$
> r_\pm = \frac{L^2}{2M}\Bigl(1 \pm \sqrt{1 - \frac{12M^2}{L^2}}\Bigr) :
> $$
>
>    two circular orbits if $L^2 > 12M^2$, one if $L^2 = 12M^2$, none if $L^2 < 12M^2$.
> 2. **By radius.** A circular orbit of radius $r$ has
>
> $$
> L^2 = \frac{Mr^2}{r - 3M}, \qquad E^2 = \frac{(1 - 2M/r)^2}{1 - 3M/r}, \qquad \Omega^2 \equiv \Bigl(\frac{d\varphi}{dt}\Bigr)^2 = \frac{M}{r^3} ,
> $$
>
>    so circular orbits of a body exist exactly for $r > 3M$, and Kepler's third law holds in the coordinates $t$ and $r$.
> 3. **Stability.** On a circular orbit $V''(r) = 2M(r - 6M)/[r^3(r - 3M)]$: the orbit at $r_+ \ge 6M$ is a minimum of $V$ (stable), the one at $3M < r_- \le 6M$ a maximum (unstable).
> 4. **Innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO).** At $L = 2\sqrt3\,M$ the two merge at $r = 6M$, with $E = 2\sqrt2/3$. No stable circular orbit lies inside $6M$.
> 5. **Marginally bound orbit.** $E = 1$ on the circular orbit at $r = 4M$ ($L = 4M$). Circular orbits with $3M < r < 4M$ have $E > 1$. A body falling from rest at infinity ($E = 1$) is captured if $L < 4M$ and turned back if $L > 4M$.
> 6. **Binding energy at the ISCO.** $1 - E = 1 - \sqrt{8/9} = 5.72\%$ of the rest energy.
> 7. **⚑ Nearly circular orbits precess.** A small radial oscillation about the stable orbit at $r$ returns to the same $r$ after the angle (apsidal angle, [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^def-b2-3-1|CM Def. §B2.3.1]])
>
> $$
> \Phi = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{1 - 6M/r}} > \pi ,
> $$
>
>    not Newton's $\pi$; $\Phi \to \infty$ at the ISCO.
>
> Restored: $r_{\text{ISCO}} = 6GM/c^2$, $L_{\text{ISCO}} = 2\sqrt3\,GM/c$ per unit mass, marginally bound radius $4GM/c^2$, binding energy $0.0572\,mc^2$, $\Omega^2 = GM/r^3$, $\Phi = \pi(1 - 6GM/c^2r)^{-1/2}$.
>
> *Model:* as in [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], body; *applies:* exactly; part 7 to first order in the amplitude of the radial oscillation.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.2, eqs. (6.41)–(6.46), pp. 121–122 (PDF 122–123) (with the discriminant corrected, [[#^cau-c6-3-1|Caution: Normalizations of the effective potential]]) · Hartle §9.3, eqs. (9.34), (9.43)–(9.46), pp. 195–200 (PDF 196–201); Box 13.1, eq. (d), p. 289 (PDF 290) ("6%") · Schutz §11.1, eqs. (11.17)–(11.25), pp. 301–303 (PDF 319–321) · d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 16.12 (ii)–(iii), p. 317 (PDF 332)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (condition). $r \equiv r_c$ means $\dot r \equiv 0$ and $\ddot r \equiv 0$. By [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], part 2, $\ddot r = -\tfrac12V'(r_c) = 0$, and by part 1, $E^2 = V(r_c)$. Conversely, starting at a zero of $V'$ with $\dot r = 0$, $r \equiv r_c$ solves part 2, and by uniqueness it is the motion.
>
> **Step 2** (the roots for given $L$). From [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], Step 6, with $\varepsilon = 1$,
>
> $$
> V'(r) = \frac{2M}{r^2} - \frac{2L^2}{r^3} + \frac{6ML^2}{r^4} = \frac{2}{r^4}\bigl(Mr^2 - L^2r + 3ML^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The quadratic $Mr^2 - L^2r + 3ML^2$ has discriminant $L^4 - 12M^2L^2$ and roots $r_\pm = \bigl(L^2 \pm \sqrt{L^4 - 12M^2L^2}\bigr)/2M$, real exactly when $L^2 \ge 12M^2$: part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (by radius). Solve $Mr^2 - L^2r + 3ML^2 = 0$ for $L^2$: $L^2(r - 3M) = Mr^2$, so $L^2 = Mr^2/(r - 3M)$, positive exactly for $r > 3M$. Then
>
> $$
> E^2 = V(r) = \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)\Bigl(1 + \frac{M}{r - 3M}\Bigr) = \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)\frac{r - 2M}{r - 3M} = \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)^2\frac{r}{r - 3M} = \frac{(1 - 2M/r)^2}{1 - 3M/r} .
> $$
>
> With $\dot\varphi = L/r^2$ and $\dot t = E/(1 - 2M/r)$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]),
>
> $$
> \Omega^2 = \frac{\dot\varphi^2}{\dot t^2} = \frac{L^2}{r^4}\,\frac{(1 - 2M/r)^2}{E^2} = \frac{M}{r^2(r - 3M)}\Bigl(1 - \frac{3M}{r}\Bigr) = \frac{M}{r^2(r - 3M)}\cdot\frac{r - 3M}{r} = \frac{M}{r^3} .
> $$
>
> This is part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (second derivative). Differentiating Step 2 once more, $V'' = -4M/r^3 + 6L^2/r^4 - 24ML^2/r^5$. With $L^2 = Mr^2/(r - 3M)$ and the common denominator $r^3(r - 3M)$,
>
> $$
> V'' = \frac{-4M(r - 3M) + 6Mr - 24M^2}{r^3(r - 3M)} = \frac{-4Mr + 12M^2 + 6Mr - 24M^2}{r^3(r - 3M)} = \frac{2M(r - 6M)}{r^3(r - 3M)} .
> $$
>
> For $r > 3M$ the sign is that of $r - 6M$. A minimum of $V$ is stable and a maximum unstable, as for any one-dimensional motion ([[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-1|CM Theorem §B2.3.1]], with $\tfrac12V$ in place of $U_{\text{eff}}$). Since $r_+r_- = 3L^2$ and $r_+ + r_- = L^2/M$, the larger root has $r_+ \ge 6M$ and the smaller $r_- \le 6M$; as $L \to \infty$, $r_- = 6M/\bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 - 12M^2/L^2}\bigr) \to 3M$. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (ISCO). At $L^2 = 12M^2$ the discriminant vanishes and $r_+ = r_- = L^2/2M = 6M$. There $E^2 = (1 - \tfrac13)^2/(1 - \tfrac12) = \tfrac49\cdot 2 = \tfrac89$, so $E = 2\sqrt2/3 = 0.94281$. Every stable circular orbit has $r = r_+ \ge 6M$. This is part 4.
>
> **Step 6** (marginally bound). $E^2 = 1$ on a circular orbit means $(1 - 2M/r)^2 = 1 - 3M/r$, i.e. $1 - 4M/r + 4M^2/r^2 = 1 - 3M/r$, i.e. $\frac Mr\bigl(\frac{4M}{r} - 1\bigr) = 0$: $r = 4M$, and $L^2 = M(4M)^2/M = 16M^2$. More generally
>
> $$
> E^2 - 1 = \frac{(1 - 2M/r)^2 - (1 - 3M/r)}{1 - 3M/r} = \frac{(M/r)(4M/r - 1)}{1 - 3M/r} ,
> $$
>
> positive exactly for $3M < r < 4M$. For capture: the barrier top is $V(r_-) = E^2(r_-)$, and $r_- = 6M/\bigl(1 + \sqrt{1 - 12M^2/L^2}\bigr)$ decreases from $6M$ to $3M$ as $L$ grows, passing $4M$ at $L = 4M$ (where $\sqrt{1 - 12/16} = \tfrac12$). So $V(r_-) > 1$ exactly when $r_- < 4M$, i.e. $L > 4M$, and then a body with $E = 1$ turns back outside $r_-$. For $2\sqrt3\,M \le L < 4M$ the top is below $1$, and for $L < 2\sqrt3\,M$ there is no maximum and $V < 1$ everywhere (it increases monotonically to $1$); in both cases $\dot r^2 = 1 - V > 0$ all the way to $r = 2M$. This is part 5.
>
> **Step 7** (binding energy). A body that spirals slowly from rest at infinity ($E = 1$) to the ISCO loses $1 - 2\sqrt2/3 = 0.0572$ of its rest energy (script). This is part 6.
>
> **Step 8** (nearly circular orbits). Put $r = r_c + \delta$ with small $\delta$ and the same $L$. Expanding part 2 of [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], $\ddot\delta = -\tfrac12V'(r_c) - \tfrac12V''(r_c)\delta + O(\delta^2) = -\omega_r^2\delta$ with $\omega_r^2 = \tfrac12V''(r_c) = M(r_c - 6M)/[r_c^3(r_c - 3M)]$. The radius returns from a minimum to the next maximum in the proper time $\pi/\omega_r$, while $\varphi$ advances at the rate $\dot\varphi = L/r_c^2$, with $\dot\varphi^2 = M/[r_c^2(r_c - 3M)]$ (Step 3). Hence
>
> $$
> \Phi^2 = \frac{\pi^2\dot\varphi^2}{\omega_r^2} = \pi^2\,\frac{M}{r_c^2(r_c - 3M)}\cdot\frac{r_c^3(r_c - 3M)}{M(r_c - 6M)} = \frac{\pi^2r_c}{r_c - 6M} ,
> $$
>
> which is part 7 (the same construction as [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-3|CM Theorem §B2.3.3]]). For $r \gg M$, $\Phi = \pi(1 + 3M/r + \dots)$, an advance of $2\Phi - 2\pi = 6\pi M/r$ per orbit, the circular limit of the perihelion advance of [[§C6.4 Perihelion Precession#^thm-c6-4-2|Theorem §C6.4.2]].
>
> **Step 9** (restore). $M \to GM/c^2$ in every radius; $L = \mathcal L/(mc)$ gives $\mathcal L/m = 2\sqrt3\,GM/c$; $\Omega = d\varphi/dt$ with $t \to ct$ gives $\Omega^2 = GM/r^3$; $1 - E$ is the fraction of $mc^2$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The ISCO is where the minimum and the maximum of $V$ merge, and it is the same place where the radial oscillation frequency $\omega_r$ of nearly circular orbits drops to zero. Between $3M$ and $6M$ circular orbits exist but are maxima of $V$, and below $4M$ they are not even bound. The two radii $6M$ and $4M$ are properties of the geometry alone; they do not depend on the body.

## Light: the photon sphere and capture

For light $\varepsilon = 0$, and only the ratio $b = L/E$ is a property of the ray ([[#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]], part 4). Dividing the radial equation by $L^2$ removes the arbitrary scale.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.3.5: Light: the Photon Sphere at 3M, the Critical Impact Parameter 3√3M, Capture
> For a null geodesic in the plane $\theta = \pi/2$ with $L > 0$ and $b \equiv L/E$:
> 1. **Radial equation.**
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{L^2}\Bigl(\frac{dr}{d\lambda}\Bigr)^2 + W(r) = \frac1{b^2}, \qquad W(r) \equiv \frac{V}{L^2} = \frac{1}{r^2}\Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 2. **Impact parameter.** For a ray that comes from infinity, $b$ is the distance of its incoming straight line from the parallel line through the centre.
> 3. **Photon sphere.** $W$ has a single maximum, $W(3M) = 1/(27M^2)$. Light can circle at $r = 3M$ (with $b = 3\sqrt3\,M$), and these orbits are unstable. There are no other circular null orbits.
> 4. **Capture.** A ray from infinity with $b > b_c \equiv 3\sqrt3\,M$ reaches a closest approach $r_0 > 3M$, given by
>
> $$
> b = \frac{r_0}{\sqrt{1 - 2M/r_0}} ,
> $$
>
>    and returns to infinity. A ray with $b < b_c$ has no turning point and crosses $r = 2M$. A ray with $b = b_c$ winds onto the photon sphere without reaching it.
> 5. **⚑ Capture cross-section.** A parallel beam loses to the hole every ray within $b_c$ of the axis through the centre: $\sigma_{\text{capt}} = \pi b_c^2 = 27\pi M^2$, $6.75$ times the area $\pi(2M)^2$ of a disc of radius $2M$.
>
> Restored: photon sphere $r = 3GM/c^2$, $b_c = 3\sqrt3\,GM/c^2$, $\sigma_{\text{capt}} = 27\pi G^2M^2/c^4$.
>
> *Model:* light ray on a null geodesic ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]) of the Schwarzschild metric, $r > 2M$; *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Source: Hartle §9.4, eqs. (9.60)–(9.70), Figs. 9.7–9.8, pp. 205–207 (PDF 206–208) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2, eq. (6.43) and p. 122 (PDF 123) (photon sphere) · Schutz §11.1, eqs. (11.12), (11.14), (11.18), Fig. 11.2, pp. 299–302 (PDF 317–320) · d'Inverno & Vickers, Exercise 16.15, p. 317 (PDF 332)*

^thm-c6-3-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (radial equation). [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], part 1, with $\varepsilon = 0$: $\dot r^2 = E^2 - (1 - 2M/r)L^2/r^2$. Dividing by $L^2 > 0$ gives $\dot r^2/L^2 + W = E^2/L^2 = 1/b^2$. Under $\lambda \to a\lambda$, $\dot r$ and $L$ both scale by $1/a$, so $\dot r/L$ and $b$ are unchanged. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (impact parameter). As $r \to \infty$, $1 - 2M/r \to 1$ and the metric becomes Minkowski in polar coordinates; the ray is a straight line traversed at unit speed. With $\dot t = E/(1 - 2M/r) \to E$, $b = L/E \to r^2\dot\varphi/\dot t = r^2\,d\varphi/dt$. For the line $y = d$ traversed at $dx/dt = 1$, with $x = r\cos\varphi$, $y = r\sin\varphi$: $r^2\,d\varphi/dt = x\,dy/dt - y\,dx/dt = -d$, of magnitude $d$. So $b = d$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 3** (photon sphere). $W = 1/r^2 - 2M/r^3$, so
>
> $$
> W'(r) = -\frac{2}{r^3} + \frac{6M}{r^4} = \frac{2}{r^4}(3M - r) ,
> $$
>
> positive for $r < 3M$, zero at $3M$, negative for $r > 3M$: a single maximum, $W(3M) = \frac{1}{9M^2}\bigl(1 - \frac23\bigr) = \frac{1}{27M^2}$. As in [[#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]], Step 1, a circular orbit needs $W'(r_c) = 0$ and $1/b^2 = W(r_c)$: $r_c = 3M$ and $b^2 = 27M^2$. With $\varepsilon = 0$, part 2 of [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]] reads $\ddot r = L^2(r - 3M)/r^4$: a ray displaced outward is pushed further out, one displaced inward further in. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 4** (capture). A ray from infinity moves inward ($\dot r < 0$) and turns where $W(r) = 1/b^2$. $W$ rises from $0$ at $2M$ to $1/27M^2$ at $3M$ and falls to $0$ at infinity (Step 3).
> - If $1/b^2 < 1/27M^2$ ($b > b_c$): the level $1/b^2$ meets the outer branch at one $r_0 > 3M$, reached first. There $\dot r = 0$; with $\varepsilon = 0$, $\ddot r = L^2(r_0 - 3M)/r_0^4 > 0$, so the ray turns and leaves. Writing $W(r_0) = 1/b^2$ as $b^2 = r_0^2/(1 - 2M/r_0)$ gives the displayed relation.
> - If $1/b^2 > 1/27M^2$ ($b < b_c$): $1/b^2 - W > 0$ for all $r > 2M$, so $\dot r$ never vanishes and $r$ decreases to $2M$.
> - If $b = b_c$: near $r = 3M$, $1/b^2 - W = \tfrac12|W''(3M)|(r - 3M)^2 + O\bigl((r - 3M)^3\bigr)$, so $|\dot r| \propto |r - 3M|$ and $r - 3M$ decreases exponentially in $\lambda$: the photon sphere is approached but not reached at finite $\lambda$.
>
> This is part 4. The relation $b(r_0)$ has its minimum $3\sqrt3\,M$ at $r_0 = 3M$ (script): a ray that turns cannot come closer than the photon sphere.
>
> **Step 5** (cross-section). The rays of a parallel beam with $b < b_c$ fill a disc of radius $b_c$ perpendicular to the beam; all of them are captured (Step 4), so $\sigma = \pi(3\sqrt3\,M)^2 = 27\pi M^2 = \tfrac{27}{4}\,\pi(2M)^2$. This is part 5.
>
> **Step 6** (restore). Lengths: $M \to GM/c^2$; $\sigma$ is a length squared.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** For light there is no rest-energy term and no stable orbit: $W$ has one hump and no well. The same hump that captures slow bodies with small $L$ captures rays aimed within $3\sqrt3\,M$ of the centre, a target $6.75$ times larger than a disc of radius $2M$.

## The shape of an orbit

The radial equation says how $r$ changes with proper time. The classical tests ask for the shape of the orbit, $r$ as a function of $\varphi$. In Newton's theory the substitution $u = 1/r$ makes the orbit equation linear, with conic sections as solutions ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-1|CM Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-2|CM Theorem §B2.2.2]]). The same substitution here gives Newton's equation plus one term.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.3.6: The Orbit Equation
> For a geodesic in the plane $\theta = \pi/2$ with $L > 0$, let $u(\varphi) = 1/r$ and $u' = du/d\varphi$.
> 1. **Second-order form.**
>
> $$
> u'' + u = \frac{\varepsilon M}{L^2} + 3Mu^2 :\qquad u'' + u = \frac{M}{L^2} + 3Mu^2\ \ \text{(body)}, \qquad u'' + u = 3Mu^2\ \ \text{(light)} .
> $$
>
> 2. **First integral.** $u'^2 = E^2/L^2 - (1 - 2Mu)\bigl(\varepsilon/L^2 + u^2\bigr)$; for light $u'^2 = 1/b^2 - u^2(1 - 2Mu)$.
> 3. **Against Newton.** Newton's orbit equation for $U = -M/r$ per unit mass is $u'' + u = M/L^2$ ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-1|CM Theorem §B2.2.1]]), whose bound solutions $u = (M/L^2)(1 + e\cos\varphi)$ close after $\Delta\varphi = 2\pi$. The new term $3Mu^2$ is the image of $-3ML^2/r^4$ in [[#^thm-c6-3-3|Theorem §C6.3.3]]; relative to $M/L^2$ it is $3L^2u^2 = 3L^2/r^2$. For light Newton has no source term at all: $u'' + u = 0$, straight lines $u = \sin(\varphi - \varphi_0)/b$.
>
> Here $\varphi$ is the Schwarzschild angle and $L$ is per unit mass with $\lambda = \tau$ for a body; for light only $b$ enters.
>
> Restored: $u'' + u = GM/(\mathcal L/m)^2 + 3GMu^2/c^2$; for light $u'' + u = 3GMu^2/c^2$.
>
> *Model:* as in [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], $L \neq 0$; *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.2.1, eqs. (6.47)–(6.51), p. 123 (PDF 124) (in $x = l^2/G_NMr$) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.6, eqs. (16.37)–(16.38), p. 300 (PDF 315); §16.7, eq. (16.51), p. 303 (PDF 318) · 't Hooft, ch. 12, eqs. (12.18), (12.30)*

^thm-c6-3-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (change of variable). Along the orbit $\dot\varphi = L/r^2 = Lu^2 > 0$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]), so $\varphi$ increases monotonically and can serve as the parameter. By the chain rule, $\frac{d}{d\lambda} = Lu^2\frac{d}{d\varphi}$. Hence
>
> $$
> \dot r = \frac{d}{d\lambda}\frac1u = -\frac{u'}{u^2}\,Lu^2 = -Lu', \qquad \ddot r = \frac{d}{d\lambda}\bigl(-Lu'\bigr) = -Lu''\,Lu^2 = -L^2u^2u'' .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (insert into the second-order radial equation). [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], part 2, with $1/r = u$:
>
> $$
> -L^2u^2u'' = -\varepsilon Mu^2 + L^2u^3 - 3ML^2u^4 .
> $$
>
> Dividing by $-L^2u^2 \neq 0$ (no division by $u'$, so circular orbits are included):
>
> $$
> u'' = \frac{\varepsilon M}{L^2} - u + 3Mu^2 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad u'' + u = \frac{\varepsilon M}{L^2} + 3Mu^2 .
> $$
>
> This is part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (first integral). Insert $\dot r = -Lu'$ into part 1 of [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]: $L^2u'^2 = E^2 - (1 - 2Mu)(\varepsilon + L^2u^2)$. Divide by $L^2$; for light $E^2/L^2 = 1/b^2$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (Newton). Per unit mass the force is $F = -M/r^2 = -Mu^2$, and [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-1|CM Theorem §B2.2.1]] with $\mu = 1$ gives $u'' + u = -F/(L^2u^2) = M/L^2$. Its general solution is $u = M/L^2 + A\cos(\varphi - \varphi_0)$, periodic with period $2\pi$. The ratio of the two source terms is $3Mu^2/(M/L^2) = 3L^2u^2$. For light, $M = 0$ leaves $u'' + u = 0$, solved by $u = \sin(\varphi - \varphi_0)/b$, the straight line at distance $b$ from the centre ($r\sin(\varphi - \varphi_0) = b$). This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (restore). $u'' + u$ has the dimension of $u$, $1/\text{length}$. With $M \to GM/c^2$ and geometric $L = (\mathcal L/m)/c$: $M/L^2 \to GM/(\mathcal L/m)^2$ and $3Mu^2 \to 3GMu^2/c^2$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The orbit equation is the radial equation in another variable, nothing more; the extra $3Mu^2$ is the image of the extra attraction $-3ML^2/r^4$. Because it is nonlinear in $u$, the Newtonian trick of solving a linear oscillator with a constant source no longer gives closed orbits.

^der-c6-3-6

*Uses:* [[#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]], [[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], [[#^thm-c6-3-3|Theorem §C6.3.3]], [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-1|CM Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-2|CM Theorem §B2.2.2]]

Newton's orbits close because the equation $u'' + u = M/L^2$ is linear and its solutions have period exactly $2\pi$ (Bertrand's theorem, [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-4|CM Theorem §B2.3.4]], says no other attraction does this for all orbits). The term $3Mu^2$ breaks this. By how much does a planet's perihelion move per orbit, and does it account for Mercury's anomaly? That is [[§C6.4 Perihelion Precession|§C6.4]]. For light, how much is a ray grazing the Sun deflected? That is [[§C6.5 The Bending of Light|§C6.5]].

> *Setup.* A body with $L = 4.3M$ (Hartle's illustrative value). By [[#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]], part 1, $\sqrt{1 - 12M^2/L^2} = \sqrt{1 - 12/18.49} = 0.5924$, so
>
> $$
> r_\pm = \frac{18.49}{2}\,(1 \pm 0.5924)\,M :\qquad r_+ = 14.72M,\ \ V(r_+) = 0.9379; \qquad r_- = 3.768M,\ \ V(r_-) = 1.0803 .
> $$
>
> The Newtonian potential $1 + 2U_{\text{eff}}$ for the same $L$ has its minimum at $r = L^2/M = 18.49M$ and rises without bound as $r \to 0$ (dashed in the figure).
>
> *The classes,* read off from where the level $E^2$ meets $V$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], part 1); turning points solved numerically from $V(r) = E^2$:
> 1. $E^2 = V(r_+) = 0.9379$: the stable circular orbit at $14.72M$.
> 2. $0.9379 < E^2 < 1$, e.g. $E^2 = 0.95$: **bound**, between $9.67M$ and $27.55M$. The angle from one apoapsis to the next is $\Delta\varphi = 2\int_{9.67M}^{27.55M}\frac{L/r^2}{\sqrt{E^2 - V}}\,dr$ (from $d\varphi/dr = \dot\varphi/\dot r$) $= 2\pi + 1.981$, an advance of $113.5^\circ$ per radial period, confirmed by integrating the orbit. A third root at $2.78M$ bounds a second branch inside the hump: a body there falls in.
> 3. $1 < E^2 < V(r_-)$, e.g. $E^2 = 1.04$: **scattering**. From infinity the body turns at $5.10M$ and leaves, after sweeping $\pi + 5.097 = 472^\circ$: more than one full turn about the centre, a deflection of $292^\circ$.
> 4. $E^2 = V(r_-) = 1.0803$: the unstable circular orbit at $3.768M$.
> 5. $E^2 > V(r_-)$, e.g. $E^2 = 1.10$: **plunge**. No turning point; the body crosses $r = 2M$. Newton allows no such orbit for $L \neq 0$.
>
> *Result.* The Newtonian cases bound, circular and scattering survive, with large precession and deflection this close to the hole; plunging orbits and unstable circles are new, and they come from the finite height of the hump ([[#^thm-c6-3-3|Theorem §C6.3.3]], part 4).

> [!example] Example §C6.3.2: Radial Infall from Rest at r₀
> *Setup.* A body released from rest at $r = r_0$ with $L = 0$. At $r_0$, $\dot r = 0$, so $E^2 = V(r_0) = 1 - 2M/r_0$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]] with $L = 0$), and
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\Bigr)^2 = E^2 - \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr) = \frac{2M}{r} - \frac{2M}{r_0} .
> $$
>
> *Proper time.* Substitute $r = \tfrac12r_0(1 + \cos\eta)$, $0 \le \eta \le \pi$, so $dr = -\tfrac12r_0\sin\eta\,d\eta$ and
>
> $$
> \frac{2M}{r} - \frac{2M}{r_0} = \frac{2M}{r_0}\Bigl(\frac{2}{1 + \cos\eta} - 1\Bigr) = \frac{2M}{r_0}\,\frac{1 - \cos\eta}{1 + \cos\eta} .
> $$
>
> With $\sin\eta = \sqrt{(1 - \cos\eta)(1 + \cos\eta)}$ and $d\tau = -dr/\sqrt{2M/r - 2M/r_0}$ (inward),
>
> $$
> d\tau = \frac{\tfrac12r_0\sin\eta\,d\eta}{\sqrt{2M/r_0}\,\sqrt{(1 - \cos\eta)/(1 + \cos\eta)}} = \tfrac12r_0\sqrt{\frac{r_0}{2M}}\,(1 + \cos\eta)\,d\eta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tau = \sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{8M}}\,(\eta + \sin\eta) .
> $$
>
> The body reaches $r = 2M$ at $\cos\eta_H = 4M/r_0 - 1$ and $r = 0$ at $\eta = \pi$, after the finite proper time $\tau = \pi\sqrt{r_0^3/8M}$. This is exactly the Newtonian free-fall time from rest, $\pi\sqrt{(r_0/2)^3/M}$ ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^ex-b2-2-2|CM Example §B2.2.2]]), now with proper time and areal radius.
>
> *Coordinate time.* $dt/d\tau = E/(1 - 2M/r)$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]), so
>
> $$
> \frac{dt}{dr} = -\frac{E}{(1 - 2M/r)\sqrt{2M/r - 2M/r_0}} \;\xrightarrow{\;r\to 2M\;}\; -\frac{E}{\bigl((r - 2M)/2M\bigr)\sqrt{1 - 2M/r_0}} = -\frac{2M}{r - 2M} ,
> $$
>
> using $E = \sqrt{1 - 2M/r_0}$. Hence $t \approx -2M\ln(r - 2M) + \text{const} \to \infty$: in Schwarzschild time the body never reaches $r = 2M$, and $r - 2M \propto e^{-t/2M}$.
>
> *Numbers* ($r_0 = 10M$). $E = 0.89443$; $\eta_H = 2.2143$; $\tau(2M) = 33.70M$, $\tau(0) = 35.12M$, confirmed by integrating $\ddot r = -M/r^2$ ([[#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], part 2, with $L = 0$). The coordinate time grows by $2M\ln 1000 = 13.82M$ each time $r - 2M$ shrinks by a factor $1000$. For $M = 10M_\odot$ ($GM/c^3 = 49.3\ \mu\mathrm s$, $r_0 = 148\ \mathrm{km}$): $1.66\ \mathrm{ms}$ to $r = 2M$ and $1.73\ \mathrm{ms}$ to $r = 0$ on the body's clock. Restored, $\tau(0) = \pi\sqrt{r_0^3/8GM}$.
>
> *Result.* The infalling clock crosses $r = 2M$ after a finite proper time, while $t$ diverges there: the coordinates fail at $r = 2M$, not the spacetime (C7, planned).
>

> [!example] Example §C6.3.3: The ISCO of a Solar-Mass and of a Supermassive Black Hole
> *Setup.* Gas in a thin accretion disk around a non-rotating hole moves on nearly circular orbits, loses energy and angular momentum slowly by friction, spirals inward through the stable circles and falls in from the ISCO (Hartle §11.2). The quantities follow from [[#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]] with $GM_\odot/c^2 = 1.477\ \mathrm{km}$.
>
> *Radius and period.* $r_{\text{ISCO}} = 6GM/c^2$. The period seen from far away is $P = 2\pi\sqrt{r^3/GM}$ (part 2) $= 2\pi\,6^{3/2}\,GM/c^3 = 92.3\,GM/c^3$.
>
> | Hole | $GM/c^2$ | $r_{\text{ISCO}}$ | $P$ at the ISCO |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $1\,M_\odot$ | $1.477\ \mathrm{km}$ | $8.86\ \mathrm{km}$ | $0.455\ \mathrm{ms}$ |
> | Sgr A\*, $4.15\times10^6M_\odot$ | $6.13\times10^6\ \mathrm{km}$ | $3.68\times10^7\ \mathrm{km} = 0.246\ \mathrm{AU}$ | $31.5\ \mathrm{min}$ |
>
> *Local speed.* A static observer at $r$ (four-velocity $(1 - 2M/r)^{-1/2}\partial_t$) measures the orbiting gas's Lorentz factor $\gamma = -u\cdot u_{\text{obs}} = E/\sqrt{1 - 2M/r}$ ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where $E_{\text{obs}} = p\cdot U$ without the minus sign). With part 2, $\gamma^2 = (1 - 2M/r)/(1 - 3M/r)$, so $v^2 = 1 - 1/\gamma^2 = M/(r - 2M)$: $v = c/2$ at the ISCO, for every mass.
>
> *Efficiency.* Each kilogram that reaches the ISCO has radiated $1 - \sqrt{8/9} = 5.72\%$ of its rest energy (part 6). Hydrogen fusion releases $27\ \mathrm{MeV}$ per $4\times938\ \mathrm{MeV}$, $0.72\%$ (Hartle Box 13.1), about $8$ times less. A modest accretion rate $\dot M = 10^{-8}M_\odot/\mathrm{yr} = 6.3\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{kg/s}$ gives the luminosity $0.0572\,\dot Mc^2 = 3.2\times10^{30}\ \mathrm W \approx 8.5\times10^3L_\odot$.
>
> *Result.* The ISCO scales with $M$: kilometres and milliseconds for a stellar hole, a quarter of an astronomical unit and half an hour for Sgr A\*. The efficiency $5.72\%$ and the speed $c/2$ are the same for every Schwarzschild hole.
>
> *Source: Hartle §11.2 and Box 11.1, pp. 244–245 (PDF 245–246) (disks end at the ISCO); Box 13.1, eqs. (b)–(d), p. 289 (PDF 290) ("6%") · Schutz §11.4, p. 338 (PDF 356) (Sgr A\* mass $4.15\times10^6M_\odot$; dated) · numbers checked by script*
