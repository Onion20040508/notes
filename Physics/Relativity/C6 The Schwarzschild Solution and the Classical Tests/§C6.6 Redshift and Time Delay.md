The last two classical tests are about clocks and light signals rather than orbits. Both were met at first order in C1. Light climbing out of a static field loses the fraction $\Delta\Phi$ of its frequency ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]]), and light in the weak-field metric moves at the coordinate speed $1 + 2\Phi$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]]). This section asks what the exact Schwarzschild metric ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]) says about both, and which part of each prediction an experiment can see.

First, what fails without the exact metric. Take the 511 keV photons from electron–positron annihilation at the surface of a neutron star with $M = 2.5\,M_\odot$, $R = 10\ \mathrm{km}$ (Hartle's Problem 9.2). Its compactness is $x = GM/(Rc^2) = 0.3692$. The first-order law gives $\nu_\infty/\nu_E = 1 - x = 0.6308$. But $1/(1 + x) = 0.7304$ and $e^{-x} = 0.6913$ agree with $1 - x$ to first order just as well, and the three differ by up to 16%. A first-order law cannot say which, if any, is right; at this compactness the answer needs the metric itself. The time delay fails differently. [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^ex-c1-5-3|Example §C1.5.3]] integrated the coordinate speed of light from the Sun's surface to $1\ \mathrm{AU}$ and found an excess of $52.9\ \mu\mathrm s$ over the coordinate distance, then warned that this number changes with the radial coordinate. A measurable delay must be timed by one clock, along a real round trip, and the coordinate-dependent part must be identified and removed.

In order, the section builds: the static observer ([[#^def-c6-6-1|Def. §C6.6.1]]) and the exact redshift between two of them, from the conserved Killing energy of the light ([[#^thm-c6-6-1|Theorem §C6.6.1]]); the rate of a clock on a circular orbit, $\sqrt{1 - 3M/r}$, the exact form of "gravitational rate times time dilation" ([[#^thm-c6-6-2|Theorem §C6.6.2]]); the redshift experiments and what they test ([[#^law-c6-6-3|Law §C6.6.3]]); the excess delay ([[#^def-c6-6-2|Def. §C6.6.2]]) and the Shapiro delay, derived from the null condition ([[#^thm-c6-6-4|Theorem §C6.6.4]]), with its coordinate-independent part; the radar experiments up to Cassini ([[#^law-c6-6-5|Law §C6.6.5]]). The examples compare exact and first-order redshifts, recompute the GPS budget in the exact metric, and work out the Mars echo.

**Setup.** The exterior Schwarzschild metric of a spherical, non-rotating body of mass $M$ and radius $R > 2M$, used only at $r > R$ (Birkhoff, [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-5|Theorem §C6.2.5]]):

$$
ds^2 = -f(r)\,dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{f(r)} + r^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr), \qquad f(r) = 1 - \frac{2M}{r} .
$$

Clocks and light are test systems: they do not change the metric. Light moves on null geodesics and free bodies on timelike geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]). A frequency is what an observer's own clock measures ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]); in a curved spacetime this holds in the observer's local inertial frame ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-4|Def. §C3.2.4]]). Two kinds of observers appear: static ones, held at fixed $(r, \theta, \varphi)$ by rockets or a tower, and free ones on circular orbits. For the Sun and the Earth the real bodies rotate and are slightly oblate; these corrections are named where they matter and are not computed. The numbers in boxes and examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; $G = c = 1$, every measurable result also restored (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here $M \to GM/c^2$ in lengths, $M \to GM/c^3$ in times, $f = 1 - 2GM/(rc^2)$); a dot is $d/d\lambda$ along a geodesic with affine parameter $\lambda$ ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-2|Def. §C3.7.2]]); $E = f\,\dot t$ and $L = r^2\dot\varphi$ are the conserved energy and angular momentum of [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]; "redshift" means a lower received frequency, $1 + z = \nu_{\text{em}}/\nu_{\text{rec}}$ ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^def-a2-4-1|Def. §A2.4.1]]). ## Redshift between static observers

In §C1.4 the redshift came from the equivalence principle, step by step along the path, and was correct to first order only. The exact metric has a timelike Killing vector $\partial_t$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^rem-c6-2-1|§C6.2, Remark: The Killing vectors of the Schwarzschild metric]]). The observers who measure the redshift move along it, and the light carries a quantity conserved along it. The first box names the observers.

> [!definition] Definition §C6.6.1: Static Observer
> *Structure.* In a spacetime with a timelike Killing vector field $K$ on a region $U$ ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-3|Def. §C3.13.3]]), a **static observer** is a timelike worldline in $U$ whose tangent is everywhere parallel to $K$; its four-velocity is the unit vector field
>
> $$
> u = \frac{K}{\sqrt{-K\cdot K}}, \qquad u\cdot u = -1 .
> $$
>
> In the Schwarzschild metric, with $K = \partial_t$ and $K\cdot K = g_{tt} = -f(r)$, the static observers are the worldlines of constant $(r, \theta, \varphi)$ at $r > 2M$, with
>
> $$
> u^\mu = \bigl(f(r)^{-1/2}, 0, 0, 0\bigr) .
> $$
>
> A static observer is not freely falling: a rocket or a support holds it against gravity. Its proper acceleration grows without bound as $r \to 2M$, while multiplied by the redshift factor $\sqrt f$ it tends to the surface gravity $1/4M$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^thm-c10-5-4|Theorem §C10.5.4]], part 3); near the horizon the static observers are the uniformly accelerated observers of Rindler space ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-4|Theorem §C10.2.4]], part 3). The observer at $r \to \infty$ has $u = \partial_t$, so the coordinate time $t$ is the proper time of the static observers far away. Static observers are the static clocks of [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames|§C1.4]] made exact; they do not exist at $r \le 2M$, where $\partial_t$ is not timelike ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Hartle §9.2, eqs. (9.13)–(9.17), pp. 190–191 (PDF 191–192) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2.3, eq. (6.77), p. 128 (PDF 129) · Schutz §10.2, pp. 269–271 (PDF 287–289)*

^def-c6-6-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.6.1: Redshift between Static Observers
> In the Schwarzschild metric, let a static observer at $r_E$ ([[#^def-c6-6-1|Def. §C6.6.1]]) emit light, on any null geodesic, to a static observer at $r_R$; both at $r > 2M$. Then:
> 1. **Measured frequency.** Light with four-momentum $p$ has the conserved energy $E_\gamma = -\partial_t\cdot p$ ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-2|Theorem §C3.13.2]]), and the static observer at $r$ measures
>
> $$
> 2\pi\hbar\,\nu(r) = -p\cdot u = f(r)^{-1/2}E_\gamma .
> $$
>
> 2. **Exact redshift.**
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_R}{\nu_E} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - 2M/r_E}{1 - 2M/r_R}}, \qquad\text{for a distant receiver}\quad \frac{\nu_\infty}{\nu_E} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{2M}{r_E}}, \quad 1 + z = \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r_E}\Bigr)^{-1/2} .
> $$
>
>    It depends only on the two radii, not on the path of the light or the kind of signal. For a steady wave it can be read off the phase alone, with no ray traced ([[§C4.6 Geometric Optics꞉ Light Follows Null Geodesics#^ex-c4-6-2|Example §C4.6.2]]).
> 3. **Expansion.** With $x = M/r_E$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_\infty}{\nu_E} = 1 - x - \tfrac12x^2 - \tfrac12x^3 + O(x^4), \qquad z = x + \tfrac32x^2 + \tfrac52x^3 + O(x^4) .
> $$
>
>    The first-order terms are the gravitational redshift of [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]], part 2, with $\Phi = -M/r$; the higher terms are new.
> 4. **⚑ Infinite redshift at $r_E \to 2M$.** $\nu_\infty/\nu_E \to 0$ and $z \to \infty$ as the emitter approaches $r = 2M$: a static source there would be invisible from far away. Whether anything can be at rest there is the question of C7, answered in [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]]. The surface of a static star cannot come close: its redshift stays below $2$ ([[§C9.2 The Uniform-Density Star and Buchdahl's Limit#^thm-c9-2-4|Theorem §C9.2.4]], part 3).
>
> Restored: $\nu_\infty/\nu_E = \sqrt{1 - 2GM/(r_Ec^2)}$, $z = GM/(r_Ec^2) + \tfrac32\bigl(GM/(r_Ec^2)\bigr)^2 + \dots$
>
> *Model:* exterior Schwarzschild metric, static emitter and receiver, light as a test field moving on null geodesics; *applies:* exactly, for $r_E, r_R$ outside the body and $> 2M$. A moving emitter or receiver adds its Doppler factor (Couzens' footnote 28). This is the Schwarzschild case of the general static result of [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^ex-c3-13-3|Example §C3.13.3]] (c).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.2.3, eqs. (6.77)–(6.81), pp. 128–129 (PDF 129–130) · Hartle §9.2, eqs. (9.12)–(9.20), pp. 190–191 (PDF 191–192) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.2, eq. (16.5), p. 291 (PDF 306) · expansions checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1 (the static observer's four-velocity).** With $u^\mu = (u^t, 0, 0, 0)$, the normalization $g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = -1$ reads
>
> $$
> g_{tt}\,(u^t)^2 = -f\,(u^t)^2 = -1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u^t = f^{-1/2}, \qquad u = f^{-1/2}\,\partial_t .
> $$
>
> **Step 2 (what the observer measures).** In the observer's local inertial frame ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]) $u = (1, 0, 0, 0)$ and the photon's energy is $p^{\hat 0} = -p\cdot u$, the special-relativistic rule ([[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$, where it reads $p\cdot U$). The energy is $2\pi\hbar\nu$. A scalar has the same value in every chart, so in Schwarzschild coordinates
>
> $$
> 2\pi\hbar\,\nu = -g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu p^\nu = -g_{tt}\,u^t p^t = f\cdot f^{-1/2}\,p^t = f^{1/2}\,p^t .
> $$
>
> **Step 3 (the conserved energy).** $\partial_t$ is a Killing vector, so $E_\gamma = -\partial_t\cdot p = -g_{tt}p^t = f\,p^t$ is constant along the null geodesic ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-2|Theorem §C3.13.2]], part 1, with $p^\mu = dx^\mu/d\lambda$). Substituting $p^t = E_\gamma/f$ into Step 2:
>
> $$
> 2\pi\hbar\,\nu(r) = f^{1/2}\,\frac{E_\gamma}{f} = f(r)^{-1/2}E_\gamma :
> $$
>
> part 1. (Couzens' (6.79) is this line, with $E$ the conserved energy of the null geodesic.)
>
> **Step 4 (the ratio).** The same $E_\gamma$ at both ends:
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_R}{\nu_E} = \frac{f(r_R)^{-1/2}E_\gamma}{f(r_E)^{-1/2}E_\gamma} = \sqrt{\frac{f(r_E)}{f(r_R)}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - 2M/r_E}{1 - 2M/r_R}} .
> $$
>
> For $r_R \to \infty$, $f(r_R) \to 1$, which gives the distant-receiver form and $1 + z = \nu_E/\nu_\infty = f(r_E)^{-1/2}$: part 2. Nothing about the geodesic was used except that it is null and joins the two observers.
>
> **Step 5 (expansion).** By the binomial series ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), with $y = -2x$,
>
> $$
> (1 + y)^{1/2} = 1 + \tfrac12y - \tfrac18y^2 + \tfrac1{16}y^3 + O(y^4) = 1 - x - \tfrac12x^2 - \tfrac12x^3 + O(x^4) ,
> $$
>
> $$
> (1 + y)^{-1/2} = 1 - \tfrac12y + \tfrac38y^2 - \tfrac5{16}y^3 + O(y^4) = 1 + x + \tfrac32x^2 + \tfrac52x^3 + O(x^4) ,
> $$
>
> so $z = (1 - 2x)^{-1/2} - 1 = x + \tfrac32x^2 + \tfrac52x^3 + O(x^4)$. With $\Phi(r) = -M/r$, the first order of part 2 is $\nu_R/\nu_E = 1 - M/r_E + M/r_R + O(M^2) = 1 + \Phi(r_E) - \Phi(r_R)$, which is Couzens' (6.81) and the formula of [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]]: part 3.
>
> **Step 6 (the clock route, as in §C1.4).** The flow of $\partial_t$ maps null geodesics to null geodesics and shifts $t$ by a constant. So a crest leaving the emitter at $t_1$ and arriving at $t_1 + T$ is followed by one leaving at $t_1 + \Delta t$ and arriving at $t_1 + \Delta t + T$: the coordinate intervals are equal at both ends, the exact form of Step 1 of [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^der-c1-4-6|the Derivation of Theorem §C1.4.6]]. A static clock at $r$ has $d\tau^2 = -ds^2 = f(r)\,dt^2$, so its proper interval is $\Delta\tau(r) = f(r)^{1/2}\Delta t$ (Couzens (6.83)–(6.84)). The frequencies are the inverse proper intervals:
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_R}{\nu_E} = \frac{\Delta\tau(r_E)}{\Delta\tau(r_R)} = \frac{f(r_E)^{1/2}\Delta t}{f(r_R)^{1/2}\Delta t} ,
> $$
>
> the same result.
>
> **Step 7 (part 4).** As $r_E \to 2M^+$, $f(r_E) \to 0^+$, so $\nu_\infty/\nu_E = f(r_E)^{1/2} \to 0$ and $z = f(r_E)^{-1/2} - 1 \to \infty$.
>
> **Step 8 (restore).** $M \to GM/c^2$ in $2M/r_E$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]); frequency ratios are dimensionless.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The redshift is a property of the two static observers, not of the light's path: the light carries one conserved number, $E_\gamma$, and each static observer converts it to a frequency with its own factor $f^{-1/2}$.
> - The two routes of §C1.4 (light, Theorem §C1.4.3; clocks, Theorem §C1.4.6) become one exact statement. The time independence used there is now the Killing vector $\partial_t$, and the clock rate $1 + \Phi$ becomes $\sqrt{1 - 2M/r}$.
> - Beyond first order, the redshift tests the metric component $g_{tt}$ itself, not only the equivalence principle.

## Clocks in orbit

A static clock needs a rocket. A satellite clock falls freely, so it moves relative to the static observers it passes. In §C1.4 the two effects were added at first order, $d\tau = (1 + \Phi - \tfrac12v^2)\,dt$ ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]], part 3). For a circular orbit in the exact metric the rate can be computed in closed form, and it shows how the two factors combine.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.6.2: Orbiting Clocks
> Let a clock move freely on a circular orbit of radius $r$ in the Schwarzschild metric, in the plane $\theta = \pi/2$ (no loss of generality, [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]). Circular timelike geodesics exist for $r > 3M$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]]). Then:
> 1. **Kepler's third law, exact in $t$.** The angular velocity with respect to coordinate time is
>
> $$
> \Omega \equiv \frac{d\varphi}{dt} = \sqrt{\frac{M}{r^3}} .
> $$
>
> 2. **Clock rate.** The clock's proper time runs at
>
> $$
> \frac{d\tau}{dt} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{3M}{r}} ,
> $$
>
>    so one orbit takes $2\pi\sqrt{r^3/M}$ of coordinate time (the time of a distant static observer) and $2\pi\sqrt{r^3/M}\,\sqrt{1 - 3M/r}$ on the orbiting clock.
> 3. **Gravitational rate times time dilation.** A static observer at the same radius ([[#^def-c6-6-1|Def. §C6.6.1]]) sees the orbiting clock pass at the speed $v = \sqrt{M/(r - 2M)}$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{d\tau}{dt} = \underbrace{\sqrt{1 - \frac{2M}{r}}}_{\text{static clock at } r}\;\underbrace{\sqrt{1 - v^2}}_{\text{time dilation}} \qquad\text{exactly.}
> $$
>
> 4. **First order.** $\sqrt{1 - 3M/r} = 1 - M/r - \tfrac12(M/r) + O(M^2/r^2) = 1 + \Phi - \tfrac12v^2$ with $\Phi = -M/r$ and the Newtonian $v^2 = M/r$: [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-6|Theorem §C1.4.6]], part 3, for this orbit.
> 5. **⚑ Strong field.** As $r \to 3M$, $v \to 1$ and $d\tau/dt \to 0$: the orbit becomes the circular light orbit of the photon sphere ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-5|Theorem §C6.3.5]]). At the innermost stable circular orbit $r = 6M$, $v = \tfrac12$ and $d\tau/dt = 1/\sqrt2 = 0.7071$, while a static clock there runs at $\sqrt{2/3} = 0.8165$.
>
> Restored: $\Omega^2 = GM/r^3$, $d\tau/dt = \sqrt{1 - 3GM/(rc^2)}$, $v = c\sqrt{GM/(rc^2 - 2GM)}$.
>
> *Model:* exterior Schwarzschild metric, test clock on a circular timelike geodesic, ideal clock ([[§A2.5 The Twin Paradox and Other Paradoxes#^mod-a2-5-1|Model §A2.5.1]]); *applies:* exactly, for $r > 3M$ outside the body; $t$ is the proper time of static observers far away. Orbits with $3M < r < 6M$ exist but are unstable.
>
> *Source: Schutz §11.1, eq. (11.24), p. 303 (PDF 321); Exercise 11.8, pp. 352–353 (PDF 370–371) · Hartle Problem 9.8, p. 216 (PDF 217) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2.3, eqs. (6.83)–(6.84), p. 129 (PDF 130) (static clocks only) · checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1 (circular orbit in the radial geodesic equation).** On the orbit $r = \text{const}$, $\theta = \pi/2$, so $\dot r = \ddot r = 0$, $\dot\theta = 0$. The $r$ component of the geodesic equation ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-2|Theorem §C3.7.2]]) is
>
> $$
> \ddot r + \Gamma^r_{tt}\,\dot t^2 + \Gamma^r_{rr}\,\dot r^2 + \Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}\,\dot\theta^2 + \Gamma^r_{\varphi\varphi}\,\dot\varphi^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Gamma^r_{tt}\,\dot t^2 + \Gamma^r_{\varphi\varphi}\,\dot\varphi^2 = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 2 (the two Christoffel symbols).** From [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-1|Theorem §C6.2.1]] with $e^{2A} = f$, $e^{2B} = 1/f$, $f' = 2M/r^2$:
>
> $$
> \Gamma^r_{tt} = e^{2(A - B)}A' = f^2\cdot\frac{f'}{2f} = \frac{M}{r^2}\,f, \qquad \Gamma^r_{\varphi\varphi} = -r\,e^{-2B}\sin^2\theta = -r f \quad(\theta = \pi/2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3 (part 1).** Insert into Step 1 and divide by $f\,\dot t^2 \neq 0$:
>
> $$
> \frac{M}{r^2}\,f\,\dot t^2 - r f\,\dot\varphi^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Bigl(\frac{\dot\varphi}{\dot t}\Bigr)^2 = \Bigl(\frac{d\varphi}{dt}\Bigr)^2 = \frac{M}{r^3} .
> $$
>
> The Newtonian $\Omega^2 = GM/r^3$ of [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-4|CM Theorem §A8.3.4]] holds exactly, in coordinate time.
>
> **Step 4 (part 2).** Along the orbit $dr = d\theta = 0$, $d\varphi = \Omega\,dt$, and the proper time ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]) is
>
> $$
> d\tau^2 = -ds^2 = f\,dt^2 - r^2\,d\varphi^2 = \bigl(f - r^2\Omega^2\bigr)dt^2 = \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r} - \frac{M}{r}\Bigr)dt^2 = \Bigl(1 - \frac{3M}{r}\Bigr)dt^2 .
> $$
>
> One orbit is $\Delta\varphi = 2\pi$, i.e. $\Delta t = 2\pi/\Omega = 2\pi\sqrt{r^3/M}$, and $\Delta\tau = \sqrt{1 - 3M/r}\,\Delta t$. The square is positive only for $r > 3M$, the condition for a timelike orbit.
>
> **Step 5 (the static observer's frame).** At the orbit's radius the static observer has the orthonormal frame $e_{\hat 0} = f^{-1/2}\partial_t$, $e_{\hat\varphi} = r^{-1}\partial_\varphi$ (each of norm $1$ in the metric, and orthogonal, since $g_{t\varphi} = 0$). The orbiting clock's four-velocity is
>
> $$
> u = \dot t\,(\partial_t + \Omega\,\partial_\varphi) = \dot t\,\bigl(f^{1/2}e_{\hat 0} + r\Omega\,e_{\hat\varphi}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> so the static observer measures the speed
>
> $$
> v = \frac{u^{\hat\varphi}}{u^{\hat 0}} = \frac{r\Omega}{f^{1/2}}, \qquad v^2 = \frac{r^2\,M/r^3}{1 - 2M/r} = \frac{M/r}{(r - 2M)/r} = \frac{M}{r - 2M} .
> $$
>
> **Step 6 (part 3).** Multiply the two factors:
>
> $$
> f\,(1 - v^2) = f - \frac{r - 2M}{r}\cdot\frac{M}{r - 2M} = 1 - \frac{2M}{r} - \frac{M}{r} = 1 - \frac{3M}{r} ,
> $$
>
> so $\sqrt f\,\sqrt{1 - v^2} = \sqrt{1 - 3M/r} = d\tau/dt$ by Step 4.
>
> **Step 7 (part 4).** $\sqrt{1 - 3x} = 1 - \tfrac32x + O(x^2)$ with $x = M/r$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), and $-\tfrac32x = -x - \tfrac12x = \Phi - \tfrac12v_N^2$ with $v_N^2 = M/r$ the Newtonian circular speed squared. To this order the static observer's $v^2 = M/(r - 2M) = M/r + O(M^2/r^2)$ is the same.
>
> **Step 8 (part 5).** At $r = 3M$: $v^2 = M/M = 1$ and $1 - 3M/r = 0$. At $r = 6M$: $v^2 = M/(4M) = \tfrac14$, $\sqrt{1 - 3/6} = 0.7071$, and the static clock's $\sqrt{1 - 2/6} = \sqrt{2/3} = 0.8165$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The rate of a moving clock factorizes exactly into the static clock's rate at its position and the special-relativistic dilation measured by the local static observer. This is the equivalence principle made exact: locally, special relativity; globally, the static clocks' $\sqrt{f}$.
> - Kepler's law surviving exactly in $t$ is a coordinate fact: $t$ is far-away time and $r$ the areal radius ([[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^def-c6-1-2|Def. §C6.1.2]]). In proper time, or with the static observer's speed, the corrections appear.
> - For Schutz's orbit at $r = 10M$ (Exercise 11.8), one orbit takes $2\pi\cdot10^{3/2}M = 198.7M$ of $t$ and $198.7M\times\sqrt{0.7} = 166.2M$ on the orbiting clock.

## The redshift experiments

> [!law] Law §C6.6.3: Pound–Rebka and Gravity Probe A
> - **Pound–Rebka–Snider (1960, 1964–65).** Gamma rays of ${}^{57}\mathrm{Fe}$ over the $22.5\ \mathrm m$ Harvard tower; agreement with the first-order shift $gh/c^2$ to about 1%. The experiment and its numbers are [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^law-c1-4-5|Law §C1.4.5]]; it is not repeated here.
> - **Gravity Probe A (Vessot and Levine, June 1976).** A hydrogen-maser clock was flown on a rocket to about $10\,000\ \mathrm{km}$ above the Earth and compared during the flight with a hydrogen maser on the ground. The first-order Doppler shift, about $10^{-5}$, is five orders of magnitude larger than the gravitational shift; it was removed by a transponder: a ground signal sent up and returned by the rocket carries the first-order Doppler shift twice and no net gravitational or second-order shift, and half its shift was subtracted from the clock signal automatically. Tracking gave the rocket's velocity, so the second-order Doppler shift (time dilation) could be subtracted too. The predicted gravitational shift at $10\,000\ \mathrm{km}$, $\frac{GM_\oplus}{c^2}\bigl(\frac{1}{R_\oplus} - \frac{1}{R_\oplus + 10^4\ \mathrm{km}}\bigr) = 4.25\times10^{-10}$ (checked by script), was confirmed:
>
> $$
> \Bigl|\frac{(\Delta\nu/\nu)_{\text{obs}} - (\Delta\nu/\nu)_{\text{pred}}}{(\Delta\nu/\nu)_{\text{pred}}}\Bigr| \le 2\times10^{-4} \qquad\text{(Hartle (10.3))} .
> $$
>
>   Will writes a possible violation of the equivalence principle as a shift $(1 + \alpha)\Delta\Phi/c^2$ and quotes $|\alpha| < 2\times10^{-4}$ from this flight.
> - **Astronomical redshifts.** The shift has also been seen in spectral lines from the Sun, white dwarfs and active galactic nuclei (Hartle); these are less precise, because the emitters' own motions produce Doppler shifts of the same size ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^ex-c1-4-2|Example §C1.4.2]]).
> - **Daily operation.** The GPS clock budget ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^law-c1-4-8|Law §C1.4.8]]; exact metric, [[#^ex-c6-6-2|Example §C6.6.2]]).
>
> *What is tested.* All these experiments are in weak fields, $M/r \lesssim 10^{-9}$ near the Earth. They confirm part 3 of [[#^thm-c6-6-1|Theorem §C6.6.1]] at first order, which follows from the equivalence principle alone, without field equations (Hartle: tests of the redshift are "more of a test of this principle than the details of general relativity"). The second-order term $\tfrac32(M/r)^2$ is $7\times10^{-12}$ at the Sun's surface and below $10^{-18}$ at the Earth's; no source cited here reports a test of it.
>
> *Valid when:* weak static fields (the Earth's, the Sun's); Pound–Rebka to about 1% (1960s), Gravity Probe A to $2\times10^{-4}$ (1976 flight; analysis published 1979–80). An experimental input: it confirms the first-order redshift with real clocks and leaves the exact form of Theorem §C6.6.1 as a prediction.

## The time delay of light

A radar pulse sent past the Sun to a planet and reflected back is timed by one clock on the Earth. The coordinate speed of light in the weak field is $1 + 2\Phi < 1$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]]), so the echo should come back late; Irwin Shapiro proposed the test in 1964 (d'Inverno). Two things are needed beyond §C1.5: the travel time along the actual null geodesic of the exact metric, and a definition of "late" that says what it is compared with. The comparison is the definition.

> [!definition] Definition §C6.6.2: Excess Delay
> *Structure.* Fix a static, spherically symmetric spacetime with coordinates $(t, r, \theta, \varphi)$ and a light signal from an event at radius $r_1$ to one at $r_2$ whose path has the closest approach $r_0$. The **excess delay** in these coordinates is the coordinate time of the signal minus the time light would need in flat space along the straight line with the same endpoint radii and the same closest approach:
>
> $$
> \Delta t \equiv t_{\text{signal}} - \Bigl(\sqrt{r_1^2 - r_0^2} + \sqrt{r_2^2 - r_0^2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> For a radar echo the round trip counts twice. The subtracted term treats $r$ as if it were a flat-space distance from the centre and picks one particular straight line, so $\Delta t$ depends on the choice of radial coordinate and of comparison path ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^cau-c1-5-2|§C1.5, Caution: Which parts of the metric are coordinate statements]]); [[#^thm-c6-6-4|Theorem §C6.6.4]], part 4, says which part does not. Hartle calls it the delay "over that which would be expected in Newtonian theory … using Schwarzschild coordinates".
>
> *Source: Hartle §9.4, eq. (9.91), p. 213 (PDF 214) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.8, text after eq. (16.73), p. 309 (PDF 324)*

^def-c6-6-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.6.4: The Shapiro Delay
> In the Schwarzschild metric, let light travel in the equatorial plane from radius $r_1$ (the Earth) to radius $r_2$ (a planet or spacecraft) with closest approach $r_0$, $3M < r_0 < r_1, r_2$, and impact parameter $b = L/E$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]). Then:
> 1. **Exact travel time.** Along the null geodesic $b^2 = r_0^2/f(r_0)$, and the coordinate time from $r_0$ out to $r$ is
>
> $$
> t(r, r_0) = \int_{r_0}^{r}\frac{dr'}{f(r')}\Bigl[1 - \frac{r_0^2}{r'^2}\,\frac{f(r')}{f(r_0)}\Bigr]^{-1/2} .
> $$
>
> 2. **First order in $M$.**
>
> $$
> t(r, r_0) = \sqrt{r^2 - r_0^2} + 2M\ln\frac{r + \sqrt{r^2 - r_0^2}}{r_0} + M\sqrt{\frac{r - r_0}{r + r_0}} + O(M^2) ,
> $$
>
>    and $b = r_0 + M + O(M^2/r_0)$.
> 3. **Excess delay** ([[#^def-c6-6-2|Def. §C6.6.2]]), one way, $t_{\text{signal}} = t(r_1, r_0) + t(r_2, r_0)$:
>
> $$
> \Delta t = 2M\ln\frac{\bigl(r_1 + \sqrt{r_1^2 - r_0^2}\bigr)\bigl(r_2 + \sqrt{r_2^2 - r_0^2}\bigr)}{r_0^2} + M\Bigl[\sqrt{\frac{r_1 - r_0}{r_1 + r_0}} + \sqrt{\frac{r_2 - r_0}{r_2 + r_0}}\Bigr] \;\approx\; 2M\Bigl[\ln\frac{4r_1r_2}{b^2} + 1\Bigr] \quad (r_0 \ll r_1, r_2) ;
> $$
>
>    the radar round trip is twice this, $4M\bigl[\ln(4r_1r_2/b^2) + 1\bigr]$.
> 4. **⚑ Only the logarithm is invariant.** The constant term depends on the radial coordinate and on the flat-space path used for comparison. For the same signal, one way and for $r_0 \ll r_1, r_2$: Schwarzschild $r$ with the line of the same closest approach (Def. §C6.6.2) gives $2M[\ln(4r_1r_2/b^2) + 1]$; Schwarzschild $r$ with the straight chord between the actual endpoints gives $2M[\ln(4r_1r_2/b^2) - 1]$; isotropic coordinates ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]) with the chord give $2M\ln(4r_1r_2/b^2)$. The term $2M\ln(4r_1r_2/b^2)$, and with it the change of the delay as $b$ changes, is common to all; it is what an experiment fits ([[#^cau-c6-6-2|Caution: What the radar clock actually measures]]).
> 5. **⚑ Half from time, half from space.** Of the coefficient $2M$ of the logarithm, $M$ comes from $g_{tt}$ and $M$ from the spatial metric, as for the coordinate speed of light ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]], part 3). With a spatial coefficient $1 + 2\gamma M/r$ in place of $1 + 2M/r$ (the parameter $\gamma$ of [[#^law-c6-6-5|Law §C6.6.5]]), it is $(1 + \gamma)M$.
>
> Restored: one way $\Delta t = \dfrac{2GM}{c^3}\Bigl[\ln\dfrac{4r_1r_2}{b^2} + 1\Bigr]$, with $2GM_\odot/c^3 = 9.85\ \mu\mathrm s$ for the Sun.
>
> *Model:* exterior Schwarzschild metric (the Sun spherical and non-rotating), light as a test signal on a null geodesic; Earth and target treated as at rest during the trip; *applies:* part 1 exactly; parts 2–5 to first order in $M/r_0$, for rays that pass outside the body ($r_0 > R_\odot$).
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers §16.8, eqs. (16.61)–(16.75), Fig. 16.15, pp. 307–309 (PDF 322–324) · Hartle §9.4, eqs. (9.85)–(9.92), Fig. 9.12, pp. 212–214 (PDF 213–215); §10.2, eqs. (10.6b), (10.10), pp. 222–223 (PDF 223–224) · expansions, antiderivatives and both coordinate versions checked by script*

^thm-c6-6-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1 (conserved quantities and the null condition).** In the plane $\theta = \pi/2$ the light ray has $E = f\,\dot t$ and $L = r^2\dot\varphi$ constant ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]), and $g(\dot x, \dot x) = 0$ along it ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-7|Theorem §C3.7.7]]):
>
> $$
> -f\,\dot t^2 + \frac{\dot r^2}{f} + r^2\dot\varphi^2 = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 2 (radial equation).** Substitute $\dot t = E/f$, $\dot\varphi = L/r^2$ and multiply by $f$:
>
> $$
> -\frac{E^2}{f} + \frac{\dot r^2}{f} + \frac{L^2}{r^2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot r^2 = E^2 - f\,\frac{L^2}{r^2} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3 ($r$ as a function of $t$).** $dr/dt = \dot r/\dot t = f\,\dot r/E$, so with $b = L/E$
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{dr}{dt}\Bigr)^2 = \frac{f^2}{E^2}\Bigl(E^2 - f\frac{L^2}{r^2}\Bigr) = f^2\Bigl(1 - \frac{b^2f}{r^2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 4 (turning point; part 1).** At the closest approach $dr/dt = 0$ with $f(r_0) \neq 0$, so $1 = b^2f(r_0)/r_0^2$, i.e. $b^2 = r_0^2/f(r_0)$. Inserting this,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{dr}{dt}\Bigr)^2 = f(r)^2\Bigl[1 - \frac{r_0^2}{r^2}\,\frac{f(r)}{f(r_0)}\Bigr], \qquad \frac{dt}{dr} = \frac{1}{f(r)}\Bigl[1 - \frac{r_0^2}{r^2}\,\frac{f(r)}{f(r_0)}\Bigr]^{-1/2}
> $$
>
> on the outgoing branch ($dr/dt > 0$). The bracket is positive for $r > r_0 > 3M$, because $r^2/f(r) = r^3/(r - 2M)$ increases there: its derivative is $r^2(2r - 6M)/(r - 2M)^2 > 0$. The incoming branch takes the same time from $r$ down to $r_0$ (the time reflection $t \mapsto -t$ is an isometry of the static metric, [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-6|Theorem §C3.13.6]], part 2). Integrating from $r_0$ to $r$ gives part 1 (Hartle (9.87), d'Inverno (16.68)).
>
> **Step 5 (the ratio of the $f$'s to first order).** With $1/(1 - 2M/r_0) = 1 + 2M/r_0 + O(M^2)$,
>
> $$
> \frac{f(r)}{f(r_0)} = \Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)\Bigl(1 + \frac{2M}{r_0}\Bigr) + O(M^2) = 1 - \frac{2M}{r} + \frac{2M}{r_0} + O(M^2) .
> $$
>
> **Step 6 (the bracket).** Using $\frac{2M}{r_0} - \frac{2M}{r} = \frac{2M(r - r_0)}{r r_0}$ and $r^2 - r_0^2 = (r - r_0)(r + r_0)$:
>
> $$
> 1 - \frac{r_0^2}{r^2}\Bigl(1 + \frac{2M(r - r_0)}{rr_0}\Bigr) = \frac{r^2 - r_0^2}{r^2} - \frac{2Mr_0(r - r_0)}{r^3} = \frac{r^2 - r_0^2}{r^2}\Bigl[1 - \frac{2Mr_0}{r(r + r_0)}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> **Step 7 ($dt/dr$ to first order).** $(1 - \epsilon)^{-1/2} = 1 + \tfrac12\epsilon + O(\epsilon^2)$ and $1/f = 1 + 2M/r + O(M^2)$; the product of the two corrections is $O(M^2)$:
>
> $$
> \frac{dt}{dr} = \Bigl(1 + \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)\frac{r}{\sqrt{r^2 - r_0^2}}\Bigl[1 + \frac{Mr_0}{r(r + r_0)}\Bigr] + O(M^2) = \frac{r}{\sqrt{r^2 - r_0^2}} + \frac{2M}{\sqrt{r^2 - r_0^2}} + \frac{Mr_0}{(r + r_0)\sqrt{r^2 - r_0^2}} + O(M^2) ,
> $$
>
> d'Inverno's (16.69).
>
> **Step 8 (the three integrals; part 2).** From $r_0$ to $r$:
> - $\displaystyle\int_{r_0}^{r}\frac{r'\,dr'}{\sqrt{r'^2 - r_0^2}}$: with $w = r'^2 - r_0^2$, $dw = 2r'\,dr'$, it is $\displaystyle\int_0^{r^2 - r_0^2}\frac{dw}{2\sqrt w} = \sqrt{r^2 - r_0^2}$.
> - $\displaystyle\int_{r_0}^{r}\frac{2M\,dr'}{\sqrt{r'^2 - r_0^2}}$: with $r' = r_0\cosh s$, $dr' = r_0\sinh s\,ds$, $\sqrt{r'^2 - r_0^2} = r_0\sinh s$, it is $2M\int_0^{s_1}ds = 2M s_1$ with $\cosh s_1 = r/r_0$, i.e. $s_1 = \ln\bigl[(r + \sqrt{r^2 - r_0^2})/r_0\bigr]$.
> - $\displaystyle\int_{r_0}^{r}\frac{Mr_0\,dr'}{(r' + r_0)\sqrt{r'^2 - r_0^2}}$: the same substitution gives $M\displaystyle\int_0^{s_1}\frac{r_0\cdot r_0\sinh s\,ds}{r_0(\cosh s + 1)\,r_0\sinh s} = M\int_0^{s_1}\frac{ds}{2\cosh^2(s/2)} = M\tanh\frac{s_1}{2}$, and $\tanh\frac{s}{2} = \frac{\sinh s}{1 + \cosh s} = \sqrt{\frac{\cosh s - 1}{\cosh s + 1}} = \sqrt{\frac{r - r_0}{r + r_0}}$.
>
> The sum is part 2 (d'Inverno (16.71), Hartle (9.90)). Finally $b = r_0f(r_0)^{-1/2} = r_0\bigl(1 + M/r_0 + O(M^2/r_0^2)\bigr) = r_0 + M + O(M^2/r_0)$ (Hartle (9.89)).
>
> **Step 9 (part 3).** By Def. §C6.6.2, $\Delta t = \bigl[t(r_1, r_0) - \sqrt{r_1^2 - r_0^2}\bigr] + \bigl[t(r_2, r_0) - \sqrt{r_2^2 - r_0^2}\bigr]$; the two logarithms combine into one. For $r_0 \ll r$: $r + \sqrt{r^2 - r_0^2} = 2r\bigl(1 - r_0^2/(4r^2) + \dots\bigr)$ and $\sqrt{(r - r_0)/(r + r_0)} = 1 - r_0/r + \dots$, so
>
> $$
> \Delta t = 2M\ln\frac{2r_1\cdot2r_2}{r_0^2} + M(1 + 1) + O\Bigl(\frac{Mr_0}{r_{1,2}}\Bigr) = 2M\Bigl[\ln\frac{4r_1r_2}{r_0^2} + 1\Bigr] + O\Bigl(\frac{Mr_0}{r_{1,2}}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Replacing $r_0$ by $b = r_0 + M$ changes $2M\ln(1/r_0^2)$ by $2M\cdot2M/r_0 = O(M^2/r_0)$. This is d'Inverno's (16.74) and Hartle's (9.92) (both for the round trip, $4M[\dots]$). For the Mars echo the dropped terms are $0.07\ \mu\mathrm s$ of $267\ \mu\mathrm s$ (checked by script).
>
> **Step 10 (part 4: the chord in Schwarzschild coordinates).** The comparison line of Def. §C6.6.2 is not the straight line between the actual endpoints. With $\Delta\varphi = \pi - \varepsilon$ the angle the endpoints subtend at the centre, the flat distance is $D^2 = r_1^2 + r_2^2 + 2r_1r_2\cos\varepsilon = (r_1 + r_2)^2 - r_1r_2\varepsilon^2 + O(\varepsilon^4)$. The line of closest approach $r_0$ has $\varepsilon_0 = \arcsin(r_0/r_1) + \arcsin(r_0/r_2) \approx r_0(r_1 + r_2)/(r_1r_2)$. The ray is bent toward the centre by $\delta \approx 4M/b$ in total ([[§C6.5 The Bending of Light#^thm-c6-5-2|Theorem §C6.5.2]]), so its endpoints subtend $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0 - \delta$. Keeping first order in $\delta$ (valid while $\delta \ll \varepsilon_0$: $8.5\times10^{-6}$ against $7.7\times10^{-3}$ for a ray grazing the Sun from the Earth to Mars):
>
> $$
> D_{\text{chord}} - D_0 = \frac{-r_1r_2\bigl[(\varepsilon_0 - \delta)^2 - \varepsilon_0^2\bigr]}{D_{\text{chord}} + D_0} \approx \frac{2r_1r_2\varepsilon_0\delta}{2(r_1 + r_2)} = r_0\delta = r_0\,\frac{4M}{b} = 4M + O(M^2/r_0) .
> $$
>
> The chord is longer by $4M$, so the excess measured against it is $2M[\ln(4r_1r_2/b^2) + 1] - 4M = 2M[\ln(4r_1r_2/b^2) - 1]$.
>
> **Step 11 (part 4: isotropic coordinates).** The isotropic radius $\rho$ of the weak-field metric has $r = \rho + M + O(M^2/\rho)$ ([[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^ex-c6-1-2|Example §C6.1.2]], Step 1), so $\rho_i = r_i - M + O(M^2/r_i)$ at the same angles, and the nearly collinear chord shortens by $M$ at each end: $D_{\text{chord}}^{\text{iso}} = D_{\text{chord}} - 2M$. The time coordinate is the same, so the excess grows by $2M$, to $2M\ln(4r_1r_2/b^2)$. Independently, from the weak-field metric of [[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]] with $\Phi = -M/\rho$: light has $dt = (1 + 2M/\rho)\,dl + O(M^2)$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-6|Theorem §C1.5.6]], part 1), with $dl$ the flat length element. Along the chord with closest distance $b_c$, $\rho = \sqrt{l^2 + b_c^2}$ for $-l_1 \le l \le l_2$, and
>
> $$
> \int_{-l_1}^{l_2}\frac{2M\,dl}{\sqrt{l^2 + b_c^2}} = 2M\Bigl[\operatorname{arcsinh}\frac{l_2}{b_c} + \operatorname{arcsinh}\frac{l_1}{b_c}\Bigr] = 2M\ln\frac{(l_1 + \rho_1)(l_2 + \rho_2)}{b_c^2} \approx 2M\ln\frac{4\rho_1\rho_2}{b_c^2} .
> $$
>
> The real ray deviates from the chord by $O(M)$, which changes the travel time only at $O(M^2)$, since among nearby paths the straight one has stationary flat length; $b_c$ differs from $b$ by a relative $4Mr_1r_2/[(r_1 + r_2)b^2] = 1.1\times10^{-3}$ for a ray grazing the Sun towards Mars, $0.02\ \mu\mathrm s$ on the delay; and $\ln(\rho_1\rho_2)$ differs from $\ln(r_1r_2)$ by $O(M/r)$. Both routes agree, and all three constants were confirmed on an exact null geodesic by script.
>
> **Step 12 (part 5).** In the weak-field integrand $dt/dl = (1 - 2M/\rho)^{-1/2}(1 + 2M/\rho)^{1/2}$ the first factor comes from $g_{00} = -(1 - 2M/\rho)$ and the second from $g_{ij} = (1 + 2M/\rho)\delta_{ij}$; each is $1 + M/\rho + O(M^2)$ and gives $M\ln(4\rho_1\rho_2/b^2)$. With $g_{ij} = (1 + 2\gamma M/\rho)\delta_{ij}$, $\sqrt{(1 + 2\gamma M/\rho)/(1 - 2M/\rho)} = 1 + (1 + \gamma)M/\rho + O(M^2)$, and the logarithm's coefficient is $(1 + \gamma)M$ (Hartle (10.10) has the factor $(1 + \gamma)/2$ in front of $4GM/c^3$).
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The delay is the integral of $dt/dr$ along the real ray. Its size is set by the $1/f$ of $g_{tt}$ and by the spatial metric, which together make light slow in these coordinates; the bending of the path contributes nothing at first order.
> - Only the logarithm survives every change of bookkeeping. Its dependence on $b$ makes the delay rise sharply as the ray approaches the Sun's limb, and this rise is what the experiments measure ([[#^cau-c6-6-2|Caution: What the radar clock actually measures]]).
> - The delay measures the same combination $1 + \gamma$ as the bending of light ([[§C6.5 The Bending of Light#^thm-c6-5-3|Theorem §C6.5.3]]): both are light-propagation tests of the spatial metric.

> [!caution] Caution: What the radar clock actually measures
> - **Proper time, not $t$.** The echo is timed by a clock on the Earth, which sits in the Sun's potential and moves at $29.8\ \mathrm{km/s}$: its rate relative to $t$ is $1 - M_\odot/(1\ \mathrm{AU}) - \tfrac12v^2 = 1 - 1.48\times10^{-8}$ ([[#^thm-c6-6-2|Theorem §C6.6.2]], part 4). Over the $2519\ \mathrm s$ of an Earth–Mars round trip that is $37\ \mu\mathrm s$, comparable with the delay itself. Hartle states the measured interval as the coordinate time "corrected for the influence of the Earth on spacetime and other effects".
> - **$r_1$ and $r_2$ are not measured independently.** They come from the planetary orbits, which are fitted to the same ranging data; a 1% measurement of the delay needs the orbits to about $1\ \mathrm{km}$ (Hartle).
> - **So the experiment fits a time series.** The rate offset and the constant term of [[#^thm-c6-6-4|Theorem §C6.6.4]], part 3, vary slowly over months and are absorbed in the orbit model. The logarithm rises sharply as the line of sight approaches the Sun ([[#^ex-c6-6-3|Example §C6.6.3]], figure), and that signature is what determines $\gamma$. The solar corona adds a frequency-dependent delay of up to about $100\ \mu\mathrm s$, removed by ranging at two frequencies (Hartle).
> - **Hartle's 247 μs.** Hartle evaluates $(4GM/c^3)[\log(4r_Rr_\oplus/R_\odot^2) + 1]$ for Mars as "$\approx 247\ \mu\mathrm s$". With $r_\oplus = 1\ \mathrm{AU}$, $r_R = 1.524\ \mathrm{AU}$ that expression is $267\ \mu\mathrm s$; $247\ \mu\mathrm s$ is the logarithm alone, the coordinate-independent part ([[#^ex-c6-6-3|Example §C6.6.3]]).

> [!law] Law §C6.6.5: Radar Echoes and Cassini
> *The parameter tested.* Write the metric of a static spherical body as $-A(r)\,dt^2 + B(r)\,dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$ with $A = 1 - 2M/r + \dots$, $B = 1 + 2\gamma M/r + \dots$ (Hartle (10.4), (10.6); the same $\gamma$ multiplies $2M/\rho$ in the isotropic spatial metric at this order). General relativity has $\gamma = 1$ (the Schwarzschild $1/f = 1 + 2M/r + \dots$). The delay is proportional to $\tfrac12(1 + \gamma)$ ([[#^thm-c6-6-4|Theorem §C6.6.4]], part 5; Hartle (10.10)).
> - **Passive radar (from 1964).** Shapiro proposed the test in 1964; radar pulses were reflected from Venus and Mercury as they passed behind the Sun. For Venus the measured delay was about $200\ \mu\mathrm s$, in agreement with the prediction to better than 5% (d'Inverno & Vickers).
> - **Viking (1976).** Transponders on the two Viking landers on Mars returned the radar signal; superior conjunction was on 26 November 1976. The maximum excess delay was about $250\ \mu\mathrm s$ in a round trip of about $41$ minutes ([[#^ex-c6-6-3|Example §C6.6.3]]), and the fit gave $\gamma = 1.000 \pm 0.002$ (Hartle (10.15); d'Inverno & Vickers: better than 1%).
> - **Cassini (2002 conjunction, published 2003).** Not in the typed sources; from Will's review, §4.1.2 (Bertotti, Iess and Tortora, Nature 425, 374 (2003)). The Cassini spacecraft, on its way to Saturn and $8.43\ \mathrm{AU}$ from the Sun, was tracked at its 2002 superior conjunction, with the radio path passing $1.6\,R_\odot$ from the Sun. Instead of timing echoes, the experiment measured the frequency of the returned signal, whose fractional shift is the rate of change of the round-trip delay, $|d\Delta t/dt| = 8M\,|\dot b|/b$ from the logarithm of Theorem §C6.6.4. With $\dot b \approx 28\ \mathrm{km/s}$, set mainly by the Earth's orbital motion, this is of order $10^{-9}$ (estimate added here; the round-trip delay itself would be about $262\ \mu\mathrm s$). Links in two bands, X ($7175\ \mathrm{MHz}$) and Ka ($34\,316\ \mathrm{MHz}$), removed most of the coronal effect. Result:
>
> $$
> \gamma - 1 = (2.1 \pm 2.3)\times10^{-5} .
> $$
>
> *Valid when:* solar-system weak field, $M_\odot/b \lesssim 2\times10^{-6}$; the delay is tested at first order in $M/b$ through its logarithm, by fitting the time dependence near conjunction. Viking (1976) to $2\times10^{-3}$ in $\gamma$; Cassini (2002, published 2003) to $2.3\times10^{-5}$, the best bound on $\gamma$ quoted in Will's 2014 review. An experimental input: it confirms the spatial part of the metric, which slow-motion tests cannot see ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^mod-c1-5-2|Model §C1.5.2]]), and with the deflection of light ([[§C6.5 The Bending of Light#^law-c6-5-5|Law §C6.5.5]]) rules out theories with $\gamma \neq 1$ beyond this precision.
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers §16.8, p. 309 (PDF 324) · Hartle §10.2–10.3, eqs. (10.4), (10.6), (10.10), (10.15), Fig. 10.7, pp. 221–230 (PDF 222–231) · Will (2014), §4.1.2 and Table 4 (online; Cassini, Viking 0.1 percent) · estimates checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C6.6.1: Redshift from the Sun, a White Dwarf and a Neutron Star
> *Question.* Light leaves the surface of a star and is received far away. How large is the redshift, and when does the first-order law ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]]) stop being good enough?
>
> *Setup.* Static emitter on the surface $r_E = R$, distant static receiver: [[#^thm-c6-6-1|Theorem §C6.6.1]], part 2, restored, with the compactness $x = GM/(Rc^2)$ ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^def-c1-1-1|Def. §C1.1.1]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_\infty}{\nu_E} = \sqrt{1 - 2x}, \qquad z_{\text{exact}} = (1 - 2x)^{-1/2} - 1, \qquad z_{\text{first}} = x ;
> $$
>
> by part 3 they differ by $\tfrac32x^2$ at leading order. $GM_\odot/c^2 = 1476.67\ \mathrm m$.
>
> *The Sun* ($R_\odot = 6.957\times10^8\ \mathrm m$). $x = 1476.67/6.957\times10^8 = 2.1226\times10^{-6}$; $z_{\text{exact}} - z_{\text{first}} = \tfrac32(2.1226\times10^{-6})^2 = 6.8\times10^{-12}$, three millionths of $z$. The first-order value of [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^ex-c1-4-2|Example §C1.4.2]], $z = 2.12\times10^{-6}$ ($0.0139\ \text{Å}$ on H$\alpha$), stands (so does [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^ex-c3-13-3|Example §C3.13.3]] (c)).
>
> *A white dwarf* ($0.6\,M_\odot$, $7000\ \mathrm{km}$). $x = 0.6\times1476.67/7.0\times10^6 = 1.2657\times10^{-4}$, $z_{\text{exact}} = 1.2660\times10^{-4}$: the two differ by $1.9\times10^{-4}$ of $z$, still far below the measurement errors of stellar spectra.
>
> *A neutron star* ($2.5\,M_\odot$, $10\ \mathrm{km}$; Hartle Problem 9.2: electron–positron pairs annihilating at rest on the surface emit $511\ \mathrm{keV}$ photons). $x = 2.5\times1476.67/10^4 = 0.3692$, $1 - 2x = 0.2617$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_\infty}{\nu_E} = \sqrt{0.2617} = 0.5115, \qquad E_\infty = 511\ \mathrm{keV}\times0.5115 = 261.4\ \mathrm{keV}, \qquad z = 0.955 .
> $$
>
> The first-order law would give $511\ \mathrm{keV}\times(1 - 0.3692) = 322.4\ \mathrm{keV}$, $23\%$ too high; the other first-order forms of the opening, $1/(1 + x) = 0.7304$ and $e^{-x} = 0.6913$, are worse. A distant observer looks for the line at $261\ \mathrm{keV}$. However compact a static star is, its surface redshift stays below $2$ ([[§C9.2 The Uniform-Density Star and Buchdahl's Limit#^thm-c9-2-4|Theorem §C9.2.4]], part 3); this star's $2.5\,M_\odot$ is above every neutron-star mass measured so far ([[§C9.3 White Dwarfs, Neutron Stars and Collapse#^law-c9-3-5|Law §C9.3.5]]).
>
> *Near a black hole* (Schutz Exercise 11.7(c)–(d)). A source at rest at $r = 2.2M$ has $1 - 2M/r = 1/11$: H$\alpha$ arrives at $6563\ \text{Å}\times\sqrt{11} = 21\,767\ \text{Å}$, in the infrared. At $r = 2.02M$ the factor is $10.05$ and the line arrives at $65\,957\ \text{Å}$. As $r \to 2M$ the factor diverges ([[#^thm-c6-6-1|Theorem §C6.6.1]], part 4).
>
> *What it shows.* The exact law matters only where $x$ is not small: for every star except neutron stars the first-order redshift of §C1.4 is enough, and for neutron stars and the surroundings of black holes the full $\sqrt{1 - 2M/r}$ must be used.
>
> *Source: Hartle Problem 9.2, p. 215 (PDF 216) · Schutz Exercise 11.7, p. 352 (PDF 370); §10.7 (white dwarfs about the size of the Earth) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C6.6.2: GPS in Exact Schwarzschild
> *Question.* [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^law-c1-4-8|Law §C1.4.8]] found that a GPS satellite clock gains $38.4\ \mu\mathrm s$ per day on a ground clock, to first order. What does the exact metric give, and what changes?
>
> *Setup.* The Earth as a spherical, non-rotating mass with the exterior Schwarzschild metric ($GM_\oplus/c^2 = 4.4351\ \mathrm{mm}$; oblateness and the metric of the Earth's rotation neglected). The satellite on a circular orbit of radius $a = 2.6562\times10^7\ \mathrm m$ ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^ex-c1-4-3|Example §C1.4.3]], Step 1): a free clock, [[#^thm-c6-6-2|Theorem §C6.6.2]]. The ground clock on the equator, $R_\oplus = 6.3781\times10^6\ \mathrm m$, carried round with the Earth at $\omega = 2\pi/(86\,164.1\ \mathrm s)$: not free, so its rate is computed directly from the metric with $dr = d\theta = 0$, $d\varphi = \omega\,dt$. Both rates are relative to $t$, the time of distant static observers; their ratio is what the ground sees.
>
> *Step 1 (satellite).* By Theorem §C6.6.2, part 2,
>
> $$
> \frac{d\tau_{\text{sat}}}{dt} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{3M}{a}} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{3\times4.4351\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm m}{2.6562\times10^7\ \mathrm m}} = \sqrt{1 - 5.009\times10^{-10}} = 1 - 2.5045\times10^{-10} .
> $$
>
> *Step 2 (ground).* $d\tau^2 = f(R_\oplus)\,dt^2 - R_\oplus^2\,d\varphi^2$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]), so, restored,
>
> $$
> \frac{d\tau_{\text{gr}}}{dt} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{2GM_\oplus}{R_\oplus c^2} - \frac{\omega^2R_\oplus^2}{c^2}} = \sqrt{1 - 1.3907\times10^{-9} - 2.41\times10^{-12}} = 1 - 6.9536\times10^{-10} - 1.20\times10^{-12} .
> $$
>
> *Step 3 (ratio).*
>
> $$
> \frac{d\tau_{\text{sat}}}{d\tau_{\text{gr}}} - 1 = 4.461\times10^{-10}, \qquad\times\,86\,400\ \mathrm s = +38.54\ \mu\mathrm s\ \text{per day}.
> $$
>
> Split by origin: the difference of the potentials, $M/R_\oplus - M/a$, gives $+45.65\ \mu\mathrm s$; the satellite's motion, $-\tfrac12 M/a$, gives $-7.21\ \mu\mathrm s$; the ground clock's rotation gives $+0.10\ \mu\mathrm s$ (it slows the reference clock).
>
> *Step 4 (exact against first order).* The first-order rates $1 - \tfrac32M/a$ and $1 - M/R_\oplus - \tfrac12\omega^2R_\oplus^2$ give the same $38.54\ \mu\mathrm s$ per day: the exact square roots change the result by $17$ picoseconds per day, since the neglected terms are of order $(M/R_\oplus)^2 \approx 5\times10^{-19}$. With the ground clock held static (no rotation) the exact result is $38.44\ \mu\mathrm s$, the value of §C1.4. The step from $38.4$ to $38.5$ is the rotation of the ground clock (Hartle's $0.10\ \mu\mathrm s$, [[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^ex-c1-4-3|Example §C1.4.3]], Step 7), not the exact metric.
>
> *Step 5 (relative to a distant observer).* The satellite clock alone runs at $1 - \tfrac32M/a = 1 - 2.5045\times10^{-10}$ of far-away time, $-21.64\ \mu\mathrm s$ per day: the factorization of Theorem §C6.6.2, part 3, contains Hartle's two terms $GM_\oplus/(ac^2) = 1.67\times10^{-10}$ and $v^2/(2c^2) = 0.83\times10^{-10}$ ([[§C1.4 Clocks in Gravity꞉ Redshift and the End of Global Inertial Frames#^cau-c1-4-1|§C1.4, Caution: Which clock the GPS numbers are relative to]]).
>
> *What it shows.* At the Earth's compactness the exact metric adds nothing measurable to the clock budget; what it adds is the closed form $\sqrt{1 - 3M/a}$, which shows how the gravitational and the kinematic factors combine without being added by hand.
>
> *Source: Hartle §6.4, eqs. (6.16)–(6.19), pp. 121–124 (PDF 122–125); Prob. 6.9 · Schutz Exercise 11.8 (the same computation at $r = 10M$) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C6.6.3: Radar Echo from Mars at Superior Conjunction
> *Question.* A radar pulse is sent from the Earth to a lander on Mars when Mars is behind the Sun and the signal grazes the solar limb (the Viking geometry). How long is the round trip, how large is the excess delay, and how does it change over the weeks around conjunction?
>
> *Setup.* Circular, coplanar orbits $r_1 = 1\ \mathrm{AU} = 1.496\times10^{11}\ \mathrm m$, $r_2 = 1.524\ \mathrm{AU}$; ray with $b = R_\odot = 6.957\times10^8\ \mathrm m$; [[#^thm-c6-6-4|Theorem §C6.6.4]], round trip, restored with $GM_\odot/c^3 = 4.9256\ \mu\mathrm s$.
>
> *Step 1 (the round trip).* $2(r_1 + r_2)/c = 2\times2.524\times1.496\times10^{11}\ \mathrm m/c = 2519\ \mathrm s$, about $42$ minutes (Hartle: "roughly $41$ min").
>
> *Step 2 (the logarithm).*
>
> $$
> \frac{4r_1r_2}{b^2} = \frac{4\times1.496\times10^{11}\times2.280\times10^{11}}{(6.957\times10^8)^2} = 2.82\times10^5, \qquad \ln(2.82\times10^5) = 12.549 .
> $$
>
> *Step 3 (the delay).* Coordinate-independent part and the excess of Def. §C6.6.2:
>
> $$
> \frac{4GM_\odot}{c^3}\ln\frac{4r_1r_2}{b^2} = 19.70\ \mu\mathrm s\times12.549 = 247.3\ \mu\mathrm s, \qquad \frac{4GM_\odot}{c^3}\Bigl[\ln\frac{4r_1r_2}{b^2} + 1\Bigr] = 267.0\ \mu\mathrm s .
> $$
>
> The unexpanded formula of part 3 gives $266.88\ \mu\mathrm s$, so the approximation $r_0 \ll r_{1,2}$ costs $0.07\ \mu\mathrm s$. The delay is one part in $10^7$ of the trip, and a 1% measurement needs the trip to one part in $10^9$ (Hartle).
>
> *Step 4 (the curve).* As the Earth overtakes Mars, the line of sight sweeps across the Sun and $b$ changes. With the orbits above, $b$ is $R_\odot$ about $0.96$ days from conjunction (inside that the signal would hit the Sun; the real orbits are inclined, so it passes beside it), $10.4\,R_\odot$ at $10$ days and $31\,R_\odot$ at $30$ days, and the coordinate-independent part falls from $247$ to $155$ and $111\ \mu\mathrm s$. The figure shows the whole curve. The sharp peak is what the fit to the ranging data measures ([[#^cau-c6-6-2|Caution: What the radar clock actually measures]]).
>
> *What it shows.* A $250\ \mu\mathrm s$ effect in a $42$-minute round trip, recognisable not by its size alone but by its logarithmic shape in time.
>
> *Source: Hartle §9.4, eq. (9.92), p. 214 (PDF 215); §10.3, Fig. 10.7, pp. 229–230 (PDF 230–231) · d'Inverno & Vickers §16.8, eqs. (16.73)–(16.75), pp. 308–309 (PDF 323–324) · numbers checked by script*
