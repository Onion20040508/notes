Every bound orbit of a planet about a point mass in Newton's theory is a closed ellipse ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-2|CM Theorem §B2.2.2]]), and Bertrand's theorem says that this is a rare property: among all central potentials only $-\alpha/r$ and $r^2$ close every bounded orbit ([[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-4|CM Theorem §B2.3.4]]). The reason is visible in the orbit equation. With $u = 1/r$ and the angular momentum per unit mass $L$, Newtonian gravity gives ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-1|CM Theorem §B2.2.1]], reduced mass $\mu = 1$, force constant $M$)

$$
u'' + u = \frac{M}{L^2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad u(\varphi) = \frac{M}{L^2} + C\cos(\varphi - \varphi_0) ,
$$

a linear equation with constant coefficients whose free part $u'' + u = 0$ has period exactly $2\pi$ in $\varphi$. One radial oscillation (perihelion to perihelion) takes exactly one revolution; in the language of [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-2|CM Theorem §B2.3.2]] the radial and angular frequencies are equal, $\omega_r/\omega_\varphi = \sqrt{k + 2} = 1$ for $U \propto r^{k}$ with $k = -1$. Mercury's perihelion nevertheless advances by $43''$ per century more than the Newtonian perturbations of the other planets explain ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^law-c1-1-6|Law §C1.1.6]]).

General relativity changes the orbit equation. For a timelike geodesic in the Schwarzschild spacetime it reads $u'' + u = M/L^2 + 3Mu^2$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-6|Theorem §C6.3.6]]): one extra term, quadratic in $u$, and the equation is no longer linear. For a planet the term is tiny, $3Mu^2 : M/L^2 = 3L^2u^2 \approx 3M/p \approx 8\times10^{-8}$ for Mercury, so the obvious attempt is to expand in it. The attempt fails in an instructive way: the first-order correction grows without bound, like $\varphi\sin\varphi$ ([[#^thm-c6-4-1|Theorem §C6.4.1]], part 3), while the true orbit stays between its turning points. The failure is the answer in disguise. The growing term is the first term in the expansion of a slowly turning ellipse, and the correction is not to the shape of the orbit but to its radial period in $\varphi$, which becomes $2\pi(1 + 3M^2/L^2)$ (part 4). The perihelion therefore advances by $6\pi M^2/L^2 = 6\pi M/[a(1 - e^2)]$ per orbit ([[#^thm-c6-4-2|Theorem §C6.4.2]]), $42.98''$ per century for Mercury, the observed anomaly ([[#^law-c6-4-3|Law §C6.4.3]]). The same number follows from the Newtonian perihelion-shift formula once the relativistic term is read as a $1/r^3$ correction to the potential ([[#^thm-c6-4-4|Theorem §C6.4.4]]), and for nearly circular orbits the advance can be found without assuming the field is weak ([[#^thm-c6-4-5|Theorem §C6.4.5]]).

**Setup.** A test body (planet, star, spacecraft) moving freely on a timelike geodesic of the Schwarzschild exterior ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]) outside a spherical central mass $M$; by spherical symmetry the orbit lies in the equatorial plane $\theta = \pi/2$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]). The orbit is bound, with conserved energy $E < 1$ and angular momentum $L > 0$ per unit mass. The perturbative results (Theorems §C6.4.1, §C6.4.2, §C6.4.4) are first order in $\alpha = 3M^2/L^2$, which for orbits far from the mass equals $3M/p$, three times the compactness ([[§C1.1 Why Newtonian Gravity Must Be Replaced#^def-c1-1-1|Def. §C1.1.1]]) at the semi-latus rectum; [[#^thm-c6-4-5|Theorem §C6.4.5]] is first order in the eccentricity but exact in $M/r$. Neglected: the field of the planet itself (for a binary star, the two-body problem is quoted, not derived), the other planets, the Sun's quadrupole moment and rotation. Numbers in the boxes and examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; $G = c = 1$, restored by $M \to GM/c^2$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; $M_\odot = 1476.67\ \mathrm m$); a dot is $d/d\tau$, a prime is $d/d\varphi$; $u = 1/r$ with $r$ the areal radius ([[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^def-c6-1-2|Def. §C6.1.2]]); $E = (1 - 2M/r)\dot t$, $L = r^2\dot\varphi$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]); radial equation $\dot r^2 = E^2 - V(r)$ with $V = (1 - 2M/r)(1 + L^2/r^2)$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^def-c6-3-1|Def. §C6.3.1]]; other normalizations: [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^cau-c6-3-1|§C6.3, Caution: Normalizations of the effective potential]]). The small parameter $\alpha = 3M^2/L^2$ is Couzens' (d'Inverno writes $\varepsilon$, 't Hooft $\varepsilon$ with a different normalization); it is unrelated to the force constant $\alpha$ of [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits|CM §B2.2]], which here equals $M$.

## Solving the orbit equation to first order

The orbit equation is first rescaled so that the Newtonian circular orbit sits at $1$ and the relativistic term carries one small number. Put $u = (M/L^2)\,x$ (Couzens' $x = L^2/(Mr)$, d'Inverno's $\tilde u$). Then $u'' + u = M/L^2 + 3Mu^2$ becomes

$$
\frac{M}{L^2}\,(x'' + x) = \frac{M}{L^2} + 3M\,\frac{M^2}{L^4}\,x^2 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad x'' + x = 1 + \alpha\,x^2, \qquad \alpha = \frac{3M^2}{L^2} .
$$

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.4.1: Perturbative Solution of the Orbit Equation
> Let $x(\varphi) = L^2/(Mr)$ along a bound timelike geodesic in the equatorial plane of the Schwarzschild spacetime, so that $x'' + x = 1 + \alpha x^2$ with $\alpha = 3M^2/L^2 \ll 1$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-6|Theorem §C6.3.6]]), and let $\varphi = 0$ be a perihelion.
> 1. *(Zeroth order: Kepler.)* Write $x = x_0 + x_1 + O(\alpha^2)$ with $x_1 = O(\alpha)$. Then $x_0'' + x_0 = 1$ and
>
> $$
> x_0 = 1 + e\cos\varphi \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad r_0(\varphi) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\varphi}, \qquad p = \frac{L^2}{M} ,
> $$
>
> the Kepler ellipse of semi-latus rectum $p$ and eccentricity $e$ ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^def-b2-2-1|CM Def. §B2.2.1]]).
> 2. *(First order.)* $x_1'' + x_1 = \alpha x_0^2$, and the solution with $x_1'(0) = 0$ and no free oscillation added is
>
> $$
> x_1 = \alpha\Bigl[\Bigl(1 + \frac{e^2}{2}\Bigr) + e\,\varphi\sin\varphi - \frac{e^2}{6}\cos2\varphi\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> 3. *(⚑ The secular term.)* The middle term is driven at the free frequency of $x'' + x$, the resonant case of an undamped driven oscillator ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-3|WO Theorem §B1.3.3]], part 2), and grows without bound: at $\varphi = 2\pi n + \pi/2$ it equals $\alpha e(2\pi n + \pi/2)$. The exact $x$ stays between the values at the two turning points of the radial motion ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]). The expansion $x_0 + x_1$ is therefore valid only while $\alpha\varphi \ll 1$; for Mercury $\alpha\varphi = 1$ after $1.99\times10^{6}$ orbits ($4.80\times10^{5}$ years).
> 4. *(A form valid for all $\varphi$.)* With the radial frequency shifted to $\omega = 1 - \alpha$,
>
> $$
> x = 1 + e\cos(\omega\varphi) + \alpha\Bigl[\Bigl(1 + \frac{e^2}{2}\Bigr) - \frac{e^2}{6}\cos(2\omega\varphi)\Bigr] + O(\alpha^2), \qquad \omega = 1 - \alpha ,
> $$
>
> with an error that stays $O(\alpha^2)$ over $O(1/\alpha)$ orbits. The value $\omega = 1 - \alpha$ is forced: it is the only one for which the first-order equation has no resonant forcing. Expanding $e\cos\bigl((1 - \alpha)\varphi\bigr) = e\cos\varphi + \alpha e\,\varphi\sin\varphi + O(\alpha^2\varphi^2)$ recovers the secular term of part 2.
>
> *Model:* a test body on a timelike geodesic of the Schwarzschild exterior, bound orbit, $L^2 \gg M^2$; *applies:* first order in $\alpha$; parts 1–2 for $\alpha\varphi \ll 1$, part 4 for all $\varphi$ up to $O(1/\alpha)$ orbits.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.2.1, eqs. (6.49)–(6.60) (pp. 123–124) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §16.6, eqs. (16.38)–(16.47) (pp. 300–302) · 't Hooft, ch. 12, eqs. (12.18)–(12.26) (pp. 54–55; part 4, by the resonance argument) · Hartle, §9.3 (naive expansion of the apsidal integral "a little tricky", p. 204)*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (orders). Substitute $x = x_0 + x_1$ into $x'' + x = 1 + \alpha x^2$:
>
> $$
> x_0'' + x_1'' + x_0 + x_1 = 1 + \alpha\bigl(x_0^2 + 2x_0x_1 + x_1^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Since $x_1 = O(\alpha)$, the terms $2\alpha x_0x_1$ and $\alpha x_1^2$ are $O(\alpha^2)$ and are dropped. Collecting the terms of order $\alpha^0$ and $\alpha^1$:
>
> $$
> x_0'' + x_0 = 1, \qquad\qquad x_1'' + x_1 = \alpha\,x_0^2 .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (zeroth order). A particular solution of $x_0'' + x_0 = 1$ is $1$; the free solutions are $A\cos\varphi + B\sin\varphi = C\cos(\varphi - \varphi_0)$. With $C = e \ge 0$ and the perihelion (maximum of $x$, minimum of $r$) at $\varphi_0 = 0$: $x_0 = 1 + e\cos\varphi$. Back in $r$: $1/r_0 = (M/L^2)(1 + e\cos\varphi)$, so $r_0 = p/(1 + e\cos\varphi)$ with $p = L^2/M$, which is [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-2|CM Theorem §B2.2.2]] with $\mu = 1$ and force constant $M$. Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 3** (the forcing). Square $x_0$ and use $\cos^2\varphi = \frac12(1 + \cos2\varphi)$:
>
> $$
> \alpha x_0^2 = \alpha\bigl(1 + 2e\cos\varphi + e^2\cos^2\varphi\bigr) = \alpha\Bigl(1 + \frac{e^2}{2}\Bigr) + 2\alpha e\cos\varphi + \frac{\alpha e^2}{2}\cos2\varphi .
> $$
>
> The equation $x_1'' + x_1 = \alpha x_0^2$ is linear, so its particular solution is the sum of the responses to the three terms.
>
> **Step 4** (constant term). For $x_1 = c$ constant, $x_1'' + x_1 = c$, so $c = \alpha(1 + e^2/2)$.
>
> **Step 5** ($\cos2\varphi$ term). Try $x_1 = C\cos2\varphi$: $x_1'' = -4C\cos2\varphi$, so $x_1'' + x_1 = -3C\cos2\varphi$; equating with $\frac12\alpha e^2\cos2\varphi$ gives $C = -\alpha e^2/6$.
>
> **Step 6** ($\cos\varphi$ term, resonance). A trial $C\cos\varphi$ gives $x_1'' + x_1 = 0$ and cannot work: the drive is at the free frequency. Try $x_1 = D\,\varphi\sin\varphi$:
>
> $$
> x_1' = D(\sin\varphi + \varphi\cos\varphi), \qquad x_1'' = D(2\cos\varphi - \varphi\sin\varphi), \qquad x_1'' + x_1 = 2D\cos\varphi .
> $$
>
> Equating with $2\alpha e\cos\varphi$ gives $D = \alpha e$. This is [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-3|WO Theorem §B1.3.3]], part 2, with $t \to \varphi$, $\omega_0 = 1$, $f_0 = 2\alpha e$: $x = (f_0/2\omega_0)\,\varphi\sin\omega_0\varphi = \alpha e\,\varphi\sin\varphi$.
>
> **Step 7** (assembling; the free part). Adding Steps 4–6 gives $x_1$ of part 2. A free oscillation $A_1\cos\varphi + B_1\sin\varphi$ of order $\alpha$ could be added; it only changes $e$ and the perihelion direction at order $\alpha$ and is set to zero. Then $x_1'(0) = \alpha[e(\sin0 + 0) + \frac{e^2}{3}\sin0] = 0$, so $\varphi = 0$ remains a turning point. (The solution was checked symbolically.) Part 2 is proved.
>
> **Step 8** (unboundedness). At $\varphi_n = 2\pi n + \pi/2$: $\cos\varphi_n = 0$, $\sin\varphi_n = 1$, $\cos2\varphi_n = -1$, so
>
> $$
> x_0 + x_1 = 1 + \alpha\Bigl(1 + \frac{e^2}{2} + \frac{e^2}{6}\Bigr) + \alpha e\Bigl(2\pi n + \frac\pi2\Bigr) \xrightarrow{\ n \to \infty\ } \infty .
> $$
>
> The exact $x$ satisfies the first integral $x'^2 = F(x)$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]] divided by $\dot\varphi^2$), so it moves between two consecutive zeros of $F$, the turning points, and is bounded. The two disagree by order one in $x_1$ relative to $e$ once $\alpha\varphi \sim 1$. For Mercury $\alpha = 3M/p = 7.988\times10^{-8}$ ([[#^ex-c6-4-1|Example §C6.4.1]]), and $\alpha\varphi = 1$ at $\varphi = 1/\alpha$, i.e. after $1/(2\pi\alpha) = 1.99\times10^{6}$ orbits of $87.97$ days, $4.80\times10^{5}$ years. Part 3 is proved.
>
> **Step 9** (shifted frequency). Try $x = 1 + e\cos(\omega\varphi) + \tilde x_1(\varphi)$ with $\omega = 1 - \kappa\alpha$, $\kappa$ to be found, $\tilde x_1 = O(\alpha)$. Then $\omega^2 = 1 - 2\kappa\alpha + O(\alpha^2)$ and
>
> $$
> x'' + x - 1 - \alpha x^2 = -e\omega^2\cos\omega\varphi + e\cos\omega\varphi + \tilde x_1'' + \tilde x_1 - \alpha\bigl(1 + e\cos\omega\varphi\bigr)^2 + O(\alpha^2)
> $$
>
> $$
> = 2\kappa\alpha e\cos\omega\varphi + \tilde x_1'' + \tilde x_1 - \alpha\Bigl(1 + \frac{e^2}{2}\Bigr) - 2\alpha e\cos\omega\varphi - \frac{\alpha e^2}{2}\cos2\omega\varphi + O(\alpha^2) .
> $$
>
> Setting this to zero:
>
> $$
> \tilde x_1'' + \tilde x_1 = \alpha\Bigl(1 + \frac{e^2}{2}\Bigr) + 2\alpha e\,(1 - \kappa)\cos\omega\varphi + \frac{\alpha e^2}{2}\cos2\omega\varphi .
> $$
>
> The middle term drives the oscillator $\tilde x_1'' + \tilde x_1$ at the frequency $\omega = 1 + O(\alpha)$, at resonance to this order, and would produce a secular term again (Step 6). It vanishes exactly when $\kappa = 1$: $\omega = 1 - \alpha$. Then, as in Steps 4–5 with $\cos2\omega\varphi/(1 - 4\omega^2) = -\frac13\cos2\omega\varphi + O(\alpha)$,
>
> $$
> \tilde x_1 = \alpha\Bigl(1 + \frac{e^2}{2}\Bigr) - \frac{\alpha e^2}{6}\cos2\omega\varphi + O(\alpha^2) ,
> $$
>
> bounded for all $\varphi$. The dropped terms are $O(\alpha^2)$ with bounded coefficients, so they shift the phase by $O(\alpha^2\varphi)$, small for $\varphi \ll 1/\alpha^2$. (The cancellation condition, $2B(A - \kappa) = 0$ in 't Hooft's notation with $A = 1$, $B = e$, was checked symbolically.)
>
> **Step 10** (the two forms agree). By the addition formula and $\cos(\alpha\varphi) = 1 + O(\alpha^2\varphi^2)$, $\sin(\alpha\varphi) = \alpha\varphi + O(\alpha^3\varphi^3)$:
>
> $$
> e\cos\bigl((1 - \alpha)\varphi\bigr) = e\cos\varphi\cos\alpha\varphi + e\sin\varphi\sin\alpha\varphi = e\cos\varphi + \alpha e\,\varphi\sin\varphi + O(\alpha^2\varphi^2) .
> $$
>
> Part 4 is proved.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The relativistic term contains a component, $2\alpha e\cos\varphi$, at exactly the frequency of the Newtonian radial oscillation. A forcing at resonance cannot be answered by a small bounded correction; the naive expansion shows this as a secular term ([[#^thm-c6-4-1|Theorem §C6.4.1]], part 3).
> - The resonance is removed by letting the frequency itself carry the correction. The orbit remains, at each turn, an ellipse to order $\alpha$; what changes is that it does not return to the same angle. This is the content of [[#^thm-c6-4-2|Theorem §C6.4.2]].
> - The constant and $\cos2\varphi$ terms are bounded corrections to the shape: the orbit is slightly smaller and slightly non-elliptic, effects of relative order $\alpha$ that do not accumulate.

## The advance per orbit

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.4.2: Perihelion Advance
> For a bound orbit as in [[#^thm-c6-4-1|Theorem §C6.4.1]], successive perihelia (minima of $r$) are separated by the angle $2\pi/(1 - \alpha) = 2\pi + \Delta\varphi$, so the perihelion advances, in the sense of the orbital motion, by
>
> $$
> \Delta\varphi = \frac{6\pi M^2}{L^2} = \frac{6\pi M}{p} = \frac{6\pi M}{a(1 - e^2)} \quad\text{per orbit} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \Delta\varphi = \frac{6\pi GM}{c^2a(1 - e^2)} = \frac{24\pi^3a^2}{c^2T^2(1 - e^2)}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> to first order in $\alpha = 3M/p$, where $p = L^2/M$, and $a$, $e$ are the semi-major axis and eccentricity of the orbit, defined by its perihelion and aphelion radii $r_{\min} = a(1 - e)$, $r_{\max} = a(1 + e)$, and $T$ is the orbital period ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-4|CM Theorem §B2.2.4]]).
> 1. At fixed $p$ the advance does not depend on the eccentricity; it is the same for a nearly circular orbit and for a very eccentric one.
> 2. Per unit time the advance is $\Delta\varphi/T \propto a^{-5/2}(1 - e^2)^{-1}$ (with $T \propto a^{3/2}$): it falls off steeply outward, and among the planets Mercury's is the largest.
> 3. Which $a$ and $e$ are used (Newtonian elements, or the radii of the actual orbit) changes $\Delta\varphi$ only at order $\alpha^2$.
>
> In the language of [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^def-b2-3-1|CM Def. §B2.3.1]], the apsidal angle is $\Phi = \pi(1 + \alpha) + O(\alpha^2)$ and the precession per radial period is $2\Phi - 2\pi = \Delta\varphi$.
>
> *Model:* as in [[#^thm-c6-4-1|Theorem §C6.4.1]]; *applies:* first order in $M/p$, so for orbits far outside $r = 6M$; for nearly circular orbits at any radius, [[#^thm-c6-4-5|Theorem §C6.4.5]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.2.1, eqs. (6.59)–(6.64) (pp. 124–125) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §16.6, eqs. (16.47)–(16.50) (p. 302) · Schutz, §11.1, eqs. (11.37)–(11.40) (p. 306) · 't Hooft, ch. 12, eq. (12.27), Exercise (12.40) (pp. 55–56) · Hartle, §9.3, eqs. (9.55)–(9.57) (p. 204)*

^thm-c6-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (perihelia from the uniform form). Write [[#^thm-c6-4-1|Theorem §C6.4.1]], part 4, as $x = 1 + e\cos\psi + \alpha(1 + e^2/2) - (\alpha e^2/6)\cos2\psi$ with $\psi = \omega\varphi$. Its derivative, with $\frac{d}{d\varphi}\cos2\psi = -2\omega\sin2\psi = -4\omega\sin\psi\cos\psi$:
>
> $$
> x' = -e\omega\sin\psi + \frac{2\alpha e^2\omega}{3}\sin\psi\cos\psi = -e\omega\sin\psi\Bigl(1 - \frac{2\alpha e}{3}\cos\psi\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> The bracket is positive for $\alpha < 1$, so $x' = 0$ exactly where $\sin\psi = 0$: $\psi = n\pi$. The maxima of $x$ (minima of $r$, the perihelia) are at $\cos\psi = 1$, $\psi = 2\pi n$, i.e.
>
> $$
> \varphi_n = \frac{2\pi n}{\omega} = \frac{2\pi n}{1 - \alpha} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the advance). Successive perihelia are separated by
>
> $$
> \varphi_{n+1} - \varphi_n = \frac{2\pi}{1 - \alpha} = 2\pi\bigl(1 + \alpha + \alpha^2 + \dots\bigr) = 2\pi + 2\pi\alpha + O(\alpha^2) ,
> $$
>
> so $\Delta\varphi = 2\pi\alpha = 6\pi M^2/L^2$ to first order. It is positive: the perihelion moves forward in $\varphi$, the direction of motion ($L = r^2\dot\varphi > 0$).
>
> **Step 3** (the same from the secular form). Look for the first perihelion after $\varphi = 0$ near $\varphi = 2\pi + \delta$, $\delta = O(\alpha)$. From part 2, $x' = -e\sin\varphi + \alpha e(\sin\varphi + \varphi\cos\varphi) + \frac{\alpha e^2}{3}\sin2\varphi$. At $\varphi = 2\pi + \delta$: $\sin\varphi = \delta + O(\delta^3)$, $\cos\varphi = 1 + O(\delta^2)$, $\sin2\varphi = 2\delta + O(\delta^3)$, so
>
> $$
> 0 = x' = -e\delta + \alpha e(\delta + 2\pi + \delta) + \frac{2\alpha e^2}{3}\delta + O(\alpha^3) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \delta\,\bigl(1 + O(\alpha)\bigr) = 2\pi\alpha \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \delta = 2\pi\alpha + O(\alpha^2) .
> $$
>
> The secular term, harmless over one orbit ($\alpha\varphi \le 2\pi\alpha \ll 1$), gives the right advance; it fails only when continued over $1/\alpha$ radians.
>
> **Step 4** (in terms of the orbit). From Theorem §C6.4.1, part 1, $p = L^2/M$, so $L^2 = Mp$ and
>
> $$
> \Delta\varphi = \frac{6\pi M^2}{L^2} = \frac{6\pi M^2}{Mp} = \frac{6\pi M}{p} .
> $$
>
> The zeroth-order orbit has $r_{\min} = p/(1 + e)$ and $r_{\max} = p/(1 - e)$, so $a = \frac12(r_{\min} + r_{\max}) = \frac{p}{2}\,\frac{(1 - e) + (1 + e)}{1 - e^2} = \frac{p}{1 - e^2}$ and $p = a(1 - e^2)$. The $O(\alpha)$ terms of $x$ change $r_{\min}$, $r_{\max}$, hence $a$ and $e$, by relative amounts $O(\alpha)$; inserted into $\Delta\varphi = O(\alpha)$ they change it by $O(\alpha^2)$. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (restoring units). By [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]] the mass enters as the length $GM/c^2$: $\Delta\varphi = 6\pi GM/[c^2a(1 - e^2)]$, a pure number (radians). Kepler's third law $GM = 4\pi^2a^3/T^2$ ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-4|CM Theorem §B2.2.4]], test body), valid at the order needed, gives
>
> $$
> \Delta\varphi = \frac{6\pi}{c^2a(1 - e^2)}\cdot\frac{4\pi^2a^3}{T^2} = \frac{24\pi^3a^2}{c^2T^2(1 - e^2)} .
> $$
>
> (Both forms were checked symbolically.)
>
> **Step 6** (parts 1 and 2). Part 1: $6\pi M/p$ contains no $e$. Part 2: $\Delta\varphi/T = 6\pi M/[a(1 - e^2)T]$ with $T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/M}$ gives $\Delta\varphi/T = 3M^{3/2}a^{-5/2}(1 - e^2)^{-1}$.
>
> **Step 7** (apsidal angle). Perihelion to the next aphelion is $\psi$ from $0$ to $\pi$, i.e. $\Phi = \pi/\omega = \pi(1 + \alpha) + O(\alpha^2)$, and $2\Phi - 2\pi = 2\pi\alpha = \Delta\varphi$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The advance is a frequency mismatch: the radial motion has period $2\pi(1 + \alpha)$ in $\varphi$ while the angle returns after $2\pi$. Newton's orbit closes because the two periods are equal.
> - The secular expansion and the frequency-shifted form give the same $\Delta\varphi$; the secular form is correct for one orbit and wrong for a million.

> [!remark] Remark: What the angle φ measures
> $\varphi$ is a coordinate, so it is fair to ask whether "the perihelion returns after $2\pi + \Delta\varphi$" is a statement about the spacetime or about the chart. It is about the spacetime. The perihelion is the event of smallest areal radius $r$ along the orbit, defined without coordinates ([[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^def-c6-1-2|Def. §C6.1.2]]). The angle $\varphi$ is the parameter along the orbits of the rotational Killing vector $\partial_\varphi$, normalized so that these orbits close after $2\pi$ ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-2|Def. §C3.13.2]]); "$2\pi$" therefore means one full turn of the spherical symmetry, and the excess $\Delta\varphi$ cannot be removed by a change of chart that respects the symmetry. Far from the mass the Schwarzschild chart goes over into the inertial polar coordinates in which astronomers measure directions against the distant stars, and it is there that the advance is observed.

> [!caution] Caution: Couzens' eccentricity formula

Relatedly, 't Hooft's restored formula (12.40), $\delta\varphi = 6\pi Gm_\odot/[a(1 - \varepsilon^2)c^2]$, calls $a$ "the major axis"; it is the semi-major axis, as in [[#^thm-c6-4-2|Theorem §C6.4.2]].

## Comparison with Newtonian perturbation theory

Level B already has a formula for the perihelion shift caused by a small correction $\delta U$ to the Kepler potential ([[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-5|CM Theorem §B2.3.5]]), and it ends by remarking that general relativity supplies a correction of the form $\gamma/r^3$ with $\gamma < 0$ ([[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^rem-b2-3-2|CM §B2.3, Remark: Precession as a turning Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector]]). The theorem below makes that remark exact, and finds one trap on the way.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.4.4: Agreement with the Periapsis-Shift Formula for a Perturbed Potential
> 1. *(The radial equation in Newtonian form.)* The radial equation $\dot r^2 = E^2 - V(r)$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]) can be written
>
> $$
> \frac12\dot r^2 + U_{\text{eff}}(r) = \frac{E^2 - 1}{2}, \qquad U_{\text{eff}}(r) = -\frac{M}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2r^2} - \frac{ML^2}{r^3} ,
> $$
>
> the Newtonian energy equation for unit mass ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-3|CM Def. §B2.1.3]]) in the potential $U(r) = -M/r + \gamma/r^3$ with $\gamma = -ML^2$, at the energy $(E^2 - 1)/2$, with proper time $\tau$ in place of Newtonian time. The extra term is the relativistic correction of [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-3|Theorem §C6.3.3]].
> 2. *(Same orbits.)* The geodesic with constants $(E, L)$ traces the same curve $r(\varphi)$ as the Newtonian particle with angular momentum $L$ and energy $(E^2 - 1)/2$ in the fixed potential $-M/r + \gamma/r^3$, $\gamma$ frozen at the value $-ML^2$.
> 3. *(Same shift.)* [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-5|CM Theorem §B2.3.5]] with $\mu = 1$, force constant $M$ and $\delta U = \gamma/r^3$ gives
>
> $$
> \delta\varphi = -\frac{6\pi\mu^2 M\gamma}{L^4} = -\frac{6\pi M(-ML^2)}{L^4} = \frac{6\pi M^2}{L^2} ,
> $$
>
> the advance of [[#^thm-c6-4-2|Theorem §C6.4.2]]. The relativistic $1/r^3$ term is an extra attraction ($\gamma < 0$), and an extra attraction advances the periapsis.
> 4. *(⚑ The derivative is at fixed γ.)* The Newtonian formula differentiates with respect to $L$ at fixed potential. If $\gamma = -ML^2$ is differentiated along with $L$, the result is $2\pi M^2/L^2$, one third of the correct value.
> 5. *(Bertrand.)* $-M/r + \gamma/r^3$ is neither $-\alpha/r$ nor $\frac12Kr^2$, so by [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-4|CM Theorem §B2.3.4]] not all bound geodesics close; one closes exactly when $\Delta\varphi/2\pi$ is rational ([[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-3|CM Theorem §B2.3.3]]), otherwise it fills the annulus $r_{\min} \le r \le r_{\max}$.
>
> *Model:* as in [[#^thm-c6-4-1|Theorem §C6.4.1]]; *applies:* parts 1, 2, 4, 5 exact for equatorial geodesics; part 3 first order in $\gamma$.
>
> *Source: Hartle, §9.3, eqs. (9.52)–(9.53) (the correction "affects the orbits as a small additional $1/r^3$ term in the Newtonian potential would", p. 203); §10.4, eq. (10.20) (p. 231) · Couzens, GR1, §6.2, eqs. (6.37)–(6.38) · Landau's formula as in [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-5|CM Theorem §B2.3.5]] · part 4 worked here*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (expanding $V$). Multiply out $V = (1 - 2M/r)(1 + L^2/r^2)$:
>
> $$
> V(r) = 1 + \frac{L^2}{r^2} - \frac{2M}{r} - \frac{2ML^2}{r^3} .
> $$
>
> Insert into $\dot r^2 = E^2 - V$, subtract $1$ on both sides of $\dot r^2 + (V - 1) = E^2 - 1$ and halve:
>
> $$
> \frac12\dot r^2 + \Bigl(-\frac{M}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2r^2} - \frac{ML^2}{r^3}\Bigr) = \frac{E^2 - 1}{2} .
> $$
>
> The Newtonian effective potential of unit mass in $U(r)$ is $U + L^2/(2r^2)$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-3|CM Def. §B2.1.3]]); with $U = -M/r + \gamma/r^3$ and $\gamma = -ML^2$ it is the bracket. (Checked symbolically.) Part 1 is proved.
>
> **Step 2** (the orbit). In both theories $L = r^2\,d\varphi/ds$ with $s = \tau$ (relativity) or $s = t$ (Newton), so $dr/d\varphi = (dr/ds)/(d\varphi/ds) = (r^2/L)\,dr/ds$, and Step 1 gives in both cases
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{dr}{d\varphi}\Bigr)^2 = \frac{r^4}{L^2}\Bigl[2\cdot\frac{E^2 - 1}{2} - 2U_{\text{eff}}(r)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> The right side depends on $L$ in two places, through $L^2/(2r^2)$ and through $\gamma$. For the given orbit both have the value $L$; the Newtonian particle with the same $L$, the same energy and the potential with $\gamma$ fixed at $-ML^2$ obeys the same first-order equation with the same initial point, hence traces the same $r(\varphi)$. Part 2 is proved. (Differentiating in $\varphi$ gives $u'' + u = M/L^2 - 3\gamma u^2/L^2 = M/L^2 + 3Mu^2$, the orbit equation of [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-6|Theorem §C6.3.6]].)
>
> **Step 3** (the Newtonian formula). [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-5|CM Theorem §B2.3.5]] gives, for $\delta U = \gamma/r^3$ with $\gamma$ a constant of the potential, $\delta\varphi = -6\pi\mu^2\alpha_{\text{CM}}\gamma/L^4$, where $\alpha_{\text{CM}}$ is the strength of the Kepler term. Here $\mu = 1$, $\alpha_{\text{CM}} = M$; by Step 2 the orbit is that of the fixed potential with $\gamma = -ML^2$, so substitute that value after the formula is applied:
>
> $$
> \delta\varphi = -\frac{6\pi M\gamma}{L^4}\bigg|_{\gamma = -ML^2} = \frac{6\pi M^2L^2}{L^4} = \frac{6\pi M^2}{L^2} .
> $$
>
> Part 3 is proved. The sign: $\gamma < 0$ makes $\delta U = \gamma/r^3$ negative, deepest at small $r$, an extra attraction.
>
> **Step 4** (the trap). The general formula is $\delta\varphi = \partial_L\bigl(\frac{2\mu}{L}\int_0^\pi r^2\,\delta U\,d\varphi\bigr)$, the derivative at fixed $E$ and fixed potential. Along the unperturbed ellipse $1/r = (1 + e\cos\varphi)/p$, $p = L^2/M$:
>
> $$
> \int_0^\pi r^2\,\frac{\gamma}{r^3}\,d\varphi = \gamma\int_0^\pi\frac{1 + e\cos\varphi}{p}\,d\varphi = \frac{\pi\gamma}{p} = \frac{\pi\gamma M}{L^2} .
> $$
>
> At fixed $\gamma$: $\partial_L\bigl(\frac{2}{L}\cdot\frac{\pi\gamma M}{L^2}\bigr) = \partial_L\bigl(2\pi\gamma ML^{-3}\bigr) = -6\pi\gamma M/L^4$, as in Step 3. If instead $\gamma = -ML^2$ is inserted first, the bracket is $\frac2L\cdot\frac{\pi(-ML^2)M}{L^2} = -\frac{2\pi M^2}{L}$, and
>
> $$
> \partial_L\Bigl(-\frac{2\pi M^2}{L}\Bigr) = \frac{2\pi M^2}{L^2} = \frac13\cdot\frac{6\pi M^2}{L^2} .
> $$
>
> The formula rests on $\partial_L\sqrt{2(E - U) - L^2/r^2} = -L/(r^2\sqrt{\cdots})$, which holds only if $U$ does not contain $L$. (Both results were checked symbolically.) Part 4 is proved.
>
> **Step 5** (Bertrand). Part 5 is [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-4|CM Theorem §B2.3.4]] and [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-3|CM Theorem §B2.3.3]], part 1, applied to the Newtonian orbits of Step 2.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - For orbit shapes, general relativity acts like Newtonian gravity plus an attractive $-ML^2/r^3$ potential. Its strength depends on the orbit's own $L$: the centrifugal energy $L^2/(2r^2)$ is multiplied by $(1 - 2M/r)$, i.e. it gravitates. Theorem §C6.4.4, part 4, is the warning that such a potential must be frozen before the Newtonian machinery is used.
> - The same $1/r^3$ form arises from a solar quadrupole moment (Hartle (10.20)); orbits alone cannot tell the two apart, which is why $J_{2\odot}$ enters [[#^law-c6-4-3|Law §C6.4.3]].

*Uses:* [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-6|Theorem §C6.3.6]], [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-3|CM Def. §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-5|CM Theorem §B2.3.5]], [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-4|CM Theorem §B2.3.4]], [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-3|CM Theorem §B2.3.3]]

The perturbative results need $M/p \ll 1$. Near a black hole or a neutron star that fails, and in the figure and the animation above the first-order formula falls short of the computed advance by $16\%$ ($21.6^\circ$ against $25.6^\circ$) and $20\%$ ($27.0^\circ$ against $33.8^\circ$). For orbits close to circular the advance can be found without that assumption, by the method of [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-2|CM Theorem §B2.3.2]]: compare the radial and angular frequencies of a slightly disturbed circular orbit.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.4.5: Precession of Nearly Circular Orbits at Any Radius
> Let $r_c > 6M$ be a stable circular geodesic orbit ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]]). A slightly eccentric orbit near it oscillates in $u = 1/r$ as $\cos(k\varphi)$ with
>
> $$
> k^2 = 1 - \frac{6M}{r_c} ,
> $$
>
> so its perihelion advances per orbit by
>
> $$
> \Delta\varphi = 2\pi\Bigl[\Bigl(1 - \frac{6M}{r_c}\Bigr)^{-1/2} - 1\Bigr] = \frac{6\pi M}{r_c} + O\Bigl(\frac{M^2}{r_c^2}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> to first order in the eccentricity but exactly in $M/r_c$. As $r_c \to 6M$, the innermost stable circular orbit, $k \to 0$ and $\Delta\varphi \to \infty$: the radial oscillation becomes infinitely slow compared with the revolution. Far out the formula agrees with [[#^thm-c6-4-2|Theorem §C6.4.2]], since $r_c = p\,(1 + O(M/p))$.
>
> *Model:* as in [[#^thm-c6-4-1|Theorem §C6.4.1]], orbit near a circular one; *applies:* first order in the eccentricity, any $r_c > 6M$.
>
> *Source: Schutz, §11.1, eqs. (11.34)–(11.38) (pp. 305–306; Schutz expands about the Newtonian radius $M/\tilde J^2$ and keeps first order in $M^2/\tilde J^2$) · linearization about the exact circular orbit worked here; checked by script*

^thm-c6-4-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (circular orbit). A circular orbit has $u = u_c$ constant, $u'' = 0$, so $u_c = M/L^2 + 3Mu_c^2$.
>
> **Step 2** (linearization). Put $u = u_c + w$ into $u'' + u = M/L^2 + 3Mu^2$:
>
> $$
> w'' + u_c + w = \frac{M}{L^2} + 3M\bigl(u_c^2 + 2u_cw + w^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Subtract Step 1 and drop $3Mw^2$ (second order in the eccentricity, since $w = O(e\,u_c)$):
>
> $$
> w'' + (1 - 6Mu_c)\,w = 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (frequency). For $1 - 6Mu_c > 0$, i.e. $r_c > 6M$, the solutions are $w = A\cos\bigl(k(\varphi - \varphi_0)\bigr)$ with $k = \sqrt{1 - 6M/r_c}$. Perihelia (maxima of $u$) recur when $k\varphi$ grows by $2\pi$, i.e. every $2\pi/k$ in $\varphi$, so $\Delta\varphi = 2\pi/k - 2\pi$. For $r_c < 6M$ the coefficient is negative, $w$ grows exponentially in $\varphi$, and the circular orbit is unstable, in agreement with [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]].
>
> **Step 4** (far out). $(1 - 6M/r_c)^{-1/2} = 1 + 3M/r_c + \frac{27}{2}M^2/r_c^2 + \dots$, so $\Delta\varphi = 6\pi M/r_c + O(M^2/r_c^2)$. From Step 1, $u_c = M/L^2\,(1 + O(M^2/L^2))$, i.e. $r_c = p\,(1 + O(M/p))$, and $6\pi M/r_c = 6\pi M/p + O(M^2/p^2)$, [[#^thm-c6-4-2|Theorem §C6.4.2]] for $e \to 0$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The ratio of radial to angular frequency is $k = \omega_r/\omega_\varphi$ in the sense of [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-2|CM Theorem §B2.3.2]]: Newton's $k = 1$ for every radius becomes $\sqrt{1 - 6M/r_c}$, which is below one everywhere and reaches zero at the innermost stable orbit. The stability boundary and the precession are one fact.

A ray passes the mass once, through an angle a little larger than $\pi$, so the naive expansion that failed here over many orbits is enough there. How far does it bend, and why is the answer twice the equivalence-principle estimate? That is [[§C6.5 The Bending of Light|§C6.5]].

> *Step 1: the semi-latus rectum.*
>
> $$
> 1 - e^2 = 1 - 0.205630^2 = 0.957716, \qquad p = a(1 - e^2) = 5.7909050\times10^{10}\ \mathrm m\times0.957716 = 5.5460\times10^{10}\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> *Step 2: the small parameter.*
>
> $$
> \frac{M}{p} = \frac{1476.67\ \mathrm m}{5.5460\times10^{10}\ \mathrm m} = 2.663\times10^{-8}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{3M}{p} = 7.988\times10^{-8} ,

> *Step 3: the advance per orbit* ([[#^thm-c6-4-2|Theorem §C6.4.2]]).
>
> $$
> \Delta\varphi = \frac{6\pi M}{p} = 6\pi\times2.663\times10^{-8} = 5.0188\times10^{-7}\ \text{rad} .
> $$
>
> With $1\ \text{rad} = 206\,264.8''$: $\Delta\varphi = 5.0188\times10^{-7}\times206\,264.8'' = 0.1035''$ per orbit. The restored form $24\pi^3a^2/[c^2T^2(1 - e^2)]$, with $T$ in seconds, gives $5.0187\times10^{-7}$ rad, the same to the accuracy of Kepler's third law with these data.
>
> *Step 4: per century.* A century of $36\,525$ days holds $36\,525/87.9691 = 415.20$ orbits, so
>
> $$
> \Delta\varphi_{\text{century}} = 415.20\times0.1035'' = 42.98'' .
> $$
>
> *Step 5: what the numbers mean.* The perihelion needs $2\pi/\Delta\varphi = 1.25\times10^{7}$ orbits, $3.0\times10^{6}$ years, to turn once round. The secular term of [[#^thm-c6-4-1|Theorem §C6.4.1]] would be wrong by order one after $1/(2\pi\alpha) = 1.99\times10^{6}$ orbits. Couzens' values, $5.01\times10^{-7}$ rad and $0.103''$ per orbit and $43.0''$ per century (GR1 (6.66)–(6.67)), agree to rounding. The result is compared with observation in [[#^law-c6-4-3|Law §C6.4.3]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.2.1, eqs. (6.64)–(6.67) (p. 125) · Schutz, §11.1, eqs. (11.40)–(11.42) (p. 306; with $r = 5.55\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{km}$, which is $p$) · d'Inverno & Vickers, eq. (16.50) · Hartle, Problem 10.5 table (p. 233) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C6.4.2: Other Planets and the Binary Pulsar's Periastron Advance
> *Venus and the Earth.* With Hartle's elements and periods from Kepler's third law, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/GM_\odot}$ ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-4|CM Theorem §B2.2.4]]), [[#^thm-c6-4-2|Theorem §C6.4.2]] gives:
>
> | Body | $a$ ($10^{9}\ \mathrm m$) | $e$ | $T$ (days) | $\Delta\varphi$ per orbit | GR, per century | observed (d'Inverno, Table 16.1) |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | Mercury | $57.91$ | $0.2056$ | $87.97$ | $5.019\times10^{-7}$ rad $= 0.1035''$ | $42.98''$ | $43.1'' \pm 0.5''$ |
> | Venus | $108.21$ | $0.0068$ | $224.70$ | $2.572\times10^{-7}$ rad $= 0.0531''$ | $8.62''$ | $8.4'' \pm 4.8''$ |
> | Earth | $149.60$ | $0.0167$ | $365.26$ | $1.861\times10^{-7}$ rad $= 0.0384''$ | $3.84''$ | $5.0'' \pm 1.2''$ |
> | Icarus (minor planet) | | | | | $10.3''$ (table) | $9.8'' \pm 0.8''$ (1971) |
>
> For Venus, step by step: $1 - e^2 = 0.99995$, $p = 1.0820\times10^{11}\ \mathrm m$, $\Delta\varphi = 6\pi\times1476.67/1.0820\times10^{11} = 2.572\times10^{-7}$ rad $= 0.0531''$; $36\,525/224.70 = 162.55$ orbits per century give $8.62''$. The ratio to Mercury, $8.62/42.98 = 0.2007$, is exactly $(a_\text{V}/a_\text{M})^{-5/2}(1 - e_\text{M}^2)/(1 - e_\text{V}^2)$, [[#^thm-c6-4-2|Theorem §C6.4.2]], part 2. The predictions agree with d'Inverno's ($43.0''$, $8.6''$, $3.8''$) and 't Hooft's ($43.03''$, $8.3''$, $3.8''$). The observed values for Venus and the Earth have large errors: their orbits are nearly circular ($e < 0.02$), so the perihelion direction is poorly defined. Icarus, with $e$ close to $0.83$, is the exception d'Inverno quotes.
>
> *PSR B1913+16.* Two neutron stars, one a pulsar, orbit each other with period $P_b = 27\,906.980895\ \mathrm s$ ($7.75$ h) and eccentricity $e = 0.617132$. The test-body formula holds for a binary with $M$ replaced by the total mass $M_{\text{tot}}$ (Hartle; the two-body problem is not derived here; C8 uses only the Newtonian orbit, [[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral#^mod-c8-6-1|Model §C8.6.1]]); $M_{\text{tot}} = 2.82827\,M_\odot = 4176\ \mathrm m$. Kepler's third law with $M_{\text{tot}}$ gives the semi-major axis of the relative orbit:
>
> $$
> a = \Bigl(\frac{GM_{\text{tot}}P_b^2}{4\pi^2}\Bigr)^{1/3} = 1.9491\times10^{9}\ \mathrm m, \qquad a(1 - e^2) = 1.2068\times10^{9}\ \mathrm m, \qquad \frac{M_{\text{tot}}}{p} = 3.46\times10^{-6} ,
> $$
>
> $130$ times Mercury's $M/p$. Then
>
> $$
> \Delta\varphi = \frac{6\pi\times4176\ \mathrm m}{1.2068\times10^{9}\ \mathrm m} = 6.5234\times10^{-5}\ \text{rad per orbit}, \qquad \frac{365.25\times86\,400\ \mathrm s}{27\,906.98\ \mathrm s} = 1130.81\ \text{orbits per year} ,
> $$
>
> so the periastron advances by $1130.81\times6.5234\times10^{-5}$ rad $= 4.2266^\circ$ per year, against the observed $4.22659^\circ \pm 0.00004^\circ$ per year (Hartle (11.26); Schutz quotes $4.2261^\circ \pm 0.0007^\circ$, d'Inverno $4.2^\circ$). Per unit time this is $3.5\times10^{4}$ times Mercury's rate. Schutz's first estimate, $M = 1.4\,M_\odot$ and a circular orbit of radius $1.2\times10^{9}\ \mathrm m$, gives $3.25\times10^{-5}$ rad per orbit, $2.10^\circ$ per year. His radius happens to be close to $p$ ($1.2\times10^{9}\ \mathrm m = 0.994\,p$), so the missing factor $2.02$ is the total mass, $2.82827\,M_\odot$ instead of $1.4\,M_\odot$.
>
> The agreement is not an independent test. In practice the advance is one of the measurements from which $M_{\text{tot}}$ is determined (Hartle §11.3); the test of general relativity comes from combining it with further relativistic effects in the pulse timing, the Einstein delay and the decay of the orbit by gravitational radiation ([[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral#^law-c8-6-6|Law §C8.6.6]]). The numbers are those of Hartle's text (2003).
>
> *Source: d'Inverno & Vickers, §16.6, Table 16.1 and text (pp. 302–303) · Hartle, Problem 10.5 table (p. 233); §11.3, eqs. (11.26), (11.28) and text (pp. 251–253) · Schutz, §11.1, "Binary pulsars" (p. 307) · 't Hooft, Fig. 5 (p. 54) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C6.4.3: A Strongly Relativistic Orbit Integrated Numerically
> Take $M = 1$, $L = 30$ (so $p = L^2/M = 900$, $M/p = 1/900$, $4.2\times10^{4}$ times Mercury's) and $e = 0.2$, and integrate the exact orbit equation $u'' + u = M/L^2 + 3Mu^2$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-6|Theorem §C6.3.6]]) from the perihelion $u = (1 + e)/p$, $u' = 0$, i.e. $r = 750\,M$. The orbit runs between $r_{\min} = 750.0\,M$ and $r_{\max} = 1115.5\,M$, far outside $6M$.
>
> *Result.* The next maximum of $u$ (located by event detection, relative tolerance $10^{-12}$) lies at $\varphi = 2\pi + 0.0211$:
>
> $$
> \Delta\varphi_{\text{num}} = 0.02112, \qquad \Delta\varphi_{\text{formula}} = \frac{6\pi M}{p} = \frac{6\pi}{900} = 0.02094 .
> $$
>
> The difference is $1.77\times10^{-4}$, $0.85\%$ of the advance.
>
> *Is the difference second order?* Repeat with $M/p$ halved twice ($e = 0.2$ throughout):
>
> | $M/p$ | $\Delta\varphi_{\text{num}} - 6\pi M/p$ | ratio to the next |
> |---|---|---|
> | $1/900$ | $1.774\times10^{-4}$ | $4.02$ |
> | $1/1800$ | $4.414\times10^{-5}$ | $4.01$ |
> | $1/3600$ | $1.101\times10^{-5}$ | |
>
> Halving $M/p$ divides the difference by $4$: it is of order $(M/p)^2$. The first-order formula has no first-order error, which is what [[#^thm-c6-4-2|Theorem §C6.4.2]] claims; the remainder is the $O(\alpha^2)$ term.
>
> *Near-circular check.* For $L = 30M$ the stable circular orbit is at $u_c = [1 - \sqrt{1 - 12M^2/L^2}]/(6M)$, $r_c = 896.99\,M$ (solving Step 1 of [[#^der-c6-4-5|the derivation of Theorem §C6.4.5]]), and [[#^thm-c6-4-5|Theorem §C6.4.5]] gives $2\pi[(1 - 6M/r_c)^{-1/2} - 1] = 0.02112$; an orbit started at $u = 1.0001\,u_c$ and integrated gives the same to $2\times10^{-5}$.
>
> *Stronger fields.* The orbit of the figure ($M/p = 1/50$, $e = 0.4$, $r_{\min} = 35.7\,M$) advances by $25.6^\circ$ against $21.6^\circ$ from $6\pi M/p$, the orbit of the animation ($M/p = 1/40$, $e = 0.5$, $r_{\min} = 26.7\,M$) by $33.8^\circ$ against $27.0^\circ$: once $M/p$ is a few per cent, the formula is only a first estimate.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.2.1 (the orbit equation (6.51)) · Hartle, §9.3, Fig. 9.5 (numerical evaluation of the exact advance, p. 202) · integration worked here; numbers checked by script*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The level-B home of everything Newtonian used here is [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession|CM §B2.3]]: its animation of open rosettes in power-law forces and the rosette of a $-1/r^3$ correction (Example §B2.3.1, which is the same mathematics with $r_0^2$ in place of $L^2$) are the Newtonian pictures of [[#^thm-c6-4-4|Theorem §C6.4.4]]. Level A states the anomaly as one of the departures from Newton ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton#^law-a8-5-1|CM Law §A8.5.1]]).
> - The secular term and its removal are the resonance of an undamped oscillator ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-3|WO Theorem §B1.3.3]]) and the standard cure of shifting the frequency (Lindstedt's method; 't Hooft calls the problem "well known in mathematical physics"). Mathematical Methods (planned) is the natural home of the method.
> - A turning perihelion is a turning Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-5|CM Theorem §B2.2.5]]). In hydrogen the same vector is behind the degeneracy of levels of different $\ell$; a departure from $1/r$ splits them ([[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^rem-c8-1-6|QM Remark: What this proves, and the classical orbit]]).
> - The bound on the orbit used against the secular term, and the circular orbits of [[#^thm-c6-4-5|Theorem §C6.4.5]], come from the effective potential of [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^def-c6-3-1|Def. §C6.3.1]]; the instability of circular orbits inside $6M$ and the infinitely slow radial oscillation at $6M$ are one fact.
> - Light obeys the same equation without $M/L^2$ ([[§C6.5 The Bending of Light|§C6.5]]); binary pulsars and the decay of their orbits by gravitational waves belong to C8 ([[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral#^law-c8-6-6|Law §C8.6.6]]).
