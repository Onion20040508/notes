[[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon|§C7.2]] ended with two charts that both reach through $r = 2M$. The ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein chart $(v, r)$ continues the exterior, region I, through the future horizon into the black hole ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]). The outgoing chart $(u, r)$ continues it through a different surface into the white hole ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-4|Theorem §C7.2.4]], [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-5|Def. §C7.2.5]]). This section asks: *is there one chart that contains both, and is anything still missing once it is found?*

Each chart by itself is still incomplete, and this can be computed. Follow an outgoing radial light ray of region I backwards in time, in the ingoing chart. Along it $u = t - r_\ast$ is constant ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]), so

$$
v = t + r_\ast = u + 2r_\ast(r) = u + 2r + 4M\ln\Bigl(\frac{r}{2M} - 1\Bigr) \;\xrightarrow{\;r \to 2M^+\;}\; -\infty .
$$

The ray leaves the chart $(v, r)$ as $v \to -\infty$. Yet on a radial null geodesic ($L = 0$, $\varepsilon = 0$ in [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]) the radial equation reads $(dr/d\lambda)^2 = E^2$, so $r$ itself is an affine parameter, $\lambda = (r - 2M)/E + \text{const}$. Traced back from $r = 3M$, the ray uses up only the affine length $M/E$ before it reaches the edge of the chart, and the curvature there is finite ($48M^2/r^6 \to 3/(4M^4)$, [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-7|Theorem §C6.2.7]]). The outgoing chart has the mirror defect: an ingoing ray of region I leaves it to the future at $u \to +\infty$ after finite affine length. A chart that treats both families of rays alike is the double-null chart $(u, v)$. In it the Schwarzschild metric becomes (Steps 1–3 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]])

$$
ds^2 = -\Bigl(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\Bigr)du\,dv + r^2d\Omega^2, \qquad 1 - \frac{2M}{r} = \frac{2M}{r}\,e^{-r/2M}\;e^{-u/4M}\;e^{v/4M} .
$$

This chart covers region I only. The future horizon sits at $u = +\infty$ and the past horizon at $v = -\infty$, and the metric coefficient vanishes there through the two exponentials. The cure suggests itself: take $e^{-u/4M}$ and $e^{v/4M}$ as the new coordinates.

The section builds the answer in order. The new coordinates are defined first ([[#^def-c7-3-1|Def. §C7.3.1]]) and the metric is derived in them, step by step ([[#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]]). The diagram that results is read like a Minkowski diagram ([[#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]]). It holds four regions: two exteriors, the black hole and the white hole, bounded by a spacelike singularity ([[#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]). The slice $t = 0$ joins the two exteriors through a throat, the Einstein–Rosen bridge ([[#^def-c7-3-2|Def. §C7.3.2]]). No observer can cross it ([[#^thm-c7-3-4|Theorem §C7.3.4]]), and its geometry can be drawn as a surface in flat space ([[#^thm-c7-3-5|Theorem §C7.3.5]]). The section closes with what is physical ([[#^rem-c7-3-1|Remark: Which parts are physical]]).

**Setup.** The vacuum Schwarzschild solution with $M > 0$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]), taken as a solution of the vacuum equations everywhere: no star, no collapse. Region I is the exterior $r > 2M$, $t \in \mathbf R$, and region II the interior $0 < r < 2M$ in Schwarzschild coordinates, as in [[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities|§C7.1]]. Spherical symmetry is kept throughout: $\theta$, $\varphi$ are never changed, and each point of a diagram in the radial plane stands for a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$, $r$ the areal radius ([[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^def-c6-1-2|Def. §C6.1.2]]). All results are exact for this solution. Which parts survive when a real star collapses is the subject of the closing remark. The numbers in the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$ (restore: [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]; here $M \to GM/c^2$, $t \to ct$, while $U$, $V$, $T$, $X$ are dimensionless); $f \equiv 1 - 2M/r$; tortoise coordinate $r_\ast = r + 2M\ln|r/2M - 1|$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]), $u = t - r_\ast$, $v = t + r_\ast$; Kruskal coordinates as Couzens (null $U$, $V$; $T = (U + V)/2$, $X = (V - U)/2$); other books' names in [[#^cau-c7-3-2|Caution: Names for the Kruskal coordinates]]. $dU\,dV$ is the symmetrized product $\tfrac12(dU \otimes dV + dV \otimes dU)$. Level B and d'Inverno & Vickers use $+{-}{-}{-}$.

## Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates and the metric

> [!definition] Definition §C7.3.1: Kruskal–Szekeres Coordinates
> *Structure.* A chart ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]) $(U, V, \theta, \varphi)$ on region I, obtained from the double-null chart $(u, v, \theta, \varphi)$, $u = t - r_\ast$, $v = t + r_\ast$, by changing each null coordinate separately:
>
> $$
> U \equiv -e^{-u/4M}, \qquad V \equiv e^{v/4M}, \qquad \theta,\ \varphi\ \text{unchanged} .
> $$
>
> Both maps $u \mapsto U$ and $v \mapsto V$ are smooth and strictly increasing with smooth inverses $u = -4M\ln(-U)$, $v = 4M\ln V$, so this is a change of chart ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]). Region I, $u, v \in \mathbf R$, becomes the quadrant $U < 0 < V$. The future horizon $u = +\infty$ is pushed to $U = 0$ and the past horizon $v = -\infty$ to $V = 0$.
>
> *Null coordinates.* $U$ is constant along each outgoing radial light ray and $V$ along each ingoing one, as $u$ and $v$ are ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]).
>
> *Time and space combinations.* For drawing,
>
> $$
> T \equiv \tfrac12(U + V), \qquad X \equiv \tfrac12(V - U), \qquad\text{so}\qquad U = T - X, \quad V = T + X .
> $$
>
> The **Kruskal diagram** is the $(X, T)$ plane with $T$ vertical; the $U$ and $V$ axes are its diagonals.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.3.1, eq. (6.104), p. 137 (PDF 138) (definition); p. 138 (PDF 139) ($T$, $X$) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.2, eqs. (18.6)–(18.7), p. 345 (PDF 360) (Kruskal's choice, scaled differently) · 't Hooft, ch. 11, eq. (11.44), p. 50 (PDF 51)*

> [!caution] Caution: Names for the Kruskal coordinates
> The books use different letters and scales for the same chart:
> - **Couzens** (followed here): null $U = -e^{-u/4M}$, $V = e^{v/4M}$; $T = \tfrac12(U + V)$, $X = \tfrac12(V - U)$.
> - **Schutz** (11.65)–(11.66) writes $u$ for our $X$ and $v$ for our $T$ (not the null $u$, $v$ of Eddington–Finkelstein), with metric $-(32M^3/r)e^{-r/2M}(dv^2 - du^2)$ (11.67).
> - **Hartle** (12.13) writes $U$ for our $X$ (spacelike) and $V$ for our $T$ (timelike), so his "$U$" and "$V$" are not null. His Fig. 12.6 caption says "the horizon at $r = M$"; the figure itself labels it $r = 2M$, which is correct.
> - **d'Inverno & Vickers** (18.6)–(18.8): $v' = e^{v/4m}$, $w' = -e^{-w/4m}$ with their $r_\ast = r + 2m\ln(r - 2m)$, which differs from ours by the constant $2m\ln 2m$. Hence $v' = \sqrt{2M}\,V$, $w' = \sqrt{2M}\,U$, $(t', x') = \sqrt{2M}\,(T, X)$, and their conformal factor is $16m^2e^{-r/2m}/r$; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$.
> - **'t Hooft** (11.44): $x = V$, $y = -U$, so $xy = -UV = (r/2M - 1)e^{r/2M}$ and region I is $x, y > 0$.
>

Now the centrepiece: the metric in these coordinates. The derivation starts from the Schwarzschild form, passes through the double-null form of the opening, and shows where the zero of $f$ goes.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.1: The Kruskal Metric
> On region I, in the coordinates of [[#^def-c7-3-1|Def. §C7.3.1]]:
> 1. **The two relations.**
>
> $$
> UV = \Bigl(1 - \frac{r}{2M}\Bigr)e^{r/2M} = T^2 - X^2, \qquad \frac UV = -e^{-t/2M} .
> $$
>
> 2. **The metric.**
>
> $$
> ds^2 = -\frac{32M^3}{r}\,e^{-r/2M}\,dU\,dV + r^2d\Omega^2 = \frac{32M^3}{r}\,e^{-r/2M}\bigl(-dT^2 + dX^2\bigr) + r^2d\Omega^2 ,
> $$
>
>    with $r = r(U, V)$ from part 3. Restored: $ds^2 = -(32G^3M^3/c^6r)\,e^{-c^2r/2GM}\,dU\,dV + r^2d\Omega^2$.
> 3. **$r$ as a function of $UV$.** $G(r) \equiv (1 - r/2M)e^{r/2M}$ is a smooth, strictly decreasing bijection of $(0, \infty)$ onto $(-\infty, 1)$, with $G(2M) = 0$. So $r(U, V) = G^{-1}(UV)$ is a smooth function on the whole set $UV < 1$, namely
>
> $$
> r = 2M\Bigl[1 + W_0\Bigl(-\frac{UV}{e}\Bigr)\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
>    $W_0$ the principal branch of the Lambert function ($W e^W = z$).
> 4. **Nothing degenerates at $r = 2M$.** The conformal factor $F(r) \equiv (32M^3/r)\,e^{-r/2M}$ is smooth and positive for every $r > 0$, with $F(2M) = 16M^2/e$. The components $g_{UV} = g_{VU} = -F/2$, $g_{\theta\theta} = r^2$, $g_{\varphi\varphi} = r^2\sin^2\theta$ have determinant $-\tfrac14F^2r^4\sin^2\theta \ne 0$. The formula of part 2 therefore defines a Lorentzian metric wherever $UV < 1$, including $U = 0$ or $V = 0$ ($r = 2M$), where the Schwarzschild and double-null charts fail.
>
> *Model:* the vacuum Schwarzschild metric, $M > 0$, region I; *applies:* exactly. The extension of parts 3–4 beyond region I is [[#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]].
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.3.1, eqs. (6.103)–(6.107), p. 137 (PDF 138) · Schutz §11.2, eqs. (11.67)–(11.68), p. 320 (PDF 338); Exercise 11.26 ("Prove Eq. 11.67"), p. 357 (PDF 375) · Hartle §12.3, eqs. (12.14)–(12.15), p. 270 (PDF 271); Problem 12.20 · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.2, eqs. (18.1)–(18.9), pp. 343–345 (PDF 358–360) (signature $+{-}{-}{-}$) · every step and the Lambert form checked by script; Ricci-flatness of the metric with this $r(U, V)$ checked numerically, inside the horizon included*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (null coordinates). By [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]], $dr_\ast = dr/f$. From $u = t - r_\ast$, $v = t + r_\ast$:
>
> $$
> t = \tfrac12(u + v), \quad r_\ast = \tfrac12(v - u), \qquad dt = \tfrac12(du + dv), \quad dr = f\,dr_\ast = \tfrac12 f\,(dv - du) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (double-null form). Insert into the radial part of the Schwarzschild metric ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]):
>
> $$
> -f\,dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{f} = -\frac f4\,(du + dv)^2 + \frac{f^2}{4f}\,(dv - du)^2 = -\frac f4\Bigl[(du + dv)^2 - (dv - du)^2\Bigr] = -\frac f4\cdot 4\,du\,dv = -f\,du\,dv ,
> $$
>
> using $(a + b)^2 - (b - a)^2 = 4ab$. The angular part $r^2d\Omega^2$ is untouched. This is Couzens' (6.103).
>
> **Step 3** (where the zero of $f$ sits). Exponentiate the tortoise coordinate on region I, where $r/2M - 1 > 0$:
>
> $$
> e^{r_\ast/2M} = e^{r/2M}\Bigl(\frac{r}{2M} - 1\Bigr) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f = \frac{r - 2M}{r} = \frac{2M}{r}\Bigl(\frac{r}{2M} - 1\Bigr) = \frac{2M}{r}\,e^{-r/2M}\,e^{r_\ast/2M} .
> $$
>
> With $r_\ast/2M = (v - u)/4M$ from Step 1,
>
> $$
> -f\,du\,dv = -\frac{2M}{r}\,e^{-r/2M}\,\bigl(e^{-u/4M}du\bigr)\bigl(e^{v/4M}dv\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The prefactor $(2M/r)e^{-r/2M}$ is positive and finite at $r = 2M$. All of the zero of $f$ is in $e^{-u/4M}$ (which $\to 0$ as $u \to +\infty$) and $e^{v/4M}$ (which $\to 0$ as $v \to -\infty$).
>
> **Step 4** (the differentials of $U$ and $V$). By the chain rule for differentials of functions ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^def-c2-2-6|Def. §C2.2.6]]),
>
> $$
> dU = d\bigl(-e^{-u/4M}\bigr) = \frac{1}{4M}\,e^{-u/4M}\,du, \qquad dV = d\bigl(e^{v/4M}\bigr) = \frac{1}{4M}\,e^{v/4M}\,dv ,
> $$
>
> so $e^{-u/4M}du = 4M\,dU$ and $e^{v/4M}dv = 4M\,dV$.
>
> **Step 5** (substitute). Step 3 becomes
>
> $$
> -f\,du\,dv = -\frac{2M}{r}\,e^{-r/2M}\cdot 4M\,dU\cdot 4M\,dV = -\frac{32M^3}{r}\,e^{-r/2M}\,dU\,dV .
> $$
>
> With the angular part this is the first form of part 2 (Couzens' (6.107)).
>
> **Step 6** (the relations). Multiply and divide the definitions:
>
> $$
> UV = -e^{-u/4M}e^{v/4M} = -e^{(v - u)/4M} = -e^{r_\ast/2M} = -\Bigl(\frac{r}{2M} - 1\Bigr)e^{r/2M} = \Bigl(1 - \frac{r}{2M}\Bigr)e^{r/2M} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \frac UV = -e^{-u/4M}e^{-v/4M} = -e^{-(u + v)/4M} = -e^{-2t/4M} = -e^{-t/2M} ,
> $$
>
> using Step 3 for $e^{r_\ast/2M}$ and Step 1 for $u + v = 2t$. This is part 1 (Couzens' (6.105)–(6.106)).
>
> **Step 7** ($T$ and $X$). From $U = T - X$, $V = T + X$: $UV = T^2 - X^2$, and
>
> $$
> dU\,dV = (dT - dX)(dT + dX) = dT^2 + dT\,dX - dX\,dT - dX^2 = dT^2 - dX^2 ,
> $$
>
> since the symmetrized product is commutative, $dT\,dX = dX\,dT$. Hence $-F\,dU\,dV = F(-dT^2 + dX^2)$: the second form of part 2.
>
> **Step 8** ($r$ as a function). Differentiate $G(r) = (1 - r/2M)e^{r/2M}$:
>
> $$
> G'(r) = -\frac{1}{2M}e^{r/2M} + \Bigl(1 - \frac{r}{2M}\Bigr)\frac{1}{2M}e^{r/2M} = -\frac{r}{4M^2}\,e^{r/2M} < 0 \quad (r > 0) .
> $$
>
> So $G$ is strictly decreasing, from $G(0^+) = 1$ through $G(2M) = 0$ to $G \to -\infty$ as $r \to \infty$: a bijection $(0, \infty) \to (-\infty, 1)$. Its inverse is differentiable with $(G^{-1})' = 1/(G' \circ G^{-1})$ ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^thm-29-10|451 Thm. §29.10]]). The right-hand side is again differentiable, and repeating the argument shows that $G^{-1}$ is smooth. For the explicit form put $w = r/2M - 1$: $UV = G = -w\,e^{w + 1}$, i.e. $w\,e^w = -UV/e$, so $w = W_0(-UV/e)$, the branch with $w > -1$ (that is, $r > 0$). Part 3.
>
> **Step 9** (nondegeneracy). $F(r) = 32M^3e^{-r/2M}/r$ is a product of smooth positive functions on $r > 0$, and $F(2M) = (32M^3/2M)\,e^{-1} = 16M^2/e$. In the order $(U, V, \theta, \varphi)$, $-F\,dU\,dV = -\tfrac F2(dU \otimes dV + dV \otimes dU)$ gives the matrix with $g_{UV} = g_{VU} = -F/2$ and $g_{UU} = g_{VV} = 0$. Its $2 \times 2$ radial block has determinant $0\cdot 0 - (F/2)^2 = -F^2/4$, and the angular block $r^4\sin^2\theta$. The product $-\tfrac14F^2r^4\sin^2\theta$ vanishes only at the poles $\theta = 0, \pi$ (the usual failure of spherical coordinates) and at $r = 0$, never at $r = 2M$. Part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - The degeneracy of the Schwarzschild metric at $r = 2M$ is the factor $e^{-u/4M}e^{v/4M}$ in $f$ (Step 3). It belongs to the labels $u$, $v$, which run off to infinity at the horizons, and the exponentials in the definition of $U$, $V$ absorb it exactly.
> - The rate $1/4M$ is forced. With $U = -e^{-\kappa u}$, $V = e^{\kappa v}$ the same steps give $e^{(v - u)/4M}du\,dv = (-UV)^{1/(4M\kappa) - 1}\,dU\,dV/\kappa^2$, which vanishes or diverges at $UV = 0$ unless $\kappa = 1/4M$.
> - $r$ depends on $U$ and $V$ only through the product $UV$, and $t$ only through the ratio $U/V$. This is what makes the diagram easy to read ([[#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]]).

## Reading the Kruskal diagram

The metric $F(-dT^2 + dX^2) + r^2d\Omega^2$ differs from the Minkowski metric in the radial plane only by the positive factor $F$. A positive factor does not change which directions are null, timelike or spacelike. So the causal structure can be read off the $(X, T)$ plane as in special relativity. What is new is where the Schwarzschild labels $r$ and $t$ sit in that plane.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.2: Radial Light Rays Are 45° Lines; r = const Are Hyperbolae; t = const Are Rays through the Origin
> In the Kruskal metric ([[#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]]), at fixed $\theta$, $\varphi$:
> 1. **Light cones.** The radial null curves are $U = \text{const}$ (outgoing) and $V = \text{const}$ (ingoing), i.e. the lines $T = \pm X + \text{const}$ at $45°$. A radial direction $(dX, dT)$ is timelike exactly when $|dT| > |dX|$. The light cone at every event is drawn as the cone of special relativity ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]).
> 2. **$r = \text{const}$.** These are the curves $UV = G(r)$:
>
> $$
> X^2 - T^2 = \Bigl(\frac{r}{2M} - 1\Bigr)e^{r/2M} ,
> $$
>
>    hyperbolae with the diagonals $X = \pm T$ as asymptotes. For $r > 2M$ they open sideways and are timelike curves, for $r < 2M$ they open up or down and are spacelike, and $r = 2M$ is the pair of null lines $UV = 0$.
> 3. **$t = \text{const}$** (region I). $U/V = -e^{-t/2M}$ is equivalent to
>
> $$
> T = \tanh\Bigl(\frac{t}{4M}\Bigr)X ,
> $$
>
>    a straight line through the origin with slope between $-1$ and $1$. As $t \to +\infty$ the line tends to $T = X$, the half-line $U = 0$, $V > 0$; as $t \to -\infty$ it tends to $T = -X$, the half-line $V = 0$, $U < 0$. All of them meet at $U = V = 0$.
> 4. **⚑ Schwarzschild time translation is a boost.** The Killing vector $\partial_t$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^rem-c6-2-1|§C6.2, Remark: The Killing vectors of the Schwarzschild metric]]) is
>
> $$
> \partial_t = \frac{1}{4M}\bigl(-U\,\partial_U + V\,\partial_V\bigr) = \frac{1}{4M}\bigl(X\,\partial_T + T\,\partial_X\bigr) ,
> $$
>
>    the generator of Lorentz boosts of the $(X, T)$ plane, scaled by $1/4M$. A shift $t \to t + \Delta t$ multiplies $V$ by $e^{\Delta t/4M}$ and $U$ by $e^{-\Delta t/4M}$. Its norm is $g(\partial_t, \partial_t) = -f$, as in Schwarzschild coordinates. On the future horizon $U = 0$, $V$ is an affine parameter of the generators and grows exponentially in the Killing parameter, at the rate of the surface gravity $1/4M$ ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^thm-c10-5-5|Theorem §C10.5.5]], part 3).
> 5. **Time orientation.** $\partial_T$ is timelike everywhere ($g_{TT} = -F < 0$) and $g(\partial_T, \partial_t) = -FX/4M < 0$ on region I. So $\partial_T$ is a time orientation ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]) that agrees with the exterior's: the future is up in the diagram.
>
> *Model:* the Kruskal metric; *applies:* exactly. Parts 1, 2, 5 hold wherever $UV < 1$ ([[#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]); parts 3–4 as stated on region I, with the other regions in [[#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]].
>

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (null curves). For a radial curve $d\theta = d\varphi = 0$, and $ds^2 = 0$ reads $-F\,dU\,dV = 0$. Since $F > 0$, $dU = 0$ or $dV = 0$. In $T$, $X$: $U = T - X = \text{const}$ is $T = X + \text{const}$, and $V = T + X = \text{const}$ is $T = -X + \text{const}$. A radial displacement is timelike when $F(-dT^2 + dX^2) < 0$, i.e. $dT^2 > dX^2$. Part 1.
>
> **Step 2** ($r = \text{const}$). By [[#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], parts 1 and 3, $r$ is constant exactly where $UV = T^2 - X^2$ is constant, with $X^2 - T^2 = -G(r) = (r/2M - 1)e^{r/2M}$. For $r > 2M$ the right side $a^2 > 0$: $X = \pm a\cosh s$, $T = a\sinh s$, with tangent $(dX, dT) \propto (\pm\sinh s, \cosh s)$ and $dT^2 - dX^2 = a^2(\cosh^2 s - \sinh^2 s)\,ds^2 > 0$: timelike. For $r < 2M$ the right side is $-a^2 < 0$: $T = \pm a\cosh s$, $X = a\sinh s$, with $dX^2 - dT^2 > 0$: spacelike. For $r = 2M$, $X^2 = T^2$. Part 2.
>
> **Step 3** ($t = \text{const}$). With $U = T - X$, $V = T + X$ and $k \equiv e^{-t/2M}$, the relation $U/V = -k$ gives $T - X = -k(T + X)$, so $T(1 + k) = X(1 - k)$ and
>
> $$
> T = \frac{1 - e^{-t/2M}}{1 + e^{-t/2M}}\,X = \frac{e^{t/4M} - e^{-t/4M}}{e^{t/4M} + e^{-t/4M}}\,X = \tanh\Bigl(\frac{t}{4M}\Bigr)X ,
> $$
>
> after multiplying numerator and denominator by $e^{t/4M}$. Since $\tanh \to \pm1$ as $t \to \pm\infty$, part 3 follows.
>
> **Step 4** ($\partial_t$ in Kruskal coordinates). At fixed $r$, $\theta$, $\varphi$ the Kruskal coordinates depend on $t$ through $u = t - r_\ast$ and $v = t + r_\ast$, so by the chain rule ([[§C2.2 Tangent Vectors and One-Forms#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]])
>
> $$
> \partial_t = \frac{\partial U}{\partial t}\,\partial_U + \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\,\partial_V = \frac{1}{4M}e^{-u/4M}\,\partial_U + \frac{1}{4M}e^{v/4M}\,\partial_V = \frac{1}{4M}\bigl(-U\,\partial_U + V\,\partial_V\bigr) .
> $$
>
> With $\partial_U = \tfrac12(\partial_T - \partial_X)$ and $\partial_V = \tfrac12(\partial_T + \partial_X)$ (from $T = \tfrac12(U + V)$, $X = \tfrac12(V - U)$),
>
> $$
> \partial_t = \frac{1}{8M}\bigl[(V - U)\,\partial_T + (U + V)\,\partial_X\bigr] = \frac{1}{4M}\bigl(X\,\partial_T + T\,\partial_X\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The field $X\partial_T + T\partial_X$ is tangent to the hyperbolae $X^2 - T^2 = \text{const}$ (it annihilates $X^2 - T^2$: $X\cdot(-2T) + T\cdot 2X = 0$), the generator of boosts in Minkowski spacetime ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^ex-c3-13-1|Example §C3.13.1]]). Its norm: $g(\partial_t, \partial_t) = 2g_{UV}\,(-U/4M)(V/4M) = 2(-F/2)(-UV)/16M^2 = F\,UV/16M^2$. With $F = (32M^3/r)e^{-r/2M}$ and $UV = (1 - r/2M)e^{r/2M}$ this is $(2M/r)(1 - r/2M) = 2M/r - 1 = -f$. Part 4.
>
> **Step 5** (time orientation). $g(\partial_T, \partial_T) = -F$. By Step 4, $g(\partial_T, \partial_t) = \tfrac{1}{4M}\bigl(X\,g_{TT} + T\,g_{TX}\bigr) = -FX/4M$, negative for $X > 0$, which holds on region I ($V > 0 > U$ gives $X = \tfrac12(V - U) > 0$). Since $\partial_t$ is the future-pointing static direction of region I, $\partial_T$ selects the same future. Part 5.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Near $U = V = 0$ the Kruskal plane is a Minkowski plane in which the static observers are the boosted hyperbolae and $t$ is the rapidity divided by $4M$. This is the picture of uniformly accelerated observers in flat spacetime ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$): the horizons are the asymptotes of their hyperbolae, which they never cross ([[#^ex-c7-3-2|Example §C7.3.2]]).

## The maximal extension

So far $U < 0 < V$. But the metric of Theorem §C7.3.1 makes sense for every $(U, V)$ with $UV < 1$. The quadrants $U > 0$ or $V < 0$ are new territory, and the following theorem says what they are.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.3: The Maximal Extension Has Four Regions
> Let $\mathcal K \equiv \{(U, V) \in \mathbf R^2 : UV < 1\} \times S^2$ carry the metric of [[#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], part 2, with $r(U, V)$ of part 3: the **Kruskal spacetime**, time-oriented by $\partial_T$. Then:
> 1. **A vacuum spacetime.** The metric is smooth, Lorentzian and Ricci-flat on all of $\mathcal K$, and region I is the quadrant $U < 0 < V$.
> 2. **Four regions** (figure below):
>    - **I** ($U < 0 < V$): the exterior, $r > 2M$.
>    - **II** ($U > 0$, $V > 0$): $0 < r < 2M$. Together with I and the future horizon $U = 0 < V$ it is the ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein spacetime $N$, through $V = e^{v/4M}$, $U = G(r)\,e^{-v/4M}$. So II is the **black hole** of [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]].
>    - **IV** ($U < 0$, $V < 0$): $0 < r < 2M$. Together with I it is the outgoing spacetime $N'$, through $U = -e^{-u/4M}$, $V = -G(r)\,e^{u/4M}$. So IV is the **white hole** of [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-5|Def. §C7.2.5]].
>    - **III** ($V < 0 < U$): $r > 2M$. The reflection $(U, V) \mapsto (V, U)$, i.e. $X \mapsto -X$, is an isometry of $\mathcal K$ that preserves the time orientation and maps I onto III. So III is a **second exterior**, a full copy of region I with its own spatial infinity. ⚑ Its Schwarzschild time, defined by $U = e^{-u/4M}$, $V = -e^{v/4M}$ (Couzens' (6.110)), increases toward the past: the reflection carries $t$ to $-t$.
> 3. **Horizons.** The surfaces $U = 0$ and $V = 0$ are where $r = 2M$. Both are null hypersurfaces. The **future horizon** $U = 0$, $V > 0$ separates I from II; the **past horizon** $V = 0$, $U < 0$ separates IV from I. They cross at the **bifurcation sphere** $U = V = 0$, a 2-sphere of area $4\pi(2M)^2 = 16\pi M^2$.
> 4. **Singularity.** $UV \to 1$ is $r \to 0$, where $K = 48M^2/r^6 \to \infty$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-7|Theorem §C6.2.7]]). The hyperbola $T^2 - X^2 = 1$ has two branches, $T = +\sqrt{1 + X^2}$ (the future boundary of II) and $T = -\sqrt{1 + X^2}$ (the past boundary of IV). Each is spacelike: the singularity is a moment, not a place ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]).
> 5. **Nothing more to add.** Every radial null geodesic of $\mathcal K$, followed in either direction, either runs to $r \to \infty$ with unbounded affine parameter or ends on $UV = 1$ after finite affine parameter, where the curvature diverges. Kruskal's result, quoted by d'Inverno & Vickers, is that the same holds for every geodesic: $\mathcal K$ is the **maximal** extension of the Schwarzschild solution, and it is unique. Extensions and inextendible spacetimes are defined in [[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^def-c10-4-7|Def. §C10.4.7]]; being inextendible and geodesically incomplete, $\mathcal K$ is a singular spacetime ([[§C10.4 Asymptotic Flatness and Singularities#^thm-c10-4-1|Theorem §C10.4.1]]).
>
> *Model:* the vacuum Schwarzschild solution, $M > 0$, extended as far as the metric allows; *applies:* exactly. Part 5 beyond radial null geodesics is quoted; the typed sources give no proof.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.3.1, pp. 137–139 (PDF 138–140), eqs. (6.107)–(6.110), Fig. 23 ("The Kruskal spacetime is the maximal extension of the Schwarzschild solution"; horizons $U = 0$ or $V = 0$; singularity $UV = 1$; the left quadrant "another copy of the black hole exterior") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.1–§18.2, pp. 343–345 (PDF 358–360), Fig. 18.1 (definition of maximal; "Kruskal has found the maximal analytic extension … and, moreover, this extension is unique"; regions I, II, I′, II′) · Schutz §11.2, Fig. 11.12, pp. 321–322 (PDF 339–340) · Hartle §12.3, Box 12.4, p. 273 (PDF 274) · regions, EF pull-backs and Ricci-flatness checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (smooth and Lorentzian on $\mathcal K$). By [[#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], parts 3–4, $r(U, V)$ and $F(r)$ are smooth on $UV < 1$, $F > 0$, and the determinant never vanishes away from the poles of the sphere. The radial block $-F\,dU\,dV$ has one negative and one positive direction ($\partial_T$, $\partial_X$), and the sphere adds two positive ones: signature $(-, +, +, +)$ at every point.
>
> **Step 2** (region II is the ingoing interior). On $V > 0$ define $v \equiv 4M\ln V$ and keep $r = r(U, V)$, so that $V = e^{v/4M}$ and $U = G(r)\,e^{-v/4M}$. Since $\partial r/\partial U = V/G'(r) \neq 0$, $(v, r, \theta, \varphi)$ is a chart on $V > 0$ with $r \in (0, \infty)$. Differentiate:
>
> $$
> dV = \frac{1}{4M}e^{v/4M}dv, \qquad dU = e^{-v/4M}\Bigl[G'(r)\,dr - \frac{G(r)}{4M}\,dv\Bigr] = e^{-v/4M}e^{r/2M}\Bigl[-\frac{r}{4M^2}\,dr - \frac{1}{4M}\Bigl(1 - \frac{r}{2M}\Bigr)dv\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Multiply, using $e^{-v/4M}e^{v/4M} = 1$:
>
> $$
> dU\,dV = \frac{e^{r/2M}}{4M}\Bigl[-\frac{r}{4M^2}\,dr\,dv - \frac{1}{4M}\Bigl(1 - \frac{r}{2M}\Bigr)dv^2\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Multiply by $-F = -(32M^3/r)e^{-r/2M}$; the exponentials cancel, and $(32M^3/r)/(4M) = 8M^2/r$:
>
> $$
> -F\,dU\,dV = \frac{8M^2}{r}\Bigl[\frac{r}{4M^2}\,dr\,dv + \frac{1}{4M}\Bigl(1 - \frac{r}{2M}\Bigr)dv^2\Bigr] = 2\,dv\,dr + \Bigl(\frac{2M}{r} - 1\Bigr)dv^2 = -f\,dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr .
> $$
>
> This is the ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein metric of [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]] on $N = \mathbf R_v \times (0, \infty)_r \times S^2$. On I, $v = 4M\ln V$ is the $v = t + r_\ast$ of [[#^def-c7-3-1|Def. §C7.3.1]], so the identification agrees with the one already made there. The part $U > 0$ of $V > 0$ has $UV > 0$, i.e. $r < 2M$, and is the black-hole region $\{r < 2M\} \subset N$.
>
> **Step 3** (region IV is the outgoing interior). On $U < 0$ define $u \equiv -4M\ln(-U)$, so $U = -e^{-u/4M}$ and $V = G(r)/U = -G(r)\,e^{u/4M}$. Then
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned} dU &= \frac{1}{4M}e^{-u/4M}du, \qquad dV = -e^{u/4M}e^{r/2M}\Bigl[-\frac{r}{4M^2}\,dr + \frac{1}{4M}\Bigl(1 - \frac{r}{2M}\Bigr)du\Bigr] , \\ -F\,dU\,dV &= \frac{8M^2}{r}\Bigl[-\frac{r}{4M^2}\,du\,dr + \frac{1}{4M}\Bigl(1 - \frac{r}{2M}\Bigr)du^2\Bigr] = -2\,du\,dr - f\,du^2 , \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> the outgoing metric of [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-4|Theorem §C7.2.4]] (Couzens' (6.102)). Its part $V < 0$ ($UV > 0$, $r < 2M$) is the white-hole region $W$.
>
> **Step 4** (region III). The metric depends on $U$, $V$ only through $UV$ (in $F$ and $r$) and the symmetric product $dU\,dV$. The map $(U, V) \mapsto (V, U)$ leaves both unchanged, so it is an isometry ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^def-c3-13-1|Def. §C3.13.1]]). In $T$, $X$ it is $T \mapsto T$, $X \mapsto -X$: it preserves $\partial_T$, hence the time orientation. It maps $\{U < 0 < V\}$ onto $\{V < 0 < U\}$. If a point of I has Schwarzschild time $t$, then by [[#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], part 1, $U/V = -e^{-t/2M}$. Its image $(U', V') = (V, U)$ has $U'/V' = V/U = -e^{t/2M} = -e^{-(-t)/2M}$. In Couzens' labelling (6.110) of III, $U' = e^{-u/4M}$, $V' = -e^{v/4M}$, the same computation as Step 6 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]] gives $U'/V' = -e^{-t'/2M}$, so the image point has time $t' = -t$.
>
> **Step 5** (Ricci-flat everywhere). Region I is Schwarzschild ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]). By Steps 2–3 the regions $V > 0$ and $U < 0$ are the Eddington–Finkelstein spacetimes, Ricci-flat by [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]] and [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-4|Theorem §C7.2.4]]. By Step 4 the images of these under the isometry, $U > 0$ and $V < 0$, are Ricci-flat too. These four open sets cover $\mathcal K$ except the bifurcation sphere $U = V = 0$. The Ricci components are continuous and vanish on a dense set, so they vanish there as well. Part 1.
>
> **Step 6** (horizons are null). On $U = 0$ use $(V, \theta, \varphi)$ as coordinates. Pulling the metric back ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]) sets $dU = 0$, which removes $-F\,dU\,dV$, and $r = 2M$ there: the induced metric is $4M^2d\Omega^2$. It has no $dV^2$ term, so it is degenerate: the hypersurface is null, generated by the null lines along $\partial_V$. The same holds for $V = 0$. The sphere $U = V = 0$ has the induced metric $4M^2d\Omega^2$ and area $16\pi M^2$. Part 3.
>
> **Step 7** (the singularity is spacelike). On the branch $T = \sqrt{1 + X^2}$ the tangent is $(dX, dT) = (1, X/T)\,dX$, and
>
> $$
> -dT^2 + dX^2 = \Bigl(1 - \frac{X^2}{T^2}\Bigr)dX^2 = \frac{T^2 - X^2}{T^2}\,dX^2 = \frac{dX^2}{T^2} > 0 .
> $$
>
> Likewise on $T = -\sqrt{1 + X^2}$. Part 4.
>
> **Step 8** (radial null geodesics end only at infinity or at the singularity). Take an ingoing ray $V = V_0 \neq 0$ with affine parameter $\lambda$ and tangent $\dot x = \dot U\,\partial_U$. The Killing vector $\partial_t$ ([[#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], part 4) gives the constant $E = -g(\partial_t, \dot x) = -g_{UV}\,(V_0/4M)\,\dot U = F V_0\dot U/8M$ ([[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^thm-c3-13-2|Theorem §C3.13.2]]), while $dr/d\lambda = (\partial r/\partial U)\,\dot U = (V_0/G'(r))\,\dot U$. Their ratio is
>
> $$
> \frac{dr/d\lambda}{E} = \frac{8M}{F\,G'(r)} = \frac{8M}{(32M^3/r)\,e^{-r/2M}\cdot\bigl(-(r/4M^2)\,e^{r/2M}\bigr)} = -1 ,
> $$
>
> so $r$ is an affine parameter along the whole ray, inside the horizon too. If $V_0 > 0$, $U$ runs from $-\infty$ (where $UV_0 \to -\infty$, $r \to \infty$: unbounded affine parameter) up to $U = 1/V_0$, where $r = 0$ after the finite affine length $2M/E$ from the horizon. If $V_0 < 0$, $U$ runs from $1/V_0 < 0$ (on the past singularity) up to $+\infty$ ($r \to \infty$). If $V_0 = 0$, the ray is a generator of the past horizon, $r = 2M$ throughout. There the geodesic equation for $U$, with $\Gamma^U_{UU} = g^{UV}\partial_Ug_{UV} = \partial_U\ln F = F'(r)\,V_0/G'(r) = 0$, reads $d^2U/d\lambda^2 = 0$: $U$ is affine and runs over all of $\mathbf R$. The outgoing rays $U = U_0$ are the same with $U$ and $V$ exchanged. This is part 5 for radial null geodesics; the general statement is quoted.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The four quadrants are four copies of two Schwarzschild regions, glued along the null lines $UV = 0$. The two Eddington–Finkelstein charts of §C7.2 are the halves $V > 0$ and $U < 0$ of one diagram. The quadrant III appears because the Kruskal metric is symmetric under $X \mapsto -X$, and nothing forces the spacetime to stop at $U = 0$, $V < 0$ or at $V = 0$, $U > 0$.

## The Einstein–Rosen bridge

Region III is a whole second universe outside the same black hole. How is it attached to ours? Look at one moment. In region I the slice $t = 0$ is the line $T = 0$, $X > 0$ ([[#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], part 3). Continued to $X < 0$, the same straight line runs through the bifurcation sphere into region III. There it is the slice $t = 0$ of III, since the reflection $X \mapsto -X$ carries $t$ to $-t$ ([[#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], part 2) and so fixes $t = 0$. Along this line $r = r(-X^2)$ decreases from $\infty$ to $2M$ at $X = 0$ and grows again.

> [!definition] Definition §C7.3.2: Einstein–Rosen Bridge
> *Structure.* The hypersurface ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]])
>
> $$
> \Sigma \equiv \{T = 0\} = \{U = -V\} \subset \mathcal K, \qquad \Sigma \cong \mathbf R_X \times S^2 ,
> $$
>
> of the Kruskal spacetime ([[#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]), with the induced metric $h = F\,dX^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$, $r = r(-X^2)$. It is spacelike ($h$ is positive definite, since $F > 0$). It consists of three pieces: the slice $t = 0$ of region I ($X > 0$), the bifurcation sphere $U = V = 0$ ($X = 0$, $r = 2M$), and the slice $t = 0$ of region III ($X < 0$). The areal radius has its minimum $2M$ on the sphere $X = 0$, the **throat**. $\Sigma$ with this geometry is the **Einstein–Rosen bridge** (also called the Schwarzschild wormhole).
>
> A charged black hole has the same kind of bridge, and an electric field threading it gives the two ends opposite charges although the slice contains no charge ([[§C11.5 Charges in Curved Spacetime#^thm-c11-5-5|Theorem §C11.5.5]]).
>

(18.10)–(18.11), Fig. 18.2, p. 346 (PDF 361) (the submanifold $t' = 0$; "two distinct but identical asymptotically flat Schwarzschild manifolds joined at the 'throat' r = 2m") · Hartle, Box 12.4, p. 273 (PDF 274) (the surface $V = 0$ in his letters, i.e. $T = 0$)*

^cau-c7-3-1

Couzens closes the section with a warning: one cannot travel through this wormhole. The diagram makes the reason precise. Future-directed causal curves can only move up and to the side by at most $45°$, so neither $U$ nor $V$ can ever decrease.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.4: The Bridge Is Not Traversable
> In the Kruskal spacetime, time-oriented by $\partial_T$ ([[#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], part 5):
> 1. **$U$ and $V$ never decrease.** Along every future-directed causal curve ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]), $dU/d\lambda \ge 0$ and $dV/d\lambda \ge 0$, not both zero.
> 2. **No crossing.** No future-directed causal curve runs from region I to region III or from III to I. A worldline that tried would have to be spacelike somewhere. In particular, no signal sent from one exterior is ever received in the other.
> 3. **The meeting place is the black hole.** From I a future-directed causal curve can reach only I, II and the future horizon between them. In II both $U$ and $V$ stay positive and $r$ strictly decreases, so the curve stays in II until $UV \to 1$. A signal from III can still reach an observer from I, but only after both have entered II.
> 4. **⚑ The throat closes.** On the spacelike slice $T = T_0$, $|T_0| < 1$, the areal radius is smallest at $X = 0$, with $r_{\min}$ given by $(1 - r_{\min}/2M)\,e^{r_{\min}/2M} = T_0^2$. It shrinks from $2M$ at $T_0 = 0$ to $0$ as $T_0 \to \pm1$: $r_{\min} = 1.796M$ at $T_0 = 0.5$, $1.034M$ at $0.9$, $0.375M$ at $0.99$. An observer who sits at the throat ($X = 0$) is in free fall and lives a proper time $\pi M$ from the widest moment $T = 0$ to the pinch-off at $T = 1$. Restored: $\pi GM/c^3$.
>
> *Model:* the Kruskal spacetime; *applies:* exactly, to every causal curve, with or without forces.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.3.1, p. 139 (PDF 140) ("it is not possible for an observer to cross from one to the other, nor to send a signal"; "you cannot travel through the worm-hole as the paths are space-like not time-like") · Hartle, Box 12.4, p. 273 (PDF 274) and Problems 12.24–12.25, p. 279 (PDF 280) (the throat "pinches off"; "could an observer see light from stars on the other side") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.3, p. 347 (PDF 362), Fig. 18.3 ("At $t' = 1$, the throat pinches off completely") · 't Hooft, ch. 11, p. 51 (PDF 52) (remark 3) · throat radii and the proper time $\pi M$ checked by script*

^thm-c7-3-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the causal condition). Let $\dot x = (\dot U, \dot V, \dot\theta, \dot\varphi)$ be the tangent of a causal curve. By [[#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], part 2,
>
> $$
> g(\dot x, \dot x) = -F\,\dot U\dot V + r^2\bigl(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2\bigr) \le 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot U\dot V \ \ge\ \frac{r^2}{F}\bigl(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2\bigr) \ \ge\ 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (future-directed). With $\partial_T = \partial_U + \partial_V$ (from $U = T - X$, $V = T + X$) and $g_{UV} = -F/2$,
>
> $$
> g(\partial_T, \dot x) = g_{UV}\dot V + g_{VU}\dot U = -\frac F2\bigl(\dot U + \dot V\bigr) < 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot U + \dot V > 0 ,
> $$
>
> by [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]].
>
> **Step 3** (signs). Two numbers with nonnegative product and positive sum are both nonnegative, and not both zero. Part 1.
>
> **Step 4** (no crossing). Region I has $V > 0$ and region III has $V < 0$. A future-directed causal curve from I to III would need $V$ to go from positive to negative, i.e. $\dot V < 0$ somewhere, against Step 3. From III to I, $U$ would have to go from positive to negative. At a point where $\dot V < 0$ (or $\dot U < 0$) the tangent violates Step 1 or Step 2: it is spacelike or past-directed. Part 2.
>
> **Step 5** (trapped in II). From I ($U < 0 < V$), $V$ stays positive, and $U$ can only grow, to $U = 0$ (the future horizon) and beyond into II. In II, $U, V > 0$ and $U$, $V$ do not decrease, so the curve stays in II. There
>
> $$
> \frac{d(UV)}{d\lambda} = \dot U\,V + U\,\dot V > 0 ,
> $$
>
> since $U, V > 0$ and $\dot U, \dot V \ge 0$ are not both zero. With $dr = dG^{-1}(UV)$ and $(G^{-1})' < 0$ ([[#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], Step 8), $r$ strictly decreases: the result of [[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]], seen in Kruskal coordinates. A curve from III enters II through $V = 0 < U$ in the same way. So two curves from I and III can meet, or exchange light, only in II. Part 3.
>
> **Step 6** (the throat of $T = T_0$). On $T = T_0$, $UV = T_0^2 - X^2 \le T_0^2$, with the maximum at $X = 0$. Since $r = G^{-1}(UV)$ is decreasing, $r$ is smallest there, with $G(r_{\min}) = T_0^2$. As $T_0^2 \to 1$, $G(r_{\min}) \to 1 = G(0)$, so $r_{\min} \to 0$. The numbers solve $(1 - x)e^x = T_0^2$, $x = r_{\min}/2M$, numerically. The slice is spacelike for every $|T_0| < 1$: its induced metric is $F\,dX^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$.
>
> **Step 7** (the throat observer). The worldline $X = 0$, $\theta, \varphi$ fixed is pointwise fixed by the isometry $X \mapsto -X$ ([[#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], Step 4) and by the rotations about its own radial direction. A geodesic starting on it with tangent $\partial_T$ is mapped by these isometries to geodesics with the same initial data, so by uniqueness it is that worldline: the throat observer falls freely. Along it $U = V = T$, $r$ runs from $2M$ at $T = 0$ to $0$ at $T = 1$, and $dr/d\tau = 0$ at $T = 0$ (where $r$ is largest). This is radial free fall released from rest at $r_0 = 2M$, whose proper time to $r = 0$ is
>
> $$
> \tau = \frac\pi2\,\frac{r_0^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2M}}\bigg|_{r_0 = 2M} = \frac\pi2\,\frac{(2M)^{3/2}}{(2M)^{1/2}} = \pi M
> $$
>
> ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]]; [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]]). The script also integrates $\tau = \int_0^1\sqrt{F(r(T^2))}\,dT$ directly and finds $3.14159M$. It is the longest proper time any observer can spend inside the horizon ([[§C7.4 Falling into a Black Hole#^thm-c7-4-4|Theorem §C7.4.4]]). Part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** "Not traversable" is a statement about light cones, not about forces. No rocket helps, because every future direction points into the quadrant where $U$ and $V$ both grow. Seen in time, the bridge never stays open. Its throat opens out of the past singularity, reaches the circumference $4\pi M$ at $T = 0$ and pinches off again, within a proper time $2\pi M$ on the throat's own clock, $\pi M$ from the widest moment to the pinch-off.

What does the bridge look like? The slice is a three-dimensional Riemannian space, and no picture shows it whole. But by symmetry its equatorial plane $\theta = \pi/2$ carries all of its radial geometry, and a two-dimensional surface can be drawn in flat three-dimensional space if one exists with the same lengths. The method is the one used for the throat metric of [[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^ex-c6-1-3|Example §C6.1.3]], Step 5.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.3.5: The Embedding of the t = 0 Slice
> 1. **Each side is the Schwarzschild slice.** On the Einstein–Rosen bridge ([[#^def-c7-3-2|Def. §C7.3.2]]), in the coordinate $r$ on $X > 0$ and again on $X < 0$, the induced metric is
>
> $$
> h = \frac{dr^2}{1 - 2M/r} + r^2d\Omega^2, \qquad r > 2M ,
> $$
>
>    the $t = 0$ slice of the Schwarzschild metric, twice (Couzens' (6.111)). The two copies are glued along the throat $r = 2M$, where the chart $r$ fails ($dr = 0$ there) and $X$ does not.
> 2. **Flamm's paraboloid.** The equatorial surface $\theta = \pi/2$ of the bridge, $dr^2/(1 - 2M/r) + r^2d\varphi^2$, has the same metric as the surface of revolution in Euclidean $\mathbf R^3$ (cylindrical coordinates $R$, $\varphi$, $z$)
>
> $$
> z = \pm\sqrt{8M(r - 2M)}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad R = r = 2M + \frac{z^2}{8M} ,
> $$
>
>    a paraboloid: the sheet $z > 0$ is region I, $z < 0$ region III, joined smoothly at the throat circle $z = 0$ (figure below). Restored: $z = \sqrt{8(GM/c^2)(r - 2GM/c^2)}$.
> 3. **Distance through the throat.** The proper radial distance from the throat to the sphere of areal radius $r$ is
>
> $$
> \ell(r) = \int_{2M}^r\frac{dr'}{\sqrt{1 - 2M/r'}} = \sqrt{r(r - 2M)} + 2M\ln\frac{\sqrt r + \sqrt{r - 2M}}{\sqrt{2M}} ,
> $$
>
>    longer than $r - 2M$: $\ell(3M) = 3.049M$, $\ell(4M) = 4.591M$, $\ell(6M) = 7.191M$. Two spheres of areal radius $3M$ on opposite sides of the throat are $6.098M$ apart along the slice.
>
> For a charged black hole the slice through the bifurcation sphere of the outer horizon is again a bridge between two exteriors, but its maximal extension does not end there: it continues through the inner horizon into an infinite chain of universes ([[§C11.3 Inside and Beyond꞉ Penrose Diagrams of Reissner–Nordström#^thm-c11-3-3|Theorem §C11.3.3]]).
>
> *Model:* the Kruskal spacetime, slice $T = 0$; *applies:* exactly. The embedding pictures the intrinsic geometry only; the third dimension of $\mathbf R^3$ has no physical meaning.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.3.1, eq. (6.111), p. 139 (PDF 140) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §18.3, eqs. (18.10)–(18.11), Fig. 18.2, p. 346 (PDF 361) · Hartle, Box 12.4, p. 273 (PDF 274) ("constructed according to the methods in Section 7.7"); §7.7 (embedding diagrams); Problem 12.24 · embedding, its inversion and $\ell(r)$ checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the induced metric). On $T = 0$, $dT = 0$, so [[#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], part 2, pulls back ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]) to $h = F\,dX^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$, with $UV = T^2 - X^2 = -X^2$, i.e.
>
> $$
> X^2 = \Bigl(\frac{r}{2M} - 1\Bigr)e^{r/2M} .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (change to $r$ on $X > 0$). Differentiate: $2X\,dX = \bigl[\tfrac{1}{2M} + (\tfrac{r}{2M} - 1)\tfrac{1}{2M}\bigr]e^{r/2M}dr = \tfrac{r}{4M^2}e^{r/2M}dr$. Hence
>
> $$
> F\,dX^2 = \frac{32M^3}{r}e^{-r/2M}\cdot\frac{r^2e^{r/M}}{16M^4}\cdot\frac{dr^2}{4X^2} = \frac{32M^3}{r}e^{-r/2M}\cdot\frac{r^2e^{r/M}}{64M^4}\cdot\frac{dr^2}{(r/2M - 1)e^{r/2M}} = \frac{r}{2M}\cdot\frac{dr^2}{r/2M - 1} = \frac{dr^2}{1 - 2M/r} .
> $$
>
> In the third expression the exponentials combine to $e^{-r/2M + r/M - r/2M} = 1$ and $32r/64M = r/2M$. The same holds on $X < 0$, where $X = -\sqrt{\cdots}$ and $dX^2$ is unchanged. At $X = 0$, $dr/dX = 8M^2X e^{-r/2M}/r = 0$, so $r$ is not a coordinate there. Part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (the surface of revolution). Flat $\mathbf R^3$ in cylindrical coordinates has $ds^2 = dR^2 + R^2d\varphi^2 + dz^2$. On the surface $R = r$, $z = z(r)$ the induced metric is $dr^2 + r^2d\varphi^2 + z'(r)^2dr^2 = (1 + z'^2)\,dr^2 + r^2d\varphi^2$.
>
> **Step 4** (match the two metrics). Equality with $dr^2/(1 - 2M/r) + r^2d\varphi^2$ requires
>
> $$
> 1 + z'^2 = \frac{1}{1 - 2M/r} = \frac{r}{r - 2M} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z'^2 = \frac{r}{r - 2M} - 1 = \frac{2M}{r - 2M} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (integrate). With $z(2M) = 0$ and the positive root on region I,
>
> $$
> z(r) = \int_{2M}^r\sqrt{\frac{2M}{r' - 2M}}\,dr' = \sqrt{2M}\,\Bigl[2\sqrt{r' - 2M}\Bigr]_{2M}^r = 2\sqrt{2M(r - 2M)} = \sqrt{8M(r - 2M)} .
> $$
>
> Squaring, $z^2 = 8M(r - 2M)$, i.e. $r = 2M + z^2/8M$.
>
> **Step 6** (the two sheets join smoothly). On region III take $z = -\sqrt{8M(r - 2M)}$. As a function on the whole bridge, $z = \operatorname{sign}(X)\sqrt{8M(r - 2M)}$. From Step 1, $r - 2M = 2M\,W_0(X^2/e)$, and $W_0(y) = y\,\psi(y)$ with $\psi$ smooth and $\psi(0) = 1$. So $r - 2M = (2M/e)\,X^2\psi(X^2/e)$ and $z = X\sqrt{(16M^2/e)\,\psi(X^2/e)}$, a smooth function of $X$ with $dz/dX = 4M/\sqrt e \ne 0$ at the throat. Through $z$, the surface $R = 2M + z^2/8M$, $z \in \mathbf R$, is one smooth paraboloid. Part 2.
>
> **Step 7** (proper distance). Along a radial line of the slice, $d\ell = dr/\sqrt{1 - 2M/r}$. Differentiate the claimed antiderivative:
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dr}\sqrt{r(r - 2M)} = \frac{r - M}{\sqrt{r(r - 2M)}}, \qquad \frac{d}{dr}\,2M\ln\bigl(\sqrt r + \sqrt{r - 2M}\bigr) = 2M\,\frac{\frac{1}{2\sqrt r} + \frac{1}{2\sqrt{r - 2M}}}{\sqrt r + \sqrt{r - 2M}} = \frac{M}{\sqrt{r(r - 2M)}} .
> $$
>
> The sum is $r/\sqrt{r(r - 2M)} = \sqrt{r/(r - 2M)} = 1/\sqrt{1 - 2M/r}$, and the expression vanishes at $r = 2M$ ($\ln(\sqrt{2M}/\sqrt{2M}) = 0$). The numbers follow by evaluation. Part 3.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** All the curvature of the slice sits near the throat: far out, $z \propto \sqrt r$ grows ever more slowly and the paraboloid flattens into a plane, the asymptotically flat end. Near the throat the radial direction is stretched, $d\ell/dr = 1/\sqrt{1 - 2M/r} \to \infty$. The throat is a minimal sphere, the same geometry as the static throat of [[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^ex-c6-1-3|Example §C6.1.3]], except that this one belongs to a single moment ([[#^thm-c7-3-4|Theorem §C7.3.4]], part 4).

> [!remark] Remark: Which parts are physical
> The Kruskal spacetime is the vacuum solution continued as far as it goes. A black hole that formed from a collapsing star is different, and Birkhoff's theorem ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-5|Theorem §C6.2.5]]) says exactly how much of the diagram survives. Outside the star's surface the metric is Schwarzschild. Inside, it is whatever the matter makes it. In the Kruskal diagram the surface is a timelike worldline that comes in from large $r$ in region I, crosses $U = 0$ and ends on the future singularity. Everything to its right is the true exterior: part of I, part of II, the future horizon and the future singularity. Everything to its left, including all of regions III and IV and the bridge, is replaced by the star's interior. So a black hole formed by collapse has no white hole, no second exterior and no Einstein–Rosen bridge (Schutz §11.2, p. 322, PDF 340; Hartle Fig. 12.7 and Box 12.4; 't Hooft, ch. 11, remarks 1–2). Couzens adds that white holes are "not physically relevant since … they cannot be formed by collapsing matter" (p. 137, PDF 138). d'Inverno & Vickers keep the question open: Einstein's equations fix the local geometry, not the global one (§18.3, p. 347, PDF 362). The collapse diagram and the Penrose diagrams that show all of this at once are [[§C10.3 Penrose Diagrams of Kruskal and of Collapse#^thm-c10-3-3|Theorem §C10.3.3]] and [[§C10.3 Penrose Diagrams of Kruskal and of Collapse#^thm-c10-3-2|Theorem §C10.3.2]]. Why a cold star above its maximum mass must collapse, and how its surface falls through $r = 2M$, is [[§C9.3 White Dwarfs, Neutron Stars and Collapse#^thm-c9-3-6|Theorem §C9.3.6]]; the surface's worldline in these diagrams is described in [[§C9.3 White Dwarfs, Neutron Stars and Collapse#^rem-c9-3-1|§C9.3, Remark: Collapse to a black hole]].

^rem-c7-3-1

The Kruskal diagram answers the chapter's question about the geometry: $r = 2M$ is a pair of null surfaces in an otherwise ordinary spacetime, and the only true end is the spacelike singularity. It says nothing yet about what a body feels on the way in. Tides, what the falling observer sees, and how long the trip lasts are [[§C7.4 Falling into a Black Hole|§C7.4]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C7.3.1: Two Observers in Kruskal Coordinates
> *Question* (after Hartle Prob. 12.22 and d'Inverno & Vickers' Fig. 18.1). Two observers hover together at $r = r_0 > 2M$ in region I. At $t = 0$ one of them, B, switches off her rocket and falls radially; the other, A, stays. Draw both worldlines in the Kruskal diagram. Where does B cross the horizon and reach the singularity? Until what Schwarzschild time can A still send B a radial light signal that reaches her? Which of B's signals does A receive?
>
> *Setup.* Put $b \equiv \sqrt{r_0/2M - 1}$, so $r_0 = 2M(1 + b^2)$. B falls from rest with $L = 0$ and $E = \sqrt{1 - 2M/r_0} = b/\sqrt{1 + b^2}$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]]).
>
> **Step 1** (A). $r = r_0$ is fixed, so by [[#^def-c7-3-1|Def. §C7.3.1]] $V = e^{(t + r_{\ast0})/4M}$ and $U = -e^{-(t - r_{\ast0})/4M}$, $r_{\ast0} \equiv r_\ast(r_0)$. Then
>
> $$
> X = \tfrac12(V - U) = e^{r_{\ast0}/4M}\cosh\frac{t}{4M}, \qquad T = \tfrac12(V + U) = e^{r_{\ast0}/4M}\sinh\frac{t}{4M} ,
> $$
>
> the hyperbola $X^2 - T^2 = e^{r_{\ast0}/2M} = (r_0/2M - 1)e^{r_0/2M}$ ([[#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], part 2). A's clock runs at $d\tau_A = \sqrt{1 - 2M/r_0}\,dt$.
>
> **Step 2** (B: radius and proper time). By [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]], with $0 \le \eta \le \pi$,
>
> $$
> r = \tfrac12r_0(1 + \cos\eta) = M(1 + b^2)(1 + \cos\eta), \qquad \tau = \sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{8M}}\,(\eta + \sin\eta) = M(1 + b^2)^{3/2}(\eta + \sin\eta) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (an equation for $v$ that is regular at the horizon). With $v = t + r_\ast$, $dt/d\tau = E/f$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]) and $dr/d\tau = -\sqrt{E^2 - f}$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], $L = 0$),
>
> $$
> \frac{dv}{d\tau} = \frac{dt}{d\tau} + \frac1f\frac{dr}{d\tau} = \frac{E - \sqrt{E^2 - f}}{f} = \frac{E^2 - (E^2 - f)}{f\bigl(E + \sqrt{E^2 - f}\bigr)} = \frac{1}{E + \sqrt{E^2 - f}} .
> $$
>
> The factor $1/f$, singular at $r = 2M$, has cancelled.
>
> **Step 4** (in terms of $\eta$). From Example §C6.3.2, $2M/r - 2M/r_0 = (2M/r_0)(1 - \cos\eta)/(1 + \cos\eta)$, with $2M/r_0 = 1/(1 + b^2)$ and $(1 - \cos\eta)/(1 + \cos\eta) = \tan^2(\eta/2)$. So $\sqrt{E^2 - f} = \sqrt{2M/r - 2M/r_0} = \tan(\eta/2)/\sqrt{1 + b^2}$ and $E + \sqrt{E^2 - f} = (b + \tan(\eta/2))/\sqrt{1 + b^2}$. With $1 + \cos\eta = 2\cos^2(\eta/2)$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dv}{d\eta} = \frac{dv}{d\tau}\,\frac{d\tau}{d\eta} = \frac{\sqrt{1 + b^2}}{b + \tan(\eta/2)}\cdot M(1 + b^2)^{3/2}\,2\cos^2\frac\eta2 = \frac{2M(1 + b^2)^2\cos^3(\eta/2)}{b\cos(\eta/2) + \sin(\eta/2)} .
> $$
>
> **Step 5** (integrate). The antiderivative with $v(0) = r_{\ast0}$ (B starts at $t = 0$) is
>
> $$
> \frac{v(\eta)}{2M} = 2\ln\Bigl(b\cos\frac\eta2 + \sin\frac\eta2\Bigr) + b\,\eta + \tfrac12b(1 + b^2)(\eta + \sin\eta) + \tfrac12(1 + b^2)(1 + \cos\eta) .
> $$
>
> Check by differentiating, with $c = \cos(\eta/2)$, $s = \sin(\eta/2)$, $1 + \cos\eta = 2c^2$, $\sin\eta = 2sc$:
>
> $$
> \frac{c - bs}{bc + s} + b + (1 + b^2)\,c\,(bc - s) = \frac{(1 + b^2)\,c}{bc + s} + (1 + b^2)\,c\,(bc - s) = \frac{(1 + b^2)\,c\,\bigl[1 + b^2c^2 - s^2\bigr]}{bc + s} = \frac{(1 + b^2)^2c^3}{bc + s} ,
> $$
>
> using $1 - s^2 = c^2$; this is $(dv/d\eta)/2M$. At $\eta = 0$: $v/2M = 2\ln b + 1 + b^2$, i.e. $v = r_0 + 2M\ln b^2 = r_0 + 2M\ln(r_0/2M - 1) = r_{\ast0}$.
>
> **Step 6** (B in the diagram). $V = e^{v/4M} = e^{(v/2M)/2}$ and $U = G(r)/V$ ([[#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]]):
>
> $$
> V(\eta) = \Bigl(b\cos\frac\eta2 + \sin\frac\eta2\Bigr)\exp\Bigl\{\tfrac12\bigl[b\eta + \tfrac12b(1 + b^2)(\eta + \sin\eta) + \tfrac12(1 + b^2)(1 + \cos\eta)\bigr]\Bigr\}, \qquad U(\eta) = \frac{(1 - r/2M)\,e^{r/2M}}{V(\eta)} .
> $$
>
> Both increase along the fall (script), as [[#^thm-c7-3-4|Theorem §C7.3.4]], part 1 requires.
>
> **Step 7** (horizon and singularity). $U = 0$ where $r = 2M$: $\cos\eta_H = 4M/r_0 - 1$. At $\eta = \pi$: $\cos(\pi/2) = 0$, $\sin(\pi/2) = 1$, $\sin\pi = 0$, $1 + \cos\pi = 0$, so
>
> $$
> V_{\text{end}} = \exp\Bigl\{\tfrac12\bigl[b\pi + \tfrac12b(1 + b^2)\pi\bigr]\Bigr\} = \exp\Bigl[\frac{\pi b(3 + b^2)}{4}\Bigr], \qquad U_{\text{end}} = \frac{G(0)}{V_{\text{end}}} = \frac{1}{V_{\text{end}}} ,
> $$
>
> a point of $UV = 1$.
>
> **Step 8** (A's last useful signal). A radial ingoing signal keeps $V$ fixed ([[#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], part 1). It meets B before she reaches $r = 0$ exactly when its $V$ is below $V_{\text{end}}$. A emits at time $t$ with $V = e^{(t + r_{\ast0})/4M}$, so the condition is $t < t_{\text{last}}$ with
>
> $$
> t_{\text{last}} = 4M\ln V_{\text{end}} - r_{\ast0} = \pi b(3 + b^2)\,M - r_\ast(r_0) .
> $$
>
> **Step 9** (what A receives). An outgoing signal keeps $U$ fixed. One sent by B at $U = U_e < 0$ reaches A where $-e^{-(t - r_{\ast0})/4M} = U_e$, at $t = r_{\ast0} - 4M\ln(-U_e)$, which tends to $\infty$ as $U_e \to 0^-$. Signals sent at or after the crossing ($U_e \ge 0$) never reach A.
>
> *Numbers, $r_0 = 3M$* (the figure after [[#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]). $b = 1/\sqrt2 = 0.70711$, $E = 0.57735$. Horizon: $\eta_H = 1.2310$, $\tau_H = 3.9935M$, $V_H = 5.2355$, so B crosses $U = 0$ at $(X, T) = (2.618, 2.618)$. Singularity: $\tau_{\text{end}} = 5.7715M$ ($= \tfrac\pi2(3M)^{3/2}/\sqrt{2M}$, [[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]]), $V_{\text{end}} = 6.9850$, $U_{\text{end}} = 0.1432$, $(X, T) = (3.421, 3.564)$. With $r_\ast(3M) = 1.614M$: $t_{\text{last}} = 6.161M$, i.e. $3.557M$ on A's clock. B's signals sent at $\tau = 1.261M$, $1.991M$, $2.438M$ ($U = -0.8$, $-0.5$, $-0.35$) arrive at $t = 2.506M$, $4.386M$, $5.813M$.
>
> *Numbers, $r_0 = 4M$* ($b = 1$, the radius of d'Inverno & Vickers' Fig. 18.1). $\cos\eta_H = 0$, so $\eta_H = \pi/2$ and $\tau_H = 2\sqrt2(\pi/2 + 1)M = 7.271M$; $\tau_{\text{end}} = 2\sqrt2\,\pi M = 8.886M$; $V_{\text{end}} = e^\pi = 23.14$; $r_\ast(4M) = 4M$; $t_{\text{last}} = 4M(\pi - 1) = 8.566M$, i.e. $2\sqrt2(\pi - 1)M = 6.057M$ on A's clock.
>
> *What it shows.* A's help has a deadline, $t_{\text{last}}$, though B's crossing of $r = 2M$ happens at $t = +\infty$ in A's coordinates. Conversely A goes on receiving B's earlier signals for ever, ever more redshifted ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^ex-c7-1-2|Example §C7.1.2]]). Both facts are read off $45°$ lines in one chart; in Schwarzschild coordinates neither can even be drawn.
>

> [!example] Example §C7.3.2: Local Flatness at the Bifurcation Sphere
> *Question* (Schutz, Exercise 11.27). Show that spacetime is locally flat at the centre $U = V = 0$ of the Kruskal diagram, the sphere where the two horizons cross.
>
> **Step 1** (the metric there). At $U = V = 0$, $r = 2M$ and $F = 16M^2/e$ ([[#^thm-c7-3-1|Theorem §C7.3.1]], part 4):
>
> $$
> ds^2\big|_{U = V = 0} = \frac{16M^2}{e}\bigl(-dT^2 + dX^2\bigr) + 4M^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (first derivatives in the radial plane vanish). $r = G^{-1}(T^2 - X^2)$, so by the chain rule
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial r}{\partial T} = \frac{2T}{G'(r)}, \qquad \frac{\partial r}{\partial X} = -\frac{2X}{G'(r)} ,
> $$
>
> both zero at $T = X = 0$. Every metric component is a function of $r$ (and $\theta$), so all $T$- and $X$-derivatives of $g_{TT} = -F$, $g_{XX} = F$, $g_{\theta\theta} = r^2$, $g_{\varphi\varphi} = r^2\sin^2\theta$ vanish there (checked by script). The only first derivative left is $\partial_\theta(r^2\sin^2\theta)$, the sphere's own.
>
> **Step 3** (local inertial coordinates). Rescale $\hat T = (4M/\sqrt e)\,T$, $\hat X = (4M/\sqrt e)\,X$. On the sphere of radius $2M$ use Riemann normal coordinates $(y^1, y^2)$ about the point ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]]), in which the sphere's metric is $\delta_{ab}$ with vanishing first derivatives. In $(\hat T, \hat X, y^1, y^2)$ the metric at the point is $\eta$ and all its first derivatives vanish: a local inertial frame ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]]). In the radial plane the Kruskal chart itself, rescaled, is already inertial at the point.
>
> **Step 4** (the curvature is finite and ordinary). In the orthonormal frame $\hat e_T = F^{-1/2}\partial_T$, $\hat e_X = F^{-1/2}\partial_X$, $\hat e_\theta = r^{-1}\partial_\theta$, $\hat e_\varphi = (r\sin\theta)^{-1}\partial_\varphi$ at $U = V = 0$, the script finds
>
> $$
> R_{\hat T\hat X\hat T\hat X} = -\frac{1}{4M^2}, \quad R_{\hat T\hat\theta\hat T\hat\theta} = R_{\hat T\hat\varphi\hat T\hat\varphi} = \frac{1}{8M^2}, \quad R_{\hat X\hat\theta\hat X\hat\theta} = R_{\hat X\hat\varphi\hat X\hat\varphi} = -\frac{1}{8M^2}, \quad R_{\hat\theta\hat\varphi\hat\theta\hat\varphi} = \frac{1}{4M^2} ,
> $$
>
> and no others apart from those related by the symmetries of Riemann. These are the static-frame values $-2M/r^3$, $M/r^3$, $-M/r^3$, $2M/r^3$ at $r = 2M$. Each of the six pairs contributes four equal terms to the Kretschmann scalar:
>
> $$
> K = 4\Bigl[\frac{1}{16} + 2\cdot\frac{1}{64} + 2\cdot\frac{1}{64} + \frac{1}{16}\Bigr]\frac{1}{M^4} = \frac{3}{4M^4} = \frac{48M^2}{(2M)^6} ,
> $$
>
> as [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-7|Theorem §C6.2.7]] requires.
>
> **Step 5** (what an observer there feels). The throat observer of [[#^thm-c7-3-4|Theorem §C7.3.4]], part 4, has $u = \hat e_T$ at this event. Her tidal tensor ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^def-c3-12-4|Def. §C3.12.4]]) gives the relative accelerations $-R_{\hat\imath\hat T\hat\jmath\hat T}\,\xi^{\hat\jmath}$: $+\xi^{\hat X}/4M^2$ along $\hat X$ (stretching) and $-\xi/8M^2$ across (squeezing). These are the values $+2M/r^3$, $-M/r^3$ of any radially falling observer at $r = 2M$ ([[§C7.4 Falling into a Black Hole#^thm-c7-4-1|Theorem §C7.4.1]]).
>
> *What it shows.* The bifurcation sphere is an ordinary event in the geometry; what is special about it is global. Near it the radial plane is a piece of Minkowski spacetime in which the static observers $r = \text{const}$ are the hyperbolae $\hat X^2 - \hat T^2 = \text{const}$, uniformly accelerated observers ([[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^mod-b3-3-4|Model §B3.3.4]], [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]; level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), and the horizons are the lines $\hat X = \pm\hat T$ that bound their wedge, the Rindler horizons of flat spacetime. The near-horizon metric is the Rindler metric times a round sphere of radius $2M$, and the static observers are Rindler observers ([[§C10.2 Rindler Spacetime and Horizons#^thm-c10-2-4|Theorem §C10.2.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schutz, Exercise 11.27, p. 357 (PDF 375) (the question; no solution in the book) · 't Hooft, ch. 11, p. 50 (PDF 51) (the Rindler comparison) · worked here; derivatives, orthonormal components and $K$ checked by script*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - **Charts, not spacetime.** The lesson of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^rem-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Remark: Coordinate singularities are failures of a chart, not of the manifold]] and of [[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]] is completed here: one chart, [[#^def-c7-3-1|Def. §C7.3.1]], is regular across both horizons, and only the curvature singularity $UV = 1$ remains ([[#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]).
> - **The two Eddington–Finkelstein charts.** [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]] and [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-4|Theorem §C7.2.4]] are the halves $V > 0$ and $U < 0$ of the Kruskal diagram (Steps 2–3 of the derivation of [[#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]]). The black and white hole regions of [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]] and [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-5|Def. §C7.2.5]] are regions II and IV, distinct as [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^cau-c7-2-1|§C7.2, Caution: The time-reversed extension is a different region, not the same horizon seen backwards]] says.
> - **Killing vector as boost.** The time-translation Killing vector of [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^rem-c6-2-1|§C6.2, Remark: The Killing vectors of the Schwarzschild metric]] becomes the boost generator of the Kruskal plane ([[#^thm-c7-3-2|Theorem §C7.3.2]], part 4), the flat-space boost of [[§C3.13 Isometries and Killing Vectors#^ex-c3-13-1|Example §C3.13.1]]. It vanishes on the bifurcation sphere and is null on the horizons, the property behind Killing horizons ([[§C10.5 Null Hypersurfaces and Killing Horizons#^def-c10-5-3|Def. §C10.5.3]]). Near $U = V = 0$ its orbits, the static observers, are the hyperbolic worldlines of [[§B3.3 Relativistic Force and Accelerated Motion#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]] (level B, signature $+{-}{-}{-}$), and the horizons their Rindler horizons ([[#^ex-c7-3-2|Example §C7.3.2]]).
> - **Throats.** The bridge's throat ([[#^thm-c7-3-5|Theorem §C7.3.5]]) is the dynamical cousin of the static throat of [[§C6.1 Spherically Symmetric Spacetimes#^ex-c6-1-3|Example §C6.1.3]], which needs negative energy density; this one needs none, but it pinches off ([[#^thm-c7-3-4|Theorem §C7.3.4]], part 4).
> - **Proper time inside.** The throat observer's $\pi M$ ([[#^thm-c7-3-4|Theorem §C7.3.4]], part 4) is the maximum of [[§C7.4 Falling into a Black Hole#^thm-c7-4-4|Theorem §C7.4.4]].
> - **Mathematics.** The inversion $r = G^{-1}(UV)$ is the inverse function theorem in one variable ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^thm-29-10|451 Thm. §29.10]]); the horizons are null hypersurfaces in the sense of the induced metric ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-5|Def. §C3.1.5]]).
> - **Conventions.** Kruskal coordinates in each source: [[#^cau-c7-3-2|Caution: Names for the Kruskal coordinates]]; d'Inverno & Vickers' $+{-}{-}{-}$ forms in [[General Relativity Conventions]].
