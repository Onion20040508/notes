The Schwarzschild metric ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]) describes the field outside every spherical body, and chapter C6 used it only where it is harmless, far outside the Schwarzschild radius $r_S = 2M$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^def-c6-2-1|Def. §C6.2.1]]). A star squeezed inside its own $r_S$ leaves the formula exposed down to $r = 2M$ and below. The chapter asks: **is $r = 2M$ a place in spacetime, and what happens to whatever crosses it?** Chapter C6 left three warnings. The redshift of a static source diverges as it approaches $2M$ ([[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay#^thm-c6-6-1|Theorem §C6.6.1]], part 4). A body dropped from rest needs an infinite Schwarzschild time $t$ to get there, and only a finite proper time ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]]). And the Kretschmann scalar is finite at $2M$ but infinite at $r = 0$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-7|Theorem §C6.2.7]]).

First, the naive answer, computed. Read the components literally. At $r = 2M$, $g_{tt} = -(1 - 2M/r)$ vanishes, so a clock held there would not tick, and $g_{rr} = (1 - 2M/r)^{-1}$ is infinite. At $r = 0$, $g_{tt}$ itself blows up. Yet the surface $r = 2M$ is only a finite proper distance away from any outside point ($3.049M$ from $r = 3M$). Taken at face value, the metric says that a short walk inward leads to a wall where time stops and spacetime ends ([[#^thm-c7-1-1|Theorem §C7.1.1]]). That reading is wrong at $r = 2M$ and right, in a sharper sense, at $r = 0$, and this section shows why.

The tool is the lesson of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^rem-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Remark: Coordinate singularities are failures of a chart, not of the manifold]]: components belong to a chart, and only chart-independent quantities can say whether spacetime itself misbehaves. Two such quantities are used. The first is the curvature scalars ([[#^def-c7-1-1|Def. §C7.1.1]]), which separate a defect of the chart from a defect of spacetime ([[#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]]). The second is the proper time of a freely falling clock, which reaches $2M$ in finite time while Schwarzschild $t$ diverges ([[#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]]). Radial light then shows the same failure of $t$ as light cones that close up in the $(r, t)$ diagram ([[#^thm-c7-1-3|Theorem §C7.1.3]]). Below $2M$ the formula still solves the vacuum equations, but there $r$ is a time coordinate. The singularity at $r = 0$ is then a moment in every future, not a point in space ([[#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]).

**Setup.** Vacuum, $\Lambda = 0$, $M > 0$. The Schwarzschild line element ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]])

$$
ds^2 = -f(r)\,dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{f(r)} + r^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr), \qquad f(r) = 1 - \frac{2M}{r},
$$

is read on two separate coordinate patches, $\mathrm I = \{r > 2M\}$ and $\mathrm{II} = \{0 < r < 2M\}$ (d'Inverno's regions I and II), each with $t \in \mathbf R$ and the usual angles. On both, it is a Ricci-flat metric: Step 6 of the derivation of [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]] uses only $F = 1 - 2M/r$, never $F > 0$, and all ten Ricci components vanish identically in $r$ (checked by script). It also has the same Kretschmann scalar on both. Whether I and II are two pieces of one spacetime cannot be decided in these coordinates, which fail between them. [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon|§C7.2]] decides it. Bodies are test bodies on geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]), mostly radial ($L = 0$). The numbers in the examples were checked by script.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$ ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]); $G = c = 1$, restored with [[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]] ($M \to GM/c^2$ as a length, $GM/c^3$ as a time; $t \to ct$, $\tau \to c\tau$); coordinates $(t, r, \theta, \varphi)$; $f = 1 - 2M/r$, and inside $h \equiv -f = 2M/r - 1 > 0$; a dot is $d/d\tau$ for a body and $d/d\lambda$ (affine) for light; $E = f\,\dot t$ and $L = r^2\dot\varphi$ as in [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]. Level B and its links use $+{-}{-}{-}$ with $c$ kept.

## What the components say

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.1: The Metric Components Degenerate at r = 2M and r = 0
> In the Schwarzschild coordinates of the Setup:
> 1. **At $r = 2M$.** As $r \to 2M$ from either side, $g_{tt} = -f \to 0$ and $|g_{rr}| = 1/|f| \to \infty$. The inverse metric ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]) has $g^{tt} = -1/f$, unbounded, and $g^{rr} = f \to 0$. The determinant $\det g = -r^4\sin^2\theta$ is finite and nonzero for every $r \neq 2M$, but at $r = 2M$ the components are not defined at all: the chart gives no metric there.
> 2. **At $r = 0$.** $g_{tt} = 2M/r - 1 \to +\infty$ and $g_{rr} = -(2M/r - 1)^{-1} \to 0^-$.
> 3. **Static clocks stop.** A clock at fixed $(r, \theta, \varphi)$ in region I ticks at the rate
>
> $$
> \frac{d\tau}{dt} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{2M}{r}} \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \qquad (r \to 2M^+) ,
> $$
>
>    and its light reaches infinity with $\nu_\infty/\nu = \sqrt{1 - 2M/r} \to 0$ ([[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay#^thm-c6-6-1|Theorem §C6.6.1]], part 4). At $r \le 2M$ no such clock exists: $\partial_t$ is null at $r = 2M$ and spacelike inside, so no observer has it as velocity ([[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay#^def-c6-6-1|Def. §C6.6.1]]).
> 4. **Yet $r = 2M$ is a finite distance away.** Along a radial line at fixed $t$, the proper length ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-3|Def. §C3.1.3]]) from $r = 2M$ to $r_1 > 2M$ is
>
> $$
> \ell(r_1) = \int_{2M}^{r_1}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{1 - 2M/r}} = \sqrt{r_1(r_1 - 2M)} + 2M\ln\frac{\sqrt{r_1} + \sqrt{r_1 - 2M}}{\sqrt{2M}} ,
> $$
>
>    finite although the integrand diverges: $\ell(3M) = 3.049M$, $\ell(10M) = 11.832M$.
>
> *What the components suggest.* Parts 1–4 together say: a finite distance inward there is a surface on which clocks stand still, light is infinitely redshifted and the metric ceases to exist. Read literally, spacetime ends in a wall at $r = 2M$. [[#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]] and [[#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]] show that this reading is wrong.
>
> Restored: $d\tau/dt = \sqrt{1 - r_S/r}$ with $r_S = 2GM/c^2$; $\ell(r_1) = \sqrt{r_1(r_1 - r_S)} + r_S\ln\bigl[(\sqrt{r_1} + \sqrt{r_1 - r_S})/\sqrt{r_S}\bigr]$.
>
> *Model:* the Schwarzschild line element on regions I and II; *applies:* exactly; parts 3–4 in region I.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.3, p. 130 (PDF 131) ("something funky happens with the metric") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §17.1, eq. (17.2), p. 322 (PDF 337) (character of the coordinates; signature $+{-}{-}{-}$); §17.2, p. 323 (PDF 338) ("surface of infinite redshift") · Schutz, §11.2, p. 315 (PDF 333) · proper distance and limits checked by script*

^thm-c7-1-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (components and inverse). The metric is diagonal, $g_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(-f,\ 1/f,\ r^2,\ r^2\sin^2\theta\bigr)$, so its inverse is diagonal with the reciprocal entries:
>
> $$
> g^{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}\Bigl(-\frac1f,\ f,\ \frac{1}{r^2},\ \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (determinant). For a diagonal matrix the determinant is the product of the diagonal entries:
>
> $$
> \det g = (-f)\cdot\frac1f\cdot r^2\cdot r^2\sin^2\theta = -r^4\sin^2\theta \qquad (f \neq 0) .
> $$
>
> The factors $f$ and $1/f$ cancel only where both are finite. At $r = 2M$ the product $0\cdot\infty$ is undefined: the matrix has no value there.
>
> **Step 3** (limits). As $r \to 2M$, $f = (r - 2M)/r \to 0$, so $-f \to 0$, $1/|f| \to \infty$, $g^{tt} = -1/f \to \mp\infty$ (from outside and inside), and $g^{rr} = f \to 0$. As $r \to 0^+$, $f = 1 - 2M/r \to -\infty$, so $g_{tt} = -f \to +\infty$ and $g_{rr} = 1/f \to 0^-$. This is parts 1 and 2.
>
> **Step 4** (static clock). The worldline $x^\mu = (t, r, \theta, \varphi)$ with fixed $r, \theta, \varphi$ has $d\tau/dt = \sqrt{-g_{tt}} = \sqrt f$ ([[§C1.5 Gravity as Geometry꞉ the Weak-Field Metric#^thm-c1-5-1|Theorem §C1.5.1]], part 1, which holds for any static metric). It is timelike only where $g_{tt} < 0$, i.e. $f > 0$, i.e. $r > 2M$. At $r = 2M$, $g(\partial_t, \partial_t) = -f = 0$ (null); for $r < 2M$, $g(\partial_t, \partial_t) = -f = h > 0$ (spacelike). This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (proper distance). The curve $r \mapsto (t_0, r, \theta_0, \varphi_0)$ has $g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma) = g_{rr} = 1/f$, so its length is $\int dr/\sqrt f = \int\sqrt{r/(r - 2M)}\,dr$. Substitute $r = 2M\cosh^2\chi$, $\chi \ge 0$:
>
> $$
> dr = 4M\cosh\chi\sinh\chi\,d\chi, \qquad \sqrt{\frac{r}{r - 2M}} = \frac{\cosh\chi}{\sinh\chi}, \qquad \sqrt{\frac{r}{r - 2M}}\,dr = 4M\cosh^2\chi\,d\chi = 2M(1 + \cosh 2\chi)\,d\chi .
> $$
>
> The divergent factor $1/\sinh\chi$ has cancelled against the Jacobian. With $r = 2M \leftrightarrow \chi = 0$,
>
> $$
> \ell(r_1) = \int_0^{\chi_1}2M(1 + \cosh 2\chi)\,d\chi = 2M\chi_1 + M\sinh 2\chi_1 = 2M\chi_1 + 2M\sinh\chi_1\cosh\chi_1 .
> $$
>
> Back-substitute $\cosh\chi_1 = \sqrt{r_1/2M}$, $\sinh\chi_1 = \sqrt{(r_1 - 2M)/2M}$, $\chi_1 = \ln(\cosh\chi_1 + \sinh\chi_1)$:
>
> $$
> 2M\sinh\chi_1\cosh\chi_1 = \sqrt{r_1(r_1 - 2M)}, \qquad 2M\chi_1 = 2M\ln\frac{\sqrt{r_1} + \sqrt{r_1 - 2M}}{\sqrt{2M}} .
> $$
>
> For $r_1 = 3M$: $\sqrt3\,M + 2M\ln\bigl((\sqrt3 + 1)/\sqrt2\bigr) = 1.732M + 2M(0.6585) = 3.049M$. This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Every divergence in parts 1–3 comes from the single function $f$ vanishing in the denominator of $g_{rr}$ and $g^{tt}$, a statement about two coordinate vectors, $\partial_t$ and $\partial_r$. The one geometric quantity computed, a length, stays finite because the substitution absorbs the zero of $f$. Components alone cannot tell which of the two kinds of statement the divergences are.

## Curvature invariants: asking the spacetime instead of the chart

A divergent component can be manufactured by a bad chart. Relabelling the flat plane by $r = a^2/r'$ makes the metric blow up at $r' = 0$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^rem-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Remark: Coordinate singularities are failures of a chart, not of the manifold]]). What cannot be manufactured is a divergent scalar. Its value at an event is one number, the same in every chart ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]). Curvature is the part of the geometry that no chart removes ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^thm-c3-8-2|Theorem §C3.8.2]]), so the scalars to build are made from the Riemann tensor.

> [!definition] Definition §C7.1.1: Curvature Invariant; Kretschmann Scalar
> *Structure.* On a Lorentzian manifold $(M, g)$, a **curvature invariant** is a scalar field $I : M \to \mathbf R$ built from the Riemann tensor $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}$ ([[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^def-c3-9-1|Def. §C3.9.1]]), its covariant derivatives, the metric and its inverse, by tensor products and full contractions ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^def-c2-3-4|Def. §C2.3.4]]) alone, with no chart, vector field or basis singled out. Because every index is contracted, $I(p)$ has the same value in every chart around $p$ ([[§C2.3 Tensor Fields#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]).
>
> *The first three.* In order of increasing degree in the curvature:
> - the scalar curvature $R = g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}$ ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^def-c3-11-2|Def. §C3.11.2]]), linear;
> - $R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}$, quadratic in the Ricci tensor ([[§C3.11 The Ricci and Einstein Tensors#^def-c3-11-1|Def. §C3.11.1]]);
> - the **Kretschmann scalar** $K = R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}R^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}$, the full square of the Riemann tensor.
>
> In vacuum ($R_{\mu\nu} = 0$) the first two vanish identically, so they detect nothing; $K$ is the simplest invariant that can see vacuum curvature. For the Schwarzschild metric, on regions I and II alike,
>
> $$
> R = 0, \qquad R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu} = 0, \qquad K = \frac{48M^2}{r^6} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } K = \frac{48G^2M^2}{c^4r^6}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> the value derived in [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-7|Theorem §C6.2.7]] (the same expression holds inside, checked by script). At $r = 2M$, $K = 3/(4M^4)$, finite; as $r \to 0$, $K \to \infty$.
>
> *Physical reading.* $K^{-1/4}$ is a length, the **curvature radius**. Tidal accelerations between free bodies a distance $\xi$ apart ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]]) are of order $\xi\,K^{1/2}$: the six frame components in the derivation of [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-7|Theorem §C6.2.7]] are all of size $M/r^3 \sim K^{1/2}$. At the horizon it is $(4/3)^{1/4}M = 1.07M$, i.e. $15.9\ \mathrm{km}$ for $10\,M_\odot$ and $6.6\times10^{9}\ \mathrm m$ for Sgr A$^\ast$ ($4.15\times10^{6}M_\odot$, dated). The larger the hole, the gentler its horizon (forward: [[§C7.4 Falling into a Black Hole#^thm-c7-4-3|Theorem §C7.4.3]]).
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.3, eq. (6.88), p. 131 (PDF 132) (scalars "independent of coordinates"; $R$ and $R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}$ vanish in vacuum; Kretschmann) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §17.2, p. 323 (PDF 338) ("Riemann tensor scalar invariant $R_{abcd}R^{abcd} = 48m^2r^{-6}$") · numbers checked by script*

^def-c7-1-1

The Schwarzschild invariant has opposite verdicts at the two radii. That is the distinction the next definition names, with one honest limitation: a finite invariant is evidence, not proof.

> [!definition] Definition §C7.1.2: Coordinate Singularity vs Curvature Singularity
> *Structure.* Let a metric be given by its components $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$ in a chart $x$ ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]) on an open set $U$, and let $S$ be a set of coordinate values at the edge of $x(U)$ at which some component of $g_{\mu\nu}$ or $g^{\mu\nu}$ diverges or is undefined, or where the matrix $g_{\mu\nu}$ becomes degenerate (a **singularity of the metric in the chart $x$**).
> 1. $S$ is a **coordinate singularity** if the spacetime can be extended across it: there is a Lorentzian manifold $(\tilde M, \tilde g)$ containing $(U, g)$ as an open subset, and a chart $y$ of $\tilde M$ covering points that the chart $x$ approaches at $S$, in which $\tilde g_{\mu\nu}(y)$ is smooth with nonzero determinant. The defect belongs to the chart $x$; in $\tilde M$ the points of $S$ are ordinary.
> 2. $S$ is a **curvature singularity** if some curvature invariant ([[#^def-c7-1-1|Def. §C7.1.1]]) is unbounded on a curve of $U$ that runs into $S$ within finite proper time or affine parameter. No extension in the sense of part 1 exists then: a smooth metric has continuous curvature invariants, bounded near any point.
>
> *The test, and its limit.* A divergent invariant proves a curvature singularity. A finite invariant proves nothing by itself. Couzens: one "would have to consider all possible scalar quantities", and even that is not enough, since a singular point can have all invariants zero around it ([[#^ex-c7-1-3|Example §C7.1.3]]). A coordinate singularity is proved only by exhibiting the regular chart $y$. A singularity in which a freely falling worldline simply ends, with or without divergent invariants, is the general notion; it is defined in C10 (planned).
>
> *Schwarzschild.* $r = 0$ is a curvature singularity: $K = 48M^2/r^6$ is unbounded along every radial infall, which reaches $r = 0$ in finite proper time ([[#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]]). $r = 2M$ has finite $K$, which only suggests a coordinate singularity. The proof is the Eddington–Finkelstein chart, in which the metric is smooth and nondegenerate there ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]). The axis $\theta = 0, \pi$ is a coordinate singularity of the familiar kind ([[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^ex-c2-1-3|Example §C2.1.3]]).
>
> *Layer change.* [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^rem-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Remark: Coordinate singularities are failures of a chart, not of the manifold]] described coordinate singularities on a given manifold, where another chart of the same atlas covers the bad points. Here the bad points are not yet in the manifold at all. Part 1 asks for a larger manifold, and finding it is part of the problem.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.3, p. 131 (PDF 132) ("a genuine singularity" at $r = 0$ vs "a result of our choice of coordinates"; the limits of the scalar test) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §17.2, p. 323 (PDF 338) ("removable" coordinate singularities; "intrinsic, curvature, physical, essential, or real" singularity); §18.1, p. 343 (PDF 358) (geodesics that end at an intrinsic singularity) · Schutz, §11.2, pp. 315–316 (PDF 333–334)*

## A falling clock: finite proper time, infinite coordinate time

Curvature says $r = 2M$ is probably harmless. A second chart-independent test is a physical one: drop a clock and read it. Proper time along a worldline is a number attached to the worldline, not to the chart ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]). [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]] did this for one release point. The theorem below does it for every radial infall and shows where the difference between the two clocks comes from.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.2: Radial Infall: Finite Proper Time, Infinite Coordinate Time
> Let a test body fall radially inward ($L = 0$) on a timelike geodesic in region I, with conserved energy $E > 0$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]), parametrized by proper time $\tau$.
> 1. **Equations of motion.** By [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]] with $L = 0$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\Bigr)^2 = E^2 - 1 + \frac{2M}{r}, \qquad \frac{dt}{d\tau} = \frac{E}{1 - 2M/r}, \qquad \frac{d^2r}{d\tau^2} = -\frac{M}{r^2} .
> $$
>
>    At $r = 2M$ the speed is $dr/d\tau = -E$, finite, and none of the three right-hand sides involving $r$ alone is singular there; only $dt/d\tau$ is.
> 2. **Finite proper time to $2M$.** Once the body moves inward at $r_1 > 2M$ with $dr/d\tau = -v_1 < 0$, it never turns back and reaches $r = 2M$ after a proper time
>
> $$
> \tau(r_1 \to 2M) \le \frac{r_1 - 2M}{v_1} .
> $$
>
>    Closed forms: from rest at $r_0$, $\tau = \sqrt{r_0^3/8M}\,(\eta + \sin\eta)$ with $r = \tfrac12r_0(1 + \cos\eta)$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]]); from rest at infinity ($E = 1$), $\tau_2 - \tau_1 = \tfrac23\bigl(r_1^{3/2} - r_2^{3/2}\bigr)/\sqrt{2M}$, the Newtonian free-fall law.
> 3. **Infinite coordinate time.** For every $E > 0$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dt}{dr} = -\frac{E}{f\sqrt{E^2 - f}} = -\frac{2M}{r - 2M} + O(1) \quad (r \to 2M^+), \qquad t = -2M\ln\frac{r - 2M}{2M} + \text{const} + o(1) \to +\infty ,
> $$
>
>    so $r - 2M \propto e^{-t/2M}$: in Schwarzschild time the body approaches $2M$ exponentially and never arrives. For $E = 1$ exactly, with $x = \sqrt{r/2M}$,
>
> $$
> t = -2M\Bigl[\tfrac23x^3 + 2x + \ln\Bigl|\frac{x - 1}{x + 1}\Bigr|\Bigr] + \text{const} .
> $$
>
> 4. **⚑ The worldline does not end at $2M$; the chart does.** The body reaches $r = 2M$ at a finite reading of its own clock, at which $t = +\infty$. The solution $r(\tau)$ of part 1 continues through $r = 2M$ and reaches $r = 0$ after a further finite proper time, at most $2M/E$. Whether the region $r < 2M$ it enters is region II of the Setup, joined to region I, is settled in [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon|§C7.2]].
>
> Restored: $(dr/d\tau)^2 = (\mathcal E/mc^2)^2c^2 - c^2 + 2GM/r$; from rest at $r_0$ to $r = 0$, $\tau = \tfrac{\pi}{2}r_0^{3/2}/\sqrt{2GM}$; near the horizon $t = -(r_S/c)\ln\bigl[(r - r_S)/r_S\bigr] + \text{const}$, i.e. $r - r_S \propto e^{-c^3t/2GM}$.
>
> *Model:* radial timelike geodesic of the Schwarzschild metric; *applies:* exactly; parts 1–3 in region I, part 4 continues the solution of the radial equation, whose meaning inside is fixed in §C7.2.
>
> *Source: Schutz, §11.2, eqs. (11.58)–(11.62), pp. 316–317 (PDF 334–335) (finite $\tau$ for every $\tilde E$, $\log\varepsilon$ divergence of $t$ "which does not contain $\tilde E$") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §17.5, eqs. > [!derivation]- Derivation
> Write $f = 1 - 2M/r$; $f' = 2M/r^2 > 0$, so $f$ increases with $r$.
>
> **Step 1** (first integrals). With $L = 0$, [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]], parts 1–2, gives $\dot r^2 = E^2 - V(r)$ with $V = f$, and $\ddot r = -\tfrac12f' = -M/r^2$; [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]] gives $\dot t = E/f$. So $\dot r^2 = E^2 - 1 + 2M/r$, and at $r = 2M$, $\dot r^2 = E^2$. Inward motion is $\dot r = -\sqrt{E^2 - f}$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (no turning back). For $2M \le r \le r_1$, $f(r) \le f(r_1)$, hence
>
> $$
> \dot r^2 = E^2 - f(r) \ \ge\ E^2 - f(r_1) = v_1^2 > 0 .
> $$
>
> So $\dot r$ never vanishes on the way in, $|\dot r| \ge v_1$, and
>
> $$
> \tau(r_1 \to 2M) = \int_{2M}^{r_1}\frac{dr}{|\dot r|} \le \int_{2M}^{r_1}\frac{dr}{v_1} = \frac{r_1 - 2M}{v_1} .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** ($E = 1$). Then $\dot r^2 = 2M/r$, so $d\tau = -\sqrt{r/2M}\,dr$ and
>
> $$
> \tau_2 - \tau_1 = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2M}}\int_{r_1}^{r_2}r^{1/2}\,dr = \frac{2}{3\sqrt{2M}}\bigl(r_1^{3/2} - r_2^{3/2}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> d'Inverno's (17.15). Newton's energy equation $\tfrac12\dot r^2 = M/r$ for a fall from rest at infinity gives the same integral with $t$ for $\tau$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 4** ($dt/dr$ near $2M$). Divide the two first integrals:
>
> $$
> \frac{dt}{dr} = \frac{\dot t}{\dot r} = -\frac{E}{f\sqrt{E^2 - f}} = -\frac1f\Bigl(1 - \frac{f}{E^2}\Bigr)^{-1/2} = -\frac1f\Bigl(1 + \frac{f}{2E^2} + O(f^2)\Bigr) = -\frac1f - \frac{1}{2E^2} + O(f) .
> $$
>
> With $r = 2M + \delta$: $1/f = r/(r - 2M) = (2M + \delta)/\delta = 2M/\delta + 1$. So
>
> $$
> \frac{dt}{dr} = -\frac{2M}{\delta} - 1 - \frac{1}{2E^2} + O(\delta) ,
> $$
>
> whose only non-integrable term is $-2M/\delta$, independent of $E$. Integrating from some $r_1$ down to $r = 2M + \delta$ (inward, $dr < 0$, so $t$ increases):
>
> $$
> t(\delta) - t(r_1) = -2M\ln\frac{\delta}{r_1 - 2M} + O(1) \;\longrightarrow\; +\infty \qquad (\delta \to 0) .
> $$
>
> Solving for $\delta$: $\delta = 2M\,e^{-(t - C)/2M}\,[1 + o(1)]$ for a constant $C$. This is the first half of part 3.
>
> **Step 5** ($E = 1$ in closed form). Put $x = \sqrt{r/2M}$, so $r = 2Mx^2$, $dr = 4Mx\,dx$, $f = 1 - 1/x^2 = (x^2 - 1)/x^2$ and $\sqrt{E^2 - f} = \sqrt{1 - f} = 1/x$. Then
>
> $$
> dt = -\frac{dr}{f\sqrt{1 - f}} = -\frac{x^2}{x^2 - 1}\cdot x\cdot 4Mx\,dx = -4M\,\frac{x^4}{x^2 - 1}\,dx = -4M\Bigl(x^2 + 1 + \frac{1}{x^2 - 1}\Bigr)dx ,
> $$
>
> by the polynomial division $x^4 = (x^2 - 1)(x^2 + 1) + 1$. With the partial fractions $\frac{1}{x^2 - 1} = \frac12\bigl(\frac{1}{x - 1} - \frac{1}{x + 1}\bigr)$,
>
> $$
> t = -4M\Bigl(\frac{x^3}{3} + x + \frac12\ln\Bigl|\frac{x - 1}{x + 1}\Bigr|\Bigr) + \text{const} = -2M\Bigl[\tfrac23x^3 + 2x + \ln\Bigl|\frac{x - 1}{x + 1}\Bigr|\Bigr] + \text{const} .
> $$
>
> It agrees with d'Inverno's (17.17), rearranged (checked by script), and its logarithm reproduces Step 4: near $x = 1$, $\ln(x - 1) = \ln\bigl((r - 2M)/(4M)\bigr) + O(r - 2M)$.
>
> **Step 6** (through $2M$ and on to $0$). The equation $\ddot r = -M/r^2$ has a smooth right-hand side for all $r > 0$, so its solution through the values at $r = 2M$ ($r = 2M$, $\dot r = -E$) continues to $r < 2M$, and the first integral $\dot r^2 = E^2 - f$ continues with it. Inside, $-f = h > 0$, so $\dot r^2 = E^2 + h \ge E^2$, and
>
> $$
> \tau(2M \to 0) = \int_0^{2M}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{E^2 + h}} \le \frac{2M}{E} .
> $$
>
> This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The infinite coordinate time comes from the factor $1/f$ in $\dot t = E/f$, the same zero of $f$ as in [[#^thm-c7-1-1|Theorem §C7.1.1]]. Nothing in the body's own equations ($\dot r$, $\ddot r$) is singular at $2M$. The divergence has the universal form $-2M\ln(r - 2M)$, independent of how the body was thrown in, as a property of the coordinate $t$ should be.

## Radial light: the cones close up

Light shows the same failure of $t$ without any mass or energy to choose. The light cones of the chart are the picture behind [[#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]], part 3: a body moves inside the cones, and if they close up, so does its worldline in the $(r, t)$ diagram.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.3: Radial Light in Schwarzschild Coordinates: dt/dr = ±1/f, Cones Close Up at 2M
> Radial null curves ($\theta$, $\varphi$ fixed, $ds^2 = 0$) of the Schwarzschild metric in regions I and II:
> 1. **Slopes.**
>
> $$
> \frac{dt}{dr} = \pm\frac{1}{1 - 2M/r}, \qquad t = \pm\Bigl(r + 2M\ln\Bigl|\frac{r}{2M} - 1\Bigr|\Bigr) + \text{const} .
> $$
>
>    The combination in brackets is the tortoise coordinate $r_\ast$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]); radial light is $t \mp r_\ast = \text{const}$.
> 2. **Cones close up at $2M$.** In the $(r, t)$ diagram of region I the light cone at radius $r$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]) has edges of slope $dr/dt = \pm(1 - 2M/r)$: half-opening $0.8$ at $10M$, $\tfrac13$ at $3M$, $0.091$ at $2.2M$, and $0$ in the limit $r \to 2M$. At large $r$ the cones are the $45°$ cones of flat spacetime. An ingoing ray reaches $r = 2M$ only as $t \to +\infty$, like $t = -2M\ln(r - 2M) + \text{const}$.
> 3. **Locally, light still moves at speed $1$.** A static observer at $r > 2M$ ([[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay#^def-c6-6-1|Def. §C6.6.1]]) with orthonormal frame ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]) $e_{\hat 0} = f^{-1/2}\partial_t$, $e_{\hat r} = f^{1/2}\partial_r$ sees radial light along $e_{\hat 0} \pm e_{\hat r}$: speed $1$, as at every event ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-1|Theorem §C3.2.1]]). The closing-up is a property of the coordinate basis $\partial_t$, $\partial_r$, whose norms $-f$ and $1/f$ degenerate.
> 4. **Inside.** In region II the slopes are again $dr/dt = \pm f$, with $|dr/dt| = 2M/r - 1$, growing without bound as $r \to 0$. The cones lie on their side in the $(r, t)$ diagram, both edges opening toward smaller or both toward larger $r$; which one is the future is [[#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]].
>
> Restored: coordinate speed of radial light $|dr/dt| = c\,(1 - r_S/r)$, measured locally $c$.
>
> *Model:* radial null geodesics of the Schwarzschild metric; *applies:* exactly, in each region separately.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.3, eqs. (6.89)–(6.90), Fig. 19, pp. 131–132 (PDF 132–133) ("the light cones close up") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §17.4, eqs. (17.3)–(17.11), Fig. 17.7, pp. 325–327 (PDF 340–342) (the diagram "is misleading") · Schutz, §11.2, eq. (11.64), Fig. 11.11, pp. 318–319 (PDF 336–337) (exponent: [[#^cau-c7-1-1|Caution: Schutz's slope of the light cones]])*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (null condition). With $d\theta = d\varphi = 0$,
>
> $$
> 0 = ds^2 = -f\,dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{f} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad dt^2 = \frac{dr^2}{f^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dt}{dr} = \pm\frac1f ,
> $$
>
> valid wherever $f \neq 0$, inside as well as outside.
>
> **Step 2** (integrate). $\frac1f = \frac{r}{r - 2M} = 1 + \frac{2M}{r - 2M}$, so $\int\frac{dr}{f} = r + 2M\ln|r - 2M| + c = r + 2M\ln\bigl|\frac{r}{2M} - 1\bigr| + c'$, the absolute value covering both regions. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 3** (opening and arrival time). The edges in the $(r, t)$ plane have $dr/dt = \pm f$. In region I, $0 < f < 1$ and $f \to 0$ as $r \to 2M$; values: $f(10M) = 0.8$, $f(3M) = 1/3$, $f(2.2M) = 0.0909$. Along an ingoing ray, $t = -(r + 2M\ln(r/2M - 1)) + \text{const}$, and $-2M\ln(r/2M - 1) \to +\infty$ as $r \to 2M^+$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 4** (static observer). The vector $k = \partial_t + f\,\partial_r$ has $dr/dt = f$ and is null: $g(k, k) = -f\cdot 1 + \frac1f\cdot f^2 = 0$. In the frame of part 3, $\partial_t = f^{1/2}e_{\hat 0}$ and $\partial_r = f^{-1/2}e_{\hat r}$, so
>
> $$
> k = f^{1/2}e_{\hat 0} + f\cdot f^{-1/2}e_{\hat r} = f^{1/2}\bigl(e_{\hat 0} + e_{\hat r}\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> a null vector at $45°$ in the observer's frame; the ingoing one, $\partial_t - f\,\partial_r$, gives $e_{\hat 0} - e_{\hat r}$. The frame is orthonormal: $g(e_{\hat 0}, e_{\hat 0}) = f^{-1}(-f) = -1$, $g(e_{\hat r}, e_{\hat r}) = f\cdot f^{-1} = 1$. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (inside). For $0 < r < 2M$, $|f| = 2M/r - 1$, which is $1$ at $r = M$ and $\to \infty$ as $r \to 0$. Both null directions have $dr \neq 0$ (Step 1 with $dt$ finite), so neither edge points along $\partial_t$. This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The cones close because $dt$ per unit $dr$ of light, $1/f$, diverges: the same factor as in $\dot t = E/f$ for bodies. Measured with an orthonormal frame, every cone in region I is the ordinary one. The diagram drawn in $(r, t)$ is faithful to the coordinates, not to the geometry.

## Inside r = 2M: r is time

The infalling body of [[#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]] continues to $r < 2M$. Region II is described by the same formula with the signs of $g_{tt}$ and $g_{rr}$ exchanged, and that exchange decides everything there.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.1.4: ⚑ Inside r < 2M, r Is a Time Coordinate
> On region II, with $h = 2M/r - 1 > 0$, so that $ds^2 = h\,dt^2 - h^{-1}dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2$:
> 1. **The coordinates swap roles.** $g_{tt} = h > 0$ and $g_{rr} = -1/h < 0$: $\partial_t$ is spacelike and $\partial_r$ timelike ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-1|Def. §C3.2.1]]); $\partial_\theta$, $\partial_\varphi$ stay spacelike. The label $t$ names a direction in space.
> 2. **Every causal vector moves in $r$.** A nonzero causal vector $X$ at a point of II has $X^r \neq 0$; precisely,
>
> $$
> (X^r)^2 \ \ge\ h^2(X^t)^2 + h\,r^2\bigl((X^\theta)^2 + \sin^2\theta\,(X^\varphi)^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
>    For a four-velocity $u$ this gives $|dr/d\tau| \ge \sqrt{h} = \sqrt{2M/r - 1}$.
> 3. **Time orientation.** The field $T = -\partial_r$ is timelike everywhere on II and is a time orientation ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]]); the only other one is $+\partial_r$. With $T = -\partial_r$, a causal vector is future-directed exactly when $X^r < 0$. This is the choice under which region II continues the future of bodies falling in from region I ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-2|Theorem §C7.2.2]]); the opposite choice describes a different region, the white hole ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-5|Def. §C7.2.5]]).
> 4. **⚑ The singularity is in the future, not in a place.** With $T = -\partial_r$, $r$ decreases strictly along every future-directed causal curve. No observer can stay at constant $r$, and no light signal, in any direction, moves to larger $r$. A future-directed timelike curve starting at $r_a$ has proper time at most $\int_0^{r_a}dr/\sqrt{2M/r - 1} \le \pi M$ before $r$ reaches $0$; extended as far as it goes, it ends there (which curve comes closest to the bound: [[§C7.4 Falling into a Black Hole#^thm-c7-4-4|Theorem §C7.4.4]]). The surfaces $r = \text{const} < 2M$ are spacelike, so $r = 0$ is a moment of time ahead of every observer in II, like tomorrow, rather than a point in space that could be steered around. In Kruskal coordinates this moment is the spacelike hyperbola $T = \sqrt{1 + X^2}$ ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]], part 4).
>
> Restored: $h = r_S/r - 1$; $|dr/d\tau| \ge c\sqrt{r_S/r - 1}$; the bound $\pi GM/c^3$.
>
> *Model:* the Schwarzschild metric on region II (Ricci-flat, Setup), time-oriented by $-\partial_r$; *applies:* exactly.
>
> *Source: Schutz, §11.2, eq. (11.63), pp. 317–318 (PDF 335–336) ($r$ "is a timelike coordinate", $t$ "has become spacelike"; "$r = 0$ is in the future of every timelike and null world line inside $r = 2M$") · d'Inverno & Vickers, §17.2, p. 323 (PDF 338) ($t$ and $r$ "reverse their character"); §17.4, p. 326 (PDF 341) ("cannot stay at rest") · Hartle, §12.2, p. 265 (PDF 266) · checked by script*

^thm-c7-1-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (signs). On II, $f = 1 - 2M/r < 0$; write $h = -f$. Then $g_{tt} = -f = h > 0$ and $g_{rr} = 1/f = -1/h < 0$. So $g(\partial_t, \partial_t) > 0$ (spacelike), $g(\partial_r, \partial_r) < 0$ (timelike), $g(\partial_\theta, \partial_\theta) = r^2 > 0$, $g(\partial_\varphi, \partial_\varphi) = r^2\sin^2\theta > 0$. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (causal vectors). For $X = X^t\partial_t + X^r\partial_r + X^\theta\partial_\theta + X^\varphi\partial_\varphi$, with $A \equiv r^2\bigl((X^\theta)^2 + \sin^2\theta\,(X^\varphi)^2\bigr) \ge 0$,
>
> $$
> g(X, X) = h\,(X^t)^2 - \frac{(X^r)^2}{h} + A \le 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (X^r)^2 \ge h^2(X^t)^2 + h\,A ,
> $$
>
> multiplying by $h > 0$. If $X^r = 0$, the right side, a sum of non-negative terms, must vanish: $X^t = 0$ and $A = 0$, i.e. $X^\theta = 0$ and (off the axis) $X^\varphi = 0$, so $X = 0$. For a four-velocity, $g(u, u) = -1$ gives $(u^r)^2 = h + h^2(u^t)^2 + hA \ge h$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 3** (orientation). $g(-\partial_r, -\partial_r) = g_{rr} = -1/h < 0$ at every point of II, and $-\partial_r$ is smooth, so it is a time orientation. Region II is connected, so there are exactly two time orientations, $\pm\partial_r$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-2|Def. §C3.2.2]], "exactly two"). For causal $X$,
>
> $$
> g(-\partial_r, X) = -g_{rr}X^r = \frac{X^r}{h} ,
> $$
>
> negative (future-directed) exactly when $X^r < 0$. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 4** ($r$ decreases; finite time). Along a future-directed causal curve $\lambda \mapsto x(\lambda)$ the velocity is future-directed and causal at each point, so $dr/d\lambda = \dot x^r < 0$ by Steps 2–3: $r$ is strictly decreasing. Constant $r$ would need $\dot x^r = 0$, excluded by Step 2. For a timelike curve parametrized by $\tau$, $dr/d\tau \le -\sqrt h$, so the proper time from $r_a$ to $r \to 0$ is
>
> $$
> \tau = \int_0^{r_a}\frac{dr}{|dr/d\tau|} \le \int_0^{r_a}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{2M/r - 1}} \le \int_0^{2M}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{2M/r - 1}} = \pi M ,
> $$
>
> the last integral by $r = 2M\sin^2\psi$, $dr = 4M\sin\psi\cos\psi\,d\psi$, $\sqrt{2M/r - 1} = \cos\psi/\sin\psi$:
>
> $$
> \int_0^{2M}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{2M/r - 1}} = \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\sin\psi}{\cos\psi}\,4M\sin\psi\cos\psi\,d\psi = 4M\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2\psi\,d\psi = 4M\cdot\frac{\pi}{4} = \pi M .
>
> **Step 5** ($r = \text{const}$ is spacelike). The hypersurface $r = r_a$ has normal one-form $dr$, whose squared norm is $g^{rr} = f = -h < 0$: the normal is timelike, so the hypersurface is spacelike, like $t = \text{const}$ in Minkowski spacetime. This is part 4.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** All of part 4 follows from one sign, $g_{rr} < 0$. The light cone at every event of II contains $\pm\partial_r$ in its interior, so "forward in time" and "inward in $r$" are the same statement once the orientation is fixed. A body inside cannot avoid $r = 0$ for the same reason that no one can avoid next Tuesday.

> [!remark] Remark: The coordinate-pole analogy
> A world map drawn on a rectangle with longitude across and latitude up shows the north pole as a whole horizontal line, although it is one point; nothing on the paper says so, and only a geometric measurement does (circles of latitude shrink to zero circumference). Schutz draws exactly this (his Fig. 11.10) to prepare the reader for $r = 2M$. In the figure after [[#^thm-c7-1-3|Theorem §C7.1.3]] no worldline reaches the line $r = 2M$ at finite $t$, and Schutz suggests that this line, too, might be fewer points than it looks. The suspicion is correct and is confirmed in [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension|§C7.3]]: approached at fixed finite $t$, $r \to 2M$ leads to one and the same sphere of the extended spacetime, and the infaller crosses $r = 2M$ "at $t = +\infty$", on a different surface. The lesson is that of [[§C2.1 Spacetime as a Manifold#^rem-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Remark: Coordinate singularities are failures of a chart, not of the manifold]], with one Lorentzian twist that Schutz points out: on a sphere, zero distance means the same point, but in spacetime distinct events on a light ray have zero interval, so the geometric check is harder.
>
> *Source: Schutz, §11.2, Fig. 11.10, pp. 315–316 (PDF 333–334); p. 319 (PDF 337) ("a whole line in the bad coordinates representing a point in the real space")*

^rem-c7-1-1

The verdict so far: at $r = 2M$ curvature is finite, a falling clock passes in finite proper time, and every static observer sees ordinary light cones. All the evidence points to a failure of the chart, and none of it proves one. What is missing is a chart in which the metric is regular across $r = 2M$ and the infalling worldline is a smooth curve. Such a chart would also say whether region II is attached to region I, and in which time orientation. [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon|§C7.2]] builds it by following the ingoing light rays of [[#^thm-c7-1-3|Theorem §C7.1.3]], and finds that the light cones do not close up: they tip over.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C7.1.1: Infall from 6M and from the Horizon
> *Question.* A body is released from rest at $r_0 = 6M$, the innermost stable circular orbit ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]]). How long does it take, on its own clock and in Schwarzschild time, to reach $r = 2M$ and $r = 0$? What if it is released from rest just outside $r = 2M$? Give the times for a $10\,M_\odot$ hole and for Sgr A$^\ast$.
>
> *Setup.* From rest at $r_0$, $E^2 = 1 - 2M/r_0$, and [[#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]], part 2, gives the cycloid $r = \tfrac12r_0(1 + \cos\eta)$, $\tau = \sqrt{r_0^3/8M}\,(\eta + \sin\eta)$, with $r = 2M$ at $\cos\eta_H = 4M/r_0 - 1$ and $r = 0$ at $\eta = \pi$.
>
> *(a) From $6M$.* $E^2 = 1 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac23$, $E = 0.8165$. The prefactor is $\sqrt{(6M)^3/8M} = \sqrt{27}\,M = 5.196M$. At the horizon, $\cos\eta_H = \tfrac46 - 1 = -\tfrac13$, so $\eta_H = 1.9106$ and $\sin\eta_H = \sqrt{1 - \tfrac19} = \tfrac{2\sqrt2}{3} = 0.9428$:
>
> $$
> \tau(2M) = 5.196M\,(1.9106 + 0.9428) = 14.83M, \qquad \tau(0) = 5.196M\cdot\pi = 3\sqrt3\,\pi M = 16.32M .
> $$
>
> The last $1.50M$ is spent inside, within the bound $2M/E = 2.45M$ of [[#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]], part 4. Schwarzschild time, by quadrature of $dt/dr$ (part 3):
>
> | $r$ reached | $2.1M$ | $2.001M$ | $2M + 10^{-6}M$ | $2M + 10^{-9}M$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | $t$ since release | $25.29M$ | $34.67M$ | $48.49M$ | $62.31M$ |
>
> Each factor $1000$ in $r - 2M$ costs $13.82M = 2M\ln 1000$ more, as part 3 predicts; $t$ never reaches $2M$ (figure after [[#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]]).
>
> *(b) From rest just outside the horizon.* The time inside is $\tau(0) - \tau(2M) = \sqrt{r_0^3/8M}\,(\pi - \eta_H - \sin\eta_H)$. As $r_0 \to 2M^+$, $\cos\eta_H = 4M/r_0 - 1 \to 1$, so $\eta_H \to 0$, and $\sqrt{r_0^3/8M} \to \sqrt{8M^3/8M} = M$. Hence
>
> $$
> \tau(2M \to 0) \longrightarrow \pi M; \qquad r_0 = 2.2M: 2.254M, \quad 2.02M: 2.786M, \quad 2.002M: 3.020M .
> $$
>
> This is the largest time inside that a free fall can give ([[#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]], part 4; [[§C7.4 Falling into a Black Hole#^thm-c7-4-4|Theorem §C7.4.4]]).
>
> *(c) In seconds* ($GM/c^3$; $GM_\odot/c^3 = 4.926\ \mu\mathrm s$; Sgr A$^\ast$ with Schutz's $4.15\times10^{6}M_\odot$, dated, as in [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-3|Example §C6.3.3]]):
>
> | Hole | $GM/c^3$ | $6M \to 2M$ | $2M \to 0$ (from $6M$) | $\pi GM/c^3$ | $t$ per factor $1000$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $10\,M_\odot$ | $49.26\ \mu\mathrm s$ | $0.730\ \mathrm{ms}$ | $73.8\ \mu\mathrm s$ | $154.7\ \mu\mathrm s$ | $0.681\ \mathrm{ms}$ |
> | Sgr A$^\ast$ | $20.44\ \mathrm s$ | $303.1\ \mathrm s$ | $30.6\ \mathrm s$ | $64.2\ \mathrm s$ | $282\ \mathrm s$ |
>
> *Result.* A stellar-mass hole swallows the body in under a millisecond of its own time and Sgr A$^\ast$ in about five and a half minutes, while the Schwarzschild clock runs for ever. Of the whole fall only the final tenth is spent inside $2M$, and the time inside can never exceed $\pi GM/c^3$: $155\ \mu\mathrm s$ and about a minute.
>
> *Source: Schutz, §11.2, eqs. (11.59)–(11.62), pp. 316–317 (PDF 334–335) · Schutz, §11.4, p. 338 (PDF 356) (Sgr A$^\ast$ mass; dated) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §17.5, pp. 327–328 (PDF 342–343) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C7.1.2: What the Distant Observer Sees: Exponential Redshift of an Infalling Source
> *Question* (Hartle, Example 12.2). A source falls freely from rest at $r_0 = 10M$ and emits light of fixed frequency $\nu_E$ in its own rest frame, radially outward. A static observer far away receives it. How does the received frequency fall with the time of reception, and what does the observer see of the crossing of $r = 2M$?
>
> *Setup.* Outgoing radial light keeps $u \equiv t - r_\ast$ constant ([[#^thm-c7-1-3|Theorem §C7.1.3]], part 1), with $r_\ast = r + 2M\ln(r/2M - 1)$ in region I. A receiver at radius $r_R \gg 2M$ gets the signal emitted with label $u$ at $t_R = u + r_\ast(r_R)$, and its clock reads $d\tau_R = \sqrt{f(r_R)}\,dt_R \to dt_R$. Differences of $u$ are differences of reception time. The source has $E = \sqrt{1 - 2M/10M} = 0.8944$ and $dr/d\tau = -\sqrt{E^2 - f}$, $dt/d\tau = E/f$ ([[#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]], part 1).
>
> **Step 1** (label rate along the source). With $dr_\ast/dr = 1/f$,
>
> $$
> \frac{du}{d\tau_E} = \frac{dt}{d\tau} - \frac1f\frac{dr}{d\tau} = \frac Ef + \frac{\sqrt{E^2 - f}}{f} = \frac{E + \sqrt{E^2 - f}}{f} \qquad (f = f(r_E)) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (frequency). Successive wave crests are separated by $d\tau_E$ at the source and by $dt_R = du$ at the distant receiver, so
>
> $$
> \frac{\nu_\infty}{\nu_E} = \frac{d\tau_E}{du} = \frac{f_E}{E + \sqrt{E^2 - f_E}} .
> $$
>
> At release ($\dot r = 0$, $E = \sqrt{f_0}$) this is $\sqrt{f_0} = 0.894$, the static redshift of [[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay#^thm-c6-6-1|Theorem §C6.6.1]].
>
> **Step 3** (two factors). The static observer at $r_E$ measures the source's Lorentz factor $\gamma = E/\sqrt{f_E}$ (as in [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-3|Example §C6.3.3]]), so its speed is $v = \sqrt{E^2 - f_E}/E$. Using $(E - \sqrt{E^2 - f})(E + \sqrt{E^2 - f}) = f$,
>
> $$
> \frac{f_E}{E + \sqrt{E^2 - f_E}} = \underbrace{\sqrt{f_E}}_{\text{gravitational}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\sqrt{f_E}}{E + \sqrt{E^2 - f_E}}}_{=\sqrt{(1 - v)/(1 + v)}} ,
> $$
>
> the static redshift from $r_E$ times the Doppler factor of a source receding at $v$ ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]]).
>
> **Step 4** (late times). Near $2M$, $t_E + r_{\ast E}$ tends to a constant (the $-1/f$ in $dt/dr$ of the derivation of [[#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]], Step 4, is exactly $-dr_\ast/dr$), so $u = t_E - r_{\ast E} = -2r_{\ast E} + C + o(1)$. With $r_{\ast E} = 2M + 2M\ln(r_E/2M - 1) + o(1)$:
>
> $$
> u = -4M\ln\Bigl(\frac{r_E}{2M} - 1\Bigr) + C' + o(1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_E - 2M \propto e^{-u/4M}, \qquad \frac{\nu_\infty}{\nu_E} \approx \frac{f_E}{2E} \propto e^{-u/4M} ,
> $$
>
> since $f_E = (r_E - 2M)/r_E$. The reception time scale is $4M$, twice the $2M$ of the source's own coordinate time (part 3 of the theorem), because the light's travel time out from near $2M$ diverges as well.
>
> **Step 5** (numbers, by quadrature of Steps 1–2):
>
> | $r_E$ | $9M$ | $6M$ | $4M$ | $3M$ | $2.5M$ | $2.1M$ | $2.01M$ | $2.001M$ | $2.0001M$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $u - u_{\text{release}}$ | $16.95M$ | $36.09M$ | $46.23M$ | $52.10M$ | $56.30M$ | $63.82M$ | $73.27M$ | $82.50M$ | $91.71M$ |
> | $\nu_\infty/\nu_E$ | $0.745$ | $0.529$ | $0.347$ | $0.211$ | $0.120$ | $0.0270$ | $2.79\times10^{-3}$ | $2.79\times10^{-4}$ | $2.80\times10^{-5}$ |
>
> Late on, each further factor $10$ costs $\Delta u = 9.21M = 4M\ln 10$; the fitted slope is $d\ln\nu_\infty/du = -0.2500/M$.
>
> *Result.* The distant observer sees the source slow down, freeze just above $r = 2M$ and fade: the frequency and the arrival rate of photons each drop by $e$ every $4GM/c^3 = 19.7\ \mu\mathrm s\,(M/M_\odot)$ (Hartle's $2.0\times10^{-5}(M/M_\odot)\ \mathrm s$), i.e. $197\ \mu\mathrm s$ for $10\,M_\odot$. The light emitted exactly at $r = 2M$ would arrive at $u = \infty$: the crossing is never seen, although the source crossed after a finite time on its own clock ([[#^ex-c7-1-1|Example §C7.1.1]]). What the source sends after crossing is the subject of [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]].
>
> *Source: Hartle, §12.2, Example 12.2, eqs. (12.8)–(12.12), pp. 266–267 (PDF 267–268) (done there in Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates for a collapsing stellar surface; here in Schwarzschild coordinates, exact) · Couzens, GR1, §6.3, Fig. 20, p. 132 (PDF 133) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C7.1.3: A Flat Cone: All Invariants Vanish, yet the Apex Is Singular
> *Question.* [[#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]] says that finite curvature invariants do not prove regularity. Is there a simple geometry in which every invariant vanishes near a point and yet no smooth metric exists at it?
>
> *Setup.* Cut a wedge of angle $2\pi(1 - \alpha)$, $0 < \alpha < 1$, out of a flat paper disc and glue the edges: a cone. In the coordinates $\rho$ (distance from the apex along the paper) and $\varphi \in [0, 2\pi)$ its metric is
>
> $$
> ds^2 = d\rho^2 + \alpha^2\rho^2\,d\varphi^2, \qquad \rho > 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 1** (flat away from the apex). On any range of $\varphi$ shorter than $2\pi$, put $x = \rho\cos\alpha\varphi$, $y = \rho\sin\alpha\varphi$. Then $dx = \cos\alpha\varphi\,d\rho - \alpha\rho\sin\alpha\varphi\,d\varphi$, $dy = \sin\alpha\varphi\,d\rho + \alpha\rho\cos\alpha\varphi\,d\varphi$, and
>
> $$
> dx^2 + dy^2 = (\cos^2 + \sin^2)\,d\rho^2 + 2\alpha\rho(-\cos\sin + \sin\cos)\,d\rho\,d\varphi + \alpha^2\rho^2(\sin^2 + \cos^2)\,d\varphi^2 = d\rho^2 + \alpha^2\rho^2\,d\varphi^2 .
> $$
>
> The cone is locally the Euclidean plane, so its Riemann tensor vanishes for $\rho > 0$ (checked by script), and so does every curvature invariant.
>
> **Step 2** (circles about the apex). The lines $\varphi = \text{const}$ are straight lines of the flat picture, hence geodesics, and the circle $\rho = \rho_1$ lies at distance $\rho_1$ from the apex along them. Its length is $\int_0^{2\pi}\alpha\rho_1\,d\varphi = 2\pi\alpha\rho_1$, so $C/\rho_1 = 2\pi\alpha$ for every $\rho_1$.
>
> **Step 3** (a smooth metric at the apex is impossible). If some chart around the apex $p$ carried a smooth metric agreeing with the cone for $\rho > 0$, normal coordinates at $p$ would exist ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^thm-c3-8-1|Theorem §C3.8.1]], read with $\delta$ for $\eta$), with $g_{ij} = \delta_{ij} + O(y^2)$ ([[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^thm-c3-8-2|Theorem §C3.8.2]]). A geodesic circle of radius $\rho$ would then have length $2\pi\rho\,(1 + O(\rho^2))$, as computed for the sphere in [[§C3.8 Normal Coordinates#^ex-c3-8-1|Example §C3.8.1]], and $C/\rho \to 2\pi$. The cone gives $2\pi\alpha \neq 2\pi$. For a $90°$ wedge removed, $\alpha = \tfrac34$ and $C/\rho = 1.5\pi = 4.712$ against $2\pi = 6.283$.
>
> *Result.* The apex is a genuine singular point, with no curvature invariant to announce it: all of them are zero all the way in. Regularity is shown only by a regular chart, as [[#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]] requires, and for $r = 2M$ that chart is [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]].
>
> *Source: not from the sources (example added); the limitation it illustrates: Couzens, GR1, §6.3, p. 131 (PDF 132) ("if it does not diverge we cannot say much") · checked by script*
