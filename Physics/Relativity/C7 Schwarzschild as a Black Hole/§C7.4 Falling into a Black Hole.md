A body falls radially into a Schwarzschild black hole. It crosses $r = 2M$ after a finite proper time ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]]), on a worldline that is smooth there in Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^ex-c7-2-2|Example §C7.2.2]]), and once inside it cannot return ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]]). This section asks what the body itself experiences: *what does it feel on the way in, where is it destroyed, how long does it have, and are the black holes of the sky this object?*

Gravity is felt only through tides. A freely falling body carries no weight; what it can measure are the relative accelerations of its parts, the tidal tensor of its own four-velocity ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^def-c3-12-4|Def. §C3.12.4]]). The naive way to get a number is to read a curvature component off the Schwarzschild chart. The radial components (from the Riemann components of [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^der-c6-2-7|the derivation of Theorem §C6.2.7]], converted below) are, with $f = 1 - 2M/r$,

$$
R_{trtr} = -\frac{2M}{r^3}, \qquad R^t{}_{rtr} = g^{tt}R_{trtr} = \frac{2M}{r^3 f} \;\xrightarrow{\;r\to2M\;}\; \infty, \qquad R^r{}_{trt} = g^{rr}R_{rtrt} = -\frac{2Mf}{r^3} \;\xrightarrow{\;r\to2M\;}\; 0 .
$$

One mixed component diverges at the horizon and another vanishes. If the first were the tide, every body would be ripped apart at $r = 2M$; if the second, it would feel nothing there at all. Neither is what the body feels: coordinate components carry the chart's degeneration at $r = 2M$ ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^def-c7-1-2|Def. §C7.1.2]]). A second warning sign points the same way. An observer who hovers at fixed $r$ ([[§C6.6 Redshift and Time Delay#^def-c6-6-1|Def. §C6.6.1]]) needs a rocket thrust per unit mass $(1 - 2M/r)^{-1/2}M/r^2$, which grows without bound as $r \to 2M$ (Hartle, Box 12.2). That is the cost of *not* falling, and it says nothing about a body that falls.

The section builds the answer in order. In the falling body's own parallel frame the tide is $+2M/r^3$ along the radius and $-M/r^3$ across it, the same as Newton's, for any speed of fall and through the horizon ([[#^thm-c7-4-1|Theorem §C7.4.1]]). From it follow the radius at which a body of given strength is torn apart, outside the horizon for stellar holes and inside for supermassive ones ([[#^thm-c7-4-2|Theorem §C7.4.2]]), and the statement that nothing local marks the horizon ([[#^thm-c7-4-3|Theorem §C7.4.3]]). Inside, the time left is at most $\pi M$ ([[#^thm-c7-4-4|Theorem §C7.4.4]]). Last, the black holes that astronomy finds ([[#^law-c7-4-5|Law §C7.4.5]]), and the comparison with Michell's Newtonian "dark star", whose radius level A called the event horizon ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton#^def-a8-5-1|CM Def. §A8.5.1]]).

**Setup.** The Schwarzschild metric of mass $M > 0$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]]) on the exterior $r > 2M$ and the black-hole region $0 < r < 2M$, joined across $r = 2M$ by ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]). For a hole formed by collapse this is the geometry outside the collapsing matter ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-5|Theorem §C6.2.5]]). Falling bodies are *test bodies*: their own gravity is neglected; they fall radially, on timelike geodesics ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^pr-c3-7-5|Principle §C3.7.5]]) with conserved energy per unit mass $E$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]); a body of size $L$ is treated as free particles whose separations obey the deviation equation to first order in $L/r$, until its internal forces fail. Theorems §C7.4.1, §C7.4.3 and §C7.4.4 are exact statements about this geometry; [[#^thm-c7-4-2|Theorem §C7.4.2]] adds the first-order deviation model of a body. [[#^law-c7-4-5|Law §C7.4.5]] is observational and dated; it rests on Newtonian dynamics far from the holes and on the image of one hole.

*Conventions* ([[General Relativity Conventions]]): signature $-{+}{+}{+}$; $G = c = 1$, each measurable result also restored ([[§C1.2 Signature, Units and the Dictionary to Level B#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]: $M \to GM/c^2$, $\tau \to c\tau$, a tidal component $\to$ itself times $c^2$ in $\mathrm s^{-2}$); $f \equiv 1 - 2M/r$; curvature as in [[§C3.9 The Riemann Tensor#^def-c3-9-1|Def. §C3.9.1]]; hatted indices are components in an orthonormal frame ([[§C3.1 The Metric Tensor#^def-c3-1-6|Def. §C3.1.6]]); the tidal tensor of a four-velocity $u$ is $\mathcal E_u(S) = R(S, u)u$ ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^def-c3-12-4|Def. §C3.12.4]]), so a separation obeys $d^2S^{\hat\imath}/d\tau^2 = -\mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath}S^{\hat\jmath}$ and a negative eigenvalue stretches. Solar mass $GM_\odot/c^2 = 1476.7$ m; $g = 9.80665\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.

## The tide on a falling body

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.4.1: The Tidal Tensor of a Radially Falling Observer
> In the Schwarzschild spacetime, at a point with areal radius $r > 0$:
> 1. **The curvature in covariant form.** For vectors $a, b, c, d$ in the radial plane spanned by $\partial_t$ and $\partial_r$, and with $e_\theta$, $e_\varphi$ the unit vectors along $\partial_\theta$, $\partial_\varphi$,
>
> $$
> R(a, b, c, d) = \frac{2M}{r^3}\bigl[g(a, c)g(b, d) - g(a, d)g(b, c)\bigr], \qquad R(e_\theta, a, e_\theta, b) = R(e_\varphi, a, e_\varphi, b) = -\frac{M}{r^3}\,g(a, b),
> $$
>
>    $R(e_\theta, e_\varphi, e_\theta, e_\varphi) = 2M/r^3$, and every component with an odd number of entries among $e_\theta, e_\varphi$, or with one $e_\theta$ and one $e_\varphi$ and two radial entries, vanishes.
> 2. **Every radial observer sees the same tide.** Let $u$ be any unit timelike vector in the radial plane (any observer moving radially, at any speed, falling or not) and $n$ the unit radial vector orthogonal to it. Its tidal tensor ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^def-c3-12-4|Def. §C3.12.4]]) in the frame $(u, n, e_\theta, e_\varphi)$ is
>
> $$
> \mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath} = \operatorname{diag}\Bigl(-\frac{2M}{r^3},\ \frac{M}{r^3},\ \frac{M}{r^3}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 3. **The falling body.** For radial free fall with energy $E$, the four-velocity and the frame
>
> $$
> u = \frac{E}{f}\,\partial_t - \sqrt{E^2 - f}\,\partial_r, \qquad n = -\frac{\sqrt{E^2 - f}}{f}\,\partial_t + E\,\partial_r, \qquad e_\theta = \frac1r\,\partial_\theta, \qquad e_\varphi = \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\,\partial_\varphi
> $$
>
>    are parallel-transported along the worldline, so this is the frame of a freely falling observer with non-rotating axes, and by [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]] the separations of neighbouring free particles obey
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2S^{\hat r}}{d\tau^2} = +\frac{2M}{r^3}\,S^{\hat r}, \qquad \frac{d^2S^{\hat\theta}}{d\tau^2} = -\frac{M}{r^3}\,S^{\hat\theta}, \qquad \frac{d^2S^{\hat\varphi}}{d\tau^2} = -\frac{M}{r^3}\,S^{\hat\varphi} ;
> $$
>
>    restored, the coefficients are $+2GM/r^3$ and $-GM/r^3$ in $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$, with $\tau$ in seconds. These are the Newtonian tidal accelerations of a point mass ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]], part 4), now exact, with $r$ the areal radius and $\tau$ proper time.
> 4. **Through the horizon.** Parts 1–3 hold for $r < 2M$ as well, and at $r = 2M$ the tidal eigenvalues are finite, $-1/(4M^2)$ and $1/(8M^2)$ (restored $-c^6/(4G^2M^2)$ and $c^6/(8G^2M^2)$). They grow without bound only as $r \to 0$.
>
> *Model:* the Schwarzschild metric; radial observers, free particles in part 3; *applies:* exactly for the tidal tensor; the deviation equations to first order in the separation.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, §21.3, eqs. (21.30)–(21.31), p. 455 (PDF 454) (falling from rest at infinity, $E = 1$) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §17.10, eqs. (17.31)–(17.39), pp. 335–336 (PDF 350–351); Ex. 17.18 · general $E$, part 2 and part 4 worked here; checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (coordinate components). [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^der-c6-2-7|The derivation of Theorem §C6.2.7]], Step 1, gives the components in the static orthonormal frame $e_{\hat t} = f^{-1/2}\partial_t$, $e_{\hat r} = f^{1/2}\partial_r$, $e_{\hat\theta} = r^{-1}\partial_\theta$, $e_{\hat\varphi} = (r\sin\theta)^{-1}\partial_\varphi$: $R_{\hat t\hat r\hat t\hat r} = -2M/r^3$, $R_{\hat t\hat\theta\hat t\hat\theta} = M/r^3$, $R_{\hat r\hat\theta\hat r\hat\theta} = -M/r^3$, $R_{\hat\theta\hat\varphi\hat\theta\hat\varphi} = 2M/r^3$, and the same with $\varphi$ for $\theta$. Multiplying by the coordinate lengths ($\partial_t = f^{1/2}e_{\hat t}$, $\partial_r = f^{-1/2}e_{\hat r}$, $\partial_\theta = r\,e_{\hat\theta}$):
>
> $$
> R_{trtr} = f\cdot f^{-1}\cdot\Bigl(-\frac{2M}{r^3}\Bigr) = -\frac{2M}{r^3}, \qquad R_{t\theta t\theta} = f r^2\,\frac{M}{r^3} = \frac{Mf}{r}, \qquad R_{r\theta r\theta} = \frac{r^2}{f}\Bigl(-\frac{M}{r^3}\Bigr) = -\frac{M}{rf}, \qquad R_{\theta\varphi\theta\varphi} = 2Mr\sin^2\theta .
> $$
>
> These are rational functions of $r$ computed from the metric components, so the same formulas hold in the Schwarzschild chart of the region $r < 2M$, where $f < 0$ (checked by script there too).
>
> **Step 2** (covariant form, part 1). The radial block of the metric has $g_{tt}g_{rr} - g_{tr}^2 = (-f)(1/f) - 0 = -1$, so $\frac{2M}{r^3}(g_{tt}g_{rr} - g_{tr}g_{rt}) = -\frac{2M}{r^3} = R_{trtr}$. Both sides of the first formula of part 1 are antisymmetric in $(a, b)$ and in $(c, d)$ ([[§C3.10 Symmetries of the Riemann Tensor and the Bianchi Identities#^thm-c3-10-1|Theorem §C3.10.1]]), so on a two-dimensional plane they agree once they agree on $(\partial_t, \partial_r, \partial_t, \partial_r)$. For the second, with $g_{\theta\theta} = r^2$, $e_\theta = r^{-1}\partial_\theta$:
>
> $$
> R(e_\theta, \partial_t, e_\theta, \partial_t) = \frac{R_{t\theta t\theta}}{r^2} = \frac{Mf}{r^3} = -\frac{M}{r^3}\,g_{tt}, \qquad R(e_\theta, \partial_r, e_\theta, \partial_r) = \frac{R_{r\theta r\theta}}{r^2} = -\frac{M}{r^3 f} = -\frac{M}{r^3}\,g_{rr},
> $$
>
> and $R_{t\theta r\theta} = 0 = -\frac{M}{r^3}g_{tr}r^2$. The remaining components vanish (Step 1 lists all nonzero ones up to symmetries; checked by script).
>
> **Step 3** (any radial observer, part 2). With $g(u, u) = -1$, $g(n, n) = 1$, $g(u, n) = 0$ and part 1:
>
> $$
> \mathcal E_{\hat r\hat r} = R(n, u, n, u) = \frac{2M}{r^3}\bigl[g(n, n)g(u, u) - g(n, u)^2\bigr] = \frac{2M}{r^3}(1\cdot(-1) - 0) = -\frac{2M}{r^3},
> $$
>
> $$
> \mathcal E_{\hat\theta\hat\theta} = R(e_\theta, u, e_\theta, u) = -\frac{M}{r^3}\,g(u, u) = \frac{M}{r^3}, \qquad \mathcal E_{\hat\varphi\hat\varphi} = \frac{M}{r^3},
> $$
>
> using $\mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath} = R_{\hat\imath\hat0\hat\jmath\hat0}$ ([[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^def-c3-12-4|Def. §C3.12.4]]). The off-diagonal entries $R(n, u, e_\theta, u)$, $R(n, u, e_\varphi, u)$ have one angular entry and $R(e_\theta, u, e_\varphi, u)$ has one $e_\theta$ and one $e_\varphi$, so they vanish by part 1. Nothing in this step used the speed of $u$: the radial plane is two-dimensional, and the combination $g(n, n)g(u, u) - g(n, u)^2$ is the same for every orthonormal pair in it.
>
> **Step 4** (the falling frame, part 3). By [[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]] and the normalization, a radial geodesic has $u^t = E/f$ and $(u^r)^2 = E^2 - f$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]] with $L = 0$); falling in, $u^r = -\sqrt{E^2 - f}$. The vector $n$ is orthogonal and unit:
>
> $$
> g(u, n) = -f\,\frac{E}{f}\Bigl(-\frac{\sqrt{E^2 - f}}{f}\Bigr) + \frac1f\bigl(-\sqrt{E^2 - f}\bigr)E = 0, \qquad g(n, n) = -f\,\frac{E^2 - f}{f^2} + \frac{E^2}{f} = \frac{-E^2 + f + E^2}{f} = 1 .
> $$
>
> Parallel transport ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^def-c3-7-1|Def. §C3.7.1]]): $u$ is parallel because it is geodesic. The Christoffel symbols of the metric with two radial-plane indices have a radial-plane upper index ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-1|Theorem §C6.2.1]]), so $Dn/d\tau$ lies in the radial plane. Differentiating $g(u, n) = 0$ and $g(n, n) = 1$ along the curve ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-1|Theorem §C3.7.1]], metric compatibility) gives $g(u, Dn/d\tau) = 0$ and $g(n, Dn/d\tau) = 0$; a vector of the radial plane orthogonal to both $u$ and $n$ is zero. For $e_\theta$, whose only component is $e^\theta = 1/r$, with $\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta} = 1/r$:
>
> $$
> \frac{De^\theta}{d\tau} = \frac{d}{d\tau}\Bigl(\frac1r\Bigr) + \Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}\,u^r e^\theta = -\frac{u^r}{r^2} + \frac1r\,u^r\,\frac1r = 0 ,
> $$
>
> and its $t$ and $r$ components get $\Gamma^t{}_{a\theta} = \Gamma^r{}_{a\theta} = 0$ for $a = t, r$; likewise for $e_\varphi$ (checked by script). Part 1 of [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]] with Step 3 gives the three equations of part 3.
>
> **Step 5** (the horizon, part 4). In ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates, $v = t + r_\ast$ with $dr_\ast/dr = 1/f$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-2|Def. §C7.2.2]]), the frame of Step 4 has components $u^v = u^t + u^r/f$, $n^v = n^t + n^r/f$:
>
> $$
> u^v = \frac{E - \sqrt{E^2 - f}}{f} = \frac{1}{E + \sqrt{E^2 - f}} = n^v, \qquad u^r = -\sqrt{E^2 - f}, \qquad n^r = E ,
> $$
>
> multiplying numerator and denominator by $E + \sqrt{E^2 - f}$. These are smooth at $f = 0$, where $u^v = 1/(2E)$; the metric is smooth there ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]), so its Riemann tensor is too. The identities of part 1 are equalities of smooth tensor fields on the Eddington–Finkelstein chart that hold on $r \neq 2M$; by continuity they hold at $r = 2M$, and Step 3 applies there. At $r = 2M$: $2M/(2M)^3 = 1/(4M^2)$ and $M/(2M)^3 = 1/(8M^2)$. Restoring, $2GM/r^3$ at $r = 2GM/c^2$ is $2GMc^6/(8G^3M^3) = c^6/(4G^2M^2)$.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The radial plane of Schwarzschild is curved by the same amount, $2M/r^3$, whichever observer slices it, and the spheres by $M/r^3$; a radial boost cannot change either. So the tide does not depend on how fast the body falls, and it is the Newtonian tide exactly. The divergence of $R^t{}_{rtr}$ in the opening is the factor $g^{tt} = -1/f$, a property of the chart.

> [!caution] Caution: The frame in d'Inverno & Vickers' §17.10
> d'Inverno & Vickers derive (17.37)–(17.39) from the deviation equation (17.31) in the frame (17.32)–(17.35), whose time leg is $(1 - 2m/r)^{-1/2}\partial_t$: the four-velocity of a *static* observer, not of the falling particles whose separation (17.31) describes, and a frame that exists only for $r > 2m$. The stated equations are nevertheless right, also inside the horizon, because by part 2 of [[#^thm-c7-4-1|Theorem §C7.4.1]] every radially moving observer has the same tidal tensor. Hartle's (21.30) uses the freely falling frame from rest at infinity.

A body is not a cloud of free particles: its internal forces resist the tide up to some limit. Where along the fall is that limit reached, and on which side of the horizon?

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.4.2: Where a Body Is Torn Apart
> A body of length $L$ along the radius falls radially into the hole. Model it as two free particles at its ends, which its internal forces can hold together as long as the relative acceleration they must supply stays below $a_{\max}$ (restored: in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$).
> 1. **Tear radius.** The tide demands the relative acceleration $2ML/r^3$ ([[#^thm-c7-4-1|Theorem §C7.4.1]], part 3) at areal radius $r$, whatever the energy of the fall, so the body is torn apart at
>
> $$
> r_{\text{tear}} = \Bigl(\frac{2ML}{a_{\max}}\Bigr)^{1/3} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } r_{\text{tear}} = \Bigl(\frac{2GML}{a_{\max}}\Bigr)^{1/3}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
>    The transverse squeeze, $ML/r^3$ across a width $L$, reaches $a_{\max}$ at the smaller radius $2^{-1/3}r_{\text{tear}}$.
> 2. **Inside or outside.** $r_{\text{tear}} > 2M$ exactly when $M < M_{\text{crit}}$, with
>
> $$
> M_{\text{crit}} = \frac12\sqrt{\frac{L}{a_{\max}}} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } M_{\text{crit}} = \frac{c^3}{2G}\sqrt{\frac{L}{a_{\max}}}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
>    Holes lighter than $M_{\text{crit}}$ tear the body before it reaches the horizon; heavier holes let it cross intact. Since $r_{\text{tear}} \propto M^{1/3}$ and $2M \propto M$, the heavier the hole, the deeper inside the horizon the tearing happens.
> 3. **⚑ The time left is universal.** For a fall from rest at infinity ($E = 1$) the proper time from $r_{\text{tear}}$ to $r = 0$ is
>
> $$
> \tau_{\text{tear}} = \frac23\sqrt{\frac{L}{a_{\max}}} ,
> $$
>
>    the same for every mass of hole (restored: the same formula, in seconds).
> 4. **Numbers** for a human-sized body, $L = 2$ m, $a_{\max} = 10g = 98.1\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$: $M_{\text{crit}} = 2.88\times10^{34}\ \mathrm{kg} = 1.45\times10^4M_\odot$ and $\tau_{\text{tear}} = 0.095$ s. A $10M_\odot$ hole tears the body at $r_{\text{tear}} = 3.78\times10^6$ m $= 128$ horizon radii out; Sgr A$^\ast$ ($4.15\times10^6M_\odot$) at $2.82\times10^8$ m, $0.023$ of its horizon radius.
>
> *Model:* [[#^thm-c7-4-1|Theorem §C7.4.1]]; the body as two free test particles held by internal forces up to $a_{\max}$; *applies:* to first order in $L/r$ (here $L/r_{\text{tear}} \le 10^{-6}$), radial fall, the body's own gravity and rotation neglected; part 3 for $E = 1$ exactly.
>
> *Source: Schutz Ex. 11.25, p. 357 (PDF 375) (minimum mass for survival to the horizon); Ex. 11.28 (tidal radius of a star) · Hartle, Gravity, Ex. 21.3, pp. 455–456 (PDF 454–455) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §17.10, p. 336 (PDF 351) (the astronaut falling feet first) · formulas of parts 1–3 worked here; numbers checked by script*

^thm-c7-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (tear radius). With the separation $S^{\hat r} = L$ between the ends, part 3 of [[#^thm-c7-4-1|Theorem §C7.4.1]] says that free ends would separate with $d^2S^{\hat r}/d\tau^2 = 2ML/r^3$. Holding them together requires internal forces supplying this relative acceleration. Setting it equal to the limit:
>
> $$
> \frac{2ML}{r^3} = a_{\max} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^3 = \frac{2ML}{a_{\max}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_{\text{tear}} = \Bigl(\frac{2ML}{a_{\max}}\Bigr)^{1/3} .
> $$
>
> The tide grows monotonically as $r$ decreases, so the limit is first reached there. Across the body, $ML/r^3 = a_{\max}$ gives $r^3 = ML/a_{\max} = \tfrac12r_{\text{tear}}^3$. Restoring $M \to GM$ (the tide $2GML/r^3$ is in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$) gives the restored form. This is part 1.
>
> **Step 2** (compare with the horizon). All quantities are positive, so
>
> $$
> r_{\text{tear}} > 2M \iff \frac{2ML}{a_{\max}} > 8M^3 \iff M^2 < \frac{L}{4a_{\max}} \iff M < \frac12\sqrt{\frac{L}{a_{\max}}} .
> $$
>
> In geometric units $a_{\max}$ is $a_{\max}/c^2$ (a length$^{-1}$) and $M$ is $GM/c^2$; substituting, $GM/c^2 < \frac12\sqrt{Lc^2/a_{\max}}$, i.e. $M < \frac{c^3}{2G}\sqrt{L/a_{\max}}$. Finally $r_{\text{tear}}/2M = (2L/a_{\max})^{1/3}M^{-2/3}/2$ decreases with $M$. This is part 2.
>
> **Step 3** (time left). For $E = 1$, $(dr/d\tau)^2 = E^2 - f = 2M/r$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]] with $L = 0$; the same relation holds inside, [[#^der-c7-4-4|derivation of Theorem §C7.4.4]], Step 5). Falling inward, $d\tau = -\sqrt{r/2M}\,dr$, so
>
> $$
> \tau(r \to 0) = \int_0^{r}\sqrt{\frac{r'}{2M}}\,dr' = \frac{2}{3}\,\frac{r^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2M}} .
> $$
>
> At $r = r_{\text{tear}}$, $r^{3/2} = \sqrt{r^3} = \sqrt{2ML/a_{\max}}$, so $\tau_{\text{tear}} = \frac23\sqrt{2ML/a_{\max}}/\sqrt{2M} = \frac23\sqrt{L/a_{\max}}$. Restored, $r^{3/2}/\sqrt{2GM}$ is in seconds and the same cancellation gives the same formula. This is part 3.
>
> **Step 4** (numbers). $c^3/(2G) = (2.998\times10^8)^3/(2\times6.674\times10^{-11}) = 2.019\times10^{35}\ \mathrm{kg/s}$ and $\sqrt{2/98.1} = 0.1428$ s, so $M_{\text{crit}} = 2.88\times10^{34}$ kg; divided by $1.988\times10^{30}$ kg, $1.45\times10^4M_\odot$. $\tau_{\text{tear}} = \frac23\times0.1428\ \mathrm s = 0.0952$ s. For $10M_\odot$: $GM = 1.327\times10^{21}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}$, $r_{\text{tear}} = (2\times1.327\times10^{21}\times2/98.1)^{1/3} = 3.78\times10^6$ m, and $2GM/c^2 = 2.95\times10^4$ m. For $4.15\times10^6M_\odot$: $r_{\text{tear}} = 2.82\times10^8$ m against $2GM/c^2 = 1.226\times10^{10}$ m (checked by script).
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The tide at fixed $r$ grows like $M$, but the horizon radius grows like $M$ too, so the tide *at the horizon*, $2M/(2M)^3 = 1/(4M^2)$, falls like $1/M^2$. Small holes are violent outside, large holes are gentle at the horizon. The universal time in part 3 is the free-fall time of the stretching mode itself: once the tide has reached $a_{\max}$, the remaining fall to $r = 0$ always takes a fixed multiple of $\sqrt{L/a_{\max}}$.

## The horizon is not a place you can feel

A body heavy enough to cross intact ([[#^thm-c7-4-2|Theorem §C7.4.2]], part 2) passes $r = 2M$ with tides of order $1/M^2$. Is there any local sign that it has crossed?

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.4.3: ⚑ Nothing Special Happens Locally at the Horizon
> 1. **Curvature.** At $r = 2M$ every radially moving observer measures the tidal eigenvalues $-1/(4M^2)$ (radial) and $1/(8M^2)$ (twice, transverse) ([[#^thm-c7-4-1|Theorem §C7.4.1]], part 4), and the Kretschmann scalar is $3/(4M^4)$ ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-7|Theorem §C6.2.7]], part 2): finite, and smaller the heavier the hole. Restored, the radial tide at the horizon is $c^6/(4G^2M^2)$: $1.03\times10^8\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$ for $10M_\odot$, $6.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$ for Sgr A$^\ast$ ($4.15\times10^6M_\odot$), about $200$ times the vertical tide at the Earth's surface, $3.07\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$.
> 2. **Worldline and frame.** The infalling worldline, its parallel frame and the metric are smooth at $r = 2M$ in Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]; [[#^der-c7-4-1|derivation of Theorem §C7.4.1]], Step 5), and the crossing happens at a finite proper time ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]]).
> 3. **No local test.** A freely falling laboratory of size $L$ and duration $T$ at the horizon is a local inertial frame ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^def-c1-3-2|Def. §C1.3.2]]; [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-4|Def. §C3.2.4]]) under the same condition as anywhere with the same curvature, $\tfrac12|\mathcal E|LT^2 < \delta$ with $|\mathcal E| = 1/(4M^2)$. By the Einstein equivalence principle ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]; geometric form [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]]) every non-gravitational experiment in it gives the results of special relativity, before, at and after the crossing. No local measurement tells the body that it has crossed.
> 4. **What singles out the horizon is global.** Whether an event lies inside is decided by whether outgoing light sent from it ever reaches large $r$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]]): a property of the whole future of the spacetime, not of the neighbourhood of the event.
>
> *Model:* the Schwarzschild metric; a freely falling laboratory; *applies:* exactly for parts 1, 2 and 4; part 3 within the stated bound on $L$ and $T$.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, §21.3, after eq. (21.31), p. 455 (PDF 454) (tidal accelerations at $2M$ finite, of order $1/M^2$) · Schutz §11.1, p. 298 (PDF 316) (the horizon "a mere separatrix" in empty space) · d'Inverno & Vickers, §17.12, p. 339 (PDF 354) (horizon tide of a supermassive hole small) · numbers checked by script*

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (curvature). Part 4 of [[#^thm-c7-4-1|Theorem §C7.4.1]] and part 2 of [[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-7|Theorem §C6.2.7]]. Restored: $c^6/(4G^2M^2)$ with $c^6 = 7.27\times10^{50}\ \mathrm{m^6/s^6}$ and, for $10M_\odot$, $GM = 1.327\times10^{21}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}$: $7.27\times10^{50}/(4\times1.761\times10^{42}) = 1.03\times10^8\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$; for $4.15\times10^6M_\odot$, $GM = 5.51\times10^{26}$: $7.27\times10^{50}/(4\times3.04\times10^{53}) = 6.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$. The Earth's value is $2GM_\oplus/R_\oplus^3 = 3.07\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$ ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^cau-c1-3-1|§C1.3, Caution: "Equivalence" is local]]); the ratio is $195$.
>
> **Step 2** (smoothness). In ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates the metric components are polynomials in $f$ and $r$ with $\det g = -r^4\sin^2\theta \neq 0$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]); the frame components $u^v = n^v = 1/(E + \sqrt{E^2 - f})$, $u^r = -\sqrt{E^2 - f}$, $n^r = E$ are smooth for $E > 0$ near $f = 0$ ([[#^der-c7-4-1|derivation of Theorem §C7.4.1]], Step 5).
>
> **Step 3** (local inertial frame). Near the event of crossing, [[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]] gives coordinates with $g = \eta$ and $\partial g = 0$ at the event, the departure from $\eta$ being of second order with coefficients set by the curvature, here of size $1/(4M^2)$. Free particles in the laboratory drift apart by about $\tfrac12|\mathcal E|LT^2$ ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^thm-c1-3-6|Theorem §C1.3.6]], with $E_{ij}$ replaced by $\mathcal E_{\hat\imath\hat\jmath}$ through [[§C3.12 Geodesic Deviation#^thm-c3-12-4|Theorem §C3.12.4]], part 1). Every ingredient of this condition is a scalar or a frame component, the same as at any other event with the same tide; nothing in it refers to $r = 2M$. The equivalence principle then applies as stated.
>
> **Step 4** (global). [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]] defines the horizon by which events can send signals to large $r$, a condition on the null curves leaving the event all the way to the future. Steps 1–3 involve only the curvature and the metric near the event.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** The horizon is where the light cones have tipped so far that outgoing light no longer gains radius ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-2|Theorem §C7.2.2]]), and that tipping is visible only by comparing cones at different places. In the body's own frame the cone is the cone of special relativity, and the curvature is the same mild $1/(4M^2)$ found just outside and just inside.

## How long inside

Inside the horizon $r$ decreases along every future-directed worldline ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]), so $r = 0$ lies in the future of everyone who has crossed. Hartle's victim of space pirates hopes for "about a minute" to look around ([[#^ex-c7-4-1|Example §C7.4.1]]). How much proper time is there, and does firing a rocket buy more?

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.4.4: The Maximum Proper Time inside the Horizon Is πM
> 1. **Bound.** Along every future-directed timelike curve in the region $0 < r < 2M$, the proper time spent there is at most
>
> $$
> \tau_{\max} = \pi M \qquad (\text{restored: } \pi GM/c^3) ,
> $$
>
>    whatever forces act on the body.
> 2. **When it is reached.** Equality needs $dt = d\theta = d\varphi = 0$ along the curve: the radial geodesic with $E = 0$, which lies entirely inside the horizon. In the Kruskal extension this is the observer at the throat of the Einstein–Rosen bridge, who lives $\pi M$ from the widest moment to the pinch-off ([[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-4|Theorem §C7.3.4]], part 4). A body that enters from outside, with or without rockets, spends strictly less than $\pi M$; falling freely after release from rest at $r_0$, it comes arbitrarily close to $\pi M$ as $r_0 \to 2M$.
> 3. **Free fall with energy $E$.** On a radial geodesic the proper time from $r = 2M$ to $r = 0$ is
>
> $$
> \tau_{\text{in}}(E) = \int_0^{2M}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{E^2 - 1 + 2M/r}} ,
> $$
>
>    strictly decreasing in $|E|$: $\pi M$ for $E \to 0$, $1.42M$ after release from rest at $10M$ ($E^2 = 0.8$), $\tfrac43M$ for a fall from rest at infinity ($E = 1$).
> 4. **Numbers.** $\pi GM/c^3 = 155\ \mu$s for $10M_\odot$, $64.2$ s for Sgr A$^\ast$ ($4.15\times10^6M_\odot$), $46.4$ s for Hartle's $3\times10^6M_\odot$.
>
> *Model:* the Schwarzschild metric in the black-hole region; any timelike worldline for parts 1–2, radial geodesics for part 3; *applies:* exactly, up to the singularity, where the classical description ends.
>
> *Source: Hartle, Gravity, Prob. 12.14, p. 278 (PDF 279) (the question; no answer printed) · Schutz §11.2 (inside $r = 2M$ every worldline reaches $r = 0$), p. 318 (PDF 336) · bound, equality case and part 3 proved here; checked by script*

^thm-c7-4-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the metric inside). For $r < 2M$ put $h \equiv -f = 2M/r - 1 > 0$. The Schwarzschild metric ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-3|Theorem §C6.2.3]], read on region II, $0 < r < 2M$, as in [[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities|§C7.1]]) reads
>
> $$
> ds^2 = h\,dt^2 - \frac{dr^2}{h} + r^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** ($r$ as a parameter). On a future-directed causal curve $dr/d\lambda < 0$ ([[§C7.1 Coordinate and Curvature Singularities#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]), so $r$ is strictly monotonic and can label the points of the curve, running from $2M$ down toward $0$.
>
> **Step 3** (inequality). For a timelike curve the proper time is $d\tau^2 = -ds^2$ ([[§C3.2 Lorentzian Manifolds꞉ Light Cones, Proper Time, Local Inertial Frames#^def-c3-2-3|Def. §C3.2.3]]):
>
> $$
> d\tau^2 = \frac{dr^2}{h} - h\,dt^2 - r^2\bigl(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\bigr) \le \frac{dr^2}{h} ,
> $$
>
> since $h > 0$ and the dropped terms are squares. Hence $d\tau \le |dr|/\sqrt h$, with equality at a point exactly when $dt = d\theta = d\varphi = 0$ there.
>
> **Step 4** (the integral). Summing over the curve, $\tau \le \int_0^{2M}dr/\sqrt{2M/r - 1} = \int_0^{2M}\sqrt{r/(2M - r)}\,dr$. Substitute $r = 2M\sin^2\alpha$, $0 \le \alpha \le \pi/2$: $dr = 4M\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\,d\alpha$ and $\sqrt{r/(2M - r)} = \sin\alpha/\cos\alpha$, so
>
> $$
> \int_0^{2M}\sqrt{\frac{r}{2M - r}}\,dr = \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha}\,4M\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\,d\alpha = 4M\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2\alpha\,d\alpha = 4M\cdot\frac{\pi}{4} = \pi M .
> $$
>
> This is part 1 (no equation of motion was used: rockets are allowed).
>
> **Step 5** (radial geodesics). The conserved energy $E = -g(\partial_t, u) = f\,u^t$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-1|Theorem §C6.3.1]]) is defined inside as well, because $\partial_t$ is a Killing vector there ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^rem-c6-2-1|§C6.2, Remark: The Killing vectors of the Schwarzschild metric]]), now spacelike. For a radial worldline the normalization $-f(u^t)^2 + (u^r)^2/f = -1$ multiplied by $f$ gives
>
> $$
> -E^2 + (u^r)^2 = -f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Bigl(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\Bigr)^2 = E^2 - f = E^2 - 1 + \frac{2M}{r} ,
> $$
>
> the same algebra as outside ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-2|Theorem §C6.3.2]]). With $dr/d\tau < 0$, $\tau_{\text{in}}(E) = \int_0^{2M}dr/\sqrt{E^2 - 1 + 2M/r}$. The integrand is strictly decreasing in $E^2$ at each $r$, so $\tau_{\text{in}}$ is. For $E = 0$ it is the integral of Step 4. For $E = 1$: $\int_0^{2M}\sqrt{r/2M}\,dr = \frac{2}{3}(2M)^{3/2}/\sqrt{2M} = \frac43M$. For release from rest at $10M$, $E^2 = 1 - 2M/r_0 = 0.8$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^ex-c6-3-2|Example §C6.3.2]]) and the integral is $1.4232M$ (numerically; also from the cycloid of that example, $\tau(0) - \tau(2M) = 35.12M - 33.70M$). This is part 3.
>
> **Step 6** (equality case). Equality in Step 3 at every point means $dt = 0$, i.e. $u^t = 0$, i.e. $E = f u^t = 0$, together with $d\theta = d\varphi = 0$: a radial curve with $d\tau = |dr|/\sqrt h$, which by Step 5 is the radial geodesic with $E = 0$. A curve that enters from outside crosses $r = 2M$ at a finite value of $v$, and there $t = v - r_\ast \to +\infty$ on both sides, because $r_\ast \to -\infty$ ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]). So $t$ is not constant on any stretch of the curve just inside the horizon, $dt \neq 0$ there, and the inequality of Step 3 is strict, rockets or not. Falling freely from rest at $r_0 > 2M$, the body has $E^2 = 1 - 2M/r_0 > 0$, and by Step 5 its time inside is $\tau_{\text{in}}(E) < \pi M$, which tends to $\pi M$ as $r_0 \to 2M$. (Outside, $E^2 \ge f > 0$ rules out $E = 0$: the $E = 0$ geodesic never was outside; it crosses $r = 2M$ only at the bifurcation sphere of the Kruskal extension, [[§C7.3 The Kruskal Extension#^thm-c7-3-3|Theorem §C7.3.3]].) This is part 2.
>
> **Step 7** (numbers). $GM_\odot/c^3 = 1476.7\ \mathrm m/c = 4.926\ \mu\mathrm s$; $\pi\times10\times4.926\ \mu\mathrm s = 155\ \mu\mathrm s$; $\pi\times4.15\times10^6\times4.926\ \mu\mathrm s = 64.2$ s; $\pi\times3\times10^6\times4.926\ \mu\mathrm s = 46.4$ s.
>
> **What the derivation shows.** Inside the horizon $r$ is the clock of the region, and the longest stay is the one that spends nothing on motion in $t$ or across the sphere: every bit of $dt$ or $d\Omega$ is paid for in proper time, exactly as moving through space costs proper time in special relativity ([[§C3.7 Parallel Transport and Geodesics#^thm-c3-7-4|Theorem §C3.7.4]]). A rocket can help only by reducing $|E|$; thrusting "outward" in the sense of trying to escape adds motion and shortens the stay.

## Black holes in nature

> [!law] Law §C7.4.5: Astrophysical Black Holes
> Compact dark masses too large, for their size, to be any known kind of star are observed in two classes, and one has been imaged. As stated in the typed sources, with their dates:
> 1. **X-ray binaries.** A normal star orbits an unseen compact companion that accretes its gas into a hot, X-ray emitting disk. The companion's mass follows from the star's radial-velocity curve through the mass function $f = (M_2\sin i)^3/(M_1 + M_2)^2$ and Kepler's third law ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-4|CM Theorem §A8.3.4]]); a mass above the maximum mass of a neutron star marks a black-hole candidate (observed neutron stars, and a maximum not yet known but generally put below $3M_\odot$: [[§C9.3 White Dwarfs, Neutron Stars and Collapse#^law-c9-3-5|Law §C9.3.5]]). Examples: Nova Muscae, orbital period $0.423$ d, mass function about $3M_\odot$ (Hartle, 2003); Cygnus X-1, discovered by its rapid X-ray variations in 1971, unseen mass above $9M_\odot$ (d'Inverno & Vickers, 2022). Known stellar-mass candidates have masses of about $6$–$20M_\odot$ (Schutz, 2022).
> 2. **Galactic centres.** Orbits of masers, stars or gas around the centres of galaxies, read with Newtonian dynamics, show masses of $10^6$ to $10^{10}M_\odot$ in regions too small for anything but a black hole. NGC 4258: $3.5\times10^7M_\odot$ inside $0.13$ pc, with deviations from a Keplerian profile below $0.3\%$ (Hartle, 2003). Sgr A$^\ast$, at the centre of the Milky Way: $3\times10^6M_\odot$ from the orbits of nearby stars (Hartle, 2003, data of Ghez et al. 2002); $4.15\times10^6M_\odot$, with the mass confined to a region not much larger than the innermost stable circular orbit of such a hole (Schutz, 2022, citing the GRAVITY collaboration 2018).
> 3. **An image.** The Event Horizon Telescope combined millimetre-wave radio observations from around the Earth into an image of the hole at the centre of the galaxy M87, published in 2019: a ring of emission from hot gas, partly lensed toward us from behind the hole, around a dark centre, the inner edge of the ring lying inside $6M$ (Schutz, Fig. 11.9, 2022; d'Inverno & Vickers, §17.11, 2022: "agreed very well with the predictions of general relativity").
>
> *Valid when:* as of the sources' dates (Hartle 2003; Schutz and d'Inverno & Vickers 2022). Masses are inferred far from the holes, where Newtonian dynamics suffices; that the dark masses are black holes rather than unknown compact objects is the only known explanation, not a measurement of a horizon. The image probes the geometry near the photon sphere ([[#^ex-c7-4-2|Example §C7.4.2]]); gravitational waves from merging holes are the other direct probe ([[§C8.6 Binary Inspiral#^law-c8-6-7|Law §C8.6.7]]). Real holes rotate; the Schwarzschild geometry is the limit of zero spin (Hartle §13.2).
>
> *Model:* Newtonian orbits far from the hole for the masses; the Schwarzschild (or rotating) geometry near it for the image; *applies:* as in the Valid-when line.
>

> [!remark] Remark: Why a black hole is not a cosmic vacuum cleaner
> "Black holes irresistibly suck things in" is the myth Hartle's Box 12.2 corrects. Outside a spherical body of mass $M$ the geometry is the Schwarzschild geometry whether the body is a star or a hole ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^thm-c6-2-5|Theorem §C6.2.5]]), so every orbit that stays outside the star's radius is the same around both: replacing the Sun by a hole of the same mass would leave the Earth's orbit unchanged. A hole differs only in letting orbits continue below the radius where a star's surface would be, into the region where the effective potential has lost its barrier: no stable circular orbits inside $6M$, capture from infinity for $L < 4M$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-4|Theorem §C6.3.4]]), capture of light with $b < 3\sqrt3\,M$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-5|Theorem §C6.3.5]]). Gas reaches a hole by losing angular momentum in a disk, not by being pulled harder (Hartle §13.1). What does grow without bound near $2M$ is the thrust needed to *hover*, the cost of not falling (the opening of this section).

^rem-c7-4-1

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Michell's dark star and why it is not a black hole
> Level A introduced the radius $2GM/c^2$ with Michell's and Laplace's argument: the Newtonian escape speed $\sqrt{2GM/R}$ equals $c$ there ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton#^def-a8-5-1|CM Def. §A8.5.1]], [[§A8.5 Beyond Newton#^cau-a8-5-1|CM Caution: A Newtonian coincidence]]; Schutz (11.2)–(11.3); d'Inverno & Vickers (17.27)–(17.30)). The number survives as the Schwarzschild radius ([[§C6.2 Solving the Vacuum Equations; Birkhoff's Theorem#^def-c6-2-1|Def. §C6.2.1]]); the picture does not.
> - *A dark star still shines locally.* Its light leaves the surface, rises, stops and falls back, as a thrown ball does, and an observer anywhere below the turning point sees it. Light can also escape to any finite height with a boost. A dark star is dark only from far away.
> - *A black hole lets nothing out at all.* Light sent outward from inside $r = 2M$ never gains radius ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-2|Theorem §C7.2.2]], [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]]). Schutz's argument why the dark-star picture cannot be relativistic: light that turned around would be momentarily at rest next to a freely falling observer at the turning point, and light moves at $c$ in every local inertial frame ([[§C1.3 The Equivalence Principle#^pr-c1-3-5|Principle §C1.3.5]]).
> - *The horizon is not a surface of matter.* For Michell the dark star was a star. The horizon of [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]] lies in empty space, left behind by the collapsing matter, and is felt by no one who crosses it ([[#^thm-c7-4-3|Theorem §C7.4.3]]).
> - *The mean density argument carries over.* Both pictures share $\bar\rho = 3c^6/(32\pi G^3M^2)$ for the flat-space volume (Schutz (11.4)): large holes need no extreme density to form ([[#^cau-c7-4-2|Caution: Two numbers in d'Inverno & Vickers' §17.12]] has the numbers).
>
> So CM Def. §A8.5.1's "event horizon" is a Newtonian escape radius that happens to coincide in value; the event horizon proper is the null surface of [[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]], and its general definition comes with the causal structure of [[· C10 Causal Structure and Penrose Diagrams|C10]] ([[§C10.7 Black Holes and Event Horizons#^def-c10-7-3|Def. §C10.7.3]]).

^rem-c7-4-2

The tides of this section are those of a static hole: they ride along with the body and fade as $M/r^3$. Can a change in a tidal field travel through empty spacetime on its own, at the speed of light, and stretch and squeeze a ring of particles far from any source? C8 ([[§C8.1 Linearised Gravity and Its Gauge Freedom|§C8.1]]) linearises the field equations around flat spacetime to answer this.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C7.4.1: Falling into a Stellar-Mass versus a Supermassive Black Hole
> *Question* (Schutz Ex. 11.25; Hartle Ex. 21.3). How heavy must a hole be for a person to reach its horizon alive, and what happens to someone dropped into the hole at the centre of the Galaxy?
>
> *Setup.* Radial free fall; a person modelled as in [[#^thm-c7-4-2|Theorem §C7.4.2]]. Schutz's crush limit: an acceleration gradient of $400\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ per metre, i.e. a radial tide of $400\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$. Hartle's victim: mass $m = 80$ kg, height $h = 2$ m, able to stand a stretching force of $10^4$ N, dropped from far away ($E = 1$) into a hole of $3\times10^6M_\odot$. $GM_\odot/c^2 = 1476.7$ m, $GM_\odot/c^3 = 4.926\ \mu$s.
>
> **Step 1** (Schutz's threshold). The radial tide at the horizon is $c^6/(4G^2M^2)$ for every radial observer ([[#^thm-c7-4-3|Theorem §C7.4.3]], part 1; Schutz's trajectory of his Ex. 11.22, with $E = 0.95$, gives the same, by [[#^thm-c7-4-1|Theorem §C7.4.1]], part 2). Survival to the horizon needs
>
> $$
> \frac{c^6}{4G^2M^2} \le 400\ \mathrm{s^{-2}} \iff M \ge \frac{c^3}{2G\times20\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}} = \frac{c^3}{40\,G} = 1.01\times10^{34}\ \mathrm{kg} = 5.1\times10^3M_\odot .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (a $10M_\odot$ hole). Its horizon tide is $1.03\times10^8\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$, $2.06\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ head to foot over 2 m. With $a_{\max} = 10g$ the body is torn apart at $r_{\text{tear}} = 3.78\times10^6$ m, $128$ times the horizon radius $2.95\times10^4$ m ([[#^thm-c7-4-2|Theorem §C7.4.2]], part 4): far outside.
>
> **Step 3** (Hartle's pirates: the force at the horizon). For $3\times10^6M_\odot$, $GM = 3.98\times10^{26}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}$ and $r_S = 2GM/c^2 = 8.86\times10^9$ m. The stretching force is $m$ times the relative acceleration of head and feet, $2GMh/r^3$ ([[#^thm-c7-4-1|Theorem §C7.4.1]], part 3):
>
> $$
> F(r_S) = \frac{2GMmh}{r_S^3} = \frac{2\times3.98\times10^{26}\times80\times2}{(8.86\times10^9)^3}\ \mathrm N = 0.18\ \mathrm N .
> $$
>
> Hartle's rough estimate $(GmM/r^2)(h/r)$ omits the factor 2 and gives $0.09$ N, his ".1 N". Either way the crossing is unnoticeable.
>
> **Step 4** (where the victim is torn). $F(r) = 10^4$ N at
>
> $$
> r = \Bigl(\frac{2GMmh}{10^4\ \mathrm N}\Bigr)^{1/3} = \Bigl(\frac{2\times3.98\times10^{26}\times160}{10^4}\Bigr)^{1/3}\ \mathrm m = 2.34\times10^8\ \mathrm m = 2.34\times10^5\ \mathrm{km}
> $$
>
> (Hartle's estimate: $1.85\times10^5$ km, "$\sim 2\times10^5$ km"), $0.026$ of the horizon radius: most of the way down.
>
> **Step 5** (how long it takes). With $E = 1$, the time from the horizon to $r = 0$ is $\tfrac43GM/c^3 = \tfrac43\times3\times10^6\times4.926\ \mu\mathrm s = 19.7$ s ([[#^thm-c7-4-4|Theorem §C7.4.4]], part 3). The force reaches $10^4$ N, i.e. $a_{\max} = 10^4\ \mathrm N/80\ \mathrm{kg} = 125\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, only $\tfrac23\sqrt{2/125}\ \mathrm s = 0.084$ s before the end ([[#^thm-c7-4-2|Theorem §C7.4.2]], part 3). The victim's hope of "about a minute" inside is optimistic: no strategy gives more than $\pi GM/c^3 = 46.4$ s ([[#^thm-c7-4-4|Theorem §C7.4.4]], part 1), and a fall from far away gives $19.7$ s.
>
> **Step 6** (what it shows). The line between the two cases is a few thousand solar masses (Step 1; $1.45\times10^4M_\odot$ for $10g$ over 2 m, [[#^thm-c7-4-2|Theorem §C7.4.2]]). Every hole formed from a star lies below it and destroys an infaller well outside its horizon; every hole in a galactic centre lies above it ([[#^law-c7-4-5|Law §C7.4.5]]), and there the horizon passes unnoticed and the destruction comes a fraction of a second before $r = 0$.
>
> *Source: Schutz Ex. 11.25, p. 357 (PDF 375) · Hartle, Gravity, Ex. 21.3, pp. 455–456 (PDF 454–455) · numbers checked by script*

> [!example] Example §C7.4.2: The Size of the M87$^\ast$ Shadow
> *Question.* The Event Horizon Telescope image shows a dark centre ([[#^law-c7-4-5|Law §C7.4.5]], part 3). How large an angle should it subtend from the Earth?
>
> *Setup.* An observer at distance $D \gg M$ from a Schwarzschild hole, in nearly flat space (cosmological expansion neglected at $16$ Mpc). Light reaching her moves on null geodesics; trace each ray backward from her telescope. A ray arriving at a small angle $\alpha$ from the direction of the hole travelled, far from the hole, on a straight line at distance $b = D\alpha$ from the parallel line through the centre: its impact parameter ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-5|Theorem §C6.3.5]], part 2).
>
> **Step 1** (which directions are dark). Followed backward, a ray with $b > b_c = 3\sqrt3\,M$ has a turning point outside the photon sphere and came from far away or from gas outside the hole; a ray with $b < b_c$ has no turning point and, traced backward, reaches $r = 2M$ ([[§C6.3 Geodesics꞉ Conserved Quantities and the Effective Potential#^thm-c6-3-5|Theorem §C6.3.5]], part 4). Forward in time it would have to have left the horizon, which no light does ([[§C7.2 Eddington–Finkelstein Coordinates and the Horizon#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]]). So no light from behind or around the hole arrives from directions with $b < b_c$: a dark disc.
>
> **Step 2** (its angle). The disc has angular radius $b_c/D$, so its angular diameter is
>
> $$
> \theta_{\text{sh}} = \frac{2\cdot3\sqrt3\,M}{D} = \frac{10.39\,M}{D} \qquad \Bigl(\text{restored: } \frac{10.39\,GM}{c^2D}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> **Step 3** (numbers). With $1\ \mathrm{pc} = 3.0857\times10^{16}$ m, $D = 16$ Mpc $= 4.937\times10^{23}$ m, and $1\ \mathrm{rad} = 2.063\times10^{11}\ \mu\mathrm{as}$ (microarcseconds):
>
> | mass | $GM/c^2$ | $\theta_{\text{sh}}$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | $2\times10^9M_\odot$ (Schutz) | $2.953\times10^{12}$ m | $6.22\times10^{-11}$ rad $= 12.8\ \mu$as |
> | $6.5\times10^9M_\odot$ (EHT collaboration) | $9.598\times10^{12}$ m | $2.02\times10^{-10}$ rad $= 41.7\ \mu$as |
>
> For example $10.39\times9.598\times10^{12}/4.937\times10^{23} = 2.02\times10^{-10}$. With the collaboration's mass the dark centre is about $40\ \mu$as across; with Schutz's, a third of that ([[#^cau-c7-4-3|Caution: The mass of the M87 hole in Schutz]]).
>
> **Step 4** (lensing enlarges the hole). Without bending, the horizon would subtend $4M/D$ and the photon sphere $6M/D$: $16.0$ and $24.1\ \mu$as for $6.5\times10^9M_\odot$. The shadow is $3\sqrt3/2 = 2.60$ times the horizon's naive size, because rays passing up to $b_c$ from the centre are bent into the hole. Schutz's remark that the inner edge of the ring lies inside $6M$ and subtends less than $10^{-10}$ rad holds for his mass: $6M/D = 3.6\times10^{-11}$ rad.
>
> **Step 5** (what it shows). The size of the dark centre is set by the photon sphere, $3\sqrt3\,M$, not by the horizon, and it scales as $M/D$: with the distance known, measuring it weighs the hole. The measured diameter of the ring is not in the typed sources. Couzens notes that the light from a disk around a non-rotating hole is focused near the photon sphere and that the images do not yet resolve that fine structure.
>
> *Source: Couzens, GR1, §6.2, p. 122 (PDF 123) (photon sphere and the Event Horizon Telescope) · Schutz p. 314, Fig. 11.9 (PDF 332–333) ($2\times10^9M_\odot$, about $16$ Mpc, inner edge inside $6M$) · Hartle §9.4, pp. 205–207 (PDF 206–208) (capture for $b < b_c$) · mass $6.5\times10^9M_\odot$: Akiyama et al. 2019 (abstract), cited by Schutz · numbers checked by script*
