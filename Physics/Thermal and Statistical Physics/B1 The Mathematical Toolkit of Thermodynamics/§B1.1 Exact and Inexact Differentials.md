*Sources: Blundell & Blundell, Concepts in Thermal Physics, ch. 11, App. C.7 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes · PHY 306 (Ringer, Spring 2025).*

Thermodynamics constantly asks whether a quantity belongs to a state or only to a process. Level A answered case by case: work depends on the path because $p\,dV$ fails the two-variable test for a conservative field ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^rem-a3-2-1|Remark: Work is a line integral]]), the internal energy is a state function by the first law ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^rem-a3-3-2|Remark: Exact and inexact differentials]]), and dividing heat by $T$ produces the state function $S$ ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]). This section turns those observations into a small theory: when a differential expression is the differential of a state function, how to test it, how to find the function, and what an integrating factor is. The last of these is the precise form of the statement "entropy exists", proved for every system in [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy|§B2.2]].

## Exact and inexact differentials

> [!definition] Definition §B1.1.1: Differential Form; Exact and Inexact Differentials
> Let the equilibrium states of a system be labelled by state variables $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ ranging over an open connected region $D \subset \mathbb R^n$ ([[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^def-a3-1-2|Def. §A3.1.2]]). A **differential form** (1-form) on $D$ is an expression
>
> $$
> \omega = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i(x)\,dx_i
> $$
>
> with continuous coefficients $X_i$. It assigns to every small step $dx$ the number $\omega$, for instance the heat absorbed or the work done in that step. The form is **exact** on $D$ if $\omega = df$ for some $C^1$ function $f$ on $D$, that is, $X_i = \partial f/\partial x_i$ for all $i$; then $f$ is a state function ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]]), determined up to an additive constant. Otherwise $\omega$ is **inexact**. Inexact differentials are written with a barred d, $đQ$, $đW$: the symbol names a small amount, not the change of anything.
>
> *Source: Blundell §11.1.2, App. C.7 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §2.4, §3.3*

^def-b1-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.1: Exact Differentials and Path Independence
> For a differential form $\omega$ with continuous coefficients on an open connected region $D$, the following are equivalent:
> 1. $\omega$ is exact on $D$;
> 2. the line integral $\int_{\mathcal C}\omega$ along a piecewise-$C^1$ path $\mathcal C$ in $D$ depends only on the endpoints of $\mathcal C$;
> 3. $\oint_{\mathcal C}\omega = 0$ around every closed piecewise-$C^1$ path in $D$.
>
> If $\omega = df$, then $\int_{\mathcal C} df = f(B) - f(A)$ for every path from $A$ to $B$.
>
> *Source: Blundell eqs. (11.1), (C.46)–(C.47) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §2.3–2.5*

^thm-b1-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Line integrals.* A path $\mathcal C$ is a map $t \mapsto x(t)$, $t \in [a, b]$, and $\int_{\mathcal C}\omega = \int_a^b \sum_i X_i(x(t))\,\dot x_i(t)\,dt$, the work line integral of the "field" $(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ ([[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^def-16-2|452 Def. §16.2]]).
>
> *$1 \Rightarrow 2$.* If $X_i = \partial f/\partial x_i$, the chain rule ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]) gives $\sum_i X_i\,\dot x_i = \frac{d}{dt}f(x(t))$, so by the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]) the integral is $f(x(b)) - f(x(a)) = f(B) - f(A)$; for a piecewise-$C^1$ path add the pieces.
>
> *$2 \Leftrightarrow 3$.* Reversing a path changes the sign of the integral. A closed path through $A$ and $B$ is a path $\mathcal C_1$ from $A$ to $B$ followed by a path $\mathcal C_2$ back, and $\oint = \int_{\mathcal C_1} - \int_{\mathcal C_2^{\text{rev}}}$; this vanishes for all such pairs exactly when integrals between the same endpoints agree.
>
> *$2 \Rightarrow 1$.* Fix $x_0 \in D$ and set $f(x) = \int_{x_0}^{x}\omega$ along any path in $D$ (one exists: an open connected set is path-connected, and by 2 the value does not depend on the choice). For small $h$ the straight segment from $x$ to $x + h e_i$ lies in $D$, so
>
> $$
> f(x + he_i) - f(x) = \int_0^h X_i(x + te_i)\,dt, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac1h\int_0^h X_i(x + te_i)\,dt = X_i(x)
> $$
>
> by continuity of $X_i$. So $df = \omega$, and $f$ is $C^1$ because its partials are continuous. This is the construction of a potential energy from a conservative force ([[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-3|CM Theorem §B1.2.3]]), with the state variables in place of the coordinates.

^der-b1-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]], [[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^def-16-2|452 Def. §16.2]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.2: Test for Exactness
> Let $\omega = \sum_i X_i\,dx_i$ have $C^1$ coefficients on an open region $D$.
> 1. If $\omega$ is exact, then for all $i, j$
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial X_i}{\partial x_j} = \frac{\partial X_j}{\partial x_i} .
> $$
>
> In two variables, $\omega = X\,dx + Y\,dy$, this is the single condition $\partial X/\partial y = \partial Y/\partial x$.
> 2. Conversely, if these conditions hold and $D$ is star-shaped (for example convex: a rectangle $T_1 < T < T_2$, $V_1 < V < V_2$ of states, or the whole quadrant $T > 0$, $V > 0$), then $\omega$ is exact on $D$.
>
> *Source: Blundell App. C.7, eq. (C.48) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §2.4 (test for exactness), §3.6 · PHY 306, Problem Set 1, Problem 3; Midterm, Problem 2*

^thm-b1-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Part 1.* If $X_i = \partial f/\partial x_i$ with $X_i \in C^1$, then $f \in C^2$, and $\partial X_i/\partial x_j = \partial^2 f/\partial x_j\partial x_i = \partial^2 f/\partial x_i\partial x_j = \partial X_j/\partial x_i$ by the equality of mixed partials ([[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]). In the language of forms, the conditions say that $\omega$ is closed, $d\omega = 0$ ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^def-22-8|452 Def. §22.8]]), and part 1 is "exact implies closed" ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-10|452 Prop. §22.10]]).
>
> *Part 2, constructively, for a rectangle in two variables.* Pick a base point $(x_0, y_0)$ in the rectangle and integrate first along $y = y_0$, then along the vertical line through $x$:
>
> $$
> f(x, y) = \int_{x_0}^{x} X(t, y_0)\,dt + \int_{y_0}^{y} Y(x, t)\,dt .
> $$
>
> Then $\partial f/\partial y = Y(x, y)$ directly, and, differentiating under the integral sign (allowed since $\partial Y/\partial x$ is continuous on the closed rectangle) and using the hypothesis,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = X(x, y_0) + \int_{y_0}^{y}\frac{\partial Y}{\partial x}(x, t)\,dt = X(x, y_0) + \int_{y_0}^{y}\frac{\partial X}{\partial t}(x, t)\,dt = X(x, y) .
> $$
>
> So $df = \omega$. The same formula is the practical recipe for finding the state function: integrate $X$ in $x$, then fix the remaining function of $y$ from $Y$ (see [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^ex-b1-1-1|Example §B1.1.1]]).
>
> *Part 2 in general.* A closed form on a star-shaped region is exact: this is the Poincaré lemma ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-12|452 Prop. §22.12]]). The force version, "curl-free implies conservative", is [[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-3|CM Theorem §B1.2.3]].

^der-b1-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]], [[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]], [[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^def-22-8|452 Def. §22.8]], [[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-10|452 Prop. §22.10]], [[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-12|452 Prop. §22.12]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: The test needs a region without holes
> On a region with a hole the cross-derivative conditions are necessary but not sufficient. The standard example is the angle form $(-y\,dx + x\,dy)/(x^2 + y^2)$ on the plane without the origin: it passes the test, yet its integral around the unit circle is $2\pi$, so by [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]] it is not exact ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-11|452 Prop. §22.11]]; [[Angle form on the punctured plane]]). By contrast $(x\,dx + y\,dy)/(x^2 + y^2) = d\bigl(\tfrac12\ln(x^2 + y^2)\bigr)$ is exact on the same punctured plane, and its integral from $(-1, 0)$ to $(1, 0)$ is $0$ along every path (the user's notes, §2.3–2.4). Equilibrium states of a simple system usually fill a convex region such as $T > 0$, $V > 0$, so the test is safe there; it has to be applied with care on state spaces that wrap around or exclude a region.

^cau-b1-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Heat in the language of forms
> For a quasi-static step of a closed fluid the first law ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]) gives $đQ = dU + p\,dV$. In the coordinates $(T, V)$, with $U = U(T, V)$,
>
> $$
> đQ = \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V dT + \Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T + p\Bigr] dV .
> $$
>
> The cross-derivative test fails as soon as $(\partial p/\partial T)_V \neq 0$: the mixed partials of $U$ cancel, and what is left is $\partial p/\partial T$ on one side and $0$ on the other. So heat is inexact for every substance whose pressure depends on temperature at fixed volume, and "the heat of a state" is meaningless. Level A showed the same for work in the $(V, p)$ plane ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^rem-a3-2-1|§A3.2]]). The first law says that the difference $đQ - đW$ of two inexact forms is exact; the second law will say that $đQ_{\text{rev}}/T$ is exact as well.

^rem-b1-1-1

## Integrating factors

> [!definition] Definition §B1.1.2: Integrating Factor
> A function $\lambda(x)$, nonzero on $D$, is an **integrating factor** of the form $\omega$ if $\lambda\,\omega$ is exact: $\lambda\,\omega = df$ for some state function $f$. Along any path on which $\omega = 0$ (for heat: along a reversible adiabat), $f$ is then constant, so the level sets of $f$ are the curves (surfaces) $\omega = 0$.
>
> *Source: the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §3.6 (heading) · PHY 306, Problem Set 1, Problem 3*

^def-b1-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.3: Integrating Factors Are Not Unique
> If $\lambda$ is an integrating factor of $\omega$ with $\lambda\omega = df$, then for every $C^1$ function $\varphi$ of one variable with $\varphi' \neq 0$, the function $\mu = \varphi'(f)\,\lambda$ is also an integrating factor, with $\mu\,\omega = d\bigl(\varphi(f)\bigr)$.
>
> *Source: the chain rule; the sources use integrating factors without stating this.*

^thm-b1-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the chain rule, $d\bigl(\varphi(f)\bigr) = \varphi'(f)\,df = \varphi'(f)\,\lambda\,\omega = \mu\,\omega$, and $\mu \neq 0$ because $\varphi' \neq 0$ and $\lambda \neq 0$.

^der-b1-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: What is special about entropy
> The formal statement of the existence of entropy is: *$1/T$ is an integrating factor of the reversible heat*,
>
> $$
> dS = \frac{đQ_{\text{rev}}}{T} ,
> $$
>
> for every system, with $T$ the absolute temperature. Level A made this plausible for a gas with two state variables, by cutting a reversible cycle into Carnot strips ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]], [[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]); [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]] proves it for any system from the Kelvin–Planck principle.
>
> Why is this a physical statement and not a mathematical triviality? For a fluid with two state variables, an integrating factor of $đQ_{\text{rev}}$ always exists near any state where $đQ_{\text{rev}} \neq 0$: the curves $đQ_{\text{rev}} = 0$ are the solutions of a first-order differential equation, and a function constant along them provides one (standard ODE theory, not in the sources). By [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]] there are then infinitely many ([[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^ex-b1-1-2|Example §B1.1.2]] shows two for the ideal gas). The content of the second law is twofold.
> - **Universality.** One integrating factor can be chosen as a function of the temperature alone, $1/T$, the same function for every substance.
> - **Existence in more variables.** For a system with three or more state variables (two fluids in thermal contact, a fluid with a variable amount of matter) an integrating factor need not exist at all. For example, $\omega = y\,dx + dz$ in $(x, y, z)$ has none: if $\lambda\omega = df$, then $\partial f/\partial y = 0$, so $f$ and $\lambda = \partial f/\partial z$ do not depend on $y$, and then $\partial f/\partial x = \lambda y$ for all $y$ forces $\lambda = 0$. The second law asserts that $đQ_{\text{rev}}$ is never like this.

^rem-b1-1-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.1.1: An Inexact Differential and Its Integrating Factor
> Consider $df = (x^2 - y)\,dx + x\,dy$ on the region $x > 0$ (the PHY 306 notation; despite the "$d$", it is a form, not yet known to be a differential).
>
> *Test.* $\partial(x^2 - y)/\partial y = -1$ but $\partial x/\partial x = 1$: the form is inexact ([[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]).
>
> *Integrals from $(1, 1)$ to $(2, 2)$.* On a vertical segment $dx = 0$, on a horizontal one $dy = 0$:
> - up, then right: $\int_1^2 1\,dy + \int_1^2 (x^2 - 2)\,dx = 1 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac43$;
> - right, then up: $\int_1^2 (x^2 - 1)\,dx + \int_1^2 2\,dy = \tfrac43 + 2 = \tfrac{10}3$;
> - straight, $y = x$: $\int_1^2 (x^2 - x + x)\,dx = \tfrac73$;
> - parabola $y = (x - 1)^2 + 1$, $dy = 2(x - 1)\,dx$: $\int_1^2 \bigl[x^2 - (x^2 - 2x + 2) + 2x(x - 1)\bigr]dx = \int_1^2 (2x^2 - 2)\,dx = \tfrac83$.
>
> Four paths, four values. The difference of the first two is a closed loop, counterclockwise around the unit square $[1, 2]^2$: $\tfrac{10}3 - \tfrac43 = 2 = \iint (\partial_x x - \partial_y(x^2 - y))\,dA = \iint 2\,dA$, as Green's theorem says ([[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^thm-16-1|452 Thm. §16.1]]): the failure of the test, integrated over the enclosed area.
>
> *Integrating factor.* Divide by $x^2$: $dg = (1 - y/x^2)\,dx + (1/x)\,dy$. Now $\partial(1 - y/x^2)/\partial y = -1/x^2 = \partial(1/x)/\partial x$, and $x > 0$ is convex, so $dg$ is exact. The recipe of [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^der-b1-1-2|the derivation of Theorem §B1.1.2]]: $\partial g/\partial y = 1/x$ gives $g = y/x + h(x)$, and $\partial g/\partial x = -y/x^2 + h'(x) = 1 - y/x^2$ gives $h = x$. So $g = x + y/x$, $1/x^2$ is an integrating factor, and $\int dg = g(2, 2) - g(1, 1) = 3 - 2 = 1$ along every path.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 1, Problem 3 (and its solution)*

> [!example] Example §B1.1.2: Two Integrating Factors for the Heat of an Ideal Gas
> For $n$ moles of an ideal gas with constant molar $C_V$, a quasi-static step has $dU = nC_V\,dT$ ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]) and $p = nRT/V$ ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]), so in the coordinates $(T, V)$
>
> $$
> đQ = nC_V\,dT + \frac{nRT}{V}\,dV .
> $$
>
> *Inexact:* $\partial(nC_V)/\partial V = 0$ but $\partial(nRT/V)/\partial T = nR/V$.
>
> *Factor $1/T$:* $đQ/T = nC_V\,dT/T + nR\,dV/V$ passes the test (each coefficient depends only on its own variable) on the convex quadrant $T, V > 0$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{đQ}{T} = d\bigl(nC_V\ln T + nR\ln V\bigr) = dS ,
> $$
>
> the entropy of [[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-4|Theorem §A4.5.4]], up to a constant.
>
> *Factor $V^{\gamma - 1}$,* with $\gamma = C_p/C_V = 1 + R/C_V$ ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]): since $R/C_V = \gamma - 1$,
>
> $$
> V^{\gamma - 1}\,đQ = nC_V\Bigl[V^{\gamma - 1}\,dT + (\gamma - 1)\,T\,V^{\gamma - 2}\,dV\Bigr] = nC_V\,d\bigl(TV^{\gamma - 1}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Its level curves $TV^{\gamma - 1} = \text{const}$ are the reversible adiabats ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-2|Theorem §A3.6.2]]), as [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]] requires. The two factors are related as in [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]: $TV^{\gamma - 1} = \text{const}\cdot e^{S/nC_V}$, so $nC_V\,d(TV^{\gamma-1}) = \varphi'(S)\,dS$ with $\varphi(S) \propto e^{S/nC_V}$. Only $1/T$ is a function of the temperature alone; $V^{\gamma - 1}$ works for this gas only.
>
> *Source: the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §5.2, §6.2 · Blundell §14.1*

^ex-b1-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Exact forms, closed forms and the Poincaré lemma: [[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^def-22-8|452 Def. §22.8]], [[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^prop-22-12|452 Prop. §22.12]]; the cross-derivative test is the equality of mixed partials, [[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]; line integrals and Green's theorem, [[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^def-16-2|452 Def. §16.2]], [[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^thm-16-1|452 Thm. §16.1]].
> - The same mathematics in mechanics: a force is conservative exactly when $F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy + F_z\,dz$ is exact, with potential energy as the state function ([[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-3|CM Theorem §B1.2.3]]).
> - The exactness of $dS$ is what makes the Maxwell relations true: each one is [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]] applied to the differential of a potential ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions]]).
