An equilibrium state of a fixed amount of fluid is fixed by any two of $p$, $V$, $T$, because the equation of state ties the three together ([[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^rem-a3-1-3|Remark: Two variables fix the state]]). Every other state function can therefore be written as a function of several different pairs, and a "partial derivative" means nothing until one says which variable is held fixed. This section collects the identities that relate derivatives taken with different variables fixed, the reciprocal and triple-product rules above all, and the Legendre transform, the operation that trades a variable for the slope conjugate to it. Together with [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials|§B1.1]] they are the whole of the mathematics behind the thermodynamic potentials and Maxwell relations of Chapter B3.

## Derivatives with a variable held fixed

> [!definition] Definition §B1.2.1: Partial Derivative at Fixed $y$
> Let $w$, $x$, $y$ be state functions of a system with two independent variables, such that $(x, y)$ can serve as coordinates of the states near some state. Then $w$ is a function $w = \hat w(x, y)$, and
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\Bigr)_y = \frac{\partial \hat w}{\partial x}(x, y)
> $$
>
> is the rate of change of $w$ along the curve of states on which $y$ is constant. The subscript names the variable held fixed. Its total differential is $dw = (\partial w/\partial x)_y\,dx + (\partial w/\partial y)_x\,dy$ ([[§10 The Differential#^def-10-1|452 Def. §10.1]]).
>
> *Source: Blundell App. C.6, eq. (C.39) · Schroeder §1.6 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §1.2.1*

^def-b1-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Same letter, different functions
> $(\partial w/\partial x)_y$ and $(\partial w/\partial x)_z$ are derivatives of two *different* functions, $w$ written in terms of $(x, y)$ and in terms of $(x, z)$, and are generally different numbers. Blundell's puzzle: with $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, one finds $\partial x/\partial r = \cos\theta = x/r$ from the first formula and $\partial x/\partial r = r/x$ from $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$, so apparently $\partial x/\partial r = \partial r/\partial x$. There is no contradiction: the first derivative holds $\theta$ fixed, the second holds $y$ fixed, and the correct statements are $(\partial x/\partial r)_\theta = x/r$ and $(\partial x/\partial r)_y = r/x = 1/(\partial r/\partial x)_y$. In thermodynamics the danger is constant: $(\partial U/\partial T)_V$ is the heat capacity $C_V$, while $(\partial U/\partial T)_p$ is not ([[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]). [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^ex-b1-2-2|Example §B1.2.2]] works one function through six different derivatives.
>
> *Source: Blundell, Exercise 11.4 · Schroeder, Problem 1.46 · PHY 306, Problem Set 3, Problem 2*

^cau-b1-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.1: Reciprocal and Reciprocity Theorems
> Let $x$, $y$, $z$ be related by one equation $f(x, y, z) = 0$, with $f$ of class $C^1$ and all three partials $f_x, f_y, f_z$ nonzero at the point considered, so that near it each variable is a function of the other two. Then
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\Bigr)_z = -\frac{f_y}{f_x}, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\Bigr)_z = \frac{1}{(\partial y/\partial x)_z}, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\Bigr)_z\Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial z}\Bigr)_x\Bigl(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\Bigr)_y = -1 .
> $$
>
> The second identity is the **reciprocal theorem**, the third the **reciprocity theorem** (cyclic or triple-product rule). Combined, $(\partial x/\partial y)_z = -(\partial x/\partial z)_y(\partial z/\partial y)_x$.
>
> *Source: Blundell App. C.6, eqs. (C.41)–(C.43) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §1.2.1, §4.3 · PHY 306, Problem Set 1, Problem 1*

^thm-b1-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the implicit function theorem ([[§15 The Implicit Function Theorem#^thm-15-2|452 Thm. §15.2]]), $f_x \neq 0$ lets us solve $f = 0$ locally for $x = x(y, z)$, of class $C^1$. Differentiating $f(x(y, z), y, z) = 0$ with respect to $y$ at fixed $z$ by the chain rule ([[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]) gives $f_x\,(\partial x/\partial y)_z + f_y = 0$, which is the first formula ([[§15 The Implicit Function Theorem#^rem-15-2|452, Remark: The Derivative Formula]]). Applying it to each factor,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\Bigr)_z = -\frac{f_y}{f_x}, \quad \Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\Bigr)_z = -\frac{f_x}{f_y}, \quad \Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial z}\Bigr)_x = -\frac{f_z}{f_y}, \quad \Bigl(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\Bigr)_y = -\frac{f_x}{f_z} .
> $$
>
> The first two are reciprocals. The product of the three in the cyclic rule is $(-1)^3\,\dfrac{f_y}{f_x}\dfrac{f_z}{f_y}\dfrac{f_x}{f_z} = -1$.
>
> Blundell's route avoids $f$: write $dx = (\partial x/\partial y)_z\,dy + (\partial x/\partial z)_y\,dz$ and $dz = (\partial z/\partial x)_y\,dx + (\partial z/\partial y)_x\,dy$, substitute the second into the first, and compare coefficients of the independent $dx$ and $dy$. The $dx$ coefficient gives $1 = (\partial x/\partial z)_y(\partial z/\partial x)_y$, the $dy$ coefficient $0 = (\partial x/\partial y)_z + (\partial x/\partial z)_y(\partial z/\partial y)_x$.

^der-b1-2-1

*Uses:* [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]], [[§15 The Implicit Function Theorem#^thm-15-2|452 Thm. §15.2]], [[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: The product is $-1$, not $+1$
> Cancelling the "$\partial x$", "$\partial y$", "$\partial z$" as if they were numbers suggests $+1$. They are not numbers, and each factor holds a different variable fixed. For the ideal gas the three factors are $(\partial p/\partial V)_T = -p/V$, $(\partial V/\partial T)_p = V/T$ and $(\partial T/\partial p)_V = T/p$: their magnitudes multiply to $1$, and the sign comes from the one constraint, fixed $T$, along which the other two variables move in opposite directions.

^cau-b1-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.2: Changing the Variable Held Fixed
> Let $w$, $x$, $y$, $z$ be state functions of a system with two independent variables, with $(x, y)$ and $(x, z)$ both admissible coordinates. Then
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\Bigr)_z = \Bigl(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\Bigr)_y + \Bigl(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\Bigr)_x\Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\Bigr)_z ,
> $$
>
> and, for any further state function $u$ with $(u, z)$ admissible, the chain rule at fixed $z$:
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\Bigr)_z = \Bigl(\frac{\partial w}{\partial u}\Bigr)_z\Bigl(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\Bigr)_z .
> $$
>
> *Source: Blundell §11.3, eq. (11.17) (an instance) · Schroeder §1.6, eq. (1.48) and Problem 1.46 · PHY 306, Problem Set 3, Problem 2*

^thm-b1-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Along the curve $z = \text{const}$, parametrised by $x$, the state is $(x, y(x, z))$. The function $w = \hat w(x, y)$ restricted to it is $x \mapsto \hat w(x, y(x, z))$, and the chain rule ([[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]) differentiates it into the first formula. Equivalently, divide $dw = (\partial w/\partial x)_y\,dx + (\partial w/\partial y)_x\,dy$ by $dx$ along a step with $dz = 0$. The second formula is the one-variable chain rule along the same curve, on which $w$, $u$ and $x$ all vary with a single parameter.

*Uses:* [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]], [[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why heat capacities of liquids are measured at constant pressure
> The reciprocity theorem expresses a derivative that is hard to measure through two that are easy. For a substance with equation of state $f(p, V, T) = 0$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p = -\frac{(\partial V/\partial T)_p}{(\partial V/\partial p)_T} = \frac{\alpha}{\kappa_T},
> $$
>
> with the volume expansivity $\alpha = V^{-1}(\partial V/\partial T)_p$ (level A's $\beta$, [[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^law-a1-2-2|Law §A1.2.2]]) and the isothermal compressibility $\kappa_T = -V^{-1}(\partial V/\partial p)_T$, both response functions of [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^def-b3-2-1|Def. §B3.2.1]]. For water at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, $\alpha = 2.57 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm K^{-1}$ and $\kappa_T = 4.52 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm{Pa^{-1}}$, so $(\partial p/\partial T)_V = 5.7 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{Pa/K}$: keeping the volume of water fixed while warming it from $20$ to $30\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ takes about $5.7 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{Pa} \approx 56\ \mathrm{atm}$. For mercury ($\alpha = 1.81 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm K^{-1}$, $\kappa_T = 4.04 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{Pa^{-1}}$) it is about $440\ \mathrm{atm}$. No ordinary container holds that, which is why measured heat capacities of solids and liquids are $C_p$, and $C_V$ is computed from them ([[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]], [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder, Problem 1.47*

## The Legendre transform

> [!definition] Definition §B1.2.2: Legendre Transform (Thermodynamic Convention)
> Let $f(x, y)$ be of class $C^2$ with $\partial^2 f/\partial x^2 \neq 0$ on the region considered (so that $f$ is strictly convex or strictly concave in $x$), and let
>
> $$
> u = \Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Bigr)_y
> $$
>
> be the slope conjugate to $x$. Then $u$ determines $x$ at fixed $y$, $x = x(u, y)$, and the **Legendre transform of $f$ with respect to $x$** is
>
> $$
> g(u, y) = f - u\,x ,
> $$
>
> expressed as a function of $(u, y)$. The variable $y$ is passive. With several active variables, $g = f - \sum_i u_i x_i$ with $u_i = \partial f/\partial x_i$ and a nonsingular Hessian. Classical mechanics uses the opposite overall sign, $G = sx - F$ ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-1|CM Def. §B7.1.1]]); thermodynamics uses $f - ux$ so that the potentials keep the sign of an energy. The prototype is $F = U - TS$: the transform of $U(S, V)$ with respect to $S$, whose slope is $T = (\partial U/\partial S)_V$ ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]; the potentials themselves are [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables|§B3.1]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder §5.1 (the term) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8 (F = E − TS as a Legendre transform) · Blundell §16.2–16.4 (the transforms U → H, F, G)*

^def-b1-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.3: Properties of the Legendre Transform
> With $f$, $u$, $g$ as in [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|Def. §B1.2.2]]:
> 1. **Differential.** $dg = -x\,du + (\partial f/\partial y)_x\,dy$; hence
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}\Bigr)_y = -x, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}\Bigr)_u = \Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\Bigr)_x .
> $$
>
> 2. **Inversion.** Transforming $g$ with respect to $u$ (slope $-x$) gives back $f$: $f = g - u\,(\partial g/\partial u)_y = g + ux$.
> 3. **Curvature.** At corresponding points, $\bigl(\partial^2 g/\partial u^2\bigr)_y = -1\big/\bigl(\partial^2 f/\partial x^2\bigr)_y$; a function convex in $x$ has a transform concave in $u$, and vice versa.
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.2–16.4 (each case: dH, dF, dG) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §7*

^thm-b1-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* From $df = u\,dx + (\partial f/\partial y)_x\,dy$ and $g = f - ux$,
>
> $$
> dg = df - u\,dx - x\,du = -x\,du + \Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\Bigr)_x dy .
> $$
>
> The $dx$ terms cancel; that cancellation is the purpose of subtracting $ux$. What remains is the differential of a function of $(u, y)$ whose coefficients are its partial derivatives, read off as in [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]].
>
> *2.* By part 1 the slope of $g$ in $u$ is $-x$, so its transform is $g - u(-x) = f$.
>
> *3.* Differentiate $(\partial g/\partial u)_y = -x(u, y)$ once more and use the reciprocal theorem ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]]): $(\partial^2 g/\partial u^2)_y = -(\partial x/\partial u)_y = -1/(\partial u/\partial x)_y = -1/(\partial^2 f/\partial x^2)_y$. This is also why the hypothesis $\partial^2 f/\partial x^2 \neq 0$ is needed: it makes $x \mapsto u$ invertible ([[§16 The Inverse Function Theorem#^thm-16-2|452 Thm. §16.2]]).

^der-b1-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|Def. §B1.2.2]], [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]], [[§16 The Inverse Function Theorem#^thm-16-2|452 Thm. §16.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The geometry, and why not simply substitute
> Fix $y$ and draw the graph of $f$ against $x$. The tangent line at $x$ has slope $u$ and meets the vertical axis $x = 0$ at height $f - ux = g(u)$: the Legendre transform records, for each slope, the intercept of the tangent line with that slope. A strictly convex curve is the envelope of its tangent lines, so slope and intercept together determine it, and part 2 of [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]] reconstructs $f$. Substituting $x = x(u)$ into $f$ instead would lose information: $f$ and $f(x - a)$ have the same set of slopes and values, so $f$ "as a function of its slope" cannot tell them apart ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^rem-b7-1-1|CM Remark: Why not simply substitute?]]). The intercept depends on where $x = 0$ is: shifting the zero of $S$ by $S_0$ changes $F = U - TS$ by $-TS_0$. Free energies are therefore fixed absolutely only once the entropy is, which the third law does ([[§B2.3 The Third Law#^rem-b2-3-1|Remark: The third law, stated precisely]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.2.1: The Triple Product for a Non-Ideal Equation of State
> A gas obeys $pV = NkT\,e^{-\varepsilon}$ with $\varepsilon = a/(NTV)$, $a$ a constant. Check the reciprocity theorem.
>
> Write $f(p, V, T) = pV - NkT\,e^{-\varepsilon} = 0$ and use $NkT\,e^{-\varepsilon} = pV$ after differentiating; since $\partial\varepsilon/\partial V = -\varepsilon/V$ and $\partial\varepsilon/\partial T = -\varepsilon/T$,
>
> $$
> f_p = V, \qquad f_V = p - NkT e^{-\varepsilon}\frac{\varepsilon}{V} = p(1 - \varepsilon), \qquad f_T = -Nk e^{-\varepsilon} - NkT e^{-\varepsilon}\frac{\varepsilon}{T} = -\frac{pV}{T}(1 + \varepsilon) .
> $$
>
> By the first formula of [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]],
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T = -\frac pV(1 - \varepsilon), \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p = \frac VT\,\frac{1 + \varepsilon}{1 - \varepsilon}, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\partial T}{\partial p}\Bigr)_V = \frac Tp\,\frac{1}{1 + \varepsilon},
> $$
>
> and the product is $-\frac pV(1 - \varepsilon)\cdot\frac VT\frac{1 + \varepsilon}{1 - \varepsilon}\cdot\frac Tp\frac{1}{1 + \varepsilon} = -1$. For $a = 0$ the three derivatives reduce to the ideal-gas values $-p/V$, $V/T$, $T/p$. At states with $\varepsilon = 1$ the isotherm is flat, $f_V = 0$, $(\partial V/\partial T)_p$ is infinite and the theorem does not apply there, as its hypothesis $f_V \neq 0$ warns.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 1, Problem 1 (and its solution)*

^ex-b1-2-1

> [!example] Example §B1.2.2: One Function, Six Derivatives
> A height is $z = x^2 + 2y^2$ in Cartesian coordinates, with $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$. Written in other pairs of variables,
>
> $$
> z = 2r^2 - x^2 = x^2(1 + 2\tan^2\theta) = r^2 + y^2 = y^2(2 + \cot^2\theta) .
> $$
>
> Differentiating the appropriate form each time:
>
> | derivative | $(\partial z/\partial x)_y$ | $(\partial z/\partial x)_r$ | $(\partial z/\partial x)_\theta$ | $(\partial z/\partial y)_x$ | $(\partial z/\partial y)_r$ | $(\partial z/\partial y)_\theta$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | value | $2x$ | $-2x$ | $2x(1 + 2\tan^2\theta)$ | $4y$ | $2y$ | $2y(2 + \cot^2\theta)$ |
>
> [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]] produces the same numbers from the Cartesian ones without rewriting $z$. At fixed $r$, $y = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}$ and $(\partial y/\partial x)_r = -x/y$; at fixed $\theta$, $y = x\tan\theta$ and $(\partial y/\partial x)_\theta = \tan\theta$. Hence
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\Bigr)_r = 2x + 4y\Bigl(-\frac xy\Bigr) = -2x, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\Bigr)_\theta = 2x + 4y\tan\theta = 2x(1 + 2\tan^2\theta) .
> $$
>
> Moving along $x$ with $y$ fixed, with $r$ fixed and with $\theta$ fixed are three different directions in the plane, and the height changes at three different rates. A mixed derivative also needs its fixed variables named: from $z = x^2(1 + 2\tan^2\theta)$, $\partial^2 z/\partial x\,\partial\theta = 8x\tan\theta\sec^2\theta$.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 3, Problem 2 (and its solution)*

^ex-b1-2-2

> [!example] Example §B1.2.3: From $U(S)$ to $F(T)$ for an Ideal Gas
> Hold the volume of $n$ moles of ideal gas with constant molar $C_V$ fixed; $U$ is the internal energy (level A's $E_{\text{int}}$). By [[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-4|Theorem §A4.5.4]] with $U = nC_VT$, $S = S_0 + nC_V\ln(U/U_0)$, so
>
> $$
> U(S) = U_0\,e^{(S - S_0)/nC_V}, \qquad T = \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\Bigr)_V = \frac{U}{nC_V}, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial S^2}\Bigr)_V = \frac{U}{(nC_V)^2} > 0 .
> $$
>
> $U$ is convex in $S$; the slope equation gives back $U = nC_VT$. With $T_0 = U_0/nC_V$, the transform is
>
> $$
> F(T) = U - TS = nC_VT - T\Bigl[S_0 + nC_V\ln\frac{T}{T_0}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Checks against [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]: $-(\partial F/\partial T)_V = -nC_V + S_0 + nC_V\ln(T/T_0) + nC_V = S$ (part 1); $(\partial^2 F/\partial T^2)_V = -nC_V/T$, which equals $-1/(\partial^2 U/\partial S^2)_V = -(nC_V)^2/U$ (part 3), so $F$ is concave in $T$. The constant $S_0$ survives in $F$ as $-TS_0$, as the geometric remark predicts. The figure above plots this $U(S)$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.3 (F = U − TS and S = −(∂F/∂T)_V), applied to the level-A entropy of the ideal gas*
