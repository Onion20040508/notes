Level A obtained the Dulong–Petit value $3R$ for the molar heat capacity of a solid from equipartition ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-3|Theorem §A2.3.3]]) and noted that it fails at low temperature ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^cau-a2-3-2|Caution: Where Dulong–Petit fails]]); a level-A example even used the low-temperature $T^3$ law of rock salt as given ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^ex-a1-3-3|Example §A1.3.3]]). [[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^thm-b8-2-6|Theorem §B8.2.6]] justified $3R$ by the $3N$ normal modes of a harmonic crystal and showed that Einstein's single frequency makes the heat capacity vanish, but exponentially, not as $T^3$. This section treats the quantized normal modes as a gas of bosons with $\mu = 0$, the phonons, exactly as [[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics]] treated the modes of the electromagnetic field. Debye's model, sound waves with a cutoff, then gives the $T^3$ law with its coefficient, the Dulong–Petit limit, and one curve in between fixed by a single parameter, the Debye temperature.

## Phonons

> [!definition] Definition §B11.2.1: Phonon
> A crystal of $N$ atoms vibrating harmonically about their equilibrium positions has $3N$ normal modes of frequencies $\omega_r$ ([[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|WO Theorem §B2.1.5]], [[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^thm-b6-5-3|CM Theorem §B6.5.3]]). Quantized, each mode is an oscillator with levels $(m_r + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega_r$, $m_r = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|QM Theorem §A4.5.3]]). A **phonon** is one quantum $\hbar\omega_r$ of a mode: the state with excitation numbers $\{m_r\}$ is described as $m_r$ phonons in mode $r$. Phonons are bosons (any number can occupy a mode), and their number is not conserved. The density of vibrational states $g(\omega)$ ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^def-b10-1-2|Def. §B10.1.2]]) satisfies $\int_0^\infty g(\omega)\,d\omega = 3N$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, §2.1–2.2; Blundell ch. 24 (introduction), eq. (24.9); Schroeder §7.5*

^def-b11-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.2.1: Thermal Energy and Heat Capacity of a Harmonic Crystal
> In thermal equilibrium the mean number of phonons in a mode of frequency $\omega$ is $1/(e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1)$ (Bose–Einstein with $\mu = 0$), and
>
> $$
> U = E_0 + \int_0^\infty \frac{\hbar\omega\,g(\omega)\,d\omega}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1}, \qquad C_V = k\int_0^\infty g(\omega)\,\frac{x^2e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2}\,d\omega, \quad x = \frac{\hbar\omega}{kT},
> $$
>
> where $E_0$ (static energy plus zero-point energies $\sum\tfrac12\hbar\omega_r$) does not depend on $T$. For $kT$ above every $\hbar\omega_r$, $C_V \to 3Nk$ whatever the spectrum (Dulong–Petit). The Einstein solid is the special case $g(\omega) = 3N\delta(\omega - \omega_E)$ ([[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^thm-b8-2-6|Theorem §B8.2.6]]; Blundell eq. (24.10)).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, eqs. (11)–(12), (19)–(21); Blundell §24.2, eqs. (24.19)–(24.24); Schroeder §7.5, eqs. (7.105)–(7.106)*

^thm-b11-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The modes are independent, so the partition function is the product of the mode partition functions and $\ln Z$, $U$ and $C_V$ are sums over modes ([[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]]). Each mode is the oscillator of [[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^thm-b8-2-4|Theorem §B8.2.4]]: mean energy $\hbar\omega(\tfrac12 + \langle m\rangle)$ with $\langle m\rangle = 1/(e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1)$, and heat capacity $kx^2e^x/(e^x - 1)^2$. Read as particles, the phonons of a mode are bosons in one single-particle state whose number is free, so their chemical potential vanishes for the reasons of [[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-2|Theorem §B11.1.2]] (a phonon is created by any collision with the wall or with air molecules, "air $\to$ air $+$ phonon"; Schwartz), and $\langle m\rangle$ is the Bose–Einstein distribution ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]). Replacing the sum over the $3N$ modes by $\int g\,d\omega$ gives the formulas. For $x \ll 1$, $x^2e^x/(e^x - 1)^2 \to 1$, and $\int g = 3N$ gives $3Nk$.

## The Debye model

> [!model] Model §B11.2.2: Debye Model of a Solid
> The $3N$ vibrational modes of a crystal of $N$ atoms in volume $V$ are replaced by sound waves in an elastic continuum. Assumptions:
> - linear dispersion $\omega = c_sk$ with one speed of sound $c_s$ for all wavelengths, directions and the three polarizations (one longitudinal, two transverse), so that by [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-4|Theorem §B10.1.4]] with $g_s = 3$
>
>   $$
>   g(\omega) = \frac{3V\omega^2}{2\pi^2c_s^3} ;
>   $$
> - the modes stop at a cutoff frequency $\omega_D$ chosen so that the total number of modes is $3N$; in $n$-space, the cube of allowed mode numbers is replaced by an eighth of a sphere of the same volume (Schroeder).
>
> *Fails:* real dispersion relations bend over at short wavelengths, transverse and longitudinal waves have different speeds, and crystals with several atoms per cell have optic branches; see [[§B11.2★ Phonons and the Debye Model#^rem-b11-2-3|Remark: Real solids]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, §2.1–2.2; Schroeder §7.5; Blundell §24.2*

^mod-b11-2-2

> [!definition] Definition §B11.2.2: Debye Frequency; Debye Temperature
> The **Debye frequency** $\omega_D$ and the **Debye temperature** $T_D$ (Blundell's $\Theta_D$) of [[§B11.2★ Phonons and the Debye Model#^mod-b11-2-2|Model §B11.2.2]] are
>
> $$
> \omega_D = c_s\Bigl(\frac{6\pi^2N}{V}\Bigr)^{1/3}, \qquad T_D = \frac{\hbar\omega_D}{k} = \frac{\hbar c_s}{k}\Bigl(\frac{6\pi^2N}{V}\Bigr)^{1/3},
> $$
>
> fixed by $\int_0^{\omega_D}g(\omega)\,d\omega = 3N$; in terms of them $g(\omega) = 9N\omega^2/\omega_D^3$ for $\omega \le \omega_D$ and $0$ above. $T_D$ is the temperature at which the highest-frequency phonon has energy $kT$. Typical values (Blundell): Ne 63 K, Na 150 K, NaCl 321 K, Al 394 K, Si 625 K, diamond 1860 K: high for hard, light solids.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, eqs. (16)–(18), (23); Blundell §24.2, eqs. (24.15)–(24.18) and margin table; Schroeder §7.5, eqs. (7.107), (7.111)*

^def-b11-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.2.3: Heat Capacity of the Debye Solid
> In the Debye model, with $x_D = T_D/T$,
>
> $$
> U = E_0 + 9NkT\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_D}\Bigr)^3\int_0^{x_D}\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x - 1}, \qquad C_V = 9Nk\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_D}\Bigr)^3\int_0^{x_D}\frac{x^4e^x\,dx}{(e^x - 1)^2} .
> $$
>
> - *High temperature,* $T \gg T_D$: $U = E_0 + 3NkT$ and $C_V = 3Nk\bigl[1 - \tfrac{1}{20}(T_D/T)^2 + \cdots\bigr] \to 3Nk$, the law of Dulong and Petit.
> - *Low temperature,* $T \ll T_D$: $U = E_0 + \dfrac{3\pi^4}{5}\dfrac{NkT^4}{T_D^3}$ and
>
>   $$
>   C_V = \frac{12\pi^4}{5}\,Nk\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_D}\Bigr)^3 \qquad\text{(Debye } T^3 \text{ law)},
>   $$
>
>   that is $1944\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}\times(T/T_D)^3$ per mole of atoms.
>
> In between, $C_V$ depends on $T/T_D$ only; at $T = T_D$ it is $0.952 \times 3Nk$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.5, eqs. (7.112)–(7.117), Problem 7.62; Blundell §24.2, Examples 24.2–24.3, eqs. (24.22)–(24.28); Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, eqs. (20)–(25)*

^thm-b11-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert $g(\omega) = 9N\omega^2/\omega_D^3$ on $[0, \omega_D]$ into [[§B11.2★ Phonons and the Debye Model#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]] and put $x = \hbar\omega/kT$, so $\omega = kTx/\hbar$ and the upper limit is $x_D = \hbar\omega_D/kT = T_D/T$:
>
> $$
> U - E_0 = \frac{9N}{\omega_D^3}\int_0^{\omega_D}\frac{\hbar\omega^3\,d\omega}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1} = \frac{9N\hbar}{\omega_D^3}\Bigl(\frac{kT}{\hbar}\Bigr)^4\int_0^{x_D}\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x - 1} = 9NkT\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_D}\Bigr)^3\int_0^{x_D}\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x - 1} .
> $$
>
> The same substitution in the $C_V$ integral gives the second formula (or differentiate $U$ under the integral, Schroeder's route; the upper limit also depends on $T$, which is why $C_V$ is easier to compute from Theorem §B11.2.1 directly).
>
> *High $T$:* on $[0, x_D]$ with $x_D \ll 1$, $x^4e^x/(e^x - 1)^2 = x^2\bigl(1 - x^2/12 + O(x^4)\bigr)$ by Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), so the integral is $x_D^3/3 - x_D^5/60 + \cdots$ and $C_V = 9Nk\,x_D^{-3}(x_D^3/3 - x_D^5/60) = 3Nk(1 - x_D^2/20)$. In $U$, $x^3/(e^x - 1) \approx x^2$ gives $3NkT$ (Schwartz, Schroeder).
>
> *Low $T$:* $x_D \to \infty$; the integrands decay like $x^4e^{-x}$, so the upper limit can be taken to infinity with an exponentially small error: the modes near the cutoff are frozen anyway. By [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]], $\int_0^\infty x^3\,dx/(e^x - 1) = \pi^4/15$, giving $U - E_0 = \tfrac35\pi^4NkT^4/T_D^3$. For $C_V$, integrate by parts with $e^x/(e^x - 1)^2 = -\frac{d}{dx}(e^x - 1)^{-1}$:
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty\frac{x^4e^x\,dx}{(e^x - 1)^2} = \Bigl[-\frac{x^4}{e^x - 1}\Bigr]_0^\infty + 4\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x - 1} = \frac{4\pi^4}{15}
> $$
>
> (Blundell eq. C.31), so $C_V = 9Nk(T/T_D)^3\cdot4\pi^4/15 = \tfrac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk(T/T_D)^3$; equivalently $C_V = dU/dT$. Per mole $Nk = R$ and $12\pi^4R/5 = 1944\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}$. The value at $T = T_D$ is a numerical integral.

> [!remark] Remark: Why so simple a model works so well
> - *Exact at both ends* (Schroeder). At high temperature only the total number of modes matters, and the cutoff is chosen to get it right. At low temperature only long-wavelength modes are excited, $\hbar\omega \lesssim kT \ll kT_D$; for them the continuum description and the linear dispersion are exact, and how the short-wavelength modes are counted is irrelevant because they are frozen. The $T^3$ law is therefore a theorem about any three-dimensional crystal, not just about Debye's model; it holds to 1% below about $T_D/12$ (computed).
> - *Einstein versus Debye.* Einstein's model freezes every mode together below $\Theta_E$; Debye's spectrum reaches down to $\omega = 0$, and the number of modes with $\hbar\omega < kT$ grows as $T^3$, each contributing about $k$: hence $C_V \propto T^3$. Blundell's "density of states" view: $g(\omega) \propto \omega^2$ at low frequency against a delta function.
> - *Third law.* $C_V \to 0$ as $T \to 0$ is required for the entropy $S(T) = \int_0^TC_V\,dT'/T'$ to be finite ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-3|Principle §A4.6.3]], [[§B2.3 The Third Law#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]]); a constant $C_V$ would make it diverge (Schwartz).
> - *Photons and phonons.* The phonon gas at low $T$ is the photon gas with $c \to c_s$, three polarizations instead of two, and the energy density $\tfrac{3}{2}\cdot\pi^2(kT)^4/(15\hbar^3c_s^3)$; the cutoff $\omega_D$, absent for light, matters only near and above $T_D$ (Schwartz).

^rem-b11-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: The T³ law at level A
> The rock-salt example of level A used $C(T) = k\,(T/\theta)^3$ with $k = 1940\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}$ and $\theta = 281\ \mathrm K$ ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^ex-a1-3-3|Example §A1.3.3]]). Theorem §B11.2.3 explains both numbers: $k = 12\pi^4R/5 = 1944\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}$ per mole of atoms (here ions), and $\theta$ plays the role of the Debye temperature $T_D$. (Blundell lists $T_D = 321\ \mathrm K$ for NaCl; counted per mole of NaCl formula units, $2N_A$ ions, the same law would read $1944\,(T/\theta)^3$ with $\theta = T_D/2^{1/3} = 255\ \mathrm K$; the level-A value $281\ \mathrm K$ lies between.) The closing sentence of that example, that the heat capacity of solids falls towards zero at low temperature, "a quantum effect first explained by Debye in 1912", is now derived.

^rem-b11-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Real solids
> - *Dispersion.* A chain of atoms has $\omega = 2\omega_0|\sin(ka/2)|$, linear only for $ka \ll 1$ and with a ceiling $2\omega_0$ ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-3|WO Theorem §B3.1.3]]), and exactly $N$ modes per chain ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-4|WO Theorem §B3.1.4]]). Debye's cutoff mimics the ceiling. Measured dispersion curves (copper, by inelastic neutron scattering) bend over, and the density of states $g(\omega)$ has peaks where they are flat, while staying $\propto\omega^2$ at low frequency (Blundell §24.3). (Schwartz writes the chain dispersion as $\omega \sim \sin^2(\cdot)$; it is $\omega^2$, not $\omega$, that goes as $\sin^2$.)
> - *Two speeds.* Transverse waves are slower than longitudinal ones; the low-temperature count needs the average $3/c_s^3 = 2/c_T^3 + 1/c_L^3$ (Blundell), not the longitudinal speed alone. This is one reason why $T_D$ computed from the speed of sound comes out too high ([[§B11.2★ Phonons and the Debye Model#^ex-b11-2-1|Example §B11.2.1]]).
> - *Optic modes.* With two or more atoms per cell, the additional optic branches have frequencies that vary little with $k$ and are well described by Einstein terms; the acoustic branches by Debye (Blundell).
> - *Metals.* The conduction electrons add a term linear in $T$ ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^thm-a6-2-5|QM Theorem §A6.2.5]]), so $C = \gamma T + \tfrac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk\,T^3/T_D^3$; plotting $C/T$ against $T^2$ gives a straight line whose intercept is $\gamma$ (Schroeder Figure 7.28). The linear term is derived in [[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-4|Theorem §B13.1.4]], and copper's $C/T$ against $T^2$ is worked out in [[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^ex-b13-1-2|Example §B13.1.2]].
> - *Fitting.* Debye's formula with one fitted parameter $T_D$ describes measured heat capacities of many metals well over the whole range (Schwartz, Figure 2; Schroeder Figure 1.14); the $T_D$ obtained by fitting is the one listed in tables.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B11.2.1: The Debye Temperature of Aluminium from the Speed of Sound
> Aluminium has density $2700\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$ and molar mass $26.98\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, so $N/V = 6.03\times10^{28}\ \mathrm m^{-3}$, and a speed of sound $c_s = 5100\ \mathrm{m/s}$. By [[§B11.2★ Phonons and the Debye Model#^def-b11-2-2|Def. §B11.2.2]],
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{6\pi^2N}{V}\Bigr)^{1/3} = 1.53\times10^{10}\ \mathrm m^{-1}, \qquad \omega_D = 7.8\times10^{13}\ \mathrm s^{-1}, \qquad T_D = \frac{\hbar\omega_D}{k} = 595\ \mathrm K .
> $$
>
> The value fitted to the measured heat capacity is $394\ \mathrm K$ (Schwartz quotes $591\ \mathrm K$ against a fit of $396\ \mathrm K$; Blundell lists $394\ \mathrm K$). The estimate is too high because it uses one speed for all three polarizations, whereas the two transverse branches are slower, and because it ignores the bending of the dispersion curves near the zone boundary ([[§B11.2★ Phonons and the Debye Model#^rem-b11-2-3|Remark: Real solids]]). The minimum wavelength $2\pi/k_D = 0.41\ \mathrm{nm}$ is of the order of the atomic spacing ($n^{-1/3} = 0.26\ \mathrm{nm}$), as it must be. At room temperature $T/T_D \approx 0.75$, and $C_V$ is already $0.92 \times 3R$ (Figure, panel a): aluminium nearly obeys Dulong–Petit.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, §2.2 (numbers); Blundell §24.2 (table); Schroeder Problem 7.58 (the same estimate for copper)*

^ex-b11-2-1

> [!example] Example §B11.2.2: Phonons in Liquid Helium-4
> Below $0.6\ \mathrm K$ the measured heat capacity of liquid $^4$He is $C_V/Nk = (T/4.67\ \mathrm K)^3$. A liquid carries only longitudinal sound, so treat it as a Debye solid with one polarization ($g_s = 1$), $c_s = 238\ \mathrm{m/s}$, density $0.145\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$, $n = 2.18\times10^{28}\ \mathrm m^{-3}$. At low $T$, by the integral of Theorem §B11.2.3 with $g(\omega) = V\omega^2/2\pi^2c_s^3$,
>
> $$
> U - E_0 = \frac{V\hbar}{2\pi^2c_s^3}\Bigl(\frac{kT}{\hbar}\Bigr)^4\frac{\pi^4}{15} = \frac{\pi^2V(kT)^4}{30\hbar^3c_s^3}, \qquad \frac{C_V}{Nk} = \frac{2\pi^2}{15\,n}\Bigl(\frac{kT}{\hbar c_s}\Bigr)^3 .
> $$
>
> With $\hbar c_s/k = 1.82\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{K\cdot m}$ this is $C_V/Nk = 0.0100\,(T/\mathrm K)^3 = (T/4.64\ \mathrm K)^3$, against the measured $(T/4.67\ \mathrm K)^3$ (coefficients within 2%): at the lowest temperatures the heat of superfluid helium is carried by sound quanta.
>
> *Source: Schroeder Problem 7.61 (solved here)*
