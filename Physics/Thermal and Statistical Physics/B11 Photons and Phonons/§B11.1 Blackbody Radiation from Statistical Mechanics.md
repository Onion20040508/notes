Level A took the blackbody as a model ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^mod-a1-5-5|Model §A1.5.5]]) and the Stefan–Boltzmann law from experiment ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-6|Law §A1.5.6]]); Quantum Mechanics derived Planck's law, Wien's law and $\sigma$ from Planck's quantized oscillators and a classical count of cavity modes, quoting the factor $c/4$ between energy density and emitted power ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-4|QM Theorem §A1.1.4]], [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-6|QM Theorem §A1.1.6]]). Here the radiation in a cavity is treated as an ideal gas of bosons whose number is not conserved, so that $\mu = 0$ ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]). From one grand partition function come the Planck spectrum, the energy, pressure, entropy, heat capacity and photon number of the radiation, the factor $c/4$ and with it the Stefan–Boltzmann law as a theorem (a layer change), Kirchhoff's law, and the cooling of radiation by adiabatic expansion that makes the cosmic background cold today.

## The photon gas

> [!model] Model §B11.1.1: Photon Gas
> Electromagnetic radiation in a cavity of volume $V$ whose walls are held at temperature $T$, in equilibrium with the walls. Assumptions:
> - the radiation is a gas of photons, bosons of energy $\varepsilon = \hbar\omega = \hbar ck$ with two polarization states per wave vector ($g_s = 2$), occupying the cavity modes as single-particle states;
> - photons do not interact with one another (Maxwell's equations in vacuum are linear); they come to equilibrium only through absorption and emission by the walls;
> - their number is not conserved: the walls create and destroy photons freely;
> - the cavity is large compared with the thermal wavelengths $hc/kT$, so that the mode count of [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-4|Theorem §B10.1.4]] applies and the shape and material of the cavity do not matter.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, §3.2; Schroeder §7.4; Blundell §23.5*

^mod-b11-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.2: The Chemical Potential of Photons Is Zero
> In the photon gas, $\mu = 0$, so the mean number of photons in a mode of frequency $\omega$ is the Planck distribution
>
> $$
> \langle n\rangle = \frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1} .
> $$
>
> The same holds for any boson excitations whose number is not conserved, such as phonons ([[§B11.2 Phonons and the Debye Model]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.4, eqs. (7.63)–(7.68); Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, §1, §2.2, eqs. (42)–(43); Blundell Exercise 23.5(b)*

^thm-b11-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Free energy.* At fixed $T$ and $V$ the photon number is not constrained and takes the value that minimizes $F$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]): $(\partial F/\partial N)_{T,V} = 0$, and this derivative is $\mu$ ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]]).
>
> *Reactions.* A charged particle in the wall can emit or absorb a photon, $e \rightleftharpoons e + \gamma$. In equilibrium the chemical potentials balance like the reaction ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]): $\mu_e = \mu_e + \mu_\gamma$, so $\mu_\gamma = 0$.
>
> *Thermodynamics alone* (Schwartz, Blundell). By [[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]], $F = -pV$ and $G = F + pV = 0 = \mu N$.
>
> With $\mu = 0$, [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]] gives the occupation.

## What thermodynamics alone gives

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.3: Radiation Pressure and Stefan's Law from Thermodynamics
> The energy density $u = U/V$ of cavity radiation depends on $T$ only, and the pressure of isotropic radiation is
>
> $$
> p = \frac u3 .
> $$
>
> Together these force
>
> $$
> u = aT^4, \qquad F = -pV = -\tfrac13\,aVT^4,
> $$
>
> with a constant $a$ that thermodynamics cannot determine. For a collimated beam instead, $p = u$ on an absorber ([[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure#^thm-a12-4-2|EM Theorem §A12.4.2]]).
>
> *Source: Blundell §23.1–23.2, §23.4, eqs. (23.4)–(23.11), (23.24); Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, §3.1, eqs. (37)–(41); Schroeder Problem 7.45*

^thm-b11-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *$u$ depends on $T$ only.* Join two cavities at the same temperature, of different shape, size or wall material, by a tube. If one held a higher energy density, a net energy flux would run along the tube from one body to another at the same temperature, contradicting the second law ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]]). With a filter in the tube the same holds band by band, so even the spectral density $u_\lambda$ is a universal function of $\lambda$ and $T$ (Blundell §23.2).
>
> *$p = u/3$.* A plane wave of energy density $u'$ carries momentum density $u'/c$ along its direction ([[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure#^thm-a12-4-1|EM Theorem §A12.4.1]]). Radiation travelling at angle $\theta$ to the normal of a wall delivers per unit area and time the normal momentum $(u'/c)\cos\theta \cdot c\cos\theta = u'\cos^2\theta$ when absorbed, twice that when reflected, with only the half of the directions that approach the wall contributing. Averaged over isotropic directions, $\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle = \tfrac13$, so $p = u/3$. (Exactly the same follows from $\varepsilon \propto V^{-1/3}$ for every mode, [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]].)
>
> *$u \propto T^4$.* The energy equation $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = T(\partial p/\partial T)_V - p$, which follows from $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$ and the Maxwell relation $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial p/\partial T)_V$ ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]], [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]]), becomes with $U = u(T)V$ and $p = u/3$
>
> $$
> u = \frac T3\,\frac{du}{dT} - \frac u3 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{du}{u} = 4\,\frac{dT}{T} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u = aT^4 .
> $$
>
> *Free energy.* $p$ depends on $T$ only, so integrating $(\partial F/\partial V)_T = -p$ at fixed $T$ from $V = 0$ gives $F = -pV = -U/3$ (Schwartz).

## The Planck spectrum from Bose statistics

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.4: Planck Spectrum of the Photon Gas
> The energy density of cavity radiation per unit angular frequency, frequency and wavelength is
>
> $$
> u(\omega) = \frac{\hbar}{\pi^2c^3}\,\frac{\omega^3}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1}, \qquad u_\nu = \frac{8\pi h}{c^3}\,\frac{\nu^3}{e^{h\nu/kT} - 1}, \qquad u_\lambda = \frac{8\pi hc}{\lambda^5}\,\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda kT} - 1},
> $$
>
> with $u = \int u(\omega)\,d\omega = \int u_\nu\,d\nu = \int u_\lambda\,d\lambda$. The spectrum per unit frequency peaks at $h\nu = 2.821\,kT$; per unit wavelength at $hc/\lambda = 4.965\,kT$ (Wien's law, [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-5|QM Theorem §A1.1.5]]). For $h\nu \ll kT$ it reduces to the Rayleigh–Jeans law $u_\nu = 8\pi\nu^2kT/c^3$ ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-1|QM Theorem §A1.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, eqs. (52)–(56), (64); Schroeder §7.4, eqs. (7.81)–(7.84); Blundell §23.5–23.6, eqs. (23.30)–(23.47), (23.53)–(23.54)*

^thm-b11-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-4|Theorem §B10.1.4]] with $g_s = 2$ and $\omega = ck$, the cavity has $g(\omega)\,d\omega = V\omega^2\,d\omega/\pi^2c^3$ modes between $\omega$ and $\omega + d\omega$. Each holds on average $\langle n\rangle = 1/(e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1)$ photons ([[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-2|Theorem §B11.1.2]]) of energy $\hbar\omega$, so
>
> $$
> U = \sum_{\text{modes}}\hbar\omega\,\langle n\rangle = \int_0^\infty \hbar\omega\,\frac{g(\omega)\,d\omega}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1} = V\int_0^\infty \frac{\hbar}{\pi^2c^3}\,\frac{\omega^3\,d\omega}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1} .
> $$
>
> The zero-point energies $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$ of the modes are omitted: they do not depend on $T$ and their sum diverges; the zero of energy is set at the vacuum (Blundell). Changing variables, $u(\omega)\,d\omega = u_\nu\,d\nu$ with $\omega = 2\pi\nu$, and $u_\nu|d\nu| = u_\lambda|d\lambda|$ with $\nu = c/\lambda$, $|d\nu/d\lambda| = c/\lambda^2$. The peaks: with $x = h\nu/kT$, $u_\nu \propto x^3/(e^x - 1)$ and $u_\lambda \propto x^5/(e^x - 1)$; setting the derivative to zero gives $x = 3(1 - e^{-x})$ and $x = 5(1 - e^{-x})$, solved by iteration as $2.821$ and $4.965$ (Blundell Exercise 23.2). For $x \ll 1$, $e^x - 1 \approx x$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]).

^der-b11-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-4|Theorem §B10.1.4]], [[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-2|Theorem §B11.1.2]], [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Two derivations of one law
> [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-4|QM Theorem §A1.1.4]] gave each cavity mode the mean energy of a quantized oscillator, $\hbar\omega/(e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1)$ ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-3|QM Theorem §A1.1.3]]). Here each mode is a single-particle state holding a Bose-distributed number of photons. The two readings are the same arithmetic: the excitation number of a mode oscillator *is* the number of photons in that mode ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^rem-b10-1-5|Remark: Fluctuations, bunching and the Planck oscillator]]), and $\mu = 0$ expresses that excitations come and go freely. Historically Bose (1924) obtained Planck's law in the second way, treating light as a gas of particles in phase-space cells of size $h^3$ and maximizing the entropy (Blundell's biography of Bose); Einstein then extended the method to atoms with conserved number, which led to [[§B12.1 Bose–Einstein Condensation]]. What the gas picture adds is the whole thermodynamics of the radiation, below.

## Thermodynamics of black-body radiation

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.5: Thermodynamic Functions of the Photon Gas
> With
>
> $$
> a = \frac{\pi^2k^4}{15\hbar^3c^3} = \frac{8\pi^5k^4}{15h^3c^3} = 7.566\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-4}},
> $$
>
> the photon gas in volume $V$ at temperature $T$ has
>
> $$
> \ln\mathcal Z = \frac{\pi^2V(kT)^3}{45\hbar^3c^3}, \qquad F = \Phi = -\tfrac13\,aVT^4, \qquad U = aVT^4, \qquad p = \tfrac13\,aT^4 = \frac{u}{3},
> $$
>
> $$
> S = \tfrac43\,aVT^3 = \frac{4U}{3T}, \qquad C_V = 4aVT^3, \qquad N = \frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}\Bigl(\frac{kT}{\hbar c}\Bigr)^3V = 16\pi\zeta(3)\Bigl(\frac{kT}{hc}\Bigr)^3V .
> $$
>
> Per photon, $U/N = \dfrac{\pi^4}{30\zeta(3)}\,kT = 2.701\,kT$ and $S/N = \dfrac{2\pi^4}{45\zeta(3)}\,k = 3.602\,k$, independent of $T$. $C_V$ and $S$ vanish as $T \to 0$, as the third law requires ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-3|Principle §A4.6.3]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, §3.5, eqs. (68)–(74); Schroeder §7.4, eqs. (7.86)–(7.89), Problems 7.44, 7.46; Blundell §23.5, eqs. (23.37)–(23.40), Exercises 23.5–23.7*

^thm-b11-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]] and [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]] with $\mu = 0$, $\ln\mathcal Z = -\int_0^\infty g(\omega)\ln(1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})\,d\omega$ with $g(\omega) = V\omega^2/\pi^2c^3$. Integrate by parts ($\omega^2 = d(\omega^3/3)/d\omega$; the boundary terms vanish):
>
> $$
> \ln\mathcal Z = \frac{V}{\pi^2c^3}\int_0^\infty\frac{\omega^3}{3}\,\frac{\beta\hbar\,d\omega}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1} = \frac{V}{3\pi^2c^3}\Bigl(\frac{kT}{\hbar}\Bigr)^3\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x - 1} = \frac{V}{3\pi^2c^3}\Bigl(\frac{kT}{\hbar}\Bigr)^3\frac{\pi^4}{15},
> $$
>
> using [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]]. Then $\Phi = -kT\ln\mathcal Z = -\tfrac13aVT^4$ ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-3|Theorem §B7.3.3]]), which equals $F = \Phi + \mu N$ since $\mu = 0$. Hence $S = -(\partial F/\partial T)_V = \tfrac43aVT^3$, $p = -(\partial F/\partial V)_T = \tfrac13aT^4$, $U = F + TS = aVT^4$ (which agrees with $\int u(\omega)\,d\omega$ of [[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]] and fixes the constant of Theorem §B11.1.3), and $C_V = (\partial U/\partial T)_V = 4aVT^3$; integrating $C_V/T$ from $0$ gives the same $S$ (Schroeder). The photon number is $N = \int g(\omega)\,d\omega/(e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1) = (V/\pi^2c^3)(kT/\hbar)^3\int x^2\,dx/(e^x - 1)$ with $\int_0^\infty x^2\,dx/(e^x - 1) = \Gamma(3)\zeta(3) = 2\zeta(3)$; and $2\zeta(3)/\pi^2\cdot(2\pi)^3 = 16\pi\zeta(3)$. Dividing, $U/N = (\pi^4/15)kT/(2\zeta(3))$ and $S/N = \tfrac43(\pi^4/15)k/(2\zeta(3))$.

*Uses:* [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]], [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]], [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]], [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-3|Theorem §B7.3.3]], [[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-2|Theorem §B11.1.2]], [[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: A gas that is always quantum
> - *Dimensional analysis* (Schroeder). Each photon carries energy of order $kT$, and the only length is the typical wavelength $\ell = hc/kT$; so $U \sim VkT/\ell^3 \propto VT^4$. The statistics supply the number $8\pi^5/15$.
> - *Never classical.* The photon density is itself of order $\ell^{-3}$, about one photon per thermal wavelength cubed ($N\ell^3/V = 16\pi\zeta(3) = 60$): the degeneracy parameter of [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-1|Def. §B10.2.1]] is fixed at order one, and no temperature makes radiation a classical gas.
> - *Unlike an ideal gas*, $U/N = 2.70\,kT$ rather than $\tfrac32kT$, and $pV = U/3$ rather than $\tfrac23U$ ([[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]]): $\varepsilon = pc$ instead of $p^2/2m$ (Blundell Exercise 23.7).
> - *$C_p = \infty$.* Since $p$ depends on $T$ alone, isotherms are isobars: heat added at constant pressure goes into making more radiation at the same temperature, never into raising $T$ (Blundell Exercise 23.5).

## Emission: the Stefan–Boltzmann law as a theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.6: Emission from a Cavity; Stefan–Boltzmann and Kirchhoff Laws
> 1. Radiation of energy density $u$, isotropic inside a cavity, leaves through a small hole with power per unit area $J = \tfrac c4u$; the same factor relates the spectral emission to $u_\lambda$, so the spectrum outside equals the spectrum inside. With $u = aT^4$,
>
>    $$
>    J = \sigma T^4, \qquad \sigma = \frac{ac}{4} = \frac{\pi^2k^4}{60\hbar^3c^2} = \frac{2\pi^5k^4}{15h^3c^2} = 5.670\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}} .
>    $$
> 2. (*Kirchhoff's law*) A surface at temperature $T$ that absorbs the fraction $\alpha_\lambda$ of radiation of wavelength $\lambda$ emits, per unit area and wavelength, $e_\lambda = \alpha_\lambda\,\tfrac c4u_\lambda(T)$. A black surface ($\alpha_\lambda = 1$) emits exactly like the hole; a surface with $\alpha_\lambda = e$ at all relevant wavelengths emits $P = e\sigma AT^4$.
>
> Statement 2 with statement 1 is the Stefan–Boltzmann law of [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-6|Law §A1.5.6]], now a theorem: $\sigma$ is computed from $h$, $c$ and $k$, and the emissivity of level A is the absorptivity.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.4, "Photons escaping through a hole" and "Radiation from other objects", eqs. (7.90)–(7.99); Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, eqs. (57)–(63); Blundell §23.1–23.3, eqs. (23.6), (23.12)–(23.17), (23.40)*

^thm-b11-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The factor $c/4$* (Schroeder). The photons leaving through a hole of area $A$ in time $dt$ were, a time $R/c$ earlier, in a hemispherical shell of radius $R$ and thickness $c\,dt$ centred on the hole. A chunk of the shell at polar angle $\theta$ from the normal has volume $R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi\cdot c\,dt$ and energy $u$ times that. Its radiation travels equally in all directions, and the fraction aimed at the hole is the hole's apparent area over the area of a sphere: $A\cos\theta/4\pi R^2$. Hence
>
> $$
> dE = \int_0^{2\pi}\!d\phi\int_0^{\pi/2}\! d\theta\ u\,c\,dt\,R^2\sin\theta\,\frac{A\cos\theta}{4\pi R^2} = \frac{uc\,A\,dt}{4\pi}\cdot2\pi\cdot\frac12 = \frac c4\,u\,A\,dt .
> $$
>
> The factor $\cos\theta$ is Lambert's projection; Schwartz phrases the same integral as an effective solid angle $\int\cos\theta\,d\Omega = \pi$ out of $4\pi$. All photons move at $c$, so every frequency leaves at the same rate and the spectrum is unchanged. With $a$ from [[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-5|Theorem §B11.1.5]], $\sigma = ac/4 = \pi^2k^4/60\hbar^3c^2$; with $\hbar = h/2\pi$ this is $2\pi^5k^4/15h^3c^2$, the constant of [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-6|QM Theorem §A1.1.6]].
>
> *Kirchhoff* (Schroeder, Blundell). Put the surface inside the cavity, or facing the hole, at the same temperature. Per unit area it receives $\tfrac c4u_\lambda\,d\lambda$ in each band and absorbs $\alpha_\lambda\tfrac c4u_\lambda\,d\lambda$. Insert a filter passing only that band. If the surface emitted more or less than it absorbs in that band, net energy would flow between two bodies at the same temperature and one would warm while the other cooled, contradicting the second law ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]]). So $e_\lambda = \alpha_\lambda\tfrac c4u_\lambda$, and for $\alpha_\lambda = e$ integrating over $\lambda$ gives $e\sigma T^4$ per unit area.

*Uses:* [[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-5|Theorem §B11.1.5]], [[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]], [[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-6|452 Ex. §15.6]]

> [!remark] Remark: From law to theorem
> The chain of layers is now complete. In [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-6|Law §A1.5.6]] the $T^4$ law and $\sigma$ are measured, and the blackbody is an idealization ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^mod-a1-5-5|Model §A1.5.5]]) whose emissivity–absorptivity link is argued in [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^rem-a1-5-5|Remark: The thermal spectrum]]. [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-6|QM Theorem §A1.1.6]] integrates Planck's law but quotes the factor $c/4$ (step 1 of [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^der-a1-1-1|its mode-count derivation]]). Here thermodynamics gives $T^4$ (Theorem §B11.1.3), Bose statistics gives the spectrum and $a$ (Theorems §B11.1.4–5), geometry gives $c/4$, and the second law gives Kirchhoff's law. Conversely, measured $\sigma$ and Wien's constant determine $h$ and $k$.

## Adiabatic expansion

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.7: Adiabatic Expansion of Black-Body Radiation
> In a reversible adiabatic change of volume, radiation keeps
>
> $$
> VT^3 = \text{const}, \qquad pV^{4/3} = \text{const}, \qquad T \propto V^{-1/3} .
> $$
>
> If the radiation expands without exchanging photons with anything, every mode keeps its occupation number, every mode frequency scales as $V^{-1/3}$, and the spectrum stays exactly Planckian, at the temperature $T \propto V^{-1/3}$; the photon number $N \propto VT^3$ is conserved as well.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.4, "The cosmic background radiation"; Blundell §23.7; Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, §3.5 (constant baryon-to-photon ratio)*

^thm-b11-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Reversible and adiabatic means isentropic ([[§A4.5 Entropy]]), and $S = \tfrac43aVT^3$ ([[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-5|Theorem §B11.1.5]]), so $VT^3$ is constant; with $p = \tfrac13aT^4 \propto T^4 \propto V^{-4/3}$, $pV^{4/3}$ is constant. Compare $pV^{5/3}$ for a monatomic ideal gas ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-2|Theorem §A3.6.2]]).
>
> *The spectrum.* Each mode has $\omega_i = c|\vec k_i|$ with $|\vec k_i| \propto 1/L = V^{-1/3}$. Suppose each mode keeps its occupation $\langle n_i\rangle$ (no photons are created, destroyed or scattered between modes). Initially $\langle n_i\rangle = 1/(e^{\hbar\omega_i/kT} - 1)$; after the expansion $\omega_i' = \omega_iV^{1/3}/V'^{1/3}$, and $\langle n_i\rangle = 1/(e^{\hbar\omega_i'/kT'} - 1)$ holds for every mode at once with $T' = TV^{1/3}/V'^{1/3}$: a Planck distribution at the lower temperature. This is consistent with the entropy: $S$ is a sum over modes of a function of the occupation alone ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^rem-b10-1-8|Remark: The same distributions from maximal entropy]]), so unchanged occupations mean unchanged $S$; indeed $T'^3V' = T^3(V/V')V' = T^3V$. The photon number $\sum_i\langle n_i\rangle$ is unchanged, consistent with $N \propto VT^3$.

> [!remark] Remark: The cosmic background
> The cosmic microwave background ([[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^def-a8-4-2|QM Def. §A8.4.2]]) was released when the universe became transparent at about $3000\ \mathrm K$; since then the photons have travelled freely while space expanded about a thousandfold in every direction. By Theorem §B11.1.7 the radiation keeps an exact Planck spectrum at a temperature inversely proportional to the scale factor. This supplies the step that [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^der-a8-4-8|QM Derivation of Theorem §A8.4.8]] left to a higher level: not only the peak but the whole spectrum is that of a blackbody at $T_0 = aT$. Since the photon number and the number of baryons both scale as $V^{-1}$, their ratio has stayed fixed (Schwartz).

^rem-b11-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: Stimulated emission and lasers
> Einstein's argument for stimulated emission ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-6|QM Theorem §A5.4.6]]) consists in demanding that atoms in thermal equilibrium with radiation reproduce the Planck spectrum of Theorem §B11.1.4 (Blundell §23.8, Schroeder Problem 7.41). In equilibrium the upper level is always less populated, so amplification needs a population inversion maintained by pumping ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-7|QM Theorem §A5.4.7]]): a laser is not a photon gas in equilibrium. Schwartz calls it a condensation of photons into one mode that is possible only out of equilibrium ([[§B12.1 Bose–Einstein Condensation]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B11.1.1: Radiation Pressure in a Kiln and in the Sun
> *A kiln* at $1500\ \mathrm K$ ($1\ \mathrm m^3$): $U = aVT^4 = (7.57\times10^{-16})(1500)^4\ \mathrm J = 3.8\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm J$, and $p = U/3V = 1.3\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Pa}$, against $10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$ for the air in it: eight orders of magnitude less. The radiation holds $N = 16\pi\zeta(3)(kT/hc)^3V = 6.8\times10^{16}$ photons.
>
> *The centre of the Sun,* $T = 1.5\times10^7\ \mathrm K$, density $\rho \approx 10^5\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$ of ionized hydrogen. Radiation: $p_{\text{rad}} = \tfrac13aT^4 = \tfrac13(7.57\times10^{-16})(1.5\times10^7)^4\ \mathrm{Pa} = 1.3\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{Pa}$. Gas: a proton and an electron per proton mass, $n = 2\rho/m_p = 1.2\times10^{32}\ \mathrm m^{-3}$, $p_{\text{gas}} = nkT = 2.5\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{Pa}$. The radiation pressure is $5\times10^{-4}$ of the gas pressure. Because $p_{\text{rad}} \propto T^4$ and $p_{\text{gas}} \propto T$, radiation matters only in the hottest, most massive stars ([[§B13.3 White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder Problems 7.42 and 7.45 (solved here)*

^ex-b11-1-1

> [!example] Example §B11.1.2: The Photon Gas of the Cosmic Background
> For $T_0 = 2.725\ \mathrm K$, Theorem §B11.1.5 gives
>
> $$
> u = aT_0^4 = 4.17\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{J/m^3} = 0.260\ \mathrm{MeV/m^3}, \qquad n_\gamma = 16\pi\zeta(3)\Bigl(\frac{kT_0}{hc}\Bigr)^3 = 4.1\times10^8\ \mathrm m^{-3} = 411\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}},
> $$
>
> $$
> \frac{S}{V} = 3.602\,k\,n_\gamma = 1.5\times10^9\,k\ \mathrm{per\ m^3}, \qquad \langle\varepsilon\rangle = 2.701\,kT_0 = 6.3\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV} .
> $$
>
> The spectrum peaks at $\nu = 2.821\,kT_0/h = 160\ \mathrm{GHz}$ per unit frequency and at $\lambda = hc/4.965kT_0 = 1.06\ \mathrm{mm}$ per unit wavelength ([[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^thm-a8-4-8|QM Theorem §A8.4.8]]). Ordinary matter, of order one proton per cubic metre, has an energy density several hundred times larger ($\sim 10^2$–$10^3\ \mathrm{MeV/m^3}$; Schwartz, Schroeder) but an entropy of only a few $k$ per cubic metre: the entropy of the universe is almost all in the background photons. With about $0.25$ baryons per cubic metre the baryon-to-photon ratio is $\eta \approx 6\times10^{-10}$ (Schwartz), constant since decoupling by Theorem §B11.1.7. Schwartz's lecture gives $n_\gamma = 8\times10^8\ \mathrm m^{-3}$ and $3\times10^9\,k$ per $\mathrm m^3$, twice the values computed here; the entropy density agrees with Schroeder's $1.5\times10^9\,k$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, §3.5; Schroeder §7.4, Problem 7.44; Blundell Exercise 23.3*
