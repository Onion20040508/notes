What holds up a star that has burned all its fuel? Not thermal pressure, which would leak away as the star radiates, but the degeneracy pressure of its electrons ([[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-2|Theorem §B13.1.2]]), which persists at $T = 0$. This section balances it against Newtonian gravity ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]], [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-1|CM Theorem §A8.2.1]]) through hydrostatic equilibrium and the virial theorem ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-6|CM Theorem §B3.1.6]]). Two results follow. Nonrelativistic electrons give a stable star whose radius shrinks as its mass grows, $R \propto M^{-1/3}$. In a heavier star the electrons become relativistic ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|REL Theorem §A3.3.1]]), the pressure softens, and above the Chandrasekhar mass of about $1.4\,M_\odot$ no cold star can exist. The same estimates with neutrons give neutron stars. General-relativistic corrections, which matter for neutron stars, belong to Relativity C5 (planned).

*Notation.* $M_\odot = 1.99\times10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $R_\oplus = 6.37\times10^6\ \mathrm m$; $\mu_e$ is the mass per electron in units of $m_p$; $x = p_F/m_ec$; $\lambda_C = \hbar/m_ec$; $k = k_B$; $P$ is the pressure and $p$ a momentum (as in [[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals]]). "Star" means a spherical, nonrotating, Newtonian, self-gravitating body.

## The cold electron gas at any density

> [!model] Model §B13.3.1: Cold White Dwarf
> A white dwarf is the exhausted core of a star, of mass $M$ and radius $R$, made of fully ionized helium, carbon or oxygen. Assumptions:
> - The atoms are fully ionized ($kT \sim 1\ \mathrm{keV}$ inside, far above atomic binding energies), and charge neutrality holds locally. Each electron comes with mass $\mu_em_p$, where $\mu_e = A/Z = 2$ for He, C and O, so $n_e = \rho/\mu_em_p$.
> - The nuclei provide the mass; their pressure is negligible beside that of the electrons, since a degenerate gas's pressure varies inversely with the particle mass (Theorem §B13.3.2).
> - The electrons form an ideal Fermi gas ([[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^mod-b13-1-1|Model §B13.1.1]]) at $T = 0$, with the relativistic dispersion $\varepsilon = \sqrt{m_e^2c^4 + p^2c^2}$. This is justified because $T \sim 10^7\ \mathrm K$ while $T_F \sim 10^9\ \mathrm K$ (Example §B13.3.1).
> - Gravity is Newtonian; the star does not rotate and has no nuclear burning.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 15, §4, eq. (43) · Blundell §36.1, eq. (36.6) · Schroeder §7.3, Problem 7.23*

^mod-b13-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.3.2: Pressure of a Degenerate Electron Gas at Any Density
> A $T = 0$ gas of electrons with density $n$ has Fermi momentum $p_F = \hbar(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$, that is, $n = x^3/(3\pi^2\lambda_C^3)$ with $x = p_F/m_ec$, and pressure
>
> $$
> P = \frac{m_e^4c^5}{8\pi^2\hbar^3}\Bigl[\tfrac13\,x\,(2x^2 - 3)\sqrt{1 + x^2} + \operatorname{arsinh}x\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> In the two limits:
>
> $$
> x \ll 1:\quad P = \frac{\hbar^2}{5m_e}\bigl(3\pi^2\bigr)^{2/3}n^{5/3} = \tfrac25\,n\varepsilon_F, \qquad\qquad x \gg 1:\quad P = \frac{\hbar c}{4}\bigl(3\pi^2\bigr)^{1/3}n^{4/3} = \frac13\,\frac UV ,
> $$
>
> where in the ultrarelativistic limit $\varepsilon_F = p_Fc = \hbar c(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$ and $U = \tfrac34 N\varepsilon_F$. With $n = \rho/\mu_em_p$, the two limits are **polytropes** $P = K\rho^{1 + 1/n_p}$, of index $n_p = \tfrac32$ ($P \propto \rho^{5/3}$) and $n_p = 3$ ($P \propto \rho^{4/3}$). The crossover is at $x \approx 1$, that is, $\rho \approx \mu_em_p/(3\pi^2\lambda_C^3) \approx 2\times10^9\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$ for $\mu_e = 2$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 15, eqs. (44)–(52), (56)–(70) · Blundell §36.1–36.2, eqs. (36.1)–(36.5), (36.13)–(36.18) · Schroeder §7.3, Problem 7.22*

^thm-b13-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With two spin states the number of states with momentum below $p$ is $2\cdot\tfrac{4\pi p^3/3}{(2\pi\hbar)^3}V$, so the density of momentum states is $g(p) = Vp^2/\pi^2\hbar^3$ and $N = Vp_F^3/3\pi^2\hbar^3$. With the energy–momentum relation ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|REL Theorem §A3.3.1]]) the energy density, rest energy included, is
>
> $$
> u = \frac{1}{\pi^2\hbar^3}\int_0^{p_F}p^2\sqrt{m_e^2c^4 + p^2c^2}\,dp = \frac{m_e^4c^5}{\pi^2\hbar^3}\int_0^xt^2\sqrt{1 + t^2}\,dt = \frac{m_e^4c^5}{8\pi^2\hbar^3}\Bigl[x(1 + 2x^2)\sqrt{1 + x^2} - \operatorname{arsinh}x\Bigr]
> $$
>
> (differentiate the bracket to check). At $T = 0$, $P = -\partial(uV)/\partial V$ at fixed $N$, as in [[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^der-b13-1-2|Derivation §B13.1.2]]; the rest energy $Nm_ec^2$ does not depend on $V$ and drops out. Since $p_F \propto V^{-1/3}$, $dp_F/dV = -p_F/3V$, and
>
> $$
> P = -u + \frac{p_F}{3}\,\frac{du}{dp_F} = -u + \frac{p_F^3}{3\pi^2\hbar^3}\sqrt{m_e^2c^4 + p_F^2c^2} = n\,\varepsilon(p_F) - u ,
> $$
>
> which is $G = \mu N = U + PV$ at $T = 0$ ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]], with $\mu$ the energy of the top electron). Inserting $u$ and $n\varepsilon(p_F) = \tfrac{m_e^4c^5}{3\pi^2\hbar^3}x^3\sqrt{1+x^2}$ gives the bracket $\tfrac83x^3\sqrt{1 + x^2} - x(1 + 2x^2)\sqrt{1 + x^2} + \operatorname{arsinh}x = \tfrac13x(2x^2 - 3)\sqrt{1 + x^2} + \operatorname{arsinh}x$.
>
> *Limits.* For small $x$, expanding $\sqrt{1+x^2}$ and $\operatorname{arsinh}x = x - x^3/6 + 3x^5/40 - \cdots$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), the terms in $x$ and $x^3$ cancel and the bracket is $\tfrac{8}{15}x^5$; for large $x$ it is $\tfrac23x^4$. Substituting $x = \lambda_C(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$ gives the two forms. In the ultrarelativistic case directly: $\varepsilon = pc$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-2|REL Theorem §A3.3.2]]), $g(\varepsilon) \propto \varepsilon^2$, $\varepsilon_F = \hbar c(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$, $U = \tfrac34N\varepsilon_F \propto V^{-1/3}$ and $P = -dU/dV = U/3V$.

## Gravity: hydrostatic equilibrium and the virial theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.3.3: Hydrostatic Equilibrium and the Virial Theorem for a Star
> In a static spherical star with density $\rho(r)$, pressure $P(r)$, mass $m(r)$ inside radius $r$, and $P(R) = 0$ at the surface,
>
> $$
> \frac{dm}{dr} = 4\pi r^2\rho, \qquad \frac{dP}{dr} = -\frac{G\,m(r)\,\rho(r)}{r^2}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad 3\int_0^R P\,dV = -E_{\text{grav}}, \quad E_{\text{grav}} = -\int_0^M\frac{G\,m\,dm}{r} .
> $$
>
> For uniform density, $E_{\text{grav}} = -\tfrac35\,GM^2/R$. With $3PV = 2U$ (nonrelativistic particles, [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]]) the virial relation reads $2U + E_{\text{grav}} = 0$: this is the mechanical virial theorem $2\langle T\rangle = -\langle U\rangle$ for gravity ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-6|CM Theorem §B3.1.6]], degree $-1$; CM's $T$ and $U$ are the kinetic and potential energies, here $U$ and $E_{\text{grav}}$), now for a static continuum held up by pressure, with no time average. With $3PV = U$ (ultrarelativistic) it reads $U + E_{\text{grav}} = 0$, and the total energy $U + E_{\text{grav}}$ is zero.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 15, eqs. (14)–(19), (71) · Blundell §35.1.2–35.1.3, eqs. (35.12)–(35.16), (35.25)–(35.34); §36.1, eq. (36.7) · Schroeder §7.3, Problem 7.23(a)*

^thm-b13-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Mass.* $m(r) = \int_0^r4\pi r'^2\rho\,dr'$; differentiate.
>
> *Force balance.* A thin piece of a shell, of area $\Delta A$ and thickness $\Delta r$, is pushed outward by the pressure difference, $-(dP/dr)\,\Delta r\,\Delta A$, and pulled inward by the mass inside $r$ alone (shell theorem, [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|CM Theorem §A8.1.5]]) with $G\,m(r)\,\rho\,\Delta A\,\Delta r/r^2$. Setting the sum to zero gives the equation for $dP/dr$; it is the level-A $dp/dy = -\rho g$ ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-1|CM Theorem §A9.1.1]]) with a field $g(r) = Gm(r)/r^2$ that changes with depth.
>
> *Virial relation.* Multiply by $4\pi r^3$ and integrate over $[0, R]$, by parts on the left ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]):
>
> $$
> \bigl[4\pi r^3P\bigr]_0^R - 3\int_0^R4\pi r^2P\,dr = -\int_0^R\frac{G\,m(r)}{r}\,4\pi r^2\rho\,dr = -\int_0^M\frac{G\,m\,dm}{r} .
> $$
>
> The boundary term vanishes ($P(R) = 0$), and the right side is $E_{\text{grav}}$: assembling the star shell by shell, a shell $dm$ brought in from infinity onto the mass $m(r)$ below it has potential energy $-Gm\,dm/r$ ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-1|CM Theorem §A8.2.1]]).
>
> *Uniform sphere.* With $m = \tfrac43\pi r^3\rho$ and $dm = 4\pi r^2\rho\,dr$, $E_{\text{grav}} = -\int_0^R G\,\tfrac43\pi r^3\rho\cdot4\pi r^2\rho\,dr/r = -\tfrac{16\pi^2}{15}G\rho^2R^5 = -\tfrac35GM^2/R$.
>
> *Kinetic forms.* For an ideal gas of nonrelativistic particles $P = \tfrac23u$ locally, so $3\int P\,dV = 2U$; for ultrarelativistic particles $P = \tfrac13u$ and $3\int P\,dV = U$ ([[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]], [[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^thm-b13-3-2|Theorem §B13.3.2]]).

## White dwarfs

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.3.4: Mass–Radius Relation of a White Dwarf
> If the electrons are nonrelativistic and the density is taken uniform, the energy of the star as a function of its radius is
>
> $$
> E(R) = \frac{a}{R^2} - \frac35\,\frac{GM^2}{R}, \qquad a = \frac{3\hbar^2}{10m_e}\Bigl(\frac{9\pi}{4}\Bigr)^{2/3}\Bigl(\frac{M}{\mu_em_p}\Bigr)^{5/3} ,
> $$
>
> and its minimum, the equilibrium, lies at
>
> $$
> R = \Bigl(\frac{9\pi}{4}\Bigr)^{2/3}\frac{\hbar^2}{G\,m_e\,(\mu_em_p)^{5/3}}\;M^{-1/3} \approx 7.2\times10^3\ \mathrm{km}\,\Bigl(\frac{M}{M_\odot}\Bigr)^{-1/3} \qquad(\mu_e = 2) .
> $$
>
> The heavier the white dwarf, the smaller it is: $R \propto M^{-1/3}$, $MV$ is constant, and the mean density grows as $M^2$. The equilibrium is stable. Solving the hydrostatic equations exactly for $P \propto \rho^{5/3}$ gives the same law with a coefficient 1.22 times larger.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.3, Problem 7.23(b)–(d) · Blundell §36.1–36.2, eqs. (36.8)–(36.12), Example 36.1, Exercise 36.1 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 15, §5*

^thm-b13-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $N = M/\mu_em_p$ electrons fill a sphere of volume $\tfrac43\pi R^3$, so $3\pi^2n = 9\pi N/4R^3$ and, by [[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-2|Theorem §B13.1.2]], $E_{\text{kin}} = \tfrac35N\varepsilon_F = \tfrac35N\,\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\bigl(\tfrac{9\pi N}{4}\bigr)^{2/3}R^{-2} = a/R^2$. Add $E_{\text{grav}} = -\tfrac35GM^2/R$ ([[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^thm-b13-3-3|Theorem §B13.3.3]]). Then $dE/dR = -2a/R^3 + \tfrac35GM^2/R^2 = 0$ gives $R = 10a/3GM^2$, which is the formula; $d^2E/dR^2 > 0$ there. The condition $dE/dR = 0$ is $2E_{\text{kin}} + E_{\text{grav}} = 0$, the virial relation of Theorem §B13.3.3. Blundell's form of the same argument compares pressures: the electron pressure grows as $\rho^{5/3}$ on compression, the gravitational "pressure" $-E_{\text{grav}}/3V$ only as $\rho^{4/3}$, so a squeezed star pushes back, which is stability. The numerical value uses $M_\odot$, $\mu_e = 2$; the factor 1.22 comes from the $n_p = \tfrac32$ Lane–Emden solution ($\xi_1 = 3.654$, Derivation §B13.3.5).

^der-b13-3-4

*Uses:* [[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-2|Theorem §B13.1.2]], [[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^thm-b13-3-3|Theorem §B13.3.3]], [[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^mod-b13-3-1|Model §B13.3.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.3.5: Chandrasekhar Limit
> No cold white dwarf (Model §B13.3.1) exists with mass above
>
> $$
> M_{\text{Ch}} = C\,\Bigl(\frac{\hbar c}{G}\Bigr)^{3/2}\frac{1}{(\mu_em_p)^2}, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\hbar c}{G}\Bigr)^{3/2}\frac{1}{m_p^2} = 1.85\,M_\odot .
> $$
>
> As $M \to M_{\text{Ch}}$ the electrons become ultrarelativistic and the equilibrium radius shrinks to zero. The number $C$ depends on the density profile:
> - in the uniform-density energy estimate, $C = (5/4)^{3/2}(9\pi/4)^{1/2} = 3.72$, so $M_{\text{Ch}} = 1.72\,M_\odot$ for $\mu_e = 2$;
> - in hydrostatic equilibrium (the $n_p = 3$ polytrope), $C = \omega_3\sqrt{3\pi}/2 = 3.10$ with the Lane–Emden constant $\omega_3 = 2.018$, so $M_{\text{Ch}} = 5.74\,M_\odot/\mu_e^2 = 1.43\,M_\odot$ for $\mu_e = 2$ (with the atomic mass unit in place of $m_p$, $1.46\,M_\odot$; the often-quoted $1.44\,M_\odot$ and Schwartz's $1.41\,M_\odot$ differ only in constants and rounding).
>
> The mass combination is fixed by $\hbar$, $c$, $G$ and the nucleon mass alone; the electron mass does not appear.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 15, §3.4, eqs. (26)–(33); §4, eqs. (52)–(55); §5.3, eqs. (82)–(83) · Blundell §36.2, eqs. (36.13)–(36.19) · Schroeder §7.3, Problem 7.23(f)–(g)*

^thm-b13-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Energy argument.* For ultrarelativistic electrons ([[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^thm-b13-3-2|Theorem §B13.3.2]]) $E_{\text{kin}} = \tfrac34N\varepsilon_F = \tfrac34\hbar c\bigl(\tfrac{9\pi}{4}\bigr)^{1/3}N^{4/3}/R$, which falls as $1/R$, exactly like $E_{\text{grav}}$:
>
> $$
> E(R) = \frac{1}{R}\Bigl[\tfrac34\hbar c\bigl(\tfrac{9\pi}{4}\bigr)^{1/3}\Bigl(\frac{M}{\mu_em_p}\Bigr)^{4/3} - \tfrac35GM^2\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> If the bracket is positive, $E$ decreases as $R$ grows; the star expands until its electrons are no longer ultrarelativistic and settles at the radius of Theorem §B13.3.4 (corrected for relativity). If it is negative, $E$ decreases without bound as $R \to 0$ and the star collapses. The bracket vanishes at $M^{2/3} = \tfrac54\hbar c\,(9\pi/4)^{1/3}/G(\mu_em_p)^{4/3}$, which is the first value of $C$. Equivalently, with the full pressure of Theorem §B13.3.2 at the mean density, $dE/dR = 0$ reads $P = \tfrac35GM^2/4\pi R^4$; this equation gives the dashed curve in the figure, and its solutions exist only for $M < 1.72\,M_\odot$.
>
> *Hydrostatic equilibrium.* Eliminating $m$ between the two equations of Theorem §B13.3.3 gives $\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\bigl(\frac{r^2}{\rho}\frac{dP}{dr}\bigr) = -4\pi G\rho$. For a polytrope $P = K\rho^{1 + 1/n_p}$ substitute $\rho = \rho_c\theta^{n_p}$ and $r = r_0\xi$ with $r_0^2 = (n_p + 1)K\rho_c^{1/n_p - 1}/4\pi G$; the result is the **Lane–Emden equation**
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{\xi^2}\frac{d}{d\xi}\Bigl(\xi^2\frac{d\theta}{d\xi}\Bigr) = -\theta^{n_p}, \qquad \theta(0) = 1,\quad \theta'(0) = 0 .
> $$
>
> The surface is the first zero $\xi_1$ of $\theta$, and integrating the equation itself gives $M = 4\pi r_0^3\rho_c\int_0^{\xi_1}\theta^{n_p}\xi^2d\xi = 4\pi r_0^3\rho_c\,\omega_{n_p}$, $\omega_{n_p} = -\xi_1^2\theta'(\xi_1)$. Solved numerically: $\xi_1 = 3.654$, $\omega = 2.714$ for $n_p = \tfrac32$; $\xi_1 = 6.897$, $\omega = 2.018$ for $n_p = 3$. For $n_p = 3$, $r_0 = (K/\pi G)^{1/2}\rho_c^{-1/3}$, so $r_0^3\rho_c = (K/\pi G)^{3/2}$ and **the mass does not depend on $\rho_c$**: an $n_p = 3$ polytrope with given $K$ has one mass only, $M = 4\pi\omega_3(K/\pi G)^{3/2}$. With $K = \tfrac{\hbar c}{4}(3\pi^2)^{1/3}(\mu_em_p)^{-4/3}$ from Theorem §B13.3.2, $4\pi(3\pi^2)^{1/2}/8\pi^{3/2} = \sqrt{3\pi}/2$ gives the second value of $C$. A real white dwarf, with the full pressure, approaches this polytrope as $\rho_c \to \infty$, since its electrons then become ultrarelativistic almost everywhere; its mass rises toward $M_{\text{Ch}}$ and never exceeds it.

> [!remark] Remark: Why relativity is fatal
> Blundell's virial form makes the instability plain. A gas with $P = (\gamma - 1)u$ has total energy $E = (4 - 3\gamma)U$ in equilibrium (Theorem §B13.3.3). For $\gamma = \tfrac53$, $E = -U < 0$: the star is bound, and losing energy makes it contract and heat up, a negative heat capacity. For $\gamma = \tfrac43$, $E = 0$: the star is marginal, and nothing restores it after a squeeze. Equivalently, both the electron pressure and the gravitational pressure grow as $\rho^{4/3}$ in the ultrarelativistic limit, and if gravity wins at one density it wins at all. Blundell estimates the limit by asking when the nonrelativistic star of Theorem §B13.3.4 reaches $p_F = m_ec$, which gives $(3\sqrt\pi/2)(\hbar c/G)^{3/2}/(\mu_em_p)^2 \approx 1.2\,M_\odot$, the same combination of constants again.
>
> *Source: Blundell §35.1.3, Example 35.3; §36.2, eqs. (36.16)–(36.19)*

^rem-b13-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Schwartz's gravitational factor
> In Lecture 15, §5.2, Schwartz writes $E_{\text{grav}} = -D\,GM^2/R$ and computes $D_3 = 0.68$ for the $n_p = 3$ profile from $E_{\text{grav}} = -\tfrac{16\pi^2}{3}G\int\rho^2r^4\,dr$. That formula assumes $m(r) = \tfrac43\pi r^3\rho(r)$, which holds only for uniform density. For a polytrope of index $n_p$ the correct value is $D = 3/(5 - n_p)$, so $D_3 = \tfrac32$. He then balances the pressure at the mean density against $D\,GM^2/4\pi R^4$. That balance is consistent only with the uniform-density value $D = \tfrac35$, which gives $1.72\,M_\odot$. With $D_3 = 0.68$ it returns $1.43\,M_\odot$ (he quotes $1.41\,M_\odot$), which agrees with the Lane–Emden mass only by coincidence. The radii of his Figure 6 inherit the mismatch: for Sirius B ($1.02\,M_\odot$) he finds $0.54\,R_\oplus$, the hydrostatic model $0.85\,R_\oplus$. The Lane–Emden mass of his §4 is the reliable one.

## Neutron stars

> [!model] Model §B13.3.6: Neutron Star
> Above the Chandrasekhar mass the core of a massive star collapses, and at the densities reached electrons are captured by protons, $p + e^- \to n + \nu_e$; the neutrinos escape. A **neutron star** is modelled as a cold ideal Fermi gas of $N = M/m_n$ neutrons (spin ½) held together by Newtonian gravity, each fermion now carrying its own mass. Ignored: the remaining protons and electrons, the strong interaction between neutrons, rotation, and general relativity.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 15, §2, §6 · Blundell §36.3 · Schroeder §7.3, Problem 7.24*

^mod-b13-3-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.3.7: Size and Maximum Mass of a Neutron Star
> In Model §B13.3.6, with uniform density and nonrelativistic neutrons,
>
> $$
> R_{\text{NS}} = \Bigl(\frac{9\pi}{4}\Bigr)^{2/3}\frac{\hbar^2}{G\,m_n^{8/3}}\,M^{-1/3} \approx 12\ \mathrm{km}\,\Bigl(\frac{M}{M_\odot}\Bigr)^{-1/3} ,
> $$
>
> smaller than a white dwarf of the same mass by the factor $(m_e/m_n)(\mu_em_p/m_n)^{5/3} \approx 1/580$. The neutrons become relativistic, and the star unstable, near
>
> $$
> M_{\max} = C\,\Bigl(\frac{\hbar c}{G}\Bigr)^{3/2}\frac{1}{m_n^2} \approx \mu_e^2\,M_{\text{Ch}} \approx 5.7\,M_\odot
> $$
>
> (hydrostatic value of $C$; Schwartz $5.6\,M_\odot$, Blundell about $5\,M_\odot$). This free-gas estimate is not reliable: neutron stars are compact enough that general relativity matters, and dense enough that nuclear forces do.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 15, §6, eqs. (84)–(87) · Blundell §36.3, Example 36.4, eq. (36.22) · Schroeder §7.3, Problem 7.24*

^thm-b13-3-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In Theorems §B13.3.4 and §B13.3.5 the fermion mass enters only through the kinetic energy (as $1/m$ in the nonrelativistic case, not at all in the ultrarelativistic case), and the nucleon mass only through the number of fermions per unit mass, $N = M/\mu_em_p$. For neutrons both masses are $m_n$: replace $m_e \to m_n$ and $\mu_em_p \to m_n$. Then $R \propto 1/(m\,(\mu_em_p)^{5/3})$ gives the radius and the ratio, and $M_{\max} \propto (\mu_em_p)^{-2}$ is multiplied by $(\mu_em_p/m_n)^2 \approx \mu_e^2 = 4$.

> [!remark] Remark: Why neutrons, and what the estimate leaves out
> - *Neutronization.* A free neutron beta-decays, $n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu_e$ ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^def-a7-2-4|QM Def. §A7.2.4]]). The reverse, electron capture $p + e^- \to n + \nu_e$, needs $(m_n - m_p - m_e)c^2 = 0.78\ \mathrm{MeV}$ for free particles ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-4|QM Theorem §A7.2.4]]); in a dense star the electrons' Fermi energy supplies it, and the reverse wins. With the neutrinos escaping, equilibrium of the reaction requires $\mu_{\text n} = \mu_{\text p} + \mu_{e^-}$ ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]; these are chemical potentials, not the mass ratio $\mu_e$). The relativistic electrons' chemical potential $\mu_{e^-} \approx p_Fc$ climbs with density, and once it is large, turning electrons and protons into neutrons lowers the energy, so the gas becomes mostly neutrons with few electrons (Blundell §36.3; Schwartz §2).
> - *General relativity.* For $1.4\,M_\odot$ the Schwarzschild radius $2GM/c^2$ ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton#^def-a8-5-1|CM Def. §A8.5.1]]) is $4.1\ \mathrm{km}$, against $R \approx 11\ \mathrm{km}$: the ratio $2GM/Rc^2 \approx 0.4$ is far from small, so Newtonian gravity is only an estimate (Relativity C5, planned). Blundell notes that general relativity alone lowers the free-neutron limit to about $0.7\,M_\odot$, while a realistic nuclear equation of state raises it again to about 2–3 $M_\odot$; Schwartz quotes the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff estimate of about $3\,M_\odot$ and calls the precise value an open question. Heavier collapsed cores become black holes.
> - *A giant nucleus.* The mean density, $10^{17}$–$10^{18}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, is that of nuclear matter, $2\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$ ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^thm-a7-1-2|QM Theorem §A7.1.2]]): a white dwarf is like a huge piece of metal, a neutron star like a nucleus with $A \sim 10^{57}$ (Schwartz).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B13.3.1: A White Dwarf of One Solar Mass
> Take $M = M_\odot$ and $\mu_e = 2$ (carbon–oxygen).
> - *Nonrelativistic estimate* (Theorem §B13.3.4): $R = 7.2\times10^6\ \mathrm m = 1.1\,R_\oplus$, about the size of the Earth, and mean density $\rho = 1.3\times10^9\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, a million times that of water. The electron density is $n = \rho/2m_p = 3.9\times10^{35}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, so $\varepsilon_F = (\hbar^2/2m_e)(3\pi^2n)^{2/3} = 0.19\ \mathrm{MeV}$ and $T_F = 2.2\times10^9\ \mathrm K$. The interior temperature, around $10^7\ \mathrm K$, is two hundred times smaller: the $T = 0$ approximation is excellent.
> - *But not nonrelativistic.* $x = p_F/m_ec = \lambda_C(3\pi^2n)^{1/3} = 0.87$, and $\varepsilon_F$ is a third of $m_ec^2 = 0.511\ \mathrm{MeV}$: relativity cannot be neglected (Schroeder, Problem 7.23(g)). The hydrostatic model with the full pressure (figure) gives $R = 5.6\times10^3\ \mathrm{km} = 0.87\,R_\oplus$, with a central density of about $3\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, where $x \approx 2.6$.
> - *Sirius B*, the faint companion of the brightest star in the sky, has $M = 1.02\,M_\odot$ from its orbit (Schwartz, Schroeder, Blundell); the model gives $R \approx 0.85\,R_\oplus$. Most white dwarfs have about $0.5$–$0.6\,M_\odot$ (Schwartz), with $R \approx 1.4\,R_\oplus$ for $0.6\,M_\odot$: lighter white dwarfs are larger.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.3, Problem 7.23(d)–(e), (g) · Blundell §36.2, Exercise 36.2 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 15, §4–5*

^ex-b13-3-1

> [!example] Example §B13.3.2: A Neutron Star of Two Solar Masses
> For $M = 2M_\odot$, Theorem §B13.3.7 gives $R \approx 9.8\ \mathrm{km}$ and a mean density $\rho = 1.0\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, four times nuclear density. The neutron Fermi energy is $\varepsilon_F = p_F^2/2m_n = 140\ \mathrm{MeV}$ ($T_F = 1.6\times10^{12}\ \mathrm K$, so even the $5\times10^9\ \mathrm K$ of a collapsing core, Schwartz's figure, leaves it degenerate), and $p_F/m_nc = 0.55$: the neutrons are mildly relativistic, and the star is not far from the instability.
>
> *Fastest possible spin.* Matter at the equator stays bound only if gravity supplies the centripetal acceleration, $GM/R^2 \ge \omega^2R$, so the rotation period is at least
>
> $$
> \tau_{\min} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{R^3}{GM}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(9.8\times10^3\ \mathrm m)^3}{(6.67\times10^{-11})(3.98\times10^{30})\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}}} = 0.37\ \mathrm{ms} .
> $$
>
> Only an object this dense can turn hundreds of times a second; a body of the same mass but white-dwarf size (the nonrelativistic radius of Theorem §B13.3.4 with the Lane–Emden factor, about $7000\ \mathrm{km}$) would fly apart at periods below about $7\ \mathrm s$. The pulsar in the Crab Nebula, the remnant of the supernova recorded in 1054, rotates about 30 times per second (Blundell). Here $2GM/Rc^2 \approx 0.6$, so these numbers are estimates only.
>
> *Source: Blundell §36.3, Example 36.3, eq. (36.21), Exercise 36.3 · Schroeder §7.3, Problem 7.24*
