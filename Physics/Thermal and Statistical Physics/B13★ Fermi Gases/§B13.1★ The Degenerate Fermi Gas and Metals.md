What does a gas of fermions do when it is far too dense, or too cold, to be classical? The free-electron model of a metal ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^mod-a6-2-2|QM Model §A6.2.2]]) already gave the answer at $T = 0$: the electrons fill every state up to the Fermi energy ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^thm-a6-2-4|QM Theorem §A6.2.4]]), and only those near the top can take up heat, which is why metals obey Dulong and Petit ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-3|Theorem §A2.3.3]]) as if their electrons were not there. That level-A account quoted the electronic heat capacity with its coefficient but could justify it only up to a numerical factor ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^thm-a6-2-5|QM Theorem §A6.2.5]]). Here the same gas is treated with the Fermi–Dirac distribution ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]) at $T > 0$. Three things are new: the pressure and stiffness of the gas at $T = 0$ (degeneracy pressure), the Sommerfeld expansion, which yields $\mu(T)$ and the heat capacity with their coefficients, and the comparison with metals. The same degeneracy pressure holds up white dwarfs ([[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars]]).

*Notation.* $U$ is the internal energy (level A's $E_{\text{int}}$), $\varepsilon$ a single-particle energy, $f(\varepsilon) = 1/\bigl(e^{(\varepsilon - \mu)/kT} + 1\bigr)$ the Fermi–Dirac occupation, $g(\varepsilon)$ the density of states (number of single-particle states per unit energy, both spin states counted), $n = N/V$, and $k = k_B$. Pressure is written $P$ in this chapter (the other notes write $p$), because $p$ is needed for momentum. The quantum notes write $E$, $E_F$ and $F(E)$ for $\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_F$ and $f$.

## The Fermi gas at zero temperature

> [!model] Model §B13.1.1: Ideal Fermi Gas (Free-Electron Gas)
> $N$ identical spin-½ fermions of mass $m$ move freely in a box of volume $V$, with single-particle energies $\varepsilon = p^2/2m$, and do not interact. Each orbital state holds at most one fermion per spin state ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|QM Principle §A5.3.2]]). In equilibrium at temperature $T$ and chemical potential $\mu$ the states are occupied independently, with mean occupation $f(\varepsilon)$ ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]), and the density of states is
>
> $$
> g(\varepsilon) = \frac{V}{2\pi^2}\Bigl(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}\sqrt\varepsilon = \frac{3N}{2\varepsilon_F^{3/2}}\sqrt\varepsilon \qquad (\varepsilon > 0),
> $$
>
> ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^thm-a6-2-3|QM Theorem §A6.2.3]], [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-4|Theorem §B10.1.4]]), with $\varepsilon_F$ as in Def. §B13.1.1. $N$ and $U$ follow from
>
> $$
> N = \int_0^\infty g(\varepsilon)f(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon, \qquad U = \int_0^\infty \varepsilon\,g(\varepsilon)f(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon ;
> $$
>
> at fixed $N$ and $T$ the first equation fixes $\mu$.
>
> Applied to the conduction electrons of a metal, this is **Sommerfeld's free-electron model**, the model of [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^mod-a6-2-2|QM Model §A6.2.2]], now at any temperature and with the occupation fixed by the grand canonical ensemble. *Assumptions:* the attraction of the ions and the repulsion between electrons are both ignored, and so are the effects of the lattice (bands, [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-1|QM Theorem §A6.3.1]]); the sum over states is replaced by an integral. Schwartz argues that the neglect is less crazy than it looks: the exclusion principle keeps electrons apart, and the inner electrons screen the nuclear charge; interactions mainly replace $m$ by an effective mass (Fermi-liquid theory).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 13, §2–3, eqs. (5)–(11), (24) · Schroeder §7.3, eqs. (7.51)–(7.54) · Blundell §30.1–30.2, eqs. (30.4)–(30.10)*

^mod-b13-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B13.1.1: Fermi Energy, Fermi Wave Number, Fermi Temperature; Degenerate Gas
> - The **Fermi energy** is the chemical potential at zero temperature, $\varepsilon_F = \mu(T = 0)$. At $T = 0$ the occupation is the step $f(\varepsilon) = \theta(\varepsilon_F - \varepsilon)$: all states below $\varepsilon_F$ are filled, all above are empty. This agrees with the level-A definition as the top of the filled levels ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^def-a6-2-3|QM Def. §A6.2.3]]).
> - The **Fermi wave number** $k_F$ and **Fermi momentum** $p_F = \hbar k_F$ are those of a particle at $\varepsilon_F$; in the free gas $\varepsilon_F = \hbar^2k_F^2/2m$. The set of momenta with $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_F$ is the **Fermi surface** (a sphere for the free gas).
> - The **Fermi temperature** is $T_F = \varepsilon_F/k$, as in QM Def. §A6.2.3.
> - The gas is **degenerate** when $T \ll T_F$: nearly every state well below $\varepsilon_F$ is full and nearly every state well above is empty. For the free gas $n/2n_Q = \tfrac{4}{3\sqrt\pi}(T_F/T)^{3/2}$ (with $n_Q$ of [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]]), so $T \ll T_F$ is the degenerate regime $n \gg 2n_Q$ of [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-1|Def. §B10.2.1]], the opposite of the classical condition. The filled states deep below $\varepsilon_F$ are the **Fermi sea**.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 13, §1, eq. (4) · Schroeder §7.3, eqs. (7.33), (7.43) · Blundell §30.2, eqs. (30.21)–(30.24)*

^def-b13-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.1.2: Ground State of the Ideal Fermi Gas
> At $T = 0$ the gas of Model §B13.1.1 has
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\bigl(3\pi^2 n\bigr)^{2/3}, \qquad k_F = \bigl(3\pi^2n\bigr)^{1/3}, \qquad U_0 = \tfrac35 N\varepsilon_F ,
> $$
>
> a **degeneracy pressure** and a bulk modulus $B = -V(\partial P/\partial V)$
>
> $$
> P = \frac23\,\frac{U_0}{V} = \frac25\,n\varepsilon_F, \qquad B = \frac{10}{9}\,\frac{U_0}{V} = \frac23\,n\varepsilon_F = \frac53\,P .
> $$
>
> Both are nonzero at $T = 0$, where a classical gas has neither, and both depend only on the density.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 13, eqs. (10), (13), (14), (18) · Schroeder §7.3, eqs. (7.39), (7.42)–(7.45) · Blundell §30.2, eqs. (30.29)–(30.32), Exercise 30.2*

^thm-b13-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At $T = 0$, $N = \int_0^{\varepsilon_F} g\,d\varepsilon$ and $U_0 = \int_0^{\varepsilon_F}\varepsilon g\,d\varepsilon$; with $g \propto \sqrt\varepsilon$ these give $\varepsilon_F$ and $U_0/N = \tfrac35\varepsilon_F$, as in [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^der-a6-2-4|QM Derivation §A6.2.4]]. For the pressure use the thermodynamic identity $dU = T\,dS - P\,dV + \mu\,dN$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]), so that $P = -(\partial U/\partial V)_{S,N}$. The ground state is a single microstate, so $S = k\ln 1 = 0$ ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]]), and it stays $0$ under slow compression at $T = 0$: the derivative at fixed $S$ is the derivative of $U_0(V)$ at fixed $N$. Since $\varepsilon_F \propto V^{-2/3}$, $U_0 = \tfrac35 N\varepsilon_F \propto V^{-2/3}$, and
>
> $$
> P = -\frac{dU_0}{dV} = \frac23\,\frac{U_0}{V} = \frac25\,\frac{N}{V}\,\varepsilon_F, \qquad B = -V\frac{dP}{dV} = V\,\frac{d^2U_0}{dV^2} = \frac23\cdot\frac53\,\frac{U_0}{V} = \frac{10}{9}\,\frac{U_0}{V} .
> $$
>
> With $U_0/V = \tfrac35 n\varepsilon_F$ this is $B = \tfrac23 n\varepsilon_F = \tfrac53 P$. At $T = 0$ the adiabatic and isothermal moduli coincide.

> [!remark] Remark: What degeneracy pressure is
> For a classical gas, pressure is the momentum delivered by thermally moving molecules ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]]) and vanishes at $T = 0$. In a Fermi gas, compressing the box shortens every wavelength, so every single-particle energy rises as $V^{-2/3}$. A Bose or classical gas can move particles into lower levels and keep its energy; a degenerate Fermi gas cannot, since all the lower levels are taken, so its energy simply rises and it pushes back even at $T = 0$. Degeneracy pressure owes nothing to the electrostatic repulsion of the electrons, which the model ignores: it comes from the exclusion principle alone. In a metal it is of order $10^5$ atm (Example §B13.1.1) and is balanced by the attraction of the ions, so it cannot be measured directly. The bulk modulus, which sets how hard the metal resists further compression and hence the speed of sound $c_s = \sqrt{B/\rho}$, can be.

^rem-b13-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: The same law as kinetic theory
> $P = \tfrac23 U/V$ is the level-A kinetic-theory result $pV = \tfrac23 E_{\text{int}}$ ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]], [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]]). It holds for every nonrelativistic ideal gas, classical or quantum, at every temperature ([[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]]); the derivation above is its $T = 0$ case. What changes in the degenerate gas is $U$, which no longer vanishes with $T$. For ultrarelativistic particles, with $\varepsilon \propto p$, the same theorem gives $P = \tfrac13 U/V$ (photons: [[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics]]; electrons in white dwarfs: [[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars#^thm-b13-3-2|Theorem §B13.3.2]]).

## Low temperatures: the Sommerfeld expansion

At $T > 0$ the integrals for $N$ and $U$ are Fermi integrals, $-\Gamma(s)\mathrm{Li}_s(-z)$ with $z = e^{\mu/kT}$ ([[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]], [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]]), but they cannot be inverted for $\mu$ in closed form. For $kT \ll \varepsilon_F$ they can be expanded instead.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.1.3: Sommerfeld Expansion
> Let $\varphi(\varepsilon)$, defined for $\varepsilon \ge 0$, be smooth near $\varepsilon = \mu$ and grow no faster than a power of $\varepsilon$, and let $kT \ll \mu$. Then
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty \varphi(\varepsilon)\,f(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon = \int_0^\mu \varphi(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon + \frac{\pi^2}{6}(kT)^2\,\varphi'(\mu) + \frac{7\pi^4}{360}(kT)^4\,\varphi'''(\mu) + \cdots ,
> $$
>
> up to corrections of order $e^{-\mu/kT}$, which are smaller than every power of $kT/\mu$. The general coefficient of $(kT)^s\varphi^{(s-1)}(\mu)$, $s$ even, is $2(1 - 2^{1-s})\zeta(s)$; odd powers do not occur. The step function at $T = 0$ gives the first term; temperature enters only through derivatives of $\varphi$ at $\mu$. For $\varphi \propto \varepsilon^{s-1}$ the left side is a Fermi integral of [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]]; what is new here is its asymptotic form for $\mu \gg kT$ (fugacity $z \gg 1$), for any smooth $\varphi$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §30.2, Example 30.4, eqs. (30.33)–(30.38) · Schroeder §7.3, eqs. (7.56)–(7.64) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 13, §3.1, eqs. (25)–(29)*

^thm-b13-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Integrate by parts.* Let $\psi(\varepsilon) = \int_0^\varepsilon\varphi(\varepsilon')\,d\varepsilon'$. Then ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]], on $[0, L]$ with $L \to \infty$)
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty\varphi f\,d\varepsilon = \bigl[\psi f\bigr]_0^\infty + \int_0^\infty\psi(\varepsilon)\,\bigl(-f'(\varepsilon)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon = \int_0^\infty\psi(\varepsilon)\,\bigl(-f'(\varepsilon)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon ,
> $$
>
> since $\psi(0) = 0$ and $f$ dies exponentially while $\psi$ grows only as a power.
>
> *The kernel.* With $x = (\varepsilon - \mu)/kT$,
>
> $$
> -f'(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon = \frac{e^x}{(e^x + 1)^2}\,dx = \frac{dx}{2 + 2\cosh x} ,
> $$
>
> an even, positive function of $x$ with total integral $f(-\infty) - f(\infty) = 1$, concentrated within a few $kT$ of $\mu$. The lower limit $x = -\mu/kT$ may be moved to $-\infty$: the added piece is of order $e^{-\mu/kT}$.
>
> *Taylor-expand $\psi$ about $\mu$* ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]): $\psi(\varepsilon) = \sum_s \psi^{(s)}(\mu)\,(kT\,x)^s/s!$, and integrate term by term against the kernel. The moments $I_s = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x^s e^x(e^x + 1)^{-2}\,dx$ vanish for odd $s$ because the kernel is even. For even $s \ge 2$, write $e^x(e^x+1)^{-2} = -\frac{d}{dx}(e^x + 1)^{-1}$ on $x > 0$ and integrate by parts (the boundary terms vanish); the Fermi integral of [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]] at $z = 1$ then gives
>
> $$
> I_s = 2s\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{s-1}\,dx}{e^x + 1} = 2s\bigl(1 - 2^{1-s}\bigr)\Gamma(s)\zeta(s) = 2\,s!\,\bigl(1 - 2^{1-s}\bigr)\zeta(s), \qquad I_0 = 1,\quad I_2 = \frac{\pi^2}{3},\quad I_4 = \frac{7\pi^4}{15},
> $$
>
> using $s\,\Gamma(s) = s!$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]) and $\zeta(2) = \pi^2/6$, $\zeta(4) = \pi^4/90$ ([[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-2|Def. §B10.2.2]]). Hence
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty\varphi f\,d\varepsilon = \psi(\mu) + \frac{I_2}{2!}(kT)^2\psi''(\mu) + \frac{I_4}{4!}(kT)^4\psi''''(\mu) + \cdots = \int_0^\mu\varphi\,d\varepsilon + \frac{\pi^2}{6}(kT)^2\varphi'(\mu) + \frac{7\pi^4}{360}(kT)^4\varphi'''(\mu) + \cdots .
> $$
>
> Integrating the Taylor series of $\psi$ term by term against the kernel is where the expansion becomes asymptotic rather than convergent (Caution below); the moments themselves are convergent improper integrals ([[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-2|451 Def. §36.2]]).

*Uses:* [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]], [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]], [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-2|Def. §B10.2.2]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-2|451 Def. §36.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: An asymptotic series that needs a smooth density of states
> - The expansion is asymptotic in $kT/\mu$, not a convergent series, and it misses terms like $e^{-\mu/kT}$ entirely. When all derivatives of $\varphi$ vanish, it predicts no temperature dependence at all, while the exact answer has an exponentially small one (Example §B13.1.3).
> - It needs $\varphi$ smooth across the window $\mu \pm$ a few $kT$. It fails when $\mu$ lies in a gap of $g(\varepsilon)$, as in a semiconductor: there the right tool is the opposite limit, Boltzmann tails of $f$ on both sides of the gap ([[§B13.2★ Semiconductors]]).
> - Schwartz and Schroeder carry out these steps for the particular functions $\sqrt\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon^{3/2}$ of the free gas, Blundell for a general $\varphi$; it is one calculation. Schwartz's electron–hole version (Remark: Electrons and holes) is a shortcut that keeps only the $T^2$ term.

^cau-b13-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.1.4: The Fermi Gas at Low Temperature
> For fermions with any smooth density of states $g(\varepsilon)$, to order $T^2$ at fixed $N$ and $V$,
>
> $$
> \mu(T) = \varepsilon_F - \frac{\pi^2}{6}(kT)^2\,\frac{g'(\varepsilon_F)}{g(\varepsilon_F)}, \qquad U(T) = U_0 + \frac{\pi^2}{6}\,g(\varepsilon_F)\,(kT)^2, \qquad C_V = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\,g(\varepsilon_F)\,k^2T .
> $$
>
> For the free gas ($g \propto \sqrt\varepsilon$, $g(\varepsilon_F) = 3N/2\varepsilon_F$):
>
> $$
> \mu = \varepsilon_F\Bigl[1 - \frac{\pi^2}{12}\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_F}\Bigr)^2\Bigr], \qquad U = \frac35 N\varepsilon_F\Bigl[1 + \frac{5\pi^2}{12}\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_F}\Bigr)^2\Bigr], \qquad C_V = \frac{\pi^2}{2}\,Nk\,\frac{T}{T_F} .
> $$
>
> The heat capacity is linear in $T$ and smaller than the classical $\tfrac32 Nk$ by a factor of order $T/T_F$; the next corrections are of relative order $(T/T_F)^2$ (no $T^3$ term in $\mu$ or $U$). For one mole of a metal with one conduction electron per atom ($Nk = R$) the last formula is $C_{V,\text{el}} = \tfrac{\pi^2}{2}R\,T/T_F$, the result that [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^thm-a6-2-5|QM Theorem §A6.2.5]] quotes and derives only up to its numerical factor; here it is proved, with its range of validity $T \ll T_F$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 13, eqs. (30), (34), (43) · Schroeder §7.3, eqs. (7.66)–(7.68), (7.47)–(7.48), Problems 7.29–7.30 · Blundell §30.2, eqs. (30.39)–(30.42), Exercise 30.3*

^thm-b13-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Chemical potential.* Apply Theorem §B13.1.3 with $\varphi = g$ and compare with $T = 0$, where $N = \int_0^{\varepsilon_F}g\,d\varepsilon$:
>
> $$
> \int_0^{\varepsilon_F}g\,d\varepsilon = \int_0^{\mu}g\,d\varepsilon + \frac{\pi^2}{6}(kT)^2g'(\mu) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (\mu - \varepsilon_F)\,g(\varepsilon_F) + \frac{\pi^2}{6}(kT)^2g'(\varepsilon_F) = 0
> $$
>
> to order $T^2$ (since $\mu - \varepsilon_F$ is itself of order $T^2$, $\int_{\varepsilon_F}^\mu g = (\mu - \varepsilon_F)g(\varepsilon_F) + O(T^4)$, and $g'(\mu) = g'(\varepsilon_F) + O(T^2)$). This is the formula for $\mu$.
>
> *Energy.* With $\varphi = \varepsilon g$, $\varphi' = g + \varepsilon g'$:
>
> $$
> U = \int_0^\mu\varepsilon g\,d\varepsilon + \frac{\pi^2}{6}(kT)^2\bigl[g(\varepsilon_F) + \varepsilon_Fg'(\varepsilon_F)\bigr] = U_0 + \varepsilon_F\,g(\varepsilon_F)(\mu - \varepsilon_F) + \frac{\pi^2}{6}(kT)^2\bigl[g + \varepsilon_Fg'\bigr] .
> $$
>
> By the first result $\varepsilon_F g(\varepsilon_F)(\mu - \varepsilon_F) = -\tfrac{\pi^2}{6}(kT)^2\varepsilon_F g'(\varepsilon_F)$, which cancels the $g'$ term: $U = U_0 + \tfrac{\pi^2}{6}g(\varepsilon_F)(kT)^2$. Then $C_V = (\partial U/\partial T)_{V,N} = \tfrac{\pi^2}{3}g(\varepsilon_F)k^2T$.
>
> *Free gas.* $g'/g = 1/(2\varepsilon)$ gives $\mu = \varepsilon_F - \tfrac{\pi^2}{12}(kT)^2/\varepsilon_F$, and $g(\varepsilon_F) = 3N/2\varepsilon_F$ gives $U - U_0 = \tfrac{\pi^2}{4}N(kT)^2/\varepsilon_F = \tfrac35 N\varepsilon_F\cdot\tfrac{5\pi^2}{12}(kT/\varepsilon_F)^2$ and $C_V = \tfrac{\pi^2}{2}Nk^2T/\varepsilon_F$. The $T^4$ terms come from the $\varphi'''$ term of Theorem §B13.1.3 and from expanding $\mu$ one order further; no odd powers can appear, because Theorem §B13.1.3 has none.

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the result
> - *Why $\mu$ falls.* $f$ is symmetric about $\mu$: $f(\mu + \Delta) = 1 - f(\mu - \Delta)$. If $\mu$ stayed at $\varepsilon_F$, warming would add more electrons above $\varepsilon_F$ (where $g$ is larger) than it removes below, and $N$ would grow; $\mu$ must drop to compensate, by an amount set by the slope $g'$ (Schroeder). For copper the shift is tiny: $\mu = 0.99977\,\varepsilon_F$ even at the melting point (Example §B13.1.1), which is why $\mu$ and $\varepsilon_F$ are used interchangeably for metals.
> - *Why $C_V \propto T$.* Only the electrons within about $kT$ of $\varepsilon_F$, some $g(\varepsilon_F)kT$ of them, can be excited, and each gains about $kT$, so $U - U_0 \sim g(\varepsilon_F)(kT)^2$; dimensional analysis alone gives $N(kT)^2/\varepsilon_F$ (Schwartz, Schroeder). The Sommerfeld expansion supplies the coefficient $\pi^2/4$ that level A could only quote ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^der-a6-2-5|QM Derivation §A6.2.5]]): QM Theorem §A6.2.5 is now a theorem with a proof. For phonons, by contrast, $g \propto \varepsilon^2$ and every mode with $\varepsilon \lesssim kT$ is excitable, which gives $U \propto T^4$ and the Debye $T^3$ law ([[§B11.2★ Phonons and the Debye Model#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]]).
> - *Dulong and Petit.* Equipartition would give each conduction electron $\tfrac32 kT$ and a metal $\tfrac92 R$ per mole instead of the observed $3R$ ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^rem-a6-2-3|QM Remark: Contrast with kinetic theory]]). Fermi–Dirac statistics reduce the electronic part by $\tfrac{\pi^2}{3}T/T_F \approx 0.01$ at room temperature, which resolves the puzzle.
> - *Third law.* The entropy is $S = \int_0^T (C_V/T')\,dT' = \tfrac{\pi^2}{2}Nk\,T/T_F$, which vanishes as $T \to 0$ ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-3|Principle §A4.6.3]]), together with $C_V$ ([[§B2.3 The Third Law#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]]): the degenerate gas has a unique ground state.

^rem-b13-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Electrons and holes
> Schwartz's shortcut measures energies from $\mu$. An electron excited to $\varepsilon = \mu + \Delta$ is found with probability $f(\mu + \Delta)$; a **hole**, an empty state at $\mu - \Delta$, with probability $1 - f(\mu - \Delta) = f(\mu + \Delta)$. Electron and hole excitations therefore have the same distribution, $1/(e^{\Delta/kT} + 1)$, and each contributes $+\Delta$ to the energy. Near $\mu$, $g \approx g(\varepsilon_F)$, so each kind contributes
>
> $$
> g(\varepsilon_F)\int_0^\infty\frac{\Delta\,d\Delta}{e^{\Delta/kT} + 1} = g(\varepsilon_F)\,\frac{\pi^2}{12}(kT)^2
> $$
>
> (the Fermi integral of [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]] with $s = 2$, $z = 1$), and the two together give $U - U_0 = \tfrac{\pi^2}{6}g(\varepsilon_F)(kT)^2$, as in Theorem §B13.1.4. Extending the hole integral to $\Delta = \infty$ (holes cannot lie below $\varepsilon = 0$) is the same exponentially small error as moving the lower limit in Theorem §B13.1.3. A degenerate Fermi gas at low $T$ thus behaves as a dilute gas of electron and hole excitations on top of an inert Fermi sea; holes return as charge carriers in semiconductors ([[§B13.2★ Semiconductors]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 13, §3.2, eqs. (35)–(42)*

^rem-b13-1-4

## Pauli paramagnetism

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.1.5: Pauli Paramagnetism
> Give each electron of the free gas the magnetic moment $\pm\mu_B$ along a field $B$ in the $z$ direction, so that its energy is $\varepsilon \mp \mu_B B$, and take $\mu_B B \ll kT \ll \varepsilon_F$ (orbital motion is ignored). The magnetization $M = \mu_B(N_\uparrow - N_\downarrow)$ is
>
> $$
> M = \mu_B^2\,g(\varepsilon_F)\,B\Bigl[1 - \frac{\pi^2}{12}\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_F}\Bigr)^2\Bigr] = \frac{3N\mu_B^2}{2\varepsilon_F}\,B\Bigl[1 - \frac{\pi^2}{12}\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_F}\Bigr)^2\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> to first order in $B$; at $T = 0$ the bracket is $1$ and only $\mu_B B \ll \varepsilon_F$ is needed. To leading order $M$ does not depend on temperature.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.3, Problem 7.36*

^thm-b13-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each spin direction has density of states $g/2$ in the kinetic energy $\varepsilon$. A moment along $B$ has total energy $\varepsilon - \mu_B B$, one against it $\varepsilon + \mu_B B$, so
>
> $$
> N_\uparrow - N_\downarrow = \int_0^\infty\frac{g(\varepsilon)}{2}\bigl[f(\varepsilon - \mu_BB) - f(\varepsilon + \mu_BB)\bigr]d\varepsilon = \mu_BB\int_0^\infty g(\varepsilon)\bigl(-f'(\varepsilon)\bigr)d\varepsilon + O(B^3).
> $$
>
> $N_\uparrow + N_\downarrow$ changes only at order $B^2$, so $\mu$ is that of Theorem §B13.1.4. The remaining integral is $\partial_\mu\int_0^\infty gf\,d\varepsilon$, which by Theorem §B13.1.3 is $g(\mu) + \tfrac{\pi^2}{6}(kT)^2g''(\mu)$. For $g \propto \sqrt\varepsilon$, $g''/g = -1/(4\varepsilon^2)$, and $g(\mu) = g(\varepsilon_F)(\mu/\varepsilon_F)^{1/2} = g(\varepsilon_F)\bigl[1 - \tfrac{\pi^2}{24}(kT/\varepsilon_F)^2\bigr]$ by Theorem §B13.1.4. The two corrections of $-\tfrac{\pi^2}{24}(kT/\varepsilon_F)^2$ add to $-\tfrac{\pi^2}{12}(kT/\varepsilon_F)^2$. At $T = 0$, $-f'$ is the delta function at $\varepsilon_F$, and the integral is $g(\varepsilon_F)$ exactly.

^der-b13-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-3|Theorem §B13.1.3]], [[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-4|Theorem §B13.1.4]], [[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^mod-b13-1-1|Model §B13.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why the degenerate gas is a weak paramagnet
> $N$ independent moments $\pm\mu_B$ obey Curie's law $M = N\mu_B^2B/kT$ for $\mu_B B \ll kT$ (independent moments in the Boltzmann distribution, [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]]; Curie's law, [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^law-b6-2-10|Law §B6.2.10]]). The Fermi gas gives $M_{\text{Pauli}}/M_{\text{Curie}} = \tfrac32\,T/T_F$, about $0.005$ for copper at room temperature. An electron deep in the Fermi sea cannot flip its spin, because the state of opposite spin and equal energy is already filled; only the fraction $\sim kT/\varepsilon_F$ near the Fermi surface can respond, the same fraction that carries the heat capacity (Schroeder, Problem 7.36(a)).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B13.1.1: Degeneracy Pressure and Stiffness of Metals
> Take one conduction electron per atom for the alkali and noble metals below (densities $n$ from Blundell's Table 30.1). Theorem §B13.1.2 gives
>
> | metal | $n$ ($10^{28}\ \mathrm m^{-3}$) | $\varepsilon_F$ (eV) | $T_F$ ($10^4$ K) | $P = \tfrac25 n\varepsilon_F$ (GPa) | $B = \tfrac23 n\varepsilon_F$ (GPa) | measured $B$ (GPa) |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | Li | 4.70 | 4.75 | 5.5 | 14 | 23.8 | 11.1 |
> | Na | 2.65 | 3.24 | 3.8 | 5.5 | 9.2 | 6.3 |
> | K | 1.40 | 2.12 | 2.5 | 1.9 | 3.2 | 3.1 |
> | Cu | 8.47 | 7.03 | 8.2 | 38 | 64 | 138 |
> | Ag | 5.86 | 5.50 | 6.4 | 21 | 34 | 104 |
>
> For copper, $\varepsilon_F = (\hbar^2/2m_e)(3\pi^2\cdot8.47\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}})^{2/3} = 1.12\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm J = 7.0\ \mathrm{eV}$, far below $m_ec^2 = 511\ \mathrm{keV}$ (nonrelativistic) and far above $kT = 0.026\ \mathrm{eV}$ (degenerate, $T_F/T \approx 270$ at room temperature). At the melting point, $1358\ \mathrm K$, Theorem §B13.1.4 gives $\mu = \varepsilon_F\bigl[1 - \tfrac{\pi^2}{12}(1358/81\,600)^2\bigr] = 0.99977\,\varepsilon_F$, the same as the numerical solution of $N = \int gf$. The degeneracy pressure, $3.8\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{Pa} \approx 4\times10^5$ atm, is held in by the attraction of the ions.
>
> The bulk modulus is the testable number. For potassium the free-electron value $3.2\ \mathrm{GPa}$ is within 3% of experiment, and with $\rho = 856\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$ it predicts a speed of sound $c_s = \sqrt{B/\rho} \approx 1.9\times10^3\ \mathrm{m/s}$ against a measured $2.0\times10^3\ \mathrm{m/s}$: the springiness of potassium is the exclusion principle at work. For the other alkali metals the model is within a factor of about 2, but for copper and silver it is 2–3 times too soft. The free gas gets the order of magnitude right everywhere and the number right only for the metals best described by one loosely bound electron per atom.
>
> *Source: Blundell §30.2, Table 30.1, eq. (30.32) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 13, §3, eqs. (15)–(18) · Schroeder §7.3, Problem 7.19*

> [!example] Example §B13.1.2: The Heat Capacity of Copper at Low Temperature
> With $T_F = 8.16\times10^4\ \mathrm K$, the electrons of one mole of copper contribute $C_{\text{el}} = \tfrac{\pi^2}{2}R\,T/T_F$. At $300\ \mathrm K$ this is $0.018\,R$, against $3R$ from the lattice ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-3|Theorem §A2.3.3]]): a ratio $\tfrac{\pi^2}{6}T/T_F = 0.006$, invisible in a measurement of $C$.
>
> At low $T$ the lattice part falls as $T^3$ (Debye, [[§B11.2★ Phonons and the Debye Model#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]]) while the electronic part falls only as $T$, so the electrons win in the end. Per mole,
>
> $$
> \frac{C}{T} = \gamma + A T^2, \qquad \gamma = \frac{\pi^2R}{2T_F} = 0.50\ \mathrm{mJ\,mol^{-1}K^{-2}}, \qquad A = \frac{12\pi^4R}{5T_D^3} = 6.2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-4}}
> $$
>
> with $T_D = 315\ \mathrm K$. Plotted against $T^2$, the data fall on a straight line: the intercept measures the electronic coefficient $\gamma$, the slope the Debye temperature. The two terms are equal at
>
> $$
> T_\ast = \sqrt{\gamma/A} = \sqrt{\frac{5\,T_D^3}{24\pi^2\,T_F}} = 2.8\ \mathrm K .
> $$
>
> Schwartz's estimate $T_\ast \sim \sqrt{T_D^3/T_F} \approx 19\ \mathrm K$ drops the numerical factors, and they matter here: $\sqrt{5/24\pi^2} = 0.15$. The measured $\gamma$ of copper is about 40% larger than the free-electron value (Schroeder). The linear law itself is confirmed; its coefficient is what interactions and the lattice modify, through an effective mass in place of $m_e$ (Schwartz, Lecture 13, footnote 1).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 13, §3.3, eqs. (43)–(49) · Schroeder §7.3, eq. (7.48), Problem 7.25*

> [!example] Example §B13.1.3: The Two-Dimensional Fermi Gas
> Fermions of spin ½ confined to an area $A$ (electrons in a thin layer, say) have $\varepsilon = p^2/2m$ with $\vec p$ in a plane. Counting states in a disc instead of a sphere gives a constant density of states, $g(\varepsilon) = g_0 = mA/\pi\hbar^2$, so $\varepsilon_F = N/g_0 = \pi\hbar^2N/mA$ and the mean energy at $T = 0$ is $\varepsilon_F/2$.
>
> Now $N = g_0\int_0^\infty d\varepsilon/(e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/kT} + 1) = g_0kT\ln\bigl(1 + e^{\mu/kT}\bigr)$ can be inverted exactly:
>
> $$
> \mu = kT\ln\bigl(e^{\varepsilon_F/kT} - 1\bigr) = \varepsilon_F + kT\ln\bigl(1 - e^{-T_F/T}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> - For $T \ll T_F$, $\mu \approx \varepsilon_F - kT\,e^{-T_F/T}$. Theorem §B13.1.4 predicts no shift at all, since $g' = 0$, and indeed every power of $T$ in the Sommerfeld series vanishes; the true shift is smaller than any power of $T/T_F$ (Caution above). The heat capacity, $C = \tfrac{\pi^2}{3}g_0k^2T = \tfrac{\pi^2}{3}Nk\,T/T_F$, is still linear in $T$.
> - For $T \gg T_F$, $e^{\mu/kT} \approx \varepsilon_F/kT = N\lambda_{\text{th}}^2/2A \ll 1$ with $\lambda_{\text{th}} = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mkT}$: $\mu$ is large and negative, the chemical potential of a classical ideal gas with two spin states per particle ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]]; [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.3, Problems 7.28 and 7.31*
