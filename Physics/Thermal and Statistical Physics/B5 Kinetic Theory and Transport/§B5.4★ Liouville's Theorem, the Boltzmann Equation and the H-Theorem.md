Gases left alone reach the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution ([[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion|§B5.1]]); heat flows from hot to cold and never back ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]]). Yet the molecules obey Newton's laws, which run equally well backwards. This section asks how the one can come from the other. Its mechanical starting point is Liouville's theorem, proved in Classical Mechanics ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-3|CM Theorem §B7.2.3]], [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-4|CM Theorem §B7.2.4]]) and not repeated here; what is added is its statistical use: the density of an ensemble, why its exact (fine-grained) entropy can never grow, and how coarse-graining, chaos and molecular chaos let it grow after all. The kinetic description follows — Boltzmann's equation for the one-molecule distribution, and its relaxation-time form, from which Fick's law is derived a third time — and then the H-theorem, in the master-equation form in which Schwartz proves it, with the objections of Loschmidt and Zermelo and their answers.

## Ensembles in phase space

> [!definition] Definition §B5.4.1: Ensemble Density; One-Particle Distribution Function
> For a system of $N$ molecules, an **ensemble** is a probability density $\rho(q, p, t)$ on the $6N$-dimensional phase space ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^def-b7-2-1|CM Def. §B7.2.1]]), $\int\rho\,d\Gamma = 1$ with $d\Gamma = d^{3N}q\,d^{3N}p$; it expresses what is known about a system whose exact microstate is not. Averages of observables are $\langle A\rangle = \int A\,\rho\,d\Gamma$. Integrating out all molecules but one gives the **one-particle distribution function** $f(\vec r, \vec v, t)$: $f\,d^3r\,d^3v$ is the expected number of molecules in $d^3r$ around $\vec r$ with velocity in $d^3v$ around $\vec v$, so that $n(\vec r, t) = \int f\,d^3v$. In equilibrium $f = n\,g(\vec v)$ with the Maxwell–Boltzmann $g$ of [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §3.2, eqs. (15)–(16)*

^def-b5-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.4.1: Stationary Ensembles; Constancy of the Fine-Grained Entropy
> Let $\rho$ evolve under a time-independent Hamiltonian $H$ by Liouville's equation $\partial_t\rho + \{\rho, H\} = 0$ ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-4|CM Theorem §B7.2.4]]).
> 1. Any $\rho = F(H)$ is stationary (noted in [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^cau-b7-2-1|CM Caution: Constant along the flow, not at a fixed point]]); in particular the **microcanonical** ensemble, uniform on the energy shell $E \le H \le E + \delta E$ and zero elsewhere, does not change in time.
> 2. For every smooth $\Phi$ with $\Phi(0) = 0$, $\int\Phi(\rho)\,d\Gamma$ is constant in time. In particular the **fine-grained Gibbs entropy**
>
> $$
> S_{\text{fg}} = -k_B\int\rho\ln\rho\,d\Gamma
> $$
>
> never changes: exact Hamiltonian evolution cannot make an ensemble approach equilibrium in this sense.
>
> (Assumed: the phase-space flux of $\Phi(\rho)$ through the boundary of the accessible region vanishes — molecules confined in a box, momenta bounded by the energy.)
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §3.2 (fragments keep their total volume), §4 (with exact knowledge $H$ stays constant) · CM §B7.2, Caution: Constant along the flow*

^thm-b5-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) $\{F(H), H\} = F'(H)\{H, H\} = 0$, so $\partial_t\rho = 0$ ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^cau-b7-2-1|CM Caution: Constant along the flow, not at a fixed point]]); a step function of $H$ is the limit of smooth ones.
>
> (2) Write Liouville's equation as $\partial_t\rho = -X\cdot\nabla\rho$, with $X$ the Hamiltonian vector field, which is divergence-free ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^der-b7-2-3|derivation of CM Theorem §B7.2.3]]). Then
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\int\Phi(\rho)\,d\Gamma = \int\Phi'(\rho)\,\partial_t\rho\,d\Gamma = -\int X\cdot\nabla\Phi(\rho)\,d\Gamma = -\int\nabla\cdot\bigl(X\,\Phi(\rho)\bigr)\,d\Gamma = 0 ,
> $$
>
> using $\nabla\cdot X = 0$ and the divergence theorem ([[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-28-1|452 Thm. §28.1]]) with vanishing boundary flux. Take $\Phi(\rho) = -k_B\rho\ln\rho$ (continuous at $0$ with value $0$). In words: $\rho$ is carried along unchanged on each trajectory, and phase volume is preserved, so the *distribution* of the values of $\rho$ over phase space never changes, only where they sit.

^der-b5-4-1

*Uses:* [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^def-b5-4-1|Def. §B5.4.1]], [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-3|CM Theorem §B7.2.3]], [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-4|CM Theorem §B7.2.4]], [[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-28-1|452 Thm. §28.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Chaos stretches phase-space regions into filaments
> A gas of hard spheres is chaotic: a small change $\delta\theta_1$ in one deflection angle shifts the next impact parameter by $\lambda\,\delta\theta_1$ and so the next angle by about $(\lambda/R)\delta\theta_1$, with $R$ the molecular radius. After $N$ collisions $\delta\theta_N \sim (\lambda/R)^N\delta\theta_1$, and in air $\lambda/R \sim 10^3$ ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^ex-b5-4-1|Example §B5.4.1]]). Any initial region of phase space, however small — and no state is ever known better than to within some region — is therefore drawn out into an ever finer web of filaments. By Liouville's theorem its total volume stays the same; but it comes to thread through an enormously larger part of the energy shell, and points that started close end up in unrelated places. The correlations built up by collisions are not destroyed; they are hidden in this fine structure.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §2, §3.2, Fig. 4*

^rem-b5-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §B5.4.2: Coarse-Graining
> To **coarse-grain** an ensemble, divide phase space into cells of volume $(\Delta q\,\Delta p)^{3N}$, the resolution with which states can be distinguished, and replace $\rho$ by its average $\bar\rho$ over each cell. The **coarse-grained entropy** is $S_{\text{cg}} = -k_B\int\bar\rho\ln\bar\rho\,d\Gamma$. Since $x\ln x$ is convex, coarse-graining never lowers the entropy: $S_{\text{cg}} \ge S_{\text{fg}}$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §3.2, §6 (cells $\Delta q\,\Delta p$)*

^def-b5-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Why the inequality holds, and what it buys
> Convexity: $x\ln x \ge y\ln y + (1 + \ln y)(x - y)$ for $x, y > 0$ (the graph lies above its tangent). Put $y = \bar\rho$, $x = \rho$, and integrate over a cell: the last term integrates to zero, because $\bar\rho$ is the cell average, so $\int_{\text{cell}}\rho\ln\rho \ge \int_{\text{cell}}\bar\rho\ln\bar\rho$. Summing over cells gives $S_{\text{fg}} \le S_{\text{cg}}$, with equality only if $\rho$ is constant in each cell. Now combine with the filaments: the fine-grained entropy is frozen ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-1|Theorem §B5.4.1]]), but once the filaments are thinner than the cells, the coarse-grained density spreads over all the cells they pass through, and $S_{\text{cg}}$ grows. Irreversibility, on this account, is the growth of what we cannot know about correlations that we cannot measure. Schwartz: "The arrow of time is the direction in which our ignorance grows."
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §3.2*

^rem-b5-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Ergodicity
> A system is **ergodic** if time averages along a trajectory equal averages over the accessible states (the energy shell): the trajectory eventually passes close to every accessible point. Ergodicity would justify replacing time averages by ensemble averages — as when the time-averaged number density of a diffusing gas is identified with $N$ times one molecule's probability density ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]]). It is rarely provable and often false: a billiard ball in a circular table never comes near the centre, while a cardioid table is ergodic. And even where it holds, the time to explore a $10^{24}$-dimensional shell is astronomical. Schwartz's point is that this hardly matters: after coarse-graining to the one-particle description, what must be explored is the six-dimensional space of one molecule, and among $10^{23}$ molecules some molecule is near any given position and velocity at any time. In this vault the equal-probability postulate remains a principle ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]); ergodicity and the H-theorem are the attempts to justify it.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §5, Fig. 5*

^rem-b5-4-3

## The Boltzmann equation

> [!definition] Definition §B5.4.3: Molecular Chaos (Stoßzahlansatz)
> A gas satisfies **molecular chaos** if the velocities of two molecules that are about to collide are statistically independent of each other and of the position of the collision: the probability of finding a pair at $(\vec r, \vec v)$ and $(\vec r, \vec u)$ is $f(\vec r, \vec v, t)\,f(\vec r, \vec u, t)$ (up to normalization), and more generally the $N$-particle density factorizes into one-particle densities.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §3.2, eq. (15) · Blundell §34.5*

^def-b5-4-3

> [!caution] Caution: Molecular chaos is never exactly true
> Two molecules leaving a collision *are* correlated — after a truck hits a bicycle both move roughly along the truck's original direction — and outgoing molecules are tomorrow's incoming ones. So the assumption cannot hold for all pairs at all times; it is applied to pairs *about to* collide only, and it is there that the time asymmetry enters. Run the motion backwards and the incoming pairs carry the correlations — "harbingers of collisions to come", in the phrase Blundell quotes from Lockwood. Molecular chaos is justified, approximately, by the chaotic scattering of [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^rem-b5-4-1|Remark: Chaos stretches phase-space regions into filaments]] followed by coarse-graining.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §3.2, Fig. 2 · Blundell §34.5*

^cau-b5-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.4.2: Free Streaming (Collisionless Boltzmann Equation)
> If the molecules do not interact with each other and move in an external potential $U(\vec r)$, with force $\vec F = -\nabla U$, the one-particle distribution obeys
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \vec v\cdot\nabla_{\vec r}f + \frac{\vec F}{m}\cdot\nabla_{\vec v}f = 0 :
> $$
>
> $f$ is constant along each single-molecule trajectory. Every function of the single-molecule energy, in particular $f \propto e^{-U(\vec r)/k_BT}\,g(\vec v)$, is a stationary solution.
>
> *Source: CM §B7.2 (Liouville's equation), applied here to one molecule · Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §3.2*

^thm-b5-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Without interactions each molecule moves independently under the one-particle Hamiltonian $h = \lvert\vec p\rvert^2/2m + U(\vec r)$, so $f$, as a function of $(\vec r, \vec p = m\vec v)$, is $N$ times the ensemble density of one molecule and obeys Liouville's equation $\partial_tf + \{f, h\} = 0$ in the six-dimensional phase space ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-4|CM Theorem §B7.2.4]]). With $\partial h/\partial\vec p = \vec v$ and $\partial h/\partial\vec r = -\vec F$, $\{f, h\} = \vec v\cdot\nabla_{\vec r}f + \vec F\cdot\nabla_{\vec p}f$, and $\nabla_{\vec p} = m^{-1}\nabla_{\vec v}$. Functions of $h$ are stationary by [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-1|Theorem §B5.4.1]]; $e^{-h/k_BT}$ is the Maxwellian times $e^{-U/k_BT}$.

^der-b5-4-2

*Uses:* [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^def-b5-4-1|Def. §B5.4.1]], [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-4|CM Theorem §B7.2.4]], [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-1|Theorem §B5.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The isothermal atmosphere
> With $U = mgz$ the stationary solution is $f \propto e^{-mgz/k_BT}g(\vec v)$: the density falls off exponentially with height while the velocity distribution is the same Maxwellian at every height. This is the barometric law of the isothermal atmosphere ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-4|CM Theorem §A9.1.4]]) and the instance of Boltzmann's generalisation noted at level A ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^rem-a2-4-2|Remark: Boltzmann's generalisation]]). Free streaming alone does not say *which* function of $h$ the gas settles into; that takes collisions.

^rem-b5-4-4

> [!model] Model §B5.4.3: Boltzmann Equation
> In a dilute gas, collisions change $f$ in addition to streaming:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \vec v\cdot\nabla_{\vec r}f + \frac{\vec F}{m}\cdot\nabla_{\vec v}f = \Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\Bigr)_{\text{coll}} ,
> $$
>
> where the **collision term** is a gain minus a loss. *Loss:* molecules of velocity $\vec v$ are knocked out at the rate $f(\vec v)\int\sigma\lvert\vec v - \vec u\rvert f(\vec u)\,d^3u$ — the collision rate of [[§B5.2★ Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]], with molecular chaos used to write the pair density as $f(\vec v)f(\vec u)$. *Gain:* collisions of other pairs $(\vec v\,', \vec u\,')$ send a molecule into $\vec v$, at a rate $\propto f(\vec v\,')f(\vec u\,')$. For hard spheres, which scatter isotropically ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-4|CM Theorem §B4.2.4]]), the standard explicit form (not derived in the sources) is
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\Bigr)_{\text{coll}} = \int d^3u\int d\Omega\;\frac{d^2}{4}\,\lvert\vec v - \vec u\rvert\,\bigl[f(\vec v\,')f(\vec u\,') - f(\vec v)f(\vec u)\bigr],
> $$
>
> with $\vec v\,', \vec u\,'$ the velocities into which $\vec v, \vec u$ are scattered in the direction $\Omega$.
> - *Assumes:* binary collisions only ($d \ll \lambda$), molecular chaos, collisions short and local compared with the scales on which $f$ varies; classical molecules.
> - *Gives:* conservation of number, momentum and energy by the collisions; transport coefficients, systematically, in the Chapman–Enskog expansion ([[§B5.2★ Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^rem-b5-2-3|Remark: A better theory]]); and the H-theorem.
>
> *Source: Blundell, biography of Boltzmann (ch. 3), §8.3 note 5 (the collision rate), §34.5 (Stoßzahlansatz) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §3 (gain–loss idea)*

^mod-b5-4-3

> [!remark] Remark: Why a local Maxwellian makes the collision term vanish
> In an elastic collision $\vec v + \vec u = \vec v\,' + \vec u\,'$ and $v^2 + u^2 = v'^2 + u'^2$. If $\ln f(\vec v) = a + \vec b\cdot\vec v - cv^2$ — a Maxwellian of any density, mean velocity and temperature — then $\ln f(\vec v\,') + \ln f(\vec u\,') = \ln f(\vec v) + \ln f(\vec u)$, so $f(\vec v\,')f(\vec u\,') = f(\vec v)f(\vec u)$ and gain equals loss for every collision. Boltzmann's H-theorem shows the converse: these are the *only* distributions the collisions leave alone, and every other one is driven toward them. This is the dynamical route to [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]].

^rem-b5-4-5

> [!model] Model §B5.4.4: Relaxation-Time Approximation
> Replace the collision term by
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\Bigr)_{\text{coll}} = -\,\frac{f - f_0}{\tau},
> $$
>
> where $f_0$ is the local equilibrium distribution — a Maxwellian with the same local density (and, as appropriate, temperature and mean velocity) as $f$ — and $\tau$, of the order of the mean free time, is the **relaxation time**: any deviation from local equilibrium decays as $e^{-t/\tau}$ in the absence of streaming.
> - *Assumes:* deviations from local equilibrium small; one relaxation time for all molecules and all kinds of deviation.
>
> *Source: Blundell, Exercise 10.6, eq. (10.79) (sign corrected; see the caution below)*

^mod-b5-4-4

> [!caution] Caution: The sign of the relaxation term
> Blundell's Exercise 10.6 writes $\partial n/\partial t = -\vec v\cdot\nabla n + (n - n_0)/\tau$. With the plus sign a deviation from equilibrium would *grow* as $e^{+t/\tau}$; relaxation needs $-(n - n_0)/\tau$, as in [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^mod-b5-4-4|Model §B5.4.4]].

^cau-b5-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.4.5: Diffusion from the Relaxation-Time Equation
> Let labelled molecules (density $n^*$) move through a uniform gas at temperature $T$ without external forces, obeying [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^mod-b5-4-4|Model §B5.4.4]] with $f_0 = n^*(\vec r, t)\,g(\vec v)$. If $f$ stays close to $f_0$, their flux $\vec\Phi = \int\vec vf\,d^3v$ obeys
>
> $$
> \vec\Phi + \tau\,\frac{\partial\vec\Phi}{\partial t} = -D\,\nabla n^*, \qquad D = \frac{\tau k_BT}{m},
> $$
>
> and the density obeys the **telegraph equation** $\partial_tn^{\ast} + \tau\,\partial_t^2n^{\ast} = D\nabla^2n^{\ast}$. For changes slow compared with $\tau$ this is Fick's law and the diffusion equation ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^law-b4-2-3|Law §B4.2.3]], [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]]); on shorter time scales disturbances travel at the finite speed $\sqrt{D/\tau} = \sqrt{k_BT/m}$.
>
> *Source: Blundell, Exercise 10.6 (the same derivation for the heat current) · worked out here for particles*

^thm-b5-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Zeroth moment.* Integrate the equation over $\vec v$. The collision term gives $-\int(f - f_0)d^3v/\tau = 0$, since $f$ and $f_0$ have the same density: collisions conserve the labelled molecules. So $\partial_tn^{\ast} + \nabla\cdot\vec\Phi = 0$, the continuity equation.
>
> *First moment.* Multiply by $v_i$ and integrate:
>
> $$
> \partial_t\Phi_i + \partial_j\int v_iv_j\,f\,d^3v = -\frac1\tau\int v_i(f - f_0)\,d^3v = -\frac{\Phi_i}{\tau},
> $$
>
> because $\int v_if_0\,d^3v = n^*\langle v_i\rangle = 0$. For $f$ close to $f_0$, $\int v_iv_jf\,d^3v \approx n^*\langle v_iv_j\rangle = n^*\frac{k_BT}{m}\delta_{ij}$ ([[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]), with $T$ uniform. Hence $\partial_t\vec\Phi + \frac{k_BT}{m}\nabla n^* = -\vec\Phi/\tau$; multiply by $\tau$.
>
> *Telegraph equation.* Take the divergence and use $\nabla\cdot\vec\Phi = -\partial_tn^{\ast}$: $-\partial_tn^{\ast} - \tau\partial_t^2n^{\ast} = -D\nabla^2n^{\ast}$. For a time scale $t \gg \tau$ the second-derivative term is smaller by $\tau/t$. A one-dimensional wave $n^{\ast} \propto e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ with $\omega\tau \gg 1$ moves at $\omega/k \to \sqrt{D/\tau}$.

^der-b5-4-5

*Uses:* [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^mod-b5-4-4|Model §B5.4.4]], [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]], [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^def-b4-2-2|Def. §B4.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Three derivations of one diffusion constant
> The relaxation-time result $D = \tau k_BT/m$ is the Einstein relation $D = k_BT/\gamma$ ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-8|Theorem §B4.2.8]]) with the friction $\gamma = m/\tau$: a molecule loses its momentum in a time $\tau$, which is what a drag coefficient measures. It is also $\tfrac13\langle v^2\rangle\tau$, against the mean-free-path value $\tfrac13\lambda\langle v\rangle = \tfrac13\langle v\rangle^2\tau$ ([[§B5.2★ Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]]); the two differ by $\langle v^2\rangle/\langle v\rangle^2 = 3\pi/8 = 1.18$, a reminder that each simple theory fixes the numerical factor only roughly.

^rem-b5-4-6

## The H-theorem

> [!definition] Definition §B5.4.4: Master Equation; Microscopic Reversibility
> Let a system have states $a = 1, \dots, M$ (coarse-grained cells of phase space, configurations of coins, energy levels) occupied with probabilities $P_a(t)$, and let $T_{ab} \ge 0$ be the transition rate from $a$ to $b$. The **master equation** is
>
> $$
> \frac{dP_a}{dt} = \sum_b\bigl[P_b\,T_{ba} - P_a\,T_{ab}\bigr],
> $$
>
> gain from all other states minus loss to them. The rates are **microscopically reversible** if $T_{ab} = T_{ba}$ — in classical mechanics a consequence of time-reversal invariance, in quantum mechanics of the hermiticity of the Hamiltonian, $\lvert\langle a\rvert H_{\text{int}}\lvert b\rangle\rvert^2 = \lvert\langle b\rvert H_{\text{int}}\lvert a\rangle\rvert^2$. Then $\frac{dP_a}{dt} = \sum_bT_{ab}(P_b - P_a)$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §4, eqs. (18)–(20) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §20.2 (the random walk's master equation)*

^def-b5-4-4

> [!caution] Caution: "Detailed balance"
> Schwartz calls $T_{ab} = T_{ba}$ the principle of detailed balance. The more common usage reserves that name for the equilibrium condition $P_a^{\text{eq}}T_{ab} = P_b^{\text{eq}}T_{ba}$, in which each transition is balanced by its reverse; with symmetric rates it says that the equilibrium probabilities are equal. When the states have different energies and exchange energy with a reservoir, the rates are not symmetric, and detailed balance in the second sense gives the Boltzmann distribution instead ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]]).

^cau-b5-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.4.6: H-Theorem (Master-Equation Form)
> For the master equation with symmetric rates $T_{ab} = T_{ba}$, the function
>
> $$
> H(t) = -\sum_aP_a(t)\ln P_a(t)
> $$
>
> never decreases:
>
> $$
> \frac{dH}{dt} = \frac12\sum_{a,b}T_{ab}\,\bigl(P_a - P_b\bigr)\bigl(\ln P_a - \ln P_b\bigr) \;\ge\; 0 ,
> $$
>
> with equality exactly when $P_a = P_b$ for every pair of states connected by a nonzero rate. Total probability is conserved, $\frac{d}{dt}\sum_aP_a = 0$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §4, eqs. (21)–(26)*

^thm-b5-4-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\frac{d}{dt}\sum_aP_a = \sum_{a,b}T_{ab}(P_b - P_a) = 0$, the summand being antisymmetric in $a \leftrightarrow b$. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{dH}{dt} = -\sum_a\dot P_a\ln P_a - \sum_a\dot P_a = \sum_{a,b}T_{ab}\,(P_a - P_b)\ln P_a .
> $$
>
> Exchanging the summation labels and using $T_{ba} = T_{ab}$ gives the same sum as $\sum_{a,b}T_{ab}(P_b - P_a)\ln P_b$; averaging the two expressions yields the symmetric form. Since $\ln$ is increasing, $P_a - P_b$ and $\ln P_a - \ln P_b$ have the same sign, so every term is $\ge 0$, and a term vanishes only if $T_{ab} = 0$ or $P_a = P_b$. (If some $P_a = 0$, read $0\ln0 = 0$; such a state is immediately fed from its neighbours.)

^der-b5-4-6

*Uses:* [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^def-b5-4-4|Def. §B5.4.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.4.7: Approach to Equal Probabilities
> If, in addition, all $M$ states are connected (every state can be reached from every other through transitions with nonzero rates), then for any initial probabilities
>
> $$
> P_a(t) \to \frac1M \quad\text{for all } a, \qquad H(t) \to \ln M ,
> $$
>
> exponentially fast, at a rate no smaller than the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of the rate matrix defined below.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §4 (the conclusion), §6 (equal a priori probabilities for accessible states); the spectral argument is added here*

^thm-b5-4-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write the master equation as $\dot{\vec P} = -\mathsf L\vec P$ with $\mathsf L_{ab} = \delta_{ab}\sum_cT_{ac} - T_{ab}$. $\mathsf L$ is real and symmetric, and for any vector $\vec x$,
>
> $$
> \vec x^{\mathsf T}\mathsf L\,\vec x = \tfrac12\sum_{a,b}T_{ab}(x_a - x_b)^2 \ge 0 ,
> $$
>
> zero only if $x_a = x_b$ along every nonzero rate — that is, by connectedness, only if $\vec x$ is a multiple of $\vec u = (1, \dots, 1)$. By the real spectral theorem ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]) $\mathsf L$ has an orthonormal eigenbasis $\vec e_0 = \vec u/\sqrt M, \vec e_1, \dots, \vec e_{M-1}$ with eigenvalues $0 = \lambda_0 < \lambda_1 \le \cdots$. Hence
>
> $$
> \vec P(t) = \sum_kc_k\,e^{-\lambda_kt}\,\vec e_k \;\longrightarrow\; c_0\vec e_0 ,
> $$
>
> with every other term decaying at least as fast as $e^{-\lambda_1t}$. The surviving component is uniform, and $\sum_aP_a = 1$ is conserved, so it is $1/M$ in each state. $H$ is continuous in $\vec P$, so $H \to -M\cdot\frac1M\ln\frac1M = \ln M$, its largest possible value. The H-theorem is the monotone version of the same statement.

^der-b5-4-7

*Uses:* [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^def-b5-4-4|Def. §B5.4.4]], [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-6|Theorem §B5.4.6]], [[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]

> [!remark] Remark: H, entropy and the second law
> Schwartz's $H = -\sum P_a\ln P_a$ is, up to the factor $k_B$, the Gibbs entropy of [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^def-b6-3-1|Def. §B6.3.1]]; for $M$ equally likely states it is $\ln M$, Boltzmann's $S = k_B\ln W$ ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-1|Principle §A4.6.1]]). The H-theorem is thus a statistical version of the second law in its entropy form ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-5|Theorem §A4.5.5]]), and its conclusion — in equilibrium all accessible states are equally likely — is the fundamental postulate, [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]. "Accessible" matters: states separated by a wall or by a conservation law (energy, particle number) are not connected, and equalization happens only within each connected set.
>
> *Signs.* Boltzmann's own $H$ was $\int f\ln f\,d^3v$ for the one-particle distribution, which *decreases* under the Boltzmann equation ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^mod-b5-4-3|Model §B5.4.3]]) and is stationary exactly for local Maxwellians ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^rem-b5-4-5|Remark: Why a local Maxwellian makes the collision term vanish]]). Its proof (not in the sources) has the same two ingredients as the one above: the symmetry of the collision rates under time reversal, and the monotonicity of the logarithm.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §4, §6; Lecture 4, §6 (H and entropy) · Blundell §34.5*

^rem-b5-4-7

> [!remark] Remark: Loschmidt's objection
> The microscopic equations are time-reversal invariant: reverse every velocity at some instant, and the gas retraces its history. For each evolution in which $H$ increases there is one in which it decreases, so a theorem deriving a one-way increase from reversible mechanics must have assumed something extra (**Loschmidt's paradox**). The extra assumption is the master equation itself: a state $a$ that can go to many states $b$ with fixed probabilities. With exact knowledge of the microstate, $P_a$ is $1$ for one state and $0$ for the others at every time, and $H = 0$ forever — consistent with the theorem, but empty. Probabilities that spread out describe either time-averages, or ensembles over the unknown details of the initial state (the flea across the universe), or coarse-grained cells; in each case information that is in principle available is discarded, and the discarding is what has a direction. In the Boltzmann equation the same assumption is molecular chaos for *incoming* pairs ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^cau-b5-4-1|Caution: Molecular chaos is never exactly true]]). Blundell adds the cosmological answer: the asymmetry may lie in the boundary conditions — systems, and the universe, are prepared in low-entropy states and only the initial condition is special.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §4 · Blundell §34.5*

^rem-b5-4-8

> [!remark] Remark: The recurrence objection
> A second objection (Zermelo's) uses Liouville's theorem against the H-theorem. For an isolated system confined to a bounded region of phase space, Poincaré's recurrence theorem — a consequence of volume preservation, not proved in the sources — says that almost every initial state returns arbitrarily close to itself after a long enough time; so the gas that spread out of one half of a box will one day gather there again, and $H$ cannot increase for ever. The answer is quantitative: recurrence requires wandering through a fair part of a $6N$-dimensional shell, and for $N \sim 10^{23}$ the time is beyond astronomical (compare the exploration times in [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^rem-b5-4-3|Remark: Ergodicity]]). On every time scale that can be observed the H-theorem holds; it is a statistical law, not an absolute one.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B5.4.1: How Fast a Gas Forgets
> In air, $\lambda \sim 10^{-7}\ \mathrm m$ and the molecular radius $R \sim 10^{-10}\ \mathrm m$, so each collision multiplies a small angular error by $\lambda/R \sim 10^3$. Waving an arm ($1\ \mathrm{kg}$, moved by $1\ \mathrm m$) $10\ \mathrm{km}$ away changes the gravitational force on an atom ($10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$) by $\delta F = 2Gm_1m_2\,\delta r/r^3 \approx 1.3 \times 10^{-49}\ \mathrm N$, an acceleration of about $10^{-22}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$; over one free time (Schwartz's round $10^{-9}\ \mathrm s$; [[§B5.2★ Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^rem-b5-2-1|Remark: Numbers to remember]] gives $0.1$–$0.2\ \mathrm{ns}$) this displaces the atom by $a\tau^2 \sim 10^{-22} \cdot 10^{-18} = 10^{-40}\ \mathrm m$ and deflects the next collision by $\delta\theta_1 \sim 10^{-40}/10^{-10} = 10^{-30}$. After $N$ collisions $\delta\theta_N \sim 10^{-30}\cdot10^{3N}$, of order $1$ after only $N \approx 10$ collisions — about ten nanoseconds. Schwartz estimates that even the gravitational pull of a flea moving $1\ \mu\mathrm m$ at the far side of the universe ($10^{27}\ \mathrm m$), with $\delta\theta_1 \sim 10^{-97}$, randomizes the trajectory after about 33 collisions, $10^{-7}\ \mathrm s$. (Schwartz's own arithmetic for the arm, $a\tau^2 \sim 10^{-32}\ \mathrm m$ and 8 collisions, puts the displacement eight or nine orders of magnitude too high; the conclusion is unchanged.) The microstate of a gas is unpredictable in principle, not only in practice: describing it by probabilities is forced.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §2, eqs. (4)–(7)*

^ex-b5-4-1

> [!example] Example §B5.4.2: Five Coins and a Stream of Golf Balls
> Five coins lie heads up in a box; golf balls thrown in at random hit each coin at rate $r$, and a hit coin lands heads or tails with probability $\tfrac12$ each. The 32 configurations are the states of a master equation in which neighbouring configurations (differing in one coin) are connected by the symmetric rate $T_{ab} = r/2$ ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^def-b5-4-4|Def. §B5.4.4]]). The coins are independent, so solve for one: its probability of heads obeys $\dot p = \tfrac r2(1 - p) - \tfrac r2p$, so
>
> $$
> p(t) = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + e^{-rt}\bigr), \qquad P_{\mathrm{HHHHH}}(t) = p(t)^5 \;\to\; \tfrac1{32},
> $$
>
> and $H(t) = 5\bigl[-p\ln p - (1 - p)\ln(1 - p)\bigr]$ rises monotonically from $0$ to $5\ln2 = \ln32$, as [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-6|Theorem §B5.4.6]] and [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-7|Theorem §B5.4.7]] require; the slowest decay rate is $r$, the smallest nonzero eigenvalue. Yet any *particular* sequence of throws takes the box through one definite configuration after another, and for that sequence nothing spreads out: the probabilities describe our ignorance of which sequence occurred.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §4 (the example); the solution is worked out here*
