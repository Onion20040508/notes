Level A stated the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of molecular speeds ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]]) with only the idea of a derivation, and computed from it the three characteristic speeds ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]]). Here the distribution is **proved**: for an isolated ideal gas, from the single assumption that all its microstates of a given energy are equally likely, by counting how many ways the remaining molecules can share the remaining energy (Schwartz). The **layer changes**: what level A quoted becomes a theorem of statistical mechanics, resting on the fundamental postulate ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]). With the distribution in hand, the section computes what level A could not: all its moments, the flux of molecules onto a wall, and effusion through a small hole, where the escaping molecules are faster than those left behind.

## The velocity distribution

> [!definition] Definition §B5.1.1: Velocity Distribution Function
> The **velocity distribution function** $g(\vec v)$ of a gas is the probability density of one molecule's velocity: the fraction of molecules with velocity in the element $d^3v$ around $\vec v$ is $g(\vec v)\,d^3v$, and in a gas of number density $n$ there are $n\,g(\vec v)\,d^3v$ such molecules per unit volume. If $g$ depends only on the speed $v = \lvert\vec v\rvert$ (an **isotropic** gas), the speeds have the density
>
> $$
> f(v) = 4\pi v^2\,g(v),
> $$
>
> because the velocities with speed in $[v, v + dv]$ fill a spherical shell of volume $4\pi v^2dv$ in velocity space. $f$ is the speed distribution function of [[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^def-a2-4-1|Def. §A2.4.1]].
>
> *Source: Blundell §5.1–5.2, eqs. (5.8)–(5.10) · Schroeder §6.4, eqs. (6.45)–(6.47)*

^def-b5-1-1

> [!model] Model §B5.1.1: Classical Monatomic Ideal Gas, Microcanonical
> $N$ identical point molecules of mass $m$ in a box of volume $V$, isolated, with total energy
>
> $$
> E = \sum_{j=1}^N\frac{\lvert\vec p_j\rvert^2}{2m}
> $$
>
> fixed: no potential energy, no external field, no internal motion. Collisions (short-range, elastic) exchange energy between molecules but are otherwise ignored, as in the ideal gas of level A ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]]). Motion is classical. A microstate is a point $(\vec q_1, \vec p_1, \dots, \vec q_N, \vec p_N)$ of the $6N$-dimensional phase space ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^def-b7-2-1|CM Def. §B7.2.1]]). In equilibrium **every microstate with energy between $E$ and $E + \delta E$ is equally likely** (the fundamental postulate, [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]; its justification by the H-theorem, [[§B5.4 Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem|§B5.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §1, §6 · Lecture 4, §3*

^mod-b5-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.2: Maxwell–Boltzmann Velocity Distribution
> In the gas of [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^mod-b5-1-1|Model §B5.1.1]], let $N \to \infty$ at fixed energy per molecule $\varepsilon = E/N$, and define $T$ by $\varepsilon = \tfrac32k_BT$. Then the velocity of any one molecule has the density
>
> $$
> g(\vec v) = \Bigl(\frac{m}{2\pi k_BT}\Bigr)^{3/2}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{m\lvert\vec v\rvert^2}{2k_BT}\Bigr):
> $$
>
> the three components are independent, each $\mathcal N(0, k_BT/m)$, and every direction is equally likely. The temperature so defined is the absolute temperature of the gas: it is the $T$ of the ideal gas law, by [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], and the statistical temperature $1/T = \partial S/\partial E$ of [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §7, eqs. (42)–(51); Lecture 4, eqs. (21)–(23) · Blundell §5.1, eqs. (5.2)–(5.8) · Schroeder §6.4, eq. (6.47)*

^thm-b5-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Positions.* The energy does not depend on the positions, so each molecule is uniformly distributed over $V$, independently of the momenta; only the momenta need counting.
>
> *Counting the ways to share the energy.* The momenta $\vec P = (\vec p_1, \dots, \vec p_N) \in \mathbb R^{3N}$ are uniformly distributed over the thin spherical shell $2mE \le \lvert\vec P\rvert^2 \le 2m(E + \delta E)$. Fix the momentum $\vec p_1$ of one molecule. The other $3N - 3$ components must then lie on a shell of radius $R' = \sqrt{2mE - p_1^2}$ and thickness $\delta R' = m\,\delta E/R'$, whose volume is proportional to $R'^{\,3N - 4}\,\delta R' \propto R'^{\,3N - 5}$. The probability density of $\vec p_1$ is proportional to the number of ways the remaining molecules can carry the remaining energy:
>
> $$
> P_N(\vec p_1) \propto \bigl(2mE - p_1^2\bigr)^{(3N - 5)/2} \propto \Bigl(1 - \frac{x}{N}\Bigr)^{(3N - 5)/2}, \qquad x = \frac{p_1^2}{2m\varepsilon}, \quad 0 \le x \le N .
> $$
>
> *The limit.* For fixed $x$, $\ln\bigl[(1 - x/N)^{(3N-5)/2}\bigr] = \tfrac{3N - 5}{2}\bigl(-\tfrac xN + O(N^{-2})\bigr) \to -\tfrac32x$, so the unnormalized density tends to $e^{-3x/2} = \exp\bigl(-3p_1^2/4m\varepsilon\bigr)$. The normalizing integrals converge too: since $1 - y \le e^{-y}$, the integrand is bounded by $e^{-x(3N-5)/2N} \le e^{-x}$ for $N \ge 5$, an integrable function of $\vec p_1$, so dominated convergence ([[§15 The General Lebesgue Integral#^thm-15-8|551 Thm. §15.8]]) passes the limit through the integral. Hence the normalized density tends to the normalized Gaussian
>
> $$
> P(\vec p_1) = (2\pi mk_BT)^{-3/2}\,e^{-p_1^2/2mk_BT}, \qquad k_BT \equiv \tfrac23\varepsilon ,
> $$
>
> normalized by [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]]. It factorizes into three one-dimensional Gaussians of variance $mk_BT$, so the components are independent ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-3|Def. §B4.1.3]]). Change variables to $\vec v = \vec p_1/m$ (a Jacobian $m^3$).
>
> *The temperature.* The distribution gives $\langle\tfrac12m\lvert\vec v\rvert^2\rangle = 3\times\tfrac12m\cdot\frac{k_BT}{m} = \tfrac32k_BT = \varepsilon$: all of the energy is kinetic and shared equally, as it must be. Level A found from the kinetic pressure and the ideal gas law that $\langle\tfrac12mv^2\rangle = \tfrac32k_BT$ with the gas-law temperature ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]); so the parameter defined by $\varepsilon = \tfrac32k_BT$ is that temperature. Schwartz instead computes $1/T = \partial(k_B\ln\Omega)/\partial E$ for the same gas, where the multiplicity $\Omega \propto E^{3N/2}$ gives $E = \tfrac32Nk_BT$ ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-8|Theorem §B6.2.8]]).

^der-b5-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^mod-b5-1-1|Model §B5.1.1]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-3|Def. §B4.1.3]], [[§15 The General Lebesgue Integral#^thm-15-8|551 Thm. §15.8]], [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Equally likely microstates, yet an exponential
> Every microstate of the whole gas is equally likely, so why are large velocities of *one* molecule exponentially unlikely? Because the more energy that molecule takes, the less is left for the others, and the number of ways of sharing what is left falls steeply: $(E - \tfrac12mv^2)^{(3N-5)/2}$ is, for large $N$, $E^{(3N-5)/2}e^{-mv^2/2k_BT}$. The single molecule behaves like a small system in contact with a reservoir, "all the other molecules", and its states are weighted by the **Boltzmann factor** $e^{-(\text{energy})/k_BT}$. Blundell and Schroeder derive the distribution that way, from the Boltzmann factor proved later ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]]): the probability of a velocity $\vec v$ is $\propto e^{-mv^2/2k_BT}$, and the number of velocities with speed $v$ is $\propto 4\pi v^2$. The computation above is that general argument carried out for one system; it also shows why the components come out independent, the property Maxwell had assumed.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §7; Lecture 4, §4.1 · Blundell §5.1 · Schroeder §6.4*

^rem-b5-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from level A
> Level A's [[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]] carried a theorem box, but its derivation only sketched the idea and invoked the Boltzmann factor without proof ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^der-a2-4-1|its derivation]]). Now the distribution is derived: from the equal-probability postulate (a principle of statistical mechanics) and the classical description of an ideal monatomic gas. A second, dynamical route — the gas *approaches* this distribution because of collisions — is Boltzmann's H-theorem ([[§B5.4 Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem|§B5.4]]). The classical treatment fails when the gas becomes so cold and dense that quantum statistics matter ([[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-1|Def. §B10.2.1]]).

^rem-b5-1-2

## Speeds and moments

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.3: Moments of the Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution
> With $v_p = \sqrt{2k_BT/m}$:
> 1. *Components:* $\langle v_x\rangle = 0$, $\langle v_x^2\rangle = k_BT/m$, $\langle\lvert v_x\rvert\rangle = \sqrt{2k_BT/\pi m} = \tfrac12\langle v\rangle$, and $\langle v_xv_y\rangle = 0$.
> 2. *Speeds:* the speed density is $f(v) = 4\pi v^2g(v)$, the formula of [[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]], and for every real $s > -3$
>
> $$
> \langle v^s\rangle = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\,\Gamma\Bigl(\frac{s + 3}{2}\Bigr)\,v_p^{\,s} .
> $$
>
> $s = 1, 2$ give $\langle v\rangle = \sqrt{8k_BT/\pi m}$ and $\langle v^2\rangle = 3k_BT/m$ ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]]); $s = -1$ gives $\langle 1/v\rangle = \sqrt{2m/\pi k_BT}$, so that $\langle v\rangle\langle 1/v\rangle = 4/\pi$; $s = 4$ gives $\langle v^4\rangle = \tfrac{15}{4}v_p^4$.
> 3. *The fast tail:* the fraction of molecules faster than $v_0$ is, with $x_0 = v_0/v_p$,
>
> $$
> P(v > v_0) = \operatorname{erfc}(x_0) + \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\,x_0\,e^{-x_0^2}, \qquad \operatorname{erfc}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_x^\infty e^{-t^2}dt .
> $$
>
> *Source: Blundell §5.1–5.2, eqs. (5.5)–(5.16), Exercises 5.4, 6.6–6.7 · Schroeder §6.4, eqs. (6.48)–(6.55) · PHY 306, Problem Set 8, Problem 1 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §29*

^thm-b5-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) Each component is $\mathcal N(0, k_BT/m)$ ([[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]]), so the first two follow from [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]], and $\langle v_xv_y\rangle = \langle v_x\rangle\langle v_y\rangle = 0$ by independence. $\langle\lvert v_x\rvert\rangle = 2\sqrt{m/2\pi k_BT}\int_0^\infty v_xe^{-mv_x^2/2k_BT}dv_x = 2\sqrt{m/2\pi k_BT}\cdot k_BT/m = \sqrt{2k_BT/\pi m}$.
>
> (2) In spherical coordinates in velocity space ([[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-17|452 Thm. §15.17]]), $d^3v = v^2dv\,d\Omega$ and the angular integral of an isotropic integrand is $4\pi$. With $a = 1/v_p^2$ and the general Gaussian moment of [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]],
>
> $$
> \langle v^s\rangle = 4\pi\Bigl(\frac a\pi\Bigr)^{3/2}\int_0^\infty v^{s+2}e^{-av^2}dv = 4\pi\Bigl(\frac a\pi\Bigr)^{3/2}\frac{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{s+3}{2}\bigr)}{2a^{(s+3)/2}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\Gamma\Bigl(\frac{s+3}{2}\Bigr)a^{-s/2},
> $$
>
> convergent at $v = 0$ exactly when $s + 2 > -1$. Then $\Gamma(2) = 1$, $\Gamma(\tfrac52) = \tfrac34\sqrt\pi$, $\Gamma(1) = 1$, $\Gamma(\tfrac72) = \tfrac{15}{8}\sqrt\pi$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]); and $\langle v\rangle\langle 1/v\rangle = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}v_p\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi\,v_p} = \frac4\pi$. Since $\langle\lvert v_x\rvert\rangle$ is the average of $v\lvert\cos\theta\rvert$ over an isotropic distribution and $\langle\lvert\cos\theta\rvert\rangle = \tfrac12$, it equals $\tfrac12\langle v\rangle$ for any isotropic gas (Blundell, Exercise 6.6).
>
> (3) With $x = v/v_p$, $P(v > v_0) = \frac{4}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{x_0}^\infty x^2e^{-x^2}dx$. Integrate by parts with $u = x$, $dv = xe^{-x^2}dx$ ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]): $\int_{x_0}^\infty x^2e^{-x^2}dx = \tfrac12x_0e^{-x_0^2} + \tfrac12\int_{x_0}^\infty e^{-x^2}dx = \tfrac12x_0e^{-x_0^2} + \tfrac{\sqrt\pi}{4}\operatorname{erfc}(x_0)$. Multiply by $4/\sqrt\pi$.

## Molecules hitting a wall

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.4: Molecular Flux onto a Wall
> In a gas of number density $n$ with an isotropic speed distribution $f(v)$, the number of molecules striking unit area of a wall per unit time, with speed in $[v, v + dv]$ and direction at angle $[\theta, \theta + d\theta]$ to the normal, is
>
> $$
> d\Phi = v\cos\theta\;n f(v)\,dv\;\tfrac12\sin\theta\,d\theta, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le \tfrac\pi2 ,
> $$
>
> and the total **molecular flux** is
>
> $$
> \Phi = \tfrac14\,n\langle v\rangle .
> $$
>
> For the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, with $p = nk_BT$, $\;\Phi = \dfrac{p}{\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}}$. The molecules that strike the wall have speeds distributed as $v f(v)$ — the fast ones arrive more often — and arrive at a mean $\langle\cos\theta\rangle = \tfrac23$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §6.1.2–6.1.3, eq. (6.13); §7.1, eqs. (7.5)–(7.9); Exercises 6.5, 6.9*

^thm-b5-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By isotropy, the fraction of molecules moving within the solid angle $d\Omega$ is $d\Omega/4\pi$, and the ring of directions at angle $\theta$ to the normal has $d\Omega = 2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta$, a fraction $\tfrac12\sin\theta\,d\theta$. (The user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §30.2, write the fraction as $\sin\theta\,d\theta/4\pi$, without the azimuthal $2\pi$; with it the pressure integral there gives the stated $\tfrac13nm\langle v^2\rangle$.) In a time $dt$ the molecules of speed $v$ and angle $\theta$ that reach an area $A$ of the wall are those in a slanted cylinder of base $A$ and length $v\,dt$, volume $Av\,dt\cos\theta$. Multiplying the volume by their number density $nf(v)\,dv\,\tfrac12\sin\theta\,d\theta$ and dividing by $A\,dt$ gives $d\Phi$. Integrating, with $\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \tfrac12$,
>
> $$
> \Phi = \frac n2\int_0^\infty vf(v)\,dv\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac n4\langle v\rangle .
> $$
>
> With $\langle v\rangle = \sqrt{8k_BT/\pi m}$ ([[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]) and $n = p/k_BT$, $\Phi = \frac p{4k_BT}\sqrt{\frac{8k_BT}{\pi m}} = \frac{p}{\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}}$. A check with the components (Blundell, Exercise 6.9): only $v_x > 0$ approach a wall at $x = $ const, and $\Phi = n\int_0^\infty v_x\,g(v_x)\,dv_x = n\sqrt{k_BT/2\pi m}$, the same. The weight of $d\Phi$ in $v$ is $vf(v)$; in $\theta$ it is $\cos\theta\sin\theta$, whose mean of $\cos\theta$ is $\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta\big/\tfrac12 = \tfrac23$.

^der-b5-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^def-b5-1-1|Def. §B5.1.1]], [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Pressure from the same count
> Each molecule striking the wall reverses its normal momentum, delivering $2mv\cos\theta$. Weighting $d\Phi$ by it, $p = \int 2mv\cos\theta\,d\Phi = mn\int v^2f\,dv\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \tfrac13nm\langle v^2\rangle$, the kinetic pressure of level A ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]]), now for any isotropic distribution and without the box geometry; with $\langle v^2\rangle = 3k_BT/m$ it is $p = nk_BT$. Level A's shortcut — of the molecules with $x$-velocity of size $\lvert v_x\rvert$, half move toward the wall ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^rem-a2-2-1|Remark: Counting collisions instead of round trips]]) — gives the flux too if it is averaged correctly: $\Phi = \tfrac12n\langle\lvert v_x\rvert\rangle = \tfrac14n\langle v\rangle$ by [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]](1). What fails is the cruder picture of molecules moving at the mean speed along the three axes, a sixth toward each wall, which gives $\tfrac16n\langle v\rangle$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §6.2, eqs. (6.14)–(6.18)*

## Effusion

> [!definition] Definition §B5.1.2: Effusion
> **Effusion** is the escape of gas through a small hole of area $A$ in a thin wall, into vacuum (or into gas at much lower pressure), when the hole's diameter $D$ is much smaller than the mean free path $\lambda$ ([[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]]). Then each molecule that reaches the hole simply leaves, without a pressure gradient forming near it, and the gas inside stays in equilibrium.
>
> *Source: Blundell §7.2*

^def-b5-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.5: Effusion
> For a Maxwellian gas at pressure $p$ and temperature $T$ effusing through a hole of area $A$:
> 1. the rate of escape is $\Phi A = \dfrac{pA}{\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}}$: at given $p$ and $T$ it is proportional to $m^{-1/2}$ (**Graham's law**);
> 2. the effusing molecules have the speed distribution
>
> $$
> f_{\text{eff}}(v) = \frac12\Bigl(\frac{m}{k_BT}\Bigr)^2v^3e^{-mv^2/2k_BT},
> $$
>
> with most probable speed $\sqrt{3k_BT/m}$, mean speed $\sqrt{9\pi k_BT/8m}$, and mean kinetic energy $2k_BT$ instead of $\tfrac32k_BT$;
> 3. two vessels joined by such a hole reach a steady state with $p_1/\sqrt{T_1} = p_2/\sqrt{T_2}$ (the **Knudsen effect**), not $p_1 = p_2$;
> 4. a vessel of volume $V$ emptying through the hole loses pressure as $p(t) = p(0)\,e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = \dfrac VA\sqrt{\dfrac{2\pi m}{k_BT}}$, at constant $T$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §7.2, eqs. (7.11)–(7.17), Examples 7.4–7.6, Exercises 7.4, 7.8*

^thm-b5-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) The molecules leaving per unit time are those that would have struck the area $A$ of wall ([[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]]). (2) Their speeds are weighted by $vf(v) \propto v^3e^{-av^2}$, $a = m/2k_BT$. By [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]], $\int_0^\infty v^3e^{-av^2}dv = 1/2a^2$, $\int v^4e^{-av^2}dv = \tfrac38\sqrt\pi\,a^{-5/2}$ and $\int v^5e^{-av^2}dv = 1/a^3$; so the normalization is $2a^2 = \tfrac12(m/k_BT)^2$, the mean speed is $\tfrac34\sqrt\pi\,a^{-1/2} = \sqrt{9\pi k_BT/8m}$, and $\langle v^2\rangle_{\text{eff}} = 2/a = 4k_BT/m$, a mean kinetic energy $2k_BT$. The maximum of $v^3e^{-av^2}$ is where $3v^2 = 2av^4$, at $v^2 = 3k_BT/m$. (3) In the steady state the fluxes through the hole balance, $p_1/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT_1} = p_2/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT_2}$. (4) $\frac{dN}{dt} = -\frac{A}{4}\frac NV\langle v\rangle$, so $N$, and with it $p = Nk_BT/V$, decays with $\tau = 4V/A\langle v\rangle$, which is the stated $\tau$.

*Nitrogen at $300\ \mathrm K$: the speed distribution of the molecules inside (blue, $\propto v^2e^{-mv^2/2k_BT}$, mean $476\ \mathrm{m/s}$) and of those effusing through a small hole (red, $\propto v^3e^{-mv^2/2k_BT}$, mean $561\ \mathrm{m/s}$). The extra factor $v$ is the rate at which molecules of speed $v$ reach the hole: the beam is not a fair sample of the gas.*

> [!caution] Caution: The hole must be small, and the beam is biased
> - If $D \gg \lambda$, gas flows out hydrodynamically, as a jet, and two vessels joined by the opening equalize their pressures; Graham's $m^{-1/2}$ law and the Knudsen relation hold only for $D \ll \lambda$.
> - The effusing molecules are faster than the gas they came from: the gas left behind cools if it is not heated, and a box that catches the beam ends up at a temperature above that of the source (Blundell, Exercise 7.2).
> - In Maxwell–Boltzmann experiments with an oven and a rotating velocity selector, the measured intensity goes as $v^4e^{-mv^2/2k_BT}$, not $v^2e^{-mv^2/2k_BT}$: one factor $v$ from effusion out of the oven, another because the selector's window $\Delta v \propto v$ widens with the selected speed (Blundell §5.3, after Miller and Kusch).

^cau-b5-1-1

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Isotope separation
> Graham's law separates isotopes, which chemistry cannot. For uranium hexafluoride, $M(^{238}\mathrm{UF}_6) = 352.04$ and $M(^{235}\mathrm{UF}_6) = 349.03\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, so the lighter molecule effuses faster by $\sqrt{352.04/349.03} = 1.0043$; the enrichment of each stage is tiny, and the Manhattan project's gaseous diffusion plant used thousands of stages in series. (Blundell's Example 7.1 gives $348.03$ for the lighter molecule, hence $1.0057$; $235.04 + 6 \times 19.00 = 349.03$.)
>
> *Source: Blundell, Example 7.1 (masses corrected)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B5.1.1: How Many Nitrogen Molecules Exceed 1000 m/s?
> At $300\ \mathrm K$, nitrogen ($m = 4.65 \times 10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$) has $v_p = \sqrt{2k_BT/m} = 422\ \mathrm{m/s}$, so $x_0 = 1000/422 = 2.37$, and by [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]
>
> $$
> P(v > 1000\ \mathrm{m/s}) = \operatorname{erfc}(2.37) + \tfrac{2}{\sqrt\pi}(2.37)\,e^{-5.62} = 0.0008 + 0.0097 = 0.0105 .
> $$
>
> About one molecule in a hundred, as Schroeder obtains by integrating numerically. The second term dominates: in the far tail $P \approx \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}x_0e^{-x_0^2}$, and the fraction depends on temperature essentially through $e^{-mv_0^2/2k_BT}$. At $100\ \mathrm K$ the same fraction is $2 \times 10^{-7}$, at $1000\ \mathrm K$ it is $0.34$. This steep dependence is why evaporation and the escape of light gases from planetary atmospheres ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^rem-a2-4-1|Remark: How the distribution moves]]) are so sensitive to temperature.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §6.4, eqs. (6.53)–(6.56) (closed form derived here)*

^ex-b5-1-1

> [!example]- ★ Example §B5.1.2: Doppler Width of the 21-cm Line
> An atom moving with velocity component $v_x$ toward the observer emits at the Doppler-shifted frequency $\omega = \omega_0(1 + v_x/c)$ for $v_x \ll c$. Since $v_x$ is $\mathcal N(0, k_BT/m)$, the line shape is the Gaussian
>
> $$
> I(\omega) \propto \exp\Bigl[-\frac{mc^2(\omega - \omega_0)^2}{2k_BT\,\omega_0^2}\Bigr], \qquad \frac{\Delta\omega_{\text{FWHM}}}{\omega_0} = 2\sqrt{\frac{2\ln2\;k_BT}{mc^2}} ,
> $$
>
> the full width at half maximum following from $I = \tfrac12I_{\max}$ at $(\omega - \omega_0)^2 = 2\ln2\,k_BT\omega_0^2/mc^2$. For atomic hydrogen in an interstellar cloud at $100\ \mathrm K$ emitting the $21\ \mathrm{cm}$ line ($f_0 = 1420.4\ \mathrm{MHz}$):
>
> $$
> \frac{\Delta f}{f_0} = 2\sqrt{\frac{2\ln2\,(1.38 \times 10^{-23})(100)}{(1.67 \times 10^{-27})(3.00 \times 10^8)^2}} = 7.1 \times 10^{-6}, \qquad \Delta f = 10\ \mathrm{kHz} .
> $$
>
> Radio astronomers read cloud temperatures off such widths; collisions add a further (pressure) broadening, negligible at interstellar densities.
>
> *Source: Blundell §5.3, eqs. (5.24)–(5.26), Exercise 5.6*

^ex-b5-1-2

> [!example] Example §B5.1.3: Vapour Pressure of Mercury by Effusion
> In Knudsen's method a liquid sits in a closed cell with a small hole in the top; the vapour above it is at the saturated vapour pressure $p$, and the cell loses mass at the rate $\lvert dM/dt\rvert = m\,\Phi A$. By [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-5|Theorem §B5.1.5]],
>
> $$
> p = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi k_BT}{m}}\;\frac1A\Bigl\lvert\frac{dM}{dt}\Bigr\rvert .
> $$
>
> A cell of mercury ($m = 200.59\ \mathrm u = 3.33 \times 10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$) at $273\ \mathrm K$ with a hole of $10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ loses $2.4 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{kg}$ in 30 days, $\lvert dM/dt\rvert = 9.3 \times 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{kg/s}$. Then $\sqrt{2\pi k_BT/m} = 267\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and
>
> $$
> p = (267\ \mathrm{m/s})\,\frac{9.3 \times 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{kg/s}}{10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2}} = 0.025\ \mathrm{Pa} = 1.9 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{torr} .
> $$
>
> The method works because the vapour is so thin: its mean free path, tens of centimetres at this pressure, far exceeds any practical hole.
>
> *Source: Blundell, Example 7.4, Exercise 7.3*
