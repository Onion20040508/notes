At $500\ \mathrm{m/s}$ a gas molecule would cross a room in a hundredth of a second; smells and heat take far longer because the molecule keeps colliding. Level A defined the mean free path and found $\lambda \propto k_BT/(r^2p)$ by a sweeping-cylinder argument with an approximate $\sqrt2$ for the relative motion ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^def-a2-2-4|Def. §A2.2.4]], [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-6|Theorem §A2.2.6]]). Level B makes the collision statistics precise — free paths are exponentially distributed, and with the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion|§B5.1]] the factor $\sqrt2$ is exact — and then uses the mean free path to compute the three **transport coefficients** of a dilute gas: viscosity (transport of momentum), thermal conductivity (of energy) and the diffusion constant (of particles). The thermal conductivity is the coefficient of Fourier's law ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-1|Law §A1.5.1]]), there an empirical constant of the material; here it is derived for a gas, with its approximations stated.

*Notation.* The thermal conductivity is written $\kappa$ (Blundell); level A writes $k$, which here would clash with $k_B$.

## Collisions

> [!model] Model §B5.2.1: Dilute Hard-Sphere Gas
> The molecules are rigid spheres of diameter $d$ (radius $a = d/2$) moving in straight lines between collisions ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^mod-b4-2-3|CM Model §B4.2.3]]). Further:
> - *dilute:* $d \ll \lambda$, so only two-body collisions occur, and the gas is ideal between them;
> - *molecular chaos:* the velocities of two molecules about to collide are independent, each Maxwellian ([[§B5.4 Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^def-b5-4-3|Def. §B5.4.3]]);
> - *randomizing collisions:* after a collision a molecule's velocity is uncorrelated with its velocity before (hard-sphere scattering is isotropic in the centre-of-mass frame, [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-4|CM Theorem §B4.2.4]]);
> - for transport: *local equilibrium*, a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution at the local temperature, density and flow velocity, with gradients small over a distance $\lambda$; and a container large compared with $\lambda$.
>
> Real molecules are slightly soft, so their effective $d$ shrinks somewhat as the temperature (the violence of collisions) rises.
>
> *Source: Blundell §8.2, eqs. (8.12)–(8.14), note 1, §9.1 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 3, §2 (hard spheres)*

^mod-b5-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §B5.2.1: Collision Cross-Section; Collision Rate; Mean Free Time and Path
> Two hard spheres of radii $a_1, a_2$ collide when the distance of closest approach of their centres on undeflected paths — the impact parameter ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^def-b4-2-1|CM Def. §B4.2.1]]) — is less than $a_1 + a_2$. The **collision cross-section** is the area of this target disc,
>
> $$
> \sigma = \pi(a_1 + a_2)^2, \qquad \sigma = \pi d^2 \ \text{ for identical molecules} .
> $$
>
> The **collision rate** $R$ is the mean number of collisions a molecule suffers per unit time; the **mean free time** is $\tau = 1/R$, and the **mean free path** is the mean distance travelled between collisions, $\lambda = \langle v\rangle\tau$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §8.1–8.3, eqs. (8.10), (8.13), (8.15) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, §3.1*

^def-b5-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.2: Free Times and Free Paths Are Exponentially Distributed
> Let a molecule move with speed $v$ through targets of number density $n$ and cross-section $\sigma$, assumed at rest (or replace $v$ by the relative speed). The probability that it has not collided by time $t$ is
>
> $$
> P_0(t) = e^{-n\sigma vt} = e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{n\sigma v} ,
> $$
>
> so the time to the next collision has the exponential density $\tau^{-1}e^{-t/\tau}$, of mean $\tau$. The number of collisions in time $t$ is Poisson with mean $t/\tau$. Likewise a free path exceeds $x$ with probability $e^{-x/\lambda}$; its mean is $\lambda$, its most probable value is $0$, $\langle x^2\rangle = 2\lambda^2$, and $37\,\%$, $14\,\%$, $0.7\,\%$ of free paths exceed $\lambda$, $2\lambda$, $5\lambda$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §8.1, eqs. (8.1)–(8.11), Exercise 8.2*

^thm-b5-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In a time $dt$ the molecule sweeps a cylinder of volume $\sigma v\,dt$, which contains a target with probability $n\sigma v\,dt$. Not colliding up to $t + dt$ means not colliding up to $t$ *and* not in the next $dt$, independent events by molecular chaos: $P_0(t + dt) = P_0(t)(1 - n\sigma v\,dt)$, so $dP_0/dt = -n\sigma vP_0$ and $P_0 = e^{-n\sigma vt}$ with $P_0(0) = 1$. The density of the first collision time is $-dP_0/dt = n\sigma ve^{-n\sigma vt}$, and $\int_0^\infty t\,n\sigma v\,e^{-n\sigma vt}dt = 1/n\sigma v$ by the factorial integral ($\int_0^\infty xe^{-x}dx = 1! = 1$, [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]). The collisions form exactly the rare-event process of [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-9|Theorem §B4.1.9]]: slicing $t$ into $N$ intervals of collision probability $t/N\tau$ each and letting $N \to \infty$ gives the Poisson distribution of mean $t/\tau$. At constant speed, distance is $x = vt$, so free paths have density $\lambda^{-1}e^{-x/\lambda}$, with $\langle x^2\rangle = \lambda^2\int_0^\infty u^2e^{-u}du = 2\lambda^2$, and $e^{-1}$, $e^{-2}$, $e^{-5}$ give the percentages.

^der-b5-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^mod-b5-2-1|Model §B5.2.1]], [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^def-b5-2-1|Def. §B5.2.1]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-9|Theorem §B4.1.9]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.3: Mean Free Path in a Maxwellian Gas
> In a hard-sphere gas of one species with the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, the collision rate per molecule and the mean free path are
>
> $$
> R = \sqrt2\,n\sigma\langle v\rangle, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\langle v\rangle}{R} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2\,n\sigma} = \frac{k_BT}{\sqrt2\,p\,\sigma} ,
> $$
>
> with $\langle v\rangle = \sqrt{8k_BT/\pi m}$, and $\tau = \lambda/\langle v\rangle$. The factor $\sqrt2$ is exact: the mean *relative* speed of two molecules is exactly $\sqrt2\langle v\rangle$. With $\sigma = \pi(2r)^2$ this is the formula of level A ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-6|Theorem §A2.2.6]]), now derived without approximation.
>
> *Source: Blundell §8.3, eqs. (8.16)–(8.21) and note 5*

^thm-b5-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A molecule of velocity $\vec v$ meets molecules of velocity $\vec u$, density $n\,g(\vec u)\,d^3u$, at the relative speed $\lvert\vec v - \vec u\rvert$: in the rest frame of those targets it sweeps $\sigma\lvert\vec v - \vec u\rvert$ per unit time. Averaging over $\vec v$ too, with independent Maxwellian $g$ ([[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]]),
>
> $$
> R = n\sigma\iint\lvert\vec v - \vec u\rvert\,g(\vec u)\,g(\vec v)\,d^3u\,d^3v .
> $$
>
> Change variables to $\vec x = (\vec v - \vec u)/\sqrt2$, $\vec y = (\vec v + \vec u)/\sqrt2$: a rotation in $\mathbb R^6$, with Jacobian $1$ ([[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-17|452 Thm. §15.17]]). Since $\lvert\vec u\rvert^2 + \lvert\vec v\rvert^2 = \lvert\vec x\rvert^2 + \lvert\vec y\rvert^2$, the product of Gaussians is $g(\vec x)\,g(\vec y)$, and
>
> $$
> R = n\sigma\sqrt2\int\lvert\vec x\rvert\,g(\vec x)\,d^3x\int g(\vec y)\,d^3y = \sqrt2\,n\sigma\langle v\rangle .
> $$
>
> Then $\lambda = \langle v\rangle/R$, and $n = p/k_BT$ for the ideal gas. Level A's argument used $\langle v_{\text{rel}}^2\rangle = 2\langle v^2\rangle$, which holds for any isotropic distribution, and the approximation $\langle v_{\text{rel}}\rangle \approx \sqrt{\langle v_{\text{rel}}^2\rangle}$; for Maxwellian speeds the ratio of mean to rms is the same, $\sqrt{8/3\pi} = 0.92$, for relative and absolute motion, so the $\sqrt2$ survives exactly.

^der-b5-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Numbers to remember
> In air at room conditions molecules move at about $500\ \mathrm{m/s}$, are about $0.3$–$0.4\ \mathrm{nm}$ across and some $3\ \mathrm{nm}$ apart ($n^{-1/3}$ with $n = 2.5 \times 10^{25}\ \mathrm m^{-3}$); they travel $\lambda \approx 70\ \mathrm{nm}$, a couple of hundred diameters, between collisions and collide every $0.1$–$0.2\ \mathrm{ns}$. Since $\lambda \propto 1/p$ at fixed $T$, lowering the pressure a millionfold stretches the free path to centimetres. Schwartz's chaos estimate ([[§B5.4 Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^ex-b5-4-1|Example §B5.4.1]]) uses exactly these numbers: $\lambda/r \sim 10^3$, with $r \approx 0.1\ \mathrm{nm}$ the molecular radius.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, §3.1 · Blundell, Example 8.1*

## Transport coefficients

> [!definition] Definition §B5.2.2: Viscosity, Thermal Conductivity, Self-Diffusion Constant
> Near equilibrium, a gradient drives a flux of the quantity that is out of balance, and the flux is proportional to the gradient:
> - **Viscosity** $\eta$: in a flow $\langle u_x\rangle(z)$ with layers sliding over each other, the $x$-momentum carried across unit area of a plane $z = $ const per unit time (the **momentum flux**, equal to the shear stress $F/A$ up to sign) is $\Pi_z = -\eta\,\partial\langle u_x\rangle/\partial z$. Units $\mathrm{Pa\,s}$. (Fluids obeying this are "Newtonian".) The kinematic viscosity is $\nu = \eta/\rho$.
> - **Thermal conductivity** $\kappa$: the heat flux is $\vec J = -\kappa\nabla T$ (Fourier's law, [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-1|Law §A1.5.1]], in the local form of [[§B5.3 The Thermal Diffusion Equation#^law-b5-3-1|Law §B5.3.1]]). Units $\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}K^{-1}}$.
> - **Self-diffusion constant** $D$: labelled molecules of density $n^*$, otherwise identical to the rest of a uniform gas, have flux $\vec\Phi = -D\nabla n^*$ (Fick's law, [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^law-b4-2-3|Law §B4.2.3]]). Units $\mathrm{m^2/s}$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §9.1–9.3, eqs. (9.1)–(9.2), (9.14)–(9.15), (9.26)*

^def-b5-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.4: Transport Coefficients of a Dilute Gas (Mean-Free-Path Theory)
> In the gas of [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^mod-b5-2-1|Model §B5.2.1]], if a molecule carries the property of the place where it last collided, then
>
> $$
> \eta = \tfrac13\,nm\lambda\langle v\rangle, \qquad \kappa = \tfrac13\,C\lambda\langle v\rangle, \qquad D = \tfrac13\,\lambda\langle v\rangle ,
> $$
>
> where $C = nc_{\text{m}}$ is the heat capacity per unit volume ($c_{\text{m}}$ per molecule, $\tfrac32k_B$ for a monatomic gas). With $\lambda = 1/\sqrt2n\pi d^2$ and $\langle v\rangle = \sqrt{8k_BT/\pi m}$,
>
> $$
> \eta = \frac{2}{3\pi d^2}\sqrt{\frac{mk_BT}{\pi}}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{2}{3\pi d^2}\,c_{\text{m}}\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{\pi m}}, \qquad D = \frac{2}{3\pi nd^2}\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{\pi m}} .
> $$
>
> *Valid when* $L \gg \lambda \gg d$ ($L$ the size of the container or the scale of the gradients). The factor $\tfrac13$ is approximate; see [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^rem-b5-2-3|Remark: A better theory]].
> *Source: Blundell §9.1–9.3, eqs. (9.3)–(9.6), (9.16)–(9.19), (9.37)–(9.40), (9.7)*

^thm-b5-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *One computation for all three.* Let each molecule carry a quantity $Q$ whose local average $\langle Q\rangle(z)$ varies slowly with $z$. By [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]], the molecules crossing unit area of the plane $z = z_0$ per unit time with speed in $dv$ and direction at angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ to the $+z$ axis number $v\cos\theta\,nf(v)\,dv\,\tfrac12\sin\theta\,d\theta$, counted positive upward ($\theta < \pi/2$) and negative downward. Each last collided about $\lambda$ back along its path, at height $z_0 - \lambda\cos\theta$, and brings the value there,
>
> $$
> \langle Q\rangle(z_0 - \lambda\cos\theta) \approx \langle Q\rangle(z_0) - \lambda\cos\theta\,\frac{\partial\langle Q\rangle}{\partial z} .
> $$
>
> The first term carries no net flux, because as many molecules cross up as down ($\int_0^\pi\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = 0$). The second gives
>
> $$
> \text{flux of }Q = -\lambda\frac{\partial\langle Q\rangle}{\partial z}\cdot\frac n2\int_0^\infty vf(v)\,dv\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = -\frac13\,n\lambda\langle v\rangle\,\frac{\partial\langle Q\rangle}{\partial z} .
> $$
>
> *Viscosity:* $Q = mu_x$, so $\Pi_z = -\tfrac13nm\lambda\langle v\rangle\,\partial_z\langle u_x\rangle$. *Conduction:* $Q$ is the thermal energy of a molecule, with $\partial_z\langle Q\rangle = c_{\text{m}}\,\partial_zT$, so $J_z = -\tfrac13nc_{\text{m}}\lambda\langle v\rangle\,\partial_zT$. *Diffusion:* $Q = 1$ for a labelled molecule and $0$ otherwise, so $n\langle Q\rangle = n^*$ and $\Phi_z = -\tfrac13\lambda\langle v\rangle\,\partial_zn^*$. Compare with [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^def-b5-2-2|Def. §B5.2.2]]; substitute [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]] for $\lambda$ and [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]] for $\langle v\rangle$.
>
> *Where the approximations sit.* Using one length $\lambda$ for every molecule (instead of averaging over the exponential distribution of [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]], whose speed dependence is ignored); assuming each collision wipes out the previous velocity completely; and assuming the distribution of the crossing molecules is the equilibrium one, although the gradient itself distorts it. The condition $\lambda \gg d$ excludes three-body collisions; $\lambda \ll L$ makes molecules collide with each other rather than with the walls, and the linear expansion over a distance $\lambda$ needs gradients slow on that scale.

*Uses:* [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^mod-b5-2-1|Model §B5.2.1]], [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^def-b5-2-2|Def. §B5.2.2]], [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]], [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]], [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.5: How the Transport Coefficients Scale
> Within mean-free-path theory:
>
> | | pressure (fixed $T$) | temperature | mass and size |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $\eta$ | independent | $\propto T^{1/2}$ | $\propto m^{1/2}d^{-2}$ |
> | $\kappa$ | independent | $\propto T^{1/2}$ | $\propto m^{-1/2}d^{-2}$ |
> | $D$ | $\propto p^{-1}$ | $\propto T^{3/2}$ at fixed $p$ | $\propto m^{-1/2}d^{-2}$ |
>
> and the coefficients are linked by $\kappa = c_V\,\eta$ and $\rho D = \eta$, with $c_V = c_{\text{m}}/m$ the specific heat and $\rho = nm$ the density.
>
> *Source: Blundell §9.1–9.3, eqs. (9.20)–(9.21), (9.39), chapter summary*

^thm-b5-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The hard-sphere forms of [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]] for $\eta$ and $\kappa$ contain no $n$: more molecules carry momentum or energy, but each carries it a proportionally shorter distance, $\lambda \propto 1/n$. Their temperature dependence is that of $\langle v\rangle \propto T^{1/2}$. $D$ has the factor $1/n = k_BT/p$, giving $T^{1/2}\cdot T/p$. The ratios are read off from the forms $\tfrac13(\ldots)\lambda\langle v\rangle$: $\kappa/\eta = c_{\text{m}}/m$ and $\eta/D = nm$.

^der-b5-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Viscosity that does not depend on pressure
> Maxwell's prediction that the viscosity of a gas is independent of its density surprised him, and he confirmed it by measuring the damping of a torsion pendulum between fixed discs. It explains an older puzzle: Boyle (1660) found that pumping air out of a vessel did not change the damping of a pendulum inside — his pump never got near the pressures at which $\lambda$ becomes comparable to the apparatus. The independence holds over a wide range and fails at both ends: at high density ($\lambda$ approaching $d$) and at low pressure, when $\lambda$ exceeds the container size $L$ and molecules carry momentum or heat from wall to wall without colliding (a **Knudsen gas**). A Pirani vacuum gauge works in this second regime: below about $1\ \mathrm{mbar}$ the heat lost by a hot wire is proportional to the pressure. Also unlike liquids, gases become *more* viscous when heated, $\eta \propto T^{1/2}$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §9.1–9.2 (boxes on measurement), Exercise 9.3*

^rem-b5-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: A better theory
> The mean-free-path argument gets the dependences right and the numbers only roughly. Chapman and Enskog solved the Boltzmann equation ([[§B5.4 Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^mod-b5-4-3|Model §B5.4.3]]) for hard spheres; their results, quoted by Blundell without derivation, replace the factor $\frac{2}{3\pi} = 0.212$ in the hard-sphere forms by $\frac{5}{16} = 0.313$ for $\eta$, $\frac{25}{32} = 0.781$ for $\kappa$ and $\frac38 = 0.375$ for $D$, so that $\rho D = \tfrac65\eta$. For conduction the correction is largest: fast molecules carry more energy *and* have longer free paths, so $\kappa = c_V\eta$ is replaced by Eucken's formula
>
> $$
> \kappa = \tfrac14(9\gamma - 5)\,c_V\,\eta \qquad\bigl(= \tfrac52c_V\eta \text{ for a monatomic gas, } \gamma = \tfrac53\bigr),
> $$
>
> with $\gamma = c_p/c_V$ and $c_V$ the specific heat. The **Prandtl number** $\Pr = \eta c_p/\kappa = \nu/(\kappa/\rho c_p)$, the ratio of the diffusivities of momentum and heat, is then $\tfrac23$ for a monatomic gas, close to the measured values for many gases (simple theory, $\kappa = c_V\eta$, would give $\Pr = \gamma = \tfrac53$).
>
> *Source: Blundell §9.4, eqs. (9.42)–(9.49); §10.6, eqs. (10.59)–(10.60)*

^rem-b5-2-3

> [!caution] Caution: Specific heats in Eucken's formula
> In $\kappa = (\tfrac52c_V' + c_V'')\,\eta$ and $\kappa = \tfrac14(9\gamma - 5)\,c_V\eta$, the heat capacities must be per unit *mass* (translational part $c_V'$, internal part $c_V''$) for the units to match; Blundell introduces them as molar heat capacities (§9.4, eq. (9.42)), which would need a division by the molar mass. In the same chapter, the bullet "$\kappa \propto T^{1/2}$" concludes "hence $\eta \propto T^{1/2}$", a slip for $\kappa$.

^cau-b5-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: The random-walk view of diffusion
> A diffusing molecule is a random walker ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]]) whose steps are its free paths, in random directions in $d = 3$, one per mean free time. Those steps are not of fixed length: by [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]] their mean square is $\langle x^2\rangle = 2\lambda^2$. So $D = \langle x^2\rangle/6\tau = \lambda^2/3\tau = \tfrac13\lambda\langle v\rangle$, the kinetic-theory value, and not $\tfrac16\lambda\langle v\rangle$ (fixed steps) or Schwartz's one-dimensional $\tfrac12\lambda\langle v\rangle$ ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^cau-b4-2-2|Caution: Dimension-dependent factors]]). The agreement is partly luck — both arguments ignore the spread of speeds — but it shows that the $\tfrac13$ is the $\tfrac1d$ of three dimensions times the $2$ of exponential free paths.

^rem-b5-2-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B5.2.1: An Ultra-High Vacuum
> Nitrogen ($d = 0.37\ \mathrm{nm}$, $\sigma = \pi d^2 = 4.3 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{m^2}$) at $300\ \mathrm K$ has, by [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]],
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{k_BT}{\sqrt2\,p\sigma} = \frac{4.14 \times 10^{-21}\ \mathrm J}{\sqrt2\,(10^5\ \mathrm{Pa})(4.3 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{m^2})} = 6.8 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm m
> $$
>
> at atmospheric pressure. In an ultra-high-vacuum chamber at $10^{-10}\ \mathrm{mbar} = 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{Pa}$, the free path is $10^{13}$ times longer, $680\ \mathrm{km}$, and the mean free time $\lambda/\langle v\rangle = 6.8 \times 10^5\ \mathrm m/(476\ \mathrm{m/s}) = 1.4 \times 10^3\ \mathrm s$, about 24 minutes. In a chamber $0.5\ \mathrm m$ across a molecule hits the walls about a million times for each collision with another molecule: the gas is a Knudsen gas, and the formulas of [[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]] do not apply. Even at $10^{-6}\ \mathrm{mbar}$, $\lambda = 68\ \mathrm m$, still far beyond the chamber.
>
> *Source: Blundell, Example 8.1, Exercise 8.1*

^ex-b5-2-1

> [!example] Example §B5.2.2: The Size of an Argon Atom from Its Viscosity
> Argon ($m = 39.95\ \mathrm u$) has $\eta = 3.5 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}$ at $500\ \mathrm K$ and $8.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}$ at $2000\ \mathrm K$, independent of pressure. Solve the hard-sphere viscosity for $d$: $d^2 = c\,\sqrt{mk_BT/\pi}/\eta$, with $c = \frac{2}{3\pi}$ (simple theory) or $\frac5{16}$ (Chapman–Enskog). At $500\ \mathrm K$, $\sqrt{mk_BT/\pi} = 1.21 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$, and
>
> $$
> d_{\text{simple}} = 0.27\ \mathrm{nm}, \qquad d_{\text{CE}} = 0.33\ \mathrm{nm};
> $$
>
> at $2000\ \mathrm K$ the Chapman–Enskog value is $0.31\ \mathrm{nm}$. The diameter $0.34\ \mathrm{nm}$ deduced from the density of solid argon agrees well with the better theory. And the viscosity grows by $8.0/3.5 = 2.29$ while $T^{1/2}$ only doubles: the effective diameter shrinks as the atoms collide harder, the softness that the hard-sphere model leaves out.
>
> *Source: Blundell, Exercise 9.5 (data); §9.1, §9.4; numbers computed here*

^ex-b5-2-2

> [!example] Example §B5.2.3: Argon at STP — From Viscosity to Conductivity and Free Path
> At $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ and $1\ \mathrm{atm}$, argon has $\eta = 2.1 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}$. Its specific heat is $c_V = \tfrac32k_B/m = 312\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}K^{-1}}$, so Eucken's monatomic formula ([[§B5.2 Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^rem-b5-2-3|Remark: A better theory]]) predicts
>
> $$
> \kappa = \tfrac52c_V\eta = \tfrac52(312)(2.1 \times 10^{-5})\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}K^{-1}} = 0.0164\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}K^{-1}},
> $$
>
> against the measured $0.016$; simple theory, $\kappa = c_V\eta$, would be $2.5$ times too small. The Chapman–Enskog viscosity gives $d = 0.36\ \mathrm{nm}$, hence $\lambda = k_BT/\sqrt2p\pi d^2 = 63\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $\tau = \lambda/\langle v\rangle = 63\ \mathrm{nm}/(380\ \mathrm{m/s}) = 1.7 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm s$ — in line with the cruder level-A estimate for argon at STP ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^ex-a2-2-3|Example §A2.2.3]]), but now from a measured transport property rather than an assumed atomic radius.
>
> *Source: Blundell, Exercises 9.2 and 9.4 (data); numbers computed here*
