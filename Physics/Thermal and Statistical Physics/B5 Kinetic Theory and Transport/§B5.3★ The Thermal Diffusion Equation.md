Level A treated conduction through slabs in the steady state — Fourier's law ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-1|Law §A1.5.1]]), resistances in series ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]), a spherical shell ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^rem-a1-5-3|Remark: When the area varies: a spherical shell]]) — and the cooling of a whole body as a single exponential ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^thm-a1-5-4|Theorem §A1.5.4]]). It did not ask how the temperature *inside* a body changes in time. Combining Fourier's law with conservation of energy gives a partial differential equation for $T(\vec r, t)$, the **thermal diffusion** (heat) **equation**, the same equation that governs particle diffusion ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]]). This section derives it and solves it in the standard situations: a pulse of heat spreading as a Gaussian, two bodies brought into contact, temperature waves penetrating the ground, steady states, and a sphere heating up — from which Newton's exponential cooling emerges as the long-time limit.

*Notation.* As in [[§B5.2★ Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients|§B5.2]], $\kappa$ is the thermal conductivity (level A's $k$) and $C$ a heat capacity per unit volume, not the extensive $C$ of the rest of level B.

## The equation

> [!definition] Definition §B5.3.1: Heat Flux; Heat Capacity per Volume; Thermal Diffusivity
> The **heat flux** $\vec J(\vec r, t)$ is the heat crossing unit area per unit time (W/m$^2$), along the direction of flow; level A's heat current through an area $A$ is $P = JA$ for uniform flow ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^def-a1-5-1|Def. §A1.5.1]]). The **heat capacity per unit volume** of a material is $C = \rho c$, with $\rho$ the density and $c$ the specific heat. The **thermal diffusivity** is
>
> $$
> D_{\text{th}} = \frac{\kappa}{C} = \frac{\kappa}{\rho c}\qquad(\mathrm{m^2/s}),
> $$
>
> with $\kappa$ the thermal conductivity (level A's $k$).
>
> *Source: Blundell §9.2, §10.1, eqs. (9.15), (10.1)–(10.8)*

^def-b5-3-1

> [!law] Law §B5.3.1: Fourier's Law, Local Form
> In an isotropic medium the heat flux at each point is
>
> $$
> \vec J = -\kappa\,\nabla T .
> $$
>
> This is the three-dimensional, local form of [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-1|Law §A1.5.1]]: for one-dimensional flow through a cross-section $A$ it gives back $P = -\kappa A\,dT/dx$. For a dilute gas $\kappa$ was computed in [[§B5.2★ Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]]; for solids and liquids it is measured.
>
> *Valid when:* the medium is in local equilibrium with temperature gradients small over the mean free path of the heat carriers; no convection or radiation; isotropic material (in crystals $\kappa$ becomes a tensor); for a gas, container large compared with the mean free path ([[§B5.2★ Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^rem-b5-2-2|Remark: Viscosity that does not depend on pressure]]).
> *Source: Blundell §9.2, eq. (9.15); §10.1, eq. (10.1) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, §4*

^law-b5-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.2: Thermal Diffusion Equation
> In a body at rest, with constant $\kappa$ and $C$, in which heat is generated at the rate $H(\vec r, t)$ per unit volume,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = D_{\text{th}}\,\nabla^2T + \frac HC .
> $$
>
> Without sources it is the diffusion equation of [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]] for the temperature, so all its solutions carry over with $D \to D_{\text{th}}$; in a steady state, $\nabla^2T = -H/\kappa$, and $D_{\text{th}}$ drops out.
>
> *Source: Blundell §10.1, eqs. (10.4)–(10.8); §10.7, eqs. (10.61)–(10.63) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, eq. (40)*

^thm-b5-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take any fixed region $V$ with boundary $S$. Its thermal energy is $\int_VCT\,dV$ (up to an additive constant), and energy is conserved: it changes by the heat flowing out through $S$ and the heat generated inside,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\int_VCT\,dV = -\oint_S\vec J\cdot d\vec S + \int_VH\,dV = \int_V\bigl(-\nabla\cdot\vec J + H\bigr)\,dV ,
> $$
>
> by the divergence theorem ([[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-28-1|452 Thm. §28.1]]). Since $V$ is arbitrary and the integrands continuous, $C\,\partial_tT = -\nabla\cdot\vec J + H$, the argument of [[§29 Conservation of Mass and Laplace's Equation#^thm-29-1|452 Thm. §29.1]]. Insert $\vec J = -\kappa\nabla T$ with constant $\kappa$, and divide by $C$. "At rest" means no work is done and no convection carries energy: for solids and liquids $C$ is then essentially $\rho c_p$, for a gas confined at constant volume $\rho c_V$.

^der-b5-3-2

## Heat spreading in time

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.3: A Pulse of Heat; Two Bodies in Contact
> In a long uniform rod (or a slab, with flow along $x$ only), initially at $T_0$:
> 1. *Pulse.* If heat $Q$ per unit cross-sectional area is released in the plane $x = 0$ at $t = 0$, then
>
> $$
> T(x, t) - T_0 = \frac{Q}{C}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D_{\text{th}}t}}\;e^{-x^2/4D_{\text{th}}t} :
> $$
>
> the heat spreads as a Gaussian of width $\sqrt{2D_{\text{th}}t}$, the peak falls as $t^{-1/2}$, and the total heat $\int C(T - T_0)\,dx = Q$ is conserved.
> 2. *Contact.* If instead the half $x < 0$ is at $T_1$ and the half $x > 0$ at $T_2$ when they are put in contact at $t = 0$,
>
> $$
> T(x, t) = T_1 + \frac{T_2 - T_1}{2}\Bigl[1 + \operatorname{erf}\Bigl(\frac{x}{\sqrt{4D_{\text{th}}t}}\Bigr)\Bigr], \qquad \operatorname{erf}(u) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^ue^{-s^2}ds .
> $$
>
> The interface jumps at once to the mean $\tfrac12(T_1 + T_2)$ and stays there; the heat flux across it, of magnitude $\kappa\lvert T_2 - T_1\rvert/\sqrt{4\pi D_{\text{th}}t}$ from the hotter side, is infinite at first and decays as $t^{-1/2}$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, eqs. (30)–(31), (40) (Green's function, transferred to $T$) · Blundell §10.8 (the analogy) · solutions worked out here*

^thm-b5-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) The one-dimensional Green's function $G(x, t) = (4\pi D_{\text{th}}t)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/4D_{\text{th}}t}$ solves the equation, has unit integral and tends to $\delta(x)$ ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]). $T - T_0$ also solves it (constants do), and its initial value is the deposited energy density divided by $C$, $(Q/C)\,\delta(x)$. Conservation: $\int G\,dx = 1$.
>
> (2) Superpose Green's functions over the initial profile ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]): $T(x, t) = T_1\int_{-\infty}^0G(x - x', t)\,dx' + T_2\int_0^\infty G(x - x', t)\,dx'$. With $s = (x' - x)/\sqrt{4D_{\text{th}}t}$, $\int_0^\infty G(x - x', t)\,dx' = \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-x/\sqrt{4D_{\text{th}}t}}^\infty e^{-s^2}ds = \frac12\bigl[1 + \operatorname{erf}\bigl(x/\sqrt{4D_{\text{th}}t}\bigr)\bigr]$, and the first integral is $1$ minus this. At $x = 0$, $\operatorname{erf}(0) = 0$. The flux is $-\kappa\,\partial_xT$ at $x = 0$, with $\frac{d}{du}\operatorname{erf}(u) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}e^{-u^2}$; its sign says heat flows from the hotter side.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.4: Temperature Waves; Thermal Skin Depth
> If the surface $x = 0$ of a half-space $x \ge 0$ is held at $T(0, t) = T_0 + \Delta T\cos\Omega t$, the periodic temperature inside (after transients have died away) is
>
> $$
> T(x, t) = T_0 + \Delta T\,e^{-x/\delta}\cos\Bigl(\Omega t - \frac x\delta\Bigr), \qquad \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2D_{\text{th}}}{\Omega}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\kappa}{\Omega C}} .
> $$
>
> The oscillation is damped by $e^{-1}$ every **skin depth** $\delta$ and delayed by $x/\delta$ radians; faster oscillations penetrate less, $\delta \propto \Omega^{-1/2}$.
>
> *Source: Blundell, Example 10.1, eqs. (10.10)–(10.22)*

^thm-b5-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write the boundary value as $T_0 + \operatorname{Re}\bigl(\Delta T\,e^{i\Omega t}\bigr)$ and look for $T = T_0 + \operatorname{Re}\bigl(\Delta T\,e^{i\Omega t}e^{-\beta x}\bigr)$ (the complex-exponential method of [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-4|WO Theorem §B1.1.4]], valid because the equation is linear and real). Substituting, $i\Omega = D_{\text{th}}\beta^2$, so $\beta = \pm(1 + i)\sqrt{\Omega/2D_{\text{th}}} = \pm(1 + i)/\delta$. The root with $\operatorname{Re}\beta < 0$ grows without bound into the material and is discarded; with $\beta = (1 + i)/\delta$, $\operatorname{Re}\bigl(e^{i\Omega t}e^{-x/\delta}e^{-ix/\delta}\bigr) = e^{-x/\delta}\cos(\Omega t - x/\delta)$, which equals $\cos\Omega t$ at $x = 0$. The constant $T_0$ is the $\Omega = 0$ part of the boundary value. Blundell does the same with $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$, summing over $\pm\Omega$.

*Uses:* [[§B5.3★ The Thermal Diffusion Equation#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-4|WO Theorem §B1.1.4]]

![[ph-th-b5-3-2.svg]]
*A temperature wave in the ground, at four instants a quarter period apart. All profiles stay inside the envelope $\pm e^{-x/\delta}$ (dashed); at a depth of $\pi\delta$ the temperature swings in antiphase with the surface, but with only $4\,\%$ of its amplitude.*

> [!caution] Caution: Heat that travels faster than its carriers
> The wave crests of [[§B5.3★ The Thermal Diffusion Equation#^thm-b5-3-4|Theorem §B5.3.4]] move at $\Omega\delta = \sqrt{2D_{\text{th}}\Omega}$, without bound as $\Omega \to \infty$, and the solutions of [[§B5.3★ The Thermal Diffusion Equation#^thm-b5-3-3|Theorem §B5.3.3]] are nonzero everywhere at every $t > 0$: the diffusion equation propagates disturbances instantaneously, though with exponentially small amplitude far away. Since heat is carried by molecules (or electrons, or phonons) of finite speed, the equation cannot be exact. It fails on time scales comparable with the carriers' relaxation time $\tau$; there Fourier's law is replaced by $\vec J + \tau\,\partial_t\vec J = -\kappa\nabla T$, giving $\partial_tT + \tau\,\partial_t^2T = D_{\text{th}}\nabla^2T$, with finite propagation speed. Blundell derives this as an exercise (Exercise 10.6; the same structure for particles in [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-5|Theorem §B5.4.5]]). In ordinary matter $\tau \sim 10^{-10}$ s or less, and the correction is almost never needed.

## Steady states and the approach to them

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.5: Steady States
> In a steady state $\nabla^2T = -H/\kappa$, and with given boundary temperatures the solution is unique. In symmetric geometries without sources:
> - *slab* $0 \le x \le L$, faces at $T_1, T_2$: $T$ linear, flux $J = \kappa(T_1 - T_2)/L$ (level A, [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-1|Law §A1.5.1]]);
> - *spherical symmetry*: $T = A + B/r$; the outward heat current through every sphere is $P = 4\pi\kappa B$. A sphere of radius $a$ kept at $T_0$ in an infinite medium at $T_1$ far away loses $P = 4\pi\kappa a\,(T_0 - T_1)$;
> - *cylindrical symmetry*: $T = A + B\ln r$; the heat current per unit length is $-2\pi\kappa B$. Between coaxial cylinders of radii $a < b$ at $T_a$, $T_b$, it is $2\pi\kappa(T_a - T_b)/\ln(b/a)$.
>
> With a uniform source $H$ in a slab or bar of length $L$ whose ends are held at $T_0$: $T = T_0 + \frac{H}{2\kappa}x(L - x)$, highest at the centre, $T_0 + HL^2/8\kappa$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §10.3–10.4, Examples 10.2, 10.3, 10.5, 10.7; §9.2 (hot-wire method), eqs. (9.22)–(9.25); Exercise 10.8*

^thm-b5-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Set $\partial_tT = 0$ in [[§B5.3★ The Thermal Diffusion Equation#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]]. *Uniqueness:* the difference of two solutions with the same boundary values is harmonic and vanishes on the boundary, hence vanishes ([[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^ex-28-1|452 Ex. §28.1]]).
>
> *Spheres.* For a radial function the Laplacian is $\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\bigl(r^2\frac{dT}{dr}\bigr)$. If it vanishes, $r^2T' = -B$ is constant, and $T = A + B/r$. The heat current through the sphere of radius $r$ is $P = -\kappa T'\cdot4\pi r^2 = 4\pi\kappa B$, the same for every $r$, as energy conservation in a steady state requires. For the sphere in a medium, $T(\infty) = T_1$ and $T(a) = T_0$ give $A = T_1$, $B = a(T_0 - T_1)$.
>
> *Cylinders.* Here the Laplacian is $\frac1r\frac{d}{dr}\bigl(r\frac{dT}{dr}\bigr)$; $rT' = B$ gives $T = A + B\ln r$, and the current per unit length is $-\kappa T'\cdot2\pi r = -2\pi\kappa B$. Fitting $T_a$ and $T_b$ gives $B = -(T_a - T_b)/\ln(b/a)$.
>
> *Source term.* In one dimension $T'' = -H/\kappa$ integrates to $T = \alpha x + \beta - Hx^2/2\kappa$; $T(0) = T(L) = T_0$ fixes $\beta = T_0$ and $\alpha = HL/2\kappa$.

^der-b5-3-5

*Uses:* [[§B5.3★ The Thermal Diffusion Equation#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]], [[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^ex-28-1|452 Ex. §28.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Measuring κ with a hot wire
> The cylindrical solution is a measurement: a wire of radius $a$ along the axis of a tube of radius $b$ held at $T_b$, heated electrically with power $Q$ per unit length, settles at $T_a$, and $\kappa = \dfrac{Q\ln(b/a)}{2\pi(T_a - T_b)}$. At low pressure, where $\lambda$ exceeds the dimensions of the tube, $\kappa$ depends on the pressure, and the same device becomes the Pirani vacuum gauge ([[§B5.2★ Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^rem-b5-2-2|Remark: Viscosity that does not depend on pressure]]).
>
> *Source: Blundell §9.2, box "Measurement of thermal conductivity"*

^rem-b5-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.6: Heating or Cooling of a Sphere
> A sphere of radius $a$ and diffusivity $D_{\text{th}}$, uniformly at $T_0$, has its surface held at $T_1$ from $t = 0$ on. Then for $0 < r \le a$, $t > 0$,
>
> $$
> T(r, t) = T_1 + \frac{2a}{\pi}(T_1 - T_0)\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n}{n}\,\frac{\sin(n\pi r/a)}{r}\,e^{-n^2t/t_a}, \qquad t_a = \frac{a^2}{\pi^2D_{\text{th}}} ,
> $$
>
> and at the centre
>
> $$
> T(0, t) = T_1 + 2(T_1 - T_0)\sum_{n=1}^\infty(-1)^ne^{-n^2t/t_a} \;\approx\; T_1 - 2(T_1 - T_0)\,e^{-t/t_a} \quad (t \gtrsim t_a).
> $$
>
> At late times the whole sphere approaches $T_1$ exponentially with the single time constant $t_a \propto a^2$: the form of Newton's law of cooling ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-3|Law §A1.5.3]], [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^thm-a1-5-4|Theorem §A1.5.4]]), here emerging from conduction as the slowest mode outlasting the others.
>
> *Source: Blundell, Example 10.4, eqs. (10.32)–(10.54)*

^thm-b5-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Reduction to one dimension.* For radial $T$, $\partial_tT = D_{\text{th}}\,r^{-2}\partial_r(r^2\partial_rT)$. Put $T = T_1 + B(r, t)/r$, suggested by the steady solution $A + B/r$. Then $\partial_tT = r^{-1}\partial_tB$ and $r^2\partial_rT = -B + r\,\partial_rB$, so $\partial_r(r^2\partial_rT) = r\,\partial_r^2B$, and
>
> $$
> \partial_tB = D_{\text{th}}\,\partial_r^2B, \qquad B(0, t) = 0, \quad B(a, t) = 0, \quad B(r, 0) = r(T_0 - T_1) ,
> $$
>
> a rod of length $a$ with both ends held at zero ($B(0, t) = 0$ because $T$ is finite at the centre).
>
> *Modes.* Separated solutions satisfying both end conditions are $\sin(n\pi r/a)\,e^{-D_{\text{th}}(n\pi/a)^2t}$, $n = 1, 2, \dots$, each decaying at its own rate. Expand the initial value in them: by orthogonality of the sines on $[0, a]$ ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-1|WO Theorem §B4.1.1]], [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-2|WO Theorem §B4.1.2]]),
>
> $$
> A_n = \frac2a\int_0^a r(T_0 - T_1)\sin\frac{n\pi r}{a}\,dr = \frac2a(T_0 - T_1)\frac{a^2(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi} = \frac{2a}{n\pi}(T_1 - T_0)(-1)^n ,
> $$
>
> integrating by parts. Divide $B$ by $r$; at the centre $\sin(n\pi r/a)/r \to n\pi/a$. For $t \ge t_a$ the $n = 2$ term is smaller than the first by $e^{-3t/t_a} \le 0.05$, and the first term alone remains.

^der-b5-3-6

*Uses:* [[§B5.3★ The Thermal Diffusion Equation#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]], [[§B5.3★ The Thermal Diffusion Equation#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]], [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-1|WO Theorem §B4.1.1]], [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-2|WO Theorem §B4.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Newton's law of cooling, and convection
> Level A stated Newton's law of cooling as an empirical law for a body losing heat to a fluid, the loss proportional to the area and to the temperature difference ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-3|Law §A1.5.3]]). Conduction theory explains half of it: inside a body the slow mode dominates after a while, giving a single exponential with a time constant $\propto a^2/D_{\text{th}}$ — for a cooling or warming body a "low-pass filter" that keeps only the lowest decay rate. The other half, the heat transfer coefficient $h$ at the surface, is set mostly by convection in the surrounding fluid and is not a material constant: Blundell notes that the law holds for forced convection, while for free convection $h$ grows with $\Delta T$ (as $\Delta T^{1/4}$ in laminar flow). Conduction alone governs solids and fluids kept still.
>
> *Source: Blundell §10.5, Example 10.6 and note 3*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B5.3.1: A Cellar under 3 m of Limestone
> Limestone has $\kappa = 1.6\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}K^{-1}}$ and $C = 2.5 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}m^{-3}}$, so $D_{\text{th}} = 6.4 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$. Treat the ceiling as the top of a half-space ([[§B5.3★ The Thermal Diffusion Equation#^thm-b5-3-4|Theorem §B5.3.4]]):
> - *daily cycle*, $\Omega = 2\pi/(1\ \mathrm d) = 7.27 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm s^{-1}$: $\delta = \sqrt{2D_{\text{th}}/\Omega} = 0.13\ \mathrm m$. At $3\ \mathrm m$ the $10\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ daily swing is reduced by $e^{-3/0.13} = 1.5 \times 10^{-10}$: nothing reaches the cellar;
> - *annual cycle*: $\Omega$ is $365$ times smaller, so $\delta = 0.13\ \mathrm m\times\sqrt{365} = 2.5\ \mathrm m$. The $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ annual swing arrives reduced by $e^{-3/2.5} = 0.31$, as about $\pm6\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, and delayed by $3/2.5 = 1.18$ rad, which is $1.18/2\pi$ of a year, about 69 days.
>
> If the coldest month outside is January, the cellar is coldest about $2\tfrac14$ months later, in late March to early April, and warmest in autumn. (The half-space solution ignores the cellar beneath; it fixes the orders of magnitude.)
>
> *Source: Blundell, Exercise 10.1 (data); numbers computed here*

^ex-b5-3-1

> [!example] Example §B5.3.2: Why Small Animals Run Hot and Bacteria Stay Small
> *Heat loss.* An animal of radius $a$ keeps its surface at $T_0$ in still air or water of conductivity $\kappa$ at $T_1$. By [[§B5.3★ The Thermal Diffusion Equation#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]] it loses heat by conduction at the rate $4\pi\kappa a(T_0 - T_1)$: proportional to the radius, while the heat it produces scales with its volume, $a^3$. The ratio of loss to production goes as $1/a^2$, so a small animal must burn food much faster per unit mass to stay warm.
>
> *Particle uptake.* The same mathematics governs particles ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]]). A sphere of radius $a$ that absorbs every molecule reaching it, in a medium where the concentration far away is $n_0$, has the steady profile $n(r) = n_0(1 - a/r)$, inward flux $Dn_0/a$ at its surface, and absorption rate
>
> $$
> 4\pi a^2\cdot\frac{Dn_0}{a} = 4\pi D\,a\,n_0 .
> $$
>
> A bacterium can take up oxygen at most at this rate, $\propto a$, but consumes it in proportion to its volume, $\propto a^3$: beyond some radius diffusion cannot feed it, which caps the size of single cells. (Blundell's eq. (10.71) omits the factor $D$.)
>
> *Source: Blundell, Examples 10.5 and 10.8*

^ex-b5-3-2

> [!example] Example §B5.3.3: Cooking a Spherical Chicken
> Model a chicken as a sphere of radius $a = 7\ \mathrm{cm}$ (mass $1.4\ \mathrm{kg}$ at the density of water) with the thermal properties of water, $\kappa = 0.6\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}K^{-1}}$ and $C = 4.19 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}m^{-3}}$ ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-1|Law §A1.5.1]], [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry|§A1.3]]): $D_{\text{th}} = 1.43 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$ and
>
> $$
> t_a = \frac{a^2}{\pi^2D_{\text{th}}} = \frac{(0.07\ \mathrm m)^2}{\pi^2\,(1.43 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2/s})} = 3.5 \times 10^3\ \mathrm s \approx 58\ \mathrm{min} .
> $$
>
> Put into an oven at $T_1 = 180\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ from $T_0 = 5\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, with the surface assumed to follow the oven at once, the centre reaches $75\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ — $40\,\%$ of the way — when $2e^{-t/t_a} = 0.6$ ([[§B5.3★ The Thermal Diffusion Equation#^thm-b5-3-6|Theorem §B5.3.6]]), at $t = t_a\ln(10/3) = 1.2\,t_a \approx 70\ \mathrm{min}$; reaching $90\,\%$ of the way would take $t_a\ln20 = 3.0\,t_a$ (Blundell, Exercise 10.3). The time scales as $a^2$, so as $m^{2/3}$ with the mass: doubling the bird lengthens the roasting by $2^{2/3} = 1.6$, not 2. Blundell compares this with the cookbook rule "40 minutes per kilogram plus 30 minutes": the two agree for ordinary birds, and the rule fails badly for small pieces, which cook in a stir-fry in seconds.

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The derivation of the equation is the divergence theorem ([[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-28-1|452 Thm. §28.1]]) and the continuity equation ([[§29 Conservation of Mass and Laplace's Equation#^thm-29-1|452 Thm. §29.1]]); uniqueness of steady states is the uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem ([[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^ex-28-1|452 Ex. §28.1]]), and the spherical solution is the mathematics of the Coulomb potential, $A + B/r$.
> - The sphere is solved by a Fourier sine series ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-2|WO Theorem §B4.1.2]]), the same separation into modes as for a vibrating string ([[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String|WO §B4.2]]) — but each mode decays instead of oscillating, because the equation is first order in time. The thermal skin depth is the analogue of the electromagnetic skin depth in a conductor ([[§B10.3★ Conductors and Dispersion#^thm-b10-3-3|EM Theorem §B10.3.3]]), where in a good conductor the field obeys a diffusion equation too ([[§B10.3★ Conductors and Dispersion#^rem-b10-3-1|EM Remark: In a good conductor the field diffuses]]).
> - The particle version of every solution here belongs to [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion|§B4.2]]; the finite-speed correction to Fourier's law follows from the relaxation-time Boltzmann equation of [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-5|Theorem §B5.4.5]].
> - Level A's slabs in series ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]) are the steady states of a piecewise-uniform rod: $J$ is continuous across each interface and $T$ is linear within each layer.
