Statistical mechanics never asks what each of $10^{23}$ molecules is doing; it asks how likely each possibility is, and then averages. Level A met one probability distribution, the distribution of molecular speeds ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^def-a2-4-1|Def. §A2.4.1]]), and averaged over it with Gaussian integrals quoted for the purpose ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^der-a2-4-1|derivation of Theorem §A2.4.1]]). This section sets up the general language — distributions, moments, independence, generating functions — and proves the mathematical facts the rest of level B leans on: the Gaussian integrals and moments, the gamma function and Stirling's formula, and the passage from the binomial and Poisson distributions to the Gaussian. Sums of many independent variables, and the central limit theorem, are the subject of [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion|§B4.2]].

*Notation.* Averages are written $\langle x\rangle$ (Blundell, Schwartz); level A wrote $\bar v$. $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is the Gaussian of mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$.

## Distributions and moments

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.1: Probability Distribution; Expectation Value
> A **discrete** random variable $x$ takes values $x_i$ with probabilities $P_i \ge 0$; a **continuous** one has a **probability density** $P(x) \ge 0$, such that the probability of finding $x$ in $[x_1, x_2]$ is $\int_{x_1}^{x_2}P(x)\,dx$. Either is **normalized**,
>
> $$
> \sum_i P_i = 1, \qquad \int P(x)\,dx = 1 .
> $$
>
> The **expectation value** (mean, average) of a function $g$ of $x$ is
>
> $$
> \langle g(x)\rangle = \sum_i g(x_i)\,P_i \qquad\text{or}\qquad \langle g(x)\rangle = \int g(x)\,P(x)\,dx .
> $$
>
> A density has the units of $1/x$ and becomes a probability only when integrated over an interval: the probability of any exact value is zero ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^cau-a2-4-1|Caution: f(v) is a density, not a probability]]). The speed distribution $f(v)$ of level A is one example; $x$ may also be a vector, with $\int P(\vec x)\,d^3x = 1$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, §1 · Blundell §3.1–3.2 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §14*

^def-b4-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.2: Mean, Variance, Standard Deviation; Moments
> For a distribution with mean $\mu = \langle x\rangle$:
> - the **variance** is the mean squared deviation $\sigma^2 = \langle (x - \mu)^2\rangle$, and the **standard deviation** $\sigma$ its square root, the rms spread about the mean;
> - the $n$th **central moment** is $\langle (x - \mu)^n\rangle$; the normalized ones $M_n = \langle (x - \mu)^n\rangle/\sigma^n$ are dimensionless. $M_3$ is the **skewness** (asymmetry about the mean), $M_4$ the **kurtosis** (weight of the tails).
>
> When $\mu = 0$, $\sigma$ is the root-mean-square value $x_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle}$; this is why the velocity components of a gas, whose mean is zero, are characterised by $v_{\text{rms}}$ ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^def-a2-2-1|Def. §A2.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, eqs. (7)–(14), (32)–(36) · Blundell §3.4, note 3*

^def-b4-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.1: Variance Identity; Linear Change of Variable
> For any distribution with finite variance,
>
> $$
> \sigma^2 = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2 .
> $$
>
> If $y = ax + b$ with constants $a, b$, then $\langle y\rangle = a\langle x\rangle + b$ and $\sigma_y = \lvert a\rvert\,\sigma_x$: a shift moves the distribution without changing its width.
>
> *Source: Blundell §3.3–3.5, eqs. (3.16), (3.22), (3.29) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §14.2*

^thm-b4-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Expectation is linear, because sums and integrals are: $\langle ag + bh\rangle = a\langle g\rangle + b\langle h\rangle$, and $\langle 1\rangle = 1$ by normalization. Hence $\langle (x - \mu)^2\rangle = \langle x^2\rangle - 2\mu\langle x\rangle + \mu^2 = \langle x^2\rangle - \mu^2$. For $y = ax + b$: $\langle y\rangle = a\mu + b$, and $y - \langle y\rangle = a(x - \mu)$, so $\sigma_y^2 = a^2\sigma_x^2$.

^der-b4-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-2|Def. §B4.1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: The width is not always a 68 % interval
> - For a Gaussian, 68 % of the probability lies within $\mu \pm \sigma$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]]). For other shapes it does not: a molecule equally likely anywhere in a box of length $L$ has $\sigma = L/\sqrt{12} = 0.29L$, and only $2\sigma/L = 58\,\%$ of the probability lies within $\pm\sigma$ of the centre (Schwartz, Lecture 1, §1.1).
> - Blundell writes $\sigma_y = a\sigma_x$ (eq. 3.30); with $a < 0$ the absolute value is needed, since a standard deviation is never negative.

## Independence and sums

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.3: Independence; Covariance and Correlation
> Two random variables are **independent** if their joint density factorizes, $P(u, v) = P_u(u)\,P_v(v)$: knowing one tells nothing about the other. Their **covariance** and **correlation coefficient** are
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Cov}(u, v) = \langle (u - \mu_u)(v - \mu_v)\rangle = \langle uv\rangle - \mu_u\mu_v, \qquad \rho_{uv} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(u, v)}{\sigma_u\sigma_v} \in [-1, 1] .
> $$
>
> For $N$ variables the **covariance matrix** $K_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(x_i, x_j)$ has the variances on its diagonal.
>
> *Source: Blundell §3.6 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §16, §23*

^def-b4-1-3

> [!caution] Caution: Uncorrelated is weaker than independent
> Independent variables are uncorrelated: for a factorized density the double integral splits, $\langle uv\rangle = \langle u\rangle\langle v\rangle$ (Blundell eq. 3.33). The converse fails. If $u$ is $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ and $v = u^2$, then $\operatorname{Cov}(u, v) = \langle u^3\rangle = 0$, yet $v$ is fixed by $u$. For jointly Gaussian variables, and only there among the common cases, zero covariance does imply independence ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^ex-b4-1-2|Example §B4.1.2]]).

^cau-b4-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.2: Sums of Independent Random Variables
> Let $X_1, \dots, X_N$ be independent, with means $\mu_j$ and variances $\sigma_j^2$, and $Y = X_1 + \cdots + X_N$.
> 1. Means always add: $\langle Y\rangle = \sum_j\mu_j$.
> 2. Variances add: $\sigma_Y^2 = \sum_j\sigma_j^2$ (uncorrelated is enough).
> 3. The density of $X_1 + X_2$ is the **convolution** of the two densities,
>
> $$
> P_{X_1 + X_2}(z) = (P_1 * P_2)(z) = \int P_1(z - x)\,P_2(x)\,dx .
> $$
>
> 4. *Identically distributed* $X_j$ (mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$): $\langle Y\rangle = N\mu$, $\sigma_Y = \sqrt N\,\sigma$, and the **sample mean** $\bar X = Y/N$ has mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt N$, whatever the shape of the parent distribution.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, §2, eqs. (15), (22)–(31), (47) · Blundell §3.6, Example 3.7 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §17*

^thm-b4-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) is linearity. (2): $Y - \langle Y\rangle = \sum_j(X_j - \mu_j)$, so
>
> $$
> \sigma_Y^2 = \sum_j\langle (X_j - \mu_j)^2\rangle + \sum_{i \ne j}\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = \sum_j\sigma_j^2 ,
> $$
>
> because the $N(N - 1)$ cross terms vanish for independent (or merely uncorrelated) variables: the double integral of a product against a product density factorizes ([[§17 Invariance Properties and Fubini's Theorem#^thm-17-6|551 Thm. §17.6]]). (3): the probability that $X_1 + X_2 \le z$ is $\int dx\,P_2(x)\int_{-\infty}^{z - x}P_1(y)\,dy$; differentiating with respect to $z$ gives $\int P_2(x)P_1(z - x)\,dx$. Schwartz writes the same as $\iint P_1(x_1)P_2(x_2)\,\delta(x_1 + x_2 - z)\,dx_1dx_2$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|WO Def. §B4.4.2]]). (4): put $\mu_j = \mu$, $\sigma_j = \sigma$ in (1)–(2), and use [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]] with $a = 1/N$: $\sigma_{\bar X} = \sqrt N\sigma/N$.

^der-b4-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-3|Def. §B4.1.3]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]], [[§17 Invariance Properties and Fubini's Theorem#^thm-17-6|551 Thm. §17.6]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|WO Def. §B4.4.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The law of large numbers, and why statistical mechanics works
> Part (4) is Schwartz's form of the law of large numbers: averaging $N$ independent draws shrinks the spread by $1/\sqrt N$, for *any* parent distribution. The centre of mass of $N \sim 10^{22}$ molecules spread uniformly through a box of length $L$ is known to $\sigma_N = L/\sqrt{12N} \approx 3 \times 10^{-12}L$, although no single molecule is located better than $0.29L$. Macroscopic quantities — the centre of mass, the force on a wall, the energy of a subsystem — are such sums, which is why they come out sharp although their microscopic ingredients are random. The relative width of a sum, $\sigma_Y/\langle Y\rangle = (\sigma/\mu)/\sqrt N$, falls as $N^{-1/2}$; for $10^{20}$ coin tosses it is $10^{-10}$ (Blundell, Example 3.9). Repeating a measurement $n$ times likewise cuts the *random* error by $\sqrt n$, never a systematic one.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, §2 · Blundell §3.6, §3.7*

## The Gaussian

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.4: Gaussian (Normal) Distribution
> The **Gaussian** or **normal** distribution $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ has the density
>
> $$
> P(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\,\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr], \qquad -\infty < x < \infty ,
> $$
>
> with $\sigma > 0$. Its logarithm is a downward parabola, $\ln P = -(x - \mu)^2/2\sigma^2 + \text{const}$. **Several variables** $\vec x = (x_1, \dots, x_N)$ are **jointly Gaussian** with mean vector $\vec\mu$ and (symmetric, positive definite) covariance matrix $\Sigma$ if
>
> $$
> P(\vec x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}\,\exp\Bigl[-\tfrac12(\vec x - \vec\mu)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}(\vec x - \vec\mu)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, eq. (1) · Blundell App. C.2, eq. (C.9) · PHY 306, Problem Set 7 (the bivariate case) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §15, §23.2*

^def-b4-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.3: Gaussian Integrals and the Moments of the Gaussian
> For $\alpha > 0$ and integers $n \ge 0$:
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x^2}dx = \sqrt{\frac\pi\alpha}, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{2n}e^{-\alpha x^2}dx = \frac{(2n - 1)!!}{(2\alpha)^n}\sqrt{\frac\pi\alpha}, \qquad \int_0^\infty x^{2n+1}e^{-\alpha x^2}dx = \frac{n!}{2\alpha^{n+1}},
> $$
>
> with $(2n - 1)!! = 1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2n - 1)$ and $(-1)!! = 1$; the odd integrals over the whole line vanish. More generally, for real $s > -1$,
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty x^s e^{-\alpha x^2}dx = \frac{\Gamma\bigl(\tfrac{s + 1}{2}\bigr)}{2\,\alpha^{(s+1)/2}}
> $$
>
> with the gamma function of [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-5|Def. §B4.1.5]]. Consequently $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is normalized, has mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and its central moments are
>
> $$
> \langle (x - \mu)^{2n}\rangle = (2n - 1)!!\,\sigma^{2n}, \qquad \langle (x - \mu)^{2n+1}\rangle = 0 ;
> $$
>
> so the skewness is $0$, the kurtosis $3$, $M_6 = 15$, $M_8 = 105$. The probability within $1\sigma$, $2\sigma$, $3\sigma$ of the mean is $68.3\,\%$, $95.4\,\%$, $99.7\,\%$.
>
> *Source: Blundell App. C.2, eqs. (C.3)–(C.9) · Schroeder App. B.1, eqs. (B.1)–(B.9) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, eqs. (11)–(13), (37)–(38) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §11–12*

^thm-b4-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The basic integral.* Let $I = \int e^{-\alpha x^2}dx$. Then $I^2 = \iint e^{-\alpha(x^2 + y^2)}dx\,dy$, and in polar coordinates ([[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-7|452 Ex. §15.7]])
>
> $$
> I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\int_0^\infty e^{-\alpha r^2}r\,dr = 2\pi\cdot\frac1{2\alpha} = \frac\pi\alpha .
> $$
>
> *Even powers.* Differentiate $\int e^{-\alpha x^2}dx = \sqrt\pi\,\alpha^{-1/2}$ $n$ times with respect to $\alpha$. On the left each derivative brings down $-x^2$; this is legitimate because for $\alpha \ge \alpha_0 > 0$ every $\alpha$-derivative of the integrand is dominated by $x^{2n}e^{-\alpha_0x^2}$, which is integrable ([[§15 The General Lebesgue Integral#^thm-15-8|551 Thm. §15.8]]). On the right, $\frac{d^n}{d\alpha^n}\alpha^{-1/2} = (-1)^n\,\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac32\cdots\tfrac{2n - 1}{2}\,\alpha^{-n - 1/2}$. Cancelling $(-1)^n$ gives the second formula. Odd powers over the whole line vanish because the integrand is odd.
>
> *Odd powers on the half-line, and general $s$.* Substitute $y = \alpha x^2$, $dx = dy/(2\sqrt{\alpha y})$:
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty x^se^{-\alpha x^2}dx = \frac{1}{2\alpha^{(s+1)/2}}\int_0^\infty y^{(s-1)/2}e^{-y}\,dy = \frac{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{s+1}{2}\bigr)}{2\alpha^{(s+1)/2}} ,
> $$
>
> which converges at $0$ exactly when $s > -1$. For $s = 2n + 1$, $\Gamma(n + 1) = n!$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]).
>
> *The Gaussian distribution.* With $u = x - \mu$ and $\alpha = 1/2\sigma^2$, normalization is the basic integral, $\langle u\rangle = 0$ by oddness (so the mean is $\mu$), and $\langle u^{2n}\rangle = (2n - 1)!!/(2\alpha)^n = (2n - 1)!!\,\sigma^{2n}$; $n = 1$ is the variance. The $k\sigma$ probabilities are $\operatorname{erf}(k/\sqrt2)$, evaluated numerically.

^der-b4-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-4|Def. §B4.1.4]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-5|Def. §B4.1.5]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-7|452 Ex. §15.7]], [[§15 The General Lebesgue Integral#^thm-15-8|551 Thm. §15.8]]

> [!caution] Caution: Where the factors of 2 go
> For the weight $e^{-x^2}$ (that is, $\sigma^2 = \tfrac12$) one has $\int x^{2n}e^{-x^2}dx = (2n - 1)!!\,\sqrt\pi/2^n$, and for a normalized Gaussian $\langle (x - \mu)^{2n}\rangle = (2n - 1)!!\,\sigma^{2n}$. The user's Stat Mech concepts notes (§11 and §12.3) put the factor $2^n$ on the wrong side, writing $(2n - 1)!!\sqrt\pi$ and $(2n - 1)!!(2\sigma^2)^n$; the check $n = 1$ (the variance must be $\sigma^2$) catches it. The moments come from differentiating with respect to the width parameter $\alpha$ (or $\sigma$), not the shift $\mu$: the integral does not depend on $\mu$ at all.

## The gamma function and Stirling's formula

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.5: Gamma Function
> For real $s > 0$ the **gamma function** is
>
> $$
> \Gamma(s) = \int_0^\infty x^{s-1}e^{-x}\,dx .
> $$
>
> It is "out of step" with the factorial by one: $\Gamma(n + 1) = n!$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]), which is why $n! = \int_0^\infty x^ne^{-x}dx$ is called the factorial integral.
>
> *Source: Blundell App. C.1, eqs. (C.1)–(C.2) · Schroeder App. B.2, eqs. (B.11)–(B.13)*

^def-b4-1-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.4: Properties of the Gamma Function
> $\Gamma(s + 1) = s\,\Gamma(s)$ for $s > 0$; $\Gamma(1) = 1$, hence $\Gamma(n + 1) = n!$; and $\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \sqrt\pi$, hence $\Gamma(\tfrac32) = \tfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2}$, $\Gamma(\tfrac52) = \tfrac{3\sqrt\pi}{4}$, and in general $\Gamma(n + \tfrac12) = (2n - 1)!!\,\sqrt\pi/2^n$.
>
> *Source: Blundell App. C.1, Table C.1 · Schroeder App. B.2, eqs. (B.14)–(B.15)*

^thm-b4-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Integrate by parts ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]) on $[\epsilon, R]$ and let $\epsilon \to 0$, $R \to \infty$: $\int x^se^{-x}dx = \bigl[-x^se^{-x}\bigr] + s\int x^{s-1}e^{-x}dx$, and the boundary terms vanish for $s > 0$. $\Gamma(1) = \int_0^\infty e^{-x}dx = 1$, and induction gives $n!$. With $x = y^2$, $\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \int_0^\infty x^{-1/2}e^{-x}dx = 2\int_0^\infty e^{-y^2}dy = \sqrt\pi$ by [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]] (the two results do not lean on each other circularly: the basic Gaussian integral was proved directly). The recursion then gives the half-integer values.

^der-b4-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-5|Def. §B4.1.5]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.5: Stirling's Formula
> As $n \to \infty$,
>
> $$
> n! = \sqrt{2\pi n}\;n^ne^{-n}\,\bigl(1 + O(1/n)\bigr), \qquad \ln n! = n\ln n - n + \tfrac12\ln(2\pi n) + O(1/n) .
> $$
>
> For the large numbers of statistical mechanics the cruder form $\ln n! \approx n\ln n - n$ suffices: the neglected $\tfrac12\ln(2\pi n)$ is about $27$ for $n = 10^{23}$, against $5 \times 10^{24}$ for the terms kept. The full formula is already good to $8\,\%$ at $n = 1$, $0.8\,\%$ at $n = 10$, $0.08\,\%$ at $n = 100$; the relative error is close to $1/(12n)$.
>
> *Source: Blundell App. C.3, eqs. (C.10)–(C.20) · Schroeder App. B.3, eqs. (B.17)–(B.24) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, App. B*

^thm-b4-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Crude form, with bounds.* $\ln n! = \sum_{j=1}^n\ln j$. Since $\ln$ is increasing, $\ln j \ge \int_{j-1}^{j}\ln x\,dx$ and $\ln j \le \int_j^{j+1}\ln x\,dx$, so
>
> $$
> n\ln n - n + 1 = \int_1^n\ln x\,dx \;\le\; \ln n! \;\le\; \int_1^{n+1}\ln x\,dx = (n + 1)\ln(n + 1) - n ,
> $$
>
> and both bounds are $n\ln n - n + O(\ln n)$.
>
> *Full form (Laplace's method).* Write $n! = \Gamma(n + 1) = \int_0^\infty e^{f(x)}dx$ with $f(x) = n\ln x - x$. The exponent is largest where $f'(x) = n/x - 1 = 0$, at $x = n$, with $f''(n) = -1/n$. With $x = n + y$ and $\ln(1 + y/n) = y/n - y^2/2n^2 + y^3/3n^3 - \cdots$ ([[§26 Differentiation and Integration of Power Series#^ex-26-4|451 Ex. §26.4]]),
>
> $$
> f(n + y) = n\ln n - n - \frac{y^2}{2n} + \frac{y^3}{3n^2} - \cdots ,
> $$
>
> the linear term cancelling. The integrand is a Gaussian of width $\sqrt n$ centred at $n$; the cubic term is small where the integrand matters ($y^3/n^2 \sim n^{-1/2}$ at $y \sim \sqrt n$), and extending the lower limit from $y = -n$ to $-\infty$ adds only an amount of order $e^{-n/2}$. By [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]],
>
> $$
> n! \approx n^ne^{-n}\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-y^2/2n}\,dy = n^ne^{-n}\sqrt{2\pi n} .
> $$
>
> Carrying the cubic and quartic terms to the next order gives the factor $1 + \frac1{12n}$, stated here, not derived.

^der-b4-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-5|Def. §B4.1.5]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]], [[§26 Differentiation and Integration of Power Series#^ex-26-4|451 Ex. §26.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why statistical mechanics takes logarithms
> A sharply peaked distribution is hard to expand directly — the Taylor series of $e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}$ needs every term — but its logarithm is a parabola, exact after two orders. So one expands $\ln P$ about its maximum: the first derivative vanishes there, and the second fixes the width, $\sigma^2 = -1/(\ln P)''$. This is how Stirling's formula was just obtained, how the binomial and Poisson distributions become Gaussians below, and how entropy, the logarithm of a multiplicity, enters at [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium|§B6.1]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, §3.3*

## Generating functions

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.6: Generating Function; Characteristic Function
> For a variable $k$ taking integer values, the **(probability) generating function** is $G(s) = \langle s^k\rangle = \sum_kP(k)\,s^k$. For any real variable, the **moment generating function** is $M(t) = \langle e^{tx}\rangle$ and the **characteristic function** is
>
> $$
> \varphi(q) = \langle e^{iqx}\rangle = \int e^{iqx}P(x)\,dx ,
> $$
>
> the Fourier transform of the density (Schwartz writes $\tilde P(k)$). $\varphi$ exists for every distribution, since $\lvert e^{iqx}\rvert = 1$; $M$ and $G$ may diverge away from $t = 0$, $s = 1$.
>
> *Source: Blundell, Exercise 3.8 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §14.3, §20.3 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, App. C, eq. (91) · PHY 306, Problem Set 6*

^def-b4-1-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.6: Properties of Generating Functions
> 1. *Moments.* $G(1) = 1$, $\langle k\rangle = G'(1)$, $\langle k(k - 1)\rangle = G''(1)$, so $\sigma^2 = G''(1) + G'(1) - G'(1)^2$. Likewise $\langle x^n\rangle = M^{(n)}(0) = i^{-n}\varphi^{(n)}(0)$ whenever the $n$th moment is finite.
> 2. *Sums.* If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $\varphi_{X+Y} = \varphi_X\,\varphi_Y$, and the same for $M$ and $G$: the convolution of densities ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]]) becomes a product.
> 3. *Gaussian.* For $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, $\varphi(q) = e^{i\mu q - \sigma^2q^2/2}$: the transform of a Gaussian of width $\sigma$ is a Gaussian of width $1/\sigma$.
>
> *Source: Blundell, Exercise 3.8 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §14.3, §20.4 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, App. C, eqs. (92)–(94)*

^thm-b4-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) Differentiate under the sum: $G'(s) = \sum kP(k)s^{k-1}$ and $G''(s) = \sum k(k - 1)P(k)s^{k-2}$; put $s = 1$. Similarly $\frac{d^n}{dt^n}e^{tx} = x^ne^{tx}$ and $\frac{d^n}{dq^n}e^{iqx} = (ix)^ne^{iqx}$. (2) Independence factorizes the expectation of the product $e^{iqX}e^{iqY}$ (as in [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^cau-b4-1-2|Caution: Uncorrelated is weaker than independent]]). (3) For $\mu = 0$, $\varphi(q) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\int e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2 + iqx}dx$. This is $2\pi$ times the Fourier transform of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|WO Def. §B4.4.1]] at $-q$ (the conventions differ: [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^cau-b4-4-1|WO Caution: Conventions]]), and [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|WO Theorem §B4.4.2]] gives $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\cdot\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\sigma^2q^2/2}\cdot2\pi = e^{-\sigma^2q^2/2}$. A shift by $\mu$ multiplies by $e^{i\mu q}$.

## The binomial distribution and its Gaussian limit

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.7: Bernoulli Trial; Binomial Distribution
> A **Bernoulli trial** has two outcomes, "success" with probability $p$ and "failure" with $q = 1 - p$. The number $k$ of successes in $n$ independent trials has the **binomial distribution**
>
> $$
> P(n, k) = \binom nk p^kq^{n-k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n, \qquad \binom nk = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n - k)!} :
> $$
>
> a given sequence with $k$ successes has probability $p^kq^{n-k}$, and $\binom nk$ sequences have $k$ successes. It is normalized by the binomial theorem, $\sum_k\binom nkp^kq^{n-k} = (p + q)^n = 1$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §3.7, eq. (3.43) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, eqs. (1)–(4) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §21*

^def-b4-1-7

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.7: Mean and Variance of the Binomial Distribution
> $\langle k\rangle = np$ and $\sigma_k^2 = npq$; the generating function is $G(s) = (q + ps)^n$. The relative width $\sigma_k/\langle k\rangle = \sqrt{q/np}$ falls as $n^{-1/2}$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §3.7, eqs. (3.46)–(3.47) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, eqs. (5)–(8) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §21.2*

^thm-b4-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $k$ is the sum of $n$ independent Bernoulli variables $x_j \in \{0, 1\}$, each with $\langle x_j\rangle = p$ and $\langle x_j^2\rangle = p$, hence variance $p - p^2 = pq$; [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]] adds them. Equivalently, each trial has $G = q + ps$ and [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]] multiplies them. Schwartz's trick reaches the mean without either: treat $p$ and $q$ as independent, so that $\sum_kk\binom nkp^kq^{n-k} = p\,\partial_p(p + q)^n = np(p + q)^{n-1}$, and only then set $p + q = 1$.

^der-b4-1-7

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-7|Def. §B4.1.7]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.8: Gaussian Limit of the Binomial Distribution (de Moivre–Laplace)
> If $npq \gg 1$, then for $k$ within a few standard deviations of $np$,
>
> $$
> P(n, k) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi npq}}\,\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(k - np)^2}{2npq}\Bigr],
> $$
>
> the Gaussian with the same mean and variance. The relative error is of order $1/\sqrt{npq}$ for $p \ne q$ and $1/(npq)$ for $p = q$, and it grows in the far tails, where the binomial is bounded by $0 \le k \le n$ and the Gaussian is not.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, eq. (9) · Blundell, Exercise 3.7(b)*

^thm-b4-1-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply Stirling's formula ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]) to the three factorials, all large when $k$ and $n - k$ are near $np$ and $nq$:
>
> $$
> \ln P(n, k) = \phi(k) + \tfrac12\ln\frac{n}{2\pi k(n - k)} + O\Bigl(\frac1k\Bigr), \qquad \phi(k) = n\ln n - k\ln k - (n - k)\ln(n - k) + k\ln p + (n - k)\ln q .
> $$
>
> Treat $k$ as continuous and expand $\phi$ about its maximum ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]):
> - $\phi(np) = n\ln n - np\ln(np) - nq\ln(nq) + np\ln p + nq\ln q = n\ln n - (np + nq)\ln n = 0$;
> - $\phi'(k) = \ln\dfrac{(n - k)\,p}{k\,q}$, which vanishes at $k = np$;
> - $\phi''(k) = -\dfrac1k - \dfrac1{n - k}$, which at $k = np$ equals $-\dfrac{1}{npq}$;
> - $\phi'''(np) = \dfrac{q^2 - p^2}{n^2p^2q^2}$, so the cubic term, at $k - np \sim \sqrt{npq}$, is of order $(q - p)/\sqrt{npq}$; it vanishes for $p = q$, leaving a quartic term of order $1/(npq)$.
>
> Hence $\phi \approx -(k - np)^2/2npq$, and the prefactor at $k = np$ is $\sqrt{n/(2\pi\,np\,nq)} = 1/\sqrt{2\pi npq}$. Schwartz obtains the same in one line from the central limit theorem, since $k$ is a sum of $n$ independent Bernoulli variables ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion|§B4.2]]).

^der-b4-1-8

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-7|Def. §B4.1.7]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-7|Theorem §B4.1.7]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

![[ph-th-b4-1-1.svg]]
*The binomial distribution with $p = 0.3$ (blue bars) against the Gaussian of the same mean and variance (red). At $n = 10$ ($npq = 2.1$) the binomial is visibly skewed: its bulk leans to the left of the mean ($P(2) = 0.23$ against $P(4) = 0.20$, where the Gaussian has equal values) and its right tail is longer ($P(6) = 0.037$ against $P(0) = 0.028$). At $n = 50$ ($npq = 10.5$) the two agree closely; the remaining skewness, $(q - p)/\sqrt{npq}$, falls like $(npq)^{-1/2}$.*

## The Poisson distribution

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.8: Poisson Distribution
> A variable $m = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ has the **Poisson distribution** with mean $\lambda > 0$ if
>
> $$
> P_\lambda(m) = \frac{\lambda^m}{m!}\,e^{-\lambda} .
> $$
>
> It counts independent rare events: decays of a radioactive sample with decay rate $\Gamma$ per atom ($\lambda = N\Gamma t$ for $N$ atoms in time $t$), raindrops falling into a glass, molecules found in a small volume, collisions suffered by a molecule in time $t$ ([[§B5.2★ Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]]). As a function of the continuous time $t$ at fixed $m$ it is not a probability density.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, §4, eqs. (60)–(68) · Blundell, Exercise 3.3 · PHY 306, Problem Set 6, Problem 3*

^def-b4-1-8

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.9: Properties of the Poisson Distribution
> 1. It is normalized, with generating function $G(s) = e^{\lambda(s - 1)}$, mean $\lambda$ and variance $\lambda$; the relative width is $1/\sqrt\lambda$.
> 2. *Rare-event limit of the binomial.* If $n \to \infty$ and $p \to 0$ with $np = \lambda$ fixed, $P(n, m) \to P_\lambda(m)$ for each $m$.
> 3. *Gaussian limit.* For $\lambda \gg 1$, $P_\lambda(m) \approx (2\pi\lambda)^{-1/2}\exp[-(m - \lambda)^2/2\lambda]$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, eqs. (69)–(80); Lecture 2, eq. (13) · PHY 306, Problem Set 6, Problem 3 · Blundell, Exercises 3.3, 3.7(a), 3.8(c)*

^thm-b4-1-9

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) $G(s) = e^{-\lambda}\sum_m(\lambda s)^m/m! = e^{\lambda(s - 1)}$, so $G(1) = 1$, $G'(1) = \lambda$, $G''(1) = \lambda^2$, and [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]] gives $\sigma^2 = \lambda^2 + \lambda - \lambda^2 = \lambda$. (2) With $p = \lambda/n$,
>
> $$
> \binom nm p^m(1 - p)^{n-m} = \frac{n(n - 1)\cdots(n - m + 1)}{n^m}\cdot\frac{\lambda^m}{m!}\cdot\Bigl(1 - \frac\lambda n\Bigr)^{n}\Bigl(1 - \frac\lambda n\Bigr)^{-m} \;\longrightarrow\; 1\cdot\frac{\lambda^m}{m!}\cdot e^{-\lambda}\cdot1 ,
> $$
>
> using $(1 - \lambda/n)^n \to e^{-\lambda}$. (3) With Stirling, $\ln P_\lambda(m) \approx m\ln\lambda - \lambda - m\ln m + m - \tfrac12\ln(2\pi m)$. The first derivative in $m$, $\ln(\lambda/m)$, vanishes at $m = \lambda$, where the expression itself is $-\frac12\ln(2\pi\lambda)$; the second derivative is $-1/m = -1/\lambda$. This is Schwartz's derivation.

^der-b4-1-9

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-8|Def. §B4.1.8]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-7|Def. §B4.1.7]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: Binomial or Poisson?
> The two describe different situations (Schwartz, Lecture 2). The binomial has a fixed number of discrete trials and a bounded count ($k \le n$); coin flips with $p = \tfrac12$ never become Poissonian. The Poisson distribution has continuous time and no upper bound; each event is rare in each short interval, and the count only grows. The limit (2) connects them by slicing time into $n$ short intervals, each with a small probability $p = \lambda/n$ of an event.

^rem-b4-1-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B4.1.1: Counting Decays
> A mole of $^{235}$U ($6.0 \times 10^{23}$ atoms, decay rate $\Gamma = 3 \times 10^{-17}\ \mathrm s^{-1}$ per atom) has on average $N\Gamma = 1.8 \times 10^7$ decays per second. In a window of $1\ \mu\mathrm s$ the count is Poisson with $\lambda = 18$:
> - by [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-9|Theorem §B4.1.9]] it is $18 \pm 4.2$, and the probability of seeing no decay at all is $e^{-18} = 1.5 \times 10^{-8}$;
> - in one second the count is $1.8 \times 10^7 \pm 4.2 \times 10^3$: relatively, the fluctuation has dropped from $24\,\%$ to $0.02\,\%$. Waiting longer makes the count more predictable, the $1/\sqrt\lambda$ of the law of large numbers.
>
> The mean and variance can also be had without a generating function (PHY 306): shifting the summation index gives $\langle m\rangle = \lambda\sum_{m \ge 1}P_\lambda(m - 1) = \lambda$, and the same trick twice gives $\langle m(m - 1)\rangle = \lambda^2$, so $\sigma^2 = \langle m^2\rangle - \langle m\rangle^2 = \lambda^2 + \lambda - \lambda^2 = \lambda$ — the same three numbers $G(1), G'(1), G''(1)$ in disguise.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, §4 · PHY 306, Problem Set 6, Problem 3 and solutions*

^ex-b4-1-1

> [!example] Example §B4.1.2: Two Correlated Gaussian Variables
> Let $(x_1, x_2)$ be jointly Gaussian ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-4|Def. §B4.1.4]]) with zero means and $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & c \\ c & 1\end{pmatrix}$, $-1 < c < 1$. Then $\det\Sigma = 1 - c^2$, $\Sigma^{-1} = \frac1{1 - c^2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -c \\ -c & 1\end{pmatrix}$, and
>
> $$
> P(x_1, x_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1 - c^2}}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{x_1^2 - 2cx_1x_2 + x_2^2}{2(1 - c^2)}\Bigr] = \frac{e^{-x_1^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1 - c^2)}}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(x_2 - cx_1)^2}{2(1 - c^2)}\Bigr],
> $$
>
> by completing the square, $x_1^2 - 2cx_1x_2 + x_2^2 = (1 - c^2)x_1^2 + (x_2 - cx_1)^2$. Read the second factor as a Gaussian in $x_2$ with mean $cx_1$ and variance $1 - c^2$: the $x_2$-integral of $x_2$ against it is $cx_1$, and then
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Cov}(x_1, x_2) = \langle x_1x_2\rangle = c\,\langle x_1^2\rangle = c .
> $$
>
> So the off-diagonal entry of $\Sigma$ is the covariance, and the diagonal entries are the variances. If $c = 0$ the density is a product of two one-dimensional Gaussians: for Gaussians, uncorrelated means independent. The same holds for any number of variables with diagonal $\Sigma$.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 7, Problems 1–2 and solutions*

^ex-b4-1-2

> [!example] Example §B4.1.3: How Good Is the Gaussian for 100 Coin Tosses?
> For $n = 100$ fair tosses, $np = 50$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{npq} = 5$. Exact binomial probabilities against [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-8|Theorem §B4.1.8]]:
>
> | $k$ | $50$ | $55$ | $60$ | $65$ | $70$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $(k - 50)/\sigma$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ |
> | exact $P(100, k)$ | $0.07959$ | $0.04847$ | $0.01084$ | $8.64 \times 10^{-4}$ | $2.32 \times 10^{-5}$ |
> | Gaussian | $0.07979$ | $0.04839$ | $0.01080$ | $8.86 \times 10^{-4}$ | $2.68 \times 10^{-5}$ |
> | ratio | $1.003$ | $0.998$ | $0.996$ | $1.026$ | $1.155$ |
>
> Within $2\sigma$ the agreement is a few parts in a thousand, well inside the order-of-magnitude estimate $1/(npq) = 0.04$ for $p = q$; at $4\sigma$ the Gaussian overestimates by $15\,\%$. Near the peak, where statistical mechanics needs it, the Gaussian is excellent; deep in the tails one must go back to the exact distribution.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, §2 (suggests the check) · Blundell, Example 3.9; numbers computed here*
