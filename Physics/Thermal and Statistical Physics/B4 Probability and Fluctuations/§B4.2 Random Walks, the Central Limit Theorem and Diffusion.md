A molecule in a gas at room conditions collides several billion times a second ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-6|Theorem §A2.2.6]]), and after each collision it sets off in a nearly random direction. How far does it get? This section answers with the random walk: the displacement is a sum of many independent steps, so its mean square grows linearly in the number of steps ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]]) and its distribution becomes Gaussian — the central limit theorem, the reason Gaussians are everywhere in statistical mechanics. In the continuum limit the walk becomes the diffusion equation, whose Gaussian solution spreads like $\sqrt t$. Level A had Fourier's law of conduction as an empirical law ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-1|Law §A1.5.1]]); its particle analogue, Fick's law, is derived here from the walk, and the Langevin model of Brownian motion ties the diffusion constant to friction and temperature (the Einstein relation). Heat conduction itself returns in [[§B5.3★ The Thermal Diffusion Equation|§B5.3]], the kinetic-theory value of $D$ in [[§B5.2★ Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients|§B5.2]].

## Random walks

> [!definition] Definition §B4.2.1: Random Walk
> A **random walk** in $\mathbb R^d$ starts at the origin and takes $N$ steps $\vec\xi_1, \dots, \vec\xi_N$ that are **independent and identically distributed**; its position after $N$ steps is $\vec S_N = \vec\xi_1 + \cdots + \vec\xi_N$. Two cases recur:
> - the **one-dimensional lattice walk**: each step is $+\ell$ with probability $p$ and $-\ell$ with probability $q = 1 - p$ (unbiased if $p = q = \tfrac12$);
> - the **isotropic walk**: steps of fixed length $\ell$ (or of a fixed distribution of lengths) in uniformly random directions — the "drunkard's walk" in $d = 2$, a molecule between collisions in $d = 3$.
>
> If one step is taken every time $\tau$, the walk after time $t$ has $N = t/\tau$ steps.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, §§1–2.1 · Blundell §3.6, Example 3.10 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §20.1*

^def-b4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.1: Moments of a Random Walk
> 1. *Lattice walk.* The number of steps to the right is binomial, the position $x = k\ell$ (with $k$ the net number of steps to the right) has generating function $G_N(s) = (ps + q/s)^N$, and
>
> $$
> \langle x\rangle = N(p - q)\,\ell, \qquad \sigma_x^2 = 4Npq\,\ell^2 ;
> $$
>
> unbiased: $\langle x\rangle = 0$ and $x_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt N\,\ell$.
> 2. *Any walk with mean-zero steps.* If $\langle\vec\xi\rangle = \vec 0$ and $\langle\lvert\vec\xi\rvert^2\rangle = a^2$, then
>
> $$
> \langle\vec S_N\rangle = \vec 0, \qquad \langle\lvert\vec S_N\rvert^2\rangle = N a^2 ,
> $$
>
> in any dimension; if the steps are isotropic, each Cartesian component has variance $Na^2/d$. The rms distance grows like $\sqrt N$, not $N$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, eqs. (5)–(12), (14)–(19) · Blundell, Example 3.10 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §20.2–20.4 · PHY 306, Problem Set 6, Problem 2*

^thm-b4-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) Let $P_N(k)$ be the probability of net displacement $k$ steps. The walker reaches $k$ from $k - 1$ or from $k + 1$, so $P_{N+1}(k) = pP_N(k - 1) + qP_N(k + 1)$, with $P_0(k) = \delta_{k0}$. Multiplying by $s^k$ and summing gives $G_{N+1}(s) = (ps + q/s)\,G_N(s)$, hence $G_N = (ps + q/s)^N$ — equivalently, [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]] for a sum of $N$ independent steps. Then $G_N'(1) = N(p - q)$ and
>
> $$
> G_N''(1) = N(N - 1)(p - q)^2 + 2Nq, \qquad \langle k^2\rangle = G_N''(1) + G_N'(1) = N + N(N - 1)(p - q)^2 ,
> $$
>
> so $\sigma_k^2 = N\bigl[1 - (p - q)^2\bigr] = N\bigl[(p + q)^2 - (p - q)^2\bigr] = 4Npq$. Multiply by $\ell$ and $\ell^2$.
>
> (2) $\lvert\vec S_N\rvert^2 = \sum_j\lvert\vec\xi_j\rvert^2 + \sum_{i \ne j}\vec\xi_i\cdot\vec\xi_j$, and for $i \ne j$ independence and zero mean give $\langle\vec\xi_i\cdot\vec\xi_j\rangle = \langle\vec\xi_i\rangle\cdot\langle\vec\xi_j\rangle = 0$: the angle between two steps is random, so the dot product averages to zero (Schwartz's argument). Only the $N$ diagonal terms survive, each $a^2$. Isotropy makes the $d$ components equivalent, and they share $Na^2$ equally.

## The central limit theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.2: Central Limit Theorem
> Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent and identically distributed, with mean $\mu$ and **finite** variance $\sigma^2 > 0$, and $S_N = X_1 + \cdots + X_N$. Then the standardized sum
>
> $$
> Z_N = \frac{S_N - N\mu}{\sigma\sqrt N}
> $$
>
> converges in distribution to $\mathcal N(0, 1)$: for every $z$, $\;P(Z_N \le z) \to \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^ze^{-u^2/2}du$ as $N \to \infty$. Informally, $S_N$ is approximately $\mathcal N(N\mu, N\sigma^2)$ and the mean $S_N/N$ approximately $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2/N)$, whatever the parent distribution: all its features beyond $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are forgotten.
>
> *Source: the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §18 (the statement with its conditions) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, §3, eqs. (53)–(54), App. C · Blundell, Exercise 3.7*

^thm-b4-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Proof idea via characteristic functions* (Schwartz, App. C; [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-6|Def. §B4.1.6]]). Let $\varphi(u) = \langle e^{iu(X - \mu)}\rangle$. A finite variance makes $\varphi$ twice differentiable at $0$ with $\varphi(0) = 1$, $\varphi'(0) = 0$, $\varphi''(0) = -\sigma^2$, so by Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]], in Peano form) $\varphi(u) = 1 - \tfrac12\sigma^2u^2 + o(u^2)$. The variables $(X_j - \mu)/\sigma\sqrt N$ are independent, so by [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]
>
> $$
> \langle e^{iqZ_N}\rangle = \Bigl[\varphi\Bigl(\frac{q}{\sigma\sqrt N}\Bigr)\Bigr]^N = \Bigl[1 - \frac{q^2}{2N} + o\Bigl(\frac1N\Bigr)\Bigr]^N \;\longrightarrow\; e^{-q^2/2}
> $$
>
> for each fixed $q$, using $(1 + c/N + o(1/N))^N \to e^c$. The limit is the characteristic function of $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]). The remaining step — pointwise convergence of characteristic functions to one continuous at $q = 0$ implies convergence in distribution — is Lévy's continuity theorem, stated here and not proved; Schwartz instead inverts the Fourier transform directly.
>
> *The moment route* (Schwartz, §3.1) shows what is being forgotten. For the sum (or the mean) of $N$ draws, the skewness and kurtosis of [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-2|Def. §B4.1.2]] are, in terms of those of one draw,
>
> $$
> M_3^{(N)} = \frac{M_3^{(1)}}{\sqrt N}, \qquad M_4^{(N)} = 3 + \frac{M_4^{(1)} - 3}{N} ,
> $$
>
> because in $\langle(\sum_j u_j)^3\rangle$ (with $u_j = X_j - \mu$) only the $N$ terms $\langle u_j^3\rangle$ survive, and in $\langle(\sum u_j)^4\rangle$ only $N$ terms $\langle u^4\rangle$ and $3N(N - 1)$ terms $\langle u^2\rangle^2$. Every higher normalized moment likewise tends to its Gaussian value ($0$ for odd, $(2n - 1)!!$ for even: [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]]); the asymmetry dies slowest, as $N^{-1/2}$.

> [!caution] Caution: What the central limit theorem does not say
> - *Finite variance is essential.* The Lorentzian (Cauchy) density $\frac{\Gamma/2\pi}{(x - x_0)^2 + (\Gamma/2)^2}$, one of Schwartz's examples, has no variance. Its characteristic function is $e^{ix_0q - \Gamma\lvert q\rvert/2}$ (a standard result, not in the sources), so the mean of $N$ draws has $\bigl[e^{ix_0q/N - \Gamma\lvert q\rvert/2N}\bigr]^N$, the same Lorentzian: averaging does not narrow it at all.
> - *Independence matters.* Strongly correlated terms need not average out; the "molecular chaos" that makes collisions independent is itself an assumption ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^def-b5-4-3|Def. §B5.4.3]]). Identical distribution can be relaxed under conditions not treated here.
> - *Convergence is of probabilities of intervals, near the centre.* The density of a lattice walk lives on every other site (after $N$ steps $k$ has the parity of $N$), so $P_N(k) \approx 2/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}\;e^{-k^2/2\sigma^2}$ on the allowed sites and $0$ on the others; only the average over neighbouring sites is Gaussian. And far in the tails, at deviations of order $N$ rather than $\sqrt N$, the relative error need not be small ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^ex-b4-1-3|Example §B4.1.3]]).

^cau-b4-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why macroscopic quantities are Gaussian and sharp
> A macroscopic observable — the momentum delivered to a wall in a second, the energy of a subvolume, the number of molecules in a cubic millimetre — is a sum over enormous numbers of nearly independent microscopic contributions. By the central limit theorem its distribution is Gaussian about the mean, with relative width of order $N^{-1/2}$. The theorem says nothing about *what* the mean is; that is physics. But it frees the macroscopic description from the details of the microscopic one, and it is why the energy fluctuations of [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]] are Gaussian and tiny.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 1, §3.3*

## Diffusion

> [!definition] Definition §B4.2.2: Number Density; Particle Flux; Continuity Equation
> For many particles of one kind (dye in water, a labelled tracer in a gas), the **number density** $n(\vec r, t)$ is the number per unit volume, averaged over a small region or a short time, and the **particle flux** $\vec J(\vec r, t)$ is the number crossing unit area per unit time, $\vec J = n\vec v$ with $\vec v$ the local mean velocity. If particles are neither created nor destroyed, they obey the **continuity equation**
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0 ,
> $$
>
> the local form of "the number in a region changes only by flow across its boundary" ([[§29 Conservation of Mass and Laplace's Equation#^thm-29-1|452 Thm. §29.1]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, §4, eqs. (32)–(36) · Blundell §9.3, eqs. (9.27)–(9.35)*

^def-b4-2-2

> [!law] Law §B4.2.3: Fick's First Law
> In a medium at rest, the diffusive flux of a species is proportional to its concentration gradient and points down it:
>
> $$
> \vec J = -D\,\nabla n .
> $$
>
> The **diffusion constant** $D > 0$ (units m$^2$/s) depends on the species, the medium and the temperature: about $2 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$ for CO in air at room conditions, $2 \times 10^{-9}$ for N$_2$ in water, $1 \times 10^{-9}$ for benzene in water, $10^{-11}$ for proteins in water.
>
> *Valid when:* motion is by random molecular motion alone — no bulk flow (convection) and no external force on the species; gradients small over a mean free path; the medium itself uniform in temperature and pressure. Fourier's law ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-1|Law §A1.5.1]]) is the same statement for heat.
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, §4, eq. (37) and numbers · Blundell §9.3, eq. (9.26)*

^law-b4-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.4: The Diffusion Equation
> Fick's first law and the continuity equation give **Fick's second law**, the **diffusion equation**
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} = D\,\nabla^2n ,
> $$
>
> for constant $D$. It is linear, first order in time and second order in space: $n$ changes where it is curved, rising in dips and falling at peaks, and a steady state ($\partial_tn = 0$) solves Laplace's equation.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, eqs. (27)–(28), (38) · Blundell §9.3, eqs. (9.31), (9.36)*

^thm-b4-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Substitute $\vec J = -D\nabla n$ into $\partial_tn = -\nabla\cdot\vec J$: $\partial_tn = \nabla\cdot(D\nabla n) = D\nabla^2n$ when $D$ does not vary in space ([[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^def-28-3|452 Def. §28.3]] for the Laplacian).

^der-b4-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^def-b4-2-2|Def. §B4.2.2]], [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^law-b4-2-3|Law §B4.2.3]], [[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^def-28-3|452 Def. §28.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.5: Diffusion from a Random Walk
> Let a particle perform a random walk in $\mathbb R^d$ with one step every time $\tau$, steps of mean zero and covariance $\langle\xi_i\xi_j\rangle = (a^2/d)\,\delta_{ij}$ (isotropic, mean square length $a^2$). On length scales large compared with $a$ and times large compared with $\tau$, its probability density $P(\vec r, t)$ obeys the diffusion equation with
>
> $$
> D = \frac{a^2}{2d\,\tau} \qquad\Bigl(\text{1D lattice walk: } D = \frac{\ell^2}{2\tau}\Bigr),
> $$
>
> and so does the density $n = NP$ of $N$ independent walkers. Fick's law is thereby derived for this model, with $\langle\lvert\vec r\rvert^2\rangle = 2dDt$. A bias $\langle\vec\xi\rangle = \vec u\,\tau$ adds a drift: $\partial_tP = -\vec u\cdot\nabla P + D\nabla^2P$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, §3.2, eqs. (23)–(29), §4 (the lemming argument) · Blundell, Exercise 3.7(b) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §20.2*

^thm-b4-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The position after the next step is the present position plus an independent step, so the densities obey the master equation $P(\vec r, t + \tau) = \langle P(\vec r - \vec\xi, t)\rangle_{\vec\xi}$ (in one dimension, $P(x, t + \tau) = \tfrac12P(x - \ell, t) + \tfrac12P(x + \ell, t)$). Expand both sides by Taylor's theorem ([[§11 Taylor's Theorem for Multivariable Functions#^thm-11-2|452 Thm. §11.2]]), assuming $P$ varies slowly on the scales $a$ and $\tau$:
>
> $$
> P + \tau\,\partial_tP + \cdots = P - \langle\xi_i\rangle\,\partial_iP + \tfrac12\langle\xi_i\xi_j\rangle\,\partial_i\partial_jP + \cdots = P + \frac{a^2}{2d}\nabla^2P + \cdots .
> $$
>
> Dividing by $\tau$ and keeping the leading terms, $\partial_tP = (a^2/2d\tau)\nabla^2P$; the neglected terms are smaller by factors $\tau/t$ and $a^2/L^2$, with $L$ the scale on which $P$ varies. With a bias, the first-order term $-\langle\vec\xi\rangle\cdot\nabla P$ survives and gives the drift. Consistency: by [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]] $\langle\lvert\vec r\rvert^2\rangle = (t/\tau)a^2 = 2dDt$, which is also the second moment of the solution in [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]. Independent walkers add, so $n = NP$ obeys the same linear equation, and $\vec J = -D\nabla n$ follows by comparing with the continuity equation.
>
> Schwartz's lemming picture is the same computation in words: the change at $x$ is proportional to $[n(x + \ell) - n(x)] + [n(x - \ell) - n(x)] \approx \ell^2\,\partial_x^2n$, so diffusion happens only where $n$ is curved.

> [!remark] Remark: The layer of Fick's law
> Fick's first law enters as a law, an empirical regularity (Schwartz: "It's a law because we didn't derive it (neither did Fick)"). The random-walk model derives it, with $D$ expressed through microscopic quantities; for a gas, kinetic theory refines the numerical factor to $D = \tfrac13\lambda\langle v\rangle$ ([[§B5.2★ Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]]), and the relaxation-time Boltzmann equation derives it again ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-5|Theorem §B5.4.5]]). The law stays the statement for liquids and solids, where $D$ is measured.

^rem-b4-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.6: Spreading from a Point; Green's Function of the Diffusion Equation
> In $\mathbb R^d$, the function
>
> $$
> G(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{(4\pi Dt)^{d/2}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\lvert\vec r\rvert^2}{4Dt}\Bigr), \qquad t > 0 ,
> $$
>
> solves the diffusion equation, has unit integral for every $t$, and tends to $\delta^d(\vec r)$ as $t \to 0^+$: it describes particles released at the origin. Each component of $\vec r$ is $\mathcal N(0, 2Dt)$, so $x_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{2Dt}$ and $\langle\lvert\vec r\rvert^2\rangle = 2dDt$. For any initial density $n_0$,
>
> $$
> n(\vec r, t) = \int G(\vec r - \vec r\,', t)\,n_0(\vec r\,')\,d^d r' :
> $$
>
> the particles at $\vec r$ at time $t$ are those that walked there from everywhere else.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, eqs. (24), (30)–(31), (39) · Blundell, Exercise 3.7(b), eq. (3.50)*

^thm-b4-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Direct check.* With $\ln G = -\tfrac d2\ln(4\pi Dt) - r^2/4Dt$: $\partial_tG/G = -\frac{d}{2t} + \frac{r^2}{4Dt^2}$, and $\nabla G = -\frac{\vec r}{2Dt}G$, so $\nabla^2G = \bigl(\frac{r^2}{4D^2t^2} - \frac{d}{2Dt}\bigr)G$; hence $\partial_tG = D\nabla^2G$. $G$ is a product of $d$ normalized Gaussians of variance $2Dt$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]]), whose width tends to zero, which is the Gaussian representation of the delta function ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|WO Def. §B4.4.2]]).
>
> *General solution.* The equation is linear, so superpositions of solutions are solutions; the convolution integral solves it (differentiate under the integral) and at $t \to 0^+$ reproduces $n_0$ by the sifting property ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|WO Theorem §B4.4.3]]).
>
> *Via Fourier modes.* A plane wave $e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}$ decays as $e^{-Dk^2t}$: short wavelengths are smoothed fastest. A point source contains all $k$ equally, and the inverse transform of the Gaussian $e^{-Dk^2t}$ is the Gaussian $G$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|WO Theorem §B4.4.2]]). Equivalently, $G$ is the central limit theorem applied to the walk: the sum of $t/\tau$ steps is Gaussian with variance $2Dt$ per component.

> [!caution] Caution: Dimension-dependent factors
> In $d$ dimensions the normalization is $(4\pi Dt)^{-d/2}$, and $\langle r^2\rangle = 2dDt$ — that is $6Dt$ in three dimensions, while $x_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{2Dt}$ holds per component. Schwartz writes the three-dimensional point-source solution with the one-dimensional prefactor $(4\pi Dt)^{-1/2}$ (Lecture 2, eqs. (30)–(31)) and uses the one-dimensional $D = \tfrac12\ell\bar v$ also in three dimensions (eq. (29)); for isotropic steps of fixed length $\ell$ in three dimensions [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]] gives $D = \ell^2/6\tau = \tfrac16\ell\bar v$. Only orders of magnitude survive such estimates in any case ([[§B5.2★ Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients|§B5.2]]).

^cau-b4-2-2

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Diffusion is slow, and needs calm
> Since $x_{\text{rms}} \propto \sqrt t$, diffusing ten times as far takes a hundred times as long, and a "diffusion speed" $x/t \propto t^{-1/2}$ means nothing (Blundell, Exercise 3.7(c)). A random walk covers in time $t$ only $\sqrt{\ell\,\bar vt}$ instead of $\bar vt$. Over everyday distances diffusion is therefore beaten by any bulk motion: stirring, convection currents from slight temperature differences, the momentum of a drop falling in. Visible diffusion needs a calm medium and short distances — cells, thin layers, porous solids. Schwartz stresses that the diffusion equation describes a small subset of molecules (a tracer) in an equilibrium bath; a gas expanding into vacuum, or dye injected violently, is not governed by it.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, §4.1, §6*

## Brownian motion

> [!model] Model §B4.2.7: Langevin Model of a Brownian Particle
> A particle of mass $m$, large compared with the molecules of a fluid at temperature $T$, obeys (one Cartesian component)
>
> $$
> m\frac{dv}{dt} = -\gamma v + F(t) .
> $$
>
> - $-\gamma v$ is the **drag**, linear in the velocity for slow motion; $\gamma$ is the drag coefficient (for a sphere of radius $R$ in a fluid of viscosity $\eta$, Stokes' law $\gamma = 6\pi\eta R$, quoted from fluid mechanics), and $1/\gamma$ the **mobility** (terminal velocity per unit applied force).
> - $F(t)$ is the rapidly fluctuating force of molecular impacts, with $\langle F\rangle = 0$ and no correlation with the particle's position: $\langle xF\rangle = 0$.
> - The particle is in thermal equilibrium with the fluid, so its velocity obeys equipartition, $\tfrac12m\langle v^2\rangle = \tfrac12k_BT$ per component ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]; proved in [[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]]).
>
> Averages are over an ensemble of such particles (or over many independent intervals).
>
> *Source: Blundell §33.1, eq. (33.1) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, §5, eqs. (43)–(47), §5.1, eq. (58)*

^mod-b4-2-7

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.8: Einstein Relation
> For a particle of [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^mod-b4-2-7|Model §B4.2.7]] released at $x = 0$,
>
> $$
> \langle x^2\rangle = \frac{2k_BT}{\gamma}\Bigl[t - \frac m\gamma\bigl(1 - e^{-\gamma t/m}\bigr)\Bigr] \;\approx\;
> \begin{cases} \dfrac{k_BT}{m}\,t^2, & t \ll m/\gamma \ \text{(ballistic)},\\[2mm] \dfrac{2k_BT}{\gamma}\,t, & t \gg m/\gamma \ \text{(diffusive)}. \end{cases}
> $$
>
> At long times the particle diffuses with
>
> $$
> D = \frac{k_BT}{\gamma}, \qquad\text{for a sphere}\qquad D = \frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta R} \quad\text{(Stokes–Einstein)} .
> $$
>
> The velocity forgets its value on the time scale $m/\gamma$: $\langle v(0)v(t)\rangle = (k_BT/m)\,e^{-\gamma t/m}$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §33.1, eqs. (33.4)–(33.15), Example 33.2 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, §5, eqs. (44)–(52), (59)*

^thm-b4-2-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Multiply the equation of motion by $x$ and use $x\ddot x = \frac{d}{dt}(x\dot x) - \dot x^2$:
>
> $$
> m\frac{d}{dt}(x\dot x) = m\dot x^2 - \gamma\,x\dot x + xF .
> $$
>
> Average over the ensemble: $\langle xF\rangle = 0$ and $m\langle\dot x^2\rangle = k_BT$, so $y = \langle x\dot x\rangle$ obeys $m\dot y = k_BT - \gamma y$, with $y(0) = 0$ since every particle starts at $x = 0$. Hence $y(t) = \frac{k_BT}{\gamma}(1 - e^{-\gamma t/m})$. Since $\frac{d}{dt}\langle x^2\rangle = 2y$, integrating from $0$ gives the displayed formula. For $t \ll m/\gamma$, expanding $1 - e^{-s} = s - s^2/2 + \cdots$ with $s = \gamma t/m$ leaves $\frac{2k_BT}{\gamma}\cdot\frac{\gamma t^2}{2m} = \frac{k_BT}{m}t^2$: free flight with the thermal velocity. For $t \gg m/\gamma$ the bracket is $t$ minus a constant, and comparing with $\langle x^2\rangle = 2Dt$ ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]], $d = 1$) gives $D = k_BT/\gamma$.
>
> For the correlation, multiply the equation of motion at time $t$ by $v(0)$ and average; the impacts after time $0$ are uncorrelated with $v(0)$, so $\frac{d}{dt}\langle v(0)v(t)\rangle = -\frac\gamma m\langle v(0)v(t)\rangle$, with initial value $\langle v^2\rangle = k_BT/m$.

^der-b4-2-8

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^mod-b4-2-7|Model §B4.2.7]], [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]], [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Fluctuation and dissipation; Einstein's count of molecules
> The same molecular impacts that make the particle wander (fluctuation, measured by $D$) also brake it when it is pushed (dissipation, measured by $\gamma$), and $D = k_BT\times(\text{mobility})$ ties the two through the temperature. It is the simplest case of the fluctuation–dissipation theorem (Blundell §33.5–33.6); Johnson noise in a resistor, $\langle V^2\rangle = 4k_BTR\,\Delta f$, is another. Einstein turned it into a measurement of Avogadro's number (1905): $\gamma$ is measured by pulling the particle with a known force, $D$ by watching it wander, and $N_A = R_{\text{gas}}T/(\gamma D)$ with $k_B = R_{\text{gas}}/N_A$. Note that $D$ does not depend on the particle's mass; the mass only sets how long the ballistic regime lasts.
>
> *Source: Blundell §33.1–33.2 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, §5*

^rem-b4-2-4

> [!caution] Caution: A factor of 3, and the word "mobility"
> - Schwartz averages $\vec x\cdot\vec v$ in three dimensions, obtaining $\langle\lvert\vec x\rvert^2\rangle = (2m\langle\lvert\vec v\rvert^2\rangle/\gamma)\,t$, and compares it with the one-dimensional $x_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{2Dt}$; hence his $D = \langle m\lvert\vec v\rvert^2\rangle/\gamma$, $N_A = 3RT/\gamma D$ and $D = \langle mv^2\rangle/6\pi\eta R$ (Lecture 2, eqs. (52), (53), (59)) are three times too large. With $\langle\lvert\vec x\rvert^2\rangle = 6Dt$ and $m\langle\lvert\vec v\rvert^2\rangle = 3k_BT$ his calculation gives $D = k_BT/\gamma$, as above.
> - Schwartz calls $\gamma$ itself the "mobility"; in Blundell, and here, the mobility is $1/\gamma$.
> - Blundell's eq. (33.12), as transcribed, reads $t - \frac m\gamma e^{-\gamma t/m}$; it must be $t - \frac m\gamma(1 - e^{-\gamma t/m})$, which vanishes at $t = 0$ and has the limit (33.13) that Blundell states.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B4.2.1: A Walker Who Sometimes Stays
> A one-dimensional walker steps right with probability $p$, left with $q$, and stays put with $r = 1 - p - q$. The recursion $P_n(k) = pP_{n-1}(k - 1) + rP_{n-1}(k) + qP_{n-1}(k + 1)$ gives the generating function $G_n(s) = (ps + r + q/s)^n$. Then $G_n'(1) = n(p - q)$, and
>
> $$
> \langle k^2\rangle = \Bigl[s\frac{d}{ds}\Bigl(s\frac{dG_n}{ds}\Bigr)\Bigr]_{s=1} = n(p + q) + n(n - 1)(p - q)^2, \qquad \sigma_n^2 = n\bigl[(p + q) - (p - q)^2\bigr].
> $$
>
> For $r = 0$ this is $4npq$ ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]]). Resting slows the spreading: for $p = q$ the variance is $2pn = (1 - r)n$, a walker who moves only a fraction $1 - r$ of the time — exactly what [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]] gives directly from the single-step variance $p + q - (p - q)^2$. In the language of [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]], $D = (1 - r)\ell^2/2\tau$.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 6, Problem 2 and solutions*

^ex-b4-2-1

> [!example] Example §B4.2.2: The Sum of 100 Dice
> One fair die has $\mu = 3.5$ and $\sigma^2 = \langle x^2\rangle - \mu^2 = \tfrac{91}{6} - \tfrac{49}{4} = \tfrac{35}{12}$. The sum of 100 dice therefore has mean $350$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{100 \cdot 35/12} = 17.1$, and by the central limit theorem it is approximately $\mathcal N(350, 17.1^2)$; a 95 % interval is $350 \pm 1.96 \times 17.1 = 350 \pm 33$.
>
> How good is this? The probability that the sum is at least 380: the exact distribution, by convolving 100 copies of the single-die distribution ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]]), gives $0.0420$. The Gaussian gives $0.0421$ if the integer cut is placed at $379.5$ (the "continuity correction": each integer owns the interval of width 1 around it), but $0.0395$ if placed at $380$. Already for 100 terms from a flat, decidedly non-Gaussian parent the theorem is accurate to a part in a thousand — once the discreteness is treated correctly.
>
> The user's Stat Mech concepts notes (§18) quote $\mathcal N(350, 29.2^2)$ for this sum; $29.2$ is the variance divided by ten, not the standard deviation, which is $17.1$.
>
> *Source: the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §18 (the example; σ corrected) · exact distribution computed here*

> [!example] Example §B4.2.3: How Far Does Diffusion Carry?
> *A Brownian sphere.* A sphere of radius $R = 0.5\ \mu\mathrm m$ and density $10^3\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$ in water ($\eta = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}$) at $300\ \mathrm K$ has $\gamma = 6\pi\eta R = 9.4 \times 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{kg/s}$, so by [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-8|Theorem §B4.2.8]]
>
> $$
> D = \frac{k_BT}{\gamma} = \frac{4.14 \times 10^{-21}\ \mathrm J}{9.4 \times 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{kg/s}} = 4.4 \times 10^{-13}\ \mathrm{m^2/s} .
> $$
>
> In one minute each coordinate wanders $\sqrt{2Dt} = 7\ \mu\mathrm m$ — easily seen under a microscope, which is why Brown saw it. The particle's mass is $5.2 \times 10^{-16}\ \mathrm{kg}$, so the ballistic regime lasts $m/\gamma = 6 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm s$: invisible, and the motion is purely diffusive on any observable time scale.
>
> *A small molecule.* With $D = 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$ in water, the time $t = x^2/2D$ to cover a distance $x$ is $0.5\ \mathrm{ms}$ for $1\ \mu\mathrm m$ (across a bacterium), $5 \times 10^4\ \mathrm s \approx 14\ \mathrm h$ for $1\ \mathrm{cm}$ (across a test tube), and $5 \times 10^8\ \mathrm s \approx 16$ years for $1\ \mathrm m$. In air ($D = 2 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$ for CO) a centimetre takes $2.5\ \mathrm s$, but a metre still $7$ hours: the smell of a kitchen reaches the next room by convection, not by diffusion.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 2, §4–5 (the diffusion constants) · Blundell, Exercise 3.7(c), §33.1; numbers computed here*
