Photons and phonons have $\mu = 0$ because their number is free. For bosons whose number is fixed, such as atoms, $\mu$ must be found from $N$ ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-5|Theorem §B10.1.5]]), and [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^cau-b10-2-1|Caution: The Bose formulas miss the ground state]] showed that the integral over excited states can hold only a limited number of them. This section follows the consequence: below a critical temperature a macroscopic fraction of the atoms collects in the single lowest state, although $kT$ is still far above the level spacing. This is Bose–Einstein condensation, a phase transition driven by exchange symmetry alone, with no interaction between the particles. It is computed here for the ideal gas in a box, with the condensate fraction, the transition temperature with $\zeta(\tfrac32)$, and the cusp in the heat capacity; real condensates (ultracold atoms, liquid helium, Cooper pairs) appear as remarks.

## The ground state is special

> [!model] Model §B12.1.1: Ideal Bose Gas at Fixed Particle Number
> $N$ identical non-interacting bosons of mass $m$ with $g_s$ spin states (usually $g_s = 1$, spin 0) in a box of volume $V$, in thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$; the particle number is fixed and large. Energies are measured from the single-particle ground state, $\varepsilon_0 = 0$, so $\mu < 0$ ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]). The grand canonical formulas are used with $\mu$ fixed by $\langle N\rangle = N$.
>
> *Assumes:* no interactions (real atoms interact weakly in dilute gases and strongly in liquid helium), and a box potential (laboratory gases are held in harmonic traps; see [[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation#^rem-b12-1-3|Remark: Dimension and trap]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 12, §1, §3; Schroeder §7.6; Blundell §30.3–30.4*

^mod-b12-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.1.2: Ground-State and Excited-State Occupations
> In [[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation#^mod-b12-1-1|Model §B12.1.1]] the ground state holds
>
> $$
> N_0 = \frac{1}{e^{-\beta\mu} - 1}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad -\mu = kT\ln\Bigl(1 + \frac{1}{N_0}\Bigr) \approx \frac{kT}{N_0}\quad (N_0 \gg 1),
> $$
>
> while every excited state $i$, whatever $\mu$ is, holds fewer than
>
> $$
> N_i < \frac{1}{e^{\beta\varepsilon_i} - 1} \le \frac{kT}{\varepsilon_i} .
> $$
>
> Only the ground state can hold an unbounded number of particles: as $\mu \to 0^-$, $N_0 \to \infty$ while each $N_i$ stays below a bound independent of $N$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 12, eqs. (13)–(18); Schroeder §7.6, eqs. (7.119)–(7.120); Blundell §30.4, eq. (30.55), footnote 11, Exercise 30.6*

^thm-b12-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]] with $\varepsilon_0 = 0$ gives $N_0$; solving for $\mu$ and using $\ln(1 + x) \approx x$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) gives $-\mu \approx kT/N_0$. For $\varepsilon_i > 0$, $\mu < 0$ gives $\varepsilon_i - \mu > \varepsilon_i$, so $N_i = 1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon_i - \mu)} - 1) < 1/(e^{\beta\varepsilon_i} - 1)$, and $e^x - 1 \ge x$ gives the last bound. The gap between $\mu$ and $\varepsilon_i$ never closes, so no excited state can be macroscopically occupied (Schwartz).

> [!example] Example §B12.1.1: Condensation with Two Levels
> Schwartz's toy model: $N$ particles, two single-particle levels $0$ and $\varepsilon$, small enough for the canonical ensemble. With $q = e^{-\beta\varepsilon}$:
> - *Maxwell–Boltzmann* (independent particles, $Z_N = (1 + q)^N/N!$): each particle is in the ground state with probability $1/(1 + q)$, so $\langle N_{\text{gr}}\rangle/N = 1/(1 + e^{-\beta\varepsilon}) \to \tfrac12$ once $kT \gtrsim \varepsilon$.
> - *Bosons:* a state is fixed by the number $j = 0, \dots, N$ in the upper level, $Z_N = \sum_{j=0}^Nq^j = (1 - q^{N+1})/(1 - q)$, and $\langle j\rangle = q\,\partial\ln Z_N/\partial q$ gives
>
> $$
> \langle N_{\text{gr}}\rangle = N - \langle j\rangle = \frac{N + 1}{1 - q^{N+1}} - \frac{1}{1 - q} .
> $$
>
> For $N = 100$ and $kT = 10\,\varepsilon$, $q = e^{-0.1}$: Maxwell–Boltzmann gives $52\%$ in the ground state, bosons $90.5\%$. For $1 \ll kT/\varepsilon \ll N$, $q^{N+1} \approx 0$ and $1/(1 - q) \approx kT/\varepsilon + \tfrac12$, so $\langle N_{\text{gr}}\rangle/N \approx 1 - kT/N\varepsilon$: the upper level holds only about $kT/\varepsilon$ particles, the bound of Theorem §B12.1.2, and the rest pile into the ground state even though $kT \gg \varepsilon$. Only for $kT \gtrsim N\varepsilon$ does the classical $\tfrac12$ return. With ten million particles at $kT = 10^6\varepsilon$, $90\%$ are in the ground state.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 12, §2, eqs. (1)–(12) (checked here)*

## The condensation temperature

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.1.3: Saturation of the Excited States; Condensation Temperature
> For the gas in a box, in the continuum approximation ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^def-b10-1-2|Def. §B10.1.2]]), the number of particles in excited states is at most
>
> $$
> N_{\text{ex}} \le N_{\text{ex}}^{\max}(T) = \zeta(\tfrac32)\,\frac{g_sV}{\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3} = 2.612\,g_sV\Bigl(\frac{2\pi mkT}{h^2}\Bigr)^{3/2},
> $$
>
> attained as $\mu \to 0^-$. It equals $N$ at the **condensation temperature**
>
> $$
> kT_c = \frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m}\Bigl(\frac{n}{\zeta(\tfrac32)\,g_s}\Bigr)^{2/3}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}(T_c)^3 = 2.612\,g_s ;
> $$
>
> for $g_s = 1$, $kT_c = 0.527\,(h^2/2\pi m)\,n^{2/3} = 3.31\,\hbar^2n^{2/3}/m$. Below $T_c$ the excited states cannot hold all the particles.
>
> *Source: Blundell §30.3–30.4, eqs. (30.51)–(30.53), (30.57); Schroeder §7.6, eqs. (7.122)–(7.126); Schwartz 181, Lecture 12, §3.2, eqs. (24)–(33)*

^thm-b12-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Treat the excited states by the density of states $g(\varepsilon) \propto \sqrt\varepsilon$; the ground state gets zero weight in the integral and is counted separately ([[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation#^cau-b12-1-1|Caution: Why the integral misses the condensate]]). By [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]], $N_{\text{ex}} = g_sV\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^{-3}\,\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}(z)$ with $z = e^{\beta\mu} \in (0, 1)$. $\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}(z) = \sum_kz^k/k^{3/2}$ increases with $z$ and tends to $\zeta(\tfrac32) = 2.612$ as $z \to 1$ ([[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-2|Def. §B10.2.2]]); the series converges at $z = 1$ because $\sum k^{-3/2}$ does. Equivalently, the Bose integral $\int_0^\infty x^{1/2}\,dx/(e^x - 1) = \Gamma(\tfrac32)\zeta(\tfrac32) = 2.315$ is finite, since the integrand behaves as $x^{-1/2}$ at $0$ (Schroeder). Setting $N_{\text{ex}}^{\max}(T_c) = N$ and solving with $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^{-2} = 2\pi mkT/h^2$ gives $kT_c = (h^2/2\pi m)(n/2.612g_s)^{2/3}$; with $h^2/2\pi = 2\pi\hbar^2$ and $2\pi/2.612^{2/3} = 3.31$.

^der-b12-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]], [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-2|Def. §B10.2.2]], [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]], [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^def-b10-1-2|Def. §B10.1.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §B12.1.1: Bose–Einstein Condensation; Condensate Fraction
> **Bose–Einstein condensation** is the macroscopic occupation of the single-particle ground state, $N_0/N$ of order one as $N \to \infty$ at fixed density, below the condensation temperature $T_c$. The particles in the ground state form the **condensate**, and $N_0/N$ is the **condensate fraction**; it is zero (in the limit $N \to \infty$) above $T_c$ and plays the role of an order parameter for the transition ([[§B9.3★ The Classification of Phase Transitions#^def-b9-3-3|Def. §B9.3.3]]). The condensation is in momentum space, not in position space: the condensate occupies the whole container in its lowest-momentum state.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.6; Blundell §30.4; Schwartz 181, Lecture 12, §3.2*

^def-b12-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.1.4: Condensate Fraction and Chemical Potential
> In the limit of large $N$ at fixed density,
>
> $$
> \frac{N_0}{N} = \begin{cases} 1 - (T/T_c)^{3/2}, & T < T_c,\\ 0, & T \ge T_c, \end{cases} \qquad N_{\text{ex}} = N\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_c}\Bigr)^{3/2}\quad (T < T_c) .
> $$
>
> Below $T_c$, $\mu = -kT/N_0 \approx 0$, within an amount of order $kT/N$ of the ground-state energy; above $T_c$, $\mu < 0$ is determined by $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3/g_s = \mathrm{Li}_{3/2}(e^{\beta\mu})$, i.e. $\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}(z) = \zeta(\tfrac32)(T_c/T)^{3/2}$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.6, eqs. (7.127)–(7.129), Figures 7.32–7.33; Blundell §30.4, eqs. (30.54)–(30.62); Schwartz 181, Lecture 12, eqs. (30)–(35)*

^thm-b12-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $N = N_0 + N_{\text{ex}}$ (Blundell). *Below $T_c$:* $N > N_{\text{ex}}^{\max}(T)$, so $N_0 = N - N_{\text{ex}} \ge N - N_{\text{ex}}^{\max} > 0$ is macroscopic, and by [[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation#^thm-b12-1-2|Theorem §B12.1.2]] $-\mu \approx kT/N_0 = O(kT/N)$. Then $z = e^{\beta\mu} = 1 - O(1/N)$, and $N_{\text{ex}}$ equals its maximum up to corrections that vanish as $N \to \infty$. By [[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation#^thm-b12-1-3|Theorem §B12.1.3]], $N_{\text{ex}}^{\max} \propto T^{3/2}$ with $N_{\text{ex}}^{\max}(T_c) = N$, so $N_{\text{ex}} = N(T/T_c)^{3/2}$ and $N_0/N = 1 - (T/T_c)^{3/2}$. *Above $T_c$:* a $z < 1$ with $g_sV\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^{-3}\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}(z) = N$ exists, the ground state holds $N_0 = z/(1 - z)$, which is of order one, so $N_0/N \to 0$. Dividing the equation by its value at $T_c$, where $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^{-3} \propto T^{3/2}$, gives $\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}(z) = \zeta(\tfrac32)(T_c/T)^{3/2}$, solved numerically for the figure of [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^rem-b10-2-3|Remark: Reading the chemical potential]].

^der-b12-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation#^thm-b12-1-2|Theorem §B12.1.2]], [[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation#^thm-b12-1-3|Theorem §B12.1.3]], [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Why the integral misses the condensate
> Converting $\sum_i$ into $\int g(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon$ assumes that the summand varies little from level to level. Near $\varepsilon = 0$ with $\mu \to 0$ this fails badly: $f_{\mathrm{BE}} \approx kT/\varepsilon$ diverges while $g \propto \sqrt\varepsilon$ vanishes, and the integral assigns weight zero to the one state that holds most of the particles. The integral is still right for the excited states, because $\mu$ stays a whole level spacing below each of them (Theorem §B12.1.2). The scales are (Schroeder)
>
> $$
> \varepsilon_0 - \mu \;\ll\; \varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_0 \;\ll\; kT_c, \qquad \frac{kT_c}{\varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_0} \sim N^{2/3} :
> $$
>
> condensation sets in when $kT$ is many level spacings above the ground state, unlike a single particle, which is likely to be in its ground state only when $kT \lesssim \varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_0$. Each low excited state holds about $kT/(\varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_0) \sim N^{2/3}$ particles, a vanishing fraction $\sim N^{-1/3}$ of the total, while the ground state holds a finite fraction (Blundell Exercise 30.6). For small $N$ the separation is only modest ([[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation#^ex-b12-1-2|Example §B12.1.2]]), and the transition is smeared over a range of $T$ (figure).

## Thermodynamics of the ideal Bose gas

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.1.5: Energy, Heat Capacity, Entropy and Pressure
> Below $T_c$, with $\mu = 0$ and the condensate contributing no energy, entropy or pressure,
>
> $$
> U = \frac32\,\frac{\zeta(\tfrac52)}{\zeta(\tfrac32)}\,NkT\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_c}\Bigr)^{3/2} = 0.770\,NkT_c\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_c}\Bigr)^{5/2}, \qquad C_V = \frac{15}{4}\,\frac{\zeta(\tfrac52)}{\zeta(\tfrac32)}\,Nk\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_c}\Bigr)^{3/2} = 1.926\,Nk\Bigl(\frac{T}{T_c}\Bigr)^{3/2},
> $$
>
> $$
> S = \frac53\,\frac UT = \frac52\,\zeta(\tfrac52)\,k\,\frac{g_sV}{\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3}, \qquad p = \frac{2U}{3V} = \zeta(\tfrac52)\,\frac{g_s\,kT}{\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3}\ \ (\text{independent of } V) .
> $$
>
> Above $T_c$,
>
> $$
> \frac{C_V}{Nk} = \frac{15}{4}\,\frac{\mathrm{Li}_{5/2}(z)}{\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}(z)} - \frac94\,\frac{\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}(z)}{\mathrm{Li}_{1/2}(z)} ,
> $$
>
> which tends to the classical $\tfrac32$ as $T \to \infty$. $C_V$ is continuous at $T_c$, where it reaches its maximum $1.926\,Nk$, but its slope jumps: a cusp.
>
> *Source: Blundell Examples 30.6 and 30.8, eqs. (30.46), (30.60)–(30.61), Exercise 30.4, eq. (30.65); Schroeder Problems 7.70–7.71, Figure 7.37*

^thm-b12-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Below $T_c$.* Only the excited particles carry energy, and they have $z = 1$: by [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]], $U = \tfrac32kT\,g_sV\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^{-3}\zeta(\tfrac52)$. With $g_sV\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^{-3} = N_{\text{ex}}/\zeta(\tfrac32) = (N/\zeta(\tfrac32))(T/T_c)^{3/2}$ ([[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation#^thm-b12-1-4|Theorem §B12.1.4]]) this is the first form; $\tfrac32\cdot1.341/2.612 = 0.770$. Since $U \propto T^{5/2}$ at fixed $V$, $C_V = \tfrac52U/T$, giving $1.926$. $p = 2U/3V$ ([[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]]), and $U/V \propto T^{5/2}$ does not involve $N$ or $V$. For $S$: $\Phi = -pV$ and $\mu = 0$ give $TS = U + pV - \mu N = \tfrac53U$ ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-3|Theorem §B7.3.3]]); integrating $C_V/T$ from $0$ gives the same.
>
> *Above $T_c$* ($g_s = 1$ for brevity). $U = \tfrac32NkT\,\mathrm{Li}_{5/2}(z)/\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}(z)$ with $z(T)$ fixed by $\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}(z) \propto T^{-3/2}$. Using $z\,\frac{d}{dz}\mathrm{Li}_s = \mathrm{Li}_{s-1}$ (Def. §B10.2.2) and logarithmic derivatives $D = d/d\ln T$: $D\ln\mathrm{Li}_{3/2} = -\tfrac32$ gives $D\ln z = -\tfrac32\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}/\mathrm{Li}_{1/2}$. Then, with $U \propto T^{5/2}\mathrm{Li}_{5/2}(z)$ at fixed $V$,
>
> $$
> C_V = \frac UT\,D\ln U = \frac UT\Bigl[\frac52 + \frac{\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}}{\mathrm{Li}_{5/2}}\,D\ln z\Bigr] = \frac32Nk\Bigl[\frac52\frac{\mathrm{Li}_{5/2}}{\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}} - \frac32\frac{\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}}{\mathrm{Li}_{1/2}}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> As $T \to T_c^+$, $z \to 1$ and $\mathrm{Li}_{1/2}(z) = \sum_kz^k/\sqrt k \to \infty$, so the second term vanishes and $C_V \to \tfrac{15}{4}(\zeta(\tfrac52)/\zeta(\tfrac32))Nk$, the value from below. As $T \to \infty$, $z \to 0$, every $\mathrm{Li}_s(z) \approx z$ and $C_V \to (\tfrac{15}{4} - \tfrac94)Nk = \tfrac32Nk$. The slopes at $T_c$ differ (computed: $+2.89\,Nk/T_c$ below, $-0.78\,Nk/T_c$ above).

> [!remark] Remark: A pressure that ignores the volume
> Below $T_c$ the pressure depends on $T$ alone (Schroeder Problem 7.71). Compressing the gas at fixed $T$ does not raise $p$; it only moves particles from the excited states into the condensate, which has no momentum to give the walls. The isotherms in the $p$–$V$ plane are flat below the volume at which $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3 = 2.612\,g_s$, like the coexistence region of a liquid and its vapour ([[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation]]), with the condensate in the role of a phase of zero volume and zero entropy. The entropy per excited particle is $S/N_{\text{ex}} = \tfrac52(\zeta(\tfrac52)/\zeta(\tfrac32))k = 1.28\,k$.

## Why it happens, and where

> [!remark] Remark: Why the bosons condense
> Schroeder's argument by counting. For $N$ *distinguishable* particles, a state with most particles at energies $\sim kT$ has a tiny Boltzmann factor $\sim e^{-N}$, but there are about $Z_1^N$ such states ($Z_1$ the number of thermally accessible orbitals), which overwhelms $e^{-N}$: nothing condenses. For *identical bosons* the number of such system states is only the number of ways to put $N$ indistinguishable particles into $Z_1$ orbitals, $\binom{N + Z_1 - 1}{N}$, as for energy units in an Einstein solid ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]): about $(eZ_1/N)^N$ if $Z_1 \gg N$ and $(eN/Z_1)^{Z_1}$ if $Z_1 \ll N$. When $Z_1 \ll N$ this no longer beats $e^{-N}$; the counting supplies about one boson per accessible excited orbital, and the rest drop into the ground state, which the Boltzmann factor favours. Since $Z_1 \approx V/\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3$, the crossover $Z_1 \approx N$ is $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3 \approx 1$, the degeneracy criterion of [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-1|Def. §B10.2.1]].

^rem-b12-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Dimension and trap
> Condensation needs $\int_0 g(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon/\varepsilon < \infty$, so that the excited states saturate as $\mu \to 0$.
> - *Two dimensions* (Schroeder Problem 7.72, Blundell Exercise 30.5): $g$ is constant, $N_{\text{ex}} \propto \int_0^\infty d\varepsilon/(z^{-1}e^{\beta\varepsilon} - 1) = -kT\ln(1 - z)$, which grows without bound as $z \to 1$. Every $N$ fits into the excited states with $\mu < 0$; there is no abrupt condensation in a two-dimensional box.
> - *Harmonic trap* (Schroeder Problem 7.73; the laboratory case). An isotropic trap of frequency $\omega$ has levels $n\hbar\omega$ with degeneracy $\tfrac12(n + 1)(n + 2)$, so $g(\varepsilon) \approx \varepsilon^2/2(\hbar\omega)^3$. Then $N_{\text{ex}}^{\max} = \tfrac12(kT/\hbar\omega)^3\Gamma(3)\zeta(3) = \zeta(3)(kT/\hbar\omega)^3$ ([[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]]), so $kT_c = \hbar\omega\,(N/\zeta(3))^{1/3}$ and $N_0/N = 1 - (T/T_c)^3$. The trap confines particles of energy $kT$ to a size $\sim\sqrt{kT/m\omega^2}$; with this as the "box", the result agrees in order of magnitude with Theorem §B12.1.3.

^rem-b12-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Real condensates
> - *Dilute alkali gases.* Bose–Einstein condensation of weakly interacting atoms was first achieved in 1995 with $^{87}$Rb by Cornell and Wieman (JILA), and with sodium by Ketterle (MIT); Nobel Prize 2001. Laser cooling, magnetic trapping and evaporative cooling reach $T \sim 10^{-8}$–$10^{-6}\ \mathrm K$ at densities more than seven orders of magnitude below that of liquid helium (Blundell), low enough that the gas does not solidify. Released from the trap, the condensate expands slowly and shows up as a sharp peak in the velocity distribution on top of the broad thermal cloud (Schwartz: $T_c \approx 170\ \mathrm{nK}$ for the JILA cloud; Blundell Figure 30.7). Hydrogen followed in 1998.
> - *Liquid $^4$He* becomes superfluid below $2.17\ \mathrm K$, close to the ideal-gas $T_c$ ([[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation#^ex-b12-1-3|Example §B12.1.3]]), which suggested (London, 1938) that superfluidity is a Bose–Einstein condensation. But helium is a strongly interacting liquid, and superfluidity itself cannot be understood without the interactions (Schroeder, Blundell); a non-interacting Bose gas is not superfluid.
> - *Pairs of fermions.* $^3$He atoms are fermions, yet liquid $^3$He has superfluid phases below about $3\ \mathrm{mK}$, formed by pairs of atoms, which are bosons. In a superconductor, electrons form Cooper pairs that condense ([[§A6.4 Superconductivity#^mod-a6-4-6|QM Model §A6.4.6]]); BCS theory describes many but not all superconductors (Blundell).
> - *Lasers* put macroscopic numbers of photons into one mode, but only under pumping, out of equilibrium (Schwartz; [[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^rem-b11-1-5|Remark: Stimulated emission and lasers]]).
>
> History (Blundell): Bose treated the photon gas in 1924; Einstein extended the method to atoms and predicted the condensation in 1924–25.

^rem-b12-1-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B12.1.2: Ten Thousand Rubidium Atoms in a Box
> Put $N = 10^4$ atoms of $^{87}$Rb ($m = 1.443\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$) in a cube of side $L = 10\ \mu\mathrm m$, so $n = 10^{19}\ \mathrm m^{-3}$.
> - *Ground state:* $\varepsilon_0 = 3h^2/8mL^2 = 1.14\times10^{-32}\ \mathrm J = 7.1\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{eV}$, i.e. $\varepsilon_0/k = 0.83\ \mathrm{nK}$. The three first excited states $(2, 1, 1)$, $(1, 2, 1)$, $(1, 1, 2)$ lie $3h^2/8mL^2$ higher, again $0.83\ \mathrm{nK}$.
> - *Condensation temperature* (Theorem §B12.1.3, $g_s = 1$): $T_c = 86\ \mathrm{nK}$, about $100$ times $\varepsilon_0/k$.
> - *At $T = 0.9\,T_c = 77\ \mathrm{nK}$:* $N_0 = N(1 - 0.9^{3/2}) = 1460$ (Theorem §B12.1.4). The chemical potential lies below the ground-state energy by $kT/N_0$, i.e. $0.053\ \mathrm{nK}$, far less than the gap to the first excited states. Each of these holds $1/(e^{(0.83 + 0.053)/77} - 1) \approx 87$ atoms, $262$ in all.
>
> The ground state holds 17 times as much as any other state, but the first excited states are far from empty: for so few atoms the continuum formula for $N_{\text{ex}}$ is only roughly right and the transition is smeared (figure, $N = 10^3$). With $10^6$ atoms in the same box, $T_c = 1.85\ \mu\mathrm K$, and at $0.9\,T_c$, $N_0 = 1.46\times10^5$ against about $2.0\times10^3$ in each first excited state: the ratio grows as $N^{1/3}$ (Caution above).
>
> *Source: Schroeder Problem 7.66 (solved here)*

> [!example] Example §B12.1.3: Helium-4 and Atomic Hydrogen
> *Liquid $^4$He,* density $145\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $m = 6.646\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$: $n = 2.18\times10^{28}\ \mathrm m^{-3}$, and Theorem §B12.1.3 gives $T_c = 3.1\ \mathrm K$, against the observed superfluid transition at $2.17\ \mathrm K$. The order of magnitude is right; the difference is no surprise in a dense liquid with strong interactions ([[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation#^rem-b12-1-4|Remark: Real condensates]]).
>
> *Trapped atomic hydrogen* (Fried et al., 1998): peak density $1.8\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}} = 1.8\times10^{20}\ \mathrm m^{-3}$, $m = 1.674\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$, so $T_c = 51\ \mu\mathrm K$, against the measured $50\ \mu\mathrm K$. The light mass gives a relatively high $T_c$ for its density; Schwartz notes that hydrogen was not used first only because suitable cooling lasers were lacking.
>
> *Source: Blundell Example 30.9; Schroeder Problems 7.67–7.68 (solved here); Schwartz 181, Lecture 12, §4*
