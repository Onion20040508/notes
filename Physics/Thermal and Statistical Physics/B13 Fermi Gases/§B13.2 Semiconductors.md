How many electrons conduct in a semiconductor, and how does doping change that number? Level A described the band picture: a full valence band and an empty conduction band separated by a gap ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^def-a6-3-1|QM Def. §A6.3.1]], [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-4|QM Theorem §A6.3.4]]), holes ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^def-a6-3-2|QM Def. §A6.3.2]]), donors and acceptors ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^def-a6-3-4|QM Def. §A6.3.4]]) and the diode ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-6|QM Theorem §A6.3.6]]). It estimated the occupation of the conduction band as $e^{-E_g/2k_BT}$ by putting the chemical potential at mid-gap without proof ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^ex-a6-3-1|QM Example §A6.3.1]]). Here the counting is done with the Fermi–Dirac distribution ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]) and the band densities of states. This gives the carrier densities $n$ and $p$, the law of mass action $np = n_i^2$, the position of $\mu$, the ionization of donors from Gibbs factors ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^def-b7-3-2|Def. §B7.3.2]]), and the built-in potential of a p–n junction. Unlike in a metal ([[§B13.1 The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals]]), $\mu$ lies where there are no states, and the useful limit is the opposite one: the carriers form a classical gas.

*Notation.* $\varepsilon_c$ is the bottom of the conduction band, $\varepsilon_v$ the top of the valence band, $\varepsilon_g = \varepsilon_c - \varepsilon_v$ the gap (QM's $E_g$, Schwartz's $\varepsilon_{\text{bg}}$). $n$ and $p$ are the densities (per unit volume) of conduction electrons and of holes, so $n$ no longer means $N/V$ of all electrons. $k = k_B$.

## Carriers in thermal equilibrium

> [!model] Model §B13.2.1: Two-Band Semiconductor
> The single-electron states near the gap are those of two parabolic bands: near its bottom the conduction band has the free-particle density of states of [[§B13.1 The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^mod-b13-1-1|Model §B13.1.1]] with an **effective mass** $m_e^\ast$ ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^def-a6-3-3|QM Def. §A6.3.3]]), and the valence band near its top is its mirror image with mass $m_h^\ast$:
>
> $$
> g_c(\varepsilon) = \frac{V}{2\pi^2}\Bigl(\frac{2m_e^\ast}{\hbar^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}\sqrt{\varepsilon - \varepsilon_c}\quad(\varepsilon > \varepsilon_c), \qquad g_v(\varepsilon) = \frac{V}{2\pi^2}\Bigl(\frac{2m_h^\ast}{\hbar^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}\sqrt{\varepsilon_v - \varepsilon}\quad(\varepsilon < \varepsilon_v),
> $$
>
> and $g = 0$ in the gap (apart from impurity levels, Theorem §B13.2.5). At $T = 0$ the valence band is full and the conduction band empty. The electrons do not interact and occupy states with the Fermi–Dirac distribution $f(\varepsilon)$ at one temperature $T$ and one chemical potential $\mu$ throughout the crystal. Schwartz takes $m_e^\ast = m_h^\ast = m_e$ for simplicity; Schroeder writes $g_c = g_{0c}\sqrt{\varepsilon - \varepsilon_c}$, $g_v = g_{0v}\sqrt{\varepsilon_v - \varepsilon}$ and quotes $g_{0c}/g_0 = 1.09$, $g_{0v}/g_0 = 0.44$ for silicon, with $g_0$ the free-electron constant, which corresponds to $m_e^\ast = 1.06\,m_e$ and $m_h^\ast = 0.58\,m_e$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 14, §3, eqs. (1)–(4) and footnote 3 · Schroeder §7.3, Problems 7.33–7.34*

^mod-b13-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §B13.2.1: Carrier Densities; Intrinsic and Extrinsic; Nondegenerate Semiconductor
> - The densities of conduction electrons and of holes are
>
> $$
> n = \frac1V\int_{\varepsilon_c}^\infty g_c(\varepsilon)\,f(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon, \qquad p = \frac1V\int_{-\infty}^{\varepsilon_v} g_v(\varepsilon)\,\bigl[1 - f(\varepsilon)\bigr]\,d\varepsilon ,
> $$
>
> since $1 - f$ is the probability that a valence state is empty, that is, holds a hole.
> - A semiconductor is **intrinsic** if all its carriers come from excitation across the gap, so that $n = p$; **extrinsic** if impurities supply most of them.
> - It is **nondegenerate** if $\mu$ lies more than a few $kT$ inside the gap, $\varepsilon_c - \mu \gg kT$ and $\mu - \varepsilon_v \gg kT$. Then $f$ is a Boltzmann tail across the whole conduction band, and $1 - f$ across the whole valence band.
>
> In semiconductor usage $\mu$ is often called the **Fermi level** and written $\varepsilon_F$ even at $T > 0$ (Schwartz does so). It is not the Fermi energy of [[§B13.1 The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^def-b13-1-1|Def. §B13.1.1]]: no state need exist at $\mu$, which sits in the gap (Blundell §30.2, note 4).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 14, §3 · Schroeder §7.3, Problem 7.34(b)–(c) · Blundell §30.2, note 4*

^def-b13-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.2.2: Carrier Densities of a Nondegenerate Semiconductor
> In Model §B13.2.1, if the semiconductor is nondegenerate,
>
> $$
> n = N_c\,e^{-(\varepsilon_c - \mu)/kT}, \qquad p = N_v\,e^{-(\mu - \varepsilon_v)/kT}, \qquad N_{c,v} = 2\Bigl(\frac{m_{e,h}^\ast kT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\Bigr)^{3/2} .
> $$
>
> The **effective densities of states** $N_c$ and $N_v$ are twice the quantum concentration of a particle of mass $m_e^\ast$ or $m_h^\ast$ ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]]), the factor 2 counting spin. Conduction electrons and holes are each a classical ideal gas, whose chemical potential measured from the band edge is $-kT\ln(N_c/n)$, respectively $-kT\ln(N_v/p)$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 14, eqs. (3)–(4) · Schroeder §7.3, Problems 7.33(b), 7.34(b)–(c)*

^thm-b13-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $\varepsilon \ge \varepsilon_c$ and $\varepsilon_c - \mu \gg kT$, $f(\varepsilon) = 1/(e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/kT} + 1) \approx e^{-(\varepsilon - \mu)/kT}$. With $x = (\varepsilon - \varepsilon_c)/kT$,
>
> $$
> n = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\Bigl(\frac{2m_e^\ast kT}{\hbar^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}e^{-(\varepsilon_c - \mu)/kT}\int_0^\infty x^{1/2}e^{-x}\,dx .
> $$
>
> The integral is $\Gamma(\tfrac32) = \tfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2}$ (substitute $x = u^2$ to get half the Gaussian integral, [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-7|452 Ex. §15.7]]), and $\frac{\sqrt\pi}{4\pi^2}\bigl(\frac{2m kT}{\hbar^2}\bigr)^{3/2} = 2\bigl(\frac{mkT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\bigr)^{3/2}$. For holes, $1 - f(\varepsilon) = 1/(e^{(\mu - \varepsilon)/kT} + 1) \approx e^{-(\mu - \varepsilon)/kT}$ for $\varepsilon \le \varepsilon_v$, and the same integral with $x = (\varepsilon_v - \varepsilon)/kT$ gives $p$. Solving $n = N_ce^{(\mu - \varepsilon_c)/kT}$ for $\mu - \varepsilon_c$ gives the ideal-gas form of the chemical potential ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]]).

^der-b13-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B13.2 Semiconductors#^mod-b13-2-1|Model §B13.2.1]], [[§B13.2 Semiconductors#^def-b13-2-1|Def. §B13.2.1]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-7|452 Ex. §15.7]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.2.3: Law of Mass Action
> In a nondegenerate semiconductor in equilibrium,
>
> $$
> np = n_i^2, \qquad n_i = \sqrt{N_cN_v}\;e^{-\varepsilon_g/2kT} ,
> $$
>
> whatever the position of $\mu$, hence whatever the doping. The **intrinsic carrier density** $n_i$ depends only on the material and the temperature.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 14, eq. (6) · Schroeder §7.3, Problem 7.34(d)*

^thm-b13-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Multiply the two results of Theorem §B13.2.2: $np = N_cN_v\,e^{-(\varepsilon_c - \mu)/kT}e^{-(\mu - \varepsilon_v)/kT} = N_cN_v\,e^{-\varepsilon_g/kT}$, and $\mu$ cancels. The name is borrowed from chemistry: electron + hole $\rightleftharpoons$ nothing (recombination, generation) is a reaction, and its equilibrium constant depends on $T$ only ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-6|Theorem §B3.3.6]]).

^der-b13-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B13.2 Semiconductors#^thm-b13-2-2|Theorem §B13.2.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.2.4: The Intrinsic Semiconductor
> In a pure semiconductor ($n = p$),
>
> $$
> n = p = n_i, \qquad \mu = \frac{\varepsilon_c + \varepsilon_v}{2} + \frac{kT}{2}\ln\frac{N_v}{N_c} = \frac{\varepsilon_c + \varepsilon_v}{2} + \frac34\,kT\ln\frac{m_h^\ast}{m_e^\ast} .
> $$
>
> For mirror-image bands ($m_e^\ast = m_h^\ast$) $\mu$ lies exactly at mid-gap at every temperature; otherwise it moves from mid-gap, linearly in $T$, toward the band with the smaller density of states. The occupation of the bottom of the conduction band is then $\approx e^{-\varepsilon_g/2kT}$, the estimate of QM Example §A6.3.1.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 14, §3, eqs. (2)–(5) · Schroeder §7.3, Problems 7.33(a), 7.34(a), (d)*

^thm-b13-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $n = p$ and Theorem §B13.2.3 give $n = p = n_i$. Equating the two forms of Theorem §B13.2.2, $N_ce^{(\mu - \varepsilon_c)/kT} = N_ve^{(\varepsilon_v - \mu)/kT}$, so $e^{(2\mu - \varepsilon_c - \varepsilon_v)/kT} = N_v/N_c = (m_h^\ast/m_e^\ast)^{3/2}$; take logarithms. The symmetry argument behind the mid-gap result without approximation (Schroeder, Problem 7.33(a); Schwartz §3): $f(\mu + \Delta) = 1 - f(\mu - \Delta)$, so if $g$ is symmetric about $\mu$, every electron state at $\mu + \Delta$ is occupied exactly as often as its mirror state at $\mu - \Delta$ is empty, and $n = p$ holds for mid-gap $\mu$ even beyond the Boltzmann approximation.

^der-b13-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B13.2 Semiconductors#^thm-b13-2-2|Theorem §B13.2.2]], [[§B13.2 Semiconductors#^thm-b13-2-3|Theorem §B13.2.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: What moves $\mu$ off mid-gap
> Schwartz's footnote 2 (Lecture 14) attributes the departure from mid-gap to the density of states "not being exactly symmetric around $\varepsilon_F$" and puts it at order $(\varepsilon_{\text{bg}}/\varepsilon_F)^2 \sim 0.01$. In the two-band model the departure is $\tfrac34 kT\ln(m_h^\ast/m_e^\ast)$: it is set by the ratio of the band curvatures, vanishes for equal effective masses, and grows linearly with $T$. For silicon at $300\ \mathrm K$ it is $-0.012\ \mathrm{eV}$ (Example §B13.2.1). Also, the Sommerfeld expansion of [[§B13.1 The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-3|Theorem §B13.1.3]] cannot be used here: $g$ is not smooth at $\mu$, which lies in a gap.

## Doping

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.2.5: Occupation of a Donor Level
> A donor atom whose extra electron is bound with ionization energy $I$ has its bound level at $\varepsilon_d = \varepsilon_c - I$. In equilibrium with the electrons of the crystal at $T$ and $\mu$, the donor is ionized with probability
>
> $$
> P_{\text{ion}} = \frac{1}{1 + 2\,e^{(\mu - \varepsilon_d)/kT}} ,
> $$
>
> so the density of ionized donors is $N_d^+ = N_dP_{\text{ion}}$, with $N_d$ the donor density. The factor 2 counts the two spin states of the bound electron; the occupation is not the Fermi function because the site cannot hold a second electron.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.1, Problem 7.5(a); Problem 7.35 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 14, §3.1*

^thm-b13-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Treat one donor site as a system that exchanges energy and electrons with the rest of the crystal, a reservoir at $T$ and $\mu$, so that each of its states has probability proportional to the Gibbs factor $e^{-(E - \mu N)/kT}$ ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^def-b7-3-2|Def. §B7.3.2]], [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-1|Theorem §B7.3.1]]). The site has three states: empty (ionized donor), $E = 0$, $N = 0$, factor $1$; and occupied with spin up or spin down, $E = \varepsilon_d$, $N = 1$, factor $e^{-(\varepsilon_d - \mu)/kT}$ each. Double occupation costs a large Coulomb energy and is left out. The grand partition function of the site is $\mathcal Z = 1 + 2e^{-(\varepsilon_d - \mu)/kT}$, and $P_{\text{ion}} = 1/\mathcal Z$.

^der-b13-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B13.2 Semiconductors#^mod-b13-2-1|Model §B13.2.1]], [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-1|Theorem §B7.3.1]], [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-5|QM Theorem §A6.3.5]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.2.6: Carrier Density of an n-Type Semiconductor
> Let a nondegenerate semiconductor contain $N_d$ donors per unit volume, of ionization energy $I$, and no acceptors. Charge neutrality, $n = N_d^+ + p$, fixes $\mu$, and three regimes follow.
> 1. *Freeze-out and saturation* ($n_i \ll N_d$, so $p$ is negligible):
>
> $$
> n = \frac{2N_d}{1 + \sqrt{1 + 8\,(N_d/N_c)\,e^{I/kT}}}
> \;\approx\;
> \begin{cases} \sqrt{N_cN_d/2}\;e^{-I/2kT}, & 8(N_d/N_c)e^{I/kT} \gg 1 \quad\text{(freeze-out)},\\[2pt] N_d, & 8(N_d/N_c)e^{I/kT} \ll 1 \quad\text{(saturation)}. \end{cases}
> $$
>
> 2. In saturation the electrons are the **majority carriers**, the holes the **minority carriers**, with
>
> $$
> n \approx N_d, \qquad p = \frac{n_i^2}{N_d} \ll n_i, \qquad \mu = \varepsilon_c - kT\ln\frac{N_c}{N_d} .
> $$
>
> 3. *Intrinsic regime.* With all donors ionized, $n = \tfrac12N_d + \sqrt{\tfrac14N_d^2 + n_i^2}$, which tends to $n_i$ once $n_i \gg N_d$, and $\mu$ approaches its intrinsic value of Theorem §B13.2.4.
>
> A p-type semiconductor (acceptors) is the mirror image, with holes as majority carriers and $\mu$ near the valence band.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.1, Problem 7.5(b)–(d); §7.3, Problem 7.35 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 14, §3.1, §4*

^thm-b13-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By Theorem §B13.2.2, $e^{(\mu - \varepsilon_c)/kT} = n/N_c$, so $e^{(\mu - \varepsilon_d)/kT} = (n/N_c)\,e^{I/kT}$ and, by Theorem §B13.2.5, $N_d^+ = N_d/\bigl(1 + 2(n/N_c)e^{I/kT}\bigr)$. With $p$ neglected, $n = N_d^+$ is the quadratic $2(e^{I/kT}/N_c)\,n^2 + n - N_d = 0$, whose positive root, after rationalizing, is the formula in (1). If $8(N_d/N_c)e^{I/kT} \gg 1$ the square root dominates and $n \approx 2N_d/\sqrt{8(N_d/N_c)e^{I/kT}}$; if it is $\ll 1$, $n \approx N_d$. (2) follows from Theorem §B13.2.3 and Theorem §B13.2.2. For (3), put $N_d^+ = N_d$ and $p = n_i^2/n$ into $n = N_d + p$: $n^2 - N_dn - n_i^2 = 0$.

> [!remark] Remark: Why doping works
> Phosphorus has one valence electron more than silicon. In Schwartz's tight-binding language, that electron would have to go into an antibonding state, so its level lies just below the conduction band (45 meV for P in Si, 57 meV above the valence band for an Al acceptor); the hydrogen-atom estimate with the dielectric constant and effective mass gives the same order ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-5|QM Theorem §A6.3.5]]). Since $I$ is comparable to $kT$ at room temperature, nearly all donors are ionized. A doping fraction of one part per million then fixes $n$ at $N_d \sim 10^{16}$–$10^{17}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, millions of times $n_i$, and by mass action pushes the minority density down by the same factor. Doping fractions in practice range from $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-9}$ (Schwartz).

## The p–n junction

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.2.7: Built-in Potential of a p–n Junction
> Join an n-type region (donor density $N_d$) and a p-type region (acceptor density $N_a$) of one crystal, both in saturation. In equilibrium the electrostatic potential energy of an electron is higher on the p side by
>
> $$
> eV_0 = kT\ln\frac{n_n}{n_p} = kT\ln\frac{p_p}{p_n} = kT\ln\frac{N_dN_a}{n_i^2} ,
> $$
>
> where $n_n$, $p_n$ ($n_p$, $p_p$) are the carrier densities far inside the n (p) side.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 14, §4, eqs. (7)–(8)*

^thm-b13-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Electrons diffuse from the n side, where they are many, to the p side, and holes the other way; the charged donor and acceptor ions they leave behind set up the field of the depletion layer ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^def-a6-3-5|QM Def. §A6.3.5]]). The flow stops when $\mu$ is the same everywhere, the condition for diffusive equilibrium ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]]). An electrostatic potential $\phi(x)$ shifts every electron level by $-e\phi(x)$, so the band edges are $\varepsilon_c - e\phi(x)$ and $\varepsilon_v - e\phi(x)$, while $\mu$ is constant. Apply Theorem §B13.2.2 deep in each region:
>
> $$
> \frac{n_p}{n_n} = \frac{N_c\,e^{-(\varepsilon_c^{(p)} - \mu)/kT}}{N_c\,e^{-(\varepsilon_c^{(n)} - \mu)/kT}} = e^{-(\varepsilon_c^{(p)} - \varepsilon_c^{(n)})/kT} = e^{-eV_0/kT} ,
> $$
>
> with $eV_0 = \varepsilon_c^{(p)} - \varepsilon_c^{(n)}$; the valence edges shift by the same amount, which gives the hole form. In saturation $n_n = N_d$ and $p_p = N_a$, and by Theorem §B13.2.3 $n_p = n_i^2/N_a$.

^der-b13-2-7

*Uses:* [[§B13.2 Semiconductors#^thm-b13-2-2|Theorem §B13.2.2]], [[§B13.2 Semiconductors#^thm-b13-2-3|Theorem §B13.2.3]], [[§B13.2 Semiconductors#^thm-b13-2-6|Theorem §B13.2.6]], [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Bias, rectification and light
> The same Boltzmann factor runs the diode. A forward bias lowers the step $eV_0$ by $eV_b$ and multiplies by $e^{eV_b/kT}$ the number of majority carriers able to cross; the minority current does not depend on the bias. This is the diode law $I = I_0(e^{eV_b/kT} - 1)$ of [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-6|QM Theorem §A6.3.6]]. Schwartz describes the same thing more coarsely: current flows once the applied voltage exceeds the junction potential, about $0.6\ \mathrm V$ for silicon, and under reverse bias only beyond breakdown, typically 30–50 V. In forward bias, electrons that cross into the p side fall into holes in the valence band and emit photons of energy $\approx\varepsilon_g$: for silicon, $\lambda = hc/\varepsilon_g \approx 1.1\ \mu\mathrm m$, in the infrared (a light-emitting diode).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B13.2.1: Pure Silicon at Room Temperature
> At $T = 300\ \mathrm K$, $kT = 0.02585\ \mathrm{eV}$, and for the free-electron mass $2(m_ekT/2\pi\hbar^2)^{3/2} = 2.51\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. With Schroeder's band factors, $N_c = 1.09 \times 2.51\times10^{19} = 2.74\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $N_v = 0.44 \times 2.51\times10^{19} = 1.10\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. With $\varepsilon_g = 1.11\ \mathrm{eV}$, Theorem §B13.2.3 gives
>
> $$
> n_i = \sqrt{N_cN_v}\,e^{-\varepsilon_g/2kT} = (1.74\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}})\,e^{-21.5} = 8\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}} .
> $$
>
> (Schwartz, with $m^\ast = m_e$, $\varepsilon_g = 1.08\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $kT = 0.025\ \mathrm{eV}$, gets $10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$; later he uses the commonly quoted $1.5\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$.)
>
> - *Compared with copper*, with $8.5\times10^{22}$ conduction electrons per $\mathrm{cm^3}$, that is $10^{13}$ times fewer carriers: a semiconductor is a poor conductor.
> - *Position of $\mu$.* By Theorem §B13.2.4, $\mu$ lies $\tfrac{kT}{2}\ln(N_c/N_v) = 0.012\ \mathrm{eV}$ below mid-gap, $0.54\ \mathrm{eV}$ from either band edge, far more than $kT$: the nondegenerate approximation is excellent.
> - *Temperature dependence.* $d\ln n_i/dT = \tfrac{3}{2T} + \tfrac{\varepsilon_g}{2kT^2} = 0.077\ \mathrm K^{-1}$, so $n_i$ doubles every $9\ \mathrm K$. The conductivity of a semiconductor rises steeply with $T$, while that of copper falls ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^rem-a6-3-1|QM Remark: Semiconductors get better with heat]]).
> - *Insulator or semiconductor?* A gap of $3\ \mathrm{eV}$ would give $n_i \approx 2.5\times10^{19}\,e^{-58}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}} \approx 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, less than one carrier in a cubic metre; diamond ($5.4\ \mathrm{eV}$) is an insulator. The dividing line lies at gaps of order 2 eV, as Schwartz argues from $e^{-\varepsilon_g/2kT}$ against $N_A$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.3, Problems 7.33–7.34 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 14, §3, eq. (5)*

^ex-b13-2-1

> [!example] Example §B13.2.2: Phosphorus-Doped Silicon
> Silicon contains $N_d = 10^{17}$ phosphorus atoms per $\mathrm{cm^3}$, with $I = 0.044\ \mathrm{eV}$. At $300\ \mathrm K$, $(N_d/N_c)\,e^{I/kT} = 0.00365 \times e^{1.70} = 0.020$, so by Theorem §B13.2.6
>
> $$
> n = \frac{2N_d}{1 + \sqrt{1 + 0.160}} = 0.963\,N_d = 9.6\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}} :
> $$
>
> 96% of the donors are ionized. Then
> - $\varepsilon_c - \mu = kT\ln(N_c/n) = 0.146\ \mathrm{eV}$, so $\mu$ lies $0.10\ \mathrm{eV}$ below the donor level, which is therefore mostly empty, consistently;
> - $n/N_c = 0.0035$: the conduction electrons are far from degenerate, and treating them as a classical gas is justified (Schroeder, Problem 7.35(b));
> - $p = n_i^2/n = (8\times10^9)^2/9.6\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}} \approx 7\times10^2\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ minority holes, against $8\times10^9$ in pure silicon.
>
> Freeze-out: at $80\ \mathrm K$ only about a sixth of the donors are ionized. The intrinsic carriers catch up with the donor electrons when $n_i = N_d$, at about $940\ \mathrm K$ (with the gap held fixed; the real gap shrinks somewhat on heating, which lowers this temperature). At room temperature the donors are by far the more important source (Schroeder, Problem 7.35(c)). The figure above shows the whole range.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.1, Problem 7.5(d); §7.3, Problem 7.35*

> [!example] Example §B13.2.3: A Silicon p–n Junction
> The n side has $N_d = 1.0\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ donors and the p side $N_a = 1.0\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ acceptors; $n_i = 1.5\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ (Schwartz's values). By mass action the minority densities are $p_n = n_i^2/N_d = 2.2\times10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and $n_p = n_i^2/N_a = 2.2\times10^5\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, and by Theorem §B13.2.7, with $kT = 0.025\ \mathrm{eV}$,
>
> $$
> eV_0 = kT\ln\frac{N_dN_a}{n_i^2} = (0.025\ \mathrm{eV})\ln\frac{10^{31}}{2.25\times10^{20}} = (0.025\ \mathrm{eV})(24.5) = 0.61\ \mathrm{eV} .
> $$
>
> The built-in potential, $V_0 = 0.61\ \mathrm V$, is the familiar "0.6 V" of a silicon diode. It depends on the doping only logarithmically, but on the gap linearly: $kT\ln(N_dN_a/n_i^2) = \varepsilon_g - kT\ln(N_cN_v/N_dN_a)$, which is $\varepsilon_g$ less a few tenths of an eV.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 14, §4, eqs. (7)–(8)*
