The ideal gas law ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]) was stated as an experimental fact. **Kinetic theory** explains it: pressure is the average force of molecules bouncing off the walls, computed from Newton's laws. Comparing the result with the law gives temperature a mechanical meaning, the average kinetic energy of a molecule, and with it a typical molecular speed.

## Pressure from molecular motion

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.1: Pressure of an Ideal Gas from Kinetic Theory
> For $N$ molecules of mass $m$ in a container of volume $V$, under the assumptions of the ideal gas model ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]]),
>
> $$
> pV = \tfrac{1}{3} N m\,\overline{v^2},
> $$
>
> where $\overline{v^2}$ is the average over the molecules of the squared speed.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.2, eq. (2.5)*

^thm-a2-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take a rectangular box with length $l$ along $x$ and a wall of area $A$ perpendicular to $x$, so $V = Al$; the result does not depend on this choice. Molecule $i$ has velocity components $(v_{ix}, v_{iy}, v_{iz})$.
> 1. *One collision.* The wall is rigid and the collision elastic, so $v_{ix}$ reverses while $v_{iy}, v_{iz}$ are unchanged. The molecule's momentum changes by $-2mv_{ix}$, and by Newton's third law the wall receives $+2mv_{ix}$ along $+x$.
> 2. *Average force of one molecule.* The molecule returns to the same wall after crossing the box and back, a distance $2l$, in time $\Delta t = 2l/v_{ix}$. By the impulse–momentum theorem the time-averaged force it exerts is $F_i = 2mv_{ix}/\Delta t = m v_{ix}^2 / l$.
> 3. *All molecules.* $F = \sum_{i=1}^{N} m v_{ix}^2/l = N m\,\overline{v_x^2}/l$.
> 4. *Isotropy.* $v^2 = v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2$, and no direction is preferred, so $\overline{v_x^2} = \overline{v_y^2} = \overline{v_z^2} = \tfrac13 \overline{v^2}$.
> 5. *Pressure.* $p = F/A = N m\,\overline{v^2}/(3Al) = N m\,\overline{v^2}/(3V)$.
>
> Collisions between molecules were ignored. They do not change the result: they exchange velocities but keep the motion random and isotropic, and if the motion starts out non-random, they are what makes it so. With $\sim 10^{23}$ molecules hitting the wall, the fluctuation of the force about its average is far too small to observe.

^der-a2-2-1

![[ph-th-a2-2-1.svg]]
*Step 1–2 of the derivation: the blue velocity loses its $x$-component's sign at the wall while $v_y$ is kept, so the wall receives the red impulse $2mv_x$ once per round trip of length $2l$.*

> [!remark] Remark: Counting collisions instead of round trips
> The box shape plays no role. Count instead the molecules that hit an area $A$ of the wall in a short time $dt$: those with $x$-velocity $v_x$ lie in a cylinder of volume $A v_x\,dt$, and of these only half move toward the wall. They deliver momentum $dP = \tfrac12 (N/V) A v_x\,dt \cdot 2mv_x$, so $p = \frac{1}{A}\frac{dP}{dt} = (N/V) m v_x^2$. Averaging $v_x^2$ over the molecules and using isotropy gives the same $pV = \tfrac13 N m\,\overline{v^2}$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 4 slides*

## Temperature and kinetic energy

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.2: Average Kinetic Energy and Temperature
> The average translational kinetic energy of a molecule of an ideal gas is proportional to the absolute temperature:
>
> $$
> \overline{K} = \tfrac12 m\,\overline{v^2} = \tfrac32 k_B T .
> $$
>
> It depends on nothing but $T$: not on the molecular mass, the kind of gas, the pressure or the volume.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.2, eq. (2.6)*

^thm-a2-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Both [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]] and the ideal gas law give $pV$: $\tfrac13 N m\,\overline{v^2} = N k_B T$. Cancel $N$ and multiply by $\tfrac32$.

^der-a2-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What temperature is
> Helium and xenon at the same temperature have the same $\overline K$, although a xenon atom is 33 times heavier; the xenon atoms simply move more slowly. Two gases mixed together exchange energy in collisions until their average kinetic energies, hence their temperatures, agree: this is thermal equilibrium ([[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]) seen molecule by molecule. The universality of $k_B$ in the gas law is the same fact.

^rem-a2-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.3: Internal Energy of a Monatomic Ideal Gas
> The internal energy of a monatomic ideal gas of $N$ atoms ($n$ moles) is
>
> $$
> E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac32 N k_B T = \tfrac32 nRT .
> $$
>
> It depends on the temperature alone.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.2, eq. (2.7)*

^thm-a2-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The internal energy is the sum of the mechanical energies of the molecules ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]). In a monatomic ideal gas there are no forces between atoms, so no potential energy, and an atom has only translational kinetic energy (see the remark below). Hence $E_{\text{int}} = N\overline K = \tfrac32 N k_B T$, and $N k_B = nR$.

^der-a2-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]], [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why an atom does not rotate
> A real atom is not a point, so one may ask about rotational kinetic energy. Quantum mechanics answers: rotational energy comes in discrete steps, the smallest inversely proportional to the moment of inertia. Nearly all of an atom's mass sits in a nucleus of radius below $10^{-14}\ \mathrm m$, so the first step is far above $\tfrac12 k_B T$ at any attainable temperature, and collisions cannot set an atom spinning. Molecules are different: [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^rem-a2-3-2|§A2.3]].

^rem-a2-2-3

## RMS speed

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.1: Root-Mean-Square Speed
> The **root-mean-square (rms) speed** of the molecules is the square root of the average squared speed, $v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\overline{v^2}}$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.2*

^def-a2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.4: RMS Speed and Temperature
> In an ideal gas of molecular mass $m$ (molar mass $M$, in kg/mol),
>
> $$
> v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 k_B T}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.2, eqs. (2.8)–(2.9)*

^thm-a2-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Solve $\tfrac12 m\,\overline{v^2} = \tfrac32 k_B T$ for $\sqrt{\overline{v^2}}$, then multiply numerator and denominator under the root by $N_A$: $k_B/m = N_A k_B/(N_A m) = R/M$.

^der-a2-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^def-a2-2-1|Def. §A2.2.1]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^def-a2-1-1|Def. §A2.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: rms speed is not "the" speed
> - $v_{\text{rms}}$ is neither the average speed nor the most probable speed; the molecules have a spread of speeds, and $v_{\text{rms}}$ is the largest of the three typical values ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]]).
> - The average *velocity* is zero: the molecules move in all directions equally. Large molecular speeds produce no wind.
> - Use $M$ in kg/mol in $\sqrt{3RT/M}$, not g/mol.

^cau-a2-2-1

## Mixtures of gases and vapour pressure

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.2: Partial Pressure
> The **partial pressure** of one gas in a mixture is the pressure it would exert if it alone occupied the whole volume.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.2*

^def-a2-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.5: Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures
> In a mixture of ideal gases in thermal equilibrium, with no chemical reactions between them, the total pressure is the sum of the partial pressures, $p = p_1 + p_2 + \cdots$, and each partial pressure is proportional to the number of moles of that gas: $p_1/n_1 = p_2/n_2 = \cdots = RT/V$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.2*

^thm-a2-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In equilibrium the gases share one temperature, so the molecules of each gas have the same average kinetic energy ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]). Ideal-gas molecules do not interact, so each gas strikes the walls just as it would alone and obeys $p_i V = n_i RT$ separately. The forces on a wall add, so $p = \sum p_i = \bigl(\sum n_i\bigr)RT/V$, and $p_i/n_i = RT/V$ is the same for all.

^der-a2-2-5

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^def-a2-2-2|Def. §A2.2.2]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.3: Relative Humidity; Dew Point
> In a mixture such as air, a liquid (or solid) at temperature $T$ is in equilibrium with its vapour when the *partial pressure* of the vapour equals the vapour pressure of the substance at $T$ ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^def-a1-4-3|Def. §A1.4.3]]). For water in air, the **relative humidity** is
>
> $$
> \text{R.H.} = \frac{\text{partial pressure of water vapour at } T}{\text{vapour pressure of water at } T} \times 100\% ;
> $$
>
> at $100\%$ the air is saturated. The **dew point** is the temperature to which the air must be cooled for its water vapour to start condensing, the temperature at which its partial pressure equals the vapour pressure.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.2*

^def-a2-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Partial pressures in practice
> - The partial pressure of water cannot exceed the vapour pressure: excess water condenses (dew). With air at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ and dew point $15\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, the water partial pressure is the vapour pressure at $15\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, so $\text{R.H.} = 1705\ \mathrm{Pa}/3167\ \mathrm{Pa} = 53.8\%$.
> - Breathing air with an oxygen partial pressure below about $0.16\ \mathrm{atm}$ impairs judgement, and below $0.06\ \mathrm{atm}$ can be quickly fatal. The urge to breathe responds to excess CO$_2$, not to lack of O$_2$, so an oxygen-poor room gives no warning.

^rem-a2-2-4

## Mean free path

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.4: Mean Free Path; Mean Free Time
> The **mean free path** $\lambda$ is the average distance a molecule travels between collisions with other molecules; the **mean free time** $\tau$ is the average time between its collisions.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.2*

^def-a2-2-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.6: Mean Free Path and Mean Free Time
> For an ideal gas of spherical molecules of radius $r$,
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{V}{4\sqrt2\,\pi r^2 N} = \frac{k_B T}{4\sqrt2\,\pi r^2 p}, \qquad \tau = \frac{\lambda}{v_{\text{rms}}} = \frac{k_B T}{4\sqrt2\,\pi r^2 p\, v_{\text{rms}}} ,
> $$
>
> taking the rms speed as the typical speed.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.2, eqs. (2.10)–(2.12)*

^thm-a2-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Two molecules collide when their centres come within $2r$, so a moving molecule sweeps out a cylinder of cross-section $\pi(2r)^2 = 4\pi r^2$. Suppose first that all other molecules are at rest. Over a path of length $\lambda$ the cylinder has volume $4\pi r^2\lambda$ and contains on average $(N/V)\,4\pi r^2 \lambda$ molecules; setting this equal to 1 gives $\lambda = V/(4\pi r^2 N)$.
>
> The others do move. What sets the collision rate is the *relative* velocity $\vec v_A - \vec v_B$ of two molecules. Its mean square is $\overline{v_A^2} - 2\,\overline{\vec v_A\cdot\vec v_B} + \overline{v_B^2} = 2\,\overline{v^2}$, because independent random directions give $\overline{\vec v_A\cdot\vec v_B} = 0$. So the relative speed is larger by a factor $\sqrt2$: in a given time a molecule meets $\sqrt 2$ times as many others while covering the same distance, and $\lambda$ shrinks by $\sqrt2$. Finally $V/N = k_B T/p$ from the gas law, and $\tau$ is $\lambda$ divided by a typical speed.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 4 slides (the relative-velocity factor)*

^der-a2-2-6

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^def-a2-2-4|Def. §A2.2.4]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-1|CM Theorem §A1.5.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: How the mean free path scales
> At fixed temperature $\lambda \propto 1/p$: halving the pressure doubles the free path. Doubling the molecular radius cuts it by four. The mass does not enter $\lambda$, only $\tau$ through $v_{\text{rms}}$.

^rem-a2-2-5

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.2.1: Energy and Speed of an Air Molecule
> At room temperature, $T = 20.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C = 293\ \mathrm K$, [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]] gives for *any* gas molecule
>
> $$
> \overline K = \tfrac32 k_B T = \tfrac32 (1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K})(293\ \mathrm K) = 6.07 \times 10^{-21}\ \mathrm J .
> $$
>
> A nitrogen molecule has $m = M/N_A = (0.0280\ \mathrm{kg/mol})/(6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}) = 4.65 \times 10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$, so
>
> $$
> v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3(1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K})(293\ \mathrm K)}{4.65 \times 10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}}} = 511\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> The speed is comparable to the speed of sound in air, $340\ \mathrm{m/s}$, which molecular motion carries. The energy is tiny on a human scale but dwarfs the change in gravitational potential energy across a room, $mgh \approx 1.4 \times 10^{-24}\ \mathrm J$ for $h = 3\ \mathrm m$: neglecting gravity in the model is justified.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 2.4*

^ex-a2-2-1

> [!example] Example §A2.2.2: Helium and Earth's Escape Speed
> At what temperature would helium atoms ($M = 4.00\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, $m = 6.65 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$) have an rms speed equal to the escape speed $11.1\ \mathrm{km/s}$ from the top of the atmosphere? Solving [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-4|Theorem §A2.2.4]] for $T$,
>
> $$
> T = \frac{m v_{\text{rms}}^2}{3k_B} = \frac{(6.65 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg})(11.1 \times 10^{3}\ \mathrm{m/s})^2}{3(1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K})} = 1.98 \times 10^{4}\ \mathrm K ,
> $$
>
> far above the $\approx 250\ \mathrm K$ of the upper atmosphere. Yet Earth has lost almost all its helium: speeds are spread out, and over geological time the few atoms in the fast tail of the distribution ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]]) escape. Heavier N$_2$ and O$_2$ have smaller speeds and stay.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 2.5*

^ex-a2-2-2

> [!example] Example §A2.2.3: Collisions in Argon at STP
> Argon ($M = 39.9\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, atomic radius $r = 1.70 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm m$) at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ and $1.00\ \mathrm{atm}$ has $v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3RT/M} = 413\ \mathrm{m/s}$. By [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-6|Theorem §A2.2.6]]
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{(1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K})(273\ \mathrm K)}{4\sqrt2\,\pi (1.70 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm m)^2 (1.01 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa})} = 7.3 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm m, \qquad \tau = \frac{\lambda}{v_{\text{rms}}} = 1.76 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm s .
> $$
>
> An atom collides about $6 \times 10^9$ times a second. Its free path is some 200 atomic diameters, and about 20 times the average spacing $(V/N)^{1/3} = (k_BT/p)^{1/3} = 3.3\ \mathrm{nm}$: a dilute gas, as the model assumes, yet one in which no molecule gets far before being deflected.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 2.7*

^ex-a2-2-3
