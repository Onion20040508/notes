Chapter A1 treated heat and temperature for any material. Gases deserve a chapter of their own, because they all behave nearly alike: they expand at almost the same rate ([[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion]]), and their pressure, volume and temperature obey one relation whose constant does not depend on the gas at all. This section states that relation, the ideal gas law, as an empirical law and fixes the model behind it; the next section derives it from molecular motion.

## The gas laws

> [!remark] Remark: Three proportionalities
> For a fixed amount of a dilute gas, each pair of the variables $p$, $V$, $T$ (pressure, volume, absolute temperature) is related simply when the third is held fixed:
> - **Boyle's law:** at constant $T$, $p \propto 1/V$.
> - **Charles's law:** at constant $p$, $V \propto T$.
> - **Amontons's (Gay-Lussac's) law:** at constant $V$, $p \propto T$. This is the principle of the constant-volume gas thermometer ([[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium]]).
>
> Moreover, at fixed $T$ and $V$ the pressure is proportional to the number of molecules. Combined, these say $pV/(NT)$ is a constant. The surprise is that it is the *same* constant for oxygen, helium or uranium hexafluoride.

^rem-a2-1-1

> [!law] Law §A2.1.1: Ideal Gas Law
> A gas of $N$ molecules at absolute pressure $p$, volume $V$ and absolute temperature $T$ satisfies
>
> $$
> pV = N k_B T, \qquad k_B = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K},
> $$
>
> where $k_B$ is **Boltzmann's constant**, the same for every gas. For a fixed amount of gas (a sealed container), $pV/T$ is constant, so any two states 1 and 2 obey
>
> $$
> \frac{p_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{p_2 V_2}{T_2} .
> $$
>
> *Valid when: the density is low, so that the molecules fill a negligible fraction of the volume, and the temperature is well above the boiling point (far from liquefaction). Most gases near room temperature and atmospheric pressure qualify; it fails near condensation and at pressures far above atmospheric.*
> *Source: UP2 §2.1, eqs. (2.1)–(2.2)*

^law-a2-1-1

> [!model] Model §A2.1.2: Ideal Gas
> An **ideal gas** is a gas that obeys [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]] exactly. Microscopically it is pictured as follows:
> 1. a very large number $N$ of identical molecules, each of mass $m$;
> 2. the molecules obey Newton's laws and move continually, at random and isotropically (no direction preferred);
> 3. the molecules are much smaller than their typical separation, so their own volume is negligible against the container's;
> 4. the molecules collide elastically with the walls and with each other and exert no other forces: no attraction between molecules, gravity neglected.
>
> - *Assumes:* a dilute gas far from condensation. Then the molecules are far apart compared with their size, and the forces between them act only during brief collisions.
> - *Fails:* at high density or near the boiling point, where molecular volume and attraction matter (corrected by [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-4|Law §A2.1.4]]); for liquids and solids, whose molecules interact strongly all the time.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.1–§2.2*

^mod-a2-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Absolute pressure, kelvin temperature
> The law holds only with **absolute** pressure (gauge pressure plus atmospheric pressure, [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-4|CM Def. §A9.1.4]]) and **absolute** temperature in kelvin. Tire gauges read gauge pressure; a Celsius temperature must be converted, $T = (T_C + 273.15)\ \mathrm K$. With Celsius degrees, "doubling the temperature" from $10\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ to $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ raises $p$ by only $3.5\%$, not $100\%$.

^cau-a2-1-1

## Moles and Avogadro's number

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.1: Mole; Avogadro's Number; Molar Mass
> A **mole** (mol) is the amount of substance containing as many molecules as there are atoms in $12\ \mathrm g$ of carbon-12. The number of molecules per mole is **Avogadro's number**
>
> $$
> N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}, \qquad N = n N_A ,
> $$
>
> where $n$ is the number of moles. The **molar mass** $M$ is the mass of one mole; in g/mol it equals numerically the mass of one molecule in unified atomic mass units u (a carbon-12 atom has exactly $12\ \mathrm u$). A sample of mass $m_s$ has $m_s = nM$, and the molecular mass is $m = M/N_A$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.1*

^def-a2-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.1.3: Ideal Gas Law in Terms of Moles
> For $n$ moles of an ideal gas,
>
> $$
> pV = nRT, \qquad R = N_A k_B = 8.31\ \frac{\mathrm J}{\mathrm{mol\cdot K}} = 1.99\ \frac{\mathrm{cal}}{\mathrm{mol\cdot K}} = 0.0821\ \frac{\mathrm{L\cdot atm}}{\mathrm{mol\cdot K}} ,
> $$
>
> where $R$ is the **universal gas constant**. Equal volumes of any ideal gases at equal $p$ and $T$ contain equal numbers of molecules (Avogadro's hypothesis); one mole occupies $22.4\ \mathrm L$ at STP ($0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, $1\ \mathrm{atm}$).
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.1, eq. (2.3)*

^thm-a2-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Multiply and divide the right side of $pV = N k_B T$ by $N_A$: $pV = (N/N_A)(N_A k_B)\,T = nRT$. Numerically $R = (6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}})(1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}) = 8.31\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}$. Since $n = pV/(RT)$ involves nothing about the gas, equal $p$, $V$, $T$ give equal $n$; at STP, $V/n = RT/p = (8.31)(273)/(1.01 \times 10^5)\ \mathrm{m^3} = 22.4\ \mathrm L$.

^der-a2-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^def-a2-1-1|Def. §A2.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: How large $N$ is, and why $pV$ is an energy
> - One breath of $500\ \mathrm{mL}$ of air at STP holds $N = pV/(k_B T) = 1.34 \times 10^{22}$ molecules, whatever the gas. This hugeness is why averages over molecules are so sharp.
> - $\mathrm{Pa\cdot m^3} = \mathrm{N\cdot m} = \mathrm J$: both sides of the law are energies. The right side $N k_B T$ is, up to a factor $3/2$, the total translational kinetic energy of the molecules ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]); this is why the constant is universal.
> - Use $pV = N k_B T$ when molecules are counted and $pV = nRT$ when moles are; with a non-SI value of $R$, keep the matching units.

^rem-a2-1-2

## The van der Waals equation

> [!law] Law §A2.1.4: Van der Waals Equation of State
> A better description of a real gas of $n$ moles is
>
> $$
> \Bigl[p + a\Bigl(\frac{n}{V}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr](V - nb) = nRT ,
> $$
>
> with positive constants $a$ and $b$ measured for each gas. The term $a(n/V)^2$ accounts for the attraction between molecules, which lowers the pressure on the walls and grows with density; $b$ is roughly the volume of a mole of molecules, subtracted from the volume they can move in.
>
> *Valid when: over a much wider range than the ideal gas law, including densities close to condensation; at low density ($n/V \to 0$) both corrections vanish and it reduces to $pV = nRT$. It is still approximate.*
> *Source: UP2 §2.1, eq. (2.4)*

^law-a2-1-4

## pV diagrams

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.2: pV Diagram; Isotherm
> A **$pV$ diagram** plots pressure against volume; for a fixed amount of gas each point is a state. An **isotherm** is the curve of states at one fixed temperature. For an ideal gas, $pV = $ const along an isotherm: the isotherms are hyperbolas, higher ones at higher temperature.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.1*

^def-a2-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Isotherms of a real gas and the critical point
> With the van der Waals equation the isotherms change shape as $T$ falls. Far above a **critical temperature** $T_c$ they are nearly hyperbolas. At $T_c$ the isotherm has a point of zero slope, the **critical point** $(p_c, V_c)$. Below $T_c$ the van der Waals isotherm oscillates, with a stretch where pressure *rises* as the volume grows, which is unphysical. In reality that stretch is replaced by a horizontal plateau: at the boiling pressure the volume changes at constant $p$ and $T$ as liquid turns into vapour or back (phase changes: [[§A1.4 Phase Changes]]). Above $T_c$ there is no plateau and no liquid; the fluid is **supercritical**. Carbon dioxide, for instance, has $T_c = 304.2\ \mathrm K$ ($31.1\ ^\circ\mathrm C$), $p_c = 73.2\ \mathrm{atm}$; water has $T_c = 647.4\ \mathrm K$, $p_c = 219\ \mathrm{atm}$.

^rem-a2-1-3

![[ph-th-a2-1-1.svg]]

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.1.1: A Rising Bubble
> A bubble of air leaves the bottom of a lake at $p_1 = 5.0\ \mathrm{atm}$ (absolute) and $T_1 = 4\ ^\circ\mathrm C = 277\ \mathrm K$ and reaches the surface at $p_2 = 1.0\ \mathrm{atm}$, $T_2 = 20\ ^\circ\mathrm C = 293\ \mathrm K$. How much does its radius grow? The amount of air is fixed, so by [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]
>
> $$
> \frac{V_2}{V_1} = \frac{p_1}{p_2}\,\frac{T_2}{T_1} = (5.0)\,\frac{293\ \mathrm K}{277\ \mathrm K} = 5.29, \qquad \frac{r_2}{r_1} = \Bigl(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\Bigr)^{1/3} = 1.74 .
> $$
>
> The pressure drop does most of the work; the warming adds $6\%$ to the volume. Only ratios enter, so no gas constant is needed, but both $p$ and $T$ must be absolute.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 3*

^ex-a2-1-1

> [!example] Example §A2.1.2: Density of Air; a Hot-Air Balloon
> (a) Dry air is $78\%$ N$_2$, $21\%$ O$_2$ and $1\%$ Ar by number of molecules, so its molar mass is the weighted average $M = 0.78(28.0) + 0.21(32.0) + 0.01(39.9)\ \mathrm{g/mol} = 29.0\ \mathrm{g/mol}$. At STP, one cubic metre holds
>
> $$
> n = \frac{pV}{RT} = \frac{(1.01 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa})(1\ \mathrm{m^3})}{(8.31\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)})(273\ \mathrm K)} = 44.5\ \mathrm{mol}, \qquad \rho = \frac{nM}{V} = 1.29\ \mathrm{kg/m^3} .
> $$
>
> (b) Inside a hot-air balloon at $120\ ^\circ\mathrm C = 393\ \mathrm K$ the pressure is still $1\ \mathrm{atm}$ (the balloon is open at the bottom). At fixed $p$, $\rho = pM/(RT) \propto 1/T$, so $\rho_{\text{hot}} = (273/393)(1.29\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}) = 0.896\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$. By Archimedes' principle ([[§A9.3 Buoyancy#^thm-a9-3-1|CM Theorem §A9.3.1]]) $2200\ \mathrm{m^3}$ of this hot air feel a net upward force $(\rho_{\text{air}} - \rho_{\text{hot}})Vg = 8.5\ \mathrm{kN}$, enough to lift about $870\ \mathrm{kg}$.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 2.3*

^ex-a2-1-2

> [!example] Example §A2.1.3: How Ideal Is Carbon Dioxide at STP?
> One mole of an ideal gas at $T = 273.15\ \mathrm K$ and $p = 1.013 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$ occupies $V = RT/p = 2.242 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m^3}$. For CO$_2$, $a = 0.364\ \mathrm{J\cdot m^3/mol^2}$ and $b = 4.27 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^3/mol}$. Solving [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-4|Law §A2.1.4]] for $p$ at the same $n = 1$, $V$, $T$:
>
> $$
> p = \frac{RT}{V - b} - \frac{a}{V^2} = (1.0149 \times 10^5 - 724)\ \mathrm{Pa} = 1.008 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa} .
> $$
>
> This is $0.5\%$ below the ideal value: the finite molecular volume raises the pressure by $0.2\%$, the attraction lowers it by $0.7\%$. Even a strongly attracting molecule like CO$_2$ is nearly ideal at STP.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 4*

^ex-a2-1-3
