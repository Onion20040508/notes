Knowing the internal energy of an ideal gas as a function of temperature ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]]) means knowing how much heat it takes to warm it. For a monatomic gas this gives a heat capacity that matches experiment exactly. Molecules can also rotate and vibrate, and the rule that sorts out how much energy goes where is equipartition.

**Setup.** [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]] heated at constant volume, so no work is done and all the heat goes into the internal energy. Equipartition is classical: it counts only the active degrees of freedom, and rotations and vibrations freeze out at low temperature for a quantum reason ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^rem-a2-3-2|Remark: Frozen degrees of freedom]]). A solid is modelled as atoms on springs, classical, near room temperature. Heat capacities are molar.

## Heat capacity at constant volume

> [!definition] Definition §A2.3.1: Molar Heat Capacity at Constant Volume
> The **molar heat capacity at constant volume** of a substance is
>
> $$
> C_V = \frac{1}{n}\,\frac{Q}{\Delta T} \quad (V \text{ held constant}), \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad Q = n C_V\,\Delta T ,
> $$
>
> where $Q$ is the heat that raises the temperature of $n$ moles by $\Delta T$. It is the per-mole counterpart of the specific heat $c$ in $Q = mc\,\Delta T$ ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^def-a1-3-2|Def. §A1.3.2]]). For a gas the condition matters: heated at constant pressure it also expands and takes more heat ($C_p$, [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^def-a3-5-1|Def. §A3.5.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.3, eq. (2.13)*

^def-a2-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.3.1: Heat Capacity of a Monatomic Ideal Gas
> For a monatomic ideal gas
>
> $$
> C_V = \tfrac32 R = 12.5\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)},
> $$
>
> independent of temperature and the same for every monatomic gas.
>
> *Model:* [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]], monatomic; *applies:* classical, constant volume, translational energy only.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.3*

^thm-a2-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At constant volume the walls do not move, so no work is done, and by energy conservation all the heat goes into internal energy: $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q$ (this is the first law with $W = 0$, [[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]). With $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac32 nRT$, $Q = \Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac32 nR\,\Delta T$; comparing with $Q = nC_V\,\Delta T$ gives $C_V = \tfrac32 R$. Since this does not depend on $T$, finite differences are as good as derivatives here.

^der-a2-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^def-a2-3-1|Def. §A2.3.1]], [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]], [[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]

## Degrees of freedom and equipartition

> [!definition] Definition §A2.3.2: Degree of Freedom
> A **degree of freedom** is an independent way in which a molecule can hold energy: each of the three directions of translation, each axis of rotation, and, for each mode of vibration, two (its kinetic and its potential energy). Their number is denoted $d$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.3*

^def-a2-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.3.2: Equipartition of Energy
> In a classical system in thermal equilibrium, energy is shared equally among the active degrees of freedom: on average each holds $\tfrac12 k_B T$ per molecule. Hence an ideal gas whose molecules have $d$ active degrees of freedom has
>
> $$
> E_{\text{int}} = \frac d2\, N k_B T = \frac d2\, nRT, \qquad C_V = \frac d2\, R .
> $$
>
> *Model:* any classical system in thermal equilibrium; the gas formulas for [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]] with $d$ active degrees of freedom; *applies:* only unfrozen degrees of freedom count ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^rem-a2-3-2|Remark: Frozen degrees of freedom]]); level B: [[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]].
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.3, eq. (2.14)*

^thm-a2-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Translation (proved here).* By [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]] $\tfrac12 m\,\overline{v^2} = \tfrac32 k_BT$, and by isotropy $\overline{v_x^2} = \overline{v_y^2} = \overline{v_z^2} = \tfrac13\overline{v^2}$, so $\tfrac12 m\,\overline{v_x^2} = \tfrac12 k_B T$, and the same for $y$ and $z$: each translational degree of freedom carries exactly $\tfrac12 k_BT$.
>
> *Rotation and vibration (idea only).* Collisions pass energy between all forms of molecular motion: a molecule that only translated would soon be set rotating and vibrating. The claim that the sharing ends with $\tfrac12 k_BT$ in *every* degree of freedom is a result of statistical mechanics. It follows from the Boltzmann factor $e^{-E/k_BT}$ ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^rem-a2-4-2|§A2.4]]) for every energy term that is quadratic in a velocity or a coordinate, under technical conditions beyond level A; the proof is at level B: [[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]], with the conditions spelled out in [[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^rem-b8-2-1|Remark: The layer change from level A]].
>
> *Consequence.* Adding $\tfrac12 k_BT$ per degree of freedom over $N$ molecules gives $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac d2 N k_BT$. At constant volume $Q = \Delta E_{\text{int}}$ as in [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]], so $C_V = \tfrac d2 R$.

^der-a2-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^def-a2-3-2|Def. §A2.3.2]], [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Status of equipartition
> University Physics quotes the theorem (due to Maxwell) without proof. It is kept here as a theorem rather than a principle because it *is* derivable, from statistical mechanics; level A proves only the translational part. It is also only classical: quantum mechanics decides which degrees of freedom are active, as the next remark shows.

^rem-a2-3-1

> [!law] Law §A2.3.3: Measured Molar Heat Capacities of Gases
> Measured $C_V/R$ at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ and $1\ \mathrm{atm}$:
>
> | Molecules | Gas: $C_V/R$ |
> | --- | --- |
> | monatomic | Ar $1.50$, He $1.50$, Ne $1.50$ |
> | diatomic | CO $2.50$, N$_2$ $2.50$, H$_2$ $2.47$, O$_2$ $2.53$, F$_2$ $2.8$ |
> | triatomic | CO$_2$ $3.48$, H$_2$S $3.13$, N$_2$O $3.66$ |
>
> Near room temperature, then, $C_V \approx \tfrac32 R$ for monatomic and $C_V \approx \tfrac52 R$ for most diatomic gases, while triatomic gases scatter. The $C_V$ of a diatomic gas depends on temperature. For hydrogen it rises in three plateaus: $C_V \approx \tfrac32 R$ below about $60\ \mathrm K$, $\tfrac52 R$ from about room temperature (a bit below $300\ \mathrm K$) to about $600\ \mathrm K$, and $\tfrac72 R$ above about $3000\ \mathrm K$.
>
> *Valid when:* dilute gases heated at constant volume ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^def-a2-3-1|Def. §A2.3.1]]); the table holds at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ and $1\ \mathrm{atm}$. The temperatures of the hydrogen plateaus are approximate, and the steps between them are gradual.
> *Source: UP2 §2.3, Table 2.3, Figure 2.13*

^law-a2-3-3

> [!remark] Remark: Frozen degrees of freedom
> Rotational and vibrational energies are quantised. A degree of freedom contributes only when $k_BT$ is comparable to its first energy step; below that, collisions cannot excite it and it is "frozen out". This accounts for the plateaus of hydrogen H$_2$ in [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^law-a2-3-3|Law §A2.3.3]]:
> - below about $60\ \mathrm K$ only translation is active: $d = 3$, $C_V = \tfrac32 R$;
> - from about room temperature to $600\ \mathrm K$ the two rotations (about axes perpendicular to the molecule) are fully active: $d = 5$, $C_V = \tfrac52 R$;
> - above about $3000\ \mathrm K$ the vibration along the axis adds two more: $d = 7$, $C_V = \tfrac72 R$, the classical value.
>
> Rotations unfreeze at much lower temperature in molecules than in atoms because molecules have much larger moments of inertia. At room temperature one therefore takes $d = 3$ (monatomic), $d = 5$ (diatomic) and $d = 6$ (polyatomic: a non-linear molecule rotates about all three axes).

^rem-a2-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Comparison with experiment
> Set against equipartition with the room-temperature values of $d$ ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]), the measured values of [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^law-a2-3-3|Law §A2.3.3]] agree excellently for monatomic and well for diatomic gases, only fairly for triatomic ones: interactions within and between molecules, ignored here, start to matter (partly active vibrations push $C_V$ up).

^rem-a2-3-3

> [!caution] Caution: $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac52 nRT$ is not literally true
> Because $C_V$ of a diatomic gas changes with temperature, its internal energy is not $\tfrac52 nRT$ measured from absolute zero. What equipartition gives near room temperature is the *change*: $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac52 nR\,\Delta T$. For monatomic ideal gases $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac32 nRT$ holds as it stands.

## Solids

> [!law] Law §A2.3.4: Law of Dulong and Petit
> At room temperature the molar heat capacity of most solid elements is close to
>
> $$
> C \approx 3R \approx 25\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)} .
> $$
>
> *Valid when:* most elements, near room temperature; the agreement is only fair. It fails for every element at low temperature, for the same quantum reason as the frozen degrees of freedom of a gas ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^rem-a2-3-2|Remark: Frozen degrees of freedom]]); already at room temperature for some light elements, such as beryllium and carbon; and for some heavier elements, for reasons beyond this level.
> *Source: UP2 §2.3*

^law-a2-3-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.3.5: Dulong–Petit Value from Equipartition
> Model a solid element as atoms each bound to its neighbours by springs, free to vibrate along $x$, $y$ and $z$. Then classical equipartition gives the molar heat capacity
>
> $$
> C = 3R \approx 25\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)} ,
> $$
>
> the value observed in [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^law-a2-3-4|Law §A2.3.4]].
>
> *Model:* atoms on springs, three classical oscillators per atom (the classical case of level B's [[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^mod-b8-2-6|Model §B8.2.6]]); *applies:* classical equipartition, so high temperature only; at low temperature the vibrations freeze ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^rem-a2-3-2|Remark: Frozen degrees of freedom]]; level B: [[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^thm-b8-2-7|Theorem §B8.2.7]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §2.3*

^thm-a2-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each atom vibrates in three independent directions, and each vibration has a kinetic and a potential degree of freedom, so $d = 6$. Equipartition gives $\tfrac62 k_BT = 3k_BT$ per atom, $E_{\text{int}} = 3nRT$ per $n$ moles, and $C = 3R$. (For a solid the volume hardly changes on heating, so the distinction between constant volume and constant pressure is negligible.)

^der-a2-3-5

*Uses:* [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]], [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^def-a2-3-2|Def. §A2.3.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Where Dulong–Petit fails
> The range of [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^law-a2-3-4|Law §A2.3.4]] is narrow (its *Valid when* line). For calculations, look up the measured specific heat ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^law-a1-3-2|Law §A1.3.2]]) rather than estimating it from $3R$.

^cau-a2-3-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.3.1: Which Gas Warms More?
> One mole of a monatomic ideal gas and one mole of a diatomic ideal gas whose molecules can rotate start at room temperature, and each receives the same heat $Q$ at constant volume. Then $\Delta T = Q/(nC_V)$, with $C_V = \tfrac32 R$ and $\tfrac52 R$ respectively:
>
> $$
> \frac{\Delta T_{\text{mono}}}{\Delta T_{\text{di}}} = \frac{5/2}{3/2} = \frac53 .
> $$
>
> The monatomic gas warms more, because the diatomic gas stores part of the heat in rotation. The molar masses are irrelevant (equipartition counts degrees of freedom, not mass), and active vibrations would only raise the diatomic $C_V$ further, so the answer does not change.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Midterm 1 practice*

^ex-a2-3-1

> [!example] Example §A2.3.2: Heating Xenon in a Rigid Cylinder
> A rigid cylinder holds $0.125\ \mathrm{kg}$ of xenon ($M = 131.3\ \mathrm{g/mol}$), an ideal monatomic gas, at $20.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. Outside on a hot day, $180\ \mathrm J$ of heat flow into it. The volume is fixed and $n = 125\ \mathrm g/(131.3\ \mathrm{g/mol}) = 0.952\ \mathrm{mol}$, so by [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]]
>
> $$
> \Delta T = \frac{Q}{n C_V} = \frac{2Q}{3nR} = \frac{2(180\ \mathrm J)}{3(0.952\ \mathrm{mol})(8.31\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)})} = 15.2\ \mathrm K ,
> $$
>
> and the gas ends at $35.2\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. A kelvin and a Celsius degree are the same size, so temperature *differences* may be taken in either.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 2.8*

^ex-a2-3-2

> [!example] Example §A2.3.3: Calorimetry with a Gas
> In an insulated container, $12.0\ \mathrm{mol}$ of air (diatomic, $C_V = \tfrac52 R$) at $95.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ is in contact with $300\ \mathrm g$ of solid gallium at its melting point, $30.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. At equilibrium $202\ \mathrm g$ of the gallium has melted; the volume of air is fixed. Some gallium is still solid, so the final temperature is the melting point. The heats sum to zero ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^thm-a1-3-5|Theorem §A1.3.5]]): the air loses $n C_V\,\Delta T$, the melting gallium gains $m L_f$:
>
> $$
> (12.0\ \mathrm{mol})\bigl(\tfrac52\bigr)\bigl(8.31\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}\bigr)(30.0 - 95.0)\ \mathrm K + (0.202\ \mathrm{kg})\,L_f = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad L_f = 80.2\ \mathrm{kJ/kg} .
> $$
>
> Equipartition supplies the heat capacity of the gas from the number of atoms in its molecule alone; the latent heat ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^law-a1-4-3|Law §A1.4.3]]) does the rest.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 2.9*

^ex-a2-3-3
