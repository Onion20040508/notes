The first law ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]) relates heat, work and internal energy for any change of state. To use it one needs to know *how* the state changes. This section names the idealised ways of changing state that the rest of thermodynamics is built from, and reads off what the first law says about each.

## Processes and the quasi-static idealisation

> [!definition] Definition §A3.4.1: Thermodynamic Process
> A system's state is described by its **thermodynamic variables**: for an ideal gas, pressure, volume, temperature and the number of moles; for a stretched rubber band, tension, length, temperature and mass. A **thermodynamic process** is the manner in which the system changes from an initial state to a final state through interaction with its surroundings.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.4*

^def-a3-4-1

> [!model] Model §A3.4.1: Quasi-Static Process
> A **quasi-static process** is carried out so slowly, in infinitesimal steps, that at every instant the system is in thermodynamic equilibrium, internally and with its surroundings. Its variables ($p$, $T$, …) are then well defined throughout, and the process is a curve in the space of states, for instance on a $pV$ diagram. A process that is not quasi-static has no such curve: only its end states are known.
>
> - *Assumes:* the changes imposed (moving a piston, warming a bath) are slow compared with the time the system takes to return to equilibrium. Heating $1\ \mathrm{kg}$ of water from $20$ to $21\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ in a bath whose temperature is raised infinitesimally slowly is the model.
> - *Fails:* every real finite change is to some degree non-quasi-static. Placing the water directly in a $21\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ bath, a gas rushing into a vacuum, or an explosion are far from it.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.4*

^mod-a3-4-1

> [!model] Model §A3.4.2: Heat Reservoir
> A **heat reservoir** (heat bath) is a system so large that it can exchange any amount of heat with another system without changing its own temperature.
>
> - *Assumes:* the reservoir's heat capacity is effectively infinite compared with the system's: a large water tank, the atmosphere.
> - *Fails:* for a finite bath, whose temperature has to be held by adding or removing energy.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.4*

^mod-a3-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Quasi-static versus reversible
> A **reversible** process can be made to retrace its path, system and surroundings both, by infinitesimal changes in the surroundings. A reversible process must be quasi-static, but a quasi-static one need not be reversible: with friction between piston and cylinder, the energy dissipated cannot be recovered by reversing the motion. Reversibility belongs to the second law: [[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes]].

^rem-a3-4-1

## The basic processes

> [!definition] Definition §A3.4.2: Isothermal, Isobaric, Isochoric, Adiabatic and Cyclic Processes
> A process is
> - **isothermal** if the temperature of the system stays constant;
> - **isobaric** if its pressure stays constant;
> - **isochoric** if its volume stays constant;
> - **adiabatic** if no heat enters or leaves the system, $Q = 0$;
> - **cyclic** if the system ends in its initial state.
>
> Many processes are none of these.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.4*

^def-a3-4-2

![[ph-th-a3-4-1.svg]]
*Four quasi-static expansions of an ideal gas from the same state $A$. Blue: isobaric, a horizontal line. Green: isothermal, a hyperbola $pV = $ const (the dashed grey line continues it). Red: adiabatic, $pV^\gamma = $ const, steeper than the isotherm because the gas cools as it expands ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes]]). Orange: isochoric, a vertical line, along which no work is done.*

> [!remark] Remark: How the processes are realised
> - *Isothermal.* A cylinder of gas with a free piston sits in a large water tank (a reservoir); weights on the piston balance the gas pressure. Removing the weights one tiny step at a time lets the gas expand; each step cools it momentarily, and heat flows in from the bath until the temperature is restored. The energy for the work ultimately comes from the bath. To be isothermal throughout, the process must be quasi-static: the temperature has to be defined at every step.
> - *Adiabatic.* The system is insulated, or the process is so fast that no appreciable heat flows (the compression stroke of an engine). Unlike the isothermal process, an adiabatic one may be quasi-static or not: a free expansion into vacuum is adiabatic but far from quasi-static.

^rem-a3-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.4.3: The First Law in the Basic Processes
> | Process | Condition | First law gives |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | isochoric | $W = 0$ | $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q$ |
> | isobaric | $W = p\,\Delta V$ | $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q - p\,\Delta V$ |
> | adiabatic | $Q = 0$ | $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = -W$ |
> | cyclic | $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$ | $Q = W$ |
> | isothermal, ideal gas | $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$ | $Q = W = nRT\ln(V_2/V_1)$ |
>
> In an adiabatic expansion ($W > 0$) the internal energy falls, and for a gas the temperature drops; an adiabatic compression heats it.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.4*

^thm-a3-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert each condition into $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q - W$. The isobaric and isochoric works are [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]]; the cyclic case is [[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]. For an ideal gas the internal energy depends on $T$ alone ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^rem-a3-2-3|§A3.2]]), so an isothermal process has $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$, and $W$ is [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-3|Theorem §A3.2.3]]: all the heat absorbed leaves again as work.

^der-a3-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]], [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-3|Theorem §A3.2.3]], [[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]], [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Isothermal is not adiabatic
> "The internal energy of an ideal gas does not change in an isothermal process" does not make the process adiabatic. Isothermal means $\Delta T = 0$, hence $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$ for an ideal gas, while heat flows in or out ($Q = W$). Adiabatic means $Q = 0$, and then the temperature changes unless $W = 0$. The two coincide only when $Q = W = 0$, as in a free expansion ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-1|Theorem §A3.6.1]]).

^cau-a3-4-1

## Work in a cycle

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.4.4: Net Work of a Cycle
> In a quasi-static cyclic process the net work done by the system equals the area enclosed by the cycle on the $pV$ diagram, counted positive if the cycle runs clockwise and negative if it runs counterclockwise. By the first law, the net heat absorbed is the same.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.3–§3.4 (Example 3.4)*

^thm-a3-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let the cycle run clockwise between volumes $V_1 < V_2$: it expands along an upper branch $p_u(V)$ and is compressed along a lower branch $p_\ell(V)$. By [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]] the expansion contributes the area under the upper branch, and the compression minus the area under the lower one:
>
> $$
> W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} p_u\,dV + \int_{V_2}^{V_1} p_\ell\,dV = \int_{V_1}^{V_2}(p_u - p_\ell)\,dV ,
> $$
>
> the enclosed area. Running the cycle the other way changes the sign of every $dV$, hence of $W$. Finally $Q = W$ by [[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]. For cycles whose shape is not a simple upper and lower branch, Green's theorem gives the same result (see Connections).

^der-a3-4-4

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]], [[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^thm-16-1|452 Thm. §16.1]]

## Example

> [!example] Example §A3.4.1: One Expansion, Three Ways
> An ideal monatomic gas expands slowly to twice its volume, doing $W = 300\ \mathrm J$ of work. Find $Q$ and $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$ if the expansion is (a) isothermal, (b) adiabatic, (c) isobaric. Use [[§A3.4 Thermodynamic Processes#^thm-a3-4-3|Theorem §A3.4.3]] and $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac32 nRT$.
> - (a) Isothermal: $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$, so $Q = W = 300\ \mathrm J$.
> - (b) Adiabatic: $Q = 0$, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = -W = -300\ \mathrm J$; the gas cools.
> - (c) Isobaric: $W = p\,\Delta V = nR\,\Delta T = 300\ \mathrm J$, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac32 nR\,\Delta T = 450\ \mathrm J$ and $Q = \Delta E_{\text{int}} + W = 750\ \mathrm J$; the gas warms.
>
> The same work costs $750\ \mathrm J$, $300\ \mathrm J$ or no heat at all, depending on how much the internal energy changes along the way. The doubling of the volume is not even needed: only the type of process and $W$ enter.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 4*

^ex-a3-4-1

> [!remark]- Connections
> - In general the net work of a cycle $\mathcal C$ is $W = \oint_{\mathcal C} p\,dV$. Green's theorem ([[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^thm-16-1|452 Thm. §16.1]]) in the $(V, p)$ plane, with the form $p\,dV + 0\,dp$, turns it into $\iint_D (0 - 1)\,dV\,dp = -\operatorname{area}(D)$ for the positive (counterclockwise) orientation: clockwise cycles give $+\operatorname{area}$. The same computation in mechanics measures how far a force is from conservative ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces]]).
> - Cycles that turn heat into work are heat engines: [[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps]].
