Chapter A2 looked inside a gas, at its molecules. Thermodynamics takes the outside view: it describes a system by a few macroscopic variables and asks how energy enters and leaves it. As in mechanics, where the first step is to choose the system and list the external forces, the first step here is to say what the system is, what surrounds it, and which variables fix its state.

## System and surroundings

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.1: Thermodynamic System; Surroundings; Boundary
> A **thermodynamic system** is whatever part of the world is chosen for study. Everything else is its **surroundings** (or environment), and the imagined surface separating the two is the **boundary**. Across the boundary the system can exchange heat with its surroundings, have work done on it or do work on them, and possibly exchange matter. Systems are classified by what crosses the boundary:
> - **open**: both matter and energy can cross it;
> - **closed**: energy (heat, work) can cross it, but no matter, so the amount of matter is fixed;
> - **isolated**: neither matter nor energy crosses it.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.1 · PHY 287, week 1 slides*

^def-a3-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Choosing the boundary
> In a car engine one may take as the system the burning fuel–air mixture in one cylinder. The piston, exhaust, radiator and outside air are the surroundings, and the boundary is the inner surface of the cylinder and the piston face. The immediate surroundings interact most strongly with the system. A boiling kettle is open: it loses heat and steam. A pressure cooker lets out only a little steam, so it exchanges heat but almost no matter: nearly closed. A gas sealed in a cylinder with a movable piston is closed: heat flows through the walls and the piston does work, but no gas enters or leaves. A truly isolated system is an idealisation: a gas within rigid, insulating walls, or the whole universe. Just as with external forces in mechanics ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^def-a3-3-1|CM Def. §A3.3.1]]), what counts as heat or work *on the system* depends on where the boundary is drawn, so it must be drawn first.

^rem-a3-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: University Physics uses other words
> University Physics has only two classes: it calls every system that exchanges energy or matter "open", so a gas sealed in a piston cylinder counts as open there, and it uses "closed system" as a synonym for isolated ("isolated or closed system"), while also writing the equation of state "for a closed system" in the sense used here (fixed amount of matter). These notes follow the standard three-way usage of Definition §A3.1.1: a sealed piston cylinder is **closed**, and a system exchanging nothing is **isolated**. When the book says "closed" about the second law (entropy of a "closed system" never decreases), read **isolated**.

^cau-a3-1-1

## Equilibrium and the equation of state

> [!remark] Remark: Thermal equilibrium and temperature
> Thermodynamics treats a system as uniform: its macroscopic properties are averages over its molecules, the same throughout, and the system is in **equilibrium**. For heat exchange the balance condition is thermal equilibrium: bodies in thermal contact reach a state with no net heat flow, and are then at the same temperature. That this defines temperature consistently is the zeroth law ([[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^pr-a1-1-1|Principle §A1.1.1]]): if body 1 is in thermal equilibrium with bodies 2 and 3, then 2 and 3 are in thermal equilibrium with each other. Applied to the parts of one system, it says a system in thermal equilibrium has one temperature throughout.

^rem-a3-1-2

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.2: State Variables; Equation of State
> A system in equilibrium is described by a few measurable macroscopic quantities chosen as its coordinates, its **state variables**, such as pressure $p$, volume $V$ and temperature $T$ for a fluid (for a stretched rubber band: tension, length and temperature). For a fixed amount of a uniform substance, three such variables are not independent; the relation between them is the system's **equation of state**,
>
> $$
> f(p, V, T) = 0 .
> $$
>
> It exists for every such system in principle, but is known explicitly only for some, found by experiment or theory. For an ideal gas it is $f(p, V, T) = pV - nRT$ ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]); the van der Waals equation ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-4|Law §A2.1.4]]) is another.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.1, eq. (3.2)*

^def-a3-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Two variables fix the state
> Because of the equation of state, any two of $p$, $V$, $T$ determine the third: wherever $\partial f/\partial T \ne 0$, the equation $f(p, V, T) = 0$ can be solved locally for $T = T(p, V)$, and similarly for the others. So an equilibrium state of a fixed amount of fluid is a point of the $(V, p)$ plane, and the $pV$ diagram ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^def-a2-1-2|Def. §A2.1.2]]) is a map of all its equilibrium states. Every other state property, such as the internal energy, is then a function of two variables, for instance $E_{\text{int}}(p, V)$ or $E_{\text{int}}(T, V)$.

^rem-a3-1-3

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.3: Extensive and Intensive Variables
> A state variable is **extensive** if it is proportional to the amount of matter: it doubles when the amount of matter doubles with all intensive variables unchanged (volume, number of moles, total energy). It is **intensive** if it does not depend on the amount of matter (pressure, temperature).
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.1*

^def-a3-1-3

## Example

> [!example] Example §A3.1.1: Doubling a System
> Put two identical samples of gas, each at pressure $p$ and temperature $T$ with volume $V$, $n$ moles and internal energy $E_{\text{int}}$, side by side and remove the wall between them. Nothing happens: the combined system has $p$ and $T$ (intensive, unchanged) but $2V$, $2n$ and $2E_{\text{int}}$ (extensive, doubled). The ratio of two extensive variables is intensive: the molar volume $V/n$, the density, and the energy per mole are the same for the combined system. In $pV = nRT$ both sides are extensive, as they must be.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.1*

^ex-a3-1-1
