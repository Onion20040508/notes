In an adiabatic process no heat crosses the boundary, so by the first law all the work done by the gas comes out of its internal energy: $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = -W$ ([[§A3.4 Thermodynamic Processes#^thm-a3-4-3|Theorem §A3.4.3]]). An adiabatically compressed gas heats up, as in an engine cylinder or a bicycle pump; an expanding one cools. This section works out the two extreme cases for an ideal gas: the free expansion, which does no work, and the quasi-static adiabatic expansion, which follows the curve $pV^\gamma = $ const.

## Free expansion

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.6.1: Free Expansion of an Ideal Gas
> A gas confined to one side of a thermally insulated, rigid container is released into the evacuated other side (a **free expansion**). Then $Q = 0$, $W = 0$ and $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$. For an ideal gas the temperature does not change: $T_f = T_i$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.6*

^thm-a3-6-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The container is insulated, so $Q = 0$. The gas expands against a vacuum ($p = 0$ on the other side) and the outer walls do not move, so it does no work on its surroundings: $W = 0$. The first law gives $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$. The process is violent and not quasi-static, but the first law needs only equilibrium end states. For an ideal gas $E_{\text{int}}$ depends on $T$ alone, so $T$ is unchanged.

^der-a3-6-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Free expansion of real gases
> For a real gas $E_{\text{int}} = \sum(\overline K_i + \overline U_i)$ still stays fixed, but the potential energy of interaction now changes as the molecules move apart. Real molecules attract each other, so separating them *raises* $\sum \overline U_i$ (makes it less negative); the kinetic energy, hence the temperature, must fall. A real gas cools slightly in a free expansion. A gas of particles that repel each other, such as an electron gas, would warm up instead, because separating them lowers their potential energy.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 4*

^rem-a3-6-1

## Quasi-static adiabatic processes

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.6.2: Adiabatic Condition for an Ideal Gas
> In a quasi-static adiabatic process of an ideal gas with constant heat capacities and $\gamma = C_p/C_V$,
>
> $$
> pV^{\gamma} = \text{const}, \qquad TV^{\gamma - 1} = \text{const}, \qquad p^{1-\gamma}\,T^{\gamma} = \text{const} .
> $$
>
> For two states on the same adiabat, $p_1V_1^\gamma = p_2V_2^\gamma$ and $T_1V_1^{\gamma-1} = T_2V_2^{\gamma-1}$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.6, eqs. (3.12)–(3.14)*

^thm-a3-6-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In a small quasi-static step $dQ = 0$, $dW = p\,dV$, and $dE_{\text{int}} = nC_V\,dT$ ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]), so the first law reads
>
> $$
> nC_V\,dT = -p\,dV = -\frac{nRT}{V}\,dV \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dT}{T} = -\frac{R}{C_V}\,\frac{dV}{V} = -(\gamma - 1)\,\frac{dV}{V},
> $$
>
> using $pV = nRT$ and $R/C_V = (C_p - C_V)/C_V = \gamma - 1$. Along the process, then, $\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[\ln T + (\gamma - 1)\ln V\bigr] = 0$ for any parameter $t$ describing it, so $\ln T + (\gamma - 1)\ln V = \ln\bigl(TV^{\gamma-1}\bigr)$ is constant: $TV^{\gamma-1} = $ const. Substituting $T = pV/(nR)$ gives $pV^\gamma = $ const; substituting $V = nRT/p$ gives $T^\gamma p^{1-\gamma} = $ const.
>
> University Physics reaches the same result by eliminating $dT$ between the first law and $p\,dV + V\,dp = nR\,dT$, which gives $\dfrac{dp}{p} + \gamma\,\dfrac{dV}{V} = 0$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Oct 10 notes*

^der-a3-6-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^def-a3-5-2|Def. §A3.5.2]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.6.3: Adiabats Are Steeper than Isotherms
> Through any state $(V, p)$ of an ideal gas, the quasi-static adiabat has slope $dp/dV = -\gamma\,p/V$ and the isotherm has slope $dp/dV = -p/V$. Since $\gamma > 1$ the adiabat is steeper: in an adiabatic expansion the pressure falls faster than in an isothermal one, and the gas crosses to isotherms of lower temperature.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.6*

^thm-a3-6-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> On the adiabat $p = KV^{-\gamma}$ with $K$ constant, so $dp/dV = -\gamma KV^{-\gamma-1} = -\gamma p/V$. On the isotherm $p = nRT/V$, so $dp/dV = -nRT/V^2 = -p/V$. Along the adiabat $TV^{\gamma - 1}$ is constant, so $T$ falls as $V$ grows.

^der-a3-6-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-2|Theorem §A3.6.2]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^def-a3-5-2|Def. §A3.5.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.6.4: Work in a Quasi-Static Adiabatic Process
> When an ideal gas goes quasi-statically and adiabatically from $(p_1, V_1, T_1)$ to $(p_2, V_2, T_2)$, it does work
>
> $$
> W = \frac{p_1V_1 - p_2V_2}{\gamma - 1} = nC_V\,(T_1 - T_2) .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.6 (Example 3.7) · PHY 287, Problem Set 5*

^thm-a3-6-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *From the first law.* With $Q = 0$, $W = -\Delta E_{\text{int}} = -nC_V(T_2 - T_1)$. Since $nT = pV/R$ and $C_V/R = 1/(\gamma - 1)$, this is $(p_1V_1 - p_2V_2)/(\gamma - 1)$.
>
> *By integration.* Write $p = K/V^\gamma$ with $K = p_1V_1^\gamma = p_2V_2^\gamma$. Then
>
> $$
> W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2}\frac{K}{V^\gamma}\,dV = \frac{K}{1-\gamma}\bigl(V_2^{1-\gamma} - V_1^{1-\gamma}\bigr) = \frac{p_2V_2 - p_1V_1}{1 - \gamma} ,
> $$
>
> the same thing. The two routes agree because the first law holds along the process.

^der-a3-6-4

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-2|Theorem §A3.6.2]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Real adiabatic processes are rarely quasi-static
> Fast processes are the ones that are adiabatic (no time for heat to flow), and fast processes are not quasi-static. $pV^\gamma = $ const is then an approximation, good when the gas stays nearly uniform. The free expansion shows the extreme: adiabatic, yet $pV$ (not $pV^\gamma$) ends up unchanged for an ideal gas.

^cau-a3-6-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.6.1: Compression in an Engine Cylinder
> A gas–air mixture ($\gamma = 1.40$) at $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ ($293\ \mathrm K$), $p_1 = 1.00 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$ and $V_1 = 240\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ is compressed adiabatically to $V_2 = 40\ \mathrm{cm^3}$, treated as quasi-static. By [[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-2|Theorem §A3.6.2]] and the ideal gas law,
>
> $$
> p_2 = p_1\Bigl(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\Bigr)^{\gamma} = (1.00 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa})\,(6)^{1.40} = 1.23 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{Pa}, \qquad T_2 = \frac{p_2V_2}{p_1V_1}\,T_1 = 600\ \mathrm K \ (327\ ^\circ\mathrm C) .
> $$
>
> By [[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-4|Theorem §A3.6.4]] the work done by the mixture is
>
> $$
> W = \frac{p_1V_1 - p_2V_2}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{(1.00 \times 10^5)(240 \times 10^{-6}) - (1.23 \times 10^6)(40 \times 10^{-6})}{0.40}\ \mathrm J = -63\ \mathrm J ;
> $$
>
> the piston does $63\ \mathrm J$ of work on the gas. A sixfold compression doubles the absolute temperature, enough for the mixture to ignite by itself if the octane rating is too low ("knocking").
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 3.7*

^ex-a3-6-1

> [!example] Example §A3.6.2: Work in a Three-Step Cycle
> Four moles of an ideal gas with $C_p = 29.1\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}$ (so $C_V = C_p - R = 20.8\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}$, $\gamma = 1.40$) run the cycle $a \to c \to b \to a$: $a \to c$ at constant pressure, $c \to b$ adiabatic, $b \to a$ at constant volume, with $T_a = 300\ \mathrm K$, $T_c = 500\ \mathrm K$, $T_b = 613\ \mathrm K$. Work on each leg:
> - $a \to c$ (isobaric): $W = p\,\Delta V = nR\,\Delta T = (4)(8.314)(200)\ \mathrm J = 6.65\ \mathrm{kJ}$;
> - $c \to b$ (adiabatic): $W = -\Delta E_{\text{int}} = -nC_V(T_b - T_c) = -(4)(20.8)(113)\ \mathrm J = -9.40\ \mathrm{kJ}$;
> - $b \to a$ (isochoric): $W = 0$.
>
> In total $W = -2.74\ \mathrm{kJ}$, and by [[§A3.4 Thermodynamic Processes#^thm-a3-4-4|Theorem §A3.4.4]] the net heat is the same: $Q_{ac} = nC_p\,\Delta T = 23.3\ \mathrm{kJ}$ in, $Q_{ba} = nC_V(T_a - T_b) = -26.0\ \mathrm{kJ}$ out. The data are consistent: on the adiabat $V_c/V_b = (T_b/T_c)^{1/(\gamma-1)} = 1.66$, matching $V_c/V_a = T_c/T_a = \tfrac53$ from the isobar.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 4*

> [!example] Example §A3.6.3: Which Formula Applies Where?
> For each relation, the processes in which it holds:
> - $p_1V_1^\gamma = p_2V_2^\gamma$: quasi-static adiabatic processes of an ideal gas only ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-2|Theorem §A3.6.2]]).
> - $Q = nC_p\,\Delta T$: isobaric processes; $Q = nC_V\,\Delta T$: isochoric processes (definitions of $C_p$ and $C_V$).
> - $dE_{\text{int}} = nC_V\,dT$: every process of an ideal gas ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]).
> - $dQ = dE_{\text{int}} + dW$: every process; it is the first law.
> - $dW = p\,dV$: every quasi-static process, of any substance ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]).
>
> The pattern: the first law and $dW = p\,dV$ are general; anything involving $C_V$ for the *internal energy* needs an ideal gas; anything involving $C_p$ or $C_V$ for the *heat* needs the matching process; $pV^\gamma$ needs all of ideal gas, adiabatic and quasi-static.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Midterm 1 practice*
