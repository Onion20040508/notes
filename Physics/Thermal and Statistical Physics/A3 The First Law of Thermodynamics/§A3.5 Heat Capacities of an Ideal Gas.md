How much heat it takes to warm a gas by one kelvin depends on what else happens meanwhile. At constant volume all of it goes into internal energy ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^def-a2-3-1|Def. §A2.3.1]]); at constant pressure the gas also expands and does work, so it needs more. The first law turns this into an exact relation between the two heat capacities of an ideal gas.

## Two heat capacities

> [!definition] Definition §A3.5.1: Molar Heat Capacity at Constant Pressure
> The **molar heat capacity at constant pressure** $C_p$ is defined by
>
> $$
> dQ = n C_p\,dT \quad (p \text{ held constant}),
> $$
>
> the heat that raises the temperature of $n$ moles by $dT$ at constant pressure. Its constant-volume counterpart $C_V$, with $dQ = nC_V\,dT$ at constant $V$, is [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^def-a2-3-1|Def. §A2.3.1]].
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.5*

^def-a3-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.1: Internal Energy Change of an Ideal Gas
> For an ideal gas, in **any** process with temperature change $dT$,
>
> $$
> dE_{\text{int}} = nC_V\,dT, \qquad \Delta E_{\text{int}} = nC_V\,\Delta T \ \ (C_V \text{ constant}),
> $$
>
> whether or not the volume is constant.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.5, eq. (3.9)*

^thm-a3-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Heat the gas at constant volume from $T$ to $T + dT$. No work is done, so by the first law $dE_{\text{int}} = dQ = nC_V\,dT$. The internal energy is a state function ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]) and, for an ideal gas, depends on the temperature alone ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^rem-a3-2-3|§A3.2]]). Any other process between the isotherms $T$ and $T + dT$ therefore produces the same $dE_{\text{int}}$.

^der-a3-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^def-a2-3-1|Def. §A2.3.1]], [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]], [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.2: $C_p = C_V + R$
> For an ideal gas the molar heat capacities differ by the gas constant:
>
> $$
> C_p = C_V + R .
> $$
>
> In particular $C_p > C_V$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.5, eq. (3.10)*

^thm-a3-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Heat $n$ moles at constant pressure from $T$ to $T + dT$: $dQ = nC_p\,dT$. The gas expands and does work $dW = p\,dV$. Differentiating $pV = nRT$ at constant $p$ gives $p\,dV = nR\,dT$. The first law then gives
>
> $$
> dE_{\text{int}} = dQ - p\,dV = n(C_p - R)\,dT .
> $$
>
> By [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]] also $dE_{\text{int}} = nC_V\,dT$. Equate and cancel $n\,dT$.

^der-a3-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^def-a3-5-1|Def. §A3.5.1]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]], [[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]

![[ph-th-a3-5-1.svg]]
*Two ways from the isotherm $T$ to the isotherm $T + dT$. Along $A \to B$ (constant volume) all the heat becomes internal energy. Along $A \to C$ (constant pressure) the internal energy rises by the same amount, since $B$ and $C$ have the same temperature, but the gas also does the work shaded in red, $p\,dV = nR\,dT$. That extra heat per mole and kelvin is $C_p - C_V = R$.*

## Ratio of heat capacities

> [!definition] Definition §A3.5.2: Ratio of Heat Capacities
> The **ratio of heat capacities** of a gas is $\gamma = C_p/C_V$. For an ideal gas $\gamma > 1$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.6, eq. (3.11)*

^def-a3-5-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.3: Heat Capacities from Degrees of Freedom
> An ideal gas whose molecules have $d$ active degrees of freedom has
>
> $$
> C_V = \frac d2 R, \qquad C_p = \Bigl(\frac d2 + 1\Bigr) R, \qquad \gamma = 1 + \frac 2d ;
> $$
>
> conversely, $C_V = R/(\gamma - 1)$ and $C_p = \gamma R/(\gamma - 1)$. At room temperature:
>
> | Molecule | $d$ | $C_V$ (J/(mol·K)) | $C_p$ (J/(mol·K)) | $\gamma$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | monatomic | 3 | $\tfrac32 R = 12.47$ | $\tfrac52 R = 20.79$ | $\tfrac53 = 1.67$ |
> | diatomic | 5 | $\tfrac52 R = 20.79$ | $\tfrac72 R = 29.10$ | $\tfrac75 = 1.40$ |
> | polyatomic | 6 | $3R = 24.94$ | $4R = 33.26$ | $\tfrac43 = 1.33$ |
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.5, Table 3.3*

^thm-a3-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $C_V = \tfrac d2 R$ is equipartition ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]); add $R$ for $C_p$ ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]) and divide. Conversely, $\gamma C_V = C_p = C_V + R$ gives $C_V = R/(\gamma - 1)$.

^der-a3-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^def-a3-5-2|Def. §A3.5.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Real gases
> The derivation of $C_p - C_V = R$ used only the ideal gas law and the fact that $E_{\text{int}}$ depends on $T$ alone (no intermolecular forces), not the number of degrees of freedom. Both hold well for every dilute gas, so the relation holds approximately for all of them: monatomic like He, diatomic like O$_2$, polyatomic like CO$_2$ or NH$_3$. The individual heat capacities of real molecular gases come out somewhat *higher* than $\tfrac d2 R$, showing that vibrations of polyatomic molecules already contribute at room temperature, but their difference stays very close to $R$.

^rem-a3-5-1

> [!caution] Caution: Which heat capacity, when
> - $Q = nC_V\,\Delta T$ holds only at constant volume, and $Q = nC_p\,\Delta T$ only at constant pressure. In other processes the heat must come from the first law.
> - $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = nC_V\,\Delta T$ holds for an ideal gas in *every* process, including isobaric ones. Using $C_p$ for the internal energy at constant pressure is a common slip.

^cau-a3-5-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.5.1: Where the Heat Goes at Constant Pressure
> Heat $Q$ flows into an ideal diatomic gas at constant pressure. What fraction becomes work? With $Q = nC_p\,\Delta T$ and $W = p\,\Delta V = nR\,\Delta T$,
>
> $$
> \frac WQ = \frac{R}{C_p} = \frac{R}{\tfrac72 R} = \frac27 \approx 0.29 ,
> $$
>
> and the remaining $\tfrac57 = C_V/C_p$ raises the internal energy. For a monatomic gas the work fraction is $\tfrac25$: with fewer degrees of freedom to fill, a larger share of the heat leaves as work.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 5*

^ex-a3-5-1

> [!example] Example §A3.5.2: Cooling a Room
> A room holds $n = 2500\ \mathrm{mol}$ of air, an ideal gas with $\gamma = 1.40$, cooled at constant pressure $1\ \mathrm{atm}$ from $35\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ to $26\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ (treat $n$ as fixed). By [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]], $C_V = R/(\gamma - 1) = 20.8\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}$, and by [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]
>
> $$
> \Delta E_{\text{int}} = nC_V\,\Delta T = (2500\ \mathrm{mol})(20.8\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)})(-9\ \mathrm K) = -4.68 \times 10^5\ \mathrm J .
> $$
>
> The heat the air conditioner must remove is larger, $Q = nC_p\,\Delta T = -6.55 \times 10^5\ \mathrm J$, because the shrinking air also has work $W = nR\,\Delta T = -1.87 \times 10^5\ \mathrm J$ done on it by the atmosphere; indeed $Q - W = \Delta E_{\text{int}}$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 5*

^ex-a3-5-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $C_V = \frac1n\bigl(\partial E_{\text{int}}/\partial T\bigr)_V$ is a partial derivative of the state function $E_{\text{int}}(T, V)$ ([[§4 Partial Derivatives#^def-4-1|452 Def. §4.1]]); for an ideal gas the other partial, $(\partial E_{\text{int}}/\partial V)_T$, vanishes, which is why $C_V$ also gives $dE_{\text{int}}$ off the isochores ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^rem-a3-3-2|§A3.3]]).
> - $\gamma$ fixes the shape of adiabats: [[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-2|Theorem §A3.6.2]].
