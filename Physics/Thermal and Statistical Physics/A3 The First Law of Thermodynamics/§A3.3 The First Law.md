Heat and work both move energy across a system's boundary, and both depend on the path ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy]]). The first law of thermodynamics says that their difference does not: it equals the change of the internal energy, a property of the state alone. It is the law of conservation of energy, extended to include heat.

## The law

> [!definition] Definition §A3.3.1: State Function
> A **state function** is a quantity whose value is fixed by the equilibrium state of a system, whatever process led there. Its change between two states depends only on those states, not on the path. Pressure, volume, temperature and internal energy are state functions; work and heat are not. The state variables of [[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^def-a3-1-2|Def. §A3.1.2]] are the measurable state functions chosen as coordinates of the state; every other state function, such as $E_{\text{int}}$, is a function of them.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.3*

^def-a3-3-1

> [!principle] Principle §A3.3.1: First Law of Thermodynamics
> Every equilibrium state of a system has an internal energy $E_{\text{int}}$, a state function. For any process between two equilibrium states,
>
> $$
> \Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q - W ,
> $$
>
> where $Q$ is the heat added to the system and $W$ the work done by the system, with the signs
>
> | Process | Sign |
> | --- | --- |
> | heat added to the system | $Q > 0$ |
> | heat removed from the system | $Q < 0$ |
> | work done by the system | $W > 0$ |
> | work done on the system | $W < 0$ |
>
> *Domain: every process that begins and ends in equilibrium, quasi-static or not; the intermediate states need not be equilibrium states. It is conservation of energy ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]) with heat counted as a transfer of energy; no exception is known.*
> *Source: UP2 §3.3, eq. (3.7), Table 3.1*

> [!remark] Remark: What the law asserts
> - *A bookkeeping rule.* Energy enters as heat and leaves as work; what is left over changes the stored internal energy. If a gas expands and does work with no heat supplied, its internal energy must fall.
> - *A statement about states.* Experiment shows that along different paths $1, 2, \dots$ from $A$ to $B$ the heats $Q_i$ and works $W_i$ differ, but $Q_1 - W_1 = Q_2 - W_2 = \cdots$. That is what makes $E_{\text{int}}$ well defined as a state function, just as path-independent work makes potential energy well defined in mechanics ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-1|CM Theorem §A4.5.1]]).
> - *No knowledge of $E_{\text{int}}$ needed.* The law holds even when no formula for $E_{\text{int}}$ is known, which is usually the case; only differences between states enter.
> - *Not only for slow processes.* A gas mixture exploded by a spark in a steel container passes through turbulent non-equilibrium states, but it starts and ends in equilibrium, and the law relates the two ends.
> - *Relation to mechanics.* Conservation of energy ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]) says that the total energy of an isolated system is constant and that energy changes only by transfer or conversion. For a system at rest as a whole, the only energy that can change is $E_{\text{int}}$, and the first law is that principle with the transfers across the boundary sorted into two kinds: work, done by forces through a moving boundary ([[§A4.1 Work#^def-a4-1-1|CM Def. §A4.1.1]]), and heat, driven by a temperature difference. For an isolated system ($Q = W = 0$) it reduces to the mechanical statement ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 5 slides (the third point)*

^rem-a3-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Signs, and heat versus temperature
> - $W$ here is the work done **by** the system. Some texts (chemistry in particular) use the work done *on* the system, $W_{\text{on}} = -W$, and write $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q + W_{\text{on}}$. It is the same law; check the convention before using a formula.
> - Adding heat does not always raise the temperature: in an isothermal expansion all the heat leaves again as work ([[§A3.4 Thermodynamic Processes#^thm-a3-4-3|Theorem §A3.4.3]]), and in boiling it goes into the internal energy at constant temperature ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^ex-a3-3-3|Example §A3.3.3]]).

^cau-a3-3-1

## Consequences

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.2: First Law in Differential Form
> For an infinitesimal step of a process,
>
> $$
> dE_{\text{int}} = dQ - dW ,
> $$
>
> and for a quasi-static step of a gas, $dE_{\text{int}} = dQ - p\,dV$. Here $dE_{\text{int}}$ is the change of a state function, while $dQ$ and $dW$ are small amounts of heat and work.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.3, eq. (3.8)*

^thm-a3-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply [[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]] to a process between two neighbouring equilibrium states; for a quasi-static step $dW = p\,dV$ by [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]].

^der-a3-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.3: Cycles and Isolated Systems
> 1. In a **cyclic process**, which returns the system to its initial state, $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$ and therefore $Q = W$: the net heat absorbed over the cycle equals the net work done.
> 2. The internal energy of an **isolated system** is constant.
> 3. Reversing a process between $A$ and $B$ reverses the sign of $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$: $\Delta E_{\text{int},BA} = -\Delta E_{\text{int},AB}$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.3–§3.4*

^thm-a3-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. $E_{\text{int}}$ is a state function and the cycle ends in its initial state, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = E_{\text{int}}(A) - E_{\text{int}}(A) = 0$, and the first law gives $0 = Q - W$.
> 2. An isolated system exchanges neither heat nor work: $Q = W = 0$, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$ for any process inside it.
> 3. $\Delta E_{\text{int},BA} = E_{\text{int}}(A) - E_{\text{int}}(B) = -\Delta E_{\text{int},AB}$, whatever the paths.

^der-a3-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.3 The First Law#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Exact and inexact differentials
> Since an equilibrium state of a fixed amount of fluid is fixed by two variables ([[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^rem-a3-1-3|§A3.1]]), the internal energy is a function such as $E_{\text{int}}(T, V)$, and its change in a small step is a genuine differential,
>
> $$
> dE_{\text{int}} = \Bigl(\frac{\partial E_{\text{int}}}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V dT + \Bigl(\frac{\partial E_{\text{int}}}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T dV ,
> $$
>
> whose integral along any path is $E_{\text{int}}(B) - E_{\text{int}}(A)$ and around any cycle is zero. For an ideal gas the second coefficient vanishes, since $E_{\text{int}}$ depends on $T$ only. The forms $dW = p\,dV$ and $dQ$ are not differentials of any function ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^rem-a3-2-1|§A3.2]]). The first law says that their *difference* is: the difference of two inexact forms is exact. In mechanics the same structure separates the work of a conservative force (minus the change of a potential energy) from that of friction.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 5 slides*

^rem-a3-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: No perpetual motion of the first kind
> A machine that runs in a cycle has $W = Q$ per cycle ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]), so it cannot deliver more work than the heat it takes in; a machine producing work from nothing would violate energy conservation. C. P. Snow summarised the first law as "you can't win". Whether it can at least convert *all* the heat into work is the subject of the second law: it cannot ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^pr-a4-3-1|Principle §A4.3.1]]).

^rem-a3-3-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.3.1: Using a State Function
> A system goes from state $A$ to state $B$, absorbing $Q_{AB} = 400\ \mathrm J$ and doing $W_{AB} = 100\ \mathrm J$ of work.
> - (a) By the first law, $\Delta E_{\text{int},AB} = 400\ \mathrm J - 100\ \mathrm J = 300\ \mathrm J$.
> - (b) Back from $B$ to $A$, by any path: $\Delta E_{\text{int},BA} = -300\ \mathrm J$ ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]).
> - (c) Along another path from $A$ to $B$ on which $400\ \mathrm J$ of work is done *on* the system, $W'_{AB} = -400\ \mathrm J$. The internal energy change is still $300\ \mathrm J$, so $Q'_{AB} = \Delta E_{\text{int},AB} + W'_{AB} = 300\ \mathrm J - 400\ \mathrm J = -100\ \mathrm J$: the system *gives off* $100\ \mathrm J$ of heat.
>
> Nothing about the system or the paths had to be known, not even whether the processes were quasi-static.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 3.2*

^ex-a3-3-1

> [!example] Example §A3.3.2: Heat along Different Paths
> Return to the ideal gas of [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^ex-a3-2-1|Example §A3.2.1]], taken from $A$ to $C$ along $ABC$ ($W = 473\ \mathrm J$), along $ADC$ ($W = 236\ \mathrm J$) and along the isotherm $AC$ ($W = 328\ \mathrm J$). Whatever the system, $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$ is the same on all paths, so the heats differ exactly as the works do:
>
> $$
> Q_{ADC} - Q_{ABC} = W_{ADC} - W_{ABC} = -236\ \mathrm J ,
> $$
>
> minus the area of the rectangle $ABCD$. Here more is known: $A$ and $C$ lie on one isotherm and the internal energy of an ideal gas depends on $T$ only, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$ and $Q = W$ on every path: $Q_{ABC} = 473\ \mathrm J$, $Q_{ADC} = 236\ \mathrm J$, $Q_{AC} = 328\ \mathrm J$. Heat, like work, depends on the path.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 3.4*

^ex-a3-3-2

> [!example] Example §A3.3.3: Boiling Water
> One gram of water at $100\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ vaporises at atmospheric pressure, $p = 1.01 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$, and its volume grows by $1.67 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3}$. The heat needed is $Q = mL_v = (1.00\ \mathrm g)(2.26 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{J/g}) = 2.26 \times 10^3\ \mathrm J$. At constant pressure the steam does work against the atmosphere $W = p\,\Delta V = (1.01 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa})(1.67 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3}) = 169\ \mathrm J$ ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]]). So
>
> $$
> \Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q - W = 2260\ \mathrm J - 169\ \mathrm J = 2.09 \times 10^3\ \mathrm J .
> $$
>
> The internal energy rises by $2.09\ \mathrm{kJ}$ although the temperature does not change: it goes into the potential energy of pulling the molecules apart. Only $7.5\%$ of the heat leaves as work.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 3.6*

^ex-a3-3-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $dE_{\text{int}}$ is an exact 1-form on the plane of states and $dQ$, $dW$ are not ([[§39 Closed and Exact Forms#^def-39-3|452 Def. §39.3]]); its expression in partial derivatives is the differential of [[§10 The Differential#^def-10-1|452 Def. §10.1]]. A closed form on a star-shaped region (no holes) is exact (Poincaré lemma, [[§39 Closed and Exact Forms#^prop-39-7|452 Prop. §39.7]]), so path independence can be tested locally.
> - The second law adds a second state function, the entropy, built from the inexact $dQ$ by dividing by $T$: [[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]].
