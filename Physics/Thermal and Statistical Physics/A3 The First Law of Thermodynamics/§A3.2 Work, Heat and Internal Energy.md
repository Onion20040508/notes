With the system chosen ([[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems]]), there are two ways for energy to cross its boundary, work and heat, and one place where it is stored, the internal energy. This section makes each precise for a gas in a cylinder. The key finding is that work, unlike the internal energy, depends on the path from one state to another, not only on the end states.

## Work done by a system

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.1: Work Done by a System
> The **work $W$ done by a system** is the work done by the forces the system exerts on its surroundings as its boundary moves ([[§A4.1 Work#^def-a4-1-1|CM Def. §A4.1.1]]). It is positive when the system pushes its surroundings outward (a gas expanding against a piston) and negative when the surroundings do work on the system (a gas being compressed); the work done *on* the system is $-W$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.2*

^def-a3-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.1: Work Done by a Gas
> When a gas at pressure $p$ changes its volume by $dV$ in a quasi-static process ([[§A3.4 Thermodynamic Processes#^mod-a3-4-1|Model §A3.4.1]]), it does work
>
> $$
> dW = p\,dV, \qquad W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} p\,dV ,
> $$
>
> where $p$ is evaluated along the process. On a $pV$ diagram $W$ is the area under the curve of the process: positive for expansion ($V_2 > V_1$), negative for compression.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.2, eqs. (3.3)–(3.4)*

^thm-a3-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let the gas push on a piston of area $A$. By the definition of pressure ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-3|CM Def. §A9.1.3]]) the force on the piston is $pA$, along its outward displacement $dx$, so the gas does work $dW = pA\,dx$. The volume grows by $dV = A\,dx$, hence $dW = p\,dV$; summing over the process gives the integral. For a container of any shape, each patch $dA$ of a moving boundary contributes $p\,dA\,dx_\perp$, and these add up to $p\,dV$ again. The integral makes sense only if the gas has a well-defined pressure at every stage, i.e. passes through equilibrium states, so that $p$ is a function of $V$ along the process; this is the quasi-static assumption.

^der-a3-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]], [[§A4.1 Work#^def-a4-1-1|CM Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-3|CM Def. §A9.1.3]], [[§A3.4 Thermodynamic Processes#^mod-a3-4-1|Model §A3.4.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.2: Work at Constant Pressure or Volume
> In an **isobaric** process (constant $p$) the work done is $W = p\,(V_2 - V_1)$. In an **isochoric** process (constant $V$) no work is done, $W = 0$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.2*

^thm-a3-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $p$ constant, $\int_{V_1}^{V_2} p\,dV = p\int_{V_1}^{V_2} dV = p(V_2 - V_1)$. With $V$ constant, $dV = 0$ throughout.

^der-a3-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.3: Work in an Isothermal Process of an Ideal Gas
> When $n$ moles of an ideal gas expand quasi-statically at constant temperature $T$ from $V_1$ to $V_2$,
>
> $$
> W = nRT\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = nRT \ln\frac{p_1}{p_2} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.2*

^thm-a3-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The gas is kept at $T$ by contact with a heat reservoir ([[§A3.4 Thermodynamic Processes#^mod-a3-4-2|Model §A3.4.2]]). Along the process $p = nRT/V$, with $n$, $R$, $T$ constant, so
>
> $$
> W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} \frac{nRT}{V}\,dV = nRT\int_{V_1}^{V_2}\frac{dV}{V} = nRT\,(\ln V_2 - \ln V_1) = nRT\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} ,
> $$
>
> since $\ln$ is an antiderivative of $1/V$. On an isotherm $p_1V_1 = p_2V_2$, so $V_2/V_1 = p_1/p_2$.

^der-a3-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

## Work depends on the path

> [!example] Example §A3.2.1: Three Paths between the Same States
> An ideal gas goes from state $A$ ($p_1 = 2.10 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$, $V_1 = 2.25 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3}$) to state $C$ ($p_2 = 1.05 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$, $V_2 = 4.50 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3}$). Since $p_1V_1 = p_2V_2 = 472.5\ \mathrm J$, $A$ and $C$ lie on one isotherm. Compare three quasi-static paths:
> - along the isotherm $AC$, by [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-3|Theorem §A3.2.3]]: $W_{AC} = p_1V_1\ln(V_2/V_1) = (472.5\ \mathrm J)\ln 2 = 328\ \mathrm J$;
> - isobaric $A \to B$ at $p_1$, then isochoric $B \to C$: $W_{ABC} = p_1(V_2 - V_1) + 0 = 473\ \mathrm J$;
> - isochoric $A \to D$, then isobaric $D \to C$ at $p_2$: $W_{ADC} = 0 + p_2(V_2 - V_1) = 236\ \mathrm J$.
>
> Same initial and final states, three different amounts of work. The work is the area under the path, and the paths enclose different areas.
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.2 and Example 3.4*

> [!remark] Remark: Work is a line integral, and $p\,dV$ is not exact
> A quasi-static process is a curve $\mathcal C$ in the $(V, p)$ plane, and $W = \int_{\mathcal C} p\,dV$ is the work line integral of the "field" with components $(p, 0)$ along it ([[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^def-16-2|452 Def. §16.2]]), exactly as mechanical work is the line integral of a force. Mechanical work is path independent only for conservative forces ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^def-a4-5-1|CM Def. §A4.5.1]]), and the two-dimensional test for that ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-3|CM Theorem §A4.5.3]]) fails here: $\partial(p)/\partial p = 1$, while $\partial(0)/\partial V = 0$. Equivalently, there is no state function $F(V, p)$ with $dF = p\,dV$: it would need $\partial F/\partial V = p$ and $\partial F/\partial p = 0$, whose mixed second partials are $1$ and $0$, contradicting their equality ([[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]). In the language of forms, $p\,dV$ is not closed, hence not exact ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^def-22-8|452 Def. §22.8]]). A system therefore does not "have" an amount of work; work belongs to a process.

^rem-a3-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: $dW$ is not the differential of anything
> The notation $dW = p\,dV$ (and $dQ$ below) means "a small amount of work in a small step of the process", not the change of a function $W$ of the state. Some texts write $đW$ to flag this. In particular, $\int dW$ around a closed cycle need not vanish, and "the work of state $A$" means nothing.

^cau-a3-2-1

> [!example] Example §A3.2.2: Isothermal Expansion of a van der Waals Gas
> One mole of a van der Waals gas, $(p + a/V^2)(V - b) = RT$ ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-4|Law §A2.1.4]] with $n = 1$), expands quasi-statically at constant $T$ from $V_1$ to $V_2$. Solve for the pressure, $p = \dfrac{RT}{V - b} - \dfrac{a}{V^2}$, and integrate with $T$ fixed:
>
> $$
> W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2}\Bigl(\frac{RT}{V - b} - \frac{a}{V^2}\Bigr)dV = \Bigl[RT\ln(V - b) + \frac aV\Bigr]_{V_1}^{V_2} = RT\ln\frac{V_2 - b}{V_1 - b} + a\Bigl(\frac{1}{V_2} - \frac{1}{V_1}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> The attraction term ($a > 0$, $V_2 > V_1$) reduces the work: the molecules pull back on each other as the gas expands. With $a = b = 0$ this is $RT\ln(V_2/V_1)$, the ideal-gas result. Any equation of state works the same way: solve for $p(V)$ along the path and integrate.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 3.1*

^ex-a3-2-2

## Heat and internal energy

> [!remark] Remark: Heat, recalled
> **Heat** $Q$ is energy transferred between the system and its surroundings (or between parts of a system) because of a temperature difference ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^def-a1-3-2|Def. §A1.3.2]]). Like work, the heat exchanged between two states depends on the path: along paths $ABC$ and $ADC$ above the gas absorbs different amounts of heat ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^ex-a3-3-2|Example §A3.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 5 slides*

^rem-a3-2-2

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.2: Internal Energy
> The **internal energy** $E_{\text{int}}$ of a thermodynamic system is the sum of the average mechanical energies of all its molecules (or other constituents),
>
> $$
> E_{\text{int}} = \sum_i \bigl(\overline{K}_i + \overline{U}_i\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Here $K_i$ is the kinetic energy of molecule $i$, translational ($\tfrac12 m_i v_i^2$, with $v_i$ measured relative to the system's centre of mass) plus rotational and vibrational, and $U_i$ is the potential energy of its interactions with the other molecules of the system. The position and motion of the system as a whole do not enter: carrying it to the roof or putting it on a moving train leaves $E_{\text{int}}$ unchanged. This makes precise the internal energy introduced in [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^def-a1-3-1|Def. §A1.3.1]].
>
> *Source: UP2 §3.2, eq. (3.5)*

^def-a3-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Internal energy of an ideal gas
> In a monatomic ideal gas the atoms have only translational kinetic energy and no interactions, so $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac32 nRT$ ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]]): it depends on the temperature alone, not on $p$ or $V$ separately. For other ideal gases equipartition gives $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac d2 nR\,\Delta T$ ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]). For real substances $E_{\text{int}}$ has no simple formula and also depends on the volume through the potential energies, but a rise in internal energy usually goes with a rise in temperature. Phase changes are the exception ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^ex-a3-3-3|Example §A3.3.3]]).

^rem-a3-2-3

> [!caution] Caution: A system contains energy, not heat or work
> A system has an internal energy, a property of its state. It does not contain "heat" or "work": these name energy *in transit* across the boundary, and only make sense for a process. Saying a hot body "has a lot of heat" means it has a large internal energy.

^cau-a3-2-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Work as a line integral: [[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^def-16-2|452 Def. §16.2]]; the inexactness of $p\,dV$ is the same phenomenon as a non-conservative force ([[§11 The Three Differential Operators꞉ Gradient, Curl, Divergence#^def-11-1|452 Def. §11.1]]: $(p, 0)$ is not a gradient field), and its failure to be closed is detected by the exterior derivative, $d(p\,dV) = dp\wedge dV \ne 0$ ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^def-22-8|452 Def. §22.8]]).
> - The internal energy, by contrast, *is* a state function: the first law ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]).
