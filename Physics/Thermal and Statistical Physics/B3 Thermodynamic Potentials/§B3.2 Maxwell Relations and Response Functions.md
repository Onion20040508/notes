What can be measured about a substance are its responses: how much heat raises its temperature ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^def-a3-5-1|Def. §A3.5.1]]), how much it expands on warming ([[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^law-a1-2-2|Law §A1.2.2]]), how much it shrinks under pressure ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-3|CM Def. §A7.2.3]]). Level A treated these as separate material constants, and related two of them only for the ideal gas ($C_p - C_V = R$, [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]). Because the potentials of [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables]] are state functions, their second derivatives are symmetric. That gives the Maxwell relations, and with them identities that hold for every substance: entropy derivatives in terms of measurable ones, $C_p - C_V = TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T$, $C_p/C_V = \kappa_T/\kappa_S$, and formulas for the temperature change in a free expansion and in a throttling process. The partial-derivative identities used throughout (reciprocal and triple-product rules) are those of [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]] and [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]; notation and signs are those of [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^rem-b3-1-1|Remark: Notation and signs]].

## Maxwell relations

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.1: Maxwell Relations
> For a simple system, at fixed $N$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\Bigr)_S = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial S}\Bigr)_V, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial T}{\partial p}\Bigr)_S = \Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\Bigr)_p, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T = \Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial p}\Bigr)_T = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p ,
> $$
>
> from $U$, $H$, $F$ and $G$ respectively. Relations involving the particle number follow in the same way, for example
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial \mu}{\partial p}\Bigr)_{T,N} = \Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{T,p}, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial \mu}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{p,N} = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{T,p}
> $$
>
> from $G$, and $(\partial S/\partial\mu)_{T,V} = (\partial N/\partial T)_{V,\mu}$, $(\partial p/\partial\mu)_{T,V} = (\partial N/\partial V)_{T,\mu}$ from $\Phi$.
>
> All of them hold wherever the potentials are twice continuously differentiable; this fails at phase transitions ([[§B9.3★ The Classification of Phase Transitions#^def-b9-3-1|Def. §B9.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.6, eqs. (16.40)–(16.51), Example 16.3 · Schroeder Problem 5.12 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, eq. (4) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §8.1*

^thm-b3-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each potential has a differential of the form $d\Psi = a\,dx + b\,dy$ (with any further natural variables held fixed), so $a = (\partial\Psi/\partial x)_y$ and $b = (\partial\Psi/\partial y)_x$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]). If $\Psi$ is $C^2$, its mixed second partial derivatives are equal ([[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]), so
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial a}{\partial y}\Bigr)_x = \frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial y\,\partial x} = \frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x\,\partial y} = \Bigl(\frac{\partial b}{\partial x}\Bigr)_y .
> $$
>
> This is the test that $a\,dx + b\,dy$ is exact ([[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]), used in the opposite direction: a potential's differential is exact, so the test holds. Insert the four differentials:
> - $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$: $a = T$, $b = -p$, so $(\partial T/\partial V)_S = -(\partial p/\partial S)_V$;
> - $dH = T\,dS + V\,dp$: $(\partial T/\partial p)_S = (\partial V/\partial S)_p$;
> - $dF = -S\,dT - p\,dV$: $-(\partial S/\partial V)_T = -(\partial p/\partial T)_V$;
> - $dG = -S\,dT + V\,dp$: $-(\partial S/\partial p)_T = (\partial V/\partial T)_p$.
>
> With $N$ as one of the variables, $dG = -S\,dT + V\,dp + \mu\,dN$ gives $(\partial V/\partial N)_{T,p} = (\partial\mu/\partial p)_{T,N}$ and $(\partial(-S)/\partial N)_{T,p} = (\partial\mu/\partial T)_{p,N}$; $d\Phi = -S\,dT - p\,dV - N\,d\mu$ gives the last two.

> [!remark] Remark: Remembering them
> Blundell advises re-deriving them from the four differentials, which takes a few seconds. For checking, a pattern: each relation reads $(\partial\ast/\partial\ddagger)_\star = \pm(\partial\dagger/\partial\star)_\ddagger$, where $\ast, \star$ and $\dagger, \ddagger$ are conjugate pairs from $(T, S)$ and $(p, V)$. The variable held fixed is conjugate to the one differentiated, and variables diagonally opposite across the equals sign are conjugate. The sign is minus exactly when $T$ and $V$ stand in the same derivative (as numerator and denominator), as in the first and last relations. Their use is always the same: an entropy derivative, which cannot be measured directly, is traded for a derivative of $p$, $V$, $T$, which can. For instance $(\partial S/\partial p)_T$, the entropy change on isothermal compression, equals $-(\partial V/\partial T)_p$, minus the thermal expansion.
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.6 (margin note 14)*

^rem-b3-2-1

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: All four in one Jacobian
> For any reversible cycle $\oint dU = 0$, so $\oint T\,dS = \oint p\,dV$: the heat absorbed equals the work done, and the cycle encloses the same oriented area in the $(S, T)$ plane as in the $(V, p)$ plane. By Green's theorem ([[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^thm-16-1|452 Thm. §16.1]]) and the change-of-variables formula ([[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-20|452 Thm. §15.20]]), the map $(p, V) \mapsto (T, S)$ preserves area for every cycle, so its Jacobian is
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial(T, S)}{\partial(p, V)} = 1 .
> $$
>
> Conversely, writing $\partial(T, S)/\partial(p, V) = \bigl[\partial(T, S)/\partial(T, V)\bigr]\bigl[\partial(T, V)/\partial(p, V)\bigr] = (\partial S/\partial V)_T\,(\partial T/\partial p)_V$ shows that the Jacobian is $1$ exactly when the third Maxwell relation holds. Choosing other intermediate pairs $(T, p)$, $(p, S)$, $(S, V)$ gives the other three. Blundell derives all four this way in one stroke.
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.6 (box "An alternative derivation"), eqs. (16.52)–(16.55)*

## Response functions

> [!definition] Definition §B3.2.1: Response Functions
> At fixed $N$, the **heat capacities** at constant volume and pressure ([[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]),
>
> $$
> C_V = \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V, \qquad C_p = \Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p ,
> $$
>
> their ratio $\gamma = C_p/C_V$, the **isobaric expansivity** (coefficient of volume expansion)
>
> $$
> \alpha = \frac1V\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p ,
> $$
>
> and the **isothermal** and **adiabatic (isentropic) compressibilities**
>
> $$
> \kappa_T = -\frac1V\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial p}\Bigr)_T, \qquad \kappa_S = -\frac1V\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial p}\Bigr)_S .
> $$
>
> These are the **response functions** (Blundell: generalized susceptibilities): each measures how a variable of the system responds to a change of $T$ or $p$ imposed on it. The minus signs make the compressibilities positive. $1/\kappa_T$ and $1/\kappa_S$ are the isothermal and adiabatic bulk moduli ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-3|CM Def. §A7.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.6, eqs. (16.56)–(16.57), (16.66)–(16.69) · Schroeder Problems 1.7, 1.46, 5.16 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §8.3, §10*

^def-b3-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Symbols differ between levels and books
> - Here $\alpha$ is the *volume* expansivity: level A's $\beta$ ([[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^law-a1-2-2|Law §A1.2.2]]), which is $3\times$ level A's linear $\alpha$ for an isotropic solid ([[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^thm-a1-2-3|Theorem §A1.2.3]]). Blundell writes $\beta_p$, Schroeder $\beta$, the user's notes $\alpha_P$; $\beta$ is kept for $1/k_BT$.
> - $C_V$ and $C_p$ here belong to the whole system (unit $\mathrm{J/K}$, extensive). Level A's $C_V$, $C_p$ are *molar* heat capacities ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^def-a2-3-1|Def. §A2.3.1]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^def-a3-5-1|Def. §A3.5.1]]), equal to these divided by the number of moles $n$.
> - What is held fixed is part of the definition. Level A's $\beta$ is measured at constant pressure ([[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^law-a1-2-2|Law §A1.2.2]], *Valid when*), which is $\alpha$ here. The adiabatic expansivity $(1/V)(\partial V/\partial T)_S = -C_V\kappa_T/(TV\alpha)$ (from [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-5|Theorem §B3.2.5]]) is a different quantity, negative whenever $\alpha > 0$: a substance that expands on heating at constant pressure cools when it expands adiabatically.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.2: Heat Capacities from the Entropy; the T dS Equations
> At fixed $N$,
>
> $$
> C_V = T\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V, \qquad C_p = T\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p ,
> $$
>
> and for any change of state
>
> $$
> T\,dS = C_V\,dT + T\Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V dV = C_V\,dT + \frac{T\alpha}{\kappa_T}\,dV, \qquad
> T\,dS = C_p\,dT - T\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p dp = C_p\,dT - TV\alpha\,dp .
> $$
>
> In particular the pressure coefficient is $(\partial p/\partial T)_V = \alpha/\kappa_T$.
>
> *Source: Blundell Example 16.4, eqs. (16.56)–(16.57), (16.65), Example 16.6, eq. (16.76) · Schroeder Problems 1.46(c), 3.33 · PHY 306, Problem Set 5, Problem 3(b)*

^thm-b3-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At constant $V$, $dU = T\,dS$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]); dividing by $dT$ gives $C_V = (\partial U/\partial T)_V = T(\partial S/\partial T)_V$. At constant $p$, $dH = T\,dS$ gives $C_p = T(\partial S/\partial T)_p$.
>
> Regard $S$ as a function of $T$ and $V$: $dS = (\partial S/\partial T)_V\,dT + (\partial S/\partial V)_T\,dV$. Multiply by $T$, use $C_V$ for the first coefficient and the third Maxwell relation ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]]) for the second. Regarding $S$ as a function of $T$ and $p$ instead, the fourth Maxwell relation gives $T(\partial S/\partial p)_T = -T(\partial V/\partial T)_p = -TV\alpha$. Finally, $p$, $V$, $T$ are related by the equation of state, so the triple-product rule ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]]) gives
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V = -\frac{(\partial V/\partial T)_p}{(\partial V/\partial p)_T} = \frac{V\alpha}{V\kappa_T} = \frac{\alpha}{\kappa_T} .
> $$

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.3: The Energy Equations
> At fixed $N$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T = T\Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V - p, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\Bigr)_T = V - T\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p = V(1 - T\alpha) ,
> $$
>
> and the heat capacities depend on volume and pressure through the equation of state alone:
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial C_V}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T = T\Bigl(\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial T^2}\Bigr)_V, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial C_p}{\partial p}\Bigr)_T = -T\Bigl(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial T^2}\Bigr)_p .
> $$
>
> For any substance with the equation of state $pV = Nk_BT$, all four vanish: $U$ and $H$ depend on $T$ alone, and so do $C_V$ and $C_p$.
>
> *Source: Blundell Example 16.4, eqs. (16.58)–(16.60), Exercises 16.3, 16.5, eqs. (27.3)–(27.4), (27.18)–(27.19) · PHY 306, Problem Set 4, Problems 2–3*

^thm-b3-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$, divide by $dV$ at constant $T$: $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = T(\partial S/\partial V)_T - p$, and the third Maxwell relation ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]]) replaces $(\partial S/\partial V)_T$ by $(\partial p/\partial T)_V$. From $dH = T\,dS + V\,dp$ at constant $T$: $(\partial H/\partial p)_T = T(\partial S/\partial p)_T + V = V - T(\partial V/\partial T)_p$ by the fourth.
>
> For the heat capacities, with $C_V = T(\partial S/\partial T)_V$ ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]) and $S(T, V)$ of class $C^2$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial C_V}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T = T\,\frac{\partial}{\partial V}\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V = T\,\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T = T\Bigl(\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial T^2}\Bigr)_V ,
> $$
>
> exchanging the derivatives by [[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]] and using the third Maxwell relation again. The same steps with $S(T, p)$ and the fourth relation give $(\partial C_p/\partial p)_T$.
>
> For $p = Nk_BT/V$: $T(\partial p/\partial T)_V = Nk_BT/V = p$, so $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = 0$; $\alpha = (1/V)(Nk_B/p) = 1/T$, so $(\partial H/\partial p)_T = 0$; and $p$ is linear in $T$ at fixed $V$ and $V$ linear in $T$ at fixed $p$, so both second derivatives vanish.
>
> The energy equation and the formula for $(\partial C_V/\partial V)_T$ were first obtained in [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]], from the exactness of $dS$; here they appear as consequences of the Maxwell relations, together with their enthalpy counterparts.

> [!remark] Remark: What level A assumed, now proved
> Level A took the internal energy of an ideal gas to depend on its temperature alone ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^rem-a3-2-3|§A3.2]]), from the molecular picture of [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]] (no forces between molecules) and kinetic theory. It used this in $dE_{\text{int}} = nC_V\,dT$ for every process ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]) and in the free expansion ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-1|Theorem §A3.6.1]]). [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]] (and, from the exactness of $dS$, [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]) derives it from the equation of state $pV = Nk_BT$ and the second law alone, with no molecular model: a gas cannot obey $pV = Nk_BT$ with the absolute temperature unless its energy is independent of volume, so any substance with this equation of state has $U = U(T)$. Conversely, the size of $(\partial U/\partial V)_T$ for a real gas measures its departure from ideality, through $T(\partial p/\partial T)_V - p$ (for the van der Waals gas, $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = aN^2/V^2$, [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.4: The General Relation between C_p and C_V
> For any simple system, at fixed $N$,
>
> $$
> C_p - C_V = T\Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p = \frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T} .
> $$
>
> Hence $C_p \ge C_V$ for every substance with $\kappa_T > 0$ (compressing it at constant temperature shrinks it), with equality exactly where $\alpha = 0$. For an ideal gas, $\alpha = 1/T$ and $\kappa_T = 1/p$ give $C_p - C_V = pV/T = Nk_B = nR$: level A's [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]] (per mole, $C_p - C_V = R$) is the special case.
>
> *Source: Blundell Example 16.5, eq. (16.73) · Schroeder Problem 5.14(c), (e) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §10*

^thm-b3-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Equate the two expressions for $T\,dS$ in [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]] along a change at constant pressure. Then $dp = 0$ and $dV = (\partial V/\partial T)_p\,dT = V\alpha\,dT$, so
>
> $$
> C_V\,dT + \frac{T\alpha}{\kappa_T}\,V\alpha\,dT = C_p\,dT \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_p - C_V = \frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T} .
> $$
>
> The first form follows by writing $\alpha/\kappa_T = (\partial p/\partial T)_V$ and $V\alpha = (\partial V/\partial T)_p$. The right side is $TV\kappa_T^{-1}$ times $\alpha^2 \ge 0$, so it is non-negative when $\kappa_T > 0$, and zero exactly when $\alpha = 0$. For the ideal gas, $V = Nk_BT/p$ gives $\alpha = 1/T$ and $\kappa_T = 1/p$.
>
> Equivalently (Blundell's route), differentiate $S(T, V)$ along a constant-pressure path: $(\partial S/\partial T)_p = (\partial S/\partial T)_V + (\partial S/\partial V)_T(\partial V/\partial T)_p$, then multiply by $T$ and use the Maxwell relation for $(\partial S/\partial V)_T$ (the rule for changing the variable held fixed, [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]). A third route inserts the energy equation into the first-law form $C_p - C_V = [(\partial U/\partial V)_T + p](\partial V/\partial T)_p$ of [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]], as [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]] does.

> [!caution] Caution: The C_p formula in the user's notes, §8.2
> The user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §8.2, write $C_P = T(\partial S/\partial T)_P = -T(\partial^2 G/\partial T^2)_P = T(\partial V/\partial T)_P\,(\partial P/\partial T)_V^{-1}$. The first two equalities are right; the last expression is not a heat capacity (its unit is $\mathrm{K\,m^3/Pa}$, not $\mathrm{J/K}$). What the Maxwell relation gives is the difference $C_P - C_V = T(\partial V/\partial T)_P(\partial P/\partial T)_V$, with the factor $(\partial P/\partial T)_V$ to the first power, and §10 of the same notes states the correct form $C_P - C_V = TV\alpha_P^2/\kappa_T$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.5: Adiabatic Responses; the Ratio of Compressibilities
> At fixed $N$, the temperature changes along an adiabat by
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial T}{\partial p}\Bigr)_S = \frac{TV\alpha}{C_p}, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\Bigr)_S = -\frac{T}{C_V}\Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V = -\frac{T\alpha}{C_V\kappa_T} ,
> $$
>
> and the adiabatic compressibility is
>
> $$
> \kappa_S = \kappa_T - \frac{TV\alpha^2}{C_p}, \qquad \frac{\kappa_T}{\kappa_S} = \frac{C_p}{C_V} = \gamma .
> $$
>
> So the slope of an adiabat in the $pV$ plane is $\gamma$ times that of the isotherm through the same point, $(\partial p/\partial V)_S = \gamma\,(\partial p/\partial V)_T$, and adiabats are steeper than isotherms for every substance with $\alpha \ne 0$, not only for the ideal gas of [[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-3|Theorem §A3.6.3]].
>
> *Source: Blundell Example 16.7, eqs. (16.80)–(16.81), Exercise 16.2(ii) · Schroeder Problem 5.16 · PHY 306, Problem Set 4, Problem 2; Problem Set 5, Problem 3(b) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §8.3, §10*

^thm-b3-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Set $dS = 0$ in the two $T\,dS$ equations of [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]: the second gives $C_p\,dT = TV\alpha\,dp$, the first gives $C_V\,dT = -(T\alpha/\kappa_T)\,dV$. These are the two adiabatic temperature changes.
>
> Now regard $V$ as a function of $T$ and $p$: $dV = V\alpha\,dT - V\kappa_T\,dp$. Along the adiabat insert $dT = (TV\alpha/C_p)\,dp$:
>
> $$
> dV = \Bigl(\frac{TV^2\alpha^2}{C_p} - V\kappa_T\Bigr)dp \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \kappa_S = -\frac1V\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial p}\Bigr)_S = \kappa_T - \frac{TV\alpha^2}{C_p} .
> $$
>
> By [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]], $TV\alpha^2 = \kappa_T(C_p - C_V)$, so $\kappa_S = \kappa_T\bigl[1 - (C_p - C_V)/C_p\bigr] = \kappa_T C_V/C_p$. Finally, by the reciprocal rule ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]]), $(\partial p/\partial V)_S = -1/(V\kappa_S)$ and $(\partial p/\partial V)_T = -1/(V\kappa_T)$, whose ratio is $\kappa_T/\kappa_S = \gamma$. This slope relation needs only the first law: [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]] obtains it without entropy; what the Maxwell relations add is $\kappa_T - \kappa_S$ and the adiabatic temperature changes in terms of $\alpha$.

## Free expansion and throttling

> [!definition] Definition §B3.2.2: Joule and Joule–Thomson Coefficients
> The **Joule coefficient** is $\mu_J = (\partial T/\partial V)_U$, the temperature change per unit volume in a free (Joule) expansion, in which a gas expands into a vacuum inside a rigid insulated vessel, so that $U$ is unchanged ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-1|Theorem §A3.6.1]]).
>
> The **Joule–Thomson coefficient** is $\mu_{JT} = (\partial T/\partial p)_H$, the temperature change per unit pressure drop in a **throttling process**: gas at pressure $p_1$ is pushed steadily through a porous plug or valve into a region at $p_2 < p_1$, with no heat exchanged. A parcel of volume $V_1$ has work $p_1V_1$ done on it as it is pushed in and does work $p_2V_2$ as it emerges, so $U_2 - U_1 = p_1V_1 - p_2V_2$, that is, $H_1 = H_2$: throttling conserves enthalpy. Blundell calls it the Joule–Kelvin coefficient $\mu_{JK}$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §27.1, eq. (27.1), §27.3, eqs. (27.14)–(27.16), Exercise 16.2*

^def-b3-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.6: Joule and Joule–Thomson Coefficients
> At fixed $N$,
>
> $$
> \mu_J = -\frac{1}{C_V}\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T = -\frac{1}{C_V}\Bigl[T\Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V - p\Bigr], \qquad
> \mu_{JT} = -\frac{1}{C_p}\Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\Bigr)_T = \frac{1}{C_p}\Bigl[T\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p - V\Bigr] = \frac{V}{C_p}\,(T\alpha - 1) .
> $$
>
> Both vanish for a gas with $pV = Nk_BT$. A gas cools in a free expansion when $(\partial U/\partial V)_T > 0$, and cools on throttling exactly when $T\alpha > 1$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §27.1, eqs. (27.2)–(27.5), §27.3, eqs. (27.17)–(27.20), Exercise 16.2(i), (iii) · PHY 306, Problem Set 4, Problem 2*

^thm-b3-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $T$, $V$, $U$ are related by the equation of state, so the triple-product rule ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]]) gives $(\partial T/\partial V)_U = -(\partial T/\partial U)_V\,(\partial U/\partial V)_T = -(1/C_V)(\partial U/\partial V)_T$, using $(\partial T/\partial U)_V = 1/C_V$ (reciprocal rule). The energy equation ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]]) supplies $(\partial U/\partial V)_T$. In the same way $(\partial T/\partial p)_H = -(\partial T/\partial H)_p\,(\partial H/\partial p)_T = -(1/C_p)(\partial H/\partial p)_T$, and $(\partial H/\partial p)_T = V(1 - T\alpha)$. For $pV = Nk_BT$ both energy-equation derivatives vanish (Theorem §B3.2.3). Since $C_V, C_p > 0$, the sign of each coefficient is the sign of the bracket.

> [!remark] Remark: Real gases
> Level A argued from the molecular picture that a real gas cools slightly in a free expansion, because separating attracting molecules raises their potential energy ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^rem-a3-6-1|§A3.6]]); [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-6|Theorem §B3.2.6]] turns this into a formula in terms of the equation of state. The Joule–Thomson coefficient changes sign at the **inversion temperature**, where $T\alpha = 1$; below it throttling cools the gas, which is how gases are liquefied. Evaluating both coefficients for the van der Waals gas, the inversion curve and the cooling cycles belong to [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]] and [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-9|Theorem §B9.1.9]]. The symbols $\mu_J$, $\mu_{JT}$ have nothing to do with the chemical potential $\mu$.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.2.1: The Ideal Gas, Recovered
> For $pV = Nk_BT$ with constant $C_V$, the general results reproduce level A without kinetic theory. The response functions are $\alpha = (1/V)(\partial V/\partial T)_p = 1/T$ and $\kappa_T = -(1/V)(\partial V/\partial p)_T = 1/p$. Then:
> - $C_p - C_V = TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T = pV/T = Nk_B$ ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]]), which is $C_p - C_V = R$ per mole ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]); so $\gamma - 1 = Nk_B/C_V$.
> - Along an adiabat ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-5|Theorem §B3.2.5]]), $(\partial T/\partial V)_S = -(T/C_V)(\partial p/\partial T)_V = -(T/C_V)(Nk_B/V)$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{dT}{T} = -\frac{Nk_B}{C_V}\,\frac{dV}{V} = -(\gamma - 1)\frac{dV}{V} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}, \qquad pV^\gamma = \text{const} ,
> $$
>
> which is [[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-2|Theorem §A3.6.2]].
> - $\kappa_S = \kappa_T/\gamma = 1/(\gamma p)$, the adiabatic compressibility of a gas, which fixes the speed of sound in it.
> - $\mu_J = \mu_{JT} = 0$ ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-6|Theorem §B3.2.6]]): neither free expansion nor throttling changes an ideal gas's temperature.
>
> Level A needed $E_{\text{int}} = E_{\text{int}}(T)$ as an input for the first two results; here it is an output ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^rem-b3-2-3|Remark: What level A assumed]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 4, Problem 2(3)–(4) · Blundell Example 16.7, eqs. (16.82)–(16.85) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §10*

^ex-b3-2-1

> [!example]- ★ Example §B3.2.2: Water: C_p, C_V and the 4 °C Point
> For water at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, $\alpha = 2.57\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm K^{-1}$ and $\kappa_T = 4.52\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{Pa^{-1}}$. Per kilogram ($V = 1.00\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3}$), [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]] gives
>
> $$
> c_p - c_V = \frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T} = \frac{(298\ \mathrm K)(1.00\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3})(2.57\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm K^{-1})^2}{4.52\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{Pa^{-1}}} = 44\ \mathrm{J/K},
> $$
>
> about $1\%$ of $c_p = 4186\ \mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$ ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^law-a1-3-2|Law §A1.3.2]]), so $\gamma \approx 1.01$. For liquids and solids the distinction is small, but $c_V$ is the hard one to measure: holding the volume of warming water fixed takes more than five atmospheres per kelvin ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^rem-b1-2-1|Remark: Why heat capacities of liquids are measured at constant pressure]]), so tabulated heat capacities of condensed matter are $c_p$ and $c_V$ is computed from them, as here.
>
> At $4\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ water's expansivity vanishes ([[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^rem-a1-2-3|§A1.2]]) and $c_p = c_V$ exactly. Between $0$ and $4\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, $\alpha < 0$, yet still $c_p > c_V$: the difference depends on $\alpha^2$. Water warmed at constant pressure in that range contracts, and the contraction raises its internal energy by more than the work the surroundings do on it, so heat beyond the constant-volume amount is still needed.
>
> *Source: Schroeder Problems 1.46(e), 5.14(f)*

^ex-b3-2-2

> [!example] Example §B3.2.3: The Third Law Stops Thermal Expansion
> Near absolute zero, measured heat capacities have the form $C_p = T^n\,(a + bT + cT^2 + \dots)$ with $n > 0$, where the coefficients may depend on pressure. Show that $\alpha \to 0$ as $T \to 0$, and that $V\alpha/C_p$ tends to a finite limit.
>
> By the fourth Maxwell relation ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]]), $V\alpha = (\partial V/\partial T)_p = -(\partial S/\partial p)_T$, the entropy change on isothermal compression. The third law ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-3|Principle §A4.6.3]]) says this tends to zero with $T$, so $\alpha \to 0$: this is [[§B2.3 The Third Law#^thm-b2-3-2|Theorem §B2.3.2]], reached here without any assumption on $\kappa_T$. The problem set makes it quantitative: with $S(0, p) = 0$ and $C_p = T(\partial S/\partial T)_p$ ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]),
>
> $$
> S(T, p) = \int_0^T \frac{C_p}{T'}\,dT' = T^n\Bigl(\frac an + \frac{bT}{n+1} + \dots\Bigr), \qquad V\alpha = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial p}\Bigr)_T = -T^n\Bigl(\frac{a'}{n} + \frac{b'T}{n + 1} + \dots\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> with $a' = \partial a/\partial p$. Hence $V\alpha/C_p \to -a'/(na)$, a finite constant. Along an adiabat, by [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-5|Theorem §B3.2.5]],
>
> $$
> \frac{dT}{T} = \frac{V\alpha}{C_p}\,dp \;\longrightarrow\; -\frac{a'}{na}\,dp \qquad (T \to 0) .
> $$
>
> A finite pressure change multiplies $T$ by a finite factor, so no finite sequence of adiabatic compressions and expansions brings $T$ to zero: the unattainability form of the third law ([[§B2.3 The Third Law#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 5, Problem 3 · Schroeder Problem 5.13*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The Maxwell relations are the equality of mixed partials, [[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]], applied to the potentials; in the language of forms, $d(dU) = 0$ gives $dT\wedge dS = dp\wedge dV$ ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^def-22-8|452 Def. §22.8]]), the area-preservation of [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^rem-b3-2-2|Remark: All four in one Jacobian]].
> - Every manipulation here also uses the reciprocal and triple-product rules of [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]], which follow from the chain rule ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]) and the implicit function theorem ([[§12 The Implicit Function Theorem#^thm-12-1|452 Thm. §12.1]]).
> - Statistical mechanics computes the response functions from a partition function: $C_V$ from the energy fluctuations ([[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]]).
> - Magnetic and elastic analogues of these relations: [[§B3.4★ Rods, Bubbles and Magnets]].
