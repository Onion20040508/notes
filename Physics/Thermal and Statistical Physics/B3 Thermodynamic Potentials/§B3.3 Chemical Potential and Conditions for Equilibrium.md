Two bodies in thermal contact end at a common temperature ([[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^pr-a1-1-1|Principle §A1.1.1]]), and heat flows from the hotter to the colder ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]]). Level A stops there. This section adds the variable that governs the exchange of *particles*, the chemical potential $\mu$, and derives from the entropy maximum all three equilibrium conditions at once: equal $T$ for heat exchange, equal $p$ for volume exchange, equal $\mu$ for particle exchange. Extensivity then gives $G = \mu N$ and the Gibbs–Duhem relation. The minimum of $G$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]) gives the equilibrium condition for chemical reactions and, for ideal gases, the law of mass action. The statistical meaning of $\mu$, as a derivative of $k_B\ln\Omega$, is [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-3|Def. §B6.2.3]]. Notation and signs are those of [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^rem-b3-1-1|Remark: Notation and signs]].

## The chemical potential

> [!definition] Definition §B3.3.1: Chemical Potential
> For a system whose particle number $N$ can change, the **chemical potential** $\mu$ is the coefficient of $dN$ in the fundamental relation $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV + \mu\,dN$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]]). Equivalently, by [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]],
>
> $$
> \mu = \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{S,V} = -T\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{U,V} = \Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{T,V} = \Bigl(\frac{\partial G}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{T,p} :
> $$
>
> the change of each potential when one particle is added with its own natural variables held fixed. For several species, $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV + \sum_i \mu_i\,dN_i$, and $\mu_i$ is the derivative with respect to $N_i$ with all the other $N_j$ held fixed as well. Chemists use the chemical potential per mole, $N_A\mu$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §22.1, eqs. (22.2)–(22.7), §22.6 · Schroeder §3.5, eqs. (3.55), (3.58)–(3.60), (3.65)–(3.67), §5.1, eqs. (5.22), (5.24) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, eq. (2)*

^def-b3-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: What μ measures
> - The form $-T(\partial S/\partial N)_{U,V}$ follows from solving the fundamental relation for $dS = dU/T + (p/T)\,dV - (\mu/T)\,dN$. A system whose entropy rises a lot when it receives a particle (with no energy) has a strongly negative $\mu$, and "wants" particles: $\mu$ plays for particle exchange the role that $1/T$ plays for energy exchange, with the opposite sign.
> - The form $(\partial U/\partial N)_{S,V}$ is the energy a particle brings in when the entropy is kept fixed. Adding a particle normally raises the multiplicity, so energy must be *removed* to keep $S$ fixed, and $\mu$ is negative for a classical gas: for helium at $300\ \mathrm K$ and $1\ \mathrm{atm}$, $\mu \approx -0.33\ \mathrm{eV}$ ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]]; Schroeder's eq. (3.64) prints $-0.32$). Raising the density makes $\mu$ less negative. Any energy the particle must be given to enter, such as a gravitational $mgz$ or a binding energy, adds to $\mu$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.5, eqs. (3.56)–(3.64), Problem 3.37 · Blundell §22.2*

## Conditions for equilibrium

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.1: Thermal, Mechanical and Diffusive Equilibrium
> Let two subsystems 1 and 2 together form an isolated system, separated by a wall that lets energy, volume (a movable wall) and particles (a permeable wall) pass. In equilibrium
>
> $$
> T_1 = T_2, \qquad p_1 = p_2, \qquad \mu_1 = \mu_2 ,
> $$
>
> each condition holding when the corresponding exchange is allowed. Out of equilibrium, energy flows from higher to lower $T$; at equal temperatures the subsystem at higher $p$ expands, and particles flow from higher to lower $\mu$. The conditions on $T$ and $p$ are [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]]; what is new here is the condition on $\mu$. With several species, $\mu_i$ is equalised separately for each species the wall lets through.
>
> *Source: Blundell §22.2, eqs. (22.8)–(22.12) · Schroeder §3.4, eqs. (3.36)–(3.39), §3.5, eqs. (3.52)–(3.56) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §6.5*

^thm-b3-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Extend the derivation of [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]] by particle exchange. Now $N = N_1 + N_2$ is fixed as well, $dN_2 = -dN_1$, and each subsystem obeys $dS_j = dU_j/T_j + (p_j/T_j)\,dV_j - (\mu_j/T_j)\,dN_j$ ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]]). The total entropy change acquires one more term:
>
> $$
> dS = dS_1 + dS_2 = \Bigl(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\Bigr)dU_1 + \Bigl(\frac{p_1}{T_1} - \frac{p_2}{T_2}\Bigr)dV_1 - \Bigl(\frac{\mu_1}{T_1} - \frac{\mu_2}{T_2}\Bigr)dN_1 .
> $$
>
> At the entropy maximum ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], first row) all three partial derivatives vanish ([[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-1|452 Thm. §14.1]]): $T_1 = T_2$ and $p_1 = p_2$ as before, and then $\mu_1 = \mu_2$. Away from equilibrium $dS > 0$; with $T_1 = T_2$ and particle exchange this reads $-(\mu_1 - \mu_2)\,dN_1 > 0$, so $N_1$ grows exactly when $\mu_1 < \mu_2$.
>
> The same conditions follow at fixed $T$ from the minimum of $F$: with $F = F_1 + F_2$ and $V_1 + V_2$ fixed, $dF = (-p_1 + p_2)\,dV_1 + (\mu_1 - \mu_2)\,dN_1 = 0$ (Schwartz's derivation of $p_1 = p_2$).
>
> Their statistical counterparts, read off from the multiplicity, are [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-4|Theorem §B6.2.4]] and [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-6|Theorem §B6.2.6]].

^der-b3-3-1

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]], [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]], [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], [[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-1|452 Thm. §14.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: What the wall allows decides what is equalised
> - A rigid wall that passes heat equalises $T$ only. A semipermeable membrane passes some species and not others: only the $\mu_i$ of the species that pass are equalised, and if the membrane is rigid the pressures on its two sides can differ in equilibrium. That pressure difference is the osmotic pressure ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^ex-b3-3-3|Example §B3.3.3]]).
> - A movable wall that does *not* pass heat does not simply give $p_1 = p_2$ with independent temperatures; the derivation above assumed thermal contact, and without it the equilibrium is more delicate.
> - Blundell's text (§22.2), after showing that particles flow from 1 to 2 when $\mu_1 > \mu_2$, continues "Similarly, if $\mu_2 < \mu_1$, then $dN < 0$"; the condition meant is $\mu_2 > \mu_1$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.4 (remark after eq. (3.39)), §5.5*

## Extensivity: G = μN and the Gibbs–Duhem relation

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.2: Euler Relation; G = μN; Φ = −pV
> Suppose the entropy is extensive: $S(\lambda U, \lambda V, \lambda N) = \lambda S(U, V, N)$ for all $\lambda > 0$. Then
>
> $$
> U = TS - pV + \mu N ,
> $$
>
> and therefore
>
> $$
> G = U + pV - TS = \mu N, \qquad \Phi = U - TS - \mu N = -pV .
> $$
>
> So $\mu$ is the Gibbs free energy per particle, $\mu = G/N$. With several species, $U = TS - pV + \sum_i\mu_iN_i$ and $G = \sum_i \mu_iN_i$; here $\mu_i$ is the chemical potential of species $i$ *in the mixture*, which in general differs from that of the pure substance.
>
> *Source: Blundell §22.5, eqs. (22.42)–(22.49) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §2, eqs. (12)–(17), §5, §7, eq. (103) · Schroeder §5.2, eqs. (5.34)–(5.37), Problem 5.23(c)*

^thm-b3-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate $S(\lambda U, \lambda V, \lambda N) = \lambda S(U, V, N)$ with respect to $\lambda$ by the chain rule ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]):
>
> $$
> U\,\frac{\partial S}{\partial U}(\lambda U, \lambda V, \lambda N) + V\,\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}(\dots) + N\,\frac{\partial S}{\partial N}(\dots) = S(U, V, N) .
> $$
>
> Set $\lambda = 1$ and insert $\partial S/\partial U = 1/T$, $\partial S/\partial V = p/T$, $\partial S/\partial N = -\mu/T$ ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]]): $S = U/T + pV/T - \mu N/T$. Multiply by $T$ and rearrange to get the Euler relation; subtract $TS - pV$ for $G$, and $TS + \mu N$ for $\Phi$, using [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^def-b3-1-2|Def. §B3.1.2]]. With several species the same steps run over all the $N_i$.
>
> Schroeder's direct argument for $G = \mu N$: by $\mu = (\partial G/\partial N)_{T,p}$, each particle added at fixed $T$ and $p$ raises $G$ by $\mu$. Since $T$ and $p$ are intensive, adding particles at fixed $T$, $p$ only enlarges a system of unchanged intensive state, so $\mu$ does not drift as $N$ grows, and $G$, built up from $G = 0$ at $N = 0$, is $\mu N$. Likewise $\Phi(T, V, \mu)$ is extensive with $V$ its only extensive variable, so $\Phi = V\,\partial\Phi/\partial V = -pV$.

^der-b3-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]], [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^def-b3-1-2|Def. §B3.1.2]], [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Why G, and when extensivity fails
> - $F$ is extensive too, but $F \ne \mu N$: $\mu = (\partial F/\partial N)_{T,V}$ holds $V$ fixed, so adding particles makes the system denser and $\mu$ changes as $N$ grows. The argument for $G$ works only because both variables held fixed, $T$ and $p$, are intensive.
> - Extensivity of entropy is an assumption, not a definition. It fails with long-range forces: a self-gravitating star's entropy is not extensive, and a black hole's grows with the area of its horizon. Schwartz therefore avoids the Euler relation where it is not checked. It also needs the system to be large, so that surface contributions are negligible.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §5.2 (discussion after eq. (5.37)) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §2*

^cau-b3-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.3: Gibbs–Duhem Relation
> Under the hypothesis of [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]],
>
> $$
> S\,dT - V\,dp + N\,d\mu = 0 , \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad d\mu = -\frac SN\,dT + \frac VN\,dp .
> $$
>
> The three intensive variables $T$, $p$, $\mu$ of a single-phase, single-species system are not independent: any two determine the third. In particular $(\partial\mu/\partial p)_T = V/N$ and $(\partial\mu/\partial T)_p = -S/N$, the volume and minus the entropy per particle. With several species, $S\,dT - V\,dp + \sum_i N_i\,d\mu_i = 0$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, eqs. (18)–(20) · Schroeder §5.2, eq. (5.38)*

^thm-b3-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the differential of the Euler relation $U = TS - pV + \mu N$:
>
> $$
> dU = T\,dS + S\,dT - p\,dV - V\,dp + \mu\,dN + N\,d\mu ,
> $$
>
> and subtract the fundamental relation $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV + \mu\,dN$: what remains is $0 = S\,dT - V\,dp + N\,d\mu$. Dividing by $N$ and reading off coefficients gives the partial derivatives; they agree with $G = \mu N$ and $(\partial G/\partial p)_T = V$, $(\partial G/\partial T)_p = -S$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]).

^der-b3-3-3

*Uses:* [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]], [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]], [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.4: Chemical Potential of an Ideal Gas
> For an ideal gas at temperature $T$ and pressure $p$,
>
> $$
> \mu(T, p) = \mu^\circ(T) + k_BT\ln\frac{p}{p^\circ} ,
> $$
>
> where $\mu^\circ(T) = \mu(T, p^\circ)$ is the value at a reference pressure $p^\circ$, conventionally $1\ \mathrm{bar}$ (the standard chemical potential). In a mixture of ideal gases, each species $i$ has the chemical potential it would have alone at its **partial pressure** $p_i = (N_i/N)\,p$:
>
> $$
> \mu_i = \mu_i^\circ(T) + k_BT\ln\frac{p_i}{p^\circ} .
> $$
>
> Per mole, $k_BT$ becomes $RT$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §5.2, eqs. (5.38)–(5.40), Problems 3.38, 5.22 · Blundell Example 22.3, eqs. (22.63)–(22.65) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §5.1–5.2, eqs. (57)–(59), (67)–(68)*

^thm-b3-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the Gibbs–Duhem relation ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]]) at fixed $T$, $d\mu = (V/N)\,dp = (k_BT/p)\,dp$ by the ideal gas law ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]). Integrate from $p^\circ$ to $p$: $\mu(T, p) - \mu(T, p^\circ) = k_BT\ln(p/p^\circ)$.
>
> For the mixture: the molecules of different ideal gases do not interact, so the free energy of the mixture is the sum of the free energies each gas would have alone in the whole volume $V$, $F = F_1(T, V, N_1) + F_2(T, V, N_2) + \dots$ (Schwartz derives this from the factorised partition function, [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]]). Hence $\mu_1 = (\partial F/\partial N_1)_{T,V,N_2}$ depends on $T$, $V$, $N_1$ only: it is the chemical potential of gas 1 alone in $V$, at its own pressure $N_1k_BT/V = (N_1/N)\,p = p_1$ (Dalton's law, with $p = Nk_BT/V$ for the whole mixture).

^der-b3-3-4

*Uses:* [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]], [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: The absolute value of μ
> Thermodynamics gives only the pressure dependence; $\mu^\circ(T)$ is taken from tables ($\mu = G/N$). Statistical mechanics computes it: for a monatomic ideal gas $\mu = k_BT\ln(n\lambda_{\text{th}}^3)$, with $n = N/V$ and $\lambda_{\text{th}}$ the thermal wavelength ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]]; [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]]; from the multiplicity, [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^ex-b6-2-2|Example §B6.2.2]]). This is negative as long as $n\lambda_{\text{th}}^3 \ll 1$, the classical regime ([[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-1|Def. §B10.2.1]]), and it reproduces $k_BT\ln p$ at fixed $T$.
>
> *Source: Blundell Example 22.1, eq. (22.15) · Schroeder §3.5, eq. (3.63) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, eq. (56)*

## Chemical equilibrium

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.5: Equilibrium Condition for a Reaction
> Write a chemical reaction as $\sum_i \nu_i A_i = 0$, with stoichiometric coefficients $\nu_i > 0$ for products and $\nu_i < 0$ for reactants (for $\mathrm N_2 + 3\mathrm H_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NH_3}$: $\nu_{\mathrm N_2} = -1$, $\nu_{\mathrm H_2} = -3$, $\nu_{\mathrm{NH_3}} = 2$). At fixed $T$ and $p$ the reaction is in equilibrium when
>
> $$
> \sum_i \nu_i\,\mu_i = 0 ,
> $$
>
> the reaction equation with each species replaced by its chemical potential. If $\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i < 0$ the reaction runs forward, if $> 0$ backward. The same condition holds at fixed $T$ and $V$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §22.8, Examples 22.4–22.5, eqs. (22.77)–(22.79) · Schroeder §5.6, eqs. (5.95)–(5.102) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §5.2*

^thm-b3-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> As the reaction proceeds, the particle numbers change in the fixed proportions of the reaction equation: $dN_i = \nu_i\,d\xi$, where $\xi$ (the **extent of reaction**) counts how many times the reaction has occurred. At fixed $T$ and $p$, $dG = \sum_i\mu_i\,dN_i = \bigl(\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i\bigr)d\xi$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]], several species). $G$ decreases in every spontaneous change and is minimum in equilibrium ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]). So $d\xi > 0$ requires $\sum\nu_i\mu_i < 0$, $d\xi < 0$ requires $\sum\nu_i\mu_i > 0$, and equilibrium, where $G$ is stationary in $\xi$, requires $\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i = 0$. At fixed $T$ and $V$ the same argument applies to $F$, with $dF = \sum_i\mu_i\,dN_i$.

^der-b3-3-5

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]], [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.6: Law of Mass Action; van 't Hoff Equation
> For a reaction $\sum_i\nu_iA_i = 0$ among ideal gases at temperature $T$, the equilibrium partial pressures satisfy
>
> $$
> \prod_i \Bigl(\frac{p_i}{p^\circ}\Bigr)^{\nu_i} = K(T), \qquad K(T) = \exp\Bigl(-\frac{\Delta_rG^\circ}{RT}\Bigr), \qquad \Delta_rG^\circ = N_A\sum_i\nu_i\,\mu_i^\circ(T) ,
> $$
>
> where $\Delta_rG^\circ$ is the **standard reaction Gibbs energy**, the change of $G$ when the reaction runs once per mole with every species pure at $p^\circ$. $K$ is the **equilibrium constant**. In mole fractions $x_i = p_i/p$, $\prod_i x_i^{\nu_i} = K\,(p/p^\circ)^{-\sum_i\nu_i}$. The equilibrium constant depends on temperature through the **van 't Hoff equation**
>
> $$
> \frac{d\ln K}{dT} = \frac{\Delta_rH^\circ}{RT^2} ,
> $$
>
> with $\Delta_rH^\circ$ the standard reaction enthalpy: $K$ rises with $T$ for an endothermic reaction and falls for an exothermic one.
>
> *Source: Blundell §22.8, eqs. (22.80)–(22.89) · Schroeder §5.6, eqs. (5.103)–(5.108), Problem 5.85 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §5.2–5.3, eqs. (76)–(77)*

^thm-b3-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert $\mu_i = \mu_i^\circ + k_BT\ln(p_i/p^\circ)$ ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-4|Theorem §B3.3.4]]) into $\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i = 0$ ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]):
>
> $$
> \sum_i\nu_i\mu_i^\circ + k_BT\sum_i\nu_i\ln\frac{p_i}{p^\circ} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln\prod_i\Bigl(\frac{p_i}{p^\circ}\Bigr)^{\nu_i} = -\frac{\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i^\circ}{k_BT} = -\frac{\Delta_rG^\circ}{RT} ,
> $$
>
> multiplying numerator and denominator by $N_A$ ($N_Ak_B = R$). Exponentiate. For the mole-fraction form substitute $p_i = x_ip$.
>
> For the temperature dependence, $\mu_i^\circ = G_i^\circ/N_i$ for the pure gas at $p^\circ$ (by [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]]), so the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]) at the fixed pressure $p^\circ$ gives $d(\mu_i^\circ/T)/dT = -h_i^\circ/T^2$, with $h_i^\circ$ the enthalpy per particle. Summing with weights $N_A\nu_i$, $d(\Delta_rG^\circ/T)/dT = -\Delta_rH^\circ/T^2$, and $\ln K = -\Delta_rG^\circ/(RT)$ then gives $d\ln K/dT = \Delta_rH^\circ/(RT^2)$.

![[ph-th-b3-3-1.svg]]
*Gibbs free energy of an ideal-gas reaction $A \rightleftharpoons B$ at fixed $T$ and $p = p^\circ$, as a function of the fraction $\xi$ converted to $B$, in units of $Nk_BT$ and relative to pure $A$; $\Delta = (\mu_B^\circ - \mu_A^\circ)/k_BT$. Without mixing (dashed) $G$ would be linear and the minimum would sit at an end. The mixing entropy adds $k_BT[\xi\ln\xi + (1 - \xi)\ln(1 - \xi)]$ per particle, whose slope is infinite at both ends, so the minimum (dots) lies inside, at $\xi/(1 - \xi) = e^{-\Delta} = K$: no reaction goes strictly to completion.*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Why reactions do not go to completion, and Le Chatelier's principle
> - The mixing term makes $G(\xi)$ convex with infinite slope at both ends (figure), so the equilibrium always contains some of every species, however large $|\Delta_rG^\circ|$. Since $|\Delta_rG^\circ|$ is typically hundreds of $\mathrm{kJ/mol}$ while $RT \approx 2.5\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$ at room temperature, $K$ is usually either enormous or tiny, and the sign of $\Delta_rG^\circ$ alone tells which way a reaction goes, unless one side is present only in exponentially small amounts.
> - The law of mass action expresses **Le Chatelier's principle**: a system in equilibrium responds to a disturbance in a way that partly undoes it. Adding a reactant drives the reaction forward. Raising the pressure favours the side with fewer gas molecules: for $\sum_i\nu_i < 0$ the mole-fraction product must grow as $p^{|\sum\nu_i|}$. Raising the temperature favours the endothermic direction (van 't Hoff). For the lime kiln $\mathrm{CaCO_3} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CaO} + \mathrm{CO_2}$, with $\Delta_rH^\circ = 178\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$ and $\Delta_rS^\circ = 161\ \mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}$, $\Delta_rG^\circ = \Delta_rH^\circ - T\Delta_rS^\circ$ is $+130\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$ at $298\ \mathrm K$ but $-64\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$ at $1500\ \mathrm K$: heating reverses the direction of the reaction.
> - Equilibrium thermodynamics says nothing about rates: ammonia synthesis is strongly favoured at room temperature but negligibly slow without a catalyst.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §5.6 (Figure 5.41 discussion, nitrogen fixation) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §5.2–5.3, eqs. (79)–(83) · Blundell §22.8*

^rem-b3-3-3

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Particles without a conservation law have μ = 0
> If particles can be created and destroyed freely, nothing fixes their number, and at fixed $T$ and $V$ the system chooses $N$ to minimise $F$: $(\partial F/\partial N)_{T,V} = \mu = 0$. Photons in a cavity are the standard case ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-2|Theorem §B11.1.2]]). Where a conservation law does act, [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]] fixes combinations of $\mu$'s instead: for $e^- + e^+ \rightleftharpoons \gamma$ with $\mu_\gamma = 0$, $\mu_{e^-} + \mu_{e^+} = 0$. The value of $\mu_{e^-}$ itself is then set by the net number $N_{e^-} - N_{e^+}$, which charge conservation fixes. (Photons can carry $\mu \ne 0$ when their source is out of equilibrium, as in a light-emitting diode.)
>
> *Source: Blundell §22.7, eqs. (22.54)–(22.62)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.3.1: The Isothermal Atmosphere from Diffusive Equilibrium
> An ideal gas of molecules of mass $m$ fills a tall column at uniform temperature $T$, in a uniform gravitational field $g$. Find how the pressure varies with height.
>
> Divide the column into thin horizontal slabs, each able to exchange molecules with its neighbours. A molecule added to the slab at height $z$ brings the potential energy $mgz$, which adds to its chemical potential ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^rem-b3-3-1|Remark: What μ measures]]): by [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-4|Theorem §B3.3.4]],
>
> $$
> \mu(z) = \mu^\circ(T) + k_BT\ln\frac{p(z)}{p^\circ} + mgz .
> $$
>
> Diffusive equilibrium between all slabs requires $\mu(z)$ to be the same at every height ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]]), so $k_BT\ln p(z) + mgz$ is constant:
>
> $$
> p(z) = p(0)\,e^{-mgz/k_BT}, \qquad n(z) = n(0)\,e^{-mgz/k_BT} .
> $$
>
> Classical Mechanics reaches the same result from hydrostatic equilibrium, $dp/dz = -\rho g$ with $\rho = pm/k_BT$ ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-4|CM Theorem §A9.1.4]]). Here the pressure is not uniform, yet the column is in equilibrium: the quantity equalised by particle exchange is the total $\mu$, internal plus external, not the density or the pressure. The factor $e^{-mgz/k_BT}$ is a Boltzmann factor ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]]); kinetic theory finds the same profile as a stationary solution of the Boltzmann equation ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^rem-b5-4-4|§B5.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder Problem 3.37*

^ex-b3-3-1

> [!example]- ★ Example §B3.3.2: Ammonia Synthesis
> For $\mathrm N_2 + 3\mathrm H_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NH_3}$, tables give $\Delta_rG^\circ = -32.9\ \mathrm{kJ}$ at $298\ \mathrm K$ (for two moles of ammonia formed). (a) Find $K$. (b) At $500\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, $K = 6.9\times10^{-5}$; what fraction of the nitrogen is converted when a 1 : 3 mixture of $\mathrm N_2$ and $\mathrm H_2$ reaches equilibrium at a total pressure of $400\ \mathrm{atm}$ ($p/p^\circ = 405$), treating the gases as ideal? Compare with $1\ \mathrm{bar}$.
>
> (a) By [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-6|Theorem §B3.3.6]],
>
> $$
> K = \frac{(p_{\mathrm{NH_3}}/p^\circ)^2}{(p_{\mathrm N_2}/p^\circ)(p_{\mathrm H_2}/p^\circ)^3} = \exp\Bigl(\frac{32\,900\ \mathrm J}{(8.31\ \mathrm{J/K})(298\ \mathrm K)}\Bigr) = 5.9\times10^5 ,
> $$
>
> strongly favouring ammonia. The reaction is exothermic, $\Delta_rH^\circ = -91.9\ \mathrm{kJ}$, so $K$ falls on heating; the van 't Hoff equation with $\Delta_rH^\circ$ taken as constant gives $\ln K(773\ \mathrm K) = \ln(5.9\times10^5) - (\Delta_rH^\circ/R)(1/773 - 1/298)\ \mathrm{K^{-1}} = -9.5$, $K \approx 7\times10^{-5}$, close to the tabulated value.
>
> (b) Start from $1$ mol $\mathrm N_2$ and $3$ mol $\mathrm H_2$ and let $x$ mol of $\mathrm N_2$ react: the amounts are $1 - x$, $3(1 - x)$, $2x$, of total $2(2 - x)$. With $p_i = x_ip$ and $\sum_i\nu_i = -2$,
>
> $$
> \frac{x_{\mathrm{NH_3}}^2}{x_{\mathrm N_2}\,x_{\mathrm H_2}^3} = \frac{16\,x^2(2 - x)^2}{27\,(1 - x)^4} = K\Bigl(\frac{p}{p^\circ}\Bigr)^2 = (6.9\times10^{-5})(405)^2 = 11.3 .
> $$
>
> Taking the square root turns this into the quadratic $4x(2 - x) = \sqrt{27\times11.3}\,(1 - x)^2 = 17.5\,(1 - x)^2$, whose root in $(0, 1)$ is $x = 0.57$: $57\%$ of the nitrogen is converted, and ammonia is $40\%$ of the gas. At $p = 1\ \mathrm{bar}$ the right side is $6.9\times10^{-5}$ and $x = 0.005$. The factor $(p/p^\circ)^2$ is Le Chatelier's principle at work: four molecules become two, so high pressure pushes the equilibrium towards ammonia. That is why the industrial (Haber) process runs at hundreds of atmospheres; the high temperature is needed only for the rate.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §5.6 (nitrogen fixation), eqs. (5.103)–(5.109), Problems 5.84–5.86 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §4.2 ($\Delta_rH^\circ$)*

> [!example]- ★ Example §B3.3.3: Osmotic Pressure
> A solution of $N_B$ solute molecules in $N_A$ solvent molecules ($N_B \ll N_A$) is separated from pure solvent by a rigid membrane that passes only the solvent. Find the pressure difference in equilibrium.
>
> In a dilute solution the solvent's chemical potential is lowered by the mixing entropy:
>
> $$
> \mu_A(T, p) = \mu_A^*(T, p) - k_BT\,\frac{N_B}{N_A} ,
> $$
>
> with $\mu_A^*$ that of the pure solvent. (Adding $N_B$ solute molecules to the solvent adds the entropy of mixing $k_BN_B\bigl[1 - \ln(N_B/N_A)\bigr]$ to first order in $N_B/N_A$, so $G = N_A\mu_A^* + N_Bf(T, p) - k_BTN_B\bigl[1 - \ln(N_B/N_A)\bigr]$, and $\mu_A = (\partial G/\partial N_A)_{T,p,N_B}$.) Only the solvent can cross, so only its chemical potential is equalised ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^cau-b3-3-1|Caution: What the wall allows]]). With $p_1$ on the pure side and $p_2$ on the solution side,
>
> $$
> \mu_A^*(T, p_1) = \mu_A^*(T, p_2) - k_BT\,\frac{N_B}{N_A} \approx \mu_A^*(T, p_1) + (p_2 - p_1)\frac{V}{N_A} - k_BT\,\frac{N_B}{N_A} ,
> $$
>
> expanding to first order in $p_2 - p_1$ with $(\partial\mu_A^{\ast}/\partial p)_T = V/N_A$, the volume per solvent molecule ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]]). Hence the **osmotic pressure**
>
> $$
> \Pi = p_2 - p_1 = \frac{N_Bk_BT}{V} = \frac{n_BRT}{V}
> $$
>
> (van 't Hoff's formula), with $n_B$ the moles of solute: the pressure an ideal gas of the solute molecules would exert in the same volume. It depends only on the concentration of solute particles, not on what they are. A biological cell has about one solute molecule per 200 water molecules, $n_B/V \approx 278\ \mathrm{mol/m^3}$, so at $300\ \mathrm K$ placed in pure water it would need $\Pi = (278)(8.31)(300)\ \mathrm{Pa} = 6.9\times10^5\ \mathrm{Pa} \approx 7\ \mathrm{atm}$ to stop water flowing in: animal cells burst, plant cells' rigid walls withstand it.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §5.5, eqs. (5.68)–(5.69), (5.73)–(5.80) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §6, eqs. (84)–(93) · Blundell §22.9, eqs. (22.96)–(22.101)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The equilibrium conditions are the vanishing of the partial derivatives at an interior maximum of the total entropy ([[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-1|452 Thm. §14.1]]); with the conservation laws imposed as constraints instead of substituted, they are Lagrange-multiplier conditions ([[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-3|452 Thm. §14.3]]), with $1/T$, $p/T$, $-\mu/T$ as the multipliers.
> - The Euler relation is Euler's theorem on functions homogeneous of degree one, proved here by the chain rule ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]).
> - Statistical mechanics: $\mu$ from the multiplicity, [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-3|Def. §B6.2.3]]; the grand canonical ensemble, in which $\mu$ is fixed by a particle reservoir and $\Phi = -k_BT\ln\mathcal Z = -pV$, [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-3|Theorem §B7.3.3]]; $\mu$ of the quantum gases, [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions]].
> - Equality of $\mu$ between two phases of one substance gives phase coexistence and the Clausius–Clapeyron equation ([[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]]); the Saha equation for the ionisation of hydrogen is the law of mass action for $\mathrm H \rightleftharpoons p + e$, with the $\mu^\circ$'s computed from statistical mechanics (Schroeder §5.6).
