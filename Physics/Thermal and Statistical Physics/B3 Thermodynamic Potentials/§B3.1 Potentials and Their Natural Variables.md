Level A has one energy-like state function, the internal energy ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]], governed by the first law, [[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]), and the entropy ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]). Combined, they give $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$ ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]), which makes $U$ a function of $S$ and $V$. Entropy, however, cannot be held fixed with laboratory equipment, while temperature and pressure can. This section uses Legendre transforms ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|Def. §B1.2.2]]) to build the energy-like functions whose natural variables are the ones an experiment controls: the enthalpy, the Helmholtz and Gibbs free energies, and the grand potential. It then proves the minimum principles that make them useful. At fixed $T$ and $V$ a system settles into the minimum of $F$; at fixed $T$ and $p$, into the minimum of $G$. None of this appears at level A.

> [!remark] Remark: Notation and signs
> - The internal energy is written $U$, as in all three sources; level A writes $E_{\text{int}}$. Boltzmann's constant is $k_B$.
> - Work keeps level A's sign: $dU = đQ - đW$, with $đW$ the work done *by* the system ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]]), so a quasi-static expansion has $đW = p\,dV$. Blundell and Schroeder write $dU = Q + W$, with $W$ the work done *on* the system, and their inequalities ($\Delta F \le W$, …) appear here with the sign flipped. The bar in $đ$ marks an inexact differential ([[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]]).
> - Unless stated otherwise the system is *simple*: one species of $N$ particles, whose only work is $p\,dV$. Other kinds of work are added in [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets]].

## The fundamental relation

> [!definition] Definition §B3.1.1: Fundamental Relation; Natural Variables
> The equilibrium states of a simple system are labelled by the three extensive variables $S$, $V$, $N$. The function $U(S, V, N)$ is the system's **fundamental relation**. Its differential is
>
> $$
> dU = T\,dS - p\,dV + \mu\,dN ,
> $$
>
> so its first partial derivatives are the intensive variables
>
> $$
> T = \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\Bigr)_{V,N}, \qquad p = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_{S,N}, \qquad \mu = \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{S,V} ,
> $$
>
> where $\mu$ is the chemical potential ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]]). The variables in which a state function's differential takes this form, with coefficients that are themselves state variables, are its **natural variables**: $S$, $V$, $N$ for $U$. An energy-like state function expressed in its natural variables is a **thermodynamic potential**. Known as a function of its natural variables, a potential determines every equilibrium property of the system by differentiation. The pairs $(T, S)$, $(-p, V)$, $(\mu, N)$ are **conjugate**: one intensive, one extensive, and their product has the dimensions of energy.
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.1, eqs. (16.1)–(16.4), §22.1 · Schroeder §3.5, eqs. (3.58)–(3.60) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §1*

^def-b3-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Where the fundamental relation comes from
> For fixed $N$, $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$ is [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]: the first law for a reversible step, with $đQ = T\,dS$ and $đW = p\,dV$, extended to any change between neighbouring equilibrium states because it relates state functions only. For an irreversible step only the identification of the two terms with heat and work fails ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^cau-b2-2-2|Caution: Valid always, but not term by term]]). What is new here is the term $\mu\,dN$, the energy brought in with added particles, sometimes called chemical work; its physics is in [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium]].
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.4 (entropy and heat revisited) · Blundell §16.1*

## The potentials

> [!definition] Definition §B3.1.2: Enthalpy, Free Energies, Grand Potential
> For a simple system,
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> H &= U + pV &&\text{(enthalpy)},\\
> F &= U - TS &&\text{(Helmholtz free energy)},\\
> G &= U + pV - TS = H - TS = F + pV &&\text{(Gibbs free energy)},\\
> \Phi &= U - TS - \mu N = F - \mu N &&\text{(grand potential)}.
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> Each is an extensive state function with the dimensions of energy. Blundell calls $F$ and $G$ the Helmholtz and Gibbs *functions*, and writes $\Phi_G$ for the grand potential; Schroeder calls $\Phi$ the grand free energy; Schwartz writes $E$ for $U$ and $\Omega$ for $\Phi$. Here $\Phi$ is used, keeping $\Omega$ for the multiplicity ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-2|Def. §B6.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.2–16.4, §22.4 · Schroeder §5.1, eqs. (5.1)–(5.3), Problem 5.23 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, eqs. (8)–(11)*

^def-b3-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.1: Differentials and Natural Variables of the Potentials
> For a simple system,
>
> | Potential | Differential | Natural variables |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | $U$ | $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV + \mu\,dN$ | $S, V, N$ |
> | $H$ | $dH = T\,dS + V\,dp + \mu\,dN$ | $S, p, N$ |
> | $F$ | $dF = -S\,dT - p\,dV + \mu\,dN$ | $T, V, N$ |
> | $G$ | $dG = -S\,dT + V\,dp + \mu\,dN$ | $T, p, N$ |
> | $\Phi$ | $d\Phi = -S\,dT - p\,dV - N\,d\mu$ | $T, V, \mu$ |
>
> Hence the first derivatives
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> T &= \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\Bigr)_{V,N} = \Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial S}\Bigr)_{p,N}, &
> S &= -\Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{V,N} = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{p,N} = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{V,\mu},\\
> p &= -\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_{S,N} = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\Bigr)_{T,N} = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial V}\Bigr)_{T,\mu}, &
> V &= \Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\Bigr)_{S,N} = \Bigl(\frac{\partial G}{\partial p}\Bigr)_{T,N},\\
> \mu &= \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{S,V} = \Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{S,p} = \Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{T,V} = \Bigl(\frac{\partial G}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{T,p}, &
> N &= -\Bigl(\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \mu}\Bigr)_{T,V} .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> In particular $dU = T\,dS$ at constant $V, N$; $dH = T\,dS$ at constant $p, N$; $dF = -p\,dV$ at constant $T, N$; and $dG = 0$ in any change at constant $T$, $p$, $N$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.1–16.4 (summary table), eqs. (22.4)–(22.7), (22.31)–(22.34) · Schroeder §5.1, eqs. (5.18)–(5.25), Problem 5.23(a) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, eqs. (22)–(24), (41)–(42), (52)–(53), (102) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §7*

^thm-b3-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate each definition with the product rule, $d(pV) = p\,dV + V\,dp$, $d(TS) = T\,dS + S\,dT$, $d(\mu N) = \mu\,dN + N\,d\mu$, and insert the fundamental relation ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]]):
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> dH &= dU + p\,dV + V\,dp = T\,dS + V\,dp + \mu\,dN,\\
> dF &= dU - T\,dS - S\,dT = -S\,dT - p\,dV + \mu\,dN,\\
> dG &= dF + p\,dV + V\,dp = -S\,dT + V\,dp + \mu\,dN,\\
> d\Phi &= dF - \mu\,dN - N\,d\mu = -S\,dT - p\,dV - N\,d\mu .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> In each line the term that was added or subtracted cancels one term of $dU$ and replaces it by its mirror image: $-T\,dS$ is traded for $-S\,dT$, $-p\,dV$ for $+V\,dp$, $\mu\,dN$ for $-N\,d\mu$. The differential of each potential therefore contains only the differentials of its natural variables. A function $f(x, y, z)$ that is differentiable has $df = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy + f_z\,dz$ with unique coefficients ([[§6 Differentiability#^def-6-2|452 Def. §6.2]]), so comparing coefficients gives the partial derivatives. For example $dF = -S\,dT - p\,dV + \mu\,dN$ gives $(\partial F/\partial T)_{V,N} = -S$. As Schwartz stresses, the content of $dF = -S\,dT - \dots$ is precisely that no $dS$ appears: to know how $F$ changes one needs $dT$, $dV$, $dN$ and nothing else.

> [!remark] Remark: Each potential is a Legendre transform
> Passing from $U(S, V, N)$ to $F(T, V, N) = U - TS$ with $T = \partial U/\partial S$ replaces a variable by the slope conjugate to it: $F$ is the Legendre transform of $U$ with respect to $S$, in the thermodynamic convention $g = f - ux$ ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|Def. §B1.2.2]]), which keeps the sign of an energy. Mechanics uses the opposite overall sign when it turns the Lagrangian into the Hamiltonian ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-1|CM Def. §B7.1.1]]). Adding $pV$ transforms in $V$ (slope $-p$), subtracting $\mu N$ transforms in $N$ (slope $\mu$), and $G$ is the double transform in $S$ and $V$. The transform loses no information as long as the slope determines the variable ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]): $T = (\partial U/\partial S)_V$ is a strictly increasing function of $S$ exactly when $(\partial T/\partial S)_V = T/C_V > 0$ ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]). Then $U$ is recovered from $F$, by $S = -\partial F/\partial T$ and $U = F + TS$ (also [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]). Merely substituting $S = S(T, V)$ into $U$, by contrast, loses information ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^ex-b3-1-1|Example §B3.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §3 · Schroeder §5.1 (footnote on Legendre transformation)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.2: Gibbs–Helmholtz Equations
> The energy and the enthalpy are recovered from the free energies by
>
> $$
> U = F - T\Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{V,N} = -T^2\Bigl(\frac{\partial (F/T)}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{V,N}, \qquad
> H = G - T\Bigl(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{p,N} = -T^2\Bigl(\frac{\partial (G/T)}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{p,N} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.4, Example 16.1, eqs. (16.25)–(16.26) · PHY 306, Problem Set 5, Problem 1*

^thm-b3-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]], $S = -(\partial F/\partial T)_{V,N}$, so $U = F + TS = F - T(\partial F/\partial T)_{V,N}$. By the quotient rule,
>
> $$
> -T^2\,\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\Bigl(\frac FT\Bigr) = -T^2\Bigl(\frac1T\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial T} - \frac{F}{T^2}\Bigr) = F - T\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial T} ,
> $$
>
> all at fixed $V, N$. The same steps with $S = -(\partial G/\partial T)_{p,N}$ and $H = G + TS$ give the second equation.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.3: Enthalpy as Heat at Constant Pressure
> Let a system go between two equilibrium states at the same pressure $p$, against surroundings held at that pressure, doing no work other than expansion work against them. Then the heat it absorbs is
>
> $$
> Q = \Delta H .
> $$
>
> In particular, for reversible heating at constant $p$ and $N$, $đQ = dH = T\,dS$, and the heat capacities are derivatives of potentials:
>
> $$
> C_V = \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{V,N}, \qquad C_p = \Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{p,N} .
> $$
>
> So $H$ plays at constant pressure the role that $U$ plays at constant volume. A reaction with $\Delta H < 0$ releases heat (exothermic), one with $\Delta H > 0$ absorbs it (endothermic).
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.2, eqs. (16.10)–(16.12), Example 16.2 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §4, eqs. (43)–(44) · Schroeder §5.1*

^thm-b3-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Against surroundings at fixed pressure $p$ the system does work $W = p\,\Delta V$, whatever happens in between: the surroundings' boundary moves through $\Delta V$ under the constant pressure $p$ (the isobaric case of [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]]). By the first law ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]), $Q = \Delta U + p\,\Delta V$. Since the pressure is $p$ in both end states, $p\,\Delta V = \Delta(pV)$, so $Q = \Delta(U + pV) = \Delta H$. For a reversible infinitesimal step at constant $p$ and $N$, $đQ = T\,dS = dH$ by [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]. With the heat capacities $C_p = (đQ/dT)_p$ and $C_V = (đQ/dT)_V$ ([[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]; the same two formulas are [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]) this gives $C_p = (\partial H/\partial T)_{p,N}$, and at constant $V$, $đQ = dU$ gives $C_V = (\partial U/\partial T)_{V,N}$.

> [!remark] Remark: How much $pV$ matters
> $\Delta H - \Delta U = \Delta(pV)$ is small for solids and liquids and of order $(\Delta n_{\text{gas}})RT \approx 2.5\ \mathrm{kJ}$ per mole of gas produced at room temperature. When aragonite turns into calcite, $\Delta(pV)$ is about $0.1\%$ of $\Delta H$. In the ammonia synthesis $3\mathrm H_2 + \mathrm N_2 \to 2\mathrm{NH_3}$, which turns 4 moles of gas into 2, $\Delta(pV) = -4.9\ \mathrm{kJ}$ against $\Delta_r H^\circ = -91.9\ \mathrm{kJ}$, about $5\%$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §4.2*

## Minimum principles

> [!definition] Definition §B3.1.3: Availability
> Let a system be in contact with surroundings that act as a reservoir at fixed temperature $T_0$ and pressure $p_0$. Its **availability** is
>
> $$
> A = U + p_0 V - T_0 S .
> $$
>
> If the wall also lets particles through, from a reservoir at chemical potential $\mu_0$, the availability is $A = U + p_0 V - T_0 S - \mu_0 N$. Unlike $F$ and $G$, $A$ contains the surroundings' $T_0$, $p_0$, $\mu_0$, which are constants, not the system's own variables.
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.5, eqs. (16.32)–(16.33) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §9.2 · Schroeder Problem 5.23(b) (particle reservoir)*

^def-b3-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.4: Availability Never Increases; the Minimum Principles
> Let the system of [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^def-b3-1-3|Def. §B3.1.3]] exchange heat (and possibly volume and particles) with its surroundings and, in addition, deliver work $đW_{\text{other}}$ to an external mechanical device. Then in every change
>
> $$
> đW_{\text{other}} \le -dA ,
> $$
>
> with equality exactly for reversible changes. With no other work, $dA \le 0$: $A$ decreases until equilibrium is reached, where it is minimum. Depending on what is held fixed, this says:
>
> | Held fixed | $dA$ reduces to | In equilibrium |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | $U, V, N$ (isolated system) | $-T_0\,dS$ | $S$ is maximum |
> | $S, V, N$ | $dU$ | $U$ is minimum |
> | $S, p = p_0, N$ | $dH$ | $H$ is minimum |
> | $T = T_0, V, N$ | $dF$ | $F$ is minimum |
> | $T = T_0, p = p_0, N$ | $dG$ | $G$ is minimum |
> | $T = T_0, V, \mu = \mu_0$ | $d\Phi$ | $\Phi$ is minimum |
>
> The minimum is over the states the system can reach by internal changes while the constraints are held: moving an internal partition, passing particles between phases or regions, a chemical reaction.
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.5, eqs. (16.27)–(16.38) and summary · Schroeder §5.2, eqs. (5.27)–(5.30), Problem 5.23(b) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §3.1, §5 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §8.4, §9.2*

^thm-b3-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Treat the surroundings as a reservoir in internal equilibrium. By the fundamental relation applied to it, a change $dU_R$, $dV_R$, $dN_R$ changes its entropy by
>
> $$
> dS_R = \frac{dU_R + p_0\,dV_R - \mu_0\,dN_R}{T_0} ,
> $$
>
> with $T_0$, $p_0$, $\mu_0$ unchanged because the reservoir is large ([[§A3.4 Thermodynamic Processes#^mod-a3-4-2|Model §A3.4.2]]). The device receiving the other work is purely mechanical and carries no entropy. Energy, volume and particle number are conserved:
>
> $$
> dU_R = -dU - đW_{\text{other}}, \qquad dV_R = -dV, \qquad dN_R = -dN .
> $$
>
> System, reservoir and device together form an isolated system, whose entropy cannot decrease, and stays constant exactly in reversible changes ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-5|Theorem §A4.5.5]]; in general form [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]], from the Clausius inequality):
>
> $$
> 0 \le dS + dS_R = dS - \frac{dU + đW_{\text{other}} + p_0\,dV - \mu_0\,dN}{T_0} .
> $$
>
> Multiply by $T_0 > 0$ and rearrange: $đW_{\text{other}} \le -(dU + p_0\,dV - T_0\,dS - \mu_0\,dN) = -dA$, since $p_0$, $T_0$, $\mu_0$ are constants. (For a closed system $dN = 0$ and the $\mu_0$ term drops.)
>
> For the table, set $đW_{\text{other}} = 0$ and insert the constraints. With $U$, $V$, $N$ fixed, $dA = -T_0\,dS \le 0$, so $dS \ge 0$ (the entropy maximum of [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]]). With $S$, $V$, $N$ fixed, $dA = dU$. With $S$, $N$ fixed and $p = p_0$, $dA = dU + p_0\,dV = d(U + pV)$, since $dp = 0$. With $T = T_0$, $V$, $N$ fixed, $dF = dU - T\,dS - S\,dT = dU - T_0\,dS = dA$. With $T = T_0$ and $p = p_0$, $dG = dU + p\,dV + V\,dp - T\,dS - S\,dT = dU + p_0\,dV - T_0\,dS = dA$. With $T = T_0$, $V$ fixed and $\mu = \mu_0$, $d\Phi = dU - T\,dS - S\,dT - \mu\,dN - N\,d\mu = dU - T_0\,dS - \mu_0\,dN = dA$. In each case a function that can only decrease is minimum when nothing can change any more, which is equilibrium.

> [!remark] Remark: Energy minimisation is entropy maximisation
> Energy is conserved, so the familiar "systems seek the lowest energy" (a ball rolling to the bottom of a bowl and stopping) cannot be literal. What decreases is the free energy of the system, because
>
> $$
> -\frac{dF}{T} = dS + \Bigl(-\frac{dU}{T}\Bigr) = dS + dS_R \qquad (T, V \text{ fixed}),
> $$
>
> the entropy gained by the system plus the entropy the reservoir gains from the energy $-dU$ the system hands it. Minimising $F = U - TS$ is a compromise. At low $T$ an energy $-dU$ given to the reservoir buys a large entropy $-dU/T$, and low energy wins; at high $T$ the system's own entropy dominates. A free expansion of an ideal gas at constant $T$ has $\Delta U = 0$ and $\Delta F = -Nk_BT\ln(V_2/V_1) < 0$: it is driven by entropy alone. A spring and piston rubbing on air comes to rest at the minimum of its potential energy, because the piston has no entropy and $F = U$ for it, and the energy it loses goes into the air as heat. The same accounting with volume exchange explains why $U$ and $V$ "want" to decrease and $S$ to increase at fixed $T$ and $p$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §3.3–3.4, eqs. (34), (39) · Schroeder §5.2*

^rem-b3-1-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.5: Free Energy as Available Work
> Let a system exchange heat with a reservoir at temperature $T$ only, and go between equilibrium states at that temperature. Then the total work it does satisfies
>
> $$
> W \le -\Delta F .
> $$
>
> If the surroundings also hold the pressure at $p$, and the end states are at $p$, then the work it does in excess of the expansion work against the surroundings satisfies
>
> $$
> W_{\text{other}} \le -\Delta G .
> $$
>
> Equality holds exactly for reversible processes. So the decrease of $F$ (at constant $T$) or of $G$ (at constant $T$ and $p$) is the maximum work that can be obtained, and an increase $\Delta F$ or $\Delta G$ is the minimum work that must be supplied. This is why they are called *free* energies.
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.5, eq. (16.28) · Schroeder §5.1, eqs. (5.4)–(5.9) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §3.1, eq. (26), §5*

^thm-b3-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For the first inequality, count all work as "other" and leave out the surroundings' pressure ($p_0 = 0$ in [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]), with $N$ fixed: $đW \le -(dU - T\,dS)$. Integrating at constant $T$, $W \le -(\Delta U - T\,\Delta S) = -\Delta F$, since the end states have the same temperature $T$. For the second, the total work done is $p\,\Delta V + W_{\text{other}}$, so $W_{\text{other}} \le -\Delta F - p\,\Delta V = -\Delta(F + pV) = -\Delta G$, using that the end states have the same $p$. Equality throughout requires equality in the second law, which holds exactly for reversible processes.

^der-b3-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^def-b3-1-2|Def. §B3.1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Which sign, and which work
> - Blundell and Schroeder count work done *on* the system, and state these results as $\Delta F \le W$ and $\Delta G \le W_{\text{other}}$. Here the work done *by* the system is bounded: $W \le -\Delta F$.
> - $F$ bounds *all* the work done at constant temperature, expansion work included; $G$ bounds only the work beyond the expansion work against a constant-pressure surrounding.
> - The user's Stat Mech concepts notes (§7.2, §9.1) write $dF = -p\,dV = đW_{\text{rev}}$ and conclude "$\Delta F$ = maximum useful (non-$PV$) work obtainable at fixed $T, V$". In that section's convention (§6.4: $đW = -p\,dV$ is work done *on* the system) the reversible result is $\Delta F = W_{\text{on}}$, so the work *obtainable* from the system is $-\Delta F$, the decrease of $F$. And at constant $T$ the bound covers expansion work as well; "non-$PV$" work is what $-\Delta G$ bounds, at constant $T$ and $p$.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.1.1: Knowing U(T, V) Is Not Enough
> Suppose calorimetry gives the energy of a system as a function $U(T, V)$ (at fixed $N$). Show that the pressure is fixed only up to an unknown function of $V$:
>
> $$
> \frac pT = \int \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T \frac{dT}{T^2} + f(V) .
> $$
>
> By the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]), $\partial(F/T)/\partial T = -U/T^2$ at fixed $V$. Integrating in $T$ at fixed $V$ gives $F/T = -\int U\,dT/T^2 + \varphi(V)$, with an integration "constant" $\varphi(V)$ that $U$ cannot fix. Then $p = -(\partial F/\partial V)_T$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]) gives
>
> $$
> \frac pT = \int \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T \frac{dT}{T^2} - \varphi'(V) ,
> $$
>
> which is the claim with $f = -\varphi'$. For a monatomic ideal gas $U = \tfrac32 Nk_BT$ has $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = 0$, and every equation of state $p = T f(V)$ is compatible with it: $U(T, V)$ cannot tell $p = Nk_BT/V$ from $p = Nk_BT/V^2$. Known as $U(S, V)$, instead, the same energy gives the pressure directly as $-(\partial U/\partial V)_S$. $U(T, V)$ is a perfectly good state function, but $T$ and $V$ are not its natural variables. PHY 306 reaches the same formula through the energy equation of [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]].
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 4, Problem 3 · Blundell Exercise 16.4*

> [!example] Example §B3.1.2: A Free Energy from Work and Entropy Data
> Measurements on a system of $N$ molecules give the work done in an isothermal expansion at $T_0$ from $V_0$ to $V$, $W = Nk_BT_0\ln(V/V_0)$, and the entropy $S = Nk_B\,(V_0/V)\,(T/T_0)^a$ with a constant $a > 0$. Find $F(V, T)$ and the equation of state.
>
> $dF = -S\,dT - p\,dV$ is exact, so $F$ may be integrated along any path ([[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]); take $(V_0, T_0) \to (V, T_0) \to (V, T)$. On the isotherm $dF = -p\,dV$, so $F(V, T_0) - F(V_0, T_0) = -W = -Nk_BT_0\ln(V/V_0)$; this is [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]] with equality, the measured expansion being reversible. At fixed $V$, $dF = -S\,dT$:
>
> $$
> F(V, T) - F(V, T_0) = -\int_{T_0}^{T} Nk_B\,\frac{V_0}{V}\Bigl(\frac{T'}{T_0}\Bigr)^a dT' = \frac{Nk_BT_0}{a + 1}\,\frac{V_0}{V}\Bigl[1 - \Bigl(\frac{T}{T_0}\Bigr)^{a+1}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Adding the two pieces,
>
> $$
> F(V, T) = Nk_BT_0\Bigl(-\ln\frac{V}{V_0} + \frac{V_0}{(a + 1)V}\Bigl[1 - \Bigl(\frac{T}{T_0}\Bigr)^{a+1}\Bigr]\Bigr) + F(V_0, T_0) .
> $$
>
> The equation of state follows by one differentiation, $p = -(\partial F/\partial V)_T$:
>
> $$
> pV = Nk_BT_0\Bigl(1 + \frac{V_0}{(a + 1)V}\Bigl[1 - \Bigl(\frac{T}{T_0}\Bigr)^{a+1}\Bigr]\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> which reduces to $pV = Nk_BT_0$ on the isotherm $T = T_0$, as the work data require. The two data sets are compatible: $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = -Nk_BV_0T^a/(V^2T_0^a)$ equals $(\partial p/\partial T)_V$ computed from this equation of state, as the Maxwell relation [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]] demands of any $F$.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 5, Problem 2*

> [!example] Example §B3.1.3: Electrolysis of Water and the Fuel Cell
> Split one mole of liquid water into hydrogen and oxygen gas at $298\ \mathrm K$ and $1\ \mathrm{bar}$: $\mathrm{H_2O} \to \mathrm H_2 + \tfrac12\mathrm O_2$. Tables give $\Delta H = 286\ \mathrm{kJ}$ and the molar entropies $S_{\mathrm{H_2O}} = 70\ \mathrm{J/K}$, $S_{\mathrm H_2} = 131\ \mathrm{J/K}$, $S_{\mathrm O_2} = 205\ \mathrm{J/K}$. How much of the energy must be supplied as electrical work?
>
> The entropy rises by $\Delta S = 131 + \tfrac12(205) - 70 = 163.5\ \mathrm{J/K}$, so at most $T\,\Delta S = (298\ \mathrm K)(163.5\ \mathrm{J/K}) = 49\ \mathrm{kJ}$ can enter as heat from the room. By [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]] the electrical work needed is at least
>
> $$
> \Delta G = \Delta H - T\,\Delta S = 286\ \mathrm{kJ} - 49\ \mathrm{kJ} = 237\ \mathrm{kJ} .
> $$
>
> Of $\Delta H$, a part $p\,\Delta V \approx \tfrac32 RT = 4\ \mathrm{kJ}$ pushes back the atmosphere to make room for 1.5 mol of gas, and $\Delta U = 282\ \mathrm{kJ}$ stays in the system. Each water molecule split moves two electrons through the circuit, so the minimum voltage is $\Delta G/(2N_Ae) = (237\times10^3\ \mathrm J)/(2 \times 96\,485\ \mathrm C) = 1.23\ \mathrm V$.
>
> Run backwards in a hydrogen fuel cell, the reaction delivers at most $237\ \mathrm{kJ}$ of electrical work per mole of $\mathrm H_2$ and must reject at least $49\ \mathrm{kJ}$ of heat to dispose of the excess entropy. Burning the hydrogen instead releases all $286\ \mathrm{kJ}$ as heat, of which a heat engine converts only a Carnot fraction ([[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]]). The ideal fuel cell's "efficiency" $237/286 = 83\%$ is not limited by Carnot's bound, because no heat is converted into work.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §5.1 (electrolysis, fuel cells), eqs. (5.10)–(5.12), Problem 5.4*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The potentials are successive Legendre transforms of $U$ ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|Def. §B1.2.2]]); the same transform takes the Lagrangian to the Hamiltonian in mechanics ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-1|CM Theorem §B7.1.1]]).
> - Reading partial derivatives off a differential uses the uniqueness of the total derivative ([[§6 Differentiability#^def-6-2|452 Def. §6.2]]); the second derivatives give the Maxwell relations of [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]].
> - Statistical mechanics computes the potentials directly: $F = -k_BT\ln Z$ ([[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]]), for which the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation becomes $U = -\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta$, and $\Phi = -k_BT\ln\mathcal Z$ ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-3|Theorem §B7.3.3]]).
> - The availability's minimum, $G$ in particular, decides phase equilibrium ([[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]]) and chemical equilibrium ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]).
