Level A computed work only for a gas, $W = \int p\,dV$ ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]). Nothing in the potentials of [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables]] or the Maxwell relations of [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions]] depends on the work being $p\,dV$. With the work written as a generalized force times a generalized displacement, the same machinery describes a stretched wire or rubber band, a liquid surface and a magnetic material. It answers questions such as: why does a rubber band warm when stretched? why is a small droplet compressed? and how does demagnetizing a paramagnetic salt cool it to millikelvin temperatures?

## Generalized work

> [!definition] Definition §B3.4.1: Generalized Force and Displacement
> For a system with one mechanical degree of freedom besides its thermal ones, the reversible work done *on* it is written
>
> $$
> đW_{\text{on}} = X\,dx ,
> $$
>
> with $X$ an intensive **generalized force** and $x$ an extensive **generalized displacement**. The fundamental relation ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]]) becomes $dU = T\,dS + X\,dx$ at fixed $N$. Examples:
>
> | System | $X$ | $x$ | $đW_{\text{on}}$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | fluid | $-p$ (pressure) | $V$ (volume) | $-p\,dV$ |
> | elastic rod or wire | $f$ (tension) | $L$ (length) | $f\,dL$ |
> | liquid surface or film | $\gamma$ (surface tension) | $A$ (area) | $\gamma\,dA$ |
> | magnetic sample in an applied field | $-m$ (magnetic moment) | $B$ (applied field) | $-m\,dB$ ([[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^def-b3-4-3\|Def. §B3.4.3]]) |
>
> For a fluid this is level A's $đW = p\,dV$ done *by* the system ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]]). (Blundell's table also lists a dielectric in a field $\vec E$, with $đW_{\text{on}} = -\vec p_E\cdot d\vec E$; in the magnetic row the roles are reversed, the "displacement" $B$ being intensive. The formulas below use only the form $X\,dx$.)
>
> *Source: Blundell ch. 17, eq. (17.2), Table 17.1*

^def-b3-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.4.1: Thermodynamics of a System with Work X dx
> Let $dU = T\,dS + X\,dx$. Then $F = U - TS$ has $dF = -S\,dT + X\,dx$, and
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial x}\Bigr)_T = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial X}{\partial T}\Bigr)_x, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\Bigr)_T = X - T\Bigl(\frac{\partial X}{\partial T}\Bigr)_x, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\Bigr)_S = \frac{T}{C_x}\Bigl(\frac{\partial X}{\partial T}\Bigr)_x ,
> $$
>
> with $C_x = T(\partial S/\partial T)_x$ the heat capacity at constant $x$. A reversible isothermal change of $x$ absorbs the heat
>
> $$
> Q = T\,\Delta S = -T\int \Bigl(\frac{\partial X}{\partial T}\Bigr)_x dx .
> $$
>
> For a fluid ($X = -p$, $x = V$) these are the Maxwell relation $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial p/\partial T)_V$, the energy equation and the adiabatic temperature change of [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]] and [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-5|Theorem §B3.2.5]].
>
> *Source: Blundell §17.1–17.4, eqs. (17.7)–(17.14), (17.21)–(17.24), (17.34)–(17.38)*

^thm-b3-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $dF = dU - T\,dS - S\,dT = -S\,dT + X\,dx$, so $S = -(\partial F/\partial T)_x$ and $X = (\partial F/\partial x)_T$. Equality of the mixed second derivatives of $F$ ([[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]) gives $-(\partial S/\partial x)_T = (\partial X/\partial T)_x$, as in [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]]. Dividing $dU = T\,dS + X\,dx$ by $dx$ at constant $T$ gives $(\partial U/\partial x)_T = T(\partial S/\partial x)_T + X$; insert the Maxwell relation. The triple-product rule ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]]) gives
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\Bigr)_S = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial T}{\partial S}\Bigr)_x\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial x}\Bigr)_T = -\frac{T}{C_x}\Bigl(-\Bigl(\frac{\partial X}{\partial T}\Bigr)_x\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Finally, in a reversible isothermal change $đQ = T\,dS = T(\partial S/\partial x)_T\,dx$; integrate at fixed $T$.

## The elastic rod

> [!definition] Definition §B3.4.2: Isothermal Young's Modulus; Expansivity at Constant Tension
> For a rod or wire of length $L$ and cross-section $A$ under tension $f$, the **isothermal Young's modulus** and the **linear expansivity at constant tension** are
>
> $$
> E_T = \frac{L}{A}\Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial L}\Bigr)_T, \qquad \alpha_f = \frac1L\Bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial T}\Bigr)_f .
> $$
>
> $E_T$ is Young's modulus, stress over strain ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-2|CM Def. §A7.2.2]]), measured at constant temperature, and is always positive. $\alpha_f$ is level A's coefficient of linear expansion ([[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^law-a1-2-1|Law §A1.2.1]]) with the tension held fixed. It is positive for metals (a loaded wire lengthens when heated) and negative for rubber (a loaded rubber band shortens when heated).
>
> *Source: Blundell §17.1, eqs. (17.3)–(17.4)*

^def-b3-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.4.2: Thermodynamics of an Elastic Rod
> With $dU = T\,dS + f\,dL$ and the cross-section $A$ treated as constant,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial T}\Bigr)_L = -AE_T\alpha_f, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial L}\Bigr)_T = AE_T\alpha_f, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial L}\Bigr)_T = f + ATE_T\alpha_f, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\partial T}{\partial L}\Bigr)_S = -\frac{ATE_T\alpha_f}{C_L} .
> $$
>
> So a metal wire ($\alpha_f > 0$) absorbs heat $Q = ATE_T\alpha_f\,\Delta L$ when stretched isothermally and cools when stretched adiabatically; a rubber band ($\alpha_f < 0$) gives off heat when stretched isothermally and warms when stretched adiabatically.
>
> *Source: Blundell §17.1, Examples 17.1–17.2, eqs. (17.5)–(17.14), Exercises 17.1–17.2*

^thm-b3-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $f$, $L$, $T$ are related by an equation of state, so by the triple-product rule ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]]) and [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^def-b3-4-2|Def. §B3.4.2]],
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial T}\Bigr)_L = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial L}\Bigr)_T\Bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial T}\Bigr)_f = -\frac{AE_T}{L}\,L\alpha_f = -AE_T\alpha_f .
> $$
>
> The other three are [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]] with $X = f$, $x = L$. The heat is $Q = T(\partial S/\partial L)_T\,\Delta L$ for a small stretch, and $E_T > 0$, $T > 0$, $C_L > 0$, so all the signs are those of $\alpha_f$.

^der-b3-4-2

*Uses:* [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^def-b3-4-2|Def. §B3.4.2]], [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]], [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Thermal stress and the meaning of the signs
> - Level A's thermal stress in a clamped rod, $F/A = Y\alpha\,\Delta T$, compressive on heating ([[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^thm-a1-2-4|Theorem §A1.2.4]]), is the first formula integrated over a small $\Delta T$: at fixed $L$ the tension changes by $\Delta f = -AE_T\alpha_f\,\Delta T$. The level-A derivation (free expansion, then elastic compression back) is the triple-product rule spelt out in two steps. A string held at constant length on a guitar slackens on a hot day for the same reason.
> - The sign of $(\partial S/\partial L)_T$ says what stretching does microscopically. In a metal it distorts the crystallites and increases the volume per atom, raising the entropy, so heat flows in. In rubber it uncoils long-chain molecules, lowering their entropy, so heat flows out ([[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^ex-b3-4-1|Example §B3.4.1]]). The tension of rubber is mainly *entropic*.
>
> *Source: Blundell §17.1 (margin note to Example 17.1; Fig. 17.2)*

^rem-b3-4-1

## Liquid surfaces

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.4.3: Thermodynamics of a Liquid Surface
> For a liquid surface of area $A$, with surface tension $\gamma(T)$ (the work per unit area needed to create new surface), $dU = T\,dS + \gamma\,dA$ and
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial A}\Bigr)_T = -\frac{d\gamma}{dT}, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial A}\Bigr)_T = \gamma - T\,\frac{d\gamma}{dT} .
> $$
>
> Since $\gamma$ decreases as $T$ rises, $d\gamma/dT < 0$ ($\gamma$ must vanish at the critical point, where liquid and vapour become indistinguishable, [[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^def-a1-4-2|Def. §A1.4.2]]; Blundell's Fig. 17.5 places the zero at the boiling temperature, which is not right: a liquid at its normal boiling point still has a finite surface tension): stretching a surface isothermally absorbs the heat $Q = -T(d\gamma/dT)\,\Delta A > 0$, a surface carries entropy as well as energy, and its energy per unit area exceeds $\gamma$. At fixed $T$, $F = \gamma A + \text{const}$: $\gamma$ is the *free* energy per unit area.
>
> *Source: Blundell §17.2, eqs. (17.15), (17.20)–(17.25), Exercise 17.4*

^thm-b3-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> This is [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]] with $X = \gamma$ and $x = A$. For a pure liquid, $\gamma$ depends on $T$ only, not on how much surface there is, so $(\partial\gamma/\partial T)_A = d\gamma/dT$, and $dF = -S\,dT + \gamma\,dA$ integrates at fixed $T$ to $F = \gamma A + \text{const}$.

^der-b3-4-3

*Uses:* [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.4.4: Pressure Inside a Drop and a Bubble
> In equilibrium, the pressure inside a spherical liquid drop of radius $r$ exceeds the pressure outside by
>
> $$
> \Delta p = \frac{2\gamma}{r} ,
> $$
>
> and the gas inside a thin-walled bubble (a film with an inner and an outer surface, as in a soap bubble) is at an excess pressure
>
> $$
> \Delta p = \frac{4\gamma}{r} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Blundell §17.2, eqs. (17.16)–(17.19), Example 17.3*

^thm-b3-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Hold everything at temperature $T$, with the total volume of drop and surroundings fixed; in equilibrium the total free energy is minimum ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]). Let the radius change by $dr$. The drop's volume grows by $dV = 4\pi r^2\,dr$, the surroundings' shrinks by the same amount, and the surface grows by $dA = 4\pi(r + dr)^2 - 4\pi r^2 = 8\pi r\,dr$ to first order. With $dF = -p\,dV$ for each bulk part at fixed $T$ and $dF = \gamma\,dA$ for the surface,
>
> $$
> dF = -p_{\text{in}}\,dV + p_{\text{out}}\,dV + \gamma\,dA = \bigl[-(p_{\text{in}} - p_{\text{out}})\,4\pi r^2 + 8\pi\gamma r\bigr]dr = 0
> $$
>
> for all $dr$, so $p_{\text{in}} - p_{\text{out}} = 2\gamma/r$. Blundell reaches the same result by equating the work $p\,dV$ pushed into the drop through a pipe with the work $\gamma\,dA$ stored in its surface. A thin bubble has two surfaces of nearly equal radius, so $dA$ doubles and $\Delta p = 4\gamma/r$.

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^thm-b3-4-3|Theorem §B3.4.3]], [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]

## Magnetic systems

> [!definition] Definition §B3.4.3: Magnetic Work; Choice of Energy
> A sample of volume $V$ with total magnetic moment $m$ (magnetization $M = m/V$; [[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^def-a8-6-2|EM Def. §A8.6.2]]) sits in an applied field $B = \mu_0H$, produced by a solenoid whose current fixes $H$; moments and fields point along the solenoid's axis. Two conventions for "the energy of the system" are in use:
> - **Blundell's** (used here): the system is the sample together with its interaction energy $-mB$ with the applied field ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-3|EM Theorem §A7.4.3]]). The reversible work done on it is $đW_{\text{on}} = -m\,dB$, and
>
> $$
> dU = T\,dS - m\,dB .
> $$
>
> - **Schroeder's** (Problem 5.17): the interaction is counted as work done by the power supply, and the field energy of the empty solenoid is subtracted. The work on the sample is then $\mu_0H\,dM_{\text{tot}}$, which is $B\,dm$ in this notation, and its energy $U^* = U + mB$ obeys $dU^* = T\,dS + B\,dm$.
>
> The two energies differ by the Legendre transform $mB$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^rem-b3-1-3|Remark: Each potential is a Legendre transform]]), and the physics is the same. In both, the function with natural variables $T$ and $B$ is $F = U - TS = U^* - TS - mB$, with
>
> $$
> dF = -S\,dT - m\,dB .
> $$
>
> The **susceptibility** is $\chi = \lim_{H\to0} M/H$. For a paramagnet $\chi \ll 1$, so the field inside the sample is close to the applied one and $\chi \approx \mu_0M/B$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §16.5 (margin note 6), §17.3–17.4, eqs. (17.28)–(17.31) · Schroeder Problem 5.17*

^def-b3-4-3

> [!caution] Caution: Which energy?
> With Blundell's convention, the "internal energy" of an ideal paramagnet is entirely the interaction energy, $U = -mB$; this is the energy $U = \mu B(N_\downarrow - N_\uparrow)$ of Schroeder's two-state paramagnet (§3.3), whose thermodynamic identity is written at fixed $B$. With Schroeder's Problem 5.17 convention the same system has $U^* = 0$. Formulas taken from different books mix the two only at the cost of sign errors. The field energy of the solenoid itself is in neither. Here $m$ is the *total* moment, not the magnetization $M = m/V$; Schroeder writes $M$ for the total moment.

^cau-b3-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Curie's law, in the language of this section
> For a paramagnet in a weak field, the moment is proportional to $B/T$: Curie's law, an empirical law at the level of thermodynamics ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^law-b6-2-10|Law §B6.2.10]]), derived from the Boltzmann distribution of independent moments in [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]]. In terms of the susceptibility of [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^def-b3-4-3|Def. §B3.4.3]] it reads $\chi = C/T$, with the Curie constant $C$ a property of the material, so
>
> $$
> m \approx c\,\frac BT, \qquad c = \frac{CV}{\mu_0}, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\partial m}{\partial T}\Bigr)_B = -\frac mT < 0 .
> $$
>
> It holds while $\mu B \ll k_BT$ and the moments do not interact. At low temperature or in strong fields the moment saturates at $N\mu$ (for two-state moments $m = N\mu\tanh(\mu B/k_BT)$); Schroeder's data on the free radical DPPH follow Curie's law down to a few kelvin and then bend over to the $\tanh$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §17.4, eqs. (17.30)–(17.33) · Schroeder §3.3, eq. (3.35), Fig. 3.12*

^rem-b3-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.4.5: The Magnetocaloric Effect
> For a magnetic system with $dF = -S\,dT - m\,dB$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial B}\Bigr)_T = \Bigl(\frac{\partial m}{\partial T}\Bigr)_B, \qquad
> Q_{\text{isothermal}} = T\int\Bigl(\frac{\partial m}{\partial T}\Bigr)_B dB, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial T}{\partial B}\Bigr)_S = -\frac{T}{C_B}\Bigl(\frac{\partial m}{\partial T}\Bigr)_B ,
> $$
>
> with $C_B = T(\partial S/\partial T)_B$ the heat capacity at constant applied field. For a paramagnet $(\partial m/\partial T)_B < 0$ ([[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^rem-b3-4-2|Remark: Curie's law]]), so
> - **isothermal magnetization** (raising $B$ at constant $T$) lowers the entropy and *releases* heat; in the Curie regime, $m = cB/T$ with $c = CV/\mu_0$, raising the field from $0$ to $B$ releases $|Q| = cB^2/2T$;
> - **adiabatic demagnetization** (lowering $B$ at constant $S$) *lowers* the temperature, since $(\partial T/\partial B)_S > 0$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §17.4, Example 17.4, eqs. (17.34)–(17.38)*

^thm-b3-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> This is [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]] with $X = -m$ and $x = B$ ([[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^def-b3-4-3|Def. §B3.4.3]]): $(\partial S/\partial B)_T = -(\partial(-m)/\partial T)_B$, $Q = T\int(\partial S/\partial B)_T\,dB$, and $(\partial T/\partial B)_S = (T/C_B)(\partial(-m)/\partial T)_B$. With Curie's law $m = cB/T$ ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^law-b6-2-10|Law §B6.2.10]]), $(\partial m/\partial T)_B = -cB/T^2$, so $Q = T\int_0^B(-cB'/T^2)\,dB' = -cB^2/2T$, and $(\partial T/\partial B)_S = cB/(C_BT) > 0$.

^der-b3-4-5

*Uses:* [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]], [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^def-b3-4-3|Def. §B3.4.3]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^law-b6-2-10|Law §B6.2.10]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.4.6: Adiabatic Demagnetization of an Ideal Paramagnet
> Consider the ideal two-state paramagnet ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^mod-b6-1-3|Model §B6.1.3]]): $N$ non-interacting moments, each with component $\pm\mu$ along the field, so that each has energy $\mp\mu B$. Its entropy and magnetic moment are
>
> $$
> S = Nk_B\bigl[\ln(2\cosh x) - x\tanh x\bigr], \qquad m = N\mu\tanh x, \qquad x = \frac{\mu B}{k_BT} .
> $$
>
> They depend on $B$ and $T$ only through $B/T$. Hence in a reversible adiabatic change of the field, with other contributions to the entropy negligible,
>
> $$
> \frac{B}{T} = \text{const}, \qquad T_f = T_i\,\frac{B_f}{B_i} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.3, eqs. (3.31)–(3.32), Problem 3.23 · Blundell §17.4 (Figs. 17.7–17.9)*

^thm-b3-4-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The magnetic moment is that of [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-9|Theorem §B6.2.9]] (or [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]]). It fixes the fractions of moments up and down, $p_\pm = N_{\uparrow,\downarrow}/N = \tfrac12(1 \pm \tanh x) = e^{\pm x}/(2\cosh x)$. The entropy of Theorem §B6.2.9, $S/k_B = N\ln N - N_\uparrow\ln N_\uparrow - N_\downarrow\ln N_\downarrow$, is $-Nk_B(p_+\ln p_+ + p_-\ln p_-)$, and with $\ln p_\pm = \pm x - \ln(2\cosh x)$,
>
> $$
> \frac{S}{Nk_B} = \ln(2\cosh x) - x\,(p_+ - p_-) = \ln(2\cosh x) - x\tanh x .
> $$
>
> So $S$ is a function of $x$ alone, and it is strictly decreasing for $x > 0$:
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln(2\cosh x) - x\tanh x\bigr] = \tanh x - \tanh x - \frac{x}{\cosh^2 x} = -\frac{x}{\cosh^2x} < 0 .
> $$
>
> So constant $S$ means constant $x = \mu B/k_BT$, that is constant $B/T$. Blundell's picture of the energy levels says the same: in an adiabatic change no transitions occur, so the populations of the levels $\mp\mu B$ stay fixed, and the Boltzmann ratio $e^{-2\mu B/k_BT}$ ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]]) of those fixed populations can only stay fixed if $B/T$ does.

![[ph-th-b3-4-1.svg]]
*Entropy of an ideal two-state paramagnet, $S/Nk_B = \ln(2\cosh x) - x\tanh x$ with $x = \mu B/k_BT$, against temperature (logarithmic scale), in a strong field $B_1$ and in a field ten times weaker. Magnetic cooling: isothermal magnetization $a \to b$ at $T_i$ (the entropy falls and heat flows into the bath), then adiabatic demagnetization $b \to c$ at constant $S$, which lowers $T$ by the factor $10$. Each curve rises to $\ln 2$ at high $T$; the weak-field curve is the strong-field curve shifted ten times lower in temperature.*

> [!remark] Remark: How cold, and why not to absolute zero
> - In practice the salt is first cooled with liquid helium ($4.2\ \mathrm K$, lower under reduced pressure), magnetized in contact with the bath through exchange gas, then isolated by pumping the gas away, and the field is lowered slowly so that the process is quasi-static. During demagnetization the spins' entropy rises as they disorder; at constant total entropy this is paid for by the lattice vibrations, whose entropy (about $\propto T^3$) falls: the sample cools.
> - With $B_f \to 0$, Theorem §B3.4.6 would give $T_f \to 0$. Real salts have a small residual internal field $B_r$ from the interactions between moments, which orders them and removes their entropy just above absolute zero, and the final temperature is roughly $T_iB_r/B_i$. Electronic paramagnets reach a few millikelvin, nuclear ones (moments about 2000 times smaller) microkelvin. That the entropy reaches zero before $T$ does is the third law at work ([[§B2.3 The Third Law#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]]); Curie's law must fail as $T \to 0$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §17.4 · Schroeder §3.3*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.4.1: A Rubber Band as an Entropic Spring
> Model a rubber molecule as a chain of $N$ links of length $a$, each pointing either along $+x$ or along $-x$, with no energy depending on the directions. Find the tension at length $L$ and its thermal consequences.
>
> With $N_+$ links pointing forward and $N_- = N - N_+$ back, $L = a(N_+ - N_-)$, and the number of arrangements is $\Omega(L) = N!/(N_+!\,N_-!)$. By Boltzmann's formula ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-1|Principle §A4.6.1]]) and Stirling's approximation ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]), with $N_\pm = \tfrac12(N \pm L/a)$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial L}\Bigr)_T = \frac{k_B}{2a}\ln\frac{N_-}{N_+} = -\frac{k_B}{2a}\ln\frac{Na + L}{Na - L}, \qquad S \approx Nk_B\Bigl[\ln 2 - \frac{L^2}{2N^2a^2}\Bigr] \quad (L \ll Na) .
> $$
>
> The energy of the chain does not depend on $L$, so $f = (\partial F/\partial L)_T = -T(\partial S/\partial L)_T$:
>
> $$
> f = \frac{k_BT}{2a}\ln\frac{Na + L}{Na - L} \approx \frac{k_BT}{Na^2}\,L .
> $$
>
> For small extensions this is Hooke's law, with a stiffness proportional to $T$ and of purely entropic origin: the chain resists stretching because straight configurations are few. Consequences, by [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]] with $X = f$, $x = L$:
> - $(\partial f/\partial T)_L = f/T$, so $(\partial U/\partial L)_T = f - T(\partial f/\partial T)_L = 0$: $U$ depends on $T$ alone, and all the work done in an isothermal stretch leaves as heat, $Q = T\Delta S = -k_BTL^2/2Na^2 < 0$.
> - At constant tension $L \propto 1/T$, so $\alpha_f = -1/T < 0$: a weighted rubber band contracts when warmed and lifts the weight.
> - $(\partial T/\partial L)_S = (T/C_L)(f/T) = f/C_L > 0$: a band stretched quickly warms (check it against your lips), and one held stretched and suddenly released cools. Here $C_L$ comes from the molecular vibrations, whose entropy depends on $T$ but not on $L$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder Problem 3.34 · Blundell Exercises 17.2, 17.3, 17.6*

> [!example] Example §B3.4.2: Droplets, and the Energy of a Surface Molecule
> Water at $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ has $\gamma = 72\ \mathrm{mJ/m^2}$. (a) What is the excess pressure in a droplet of radius $1\ \mu\mathrm m$? (b) Estimate the surface energy per surface molecule and compare it with the latent heat of vaporization per molecule ($4.4\times10^4\ \mathrm{J/mol}$).
>
> (a) By [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^thm-b3-4-4|Theorem §B3.4.4]], $\Delta p = 2\gamma/r = 2(0.072\ \mathrm{N/m})/(10^{-6}\ \mathrm m) = 1.4\times10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$, about $1.4\ \mathrm{atm}$; the excess grows as $1/r$ for smaller drops.
>
> (b) The number density of water molecules is $\rho N_A/M = (10^3\ \mathrm{kg/m^3})(6.02\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}})/(0.018\ \mathrm{kg/mol}) = 3.3\times10^{28}\ \mathrm m^{-3}$, so a surface holds about $(3.3\times10^{28})^{2/3} = 1.0\times10^{19}$ molecules per $\mathrm m^2$. The surface free energy per molecule is
>
> $$
> \frac{0.072\ \mathrm{J/m^2}}{1.0\times10^{19}\ \mathrm m^{-2}} = 6.9\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm J = 0.043\ \mathrm{eV},
> $$
>
> against a latent heat of $(4.4\times10^4\ \mathrm{J/mol})/N_A = 7.3\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm J = 0.46\ \mathrm{eV}$ per molecule, about ten times more. Evaporating a molecule breaks all its bonds to its neighbours; a molecule at the surface has lost only a fraction of them. Both energies measure the same intermolecular attraction, which is why liquids with strong cohesion have both a large $\gamma$ and a large latent heat.
>
> *Source: Blundell §17.2, eq. (17.18), Exercise 17.5*

> [!example] Example §B3.4.3: Cooling a Paramagnetic Salt
> An electronic two-state paramagnet ($\mu = \mu_B = 5.79\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/T}$) is held at $T_i = 1.0\ \mathrm K$ by a pumped helium bath and magnetized from nearly zero field to $B_i = 2.0\ \mathrm T$; then it is isolated and the field is lowered to the residual internal field, $B_r \approx 0.01\ \mathrm T$. Find the heat given to the bath per mole of moments and the final temperature, neglecting the lattice entropy.
>
> At the start, $x \approx 0$ and $S = Nk_B\ln 2 = 0.693\,Nk_B$. At $B_i$, $x_i = \mu B_i/k_BT_i = (5.79\times10^{-5})(2.0)/(8.62\times10^{-5}) = 1.34$, and by [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^thm-b3-4-6|Theorem §B3.4.6]]
>
> $$
> \frac{S}{Nk_B} = \ln(2\cosh 1.34) - 1.34\tanh 1.34 = 1.409 - 1.172 = 0.237 .
> $$
>
> The isothermal step releases $Q = T_i\,\Delta S = (1.0\ \mathrm K)(0.693 - 0.237)(8.31\ \mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}) = 3.8\ \mathrm J$ per mole into the bath, in agreement with the sign of [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^thm-b3-4-5|Theorem §B3.4.5]]. In the adiabatic step $B/T$ stays fixed, so
>
> $$
> T_f = T_i\,\frac{B_r}{B_i} = (1.0\ \mathrm K)\,\frac{0.01\ \mathrm T}{2.0\ \mathrm T} = 5\ \mathrm{mK} .
> $$
>
> The final state has the same $x = 1.34$ as state $b$: the moments are as well aligned with the weak residual field at $5\ \mathrm{mK}$ as they were with $2\ \mathrm T$ at $1\ \mathrm K$. A weaker field $B_i$ would remove less entropy in the first step and, by $T_f = T_iB_r/B_i$, end warmer. Treating $B_r$ like an applied field makes $T_f$ an estimate.
>
> *Source: Blundell §17.4 (procedure, Fig. 17.8) · Schroeder §3.3, eq. (3.34) ($\mu_B$), Problem 3.23*
