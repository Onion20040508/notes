How much of the heat taken from a hot source can be turned into work? The question is about devices that run in cycles, heat engines, and their reversed versions, refrigerators and heat pumps. This section sets up their bookkeeping with the first law ([[§A3.3 The First Law|§A3.3]]); the limits the second law puts on them follow in [[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law|§A4.3]] and [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle|§A4.4]].

## Heat engines

> [!model] Model §A4.2.1: Heat Reservoir
> A **heat reservoir** is a body so large that it can give or take any amount of heat without its temperature changing. A **hot reservoir** (heat source) is kept at $T_h$, a **cold reservoir** (heat sink) at $T_c < T_h$. The furnace or reactor of a power plant plays the hot reservoir, the atmosphere or a river the cold one.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.2*

^mod-a4-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §A4.2.1: Heat Engine
> A **heat engine** is a device that carries a **working substance** through a **cycle**, a sequence of processes that returns it to its initial state. In each cycle it absorbs heat $Q_h$ from a hot reservoir at $T_h$, delivers work $W$ to its surroundings, and discards heat $Q_c$ to a cold reservoir at $T_c$.
>
> Steam power plants (working substance water, boiled, sent through a turbine and condensed again) and internal combustion engines are examples.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.2*

^def-a4-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: In this chapter heats and work are magnitudes
> For engines, refrigerators and heat pumps, $Q_h$, $Q_c$ and $W$ denote **amounts**, all positive. Whether heat enters or leaves and whether work is done by or on the substance is shown by the arrows in the energy-flow diagram and by the signs written in front of the symbols. This differs from the sign convention of the first law, $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q - W$ ([[§A3.3 The First Law|§A3.3]]), where $Q$ and $W$ are signed. From [[§A4.5 Entropy|§A4.5]] on, $Q$ in $\Delta S = Q/T$ is again signed.

^cau-a4-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §A4.2.2: Efficiency
> The **efficiency** of a heat engine is what one gets out divided by what one pays for, per cycle:
>
> $$
> e = \frac{W}{Q_h} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.2, eq. (4.2)*

^def-a4-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.2.2: Energy Balance of a Heat Engine
> Over one cycle,
>
> $$
> W = Q_h - Q_c, \qquad\text{so}\qquad e = 1 - \frac{Q_c}{Q_h} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.2, eqs. (4.1)–(4.2)*

^thm-a4-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Internal energy is a state function, and after a full cycle the working substance is back in its initial state, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$. The signed heat absorbed in the cycle is $Q_h - Q_c$ and the signed work done by the substance is $W$, so the first law gives $0 = (Q_h - Q_c) - W$. Divide by $Q_h$ for the efficiency. Nothing here assumes the cycle is reversible.

^der-a4-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 The First Law|§A3.3]], [[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^def-a4-2-1|Def. §A4.2.1]], [[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^def-a4-2-2|Def. §A4.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Net work is the area of the loop
> If the cycle is quasi-static, it is a closed curve in the $pV$ plane and the net work done by the substance is $W = \oint p\,dV$ ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy|§A3.2]]). This is the area enclosed by the loop, counted positive when the loop runs clockwise (expansion at high pressure, compression at low pressure). That is how an engine cycle looks; a refrigerator runs the loop counterclockwise and needs net work done on it.

^rem-a4-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why a cold reservoir?
> By the energy balance, $e = 1$ would need $Q_c = 0$: all the heat drawn from the hot reservoir would become work. The first law allows this. The second law forbids it (Kelvin–Planck statement, [[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law|§A4.3]]), so every engine needs a cold reservoir to dump heat into and $e < 1$. How large $e$ can be for given $T_h$ and $T_c$ is the subject of [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle|§A4.4]].

^rem-a4-2-2

## Refrigerators and heat pumps

> [!definition] Definition §A4.2.3: Refrigerator; Heat Pump
> A **refrigerator** or **heat pump** is a heat engine run in reverse: in each cycle work $W$ is done on the working substance, which absorbs heat $Q_c$ from a cold reservoir at $T_c$ and discards heat $Q_h$ to a hot reservoir at $T_h$. The device is called a refrigerator when the purpose is to remove heat from the cold side (food, the inside of a building), and a heat pump when the purpose is to deliver heat to the hot side (a house in winter).
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.3*

^def-a4-2-3

> [!definition] Definition §A4.2.4: Coefficients of Performance
> The **coefficient of performance** of a refrigerator is the heat removed from the cold reservoir per unit work, and that of a heat pump is the heat delivered to the hot reservoir per unit work:
>
> $$
> K_R = \frac{Q_c}{W}, \qquad K_P = \frac{Q_h}{W} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.3, eqs. (4.3)–(4.4)*

^def-a4-2-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.2.3: Coefficients of Performance from the Heats
> Over one cycle $W = Q_h - Q_c$, so
>
> $$
> K_R = \frac{Q_c}{Q_h - Q_c}, \qquad K_P = \frac{Q_h}{Q_h - Q_c}, \qquad K_P = K_R + 1 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.3, eqs. (4.3)–(4.4)*

^thm-a4-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> As in [[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]], $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$ over a cycle. Now heat $Q_c$ enters, heat $Q_h$ leaves and work $W$ is done on the substance, so the first law reads $0 = (Q_c - Q_h) - (-W)$, i.e. $W = Q_h - Q_c$. Substitute into the definitions. Finally $K_P - K_R = (Q_h - Q_c)/(Q_h - Q_c) = 1$.

^der-a4-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 The First Law|§A3.3]], [[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^def-a4-2-3|Def. §A4.2.3]], [[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^def-a4-2-4|Def. §A4.2.4]]

![[ph-th-a4-2-1.svg]]
*Energy flow per cycle. Left: a heat engine takes $Q_h$ from the hot reservoir, delivers work $W$ (red) and discards $Q_c$; arrow widths are drawn to the energy balance $Q_h = W + Q_c$. Right: a refrigerator or heat pump receives work $W$ (red), takes $Q_c$ from the cold reservoir and delivers $Q_h = Q_c + W$ to the hot one.*

> [!remark] Remark: A coefficient of performance above 1 is normal
> Unlike an efficiency, $K_R$ and $K_P$ are usually larger than 1, and $K_P > 1$ always. Nothing is created: the work only *moves* heat that already exists in the cold reservoir. Resistive heating turns one joule of electrical energy into one joule of heat; a heat pump delivers $K_P$ joules per joule of work, which is why it is the cheaper way to heat a house. In a household refrigerator the work is done by the compressor motor: a coolant that boils below $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ evaporates inside the cabinet, absorbing $Q_c$, is compressed, and condenses in the coils outside, releasing $Q_h$.

^rem-a4-2-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.2.1: A Lawn Mower's Waste Heat
> A lawn mower engine has efficiency $e = 0.250$ and average power $P = 3.00\ \mathrm{kW}$. In one minute of use, the work it delivers is
>
> $$
> W = P\,\Delta t = (3.00 \times 10^3\ \mathrm W)(60.0\ \mathrm s) = 180\ \mathrm{kJ} .
> $$
>
> The heat it must draw from burning fuel is $Q_h = W/e = 720\ \mathrm{kJ}$, and by the energy balance it discards
>
> $$
> Q_c = Q_h - W = W\Bigl(\frac1e - 1\Bigr) = (180\ \mathrm{kJ})(4.00 - 1) = 540\ \mathrm{kJ}
> $$
>
> into the air. Three quarters of the fuel's energy leaves as waste heat; a higher efficiency would reduce it.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 4.1*

^ex-a4-2-1

> [!example] Example §A4.2.2: The Otto Cycle
> The **Otto cycle** idealises a gasoline engine as a closed cycle of an ideal gas ($n$ moles, $\gamma = C_p/C_V$) between volumes $V$ and $rV$, where $r$ is the **compression ratio**:
> - $a \to b$: adiabatic compression from $rV$ to $V$ (compression stroke);
> - $b \to c$: heating at constant volume $V$ (ignition), heat $Q_h = nC_V(T_c - T_b)$ absorbed;
> - $c \to d$: adiabatic expansion from $V$ to $rV$ (power stroke);
> - $d \to a$: cooling at constant volume $rV$ (exhaust), heat $Q_c = nC_V(T_d - T_a)$ discarded.
>
> No heat flows on the adiabats. By the energy balance,
>
> $$
> e = 1 - \frac{Q_c}{Q_h} = 1 - \frac{T_d - T_a}{T_c - T_b} .
> $$
>
> On each adiabat $TV^{\gamma - 1}$ is constant ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes|§A3.6]]): $T_b V^{\gamma-1} = T_a (rV)^{\gamma-1}$ and $T_c V^{\gamma-1} = T_d (rV)^{\gamma-1}$, so $T_b = r^{\gamma-1}T_a$ and $T_c = r^{\gamma-1}T_d$. Hence $T_c - T_b = r^{\gamma-1}(T_d - T_a)$ and
>
> $$
> e = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\gamma - 1}} .
> $$
>
> For $r = 8$ and air ($\gamma = 1.4$), $e = 1 - 8^{-0.4} = 0.56$. Real gasoline engines reach about $35\,\%$: friction, heat loss through the cylinder walls and a combustion that is far from quasi-static all lower it. The real engine is also not a closed cycle: it takes in fresh mixture and exhausts burnt gas, which the model replaces by the two constant-volume steps. The efficiency rises with the compression ratio.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 6 and week 6 slides (Oct 13)*

^ex-a4-2-2

*The Otto cycle on a $pV$ diagram (drawn to scale for $r = 3$, $\gamma = 1.4$): two adiabats (blue) joined by two constant-volume steps. Heat $Q_h$ enters on $b \to c$ and $Q_c$ leaves on $d \to a$ (red); the shaded area is the net work per cycle.*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - "Net work = enclosed area" is Green's theorem in the $pV$ plane: with $x = V$, $y = p$, $\oint p\,dV = -\iint dV\,dp$ for a counterclockwise loop ([[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^thm-16-1|452 Thm. §16.1]]), so a clockwise engine loop gives $+$area. The integral is a work line integral ([[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^def-16-2|452 Def. §16.2]]).
> - The energy balance is energy conservation, [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]], applied to a device that returns to its initial state.
