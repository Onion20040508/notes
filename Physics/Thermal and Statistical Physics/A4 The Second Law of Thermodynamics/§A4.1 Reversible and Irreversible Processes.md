The first law ([[§A3.3 The First Law|§A3.3]]) says which processes conserve energy. It says nothing about direction: heat could flow from cold to hot, or a gas that has spread through a box could gather back into one half, without breaking it. This section names the distinction that is missing, reversible against irreversible processes, and states the first form of the law that supplies it.

## Reversible and irreversible processes

> [!definition] Definition §A4.1.1: Reversible and Irreversible Process
> A process is **reversible** if the system *and its environment* can both be returned to exactly their initial states by running the process backwards along the same path. Otherwise it is **irreversible**. Irreversible processes are the ones that occur in nature, so they are also called **natural processes**.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.1*

^def-a4-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: The environment is the hard part
> Any system can be put back in its initial state; that alone proves nothing. The gas of Example §A4.1.1 below can be pushed back into half the box with a piston and cooled back to its old temperature. But the piston does work on it and the heat removed goes somewhere, so the surroundings end up changed. Reversibility asks for *both* to be restored at once.

^cau-a4-1-1

> [!model] Model §A4.1.1: Reversible Process
> A reversible process is an idealisation, approached but never reached. It needs:
> - **quasi-static** change ([[§A3.4 Thermodynamic Processes|§A3.4]]): the system passes through equilibrium states, so the path is a curve in the $pV$ plane and can be traced backwards;
> - **no dissipation**: no friction, no turbulence, no electrical resistance;
> - **no heat flow across a finite temperature difference**: heat is exchanged only with a body at the system's own temperature (to within $dT$).
>
> Quasi-static isothermal and adiabatic changes of a frictionless gas meet these conditions, which is why they are the building blocks of the ideal cycles in this chapter.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.1*

^mod-a4-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Quasi-static is necessary, not sufficient
> The book lists the isobaric, isochoric, isothermal and adiabatic processes of [[§A3.4 Thermodynamic Processes|§A3.4]] as the common reversible ones. Each is reversible only if it is also run without dissipation and with heat exchanged across infinitesimal temperature differences. Slowly heating a gas at constant pressure by putting it on a single hot plate is quasi-static enough to draw on a $pV$ diagram, but the heat crosses a finite temperature gap and the process is irreversible. To make it reversible one needs a whole sequence of reservoirs at gradually rising temperatures ([[§A4.5 Entropy|§A4.5]]).

^cau-a4-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: The signs of irreversibility
> Irreversibility shows itself through **finite gradients**: a finite temperature difference between bodies exchanging heat, a finite pressure difference that makes a gas rush, friction between surfaces in relative motion. While such a gradient acts, the system is generally not in equilibrium and has no well-defined pressure or temperature, so the process has no path on a $pV$ diagram at all.

^rem-a4-1-1

## The Clausius statement

> [!principle] Principle §A4.1.2: Second Law of Thermodynamics (Clausius Statement)
> Heat never flows spontaneously from a colder body to a hotter body. Equivalently: no process is possible whose **sole result** is the transfer of heat from a colder body to a hotter one.
>
> "Spontaneously" and "sole result" mean that nothing else, no third body and no external agent, has been changed in the process.
>
> *Domain: macroscopic systems of very many particles ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale|§A4.6]]).*
> *Source: UP2 §4.1; PHY 287, week 6 slides (Oct 13)*

^pr-a4-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: What the principle adds to the first law
> - It summarises every experiment on heat flow, from ice in a warm hand to a metal rod held in a fire. Heat *can* be moved from cold to hot, and a refrigerator does it, but only at the price of another change elsewhere: work supplied by a motor ([[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps|§A4.2]]).
> - It makes heat flow between bodies at different temperatures irreversible: running such a flow backwards would be exactly the forbidden process.
> - It is one of several equivalent forms of the second law. The Kelvin–Planck form and the proof of equivalence are in [[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law|§A4.3]], and the quantitative form, entropy never decreases, is in [[§A4.5 Entropy|§A4.5]].
> - Newton's laws for one particle are unchanged when the direction of time is reversed. A gas of $10^{23}$ molecules is different in practice: collisions erase all memory of the initial trajectories, and the reversed sequence, though allowed, is so improbable that it is never seen ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale|§A4.6]]).

^rem-a4-1-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.1.1: Free Expansion into a Vacuum
> An ideal gas at pressure $p_0$ fills one half of a thermally insulated box; the other half is empty. The wall between them is removed quickly, and the gas fills the whole box. Take the gas as the system.
> - **Energy.** The gas pushes against nothing, so $W = 0$; the box is insulated, so $Q = 0$. The first law gives $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q - W = 0$. The internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on $T$, so the temperature is unchanged.
> - **Final state.** At the same $T$ with twice the volume, $p = nRT/V = p_0/2$.
> - **Irreversibility.** While the gas rushes into the empty half it is not in equilibrium, so the process is not even quasi-static. Restoring the initial state takes a piston (work done on the gas) and heat removed to a colder body: the environment is changed.
>
> Nothing in the first law forbids the molecules from all returning to the original half one day. The second law says they will not ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale|§A4.6]] estimates how unlikely it is).
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.1*

^ex-a4-1-1

> [!example] Example §A4.1.2: A Pot of Water That Freezes Itself
> A copper pot at room temperature is filled with water at room temperature. Imagine that the water spontaneously freezes while the pot becomes hot. Why can this not happen?
>
> Energy is not the obstacle: if the heat released by the freezing water ($mL_f$, [[§A1.4 Phase Changes|§A1.4]]) went into the pot, energy would be conserved and the first law satisfied. The process is impossible because its sole result would be a transfer of heat from a colder body (the freezing water) to a hotter one (the warming pot): it violates the second law ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]]), not the first.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Midterm 1 practice*

^ex-a4-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The same question, "does it depend on the path?", separates conservative from non-conservative forces in mechanics ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^def-a4-5-1|CM Def. §A4.5.1]]); friction is the mechanical example of a dissipative, irreversible process.
