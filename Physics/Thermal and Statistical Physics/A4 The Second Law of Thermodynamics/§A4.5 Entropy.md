For every reversible engine $Q_c/Q_h = T_c/T_h$ ([[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]]), that is, $Q_h/T_h = Q_c/T_c$: heat divided by temperature is passed through a reversible cycle unchanged. This section turns that observation into a new state function, the entropy, and into the quantitative form of the second law: the entropy of an isolated system never decreases.

## A state function from the Carnot cycle

In this section $Q$ and $dQ$ are **signed** again: heat absorbed by the system is positive, heat given up is negative.

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.1: Heat over Temperature in a Carnot Cycle
> Around a Carnot cycle, with any working substance,
>
> $$
> \sum \frac{Q}{T} = \frac{Q_h}{T_h} - \frac{Q_c}{T_c} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> where the sum runs over the two isotherms ($+Q_h$ absorbed at $T_h$, $-Q_c$ given up at $T_c$); the adiabats exchange no heat.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.6*

^thm-a4-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A Carnot engine is reversible, so $Q_c/Q_h = T_c/T_h$ ([[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]]); rearranged, $Q_h/T_h = Q_c/T_c$.

^der-a4-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^mod-a4-4-1|Model §A4.4.1]], [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.2: Heat over Temperature in Any Reversible Cycle
> For every reversible cycle,
>
> $$
> \oint \frac{dQ}{T} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> where $dQ$ is the heat absorbed in each small step and $T$ the temperature at which it is absorbed.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.6, eq. (4.11)*

^thm-a4-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The book proves this only for the Carnot cycle (Theorem §A4.5.1) and states that it holds in general. The standard argument for the general case goes as follows; it is made rigorous at level B, for any system, through the Clausius inequality ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]).
>
> Draw a family of closely spaced reversible adiabats across the cycle in the $pV$ plane. Between two neighbouring adiabats the cycle has two short arcs, one on each side. Replace each arc by a short isotherm between the same two adiabats, placed so that it exchanges the same heat as the arc. The cycle is then replaced by a zig-zag path, and each strip between neighbouring adiabats is a small Carnot cycle. By Theorem §A4.5.1, for strip $i$ with heats $\Delta Q_i$ at $T_i$ and $\Delta Q_i'$ at $T_i'$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\Delta Q_i}{T_i} + \frac{\Delta Q_i'}{T_i'} = 0 .
> $$
>
> Add these over all strips. The adiabatic pieces carry no heat, and each interior adiabat is run once upwards and once downwards by the two strips it separates, so the sum is exactly $\sum \Delta Q/T$ along the zig-zag path, and it vanishes. As the adiabats are packed closer, the zig-zag path approaches the cycle and the sum approaches $\oint dQ/T$, which is therefore $0$.
>
> *Source: the standard (Clausius) argument; UP2 and PHY 287 assert the general case without proof.*

^der-a4-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-1|Theorem §A4.5.1]], [[§A3.4 Thermodynamic Processes#^def-a3-4-2|Def. §A3.4.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.3: Entropy Is a State Function
> For two equilibrium states $A$ and $B$, the integral
>
> $$
> \int_A^B \frac{dQ}{T}
> $$
>
> has the same value along every reversible path from $A$ to $B$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.6*

^thm-a4-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let I and II be two reversible paths from $A$ to $B$. Running II backwards is again reversible and retraces every step with the heat reversed, $dQ \to -dQ$, at the same temperatures; so $\int_{B \to A,\ \text{II}} dQ/T = -\int_{A \to B,\ \text{II}} dQ/T$. Path I followed by II backwards is a reversible cycle, and by Theorem §A4.5.2
>
> $$
> 0 = \oint \frac{dQ}{T} = \int_{A \to B,\ \text{I}} \frac{dQ}{T} - \int_{A \to B,\ \text{II}} \frac{dQ}{T} .
> $$
>
> Since I and II were arbitrary, the integral depends only on $A$ and $B$.

^der-a4-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]], [[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A4.5.1: Entropy
> The **entropy** $S$ is the state function whose change between equilibrium states $A$ and $B$ is
>
> $$
> \Delta S = S_B - S_A = \int_A^B \frac{dQ}{T} \qquad \text{(along any reversible path from } A \text{ to } B\text{)} .
> $$
>
> For a reversible process at constant temperature this is $\Delta S = Q/T$. The unit is $\mathrm{J/K}$. Only changes of $S$ are fixed; its zero is a convention (but see the third law, [[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-3|Principle §A4.6.3]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.6, eqs. (4.8)–(4.10)*

^def-a4-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why $dQ/T$, and not $dQ$?
> Heat itself is not a state function: the heat absorbed between two states depends on the path ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^ex-a3-3-2|Example §A3.3.2]]). Dividing by $T$ repairs this. PHY 287 motivated the choice with an ideal gas expanding isothermally by $dV$: then $dE_{\text{int}} = 0$, $dQ = p\,dV = nRT\,dV/V$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{dQ}{T} = nR\,\frac{dV}{V} :
> $$
>
> the heat over temperature measures the *fractional* growth of the volume available to the molecules, that is, how much more spread out (disordered) the gas becomes. The same heat counts for less at a high temperature, where the molecules are already moving wildly. Boltzmann's formula ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-1|Principle §A4.6.1]]) makes this disorder picture quantitative.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 7 notes (Oct 16)*

^rem-a4-5-1

> [!caution] Caution: Entropy changes of irreversible processes
> The formula $\Delta S = \int dQ/T$ holds only along a **reversible** path. For an irreversible process between equilibrium states $A$ and $B$ (rapid cooling, free expansion, heat flow across a finite temperature difference), $\Delta S$ is still $S_B - S_A$, because $S$ is a state function, but it must be computed along some *other*, reversible path joining the same two states. The heat actually exchanged in the irreversible process, divided by $T$, does not give it.

^cau-a4-5-1

## Entropy changes in simple processes

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.4: Entropy Changes in Simple Reversible Processes
> 1. At constant temperature $T$: $\Delta S = Q/T$. In particular a phase change of mass $m$ with latent heat $L$ has $\Delta S = \pm mL/T$.
> 2. Reversible adiabatic (**isentropic**) process: $\Delta S = 0$.
> 3. A body of mass $m$ and constant specific heat $c$ heated or cooled from $T_1$ to $T_2$: $\Delta S = mc\ln(T_2/T_1)$.
> 4. $n$ moles of an ideal gas going from $(T_1, V_1)$ to $(T_2, V_2)$:
>
> $$
> \Delta S = nC_V \ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} + nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} ;
> $$
>
> special cases: isothermal $nR\ln(V_2/V_1)$, isochoric $nC_V\ln(T_2/T_1)$, isobaric $nC_p\ln(T_2/T_1)$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.6, Examples 4.4–4.6; PHY 287, week 7 notes (Oct 16)*

^thm-a4-5-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) $T$ is constant, so it comes out of the integral of [[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]; for a phase change $Q = \pm mL$ ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^law-a1-4-1|Law §A1.4.1]]). (2) $dQ = 0$ in every step. (3) Each step exchanges $dQ = mc\,dT$ ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^law-a1-3-2|Law §A1.3.2]]), so $\Delta S = mc\int_{T_1}^{T_2} dT/T = mc\ln(T_2/T_1)$; the reversible path heats the body through a sequence of reservoirs whose temperatures differ by $dT$. (4) For a reversible step of an ideal gas the first law gives $dQ = dE_{\text{int}} + p\,dV = nC_V\,dT + nRT\,dV/V$ ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]). Dividing by $T$,
>
> $$
> dS = nC_V\,\frac{dT}{T} + nR\,\frac{dV}{V} ,
> $$
>
> which integrates to the formula. At constant pressure $V_2/V_1 = T_2/T_1$, and $C_V + R = C_p$ gives $nC_p\ln(T_2/T_1)$.

^der-a4-5-4

*Uses:* [[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]], [[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]], [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^law-a1-3-2|Law §A1.3.2]], [[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^law-a1-4-1|Law §A1.4.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The ideal-gas formula shows the state function directly
> The right side of the ideal-gas formula depends only on the end states $(T_1, V_1)$ and $(T_2, V_2)$: here Theorem §A4.5.3 can be seen without any cycle. It also shows the two ways a gas gains entropy: by getting hotter and by spreading into more volume. On a reversible adiabat the two terms cancel, $C_V\ln(T_2/T_1) = -R\ln(V_2/V_1)$, which is $TV^{\gamma - 1} = \text{const}$ again ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-2|Theorem §A3.6.2]]).

^rem-a4-5-2

## The entropy statement of the second law

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.5: Entropy Statement of the Second Law
> The total entropy of an isolated system ([[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]]; for instance a system together with its environment, "the universe") never decreases:
>
> $$
> \Delta S_{\text{total}} \ge 0 ,
> $$
>
> with equality exactly when every process involved is reversible.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.6, eq. (4.12)*

^thm-a4-5-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The book takes this as a further statement of the second law and remarks that it is consistent with the others. Here is that consistency, for the processes met so far.
>
> **Reversible processes.** Whenever the system absorbs $dQ$ reversibly at temperature $T$, its environment gives up the same $dQ$ at the same $T$, so $dS_{\text{sys}} + dS_{\text{env}} = dQ/T - dQ/T = 0$.
>
> **Heat flow across a temperature difference.** Heat $Q > 0$ passes from a reservoir at $T_h$ to one at $T_c$. Each reservoir's temperature stays fixed, so by [[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-4|Theorem §A4.5.4]](1)
>
> $$
> \Delta S_{\text{total}} = -\frac{Q}{T_h} + \frac{Q}{T_c} ,
> $$
>
> which is positive exactly when $T_h > T_c$. So "$\Delta S_{\text{total}} \ge 0$" allows heat to flow from hot to cold and forbids the reverse: it is the Clausius statement.
>
> **Engines.** Over a cycle the working substance returns to its state, so $\Delta S_{\text{engine}} = 0$, and only the reservoirs change:
>
> $$
> \Delta S_{\text{total}} = -\frac{Q_h}{T_h} + \frac{Q_c}{T_c} \ge 0 \iff \frac{Q_c}{Q_h} \ge \frac{T_c}{T_h} \iff e \le 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_h} ,
> $$
>
> which is Carnot's theorem ([[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]]), with equality exactly for reversible engines. A perfect engine ($Q_c = 0$) would give $-Q_h/T_h < 0$; that is the Kelvin–Planck statement.
>
> **The general case** needs the inequality $\oint dQ/T \le 0$ for an arbitrary cycle containing irreversible steps (the Clausius inequality). It follows from Carnot's theorem by refining the strip argument of Theorem §A4.5.2, and is proved at level B: [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]; the general entropy statement is then [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]].

^der-a4-5-5

*Uses:* [[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]], [[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-4|Theorem §A4.5.4]], [[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]], [[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^pr-a4-3-1|Principle §A4.3.1]], [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Only the total has to grow
> The entropy of a *part* can decrease: a hot object that cools loses entropy (Example §A4.5.2). The law constrains the total of everything that interacts. The book calls such a total a "closed system"; what is meant is an **isolated** one, exchanging neither heat nor work with anything outside. Over an irreversible cycle the working substance's entropy change is still zero, because $S$ is a state function; the increase shows up in the surroundings.

^cau-a4-5-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.5.1: Melting Ice
> Heat is added slowly to $m = 50\ \mathrm g$ of ice at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ until it has all melted to water at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. The process is slow, so treat it as reversible at the constant temperature $T = 273\ \mathrm K$. With $L_f = 335\ \mathrm{J/g}$,
>
> $$
> Q = mL_f = (50\ \mathrm g)(335\ \mathrm{J/g}) = 1.675 \times 10^4\ \mathrm J, \qquad \Delta S = \frac{Q}{T} = \frac{1.675 \times 10^4\ \mathrm J}{273\ \mathrm K} = 61.4\ \mathrm{J/K} .
> $$
>
> The entropy of the water rises: the molecules leave fixed lattice sites for the freer liquid. The same formula serves for boiling, where $T$ is also constant.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 4.4*

^ex-a4-5-1

> [!example] Example §A4.5.2: Heat Flow between Two Steel Blocks
> A $4.00\ \mathrm{kg}$ steel block at $400\ \mathrm K$ is put in contact with an identical block at $300\ \mathrm K$, the pair isolated from everything else. They come to a common temperature of $350\ \mathrm K$. The heat flow crosses a finite temperature difference and is irreversible, so compute each block's $\Delta S$ along a substitute reversible path, heating or cooling through a sequence of reservoirs ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-4|Theorem §A4.5.4]](3)), with $c = 450\ \mathrm{J/(kg \cdot K)}$, $mc = 1800\ \mathrm{J/K}$:
>
> $$
> \Delta S_h = mc\ln\frac{350}{400} = -240\ \mathrm{J/K}, \qquad \Delta S_c = mc\ln\frac{350}{300} = +277\ \mathrm{J/K} .
> $$
>
> The total, and so the entropy change of the universe, is $\Delta S_h + \Delta S_c = +37\ \mathrm{J/K} > 0$. Equal amounts of heat left one block and entered the other, but the cold block received it at lower temperatures and gained more entropy than the hot block lost.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 4.9*

^ex-a4-5-2

> [!example] Example §A4.5.3: The Stirling Cycle
> $n = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol}$ of a monatomic ideal gas ($C_V = \tfrac32 R$) runs a reversible cycle: $A \to B$ isothermal expansion at $T_h = 406\ \mathrm K$ ($133\ ^\circ\mathrm C$) from $0.10$ to $0.20\ \mathrm{m^3}$; $B \to C$ cooling at constant volume to $T_c = 306\ \mathrm K$ ($33\ ^\circ\mathrm C$); $C \to D$ isothermal compression at $T_c$ back to $0.10\ \mathrm{m^3}$; $D \to A$ heating at constant volume back to $T_h$. Signed $W$ (by the gas), $Q$ (into the gas) and $\Delta S$ from Theorem §A4.5.4:
>
> | step | $W$ (J) | $Q$ (J) | $\Delta S$ (J/K) |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $A \to B$: $nRT_h\ln 2$ | $+2.34$ | $+2.34$ | $nR\ln 2 = +5.76 \times 10^{-3}$ |
> | $B \to C$: $\tfrac32 nR(T_c - T_h)$ | $0$ | $-1.25$ | $\tfrac32 nR\ln\tfrac{306}{406} = -3.53 \times 10^{-3}$ |
> | $C \to D$: $-nRT_c\ln 2$ | $-1.76$ | $-1.76$ | $-5.76 \times 10^{-3}$ |
> | $D \to A$: $\tfrac32 nR(T_h - T_c)$ | $0$ | $+1.25$ | $+3.53 \times 10^{-3}$ |
> | cycle | $+0.58$ | $+0.58$ | $0$ |
>
> The entropy changes add to zero, as they must around a reversible cycle (Theorem §A4.5.2). The heat absorbed is $Q_{AB} + Q_{DA} = 3.59\ \mathrm J$, so $e = 0.58/3.59 = 0.16$, below the Carnot value $1 - 306/406 = 0.25$ for the same extreme temperatures.
>
> This does not contradict Carnot's theorem, which compares engines using *two* reservoirs. A reversible Stirling engine absorbs heat on $D \to A$ at every temperature between $T_c$ and $T_h$, so it needs a whole range of reservoirs. Real Stirling engines carry a **regenerator** that stores the heat released on $B \to C$ and returns it on $D \to A$; then only $Q_{AB}$ is drawn from the hot reservoir and $e = 0.58/2.34 = 0.25$, the Carnot value.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 4.6 (numbers recomputed)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The heat $dQ$ is not the differential of any function of state, while $dQ/T$ is: $1/T$ is an integrating factor that turns an inexact differential into an exact one, $dS$ ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^def-22-8|452 Def. §22.8]]). On the $(T, V)$ plane of an ideal gas, $dS = nC_V\,dT/T + nR\,dV/V$ is visibly exact. Level B treats integrating factors in general and what singles out $1/T$ ([[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^def-b1-1-2|Definition §B1.1.2]], [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^rem-b1-1-2|Remark: What is special about entropy]]), and proves that $1/T$ is one for every system ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]).
> - Path independence from $\oint = 0$ is the same argument as for conservative forces in mechanics ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-1|CM Theorem §A4.5.1]]): entropy is to $dQ/T$ what potential energy is to a conservative force.
> - The strip argument for Theorem §A4.5.2 is the same cancellation of shared interior edges that glues Green's theorem together from small cells ([[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^thm-16-1|452 Thm. §16.1]]).
> - Level B: the ideal-gas entropy of Theorem §A4.5.4 as a function of state, $S(T, V)$ and $S(T, p)$ — [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]]; the steel blocks of Example §A4.5.2 joined by an engine instead, which gives the most work they can deliver — [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^ex-b2-2-2|Example §B2.2.2]].
> - Level B: entropy from statistical mechanics. The statistical entropy agrees with the Clausius entropy of Definition §A4.5.1 — [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-7|Theorem §B6.2.7]]; Theorem §A4.5.5 holds because the equilibrium macrostate is overwhelmingly the most probable — [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-10|Theorem §B6.1.10]] — and as an H-theorem for the evolution of probabilities — [[§B5.4 Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-6|Theorem §B5.4.6]], [[§B5.4 Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^rem-b5-4-7|Remark: H, entropy and the second law]].
