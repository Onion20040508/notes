Entropy was defined through heat and temperature ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]). What does it measure in terms of the molecules? This section gives the statistical answer, entropy as a count of the microscopic arrangements compatible with what is seen, which explains why the second law holds for large systems. It closes with the third law, which concerns the limit $T \to 0$.

## Entropy and disorder

> [!remark] Remark: Entropy measures disorder
> The more disordered or randomised a system is, the higher its entropy. A new deck of cards, sorted by suit and number, is highly ordered; shuffling randomises it and raises its entropy, and after shuffling the top card tells nothing about the next one. In these terms the second law says: **in any irreversible process the universe becomes more disordered**. In a free expansion ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^ex-a4-1-1|Example §A4.1.1]]) the gas gets a larger volume, with more possible arrangements for the same molecules. When ice melts ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^ex-a4-5-1|Example §A4.5.1]]) the molecules leave fixed lattice sites for a liquid in which they move freely.
>
> The book keeps this picture qualitative and leaves the details beyond its scope. PHY 287 made it quantitative by counting arrangements, as follows.

^rem-a4-6-1

## Counting microstates

> [!definition] Definition §A4.6.1: Microstate; Macrostate; Multiplicity
> A **microstate** of a system is a complete specification of the state of every particle (which coin shows heads, which half of the box each molecule is in). A **macrostate** is specified only by the macroscopic quantities one observes (how many heads; the pressure, volume and temperature of the gas). The **multiplicity** $W$ of a macrostate is the number of microstates that produce it.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 7 notes (Oct 18–20)*

^def-a4-6-1

> [!principle] Principle §A4.6.1: Boltzmann's Entropy Formula
> The entropy of a macrostate is
>
> $$
> S = k_B \ln W ,
> $$
>
> where $W$ is its multiplicity and $k_B = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}$ is Boltzmann's constant ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]).
>
> *Domain: a system with very many particles, all of its microstates being equally likely. At this level the formula is postulated; its justification belongs to statistical mechanics (Chapter B, planned).*
> *Source: PHY 287, week 7 notes (Oct 18–20)*

^pr-a4-6-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why a logarithm, and why it explains the second law
> - For two independent systems the multiplicities multiply, $W = W_1W_2$, while their entropies add, as the entropy changes $\int dQ/T$ of separate parts do; the logarithm turns the product into a sum, $S = S_1 + S_2$.
> - If all microstates are equally likely, the probability of a macrostate is proportional to $W = e^{S/k_B}$. A system left alone wanders among its microstates and so ends up, with overwhelming probability, in the macrostates of largest $W$, that is, of largest $S$. For $10^{23}$ particles "overwhelming" means that the reverse is never seen in the age of the universe (Example §A4.6.1). This is why the second law has a *Domain:* line: it is a statistical law, exact in practice for macroscopic systems, but a handful of molecules can and does fluctuate against it.

^rem-a4-6-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.6.2: Entropy of an Ideal Gas Changing Volume, Counted
> When $N$ molecules of an ideal gas go from volume $V_1$ to volume $V_2$ at the same temperature, Boltzmann's formula gives
>
> $$
> \Delta S = N k_B \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} ,
> $$
>
> the same as the thermodynamic result ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-4|Theorem §A4.5.4]]). For a doubling of the volume, $\Delta S = Nk_B\ln 2$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 7 notes (Oct 18–20); UP2 Example 4.8*

^thm-a4-6-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> **Counting.** Divide the box into small cells of fixed size. The molecules of an ideal gas do not interact, so each one independently can sit in any cell, and the number of positions open to one molecule is proportional to $V$. For $N$ molecules the number of position arrangements is proportional to $V^N$. The temperature is the same at the start and the end, so the spread of molecular velocities is too, and the velocity part of the count is unchanged. The cell size cancels in the ratio, so
>
> $$
> \Delta S = k_B\ln W_2 - k_B\ln W_1 = k_B \ln\Bigl(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\Bigr)^{N} = Nk_B\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} .
> $$
>
> With $N = nN_A$ and $R = N_Ak_B$ ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^thm-a2-1-3|Theorem §A2.1.3]]), $Nk_B = nR$.
>
> **Thermodynamics.** Entropy is a state function, so the change can be computed along a reversible isothermal expansion between the same states, whatever the actual process was: there $Q = W = nRT\ln(V_2/V_1)$ and $\Delta S = Q/T = nR\ln(V_2/V_1)$. The two answers agree, which is the consistency check that ties $S = k_B\ln W$ to the entropy of [[§A4.5 Entropy|§A4.5]].

^der-a4-6-2

## The third law

> [!principle] Principle §A4.6.3: Third Law of Thermodynamics
> The absolute zero of temperature cannot be reached in any finite number of cooling steps. Equivalently, the entropy change of a system along an isotherm vanishes as the temperature approaches zero:
>
> $$
> \lim_{T \to 0}\, (\Delta S)_T = 0 .
> $$
>
> As $T \to 0$ a system becomes perfectly ordered and its entropy approaches its absolute minimum.
>
> *Domain: all systems; its explanation requires quantum mechanics and is not given at this level.*
> *Source: UP2 §4.7, eq. (4.13)*

^pr-a4-6-3

> [!remark] Remark: What the third law adds
> - The second law says nothing about $T \to 0$: $\Delta S = Q/T$ alone does not decide what happens there. The third law is a separate principle.
> - Near $T = 0$ the heat that can still be removed becomes vanishingly small, so the entropy change along an isotherm goes to zero: every isotherm becomes isentropic, like a reversible adiabat. Consistently, the best refrigerator has $K_R = T_c/(T_h - T_c) \to 0$ as $T_c \to 0$: each joule removed from a body near absolute zero costs ever more work ([[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^rem-a4-4-4|§A4.4]]).
> - A reservoir at $T_c = 0$ would allow an engine with $e = 1 - T_c/T_h = 1$, a perfect engine; the third law keeps the second law safe from this loophole.
> - In the language of Principle §A4.6.1, a perfectly ordered state is a single microstate, $W = 1$, so $S = k_B\ln 1 = 0$: the third law gives entropy a natural zero, which thermodynamics alone left open ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]).
> - Experiments have come close: about $1 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm K$ was reached at the Helsinki University of Technology in 2008.

^rem-a4-6-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.6.1: Four Coins, and a Box of Gas
> Toss 4 coins. A microstate lists each coin (HTTH, …): there are $2^4 = 16$, all equally likely. A macrostate gives only the number of heads, $0, 1, 2, 3, 4$, with multiplicities
>
> $$
> W = 1,\ 4,\ 6,\ 4,\ 1 ,
> $$
>
> the binomial coefficients $\binom{4}{n}$. "Two heads" is the most probable macrostate ($6/16$); "all heads" has probability $1/16$, unlikely but common enough to see in a few tries.
>
> Now let the coins be $N$ molecules of a gas and "heads" mean "in the left half of the box". The probability that all of them are in the left half at once is $(1/2)^N$. For $N = 100$ that is $8 \times 10^{-31}$; for one mole, $N = 6.02 \times 10^{23}$, it is $2^{-6.02 \times 10^{23}}$, a number with about $1.8 \times 10^{23}$ zeros after the decimal point. This is why a free expansion ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^ex-a4-1-1|Example §A4.1.1]]) never runs backwards, although no law of mechanics forbids it. As $N$ grows the multiplicities concentrate ever more sharply around the even split, and the macrostate of maximum $W$ becomes the only one ever observed: equilibrium.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 7 notes (Oct 18–20); UP2 §4.1*

> [!example] Example §A4.6.2: Free Expansion of One Mole
> One mole of an ideal gas expands freely into a vacuum, doubling its volume inside an insulated box ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^ex-a4-1-1|Example §A4.1.1]]). By Theorem §A4.6.2,
>
> $$
> \Delta S_{\text{gas}} = nR\ln 2 = (1.00\ \mathrm{mol})(8.31\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)})(0.693) = 5.76\ \mathrm{J/K} .
> $$
>
> The walls are insulating, no heat flows, and the environment's entropy is unchanged, so $\Delta S_{\text{universe}} = +5.76\ \mathrm{J/K} > 0$: the process is irreversible, as the entropy statement requires ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-5|Theorem §A4.5.5]]). Microscopically the multiplicity has grown by the factor $W_2/W_1 = e^{\Delta S/k_B} = 2^{N_A}$, the inverse of the probability of finding all molecules back in the original half (Example §A4.6.1).
>
> Note that the gas absorbed no heat, yet its entropy rose: $\Delta S$ is *not* $\int dQ/T$ along the actual, irreversible process ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^cau-a4-5-1|§A4.5]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 4.8*

^ex-a4-6-2

> [!remark] Remark: Life does not violate the second law
> A living cell gathers molecules into a highly ordered organism, which looks like a local decrease of entropy. It is more than compensated by the disordering of the rest of the universe, so the entropy of the universe still increases ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^cau-a4-5-2|§A4.5]]).

^rem-a4-6-4
