Carnot's theorem ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^thm-a4-3-3|Theorem §A4.3.3]]) says that all reversible engines between two reservoirs share one efficiency, the largest possible, without saying what it is. This section builds the simplest reversible engine, the Carnot engine with an ideal gas, computes its efficiency, and so obtains the maximum efficiency of every engine and the maximum performance of every refrigerator and heat pump.

## The cycle

> [!model] Model §A4.4.1: Carnot Cycle; Carnot Engine
> A **Carnot cycle** is a reversible cycle of a working substance between two reservoirs at $T_h > T_c$, made of four quasi-static, frictionless steps:
> 1. $a \to b$, **isothermal expansion** at $T_h$, in contact with the hot reservoir: the substance absorbs heat $Q_h$;
> 2. $b \to c$, **adiabatic expansion**, thermally isolated: the temperature falls from $T_h$ to $T_c$;
> 3. $c \to d$, **isothermal compression** at $T_c$, in contact with the cold reservoir: the substance discards heat $Q_c$;
> 4. $d \to a$, **adiabatic compression**: the temperature rises from $T_c$ back to $T_h$.
>
> An engine running on this cycle is a **Carnot engine**; run backwards it is a Carnot refrigerator or heat pump.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.5*

^mod-a4-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why exactly these four steps
> A reversible engine that uses only two reservoirs must be a Carnot engine. Heat can pass reversibly between the substance and a reservoir only when they are at the same temperature ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^mod-a4-1-1|Model §A4.1.1]]), so heat is exchanged only on isotherms at $T_h$ and $T_c$. Between them the substance must change temperature without exchanging heat, which is what a reversible adiabat does. The working substance is free: an ideal gas is just the easiest to calculate with.

^rem-a4-4-1

## Efficiency

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.4.2: Efficiency of the Ideal-Gas Carnot Engine
> For a Carnot engine whose working substance is an ideal gas,
>
> $$
> \frac{Q_c}{Q_h} = \frac{T_c}{T_h}, \qquad e = 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_h} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.5, eq. (4.5)*

^thm-a4-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> **Isotherms.** The internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on its temperature, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$ on each isotherm and the heat equals the work, $nRT\ln(V_{\text{final}}/V_{\text{initial}})$ ([[§A3.4 Thermodynamic Processes|§A3.4]]). As magnitudes,
>
> $$
> Q_h = nRT_h \ln\frac{V_b}{V_a}, \qquad Q_c = nRT_c \ln\frac{V_c}{V_d}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{Q_c}{Q_h} = \frac{T_c}{T_h}\,\frac{\ln(V_c/V_d)}{\ln(V_b/V_a)} .
> $$
>
> **Adiabats.** On a reversible adiabat $TV^{\gamma-1}$ is constant ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes|§A3.6]]):
>
> $$
> T_h V_b^{\gamma-1} = T_c V_c^{\gamma-1}, \qquad T_h V_a^{\gamma-1} = T_c V_d^{\gamma-1} .
> $$
>
> Dividing the first equation by the second gives $(V_b/V_a)^{\gamma-1} = (V_c/V_d)^{\gamma-1}$, so $V_c/V_d = V_b/V_a$: the two isotherms have the same volume ratio. The logarithms cancel, $Q_c/Q_h = T_c/T_h$, and with $e = 1 - Q_c/Q_h$ ([[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]) the efficiency follows.

^der-a4-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^mod-a4-4-1|Model §A4.4.1]], [[§A3.4 Thermodynamic Processes|§A3.4]], [[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes|§A3.6]], [[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas|§A2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.4.3: Carnot Efficiency
> Every reversible engine working between reservoirs at $T_h$ and $T_c$, whatever its working substance, has
>
> $$
> \frac{Q_c}{Q_h} = \frac{T_c}{T_h}, \qquad e_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_h},
> $$
>
> and every engine working between these reservoirs has $e \le 1 - T_c/T_h$, with $<$ if it is irreversible.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.5*

^thm-a4-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The ideal-gas Carnot engine is reversible, and its efficiency is $1 - T_c/T_h$ ([[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-2|Theorem §A4.4.2]]). By part 2 of Carnot's theorem ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^thm-a4-3-3|Theorem §A4.3.3]]) every reversible engine between the same reservoirs has the same efficiency, hence the same ratio $Q_c/Q_h = 1 - e$. By part 1 no engine does better, and an irreversible one does strictly worse ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^rem-a4-3-3|§A4.3]]).

^der-a4-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-2|Theorem §A4.4.2]], [[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^thm-a4-3-3|Theorem §A4.3.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Kelvin temperatures, and $e < 1$
> - $T_h$ and $T_c$ must be in kelvin. Between $400\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ and $30\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ the Carnot efficiency is $1 - 303/673 = 0.55$, not $1 - 30/400$.
> - $e_{\text{Carnot}} < 1$ for every $T_c > 0$. A Carnot engine is the best possible engine, not a perfect one. Reaching $e = 1$ would need a reservoir at absolute zero, which the third law rules out ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale|§A4.6]]).
> - The efficiency grows with the temperature ratio $T_h/T_c$: hotter sources and colder sinks make better engines.

^cau-a4-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Carnot's principle as a form of the second law
> Carnot summarised his work in **Carnot's principle**: no engine working between two reservoirs at constant temperatures can be more efficient than a reversible engine. It is equivalent to the other two statements. Clausius implies it ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^thm-a4-3-3|Theorem §A4.3.3]]). It implies Kelvin–Planck: a perfect engine, drawing on the hot reservoir only, could be counted as working between $T_h$ and any colder reservoir, and its $e = 1$ would beat $1 - T_c/T_h$. And Kelvin–Planck implies Clausius ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]). The three statements thus imply each other in a ring.

^rem-a4-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: A temperature scale independent of any substance
> Because $Q_c/Q_h = T_c/T_h$ holds for *every* reversible engine, a ratio of two temperatures can be measured as a ratio of heats exchanged by a reversible engine, with no reference to the properties of any particular material. This defines an absolute (thermodynamic) temperature scale. Theorem §A4.4.2 shows that it agrees with the ideal-gas Kelvin scale ([[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium|§A1.1]]).

^rem-a4-4-3

## Carnot refrigerators and heat pumps

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.4.4: Carnot Refrigerator and Heat Pump
> A reversible refrigerator or heat pump between reservoirs at $T_h$ and $T_c$ has
>
> $$
> K_R = \frac{T_c}{T_h - T_c}, \qquad K_P = \frac{T_h}{T_h - T_c},
> $$
>
> and no refrigerator or heat pump between these reservoirs does better.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.5, eqs. (4.6)–(4.7)*

^thm-a4-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> **Values.** A reversible refrigerator is a reversible engine run backwards, exchanging the same heats ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^der-a4-3-3|§A4.3]]), so $Q_c/Q_h = T_c/T_h$ by [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]]. Dividing numerator and denominator of $K_R = Q_c/(Q_h - Q_c)$ and $K_P = Q_h/(Q_h - Q_c)$ ([[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^thm-a4-2-3|Theorem §A4.2.3]]) by $Q_h$ and replacing $Q_c/Q_h$ by $T_c/T_h$ gives the formulas.
>
> **Maximality.** Suppose a refrigerator $X$ had $K_X > K_R$. Run the Carnot refrigerator forwards as an engine, sized to deliver work $W$: it takes $(K_R + 1)W$ from the hot reservoir and discards $K_R W$ to the cold one. Let this $W$ drive $X$, which takes $K_X W$ from the cold reservoir and delivers $(K_X + 1)W$ to the hot one. Per cycle no work enters from outside, the cold reservoir loses $(K_X - K_R)W > 0$ and the hot one gains the same amount: a perfect refrigerator, forbidden by the Clausius statement. So $K_X \le K_R$, and since $K_P = K_R + 1$ the same holds for heat pumps.

^der-a4-4-4

*Uses:* [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]], [[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^thm-a4-2-3|Theorem §A4.2.3]], [[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Small temperature differences are cheap
> Both coefficients blow up as $T_h - T_c \to 0$: moving heat across a small temperature difference costs little work. As $T_c$ falls, $K_R \to 0$: extracting heat from a very cold body takes ever more work per joule, one face of the unattainability of absolute zero ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale|§A4.6]]). For the same reason a heat pump does worse the colder it is outside.

^rem-a4-4-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.4.1: A Carnot Cycle State by State
> $n = 0.200\ \mathrm{mol}$ of a diatomic ideal gas ($C_V = \tfrac52 R$, $\gamma = 1.40$) runs a Carnot cycle between $T_h = 500\ \mathrm K$ ($227\ ^\circ\mathrm C$) and $T_c = 300\ \mathrm K$ ($27\ ^\circ\mathrm C$). It starts at $p_a = 1.00 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{Pa}$, and the isothermal expansion doubles the volume. In this example $Q$ (heat into the gas), $W$ (work by the gas) and $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$ are signed, as in the first law.
>
> **States.** $V_a = nRT_h/p_a = 8.31 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^3}$ and $V_b = 2V_a = 1.66 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3}$, $p_b = 5.00 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$. On the adiabats $V_c/V_b = V_d/V_a = (T_h/T_c)^{1/(\gamma-1)} = (5/3)^{2.5} = 3.59$, so $V_c = 5.96 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3}$ and $V_d = 2.98 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3}$; then $p = nRT/V$ gives $p_c = 8.37 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{Pa}$ and $p_d = 1.67 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$.
>
> **Steps.** Isotherms: $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$, $Q = W = nRT\ln(V_f/V_i)$. Adiabats: $Q = 0$, $W = -\Delta E_{\text{int}} = -nC_V\Delta T$.
>
> | step | $Q$ (J) | $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$ (J) | $W$ (J) |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $a \to b$ (isothermal, $T_h$) | $nRT_h\ln 2 = +576$ | $0$ | $+576$ |
> | $b \to c$ (adiabatic) | $0$ | $nC_V(T_c - T_h) = -831$ | $+831$ |
> | $c \to d$ (isothermal, $T_c$) | $nRT_c\ln\tfrac12 = -346$ | $0$ | $-346$ |
> | $d \to a$ (adiabatic) | $0$ | $+831$ | $-831$ |
> | cycle | $+230$ | $0$ | $+230$ |
>
> So $Q_h = 576\ \mathrm J$, $Q_c = 346\ \mathrm J$, $W = 230\ \mathrm J$, and $e = 230/576 = 0.400 = 1 - 300/500$, as Theorem §A4.4.2 says. The work of the two adiabats cancels exactly: the net work comes from the isotherms.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 6*

^ex-a4-4-1

> [!example] Example §A4.4.2: A Carnot Heat Pump
> A Carnot heat pump keeps a house at $20.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ ($T_h = 293\ \mathrm K$) while it is $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ outside ($T_c = 273\ \mathrm K$). How much work does it need to deliver $Q_h = 30.0\ \mathrm{kJ}$ to the house?
>
> $$
> K_P = \frac{T_h}{T_h - T_c} = \frac{293\ \mathrm K}{20\ \mathrm K} = 14.7, \qquad W = \frac{Q_h}{K_P} = \frac{30.0\ \mathrm{kJ}}{14.7} = 2.0\ \mathrm{kJ} .
> $$
>
> The other $28\ \mathrm{kJ}$ is pumped in from the cold outside air; resistive heating would need the full $30\ \mathrm{kJ}$ of electrical energy. The work needed depends on both temperatures and grows as the outside gets colder. Real heat pumps fall well short of the Carnot value.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 4.3*

^ex-a4-4-2
