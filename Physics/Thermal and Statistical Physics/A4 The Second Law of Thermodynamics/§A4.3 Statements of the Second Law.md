The Clausius statement ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]]) is about heat flow. In terms of the devices of [[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps|§A4.2]] the second law has a second form, about engines: no engine turns heat entirely into work. This section states it, proves that the two forms say the same thing, and derives their first quantitative consequence, Carnot's theorem: a reversible engine is the most efficient one between two given reservoirs.

## The Kelvin–Planck statement

> [!principle] Principle §A4.3.1: Second Law of Thermodynamics (Kelvin–Planck Statement)
> It is impossible for any system to undergo a process in which it absorbs heat from a reservoir at a single temperature and converts it completely into work, **without any other effect**.
>
> *Domain: macroscopic systems of very many particles ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^rem-a4-6-2|§A4.6]]).*
> *Source: UP2 §4.4; PHY 287, week 6 slides (Oct 13)*

^pr-a4-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: "Without any other effect" is a strong restriction
> A system *can* absorb heat and turn all of it into work, but only by ending up in a different state, which is an "other effect" (Example §A4.3.1). A device that works in cycles returns to its initial state every cycle, so it can comply only by discarding part of the heat somewhere else. The first law would allow a 100 % efficient engine; the second law forbids it. This is why every engine needs a cold reservoir ([[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^rem-a4-2-3|§A4.2]]).

^rem-a4-3-1

> [!definition] Definition §A4.3.1: Perfect Engine; Perfect Refrigerator
> A **perfect engine** is a cyclic device that takes heat $Q$ from a single reservoir and delivers work $W = Q$, discarding no heat ($e = 1$). A **perfect refrigerator** is a cyclic device that moves heat $Q$ from a cold reservoir to a hot one with no work input ($W = 0$, $K_R = \infty$).
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.4*

^def-a4-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Both statements as "impossible devices"
> Applied to a device working in cycles between reservoirs:
> - the **Kelvin–Planck** statement says that **no perfect engine exists** (the "engine statement");
> - the **Clausius** statement says that **no perfect refrigerator exists** (the "refrigerator statement"): heat cannot be moved from a cold reservoir to a hot one without aid from an external source, because that would be a process whose sole result is a cold-to-hot heat transfer.

^rem-a4-3-2

## Equivalence of the two statements

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.3.2: Equivalence of the Clausius and Kelvin–Planck Statements
> The Clausius statement and the Kelvin–Planck statement of the second law are equivalent: each implies the other.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.4; PHY 287, week 6 slides (Oct 13)*

^thm-a4-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> To show that two statements are equivalent, show that the failure of either one implies the failure of the other. Both halves combine an impossible device with an ordinary one working between the same reservoirs at $T_h > T_c$. Ordinary engines and refrigerators exist: any real one will do, or the Carnot devices of [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^mod-a4-4-1|Model §A4.4.1]].
>
> **Clausius false $\Rightarrow$ Kelvin–Planck false.** Suppose a perfect refrigerator exists. Each cycle it takes heat $Q$ from the cold reservoir and gives $Q$ to the hot one with no work. Run beside it an ordinary engine sized so that it discards exactly $Q$ to the cold reservoir per cycle: it takes $Q + \Delta Q$ from the hot reservoir and, by the energy balance ([[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]]), delivers $W = (Q + \Delta Q) - Q = \Delta Q$. Per cycle the combination
> - takes net heat $(Q + \Delta Q) - Q = \Delta Q$ from the hot reservoir,
> - exchanges no net heat with the cold reservoir ($Q$ in, $Q$ out),
> - delivers work $W = \Delta Q$,
>
> and both devices are back in their initial states. It is a perfect engine using the hot reservoir alone, which Kelvin–Planck forbids.
>
> **Kelvin–Planck false $\Rightarrow$ Clausius false.** Suppose a perfect engine exists. Each cycle it takes heat $Q$ from the hot reservoir and delivers work $W = Q$. Use this work to drive an ordinary refrigerator, which takes $Q_c$ from the cold reservoir and gives $Q_c + W$ to the hot one. Per cycle the combination
> - needs no work from outside (the engine's output drives the refrigerator),
> - takes $Q_c$ from the cold reservoir,
> - gives net heat $(Q_c + W) - Q = Q_c$ to the hot reservoir,
>
> and both devices are back in their initial states. Its sole result is the transfer of $Q_c$ from cold to hot: it is a perfect refrigerator, which Clausius forbids.
>
> Each statement fails exactly when the other does, so they are equivalent.

^der-a4-3-2

## Carnot's theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.3.3: Carnot's Theorem
> For engines working between the same two reservoirs at $T_h$ and $T_c$:
> 1. no engine is more efficient than a reversible engine;
> 2. all reversible engines have the same efficiency.
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.4, §4.5 (Carnot's principle)*

^thm-a4-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A reversible engine $R$ can be run backwards as a refrigerator ([[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^def-a4-2-3|Def. §A4.2.3]]): every step is retraced, so the same amounts $Q_h$, $Q_c$, $W$ flow per cycle, in the opposite directions. This is the one property of reversibility the proof needs.
>
> **Part 1.** Let $E$ be any engine between the same reservoirs, absorbing $Q_h'$ and discarding $Q_c'$. Choose the sizes of the engines (or let one run several cycles while the other runs one) so that both deliver the same work $W$; efficiencies are ratios and do not change. Suppose, contrary to the claim, that $e_E > e_R$. Then
>
> $$
> Q_h' = \frac{W}{e_E} < \frac{W}{e_R} = Q_h, \qquad Q_c' = Q_h' - W < Q_h - W = Q_c .
> $$
>
> Now run $R$ backwards, driven by the work $W$ that $E$ delivers. Per cycle of the coupled pair:
> - no work is exchanged with the outside;
> - the hot reservoir receives $Q_h$ from $R$ and gives $Q_h'$ to $E$: a net gain of $Q_h - Q_h' > 0$;
> - the cold reservoir gives $Q_c$ to $R$ and receives $Q_c'$ from $E$: a net loss of $Q_c - Q_c' = (Q_h - W) - (Q_h' - W) = Q_h - Q_h'$;
> - both engines are back in their initial states.
>
> The sole result is the transfer of heat $Q_h - Q_h' > 0$ from the cold reservoir to the hot one, which the Clausius statement forbids. So $e_E \le e_R$.
>
> **Part 2.** Let $R$ and $R'$ both be reversible. Part 1 with $E = R'$ gives $e_{R'} \le e_R$. Since $R'$ can also be reversed, the same argument with the roles exchanged gives $e_R \le e_{R'}$. Hence $e_R = e_{R'}$.

^der-a4-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]], [[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^def-a4-2-2|Def. §A4.2.2]], [[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]], [[§A4.2 Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Heat Pumps#^def-a4-2-3|Def. §A4.2.3]]

*The coupling in the proof of Carnot's theorem. A hypothetical engine $E$ (blue) more efficient than the reversible engine $R$ drives $R$ backwards (red). The work $W$ circulates inside; what is left over is the heat $Q_h - Q_h'$ carried from the cold reservoir to the hot one with nothing else changed, a perfect refrigerator.*

> [!remark] Remark: Irreversible engines are strictly less efficient
> The book states part 1 in the sharper form "any irreversible engine is less efficient than a reversible one". The argument above gives $\le$; equality is excluded as follows. If an irreversible engine $E$ had $e_E = e_R$, then $Q_h' = Q_h$ and $Q_c' = Q_c$, and the coupled pair would leave both reservoirs, both engines and the outside exactly as they were. Running $R$ backwards would then undo every trace of $E$'s operation, which is what irreversibility of $E$ rules out. So $e_E < e_R$ for every irreversible engine.

^rem-a4-3-3

> [!remark] Remark: What Carnot's theorem achieves
> Part 2 says the efficiency of a reversible engine between two reservoirs cannot depend on the working substance or the design: it is a function of $T_h$ and $T_c$ alone. Computing it once, for the simplest reversible engine with the simplest working substance, therefore gives it for all of them. That is done in [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]], with the result $e = 1 - T_c/T_h$.

^rem-a4-3-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.3.1: An Isothermal Expansion Turns All Its Heat into Work
> A gas ($n$ moles, ideal) in contact with a reservoir at temperature $T$ expands quasi-statically from $V_1$ to $V_2$, pushing a piston. Its temperature is constant, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$ and the first law gives
>
> $$
> Q = W = nRT \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}
> $$
>
> ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-3|Theorem §A3.2.3]]): every joule of heat taken from the single reservoir becomes work. This does not contradict the Kelvin–Planck statement, because there is another effect: the gas ends at a larger volume and a lower pressure. To use it again it must be returned to $V_1$. Compressing it at the same temperature takes exactly the work $nRT\ln(V_2/V_1)$ and gives the same heat back to the reservoir, so the cycle delivers nothing. Net work needs the compression to happen at a lower pressure, that is, at a lower temperature, with heat discarded to a *colder* reservoir. Completing the cycle in this way is the Carnot cycle ([[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^mod-a4-4-1|Model §A4.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §4.4*

^ex-a4-3-1
