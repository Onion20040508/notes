During relaxation the probabilities change, and so does the Shannon entropy of the retained system. In the simplest case it rises monotonically, as the second law suggests. In general it need not: the system exchanges heat with its environment, and the second law concerns the total. This section computes the simple case, says what the general case needs, and records the quantum and kinetic-theory versions.

**Setup.** A master equation ([[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^thm-c5-5-1|Theorem §C5.5.1]]); the system's entropy $S_S(t) = -k_B\sum_ip_i(t)\ln p_i(t)$, the Gibbs entropy of Unit 1 ([[§C4a.4 Why the Maximum Is Thermodynamic Entropy#^thm-c4a-4-6|Theorem §C4a.4.6]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.7.1: System Entropy Rises in Equal-Rate Relaxation
> For two states with equal rates $k_+ = k_- = k$ (two equal-energy states in one bath),
>
> $$
> p_1 = \tfrac12 + \delta(t), \quad p_0 = \tfrac12 - \delta(t), \quad \delta(t) = \delta_0e^{-t/\tau}, \quad \tau = \frac{1}{2k} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \dot S_S = -k_B\dot p_1\ln\frac{p_1}{p_0} = \frac{k_B}\tau\,\delta\ln\frac{1/2 + \delta}{1/2 - \delta} \ge 0 ,
> $$
>
> so $S_S \to k_B\ln2$ and $\dot S_S \to 0$, with $k_B\ln2 - S_S \simeq 2k_B\delta_0^2e^{-2t/\tau}$ near the end. No heat flows between equal-energy states, so this is also the total entropy production.
>
> *Model:* [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^ex-c5-5-1|Example §C5.5.1]] with $k_\pm = k$; *applies:* exact.
>
> *Source: Mao §5.6, eqs. (5.45)–(5.47)*

^thm-c5-7-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1.** [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^ex-c5-5-1|Example §C5.5.1]] with $k_+ = k_- = k$: $p^{\text{ss}} = \tfrac12$, $\tau = 1/(2k)$.
>
> **Step 2.** $\dot S_S = -k_B\sum_i\dot p_i(\ln p_i + 1) = -k_B(\dot p_1\ln p_1 + \dot p_0\ln p_0)$ since $\sum\dot p_i = 0$; with $\dot p_0 = -\dot p_1$, $\dot S_S = -k_B\dot p_1\ln(p_1/p_0)$. Insert $\dot p_1 = \dot\delta = -\delta/\tau$.
>
> **Step 3** (sign). $\ln\frac{1/2 + \delta}{1/2 - \delta}$ has the sign of $\delta$, so $\delta$ times it is $\ge 0$.
>
> **Step 4** (approach). $-p\ln p$ summed near $p = \tfrac12 \pm \delta$: $S_S/k_B = \ln2 - 2\delta^2 + O(\delta^4)$.

*Uses:* [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^ex-c5-5-1|Example §C5.5.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: The system's entropy alone need not increase
> When the stationary probabilities are unequal, relaxation can lower $S_S$: the system exchanges heat with its surroundings. The second law concerns the total, $\dot S_{\text{tot}} = \dot S_S + \dot S_E \ge 0$ ([[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-9|Theorem §C1.5.9]]). In a driven ring ([[§C5.6 Detailed Balance#^ex-c5-6-1|Example §C5.6.1]]) the populations are constant, so $\dot S_S = 0$, yet the circulating current is kept up by work dissipated into the environment, $\dot S_E > 0$. Computing $S_E$ needs the heat or work of each transition (local detailed balance), not only the stationary probabilities (Seifert, Secs. 6.2.2–6.2.3; Schnakenberg, Secs. VII–IX, as cited by Mao).

^cau-c5-7-1

> [!remark] Remark: The quantum version
> For a quantum system the retained state is $\hat\rho_S$, not a probability vector. A time-homogeneous, completely positive Markov evolution has a Lindblad generator. When its jump operators connect energy eigenstates, the diagonal populations obey a classical master equation of the form [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^thm-c5-5-1|Theorem §C5.5.1]], and the same environment damps the coherences. Thermal rates obey the quantum form of local detailed balance, and the Gibbs density operator is stationary. > [!remark]- ★ Remark: The Boltzmann equation and kinetic irreversibility
> Kinetic theory keeps far less than the full microstate: the one-particle state $(\mathbf r, \mathbf v)$ and its density $f(\mathbf r, \mathbf v, t)$. With short-range binary collisions,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \mathbf v\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf r}f + \frac{\mathbf F}m\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf v}f = C[f], \qquad C[f_1] = \int d^3v_2\int d\Omega\,g\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\bigl(f_1'f_2' - f_1f_2\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> with $g = |\mathbf v_1 - \mathbf v_2|$ and primes the velocities after an elastic collision: gain minus loss, as in a master equation, but nonlinear because collisions involve two particles. The closure is **molecular chaos**: particles about to collide are uncorrelated, $f^{(2)}_{\text{in}} \simeq f_1f_2$. Collisions create correlations that the equation does not keep, which is the kinetic version of the Markov step of [[§C5.4 Markov Dynamics and the Apparent Arrow of Time#^thm-c5-4-1|Theorem §C5.4.1]]; the same factorization applied to outgoing states would not describe the reversed motion. Boltzmann's functional $H[f] = \int d^3r\,d^3v\,f\ln f$ then obeys
>
> $$
> \frac{dH}{dt} = -\frac14\int d\Gamma_{\text{coll}}\bigl(f_1'f_2' - f_1f_2\bigr)\ln\frac{f_1'f_2'}{f_1f_2} \le 0 ,
> $$
>
> from $(x - y)\ln(x/y) \ge 0$; with $S = -k_BH$ up to additive constants, the kinetic equation has an arrow of time. Lanford derived the Boltzmann equation for short times from reversible hard-sphere motion in the dilute Boltzmann–Grad limit; Deng, Hani and Ma extended the derivation (cited by Mao). The arrow appears for suitably prepared statistical data in a controlled limit, while each collision stays reversible.
>
