Unit 1 explained why equilibrium is overwhelmingly likely; it did not explain how a system gets there, or why the equations of an experiment have a direction of time when Newton's do not ([[§C0.2 The Arrow of Time and the Statistical Program|§C0.2]]). Mao's answer starts from what is observed: a small part of a larger system, the rest carrying energy and information away. Eliminating the unobserved part exactly gives an equation with memory; a later approximation removes the memory and with it the reversibility. This section sets up the exact elimination. Its static counterpart, the marginal and the partial trace, is [[§C3.4 Composite Systems and Reduced States|§C3.4]].

**Setup.** A complete system $S + E$: an observed part $S$ with variables $s$ (classically $X_S$) and an environment $E$ with variables $e$ ($X_E$), together closed and obeying their microscopic equations.

> [!definition] Definition §C5.1.1: Open System; Reduced State
> An **open system** $S$ is the part of a larger system whose variables are followed. If the joint probability is $p_{SE}(s, e)$, its **reduced state** is the marginal
>
> $$
> p_S(s) = \sum_ep_{SE}(s, e) ,
> $$
>
> an integral for continuous variables; classically $\rho_S(X_S, t) = \int d\Gamma_E\,\rho_{SE}(X_S, X_E, t)$, with $\rho_{SE}$ obeying the Liouville equation $\partial_t\rho_{SE} + \{\rho_{SE}, H_{SE}\}_{\text{PB}} = 0$. The environment's variables are summed over, not set to zero.
>
> *Model:* [[§C3.4 Composite Systems and Reduced States#^def-c3-4-1|Def. §C3.4.1]] with $A = S$, $B = E$; *applies:* any joint state.
>
> *Source: Mao §5.1, box "Open system, marginal probability, and reduced state", eqs. (5.1)–(5.2) · Liouville: [[§C3.3 Liouville Theorems and Stationary Ensembles#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]]*

^def-c5-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.1.1: A Reduced State Does Not Evolve on Its Own
> Two complete states with the same $\rho_S(t)$ can carry different environmental records and so lead to different $\rho_S$ later. For a linear projection onto a fixed reference environment, eliminating $E$ exactly gives an equation of the schematic form
>
> $$
> \frac{d\rho_S(t)}{dt} = \underbrace{\mathcal L_{\text{inst}}\rho_S(t)}_{\text{instantaneous}} + \underbrace{I(t)}_{\text{initial environmental information}} + \underbrace{\int_0^td\tau\,K(\tau)\rho_S(t - \tau)}_{\text{memory}} ,
> $$
>
> where $K(\tau)$ is delayed feedback from the environment and $I(t)$ carries what was initially omitted, including initial $S$–$E$ correlations. No irreversible approximation has been made, and the microscopic forces need not be linear: the linearity is that of the evolution of a probability density.
>
> *Model:* [[§C5.1 Complete and Reduced Dynamics#^def-c5-1-1|Def. §C5.1.1]]; *applies:* exact; the form shown is schematic (projection-operator methods give it precisely).
>
> *Source: Mao §5.1, eq. (5.3) · Zwanzig, Nonequilibrium Statistical Mechanics (cited by Mao)*

^thm-c5-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: What elimination does and does not do
> Splitting off $E$ changes only what is kept track of: information can leave $S$, spread through $E$, and come back. The question for the rest of the chapter is when that return can be ignored. [[§C5.2 A Spring Chain: Memory from Reversible Springs#^mod-c5-2-1|Model §C5.2.1]] shows memory arising from reversible springs; [[§C5.4 Markov Dynamics and the Apparent Arrow of Time#^thm-c5-4-1|Theorem §C5.4.1]] shows where the arrow of time enters.
