A Markov generator on a discrete set of resolved states is a matrix of transition rates, and the evolution equation it defines is the master equation: a continuity equation for probability, flowing between states instead of being carried along trajectories. This section defines the four quantities that are easily confused (transition probability, rate, flow, current), derives the equation, and proves from its two structural rules that it always has a stationary distribution and that every other mode decays. Level B states a master equation with symmetric rates ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^def-b5-4-4|Def. §B5.4.4]]); here the rates are general.

**Setup.** States $i$ of the retained system $S$ (or cells of its phase space), probabilities $p_i(t)$, a time-homogeneous Markov process ([[§C5.4 Markov Dynamics and the Apparent Arrow of Time#^def-c5-4-1|Def. §C5.4.1]], [[§C5.4 Markov Dynamics and the Apparent Arrow of Time#^thm-c5-4-2|Theorem §C5.4.2]]).

> [!caution] Caution: Two pictures of moving probability
> In the exact Liouville picture ([[§C3.3 Liouville Theorems and Stationary Ensembles#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]]) a complete microstate of $S + E$ follows a smooth trajectory and carries its weight with it; probability in a fixed cell changes only by deterministic flux through its walls. In the master equation, $i$ labels a state of $S$ alone and probability jumps between states at given rates. The jumps belong to the effective description: they replace environmental motion that is no longer resolved, and are not jumps of the complete microstate. Constant rates are a good description when environmental feedback dephases long before a typical transition and recurrences come much later.

^cau-c5-5-1

> [!definition] Definition §C5.5.1: Transition Probability, Rate, Flow and Current
> 1. **Transition probability** $T_{ij}(t) = P(I_t = i\,|\,I_0 = j)$: dimensionless, with $T_{ij} \ge 0$, $\sum_iT_{ij} = 1$, and $T(t + s) = T(t)T(s)$.
> 2. **Transition rate** $W_{ij} \ge 0$ from $j$ to $i$ ($i \neq j$), conditioned on being in $j$: $T_{ij}(\Delta t) = W_{ij}\Delta t + o(\Delta t)$.
> 3. **One-way flow** $F_{ij} = W_{ij}p_j$: probability moved from $j$ to $i$ per unit time; zero if $p_j = 0$ however large $W_{ij}$ is.
> 4. **Net current** $J_{ij} = F_{ij} - F_{ji} = W_{ij}p_j - W_{ji}p_i$, with $J_{ij} = -J_{ji}$.
>
> The diagonal is fixed by conservation, $W_{jj} = -\sum_{i\neq j}W_{ij}$, so $-W_{jj}$ is the total escape rate from $j$, every column of $W$ sums to zero, and $T(\Delta t) = \mathbb 1 + W\Delta t + o(\Delta t)$.
>
> *Source: Mao §5.4, eqs. (5.23)–(5.30)*

^def-c5-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.5.1: The Master Equation
> $$
> \frac{dp_i}{dt} = \sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij} = \sum_{j\neq i}\bigl(W_{ij}p_j - W_{ji}p_i\bigr) = \sum_jW_{ij}p_j, \qquad \dot{\mathbf p} = W\mathbf p ,
> $$
>
> a discrete continuity equation, with solution $\mathbf p(t) = e^{tW}\mathbf p(0)$ for $t \ge 0$ and $T_{ij}(t) = [e^{tW}]_{ij}$. $W$ need not be symmetric; the column rule alone conserves probability.
>
> *Model:* [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^def-c5-5-1|Def. §C5.5.1]]; *applies:* time-homogeneous Markov process.
>
> *Source: Mao §5.4, eqs. (5.31)–(5.32), box "Master equation: definitions and equation" · PHY 510 Problem Set 7, Problem 2(a) · level-B home: [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^def-b5-4-4|Def. §B5.4.4]]*

^thm-c5-5-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1.** $p_i(t + \Delta t) = \sum_jT_{ij}(\Delta t)p_j(t) = p_i(t)\bigl(1 - \Delta t\sum_{k\neq i}W_{ki}\bigr) + \Delta t\sum_{j\neq i}W_{ij}p_j(t) + o(\Delta t)$: what stays plus what arrives.
>
> **Step 2.** Subtract $p_i(t)$, divide by $\Delta t$, let $\Delta t \to 0$; with $W_{ii} = -\sum_{k\neq i}W_{ki}$ the right side is $\sum_jW_{ij}p_j$.
>
> **Step 3** (column rule, second route). $\frac{d}{dt}\sum_ip_i = \sum_j\bigl(\sum_iW_{ij}\bigr)p_j$ must vanish for every $\mathbf p$, so each column sums to zero: the same rule as Step 2's diagonal.
>
> **Step 4.** $e^{tW}\mathbf p(0)$ solves $\dot{\mathbf p} = W\mathbf p$ ([[§C5.4 Markov Dynamics and the Apparent Arrow of Time#^thm-c5-4-2|Theorem §C5.4.2]]).

^der-c5-5-1

*Uses:* [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^def-c5-5-1|Def. §C5.5.1]], [[§C5.4 Markov Dynamics and the Apparent Arrow of Time#^thm-c5-4-2|Theorem §C5.4.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.5.2: The Effective Equation Has No Backward Stochastic Evolution
> For $t \ge 0$, $e^{tW}$ keeps probabilities nonnegative and normalized. The algebraic inverse $e^{-tW}$ generally does not: run backward, a relaxing distribution develops negative entries. So the master equation has no physical backward-in-time stochastic evolution, even when the complete $S + E$ dynamics is reversible.
>
> *Model:* [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^thm-c5-5-1|Theorem §C5.5.1]]; *applies:* any $W$ with some decaying mode.
>
> *Source: Mao §5.4 · example: [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^ex-c5-5-1|Example §C5.5.1]]*

^thm-c5-5-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Forward: for small $\Delta t$, $\mathbb 1 + W\Delta t$ has nonnegative entries ($W_{ij} \ge 0$ off the diagonal, $1 + W_{jj}\Delta t \ge 0$) and columns summing to $1$; products of such matrices keep both properties, and $e^{tW} = \lim(\mathbb 1 + Wt/n)^n$. Backward: in [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^ex-c5-5-1|Example §C5.5.1]], $p_1(-t) = p_1^{\text{ss}} + [p_1(0) - p_1^{\text{ss}}]e^{t/\tau}$ leaves $[0, 1]$ for large $t$ unless $p_1(0) = p_1^{\text{ss}}$.

^der-c5-5-2

*Uses:* [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^thm-c5-5-1|Theorem §C5.5.1]]

> [!example] Example §C5.5.1: Relaxation of Two States
> States $0, 1$ with $W_{10} = k_+$ ($0 \to 1$) and $W_{01} = k_-$ ($1 \to 0$):
>
> $$
> W = \begin{pmatrix}-k_+ & k_-\\ k_+ & -k_-\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \dot p_1 = k_+p_0 - k_-p_1 = k_+ - (k_+ + k_-)p_1 ,
> $$
>
> $$
> p_1(t) = p_1^{\text{ss}} + [p_1(0) - p_1^{\text{ss}}]e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad p_1^{\text{ss}} = \frac{k_+}{k_+ + k_-}, \quad p_0^{\text{ss}} = \frac{k_-}{k_+ + k_-}, \quad \tau = \frac{1}{k_+ + k_-} .
> $$
>
> The ratio of rates sets where the probabilities settle; their sum sets how fast. At stationarity the flows balance, $k_+p_0^{\text{ss}} = k_-p_1^{\text{ss}}$, though $k_+ \neq k_-$ in general. Under $t \to -t$ the decaying deviation would grow: the relaxation law is not time-reversal invariant.
>
> *Source: Mao §5.4, eqs. (5.33)–(5.36) and Figure 5.5*

^ex-c5-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.5.3: Every Rate Matrix Has a Stationary Distribution
> For a finite rate matrix $W$ (off-diagonal entries $\ge 0$, columns summing to zero):
>
> 1. $W$ has eigenvalue $0$, with left eigenvector $(1, \dots, 1)$;
> 2. there is a stationary distribution $\mathbf p^{\text{ss}}$: $W\mathbf p^{\text{ss}} = 0$, $p_i^{\text{ss}} \ge 0$, $\sum_ip_i^{\text{ss}} = 1$.
>
> If the process is irreducible (every state reachable from every other), $\mathbf p^{\text{ss}}$ is unique, all its entries are positive, and every initial distribution approaches it.
>
> *Model:* [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^thm-c5-5-1|Theorem §C5.5.1]]; *applies:* finitely many states.
>
> *Source: PHY 510 Problem Set 7, Problem 2(b) (proof worked here following the problem's hints; no official solution) · Mao §5.4, eq. (5.37) (statement of uniqueness and approach) · Linear Algebra: Perron–Frobenius*

^thm-c5-5-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (zero eigenvalue). The column rule is $(1, \dots, 1)W = 0$, so $0$ is an eigenvalue of $W^T$, hence of $W$.
>
> **Step 2** (real null vector). $W$ is real, so if $W\mathbf v = 0$ then $W(\operatorname{Re}\mathbf v) = W(\operatorname{Im}\mathbf v) = 0$, and one of them is nonzero; take $\mathbf v$ real.
>
> **Step 3** (absolute values). Let $u_i = |v_i|$. Row $i$ of $W\mathbf v = 0$ says $\sum_{j\neq i}W_{ij}v_j = -W_{ii}v_i$, so by the triangle inequality and $W_{ij} \ge 0$,
>
> $$
> (W\mathbf u)_i = W_{ii}|v_i| + \sum_{j\neq i}W_{ij}|v_j| \ge W_{ii}|v_i| + \Bigl|\sum_{j\neq i}W_{ij}v_j\Bigr| = W_{ii}|v_i| + |W_{ii}||v_i| = 0 ,
> $$
>
> using $W_{ii} \le 0$. Summing over $i$, $\sum_i(W\mathbf u)_i = 0$ by the column rule; a sum of nonnegative terms that vanishes has every term zero. So $W\mathbf u = 0$ with $\mathbf u \ge 0$, $\mathbf u \neq 0$.
>
> **Step 4.** $\mathbf p^{\text{ss}} = \mathbf u/\sum_iu_i$. Positivity and uniqueness under irreducibility, and the approach, are the Perron–Frobenius theorem applied to $e^{tW}$, whose entries are then all positive for $t > 0$; with [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^thm-c5-5-4|Theorem §C5.5.4]] every other mode dies out.

^der-c5-5-3

*Uses:* [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^thm-c5-5-1|Theorem §C5.5.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.5.4: Every Nonstationary Mode Decays
> Every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of a rate matrix lies in a disk $|\lambda + \gamma_j| \le \gamma_j$ for some $j$, where $\gamma_j = -W_{jj}$ is the escape rate from $j$. Hence every nonzero eigenvalue has $\operatorname{Re}\lambda < 0$. Expanding in eigenvectors $W\mathbf v_\alpha = \lambda_\alpha\mathbf v_\alpha$,
>
> $$
> \mathbf p(t) = \mathbf p^{\text{ss}} + \sum_{\alpha\neq0}c_\alpha e^{\lambda_\alpha t}\mathbf v_\alpha, \qquad \tau_{\text{rel}} = \frac{1}{-\operatorname{Re}\lambda_\ast} ,
> $$
>
> with $\lambda_\ast$ the nonzero eigenvalue present in $\mathbf p(0)$ with real part closest to zero. Each $\mathbf v_\alpha$ ($\alpha \neq 0$) has components summing to zero: it only redistributes probability. Complex eigenvalues give damped oscillations.
>
> *Model:* [[§C5.5 The Master Equation#^thm-c5-5-1|Theorem §C5.5.1]]; *applies:* finitely many states; the expansion assumes a complete set of eigenvectors.
>
> *Source: PHY 510 Problem Set 7, Problem 2(c) (worked here; no official solution) · Mao §5.4, eqs. (5.38)–(5.39)*

^thm-c5-5-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Gershgorin, by columns). Every eigenvalue lies in some disk centred at $W_{jj} = -\gamma_j$ with radius $\sum_{i\neq j}|W_{ij}| = \sum_{i\neq j}W_{ij} = \gamma_j$ (the column rule).
>
> **Step 2.** With $\lambda = x + iy$: $(x + \gamma_j)^2 + y^2 \le \gamma_j^2$, so $x^2 + y^2 \le -2\gamma_jx$. Hence $x \le 0$, and $x = 0$ forces $y = 0$, $\lambda = 0$.
>
> **Step 3.** A mode evolves as $e^{\lambda t} = e^{xt}e^{iyt}$: it decays on the time $-1/x > 0$ and oscillates at frequency $y$. The slowest decay dominates at long times.
>
> **Step 4.** $(1, \dots, 1)W\mathbf v_\alpha = 0 = \lambda_\alpha(1, \dots, 1)\mathbf v_\alpha$, so for $\lambda_\alpha \neq 0$ the components of $\mathbf v_\alpha$ sum to zero.
