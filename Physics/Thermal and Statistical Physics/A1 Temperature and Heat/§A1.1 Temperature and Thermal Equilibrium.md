Thermal physics starts by turning the everyday words "hot" and "cold" into a measurable quantity. Two questions have to be answered first: when do two bodies stop exchanging energy, and why does the reading of one thermometer let us compare two different bodies? The answers are thermal equilibrium and the zeroth law, which together make temperature a well-defined quantity. The section ends with the scales used to give it numbers.

## Thermal equilibrium

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.1: Thermal Contact; Thermal Equilibrium
> A thermodynamic **system** is a macroscopic body or collection of bodies, made of a huge number of molecules (of order $10^{23}$). Two systems are in **thermal contact** if energy can pass between them because of the random molecular motion at their common boundary, as when they touch. They are in **thermal equilibrium** if, in thermal contact, no net energy passes between them. Two systems that are not in contact are in thermal equilibrium if no net energy would pass between them once they were brought into contact. A system is in thermal equilibrium with itself (internal equilibrium) if all of its parts are in thermal equilibrium with one another.
>
> Experience shows that two systems left in thermal contact long enough reach thermal equilibrium. Systems, their surroundings and the kinds of boundary are described in more detail in [[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems|§A3.1]].
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.1*

^def-a1-1-1

> [!principle] Principle §A1.1.1: Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics
> If system $A$ is in thermal equilibrium with system $B$, and $B$ is in thermal equilibrium with system $C$, then $A$ is in thermal equilibrium with $C$.
>
> *Domain: macroscopic systems, each in internal equilibrium.*
> *Source: UP2 §1.1*

^pr-a1-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why "zeroth"
> The first, second and third laws had been numbered by the time Ralph Fowler stated this one explicitly in the 1930s. It is logically prior to them, since the others use temperature, so it got the smaller number. It is not obvious: it says that a property of $A$ and $C$ separately decides whether they would exchange energy, without ever bringing them together.

^rem-a1-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.1.2: Existence of Temperature
> There is a quantity, the **temperature** $T$, such that two systems are in thermal equilibrium if and only if they have the same temperature.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.1*

^thm-a1-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $A \sim B$ for "$A$ is in thermal equilibrium with $B$". The relation is symmetric, because "no net energy passes between them" does not distinguish the two systems; it is reflexive, because a system in internal equilibrium exchanges no net energy with an identical copy of itself; and it is transitive by the zeroth law ([[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^pr-a1-1-1|Principle §A1.1.1]]). So $\sim$ is an equivalence relation, and it splits all systems into disjoint classes of mutually equilibrated systems. Give each class a number: put a small reference system (a thermometer) into contact with any member of the class and record the value of some property of the thermometer that varies with its state, such as the length of a liquid column. By transitivity, every member of the same class gives the same reading, so the label belongs to the class and not to the member chosen. That label is the temperature, and two systems are in equilibrium exactly when they lie in the same class, that is, when their temperatures agree.

^der-a1-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^def-a1-1-1|Def. §A1.1.1]], [[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^pr-a1-1-1|Principle §A1.1.1]], [[§22 Equivalence Relations and Partitions#^prop-22-1|493 Prop. §22.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.2: Thermometer; Temperature (Operational)
> A **thermometer** is a system, small compared with what it measures, with a **thermometric property** that changes reproducibly with temperature: the volume of a liquid (alcohol, mercury), the electrical resistance of a wire, the colour of a liquid crystal, the infrared radiation emitted (pyrometer, ear thermometer). Operationally, **temperature** is what a thermometer reads after it has come to thermal equilibrium with the system.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.1–1.2*

^def-a1-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: What the thermometer actually measures
> - A thermometer measures its own temperature. It is the zeroth law that lets us read this as the temperature of the body it touches, and say that two bodies that give the same reading would not exchange energy.
> - The zeroth law gives *equal or not equal*, not *hotter or colder*. Which body is hotter is fixed by the direction of the spontaneous energy flow, from the higher to the lower temperature ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry|§A1.3]]); why it never goes the other way is the second law ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law|§A4.3]]).
> - Microscopically, temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the random translational motion of the molecules ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed|§A2.2]]). Our skin, by contrast, senses the rate of heat flow, not temperature ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer|§A1.5]]).

^rem-a1-1-2

## Temperature scales

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.3: Celsius and Fahrenheit Scales
> A temperature scale is fixed by assigning numbers to two reproducible temperatures and dividing the interval evenly. The usual fixed points are the freezing and boiling points of water at standard atmospheric pressure (1 atm).
> - **Celsius scale:** freezing point $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, boiling point $100\ ^\circ\mathrm C$.
> - **Fahrenheit scale:** freezing point $32\ ^\circ\mathrm F$, boiling point $212\ ^\circ\mathrm F$.
>
> The same interval spans 100 Celsius degrees and 180 Fahrenheit degrees, so $\Delta T_F = \tfrac95\,\Delta T_C$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.2*

^def-a1-1-3

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.4: Absolute Zero; Kelvin Scale
> **Absolute zero** is the lowest possible temperature, at which the random molecular motion is as small as possible (the minimum allowed by quantum mechanics); experiments confirm that such a limit exists. An **absolute temperature scale** has its zero at absolute zero. The **Kelvin scale** is the absolute scale of the SI: its unit is the **kelvin** (K, written without a degree sign), $0\ \mathrm K$ is absolute zero, and its degree has the size of the Celsius degree, so that water freezes at $273.15\ \mathrm K$ and boils at $373.15\ \mathrm K$, and $\Delta T_K = \Delta T_C$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.2*

^def-a1-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.1.3: Conversion between Scales
>
> $$
> T_K = T_C + 273.15, \qquad T_F = \tfrac95\, T_C + 32, \qquad T_C = \tfrac59\,(T_F - 32).
> $$
>
> To convert between Fahrenheit and kelvin, go through Celsius.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.2, Table 1.1*

^thm-a1-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each scale divides the same physical interval evenly, so any two scales are related by a linear function $T' = aT + b$, and two points fix $a$ and $b$. For Fahrenheit against Celsius, the points $(0, 32)$ and $(100, 212)$ give $a = 180/100 = 9/5$ and $b = 32$. For kelvin against Celsius the degree has the same size, $a = 1$, and the freezing point gives $b = 273.15$.

^der-a1-1-3

> [!caution] Caution: Temperatures and temperature differences
> - Convert a *temperature difference* with the factor alone: a rise of $10\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ is a rise of $18\ ^\circ\mathrm F$ and of $10\ \mathrm K$, not of $50\ ^\circ\mathrm F$ or $283\ \mathrm K$. Coefficients "per $^\circ\mathrm C$" and "per K" are therefore the same number.
> - Wherever a temperature itself enters multiplicatively (ratios, $T^4$, the ideal gas law), it must be absolute, in kelvins.

^cau-a1-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.5: Triple Point; Constant-Volume Gas Thermometer
> The **triple point** of water is the unique combination of temperature and pressure at which ice, liquid water and water vapour coexist in equilibrium ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^def-a1-4-2|Def. §A1.4.2]]). Unlike the freezing and boiling points it does not depend on a chosen pressure, and the kelvin scale described in UP assigns it $T_{\mathrm{TP}} = 273.16\ \mathrm K$ (that is, $0.01\ ^\circ\mathrm C$). The standard thermometer is the **constant-volume gas thermometer**: a fixed volume of gas whose pressure $p$ is measured, with
>
> $$
> T = T_{\mathrm{TP}}\,\frac{p}{p_{\mathrm{TP}}},
> $$
>
> where $p_{\mathrm{TP}}$ is its pressure at the triple point. Different gases give slightly different readings, but the readings agree when extrapolated to zero gas density, and zero pressure then corresponds to absolute zero.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.2*

^def-a1-1-5

> [!remark] Remark: Why a gas, and why one fixed point
> All gases at low density behave alike (the ideal gas law, [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas|§A2.1]]), so a gas thermometer gives a scale that does not depend on the properties of one particular material, as a mercury-in-glass scale would. Because the scale is proportional to $p$ and must vanish at absolute zero, a single fixed point is enough to set its size. Gas thermometers are bulky and slow to reach equilibrium, so they serve to calibrate the practical thermometers of [[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^def-a1-1-2|Def. §A1.1.2]].

^rem-a1-1-3

## Example

> [!example] Example §A1.1.1: Room Temperature on Three Scales
> Room temperature is taken in physics to be $T_C = 25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. By [[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]],
>
> $$
> T_F = \tfrac95(25) + 32 = 77\ ^\circ\mathrm F, \qquad T_K = 25 + 273.15 = 298\ \mathrm K .
> $$
>
> By contrast, warming the room *by* $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ (say from $0$ to $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$) is a change of $45\ ^\circ\mathrm F$ and of $25\ \mathrm K$: differences take the factor and drop the offset.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 1.1*

^ex-a1-1-1

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The derivation of [[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]] is the statement that an equivalence relation partitions a set into classes ([[§22 Equivalence Relations and Partitions#^def-22-1|493 Def. §22.1]], [[§22 Equivalence Relations and Partitions#^prop-22-1|493 Prop. §22.1]], Group Theory); temperature is a label of the classes.
> - Temperature as a measure of molecular kinetic energy is derived from kinetic theory in [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed|§A2.2]]; a scale that does not depend on any substance at all follows from the Carnot cycle ([[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle|§A4.4]]).
