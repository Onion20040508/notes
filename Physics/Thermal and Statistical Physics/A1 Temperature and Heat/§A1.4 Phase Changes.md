The rule $Q = mc\,\Delta T$ of [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^law-a1-3-2|Law §A1.3.2]] fails when ice melts or water boils: heat goes in and the temperature does not move. This section describes when the phases of a substance coexist, summarised in its phase diagram, and how much heat a change of phase takes (the latent heat). With both, the calorimetry of [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^thm-a1-3-4|Theorem §A1.3.4]] extends to bodies that melt, freeze, boil or condense.

## Phases and the phase diagram

> [!definition] Definition §A1.4.1: Phase Transitions
> A substance can be solid, liquid or gas (its **phases**). In **melting** (fusion) a solid becomes liquid, and the reverse is **freezing**; in **vaporization** (evaporation; rapid evaporation throughout the liquid is **boiling**) a liquid becomes gas, and the reverse is **condensation**; in **sublimation** a solid becomes gas directly, and frost forming on a cold window is the reverse. A substance melts and freezes at its **melting point** and boils and condenses at its **boiling point**; both depend on pressure. High pressure favours the denser phase, so it usually raises both: water boils at $100\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ at $1.00\ \mathrm{atm}$, above it at higher pressure, below it at lower pressure.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.5*

^def-a1-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.4.2: Phase Diagram; Triple Point; Critical Point
> The **phase diagram** of a substance is a graph of pressure $p$ ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-3|CM Def. §A9.1.3]]) against temperature $T$, divided into the regions where each phase is stable. On the boundary curves two phases coexist in equilibrium:
> - the **vaporization curve** (liquid–vapour) gives the boiling point at each pressure; it ends at the **critical point**, at the **critical temperature** and **critical pressure**, beyond which liquid and gas can no longer be distinguished and the substance is a **supercritical fluid**;
> - the **fusion curve** (solid–liquid) gives the melting point at each pressure;
> - the **sublimation curve** (solid–vapour) lies below the **triple point**, the single temperature and pressure at which solid, liquid and vapour coexist. Below the triple-point pressure there is no liquid phase.
>
> For water the triple point is at $273.16\ \mathrm K$ ($0.01\ ^\circ\mathrm C$) and $611.2\ \mathrm{Pa}$ (about $0.006\ \mathrm{atm}$), and the critical point at $374\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ and $218\ \mathrm{atm}$. Carbon dioxide is supercritical above $31.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ at high enough pressure; it has no liquid phase at $1\ \mathrm{atm}$, where solid $\mathrm{CO_2}$ (dry ice) sublimes at $-78\ ^\circ\mathrm C$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.5, Figure 1.12*

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the diagram for water
> - Along the vaporization curve the boiling point rises with pressure, from $100\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ at $1\ \mathrm{atm}$ to $374\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ at $218\ \mathrm{atm}$. A pressure cooker keeps water liquid above $100\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, so food cooks faster.
> - Water is unusual: ice is less dense than liquid water, so its fusion curve slopes backwards (negative slope) and the melting point *falls* as pressure rises. At a fixed temperature just below $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, enough pressure melts ice; snow pressed under a car's tyres melts and refreezes as an ice layer.
> - The triple point is a better calibration point than the melting point at $1\ \mathrm{atm}$ ($273.15\ \mathrm K$) because it needs no chosen pressure ([[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^def-a1-1-5|Def. §A1.1.5]]).

^rem-a1-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.4.3: Vapour; Vapour Pressure; Boiling
> The gas phase of a substance below its boiling temperature is called a **vapour**. A liquid in a closed container at fixed temperature evaporates until the pressure of its vapour reaches the **vapour pressure**, which depends on the substance and the temperature; there the rates of evaporation and condensation balance and liquid and vapour coexist. A liquid **boils** when its vapour pressure equals the total ambient pressure, so the boiling point at a given pressure is the temperature at which the vapour pressure equals that pressure: the vaporization curve of the phase diagram is the vapour pressure as a function of temperature.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.5*

^def-a1-4-3

> [!remark] Remark: Why an open pot boils dry
> In a closed container, pure water and steam at $100\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ and $1\ \mathrm{atm}$ coexist indefinitely, boiling and condensing at equal rates. Over an open pot, part of the ambient pressure is due to air, so the pressure of the steam itself is below the vapour pressure; fewer molecules return than leave, and the water boils away. Solids also have a nonzero vapour pressure, which is why snow and ice cubes in a freezer slowly sublime.

^rem-a1-4-2

## Latent heat

> [!definition] Definition §A1.4.4: Latent Heats
> The **heat of fusion** $L_f$ is the energy per unit mass needed to melt a solid at its melting point; the same energy per unit mass is released when the liquid freezes. The **heat of vaporization** $L_v$ is the energy per unit mass needed to vaporize a liquid (released on condensation), and the **heat of sublimation** $L_s$ the energy per unit mass to turn a solid directly into vapour. They are called **latent** ("hidden") heats because the energy enters or leaves without changing the temperature.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.5*

^def-a1-4-4

> [!law] Law §A1.4.1: Heat of a Phase Change
> While a substance changes phase at constant pressure, its temperature stays fixed at the transition temperature, and the heat absorbed by a mass $m$ that changes phase is
>
> $$
> Q = \pm\, m L_f \ (\text{melting} +,\ \text{freezing} -), \qquad Q = \pm\, m L_v \ (\text{vaporization} +,\ \text{condensation} -),
> $$
>
> and $Q = \pm m L_s$ for sublimation, with the latent heats measured constants of the material. Values at $1\ \mathrm{atm}$:
>
> | Substance | melting point | $L_f$ (kJ/kg) | boiling point | $L_v$ (kJ/kg) |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | Nitrogen | $-210.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | 25.5 | $-195.8\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | 201 |
> | Ethanol | $-114\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | 104 | $78.3\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | 854 |
> | Mercury | $-38.9\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | 11.8 | $357\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | 272 |
> | Water | $0.00\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | 334 | $100.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | 2256 |
> | Lead | $327\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | 24.5 | $1750\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | 871 |
> | Copper | $1083\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | 134 | $2595\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | 5069 |
>
> In general $L_v > L_f$.
>
> *Valid when:* the transition happens at the tabulated temperature and pressure. A latent heat depends on the temperature at which the change takes place: water evaporating at body temperature, $37\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, needs $L_v \approx 2430\ \mathrm{kJ/kg}$, about 8% more than at $100\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, because the molecules have less thermal kinetic energy to start with.
> *Source: UP2 §1.5, eqs. (1.7)–(1.8), Table 1.4*

^law-a1-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Where the latent heat goes
> - Melting must partly overcome the attractive forces that hold molecules in place in the solid; vaporizing must overcome them completely, separating the molecules by large distances. The energy goes into intermolecular potential energy, not into random kinetic energy, so the temperature does not rise; and vaporizing needs much more energy than melting, $L_v > L_f$.
> - In freezing and condensation the attractive forces do work as the molecules come together, and this energy has to leave, usually as heat. Condensation therefore happens on cold surfaces and warms them: dew on a glass of iced tea speeds the melting of the ice. Growers spray orchards with water on frosty nights, and the heat released as it freezes keeps the fruit from dropping below $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$.
> - Because evaporation and condensation also happen below the boiling point, phase changes stabilise climate: in humid regions much of the incoming heat goes into evaporating water, so air temperatures rarely exceed about $38\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, and they rarely fall below the dew point, where condensation releases heat.
> - The heat of sublimation at a given temperature equals the heat of fusion plus the heat of vaporization at that temperature: going from solid to vapour directly or through the liquid connects the same two states. The precise reason, that the heat absorbed at constant pressure is the change of a state variable, belongs to level B (planned).

^rem-a1-4-3

> [!remark] Remark: Problem-solving with phase changes
> 1. Decide which bodies change temperature and which change phase. When it is not obvious, first solve as if no phase changed; if the result passes a melting or boiling point, redo the problem in stages.
> 2. Each stage contributes one term: $mc\,\Delta T$ for a temperature change within one phase ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^law-a1-3-2|Law §A1.3.2]]), $\pm mL$ for a change of phase ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^law-a1-4-1|Law §A1.4.1]]).
> 3. In a calorimetry problem all terms go into the single equation $\sum Q = 0$ ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^thm-a1-3-4|Theorem §A1.3.4]]). Check at the end that no stage was missed: a temperature change must not carry a body past a transition you did not include.

^rem-a1-4-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.4.1: Heating Curve of Ice
> Heat $1.00\ \mathrm{kg}$ of ice at $1\ \mathrm{atm}$ from $-20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ to steam at $120\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, keeping all the vapour in the system. With $c_{\text{ice}} = 2090$, $c_{\text{water}} = 4186$ and $c_{\text{steam}} = 2020\ \mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$ (at constant pressure):
>
> | stage | law | heat |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | ice, $-20 \to 0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | $mc\,\Delta T$ | $41.8\ \mathrm{kJ}$ |
> | melting at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | $mL_f$ | $334\ \mathrm{kJ}$ |
> | water, $0 \to 100\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | $mc\,\Delta T$ | $418.6\ \mathrm{kJ}$ |
> | boiling at $100\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | $mL_v$ | $2256\ \mathrm{kJ}$ |
> | steam, $100 \to 120\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ | $mc\,\Delta T$ | $40.4\ \mathrm{kJ}$ |
>
> In total $3.09\ \mathrm{MJ}$, of which the two plateaus take 84%: boiling the water away needs 5.4 times the heat that warms it from $0$ to $100\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. The process runs in exact reverse on cooling.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.5, Figure 1.15*

> [!example] Example §A1.4.2: Ice Cubes in a Soda
> Three ice cubes of $6.0\ \mathrm g$ each at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ are dropped into $0.25\ \mathrm{kg}$ of soda (specific heat of water) at $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ in a foam cup; find the final temperature. *Does all the ice melt?* Cooling the soda to $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ releases $(0.25)(4186)(20)\ \mathrm J = 20.9\ \mathrm{kJ}$, while melting $m_{\text{ice}} = 0.018\ \mathrm{kg}$ needs $m_{\text{ice}}L_f = 6.0\ \mathrm{kJ}$, so yes, and $T_f > 0$. The ice melts, then the meltwater warms from $0$ to $T_f$ while the soda cools from $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ to $T_f$:
>
> $$
> m_{\text{ice}}L_f + m_{\text{ice}}c_w\,(T_f - 0) + m_{\text{soda}}c_w\,(T_f - 20\ ^\circ\mathrm C) = 0
> \;\Longrightarrow\;
> T_f = \frac{m_{\text{soda}}c_w(20\ ^\circ\mathrm C) - m_{\text{ice}}L_f}{(m_{\text{soda}} + m_{\text{ice}})\,c_w} = \frac{20\,930\ \mathrm J - 6012\ \mathrm J}{1122\ \mathrm{J/K}} = 13\ ^\circ\mathrm C .
> $$
>
> Without the latent heat, mixing with $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ water would only bring the soda to $19\ ^\circ\mathrm C$: melting accounts for most of the cooling, though the ice is only 7% of the mass.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 1.9*

^ex-a1-4-2

> [!example] Example §A1.4.3: Cooling by Sweating
> How much sweat must evaporate from the skin of a $70.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ person (average specific heat $3480\ \mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$) to lower their temperature by $1.00\ \mathrm K$? At body temperature $L_v = 2.42\times10^6\ \mathrm{J/kg}$. The heat taken from the body equals the heat absorbed by the evaporating water:
>
> $$
> m_w = \frac{m c\,\Delta T}{L_v} = \frac{(70.0\ \mathrm{kg})(3480\ \mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)})(1.00\ \mathrm K)}{2.42\times10^6\ \mathrm{J/kg}} = 0.101\ \mathrm{kg} ,
> $$
>
> about $101\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ of water to drink back, under a third of a $355\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ soft-drink can. Evaporation well below the boiling point, with its large latent heat, is an efficient coolant; humid air slows it, which is why humid heat feels oppressive.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 3*
