The liquid thermometer of [[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^def-a1-1-2|Def. §A1.1.2]] works because bodies change size when their temperature changes. This section says by how much: for small temperature changes the change in length, area or volume is proportional to the temperature change and to the original size. It then asks what happens when the expansion is prevented: the body is put under a stress that can be very large.

## Why bodies expand

> [!remark] Remark: Molecular origin
> In a solid each atom vibrates about an equilibrium position, held by the forces of its neighbours. The potential energy between two neighbours rises more steeply when they are pushed together than when they are pulled apart, so the curve is asymmetric. At a higher temperature the vibrational energy is larger, and the turning points of the vibration ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^def-a4-7-1|CM Def. §A4.7.1]]) move out further on the gentle (outer) side than in on the steep side: the *average* separation grows, and the body expands. In an isotropic material (no preferred direction, the usual case) every length grows by the same fraction, so a freely expanding body keeps its shape and only its size changes.

^rem-a1-2-1

## Linear and volume expansion

> [!law] Law §A1.2.1: Linear Thermal Expansion
> A length $L$ of a solid changes with temperature as
>
> $$
> \frac{dL}{dT} = \alpha L ,
> $$
>
> where the **coefficient of linear expansion** $\alpha$ is a property of the material (unit $1/\mathrm K$, the same number as $1/{}^\circ\mathrm C$). Because $\alpha$ is small and nearly constant, for a temperature change $\Delta T$
>
> $$
> \Delta L = \alpha L\,\Delta T , \qquad L' = L\,(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T).
> $$
>
> Typical values ($10^{-6}/\mathrm K$): aluminium 25, copper 17, steel 12, concrete about 12, ordinary glass 9, Pyrex 3, Invar (nickel–iron alloy) 0.9, quartz 0.4.
>
> *Valid when:* the solid expands freely, with no phase change; $\alpha$ varies slowly with $T$, so the linear form needs $\alpha\,\Delta T \ll 1$ (in practice accurate even for changes of tens of kelvins). Some materials (for instance polymers and carbon fibres) have a strongly temperature-dependent or direction-dependent $\alpha$.
> *Source: UP2 §1.3, eqs. (1.1)–(1.2), Table 1.2; PHY 287, week 2 slides*

^law-a1-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: The linear form is a first-order approximation
> With $\alpha$ constant, $dL/dT = \alpha L$ integrates to $L' = L\,e^{\alpha\Delta T} = L\bigl(1 + \alpha\Delta T + \tfrac12(\alpha\Delta T)^2 + \dots\bigr)$, and $\Delta L = \alpha L \Delta T$ keeps the first-order term of the Taylor expansion ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]). The neglected term is a fraction $\tfrac12\alpha\Delta T$ of the kept one: for steel heated by $100\ \mathrm K$, $\alpha\Delta T = 1.2\times10^{-3}$, and the correction is below one part in a thousand of $\Delta L$. A 1275 m steel bridge span that sees temperatures from $-15$ to $40\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ changes length by $\alpha L \Delta T = (12\times10^{-6}/\mathrm K)(1275\ \mathrm m)(55\ \mathrm K) = 0.84\ \mathrm m$, which is why bridges and rails have expansion joints.

^rem-a1-2-2

> [!law] Law §A1.2.2: Volume Expansion
> The volume $V$ of a solid, liquid or gas changes with temperature as $dV/dT = \beta V$, and for small $\Delta T$
>
> $$
> \Delta V = \beta V\,\Delta T ,
> $$
>
> with the **coefficient of volume expansion** $\beta$ a property of the material. For liquids only $\beta$ is defined, since a liquid's length and area depend on its container. Typical values ($10^{-6}/\mathrm K$): mercury 180, water (above $4\ ^\circ\mathrm C$) 210, glycerin 500, gasoline 950, ethyl alcohol 1100, air at atmospheric pressure about 3400.
>
> *Valid when:* no phase change, $\beta\,\Delta T \ll 1$; for a gas, at constant pressure (otherwise the volume is set by the pressure as well). Water between $0$ and $4\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ is an exception (below).
> *Source: UP2 §1.3, eq. (1.4), Table 1.2*

^law-a1-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.2.3: Area and Volume Expansion of a Solid
> For an isotropic solid with coefficient of linear expansion $\alpha$,
>
> $$
> \Delta A = 2\alpha A\,\Delta T, \qquad \Delta V = 3\alpha V\,\Delta T, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \beta = 3\alpha .
> $$
>
> More generally, if a solid expands with different coefficients $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3$ along three perpendicular directions, then $\beta = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \alpha_3$. Holes and cavities expand exactly as the material that would fill them.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.3, eqs. (1.3)–(1.4); PHY 287, Problem Set 1*

^thm-a1-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take a rectangular block with edges $L_1, L_2, L_3$ along the three directions, so $V = L_1 L_2 L_3$. By the product rule and [[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^law-a1-2-1|Law §A1.2.1]] for each edge, $dL_i/dT = \alpha_i L_i$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dV}{dT} = \frac{dL_1}{dT}L_2L_3 + L_1\frac{dL_2}{dT}L_3 + L_1L_2\frac{dL_3}{dT} = (\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \alpha_3)\,V ,
> $$
>
> so $\beta = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \alpha_3$, which is $3\alpha$ when all three are equal. A body of any shape is a union of small blocks, each of which grows by this fraction, so the result holds for any shape. The same computation with $A = L_1 L_2$ gives $\Delta A = 2\alpha A\,\Delta T$. A hole: since every length in the material, including the distances across the hole, grows by the same fraction $\alpha\Delta T$, the hole grows exactly as the removed piece would have.

^der-a1-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^law-a1-2-1|Law §A1.2.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Gases, and water's anomaly
> - For a gas at constant pressure the ideal gas law ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas|§A2.1]]) gives $V \propto T$, so $\beta = (1/V)(dV/dT) = 1/T$, with $T$ in kelvins: at $293\ \mathrm K$, $\beta = 3.4\times10^{-3}/\mathrm K$, the tabulated value for air. A gas expands several times more than a liquid and about a hundred times more than a solid, which is why heated air rises and drives natural convection ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer|§A1.5]]).
> - Water expands on warming only above $4\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. Between $0$ and $4\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ it *contracts* on warming, so it is densest at $4\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ (only $0.012\%$ denser than at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$). A pond cooling in winter turns over until it is all at $4\ ^\circ\mathrm C$; colder water then stays on top and freezes there, and the ice insulates the liquid below, in which fish survive.

^rem-a1-2-3

## Thermal stress

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.2.4: Thermal Stress in a Clamped Rod
> A rod held rigidly at both ends, so that it cannot change length, and whose temperature changes by $\Delta T$, is under a stress
>
> $$
> \frac{F}{A} = Y\alpha\,\Delta T ,
> $$
>
> where $Y$ is its Young's modulus. The stress is compressive when the rod is heated and tensile when it is cooled, and it does not depend on the rod's length or cross-section.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.3 (Thermal Stress)*

^thm-a1-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Think of the stress as building up in two steps. First let the rod expand freely: by [[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^law-a1-2-1|Law §A1.2.1]] it lengthens by $\Delta L = \alpha L_0\,\Delta T$. Then the walls push it back to its original length $L_0$: an elastic compression of the same $\Delta L$, which by the definition of Young's modulus ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-2|CM Def. §A7.2.2]]) needs a stress $F/A = Y\,\Delta L/L_0 = Y\alpha\,\Delta T$. The length $L_0$ cancels, and $A$ appears only in the stress $F/A$ itself.

^der-a1-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^law-a1-2-1|Law §A1.2.1]], [[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-2|CM Def. §A7.2.2]], [[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^law-a7-2-1|CM Law §A7.2.1]]

![[ph-th-a1-2-1.svg]]
*The two steps of the derivation (expansion exaggerated). Top: the rod, in blue, fits between the walls at $T_0$. Middle: free to expand at $T_0 + \Delta T$, it would lengthen by $\Delta L$. Bottom: the walls push it back with the red forces $\vec F$, a compressive stress that undoes the strain $\Delta L/L_0$.*

> [!remark] Remark: Living with thermal stress
> Engineers avoid thermal stress by letting parts move (gaps between concrete slabs, expansion joints), or by pairing materials with equal $\alpha$: reinforcing rods in concrete are steel because steel and concrete have nearly the same $\alpha$. Where two bonded materials have different $\alpha$, the mismatch bends them: a **bimetallic strip**, two metals joined along their length, curves towards the less-expanding metal when heated, which moves a pointer or opens a switch in older thermostats.

^rem-a1-2-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.2.1: Gasoline Overflowing a Steel Tank
> A $60.0\ \mathrm L$ steel tank is filled to the brim with gasoline at $15.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$; both warm to $35.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. How much spills? Both volumes grow ([[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^law-a1-2-2|Law §A1.2.2]]); the tank's interior grows as if it were solid steel ([[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^thm-a1-2-3|Theorem §A1.2.3]]), with $\beta_s = 35\times10^{-6}/\mathrm K$. The spill is the difference:
>
> $$
> V_{\text{spill}} = (\beta_{\text{gas}} - \beta_s)\,V\,\Delta T = (950 - 35)\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm K^{-1}\,(60.0\ \mathrm L)(20.0\ \mathrm K) = 1.10\ \mathrm L .
> $$
>
> Almost 2% of the tank. Capping it tightly does not help: liquids resist compression with very large forces (large bulk modulus, [[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-3|CM Def. §A7.2.3]]), so the tank would leak or burst; rigid containers are given air gaps.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 1.3*

^ex-a1-2-1

> [!example] Example §A1.2.2: Concrete Slabs on a Hot Day
> Concrete highway blocks are laid edge to edge with no gaps at $5\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. On a $38\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ day they cannot expand, so by [[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^thm-a1-2-4|Theorem §A1.2.4]], with $Y = 20\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{N/m^2}$ and $\alpha = 12\times10^{-6}/\mathrm K$,
>
> $$
> \frac{F}{A} = Y\alpha\,\Delta T = (20\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{N/m^2})(12\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm K^{-1})(33\ \mathrm K) = 7.9\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{N/m^2} .
> $$
>
> This is below the compressive strength of concrete ($20\times10^6\ \mathrm{N/m^2}$), so the blocks do not crush, but above its shear strength ($2\times10^6\ \mathrm{N/m^2}$), so edges may chip.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 1.4*

^ex-a1-2-2

> [!example] Example §A1.2.3: A Pendulum Clock in the Cold
> A pendulum clock with a light aluminium rod ($\alpha = 2.4\times10^{-5}/\mathrm K$) keeps perfect time at $20.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. Its period is $\tau = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ (Oscillations, Waves and Optics, planned), so by the chain rule $d\tau/\tau = \tfrac12\,dL/L$, and with [[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^law-a1-2-1|Law §A1.2.1]]
>
> $$
> \frac{\Delta\tau}{\tau} = \frac12\,\frac{\Delta L}{L} = \frac12\,\alpha\,\Delta T .
> $$
>
> At $10.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, $\Delta\tau/\tau = \tfrac12(2.4\times10^{-5})(-10\ \mathrm K) = -1.2\times10^{-4}$: the shorter rod swings faster. In one true day ($86\,400\ \mathrm s$) the clock counts $(1.2\times10^{-4})(86\,400) = 10\ \mathrm s$ too many, so it runs fast and shows a later time; in hot weather it runs slow. To stay within $1\ \mathrm s$ per day it needs $\tfrac12\alpha\,|\Delta T| \le 1/86\,400$, that is $|\Delta T| \le 0.96\ \mathrm K$. An Invar rod ($\alpha = 0.9\times10^{-6}/\mathrm K$) relaxes this to about $\pm 26\ \mathrm K$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 1*

^ex-a1-2-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $\beta = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \alpha_3$ is the product rule ([[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]]); the fractional-change rule of [[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^ex-a1-2-3|Example §A1.2.3]], $d\tau/\tau = \tfrac12\,dL/L$, is the chain rule ([[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]) applied to $\ln\tau = \tfrac12\ln L + \text{const}$.
> - The linear law is the first-order Taylor polynomial of $L_0 e^{\alpha\Delta T}$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]).
