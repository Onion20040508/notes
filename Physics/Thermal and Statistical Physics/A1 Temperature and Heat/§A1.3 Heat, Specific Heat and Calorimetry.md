Two bodies at different temperatures, put in contact, reach a common temperature, thermal equilibrium ([[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^def-a1-1-1|Def. §A1.1.1]]). Something passes from the hotter to the colder one on the way, and this section names it: heat, a transfer of energy. It then measures how much heat changes a body's temperature by a given amount (the specific heat), shows that work can do the same job, and uses energy conservation to predict the final temperature of bodies that share heat among themselves (calorimetry).

## Internal energy and heat

> [!remark] Remark: Internal energy, a first look
> A body stores energy in its molecules: the kinetic energy of their random motion plus the potential energy of their interactions with one another. This total is its **internal energy** (or thermal energy) $E_{\text{int}}$. It does not include the kinetic or potential energy of the body as a whole. The precise definition, as a sum of average molecular energies, is [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]; this chapter only needs the idea that heating a body raises its internal energy.

^rem-a1-3-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.1: Heat
> **Heat** $Q$ is energy transferred from one system to another solely because of a temperature difference between them. It flows spontaneously from the system at the higher temperature to the one at the lower temperature, until they reach thermal equilibrium; no macroscopic force acts through a distance, so no work is done. By convention $Q > 0$ when the system absorbs heat and $Q < 0$ when it gives heat off.
>
> Heat is energy, so its SI unit is the joule. Older units: the **calorie** (cal), the heat that raises the temperature of $1.00\ \mathrm g$ of water from $14.5$ to $15.5\ ^\circ\mathrm C$; the **kilocalorie** ($1\ \mathrm{kcal} = 1000\ \mathrm{cal}$), which is also the food "Calorie"; the British thermal unit, which raises $1\ \mathrm{lb}$ of water by $1\ ^\circ\mathrm F$, $1\ \mathrm{Btu} = 1055.1\ \mathrm J$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.4, §1.6*

^def-a1-3-1

> [!law] Law §A1.3.1: Mechanical Equivalent of Heat
> Doing work on a system can produce exactly the same change in it as supplying heat, and the amounts are equivalent at the rate
>
> $$
> 1.000\ \mathrm{kcal} = 4186\ \mathrm J, \qquad 1\ \mathrm{cal} = 4.186\ \mathrm J .
> $$
>
> In Joule's experiment, falling weights turned paddles in an insulated tank of water. The work $W = mgh$ done by the paddles was dissipated by viscosity and turbulence into random molecular motion, and the temperature rise of the water was proportional to $W$; equating it to the heat that gives the same rise yields the equivalence.
>
> *Valid when:* the calorie is the $14.5$–$15.5\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ calorie (calories defined over other ranges differ slightly).
> *Source: UP2 §1.4*

^law-a1-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Heat and work are both energy in transit
> Joule's result made heat a form of energy transfer, and with it energy conservation ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]) became a law that includes thermal phenomena: it is the first law of thermodynamics ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]). Since heating and doing work raise the internal energy in the same way, a body cannot be said to contain a certain amount of "heat" or "work"; it contains internal energy.
>
> In the language of UP §1.4, temperature and internal energy are **state variables**: they depend only on the present state of a system, not on its history. Heat and work are not, because the same change of state can be reached with different amounts of each. The precise notion is the state function, [[§A3.3 The First Law#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]].

^rem-a1-3-2

> [!caution] Caution: Heat, temperature, internal energy
> - "The heat was unbearable" means the *temperature* was high. Heat is a transfer, not something a body has.
> - Adding heat does not always raise the temperature: during melting or boiling the internal energy rises at constant temperature ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^law-a1-4-1|Law §A1.4.1]]).
> - Heat is not the only way to raise a temperature: friction or stirring (work) does it too ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^ex-a1-3-2|Example §A1.3.2]]).

^cau-a1-3-1

## Specific heat

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.2: Specific Heat; Molar Heat Capacity
> If a heat $dQ$ raises the temperature of a body of mass $m$ by $dT$, with no phase change and no work done, the **specific heat** (specific heat capacity) of its material is
>
> $$
> c = \frac{1}{m}\,\frac{dQ}{dT}, \qquad \text{unit } \mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)} = \mathrm{J/(kg\cdot {}^\circ C)} .
> $$
>
> The product $mc$ is the heat capacity of the body. Per mole instead of per kilogram, with $n$ the number of moles and $M = m/n$ the molar mass, the **molar heat capacity** is $C = \dfrac{1}{n}\dfrac{dQ}{dT} = Mc$. For gases the value depends on what is held fixed during the heating, the volume or the pressure ($C_V$, $C_p$; [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^def-a2-3-1|Def. §A2.3.1]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^def-a3-5-1|Def. §A3.5.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.4; PHY 287, week 2 slides*

^def-a1-3-2

> [!law] Law §A1.3.2: Heat and Temperature Change
> The heat needed to change the temperature of a mass $m$ by $\Delta T$ is
>
> $$
> Q = mc\,\Delta T \qquad (\text{or } Q = nC\,\Delta T),
> $$
>
> with $c$ taken as a constant of the material and phase. Typical values in $\mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$: water $4186$, human body $3500$ (average), ice $2090$, aluminium $900$, glass $840$, iron $452$, copper $387$, lead $128$; air $721$ at constant volume and $1015$ at constant pressure.
>
> *Valid when:* no phase change, no work done on or by the system, and $c$ nearly constant over the temperature range: true for most solids and liquids near room temperature, false at low temperatures ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^thm-a1-3-3|Theorem §A1.3.3]], [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^ex-a1-3-3|Example §A1.3.3]]).
> *Source: UP2 §1.4, eq. (1.5), Table 1.3*

^law-a1-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Why proportional to $m$ and $\Delta T$; why water is special
> - The heat goes into internal energy. Under ordinary conditions a fixed fraction of the internal energy is random kinetic energy, and the average kinetic energy per molecule is proportional to the absolute temperature ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]). So $\Delta E_{\text{int}} \propto N\,\Delta T$, with $N$ the number of molecules, and for a given substance $N \propto m$. Different substances differ mostly through the masses of their molecules (per molecule, heat capacities are often similar; [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]) and through their intermolecular potential energy.
> - Water's specific heat is one of the largest of any material: five times glass's and ten times iron's. A lake therefore changes temperature little between day and night, even when the air temperature swings widely.

^rem-a1-3-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.3.3: Heat with a Temperature-Dependent Specific Heat
> If the specific heat varies with temperature, the heat needed to take a mass $m$ from $T_1$ to $T_2$ is
>
> $$
> Q = m\int_{T_1}^{T_2} c(T)\,dT \qquad \Bigl(\text{or } Q = n\int_{T_1}^{T_2} C(T)\,dT\Bigr).
> $$
>
> For constant $c$ this reduces to $Q = mc\,\Delta T$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.4 (Example 1.8); PHY 287, week 2 slides*

^thm-a1-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Along the heating, let $Q(T)$ be the heat absorbed since the start, as a function of the temperature reached. By [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^def-a1-3-2|Def. §A1.3.2]], $dQ/dT = m\,c(T)$. By the fundamental theorem of calculus, $Q(T_2) - Q(T_1) = \int_{T_1}^{T_2} (dQ/dT)\,dT = m\int_{T_1}^{T_2} c(T)\,dT$. With $c$ constant the integral is $c\,(T_2 - T_1)$.

^der-a1-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^def-a1-3-2|Def. §A1.3.2]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

## Calorimetry

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.3: Calorimeter; Calorimetry
> A **calorimeter** is a container that prevents heat transfer into or out of it. **Calorimetry** is the measurement of heats or specific heats with one. More generally, a *calorimetry problem* is any problem in which the bodies concerned exchange heat only with one another, being thermally isolated from everything else.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.4*

^def-a1-3-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.3.4: Calorimetry
> When bodies thermally isolated from their surroundings exchange heat, with no work done, the heats they absorb add to zero:
>
> $$
> \sum_i Q_i = 0 \qquad (\text{for two bodies: } Q_{\text{cold}} + Q_{\text{hot}} = 0).
> $$
>
> If no phase changes occur and the specific heats are constant, the final common temperature is the heat-capacity-weighted mean of the initial temperatures,
>
> $$
> T_f = \frac{\sum_i m_i c_i\,T_i}{\sum_i m_i c_i} ,
> $$
>
> which always lies between the lowest and the highest initial temperature.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.4, eq. (1.6); PHY 287, week 2 slides*

^thm-a1-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The bodies together form an isolated system ([[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]]) on which no work is done, so its total energy is constant ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]): whatever heat one body gives off another absorbs, and $\sum_i Q_i = 0$. The bodies end in thermal equilibrium, at one temperature $T_f$ ([[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]), and by [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^law-a1-3-2|Law §A1.3.2]] body $i$ absorbs $Q_i = m_i c_i (T_f - T_i)$. Then $\sum_i m_i c_i (T_f - T_i) = 0$ is linear in $T_f$; solving gives the weighted mean. A weighted mean with positive weights lies between the smallest and largest of the averaged values.

^der-a1-3-4

*Uses:* [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^law-a1-3-2|Law §A1.3.2]], [[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]], [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]], [[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Let the signs take care of themselves
> - Write every heat as $Q_i = m_i c_i\,(T_f - T_i)$, final minus initial, with the unknown $T_f$. Bodies that cool then automatically have $Q_i < 0$. Inserting minus signs by hand as well ("heat lost $=$ heat gained" *and* negative $Q$'s) double counts them.
> - Check the answer against phase changes: if $T_f$ lies beyond a melting or boiling point of one of the bodies, $Q = mc\,\Delta T$ was the wrong law for it, and the problem must be redone in steps ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^rem-a1-4-4|Remark: Problem-solving with phase changes]]).

^cau-a1-3-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.3.1: Water Poured into a Hot Pan
> $0.250\ \mathrm{kg}$ of water at $20.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ is poured into a $0.500\ \mathrm{kg}$ aluminium pan at $150\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ standing on an insulating pad. Neglect heat to the air and evaporation. By [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^thm-a1-3-4|Theorem §A1.3.4]], with heat capacities $m_{\text{Al}}c_{\text{Al}} = (0.500)(900) = 450\ \mathrm{J/K}$ and $m_w c_w = (0.250)(4186) = 1046.5\ \mathrm{J/K}$,
>
> $$
> T_f = \frac{(450)(150\ ^\circ\mathrm C) + (1046.5)(20.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C)}{450 + 1046.5} = 59.1\ ^\circ\mathrm C .
> $$
>
> Although the pan has twice the mass, the water has more than twice the heat capacity, so the final temperature lies much nearer the water's.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 1.7*

^ex-a1-3-1

> [!example] Example §A1.3.2: Hot Brakes from Work
> A $10\,000\ \mathrm{kg}$ truck descends $75.0\ \mathrm m$ (vertically) at constant speed, held back by its brakes; $10\%$ of the energy dissipated stays in $10\ \mathrm{kg}$ of brake material with $c = 800\ \mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$. The kinetic energy does not change, so the lost gravitational potential energy ([[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-1|CM Theorem §A4.4.1]]) $Mgh = (10\,000\ \mathrm{kg})(9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2})(75.0\ \mathrm m) = 7.35\times10^6\ \mathrm J$ is dissipated by friction. The brakes receive $7.35\times10^5\ \mathrm J$ as work, which by [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^law-a1-3-1|Law §A1.3.1]] raises their temperature exactly as that much heat would:
>
> $$
> \Delta T = \frac{7.35\times10^5\ \mathrm J}{(10\ \mathrm{kg})(800\ \mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)})} = 92\ \mathrm K .
> $$
>
> Added to brakes already warm from use, this is too much; trucks use engine braking instead, and hybrid cars store the energy in a battery (regenerative braking).
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 1.6*

^ex-a1-3-2

> [!example] Example §A1.3.3: Rock Salt near Absolute Zero
> At very low temperatures the molar heat capacity of rock salt follows Debye's $T^3$ law, $C(T) = k\,(T/\theta)^3$ with $k = 1940\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}$ and $\theta = 281\ \mathrm K$. Heating $n = 1.50\ \mathrm{mol}$ from $10.0$ to $40.0\ \mathrm K$ takes, by [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^thm-a1-3-3|Theorem §A1.3.3]],
>
> $$
> Q = n\,\frac{k}{\theta^3}\int_{10\ \mathrm K}^{40\ \mathrm K} T^3\,dT = \frac{(1.50)(1940\ \mathrm{J/K})}{(281\ \mathrm K)^3}\,\frac{(40.0\ \mathrm K)^4 - (10.0\ \mathrm K)^4}{4} = 83.6\ \mathrm J .
> $$
>
> At the upper end $C(40.0\ \mathrm K) = 1940\,(40.0/281)^3\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)} = 5.60\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}$; using this end value as a constant would give $n C\,\Delta T = 252\ \mathrm J$, three times too much. The heat capacity of solids falls towards zero at low temperature, a quantum effect first explained by Debye in 1912 (level B: [[§B11.2★ Phonons and the Debye Model#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]], which also accounts for the constants $k$ and $\theta$, [[§B11.2★ Phonons and the Debye Model#^rem-b11-2-2|Remark: The T³ law at level A]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Midterm 1 practice*

^ex-a1-3-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $Q = m\int c\,dT$ is the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]) applied to the heat absorbed as a function of temperature along one particular heating process; heat is not a state variable, so this needs the process to be specified (at constant volume or pressure for a gas).
> - The calorimetry rule is energy conservation before the first law is stated; the first law ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]) adds work, and internal energy as a state variable.
