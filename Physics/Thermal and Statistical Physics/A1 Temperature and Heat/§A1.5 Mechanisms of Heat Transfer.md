Sections [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry|§A1.3]] and [[§A1.4 Phase Changes|§A1.4]] said how much heat a change of temperature or phase takes; they did not say how fast heat flows. This section answers that for the three ways heat travels: conduction through matter at rest, convection by moving fluid, and radiation by electromagnetic waves. Each runs only because of a temperature difference, and faster the larger the difference. Combined with $Q = mc\,\Delta T$, the rates also tell how a body's temperature approaches that of its surroundings in time.

## Three mechanisms

> [!definition] Definition §A1.5.1: Conduction, Convection, Radiation; Heat Current
> Heat is transferred in only three ways:
> - **conduction**: through matter that is macroscopically at rest, by collisions between neighbouring molecules (and free electrons in metals), as through the bottom of a pan;
> - **convection**: by the macroscopic flow of a fluid that carries its internal energy with it. In **forced convection** the flow is driven by fans or pumps; in **natural** (free) **convection** it is driven by buoyancy, warm fluid rising and cold fluid sinking;
> - **radiation**: by electromagnetic waves (mostly infrared at ordinary temperatures) emitted and absorbed by bodies, with no medium needed ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^rem-a12-1-4|EM Remark: The mechanism of an electromagnetic wave]]), as from the Sun to Earth.
>
> The **rate of heat transfer** or **heat current** is $P = dQ/dt$, in watts (PHY 287 writes $H$).
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.6; PHY 287, week 3 slides*

## Conduction

> [!law] Law §A1.5.1: Fourier's Law of Heat Conduction
> Through a slab of thermal conductivity $k$, area $A$ and thickness $d$, whose faces are held at temperatures $T_h > T_c$, the heat current is
>
> $$
> P = \frac{kA\,(T_h - T_c)}{d} .
> $$
>
> More generally, for heat flowing along $x$ through a cross-section $A$, $P = -kA\,\dfrac{dT}{dx}$: heat flows down the temperature gradient. The **thermal conductivity** $k$ is a property of the material, in $\mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)}$. Values near $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$: diamond 2000, silver 420, copper 390, aluminium 220, iron and steel 80, stainless steel 14, ice 2.2, glass and concrete 0.84, water 0.6, wood 0.08–0.16, dry snow 0.10, wool 0.04, down 0.025, air 0.023, polystyrene foam 0.010.
>
> *Valid when:* the flow is one-dimensional (along $x$, through the area $A$) and, for the slab form, steady, with $k$ constant over the temperature range. (UP calls it the rate of conductive heat transfer.)
> *Source: UP2 §1.6, eq. (1.9), Table 1.5; PHY 287, week 3 slides*

^law-a1-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why these factors
> - At the hot face the molecules have more kinetic energy on average; collisions pass energy from faster to slower molecules, and the net effect of all collisions is a flow from hot to cold, zero when the temperatures are equal. More area means more collisions, a thicker slab means more steps from hot to cold.
> - Metals conduct heat well for the same reason they conduct electricity: free electrons carry the energy ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^def-a5-2-1|EM Def. §A5.2.1]]). Diamond, an electrical insulator, conducts heat by atomic vibrations. Good insulators (wool, down, foam, fibreglass) are mostly air, trapped in pockets too small for convection.
> - In a steady state no part of the slab gains or loses energy, so the same current $P$ crosses every cross-section; for a uniform slab $dT/dx$ is then constant and the temperature falls linearly from $T_h$ to $T_c$.

^rem-a1-5-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.5.2: Thermal Resistance (R Factor)
> The **thermal resistance** of a slab per unit area (its **R factor**) is $R = d/k$, so that
>
> $$
> P = \frac{A\,(T_h - T_c)}{R} .
> $$
>
> Good insulators have large $R$. In the United States $R$ is quoted in $\mathrm{ft^2\cdot{}^\circ F\cdot h/Btu}$: $3.5$-inch fibreglass batts (walls) are R-11, $6.5$-inch batts (ceilings) R-19. PHY 287 uses the resistance of the whole slab, $R_T = d/(kA) = R/A$, with $P = \Delta T/R_T$.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.6; PHY 287, Problem Set 2*

^def-a1-5-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.5.2: Slabs in Series and in Parallel
> In the steady state:
> - **Series.** Slabs of equal area $A$ stacked along the flow, with resistances $R_1, R_2, \dots$ and outer faces at $T_h$ and $T_c$, carry
>
> $$
> P = \frac{A\,(T_h - T_c)}{R_1 + R_2 + \cdots},
> $$
>
> and the temperature drop across slab $i$ is $\Delta T_i = P R_i/A$: resistances add, and the most resistive layer takes the largest share of the drop.
> - **Parallel.** Rods side by side between the same two temperatures, with $k_i$, $A_i$ and $L_i$, carry $P = (T_h - T_c)\sum_i k_iA_i/L_i$: the conductances $k_iA_i/L_i$ add.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 1.11; PHY 287, Problem Set 2*

^thm-a1-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Series.* In the steady state the temperature of each junction is constant, so the heat flowing into it equals the heat flowing out, and every slab carries the same $P$. By [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-1|Law §A1.5.1]] and [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]] the drop across slab $i$ is $\Delta T_i = PR_i/A$. The drops add up to the total, $T_h - T_c = (P/A)(R_1 + R_2 + \cdots)$; solve for $P$.
> *Parallel.* Each rod has the full difference $T_h - T_c$ across it and carries $k_iA_i(T_h - T_c)/L_i$ independently; the currents add.

^der-a1-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-1|Law §A1.5.1]], [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The electrical analogy
> Heat current, temperature difference and thermal resistance $R_T = L/(kA)$ correspond to electric current, potential difference and resistance $R = \rho L/A$ ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^thm-a5-3-3|EM Theorem §A5.3.3]]), with $1/k$ in the role of the resistivity; $P = \Delta T/R_T$ is the analogue of $I = V/R$. [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]] is then the rule for resistors in series and in parallel ([[§A6.2 Resistors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a6-2-1|EM Theorem §A6.2.1]], [[§A6.2 Resistors in Series and Parallel#^thm-a6-2-2|EM Theorem §A6.2.2]]).

^rem-a1-5-2

> [!remark] Remark: When the area varies: a spherical shell
> The slab formula needs a constant cross-section; otherwise use $P = -kA\,dT/dx$ with the area at each place. For a spherical shell with inner radius $r_a$ at $T_a$ and outer radius $r_b$ at $T_b$, the area at radius $r$ is $4\pi r^2$, and in the steady state the same $P$ crosses every sphere: $P = -4\pi k r^2\,dT/dr$. Separating and integrating from $r_a$ to $r_b$, $T_b - T_a = -\dfrac{P}{4\pi k}\Bigl(\dfrac{1}{r_a} - \dfrac{1}{r_b}\Bigr)$, so
>
> $$
> P = 4\pi k\,\frac{r_a r_b}{r_b - r_a}\,(T_a - T_b), \qquad T(r) = T_a + (T_b - T_a)\,\frac{r_b\,(r - r_a)}{r\,(r_b - r_a)} .
> $$
>
> For a thin shell ($r_b - r_a = d \ll r_a$) this reduces to the slab law with $A = 4\pi r_a^2$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 2*

## Convection and Newton's law of cooling

> [!remark] Remark: How convection works
> - Natural convection runs on thermal expansion ([[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^rem-a1-2-3|§A1.2]]): heated fluid becomes less dense than its surroundings and the buoyant force ([[§A9.3 Buoyancy#^thm-a9-3-2|CM Theorem §A9.3.2]]) lifts it, while cooled fluid sinks. A pot of water on a stove, a room heated by a single furnace, ocean currents and the atmosphere's circulation are convection loops; convection in Earth's mantle drives plate tectonics.
> - The rate depends on the area, the fluid's viscosity, the shape of the surface and the temperature difference; there is no simple general formula, but the rate is often roughly proportional to $\Delta T$.
> - Air is a poor conductor, so in air convection dominates, and stopping the flow insulates: the air in a $1\ \mathrm{cm}$ double-glazing gap, in fur, cloth or fibreglass hardly moves, while a $9\ \mathrm{cm}$ empty wall cavity lets convection work and needs insulation.
> - With a phase change convection is very effective. Air flow replaces the saturated air near sweating skin, so evaporation continues ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^ex-a1-4-3|Example §A1.4.3]]). Water evaporated from the ocean releases its latent heat where it condenses into clouds; the released energy warms the air, which rises and condenses more, a positive feedback that builds thunderheads and drives hurricanes.

^rem-a1-5-4

> [!law] Law §A1.5.3: Newton's Law of Cooling
> A body at uniform temperature $T$ in surroundings at temperature $T_{\text{env}}$ loses heat at a rate proportional to the temperature difference,
>
> $$
> P_{\text{out}} = K\,(T - T_{\text{env}}),
> $$
>
> with $K > 0$ a constant set by the body's surface and its surroundings.
>
> *Valid when:* $|T - T_{\text{env}}|$ is small compared with the absolute temperature (so that the radiative part is also linear, [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^rem-a1-5-6|Remark: Small temperature differences]]), the body's temperature is uniform, and the surroundings and the flow conditions stay fixed. For conduction through a wall of negligible heat capacity it follows from Fourier's law, with $K = kA/d$.
> *Source: PHY 287, week 3 slides*

^law-a1-5-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.5.4: Exponential Approach to Equilibrium
> A body of mass $m$ and specific heat $c$ that exchanges heat with its surroundings according to [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-3|Law §A1.5.3]] (and does no work) has temperature
>
> $$
> T(t) = T_{\text{env}} + \bigl(T_0 - T_{\text{env}}\bigr)\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{mc}{K},
> $$
>
> where $T_0 = T(0)$. The difference from the surroundings decays by the factor $e$ in each **time constant** $\tau$; after $3\tau$ only 5% of it is left. For a container that loses heat by conduction through walls of area $A$ and thickness $d$, $\tau = mcd/(kA)$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 3 slides, Problem Set 3*

^thm-a1-5-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In a time $dt$ the body gives off $P_{\text{out}}\,dt$, and by [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^law-a1-3-2|Law §A1.3.2]] its temperature changes by $dT$ with $mc\,dT = -P_{\text{out}}\,dt$. So
>
> $$
> \frac{dT}{dt} = -\frac{K}{mc}\,(T - T_{\text{env}}) = -\frac{T - T_{\text{env}}}{\tau} .
> $$
>
> Put $u = T - T_{\text{env}}$, so $du/dt = -u/\tau$. Then $\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl(u\,e^{t/\tau}\bigr) = \bigl(\dfrac{du}{dt} + \dfrac{u}{\tau}\bigr)e^{t/\tau} = 0$, so $u\,e^{t/\tau}$ is constant, equal to its value $u(0) = T_0 - T_{\text{env}}$ at $t = 0$. Hence $u = (T_0 - T_{\text{env}})e^{-t/\tau}$. For conduction through walls, $K = kA/d$ by [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-1|Law §A1.5.1]].

^der-a1-5-4

*Uses:* [[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^law-a1-3-2|Law §A1.3.2]], [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-3|Law §A1.5.3]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]

## Radiation

> [!definition] Definition §A1.5.3: Emissivity
> The **emissivity** $e$ of a surface, $0 \le e \le 1$, measures how well it radiates compared with an ideal radiator at the same temperature. The same number measures how well it absorbs: black surfaces absorb and emit well, white, shiny or transparent ones poorly. A perfect reflector or perfectly transparent body has $e = 0$. Examples: carbon black about $0.95$, a tungsten filament about $0.5$, human skin $0.97$ in the infrared (so all skin is nearly black in the infrared, whatever its visible colour).
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.6*

^def-a1-5-3

> [!model] Model §A1.5.5: Blackbody
> A **blackbody** (ideal radiator) absorbs all radiation that falls on it, $e = 1$, and emits more than any other surface at the same temperature. Its emission spectrum depends only on its temperature.
>
> - *Assumes:* $e = 1$ at every wavelength. A body whose emissivity is less than 1 but the same at all wavelengths is called gray.
> - *Fails:* for real surfaces, whose emissivity depends on wavelength: a red object reflects red light more and therefore emits less of it when hot, and skin is nearly black in the infrared but not in the visible. Then $e$ must be taken in the wavelength range where the body actually radiates.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.6; PHY 287, week 3 slides*

^mod-a1-5-5

> [!remark] Remark: The thermal spectrum; why absorbing and emitting go together
> - A hotter body emits more at every wavelength, and its spectrum shifts towards shorter wavelengths: a stove element glows red to orange, steel in a blast furnace yellow to white. Bodies cooler than these radiate mostly in the infrared ([[§A12.5 The Electromagnetic Spectrum#^def-a12-5-1|EM Def. §A12.5.1]], [[§A12.5 The Electromagnetic Spectrum#^rem-a12-5-2|EM Remark: Infrared to gamma]]). The shape of the spectrum (Planck's law) is quantum physics ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-4|QM Theorem §A1.1.4]]).
> - Put a black and a silvered body, at the same temperature, where they can exchange radiation only with each other. Experience (and the second law in Clausius's form, [[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]]) says they stay at that temperature. So each must emit exactly as much as it absorbs: the black body, a good absorber, must be a good emitter, and the silvered one a poor emitter. One number, the emissivity, governs both.

^rem-a1-5-5

> [!law] Law §A1.5.6: Stefan–Boltzmann Law
> A body of surface area $A$, emissivity $e$ and absolute temperature $T$ radiates energy at the rate
>
> $$
> P = \sigma A e\,T^4, \qquad \sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W/(m^2\cdot K^4)} .
> $$
>
> *Valid when:* $T$ is in kelvins and $e$ is the emissivity over the wavelengths the body emits (exact for a blackbody, $e = 1$). At this level the law is taken from experiment; it follows from Planck's radiation law ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-6|QM Theorem §A1.1.6]]).
> *Source: UP2 §1.6; PHY 287, week 3 slides*

^law-a1-5-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.5.7: Net Radiative Heat Transfer
> A body of area $A$, emissivity $e$ and temperature $T$, surrounded by an environment at uniform temperature $T_s$, gains energy by radiation at the net rate
>
> $$
> P_{\text{net}} = \sigma e A\,\bigl(T_s^4 - T^4\bigr) .
> $$
>
> $P_{\text{net}} > 0$ when the surroundings are hotter: the net flow is from hot to cold. Only the body's emissivity enters, not the colour of the surroundings.
>
> *Source: UP2 §1.6, eq. (1.10)*

^thm-a1-5-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The body emits $\sigma e A T^4$ ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-6|Law §A1.5.6]]). The radiation it absorbs depends on the surroundings (through $T_s$) and on the body's ability to absorb, which is the same number $e$ ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^def-a1-5-3|Def. §A1.5.3]]), but not on the body's own temperature; write it $e A\,g(T_s)$. If $T = T_s$ the body is in thermal equilibrium with its surroundings ([[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]), so the net transfer vanishes: $e A\,g(T_s) = \sigma e A T_s^4$, which fixes $g(T_s) = \sigma T_s^4$ for every $T_s$. For any $T$, then, $P_{\text{net}} = \sigma e A T_s^4 - \sigma e A T^4$.

^der-a1-5-7

*Uses:* [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-6|Law §A1.5.6]], [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^def-a1-5-3|Def. §A1.5.3]], [[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Small temperature differences; a person in a room
> If $T = T_s - \Delta T$ with $|\Delta T| \ll T_s$, the first-order Taylor expansion of $T^4$ about $T_s$ gives $T_s^4 - T^4 \approx 4T_s^3\,\Delta T$, so $P_{\text{net}} \approx 4\sigma e A T_s^3\,(T_s - T)$: for small differences radiation also obeys Newton's law of cooling ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-3|Law §A1.5.3]]). Example: an unclothed person ($A = 1.50\ \mathrm{m^2}$, $e = 0.97$, skin at $33.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C = 306\ \mathrm K$) in a dark room at $22.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C = 295\ \mathrm K$ has
>
> $$
> P_{\text{net}} = (5.67\times10^{-8})(0.97)(1.50)\bigl[(295)^4 - (306)^4\bigr]\ \mathrm W = -99\ \mathrm W ,
> $$
>
> a net loss comparable to the $\sim 120\ \mathrm W$ the body produces at rest, before conduction and convection are counted; clothing reduces all three. The steep $T^4$ dependence also makes small temperature differences visible in thermographs (infrared images of bodies or of heat leaks in houses).

^rem-a1-5-6

> [!remark] Remark: The greenhouse effect
> The atmosphere is transparent to most incoming sunlight (visible and near infrared). Earth's surface, much cooler than the Sun, re-emits the energy in the far infrared, which molecules of three or more atoms ($\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{CO_2}$, $\mathrm{CH_4}$) absorb far better than the $\mathrm{N_2}$ and $\mathrm{O_2}$ that make up most of the air. They re-radiate it in all directions, about half back down, so the surface is warmer than it would be without an atmosphere ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^ex-a1-5-4|Example §A1.5.4]]). Raising the concentration of these gases raises the surface temperature. A glass greenhouse, despite the name, stays warm mainly by blocking convection, not radiation.

^rem-a1-5-7

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.5.1: Cold Metal, Warm Wood
> A chair has a wooden seat and metal legs, all at room temperature, yet the legs feel colder to the touch. Why? Not because the metal is colder: everything in the room is in thermal equilibrium at one temperature. The hand senses the rate at which heat leaves the skin, and by [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-1|Law §A1.5.1]] that rate is proportional to $k$: metal (steel, $k \approx 80$) conducts heat away from the skin hundreds of times faster than wood ($k \approx 0.1\ \mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)}$). Bare feet on a tiled floor and on a carpet at the same temperature feel the same difference.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Midterm 1 practice; UP2 §1.6*

^ex-a1-5-1

> [!example] Example §A1.5.2: Steel and Aluminium Rods End to End
> A steel rod and an aluminium rod, each $1.00\ \mathrm{cm}$ in diameter and $25.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ long, are welded end to end and insulated on the sides. The free end of the steel is in boiling water ($100\ ^\circ\mathrm C$), that of the aluminium in water at $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. Find the steady heat current and the temperature of the joint. Use [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]] with $A = \pi(0.005\ \mathrm m)^2 = 7.85\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ and
>
> $$
> R_{\text{steel}} = \frac{0.25\ \mathrm m}{80\ \mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)}} = 3.13\times10^{-3}, \qquad R_{\text{Al}} = \frac{0.25\ \mathrm m}{220\ \mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)}} = 1.14\times10^{-3}\ \ \mathrm{m^2\cdot K/W},
> $$
>
> $$
> P = \frac{A\,(80\ \mathrm K)}{R_{\text{steel}} + R_{\text{Al}}} = 1.47\ \mathrm W, \qquad \Delta T_{\text{Al}} = \frac{PR_{\text{Al}}}{A} = 21.3\ \mathrm K, \qquad T_{\text{joint}} = 20\ ^\circ\mathrm C + 21.3\ \mathrm K = 41.3\ ^\circ\mathrm C .
> $$
>
> The poorer conductor, steel, takes almost three quarters of the temperature drop over the same length.
>
> *Source: UP2 Example 1.11; PHY 287, week 3 slides*

> [!example] Example §A1.5.3: A Glass of Iced Tea Warming Up
> A glass of iced tea at $5\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ stands in a room at $30\ ^\circ\mathrm C$; after $5.0\ \mathrm{min}$ it is at $10\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. Assuming Newton's law of cooling, by [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^thm-a1-5-4|Theorem §A1.5.4]]
>
> $$
> T(t) = 30\ ^\circ\mathrm C - (25\ \mathrm K)\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad e^{-(5.0\ \mathrm{min})/\tau} = \frac{30 - 10}{25} = 0.80 \;\Longrightarrow\; \tau = \frac{5.0\ \mathrm{min}}{\ln 1.25} = 22.4\ \mathrm{min} .
> $$
>
> After $10\ \mathrm{min}$, $T = 30 - 25(0.80)^2 = 14\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ (equal time steps multiply the remaining difference by equal factors). The tea reaches $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ when $e^{-t/\tau} = 0.40$, at $t = \tau\ln 2.5 = 20.5\ \mathrm{min}$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 2*

> [!example] Example §A1.5.4: The Temperature of the Earth
> Estimate Earth's mean surface temperature from radiation balance. Treat the Sun as a blackbody of radius $R_S = 6.96\times10^8\ \mathrm m$ at $T_S = 5800\ \mathrm K$; Earth, of radius $R_E$, is a distance $D = 1.496\times10^{11}\ \mathrm m$ away, reflects a fraction $a \approx 0.3$ (its albedo) of the sunlight and radiates as a blackbody in the infrared at $T_E$.
> - *Sun's output:* $P_S = \sigma\,4\pi R_S^2\,T_S^4 = 3.9\times10^{26}\ \mathrm W$ ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-6|Law §A1.5.6]]), spread over a sphere of radius $D$ ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-4|EM Theorem §A12.3.4]]): at Earth the intensity ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^def-a12-3-2|EM Def. §A12.3.2]]) is $P_S/(4\pi D^2) = 1.39\ \mathrm{kW/m^2}$. Earth's disc intercepts the fraction $\pi R_E^2/(4\pi D^2)$ of it and absorbs $(1-a)$ of that.
> - *Balance:* absorbed $=$ emitted,
>
> $$
> (1-a)\,\sigma 4\pi R_S^2 T_S^4\,\frac{\pi R_E^2}{4\pi D^2} = \sigma\,4\pi R_E^2\,T_E^4 \;\Longrightarrow\; T_E = T_S\,(1-a)^{1/4}\sqrt{\frac{R_S}{2D}} = 256\ \mathrm K \approx -17\ ^\circ\mathrm C .
> $$
>
> $R_E$ cancels: every planet at Earth's distance without an atmosphere would have this temperature. Earth's internal heat ($47\ \mathrm{TW}$) is negligible, about $1/2600$ of the absorbed sunlight ($1.2\times10^{17}\ \mathrm W$); on its own it would keep the surface at only $36\ \mathrm K$.
> - *Greenhouse correction:* if only a fraction $f \approx 0.6$ of Earth's infrared escapes to space, the right side is multiplied by $f$, and $T_E = 256\ \mathrm K/f^{1/4} \approx 291\ \mathrm K$ ($18\ ^\circ\mathrm C$), close to the observed average. The same effect, much stronger, makes Venus hotter than Mercury although it is farther from the Sun.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 3 slides and class notes; Problem Set 3*

> [!remark]- Connections

The spherical-shell result ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^rem-a1-5-3|Remark: When the area varies]]) is the divergence theorem ([[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]]) applied to a steady, source-free flux: the same $P$ crosses every sphere.
