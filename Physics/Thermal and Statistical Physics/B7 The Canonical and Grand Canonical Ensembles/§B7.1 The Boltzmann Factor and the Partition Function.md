*Sources: Schwartz, Physics 181 lectures, Lecture 7 · Schroeder, An Introduction to Thermal Physics, §6.1–6.2 · Blundell & Blundell, Concepts in Thermal Physics, ch. 4, 20 · PHY 306 (Ringer, Spring 2025).*

The microcanonical ensemble of Chapter B6 describes an isolated system of fixed energy. Most systems are not isolated: a molecule in a gas, a spin in a crystal or a cup of coffee on a table exchanges energy with surroundings whose temperature is fixed. This section answers the question: with what probability is such a system found in a given microstate? The answer follows from the fundamental postulate ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]) and the statistical temperature $1/T = \partial S/\partial U$ ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]]). It is the Boltzmann factor, which level A met only as an idea ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^rem-a2-4-2|Remark: Boltzmann's generalisation]]) and which here becomes a theorem. Its normalising sum, the partition function, turns out to contain all the thermodynamics: this section extracts the mean energy and heat capacity and works out the two-level system and the paramagnet.

*Notation for Chapters B7–B8.* The internal energy is $U$ (level A writes $E_{\text{int}}$); $k = k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]); $\beta = 1/kT$. Energies of microstates of the system are $E_s$, those of single particles $\varepsilon$. The first law is written as at level A, $dU = đQ - p\,dV$, so that $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV + \mu\,dN$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]).

## The canonical ensemble

> [!definition] Definition §B7.1.1: Heat Reservoir; Canonical Ensemble
> A **heat reservoir** (heat bath) at temperature $T$ is a system so large that any energy it exchanges with the system of interest leaves its temperature unchanged. A system that can exchange energy, but neither particles nor volume, with a reservoir at $T$ is said to be in the **canonical ensemble**: the idealised collection of copies of the system, each in thermal contact with the reservoir, with $N$, $V$ and $T$ fixed and the energy free to fluctuate. In contrast, the **microcanonical ensemble** of an isolated system fixes $N$, $V$ and $U$ ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-3|Def. §B6.1.3]]).
>
> *Source: Blundell §4.5–4.6; Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, §1–2; Schroeder §6.2*

^def-b7-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B7.1.2: Boltzmann Factor; Partition Function
> For a system with microstates $s$ of energy $E_s$, in contact with a reservoir at temperature $T$, put $\beta = 1/kT$. The **Boltzmann factor** of state $s$ is $e^{-\beta E_s}$, and the **partition function** (*Zustandssumme*, "sum over states") is
>
> $$
> Z(T, V, N) = \sum_{s} e^{-\beta E_s} = \sum_{\text{levels } i} g_i\,e^{-\beta E_i} ,
> $$
>
> where the second sum runs over distinct energy levels $E_i$ with **degeneracy** $g_i$, the number of microstates of energy $E_i$. For a continuous spectrum with density of states $g(E)$, $Z = \int g(E)\,e^{-\beta E}\,dE$. $Z$ depends on $T$ through $\beta$ and on $V$, $N$ (and fields) through the energies.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §6.1, eqs. (6.8)–(6.10); Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, eqs. (10)–(11); Blundell eq. (20.1)*

^def-b7-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.1: Boltzmann Distribution
> A system in equilibrium with a heat reservoir at temperature $T$ is found in microstate $s$ with probability
>
> $$
> P(s) = \frac{e^{-\beta E_s}}{Z}, \qquad\text{and in the level } E_i \text{ with probability}\qquad P(E_i) = \frac{g_i\,e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z} .
> $$
>
> The ratio of the probabilities of two states is the ratio of their Boltzmann factors, $P(s_2)/P(s_1) = e^{-\beta[E_{s_2} - E_{s_1}]}$; $T$ is the temperature of the reservoir. The system itself may be small, even a single atom.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §6.1, eqs. (6.1)–(6.8); Blundell §4.6, eqs. (4.9)–(4.14); Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, eqs. (5)–(9)*

^thm-b7-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Step 1: the reservoir counts.* System and reservoir together are isolated, with fixed total energy $U_{\text{tot}}$. By the fundamental postulate ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]) all microstates of the combined system are equally probable. Fix the system in microstate $s$: the number of combined microstates is then the number of microstates of the reservoir with energy $U_{\text{tot}} - E_s$, so
>
> $$
> P(s) \propto \Omega_R(U_{\text{tot}} - E_s) = e^{S_R(U_{\text{tot}} - E_s)/k},
> $$
>
> with $S_R = k\ln\Omega_R$ ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]]). (If the reservoir had twice as many microstates when the system is in $s_1$ as when it is in $s_2$, then $s_1$ would be twice as likely: Schroeder's way of putting it.)
>
> *Step 2: expand the reservoir's entropy.* $E_s \ll U_{\text{tot}}$, so Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) gives
>
> $$
> S_R(U_{\text{tot}} - E_s) = S_R(U_{\text{tot}}) - E_s\,\frac{\partial S_R}{\partial U_R} + \frac{E_s^2}{2}\,\frac{\partial^2 S_R}{\partial U_R^2} - \cdots = S_R(U_{\text{tot}}) - \frac{E_s}{T} - \frac{E_s^2}{2T^2C_R} - \cdots ,
> $$
>
> using $\partial S_R/\partial U_R = 1/T$ ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]]) and $\partial(1/T)/\partial U_R = -1/(T^2 C_R)$, with $C_R$ the reservoir's heat capacity. $C_R$ grows in proportion to the size of the reservoir while $E_s$ does not, so the quadratic and higher terms vanish in the reservoir limit: this is what "the reservoir's temperature does not change" means. The reservoir's volume and particle number are fixed, so its thermodynamic identity $dS_R = (dU_R + p\,dV_R - \mu\,dN_R)/T$ has no other terms. (Schroeder notes that even if the system's volume changes slightly between states, $p\,\Delta V$ is negligible: for an atom swelling by a cubic ångström at atmospheric pressure it is of order $10^{-25}\ \mathrm J$, a millionth of the electron-volt energies involved.)
>
> *Step 3: normalise.* Hence $P(s) = C\,e^{-E_s/kT}$ with $C$ independent of $s$. Summing over all states, $1 = C\sum_s e^{-\beta E_s} = CZ$, so $C = 1/Z$. Grouping the $g_i$ states of equal energy gives $P(E_i)$.

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the distribution
> - If the ground state has energy $0$, it has probability $1/Z$, the largest of all. States with $E_s \ll kT$ are nearly as likely; states with $E_s \gg kT$ are exponentially suppressed. So $kT$ is the energy scale that separates "accessible" from "frozen" states, and $Z$ counts, roughly, the accessible states: $Z \to g_0$ as $T \to 0$, and $Z$ grows with $T$.
> - Shifting every energy by $E_0$ multiplies every Boltzmann factor and $Z$ by $e^{-\beta E_0}$: the probabilities do not change. Quantities built from $\ln Z$ shift by a constant (which may stand for a rest energy or a bond energy); quantities built from derivatives of $\ln Z$ do not change at all (Blundell ch. 20).
> - The canonical ensemble is the microcanonical ensemble of system plus reservoir, viewed from the system. The reservoir never appears in $Z$: only the temperature it imposes does. For a macroscopic system the energy hardly fluctuates, and the two ensembles give the same thermodynamics ([[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-5|Theorem §B7.2.5]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder §6.1; Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, §2; Blundell ch. 20*

^rem-b7-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: From an idea at level A to a theorem
> Level A quoted the weight $e^{-E/k_BT}$ to motivate the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^der-a2-4-1|derivation of Theorem §A2.4.1]], [[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^rem-a2-4-2|Remark: Boltzmann's generalisation]]) and the isothermal atmosphere ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-4|CM Theorem §A9.1.4]]). Theorem §B7.1.1 derives it from the fundamental postulate, so both become consequences: for the atmosphere take one molecule as the system and $E = mgz$ (Blundell Example 4.4); for the speeds, derived microcanonically, see [[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]]. Two other routes reach the same distribution: Schwartz first obtains it by maximising the multiplicity of $N$ particles shared among energy groups, with Lagrange multipliers for $N$ and $E$ (Lecture 4, §6), which is the maximum-entropy distribution of [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-4|Theorem §B6.3.4]]. There $\beta$ is only a Lagrange multiplier fixed by the mean energy; the reservoir derivation above identifies it, once and for all, as $1/kT$ with $T$ the statistical temperature of [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]]. Finally, Blundell watches it emerge in a simulation in which energy quanta hop at random between 400 lattice sites (Example 4.2).

^rem-b7-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: A state is not an energy
> The Boltzmann factor gives the probability of one **microstate**. The probability of an **energy** carries the degeneracy $g_i$, and this can reverse the conclusion: the first excited level of hydrogen has four states (Example §B7.1.1), and the most probable *velocity* of a gas molecule is zero while its most probable *speed* is not, because many velocity vectors share one speed ([[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]).

^cau-b7-1-1

## Averages from the partition function

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.2: Mean Values; Energy and Heat Capacity from Z
> In the canonical ensemble the mean of any quantity $X$ with value $X(s)$ in state $s$ is
>
> $$
> \langle X\rangle = \sum_s X(s)\,P(s) = \frac1Z\sum_s X(s)\,e^{-\beta E_s} .
> $$
>
> In particular the mean energy and the heat capacity at constant volume follow from $\ln Z$ by differentiation at fixed $V$, $N$:
>
> $$
> U = \langle E\rangle = -\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial\beta} = kT^2\,\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial T}, \qquad C_V = \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} = k\beta^2\,\frac{\partial^2 \ln Z}{\partial\beta^2} .
> $$
>
> Means add: the mean total energy of $N$ particles is the sum of their mean energies, $U = N\langle\varepsilon\rangle$ for identical ones.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §6.2, eqs. (6.16)–(6.25), Problem 6.16; Blundell §20.2, eqs. (20.7)–(20.10); Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, eqs. (12)–(13), (22)*

^thm-b7-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The mean is the probability-weighted sum, with $P(s)$ from [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]]. Differentiating $Z$ term by term (legitimate where the differentiated series converges uniformly, as for every spectrum met here),
>
> $$
> -\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta} = \sum_s E_s\,e^{-\beta E_s} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle E\rangle = -\frac1Z\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta} = -\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta} .
> $$
>
> Since $\beta = 1/kT$, $d\beta/dT = -1/kT^2 = -k\beta^2$, so $\partial/\partial\beta = -kT^2\,\partial/\partial T$ and $\partial/\partial T = -k\beta^2\,\partial/\partial\beta$ (chain rule). This gives the second form of $U$, and $C_V = -k\beta^2\,\partial U/\partial\beta = k\beta^2\,\partial^2\ln Z/\partial\beta^2$. Holding $V$ and $N$ fixed holds every $E_s$ fixed, which is why the derivative is the one at constant volume. Additivity is linearity of the mean: $\langle E_1 + \cdots + E_N\rangle = \langle E_1\rangle + \cdots + \langle E_N\rangle$, with no assumption of independence ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]]); the particles need only all be at temperature $T$. Schroeder's trick is to treat each particle in turn as the "system" and the rest as its reservoir.

^der-b7-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Z as a generating function
> $Z(\beta)$ is a generating function ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-6|Def. §B4.1.6]]): each $\beta$-derivative of $\ln Z$ brings down one more power of $-E$. The first gives the mean, the second the variance of the energy, which is why $C_V$ measures fluctuations ([[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]]). Derivatives with respect to other parameters in the energies give other averages: a field $B$ gives the magnetisation (Theorem §B7.1.5), the volume gives the pressure ([[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-2|Theorem §B7.2.2]]). Blundell's summary is the "sausage machine": (1) write down $Z$; (2) differentiate.

## The two-level system

> [!model] Model §B7.1.3: Two-Level System
> Each of $N$ independent, distinguishable units has exactly two states: a ground state of energy $0$ and an excited state of energy $\Delta > 0$, both nondegenerate. All other states lie so high that they are never populated, and the units do not interact. Examples are an atom with one low-lying excited level, a spin $\tfrac12$ in a magnetic field (the paramagnet below) and defect or impurity levels in a solid. Blundell writes the levels as $\pm\Delta/2$; that shifts $U$ by $-N\Delta/2$ and changes nothing else.
>
> *Source: Blundell Examples 4.3, 20.1(a), 20.3(a); PHY 306, Problem Set 8, Problems 2–3*

^mod-b7-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.4: Thermodynamics of the Two-Level System; Schottky Anomaly
> For [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^mod-b7-1-3|Model §B7.1.3]] at temperature $T$, with $x = \beta\Delta = \Delta/kT$:
>
> $$
> Z_1 = 1 + e^{-x}, \qquad P_{\text{exc}} = \frac{1}{e^{x} + 1}, \qquad U = \frac{N\Delta}{e^{x} + 1}, \qquad C = Nk\,\frac{x^2 e^{x}}{(e^{x} + 1)^2} = Nk\Bigl(\frac x2\Bigr)^2\operatorname{sech}^2\frac x2 .
> $$
>
> - $kT \ll \Delta$: $U \approx N\Delta\,e^{-\Delta/kT}$ and $C \approx Nk\,(\Delta/kT)^2 e^{-\Delta/kT}$, exponentially small (the gap freezes the system).
> - $kT \gg \Delta$: $U \approx \tfrac12 N\Delta - N\Delta^2/4kT$ and $C \approx Nk\,(\Delta/2kT)^2$: both levels are nearly equally filled, and further heating has little to change.
> - In between, $C$ has a smooth maximum, the **Schottky anomaly**: $C_{\max} = 0.439\,Nk$ at $kT = 0.417\,\Delta$.
>
> At no positive temperature is more than half of the population excited.
>
> *Source: Blundell §20.2, eqs. (20.23)–(20.25), Fig. 20.4(a); Schroeder §6.2; PHY 306, Problem Set 8, Problems 2–3*

^thm-b7-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The two states have Boltzmann factors $1$ and $e^{-x}$; their sum is $Z_1$, and $P_{\text{exc}} = e^{-x}/(1 + e^{-x}) = 1/(e^x + 1)$ ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]]). Each unit has $\langle\varepsilon\rangle = 0\cdot P_0 + \Delta\,P_{\text{exc}}$, and $U = N\langle\varepsilon\rangle$ by [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]]; equivalently $-\partial\ln Z_1/\partial\beta = \Delta e^{-\beta\Delta}/(1 + e^{-\beta\Delta})$. Differentiating with $dx/dT = -x/T$,
>
> $$
> C = \frac{dU}{dT} = N\Delta\,\frac{e^x}{(e^x + 1)^2}\,\frac{x}{T} = Nk\,\frac{x^2e^x}{(e^x + 1)^2},
> $$
>
> and $e^x/(e^x + 1)^2 = 1/(e^{x/2} + e^{-x/2})^2 = \tfrac14\operatorname{sech}^2(x/2)$ gives the second form. *Limits.* For $x \gg 1$, $1/(e^x + 1) \approx e^{-x}$ and $(e^x + 1)^2 \approx e^{2x}$. For $x \ll 1$, $1/(e^x + 1) = \tfrac12 - \tfrac x4 + O(x^3)$ (Taylor, [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), and $\operatorname{sech}^2(x/2) \to 1$. *Maximum.* $dC/dx = 0$ gives $x\tanh(x/2) = 2$, solved numerically by $x = 2.399$; then $C/Nk = 0.439$. Finally $P_{\text{exc}} = 1/(e^x + 1) < \tfrac12$ for every $x > 0$.

*Two-level system with levels $0$ and $\Delta$. The heat capacity (blue) rises from an exponentially small value, peaks at $k_BT = 0.417\,\Delta$ (the Schottky anomaly) and falls off as $(\Delta/2k_BT)^2$; the mean energy (red, dashed) climbs toward $N\Delta/2$, equal filling of the two levels, but never reaches it.*

> [!remark] Remark: Reading a Schottky anomaly
> - The heat capacity is small at both ends for opposite reasons (Blundell): at low $T$ the reservoir cannot supply a quantum $\Delta$, so the system is stuck in its ground state; at high $T$ both levels are already equally occupied, so adding energy changes almost nothing. Large $C$ needs $kT \sim \Delta$, where heating actually moves population.
> - The low-$T$ form $C \propto e^{-\Delta/kT}$ is the signature of an energy **gap**. The high-$T$ fall-off is the signature of a **finite** set of levels. A ladder of levels without top, the oscillator, instead saturates at $C = k$ ([[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^thm-b8-2-4|Theorem §B8.2.4]]).
> - The peak is smooth and broad, not the spike or jump of a phase transition ([[§B9.3★ The Classification of Phase Transitions#^thm-b9-3-1|Theorem §B9.3.1]]). Its position measures $\Delta$, and its height, with $C_{\max}/Nk = 0.439$ known, counts the number $N$ of two-level units in a sample.
>
> *Source: Blundell §20.2, §20.3*

## The paramagnet

The ideal two-state paramagnet ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^mod-b6-1-3|Model §B6.1.3]]) has $N$ non-interacting dipoles fixed at lattice sites, hence distinguishable, each with moment $\pm\mu$ along the field $B$ and energy $\mp\mu B$. For an electron spin $\mu = \mu_B$ ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-7|QM Theorem §A5.2.7]]). Each dipole is the two-level system of [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^mod-b7-1-3|Model §B7.1.3]] with $\Delta = 2\mu B$ and the zero of energy midway. Its microcanonical treatment is [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-9|Theorem §B6.2.9]], its thermodynamics as a magnetic system in [[§B3.4★ Rods, Bubbles and Magnets]].

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.5: Paramagnet from the Partition Function
> For the ideal two-state paramagnet ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^mod-b6-1-3|Model §B6.1.3]]), with $y = \beta\mu B$:
>
> $$
> Z_1 = 2\cosh y, \qquad P_{\uparrow,\downarrow} = \frac{e^{\pm y}}{2\cosh y}, \qquad U = -N\mu B\tanh y, \qquad M = N\langle\mu_z\rangle = N\mu\tanh y ,
> $$
>
> where $M$ is the total magnetic moment along $B$. At high temperature, $\mu B \ll kT$, the magnetisation obeys **Curie's law** (empirical at this level: [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^law-b6-2-10|Law §B6.2.10]]),
>
> $$
> M \approx \frac{N\mu^2 B}{kT} ;
> $$
>
> at low temperature $M \to N\mu$, saturation. The mean moment is also $\langle\mu_z\rangle = \dfrac1\beta\,\dfrac{\partial\ln Z_1}{\partial B}$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §6.2, eqs. (6.20)–(6.28); Blundell Example 20.5, eqs. (20.41)–(20.48)*

^thm-b7-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The two Boltzmann factors are $e^{\pm\beta\mu B}$, whose sum is $2\cosh y$; [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]] gives the probabilities. Then $\langle\varepsilon\rangle = -\mu B P_\uparrow + \mu B P_\downarrow = -\mu B\tanh y$, which agrees with $-\partial\ln(2\cosh\beta\mu B)/\partial\beta = -\mu B\tanh y$, and $\langle\mu_z\rangle = \mu P_\uparrow - \mu P_\downarrow = \mu\tanh y$. Multiply by $N$ ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]]). For $y \ll 1$, $\tanh y \approx y$, which is Curie's law; for $y \gg 1$, $\tanh y \to 1$. In general, a state with moment $\mu_z(s)$ has energy $-\mu_z(s)B$, so $\partial Z_1/\partial B = \beta\sum_s \mu_z(s)\,e^{\beta\mu_z(s)B}$, and dividing by $\beta Z_1$ gives $\langle\mu_z\rangle$.

^der-b7-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^mod-b6-1-3|Model §B6.1.3]], [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]], [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Three lines instead of three pages
> Schroeder derived the same $U(T)$ and $M(T)$ microcanonically: the exact multiplicity of $N_\uparrow$ up-spins, Stirling's approximation for the entropy, a derivative for $1/T$, and algebra to invert it ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-9|Theorem §B6.2.9]]). With Boltzmann factors the system is one dipole and the result takes three lines. The two routes agree, as they must for large $N$ ([[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-5|Theorem §B7.2.5]]). The heat capacity of the paramagnet is the Schottky curve of Theorem §B7.1.4 with $\Delta = 2\mu B$: $C = Nk\,y^2\operatorname{sech}^2 y$ (Blundell Exercise 20.5).

^rem-b7-1-5

> [!caution] Caution: Which magnetisation, which field
> Schroeder's $M$ is the total moment of the sample; Blundell's $M$ is the moment per unit volume, and the susceptibility follows as $\chi \approx \mu_0 M/B = N\mu_0\mu_B^2/(VkT)$ in a weak field (Blundell eq. 20.48). PHY 306 (Problem Set 9) writes $H$ for the applied field in the energy $-m\mu H$. The physics is the same; check which convention a formula uses before comparing numbers.

## Examples

> [!example]- ★ Example §B7.1.1: Excited Hydrogen in the Solar Atmosphere
> The Sun's atmosphere has $T \approx 5800\ \mathrm K$, so $kT = (8.62\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/K})(5800\ \mathrm K) = 0.50\ \mathrm{eV}$. The first excited level of hydrogen lies $10.2\ \mathrm{eV}$ above the ground state ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-3|QM Theorem §A5.1.3]]). By [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]], the probability of one particular excited state relative to the ground state is
>
> $$
> \frac{P(s_2)}{P(s_1)} = e^{-(10.2\ \mathrm{eV})/(0.50\ \mathrm{eV})} = e^{-20.4} = 1.4\times10^{-9} .
> $$
>
> The level has four states ($2s$ and three $2p$, ignoring spin; [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-6|QM Theorem §A5.1.6]]), so about $5.5$ atoms per billion are in it. Only these atoms absorb the visible Balmer lines, yet the Balmer lines are prominent in the solar spectrum: hydrogen must vastly outnumber the other elements in the Sun's atmosphere, as Cecilia Payne concluded. Including spin doubles every degeneracy and changes no ratio.
>
> Only the *ratio* was needed. The absolute probability needs $Z$, and summing $2n^2e^{13.6\,\mathrm{eV}/n^2kT}$ over all bound levels diverges, since the terms tend to $2n^2$. The cure is physical, not mathematical: highly excited orbits have radii $\sim n^2a_0$, so in a gas they overlap neighbours and do not exist as states of an isolated atom; Schroeder traces this to the $p\,dV$ term dropped in Step 2 of the derivation. Truncating at the first few levels, with energies measured from the ground state, gives $Z \approx 1$ at $5800\ \mathrm K$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §6.1, Problem 6.9; Blundell Exercise 20.8*

> [!example] Example §B7.1.2: When Is a Quarter of the Atoms Excited?
> $N$ atoms each have a ground state $0$ and an excited state $\Delta$. By [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]], $N/4$ are excited when $1/(e^{\Delta/kT} + 1) = 1/4$, so $e^{\Delta/kT} = 3$ and
>
> $$
> T = \frac{\Delta}{k\ln 3} = 0.910\,\frac{\Delta}{k} .
> $$
>
> No temperature makes more than half excited; a population inversion, needed in a laser, is not a canonical state at positive $T$ (formally it has a negative temperature, [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-11|Theorem §B6.2.11]]).
>
> *Asymptotics with a general zero of energy.* For levels $E_0$ and $E_1 = E_0 + \Delta E$, $\langle\varepsilon\rangle = (E_0 + E_1e^{-\beta\Delta E})/(1 + e^{-\beta\Delta E}) = E_0 + \Delta E/(e^{\beta\Delta E} + 1)$. As $T \to 0$ the natural small parameter is $e^{-\beta\Delta E}$, not $1/\beta$ ($e^{-\beta\Delta E}$ has no power series in $T$), and $\langle\varepsilon\rangle \approx E_0 + \Delta E\,e^{-\beta\Delta E}$. As $T \to \infty$, expand in $\beta$: $\langle\varepsilon\rangle \approx \tfrac12(E_0 + E_1) - \tfrac14\beta(\Delta E)^2$. The mean approaches the midpoint from below, like $1/T$.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 8, Problems 2 and 3(1)*

> [!example] Example §B7.1.3: A Paramagnet with 2J + 1 Orientations
> An ion of angular momentum quantum number $J$ has $2J + 1$ states $m = -J, \dots, J$ with moments $m\mu$ along $B$ and energies $-m\mu B$. With $x = \beta\mu B$ and the finite geometric series ([[§14 Series#^ex-14-4|451 Ex. §14.4]]; Schroeder Problem 6.22),
>
> $$
> Z_1 = \sum_{m=-J}^{J} e^{mx} = e^{-Jx}\,\frac{e^{(2J+1)x} - 1}{e^x - 1} = \frac{e^{(J+\frac12)x} - e^{-(J+\frac12)x}}{e^{x/2} - e^{-x/2}} = \frac{\sinh\bigl[(J + \tfrac12)x\bigr]}{\sinh(x/2)} ,
> $$
>
> after multiplying numerator and denominator by $e^{-x/2}$. By [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]], $M = (N/\beta)\,\partial\ln Z_1/\partial B = N\mu\,\partial\ln Z_1/\partial x$:
>
> $$
> M = \frac{N\mu}{2}\Bigl[(2J + 1)\coth\frac{(2J + 1)x}{2} - \coth\frac x2\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> *High temperature.* With $\coth u \approx 1/u + u/3$, the $1/u$ terms cancel and $M \approx N\mu\,x\,[(2J+1)^2 - 1]/12 = N J(J+1)\mu^2B/3kT$: Curie's law, with the Curie constant growing like $J(J+1)$. Equivalently, for small $x$, $\langle m\rangle \approx x\langle m^2\rangle_{x=0} = xJ(J+1)/3$, since $\sum_{m=-J}^J m^2 = J(J+1)(2J+1)/3$. *Low temperature:* $M \to NJ\mu$. *Check:* for $J = \tfrac12$ the moments are $\pm\mu/2$, and the identity $2\coth 2u - \coth u = \tanh u$ turns $M$ into $(N\mu/2)\tanh(\beta\mu B/2)$, Theorem §B7.1.5 with moment $\mu/2$.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 9, Problem 3; Schroeder Problem 6.22*
