For an isolated system the bridge from microscopic counting to thermodynamics is $S = k\ln\Omega$ ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-1|Principle §A4.6.1]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]]). What plays that role at fixed temperature? This section shows that $\ln Z$ is the Helmholtz free energy, $F = -kT\ln Z$, so that the whole machinery of the potentials ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables]]) applies to the partition function of [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]]: entropy, pressure and chemical potential become derivatives of $\ln Z$. Two structural facts make this practical: partition functions of independent parts multiply, and for a large system the energy hardly fluctuates, so the canonical and microcanonical descriptions agree.

## The free energy

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.1: Free Energy and Entropy from the Partition Function
> For a system in the canonical ensemble,
>
> $$
> F = U - TS = -kT\ln Z, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad Z = e^{-\beta F},
> $$
>
> and the entropy is
>
> $$
> S = \frac UT + k\ln Z = -k\sum_s P(s)\ln P(s) .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schroeder §6.5, eqs. (6.56)–(6.63); Blundell §20.2, eqs. (20.11)–(20.15); Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, eqs. (14)–(15), and Lecture 8, eq. (28)*

^thm-b7-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Route 1: Gibbs entropy.* The statistical entropy of a probability distribution over microstates is $S = -k\sum_s P(s)\ln P(s)$ ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox]]); for $P(s) = 1/\Omega$ on $\Omega$ states it is $k\ln\Omega$. With the Boltzmann distribution ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]]), $\ln P(s) = -\beta E_s - \ln Z$, so
>
> $$
> S = k\sum_s P(s)\bigl(\beta E_s + \ln Z\bigr) = k\beta U + k\ln Z ,
> $$
>
> using $\sum_s P(s)E_s = U$ and $\sum_s P(s) = 1$. Multiply by $T$ and rearrange: $U - TS = -kT\ln Z$.
>
> *Route 2: without the Gibbs entropy (Schroeder).* Thermodynamics gives $S = -(\partial F/\partial T)_{V,N}$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]), so with $F = U - TS$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial}{\partial T}\Bigl(\frac FT\Bigr) = \frac1T\frac{\partial F}{\partial T} - \frac{F}{T^2} = \frac{-S}{T} - \frac{U - TS}{T^2} = -\frac{U}{T^2} ,
> $$
>
> the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]). The candidate $\tilde F = -kT\ln Z$ obeys the same equation: $\partial(\tilde F/T)/\partial T = -k\,\partial\ln Z/\partial T = -U/T^2$ by [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]]. Hence $F/T - \tilde F/T = c$, a constant, and $F = \tilde F + cT$ for all $T$, i.e. $S = \tilde S - c$. To fix $c$, look at $T \to 0$: only the $g_0$ ground states survive in $Z$, so $\ln Z \to \ln g_0 - \beta U_0$ and $\tilde S = U/T + k\ln Z \to k\ln g_0$, the Boltzmann entropy of the ground level ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]]); with the third-law convention $S(T \to 0) = k\ln g_0$ ([[§B2.3 The Third Law]]) one gets $c = 0$. (Matching only $F(0) = \tilde F(0) = U_0$, as Schroeder does, does not fix $c$: every $\tilde F + cT$ takes the value $U_0$ at $T = 0$.)

> [!remark] Remark: Why the logarithm of Z is a free energy
> - For an isolated system $\Omega$ counts the available microstates and $S = k\ln\Omega$ tends to increase. At fixed temperature $Z$ counts the available microstates, each weighted by its probability, and the quantity that tends to *decrease* is $F$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]); so $-F/kT$ is the natural guess for $\ln Z$ (Schroeder's "giant intuitive leap", which the theorem confirms). If there is only one microstate, $F = E$.
> - The same structure holds level by level: the probability of an energy level $E$ with $g(E)$ states is $P(E) = e^{-\beta F(E)}/Z$, with $F(E) = E - TS(E)$ and $S(E) = k\ln g(E)$ (Schroeder Problem 6.2). Low energy and many states compete, and $T$ sets the exchange rate, which is the paramagnet's balance between aligned order and disordered multiplicity (Blundell Example 20.5).
> - Schwartz stresses what is actually minimised. $-\Delta F/T = \Delta S - \Delta U/T$ is the entropy change of the system plus that of the surroundings, which receive heat $-\Delta U$ at temperature $T$. So "energy is minimised" (a ball coming to rest at the bottom of a valley) really means "free energy is minimised", which really means "total entropy is maximised".
>
> *Source: Schroeder §6.5, Problem 6.2; Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, §3; Blundell Example 20.5*

^rem-b7-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.2: Thermodynamics from the Partition Function
> With $F = -kT\ln Z(T, V, N)$, the entropy, pressure and chemical potential are
>
> $$
> S = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{V,N} = k\ln Z + kT\Bigl(\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{V,N}, \qquad p = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\Bigr)_{T,N} = kT\Bigl(\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial V}\Bigr)_{T,N}, \qquad \mu = \Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{T,V} = -kT\Bigl(\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{T,V},
> $$
>
> and then $H = U + pV$, $G = F + pV$. The pressure is the mean of the "force on the walls" in each microstate:
>
> $$
> p = \Bigl\langle -\frac{\partial E_s}{\partial V}\Bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> | quantity | from $Z$ |
> |---|---|
> | $U$ | $-\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta$ |
> | $F$ | $-kT\ln Z$ |
> | $S$ | $(U - F)/T$ |
> | $p$ | $kT\,\partial\ln Z/\partial V$ |
> | $C_V$ | $k\beta^2\,\partial^2\ln Z/\partial\beta^2$ |
>
> *Source: Schroeder §6.5, eq. (6.64); Blundell §20.2, eqs. (20.16)–(20.22), Table 20.1; Schwartz 181, Lecture 8, eqs. (31)–(33)*

^thm-b7-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The differential of the Helmholtz free energy is $dF = -S\,dT - p\,dV + \mu\,dN$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]; for $\mu$ see [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium]]); read off the partial derivatives and insert $F = -kT\ln Z$ ([[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]]). For the first, $-\partial(-kT\ln Z)/\partial T = k\ln Z + kT\,\partial\ln Z/\partial T$, which by [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]] equals $k\ln Z + U/T$, as in Theorem §B7.2.1. For the pressure, the energies $E_s(V)$ depend on the volume (a particle in a smaller box has higher levels), and
>
> $$
> kT\,\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial V} = \frac{kT}{Z}\sum_s\Bigl(-\beta\,\frac{\partial E_s}{\partial V}\Bigr)e^{-\beta E_s} = \sum_s P(s)\Bigl(-\frac{\partial E_s}{\partial V}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Treating $N$ as continuous in $\partial/\partial N$ is the usual large-$N$ convention; strictly $\mu = F(N+1) - F(N)$.

## Composite systems

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.3: Partition Function of Independent Parts
> Suppose the states of a system are labelled by independent pairs $(i, j)$ with energies $E_{ij} = E^{(a)}_i + E^{(b)}_j$: two non-interacting, distinguishable subsystems, or two independent kinds of motion of one particle (translation and rotation, $x$- and $y$-motion). Then
>
> $$
> Z = Z_a\,Z_b, \qquad \ln Z = \ln Z_a + \ln Z_b ,
> $$
>
> so $F$, $U$, $S$ and $C_V$ are sums of the contributions of the parts. For $N$ non-interacting **distinguishable** units with identical single-unit partition functions $Z_1$ (atoms or spins fixed at lattice sites),
>
> $$
> Z_N = Z_1^{\,N}, \qquad F = -NkT\ln Z_1 .
> $$
>
> For identical, **indistinguishable** particles that are free to move (a gas), $Z_1^N$ counts each state many times over; the dilute-gas correction $Z_N = Z_1^N/N!$ is [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]].
>
> *Source: Schroeder §6.6, eqs. (6.65)–(6.69); Blundell §20.4, eqs. (20.36)–(20.39), §21.3; Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, eq. (37)*

^thm-b7-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Summing over all pairs, the Boltzmann factor factorises and so does the double sum:
>
> $$
> Z = \sum_i\sum_j e^{-\beta(E^{(a)}_i + E^{(b)}_j)} = \Bigl(\sum_i e^{-\beta E^{(a)}_i}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_j e^{-\beta E^{(b)}_j}\Bigr) = Z_aZ_b ,
> $$
>
> since the inner sum over $j$ does not depend on $i$ (for infinite sums of positive terms the rearrangement is always allowed). The logarithm turns the product into a sum, and every quantity in [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-2|Theorem §B7.2.2]] is linear in $\ln Z$. Induction on the number of parts gives $Z_1^N$. What needs care is the hypothesis that the composite states are exactly the pairs: for distinguishable units, "unit 1 in state $A$, unit 2 in state $B$" and "unit 1 in $B$, unit 2 in $A$" are different states, but for identical mobile particles they are one and the same state.

^der-b7-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]], [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-2|Theorem §B7.2.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Identical is not the same as indistinguishable
> Identical particles become distinguishable when they are localised: the spins of a paramagnetic crystal, one per lattice site, can be told apart by their positions, so $Z_N = Z_1^N$ is exact for them (Blundell §21.3). Electrons roaming through a metal, or molecules in a gas, cannot be labelled, and $Z_1^N$ is then wrong by roughly $N!$ ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox]]).

## Fluctuations and the equivalence of ensembles

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.4: Energy Fluctuations and Heat Capacity
> In the canonical ensemble the variance of the energy is
>
> $$
> \sigma_E^2 = \langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2 = \frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2} = kT^2\,C_V, \qquad\text{with}\qquad \langle E^2\rangle = \frac1Z\frac{\partial^2 Z}{\partial\beta^2} .
> $$
>
> In particular $C_V \ge 0$ for every system in canonical equilibrium.
>
> *Source: Schroeder Problems 6.16–6.18; Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, footnote 2, eqs. (24)–(25); PHY 306, Problem Set 8, Problem 3*

^thm-b7-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> As for the mean ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]]), each $\beta$-derivative of $Z$ brings down a factor $-E_s$, so $\partial^2Z/\partial\beta^2 = \sum_s E_s^2e^{-\beta E_s} = Z\langle E^2\rangle$. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2} = \frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\Bigl(\frac1Z\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta}\Bigr) = \frac1Z\frac{\partial^2Z}{\partial\beta^2} - \Bigl(\frac1Z\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta}\Bigr)^2 = \langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2 .
> $$
>
> Also $\partial^2\ln Z/\partial\beta^2 = -\partial U/\partial\beta = kT^2\,\partial U/\partial T = kT^2C_V$. A variance is never negative ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian]]), so neither is $C_V$.

^der-b7-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian]]

> [!remark] Remark: Heating without heating
> The heat capacity is a response: how much $U$ changes when $T$ is changed. Theorem §B7.2.4 says it is fixed by the size of the spontaneous fluctuations of $E$ in equilibrium, so it could be measured without ever heating the sample. Schwartz presents this as an instance of the fluctuation–dissipation theorem, whose other face is the link between the drag on a Brownian particle and its random walk ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion]]). The higher derivatives of $\ln Z$ give the higher cumulants of $E$: $\ln Z(\beta)$ is, up to sign conventions, the cumulant generating function of the energy.

^rem-b7-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.5: Equivalence of the Canonical and Microcanonical Ensembles
> Let a system of $N$ particles have an extensive energy and heat capacity ($U, C_V \propto N$). Then in the canonical ensemble at temperature $T$:
> 1. the relative energy fluctuation is small, $\sigma_E/U = \sqrt{kT^2C_V}/U = O(N^{-1/2})$;
> 2. the energy distribution $P(E) \propto \Omega(E)\,e^{-\beta E}$ is sharply peaked at the energy $U$ where $\partial S/\partial E = 1/T$, and near the peak it is Gaussian with variance $kT^2C_V$;
> 3. $-kT\ln Z = U - TS_{\text{micro}}(U) + O(kT\ln N)$, where $S_{\text{micro}} = k\ln\Omega$ is the microcanonical entropy; the correction is negligible against the $O(N)$ terms.
>
> So for macroscopic systems the canonical $F$, $S$ and $U(T)$ agree with those of the microcanonical ensemble at energy $E = \langle E\rangle$, and one may use whichever ensemble is easier.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §6.2 and Problems 6.18–6.19; Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, §2; Blundell ch. 4*

^thm-b7-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* By [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]], $\sigma_E = \sqrt{kT^2C_V} \propto \sqrt N$, while $U \propto N$.
>
> *2.* Group the states into energy shells of width $\delta E$ containing $\Omega(E) = g(E)\,\delta E$ states, with $S(E) = k\ln\Omega(E)$ the microcanonical entropy ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]]). The probability of the shell at $E$ is $\Omega(E)e^{-\beta E}/Z = e^{[S(E) - E/T]/k}/Z$ ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]]). The exponent is stationary where $\partial S/\partial E = 1/T$ ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy]]), i.e. at the energy $U$ that an isolated system at temperature $T$ would have. Its second derivative there is $S''(U)/k = -1/(kT^2C_V)$, because $\partial(1/T)/\partial E = -(1/T^2)\,\partial T/\partial E = -1/(T^2C_V)$. Taylor expansion to second order ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) gives
>
> $$
> P(E) \propto \exp\Bigl[-\frac{(E - U)^2}{2kT^2C_V}\Bigr],
> $$
>
> a Gaussian of variance $kT^2C_V$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian]]), consistent with part 1. The cubic and higher terms of the expansion change the distribution only at relative order $N^{-1/2}$, because each further derivative of $S$ with respect to $E$ carries one more factor $1/N$ while $E - U$ is of order $\sqrt N$.
>
> *3.* Replace the sum over states by an integral over shells and use the Gaussian integral ([[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-7|452 Ex. §15.7]]):
>
> $$
> Z = \int \frac{dE}{\delta E}\,e^{[S(E) - E/T]/k} \approx e^{[S(U) - U/T]/k}\,\frac{\sqrt{2\pi kT^2C_V}}{\delta E} .
> $$
>
> Taking $-kT\ln$: $F = U - TS(U) - kT\ln\bigl(\sqrt{2\pi kT^2C_V}/\delta E\bigr)$. The last term is the logarithm of a power of $N$, which is $O(\ln N)$, while $U$ and $TS$ are $O(N)$; the arbitrary shell width $\delta E$ enters only there.

^der-b7-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]], [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-7|452 Ex. §15.7]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian]]

> [!remark] Remark: How small the fluctuations are
> For $N$ classical oscillators, $U = NkT$ and $C_V = Nk$, so $\sigma_E/U = 1/\sqrt N$ exactly (Schroeder Problem 6.19): $1$ for one oscillator, $10^{-2}$ for $10^4$, $10^{-10}$ for $10^{20}$. This is why Schwartz can compute in the canonical ensemble and then simply set $\langle E\rangle = E$ for an isolated system, and why Schroeder can write $U$ for the "average" energy of a macroscopic body without comment.

^rem-b7-2-3

> [!caution] Caution: When the ensembles differ
> The argument needs $C_V$ finite and extensive. For a small system (one atom, one spin) the canonical energy fluctuates by as much as its mean, and the ensembles describe genuinely different situations: a spin in a crystal at temperature $T$ is not an isolated spin of fixed energy. At a first-order phase transition the canonical $P(E)$ has two peaks and $C_V$ diverges, and near a critical point fluctuations are large ([[§B9.3 The Classification of Phase Transitions]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B7.2.1: Free Energy and Entropy of N Oscillators
> A solid modelled as $N$ independent, distinguishable quantum oscillators of frequency $\omega$ (the Einstein solid, [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^mod-b6-1-4|Model §B6.1.4]]), with levels $(n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega$ ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|QM Theorem §A4.5.3]]). One oscillator has, summing the geometric series ([[§14 Series#^ex-14-4|451 Ex. §14.4]]),
>
> $$
> Z_1 = \sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-\beta\hbar\omega(n + \frac12)} = \frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}}{1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}} = \frac{1}{2\sinh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)}, \qquad U = -N\frac{\partial\ln Z_1}{\partial\beta} = \frac{N\hbar\omega}{2}\coth\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2} ,
> $$
>
> since $\hbar\omega\bigl(\tfrac12 + \tfrac{1}{e^{x} - 1}\bigr) = \tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}\,\tfrac{e^{x} + 1}{e^{x} - 1} = \tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}\coth\tfrac x2$ with $x = \beta\hbar\omega$. By [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]], $Z_N = Z_1^N$, and [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]] gives
>
> $$
> F = \frac{N\hbar\omega}{2} + NkT\ln\bigl(1 - e^{-x}\bigr), \qquad S = \frac{U - F}{T} = Nk\Bigl[\frac{x}{e^x - 1} - \ln\bigl(1 - e^{-x}\bigr)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> The zero-point energy $N\hbar\omega/2$ appears in $U$ and $F$ but cancels from $S$ and $C_V$ (Blundell Exercise 20.4). As $T \to 0$, $S \to 0$: one ground state.
>
> *High temperature,* $kT \gg \hbar\omega$: $1 - e^{-x} \approx x$, so $Z_1 \approx 1/\beta\hbar\omega$ and
>
> $$
> U = NkT, \qquad F = NkT\ln\frac{\hbar\omega}{kT}, \qquad S = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial T} = Nk\Bigl[\ln\frac{kT}{\hbar\omega} + 1\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Check against the microcanonical ensemble.* The Einstein solid with $q \gg N$ energy quanta has $\Omega \approx (eq/N)^N$, $S = Nk[\ln(q/N) + 1]$ ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-7|Theorem §B6.1.7]]; Schroeder eq. 2.21). At $U = q\hbar\omega = NkT$, $q/N = kT/\hbar\omega$: the same entropy, as [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-5|Theorem §B7.2.5]] promises. The canonical route needed one geometric series; the microcanonical one needed a binomial coefficient and Stirling's formula.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 9, Problem 2; Blundell Example 20.3(b), Exercises 20.1, 20.3–20.4; Schroeder Problem 6.42*

^ex-b7-2-1

> [!example] Example §B7.2.2: Energy Fluctuations of Sixteen Two-Level Atoms
> Each atom has levels $0$ and $\Delta$, with $\langle\varepsilon\rangle = \Delta/(e^{\beta\Delta} + 1)$ ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]]). Since $\varepsilon^2 = \Delta\,\varepsilon$ for the two values $0$ and $\Delta$, $\langle\varepsilon^2\rangle = \Delta\langle\varepsilon\rangle$, and
>
> $$
> \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = \frac{\Delta^2}{e^{\beta\Delta} + 1}\Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{e^{\beta\Delta} + 1}\Bigr) = \frac{\Delta^2}{(e^{\beta\Delta} + 1)(e^{-\beta\Delta} + 1)} = \frac{\Delta^2}{2 + 2\cosh\beta\Delta} .
> $$
>
> This is $kT^2$ times the one-atom heat capacity of Theorem §B7.1.4, as [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]] requires. The relative fluctuation of one atom is $\sigma_\varepsilon/\langle\varepsilon\rangle = e^{\beta\Delta/2}$: about $1$ at high temperature (each atom is a fair coin, up or down) and huge at low temperature (the atom is almost always in the ground state, and an occasional excitation is a large relative jump).
>
> For $16$ independent atoms, $E = \varepsilon_1 + \cdots + \varepsilon_{16}$, so $\langle E\rangle = 16\langle\varepsilon\rangle$ and, the atoms being independent, $\sigma_E^2 = 16\,\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian]]). Then
>
> $$
> \langle E^2\rangle = \langle E\rangle^2 + \sigma_E^2 = 16^2\langle\varepsilon\rangle^2\Bigl(1 + \frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{16\,\langle\varepsilon\rangle^2}\Bigr) = 16^2\langle\varepsilon\rangle^2\Bigl(1 + \frac{e^{\beta\Delta}}{16}\Bigr),
> $$
>
> and $\sigma_E/\langle E\rangle = e^{\beta\Delta/2}/4$, which is $0.41$ at $\beta\Delta = 1$. With $N$ atoms the factor $\tfrac14$ becomes $1/\sqrt N$: at $N = 10^{20}$ the fluctuation is invisible unless $\beta\Delta$ is so large that hardly any atom is excited.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 8, Problem 3(2)–(4)*
