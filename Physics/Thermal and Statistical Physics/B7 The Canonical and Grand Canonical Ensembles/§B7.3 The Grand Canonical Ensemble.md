*Sources: Schwartz, Physics 181 lectures, Lectures 7–8 · Schroeder, An Introduction to Thermal Physics, §7.1 · Blundell & Blundell, Concepts in Thermal Physics, ch. 22.*

The canonical ensemble lets energy flow between a system and its surroundings but keeps the number of particles fixed. Often particles flow too: molecules stick to and leave a surface, oxygen binds to haemoglobin, a small volume of gas exchanges molecules with the rest, and in a quantum gas the "system" will be a single orbital whose occupation number changes ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions]]). This section repeats the reservoir argument of [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]] with particle exchange. The reservoir now fixes the chemical potential $\mu$ as well as $T$, whose statistical meaning, $\mu = -T(\partial S/\partial N)_{U,V}$, is [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-3|Def. §B6.2.3]] and whose thermodynamics is [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]]. The result is the Gibbs factor, and the logarithm of its normalising sum is the grand potential $\Phi = F - \mu N$ of [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^def-b3-1-2|Def. §B3.1.2]].

## The Gibbs distribution

> [!definition] Definition §B7.3.1: Grand Canonical Ensemble
> A system that can exchange both energy and particles with a reservoir at temperature $T$ and chemical potential $\mu$, at fixed volume $V$, is in the **grand canonical ensemble**. The reservoir is so large that the energy and particles it exchanges leave $T$ and $\mu$ unchanged. The energy $E$ and the particle number $N$ of the system both fluctuate. With several species, the reservoir fixes one chemical potential $\mu_i$ per conserved species.
>
> *Source: Blundell §4.5, §22.3; Schroeder §7.1; Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, §1 and §6*

^def-b7-3-1

> [!definition] Definition §B7.3.2: Gibbs Factor; Grand Partition Function; Fugacity
> For a state $s$ with energy $E_s$ and particle number $N_s$, the **Gibbs factor** is $e^{-\beta(E_s - \mu N_s)}$, and the **grand partition function** (Gibbs sum) is
>
> $$
> \mathcal Z(T, V, \mu) = \sum_s e^{-\beta(E_s - \mu N_s)} = \sum_{N=0}^\infty z^N Z_N(T, V), \qquad z = e^{\beta\mu},
> $$
>
> where the first sum runs over all states with any number of particles, $Z_N$ is the canonical partition function with exactly $N$ particles ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]]), and $z$ is the **fugacity**. With several species the exponent is $-\beta(E_s - \sum_i\mu_i N_{i,s})$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.1, eqs. (7.5)–(7.8); Blundell §22.3, eq. (22.20), Exercise 22.2; Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, eqs. (57)–(58)*

^def-b7-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.3.1: Gibbs Distribution
> A system in thermal and diffusive equilibrium with a reservoir at temperature $T$ and chemical potential $\mu$ is found in state $s$ with probability
>
> $$
> P(s) = \frac{e^{-\beta(E_s - \mu N_s)}}{\mathcal Z} .
> $$
>
> For $\mu = 0$, or for states of one fixed $N$, it reduces to the Boltzmann distribution.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.1, eqs. (7.1)–(7.7); Blundell §22.3, eqs. (22.16)–(22.19); Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, §6.1, eqs. (56)–(57)*

^thm-b7-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> As in [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^der-b7-1-1|the derivation of Theorem §B7.1.1]], system plus reservoir is isolated, with fixed total energy and particle number, and by the fundamental postulate ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]) $P(s) \propto \Omega_R(U_{\text{tot}} - E_s, N_{\text{tot}} - N_s) = e^{S_R/k}$. Expand $S_R$ to first order in both small quantities, using $\partial S_R/\partial U_R = 1/T$ and $\partial S_R/\partial N_R = -\mu/T$ ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-3|Def. §B6.2.3]]):
>
> $$
> S_R(U_{\text{tot}} - E_s, N_{\text{tot}} - N_s) = S_R(U_{\text{tot}}, N_{\text{tot}}) - \frac{E_s}{T} + \frac{\mu N_s}{T} ,
> $$
>
> the higher terms vanishing in the reservoir limit as before. So $P(s) \propto e^{-(E_s - \mu N_s)/kT}$, and normalisation gives the factor $1/\mathcal Z$. Grouping the states by their particle number, the states with $N$ particles contribute $e^{\beta\mu N}\sum_{s:\,N_s = N}e^{-\beta E_s} = z^NZ_N$, which is the second form of $\mathcal Z$.

> [!remark] Remark: What μ does in the Gibbs factor
> A state with more particles is favoured when $\mu$ is high (a crowded, "particle-rich" reservoir pushes particles in) and suppressed when $\mu$ is low. Only $E_s - \mu N_s$ appears: shifting the energy of every particle by $\varepsilon_0$ is compensated by shifting $\mu$ by $\varepsilon_0$, so $\mu$, like a potential energy, is measured from the particle's ground-state energy (Schwartz, Lecture 7, §7.2). For a classical ideal gas $\mu$ lies below that ground-state energy, by an amount of order $kT\ln(1/n\lambda_{\text{th}}^3)$ ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]]).

^rem-b7-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.3.2: Mean Particle Number, Energy and Number Fluctuations
> In the grand canonical ensemble, with derivatives at fixed $V$ and the other one of $T$ or $\beta$, $\mu$ fixed:
>
> $$
> \langle N\rangle = kT\,\frac{\partial\ln\mathcal Z}{\partial\mu} = z\,\frac{\partial\ln\mathcal Z}{\partial z}, \qquad U = \langle E\rangle = -\frac{\partial\ln\mathcal Z}{\partial\beta}\Big|_{\mu} + \mu\langle N\rangle = -\frac{\partial\ln\mathcal Z}{\partial\beta}\Big|_{z} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \sigma_N^2 = \langle N^2\rangle - \langle N\rangle^2 = (kT)^2\,\frac{\partial^2\ln\mathcal Z}{\partial\mu^2} = kT\,\Bigl(\frac{\partial\langle N\rangle}{\partial\mu}\Bigr)_{T,V} .
> $$
>
> For a macroscopic system $\langle N\rangle$ and $\partial\langle N\rangle/\partial\mu$ are both proportional to $V$, so $\sigma_N/\langle N\rangle = O(\langle N\rangle^{-1/2})$, and the grand canonical and canonical ensembles give the same thermodynamics, as in [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-5|Theorem §B7.2.5]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, eqs. (59)–(61); Blundell §22.3, eqs. (22.21)–(22.22); Schroeder Problem 7.6*

^thm-b7-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate $\mathcal Z = \sum_s e^{-\beta E_s + \beta\mu N_s}$ term by term. A $\mu$-derivative brings down $\beta N_s$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\mathcal Z}{\partial\mu} = \beta\sum_s N_s\,e^{-\beta(E_s - \mu N_s)} = \beta\,\mathcal Z\,\langle N\rangle, \qquad \frac{\partial^2\mathcal Z}{\partial\mu^2} = \beta^2\,\mathcal Z\,\langle N^2\rangle .
> $$
>
> The first gives $\langle N\rangle$; with $z = e^{\beta\mu}$, $z\,\partial/\partial z = kT\,\partial/\partial\mu$ at fixed $T$. As in [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^der-b7-2-4|the derivation of Theorem §B7.2.4]], $\partial^2\ln\mathcal Z/\partial\mu^2 = \mathcal Z''/\mathcal Z - (\mathcal Z'/\mathcal Z)^2 = \beta^2(\langle N^2\rangle - \langle N\rangle^2)$, and also $= \partial(\beta\langle N\rangle)/\partial\mu$. A $\beta$-derivative at fixed $\mu$ brings down $-(E_s - \mu N_s)$, so $-\partial\ln\mathcal Z/\partial\beta = \langle E\rangle - \mu\langle N\rangle$; at fixed $z$, with $\mathcal Z = \sum_s z^{N_s}e^{-\beta E_s}$, it brings down only $-E_s$ and gives $\langle E\rangle$ directly. (Which variable is held fixed matters: $-\partial\ln\mathcal Z/\partial\beta$ at fixed $\mu$ is not $U$.) The scaling statement follows because $\sigma_N \propto \sqrt V$ while $\langle N\rangle \propto V$.

## The grand potential

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.3.3: Grand Potential from the Grand Partition Function
> The grand potential $\Phi = F - \mu N = U - TS - \mu N$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^def-b3-1-2|Def. §B3.1.2]]) is
>
> $$
> \Phi(T, V, \mu) = -kT\ln\mathcal Z, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \mathcal Z = e^{-\beta\Phi},
> $$
>
> with $N$ and $U$ the ensemble means. Its differential is $d\Phi = -S\,dT - p\,dV - N\,d\mu$, so
>
> $$
> S = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{V,\mu}, \qquad p = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial V}\Bigr)_{T,\mu}, \qquad \langle N\rangle = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\mu}\Bigr)_{T,V} .
> $$
>
> For a homogeneous system whose entropy is extensive, $\Phi = -pV$ (the Euler relation, [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]]), so $pV = kT\ln\mathcal Z$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §22.4–22.5, eqs. (22.23)–(22.34), (22.49); Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, eqs. (62)–(64), and Lecture 8, §7, eqs. (97)–(103); Schroeder Problem 7.7*

^thm-b7-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Logarithm.* The Gibbs entropy ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^def-b6-3-1|Def. §B6.3.1]]) with $\ln P(s) = -\beta(E_s - \mu N_s) - \ln\mathcal Z$ from [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-1|Theorem §B7.3.1]] is
>
> $$
> S = -k\sum_s P(s)\ln P(s) = k\beta\bigl(U - \mu\langle N\rangle\bigr) + k\ln\mathcal Z ,
> $$
>
> so $-kT\ln\mathcal Z = U - TS - \mu\langle N\rangle = \Phi$, as in [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^der-b7-2-1|Route 1 for Theorem §B7.2.1]].
>
> *Differential.* $d\Phi = dF - \mu\,dN - N\,d\mu$ and $dF = -S\,dT - p\,dV + \mu\,dN$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]) give $d\Phi = -S\,dT - p\,dV - N\,d\mu$ (a Legendre transform of $F$ in $N$; [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|Def. §B1.2.2]]). The third derivative agrees with [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-2|Theorem §B7.3.2]].
>
> *Euler relation.* $\Phi(T, V, \mu)$ has only one extensive argument, $V$. Scaling the system by $\lambda$ at fixed $T$ and $\mu$ scales $\Phi$ by $\lambda$, so $\Phi = V\phi(T, \mu)$, and $p = -\partial\Phi/\partial V = -\phi$: $\Phi = -pV$. This is the Euler relation of [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]], seen from the one extensive argument of $\Phi$.

> [!caution] Caution: Names and symbols
> This vault writes $\Phi$ for the grand potential and $\mathcal Z$ for the grand partition function; $\Omega$ is reserved for the multiplicity. Blundell writes $\Phi_G$ ("grand potential"), Schroeder $\Phi$ ("grand free energy", introduced in his Problem 5.23), Schwartz calls it the grand free energy; many other books write $\Omega$. Schroeder writes $\mathcal Z$ for the Gibbs sum and calls it the grand partition function, as here.

^cau-b7-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Three ensembles, one pattern
> | ensemble | fixed | fluctuating | weight of a state | sum | potential |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | microcanonical | $U, V, N$ | — | $1/\Omega$ | $\Omega$ | $S = k\ln\Omega$ |
> | canonical | $T, V, N$ | $E$ | $e^{-\beta E_s}/Z$ | $Z$ | $F = -kT\ln Z$ |
> | grand canonical | $T, V, \mu$ | $E, N$ | $e^{-\beta(E_s - \mu N_s)}/\mathcal Z$ | $\mathcal Z$ | $\Phi = -kT\ln\mathcal Z$ |
>
> Each step trades a conserved quantity for its conjugate intensive variable, $U \to T$, then $N \to \mu$, and each sum is related to the previous one by a Laplace-type transform: $Z = \sum_E\Omega(E)e^{-\beta E}$, $\mathcal Z = \sum_N z^NZ_N$. On the thermodynamic side the same step is a Legendre transform ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|Def. §B1.2.2]]). Schwartz adds a fourth, the "Gibbs ensemble" with variable volume, whose conjugate variable is the pressure; he uses it to identify $p = T(\partial S/\partial V)_{E}$ rather than to compute. Which ensemble to use is a matter of convenience: for large systems they agree.
>
> *Source: Blundell §22.3; Schwartz 181, Lecture 7, §5–6*

## Adsorption

> [!model] Model §B7.3.4: Langmuir Adsorption Sites
> A surface (or a protein) carries independent adsorption sites. Each site can be empty, with energy $0$, or hold one molecule bound with energy $\varepsilon < 0$; it cannot hold two. The sites exchange molecules and energy with a surrounding gas at temperature $T$ and chemical potential $\mu$, which acts as the reservoir. Bound molecules do not interact with one another, so the occupation of one site does not affect another. Myoglobin, with one binding site per molecule, fits the model well; haemoglobin, whose four sites bind cooperatively, does not exactly.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.1 (example and Problems 7.1–7.2)*

^mod-b7-3-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.3.5: Langmuir Adsorption Isotherm
> For [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^mod-b7-3-4|Model §B7.3.4]], the probability that a site is occupied is
>
> $$
> f = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)} + 1} .
> $$
>
> If the gas is a classical ideal gas at partial pressure $p$, so that $e^{\beta\mu} = p/p_Q(T)$ with $p_Q = kT\,Z_{\text{int}}/\lambda_{\text{th}}^3$ ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]]), then at fixed temperature
>
> $$
> f = \frac{p}{p + p_0(T)}, \qquad p_0(T) = p_Q(T)\,e^{\beta\varepsilon} ,
> $$
>
> the **Langmuir adsorption isotherm**: linear in $p$ at low pressure, saturating at full coverage for $p \gg p_0$. With a second species competing for the same sites, $f_1 = e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_1 - \mu_1)}/\bigl(1 + e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_1 - \mu_1)} + e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_2 - \mu_2)}\bigr)$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.1, eqs. (7.9)–(7.15) and Problem 7.1*

^thm-b7-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take one site as the system ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^def-b7-3-1|Def. §B7.3.1]]). Its two states have $(E, N) = (0, 0)$ and $(\varepsilon, 1)$, so $\mathcal Z = 1 + e^{-\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)}$, and by [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-1|Theorem §B7.3.1]] the occupied state has probability $e^{-\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)}/\mathcal Z = 1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)} + 1)$; equivalently $f = \langle N\rangle = kT\,\partial\ln\mathcal Z/\partial\mu$ ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-2|Theorem §B7.3.2]]). Insert $e^{\beta\mu} = p/p_Q$: $f = 1/\bigl(1 + (p_Q/p)e^{\beta\varepsilon}\bigr) = p/(p + p_0)$. The independent sites are distinguishable, so the fraction of occupied sites equals $f$ ([[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]]). With a second species the site has three states, $\mathcal Z = 1 + e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_1 - \mu_1)} + e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_2 - \mu_2)}$.

^der-b7-3-5

*Uses:* [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^mod-b7-3-4|Model §B7.3.4]], [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-1|Theorem §B7.3.1]], [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-2|Theorem §B7.3.2]], [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]], [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]]

> [!remark] Remark: The Fermi–Dirac shape
> The occupancy $1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)} + 1)$ of a site that holds at most one particle is exactly the Fermi–Dirac distribution for a single-particle state that the exclusion principle allows to hold at most one fermion; allowing any number of particles in one state gives the Bose–Einstein form instead. Both follow by applying the Gibbs distribution to one orbital at a time ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]). This is the main use of the grand canonical ensemble in Chapters B10–B13.

## Examples

> [!example]- ★ Example §B7.3.1: Oxygen, Carbon Monoxide and Haemoglobin
> Model one haem site ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^mod-b7-3-4|Model §B7.3.4]]) with binding energy $\varepsilon = -0.7\ \mathrm{eV}$ for O$_2$ (Schroeder's illustrative value). Near the lungs the blood is in diffusive equilibrium with air in which O$_2$ has partial pressure $0.2\ \mathrm{atm}$; at body temperature, $310\ \mathrm K$, $kT = 0.0267\ \mathrm{eV}$, and the ideal-gas formula gives $\mu \approx -0.6\ \mathrm{eV}$ ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^ex-b8-1-2|Example §B8.1.2]]). The Gibbs factor of the occupied state is
>
> $$
> e^{-(\varepsilon - \mu)/kT} \approx e^{(0.1\ \mathrm{eV})/(0.0267\ \mathrm{eV})} \approx 40, \qquad P(\mathrm O_2) = \frac{40}{1 + 40} = 98\% .
> $$
>
> Now add carbon monoxide at $1/100$ of the oxygen concentration. Its chemical potential is lower by $kT\ln 100 = 0.12\ \mathrm{eV}$, $\mu' \approx -0.72\ \mathrm{eV}$, but it binds more strongly, $\varepsilon' \approx -0.85\ \mathrm{eV}$, so its Gibbs factor is $e^{(0.13\ \mathrm{eV})/(0.0267\ \mathrm{eV})} \approx 130$ (Schroeder rounds to $120$). By the three-state form of [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-5|Theorem §B7.3.5]],
>
> $$
> P(\mathrm O_2) = \frac{40}{1 + 40 + 130} = 23\%, \qquad P(\mathrm{CO}) = 76\% .
> $$
>
> A trace of CO takes three quarters of the sites: the exponential weighting turns a binding-energy advantage of $0.15\ \mathrm{eV}$, a few $kT$, into a factor of hundreds, which more than beats a hundredfold dilution. (The energies are rough; the point is the mechanism.)
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.1, eqs. (7.9)–(7.15)*

^ex-b7-3-1

> [!example] Example §B7.3.2: The Ideal Gas in the Grand Canonical Ensemble
> For a classical ideal gas $Z_N = Z_1^N/N!$ ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]), so by [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^def-b7-3-2|Def. §B7.3.2]] and the exponential series,
>
> $$
> \mathcal Z = \sum_{N=0}^\infty \frac{(zZ_1)^N}{N!} = e^{zZ_1}, \qquad \ln\mathcal Z = zZ_1 .
> $$
>
> [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-2|Theorem §B7.3.2]] gives $\langle N\rangle = z\,\partial(zZ_1)/\partial z = zZ_1$, so $z = e^{\beta\mu} = \langle N\rangle/Z_1$, i.e. $\mu = -kT\ln(Z_1/\langle N\rangle)$, the canonical result of Theorem §B8.1.6. The probability of exactly $N$ particles is $z^NZ_N/\mathcal Z = e^{-\langle N\rangle}\langle N\rangle^N/N!$, a **Poisson distribution** ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-8|Def. §B4.1.8]], whose variance equals its mean, [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-9|Theorem §B4.1.9]]), and
>
> $$
> \sigma_N^2 = z\frac{\partial\langle N\rangle}{\partial z} = zZ_1 = \langle N\rangle, \qquad \frac{\sigma_N}{\langle N\rangle} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\langle N\rangle}} .
> $$
>
> A sub-volume of air containing $10^6$ molecules on average has its number fluctuating by $0.1\%$; one containing $10^{20}$, by $10^{-10}$. Finally [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-3|Theorem §B7.3.3]] gives $\Phi = -kT\ln\mathcal Z = -kT\langle N\rangle$, and with $\Phi = -pV$ this is $pV = \langle N\rangle kT$, the ideal gas law. Blundell runs the check the other way: from $F$ and $\mu$ of the ideal gas he finds $\Phi_G = F - \mu N = -NkT = -pV$. The fugacity of an ideal gas is $z = \langle N\rangle/Z_1 = n\lambda_{\text{th}}^3$ for a monatomic gas: $z \ll 1$ is the classical, dilute regime.
>
> *Source: Schroeder Problem 7.6; Blundell Example 22.2, Exercise 22.2*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The step from $Z$ to $\mathcal Z = \sum_N z^NZ_N$ is a generating function in the fugacity; the step from $F$ to $\Phi$ is a Legendre transform ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|Def. §B1.2.2]]); the ideal-gas $\mathcal Z = e^{zZ_1}$ is the exponential series. Generating functions in general belong to Mathematical Methods (planned).
> - The Gibbs distribution applied to one orbital gives the Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac occupation numbers, [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]] and [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]; the photon gas, with no conserved particle number, has $\mu = 0$ ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-2|Theorem §B11.1.2]]).
> - Chemical equilibrium and the law of mass action, where several $\mu_i$ meet, are developed from the Gibbs free energy in [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]] and [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-6|Theorem §B3.3.6]].
