Chapter [[§C1.1 Systems, Equilibrium and the First Two Laws|C1]] treated thermodynamics without atoms. Statistical mechanics trades the question "which trajectory?", which cannot be answered, for one that can: how many microstates look the same from the outside ([[§C0.2 The Arrow of Time and the Statistical Program#^rem-c0-2-4|§C0.2, Remark: The program of the course]]). The whole of equilibrium statistical mechanics rests on one assumption about that state space, stated here. Level B stated the same postulate ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]); level C adds the precise meaning of "accessible" and keeps the microstate an abstract label $\nu$ until Chapter C3 says what it is classically and quantum mechanically.

**Setup.** An isolated system in equilibrium: its exact conserved quantities (energy, and any others) and the imposed macroscopic constraints are fixed. A microstate is a label $\nu$; nothing about classical or quantum mechanics is used yet.

## The assumption

> [!definition] Definition §C2.1.1: Microstate
> A **microstate** is a complete microscopic description of the system: every component specified at the microscopic level. Examples: the positions and momenta of all particles of a classical gas, a quantum state of the whole system, the up/down value of every spin. Many microstates correspond to the same macroscopic properties.
>
> *Model:* any system; *applies:* the state of the whole system, not of one particle. Chapter C3 makes the label precise.
>
> *Source: Mao §2.1.1 · Lecture 9/8 · level-B home: [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-1|Def. §B6.1.1]]*

^def-c2-1-1

> [!principle] Principle §C2.1.1: Fundamental Assumption of Statistical Mechanics
> For an isolated system in equilibrium, all accessible microstates, those compatible with the exact conserved quantities and the imposed macroscopic constraints, are assigned equal probability.
>
> *Domain:* isolated systems in equilibrium; "accessible" refers to the dynamically connected sector relevant to the preparation and the observation time ([[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^cau-c2-1-1|§C2.1, Caution: Two qualifiers that carry the content]]).
>
> *Source: Mao §2.1.1 · Lecture 9/8 · level-B home: [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]*

^pr-c2-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Two qualifiers that carry the content
> 1. **Accessible.** Besides energy, any conserved quantity (particle number, momentum, angular momentum, charge, a topological sector) can cut the state space into pieces that the motion never joins. The equal weights apply within the piece the system was prepared in, over the time it is watched.
> 2. **In equilibrium.** The assumption describes equilibrium once it is reached. Whether a given initial state gets there, and how fast, is a separate question, taken up in Unit 2 ([[§C5.4 Markov Dynamics and the Apparent Arrow of Time|§C5.4]]).

^cau-c2-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §C2.1.2: Accessible Set; Number of Accessible Microstates
> $\Gamma_{\text{acc}}$ is the set of microstates compatible with the exact constraints of an isolated system, and
>
> $$
> \Omega_{\text{acc}} = |\Gamma_{\text{acc}}| ,
> $$
>
> with $|\cdot|$ a count in a discrete model, or an appropriate dimensionless measure in a continuous one (phase-space volume in units of a cell, Chapter C3).
>
> *Source: Mao §2.1.1, eq. (2.1)*

^def-c2-1-2

## Ensembles

> [!definition] Definition §C2.1.3: Statistical Ensemble
> A **statistical ensemble** is a probability distribution over the microstates of a system:
>
> $$
> p_\nu \ge 0, \qquad \sum_\nu p_\nu = 1 .
> $$
>
> It fixes both which microstates occur and with what weight.
>
> *Source: Mao §2.1.1 · Lecture 9/8*

^def-c2-1-3

> [!definition] Definition §C2.1.4: Microcanonical Ensemble
> For an isolated system in equilibrium with fixed macroscopic constraints, the **microcanonical ensemble** is
>
> $$
> p_\nu = \begin{cases}1/\Omega_{\text{acc}}, & \nu \in \Gamma_{\text{acc}},\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
> $$
>
> It is the ensemble ([[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^def-c2-1-3|Def. §C2.1.3]]) that [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^pr-c2-1-1|Principle §C2.1.1]] defines.
>
> *Source: Mao §2.1.1, eq. (2.2) · level-B home: [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-3|Def. §B6.1.3]]*

^def-c2-1-4

## Macrostates

> [!definition] Definition §C2.1.5: Macrostate
> A **macrostate** $M$ fixes the values of a few coarse, measurable variables, such as the energy, the magnetization, a density, or how many particles are on one side of a box. It is the set
>
> $$
> \Gamma_M \subseteq \Gamma_{\text{acc}}
> $$
>
> of microstates that have those values.
>
> *Source: Mao §2.1.1, eq. (2.3)*

^def-c2-1-5

> [!definition] Definition §C2.1.6: Multiplicity
> The **multiplicity** of a macrostate is $\Omega(M) = |\Gamma_M|$.
>
> *Source: Mao §2.1.1, eq. (2.4) · level-B home: [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-2|Def. §B6.1.2]]*

^def-c2-1-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.1.2: Probability of a Macrostate
> In the microcanonical ensemble,
>
> $$
> P(M) = \frac{\Omega(M)}{\Omega_{\text{acc}}} .
> $$
>
> Microstates are equally likely, macrostates are not: the weight of a macrostate is its share of the microstates.
>
> *Model:* [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]; *applies:* any macrostate of an isolated system in equilibrium.
>
> *Source: Mao §2.1.1, eq. (2.5) · level-B home: [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]]*

^thm-c2-1-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> The events "$\nu$" for $\nu \in \Gamma_M$ are disjoint, so $P(M) = \sum_{\nu\in\Gamma_M}p_\nu$. Each term is $1/\Omega_{\text{acc}}$ by [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]], and there are $\Omega(M)$ terms ([[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^def-c2-1-6|Def. §C2.1.6]]).

^der-c2-1-1

## Two binary systems

The two counting problems below have the same combinatorics. They run through the whole chapter ([[§C2.5 Two-State Units, Free Expansion and Oscillators|§C2.5]]).

> [!example] Example §C2.1.1: $N$ Two-State Spins
> Let $s_i = \uparrow, \downarrow$ for $i = 1, \dots, N$. A microstate is the whole string $\nu = (s_1, \dots, s_N)$. With no further constraint every spin is free, so
>
> $$
> \Omega_{\text{acc}} = 2^N, \qquad P(\nu) = 2^{-N} .
> $$
>
> Keep only the number $N_\uparrow$ of up spins as the macrostate. Choosing which spins are up,
>
> $$
> \Omega_N(N_\uparrow) = \binom{N}{N_\uparrow}, \qquad P_N(N_\uparrow) = 2^{-N}\binom{N}{N_\uparrow}
> $$
>
> by [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^thm-c2-1-2|Theorem §C2.1.2]].
>
> *Source: Mao §2.1.2, eqs. (2.6)–(2.9)*

^ex-c2-1-1

> [!example] Example §C2.1.2: A Gas in a Box, Left or Right
> Divide a box into equal halves and keep only which half each of $N$ particles is in: $\nu = (\ell_1, \dots, \ell_N)$, $\ell_i = L, R$. If the halves are equivalent, all $2^N$ assignments are equally weighted, and with $N_L$ particles on the left as the macrostate,
>
> $$
> \Omega(M_{N_L}) = \binom{N}{N_L}, \qquad P(M_{N_L}) = 2^{-N}\binom{N}{N_L} .
> $$
>
> This is [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^ex-c2-1-1|Example §C2.1.1]] under $\uparrow \leftrightarrow L$, $\downarrow \leftrightarrow R$. Nothing about large $N$ has been used.
>
> *Source: Mao §2.1.2, eqs. (2.10)–(2.12)*

^ex-c2-1-2
