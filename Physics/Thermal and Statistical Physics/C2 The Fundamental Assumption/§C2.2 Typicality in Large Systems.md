The binomials of [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^ex-c2-1-1|Example §C2.1.1]] and [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^ex-c2-1-2|Example §C2.1.2]] are exact for any $N$. For large $N$ they become extremely sharply concentrated: almost every accessible microstate gives nearly the same macroscopic values. That concentration is why thermodynamic measurements are reproducible, and it is the statistical half of irreversibility ([[§C2.3 Typicality and Irreversibility|§C2.3]]). Level B proved the Gaussian limit and the central limit theorem ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-8|Theorem §B4.1.8]], [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]]); level C adds the large-deviation form and the notion of typicality.

**Setup.** The binomial distribution $P_N(N_\uparrow) = 2^{-N}\binom{N}{N_\uparrow}$ of either binary system, with the intensive variable $n = N_\uparrow/N$: the fraction of up spins, or of particles on the left.

## Stirling and the exponent

> [!definition] Definition §C2.2.1: Binary Entropy Function
> $$
> h(n) = -n\ln n - (1 - n)\ln(1 - n), \qquad 0 \le n \le 1 .
> $$
>
> It is maximal at $n = \tfrac12$, with $h(\tfrac12) = \ln2$, and vanishes at $n = 0, 1$.
>
> *Source: Mao §2.2.1, eq. (2.17)*

^def-c2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.1: Logarithm of the Multiplicity
> For $N \gg 1$ and $0 < n < 1$ fixed,
>
> $$
> \ln\Omega_N(Nn) \simeq Nh(n) - \tfrac12\ln\bigl[2\pi Nn(1 - n)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Model:* [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^ex-c2-1-1|Example §C2.1.1]]; *applies:* $Nn \gg 1$ and $N(1 - n) \gg 1$; error $O(1/N)$.
>
> *Source: Mao §2.2.1, eqs. (2.15)–(2.16)*

^thm-c2-2-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (Stirling). $j! \simeq \sqrt{2\pi j}\,(j/e)^j$, so $\ln j! \simeq j\ln j - j + \tfrac12\ln(2\pi j)$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]).
>
> **Step 2** (three factorials). $\ln\binom{N}{Nn} = \ln N! - \ln(Nn)! - \ln(N(1-n))!$. The terms $-j$ cancel because $N = Nn + N(1 - n)$. The $j\ln j$ terms give
>
> $$
> N\ln N - Nn\ln(Nn) - N(1-n)\ln(N(1-n)) = -Nn\ln n - N(1-n)\ln(1-n) = Nh(n) ,
> $$
>
> after expanding $\ln(Nn) = \ln N + \ln n$ and cancelling $N\ln N$ against $(Nn + N(1-n))\ln N$.
>
> **Step 3** (prefactors). $\tfrac12\ln(2\pi N) - \tfrac12\ln(2\pi Nn) - \tfrac12\ln(2\pi N(1-n)) = -\tfrac12\ln\bigl[2\pi Nn(1 - n)\bigr]$.

^der-c2-2-1

*Uses:* [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.2: Exponential Form of the Binomial
> $$
> P_N(Nn) \simeq \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi Nn(1 - n)}}\exp\bigl\{N\bigl[h(n) - \ln2\bigr]\bigr\} .
> $$
>
> The exponent is maximal, and zero, at $n = \tfrac12$, and negative elsewhere, so every macroscopically different $n$ is exponentially suppressed in $N$.
>
> *Model:* [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^ex-c2-1-1|Example §C2.1.1]] or [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^ex-c2-1-2|Example §C2.1.2]]; *applies:* as in [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]].
>
> *Source: Mao §2.2.1, eq. (2.18)*

^thm-c2-2-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Multiply $e^{\ln\Omega_N}$ from [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]] by $2^{-N} = e^{-N\ln2}$ ([[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^ex-c2-1-1|Example §C2.1.1]]).

^der-c2-2-2

*Uses:* [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-1|Theorem §C2.2.1]], [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^ex-c2-1-1|Example §C2.1.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.3: Gaussian Peak
> Near the maximum,
>
> $$
> P_N(N_\uparrow) \simeq \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi N}}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{2(N_\uparrow - N/2)^2}{N}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Model:* as in [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]; *applies:* $|N_\uparrow - N/2| = O(\sqrt N)$, not in the tails ([[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^cau-c2-2-1|§C2.2, Caution: The Gaussian is wrong for macroscopic deviations]]).
>
> *Source: Mao §2.2.1, eqs. (2.19)–(2.20) · level-B home: [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-8|Theorem §B4.1.8]]*

^thm-c2-2-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (expand $h$). $h'(n) = \ln\frac{1-n}{n}$, $h''(n) = -\frac{1}{n(1-n)}$, so $h'(\tfrac12) = 0$, $h''(\tfrac12) = -4$, and $h'''(\tfrac12) = 0$ by the symmetry $n \leftrightarrow 1 - n$. With $n = \tfrac12 + \delta$,
>
> $$
> h(\tfrac12 + \delta) = \ln2 - 2\delta^2 + O(\delta^4) .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (exponent). $N[h - \ln2] = -2N\delta^2$ with $\delta = (N_\uparrow - N/2)/N$, which is $-2(N_\uparrow - N/2)^2/N$.
>
> **Step 3** (prefactor). Evaluate it at $n = \tfrac12$: $1/\sqrt{2\pi N/4} = \sqrt{2/(\pi N)}$. It depends on $n(1 - n) = \tfrac14 - \delta^2$, with no linear term by symmetry, so over the peak ($\delta = O(N^{-1/2})$) it changes only at relative order $1/N$.
>
> **Step 4** (dropped term). $N\delta^4 = (N_\uparrow - N/2)^4/N^3$ is $O(1/N)$ for $|N_\uparrow - N/2| = O(\sqrt N)$.

^der-c2-2-3

*Uses:* [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^def-c2-2-1|Def. §C2.2.1]]

## Width of the peak

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.4: Mean, Variance and Relative Width
> Exactly,
>
> $$
> \langle N_\uparrow\rangle = \frac N2, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(N_\uparrow) = \frac N4 .
> $$
>
> So the absolute fluctuation grows, $\Delta N_\uparrow \sim \sqrt N$, while the fluctuation of the intensive variable shrinks:
>
> $$
> \Delta n = \frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(N_\uparrow)}}{N} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt N} .
> $$
>
> *Model:* [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^ex-c2-1-1|Example §C2.1.1]]; *applies:* exact, any $N$.
>
> *Source: Mao §2.2.1, eqs. (2.21)–(2.23) · level-B home: [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-7|Theorem §B4.1.7]]*

^thm-c2-2-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Write $N_\uparrow = \sum_iX_i$ with $X_i = 1$ for an up spin and $0$ otherwise. Under the uniform distribution the $X_i$ are independent with $\langle X_i\rangle = \tfrac12$ and $\langle X_i^2\rangle = \tfrac12$, so $\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \tfrac14$. Means add, and for independent variables variances add ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]]): $\langle N_\uparrow\rangle = N/2$, $\operatorname{Var}(N_\uparrow) = N/4$. Divide the standard deviation by $N$ for $n$.

^der-c2-2-4

*Uses:* [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^ex-c2-1-1|Example §C2.1.1]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]]

![[ph-th-c2-2-1.svg]]

*Figure: the distribution of $n = N_\uparrow/N$ for $N = 10$, $100$ and $1000$, drawn as the density $N\,P_N(Nn)$ from [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]. The width shrinks as $1/(2\sqrt N)$ ([[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]]) while the area stays $1$.*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.5: What Becomes Certain, and What Does Not
> 1. The probability of the single exact value tends to zero:
>
> $$
> P_N(N/2) \simeq \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi N}} \longrightarrow 0 .
> $$
>
> 2. Every fixed macroscopic neighbourhood of $n = \tfrac12$ has probability tending to one: for every fixed $\epsilon > 0$,
>
> $$
> P\bigl(|n - \tfrac12| < \epsilon\bigr) \longrightarrow 1 \qquad (N \to \infty) .
> $$
>
> In counting terms: the share of microstates whose imbalance is a fixed fraction of $N$ goes to zero.
>
> *Model:* [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^ex-c2-1-1|Example §C2.1.1]] or [[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^ex-c2-1-2|Example §C2.1.2]]; *applies:* $N \to \infty$ at fixed $\epsilon$.
>
> *Source: Mao §2.2.2, eqs. (2.24)–(2.25) · the Chebyshev step is written here*

^thm-c2-2-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Part 1.** Set $N_\uparrow = N/2$ in [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]].
>
> **Part 2.** Chebyshev's inequality for $n$, with $\langle n\rangle = \tfrac12$ and $\operatorname{Var}(n) = 1/(4N)$ from [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]]:
>
> $$
> P\bigl(|n - \tfrac12| \ge \epsilon\bigr) \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(n)}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{1}{4N\epsilon^2} \longrightarrow 0 .
> $$
>
> Chebyshev's inequality is $P(|Y - \langle Y\rangle| \ge \epsilon) \le \operatorname{Var}(Y)/\epsilon^2$, from $\operatorname{Var}(Y) \ge \epsilon^2P(|Y - \langle Y\rangle| \ge \epsilon)$, keeping only the part of the sum or integral where $|Y - \langle Y\rangle| \ge \epsilon$.

^der-c2-2-5

*Uses:* [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]], [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-4|Theorem §C2.2.4]]

## Typicality

> [!definition] Definition §C2.2.2: Typical Property
> A macroscopic property is **typical** if, as the system grows, the accessible microstates without it become a vanishing fraction of all accessible microstates.
>
> In the binary examples, "$n$ is close to $\tfrac12$" is typical ([[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-5|Theorem §C2.2.5]], part 2).
>
> *Source: Mao §2.2.2 · Lecture 9/10*

^def-c2-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Typical is more than most probable
> The most probable macrostate can itself be improbable, as $P_N(N/2) \to 0$ shows. Typicality is a statement about almost all microstates at once: they agree on the macroscopic variables to within a shrinking window. Which microstate the system is in remains unknown; what it looks like from outside does not.

## General large systems

The binary peak is a case of the central limit theorem. Its home is level B:

![[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.6: Central Limit Theorem for an Intensive Average
> Let $A = \sum_{i=1}^NX_i$ with independent, identically distributed $X_i$ of mean $\mu$ and finite nonzero variance $\sigma^2$; the distribution of $X_i$ need not be Gaussian. For large $N$, the density of $a = A/N$ near its mean is
>
> $$
> p_N(a) \simeq \sqrt{\frac{N}{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{N(a - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr], \qquad \langle a\rangle = \mu, \quad \operatorname{Var}(a) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}, \quad \Delta a = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt N} .
> $$
>
> For discrete $a$ the density is a smooth approximation: values of $a$ are spaced by $1/N$, so $p_N(a) \simeq N\,P_N(Na)$. With $X_i = 1, 0$ for up and down spins, $A = N_\uparrow$ and $a = n$; with $X_i = \pm1$, $A$ is the magnetization.
>
> *Model:* independent identical contributions; *applies:* $|a - \mu| = O(N^{-1/2})$.
>
> *Source: Mao §2.2.3, eqs. (2.26)–(2.28) · proof at level B: [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]]*

^thm-c2-2-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> The general statement is the embedded theorem above, applied to the sum $A$ and rescaled by $1/N$: $\operatorname{Var}(A/N) = \operatorname{Var}(A)/N^2 = \sigma^2/N$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]]). The density of $a$ is $N$ times that of $A$ at $A = Na$. For the binary case it reproduces [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]] with $\mu = \tfrac12$, $\sigma^2 = \tfrac14$.

^der-c2-2-6

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Law of large numbers, central limit, large deviations
> Three statements of increasing detail. The law of large numbers: $a$ tends to $\mu$. The central limit theorem: the remaining spread is Gaussian with width $\propto N^{-1/2}$. Large deviations: a deviation that is a fixed fraction, not shrinking with $N$, has probability falling exponentially in $N$, with a rate to be computed. For the binary case the answer is the exponent of [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]].

^rem-c2-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: The Gaussian is wrong for macroscopic deviations
> The Gaussian of [[§C2.2 Typicality in Large Systems#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]] has exponent $-2N\delta^2$; the exact exponent is $-N[\ln2 - h(\tfrac12 + \delta)]$. They agree only to order $\delta^2$. At $n = 1$, all spins up, the exact probability is $2^{-N} = e^{-0.693N}$, while the Gaussian gives $e^{-N/2}$, wrong by a factor $e^{0.19N}$. For deviations of order one in $n$, use the exponent $N[h(n) - \ln2]$, not the Gaussian.
