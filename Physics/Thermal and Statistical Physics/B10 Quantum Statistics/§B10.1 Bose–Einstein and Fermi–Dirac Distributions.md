How do many identical particles share the single-particle states of a box? Level A met the answer twice without deriving it: the Fermi factor of the electrons in a metal ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^def-a6-2-3|QM Def. §A6.2.3]], [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^rem-a6-2-2|QM Remark: Temperature above zero]]) and Planck's mean energy of an oscillator ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-3|QM Theorem §A1.1.3]]), while the classical Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^thm-a2-4-1|TH Theorem §A2.4.1]]) treated the molecules as independent. This section combines the exchange symmetry of identical particles ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^def-a5-3-1|QM Def. §A5.3.1]], [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|QM Principle §A5.3.2]]) with the grand canonical ensemble ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-1|Theorem §B7.3.1]]): each single-particle state is treated as a system of its own, and the Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac distributions follow in a few lines. It also derives the density of states of a box and shows how the chemical potential is fixed by the particle number. The gases themselves follow in [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases]] and Chapters B11–B13.

> [!remark] Remark: Notation
> $\varepsilon_i$ is the energy of the single-particle state $i$ (an "orbital": a spatial wave function together with a spin orientation), $n_i$ its occupation number, $\langle n_i\rangle$ the mean occupation, and $f(\varepsilon)$ the mean occupation as a function of the energy. $\beta = 1/k_BT$, and $k = k_B$ from here on. $\mathcal Z$ is the grand partition function, $z = e^{\beta\mu}$ the fugacity ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^def-b7-3-2|Def. §B7.3.2]]) and $\Phi = -kT\ln\mathcal Z$ the grand potential ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^cau-b7-3-1|Caution: Names and symbols]]); Schwartz calls $\Phi$ the grand free energy, Blundell writes $\Phi_G$. $g_s = 2S + 1$ counts the spin states of a particle of spin $S$ (Blundell's $2S + 1$).

## Bosons, fermions and occupation numbers

> [!definition] Definition §B10.1.1: Occupation Numbers; Bosons and Fermions
> Let $N$ identical, non-interacting particles move in a fixed external potential (for instance a box), with single-particle states $i = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ of energies $\varepsilon_0 \le \varepsilon_1 \le \cdots$. Because identical particles carry no labels, a state of the whole system is specified by the **occupation numbers** $\{n_i\}$, the number of particles in each single-particle state, with
>
> $$
> N = \sum_i n_i, \qquad E = \sum_i n_i\,\varepsilon_i .
> $$
>
> - For **bosons** (wave function symmetric under exchange) every set $\{n_i\}$ with $n_i \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ is one state of the system.
> - For **fermions** (wave function antisymmetric under exchange) $n_i \in \{0, 1\}$: the exclusion principle ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|QM Principle §A5.3.2]]).
>
> In both cases each allowed set $\{n_i\}$ is exactly one state: the symmetrised (or antisymmetrised) product of the occupied orbitals ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^thm-a5-3-1|QM Theorem §A5.3.1]]) is unique up to a phase.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 10, §2–3; Schroeder §7.2; Blundell §29.1–29.3*

^def-b10-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Which particles are which
> By the spin–statistics theorem, particles of integer spin are bosons and particles of half-integer spin are fermions. Schwartz sketches why: a rotation by $\theta$ multiplies a state of spin $s$ by $e^{is\theta}$, and a rotation by $\pi$ about the midpoint of two identical particles exchanges them while giving each a phase $e^{is\pi}$, in total $e^{2\pi is} = \pm1$. The full proof needs relativistic quantum field theory ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^rem-a5-3-1|QM Remark: Status of the principle]]). For a composite particle only the total count of fermions inside matters: an odd number makes a fermion, an even number a boson.
> - Bosons: photons, phonons, $^4$He atoms (2 protons, 2 neutrons, 2 electrons), $^{87}$Rb atoms (37 protons, 50 neutrons, 37 electrons).
> - Fermions: electrons, protons, neutrons, neutrinos, $^3$He atoms (2 protons, 1 neutron, 2 electrons).
>
> Blundell adds that in two dimensions the exchange argument allows any phase ("anyons"), which matters for the fractional quantum Hall effect; in three dimensions only $\pm1$ occurs.

^rem-b10-1-2

> [!example] Example §B10.1.1: Two Particles in m States
> Two particles share $m$ single-particle states, all of energy $\varepsilon$ (or with $kT$ much larger than all energy differences, so that every Boltzmann factor is about the same). Count the states of the pair.
> - *Distinguishable particles:* each has $m$ choices, $m^2$ states.
> - *Bosons:* the states with the particles in different orbitals, $\binom m2 = \tfrac12 m(m - 1)$, plus the $m$ doubly occupied ones: $\tfrac12 m(m + 1)$.
> - *Fermions:* only different orbitals, $\tfrac12 m(m - 1)$.
> - *Classical counting with the Gibbs factor* $1/N!$ ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox]]): $m^2/2!$.
>
> For $m = 3$ the counts are $9$, $6$, $3$ and $4.5$; for Schroeder's $m = 5$ they are $25$, $15$, $10$ and $12.5$ (not even an integer). The factor $1/N!$ is right for the configurations with the particles in different orbitals and wrong by a factor $\tfrac12$ for the doubly occupied ones; it interpolates between the boson and fermion counts. As $m$ grows, $\tfrac12 m(m \pm 1)/(\tfrac12 m^2) = 1 \pm 1/m \to 1$: when the accessible orbitals far outnumber the particles, multiple occupation is rare and all three countings agree. That is the regime of the classical ideal gas ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas]]), and the reason the Gibbs factor works there.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 10, §2.1, eqs. (6)–(11); Schroeder §7.2 (Figure 7.3); Blundell Example 29.2; Schroeder Problem 7.8*

## The grand canonical ensemble factorises

> [!remark] Remark: Why not the canonical ensemble
> At fixed $N$ the occupation numbers are coupled by $\sum_i n_i = N$, and the canonical partition function of $N$ bosons is a sum over ordered index sets $i_1 \le i_2 \le \cdots \le i_N$ (strictly ordered for fermions), which cannot be done in closed form even for simple spectra (Schwartz, Lecture 10, §3). The grand canonical ensemble removes the constraint: $N$ fluctuates, $\mu$ is fixed, and the states decouple. For a macroscopic system the two ensembles agree ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-2|Theorem §B7.3.2]]), and $\mu$ is fixed at the end by the mean particle number ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-5|Theorem §B10.1.5]]).

^rem-b10-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.1: The Grand Partition Function of an Ideal Quantum Gas
> For non-interacting identical bosons or fermions in equilibrium with a reservoir at $T$ and $\mu$, the grand partition function is a product over single-particle states,
>
> $$
> \mathcal Z = \prod_i \mathcal Z_i, \qquad \mathcal Z_i = \sum_{n\ \text{allowed}} e^{-\beta n(\varepsilon_i - \mu)}, \qquad \Phi = \sum_i \Phi_i, \quad \Phi_i = -kT\ln\mathcal Z_i ,
> $$
>
> the occupation numbers of different states are statistically independent, and the mean occupation of state $i$ is
>
> $$
> \langle n_i\rangle = kT\,\frac{\partial\ln\mathcal Z_i}{\partial\mu} = -\frac{\partial\Phi_i}{\partial\mu} = \frac{\partial\Phi_i}{\partial\varepsilon_i} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 10, eqs. (14)–(22); Blundell Example 29.3, eqs. (29.24)–(29.26); Schroeder §7.2*

^thm-b10-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^def-b7-3-2|Def. §B7.3.2]] and [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^def-b10-1-1|Def. §B10.1.1]], with $E - \mu N = \sum_i n_i(\varepsilon_i - \mu)$,
>
> $$
> \mathcal Z = \sum_{\{n_i\}} e^{-\beta\sum_i n_i(\varepsilon_i - \mu)} = \sum_{\{n_i\}}\prod_i e^{-\beta n_i(\varepsilon_i - \mu)} .
> $$
>
> The allowed sets $\{n_i\}$ are all combinations in which each $n_i$ runs independently over its allowed values ($\{0, 1\}$ or $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$), because no constraint on $\sum n_i$ remains. A sum over such a product set of a product of factors is the product of the single sums, which is the first formula. (Schwartz checks it by multiplying out $(1 + x_1 + x_1^2 + \cdots)(1 + x_2 + \cdots)(1 + x_3 + \cdots)$ and recovering the two-particle states of Example §B10.1.1.) The probability of a set $\{n_i\}$ is $\prod_i e^{-\beta n_i(\varepsilon_i - \mu)}/\mathcal Z_i$, a product of one factor per state: the occupations are independent. Taking logarithms turns the product into the sum for $\Phi$. Applying [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-2|Theorem §B7.3.2]] to the single state $i$ as the system gives $\langle n_i\rangle = kT\,\partial\ln\mathcal Z_i/\partial\mu$. Finally $\mathcal Z_i$ depends on $\varepsilon_i$ and $\mu$ only through $\varepsilon_i - \mu$, so $\partial\Phi_i/\partial\varepsilon_i = -\partial\Phi_i/\partial\mu$.

*Uses:* [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^def-b10-1-1|Def. §B10.1.1]], [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^def-b7-3-2|Def. §B7.3.2]], [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-1|Theorem §B7.3.1]], [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-2|Theorem §B7.3.2]], [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-3|Theorem §B7.3.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.2: Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions
> A single-particle state of energy $\varepsilon$ in equilibrium at $T$ and $\mu$ has
>
> $$
> \text{bosons:}\quad \mathcal Z_\varepsilon = \frac{1}{1 - e^{-\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)}}, \qquad \langle n\rangle = f_{\mathrm{BE}}(\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)} - 1}, \qquad \text{valid only for } \mu < \varepsilon ;
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{fermions:}\quad \mathcal Z_\varepsilon = 1 + e^{-\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)}, \qquad \langle n\rangle = f_{\mathrm{FD}}(\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)} + 1}, \qquad \text{any } \mu .
> $$
>
> Hence for bosons $\mu < \varepsilon_0$, the lowest single-particle energy. The occupation itself is distributed geometrically for bosons and as a 0–1 (Bernoulli) variable for fermions, with $q = e^{-\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)}$:
>
> $$
> P_{\mathrm{BE}}(n) = (1 - q)\,q^n \quad (n = 0, 1, 2, \dots), \qquad P_{\mathrm{FD}}(1) = f_{\mathrm{FD}}, \quad P_{\mathrm{FD}}(0) = 1 - f_{\mathrm{FD}},
> $$
>
> and its variance is
>
> $$
> \sigma_n^2 = kT\,\frac{\partial\langle n\rangle}{\partial\mu} = \langle n\rangle\bigl(1 \pm \langle n\rangle\bigr) \qquad (+ \text{ bosons}, \ - \text{ fermions}) .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 10, eqs. (23)–(31); Schroeder §7.2, eqs. (7.20)–(7.28); Blundell §29.3, eqs. (29.15)–(29.36)*

^thm-b10-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]] with $q = e^{-\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)}$.
>
> *Bosons.* $\mathcal Z_\varepsilon = \sum_{n\ge0}q^n$ is a geometric series ([[§14 Series#^ex-14-4|451 Ex. §14.4]]), convergent exactly when $q < 1$, i.e. $\mu < \varepsilon$; then $\mathcal Z_\varepsilon = 1/(1 - q)$. If $\mu \ge \varepsilon$ the Gibbs factors of $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ particles do not decrease and no equilibrium exists; since this must hold for every state, $\mu < \varepsilon_0$. Differentiating, with $\partial q/\partial\mu = \beta q$,
>
> $$
> \langle n\rangle = kT\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\bigl[-\ln(1 - q)\bigr] = kT\,\frac{\beta q}{1 - q} = \frac{q}{1 - q} = \frac{1}{q^{-1} - 1} .
> $$
>
> Equivalently, $\langle n\rangle = (1 - q)\sum_n nq^n = (1 - q)\,q/(1 - q)^2$ by term-by-term differentiation of the geometric series ([[§26 Differentiation and Integration of Power Series#^ex-26-1|451 Ex. §26.1]]), the sum that gave Planck's oscillator in [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^der-a1-1-3|QM Derivation of Theorem §A1.1.3]].
>
> *Fermions.* $\mathcal Z_\varepsilon = 1 + q$, and $\langle n\rangle = kT\,\beta q/(1 + q) = 1/(q^{-1} + 1)$. No condition on $\mu$ arises, since the sum is finite.
>
> *Distributions.* $P(n) = q^n/\mathcal Z_\varepsilon$ by [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-1|Theorem §B7.3.1]] applied to the one state: $(1 - q)q^n$ for bosons, and $1/(1 + q)$, $q/(1 + q)$ for $n = 0, 1$ for fermions.
>
> *Variance.* By [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-2|Theorem §B7.3.2]] applied to the one state, $\sigma_n^2 = kT\,\partial\langle n\rangle/\partial\mu$. With $x = \beta(\varepsilon - \mu)$, $\partial x/\partial\mu = -\beta$ and
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\frac{1}{e^x \mp 1} = \beta\,\frac{e^x}{(e^x \mp 1)^2} = \beta\,\langle n\rangle\,\frac{e^x}{e^x \mp 1} = \beta\,\langle n\rangle\bigl(1 \pm \langle n\rangle\bigr),
> $$
>
> since $e^x/(e^x \mp 1) = 1 \pm 1/(e^x \mp 1)$.

^der-b10-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]], [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-1|Theorem §B7.3.1]], [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-2|Theorem §B7.3.2]], [[§14 Series#^ex-14-4|451 Ex. §14.4]], [[§26 Differentiation and Integration of Power Series#^ex-26-1|451 Ex. §26.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the distributions
> All three distributions are written at once as $\langle n\rangle = 1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)} + c)$, with $c = -1$ (Bose–Einstein), $0$ (Maxwell–Boltzmann, [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]]) and $+1$ (Fermi–Dirac).
> - $f_{\mathrm{FD}}$ runs from $1$ for $\varepsilon \ll \mu$ to $0$ for $\varepsilon \gg \mu$, equals $\tfrac12$ at $\varepsilon = \mu$, and falls over a width of a few $kT$. It is symmetric about $\mu$: $1 - f_{\mathrm{FD}}(\mu - x) = f_{\mathrm{FD}}(\mu + x)$, the probability that a state $x$ below $\mu$ is empty equals the probability that a state $x$ above $\mu$ is full (Schroeder Problem 7.12; Blundell Exercise 29.3 writes $f_{\mathrm{FD}} = \tfrac12[1 - \tanh\tfrac12\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)]$). At $T = 0$ it is a step at $\mu$: this is the Fermi factor of [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^def-a6-2-3|QM Def. §A6.2.3]], with $\mu(0) = \varepsilon_F$ ([[§B13.1 The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals]]).
> - $f_{\mathrm{BE}}$ diverges as $\varepsilon \to \mu^+$: a boson state close to $\mu$ can hold any number of particles. This is the seed of Bose–Einstein condensation ([[§B12.1 Bose–Einstein Condensation]]).
> - When $\varepsilon - \mu \gg kT$ the $\pm1$ is negligible and both become $e^{-\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)}$: rarely occupied states do not care about exchange symmetry.
>
> The Langmuir adsorption site holds at most one molecule and therefore has the Fermi–Dirac occupancy ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^rem-b7-3-3|Remark: The Fermi–Dirac shape]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Fluctuations, bunching and the Planck oscillator
> - The variance $\langle n\rangle(1 + \langle n\rangle)$ of a boson mode exceeds the Poisson value $\langle n\rangle$ of independent classical particles ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^ex-b7-3-2|Example §B7.3.2]]); a fermion state has $\langle n\rangle(1 - \langle n\rangle)$, less than Poisson. A thermal light mode with $\langle n\rangle \gg 1$ fluctuates by about its own mean: thermal photons arrive "bunched". Laser light is not a thermal state ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^mod-a5-4-8|QM Model §A5.4.8]]); its statistics are beyond the sources.
> - With $\mu = 0$ and $\varepsilon = \hbar\omega$, $\hbar\omega f_{\mathrm{BE}}(\hbar\omega) = \hbar\omega/(e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1)$ is Planck's mean oscillator energy ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-3|QM Theorem §A1.1.3]]), and with the zero-point energy, $\hbar\omega(\tfrac12 + f_{\mathrm{BE}}) = \tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}\coth\tfrac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2}$ (PHY 306, Problem Set 9, Problem 2; [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^ex-b7-2-1|Example §B7.2.1]]). The geometric distribution $P(n) = (1 - q)q^n$ is the Boltzmann distribution over the oscillator's ladder. Counting the quanta of an oscillator mode *is* counting bosons in one state; why $\mu = 0$ for them is [[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics]].

## The classical limit

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.3: The Maxwell–Boltzmann Limit
> If $e^{\beta(\varepsilon_i - \mu)} \gg 1$ for every state, equivalently $\langle n_i\rangle \ll 1$ for every state, equivalently (with $\varepsilon_0 = 0$) $z = e^{\beta\mu} \ll 1$, then for bosons and fermions alike
>
> $$
> \langle n_i\rangle \approx f_{\mathrm{MB}}(\varepsilon_i) = e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_i - \mu)}, \qquad \ln\mathcal Z \approx z Z_1, \quad Z_1 = \sum_i e^{-\beta\varepsilon_i},
> $$
>
> with relative errors of order $z$. This $\mathcal Z = e^{zZ_1}$ is the grand partition function of $N$-particle states counted classically with the Gibbs factor, $Z_N = Z_1^N/N!$. At fixed mean particle number $N$, $z = N/Z_1$ and
>
> $$
> \langle n_i\rangle = \frac{N}{Z_1}\,e^{-\beta\varepsilon_i},
> $$
>
> $N$ times the Boltzmann probability of the state ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]]). For a gas this is the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution ([[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion]], [[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^thm-a2-4-1|TH Theorem §A2.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 10, §3.3–4, eqs. (32)–(46); Schroeder §7.2, eqs. (7.29)–(7.31); Blundell §29.3*

^thm-b10-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]], $\langle n_i\rangle = y_i/(1 \mp y_i)$ with $y_i = e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_i - \mu)} = z\,e^{-\beta\varepsilon_i} \le z$ (upper sign bosons). If $y_i \ll 1$, $\langle n_i\rangle = y_i(1 + O(y_i))$, and conversely $\langle n_i\rangle \ll 1$ forces $y_i \ll 1$. For the partition function, $\ln\mathcal Z = \mp\sum_i\ln(1 \mp y_i) = \sum_i y_i\,(1 + O(y_i))$ by Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), so $\ln\mathcal Z = zZ_1(1 + O(z))$.
>
> On the classical side, $\mathcal Z = \sum_N z^NZ_N$ with $Z_N = Z_1^N/N!$ is $e^{zZ_1}$ ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^ex-b7-3-2|Example §B7.3.2]]). Schwartz writes it as $\prod_i\exp(ze^{-\beta\varepsilon_i}) = \prod_i\sum_n \tfrac{1}{n!}(ze^{-\beta\varepsilon_i})^n$: Maxwell–Boltzmann counting gives an $n$-fold occupied state the weight $1/n!$ instead of $1$, which is harmless when multiple occupation is negligible. Finally $\langle N\rangle = z\,\partial\ln\mathcal Z/\partial z = zZ_1$ ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-2|Theorem §B7.3.2]]), so $z = N/Z_1$ and $\langle n_i\rangle = (N/Z_1)e^{-\beta\varepsilon_i}$; equivalently $\mu = -kT\ln(Z_1/N)$ ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas]]).

^der-b10-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]], [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^ex-b7-3-2|Example §B7.3.2]], [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-2|Theorem §B7.3.2]], [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics is not a third kind of particle
> No particles obey Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics exactly (Schwartz). It is the counting "distinguishable particles, then divide by $N!$", which over-corrects every configuration with two particles in one state ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^ex-b10-1-1|Example §B10.1.1]]); it is right only when such configurations are negligible. The condition $z \ll 1$ looks like a low-temperature condition at fixed $\mu$, but $\mu$ is not fixed in practice: at fixed density $\mu = kT\ln(n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3/g_s)$ becomes large and negative as $T$ rises, and high temperature is the classical regime. The quantitative criterion, $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3 \ll g_s$, is [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-1|Def. §B10.2.1]].

^cau-b10-1-1

> [!example] Example §B10.1.2: Occupancies at Room Temperature
> At $T = 300\ \mathrm K$, $kT = 0.02585\ \mathrm{eV}$. For a state at $\varepsilon - \mu = 0.001$, $0.01$, $0.1$ and $1\ \mathrm{eV}$, $x = \beta(\varepsilon - \mu) = 0.0387$, $0.387$, $3.87$, $38.7$, and
>
> | $\varepsilon - \mu$ | $f_{\mathrm{BE}}$ | $f_{\mathrm{MB}}$ | $f_{\mathrm{FD}}$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $0.001\ \mathrm{eV}$ | $25.4$ | $0.962$ | $0.490$ |
> | $0.01\ \mathrm{eV}$ | $2.12$ | $0.679$ | $0.404$ |
> | $0.1\ \mathrm{eV}$ | $0.0213$ | $0.0209$ | $0.0205$ |
> | $1\ \mathrm{eV}$ | $1.6\times10^{-17}$ | $1.6\times10^{-17}$ | $1.6\times10^{-17}$ |
>
> Within a few $kT$ of $\mu$ the three differ completely; four $kT$ away they agree to 2%. For the boson state at $0.01\ \mathrm{eV}$, $q = e^{-0.387} = 0.679$ and $P(0), P(1), P(2), P(3) = 0.321, 0.218, 0.148, 0.101$: although the mean is $2.1$, the most probable occupation is zero, and the spread $\sigma_n = \sqrt{2.12 \times 3.12} = 2.6$ exceeds the mean. A fermion state $0.01\ \mathrm{eV}$ *below* $\mu$ is occupied with probability $1 - 0.404 = 0.596$, by the symmetry of $f_{\mathrm{FD}}$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder Problems 7.11 and 7.13 (solved here)*

## The density of states

> [!definition] Definition §B10.1.2: Density of States
> For a system with very many, closely spaced single-particle states, the **density of states** $g(\varepsilon)$ is defined by: $g(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon$ is the number of single-particle states with energy between $\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon + d\varepsilon$, spin states included. Then sums over states of slowly varying functions become integrals:
>
> $$
> \sum_i F(\varepsilon_i) \approx \int_0^\infty g(\varepsilon)\,F(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon .
> $$
>
> In particular $N = \int g\,f\,d\varepsilon$, $U = \int\varepsilon\,g\,f\,d\varepsilon$ and $\Phi = \int g(\varepsilon)\,\Phi_\varepsilon\,d\varepsilon$. The same definition with $\omega$ in place of $\varepsilon$ gives the density of modes $g(\omega)$. Level A met the density of states of the free-electron gas ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^def-a6-2-3|QM Def. §A6.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder §7.3, eqs. (7.51)–(7.54); Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, eq. (4); Blundell §30.1, eqs. (30.3)–(30.5)*

^def-b10-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.4: Density of States in a Three-Dimensional Box
> For a particle with $g_s$ internal (spin or polarization) states in a box of volume $V$, to leading order for large $V$ the number of states with wave number below $k$ and the density of states in $k$ are
>
> $$
> \mathcal N(k) = g_s\,\frac{Vk^3}{6\pi^2}, \qquad g(k) = g_s\,\frac{Vk^2}{2\pi^2},
> $$
>
> independently of the shape of the box and of the boundary conditions. Hence:
> - nonrelativistic particles, $\varepsilon = \hbar^2k^2/2m$:
>
>   $$
>   g(\varepsilon) = g_s\,\frac{V}{4\pi^2}\Bigl(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}\varepsilon^{1/2} = g_s\,2\pi V\Bigl(\frac{2m}{h^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}\varepsilon^{1/2} ;
>   $$
> - massless particles or long-wavelength waves, $\varepsilon = \hbar ck$ ($\omega = ck$):
>
>   $$
>   g(\varepsilon) = g_s\,\frac{V\varepsilon^2}{2\pi^2\hbar^3c^3}, \qquad g(\omega) = g_s\,\frac{V\omega^2}{2\pi^2c^3} .
>   $$
>
> Equivalently, a sum over states becomes a phase-space integral with one state per cell of volume $h^3$: $\sum_i \to g_s\int d^3q\,d^3p/h^3$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §30.1, eqs. (30.4)–(30.5), Example 30.7; Schwartz 181, Lecture 10, eq. (1), and Lecture 11, eqs. (13)–(15), (52)–(53); Schroeder §7.3, eq. (7.51), §7.4, §7.6, eq. (7.123)*

^thm-b10-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Periodic boundary conditions* (Blundell), in a cube of side $L$: $\psi \propto e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}$ with $\vec k = (2\pi/L)(n_x, n_y, n_z)$, $n_j \in \mathbb Z$. The allowed $\vec k$ form a lattice with one point per volume $(2\pi)^3/V$ of $k$-space, so a sphere of radius $k$, of volume $\tfrac43\pi k^3$ (spherical coordinates, [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-6|452 Ex. §15.6]]), holds $\tfrac43\pi k^3\,V/(2\pi)^3 = Vk^3/6\pi^2$ points, each carrying $g_s$ states.
>
> *Fixed walls* (Schroeder, and [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^der-a6-2-3|QM Derivation of Theorem §A6.2.3]]): standing waves $\sin(k_xx)\sin(k_yy)\sin(k_zz)$ with $\vec k = (\pi/L)(n_x, n_y, n_z)$, $n_j \ge 1$. One point per volume $\pi^3/V$, but only in one octant: $\tfrac18\cdot\tfrac43\pi k^3\,V/\pi^3 = Vk^3/6\pi^2$ again. Schwartz's walls with $\partial\psi/\partial n = 0$ give $n_j \ge 0$ and the same leading term. The boundary conditions change only the points on the coordinate planes, a correction of relative order $1/kL$ ([[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^rem-b7-2-4|WO Remark: Counting modes]]): for macroscopic $V$ and thermal wavelengths it is negligible, except for the very lowest states (see [[§B12.1 Bose–Einstein Condensation]]).
>
> *Changing variables.* $g(k) = d\mathcal N/dk = g_sVk^2/2\pi^2$, and for a monotonic dispersion $g(\varepsilon) = g(k)\,dk/d\varepsilon$. For $\varepsilon = \hbar^2k^2/2m$: $k = \sqrt{2m\varepsilon}/\hbar$, $dk/d\varepsilon = \sqrt{2m}/(2\hbar\sqrt\varepsilon)$, so $g(\varepsilon) = g_s(V/2\pi^2)(2m\varepsilon/\hbar^2)\sqrt{2m}/(2\hbar\sqrt\varepsilon) = g_s(V/4\pi^2)(2m/\hbar^2)^{3/2}\varepsilon^{1/2}$; with $\hbar = h/2\pi$ this is $g_s\,2\pi V(2m/h^2)^{3/2}\varepsilon^{1/2}$. For $\varepsilon = \hbar ck$: $g(\varepsilon) = g_s(V/2\pi^2)(\varepsilon/\hbar c)^2/(\hbar c)$.
>
> *Phase space.* With $\vec p = \hbar\vec k$, $V\,d^3k/(2\pi)^3 = V\,d^3p/h^3 = \int_V d^3q\,d^3p/h^3$.

*Uses:* [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^def-b10-1-2|Def. §B10.1.2]], [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^thm-a6-2-3|QM Theorem §A6.2.3]], [[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^rem-b7-2-4|WO Remark: Counting modes]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-6|452 Ex. §15.6]]

> [!remark] Remark: Checks and uses
> - For electrons, $g_s = 2$ reproduces $g(E) = \tfrac{\pi V}{2}(8m_e/h^2)^{3/2}E^{1/2}$ of [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^thm-a6-2-3|QM Theorem §A6.2.3]]. For photons $g_s = 2$ (two transverse polarizations; spin 1 without the longitudinal state, Blundell) gives $g(\omega) = V\omega^2/\pi^2c^3$, twice the mode count of [[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^rem-b7-2-4|WO Remark: Counting modes]]; for sound waves in a solid $g_s = 3$ ([[§B11.2 Phonons and the Debye Model]]).
> - The rule "one state per $h^3$ of phase space" is what justifies the cell size $\Delta q\,\Delta p = h$ that classical statistical mechanics inserts by hand into $Z_1$ and the Sackur–Tetrode entropy ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas]]); quantum mechanics removes the arbitrary cutoff (Schwartz, Lecture 10, §1).
> - The density of states is pure quantum mechanics of one particle; once it is known, the thermal physics is the integral $\int g\,f\,d\varepsilon$ (Schroeder).

## How the chemical potential is fixed

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.5: The Chemical Potential Fixed by the Particle Number
> For an ideal gas of bosons or fermions at given $T$ and $V$, the mean particle number
>
> $$
> N(\mu) = \sum_i \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon_i - \mu)} \mp 1}
> $$
>
> is a strictly increasing function of $\mu$, so each $N$ determines at most one $\mu(T, V, N)$. Moreover:
> 1. **Bosons:** $\mu < \varepsilon_0$, and as $T \to 0$ at fixed $N$, $\varepsilon_0 - \mu \approx kT/N \to 0$: all particles go to the ground state and $\mu$ approaches $\varepsilon_0$ from below.
> 2. **Fermions:** $\mu$ may have either sign; as $T \to 0$, $f_{\mathrm{FD}}$ becomes a step, the $N$ lowest states are filled, and $\mu(0) = \varepsilon_F$ lies between the highest filled and the lowest empty level (the Fermi energy, [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^def-a6-2-3|QM Def. §A6.2.3]]; [[§B13.1 The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals]]).
> 3. **Classical regime:** when $N$ is small compared with the number of thermally accessible states, $\mu = -kT\ln(Z_1/N)$ is large and negative ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 10, §3.1–3.2, §4; Schroeder §7.2–7.3, §7.6, eqs. (7.119)–(7.121); Blundell §29.3, §30.2, §30.4, footnote 11*

^thm-b10-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each term increases strictly with $\mu$: $\partial\langle n_i\rangle/\partial\mu = \beta\langle n_i\rangle(1 \pm \langle n_i\rangle) > 0$ ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]; for fermions $0 < \langle n_i\rangle < 1$), so the sum does too. This is also $\sigma_N^2/kT > 0$ ([[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-2|Theorem §B7.3.2]]).
>
> 1. For bosons $\mu < \varepsilon_0$ by [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]. As $\mu \to \varepsilon_0^-$ the ground-state term $1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon_0 - \mu)} - 1)$ grows without bound, so in a finite box every $N$ is reached. As $T \to 0$ the excited terms are at most $1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_0)} - 1) \to 0$, so the ground state holds $N_0 \to N$; inverting $N_0 = 1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon_0 - \mu)} - 1)$ gives $\varepsilon_0 - \mu = kT\ln(1 + 1/N_0) \approx kT/N_0$.
> 2. For fermions, as $T \to 0$, $f_{\mathrm{FD}} \to 1$ for $\varepsilon_i < \mu$ and $\to 0$ for $\varepsilon_i > \mu$; $N$ is the number of states below $\mu$, which forces $\mu(0)$ between the $N$th and the $(N + 1)$st level. Since $\varepsilon_F$ exceeds $\varepsilon_0$ by a macroscopic amount, $\mu > 0$ at low $T$ (energies measured from $\varepsilon_0 = 0$).
> 3. This is [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]] solved for $\mu$.

> [!remark] Remark: The path of μ as a gas is cooled
> At fixed density, cooling raises $\mu$ from large negative values. When it comes within a few $kT$ of the ground-state energy, the statistics start to matter: a Bose gas's $\mu$ creeps up to $\varepsilon_0$ but never reaches it, and the ground state fills (Bose–Einstein condensation, [[§B12.1 Bose–Einstein Condensation]]); a Fermi gas's $\mu$ passes through $\varepsilon_0$ and ends at $\varepsilon_F > \varepsilon_0$ (Schwartz, Lecture 10, §4). The computed curves for gases in a box are in [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^rem-b10-2-3|Remark: Reading the chemical potential]]. For photons and phonons, whose number is not conserved, $\mu = 0$ ([[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics]]).

^rem-b10-1-7

> [!remark] Remark: The same distributions from maximal entropy
> Blundell (Exercise 29.6) derives both distributions in the microcanonical ensemble. Group the states into bunches $j$ of $g_j$ states at nearly equal energy $E_j$, holding $n_j$ particles. The number of arrangements is $g_j!/[n_j!(g_j - n_j)!]$ for fermions and $(g_j + n_j - 1)!/[n_j!(g_j - 1)!]$ for bosons, and with Stirling's formula ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian]]) the entropy, in terms of the mean occupations $\bar n_j = n_j/g_j$, is
>
> $$
> S = -k\sum_j g_j\bigl[\bar n_j\ln\bar n_j \mp (1 \pm \bar n_j)\ln(1 \pm \bar n_j)\bigr] \qquad (\text{upper sign bosons}) .
> $$
>
> Maximising $S$ at fixed $\sum n_j = N$ and $\sum n_jE_j = U$ with two Lagrange multipliers ([[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-3|452 Thm. §14.3]]) gives $\bar n_j = 1/(e^{\alpha + \beta E_j} \mp 1)$, and comparing with $dS = (dU - \mu\,dN)/T$ identifies $\beta = 1/kT$, $\alpha = -\beta\mu$. The entropy formula is useful in its own right: it is a sum over states of a function of the occupation alone, so a process that leaves every occupation unchanged is isentropic ([[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-7|Theorem §B11.1.7]]).
