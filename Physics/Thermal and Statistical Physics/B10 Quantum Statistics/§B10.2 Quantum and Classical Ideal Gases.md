The ideal gas of level A ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]) and its partition function $Z_N = Z_1^N/N!$ ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]) are, by [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]], the dilute limit of a gas of bosons or fermions. This section makes "dilute" quantitative: it introduces the quantum concentration and the degeneracy parameter $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3$, writes the number, energy and pressure of an ideal quantum gas through the Bose and Fermi integrals (polylogarithms), proves $pV = \tfrac23U$ for every ideal nonrelativistic gas, and computes the first quantum correction to the ideal gas law. It ends with the systems in which quantum statistics cannot be ignored. The internal energy is written $U$ (level A's $E_{\text{int}}$).

## When is a gas quantum?

> [!definition] Definition §B10.2.1: Degeneracy Parameter; Degenerate and Non-Degenerate Gases
> For a gas of number density $n = N/V$ of particles with $g_s$ spin states, with thermal wavelength $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}} = h/\sqrt{2\pi mkT}$ and quantum concentration $n_Q = \lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^{-3}$ ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]]), the **degeneracy parameter** is
>
> $$
> \frac{n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3}{g_s} = \frac{n}{g_sn_Q} .
> $$
>
> The gas is **non-degenerate** (classical) when $n/g_sn_Q \ll 1$, i.e. when the mean spacing $n^{-1/3}$ is much larger than $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}$, and **degenerate** (quantum) when $n/g_sn_Q \gtrsim 1$. Schroeder states the criterion as $V/N \gg v_Q$ with the quantum volume $v_Q = \lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3$; Schwartz as $\lambda \ll (V/N)^{1/3}$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 10, §4, eqs. (46)–(47), (55); Schroeder §7.2, eqs. (7.17)–(7.19); Blundell §30.1, Example 30.2*

^def-b10-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: What the criterion means
> $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}$ is roughly the de Broglie wavelength $h/p$ at the thermal momentum. Picture each particle as a wave packet of size $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}$: in a normal gas the packets are far apart, and whether the particles are bosons or fermions never matters; when the packets begin to overlap, multiple occupation of the same orbital becomes likely and the statistics take over (Schroeder, Figure 7.4). Three things push a gas into the quantum regime: high density (a neutron star), low temperature (liquid helium, ultracold atoms) and small mass (electrons in a metal; photons, which are massless). Equivalently (Schwartz), the classical counting fails when the ground state would hold one particle or more: with $f_{\mathrm{MB}}(0) = z$ and $z = n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3/g_s$ in the classical limit, this is $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3/g_s \approx 1$. The user's notes put it the same way: "when $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}} \ll n^{-1/3}$ … quantum statistics reduce to Maxwell–Boltzmann" (Stat Mech concepts notes, §32.6).

^rem-b10-2-1

## The Bose and Fermi integrals

> [!definition] Definition §B10.2.2: Zeta and Polylogarithm Functions
> - **Gamma function** ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-5|Def. §B4.1.5]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]), recalled: $\Gamma(s) = \int_0^\infty x^{s-1}e^{-x}\,dx$ for $s > 0$, with $\Gamma(s + 1) = s\Gamma(s)$, $\Gamma(n) = (n - 1)!$, $\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \sqrt\pi$, $\Gamma(\tfrac32) = \tfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2}$, $\Gamma(\tfrac52) = \tfrac{3\sqrt\pi}{4}$.
> - **Riemann zeta function:** $\zeta(s) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty k^{-s}$ for $s > 1$.
> - **Polylogarithm:** $\mathrm{Li}_s(z) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty z^k/k^s$ for $|z| < 1$ (and $|z| = 1$ when $s > 1$); $\mathrm{Li}_s(1) = \zeta(s)$, $\mathrm{Li}_s(z) \approx z$ for small $z$, and $z\,\frac{d}{dz}\mathrm{Li}_s(z) = \mathrm{Li}_{s-1}(z)$.
>
> | $s$ | $\tfrac32$ | $2$ | $\tfrac52$ | $3$ | $4$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $\zeta(s)$ | $2.612$ | $\pi^2/6 = 1.645$ | $1.341$ | $1.202$ | $\pi^4/90 = 1.0823$ |
>
> *Source: Blundell App. C.1, C.4–C.5, Table C.1–C.2*

^def-b10-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.1: Bose and Fermi Integrals
> For $s > 0$ and $0 < z < 1$ (and also $z = 1$: for the Bose integral if $s > 1$, for the Fermi integral for every $s > 0$),
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty \frac{x^{s-1}\,dx}{z^{-1}e^x - 1} = \Gamma(s)\,\mathrm{Li}_s(z), \qquad \int_0^\infty \frac{x^{s-1}\,dx}{z^{-1}e^x + 1} = -\Gamma(s)\,\mathrm{Li}_s(-z) .
> $$
>
> In particular
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty \frac{x^{s-1}\,dx}{e^x - 1} = \Gamma(s)\,\zeta(s), \qquad \int_0^\infty \frac{x^{s-1}\,dx}{e^x + 1} = \bigl(1 - 2^{1-s}\bigr)\Gamma(s)\,\zeta(s),
> $$
>
> e.g. $\int_0^\infty x^3\,dx/(e^x - 1) = 6\zeta(4) = \pi^4/15$ and $\int_0^\infty x^2\,dx/(e^x - 1) = 2\zeta(3)$. For $z > 1$ the Fermi integral defines (the analytic continuation of) $-\mathrm{Li}_s(-z)$, which Blundell uses for degenerate Fermi gases.
>
> *Source: Blundell App. C.4–C.5, eqs. (C.22)–(C.25), (C.33)–(C.38), and §30.2, eq. (30.37); Schroeder App. B*

^thm-b10-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $x > 0$, $ze^{-x} < 1$, and the geometric series ([[§14 Series#^ex-14-4|451 Ex. §14.4]]) gives
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{z^{-1}e^x \mp 1} = \frac{ze^{-x}}{1 \mp ze^{-x}} = \sum_{k=1}^\infty (\pm1)^{k-1}z^ke^{-kx} .
> $$
>
> Each term integrates to $\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}e^{-kx}\,dx = \Gamma(s)/k^s$ (substitute $y = kx$). *Bose case:* all terms are non-negative, so the partial sums increase to the integrand and the monotone convergence theorem ([[§14 The Lebesgue Integral for Simple Functions#^thm-14-4|551 Thm. §14.4]]) allows integrating term by term: the integral is $\Gamma(s)\sum_k z^k/k^s = \Gamma(s)\mathrm{Li}_s(z)$, finite for $z < 1$, and for $z = 1$ when $s > 1$ (near $x = 0$ the integrand behaves as $x^{s-2}$; [[§36 Improper Integrals#^thm-36-1|451 Thm. §36.1]]). *Fermi case:* the series alternates, but the partial sums are dominated in absolute value by the Bose integrand $x^{s-1}ze^{-x}/(1 - ze^{-x})$, integrable for $z < 1$, so the dominated convergence theorem ([[§15 The General Lebesgue Integral#^thm-15-8|551 Thm. §15.8]]) again allows term-by-term integration, giving $\Gamma(s)\sum_k(-1)^{k-1}z^k/k^s = -\Gamma(s)\mathrm{Li}_s(-z)$; at $z = 1$ the integral is continuous in $z$ and the alternating series converges. *Special values:* $\sum_k(-1)^{k-1}k^{-s} = \zeta(s) - 2\sum_k(2k)^{-s} = (1 - 2^{1-s})\zeta(s)$; $\Gamma(4) = 6$ and $\zeta(4) = \pi^4/90$ (Blundell, Table C.2) give $\pi^4/15$.

^der-b10-2-1

## The ideal quantum gas in a box

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.2: Number and Energy of an Ideal Quantum Gas
> For $N$ non-interacting nonrelativistic particles with $g_s$ spin states in a box of volume $V$, with fugacity $z = e^{\beta\mu}$ and energies measured from the ground state, write $F_s(z) = \mathrm{Li}_s(z)$ for bosons and $F_s(z) = -\mathrm{Li}_s(-z)$ for fermions. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3}{g_s} = F_{3/2}(z), \qquad U = \frac32\,kT\,\frac{g_sV}{\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3}\,F_{5/2}(z) = \frac32\,NkT\,\frac{F_{5/2}(z)}{F_{3/2}(z)} .
> $$
>
> The first equation fixes $z$, hence $\mu$, for given $n$ and $T$ ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-5|Theorem §B10.1.5]]). For bosons it applies only while $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3/g_s < \zeta(\tfrac32) = 2.612$ ([[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^cau-b10-2-1|Caution: The Bose formulas miss the ground state]]).
>
> *Source: Blundell §30.1, eqs. (30.12)–(30.16), §30.3, eqs. (30.43)–(30.44); Schroeder §7.3, eqs. (7.53)–(7.54), §7.6, eq. (7.122)*

^thm-b10-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^def-b10-1-2|Def. §B10.1.2]] and [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-4|Theorem §B10.1.4]], with $g(\varepsilon) = g_s\,2\pi V(2m/h^2)^{3/2}\varepsilon^{1/2}$ and $x = \beta\varepsilon$,
>
> $$
> N = \int_0^\infty \frac{g(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon}{z^{-1}e^{\beta\varepsilon} \mp 1} = g_s\,2\pi V\Bigl(\frac{2mkT}{h^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{1/2}\,dx}{z^{-1}e^x \mp 1} = g_s\,2\pi V\Bigl(\frac{2mkT}{h^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}\,\Gamma(\tfrac32)\,F_{3/2}(z)
> $$
>
> by [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]]. Since $2\pi\,\Gamma(\tfrac32) = \pi^{3/2}$, the prefactor is $g_sV(2\pi mkT/h^2)^{3/2} = g_sV/\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3$. ($\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \sqrt\pi$ is the Gaussian integral, [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-7|452 Ex. §15.7]].) For $U$ the extra factor $\varepsilon = kTx$ turns $x^{1/2}$ into $x^{3/2}$ and $\Gamma(\tfrac32)$ into $\Gamma(\tfrac52) = \tfrac32\Gamma(\tfrac32)$. Dividing the two gives the last form.

^der-b10-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-4|Theorem §B10.1.4]], [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]], [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-7|452 Ex. §15.7]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.3: Pressure and Energy of an Ideal Gas
> In a box of volume $V$, let every single-particle energy scale with the volume as $\varepsilon_i \propto V^{-\gamma}$. Then an ideal gas of bosons, fermions or classical particles has, at any temperature,
>
> $$
> pV = \gamma\,U .
> $$
>
> - Nonrelativistic particles, $\varepsilon \propto k^2 \propto V^{-2/3}$: $pV = \tfrac23U$.
> - Massless particles (photons, and ultrarelativistic particles), $\varepsilon \propto k \propto V^{-1/3}$: $pV = \tfrac13U$.
>
> In terms of the grand potential, $\Phi = -pV = -\tfrac23U$ for the nonrelativistic gas, so with [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]], $p = kT\,g_s F_{5/2}(z)/\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §30.1, eq. (30.17), §30.2, eq. (30.30); Schwartz 181, Lecture 11, eq. (50) (photons); Schroeder Problem 7.45*

^thm-b10-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *From the scaling of the levels.* By [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-3|Theorem §B7.3.3]], $p = -(\partial\Phi/\partial V)_{T,\mu}$, and $\Phi = \sum_i\Phi_i$ depends on $V$ only through the levels $\varepsilon_i$. With $\partial\Phi_i/\partial\varepsilon_i = \langle n_i\rangle$ ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]]; the same holds for $\Phi_i = -kTe^{-\beta(\varepsilon_i - \mu)}$ in the Maxwell–Boltzmann case) and $d\varepsilon_i/dV = -\gamma\varepsilon_i/V$,
>
> $$
> p = -\sum_i\frac{\partial\Phi_i}{\partial\varepsilon_i}\frac{d\varepsilon_i}{dV} = \frac{\gamma}{V}\sum_i\langle n_i\rangle\varepsilon_i = \frac{\gamma U}{V} .
> $$
>
> In a box of side $L = V^{1/3}$ the wave numbers are multiples of $1/L$, so $\varepsilon = \hbar^2k^2/2m \propto V^{-2/3}$ and $\varepsilon = \hbar ck \propto V^{-1/3}$. This is Schwartz's computation for the photon gas. (If energies are measured from the ground state, the ground-state energy itself also scales as $V^{-2/3}$; it is of order $\hbar^2/mL^2$ per particle, negligible except in a condensate, where it contributes nothing to $U$ anyway.)
>
> *By parts* (Blundell). For the nonrelativistic gas $\Phi = \mp kT\int_0^\infty g(\varepsilon)\ln(1 \mp ze^{-\beta\varepsilon})\,d\varepsilon$ ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]). With $G(\varepsilon) = \int_0^\varepsilon g = \tfrac23\varepsilon g(\varepsilon)$, integrate by parts; the boundary terms vanish ($G(0) = 0$, the logarithm decays exponentially), and $\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}[\mp kT\ln(1 \mp ze^{-\beta\varepsilon})] = 1/(z^{-1}e^{\beta\varepsilon} \mp 1)$, so
>
> $$
> \Phi = -\int_0^\infty G(\varepsilon)\,\frac{d\varepsilon}{z^{-1}e^{\beta\varepsilon} \mp 1} = -\frac23\int_0^\infty\frac{\varepsilon\,g(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon}{z^{-1}e^{\beta\varepsilon} \mp 1} = -\frac23\,U .
> $$
>
> With $\Phi = -pV$ this is $pV = \tfrac23U$, and inserting $U$ from Theorem §B10.2.2 gives $p$.

^der-b10-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B7.3 The Grand Canonical Ensemble#^thm-b7-3-3|Theorem §B7.3.3]], [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]], [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]], [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-4|Theorem §B10.1.4]], [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: One relation, three regimes
> $pV = \tfrac23U$ is the kinetic-theory result $pV = \tfrac13Nm\overline{v^2} = \tfrac23K_{\text{tr}}$ ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]]), now exact for quantum gases at any degeneracy: for a classical gas it gives $pV = NkT$, for a degenerate Fermi gas at $T = 0$ the degeneracy pressure $p = \tfrac23(\tfrac35N\varepsilon_F)/V = \tfrac25n\varepsilon_F$ ([[§B13.1 The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-2|Theorem §B13.1.2]]), and for a Bose gas below its condensation temperature a pressure that does not depend on $V$ ([[§B12.1 Bose–Einstein Condensation]]). For radiation $p = u/3$ ([[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics]]); the factor $\tfrac13$ instead of $\tfrac23$ reflects $\varepsilon = pc$ in place of $p^2/2m$ (Blundell §23.1).

^rem-b10-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: The Bose formulas miss the ground state
> Replacing the sum over states by $\int g\,f\,d\varepsilon$ gives the ground state, where $g(\varepsilon) \propto \sqrt\varepsilon$ vanishes, zero weight. For fermions and for non-degenerate bosons this costs nothing. But $\mathrm{Li}_{3/2}(z) \le \zeta(\tfrac32)$ for $z \le 1$, so the first equation of Theorem §B10.2.2 has no solution for bosons once $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3/g_s > 2.612$: the missing particles are in the ground state, which the integral cannot see. This is Bose–Einstein condensation, [[§B12.1 Bose–Einstein Condensation]].

^cau-b10-2-1

## The classical limit and the first quantum correction

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.4: Classical Limit and First Quantum Correction
> Let $y = n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3/g_s \ll 1$. Then, with the upper sign for bosons and the lower for fermions,
>
> $$
> \frac{\mu}{kT} = \ln y \mp \frac{y}{2^{3/2}} + O(y^2), \qquad p = nkT\Bigl(1 \mp \frac{y}{2^{5/2}} + O(y^2)\Bigr), \qquad U = \frac32\,pV .
> $$
>
> To leading order, $\mu = kT\ln(n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3/g_s)$ ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]]), $pV = NkT$ and $U = \tfrac32NkT$: the ideal gas law ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]) and the internal energy of [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]] are the dilute limit of quantum statistics, as [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-4|Theorem §B8.1.4]] found from the classical partition function. The first correction has the form of a second virial coefficient ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^def-b9-1-2|Def. §B9.1.2]]),
>
> $$
> p = nkT\bigl(1 + B_2n + \cdots\bigr), \qquad B_2 = \mp\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3}{2^{5/2}g_s} ,
> $$
>
> negative for bosons and positive for fermions, although the particles do not interact.
>
> *Source: Schroeder Problem 7.75 (result stated there for spin-0 bosons and spin-½ fermions; derived here); Blundell Example 30.2; Schwartz 181, Lecture 10, §4*

^thm-b10-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-2|Def. §B10.2.2]], $F_s(z) = z \pm z^2/2^s + O(z^3)$. [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]] gives $y = z \pm z^2/2^{3/2} + O(z^3)$, so $z = O(y)$, and inverting order by order, $z = y \mp y^2/2^{3/2} + O(y^3)$. Then $\ln z = \ln y + \ln(1 \mp y/2^{3/2} + \cdots) = \ln y \mp y/2^{3/2} + O(y^2)$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]). By [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]],
>
> $$
> \frac{p}{kT}\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3}{g_s} = F_{5/2}(z) = z \pm \frac{z^2}{2^{5/2}} + O(z^3) = y \mp \frac{y^2}{2^{3/2}} \pm \frac{y^2}{2^{5/2}} + O(y^3) = y \mp \frac{y^2}{2^{5/2}} + O(y^3),
> $$
>
> since $2^{-3/2} - 2^{-5/2} = 2^{-5/2}$. Multiplying by $g_s/\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3$ and using $g_sy/\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3 = n$ gives $p$; $U = \tfrac32pV$ is Theorem §B10.2.3. Reading off the coefficient of $n^2kT$ gives $B_2$.

^der-b10-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-2|Def. §B10.2.2]], [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]], [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the chemical potential
> The figure solves $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3 = F_{3/2}(z)$ at fixed density for $g_s = 1$, with temperatures measured in units of $T_d$, defined by $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}(T_d)^3 = 1$, and compares with the classical $\mu = kT\ln(n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3)$.
> - At high $T$ all three coincide, as Theorem §B10.2.4 says; already at $T = 2T_d$ each quantum $\mu$ differs from the classical one by only about $0.25\,kT_d$.
> - The boson $\mu$ lies below the classical one and the fermion $\mu$ above it, the same ordering as the $\mp$ in $B_2$: exchange symmetry acts like an attraction for bosons and like a repulsion for fermions. Schroeder: "quantum statistics results in a lowering of the pressure of a boson gas, as one might expect".
> - The boson $\mu$ reaches $0$ at $T_c = \zeta(\tfrac32)^{-2/3}T_d = 0.527\,T_d$ and stays (just below) $0$ for all lower $T$: the condensate ([[§B12.1 Bose–Einstein Condensation]]).
> - The fermion $\mu$ rises to $\varepsilon_F = (6\pi^2)^{2/3}kT_d/4\pi = 1.209\,kT_d$ at $T = 0$, and changes sign near $T \approx T_F = \varepsilon_F/k$ (computed: $0.99\,T_F$), as in Schroeder's Figure 7.16 ([[§B13.1 The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals]]).
> - The classical curve turns positive below $T_d$, where it is no longer valid.

## Which systems are degenerate?

> [!example] Example §B10.2.1: Classical or Quantum?
> Compute $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3/g_s$ (Definition §B10.2.1) for five systems.
>
> | system | $T$ | $n\ (\mathrm m^{-3})$ | $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}$ | $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3/g_s$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | N$_2$ in air, 1 atm | $300\ \mathrm K$ | $2.45\times10^{25}$ | $0.019\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $1.7\times10^{-7}$ |
> | $^4$He gas, 1 atm | $4.2\ \mathrm K$ | $1.75\times10^{27}$ | $0.43\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $0.13$ |
> | liquid $^4$He | $2.17\ \mathrm K$ | $2.18\times10^{28}$ | $0.59\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $4.5$ |
> | $^{87}$Rb, trapped cloud | $100\ \mathrm{nK}$ | $1\times10^{19}$ | $0.59\ \mu\mathrm m$ | $2.1$ |
> | conduction electrons in Cu ($g_s = 2$) | $300\ \mathrm K$ | $8.47\times10^{28}$ | $4.3\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $3.4\times10^3$ |
>
> - *Air:* $n = p/kT$, and the mean spacing $n^{-1/3} = 3.4\ \mathrm{nm}$ is 180 times $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}$; the quantum correction to the pressure is $10^{-8}$. At 1 atm, $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3 \propto T^{-5/2}$ would reach 1 only at $T = 300\ \mathrm K\times(1.7\times10^{-7})^{2/5} \approx 0.6\ \mathrm K$, far below where air liquefies (Schwartz: $0.57\ \mathrm K$).
> - *Helium gas at its boiling point* is mildly degenerate: Theorem §B10.2.4 lowers the pressure of $^4$He by $0.135/2^{5/2} = 2.4\%$, while for $^3$He (a fermion with $g_s = 2$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}} = 0.49\ \mathrm{nm}$, $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3/g_s = 0.10$) it raises it by $1.8\%$. In real helium these statistical terms compete with the effects of the interatomic forces.
> - *Liquid helium-4* exceeds $\zeta(\tfrac32) = 2.612$: as an ideal Bose gas it would be condensed ([[§B12.1 Bose–Einstein Condensation#^ex-b12-1-3|Example §B12.1.3]]).
> - *Ultracold rubidium* comes close to the critical value $\zeta(\tfrac32) = 2.612$: this is how Bose–Einstein condensates are made, at nanokelvin temperatures and densities $10^{-9}$ of a liquid.
> - *Electrons in a metal* are degenerate by a factor of thousands at room temperature, because the electron mass is small (Schroeder: $v_Q = (4.3\ \mathrm{nm})^3$ against $(0.2\ \mathrm{nm})^3$ per electron); so are the electrons of a white dwarf and the neutrons of a neutron star ([[§B13.1 The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals]], [[§B13.3 White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars]]).
>
> A photon gas has no fixed density: its density is itself of order $\lambda^{-3}$ with $\lambda = hc/kT$, so it is never classical ([[§B11.1 Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 10, §4 (air); Schroeder §7.2, §7.3, Problems 7.9, 7.68, 7.75; Blundell Example 30.9 (density of liquid helium)*
