How does a real gas differ from an ideal one, and what do the differences say about its molecules? Level A stated the van der Waals equation as an empirical improvement on the ideal gas law ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-4|Law §A2.1.4]]) and showed its isotherms qualitatively ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^rem-a2-1-3|Remark: Isotherms of a real gas and the critical point]]). Here the equation changes layer: it becomes a **model**, a partition function built on two stated assumptions, from which the equation of state, the energy, the entropy and the critical point are derived. The virial expansion then makes the low-density corrections exact in terms of the intermolecular potential and gives the two van der Waals constants a microscopic meaning. Last, the same corrections explain why a real gas cools in a free expansion and why throttling cools it only below an inversion temperature. Where liquid and vapour coexist is the subject of [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation]].

Notation: $U$ is the internal energy (level A's $E_{\text{int}}$), $k = k_B$, $N$ the number of molecules, $n = N/V$ the number density, $\beta = 1/kT$, and $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mkT}$ the thermal wavelength of [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas]]. The van der Waals constants $a$ and $b$ are **per molecule** (Schroeder); the molar constants of level A and Blundell are $N_A^2a$ and $N_Ab$, and with $N = n_{\text{mol}}N_A$, $Nk = n_{\text{mol}}R$ every formula below converts to theirs. The first law is used as at level A, $dU = đQ - p\,dV$.

## The van der Waals model

> [!model] Model §B9.1.1: The van der Waals Gas
> $N$ identical classical molecules of mass $m$ in a volume $V$ at temperature $T$, with two corrections to the ideal gas ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]]):
> 1. **Hard-core repulsion.** Each molecule has a finite size, so the volume available to any one molecule is not $V$ but $V - Nb$, with $b$ the excluded volume per molecule.
> 2. **Mean-field attraction.** The molecules attract weakly at longer range. The attraction is replaced by its average over a *uniform* density: each molecule interacts with a number of neighbours proportional to $N/V$, so the total potential energy is the constant $-aN^2/V$, with $a > 0$.
>
> The canonical partition function ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function]]) is then that of the ideal gas with $V \to V - Nb$, times the Boltzmann factor of the mean potential energy:
>
> $$
> Z = \frac{1}{N!}\Bigl(\frac{V - Nb}{\lambda^3}\Bigr)^{N} e^{\beta aN^2/V} .
> $$
>
> - *Assumes:* classical translational motion ($\lambda$ much smaller than the spacing); the density is uniform on the scale of the attraction, with no clustering or correlations (the mean-field assumption); internal degrees of freedom, if any, contribute a factor $z_{\text{int}}^N$ independent of $V$.
> - *Fails:* quantitatively everywhere outside the dilute limit, and qualitatively near the critical point, where the density fluctuates on all scales ([[§B9.3 The Classification of Phase Transitions]]). It is a model for understanding, not for numbers.
>
> *Source: Blundell §26.1 (box: Origin of the a/V² term; eq. 26.10) · Schroeder §5.3*

^mod-b9-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Where the two corrections come from
> - The excluded volume is the cheaper idea: a fluid cannot be compressed below the volume $Nb$ at which the molecules touch, so $p \to \infty$ as $V \to Nb$.
> - For the attraction, freeze all molecules in place. Each one interacts with neighbours whose number is proportional to $N/V$, so its share of the potential energy is proportional to $-N/V$ and the total to $-aN^2/V$. At fixed entropy (nothing thermal is moving) $p = -(\partial U/\partial V)_S$, so this energy contributes $-\partial(-aN^2/V)/\partial V = -aN^2/V^2$ to the pressure: the attraction pulls the molecules near a wall back into the gas.
> - The model is the simplest that has both ingredients. Repulsion alone gives no liquid; attraction alone lets the gas collapse.

^rem-b9-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.2: Thermodynamics of the van der Waals Gas
> For the van der Waals gas ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^mod-b9-1-1|Model §B9.1.1]]), for large $N$,
>
> $$
> F = -NkT\Bigl[\ln\frac{V - Nb}{N\lambda^3} + 1\Bigr] - \frac{aN^2}{V},
> $$
>
> and from it the **van der Waals equation of state**, the internal energy and the entropy of a monatomic gas:
>
> $$
> p = \frac{NkT}{V - Nb} - \frac{aN^2}{V^2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Bigl(p + \frac{aN^2}{V^2}\Bigr)(V - Nb) = NkT,
> $$
>
> $$
> U = \tfrac32 NkT - \frac{aN^2}{V}, \qquad S = Nk\Bigl[\ln\frac{V - Nb}{N\lambda^3} + \frac52\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> With internal degrees of freedom, $U = U_{\text{ideal}}(T) - aN^2/V$ and $C_V = C_V^{\text{ideal}}(T)$, independent of $V$. The energy depends on $a$ but not on $b$; the entropy on $b$ but not on $a$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §26.1, eq. 26.10; §27.2, Example 27.3; Exercise 26.5 · Schroeder §5.3*

^thm-b9-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With Stirling's formula $\ln N! \approx N\ln N - N$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian]]),
>
> $$
> \ln Z = N\ln\frac{V - Nb}{N\lambda^3} + N + \frac{\beta aN^2}{V} ,
> $$
>
> and $F = -kT\ln Z$ ([[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function]]) gives the first formula. Then $p = -(\partial F/\partial V)_{T,N}$: the logarithm gives $NkT/(V - Nb)$ and the last term $-aN^2/V^2$. For the energy, $U = -\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta$; since $\lambda \propto \beta^{1/2}$, $-3N\ln\lambda = -\tfrac32 N\ln\beta + \text{const}$, which contributes $\tfrac32 N/\beta = \tfrac32 NkT$, and $\beta aN^2/V$ contributes $-aN^2/V$. Finally $S = (U - F)/T$; the $-aN^2/V$ terms cancel, leaving the Sackur–Tetrode form ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas]]) with $V - Nb$ in place of $V$. A factor $z_{\text{int}}(T)^N$ adds to $F$ and $U$ terms that depend on $T$ only, so it changes neither $p$ nor the $V$-dependence of $U$.

^der-b9-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^mod-b9-1-1|Model §B9.1.1]], [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian]], [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas]]

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from level A, and why energy and entropy each see one constant
> - At level A the van der Waals equation was a measured fact with fitted constants ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-4|Law §A2.1.4]]). Here it follows from a partition function, so its assumptions are explicit, and the same $Z$ also yields $U$, $S$, $G$ and, in [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation]], the liquid–vapour transition. With $a = b = 0$ everything reduces to the ideal gas, whose law is a theorem at this level ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas]]).
> - $U$ "cares" about the attraction, which is potential energy; it does not care about the molecules' size, which only removes volume. $S$ counts the accessible microstates, which depend on the free volume $V - Nb$ but not on a constant energy shift (Blundell §27.2). The isothermal expansion of [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^ex-b9-1-2|Example §B9.1.2]] shows both effects at work.

## The critical point and corresponding states

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.3: Critical Point of the van der Waals Gas
> The isotherms $p(V)$ of the van der Waals gas decrease monotonically for $T > T_c$ and are non-monotonic (rise over an interval of $V$) for $T < T_c$. The **critical isotherm** $T = T_c$ has a horizontal inflection, $(\partial p/\partial V)_T = (\partial^2p/\partial V^2)_T = 0$, at the critical point
>
> $$
> V_c = 3Nb, \qquad kT_c = \frac{8a}{27b}, \qquad p_c = \frac{a}{27b^2}, \qquad \frac{p_cV_c}{NkT_c} = \frac38 = 0.375 .
> $$
>
> Where $(\partial p/\partial V)_T > 0$ the isothermal compressibility $\kappa_T = -V^{-1}(\partial V/\partial p)_T$ ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions]]) is negative, and the homogeneous fluid is unstable.
>
> *Source: Blundell §26.1, Example 26.1 · Schroeder §5.3, Problem 5.48*

^thm-b9-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]],
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T = -\frac{NkT}{(V - Nb)^2} + \frac{2aN^2}{V^3} < 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad kT > g(V) := \frac{2aN(V - Nb)^2}{V^3} .
> $$
>
> On $V > Nb$, $g'(V) = 2aN(V - Nb)(3Nb - V)/V^4$, so $g$ rises to a single maximum at $V = 3Nb$ and falls after, with $g(3Nb) = 2aN(2Nb)^2/(27N^3b^3) = 8a/(27b)$. Hence the isotherm decreases everywhere exactly when $kT \ge 8a/(27b) =: kT_c$; for $T < T_c$ it rises on the interval where $g(V) > kT$. At $T = T_c$ the slope vanishes only at $V = 3Nb$, where it touches zero from below, so $(\partial^2p/\partial V^2)_T = 0$ there too: a horizontal inflection. Substituting, $p_c = NkT_c/(2Nb) - aN^2/(9N^2b^2) = 4a/(27b^2) - 3a/(27b^2) = a/(27b^2)$, and $p_cV_c/(NkT_c) = (a/27b^2)(3Nb)(27b/8aN) = 3/8$.

^der-b9-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-7|451 Cor. §29.7]]

> [!caution] Caution: A qualitative model
> The combination $p_cV_c/(NkT_c)$ is independent of $a$ and $b$, so it tests the model's *form*. The van der Waals value $0.375$ is well off the measured values: $0.29$ for Ne, Ar, Kr and Xe (Blundell Table 26.1), $0.274$ for CO$_2$, $0.227$ for H$_2$O (Schroeder §5.3). Other phenomenological equations of state do better on this number; Dieterici's $p(V - Nb) = NkT\,e^{-aN/(kTV)}$ gives $2/e^2 = 0.271$ (Blundell §26.2). Tabulated $a$ and $b$ are therefore usually fitted to $T_c$ and $p_c$, and the van der Waals equation is used for what it gets right qualitatively: the liquid–gas transition, the shape of the coexistence curve and the existence of a critical point (Schroeder §5.3).

^cau-b9-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B9.1.1: Reduced Variables
> For a fluid with critical point $(p_c, V_c, T_c)$, the **reduced variables** are
>
> $$
> \tilde p = \frac{p}{p_c}, \qquad \tilde V = \frac{V}{V_c}, \qquad \tilde T = \frac{T}{T_c} ,
> $$
>
> and likewise $\tilde n = n/n_c = 1/\tilde V$ at fixed $N$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §26.4, eq. 26.61 · Schroeder §5.3, Problem 5.51 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, eq. (30)*

^def-b9-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.4: The van der Waals Equation in Reduced Form
> In reduced variables ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^def-b9-1-1|Def. §B9.1.1]]) the van der Waals equation of state reads
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\tilde p + \frac{3}{\tilde V^2}\Bigr)(3\tilde V - 1) = 8\tilde T, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \tilde p = \frac{8\tilde T}{3\tilde V - 1} - \frac{3}{\tilde V^2} .
> $$
>
> The constants $a$ and $b$ have disappeared: every van der Waals gas has the same reduced isotherms.
>
> *Source: Blundell §26.4, Example 26.3 · Schroeder §5.3, Problem 5.51*

^thm-b9-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert $p = \tilde p\,a/(27b^2)$, $V = 3Nb\,\tilde V$ and $kT = 8a\tilde T/(27b)$ from [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]] into the equation of state of [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]:
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{a\tilde p}{27b^2} + \frac{aN^2}{9N^2b^2\tilde V^2}\Bigr)Nb\,(3\tilde V - 1) = \frac{8aN\tilde T}{27b} .
> $$
>
> Multiplying by $27b/(aN)$ gives $(\tilde p + 3/\tilde V^2)(3\tilde V - 1) = 8\tilde T$.

^der-b9-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]], [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]]

> [!law] Law §B9.1.5: Law of Corresponding States
> Fluids whose molecules interact in similar ways have, when expressed in reduced variables, the same equation of state and the same liquid–vapour coexistence curve, although their critical constants differ widely (for example $T_c = 44$ K for Ne, $151$ K for Ar, $191$ K for CH$_4$). In particular $p_cV_c/(NkT_c)$ is nearly the same for all of them ($0.287$–$0.292$ for Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe), and the reduced coexistence densities $\tilde n_\ell(\tilde T)$, $\tilde n_g(\tilde T)$ of Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, N$_2$, O$_2$, CO and CH$_4$ lie on one curve.
>
> *Valid when:* the molecules are small, nearly spherical and nonpolar, and classical. It fails for strongly polar molecules (water), for elongated molecules, and for the light quantum fluids H$_2$ and He.
> *Source: Blundell §26.4, Table 26.1, Fig. 26.11 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §4.2, eq. (31) and Fig. 13*

^law-b9-1-5

> [!remark] Remark: Why corresponding states works
> Blundell's explanation can be made precise. Suppose every pair of molecules interacts through a potential of one common shape, $u(r) = \varepsilon\,\phi(r/\sigma)$, with only an energy scale $\varepsilon$ and a length $\sigma$ differing between substances (the Lennard-Jones potential of [[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^ex-a1-2-2|WO Example §A1.2.2]] is of this form). In the classical partition function the momentum integrals give $\lambda^{-3N}$, which does not depend on $V$, and the substitution $\vec r_i = \sigma\vec x_i$ turns the position integrals into a function of $V/\sigma^3$, $N$ and $\varepsilon/kT$ alone. Then $p = kT\,\partial\ln Z/\partial V$ makes $p\sigma^3/\varepsilon$ a universal function of $kT/\varepsilon$ and $V/(N\sigma^3)$. So $kT_c \propto \varepsilon$, $V_c/N \propto \sigma^3$, $p_c \propto \varepsilon/\sigma^3$ with universal factors, and dividing by them leaves one universal equation in reduced variables. The van der Waals form is one approximation to it ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-4|Theorem §B9.1.4]]); the true universal function is not van der Waals, which is why the law holds better than the model. The argument needs pairwise forces of one shape and classical motion, which is exactly the *Valid when* of the law: water's dipoles add an angle dependence, and for He and H$_2$ the length $\lambda$ enters as a third scale.

## The virial expansion

> [!definition] Definition §B9.1.2: Virial Expansion; Virial Coefficients; Boyle Temperature
> The **virial expansion** writes the equation of state of a gas as a power series in the number density $n = N/V$:
>
> $$
> \frac{pV}{NkT} = 1 + B(T)\,\frac NV + C(T)\Bigl(\frac NV\Bigr)^2 + \cdots .
> $$
>
> $B(T)$ is the **second virial coefficient** (a volume per molecule), $C(T)$ the third, and so on. In molar form $pV_m/(RT) = 1 + B_m/V_m + C_m/V_m^2 + \cdots$ with $B_m = N_AB$ and $C_m = N_A^2C$. The ratio $pV/(NkT)$ is the **compression factor**. The **Boyle temperature** $T_B$ is the temperature at which $B(T_B) = 0$; there, to first order in the density, $pV$ is independent of $V$ at fixed $T$, as in Boyle's law.
>
> *Source: Blundell §26.3, eq. 26.40 · Schroeder §8.1 (and Problem 1.17)*

^def-b9-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.6: Second Virial Coefficient from the Pair Potential
> For a classical gas of molecules interacting through a spherically symmetric pair potential $u(r)$ that falls off faster than $r^{-3}$,
>
> $$
> B(T) = -\frac12\int d^3r\,f(r) = 2\pi\int_0^\infty\bigl(1 - e^{-u(r)/kT}\bigr)\,r^2\,dr, \qquad f(r) = e^{-u(r)/kT} - 1 ,
> $$
>
> where $f$ is the **Mayer $f$-function**. A deep attractive well makes $f > 0$ over a range of $r$ and $B$ negative; a repulsive core makes $f = -1$ inside it and $B$ positive.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §8.1 · Blundell §26.3, eqs. 26.46–26.60*

^thm-b9-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Factorise.* The energy is $\sum_i p_i^2/2m + U_{\text{pot}}$ with $U_{\text{pot}} = \sum_{\text{pairs}}u_{ij}$, $u_{ij} = u(|\vec r_i - \vec r_j|)$. The $3N$ momentum integrals of the classical partition function are those of the ideal gas, so $Z = Z_{\text{ideal}}\,Z_c$ with the **configuration integral**
>
> $$
> Z_c = \frac{1}{V^N}\int d^3r_1\cdots d^3r_N\,\prod_{\text{pairs}}e^{-\beta u_{ij}} = \frac{1}{V^N}\int d^3r_1\cdots d^3r_N\,\prod_{\text{pairs}}(1 + f_{ij}) .
> $$
>
> *One pair.* Multiplying out, the first term is $1$. A term with a single $f_{12}$ gives $V^{N-2}\int d^3r_1\,d^3r_2\,f_{12}$; with $\vec r = \vec r_2 - \vec r_1$ the inner integral $\int d^3r\,f(r)$ is the same for every $\vec r_1$ not within a few molecular diameters of a wall, so the term is $V^{-1}\int d^3r\,f(r)$. There are $N(N-1)/2 \approx N^2/2$ such terms, totalling $(N^2/2V)\int d^3r\,f$.
>
> *Exponentiate.* This total is not small: $\int d^3r\,f$ is of the order of a molecular volume $v_0$, so it is of order $N\cdot nv_0$, huge for macroscopic $N$ even when $nv_0 \ll 1$. The series cannot be cut after this term. But terms with $k$ factors $f$ on $k$ *disjoint* pairs are the most numerous of order $k$, and they add up to $\frac{1}{k!}\bigl((N^2/2V)\int f\bigr)^k$ for $k \ll N$; summed over $k$,
>
> $$
> Z_c \approx \exp\Bigl(\frac{N^2}{2V}\int d^3r\,f(r)\Bigr),
> $$
>
> with corrections from clusters of three or more interacting molecules that are smaller by further powers of $nv_0$ (the cluster expansion; Schroeder §8.1). *Pressure.* So $\ln Z_c = -N^2B/V$ with $B$ as stated, $F = F_{\text{ideal}} + NkT\,NB/V$, and $p = -(\partial F/\partial V)_{T,N} = NkT/V + NkT\,NB/V^2$, which is the virial expansion with second coefficient $B$. *Spherical coordinates.* $f$ depends on $r$ only, so $\int d^3r\,f = 4\pi\int_0^\infty f(r)\,r^2\,dr$ ([[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-20|452 Thm. §15.20]]); the improper integral ([[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-1|451 Def. §36.1]]) converges at large $r$ because $f \approx -\beta u$ falls faster than $r^{-3}$.

^der-b9-1-6

*Uses:* [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^def-b9-1-2|Def. §B9.1.2]], [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas]], [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-20|452 Thm. §15.20]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-1|451 Def. §36.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: The single-pair term is not small
> It is tempting to write $Z_c \approx 1 + (N^2/2V)\int f$ and then $\ln Z_c \approx (N^2/2V)\int f$ "because $\ln(1 + x) \approx x$". The result is right but the step is not: $x$ is of order $N\,nv_0$, not small. What makes the answer right is that the disconnected terms build up the exponential, so the *logarithm* of $Z_c$, not $Z_c$ itself, is the quantity that is extensive and has a good expansion in the density.

^cau-b9-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.7: Virial Expansion of the van der Waals Gas; Boyle Temperature
> For $Nb < V$ the van der Waals equation of state expands as
>
> $$
> \frac{pV}{NkT} = 1 + \Bigl(b - \frac{a}{kT}\Bigr)\frac NV + b^2\Bigl(\frac NV\Bigr)^2 + b^3\Bigl(\frac NV\Bigr)^3 + \cdots ,
> $$
>
> so $B(T) = b - a/kT$, and every higher coefficient is a power of $b$. The Boyle temperature is
>
> $$
> kT_B = \frac ab, \qquad T_B = \frac{27}{8}\,T_c .
> $$
>
> *Source: Blundell §26.3, Example 26.2 · Schroeder §8.1, Problem 8.12*

^thm-b9-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]], $pV/(NkT) = (1 - Nb/V)^{-1} - aN/(kTV)$. For $Nb/V < 1$ the geometric series gives $(1 - Nb/V)^{-1} = \sum_{k\ge0}(Nb/V)^k$; collecting powers of $N/V$ gives the expansion. $B(T_B) = 0$ gives $kT_B = a/b$, and with $kT_c = 8a/(27b)$ ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]]) $T_B/T_c = 27/8$.

^der-b9-1-7

*Uses:* [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]], [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]], [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^def-b9-1-2|Def. §B9.1.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.8: Microscopic Meaning of the van der Waals Constants
> Let the pair potential be a hard core of diameter $\sigma$ ($u = \infty$ for $r < \sigma$) with a weak attractive tail, $|u(r)| \ll kT$ for $r > \sigma$. Then the second virial coefficient ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-6|Theorem §B9.1.6]]) has exactly the van der Waals form $B = b - a/kT$ of [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-7|Theorem §B9.1.7]], with
>
> $$
> b = \frac{2\pi}{3}\,\sigma^3 = 4\times\frac{4\pi}{3}\Bigl(\frac\sigma2\Bigr)^3, \qquad a = -2\pi\int_\sigma^\infty u(r)\,r^2\,dr = -\frac12\int_{r > \sigma}u\,d^3r > 0 .
> $$
>
> So $b$ is four times the volume of one molecule (half the volume of the sphere of radius $\sigma$ that one molecule denies to the centre of another), and $a$ is half the integrated strength of the attraction.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §8.1, Problems 8.11–8.12*

^thm-b9-1-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Split the integral of [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-6|Theorem §B9.1.6]] at $\sigma$. Inside, $e^{-u/kT} = 0$, so $2\pi\int_0^\sigma r^2\,dr = 2\pi\sigma^3/3$. Outside, $|u|/kT \ll 1$ and $1 - e^{-u/kT} \approx u/kT$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), so $2\pi\int_\sigma^\infty(u/kT)\,r^2\,dr = -a/kT$ with $a$ as stated; $a > 0$ because $u < 0$ outside the core. The mean-field energy of [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^mod-b9-1-1|Model §B9.1.1]] agrees: with uniform density each of the $N$ molecules sees $n\int_{r>\sigma}u\,d^3r$, and halving to count each pair once gives $\tfrac12 Nn\int u\,d^3r = -aN^2/V$.

^der-b9-1-8

*Uses:* [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-6|Theorem §B9.1.6]], [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-7|Theorem §B9.1.7]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The shape of B(T) for real gases
> Real intermolecular potentials are soft: strongly repulsive at short range and weakly attractive further out, often modelled by the Lennard-Jones form $u = 4\varepsilon[(\sigma/r)^{12} - (\sigma/r)^6]$, whose $r^{-6}$ tail is the fluctuating-dipole attraction between nonpolar molecules ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^ex-a1-2-2|WO Example §A1.2.2]]). Then:
> - at low $T$ the integrand of [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-6|Theorem §B9.1.6]] is dominated by the well, where $f$ is large and positive: $B$ is large and negative, and the gas is easier to compress than an ideal one;
> - as $T$ rises the well matters less, the core dominates, and $B$ changes sign at $T_B$ and becomes small and positive;
> - at very high $T$ fast molecules partly penetrate the soft core and $B$ falls slowly again.
>
> With $\varepsilon$ and $\sigma$ fitted for each gas, the computed $B(T)$ matches measurements for simple gases; the misfits are for elongated or polar molecules (CO$_2$), and for H$_2$ and He at low $T$, where quantum effects matter (Schroeder §8.1, Fig. 8.2; Blundell §26.3, Fig. 26.9). Measured $B(T)$ is therefore a way to *measure* the intermolecular potential.

^rem-b9-1-4

## Cooling a real gas

> [!remark] Remark: Why throttling conserves enthalpy
> In a **Joule–Kelvin** (Joule–Thomson) expansion gas is pushed steadily through a porous plug or valve from pressure $p_1$ to $p_2 < p_1$, with no heat exchanged. A parcel of volume $V_1$ has work $p_1V_1$ done on it as it is pushed in and does work $p_2V_2$ as it pushes its way out, so $U_2 - U_1 = p_1V_1 - p_2V_2$: the enthalpy $H = U + pV$ is the same before and after, $H_1 = H_2$. The temperature change per unit pressure drop is the Joule–Kelvin coefficient $\mu_{JK} = (\partial T/\partial p)_H$, whose general form $\mu_{JK} = [T(\partial V/\partial T)_p - V]/C_p$ is derived from a Maxwell relation in [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions]]. It vanishes for an ideal gas; the process is irreversible, with $\Delta S = -\int_{p_1}^{p_2}(V/T)\,dp > 0$.

^rem-b9-1-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.9: Joule–Kelvin Inversion Temperature
> At low density, to first order in $N/V$,
>
> $$
> \mu_{JK} = \frac{N}{C_p}\Bigl[T\,\frac{dB}{dT} - B(T)\Bigr],
> $$
>
> so a throttled gas cools ($\mu_{JK} > 0$, since $dp < 0$) when $T\,B' > B$. For the van der Waals gas
>
> $$
> \mu_{JK} = \frac{N}{C_p}\Bigl(\frac{2a}{kT} - b\Bigr), \qquad kT_{\text{inv}} = \frac{2a}{b}, \qquad T_{\text{inv}} = 2T_B = \frac{27}{4}\,T_c ,
> $$
>
> and throttling at low pressure cools the gas only below the **maximum inversion temperature** $T_{\text{inv}}$. At any pressure, the van der Waals **inversion curve** $\mu_{JK} = 0$ is, in reduced variables,
>
> $$
> \tilde p = 24\sqrt{3\tilde T} - 12\tilde T - 27, \qquad \tfrac34 \le \tilde T \le \tfrac{27}{4} ,
> $$
>
> a dome with its top at $(\tilde T, \tilde p) = (3, 9)$; throttling cools inside it and warms outside.
>
> *Source: Blundell §27.3, eqs. 27.20–27.23, Fig. 27.3; Exercise 27.2*

^thm-b9-1-9

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Low density.* Invert the virial expansion to first order: $p = (NkT/V)(1 + NB/V)$ gives $V = NkT/p + NB(T) + O(p)$. Then $T(\partial V/\partial T)_p - V = NkT/p + NTB' - NkT/p - NB = N(TB' - B)$, and the general formula for $\mu_{JK}$ ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^rem-b9-1-5|Remark: Why throttling conserves enthalpy]]) gives the first result. For $B = b - a/kT$ ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-7|Theorem §B9.1.7]]), $TB' = a/kT$, so $TB' - B = 2a/kT - b$, which vanishes at $kT = 2a/b = 2kT_B$; with $kT_c = 8a/27b$, $T_{\text{inv}}/T_c = 27/4$.
>
> *Any density.* $\mu_{JK} = 0$ means $T(\partial V/\partial T)_p = V$. By the triple-product rule ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms]]) $(\partial V/\partial T)_p = -(\partial p/\partial T)_V/(\partial p/\partial V)_T$, so the condition is $-T(\partial p/\partial T)_V = V(\partial p/\partial V)_T$. With $(\partial p/\partial T)_V = Nk/(V - Nb)$ and $(\partial p/\partial V)_T$ from [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^der-b9-1-3|the derivation of Theorem §B9.1.3]],
>
> $$
> -\frac{NkT}{V - Nb} = -\frac{NkTV}{(V - Nb)^2} + \frac{2aN^2}{V^2} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{NkT\,Nb}{(V - Nb)^2} = \frac{2aN^2}{V^2} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad kT = \frac{2a}{b}(1 - x)^2 ,
> $$
>
> with $x = Nb/V$. Putting this into the equation of state, $p = (a/b^2)(2x - 3x^2)$. In reduced units ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]]) $\tilde T = \tfrac{27}{4}(1 - x)^2$ and $\tilde p = 27(2x - 3x^2)$. Eliminating $x = 1 - \tfrac29\sqrt{3\tilde T}$ gives the stated curve; $\tilde p \ge 0$ needs $0 \le x \le \tfrac23$, i.e. $\tfrac34 \le \tilde T \le \tfrac{27}{4}$, and $d\tilde p/dx = 27(2 - 6x) = 0$ at $x = \tfrac13$ gives the top $(3, 9)$. At low pressure ($x \to 0$) the curve meets $\tilde T = 27/4$, the first result again.

> [!remark] Remark: The physics of the sign, and liquefaction
> - Throttling has two competing effects. The attraction ($a$) acts as in a free expansion: separating the molecules raises their potential energy at the expense of kinetic energy, which cools. The finite size ($b$) makes $pV$ larger than $NkT$, so pushing the gas through costs more flow work on the inlet side than it returns at the outlet, which warms. At low $T$ the attraction wins.
> - A liquefier (Linde process) throttles compressed gas that has been precooled below $T_{\text{inv}}$ by a counter-current heat exchanger fed with the cold exhaust. Its liquid yield is largest when the inlet state lies on the inversion curve, because there the inlet enthalpy at the working temperature is smallest ($(\partial H/\partial p)_T = -C_p\mu_{JK} = 0$; Blundell §27.4).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B9.1.1: Argon: the van der Waals Constants and the Molecule
> Argon has $T_c = 151$ K and $p_c = 4.86$ MPa. Inverting [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]], $b = kT_c/(8p_c)$ and $a = 27(kT_c)^2/(64p_c)$:
>
> $$
> kT_c = 2.085\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm J, \qquad b = 5.36\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm{m^3} = 53.6\ \text{Å}^3, \qquad a = 3.77\times10^{-49}\ \mathrm{J\,m^3} = 2.35\ \mathrm{eV\,\text{Å}^3} .
> $$
>
> (Per mole, $N_Ab = 32.3\ \mathrm{cm^3/mol}$ and $N_A^2a = 0.137\ \mathrm{J\,m^3/mol^2}$.)
>
> Now compare with the molecule. Argon's Lennard-Jones parameters, fitted to its measured $B(T)$, are a well depth $\varepsilon = 0.0105$ eV at a minimum $r_0 = 3.86$ Å, so the potential crosses zero at $\sigma = r_0/2^{1/6} = 3.44$ Å. Treat $r < \sigma$ as a hard core and $r > \sigma$ as the weak tail, and apply [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-8|Theorem §B9.1.8]]:
>
> $$
> b = \frac{2\pi}{3}\sigma^3 = 85\ \text{Å}^3, \qquad a = -2\pi\int_\sigma^\infty 4\varepsilon\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac\sigma r\Bigr)^{12} - \Bigl(\frac\sigma r\Bigr)^{6}\Bigr]r^2\,dr = 8\pi\varepsilon\sigma^3\Bigl(\frac13 - \frac19\Bigr) = \frac{16\pi}{9}\,\varepsilon\sigma^3 = 2.39\ \mathrm{eV\,\text{Å}^3} .
> $$
>
> The attraction constant agrees with the critical-point value to 2%, closer than the model deserves; the excluded volume comes out 60% too large, since a soft core lets molecules approach closer than $\sigma$ at $T \sim T_c$, and the condition $kT \gg \varepsilon$ ($\varepsilon/k = 122$ K) is not met. Both constants have the right size: $b$ is a few times a molecular volume, and $a$ is a hundredth of an eV times some tens of Å$^3$, a weak interaction acting over a molecular volume.
>
> *Source: Blundell §26.3, Fig. 26.9 (critical data) · Schroeder §8.1, Fig. 8.2 (Lennard-Jones parameters), Problem 8.12; §5.3 (orders of magnitude)*

^ex-b9-1-1

> [!example] Example §B9.1.2: Isothermal and Free Expansion of a van der Waals Gas
> A van der Waals gas expands from $V$ to $\alpha V$ ($\alpha > 1$).
>
> *(a) Reversibly at constant $T$.* At constant $T$, $dS = (\partial p/\partial T)_V\,dV$ by a Maxwell relation ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions]]), and $(\partial p/\partial T)_V = Nk/(V - Nb)$:
>
> $$
> \Delta S = Nk\ln\frac{\alpha V - Nb}{V - Nb}, \qquad Q = T\Delta S, \qquad \Delta U = aN^2\Bigl(\frac1V - \frac{1}{\alpha V}\Bigr) > 0 ,
> $$
>
> the last from [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]. The entropy depends on $b$ only and reduces to $Nk\ln\alpha$ for $b = 0$. The first law then gives the work, $W = Q - \Delta U = NkT\ln\frac{\alpha V - Nb}{V - Nb} - aN^2\bigl(\frac1V - \frac1{\alpha V}\bigr)$, which is what level A found by integrating $p\,dV$ directly ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^ex-a3-2-2|Example §A3.2.2]]).
>
> *(b) Freely into a vacuum* (Joule expansion: insulated, no work, $\Delta U = 0$; [[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-1|Theorem §A3.6.1]]). Since $U = U_{\text{ideal}}(T) - aN^2/V$ with constant $C_V$,
>
> $$
> C_V\,(T_f - T_i) = -aN^2\Bigl(\frac1V - \frac1{\alpha V}\Bigr) < 0 :
> $$
>
> the gas cools, as level A argued qualitatively ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^rem-a3-6-1|Remark: Free expansion of real gases]]). For one mole of argon ($C_V = \tfrac32R$, $N_A^2a = 0.137\ \mathrm{J\,m^3/mol^2}$ from [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^ex-b9-1-1|Example §B9.1.1]]) doubling from 1 L to 2 L gives $\Delta T = -(0.137)(500\ \mathrm{m^{-3}})/(12.5\ \mathrm{J/K}) = -5.5$ K. The entropy change is the same as in (a) for the same end states with $T_f \approx T_i$, plus the small $C_V\ln(T_f/T_i)$; it is positive, as it must be for an irreversible process in an isolated system.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Midterm, Problem 1 · Blundell §27.1–27.2, Examples 27.1–27.3*

^ex-b9-1-2

> [!example] Example §B9.1.3: Why Helium Must Be Precooled
> Helium's second virial coefficient vanishes at $T_B = 19$ K. If $B$ has the van der Waals form, [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-9|Theorem §B9.1.9]] predicts a maximum inversion temperature $T_{\text{inv}} = 2T_B = 38$ K. From the critical temperature instead ($T_c = 5.2$ K for $^4$He), $T_{\text{inv}} = \tfrac{27}{4}T_c = 35$ K. The measured value is 43 K. So helium at room temperature *warms* when throttled: it must first be cooled below about 40 K by other means. Kamerlingh Onnes first liquefied helium in 1908 by precooling it with liquid hydrogen (measured $T_{\text{inv}} = 204$ K) before the Joule–Kelvin stage. Nitrogen and argon, with measured $T_{\text{inv}} = 607$ K and $794$ K, cool on throttling from room temperature, which is why air can be liquefied by the Linde process directly. The van der Waals estimate $\tfrac{27}{4}T_c$ is rough (for N$_2$, with $T_c = 126$ K, it gives 851 K), but it gets the order of magnitude and the ranking right.
>
> *Source: Blundell §27.3, Table 27.1; Exercise 27.2; §27.4 · Schroeder §5.3 (critical points of He and N$_2$)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The ideal-gas limit: the van der Waals gas reduces to [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]] for $a = b = 0$ or $N/V \to 0$; its partition function is that of [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas]] with two modifications. The same mean-field idea (replace each neighbour's state by the average) gives the mean-field theory of the Ising ferromagnet (Schroeder §8.2), the subject of level C (C2, planned).
