*Sources: Schwartz, Physics 181 lectures, Lecture 9 · Schroeder, An Introduction to Thermal Physics, §5.3, §8.2 · Blundell & Blundell, Concepts in Thermal Physics, ch. 26, 28.*

Boiling has a latent heat; a magnet losing its magnetization at the Curie temperature has none, and neither does a fluid passing through its critical point. What, precisely, distinguishes such transitions? This section classifies them by which derivatives of the Gibbs function jump, introduces the order parameter and the idea of a broken symmetry, and uses the van der Waals model of [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation]] to compute how quantities vanish or diverge at a critical point. The model's predictions, the mean-field critical exponents, are simple and wrong in an instructive way: real critical points share *different* exponents, the same for fluids and magnets. Level A met only the latent heat of melting and boiling ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^law-a1-4-1|Law §A1.4.1]]) and the critical point as the end of the vaporization curve ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^def-a1-4-2|Def. §A1.4.2]]).

Notation as in [[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation]]; $k = k_B$; reduced variables $\tilde p, \tilde V, \tilde T, \tilde n$ as in [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^def-b9-1-1|Def. §B9.1.1]].

## First-order and continuous transitions

> [!definition] Definition §B9.3.1: Ehrenfest Classification
> A phase transition is of **order $n$** (Ehrenfest) if the $n$-th derivatives of the Gibbs function $G(T, p)$ (or of $\mu = G/N$) are discontinuous across the phase boundary while all lower derivatives are continuous. **First order:** the first derivatives $S = -(\partial G/\partial T)_p$ and $V = (\partial G/\partial p)_T$ jump. **Second order:** $S$ and $V$ are continuous but second derivatives, such as $C_p = -T(\partial^2G/\partial T^2)_p$ and $\kappa_T = -V^{-1}(\partial^2G/\partial p^2)_T$, jump.
>
> *Source: Blundell §28.7, Fig. 28.12 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §4 · Schroeder §5.3 (footnote on classification)*

^def-b9-3-1

> [!definition] Definition §B9.3.2: First-Order and Continuous Transitions
> In current usage a transition is **first order** if it has a latent heat (first derivatives of $G$ jump), and **continuous** otherwise. Continuous transitions lump together all of Ehrenfest's orders $n \ge 2$; they are also loosely called "second order".
>
> *Source: Blundell §28.7 · Schroeder §5.3 (footnote) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §4*

^def-b9-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.3.1: Signatures of a First-Order Transition
> At a first-order transition at fixed pressure and temperature $T_t$:
> 1. the equilibrium $G(T)$ is continuous with a kink: it follows the lower of the two phases' curves, which cross at $T_t$ with different slopes;
> 2. $S$ and $V$ jump, by $\Delta S = L/T_t$ and $\Delta V$, related by the Clausius–Clapeyron equation ([[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]]);
> 3. the heat capacity $C_p = T(\partial S/\partial T)_p$ contains a term $L\,\delta(T - T_t)$: the heat $L$ is absorbed at a single temperature, and a measured $C_p(T)$ shows a sharp spike.
>
> At a continuous transition, instead, the two phases become identical at the transition point: $\Delta S = \Delta V = 0$ and $L = 0$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §28.1, §28.7 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §2 and §4*

^thm-b9-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each phase's $\mu_i(T, p)$ is smooth inside its own region ([[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^def-b9-2-1|Def. §B9.2.1]]); in equilibrium $G = N\min_i\mu_i$ ([[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]]). At $T_t$ the curves cross with slopes $-s_1 \ne -s_2$, so $\min$ is continuous with a corner, and its derivative $-S$ jumps by $N(s_2 - s_1) = L/T_t$ ([[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]]); the same argument in $p$ gives the jump of $V$. Heating through $T_t$ at constant $p$ needs $\int C_p\,dT = \Delta H$, which includes the finite $L$ over a zero temperature interval, hence the $\delta$-function. If $L = 0$ and $\Delta V = 0$, the first derivatives are continuous, which is the definition of a continuous transition.

^der-b9-3-1

*Uses:* [[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^def-b9-2-1|Def. §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]], [[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why Ehrenfest's scheme was abandoned
> Ehrenfest pictured higher-order transitions as finite jumps in $C_p$ or $\kappa_T$. Real continuous transitions instead show *singularities*: the heat capacity of a fluid near its critical point grows without bound rather than stepping (Schwartz Fig. 12, propane). The cause is fluctuations: thermodynamics assumes that averages such as the density are sharply defined, but near a continuous transition fluctuations grow on all length scales, visible as the clouding called critical opalescence (Blundell §28.7). So the modern scheme asks only whether there is a latent heat. First-order transitions, having one, also have a barrier: forming the new phase costs surface free energy, which allows the metastable states of [[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^rem-b9-2-2|Remark: Reading chemical potentials, and metastability]]. Continuous transitions have no such barrier (Schwartz L9 §4).

^rem-b9-3-1

> [!law] Law §B9.3.2: Trouton's Rule
> The molar latent heat of vaporization of a liquid at its normal boiling point $T_b$ (at 1 atm) is roughly proportional to $T_b$:
>
> $$
> \frac{L_{\text{vap}}}{RT_b} \approx 8\text{–}10 .
> $$
>
> | Substance | Ne | Ar | Kr | Xe | CH$_4$ | C$_6$H$_6$ | H$_2$O | He |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $T_b$ (K) | 27.1 | 87.3 | 119.8 | 165.0 | 111.7 | 353.9 | 373.15 | 4.22 |
> | $L_{\text{vap}}/RT_b$ | 7.85 | 8.98 | 9.06 | 9.21 | 8.80 | 10.5 | 13.1 | 2.39 |
>
> *Valid when:* ordinary nonpolar liquids. It fails for strongly hydrogen-bonded liquids (water, above) and for quantum liquids (helium, far below).
> *Source: Blundell §28.1, eqs. 28.5–28.7, Table 28.1*

^law-b9-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: An estimate of the entropy jump
> Trouton's rule says that the entropy of vaporization $\Delta S = L/T_b$ is about the same, roughly $9R$ per mole, for many liquids. A crude count explains the size. The number of positions available to one molecule is proportional to the volume it can roam, so for one mole $\Omega_{\text{vap}}/\Omega_{\text{liq}} \approx (v_g/v_\ell)^{N_A} \approx (10^3)^{N_A}$, and Boltzmann's formula ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-1|Principle §A4.6.1]]; statistical form [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]]) gives $\Delta S \approx R\ln 10^3 \approx 7R$. The rest of the observed value comes from the attraction the molecules leave behind. That the ratio is nearly universal is the law of corresponding states ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^law-b9-1-5|Law §B9.1.5]]): normal boiling points sit at a similar reduced temperature, near $0.6\,T_c$.

^rem-b9-3-2

## Order parameters and broken symmetry

> [!definition] Definition §B9.3.3: Order Parameter
> An **order parameter** of a transition is a quantity that is zero in one phase (usually the high-temperature, disordered one) and nonzero in the other, so that its value tells the phases apart. Examples: the magnetization $M$ of a ferromagnet in zero applied field; for the liquid–vapour transition, the density difference $n_\ell - n_g$ between the coexisting phases, which vanishes at and above $T_c$. At a continuous transition the order parameter rises continuously from zero; at a first-order transition it jumps.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §2 and §4 · Schroeder §8.2 · Blundell §28.7*

^def-b9-3-3

> [!definition] Definition §B9.3.4: Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking
> A symmetry is **spontaneously broken** when the energy (Hamiltonian) of a system is invariant under a symmetry operation but its equilibrium state is not. Then several equivalent states exist, mapped into each other by the symmetry, and the system settles into one of them. Examples: a ferromagnet below its Curie temperature, whose spin interactions do not prefer a direction but whose magnetization picks one; a crystal, whose atoms interact through translation- and rotation-invariant forces but which occupies a lattice with only discrete symmetries, while the liquid it freezes from is, on average, invariant under all translations and rotations.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §4.1 · Blundell §28.7, Fig. 28.13 · Schroeder §8.2*

^def-b9-3-4

> [!remark] Remark: Symmetry decides whether a boundary can end
> A symmetry is either present or absent; it cannot change gradually. So two phases of different symmetry, such as a solid and a liquid, can never be joined continuously, and the melting curve cannot end in a critical point: it continues indefinitely (Blundell §28.3, §28.7; Schroeder §5.3). Liquid and vapour have the *same* symmetry and differ only in density, so their boundary can end and they can be connected by a path around the critical point ([[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^rem-b9-2-1|Remark: Why the definition is about smoothness]]). A broken continuous symmetry also brings excitations of arbitrarily low energy (Goldstone's theorem; Schwartz L9 §4.1): sound waves in a crystal, the phonons of [[§B11.2★ Phonons and the Debye Model]], and spin waves in a magnet.

^rem-b9-3-3

## Critical exponents

> [!definition] Definition §B9.3.5: Critical Exponents
> Near a critical point, with $T_c$, $p_c$, $V_c$ its coordinates, the **critical exponents** describe the power laws
>
> $$
> n_\ell - n_g \propto (T_c - T)^{\beta}, \qquad |p - p_c| \propto |V - V_c|^{\delta}\ (T = T_c), \qquad \kappa_T \propto \begin{cases}(T - T_c)^{-\gamma}, & T \downarrow T_c,\\ (T_c - T)^{-\gamma'}, & T \uparrow T_c,\end{cases}
> $$
>
> the first along the coexistence curve, the second on the critical isotherm, the third on the critical isochore above $T_c$ and on the coexistence curve below; similarly $C_V \propto |T - T_c|^{-\alpha}$. For a ferromagnet the same letters describe $M \propto (T_c - T)^\beta$ and the susceptibility $\chi = (\partial M/\partial B)_T \propto |T - T_c|^{-\gamma}$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §5.3, Problem 5.55; §8.2, Problem 8.24 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §4.2*

^def-b9-3-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.3.3: The van der Waals Fluid near Its Critical Point
> Write $\tau = \tilde T - 1$ and $\varphi = \tilde V - 1$. Near the critical point the van der Waals equation ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-4|Theorem §B9.1.4]]) is
>
> $$
> \tilde p = 1 + 4\tau - 6\tau\varphi - \tfrac32\varphi^3 + O(\tau\varphi^2, \varphi^4),
> $$
>
> and for $|\tau| \ll 1$, to leading order:
> 1. *Coexistence* ($\tau < 0$): $\tilde p_v = 1 + 4\tau$, so the vapour-pressure curve reaches the critical point with slope $dp_v/dT = 4p_c/T_c$; the coexisting volumes are $\tilde V_{g,\ell} = 1 \pm 2\sqrt{-\tau}$, so $\tilde n_\ell - \tilde n_g = 4\sqrt{-\tau}$: $\beta = \tfrac12$.
> 2. *Critical isotherm* ($\tau = 0$): $\tilde p - 1 = -\tfrac32\varphi^3$: $\delta = 3$.
> 3. *Compressibility:* $\kappa_T = 1/(6p_c\tau)$ on the critical isochore above $T_c$ and $\kappa_T = 1/(12p_c|\tau|)$ on the coexistence curve below: $\gamma = \gamma' = 1$, with amplitudes in the ratio 2.
> 4. *Latent heat:* $L = 6NkT_c\sqrt{1 - T/T_c}$, which vanishes at $T_c$: the first-order liquid–vapour transition becomes continuous at the critical point.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §5.3, Problem 5.55 · Blundell §26.1, Example 26.1, Exercise 26.1 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §4.2*

^thm-b9-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Expansion.* With $3\tilde V - 1 = 2(1 + \tfrac32\varphi)$, Taylor expansion ([[§11 Taylor's Theorem for Multivariable Functions#^thm-11-2|452 Thm. §11.2]]) gives
>
> $$
> \frac{8\tilde T}{3\tilde V - 1} = 4(1 + \tau)\Bigl(1 - \tfrac32\varphi + \tfrac94\varphi^2 - \tfrac{27}{8}\varphi^3 + \cdots\Bigr), \qquad \frac{3}{\tilde V^2} = 3\bigl(1 - 2\varphi + 3\varphi^2 - 4\varphi^3 + \cdots\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Subtracting, the constant terms give $4 - 3 = 1$, the $\varphi$ terms $-6\varphi + 6\varphi = 0$, the $\varphi^2$ terms $9\varphi^2 - 9\varphi^2 = 0$ (the inflection of [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]]), and the $\varphi^3$ terms $-\tfrac{27}{2}\varphi^3 + 12\varphi^3 = -\tfrac32\varphi^3$; the $\tau$ terms give $4\tau - 6\tau\varphi + 9\tau\varphi^2 + \cdots$. On the scale $\varphi \sim |\tau|^{1/2}$ the terms $\tau\varphi$ and $\varphi^3$ are of the same order $|\tau|^{3/2}$, while $\tau\varphi^2$ and $\varphi^4$ are of order $\tau^2$ and are dropped.
>
> *1.* The truncated $\tilde p - 1 - 4\tau = -\varphi(6\tau + \tfrac32\varphi^2)$ is odd in $\varphi$. So the horizontal line $\tilde p = 1 + 4\tau$ cuts the loop symmetrically about $\varphi = 0$, the two lobes have equal areas, and by [[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-6|Theorem §B9.2.6]] it is the coexistence pressure. Its outer intersections solve $6\tau + \tfrac32\varphi^2 = 0$, i.e. $\varphi = \pm2\sqrt{-\tau}$. With $\tilde n = 1/\tilde V \approx 1 - \varphi$, $\tilde n_\ell - \tilde n_g = 4\sqrt{-\tau}$.
>
> *2.* Set $\tau = 0$.
>
> *3.* $\kappa_T = -V^{-1}(\partial V/\partial p)_T = -1/\bigl(p_c\tilde V\,\partial\tilde p/\partial\varphi\bigr) \approx -1/(p_c\,\partial\tilde p/\partial\varphi)$, and $\partial\tilde p/\partial\varphi = -6\tau - \tfrac92\varphi^2$. At $\varphi = 0$, $\tau > 0$ this is $-6\tau$; at $\varphi^2 = -4\tau$, $\tau < 0$ it is $-6\tau + 18\tau = -12|\tau|$.
>
> *4.* By [[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-7|Theorem §B9.2.7]], $L = NkT\ln\frac{2 + 3\varphi_g}{2 + 3\varphi_\ell} = NkT\ln\frac{1 + 3\epsilon/2}{1 - 3\epsilon/2}$ with $\epsilon = 2\sqrt{-\tau}$, and $\ln\frac{1 + x}{1 - x} = 2x + O(x^3)$ gives $L \approx 3\epsilon\,NkT_c = 6NkT_c\sqrt{-\tau}$. Check with Clausius–Clapeyron: $L = T\Delta V\,dp_v/dT \approx T_c\,(4\sqrt{-\tau}\,V_c)(4p_c/T_c) = 16p_cV_c\sqrt{-\tau}$, and $p_cV_c = \tfrac38 NkT_c$ makes this $6NkT_c\sqrt{-\tau}$.

> [!law] Law §B9.3.4: Universality of Critical Exponents
> Near their critical points, different substances have the *same* critical exponents, and these are not the van der Waals (mean-field) values $\beta = \tfrac12$, $\gamma = 1$, $\delta = 3$. For liquid–vapour critical points $\beta \approx \tfrac13$ and $\delta \approx 4$–$5$. Uniaxial ferromagnets in three dimensions, and the three-dimensional Ising model, share these values ($\beta \approx \tfrac13$, $\gamma \approx 1.24$). The exponents depend only on the dimension of space and on the number of components of the order parameter (one for a fluid's density difference and for a uniaxial magnet), not on the microscopic details; in two dimensions the Ising values are $\beta = \tfrac18$, $\gamma = \tfrac74$ (Onsager's exact solution).
>
> *Valid when:* close to the critical point, $|T - T_c| \ll T_c$, for systems with short-range interactions.
> *Source: Schroeder §5.3, Problem 5.55; §8.2, Problem 8.24 and the closing problem on block-spin transformations · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §4.2, eq. (32), Fig. 13*

^law-b9-3-4

> [!remark] Remark: Why mean-field theory fails, and where this goes next
> - The van der Waals model replaces the attraction a molecule feels by its average over a uniform density ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^mod-b9-1-1|Model §B9.1.1]]). The mean-field theory of the Ising ferromagnet does the same with the neighbours' spins, and gives the same exponents, $\beta = \tfrac12$, $\gamma = 1$ (Schroeder §8.2). Both ignore exactly what dominates near $T_c$: correlated fluctuations of the density or magnetization over regions much larger than the molecular spacing. Far from $T_c$ the fluctuations are small and mean-field theory is qualitatively right.
> - Mean-field theory errs most in low dimension. It predicts that a one-dimensional Ising chain orders below $kT_c = 2J$ ($J$ the nearest-neighbour coupling, Schroeder's $\epsilon$), but the exact solution has no transition at any $T > 0$: one break in a chain of $N$ aligned spins costs a fixed energy but gains entropy $k\ln N$, so breaks always win (Blundell §28.8; Schroeder §8.2). In two dimensions Onsager's exact solution orders at $kT_c = 2J/\ln(1 + \sqrt2) = 2.269\,J$ (Blundell §28.8).
> - Landau's theory of continuous transitions, the Ising model (exact in one and two dimensions, mean-field, Monte Carlo), and the renormalization group, which explains universality, are the subject of level C (C2–C3, planned).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B9.3.1: Classifying Familiar Transitions
> | Transition | Order parameter | Type | Why |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | melting, boiling, sublimation ($T < T_c$) | density difference | first order | latent heat; $S$ and $V$ jump |
> | liquid–vapour at the critical point | $n_\ell - n_g \to 0$ | continuous | $L \to 0$ ([[§B9.3★ The Classification of Phase Transitions#^thm-b9-3-3\|Theorem §B9.3.3]]) |
> | graphite–diamond | crystal structure | first order | $\Delta S$, $\Delta V \ne 0$ (Schroeder §5.3) |
> | ferromagnet at the Curie point, zero field (iron: 1043 K) | $M$ | continuous | $M$ rises continuously from zero; $C_B$ has a finite peak |
> | ferromagnet below $T_C$, field reversed through zero | $M$ | first order | $M$ jumps from $+M_0$ to $-M_0$; the up and down phases coexist on the line $B = 0$, which ends at the Curie point like a liquid–vapour line at its critical point |
> | superconductor at $T_c$, zero field | (wavefunction phase) | continuous | no latent heat |
> | $^4$He normal to superfluid (2.2 K) | (superfluid fraction) | continuous | no latent heat |
> | $\beta$-brass (CuZn) order–disorder | sublattice occupation | continuous | Cu and Zn order on two sublattices below $T_c$ |
>
> The ferromagnet shows that "first order" or "continuous" belongs to the path, not the substance: crossing the coexistence line $B = 0$ below $T_C$ is first order; passing through its end point is continuous, exactly as for liquid and vapour. The superfluid transition of helium is related to Bose–Einstein condensation ([[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation]]).
>
> *Source: Blundell §28.7, Example 28.8; §28.8 · Schroeder §5.3, Fig. 5.15 and footnote · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §4.1*

> [!example] Example §B9.3.2: How Fast the Latent Heat Vanishes
> (a) *Van der Waals.* From the exact Maxwell construction ([[§B9.2★ Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-7|Theorem §B9.2.7]]) and from the critical-point formula ([[§B9.3★ The Classification of Phase Transitions#^thm-b9-3-3|Theorem §B9.3.3]]):
>
> | $T/T_c$ | $\tilde V_\ell$ | $\tilde V_g$ | exact $L/NkT_c$ | $6\sqrt{1 - T/T_c}$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | 0.90 | 0.603 | 2.349 | 1.809 | 1.897 |
> | 0.99 | 0.831 | 1.243 | 0.597 | 0.600 |
>
> The square-root law is 5% high at $0.9\,T_c$ and 0.5% high at $0.99\,T_c$.
>
> (b) *Water.* Schroeder's table gives $L = 30.90$, $25.30$ and $16.09$ kJ/mol at 250, 300 and 350 °C, with $T_c = 647.15$ K. Fitting $L \propto (1 - T/T_c)^x$ to successive pairs, $x = \ln(25.30/16.09)/\ln(0.1143/0.0371) = 0.40$ between 300 and 350 °C, and $0.39$ between 250 and 300 °C. Since $dp_v/dT$ stays finite at $T_c$, $L = T\Delta V\,dp_v/dT$ vanishes like $\Delta V$, i.e. with the exponent $\beta$. The effective $0.4$, measured still 24–74 K below $T_c$, is already well below the mean-field $\tfrac12$ and on its way toward the universal $\beta \approx \tfrac13$ ([[§B9.3★ The Classification of Phase Transitions#^law-b9-3-4|Law §B9.3.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder §5.3 (table in Fig. 5.11), Problem 5.55 · Blundell §28.3, Fig. 28.4*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The critical-point expansion is a two-variable Taylor expansion ([[§11 Taylor's Theorem for Multivariable Functions#^thm-11-2|452 Thm. §11.2]]); the exponents of Theorem §B9.3.3 come from the lowest terms that survive, which is why every mean-field theory gives the same ones.
> - Response functions $C_p$, $\kappa_T$ as second derivatives of $G$: [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^def-b3-2-1|Def. §B3.2.1]]. Their divergence near $T_c$ is the thermodynamic face of large fluctuations, which are tied to response functions in the canonical ensemble, as $C_V$ to the energy fluctuations ([[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]]).
> - Other phase transitions in this subject: Bose–Einstein condensation ([[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation]]). Landau theory, the Ising model and the renormalization group: level C (C2–C3, planned).
> - Electromagnetism: the ferromagnet as a material, with domains as regions of uniform order parameter ([[§B9.3★ The Classification of Phase Transitions#^def-b9-3-3|Def. §B9.3.3]]) and spontaneous alignment ([[§B9.3★ The Classification of Phase Transitions#^def-b9-3-4|Def. §B9.3.4]]) lost at the Curie point — [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^mod-b7-2-7|EM Model §B7.2.7]].
