Level A described phase diagrams, vapour pressure and latent heat as observed facts ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^def-a1-4-2|Def. §A1.4.2]], [[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^def-a1-4-3|Def. §A1.4.3]], [[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^law-a1-4-1|Law §A1.4.1]]). This section derives them from one condition: two phases coexist where their chemical potentials are equal. From it follow the latent heat as a jump of entropy and enthalpy, the Clausius–Clapeyron equation for the slope of every coexistence line, the vapour-pressure curve, and the Gibbs phase rule, which says why coexistence happens on lines and triple points. For the van der Waals model of [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation]] the same condition becomes the Maxwell construction, which replaces the unphysical loops of its isotherms by liquid–vapour coexistence.

Notation: $k = k_B$; for a pure phase of $N$ molecules, $\mu = G/N$ is the chemical potential ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]]) and $s = S/N$, $v = V/N$, $h = H/N$ are the entropy, volume and enthalpy per molecule. A latent heat $L$ refers to whatever amount of substance is being converted (molar when quoted in kJ/mol, with $R = N_Ak$); per molecule it is $\ell$; level A's latent heats are per unit mass.

## Coexistence

> [!definition] Definition §B9.2.1: Phase; Phase Boundary
> A **phase** of a substance is a state of matter whose properties (density, entropy per molecule, compressibility, …) vary smoothly, as analytic functions of the state variables such as $T$ and $p$, throughout a region of the phase diagram. A **phase boundary** is a set of states across which some property changes non-analytically; crossing it is a **phase transition**. This sharpens level A's descriptive [[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^def-a1-4-1|Def. §A1.4.1]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §1 · Schroeder §5.3*

^def-b9-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why the definition is about smoothness
> "A uniform state of matter" is too broad (water at 20 °C and at 30 °C would be different phases) and "solid, liquid, gas" too narrow (ferromagnetic, superconducting, superfluid and the many crystal structures of ice are phases too). The smoothness definition fixes both, at a price: since the liquid–vapour boundary *ends* at the critical point ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^def-a1-4-2|Def. §A1.4.2]]), a liquid can be turned into a gas along a path that never crosses a boundary, so "liquid" and "gas" are strictly one phase in two regimes. In practice, as Schwartz notes, physicists use "phase" for a region where one description (one approximate partition function) works, and phase boundaries are where two such descriptions meet ([[§B9.3 The Classification of Phase Transitions]]).

^rem-b9-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.1: Conditions for Phase Equilibrium
> Consider a pure substance at fixed $T$ and $p$ that can exist in phases $1, 2, \dots$ with chemical potentials $\mu_1(T, p), \mu_2(T, p), \dots$, and neglect surface energies.
> 1. In equilibrium all the substance is in the phase of lowest chemical potential.
> 2. Two phases coexist in equilibrium only where $\mu_1(T, p) = \mu_2(T, p)$. Where $v_1 \ne v_2$, this equation defines a smooth curve $p = p_{12}(T)$ in the $p$–$T$ plane, the **coexistence curve** (phase boundary).
> 3. Three phases coexist only where $\mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu_3$: two equations for two unknowns, so generically at isolated **triple points**.
>
> *Source: Blundell §28.2 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §2 · Schroeder §5.3*

^thm-b9-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At fixed $T$ and $p$ the equilibrium state minimises $G$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]). A pure phase has $G = N\mu(T, p)$ because $G$ is extensive and its intensive variables $T$, $p$ do not depend on $N$ ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]]). With $N_1$ molecules in phase 1 and $N - N_1$ in phase 2,
>
> $$
> G = N_1\mu_1 + (N - N_1)\mu_2, \qquad \frac{\partial G}{\partial N_1} = \mu_1 - \mu_2 .
> $$
>
> There is no entropy of mixing, because the phases separate in space rather than intermix, and the surface energy is negligible for macroscopic amounts. So $G$ is linear in $N_1$: if $\mu_1 < \mu_2$ it is smallest at $N_1 = N$, if $\mu_1 > \mu_2$ at $N_1 = 0$; molecules move from the higher to the lower chemical potential until one phase is used up. Only if $\mu_1 = \mu_2$ is every split equally good, which is (1) and the first part of (2).
>
> For the curve, put $\Phi(T, p) = \mu_1 - \mu_2$. Its partial derivatives are $\partial\Phi/\partial T = -(s_1 - s_2)$ and $\partial\Phi/\partial p = v_1 - v_2$, from $d\mu = -s\,dT + v\,dp$ (the differential of $G/N$ at fixed $N$). If $v_1 \ne v_2$ at a point where $\Phi = 0$, the implicit function theorem ([[§12 The Implicit Function Theorem#^thm-12-1|452 Thm. §12.1]]) gives a unique differentiable solution $p = p_{12}(T)$ nearby. For three phases the two equations $\Phi_{12} = \Phi_{23} = 0$ in the two unknowns $T$, $p$ generically have isolated solutions.

^der-b9-2-1

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]], [[§12 The Implicit Function Theorem#^thm-12-1|452 Thm. §12.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reading chemical potentials, and metastability
> Since $(\partial\mu/\partial T)_p = -s < 0$ and $(\partial\mu/\partial p)_T = v > 0$:
> - Every $\mu(T)$ curve at fixed $p$ falls, and the phase with more entropy falls faster. Raising $T$ therefore favours the higher-entropy phase: solids melt and liquids boil on heating because $s_{\text{solid}} < s_{\text{liquid}} < s_{\text{gas}}$. At $T \to 0$ the solid's curve starts out flat, since its entropy vanishes by the third law ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-3|Principle §A4.6.3]]).
> - Raising $p$ raises every $\mu$, the least dense phase most: high pressure favours the densest phase. For water, ice is less dense than the liquid, so pressure raises $\mu_{\text{ice}}$ more than $\mu_{\text{water}}$ and lowers the melting point (Schwartz Fig. 7).
> - A phase can persist for a while on the wrong side of its boundary, following its own $\mu$ curve past the crossing: **superheated** liquid, **supercooled** vapour or liquid. Such **metastable** states do not have the lowest $\mu$, but converting them requires first forming a small droplet or bubble of the new phase, and surface tension makes small droplets costly. Kelvin's formula $p = p_\infty\exp[2\gamma v_\ell/(rkT)]$ for the vapour pressure over a droplet of radius $r$ ($\gamma$ the surface tension) shows that small droplets evaporate even when the bulk liquid is stable; clouds therefore form on dust particles, and boiling liquids "bump" unless rough surfaces supply nucleation sites (Blundell §28.4).

^rem-b9-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.2: Latent Heat as a Jump in Entropy and Enthalpy
> When an amount of substance changes from phase 1 to phase 2 at a point $(T, p)$ of their coexistence curve, the heat absorbed is
>
> $$
> L = T\,(S_2 - S_1) = H_2 - H_1 .
> $$
>
> Consequently, at a triple point the latent heats of sublimation, fusion and vaporization satisfy $L_{\text{sub}} = L_{\text{fus}} + L_{\text{vap}}$ (per molecule or per mole).
>
> *Source: Blundell §28.1, eq. 28.2; §28.3 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §3.1 · Schroeder §5.3*

^thm-b9-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The conversion happens at constant $T$ and $p$ and, done slowly, reversibly, so the heat absorbed is $Q = T\Delta S$ ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]). At constant $p$ the first law $dU = đQ - p\,dV$ gives $đQ = d(U + pV) = dH$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]), so also $Q = \Delta H$. The two agree because $\mu_1 = \mu_2$ on the curve: $h_1 - Ts_1 = h_2 - Ts_2$, i.e. $\Delta h = T\Delta s$. At a triple point all three phases are at the same $(T, p)$, and $H$ is a state function ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]]), so $h_g - h_s = (h_g - h_\ell) + (h_\ell - h_s)$.

^der-b9-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]], [[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]], [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]], [[§A3.3 The First Law#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What level A's latent heat is
> Level A introduced latent heat as a measured constant of the material ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^law-a1-4-1|Law §A1.4.1]]) and noted that sublimation should cost the sum of fusion and vaporization, leaving the reason to level B ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^rem-a1-4-3|Remark: Where the latent heat goes]]). The reason is that $L$ is the jump of the state function $H$. For water at the triple point, Schroeder's table gives $L_{\text{sub}} = 51.07$ kJ/mol and $L_{\text{vap}} = 45.05$ kJ/mol, hence $L_{\text{fus}} = 6.02$ kJ/mol, matching the measured $6.0$ kJ/mol. And since $L = T\Delta S$, a latent heat is a *discontinuity of the first derivative* $S = -(\partial G/\partial T)_p$, the mark of a first-order transition ([[§B9.3 The Classification of Phase Transitions]]).

^rem-b9-2-3

## The Clausius–Clapeyron equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.3: Clausius–Clapeyron Equation
> Along the coexistence curve of two phases,
>
> $$
> \frac{dp}{dT} = \frac{s_2 - s_1}{v_2 - v_1} = \frac{L}{T\,\Delta V} ,
> $$
>
> where $L$ is the latent heat and $\Delta V = V_2 - V_1$ the volume change for the same amount converted from phase 1 to phase 2. The ratio $L/\Delta V$ is intensive.
>
> *Domain: any coexistence curve with $\Delta V \ne 0$ (liquid–vapour, solid–liquid, solid–vapour, solid–solid).*
> *Source: Blundell §28.3, eqs. 28.13–28.19 · Schroeder §5.3 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §3, eqs. (5)–(8)*

^thm-b9-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Moving along the curve from $(T, p)$ to $(T + dT, p + dp)$ keeps $\mu_1 = \mu_2$ ([[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]]), so $d\mu_1 = d\mu_2$:
>
> $$
> -s_1\,dT + v_1\,dp = -s_2\,dT + v_2\,dp \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dp}{dT} = \frac{s_2 - s_1}{v_2 - v_1} .
> $$
>
> This is the derivative $p_{12}'(T) = -(\partial\Phi/\partial T)/(\partial\Phi/\partial p)$ that the implicit function theorem supplies ([[§12 The Implicit Function Theorem#^thm-12-1|452 Thm. §12.1]]). Multiply numerator and denominator by the number of molecules converted and use $T\Delta S = L$ ([[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]]).

^der-b9-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]], [[§12 The Implicit Function Theorem#^thm-12-1|452 Thm. §12.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reading slopes off the equation
> - A large entropy jump makes the boundary steep (a small change in $T$ shifts the balance a lot, and much pressure is needed to compensate); a large volume jump makes it shallow.
> - **Liquid–vapour and solid–vapour:** $L > 0$ and $\Delta V \approx V_{\text{gas}}$ is large, so the slope is positive and moderate.
> - **Solid–liquid:** $L_{\text{fus}} > 0$ but $\Delta V$ is small, so the melting curve is very steep. Its sign is the sign of $\Delta V$: positive for most substances, which are denser as solids, negative for water, silicon and gallium, whose open tetrahedral solids are less dense than their liquids. This is level A's backward-leaning fusion curve of water ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^rem-a1-4-1|Remark: Reading the diagram for water]]).
> - **Near $T = 0$:** the third law ([[§B2.3 The Third Law]]) makes $\Delta s \to 0$, so every coexistence curve that reaches $T = 0$ does so with zero slope, as the $^4$He melting curve does below 1 K (Schroeder §5.3).
> - *Names.* Schwartz calls the general equation the Clapeyron equation and reserves "Clausius–Clapeyron" for its ideal-vapour form [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-4|Theorem §B9.2.4]]; Blundell and Schroeder, followed here, use "Clausius–Clapeyron" for both.

^rem-b9-2-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.4: The Vapour-Pressure Curve
> For coexistence of a condensed phase (liquid or solid) with its vapour, assume that the vapour is an ideal gas and that the molar volume of the condensed phase is negligible against the vapour's. With $L$ the molar latent heat:
> 1. If $L$ is constant, the vapour pressure is
>
> $$
> p(T) = p_0\exp\Bigl[-\frac LR\Bigl(\frac1T - \frac1{T_0}\Bigr)\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> with $(T_0, p_0)$ any point of the curve: $\ln p$ is linear in $1/T$ with slope $-L/R$, a Boltzmann factor $e^{-\ell/kT}$ in the latent heat per molecule.
> 2. Along the curve the latent heat varies as $dL/dT = \Delta C_p = C_p^{\text{vap}} - C_p^{\text{cond}}$ (molar heat capacities). If $\Delta C_p$ is constant, $L = L_0 + \Delta C_p\,T$ and
>
> $$
> \ln p = -\frac{L_0}{RT} + \frac{\Delta C_p}{R}\ln T + \text{const} .
> $$
>
> *Valid when: well below the critical point, where the vapour is dilute and $v_{\text{cond}} \ll v_{\text{vap}}$; result 1 over a range of $T$ small enough that $L$ hardly changes.*
> *Source: Blundell §28.3, Examples 28.2–28.3 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §3.1, eqs. (10)–(14) · Schroeder §5.3, Problem 5.35*

^thm-b9-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* With $\Delta V \approx V_{\text{vap}} = RT/p$ per mole, [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]] becomes $dp/dT = Lp/(RT^2)$, i.e. $d(\ln p)/dT = L/(RT^2)$. For constant $L$ integrate from $T_0$ to $T$ ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]): $\ln(p/p_0) = -(L/R)(1/T - 1/T_0)$.
>
> *2.* Along the curve $L/T = \Delta s$ (molar), and the total derivative of a function of $(T, p)$ along $p = p(T)$ is $d/dT = (\partial/\partial T)_p + (dp/dT)(\partial/\partial p)_T$ ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]). With $(\partial s/\partial T)_p = c_p/T$ and the Maxwell relation $(\partial s/\partial p)_T = -(\partial v/\partial T)_p$ ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]]),
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dT}\Bigl(\frac LT\Bigr) = \frac{\Delta C_p}{T} - \frac{\partial\,\Delta v}{\partial T}\Big|_p\frac{dp}{dT} = \frac{\Delta C_p}{T} - \frac Rp\cdot\frac{Lp}{RT^2} = \frac{\Delta C_p}{T} - \frac{L}{T^2},
> $$
>
> using $\Delta v \approx v_{\text{vap}} = RT/p$. The left side is $L'/T - L/T^2$, so $L' = \Delta C_p$. Then $d(\ln p)/dT = (L_0 + \Delta C_p T)/(RT^2)$ integrates to the stated form.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.5: Gibbs Phase Rule
> A system of $C$ chemically independent components, in $P$ phases that are in equilibrium with each other, with every component present in every phase, has
>
> $$
> F = C - P + 2
> $$
>
> independent intensive variables (degrees of freedom). For a pure substance ($C = 1$), $F = 3 - P$: one phase fills regions of the $p$–$T$ plane ($F = 2$), two coexist on lines ($F = 1$), three at isolated triple points ($F = 0$), and four phases of one substance cannot coexist.
>
> *Source: Blundell §28.5, Example 28.7*

^thm-b9-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The intensive state of each phase is fixed by $T$, $p$ and the mole fractions of its components, of which $C - 1$ are independent since they sum to 1. With $T$ and $p$ common to all phases (thermal and mechanical equilibrium), there are $2 + P(C - 1)$ variables. Diffusive equilibrium of each component between all phases ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]]) requires $\mu_i(\text{phase }1) = \cdots = \mu_i(\text{phase }P)$, which is $P - 1$ equations for each of the $C$ components. So $F = 2 + P(C - 1) - C(P - 1) = C - P + 2$. For $C = 1$ this is the counting of [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]].

^der-b9-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]], [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Solutes shift the coexistence curves
> A dissolved substance that stays in the liquid (salt in water) lowers the liquid's chemical potential by its entropy of mixing, $\mu_{\text{solution}} \approx \mu_{\text{pure}} - kT\,x_B$ for a small mole fraction $x_B$ of solute particles, the result behind osmotic pressure ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^ex-b3-3-3|Example §B3.3.3]]). The liquid is then favoured against both vapour and solid: the vapour pressure drops by $\Delta p/p = -x_B$ (Raoult's law), the boiling point rises and the freezing point falls by $|\Delta T| \approx x_B\,RT^2/L$, with the molar latent heat of vaporization or fusion respectively. The boiling-point shift follows from Raoult's law and the slope of [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-4|Theorem §B9.2.4]]: moving back up the curve by $|\Delta p|$ costs $\Delta T = |\Delta p|\,RT^2/(pL)$ (Schwartz L9 §3.2; Blundell §28.6). Because $L_{\text{fus}} \approx L_{\text{vap}}/7$ for water, the freezing point moves seven times more than the boiling point, which is why road salt works and salted pasta water is barely hotter.

## Coexistence in the van der Waals model

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.6: Maxwell Construction
> Below $T_c$ the van der Waals isotherm $p(V)$ ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]) takes some pressures at three volumes. The liquid and vapour that coexist at temperature $T$ have the volumes $V_\ell < V_g$ and the pressure $p_v(T)$ fixed by
>
> $$
> p(V_\ell) = p(V_g) = p_v, \qquad \int_{V_\ell}^{V_g}p(V)\,dV = p_v\,(V_g - V_\ell) ,
> $$
>
> i.e. the horizontal line at $p_v$ cuts the loop into two regions of **equal area**. For $V_\ell < V < V_g$ the equilibrium state is a mixture at pressure $p_v$, with a fraction $x = (V - V_\ell)/(V_g - V_\ell)$ of the molecules in the vapour (**lever rule**); the isotherm is horizontal there.
>
> *Source: Blundell §26.1, eqs. 26.24–26.30, Fig. 26.5 · Schroeder §5.3, Figs. 5.21–5.22*

^thm-b9-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Equal $G$.* Coexisting phases have equal chemical potential ([[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]]), i.e. equal $G$ for the same $N$, at the same $T$ and $p$. Along an isotherm at fixed $N$, $dG = V\,dp$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]), so following the van der Waals curve from the liquid state to the vapour state,
>
> $$
> 0 = G_g - G_\ell = \int_{\ell}^{g}V\,dp = \bigl[pV\bigr]_\ell^g - \int_{V_\ell}^{V_g}p\,dV = p_v(V_g - V_\ell) - \int_{V_\ell}^{V_g}p\,dV ,
> $$
>
> integrating by parts along the curve. *Equal areas.* $p_v(V_g - V_\ell)$ is the rectangle under the horizontal line; the integral is the area under the loop. They are equal exactly when the part of the loop below the line (region $A$) and the part above it (region $B$) have equal areas.
>
> *Mixtures.* For a total volume $V$ between $V_\ell$ and $V_g$, a split into $(1 - x)N$ molecules of liquid and $xN$ of vapour, both at $p_v$, has $V = (1 - x)V_\ell + xV_g$, which gives $x$. Its Gibbs function $(1 - x)G_\ell + xG_g = G_\ell$ lies below that of any homogeneous van der Waals state at the same $T$ and $p$: plotted against $p$, the explicit
>
> $$
> G = -NkT\ln(V - Nb) + \frac{NkT\,Nb}{V - Nb} - \frac{2aN^2}{V} + c(T)
> $$
>
> forms a closed loop whose middle branch, the states with $(\partial p/\partial V)_T > 0$, and whose two metastable ends lie above the crossing point where $G_\ell = G_g$ (Schroeder Fig. 5.21; Blundell Fig. 26.3).

Between it and the spinodal (dotted), where $(\partial p/\partial V)_T = 0$, the homogeneous fluid is metastable (superheated liquid on the left, supercooled vapour on the right); inside the spinodal it is unstable.*

> [!remark] Remark: Stability, the spinodal, and the same construction for F
> - A homogeneous state with $(\partial p/\partial V)_T > 0$, a negative compressibility, cannot exist: a small compression would lower the pressure and the fluctuation would grow (Blundell §26.1). The boundary of this region, where $(\partial p/\partial V)_T = 0$, is the **spinodal**, $kT = 2aN(V - Nb)^2/V^3$ (the function $g$ of [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^der-b9-1-3|the derivation of Theorem §B9.1.3]]). Between the spinodal and the coexistence curve the fluid is locally stable but not the global minimum of $G$: these are the metastable states of [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^rem-b9-2-2|Remark: Reading chemical potentials, and metastability]].
> - Equivalently, plot $F(V)$ at fixed $T$. Since $p = -\partial F/\partial V$, the coexisting states have equal slopes, and equal $G = F + pV$ means they lie on one common tangent line. A liquid–vapour mixture has $F$ on that straight line, below the non-convex van der Waals $F$ between the tangent points: the Maxwell construction is the convex hull of $F$ (Schroeder §5.3, Problem 5.54).

^rem-b9-2-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.7: Latent Heat of the van der Waals Fluid
> At temperature $T < T_c$, with $V_\ell$, $V_g$ from [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-6|Theorem §B9.2.6]], vaporizing $N$ molecules of a van der Waals fluid absorbs
>
> $$
> L = NkT\ln\frac{V_g - Nb}{V_\ell - Nb} = NkT\ln\frac{3\tilde V_g - 1}{3\tilde V_\ell - 1}, \qquad\text{with}\qquad \Delta U = aN^2\Bigl(\frac1{V_\ell} - \frac1{V_g}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> $L$ decreases to zero as $T \to T_c$, where $V_\ell$ and $V_g$ merge at $V_c$. The vapour pressures $p_v(T)$ trace a coexistence curve in the $p$–$T$ plane that ends at the critical point.
>
> *Source: Blundell §26.1, Fig. 26.6; §27.2 · Schroeder §5.3, Fig. 5.23*

^thm-b9-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]], $L = T(S_g - S_\ell)$, and the entropy of [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]] depends on $V$ only through $Nk\ln(V - Nb)$; the reduced form uses $V = 3Nb\tilde V$. The energy change follows from $U = U_{\text{ideal}}(T) - aN^2/V$. As a check, $L = \Delta U + p_v\,\Delta V$ (enthalpy jump) is exactly the equal-area condition, since $\int_{V_\ell}^{V_g}p\,dV = NkT\ln\frac{V_g - Nb}{V_\ell - Nb} - aN^2\bigl(\frac1{V_\ell} - \frac1{V_g}\bigr)$. Finally $V_\ell, V_g \to V_c$ as $T \to T_c$ because the loop shrinks to the horizontal inflection of [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]].

^der-b9-2-7

*Uses:* [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]], [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-6|Theorem §B9.2.6]], [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]], [[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: A model that predicts its own phase diagram
> The van der Waals model was built for a gas, yet the Maxwell construction makes it predict a liquid, a vapour-pressure curve, a latent heat that vanishes at a critical point, and the "supercritical" region where liquid and gas can be joined without a transition: everything level A's phase diagram shows for liquid and vapour ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^def-a1-4-2|Def. §A1.4.2]]). It cannot produce a solid; that needs the spatial order of a crystal, which a uniform mean field cannot describe. At $T = 0.9\,T_c$ it gives $L = 2.0\,NkT$; real liquids at their normal boiling points, around $0.6\,T_c$, have $L \approx 9\,NkT$ (Trouton's rule, [[§B9.3 The Classification of Phase Transitions]]), and the model's $L/NkT$ at $0.6\,T_c$ is only $5.1$.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B9.2.1: A Latent Heat from Vapour Pressures, and Tea on a Mountain
> (a) The vapour pressure of water is 611 Pa at 0 °C and 12 349 Pa at 50 °C. By [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-4|Theorem §B9.2.4]] (1), $\ln p$ is linear in $1/T$ with slope $-L/R$:
>
> $$
> L = R\,\frac{\ln(12\,349/611)}{1/273.15\ \mathrm K - 1/323.15\ \mathrm K} = (8.314\ \mathrm{J/(mol\,K)})\,\frac{3.006}{5.66\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}} = 44.1\ \mathrm{kJ/mol} .
> $$
>
> This is the latent heat at the middle of the range; the tabulated value at 25 °C is 43.99 kJ/mol. It is 8% above the 40.7 kJ/mol at 100 °C, because $L$ falls as $T$ rises ($dL/dT = \Delta C_p < 0$, part 2).
>
> (b) On the summit of Mauna Kea the air pressure is 615 mbar. Water boils where its vapour pressure equals the ambient pressure. From $p_0 = 1.013$ bar at $T_0 = 373.15$ K with $L = 40.7$ kJ/mol,
>
> $$
> \frac1T = \frac1{T_0} - \frac RL\ln\frac{p}{p_0} = \frac{1}{373.15\ \mathrm K} + \frac{8.314}{40\,700\ \mathrm K}\ln\frac{1.013}{0.615} = 2.782\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}, \qquad T = 359\ \mathrm K = 86\ ^\circ\mathrm C .
> $$
>
> An astronomer who insists on 97 °C water for tea needs a pressure vessel. Using the 44 kJ/mol of part (a) instead changes $T$ by only 1 K. The same formula gives Schwartz's estimate that water at 1000 m (0.9 bar) boils at 370 K.
>
> *Source: Blundell ch. 28, Exercises 28.2 and 28.5 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 9, §3.1, eq. (15) · Schroeder §5.3 (table in Fig. 5.11)*

^ex-b9-2-1

> [!example] Example §B9.2.2: Ice under Pressure
> (a) The melting curve of water near 0 °C has slope $dp/dT = -1.4\times10^7$ Pa/K, and the specific volumes are $1.00\times10^{-3}$ m$^3$/kg for water and $1.09\times10^{-3}$ m$^3$/kg for ice. [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]] per kilogram, for ice → water, gives the latent heat of fusion:
>
> $$
> L_f = T\,\Delta v\,\frac{dp}{dT} = (273\ \mathrm K)(-0.09\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3/kg})(-1.4\times10^7\ \mathrm{Pa/K}) = 3.4\times10^5\ \mathrm{J/kg} ,
> $$
>
> within the rounding of the data of level A's 334 kJ/kg ([[§A1.4 Phase Changes#^law-a1-4-1|Law §A1.4.1]]). The negative slope is the negative $\Delta v$: ice floats.
>
> (b) Does a skater glide on water melted by the pressure of the blade? On a rink at $-5$ °C, melting needs an extra pressure of about $5\ \mathrm K \times 1.4\times10^7\ \mathrm{Pa/K} = 7\times10^7$ Pa, some 700 atm. A 70 kg skater on a blade contact of roughly 20 cm × 3 mm $= 6\times10^{-4}$ m$^2$ exerts $(70)(9.8)/(6\times10^{-4})\ \mathrm{Pa} \approx 1\times10^6$ Pa, about 11 atm: sixty times too little, which lowers the melting point by under 0.1 K. Pressure melting is not why ice is slippery; frictional heating does much more (Blundell's comment on the exercise).
>
> *Source: Blundell ch. 28, Exercises 28.3 and 28.6 · Schroeder §5.3, Problem 5.32*

> [!example] Example §B9.2.3: How Good Is van der Waals? The Vapour Pressure of CO₂
> In reduced variables every van der Waals fluid has the same vapour-pressure curve $\tilde p_v(\tilde T)$ ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^thm-b9-1-4|Theorem §B9.1.4]]), found numerically from [[§B9.2 Phase Equilibrium and the Clausius–Clapeyron Equation#^thm-b9-2-6|Theorem §B9.2.6]]. Compare with carbon dioxide ($T_c = 304.2$ K, $p_c = 73.8$ bar):
>
> | $T$ | $\tilde T$ | $p_v$ measured | $\tilde p_v$ measured | $\tilde p_v$ van der Waals |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $-40$ °C | 0.767 | 10.07 bar | 0.136 | 0.314 |
> | $-20$ °C | 0.832 | 19.72 bar | 0.267 | 0.459 |
> | $0$ °C | 0.898 | 34.85 bar | 0.472 | 0.641 |
> | $20$ °C | 0.964 | 57.2 bar | 0.775 | 0.862 |
>
> The model gets the shape right, a curve rising to the critical point, but its pressures are too high, by 11% just below $T_c$ and by a factor 2.3 at $0.77\,T_c$: the real curve falls more steeply from the critical point. Near $T_c$ the model's slope is $dp_v/dT = 4p_c/T_c$ ([[§B9.3 The Classification of Phase Transitions]]); the last two CO$_2$ points give $(73.8 - 57.2)\ \mathrm{bar}/11\ \mathrm K = 1.5$ bar/K, i.e. $6.2\,p_c/T_c$. The Clausius–Clapeyron equation turns the difference into a statement about latent heats: the real fluid's $L/\Delta V$ is larger than the model's.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §5.3 (table in Fig. 5.12 and the comparison in the text) · Blundell §26.1*
