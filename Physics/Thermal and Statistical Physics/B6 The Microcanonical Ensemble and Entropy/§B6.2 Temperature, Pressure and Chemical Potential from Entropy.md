What *is* temperature? Level A defined it as the label shared by systems in thermal equilibrium ([[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^pr-a1-1-1|Principle §A1.1.1]], [[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]), found pressure from molecular impacts ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]]), took the ideal gas law from experiment ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]) and defined entropy through heat ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]). Here all of these come out of one function, the entropy $S(U, V, N) = k\ln\Omega$ of [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]]. Two systems that exchange energy, volume or particles settle where their total entropy is largest; the three conditions for that maximum define temperature, pressure and chemical potential as derivatives of $S$. The zeroth law, the Clausius relation $dS = dQ/T$ and $pV = NkT$ become theorems, and the definition of temperature allows something level A could not: negative absolute temperatures.

*Conventions.* As at level A, the first law is $dU = dQ - p\,dV$ for a quasi-static step, with $dQ$ the heat absorbed and $p\,dV$ the work done *by* the system ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]). Schroeder writes $dU = Q + W$ with $W$ the work done *on* the system, and $P$ for pressure; his formulas are converted.

## Temperature

> [!definition] Definition §B6.2.1: Temperature
> The **temperature** $T$ of a system with entropy $S(U, V, N)$ is defined by
>
> $$
> \frac1T = \Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial U}\Bigr)_{V, N}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \beta \equiv \frac{1}{kT} = \Bigl(\frac{\partial\ln\Omega}{\partial U}\Bigr)_{V, N} .
> $$
>
> Any other external parameters (such as the field $B$ on a paramagnet) are also held fixed. The derivative needs a large system, for which adding one energy unit is an infinitesimal change.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.1, eq. (3.5) · Schwartz, Lecture 4, eqs. (13)–(16) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §26.2*

^def-b6-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.1: Thermal Equilibrium and the Direction of Heat Flow
> Let two large systems $A$ and $B$, together isolated, exchange energy through a weak coupling at fixed volumes and particle numbers ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-8|Theorem §B6.1.8]]).
> 1. In the most probable macrostate, their temperatures are equal: $T_A = T_B$.
> 2. Away from it, energy flows into the system with the larger $1/T$: $dU_A > 0$ when $1/T_A > 1/T_B$. For positive temperatures, energy flows from the hotter to the colder system.
>
> Statement 2 is the Clausius statement of the second law ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]]), now a consequence of the statistical second law.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.1, eqs. (3.1)–(3.3), Figure 3.1 · Schwartz, Lecture 4, §2, eqs. (10)–(14); Lecture 5, §2*

^thm-b6-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The entropy of the composite in the macrostate $U_A$ is $S_{\text{tot}}(U_A) = S_A(U_A) + S_B(U - U_A)$ ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-11|Theorem §B6.1.11]]). Since $dU_B = -dU_A$,
>
> $$
> \frac{dS_{\text{tot}}}{dU_A} = \frac{\partial S_A}{\partial U_A} - \frac{\partial S_B}{\partial U_B} = \frac{1}{T_A} - \frac{1}{T_B} .
> $$
>
> (1) The most probable macrostate maximizes $\Omega$, hence $S_{\text{tot}}$; at an interior maximum the derivative vanishes ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^thm-29-1|451 Thm. §29.1]]). (2) By the statistical second law ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-10|Theorem §B6.1.10]]) the system moves toward macrostates of larger $S_{\text{tot}}$, so $dS_{\text{tot}} = (1/T_A - 1/T_B)\,dU_A > 0$: $dU_A$ has the sign of $1/T_A - 1/T_B$. For $T_A, T_B > 0$, $1/T_A > 1/T_B$ means $T_A < T_B$.

> [!remark] Remark: Why the reciprocal slope, and why 1/T is the natural variable
> At a point away from equilibrium the system with the *steeper* $S(U)$ graph gains more entropy from a unit of energy than the other loses, so it takes energy: a steep slope means a cold system. Schroeder's analogy: energy is money, entropy is happiness, temperature is generosity; a "greedy" system (large $\partial S/\partial U$) is cold. The slope has units $\mathrm{J/K}$ per $\mathrm J$, so its reciprocal is a temperature. Schwartz takes $\beta = \partial\ln\Omega/\partial U$ as the primary quantity and $T = 1/k\beta$ as the customary name; $\beta$ is the better variable whenever $S(U)$ is not increasing ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-11|Theorem §B6.2.11]]). Most systems have concave $S(U)$, so $1/T$ falls as $U$ rises and the temperature rises: a positive heat capacity. A system with a convex $S(U)$, such as a star or star cluster held together by gravity, has a negative heat capacity and cannot be in stable thermal equilibrium with a reservoir.

^rem-b6-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.2: Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics
> Call two systems in thermal equilibrium when, in thermal contact, they are in their most probable macrostate. If $A$ is in thermal equilibrium with $B$, and $B$ with $C$, then $A$ is in thermal equilibrium with $C$.
>
> At level A this was a principle ([[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^pr-a1-1-1|Principle §A1.1.1]]); here it is a theorem about the function $T = (\partial S/\partial U)^{-1}$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder, Problem 3.2 (solved here) · Schwartz, Lecture 5, §1*

^thm-b6-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]](1), $T_A = T_B$ and $T_B = T_C$, so $T_A = T_C$. Conversely, if $A$ and $C$ with $T_A = T_C$ are brought into contact, $dS_{\text{tot}}/dU_A = 1/T_A - 1/T_C = 0$ at the present energy split; when $S_A$ and $S_C$ are concave (positive heat capacities), $S_{\text{tot}}$ is concave and a stationary point is its maximum, the most probable macrostate. So $A$ and $C$ are in equilibrium. Equality of a number is transitive; that is all the zeroth law says once temperature is a function of each system's own state.

^der-b6-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.3: Energy and Temperature of the Model Systems
> 1. An Einstein solid with $q \gg N$ ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^mod-b6-1-4|Model §B6.1.4]]) has $U = NkT$ and $C_V = Nk$.
> 2. A monatomic ideal gas has $U = \tfrac32 NkT$ and $C_V = \tfrac32 Nk$.
> 3. More generally, a system with $\Omega \propto U^{N_f/2}$ has $U = \tfrac12 N_f kT$: $\tfrac12 kT$ per quadratic degree of freedom.
>
> Statement 2 is the level-A relation between kinetic energy and temperature ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]]); 1 and 3 agree with equipartition ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]; theorem home [[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]]). This confirms that no extra numerical factor is needed in [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]].
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.1, eqs. (3.9)–(3.13); §3.2, eqs. (3.14)–(3.16) · Schwartz, Lecture 4, §3, eqs. (17)–(21)*

^thm-b6-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) By [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-7|Theorem §B6.1.7]], $S = Nk[\ln(q/N) + 1]$ with $q = U/\epsilon$, so $S = Nk\ln U + (\text{terms without } U)$ and $1/T = \partial S/\partial U = Nk/U$. (2) By [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]], $S = Nk\ln V + \tfrac32 Nk\ln U + k\ln f(N) + k\ln(\delta U/U)$. The last term contributes $-k/U$ to $\partial S/\partial U$, negligible against $\tfrac32 Nk/U$; so $1/T = \tfrac32 Nk/U$. (3) $S = \tfrac12 N_f k\ln U + \text{const}$ gives $1/T = \tfrac12 N_f k/U$. Then $C_V = (\partial U/\partial T)_V$ in each case.

^der-b6-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-7|Theorem §B6.1.7]]

> [!remark] Remark: Is this the thermometer's temperature?
> Level A's operational temperature is what a thermometer reads, ultimately a constant-volume gas thermometer, which measures $pV/Nk$ ([[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^def-a1-1-5|Def. §A1.1.5]]). By [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-8|Theorem §B6.2.8]] an ideal gas has $pV/Nk = T$ with $T$ from [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]]; and any system in equilibrium with the thermometer has the same $T$ ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]]). So the two temperatures agree wherever a gas thermometer works. The statistical definition reaches further: Schroeder stresses that every operational definition is limited by its instrument, and some systems (the paramagnet below) have temperatures no thermometer could read.

## Pressure

> [!definition] Definition §B6.2.2: Pressure (Statistical)
> The **pressure** of a system with entropy $S(U, V, N)$ is
>
> $$
> p = T\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\Bigr)_{U, N} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.4, eq. (3.39) · Schwartz, Lecture 7, §5, eq. (43)*

^def-b6-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.4: Mechanical Equilibrium
> Let two large systems, together isolated, exchange energy and volume through a movable, heat-conducting partition, at fixed particle numbers. In the most probable macrostate $T_A = T_B$ and $p_A = p_B$. At equal temperatures and unequal pressures, the system at higher pressure expands.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.4, eqs. (3.36)–(3.39) · Schwartz, Lecture 7, §5, eqs. (40)–(41) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §6.5*

^thm-b6-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Now $S_{\text{tot}}(U_A, V_A) = S_A(U_A, V_A) + S_B(U - U_A, V - V_A)$, a function of two variables, and at the maximum both partial derivatives vanish. The $U_A$ derivative gives $T_A = T_B$ as in [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]]. Since $dV_B = -dV_A$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial S_{\text{tot}}}{\partial V_A} = \frac{\partial S_A}{\partial V_A} - \frac{\partial S_B}{\partial V_B} = \frac{p_A}{T_A} - \frac{p_B}{T_B},
> $$
>
> which vanishes, with $T_A = T_B$, when $p_A = p_B$. Away from the maximum at a common $T$, $dS_{\text{tot}} = (p_A - p_B)\,dV_A/T > 0$, so $V_A$ grows when $p_A > p_B$.

^der-b6-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-2|Def. §B6.2.2]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-10|Theorem §B6.1.10]], [[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-1|452 Thm. §14.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: A movable wall that does not conduct heat
> The argument assumes that the partition also passes energy as heat, so that the two temperatures equalize. If the wall moves but is insulating, the energies are not free to adjust independently of the volumes, and the equilibrium condition is more complicated than $p_A = p_B$ at a common temperature (Schroeder §3.4).

^cau-b6-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.5: Statistical Pressure Is Mechanical Pressure
> The pressure of [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-2|Def. §B6.2.2]] equals the force per unit area that the system exerts on a slowly moved wall:
>
> $$
> p_{\text{mech}} = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_{S, N} = T\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\Bigr)_{U, N} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.6 (slow compression leaves the entropy unchanged), §3.4 and Problem 3.27 · Schwartz, Lecture 7, §5*

^thm-b6-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *A slow adiabatic compression keeps $S$ fixed.* Picture each particle in one of the discrete standing-wave states of the box. Squeezing the box raises the energies of all the levels, but if the compression is slow enough no particle is kicked into a different level: a particle in the $n$-th level stays in the $n$-th level. The number of ways of arranging the particles among the levels is unchanged, so $\Omega$ and $S$ are constant (Schroeder §2.6; this is the quantum adiabatic theorem, which the sources use without proof). A violent compression that does kick particles up raises $S$.
>
> *Mechanical pressure.* In such a step no heat flows, and the wall does work $-p_{\text{mech}}\,dV$ on the system ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]), so by the first law ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]) $dU = -p_{\text{mech}}\,dV$ at constant $S$ and $N$: $p_{\text{mech}} = -(\partial U/\partial V)_{S,N}$.
>
> *Change of variables.* Along this path $S(U, V)$ is constant, so $0 = (\partial S/\partial U)_V\,dU + (\partial S/\partial V)_U\,dV$ by the chain rule ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]), and
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_S = -\frac{(\partial S/\partial V)_U}{(\partial S/\partial U)_V} = -T\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\Bigr)_U ,
> $$
>
> an instance of the triple-product rule ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]]). Hence $p_{\text{mech}} = T(\partial S/\partial V)_U$.
>
> Schroeder and Schwartz argue instead by elimination: $T(\partial S/\partial V)_U$ is equal for systems in mechanical equilibrium, as pressure is; it has the units of pressure; and for the ideal gas it gives $p = NkT/V$ ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-8|Theorem §B6.2.8]]). Schwartz calls this the "law of syllogism".

## Chemical potential

> [!definition] Definition §B6.2.3: Chemical Potential (Statistical)
> The **chemical potential** of a system with entropy $S(U, V, N)$ is
>
> $$
> \mu = -T\Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial N}\Bigr)_{U, V} .
> $$
>
> For several species, $\mu_i = -T(\partial S/\partial N_i)_{U, V, N_{j \ne i}}$, one chemical potential per conserved particle number. The minus sign is a convention that makes $\mu$ negative in most circumstances. Chemists use $-T(\partial S/\partial n)$ per mole, which is $N_A$ times larger.
>
> The thermodynamic chemical potential and its uses (phase and chemical equilibrium) are homed in [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]], which lists the same derivative among its equivalent forms; [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-7|Theorem §B6.2.7]] shows the two agree, $\mu = (\partial U/\partial N)_{S,V}$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.5, eqs. (3.55), (3.65), (3.67) · Schwartz, Lecture 7, §6, eqs. (49)–(50); Lecture 4, §6.3*

^def-b6-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.6: Diffusive Equilibrium
> Let two large systems of the same species, together isolated, exchange energy and particles at fixed volumes. In the most probable macrostate $T_A = T_B$ and $\mu_A = \mu_B$. At equal temperatures, particles flow from the system of higher $\mu$ to that of lower $\mu$. For mixtures, the chemical potential of each exchanged species is separately equal.
>
> Together with [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]] and [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-4|Theorem §B6.2.4]] these are the statistical forms of the equilibrium conditions that thermodynamics derives from the entropy maximum ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]]; for $T$ and $p$ alone, [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.5, eqs. (3.52)–(3.56), Figure 3.19 · Schwartz, Lecture 7, §6, eq. (48) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §33.4*

^thm-b6-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> As for [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-4|Theorem §B6.2.4]], with $N$ in place of $V$ and $dN_B = -dN_A$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial S_{\text{tot}}}{\partial N_A} = \frac{\partial S_A}{\partial N_A} - \frac{\partial S_B}{\partial N_B} = -\frac{\mu_A}{T_A} + \frac{\mu_B}{T_B} .
> $$
>
> At the maximum this vanishes, and with $T_A = T_B$ it gives $\mu_A = \mu_B$. Away from it at a common $T$, $dS_{\text{tot}} = (\mu_B - \mu_A)\,dN_A/T > 0$, so $N_A$ decreases when $\mu_A > \mu_B$. With several species each $N_{i,A}$ is a separate variable of $S_{\text{tot}}$, and each partial derivative vanishes.

## The thermodynamic identity and the Clausius entropy

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.7: Thermodynamic Identity; Statistical and Clausius Entropy Agree
> 1. For any system whose entropy $S(U, V, N)$ is differentiable,
>
> $$
> dS = \frac1T\,dU + \frac pT\,dV - \frac\mu T\,dN, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad dU = T\,dS - p\,dV + \mu\,dN .
> $$
>
> In particular $T = (\partial U/\partial S)_{V,N}$, $p = -(\partial U/\partial V)_{S,N}$ and $\mu = (\partial U/\partial N)_{S,V}$.
> 2. In a quasi-static process at fixed $N$ with no work other than $p\,dV$, the heat absorbed is $dQ = T\,dS$. Hence $\Delta S = \int dQ/T$ along every reversible path: $S = k\ln\Omega$ is the entropy of [[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]], up to the additive constant that thermodynamics leaves open and that $k\ln\Omega$ fixes ($S = 0$ for a unique ground state, $\Omega = 1$; [[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-3|Principle §A4.6.3]], [[§B2.3 The Third Law#^rem-b2-3-1|Remark: The third law, stated precisely]]). Adiabatic and quasi-static together mean isentropic.
> 3. In a change between nearby equilibrium states in which more work is done on the system than $-p\,dV$ (a piston struck hard) or the gas expands into vacuum, $dS > dQ/T$: entropy is created.
>
> This is the layer change announced at level A: the agreement of $k\ln W$ with the Clausius entropy, checked there for one process ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]), holds for every quasi-static process. The thermodynamic side of each statement is proved in Chapter B2 from the level-A second law: the Clausius entropy as a state function of every system ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]), the fundamental relation $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$ ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]; with $\mu\,dN$, [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]]) and $dS \ge dQ/T$ ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]). Statements 1–3 reach the same results from counting, so $k\ln\Omega$ and the Clausius entropy are one function.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.4, eqs. (3.45)–(3.51); §3.5, eqs. (3.57)–(3.60) · Schwartz, Lecture 7, eqs. (44)–(45), (53)–(55); Lecture 5, §2.3; Lecture 6, §1*

^thm-b6-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) The total differential of $S(U, V, N)$ is $dS = (\partial S/\partial U)\,dU + (\partial S/\partial V)\,dV + (\partial S/\partial N)\,dN$ ([[§4 Partial Derivatives#^def-4-1|452 Def. §4.1]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]); insert Defs. [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|§B6.2.1]]–[[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-3|§B6.2.3]] and solve for $dU$. Reading off coefficients of $dS$, $dV$, $dN$ gives the three partial derivatives of $U(S, V, N)$.
>
> (2) For a quasi-static step at fixed $N$ the first law reads $dU = dQ - p\,dV$ ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]), with $p$ the mechanical pressure, which is the $p$ of (1) by [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-5|Theorem §B6.2.5]]. Comparing with (1) at $dN = 0$ gives $dQ = T\,dS$. Integrating along a reversible path gives $\Delta S = \int dQ/T$, so $S$ and the Clausius entropy have the same differences between all equilibrium states. With $dQ = 0$, $dS = 0$.
>
> (3) The identity (1) relates *equilibrium states* and holds however the step was made. If the work done on the system is $dW_{\text{on}} > -p\,dV$, the first law $dU = dQ + dW_{\text{on}}$ gives $T\,dS = dU + p\,dV = dQ + (dW_{\text{on}} + p\,dV) > dQ$. In a free expansion $dQ = 0$ and $dU = 0$, so $T\,dS = p\,dV > 0$.

^der-b6-2-7

*Uses:* [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-2|Def. §B6.2.2]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-3|Def. §B6.2.3]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-5|Theorem §B6.2.5]], [[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]], [[§4 Partial Derivatives#^def-4-1|452 Def. §4.1]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: $dQ = T\,dS$ only under the stated conditions
> $dQ = T\,dS$ and $dW = p\,dV$ hold separately only for quasi-static changes with no other work and fixed particle numbers; the identity $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV + \mu\,dN$ holds for any change between equilibrium states, because it involves only state functions. Schroeder's own picture of entropy as a fluid carried by heat, $Q/T$ per unit of heat, is a useful bookkeeping device that he warns is "simply wrong" as physics: entropy can be created, as in (3) and in every heat flow across a temperature difference ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^ex-a4-5-2|Example §A4.5.2]]). The general inequality $\oint dQ/T \le 0$ is the Clausius inequality ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]).

^cau-b6-2-2

## The monatomic ideal gas

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.8: The Monatomic Ideal Gas from Its Multiplicity
> For the multiplicity of [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]], to leading order in $N$,
>
> $$
> S(U, V, N) = Nk\Bigl[\ln\Bigl(\frac VN\Bigl(\frac{4\pi mU}{3Nh^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}\Bigr) + \frac52\Bigr],
> $$
>
> the Sackur–Tetrode equation, reached here by counting states; its home, with the derivation from the partition function and its range of validity, is [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-5|Theorem §B8.1.5]]. Its derivatives give
>
> $$
> U = \tfrac32 NkT, \qquad pV = NkT, \qquad \mu = -kT\ln\Bigl[\frac VN\Bigl(\frac{2\pi mkT}{h^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}\Bigr] = -kT\ln\frac{n_Q}{n},
> $$
>
> with $n = N/V$ and the quantum concentration $n_Q = (2\pi mkT/h^2)^{3/2}$ ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]]); this is the monatomic case of [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]]. The ideal gas law, an empirical law at level A ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]), is now a theorem: derived here from counting states, and from the partition function in [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-4|Theorem §B8.1.4]], its home ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^rem-b8-1-4|Remark: The ideal gas law changes layer]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.6, eq. (2.49); §3.1, eq. (3.13); §3.4, eqs. (3.40)–(3.43); §3.5, eq. (3.63) · Schwartz, Lecture 4, eqs. (17)–(20); Lecture 7, eq. (42)*

^thm-b6-2-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Entropy.* Take the logarithm of [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]] and apply Stirling's approximation ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]) to $\ln N! \approx N\ln N - N$ and to $\ln\Gamma(\tfrac{3N}{2}) = \ln\bigl((\tfrac{3N}{2})!\bigr) - \ln\tfrac{3N}{2} \approx \tfrac{3N}{2}\ln\tfrac{3N}{2} - \tfrac{3N}{2}$, dropping all terms of order $\ln N$ (including $\ln(\delta U/U)$):
>
> $$
> \ln\Omega \approx N\ln V - N\ln N + N + \tfrac{3N}{2}\ln\frac{2\pi mU}{h^2} - \tfrac{3N}{2}\ln\frac{3N}{2} + \tfrac{3N}{2} = N\Bigl[\ln\frac VN + \tfrac32\ln\frac{4\pi mU}{3Nh^2} + \tfrac52\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Temperature and pressure.* $\partial S/\partial U = \tfrac32 Nk/U = 1/T$, and $\partial S/\partial V = Nk/V = p/T$ (Defs. [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|§B6.2.1]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-2|§B6.2.2]]).
>
> *Chemical potential.* $N$ appears in front and, through $\ln(V/N)$ and $\ln(U/N)^{3/2}$, inside the bracket; the inner terms contribute $Nk\,(-1/N - \tfrac32/N) = -\tfrac52 k$, which cancels the $\tfrac52 k$ from the outer factor:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial S}{\partial N} = k\ln\Bigl[\frac VN\Bigl(\frac{4\pi mU}{3Nh^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> With $U = \tfrac32 NkT$, $4\pi mU/3N = 2\pi mkT$, and $\mu = -T\,\partial S/\partial N$ gives the formula.

^der-b6-2-8

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-2|Def. §B6.2.2]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-3|Def. §B6.2.3]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the ideal-gas formulas say
> - At fixed $U$ and $N$, $S = Nk\ln V + \text{const}$: an expansion from $V_i$ to $V_f$ raises $S$ by $Nk\ln(V_f/V_i)$, whether by a quasi-static isotherm (entropy brought in with heat) or a free expansion (entropy created on the spot). This is [[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]] again, now from the full multiplicity.
> - $\mu < 0$ when $n \ll n_Q$, the classical regime; raising the density at fixed $T$ raises $\mu$, so a denser gas is readier to give up particles. As $T \to 0$ at fixed density the formula for $S$ goes negative, which is impossible for $k\ln\Omega$: the classical counting fails when $n$ approaches $n_Q$ (Schroeder, Problem 2.35; [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-1|Def. §B10.2.1]]).
> - The $N$ under $V$ comes from the $1/N!$ for identical atoms; without it $S$ would not be extensive ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-7|Theorem §B6.3.7]]).

## The two-state paramagnet and negative temperature

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.9: Thermodynamics of the Two-State Paramagnet
> For the paramagnet of [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^mod-b6-1-3|Model §B6.1.3]] with $N$, $N_\uparrow$, $N_\downarrow$ all large,
>
> $$
> \frac Sk = N\ln N - N_\uparrow\ln N_\uparrow - N_\downarrow\ln N_\downarrow, \qquad \frac1T = \frac{k}{2\mu B}\ln\frac{N - U/\mu B}{N + U/\mu B} .
> $$
>
> Solved for the energy and magnetization, with $x = \mu B/kT$:
>
> $$
> U = -N\mu B\tanh x, \qquad M = N\mu\tanh x, \qquad C_B = \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{N, B} = Nk\,\frac{x^2}{\cosh^2 x} .
> $$
>
> $T$ and $U$ have opposite signs: $U < 0$ (more dipoles up) for $T > 0$, $U = 0$ (half up) for $T = \pm\infty$, and $U > 0$ (more dipoles down) for $T < 0$. The heat capacity vanishes at both low and high $|T|$. For $\mu B \ll kT$, $M \approx N\mu^2B/kT$ and $C_B \approx Nk(\mu B/kT)^2$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.3, eqs. (3.28)–(3.35)*

^thm-b6-2-9

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Entropy.* Apply Stirling's approximation to $\ln\Omega = \ln N! - \ln N_\uparrow! - \ln N_\downarrow!$ ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]); the linear terms $-N + N_\uparrow + N_\downarrow$ cancel.
>
> *Temperature.* $N_\uparrow = \tfrac12(N - U/\mu B)$, so $\partial N_\uparrow/\partial U = -1/2\mu B$, and with $N_\downarrow = N - N_\uparrow$, $\partial(S/k)/\partial N_\uparrow = -\ln N_\uparrow - 1 + \ln N_\downarrow + 1 = \ln(N_\downarrow/N_\uparrow)$. By the chain rule ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]])
>
> $$
> \frac1T = \frac{\partial N_\uparrow}{\partial U}\,\frac{\partial S}{\partial N_\uparrow} = \frac{k}{2\mu B}\ln\frac{N_\uparrow}{N_\downarrow} = \frac{k}{2\mu B}\ln\frac{N - U/\mu B}{N + U/\mu B} .
> $$
>
> *Inversion.* With $u = U/\mu B$, $e^{2x} = (N - u)/(N + u)$, so $u = N(1 - e^{2x})/(1 + e^{2x}) = -N\tanh x$. Then $M = -U/B$ ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^mod-b6-1-3|Model §B6.1.3]]). Differentiating, $dU/dT = -N\mu B\,\cosh^{-2}x\cdot dx/dT$ with $dx/dT = -\mu B/kT^2$, which gives $C_B$. The logarithm is positive exactly when $N_\uparrow > N_\downarrow$, that is $U < 0$. For small $x$, $\tanh x \approx x$ and $\cosh x \approx 1$.

^der-b6-2-9

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^mod-b6-1-3|Model §B6.1.3]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]

> [!law] Law §B6.2.10: Curie's Law
> The magnetization of a paramagnet in a field $B$ at temperature $T$ is proportional to $B/T$:
>
> $$
> M \propto \frac BT .
> $$
>
> *Valid when: $\mu B \ll kT$ (weak field or high temperature) and the magnetic moments interact negligibly with each other; it fails as the magnetization approaches saturation.*
> *Source: Schroeder §3.3, eq. (3.35) and Figure 3.12 (found experimentally by Pierre Curie; the organic radical DPPH follows it from 300 K down to a few kelvin)*

^law-b6-2-10

> [!remark] Remark: Curie's law derived
> For the two-state paramagnet, [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-9|Theorem §B6.2.9]] gives Curie's law with the constant: $M = N\mu^2B/kT$. Schroeder notes that the law holds at high temperature for all ideal paramagnets, including those with more than two states per dipole (PHY 306, Problem Set 9, treats that case with the partition function). At low $T$ the data for DPPH leave the straight line and follow $\tanh(\mu B/kT)$ to saturation.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.11: Negative Temperatures Are Hotter than All Positive Ones
> 1. $T < 0$ exactly where $S(U)$ decreases. This needs a system whose energy is bounded above and which is isolated from any unbounded degrees of freedom: a system with kinetic (quadratic) degrees of freedom, $\Omega \propto U^{N_f/2}$, has $\Omega$ increasing in $U$ and $T > 0$.
> 2. A system at negative temperature, in thermal contact with any system at positive temperature, gives energy to it.
>
> Hence the order of increasing hotness is the order of decreasing $1/T$:
>
> $$
> T = +0,\ \dots,\ +300\ \mathrm K,\ \dots,\ +\infty \;=\; -\infty,\ \dots,\ -300\ \mathrm K,\ \dots,\ -0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.3 · Schwartz, Lecture 4, §2 ($\beta$ as the defining variable)*

^thm-b6-2-11

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) $1/T = \partial S/\partial U$ is negative exactly where $S$ decreases. If the energy has a maximum, the multiplicity falls back towards the few states at the top (for the paramagnet, $\Omega = 1$ when all dipoles point down), so $S$ must decrease somewhere. With unbounded quadratic degrees of freedom, $\Omega \propto U^{N_f/2}$ grows without limit and $1/T > 0$ ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-3|Theorem §B6.2.3]]). (2) By [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]], energy flows into the system with the larger $1/T$; a negative $1/T$ is smaller than every positive one.

^der-b6-2-11

*Uses:* [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-3|Theorem §B6.2.3]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-9|Theorem §B6.2.9]]

> [!caution] Caution: What negative temperature is not
> - It is not colder than absolute zero; it is hotter than infinity. The coldest states are $T \to +0$ (all dipoles up), the hottest $T \to -0$ (all down).
> - It needs a subsystem whose energy is bounded and which is isolated from unbounded degrees of freedom long enough to equilibrate internally. In LiF the nuclear spins reach equilibrium among themselves in about $10^{-5}\ \mathrm s$ but take about five minutes to exchange energy with the lattice vibrations; on the short time scale the spins alone are the system (Purcell and Pound, 1951; [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^ex-b6-2-3|Example §B6.2.3]]). Afterwards spins and lattice share one positive temperature.
> - The heat capacity $C_B = Nk\,x^2/\cosh^2x$ is positive on both branches: adding energy moves the temperature from $-\infty$ towards $-0$.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B6.2.1: The Einstein Solid at Any Temperature
> Schroeder's five steps (multiplicity, entropy, temperature, $U(T)$, heat capacity) for an Einstein solid with $N$ oscillators and $q$ units of size $\epsilon$, both large but in any ratio. By [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-7|Theorem §B6.1.7]], $S/k = (q + N)\ln(q + N) - q\ln q - N\ln N$, so
>
> $$
> \frac1T = \frac1\epsilon\frac{\partial S}{\partial q} = \frac k\epsilon\ln\frac{q + N}{q} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad U = q\epsilon = \frac{N\epsilon}{e^{\epsilon/kT} - 1}, \qquad C = \frac{dU}{dT} = Nk\Bigl(\frac{\epsilon}{kT}\Bigr)^2\frac{e^{\epsilon/kT}}{(e^{\epsilon/kT} - 1)^2} .
> $$
>
> - *High temperature*, $kT \gg \epsilon$: with $e^{y} \approx 1 + y + \tfrac12 y^2$, $U \approx NkT - \tfrac12 N\epsilon$ and $C \to Nk$, that is $3k$ per atom: the law of Dulong and Petit ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-3|Theorem §A2.3.3]]) and [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-3|Theorem §B6.2.3]](1).
> - *Low temperature*, $kT \ll \epsilon$: $U \approx N\epsilon\,e^{-\epsilon/kT}$ and $C \approx Nk(\epsilon/kT)^2e^{-\epsilon/kT} \to 0$. The oscillators "freeze out": a heat capacity that vanishes as $T \to 0$ is what the third law requires ([[§B2.3 The Third Law#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]]).
>
> Real solids do have $C \to 0$, but as $T^3$ rather than exponentially; the Debye model ([[§B11.2 Phonons and the Debye Model#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]]) repairs the Einstein model's single frequency. The same heat capacity from the partition function, the Einstein solid's home, is [[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^thm-b8-2-6|Theorem §B8.2.6]].
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.2 (the five-step procedure); Problems 3.5, 3.8 and 3.25 (solved here)*

^ex-b6-2-1

> [!example] Example §B6.2.2: The Chemical Potential of Helium
> Helium at $T = 300\ \mathrm K$ and $p = 1.0 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$ ($m = 4.00\ \mathrm u = 6.65 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$). The volume per atom is $V/N = kT/p = 4.14 \times 10^{-26}\ \mathrm{m^3}$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{n_Q} = \Bigl(\frac{h^2}{2\pi mkT}\Bigr)^{3/2} = 1.28 \times 10^{-31}\ \mathrm{m^3}, \qquad \frac{n_Q}{n} = \frac{4.14 \times 10^{-26}}{1.28 \times 10^{-31}} = 3.2 \times 10^5, \qquad \ln\frac{n_Q}{n} = 12.7 .
> $$
>
> By [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-8|Theorem §B6.2.8]], $\mu = -kT \times 12.7 = -(0.0259\ \mathrm{eV})(12.7) = -0.33\ \mathrm{eV}$, and the molar entropy is $S = R(12.7 + 2.5) = 126\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}$. For argon, ten times heavier, $n_Q$ is $10^{3/2}$ times larger: $\ln(n_Q/n) = 16.1$, $\mu = -0.42\ \mathrm{eV}$, $S = 155\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}$. At fixed $T$ and $p$ the heavier gas has the lower chemical potential, and compressing either gas raises its $\mu$ by $kT\ln(n_2/n_1)$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.6, eq. (2.50); §3.5, eq. (3.64); Problem 2.33 (argon, solved here)*

^ex-b6-2-2

> [!example] Example §B6.2.3: Making a Negative Temperature
> Purcell and Pound used the lithium nuclei in a LiF crystal, in a field $B = 0.63\ \mathrm T$ at room temperature, $300\ \mathrm K$. Treat each nucleus as a two-state dipole (it really has four states) with $\mu = 5 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{eV/T}$. Then $x = \mu B/kT = (3.2 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{eV})/(0.0259\ \mathrm{eV}) = 1.2 \times 10^{-6}$, and by [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-9|Theorem §B6.2.9]] $M/N\mu = \tanh x \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-6}$: about one nucleus in a million more points up than down.
>
> Now reverse the field suddenly. The spins have no time to flip, so the same tiny excess now points *against* the field: $U > 0$, and by the theorem the spin system is at $T \approx -300\ \mathrm K$. It remained so for minutes, until contact with the lattice brought it back to $+300\ \mathrm K$, giving up energy as [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-11|Theorem §B6.2.11]] requires. The tiny magnetization was detected by radio-frequency absorption and emission: flipping one nucleus costs $2\mu B = 6.3 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{eV}$, a photon of frequency $2\mu B/h = 15\ \mathrm{MHz}$ and wavelength $20\ \mathrm m$. With more nuclei in the lower state the sample absorbs such photons on balance; at negative temperature, with more in the upper state, it emits them.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.3 and Problem 3.21 (solved here)*

^ex-b6-2-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Each equilibrium condition is the vanishing of a partial derivative of $S_{\text{tot}}$ at an interior maximum ([[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-1|452 Thm. §14.1]]); the identification of the statistical with the mechanical pressure is a change of variables by the chain rule ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]), the triple-product rule of [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]].
> - Inverting $S(U, V, N)$ to $U(S, V, N)$ makes $U$ the first of the thermodynamic potentials, with natural variables $S, V, N$ ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]]); the chemical potential's thermodynamic home is [[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]].
> - The same maximum-entropy reasoning with a reservoir in place of system $B$ gives the Boltzmann factor $e^{-\epsilon/kT}$ ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]]); the paramagnet and the Einstein solid are redone there more easily.
