Why does a large system settle into one macroscopic state and stay there? Level A counted the arrangements behind a macrostate ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^def-a4-6-1|Def. §A4.6.1]]), *postulated* Boltzmann's formula ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-1|Principle §A4.6.1]]) and checked it against the Clausius entropy for one process ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]). Level B replaces the postulated formula by one sharper assumption about probabilities, the fundamental postulate, and *derives* what level A asserted: multiplicities of interacting systems are so sharply peaked that a macroscopic system is found in the macrostate of greatest multiplicity, with relative fluctuations of order $N^{-1/2}$. That is the second law as a statement of overwhelming probability. Entropy becomes a definition, $S = k\ln\Omega$; that it agrees with the Clausius entropy is a theorem of the next section.

*Notation.* $U$ is the internal energy (level A writes $E_{\text{int}}$, Schwartz $E$); $k = k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant; the multiplicity is $\Omega$ (level A writes $W$).

## Microstates and macrostates

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.1: Microstate; Macrostate; Accessible Microstate
> A **microstate** of a system is a complete specification of its microscopic state: in quantum mechanics, one stationary state of the whole system; classically, one cell of phase space, that is, the positions and momenta of all particles to a fixed resolution. A **macrostate** is specified by a few macroscopic variables: for an isolated system its energy $U$, volume $V$ and particle number $N$, together with the values of any internal constraints, such as the energies of two parts separated by a slowly conducting wall. A microstate is **accessible** if it is compatible with the constraints (conservation laws, walls, applied fields) and can be reached from the others on the time scale considered.
>
> This sharpens [[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^def-a4-6-1|Def. §A4.6.1]]: microstates must now be counted by a definite rule, and what is "accessible", like what is a macrostate, depends on the time scale.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.1, §2.3 · Schwartz, Lecture 3, §6 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §25*

^def-b6-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.2: Multiplicity
> The **multiplicity** $\Omega(U, V, N)$ of an isolated system is the number of its accessible microstates with energy $U$ (in a narrow range $U$ to $U + \delta U$ when the levels are dense), volume $V$ and $N$ particles. The multiplicity $\Omega(M)$ of a macrostate $M$ of a larger set of variables is the number of accessible microstates that realise it.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.1 · Schwartz, Lecture 4, §2*

^def-b6-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Cells and shells
> A classical phase space is a continuum, so counting needs a cell size. Schwartz keeps an arbitrary resolution $\Delta q\,\Delta p$ per coordinate pair, which drops out of every entropy *difference*. Quantum mechanics fixes it: by the uncertainty relation ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|QM Theorem §A3.5.1]]) a region of length $L$ and momentum range $L_p$ holds about $LL_p/h$ independent states, so one state occupies a phase-space volume $h$ per coordinate pair, $h^{3N}$ for $N$ particles in three dimensions (Schroeder's heuristic; the count from the states of a particle in a box is [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]], with [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^rem-b8-1-2|Remark: Why h³, and why it does not matter]]). Likewise the energy shell $\delta U$ is arbitrary; it enters $\Omega$ only as a factor $\delta U/U$ ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]]), whose logarithm is negligible against $\ln\Omega \sim N$.

## The fundamental postulate

> [!principle] Principle §B6.1.1: Fundamental Postulate of Statistical Mechanics
> In an isolated system in equilibrium, all accessible microstates are equally probable:
>
> $$
> P_s = \frac{1}{\Omega(U, V, N)} \qquad \text{for every accessible microstate } s .
> $$
>
> *Domain: an isolated system (fixed $U$, $V$, $N$) that has had time to equilibrate; "accessible" on the time scale considered; classical systems counted in phase-space cells of fixed size. The postulate concerns microstates: macrostates are not equally probable ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]]).*
> *Source: Schroeder §2.3 ("fundamental assumption of statistical mechanics") · Schwartz, Lecture 3, §1 and §6 ("postulate of equal a priori probabilities") · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §26*

^pr-b6-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.3: Microcanonical Ensemble
> The **microcanonical ensemble** of an isolated system is the probability distribution of [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]] on its microstates: $P_s = 1/\Omega$ for each accessible microstate with energy between $U$ and $U + \delta U$, and $P_s = 0$ otherwise. A macroscopic quantity $O$ is predicted by its ensemble average $\langle O\rangle = \sum_s P_s O_s$, an average over imagined copies of the system rather than over time.
>
> *Source: the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §27.1–27.2 · Schwartz, Lecture 3, §5*

^def-b6-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.2: Probability of a Macrostate
> In the microcanonical ensemble the probability of a macrostate $M$ is its share of the accessible microstates:
>
> $$
> P(M) = \frac{\Omega(M)}{\Omega_{\text{all}}}, \qquad \Omega_{\text{all}} = \sum_{M} \Omega(M) .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.1, eq. (2.1), and §2.3 · Schwartz, Lecture 4, eq. (4)*

^thm-b6-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The macrostates split the accessible microstates into disjoint classes, and every microstate belongs to exactly one, so $\Omega_{\text{all}} = \sum_M \Omega(M)$. Probabilities of disjoint events add; by [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]] each of the $\Omega(M)$ microstates of $M$ has probability $1/\Omega_{\text{all}}$.

^der-b6-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-2|Def. §B6.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why equal probabilities? Ergodicity and its limits
> The postulate is not proved; the sources give reasons to believe it and say where it is fragile. The dynamics behind each reason is developed in [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem|§B5.4]].
> - *Microscopic reversibility.* Any transition $X \to Y$ between microstates can run backwards, $Y \to X$, equally easily, so the dynamics has no reason to prefer one microstate (Schroeder). With symmetric transition rates the master equation drives every initial distribution to equal probabilities on the connected states ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-7|Theorem §B5.4.7]]; the H-theorem, [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-6|Theorem §B5.4.6]]).
> - *Consistency with the dynamics.* By Liouville's equation ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-4|CM Theorem §B7.2.4]]) a phase-space density that depends on the coordinates only through the energy, $\rho = f(H)$, has $\{\rho, H\} = 0$ and does not change in time. The microcanonical distribution is such a density, so equal probabilities on the energy shell are *stationary* ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-1|Theorem §B5.4.1]]). The same theorem says that exact Hamiltonian evolution never changes the fine-grained entropy: equal probabilities are *approached* only for coarse-grained probabilities ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^def-b5-4-2|Def. §B5.4.2]]). Schwartz calls the postulate "really a theorem, to the extent that Boltzmann's H theorem is a theorem".
> - *The ergodic hypothesis* (Schwartz, Lecture 3, §5; [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^rem-b5-4-3|Remark: Ergodicity]]): a system is **ergodic** if the average over all accessible states equals the time average along one trajectory. Many systems are not strictly ergodic (a billiard ball on a circular table, unlike one on a cardioid table). And even for an ergodic gas the time to visit the $6N$-dimensional phase space is absurdly long ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^ex-b6-1-3|Example §B6.1.3]]). What rescues the method is coarse graining: macroscopic quantities depend on the distribution of *one* particle's position and momentum, and each of the $10^{24}$ molecules samples that six-dimensional space quickly.
>
> Level B keeps the postulate as a principle, the one assumption from which the rest of the microcanonical theory follows.

^rem-b6-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Microstates, not macrostates; and "accessible" has a time scale
> - The postulate makes *microstates* equally likely. Macrostates have probabilities proportional to their multiplicities, which differ enormously; confusing the two is the commonest error.
> - Which microstates count as accessible depends on how long one waits. Over laboratory times the molecular orientations in ice or solid CO stay frozen in random arrangements; the true ordered ground state is not reached, which leaves a residual entropy (Schroeder §3.2). Likewise two subsystems separated by an insulating wall have separately fixed energies until the wall conducts.

## Three model systems

> [!model] Model §B6.1.3: Two-State Paramagnet
> $N$ magnetic dipoles sit at fixed lattice sites in a uniform field $\vec B$ along $+z$. Each dipole's moment along $z$ is either $+\mu$ ("up") or $-\mu$ ("down"), with energy $-\mu B$ or $+\mu B$ ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-3|EM Theorem §A7.4.3]]). Assumptions:
> - the dipoles do not interact, except weakly enough to exchange energy (an **ideal paramagnet**);
> - they are distinguishable by their sites;
> - $N$ is fixed.
>
> A macrostate is fixed by the number $N_\uparrow$ of up dipoles ($N_\downarrow = N - N_\uparrow$), equivalently by the energy and the magnetization
>
> $$
> U = \mu B\,(N_\downarrow - N_\uparrow) = \mu B\,(N - 2N_\uparrow), \qquad M = \mu\,(N_\uparrow - N_\downarrow) = -\frac{U}{B} .
> $$
>
> Two states per dipole occur for spin-$\tfrac12$ particles ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^pr-a5-2-6|QM Principle §A5.2.6]]); for an electron $\mu$ is the Bohr magneton ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^def-a5-2-1|QM Def. §A5.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.1, §3.3, eqs. (3.25)–(3.26)*

^mod-b6-1-3

> [!model] Model §B6.1.4: Einstein Solid
> $N$ identical quantum harmonic oscillators of one frequency $f$, each with levels $0, \epsilon, 2\epsilon, \dots$, where $\epsilon = hf$ and energies are measured from the ground state (the zero-point energy $\tfrac12 hf$ of each oscillator, [[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|QM Theorem §A4.5.3]], is fixed and plays no part in exchanges). Assumptions:
> - the oscillators interact strongly enough to exchange energy but too weakly to shift their levels;
> - they are distinguishable (each belongs to an atom at a definite lattice site).
>
> A macrostate is fixed by $N$ and the number $q$ of energy units, $U = q\epsilon$. A crystal of $N/3$ atoms, each vibrating in three directions, is an Einstein solid of $N$ oscillators.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.2, and the footnote to §2.3*

^mod-b6-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.5: Multiplicities of the Paramagnet and the Einstein Solid
> The two-state paramagnet with $N_\uparrow$ of $N$ dipoles up and the Einstein solid with $N$ oscillators and $q$ energy units have
>
> $$
> \Omega_{\text{para}}(N, N_\uparrow) = \binom{N}{N_\uparrow} = \frac{N!}{N_\uparrow!\,N_\downarrow!}, \qquad \Omega_{\text{E}}(N, q) = \binom{q + N - 1}{q} = \frac{(q + N - 1)!}{q!\,(N - 1)!} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.1, eq. (2.7); §2.2, eq. (2.9)*

^thm-b6-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Paramagnet.* A microstate says which dipoles are up; the macrostate fixes how many. The number of ways to choose the $N_\uparrow$ up dipoles among $N$ distinguishable sites is $\binom{N}{N_\uparrow}$.
>
> *Einstein solid.* Write a microstate as a row of $q$ dots (energy units) and $N - 1$ bars (walls between consecutive oscillators): $\bullet\,|\,\bullet\bullet\bullet\,|\,|\,\bullet\bullet\bullet\bullet$ means oscillator 1 has one unit, oscillator 2 three, oscillator 3 none, oscillator 4 four. Every microstate gives exactly one such row and every row of $q$ dots and $N - 1$ bars gives a microstate, so the microstates are counted by the rows: choose which $q$ of the $q + N - 1$ positions hold dots.

^der-b6-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^mod-b6-1-3|Model §B6.1.3]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^mod-b6-1-4|Model §B6.1.4]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-2|Def. §B6.1.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.6: Multiplicity of a Monatomic Ideal Gas
> Take $N$ identical atoms of mass $m$ in a volume $V$ with no interaction energy ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]]). Assume one quantum state per phase-space volume $h^{3N}$ ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^rem-b6-1-1|Remark: Cells and shells]]) and divide by the $N!$ permutations of identical atoms, which is correct when the gas is dilute enough that no two atoms share a one-particle state ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^def-b6-3-3|Def. §B6.3.3]]). Then the number of microstates with energy between $U$ and $U + \delta U$ is
>
> $$
> \Omega(U, V, N) = \frac{V^N}{N!}\Bigl(\frac{2\pi mU}{h^2}\Bigr)^{3N/2}\frac{1}{\Gamma(3N/2)}\,\frac{\delta U}{U}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \Omega = f(N)\,V^N\,U^{3N/2}
> $$
>
> up to the factor $\delta U/U$, with $f(N)$ depending on $N$ only. Here $\Gamma$ is the Gamma function ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-5|Def. §B4.1.5]]), with $\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!$ for integers $n \ge 1$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.5, eqs. (2.39)–(2.41) · Schwartz, Lecture 3, §6, eqs. (27)–(39)*

^thm-b6-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Positions.* With no interaction energy each atom may be anywhere in the box, independently of the others: position-space volume $V^N$.
>
> *Momenta.* The energy is all kinetic, $U = \sum_{j=1}^{3N} p_j^2/2m$ over the $3N$ momentum components, so the momenta lie on the sphere of radius $R = \sqrt{2mU}$ in $d = 3N$ dimensions. The shell from $U$ to $U + \delta U$ has radial thickness $\delta R = (dR/dU)\,\delta U = R\,\delta U/2U$ and volume $A_d R^{d-1}\delta R$, where $A_d$ is the area of the unit sphere in $\mathbb R^d$.
>
> *The area $A_d$.* Integrate $e^{-|\vec p|^2}$ over $\mathbb R^d$ in two ways. As a product of $d$ one-dimensional Gaussian integrals ([[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^ex-25-4|452 Ex. §25.4]]) it is $\pi^{d/2}$. In spherical shells it is $A_d\int_0^\infty r^{d-1}e^{-r^2}dr = \tfrac12 A_d\int_0^\infty t^{d/2-1}e^{-t}dt = \tfrac12 A_d\,\Gamma(d/2)$, with $t = r^2$ (an improper integral, [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-1|451 Def. §36.1]]). Hence
>
> $$
> A_d = \frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)} ;
> $$
>
> check: $A_2 = 2\pi$ and $A_3 = 2\pi^{3/2}/\Gamma(\tfrac32) = 4\pi$, using $\Gamma(\tfrac32) = \tfrac12\sqrt\pi$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]).
>
> *Counting.* Divide the phase-space volume by $h^{3N}$ and by $N!$:
>
> $$
> \Omega = \frac{1}{N!}\,\frac{V^N}{h^{3N}}\,\frac{2\pi^{3N/2}}{\Gamma(3N/2)}\,(2mU)^{(3N-1)/2}\,\sqrt{2mU}\,\frac{\delta U}{2U} = \frac{V^N}{N!}\Bigl(\frac{2\pi mU}{h^2}\Bigr)^{3N/2}\frac{1}{\Gamma(3N/2)}\,\frac{\delta U}{U} .
> $$
>
> Schroeder writes $(3N/2)!$ for $\Gamma(3N/2)$ and drops $\delta U/U$; the two differ by factors whose logarithms are $O(\ln N)$, negligible in $\ln\Omega$ ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^rem-b6-1-4|Remark: Large and very large numbers]]).

^der-b6-1-6

*Uses:* [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^rem-b6-1-1|Remark: Cells and shells]], [[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^ex-25-4|452 Ex. §25.4]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-1|451 Def. §36.1]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^def-b4-1-5|Def. §B4.1.5]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Multiplicity and degrees of freedom
> The exponent of $U$ in $\Omega_{\text{gas}} \propto U^{3N/2}$ is half the number of quadratic degrees of freedom ($3N$ momentum components). The Einstein solid at high energy behaves alike: $\Omega_{\text{E}} \approx (eq/N)^N \propto U^N$ ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-7|Theorem §B6.1.7]]), and each oscillator has two quadratic degrees of freedom. Schroeder quotes the general statement: for any system with only quadratic degrees of freedom and so much energy that quantization is unnoticeable, $\Omega \propto U^{N_f/2}$ with $N_f$ the number of degrees of freedom. This is the counting behind equipartition ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]; derived as a theorem in [[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]]).

^rem-b6-1-3

## Large numbers

> [!remark] Remark: Large and very large numbers
> Schroeder distinguishes *large* numbers, such as $N \sim 10^{23}$, from *very large* ones, such as $\Omega \sim 10^{10^{23}}$. Adding a small number to a large one changes nothing ($10^{23} + 23 \approx 10^{23}$), and multiplying a very large number by a large one changes nothing either: $10^{10^{23}} \times 10^{23} = 10^{10^{23} + 23} \approx 10^{10^{23}}$. Taking logarithms turns very large numbers into large ones, and then factors that are merely large (powers of $N$) contribute $O(\ln N)$ to $\ln\Omega = O(N)$, which is negligible. The workhorse is Stirling's approximation, $\ln N! = N\ln N - N + O(\ln N)$ ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]).

^rem-b6-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.7: Multiplicity of a Large Einstein Solid
> For $N, q \gg 1$,
>
> $$
> \ln\Omega_{\text{E}}(N, q) = (q + N)\ln(q + N) - q\ln q - N\ln N + O\bigl(\ln(qN)\bigr), \qquad \Omega_{\text{E}} \approx \Bigl(\frac{q + N}{q}\Bigr)^{q}\Bigl(\frac{q + N}{N}\Bigr)^{N} .
> $$
>
> In the high-temperature limit $q \gg N$ (many units per oscillator) and the low-temperature limit $q \ll N$,
>
> $$
> \Omega_{\text{E}} \approx \Bigl(\frac{eq}{N}\Bigr)^{N} \quad (q \gg N), \qquad \Omega_{\text{E}} \approx \Bigl(\frac{eN}{q}\Bigr)^{q} \quad (q \ll N) .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.4, eqs. (2.17)–(2.21); Problems 2.17–2.18*

^thm-b6-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> First, $\dfrac{(q + N - 1)!}{q!\,(N - 1)!} = \dfrac{N}{q + N}\cdot\dfrac{(q + N)!}{q!\,N!}$, and the prefactor lies between $1/(q + 1)$ and $1$, so its logarithm is $O(\ln q)$. Stirling's approximation applied to the three factorials gives
>
> $$
> \ln\Omega_{\text{E}} = \bigl[(q + N)\ln(q + N) - (q + N)\bigr] - \bigl[q\ln q - q\bigr] - \bigl[N\ln N - N\bigr] + O\bigl(\ln(qN)\bigr),
> $$
>
> and the linear terms cancel. Writing $(q + N)\ln(q + N) = q\ln(q + N) + N\ln(q + N)$ gives the product form. For $q \gg N$, $\ln(q + N) = \ln q + \ln(1 + N/q) = \ln q + N/q - N^2/2q^2 + O(N^3/q^3)$ by the series of the logarithm ([[§26 Differentiation and Integration of Power Series#^ex-26-4|451 Ex. §26.4]]), so $(q + N)\ln(q + N) = (q + N)\ln q + N + N^2/2q + O(N^3/q^2)$ and
>
> $$
> \ln\Omega_{\text{E}} \approx N\ln\frac qN + N + \frac{N^2}{2q} \approx N\ln\frac{eq}{N} ,
> $$
>
> the last term being negligible for $q \gg N$. (Schroeder, eq. (2.20), writes $N^2/q$ for this correction, because he keeps only the first-order term of the logarithm; it is dropped either way.) The formula is symmetric under $q \leftrightarrow N$, which gives the low-temperature limit.

## Interacting systems and the sharp peak

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.8: Two Weakly Coupled Systems
> Let systems $A$ and $B$, together isolated, exchange energy through a weak coupling: the exchange is slow compared with relaxation inside each system, and the coupling energy is negligible, so that $U_A + U_B = U$ is fixed. Label the macrostates of the composite system by $U_A$. Then
>
> $$
> \Omega(U_A) = \Omega_A(U_A)\,\Omega_B(U - U_A), \qquad \Omega_{\text{all}} = \sum_{U_A}\Omega_A(U_A)\,\Omega_B(U - U_A), \qquad P(U_A) = \frac{\Omega_A(U_A)\,\Omega_B(U - U_A)}{\Omega_{\text{all}}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.3 · Schwartz, Lecture 4, eqs. (3)–(4) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §26.1*

^thm-b6-1-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $U_A$ given, a microstate of the composite is a pair (microstate of $A$ with energy $U_A$, microstate of $B$ with energy $U - U_A$), and with negligible coupling energy every pair is allowed: for each of the $\Omega_A$ states of $A$ there are $\Omega_B$ states of $B$. Summing over the macrostates counts every accessible microstate once, and [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]] gives $P(U_A)$.

^der-b6-1-8

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-2|Def. §B6.1.2]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.9: Sharpness of the Multiplicity Peak
> In [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-8|Theorem §B6.1.8]] let $\Omega_A \propto U_A^{\alpha N_A}$ and $\Omega_B \propto U_B^{\alpha N_B}$ with a common $\alpha > 0$ ($\alpha = 1$ for Einstein solids with $q \gg N$, $\alpha = \tfrac32$ for monatomic ideal gases), and $N = N_A + N_B$. Then $\Omega(U_A)$ is largest when the energy per particle is shared equally,
>
> $$
> \frac{\bar U_A}{N_A} = \frac{\bar U_B}{N_B} = \frac UN ,
> $$
>
> and near the maximum it is a Gaussian,
>
> $$
> \Omega(U_A) \approx \Omega_{\max}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(U_A - \bar U_A)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr], \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{\bar U_A\,\bar U_B}{\alpha N}, \qquad \frac{\sigma}{\bar U_A} = \sqrt{\frac{N_B}{\alpha N N_A}} \sim N^{-1/2} .
> $$
>
> For two identical Einstein solids of $N$ oscillators each sharing $q \gg N$ units, with $q_A = q/2 + x$, this reads $\Omega = \Omega_{\max}\,e^{-N(2x/q)^2}$: the multiplicity falls to $1/e$ of its peak at $x = q/(2\sqrt N)$.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.4, eqs. (2.22)–(2.28); §2.5, eqs. (2.43)–(2.44) · Schwartz, Lecture 4, §2, eqs. (5)–(9)*

^thm-b6-1-9

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $g(U_A) = \ln\Omega(U_A) = \text{const} + \alpha N_A\ln U_A + \alpha N_B\ln(U - U_A)$. Then
>
> $$
> g'(U_A) = \frac{\alpha N_A}{U_A} - \frac{\alpha N_B}{U - U_A}, \qquad g''(U_A) = -\frac{\alpha N_A}{U_A^2} - \frac{\alpha N_B}{U_B^2} < 0 .
> $$
>
> $g' = 0$ gives $U_A/N_A = U_B/N_B$, so $\bar U_A = UN_A/N$ and $\bar U_B = UN_B/N$; since $g'' < 0$ this is the maximum. There
>
> $$
> -g''(\bar U_A) = \frac{\alpha N^2}{U^2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{N_A} + \frac{1}{N_B}\Bigr) = \frac{\alpha N^3}{U^2 N_A N_B} = \frac{\alpha N}{\bar U_A\bar U_B} \equiv \frac{1}{\sigma^2} .
> $$
>
> Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) to second order gives $g(U_A) = g(\bar U_A) - (U_A - \bar U_A)^2/2\sigma^2 + R_3$. The third derivative is of size $\alpha N/\bar U^3$, so within the peak, $|U_A - \bar U_A| \sim \sigma$, the remainder $R_3 \sim \alpha N\sigma^3/\bar U^3$ is a fraction $\sim\sigma/\bar U \sim N^{-1/2}$ of the quadratic term, and the Gaussian is accurate. Exponentiating gives the result. For identical solids, $\alpha = 1$, $N_A = N_B = N$ (so the total is $2N$) and $\bar U_A = \bar U_B = q/2$ in units of $\epsilon$: $2\sigma^2 = 2(q/2)^2/2N = q^2/4N$, and $e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2} = e^{-N(2x/q)^2}$.

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-8|Theorem §B6.1.8]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-7|Theorem §B6.1.7]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The peak as a central limit
> Schwartz reads the Gaussian as the central limit theorem at work ([[§B4.2 Random Walks, the Central Limit Theorem and Diffusion#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]]): $U_A$ is a sum of the energies of $N_A$ particles, each a draw from nearly the same distribution, so its fluctuations grow like $\sqrt{N_A}$ while its mean grows like $N_A$. For gases the same argument applies to every exchanged quantity: with volume exchange the peak in $V_A$ has width $\sim V_{\text{total}}/\sqrt N$, and with particle exchange the peak is at equal densities (Schroeder §2.5). The figure shows the narrowing, computed exactly from [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]] rather than from the Gaussian.

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.4: Thermodynamic Limit
> The **thermodynamic limit** is the limit of an infinitely large system, $N \to \infty$ and $V \to \infty$ at fixed density $n = N/V$ (and fixed energy per particle), keeping only the leading terms in $1/N$. In it the relative fluctuations about the most probable macrostate, of order $N^{-1/2}$, vanish, and the most probable values of macroscopic variables coincide with their mean values.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.4 · Schwartz, Lecture 5, §1*

^def-b6-1-4

## Entropy and the second law

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.5: Statistical Entropy
> The **entropy** of an isolated system with multiplicity $\Omega(U, V, N)$, and of a macrostate $M$, is
>
> $$
> S = k\ln\Omega, \qquad S(M) = k\ln\Omega(M), \qquad k = k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K} .
> $$
>
> The factor $k$ is historical and gives $S$ the units of the Clausius entropy, $\mathrm{J/K}$; $S/k = \ln\Omega$ is a pure number.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.6, eq. (2.45) · Schwartz, Lecture 4, eq. (15); Lecture 6, eq. (3)*

^def-b6-1-5

> [!remark] Remark: A principle becomes a definition (layer change)
> At level A, $S = k_B\ln W$ was a principle ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-1|Principle §A4.6.1]]): entropy was already defined by heat, $dS = dQ/T$ along reversible paths ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]), and the formula was a claim about that quantity, checked for one ideal-gas process ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]). At level B the logic is reversed. $S = k\ln\Omega$ is the *definition* of entropy, and the statements about it are theorems: that it is additive and tends to its maximum (below), that its derivatives are $1/T$, $p/T$ and $-\mu/T$, and that along every quasi-static path $dS = dQ/T$, so that it *is* the Clausius entropy up to an additive constant (Defs. [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-1|§B6.2.1]]–[[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^def-b6-2-3|§B6.2.3]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-7|Theorem §B6.2.7]]). The only assumption behind all of them is [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]], which is the condition written into the *Domain* of level A's principle (all microstates equally likely), now made the principle itself.

^rem-b6-1-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.10: Second Law as Overwhelming Probability
> Let an isolated system consist of two parts that exchange energy, with multiplicities as in [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-9|Theorem §B6.1.9]], and let $\mathcal M$ be the number of macrostates $U_A$. Under [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]:
> 1. **Equilibrium is the macrostate of greatest multiplicity.** For every $\varepsilon > 0$,
>
> $$
> P\bigl(|U_A - \bar U_A| \ge \varepsilon U\bigr) \le \mathcal M\,e^{-\alpha N\varepsilon^2/2} .
> $$
>
> Since $\mathcal M$ grows only like a power of $N$, relative deviations larger than about $\sqrt{(2\ln\mathcal M)/\alpha N} \to 0$ have exponentially small probability.
> 2. **Multiplicity tends to increase.** If an internal constraint is released (an insulating wall made conducting, a wall made movable or permeable), the set of accessible microstates can only grow, $\Omega_{\text{after}} \ge \Omega_{\text{before}}$, and with overwhelming probability the system is next found in macrostates near the new maximum, whose multiplicity is at least that of the old macrostate. In terms of entropy: $\Delta S \ge 0$, and a large system in equilibrium is found in the macrostate of greatest entropy, apart from fluctuations normally too small to measure.
>
> This is the second law of thermodynamics ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-5|Theorem §A4.5.5]]) derived as a statement of probability, not of certainty.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.3 ("law of increase of multiplicity"), §2.6 · Schwartz, Lecture 4, §2; Lecture 5, §2*

^thm-b6-1-10

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) Use $g = \ln\Omega(U_A)$ from the derivation of [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-9|Theorem §B6.1.9]]. Since $U_A, U_B \le U$, everywhere
>
> $$
> g''(U_A) = -\frac{\alpha N_A}{U_A^2} - \frac{\alpha N_B}{U_B^2} \le -\frac{\alpha N_A}{U^2} - \frac{\alpha N_B}{U^2} = -\frac{\alpha N}{U^2} .
> $$
>
> Taylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder about the maximum, where $g' = 0$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), gives for some $\xi$ between $\bar U_A$ and $U_A$
>
> $$
> g(U_A) = g(\bar U_A) + \tfrac12 g''(\xi)(U_A - \bar U_A)^2 \le g(\bar U_A) - \frac{\alpha N}{2}\Bigl(\frac{U_A - \bar U_A}{U}\Bigr)^2 .
> $$
>
> So every macrostate with $|U_A - \bar U_A| \ge \varepsilon U$ has $\Omega(U_A) \le \Omega_{\max}e^{-\alpha N\varepsilon^2/2}$. There are at most $\mathcal M$ of them, and $\Omega_{\text{all}} \ge \Omega_{\max}$; by [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-8|Theorem §B6.1.8]] their total probability is at most $\mathcal M\Omega_{\max}e^{-\alpha N\varepsilon^2/2}/\Omega_{\max}$.
>
> (2) Releasing a constraint removes a restriction on the microstates, so every microstate accessible before remains accessible: $\Omega_{\text{after}} \ge \Omega_{\text{before}}$. The system now equilibrates over the larger set; by (1) it is then found near $\bar U_A$, and $\Omega(\bar U_A) = \Omega_{\max}$ is at least the multiplicity of any macrostate, the initial one included. Taking $k\ln$ of these inequalities gives the entropy form, since $\ln$ is increasing.

> [!remark] Remark: What has been derived, and what has not
> - The direction of time enters only through the initial condition: a system prepared in an improbable macrostate (all the energy in solid $B$) moves to probable ones because there are overwhelmingly more of them. Nothing in the microscopic laws forbids the reverse; it is merely never seen ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^ex-a4-6-1|Example §A4.6.1]]). Schroeder: irreversible processes "are not inevitable, they are just overwhelmingly probable".
> - The bound in (1) is proved here for power-law multiplicities. Schroeder notes that nobody has proved the statement for *all* large systems; the success of thermodynamics says exceptions must be very rare. Systems held together by gravity, with negative heat capacity, already need care ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^rem-b6-2-1|Remark: Why the reciprocal slope]]).
> - The Clausius form (heat flows from hot to cold, [[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]]) follows once temperature is defined from $S$ ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.11: Additivity of Entropy; Equilibrium Entropy of a Composite System
> 1. For two systems whose macrostates are specified separately, $S = S_A + S_B$.
> 2. If they exchange energy as in [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-8|Theorem §B6.1.8]], the entropy counted over long times, $S_{\text{eq}} = k\ln\Omega_{\text{all}}$, and the entropy of the most probable macrostate, $S_A(\bar U_A) + S_B(\bar U_B)$, satisfy
>
> $$
> S_A(\bar U_A) + S_B(\bar U_B) \le S_{\text{eq}} \le S_A(\bar U_A) + S_B(\bar U_B) + k\ln\mathcal M .
> $$
>
> The difference is $O(k\ln N)$ while $S$ is $O(Nk)$: for a large system it does not matter whether the entropy is computed over all macrostates or for the most probable one.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.6, eq. (2.48) and Problems 2.29–2.30 · Schwartz, Lecture 6, §3.4*

^thm-b6-1-11

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) The multiplicities multiply ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-8|Theorem §B6.1.8]]) and $\ln(\Omega_A\Omega_B) = \ln\Omega_A + \ln\Omega_B$. (2) $\Omega_{\text{all}}$ is a sum of $\mathcal M$ nonnegative terms, the largest of which is $\Omega_{\max} = \Omega_A(\bar U_A)\Omega_B(\bar U_B)$, so $\Omega_{\max} \le \Omega_{\text{all}} \le \mathcal M\,\Omega_{\max}$; take $k\ln$ and use (1). For Einstein solids $\mathcal M = q + 1$, a power of $N$ at fixed energy per oscillator.

^der-b6-1-11

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-8|Theorem §B6.1.8]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]]

> [!caution] Caution: Entropy curves are not sharp
> Plotted against $U_A$, $S_{\text{total}} = k\ln\Omega$ has only a broad, gentle maximum; the logarithm smooths away the sharp peak of $\Omega$. This does not weaken the conclusion: a difference $\Delta S$ is a ratio of probabilities $e^{\Delta S/k}$, and $1\ \mathrm{J/K}$ is $7 \times 10^{22}\,k$. Small-looking entropy differences are overwhelming odds.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B6.1.1: Two Small Einstein Solids
> Solids $A$ and $B$ have three oscillators each and share $q = 6$ energy units. By [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]], $\Omega_{\text{E}}(3, q) = \binom{q + 2}{q}$, and [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-8|Theorem §B6.1.8]] gives:
>
> | $q_A$ | $\Omega_A$ | $q_B$ | $\Omega_B$ | $\Omega_A\Omega_B$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 0 | 1 | 6 | 28 | 28 |
> | 1 | 3 | 5 | 21 | 63 |
> | 2 | 6 | 4 | 15 | 90 |
> | 3 | 10 | 3 | 10 | 100 |
> | 4 | 15 | 2 | 6 | 90 |
> | 5 | 21 | 1 | 3 | 63 |
> | 6 | 28 | 0 | 1 | 28 |
>
> The total is $462$, which checks: the composite is one Einstein solid of 6 oscillators with 6 units, $\binom{11}{6} = 462$. All 462 microstates are equally likely, but the macrostates are not: the even split $q_A = 3$ has probability $100/462 = 0.22$, while "all the energy in $B$" has $28/462 = 0.06$. Start with all the energy in $B$ and wait: the energy will most likely be found spread more evenly. With six oscillators the tendency is mild; the next example shows what large numbers do to it.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.3, Figure 2.4*

^ex-b6-1-1

> [!example] Example §B6.1.2: How Sharp Is Sharp?
> *A few hundred oscillators.* For $N_A = 300$, $N_B = 200$, $q = 100$ (figure, left), the exact multiplicities peak at $q_A = 60$, where each oscillator holds $0.2$ units on average. The most probable macrostate has probability only $7.4\%$, since its neighbours are almost as likely, but $\Omega(60)/\Omega(0) = 6.9 \times 10^{114}/2.8 \times 10^{81} \approx 2.5 \times 10^{33}$: found with all the energy in $B$, the system would essentially never return there.
>
> *A macroscopic solid.* Take two identical solids of $N = 10^{20}$ oscillators each, with $q \gg N$. By [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-9|Theorem §B6.1.9]] the multiplicity falls to $1/e$ at $x = q/(2\sqrt N) = 5 \times 10^{-11}\,q$. If the peak were drawn $1\ \mathrm{cm}$ wide, the full axis from $q_A = 0$ to $q$ would be about $10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm} = 10^5\ \mathrm{km}$ long. Ten peak-widths out, $\Omega/\Omega_{\max} = e^{-100} \approx 4 \times 10^{-44}$. A fluctuation away from equal sharing would have to be detected in the tenth significant figure of the energy: this is the thermodynamic limit ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-4|Def. §B6.1.4]]) in practice.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.3, Figure 2.5; §2.4, eqs. (2.27)–(2.28)*

^ex-b6-1-2

> [!example] Example §B6.1.3: Ten Billion Years Is Not Enough
> A two-state paramagnet of $N = 10^{23}$ dipoles has total energy zero, so exactly half the dipoles point up. Its multiplicity is $\binom{N}{N/2} \approx 2^N\sqrt{2/\pi N} \approx 2^{10^{23}} = 10^{3.0 \times 10^{22}}$. Suppose the microstate changes $10^9$ times per second. In the age of the universe, $10^{10}\ \mathrm{yr} = 3.2 \times 10^{17}\ \mathrm s$, the system visits at most $3.2 \times 10^{26} = 10^{26.5}$ microstates, a fraction $10^{26.5 - 3.0\times 10^{22}}$ of those accessible: zero, for every purpose. So "equilibrium" cannot mean that the system has visited its accessible microstates. What the postulate needs is that the microstates actually visited are a representative sample, which holds when the macroscopic quantities of interest are the same for nearly all microstates ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^rem-b6-1-2|Remark: Why equal probabilities?]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder, Problem 2.23 (solved here) · Schwartz, Lecture 3, §5*
