$S = k\ln\Omega$ ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]]) counts microstates that are all equally likely. What is the entropy when the probabilities differ, and what exactly is being counted? This section gives the general answer, the Gibbs entropy $-k\sum P_i\ln P_i$, shows that it is Shannon's measure of missing information, and uses it to state the maximum-entropy principle and Landauer's bound on the cost of erasing a bit. It then settles a counting question left open in [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]]: why the multiplicity of a gas carries a factor $1/N!$, and what goes wrong without it (the Gibbs paradox). Level A met entropy as disorder ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^rem-a4-6-1|Remark: Entropy measures disorder]]); level B replaces "disorder" by "missing information".

## The Gibbs entropy

> [!definition] Definition §B6.3.1: Gibbs Entropy
> If a system is in microstate $i$ with probability $P_i$ ($\sum_i P_i = 1$), its **Gibbs entropy** is
>
> $$
> S = -k\sum_i P_i\ln P_i ,
> $$
>
> with the convention $0\ln 0 = 0$ (the limit of $P\ln P$ as $P \to 0$), so that states of zero probability contribute nothing. It applies to any probability distribution, in equilibrium or not.
>
> *Source: Schwartz, Lecture 6, eq. (6) and §9 · Blundell §14.8, eq. (14.48) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §30.3*

^def-b6-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.1: Gibbs Entropy of Equal and of Grouped Probabilities
> 1. If $\Omega$ microstates each have probability $1/\Omega$ (the microcanonical ensemble, [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-3|Def. §B6.1.3]]), the Gibbs entropy is $S = k\ln\Omega$.
> 2. Let $\Omega_{\text{tot}}$ equally likely microstates be grouped into macrostates, the $i$-th containing $n_i$ of them, so that macrostate $i$ has probability $P_i = n_i/\Omega_{\text{tot}}$. Then
>
> $$
> k\ln\Omega_{\text{tot}} = \underbrace{-k\sum_i P_i\ln P_i}_{\text{which macrostate}} + \underbrace{\sum_i P_i\,k\ln n_i}_{\text{which microstate within it}} .
> $$
>
> The total entropy splits into the Gibbs entropy of the macrostate distribution and the average Boltzmann entropy of the microstates within a macrostate.
>
> *Source: Blundell §14.8, eqs. (14.42)–(14.49), Example 14.6 · Schwartz, Lecture 6, eq. (39)*

^thm-b6-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) $-k\sum_{i=1}^{\Omega}\frac1\Omega\ln\frac1\Omega = k\ln\Omega\cdot\sum_i\frac1\Omega = k\ln\Omega$. (2) $-\ln P_i = \ln\Omega_{\text{tot}} - \ln n_i$, so $-\sum_i P_i\ln P_i + \sum_i P_i\ln n_i = \ln\Omega_{\text{tot}}\sum_i P_i = \ln\Omega_{\text{tot}}$; multiply by $k$. Statement (1) is (2) with every group a single microstate.

^der-b6-3-1

*Uses:* [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^def-b6-3-1|Def. §B6.3.1]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-3|Def. §B6.1.3]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.2: Boltzmann's Counting Formula
> The number of ways of distributing $N$ distinguishable particles over $m$ groups with $n_i$ in group $i$ is the multinomial coefficient, and for large $n_i$, with $f_i = n_i/N$,
>
> $$
> \Omega = \frac{N!}{n_1!\,n_2!\cdots n_m!}, \qquad \ln\Omega \approx -N\sum_{i=1}^m f_i\ln f_i .
> $$
>
> So $k\ln\Omega$ is $N$ times the Gibbs entropy of the one-particle distribution $f_i$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz, Lecture 4, §6.1, eqs. (53)–(55); Lecture 6, eq. (5)*

^thm-b6-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Line the $N$ particles up in all $N!$ orders and put the first $n_1$ in group 1, the next $n_2$ in group 2, and so on. Reordering within a group gives the same assignment, so each assignment arises $n_1!\cdots n_m!$ times. With Stirling's approximation ([[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]) and $\sum_i n_i = N$, the linear terms cancel:
>
> $$
> \ln\Omega \approx N\ln N - \sum_i n_i\ln n_i = -\sum_i n_i\ln\frac{n_i}{N} = -N\sum_i f_i\ln f_i .
> $$

^der-b6-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]], [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^def-b6-3-1|Def. §B6.3.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.3: Bounds and Additivity of the Gibbs Entropy
> For a distribution on $m$ microstates:
> 1. $0 \le S \le k\ln m$. $S = 0$ exactly when one microstate is certain; $S = k\ln m$ exactly when all are equally likely.
> 2. If $A$ and $B$ are independent, $P_{ij}^{AB} = P_i^AP_j^B$, then $S_{AB} = S_A + S_B$.
>
> The key inequality, **Gibbs' inequality**, is: for any two distributions $P$ and $Q$, $-\sum_i P_i\ln P_i \le -\sum_i P_i\ln Q_i$, with equality only if $P = Q$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz, Lecture 6, §3.4, eqs. (22)–(24) and §4.3 · Blundell §15.1, Example 15.2*

^thm-b6-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *$\ln x \le x - 1$, with equality only at $x = 1$.* By Taylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder about $1$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), $\ln x = (x - 1) - (x - 1)^2/2\xi^2$ for some $\xi$ between $1$ and $x$.
>
> *Gibbs' inequality.* Summing over the $i$ with $P_i > 0$,
>
> $$
> \sum_i P_i\ln\frac{Q_i}{P_i} \le \sum_i P_i\Bigl(\frac{Q_i}{P_i} - 1\Bigr) = \sum_{P_i > 0}Q_i - 1 \le 0 ,
> $$
>
> with equality only if $Q_i = P_i$ for all $i$.
>
> (1) Each term $-P_i\ln P_i \ge 0$ since $0 \le P_i \le 1$, and it vanishes only for $P_i \in \{0, 1\}$; so $S = 0$ only when one $P_i = 1$. For the upper bound take $Q_i = 1/m$: $S/k \le -\sum_i P_i\ln(1/m) = \ln m$, with equality only for $P_i = 1/m$.
>
> (2) $\ln(P_i^AP_j^B) = \ln P_i^A + \ln P_j^B$, and summing over the index that does not appear uses $\sum_j P_j^B = 1$ or $\sum_i P_i^A = 1$:
>
> $$
> -\sum_{i,j}P_i^AP_j^B\ln(P_i^AP_j^B) = -\sum_iP_i^A\ln P_i^A - \sum_jP_j^B\ln P_j^B .
> $$

^der-b6-3-3

*Uses:* [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^def-b6-3-1|Def. §B6.3.1]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

## Information

> [!definition] Definition §B6.3.2: Information Content; Shannon Entropy
> The **information content** of an outcome of probability $P$ is $Q = -\log_2 P$ **bits**. The **Shannon entropy** of a random variable whose outcomes have probabilities $P_i$ is the average information content
>
> $$
> H = -\sum_i P_i\log_2 P_i\ \ \text{bits} .
> $$
>
> A certain outcome carries no information, and the information of independent outcomes adds, because their probabilities multiply. The Gibbs entropy of a distribution of microstates is its Shannon entropy in other units: since $\ln x = \ln 2\cdot\log_2 x$,
>
> $$
> S = k\ln 2\cdot H ,
> $$
>
> so one bit corresponds to $k\ln 2 = 9.57 \times 10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/K}$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §15.1, eqs. (15.1)–(15.2) · Schwartz, Lecture 6, §4 and §5.1, eqs. (26), (38)*

^def-b6-3-2

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: What $H$ measures
> - *Uncertainty before, information after.* $H$ is the average information gained on learning the outcome, equivalently the uncertainty about it beforehand (Blundell). A fair coin has $H = 1$ bit, a two-headed coin $0$; a fair die $\log_2 6 = 2.58$ bits.
> - *Compression.* Shannon's source (noiseless channel) coding theorem, stated without proof in both sources: data from a source of entropy $H$ per symbol cannot be losslessly encoded in fewer than $H$ bits per symbol on average, and $H$ can be approached. Blundell's motivation: in a long sequence of $n$ independent symbols, almost all sequences that actually occur are "typical", each with probability about $2^{-nH}$, so there are about $2^{nH}$ of them and $nH$ bits suffice to label them.
> - *Uniqueness.* Schwartz quotes that $-\sum P_i\log P_i$ is, up to the base of the logarithm, the only function that is unchanged by adding outcomes of probability zero, is largest for equal probabilities and adds over independent variables ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-3|Theorem §B6.3.3]]).
> - *Entropy as ignorance.* In this light the thermodynamic entropy of a gas measures the information one lacks about its microstate, given $U$, $V$ and $N$. Knowing the exact microstate would make $S = 0$. Schwartz's summary: entropy is not disorder but ignorance.

^rem-b6-3-1

## Maximum entropy

> [!remark] Remark: The maximum-entropy principle
> Jaynes (1957) turned the equal-probability postulate into a rule of inference: *given only some constraints on a system (normalization, a known mean energy, a known particle number), assign the probability distribution that maximizes the Gibbs entropy subject to those constraints.* Any other choice would claim information one does not have. Schwartz presents it as a general method, valid in statistics and information theory as well as physics; Blundell uses the same reasoning to recover the Boltzmann distribution. Its predictions are the theorem below; whether a physical system actually reaches the maximum-entropy distribution is the dynamical question of [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^rem-b6-1-2|Remark: Why equal probabilities?]].

^rem-b6-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.4: Maximum-Entropy Distributions
> 1. Among all distributions on $m$ microstates, the Gibbs entropy is largest for the uniform one, $P_i = 1/m$: maximum entropy with no constraint but normalization reproduces the fundamental postulate ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]).
> 2. Let microstate $i$ have energy $E_i$, and let $\bar E$ lie strictly between the smallest and largest $E_i$. Among all distributions with mean energy $\sum_i P_iE_i = \bar E$, the Gibbs entropy is largest exactly for
>
> $$
> P_i^* = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z}, \qquad Z = \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i},
> $$
>
> with $\beta$ fixed by $\sum_iP_i^*E_i = \bar E$, and then $S^* = k(\beta\bar E + \ln Z)$. The physical derivation of this Boltzmann distribution, and the identification $\beta = 1/kT$, are [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]], its home.
>
> *Source: Schwartz, Lecture 4, §6.1–6.2, eqs. (57)–(74) · Blundell §14.8, Example 14.7; §15.2 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §30.4*

^thm-b6-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Finding the candidate.* With Lagrange multipliers ([[§17 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-17-3|452 Thm. §17.3]]) make stationary $-\sum_iP_i\ln P_i - \alpha(\sum_iP_i - 1) - \beta(\sum_iP_iE_i - \bar E)$. Differentiating with respect to $P_j$ gives $-\ln P_j - 1 - \alpha - \beta E_j = 0$, so $P_j = e^{-1-\alpha}e^{-\beta E_j}$; normalization sets $e^{1+\alpha} = Z$. Without the energy constraint ($\beta = 0$), $P_j = 1/m$. This is how Schwartz and Blundell proceed; it shows stationarity only.
>
> *Proving the maximum.* (1) is [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-3|Theorem §B6.3.3]](1). For (2), the mean energy $\sum_iE_ie^{-\beta E_i}/Z$ runs continuously and monotonically from the largest $E_i$ (as $\beta \to -\infty$) to the smallest (as $\beta \to +\infty$), so some $\beta$ gives $\bar E$. For any $P$ with the same mean energy, Gibbs' inequality with $Q = P^{\ast}$ gives
>
> $$
> \frac{S[P]}k \le -\sum_iP_i\ln P_i^* = \sum_iP_i(\beta E_i + \ln Z) = \beta\bar E + \ln Z = \frac{S[P^*]}{k},
> $$
>
> where the last step is the same computation with $P^*$ in place of $P$. Equality holds only if $P = P^*$.

^der-b6-3-4

*Uses:* [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-3|Theorem §B6.3.3]], [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^def-b6-3-1|Def. §B6.3.1]], [[§17 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-17-3|452 Thm. §17.3]]

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Fine-grained entropy does not grow; coarse-grained entropy does
> Schwartz's puzzle: in a free expansion from $V_1$ to $V_2$ the Boltzmann entropy rises by $Nk\ln(V_2/V_1)$, yet each microstate in $V_1$ evolves into exactly one microstate in $V_2$, so a Gibbs entropy computed from the exact evolved probabilities does not change. (For a classical phase-space density this is Liouville's theorem, [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-3|CM Theorem §B7.2.3]]: the occupied volume is conserved.) The resolution is that the two entropies were computed with different information: the exact probabilities remember that the gas started in $V_1$, the Boltzmann count uses only $U$, $V$, $N$. Entropy is defined with *coarse-grained* probabilities, compatible with the macroscopic variables; the evolved region frays into filaments finer than any resolution, and coarse graining fills the whole of $V_2$. Defined that way, the Gibbs entropy of a diffusing density obeys $dS/dt \ge 0$, with equality only for uniform density (Schwartz, Lecture 6, eq. (8)); the H-theorem is the general statement ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-6|Theorem §B5.4.6]]; fine-grained entropy constant, [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-1|Theorem §B5.4.1]]; coarse-graining, [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^def-b5-4-2|Def. §B5.4.2]]).

## The physical cost of information

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.5: Landauer's Bound
> Erasing $N$ bits of unknown content, that is, resetting a memory whose $2^N$ states are equally likely to one definite state, while the memory is in contact with a reservoir at temperature $T$, delivers to the reservoir a heat of at least
>
> $$
> Q \ge NkT\ln 2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Blundell §15.2 · Schwartz, Lecture 6, §5.2–5.3 ("Landauer's principle") · Schroeder, Problem 3.16*

^thm-b6-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Before erasure the memory has $2^N$ equally likely states, Gibbs entropy $Nk\ln 2$ ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]]); afterwards one state, entropy $0$. Erasure cannot be done by reversible microscopic dynamics alone, which is one-to-one and cannot map two states onto one (Schwartz): the information must be carried off into other degrees of freedom. The total entropy of memory and reservoir cannot decrease ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-10|Theorem §B6.1.10]]), so the reservoir's entropy rises by at least $Nk\ln 2$, and for a reservoir at fixed $T$ this takes a heat $Q = T\Delta S_{\text{res}} \ge NkT\ln 2$ ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-7|Theorem §B6.2.7]]).

^der-b6-3-5

*Uses:* [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-10|Theorem §B6.1.10]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-7|Theorem §B6.2.7]]

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Szilard's engine and Maxwell's demon
> - *Information can do work.* A single molecule in a box at temperature $T$, known to be in the left half: insert a piston at the middle and let the molecule push it isothermally to the right wall. It does work $kT\ln(V_2/V_1) = kT\ln 2$, drawing that heat from the reservoir, and the knowledge of which half is used up (Szilard's engine, in Bennett's tape version: Schwartz, Lecture 6, §5.3). Restoring the known state (setting the bit) costs at least the same $kT\ln 2$, so no net work is gained from a reservoir at one temperature.
> - *The demon.* Maxwell's demon opens a shutter for fast (or for helium) molecules only and so seems to lower the entropy of the gas with no work. Measurement can be made with arbitrarily little dissipation, so it is not the culprit, as Szilard and Brillouin had thought. The demon must *record* each result, and with a finite memory it must eventually erase old records, paying at least $kT\ln 2$ per bit; if it never erases, the disorder has moved from the gas into its memory tape. Either way the total entropy, counting information, does not fall (Bennett, 1982; Schwartz §6; Blundell §15.2). The second law holds for thermodynamic and information entropy together.

## The Gibbs paradox

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.6: Entropy of Mixing
> Two *different* monatomic ideal gases at the same temperature and pressure, $N_A = (1 - x)N$ and $N_B = xN$ atoms in volumes $(1 - x)V$ and $xV$, are allowed to mix by removing the partition between them. The entropy increases by
>
> $$
> \Delta S_{\text{mix}} = -Nk\bigl[x\ln x + (1 - x)\ln(1 - x)\bigr] ,
> $$
>
> which is $2N'k\ln 2$ for equal amounts $N' = N/2$ of each. If the two gases are the *same*, $\Delta S = 0$.
>
> Thermodynamics obtains the same result, for any number of gases, from a reversible route with semipermeable pistons ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-8|Theorem §B2.2.8]]); here it comes from counting, which also explains why identical gases give zero ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-7|Theorem §B6.3.7]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.6, eqs. (2.53)–(2.54) and Problem 2.37 · Schwartz, Lecture 6, §3, eqs. (9)–(10) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §30.3*

^thm-b6-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The atoms do not interact, so each gas has its own multiplicity and the entropies add ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-11|Theorem §B6.1.11]]). Each gas keeps its energy and atom number and expands into the whole volume; at fixed $U$ and $N$, $S = Nk\ln V + \text{const}$ ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-8|Theorem §B6.2.8]]). So $\Delta S_B = xNk\ln(V/xV) = -xNk\ln x$ and $\Delta S_A = -(1 - x)Nk\ln(1 - x)$. For one gas on both sides, the state after removing the partition (all atoms of one kind at the original density, temperature and total energy) has the extensive Sackur–Tetrode entropy of the whole, which equals the sum for the two halves ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-7|Theorem §B6.3.7]]), so nothing changes.

^der-b6-3-6

*Uses:* [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-8|Theorem §B6.2.8]], [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-11|Theorem §B6.1.11]], [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-7|Theorem §B6.3.7]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.7: Gibbs Paradox; Extensivity Requires the 1/N!
> Count the microstates of a monatomic ideal gas *without* the factor $1/N!$ of [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]], as if the atoms were distinguishable. Then
>
> $$
> S_{\text{dist}}(U, V, N) = Nk\Bigl[\ln\Bigl(V\Bigl(\frac{4\pi mU}{3Nh^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}\Bigr) + \frac32\Bigr] = S + Nk(\ln N - 1),
> $$
>
> with $S$ the Sackur–Tetrode entropy of [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-8|Theorem §B6.2.8]], and
>
> $$
> S_{\text{dist}}(2U, 2V, 2N) = 2S_{\text{dist}}(U, V, N) + 2Nk\ln 2 :
> $$
>
> $S_{\text{dist}}$ is not extensive. Inserting a partition into a box of one gas would lower its entropy by $2Nk\ln 2$, in violation of the second law, and removing it would produce an entropy of mixing for identical gases. With the $1/N!$, $S$ depends on $U$, $V$, $N$ only through $U/N$, $V/N$ and an overall factor $N$, so $S(\lambda U, \lambda V, \lambda N) = \lambda S(U, V, N)$ (to leading order in $N$): the partition changes nothing, and mixing entropy appears only for different gases. This is the explanation that thermodynamics lacks for the jump of the mixing entropy to zero for identical gases ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-8|Theorem §B2.2.8]], [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^cau-b2-2-3|Caution: "Different" must mean distinguishable]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.6, eqs. (2.55)–(2.57) · Schwartz, Lecture 6, §3.1–3.4, eqs. (11)–(18) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §33.1–33.2*

^thm-b6-3-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Without the $1/N!$, $\ln\Omega_{\text{dist}} = \ln\Omega + \ln N! \approx \ln\Omega + N\ln N - N$; adding $Nk(\ln N - 1)$ to the Sackur–Tetrode formula turns $\ln(V/N)$ into $\ln V$ and $\tfrac52$ into $\tfrac32$. Doubling $U$, $V$, $N$ leaves $U/N$ unchanged but doubles $V$ inside the logarithm:
>
> $$
> S_{\text{dist}}(2U, 2V, 2N) = 2Nk\Bigl[\ln\Bigl(2V\Bigl(\frac{4\pi mU}{3Nh^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}\Bigr) + \frac32\Bigr] = 2S_{\text{dist}}(U, V, N) + 2Nk\ln 2 .
> $$
>
> A box of $2N$ atoms in $2V$ with energy $2U$, divided in the middle, becomes two boxes each with $N$, $V$, $U$ (to within fluctuations of relative size $N^{-1/2}$), with total entropy $2S_{\text{dist}}(U, V, N)$: lower by $2Nk\ln 2$. With the $1/N!$ the Sackur–Tetrode formula contains only $V/N$ and $U/N$ inside the logarithm and $N$ outside, so scaling all three by $\lambda$ multiplies $S$ by $\lambda$.

^der-b6-3-7

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^thm-b6-2-8|Theorem §B6.2.8]], [[§B4.1 Probability Distributions and the Gaussian#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]

> [!definition] Definition §B6.3.3: Indistinguishable Particles; Correct Boltzmann Counting
> Particles are **indistinguishable** if exchanging any two of them does not produce a different microstate. For $N$ indistinguishable particles of which no two occupy the same one-particle state, each microstate is counted $N!$ times by a count that labels the particles, and the correct multiplicity is
>
> $$
> \Omega_{\text{indist}} = \frac{\Omega_{\text{labelled}}}{N!} .
> $$
>
> This **correct Boltzmann counting** is valid when the number of accessible one-particle states greatly exceeds $N$, so that double occupancy is negligible: a dilute gas, $n \ll n_Q$ ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]]; degeneracy criterion, [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases#^def-b10-2-1|Def. §B10.2.1]]). Identical particles in quantum mechanics (same species, no measurement can tell them apart, [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^def-a5-3-1|QM Def. §A5.3.1]]) are indistinguishable in this sense: their many-particle states are symmetric or antisymmetric under exchange ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^thm-a5-3-1|QM Theorem §A5.3.1]]), so an exchange gives the same state.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.5, eqs. (2.37)–(2.38) and footnote · Schwartz, Lecture 6, §3.2 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §33.1*

^def-b6-3-3

> [!remark] Remark: Resolving the paradox, two ways
> - *Physical identity.* Schroeder: there is no paradox, because real atoms of one species are indistinguishable (quantum mechanics, Chapter B10), and the $1/N!$ is simply correct counting. What the paradox teaches is that entropy depends on what counts as "distinguishable".
> - *Information.* Schwartz asks what one is able, or intends, to tell apart. With $2N$ coloured balls that one could sort, splitting the box in two leaves one of $\binom{2N}{N} \approx 2^{2N}$ possible assignments of balls to sides; counting all of them, the entropy does not fall on splitting. Looking to see which ball went where removes that uncertainty, but the measurement must be recorded and eventually erased, which costs at least the entropy gained ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-5|Theorem §B6.3.5]]). For a gas of $10^{24}$ helium atoms no device will ever label them, so the $1/N!$ is the right count whatever one's view of quantum identity; distinguishing ³He from ⁴He changes the count by an amount that does not matter. In both views, including all information, entropy never decreases.

^rem-b6-3-5

> [!caution] Caution: When to divide by $N!$
> - *Gas:* yes. The atoms roam the whole volume and exchanging two gives the same state.
> - *Atoms at lattice sites* (the Einstein solid, the paramagnet of [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^mod-b6-1-3|Model §B6.1.3]]): no. The oscillators or dipoles are labelled by their sites, which is why [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]] has no $1/N!$ (Schwartz: atoms in a solid are distinguishable by position even if chemically identical).
> - *Dense or cold gas* ($n \gtrsim n_Q$): neither labelled counting nor the $1/N!$ is right, because double occupancy matters; Bose–Einstein or Fermi–Dirac counting replaces both ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]).
> - The entropy of mixing is real only when the gases differ by something some device could in principle use, a semipermeable membrane for instance; that membrane is also how mixing entropy is turned into work, as osmotic pressure (Schwartz, Lecture 6, §3).

## Examples

> [!example]- ★ Example §B6.3.1: Compressing a Biased Coin
> A coin lands heads with probability $0.9$. Its Shannon entropy is
>
> $$
> H = -(0.9\log_2 0.9 + 0.1\log_2 0.1) = 0.469\ \text{bits per flip},
> $$
>
> so by the source coding theorem no lossless code can use fewer than $0.469$ bits per flip on average, while the plain code (H = 0, T = 1) uses $1$. Encode pairs of flips with the prefix code $\text{HH} \to 0$, $\text{HT} \to 10$, $\text{TH} \to 110$, $\text{TT} \to 111$ (no code word begins another, so a string decodes uniquely). The pair probabilities are $0.81, 0.09, 0.09, 0.01$, so the mean length is
>
> $$
> 0.81 \cdot 1 + 0.09 \cdot 2 + 0.09 \cdot 3 + 0.01 \cdot 3 = 1.29\ \text{bits per pair} = 0.645\ \text{bits per flip}.
> $$
>
> That beats one bit per flip, and beats Schwartz's own scheme ($0.785$), but not the bound $0.469$, which is approached only by coding ever longer blocks: of the $2^n$ sequences of $n$ flips, only about $2^{0.469n}$ are typical. The fair coin, $H = 1$, cannot be compressed at all.
>
> *Source: Schwartz, Lecture 6, §4.2, eqs. (32)–(35) ("check your understanding", solved here) · Blundell §15.3, Example 15.3*

^ex-b6-3-1

> [!example] Example §B6.3.2: Mixing, and What Distinguishability Would Cost
> One mole of helium and one mole of argon, each in a volume $V$ at the same $T$ and $p$, mix when the partition is removed. By [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-6|Theorem §B6.3.6]],
>
> $$
> \Delta S_{\text{mix}} = 2R\ln 2 = 2(8.314\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)})(0.693)(1\ \mathrm{mol}) = 11.5\ \mathrm{J/K} .
> $$
>
> Two moles of helium mixed with each other: $\Delta S = 0$.
>
> Had the helium atoms been counted as distinguishable, [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-7|Theorem §B6.3.7]] would add $Nk(\ln N - 1) = R(\ln N_A - 1) = (8.314)(54.75 - 1)\ \mathrm{J/K} = 447\ \mathrm{J/K}$ per mole, giving $S_{\text{dist}} \approx 573\ \mathrm{J/K}$ instead of $126\ \mathrm{J/K}$ ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^ex-b6-2-2|Example §B6.2.2]]), and the wrong answer would also be non-extensive.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §2.6, eq. (2.54); Problem 2.39 (solved here) · Schwartz, Lecture 6, §3*

^ex-b6-3-2

> [!example]- ★ Example §B6.3.3: The Price of Forgetting
> *A gigabyte.* Erasing one gigabyte, $2^{30}$ bytes $= 8.59 \times 10^9$ bits, of unknown content raises the entropy of the surroundings by at least
>
> $$
> \Delta S = (8.59 \times 10^9)\,k\ln 2 = 8.2 \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm{J/K},
> $$
>
> and at room temperature, $300\ \mathrm K$, dissipates at least $Q = T\Delta S = 2.5 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm J$ ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-5|Theorem §B6.3.5]]). Negligible.
>
> *A real bit.* A microchip bit stored on a $5\ \mathrm{fF}$ capacitor at $3\ \mathrm V$ holds $\tfrac12CV^2 = 2.25 \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm J = 1.4 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{eV}$, against the Landauer minimum $kT\ln 2 = 0.018\ \mathrm{eV}$ at $300\ \mathrm K$: present technology dissipates some $10^7$ times the thermodynamic limit per bit, so the limit is a matter of principle, not yet of engineering.
>
> *Source: Schroeder, Problem 3.16 · Blundell, Exercise 15.1 (both solved here)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The maximum-entropy problem is optimization with constraints ([[§17 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-17-3|452 Thm. §17.3]]); its global maximum follows from Gibbs' inequality, which rests on $\ln x \le x - 1$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]). Equivalently, $-x\ln x$ is concave, so the entropy is a concave function on the simplex of distributions.
> - Fine-grained entropy is conserved by Hamiltonian flow ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-3|CM Theorem §B7.2.3]], [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-1|Theorem §B5.4.1]]); its coarse-grained growth is the H-theorem ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-6|Theorem §B5.4.6]]).
> - Indistinguishability is the classical shadow of exchange symmetry ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^thm-a5-3-1|QM Theorem §A5.3.1]]); the full quantum counting is [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]], and the $1/N!$ reappears in the ideal-gas partition function $Z_1^N/N!$ ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]).
> - Maximum entropy at fixed mean energy gives the Boltzmann factor, the starting point of the canonical ensemble ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]]).
> - Quantum Mechanics level C: the Gibbs entropy (Def. §B6.3.1) for a density operator, the von Neumann entropy, with the bounds and additivity of Theorem §B6.3.3 — [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^def-c11-2-1|QM Def. §C11.2.1]], [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-4|QM Theorem §C11.2.4]], [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^rem-c11-2-2|QM Remark: What changes from the Gibbs entropy]]; maximum entropy (Theorem §B6.3.4) gives the canonical density operator — [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-5|QM Theorem §C11.2.5]]; for a part of an entangled pure state the same entropy measures entanglement — [[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^thm-c11-3-3|QM Theorem §C11.3.3]].
