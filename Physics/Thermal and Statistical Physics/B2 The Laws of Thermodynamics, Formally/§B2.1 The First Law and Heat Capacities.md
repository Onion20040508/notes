The first law is a level-A principle: the internal energy is a state function and $\Delta U = Q - W$ for any process between equilibrium states ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]), or $dU = đQ - p\,dV$ for a quasi-static step of a fluid ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]). Level A then used it almost only for the ideal gas, whose energy depends on $T$ alone ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]). This section applies the first law to an arbitrary fluid with energy $U(T, V)$, using the tools of Chapter B1: it writes the heat of a quasi-static step as a differential form, defines heat capacities as partial derivatives, derives $C_p - C_V$ for any substance, and shows that the adiabats of every substance are $\gamma$ times steeper than its isotherms.

## The first law as a differential form

> [!caution] Caution: Notation and sign conventions
> - These notes write the internal energy $U$, as Blundell, Schroeder and Schwartz do (level A writes $E_{\text{int}}$; Schwartz also writes $E$), and $k = k_B$.
> - They keep level A's sign convention: $đW$ is the work done **by** the system, so $dU = đQ - đW$ and, for a quasi-static step of a fluid, $đW = p\,dV$ ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]).
> - Blundell, Schroeder, Schwartz, the user's notes and PHY 306 write $dU = đQ + đW$ with $đW$ the work done **on** the system, so their $đW = -p\,dV$. Formulas from them are converted here by $đW_{\text{on}} = -đW$. Example: PHY 306's "$\Delta U = \Delta Q + \Delta W$" is $\Delta U = Q - W$ below.
> - The user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §3.3, define $\delta W$ as the work done on the system ($dU = \delta Q + \delta W$, $\delta W = -P\,dV$) and then write the finite work as $W = \int P\,dV$, the work done by the system; §3.4 uses both readings side by side. The isobaric formula $Q = \Delta U + W$ and "the work (by the gas) $W = Nk_BT\ln(V_f/V_i)$" count work done by the gas, while the isothermal $Q = -W$ and the adiabatic $\Delta U = W$ count work done on it. In the convention here every case reads $Q = \Delta U + W$: an isothermal ideal gas has $Q = W = Nk_BT\ln(V_f/V_i)$, an adiabatic process $\Delta U = -W$.
> - $đQ$ and $đW$ are inexact ([[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]]); $dU$ is exact.
>
> *Source: Blundell §11.2, eqs. (11.6)–(11.9) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 5, §2.2 · Schroeder §1.6*

^cau-b2-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.1: Heat in a Quasi-Static Step
> For a closed fluid with internal energy $U(T, V)$, the heat absorbed in a quasi-static step is the differential form
>
> $$
> đQ = \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V dT + \Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T + p\Bigr] dV ,
> $$
>
> and the internal energy changes by $dU = (\partial U/\partial T)_V\,dT + (\partial U/\partial V)_T\,dV$ in any step, quasi-static or not.
>
> *Source: Blundell §11.3, eqs. (11.10)–(11.12)*

^thm-b2-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A fixed amount of fluid has two independent state variables, so $U$ is a function of $(T, V)$ and its change is the total differential ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]); this needs only that the end states be equilibrium states. In a quasi-static step the first law gives $đQ = dU + p\,dV$ ([[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]). Substitute $dU$ and collect the $dV$ terms.

^der-b2-1-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the coefficient of $dV$
> The coefficient of $dV$ is the heat needed per unit volume of isothermal expansion. It has two parts: $p$, which pays for the work done on the surroundings, and $(\partial U/\partial V)_T$, which pays for pulling the molecules apart against their mutual attraction (sometimes called the internal pressure). For an ideal gas the second part vanishes; for a gas with attractions it is positive, which is why a real gas cools in a free expansion ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^rem-a3-6-1|Remark: Free expansion of real gases]]). By the reciprocity theorem ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]]) the temperature change per unit volume in a free expansion, where $U$ is fixed, is
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\Bigr)_U = -\frac{(\partial U/\partial V)_T}{(\partial U/\partial T)_V} ,
> $$
>
> so Joule's free-expansion experiment measures $(\partial U/\partial V)_T$. The first law alone cannot predict it: it fixes neither $U(T, V)$ nor $p(T, V)$. The second law ties it to the equation of state ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]).

^rem-b2-1-1

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Other kinds of work
> Nothing above is special to a gas. For a wire under tension $f$ whose length changes by $dL$, the work done on it is $f\,dL$, so $đW = -f\,dL$ and $dU = đQ + f\,dL$; for a film with surface tension or a magnetised body the same structure holds with other pairs of variables. In each case the work term is an intensive "force" times the change of an extensive "displacement". These systems are treated in [[§B3.4★ Rods, Bubbles and Magnets|§B3.4]].
>
> *Source: Blundell §11.2, eq. (11.8)*

^rem-b2-1-2

## Heat capacities as partial derivatives

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.1: Heat Capacity along a Constraint
> The **heat capacity** of a system at constant $x$ (a state variable, or a combination such as $p$ held by a piston) is
>
> $$
> C_x = \Bigl(\frac{đQ}{dT}\Bigr)_x ,
> $$
>
> the heat absorbed per unit temperature rise in a quasi-static step along which $x$ stays constant. The two standard ones for a fluid are $C_V$ and $C_p$. They are extensive (unit $\mathrm{J/K}$); per mole they are the molar heat capacities of level A ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^def-a2-3-1|Def. §A2.3.1]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^def-a3-5-1|Def. §A3.5.1]]), per unit mass the specific heats ([[§A1.3 Heat, Specific Heat and Calorimetry#^def-a1-3-2|Def. §A1.3.2]]). The constraint is part of the definition: $đQ$ is inexact, so $đQ/dT$ has no meaning until the direction of the step is fixed.
>
> *Source: Blundell §11.3, eqs. (11.14), (11.16), (11.19)–(11.20) · Schroeder §1.6, eqs. (1.41)–(1.43) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 5, eq. (17)*

^def-b2-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.2: Heat Capacities of a General Fluid
> For a closed fluid with internal energy $U(T, V)$ and equation of state $V = V(T, p)$,
>
> $$
> C_V = \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V, \qquad C_p = \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p + p\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p = \Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p \quad (H = U + pV),
> $$
>
> $$
> C_p - C_V = \Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T + p\Bigr]\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p .
> $$
>
> For an ideal gas, $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = 0$ and $(\partial V/\partial T)_p = nR/p$, so $C_p - C_V = nR$ ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]) for every ideal gas, monatomic or not (the user's notes, §4.1, label the result "monatomic ideal gas", but the derivation uses only $pV = nRT$ and $U = U(T)$). This is the first-law content; the enthalpy form of $C_p$ has its home with the potentials ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]), and the second law turns the bracket into a property of the equation of state ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]]).
>
> *Source: Blundell §11.3, eqs. (11.15)–(11.18), Example 11.2 · Schroeder §1.6, eqs. (1.43)–(1.49), (1.56) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 5, eqs. (17)–(25) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §4.1*

^thm-b2-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Divide the form of [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]] by $dT$ along a step of the chosen kind:
>
> $$
> \frac{đQ}{dT} = \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V + \Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T + p\Bigr]\frac{dV}{dT} .
> $$
>
> At constant $V$ the second term drops out: $C_V = (\partial U/\partial T)_V$. At constant $p$, $dV/dT = (\partial V/\partial T)_p$, which gives the formula for $C_p - C_V$ directly. For the other form of $C_p$, change the variable held fixed ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]): $(\partial U/\partial T)_p = (\partial U/\partial T)_V + (\partial U/\partial V)_T(\partial V/\partial T)_p$, so $C_p = (\partial U/\partial T)_p + p(\partial V/\partial T)_p$; and at constant $p$, $d(U + pV) = dU + p\,dV$, which is $(\partial H/\partial T)_p\,dT$. For the ideal gas, $U$ depends on $T$ only ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^rem-a3-2-3|Remark: Internal energy of an ideal gas]]) and $V = nRT/p$.

^der-b2-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]], [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]], [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: $dU = C_V\,dT$ is an ideal-gas formula
> In general $dU = C_V\,dT + (\partial U/\partial V)_T\,dV$, and $dU = C_V\,dT$ holds along every path only when $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = 0$, as for the ideal gas. Likewise $(\partial U/\partial T)_p$ is not $C_V$: holding $p$ instead of $V$ fixed changes the derivative ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^cau-b1-2-1|Caution: Same letter, different functions]]). Schroeder's names make the point: $C_V$ is really an "energy capacity" and $C_p$ an "enthalpy capacity", since neither requires that the energy arrive as heat.
>
> *Source: Blundell, Example 11.3 · Schroeder §1.6*

^cau-b2-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: What sets $C_p - C_V$
> The formula of [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]] says that the extra heat at constant pressure pays for the expansion that comes with heating: the work against the surroundings ($p$) and against the internal attraction ($(\partial U/\partial V)_T$), per unit of the volume gained, $(\partial V/\partial T)_p$. For solids and liquids $(\partial V/\partial T)_p$ is small and $C_p \approx C_V$ for most purposes. The first law cannot go further, because $(\partial U/\partial V)_T$ is unknown to it. With the second law, $(\partial U/\partial V)_T + p = T(\partial p/\partial T)_V$ ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]), and the difference becomes a property of the equation of state alone, $C_p - C_V = T(\partial p/\partial T)_V(\partial V/\partial T)_p = TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T$ ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]]).

^rem-b2-1-3

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Heat capacities can be negative
> $C_V > 0$ is not a theorem of the first law. A self-gravitating body such as a star has $C_V < 0$: when it loses energy it contracts, its gravitational potential energy falls by more than the energy lost, and its kinetic energy, hence its temperature, rises. Negative heat capacities need long-range forces like gravity. A system with $C_V < 0$ cannot be in stable equilibrium with a reservoir: a small heat loss would make it hotter than the reservoir, so it would keep losing heat.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 5, §2 · Schroeder §3.1*

^rem-b2-1-4

## Adiabats and isotherms of any substance

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.3: Adiabatic and Isothermal Slopes
> For a closed fluid with $\gamma = C_p/C_V$, along a quasi-static adiabatic path ($đQ = 0$; subscript "ad")
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial V}\Bigr)_{\text{ad}} = \gamma\Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{\text{ad}} = \frac{1}{1 - \gamma}\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p, \qquad
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Bigr)_{\text{ad}} = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1}\Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V ,
> $$
>
> wherever the derivatives involved are finite and $C_p \neq C_V$. No equation of state is assumed. (After [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy|§B2.2]], "ad" is constant entropy, $S$.)
>
> *Source: Blundell, Exercise 12.3 · PHY 306, Problem Set 3, Problem 1; Midterm, Problem 4*

^thm-b2-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The heat of a quasi-static step is one differential form, which can be written in the coordinates $(T, p)$ or $(T, V)$:
>
> $$
> đQ = C_p\,dT + A\,dp = C_V\,dT + B\,dV ,
> $$
>
> because the coefficient of $dT$ with $p$ (or $V$) fixed is $C_p$ (or $C_V$) by [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]; $A$ and $B$ are state functions, the heat per unit change of $p$ (of $V$) at fixed $T$, with $B = (\partial U/\partial V)_T + p$ by [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]]. Subtracting,
>
> $$
> (C_p - C_V)\,dT = B\,dV - A\,dp
> $$
>
> for every quasi-static step. Read this along three kinds of step:
> - at constant $T$: $B\,dV = A\,dp$, so $(\partial p/\partial V)_T = B/A$;
> - at constant $p$: $C_p - C_V = B(\partial V/\partial T)_p$; at constant $V$: $C_p - C_V = -A(\partial p/\partial T)_V$;
> - along an adiabat, $đQ = 0$ in both forms: $C_p\,dT = -A\,dp$ and $C_V\,dT = -B\,dV$.
>
> Dividing the two adiabatic relations, $(dp/dV)_{\text{ad}} = (C_p/C_V)(B/A) = \gamma(\partial p/\partial V)_T$. From the second, $(dV/dT)_{\text{ad}} = -C_V/B = -C_V(\partial V/\partial T)_p/(C_p - C_V) = (\partial V/\partial T)_p/(1 - \gamma)$. From the first, $(dp/dT)_{\text{ad}} = -C_p/A = C_p(\partial p/\partial T)_V/(C_p - C_V) = \tfrac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1}(\partial p/\partial T)_V$.

^der-b2-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]], [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]], [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Adiabats are steeper for every substance
> Level A proved that an ideal gas's adiabats are steeper than its isotherms, from $pV^\gamma = \text{const}$ ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-3|Theorem §A3.6.3]]). The first identity says the same for any fluid, with the factor $\gamma$, as soon as $\gamma > 1$; that $C_p \ge C_V$ for every stable substance, with equality only where $\alpha = 0$ (water at $4\ ^\circ\mathrm C$), follows from $C_p - C_V = TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T$ with $\kappa_T > 0$ ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]]). In terms of compressibilities, the adiabatic one is smaller by the factor $\gamma$ ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-5|Theorem §B3.2.5]], which adds $\kappa_T - \kappa_S = TV\alpha^2/C_p$), which is why sound, an adiabatic compression, travels faster than an isothermal estimate predicts. For the ideal gas, $(\partial p/\partial V)_T = -p/V$ turns the first identity into $dp/p = -\gamma\,dV/V$, i.e. $pV^\gamma = \text{const}$ ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-2|Theorem §A3.6.2]]) when $\gamma$ is constant.

^rem-b2-1-5

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.1.1: Heat along Three Paths, with $\Delta U$ from an Adiabat
> For a certain gas the quasi-static adiabat through state $A$ ($V_A = 1\ \mathrm L$, $p_A = 32 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$) is $p = \alpha V^{-5/3}$; it reaches $B$ ($V_B = 8\ \mathrm L$, $p_B = 1 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$). Find $W$ and $Q$ for three other quasi-static paths from $A$ to $B$: (1) constant pressure to $B' = (8\ \mathrm L, p_A)$, then constant volume down to $B$; (2) pressure falling linearly with volume; (3) constant volume down to $A' = (1\ \mathrm L, p_B)$, then constant pressure to $B$. Nothing else about the gas is known.
>
> Work in units of $10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$ and litres, where $1$ unit of $pV$ is $100\ \mathrm J$; then $\alpha = p_AV_A^{5/3} = 32$.
>
> *$\Delta U$ from the adiabat.* Along it $Q = 0$, so $\Delta U = -W_{\text{ad}}$ with
>
> $$
> W_{\text{ad}} = \int_1^8 32\,V^{-5/3}\,dV = 32\cdot\tfrac32\bigl(1 - 8^{-2/3}\bigr) = 36 \ \Rightarrow\ \Delta U = U_B - U_A = -3.6\ \mathrm{kJ} .
> $$
>
> $U$ is a state function, so this is $\Delta U$ on every path, although no formula for $U$ is known.
>
> *The three paths.* $W$ is the area under each path ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]) and $Q = \Delta U + W$:
>
> | path | $W$ (by the gas) | $Q = \Delta U + W$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | (1) $A \to B' \to B$ | $32 \times 7 = 224 \to 22.4\ \mathrm{kJ}$ | $+18.8\ \mathrm{kJ}$ |
> | (2) straight line | $\tfrac12(32 + 1) \times 7 = 115.5 \to 11.55\ \mathrm{kJ}$ | $+7.95\ \mathrm{kJ}$ |
> | (3) $A \to A' \to B$ | $1 \times 7 = 7 \to 0.7\ \mathrm{kJ}$ | $-2.9\ \mathrm{kJ}$ |
> | adiabat | $3.6\ \mathrm{kJ}$ | $0$ |
>
> The work ranges from $0.7$ to $22.4\ \mathrm{kJ}$ and the heat from $-2.9$ to $+18.8\ \mathrm{kJ}$; along path (3) the gas even gives off net heat. Their difference $Q - W$ is $-3.6\ \mathrm{kJ}$ every time. As a check, the exponent $5/3$ is that of a monatomic ideal gas, for which $U = \tfrac32 pV$, and indeed $\tfrac32(p_BV_B - p_AV_A) = \tfrac32(8 - 32) = -36$ units.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Problem Set 2, Problem 3 (and its solution)*

> [!example] Example §B2.1.2: The Dry Adiabatic Lapse Rate
> In the lower atmosphere, a parcel of air that rises expands into lower pressure without time to exchange heat: model its rise as quasi-static and adiabatic, and the atmosphere as mixed by such parcels (convective equilibrium), so that the temperature profile $T(z)$ is the parcel's. Treat dry air as an ideal gas of molar mass $M = 0.029\ \mathrm{kg/mol}$ with $C_{p,\text{m}} = \tfrac72 R$.
>
> *Along the parcel's path*, the third identity of [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]] with $(\partial p/\partial T)_V = p/T$ gives
>
> $$
> \frac{dp}{dT} = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1}\,\frac pT .
> $$
>
> *Hydrostatic equilibrium* ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-1|CM Theorem §A9.1.1]]) with the ideal-gas density $\rho = pM/RT$ gives $dp/dz = -\rho g = -pMg/RT$. Dividing,
>
> $$
> \frac{dT}{dz} = \frac{dT}{dp}\,\frac{dp}{dz} = -\frac{\gamma - 1}{\gamma}\,\frac{Mg}{R} = -\frac{Mg}{C_{p,\text{m}}} ,
> $$
>
> using $(\gamma - 1)/\gamma = (C_p - C_V)/C_p = R/C_{p,\text{m}}$. Numerically,
>
> $$
> \Bigl|\frac{dT}{dz}\Bigr| = \frac{(0.029\ \mathrm{kg/mol})(9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2})}{29.1\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)}} = 9.8\ \mathrm{K/km} .
> $$
>
> The temperature falls linearly with height, unlike the isothermal atmosphere of [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-4|CM Theorem §A9.1.4]]. Observed lapse rates are nearer $6$–$7\ \mathrm{K/km}$, because moist air releases latent heat as its water vapour condenses.
>
> *Source: Blundell §12.4, eqs. (12.17)–(12.22) · Schroeder, Problem 1.40 · PHY 306, Midterm, Problem 4 (the identity)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The heat of a quasi-static step is a differential form on the plane of states, inexact because $(\partial p/\partial T)_V \neq 0$ ([[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^rem-b1-1-1|Remark: Heat in the language of forms]]); the derivations above are [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]] and the comparison of coefficients of one form in two coordinate systems, i.e. the chain rule [[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]].
> - Statistical mechanics computes $C_V$ from first principles: $C_V = \partial\langle E\rangle/\partial T$ and its relation to energy fluctuations ([[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function]]), the frozen degrees of freedom of molecules ([[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules]]) and the $T^3$ law of solids ([[§B11.2★ Phonons and the Debye Model]]).
> - The enthalpy $H = U + pV$ appearing in $C_p = (\partial H/\partial T)_p$ is the Legendre transform of $U$ in $V$ ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|Def. §B1.2.2]]); its home is [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables]].
