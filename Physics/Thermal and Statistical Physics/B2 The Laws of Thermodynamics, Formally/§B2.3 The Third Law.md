The second law defines entropy only up to a constant ($dS = đQ_{\text{rev}}/T$, [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]) and says nothing about what happens as $T \to 0$. Level A stated the third law as a principle in two forms, the unattainability of absolute zero and the vanishing of isothermal entropy changes, $\lim_{T\to0}(\Delta S)_T = 0$, and left their equivalence to level B ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-3|Principle §A4.6.3]], [[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^rem-a4-6-3|Remark: What the third law adds]]). This section states the law precisely, in Nernst's, Planck's and Simon's versions, proves its consequences (heat capacities and expansivities vanish at $T = 0$, the classical ideal gas must fail there) and proves that the entropy form and the unattainability form are equivalent.

## Statements

> [!remark] Remark: The third law, stated precisely
> The level-A principle is used here in the following form. For a system in internal equilibrium, let $x$ be any parameter that can be varied at fixed temperature (volume, pressure, magnetic field, the progress of a chemical reaction).
> - **Nernst (1906).** As $T \to 0$ the entropy tends to a limit $S_0$ that is the same for all values of $x$: $\lim_{T\to0}\bigl[S(T, x_1) - S(T, x_2)\bigr] = 0$. Equivalently, all isothermal processes, reactions included, take place without change of entropy near $T = 0$. Nernst inferred this from electrochemical data: the reaction quantities $\Delta G$ and $\Delta H$ (Gibbs function and enthalpy, [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables|§B3.1]]) approach each other ever more closely, not just at $T = 0$, as $T \to 0$.
> - **Planck (1911).** The common limit may be taken to be zero: $S \to 0$ as $T \to 0$. Planck stated it for perfect crystals.
> - **Simon (1937).** The contribution to the entropy of each *aspect* of a system (lattice, electrons, nuclear spins) that is in internal equilibrium tends to zero as $T \to 0$. This allows a subsystem, say the nuclear spins, to keep its entropy down to much lower temperatures than the rest.
>
> Schwartz's "modern version" asks only that $S$ tend to a finite constant as $T \to 0$ ($k$ times the logarithm of the ground-state degeneracy); Nernst's form adds that the constant is the same for all $x$. Since only entropy differences are measured, the value of the constant is a convention, but the law keeps its content: $S(T)$ must reach a finite limit, so near $T = 0$ it can only vary as a positive power of $T$ (Schwartz), and this is what the theorems below use.
>
> *Source: Blundell §18.1 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 5, §1 · Schroeder §3.2 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §9.3*

^rem-b2-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Internal equilibrium, and residual entropy
> The law applies to systems in internal equilibrium. A glass, or a crystal whose molecules are frozen in random orientations, is not: rearranging into the true ground state may take longer than the age of the universe, and such a system keeps a **residual entropy** $k\ln(\text{number of frozen arrangements})$ as $T \to 0$ ([[§B2.3 The Third Law#^ex-b2-3-1|Example §B2.3.1]]). Random mixtures of isotopes in a crystal carry a frozen mixing entropy ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-8|Theorem §B2.2.8]]), and nuclear spins keep $Nk\ln(2I + 1)$ until temperatures far below a kelvin, where their weak mutual interactions finally order them. By convention, tabulated "third-law" entropies include residual entropy from molecular orientations but not isotopic mixing or nuclear spin entropy.
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.2 · Blundell §18.1*

## Consequences for response functions

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.1: Heat Capacities Vanish at Absolute Zero
> For any constraint $x$, the heat capacity is $C_x = T(\partial S/\partial T)_x$, and
>
> $$
> S(T, x) = S(0, x) + \int_0^T \frac{C_x(T')}{T'}\,dT' .
> $$
>
> If the entropy has a finite limit as $T \to 0$, this improper integral converges; in particular, if $C_x$ has a limit as $T \to 0$, the limit is zero, and a power law $C_x \propto T^{m}$ must have $m > 0$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §18.2, eqs. (18.1)–(18.5) · Schroeder §3.2, eqs. (3.21)–(3.22) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §9.3*

^thm-b2-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Along a reversible step at constant $x$, $đQ_{\text{rev}} = T\,dS = T(\partial S/\partial T)_x\,dT$ ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]), so by [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]] $C_x = T(\partial S/\partial T)_x$. Hence $(\partial S/\partial T)_x = C_x/T$, and integrating from $\varepsilon$ to $T$ and letting $\varepsilon \to 0$ gives the formula, with an improper integral at $0$ ([[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-1|451 Def. §36.1]]) that converges exactly when $S(0, x)$ is finite. If $C_x(T') \to c > 0$, then $C_x(T')/T' > c/(2T')$ for small $T'$, and $\int_0 dT'/T'$ diverges like $-\ln\varepsilon$, so $S(0, x) = -\infty$: impossible. The same comparison excludes $C_x \propto T^m$ with $m \le 0$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.2: Thermal Expansion and Pressure Coefficient Vanish
> For a fluid obeying Nernst's form of the third law, as $T \to 0$
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V \to 0, \qquad \alpha = \frac1V\Bigl(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Bigr)_p \to 0 ,
> $$
>
> assuming, as Blundell does, that the limit $T \to 0$ may be taken inside the $x$-derivatives of $S$, and, on the route below, that the compressibility $\kappa_T$ stays finite (the Maxwell-relation route of [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^ex-b3-2-3|Example §B3.2.3]] does without this).
>
> *Source: Blundell §18.2, eqs. (18.6)–(18.7)*

^thm-b2-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the energy equation, $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial p/\partial T)_V$ ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]). Nernst's form says that $S(T, V) - S(T, V')$ tends to $0$ for every pair of volumes, i.e. $S(0, V)$ does not depend on $V$; with the interchange of limits, $(\partial S/\partial V)_T \to 0$, hence $(\partial p/\partial T)_V \to 0$. By the reciprocity theorem $(\partial p/\partial T)_V = \alpha/\kappa_T$ ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^rem-b1-2-1|Remark: Why heat capacities of liquids are measured at constant pressure]]), so $\alpha = \kappa_T(\partial p/\partial T)_V \to 0$ when $\kappa_T$ is finite. Blundell argues directly with the Maxwell relation $(\partial S/\partial p)_T = -(\partial V/\partial T)_p$ ([[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]]), which gives $\alpha \to 0$ without the assumption on $\kappa_T$; that argument, made quantitative for $C_p \propto T^n$, is [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions#^ex-b3-2-3|Example §B3.2.3]].

^der-b2-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]], [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]], [[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-3|Principle §A4.6.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: No classical ideal gas near absolute zero
> The classical ideal gas violates every consequence above. Its entropy $nC_V\ln T + nR\ln(V/n) + \text{const}$ ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]]) tends to $-\infty$; its $C_V$ is a nonzero constant; $C_p - C_V = nR$ cannot hold if both vanish; and its expansivity $\alpha = 1/T$ diverges instead of vanishing. So the model must fail at low temperature, not because of a small correction but in principle: the molecules' interactions, and above all quantum mechanics, take over. The degenerate quantum gases are the correct low-temperature replacements ([[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases]], [[§B12.1 Bose–Einstein Condensation]], [[§B13.1 The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals]]). The same logic rules out the classical equipartition values of $C_V$ as $T \to 0$ ([[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]]) and Curie's law for a paramagnet ([[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy#^law-b6-2-10|Law §B6.2.10]]; [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^rem-b3-4-3|§B3.4]]).
>
> *Source: Blundell §18.2 · Schroeder §3.2 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 5, eq. (44) (α = 1/T) · PHY 306, Midterm, Problem 3*

## Unattainability of absolute zero

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.3: Unattainability Is Equivalent to Nernst's Form
> Consider a system with a parameter $x$, whose heat capacity $C_x$ is positive for $T > 0$ and whose entropy $S(T, x)$ is continuous, with a limit $S(0, x)$ as $T \to 0$. Cooling proceeds by steps of two kinds: isothermal changes of $x$ in contact with a reservoir, and adiabatic changes of $x$ with the system thermally isolated.
> 1. If $S(0, x)$ is the same for all $x$ (Nernst), no finite sequence of such steps starting at $T > 0$ reaches $T = 0$.
> 2. If $S(0, x_1) < S(0, x_2)$ for some $x_1$, $x_2$, and $S(T, x_1)$ exceeds $S(0, x_2)$ at some temperature, then a single reversible adiabatic change from $x_1$ to $x_2$, started at a suitable temperature $T_1 > 0$, ends at $T = 0$.
>
> So absolute zero is unattainable in finitely many steps exactly when Nernst's form holds: the two forms of [[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-3|Principle §A4.6.3]] are equivalent.
>
> *Source: Blundell §18.2 (unattainability, argued from a diagram) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §9.3*

^thm-b2-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Part 1.* An isothermal step does not change $T$, and heat can only be drawn off into a colder reservoir, so a step that ends at $T = 0$ from $T_1 > 0$ must be adiabatic. Let it take the state $(T_1, x_1)$ to $(0, x_2)$. No heat enters, so by [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]], reversible or not, $S(0, x_2) \ge S(T_1, x_1)$. But by [[§B2.3 The Third Law#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]] and $C_{x_1} > 0$,
>
> $$
> S(T_1, x_1) = S(0, x_1) + \int_0^{T_1}\frac{C_{x_1}}{T}\,dT > S(0, x_1) = S(0, x_2) ,
> $$
>
> a contradiction. So every step ends at a positive temperature, and so does any finite sequence of steps. Each step can still lower $T$, but by ever smaller amounts as the curves $S(T, x)$ for different $x$ crowd together near $T = 0$ (figure below).
>
> *Part 2.* $S(T, x_1)$ increases continuously from $S(0, x_1)$ as $T$ rises ($\partial S/\partial T = C_{x_1}/T > 0$). By hypothesis it rises above $S(0, x_2)$ at some temperature (it does for any system whose entropy keeps growing with $T$), so the intermediate value theorem gives $T_1 > 0$ with $S(T_1, x_1) = S(0, x_2)$. A reversible adiabatic change from $x_1$ to $x_2$ keeps $S$ fixed ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]) and so ends at a state with $S(T, x_2) = S(0, x_2)$; since $S(\cdot, x_2)$ is strictly increasing, $T = 0$.

The curves are the entropy $S/Nk = \ln(2\cosh x) - x\tanh x$, $x = \mu B/kT$, of the ideal two-state paramagnet ([[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^thm-b3-4-6|Theorem §B3.4.6]]; one magnetic cooling cycle with real numbers is [[§B3.4 Rods, Bubbles and Magnets#^ex-b3-4-3|Example §B3.4.3]]). > [!remark]- ★ Remark: The third law is not a consequence of the second
> A Carnot engine with a cold reservoir at $T_c = 0$ would have efficiency $1$ and violate Kelvin–Planck, which suggests that unattainability follows from the second law (level A reads the same observation the other way: the third law closes this loophole, [[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^rem-a4-6-3|Remark: What the third law adds]]). Blundell's objection: an isothermal process at $T = 0$ cannot be carried out, since any change of state warms the system, so the engine cannot be built and the argument fails. The third law is a separate principle; its origin is quantum mechanical (next remark).
>
> *Source: Blundell §18.2 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 5, §5*

^rem-b2-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: The statistical view
> With $S = k\ln\Omega$ ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^pr-a4-6-1|Principle §A4.6.1]]; at level B the definition of statistical entropy, [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]]), a system at $T = 0$ sits in its quantum ground level, and $S_0 = k\ln g_0$ with $g_0$ the ground-state degeneracy. Planck's $S_0 = 0$ means a non-degenerate ground state; Schwartz's weaker "$S$ tends to a constant" allows $g_0 > 1$. Since $k\ln g_0$ is of order $k$ unless $g_0$ grows exponentially with the number of particles, it is negligible next to entropies of order $Nk$; residual entropies of order $Nk$, as in [[§B2.3 The Third Law#^ex-b2-3-1|Example §B2.3.1]], belong to frozen, non-equilibrium arrangements. Classical statistical mechanics, with a continuum of states, gives $S \to -\infty$ instead, as for the ideal gas: the third law is a quantum-mechanical statement, and Schwartz returns to it with quantum statistics (Lectures 10 onward; [[§B10.2 Quantum and Classical Ideal Gases]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 181, Lecture 5, §1 · Blundell §18.1 · Schroeder §3.2*

## Examples

> [!example]- ★ Example §B2.3.1: Residual Entropy of Carbon Monoxide
> In solid carbon monoxide each linear molecule can sit in the lattice in two orientations, CO or OC, with almost the same energy. On cooling, the orientations freeze in at random long before the crystal could find the ordered ground state. Treating the $N_A$ orientations in a mole as independent and random, the number of arrangements is $2^{N_A}$, so the entropy left at $T \to 0$ is
>
> $$
> S_{\text{res}} = k\ln 2^{N_A} = N_Ak\ln 2 = R\ln 2 = 5.76\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)} .
> $$
>
> Calorimetry, which integrates $C_p/T$ upward from an assumed $S = 0$, misses this amount: the crystal never reached $S = 0$. The law is not violated, because the frozen crystal is not in internal equilibrium ([[§B2.3 The Third Law#^cau-b2-3-1|Caution: Internal equilibrium, and residual entropy]]). The number is the entropy of mixing of equal amounts of two "species" ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-8|Theorem §B2.2.8]]), here the two orientations.
>
> *Source: Schroeder, Problem 3.8 (and §3.2)*

^ex-b2-3-1

> [!example] Example §B2.3.2: Entropy of a Solid from Its Low-Temperature Heat Capacity
> At low temperature the heat capacity of an insulating crystal follows $C = aT^3$ (the Debye law, [[§B11.2 Phonons and the Debye Model#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]]). By [[§B2.3 The Third Law#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]] with $S(0) = 0$,
>
> $$
> S(T) = \int_0^T \frac{aT'^3}{T'}\,dT' = \frac{aT^3}{3} = \frac{C(T)}{3} .
> $$
>
> The integral converges because $C/T = aT^2 \to 0$, and the entropy vanishes like the heat capacity itself, as $T^3$. Had $C$ stayed at its classical value $3R$ per mole (Dulong–Petit, [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-3|Theorem §A2.3.3]]), the same integral would have diverged at its lower limit. This is how tabulated absolute entropies are made: measure $C_p$ down to as low a temperature as possible, extrapolate with the $T^3$ law, integrate $C_p/T$ and add $L/T$ for each phase change ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-4|Theorem §A4.5.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schroeder §3.2, eqs. (3.21)–(3.22) · Blundell §18.1, eqs. (18.1)–(18.3)*

^ex-b2-3-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The integral $\int_0^T C/T'\,dT'$ is an improper Riemann integral at its lower end ([[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-1|451 Def. §36.1]]); the third law is the statement that it converges.
> - The vanishing of response functions at $T = 0$ uses the energy equation ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]) and, more generally, the Maxwell relations of [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions]].
> - Low-temperature heat capacities computed from quantum statistics, all vanishing at $T = 0$ as the third law requires: two-level systems ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]]), the Debye $T^3$ law ([[§B11.2 Phonons and the Debye Model#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]]), the linear electronic heat capacity of metals ([[§B13.1 The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-4|Theorem §B13.1.4]]).
