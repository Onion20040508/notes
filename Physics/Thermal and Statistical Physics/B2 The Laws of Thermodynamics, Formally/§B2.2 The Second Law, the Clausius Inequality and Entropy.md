Level A stated the second law as two principles, about heat flow ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]]) and about engines ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^pr-a4-3-1|Principle §A4.3.1]]), derived Carnot's theorem and efficiency ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^thm-a4-3-3|Theorem §A4.3.3]], [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]]), and built the entropy from $\oint đQ/T = 0$ for reversible cycles of a gas ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]], [[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]). Two steps were left open there: the strip argument covered only cycles of a two-variable system, and $\Delta S_{\text{total}} \ge 0$ was checked only in special cases ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-5|Theorem §A4.5.5]]). This section closes both with one theorem, the Clausius inequality, proved from the Kelvin–Planck principle for any cycle of any system. From it follow the existence of entropy as a state function of every system, $dS \ge đQ/T$, the entropy maximum of isolated systems, the fundamental relation $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$, and the equation that fixes $(\partial U/\partial V)_T$, which the first law left open ([[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^rem-b2-1-1|§B2.1]]).

## The Clausius inequality

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.1: Clausius Inequality
> Let a system run through a cycle, reversible or not, during which it receives heats $đQ_i$ (positive or negative) from heat reservoirs at absolute temperatures $T_i$. Then
>
> $$
> \sum_{\text{cycle}} \frac{đQ_i}{T_i} \le 0, \qquad \text{in the continuum limit} \qquad \oint \frac{đQ}{T} \le 0 .
> $$
>
> If the cycle is reversible, equality holds: $\oint đQ_{\text{rev}}/T = 0$. Here $T$ is the absolute (thermodynamic) temperature, the one defined by Carnot ratios, and the theorem holds wherever the Kelvin–Planck principle does.
>
> *Source: Blundell §13.7, eqs. (13.27)–(13.34) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §6.2*

^thm-b2-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Bring in one more reservoir, at an arbitrary temperature $T_0 > 0$, and for each $i$ a reversible Carnot engine $C_i$ working between $T_0$ and $T_i$ ([[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^mod-a4-4-1|Model §A4.4.1]]). Size and run $C_i$ so that it delivers to the reservoir at $T_i$ exactly the heat $đQ_i$ that reservoir gives the system (if $đQ_i < 0$, $C_i$ runs backwards, as a refrigerator, and removes it). For a reversible engine the heats are in the ratio of the absolute temperatures ([[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]]; [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^rem-a4-4-3|Remark: A temperature scale independent of any substance]]), so $C_i$ draws from $T_0$ the heat
>
> $$
> đQ_{0,i} = \frac{T_0}{T_i}\,đQ_i .
> $$
>
> Consider the composite of the system, the engines $C_i$ and the reservoirs $T_i$, over one cycle of the system:
> - the system and every $C_i$ are back in their initial states;
> - each reservoir $T_i$ gave $đQ_i$ to the system and received $đQ_i$ from $C_i$: no net change;
> - the reservoir $T_0$ gave the net heat $Q_0 = T_0\sum_i đQ_i/T_i$.
>
> By the first law the composite has delivered net work $W = Q_0$ to the outside. If $Q_0 > 0$, its sole effect would be to turn heat from the single reservoir $T_0$ completely into work, which the Kelvin–Planck principle ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^pr-a4-3-1|Principle §A4.3.1]]) forbids. Hence $Q_0 \le 0$, and since $T_0 > 0$, $\sum_i đQ_i/T_i \le 0$.
>
> If the cycle is reversible, run it backwards: every $đQ_i$ changes sign at the same $T_i$, and the inequality gives $-\sum_i đQ_i/T_i \le 0$ as well. So the sum vanishes.

^der-b2-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^pr-a4-3-1|Principle §A4.3.1]], [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^mod-a4-4-1|Model §A4.4.1]], [[§A4.4 The Carnot Cycle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]], [[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]], [[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Whose temperature?
> $T_i$ is the temperature of the **reservoir** that supplies $đQ_i$, not of the system. During an irreversible step the system may not even have a temperature. In a reversible step heat is exchanged only across an infinitesimal temperature difference ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^mod-a4-1-1|Model §A4.1.1]]), so the two temperatures agree, and in the equality case $T$ may be read as the system's own temperature.

^cau-b2-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: What the theorem adds to level A
> - Level A obtained $\oint đQ/T = 0$ for reversible cycles of a gas by approximating the cycle with Carnot strips ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^der-a4-5-2|derivation of Theorem §A4.5.2]]). Here it is the equality case of a theorem valid for any system with any number of state variables, and no approximation of paths is needed: the auxiliary Carnot engines are attached to the reservoirs, not fitted to the path. What was a theorem with a sketched proof at level A is now proved.
> - Level A remarked that the general entropy statement "needs the inequality $\oint dQ/T \le 0$ for an arbitrary cycle containing irreversible steps" ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^der-a4-5-5|derivation of Theorem §A4.5.5]]). This is that inequality; [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]] draws the conclusion.
> - The proof used only Kelvin–Planck. Since Clausius's principle is equivalent to it ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]), either principle implies the inequality; conversely the inequality implies both (a perfect engine would have $\oint đQ/T = Q/T_0 > 0$).

## Entropy for every system

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.2: Entropy Is a State Function of Every System
> Let the equilibrium states of a system form a connected region in which any two states can be joined by a reversible path. Then $1/T$ is an integrating factor of the reversible heat ([[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]]): the integral $\int_A^B đQ_{\text{rev}}/T$ is the same along every reversible path from $A$ to $B$, and the entropy
>
> $$
> S(B) = S(A_0) + \int_{A_0}^{B}\frac{đQ_{\text{rev}}}{T}, \qquad dS = \frac{đQ_{\text{rev}}}{T},
> $$
>
> is a state function, defined up to the constant $S(A_0)$ ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]). For a system made of parts each in internal equilibrium, entropies add: $dS = \sum_k đQ_{\text{rev},k}/T_k$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §14.1, eqs. (14.1)–(14.2) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 5, §2.3 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §5.2, §6.2*

^thm-b2-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Two reversible paths from $A$ to $B$, the second one run backwards, form a reversible cycle, on which $\oint đQ_{\text{rev}}/T = 0$ by [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]. Reversing a reversible path changes the sign of each $đQ_{\text{rev}}$ at the same $T$, so the two path integrals are equal. By [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], applied to the form $đQ_{\text{rev}}/T$ on the region of states, the form is exact and $S$ as defined is a state function with $dS = đQ_{\text{rev}}/T$. For the parts, the heat entering the composite is the sum of the heats entering the parts, each at its own temperature in a reversible step.

^der-b2-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]], [[§A4.5 Entropy#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.3: Clausius's Form of the Second Law; Entropy Increase
> For any process between equilibrium states $A$ and $B$ in which the system receives heat $đQ$ from reservoirs at temperatures $T$,
>
> $$
> S(B) - S(A) \ge \int_A^B\frac{đQ}{T}, \qquad \text{for a small step} \qquad dS \ge \frac{đQ}{T},
> $$
>
> with equality for a reversible process. In particular, for a thermally isolated system ($đQ = 0$),
>
> $$
> \Delta S \ge 0 :
> $$
>
> the entropy of an isolated system never decreases; it stays constant in reversible processes and grows in the irreversible ones.
>
> *Source: Blundell §14.2, eqs. (14.5)–(14.9) · Schroeder §3.4, eq. (3.51) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 5, §2.3; Lecture 6, §1 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §6.3*

^thm-b2-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Follow the actual process from $A$ to $B$ and return along a reversible path from $B$ to $A$. The result is a cycle, so [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]] gives
>
> $$
> \int_A^B\frac{đQ}{T} + \int_B^A\frac{đQ_{\text{rev}}}{T} \le 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int_A^B\frac{đQ}{T} \le \int_A^B\frac{đQ_{\text{rev}}}{T} = S(B) - S(A)
> $$
>
> by [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]. If the process is itself reversible, the cycle is reversible and equality holds. With $đQ = 0$ throughout, $\Delta S \ge 0$. An isolated system is in particular thermally isolated, and so is "system plus surroundings" when everything that interacts is included, which is level A's $\Delta S_{\text{total}} \ge 0$ ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-5|Theorem §A4.5.5]]), now for any process.
>
> That the inequality is strict for every irreversible process is how Schroeder and Schwartz characterise irreversibility: a process that creates entropy cannot be undone without creating at least as much again elsewhere, while one with $\Delta S_{\text{total}} = 0$ can be run backwards without violating the inequality.

^der-b2-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]], [[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: No heat, yet more entropy
> In a free (Joule) expansion of a gas into a vacuum no heat flows, no work is done and $U$ is unchanged ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-1|Theorem §A3.6.1]]); for an ideal gas doubling its volume, nevertheless, $\Delta S = nR\ln 2$ ([[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^ex-a4-6-2|Example §A4.6.2]]). There is no paradox: $dS = đQ/T$ holds only for reversible steps, and here $đQ = 0 < T\,dS$, exactly as [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]] allows. Schroeder's phrase: the entropy was not carried in by heat but "manufactured on the spot". Compressing the gas back reversibly and isothermally costs the work $nRT\ln 2 = T\,\Delta S$, which then leaves as heat into the surroundings.
>
> *Source: Blundell §14.4 (A paradox?) · Schroeder §2.6 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 5, §3.3*

## The fundamental relation

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.4: Fundamental Thermodynamic Relation
> For a closed fluid, between any two neighbouring equilibrium states,
>
> $$
> dU = T\,dS - p\,dV ,
> $$
>
> whatever the process connecting them. Hence $U$ is naturally a function of $(S, V)$, and
>
> $$
> T = \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\Bigr)_V, \quad p = -\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_S, \qquad \frac1T = \Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial U}\Bigr)_V, \quad \frac pT = \Bigl(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\Bigr)_U .
> $$
>
> In a step that is not reversible, $đQ$ and $đW$ separately differ from $T\,dS$ and $p\,dV$, by the same amount: $T\,dS - đQ = p\,dV - đW \ge 0$ (when heat enters at the system's own temperature).
>
> *Source: Blundell §14.3, eqs. (14.14)–(14.23) · Schroeder §3.4, eqs. (3.46)–(3.49) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §6.4*

^thm-b2-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In a reversible step $đQ = T\,dS$ ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]) and $đW = p\,dV$ ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]), so the first law gives $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$. Reversible steps exist in every direction $(dS, dV)$ from a state, so comparing with $dU = (\partial U/\partial S)_V\,dS + (\partial U/\partial V)_S\,dV$ identifies the coefficients: $(\partial U/\partial S)_V = T$ and $(\partial U/\partial V)_S = -p$. Now $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$ is a relation among state functions of the two end states, so it holds for any process joining them (Blundell's argument). The reciprocal theorem ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]]) gives $(\partial S/\partial U)_V = 1/T$, and the cyclic rule $(\partial S/\partial V)_U = -(\partial S/\partial U)_V(\partial U/\partial V)_S = p/T$. Finally the first law $dU = đQ - đW$ for the actual step, subtracted from the relation, gives $T\,dS - đQ = p\,dV - đW$; the left side is $\ge 0$ by [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]] when the heat enters at the system's temperature.

^der-b2-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]], [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]], [[§A3.3 The First Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]], [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Valid always, but not term by term
> Blundell's summary is worth memorising: $dU = đQ - đW$ and $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$ are always true; $đQ = T\,dS$ and $đW = p\,dV$ are true only for reversible changes. In a free expansion $đQ = đW = 0$ while $T\,dS = p\,dV > 0$. Schroeder adds a second example: a piston struck so hard that it moves faster than the molecules does more work on the gas than $-p\,dV$, and the gas gains extra entropy, $dS > đQ/T$. (The sources state this with work done *on* the system, $đW_{\text{on}} \ge -p\,dV$; that is $đW \le p\,dV$ here.)
>
> *Source: Blundell §14.3, summary table · Schroeder §3.4, eq. (3.51)*

## Maximum entropy

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.5: Entropy Maximum of an Isolated System
> An isolated system ([[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]]) left to itself after an internal constraint is released (a partition removed, a wall made conducting or movable) evolves until its entropy is the largest compatible with the remaining constraints; equilibrium is a state of maximum entropy at fixed $U$, $V$ and amount of matter. For two parts that can exchange energy and volume, maximum entropy requires
>
> $$
> T_1 = T_2, \qquad p_1 = p_2 ,
> $$
>
> and on the way there energy flows from the hotter part to the colder, and at equal temperatures volume is gained by the part at higher pressure.
>
> *Source: Blundell §14.2, Example 14.2 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §6.5 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 5, §2*

^thm-b2-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]] every spontaneous change of an isolated system has $\Delta S \ge 0$. A state from which a spontaneous change to a state of higher entropy is possible is therefore not final; the evolution stops only when no reachable state has more entropy, i.e. at a maximum of $S$ over the states allowed by the constraints.
>
> For the two parts, $U_1 + U_2$ and $V_1 + V_2$ are fixed, so $dU_2 = -dU_1$ and $dV_2 = -dV_1$, and with $dS_k = dU_k/T_k + (p_k/T_k)\,dV_k$ ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]) and additivity ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]),
>
> $$
> dS = dS_1 + dS_2 = \Bigl(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\Bigr)dU_1 + \Bigl(\frac{p_1}{T_1} - \frac{p_2}{T_2}\Bigr)dV_1 .
> $$
>
> At a maximum $dS = 0$ for all $dU_1$, $dV_1$ ([[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-1|452 Thm. §14.1]]), so both brackets vanish: $T_1 = T_2$, then $p_1 = p_2$. Away from equilibrium $dS > 0$: if $T_1 < T_2$ the first bracket is positive and $dU_1 > 0$, energy flows into the colder part; if $T_1 = T_2$ and $p_1 > p_2$, then $dV_1 > 0$.

^der-b2-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]], [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]], [[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-1|452 Thm. §14.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The statistical reading
> Statistical mechanics turns the maximum principle around: $S = k\ln\Omega$ counts microstates, the macrostate with most microstates is overwhelmingly the most probable, and "entropy increases" means "the system wanders into the biggest region of its state space" (Schwartz, Lecture 5, §2; level A's [[§A4.6 Entropy on a Microscopic Scale#^rem-a4-6-2|Remark: Why a logarithm]]). There $1/T = \partial S/\partial U$ becomes the *definition* of temperature and the equilibrium conditions above are derived from counting ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium]], [[§B6.2 Temperature, Pressure and Chemical Potential from Entropy]]); with exchange of particles a third condition, equal chemical potentials, joins them ([[§B3.3 Chemical Potential and Conditions for Equilibrium]]).

## The energy equation and the entropy of a fluid

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.6: Energy Equation
> For a closed fluid with equation of state $p(T, V)$ and heat capacity $C_V$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T = T\Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V - p, \qquad dS = \frac{C_V}{T}\,dT + \Bigl(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V dV .
> $$
>
> Consequences:
> 1. $C_p - C_V = T\,(\partial p/\partial T)_V\,(\partial V/\partial T)_p$, a property of the equation of state alone;
> 2. $(\partial C_V/\partial V)_T = T\,(\partial^2 p/\partial T^2)_V$;
> 3. for any gas obeying $pV = nRT$, with $T$ the absolute temperature, $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = 0$: its internal energy depends on $T$ alone.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Midterm, Problems 1 and 6 · Blundell §11.3, eq. (11.18); Exercise 14.7 · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §6.4*

^thm-b2-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]] and the heat of a quasi-static step ([[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]]),
>
> $$
> dS = \frac{đQ_{\text{rev}}}{T} = \frac1T\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\Bigr)_V dT + \frac1T\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\Bigr)_T + p\Bigr] dV
> $$
>
> is exact, so its coefficients pass the test of [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]. With $U_T$, $U_V$, $p_T$ for the partials,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial}{\partial V}\Bigl(\frac{U_T}{T}\Bigr) = \frac{U_{TV}}{T}, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial T}\Bigl(\frac{U_V + p}{T}\Bigr) = -\frac{U_V + p}{T^2} + \frac{U_{VT} + p_T}{T} .
> $$
>
> The mixed partials of $U$ are equal ([[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]) and cancel, leaving $U_V + p = Tp_T$: the energy equation. Substituting it back, the coefficient of $dV$ in $dS$ is $p_T$, and the coefficient of $dT$ is $C_V/T$ ([[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]).
>
> 1. Insert $U_V + p = Tp_T$ into $C_p - C_V = (U_V + p)(\partial V/\partial T)_p$ ([[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]).
> 2. Differentiate the energy equation with respect to $T$ at fixed $V$: $U_{VT} = p_T + Tp_{TT} - p_T = Tp_{TT}$, and $U_{VT} = U_{TV} = (\partial C_V/\partial V)_T$. (PHY 306 obtains the same from $C_V = T(\partial S/\partial T)_V$ and a Maxwell relation.)
> 3. With $p = nRT/V$, $Tp_T - p = nRT/V - p = 0$.

> [!remark] Remark: What changed since level A
> Level A took "the internal energy of an ideal gas depends on $T$ only" from kinetic theory ([[§A3.2 Work, Heat and Internal Energy#^rem-a3-2-3|Remark: Internal energy of an ideal gas]]). Consequence 3 derives it from the equation of state and the second law: a gas cannot obey $pV = nRT$ with the absolute temperature unless its energy is independent of volume. More generally the second law converts the unknown $(\partial U/\partial V)_T$ of [[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^rem-b2-1-1|§B2.1]] into a measurable property of $p(T, V)$, and $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial p/\partial T)_V$ is the first Maxwell relation, here obtained directly from the exactness of $dS$; the full set, from the potentials, is in [[§B3.2 Maxwell Relations and Response Functions|§B3.2]].

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.7: Entropy of an Ideal Gas
> For $n$ moles of an ideal gas with constant molar heat capacities $C_V$, $C_p = C_V + R$,
>
> $$
> S = nC_V\ln T + nR\ln V + \text{const} = nC_p\ln T - nR\ln p + \text{const}' .
> $$
>
> If $S$ is also required to be extensive ([[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^def-a3-1-3|Def. §A3.1.3]]), the dependence on the amount of gas is fixed up to one constant $s_0$ per mole:
>
> $$
> S(T, V, n) = n\Bigl[C_V\ln T + R\ln\frac Vn + s_0\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Thermodynamics cannot determine $s_0$, and the formula fails as $T \to 0$, where it gives $S \to -\infty$ ([[§B2.3 The Third Law|§B2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Blundell §18.2 (S = C_V ln T + R ln V + const) · Schwartz 181, Lecture 5, eqs. (28)–(29) · Schroeder §2.6 (V/N inside the logarithm) · the user's Stat Mech concepts notes, §9.3*

^thm-b2-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]] with $(\partial p/\partial T)_V = nR/V$, $dS = nC_V\,dT/T + nR\,dV/V$; integrate (the region $T, V > 0$ is convex, so path independence is guaranteed, [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]). With $V = nRT/p$, $nR\ln V = nR\ln T - nR\ln p + \text{const}$, and $C_V + R = C_p$ gives the second form.
>
> *Extensivity.* For fixed $n$ the constant may still depend on $n$: $S = nC_V\ln T + nR\ln V + c(n)$. Scaling $V \to \lambda V$, $n \to \lambda n$ at fixed $T$ must multiply $S$ by $\lambda$:
>
> $$
> \lambda\,nC_V\ln T + \lambda nR\ln(\lambda V) + c(\lambda n) = \lambda\bigl[nC_V\ln T + nR\ln V + c(n)\bigr] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c(\lambda n) = \lambda\,c(n) - \lambda nR\ln\lambda .
> $$
>
> Setting $n = 1$ and renaming $\lambda$ as $n$: $c(n) = n\,c(1) - nR\ln n$, which is the stated form with $s_0 = c(1)$.

^der-b2-2-7

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]], [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]], [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]], [[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^def-a3-1-3|Def. §A3.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Layer change from level A
> Level A gave the entropy *changes* of an ideal gas between two states ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-4|Theorem §A4.5.4]]). Here $S$ is a function on the whole space of states, including its dependence on the amount of gas. The $V/n$ inside the logarithm is what thermodynamics can say about it; Schwartz's Lecture 5 form, $Nk\ln V + C_V\ln(E/N) + \text{const}$, puts all the $N$-dependence into the constant, and Lecture 6 restores the $V/N$ through indistinguishability. The value of $s_0$ needs quantum mechanics and counting: the Sackur–Tetrode formula ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas]]); why $V/n$ and not $V$, the Gibbs paradox ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox]]).

## Free expansion and mixing

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.8: Entropy of Mixing of Ideal Gases
> Let $k$ different ideal gases, $n_i$ moles of gas $i$ with mole fraction $x_i = n_i/n$, $n = \sum_i n_i$, be held in separate compartments of volumes $x_iV$ at a common temperature $T$ and pressure $p$. When the partitions are removed and the gases mix in the total volume $V$ at the same $T$, the entropy increases by
>
> $$
> \Delta S_{\text{mix}} = -nR\sum_i x_i\ln x_i > 0 .
> $$
>
> For two gases in equal amounts, $\Delta S_{\text{mix}} = nR\ln 2$, the largest value for two gases at given $n$. If the "different" gases are in fact the same gas, removing the partitions changes nothing and $\Delta S = 0$.
>
> *Source: Blundell §14.6, eqs. (14.39)–(14.40) · Schroeder §2.6, eqs. (2.53)–(2.54), Problem 2.38 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 6, §3*

^thm-b2-2-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Mixing in an insulated box at constant total volume does no work and exchanges no heat, so $U$, hence (for ideal gases) $T$, is unchanged; the process is irreversible, and its $\Delta S$ is found along a reversible path between the same end states ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^cau-a4-5-1|Caution: Entropy changes of irreversible processes]]). Ideal gases do not interact, so in the mixture each gas fills $V$ as if the others were absent, with its own partial pressure ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-5|Theorem §A2.2.5]]). A reversible route: let each gas expand isothermally from $x_iV$ to $V$ against a piston that is permeable to all the other gases but not to gas $i$ (Schwartz's semi-permeable membrane), with heat supplied from a reservoir at $T$. By [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]] gas $i$ gains
>
> $$
> \Delta S_i = n_iR\ln\frac{V}{x_iV} = -n_iR\ln x_i ,
> $$
>
> and the total is $\sum_i\Delta S_i = -nR\sum_i x_i\ln x_i$. Each $x_i < 1$, so every term is positive. For two gases, $-x\ln x - (1 - x)\ln(1 - x)$ has derivative $\ln\bigl((1 - x)/x\bigr)$, zero only at $x = \tfrac12$, where it equals $\ln 2$. For one and the same gas the state after removing the partition is the state before (same $T$, $p$, $n/V$ everywhere), and reinserting the partition restores it: the change is reversible and $\Delta S = 0$.

^der-b2-2-8

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]], [[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-5|Theorem §A2.2.5]], [[§A4.5 Entropy#^cau-a4-5-1|§A4.5]], [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: "Different" must mean distinguishable
> The mixing entropy jumps from $nR\ln 2$ to $0$ as two gases become identical, however similar they were before; it does not shrink gradually. Thermodynamics records this but cannot explain it. The explanation, that identical molecules are indistinguishable and that this is what the $V/n$ of [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]] encodes, is the Gibbs paradox ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox]]). The mixing entropy is real: a semi-permeable membrane between the two gases feels an osmotic pressure that can do work (Schwartz, Lecture 6, §3).
>
> *Source: Blundell §14.6 · Schroeder §2.6 · Schwartz 181, Lecture 6, §3–3.1*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.2.1: A van der Waals Gas Expanding
> $n$ moles of a van der Waals gas, $\bigl(p + n^2a/V^2\bigr)(V - nb) = nRT$ ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-4|Law §A2.1.4]]), go from volume $V$ to $\alpha V$ ($\alpha > 1$), starting at temperature $T$, (a) by a reversible isothermal expansion, (b) by a free expansion into a vacuum.
>
> *Ingredients.* $p = nRT/(V - nb) - n^2a/V^2$, so $(\partial p/\partial T)_V = nR/(V - nb)$ and $(\partial^2p/\partial T^2)_V = 0$. By [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]: $C_V$ does not depend on $V$ (consequence 2; take it constant), and $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = nRT/(V - nb) - p = n^2a/V^2$, so
>
> $$
> U(T, V) = C_VT - \frac{n^2a}{V} + \text{const}, \qquad dS = \frac{C_V}{T}\,dT + \frac{nR}{V - nb}\,dV .
> $$
>
> *(a) Reversible isothermal.* $\Delta S = \int_V^{\alpha V}\frac{nR\,dV'}{V' - nb} = nR\ln\dfrac{\alpha V - nb}{V - nb}$. It does not depend on $a$, and for $b = 0$ it is the ideal-gas $nR\ln\alpha$; the excluded volume makes it larger, since the "free" volume grows by a bigger factor. The heat absorbed is $Q = T\,\Delta S$.
>
> *(b) Free expansion.* $U$ is unchanged ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-1|Theorem §A3.6.1]]), so $C_V(T_f - T) = n^2a\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\alpha V} - \tfrac1V\bigr)$:
>
> $$
> T_f - T = -\frac{n^2a}{C_VV}\,\frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha} < 0 .
> $$
>
> The gas cools, by an amount proportional to $(\alpha - 1)/\alpha$, because the molecules climb out of each other's attraction (compare [[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^rem-a3-6-1|Remark: Free expansion of real gases]]). Its entropy change, along any reversible path to the same final state, is
>
> $$
> \Delta S = C_V\ln\frac{T_f}{T} + nR\ln\frac{\alpha V - nb}{V - nb} ,
> $$
>
> the ideal-type volume term reduced by the cooling term. It must be positive, since no heat entered ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]); to first order in $a$ the cooling term is $-\tfrac{n^2a}{TV}\tfrac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha}$, small compared with the volume term for a dilute gas.
>
> *Source: PHY 306, Midterm, Problem 1 (and its solution) · Blundell, Exercise 14.7*

^ex-b2-2-1

> [!example] Example §B2.2.2: The Most Work from Two Hot Bodies
> Two bodies of constant heat capacities $C_h$, $C_c$ start at $T_h > T_c$. A heat engine runs between them, using them as finite reservoirs, until they reach a common temperature $T_f$. How much work can it deliver?
>
> The engine works in cycles, so its entropy does not change, and the work is the energy the bodies lose:
>
> $$
> W = C_h(T_h - T_f) - C_c(T_f - T_c) = C_hT_h + C_cT_c - (C_h + C_c)\,T_f .
> $$
>
> $W$ is largest when $T_f$ is smallest. The total entropy change is $\Delta S = C_h\ln(T_f/T_h) + C_c\ln(T_f/T_c)$, which must be $\ge 0$ ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]) and increases with $T_f$. So the smallest allowed $T_f$, and the most work, is reached by a reversible engine, with $\Delta S = 0$:
>
> $$
> T_f^{\,C_h + C_c} = T_h^{\,C_h}\,T_c^{\,C_c}, \qquad \text{for } C_h = C_c = C: \quad T_f = \sqrt{T_hT_c}, \quad W_{\max} = C\bigl(\sqrt{T_h} - \sqrt{T_c}\bigr)^2 .
> $$
>
> For the two steel blocks of [[§A4.5 Entropy#^ex-a4-5-2|Example §A4.5.2]] ($C = 1800\ \mathrm{J/K}$, $T_h = 400\ \mathrm K$, $T_c = 300\ \mathrm K$): $T_f = 346.4\ \mathrm K$ and $W_{\max} = 1800\,(700 - 692.8)\ \mathrm J = 12.9\ \mathrm{kJ}$. Put in direct contact instead, the blocks end at $350\ \mathrm K$, deliver no work and create $37\ \mathrm{J/K}$ of entropy; the $12.9\ \mathrm{kJ}$ is exactly the extra internal energy, $2C\,(350 - 346.4)\ \mathrm K$, that they keep. Every intermediate, partly irreversible, operation lands in between.
>
> *Source: Blundell, Example 13.5; Exercise 13.7*
