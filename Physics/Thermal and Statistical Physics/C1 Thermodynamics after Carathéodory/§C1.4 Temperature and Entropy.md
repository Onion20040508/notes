(1.5)–(1.7) · PHY 510 Lectures 9/3, 9/8 · the composite-system argument of [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]] is the "standard argument" Mao cites to Adkins and Callen without giving it; it is written out here.*

So far $\Sigma$ is a label and $\Theta$ an unnamed function ([[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]]). Neither has a physical meaning, and $\Theta$ depends on how the leaves were labelled. Meaning comes from thermal contact: two systems share an empirical temperature ([[§C1.1 Systems, Equilibrium and the First Two Laws#^thm-c1-1-3|Theorem §C1.1.3]]), and asking that the pair also obey Carathéodory's principle forces $\Theta$ to split into a universal function of temperature times a function of the leaf. The universal part is the absolute temperature, and the rest can be absorbed into a new label, the entropy. This is $\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = T\,dS$, reached without an engine; level B reached the same formula from Kelvin–Planck ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]).

**Setup.** Two systems $A$, $B$ with equilibrium manifolds as in [[§C1.2 The Equilibrium Manifold and the Heat One-Form#^def-c1-2-1|Def. §C1.2.1]], each obeying [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^pr-c1-3-1|Principle §C1.3.1]], brought into thermal contact.

## Two systems in contact

> [!model] Model §C1.4.1: Two Systems in Thermal Contact
> Systems $A$ and $B$ are in thermal equilibrium, so they share one empirical temperature $t$ ([[§C1.1 Systems, Equilibrium and the First Two Laws#^thm-c1-1-3|Theorem §C1.1.3]]). Near a state, replace the coordinates of $A$ by $(t, \Sigma_A, a)$, with $\Sigma_A$ the leaf label of $A$ and $a$ the remaining $d_A - 2$ variables, and likewise $(t, \Sigma_B, b)$ for $B$. The composite has coordinates $(t, \Sigma_A, a, \Sigma_B, b)$, so its equilibrium manifold has dimension $d_A + d_B - 1$. Heat adds:
>
> $$
> \delta Q_{\text{rev}} = \delta Q_{\text{rev}}^A + \delta Q_{\text{rev}}^B .
> $$
>
> For two gases, $d_A = d_B = 2$ and the composite has $d = 3$, with coordinates $(t, \Sigma_A, \Sigma_B)$.
>
> *Assumes:* the isotherms cross the reversible adiabats on each manifold, $dt\wedge d\Sigma_A \neq 0$ and $dt\wedge d\Sigma_B \neq 0$, so that $(t, \Sigma_A, a)$ and $(t, \Sigma_B, b)$ are coordinates; the composite is itself a thermodynamic system, so it obeys [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^pr-c1-3-1|Principle §C1.3.1]] and [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^thm-c1-3-2|Theorem §C1.3.2]] applies to it. The crossing fails on special sets, such as water at $4\,^\circ$C and $1$ atm, where the thermal expansion vanishes and an adiabat is tangent to an isotherm; [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]] holds off such a set and extends to it by continuity when the set has no interior.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.2.3 · Lecture 9/3 (the construction described) · coordinates written here*

^mod-c1-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.2: The Integrating Factor Splits into Temperature and Leaf
> For every system obeying [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^pr-c1-3-1|Principle §C1.3.1]], the integrating factor of [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]] has the form
>
> $$
> \Theta = T\,g(\Sigma) ,
> $$
>
> where $T = T(t)$ is a positive function of the empirical temperature alone, the **same function for every system**, and $g(\Sigma)$ depends only on the leaf and on how the leaves were labelled.
>
> *Model:* [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^mod-c1-4-1|Model §C1.4.1]]; *applies:* every pair of systems that can be put in thermal contact. $T$ is fixed up to a positive constant factor.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.2.3 (statement, citing Adkins and Callen) · derivation written here*

^thm-c1-4-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (heat of the composite). By [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]] on each part, $\delta Q_{\text{rev}}^A = \Theta_A\,d\Sigma_A$ with $\Theta_A = \Theta_A(t, \Sigma_A, a)$, and $\delta Q_{\text{rev}}^B = \Theta_B\,d\Sigma_B$ with $\Theta_B = \Theta_B(t, \Sigma_B, b)$. So
>
> $$
> \delta Q_{\text{rev}} = \Theta_A\,d\Sigma_A + \Theta_B\,d\Sigma_B .
> $$
>
> **Step 2** (the composite obeys the principle too). By [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]] for the composite, $\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = \Theta\,d\Sigma$ for some $\Theta$, $\Sigma$. This is where the principle does its work: a form in $d_A + d_B - 1 \ge 3$ variables need not have an integrating factor ([[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^thm-c1-3-5|Theorem §C1.3.5]]).
>
> **Step 3** (what $\Sigma$ depends on). Dividing,
>
> $$
> d\Sigma = \frac{\Theta_A}{\Theta}\,d\Sigma_A + \frac{\Theta_B}{\Theta}\,d\Sigma_B .
> $$
>
> The right side has no $dt$, $da$ or $db$ term. So $\Sigma = \Sigma(\Sigma_A, \Sigma_B)$, and the coefficients are its partial derivatives,
>
> $$
> \frac{\Theta_A}{\Theta} = \frac{\partial\Sigma}{\partial\Sigma_A}, \qquad \frac{\Theta_B}{\Theta} = \frac{\partial\Sigma}{\partial\Sigma_B} ,
> $$
>
> functions of $(\Sigma_A, \Sigma_B)$ alone. Hence so is their ratio,
>
> $$
> \frac{\Theta_A(t, \Sigma_A, a)}{\Theta_B(t, \Sigma_B, b)} = R(\Sigma_A, \Sigma_B) .
> $$
>
> **Step 4** (no dependence on $a$, $b$). $\Theta_A$ has one sign on a connected chart; reversing the label $\Sigma_A \to -\Sigma_A$ if needed, take it positive, and likewise $\Theta_B$. Take the logarithm of the ratio and differentiate with respect to a component of $a$: the $\Theta_B$ term and $R$ do not depend on $a$, so $\partial_a\ln\Theta_A = 0$. So $\Theta_A = \Theta_A(t, \Sigma_A)$, and likewise $\Theta_B = \Theta_B(t, \Sigma_B)$. If $d_A = 2$, there is no $a$ and this step is empty.
>
> **Step 5** (separating $t$). Differentiate the logarithm of the ratio with respect to $t$: $R$ has no $t$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\ln\Theta_A(t, \Sigma_A) = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\ln\Theta_B(t, \Sigma_B) .
> $$
>
> The left side does not contain $\Sigma_B$ and the right side does not contain $\Sigma_A$, so both equal one function $h(t)$. The function $h_A(t) = \partial_t\ln\Theta_A$ at fixed $\Sigma_A$ belongs to $A$ alone: it does not depend on the partner, nor on the labelling, since $\Sigma_A \to f(\Sigma_A)$ divides $\Theta_A$ by $f'(\Sigma_A)$, which has no $t$. Contact with $B$ gives $h_A = h_B$, and contact with any third system $C$ gives $h_A = h_C$. All systems share the one empirical scale $t$ of [[§C1.1 Systems, Equilibrium and the First Two Laws#^thm-c1-1-3|Theorem §C1.1.3]], so $h$ is one function of $t$ for every system, on the overlap of their ranges of $t$.
>
> **Step 6** (integrating). Integrate from a reference $t_0$: $\ln\Theta_A(t, \Sigma_A) = \int_{t_0}^th(t')\,dt' + \ln\Theta_A(t_0, \Sigma_A)$. Define
>
> $$
> T(t) = C\exp\int_{t_0}^th(t')\,dt', \qquad C > 0, \qquad g_A(\Sigma_A) = \Theta_A(t_0, \Sigma_A)/C .
> $$
>
> Then $\Theta_A = T(t)\,g_A(\Sigma_A)$, and $T$ is positive and the same for every system.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - ⚑ Universality of $T$ comes from Step 5: the separation is forced by having two independent systems. No substance is singled out.
> - ⚑ The composite's entropy is additive: [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-4|Theorem §C1.4.4]].
> - ⚑ The content of the second law for simple fluids sits in Step 2, because each fluid alone has $d = 2$.

> [!definition] Definition §C1.4.1: Absolute Temperature
> The function $T$ of [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]] is the **absolute temperature**. It is positive, independent of the substance, and defined up to a constant factor, fixed by the choice of unit.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.2.3 · Lecture 9/3*

^def-c1-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §C1.4.2: Entropy
> The **entropy** $S$ is the state function, defined up to an additive constant, with
>
> $$
> dS = g(\Sigma)\,d\Sigma ,
> $$
>
> with $g$ from [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]]. It relabels the leaves of [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^thm-c1-3-2|Theorem §C1.3.2]] so that the arbitrary factor is absorbed.
>
> *Model:* every system obeying [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^pr-c1-3-1|Principle §C1.3.1]]; *applies:* equilibrium states only. A state off $\mathcal E$ has no $S$ in this chapter.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.2.3 · Lecture 9/3*

^def-c1-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.3: Heat of a Reversible Step
> $$
> \delta Q_{\text{rev}} = T\,dS .
> $$
>
> So $1/T$ is an integrating factor of $\delta Q_{\text{rev}}$. Along a reversible adiabatic process $dS = 0$, and the system stays on one surface $S = \text{const}$. Reversibly adding or removing heat moves it between surfaces, and $T$ sets the conversion between heat and the change of entropy.
>
> *Model:* [[§C1.2 The Equilibrium Manifold and the Heat One-Form#^def-c1-2-1|Def. §C1.2.1]]; *applies:* reversible steps only. In an irreversible step $\delta Q \neq T\,dS$ ([[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-10|Theorem §C1.5.10]]).
>
> *Source: Mao §1.2.3 · Lecture 9/3 · level-B route: [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]*

^thm-c1-4-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Combine [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]] with [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]]: $\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = \Theta\,d\Sigma = T\,g(\Sigma)\,d\Sigma = T\,dS$ by [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^def-c1-4-2|Def. §C1.4.2]].

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.4: Entropy Is Additive over Systems in Contact
> For the composite of [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^mod-c1-4-1|Model §C1.4.1]], with additive constants chosen compatibly,
>
> $$
> S = S_A + S_B .
> $$
>
> *Model:* [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^mod-c1-4-1|Model §C1.4.1]]; *applies:* systems in thermal contact at one temperature.
>
> *Source: written here; by-product of [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^der-c1-4-1|Derivation §C1.4.1]]*

^thm-c1-4-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> $\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = \delta Q_{\text{rev}}^A + \delta Q_{\text{rev}}^B = T(g_A\,d\Sigma_A + g_B\,d\Sigma_B) = T(dS_A + dS_B)$, and for the composite $\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = T\,dS$. Divide by $T > 0$.

^der-c1-4-3

*Uses:* [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]], [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^mod-c1-4-1|Model §C1.4.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.5: Entropy Is Single-Valued
> If $\mathcal E$ is simply connected, in particular if it is convex in its coordinates, then there is one state function $S$ on all of $\mathcal E$ that is, near each state, a leaf label of [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^thm-c1-3-2|Theorem §C1.3.2]]. A level set of $S$ may consist of several leaves.
>
> *Model:* [[§C1.2 The Equilibrium Manifold and the Heat One-Form#^def-c1-2-1|Def. §C1.2.1]]; *applies:* the usual case of states filling a convex region such as $T > 0$, $V > 0$ (the caveat of [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^cau-b1-1-1|Caution: The test needs a region without holes]]).
>
> *Source: written here; the course treats the label as global*

^thm-c1-4-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1.** $T$ is a global function, because $t$ is. So $\eta = \delta Q_{\text{rev}}/T$ is a one-form on all of $\mathcal E$.
>
> **Step 2.** Locally $\eta = g(\Sigma)\,d\Sigma = d(G\circ\Sigma)$ with $G' = g$, so $d\eta = 0$ everywhere.
>
> **Step 3.** A closed one-form on a simply connected manifold is exact (Lee, Introduction to Smooth Manifolds, Ch. 11); on a star-shaped region, such as a convex one, this is the Poincaré lemma ([[§39 Closed and Exact Forms#^prop-39-7|452 Prop. §39.7]]). So $\eta = dS$ with one function $S$.

^der-c1-4-4

*Uses:* [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]], [[§39 Closed and Exact Forms#^prop-39-7|452 Prop. §39.7]]

> [!caution] Caution: The factor that depends only on the leaf is $g$, not $\Theta$
> It is tempting to say that the integrating factor depends only on which leaf a state is on. That holds for $g$, the factor left after removing $T$. The full $\Theta = T\,g(\Sigma)$ varies along a leaf whenever the temperature does, as on a reversible adiabat of a gas, where $T$ changes and $S$ does not. (The 9/3 lecture states this point briefly for the integrating factor; the formula in the notes, used here, is the precise version.)

^cau-c1-4-1

> [!caution] Caution: What is and is not fixed
> $S$ is fixed up to a constant, and $T$ up to a factor that $S$ absorbs inversely ($T \to cT$, $S \to S/c$). The kelvin and the value of $k_B$ remove that factor; the third law fixes the constant ([[§B2.3 The Third Law#^rem-b2-3-1|Remark: The third law, stated precisely]]). Any other integrating factor of $\delta Q_{\text{rev}}$ is $1/T$ times a function of $S$, and up to a constant factor only $1/T$ is a function of temperature alone ([[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]], [[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^ex-b1-1-2|Example §B1.1.2]]).

## The ideal-gas check

> [!example] Example §C1.4.1: The Heat of an Ideal Gas Is Inexact, and $1/T$ Integrates It
> For a gas, [[§C1.2 The Equilibrium Manifold and the Heat One-Form#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]] gives $\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = dU + p\,dV$. Two ideal-gas facts turn this into a form in $(T, V)$: $dU = C_V\,dT$ and $pV = Nk_BT$. Then
>
> $$
> \delta Q_{\text{rev}} = C_V\,dT + \frac{Nk_BT}{V}\,dV .
> $$
>
> Test it as $M\,dT + N'\,dV$ ([[§B1.1 Exact and Inexact Differentials#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]). Here
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial C_V}{\partial V} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial T}\frac{Nk_BT}{V} = \frac{Nk_B}{V} ,
> $$
>
> which differ. So there is no function $Q(T, V)$ whose differential is the heat, and how much heat flows between two states depends on the route. Dividing by $T$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\delta Q_{\text{rev}}}{T} = C_V\frac{dT}{T} + Nk_B\frac{dV}{V} = d\bigl(C_V\ln T + Nk_B\ln V\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> exact, so $S = C_V\ln T + Nk_B\ln V + \text{const}$ at fixed $N$, with constant $C_V$. This is [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]] from the other direction.
>
> *Model:* ideal gas with constant $C_V$, $N$ fixed; both facts are specific to it ([[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^cau-b2-1-2|§B2.1, Caution on the ideal-gas formula for dU]]). Since $dU = C_V\,dT$, the coordinates $(U, V)$ and $(T, V)$ are equivalent here.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.2.3, eqs. (1.5)–(1.7)*
