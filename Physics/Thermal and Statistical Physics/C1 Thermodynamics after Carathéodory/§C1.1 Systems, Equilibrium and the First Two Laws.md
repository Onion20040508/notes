The course reviews thermodynamics by a route levels A and B did not take. Level A assumed two statements about heat engines ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]], [[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^pr-a4-3-1|Principle §A4.3.1]]) and level B built entropy from them through the Clausius inequality ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]). Here the second law is a geometric statement about which states can be reached from which, due to Carathéodory, and entropy comes out of it directly, with no engine in the argument ([[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor|§C1.3]]–[[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes|§C1.5]]). This section fixes the common vocabulary. Most of it is a recap, so it links its level-A homes and says only what changes.

**Setup.** Macroscopic systems described by thermodynamic variables alone. Thermodynamics is phenomenological: it ignores microscopic degrees of freedom and works with macroscopic observables, so nothing in this chapter depends on atoms.

## Systems and variables

> [!definition] Definition §C1.1.1: Thermodynamic System
> A **thermodynamic system** is a specified collection of degrees of freedom together with a boundary that says what may cross it. Depending on the boundary, the system may exchange energy, volume or particles with its surroundings.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.1 · Lecture 9/3 · level-A home: [[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]]*

^def-c1-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §C1.1.2: Extensive and Intensive Variables
> When the amount of matter is scaled by $\lambda$:
>
> - an **extensive** variable scales, $X \to \lambda X$, and is additive for independent subsystems ($U, S, V, N$);
> - an **intensive** variable stays unchanged and is not additive ($T, p, \mu$).
>
> *Source: Mao §1.1 · level-A home: [[§A3.1 Thermodynamic Systems#^def-a3-1-3|Def. §A3.1.3]]*

^def-c1-1-2

## Equilibrium and processes

> [!definition] Definition §C1.1.3: Thermodynamic Equilibrium State
> A **thermodynamic equilibrium state** is a macroscopic state whose observable properties are stationary and spatially uniform at the level of description used: all fast internal relaxation has ceased, and no further macroscopic change occurs unless an external constraint is altered.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.1.1 · Lecture 9/3*

^def-c1-1-3

> [!definition] Definition §C1.1.4: Process
> A **process** is a change from one state to another. It need not pass through equilibrium states at intermediate times.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.1.1*

^def-c1-1-4

> [!model] Model §C1.1.1: Reversible Process
> A **reversible process** is an idealized process in which the system is in equilibrium at every moment, so it runs infinitely slowly. With the system in equilibrium throughout, nothing drives it in either direction, and it can run backward. Going out of equilibrium and being irreversible are the same thing.
>
> *Assumes:* a limit that no real process reaches. Reversible processes define thermodynamic derivatives and serve as comparison paths between equilibrium endpoints.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.1.1 · Lecture 9/3 · level-A home: [[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^mod-a4-1-1|Model §A4.1.1]]*

^mod-c1-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: "Reversible" here includes the absence of friction
> Level A separates quasi-static from reversible, because a slow process with friction is quasi-static but not reversible ([[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^cau-a4-1-2|Caution: Quasi-static is necessary, not sufficient]]). The definition above does not contradict that. In this chapter a reversible process is a path of equilibrium states whose work is $\sum_iY_i\,dX_i$ ([[§C1.2 The Equilibrium Manifold and the Heat One-Form#^def-c1-2-2|Def. §C1.2.2]]), which leaves no room for friction, so reversible and "equilibrium process" mean the same thing.

^cau-c1-1-1

## The zeroth law

> [!definition] Definition §C1.1.5: Thermal Equilibrium
> Two systems are in **thermal equilibrium** if they are in thermal contact, so that they can exchange heat, and each is in thermodynamic equilibrium ([[§C1.1 Systems, Equilibrium and the First Two Laws#^def-c1-1-3|Def. §C1.1.3]]).
>
> Thermodynamic equilibrium is a property of one system. Thermal equilibrium is a relation between two.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.1.2 · Lecture 9/3 · level-A home: [[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^def-a1-1-1|Def. §A1.1.1]]*

^def-c1-1-5

> [!principle] Principle §C1.1.2: Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics
> Mutual thermal equilibrium is transitive: if $A$ is in thermal equilibrium with $C$, and $B$ with $C$, then $A$ and $B$ are in thermal equilibrium with each other.
>
> *Domain:* every thermodynamic system.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.1.2 · Lecture 9/3 · level-A home: [[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^pr-a1-1-1|Principle §A1.1.1]]*

^pr-c1-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.1.3: Empirical Temperature
> Thermal equilibrium classes can be labelled by one scalar variable $t$, the **empirical temperature**, which takes equal values exactly on systems in thermal equilibrium. The label is arbitrary up to a relabelling: it does not yet say which of two systems is hotter.
>
> *Model:* any systems obeying [[§C1.1 Systems, Equilibrium and the First Two Laws#^pr-c1-1-2|Principle §C1.1.2]]; *applies:* exact. The label becomes the absolute temperature in [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^def-c1-4-1|Def. §C1.4.1]].
>
> *Source: Mao §1.1.2 · level-A home: [[§A1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]*

^thm-c1-1-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (equivalence relation). Thermal equilibrium of a state with itself holds trivially, it is symmetric by definition, and it is transitive by [[§C1.1 Systems, Equilibrium and the First Two Laws#^pr-c1-1-2|Principle §C1.1.2]]. So it partitions all equilibrium states into classes.
>
> **Step 2** (label). Fix one system $C$ as a thermometer and let $t$ be any variable of $C$ that distinguishes its classes. Each class of states of any system is in thermal equilibrium with exactly one class of $C$, so it gets one value of $t$.
>
> **Step 3** (no order). Any injective function of $t$ labels the classes equally well, so nothing here orders them. The zeroth law says nothing about two systems that are not in equilibrium.

^der-c1-1-1

*Uses:* [[§C1.1 Systems, Equilibrium and the First Two Laws#^pr-c1-1-2|Principle §C1.1.2]], [[§C1.1 Systems, Equilibrium and the First Two Laws#^def-c1-1-5|Def. §C1.1.5]]

## The first law

> [!principle] Principle §C1.1.4: First Law of Thermodynamics
> Heat is a form of energy, and energy is conserved when it is included:
>
> $$
> dU = \delta Q + \delta W ,
> $$
>
> with $U$ the internal energy, a state function, $\delta Q$ the energy received as heat and $\delta W$ the work done **on** the system.
>
> *Domain:* any process of any thermodynamic system.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.1.3, eq. (1.1) · Lecture 9/3 · level-A home: [[§A3.3 The First Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]*

^pr-c1-1-4

> [!caution] Caution: Sign of the work, and of the bar
> Levels A and B write $\Delta U = Q - W$ with $W$ the work done **by** the system, and $đQ$ for an inexact differential ([[§B2.1 The First Law and Heat Capacities#^cau-b2-1-1|Caution: Notation and sign conventions]]). The course writes $\delta W$ for the work done **on** the system and $\delta$ for inexact, and level C follows it. Mao's PS1 solutions use the by-the-system sign ($\Delta U = Q - W$) in places, which is flagged where it occurs ([[§C1.8 The Ideal Gas, Extensivity and the Paramagnetic Salt#^ex-c1-8-3|Example §C1.8.3]]).

^cau-c1-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: $d$ and $\delta$ are different objects
> $dU$ is an exact differential, because $U$ is a state function. $\delta Q$ and $\delta W$ are process-dependent one-forms: in general there is no state function "heat content" or "work content" of which they are the differentials. Only their sum is exact. The difference is defined precisely in [[§C1.2 The Equilibrium Manifold and the Heat One-Form#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]] and [[§C1.2 The Equilibrium Manifold and the Heat One-Form#^ex-c1-2-2|Example §C1.2.2]], and it is the point of [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^thm-c1-3-4|Theorem §C1.3.4]].

^cau-c1-1-3
