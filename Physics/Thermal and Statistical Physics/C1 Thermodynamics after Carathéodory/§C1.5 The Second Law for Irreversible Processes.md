The reversible half of the principle gave surfaces $S = \text{const}$ ([[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]]). The other half concerns irreversible adiabatic processes: they can leave a surface, but only to one side. That is the general statement of the second law, and everything else in this section follows from it. Kelvin–Planck and Clausius, which levels A and B assumed ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^pr-a4-3-1|Principle §A4.3.1]], [[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]]), become theorems here, and the Clausius inequality, which level B proved from engines ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]), comes from an entropy balance.

**Setup.** Equilibrium states at both ends of each process, so that $S$ is defined there ([[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^def-c1-4-2|Def. §C1.4.2]]). What lies between may be off $\mathcal E$ ([[§C1.2 The Equilibrium Manifold and the Heat One-Form#^rem-c1-2-1|§C1.2, Remark: An irreversible process is not a curve on the equilibrium manifold]]).

## One side only

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.5.1: Adiabatic Processes Reach One Side of Each Surface
> Let $x \in \mathcal E$. Among states reachable from $x$ by adiabatic processes:
>
> 1. a reachable state near $x$ brings with it the whole local piece of its surface $S = \text{const}$;
> 2. the values of $S$ reachable lie on one side of $S(x)$ only;
> 3. that side is the same at every state, and it is the side of larger $S$.
>
> The side of smaller $S$ near $x$ is the inaccessible one that [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^pr-c1-3-1|Principle §C1.3.1]] promises.
>
> *Model:* [[§C1.2 The Equilibrium Manifold and the Heat One-Form#^def-c1-2-1|Def. §C1.2.1]]; *applies:* assuming (i) **locally**: in every small neighbourhood $N$ of $x$ (a chart of [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^thm-c1-3-2|Theorem §C1.3.2]], so that each local surface in $N$ is connected), the values of $S$ at states of $N$ reachable from $x$ form an interval containing $S(x)$; (ii) adiabatic work such as stirring can raise $U$ at fixed $X_i$, with $T > 0$, and the states at fixed $X_i$ form an interval in $U$. A global version of (i) is not enough when surfaces of constant $S$ are disconnected ([[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^ex-c1-5-1|Example §C1.5.1]]).
>
> *Source: Mao §1.3.1 · Lecture 9/3 · derivation written here; reading assumption (ii) as the physical content of Mao's "the convention we use to define $S$" is an interpretation added here*

^thm-c1-5-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (part 1). Work in a chart $N$ of [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^thm-c1-3-2|Theorem §C1.3.2]] around $x$, in which the surfaces $S = \text{const}$ are connected slices. If $y \in N$ is reachable, walk from $y$ along its slice by reversible adiabatic curves. Each such curve is an adiabatic process, so every state of the slice is reachable.
>
> **Step 2** (part 2). Suppose that in every such $N$ values of $S$ both above and below $S(x)$ are reached. By assumption (i) they fill an interval around $S(x)$, so by Step 1 every slice of $N$ with $S$ in that interval is reachable, and these slices fill a neighbourhood of $x$. That contradicts [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^pr-c1-3-1|Principle §C1.3.1]]. So $S(x)$ is an end of the interval.
>
> **Step 3** (part 3). Stir at fixed $X_i$. Stirring is adiabatic work, so the end state $y$ has $X_i(y) = X_i(x)$ and $U(y) > U(x)$. Compare the two endpoints along the reversible path at fixed $X_i$, which lies in $\mathcal E$ by assumption (ii). On it $T\,dS = \delta Q_{\text{rev}} = dU - \sum_iY_i\,dX_i = dU$ ([[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]], [[§C1.2 The Equilibrium Manifold and the Heat One-Form#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]), so
>
> $$
> S(y) - S(x) = \int_{U(x)}^{U(y)}\frac{dU}{T} > 0 .
> $$
>
> $S$ is a state function, so this is the change in the stirring process too. Stirring is possible at every state, so with part 2 every state has the larger side.
>
> **What the derivation shows.**
> - ⚑ The direction of increase is not a free convention once $T > 0$ is fixed: [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-2|Theorem §C1.4.2]] chose $T > 0$, and then stirring decides. The orientation of a bare label $\Sigma$ was free ([[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^rem-c1-3-1|§C1.3, Remark: Why the leaves carry an ordered label]]).

^der-c1-5-1

*Uses:* [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^pr-c1-3-1|Principle §C1.3.1]], [[§C1.3 Carathéodory's Principle and the Integrating Factor#^thm-c1-3-2|Theorem §C1.3.2]], [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]], [[§C1.2 The Equilibrium Manifold and the Heat One-Form#^thm-c1-2-1|Theorem §C1.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.5.2: General Statement of the Second Law
> Entropy never decreases during an adiabatic process:
>
> $$
> \Delta S \ge 0 \qquad\text{(adiabatic process)},
> $$
>
> with equality for a reversible process and strict inequality for an irreversible one.
>
> *Model:* a system with equilibrium endpoints and $\delta Q = 0$ throughout; *applies:* in particular an isolated system. The strict inequality uses [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]].
>
> *Source: Mao §1.3.1 · Lecture 9/3*

^thm-c1-5-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1.** The inequality is parts 2 and 3 of [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-1|Theorem §C1.5.1]].
>
> **Step 2.** Reversible case: a reversible adiabatic process stays on one surface, $\Delta S = 0$ ([[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]]).
>
> **Step 3.** Irreversible case. Suppose an irreversible adiabatic process from $x$ ends at $y$ with $\Delta S = 0$. If $y$ lies on the same connected surface as $x$, walk back from $y$ to $x$ reversibly along it. The two steps form a cycle of an adiabatic system, so $\Delta U = 0$ and the net work is zero: the process is undone with no change elsewhere. That contradicts [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]. Hence $\Delta S > 0$. (If the surface $S = S(x)$ is disconnected and $y$ lies on another piece, the argument does not reach it; the strict inequality is then part of what [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]] asserts.)

^der-c1-5-2

*Uses:* [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-1|Theorem §C1.5.1]], [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]], [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^pr-c1-5-3|Principle §C1.5.3]]

> [!principle] Principle §C1.5.3: Irreversibility Cannot Be Undone
> A process that passes through nonequilibrium states cannot be undone so as to leave no change elsewhere.
>
> *Domain:* every thermodynamic system. It makes "irreversible" in the sense of [[§C1.1 Systems, Equilibrium and the First Two Laws#^mod-c1-1-1|Model §C1.1.1]] (not a path of equilibrium states) the same as "not recoverable".
>
> *Source: written here; Mao §1.3.1 states the strict inequality for irreversible processes without this input, and the proof of [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-2|Theorem §C1.5.2]] needs it (Planck's formulation)*

^pr-c1-5-3

> [!principle] Principle §C1.5.4: Entropy Is Additive over Parts
> If a system consists of parts that are each in equilibrium, weakly interacting, and not necessarily at one temperature, its entropy is the sum of theirs:
>
> $$
> S = \sum_kS_k .
> $$
>
> *Domain:* parts whose interaction energy is negligible (no surface or long-range terms). For parts in thermal contact at one temperature it is a theorem, [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-4|Theorem §C1.4.4]].
>
> *Source: written here; Mao uses it in §1.3.2 ("the system together with the reservoir") and PS1 Problem 1 adds reservoir entropies at different temperatures*

^pr-c1-5-4

> [!example] Example §C1.5.1: Why One-Sidedness Needs a Local Assumption
> The global form of assumption (i) of [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-1|Theorem §C1.5.1]] is not enough. Let $\mathcal E$ be a horseshoe in the $(X, U)$ plane, two vertical arms joined at the bottom, with $S = U$ and $T = 1$, so that high surfaces $U = c$ have two pieces. Let a state $z$ be reachable from $y$ if both are in one arm and $U(z) \ge U(y)$, if $y$ is in the left arm and $z$ in the right with $U(z) \ge U(y) - 1$, and if $y$ is in the right arm and $z$ in the left with $U(z) \ge U(y) + 2$. This relation is transitive, every state has unreachable states just below it in its own arm, and stirring raises $U$. From the left arm the reachable values of $S$ are $[U(x) - 1, \infty)$, an interval with $S(x)$ inside it, yet part 2 fails. The local version of (i) excludes this, because near $x$ only one arm is present.
>
> *Source: counterexample from the review of these notes (2026-11-07)*

^ex-c1-5-1

## The historical statements as theorems

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.5.5: Kelvin–Planck Statement
> No cyclic device can have as its sole effect the absorption of heat from a single reservoir at positive temperature and the complete conversion of that heat into work.
>
> *Model:* a device in a cycle with one reservoir ([[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^mod-c1-5-7|Model §C1.5.7]]); *applies:* reservoir at $T > 0$.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.2.3 · PHY 510 Problem Set 1, Problem 1(a), official solution · a principle at level A: [[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^pr-a4-3-1|Principle §A4.3.1]]*

^thm-c1-5-5

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (make it adiabatic). Suppose such a device exists. Take device plus reservoir as one system. It exchanges work only, so it is adiabatic, and its entropy is the sum of the two ([[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^pr-c1-5-4|Principle §C1.5.4]]).
>
> **Step 2** (entropy). In one cycle the device returns to its state, $\Delta S_{\text{device}} = 0$. The reservoir loses heat $\Delta Q > 0$ at $T$, so $\Delta S_{\text{res}} = -\Delta Q/T < 0$.
>
> **Step 3** (contradiction). The total change is $-\Delta Q/T < 0$, against [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-2|Theorem §C1.5.2]].

^der-c1-5-3

*Uses:* [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-2|Theorem §C1.5.2]], [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-8|Theorem §C1.5.8]], [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^pr-c1-5-4|Principle §C1.5.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.5.6: Clausius Statement
> No cyclic device can have as its sole effect the transfer of heat from a colder body to a hotter body.
>
> *Model:* two reservoirs at $T_C < T_H$; *applies:* no net work or other change in the surroundings.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.2.3 · PHY 510 Problem Set 1, Problem 1(b), official solution · a principle at level A: [[§A4.1 Reversible and Irreversible Processes#^pr-a4-1-2|Principle §A4.1.2]]*

^thm-c1-5-6

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Suppose heat $\Delta Q > 0$ passes from $T_C$ to $T_H$ with no other change. The two reservoirs (with the device back in its state) form an isolated system whose entropy is the sum of theirs ([[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^pr-c1-5-4|Principle §C1.5.4]]), and
>
> $$
> \Delta S_{\text{tot}} = \frac{\Delta Q}{T_H} - \frac{\Delta Q}{T_C} = \Delta Q\Bigl(\frac1{T_H} - \frac1{T_C}\Bigr) < 0 ,
> $$
>
> since $T_H > T_C$. This contradicts [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-2|Theorem §C1.5.2]].

^der-c1-5-4

*Uses:* [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-2|Theorem §C1.5.2]], [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-8|Theorem §C1.5.8]], [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^pr-c1-5-4|Principle §C1.5.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Layer change from levels A and B
> The two classical statements are principles at level A and the Clausius inequality is a theorem from them at level B. Here the order reverses: one geometric principle, then entropy, then these. Their mutual equivalence ([[§A4.3 Statements of the Second Law#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]) is not needed. Carathéodory's form states the structure geometrically instead of starting from a particular engine cycle, which is what makes it the one to generalize.

^rem-c1-5-1

## Entropy balance

> [!model] Model §C1.5.7: Heat Reservoir
> A **reservoir** at temperature $T_b$ is a body so large that it stays at $T_b$ whatever heat it exchanges. So any exchange with it is reversible on the reservoir's side, even when the process in the system is not.
>
> *Assumes:* the heat capacity is effectively infinite; internal relaxation is fast compared with the exchange.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.3.2*

^mod-c1-5-7

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.5.8: Entropy Change of a Reservoir
> Let the system receive heat $\delta Q$ from a reservoir or boundary element at $T_b$, with $\delta Q > 0$ when heat enters the system. Then
>
> $$
> dS_{\text{res}} = -\frac{\delta Q}{T_b} .
> $$
>
> *Model:* [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^mod-c1-5-7|Model §C1.5.7]]; *applies:* any process in the system. The same formula does **not** hold for the system, [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^cau-c1-5-1|§C1.5, Caution: Why the reservoir formula fails for the system]].
>
> *Source: Mao §1.3.2*

^thm-c1-5-8

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Entropy is a state function, so its change for the reservoir depends only on the reservoir's end states, and those are fixed by the heat it gave up. Connect them by a reversible exchange of the same heat at $T_b$. Then [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]] for the reservoir gives $dS_{\text{res}} = \delta Q_{\text{res}}/T_b$ with $\delta Q_{\text{res}} = -\delta Q$.

^der-c1-5-5

*Uses:* [[§C1.4 Temperature and Entropy#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]], [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^mod-c1-5-7|Model §C1.5.7]]

> [!caution] Caution: Why the reservoir formula fails for the system
> The trick of [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^der-c1-5-5|Derivation §C1.5.5]] needs the body to stay in equilibrium at $T_b$. The system need not, so it is not assigned $\delta Q/T_b$. Its change is written $\Delta S$ and found from the balance below. In a sum $\int\delta Q/T_b$ the temperature is that of the boundary or reservoir, not of the system ([[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^cau-b2-2-1|Caution: Whose temperature?]]).

^cau-c1-5-1

> [!definition] Definition §C1.5.1: Entropy Produced
> For a process from equilibrium state $A$ to equilibrium state $B$, exchanging heat with a reservoir at $T_b$, the **entropy produced** is
>
> $$
> S_i \equiv \Delta S - \int_A^B\frac{\delta Q}{T_b} .
> $$
>
> *Model:* [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^mod-c1-5-7|Model §C1.5.7]], equilibrium endpoints of the system; *applies:* any process.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.3.2*

^def-c1-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.5.9: Entropy Balance
> $$
> \Delta S = \int_A^B\frac{\delta Q}{T_b} + S_i, \qquad S_i \ge 0 .
> $$
>
> The first term is the entropy carried in with heat. The second is the entropy produced by irreversibility inside the system, and $S_i = 0$ for a reversible process.
>
> *Model:* as in [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]]; *applies:* any process with equilibrium endpoints.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.3.2, eq. (1.8)*

^thm-c1-5-9

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1.** System plus reservoir exchange only work with the rest of the world, so the pair is adiabatic. Work carries no entropy, and the pair's entropy is the sum of the two ([[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^pr-c1-5-4|Principle §C1.5.4]]).
>
> **Step 2.** [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-2|Theorem §C1.5.2]] for the pair, with [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-8|Theorem §C1.5.8]] integrated:
>
> $$
> \Delta S_{\text{tot}} = \Delta S - \int_A^B\frac{\delta Q}{T_b} \ge 0 .
> $$
>
> **Step 3.** The left side is $S_i$ by [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]]. Equality holds for a reversible process by [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-2|Theorem §C1.5.2]].

^der-c1-5-6

*Uses:* [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-2|Theorem §C1.5.2]], [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-8|Theorem §C1.5.8]], [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^def-c1-5-1|Def. §C1.5.1]], [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^pr-c1-5-4|Principle §C1.5.4]]

> [!caution] Caution: "Entropy never decreases" is about adiabatic processes
> $S_i \ge 0$ does not stop $\Delta S$ from being negative: a system that loses enough heat has $\Delta S < 0$. "Entropy never decreases" holds for adiabatic processes, and for the production term of any process. The point of [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-9|Theorem §C1.5.9]] is to separate the two terms.

^cau-c1-5-2

## The Clausius inequality

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.5.10: Clausius Inequality
> For any process between equilibrium states,
>
> $$
> \Delta S \ge \int_A^B\frac{\delta Q}{T_b} ,
> $$
>
> with equality for a reversible process.
>
> *Model:* as in [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-9|Theorem §C1.5.9]]; *applies:* any process with equilibrium endpoints.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.3.3, eq. (1.9) · Lecture 9/8 · level-B route, from engines: [[§B2.2 The Second Law, the Clausius Inequality and Entropy#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]*

^thm-c1-5-10

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> Drop the term $S_i \ge 0$ from [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-9|Theorem §C1.5.9]].

^der-c1-5-7

*Uses:* [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-9|Theorem §C1.5.9]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.5.11: Clausius Inequality for a Cycle
> For a cycle,
>
> $$
> \oint\frac{\delta Q}{T_b} \le 0 ,
> $$
>
> with equality for a reversible cycle.
>
> *Model:* a cycle, so the system returns to its state; *applies:* any cycle through equilibrium endpoints.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.3.3, eq. (1.10)*

^thm-c1-5-11

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> $S$ is a state function, so $\Delta S = 0$ for a cycle. Put this into [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-10|Theorem §C1.5.10]].

^der-c1-5-8

*Uses:* [[§C1.5 The Second Law for Irreversible Processes#^thm-c1-5-10|Theorem §C1.5.10]]

## The four kinds of process

> [!remark] Remark: Where each process lives on the equilibrium manifold
> The geometry reached so far, for processes between equilibrium states:
>
> | | General process | Adiabatic process |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | **Reversible** | continuous path on $\mathcal E$, $\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = T\,dS$ | continuous path along one leaf, $dS = 0$ |
> | **Irreversible** | generally leaves $\mathcal E$, $\Delta S > \int\delta Q/T$ | "jump" to a leaf of larger $S$, $\Delta S > 0$ |
>
> A "jump" here is not fast. It means only that the process passes through states that $\mathcal E$ does not contain ([[§C1.2 The Equilibrium Manifold and the Heat One-Form#^rem-c1-2-1|§C1.2, Remark: An irreversible process is not a curve on the equilibrium manifold]]).

^rem-c1-5-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The simplest instance of this picture is free expansion, a jump at constant $U$ — [[§C1.8 The Ideal Gas, Extensivity and the Paramagnetic Salt#^ex-c1-8-2|Example §C1.8.2]].
> - In statistical mechanics, the second law becomes a statement of overwhelming probability — [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^thm-b6-1-10|Theorem §B6.1.10]] — and the production term $S_i$ gets a formula in terms of probabilities in Mao §5.6 (level C to come).
> - The second law with reservoirs becomes the minimum principles of the potentials — [[§C1.6 Thermodynamic Potentials and Variational Principles|§C1.6]].
