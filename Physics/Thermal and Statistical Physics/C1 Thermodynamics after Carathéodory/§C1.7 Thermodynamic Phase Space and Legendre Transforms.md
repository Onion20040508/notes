The potentials of [[§C1.6 Thermodynamic Potentials and Variational Principles|§C1.6]] look like a list. This section shows that they are one object in different coordinates. Equilibrium is a $d$-dimensional surface inside a larger space of $2d + 1$ variables, generated by one function, and a Legendre transform changes which variables are independent without changing the surface. Level B defines the transform and its properties ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|Def. §B1.2.2]], [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]); what is added here is the space it acts on.

**Setup.** A system whose equilibrium states are fixed by $d$ extensive variables. Conjugate variables carry lower indices, $p_a$; the pressure stays $p$ ([[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^cau-c1-7-2|§C1.7, Caution: Conjugate of the volume, and two meanings of p]]).

## Thermodynamic phase space

> [!definition] Definition §C1.7.1: Thermodynamic Phase Space
> Let $q^a = (q^1, \dots, q^d)$ be independent extensive variables and $p_a = (p_1, \dots, p_d)$ their conjugates. The **thermodynamic phase space** $\mathcal T$ is the $(2d + 1)$-dimensional space with coordinates
>
> $$
> (U, q^a, p_a) .
> $$
>
> *Model:* variables regarded as independent before equilibrium is imposed; *applies:* a generic point of $\mathcal T$ is not an equilibrium state.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.4.2*

^def-c1-7-1

> [!definition] Definition §C1.7.2: Equilibrium States inside Phase Space
> The equilibrium states of a given system form a $d$-dimensional submanifold
>
> $$
> \mathcal E \subset \mathcal T, \qquad \dim\mathcal E = d, \qquad \dim\mathcal T = 2d + 1 ,
> $$
>
> so equilibrium imposes $d + 1$ constraints.
>
> *Model:* one given system; a different substance is a different $\mathcal E$ in the same $\mathcal T$.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.4.2*

^def-c1-7-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.7.1: One Function Generates the Equilibrium Manifold
> The $d + 1$ constraints are one **fundamental relation** $U = U(q^1, \dots, q^d)$ and the $d$ conjugate conditions
>
> $$
> p_a = \frac{\partial U}{\partial q^a}, \qquad a = 1, \dots, d .
> $$
>
> Equivalently, on $\mathcal E$,
>
> $$
> dU = \sum_{a=1}^dp_a\,dq^a .
> $$
>
> Knowing the one function $U(q)$ therefore fixes all of $\mathcal E$.
>
> *Model:* [[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^def-c1-7-2|Def. §C1.7.2]]; *applies:* on $\mathcal E$ only, with $U$ differentiable ([[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Mao §1.4.2*

^thm-c1-7-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1.** The relation $dU = \sum_ap_a\,dq^a$ is [[§C1.6 Thermodynamic Potentials and Variational Principles#^thm-c1-6-2|Theorem §C1.6.2]] with $q = (S, X_1, \dots, X_n)$ and $p = (T, Y_1, \dots, Y_n)$.
>
> **Step 2.** Compare it with the chain rule $dU = \sum_a(\partial U/\partial q^a)\,dq^a$. The $dq^a$ are independent on $\mathcal E$, so the coefficients agree. Conversely, given $U(q)$, the functions $p_a(q)$ are determined, so one function fixes $d + 1$ of the $2d + 1$ coordinates in terms of the other $d$.

^der-c1-7-1

*Uses:* [[§C1.6 Thermodynamic Potentials and Variational Principles#^thm-c1-6-2|Theorem §C1.6.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Two descriptions of one manifold
> Two coordinate choices on $\mathcal E$ appear in this chapter:
>
> - from [[§C1.2 The Equilibrium Manifold and the Heat One-Form#^def-c1-2-1|Def. §C1.2.1]], $(U, X_1, \dots, X_n)$: the energy and $n = d - 1$ work variables;
> - from [[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^thm-c1-7-1|Theorem §C1.7.1]], $(q^1, \dots, q^d)$: the extensive variables including $S$, with $U$ now a function.
>
> The dimension is $d$ in both. The switch trades $U$ for $S$ as a coordinate, which needs $T \neq 0$, so that $U$ and $S$ are locally functions of each other at fixed $X_i$.

^cau-c1-7-1

> [!caution] Caution: Conjugate of the volume, and two meanings of p
> The conjugate of $q = V$ is $p_V = \partial U/\partial V = -p$, minus the pressure. So the fluid has $q = (S, V)$, $p_a = (T, -p)$. The conjugate variables $p_a$ are general, and $p$ alone is the pressure.

^cau-c1-7-2

## Legendre transforms

> [!definition] Definition §C1.7.3: Legendre Transform of One Pair
> Replacing $q^b$ by its conjugate $p_b$ gives the potential
>
> $$
> \Phi_b = U - p_bq^b \qquad(\text{no sum on } b),
> $$
>
> a function of $(q^1, \dots, q^{b-1}, p_b, q^{b+1}, \dots, q^d)$.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.4.2 · level-B home: [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|Def. §B1.2.2]]*

^def-c1-7-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.7.2: The Transform Exchanges a Conjugate Pair
> $$
> d\Phi_b = -q^b\,dp_b + \sum_{a\neq b}p_a\,dq^a .
> $$
>
> For any subset $I$ of the pairs, $\Phi_I = U - \sum_{b\in I}p_bq^b$ has
>
> $$
> d\Phi_I = \sum_{a\notin I}p_a\,dq^a - \sum_{b\in I}q^b\,dp_b .
> $$
>
> So $\Phi_I$ has natural variables $\{q^a\}_{a\notin I}\cup\{p_b\}_{b\in I}$. Nothing about equilibrium changes: the same manifold is described with a different set of independent variables, picked to match what an experiment holds fixed.
>
> *Model:* [[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^thm-c1-7-1|Theorem §C1.7.1]]; *applies:* where the Hessian block $(\partial^2U/\partial q^b\partial q^c)_{b, c\in I}$ is nonsingular, so that the $p_b$, $b \in I$, can replace the $q^b$; for one pair this is $\partial^2U/(\partial q^b)^2 \neq 0$ ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]). Pair-by-pair invertibility is not the right test: $U = q^1q^2$ fails it for $I = \{1\}$ but transforms for $I = \{1, 2\}$.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.4.2 · Lecture 9/8*

^thm-c1-7-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1.** Differentiate: $d\Phi_b = dU - p_b\,dq^b - q^b\,dp_b$.
>
> **Step 2.** Insert $dU = \sum_ap_a\,dq^a$ from [[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^thm-c1-7-1|Theorem §C1.7.1]]. The term $a = b$ cancels $-p_b\,dq^b$, and what remains is the first formula.
>
> **Step 3.** The same with each $b \in I$ gives the second.

^der-c1-7-2

*Uses:* [[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^thm-c1-7-1|Theorem §C1.7.1]], [[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^def-c1-7-3|Def. §C1.7.3]]

> [!example] Example §C1.7.1: The Common Potentials Are One Family
> For a fluid, $q = (S, V)$ and $p_a = (T, -p)$ ([[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^cau-c1-7-2|§C1.7, Caution: Conjugate of the volume, and two meanings of p]]), with the subsets $I$ of the pairs:
>
> | $I$ | $\Phi_I$ | name | $d\Phi_I$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $\varnothing$ | $U$ | internal energy | $T\,dS - p\,dV$ |
> | $\{V\}$ | $U + pV$ | enthalpy $H$ | $T\,dS + V\,dp$ |
> | $\{S\}$ | $U - TS$ | Helmholtz $F$ | $-S\,dT - p\,dV$ |
> | $\{S, V\}$ | $U - TS + pV$ | Gibbs $G$ | $-S\,dT + V\,dp$ |
>
> The sign of $pV$ is that of $-p_Vq^V$ with $p_V = -p$. Each row is [[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^thm-c1-7-2|Theorem §C1.7.2]].
>
> *Source: Mao §1.4.1, eqs. (1.13)–(1.18), and §1.4.2*

^ex-c1-7-1

## Why not just substitute?

> [!example] Example §C1.7.2: One Variable: Roles Exchanged
> Let $U = U(q)$, $dU = p\,dq$. The transform $G(p) = U(q) - pq$ has $dG = p\,dq - p\,dq - q\,dp = -q\,dp$, so
>
> $$
> p = \frac{dU}{dq}, \qquad q = -\frac{dG}{dp} .
> $$
>
> $q$ and $p$ have exchanged roles as independent variable and derivative of the potential. Solving $q = q(p)$ and substituting into $U$ is a valid change of coordinates on $\mathcal E$, but the function $\tilde U(p) = U(q(p))$ already loses information with one variable: $U_c(q) = \tfrac12(q - c)^2$ gives $p = q - c$ and $\tilde U(p) = \tfrac12p^2$ for every $c$, so $\tilde U$ cannot recover $U$ (the argument of Callen, Thermodynamics, §5.1; level B: [[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^rem-b1-2-2|Remark: The geometry, and why not simply substitute]]). Mao's box puts the difference at two pairs, where in addition the conjugate structure of the differential is lost ([[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^thm-c1-7-3|Theorem §C1.7.3]]).
>
> *Source: Mao §1.4.2, box "Example: Why a Legendre transform?"*

^ex-c1-7-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.7.3: Substitution Loses the Conjugate Structure
> Let $U = U(q^1, q^2)$, $dU = p_1\,dq^1 + p_2\,dq^2$. Solve $p_1 = (\partial U/\partial q^1)_{q^2}$ for $q^1 = q^1(p_1, q^2)$ and put $\tilde U(p_1, q^2) = U(q^1(p_1, q^2), q^2)$. Then
>
> $$
> d\tilde U = p_1\Bigl(\frac{\partial q^1}{\partial p_1}\Bigr)_{q^2}dp_1 + \Bigl[p_2 + p_1\Bigl(\frac{\partial q^1}{\partial q^2}\Bigr)_{p_1}\Bigr]dq^2 ,
> $$
>
> so $(\partial\tilde U/\partial p_1)_{q^2} \neq q^1$ and $(\partial\tilde U/\partial q^2)_{p_1} \neq p_2$ in general. The Legendre transform $G = U - p_1q^1$ instead has
>
> $$
> dG = -q^1\,dp_1 + p_2\,dq^2, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_1}\Bigr)_{q^2} = -q^1, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\partial G}{\partial q^2}\Bigr)_{p_1} = p_2 .
> $$
>
> The transform is therefore not just a change of coordinates: it replaces one member of a conjugate pair by the other **and** keeps the conjugate structure of the differential.
>
> *Model:* [[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^thm-c1-7-1|Theorem §C1.7.1]] with $d = 2$; *applies:* $\partial^2U/(\partial q^1)^2 \neq 0$.
>
> *Source: Mao §1.4.2, box "Example: Why a Legendre transform?" · Lecture 9/8*

^thm-c1-7-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (the differential of $q^1$).
>
> $$
> dq^1 = \Bigl(\frac{\partial q^1}{\partial p_1}\Bigr)_{q^2}dp_1 + \Bigl(\frac{\partial q^1}{\partial q^2}\Bigr)_{p_1}dq^2 .
> $$
>
> **Step 2.** $d\tilde U = p_1\,dq^1 + p_2\,dq^2$, because $\tilde U$ is the same number as $U$ at the same state. Insert Step 1 and collect $dp_1$ and $dq^2$.
>
> **Step 3.** The Legendre case is [[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^thm-c1-7-2|Theorem §C1.7.2]] with $b = 1$.

^der-c1-7-3

*Uses:* [[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^thm-c1-7-2|Theorem §C1.7.2]]

> [!theorem]- ★ Theorem §C1.7.4: Thermodynamic Phase Space Is a Contact Manifold
> On $\mathcal T$ define the one-form
>
> $$
> \alpha = dU - \sum_ap_a\,dq^a .
> $$
>
> 1. $\alpha\wedge(d\alpha)^d \neq 0$ everywhere, so $\alpha$ is a contact form.
> 2. $\mathcal E$ is a **Legendrian** submanifold: $\alpha$ vanishes on it, and $d$ is the largest dimension for which this is possible.
> 3. A Legendre transform $\Phi_I$ is a change of coordinates on $\mathcal T$ that preserves the form of $\alpha$, with the pairs $b \in I$ sent to $(q^b, p_b) \to (p_b, -q^b)$.
>
> *Model:* [[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^def-c1-7-1|Def. §C1.7.1]]; *applies:* exact.
>
> *Source: written here, beyond the course; Mao's §1.4.2 names $\mathcal T$ and compares it with canonical variables in mechanics*

^thm-c1-7-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Part 1.** $d\alpha = \sum_adq^a\wedge dp_a$, so $(d\alpha)^d = d!\,dq^1\wedge dp_1\wedge\cdots\wedge dq^d\wedge dp_d$, and $\alpha\wedge(d\alpha)^d = d!\,dU\wedge dq^1\wedge dp_1\wedge\cdots$, a volume form.
>
> **Part 2.** On $\mathcal E$, $\alpha = 0$ is [[§C1.7 Thermodynamic Phase Space and Legendre Transforms#^thm-c1-7-1|Theorem §C1.7.1]]. If $L$ is a submanifold with $\alpha|_L = 0$, then $d\alpha|_L = 0$, so $T_xL$ lies in $\ker\alpha$, on which $d\alpha$ is a nondegenerate two-form of rank $2d$, and it is isotropic there. Isotropic subspaces of a $2d$-dimensional symplectic space have dimension at most $d$.
>
> **Part 3.** From $\Phi_I = U - \sum_{b\in I}p_bq^b$, $d\Phi_I = dU - \sum_{b\in I}(p_b\,dq^b + q^b\,dp_b)$, so
>
> $$
> \alpha = d\Phi_I - \sum_{a\notin I}p_a\,dq^a + \sum_{b\in I}q^b\,dp_b ,
> $$
>
> which is $\alpha$ again with $q^b$ replaced by $p_b$ and $p_b$ by $-q^b$.

^der-c1-7-4
