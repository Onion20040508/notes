A gas has many particles, and when they are identical their labels carry no physics. Classically that costs a factor $1/N!$ in the phase-space measure; quantum mechanically it restricts the states themselves to symmetric or antisymmetric ones, and the clean way to write them is by occupation numbers. The symmetrization rule has its homes in Quantum Mechanics ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^pr-b7-1-4|QM Principle §B7.1.4]], [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^pr-c12-1-4|QM Principle §C12.1.4]]) and occupation-number kets in [[§C12.2★ Second Quantization#^def-c12-2-1|QM Def. §C12.2.1]]; this section gathers what statistical mechanics needs before Chapter C4 assigns probabilities.

**Setup.** One-particle phase space $\Gamma_1$ or one-particle Hilbert space $\mathfrak h$, and $N$ particles in $d$ spatial dimensions. The coordinate $x$ of a particle includes spin when it has one.

## Distinguishable particles

> [!definition] Definition §C3.5.1: $N$ Distinguishable Particles
> Classically $\Gamma_N = \Gamma_1^N$ with $X = (X_1, \dots, X_N)$, and the label $i$ names a particular particle. Quantum mechanically $\mathcal H_N = \mathfrak h^{\otimes N}$; two labelled particles in one-particle states $|a\rangle$ and $|b\rangle$ are
>
> $$
> |a\rangle_1|b\rangle_2 \equiv |a\rangle_1\otimes|b\rangle_2, \qquad \Psi(x_1, x_2) = \phi_a(x_1)\phi_b(x_2) .
> $$
>
> Product states are special: most vectors of $\mathfrak h^{\otimes N}$ are entangled ([[§C3.4 Composite Systems and Reduced States#^def-c3-4-3|Def. §C3.4.3]]).
>
> *Source: Mao §3.5.1, eqs. (3.50)–(3.53)*

## Identical particles

> [!definition] Definition §C3.5.2: Phase-Space Measure for Identical Classical Particles
> For identical classical particles, $X$ and any permutation of its entries are one physical state, so the true phase space is $\Gamma_1^N/S_N$. Integrating over labelled coordinates instead counts each state $N!$ times, which the measure corrects:
>
> $$
> d\Gamma_N = \frac{1}{N!\,h^{dN}}\prod_{i=1}^Nd^dq_i\,d^dp_i .
> $$
>
> *Source: Mao §3.5.2, eq. (3.54) · Lecture 9/22 · level B: [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^def-b6-3-3|Def. §B6.3.3]], [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-7|Theorem §B6.3.7]]*

^def-c3-5-2

> [!principle] Principle §C3.5.1: Exchange Symmetry of Identical Particles
> For two identical particles, with $(\hat P_{12}\Psi)(x_1, x_2) = \Psi(x_2, x_1)$, an allowed state returns to the same ray under exchange. In three spatial dimensions this leaves two cases:
>
> $$
> \Psi_B(x_2, x_1) = +\Psi_B(x_1, x_2)\quad\text{(bosons)}, \qquad \Psi_F(x_2, x_1) = -\Psi_F(x_1, x_2)\quad\text{(fermions)} .
> $$
>
> The arguments $x_1$, $x_2$ are positions in a list, not names of particles.
>
> *Domain:* identical particles in three dimensions (two dimensions allows other phases).
>
> *Source: Mao §3.5.2, eqs. (3.55)–(3.56) · QM homes: [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^pr-b7-1-4|QM Principle §B7.1.4]], [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^pr-c12-1-4|QM Principle §C12.1.4]]*

^pr-c3-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.2: Symmetric and Antisymmetric Two-Particle States
> For orthonormal orbitals $\phi_a \neq \phi_b$, the normalized states with one particle in each are
>
> $$
> \Psi_{ab}^{(\pm)}(x_1, x_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\phi_a(x_1)\phi_b(x_2) \pm \phi_b(x_1)\phi_a(x_2)\bigr] ,
> $$
>
> $+$ for bosons and $-$ for fermions. Each is a single pure state ([[§C3.1 Microstates and Statistical States in Parallel#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]); it is not the mixture $\tfrac12|ab\rangle\langle ab| + \tfrac12|ba\rangle\langle ba|$ of labelled assignments, and the relative sign has observable interference effects. With both particles in one orbital, $\Psi_{aa}^{(+)} = \phi_a(x_1)\phi_a(x_2)$ and $\Psi_{aa}^{(-)} = 0$.
>
> *Model:* two identical particles, orbitals orthonormal; *applies:* [[§C3.5 Many-Particle States#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]].
>
> *Source: Mao §3.5.2, eqs. (3.57)–(3.59) · QM home: [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^pr-b7-1-4|QM Principle §B7.1.4]]*

^thm-c3-5-2

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (exchange). Swapping $x_1 \leftrightarrow x_2$ swaps the two products, so the $+$ combination is unchanged and the $-$ combination changes sign, as [[§C3.5 Many-Particle States#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]] requires.
>
> **Step 2** (norm). The two products each have norm $1$ and overlap $\langle\phi_a|\phi_b\rangle\langle\phi_b|\phi_a\rangle = 0$, so the bracket has norm squared $2$, cancelled by $1/\sqrt2$.
>
> **Step 3** (same orbital). With $b = a$ the two products coincide: their sum is $2\phi_a\phi_a$, normalized to $\phi_a\phi_a$, and their difference is zero.

^der-c3-5-1

*Uses:* [[§C3.5 Many-Particle States#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.3: $N$ Identical Particles
> Let the list $a_1, \dots, a_N$ contain $n_\ell$ copies of orbital $\ell$. The normalized bosonic and fermionic states are
>
> $$
> \Psi_B(x_1, \dots, x_N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!\prod_\ell n_\ell!}}\sum_{\pi\in S_N}\prod_{i=1}^N\phi_{a_{\pi(i)}}(x_i), \qquad \Psi_F(x_1, \dots, x_N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det\bigl[\phi_{a_j}(x_i)\bigr]_{i,j=1}^N ,
> $$
>
> the second a **Slater determinant**. $\Psi_B$ is even and $\Psi_F$ odd under any exchange $x_i \leftrightarrow x_k$.
>
> *Model:* $N$ identical particles in orthonormal orbitals; *applies:* [[§C3.5 Many-Particle States#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]].
>
> *Source: Mao §3.5.2, eqs. (3.60)–(3.61) · QM homes: [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^def-b7-1-3|QM Def. §B7.1.3]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-6|QM Theorem §B7.1.6]]*

^thm-c3-5-3

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1** (symmetry). Exchanging $x_i$ and $x_k$ composes each permutation $\pi$ with a transposition, which permutes the terms of the bosonic sum among themselves. In the determinant it exchanges two rows, which flips the sign.
>
> **Step 2** (bosonic norm). Among the $N!$ products, those that differ only by permuting equal orbitals coincide: each distinct product occurs $\prod_\ell n_\ell!$ times. The number of distinct, mutually orthogonal products is $N!/\prod_\ell n_\ell!$, so the sum is $\prod_\ell n_\ell!$ times a sum of that many orthonormal products, with norm squared $(\prod_\ell n_\ell!)^2\cdot N!/\prod_\ell n_\ell! = N!\prod_\ell n_\ell!$.
>
> **Step 3** (fermionic norm). For distinct orbitals the determinant is a sum of $N!$ orthonormal products with signs, norm squared $N!$.

^der-c3-5-2

*Uses:* [[§C3.5 Many-Particle States#^pr-c3-5-1|Principle §C3.5.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.4: Pauli Exclusion Principle
> A fermionic state built from one-particle states $\phi_{a_1}, \dots, \phi_{a_N}$ vanishes as soon as two of them are equal. Here "one-particle state" includes spin: for spin-$\tfrac12$ particles two electrons can have the same spatial orbital $\phi$ only in the spin singlet, $\phi(\mathbf r_1)\phi(\mathbf r_2)(|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle - |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle)/\sqrt2$.
>
> *Model:* identical fermions; *applies:* states written as Slater determinants.
>
> *Source: Mao §3.5.2 · QM home: [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-5|QM Theorem §B7.1.5]]*

^thm-c3-5-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> For two particles, $\Psi_{aa}^{(-)} = 0$ ([[§C3.5 Many-Particle States#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]]). For $N$, two equal orbitals make two columns of the determinant in [[§C3.5 Many-Particle States#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]] equal, and a determinant with two equal columns is zero. For spin, the singlet is the antisymmetric spin state, so the product with a symmetric spatial part $\phi(\mathbf r_1)\phi(\mathbf r_2)$ is antisymmetric overall.

^der-c3-5-3

*Uses:* [[§C3.5 Many-Particle States#^thm-c3-5-2|Theorem §C3.5.2]], [[§C3.5 Many-Particle States#^thm-c3-5-3|Theorem §C3.5.3]]

## Occupation numbers

> [!definition] Definition §C3.5.3: Occupation-Number State
> For identical particles, a state is specified by how many particles occupy each one-particle orbital,
>
> $$
> |n_1, n_2, \dots\rangle ,
> $$
>
> with no further question of which particle is where. For bosons $n_\ell = 0, 1, 2, \dots$; for spinless fermions $n_\ell = 0, 1$, which is [[§C3.5 Many-Particle States#^thm-c3-5-4|Theorem §C3.5.4]] in this language. The symmetrized state with one boson in $a$ and one in $b$ is $n_a = n_b = 1$; both in $a$ is $n_a = 2$.
>
> *Source: Mao §3.5.3, eqs. (3.62)–(3.64) · Lecture 9/22 · QM home: [[§C12.2★ Second Quantization#^def-c12-2-1|QM Def. §C12.2.1]]*

^def-c3-5-3

> [!model] Model §C3.5.5: Noninteracting Particles
> Classically $H_N = \sum_{i=1}^Nh(X_i)$. Quantum mechanically, with one-particle levels $\hat h|\ell\rangle = \epsilon_\ell|\ell\rangle$,
>
> $$
> \hat H = \sum_\ell\epsilon_\ell\hat n_\ell, \qquad \hat N = \sum_\ell\hat n_\ell, \qquad E = \sum_\ell n_\ell\epsilon_\ell, \qquad N = \sum_\ell n_\ell
> $$
>
> on the occupation-number states, which are the many-body eigenstates; $\hat n_\ell$ counts the particles in orbital $\ell$, $\hat n_\ell|\dots n_\ell\dots\rangle = n_\ell|\dots n_\ell\dots\rangle$ ([[§C12.2★ Second Quantization#^def-c12-2-2|QM Def. §C12.2.2]]).
>
> *Assumes:* no interaction term. An additive $H$ does not make the particles statistically independent: a preparation or a global constraint (fixed total energy) still correlates them. Adding $\hat H_{\text{int}}$ mixes occupation states, so the exact eigenstates no longer have definite $n_\ell$.
>
> *Source: Mao §3.5.4, eqs. (3.65)–(3.69) · level B: [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^mod-b10-1-1|Model §B10.1.1]]*
