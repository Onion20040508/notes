Chapter C2 counted microstates without saying what they are ([[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^def-c2-1-1|Def. §C2.1.1]]). This chapter supplies the two answers, classical and quantum, and keeps them side by side: a point of phase space against a ray of Hilbert space, a probability density against a density operator. The quantum objects have their full homes in Quantum Mechanics ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator|QM §C11.1]]–[[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices|QM §C11.3]]) and the classical ones in Classical Mechanics ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^def-b7-2-1|CM Def. §B7.2.1]]); what is added here is the parallel, which is how statistical mechanics uses them.

**Setup.** A classical system with $f$ coordinate–momentum pairs, or a quantum system with Hilbert space $\mathcal H$. Particles are treated as distinguishable until [[§C3.5 Many-Particle States|§C3.5]].

## Pure microstates

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.1: Classical Microstate; Phase Space
> A classical microstate is a point
>
> $$
> X = (q_1, \dots, q_f;\ p_1, \dots, p_f) \equiv (q, p) \in \Gamma ,
> $$
>
> listing all $f$ canonical coordinate–momentum pairs. $\Gamma$ is the **phase space**.
>
> *Source: Mao §3.1 · CM home: [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^def-b7-2-1|CM Def. §B7.2.1]]*

^def-c3-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.2: Quantum Pure State
> A quantum pure state is a normalized vector $|\psi\rangle \in \mathcal H$; in an orthonormal basis $\{|\alpha\rangle\}$,
>
> $$
> |\psi\rangle = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha|\alpha\rangle, \qquad \sum_\alpha|c_\alpha|^2 = 1 .
> $$
>
> Vectors that differ by an overall phase describe the same physical state, a **ray**.
>
> *Source: Mao §3.1 · QM home: [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^pr-c1-2-1|QM Principle §C1.2.1]]*

^def-c3-1-2

## Counting classical states

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.3: Dimensionless Phase-Space Measure
> Classical statistical mechanics integrates with
>
> $$
> d\Gamma(X) = \frac{d^fq\,d^fp}{h^f} .
> $$
>
> The **delta density** of a point is normalized against it, $\int d\Gamma(X')\,\delta_\Gamma(X' - X)A(X') = A(X)$, and a **classical density** satisfies
>
> $$
> \rho_{\text{cl}}(X) \ge 0, \qquad \int_\Gamma d\Gamma(X)\,\rho_{\text{cl}}(X) = 1 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Mao §3.1, eqs. (3.1)–(3.3)*

^def-c3-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.1: One State per Cell of Area $h$
> A particle on a ring of length $L$ has momenta $p_n = 2\pi\hbar n/L$, spaced by $h/L$. A momentum window $\Delta p$ therefore holds about $L\Delta p/h$ states when it contains many levels: one state per area $h$ of the $(q, p)$ plane. So the cell $h^f$ in [[§C3.1 Microstates and Statistical States in Parallel#^def-c3-1-3|Def. §C3.1.3]] makes $\int d\Gamma$ count quantum states. Classical mechanics alone fixes no cell size; quantum counting does.
>
> *Model:* free particle on a ring; *applies:* many levels in the window ($L\Delta p \gg h$).
>
> *Source: Mao §3.1 · level B: [[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^rem-b6-1-1|Remark: Cells and shells]]*

^thm-c3-1-1

> [!derivation]- Derivation
> **Step 1.** Periodicity $\psi(x + L) = \psi(x)$ for $\psi \propto e^{ipx/\hbar}$ requires $pL/\hbar = 2\pi n$, so $p_n = 2\pi\hbar n/L = hn/L$.
>
> **Step 2.** In a window of width $\Delta p$ the number of $n$ is $\Delta p/(h/L) = L\Delta p/h$, up to $\pm1$ at the edges, negligible when $L\Delta p/h \gg 1$.
>
> **Step 3.** The phase-space area of the window is $L\Delta p$ (all $q$ on the ring times $\Delta p$), so the count is that area divided by $h$.

^der-c3-1-1

## The two columns

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.2: Classical and Quantum Descriptions Side by Side
> | | Classical | Quantum |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | pure microstate | $X = (q, p) \in \Gamma$ | $\lvert\psi\rangle = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha\lvert\alpha\rangle$ |
> | its density | $\rho_X(X') = \delta_\Gamma(X' - X)$ | $\hat\rho_\psi = \lvert\psi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert$ |
> | mean of $A$ in it | $A(X) = \int d\Gamma'\,\rho_X(X')A(X')$ | $\langle\psi\rvert\hat A\lvert\psi\rangle = \sum_{\alpha\beta}c_\alpha^{\ast}c_\beta A_{\alpha\beta}$ |
> | ensemble | $\rho_{\text{cl}} = \sum_np_n\delta_\Gamma(X - X_n)$ | $\hat\rho = \sum_np_n\lvert\psi_n\rangle\langle\psi_n\rvert$ |
> | mean of $A$ in it | $\int d\Gamma\,\rho_{\text{cl}}A = \sum_np_nA(X_n)$ | $\operatorname{Tr}(\hat\rho\hat A) = \sum_np_n\langle\psi_n\rvert\hat A\lvert\psi_n\rangle$ |
> | macrostate | a region $\Gamma_M \subseteq \Gamma$ (energy shell) | a subspace $\mathcal H_M \subseteq \mathcal H$ (energy shell) |
>
> with $A_{\alpha\beta} = \langle\alpha|\hat A|\beta\rangle$ and $p_n \ge 0$, $\sum_np_n = 1$. Inside one pure quantum state amplitudes combine **coherently** (all $c_\alpha^{\ast}c_\beta$ terms); across the members of an ensemble probabilities combine **incoherently** (a weighted sum of means).
>
> *Model:* [[§C3.1 Microstates and Statistical States in Parallel#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]], [[§C3.1 Microstates and Statistical States in Parallel#^def-c3-1-2|Def. §C3.1.2]]; *applies:* discrete sums over preparations; a continuous classical ensemble uses an integral.
>
> *Source: Mao §3.1, comparison table · Lecture 9/15 · the quantum rows are proved in [[§C3.2 The Density Operator as an Ensemble|§C3.2]]*

^thm-c3-1-2

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.4: Statistical State
> A **statistical state** is a density $\rho_{\text{cl}}$ on phase space or a density operator $\hat\rho$ on $\mathcal H$: the object from which every probability and every mean of the system's observables is computed.
>
> *Source: Mao §3.1*

^def-c3-1-4

> [!caution] Caution: A macrostate is not yet a statistical state
> A macrostate only draws a boundary, a region of $\Gamma$ or a subspace of $\mathcal H$; it is silent on how probability is distributed inside. Turning one into a statistical state ([[§C3.1 Microstates and Statistical States in Parallel#^def-c3-1-4|Def. §C3.1.4]]) needs a rule, and the microcanonical ensemble ([[§C2.1 Microstates, Macrostates and the Microcanonical Ensemble#^def-c2-1-4|Def. §C2.1.4]]) is the simplest one.
