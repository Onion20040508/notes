What does a measurement do to a ket, and when do two measurements get in each other's way? Level B answered with the generalized statistical interpretation, including the collapse onto a degenerate eigenspace ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]]), the theorem that commuting observables have a common eigenbasis ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-6|Theorem §B2.4.6]]), the generalized uncertainty principle ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]]) and the notion of a complete set of commuting observables ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]]); the rigorous form of the projectors is in Functional Analysis ([[§31 The Completeness Relation#^def-31-2|556 Def. §31.2]]). Level C reads measurement as *filtration*, the action of the Stern–Gerlach filter of [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space#^mod-c1-1-2|Model §C1.1.2]] as a projector. This gives the rule for filters in sequence, where an unrecorded intermediate alternative interferes and a recorded one does not; the labelling of states by a complete set of commuting observables, with what "maximal" means; and the theorem that measurements of compatible observables do not disturb each other. The uncertainty relation is level B's; the spin examples show what it does and does not bound.

*Notation.* As in [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space|§C1.1]]. $P_{a'}$ is the projector onto the eigenspace of $A$ with eigenvalue $a'$ ($P_{a'} = \Lambda_{a'} = |a'\rangle\langle a'|$ when $a'$ is nondegenerate, [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]]). Sakurai's $\Delta A \equiv A - \langle A\rangle$ is an **operator**; see the caution below.

## Measurement as filtration

The measurement postulate is level B's: a measurement of $A$ on a normalized $|\alpha\rangle$ yields an eigenvalue $a'$ with probability $\langle\alpha|P_{a'}|\alpha\rangle$, which is $|\langle a'|\alpha\rangle|^2$ for nondegenerate $a'$, and leaves the system in $P_{a'}|\alpha\rangle$ normalized ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]]; Sakurai's (1.95)–(1.97), with Dirac's sentence "a measurement always causes the system to jump into an eigenstate of the dynamical variable that is being measured"). The average of the outcomes is $\langle A\rangle = \langle\alpha|A|\alpha\rangle = \sum_{a'}a'\,|\langle a'|\alpha\rangle|^2$ (1.98)–(1.99), level B's [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^thm-b2-3-5|Theorem §B2.3.5]]; it can be any number between the extreme eigenvalues, $0.273\,\hbar$ for $S_z$ say, while each single outcome is $\pm\hbar/2$. The user's series states the two postulates in exactly this projector form (Part IV, §4.1). Probabilities refer to an ensemble of identically prepared systems.

> [!definition] Definition §C1.3.1: Selective Measurement (Filtration); Pure Ensemble
> A **selective measurement**, or **filtration**, of an observable $A$ is a measurement that keeps only the systems found with one value $a'$ and rejects the rest. On a normalized input $|\alpha\rangle$ it acts as the projector,
>
> $$
> |\alpha\rangle \longmapsto P_{a'}|\alpha\rangle \qquad \bigl(= |a'\rangle\langle a'|\alpha\rangle \ \text{if } a' \text{ is nondegenerate}\bigr), \qquad (1.100)
> $$
>
> the fraction transmitted is $\|P_{a'}|\alpha\rangle\|^2 = \langle\alpha|P_{a'}|\alpha\rangle$, and every transmitted system is in the normalized state $P_{a'}|\alpha\rangle/\|P_{a'}|\alpha\rangle\|$. A collection of identically prepared systems all described by one ket is a **pure ensemble**; the output of a filter for a nondegenerate $a'$ is a pure ensemble in $|a'\rangle$, whatever the input. Schwinger's measurement symbol $M(a')$ is $\Lambda_{a'}$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.4.1, eq. (1.100), Fig. 1.7 · the user's 511 notes, §"Selective Measurement (Filtration)" · the user's series, Part IV, §4.1*

^def-c1-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: A projector is a yes/no observable
> Every projector $P$ is itself an observable with eigenvalues $1$ ("passes") and $0$ ("is blocked"), since $P^2 = P$ forces $\lambda^2 = \lambda$ ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]). For two normalized states with $\langle a|b\rangle = \alpha \ne 0$, measuring $P_{\bar a} = 1 - |a\rangle\langle a|$ ("is the system *not* in $|a\rangle$?") gives $0$ with certainty on $|a\rangle$, and on $|b\rangle$ gives $1$ with probability $\langle b|P_{\bar a}|b\rangle = 1 - |\alpha|^2$ and $0$ with probability $|\alpha|^2$. Measuring twice in a row gives the same answer, because $P^2 = P$: the series' "asking twice equals asking once".
>
> *Source: PHY 511, Final Practice Problems, Problem 2; solved here · the user's series, Part IV, §4.1*

^rem-c1-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.3.1: Filters in Sequence
> Let $A$, $B$, $C$ be observables with nondegenerate spectra, and let a beam leave an $A$-filter in $|a'\rangle$, normalized to unit intensity.
> 1. **Recorded routes.** A $B$-filter for $b'$ followed by a $C$-filter for $c'$ transmits the fraction $|\langle c'|b'\rangle|^2|\langle b'|a'\rangle|^2$ (1.138). Summing over the $B$-routes, opened one at a time,
>
> $$
> \sum_{b'}|\langle c'|b'\rangle|^2\,|\langle b'|a'\rangle|^2 = \sum_{b'}\langle c'|b'\rangle\langle b'|a'\rangle\langle a'|b'\rangle\langle b'|c'\rangle . \qquad (1.139)
> $$
>
> 2. **No $B$-filter.** Without the $B$-filter (or with all its routes open and nothing recorded) the fraction is
>
> $$
> |\langle c'|a'\rangle|^2 = \Bigl|\sum_{b'}\langle c'|b'\rangle\langle b'|a'\rangle\Bigr|^2 = \sum_{b'}\sum_{b''}\langle c'|b'\rangle\langle b'|a'\rangle\langle a'|b''\rangle\langle b''|c'\rangle . \qquad (1.140)
> $$
>
> 3. **Interference.** The two differ by the cross terms,
>
> $$
> (1.140) - (1.139) = 2\sum_{b' < b''}\operatorname{Re}\bigl[\langle c'|b'\rangle\langle b'|a'\rangle\langle a'|b''\rangle\langle b''|c'\rangle\bigr] :
> $$
>
> amplitudes of unrecorded alternatives add before squaring, probabilities of recorded ones add after.
> 4. **Compatible case.** If $[A, B] = 0$ or $[B, C] = 0$, the two expressions are equal (1.142).
>
> Level B's sequence $A$, $B$, $A$ ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^ex-b2-3-3|Example §B2.3.3]]) is the case $C = A$, $c' = a'$, where (1.140) is $1$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.4.4, eqs. (1.138)–(1.142), Fig. 1.8 (part 4 left there as an exercise; proved here) · the user's 511 notes, §"Sequential Measurements: The Heart of Quantum Mechanics"*

^thm-c1-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* By [[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]] the $B$-filter passes the fraction $|\langle b'|a'\rangle|^2$ of the $|a'\rangle$ beam and leaves it in $|b'\rangle$; the $C$-filter passes the fraction $|\langle c'|b'\rangle|^2$ of that. Fractions of successive filters multiply. With one route open at a time the runs are separate experiments, and their transmitted intensities add; writing $|z|^2 = zz^{\ast}$ and $\langle b'|a'\rangle^{\ast} = \langle a'|b'\rangle$ gives (1.139).
>
> *2.* Without the $B$-filter the beam goes straight from $|a'\rangle$ to the $C$-filter, which passes $|\langle c'|a'\rangle|^2$. Insert the completeness relation of $B$, $\langle c'|a'\rangle = \sum_{b'}\langle c'|b'\rangle\langle b'|a'\rangle$, and expand the square.
>
> *3.* In the double sum (1.140) the terms with $b'' = b'$ are (1.139). The terms with $b'' \ne b'$ come in pairs $(b', b'')$, $(b'', b')$ that are complex conjugates of each other, so each pair contributes twice its real part.
>
> *4.* If $[A, B] = 0$ and the spectra are nondegenerate, $|a'\rangle$ is an eigenket of $B$ ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-6|Theorem §B2.4.6]], part 2), so $|a'\rangle = e^{i\varphi}|b_0\rangle$ for one eigenvalue $b_0$, and $\langle b'|a'\rangle = e^{i\varphi}\delta_{b'b_0}$. Both (1.139) and (1.140) reduce to $|\langle c'|b_0\rangle|^2$. If $[B, C] = 0$, each $|b'\rangle$ is likewise $e^{i\psi_{b'}}$ times an eigenket of $C$, so $\langle c'|b'\rangle = e^{i\psi_{b_1}}\delta_{b'b_1}$ for the one $b_1$ whose eigenket belongs to $c'$; both expressions reduce to $|\langle b_1|a'\rangle|^2$. In either case all cross terms vanish.

With the $B$-filter keeping only $+$, the fraction is $\cos^2\frac\theta2\sin^2\frac\theta2$; opening its other route as a separate run adds $\sin^2\frac\theta2\cos^2\frac\theta2$, so the recorded sum (1.139) is $\tfrac12\sin^2\theta$. If both routes are open and nothing records which one an atom took, the beams recombine into $|+\rangle$ and (1.140) gives $|\langle-|+\rangle|^2 = 0$: the cross term $-\tfrac12\sin^2\theta$ cancels everything. > [!caution] Caution: Δ is an operator here
> Sakurai and the user's 511 notes write $\Delta A \equiv A - \langle A\rangle$ for an **operator** (1.143) and $\langle(\Delta A)^2\rangle = \langle A^2\rangle - \langle A\rangle^2$ for the **dispersion** (variance) (1.144); the uncertainty relation reads $\langle(\Delta A)^2\rangle\langle(\Delta B)^2\rangle \ge \frac14|\langle[A, B]\rangle|^2$ (1.146). Level B and Griffiths write $\sigma_A^2$ for the dispersion ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]]), and the user's series writes $\Delta A$ for the standard deviation $\sqrt{\langle(\Delta A)^2\rangle}$. So "$\Delta A\,\Delta B \ge \dots$" is a statement about numbers in the series and about operators nowhere.
>

## Compatible observables and complete sets

That commuting observables have a complete orthonormal set of simultaneous eigenkets, and that observables with such a set commute, is level B's [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-6|Theorem §B2.4.6]]. Sakurai's proof of the nondegenerate case (his Theorem 2) is one line in matrix elements: $\langle a''|[A, B]|a'\rangle = (a'' - a')\langle a''|B|a'\rangle = 0$, so $B$ is diagonal in the eigenbasis of $A$ and $B|a'\rangle = \langle a'|B|a'\rangle\,|a'\rangle$ (1.121)–(1.125). With degeneracy the same computation shows that $B$ is block-diagonal, one block per eigenspace of $A$, and each block is diagonalized separately ([[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]]). Conversely, incompatible observables have no *complete* set of simultaneous eigenkets, since on such a set $AB|a', b'\rangle = a'b'|a', b'\rangle = BA|a', b'\rangle$ (1.135)–(1.137).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.3.2: Labelling by a Complete Set of Commuting Observables
> Let $A, B, C, \dots$ be finitely many mutually commuting observables on a finite-dimensional ket space, $[A, B] = [B, C] = [A, C] = \cdots = 0$ (1.129).
> 1. There is an orthonormal basis of simultaneous eigenkets $|K'\rangle = |a', b', c', \dots\rangle$; the subspaces of kets sharing one list of eigenvalues are mutually orthogonal and together span the space, and the projector onto each is the product $P_{a'}P_{b'}P_{c'}\cdots$.
> 2. If the set is complete ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]]), each list $K' = (a', b', c', \dots)$ that occurs belongs to exactly one basis ket up to phase, and
>
> $$
> \langle K''|K'\rangle = \delta_{K'K''} = \delta_{a'a''}\delta_{b'b''}\delta_{c'c''}\cdots, \qquad \sum_{K'}|K'\rangle\langle K'| = \sum_{a'}\sum_{b'}\sum_{c'}\cdots|a', b', c', \dots\rangle\langle a', b', c', \dots| = 1 . \qquad (1.131),\ (1.132)
> $$
>
> Filtering successively for $a'$, $b'$, $c'$, … prepares the single state $|K'\rangle$, whatever the input.
> 3. **"Maximal" made precise.** The set is complete if and only if every operator that commutes with all of $A, B, C, \dots$ is a function (a polynomial) of them. So a complete set cannot be enlarged by an observable that is independent of it, which is Sakurai's description of a *maximal set of commuting observables*.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.4.3, eqs. (1.126)–(1.132) · the user's 511 notes, §"Maximal Set of Commuting Observables" · the user's series, Part IV, §4.2; parts 1 and 3 proved here*

^thm-c1-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* By induction on the number of operators. For $A$ alone, its eigenspaces are orthogonal and span the space ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], [[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]). Each operator commuting with $A$ maps every eigenspace of $A$ into itself ([[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-75|LADR Thm. 5.75]]) and restricts there to a Hermitian operator; the restrictions of $B, C, \dots$ still commute, so the induction hypothesis applies inside each eigenspace of $A$. Collecting the orthonormal bases so obtained gives the basis $\{|K'\rangle\}$ (this is [[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-76|LADR Thm. 5.76]] for a commuting family). The spectral projectors are polynomials in their operators ([[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]], part 4), hence commute, and a product of commuting orthogonal projectors projects onto the intersection of their ranges, which is the joint eigenspace.
>
> *2.* Completeness means that each joint eigenspace of part 1 is one-dimensional. Then the basis kets are fixed up to phase by their labels, two kets with different labels are orthogonal (they lie in different joint eigenspaces), and the completeness relation of the basis is (1.132). The product of filters is the projector $|K'\rangle\langle K'|$, whose output is $|K'\rangle$ for every input it does not block.
>
> *3.* Suppose the set is complete and $[X, A] = [X, B] = \cdots = 0$. Then $X$ maps each one-dimensional joint eigenspace into itself, so $X|K'\rangle = x(K')|K'\rangle$ and $X = \sum_{K'}x(K')|K'\rangle\langle K'|$. There are finitely many distinct labels $K'$; for each pair $K' \ne K''$ choose an operator in the list, say $A$, whose eigenvalues differ there, and form $F_{K'} = \prod_{K''\ne K'}(A - a'')/(a' - a'')$ (with the chosen operator for each $K''$). $F_{K'}$ is a polynomial in the list that acts as $1$ on $|K'\rangle$ and $0$ on every other basis ket, so $X = \sum_{K'}x(K')F_{K'}$. Conversely, if some joint eigenspace has dimension at least $2$, pick in it two orthonormal kets $|u\rangle$, $|v\rangle$ and let $X = |u\rangle\langle u| - |v\rangle\langle v|$. $X$ commutes with every operator of the list, since each acts as a number on the joint eigenspace, which contains the range of $X$; but every polynomial in the list acts as one number on that eigenspace, while $X$ does not.

> [!caution] Caution: "Cannot add any more observables"
> Sakurai (and the user's 511 notes after him) define a maximal set as one to which no observable can be added without violating (1.129). Taken literally this never happens: $A^2$, $AB$ or any function of the list commutes with all of it. The operative content is part 3 above: no observable *outside the algebra generated by the list* commutes with it, which is the same as level B's requirement that the eigenvalue list determines the state. With degeneracy, the label $|a'\rangle$ alone is ambiguous and a second observable is needed to split the eigenspace ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]] shows why symmetries force this); for orbital angular momentum $\ell = 1$ leaves $m_\ell$ open and $m_\ell = 1$ leaves $\ell$ open, while $(\ell, m_\ell)$ together fix the angular state.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.4.3 (after (1.128)) · the user's 511 notes, §"Maximal Set of Commuting Observables"*

^cau-c1-3-2

> [!example] Example §C1.3.1: Two Degenerate Observables That Label Together
> In a four-dimensional space with orthonormal basis $|1\rangle, \dots, |4\rangle$, let $x \ne 0$ and $q \ne z$ be real and
>
> $$
> A \doteq \begin{pmatrix} 0&x&0&0\\ x&0&0&0\\ 0&0&0&x\\ 0&0&x&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad B \doteq \begin{pmatrix} q&0&0&0\\ 0&q&0&0\\ 0&0&z&0\\ 0&0&0&z\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> *Spectrum of $A$.* $A$ is block-diagonal with two blocks $x\sigma_x$, each with eigenvalues $\pm x$; so $A$ has eigenvalues $x$ and $-x$, each **doubly degenerate**.
>
> *Commutator.* $B = q\,1$ on the first block and $z\,1$ on the second, and $A$ maps each block into itself, so $AB = BA$: $[A, B] = 0$. $B$ is diagonal already, but its eigenvalues are degenerate too, and its "eigenbasis" $\{|i\rangle\}$ is only one choice: any orthonormal basis of $\mathrm{span}\{|1\rangle, |2\rangle\}$ and of $\mathrm{span}\{|3\rangle, |4\rangle\}$ diagonalizes $B$.
>
> *Simultaneous eigenkets.* Diagonalize $A$ inside each eigenspace of $B$:
>
> | ket | $A$ | $B$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\lvert1\rangle + \lvert2\rangle)$ | $x$ | $q$ |
> | $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\lvert1\rangle - \lvert2\rangle)$ | $-x$ | $q$ |
> | $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\lvert3\rangle + \lvert4\rangle)$ | $x$ | $z$ |
> | $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\lvert3\rangle - \lvert4\rangle)$ | $-x$ | $z$ |
>
> The four lists $(a', b')$ are distinct, so neither $A$ nor $B$ alone is a complete set, but $\{A, B\}$ is ([[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty#^thm-c1-3-2|Theorem §C1.3.2]]): a measurement of $A$ that gives $x$ leaves the state anywhere in a plane, and a subsequent measurement of $B$ fixes it. The transformation matrix to this basis, with the four kets as columns, is block-diagonal with two blocks $\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$ ([[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence#^thm-c1-4-1|Theorem §C1.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 3, Problem 2; solved here*

^ex-c1-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.3.3: Compatible Measurements Do Not Disturb Each Other
> Let $[A, B] = 0$. If $A$ is measured with result $a'$, then $B$ is measured, then $A$ again, the third measurement gives $a'$ with certainty, whatever the result $b'$ of the second and whether or not $a'$ is degenerate:
>
> $$
> |\alpha\rangle \xrightarrow{A}\ |a', b'\rangle \xrightarrow{B}\ |a', b'\rangle \xrightarrow{A}\ |a', b'\rangle \quad\text{(nondegenerate case)} . \qquad (1.133)
> $$
>
> Moreover the probability of obtaining $a'$ and then $b'$ equals that of obtaining $b'$ and then $a'$, namely $\langle\alpha|P_{a'}P_{b'}|\alpha\rangle$: the order of compatible measurements does not matter.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.4.3, eqs. (1.133)–(1.134) · the user's 511 notes, §"Sequential Measurements" · the user's series, Part IV, §4.2; the order statement proved here*

^thm-c1-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $P_{a'}$ is a polynomial in $A$ and $P_{b'}$ a polynomial in $B$ ([[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]], part 4), so $[A, B] = 0$ gives $P_{a'}P_{b'} = P_{b'}P_{a'}$. After the first measurement the state is proportional to $P_{a'}|\alpha\rangle$; after the second, to $P_{b'}P_{a'}|\alpha\rangle = P_{a'}\bigl(P_{b'}|\alpha\rangle\bigr)$, which lies in the range of $P_{a'}$. On such a ket the probability of $a'$ is $\langle\cdot|P_{a'}|\cdot\rangle/\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle = 1$. The probability of $a'$ then $b'$ is $\|P_{b'}P_{a'}|\alpha\rangle\|^2 = \langle\alpha|P_{a'}P_{b'}P_{b'}P_{a'}|\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|P_{a'}P_{b'}|\alpha\rangle$, using $P^\dagger = P = P^2$ and commutativity; the expression is symmetric in the two projectors. In the nondegenerate case $P_{a'} = |a'\rangle\langle a'|$ and $|a'\rangle$ is already an eigenket of $B$ ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-6|Theorem §B2.4.6]]), which is (1.133).

> [!remark] Remark: Incompatible observables can still share some eigenkets
> Failure to commute forbids a complete set of common eigenkets, not every common eigenket. The $\ell = 0$ state is an eigenket of both $L_x$ and $L_z$, with eigenvalue $0$, although $[L_x, L_z] \ne 0$; the subspace on which (1.137) holds is one-dimensional. For **anticommuting** Hermitian operators, $\{A, B\} = 0$, a common eigenket would satisfy $a'b' = -b'a'$, so one of the two eigenvalues must vanish (Sakurai Problem 1.18). An example from PHY 511: on states $|\psi_q\rangle$ of definite charge, $Q|\psi_q\rangle = q|\psi_q\rangle$ and charge conjugation $C|\psi_q\rangle = |\psi_{-q}\rangle$; then $(CQ + QC)|\psi_q\rangle = q|\psi_{-q}\rangle - q|\psi_{-q}\rangle = 0$, so $\{C, Q\} = 0$, and the only common eigenkets are the neutral ones, $q = 0$. If a charge measurement leaves a state that is also an eigenket of $C$, the charge found was $0$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.4.4 (after (1.137)); Problem 1.18 · PHY 511, Midterm Practice, Problem M1; solved here*

## The uncertainty relation

Sakurai's proof of $\langle(\Delta A)^2\rangle\langle(\Delta B)^2\rangle \ge \frac14|\langle[A, B]\rangle|^2$ (1.146) is level B's ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]]): the Schwarz inequality for $\Delta A|\alpha\rangle$ and $\Delta B|\alpha\rangle$, then $\Delta A\,\Delta B = \frac12[A, B] + \frac12\{\Delta A, \Delta B\}$ with a purely imaginary and a purely real expectation value (part 4 of [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]), and finally dropping the anticommutator term (1.152)–(1.156). Keeping that term gives the Robertson–Schrödinger form of [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^rem-b2-4-2|Remark: A stronger form]]. What level C adds are the spin tests below. The quantities bounded are spreads of outcome statistics over many identically prepared systems, each measured for one observable; the relation does not by itself say how much a measurement of $A$ disturbs a later one of $B$ (the user's series, Part IV, §4.3).

> [!example] Example §C1.3.2: Uncertainty of an Arbitrary Spin State
> For the spin state $|\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n};+\rangle = \cos\frac\beta2|+\rangle + e^{i\alpha}\sin\frac\beta2|-\rangle$, compute $\langle(\Delta S_x)^2\rangle$ and $\langle(\Delta S_y)^2\rangle$ and test the uncertainty relation for $S_x$, $S_y$.
>
> *Dispersions.* The state has $\langle\mathbf S\rangle = \frac\hbar2\hat{\mathbf n} = \frac\hbar2(\sin\beta\cos\alpha, \sin\beta\sin\alpha, \cos\beta)$ and $S_x^2 = S_y^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}{4}$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]], [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^ex-c1-2-1|Example §C1.2.1]]), so
>
> $$
> \langle(\Delta S_x)^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}4\bigl(1 - \sin^2\beta\cos^2\alpha\bigr), \qquad \langle(\Delta S_y)^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}4\bigl(1 - \sin^2\beta\sin^2\alpha\bigr) .
> $$
>
> *The test.* The product is $\frac{\hbar^4}{16}\bigl[1 - \sin^2\beta + \sin^4\beta\sin^2\alpha\cos^2\alpha\bigr]$, and the bound is $\frac14|\langle[S_x, S_y]\rangle|^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}{4}\langle S_z\rangle^2 = \frac{\hbar^4}{16}\cos^2\beta$. Hence
>
> $$
> \langle(\Delta S_x)^2\rangle\langle(\Delta S_y)^2\rangle - \tfrac14\bigl|\langle[S_x, S_y]\rangle\bigr|^2 = \frac{\hbar^4}{64}\,\sin^4\beta\,\sin^2 2\alpha \ \ge 0 ,
> $$
>
> with equality exactly when $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is along $\pm\hat{\mathbf z}$ or in the $xz$- or $yz$-plane. For $|S_x;+\rangle$ ($\beta = \pi/2$, $\alpha = 0$): $\langle(\Delta S_x)^2\rangle = 0$, $\langle(\Delta S_y)^2\rangle = \hbar^2/4$, both sides vanish (Sakurai Problem 1.3); for $|+\rangle$ ($\beta = 0$) both sides equal $\hbar^4/16$, the state saturates the bound (Problem 1.21).
>
> *The gap is the anticommutator term.* $\{S_x, S_y\} = 0$, so $\langle\{\Delta S_x, \Delta S_y\}\rangle = -2\langle S_x\rangle\langle S_y\rangle$, and $\frac14\langle\{\Delta S_x, \Delta S_y\}\rangle^2 = \langle S_x\rangle^2\langle S_y\rangle^2 = \frac{\hbar^4}{64}\sin^4\beta\sin^22\alpha$: exactly the gap. Every spin-$\frac12$ state saturates the Robertson–Schrödinger inequality. The reason is the dimension: $\Delta A|\alpha\rangle$ and $\Delta B|\alpha\rangle$ are both orthogonal to $|\alpha\rangle$, since $\langle\alpha|\Delta A|\alpha\rangle = 0$, and in a two-dimensional space the vectors orthogonal to $|\alpha\rangle$ form a line; so the two are parallel and the Schwarz inequality, the only inequality in the stronger form, is an equality.
>
> *What the bound does not say.* On the equator ($\beta = \pi/2$) the bound is $0$, yet $S_x$ and $S_y$ are never both sharp: $\sum_i\langle(\Delta S_i)^2\rangle = \frac34\hbar^2 - |\langle\mathbf S\rangle|^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}2$ for every state, and with $\langle(\Delta S_z)^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}4$ there, $\langle(\Delta S_x)^2\rangle + \langle(\Delta S_y)^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}4$. (By the sum rule, one of the two always has a dispersion of at least $\hbar^2/8$ there.) The product is largest, $\hbar^4/16$, for the eigenkets of $S_z$ (Stony Brook HW 3, Problem 3).
>

> [!example] Example §C1.3.3: An Eavesdropper on a Spin Key
> Alice sends Bob spin-$\frac12$ atoms prepared in $|+\rangle$; Bob measures $S_z$. Eve intercepts each atom, measures its spin along an axis in the $xz$-plane at angle $\theta$ from $\hat{\mathbf z}$, chosen at random, and sends the atom on. What does Eve see, how often do Alice and Bob agree, and what is the average over a uniformly random $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$?
>
> *Eve.* With $|\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n};\pm\rangle$ for $\hat{\mathbf n}$ at angle $\theta$, she finds $+\frac\hbar2$ with probability $\cos^2\frac\theta2$ and $-\frac\hbar2$ with probability $\sin^2\frac\theta2$ ([[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^ex-c1-2-2|Example §C1.2.2]]), and the atom leaves in the corresponding eigenket.
>
> *Bob.* From $|\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n};+\rangle$ he finds $+\frac\hbar2$ with probability $\cos^2\frac\theta2$; from $|\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n};-\rangle$ with probability $\sin^2\frac\theta2$. So
>
> $$
> P(\text{same};\theta) = \cos^4\frac\theta2 + \sin^4\frac\theta2 = 1 - \frac12\sin^2\theta, \qquad \overline{P(\text{same})} = 1 - \frac12\cdot\frac12 = \frac34 ,
> $$
>
> using $\overline{\sin^2\theta} = \frac12$ over a full period. Without Eve they always agree. Eve's measurement is a filter for an observable incompatible with $S_z$ unless $\theta = 0$ or $\pi$, and it overwrites Alice's preparation (part 4 of [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space#^law-c1-1-1|Law §C1.1.1]]).
>
> *The key protocol.* In the PHY 511 activity Alice sends photons polarized in one of two bases chosen at random, "+" (horizontal/vertical) or "×" (diagonal), Bob measures in a basis of his own random choice, and afterwards they compare bases, not results, keeping only the bits where the bases matched. The two polarization bases are $45^\circ$ apart, which for spin means perpendicular axes ([[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space#^rem-c1-1-2|Remark: The polarization analogy]]); a mismatched detector returns either answer with probability $\frac12$, "just like if you used a SGz device to measure an electron in the $|+x\rangle$ state". An eavesdropper who measures in one of the two bases at random spoils the matched bits with probability $\frac12\cdot\frac12 = \frac14$, the same $\frac14$ as the random-angle Eve above. Alice and Bob detect her by publicly comparing a sample of their kept bits: an error rate near $25\%$ betrays an interception.
>
