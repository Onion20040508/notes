Two incompatible observables $A$ and $B$ give two orthonormal bases of the same ket space, $\{|a'\rangle\}$ and $\{|b'\rangle\}$: how are the descriptions in the two bases related, and how is the basis of $B$ found from the matrix of $B$ in the basis of $A$? Level B gave the transformation rules for components and matrix elements, with a unitary matrix ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]]), and Linear Algebra supplies change of basis, unitary operators, the spectral theorem and the trace ([[§10 Invertibility and Isomorphisms#^ladr-3-84|LADR Thm. 3.84]], [[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-51|LADR Def. 7.51]], [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]], [[§31 Trace꞉ A Connection Between Matrices and Operators#^ladr-8-50|LADR Thm. 8.50]]). Level C adds the *transformation operator*, an operator on the ket space that carries the old base kets into the new ones, with Sakurai's conventions for its matrix; the trace as a basis-free number; diagonalization as the construction of that operator; and *unitary equivalent observables*, which share their spectrum, the reason why $S_x$ and $S_z$ have the same eigenvalues.

*Notation.* As in [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space|§C1.1]]. The old basis is $\{|a^{(k)}\rangle\}$, the new $\{|b^{(k)}\rangle\}$, $k = 1, \dots, N$. A matrix "in the old basis" has entries $\langle a^{(k)}|X|a^{(l)}\rangle$, row $k$, column $l$.

## The transformation operator

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.1: Transformation Operator and Transformation Matrix
> Let $\{|a^{(k)}\rangle\}$ and $\{|b^{(k)}\rangle\}$ be two orthonormal, complete sets of base kets.
> 1. **The operator.** There is a unitary operator $U$, $U^\dagger U = UU^\dagger = 1$ (1.158)–(1.159), with $|b^{(k)}\rangle = U|a^{(k)}\rangle$ for every $k$ (1.157), namely
>
> $$
> U = \sum_k|b^{(k)}\rangle\langle a^{(k)}| . \qquad (1.160)
> $$
>
> 2. **Its matrix.** In the old basis $U$ has the matrix elements
>
> $$
> \langle a^{(k)}|U|a^{(l)}\rangle = \langle a^{(k)}|b^{(l)}\rangle , \qquad (1.163)
> $$
>
> the **transformation matrix** from $\{|a'\rangle\}$ to $\{|b'\rangle\}$: its $l$-th column lists the old components of the new base ket $|b^{(l)}\rangle$.
> 3. **Components.** If $|\alpha\rangle$ has the column of components $\langle a^{(l)}|\alpha\rangle$ in the old basis, its components in the new basis are
>
> $$
> \langle b^{(k)}|\alpha\rangle = \sum_l\langle a^{(k)}|U^\dagger|a^{(l)}\rangle\langle a^{(l)}|\alpha\rangle, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad (\text{new}) = (U^\dagger)(\text{old}) . \qquad (1.166)-(1.167)
> $$
>
> 4. **Operators.** The matrix of an operator $X$ in the new basis is obtained from its matrix in the old basis by a similarity transformation:
>
> $$
> \langle b^{(k)}|X|b^{(l)}\rangle = \sum_{m, n}\langle a^{(k)}|U^\dagger|a^{(m)}\rangle\langle a^{(m)}|X|a^{(n)}\rangle\langle a^{(n)}|U|a^{(l)}\rangle, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad X' = U^\dagger XU . \qquad (1.168)-(1.169)
> $$
>
> In three-dimensional space the analogue is the matrix of dot products $\hat{\mathbf x}\cdot\hat{\mathbf x}'$, $\hat{\mathbf x}\cdot\hat{\mathbf y}'$, … of old with new unit vectors (1.164). Parts 3–4 are level B's [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]] in Sakurai's convention (see the caution below); part 1 is new.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.5.1–1.5.2, eqs. (1.157)–(1.169), Theorem 3 · the user's 511 notes, §"Change of Basis"*

^thm-c1-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* By the associative axiom ([[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^pr-c1-2-1|Principle §C1.2.1]]) and orthonormality of the old set, $U|a^{(l)}\rangle = \sum_k|b^{(k)}\rangle\langle a^{(k)}|a^{(l)}\rangle = |b^{(l)}\rangle$ (1.161). By part 2 of [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]], $U^\dagger = \sum_l|a^{(l)}\rangle\langle b^{(l)}|$, so
>
> $$
> U^\dagger U = \sum_{k, l}|a^{(l)}\rangle\langle b^{(l)}|b^{(k)}\rangle\langle a^{(k)}| = \sum_k|a^{(k)}\rangle\langle a^{(k)}| = 1 \qquad (1.162)
> $$
>
> by orthonormality of the new set and completeness of the old; $UU^\dagger = \sum_k|b^{(k)}\rangle\langle b^{(k)}| = 1$ in the same way.
>
> *2.* Apply $\langle a^{(k)}|$ to $U|a^{(l)}\rangle = |b^{(l)}\rangle$.
>
> *3.* Insert the completeness relation of the old set: $\langle b^{(k)}|\alpha\rangle = \sum_l\langle b^{(k)}|a^{(l)}\rangle\langle a^{(l)}|\alpha\rangle$, and $\langle b^{(k)}|a^{(l)}\rangle = \langle a^{(l)}|b^{(k)}\rangle^* = \langle a^{(l)}|U|a^{(k)}\rangle^* = \langle a^{(k)}|U^\dagger|a^{(l)}\rangle$ by (1.53).
>
> *4.* Insert the old completeness relation on both sides of $X$ and use part 3 for $\langle b^{(k)}|a^{(m)}\rangle$ and part 2 for $\langle a^{(n)}|b^{(l)}\rangle$.

*Uses:* [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^pr-c1-2-1|Principle §C1.2.1]], [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]], [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-53|LADR Thm. 7.53]]

> [!caution] Caution: Which matrix is called U
> Level B defines the change-of-basis matrix with **rows** labelled by the new basis, $\mathsf U_{mn} = \langle e'_m|e_n\rangle$, so that $\mathsf a' = \mathsf U\mathsf a$ and $\mathsf Q' = \mathsf U\mathsf Q\mathsf U^\dagger$ ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]]). Sakurai's transformation matrix has **columns** labelled by the new basis, $\langle a^{(k)}|U|a^{(l)}\rangle = \langle a^{(k)}|b^{(l)}\rangle$, so (new) $= U^\dagger$(old) and $X' = U^\dagger XU$. The two matrices are adjoints of each other, $\mathsf U = U^\dagger$, and the formulas agree. Check a source's convention by asking which index of $U$ labels the new basis. The "Diagonalization: Detailed Explanation" pages of the user's 511 notes define $U = \sum_i|a^{(i)}\rangle\langle i|$ and state that column $j$ is the eigenvector $|a^{(j)}\rangle$, $U_{ij} = \langle i|a^{(j)}\rangle$, which is Sakurai's convention; the matrix displayed there, with first row $\langle1|a^{(1)}\rangle, \langle2|a^{(1)}\rangle, \dots$, is the transpose of that rule, while the later "Two Completeness Relations" derivation writes it correctly.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.5.2, eqs. (1.163), (1.167) · the user's 511 notes, §"Diagonalization: Detailed Explanation"*

^cau-c1-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Changing the basis is not changing the state
> Here $U$ relabels: the state ket $|\alpha\rangle$ is untouched, only its components change, as a vector in the plane stays put when the axes are turned. The same operator $U$ can also be applied to state kets, $|\alpha\rangle \to U|\alpha\rangle$, which produces a different physical state (a rotated or a time-evolved one); the predictions are then the same as if every operator were replaced by $U^\dagger XU$ ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]). The similarity transformation (1.169) appears in both readings, which is why it is worth keeping them apart.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.5.1; §2.2.1*

## The trace

> [!definition] Definition §C1.4.1: Trace
> The **trace** of an operator $X$ is the sum of its diagonal matrix elements in an orthonormal basis,
>
> $$
> \operatorname{tr}(X) = \sum_{a'}\langle a'|X|a'\rangle . \qquad (1.170)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.5.2, eq. (1.170) · the user's 511 notes, §"Trace Properties"*

^def-c1-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.2: Properties of the Trace
> On a finite-dimensional ket space:
> 1. $\operatorname{tr}(X)$ does not depend on the orthonormal basis used (1.171);
> 2. $\operatorname{tr}(XY) = \operatorname{tr}(YX)$ and $\operatorname{tr}(U^\dagger XU) = \operatorname{tr}(X)$ for unitary $U$ (1.172a–b);
> 3. $\operatorname{tr}(|a'\rangle\langle a''|) = \delta_{a'a''}$ and, for any kets, $\operatorname{tr}(|\beta\rangle\langle\alpha|) = \langle\alpha|\beta\rangle$ (1.172c–d);
> 4. the trace of an observable is the sum of its eigenvalues, each counted with its degeneracy;
> 5. for $2\times2$ matrices, $\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_i\sigma_j) = 2\delta_{ij}$, so every operator on a two-dimensional space is $X = a_0 + \mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with $a_0 = \frac12\operatorname{tr}X$, $a_k = \frac12\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_kX)$; the coefficients are real exactly when $X$ is Hermitian.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.5.2, eqs. (1.171)–(1.172); Problems 1.4, 1.6(a) · Stony Brook PHY 511, HW 1 solutions, Problems 4–5*

^thm-c1-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Insert the completeness relation of a second basis twice and use associativity to move the numbers around:
>
> $$
> \sum_{a'}\langle a'|X|a'\rangle = \sum_{a', b', b''}\langle a'|b'\rangle\langle b'|X|b''\rangle\langle b''|a'\rangle = \sum_{b', b''}\langle b''|\Bigl(\sum_{a'}|a'\rangle\langle a'|\Bigr)|b'\rangle\langle b'|X|b''\rangle = \sum_{b'}\langle b'|X|b'\rangle .
> $$
>
> *2.* $\operatorname{tr}(XY) = \sum_{a', a''}\langle a'|X|a''\rangle\langle a''|Y|a'\rangle$, symmetric under exchanging the roles of $X$ and $Y$ after renaming the summation indices. Then $\operatorname{tr}(U^\dagger XU) = \operatorname{tr}(XUU^\dagger) = \operatorname{tr}(X)$.
>
> *3.* $\operatorname{tr}(|\beta\rangle\langle\alpha|) = \sum_{a'}\langle a'|\beta\rangle\langle\alpha|a'\rangle = \langle\alpha|\bigl(\sum_{a'}|a'\rangle\langle a'|\bigr)|\beta\rangle = \langle\alpha|\beta\rangle$; with $|\beta\rangle = |a'\rangle$, $\langle\alpha| = \langle a''|$ this is $\delta_{a'a''}$.
>
> *4.* Compute the trace in the eigenbasis, where the matrix is diagonal ([[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]]), and use part 1.
>
> *5.* $\sigma_i\sigma_j = \delta_{ij} + i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k$ and $\operatorname{tr}\sigma_k = 0$ give $\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_i\sigma_j) = 2\delta_{ij}$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]). The four matrices $1, \sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z$ are a basis of the $2\times2$ matrices (same theorem), so $X = a_0 + \mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ for unique complex $a_0, \mathbf a$; taking $\frac12\operatorname{tr}$ of $X$ and of $\sigma_kX$ isolates them. If $X = X^\dagger$, $a_k^* = \frac12\operatorname{tr}\bigl((\sigma_kX)^\dagger\bigr) = \frac12\operatorname{tr}(X\sigma_k) = a_k$ by part 2, and likewise for $a_0$; the converse is immediate.

^der-c1-4-2

*Uses:* [[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence#^def-c1-4-1|Def. §C1.4.1]], [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]], [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]], [[§31 Trace꞉ A Connection Between Matrices and Operators#^ladr-8-49|LADR Thm. 8.49]], [[§31 Trace꞉ A Connection Between Matrices and Operators#^ladr-8-50|LADR Thm. 8.50]]

The decomposition in part 5 is how every two-state Hamiltonian is solved at once, $H = a + \mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with eigenvalues $a \pm |\mathbf b|$ ([[§C3.2 Two-State Systems, Spin Precession and Neutrino Oscillations#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]], from PHY 511 HW 2, Problem 1). The trace of a density operator is the total probability (Chapter C11, planned), and an infinite-dimensional trace needs a trace-class operator; the cyclic property fails for $[x, p] = i\hbar$, whose trace would be $i\hbar\operatorname{tr}1$ ([[§31 Trace꞉ A Connection Between Matrices and Operators#^ladr-8-57|LADR Thm. 8.57]]).

## Diagonalization

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.3: Diagonalization Finds the Transformation Matrix
> Let $B$ be an observable on an $N$-dimensional ket space whose matrix $B_{ij} = \langle a^{(i)}|B|a^{(j)}\rangle$ in the old basis is known (1.176a).
> 1. **Eigenvalues.** The eigenvalues $b^{(l)}$ are the $N$ roots, counted with multiplicity, of the characteristic equation $\det(B - \lambda1) = 0$ (1.177); they are real.
> 2. **Eigenkets.** For each root, the column $C^{(l)}_k = \langle a^{(k)}|b^{(l)}\rangle$ (1.176b) of old components of $|b^{(l)}\rangle$ solves
>
> $$
> \sum_j B_{kj}\,C^{(l)}_j = b^{(l)}\,C^{(l)}_k , \qquad (1.174)-(1.175)
> $$
>
> and is fixed up to a factor by normalization when $b^{(l)}$ is nondegenerate; within a degenerate eigenvalue an orthonormal set of solutions is chosen.
> 3. **The diagonalizing matrix.** The matrix whose columns are the $C^{(l)}$ is the transformation matrix $U$ of [[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence#^thm-c1-4-1|Theorem §C1.4.1]] from $\{|a'\rangle\}$ to $\{|b'\rangle\}$; it is unitary, and
>
> $$
> U^\dagger BU = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(b^{(1)}, \dots, b^{(N)}\bigr), \qquad B = U\operatorname{diag}\bigl(b^{(1)}, \dots, b^{(N)}\bigr)U^\dagger = \sum_l b^{(l)}|b^{(l)}\rangle\langle b^{(l)}| .
> $$
>
> 4. **Hermiticity matters.** An operator can be diagonalized by a unitary matrix if and only if it commutes with its adjoint ($XX^\dagger = X^\dagger X$, a *normal* operator). $S_+ \doteq \hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ (1.178) is not normal, and in fact cannot be diagonalized by any invertible matrix at all.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.5.3, eqs. (1.173)–(1.178) · the user's 511 notes, §"Diagonalization" and §"Diagonalization: Derivation with Two Completeness Relations"*

^thm-c1-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1–2.* Apply $\langle a''|$ to $B|b'\rangle = b'|b'\rangle$ and insert the completeness relation of the old basis between $B$ and $|b'\rangle$: $\sum_{a'}\langle a''|B|a'\rangle\langle a'|b'\rangle = b'\langle a''|b'\rangle$ (1.174), which is (1.175). A homogeneous linear system has a nonzero solution exactly when its determinant vanishes, $\det(B - \lambda1) = 0$, a polynomial equation of degree $N$ with $N$ complex roots counted with multiplicity ([[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-51|LADR Thm. 9.51]]). The roots are real because $B$ is Hermitian ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]).
>
> *3.* Eigenkets of different eigenvalues are orthogonal (same theorem) and those of one eigenvalue are chosen orthonormal, so the $|b^{(l)}\rangle$ form an orthonormal set; by the spectral theorem there are $N$ of them, a basis ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]). Comparing $C^{(l)}_k = \langle a^{(k)}|b^{(l)}\rangle$ with (1.163) identifies the matrix of columns $C^{(l)}$ with Sakurai's transformation matrix, which is unitary by [[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence#^thm-c1-4-1|Theorem §C1.4.1]]. By part 4 of that theorem $U^\dagger BU$ is the matrix of $B$ in the basis $\{|b'\rangle\}$, which is $\langle b^{(k)}|B|b^{(l)}\rangle = b^{(l)}\delta_{kl}$. Multiplying by $U$ and $U^\dagger$ gives the second form, the spectral decomposition of [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]] written in the old basis.
>
> *4.* If $X = UDU^\dagger$ with $D$ diagonal, then $XX^\dagger = UDD^*U^\dagger = UD^*DU^\dagger = X^\dagger X$; the converse is the complex spectral theorem ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]). For $S_+$, $S_+S_- = \hbar^2|+\rangle\langle+| \ne \hbar^2|-\rangle\langle-| = S_-S_+$. Moreover $S_+^2 = 0$ while $S_+ \ne 0$: if $S_+ = VDV^{-1}$ with $D$ diagonal, then $D^2 = V^{-1}S_+^2V = 0$, so $D = 0$ and $S_+ = 0$, a contradiction. Its only eigenvalue is $0$, with the single eigenket $|+\rangle$.

*Uses:* [[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence#^thm-c1-4-1|Theorem §C1.4.1]], [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], [[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-51|LADR Thm. 9.51]], [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]

Non-Hermitian operators do have eigenkets, but in general not an orthonormal basis of them; the coherent states, eigenkets of the oscillator's lowering operator, are the example of Chapter C3 ([[§C3.4 The Harmonic Oscillator and Coherent States Revisited]]).

> [!example] Example §C1.4.1: From Linear to Circular Polarization
> A photon's polarization space has the basis $|x\rangle$, $|y\rangle$; the circular states are $|R\rangle = \frac1{\sqrt2}(|x\rangle + i|y\rangle)$, $|L\rangle = \frac1{\sqrt2}(|x\rangle - i|y\rangle)$ (in the naming of [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space#^cau-c1-1-1|Caution: Which circular polarization is "right"]]). A state is $|\psi\rangle \doteq (a, b)^{\mathsf T}$ in the linear basis. Find the matrix that gives its components in the circular basis, and check that it is unitary.
>
> *Transformation matrix.* Its columns are the old components of the new kets (part 2 of [[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence#^thm-c1-4-1|Theorem §C1.4.1]]):
>
> $$
> U \doteq \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ i & -i\end{pmatrix}, \qquad X \equiv U^\dagger \doteq \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ 1 & i\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix}\langle R|\psi\rangle \\ \langle L|\psi\rangle\end{pmatrix} = X\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b\end{pmatrix} = \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} a - ib \\ a + ib\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> The rows of $X$ are the bras $\langle R| = \frac1{\sqrt2}(\langle x| - i\langle y|)$ and $\langle L|$, with the complex conjugation that the dual correspondence demands.
>
> *Unitarity.* $XX^\dagger = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\1&i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\i&-i\end{pmatrix} = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix} = 1$, and likewise $X^\dagger X = 1$. Consequently $|a|^2 + |b|^2 = \frac12|a - ib|^2 + \frac12|a + ib|^2$: the total probability is the same in both bases.
>
> *What it diagonalizes.* The circular kets are the eigenkets of $\sigma_y \doteq \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}$ in the linear basis, with eigenvalues $\pm1$, and indeed $X\sigma_yX^\dagger = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$ ([[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]] with $U = X^\dagger$). Under the correspondence $|x\rangle \leftrightarrow |+\rangle$, $|y\rangle \leftrightarrow |-\rangle$ of [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space#^rem-c1-1-2|Remark: The polarization analogy]], $|R\rangle$, $|L\rangle$ become $|S_y;\pm\rangle$ (1.110b), and $X$ is the change of basis from the $S_z$ to the $S_y$ representation.
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 3, Problem 1; solved here*

## Unitary equivalent observables

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.4.4: Unitary Equivalent Observables Have Identical Spectra
> Let $U$ be the transformation operator from $\{|a^{(l)}\rangle\}$, the eigenbasis of an observable $A$ with $A|a^{(l)}\rangle = a^{(l)}|a^{(l)}\rangle$, to $\{|b^{(l)}\rangle\}$, the eigenbasis of an observable $B$.
> 1. The **unitary transform** $UAU^{-1} = UAU^\dagger$ is an observable whose eigenkets are the $|b^{(l)}\rangle$ with **the same eigenvalues as $A$**:
>
> $$
> (UAU^{-1})|b^{(l)}\rangle = a^{(l)}|b^{(l)}\rangle . \qquad (1.181)
> $$
>
> $A$ and $UAU^{-1}$ are called **unitary equivalent observables**; they have identical spectra.
> 2. $UAU^{-1}$ and $B$ have the same eigenkets, hence commute and are simultaneously diagonal; they are equal exactly when $b^{(l)} = a^{(l)}$ for every $l$.
> 3. **Converse.** Two observables on the same finite-dimensional ket space with the same eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity, are unitary equivalent: $B = UAU^\dagger$ for the transformation operator that pairs each eigenket of $A$ with an eigenket of $B$ belonging to the same eigenvalue.
>
> Different spin components are the physical case: $S_x$ is a unitary transform of $S_z$, the unitary operator being the rotation through $\pi/2$ about the $y$-axis, so $S_x$ must have the eigenvalues $\pm\hbar/2$ of $S_z$. Any symmetry implemented by a unitary operator relates observables with equal spectra in the same way.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.5.4, eqs. (1.179)–(1.182), Theorem 4 · the user's 511 notes, §"Unitary Equivalent Observables"; part 3 proved here*

^thm-c1-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Insert $U^{-1}U = 1$ in $A|a^{(l)}\rangle = a^{(l)}|a^{(l)}\rangle$ and multiply by $U$ on the left: $UAU^{-1}\,U|a^{(l)}\rangle = a^{(l)}\,U|a^{(l)}\rangle$ (1.180), and $U|a^{(l)}\rangle = |b^{(l)}\rangle$. $U^{-1} = U^\dagger$ by unitarity, and $(UAU^\dagger)^\dagger = UA^\dagger U^\dagger = UAU^\dagger$ ([[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]), so the transform is Hermitian. Its eigenkets $|b^{(l)}\rangle$ form a basis, so it has no other eigenvalues.
>
> *2.* Both $UAU^\dagger = \sum_la^{(l)}|b^{(l)}\rangle\langle b^{(l)}|$ and $B = \sum_lb^{(l)}|b^{(l)}\rangle\langle b^{(l)}|$ are diagonal in the basis $\{|b^{(l)}\rangle\}$ ([[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]]); diagonal matrices commute, and they are equal iff their diagonals agree.
>
> *3.* Order the orthonormal eigenbases of $A$ and $B$ so that $a^{(l)} = b^{(l)}$ for every $l$, which is possible because the eigenvalues agree with multiplicity, and let $U$ be the transformation operator between them. By part 2, $UAU^\dagger = B$.

> [!example] Example §C1.4.2: Turning $S_z$ into $S_x$
> Find a unitary $U$ with $US_zU^\dagger = S_x$, diagonalize $S_x$ with it, and compare with the rotation that Sakurai names.
>
> *The transformation operator.* Take the old basis $|\pm\rangle$ and the new basis $|S_x;\pm\rangle$ of (1.110a). By (1.160) and (1.163),
>
> $$
> U = |S_x;+\rangle\langle+| + |S_x;-\rangle\langle-| \doteq \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{\sigma_x + \sigma_z}{\sqrt2} .
> $$
>
> This $U$ is Hermitian as well as unitary, $U^2 = \frac12(\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2 + \sigma_x\sigma_z + \sigma_z\sigma_x) = 1$, and by the Pauli product rule
>
> $$
> U\sigma_zU^\dagger = \tfrac12(\sigma_x + \sigma_z)\sigma_z(\sigma_x + \sigma_z) = \tfrac12\bigl(\sigma_x\sigma_z\sigma_x + \sigma_x + \sigma_x + \sigma_z\bigr) = \sigma_x ,
> $$
>
> since $\sigma_x\sigma_z\sigma_x = -\sigma_z$. So $S_x = US_zU^\dagger$, and $U^\dagger S_xU = \operatorname{diag}(\frac\hbar2, -\frac\hbar2)$: the same $U$ diagonalizes $S_x$, its columns being the eigenvectors $\frac1{\sqrt2}(1, \pm1)^{\mathsf T}$ found from $\det(S_x - \lambda) = \lambda^2 - \hbar^2/4 = 0$ ([[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence#^thm-c1-4-3|Theorem §C1.4.3]]). $S_x$ and $S_z$ are unitary equivalent, as [[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence#^thm-c1-4-4|Theorem §C1.4.4]] requires of two observables with the same eigenvalues.
>
> *The rotation.* $U$ has determinant $-1$, so it is not a rotation operator of spin $\frac12$ (those have determinant $1$, [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-5|Theorem §C5.2.5]]). The rotation through $\pi/2$ about $\hat{\mathbf y}$,
>
> $$
> \mathscr D_y(\tfrac\pi2) = e^{-i\sigma_y\pi/4} = \cos\tfrac\pi4 - i\sigma_y\sin\tfrac\pi4 \doteq \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}
> $$
>
> ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]]), also satisfies $\mathscr D_y(\frac\pi2)\,\sigma_z\,\mathscr D_y(\frac\pi2)^\dagger = \sigma_x$; it sends $|+\rangle$ to $|S_x;+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ to $-|S_x;-\rangle$, the overall sign of Sakurai's first guess (1.9b). The operator relating two observables is unique only up to the phases of the eigenkets it maps: $U$ times any diagonal unitary in the old basis does the same job. The general statement, that $e^{i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\phi/2}\,(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf a)\,e^{-i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\phi/2} = \boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf a'$ with $\mathbf a'$ the rotated vector, is PHY 511, HW 1, Problem 2 ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations|§C5.2]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.5.4 (after (1.182)) · the user's 511 notes, §"Example: S_x in the S_z Basis" (whose check $U^\dagger S_xU$ writes the prefactor $\frac12$ for $U^\dagger$, where $\frac1{\sqrt2}$ is meant; the result shown is right); solved here*
