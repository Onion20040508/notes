What kind of mathematical object is the state of a silver atom's spin, and how much of that can be read off from experiment? Level A recorded the Stern–Gerlach result as an empirical law ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^law-a5-2-5|Law §A5.2.5]]) and postulated spin to explain it ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^pr-a5-2-6|Principle §A5.2.6]]); level B analysed the magnet ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-5|Theorem §B6.2.5]]) and simply postulated that states are vectors of a Hilbert space ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-1|Principle §B2.3.1]]) on which spin acts through postulated commutation relations ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]). Level C, following Sakurai, runs the logic the other way. From magnets placed in sequence it reads off that the spin states of a silver atom form a two-dimensional vector space, that this space must be complex, and what the eigenkets of $S_x$ and $S_y$ are; the operators and their commutation relations then follow in [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations|§C1.2]]. The only rule borrowed from level B is the Born rule for probabilities ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]]).

*Notation for Chapter C1 (Sakurai's).* Kets $|\alpha\rangle$, bras $\langle\alpha|$; the eigenkets of an observable $A$ are labelled by their eigenvalues with primes, $A|a'\rangle = a'|a'\rangle$, and $a^{(1)}, a^{(2)}, \dots$ when an order is needed. $X$, $Y$ denote general operators and $A$, $B$, $C$ observables; operators carry no hats. $|S_z;\pm\rangle$, abbreviated $|\pm\rangle$, are the eigenkets of $S_z$ with eigenvalues $\pm\hbar/2$, and $|S_x;\pm\rangle$, $|S_y;\pm\rangle$, $|\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n};\pm\rangle$ those of $S_x$, $S_y$, $\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$. $\mathrm{SG}\hat{\mathbf z}$ is a Stern–Gerlach magnet whose field gradient points along $\hat{\mathbf z}$. Level B writes spinors $\chi_\pm$ and hats on operators ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^def-b6-1-1|Def. §B6.1.1]]). $\hbar$ is explicit throughout.

## Magnets in sequence

> [!law] Law §C1.1.1: Sequential Stern–Gerlach Experiments
> Silver atoms from an oven are sent through Stern–Gerlach magnets in sequence; a beam leaving a magnet can be blocked or passed on.
> 1. **One magnet.** An $\mathrm{SG}\hat{\mathbf n}$ magnet splits the oven beam into exactly two beams of equal intensity, $S_{\hat n}+$ and $S_{\hat n}-$, for every direction $\hat{\mathbf n}$ perpendicular to the beam ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^law-a5-2-5|Law §A5.2.5]]). The two values of the spin component are $\pm\hbar/2$.
> 2. **Repetition.** The $S_z+$ beam sent through a second $\mathrm{SG}\hat{\mathbf z}$ emerges as a single $S_z+$ beam: nothing appears in the $S_z-$ channel.
> 3. **Perpendicular axes.** The $S_z+$ beam sent through $\mathrm{SG}\hat{\mathbf x}$ splits into $S_x+$ and $S_x-$ beams of equal intensity, and so does the $S_z-$ beam. The same holds for any two perpendicular axes: an $S_x\pm$ beam sent through $\mathrm{SG}\hat{\mathbf y}$ splits equally into $S_y+$ and $S_y-$.
> 4. **Return of the blocked value.** If the $S_x+$ beam of 3 is sent through a further $\mathrm{SG}\hat{\mathbf z}$, both $S_z+$ and $S_z-$ emerge, with equal intensities, although every $S_z-$ atom was removed by the first magnet. No refinement of the apparatus removes the $S_z-$ beam: selecting $S_x+$ destroys all previous information about $S_z$.
>
> *Valid when: heavy neutral atoms whose angular momentum is that of one unpaired $s$ electron (Ag; also H), beams well separated at each magnet, no fields between the magnets that could turn the spins, and the beam axes perpendicular to the field gradients. Part 1 for the oven beam is Stern and Gerlach's 1922 result; parts 2–4 are the outcomes of the sequential arrangements as Sakurai reports them.*
> *Source: Sakurai §1.1.1–1.1.2, Figs. 1.1–1.3, eq. (1.3); §1.4.2, eqs. (1.101), (1.107) · the user's series, Part IV, §2.1 · Pierce, PHY 511 Lecture 1*

> [!model] Model §C1.1.2: Ideal Stern–Gerlach Filter
> An $\mathrm{SG}\hat{\mathbf n}$ magnet with all beams but one blocked is a device that
> - lets through exactly the atoms that leave it in the selected beam, say $S_{\hat n}+$, and absorbs the rest;
> - leaves every transmitted atom in one and the same spin state, the one with $\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n} = +\hbar/2$, whatever the atom's state was before;
> - acts on spin only: deflection is the only effect on the motion, and the transmitted beam is again a beam of the same kind.
>
> Its deflecting force is that of a magnetic moment $\boldsymbol\mu$ proportional to the spin of the 47th ($5s$) electron, $\boldsymbol\mu = (e/m_ec)\mathbf S$ in Sakurai's Gaussian units with $e < 0$ (so $\boldsymbol\mu = -2\mu_B\mathbf S/\hbar$ to $0.2\%$), in a field whose $z$-component grows along $z$:
>
> $$
> F_z = \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B) \simeq \mu_z\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z} . \qquad (1.2)
> $$
>
> Atoms with $S_z > 0$ ($\mu_z < 0$) are pushed toward weaker field. *Assumptions:* the atom is heavy enough for its centre of mass to follow a classical trajectory as a narrow packet; the other field components average out (their effect precesses away, [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-5|Theorem §B6.2.5]]); the beams separate completely and the blocking is perfect; the nuclear spin is ignored.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.1.1, eqs. (1.1)–(1.2); §1.4.1, Fig. 1.7 · the user's 511 notes, §"Selective Measurement (Filtration)"*

^mod-c1-1-2

The filter is the physical prototype of a *selective measurement*; its mathematical form, a projection operator, is [[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]].

> [!example] Example §C1.1.1: How Far Apart Are the Two Beams?
> Silver atoms leave an oven at $1000\,^\circ\mathrm C$ ($T = 1273\ \mathrm K$) with the mean thermal speed, cross a magnet of length $L = 1\ \mathrm m$ with gradient $\partial B_z/\partial z = 10\ \mathrm{T/m}$, then fly a further $L = 1\ \mathrm m$ to a screen. Take $|\mu_z| = \mu_B$. How far apart are the two spots?
>
> *Kinematics.* The force $\mu_B\,\partial B_z/\partial z$ acts during the transit time $\tau = L/v$ through the magnet, giving a deflection $\tfrac12a\tau^2$ and a transverse velocity $a\tau$, which adds $a\tau\cdot\tau$ over the drift. Each beam is displaced by $\tfrac32a\tau^2$, so the separation is
>
> $$
> d = 3a\tau^2 = \frac{3\mu_B\,(\partial B_z/\partial z)\,L^2}{m v^2} .
> $$
>
> *Numbers.* $m = 107.87\ \mathrm u = 1.791\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$; the mean speed of the Maxwell distribution is $v = \sqrt{8k_BT/\pi m} = 500\ \mathrm{m/s}$; $a = (9.27\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/T})(10\ \mathrm{T/m})/m = 518\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$; $\tau = 2.0\ \mathrm{ms}$. Hence
>
> $$
> d = 3\,(518\ \mathrm{m/s^2})(2.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm s)^2 = 6.2\ \mathrm{mm} .
> $$
>
> Since $d \propto 1/v^2$, the answer depends on which typical speed is meant: the root-mean-square speed $543\ \mathrm{m/s}$ gives $5.3\ \mathrm{mm}$, the most probable speed $443\ \mathrm{m/s}$ gives $7.9\ \mathrm{mm}$. The spread of speeds in a real beam smears each spot along $z$, but the two spots stay millimetres apart, which is why Stern and Gerlach could see them on a glass plate. Classically a continuous band of width up to $d$ would have appeared ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^rem-a5-2-3|Remark: Why two beams were a surprise]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 1.1; solved here*

^ex-c1-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why a spinning top behaves differently
> For a classical top, $\mathbf L = I\boldsymbol\omega$ can be found by watching how fast it turns about which axis, so $L_x$, $L_y$, $L_z$ are known together, and measuring one does not change another. Part 4 of [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space#^law-c1-1-1|Law §C1.1.1]] has no such reading: a model in which half the atoms of the $S_z+$ beam "are" $S_z+$ and $S_x+$ and half $S_z+$ and $S_x-$ is refuted by the reappearance of $S_z-$. The limitation is not the experimenter's incompetence; it is imposed by the experiment. The user's series draws from the same arrangement the structural conclusion that whatever mathematical objects $S_z$ and $S_x$ are, they do not commute, because the order of observations matters; this is made precise in [[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty|§C1.3]].
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.1.2, eq. (1.4) · the user's series, Part IV, §2.1*

## From the beams to a complex vector space

> [!remark] Remark: The polarization analogy
> Sakurai's guide is classical polarized light ([[§A4.5 Polarization]], [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^def-b8-2-1|WO Def. §B8.2.1]]). A polarizer passing $x$-polarized light followed by one passing $y$ transmits nothing; inserting a $45^\circ$ polarizer between them lets light through ([[§A4.5 Polarization#^ex-a4-5-2|WO Example §A4.5.2]]), just as the $\mathrm{SG}\hat{\mathbf x}$ magnet between two $\mathrm{SG}\hat{\mathbf z}$ magnets brings back $S_z-$. The correspondence is
>
> | spin of a silver atom | polarization of light |
> |---|---|
> | $S_z\pm$ | $x$-, $y$-polarized |
> | $S_x\pm$ | polarized along $x'$, $y'$, at $45^\circ$ to $x$ |
> | $S_y\pm$ | right, left circularly polarized |
>
> The field of $x'$-polarized light is $\hat{\mathbf x}' = (\hat{\mathbf x} + \hat{\mathbf y})/\sqrt2$ times the oscillating factor, which suggests $|S_x;\pm\rangle = (|S_z;+\rangle \pm |S_z;-\rangle)/\sqrt2$ (Sakurai's first guess (1.9b) has the opposite overall sign for $|S_x;-\rangle$, which changes nothing physical). Circular light is a superposition of $x$ and $y$ with a relative phase of $90^\circ$, written in complex form with the coefficient $i = e^{i\pi/2}$, $\boldsymbol\varepsilon = (\hat{\mathbf x} + i\hat{\mathbf y})/\sqrt2$ times $e^{i(kz - \omega t)}$; this suggests $|S_y;\pm\rangle = (|S_z;+\rangle \pm i|S_z;-\rangle)/\sqrt2$. The analogy uses the classical field only, not photons, and it is a heuristic: the scale of angles differs (spin states with perpendicular axes correspond to linear polarizations $45^\circ$ apart), and probabilities of single atoms have no classical counterpart. [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space#^thm-c1-1-3|Theorem §C1.1.3]] obtains the same kets from the beams alone.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.1.3, eqs. (1.5)–(1.14), Figs. 1.4–1.5*

^rem-c1-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Which circular polarization is "right"
> Sakurai calls $\hat{\mathbf x} + i\hat{\mathbf y}$ (with $e^{i(kz - \omega t)}$) right circularly polarized, and so does PHY 511 (HW 3, Problem 1: $|R\rangle = (|x\rangle + i|y\rangle)/\sqrt2$). In the optics convention used in Oscillations, Waves and Optics the same field, whose tip turns counterclockwise for an observer facing the source, is **left**-circular, and right-circular light is $\hat{\mathbf x} - i\hat{\mathbf y}$ ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^def-b8-1-2|WO Def. §B8.1.2]], [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^cau-b8-1-2|WO Caution: Handedness names are not universal]]). Sakurai's name is the helicity convention (positive helicity). Nothing in the analogy depends on the name; only the relative phase $\pm i$ matters.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.1.3, eqs. (1.10)–(1.13) · PHY 511, HW 3, Problem 1*

^cau-c1-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.1.3: The Spin States Form a Two-Dimensional Complex Vector Space
> Suppose that the spin states of a silver atom are represented by unit vectors of a vector space with an inner product, mutually exclusive outcomes by orthogonal vectors, and that the probability that a state $|\alpha\rangle$ passes a filter selecting the state $|\beta\rangle$ is $|\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle|^2$ (the Born rule, [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]]). Then [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space#^law-c1-1-1|Law §C1.1.1]] implies:
> 1. **Dimension two.** The orthonormal kets $|+\rangle$, $|-\rangle$ of the two $S_z$ beams span the space of spin states.
> 2. **The $S_x$ and $S_y$ kets.** With the coefficient of $|+\rangle$ chosen real and positive,
>
> $$
> |S_x;\pm\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}|+\rangle \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt2}e^{i\delta_1}|-\rangle, \qquad |S_y;\pm\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}|+\rangle \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt2}e^{i\delta_2}|-\rangle, \qquad (1.102),\ (1.103),\ (1.105)
> $$
>
> with real phases satisfying
>
> $$
> \delta_2 - \delta_1 = \pm\frac\pi2 . \qquad (1.109)
> $$
>
> 3. **Complex numbers are unavoidable.** No real vector space accommodates the $S_x\pm$ and $S_y\pm$ kets: the coefficients of $|S_x;\pm\rangle$ and $|S_y;\pm\rangle$ cannot all be real.
> 4. **Standard choice.** With $\delta_1 = 0$ (which orients the positive $x$-axis) and $\delta_2 = +\pi/2$ (which makes the axes right-handed),
>
> $$
> |S_x;\pm\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(|+\rangle \pm |-\rangle\bigr), \qquad |S_y;\pm\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(|+\rangle \pm i|-\rangle\bigr) . \qquad (1.110)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.1.3, eq. (1.14); §1.2.1 (dimension = number of alternatives); §1.4.2, eqs. (1.101)–(1.110) · the user's 511 notes, §"Spin-1/2 Systems, Once Again" · the user's series, Part IV, §3.5*

^thm-c1-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* By part 1 of the law every atom ends in one of the two $S_z$ beams, so for every state $|\alpha\rangle$ the probabilities add to one: $|\langle+|\alpha\rangle|^2 + |\langle-|\alpha\rangle|^2 = 1 = \langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle$. Write $|\alpha\rangle = \langle+|\alpha\rangle|+\rangle + \langle-|\alpha\rangle|-\rangle + |r\rangle$ with $|r\rangle$ orthogonal to both. Then $\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle = |\langle+|\alpha\rangle|^2 + |\langle-|\alpha\rangle|^2 + \langle r|r\rangle$, so $\langle r|r\rangle = 0$ and $|r\rangle = 0$. The two kets are orthogonal because the two beams are mutually exclusive, which part 2 confirms: an $S_z+$ atom never enters the $S_z-$ channel.
>
> *2.* By part 3 the $S_x+$ state passes an $S_z+$ or an $S_z-$ filter with probability $\tfrac12$ each, so $|\langle\pm|S_x;+\rangle| = 1/\sqrt2$ (1.101). An overall phase of a ket is unobservable, since it cancels in every $|\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle|^2$; choosing it to make the $|+\rangle$ coefficient positive leaves one relative phase $e^{i\delta_1}$. $|S_x;-\rangle$ also has coefficients of modulus $1/\sqrt2$ and must be orthogonal to $|S_x;+\rangle$, since the $S_x\pm$ alternatives are exclusive: $\tfrac12(1 + e^{i(\delta - \delta_1)}) = 0$ forces $e^{i\delta} = -e^{i\delta_1}$. The same argument with $y$ in place of $x$ gives $|S_y;\pm\rangle$. Finally part 3 for the pair $x$, $y$ requires $|\langle S_y;\pm|S_x;+\rangle| = 1/\sqrt2$ (1.107):
>
> $$
> \Bigl|\tfrac12\bigl(1 \pm e^{i(\delta_1 - \delta_2)}\bigr)\Bigr|^2 = \tfrac14\bigl(2 \pm 2\cos(\delta_1 - \delta_2)\bigr) = \tfrac12 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \cos(\delta_2 - \delta_1) = 0 ,
> $$
>
> which is (1.109).
>
> *3.* In a real vector space the relative phases could only be $e^{i\delta} = \pm1$, so $\cos(\delta_2 - \delta_1) = \pm1 \ne 0$. Equivalently: the only real unit vectors with $|\langle\pm|v\rangle|^2 = \tfrac12$ are $(|+\rangle \pm |-\rangle)/\sqrt2$ up to sign, two rays already taken by $S_x\pm$; putting $S_y\pm$ on them would make $|\langle S_y|S_x\rangle|^2$ equal to $0$ or $1$, not $\tfrac12$.
>
> *4.* $\delta_1 \to \delta_1 + \pi$ exchanges the names of $|S_x;+\rangle$ and $|S_x;-\rangle$, that is, reverses the $x$-axis, so $\delta_1 = 0$ is a choice of orientation. Given $x$ and $z$, the two values $\delta_2 = \pm\pi/2$ belong to the two orientations of $y$. Which one is right-handed can only be decided once spin is tied to rotations: the rotated spin-up ket $\mathscr D_z(\pi/2)\mathscr D_y(\pi/2)|+\rangle$, which points along $+\hat{\mathbf y}$ for right-handed rotations, is proportional to $|+\rangle + i|-\rangle$ ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-2|Theorem §C5.2.2]] with $\beta = \alpha = \pi/2$), so $\delta_2 = +\pi/2$.

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from levels A and B
> - **Layer.** Level A had two beams as a law and spin $\tfrac12$ as a principle; level B postulated the commutation relations of spin and derived its $2\times2$ matrices from them ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-3|Theorem §B6.1.3]]). Here the two-dimensional complex state space and the kets (1.110) are derived from the sequential beams and the Born rule alone; the operators $S_x$, $S_y$ then follow by the spectral decomposition, and the commutation relations become a computed property ([[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^ex-c1-2-1|Example §C1.2.1]]). For every angular momentum they are finally a theorem about rotations ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-4|Theorem §C5.1.4]]).
> - **Agreement with level B.** The kets (1.110) are level B's $\chi^{(x)}_\pm$ and $\chi^{(y)}_\pm$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]), with the same phases.
> - **Dimension from alternatives.** Sakurai fixes the dimension of a ket space by the number of alternatives a Stern–Gerlach-type experiment offers: two for silver, nine for the $F = 4$ hyperfine level of $^{133}\mathrm{Cs}$ (his Fig. 1.6), $2j + 1$ in general ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices]]), and a continuum for position ([[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets|§C2.1]]).
> - **Another reason for complex scalars.** The user's series argues from the canonical commutator: a real vector space has no Hermitian $q$, $p$ with $[q, p] = i\hbar$ ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator|§C2.2]]). The Stern–Gerlach argument needs no position or momentum at all: three mutually "unbiased" spin axes already force complex amplitudes.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.2.1, Fig. 1.6; §1.4.2 · the user's series, Part IV, §3.1*

> [!example] Example §C1.1.2: A Tilted Middle Magnet
> $N_0$ unpolarized atoms pass an $\mathrm{SG}\hat{\mathbf z}$ filter keeping $S_z+$, then an $\mathrm{SG}\hat{\mathbf n}$ filter keeping $\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n} = +\hbar/2$, where $\hat{\mathbf n}$ lies in the $xz$-plane at angle $\theta$ from $\hat{\mathbf z}$, and finally an $\mathrm{SG}\hat{\mathbf z}$ filter keeping $S_z-$. How many atoms come through, and for which $\theta$ is the number largest?
>
> *First filter.* Half the unpolarized beam passes (part 1 of the law), in the state $|+\rangle$.
>
> *Second filter.* The state selected is $|\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n};+\rangle = \cos\frac\theta2|+\rangle + \sin\frac\theta2|-\rangle$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]] with azimuth $0$), so the fraction passed is $|\langle\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n};+|+\rangle|^2 = \cos^2\frac\theta2$.
>
> *Third filter.* The fraction of those passing is $|\langle-|\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n};+\rangle|^2 = \sin^2\frac\theta2$. Altogether
>
> $$
> N = \frac{N_0}{2}\cos^2\frac\theta2\,\sin^2\frac\theta2 = \frac{N_0}{8}\sin^2\theta ,
> $$
>
> largest, $N_0/8$, for $\theta = \pi/2$: the $\mathrm{SG}\hat{\mathbf x}$ arrangement of part 4 of the law. For $\theta = 0$ or $\pi$ the middle magnet is a $z$-filter and nothing reaches the end. The same arrangement for light, with polarizer angle $\theta/2$, gives $\tfrac18\sin^2\theta$ of the unpolarized intensity ([[§A4.5 Polarization#^ex-a4-5-2|WO Example §A4.5.2]], $\frac{I_0}{8}\sin^2 2\theta_{\text{pol}}$ with the polarizer angle $\theta_{\text{pol}} = \theta/2$): spin directions $\theta$ apart behave like polarizations $\theta/2$ apart. Why removing the middle filter, or keeping both its beams without recording which one an atom took, gives zero is [[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty#^thm-c1-3-1|Theorem §C1.3.1]].
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 1, Problem 5 (its last question writes $\beta$ for the angle called $\theta$ there) · Sakurai Problem 1.15 (whose question normalizes "the $s_z = -\hbar/2$ beam surviving the first measurement"; the first measurement keeps $s_z = +\hbar/2$); solved here*
