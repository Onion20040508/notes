How is the vector space of [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space|§C1.1]] equipped to do physics: what are bras, how do operators act, and how is an observable built from its eigenkets? Level B set up Dirac notation on the function space $L^2$: bras as integrals against a function, outer products, the completeness relation and matrices of operators ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-4|Def. §B2.1.4]], [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-5|Def. §B2.1.5]], [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]), and the adjoint and Hermitian operators ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]); Functional Analysis makes bras rigorous through the Riesz map ([[§34 Bras, Kets, and the Riesz Map#^thm-34-2|556 Thm. §34.2]]) and the completeness relation through strong convergence ([[§35 The Completeness Relation#^thm-35-2|556 Thm. §35.2]]). Level C states Dirac's rules as axioms for any ket space, whatever its dimension and whether or not it consists of functions, with the *associative axiom* as the rule that makes the notation calculate by itself. New here are the rules of the bra–ket algebra obtained from the dual correspondence, the spectral decomposition of an observable with functions of it and its projectors as polynomials, and the spin-$\tfrac12$ operators built from the kets of [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space#^thm-c1-1-3|Theorem §C1.1.3]], with their commutation relations as a result.

*Notation.* As in [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space|§C1.1]]. In addition, $\doteq$ reads "is represented by" (a ket by a column, an operator by a matrix, in a stated basis), and $\Lambda_{a'} \equiv |a'\rangle\langle a'|$ is Sakurai's projector onto the eigenket $|a'\rangle$; for a degenerate eigenvalue $P_{a'}$ denotes the projector onto the whole eigenspace.

## Dirac's axioms

> [!principle] Principle §C1.2.1: Kets, Bras and Operators (Dirac's Axioms)
> 1. **Ket space.** The states of a physical system are represented by nonzero vectors $|\alpha\rangle$, *kets*, of a complex vector space; $|\alpha\rangle$ and $c|\alpha\rangle$ ($c \ne 0$) represent the same state, so states are rays. Scalars may stand on either side, $c|\alpha\rangle = |\alpha\rangle c$; $0\cdot|\alpha\rangle$ is the null ket. The dimension is fixed by the physics: the number of mutually exclusive alternatives of a Stern–Gerlach-type experiment, two for a silver atom's spin. The ket of a state contains complete information about it.
> 2. **Dual correspondence.** To every ket $|\alpha\rangle$ corresponds a *bra* $\langle\alpha|$ in a dual (bra) space, one to one, with
>
> $$
> c_\alpha|\alpha\rangle + c_\beta|\beta\rangle \ \overset{\mathrm{DC}}{\longleftrightarrow}\ c_\alpha^*\langle\alpha| + c_\beta^*\langle\beta| . \qquad (1.24)
> $$
>
> 3. **Inner product.** A bra and a ket form the number $\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle = (\langle\beta|)\cdot(|\alpha\rangle)$, with
>
> $$
> \langle\beta|\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|\beta\rangle^*, \qquad \langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle \ge 0, \ \text{with equality only for the null ket.} \qquad (1.26),\ (1.27)
> $$
>
> 4. **Operators.** An operator $X$ acts on kets from the left, $X|\alpha\rangle$, and on bras from the right, $\langle\alpha|X$; operators are linear (the time-reversal operator is the single exception). Two operators are equal if they agree on every ket. The **adjoint** $X^\dagger$ is defined by
>
> $$
> X|\alpha\rangle \ \overset{\mathrm{DC}}{\longleftrightarrow}\ \langle\alpha|X^\dagger , \qquad (1.39)
> $$
>
> and $X$ is **Hermitian** if $X = X^\dagger$. Observables are represented by Hermitian operators, and the eigenkets of an observable span the ket space (level B's [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-2|Principle §B2.3.2]] and [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]], stated here for any ket space).
> 5. **Associative axiom.** Every legal product of kets, bras and operators is associative. Legal are $\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle$, $X|\alpha\rangle$, $\langle\alpha|X$, $XY$ and the **outer product** $|\beta\rangle\langle\alpha|$; illegal are $|\alpha\rangle X$, $X\langle\alpha|$, and $|\alpha\rangle|\beta\rangle$ or $\langle\alpha|\langle\beta|$ for two vectors of the same space (for different spaces, see [[§C1.5 Composite Systems and Tensor Products|§C1.5]]).
>
> *Domain: every quantum system, finite- or infinite-dimensional. For continuous spectra the "eigenkets" $|x'\rangle$ are not normalizable and their bras are not elements of the dual of a Hilbert space; they are handled in [[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets|§C2.1]] and rigorously in [[§36 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions|556 §36]]. Antilinear operators (time reversal) are batch-2 material ([[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^def-c8-3-1|Def. §C8.3.1]], [[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^thm-c8-3-3|Theorem §C8.3.3]]).*
> *Source: Sakurai §1.2.1–1.2.5, eqs. (1.15)–(1.52); §1.3.1 · the user's 511 notes, §"Kets, Bras, and Operators" (the ket space is a Hilbert space whose dimension depends on the system, two for spin $\tfrac12$) · the user's series, Part IV, §3.1–3.2*

> [!remark] Remark: What the axioms add to level B, and what they are rigorously
> - **Layer.** Parts 3 and 4 restate for an abstract ket space what level B already has: the properties of the inner product ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]], proved there for integrals), and observables as Hermitian operators with complete eigenkets (Principles §B2.3.2 and §B2.2.5). The principle adds three things. (i) The ket space itself is the postulated object, of any dimension and not necessarily a space of functions. Level B's state postulate takes "a Hilbert space" but works with $L^2$ of one particle ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-1|Principle §B2.3.1]]) and builds its bras from integrals ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-4|Def. §B2.1.4]]). Here the two-dimensional spin space of [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space|§C1.1]] is the first example, and $L^2$ becomes one realization ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space|§C2.3]]). (ii) The dimension is fixed by the number of experimental alternatives. (iii) The dual correspondence and the associative axiom are postulated as the rules of calculation, where level B introduced bras, outer products and adjoints as definitions ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-5|Def. §B2.1.5]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]]).
> - **The dual correspondence is the Riesz map.** In a Hilbert space every ket defines the bounded functional $|\gamma\rangle \mapsto \langle\alpha|\gamma\rangle$, and every bounded functional arises from exactly one ket; the map $|\alpha\rangle \mapsto \langle\alpha|$ is conjugate-linear and isometric ([[§34 Bras, Kets, and the Riesz Map#^thm-34-2|556 Thm. §34.2]], [[§34 Bras, Kets, and the Riesz Map#^rem-34-3|556 Remark, §34]]). So Sakurai's postulate (1.24) is a theorem once the inner product is given; in finite dimensions it is "conjugate transpose the column" ([[§21 Orthonormal Bases#^ladr-6-42|LADR Thm. 6.42]]). The conventions differ: mathematics puts the conjugate on the second slot ([[§34 Bras, Kets, and the Riesz Map#^rem-34-1|556 Remark: Conventions]], [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^cau-b2-1-1|Caution: Which slot carries the complex conjugate]]).
> - **The associative axiom is a set of definitions.** In a Hilbert space, $|\beta\rangle\langle\alpha|$ is *defined* as the operator $|\gamma\rangle \mapsto \langle\alpha|\gamma\rangle\,|\beta\rangle$ ([[§34 Bras, Kets, and the Riesz Map#^def-34-4|556 Def. §34.4]]), and $\langle\beta|X$ as the functional $|\gamma\rangle \mapsto \langle\beta|(X|\gamma\rangle)$; with these definitions every bracketing of a legal product gives the same result. Dirac's axiom is the statement that the notation may be used without tracking which reading is meant.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.2.2, §1.2.5, footnote 7 · the user's series, Part IV, §3.1*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.2.2: Rules of the Bra–Ket Algebra
> For operators $X$, $Y$, kets $|\alpha\rangle$, $|\beta\rangle$ and a number $c$:
> 1. $(XY)^\dagger = Y^\dagger X^\dagger$, $(cX)^\dagger = c^*X^\dagger$, $(X^\dagger)^\dagger = X$; (1.44)
> 2. the adjoint of an outer product exchanges its factors: $X = |\beta\rangle\langle\alpha| \Rightarrow X^\dagger = |\alpha\rangle\langle\beta|$; (1.49)–(1.50)
> 3. a matrix element $\langle\beta|X|\alpha\rangle \equiv (\langle\beta|)\cdot(X|\alpha\rangle) = (\langle\beta|X)\cdot(|\alpha\rangle)$ satisfies
>
> $$
> \langle\beta|X|\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|X^\dagger|\beta\rangle^*, \qquad\text{and for Hermitian } X:\quad \langle\beta|X|\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|X|\beta\rangle^* ; \qquad (1.53),\ (1.54)
> $$
>
> 4. in particular the expectation value $\langle\alpha|X|\alpha\rangle$ of a Hermitian operator is real, and that of an anti-Hermitian one ($X^\dagger = -X$) is purely imaginary.
>
> Level B obtained the same rules by moving operators across inner products ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]); here they follow from the dual correspondence and the associative axiom alone.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.2.4–1.2.5, eqs. (1.43)–(1.54); §1.4.5, Lemmas 2–3 · the user's 511 notes, §"The Associative Axiom" · Stony Brook PHY 511, HW 1 solutions, Problem 6*

^thm-c1-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* By the associative axiom $XY|\alpha\rangle = X(Y|\alpha\rangle)$. Apply (1.39) twice: $Y|\alpha\rangle \leftrightarrow \langle\alpha|Y^\dagger$, and then $X(Y|\alpha\rangle) \leftrightarrow (\langle\alpha|Y^\dagger)X^\dagger = \langle\alpha|Y^\dagger X^\dagger$ (1.45). Since also $XY|\alpha\rangle \leftrightarrow \langle\alpha|(XY)^\dagger$ and the correspondence is one to one, $(XY)^\dagger = Y^\dagger X^\dagger$. By (1.24), $cX|\alpha\rangle \leftrightarrow c^{\ast}\langle\alpha|X^\dagger$, so $(cX)^\dagger = c^{\ast}X^\dagger$. That $X^{\dagger\dagger} = X$ follows from part 3 applied twice.
>
> *2.* $X|\gamma\rangle = |\beta\rangle\langle\alpha|\gamma\rangle$ is the ket $|\beta\rangle$ times the number $\langle\alpha|\gamma\rangle$, whose bra is $\langle\alpha|\gamma\rangle^{\ast}\langle\beta| = \langle\gamma|\alpha\rangle\langle\beta| = \langle\gamma|\bigl(|\alpha\rangle\langle\beta|\bigr)$ by (1.24), (1.26) and associativity. This is $\langle\gamma|X^\dagger$ for every $|\gamma\rangle$, so $X^\dagger = |\alpha\rangle\langle\beta|$.
>
> *3.* Associativity makes the two readings of $\langle\beta|X|\alpha\rangle$ agree (1.51). Put $|\gamma\rangle = X|\alpha\rangle$, whose bra is $\langle\gamma| = \langle\alpha|X^\dagger$. By (1.26), $\langle\beta|\gamma\rangle = \langle\gamma|\beta\rangle^{\ast} = \bigl((\langle\alpha|X^\dagger)\cdot|\beta\rangle\bigr)^{\ast} = \langle\alpha|X^\dagger|\beta\rangle^{\ast}$.
>
> *4.* With $|\beta\rangle = |\alpha\rangle$, part 3 gives $\langle\alpha|X|\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|X^\dagger|\alpha\rangle^{\ast} = \pm\langle\alpha|X|\alpha\rangle^{\ast}$ for $X^\dagger = \pm X$.

## Eigenkets as base kets

Sakurai's Theorem 1, that a Hermitian operator has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenkets for distinct eigenvalues, is proved exactly as at level B: sandwich $A|a'\rangle = a'|a'\rangle$ with $\langle a''|$ and its dual $\langle a''|A = a''^*\langle a''|$ with $|a'\rangle$, and subtract, $(a' - a''^*)\langle a''|a'\rangle = 0$ ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]). The normalized eigenkets are then an orthonormal set, $\langle a''|a'\rangle = \delta_{a''a'}$, and they span the space by part 4 of [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^pr-c1-2-1|Principle §C1.2.1]] (in finite dimensions this is the spectral theorem, [[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]; in general level B's completeness axiom, [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]]). Expanding $|\alpha\rangle = \sum_{a'}|a'\rangle\langle a'|\alpha\rangle$ for every ket gives the completeness relation $\sum_{a'}\Lambda_{a'} = 1$ (1.65), (1.70), and inserting it twice writes any operator as a matrix, $X = \sum_{a'', a'}|a''\rangle\langle a''|X|a'\rangle\langle a'|$ (1.71), with row index $a''$, column index $a'$, kets as columns of $\langle a'|\alpha\rangle$ and bras as rows of $\langle\alpha|a'\rangle = \langle a'|\alpha\rangle^*$ (1.80)–(1.85). All of this is level B's [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]] and [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]], derived the same way; by (1.53) the matrix of $X^\dagger$ is the conjugate transpose of that of $X$ (1.74). The $j$-th column of the matrix of $X$ lists the components of $X|a^{(j)}\rangle$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C1.2.3: Spectral Decomposition and Functions of an Observable
> Let $A$ be an observable on an $N$-dimensional ket space with nondegenerate eigenvalues $a'$ and orthonormal eigenkets $|a'\rangle$.
> 1. **Spectral decomposition.** $A$ is diagonal in its own eigenbasis and
>
> $$
> A = \sum_{a'} a'\,|a'\rangle\langle a'| = \sum_{a'} a'\,\Lambda_{a'}, \qquad \Lambda_{a'}\Lambda_{a''} = \delta_{a'a''}\Lambda_{a'}, \qquad \Lambda_{a'}^\dagger = \Lambda_{a'} . \qquad (1.88)
> $$
>
> Conversely, given an orthonormal basis $\{|a'\rangle\}$ and real numbers $a'$, the operator $\sum a'|a'\rangle\langle a'|$ is Hermitian with exactly these eigenkets and eigenvalues.
> 2. **Functions.** For every function $f$ defined by a power series that converges at every eigenvalue $a'$,
>
> $$
> f(A) = \sum_{a'} f(a')\,|a'\rangle\langle a'| ; \qquad\text{e.g.}\quad \exp[if(A)] = \sum_{a'} e^{if(a')}\,|a'\rangle\langle a'| .
> $$
>
> 3. **Minimal polynomial.** $\prod_{a'}(A - a') = 0$, the null operator.
> 4. **Projectors as polynomials.**
>
> $$
> \Lambda_{a'} = \prod_{a'' \ne a'}\frac{A - a''}{a' - a''} .
> $$
>
> With degenerate eigenvalues the same statements hold with $\Lambda_{a'}$ replaced by the projector $P_{a'} = \sum_i|a';i\rangle\langle a';i|$ onto the eigenspace of $a'$ and the sums and products taken over the distinct eigenvalues.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.3.3, eqs. (1.86)–(1.88); Problems 1.6(c), 1.9 · the user's 511 notes, §"Matrix Representations" · the user's series, Part IV, §3.3*

^thm-c1-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* In (1.71) with $X = A$, $\langle a''|A|a'\rangle = a'\langle a''|a'\rangle = a'\delta_{a''a'}$ (1.87), so only diagonal terms survive. $\Lambda_{a'}\Lambda_{a''} = |a'\rangle\langle a'|a''\rangle\langle a''| = \delta_{a'a''}\Lambda_{a'}$ by associativity and orthonormality, and $\Lambda_{a'}^\dagger = \Lambda_{a'}$ by part 2 of [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]. Conversely, $\bigl(\sum a'|a'\rangle\langle a'|\bigr)^\dagger = \sum a'^{\ast}|a'\rangle\langle a'|$ is the same operator for real $a'$, and it sends $|a''\rangle$ to $a''|a''\rangle$.
>
> *2.* From the projector algebra, $A^n = \sum_{a'}a'^n\Lambda_{a'}$ by induction ($A^{n+1} = \sum_{a', a''}a'^na''\Lambda_{a'}\Lambda_{a''} = \sum a'^{n+1}\Lambda_{a'}$), and $1 = \sum\Lambda_{a'}$ covers $n = 0$. A power series is summed term by term; in finite dimensions each of the $N$ coefficient series $\sum_nc_na'^n$ converges to $f(a')$. This agrees with the power-series definition of [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-6|Def. §B2.1.6]].
>
> *3–4.* All the factors are polynomials in $A$ and commute, so it suffices to apply each side to a basis ket $|a'''\rangle$, on which $A$ acts as the number $a'''$. In 3 the factor with $a' = a'''$ gives zero. In 4 the right side gives $\prod_{a''\ne a'}\frac{a''' - a''}{a' - a''}\,|a'''\rangle$: zero if $a''' \ne a'$ (one factor vanishes) and $|a'\rangle$ if $a''' = a'$, which is exactly $\Lambda_{a'}|a'''\rangle$. Two operators that agree on a basis are equal. (Part 4 is Lagrange interpolation: the polynomial taking the value $1$ at $a'$ and $0$ at the other eigenvalues.) With degeneracy the same argument runs over a basis of each eigenspace.

## Spin one-half

> [!example] Example §C1.2.1: The Spin Operators from Their Eigenkets
> Build $S_z$, $S_x$, $S_y$ on the spin space of [[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space#^thm-c1-1-3|Theorem §C1.1.3]] and compute their commutators and anticommutators.
>
> *$S_z$ and the ladder operators.* By (1.88), $S_z = \frac\hbar2\bigl(|+\rangle\langle+| - |-\rangle\langle-|\bigr)$ (1.90). Two further outer products, $S_+ \equiv \hbar|+\rangle\langle-|$ and $S_- \equiv \hbar|-\rangle\langle+|$ (1.92), are not Hermitian ($S_+^\dagger = S_-$ by part 2 of [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]): $S_+$ turns $|-\rangle$ into $\hbar|+\rangle$ and $|+\rangle$ into the null ket, raising the spin component by $\hbar$. With the basis ordered by decreasing $S_z$,
>
> $$
> |+\rangle \doteq \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \quad |-\rangle \doteq \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad S_z \doteq \frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}, \quad S_+ \doteq \hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}, \quad S_- \doteq \hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix} . \qquad (1.93)
> $$
>
> *$S_x$ and $S_y$.* The spectral decomposition with the kets (1.102)–(1.105) gives, for any phases,
>
> $$
> S_x = \tfrac\hbar2\bigl(|S_x;+\rangle\langle S_x;+| - |S_x;-\rangle\langle S_x;-|\bigr) = \tfrac\hbar2\bigl(e^{-i\delta_1}|+\rangle\langle-| + e^{i\delta_1}|-\rangle\langle+|\bigr) , \qquad (1.104)
> $$
>
> since the diagonal terms $\tfrac12|\pm\rangle\langle\pm|$ cancel between the two projectors and the cross terms add. It is Hermitian, as it must be. With $\delta_1 = 0$, $\delta_2 = \pi/2$:
>
> $$
> S_x = \tfrac\hbar2\bigl(|+\rangle\langle-| + |-\rangle\langle+|\bigr), \qquad S_y = \tfrac\hbar2\bigl(-i|+\rangle\langle-| + i|-\rangle\langle+|\bigr), \qquad S_\pm = S_x \pm iS_y , \qquad (1.111)-(1.112)
> $$
>
> whose matrices are $\frac\hbar2$ times the Pauli matrices ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^def-b6-1-2|Def. §B6.1.2]]). The column rule checks this: $S_x|+\rangle = \frac\hbar2|-\rangle$, so the first column of $S_x$ is $(0, \frac\hbar2)^{\mathsf T}$.
>
> *Products.* Only the associative axiom and $\langle\pm|\mp\rangle = 0$, $\langle\pm|\pm\rangle = 1$ are needed:
>
> $$
> S_xS_y = \frac{\hbar^2}{4}\bigl(|+\rangle\langle-| + |-\rangle\langle+|\bigr)\bigl(-i|+\rangle\langle-| + i|-\rangle\langle+|\bigr) = \frac{\hbar^2}{4}\bigl(i|+\rangle\langle+| - i|-\rangle\langle-|\bigr) = \frac{i\hbar}{2}S_z ,
> $$
>
> and in the same way $S_yS_x = -\frac{i\hbar}2S_z$ and $S_x^2 = S_y^2 = S_z^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}4(|+\rangle\langle+| + |-\rangle\langle-|) = \frac{\hbar^2}{4}$. Hence, with the cyclic products done alike,
>
> $$
> [S_i, S_j] = i\epsilon_{ijk}\hbar S_k, \qquad \{S_i, S_j\} = \tfrac12\hbar^2\delta_{ij}, \qquad \mathbf S^2 = S_x^2 + S_y^2 + S_z^2 = \tfrac34\hbar^2, \qquad [\mathbf S^2, S_i] = 0 . \qquad (1.113)-(1.118)
> $$
>
> The anticommutators are special to spin $\tfrac12$: for higher spin $\mathbf S^2$ is still $s(s+1)\hbar^2$ times the identity on the spin-$s$ space but $S_i^2$ is not a multiple of it ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-7|Theorem §B6.1.7]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.3.4, eqs. (1.89)–(1.93); §1.4.2, eqs. (1.104)–(1.118); Problem 1.10 · PHY 511, HW 1, Problem 4 · the user's 511 notes, §"Spin-1/2 Systems" and §"Why This Matrix Form? A Concrete Derivation" (whose worked example reads off the first column $(0, \frac\hbar2)^{\mathsf T}$ from $S_x|+\rangle = \frac\hbar2|-\rangle$, as above) · Stony Brook PHY 511, HW 1 solutions, Problem 3 (Pauli products, as a check)*

> [!example] Example §C1.2.2: Projectors and an Exponential of the Spin
> Apply [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]] to $A = S_z$, with eigenvalues $\pm\hbar/2$.
>
> *Minimal polynomial.* $(S_z - \tfrac\hbar2)(S_z + \tfrac\hbar2) = S_z^2 - \tfrac{\hbar^2}4 = 0$, by $S_z^2 = \hbar^2/4$ (Example §C1.2.1).
>
> *Projectors.* Part 4 gives
>
> $$
> \Lambda_\pm = \frac{S_z \pm \hbar/2}{\pm\hbar} = \frac12 \pm \frac{S_z}{\hbar} \doteq \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}, \ \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} ,
> $$
>
> which are $|+\rangle\langle+|$ and $|-\rangle\langle-|$, as they must be. The same formula for $\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$, whose eigenvalues are also $\pm\hbar/2$ and whose square is also $\hbar^2/4$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]), gives the projector onto "spin up along $\hat{\mathbf n}$" without solving for the eigenket:
>
> $$
> |\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n};+\rangle\langle\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n};+| = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + \boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Its expectation value in a state with $\langle\boldsymbol\sigma\rangle = \hat{\mathbf m}$, spin up along $\hat{\mathbf m}$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]]), is the probability $\tfrac12(1 + \hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\hat{\mathbf m}) = \cos^2(\vartheta/2)$, where $\vartheta$ is the angle between the two axes.
>
> *Exponential.* Part 2 gives
>
> $$
> e^{-iS_z\phi/\hbar} = e^{-i\phi/2}\Lambda_+ + e^{i\phi/2}\Lambda_- = \cos\frac\phi2 - \frac{2i}{\hbar}S_z\sin\frac\phi2 \doteq \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\phi/2} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\phi/2}\end{pmatrix} ,
> $$
>
> using $\Lambda_+ + \Lambda_- = 1$ and $\Lambda_+ - \Lambda_- = 2S_z/\hbar$. This is the operator that rotates a spin $\tfrac12$ about $\hat{\mathbf z}$ through $\phi$ ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]]); at $\phi = 2\pi$ it equals $-1$ ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-3|Theorem §C5.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problems 1.6(c), 1.9(c) · the user's 511 notes, §"Useful Identities", eq. (S.38); solved here*

> [!caution] Caution: Notation at levels B and C
> - Sakurai drops the hats of level B and reserves $A$, $B$ for observables, $X$, $Y$ for general operators. Eigenkets carry the eigenvalue with primes, $|a'\rangle$, $|a''\rangle$; the primes distinguish eigenvalues, they are not derivatives.
> - $\doteq$ marks a representation in a stated basis. The ket $|+\rangle$ is not the column $(1, 0)^{\mathsf T}$; in the $S_x$ basis the same ket is $\frac1{\sqrt2}(1, 1)^{\mathsf T}$ (level B: [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^cau-b6-1-1|Caution: Vectors and their components]]).
> - Products that look like algebra may be illegal: $\langle\psi\rangle$, $\langle A|$, $\langle\psi\rangle A$, $\psi|A\rangle$, $|\psi\rangle^2$ and $1 + |\psi\rangle$ are meaningless, while $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle^2$, $|\psi\rangle\langle\phi|$ and $A^2$ are fine (Stony Brook HW 1, Problem 2).
>
