Why is the phase of the free and oscillator propagators the classical action ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^rem-c4-1-2|§C4.1, Remark: The phase is the classical action]])? Classical mechanics knows the action from Hamilton's principle ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^pr-b6-2-1|CM Principle §B6.2.1]]) and, as a function of its end points, as Hamilton's principal function, which solves the Hamilton–Jacobi equation ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-4|CM Theorem §B8.3.4]]); level B met it in the phase of the WKB wave function ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^rem-b9-3-1|QM Remark: The expansion in powers of ħ, and the classical action]]). Level C answers the question with a new object: iterating the composition law of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-8|Theorem §C4.1.8]] over infinitely many short time steps writes the propagator as a sum over all paths, each weighted by $e^{iS/\hbar}$. Conversely, Feynman's sum over paths, taken as a starting point, reproduces the Schrödinger equation. In the limit $\hbar \to 0$ the sum is dominated by the paths of stationary action, so Hamilton's principle, an axiom of Classical Mechanics, becomes a consequence of interference.

*Notation (Sakurai):* one dimension, a particle of mass $m$ with $L_{\text{classical}}(x, \dot x) = \frac12m\dot x^2 - V(x)$ and $H = p^2/2m + V(x)$; the time interval $[t_1, t_N]$ is cut into $N - 1$ equal steps $\Delta t = (t_N - t_1)/(N - 1)$ at the times $t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_N$, with positions $x_n$ at $t_n$. Transition amplitudes $\langle x_n, t_n|x_{n-1}, t_{n-1}\rangle$ are the propagators of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-8|Theorem §C4.1.8]].

## From the composition law to the sum over paths

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.1: The Short-Time Propagator
> As $\Delta t = t_n - t_{n-1} \to 0$,
>
> $$
> \langle x_n, t_n|x_{n-1}, t_{n-1}\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}}\;\exp\Bigl[\frac{iS(n, n-1)}{\hbar}\Bigr], \qquad S(n, n-1) = \Delta t\Bigl\{\frac m2\Bigl[\frac{x_n - x_{n-1}}{\Delta t}\Bigr]^2 - V\Bigl(\frac{x_n + x_{n-1}}{2}\Bigr)\Bigr\} ,
> $$
>
> up to corrections that vanish faster than $\Delta t$ when the kernel acts on a wave function. $S(n, n - 1)$ is the action of the straight-line path from $(x_{n-1}, t_{n-1})$ to $(x_n, t_n)$, to first order in $\Delta t$; the potential may equally be evaluated at $x_n$ or $x_{n-1}$ (the series' choice) instead of the midpoint.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.4, eqs. (2.305), (2.310) · the user's series, Part IV, §10.3 · the user's 511 notes, §"Infinitesimal Time Interval", §"The Path Integral"*

^thm-c4-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Splitting the exponential.* With $T = p^2/2m$, $e^{-iH\Delta t/\hbar} = e^{-iV\Delta t/\hbar}\,e^{-iT\Delta t/\hbar}\,\bigl(1 + O(\Delta t^2)\bigr)$: expanding both sides in powers of $\Delta t$, they agree to first order and differ at second order by a term proportional to $(\Delta t/\hbar)^2[V, T]$. Hence
>
> $$
> \langle x_n|e^{-iH\Delta t/\hbar}|x_{n-1}\rangle \approx e^{-iV(x_n)\Delta t/\hbar}\,\langle x_n|e^{-iT\Delta t/\hbar}|x_{n-1}\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}}\,\exp\Bigl[\frac{i}{\hbar}\Bigl(\frac{m(x_n - x_{n-1})^2}{2\Delta t} - V(x_n)\Delta t\Bigr)\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> by the free propagator ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]]) and because $V(x)$ is diagonal in the position basis.
>
> *Which point for $V$.* The free kernel oscillates rapidly once $|x_n - x_{n-1}| \gg \sqrt{\hbar\Delta t/m}$, so integrated against a smooth function it only sees $|x_n - x_{n-1}| \lesssim \sqrt{\hbar\Delta t/m}$ (the Fresnel-zone size of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]). Moving the argument of $V$ by a fraction of $x_n - x_{n-1}$ changes the exponent by $O(V'\sqrt{\hbar\Delta t/m}\,\Delta t/\hbar) = O(\Delta t^{3/2})$, which over the $N \sim 1/\Delta t$ steps of a finite time adds up to $O(\Delta t^{1/2}) \to 0$. The same count disposes of the $O(\Delta t^2)$ splitting error. That the product of the split factors converges to $e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ is the Trotter product formula, $e^{-i(T + V)t/\hbar} = \lim_{N\to\infty}\bigl(e^{-iVt/N\hbar}e^{-iTt/N\hbar}\bigr)^N$, quoted in the series without proof; this note takes it as given.
>
> *Straight line.* Along $x(t) = x_{n-1} + (x_n - x_{n-1})(t - t_{n-1})/\Delta t$ the kinetic term of $\int L\,dt$ is exactly $\frac m2(x_n - x_{n-1})^2/\Delta t$, and $\int V\,dt = V(\bar x)\,\Delta t$ for some $\bar x$ between the ends, which is the midpoint value up to the same negligible correction.

> [!definition] Definition §C4.2.1: The Path Integral
> The **path integral** from $(x_1, t_1)$ to $(x_N, t_N)$ is the limit of the time-sliced integrals,
>
> $$
> \int_{x_1}^{x_N}\mathscr D[x(t)]\;\exp\Bigl[\frac i\hbar\int_{t_1}^{t_N}dt\,L_{\text{classical}}(x, \dot x)\Bigr] \equiv \lim_{N\to\infty}\Bigl(\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}\Bigr)^{(N-1)/2}\int dx_{N-1}\int dx_{N-2}\cdots\int dx_2\;\prod_{n=2}^{N}\exp\Bigl[\frac{iS(n, n-1)}{\hbar}\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> taken with $x_1$, $x_N$, $t_1$, $t_N$ fixed. Each choice of the intermediate positions $x_2, \dots, x_{N-1}$ is a broken-line path, and $\sum_nS(n, n - 1)$ is its action, a Riemann sum for $\int L\,dt$. The integration operator $\int\mathscr D[x(t)]$, with its normalization factor, is the **path measure**.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.4, eqs. (2.311)–(2.313) · the user's series, Part IV, §10.3, eq. (path-integral)*

^def-c4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.2: The Propagator as a Path Integral
> For $H = p^2/2m + V(x)$,
>
> $$
> \langle x_N, t_N|x_1, t_1\rangle = \int_{x_1}^{x_N}\mathscr D[x(t)]\;\exp\Bigl[\frac i\hbar\int_{t_1}^{t_N}dt\,L_{\text{classical}}(x, \dot x)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Every path from $(x_1, t_1)$ to $(x_N, t_N)$ contributes, with the same modulus and the phase $S[x]/\hbar$; the amplitude is the sum over all of them.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.3–2.6.4, eqs. (2.295), (2.311)–(2.313) · the user's series, Part IV, §10.3*

^thm-c4-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply the composition law ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-8|Theorem §C4.1.8]]) at each of the $N - 2$ intermediate times:
>
> $$
> \langle x_N, t_N|x_1, t_1\rangle = \int dx_{N-1}\cdots\int dx_2\;\langle x_N, t_N|x_{N-1}, t_{N-1}\rangle\langle x_{N-1}, t_{N-1}|x_{N-2}, t_{N-2}\rangle\cdots\langle x_2, t_2|x_1, t_1\rangle .
> $$
>
> This is exact for every $N$. Replace each of the $N - 1$ factors by its short-time form ([[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-1|Theorem §C4.2.1]]): the prefactors give $(m/2\pi i\hbar\Delta t)^{(N-1)/2}$ and the exponentials multiply into $\exp[(i/\hbar)\sum_nS(n, n - 1)]$. The error made in the replacement vanishes as $N \to \infty$, by the Trotter formula quoted there, which is [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^def-c4-2-1|Def. §C4.2.1]].

> [!caution] Caution: What the formula does and does not say
> - **A limit, not a measure.** $\int\mathscr D[x(t)]$ means the limit of [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^def-c4-2-1|Def. §C4.2.1]]. Each finite-$N$ integral is an iterated Fresnel integral, convergent only conditionally ($\Delta t \to \Delta t - i0$, as in [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]); the normalization $(m/2\pi i\hbar\Delta t)^{(N-1)/2}$ diverges as $N \to \infty$ and only the combination has a limit. The series marks the existence of a continuum path measure as "a genuine mathematical subtlety" and uses the sliced definition as the operational meaning; so does this note.
> - **Rough paths.** The paths that matter move by $|x_n - x_{n-1}| \sim \sqrt{\hbar\Delta t/m}$ per step, so $|x_n - x_{n-1}|/\Delta t \sim \sqrt{\hbar/m\Delta t} \to \infty$: they are continuous but not differentiable, and $\dot x$ in $L$ stands for the difference quotient. For $V(x)$ it does not matter where in each step $V$ is evaluated ([[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-1|Theorem §C4.2.1]]). For a term linear in $\dot x$, such as the vector-potential term $(e/c)\dot{\mathbf x}\cdot\mathbf A$ of [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect|§C4.3]], the choice of point is no longer harmless: moving the argument of $\mathbf A$ by a fraction of the step changes the phase by about $(e/\hbar c)|\nabla\mathbf A|\,|\mathbf x_n - \mathbf x_{n-1}|^2 \sim (e/mc)|\nabla\mathbf A|\,\Delta t$ per step, which adds up to a finite amount over the $N \sim 1/\Delta t$ steps. The sources used here do not treat this point; the Aharonov–Bohm argument there uses only the line integral $\int\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf s$ along each path, which is insensitive to it.
> - **Same weight for every path.** The classical path is not favoured in the integrand. It is singled out only by interference ([[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]]).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part IV, §10.3 (bracketed remarks on the Trotter formula and the measure)*

## Feynman's formulation, and the way back to Schrödinger

> [!principle] Principle §C4.2.3: Feynman's Formulation
> The transition amplitude of a particle between two space-time points is built from three rules:
> 1. *Superposition and composition.* Amplitudes for successive time intervals multiply, and amplitudes for alternative intermediate positions add (are integrated over).
> 2. *Dirac's correspondence.* Along a path segment of infinitesimal duration $\Delta t$, the amplitude is $\frac{1}{w(\Delta t)}\exp[iS(n, n - 1)/\hbar]$, with $S(n, n - 1)$ the classical action of the segment, and the weight $1/w$ independent of the potential.
> 3. *Normalization.* At equal times the amplitude is $\langle x_n, t_n|x_{n-1}, t_{n-1}\rangle|_{t_n = t_{n-1}} = \delta(x_n - x_{n-1})$; this fixes $1/w(\Delta t) = \sqrt{m/2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}$.
>
> The amplitude for a finite interval is then the path integral of [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^def-c4-2-1|Def. §C4.2.1]].
>
> *Domain: nonrelativistic, spinless particles whose classical Lagrangian is $\frac12m\dot x^2 - V(x)$ (in three dimensions $\frac12m\dot{\mathbf x}^2 - V(\mathbf x)$, with the factor $1/w$ cubed); with a magnetic field the Lagrangian gains $(e/c)\dot{\mathbf x}\cdot\mathbf A$ ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-7|Theorem §C4.3.7]]). Spin and relativistic particles need more than this.*
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.4, eqs. (2.300)–(2.312) and the summary following (2.313) · the user's 511 notes, §"Feynman's Formulation", §"The Weight Factor"*

^pr-c4-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Dirac's remark and what Feynman borrowed
> Dirac had observed that $\exp\bigl[i\int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\,L_{\text{classical}}/\hbar\bigr]$ "corresponds to" $\langle x_2, t_2|x_1, t_1\rangle$. Feynman asked whether "corresponds to" means "is equal to" or "is proportional to", and found that it means: is the contribution of one path, to be summed over all paths. Sakurai stresses that this is not a derivation but a new formulation, which borrows only (1) the superposition principle, (2) the composition property, and (3) the classical correspondence in the limit $\hbar \to 0$. The weight $1/w$ follows from rule 3 because $\sqrt{m/2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}\,e^{im\xi^2/2\hbar\Delta t} \to \delta(\xi)$ ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]], item 3); and it agrees with the free propagator for an interval $\Delta t$, whose exponent is exactly the free action of the segment.
>
> *Source note:* Sakurai 3rd ed. (PDF p. 91) continues: "Even though we obtained the same result as the conventional theory for the free-particle case, it is now obvious, from what we have done so far, that Feynman's formulation is completely equivalent to Schrödinger's wave mechanics." The logic of the paragraph, which goes on to prove the equivalence, requires "it is *not* obvious"; the same sentence, with "now", is in the user's 511 notes (§"Equivalence to Schrödinger's Wave Mechanics"). [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-4|Theorem §C4.2.4]] is that proof.

^rem-c4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.4: Feynman's Amplitude Obeys the Schrödinger Equation
> The amplitude $\langle x, t|x_1, t_1\rangle$ defined by [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^pr-c4-2-3|Principle §C4.2.3]] satisfies, in the variables $(x, t)$,
>
> $$
> i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\langle x, t|x_1, t_1\rangle = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\langle x, t|x_1, t_1\rangle + V(x)\,\langle x, t|x_1, t_1\rangle ,
> $$
>
> and tends to $\delta(x - x_1)$ as $t \to t_1$. It is therefore the propagator of Schrödinger's wave mechanics ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]]): Feynman's formulation and Schrödinger's are equivalent. Together with [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-2|Theorem §C4.2.2]], which runs the other way, either can be taken as the starting point.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.4, eqs. (2.314)–(2.320) · the user's 511 notes, §"Equivalence to Schrödinger's Wave Mechanics"*

^thm-c4-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Split off the last step of length $\Delta t$ (rule 1), use the short-time amplitude for it (rules 2–3, with $V$ evaluated at the final point, which [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-1|Theorem §C4.2.1]] allows), and substitute $\xi = x_N - x_{N-1}$, $x_N \to x$, $t_N \to t + \Delta t$:
>
> $$
> \langle x, t + \Delta t|x_1, t_1\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\,\exp\Bigl(\frac{im\xi^2}{2\hbar\Delta t} - \frac{iV\Delta t}{\hbar}\Bigr)\,\langle x - \xi, t|x_1, t_1\rangle .
> $$
>
> As $\Delta t \to 0$ the kernel concentrates at $\xi = 0$ ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]], item 3), so expand $\langle x - \xi, t|x_1, t_1\rangle = \langle x, t|\cdot\rangle - \xi\,\partial_x\langle x, t|\cdot\rangle + \frac{\xi^2}{2}\partial_x^2\langle x, t|\cdot\rangle + \dots$, and expand the left side and $e^{-iV\Delta t/\hbar}$ in $\Delta t$:
>
> $$
> \langle x, t|\cdot\rangle + \Delta t\,\partial_t\langle x, t|\cdot\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}}\int d\xi\,e^{im\xi^2/2\hbar\Delta t}\Bigl(1 - \frac{iV\Delta t}{\hbar} + \dots\Bigr)\Bigl[\langle x, t|\cdot\rangle + \frac{\xi^2}{2}\partial_x^2\langle x, t|\cdot\rangle + \dots\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> The term linear in $\xi$ integrates to zero (odd integrand). By [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]], $\sqrt{m/2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}\int e^{im\xi^2/2\hbar\Delta t}d\xi = 1$, so the zeroth-order terms match; and
>
> $$
> \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}}\int\xi^2e^{im\xi^2/2\hbar\Delta t}d\xi = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}}\,\sqrt{2\pi}\Bigl(\frac{i\hbar\Delta t}{m}\Bigr)^{3/2} = \frac{i\hbar\Delta t}{m} ,
> $$
>
> so a term $\xi^2$ counts as order $\Delta t$, a term $\xi^4$ as order $\Delta t^2$. Collecting the terms of first order in $\Delta t$:
>
> $$
> \Delta t\,\partial_t\langle x, t|x_1, t_1\rangle = \frac{i\hbar\Delta t}{2m}\,\partial_x^2\langle x, t|x_1, t_1\rangle - \frac{i}{\hbar}\Delta t\,V\,\langle x, t|x_1, t_1\rangle .
> $$
>
> Multiply by $i\hbar/\Delta t$: since $i\hbar\cdot i\hbar/2m = -\hbar^2/2m$, this is the Schrödinger equation. The initial condition is rule 3. A solution of the Schrödinger equation is fixed by its initial value, so the amplitude is the propagator of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^def-c4-1-1|Def. §C4.1.1]].

## The classical limit by stationary phase

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.5: Stationary Phase
> Let $g$ be smooth and $\varphi$ smooth and real on an interval containing exactly one stationary point $x_0$ of $\varphi$, nondegenerate: $\varphi'(x_0) = 0$, $\varphi''(x_0) \ne 0$, with $g$ vanishing near the ends of the interval. As $\hbar \to 0$,
>
> $$
> \int g(x)\,e^{i\varphi(x)/\hbar}\,dx = g(x_0)\,\sqrt{\frac{2\pi i\hbar}{\varphi''(x_0)}}\;e^{i\varphi(x_0)/\hbar}\,\bigl(1 + O(\hbar)\bigr), \qquad \sqrt{\frac{i}{\varphi''}} = \frac{e^{i\pi\operatorname{sgn}\varphi''/4}}{\sqrt{|\varphi''|}} .
> $$
>
> Regions without a stationary point contribute less than any power of $\hbar$. In $n$ variables, with a nondegenerate Hessian $\varphi''$, the factor is $(2\pi\hbar)^{n/2}|\det\varphi''(x_0)|^{-1/2}e^{i\pi\sigma/4}$, $\sigma$ the number of positive minus the number of negative eigenvalues of $\varphi''(x_0)$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part IV, §10.4 (stationary phase as the mechanism; the formula is the Gaussian of the series' Appendix "Gaussian Integrals" applied at the stationary point) · Sakurai §2.6.4 (the qualitative argument)*

^thm-c4-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Outline.* Away from $x_0$, $\varphi' \ne 0$, and $e^{i\varphi/\hbar} = \frac{\hbar}{i\varphi'}\frac{d}{dx}e^{i\varphi/\hbar}$; integrating by parts moves the derivative onto $g/\varphi'$ and gains a factor $\hbar$, and since $g$ vanishes at the ends this can be repeated as often as one likes. Near $x_0$, $\varphi(x) = \varphi(x_0) + \frac12\varphi''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2 + O((x - x_0)^3)$, the integrand oscillates slowly within $|x - x_0| \lesssim \sqrt{\hbar/|\varphi''|}$ and fast outside, so to leading order $g \approx g(x_0)$, the cubic term is negligible (it is of order $\hbar^{3/2}/\hbar$ there), and the Fresnel integral of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]] with $a = -i\varphi''(x_0)/2\hbar$ gives $\sqrt{\pi/a} = \sqrt{2\pi i\hbar/\varphi''(x_0)}$. In $n$ variables, diagonalize the Hessian by a rotation and apply this to each eigen-direction. A full proof with error bounds belongs to Mathematical Methods (planned).

^der-c4-2-5

*Uses:* [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.6: The Classical Limit
> When the actions involved are large compared with $\hbar$, the path integral is dominated by the neighbourhoods of the paths that make the action stationary with the end points fixed,
>
> $$
> \delta S = \delta\int_{t_1}^{t_N}L_{\text{classical}}\,dt = 0 ,
> $$
>
> that is, of the classical paths ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^pr-b6-2-1|CM Principle §B6.2.1]], [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^thm-b6-2-2|CM Theorem §B6.2.2]]). For a single classical path, to leading order in $\hbar$,
>
> $$
> \langle x_N, t_N|x_1, t_1\rangle \approx F\,e^{iS_{\text{cl}}/\hbar} ,
> $$
>
> with $S_{\text{cl}}(x_N, t_N; x_1, t_1)$ the action of the classical path, Hamilton's principal function ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-4|CM Theorem §B8.3.4]]), and a prefactor $F$ from the Gaussian fluctuations about it. The contributing paths fill a tube about the classical path whose width is set by $\delta^2S \sim \hbar$; as $\hbar \to 0$ it shrinks onto the classical path.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.4, eqs. (2.302)–(2.303) and Fig. 2.7 · the user's series, Part IV, §10.4 · the user's 511 notes, §"The Classical Limit"*

^thm-c4-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At finite $N$ the path integral is an integral over the $N - 2$ waypoints with phase $\varphi(x_2, \dots, x_{N-1}) = \sum_nS(n, n - 1)$. Its stationary points are where $\partial\varphi/\partial x_n = 0$ for every interior $n$:
>
> $$
> \frac{m(x_n - x_{n-1})}{\Delta t} - \frac{m(x_{n+1} - x_n)}{\Delta t} - \frac{\Delta t}{2}\Bigl[V'\Bigl(\tfrac{x_n + x_{n-1}}{2}\Bigr) + V'\Bigl(\tfrac{x_{n+1} + x_n}{2}\Bigr)\Bigr] = 0 ,
> $$
>
> i.e. $m(x_{n+1} - 2x_n + x_{n-1})/\Delta t^2 = -V'(x_n) + O(\Delta t)$, the discretized Lagrange equation $m\ddot x = -V'(x)$, whose solutions through the fixed ends become the classical paths as $N \to \infty$. By [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-5|Theorem §C4.2.5]] the integral is, to leading order in $\hbar$, $e^{i\varphi/\hbar}$ at the stationary point times a Gaussian factor, and the other regions cancel. At the stationary point $\varphi \to S_{\text{cl}}$. Sakurai's version of the same argument: for a path that is not stationary, a slight deformation changes $S$ to first order, and with $\hbar$ small the phase $S/\hbar$ of neighbouring paths varies wildly, so they cancel in pairs; near a path with $\delta S = 0$ the phase is unchanged to first order, and neighbouring paths add constructively. Taking $N \to \infty$ and $\hbar \to 0$ in this order and interchanging them is not justified here; the series marks the step as stated.

> [!remark] Remark: Hamilton's principle, from axiom to consequence
> Classical Mechanics states Hamilton's principle as a principle ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^pr-b6-2-1|CM Principle §B6.2.1]]) and cannot say why the action, of all functionals, should be stationary. Theorem §C4.2.6 gives the reason: nature does not choose a path; all paths contribute, and only near a stationary one do the contributions survive their mutual interference. The classical law is the $\hbar \to 0$ shadow of the quantum one, and the layer changes: what is a principle in CM is a theorem (in a limit) here. The same mechanism selects the shortest optical path in Fresnel diffraction, where only the central zones survive ([[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-2|WO Theorem §B11.3.2]]): Fermat's principle is to wave optics what Hamilton's principle is to quantum mechanics.
>
> *Stationary, not minimal.* Sakurai (followed by the user's 511 notes, §"Classical Mechanics Review") says that the classical path "minimizes the action" and calls the stationary value $S_{\min}$. Only stationarity enters the argument. The action of the oscillator is a minimum only for $T < \pi/\omega$ and a saddle beyond ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^ex-b6-2-2|CM Example §B6.2.2]]); the path integral registers each passage of a conjugate point as a factor $e^{-i\pi/2}$, the sign change of an eigenvalue of the Hessian in [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-5|Theorem §C4.2.5]] ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^rem-c4-1-3|§C4.1, Remark: Refocusing every half period, and the sign]]).

## Quadratic Lagrangians: the semiclassical form is exact

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.2.7: Quadratic Lagrangians
> If $V(x)$ is at most quadratic in $x$, then exactly
>
> $$
> K(x'', t; x', t_0) = F(T)\;e^{iS_{\text{cl}}(x'', t; x', t_0)/\hbar}, \qquad T = t - t_0 ,
> $$
>
> with $F$ independent of $x''$ and $x'$. $F$ solves
>
> $$
> \frac{F'(T)}{F(T)} = -\frac{1}{2m}\,\frac{\partial^2S_{\text{cl}}}{\partial x''^2}, \qquad F(T) \sim \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar T}}\quad (T \to 0^+) .
> $$
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part IV, §10.4, eq. (quadratic-exact) (the form $F(T)e^{iS_{\text{cl}}/\hbar}$; the equation for $F$ is derived here from the Schrödinger equation and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation)*

^thm-c4-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The form.* Write every path as $x(t) = x_{\text{cl}}(t) + \eta(t)$, with $\eta(t_0) = \eta(t) = 0$. For quadratic $L$ the Taylor expansion of $S[x_{\text{cl}} + \eta]$ in $\eta$ stops at second order, and the first-order term vanishes because $x_{\text{cl}}$ makes $S$ stationary. So $S[x] = S_{\text{cl}} + S_2[\eta]$ exactly, where $S_2[\eta] = \int\bigl(\frac12m\dot\eta^2 - \frac12V''\eta^2\bigr)dt$ involves neither end point. In the sliced integral the shift $x_n = x_{\text{cl}}(t_n) + \eta_n$ has unit Jacobian, so $K = e^{iS_{\text{cl}}/\hbar}\int\mathscr D[\eta]\,e^{iS_2[\eta]/\hbar}$, and the remaining integral, over paths from $0$ to $0$, depends on $T$ only.
>
> *The prefactor.* $K$ satisfies the Schrödinger equation in $(x'', t)$ ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]]). Write $S = S_{\text{cl}}$ and insert $K = Fe^{iS/\hbar}$, with $\partial_tF = F'(T)$ and $\partial_{x''}F = 0$:
>
> $$
> i\hbar F' - F\,\partial_tS = -\frac{i\hbar}{2m}F\,\partial_{x''}^2S + \frac{F}{2m}(\partial_{x''}S)^2 + VF .
> $$
>
> $S$ solves the Hamilton–Jacobi equation $\partial_tS + (\partial_{x''}S)^2/2m + V = 0$ ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-4|CM Theorem §B8.3.4]]), so the terms without $\hbar$ cancel and $F' = -F\,\partial_{x''}^2S/2m$. For quadratic $V$, $\partial_{x''}^2S$ does not depend on $x''$, consistent with $F = F(T)$. As $T \to 0$ every quadratic system reduces to the free particle, and $K$ must tend to $\delta$ ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]], item 3), which fixes the constant of integration.

> [!remark] Remark: What Theorem §C4.2.7 explains
> - The free propagator's exponent was exactly $iS_{\text{cl}}/\hbar$ ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]]) because the free Lagrangian is quadratic: the semiclassical form of Theorem §C4.2.6 is exact there, not just a leading term. For $V$ beyond quadratic, $F$ depends on the end points and corrections in powers of $\hbar$ appear.
> - When one path dominates, the leading term has the WKB structure: the action in the phase and a prefactor fixed by the fluctuations. The WKB wave function ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-1|QM Theorem §B9.3.1]]), with phase $\int p\,dx/\hbar$ and amplitude $p^{-1/2}$, is the fixed-energy version; this is the promise of the series' Remark "Slow potentials: WKB in brief", and the reason level B found the classical action in the WKB phase ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^rem-b9-3-1|QM Remark: The expansion in powers of ħ, and the classical action]]).
> - In both exactly solved cases, $F^2 = -\frac{1}{2\pi i\hbar}\frac{\partial^2S_{\text{cl}}}{\partial x''\partial x'}$: for the free particle $\partial^2S/\partial x''\partial x' = -m/T$, for the oscillator $-m\omega/\sin\omega T$ (the cross-derivatives of [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^ex-b8-3-1|CM Example §B8.3.1]] and [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^ex-b8-3-2|CM Example §B8.3.2]]). The prefactor blows up exactly where the classical cross-derivative does, at the conjugate points.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part IV, §10.4 and Remark "Slow potentials: WKB in brief" (§8)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C4.2.1: The Oscillator Propagator from the Path Integral
> The series calls this "Route 2" (Route 1 being the spectral sum of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-5|Theorem §C4.1.5]]); Sakurai remarks that evaluating the oscillator path integral by brute force is cumbersome, and Theorem §C4.2.7 avoids it.
>
> *Action.* The classical path from $(x', t_0)$ to $(x'', t)$ and its action are computed in [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^ex-b8-3-2|CM Example §B8.3.2]]:
>
> $$
> S_{\text{cl}} = \frac{m\omega}{2\sin\omega T}\Bigl[\bigl(x''^2 + x'^2\bigr)\cos\omega T - 2x''x'\Bigr], \qquad \frac{\partial^2S_{\text{cl}}}{\partial x''^2} = m\omega\cot\omega T .
> $$
>
> *Prefactor.* By [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-7|Theorem §C4.2.7]], $F'/F = -\frac\omega2\cot\omega T = -\frac12\frac{d}{dT}\ln\sin\omega T$, so $F = C/\sqrt{\sin\omega T}$. As $T \to 0$, $\sin\omega T \approx \omega T$ and $F \to \sqrt{m/2\pi i\hbar T}$ requires $C = \sqrt{m\omega/2\pi i\hbar}$. Hence
>
> $$
> K = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\pi i\hbar\sin\omega T}}\;\exp\Bigl\{\frac{im\omega}{2\hbar\sin\omega T}\Bigl[\bigl(x''^2 + x'^2\bigr)\cos\omega T - 2x''x'\Bigr]\Bigr\} ,
> $$
>
> the same kernel as Route 1, without Mehler's formula. (The series states the fluctuation factor, "computable as a product over the modes of $S_2$", or fixes it "more cheaply, by matching the $\omega \to 0$ free limit". That limit alone does not determine how $F$ depends on $\omega$ at finite $\omega T$: $\sqrt{m/2\pi i\hbar T}$ itself has the right limit. The differential equation supplies the dependence.) Its trace returns the ladder spectrum $\hbar\omega(n + \frac12)$ ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^ex-c4-1-3|Example §C4.1.3]]): "the path integral knows the ladder's spectrum", in the series' words.
>
> *Short times (Sakurai Problem 2.44(b)).* For small $T$, $\sin\omega T \approx \omega T(1 - \omega^2T^2/6)$ and $\cos\omega T \approx 1 - \omega^2T^2/2$, and the exponent becomes
>
> $$
> \frac i\hbar\Bigl[\frac{m(x'' - x')^2}{2T} - T\cdot\frac{\frac12m\omega^2x''^2 + \frac12m\omega^2x'^2}{2} + O\bigl(T(x'' - x')^2\bigr)\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> the short-time action of [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-1|Theorem §C4.2.1]] with $V$ averaged over the two ends; the difference from the midpoint value is of order $T(x'' - x')^2$, negligible on the scale $(x'' - x')^2 \sim \hbar T/m$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part IV, §10.4, eqs. (mehler), (trace-check) · Sakurai Problem 2.44 · the action from Series II via CM Example §B8.3.2*

> [!example] Example §C4.2.2: When Is a Motion Quantum Mechanical?
> A particle of mass $m$ goes from $(x, t) = (0, 0)$ to $(D, T)$ along one of two paths: $x_1(t) = \frac12at^2$ (the classical one) or $x_2(t) = vt$.
>
> *Force and potential.* $x_1(T) = D$ gives $a = 2D/T^2$; the force $F = ma$ is constant, so $V(x) = -max = -2mDx/T^2$. The straight path needs $v = D/T$.
>
> *Actions.* With $L = \frac12m\dot x^2 + max$,
>
> $$
> S_1 = \int_0^T\Bigl(\tfrac12ma^2t^2 + \tfrac12ma^2t^2\Bigr)dt = \frac{ma^2T^3}{3} = \frac43\,\frac{mD^2}{T}, \qquad S_2 = \tfrac12mv^2T + \tfrac12mavT^2 = \frac12\frac{mD^2}{T} + \frac{mD^2}{T} = \frac32\,\frac{mD^2}{T} ,
> $$
>
> so $S_1 < S_2$, as Hamilton's principle requires here (no conjugate points in a uniform field, [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^ex-b6-2-1|CM Example §B6.2.1]]), and $\Delta S = S_2 - S_1 = mD^2/6T$.
>
> *Numbers,* for $D = 1$ mm in $T = 1$ ms, so $D^2/T = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$:
> - a nanoparticle of 100 carbon atoms, $m = 1200\,u = 1.99\times10^{-24}$ kg: $\Delta S = 3.3\times10^{-28}$ J s, $\Delta S/\hbar \approx 3\times10^6$;
> - an electron, $m = 9.11\times10^{-31}$ kg: $\Delta S = 1.5\times10^{-34}$ J s, $\Delta S/\hbar \approx 1.4$.
>
> For the nanoparticle the two paths differ in phase by millions of radians, and so does every pair of paths that differ visibly: only a tube of paths extremely close to $x_1$ adds up, and the motion is classical. For the electron the straight path's phase differs from the classical one by about one radian: a whole range of paths, including ones far from the parabola, contributes coherently, and the motion is quantum mechanical. The criterion is $\Delta S/\hbar$, not the mass alone.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 2.41*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Imaginary time and statistical mechanics
> Put $\Delta t = -i\hbar\Delta\tau$ in the sliced integral of [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^def-c4-2-1|Def. §C4.2.1]]. Each factor becomes $\sqrt{m/2\pi\hbar^2\Delta\tau}\,\exp\{-\Delta\tau[\frac m2(\Delta x/\hbar\Delta\tau)^2 + V]\}$, real and positive: the oscillating weight turns into a Gaussian, the kinetic and potential terms now add, and the path integral becomes an honest integral over paths (the Wiener measure of Brownian motion). Taking the trace as in [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]], with $\tau$ running from $0$ to $\beta$, writes the partition function $Z = \operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H}$ as a sum over closed paths, each weighted by $e^{-(\text{Euclidean action})/\hbar}$. This is the sense in which Sakurai says path-integral methods are powerful in statistical mechanics; it is developed in [[Quantum Field Theory I]], not in PHY 511.
