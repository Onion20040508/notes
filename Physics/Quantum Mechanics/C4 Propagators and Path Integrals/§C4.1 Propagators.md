Given the wave function at one time, what is it later? Level B answered by expanding $\psi(x, 0)$ in stationary states and attaching the phases $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-4|QM Theorem §B1.3.4]]), which has to be redone for every initial state; the evolution operator behind it is [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-4|QM Principle §B2.3.4]], here [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]. Level C writes the same evolution as one integral kernel, the matrix element of $\mathscr U(t, t_0)$ between position eigenkets, using the continuous resolution of the identity $\int d^3x'\,|\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x'| = 1$ ([[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets|§C2.1]]; its rigorous form is [[§37 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-37-5|556 Prop. §37.5]]). The kernel depends on the potential only, not on the state. It is a Green's function, its trace is the partition function, and its composition law is the input to Feynman's path integral ([[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral|§C4.2]]).

*Notation (Sakurai):* primes label eigenvalues, so $|\mathbf x'\rangle$ is the eigenket of the position operator $\mathbf x$ with eigenvalue $\mathbf x'$, and $|a'\rangle$ an eigenket of an observable $A$ that commutes with $H$, with $H|a'\rangle = E_{a'}|a'\rangle$. In this section $H$ does not depend on time, so $\mathscr U(t, t_0) = \exp[-iH(t - t_0)/\hbar]$, and $\hbar$ stays explicit.

## The propagator

> [!definition] Definition §C4.1.1: Propagator
> For a time-independent Hamiltonian $H$, the **propagator** is the position-space matrix element of the time-evolution operator,
>
> $$
> K(\mathbf x'', t; \mathbf x', t_0) = \langle\mathbf x''|\,\exp\Bigl[\frac{-iH(t - t_0)}{\hbar}\Bigr]\,|\mathbf x'\rangle .
> $$
>
> For $t > t_0$, $K$ as a function of $\mathbf x''$ is the wave function at time $t$ of a particle that was localized precisely at $\mathbf x'$ at time $t_0$: $\exp[-iH(t - t_0)/\hbar]\,|\mathbf x'\rangle$ is the state ket at $t$ of that particle.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.1, eq. (2.274) · the user's 511 notes, §"Propagators in Wave Mechanics" · the user's series, Part IV, §10.1*

^def-c4-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.1: The Propagator Evolves Every Wave Function; Spectral Form
> 1. For every state, the wave function at time $t$ is the initial wave function integrated against the propagator:
>
> $$
> \psi(\mathbf x'', t) = \int d^3x'\;K(\mathbf x'', t; \mathbf x', t_0)\,\psi(\mathbf x', t_0) .
> $$
>
> 2. If $\{|a'\rangle\}$ is an orthonormal basis of eigenkets of an observable $A$ with $[A, H] = 0$, and $u_{a'}(\mathbf x') = \langle\mathbf x'|a'\rangle$ are their wave functions,
>
> $$
> K(\mathbf x'', t; \mathbf x', t_0) = \sum_{a'}\langle\mathbf x''|a'\rangle\langle a'|\mathbf x'\rangle\exp\Bigl[\frac{-iE_{a'}(t - t_0)}{\hbar}\Bigr] = \sum_{a'}u_{a'}(\mathbf x'')\,u_{a'}^*(\mathbf x')\,e^{-iE_{a'}(t - t_0)/\hbar} ,
> $$
>
> with the sum read as an integral over any continuous part of the spectrum.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.1, eqs. (2.265)–(2.272) · the user's series, Part IV, §10.1–10.2*

^thm-c4-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. The state at $t$ is $|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle = \mathscr U(t, t_0)|\alpha, t_0\rangle$. Insert $1 = \int d^3x'\,|\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x'|$ to the right of $\mathscr U$ and project on $\langle\mathbf x''|$:
>
> $$
> \psi(\mathbf x'', t) = \langle\mathbf x''|\mathscr U(t, t_0)|\alpha, t_0\rangle = \int d^3x'\,\langle\mathbf x''|\mathscr U(t, t_0)|\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x'|\alpha, t_0\rangle .
> $$
>
> 2. $A$ and $H$ commute and the $|a'\rangle$ are eigenkets of both, so $\mathscr U(t, t_0)|a'\rangle = e^{-iE_{a'}(t - t_0)/\hbar}|a'\rangle$ (a power series in $H$ acting on an eigenket), and with $1 = \sum_{a'}|a'\rangle\langle a'|$,
>
> $$
> \mathscr U(t, t_0) = \sum_{a'}|a'\rangle\,e^{-iE_{a'}(t - t_0)/\hbar}\,\langle a'| .
> $$
>
> Sandwich between $\langle\mathbf x''|$ and $|\mathbf x'\rangle$, and use $\langle a'|\mathbf x'\rangle = \langle\mathbf x'|a'\rangle^*$.
>
> Read in the opposite order, item 1 with item 2 is level B's recipe: $c_{a'}(t_0) = \int d^3x'\,u_{a'}^*(\mathbf x')\psi(\mathbf x', t_0)$ (Sakurai eq. (2.270)), then $\psi(\mathbf x'', t) = \sum_{a'}c_{a'}(t_0)\,u_{a'}(\mathbf x'')\,e^{-iE_{a'}(t - t_0)/\hbar}$.

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level B
> Level B computed $\psi(x, t)$ state by state: expand, attach phases, resum ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-4|QM Theorem §B1.3.4]]). Theorem §C4.1.1 does the expansion and the resummation once, for a particle that starts at a point, and stores the answer in $K$. After that, every initial-value problem is one integral, and $K$ is a property of the Hamiltonian alone. The analogy is electrostatics: solve for a point charge, then superpose, $\phi(\mathbf x) = \int d^3x'\,\rho(\mathbf x')/|\mathbf x - \mathbf x'|$ (Sakurai eq. (2.275), in Gaussian units; the point-charge equation is [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|EM Theorem §B1.2.2]]).
>
> Sakurai stresses what $K$ says about causality: given $\psi(\mathbf x', t_0)$, the wave function at all later times is fixed, as deterministically as a classical trajectory, *as long as the system is left undisturbed*. The only discontinuity in the theory is the change of state in a measurement ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|QM Principle §B2.3.3]]).

^rem-c4-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.2: Properties of the Propagator
> Let $H = \mathbf p^2/2m + V(\mathbf x)$.
> 1. For $t > t_0$, $K(\mathbf x'', t; \mathbf x', t_0)$ satisfies the time-dependent Schrödinger equation in the variables $(\mathbf x'', t)$, at fixed $(\mathbf x', t_0)$.
> 2. $\displaystyle\lim_{t\to t_0}K(\mathbf x'', t; \mathbf x', t_0) = \delta^3(\mathbf x'' - \mathbf x')$.
> 3. The retarded propagator $K(\mathbf x'', t; \mathbf x', t_0)\,\theta(t - t_0)$, still written $K$, is the Green's function of the time-dependent wave equation with the boundary condition $K = 0$ for $t < t_0$:
>
> $$
> \Bigl[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla''^2 + V(\mathbf x'') - i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Bigr]K(\mathbf x'', t; \mathbf x', t_0) = -i\hbar\,\delta^3(\mathbf x'' - \mathbf x')\,\delta(t - t_0) .
> $$
>
> Properties 1 and 2 determine $K$, because the Schrödinger equation has one solution for each initial wave function.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.1, eqs. (2.273), (2.276)–(2.277) · the user's 511 notes, §"Properties of the Propagator", §"Propagator as Green's Function"*

^thm-c4-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. $i\hbar\,\partial_t\mathscr U(t, t_0) = H\,\mathscr U(t, t_0)$ ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]]), so $i\hbar\,\partial_tK = \langle\mathbf x''|H\,\mathscr U|\mathbf x'\rangle$, and $\langle\mathbf x''|H|\beta\rangle = \bigl[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla''^2 + V(\mathbf x'')\bigr]\langle\mathbf x''|\beta\rangle$ for every ket $|\beta\rangle$ (the position representation of $\mathbf p$, [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]]). Term by term in the spectral form: each $u_{a'}(\mathbf x'')e^{-iE_{a'}(t - t_0)/\hbar}$ is the wave function of $\mathscr U(t, t_0)|a'\rangle$.
> 2. At $t = t_0$, $\mathscr U = 1$ and $K = \langle\mathbf x''|\mathbf x'\rangle = \delta^3(\mathbf x'' - \mathbf x')$; in the spectral form, the sum becomes $\sum_{a'}\langle\mathbf x''|a'\rangle\langle a'|\mathbf x'\rangle$, the completeness of $\{|a'\rangle\}$. The limit is meant in the sense of distributions: integrated against a wave function, $\int K\,\psi(\mathbf x', t_0)\,d^3x' \to \psi(\mathbf x'', t_0)$.
> 3. Write $L = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla''^2 + V - i\hbar\partial_t$. For $t \ne t_0$, $L\bigl[\theta(t - t_0)K\bigr] = \theta(t - t_0)\,LK = 0$ by item 1. The time derivative of the step gives $-i\hbar\,\delta(t - t_0)\,K(\mathbf x'', t_0; \mathbf x', t_0) = -i\hbar\,\delta(t - t_0)\,\delta^3(\mathbf x'' - \mathbf x')$ by item 2. The $\delta(t - t_0)$ appears because $K\theta$ jumps from $0$ to $\delta^3$ at $t = t_0$.

## Gaussian and Fresnel integrals

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.3: Gaussian and Fresnel Integrals
> 1. For complex $a$ with $\operatorname{Re}a > 0$ and any complex $b$,
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\;e^{-a\xi^2 + b\xi} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}\;e^{b^2/4a}, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\;\xi^2e^{-a\xi^2} = \frac{\sqrt\pi}{2a^{3/2}} ,
> $$
>
> with the principal branch of the root ($\operatorname{Re}\sqrt a > 0$).
> 2. The same formulas hold for $a = -i\alpha$, $\alpha$ real and nonzero, and $b$ purely imaginary, as improper (Fresnel) integrals, equivalently as the limit $\operatorname{Re}a \to 0^+$. In particular, for $\alpha = m/2\hbar\Delta t$ with $\Delta t > 0$,
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\,\exp\Bigl(\frac{im\xi^2}{2\hbar\Delta t}\Bigr) = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}{m}}, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\,\xi^2\exp\Bigl(\frac{im\xi^2}{2\hbar\Delta t}\Bigr) = \sqrt{2\pi}\Bigl(\frac{i\hbar\Delta t}{m}\Bigr)^{3/2} ,
> $$
>
> with $\sqrt i = e^{i\pi/4}$.
> 3. As $\Delta t \to 0^+$, $\sqrt{m/2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}\;\exp(im\xi^2/2\hbar\Delta t) \to \delta(\xi)$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.4, eqs. (2.309a), (2.309b), (2.319) · the user's series, Part IV, Appendix "Gaussian Integrals", eqs. (gaussian-linear), (fresnel)*

^thm-c4-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. For real $a > 0$ and $b = 0$ this is the Gaussian integral ([[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^ex-25-4|452 Ex. §25.4]]) after the substitution $\xi \to \xi/\sqrt a$. With $b$ real, complete the square, $-a\xi^2 + b\xi = -a(\xi - b/2a)^2 + b^2/4a$, and shift. Both sides of the first formula are analytic in $a$ on $\operatorname{Re}a > 0$ and entire in $b$ (the integral converges uniformly on compact sets, so it can be differentiated under the integral sign), and they agree for real $a > 0$, $b$; by the identity theorem they agree everywhere. The second formula is $-\partial/\partial a$ of the first with $b = 0$.
> 2. Put $a = \varepsilon - i\alpha$ and let $\varepsilon \to 0^+$. The left side tends to the improper integral (the Fresnel integrals converge, [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^def-b11-3-2|WO Def. §B11.3.2]]), and $\sqrt{\pi/a} \to \sqrt{\pi/(-i\alpha)} = \sqrt{i\pi/\alpha}$. For $\alpha = m/2\hbar\Delta t$ this is $\sqrt{2\pi i\hbar\Delta t/m}$; and $\sqrt\pi/2a^{3/2}$ with $1/a = 2i\hbar\Delta t/m$ is $\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\,2^{3/2}(i\hbar\Delta t/m)^{3/2} = \sqrt{2\pi}\,(i\hbar\Delta t/m)^{3/2}$. Check against WO: with $\alpha = \pi/2$, $\int e^{i\pi w^2/2}dw = \sqrt{2i} = 1 + i$, which is $2\bigl[C(\infty) + iS(\infty)\bigr]$.
> 3. By item 2 the function has integral $1$ for every $\Delta t$. Integrated against a smooth, rapidly decaying $f$, it gives (Parseval) $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\tilde f(k)\,e^{-i\hbar k^2\Delta t/2m}\,dk$, where $\tilde f(k) = \int f(\xi)e^{-ik\xi}d\xi$, because the function is the inverse Fourier transform of $e^{-i\hbar k^2\Delta t/2m}$ (item 1 with $b = ik$). As $\Delta t \to 0$ the integrand tends to $\tilde f(k)$, dominated by $|\tilde f|$, so the integral tends to $f(0)$.

## The free particle

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.4: Free-Particle Propagator
> For $H = p^2/2m$ in one dimension, with $T = t - t_0 > 0$,
>
> $$
> K(x'', t; x', t_0) = \frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp'\,\exp\Bigl[\frac{ip'(x'' - x')}{\hbar} - \frac{ip'^2T}{2m\hbar}\Bigr] = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar T}}\;\exp\Bigl[\frac{im(x'' - x')^2}{2\hbar T}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> In three dimensions, for $H = \mathbf p^2/2m$,
>
> $$
> K(\mathbf x'', t; \mathbf x', t_0) = \Bigl(\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar T}\Bigr)^{3/2}\exp\Bigl[\frac{im|\mathbf x'' - \mathbf x'|^2}{2\hbar T}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.1, eqs. (2.278)–(2.280), and Problem 2.40 · the user's series, Part IV, §10.2, eq. (free-propagator)*

^thm-c4-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Momentum commutes with $H$, and $H|p'\rangle = (p'^2/2m)|p'\rangle$. With the momentum eigenfunction $\langle x'|p'\rangle = e^{ip'x'/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$ ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]]; Sakurai eq. (1.264)) the spectral form of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-1|Theorem §C4.1.1]] is the first integral. Apply [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]] with $a = iT/2m\hbar$ (as the limit from $\operatorname{Re}a > 0$, i.e. $T \to T - i0$) and $b = i(x'' - x')/\hbar$:
>
> $$
> \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi m\hbar}{iT}}, \qquad \frac{b^2}{4a} = -\frac{(x'' - x')^2}{\hbar^2}\cdot\frac{2m\hbar}{4iT} = \frac{im(x'' - x')^2}{2\hbar T} ,
> $$
>
> and $\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\sqrt{2\pi m\hbar/iT} = \sqrt{m/2\pi i\hbar T}$. In three dimensions $\mathbf p^2/2m$ is a sum of three commuting terms, $e^{-iHT/\hbar}$ factorizes, and so does $\langle\mathbf x''|\cdot|\mathbf x'\rangle$ over the Cartesian components.

^der-c4-1-4

*Uses:* [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-1|Theorem §C4.1.1]], [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]], [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: The phase is the classical action, and the kernel is Fresnel's
> - The exponent is $iS_{\text{cl}}/\hbar$, where $S_{\text{cl}} = m(x'' - x')^2/2T$ is the action of the classical free motion from $(x', t_0)$ to $(x'', t)$, computed as Hamilton's principal function in [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^ex-b8-3-1|CM Example §B8.3.1]]. Nothing in the spectral computation mentions a Lagrangian; the series calls this the clue that only the path integral explains ([[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-6|Theorem §C4.2.6]]).
> - The free Schrödinger equation $\partial_t\psi = (i\hbar/2m)\nabla^2\psi$ has the form of the paraxial wave equation of optics, $\partial_zu = (i/2k)\nabla_\perp^2u$, for the envelope $u$ of a beam $e^{ikz}u$, with $t \leftrightarrow z$ and $m/\hbar \leftrightarrow k$. The two-dimensional free propagator is then $\frac{k}{2\pi iz}\exp\bigl(ik|\boldsymbol\rho'' - \boldsymbol\rho'|^2/2z\bigr)$: the Fresnel diffraction kernel, whose quadratic phase is the curvature term of [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-5|WO Theorem §B11.1.5]] and whose zones are [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^def-b11-3-1|WO Def. §B11.3.1]]. The spreading of a quantum packet is Fresnel diffraction in time; the far field of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^ex-c4-1-1|Example §C4.1.1]] is its Fraunhofer limit.
> - PHY 511 HW 7, Problem 4 asks for the evolution of $\psi(x, 0) = Ne^{-x^2/2a^2}$ with this propagator. One application of Theorem §C4.1.3 ($a \to \frac{1}{2a^2} - \frac{im}{2\hbar t}$) gives $\psi(x, t) = N(1 + i\tau)^{-1/2}\exp\bigl[-x^2/2a^2(1 + i\tau)\bigr]$ with $\tau = \hbar t/ma^2$, so $|\psi|^2 \propto e^{-x^2/a^2(1 + \tau^2)}$: the same packet as [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-4|QM Theorem §B1.4.4]] (with $\sigma = a/\sqrt2$ there), obtained there by Fourier transform. Same result, new route; its home stays at level B.

## The harmonic oscillator

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.5: Oscillator Propagator
> For $H = p^2/2m + \frac12m\omega^2x^2$ and $T = t - t_0$ with $\sin\omega T \ne 0$,
>
> $$
> K(x'', t; x', t_0) = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\pi i\hbar\sin\omega T}}\;\exp\Bigl\{\frac{im\omega}{2\hbar\sin\omega T}\Bigl[\bigl(x''^2 + x'^2\bigr)\cos\omega T - 2x''x'\Bigr]\Bigr\} .
> $$
>
> The root is continued from $\sqrt{m/2\pi i\hbar T}$ at small $T > 0$ through $T \to T - i0$; each time $\sin\omega T$ changes sign it contributes a factor $e^{-i\pi/2}$. The exponent is $iS_{\text{cl}}/\hbar$, with $S_{\text{cl}}$ the action of the classical oscillator path from $(x', t_0)$ to $(x'', t)$ ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^ex-b8-3-2|CM Example §B8.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.1, eqs. (2.281)–(2.283) · the user's 511 notes, §"Simple Harmonic Oscillator Propagator" · the user's series, Part IV, §10.4, eq. (mehler)*

^thm-c4-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Route 1, the spectral sum.* The energy eigenfunctions are the Hermite functions $u_n(x) = (2^nn!)^{-1/2}(m\omega/\pi\hbar)^{1/4}e^{-\xi^2/2}H_n(\xi)$, $\xi = \sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,x$, with $E_n = \hbar\omega(n + \frac12)$ ([[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-5|QM Theorem §B4.2.5]], [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-4|QM Theorem §B4.1.4]]). With $\xi = \sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,x''$, $\eta = \sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,x'$ and $\zeta = e^{-i\omega T}$, [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-1|Theorem §C4.1.1]] gives
>
> $$
> K = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}}\;e^{-i\omega T/2}\,e^{-(\xi^2 + \eta^2)/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^n}{2^nn!}H_n(\xi)H_n(\eta) .
> $$
>
> Mehler's formula, quoted by Sakurai from the special-function literature (eq. (2.283), valid for $|\zeta| < 1$, hence the prescription $T \to T - i0$), sums the series:
>
> $$
> \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta^n}{2^nn!}H_n(\xi)H_n(\eta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\exp\Bigl[\xi^2 + \eta^2 - \frac{\xi^2 + \eta^2 - 2\xi\eta\zeta}{1 - \zeta^2}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Now $e^{-i\omega T/2}(1 - e^{-2i\omega T})^{-1/2} = (e^{i\omega T} - e^{-i\omega T})^{-1/2} = (2i\sin\omega T)^{-1/2}$, which gives the prefactor. In the exponent, $\frac{1}{1 - \zeta^2} = \frac{e^{i\omega T}}{2i\sin\omega T}$, so
>
> $$
> \frac12 - \frac{1}{1 - \zeta^2} = \frac{i\sin\omega T - e^{i\omega T}}{2i\sin\omega T} = \frac{i\cos\omega T}{2\sin\omega T}, \qquad \frac{2\zeta}{1 - \zeta^2} = \frac{1}{i\sin\omega T} = \frac{-i}{\sin\omega T} ,
> $$
>
> and the exponent $(\xi^2 + \eta^2)\bigl[\frac12 - \frac{1}{1 - \zeta^2}\bigr] + \frac{2\xi\eta\zeta}{1 - \zeta^2}$ equals $\frac{i}{2\sin\omega T}\bigl[(\xi^2 + \eta^2)\cos\omega T - 2\xi\eta\bigr]$. Restoring $\xi^2 = m\omega x''^2/\hbar$, $\xi\eta = m\omega x''x'/\hbar$ gives the formula. The exponent is $i/\hbar$ times $\frac{m\omega}{2\sin\omega T}[(x''^2 + x'^2)\cos\omega T - 2x''x']$, which is the classical action of [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^ex-b8-3-2|CM Example §B8.3.2]]. A second derivation, from the path integral, is [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^ex-c4-2-1|Example §C4.2.1]].
>
> *Checks.* As $\omega \to 0$, $\sin\omega T \to \omega T$ and $(x''^2 + x'^2)\cos\omega T - 2x''x' \to (x'' - x')^2$: the free propagator. As $T \to 0^+$ at fixed $\omega$ the same holds to leading order, so property 2 of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]] holds.

*Uses:* [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-1|Theorem §C4.1.1]], [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-5|QM Theorem §B4.2.5]], [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-4|QM Theorem §B4.1.4]], [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^ex-b8-3-2|CM Example §B8.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Refocusing every half period, and the sign
> The closed form is singular where $\sin\omega T = 0$; the spectral form shows what happens there. The Hermite functions have parity $(-1)^n$ ([[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^def-b4-2-1|QM Def. §B4.2.1]]), and $e^{-i(n + \frac12)\pi} = e^{-i\pi/2}(-1)^n$, so by completeness ([[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-6|QM Theorem §B4.2.6]])
>
> $$
> K\Bigl(x'', t_0 + \frac\pi\omega; x', t_0\Bigr) = e^{-i\pi/2}\sum_nu_n(x'')u_n(-x') = e^{-i\pi/2}\,\delta(x'' + x'), \qquad K\Bigl(x'', t_0 + \frac{2\pi}\omega; x', t_0\Bigr) = -\delta(x'' - x') .
> $$
>
> Every wave function is reflected, $\psi(x, \pi/\omega) = -i\,\psi(-x, 0)$, and restored after a full period up to the sign: $\psi(x, 2\pi/\omega) = -\psi(x, 0)$. The particle that started at $x'$ is back there "with certainty" after $2\pi/\omega$, as Sakurai says; but $K$ itself is periodic only with period $4\pi/\omega$, since the zero-point phase $e^{-i\omega T/2}$ makes it change sign after $2\pi/\omega$ (Sakurai's "periodic function of $t$ with angular frequency $\omega$" holds for $|K|^2$ and for every probability). The refocusing times are the conjugate points of the classical oscillator, where every path from $x'$ returns to $-x'$ whatever its amplitude ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^cau-b8-3-1|CM Caution: The hypothesis can fail]]); the classical action blows up there, and each passage multiplies $K$ by $e^{-i\pi/2}$.
>
> *Source note:* in Sakurai 3rd ed. (PDF p. 87, below eq. (2.283)) the parenthesis "(4π/ω, and so forth)" is printed as "(4n/a, and so forth)"; the user's 511 notes have the intended $4\pi/\omega$.

## Trace, partition function and spectrum

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.6: The Trace of the Propagator and the Partition Function
> With $t_0 = 0$, set the end points equal and integrate:
>
> $$
> G(t) \equiv \int d^3x'\,K(\mathbf x', t; \mathbf x', 0) = \sum_{a'}\exp\Bigl(\frac{-iE_{a'}t}{\hbar}\Bigr) = \operatorname{Tr}\mathscr U(t, 0) .
> $$
>
> Continued to imaginary time $t = -i\hbar\beta$, with $\beta$ real and positive (Sakurai's $\beta = it/\hbar$), $G$ becomes the partition function
>
> $$
> Z = \sum_{a'}\exp(-\beta E_{a'}) .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.1, eqs. (2.284)–(2.286) · the user's 511 notes, §"Connection to Statistical Mechanics"*

^thm-c4-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the spectral form, $\int d^3x'\,|\langle\mathbf x'|a'\rangle|^2 = \langle a'|a'\rangle = 1$ for each $a'$. Equivalently: setting $\mathbf x'' = \mathbf x'$ and integrating is the trace of $\mathscr U$ in the position basis, and a trace does not depend on the basis, so it can be computed in the energy basis, where $\mathscr U$ is diagonal. Then $t = -i\hbar\beta$ turns $e^{-iE_{a'}t/\hbar}$ into $e^{-\beta E_{a'}}$, the Boltzmann factor of [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^def-b7-1-2|TH Def. §B7.1.2]], summed over states.
>
> For real $t$ the sum does not converge absolutely; $G(t)$ is defined as the limit from $t - i\varepsilon$, where it does whenever $Z$ exists. The same prescription made the Fresnel integrals of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]] meaningful.

^der-c4-1-6

*Uses:* [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-1|Theorem §C4.1.1]], [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^def-b7-1-2|TH Def. §B7.1.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.7: The Spectrum as Poles
> The Laplace–Fourier transform
>
> $$
> \tilde G(E) \equiv -\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^{\infty}dt\;G(t)\,e^{iEt/\hbar}, \qquad E \to E + i\varepsilon ,
> $$
>
> equals, in the limit $\varepsilon \to 0^+$,
>
> $$
> \tilde G(E) = \sum_{a'}\frac{1}{E - E_{a'}} .
> $$
>
> The energy spectrum is the set of simple poles of $\tilde G$ in the complex $E$-plane, each with residue equal to its degeneracy.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.1, eqs. (2.287)–(2.289) · the user's 511 notes, §"Energy Spectrum from the Propagator"*

^thm-c4-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For each term, with $E$ replaced by $E + i\varepsilon$, the integrand $e^{i(E - E_{a'} + i\varepsilon)t/\hbar}$ decays, and
>
> $$
> -\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^{\infty}e^{i(E - E_{a'} + i\varepsilon)t/\hbar}dt = -\frac i\hbar\cdot\frac{-\hbar}{i(E - E_{a'} + i\varepsilon)} = \frac{1}{E - E_{a'} + i\varepsilon} .
> $$
>
> Without the $i\varepsilon$ the integrand oscillates forever and the integral does not exist; with it, $\tilde G$ is analytic in the upper half-plane, the image of the causal condition $K = 0$ for $t < 0$ in [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]].

^der-c4-1-7

*Uses:* [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]]

> [!remark] Remark: One kernel, three readings
> Real time gives dynamics, imaginary time gives thermodynamics, and the energy transform gives the spectrum. That is why, as Sakurai notes, propagator techniques transfer to statistical mechanics: $K(\mathbf x'', -i\hbar\beta; \mathbf x', 0) = \langle\mathbf x''|e^{-\beta H}|\mathbf x'\rangle$ is the position-space density matrix of the canonical ensemble (unnormalized), and for the free particle it is a heat kernel, a Gaussian of width $\sqrt{\hbar^2\beta/m}$, the thermal wavelength up to a factor ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^def-b8-1-1|TH Def. §B8.1.1]]). The poles of $\tilde G$ are the energy-domain version of the level-B observation that a bound state is a pole of a scattering amplitude ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^rem-b3-3-4|QM Remark: The bound state as a pole of the transmission amplitude]]); scattering theory is Chapter C10 ([[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-3|Theorem §C10.1.3]], [[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^rem-c10-3-6|★ Remark: Bound states as poles of the S matrix; Levinson's theorem]]).

## The propagator as a transition amplitude

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.1.8: Transition Amplitude and Composition Law
> 1. With $|\mathbf x', t_0\rangle = \mathscr U^\dagger(t_0)|\mathbf x'\rangle$ the eigenket of the Heisenberg-picture position operator $\mathbf x_H(t_0)$ with eigenvalue $\mathbf x'$ ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]), and $\mathscr U(t) = e^{-iHt/\hbar}$,
>
> $$
> K(\mathbf x'', t; \mathbf x', t_0) = \langle\mathbf x'', t|\mathbf x', t_0\rangle ,
> $$
>
> the amplitude for a particle prepared at $\mathbf x'$ at time $t_0$ to be found at $\mathbf x''$ at time $t$.
> 2. At each time the Heisenberg position kets resolve the identity, $\int d^3x''\,|\mathbf x'', t''\rangle\langle\mathbf x'', t''| = 1$, and therefore, for $t''' > t'' > t'$,
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf x''', t'''|\mathbf x', t'\rangle = \int d^3x''\;\langle\mathbf x''', t'''|\mathbf x'', t''\rangle\,\langle\mathbf x'', t''|\mathbf x', t'\rangle ,
> $$
>
> and likewise with any number of intermediate times. In words: amplitudes for successive legs multiply; amplitudes for alternative intermediate positions add.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.6.2, eqs. (2.290)–(2.293) · the user's 511 notes, §"Propagator as a Transition Amplitude" · the user's series, Part IV, §10.1, eq. (composition)*

^thm-c4-1-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. In the spectral form write $e^{-iE_{a'}(t - t_0)/\hbar}\langle\mathbf x''|a'\rangle\langle a'|\mathbf x'\rangle = \langle\mathbf x''|e^{-iHt/\hbar}|a'\rangle\langle a'|e^{iHt_0/\hbar}|\mathbf x'\rangle$ and sum over $a'$: $K = \langle\mathbf x''|\mathscr U(t)\,\mathscr U^\dagger(t_0)|\mathbf x'\rangle = \langle\mathbf x'', t|\mathbf x', t_0\rangle$, with $\langle\mathbf x'', t| = \langle\mathbf x''|\mathscr U(t)$. That $|\mathbf x', t_0\rangle$ is an eigenket of $\mathbf x_H(t_0) = \mathscr U^\dagger(t_0)\,\mathbf x\,\mathscr U(t_0)$: $\mathbf x_H(t_0)\,\mathscr U^\dagger(t_0)|\mathbf x'\rangle = \mathscr U^\dagger(t_0)\,\mathbf x|\mathbf x'\rangle = \mathbf x'\,\mathscr U^\dagger(t_0)|\mathbf x'\rangle$. A Heisenberg state ket does not move, so this inner product is the amplitude to find the state prepared as $|\mathbf x'\rangle$ at $t_0$ at the position $\mathbf x''$ at time $t$ ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]]).
> 2. $\int d^3x''\,|\mathbf x'', t''\rangle\langle\mathbf x'', t''| = \mathscr U^\dagger(t'')\bigl[\int d^3x''\,|\mathbf x''\rangle\langle\mathbf x''|\bigr]\mathscr U(t'') = \mathscr U^\dagger(t'')\,\mathscr U(t'') = 1$. Insert it between bra and ket. Repeating gives any number of intermediate times. (As an identity between the kernels $\langle\mathbf x''|\mathscr U(t - t_0)|\mathbf x'\rangle$ it holds for any $t''$, since $\mathscr U(t''' - t') = \mathscr U(t''' - t'')\,\mathscr U(t'' - t')$; the ordering matters for the retarded Green's function.)

^der-c4-1-8

*Uses:* [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-1|Theorem §C4.1.1]], [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]], [[§37 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-37-5|556 Prop. §37.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: Time evolution as a change of basis, and its classical shadow
> - In the Heisenberg picture, $\langle\mathbf x'', t|\mathbf x', t_0\rangle$ is the transformation function between two complete sets of base kets, $\{|\mathbf x', t_0\rangle\}$ and $\{|\mathbf x'', t\rangle\}$ ([[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence|§C1.4]]): time evolution is a unitary change of basis. Classically, time evolution is a canonical transformation whose type-1 generating function is the action $S(q, t; q^0, t_0)$ ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-4|CM Theorem §B8.3.4]]). The free and oscillator propagators make the correspondence literal: the transformation function is $(\text{prefactor})\times e^{iS/\hbar}$, with exactly that $S$.
> - The composition law is the quantum version of the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation of probability theory (Sakurai, footnote 10), with amplitudes in place of probabilities. In imaginary time the free kernel becomes the diffusion kernel and the composition law becomes the Smoluchowski equation.
> - *Momentum space (PHY 511 HW 7, Problem 2; Sakurai Problem 2.43).* For the free particle, $\langle p'', t|p', t_0\rangle = \langle p''|e^{-ip^2(t - t_0)/2m\hbar}|p'\rangle = \delta(p'' - p')\,e^{-ip'^2(t - t_0)/2m\hbar}$, since $p$ commutes with $H$: momentum is conserved and only acquires a phase. The position-space kernel is complicated because $\mathbf x$ does not commute with $H$; it is the Fourier transform of this one, which is the first integral of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C4.1.1: Releasing the Delta-Well Bound State
> A particle of mass $m$ is bound by $V(x) = -\lambda\delta(x)$, $\lambda > 0$, in its only bound state; at $t = 0$ the potential is switched off. Find $\psi(x, t)$ for $t > 0$.
>
> *Before.* By [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-3|QM Theorem §B3.3.3]] (with $\alpha = \lambda$), $\psi(x, 0) = \sqrt\kappa\,e^{-\kappa|x|}$, $\kappa = m\lambda/\hbar^2$, $E_0 = -m\lambda^2/2\hbar^2 = -\hbar^2\kappa^2/2m$.
>
> *After.* The particle is free, so by [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-1|Theorem §C4.1.1]] and [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]]
>
> $$
> \psi(x, t) = \sqrt{\frac{m\kappa}{2\pi i\hbar t}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx'\,\exp\Bigl[\frac{im(x - x')^2}{2\hbar t} - \kappa|x'|\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> which is all the homework asks (the integral can be written with complementary error functions of complex argument). Three things can be read off without evaluating it.
> - *Energy.* The wave function does not jump, so just after the switch the energy is the old kinetic energy, $\langle p^2\rangle/2m = \hbar^2\kappa^2/2m = |E_0|$, and it stays $|E_0|$ for the free particle. Removing the well raised the energy by $-\langle V\rangle = \lambda|\psi(0, 0)|^2 = \lambda\kappa = 2|E_0|$.
> - *Momentum distribution.* With $\phi(p) = (2\pi\hbar)^{-1/2}\int e^{-ipx'/\hbar}\psi(x', 0)\,dx' = (2\pi\hbar)^{-1/2}\sqrt\kappa\;2\kappa/(\kappa^2 + p^2/\hbar^2)$, the momentum density $|\phi(p)|^2 = \dfrac{2\kappa^3/\pi\hbar}{(\kappa^2 + p^2/\hbar^2)^2}$ is conserved (it integrates to $1$ with $p = \hbar\kappa\tan\theta$).
> - *Far field.* Expand $(x - x')^2 = x^2 - 2xx' + x'^2$. The initial state lives in $|x'| \lesssim 1/\kappa$, so once $m/\hbar t \ll \kappa^2$, i.e. $t \gg \hbar/2|E_0|$, the $x'^2$ term is negligible (the Fraunhofer condition, a small Fresnel number $N_F$ in [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^def-b11-1-2|WO Def. §B11.1.2]]), and the integral is a Fourier transform:
>
> $$
> \psi(x, t) \approx \sqrt{\frac{m}{it}}\;e^{imx^2/2\hbar t}\,\phi\Bigl(\frac{mx}{t}\Bigr), \qquad |\psi(x, t)|^2 \approx \frac mt\,\Bigl|\phi\Bigl(\frac{mx}{t}\Bigr)\Bigr|^2 = \frac mt\cdot\frac{2\kappa^3/\pi\hbar}{\bigl(\kappa^2 + m^2x^2/\hbar^2t^2\bigr)^2} .
> $$
>
> At late times the particle is found at $x$ with the probability that its momentum was $p = mx/t$: the components sort themselves ballistically, as classical particles released from the origin would.
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 7, Problem 3 (parts (a)–(b); the energy and far-field remarks are added here)*

> [!example] Example §C4.1.2: Reading the Spectrum off the Oscillator Propagator at the Origin
> PHY 511's final practice gives the oscillator propagator in the form $K(x', t'; x, 0) = \sqrt{m\omega/\pi\hbar}\,(e^{i\omega t'} - e^{-i\omega t'})^{-1/2}\exp\{\dots\}$, which is [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-5|Theorem §C4.1.5]], since $e^{i\omega T} - e^{-i\omega T} = 2i\sin\omega T$. (a) Set $x = x' = 0$ and find the energies that contribute; (b) find $|\psi_2(0)|^2$; (c) explain the result.
>
> (a) At $x = x' = 0$ the exponent vanishes. Factor $e^{i\omega T}$ out of the root and put $y = e^{-2i\omega T}$:
>
> $$
> K(0, T; 0, 0) = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}}\;e^{-i\omega T/2}\,(1 - y)^{-1/2} = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n\,e^{-i\omega(2n + \frac12)T} ,
> $$
>
> with $(1 - y)^{-1/2} = \sum c_ny^n$, $c_0 = 1$, $c_1 = \frac12$, $c_2 = \frac38$ (convergent for $T \to T - i0$). Compared with the spectral form $K(0, T; 0, 0) = \sum_k|u_k(0)|^2e^{-iE_kT/\hbar}$, the exponents are $E = \hbar\omega(2n + \frac12) = \frac12\hbar\omega, \frac52\hbar\omega, \frac92\hbar\omega, \dots$: only every other level appears.
>
> (b) The term $n = 1$ belongs to $E_2 = \frac52\hbar\omega$, so $|u_2(0)|^2 = c_1\sqrt{m\omega/\pi\hbar} = \frac12\sqrt{m\omega/\pi\hbar}$. Check with [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-5|QM Theorem §B4.2.5]]: $H_2(0) = -2$, so $u_2(0)^2 = \frac{1}{8}\sqrt{m\omega/\pi\hbar}\cdot4$. Likewise $c_0$ gives $|u_0(0)|^2 = \sqrt{m\omega/\pi\hbar}$.
>
> (c) The weight of $E_k$ in $K(0, T; 0, 0)$ is $|u_k(0)|^2$, the probability density at the origin in the state $k$. The odd states have odd wave functions, which vanish at $x = 0$ ([[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^def-b4-2-1|QM Def. §B4.2.1]]), so they drop out: a particle released exactly at the origin has no amplitude in an odd state, and the propagator at the origin knows only the even half of the spectrum.
>
> *Source: PHY 511, Final Practice Problems, Problem 4*

> [!example] Example §C4.1.3: The Oscillator's Trace Is Its Partition Function
> Set $x'' = x' = x$ in [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-5|Theorem §C4.1.5]]. The exponent becomes $\frac{im\omega x^2}{\hbar\sin\omega T}(\cos\omega T - 1) = -\frac{im\omega}{\hbar}\tan\frac{\omega T}2\,x^2$, using $1 - \cos\omega T = 2\sin^2\frac{\omega T}2$ and $\sin\omega T = 2\sin\frac{\omega T}2\cos\frac{\omega T}2$. One more Gaussian ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]], $a = \frac{im\omega}{\hbar}\tan\frac{\omega T}{2}$) gives
>
> $$
> G(T) = \int dx\,K(x, T; x, 0) = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\pi i\hbar\sin\omega T}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi\hbar}{im\omega\tan(\omega T/2)}} = \frac{1}{2i\sin(\omega T/2)} = \frac{e^{-i\omega T/2}}{1 - e^{-i\omega T}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega(n + \frac12)T} ,
> $$
>
> the geometric series converging for $T \to T - i0$. By [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]] the exponents are the spectrum, $E_n = \hbar\omega(n + \frac12)$, zero-point energy included, here obtained from the closed-form kernel without the ladder. At $T = -i\hbar\beta$, $2i\sin(-i\beta\hbar\omega/2) = 2\sinh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)$, so
>
> $$
> Z = \frac{1}{2\sinh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)} ,
> $$
>
> the single-oscillator partition function of [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^ex-b7-2-1|TH Example §B7.2.1]], there summed level by level.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part IV, §10.4, eq. (trace-check) · Sakurai §2.6.1, eqs. (2.284)–(2.286)*
