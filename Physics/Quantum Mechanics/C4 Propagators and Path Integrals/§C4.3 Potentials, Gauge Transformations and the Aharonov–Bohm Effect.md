Classically, potentials are bookkeeping: the zero of potential energy is arbitrary, and the electromagnetic potentials are fixed only up to a gauge transformation that changes neither the fields nor any trajectory ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-2|EM Theorem §B11.1.2]], [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-1|EM Theorem §B6.4.1]]; for the Lagrangian of a charge, [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-3|REL Theorem §B3.2.3]]). Is that still true in quantum mechanics? This section takes the vector potential, its gauge freedom and the circulation-equals-flux theorem ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-3|EM Theorem §B6.4.3]]) from Electromagnetism, and the minimal-coupling Hamiltonian from Relativity ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-6|REL Theorem §B3.2.6]]). What is new at level C: the classical gauge freedom becomes a freedom of the *phase* of the state ket, $|\alpha\rangle \to e^{ie\Lambda(\mathbf x)/\hbar c}|\alpha\rangle$; potential differences that exert no force still shift interference fringes (the electric and gravitational versions); and a magnetic flux that the particle never touches shifts fringes and energy levels (the Aharonov–Bohm effect), periodically in the flux quantum. The path integral of [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral|§C4.2]] makes the last effect almost a one-line computation.

> [!caution] Caution: Gaussian units for potentials and gauge functions
> This section follows Sakurai in Gaussian units; the general rule for converting to the SI of level B, Electromagnetism and Relativity is [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^cau-c6-2-1|Caution: Gaussian and SI units for the Coulomb problem]]. What is specific to potentials and gauge functions: a particle of charge $e$ ($e < 0$ for the electron, unlike §C6.2), $\mathbf E = -\nabla\phi - \frac1c\partial_t\mathbf A$, $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$, Hamiltonian $(\mathbf p - e\mathbf A/c)^2/2m + e\phi$, gauge transformation $\mathbf A \to \mathbf A + \nabla\Lambda$, $\phi \to \phi - \frac1c\partial_t\Lambda$. **Conversion:** $e\mathbf A/c \to q\mathbf A$, $e\phi \to qV$, $\Lambda \to c\lambda$ with Griffiths' gauge function $\lambda$ ([[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^thm-b11-1-2|EM Theorem §B11.1.2]]); Relativity's $\chi$ is $-\lambda$ ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^cau-b3-2-2|REL Caution: Three ways to write a gauge transformation]]). So the phase factor $e^{ie\Lambda/\hbar c}$ is $e^{iq\lambda/\hbar}$ in SI, the Aharonov–Bohm phase $e\Phi/\hbar c$ is $q\Phi/\hbar$, and the flux quantum $2\pi\hbar c/|e| = 4.14\times10^{-7}$ G cm² is $h/|e| = 4.14\times10^{-15}$ Wb.

## Constant potentials

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.1: A Constant Potential Is a Phase
> Let $|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle$ evolve under $H = \mathbf p^2/2m + V(\mathbf x)$ and $\widetilde{|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle}$ under $\tilde H = H + V_0$, both starting from $|\alpha\rangle$ at $t_0$.
> 1. If $V_0$ is constant,
>
> $$
> \widetilde{|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle} = \exp\Bigl[\frac{-iV_0(t - t_0)}{\hbar}\Bigr]|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle, \qquad \psi(\mathbf x', t) \to \exp\Bigl[\frac{-iV_0(t - t_0)}{\hbar}\Bigr]\psi(\mathbf x', t) ,
> $$
>
> stationary energies shift as $E \to E + V_0$, and no expectation value changes.
> 2. If $V_0(t)$ is uniform in space but depends on time,
>
> $$
> \widetilde{|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle} = \exp\Bigl[-\frac i\hbar\int_{t_0}^{t}V_0(t')\,dt'\Bigr]|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.7.1, eqs. (2.321)–(2.327)*

^thm-c4-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. $V_0$ is a multiple of the identity, so it commutes with $H$, and $e^{-i(H + V_0)(t - t_0)/\hbar} = e^{-iV_0(t - t_0)/\hbar}e^{-iH(t - t_0)/\hbar}$. A stationary state $e^{-iE(t - t_0)/\hbar}$ becomes $e^{-i(E + V_0)(t - t_0)/\hbar}$. A common phase cancels in every $\langle\beta|X|\beta\rangle$; in particular the Bohr frequencies, which depend on energy differences ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-6|Theorem §C3.1.6]]), are unchanged.
> 2. Put $\theta(t) = \frac1\hbar\int_{t_0}^tV_0\,dt'$. Then $i\hbar\,\partial_t\bigl(e^{-i\theta}|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle\bigr) = e^{-i\theta}\bigl(\hbar\theta' + H\bigr)|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle = (H + V_0(t))\,e^{-i\theta}|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle$, and the initial values agree.

^der-c4-3-1

*Uses:* [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-6|Theorem §C3.1.6]]

> [!remark] Remark: The first gauge transformation
> Classically the zero of potential energy is irrelevant because only $\nabla V$ enters Newton's law. Quantum mechanically, the change of convention $V \to V + V_0$ must be accompanied by a change of the state, $|\alpha\rangle \to e^{-iV_0(t - t_0)/\hbar}|\alpha\rangle$: the classical freedom becomes a phase freedom. Sakurai calls this the first example of a **gauge transformation**. Choosing $V_0(t)$ means choosing a new zero of energy at each instant. A common phase is unobservable; but potential *differences* between two regions that a particle can reach by two routes are observable, although they exert no force ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^ex-c4-3-1|Example §C4.3.1]]).

^rem-c4-3-1

> [!example] Example §C4.3.1: A Split Beam in Two Charged Tubes
> A low-intensity beam of particles of charge $q$ is split in two. Each part enters a long metal tube; while both wave packets are well inside their tubes, from $t_0$ to $T'$, the upper tube is held at the constant potential $V_1$ and the lower one at $V_2 \neq V_1$. The beams then recombine on a screen. How does the interference pattern depend on the voltages?
>
> *No force.* Inside a closed, empty conductor the electrostatic potential is constant, equal to that of the walls ([[§B3.1 Laplace's Equation and the Uniqueness Theorems#^ex-b3-1-1|EM Example §B3.1.1]]); a long tube is closed for this purpose as long as the packet stays far from its ends. So inside tube $j$ the particle's potential energy is the spatially uniform $qV_j(t)$ (switching on and off while the packet is inside changes it only in time), and $\mathbf E = 0$ where the packet is.
>
> *Phases.* By [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]](2), packet $j$ leaves its tube multiplied by $e^{-iqV_j\tau/\hbar}$, $\tau = T' - t_0$, and is otherwise exactly what it would have been with no voltage. At a point $x$ of the screen, with $\psi_{1,2}(x)$ the amplitudes through the two tubes without voltages and $\delta(x) = \arg\psi_1(x) - \arg\psi_2(x)$,
>
> $$
> I(x) \propto \bigl|\psi_1e^{-iqV_1\tau/\hbar} + \psi_2e^{-iqV_2\tau/\hbar}\bigr|^2 = |\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2 + 2|\psi_1\psi_2|\cos\Bigl[\delta(x) + \frac{q(V_2 - V_1)\tau}{\hbar}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Answer.* The fringes shift by $q(V_2 - V_1)\tau/2\pi\hbar$ fringe spacings; the pattern depends only on the difference $V_2 - V_1$ (a common voltage is a common phase) and is periodic in it with period $\Delta V = h/q\tau$. For time-dependent voltages the phase difference is $\phi_1 - \phi_2 = \frac q\hbar\int_{t_0}^{T'}[V_2(t) - V_1(t)]\,dt$ (Sakurai eq. (2.329), there with $V_j$ denoting potential energies). For electrons kept in the tubes for $\tau = 10$ ns the period is $h/e\tau = 4.1\times10^{-7}$ V: a fraction of a microvolt shifts the pattern by a whole fringe, although no force acts. As $\hbar \to 0$ the cosine oscillates infinitely fast in the voltage and the effect disappears: it is purely quantum mechanical.
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 8, Problem 1 · Sakurai §2.7.1, eqs. (2.328)–(2.329) and Fig. 2.8*

^ex-c4-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: The electric and the magnetic effect are one effect
> Sakurai (footnote 13) calls this arrangement the "Minkowski-rotated" form of the Aharonov–Bohm experiment of [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-7|Theorem §C4.3.7]]. Here two histories differ in where the particle waited while a potential was applied; there they differ in which way it went around a flux. Combining the phase of [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]] with that of Theorem §C4.3.7, the relative phase of two histories forming a closed loop in space-time is $\frac{e}{\hbar c}\oint(\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf x - c\phi\,dt)$, in SI $\frac q\hbar\oint(\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf x - V\,dt) = -\frac q\hbar\oint A_\mu dx^\mu$ with the four-potential of [[§B4.2 The Electromagnetic Field Tensor#^def-b4-2-1|REL Def. §B4.2.1]]. A gauge transformation adds $\oint\partial_\mu\chi\,dx^\mu = 0$, so the loop integral is gauge invariant; by Stokes' theorem it is the flux of the field tensor through the loop, magnetic flux for a spatial loop and "electric flux" $\int(V_2 - V_1)\,dt$ for a loop that spans time.

^rem-c4-3-2

> [!example] Example §C4.3.2: Gravity-Induced Interference (Colella–Overhauser–Werner)
> Thermal neutrons are split and recombined in an interferometer whose two paths $A \to B \to D$ and $A \to C \to D$ form a parallelogram, with $AC$ and $BD$ of length $l_1$ and $AB$, $CD$ of length $l_2$. The plane of the loop is tilted about $AC$ by an angle $\delta$, so that $BD$ lies higher than $AC$ by $l_2\sin\delta$. Find the phase difference at $D$.
>
> *Why gravity can show up at all.* Classically $m\ddot{\mathbf x} = -m\nabla\Phi_{\text{grav}}$, the mass cancels, and free fall is geometry; Ehrenfest's theorem gives $d^2\langle\mathbf x\rangle/dt^2 = -g\hat{\mathbf z}$, with neither $\hbar$ nor $m$. The Schrödinger equation $[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + m\Phi_{\text{grav}}]\psi = i\hbar\partial_t\psi$ and the path-integral weight $\exp[\frac i\hbar\int(\frac12m\dot{\mathbf x}^2 - mgz)\,dt]$ contain the mass only through $m/\hbar$: a quantum effect of gravity must depend on $m$.
>
> *Time-domain argument (Sakurai).* The packet is small compared with the loop, so each path can be treated as a classical trajectory. On $BD$ the potential energy is higher by $m_ngl_2\sin\delta$ than on $AC$, which by [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]] makes the ket on $BD$ "rotate faster" by $e^{-im_ngl_2(\sin\delta)T/\hbar}$, $T$ the time to traverse $l_1$; the gravitational phases on $AB$ and $CD$ are the same and cancel. With $T = l_1/v$ and $v = p/m_n = \hbar/m_n\bar\lambda$, where $\bar\lambda = \lambda/2\pi$ is the reduced de Broglie wavelength (Sakurai's ƛ),
>
> $$
> \phi_{ABD} - \phi_{ACD} = -\frac{m_n^2\,g\,l_1l_2\,\bar\lambda\sin\delta}{\hbar^2} .
> $$
>
> *Fixed-energy argument (Sakurai Problem 2.46).* The beam has fixed energy, $p^2/2m_n + m_ngz = E$. Raising the path by $h = l_2\sin\delta$ changes $p^2$ by $-2m_n^2gh$, so $\Delta p = -m_n^2gh/p$ to first order, and over the length $l_1$ the phase $\int p\,dl/\hbar$ falls behind by $l_1|\Delta p|/\hbar = m_n^2gl_1l_2\sin\delta/\hbar p$. With $1/p = \bar\lambda/\hbar$ this is the same result.
>
> *Numbers.* For $\lambda = 1.42$ Å and $l_1l_2 = 10$ cm², $m_n^2gl_1l_2\bar\lambda/\hbar^2 \approx 56$ (Sakurai quotes 55.6). Turning the loop from $\delta = 0$ to $90^\circ$ runs the intensity at $D$ through about $56/2\pi \approx 9$ maxima and minima, as observed in 1975 by Colella, Overhauser and Werner, in agreement to within 1 %.
>
> *Reading.* The phase depends on $(m/\hbar)^2$: gravity is not purely geometric at the quantum level, and the effect disappears as $\hbar \to 0$. If gravitational and inertial mass differed, $m^2$ would be replaced by $m_{\text{grav}}m_{\text{inert}}$ (Sakurai, footnote 14), so the agreement also tests the equivalence principle with quantum objects (Relativity, Chapter C2, planned).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.7.2, eqs. (2.330)–(2.337), Fig. 2.9, Problem 2.46; the number recomputed here from CODATA constants*

## A charged particle in an electromagnetic field

> [!principle] Principle §C4.3.2: Hamiltonian of a Charged Particle
> A particle of charge $e$ and mass $m$ in static external potentials $\phi(\mathbf x)$, $\mathbf A(\mathbf x)$ has the Hamiltonian
>
> $$
> H = \frac{1}{2m}\Bigl(\mathbf p - \frac{e\mathbf A}{c}\Bigr)^2 + e\phi, \qquad \Bigl(\mathbf p - \frac{e\mathbf A}{c}\Bigr)^2 \equiv \mathbf p^2 - \frac ec\bigl(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A + \mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p\bigr) + \frac{e^2}{c^2}\mathbf A^2 ,
> $$
>
> where $\phi$ and $\mathbf A$ are functions of the position operator $\mathbf x$, and $\mathbf p$ is the generator of translations ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-4|Principle §C2.2.4]]). Written this way, $H$ is Hermitian although $\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf A$ do not commute.
>
> *Domain: a nonrelativistic, spinless particle in classical external fields that the particle does not change. A spin magnetic moment adds $-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B$ ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^mod-b6-2-2|QM Model §B6.2.2]]); relativistic particles need the square root of [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-6|REL Theorem §B3.2.6]] or a relativistic wave equation ([[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^pr-c13-1-3|Principle §C13.1.3]], [[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^pr-c13-2-1|Principle §C13.2.1]]); quantized fields are [[Quantum Field Theory]].*
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.7.3, eqs. (2.339)–(2.341) · Likharev §3.1 (the Hamiltonian of a charge in an electromagnetic field, SI)*

^pr-c4-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from the classical Hamiltonian
> The classical Hamiltonian of a charge is the free one with the minimal substitution $\mathbf p \to \mathbf p - e\mathbf A/c$, $H \to H - e\phi$ ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-6|REL Theorem §B3.2.6]] and its small-velocity limit [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^rem-b7-1-5|CM Remark: The charged particle]]); Relativity notes that this is "the rule quantum mechanics inherits" ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^rem-b3-2-5|REL Remark: Canonical versus mechanical momentum]]). Principle §C4.3.2 is that rule, with two quantum additions: $\mathbf p$ is now the translation generator, represented by $-i\hbar\nabla$, and the ordering of $\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf A(\mathbf x)$ has to be fixed (the symmetric form is the Hermitian one; in the Coulomb gauge, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 0$, $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A = \mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p$ and the issue disappears). Likharev notes the alternative is excluded: if $-i\hbar\nabla$ represented the kinematical momentum, the magnetic field would drop out of $H$ altogether.

^rem-c4-3-3

> [!definition] Definition §C4.3.1: Canonical and Kinematical Momentum
> The **canonical momentum** is $\mathbf p$, the generator of translations, with $[p_i, p_j] = 0$. The **kinematical** (mechanical) **momentum** is
>
> $$
> \boldsymbol\Pi \equiv \mathbf p - \frac{e\mathbf A}{c}, \qquad H = \frac{\boldsymbol\Pi^2}{2m} + e\phi .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.7.3, eqs. (2.343), (2.346)*

^def-c4-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.3: Velocity, the Commutators of Π, and the Quantum Lorentz Force
> In the Heisenberg picture, for static fields,
>
> $$
> m\frac{d\mathbf x}{dt} = \boldsymbol\Pi, \qquad [\Pi_i, \Pi_j] = \frac{i\hbar e}{c}\,\varepsilon_{ijk}B_k, \qquad m\frac{d^2\mathbf x}{dt^2} = \frac{d\boldsymbol\Pi}{dt} = e\Bigl[\mathbf E + \frac{1}{2c}\Bigl(\frac{d\mathbf x}{dt}\times\mathbf B - \mathbf B\times\frac{d\mathbf x}{dt}\Bigr)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> The last equation is Ehrenfest's theorem for the charge: its expectation value is the Lorentz force law, with the product of the operators $d\mathbf x/dt$ and $\mathbf B(\mathbf x)$ symmetrized.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.7.3, eqs. (2.342)–(2.347), Problem 2.47(a)*

^thm-c4-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Velocity.* $[x_i, \Pi_j] = [x_i, p_j] = i\hbar\delta_{ij}$, since $\mathbf A(\mathbf x)$ commutes with $\mathbf x$. Then $[x_i, \boldsymbol\Pi^2] = \Pi_j[x_i, \Pi_j] + [x_i, \Pi_j]\Pi_j = 2i\hbar\Pi_i$, and $dx_i/dt = [x_i, H]/i\hbar = \Pi_i/m$ ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]).
>
> *Commutators.* With $[p_i, f(\mathbf x)] = -i\hbar\,\partial_if$ ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]]),
>
> $$
> [\Pi_i, \Pi_j] = -\frac ec\bigl([p_i, A_j] + [A_i, p_j]\bigr) = -\frac ec\bigl(-i\hbar\,\partial_iA_j + i\hbar\,\partial_jA_i\bigr) = \frac{i\hbar e}{c}\bigl(\partial_iA_j - \partial_jA_i\bigr) = \frac{i\hbar e}{c}\,\varepsilon_{ijk}B_k .
> $$
>
> *Force.* $\boldsymbol\Pi$ has no explicit time dependence for static $\mathbf A$, so $d\Pi_i/dt = [\Pi_i, H]/i\hbar$. First, $[\Pi_i, \boldsymbol\Pi^2] = \Pi_j[\Pi_i, \Pi_j] + [\Pi_i, \Pi_j]\Pi_j = \frac{i\hbar e}{c}\varepsilon_{ijk}(\Pi_jB_k + B_k\Pi_j)$. Second, $[\Pi_i, e\phi] = [p_i, e\phi] = -i\hbar e\,\partial_i\phi = i\hbar eE_i$. Hence
>
> $$
> \frac{d\Pi_i}{dt} = eE_i + \frac{e}{2mc}\varepsilon_{ijk}\bigl(\Pi_jB_k + B_k\Pi_j\bigr) .
> $$
>
> With $\Pi_j = m\,dx_j/dt$: $\varepsilon_{ijk}\dot x_jB_k = (\dot{\mathbf x}\times\mathbf B)_i$ and $\varepsilon_{ijk}B_k\dot x_j = -(\mathbf B\times\dot{\mathbf x})_i$, which gives the formula.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.4: The Wave Equation and the Current with a Vector Potential
> In the position representation, with $\psi = \langle\mathbf x'|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle$,
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{2m}\Bigl(-i\hbar\nabla' - \frac{e\mathbf A(\mathbf x')}{c}\Bigr)\cdot\Bigl(-i\hbar\nabla' - \frac{e\mathbf A(\mathbf x')}{c}\Bigr)\psi + e\phi(\mathbf x')\psi = i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} ,
> $$
>
> where the first $\nabla'$ acts on both $\mathbf A$ and $\psi$. The density $\rho = |\psi|^2$ obeys $\partial_t\rho + \nabla'\cdot\mathbf j = 0$ with
>
> $$
> \mathbf j = \frac\hbar m\operatorname{Im}\bigl(\psi^*\nabla'\psi\bigr) - \frac{e}{mc}\mathbf A|\psi|^2 = \frac\rho m\Bigl(\nabla S - \frac{e\mathbf A}{c}\Bigr) \quad\text{for } \psi = \sqrt\rho\,e^{iS/\hbar} ,
> $$
>
> and $\int d^3x'\,\mathbf j = \langle\boldsymbol\Pi\rangle/m$, the kinematical, not the canonical, momentum.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.7.3, eqs. (2.348)–(2.354), Problem 2.47(b) · Likharev §3.1 (the probability current with a vector potential)*

^thm-c4-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Wave equation.* $\langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf p|\beta\rangle = -i\hbar\nabla'\langle\mathbf x'|\beta\rangle$ and $\langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf A(\mathbf x)|\beta\rangle = \mathbf A(\mathbf x')\langle\mathbf x'|\beta\rangle$, so $\langle\mathbf x'|\boldsymbol\Pi|\beta\rangle = (-i\hbar\nabla' - e\mathbf A(\mathbf x')/c)\langle\mathbf x'|\beta\rangle$. Applying this twice, to $|\beta\rangle = \boldsymbol\Pi|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle$ and then to $|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle$, gives the kinetic term.
>
> *Continuity.* Write $D = \nabla' - \frac{ie}{\hbar c}\mathbf A$, so $H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}D\cdot D + e\phi$. The identity $\nabla'\cdot(\psi^{\ast}D\psi) = |D\psi|^2 + \psi^{\ast}D\cdot D\psi$ (expand $\nabla'\psi^{\ast} = (D\psi)^{\ast} - \frac{ie}{\hbar c}\mathbf A\psi^{\ast}$) and its complex conjugate give $\psi^{\ast}D\cdot D\psi - \psi(D\cdot D\psi)^{\ast} = \nabla'\cdot\bigl(\psi^{\ast}D\psi - \psi(D\psi)^{\ast}\bigr)$. Then, $e\phi$ being real,
>
> $$
> \partial_t\rho = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\bigl[\psi^*H\psi - \psi(H\psi)^*\bigr] = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla'\cdot\bigl(\psi^*D\psi - \psi(D\psi)^*\bigr) = -\nabla'\cdot\Bigl[\frac\hbar m\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*D\psi)\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> and $\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*D\psi) = \operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\nabla'\psi) - \frac{e}{\hbar c}\mathbf A|\psi|^2$. For $\psi = \sqrt\rho\,e^{iS/\hbar}$, $\psi^*\nabla'\psi = \sqrt\rho\,\nabla'\sqrt\rho + \frac i\hbar\rho\,\nabla'S$, whose imaginary part is $\rho\nabla'S/\hbar$.
>
> *Integral.* $\frac\hbar m\int\operatorname{Im}(\psi^{\ast}\nabla'\psi) = \frac1m\operatorname{Re}\int\psi^{\ast}(-i\hbar\nabla'\psi) = \langle\mathbf p\rangle/m$, since $\langle\mathbf p\rangle$ is real; the $\mathbf A$ term gives $-\frac{e}{mc}\langle\mathbf A\rangle$.

> [!remark] Remark: The current, one level up
> Level B defined the current $\frac\hbar m\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\nabla\psi)$ and wrote it as $\rho\nabla S/m$ ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^def-b1-1-1|QM Def. §B1.1.1]], [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-2|QM Theorem §B1.1.2]]). With a vector potential the same results hold after the substitution $\nabla \to \nabla - \frac{ie}{\hbar c}\mathbf A$ (Sakurai eq. (2.352)): the velocity field is $(\nabla S - e\mathbf A/c)/m$, the kinematical momentum per mass. Neither $\nabla S$ nor $\mathbf A$ is gauge invariant; their difference is ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]]).

## Gauge transformations of the state

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.5: Gauge Transformations in Quantum Mechanics
> Let $|\alpha\rangle$ describe a physical situation with vector potential $\mathbf A$, and let $\tilde{\mathbf A} = \mathbf A + \nabla\Lambda$ with $\Lambda(\mathbf x)$ real and single-valued. Then
>
> $$
> |\tilde\alpha\rangle = \mathscr G|\alpha\rangle, \qquad \mathscr G = \exp\Bigl[\frac{ie\Lambda(\mathbf x)}{\hbar c}\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> describes the same physical situation with $\tilde{\mathbf A}$:
> 1. $\mathscr G$ is unitary; $\langle\mathbf x\rangle$ and $\langle\boldsymbol\Pi\rangle$ are the same in both descriptions, $\langle\tilde\alpha|\mathbf x|\tilde\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|\mathbf x|\alpha\rangle$ and $\langle\tilde\alpha|(\mathbf p - e\tilde{\mathbf A}/c)|\tilde\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|(\mathbf p - e\mathbf A/c)|\alpha\rangle$, while $\langle\mathbf p\rangle$ shifts by $\frac ec\langle\nabla\Lambda\rangle$.
> 2. If $|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle$ solves the Schrödinger equation with $\mathbf A$, then $\mathscr G|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle$ solves it with $\tilde{\mathbf A}$.
> 3. Wave functions transform by a local phase, $\tilde\psi(\mathbf x', t) = e^{ie\Lambda(\mathbf x')/\hbar c}\psi(\mathbf x', t)$: $\rho$ is unchanged, $S \to S + e\Lambda/c$, and the current $\mathbf j$ is gauge invariant.
> 4. For time-dependent gauge functions, $\mathbf A \to \mathbf A + \nabla\Lambda$, $\phi \to \phi - \frac1c\partial_t\Lambda$ (Sakurai eq. (2.357)), the same $\mathscr G$, now with $\Lambda(\mathbf x, t)$, maps solutions to solutions. The constant potential of [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-1|Theorem §C4.3.1]] is the case $\Lambda = -c\lambda t$, $V_0 = e\lambda$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.7.3, eqs. (2.355)–(2.376) (static $\Lambda$; item 4 is derived here) · Likharev §3.1 (gauge transformation of the wave function's phase)*

^thm-c4-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The key identity.* Since $[\mathbf p, f(\mathbf x)] = -i\hbar\nabla f$ ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]]; Sakurai eq. (2.97b)),
>
> $$
> \mathscr G^\dagger\,\mathbf p\,\mathscr G = \mathscr G^\dagger\bigl([\mathbf p, \mathscr G] + \mathscr G\,\mathbf p\bigr) = \mathscr G^\dagger(-i\hbar)\frac{ie}{\hbar c}(\nabla\Lambda)\mathscr G + \mathbf p = \mathbf p + \frac ec\nabla\Lambda ,
> $$
>
> so $\mathscr G^\dagger\bigl(\mathbf p - e\tilde{\mathbf A}/c\bigr)\mathscr G = \mathbf p - e\mathbf A/c$: $\mathscr G^\dagger\tilde{\boldsymbol\Pi}\mathscr G = \boldsymbol\Pi$.
>
> 1. $\mathscr G$ is a phase function of $\mathbf x$, hence unitary and commuting with $\mathbf x$: $\mathscr G^\dagger\mathbf x\mathscr G = \mathbf x$. With the key identity, $\langle\tilde\alpha|\tilde{\boldsymbol\Pi}|\tilde\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|\mathscr G^\dagger\tilde{\boldsymbol\Pi}\mathscr G|\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|\boldsymbol\Pi|\alpha\rangle$, and $\langle\tilde\alpha|\mathbf p|\tilde\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|\mathbf p + \frac ec\nabla\Lambda|\alpha\rangle$. Unitarity is needed because the norm must be preserved; Sakurai's argument is that these requirements (positions and kinematical momenta unchanged, as classically, [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^rem-b3-2-5|REL Remark: Canonical versus mechanical momentum]]) are met by $\mathscr G$.
> 2. Applying the key identity twice, $\mathscr G^\dagger\tilde{\boldsymbol\Pi}^2\mathscr G = (\mathscr G^\dagger\tilde{\boldsymbol\Pi}\mathscr G)^2 = \boldsymbol\Pi^2$, so $\mathscr G^\dagger\tilde H\mathscr G = H$ ($\phi$ commutes with $\mathscr G$). For static $\Lambda$, $i\hbar\partial_t(\mathscr G|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle) = \mathscr GH|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle = \tilde H\mathscr G|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle$.
> 3. Project on $\langle\mathbf x'|$: $\langle\mathbf x'|\mathscr G = e^{ie\Lambda(\mathbf x')/\hbar c}\langle\mathbf x'|$. The modulus is unchanged and the phase $S/\hbar$ gains $e\Lambda/\hbar c$; then $\nabla S - e\tilde{\mathbf A}/c = \nabla S + e\nabla\Lambda/c - e\mathbf A/c - e\nabla\Lambda/c$ is unchanged, and so is $\mathbf j$ by [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]].
> 4. Now $\tilde H = \tilde{\boldsymbol\Pi}^2/2m + e\phi - \frac ec\partial_t\Lambda$, so $\mathscr G^\dagger\tilde H\mathscr G = H - \frac ec\partial_t\Lambda$. On the other side, $i\hbar\partial_t(\mathscr G|\alpha\rangle) = \mathscr G\bigl(i\hbar\partial_t - \frac ec\partial_t\Lambda\bigr)|\alpha\rangle = \mathscr G\bigl(H - \frac ec\partial_t\Lambda\bigr)|\alpha\rangle = \tilde H\mathscr G|\alpha\rangle$. For $\Lambda = -c\lambda t$: $\phi \to \phi + \lambda$, $\mathbf A$ unchanged, $\mathscr G = e^{-ie\lambda t/\hbar}$, which is Theorem §C4.3.1 with $V_0 = e\lambda$.

> [!remark] Remark: The classical gauge freedom becomes a phase freedom
> Classically a gauge transformation acts on the potentials only. The fields, the trajectories and the mechanical momentum are unchanged; the Lagrangian changes by a total time derivative ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^thm-b6-2-4|CM Theorem §B6.2.4]], [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-3|REL Theorem §B3.2.3]]), and the canonical momentum shifts ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^def-b3-2-1|REL Def. §B3.2.1]]). Quantum mechanically it must also act on the state, by the local phase $e^{ie\Lambda(\mathbf x)/\hbar c}$, a different phase at every point. So the phase of $\psi$ at a point is exactly as conventional as $\Lambda$: only gauge-invariant combinations ($|\psi|^2$, $\mathbf j$, $\langle\boldsymbol\Pi\rangle$, and phase *differences* around closed loops corrected by $\oint\mathbf A$) are observable. The canonical momentum, defined as the generator of translations, is manifestly gauge dependent: in the uniform field $\mathbf B = B\hat{\mathbf z}$, the potentials $(-By/2, Bx/2, 0)$ and $(-By, 0, 0)$ differ by $\nabla(-Bxy/2)$, and $p_x$ is conserved with the second but not with the first, while the helical trajectory is the same ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^ex-b3-2-1|REL Example §B3.2.1]]).
>
> *Why "gauge".* Weyl tried to build electromagnetism from a change of *scale*, $1 \to 1 + \Sigma\cdot d\mathbf x$ between neighbouring points, with $\Sigma$ identified with $\mathbf A$; the combination $\nabla + \Sigma$ resembles the gauge-covariant $\nabla - \frac{ie}{\hbar c}\mathbf A$ except for the $i$. Fock and London recognized that quantum mechanics realizes the idea with a change of *phase*, $1 \to 1 - \frac{ie}{\hbar c}\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf x$, and the name *Eichinvarianz* (gauge invariance) stuck.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.7.3, eqs. (2.359)–(2.363), (2.377)–(2.381)*

> [!example] Example §C4.3.3: Landau Levels from the Commutator
> An electron (charge $e$, mass $m$) moves in a uniform field $\mathbf B = B\hat{\mathbf z}$. Find the energy levels without choosing a gauge.
>
> *Algebra.* By [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]], $[\Pi_x, \Pi_y] = i\hbar eB/c$, while $\Pi_z = p_z$ commutes with $\Pi_x$, $\Pi_y$ and $H = (\Pi_x^2 + \Pi_y^2 + p_z^2)/2m$. Assume $eB > 0$ (otherwise exchange $x$ and $y$) and set
>
> $$
> a = \frac{\Pi_x + i\Pi_y}{\sqrt{2\hbar eB/c}}, \qquad [a, a^\dagger] = \frac{-2i[\Pi_x, \Pi_y]}{2\hbar eB/c} = 1, \qquad a^\dagger a = \frac{\Pi_x^2 + \Pi_y^2 + i[\Pi_x, \Pi_y]}{2\hbar eB/c} = \frac{\Pi_x^2 + \Pi_y^2}{2\hbar eB/c} - \frac12 .
> $$
>
> *Spectrum.* $\Pi_x^2 + \Pi_y^2 = (2\hbar eB/c)(a^\dagger a + \frac12)$, and $a^\dagger a$ has the spectrum $0, 1, 2, \dots$ by the oscillator algebra ([[§C3.4 The Harmonic Oscillator and Coherent States Revisited#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]]; [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-4|QM Theorem §B4.1.4]]). With $p_z = \hbar k$,
>
> $$
> E_{k,n} = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} + \frac{|eB|\hbar}{mc}\Bigl(n + \frac12\Bigr), \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots
> $$
>
> The transverse motion is quantized in steps of $\hbar\omega_c$, $\omega_c = |eB|/mc$ the classical cyclotron frequency ([[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^ex-b3-2-1|REL Example §B3.2.1]], in SI $|q|B/m$); the motion along $\mathbf B$ stays free. The non-commuting kinematical momenta play the roles of $x$ and $p$ of an oscillator; the derivation used only gauge-invariant operators, so the levels are gauge invariant, as they must be.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 2.49 (statement); solved here*

## The Aharonov–Bohm effect

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.6: Energy Levels Depend on an Inaccessible Flux
> A particle of charge $e$ and mass $m_e$ is confined to a hollow cylindrical shell $\rho_a \le \rho \le \rho_b$, $0 \le z \le L$, with rigid walls ($\psi = 0$ on all of them). A long solenoid in the hole carries the flux $\Phi$, with $\mathbf B = 0$ throughout the shell. Then the energies are
>
> $$
> E = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\Bigl[k^2 + \Bigl(\frac{l\pi}{L}\Bigr)^2\Bigr], \qquad l = 1, 2, \dots ,
> $$
>
> where, for each integer $m$, $k$ runs over the positive roots of
>
> $$
> J_\nu(k\rho_b)\,N_\nu(k\rho_a) - N_\nu(k\rho_b)\,J_\nu(k\rho_a) = 0, \qquad \nu = \Bigl|m - \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_0}\Bigr|, \qquad \Phi_0 = \frac{2\pi\hbar c}{e} .
> $$
>
> The spectrum depends on $\Phi$, and only through $\Phi/\Phi_0$ modulo $1$; it coincides with the field-free spectrum exactly when $\Phi$ is an integer multiple of $\Phi_0$, i.e. $\pi\rho_a^2B = 2\pi N\hbar c/e$ for a uniform field $B$ in the hole.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.7.4, eqs. (2.382)–(2.384), Fig. 2.11, Problem 2.37 ($\Phi = 0$ spectrum and the flux-quantization condition)*

^thm-c4-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Potential.* In the shell, by Stokes' theorem ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-3|EM Theorem §B6.4.3]]) and symmetry, as for the solenoid of [[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^ex-b6-4-1|EM Example §B6.4.1]],
>
> $$
> \mathbf A = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi\rho}\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi} = \frac{B\rho_a^2}{2\rho}\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi} \quad (\rho \ge \rho_a),
> $$
>
> which is curl-free there but cannot be zero: its circulation around the hole is $\Phi$.
>
> *Wave equation.* By [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]], $\nabla$ is replaced by $\nabla - \frac{ie}{\hbar c}\mathbf A$. Only the $\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ component, $\frac1\rho\partial_\phi$, is affected: $\frac1\rho\partial_\phi \to \frac1\rho\bigl(\partial_\phi - \frac{ie\Phi}{2\pi\hbar c}\bigr) = \frac1\rho\bigl(\partial_\phi - i\Phi/\Phi_0\bigr)$, Sakurai's replacement (2.383). Since $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 0$ and $A_\phi$ depends on $\rho$ only, the Schrödinger equation reads
>
> $$
> -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\Bigl[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\rho^2} + \frac1\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho} + \frac{1}{\rho^2}\Bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi} - i\frac{\Phi}{\Phi_0}\Bigr)^2 + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial z^2}\Bigr]\psi = E\psi .
> $$
>
> *Separation.* $\psi = R(\rho)\,e^{im\phi}\sin(l\pi z/L)$ with $m \in \mathbb Z$, because the wave function is single-valued, and $l \ge 1$ from the walls at $z = 0, L$. Then $(\partial_\phi - i\Phi/\Phi_0)^2e^{im\phi} = -(m - \Phi/\Phi_0)^2e^{im\phi}$, and with $k^2 = 2m_eE/\hbar^2 - (l\pi/L)^2$,
>
> $$
> R'' + \frac1\rho R' + \Bigl(k^2 - \frac{\nu^2}{\rho^2}\Bigr)R = 0, \qquad \nu = |m - \Phi/\Phi_0| ,
> $$
>
> Bessel's equation of order $\nu$, with solutions $R = aJ_\nu(k\rho) + bN_\nu(k\rho)$. $R(\rho_a) = R(\rho_b) = 0$ has a nonzero solution $(a, b)$ exactly when the determinant vanishes, which is the root condition. For $\Phi = 0$, $\nu = |m|$: Problem 2.37(a).
>
> *Dependence on $\Phi$.* Replacing $\Phi$ by $\Phi + \Phi_0$ replaces the set $\{|m - \Phi/\Phi_0| : m \in \mathbb Z\}$ by itself (relabel $m \to m + 1$), so the spectrum is periodic with period $\Phi_0$. If $\Phi/\Phi_0 \notin \mathbb Z$, then every $\nu$ differs from every integer order, and the lowest root of each channel moves with $\nu$ (for instance, the ground state, $\nu = \min_m|m - \Phi/\Phi_0| > 0$, rises above its field-free value; figure below): the spectrum changes.

> [!remark] Remark: Can the vector potential be gauged away?
> In the shell, $\mathbf A = \nabla\Lambda$ with $\Lambda = \Phi\phi/2\pi$ (check: $\frac1\rho\partial_\phi\Lambda = \Phi/2\pi\rho$). So locally $\mathbf A$ is a pure gauge, and the transformation $\psi' = e^{-ie\Lambda/\hbar c}\psi = e^{-i\Phi\phi/\Phi_0}\psi$ turns the equation into the field-free one. But $\Lambda$ is not single-valued, and $\psi'$ is not: $\psi'(\phi + 2\pi) = e^{-2\pi i\Phi/\Phi_0}\psi'(\phi)$. The flux has moved from the Hamiltonian into the boundary condition, where it still changes the spectrum, unless $\Phi/\Phi_0$ is an integer, when the gauge function is admissible after all and the spectrum is the field-free one. [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]] requires $\Lambda$ single-valued for exactly this reason.
>
> *Source note:* Sakurai 3rd ed. (PDF p. 100) says here that "we cannot set $\mathbf A = 0$ for $\rho > \rho_a$ because the discontinuity leads to an unphysical magnetic field. However, we can use a gauge transformation to set $\mathbf B = 0$, and then apply (2.375) so that the wave function gains the appropriate phase factor." A gauge transformation cannot change $\mathbf B$, which is zero in the shell anyway; the sentence is best read as the multivalued gauge transformation described above. The computation that follows in Sakurai, with $\mathbf A$ kept, is the one used in [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]].

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.7: The Aharonov–Bohm Effect
> Particles of charge $e$ travel from a source region to an interference region, passing on either side of a long impenetrable cylinder that contains the magnetic flux $\Phi_B$; outside the cylinder $\mathbf B = 0$. Then the transition amplitude is
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf x_N, t_N|\mathbf x_1, t_1\rangle = \Bigl[\int_{\text{above}}\mathscr D[\mathbf x(t)]\,e^{iS^{(0)}/\hbar}\Bigr]\exp\Bigl[\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\int_{\mathbf x_1}^{\mathbf x_N}\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf s\Bigr]_{\text{above}} + \Bigl[\int_{\text{below}}\mathscr D[\mathbf x(t)]\,e^{iS^{(0)}/\hbar}\Bigr]\exp\Bigl[\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\int_{\mathbf x_1}^{\mathbf x_N}\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf s\Bigr]_{\text{below}} ,
> $$
>
> with $S^{(0)}$ the field-free action, and the relative phase that the flux adds between the two classes of paths is
>
> $$
> \frac{e}{\hbar c}\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf s = \frac{e\Phi_B}{\hbar c}
> $$
>
> (the loop running out along the upper paths and back along the lower ones). The probability in the interference region therefore contains a term periodic in $\Phi_B$ with period
>
> $$
> \Phi_0 = \frac{2\pi\hbar c}{|e|} = 4.135\times10^{-7}\ \text{G cm}^2 ,
> $$
>
> although the particle never enters the region where $\mathbf B \neq 0$ and feels no Lorentz force. The effect depends on $\Phi_B$ only, and is gauge invariant.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.7.4, eqs. (2.385)–(2.391), Fig. 2.12 · Likharev §3.1 (SI, derived from the phase gradient of the current in two channels)*

^thm-c4-3-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The Lagrangian.* In a magnetic field the Lagrangian is $L = L^{(0)}_{\text{classical}} + \frac ec\dot{\mathbf x}\cdot\mathbf A$ ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^rem-b6-2-6|CM Remark: Velocity-dependent potentials]], in SI $+q\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf A$; relativistically [[§B3.2 The Charged Particle#^thm-b3-2-2|REL Theorem §B3.2.2]]), and its Legendre transform is the Hamiltonian of [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^pr-c4-3-2|Principle §C4.3.2]]. Use it in the path integral ([[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^pr-c4-2-3|Principle §C4.2.3]]).
>
> *One path.* On a segment from $(\mathbf x_{n-1}, t_{n-1})$ to $(\mathbf x_n, t_n)$ the action changes by $\frac ec\int dt\,\dot{\mathbf x}\cdot\mathbf A = \frac ec\int_{\mathbf x_{n-1}}^{\mathbf x_n}\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf s$, a line integral along the segment that does not involve the time. Multiplying the factors $e^{iS(n, n-1)/\hbar}$ along a path from $\mathbf x_1$ to $\mathbf x_N$ multiplies the field-free weight by $\exp\bigl[\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\int_{\mathbf x_1}^{\mathbf x_N}\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf s\bigr]$ along that path.
>
> *All paths of one class.* Outside the cylinder $\nabla\times\mathbf A = \mathbf B = 0$. Two paths that pass on the same side bound a surface that does not meet the cylinder, so by Stokes' theorem ([[§B6.4 The Vector Potential and the Magnetic Dipole#^thm-b6-4-3|EM Theorem §B6.4.3]]) their line integrals agree: the factor is common to all paths above, and pulls out of the sum over them; likewise below. That is the displayed amplitude.
>
> *The relative phase.* The difference of the two exponents is $\frac{e}{\hbar c}\bigl[\int_{\text{above}} - \int_{\text{below}}\bigr]\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf s = \frac{e}{\hbar c}\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf s = \frac{e\Phi_B}{\hbar c}$, again by Stokes. $|\text{amplitude}|^2$ contains the cross term $2\operatorname{Re}\bigl[K_{\text{above}}K^{\ast}_{\text{below}}e^{ie\Phi_B/\hbar c}\bigr]$, periodic in $\Phi_B$ with period $2\pi\hbar c/|e|$; numerically $hc/|e| = 4.135\times10^{-7}$ G cm².
>
> *Gauge invariance.* Under $\mathbf A \to \mathbf A + \nabla\Lambda$ with $\Lambda$ single-valued, each line integral from $\mathbf x_1$ to $\mathbf x_N$ changes by $\Lambda(\mathbf x_N) - \Lambda(\mathbf x_1)$, the same for both classes; the relative phase, and every probability, is unchanged ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]]). Paths that wind around the cylinder more than once would carry further multiples of $e\Phi_B/\hbar c$; in a two-arm interferometer they do not reach the detector.

> [!remark] Remark: Is the vector potential physical?
> - *What is observed.* In both forms of the effect the particle is never where $\mathbf B \neq 0$; classically, with only the Lorentz force, nothing could happen. Some have concluded that in quantum mechanics $\mathbf A$, not $\mathbf B$, is fundamental. Sakurai notes that the observable effects depend only on $\Phi_B$, which is expressible through $\mathbf B$; what is observable is the gauge-invariant phase factor $e^{ie\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf s/\hbar c}$ around closed loops, a nonlocal property of $\mathbf B$ but a local one of $\mathbf A$.
> - *Not induction.* Likharev discusses an "almost" classical explanation: switching the flux on while the particle is in flight induces an electric field around the loop (Faraday's law, [[§B8.2 Faraday's Law and Induced Electric Fields#^pr-b8-2-1|EM Principle §B8.2.1]]), which shifts the relative phase by the same $e\Delta\Phi/\hbar c$. But the effect is also there when the flux was set before the particle entered, and no field ever acted on it; experiment confirms this. Something in the region where the particle travels carries the information, and the standard reading is that the potentials are physically real despite their gauge freedom.
> - *Experiments.* The first observation was by Chambers (1960) (Likharev). Sakurai mentions experiments with a thin magnetized iron filament, a "whisker", and cites Tonomura et al. (1982) (footnote 16). Later experiments used ferromagnetic cores and superconducting shielding to keep the electrons away from the field; Likharev's Fig. 3.3, from Tonomura et al. (1986), shows a shift of half a fringe at $\Phi = \Phi_0/2$. The effect was predicted by Ehrenberg and Siday (1949) ten years before Aharonov and Bohm (1959).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.7.4 and footnotes 15–16 · Likharev §3.1 (the induction argument, Fig. 3.3 and footnote 12)*

## Magnetic monopoles

> [!theorem] Theorem §C4.3.8: Dirac Quantization
> If a magnetic monopole of strength $e_M$ exists, $\mathbf B = e_M\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2$, then for every particle of charge $e$
>
> $$
> \frac{2ee_M}{\hbar c} = \pm N, \qquad N = 0, 1, 2, \dots ,
> $$
>
> so magnetic charges are quantized in units of $\hbar c/2|e| \approx \frac{137}{2}|e|$. Conversely, the existence of one monopole would force all electric charges to be multiples of $\hbar c/2e_M$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.7.5, eqs. (2.392)–(2.406), Fig. 2.13*

^thm-c4-3-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *No global potential.* $\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\sigma = 4\pi e_M$ over a sphere about the origin, whereas a vector potential smooth on the sphere's interior would give $\int\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A)\,d^3x = 0$. So $\mathbf A$ must be singular somewhere: $e_M(1 - \cos\theta)/(r\sin\theta)\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ is singular on the negative $z$-axis.
>
> *Two patches (Wu–Yang).* Use $\mathbf A^{(\mathrm I)} = \frac{e_M(1 - \cos\theta)}{r\sin\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ for $\theta < \pi - \varepsilon$ and $\mathbf A^{(\mathrm{II})} = -\frac{e_M(1 + \cos\theta)}{r\sin\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ for $\theta > \varepsilon$. Each gives $\nabla\times\mathbf A = \hat{\mathbf r}\,\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(A_\phi\sin\theta) = e_M\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2$ on its patch. In the overlap they differ by
>
> $$
> \mathbf A^{(\mathrm{II})} - \mathbf A^{(\mathrm I)} = -\frac{2e_M}{r\sin\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi} = \nabla\Lambda, \qquad \Lambda = -2e_M\phi ,
> $$
>
> since $\nabla\Lambda = \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\partial_\phi\Lambda\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$. By [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]](3) the wave functions of a charge $e$ in the two gauges are related by $\psi^{(\mathrm{II})} = e^{-2iee_M\phi/\hbar c}\psi^{(\mathrm I)}$ there.
>
> *Single-valuedness.* Each wave function is single valued, being the expansion coefficient of the state ket in the position eigenkets of a fixed gauge. Going once around the equator, $\phi: 0 \to 2\pi$, both return to their values, which requires $e^{-4\pi iee_M/\hbar c} = 1$, i.e. $2ee_M/\hbar c \in \mathbb Z$. With $e^2/\hbar c \approx 1/137$, the smallest magnetic charge is $\hbar c/2|e| = \frac{\hbar c}{e^2}\frac{|e|}{2} \approx \frac{137}{2}|e|$.

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the quantization condition
> The gauge function $\Lambda = -2e_M\phi$ is multivalued, as in [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^rem-c4-3-6|Remark: Can the vector potential be gauged away?]]; here the requirement that both wave functions be single-valued is what quantizes the charge. Equivalently, a singular potential $\mathbf A^{(\mathrm I)}$ everywhere has a "Dirac string" along the negative $z$-axis, carrying flux $4\pi e_M$, and the condition says that this string produces an Aharonov–Bohm phase $e\cdot4\pi e_M/\hbar c \in 2\pi\mathbb Z$, so it is unobservable. Quantum mechanics does not require monopoles; none has been found (Sakurai: the electron and proton charges are equal in magnitude to four parts in $10^{19}$, a fact a monopole would explain). Dirac's argument (1931) is given here in the form of Wu and Yang; its connection to Berry's phase is [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^rem-c9-4-5|Remark: Berry's phase, Aharonov–Bohm and the monopole]].
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.7.5 and footnotes 17–18*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Flux quantization in superconductors
> In a superconductor the charge carriers are Cooper pairs, $q = -2e$ (SI), condensed into one macroscopic wave function, so the relevant flux quantum is $\Phi_0 = \pi\hbar/e = h/2e = 2.07\times10^{-15}$ Wb. Deep inside a thick superconducting ring the current density vanishes, so by [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-4|Theorem §C4.3.4]], in SI, $\nabla S = q\mathbf A$ there. Integrating around the ring, the phase of the single-valued condensate wave function changes by $2\pi n$, so $\frac q\hbar\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l = 2\pi n$: the flux through the ring is quantized, $\Phi = n\,h/2e$. This is the shell's periodicity ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-6|Theorem §C4.3.6]]) turned into a constraint, because the ring's own current adjusts the flux. Superconducting rings with Josephson junctions (SQUIDs) use this for magnetometry and as qubits.
>
> *Source: Likharev §3.1 (the superconducting flux quantum and flux quantization)*
