Perturbation theory ([[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory]]) needs a solvable Hamiltonian close to the true one. When there is none, as for helium or a molecule, a different idea still gives the ground-state energy: any guessed wave function, inserted into $\langle H\rangle$, gives an upper bound, and the best guess within a family gives the best bound. This section proves that principle and its corollaries (bounds on excited states, the matrix method, the existence of bound states in one dimension) and tries it on problems whose exact answers are known from earlier chapters, so that its accuracy can be judged. It rests on the expansion of a state in energy eigenstates ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]]) and on level A's reading of $\langle H\rangle$ as the average of energy measurements ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^pr-a4-3-3|Principle §A4.3.3]]). The applications that need it are in [[§B9.2★ Helium and the Hydrogen Molecule]].

## The principle

> [!definition] Definition §B9.1.1: Trial Function; Variational Parameter
> A **trial function** is a square-integrable function $\psi$, in the domain of the Hamiltonian $H$ and obeying its boundary conditions, used to estimate the ground-state energy $E_{\text{gs}}$ (the smallest eigenvalue of $H$). It usually depends on adjustable **variational parameters** $b_1, b_2, \dots$, and the estimate is the **Rayleigh quotient**
>
> $$
> \langle H\rangle(b_1, b_2, \dots) = \frac{\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle},
> $$
>
> which is just $\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle$ when $\psi$ is normalized.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.1, eq. (8.1), Example 8.1, Problem 8.30, eq. (8.89)*

^def-b9-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.1: The Variational Principle
> For every normalized trial function $\psi$,
>
> $$
> E_{\text{gs}} \le \langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle \equiv \langle H\rangle ,
> $$
>
> with equality exactly when $\psi$ is a ground state. Minimizing $\langle H\rangle$ over the parameters of a family of trial functions gives the best upper bound the family can give.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §8.1, eq. (8.1) and proof*

^thm-b9-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Expand $\psi$ in the orthonormal eigenfunctions of $H$, $H\psi_n = E_n\psi_n$, which are complete ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]]): $\psi = \sum_nc_n\psi_n$. Normalization gives
>
> $$
> 1 = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \sum_m\sum_nc_m^*c_n\langle\psi_m|\psi_n\rangle = \sum_n|c_n|^2,
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> \langle H\rangle = \sum_m\sum_nc_m^*c_nE_n\langle\psi_m|\psi_n\rangle = \sum_nE_n|c_n|^2 \ \ge\ E_{\text{gs}}\sum_n|c_n|^2 = E_{\text{gs}},
> $$
>
> since every $E_n \ge E_{\text{gs}}$. Equality requires $c_n = 0$ whenever $E_n > E_{\text{gs}}$, i.e. $\psi$ in the ground eigenspace. If $H$ also has scattering states, the sum includes an integral over the continuous spectrum, whose energies also lie above $E_{\text{gs}}$, and the argument is unchanged (Griffiths footnote 1): hydrogen's bound states alone are not complete ([[§37 Bound States Need Not Be Complete꞉ Hydrogen#^cor-37-2|556 Cor. §37.2]]), but bound and scattering states together are.

^der-b9-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^def-b9-1-1|Def. §B9.1.1]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]], [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why the bound is better than the guess
> - *Physically.* $\psi$ could be the state of a real system; an energy measurement then yields one of the eigenvalues, never less than $E_{\text{gs}}$, so their average $\langle H\rangle$ cannot be less either ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^pr-a4-3-3|Principle §A4.3.3]]).
> - *Quantitatively.* If $\psi = (\psi_{\text{gs}} + \epsilon\chi)/\sqrt{1 + \epsilon^2}$ with $\chi$ normalized and orthogonal to $\psi_{\text{gs}}$, the proof gives $\langle H\rangle - E_{\text{gs}} = \epsilon^2\bigl(\langle\chi|H|\chi\rangle - E_{\text{gs}}\bigr)/(1 + \epsilon^2)$: an error of order $\epsilon$ in the wave function costs only $\epsilon^2$ in the energy. That is why crude trial functions often give "miraculously accurate" energies (Griffiths), while the same functions may predict other quantities badly.
> - *The limitation.* One never knows how close the bound is; one knows only that it is a bound. And as stated, the method addresses the ground state only (but see Theorem §B9.1.2).

^rem-b9-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: What counts as a trial function
> The trial function must be allowed by the problem: square-integrable, and satisfying the boundary conditions; a Gaussian in an infinite square well leaks into the region $V = \infty$ and gives $\langle H\rangle = \infty$, a useless bound (Griffiths footnote 3). Kinks are allowed, discontinuities are not: at a jump $\psi'$ contains a delta function, and $\langle T\rangle = (\hbar^2/2m)\int|\psi'|^2\,dx$ is infinite. At a kink, either keep the delta functions that $\psi''$ produces, or, more safely, use the Hermiticity of $\hat p$ to write $\langle T\rangle = \langle\hat p\psi|\hat p\psi\rangle/2m = (\hbar^2/2m)\int|\psi'|^2\,dx$, which needs only first derivatives (Example §B9.1.2).

^cau-b9-1-1

## Corollaries

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.2: Bound on an Excited State
> If the trial function is orthogonal to the exact ground state, $\langle\psi|\psi_{\text{gs}}\rangle = 0$, then $\langle H\rangle \ge E_{\text{fe}}$, the energy of the first excited state. More generally, if a Hermitian operator $A$ commutes with $H$, the (nondegenerate) ground state is an eigenstate of $A$, and any trial function that is an eigenstate of $A$ with a different eigenvalue is automatically orthogonal to it; for an even potential $V(x)$ every odd trial function bounds the first excited state.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 8.4(a) and footnote 6*

^thm-b9-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the expansion of Theorem §B9.1.1, orthogonality to the ground state means $c_{\text{gs}} = 0$, so $\langle H\rangle = \sum_{n\neq\text{gs}}E_n|c_n|^2 \ge E_{\text{fe}}\sum_{n\neq\text{gs}}|c_n|^2 = E_{\text{fe}}$. For the second statement, $[A, H] = 0$ makes the nondegenerate ground state an eigenstate of $A$ (its eigenspace of $H$ is one-dimensional and invariant under $A$: [[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-75|LADR Thm. 5.75]]), and eigenfunctions of a Hermitian operator with different eigenvalues are orthogonal ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]). For even $V$, take $A$ = parity: the bound states have definite parity ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]]) and the ground state has no node ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]]), so it is even, and an odd function has the other eigenvalue.

*Uses:* [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], [[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-75|LADR Thm. 5.75]], [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]], [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.3: Linear Trial Functions (Rayleigh–Ritz)
> Take as trial function a linear combination $\psi = \sum_{n=1}^Nc_n\phi_n$ of $N$ fixed orthonormal functions, with the coefficients as variational parameters. The Rayleigh quotient is stationary exactly when $(c_1, \dots, c_N)$ is an eigenvector of the $N\times N$ Hermitian matrix $H_{mn} = \langle\phi_m|H|\phi_n\rangle$,
>
> $$
> \sum_nH_{jn}c_n = \varepsilon\,c_j ,
> $$
>
> and its minimum is the smallest eigenvalue of this matrix, which is therefore an upper bound on $E_{\text{gs}}$; the corresponding eigenvector is the best trial function of this form.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 8.30, eqs. (8.88)–(8.91), footnote 26*

^thm-b9-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $\langle\phi_m|\phi_n\rangle = \delta_{mn}$ the Rayleigh quotient is $\varepsilon = \sum_{mn}c_m^*H_{mn}c_n\big/\sum_n|c_n|^2$. Treat $c_j$ and $c_j^*$ as independent variables (equivalent to varying real and imaginary parts; Griffiths footnote 26). Setting $\partial\varepsilon/\partial c_j^* = 0$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\sum_nH_{jn}c_n}{\sum_n|c_n|^2} - \frac{\sum_{mn}c_m^*H_{mn}c_n}{\bigl(\sum_n|c_n|^2\bigr)^2}\,c_j = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_nH_{jn}c_n = \varepsilon\,c_j .
> $$
>
> ($\partial/\partial c_j$ gives the complex conjugate equation, nothing new.) So the stationary points are the eigenvectors and the stationary values the eigenvalues. $H_{mn}$ is Hermitian, so it has an orthonormal eigenbasis with real eigenvalues ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]); expanding $c$ in it, as in Theorem §B9.1.1, shows $\varepsilon \ge \varepsilon_{\min}$, with equality at the lowest eigenvector. Theorem §B9.1.1 gives $\varepsilon_{\min} \ge E_{\text{gs}}$.

^der-b9-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]], [[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]

> [!remark] Remark: The matrix method in practice
> Theorem §B9.1.3 turns the minimization, a hard nonlinear problem, into the diagonalization of a matrix, which computers do well; it is how physical chemists treat molecules with many parameters (Griffiths §8.1). Enlarging the basis can only lower the bound, since the old trial functions are among the new ones. Griffiths' Problem 8.30 applies it to an infinite well with a sloping floor, using the first ten box states as the $\phi_n$.

^rem-b9-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.4: First-Order Perturbation Theory Never Underestimates the Ground State
> If the ground state of $H^0$ is nondegenerate, then for $H = H^0 + H'$
>
> $$
> E_{\text{gs}} \le E_{\text{gs}}^0 + E_{\text{gs}}^1 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 8.6(a)*

^thm-b9-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Use the unperturbed ground state $\psi_{\text{gs}}^0$ as the trial function: $\langle\psi_{\text{gs}}^0|H^0 + H'|\psi_{\text{gs}}^0\rangle = E_{\text{gs}}^0 + \langle\psi_{\text{gs}}^0|H'|\psi_{\text{gs}}^0\rangle$, which is $E_{\text{gs}}^0 + E_{\text{gs}}^1$ by [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]]. Theorem §B9.1.1 bounds it below by $E_{\text{gs}}$. Consistently, the second-order correction to the ground state is never positive ([[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^rem-b8-1-4|★ Remark: Second-order energies]]). If the unperturbed ground level is degenerate, the same argument with the good state of lowest $E^1$ ([[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]]) as trial function gives $E_{\text{gs}} \le E_{\text{gs}}^0 + \min E^1$.

^der-b9-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]], [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]], [[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.5: Every One-Dimensional Well Has a Bound State
> (a) If a potential $V_1(x)$, tending to $0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$, has at least one bound state, then every potential $V_2(x) \le V_1(x)$ that also tends to $0$ at infinity has at least one bound state. (b) Hence every one-dimensional potential well (a potential with $V(x) \le 0$ everywhere, $V \to 0$ at $\pm\infty$, and $V \le -V_0 < 0$ on some interval) has at least one bound state, however shallow.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 8.29, footnotes 24–25*

^thm-b9-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (a) Let $\psi_1$ be the ground state of $H_1 = T + V_1$, with energy $E_1 < 0$ (a bound state lies below the value $0$ of the potential at infinity). Use it as the trial function for $H_2 = T + V_2$:
>
> $$
> \langle\psi_1|H_2|\psi_1\rangle = \langle T\rangle + \int V_2|\psi_1|^2\,dx \le \langle T\rangle + \int V_1|\psi_1|^2\,dx = E_1 < 0 .
> $$
>
> By Theorem §B9.1.1, $H_2$ has spectrum below $0$. Since $V_2 \to 0$ at infinity, every energy below $0$ belongs to a normalizable state (the scattering states start at $0$), so $H_2$ has a bound state. (b) Let $V_1$ be the finite square well of depth $V_0$ on the interval where $V \le -V_0$. It always has at least one bound state ([[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^thm-a4-4-4|Theorem §A4.4.4]], item 2), and $V \le V_1$ everywhere because $V \le 0$ outside the interval. Apply (a). Part (a) holds in two and three dimensions as well, with the same proof; (b) fails in three dimensions, because the finite spherical well needs a minimum depth to bind (Griffiths Problem 8.29(c); [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^rem-b5-3-4|★ Remark: A shallow spherical well need not bind]]).

^der-b9-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]], [[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^thm-a4-4-4|Theorem §A4.4.4]]

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B9.1.1: A Gaussian Trial Function for the Delta Well
> For the delta-function well $H = -(\hbar^2/2m)\,d^2/dx^2 - \alpha\delta(x)$ the exact ground-state energy is $E_{\text{gs}} = -m\alpha^2/2\hbar^2$ ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]]). Try the Gaussian $\psi = Ae^{-bx^2}$, normalized by $A = (2b/\pi)^{1/4}$ (the Gaussian integral, [[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^ex-25-4|452 Ex. §25.4]]). Then $\langle T\rangle = \hbar^2b/2m$ and $\langle V\rangle = -\alpha|A|^2 = -\alpha\sqrt{2b/\pi}$, so
>
> $$
> \langle H\rangle(b) = \frac{\hbar^2b}{2m} - \alpha\sqrt{\frac{2b}{\pi}}, \qquad \frac{d\langle H\rangle}{db} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} - \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{2\pi b}} = 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ b = \frac{2m^2\alpha^2}{\pi\hbar^4},
> $$
>
> and $\langle H\rangle_{\min} = -m\alpha^2/\pi\hbar^2$. Since $\pi > 2$ this lies above the exact value, as it must; the error is $1 - 2/\pi = 36\%$, because the Gaussian is smooth where the true $e^{-\kappa|x|}$ has a cusp. For contrast, the same Gaussian for the oscillator $\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$ gives $\langle H\rangle = \hbar^2b/2m + m\omega^2/8b$, minimized at $b = m\omega/2\hbar$ with $\langle H\rangle_{\min} = \tfrac12\hbar\omega$: exact, because the oscillator's ground state is a Gaussian ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-4|Theorem §A4.5.4]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Examples 8.1–8.2, eqs. (8.2)–(8.9)*

> [!example] Example §B9.1.2: A Triangular Trial Function for the Infinite Well
> For the infinite well on $0 < x < a$ take the tent $\psi = Ax$ for $0 \le x \le a/2$, $\psi = A(a - x)$ for $a/2 \le x \le a$, zero outside. It vanishes at the walls, as required, and has kinks at $0$, $a/2$, $a$. Normalization: $|A|^2\bigl[\int_0^{a/2}x^2\,dx + \int_{a/2}^a(a - x)^2\,dx\bigr] = |A|^2a^3/12 = 1$, so $A = (2/a)\sqrt{3/a}$. Inside the well $H = p^2/2m$; with the Caution's first-derivative form, $\psi' = \pm A$ on the two halves and
>
> $$
> \langle H\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int_0^a|\psi'|^2\,dx = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}A^2a = \frac{12\,\hbar^2}{2ma^2} .
> $$
>
> (Keeping the kinks instead, $\psi'' = A\delta(x) - 2A\delta(x - a/2) + A\delta(x - a)$, and $-(\hbar^2/2m)\int\psi\psi''\,dx = (\hbar^2A/m)\psi(a/2) = \hbar^2A^2a/2m$: the same.) The exact value is $\pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2$ ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]), and $12 > \pi^2 = 9.87$: the bound is $22\%$ high with no adjustable parameter at all.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 8.3, eqs. (8.10)–(8.14)*

^ex-b9-1-2

> [!example] Example §B9.1.3: A Gaussian Trial Function for Hydrogen
> Try $\psi(\mathbf r) = Ae^{-br^2}$ for the hydrogen Hamiltonian. Normalization gives $A = (2b/\pi)^{3/4}$; the kinetic energy is three copies of the one-dimensional one, $\langle T\rangle = 3\hbar^2b/2m$; and
>
> $$
> \Bigl\langle\frac1r\Bigr\rangle = 4\pi|A|^2\int_0^\infty re^{-2br^2}\,dr = \frac{\pi}{b}\Bigl(\frac{2b}{\pi}\Bigr)^{3/2} = 2\sqrt{\frac{2b}{\pi}} .
> $$
>
> So $\langle H\rangle = 3\hbar^2b/2m - (e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0)\,2\sqrt{2b/\pi}$. Setting $d\langle H\rangle/db = 0$ gives $\sqrt b = \frac{2}{3a}\sqrt{2/\pi}$, i.e. $b = 8/(9\pi a^2)$, where at the minimum the potential term equals $-2\times$ the kinetic one, so
>
> $$
> \langle H\rangle_{\min} = -\frac{3\hbar^2b}{2m} = -\frac{8}{3\pi}\,\frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2} = -\frac{8}{3\pi}\,(13.6\ \mathrm{eV}) = -11.5\ \mathrm{eV},
> $$
>
> $15\%$ above the true $-13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$ ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-7|Theorem §A5.1.7]]). The Gaussian misses the cusp of $e^{-r/a}$ at the nucleus and falls off too fast at large $r$; the trial function $e^{-br}$ would have found the exact answer.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 8.19 (answer $-11.5$ eV)*
