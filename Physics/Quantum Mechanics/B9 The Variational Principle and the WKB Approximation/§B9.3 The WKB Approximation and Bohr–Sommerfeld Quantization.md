Classical Mechanics ended with a bridge: writing the wave function as $e^{A + iS/\hbar}$, the Schrödinger equation becomes the Hamilton–Jacobi equation for the phase $S$ plus a continuity equation, up to terms of order $\hbar^2$ ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^rem-b8-3-4|CM Remark: The classical limit of the Schrödinger equation]]). The WKB approximation (Wentzel, Kramers, Brillouin) turns that remark into a working method for one-dimensional stationary states: where the potential changes little over a de Broglie wavelength ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]]), $\psi$ is a wave whose local wavelength is $h/p(x)$ and whose amplitude is $1/\sqrt{p(x)}$, with $p(x)$ the classical momentum. Three results follow: the probability density of a highly excited state is the classical one, which level A observed for the oscillator ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-1|Theorem §A4.5.1]]); the energies of any well follow from one integral, the **Bohr–Sommerfeld rule**, the quantum condition of the old quantum theory behind Bohr's orbits ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^mod-a2-2-1|Model §A2.2.1]]), now derived and corrected by $\tfrac12$ for motion between turning points; and the level spacing tends to $\hbar$ times the classical frequency, the correspondence principle ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^pr-a2-2-6|Principle §A2.2.6]]) derived. Tunnelling and the matching at turning points are [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas|§B9.4]].

*Notation.* Griffiths' $p(x)$ for the local momentum; the user's WKB notes and Zwiebach use the local wave number $k(x) = p(x)/\hbar$. In forbidden regions the user's notes write $\kappa(x)$, discussed in [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^cau-b9-4-1|§B9.4]].

## The classical region

> [!definition] Definition §B9.3.1: Local Momentum; Classical and Forbidden Regions; Turning Point
> For a particle of energy $E$ in a potential $V(x)$, the **local (classical) momentum** is
>
> $$
> p(x) = \sqrt{2m\bigl[E - V(x)\bigr]} ,
> $$
>
> the magnitude of the momentum a classical particle of energy $E$ would have at $x$. Where $E > V(x)$, $p$ is real: the **classical** (allowed) **region**. Where $E < V(x)$ the region is classically **forbidden**. A **turning point** is a point $x_t$ with $V(x_t) = E$; classically the particle reverses there ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^def-a4-7-1|CM Def. §A4.7.1]]). The **local de Broglie wavelength** is $\lambda(x) = 2\pi\hbar/p(x) = h/p(x)$, and the time-independent Schrödinger equation reads
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = -\frac{p(x)^2}{\hbar^2}\,\psi .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.1, eqs. (9.1)–(9.2), Fig. 9.1 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 12, §2, eq. (2.3), Fig. 4 · the user's WKB notes, §1.1*

^def-b9-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.3.1: The WKB Wave Function in a Classical Region
> In a classical region where $V$ varies slowly, in the sense that $p$ changes by a small fraction over a local wavelength,
>
> $$
> \frac{\hbar\,|p'|}{p^2} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\Bigl|\frac{d\lambda}{dx}\Bigr| \ll 1 \qquad\text{(and } \hbar^2|p''| \ll p^3\text{)},
> $$
>
> the time-independent Schrödinger equation has the approximate solutions
>
> $$
> \psi(x) \approx \frac{C}{\sqrt{p(x)}}\;e^{\pm\frac{i}{\hbar}\int p(x)\,dx} ,
> $$
>
> and the general solution is a combination of the two signs, in real form
>
> $$
> \psi(x) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{p(x)}}\Bigl[C_1\sin\phi(x) + C_2\cos\phi(x)\Bigr], \qquad \phi(x) = \frac1\hbar\int_{x_0}^x p(x')\,dx' .
> $$
>
> The wave oscillates with the local wavelength $h/p(x)$, and its amplitude, and $|\psi|^2 \approx |C|^2/p(x)$, is largest where the particle is slowest. The approximation fails near turning points, where $p \to 0$ and $\lambda \to \infty$. Since $p' = -mV'/p$, the condition says that the change of $V$ over a reduced wavelength $\lambda/2\pi$ is small compared with $2(E - V)$, twice the kinetic energy.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.1, eqs. (9.3)–(9.15) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 12, §2, eqs. (2.3)–(2.8) · the user's WKB notes, §1.2–1.3*

^thm-b9-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Exact amplitude and phase.* Write $\psi = A\,e^{i\phi}$ with $A(x) > 0$ and $\phi(x)$ real. Then $\psi'' = \bigl[A'' + 2iA'\phi' + iA\phi'' - A(\phi')^2\bigr]e^{i\phi}$, and $\psi'' = -(p^2/\hbar^2)\psi$ splits into its real and imaginary parts,
>
> $$
> A'' - A(\phi')^2 = -\frac{p^2}{\hbar^2}A, \qquad 2A'\phi' + A\phi'' = 0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \bigl(A^2\phi'\bigr)' = 0 .
> $$
>
> Both are exact. The second integrates to $A^2\phi' = C^2$, i.e. $A = C/\sqrt{|\phi'|}$.
>
> *The approximation.* Assume the amplitude varies slowly, $|A''/A| \ll (\phi')^2,\ p^2/\hbar^2$, and drop $A''$ from the first equation: $(\phi')^2 = p^2/\hbar^2$, so $\phi = \pm\frac1\hbar\int p\,dx$ and $A = C/\sqrt{p}$ (absorbing $\sqrt\hbar$ and the constant of integration into $C$, which becomes complex). The two signs give the two solutions; with $C_\pm$ combined, $e^{\pm i\phi}$ become $\sin\phi$ and $\cos\phi$.
>
> *When is it justified?* With $A \propto p^{-1/2}$: $A'/A = -p'/2p$ and $A''/A = \tfrac34(p'/p)^2 - \tfrac12p''/p$. The neglected term is small if $\hbar^2|A''/A| \ll p^2$, i.e. $\tfrac34(\hbar p'/p^2)^2 + \tfrac12\hbar^2|p''|/p^3 \ll 1$, which is the stated condition. Since $\lambda = 2\pi\hbar/p$, $d\lambda/dx = -2\pi\hbar p'/p^2$; and with $p' = -mV'/p$, $\hbar|p'|/p^2 = (\lambda/2\pi)\,|V'|/2(E - V)$.

*Uses:* [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^def-b9-3-1|Def. §B9.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The expansion in powers of ħ, and the classical action
> Griffiths' Problem 9.2 and the user's WKB notes (§1.2) reach the same result by writing $\psi = e^{if(x)/\hbar}$ with complex $f$, which is no restriction. The Schrödinger equation becomes $i\hbar f'' - (f')^2 + p^2 = 0$. Expanding $f = f_0 + \hbar f_1 + \hbar^2f_2 + \cdots$ and collecting powers of $\hbar$:
>
> $$
> (f_0')^2 = p^2, \qquad i f_0'' = 2f_0'f_1', \qquad i f_1'' = 2f_0'f_2' + (f_1')^2, \ \dots
> $$
>
> The first gives $f_0 = \pm\int p\,dx$; the second $f_1' = \tfrac{i}{2}\,p'/p$, $f_1 = \tfrac i2\ln p$, so $e^{if_1} = p^{-1/2}$: to first order in $\hbar$, $\psi \approx p^{-1/2}e^{\pm\frac i\hbar\int p\,dx}$. The third equation measures the error, of relative size $\hbar|p'|/p^2$, the parameter of Theorem §B9.3.1.
> - *Classical meaning.* $f_0 = \pm\int p\,dx$ is Hamilton's characteristic function $W(x; E)$ ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-3|CM Theorem §B8.3.3]]). With the time factor, $\Psi \approx p^{-1/2}e^{i(W - Et)/\hbar} = p^{-1/2}e^{iS/\hbar}$ with $S = W - Et$ the solution of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation obtained by separating the time ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-1|CM Theorem §B8.3.1]]; the user's Classical notes, §4.2, steps 3–4). The phase of the WKB wave is the classical action in units of $\hbar$, and the classical limit is $\hbar \to 0$ at fixed action.
> - *The amplitude is conservation of probability.* The exact equation $(A^2\phi')' = 0$ says that the probability current $J = (\hbar/m)\,A^2\phi'$ ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]], with $S = \hbar\phi$) is constant, as it must be for a stationary state. With $\phi' = p/\hbar$ it reads $|\psi|^2\,v = $ const, $v = p/m$: the one-dimensional, stationary form of the continuity equation in [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^rem-b8-3-4|CM Remark: The classical limit of the Schrödinger equation]]. The condition for dropping $A''$ is the condition stated there for neglecting the $\hbar^2$ terms.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 9.2 · the user's WKB notes, §1.2 · the user's Classical notes, §4*

^rem-b9-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Slowly varying compared with what
> The validity condition compares the change of $V$ over a wavelength with the **kinetic** energy $E - V$, not with $V$ itself. Zwiebach (Lecture 12, eq. (2.5)) writes it as $\lambda\,|dV/dx| \ll |V(x)|$; since adding a constant to $V$ changes nothing physical, the shift-invariant form $\lambda\,|dV/dx| \ll E - V$ of Theorem §B9.3.1 is the one to use. It fails in two places: at turning points, where $E - V \to 0$ ([[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas|§B9.4]] bridges them), and at abrupt steps of $V$, where $V'$ is infinite.

^cau-b9-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.3.2: WKB Density Equals the Classical Density
> Let $\psi$ be a bound state in a well with turning points $x_1 < x_2$, in the WKB regime (many wavelengths between them). Averaged over a few local wavelengths, its probability density between the turning points is
>
> $$
> \overline{|\psi(x)|^2} \approx \frac{2}{T\,v(x)} = P_{\text{cl}}(x), \qquad v(x) = \frac{p(x)}{m}, \qquad T = 2\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{dx}{v(x)} ,
> $$
>
> the probability density of a classical particle of energy $E$ observed at a random time, with $T$ its period. This extends [[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-1|Theorem §A4.5.1]] from the oscillator to any well, and proves the large-$n$ correspondence that level A saw in a figure ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^rem-a4-5-5|Remark: Correspondence at large n]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.1, eq. (9.11) and the discussion following it · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 12, §2, eqs. (2.6)–(2.12)*

^thm-b9-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A bound state can be taken real ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]]), so between the turning points $\psi \approx (2D/\sqrt p)\sin\theta(x)$ with real $D$ and a phase $\theta$ that runs through many multiples of $\pi$ (Theorem §B9.3.1 with $C_1, C_2$ real). Averaging $\sin^2\theta$ over a few oscillations gives $\tfrac12$, so $\overline{|\psi|^2} = 2D^2/p$. Outside the turning points $\psi$ decays within about a wavelength ([[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^thm-b9-4-1|Theorem §B9.4.1]]), a negligible fraction of the probability for highly excited states. Normalization then reads $2D^2\int_{x_1}^{x_2}dx/p = 1$, and $\int_{x_1}^{x_2}dx/p = \frac1m\int_{x_1}^{x_2}dx/v = T/2m$, since the particle crosses from $x_1$ to $x_2$ in half a period ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-2|CM Theorem §B7.2.2]] (3)). So $2D^2 = 2m/T$ and $\overline{|\psi|^2} = 2m/(Tp) = 2/(Tv)$. Classically, the fraction of time spent in $dx$ is $(dx/v)/(T/2)$. For the oscillator, $T = 2\pi/\omega$ and $v = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$ give $1/\pi\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$, Theorem §A4.5.1.

## Quantization

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.3.3: Quantization in a Well with Vertical Walls
> Let $V = \infty$ outside $0 < x < a$ and $E > V(x)$ inside. In the WKB approximation the allowed energies satisfy
>
> $$
> \int_0^a p(x)\,dx = n\pi\hbar, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
> $$
>
> For a flat bottom this is exact and gives the infinite-well energies $E_n = n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2$ ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 9.1, eqs. (9.12)–(9.17)*

^thm-b9-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Inside, $\psi \approx p^{-1/2}[C_1\sin\phi + C_2\cos\phi]$ with $\phi(x) = \frac1\hbar\int_0^xp\,dx'$ (Theorem §B9.3.1; the lower limit is a convenient choice of the constant). The walls require $\psi(0) = \psi(a) = 0$ ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]). Since $\phi(0) = 0$, $\psi(0) = 0$ forces $C_2 = 0$; then $\psi(a) = 0$ with $C_1 \ne 0$ forces $\sin\phi(a) = 0$, $\phi(a) = n\pi$, and $n \ge 1$ because $\phi(a) > 0$. For $V = 0$ inside, $p = \sqrt{2mE}$ is constant, $pa = n\pi\hbar$ gives $E_n$, and the amplitude is constant, so dropping $A''$ cost nothing: the WKB solution is the exact one.

*Uses:* [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-1|Theorem §B9.3.1]], [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.3.4: Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization
> Let $x_1 < x_2$ be the turning points of a well, with $V$ approximately linear near each of them over many local wavelengths (no vertical walls). In the WKB approximation the allowed energies satisfy
>
> $$
> \int_{x_1}^{x_2}p(x)\,dx = \Bigl(n - \frac12\Bigr)\pi\hbar, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \oint p\,dx = 2\pi\hbar\Bigl(n + \frac12\Bigr), \quad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots,
> $$
>
> where $\oint$ is taken over a full classical period ($x_1 \to x_2 \to x_1$, so $\oint = 2\int_{x_1}^{x_2}$). With one vertical wall at $x = 0$ and a sloping turning point at $x_2$,
>
> $$
> \int_0^{x_2}p(x)\,dx = \Bigl(n - \frac14\Bigr)\pi\hbar, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
> $$
>
> The three rules (two, one or no vertical walls) differ only in the number subtracted from $n$: $0$, $\tfrac14$ or $\tfrac12$. They are most accurate in the semiclassical regime of large $n$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.3, Examples 9.3–9.4, eqs. (9.48), (9.52) · PHY 308, HW 1, Problem 1, eq. (1) (the form $\oint p\,dq = 2\pi\hbar(n + \frac12)$)*

^thm-b9-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The input beyond Theorem §B9.3.1 is the behaviour at a sloping turning point, derived by patching with Airy functions in [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^thm-b9-4-5|Theorem §B9.4.5]]: a solution that decays into the forbidden region beyond an upward-sloping turning point $x_2$ is, inside,
>
> $$
> \psi \approx \frac{2D}{\sqrt p}\,\sin\theta_2(x), \qquad \theta_2(x) = \frac1\hbar\int_x^{x_2}p\,dx' + \frac\pi4 ,
> $$
>
> and one that decays beyond a downward-sloping turning point $x_1$ is $\psi \approx (-2D'/\sqrt p)\sin\theta_1(x)$ with $\theta_1(x) = -\frac1\hbar\int_{x_1}^xp\,dx' - \frac\pi4$.
>
> *Two sloping walls.* A bound state decays on both sides, so both forms hold between $x_1$ and $x_2$. Two functions $c_2\sin\theta_2$ and $c_1\sin\theta_1$ with $\theta_2' = \theta_1' = -p/\hbar$ coincide for all $x$ only if $\theta_2 - \theta_1$, which is constant, is a multiple of $\pi$ (a sign is absorbed into $D$; Griffiths' footnote 13). Here $\theta_2 - \theta_1 = \frac1\hbar\int_{x_1}^{x_2}p\,dx + \frac\pi2 = n\pi$, which is the rule; $n \ge 1$ because the integral is positive. Doubling and writing $n = n' + 1$ gives $\oint p\,dx = 2\pi\hbar(n' + \tfrac12)$ with $n' \ge 0$.
>
> *One vertical wall.* $\psi(0) = 0$ requires $\sin\theta_2(0) = 0$: $\frac1\hbar\int_0^{x_2}p\,dx + \frac\pi4 = n\pi$.

*Uses:* [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-1|Theorem §B9.3.1]], [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^thm-b9-4-5|Theorem §B9.4.5]], [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: One state per cell of phase space, and the correspondence principle
> $\oint p\,dx$ is the area $\mathcal A(E)$ enclosed by the classical orbit of energy $E$ in the phase plane ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-2|CM Theorem §B7.2.2]]). The Bohr–Sommerfeld rule says $\mathcal A(E_n) = (n + \tfrac12)h$: the orbits of successive states enclose areas that differ by exactly $h = 2\pi\hbar$, so each state occupies one cell of area $h$ in phase space. That is the counting rule statistical mechanics assumes ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^rem-b6-1-1|TH Remark: Cells and shells]]), here derived for one degree of freedom.
>
> The level spacing follows from the period. Since $T(E) = d\mathcal A/dE$ (CM Theorem §B7.2.2 (3)), $\mathcal A(E_{n+1}) - \mathcal A(E_n) = h$ gives, for slowly varying $T$,
>
> $$
> E_{n+1} - E_n \approx \frac{h}{T(E_n)} = \hbar\,\omega_{\text{cl}}(E_n), \qquad \omega_{\text{cl}} = \frac{2\pi}{T} :
> $$
>
> neighbouring levels are separated by $\hbar$ times the classical angular frequency at that energy, so the photon emitted in $n \to n - 1$ has the classical frequency of the motion. This is Bohr's correspondence principle ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^pr-a2-2-6|Principle §A2.2.6]]), which level A checked for hydrogen's orbits ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-7|Theorem §A2.2.7]]), now a consequence of the Schrödinger equation for any one-dimensional well. For the oscillator $T$ does not depend on $E$, and the spacing is exactly $\hbar\omega$ (Example §B9.3.1).

^rem-b9-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: The old quantum theory, corrected
> Before the Schrödinger equation, the rule $\oint p\,dq = nh$ (without the $\tfrac12$) was imposed on each separable coordinate of a classical orbit. For a circular orbit, $\oint p_\varphi\,d\varphi = 2\pi L$, and $2\pi L = nh$ is Bohr's $L = n\hbar$ ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^mod-a2-2-1|Model §A2.2.1]]; the user's Classical notes, §5), which de Broglie read as a whole number of wavelengths around the orbit ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^thm-a3-1-3|Theorem §A3.1.3]]). WKB explains both the rule and its correction. The phase $\frac1\hbar\oint p\,dx$ accumulated over one period must close up to a multiple of $2\pi$. A circular orbit has no turning points, so the condition is exactly $2\pi n$. A libration between two turning points picks up an extra $\pi/4$ at each end in the half-cycle condition, $\frac1\hbar\int_{x_1}^{x_2}p\,dx + \frac\pi4 + \frac\pi4 = n\pi$ (Theorem §B9.3.4), i.e. $\pi/2$ per turning point over a full cycle; this shifts $n$ to $n + \tfrac12$ and produces the zero-point energy that the old rule missed.
>
> *Source: the user's Classical notes, §5 · PHY 308, HW 1, Problem 1 · Griffiths §9.3, Example 9.4*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B9.3.1: The Harmonic Oscillator by Bohr–Sommerfeld
> Use $\oint p\,dq = 2\pi\hbar(n + \tfrac12)$ to find the energy levels of $V = \tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$.
>
> At energy $E$ the turning points are $\pm A$ with $A^2 = 2E/m\omega^2$, and $p(x) = \sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2x^2} = m\omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$. Over a full period,
>
> $$
> \oint p\,dx = 2\int_{-A}^{A}m\omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}\,dx = 2\,m\omega\,\frac{\pi A^2}{2} = \pi m\omega A^2 = \frac{2\pi E}{\omega} ,
> $$
>
> the area $\pi\,(m\omega A)\,A$ of the phase-space ellipse with semi-axes $A$ and $p_{\max} = m\omega A$ ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^rem-b4-1-1|Remark: The classical oscillator, for comparison]]). The rule gives $2\pi E/\omega = 2\pi\hbar(n + \tfrac12)$:
>
> $$
> E_n = \Bigl(n + \frac12\Bigr)\hbar\omega, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots,
> $$
>
> exactly the spectrum of [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]], zero-point energy included; the old rule $\oint p\,dq = nh$ would have given $n\hbar\omega$. The agreement for every $n$, even $n = 0$, is special to the oscillator (Griffiths Problem 9.8). For the half oscillator ($V = \infty$ for $x < 0$), the one-wall rule gives $\int_0^{A}m\omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}\,dx = \pi E/2\omega = (n - \tfrac14)\pi\hbar$, i.e. $E = (2n - \tfrac12)\hbar\omega = \tfrac32, \tfrac72, \tfrac{11}2, \dots$ in units of $\hbar\omega$, again exact ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^ex-b3-1-2|Example §B3.1.2]]; Griffiths Example 9.3).
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 1, Problem 1 (solved here) · Griffiths Problem 9.8; Example 9.3, eqs. (9.49)–(9.50)*

^ex-b9-3-1

> [!example] Example §B9.3.2: A Linear Potential with a Wall
> A particle moves in $V(x) = Ax$ ($A > 0$) for $x > 0$, with an impenetrable wall at $x = 0$: a ball bouncing on a floor ($A = mg$; Griffiths Problems 9.6–9.7), or the $s$-states of the linear central potential $V = Ar$ of PHY 308's HW 5.5 ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^mod-b5-3-4|Model §B5.3.4]]). Find the WKB energies and compare with the exact ones.
>
> One vertical wall and one sloping turning point $x_2 = E/A$ (Theorem §B9.3.4):
>
> $$
> \int_0^{E/A}\sqrt{2m(E - Ax)}\,dx = \frac{\sqrt{2m}}{A}\cdot\frac23E^{3/2} = \Bigl(n - \frac14\Bigr)\pi\hbar
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> E_n = \Bigl[\frac{3\pi}{2}\Bigl(n - \frac14\Bigr)\Bigr]^{2/3}\Bigl(\frac{\hbar^2A^2}{2m}\Bigr)^{1/3} .
> $$
>
> The exact energies are $a_n\,(\hbar^2A^2/2m)^{1/3}$ with $-a_n$ the zeros of the Airy function ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]). In units of $(\hbar^2A^2/2m)^{1/3}$:
>
> | $n$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | WKB $[\frac{3\pi}{2}(n - \frac14)]^{2/3}$ | 2.320 | 4.082 | 5.517 | 6.785 |
> | exact $a_n$ | 2.338 | 4.088 | 5.521 | 6.787 |
> | error | $-0.8\%$ | $-0.15\%$ | $-0.06\%$ | $-0.03\%$ |
>
> Even the ground state is within $1\%$, and the error falls rapidly with $n$, as expected of a semiclassical approximation. The exponent $\tfrac23$ reflects the linear potential: $\int p\,dx \propto E^{3/2}$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 9.6–9.7 · PHY 308, HW 5, Problem 5 (the exact values, via §B5.3); WKB values computed here*

> [!example]- ★ Example §B9.3.3: An Infinite Well with a Shelf
> Inside the infinite well of width $a$, let $V = V_0$ on $0 < x < a/2$ and $V = 0$ on $a/2 < x < a$, with $V_0$ below the energies considered. Find the WKB energies and compare with first-order perturbation theory.
>
> With two vertical walls (Theorem §B9.3.3), $\int_0^ap\,dx = \tfrac a2\bigl[\sqrt{2m(E - V_0)} + \sqrt{2mE}\bigr] = n\pi\hbar$. With $E_n^0 = (n\pi\hbar)^2/2ma^2$, the unperturbed energies, this is $\sqrt{E - V_0} + \sqrt E = 2\sqrt{E_n^0}$. Squaring $\sqrt{E - V_0} = 2\sqrt{E_n^0} - \sqrt E$ gives $\sqrt E = (4E_n^0 + V_0)/4\sqrt{E_n^0}$, so
>
> $$
> E_n = \frac{(4E_n^0 + V_0)^2}{16E_n^0} = E_n^0 + \frac{V_0}{2} + \frac{V_0^2}{16E_n^0} .
> $$
>
> First-order perturbation theory gives $E_n^0 + \langle\psi_n^0|V|\psi_n^0\rangle = E_n^0 + V_0\cdot\frac2a\int_0^{a/2}\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,dx = E_n^0 + \frac{V_0}{2}$ ([[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]]). The two agree up to $V_0^2/16E_n^0$, which is negligible either when $V_0$ is small (the perturbative regime) or when $n$ is large (the semiclassical regime), since $E_n^0 \propto n^2$. The WKB phase simply adds across the step at $x = a/2$ and ignores the partial reflection there, which is why the method needs $E \gg V_0$ at an abrupt step.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 9.1 (solved here)*
