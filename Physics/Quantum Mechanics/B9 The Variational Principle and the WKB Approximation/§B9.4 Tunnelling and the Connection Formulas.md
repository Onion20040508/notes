Level A computed tunnelling for one barrier only, the rectangular one, where the transmission is dominated by $e^{-2\alpha L}$ ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-6|Theorem §A4.6.6]]), and described alpha decay as tunnelling through the Coulomb barrier in words ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-4|Remark: Alpha decay]], [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^rem-a7-2-4|Remark: Alpha decay is tunnelling]]), next to the empirical Geiger–Nuttall rule ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^law-a7-2-6|Law §A7.2.6]]). In a forbidden region the WKB wave function of [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization|§B9.3]] becomes a slowly varying exponential, which gives the transmission through any smooth barrier, $T \approx e^{-2\gamma}$ with $\gamma$ the action under the barrier. Applied to Gamow's model of the nucleus it turns the Geiger–Nuttall rule into a derived result. The second half of the section supplies what §B9.3 borrowed: near a turning point WKB fails, and the solution there is an Airy function; matching it to WKB on both sides gives the **connection formulas** and the phase $\pi/4$ behind the Bohr–Sommerfeld rule.

*Notation.* $|p(x)| = \sqrt{2m[V(x) - E]}$ in a forbidden region, $\gamma = \frac1\hbar\int|p|\,dx$ across it, as in Griffiths. The user's notes write $\kappa(x)$ (Caution below).

## The forbidden region and tunnelling

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.4.1: The WKB Wave Function in a Forbidden Region
> Where $E < V(x)$ and $V$ varies slowly on the scale $\hbar/|p|$, the time-independent Schrödinger equation has the approximate solutions
>
> $$
> \psi(x) \approx \frac{C}{\sqrt{|p(x)|}}\;e^{\pm\frac1\hbar\int|p(x)|\,dx}, \qquad |p(x)| = \sqrt{2m\bigl[V(x) - E\bigr]} :
> $$
>
> one growing and one decaying exponential, with slowly varying decay rate $|p(x)|/\hbar$ and amplitude.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.2, eq. (9.18), footnote 3 · the user's WKB notes, §1.3*

^thm-b9-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the forbidden region $p^2 = -|p|^2 < 0$. The expansion of [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^rem-b9-3-1|Remark: The expansion in powers of ħ]] did not use the sign of $p^2$: $(f_0')^2 = p^2 = -|p|^2$ gives $f_0' = \pm i|p|$, and $f_1 = \tfrac i2\ln f_0'$ gives the amplitude $|p|^{-1/2}$ up to a constant. So $e^{if/\hbar} \approx |p|^{-1/2}e^{\mp\frac1\hbar\int|p|\,dx}$. (Griffiths obtains it from [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-1|Theorem §B9.3.1]] by letting $p$ become imaginary; his footnote 3 notes that the amplitude–phase split is then sufficient but not forced, and points to the $\hbar$-expansion.) The validity condition is that of Theorem §B9.3.1 with $|p|$ for $p$.

> [!caution] Caution: κ in the user's WKB notes
> The user's WKB notes use $\kappa(x)$ for the decay constant with two normalizations. In §1.3, $\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V - E)}$, which is $|p|$, and the exponent is $\frac1\hbar\int\kappa\,dx$; in §3.2 and §4, $\kappa = \frac1\hbar\sqrt{2m(V - E)}$, which is $|p|/\hbar$, and the exponent is $\int\kappa\,dx$. Each formula is consistent with its own definition, so the tunnelling exponent is the same, $\gamma = \frac1\hbar\int|p|\,dx$; mixing the two definitions would lose or double a factor $\hbar$. Level A wrote $\alpha = |p|/\hbar$ for the rectangular barrier.

^cau-b9-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.4.2: Tunnelling Probability
> A particle of energy $E$ meets a barrier on which $V(x) > E$ for $x_1 < x < x_2$, with the same constant potential far to the left and far to the right. If the barrier is high or broad, so that $\gamma \gg 1$, the transmission probability is
>
> $$
> T \approx e^{-2\gamma}, \qquad \gamma = \frac1\hbar\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\bigl|p(x)\bigr|\,dx = \frac1\hbar\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\sqrt{2m\bigl[V(x) - E\bigr]}\,dx ,
> $$
>
> and $R = 1 - T \approx 1$. The exponent is reliable; prefactors of order one, which depend on the shape of the barrier near its edges, are not determined.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.2, eqs. (9.19)–(9.23), Fig. 9.4 · the user's WKB notes, §3, §5*

^thm-b9-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take Griffiths' barrier on $0 < x < a$ with $V = 0$ outside. To the left, $\psi = Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx}$ (incident and reflected), to the right $\psi = Fe^{ikx}$, $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$, and $T = |F|^2/|A|^2$ (the ratio of transmitted to incident probability currents, [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]], with equal speeds on both sides). Inside, by [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^thm-b9-4-1|Theorem §B9.4.1]],
>
> $$
> \psi(x) \approx \frac{C}{\sqrt{|p|}}\,e^{\frac1\hbar\int_0^x|p|\,dx'} + \frac{D}{\sqrt{|p|}}\,e^{-\frac1\hbar\int_0^x|p|\,dx'} .
> $$
>
> For a high or broad barrier the growing term must be small: its coefficient would have to vanish for an infinitely broad barrier, on which only a decaying solution is acceptable. The wave function then falls by the factor $e^{-\gamma}$ across the barrier, and matching at the two edges, which involves only factors of order one (powers of $|p|/\hbar k$), gives $|F|/|A| \sim e^{-\gamma}$, hence $T \approx e^{-2\gamma}$. Griffiths calls this a heuristic argument (footnote 4): the connection formulas at both edges make it precise and fix the prefactor (his Problem 9.11). For sloping walls the edges are the turning points $x_1, x_2$ where $V = E$; Griffiths notes (§9.3) that the result remains reasonably accurate there. Probability is conserved, so $R = 1 - T$.

^der-b9-4-2

*Uses:* [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^thm-b9-4-1|Theorem §B9.4.1]], [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What γ measures
> $\hbar\gamma$ is the classical action $\int|p|\,dx$ across the forbidden region, the imaginary-momentum counterpart of the phase $\frac1\hbar\int p\,dx$ in an allowed region. Level A's observations about the rectangular barrier ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-3|Remark: What controls tunnelling]]) hold for every barrier: $\gamma$ grows linearly with the width, only as the square root of the height above $E$ and of the mass, and as $1/\hbar$, so $T \to 0$ in the classical limit. Because $T$ is exponential in $\gamma$, small changes of the barrier change $T$ by large factors.

^rem-b9-4-1

> [!example] Example §B9.4.1: The Rectangular Barrier, WKB Versus Exact
> A barrier of height $V_0$ on $0 < x < L$, with $E < V_0$. Compare $T \approx e^{-2\gamma}$ with the exact result.
>
> Here $|p| = \sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}$ is constant, $\gamma = \kappa L$ with $\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}/\hbar$, and $T_{\text{WKB}} = e^{-2\kappa L}$. The exact transmission ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-6|Theorem §A4.6.6]], rederived for a barrier of width $2a$ in [[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]]) is
>
> $$
> T = \Bigl[1 + \frac{V_0^2\sinh^2\kappa L}{4E(V_0 - E)}\Bigr]^{-1} \;\approx\; \frac{16E(V_0 - E)}{V_0^2}\,e^{-2\kappa L} \qquad (\kappa L \gg 1) .
> $$
>
> The exponents agree; the prefactor $16\varepsilon(1 - \varepsilon)$, $\varepsilon = E/V_0$, lies between $0$ and $4$ and is invisible to the WKB estimate. For an electron with $E = 5$ eV, $V_0 = 10$ eV, $L = 0.5$ nm: $\kappa = 11.46\ \mathrm{nm}^{-1}$, $\kappa L = 5.73$, $e^{-2\kappa L} = 1.06\times10^{-5}$, while the exact $T = 4.24\times10^{-5}$, larger by exactly the prefactor $4$ (at $\varepsilon = \tfrac12$). Doubling $L$ to $1$ nm squares the exponential, $T = 4.5\times10^{-10}$: the exponent carries the physics, the prefactor only a factor of a few.
>
> The edges are not the smooth turning points that WKB assumes: there $V$ jumps from $0$ past $E$ to $V_0$, and the slowly-varying condition fails ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^cau-b9-3-1|Caution: Slowly varying compared with what]]). That is why only the exponent survives. The user's WKB notes (§4) describe $x_1 = 0$ and $x_2 = L$ as the turning points "since $E = V_0$ at those boundaries"; with $E < V_0$ the potential crosses $E$ there by a jump rather than equalling it, and the notes' own conclusion, agreement of the exponent "up to a prefactor" when $\kappa L$ is large, is the correct statement.
>
> *Source: the user's WKB notes, §4 · Griffiths Problem 9.3 (barrier of width $2a$ there) · the user's Potential solutions notes, §4–5; numbers computed here*

## Alpha decay

> [!model] Model §B9.4.3: Gamow's Model of Alpha Decay
> An alpha particle (charge $2e$, mass $m$) moves inside a daughter nucleus of charge $Ze$ and radius $r_1$, in the radial potential
>
> $$
> V(r) = \begin{cases} -V_0, & r < r_1 \quad\text{(nuclear binding, a finite well)},\\[2pt] \dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{2Ze^2}{r}, & r > r_1 \quad\text{(Coulomb repulsion)}. \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Assumptions: the alpha particle is preformed inside the nucleus with the energy $E$ it is emitted with, rattling back and forth with some speed $v$; the motion is treated as one-dimensional in $r$, without a centrifugal barrier, and the potential does not vanish on the inner side (Griffiths' footnote 7: really a three-dimensional problem, but the essential idea is the barrier integral); recoil of the daughter is neglected. The outer turning point $r_2$ is where the Coulomb energy equals $E$:
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\,\frac{2Ze^2}{r_2} = E .
> $$
>
> For the radius, Griffiths uses $r_1 \approx (1.07\ \mathrm{fm})\,A^{1/3}$, the charge radius ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-1|Law §A7.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 9.2, eq. (9.24), Fig. 9.5; Problem 9.4, eq. (9.30)*

^mod-b9-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.4.4: The Gamow Factor and the Alpha-Decay Lifetime
> In [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^mod-b9-4-3|Model §B9.4.3]] the tunnelling exponent is
>
> $$
> \gamma = \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}\Bigl[r_2\Bigl(\frac\pi2 - \sin^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{r_1}{r_2}}\Bigr) - \sqrt{r_1(r_2 - r_1)}\Bigr] \;\cong\; K_1\frac{Z}{\sqrt E} - K_2\sqrt{Zr_1} \qquad (r_1 \ll r_2),
> $$
>
> $$
> K_1 = \Bigl(\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\Bigr)\frac{\pi\sqrt{2m}}{\hbar} = 1.980\ \mathrm{MeV}^{1/2}, \qquad K_2 = \Bigl(\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\Bigr)^{1/2}\frac{4\sqrt m}{\hbar} = 1.485\ \mathrm{fm}^{-1/2},
> $$
>
> and the mean lifetime of the parent is
>
> $$
> \tau \approx \frac{2r_1}{v}\,e^{2\gamma} .
> $$
>
> Consequently $\log_{10}\tau = \dfrac{2K_1}{\ln 10}\,\dfrac{Z}{\sqrt E} + (\text{terms that vary slowly from nucleus to nucleus})$: the Geiger–Nuttall rule, $\log t_{1/2}$ linear in $E^{-1/2}$ ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^law-a7-2-6|Law §A7.2.6]]), with predicted coefficient $2K_1/\ln10 = 1.72\ \mathrm{MeV}^{1/2}$ per unit of $Z/\sqrt E$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 9.2, eqs. (9.25)–(9.29), Fig. 9.6*

^thm-b9-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The integral.* By [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^thm-b9-4-2|Theorem §B9.4.2]], with $2Ze^2/4\pi\epsilon_0 = Er_2$,
>
> $$
> \gamma = \frac1\hbar\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\sqrt{2m\Bigl(\frac{Er_2}{r} - E\Bigr)}\,dr = \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\sqrt{\frac{r_2}{r} - 1}\,dr .
> $$
>
> Substitute $r = r_2\sin^2u$: $dr = 2r_2\sin u\cos u\,du$ and $\sqrt{r_2/r - 1} = \cos u/\sin u$, so the integral is $\int2r_2\cos^2u\,du = r_2\bigl[u + \sin u\cos u\bigr]$ from $u_1 = \sin^{-1}\sqrt{r_1/r_2}$ to $\pi/2$. With $\sin u_1\cos u_1 = \sqrt{r_1/r_2}\sqrt{1 - r_1/r_2}$ this is $r_2(\pi/2 - u_1) - \sqrt{r_1(r_2 - r_1)}$.
>
> *Small $r_1/r_2$.* $\sin^{-1}\epsilon \approx \epsilon$ and $\sqrt{r_1(r_2 - r_1)} \approx \sqrt{r_1r_2}$, so the bracket is $\tfrac\pi2r_2 - 2\sqrt{r_1r_2}$. With $r_2 = 2Ze^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0E)$: $\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}\frac\pi2r_2 = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\pi\sqrt{2m}}{\hbar}\frac{Z}{\sqrt E}$ and $\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}2\sqrt{r_1r_2} = \bigl(\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\bigr)^{1/2}\frac{4\sqrt m}{\hbar}\sqrt{Zr_1}$. Numerically, with $e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0 = 1.440$ MeV fm, $\hbar c = 197.3$ MeV fm and $mc^2 = 3727$ MeV for the alpha particle, $K_1 = 1.440\,\pi\sqrt{7454}/197.3 = 1.980\ \mathrm{MeV}^{1/2}$ and $K_2 = 4\sqrt{1.440\times3727}/197.3 = 1.485\ \mathrm{fm}^{-1/2}$.
>
> *Lifetime.* The particle hits the wall at $r_1$ every $2r_1/v$, and escapes at each hit with probability $T = e^{-2\gamma}$, so the decay constant is $\lambda = (v/2r_1)e^{-2\gamma}$ and the mean lifetime $\tau = 1/\lambda$ ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-3|Theorem §A7.2.3]]). In $\log_{10}\tau = \frac{2}{\ln 10}\gamma + \log_{10}(2r_1/v)$ the energy enters mainly through $K_1Z/\sqrt E$: $v \propto \sqrt E$ varies little, and $\sqrt{Zr_1}$ depends on the nucleus but not on $E$. Griffiths: across the uranium and thorium isotopes $e^{2\gamma}$ ranges over some twenty-five orders of magnitude, beside which the uncertainty in $v$ is insignificant.

*Uses:* [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^thm-b9-4-2|Theorem §B9.4.2]], [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^mod-b9-4-3|Model §B9.4.3]], [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-3|Theorem §A7.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: From an empirical rule to a derived one
> Level A stated the Geiger–Nuttall rule as a law, a fit to measured half-lives, and explained it in words. Theorem §B9.4.4 derives its form, $\log t_{1/2} = aE^{-1/2} + b$ with $a \propto Z$, from tunnelling, and predicts the coefficient: $1.72$ per unit of $Z/\sqrt{E/\mathrm{MeV}}$ in $\log_{10}$, against $1.61$ in the empirical systematics quoted at level A (Taagepera and Nurmia, in [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^rem-a7-2-4|Remark: Alpha decay is tunnelling]]), which also contain a term in $Z^{2/3}$, the analogue of $-K_2\sqrt{Zr_1}$ with $r_1 \propto A^{1/3}$. Griffiths' Fig. 9.6 plots $\log_{10}t_{1/2}$ against $1/\sqrt E$ for isotopes of uranium and thorium: straight lines, one per element. Gamow's 1928 calculation was the first application of quantum mechanics to the nucleus.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 9.2, Fig. 9.6, footnote 8*

^rem-b9-4-2

> [!example] Example §B9.4.2: The Alpha Decay of Plutonium-239
> $^{239}\mathrm{Pu}$ emits alpha particles of $E = 5.16$ MeV ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^ex-a7-2-2|Example §A7.2.2]]), leaving $^{235}\mathrm U$, $Z = 92$. Estimate the barrier, the tunnelling probability and the lifetime.
>
> *Barrier.* $r_1 = 1.07\ \mathrm{fm}\times239^{1/3} = 6.64$ fm, where the Coulomb energy is $2\times92\times1.440\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm}/6.64\ \mathrm{fm} = 39.9$ MeV, nearly eight times $E$. The outer turning point is $r_2 = 265\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm}/5.16\ \mathrm{MeV} = 51.3$ fm.
>
> *Exponent.* The exact formula gives $\gamma = 44.3$; the approximation $K_1Z/\sqrt E - K_2\sqrt{Zr_1} = 80.2 - 36.7 = 43.5$ is close but, inside an exponential, not close enough: $e^{2\times0.8} \approx 5$. So $T = e^{-88.5} \approx 3.5\times10^{-39}$.
>
> *Lifetime.* From $E = \tfrac12mv^2$, $v = 0.0526\,c = 1.58\times10^7$ m/s, so the alpha particle hits the wall $v/2r_1 = 1.2\times10^{21}$ times per second, and
>
> $$
> \tau \approx \frac{2r_1}{v}\,e^{2\gamma} = (8.4\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm s)(2.8\times10^{38}) \approx 2\times10^{17}\ \mathrm s \approx 8\times10^9\ \text{years} .
> $$
>
> *How good is it?* The empirical systematics quoted at level A ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^rem-a7-2-4|Remark: Alpha decay is tunnelling]]) give $\log_{10}(t_{1/2}/\mathrm y) = 1.61(92/\sqrt{5.16} - 92^{2/3}) - 28.9 = 3.5$, a half-life of order $3\times10^3$ years: the estimate is too long by a factor of about $10^6$. That is an error of about $14$ in $2\gamma \approx 89$, i.e. $\gamma$ is off by some $16\%$. The exponent is sensitive to the radius: the alpha particle feels the nuclear force, whose range corresponds to $r_0 \approx 1.4$ fm rather than the charge radius's $1.07$ fm ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-1|Law §A7.1.1]]); with $r_1 = 1.4\ \mathrm{fm}\times239^{1/3} = 8.7$ fm one finds $\gamma = 39.4$ and $\tau \approx 6\times10^5$ years. The model's strength is the energy dependence: raising $E$ by $0.1$ MeV shortens $\tau$ by a factor of about $4.5$, so the $4$–$9$ MeV range of alpha energies spans more than twenty orders of magnitude in lifetime.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 9.2 and Problem 9.4 (the method; Griffiths' Problem 9.4 treats $^{238}$U and $^{212}$Po) · Example §A7.2.2 (the energy); numbers computed here*

## The connection formulas

> [!definition] Definition §B9.4.1: Airy's Equation; Airy Functions
> **Airy's equation** is
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2y}{dz^2} = z\,y .
> $$
>
> Its solutions are combinations of two independent **Airy functions** $\mathrm{Ai}(z)$ and $\mathrm{Bi}(z)$, fixed by their asymptotic forms
>
> $$
> \mathrm{Ai}(z) \sim \frac{e^{-\frac23z^{3/2}}}{2\sqrt\pi\,z^{1/4}}, \quad \mathrm{Bi}(z) \sim \frac{e^{\frac23z^{3/2}}}{\sqrt\pi\,z^{1/4}} \quad (z \gg 0); \qquad
> \mathrm{Ai}(z) \sim \frac{\sin\bigl[\frac23(-z)^{3/2} + \frac\pi4\bigr]}{\sqrt\pi\,(-z)^{1/4}}, \quad \mathrm{Bi}(z) \sim \frac{\cos\bigl[\frac23(-z)^{3/2} + \frac\pi4\bigr]}{\sqrt\pi\,(-z)^{1/4}} \quad (z \ll 0).
> $$
>
> $\mathrm{Ai}$ decays on the right and oscillates on the left; $\mathrm{Bi}$ grows on the right. Airy's equation is the Schrödinger equation for a linear potential in suitable units ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.3, eq. (9.37), Table 9.1, Fig. 9.9*

^def-b9-4-1

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: More about the Airy functions
> Griffiths' Table 9.1 also gives integral representations, $\mathrm{Ai}(z) = \frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\cos\bigl(\frac{s^3}{3} + sz\bigr)ds$ and $\mathrm{Bi}(z) = \frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\bigl[e^{-\frac{s^3}{3} + sz} + \sin\bigl(\frac{s^3}3 + sz\bigr)\bigr]ds$, and notes that the Airy functions are related to Bessel functions of order $\tfrac13$. The asymptotic forms are not new approximations: they are the WKB solutions of Airy's equation itself (Griffiths' footnote 12), with $p \propto \sqrt{-z}$. Their derivation from the integral representations, by the method of steepest descent, belongs to Mathematical Methods (planned). The zeros of $\mathrm{Ai}$ give the levels of the linear potential ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]), and the bouncing neutron experiment Griffiths cites (footnote 14) measured them.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.3, Table 9.1, footnotes 10, 12, 14*

^rem-b9-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.4.5: Connection Formulas at a Turning Point
> Let $x_2$ be a turning point at which $V$ slopes upward ($V'(x_2) > 0$; classical region to the left), let $V > E$ for all $x > x_2$, and suppose $V$ is close to linear over a region around $x_2$ that contains, on each side, an overlap zone where the WKB forms hold. Then the solution that decays to the right is, up to a constant $D$,
>
> $$
> \psi(x) \approx \begin{cases} \dfrac{2D}{\sqrt{p(x)}}\,\sin\Bigl[\dfrac1\hbar\displaystyle\int_x^{x_2}p(x')\,dx' + \dfrac\pi4\Bigr], & x < x_2,\\[12pt] \dfrac{D}{\sqrt{|p(x)|}}\,\exp\Bigl[-\dfrac1\hbar\displaystyle\int_{x_2}^x|p(x')|\,dx'\Bigr], & x > x_2. \end{cases}
> $$
>
> At a turning point $x_1$ where $V$ slopes downward (classical region to the right) and the solution decays to the left,
>
> $$
> \psi(x) \approx \begin{cases} \dfrac{D'}{\sqrt{|p(x)|}}\,\exp\Bigl[-\dfrac1\hbar\displaystyle\int_x^{x_1}|p(x')|\,dx'\Bigr], & x < x_1,\\[12pt] \dfrac{2D'}{\sqrt{p(x)}}\,\sin\Bigl[\dfrac1\hbar\displaystyle\int_{x_1}^xp(x')\,dx' + \dfrac\pi4\Bigr], & x > x_1. \end{cases}
> $$
>
> The decaying exponential continues into a standing wave of twice the amplitude and phase $\pi/4$ at the turning point.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.3, eqs. (9.32)–(9.47), (9.51), Figs. 9.8–9.10, footnotes 11–12; Problem 9.10 · the user's WKB notes, §2*

^thm-b9-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Shift the upward-sloping turning point to $x = 0$. By Theorems §B9.3.1 and §B9.4.1, away from it
>
> $$
> \psi(x) \approx \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\Bigl[B\,e^{\frac i\hbar\int_x^0p\,dx'} + C\,e^{-\frac i\hbar\int_x^0p\,dx'}\Bigr], & x < 0,\\[10pt] \dfrac{D}{\sqrt{|p|}}\,e^{-\frac1\hbar\int_0^x|p|\,dx'}, & x > 0, \end{cases}
> $$
>
> the growing exponential being excluded because $V > E$ all the way to $+\infty$. Both forms blow up at $x = 0$, where the true solution is finite; a **patching function** $\psi_p$ bridges the gap.
>
> *The patching function.* Near $0$ linearize, $V(x) \approx E + V'(0)\,x$. The Schrödinger equation becomes $\psi_p'' = \alpha^3x\,\psi_p$ with $\alpha = [2mV'(0)/\hbar^2]^{1/3}$, and in $z = \alpha x$ it is Airy's equation ([[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^def-b9-4-1|Def. §B9.4.1]]). So $\psi_p = a\,\mathrm{Ai}(\alpha x) + b\,\mathrm{Bi}(\alpha x)$.
>
> *Overlap zones.* These are close enough to $0$ for the linear potential to hold and far enough for $|z| \gg 1$ (Griffiths' footnote 11 and Problem 9.9 check that such zones exist, typically for all but the lowest states). There $p(x) \approx \sqrt{-2mV'(0)x} = \hbar\alpha^{3/2}\sqrt{-x}$.
>
> *Overlap 2 ($x > 0$).* $\int_0^x|p|\,dx' = \tfrac23\hbar(\alpha x)^{3/2}$, so the WKB form is $\dfrac{D}{\sqrt\hbar\,\alpha^{3/4}x^{1/4}}e^{-\frac23(\alpha x)^{3/2}}$. The large-$z$ forms of the Airy functions give $\psi_p \approx \dfrac{a}{2\sqrt\pi(\alpha x)^{1/4}}e^{-\frac23(\alpha x)^{3/2}} + \dfrac{b}{\sqrt\pi(\alpha x)^{1/4}}e^{\frac23(\alpha x)^{3/2}}$. Matching:
>
> $$
> a = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{\alpha\hbar}}\;D, \qquad b = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Overlap 1 ($x < 0$).* $\int_x^0p\,dx' = \tfrac23\hbar(-\alpha x)^{3/2} \equiv \hbar\theta$, so the WKB form is $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt\hbar\,\alpha^{3/4}(-x)^{1/4}}\bigl[Be^{i\theta} + Ce^{-i\theta}\bigr]$, while the large-negative-$z$ form of $\mathrm{Ai}$ gives
>
> $$
> \psi_p \approx \frac{a}{\sqrt\pi(-\alpha x)^{1/4}}\sin\Bigl(\theta + \frac\pi4\Bigr) = \frac{a}{\sqrt\pi(-\alpha x)^{1/4}}\,\frac{1}{2i}\Bigl[e^{i\pi/4}e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\pi/4}e^{-i\theta}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Matching the coefficients of $e^{\pm i\theta}$: $B/\sqrt{\hbar\alpha} = a\,e^{i\pi/4}/2i\sqrt\pi$ and $C/\sqrt{\hbar\alpha} = -a\,e^{-i\pi/4}/2i\sqrt\pi$. With the value of $a$,
>
> $$
> B = -i\,e^{i\pi/4}D, \qquad C = i\,e^{-i\pi/4}D .
> $$
>
> *Result.* For $x < 0$, with $\Theta = \frac1\hbar\int_x^0p\,dx'$: $Be^{i\Theta} + Ce^{-i\Theta} = -iD\bigl[e^{i(\Theta + \pi/4)} - e^{-i(\Theta + \pi/4)}\bigr] = 2D\sin(\Theta + \pi/4)$. Moving the turning point back to $x_2$ gives the first pair of formulas. The patching function has done its job and is discarded. For a downward-sloping turning point, reflect the construction, $x - x_1 \to x_1 - x$ (Griffiths Problem 9.10): the integrals change their limits accordingly and the second pair results.

*The patching argument in the variable $z = \alpha x$: the Airy function $\mathrm{Ai}(z)$ (blue), exact near the turning point $z = 0$ where the potential is linear, against its two asymptotic forms (red, dashed), which are the WKB solutions on either side. In the overlap zones (green) the two descriptions coincide, and comparing them there fixes the amplitude $2D$ and phase $\pi/4$ of the connection formula; near $z = 0$ the WKB forms diverge while $\mathrm{Ai}$ stays finite.*

> [!remark] Remark: What the connection formulas buy
> - *Quantization.* Inside a well, the two formulas give two expressions for the same bound state; requiring them to agree is the Bohr–Sommerfeld rule $\int_{x_1}^{x_2}p\,dx = (n - \tfrac12)\pi\hbar$ ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-4|Theorem §B9.3.4]]). A vertical wall forces a node at the wall, a phase of $0$ instead of $\pi/4$: hence $0$, $\tfrac14$ or $\tfrac12$ subtracted from $n$ for two, one or no vertical walls.
> - *The phase $\pi/4$.* The exact wave function does not stop at the turning point but leaks a little way into the forbidden region before decaying, so its last node lies farther inside than a hard wall would put it: a quarter of a half-wavelength's worth of phase, $\pi/4$, is "spent" at each smooth turning point.
> - *Scattering.* Applied at both edges of a barrier with sloping walls, the formulas make [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^thm-b9-4-2|Theorem §B9.4.2]] precise (Griffiths Problem 9.11; the user's WKB notes, §2.2 and §3.1, outline this route). For that one also needs the continuation of a growing exponential, which the derivation above, built for a bound state with $b = 0$, does not supply.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §9.3, Example 9.4, Problem 9.11 · the user's WKB notes, §2–3*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: More tunnelling problems
> The same formula $T \approx e^{-2\gamma}$ handles a range of problems in Griffiths' exercises. **Zener tunnelling** (Problem 9.5): a field $E_0$ tilts the bands of a semiconductor, and an electron crosses the gap $E_g$ through a triangular barrier of height $E_g$ and width $E_g/eE_0$, so $\gamma = \frac1\hbar\int_0^{E_g/eE_0}\sqrt{2m(E_g - eE_0x)}\,dx = \frac{2\sqrt{2m}\,E_g^{3/2}}{3\hbar eE_0}$. Field emission of electrons from a metal ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-5|Remark: Field emission and the scanning tunnelling microscope]]) is the same triangle with the work function in place of $E_g$. Further: tunnelling ionization of an atom in a Stark-effect field (Problem 9.18), the splitting of the levels of a symmetric double well and the oscillation of a particle between its halves (Problem 9.17; compare [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-6|Remark: Tunnel devices and the ammonia clock]]), and the time for a can of beer to tip over by tunnelling (Problem 9.19).
>
