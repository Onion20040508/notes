The hundreds of hadrons of [[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws|§A8.1]] are not elementary: they are built from six kinds of quarks, just as the hundred-odd elements are built from electrons, protons and neutrons. Together with the six leptons and the particles that carry the forces between them, the quarks make up the Standard Model, the present theory of particles and their interactions. This section states the quark model, explains colour and confinement, estimates the range of a force from the mass of its carrier, and summarises what the Standard Model contains and what it leaves out.

## Quarks

> [!model] Model §A8.2.1: The Quark Model
> - *Assumes:* hadrons are bound states of **quarks**: a baryon is three quarks ($qqq$), an antibaryon three antiquarks, a meson a quark and an antiquark ($q\bar q$). There are six quark **flavours**; every quark has spin ½ and baryon number $\tfrac13$, and each has an antiquark with opposite charge, baryon number and strangeness. Charges, baryon numbers and strangeness add.
>
> | Quark | Charge ($e$) | Spin | $B$ | $S$ | Mass (MeV/$c^2$, approx.) |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | up $u$ | $+\tfrac23$ | ½ | $\tfrac13$ | 0 | 336 |
> | down $d$ | $-\tfrac13$ | ½ | $\tfrac13$ | 0 | 338 |
> | charm $c$ | $+\tfrac23$ | ½ | $\tfrac13$ | 0 | 1500 |
> | strange $s$ | $-\tfrac13$ | ½ | $\tfrac13$ | $-1$ | 540 |
> | top $t$ | $+\tfrac23$ | ½ | $\tfrac13$ | 0 | 170 900 |
> | bottom $b$ | $-\tfrac13$ | ½ | $\tfrac13$ | 0 | 5000 |
>
> - *Explains:* every allowed quark combination corresponds to an observed hadron, and the charge, spin and strangeness of each come out right ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^thm-a8-2-2|Theorem §A8.2.2]]). The model grew from Gell-Mann's and Ne'eman's "eightfold way" (1961), which arranged hadrons in families of 8 and 10 and predicted the $\Omega^-$, found in 1964 with the predicted mass and spin. Gell-Mann and Zweig proposed quarks (u, d, s) in 1964; the charm quark appeared in the $J/\psi = c\bar c$ (1974), the bottom in the $\Upsilon = b\bar b$ (1977), and the top at Fermilab in 1995.
> - *Fails:* no free quark has ever been seen ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^law-a8-2-3|Law §A8.2.3]]). Ordinary matter needs only u and d; the heavier quarks decay quickly by the weak force, and the top too quickly to form hadrons at all. Since no quark is free, quark masses cannot be weighed directly; the values in the table are Tipler's. Polarized-scattering experiments find that the three quarks' spins carry only 20–30% of the proton's spin (the "proton spin crisis"), so a hadron is more than its three quarks.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.3, Table 11.3; Tipler §12-1, §12-4, Table 12-2*

^mod-a8-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.2.2: Quantum Numbers of a Hadron
> The charge, baryon number and strangeness of a hadron are the sums of those of its quarks. In the lowest states (no orbital angular momentum), a meson has spin 0 or 1, and a baryon spin ½ or $\tfrac32$. In particular every baryon has $B = 1$ and every meson $B = 0$, and the charge of every hadron is a whole multiple of $e$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.3, Tables 11.4–11.5; Tipler §12-4*

^thm-a8-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Charge, baryon number and strangeness are additive ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^mod-a8-2-1|Model §A8.2.1]]), so they are sums. Three quarks have $B = 3(\tfrac13) = 1$; a quark and an antiquark $\tfrac13 - \tfrac13 = 0$. Quark charges are $+\tfrac23$ or $-\tfrac13$ (antiquarks the negatives); for three quarks with $k$ up-type ones the sum is $\tfrac23k - \tfrac13(3 - k) = k - 1$, and for $q\bar q$ it is a difference of two such charges, $0$ or $\pm1$, both whole numbers. Spins add by the rule for combining angular momenta ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin|§A5.2]]): two spins ½ give total spin $0$ or $1$; adding a third spin ½ to these gives ½ (from 0), or ½ or $\tfrac32$ (from 1).

^der-a8-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^mod-a8-2-1|Model §A8.2.1]], [[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^def-a8-1-3|Def. §A8.1.3]], [[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^def-a8-1-4|Def. §A8.1.4]], [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin|§A5.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §A8.2.1: Colour
> Each quark carries one of three **colour charges**, labelled red, green and blue (names only; nothing is coloured), and each antiquark the corresponding anticolour. Colour is the source of the strong force, as electric charge is of the electromagnetic one, and it is conserved. Hadrons are **colourless**: a baryon contains one quark of each colour, a meson a colour and its anticolour.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.3; Tipler §12-1, §12-4*

^def-a8-2-1

> [!law] Law §A8.2.3: Quark Confinement
> Quarks are found only inside colourless hadrons. No isolated quark, which would be easy to recognise by its fractional charge, has ever been produced.
>
> *Valid when:* in every experiment so far. Quantum chromodynamics accounts for it: the strong force does not fall off as quarks separate, so pulling a quark out of a hadron costs ever more energy (University Physics compares a baryon to a bola, its stones tied to a centre). At very short distances, inside the hadron, the force weakens instead and quarks move almost freely (**asymptotic freedom**).
> *Source: UP3 §11.3; Tipler §12-4*

^law-a8-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: How we know quarks are there
> Around 1970 at SLAC, electrons of more than 20 GeV were fired at protons. At such energies $E \approx pc$, so the de Broglie wavelength ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]]) is $\lambda = h/p = hc/E \approx (1240\ \mathrm{MeV\cdot fm})/(20\,000\ \mathrm{MeV}) \approx 6\times10^{-17}$ m, much less than the proton's $10^{-15}$ m. Some electrons bounced back at large angles, as Rutherford's α particles had from the nucleus ([[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-4|Theorem §A2.1.4]]): the proton contains small, hard scattering centres. Their angular distribution fits spin-½ constituents, and the scattering rates fit charges of $+\tfrac23e$ and $-\tfrac13e$. Neutrino experiments at CERN agreed. The momenta of the struck quarks also came out smaller than expected, because gluons carry part of the proton's momentum.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.3, eq. (11.1), §11.5; Tipler §12-4*

^rem-a8-2-1

## Forces and their carriers

> [!definition] Definition §A8.2.2: Fundamental Interactions; Exchange Particle
> All known forces reduce to four **fundamental interactions**. In the Standard Model each is carried by an **exchange particle** (force carrier), a boson emitted and absorbed by the interacting particles as a virtual particle ([[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^def-a8-1-5|Def. §A8.1.5]]).
>
> | Interaction | Acts on | Carrier (spin) | Carrier mass | Range | Relative strength | Typical time |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | strong | colour: quarks, gluons | 8 gluons (1) | 0 | $\approx 10^{-15}$ m between hadrons | 1 | $10^{-23}$ s |
> | electromagnetic | electric charge | photon (1) | 0 | infinite | $\tfrac1{137}$ | $10^{-18}$ s |
> | weak | quarks, leptons | $W^+$, $W^-$, $Z^0$ (1) | 80, 91 GeV/$c^2$ | $\approx 10^{-18}$ m | $10^{-6}$–$10^{-5}$ | $10^{-10}$ s |
> | gravitational | all mass and energy | graviton (2), unobserved | 0 | infinite | $10^{-38}$ | — |
>
> Leptons feel all but the strong interaction; quarks feel all four. Relative strengths compare dimensionless coupling constants and are only rough, especially for the weak force (University Physics gives both $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-10}$, Tipler $10^{-5}$). The electromagnetic strength is the fine-structure constant $e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c) \approx 1/137$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.1, §11.5, Table 11.6; Tipler §12-2, Table 12-4*

^def-a8-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.2.4: Range of a Force and Mass of Its Carrier
> A force carried by the exchange of particles of mass $m$ has range of order
>
> $$
> R \approx \frac{\hbar}{mc} = \frac{\hbar c}{mc^2}, \qquad \hbar c = 197.3\ \mathrm{MeV\cdot fm}.
> $$
>
> A massless carrier, like the photon, gives a force of unlimited range.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-5, eqs. (11-48)–(11-50), Example 12-3; UP3 §11.5, eqs. (11.4)–(11.11)*

^thm-a8-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Emitting a carrier of mass $m$ from a nucleon or quark that neither recoils nor loses energy borrows at least the energy $\Delta E = mc^2$. By the energy–time uncertainty relation ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]]) such a fluctuation can last only about $\Delta t \approx \hbar/\Delta E = \hbar/(mc^2)$, and in that time the carrier, moving slower than light, covers at most $R = c\,\Delta t \approx \hbar/(mc)$. A second particle farther away than this cannot absorb it. For $m = 0$, a photon of energy $hf$ can exist for $\Delta t \approx \hbar/(hf)$ and travel $c/(2\pi f) = \lambda/2\pi$; low-energy photons have arbitrarily long wavelengths, so the range is unlimited. This is an order-of-magnitude argument, not a derivation of the force law.

^der-a8-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|REL Def. §A3.2.2]], [[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^def-a8-1-5|Def. §A8.1.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: $h$ or $\hbar$; the Yukawa potential
> University Physics writes the estimate with $h$, Tipler with $\hbar$; the two differ by $2\pi$, the size of the uncertainty in such estimates. Tipler makes it sharper: the static solution of the relativistic Klein–Gordon equation for the carrier's field is the **Yukawa potential** $U(r) \propto e^{-r/R}/r$ with $R = \hbar/(mc)$, which for $m = 0$ becomes the Coulomb $1/r$. So $R$ is the distance over which the force dies away exponentially.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-5, eqs. (11-52)–(11-55), §12-2, eq. (12-4); UP3 §11.5*

^rem-a8-2-2

## The Standard Model

> [!model] Model §A8.2.5: The Standard Model
> - *Assumes:* matter consists of six quarks (each in three colours) and six leptons, in three generations $(u, d; e, \nu_e)$, $(c, s; \mu, \nu_\mu)$, $(t, b; \tau, \nu_\tau)$, with their antiparticles. They interact by exchanging spin-1 bosons, described by two quantum field theories that combine relativity and quantum mechanics: **quantum chromodynamics** (QCD), the theory of the strong force between colour charges, carried by eight gluons; and the **electroweak theory**, which unifies quantum electrodynamics (QED, carried by the photon) with the weak force (carried by $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$). The **Higgs boson** is responsible for the masses of the particles. Gravity is not included.
> - *Explains:* the quark model and the hadron spectrum; beta decay and all weak processes at the quark level; electromagnetism to the highest precision of any theory. It predicted the W and Z masses (about 81 and 90 GeV/$c^2$) before CERN found them in 1983, and the measured shape of the $Z^0$ resonance fits exactly three generations. The Higgs boson was found at CERN's LHC.
> - *Fails:* it leaves out gravity. In its original form neutrinos are massless, but neutrino oscillations show that they have mass. It does not explain why there are three generations, the values of the masses and couplings, dark matter ([[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe|§A8.4]]), or why the universe contains matter and almost no antimatter.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.1, §11.5; Tipler §12-2, §12-3, §12-4*

^mod-a8-2-5

> [!remark] Remark: Quantum chromodynamics
> - **Gluons carry colour.** Unlike the neutral photon, a gluon carries a colour and an anticolour, so emitting one can change a quark's colour (never its flavour), and gluons interact with each other. Nine colour–anticolour pairs give eight gluons, one combination dropping out.
> - **Strong, hence hard.** The coupling is so large that QCD calculations cannot neglect higher terms and are done on supercomputers.
> - **The nuclear force.** The force between nucleons of [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-3|Law §A7.1.3]] is a residue of the colour force between their quarks; at the level of whole nucleons it looks like the exchange of pions (quark–antiquark pairs), Yukawa's picture.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.5; Tipler §12-2, §12-4*

^rem-a8-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Unification
> Maxwell unified electricity and magnetism; in the 1970s Glashow, Salam and Weinberg unified electromagnetism with the weak force. The unification shows only at high energies, comparable to the W and Z masses. The coupling constants are not truly constant: virtual pairs screen a charge, much as a dielectric does, so the effective strength depends on distance, hence on collision energy. The strengths of the forces appear to approach one another at very high energies. A **grand unified theory** would join the strong and electroweak forces, a **theory of everything** gravity as well; neither exists yet. Such energies existed only in the first instants of the universe ([[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe|§A8.4]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.1, §11.5; Tipler §12-2*

^rem-a8-2-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A8.2.1: Hadrons from Their Quarks
> By [[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^thm-a8-2-2|Theorem §A8.2.2]]:
> - proton $uud$: charge $\tfrac23 + \tfrac23 - \tfrac13 = 1$, $B = 1$, $S = 0$, spin ½. Neutron $udd$: charge $\tfrac23 - \tfrac13 - \tfrac13 = 0$.
> - $\Xi^0 = uss$: charge $\tfrac23 - \tfrac13 - \tfrac13 = 0$, $S = -2$, spin ½ or $\tfrac32$, and the observed spin is ½. All three agree with experiment.
> - $\pi^+ = u\bar d$: charge $\tfrac23 + \tfrac13 = 1$, $B = 0$, $S = 0$, spin 0. The same quarks with spins aligned make the $\rho^+$, spin 1, about 5.5 times heavier (0.768 against 0.140 GeV/$c^2$).
> - $K^+ = u\bar s$: charge 1, $S = +1$ (from the $\bar s$).
> - $\Delta^+ = uud$, like the proton but with spin $\tfrac32$, has mass 1.232 against 0.938 GeV/$c^2$: how the quark spins combine shows up in the mass.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.3, Example 11.4, Tables 11.4–11.5; Tipler Example 12-11*

^ex-a8-2-1

> [!example] Example §A8.2.2: Why Colour Is Needed
> The $\Delta^{++}$ is $uuu$ with spin $\tfrac32$: three up quarks, in the lowest state, with all spins parallel. Without another property, these three identical fermions would be in the same quantum state, which the exclusion principle forbids ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]]). Giving the three quarks different colours, one red, one green, one blue, makes them distinguishable and the state allowed. The same rule that the colours of a hadron cancel explains why only $qqq$ and $q\bar q$ combinations, and no single quarks or $qq$ pairs, are seen.
>
> *Source: Tipler §12-4; UP3 §11.3*

^ex-a8-2-2

> [!example] Example §A8.2.3: Ranges of the Nuclear and Weak Forces
> By [[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]]:
> - **Pion**, $mc^2 = 140$ MeV: $R = (197.3\ \mathrm{MeV\cdot fm})/(140\ \mathrm{MeV}) = 1.4$ fm, the range of the nuclear force ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-3|Law §A7.1.3]]). Yukawa ran this backwards in 1935: from a range of about 1 fm he predicted a carrier of about 200 MeV/$c^2$.
> - **$Z^0$**, $mc^2 = 91.16$ GeV: $R = (197.3\ \mathrm{MeV\cdot fm})/(91\,160\ \mathrm{MeV}) = 2.2\times10^{-3}$ fm $= 2.2\times10^{-18}$ m; for the W ($80$ GeV), $2.5\times10^{-18}$ m.
>
> The weak force has so short a range because its carriers are heavy, about 100 proton masses; at collision energies comparable to $m_Wc^2$ it merges with electromagnetism into the electroweak force ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^rem-a8-2-4|Remark: Unification]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 11-15, Example 12-3; UP3 §11.5*

^ex-a8-2-3
