Below the nucleus lies a zoo of particles: hundreds of them, most living for tiny fractions of a second. This section sorts them by spin and by the forces they feel, introduces antiparticles, and states the conservation laws that decide which reactions and decays can happen. Feynman diagrams give a picture of an interaction as the exchange of a particle. As in Chapter A7, PHY 284 did not cover this material; the chapter rests on University Physics and Tipler.

## Classifying particles

> [!definition] Definition §A8.1.1: Fermion; Boson
> A **fermion** has half-integral spin ($\tfrac12\hbar, \tfrac32\hbar, \dots$), a **boson** integral spin ($0, \hbar, 2\hbar, \dots$) ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^pr-a5-2-6|Principle §A5.2.6]]). Exchanging two identical particles leaves $|\psi|^2$ unchanged, so the wave function is either symmetric or antisymmetric under the exchange ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^def-a5-3-1|Def. §A5.3.1]]); which one is fixed by the spin:
>
> $$
> \psi \to -\psi \quad\text{(identical fermions)}, \qquad \psi \to +\psi \quad\text{(identical bosons)}.
> $$
>
> Fermions are thus **antisymmetric** under exchange and obey the exclusion principle ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]]); bosons are **symmetric** and any number of them may share a state. Electrons, protons and neutrons are fermions; the photon is a boson. That spin fixes the symmetry is a theorem of relativistic quantum field theory (level C, planned); here it is taken from experiment.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.1*

^def-a8-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A8.1.2: Lepton; Hadron, Baryon, Meson
> - **Leptons** are spin-½ fermions that do not feel the strong force: the electron, muon and tau and their neutrinos $\nu_e, \nu_\mu, \nu_\tau$, in three families (generations) $(e, \nu_e)$, $(\mu, \nu_\mu)$, $(\tau, \nu_\tau)$. The muon and tau are heavier copies of the electron that decay by the weak force.
> - **Hadrons** are particles that feel the strong force. They are made of quarks ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model|§A8.2]]) and come in two groups: **baryons**, fermions such as the proton and neutron, and **mesons**, bosons such as the pions and kaons.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.1; Tipler §12-1*

^def-a8-1-2

## Antiparticles

> [!principle] Principle §A8.1.1: Antiparticles
> For every particle there is an **antiparticle** with the same mass, spin and lifetime and the opposite electric charge (and opposite baryon, lepton and strangeness numbers, [[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^def-a8-1-3|Def. §A8.1.3]], [[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^def-a8-1-4|Def. §A8.1.4]]). A neutral particle whose numbers are all zero, like the photon or the $\pi^0$, is its own antiparticle. The antiparticle of the electron is the **positron** $e^+$; antiquarks and antibaryons are written with a bar, $\bar u$, $\bar p$. Antiparticles bind into antimatter just as particles bind into matter: an antihydrogen atom is a positron bound to an antiproton.
>
> *Domain: every particle found so far. Whether each neutrino is distinct from its antineutrino is still open.*
> *Source: UP3 §11.1; Tipler §12-1*

^pr-a8-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: From prediction to principle
> Dirac's relativistic quantum theory of the electron (late 1920s) had solutions of negative energy; Dirac pictured them as a filled sea whose holes would behave as particles of the electron's mass and opposite charge. Quantum electrodynamics later recast them directly as a new particle. Anderson found the positron in 1932; the antiproton followed in 1955 at Berkeley's Bevatron, the antineutron two years later, and antihydrogen at CERN in 1995. In relativistic quantum field theory (Quantum Field Theory I) the existence of antiparticles, and the equality of their masses and lifetimes with those of the particles, are theorems; at this level they are taken as a principle.
>
> *Source: Tipler §12-1, §12-3; UP3 §11.1*

^rem-a8-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.1.2: Annihilation at Rest
> A particle of mass $m$ and its antiparticle annihilating at rest into photons cannot produce a single photon. If they produce two, the photons fly off in opposite directions with equal energies
>
> $$
> E_\gamma = mc^2 .
> $$
>
> For an electron and positron, $e^- + e^+ \to 2\gamma$ with $E_\gamma = 0.511$ MeV each.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.1, §10.7; Tipler §12-1, Example 12-1*

^thm-a8-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Before annihilation the total momentum is zero and the total energy is $2mc^2$; both are conserved. A single photon of energy $E$ has momentum $E/c \neq 0$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-2|REL Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]), so it cannot carry zero momentum. Two photons have zero total momentum only if their momenta are equal and opposite, so their energies $E = pc$ are equal, and $2E_\gamma = 2mc^2$.

^der-a8-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-2|REL Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|REL Principle §A3.2.3]], [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Pair production
> Run backwards, the argument says that a single photon in empty space cannot turn into an electron–positron pair: a pair can always be viewed from its own centre-of-mass frame, where its momentum is zero, but a photon has no such frame. A gamma ray of at least $2m_ec^2 = 1.022$ MeV can make a pair in the field of a nucleus, which takes up the momentum; two photons can make one directly. Pair production and annihilation are routine in particle detectors ([[§A8.3 Particle Accelerators and Detectors|§A8.3]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.1; Tipler §12-1*

^rem-a8-1-3

## Conservation laws

Energy, momentum and angular momentum are conserved in every particle reaction ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|REL Principle §A3.2.3]], [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^pr-a3-1-1|REL Principle §A3.1.1]]), and so is electric charge ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|EM Principle §A1.1.2]]). Energy alone forbids a particle to decay into products heavier than itself. Particle physics adds further conserved numbers, discovered empirically: a reaction allowed by all of them is generally observed ("anything that can happen does happen"), and one that is never seen points to a conservation law.

> [!definition] Definition §A8.1.3: Baryon Number; Lepton Numbers
> Every particle carries a **baryon number** $B$: $+1$ for baryons, $-1$ for antibaryons, $0$ for all others (quarks have $B = \tfrac13$). It carries three **lepton numbers**: the electron number $L_e = +1$ for $e^-$ and $\nu_e$, $-1$ for $e^+$ and $\bar\nu_e$, $0$ otherwise; the muon number $L_\mu$ and tau number $L_\tau$ likewise for the muon and tau families. The numbers of a system are the sums over its particles.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.2, Table 11.2; Tipler §12-3*

^def-a8-1-3

> [!principle] Principle §A8.1.3: Conservation of Baryon Number
> The total baryon number is the same before and after every reaction and decay.
>
> *Domain: every reaction and decay observed; no violation has been seen. No symmetry is known that requires it, and grand unified theories allow violations at extremely high energies. Since the proton is the lightest baryon, energy and baryon-number conservation make it stable; experiments put its lifetime above about $10^{32}$ years.*
> *Source: UP3 §11.2; Tipler §12-3*

^pr-a8-1-3

> [!principle] Principle §A8.1.4: Conservation of Lepton Number
> Each of the lepton numbers $L_e$, $L_\mu$, $L_\tau$ is the same before and after every reaction and decay.
>
> *Domain: all reactions and decays, with one known exception: neutrinos change flavour as they travel (neutrino oscillations), which violates the separate numbers $L_e$, $L_\mu$, $L_\tau$. The total $L = L_e + L_\mu + L_\tau$ is, as far as is known, conserved.*
> *Source: UP3 §11.2; Tipler §12-3*

^pr-a8-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: Which laws are exact
> - **Charge** is conserved in every process known, and its conservation follows from a symmetry (gauge invariance) of electrodynamics. Energy, momentum and angular momentum follow likewise from the uniformity of time and space and from rotation invariance (Noether's theorem).
> - **Baryon number** and **total lepton number** have never been seen to change, but no symmetry is known behind them.
> - **Separate lepton numbers** fail by neutrino oscillations, the first sign that the Standard Model ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model|§A8.2]]) is not the whole story.
> - **Strangeness** (next) and **parity** (mirror symmetry) hold in strong and electromagnetic interactions but not in weak ones. Lee and Yang suggested parity violation in 1956, and Wu's group found it the same year: electrons from aligned $^{60}$Co nuclei come out preferentially opposite to the nuclear spin, which a mirror image would reverse.
>
> | Conserved? | Strong | Electromagnetic | Weak |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | energy, momentum, charge, $B$, $L$ | yes | yes | yes |
> | strangeness $S$ | yes | yes | no ($\Delta S = 0, \pm1$) |
> | parity | yes | yes | no |
>
> *Source: Tipler §12-3, Table 12-7; UP3 §11.2*

^rem-a8-1-4

> [!definition] Definition §A8.1.4: Strangeness
> The **strangeness** $S$ counts strange quarks ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model|§A8.2]]), each with $S = -1$ (each antiquark $\bar s$ with $+1$): $S = +1$ for $K^+$ and $K^0$, $-1$ for $\Lambda^0$ and the $\Sigma$'s, $-2$ for the $\Xi$'s, $-3$ for $\Omega^-$, and $0$ for nucleons, pions and leptons.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.2–11.3, Tables 11.2–11.3; Tipler §12-3*

^def-a8-1-4

> [!law] Law §A8.1.5: Conservation of Strangeness
> Strangeness is the same before and after every strong and electromagnetic interaction. Weak interactions can change it, by 0 or $\pm1$.
>
> *Valid when:* for processes by the strong or electromagnetic force; it is an approximate law, broken by the weak force. It was introduced (Gell-Mann and Nishijima, 1952) to explain why particles discovered in cosmic rays were made copiously by the strong force but always in pairs (associated production), and lived $10^{-10}$ s instead of the $10^{-23}$ s typical of strong decays: they can decay only by the weak force.
> *Source: UP3 §11.2; Tipler §12-3*

^law-a8-1-5

## Feynman diagrams

> [!definition] Definition §A8.1.5: Feynman Diagram; Virtual Particle
> A **Feynman diagram** is a spacetime diagram of an interaction, with time running up the page here (conventions differ; University Physics runs time to the right). Lines are particles; their shapes do not show trajectories. Lines with arrows are fermions, and a fermion line whose arrow points backward in time represents the antiparticle moving forward. Photons are wavy lines, other force carriers ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model|§A8.2]]) wavy or broken. Interactions happen at the **vertices**, where energy, momentum and charge are conserved; the basic electromagnetic vertex is a charged particle emitting or absorbing a photon. Lines that enter or leave the diagram are real, observable particles. A line that begins and ends inside the diagram is a **virtual particle**: it exists too briefly to be observed and need not have the mass of the corresponding real particle.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.5; Tipler §12-1*

^def-a8-1-5

![[ph-qm-a8-1-1.svg]]
*Three Feynman diagrams, time upward. Left: two electrons repel by exchanging a virtual photon (red), the quantum picture of the Coulomb force. Middle: an electron and a positron annihilate into two photons; the positron is the fermion line whose arrow points back in time. Right: neutron beta decay at the quark level; a down quark emits a virtual $W^-$ boson (red) and becomes an up quark, turning udd into uud, and the $W^-$ becomes an electron and an electron antineutrino.*

> [!remark] Remark: What the diagrams say
> - **Forces as exchange.** In quantum electrodynamics two charges interact by exchanging virtual photons, which carry energy and momentum between them. An exchanged particle that exists for a time $\Delta t$ may have an energy uncertainty $\Delta E \approx \hbar/\Delta t$ ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]]); this links the range of a force to the mass of its carrier ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]]).
> - **The weak force changes flavour.** In beta decay the $W^-$ turns a d quark into a u quark. A neutrino, which has no charge, can still scatter off an electron by exchanging a neutral $Z^0$.
> - **Many diagrams per process.** Compton scattering, annihilation and pair production are all built from the same basic vertex; a given process usually has several diagrams, and calculations add them.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.5; Tipler §12-1, §12-2, Example 12-4*

^rem-a8-1-5

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A8.1.1: Annihilation Photons
> By [[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]]:
> - $e^- + e^+ \to 2\gamma$ at rest: each photon has $E_\gamma = 0.511$ MeV and wavelength $\lambda = hc/E_\gamma = (1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm})/(0.511\times10^6\ \mathrm{eV}) = 2.43\times10^{-3}$ nm. These are the photons that PET scanners detect in coincidence ([[§A7.4 Biological Effects and Medical Applications#^def-a7-4-3|Def. §A7.4.3]]).
> - $p + \bar p \to 2\gamma$ at rest: each photon has $E_\gamma = m_pc^2 = 938$ MeV and $\lambda = (1240\ \mathrm{MeV\cdot fm})/(938\ \mathrm{MeV}) = 1.32$ fm, about the size of a nucleon.
>
> The absence of 0.511-MeV photons arriving from space is the evidence that the universe contains almost no antimatter ([[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe|§A8.4]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 12-1; UP3 §11.7*

^ex-a8-1-1

> [!example] Example §A8.1.2: Allowed and Forbidden Processes
> Check energy, charge, baryon number and lepton numbers (masses from [[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^rem-a8-1-1|Remark: Some particles and their properties]]):
> - $n \to e^+ + e^-$: charge $0 \to 0$ and energy are fine, but $B$: $1 \to 0$. Forbidden.
> - $p \to \pi^0 + e^+$: charge and energy fine ($938.3 > 135.0 + 0.5$ MeV), but $B$: $1 \to 0$ and $L_e$: $0 \to -1$. Never observed, consistent with a proton lifetime above $10^{32}$ y.
> - $n \to p + \pi^-$: charge and $B$ fine, but $939.6 < 938.3 + 139.6$ MeV. Forbidden by energy.
> - $n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu_e$: charge $0 \to 0$, $B$: $1 \to 1$, $L_e$: $0 \to 1 - 1 = 0$, energy $939.6 > 938.8$ MeV. Allowed: free-neutron beta decay. The lepton number fixes that the neutrino is an antineutrino.
> - $\pi^- \to \mu^- + \bar\nu_\mu$: $L_\mu$: $0 \to 1 - 1 = 0$. Allowed; it is the pion's main decay. But $\pi^- \to \mu^- + \nu_\mu + \bar\nu_\mu$ has $L_\mu = 1 + 1 - 1 = 1 \neq 0$. Forbidden.
>
> *Source: UP3 Examples 11.1–11.2; Tipler Example 12-6*

^ex-a8-1-2

> [!example] Example §A8.1.3: Strange Particles
> - $\pi^- + p \to \Lambda^0 + K^0$: strangeness $0 + 0 \to -1 + 1 = 0$. Allowed for the strong force, and it happens readily (large cross section). The products then decay, $\Lambda^0 \to p + \pi^-$ ($S$: $-1 \to 0$) and $K^0 \to \pi^+ + \pi^-$ ($S$: $+1 \to 0$). Both change $S$ by 1, so both are weak decays, with lifetimes near $10^{-10}$ s.
> - $\pi^- + p \to K^+ + K^- + n$: $S$: $0 \to +1 - 1 + 0 = 0$, and $B$: $1 \to 1$. Allowed for the strong force.
> - $K^+ \to \pi^+ + \pi^0$: $S$: $+1 \to 0$. Not allowed for the strong force, so the $K^+$, though a hadron, lives $1.24\times10^{-8}$ s, a typical weak lifetime, and not $10^{-23}$ s.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.2, Example 11.3; Tipler §12-3*

^ex-a8-1-3
