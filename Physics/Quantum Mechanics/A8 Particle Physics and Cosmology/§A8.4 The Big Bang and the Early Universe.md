The highest energies in nature were reached not in accelerators but in the first instants of the universe. Galaxies recede from us at speeds proportional to their distance, so the universe is expanding, and run backwards it was once hot and dense. This section states Hubble's law, the hot Big Bang model that explains it, and the two relics that confirm the model, the cosmic microwave background and the primordial helium; then it follows the early universe as it cooled through the particle physics of [[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model|§A8.2]], and ends with what is not understood, dark matter and dark energy. Cosmology proper, with general relativity, is planned for Relativity, Chapter C9.

## Hubble's law

> [!definition] Definition §A8.4.1: Cosmology; Redshift; Parsec
> **Cosmology** studies the origin, evolution and large-scale structure of the universe. The **redshift** of light received with wavelength $\lambda_{\text{obs}}$ from a source that emitted $\lambda_{\text{emit}}$ is
>
> $$
> z = \frac{\lambda_{\text{obs}} - \lambda_{\text{emit}}}{\lambda_{\text{emit}}} ,
> $$
>
> measured from the shift of known spectral lines. Astronomical distances are given in **parsecs**: $1\ \mathrm{pc} = 3.26$ light-years, $1\ \mathrm{Mpc} = 10^6$ pc.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.6, Example 11.8; Tipler §13-6*

^def-a8-4-1

> [!law] Law §A8.4.1: Hubble's Law
> Distant galaxies recede from us with speeds $v$ proportional to their distances $d$:
>
> $$
> v = H_0\,d, \qquad H_0 \approx 70\ \mathrm{km/s}\ \text{per Mpc}.
> $$
>
> *Valid when:* on average, for galaxies beyond our immediate neighbourhood, whose own motions, from interactions with neighbouring galaxies, are small next to $H_0d$; and for $z \ll 1$, where $v = cz$ and $d$ have unambiguous meanings ([[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^cau-a8-4-1|Caution: Large redshifts]]). The value of $H_0$ depends on the calibration of distances (Tipler gives $71 \pm 4$); it is the present value of a rate that changes with time.
> *Source: UP3 §11.6, eq. (11.12); Tipler §13-6, eq. (13-27)*

^law-a8-4-1

![[ph-qm-a8-4-1.svg]]
*Recession velocity against distance for the five galaxies whose redshifted spectra Tipler shows (dots, from NGC 221 at 2.6 Mpc to a galaxy in the Gemini cluster at 287.5 Mpc). They lie on a straight line through the origin (blue) of slope 80 km/s per Mpc; the dashed red line has the slope $H_0 = 71$ km/s per Mpc that follows from far larger samples with better distance calibration. The difference is a reminder that $H_0$ is only as good as the distance scale.*

> [!remark] Remark: No centre
> Hubble's law does not put us at the centre of the expansion. University Physics pictures the galaxies as dots on an inflating balloon: as the balloon grows every dot sees every other dot recede, faster the farther away it is, and no dot is special ([[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^thm-a8-4-3|Theorem §A8.4.3]]). The galaxies are not flying apart through a fixed space like the debris of an explosion; space itself expands, and carries everything, light included, along.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.6; Tipler §13-6*

## The hot Big Bang model

> [!model] Model §A8.4.2: The Hot Big Bang
> - *Assumes:*
>   - **Cosmological principle**: on large enough scales (billions of light-years; clusters and superclusters are still lumpy) the universe is homogeneous and isotropic.
>   - **Expansion**: all large-scale distances grow by a common **scale factor** $a(t)$, with $a = 1$ today; galaxies keep fixed **comoving** positions. Light travelling through the expanding space is stretched with it, so light emitted when the scale factor was $a$ arrives with $1 + z = 1/a$.
>   - **Hot beginning**: about $13.7\times10^9$ years ago the universe was extremely hot and dense, and it has expanded and cooled ever since.
>   - **Dynamics** from general relativity: for a universe of mass density $\rho$ and pressure $p$, $\ddot a = -\tfrac{4\pi G}{3}\bigl(\rho + 3p/c^2\bigr)a$, so ordinary matter and radiation slow the expansion and only a negative pressure can speed it up.
> - *Explains:* Hubble's law ([[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^thm-a8-4-3|Theorem §A8.4.3]]); the cosmic microwave background ([[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^thm-a8-4-8|Theorem §A8.4.8]]); the abundances of the lightest elements, about 25% helium by mass ([[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^thm-a8-4-7|Theorem §A8.4.7]]); the evolution seen when looking far, hence back in time (distant radio galaxies and quasars were more numerous or brighter); and the darkness of the night sky, since lines of sight end before the first stars formed.
> - *Fails or leaves open:* physics before about $10^{-43}$ s, when quantum gravity would be needed; the uniformity and flatness of the universe, which require a brief early **inflation** for which there is no direct evidence; the nature of dark matter and dark energy, which make up 95% of the energy content; and why matter outnumbers antimatter.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.6–11.7, eqs. (11.13)–(11.15); Tipler §13-6, §13-8, eq. (13-31)*

^mod-a8-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.4.3: Hubble's Law from Uniform Expansion
> In a uniformly expanding universe every galaxy sees every other recede with velocity
>
> $$
> \vec v = H(t)\,\vec r, \qquad H = \frac{\dot a}{a},
> $$
>
> where $\vec r$ points from the observer to the galaxy. The law has the same form, with the same $H$, for observers in every galaxy; today $H = H_0$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §13-6, §13-8, eqs. (13-37)–(13-38); UP3 §11.6*

^thm-a8-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^mod-a8-4-2|Model §A8.4.2]] a galaxy at fixed comoving position $\vec r_c$ is at $\vec r(t) = a(t)\vec r_c$ from us, so $\vec v = \dot a\,\vec r_c = (\dot a/a)\,\vec r$. For an observer in galaxy A, the velocity of galaxy B is the difference of the velocities measured from here ([[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|CM Theorem §A2.8.1]], adequate while the speeds are small next to $c$): $\vec v_B - \vec v_A = H(\vec r_B - \vec r_A)$, again proportional to the separation, with the same $H$.

^der-a8-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^mod-a8-4-2|Model §A8.4.2]], [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|CM Theorem §A2.8.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.4.4: Redshift of Nearby Galaxies
> For a galaxy at distance $d$ with $H_0d \ll c$, the cosmological redshift is
>
> $$
> z \approx \frac{H_0\,d}{c} = \frac vc ,
> $$
>
> the same as the Doppler shift of a source receding at $v = H_0d$ ([[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^thm-a2-4-1|REL Theorem §A2.4.1]]). So for nearby galaxies, measuring $z$ measures $v = cz$, and Hubble's law becomes $cz = H_0d$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §13-6, eqs. (13-29)–(13-31); UP3 §11.6*

^thm-a8-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Light received now ($a = 1$) left the galaxy a time $\Delta t \approx d/c$ ago, when the scale factor was $a \approx 1 - \dot a\,\Delta t = 1 - H_0d/c$ (first-order Taylor expansion of $a(t)$, [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]). By [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^mod-a8-4-2|Model §A8.4.2]], $1 + z = 1/a \approx 1 + H_0d/c$ to first order in $H_0d/c$. The relativistic Doppler formula for a receding source, $1 + z = \sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)}$, gives $1 + z \approx 1 + v/c$ to first order in $\beta = v/c$; with $v = H_0d$ the two agree.

^der-a8-4-4

*Uses:* [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^mod-a8-4-2|Model §A8.4.2]], [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^law-a8-4-1|Law §A8.4.1]], [[§A2.4 The Doppler Effect for Light#^thm-a2-4-1|REL Theorem §A2.4.1]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Large redshifts
> The cosmological redshift is the stretching of light by the expansion, not a Doppler shift from motion through space; the two agree only for $z \ll 1$. For $z$ of order 1 or more, converting $z$ into a speed with the relativistic Doppler formula and then into a distance with $d = v/H_0$, as University Physics does for $z = 4.5$ ($v = 0.93c$, $d \approx 3.8\times10^3$ Mpc), gives numbers without a clear meaning: the expansion rate was different when the light set out, and "distance" must be defined anew. What $z$ does say exactly is $1 + z = 1/a$: a galaxy seen at $z = 2$ is seen as it was when the universe was a third of its present size.
>
> *Source: Tipler §13-6; UP3 Example 11.8*

^cau-a8-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.4.5: Hubble Time
> If every galaxy had always receded at its present speed, all would have been together a time
>
> $$
> t_H = \frac{1}{H_0} \approx 1.4\times10^{10}\ \mathrm y
> $$
>
> ago (for $H_0 = 71$ km/s per Mpc). This **Hubble time** is close to the age of the universe, $13.7\times10^9$ y, from the full model.
>
> *Source: Tipler §13-6; UP3 §11.6*

^thm-a8-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A galaxy now at distance $d$ moving at constant $v = H_0d$ ([[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^law-a8-4-1|Law §A8.4.1]]) was at distance zero a time $d/v = 1/H_0$ ago, the same for all galaxies. Numerically, $1\ \mathrm{Mpc} = 3.086\times10^{19}$ km, so $1/H_0 = (3.086\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{km})/(71\ \mathrm{km/s}) = 4.3\times10^{17}\ \mathrm s = 1.4\times10^{10}$ y. Gravity slowed the expansion and dark energy now speeds it up, so the true age differs from $1/H_0$, here by little.

^der-a8-4-5

*Uses:* [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^law-a8-4-1|Law §A8.4.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.4.6: Critical Density
> Without dark energy, a uniform universe expanding at the present rate keeps expanding forever if its mass density is at most the **critical density**
>
> $$
> \rho_c = \frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G} \approx 9\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3},
> $$
>
> about five hydrogen atoms per cubic metre; if its density is larger, gravity eventually reverses the expansion.
>
> *Source: Tipler §13-7, eqs. (13-32)–(13-33)*

^thm-a8-4-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Consider a galaxy of mass $m$ at distance $R$ from us, and the sphere of radius $R$ around us, of mass $M = \tfrac43\pi R^3\rho$. By the cosmological principle the matter outside is a set of uniform shells around us, which exert no net force inside ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|CM Theorem §A8.1.5]]), and the sphere attracts the galaxy as a point mass at the centre. The galaxy's total energy is ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-1|CM Theorem §A8.2.1]])
>
> $$
> E = \tfrac12mv^2 - \frac{GMm}{R} = \tfrac12m(H_0R)^2 - \frac{4\pi G}{3}\rho R^2m = \frac{mR^2}{2}\Bigl(H_0^2 - \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho\Bigr),
> $$
>
> using $v = H_0R$. The galaxy escapes to infinity if $E \ge 0$ and falls back if $E < 0$ ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-3|CM Theorem §A8.2.3]]). The sign does not depend on $R$ or $m$, so all galaxies share the fate, and the boundary is $\rho = 3H_0^2/(8\pi G)$. With $H_0 = 71\ \mathrm{km/s/Mpc} = 2.30\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm s^{-1}$ this is $9.5\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, or $5.7$ hydrogen-atom masses per cubic metre.

^der-a8-4-6

*Uses:* [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|CM Theorem §A8.1.5]], [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-1|CM Theorem §A8.2.1]], [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-3|CM Theorem §A8.2.3]], [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^law-a8-4-1|Law §A8.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Geometry and fate
> In general relativity the same balance fixes the geometry of space, through a curvature constant $k$: $k > 0$ (density above critical) a closed universe that would recollapse, $k = 0$ a flat one, $k < 0$ an open one expanding forever. The Newtonian argument above reproduces this energy bookkeeping, with $-k$ playing the part of $E$; Tipler sets it up shell by shell. Observations put the total density, matter plus dark energy, very close to critical, so the universe is nearly flat. Visible matter supplies only about 4–5% of $\rho_c$ and dark matter about 22–27%; the rest is dark energy, which drives an accelerating expansion that a pure-matter universe could not have.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.6–11.7, eqs. (11.13)–(11.14); Tipler §13-7, §13-8*

## The early universe

> [!remark] Remark: A timeline
> Temperatures and characteristic energies are related roughly by $E \approx k_BT$, with $k_B = 8.62\times10^{-5}$ eV/K: $10^{13}$ K is about 1 GeV, $10^{10}$ K about 1 MeV ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|TH Theorem §A2.2.2]] gives the exact mean for a gas, $\tfrac32k_BT$). The early steps are increasingly speculative.
>
> | Time | Temperature | What happens |
> |---|---|---|
> | before $10^{-43}$ s | above $10^{32}$ K | all four forces unified; known physics does not apply |
> | $10^{-43}$ s | $10^{32}$ K | gravity separates; strong and electroweak forces still unified (grand unification); a slight excess of quarks over antiquarks, about 1 in $10^9$, arises |
> | $10^{-43}$–$10^{-35}$ s | to $10^{27}$ K | inflation: exponential expansion |
> | $10^{-35}$ s | $10^{27}$ K | strong force separates; then the weak force separates from the electromagnetic; a hot soup of quarks, leptons and photons |
> | about $10^{-6}$ s | $10^{13}$ K ($\approx 1$ GeV) | quarks bind into hadrons; photons can no longer create nucleon–antinucleon pairs, which annihilate, leaving the small excess of protons and neutrons |
> | about 10 s | $10^{10}$ K ($\approx 1$ MeV) | photons can no longer create electron–positron pairs; positrons annihilate, leaving the excess electrons |
> | a few minutes | | protons and neutrons fuse into $^2$H, $^3$He, $^4$He (about 25% of the mass) and traces of $^7$Li; expansion soon stops the fusion |
> | about $3.8\times10^5$ y | about 3000 K | electrons and nuclei form neutral atoms; photons decouple from matter and travel freely: the microwave background |
> | after | | gravity gathers matter into stars and galaxies; stars resume fusion |
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.7, Figs. 11.21–11.22; Tipler §13-8, Fig. 13-33*

^rem-a8-4-3

> [!caution] Caution: Temperatures of the timeline are rough
> The rule $E \approx k_BT$ fixes only orders of magnitude, and the two sources differ in detail. University Physics lists the "age of atoms" as below $10^5$ K and estimates decoupling at $k_BT \approx 1$ eV, $T \approx 10^4$ K; Tipler's account puts atom formation at about 3000 K, at about 380 000 years (University Physics' own figure for the last scattering of the background photons). The 3000 K value is the one consistent with the observed redshift of the background, [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^thm-a8-4-8|Theorem §A8.4.8]].
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.7; Tipler §13-8*

^cau-a8-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.4.7: Primordial Helium
> If, when nucleosynthesis began, there was one neutron for every $r$ protons ($r \approx 7$), and nearly all neutrons ended in $^4$He, then the fraction of the mass in helium is
>
> $$
> Y = \frac{2}{1 + r} = \frac{2}{1 + 7} = 0.25 .
> $$
>
> Stars cannot make this much helium, so it is a relic of the Big Bang; the predicted abundances of $^2$H, $^4$He and $^7$Li depend on the density of ordinary matter and agree with the density measured by WMAP.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.7; Tipler §13-8*

^thm-a8-4-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take $n$ neutrons and $rn$ protons, with nucleon masses taken equal. Each $^4$He nucleus uses two neutrons and two protons, so all $n$ neutrons make $n/2$ helium nuclei holding $2n$ nucleons, out of $(1 + r)n$ in total. The helium mass fraction is $2n/\bigl((1 + r)n\bigr) = 2/(1 + r)$; the nuclear binding energy changes the masses by less than 1%.

^der-a8-4-7

*Uses:* [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^mod-a8-4-2|Model §A8.4.2]], [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^ex-a7-1-3|Example §A7.1.3]]

> [!definition] Definition §A8.4.2: Cosmic Microwave Background
> The **cosmic microwave background** (CMB) is blackbody radiation filling the universe, arriving with the same intensity from every direction to a few parts in $10^5$. Its spectrum is that of a blackbody ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^mod-a1-5-5|TH Model §A1.5.5]]) at $T_0 = 2.725 \pm 0.001$ K, the most perfect blackbody spectrum known. Penzias and Wilson found it in 1965 as an unexplained noise at centimetre wavelengths; the COBE and WMAP satellites measured its spectrum and its tiny fluctuations, the seeds of galaxies.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.7; Tipler §13-8*

^def-a8-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.4.8: The Background Cools as the Universe Expands
> Radiation in thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$ when the scale factor was $a$ is seen today with its whole spectrum stretched by $1/a = 1 + z$, its peak shifted accordingly, so that it looks like blackbody radiation at
>
> $$
> T_0 = a\,T = \frac{T}{1 + z} .
> $$
>
> Matter and radiation decoupled at about 3000 K, so the background seen today, at 2.725 K, comes from redshift $1 + z \approx 3000/2.725 \approx 1100$. By Wien's law it peaks at $\lambda_{\max} = (2.898\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\cdot K})/(2.725\ \mathrm K) = 1.06$ mm, in the microwave region.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.7, eq. (11.17); Tipler §13-8*

^thm-a8-4-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^mod-a8-4-2|Model §A8.4.2]] every wavelength emitted when the scale factor was $a$ arrives multiplied by $1/a$, the peak wavelength included. For blackbody radiation the peak wavelength is inversely proportional to the temperature (Wien's law, [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-5|Theorem §A1.1.5]]), so a peak at $\lambda_{\max}/a$ is the peak of a blackbody at temperature $aT$. That the stretched spectrum keeps the blackbody shape at every wavelength, not only at its peak, also holds (it follows from Planck's law, [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-4|Theorem §A1.1.4]], once the dilution of the photons by the expansion is included); this is shown at a higher level. The numbers follow from $T_0 = 2.725$ K and the decoupling temperature of about 3000 K.

*Uses:* [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^mod-a8-4-2|Model §A8.4.2]], [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-5|Theorem §A1.1.5]], [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-4|Theorem §A1.1.4]], [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^def-a8-4-2|Def. §A8.4.2]]

![[ph-qm-a8-4-2.svg]]
*The spectrum of the cosmic microwave background: Planck's law for a blackbody at $T_0 = 2.725$ K, computed (blue), as spectral radiance per unit wavelength in arbitrary units. It peaks at $\lambda_{\max} = 1.06$ mm (red); Penzias and Wilson met it at centimetre wavelengths, on the long-wavelength side.*

> [!remark] Remark: Where the antimatter went
> The universe today is almost pure matter: if regions of antimatter existed, annihilation at their borders would fill the sky with 0.511-MeV gamma rays ([[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]]), which are not seen. In the early universe particles and antiparticles were made and annihilated in nearly equal numbers; a tiny excess of matter, about one part in $10^9$, survived the annihilations and became all the stars and galaxies. Producing it requires a slight difference between the laws for matter and antimatter, CP violation, which is seen in neutral-kaon decays, but only as a small effect; how the excess arose is not settled.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.7; Tipler §12-3, §13-8*

## Dark matter and dark energy

> [!definition] Definition §A8.4.3: Dark Matter; Dark Energy
> **Dark matter** is matter that neither emits nor absorbs light but is detected by its gravity. What it is, is unknown: it must be cold (slow-moving) and interact only weakly with ordinary matter; proposed particles, such as the neutralinos of supersymmetric theories, are speculative. **Dark energy** is the unknown form of energy, with negative pressure, held responsible for the acceleration of the expansion; it is the modern form of Einstein's cosmological constant. University Physics gives today's energy content as 68.3% dark energy, 26.8% dark matter and 4.9% ordinary matter (Tipler, from earlier data: 74%, 22%, 4%).
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.7; Tipler §13-2, §13-7, §13-8*

^def-a8-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.4.9: A Flat Rotation Curve Means Hidden Mass
> If stars or gas orbit a galaxy's centre on circles with the same speed $v$ at all radii $r$ out to beyond the visible galaxy, and the mass is distributed with spherical symmetry, then the mass inside radius $r$ is
>
> $$
> M(r) = \frac{v^2r}{G} ,
> $$
>
> growing in proportion to $r$, even where little light is emitted. Most of the galaxy's mass is therefore unseen.
>
> *Source: Tipler §13-8, Fig. 13-34*

^thm-a8-4-9

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For a spherically symmetric distribution only the mass $M(r)$ inside the orbit attracts the orbiting star, as a point mass at the centre ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|CM Theorem §A8.1.5]]). A circular orbit then has $v^2 = GM(r)/r$ ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-4|CM Theorem §A8.2.4]]), so $M(r) = v^2r/G$. If the visible matter were all there was, $M$ would stop growing beyond the visible edge and $v$ would fall off as $1/\sqrt r$, as for the planets; a constant $v$ means $M \propto r$.

^der-a8-4-9

*Uses:* [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|CM Theorem §A8.1.5]], [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-4|CM Theorem §A8.2.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: The evidence
> - **Dark matter.** Flat rotation curves, first measured for the Andromeda galaxy (M31); clusters of galaxies held together by more gravity than their visible mass provides; **gravitational lensing**, the bending of light from distant galaxies by the gravity of matter in between, which can produce double images or rings, and maps the mass even where nothing shines. In the colliding cluster 1E0657-558 the lensing mass and the hot gas seen in X-rays have separated: most of the mass is not the gas.
> - **Dark energy.** Type Ia supernovae, which all have nearly the same peak brightness, appear fainter at a given redshift than they would in a decelerating universe: the expansion is speeding up. The pattern of fluctuations in the microwave background supports the same picture.
>
> Neither is understood. As University Physics closes with Newton: we are like children finding prettier shells on the shore, "whilst the great ocean of truth lay all undiscovered before me".
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.7; Tipler §13-7, §13-8*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A8.4.1: How Far to a Galaxy in Virgo?
> A galaxy in the constellation Virgo recedes at $v = 1200$ km/s, so $z = v/c = 0.0040 \ll 1$ and [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^law-a8-4-1|Law §A8.4.1]] applies. With $H_0 = 71$ km/s per Mpc,
>
> $$
> d = \frac{v}{H_0} = \frac{1200\ \mathrm{km/s}}{71\ \mathrm{km/s\ per\ Mpc}} = 17\ \mathrm{Mpc} \approx 55\times10^6\ \text{light-years}.
> $$
>
> Standard distance methods (Cepheid variables, novae, supernovae) give 16–21 Mpc for the Virgo cluster, in agreement. Likewise a galaxy 100 Mpc away typically recedes at about 7000 km/s.
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 13-5, Table 13-4; UP3 §11.6*

^ex-a8-4-1

> [!example] Example §A8.4.2: Energies of the Early Universe
> With $k_B = 8.62\times10^{-5}$ eV/K:
> - At $T = 10^{32}$ K, just after the Planck era, $k_BT \approx 10^{28}$ eV $= 10^{19}$ GeV, some fifteen orders of magnitude beyond the most powerful accelerators (about $10^4$ GeV).
> - Nucleons ($m_pc^2 \approx 1000$ MeV) could be created from radiation while $k_BT \gtrsim 1000$ MeV, that is $T \gtrsim (1000\ \mathrm{MeV})/(8.62\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{MeV/K}) = 1.2\times10^{13}$ K, reached within the first microsecond.
> - Electron–positron pairs ($2m_ec^2 = 1.02$ MeV) were made while $T \gtrsim 10^{10}$ K.
>
> Each threshold that the cooling universe passed froze out one kind of particle–antiparticle pair; this is why the history of the universe is written in the energy scales of particle physics.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 11.9, §11.7; Tipler §13-8*
