Experimental particle physics makes particles in high-energy collisions and measures what comes out. Accelerators supply the energy, using electric fields to push charged particles and magnetic fields to steer them; detectors record the products. Two pieces of relativity do most of the work: the momentum of a charged particle follows from the curvature of its track, and the energy available for new particles is the invariant mass of the colliding pair ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation|REL §A3.3]]). This section is descriptive.

## Accelerators

> [!definition] Definition §A8.3.1: Particle Accelerators
> A **particle accelerator** raises charged particles to high energies with electric fields, magnetic fields, or both.
> - A **Van de Graaff accelerator** sends ions through a fixed potential difference, built up on a charged sphere; a two-stage (tandem) machine with a 16-MV terminal brings fully stripped oxygen ions to about 100 MeV. Its energies were enough to explore nuclei but not to create new particles.
> - A **linear accelerator** (linac) gives particles a series of synchronized kicks between successive tubes, or rides them on the oscillating field of radio-frequency (RF) cavities; the tubes lengthen as the particles speed up. Linacs bring electrons above 100 MeV (SLAC: 50 GeV) and often serve as the first stage of larger machines.
> - A **cyclotron** bends particles round in a fixed magnetic field and accelerates them twice per turn across the gap between two "dees" ([[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^thm-a7-5-5|EM Theorem §A7.5.5]]); it fails once the particles become relativistic, because their period of revolution then grows with energy.
> - A **synchrotron** keeps particles on a fixed circular orbit by raising the bending field as they gain energy from RF cavities, often with superconducting magnets. Circling charges radiate (**synchrotron radiation**), a loss for the machine and a useful light source for materials and medical research.
> - A **colliding-beam machine** (storage ring) stores two counter-rotating beams and focuses them onto each other at the centre of a detector: the Cornell Electron Storage Ring with electrons and positrons of 4.5–6 GeV; CERN's Large Hadron Collider, 27 km round, with protons of more than 6 TeV per beam.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.4; Tipler §11-7, §12-1*

^def-a8-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.3.1: Momentum from the Radius of a Track
> A particle of charge $q$ moving perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field $B$ on a circle of radius $r$ has momentum
>
> $$
> p = |q|Br ,
> $$
>
> at any speed, with $p = \gamma mv$ the relativistic momentum. For $|q| = e$, in practical units,
>
> $$
> p\ [\mathrm{GeV}/c] = 0.3\,B\ [\mathrm T]\;r\ [\mathrm m].
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.4, eq. (11.2)*

^thm-a8-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The magnetic force $q\vec v\times\vec B$ is perpendicular to $\vec v$, so it does no work ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-1|EM Theorem §A7.2.1]]): the speed, hence $\gamma$, stays constant. The relativistic equation of motion $d(\gamma m\vec v)/dt = q\vec v\times\vec B$ ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-2|REL Def. §A3.1.2]]) then reads $\gamma m\,d\vec v/dt = q\vec v\times\vec B$, the non-relativistic equation with $m$ replaced by $\gamma m$. Its solution is uniform circular motion of radius $r = \gamma mv/(|q|B) = p/(|q|B)$ ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|EM Theorem §A7.2.2]]). In units: $pc = ecBr$, and $ec = (1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm C)(2.998\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s})$; dividing by $1.602\times10^{-10}$ J/GeV gives $pc = 0.2998\,Br$ GeV with $B$ in tesla and $r$ in metres.

^der-a8-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-1|EM Theorem §A7.2.1]], [[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|EM Theorem §A7.2.2]], [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-2|REL Def. §A3.1.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §A8.3.2: Centre-of-Mass Energy
> The **centre-of-mass energy** $W$ of two colliding particles is their total energy in the frame where their total momentum is zero; it equals $c^2$ times the invariant mass of the pair ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^def-a3-3-1|REL Def. §A3.3.1]]). It is the energy available for making new particles: the products can have total rest energy at most $W$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.4*

^def-a8-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.3.2: Centre-of-Mass Energy of a Collision
> For particles of masses $m_1, m_2$, energies $E_1, E_2$ and momenta $\vec p_1, \vec p_2$,
>
> $$
> W^2 = (E_1 + E_2)^2 - |\vec p_1 + \vec p_2|^2c^2 = (m_1c^2)^2 + (m_2c^2)^2 + 2\bigl(E_1E_2 - \vec p_1\cdot\vec p_2\,c^2\bigr).
> $$
>
> - Head-on collision: $W^2 = (m_1c^2)^2 + (m_2c^2)^2 + 2\bigl[E_1E_2 + (p_1c)(p_2c)\bigr]$.
> - Symmetric collider (equal masses and energies, opposite momenta): $W = 2E_{\text{beam}}$.
> - Fixed target ($\vec p_2 = 0$, $E_2 = m_2c^2$): $W^2 = (m_1c^2)^2 + (m_2c^2)^2 + 2E_1m_2c^2$, so at high energy $W$ grows only like $\sqrt{E_1}$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.4, eq. (11.3); Tipler §12-1*

^thm-a8-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The quantity $E_{\text{tot}}^2 - |\vec p_{\text{tot}}|^2c^2$ of a system has the same value in every inertial frame ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-5|REL Theorem §A3.3.5]]). In the centre-of-mass frame $\vec p_{\text{tot}} = 0$ and $E_{\text{tot}} = W$, so $W^2 = (E_1 + E_2)^2 - |\vec p_1 + \vec p_2|^2c^2$ in any frame. Expanding and using $E_i^2 - p_i^2c^2 = (m_ic^2)^2$ gives the second form. Head-on, $\vec p_1\cdot\vec p_2 = -p_1p_2$. In a symmetric collider $\vec p_1 + \vec p_2 = 0$, so $W = E_1 + E_2 = 2E_{\text{beam}}$. With the target at rest, $E_2 = m_2c^2$ and $\vec p_2 = 0$.

^der-a8-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-5|REL Theorem §A3.3.5]], [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|REL Theorem §A3.3.1]], [[§A8.3 Particle Accelerators and Detectors#^def-a8-3-2|Def. §A8.3.2]]

## Detectors

> [!definition] Definition §A8.3.3: Particle Detector
> A **particle detector** records the products of a collision. A modern one surrounds the collision point with layers of **subdetectors**, each measuring one aspect. In the Compact Muon Solenoid (CMS) at the LHC, from the inside out:
> - a **silicon tracker** in a uniform magnetic field records points along each charged track; the fitted radius gives the momentum ([[§A8.3 Particle Accelerators and Detectors#^thm-a8-3-1|Theorem §A8.3.1]]);
> - an **electromagnetic calorimeter** of lead-based crystals absorbs electrons and photons in showers and measures their energy;
> - a **hadron calorimeter** of brass and steel layers with plastic scintillators absorbs and measures hadrons;
> - the solenoid magnet;
> - **muon chambers** between iron slabs that only muons (and neutrinos) penetrate.
>
> Earlier detectors include bubble chambers, which photograph tracks; the Geiger–Müller tube of the PHY 286 lab counts single particles: a fast particle ionizes the low-pressure gas, the freed electrons are accelerated towards a thin central wire held several hundred volts positive, ionize more atoms, and the avalanche gives a countable current pulse.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.4; Tipler §12-3; PHY 286, Radioactivity lab manual*

^def-a8-3-3

> [!model] Model §A8.3.3: Toy Model of an Electromagnetic Shower
> - *Assumes:* in each step through the material, every electron or positron radiates a photon carrying half its energy, and every photon turns into an electron–positron pair sharing its energy equally; the steps continue until the energy per particle falls to a critical value $E_c$, below which other processes absorb the particles.
> - *Gives:* after $n$ steps there are $N = 2^n$ particles, each of energy $E_0/2^n$; the shower stops after $n_{\max} = \log_2(E_0/E_c)$ steps. The number of particles, and so the signal, grows in proportion to $E_0$, while the depth of material needed grows only like $\log E_0$.
> - *Fails:* real showers fluctuate from event to event and the energy split is not exactly in halves; the model gives only the scaling.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.4*

^mod-a8-3-3

> [!remark] Remark: Reconstructing an event
> From each detected particle's momentum $p_i$ and identified mass $m_i$ the detector gets its energy $E_i = \sqrt{(p_ic)^2 + (m_ic^2)^2}$. If every particle is caught, the energies add up to $W$. Neutrinos escape, so energy and momentum missing from the balance reveal them: in 1983 the first W bosons were identified at CERN partly from such missing energy. Chains of decays are reconstructed backwards, like putting a smashed watch together. CMS found the Higgs boson partly through its decays into four muons.
>
> *Source: UP3 §11.4; Tipler §12-2*

^rem-a8-3-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A8.3.1: Reading a Track
> An electron track in a field of 1.5 T curves with radius 1.2 m. By [[§A8.3 Particle Accelerators and Detectors#^thm-a8-3-1|Theorem §A8.3.1]], $p = 0.3(1.5)(1.2) = 0.54$ GeV/$c$. Since $pc = 540$ MeV $\gg m_ec^2 = 0.511$ MeV, the energy is $E = \sqrt{(540)^2 + (0.511)^2}\ \mathrm{MeV} \approx 540$ MeV: almost all kinetic, $K = E - m_ec^2 \approx pc$. The non-relativistic $p^2/2m$ would give $2.9\times10^5$ MeV, wrong by a factor of more than 500. (University Physics sets this problem in a cyclotron, but an ordinary cyclotron cannot bring electrons to such energies: their period grows with $\gamma$ and they fall out of step with the voltage.)
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 11.6*

^ex-a8-3-1

> [!example] Example §A8.3.2: Fixed Target against Collider
> **Making antiprotons.** Baryon number allows $p + p \to p + p + p + \bar p$ ([[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^pr-a8-1-3|Principle §A8.1.3]]). At threshold the four products are at rest in the centre-of-mass frame, so $W = 4m_pc^2$. On a fixed target, [[§A8.3 Particle Accelerators and Detectors#^thm-a8-3-2|Theorem §A8.3.2]] gives
>
> $$
> (4m_pc^2)^2 = 2(m_pc^2)^2 + 2E_1m_pc^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_1 = 7m_pc^2, \qquad K_1 = 6m_pc^2 = 5.6\ \mathrm{GeV}.
> $$
>
> The Bevatron produced 6.2-GeV protons, and the antiproton was found there in 1955. In a symmetric collider each beam needs only $E = W/2 = 2m_pc^2$, a kinetic energy of $m_pc^2 = 0.94$ GeV per beam, a sixth of what the fixed-target beam needs: in a fixed-target collision most of the energy goes into moving the centre of mass.
>
> **Making the upsilon.** A symmetric electron–positron collider makes the $\Upsilon$ meson ($b\bar b$, rest energy 10.58 GeV for the state studied) when $2E_{\text{beam}} = 10.58$ GeV, that is, at a beam energy of 5.29 GeV; the electron masses are negligible. The meson appears as a sharp rise in the number of events at this energy.
>
> *Source: Tipler §12-1, Fig. 12-2; UP3 Example 11.7*
