States have been sorted into rotation multiplets; how do *operators* transform, and what does that say about their matrix elements? Level B never needed the question: it computed matrix elements one at a time, quoted the electric-dipole selection rules from level A ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-2|Theorem §A5.4.2]]), and justified the vector model of the Landé factor by citing the Wigner–Eckart theorem ([[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting#^rem-b8-4-1|Remark: The vector model, and why it is exact]]); level C was promised for it ([[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^rem-b6-3-5|★ Remark: Further couplings]]). This section is the home of all of it: vector operators defined by their rotation law, Cartesian tensors and their reduction, spherical (irreducible) tensors, products of tensors, the Wigner–Eckart theorem with a complete proof, the selection rules that follow, and the projection theorem. The tools are those of [[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients]].

*Notation.* As in [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations]]: active rotations $R$, rotation operators $\mathscr D(R)$, $\mathbf J$ the total angular momentum of whatever system the operators act on. $|\alpha, j\,m\rangle$ is a **standard multiplet**: $J_z$ and $\mathbf J^2$ eigenkets on which $J_\pm$ act with the Condon–Shortley coefficients $\hbar\sqrt{(j\mp m)(j\pm m+1)}$ and leave the other labels $\alpha$ unchanged. Spherical components of a vector are $V_{\pm1} = \mp(V_x \pm iV_y)/\sqrt2$, $V_0 = V_z$; a spherical tensor of rank $k$ is $T^{(k)}_q$, $q = -k, \dots, k$. Clebsch–Gordan coefficients are written $\langle j_1j_2;m_1m_2|j_1j_2;jm\rangle$. $\hbar$ is explicit.

## Vector operators

> [!definition] Definition §C7.2.1: Vector Operator; Scalar Operator
> A triple of operators $\mathbf V = (V_1, V_2, V_3)$ is a **vector operator** if for every rotation $R$
>
> $$
> \mathscr D^\dagger(R)\,V_i\,\mathscr D(R) = \sum_jR_{ij}V_j , \qquad (3.446)
> $$
>
> equivalently, if in every state the expectation values transform like the components of a classical vector, $\langle\alpha|V_i|\alpha\rangle \to \langle\alpha|\mathscr D^\dagger V_i\mathscr D|\alpha\rangle = \sum_jR_{ij}\langle\alpha|V_j|\alpha\rangle$ (3.445). An operator $S$ is a **scalar operator** if $\mathscr D^\dagger(R)\,S\,\mathscr D(R) = S$ for every $R$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.11.1, eqs. (3.444)–(3.446) · the user's 511 notes, §"Vector Operators" · the user's series, Part IV, §16 (how operators transform) · Fowler, Tensor Operators, pp. 3–4*

^def-c7-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.2.1: Commutator Form of the Definition
> $\mathbf V$ is a vector operator if and only if
>
> $$
> [V_i, J_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}V_k , \qquad (3.451)
> $$
>
> and $S$ is a scalar operator if and only if $[S, J_j] = 0$ for $j = 1, 2, 3$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.11.1, eqs. (3.447)–(3.453) · the user's 511 notes, §"Vector Operators", eq. (3.451) · the user's series, Part IV, §16, eq. (vector-def)*

^thm-c7-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Only if.* For an infinitesimal rotation $\mathscr D = 1 - i\epsilon\mathbf J\cdot\hat n/\hbar$ (3.447), (3.446) reads $V_i + \frac{\epsilon}{i\hbar}[V_i, \mathbf J\cdot\hat n] = \sum_jR_{ij}(\hat n;\epsilon)V_j$ (3.448). For $\hat n = \hat z$, $R(\hat z;\epsilon)$ has $-\epsilon$ in position $(1,2)$ and $+\epsilon$ in $(2,1)$ (3.449), so $[V_x, J_z] = -i\hbar V_y$, $[V_y, J_z] = i\hbar V_x$, $[V_z, J_z] = 0$ (3.450); the other axes give the rest of (3.451). A scalar is the case $R_{ij} \to \delta_{ij}$ with one component.
>
> *If.* Fix $\hat n$ and put $W_i(\phi) = \mathscr D^\dagger(\hat n, \phi)V_i\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi)$. Then $\frac{dW_i}{d\phi} = \frac i\hbar\mathscr D^\dagger[\mathbf J\cdot\hat n, V_i]\mathscr D = \frac i\hbar n_k(-i\hbar\epsilon_{ikl})W_l = -\epsilon_{ilk}n_kW_l$ by (3.451). This is the linear equation that [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-5|Theorem §C5.1.5]] solved for $\mathbf J$ itself, now with initial value $\mathbf W(0) = \mathbf V$, so $\mathbf W(\phi) = R(\hat n, \phi)\mathbf V$: (3.446) holds for rotations about any axis, hence for all $R$. Equivalently, Sakurai expands $e^{iJ_j\phi/\hbar}V_ie^{-iJ_j\phi/\hbar}$ in nested commutators (3.452)–(3.453), which by (3.451) keep returning $V_i$ and $V_k$. For a scalar, $[S, J_j] = 0$ makes $S$ commute with every $\mathscr D(R)$.

^der-c7-2-1

*Uses:* [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]], [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|Principle §C5.1.2]], [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-5|Theorem §C5.1.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: Which operators are vectors
> $\mathbf J$ is the first example: (3.451) with $\mathbf V = \mathbf J$ is the angular-momentum algebra itself ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-5|Theorem §C5.1.5]], [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^rem-c5-1-6|Remark: Operators that rotate like J]]). For a spinless particle, $\mathbf x$, $\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf L$ are vectors under $\mathbf L$: $[y, L_z] = i\hbar x$, $[x, L_z] = -i\hbar y$, $[p_x, L_z] = -i\hbar p_y$, $[p_y, L_z] = i\hbar p_x$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]], (4.122)); Sakurai's list prints the first as $[y, L_z] = i\hbar p_x$, which should be $i\hbar x$, as the user's 511 notes have it. With spin, $\mathbf x$, $\mathbf p$, $\mathbf L$, $\mathbf S$ are vectors under $\mathbf J = \mathbf L + \mathbf S$, since $\mathbf S$ commutes with $\mathbf x$, $\mathbf p$, $\mathbf L$. Scalars: $\mathbf x^2$, $\mathbf p^2$, $\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf p$, $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$, and any Hamiltonian with rotational symmetry ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]). In general the dot product of two vector operators is a scalar, $[J_j, \mathbf U\cdot\mathbf V] = i\hbar\epsilon_{jik}(U_kV_i + U_iV_k) = 0$.

^rem-c7-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Two ways of writing the rotation law
> Fowler warns that the rotation law is written both ways. Sakurai's (3.446), used here, rotates the operator as the expectation values rotate: $\mathscr D^\dagger V_i\mathscr D = \sum_jR_{ij}V_j$. Replacing $R$ by $R^{-1} = R^{\mathsf T}$ gives the equivalent $\mathscr D\,V_i\,\mathscr D^\dagger = \sum_jR_{ji}V_j$, the form "in common use" in which the components transform like basis kets. For spherical tensors the two forms are (3.465a) and (3.465b) below, with $\mathscr D^{(k)*}_{qq'}$ in one and $\mathscr D^{(k)}_{q'q}$ in the other. Mixing them inverts every rotation. The commutation relations (3.451) and (3.468) are the same in both conventions, which is one reason to use them as the definition.

## Cartesian and spherical tensors

> [!definition] Definition §C7.2.2: Cartesian Tensor Operator
> A **Cartesian tensor** of rank $n$ is a set of $3^n$ operators $T_{ijk\cdots}$ that transform with one rotation matrix per index,
>
> $$
> \mathscr D^\dagger(R)\,T_{ijk\cdots}\,\mathscr D(R) = \sum_{i'}\sum_{j'}\sum_{k'}\cdots R_{ii'}R_{jj'}R_{kk'}\cdots T_{i'j'k'\cdots} . \qquad (3.454)
> $$
>
> The simplest rank-2 example is the **dyadic** $T_{ij} = U_iV_j$ of two vector operators (3.455).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.11.2, eqs. (3.454)–(3.455) · the user's series, Part IV, §16 (Cartesian tensors) · Fowler, Tensor Operators, pp. 1, 5–6*

^def-c7-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.2.2: Reduction of the Dyadic
> The dyadic of two vector operators splits as
>
> $$
> U_iV_j = \frac{\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf V}{3}\,\delta_{ij} + \frac{U_iV_j - U_jV_i}{2} + \Bigl(\frac{U_iV_j + U_jV_i}{2} - \frac{\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf V}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\Bigr) , \qquad (3.456)
> $$
>
> a scalar, an antisymmetric tensor $\frac12\epsilon_{ijk}(\mathbf U\times\mathbf V)_k$ with 3 independent components, and a symmetric traceless tensor with 5. Each piece is carried into itself by every rotation, so the nine components never mix as nine:
>
> $$
> 3\times3 = 1 + 3 + 5 . \qquad (3.457)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.11.2, eqs. (3.456)–(3.457) · the user's 511 notes, §"Cartesian Tensors Versus Irreducible Tensors" · the user's series, Part IV, §16 · Fowler, Tensor Operators, p. 6*

^thm-c7-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The three terms add up to $U_iV_j$ identically. Write (3.454) for rank 2 as $T \to RTR^{\mathsf T}$, a product of $3\times3$ arrays of operators. The trace part transforms with $R\,\delta\,R^{\mathsf T} = \delta$ (orthogonality, [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^def-c5-1-1|Def. §C5.1.1]]), and $\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf V$ itself is invariant (Remark: Which operators are vectors). If $A^{\mathsf T} = -A$ then $(RAR^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T} = -RAR^{\mathsf T}$, and likewise for symmetric arrays; and $\operatorname{tr}(RSR^{\mathsf T}) = \operatorname{tr}(S)$, so tracelessness is kept. The antisymmetric part is a vector: $\epsilon_{ijk}(\mathbf U\times\mathbf V)_k = \epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{klm}U_lV_m = U_iV_j - U_jV_i$. Counting: an antisymmetric $3\times3$ array has 3 free entries, a symmetric one 6, less 1 for the trace.

^der-c7-2-2

*Uses:* [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^def-c7-2-2|Def. §C7.2.2]], [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]], [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^def-c5-1-1|Def. §C5.1.1]]

The numbers $1, 3, 5$ are the multiplicities $2k + 1$ of $k = 0, 1, 2$: the dyadic behaves under rotations like the product of two spin-1 multiplets, $1\otimes1 = 0\oplus1\oplus2$ ([[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]]). A Cartesian tensor is **reducible**; the irreducible pieces are spherical tensors.

> [!definition] Definition §C7.2.3: Spherical (Irreducible) Tensor Operator
> A **spherical tensor of rank $k$** is a set of $2k + 1$ operators $T^{(k)}_q$, $q = k, k-1, \dots, -k$, that transform among themselves like the multiplet $|k\,q\rangle$:
>
> $$
> \mathscr D^\dagger(R)\,T^{(k)}_q\,\mathscr D(R) = \sum_{q'=-k}^k\mathscr D^{(k)*}_{qq'}(R)\,T^{(k)}_{q'} , \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \mathscr D(R)\,T^{(k)}_q\,\mathscr D^\dagger(R) = \sum_{q'=-k}^k\mathscr D^{(k)}_{q'q}(R)\,T^{(k)}_{q'} . \qquad (3.465)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.11.2, eqs. (3.461)–(3.465) · the user's 511 notes, §"Spherical Tensors" · Fowler, Tensor Operators, p. 7*

^def-c7-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.2.3: Commutator Form of the Spherical-Tensor Law
> A set of $2k + 1$ operators is a spherical tensor of rank $k$ if and only if
>
> $$
> [J_z, T^{(k)}_q] = \hbar q\,T^{(k)}_q, \qquad [J_\pm, T^{(k)}_q] = \hbar\sqrt{(k\mp q)(k\pm q+1)}\;T^{(k)}_{q\pm1} . \qquad (3.468)
> $$
>
> These are the ladder relations $J_z|k\,q\rangle = \hbar q|k\,q\rangle$, $J_\pm|k\,q\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{(k\mp q)(k\pm q+1)}|k,q\pm1\rangle$, with the action on states replaced by the commutator.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.11.2, eqs. (3.466)–(3.468) · the user's 511 notes, §"Commutation Relations for Spherical Tensors" · the user's series, Part IV, §16, eq. (tensor-def) · Fowler, Tensor Operators, p. 7*

^thm-c7-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Only if.* For an infinitesimal rotation (3.465b) becomes
>
> $$
> \Bigl(1 - \frac{i\mathbf J\cdot\hat n\,\epsilon}\hbar\Bigr)T^{(k)}_q\Bigl(1 + \frac{i\mathbf J\cdot\hat n\,\epsilon}\hbar\Bigr) = \sum_{q'}T^{(k)}_{q'}\,\langle k\,q'|\Bigl(1 - \frac{i\mathbf J\cdot\hat n\,\epsilon}\hbar\Bigr)|k\,q\rangle, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad [\mathbf J\cdot\hat n, T^{(k)}_q] = \sum_{q'}T^{(k)}_{q'}\,\langle k\,q'|\mathbf J\cdot\hat n|k\,q\rangle . \qquad (3.466),\ (3.467)
> $$
>
> Both sides are linear in $\hat n$, so complex combinations may be taken: $\hat n \to \hat z$ gives the first relation, $\hat x \pm i\hat y$ (i.e. $J_\pm$) the second, with the matrix elements of $J_z$ and $J_\pm$ in the multiplet $k$.
>
> *If.* Let $X(\phi) = \mathscr D(\hat n, \phi)T_q\mathscr D^\dagger(\hat n, \phi)$ and $Y(\phi) = \sum_{q'}T_{q'}\mathscr D^{(k)}_{q'q}(\hat n, \phi)$, for each $q$. By (3.467), $X' = -\frac i\hbar\mathscr D[\mathbf J\cdot\hat n, T_q]\mathscr D^\dagger = -\frac i\hbar\sum_{q'}X_{q'}\langle k\,q'|\mathbf J\cdot\hat n|k\,q\rangle$, and, since $\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi)$ commutes with $\mathbf J\cdot\hat n$, $Y' = -\frac i\hbar\sum_{q'}T_{q'}\langle k\,q'|\mathscr D\,\mathbf J\cdot\hat n|k\,q\rangle = -\frac i\hbar\sum_{q''}Y_{q''}\langle k\,q''|\mathbf J\cdot\hat n|k\,q\rangle$ (inserting the completeness relation of the multiplet $k$). The two families obey the same linear differential equation with the same value $T_q$ at $\phi = 0$, so $X = Y$, which is (3.465b).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.2.4: Vectors Are Rank-1 Tensors
> $\mathbf V$ is a vector operator if and only if its spherical components
>
> $$
> V_{+1} = -\frac{V_x + iV_y}{\sqrt2}, \qquad V_0 = V_z, \qquad V_{-1} = \frac{V_x - iV_y}{\sqrt2} \qquad (3.459),\ (3.484)
> $$
>
> form a spherical tensor of rank 1. More generally, if the components of $\mathbf V$ commute with one another, $Y^q_k(\mathbf V)$ (the spherical harmonic $r^kY^q_k(\hat{\mathbf r})$, a polynomial in $x, y, z$, with $\mathbf r \to \mathbf V$) is a spherical tensor of rank $k$ (3.458); for example $T^{(2)}_{\pm2} = \sqrt{15/32\pi}\,(V_x \pm iV_y)^2$ (3.460).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.11.2, eqs. (3.458)–(3.464) · the user's 511 notes, §"Spherical Tensors", eqs. (3.458)–(3.460) · the user's series, Part IV, §16, eq. (spherical-components) · Fowler, Tensor Operators, pp. 7–8*

^thm-c7-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Using $[J_i, V_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}V_k$ (Theorem §C7.2.1):
>
> $$
> [J_z, V_{+1}] = -\frac{[J_z, V_x] + i[J_z, V_y]}{\sqrt2} = -\frac{i\hbar V_y + \hbar V_x}{\sqrt2} = \hbar V_{+1}, \qquad [J_+, V_{+1}] = -\frac{[J_x, V_x] + i[J_x, V_y] + i[J_y, V_x] - [J_y, V_y]}{\sqrt2} = -\frac{-\hbar V_z + \hbar V_z}{\sqrt2} = 0,
> $$
>
> $$
> [J_-, V_{+1}] = -\frac{[J_x, V_x] + i[J_x, V_y] - i[J_y, V_x] + [J_y, V_y]}{\sqrt2} = -\frac{-\hbar V_z - \hbar V_z}{\sqrt2} = \sqrt2\,\hbar V_0 ,
> $$
>
> which are (3.468) for $k = 1$, $q = 1$ ($\sqrt{(1+1)(1-1+1)} = \sqrt2$); $q = 0, -1$ go the same way. Conversely $V_x$, $V_y$, $V_z$ are linear combinations of the $V_q$, and (3.468) returns (3.451). For $Y^q_k(\mathbf V)$: the direction kets ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^def-c5-4-1|Def. §C5.4.1]]) satisfy $Y^m_\ell(\hat{\mathbf n}') = \sum_{m'}Y^{m'}_\ell(\hat{\mathbf n})\mathscr D^{(\ell)}_{m'm}(R^{-1})$ for $\hat{\mathbf n}' = R\hat{\mathbf n}$ (3.461)–(3.463); a polynomial in commuting components of a vector operator transforms by the same substitution $\mathbf V \to R\mathbf V$, which gives (3.464), and unitarity of $\mathscr D^{(\ell)}$ turns it into (3.465a).

*Uses:* [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]], [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-3|Theorem §C7.2.3]], [[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^def-c5-4-1|Def. §C5.4.1]]

For a rotation about $y$ the rank-1 law can be checked component by component with the explicit $d^{(1)}(\beta)$ ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-3|Theorem §C5.3.3]]): $\sum_{q'}d^{(1)}_{qq'}(\beta)V_{q'}$ are the spherical components of $R_y(\beta)\mathbf V$, which is [[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^ex-c5-3-2|Example §C5.3.2]] (PHY 511, HW 10, Sakurai Problem 3.42); with (3.465a) this is the statement $\mathscr D^\dagger V_q\mathscr D = (R\mathbf V)_q$. The definition (3.465) needs no product form either: $(U_x + iU_y)(V_x + iV_y)$ is the $q = +2$ component of a rank-2 tensor although it is not $Y^2_2$ of any single vector (Sakurai, after (3.465)). With commuting components the normalization $\sqrt{3/4\pi}$ of (3.459) is a matter of choice: any constant multiple of a tensor is a tensor.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.2.5: Product of Two Tensors
> Let $X^{(k_1)}_{q_1}$ and $Z^{(k_2)}_{q_2}$ be spherical tensors of ranks $k_1$ and $k_2$. Then for each $k = \lvert k_1 - k_2\rvert, \dots, k_1 + k_2$
>
> $$
> T^{(k)}_q = \sum_{q_1}\sum_{q_2}\langle k_1k_2;q_1q_2|k_1k_2;kq\rangle\,X^{(k_1)}_{q_1}Z^{(k_2)}_{q_2} \qquad (3.470)
> $$
>
> is a spherical tensor of rank $k$: tensor operators are combined with exactly the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients that combine angular momenta.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.11.3, Theorem 5, eq. (3.470) · the user's 511 notes, §"Product of Tensors" · the user's series, Part IV, §16*

^thm-c7-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the Leibniz rule for commutators and (3.468) for $X$ and $Z$,
>
> $$
> [J_z, X_{q_1}Z_{q_2}] = \hbar(q_1 + q_2)\,X_{q_1}Z_{q_2}, \qquad [J_\pm, X_{q_1}Z_{q_2}] = \hbar\sqrt{(k_1\mp q_1)(k_1\pm q_1+1)}\,X_{q_1\pm1}Z_{q_2} + \hbar\sqrt{(k_2\mp q_2)(k_2\pm q_2+1)}\,X_{q_1}Z_{q_2\pm1} .
> $$
>
> These are exactly the actions of $J_z = J_{1z} + J_{2z}$ and $J_\pm = J_{1\pm} + J_{2\pm}$ on the product kets $|k_1\,q_1\rangle\otimes|k_2\,q_2\rangle$. So the linear map $\Phi: |k_1\,q_1\rangle\otimes|k_2\,q_2\rangle \mapsto X_{q_1}Z_{q_2}$ turns the action of $\mathbf J$ on the product space into the commutator: $\Phi(J_a v) = [J_a, \Phi(v)]$ for $a = z, \pm$ (an *intertwining* map). The coupled kets $|k_1k_2;kq\rangle$ obey the ladder relations of a rank-$k$ multiplet ([[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^def-c7-1-1|Def. §C7.1.1]]), so their images $T^{(k)}_q = \Phi(|k_1k_2;kq\rangle)$ obey (3.468), and Theorem §C7.2.3 applies. (Sakurai proves it from the finite rotation law instead, with the Clebsch–Gordan series [[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]] and the orthogonality (3.362).)

## The Wigner–Eckart theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.2.6: Wigner–Eckart Theorem
> Let $T^{(k)}_q$ be a spherical tensor of rank $k$ and $|\alpha, j\,m\rangle$, $|\alpha', j'\,m'\rangle$ standard multiplets. Then
>
> $$
> \langle\alpha', j'm'|T^{(k)}_q|\alpha, jm\rangle = \langle jk;mq|jk;j'm'\rangle\,\frac{\langle\alpha'j'\Vert T^{(k)}\Vert\alpha j\rangle}{\sqrt{2j'+1}} , \qquad (3.474)
> $$
>
> where the **reduced (double-bar) matrix element** $\langle\alpha'j'\Vert T^{(k)}\Vert\alpha j\rangle$ is independent of $m$, $m'$ and $q$. Consequently:
> 1. ($m$-selection rule) the matrix element vanishes unless $m' = m + q$ (3.471);
> 2. (triangle rule) it vanishes unless $\lvert j - k\rvert \le j' \le j + k$ (3.475);
> 3. (scalar, $k = 0$) $\langle\alpha', j'm'|S|\alpha, jm\rangle = \delta_{jj'}\delta_{mm'}\langle\alpha'j\Vert S\Vert\alpha j\rangle/\sqrt{2j+1}$: a scalar cannot change $j$ or $m$ (3.481);
> 4. (vector, $k = 1$) $\Delta m = m' - m = 0, \pm1$ and $\Delta j = j' - j = 0, \pm1$, and $j = 0 \to j' = 0$ is forbidden (3.482).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.11.4, eqs. (3.471)–(3.482) · the user's 511 notes, §"Matrix Elements of Tensor Operators: The Wigner-Eckart Theorem" · the user's series, Part IV, §16 (the key observation and the theorem) · Fowler, Tensor Operators, pp. 8–10*

^thm-c7-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Step 1: $T^{(k)}_q|\alpha, j\,m\rangle$ transforms like $|j\,m\rangle\otimes|k\,q\rangle$.* Define the linear map $\Phi: |j\,m\rangle\otimes|k\,q\rangle \mapsto T^{(k)}_q|\alpha, j\,m\rangle$. Since $J_a(T_q|\alpha jm\rangle) = [J_a, T_q]|\alpha jm\rangle + T_q\,J_a|\alpha jm\rangle$, (3.468) and the standard ladder give, for $a = z, \pm$,
>
> $$
> J_a\,\Phi(v) = \Phi\bigl((J_a\otimes1 + 1\otimes J_a)\,v\bigr) ;
> $$
>
> for instance $J_\pm T_q|\alpha jm\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{(k\mp q)(k\pm q+1)}\,T_{q\pm1}|\alpha jm\rangle + \hbar\sqrt{(j\mp m)(j\pm m+1)}\,T_q|\alpha, j, m\pm1\rangle$, which is $\Phi$ of $J_\pm(|jm\rangle\otimes|kq\rangle)$. (This is the user's series' observation: the Leibniz rule reproduces the addition of angular momenta.)
>
> *Step 2: coupled combinations.* For each $J$ put $|\tau; J\,M\rangle = \Phi(|jk;JM\rangle) = \sum_{m,q}\langle jk;mq|jk;JM\rangle\,T_q|\alpha, jm\rangle$. By Step 1 these vectors obey the ladder relations of a multiplet $J$: $J_z|\tau;JM\rangle = M\hbar|\tau;JM\rangle$ and $J_\pm|\tau;JM\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{(J\mp M)(J\pm M+1)}\,|\tau;J,M\pm1\rangle$ (some may be zero). Hence $\mathbf J^2|\tau;JM\rangle = (J_-J_+ + J_z^2 + \hbar J_z)|\tau;JM\rangle = J(J+1)\hbar^2|\tau;JM\rangle$.
>
> *Step 3: overlaps.* Since $J_z$ and $\mathbf J^2$ are Hermitian, $\langle\alpha', j'm'|\tau;JM\rangle = 0$ unless $m' = M$ and $j' = J$. And it does not depend on $M$: for $M < J$, with $c = \hbar\sqrt{(J-M)(J+M+1)} \ne 0$,
>
> $$
> c\,\langle\alpha', J, M{+}1|\tau;J, M{+}1\rangle = \langle\alpha', J, M{+}1|J_+|\tau;JM\rangle = \bigl(J_-|\alpha', J, M{+}1\rangle\bigr)^\dagger|\tau;JM\rangle = c\,\langle\alpha', JM|\tau;JM\rangle .
> $$
>
> So $\langle\alpha', j'm'|\tau;JM\rangle = \delta_{j'J}\delta_{m'M}\,t$, with one number $t$ for given $\alpha', j', \alpha, j, k$.
>
> *Step 4: invert.* By orthogonality of the real Clebsch–Gordan coefficients (3.361), $T_q|\alpha, jm\rangle = \sum_{J,M}\langle jk;mq|jk;JM\rangle\,|\tau;JM\rangle$. Taking the component along $|\alpha', j'm'\rangle$ and Step 3 give $\langle\alpha', j'm'|T_q|\alpha, jm\rangle = \langle jk;mq|jk;j'm'\rangle\,t$; define $\langle\alpha'j'\Vert T^{(k)}\Vert\alpha j\rangle = \sqrt{2j'+1}\,t$.
>
> *Consequences.* 1–2 are the selection rules of the Clebsch–Gordan coefficient ([[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-3|Theorem §B6.3.3]] (1)); 1 also follows directly from $\langle\alpha'j'm'|([J_z, T_q] - \hbar qT_q)|\alpha jm\rangle = [(m' - m)\hbar - \hbar q]\langle\alpha'j'm'|T_q|\alpha jm\rangle = 0$. For 3, $\langle j0;m0|j0;j'm'\rangle = \delta_{jj'}\delta_{mm'}$. For 4, $q = 0, \pm1$, and the triangle $\lvert j - 1\rvert \le j' \le j + 1$ excludes $j' = 0$ when $j = 0$.

> [!remark] Remark: Geometry times dynamics
> The Clebsch–Gordan factor depends only on how the system is oriented relative to the $z$ axis; it makes no reference to the operator. The reduced matrix element carries the dynamics (radial integrals, the labels $\alpha$) and no orientation. So of all the matrix elements with given $\alpha', j', \alpha, j, k$, it suffices to compute one; the others follow from Clebsch–Gordan tables ([[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^rem-c7-1-5|Remark: Tables of Clebsch–Gordan coefficients]]). The integral of three spherical harmonics ([[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-6|Theorem §C7.1.6]]) is the case $T^{(k)}_q = Y^q_k$, $\alpha$ absent.

> [!remark] Remark: Sakurai's proof by recursion
> Sakurai (and the user's 511 notes) sandwich $[J_\pm, T_q] = \hbar\sqrt{(k\mp q)(k\pm q+1)}\,T_{q\pm1}$ between $\langle\alpha', j'm'|$ and $|\alpha, jm\rangle$:
>
> $$
> \sqrt{(j'\pm m')(j'\mp m'+1)}\,\langle\alpha', j', m'\mp1|T_q|\alpha, jm\rangle = \sqrt{(j\mp m)(j\pm m+1)}\,\langle\alpha', j'm'|T_q|\alpha, j, m\pm1\rangle + \sqrt{(k\mp q)(k\pm q+1)}\,\langle\alpha', j'm'|T_{q\pm1}|\alpha, jm\rangle , \qquad (3.477)
> $$
>
> which is the Clebsch–Gordan recursion (3.369) under $j' \to j$, $m' \to m$, $j \to j_1$, $m \to m_1$, $k \to j_2$, $q \to m_2$. Two families solving the same homogeneous linear system are proportional (3.478)–(3.480) provided the solution space is one-dimensional; that is the uniqueness part of [[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-3|Theorem §C7.1.3]], which the series flags as "stated" and Sakurai leaves implicit. The proof above avoids the recursion by working with whole multiplets.

> [!caution] Caution: Conventions for the reduced matrix element
> Only the product of the Clebsch–Gordan factor and the reduced matrix element is physical; how it is split is a convention. Sakurai's (3.474), followed by the user's 511 notes, couples in the order $j\otimes k$ and divides by $\sqrt{2j'+1}$ (consistent with Gottfried and Yan, he says); Edmonds uses a 3j symbol and another factor; Fowler writes Shankar's $T^q_k$ (rank as subscript) and divides by $\sqrt{2j_2+1}$ with the final $j$; the user's series writes $\langle k, q; j, m|j', m'\rangle\,\langle j'\Vert T^{(k)}\Vert j\rangle$, coupling in the order $k\otimes j$ and with no square root. Changing the order of coupling changes the coefficient by $(-1)^{j+k-j'}$ ([[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]), which is absorbed into the reduced element. Reduced matrix elements from two sources can be combined only after converting to one convention.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.2.7: Electric-Dipole Selection Rules
> For a spinless particle in a central potential, the matrix elements of the position operator between energy eigenstates satisfy
>
> $$
> \langle n'\ell'm'|x_q|n\ell m\rangle = 0 \quad\text{unless}\quad m' = m + q \ \text{and}\ \ell' = \ell \pm 1 , \qquad x_{\pm1} = \mp\frac{x \pm iy}{\sqrt2}, \quad x_0 = z :
> $$
>
> $\Delta\ell = \pm1$, $\Delta m = 0, \pm1$, and nothing restricts $n$. For an electron with spin, in states $|n\,\ell\,j\,m_j\rangle$ of total angular momentum, also $\Delta j = 0, \pm1$, with $j = 0 \to 0$ forbidden, and $\Delta m_j = 0, \pm1$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.11.4, eq. (3.482) and the remark after it · the user's series, Part IV, §16 (the payoff in lines: dipole selection rules) · the user's 511 notes, §"Matrix Elements of Tensor Operators: The Wigner-Eckart Theorem", Example 2*

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\mathbf x$ is a vector operator under $\mathbf L$ ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^rem-c7-2-1|Remark: Which operators are vectors]]), so the $x_q$ form a rank-1 tensor (Theorem §C7.2.4), and Theorem §C7.2.6 (4) gives $m' = m + q$ and $\lvert\ell' - \ell\rvert \le 1$. The case $\ell' = \ell$ is excluded by parity. In the position representation $\psi_{n\ell m}(-\mathbf x) = (-1)^\ell\psi_{n\ell m}(\mathbf x)$, because $Y^m_\ell(-\hat{\mathbf r}) = (-1)^\ell Y^m_\ell(\hat{\mathbf r})$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^rem-b5-2-6|★ Remark: Reading the spherical harmonics]]) and $R_{n\ell}$ depends on $r$ only. Substituting $\mathbf x \to -\mathbf x$ in $\int\psi^*_{n'\ell'm'}\,x_q\,\psi_{n\ell m}\,d^3\mathbf x$ leaves the integral unchanged and multiplies the integrand by $(-1)^{\ell' + 1 + \ell}$, so the integral vanishes unless $\ell + \ell'$ is odd. (In ket language this is the parity operator, [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^thm-c8-2-5|Theorem §C8.2.5]] and [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^rem-c8-2-5|Remark: The dipole rules completed]]; the angular integral is also [[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^ex-c7-1-2|Example §C7.1.2]].) With spin, $\mathbf x$ commutes with $\mathbf S$, so $[J_i, x_j] = [L_i, x_j]$ and $\mathbf x$ is a vector under $\mathbf J = \mathbf L + \mathbf S$; Theorem §C7.2.6 (4) in the $|j\,m_j\rangle$ multiplets gives the rules for $j$ and $m_j$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C7.2.8: Projection Theorem
> For a vector operator $\mathbf V$ and $j \ge \frac12$, within one value of $j$
>
> $$
> \langle\alpha', jm'|V_q|\alpha, jm\rangle = \frac{\langle\alpha', jm|\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf V|\alpha, jm\rangle}{\hbar^2j(j+1)}\,\langle jm'|J_q|jm\rangle , \qquad (3.483)
> $$
>
> with $J_{\pm1} = \mp(J_x \pm iJ_y)/\sqrt2 = \mp J_\pm/\sqrt2$, $J_0 = J_z$ (3.484); the factor $\langle\alpha', jm|\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf V|\alpha, jm\rangle$ does not depend on $m$. Within a multiplet of fixed $j$ the matrix elements of every vector operator are proportional to those of $\mathbf J$: only "the component of $\mathbf V$ along $\mathbf J$" survives.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.11.4, eqs. (3.483)–(3.488) · the user's 511 notes, §"The Projection Theorem"*

^thm-c7-2-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In spherical components a dot product is $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B = \sum_q(-1)^qA_qB_{-q} = A_0B_0 - A_{+1}B_{-1} - A_{-1}B_{+1}$. So
>
> $$
> \langle\alpha', jm|\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf V|\alpha, jm\rangle = m\hbar\,\langle\alpha', jm|V_0|\alpha, jm\rangle + \frac\hbar{\sqrt2}\sqrt{(j+m)(j-m+1)}\,\langle\alpha', j, m{-}1|V_{-1}|\alpha, jm\rangle - \frac\hbar{\sqrt2}\sqrt{(j-m)(j+m+1)}\,\langle\alpha', j, m{+}1|V_{+1}|\alpha, jm\rangle \qquad (3.485)
> $$
>
> (letting $J_{\mp1}$ act to the left on $\langle\alpha', jm|$). By Theorem §C7.2.6 each matrix element of $V_q$ is a Clebsch–Gordan coefficient times $\langle\alpha'j\Vert\mathbf V\Vert\alpha j\rangle$, so the whole is $c_{jm}\langle\alpha'j\Vert\mathbf V\Vert\alpha j\rangle$ with $c_{jm}$ independent of $\alpha$, $\alpha'$ and $\mathbf V$; and since $\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf V$ is a scalar (Remark: Which operators are vectors), Theorem §C7.2.6 (3) makes the left side independent of $m$, so $c_{jm} = c_j$. Taking $\mathbf V = \mathbf J$, $\alpha' = \alpha$: $j(j+1)\hbar^2 = c_j\langle\alpha j\Vert\mathbf J\Vert\alpha j\rangle$ (3.486), which is non-zero for $j \ge \frac12$. Applying Theorem §C7.2.6 to $V_q$ and to $J_q$ (whose matrix elements between different $\alpha$ vanish),
>
> $$
> \frac{\langle\alpha', jm'|V_q|\alpha, jm\rangle}{\langle\alpha, jm'|J_q|\alpha, jm\rangle} = \frac{\langle\alpha'j\Vert\mathbf V\Vert\alpha j\rangle}{\langle\alpha j\Vert\mathbf J\Vert\alpha j\rangle} = \frac{\langle\alpha', jm|\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf V|\alpha, jm\rangle}{\hbar^2j(j+1)} \qquad (3.487)
> $$
>
> whenever the denominator is non-zero; when it vanishes, so does the numerator (the same Clebsch–Gordan coefficient). This is (3.488) = (3.483).

> [!example] Example §C7.2.1: Tensors Built from Two Vectors
> Let $\mathbf U$ and $\mathbf V$ be vector operators with spherical components $U_q$, $V_q$. (a) What are $T^{(2)}_{\pm2}$ of the rank-2 tensor built from them? (b) Find the other components with lowering operators and Clebsch–Gordan coefficients. (c) Write $T^{(2)}_0$ in Cartesian components. (d) Do the same for ranks 0 and 1, and compare with Sakurai's (3.469).
>
> *(a)* In (3.470) with $k_1 = k_2 = 1$, $k = 2$, $q = 2$ only $q_1 = q_2 = 1$ contributes, with coefficient $1$: $T^{(2)}_{\pm2} = U_{\pm1}V_{\pm1}$ (for $q = -2$ by Theorem §C7.1.4 (2), the sign being $(-1)^{1+1-2} = +1$).
>
> *(b)* Lower with $[J_-, \cdot\,]$: on the left $[J_-, T^{(2)}_q] = \hbar\sqrt{(2+q)(3-q)}\,T^{(2)}_{q-1}$, on the right the Leibniz rule and $[J_-, V_q] = \hbar\sqrt{(1+q)(2-q)}\,V_{q-1}$, i.e. $\sqrt2\hbar$ for $q = 1, 0$:
>
> $$
> 2\hbar\,T^{(2)}_1 = [J_-, U_1V_1] = \sqrt2\hbar\,(U_0V_1 + U_1V_0) \;\Rightarrow\; T^{(2)}_{\pm1} = \frac{U_{\pm1}V_0 + U_0V_{\pm1}}{\sqrt2},
> $$
>
> $$
> \sqrt6\hbar\,T^{(2)}_0 = \frac{[J_-, U_0V_1 + U_1V_0]}{\sqrt2} = \hbar\,(U_{-1}V_1 + 2U_0V_0 + U_1V_{-1}) \;\Rightarrow\; T^{(2)}_0 = \frac{U_1V_{-1} + 2U_0V_0 + U_{-1}V_1}{\sqrt6} ,
> $$
>
> the coefficients of the $1\otimes1$ table's $J = 2$ column ([[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^rem-c7-1-5|Remark: Tables of Clebsch–Gordan coefficients]]).
>
> *(c)* $U_1V_{-1} + U_{-1}V_1 = -\frac12\bigl[(U_x + iU_y)(V_x - iV_y) + (U_x - iU_y)(V_x + iV_y)\bigr] = -(U_xV_x + U_yV_y)$, keeping the order $U$ before $V$. So
>
> $$
> T^{(2)}_0 = \frac{2U_zV_z - U_xV_x - U_yV_y}{\sqrt6} = \frac{3U_zV_z - \mathbf U\cdot\mathbf V}{\sqrt6} .
> $$
>
> For $\mathbf U = \mathbf V = \mathbf r$ this is $(3z^2 - r^2)/\sqrt6$, proportional to $r^2Y^0_2$ (Sakurai's check after (3.469)).
>
> *(d)* From the $J = 0$ and $J = 1$ columns of the same table: $T^{(0)}_0 = \frac{U_1V_{-1} - U_0V_0 + U_{-1}V_1}{\sqrt3} = -\frac{\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf V}{\sqrt3}$, and $T^{(1)}_0 = \frac{U_1V_{-1} - U_{-1}V_1}{\sqrt2} = \frac{i(\mathbf U\times\mathbf V)_z}{\sqrt2}$, in general $T^{(1)}_q = \frac{i}{\sqrt2}(\mathbf U\times\mathbf V)_q$. Sakurai's (3.469) lists $T^{(0)}_0 = -\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf V/3$ and $T^{(1)}_q = (\mathbf U\times\mathbf V)_q/i\sqrt2$, and the rank-2 components as above. The rank-2 entries are the products (3.470); the rank-0 and rank-1 entries are tensors too, but differ from (3.470) by the constant factors $1/\sqrt3$ and $-1$. Any constant multiple of a tensor is a tensor, so (3.469) is correct as a list of tensors (Sakurai does not derive it from (3.470)); it just does not carry the Clebsch–Gordan normalization. This is the $3\times3 = 1 + 3 + 5$ of Theorem §C7.2.2 in spherical language.
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 10, Problem 1 (and Sakurai Problem 3.43); solved here · Sakurai §3.11.3, eq. (3.469)*

> [!example] Example §C7.2.2: Dipole Matrix Elements between 1s and 2p
> For hydrogen, use the Wigner–Eckart theorem to relate the matrix elements $\langle2\,1\,m'|x_q|1\,0\,0\rangle$, and evaluate them.
>
> *Geometry.* With $j = 0$, $k = 1$, $j' = 1$, the coefficient in (3.474) is $\langle0\,1;0\,q|0\,1;1\,m'\rangle = \delta_{m'q}$ (coupling to zero angular momentum changes nothing). So all nonzero elements are equal:
>
> $$
> \langle2\,1\,{+1}|x_{+1}|1\,0\,0\rangle = \langle2\,1\,0|z|1\,0\,0\rangle = \langle2\,1\,{-1}|x_{-1}|1\,0\,0\rangle = \frac{\langle2\,1\Vert\mathbf x\Vert1\,0\rangle}{\sqrt3},
> $$

> and all others vanish. Also $\langle2\,0\,0|z|1\,0\,0\rangle = 0$ (Theorem §C7.2.7; here even the triangle rule forbids $0 \to 0$).
>
> *Dynamics: one integral.* With $R_{10} = 2a_0^{-3/2}e^{-r/a_0}$, $R_{21} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt6}a_0^{-3/2}\frac r{a_0}e^{-r/2a_0}$ ([[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^rem-c6-2-2|Remark: What this closes at level B]]) and $z = r\cos\theta$, $\cos\theta\,Y^0_0 = Y^0_1/\sqrt3$:
>
> $$
> \langle2\,1\,0|z|1\,0\,0\rangle = \frac1{\sqrt3}\int_0^\infty R_{21}\,r\,R_{10}\,r^2dr = \frac1{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{128\sqrt6}{243}\,a_0 = \frac{128\sqrt2}{243}\,a_0 = 0.745\,a_0 .
> $$

> [!example] Example §C7.2.3: The Landé g Factor for Any Multiplet
> An atomic level with orbital angular momentum $L$, spin $S$ and total $J$ (one electron, or a whole $LS$-coupled term) is placed in a weak field $B\hat z$. With the Zeeman Hamiltonian $H_B = \frac{\mu_B B}\hbar(L_z + 2S_z) = \frac{\mu_B B}\hbar(J_z + S_z)$, $\mu_B = e\hbar/2m_ec$ in Gaussian units ($e\hbar/2m_e$ in SI; [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^cau-c6-2-1|Caution: Gaussian and SI units]]), find the first-order splitting.
>
> To first order the shift is $\langle J_z + S_z\rangle$ in the states $|\alpha; L\,S\,J\,M\rangle$, which are good states for a weak field ([[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]]). $\mathbf S$ is a vector operator under $\mathbf J$, so by the projection theorem
>
> $$
> \langle S_z\rangle = \frac{\langle\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf S\rangle}{\hbar^2J(J+1)}\,M\hbar, \qquad \mathbf J\cdot\mathbf S = \tfrac12\bigl(\mathbf J^2 + \mathbf S^2 - \mathbf L^2\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> and therefore
>
> $$
> \Delta E = g_J\,\mu_BB\,M, \qquad g_J = 1 + \frac{J(J+1) + S(S+1) - L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)} .
> $$
>
> For one electron ($S = \frac12$) this is level B's [[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting#^def-b8-4-2|Def. §B8.4.2]], and $\langle S_z\rangle = \pm M\hbar/(2\ell + 1)$ for $J = \ell\pm\frac12$, which Sakurai obtains both from the $\ell\otimes\frac12$ coefficients ([[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-5|Theorem §C7.1.5]]) and from the projection theorem ((5.140), (5.142)). The projection theorem needs no coefficients and works for any $S$: a ${}^3P_1$ level ($L = S = J = 1$) has $g_J = 1 + \frac{2 + 2 - 2}{4} = \frac32$, a ${}^3P_2$ level $g_J = 1 + \frac{6 + 2 - 2}{12} = \frac32$, a ${}^3P_0$ level is not split. This is the precise content of the vector model ([[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting#^rem-b8-4-1|Remark: The vector model, and why it is exact]]): within a multiplet, $\mathbf S$ acts as its projection on $\mathbf J$. (Sakurai's (5.136c) writes $-eB/2m_ec$ with $e$ the electron's charge, which is negative.)
