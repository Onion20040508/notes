Level A postulated the electron's spin with its two values $S_z = \pm\hbar/2$ and its magnitude $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\hbar$ ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^pr-a5-2-6|Principle §A5.2.6]]), read off from the two beams of the Stern–Gerlach experiment ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^law-a5-2-5|Law §A5.2.5]]). This section asks what kind of object spin is in the formalism of Chapter B2. The answer: an angular momentum in the sense of the commutation relations of [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics]], acting on a space of its own. Everything level A assumed about spin $\tfrac12$ then follows from those relations, and the spin of one particle becomes the simplest quantum system there is: a two-dimensional state space, with $2\times2$ matrices for observables. New here are spinors, the Pauli matrices, measurement of spin along any axis, and the matrices for any spin $s$.

## Spin as an angular momentum

> [!principle] Principle §B6.1.1: Spin
> Besides any orbital angular momentum $\mathbf L = \mathbf r\times\mathbf p$, a particle may carry an **intrinsic angular momentum** $\mathbf S$, its **spin**. The components $S_x, S_y, S_z$ are Hermitian operators that obey the fundamental commutation relations of angular momentum,
>
> $$
> [S_x, S_y] = i\hbar S_z, \qquad [S_y, S_z] = i\hbar S_x, \qquad [S_z, S_x] = i\hbar S_y .
> $$
>
> They act on a **spin space** of their own, not on functions of position: they commute with $\hat{\mathbf r}$, $\hat{\mathbf p}$ and $\mathbf L$. Every particle species has a fixed, immutable **spin quantum number** $s$: in every state of the particle, $S^2 = s(s+1)\hbar^2$. Electrons, protons, neutrons and quarks have $s = \tfrac12$; photons $s = 1$; $\pi$ mesons $s = 0$; $\Delta$ baryons $s = \tfrac32$.
>
> *Domain: nonrelativistic quantum mechanics, where spin is an independent postulate and the value of $s$ is an input for each species. That the electron has $s = \tfrac12$ (and its magnetic moment, §B6.2) follows from the relativistic Dirac equation (level C).*
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4, eq. (4.134) and footnote 36; Greensite ch. 13, eqs. (13.12)–(13.15)*

^pr-b6-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.2: Spin States
> The common eigenstates $|s\,m\rangle$ of $S^2$ and $S_z$ satisfy
>
> $$
> S^2|s\,m\rangle = \hbar^2 s(s+1)\,|s\,m\rangle, \qquad S_z|s\,m\rangle = \hbar m\,|s\,m\rangle, \qquad S_\pm|s\,m\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{s(s+1) - m(m\pm1)}\;|s\,(m\pm1)\rangle ,
> $$
>
> with $S_\pm = S_x \pm iS_y$ and
>
> $$
> s = 0, \tfrac12, 1, \tfrac32, \dots; \qquad m = -s, -s+1, \dots, s-1, s .
> $$
>
> The spin space of a particle of spin $s$ has dimension $2s+1$, with orthonormal basis $|s\,s\rangle, |s\,(s-1)\rangle, \dots, |s\,(-s)\rangle$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4, eqs. (4.135)–(4.137); Greensite ch. 13, eqs. (13.13), (13.43)*

^thm-b6-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The spectrum of any angular momentum ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^def-b5-2-2|Def. §B5.2.2]]) is derived from the commutation relations and Hermiticity alone, with ladder operators: $j(j+1)\hbar^2$ and $m\hbar$ with $m$ in integer steps from $-j$ to $j$, $j \in \{0, \tfrac12, 1, \dots\}$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]]), and the ladder action with the positive coefficients ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-5|Theorem §B5.2.5]]). Principle §B6.1.1 supplies exactly these hypotheses for $\mathbf S$, so the same results hold with $s$ in place of $j$. For orbital angular momentum the half-integer values are excluded by a further condition on the eigenfunctions $Y_\ell^m(\theta, \phi) \propto e^{im\phi}$ as functions on the sphere ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^rem-b5-2-5|Remark: Half-integers, single-valuedness and spin]]). Spin states are not functions of $\theta$ and $\phi$ at all, so nothing excludes $s = \tfrac12, \tfrac32, \dots$. Finally, $S^2 = s(s+1)\hbar^2$ on the whole spin space, so the space is spanned by the $2s+1$ states $|s\,m\rangle$, which are orthonormal as eigenvectors of the Hermitian $S_z$ with distinct eigenvalues ([[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-12|LADR Thm. 7.12]]).

> [!remark] Remark: What level A assumed, and what is now derived
> [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^pr-a5-2-6|Principle §A5.2.6]] assumed that spin is "quantized like orbital angular momentum" with $s = \tfrac12$: magnitude $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\hbar$, two values $m_s = \pm\tfrac12$. At level B the assumption shrinks to the commutation relations and the value of $s$; the magnitude, the two values of $S_z$, and the fact that there are exactly two states follow from Theorem §B6.1.2 with $s = \tfrac12$. Spin is not the rotation of a small charged ball: the electron is, as far as is known, a structureless point, and a ball of the classical electron radius would need an equatorial speed far above $c$ to carry $\hbar/2$ (Griffiths Problem 4.28). The other half of the level-A principle, the spin magnetic moment with $g_s \approx 2$, stays an input; it enters in [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field]].

## Spin one-half: spinors and matrices

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.1: Spinor; Spin Up and Spin Down
> For $s = \tfrac12$ the spin space is two-dimensional, with basis $|\tfrac12\,\tfrac12\rangle$, **spin up** ($\uparrow$), and $|\tfrac12\,(-\tfrac12)\rangle$, **spin down** ($\downarrow$). In this basis a spin state is represented by a two-component column, a **spinor**,
>
> $$
> \chi = \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix} = a\chi_+ + b\chi_-, \qquad \chi_+ = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \chi_- = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},
> $$
>
> with $a, b \in \mathbb C$ and the normalisation $\chi^\dagger\chi = |a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1$. Operators on spin space are represented by $2\times2$ matrices (written $\mathsf S_x$, …, when the distinction matters), and $\langle Q\rangle = \chi^\dagger\mathsf Q\chi$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.1, eqs. (4.139)–(4.141), (4.150); Greensite §13.1, eqs. (13.39)–(13.42)*

^def-b6-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Vectors and their components
> A ket $|\tfrac12\,\tfrac12\rangle$ is a vector of the spin space; the spinor $(1, 0)^{\mathsf T}$ is the list of its components in a chosen basis, and a matrix $\mathsf S_z$ represents the operator $S_z$ in that basis (Griffiths footnote 41). Writing $S_z = \frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$ is the usual, harmless shorthand, but in another basis (the eigenbasis of $S_x$, say) the same operator has a different matrix. The general rules for this correspondence are those of [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation]].

^cau-b6-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.3: Spin Matrices for Spin One-Half
> In the basis $\chi_+, \chi_-$,
>
> $$
> \mathsf S^2 = \frac34\hbar^2\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
> \mathsf S_z = \frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
> \mathsf S_+ = \hbar\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
> \mathsf S_- = \hbar\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
> $$
>
> $$
> \mathsf S_x = \frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
> \mathsf S_y = \frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> $\mathsf S_x$, $\mathsf S_y$, $\mathsf S_z$ and $\mathsf S^2$ are Hermitian; $\mathsf S_\pm$ are not ($\mathsf S_+^\dagger = \mathsf S_-$), so they are not observables.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.1, eqs. (4.142)–(4.147); Greensite §13.1, eq. (13.45)*

^thm-b6-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The $j$-th column of the matrix of an operator is the spinor of its action on the $j$-th basis vector. By Theorem §B6.1.2 with $s = \tfrac12$: $S^2\chi_\pm = \tfrac34\hbar^2\chi_\pm$ and $S_z\chi_\pm = \pm\tfrac\hbar2\chi_\pm$, which give the diagonal matrices. The ladder action gives $S_+\chi_- = \hbar\sqrt{\tfrac34 + \tfrac14}\,\chi_+ = \hbar\chi_+$, $S_-\chi_+ = \hbar\chi_-$, and $S_+\chi_+ = S_-\chi_- = 0$ (the square root vanishes at $m = \pm s$), hence $\mathsf S_\pm$. Finally $S_x = \tfrac12(S_+ + S_-)$ and $S_y = \tfrac1{2i}(S_+ - S_-)$. Hermiticity is read off: each matrix equals its conjugate transpose ([[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-7|LADR Def. 7.7]], [[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-10|LADR Def. 7.10]]).

^der-b6-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]], [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^def-b6-1-1|Def. §B6.1.1]], [[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-7|LADR Def. 7.7]], [[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-10|LADR Def. 7.10]]

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.2: Pauli Matrices
> The **Pauli spin matrices** are
>
> $$
> \sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix},
> $$
>
> so that $\mathbf S = \frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\sigma$ for spin $\tfrac12$, with $\boldsymbol\sigma = (\sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z)$. For a vector $\mathbf a$, $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf a = a_x\sigma_x + a_y\sigma_y + a_z\sigma_z$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.1, eq. (4.148); Greensite §13.1, eqs. (13.46)–(13.47)*

^def-b6-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.4: Algebra of the Pauli Matrices
> With $j, k, l \in \{x, y, z\}$ and the Levi-Civita symbol $\epsilon_{jkl}$:
> 1. **Product rule:** $\sigma_j\sigma_k = \delta_{jk}\,\mathbb 1 + i\sum_l \epsilon_{jkl}\,\sigma_l$. In particular $\sigma_j^2 = \mathbb 1$, $\sigma_x\sigma_y = i\sigma_z$ (and cyclically), and $\sigma_j\sigma_k = -\sigma_k\sigma_j$ for $j \ne k$.
> 2. **Commutators:** $[\sigma_j, \sigma_k] = 2i\sum_l\epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l$, so $\mathbf S = \frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\sigma$ obeys the relations of Principle §B6.1.1.
> 3. **Vector form:** $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf a)(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf b) = (\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)\,\mathbb 1 + i\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)$ for vectors with commuting components; for a unit vector $\hat n$, $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n)^2 = \mathbb 1$.
> 4. **Basis:** each $\sigma_j$ is Hermitian with trace $0$ and eigenvalues $\pm1$; the four matrices $\mathbb 1, \sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z$ form a basis of the $2\times2$ complex matrices, and every Hermitian $2\times2$ matrix is $a_0\mathbb 1 + \mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with $a_0, \mathbf a$ real.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 4.29, eq. (4.153); Greensite §13.1*

^thm-b6-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Multiply the matrices: $\sigma_x^2 = \sigma_y^2 = \sigma_z^2 = \mathbb 1$ by inspection, and
>
> $$
> \sigma_x\sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{pmatrix} = i\sigma_z, \qquad
> \sigma_y\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} -i & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix} = -i\sigma_z ;
> $$
>
> the products $\sigma_y\sigma_z = i\sigma_x$, $\sigma_z\sigma_x = i\sigma_y$ and their reverses follow the same way. These nine products are the product rule. *2.* Subtract $\sigma_k\sigma_j$ from $\sigma_j\sigma_k$: the $\delta_{jk}$ terms cancel and the $\epsilon$ terms double, since $\epsilon_{kjl} = -\epsilon_{jkl}$. Multiplying by $(\hbar/2)^2$ gives $[S_j, S_k] = i\hbar\sum_l\epsilon_{jkl}S_l$. *3.* $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf a)(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf b) = \sum_{j,k}a_jb_k\sigma_j\sigma_k = \sum_j a_jb_j\,\mathbb 1 + i\sum_l\bigl(\sum_{j,k}\epsilon_{jkl}a_jb_k\bigr)\sigma_l$, and the bracket is $(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)_l$. With $\mathbf a = \mathbf b = \hat n$ the cross product vanishes. *4.* Hermiticity and the trace are read off; $\sigma_j^2 = \mathbb 1$ forces eigenvalues $\pm1$, and trace $0$ forces one of each. A general matrix $\begin{pmatrix} p & q \\ r & t\end{pmatrix}$ equals $\tfrac{p+t}2\mathbb 1 + \tfrac{q+r}2\sigma_x + \tfrac{i(q-r)}2\sigma_y + \tfrac{p-t}2\sigma_z$, uniquely, so the four matrices are a basis; the coefficients are real exactly when $t, p$ are real and $r = \bar q$, that is, when the matrix is Hermitian.

## Measuring spin along any axis

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.5: Spin Along Any Axis
> For a unit vector $\hat r = (\sin\theta\cos\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\theta)$, the spin component along $\hat r$ is represented by
>
> $$
> \mathsf S_r = \hat r\cdot\mathsf S = \frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & e^{-i\phi}\sin\theta \\ e^{i\phi}\sin\theta & -\cos\theta \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> Its eigenvalues are $\pm\hbar/2$ for every direction, with normalised eigenspinors
>
> $$
> \chi^{(r)}_+ = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\theta/2) \\ e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2) \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \chi^{(r)}_- = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\phi}\sin(\theta/2) \\ -\cos(\theta/2) \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> In particular the eigenspinors of $\mathsf S_x$ ($\theta = \pi/2$, $\phi = 0$) and $\mathsf S_y$ ($\theta = \phi = \pi/2$) are, up to phases,
>
> $$
> \chi^{(x)}_\pm = \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \pm1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \chi^{(y)}_\pm = \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \pm i \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> A measurement of $S_r$ on the state $\chi$ gives $\pm\hbar/2$ with probability $\bigl|\chi^{(r)\dagger}_\pm\chi\bigr|^2$ and leaves the particle in $\chi^{(r)}_\pm$. For $S_z$ these probabilities are $|a|^2$ and $|b|^2$; for $S_x$, writing $\chi = \frac{a+b}{\sqrt2}\chi^{(x)}_+ + \frac{a-b}{\sqrt2}\chi^{(x)}_-$, they are $\tfrac12|a+b|^2$ and $\tfrac12|a-b|^2$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.1, eqs. (4.149)–(4.152), Problems 4.32–4.33, eqs. (4.154)–(4.155); Greensite §13.1, eqs. (13.64)–(13.79)*

^thm-b6-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Matrix.* $\mathsf S_r = \sin\theta\cos\phi\,\mathsf S_x + \sin\theta\sin\phi\,\mathsf S_y + \cos\theta\,\mathsf S_z$; the off-diagonal entries are $\frac\hbar2\sin\theta(\cos\phi \mp i\sin\phi) = \frac\hbar2\sin\theta\,e^{\mp i\phi}$.
>
> *Eigenvalues.* $\mathsf S_r = \frac\hbar2\,\hat r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ and $(\hat r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2 = \mathbb 1$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]), so $\mathsf S_r^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}4\mathbb 1$ and every eigenvalue $\lambda$ satisfies $\lambda^2 = \hbar^2/4$; the trace is $0$, so both signs occur. Equivalently, the characteristic equation $\det(\mathsf S_r - \lambda\mathbb 1) = \lambda^2 - \hbar^2/4 = 0$ ([[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-51|LADR Thm. 9.51]]); Griffiths does this for $\mathsf S_x$.
>
> *Eigenspinors.* Apply $\frac2\hbar\mathsf S_r$ to $\chi^{(r)}_+$. The first component is $\cos\theta\cos\frac\theta2 + \sin\theta\sin\frac\theta2 = \cos\bigl(\theta - \frac\theta2\bigr) = \cos\frac\theta2$; the second is $e^{i\phi}\bigl(\sin\theta\cos\frac\theta2 - \cos\theta\sin\frac\theta2\bigr) = e^{i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2$. So $\chi^{(r)}_+$ has eigenvalue $+\hbar/2$; the same two identities give $-\hbar/2$ for $\chi^{(r)}_-$. Both are normalised, and they are orthogonal, as eigenvectors of a Hermitian matrix with different eigenvalues must be. For $\theta = \pi/2$: $\phi = 0$ gives $(1, \pm1)/\sqrt2$; $\phi = \pi/2$ gives $(1, i)/\sqrt2$ and $(-i, -1)/\sqrt2 = -i\,(1, -i)/\sqrt2$.
>
> *Probabilities.* The two eigenspinors form an orthonormal basis ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]), so $\chi = c_+\chi^{(r)}_+ + c_-\chi^{(r)}_-$ with $c_\pm = \chi^{(r)\dagger}_\pm\chi$, and the generalized statistical interpretation ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation]]) gives the probabilities $|c_\pm|^2$ and the collapse. For $S_x$: $c_\pm = (a \pm b)/\sqrt2$.

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-3|Theorem §B6.1.3]], [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]], [[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-51|LADR Thm. 9.51]], [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]], [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation]]

> [!remark] Remark: A particle in $\chi_+$ has no $x$-component of spin
> Prepare $\chi_+$. A measurement of $S_z$ gives $+\hbar/2$ with certainty; a measurement of $S_x$ gives $\pm\hbar/2$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each. This does not mean that $S_x$ had a value nobody knew: the state is known exactly, and it is not an eigenstate of $S_x$. After an $S_x$ measurement giving $+\hbar/2$ the particle is in $\chi^{(x)}_+$, and a second measurement of $S_z$ now gives $\pm\hbar/2$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each: the first value of $S_z$ has been erased. Since $[S_x, S_z] = -i\hbar S_y \ne 0$, no state is an eigenstate of both, and the generalized uncertainty principle ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle]]) gives
>
> $$
> \sigma_{S_x}\sigma_{S_y} \ge \frac\hbar2\,\bigl|\langle S_z\rangle\bigr| \quad\text{and cyclically}.
> $$
>
> Griffiths recommends the spin-$\tfrac12$ system as the cleanest place to think through such conceptual questions; Stern–Gerlach magnets in sequence realise the scenario ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.1, pp. 216–217*

^rem-b6-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.6: Every Spin-One-Half State Points Somewhere
> 1. Every normalised spinor $\chi$ equals, up to a phase factor, $\chi^{(r)}_+$ for exactly one direction $\hat r$ (for exactly one point of the unit sphere): every spin-$\tfrac12$ state is "spin up along some axis".
> 2. For $\chi = (a, b)^{\mathsf T}$,
>
> $$
> \langle S_x\rangle = \hbar\operatorname{Re}(\bar a b), \qquad \langle S_y\rangle = \hbar\operatorname{Im}(\bar a b), \qquad \langle S_z\rangle = \frac\hbar2\bigl(|a|^2 - |b|^2\bigr), \qquad \langle S_x^2\rangle = \langle S_y^2\rangle = \langle S_z^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}4 ,
> $$
>
> and for $\chi = \chi^{(r)}_+$ this is $\langle\mathbf S\rangle = \frac\hbar2\,\hat r$. So $|\langle\mathbf S\rangle| = \hbar/2$ for every state, and $\langle S_x^2\rangle + \langle S_y^2\rangle + \langle S_z^2\rangle = \tfrac34\hbar^2 = \langle S^2\rangle$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 4.31, 4.33*

^thm-b6-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Write $a = |a|e^{i\eta}$ and factor out $e^{i\eta}$: $\chi = e^{i\eta}(|a|, b\,e^{-i\eta})^{\mathsf T}$. Since $|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1$, there is a unique $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ with $|a| = \cos(\theta/2)$, $|b| = \sin(\theta/2)$, and for $0 < \theta < \pi$ a unique $\phi \in [0, 2\pi)$ with $b\,e^{-i\eta} = e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)$. Then $\chi = e^{i\eta}\chi^{(r)}_+$. At $\theta = 0$ or $\pi$ the direction is $\pm\hat z$ and $\phi$ is irrelevant.
>
> *2.* With [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-3|Theorem §B6.1.3]], $\chi^\dagger\mathsf S_x\chi = \frac\hbar2(\bar a b + \bar b a) = \hbar\operatorname{Re}(\bar ab)$, $\chi^\dagger\mathsf S_y\chi = \frac\hbar2(-i\bar ab + i\bar ba) = \hbar\operatorname{Im}(\bar ab)$, and $\chi^\dagger\mathsf S_z\chi = \frac\hbar2(|a|^2 - |b|^2)$. Each $\mathsf S_j^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}4\mathbb 1$. For $\chi^{(r)}_+$, $\bar ab = \cos\frac\theta2\sin\frac\theta2\,e^{i\phi} = \tfrac12\sin\theta\,e^{i\phi}$ and $|a|^2 - |b|^2 = \cos\theta$, which gives $\frac\hbar2\hat r$. A global phase does not change expectation values.

^der-b6-1-6

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-3|Theorem §B6.1.3]], [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Spin one-half as a sphere of directions
> Theorem §B6.1.6 makes spin $\tfrac12$ unexpectedly classical-looking: the physical states (spinors up to phase) correspond one to one to the points of a sphere (the **Bloch sphere**, a name not used by the sources), each state being "spin up along $\hat r$". What is not classical is the measurement: along any axis other than $\hat r$ one finds only $\pm\hbar/2$, with probabilities $\cos^2(\vartheta/2)$ and $\sin^2(\vartheta/2)$, where $\vartheta$ is the angle between the two axes. The half-angles reflect that $\chi^{(r)}_+$ and $\chi^{(-r)}_+ = \chi^{(r)}_-$, opposite on the sphere, are orthogonal. Larmor precession in [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field]] is the motion of this point.

## Spin and space together

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.3: State of a Particle with Spin One-Half
> The state of a spin-$\tfrac12$ particle that moves in space is a two-component wave function
>
> $$
> \Psi(\mathbf r, t) = \psi_+(\mathbf r, t)\,\chi_+ + \psi_-(\mathbf r, t)\,\chi_- = \begin{pmatrix} \psi_+(\mathbf r, t) \\ \psi_-(\mathbf r, t)\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \int\bigl(|\psi_+|^2 + |\psi_-|^2\bigr)\,d^3r = 1 ,
> $$
>
> an element of the tensor product of $L^2(\mathbb R^3)$ with the spin space $\mathbb C^2$ ([[§35 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-71|LADR Def. 9.71]]). $|\psi_\pm(\mathbf r, t)|^2\,d^3r$ is the probability of finding the particle in $d^3r$ at $\mathbf r$ *and* with $S_z = \pm\hbar/2$; $\int|\psi_+|^2\,d^3r$ is the probability of $S_z = +\hbar/2$ irrespective of position. When spin and position are uncoupled the state can be taken as a product $\psi(\mathbf r)\chi$, as for the hydrogen states $R_{n\ell}Y_\ell^m\chi_\pm$, labelled $|n\,\ell\,m\,s\,m_s\rangle$.
>
> *Source: Greensite §13.1, eqs. (13.48)–(13.59); Griffiths §4.4.1, footnote 40, and §5.1.3, eq. (5.27), footnote 7*

^def-b6-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Why a tensor product
> Spin and position are independent degrees of freedom: a basis of the combined space is given by the products of a basis of each factor ([[§35 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-74|LADR Thm. 9.74]]), orthonormal when both factors are ([[§35 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-83|LADR Thm. 9.83]]), and the dimensions multiply. LADR proves this for finite-dimensional spaces; for $L^2(\mathbb R^3)\otimes\mathbb C^2$ only the second factor is finite, and the two-component wave function above is the concrete form of the product, the working version used throughout. A general state is not a product; it is a sum of products, and an operator on one factor (such as $S_z$, or $\hat{\mathbf p}$) acts as the identity on the other. The same construction joins two particles into one system ([[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions]]).

^rem-b6-1-4

## Higher spin

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.7: Spin Matrices for Any Spin
> In the basis $|s\,s\rangle, |s\,(s-1)\rangle, \dots, |s\,(-s)\rangle$ of a spin-$s$ particle, the matrices are $(2s+1)\times(2s+1)$ with entries (rows and columns labelled by $m', m$)
>
> $$
> (\mathsf S_z)_{m'm} = \hbar m\,\delta_{m'm}, \qquad (\mathsf S_\pm)_{m'm} = \hbar\sqrt{s(s+1) - m(m\pm1)}\;\delta_{m',m\pm1}, \qquad \mathsf S_x = \tfrac12(\mathsf S_+ + \mathsf S_-), \quad \mathsf S_y = \tfrac1{2i}(\mathsf S_+ - \mathsf S_-) .
> $$
>
> So $\mathsf S_z$ is diagonal, $\mathsf S_+$ has nonzero entries only just above the diagonal, $\mathsf S_- = \mathsf S_+^\dagger$ only just below it, and $\mathsf S_x$, $\mathsf S_y$ are tridiagonal with zero diagonal. The same formulas, with $J$ and $j$, hold for any angular momentum.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 4.34; Greensite §13.1 (exercise), eq. (13.43)*

^thm-b6-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The entry $(\mathsf Q)_{m'm}$ is $\langle s\,m'|Q|s\,m\rangle$. By [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]] (the ladder coefficients of [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-5|Theorem §B5.2.5]]), $S_z|s\,m\rangle$ is a multiple of $|s\,m\rangle$ and $S_\pm|s\,m\rangle$ a multiple of $|s\,(m\pm1)\rangle$; orthonormality of the basis picks out the stated entries. $S_x$ and $S_y$ follow from $S_\pm = S_x \pm iS_y$. Since the square root is the same for $(m' , m) = (m+1, m)$ in $\mathsf S_+$ and $(m, m+1)$ in $\mathsf S_-$, $\mathsf S_- = \mathsf S_+^\dagger$, and $\mathsf S_x$, $\mathsf S_y$ are Hermitian. For $s = \tfrac12$ the formulas reproduce [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-3|Theorem §B6.1.3]].

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]], [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-5|Theorem §B5.2.5]]

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B6.1.1: Uncertainties in a Spin State
> An electron is in the spin state $\chi = A\begin{pmatrix} 3i \\ 4\end{pmatrix}$. Normalise it, find $\langle\mathbf S\rangle$ and the standard deviations, and check the uncertainty principles.
>
> *Normalisation:* $|A|^2(9 + 16) = 1$, so $A = \tfrac15$; $a = 3i/5$, $b = 4/5$, and $\bar ab = -12i/25$. By [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]],
>
> $$
> \langle S_x\rangle = 0, \qquad \langle S_y\rangle = -\frac{12}{25}\hbar, \qquad \langle S_z\rangle = \frac\hbar2\cdot\frac{9 - 16}{25} = -\frac{7}{50}\hbar ,
> $$
>
> a vector of length $\frac\hbar{50}\sqrt{24^2 + 7^2} = \frac\hbar2$, as it must be. With $\langle S_j^2\rangle = \hbar^2/4$, $\sigma_{S_j}^2 = \hbar^2/4 - \langle S_j\rangle^2$:
>
> $$
> \sigma_{S_x} = \frac\hbar2, \qquad \sigma_{S_y} = \hbar\sqrt{\frac14 - \frac{144}{625}} = \frac{7}{50}\hbar, \qquad \sigma_{S_z} = \hbar\sqrt{\frac14 - \frac{49}{2500}} = \frac{12}{25}\hbar .
> $$
>
> *Checks:* $\sigma_{S_x}\sigma_{S_y} = \frac{7}{100}\hbar^2 = \frac\hbar2|\langle S_z\rangle|$; $\sigma_{S_z}\sigma_{S_x} = \frac{6}{25}\hbar^2 = \frac\hbar2|\langle S_y\rangle|$; $\sigma_{S_y}\sigma_{S_z} = \frac{168}{2500}\hbar^2 > \frac\hbar2|\langle S_x\rangle| = 0$. Two of the three relations hold with equality. That is no accident: with $\langle\mathbf S\rangle = \frac\hbar2\hat n$, $\sigma_{S_x}^2\sigma_{S_y}^2 = \frac{\hbar^4}{16}(1 - n_x^2)(1 - n_y^2) = \frac{\hbar^4}{16}(n_z^2 + n_x^2n_y^2)$, which equals the bound $\frac{\hbar^4}{16}n_z^2$ exactly when $n_xn_y = 0$; here $n_x = 0$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 4.30*

^ex-b6-1-1

> [!example] Example §B6.1.2: A Spin-1 Particle Measured Along x
> A spin-1 particle is in the state $|1\,1\rangle$. What are the possible results of measuring $S_x$, and their probabilities?
>
> By [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-7|Theorem §B6.1.7]] with $s = 1$ (basis $m = 1, 0, -1$; the ladder coefficients are $\sqrt{2 - 0} = \sqrt2$ and $\sqrt{2 - (-1)(0)} = \sqrt2$),
>
> $$
> \mathsf S_x = \frac\hbar{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
> \mathsf S_y = \frac\hbar{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i & 0 \\ i & 0 & -i \\ 0 & i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
> \mathsf S_z = \hbar\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> The characteristic equation of $\mathsf S_x$ is $-\lambda(\lambda^2 - \hbar^2) = 0$: eigenvalues $\hbar, 0, -\hbar$, as for $S_z$. Solving $\mathsf S_x v = \lambda v$ gives the normalised eigenvectors
>
> $$
> v_{+\hbar} = \tfrac12\bigl(1, \sqrt2, 1\bigr)^{\mathsf T}, \qquad v_0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(1, 0, -1\bigr)^{\mathsf T}, \qquad v_{-\hbar} = \tfrac12\bigl(1, -\sqrt2, 1\bigr)^{\mathsf T} .
> $$
>
> Their first components give the amplitudes in $|1\,1\rangle = (1, 0, 0)^{\mathsf T}$: the results $\hbar$, $0$, $-\hbar$ occur with probabilities $\tfrac14$, $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$. *Check:* $\langle S_x\rangle = 0$ and $\langle S_x^2\rangle = \tfrac12\hbar^2$, in agreement with $\langle J_x^2\rangle = \tfrac12\hbar^2[j(j+1) - m^2]$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-5|Theorem §B5.2.5]]). Unlike spin $\tfrac12$, a spin-1 state can have a sharp $S_z$ and still give the middle value $0$ along $x$ half the time. The matrices for $j = \tfrac32$ and $j = 2$ (PHY 308 HW 5.2) are worked out in [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^ex-b5-2-1|Example §B5.2.1]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 4.34 (the matrices); the measurement solved here*

^ex-b6-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The Pauli matrices are Hermitian ([[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-10|LADR Def. 7.10]]) with real eigenvalues ([[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-12|LADR Thm. 7.12]]); the eigenspinors of any $\mathsf S_r$ form an orthonormal basis by the complex spectral theorem ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]), the finite-dimensional case of the completeness postulated in [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators]].
> - Spin and position combine in a tensor product ([[§35 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-71|LADR Def. 9.71]], [[§35 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-74|LADR Thm. 9.74]]).
> - The general spectrum of angular momentum, from which Theorem §B6.1.2 is taken, is [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]]; spin in a field is [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field]], adding spins [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta]]. Spin as the generator of rotations, with $e^{-i\theta\,\hat n\cdot\mathbf S/\hbar}$ turning a spinor by $\theta$ (and by $2\pi$ into its negative), is level C (planned).
