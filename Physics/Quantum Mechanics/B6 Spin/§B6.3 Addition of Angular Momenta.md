An electron in an atom has orbital angular momentum and spin; a hydrogen atom has the electron's and the proton's spins; a meson has two quark spins. What are the possible values of the *total* angular momentum? Level A answered for one electron, $j = \ell \pm \tfrac12$, by counting states and assuming that the total obeys the usual quantization rules ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-8|Theorem §A5.2.8]]). This section proves the general rule: two angular momenta $j_1$ and $j_2$ combine to every $j$ from $j_1 + j_2$ down to $|j_1 - j_2|$, once each. It builds the combined states (Clebsch–Gordan coefficients), works out the singlet and triplet of two spins $\tfrac12$, and gives the eigenvalues of $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$ on which the fine structure rests. The algebra of a single angular momentum is taken from [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics]].

## Two angular momenta in one system

> [!definition] Definition §B6.3.1: Composite System; Uncoupled Basis
> Let $\mathbf J^{(1)}$ be an angular momentum with fixed quantum number $j_1$, acting on the $(2j_1+1)$-dimensional space $V_1$ spanned by $|j_1\,m_1\rangle$, and $\mathbf J^{(2)}$ one with fixed $j_2$ on $V_2$. Either may be a spin, an orbital angular momentum of fixed $\ell$ (then $V$ is spanned by the $Y_\ell^m$), or itself a total. The state space of the combined system is the tensor product $V_1\otimes V_2$ ([[§35 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-71|LADR Def. 9.71]]), with the orthonormal **uncoupled basis** ([[§35 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-83|LADR Thm. 9.83]])
>
> $$
> |j_1\,j_2\,m_1\,m_2\rangle = |j_1\,m_1\rangle\otimes|j_2\,m_2\rangle, \qquad m_1 = -j_1, \dots, j_1, \quad m_2 = -j_2, \dots, j_2 ,
> $$
>
> $(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)$ states in all. $\mathbf J^{(1)}$ acts on the first factor and $\mathbf J^{(2)}$ on the second, so
>
> $$
> \bigl(J^{(1)}\bigr)^2|j_1\,j_2\,m_1\,m_2\rangle = j_1(j_1+1)\hbar^2|\cdots\rangle, \quad J^{(1)}_z|j_1\,j_2\,m_1\,m_2\rangle = m_1\hbar|\cdots\rangle, \quad \text{and likewise for } \mathbf J^{(2)} .
> $$
>
> The **total angular momentum** is $\mathbf J = \mathbf J^{(1)} + \mathbf J^{(2)}$. For two spins Griffiths writes $\mathbf S = \mathbf S^{(1)} + \mathbf S^{(2)}$ and $|s_1\,s_2\,m_1\,m_2\rangle$; for an electron $\mathbf J = \mathbf L + \mathbf S$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.3, eqs. (4.171)–(4.172), footnote 52; Greensite §14.1, eqs. (14.45)–(14.49)*

^def-b6-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.1: The Total Is an Angular Momentum
> 1. The components of $\mathbf J = \mathbf J^{(1)} + \mathbf J^{(2)}$ obey $[J_x, J_y] = i\hbar J_z$ and cyclically. Hence (by [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics]]) $J^2$ and $J_z$ have eigenvalues $j(j+1)\hbar^2$ and $m\hbar$, $m = -j, \dots, j$.
> 2. $z$-components add: $J_z|j_1\,j_2\,m_1\,m_2\rangle = (m_1 + m_2)\hbar\,|j_1\,j_2\,m_1\,m_2\rangle$, so $m = m_1 + m_2$.
> 3. $J^2$ commutes with $J_z$, $\bigl(J^{(1)}\bigr)^2$ and $\bigl(J^{(2)}\bigr)^2$, but not with $J^{(1)}_z$ or $J^{(2)}_z$:
>
> $$
> \bigl[J^2, \mathbf J^{(1)}\bigr] = 2i\hbar\,\bigl(\mathbf J^{(1)}\times\mathbf J^{(2)}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.3, eqs. (4.173)–(4.174), Problem 4.41, eq. (4.185); Greensite ch. 14, eq. (14.3)*

^thm-b6-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Operators acting on different factors commute: $[J^{(1)}_a, J^{(2)}_b] = 0$ for all $a, b$. *1.* $[J_x, J_y] = [J^{(1)}_x, J^{(1)}_y] + [J^{(2)}_x, J^{(2)}_y] = i\hbar\bigl(J^{(1)}_z + J^{(2)}_z\bigr) = i\hbar J_z$, the cross terms vanishing. *2.* $J_z = J^{(1)}_z + J^{(2)}_z$ acts on the product state term by term. *3.* Because the components of $\mathbf J^{(1)}$ and $\mathbf J^{(2)}$ commute,
>
> $$
> J^2 = \bigl(J^{(1)}\bigr)^2 + \bigl(J^{(2)}\bigr)^2 + 2\,\mathbf J^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf J^{(2)} .
> $$
>
> $\bigl(J^{(1)}\bigr)^2$ commutes with every component of $\mathbf J^{(1)}$ (the property $[L^2, L_i] = 0$ of [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics]]) and trivially with those of $\mathbf J^{(2)}$, hence with $J^2$; $J^2$ commutes with $J_z$ by part 1. But
>
> $$
> \bigl[J^2, J^{(1)}_z\bigr] = 2\bigl[J^{(1)}_xJ^{(2)}_x + J^{(1)}_yJ^{(2)}_y, J^{(1)}_z\bigr] = 2\bigl(-i\hbar J^{(1)}_yJ^{(2)}_x + i\hbar J^{(1)}_xJ^{(2)}_y\bigr) = 2i\hbar\bigl(\mathbf J^{(1)}\times\mathbf J^{(2)}\bigr)_z ,
> $$
>
> and the $x$- and $y$-components follow cyclically.

^der-b6-3-1

*Uses:* [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^def-b6-3-1|Def. §B6.3.1]], [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics]], [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: You cannot know $m_1$, $m_2$ and $j$ at once
> Part 3 says the uncoupled basis states (sharp $m_1$, $m_2$) are in general not eigenstates of $J^2$, and the eigenstates of $J^2$ are superpositions with different $m_1$. Two complete sets of commuting observables are available, $\bigl\{(J^{(1)})^2, (J^{(2)})^2, J^{(1)}_z, J^{(2)}_z\bigr\}$ and $\bigl\{(J^{(1)})^2, (J^{(2)})^2, J^2, J_z\bigr\}$; which basis is useful depends on the Hamiltonian. Only $m = m_1 + m_2$ is shared by both.

## The addition rule

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.2: Addition of Angular Momenta
> Combining $j_1$ and $j_2$ gives every total angular momentum quantum number
>
> $$
> j = j_1 + j_2,\ \ j_1 + j_2 - 1,\ \ j_1 + j_2 - 2,\ \ \dots,\ \ |j_1 - j_2| ,
> $$
>
> each exactly once. Precisely: $V_1\otimes V_2$ is the orthogonal direct sum of subspaces, one for each of these $j$, each spanned by $2j+1$ orthonormal common eigenstates $|j\,m\rangle$ of $J^2$ and $J_z$ ($m = -j, \dots, j$), which are also eigenstates of $\bigl(J^{(1)}\bigr)^2$ and $\bigl(J^{(2)}\bigr)^2$. The dimensions agree: $\sum_{j=|j_1-j_2|}^{j_1+j_2}(2j+1) = (2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.3, eq. (4.182) and footnote 53 (proof referred to Cohen-Tannoudji); Greensite §14.1, eqs. (14.50)–(14.56)*

^thm-b6-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Step 1: multiplets.* $J^2$, $J_z$ are commuting Hermitian operators on the finite-dimensional space $V_1\otimes V_2$, so they have a common orthonormal eigenbasis ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]], [[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-76|LADR Thm. 5.76]]), and $(J^{(1)})^2$, $(J^{(2)})^2$ are the constants $j_1(j_1+1)\hbar^2$, $j_2(j_2+1)\hbar^2$ on this space. Let $W_j$ be the eigenspace of $J^2$ for $j(j+1)\hbar^2$. Since $J_\pm$ commute with $J^2$, they map $W_j$ into itself; within $W_j$, by [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]] and the ladder algebra of [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics]], $\|J_+\psi\|^2 = \langle\psi|J_-J_+|\psi\rangle = \hbar^2[j(j+1) - m(m+1)]\|\psi\|^2 > 0$ for a $J_z$-eigenvector $\psi$ with $m < j$. So $J_+$ maps the $m$-eigenspace of $W_j$ injectively into the $(m+1)$-eigenspace, and $J_-$ maps back injectively; all $2j+1$ values of $m$ occur in $W_j$ with the same multiplicity $N_j$, the number of multiplets with that $j$.
>
> *Step 2: count by $m$.* By [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]](2), the eigenspace of $J_z$ for $m\hbar$ is spanned by the uncoupled states with $m_1 + m_2 = m$; call their number $n(m)$. Each multiplet with $j \ge |m|$ contributes one state with this $m$, so
>
> $$
> n(m) = \sum_{j \ge |m|} N_j \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad N_j = n(j) - n(j+1) .
> $$
>
> Take $j_1 \ge j_2$. For given $m \ge 0$, $m_2$ runs over $[-j_2, j_2]$ subject to $m_1 = m - m_2 \in [-j_1, j_1]$. If $m \le j_1 - j_2$, every $m_2$ is allowed: $n(m) = 2j_2 + 1$. If $j_1 - j_2 \le m \le j_1 + j_2$, the condition is $m_2 \ge m - j_1$, so $n(m) = j_1 + j_2 - m + 1$. Above $j_1 + j_2$, $n = 0$. Hence $N_j = (j_1 + j_2 - j + 1) - (j_1 + j_2 - j) = 1$ for $j_1 - j_2 \le j \le j_1 + j_2$, and $N_j = (2j_2+1) - (2j_2+1) = 0$ for $j < j_1 - j_2$ (all $j$ here differ from $j_1 + j_2$ by integers, like the $m$'s). For $j_1 < j_2$ exchange the roles.
>
> *Check of the dimension* (Greensite): with $j_1 \ge j_2$, $\sum_{j=j_1-j_2}^{j_1+j_2}(2j+1)$ is a sum of $2j_2+1$ terms with mean $2j_1 + 1$, that is, $(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)$.

*Uses:* [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]], [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics]], [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]], [[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-76|LADR Thm. 5.76]]

> [!remark] Remark: What level A assumed, now proved
> Level A obtained $j = \ell \pm \tfrac12$ by counting the $2(2\ell+1)$ states of one electron, *assuming* that $\mathbf J = \mathbf L + \mathbf S$ is quantized like any angular momentum ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^der-a5-2-8|Derivation of Theorem §A5.2.8]], marked "idea"). Both gaps are closed: Theorem §B6.3.1 shows that $\mathbf J$ obeys the angular-momentum algebra, and Theorem §B6.3.2 with $j_1 = \ell$, $j_2 = \tfrac12$ gives exactly $j = \ell + \tfrac12$ and $\ell - \tfrac12$ (only $\tfrac12$ for $\ell = 0$); the level-A counting is Step 2 above in a special case. The vector-model words "parallel" and "antiparallel" survive as a rough reading: the largest $j$ goes with aligned angular momenta, the smallest with opposed ones (Griffiths).

## Clebsch–Gordan coefficients

> [!definition] Definition §B6.3.2: Coupled Basis; Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients
> The states $|j\,m\rangle$ of Theorem §B6.3.2 (more fully $|j_1\,j_2\,j\,m\rangle$) form the **coupled basis**. Expanding them in the uncoupled basis defines the **Clebsch–Gordan coefficients** $C^{j_1j_2j}_{m_1m_2m} = \langle j_1\,j_2\,m_1\,m_2|j\,m\rangle$:
>
> $$
> |j\,m\rangle = \sum_{m_1+m_2=m} C^{j_1j_2j}_{m_1m_2m}\,|j_1\,j_2\,m_1\,m_2\rangle, \qquad |j_1\,j_2\,m_1\,m_2\rangle = \sum_j C^{j_1j_2j}_{m_1m_2m}\,|j\,m\rangle \quad (m = m_1 + m_2) .
> $$
>
> Each $|j\,m\rangle$ is fixed only up to a phase; the tables (Griffiths Table 4.8) use real coefficients, with the coefficient of $|j_1\,j_2\,j_1\,(j - j_1)\rangle$ in $|j\,j\rangle$ positive and the lower states generated by $J_-$. In a state $|j\,m\rangle$, a measurement of $J^{(1)}_z$ gives $m_1\hbar$ with probability $\bigl(C^{j_1j_2j}_{m_1,m-m_1,m}\bigr)^2$; in a state $|j_1\,j_2\,m_1\,m_2\rangle$, a measurement of $J^2$ gives $j(j+1)\hbar^2$ with probability $\bigl(C^{j_1j_2j}_{m_1m_2m}\bigr)^2$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.3, eqs. (4.183)–(4.184), Table 4.8; Greensite ch. 14, eqs. (14.4)–(14.5)*

^def-b6-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.3: Properties and Construction of the Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients
> 1. $C^{j_1j_2j}_{m_1m_2m} = 0$ unless $m = m_1 + m_2$ and $|j_1 - j_2| \le j \le j_1 + j_2$.
> 2. For each $m$ the coefficients form a real orthogonal matrix (rows labelled by $(m_1, m_2)$ with $m_1 + m_2 = m$, columns by $j$): the squares in each column add to $1$, and so do the squares in each row.
> 3. **Construction.** The top state is a single product, $|j_1{+}j_2,\ j_1{+}j_2\rangle = |j_1\,j_2\,j_1\,j_2\rangle$. Applying $J_- = J^{(1)}_- + J^{(2)}_-$ to it, with $J_-|j\,m\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{j(j+1) - m(m-1)}\,|j\,(m-1)\rangle$ on the left and the corresponding rules for $J^{(1)}_-$, $J^{(2)}_-$ on the right, produces the whole multiplet $j = j_1 + j_2$. The top state $|j\,j\rangle$ of the next multiplet, $j = j_1 + j_2 - 1$, is the combination of the two uncoupled states with $m = j$ that is orthogonal to $|j_1{+}j_2,\ j\rangle$ and normalised; $J_-$ again produces its multiplet; and so on down to $|j_1 - j_2|$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.3, eqs. (4.183)–(4.184), Table 4.8 and the remarks on row and column sums; Greensite §14.1, eqs. (14.50)–(14.62)*

^thm-b6-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $|j\,m\rangle$ lies in the $J_z$-eigenspace for $m\hbar$, spanned by the uncoupled states with $m_1 + m_2 = m$ ([[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]]); by [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]] no other $j$ occurs. *2.* For fixed $m$ both $\{|j\,m\rangle\}$ and $\{|j_1\,j_2\,m_1\,m_2\rangle : m_1 + m_2 = m\}$ are orthonormal bases of the same eigenspace (their numbers agree, $n(m)$ in the derivation of Theorem §B6.3.2), so the change-of-basis matrix is unitary, here real and therefore orthogonal; orthonormality of columns and of rows is the statement. *3.* The only uncoupled state with $m = j_1 + j_2$ is $|j_1\,j_2\,j_1\,j_2\rangle$, and it must be $|j_1{+}j_2, j_1{+}j_2\rangle$ (up to the conventional phase). $J_-$ lowers $m$ by one within a multiplet. A top state $|j\,j\rangle$ is orthogonal to all states with larger $j$ (they are eigenvectors of the Hermitian $J^2$ with different eigenvalues, [[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-22|LADR Thm. 7.22]]); in the $m = j$ eigenspace of dimension $n(j)$ these larger-$j$ states fill $n(j) - 1$ dimensions, which leaves exactly one direction.

>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.3, pp. 225–226*

## Two spins one-half

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.4: Singlet and Triplet
> Two spin-$\tfrac12$ particles combine to total spin $s = 1$ or $s = 0$. In the notation $|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle = |\tfrac12\,\tfrac12\,\tfrac12\,(-\tfrac12)\rangle$ etc., the **triplet** ($s = 1$) and the **singlet** ($s = 0$) are
>
> $$
> \left.\begin{aligned}
> |1\,1\rangle &= |{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle \\
> |1\,0\rangle &= \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle + |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle\bigr) \\
> |1\,{-1}\rangle &= |{\downarrow\downarrow}\rangle
> \end{aligned}\right\}\ s = 1, \qquad\qquad
> |0\,0\rangle = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle - |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle\bigr)\quad s = 0 ,
> $$
>
> with $S^2 = 2\hbar^2$ on the triplet and $0$ on the singlet, and
>
> $$
> \mathbf S^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf S^{(2)} = \frac{\hbar^2}4 \ \ \text{(triplet)}, \qquad \mathbf S^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf S^{(2)} = -\frac{3\hbar^2}4 \ \ \text{(singlet)} .
> $$
>
> Under exchange of the two spins ($|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle \leftrightarrow |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle$) the three triplet states are **symmetric** and the singlet is **antisymmetric**.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 4.5, eqs. (4.175)–(4.181); Greensite §14.1, eqs. (14.63)–(14.74)*

^thm-b6-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By Theorem §B6.3.2, $\tfrac12\otimes\tfrac12$ gives $s = 1$ and $s = 0$, with $3 + 1 = 4$ states. Construct them as in [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-3|Theorem §B6.3.3]]. The top state is $|{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle$. With $S_-\chi_+ = \hbar\chi_-$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-3|Theorem §B6.1.3]]),
>
> $$
> S_-|{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle = \bigl(S^{(1)}_-|{\uparrow}\rangle\bigr)|{\uparrow}\rangle + |{\uparrow}\rangle\bigl(S^{(2)}_-|{\uparrow}\rangle\bigr) = \hbar\bigl(|{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle + |{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle\bigr),
> $$
>
> while on the left $S_-|1\,1\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{2}\,|1\,0\rangle$; this gives $|1\,0\rangle$. Once more, $S_-|1\,0\rangle = \tfrac{\hbar}{\sqrt2}\bigl(|{\downarrow\downarrow}\rangle + |{\downarrow\downarrow}\rangle\bigr) = \hbar\sqrt2\,|1\,{-1}\rangle$. The singlet is the normalised $m = 0$ state orthogonal to $|1\,0\rangle$.
>
> *Direct check of the eigenvalues* (Griffiths). Since $S^2 = (S^{(1)})^2 + (S^{(2)})^2 + 2\mathbf S^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf S^{(2)}$, compute $\mathbf S^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf S^{(2)}$ with the matrices of Theorem §B6.1.3: $S_x\chi_\pm = \frac\hbar2\chi_\mp$, $S_y\chi_\pm = \pm\frac{i\hbar}2\chi_\mp$, $S_z\chi_\pm = \pm\frac\hbar2\chi_\pm$. Then
>
> $$
> \mathbf S^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf S^{(2)}|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}4\Bigl(|{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle + (i)(-i)\,|{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle - |{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle\Bigr) = \frac{\hbar^2}4\bigl(2|{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle - |{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle\bigr),
> $$
>
> and by symmetry $\mathbf S^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf S^{(2)}|{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}4\bigl(2|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle - |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle\bigr)$. Adding and subtracting: $\mathbf S^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf S^{(2)}|1\,0\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}4|1\,0\rangle$ and $\mathbf S^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf S^{(2)}|0\,0\rangle = -\frac{3\hbar^2}4|0\,0\rangle$. With $(S^{(i)})^2 = \frac34\hbar^2$: $S^2|1\,0\rangle = \bigl(\frac34 + \frac34 + \frac24\bigr)\hbar^2|1\,0\rangle = 2\hbar^2|1\,0\rangle$ and $S^2|0\,0\rangle = \bigl(\frac34 + \frac34 - \frac64\bigr)\hbar^2|0\,0\rangle = 0$. The exchange symmetry is read off.

> [!remark] Remark: The singlet is the textbook entangled state
> No choice of single-particle spinors $\chi^{(1)}$, $\chi^{(2)}$ gives $|0\,0\rangle = \chi^{(1)}\otimes\chi^{(2)}$: a product $(a\chi_+ + b\chi_-)\otimes(c\chi_+ + d\chi_-)$ has $\uparrow\uparrow$ and $\downarrow\downarrow$ coefficients $ac = bd = 0$, which forces one of the $\uparrow\downarrow$, $\downarrow\uparrow$ coefficients to vanish too. Not every tensor is a product ([[§35 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-74|LADR Thm. 9.74]] and the remark there). The singlet is Griffiths' standard example of an entangled state ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions]]). Its exchange antisymmetry is what lets two electrons share one orbital ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions]]), and the symmetry of the triplet underlies the ortho/para split of helium ([[§B7.2 Atoms]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths ch. 5, footnote 1*

## Spin–orbit eigenvalues

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.5: Eigenvalues of L·S
> For $\mathbf J = \mathbf J^{(1)} + \mathbf J^{(2)}$,
>
> $$
> \mathbf J^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf J^{(2)} = \tfrac12\Bigl(J^2 - \bigl(J^{(1)}\bigr)^2 - \bigl(J^{(2)}\bigr)^2\Bigr),
> $$
>
> so the coupled states are its eigenstates: $\mathbf J^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf J^{(2)}|j_1\,j_2\,j\,m\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}2\bigl[j(j+1) - j_1(j_1+1) - j_2(j_2+1)\bigr]|j_1\,j_2\,j\,m\rangle$, independent of $m$. In particular, for an orbital angular momentum $\ell$ and a spin $s$ with $\mathbf J = \mathbf L + \mathbf S$,
>
> $$
> \mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S\,|\ell\,s\,j\,m_j\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}2\bigl[j(j+1) - \ell(\ell+1) - s(s+1)\bigr]\,|\ell\,s\,j\,m_j\rangle ,
> $$
>
> and for $s = \tfrac12$:
>
> $$
> \mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S = \frac{\hbar^2}2\,\ell \quad (j = \ell + \tfrac12), \qquad \mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S = -\frac{\hbar^2}2\,(\ell + 1) \quad (j = \ell - \tfrac12,\ \ell \ge 1) ,
> $$
>
> and $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S = 0$ for $\ell = 0$. $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$ commutes with $J^2$, $J_z$, $L^2$, $S^2$, but not with $L_z$ or $S_z$.
>
> *Source: Greensite ch. 13, eqs. (13.90)–(13.95), ch. 14, eqs. (14.1), (14.40)–(14.41); Griffiths §4.4.3, eq. (4.177)*

^thm-b6-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Square $\mathbf J = \mathbf J^{(1)} + \mathbf J^{(2)}$; the components of the two commute, so $\mathbf J^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf J^{(2)} = \mathbf J^{(2)}\cdot\mathbf J^{(1)}$ and $J^2 = (J^{(1)})^2 + (J^{(2)})^2 + 2\mathbf J^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf J^{(2)}$. The three operators on the right of the formula act on $|j_1\,j_2\,j\,m\rangle$ as $j(j+1)\hbar^2$, $j_1(j_1+1)\hbar^2$, $j_2(j_2+1)\hbar^2$ ([[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]). For $s = \tfrac12$, $j = \ell + \tfrac12$: $(\ell + \tfrac12)(\ell + \tfrac32) - \ell(\ell+1) - \tfrac34 = \ell$; $j = \ell - \tfrac12$: $(\ell - \tfrac12)(\ell + \tfrac12) - \ell(\ell+1) - \tfrac34 = -\ell - 1$. The commutation statements follow from [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]](3): $J^2$, $L^2$, $S^2$ commute with each other and with $J_z$, while $J^2$, hence $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$, does not commute with $L_z$.

^der-b6-3-5

*Uses:* [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]], [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why the coupled basis is the right one for an atom
> The spin–orbit interaction of an electron has the form $f(r)\,\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$ (Greensite eq. (13.89); PHY 308 HW 6.1). It does not commute with $L_z$ and $S_z$, so the uncoupled states $R_{n\ell}Y_\ell^{m}\chi_\pm$ are not its eigenstates; it does commute with $J^2$, $J_z$, $L^2$, $S^2$, and on the coupled states it is diagonal with the values of Theorem §B6.3.5. The sign settles the level-A rule that $j = \ell + \tfrac12$ lies above $j = \ell - \tfrac12$ ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-9|Theorem §A5.2.9]]): for $f > 0$ the shift is $+\frac{\hbar^2}2\ell\langle f\rangle$ for the first and $-\frac{\hbar^2}2(\ell+1)\langle f\rangle$ for the second. The size of $\langle f\rangle$ and the full fine-structure formula are [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B6.3.1: The p States of One Electron
> Couple $\ell = 1$ to $s = \tfrac12$, writing $Y_1^m\chi_\pm$ for the uncoupled states. By Theorem §B6.3.2, $j = \tfrac32$ (four states) and $j = \tfrac12$ (two states). Start from $|\tfrac32\,\tfrac32\rangle = Y_1^1\chi_+$ and apply $J_- = L_- + S_-$, with $L_-Y_1^1 = \sqrt2\hbar\,Y_1^0$, $L_-Y_1^0 = \sqrt2\hbar\,Y_1^{-1}$ and $S_-\chi_+ = \hbar\chi_-$; on the left $J_-|\tfrac32\,\tfrac32\rangle = \sqrt3\hbar\,|\tfrac32\,\tfrac12\rangle$:
>
> $$
> |\tfrac32\,\tfrac12\rangle = \sqrt{\tfrac23}\,Y_1^0\chi_+ + \sqrt{\tfrac13}\,Y_1^1\chi_-, \qquad |\tfrac32\,{-\tfrac12}\rangle = \sqrt{\tfrac13}\,Y_1^{-1}\chi_+ + \sqrt{\tfrac23}\,Y_1^0\chi_-, \qquad |\tfrac32\,{-\tfrac32}\rangle = Y_1^{-1}\chi_- .
> $$
>
> The $j = \tfrac12$ top state is the combination of $Y_1^1\chi_-$ and $Y_1^0\chi_+$ orthogonal to $|\tfrac32\,\tfrac12\rangle$, with a positive $Y_1^1\chi_-$ coefficient; one more $J_-$ gives its partner:
>
> $$
> |\tfrac12\,\tfrac12\rangle = \sqrt{\tfrac23}\,Y_1^1\chi_- - \sqrt{\tfrac13}\,Y_1^0\chi_+, \qquad |\tfrac12\,{-\tfrac12}\rangle = \sqrt{\tfrac13}\,Y_1^0\chi_- - \sqrt{\tfrac23}\,Y_1^{-1}\chi_+ .
> $$
>
> These agree with the $1\times\tfrac12$ block of Griffiths' Table 4.8. By Theorem §B6.3.5, $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S = \tfrac12\hbar^2$ on the four $j = \tfrac32$ states and $-\hbar^2$ on the two $j = \tfrac12$ states. For hydrogen's $n = 2$, $\ell = 1$ level these are the $2\,{}^2P_{3/2}$ and $2\,{}^2P_{1/2}$ states of [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^def-a5-2-4|Def. §A5.2.4]]. *Check:* in $|\tfrac32\,\tfrac12\rangle$ the probability of $S_z = +\hbar/2$ is $\tfrac23$, and in $|\tfrac12\,\tfrac12\rangle$ it is $\tfrac13$; they add to $1$, as the row sum requires.
>
> *Source: Greensite ch. 14, eqs. (14.13)–(14.31); Griffiths Table 4.8*

^ex-b6-3-1

> [!example] Example §B6.3.2: A Spin-1 Particle with Spin–Orbit Terms
> A particle of spin $1$ moves in the central potential
>
> $$
> V(r) = V_1(r) + \frac{\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf L}{\hbar^2}\,V_2(r) + \frac{(\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf L)^2}{\hbar^4}\,V_3(r) .
> $$
>
> What is $V$ in the states with $j = \ell + 1$, $j = \ell$, $j = \ell - 1$?
>
> $V$ commutes with $J^2$, $L^2$, $S^2$ (Theorem §B6.3.5), so on each coupled state it acts as a number. With $s = 1$, $s(s+1) = 2$ and
>
> $$
> \frac{\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf L}{\hbar^2} = \tfrac12\bigl[j(j+1) - \ell(\ell+1) - 2\bigr] =
> \begin{cases}
> \tfrac12\bigl[(\ell+1)(\ell+2) - \ell(\ell+1) - 2\bigr] = \ell, & j = \ell + 1,\\[2pt]
> \tfrac12\bigl[-2\bigr] = -1, & j = \ell,\\[2pt]
> \tfrac12\bigl[(\ell-1)\ell - \ell(\ell+1) - 2\bigr] = -(\ell + 1), & j = \ell - 1 .
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> The square of an operator acts on its eigenvector as the square of the eigenvalue, so
>
> $$
> V = V_1 + \ell\,V_2 + \ell^2\,V_3 \ \ (j = \ell + 1), \qquad V = V_1 - V_2 + V_3 \ \ (j = \ell), \qquad V = V_1 - (\ell + 1)\,V_2 + (\ell + 1)^2\,V_3 \ \ (j = \ell - 1) .
> $$
>
> By Theorem §B6.3.2 all three occur for $\ell \ge 1$; for $\ell = 0$ only $j = 1$ exists, with $V = V_1$. The homework writes the last term as $(\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf L)^2V_3/\hbar^2$; for $V_3$ to be an energy like $V_1$ and $V_2$ the divisor must be $\hbar^4$, as used here (with $\hbar^2$ every $V_3$ coefficient above carries an extra $\hbar^2$).
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 6, Problem 6.3*

^ex-b6-3-2

> [!example] Example §B6.3.3: Spins of Mesons and Baryons
> Quarks have spin $\tfrac12$. In the ground state (no orbital angular momentum), what spins can a meson (quark and antiquark) and a baryon (three quarks) have?
>
> *Meson:* $\tfrac12\otimes\tfrac12$ gives $s = 1$ or $0$ (Theorem §B6.3.4). Spin-0 mesons are the pseudoscalars ($\pi$, $K$), spin-1 ones the vector mesons.
>
> *Baryon:* couple two quarks first, $s_{12} = 1$ or $0$, then the third. $1\otimes\tfrac12$ gives $\tfrac32$ and $\tfrac12$; $0\otimes\tfrac12$ gives $\tfrac12$. So baryons have spin $\tfrac32$ or $\tfrac12$, and the $2^3 = 8$ spin states split as $4 + 2 + 2$: one quartet and two doublets. The proton and neutron have spin $\tfrac12$; the $\Delta$ baryons have spin $\tfrac32$ (Griffiths §4.4). Which of these combinations nature actually uses is decided by the symmetrization requirement for identical quarks ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions]]), including their colour ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^ex-a8-2-2|Example §A8.2.2]]), beyond this example.
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 6, Problem 6.5; Griffiths Problem 4.38*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Further couplings
> - **Hyperfine structure.** The electron and proton spins in hydrogen's ground state combine to a triplet and a singlet; their magnetic interaction is proportional to $\mathbf S^{(e)}\cdot\mathbf S^{(p)}$, which by Theorem §B6.3.4 is $+\hbar^2/4$ on the triplet and $-3\hbar^2/4$ on the singlet. The splitting is the 21-cm line ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^def-a5-2-5|Def. §A5.2.5]]; computed in [[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting]]; Greensite eqs. (14.75)–(14.85)).
> - **Three angular momenta.** Couple in stages: an electron with orbital $\ell$ has $j = \ell \pm \tfrac12$; adding the proton spin gives an atomic total of $\ell + 1$, $\ell$ or $\ell - 1$, where $\ell$ arises in two ways, from either value of $j$ (Griffiths §4.4.3).
> - **Group theory.** Mathematically, Theorem §B6.3.2 decomposes the tensor product of two irreducible representations of the rotation group into a direct sum of irreducible ones (Griffiths' phrase); the general formula for the coefficients (Griffiths footnote 54) and the Wigner–Eckart theorem are level C (planned).

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The composite state space is a tensor product, with the products of basis vectors as a basis ([[§35 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-71|LADR Def. 9.71]], [[§35 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-74|LADR Thm. 9.74]], [[§35 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-83|LADR Thm. 9.83]]); the coupled basis is a second eigenbasis of commuting Hermitian operators ([[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-76|LADR Thm. 5.76]], [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]), and the Clebsch–Gordan matrix is the orthogonal change of basis between the two.
> - The $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$ eigenvalues are used for the fine structure ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen]]) and the singlet/triplet symmetry for identical particles ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions]], [[§B7.2 Atoms]]).
> - Many spins $J$ in a field at temperature $T$: the $(2J+1)$-state paramagnet ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^ex-b7-1-3|TH Example §B7.1.3]]).
