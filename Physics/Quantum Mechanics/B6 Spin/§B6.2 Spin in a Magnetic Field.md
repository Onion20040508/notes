*Sources: Griffiths & Schroeter, Introduction to Quantum Mechanics (3rd ed.), §4.4.2 · Greensite, Physics 430 lecture notes, ch. 13 · PHY 308 (Pérez Ríos, Spring 2025).*

Level A found the two energies $\pm\mu_BB$ of an electron spin in a field ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-7|Theorem §A5.2.7]]), argued by analogy with a top that the moment precesses ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^rem-a5-2-2|Remark: Torque and precession]]), and explained the Stern–Gerlach deflection by the force $\mu_z\,dB_z/dz$ ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-4|Theorem §A5.2.4]]). With the spin matrices of [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices]] the spin in a field becomes a solvable dynamical problem: the Hamiltonian is a $2\times2$ matrix, and the Schrödinger equation can be integrated exactly. This section derives Larmor precession, shows that the expectation value of the spin obeys the classical torque equation, analyses the Stern–Gerlach magnet, and solves exactly the spin in a field that turns about an axis (PHY 308 HW 6.7), the setting of magnetic resonance.

## The Hamiltonian

> [!definition] Definition §B6.2.1: Spin Magnetic Moment; Gyromagnetic Ratio
> A particle with spin has a **magnetic dipole moment** proportional to its spin,
>
> $$
> \boldsymbol\mu = \gamma\,\mathbf S ,
> $$
>
> and the constant $\gamma$ is its **gyromagnetic ratio**. Classically a body whose charge $q$ and mass $m$ are distributed alike has $\gamma = q/2m$ ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]]). The electron has, almost exactly, twice the classical value, $\gamma = -e/m$; in the notation of level A, $\gamma = -g_s e/2m$ with $g_s \approx 2$ ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^def-a5-2-3|Def. §A5.2.3]]). For the electron $\gamma < 0$: moment and spin point opposite ways.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.2, eq. (4.156) and footnote 43; Greensite ch. 13, eqs. (13.11), (13.18)*

^def-b6-2-1

> [!model] Model §B6.2.2: Spin at Rest in a Magnetic Field
> A particle with spin $s$ and moment $\boldsymbol\mu = \gamma\mathbf S$ sits at rest in a magnetic field $\mathbf B(t)$, uniform in space and possibly varying in time. Its state is a spinor $\chi(t)$, with Hamiltonian matrix and equation of motion
>
> $$
> \mathsf H = -\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B = -\gamma\,\mathbf B\cdot\mathsf S, \qquad i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t} = \mathsf H\chi ,
> $$
>
> the second being the Schrödinger equation ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]]; operator form [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-4|Principle §B2.3.4]]) on the spin space. *Assumptions:* the energy is the classical dipole energy $-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B$ ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-3|EM Theorem §A7.4.3]]) with $\boldsymbol\mu$ replaced by the operator $\gamma\mathbf S$; the field is a given classical field; the particle's motion in space (kinetic energy, Lorentz force) is ignored or decoupled, and orbital moments are left out.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.2, eqs. (4.157)–(4.158), (4.162) and footnote 45; Greensite ch. 13, eq. (13.61)*

^mod-b6-2-2

## Larmor precession

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.3: Larmor Precession
> Let $\mathbf B = B_0\hat k$ be constant, and let $s = \tfrac12$. Then $\mathsf H = -\gamma B_0\mathsf S_z$ has the stationary states
>
> $$
> \chi_+ \ \text{with}\ E_+ = -\frac{\gamma B_0\hbar}{2}, \qquad \chi_- \ \text{with}\ E_- = +\frac{\gamma B_0\hbar}{2},
> $$
>
> the lower one having $\boldsymbol\mu$ along $\mathbf B$. Any initial state can be written (up to a phase) $\chi(0) = \bigl(\cos\frac\alpha2,\ e^{i\beta}\sin\frac\alpha2\bigr)^{\mathsf T}$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]]), and then
>
> $$
> \chi(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\alpha/2)\,e^{i\gamma B_0t/2} \\ e^{i\beta}\sin(\alpha/2)\,e^{-i\gamma B_0t/2}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
> \langle\mathbf S\rangle = \frac\hbar2\bigl(\sin\alpha\cos(\gamma B_0t - \beta),\ -\sin\alpha\sin(\gamma B_0t - \beta),\ \cos\alpha\bigr) .
> $$
>
> $\langle\mathbf S\rangle$ keeps the angle $\alpha$ with the field and turns about it at the **Larmor frequency**
>
> $$
> \omega = \gamma B_0 ,
> $$
>
> with angular-velocity vector $-\gamma\mathbf B$ (clockwise seen from $+z$ when $\gamma > 0$, anticlockwise for the electron).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 4.3, eqs. (4.159)–(4.167), footnote 46; Greensite ch. 13, eqs. (13.80)–(13.84)*

^thm-b6-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\mathsf H = -\gamma B_0\frac\hbar2\sigma_z$ is diagonal, so its eigenvectors are $\chi_\pm$ with eigenvalues $\mp\gamma B_0\hbar/2$. $\mathsf H$ does not depend on time, so the solution is the expansion in stationary states, each turning with its phase $e^{-iE_\pm t/\hbar}$ ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]]): $\chi(t) = a\,e^{-iE_+t/\hbar}\chi_+ + b\,e^{-iE_-t/\hbar}\chi_-$ with $(a, b) = \chi(0)$, which is the stated spinor. With $\omega = \gamma B_0$, $\bar a(t)b(t) = \cos\frac\alpha2\sin\frac\alpha2\,e^{i\beta}e^{-i\omega t} = \tfrac12\sin\alpha\,e^{-i(\omega t - \beta)}$ and $|a|^2 - |b|^2 = \cos\alpha$, so [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-6|Theorem §B6.1.6]] gives the three components. The azimuth of $\langle\mathbf S\rangle$ is $\beta - \omega t$, decreasing at the rate $\omega$: rotation about $\hat k$ with angular velocity $-\omega\hat k = -\gamma\mathbf B$. (Griffiths takes $\beta = 0$; a nonzero $\beta$ only shifts the origin of time.)

> [!remark] Remark: Precession frequency and spin-flip frequency
> The two energies differ by $\Delta E = E_- - E_+ = \gamma B_0\hbar$, so the Larmor frequency is $\omega = \Delta E/\hbar$: the precession is the beat between the two stationary states. For the electron, $|\gamma| = e/m$ and $|\gamma|B_0\hbar = 2\mu_BB_0$ ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^def-a5-2-1|Def. §A5.2.1]]), the splitting of [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-7|Theorem §A5.2.7]]; the electron spin resonance frequency of [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^ex-a5-2-2|Example §A5.2.2]], $4.2\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{Hz}$ at $1.5\ \mathrm T$, is the precession frequency $\omega/2\pi$. Measuring $S_x$ at time $t$ (Griffiths Problem 4.35) gives $+\hbar/2$ with probability $\bigl|\chi^{(x)\dagger}_+\chi(t)\bigr|^2 = \tfrac12\bigl[1 + \sin\alpha\cos(\omega t - \beta)\bigr]$, which oscillates at $\omega$; $S_z$ gives $+\hbar/2$ with the constant probability $\cos^2(\alpha/2)$.

^rem-b6-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.4: The Spin Obeys the Classical Torque Equation
> For a spin of any $s$ in a field $\mathbf B(t)$ that is uniform in space (Model §B6.2.2), the expectation value of the spin satisfies
>
> $$
> \frac{d\langle\mathbf S\rangle}{dt} = \gamma\,\langle\mathbf S\rangle\times\mathbf B = \langle\boldsymbol\mu\rangle\times\mathbf B ,
> $$
>
> the classical equation $d\mathbf L/dt = \boldsymbol\tau = \boldsymbol\mu\times\mathbf B$ for a magnetic dipole with angular momentum ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-2|EM Theorem §A7.4.2]]). Hence $|\langle\mathbf S\rangle|$ is constant; for a field of fixed direction the component of $\langle\mathbf S\rangle$ along $\mathbf B$ is constant too, and $\langle\mathbf S\rangle$ precesses about $\mathbf B$ with instantaneous angular velocity $-\gamma\mathbf B(t)$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.4.2, p. 220 (Ehrenfest's theorem in the form of Problem 4.23) and footnote 47*

^thm-b6-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the time-derivative theorem for expectation values ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]]), $d\langle S_x\rangle/dt = \frac i\hbar\langle[\mathsf H, S_x]\rangle$, since $S_x$ has no explicit time dependence. With $\mathsf H = -\gamma(B_xS_x + B_yS_y + B_zS_z)$ and the commutation relations of [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]],
>
> $$
> [\mathsf H, S_x] = -\gamma\bigl(B_y[S_y, S_x] + B_z[S_z, S_x]\bigr) = -\gamma\bigl(-i\hbar B_yS_z + i\hbar B_zS_y\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> so $\frac i\hbar[\mathsf H, S_x] = \gamma(B_zS_y - B_yS_z) = \gamma(\mathbf S\times\mathbf B)_x$. The other components follow by cyclic permutation. The field components are numbers (uniform field), so they pass through the expectation value. Then $\frac{d}{dt}|\langle\mathbf S\rangle|^2 = 2\langle\mathbf S\rangle\cdot\gamma(\langle\mathbf S\rangle\times\mathbf B) = 0$, and for $\mathbf B = B(t)\hat n$ with fixed $\hat n$, $\frac{d}{dt}(\hat n\cdot\langle\mathbf S\rangle) = \gamma\,\hat n\cdot(\langle\mathbf S\rangle\times\mathbf B) = 0$. Finally $\gamma\langle\mathbf S\rangle\times\mathbf B = (-\gamma\mathbf B)\times\langle\mathbf S\rangle$ is the velocity of a vector turning with angular velocity $-\gamma\mathbf B$.

^der-b6-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^mod-b6-2-2|Model §B6.2.2]], [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]], [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-2|EM Theorem §A7.4.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: What is classical and what is not
> Theorem §B6.2.4 turns the level-A analogy with a top ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^rem-a5-2-2|Remark: Torque and precession]]; for the gyroscope, [[§A6.7 Precession#^thm-a6-7-2|CM Theorem §A6.7.2]]) into a theorem, for the expectation value. Classically the angular-momentum vector itself precesses (Griffiths footnote 47); quantum mechanically only $\langle\mathbf S\rangle$ does, while any single measurement of a component still gives $\pm\hbar/2$. The equation is exact here, unlike Ehrenfest's theorem for $\langle x\rangle$ in a general potential, because $\mathsf H$ is linear in $\mathbf S$.

## The Stern–Gerlach experiment

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.5: Analysis of the Stern–Gerlach Magnet
> A beam of heavy neutral atoms with moment $\boldsymbol\mu = \gamma\mathbf S$ travels along $y$ through the static field
>
> $$
> \mathbf B(x, y, z) = -\alpha x\,\hat\imath + (B_0 + \alpha z)\,\hat k ,
> $$
>
> a strong uniform $B_0$ with a small gradient $\alpha$ (the $x$-component is required by $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$). The force $\mathbf F = \nabla(\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B)$ is
>
> $$
> \mathbf F = \gamma\alpha\bigl(-S_x\,\hat\imath + S_z\,\hat k\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> and because the Larmor precession about $B_0\hat k$ makes the $x$-part oscillate rapidly and average to zero, the net force is
>
> $$
> F_z = \gamma\alpha S_z .
> $$
>
> An atom with $S_z = m\hbar$ is deflected in proportion to $m$: the beam splits into $2s+1$ separate streams, not a continuous smear. An atom in a superposition $\sum_m c_m|s\,m\rangle$ ends up in stream $m$ with probability $|c_m|^2$, and leaves it in the state $|s\,m\rangle$.
>
> *Assumptions:* the atoms are neutral (no Lorentz force on the beam) and heavy, so that their centre of mass moves on a classical trajectory as a localised packet; the transit time $T$ through the magnet is long compared with the precession period, $|\gamma|B_0T \gg 1$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 4.4, eqs. (4.168)–(4.170), footnotes 48–50; Greensite ch. 13, eqs. (13.19)–(13.20)*

^thm-b6-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The force is minus the gradient of the energy $\mathsf H = -\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B$ (Model §B6.2.2): $\mathbf F = \nabla(\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B)$. Here $\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B = \gamma\bigl[-\alpha x\,S_x + (B_0 + \alpha z)\,S_z\bigr]$, whose gradient with respect to the position of the atom is $\gamma\alpha(-S_x, 0, S_z)$. The field has $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = -\alpha + \alpha = 0$; a field with only a $z$-component growing along $z$ would violate it ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^rem-a7-1-3|EM Remark: No magnetic charges]]). Near the beam axis $\mathbf B \approx B_0\hat k$, so by [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-4|Theorem §B6.2.4]] $\langle S_x\rangle$ and $\langle S_y\rangle$ rotate at the Larmor frequency $\gamma B_0$ while $\langle S_z\rangle$ stays fixed. Averaged over the transit time, $\bigl|\overline{\langle S_x\rangle}\bigr| \le |\langle\mathbf S\rangle|\cdot 2/(|\gamma|B_0T)$, which is negligible; $F_z = \gamma\alpha S_z$ survives. Its eigenvalues are $\gamma\alpha m\hbar$, $m = -s, \dots, s$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]]), so a state $|s\,m\rangle$ is deflected along one definite trajectory and a superposition separates into $2s+1$ packets; which stream an atom is found in is governed by the generalized statistical interpretation ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]]). (A fully quantum treatment of the centre-of-mass motion is Griffiths' Problem 4.73.)

^der-b6-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^mod-b6-2-2|Model §B6.2.2]], [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-4|Theorem §B6.2.4]], [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]], [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^rem-a7-1-3|EM Remark: No magnetic charges]], [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: From law to explanation; preparation and measurement
> - The Stern–Gerlach result stays an empirical law ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^law-a5-2-5|Law §A5.2.5]]): it is how spin was discovered. Theorem §B6.2.5 explains it: two beams for silver mean $2s + 1 = 2$, the spin of the one unpaired outer electron (the inner electrons pair off to zero, [[§B7.2 Atoms#^thm-b7-2-5|Theorem §B7.2.5]]). The level-A force law $\mu_z\,dB_z/dz$ ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-4|Theorem §A5.2.4]]) silently assumed that $\mu_x$ does not matter; the precession is the reason.
> - A Stern–Gerlach magnet both **prepares** and **measures**. Blocking all streams but one prepares a beam in $|s\,m\rangle$; recording which stream an atom leaves in measures $S_z$. Magnets in sequence realise the scenario of [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^rem-b6-1-2|Remark: A particle in χ₊ has no x-component of spin]]: a $z$-up beam sent through a magnet oriented along $x$ splits $50 : 50$, and sending the $x$-up stream through a $z$-magnet again gives both $z$-streams.
> - The method does not work for free electrons: their charge feels the Lorentz force, which swamps the tiny spin force (Griffiths footnote 49).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 4.4, pp. 221–222, footnote 49*

## A field that turns: the rotating frame

When the field depends on time the Hamiltonian matrices at different times usually do not commute, and the expansion in stationary states fails. One family of time-dependent fields can nevertheless be solved exactly: a field of fixed strength whose direction turns uniformly about an axis.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.6: Spin One-Half in a Precessing Field
> Let the field have constant magnitude $B_0$ and turn about $\hat k$ at angular velocity $\omega$ on a cone of half-angle $\theta$,
>
> $$
> \mathbf B(t) = B_0\bigl(\sin\theta\cos\omega t,\ \sin\theta\sin\omega t,\ \cos\theta\bigr) = B_0\,\hat n(t),
> $$
>
> and write $\omega_1 = \gamma B_0$ (signed). Then $\mathsf H(t) = -\frac\hbar2\omega_1\,\hat n(t)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, and with $\mathsf U(t) = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(e^{-i\omega t/2}, e^{i\omega t/2}\bigr)$:
> 1. $\mathsf H(t) = \mathsf U(t)\,\mathsf H(0)\,\mathsf U(t)^\dagger$.
> 2. $\chi(t) = \mathsf U(t)\,\tilde\chi(t)$, where $\tilde\chi$ (the state seen from the frame rotating with the field) obeys $i\hbar\,\dot{\tilde\chi} = \tilde{\mathsf H}\tilde\chi$ with the **time-independent** Hamiltonian
>
> $$
> \tilde{\mathsf H} = -\frac\hbar2\Bigl[(\omega_1\cos\theta + \omega)\,\sigma_z + \omega_1\sin\theta\,\sigma_x\Bigr] = -\frac{\hbar\Omega}2\,\hat n'\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma, \qquad \Omega = \sqrt{\omega_1^2 + \omega^2 + 2\omega_1\omega\cos\theta}, \qquad \hat n' = \frac{\omega_1\hat n(0) + \omega\hat k}{\Omega} .
> $$
>
> 3. Hence, exactly,
>
> $$
> \chi(t) = \mathsf U(t)\Bigl[\cos\frac{\Omega t}2\,\mathbb 1 + i\sin\frac{\Omega t}2\,\hat n'\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\Bigr]\chi(0) .
> $$
>
> 4. **Rabi's formula.** If $\chi(0) = \chi_+$, the probability of finding $S_z = -\hbar/2$ at time $t$ is
>
> $$
> P_{+\to-}(t) = \frac{\omega_1^2\sin^2\theta}{\Omega^2}\,\sin^2\frac{\Omega t}2 ,
> $$
>
> which reaches $1$ only at resonance, $\omega = -\omega_1\cos\theta$, that is, when the field turns at the Larmor frequency of its own $z$-component and in the same sense as the free precession.
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 6, Problem 6.7 (the field); the rotating-frame solution is worked out here (the nearest case in Griffiths is Problem 4.36, a field of fixed direction)*

^thm-b6-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\mathsf H(t) = -\gamma\mathbf B\cdot\mathsf S = -\frac\hbar2\omega_1\,\hat n(t)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$. With $\mathsf U = \operatorname{diag}(e^{-i\omega t/2}, e^{i\omega t/2})$, conjugation multiplies the upper-right entry by $e^{-i\omega t}$ and the lower-left by $e^{i\omega t}$:
>
> $$
> \mathsf U\sigma_x\mathsf U^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & e^{-i\omega t} \\ e^{i\omega t} & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \cos\omega t\,\sigma_x + \sin\omega t\,\sigma_y, \qquad \mathsf U\sigma_z\mathsf U^\dagger = \sigma_z .
> $$
>
> Since $\hat n(0) = (\sin\theta, 0, \cos\theta)$, $\mathsf U\,(\hat n(0)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\,\mathsf U^\dagger = \hat n(t)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$.
>
> *2.* Insert $\chi = \mathsf U\tilde\chi$ into $i\hbar\dot\chi = \mathsf H\chi = \mathsf U\mathsf H(0)\mathsf U^\dagger\mathsf U\tilde\chi$: $i\hbar(\dot{\mathsf U}\tilde\chi + \mathsf U\dot{\tilde\chi}) = \mathsf U\mathsf H(0)\tilde\chi$. Multiply by $\mathsf U^\dagger$ (unitary) and use $\dot{\mathsf U} = -\frac{i\omega}2\sigma_z\mathsf U$, so that $\mathsf U^\dagger\dot{\mathsf U} = -\frac{i\omega}2\sigma_z$:
>
> $$
> i\hbar\,\dot{\tilde\chi} = \Bigl[\mathsf H(0) - i\hbar\,\mathsf U^\dagger\dot{\mathsf U}\Bigr]\tilde\chi = \Bigl[-\frac\hbar2\omega_1(\cos\theta\,\sigma_z + \sin\theta\,\sigma_x) - \frac{\hbar\omega}2\sigma_z\Bigr]\tilde\chi .
> $$
>
> The vector $(\omega_1\sin\theta, 0, \omega_1\cos\theta + \omega)$ has length $\Omega$, which gives $\hat n'$.
>
> *3.* $\tilde{\mathsf H}$ is constant. $\hat n'\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ has eigenspinors $\eta_\pm$ with eigenvalues $\pm1$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]), so $\tilde{\mathsf H}\eta_\pm = \mp\frac{\hbar\Omega}2\eta_\pm$, and the stationary-state expansion gives $\tilde\chi(t) = c_+e^{i\Omega t/2}\eta_+ + c_-e^{-i\Omega t/2}\eta_-$ with $\tilde\chi(0) = \chi(0) = c_+\eta_+ + c_-\eta_-$. Writing $e^{\pm i\Omega t/2} = \cos\frac{\Omega t}2 \pm i\sin\frac{\Omega t}2$ and using $\hat n'\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\,\eta_\pm = \pm\eta_\pm$, this is $\bigl[\cos\frac{\Omega t}2 + i\sin\frac{\Omega t}2\,\hat n'\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\bigr]\tilde\chi(0)$.
>
> *4.* $\mathsf U$ is diagonal, so $|\chi_-^\dagger\chi(t)| = |\chi_-^\dagger\tilde\chi(t)|$, and $\chi_-^\dagger\chi_+ = 0$ leaves $i\sin\frac{\Omega t}2\,\chi_-^\dagger(\hat n'\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\chi_+ = i\sin\frac{\Omega t}2\,(n'_x + in'_y) = i\sin\frac{\Omega t}2\,\frac{\omega_1\sin\theta}{\Omega}$. Squaring gives $P_{+\to-}$. Since $\Omega^2 = (\omega + \omega_1\cos\theta)^2 + \omega_1^2\sin^2\theta \ge \omega_1^2\sin^2\theta$, the prefactor is at most $1$, with equality exactly when $\omega = -\omega_1\cos\theta$. The free precession has angular velocity $-\gamma\mathbf B$ (Theorem §B6.2.4), whose $z$-component is $-\omega_1\cos\theta$.

> [!remark] Remark: The rotating frame, classically and quantum mechanically
> The term $-\frac{\hbar\omega}2\sigma_z$ that appears in $\tilde{\mathsf H}$ is the quantum version of a classical fact. Viewed from a frame turning with angular velocity $\omega\hat k$, any vector changes at the rate $(d\mathbf S/dt)_{\text{rot}} = d\mathbf S/dt - \omega\hat k\times\mathbf S$ ([[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-2|CM Theorem §B9.1.2]]). With $d\mathbf S/dt = \gamma\mathbf S\times\mathbf B$ (Theorem §B6.2.4) this is $\mathbf S\times(\gamma\mathbf B + \omega\hat k)$: in the rotating frame the field looks static, and an extra "field" $\omega\hat k/\gamma$ appears, exactly the combination $\omega_1\hat n(0) + \omega\hat k = \Omega\hat n'$ of the theorem. The trick works because $\mathsf H(t)$ is a rotated copy of $\mathsf H(0)$; a general time-dependent Hamiltonian needs time-dependent perturbation theory, which is level C (planned).

^rem-b6-2-4

> [!remark] Remark: Magnetic resonance
> Rabi's formula is the principle of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR, and medical MRI) and electron spin resonance: a strong static field $B_z = B_0\cos\theta$ along $z$ plus a weak field $B_\perp = B_0\sin\theta$ rotating in the $xy$-plane. Off resonance the spin hardly responds, $P_{+\to-} \le (\gamma B_\perp)^2/(\omega + \gamma B_z)^2$; at resonance even a weak rotating field turns the spin over completely, after the time $\pi/|\gamma B_\perp|$. The resonance frequency $|\gamma|B_z$ is the Larmor frequency of the static field, which identifies the spin species (through $\gamma$) or measures the local field. Precessing nuclear spins of spin $\tfrac12$ are what MRI images (Greensite ch. 13; PHY 308's syllabus, chapter 10). The quantum mechanics of transitions driven by oscillating fields in general is level C (planned).

## Examples

> [!example]- ★ Example §B6.2.1: An Oscillating Field Along a Fixed Axis
> An electron at rest is in the field $\mathbf B = B_0\cos(\omega t)\,\hat k$ and starts in $\chi(0) = \chi^{(x)}_+$. Find $\chi(t)$, the probability of $S_x = -\hbar/2$, and the smallest $B_0$ that can flip $S_x$ completely.
>
> $\mathsf H(t) = -\gamma B_0\cos(\omega t)\,\mathsf S_z$ depends on time, but it is diagonal at every instant, so the Schrödinger equation splits into $i\hbar\dot a = -\frac{\gamma B_0\hbar}2\cos(\omega t)\,a$ and the same for $b$ with the opposite sign. Integrating,
>
> $$
> \chi(t) = \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} e^{i\varphi(t)} \\ e^{-i\varphi(t)}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \varphi(t) = \frac{\gamma B_0}{2\omega}\sin\omega t .
> $$
>
> This is Larmor precession through the accumulated angle $2\varphi = \int_0^t\gamma B\,dt'$ (Theorem §B6.2.4). By [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]],
>
> $$
> P\bigl(S_x = -\tfrac\hbar2\bigr) = \tfrac12|a - b|^2 = \tfrac14\bigl|e^{i\varphi} - e^{-i\varphi}\bigr|^2 = \sin^2\Bigl(\frac{\gamma B_0}{2\omega}\sin\omega t\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> A complete flip needs $|\varphi| = \pi/2$ at some time. Since $|\varphi| \le |\gamma|B_0/2\omega$, this requires $B_0 \ge \pi\omega/|\gamma|$, and the minimum $B_0 = \pi\omega/|\gamma| = \pi m\omega/e$ (with $|\gamma| = e/m$) flips $S_x$ at $t = \pi/2\omega$, restores it at $t = \pi/\omega$ and flips it again at $t = 3\pi/2\omega$. A static field would flip the spin sooner or later however weak it is; an oscillating one does so only if it is strong enough to turn the spin by $\pi$ within a quarter period.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 4.36*

> [!example] Example §B6.2.2: Transitions in a Precessing Field
> A spin-$\tfrac12$ particle is in the field of Theorem §B6.2.6, $B_x = B_0\sin\theta\cos\omega t$, $B_y = B_0\sin\theta\sin\omega t$, $B_z = B_0\cos\theta$. At $t = 0$ its spin component along the field is $+\hbar/2$. What is the probability that at time $t$ its spin component along the (moved) field is $-\hbar/2$?
>
> The initial state is $\chi(0) = \chi^{(n_0)}_+ = \bigl(\cos\frac\theta2, \sin\frac\theta2\bigr)^{\mathsf T}$, the eigenspinor along $\hat n(0)$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]] with $\phi = 0$). The spin-down state along $\hat n(t)$ is $\mathsf U(t)\chi^{(n_0)}_-$, up to a phase, because $\mathsf U(\hat n(0)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\mathsf U^\dagger = \hat n(t)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$; here $\chi^{(n_0)}_- = \bigl(\sin\frac\theta2, -\cos\frac\theta2\bigr)^{\mathsf T}$. So by part 3 of the theorem the amplitude is
>
> $$
> \bigl(\mathsf U\chi^{(n_0)}_-\bigr)^\dagger\chi(t) = \chi^{(n_0)\dagger}_-\Bigl[\cos\frac{\Omega t}2 + i\sin\frac{\Omega t}2\,\hat n'\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\Bigr]\chi^{(n_0)}_+ = i\sin\frac{\Omega t}2\;\chi^{(n_0)\dagger}_-\,\frac{\omega_1\hat n(0)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma + \omega\sigma_z}{\Omega}\,\chi^{(n_0)}_+ .
> $$
>
> The first term vanishes, since $\hat n(0)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\,\chi^{(n_0)}_+ = \chi^{(n_0)}_+$ is orthogonal to $\chi^{(n_0)}_-$. In the second, $\sigma_z\chi^{(n_0)}_+ = \bigl(\cos\frac\theta2, -\sin\frac\theta2\bigr)^{\mathsf T}$ and $\chi^{(n_0)\dagger}_-\sigma_z\chi^{(n_0)}_+ = 2\sin\frac\theta2\cos\frac\theta2 = \sin\theta$. Therefore
>
> $$
> P(t) = \frac{\omega^2\sin^2\theta}{\Omega^2}\,\sin^2\frac{\Omega t}2, \qquad \Omega^2 = \omega_1^2 + \omega^2 + 2\omega_1\omega\cos\theta, \quad \omega_1 = \gamma B_0 .
> $$
>
> *Checks and limits.*
> - $\omega = 0$ (static field) or $\theta = 0$ (field along the axis): $P = 0$, the stationary state stays put.
> - *Slow turning*, $|\omega| \ll |\omega_1|$: $P \le (\omega/\omega_1)^2\sin^2\theta \ll 1$. The spin follows the field: it precesses fast about it while the field moves slowly (the adiabatic regime, level C).
> - *Fast turning*, $|\omega| \gg |\omega_1|$: $\Omega \to |\omega|$ and $P \to \sin^2\theta\,\sin^2(\omega t/2)$. Now the spin has no time to move; it stays along $\hat n(0)$, and $P$ is just the probability $\sin^2(\vartheta/2)$ of finding "down" along $\hat n(t)$, where $\cos\vartheta = \hat n(0)\cdot\hat n(t) = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta\cos\omega t$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^rem-b6-1-3|Remark: Spin one-half as a sphere of directions]]).
> - *Complete reversal* is possible: the prefactor $\omega^2\sin^2\theta/\Omega^2$ equals $1$ exactly when $\omega = -\omega_1/\cos\theta$ (for $\theta \ne \pi/2$), where $\Omega = |\omega_1\tan\theta|$. The field must turn in the sense of the free Larmor precession, faster than it by the factor $1/\cos\theta$.
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 6, Problem 6.7 (solved here)*
