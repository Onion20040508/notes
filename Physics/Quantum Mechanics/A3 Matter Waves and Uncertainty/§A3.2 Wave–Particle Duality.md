Light diffracts like a wave and is absorbed in photons; electrons are counted one by one and diffract like X-rays ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves|§A3.1]]). Is an electron a wave or a particle? The double-slit experiment, run one particle at a time, shows in its simplest form how both descriptions are needed and how they fit together: particles arrive at points, and a wave decides where they are likely to arrive. This prepares the probabilistic reading of the wave function in [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability|§A3.4]].

## Particles and waves

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.1: Classical Particle; Classical Wave
> A **classical particle** is localized: it follows a path, is scattered at a point, exchanges energy and momentum with other bodies in whole lumps in collisions, and shows no interference or diffraction. A **classical wave** is spread out in space: its energy is distributed continuously and can be exchanged in any amount, and it shows interference and diffraction.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-7*

^def-a3-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.2: Wave–Particle Duality
> **Wave–particle duality** is the observed fact that radiation and matter each show particle behaviour under some experimental conditions and wave behaviour under others. Light behaves as a wave in interference and as a stream of photons in the photoelectric and Compton effects ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect|§A1.2]], [[§A1.3 The Compton Effect|§A1.3]]); an electron behaves as a particle in a cathode-ray tube or a collision and as a wave in crystal diffraction ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^ex-a3-1-2|Example §A3.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.6 · Tipler §5-7*

^def-a3-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: An old dichotomy
> The question is older than quantum physics. Newton held light to be a stream of corpuscles and Huygens a wave; Young's double-slit experiment (1803) and Maxwell's theory (1873) settled it for the wave, until blackbody radiation and the photoelectric effect brought the particles back. Electricity, conversely, was treated as a continuous fluid until Thomson found the electron (1897), and the electron was a particle until Davisson and Germer diffracted it (1927). Neither the classical particle nor the classical wave of [[§A3.2 Wave–Particle Duality#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]] describes these objects; quantum mechanics replaces both.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.6*

^rem-a3-2-1

## The double slit, one particle at a time

> [!law] Law §A3.2.1: Single-Particle Interference
> Send particles of one momentum $p$ toward two narrow slits a distance $d$ apart, so few per second that only one is in the apparatus at a time, and record their arrivals on a screen far behind.
> 1. Each particle is detected whole, at a single point; a detector never registers part of a particle.
> 2. Where a given particle will land cannot be predicted; identically prepared particles land at different places.
> 3. The arrivals of many particles build up the interference pattern of a wave of wavelength $\lambda = h/p$ passing through both slits, with bright fringes at $d\sin\theta = n\lambda$ ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|WO Theorem §A6.1.3]]). The pattern depends only on the number of particles recorded, not on the rate.
> 4. With either slit closed the fringes disappear, and the pattern with both slits open is not the sum of the two one-slit patterns.
>
> *Valid when: nothing in the apparatus records which slit a particle passes through. Observed for photons in very weak light and for electrons (beams: Jönsson 1961; single electrons: Pozzi 1974, Tonomura 1989); neutrons, atoms and molecules show the same wave behaviour in diffraction ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^rem-a3-1-5|Remark: More evidence, and other particles]]).*
> *Source: UP3 §6.6, §7.1 · Tipler §5-4 · Tipler, More Chapter 5 #1 (PHY 284 handout)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.2: Two-Slit Probability
> Let $\Psi_1$ and $\Psi_2$ be the waves that reach a point of the screen through slit 1 alone and through slit 2 alone, so that the probability densities with one slit open are $P_1 = |\Psi_1|^2$ and $P_2 = |\Psi_2|^2$. With both slits open,
>
> $$
> P_{12} = |\Psi_1 + \Psi_2|^2 = P_1 + P_2 + 2\sqrt{P_1P_2}\,\cos\delta, \qquad \delta = \frac{2\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda} ,
> $$
>
> where $\delta$ is the phase difference of the two waves at angle $\theta$. For equal amplitudes, $P_{12} = 4P_1\cos^2(\delta/2)$: twice $P_1 + P_2$ at the bright fringes and zero at the dark ones.
>
> *Source: Tipler, More Chapter 5 #1, eq. (5-29) (PHY 284 handout) · UP3 §7.1*

^thm-a3-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Waves passing through the two slits add ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^pr-a2-5-1|WO Principle §A2.5.1]]; for matter waves this is the linearity of the Schrödinger equation, [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-2|Theorem §A4.1.2]]), so with both slits open the wave at the screen is $\Psi = \Psi_1 + \Psi_2$, and its probability density is $|\Psi_1 + \Psi_2|^2$ by the Born rule ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-1|Principle §A3.4.1]]). Write $\Psi_j = |\Psi_j|e^{i\phi_j}$. Since $|z|^2 = z\bar z$ for complex numbers ([[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-4|LADR Thm. 4.4]]),
>
> $$
> |\Psi_1 + \Psi_2|^2 = |\Psi_1|^2 + |\Psi_2|^2 + \Psi_1\Psi_2^* + \Psi_1^*\Psi_2 = |\Psi_1|^2 + |\Psi_2|^2 + 2|\Psi_1||\Psi_2|\cos(\phi_1 - \phi_2) .
> $$
>
> The two paths differ in length by $d\sin\theta$, so the phases differ by $\delta = 2\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda$, as for light ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|WO Theorem §A6.1.1]]). For $P_1 = P_2$ the result is $2P_1(1 + \cos\delta) = 4P_1\cos^2(\delta/2)$, the same $\cos^2$ fringes as for light ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-5|WO Theorem §A6.1.5]]).

^der-a3-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^pr-a2-5-1|WO Principle §A2.5.1]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-1|Principle §A3.4.1]], [[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-4|LADR Thm. 4.4]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|WO Theorem §A6.1.1]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-5|WO Theorem §A6.1.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: Amplitudes add, not probabilities
> If each electron went through one slit or the other, the electrons through slit 1 would land with density $P_1$ and those through slit 2 with $P_2$, and the total would be $P_1 + P_2$. The interference term $2\sqrt{P_1P_2}\cos\delta$ says otherwise: the *amplitudes* $\Psi_1$ and $\Psi_2$ add and are then squared. Points that receive electrons with either slit alone receive none with both open. The phases $\phi_1, \phi_2$ are not observable separately; only their difference shows, through the fringes.

^rem-a3-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Watching the slits
> Try to find out which slit each electron takes, for instance by shining light just behind the slits and seeing where each electron scatters it. Every electron is then seen at one slit or the other, never at both, and always whole. But the fringes disappear: the electrons seen at slit 1 land with density $P_1$, those seen at slit 2 with $P_2$, and the total is $P_1 + P_2$. The observation has disturbed the electrons enough to wash out the pattern. A rough estimate shows why: to tell the slits apart, the electron must be located to $\Delta y \approx d/2$; the uncertainty principle ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]), used as the order-of-magnitude estimate $\Delta y\,\Delta p_y \gtrsim \hbar$, then requires a sideways momentum spread $\Delta p_y \gtrsim 2\hbar/d$, which deflects the electron by an uncertain angle $\Delta\theta \approx \Delta p_y/p \approx \lambda/(\pi d)$. That is comparable to the angle $\lambda/(2d)$ from a bright fringe to the next dark one, so the fringes are smeared out.
>
> *Source: Tipler, More Chapter 5 #1 (PHY 284 handout) · PHY 284, class 20–21 slides*

^rem-a3-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Complementarity
> Particle aspects dominate when matter or radiation is emitted, absorbed or detected: these are events at one place with an exchange of energy and momentum, as in the Compton effect. Wave aspects dominate while it propagates: the probability of where it will be detected is found by adding waves, with interference and diffraction. Predictions are made with waves, observations are of particles. Bohr called this **complementarity**: both aspects are needed, but a given experimental arrangement displays only one of them. When the wavelength is much smaller than every aperture and obstacle, wave and particle descriptions agree, which is why geometric optics and the mechanics of baseballs work.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-7 · Tipler, More Chapter 5 #1 (PHY 284 handout)*

^rem-a3-2-4

> [!caution] Caution: Common misreadings
> - The fringes are not caused by electrons interfering with *each other*: they build up when the electrons pass one at a time, each long after the previous one has been detected. Each electron's wave goes through both slits.
> - The electron does not split into two halves at the slits: a detector at the slits always finds a whole electron at one of them.
> - The bright fringes are not where "the electron" is spread out; they are where the probability of detecting it is large ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability|§A3.4]]). Two electrons sent in exactly the same way can land in different places: the theory predicts the distribution, not the individual arrival.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.2.1: Electron Double Slit
> In an electron interference experiment two slits $62\ \mathrm{nm}$ wide and $d = 272\ \mathrm{nm}$ apart are cut in a gold-coated membrane, and electrons accelerated from rest through $600\ \mathrm V$ are sent through them. Where is the first-order bright fringe?
>
> Here $K = 600\ \mathrm{eV} \ll 511\ \mathrm{keV}$, so ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]])
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{hc}{\sqrt{2E_0K}} = \frac{1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}}{\sqrt{2(511\times10^3\ \mathrm{eV})(600\ \mathrm{eV})}} = 0.0501\ \mathrm{nm},
> $$
>
> and by [[§A3.2 Wave–Particle Duality#^law-a3-2-1|Law §A3.2.1]] with $n = 1$,
>
> $$
> \sin\theta_1 = \frac{\lambda}{d} = \frac{0.0501\ \mathrm{nm}}{272\ \mathrm{nm}} = 1.84\times10^{-4}, \qquad \theta_1 = 0.0105^\circ .
> $$
>
> The fringes are about $0.01^\circ$ apart, which is why electron interference needs slits a few hundred nanometres apart and a long flight path, or a magnified image of the pattern.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 6.15*

^ex-a3-2-1

> [!example] Example §A3.2.2: Seeing a Virus with Electrons
> A virus $20\ \mathrm{nm}$ across is smaller than the wavelength of visible light ($\approx 500\ \mathrm{nm}$), so an optical microscope cannot resolve it: the diffraction limit is of the order of the wavelength ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-1|WO Theorem §A7.4.1]]). For a sharp image with electrons, take a de Broglie wavelength $1000$ times smaller than the virus, $\lambda = 0.020\ \mathrm{nm}$. The required kinetic energy is
>
> $$
> K = \frac{p^2}{2m} = \frac{(hc/\lambda)^2}{2mc^2} = \frac{(1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}/0.020\ \mathrm{nm})^2}{2(0.511\times10^6\ \mathrm{eV})} = 3.8\ \mathrm{keV},
> $$
>
> small compared with $mc^2$, so the non-relativistic formula is fine: an accelerating voltage of about $4\ \mathrm{kV}$ suffices. Electron microscopes use exactly this wave property, with magnetic lenses in place of glass ones: transmission electron microscopes accelerate electrons to as much as $400\ \mathrm{keV}$ and resolve better than $0.05\ \mathrm{nm}$, against about $100\ \mathrm{nm}$ for the best light microscopes.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Midterm 2 practice (problem 8) and answers · UP3 §6.6 (electron microscopy)*
