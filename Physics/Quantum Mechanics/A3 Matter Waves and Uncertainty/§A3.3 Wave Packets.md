A matter wave of a single wavelength fills all of space, and its crests move at a speed that is not the particle's. A particle is somewhere, and moves at its own speed. The way out is the wave packet: a superposition of waves with a spread of wavelengths, which is localized and whose envelope moves at the group velocity. For de Broglie waves the group velocity is exactly the particle velocity. The price of localization is a spread of wave numbers; multiplied by $\hbar$, this spread becomes the uncertainty principle of [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle|§A3.5]].

## Harmonic waves and phase velocity

> [!definition] Definition §A3.3.1: Harmonic Wave; Phase Velocity
> A **harmonic** (**plane**) **wave** is a sinusoidal wave of a single wave number $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ and angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$, such as $y = y_0\cos(kx - \omega t)$ ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-2|WO Theorem §A2.2.2]]) or, for a matter wave, $\Psi = A\,e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$. Its crests move with the **phase velocity**
>
> $$
> v_p = f\lambda = \frac{\omega}{k} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-3, eqs. (5-12)–(5-14)*

^def-a3-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.1: Phase Velocity of a Matter Wave
> The de Broglie wave of a free particle of momentum $p$ and energy $E$ has $v_p = E/p$. With the Newtonian kinetic energy $E = p^2/2m$ of a free particle of speed $v$,
>
> $$
> v_p = \frac{v}{2} ;
> $$
>
> with the total relativistic energy $E = \gamma mc^2$ of a particle of speed $u$, $v_p = c^2/u > c$. In neither case is the phase velocity the speed of the particle.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-3 · UP3 §6.5, eq. (6.57) · PHY 284, class 20 slides*

^thm-a3-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]], $\omega = E/\hbar$ and $k = p/\hbar$, so $v_p = \omega/k = E/p$. With $E = p^2/2m$ and $p = mv$, $E/p = p/2m = v/2$. With $E = \gamma mc^2$ and $p = \gamma mu$ ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|REL Def. §A3.2.2]], [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-1|REL Def. §A3.1.1]]), $E/p = c^2/u$, which exceeds $c$ since $u < c$.

^der-a3-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|REL Def. §A3.2.2]], [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-1|REL Def. §A3.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: A single harmonic wave cannot be a particle
> Two features of the harmonic matter wave clash with the particle it should describe. It has the same amplitude everywhere, from $x = -\infty$ to $+\infty$, so it says nothing about where the particle is. And its crests move at $v_p \neq v$; worse, $v_p$ depends on the choice of the zero of energy (it changes from $v/2$ to $c^2/u$ when the rest energy is included), so it cannot be a physical speed. Both problems are solved by superposing many harmonic waves.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-3*

^rem-a3-3-1

## Wave packets and group velocity

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.2: Group of Two Waves
> Two harmonic waves of equal amplitude with nearby wave numbers $k_1, k_2$ and angular frequencies $\omega_1, \omega_2$ add to
>
> $$
> y_0\cos(k_1x - \omega_1t) + y_0\cos(k_2x - \omega_2t) = 2y_0\cos\Bigl(\frac{\Delta k}{2}x - \frac{\Delta\omega}{2}t\Bigr)\cos(\bar kx - \bar\omega t),
> $$
>
> with $\Delta k = k_2 - k_1$, $\Delta\omega = \omega_2 - \omega_1$, $\bar k = (k_1 + k_2)/2$, $\bar\omega = (\omega_1 + \omega_2)/2$. The fast factor moves at $\bar\omega/\bar k$, the phase velocity; the slowly varying envelope, $2y_0\cos[\tfrac12\Delta k(x - (\Delta\omega/\Delta k)t)]$, moves at $\Delta\omega/\Delta k$. Successive zeros of the envelope are $2\pi/\Delta k$ apart.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-3, eq. (5-15)*

^thm-a3-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply $\cos A + \cos B = 2\cos\frac{A - B}{2}\cos\frac{A + B}{2}$ with $A = k_2x - \omega_2t$, $B = k_1x - \omega_1t$: then $\frac{A - B}{2} = \frac{\Delta k}{2}x - \frac{\Delta\omega}{2}t$ and $\frac{A + B}{2} = \bar kx - \bar\omega t$. A function of $x - Vt$ is a shape moving at speed $V$ ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-1|WO Theorem §A2.2.1]]); the envelope is a function of $x - (\Delta\omega/\Delta k)t$ and the carrier one of $x - (\bar\omega/\bar k)t$. The envelope's zeros are where $\frac{\Delta k}{2}x$ changes by $\pi$, a distance $2\pi/\Delta k$ apart. At a fixed point the same identity gives beats in time ([[§A3.4 Beats#^thm-a3-4-1|WO Theorem §A3.4.1]]).

^der-a3-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]], [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-1|WO Theorem §A2.2.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A3.3.2: Wave Packet; Group Velocity
> A **wave packet** (**wave group**) is a superposition of harmonic waves whose wave numbers fill a range $\Delta k$ around a central value $k_0$, with amplitudes chosen so that the sum is appreciable only in a limited region $\Delta x$ and small outside it. Its slowly varying amplitude is the **envelope**. Its **group velocity** is
>
> $$
> v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk}\bigg|_{k_0} ,
> $$
>
> the speed of the envelope: for two waves it is the $\Delta\omega/\Delta k$ of [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], and for a narrow range of wave numbers $\Delta\omega/\Delta k \to d\omega/dk$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-3 · UP3 §7.2 · PHY 284, class 20 slides*

^def-a3-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: From two waves to one packet
> Two waves give an endless row of groups. Adding more waves with wave numbers spread over the same range thins the row out: Tipler adds seven cosines with evenly spaced wave numbers and gets groups that repeat at the distance over which all seven come back into step. Superposing infinitely many waves with continuously varying $k$ leaves only the central group, since no distance contains a whole number of wavelengths of every component: a single packet. Writing a given localized shape as such a superposition is Fourier analysis (Oscillations, Waves and Optics, Chapter B4, planned), and showing that its envelope moves at $d\omega/dk$ in general belongs to the theory of wave packets and dispersion (Oscillations, Waves and Optics, Chapter B6, planned).
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-3*

^rem-a3-3-2

> [!definition] Definition §A3.3.3: Dispersive and Nondispersive Media
> A wave medium is **nondispersive** if the phase velocity is the same for all wavelengths, and **dispersive** if it depends on the wavelength ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^def-a4-3-2|WO Def. §A4.3.2]]). Waves on an ideal string, sound in air and light in vacuum are nondispersive; water waves, light in glass and matter waves are dispersive.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-3*

^def-a3-3-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.3: Group and Phase Velocity
> The two velocities are related by
>
> $$
> v_g = v_p + k\,\frac{dv_p}{dk} .
> $$
>
> In a nondispersive medium $v_g = v_p$ and a wave packet keeps its shape as it moves; in a dispersive medium $v_g \neq v_p$ and the shape changes as it moves.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-3, eq. (5-16)*

^thm-a3-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $\omega = kv_p(k)$ ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]]) and differentiate with the product rule: $d\omega/dk = v_p + k\,dv_p/dk$. If $v_p = v$ is the same for every component, then $dv_p/dk = 0$, and every component $\cos(kx - \omega t) = \cos[k(x - vt)]$ is a function of $x - vt$; so is their sum, which is therefore a fixed shape moving at $v$ ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-1|WO Theorem §A2.2.1]]). If $v_p$ depends on $k$, the components drift relative to one another, their phase relations change, and so does the shape of the sum.

^der-a3-3-3

## Particle wave packets

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.4: The Group Velocity Is the Particle Velocity
> For the de Broglie waves of a free particle,
>
> $$
> v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{dE}{dp} ,
> $$
>
> and this equals the particle's velocity: $p/m = v$ in Newtonian mechanics and $pc^2/E = u$ in relativity. A particle is therefore described by a wave packet that travels with it, while the crests inside the packet move at the phase velocity.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-3 · UP3 §6.5 · PHY 284, class 20 slides*

^thm-a3-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $\omega = E/\hbar$ and $k = p/\hbar$ ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]]), $d\omega/dk = (dE/\hbar)/(dp/\hbar) = dE/dp$. Newtonian: $E = p^2/2m$ gives $dE/dp = p/m = v$. Relativistic: differentiating $E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|REL Theorem §A3.3.1]]) gives $2E\,dE = 2pc^2\,dp$, so $dE/dp = pc^2/E = \gamma muc^2/(\gamma mc^2) = u$. An additive constant in $E$, such as the rest energy, drops out of $dE/dp$, so $v_g$ does not depend on the choice of the zero of energy, unlike $v_p$.

> [!remark] Remark: Matter waves are dispersive, so free packets spread
> For a free non-relativistic particle $\omega = \hbar k^2/2m$, so $v_p = \hbar k/2m$ depends on $k$: matter waves are dispersive ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^def-a3-3-3|Def. §A3.3.3]]), and $v_g = 2v_p$ by [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]. The components of a packet with larger $k$ outrun those with smaller $k$, and the packet spreads as it moves: the position of a free particle becomes less and less well defined. De Broglie chose his relations so that $v_g$ equals the particle velocity; that it does, both in Newtonian and in relativistic mechanics, was one of his arguments for them.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-3, §5-4 (Figure 5-19)*

^rem-a3-3-3

> [!example] Example §A3.3.1: Phase and Group Velocity of an Electron
> An electron moves at $v = 1.00\times10^6\ \mathrm{m/s}$, well below $c$. Its de Broglie wavelength is $\lambda = h/mv = (6.63\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s})/[(9.11\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg})(1.00\times10^6\ \mathrm{m/s})] = 0.728\ \mathrm{nm}$. By [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]] its phase velocity is
>
> $$
> v_p = \frac{v}{2} = 5.0\times10^5\ \mathrm{m/s} \quad (E = p^2/2m), \qquad v_p = \frac{c^2}{v} = 9.0\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 300\,c \quad (E = \gamma mc^2),
> $$
>
> two values differing by a factor $180\,000$ for the same electron, while by [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]] the group velocity is $v_g = 1.00\times10^6\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in both descriptions. Only $v_g$ is the speed at which anything, the electron or a signal, travels; the faster-than-light phase velocity violates nothing.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-3 · UP3 §6.5 (numbers chosen here)*

## Classical uncertainty relations

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.5: Classical Uncertainty Relations
> A wave packet of extent $\Delta x$ in space contains wave numbers spread over a range $\Delta k$, and a pulse of duration $\Delta t$ contains angular frequencies spread over $\Delta\omega$, with
>
> $$
> \Delta k\,\Delta x \gtrsim 1, \qquad \Delta\omega\,\Delta t \gtrsim 1 .
> $$
>
> Equivalently $\Delta x\,\Delta\lambda \gtrsim \lambda^2/2\pi$. These are orders of magnitude: the exact constant depends on the shape of the packet and on how the widths are defined. With the widths taken as standard deviations, $\sigma_x\sigma_k \ge \tfrac12$, with equality for a Gaussian packet.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-3, eqs. (5-17)–(5-20) · PHY 284, class 22 slides*

^thm-a3-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The idea, not a proof. For two waves the group between two zeros of the envelope has length $\Delta x = 2\pi/\Delta k$ ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]), so $\Delta k\,\Delta x = 2\pi$: a shorter group needs a larger difference of wave numbers. For a packet whose components, with $k$ spread over $\Delta k$, are all in phase at the centre $x = 0$: at a distance $x$ their phases $kx$ differ by up to $\Delta k\,|x|$. While this is small compared with $\pi$ they still add up; once it reaches about $\pi$ they start to cancel. So the packet extends over a region of order $1/\Delta k$ to $2\pi/\Delta k$ on either side, and to make it narrower one must include a wider range of $k$; a single wave number ($\Delta k = 0$) gives a wave of infinite extent. The same argument at a fixed point, with $\omega t$ in place of $kx$, gives $\Delta\omega\,\Delta t \gtrsim 1$. Since $k = 2\pi/\lambda$, $|dk| = 2\pi\,|d\lambda|/\lambda^2$, which turns $\Delta k\,\Delta x \gtrsim 1$ into $\Delta x\,\Delta\lambda \gtrsim \lambda^2/2\pi$. The sharp inequality $\sigma_x\sigma_k \ge \tfrac12$ and the equality for Gaussians are theorems of Fourier analysis (Chapter B1, planned; Oscillations, Waves and Optics, Chapter B4, planned).

^der-a3-3-5

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^def-a3-3-2|Def. §A3.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Not only quantum
> These relations are properties of all waves, not of quantum mechanics. A short pulse needs a wide band of frequencies, which is why an amplifier or a communications link that must pass short signals needs a large bandwidth (the *response time–bandwidth relation*), and why the frequency of a signal can only be measured to within $\Delta f \approx 1/(2\pi\Delta t)$ in a time $\Delta t$. Applied to de Broglie waves, with $p = \hbar k$ and $E = \hbar\omega$, they become the Heisenberg relations ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle|§A3.5]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-3*

^rem-a3-3-4

> [!example] Example §A3.3.2: A Whistle Blast
> A police whistle of frequency $2500\ \mathrm{Hz}$ is blown for $\Delta t = 3.0\ \mathrm s$; sound travels at $343\ \mathrm{m/s}$. By [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-5|Theorem §A3.3.5]], with $\Delta\omega = 2\pi\Delta f$, the frequency is uncertain by
>
> $$
> \Delta f \approx \frac{1}{2\pi\Delta t} = \frac{1}{2\pi(3.0\ \mathrm s)} = 0.05\ \mathrm{Hz} .
> $$
>
> The wave train is $\Delta x = v\Delta t = 1029\ \mathrm m \approx 1\ \mathrm{km}$ long, with $\lambda = v/f = 0.137\ \mathrm m$ and $k = 2\pi/\lambda = 45.8\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Its wavelength is therefore defined only to within
>
> $$
> \Delta\lambda \approx \frac{\lambda^2}{2\pi\Delta x} = \frac{(0.137\ \mathrm m)^2}{2\pi(1029\ \mathrm m)} = 3\ \mu\mathrm m .
> $$
>
> A train of about $7500$ wavelengths fixes $\lambda$ to about one part in $50\,000$; factors of $2$ or $2\pi$ in such estimates are not significant.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 7 and solutions*
