Chapters A1 and A2 showed that light, a wave, also comes in particles: photons with $E = hf$ and $p = h/\lambda$. In 1924 de Broglie proposed the converse: every particle of matter also has a wave, with the same two relations. This section states his hypothesis, computes the wavelengths it predicts, shows how it turns Bohr's quantization rule into a standing-wave condition, and describes the electron-diffraction experiments that confirmed it.

## The de Broglie relations

> [!principle] Principle §A3.1.1: De Broglie Relations
> A particle of total energy $E$ and momentum $\vec p$ is accompanied by a wave whose frequency $f$ and wavelength $\lambda$ are
>
> $$
> f = \frac{E}{h}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{h}{p} .
> $$
>
> With the wave number $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ and the angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$ ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-2|WO Def. §A2.2.2]]), and with $\hbar = h/2\pi$,
>
> $$
> E = \hbar\omega, \qquad \vec p = \hbar\vec k ,
> $$
>
> where the wave vector $\vec k$ points along $\vec p$. $E$ and $p$ are the relativistic energy and momentum ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|REL Def. §A3.2.2]], [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-1|REL Def. §A3.1.1]]); for a photon they reduce to Einstein's relations ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]).
>
> *Domain: every particle, massive or massless, charged or neutral, at any speed. A single wavelength belongs to a particle of definite momentum, such as a free particle in a uniform beam.*
> *Source: UP3 §6.5, eqs. (6.53)–(6.56) · Tipler §5-1, eqs. (5-1)–(5-2)*

^pr-a3-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.1: Matter Wave; de Broglie Wavelength
> The wave that [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]] attaches to a particle is its **matter wave** (**de Broglie wave**); $\lambda = h/p$ is the particle's **de Broglie wavelength**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.5 · Tipler §5-1*

^def-a3-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why de Broglie proposed it
> For a photon, $E = pc$ together with $E = hf$ gives $p = hf/c = h/\lambda$ ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect|§A1.2]], [[§A1.3 The Compton Effect|§A1.3]]): the wave quantities $f, \lambda$ and the particle quantities $E, p$ are tied together by $h$. Radiation, long known as a wave, had turned out to have particle properties. De Broglie, in his 1924 doctoral thesis, postulated the symmetric statement for matter, with no experimental evidence yet: the same two relations hold for electrons and all other particles. Einstein supported the idea, and within three years it was confirmed by electron diffraction (below) and had been developed by Schrödinger into wave mechanics ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States|§A4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-1 · PHY 284, class 19 slides*

^rem-a3-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: What is waving?
> The de Broglie wave cannot be an ordinary wave in a medium. For a slow particle $p = mv$, so its wavelength $h/mv$ changes with the observer's frame: in a frame moving along with the particle, $p = 0$ and the wavelength is infinite. A displacement of a physical medium would have a frame-independent wavelength in Galilean physics, so $\lambda$ itself cannot be what an instrument records. What experiments do record is where particles arrive, and the wave fixes the *probability* of those arrivals: the wave function and its interpretation are the subject of [[§A3.2 Wave–Particle Duality|§A3.2]] and [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability|§A3.4]].
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 19–20 slides · Tipler §5-3*

^rem-a3-1-2

## Wavelength and kinetic energy

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.1.2: De Broglie Wavelength from the Kinetic Energy
> A particle of rest energy $E_0 = mc^2$ and kinetic energy $K$ has
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{hc}{\sqrt{K(K + 2E_0)}} .
> $$
>
> For $K \ll E_0$ this becomes $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2mK}$. An electron accelerated from rest through a potential difference $V_0$ (with $eV_0 \ll mc^2$) has
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{1.226\ \mathrm{nm}}{\sqrt{V_0/(1\ \mathrm V)}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.5, eq. (6.58) · Tipler §5-2, eqs. (5-4), (5-9)*

^thm-a3-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The total energy is $E = K + E_0$, and $E^2 = (pc)^2 + E_0^2$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|REL Theorem §A3.3.1]]). Hence $(pc)^2 = (K + E_0)^2 - E_0^2 = K(K + 2E_0)$, and $\lambda = h/p = hc/(pc)$ by [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]]. If $K \ll E_0$, then $K + 2E_0 \approx 2E_0$ and $pc \approx \sqrt{2E_0K}$, that is $p = \sqrt{2mK}$, the Newtonian relation between momentum and kinetic energy. Accelerating an electron from rest through $V_0$ gives it $K = eV_0$ ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-2|EM Def. §A3.2.2]]), and with $hc = 1239.8\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}$ and $mc^2 = 0.511\ \mathrm{MeV}$,
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{hc}{\sqrt{2\,mc^2\,eV_0}} = \frac{1239.8\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}}{\sqrt{2(0.511\times10^6\ \mathrm{eV})(1\ \mathrm{eV})}}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{V_0/(1\ \mathrm V)}} = \frac{1.226\ \mathrm{nm}}{\sqrt{V_0/(1\ \mathrm V)}} .
> $$

^der-a3-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|REL Theorem §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-2|EM Def. §A3.2.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Which energy, which momentum
> - $E$ in $f = E/h$ is the *total* relativistic energy, rest energy included. The frequency of a matter wave therefore depends on where the zero of energy is put and cannot be measured directly; only frequency differences, that is energy differences, have consequences ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets|§A3.3]]). The wavelength, fixed by $p$, has no such ambiguity.
> - Use the relativistic formula once $K$ is no longer small compared with $mc^2$ ($511\ \mathrm{keV}$ for an electron, $938\ \mathrm{MeV}$ for a proton). Since $\lambda f = E/p = c^2/u$ for a particle of speed $u$, the product $\lambda f$ exceeds $c$ for a massive particle: it is the phase velocity, not the particle's speed ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets|§A3.3]]).

^cau-a3-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why everyday matter shows no wave behaviour
> Wave effects show up only when an aperture or obstacle is not much larger than the wavelength: behind a slit of width $a$, diffraction spreads the wave over angles $\theta \sim \lambda/a$ ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-2|WO Theorem §A7.1.2]]). Because $h$ is so small, a ball or even a speck of dust has a wavelength far below any aperture that exists, and moves as a classical particle; an electron of a few eV has a wavelength of atomic size, about the spacing of atoms in a crystal. As a rough rule, wave mechanics is needed when $\lambda$ is not small compared with the size of the system: it is for an electron in an atom (in the first Bohr orbit $\lambda = 2\pi a_0$, by [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^thm-a3-1-3|Theorem §A3.1.3]]) and for a nucleon in a nucleus, but not for the $10\ \mathrm{keV}$ electrons of an old television tube, whose $\lambda \approx 0.01\ \mathrm{nm}$ is far below any feature of the screen.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-1, §5-2 · PHY 284, Midterm 2 practice*

^rem-a3-1-3

> [!example] Example §A3.1.1: How Long Are Matter Waves?
> (a) A $0.65\ \mathrm{kg}$ basketball at $10\ \mathrm{m/s}$ is non-relativistic, $p = mu = 6.5\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$:
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{6.63\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s}}{6.5\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}} = 1.0\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> (b) An electron with $K = 1.0\ \mathrm{eV} \ll 511\ \mathrm{keV}$: by [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]],
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}}{\sqrt{(1.0\ \mathrm{eV})(1.0\ \mathrm{eV} + 1.022\ \mathrm{MeV})}} = 1.23\ \mathrm{nm} .
> $$
>
> (c) An electron with $K = 108\ \mathrm{keV}$, a fifth of its rest energy:
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}}{\sqrt{(108\ \mathrm{keV})(108\ \mathrm{keV} + 1022\ \mathrm{keV})}} = 3.55\ \mathrm{pm} ;
> $$
>
> the non-relativistic formula $hc/\sqrt{2E_0K}$ would give $3.73\ \mathrm{pm}$, $5\%$ too long. The ball's wavelength is $10^{19}$ times smaller than a nucleus and undetectable; the electrons' wavelengths are of atomic size and smaller, which is what diffraction by crystals and the electron microscope ([[§A3.2 Wave–Particle Duality|§A3.2]]) need.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 6.11*

## Standing waves and Bohr's quantization rule

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.1.3: Bohr's Quantization as a Standing-Wave Condition
> Suppose the electron in a circular orbit of radius $r$ is a de Broglie wave that runs around the orbit and must join smoothly onto itself, so that a whole number of wavelengths fits on the circumference:
>
> $$
> 2\pi r = n\lambda, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
> $$
>
> Then its angular momentum is quantized as Bohr postulated ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^mod-a2-2-1|Model §A2.2.1]]):
>
> $$
> L = rp = n\hbar .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.5, eqs. (6.59)–(6.60) · Tipler §5-1, eq. (5-3)*

^thm-a3-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> On a string fixed at both ends, only standing waves with a whole number of half-wavelengths on the string persist ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|WO Theorem §A2.6.2]]). Bend the string into a closed ring: there are no ends, but the wave must have the same value and slope after one full turn, so a whole number of *full* wavelengths must fit, $2\pi r = n\lambda$. With $\lambda = h/p$ ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]]), $2\pi r = nh/p$, so $rp = nh/2\pi = n\hbar$. In a circular orbit the momentum is perpendicular to the radius, so $rp$ is the magnitude of the angular momentum $L = |\vec r \times \vec p|$ ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^def-a6-6-1|CM Def. §A6.6.1]]).

^der-a3-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]], [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|WO Theorem §A2.6.2]], [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^def-a6-6-1|CM Def. §A6.6.1]]

![[ph-qm-a3-1-1.svg]]
*Left: a de Broglie wave with exactly three wavelengths on the circumference (blue) closes on itself; this is the $n = 3$ Bohr orbit. Right: with $3.4$ wavelengths the wave arrives back out of step with itself (red mismatch) and cannot form a standing wave. > [!remark] Remark: What the standing-wave picture explains, and what it does not
> - It turns Bohr's ad hoc rule $L = n\hbar$ into a consequence of the wave nature of the electron; in the ground state the electron's wavelength is $2\pi a_0 = 0.332\ \mathrm{nm}$, the circumference of the first Bohr orbit. Discrete energies of a bound particle become a standing-wave effect, as the discrete frequencies of a string are.
> - The same idea gives the energies of a particle confined between two rigid walls a distance $L$ apart: $L = n\lambda/2$ and $E = p^2/2m$ give $E_n = n^2h^2/(8mL^2)$, which is $37.6\,n^2\ \mathrm{eV}$ for an electron in a box of atomic size, $L = 0.1\ \mathrm{nm}$ (PHY 284, HW 7). The Schrödinger equation reproduces this result in [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]].
> - The picture of a wave running along a classical orbit is not the final theory: the Schrödinger equation for hydrogen ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom|§A5.1]]) has no orbits, and its ground state has $L = 0$, not $\hbar$.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 6.12 · PHY 284, HW 7 and solutions*

## Electron diffraction

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.1.4: Diffraction by a Crystal Surface
> A wave of wavelength $\lambda$ falls perpendicularly on a crystal surface whose atoms form rows of spacing $D$. The waves scattered back by the surface atoms reinforce each other at the scattering angles $\varphi$, measured from the incident direction reversed (the surface normal), for which
>
> $$
> n\lambda = D\sin\varphi, \qquad n = 1, 2, \dots
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.5 · Tipler §5-2, eq. (5-5)*

^thm-a3-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At normal incidence the incoming wave reaches all surface atoms in phase. Consider two neighbouring atoms and the waves they scatter toward a distant detector at angle $\varphi$ from the normal; the two rays are parallel. Drop a perpendicular from the second atom onto the first ray (figure): from this wavefront on, both rays travel equal distances, and before it the first ray has an extra length equal to the projection of the spacing $D$ onto the ray direction, $D\sin\varphi$. The waves from all atoms of the row arrive in phase, and add to a strong beam, when this path difference is a whole number of wavelengths ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|WO Theorem §A6.1.1]]). The same condition follows from Bragg's law ([[§A7.5 X-Ray Diffraction and Holography#^thm-a7-5-1|WO Theorem §A7.5.1]]) for the atomic planes inside the crystal that make an angle $\alpha = \varphi/2$ with the surface: their spacing is $d = D\sin\alpha$ and the glancing angle is $\theta = 90^\circ - \alpha$, so $2d\sin\theta = 2D\sin\alpha\cos\alpha = D\sin 2\alpha = D\sin\varphi$.

^der-a3-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|WO Theorem §A6.1.1]], [[§A7.5 X-Ray Diffraction and Holography#^thm-a7-5-1|WO Theorem §A7.5.1]]

> [!example] Example §A3.1.2: The Davisson–Germer Experiment
> In 1927 Davisson and Germer scattered a beam of slow electrons, accelerated through $V_0 = 54\ \mathrm V$, off a single crystal of nickel at normal incidence. The scattered intensity, recorded against the detector angle, showed a strong peak at $\varphi = 50^\circ$, where a featureless distribution would be expected for particles. The surface spacing of nickel, known from X-ray diffraction, is $D = 0.215\ \mathrm{nm}$. Read as the first-order peak of [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^thm-a3-1-4|Theorem §A3.1.4]]:
>
> $$
> \lambda = D\sin\varphi = (0.215\ \mathrm{nm})\sin 50^\circ = 0.165\ \mathrm{nm} .
> $$
>
> De Broglie's prediction for $54\ \mathrm{eV}$ electrons ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]]):
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{1.226\ \mathrm{nm}}{\sqrt{54}} = 0.167\ \mathrm{nm} .
> $$
>
> The two agree to about $1\%$. A second-order peak cannot occur at this energy, since it would need $\sin\varphi = 2\lambda/D = 1.55 > 1$. The electrons are diffracted exactly like X-rays of the same wavelength.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-2 · UP3 §6.5, eq. (6.62)*

^ex-a3-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: More evidence, and other particles
> - Davisson and Germer did not set out to test de Broglie: they found the peaks by accident, after heating their nickel target had recrystallized it into large single crystals. They then measured $\lambda$ for electrons up to about $400\ \mathrm{eV}$ and found it proportional to $V_0^{-1/2}$, as [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]] predicts. The measured values are slightly smaller than predicted because the electrons speed up as they enter the metal and their waves are refracted at the surface.
> - Equation $n\lambda = D\sin\varphi$ alone does not guarantee a peak: a strong beam also needs atomic planes inside the crystal at the matching Bragg angle. At $40\ \mathrm V$, for instance, it would allow a peak at $64^\circ$ that is not observed. Varying the voltage at fixed angle showed the series of orders produced by the inner planes.
> - In the same year G. P. Thomson sent $10$–$40\ \mathrm{keV}$ electrons through thin polycrystalline foils and obtained rings like X-ray powder patterns. Since then helium atoms and hydrogen molecules (Stern and Estermann, 1930), neutrons, protons and molecules as large as $\mathrm C_{60}$ have been diffracted, always with $\lambda = h/p$. Being a matter wave is not tied to charge: it is a property of all particles.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-2 · UP3 §6.5*

^rem-a3-1-5

> [!example] Example §A3.1.3: Thermal Neutrons
> Neutron diffraction from a crystal needs a wavelength about equal to the lattice spacing, say $\lambda = 0.10\ \mathrm{nm}$. Then $pc = hc/\lambda = (1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm})/(0.10\ \mathrm{nm}) = 12.4\ \mathrm{keV}$, far below the neutron's rest energy $939.6\ \mathrm{MeV}$, so the neutron is non-relativistic and
>
> $$
> K = \frac{p^2}{2m_n} = \frac{(pc)^2}{2m_nc^2} = \frac{(12.4\times10^3\ \mathrm{eV})^2}{2(939.6\times10^6\ \mathrm{eV})} = 0.082\ \mathrm{eV} .
> $$
>
> The mean kinetic energy of a gas molecule at room temperature is $\tfrac32 k_BT = \tfrac32(8.62\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/K})(300\ \mathrm K) = 0.039\ \mathrm{eV}$ ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|TH Theorem §A2.2.2]]), of the same order. Neutrons slowed down to thermal equilibrium with matter at room temperature, called *thermal neutrons*, therefore have just the right wavelength for crystallography; being neutral, they probe the nuclei rather than the electrons of the crystal.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 6.13*
