What is waving in a matter wave? The double slit ([[§A3.2 Wave–Particle Duality|§A3.2]]) suggests the answer: the wave decides the probability of finding the particle. This section makes it precise. The state of a particle is a complex wave function $\Psi(x, t)$; Born's rule turns it into probabilities; normalization says the particle is somewhere; and expectation values, computed with operators for position, momentum and energy, give the averages of measurements. How $\Psi$ changes in time is the Schrödinger equation ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]]).

## The wave function and the Born rule

> [!definition] Definition §A3.4.1: Wave Function; Probability Density
> The state of a particle moving along the $x$-axis is described at each time $t$ by a complex-valued **wave function** $\Psi(x, t)$, also called the **probability amplitude**. Its **probability density** is
>
> $$
> |\Psi(x, t)|^2 = \Psi^*(x, t)\,\Psi(x, t),
> $$
>
> where $\Psi^*$, the complex conjugate, is obtained by replacing $i$ with $-i$ ([[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-2|LADR Def. 4.2]]). The density is real and non-negative.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.1, eq. (7.5) · Tipler §5-4, §6-1*

^def-a3-4-1

> [!principle] Principle §A3.4.1: Born Rule
> If the position of a particle with wave function $\Psi(x, t)$ is measured at time $t$, the probability of finding it between $x$ and $x + dx$ is
>
> $$
> P(x, x + dx) = |\Psi(x, t)|^2\,dx ,
> $$
>
> and the probability of finding it between $a$ and $b$ is
>
> $$
> P(a \le x \le b) = \int_a^b |\Psi(x, t)|^2\,dx ,
> $$
>
> the area under the probability density over the interval. A probability is the fraction of outcomes in a large number of measurements on identically prepared particles.
>
> *Domain: non-relativistic quantum mechanics of one particle in one dimension; in three dimensions, $|\Psi|^2\,dV$ is the probability of finding the particle in the volume element $dV$.*
> *Source: UP3 §7.1, eqs. (7.1), (7.3), (7.5) · Tipler §5-4, eq. (5-23); §6-1, eq. (6-8)*

> [!remark] Remark: Why the square of the wave
> For light the intensity is proportional to $|\vec E|^2$, and since the energy comes in photons of $hf$ each, $|\vec E|^2$ measures the number of photons per unit volume. When the light is so weak that photons arrive one at a time, the interference pattern still builds up, dot by dot, with the density of dots following $|\vec E|^2$ ([[§A3.2 Wave–Particle Duality#^law-a3-2-1|Law §A3.2.1]]). So for a single photon $|\vec E|^2$ is proportional to the probability of detecting it. Born (1926) proposed the same reading for the matter wave $\Psi$, against the early objections of Schrödinger and Einstein; it has been the accepted link between the theory and measurements ever since (Nobel Prize 1954).
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.1 · Tipler §5-4, §6-1*

^rem-a3-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: What is observable and what is not
> - Write $\Psi = |\Psi|e^{i\phi}$. The modulus $|\Psi|$ gives the probability; the phase $\phi$ is not measurable, and multiplying $\Psi$ by a constant phase factor $e^{i\alpha}$ changes no prediction. *Differences* of phase are observable, through interference ([[§A3.2 Wave–Particle Duality#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]]).
> - A free particle of definite momentum has the wave function $\Psi = Ae^{i(kx - \omega t)} = A[\cos(kx - \omega t) + i\sin(kx - \omega t)]$, with $p = \hbar k$ and $E = \hbar\omega$. Its probability density $|A|^2$ is the same everywhere and at all times: the particle is equally likely to be anywhere, and the de Broglie wavelength, carried by the phase, does not show in $|\Psi|^2$ (figure). This settles the puzzle of [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^rem-a3-1-2|Remark: What is waving?]]: the wave function is a computational device, and only $|\Psi|^2$ and phase differences have a direct physical meaning.
> - In this vault a free plane wave moving toward $+x$ is written $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$, as in University Physics and Tipler; the PHY 284 slides sometimes write the phase as $\omega t - kx$. That is the complex conjugate, which moves in the same direction but would give momentum $-\hbar k$ under the operator $\hat p$ below; the operators of this section go with $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.1 · Tipler §6-1 · PHY 284, class 21–22 slides*

^rem-a3-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Measurement and the Copenhagen interpretation
> Before a position measurement the particle has, in general, no definite position: $\Psi$ lists the possibilities and their amplitudes. The measurement gives one definite value, at random with the probabilities of the Born rule, and afterwards the wave function is concentrated around the value found. This sudden change is called **state reduction** or **collapse of the wave function**. The randomness is not ignorance of a position the particle secretly had, as with a rolled die; that is the **Copenhagen interpretation**, the one usually taught. How measurement works remains debated: Schrödinger's cat, both alive and dead until the box is opened, was his argument against it. Bohr's **correspondence principle** requires that quantum predictions agree with classical mechanics for macroscopic systems.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.1 · PHY 284, class 21 slides*

^rem-a3-4-3

## Normalization

> [!definition] Definition §A3.4.2: Normalization Condition
> A wave function is **normalized** if the probability of finding the particle somewhere is $1$:
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\Psi(x, t)|^2\,dx = 1 .
> $$
>
> If $\int|\Psi|^2\,dx$ is finite and nonzero, multiplying $\Psi$ by a suitable constant, the **normalization constant**, makes it normalized; this is **normalizing** the wave function.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.1, eq. (7.4) · Tipler §6-1, eq. (6-9)*

^def-a3-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Acceptable wave functions
> - For the normalization integral, an improper integral over the whole line, to be finite, $\Psi$ must go to zero fast enough as $x \to \pm\infty$. It must also be finite everywhere and single-valued, so that every probability is defined. The Schrödinger equation adds that $\Psi$ and, where the potential energy is finite, $\partial\Psi/\partial x$ are continuous ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]]). These conditions, together with the boundary conditions of a problem, are what quantize the energies of bound particles.
> - The plane wave $Ae^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ cannot be normalized: $\int|A|^2\,dx$ diverges. It is an idealization of a very long wave packet, such as a uniform beam, and is used for relative probabilities or fluxes only.
> - $\Psi$ has units: $|\Psi|^2\,dx$ is a pure number, so in one dimension $\Psi$ is measured in $\mathrm m^{-1/2}$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Handout "Expectation values and operators" · Tipler §6-1*

^rem-a3-4-4

> [!caution] Caution: Square first, then integrate
> A probability is an integral of $|\Psi|^2$, never of $\Psi$. Integrating $\Psi$ itself gives a complex number with no meaning, and parts where $\Psi$ is negative or imaginary would wrongly cancel. Likewise $|\Psi_1 + \Psi_2|^2$ is not $|\Psi_1|^2 + |\Psi_2|^2$ ([[§A3.2 Wave–Particle Duality#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 7.1*

^cau-a3-4-1

> [!example] Example §A3.4.1: Normalizing a Wave Function
> A particle has $\psi(x) = A(e^{ix} + e^{-ix})$ for $-\pi/2 < x < \pi/2$ and $\psi = 0$ elsewhere ($x$ in some unit of length). Normalize it and find the probability that $0 < x < \pi/8$.
>
> Here $\psi = 2A\cos x$; take $A$ real and positive. Then $|\psi|^2 = A^2(e^{ix} + e^{-ix})(e^{-ix} + e^{ix}) = A^2(2 + e^{2ix} + e^{-2ix}) = A^2(2 + 2\cos 2x)$, and
>
> $$
> 1 = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} A^2(2 + 2\cos 2x)\,dx = A^2\bigl[2x + \sin 2x\bigr]_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} = 2\pi A^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} .
> $$
>
> By the Born rule,
>
> $$
> P\Bigl(0 < x < \frac{\pi}{8}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\bigl[2x + \sin 2x\bigr]_0^{\pi/8} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigr) = 0.238 .
> $$
>
> The interval is an eighth of the region, but it lies where $|\psi|^2 \propto \cos^2x$ is largest, so the probability is almost twice $1/8$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Midterm 2 practice (problem 4) and answers*

## Expectation values

> [!definition] Definition §A3.4.3: Expectation Value
> The **expectation value** of position for a normalized wave function $\Psi$ is
>
> $$
> \langle x\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,|\Psi(x, t)|^2\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Psi^*(x, t)\,x\,\Psi(x, t)\,dx ,
> $$
>
> the average of the results of position measurements on a large number of particles that all have the wave function $\Psi$. For a function $f(x)$, such as $x^2$ or the potential energy $U(x)$, $\langle f\rangle = \int \Psi^*\,f(x)\,\Psi\,dx$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.1, eqs. (7.6)–(7.7) · Tipler §6-4, eqs. (6-44)–(6-46)*

^def-a3-4-3

> [!remark] Remark: An average, not a prediction for one measurement
> The definition is the usual weighted average of probability and statistics. Ten measurements giving $1$ once, $2$ five times, $3$ three times and $4$ once average to $1(0.1) + 2(0.5) + 3(0.3) + 4(0.1) = 2.4$; for a continuous variable the sum becomes an integral weighted by $|\Psi|^2$. The expectation value need not be a possible result: for a density symmetric about a point where it vanishes, $\langle x\rangle$ is that point, though no particle is ever found there. Symmetry often gives it at once: if $|\Psi|^2$ is an even function of $x$, then $x|\Psi|^2$ is odd, its integral over the whole line vanishes, and $\langle x\rangle = 0$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Handout "Expectation values and operators" and class 23 slides · Tipler §6-4 · UP3 §7.1*

^rem-a3-4-5

> [!definition] Definition §A3.4.4: Standard Deviation; Uncertainty
> The **standard deviation** of position,
>
> $$
> \sigma_x = \sqrt{\bigl\langle (x - \langle x\rangle)^2 \bigr\rangle},
> $$
>
> measures the spread of the results of position measurements about their mean; it is the **uncertainty** $\Delta x$ of position in the state $\Psi$. The standard deviation $\sigma_p$ of momentum is defined in the same way with $\langle p\rangle$ and $\langle p^2\rangle$ of [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]].
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Handout "Expectation values and operators" · Tipler §5-5*

^def-a3-4-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.4.2: Variance from Expectation Values
>
> $$
> \sigma_x^2 = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Handout "Expectation values and operators"*

^thm-a3-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Expand the square and use that the integral is linear, that $\langle x\rangle$ is a constant, and that $\int|\Psi|^2\,dx = 1$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-2|Def. §A3.4.2]]):
>
> $$
> \sigma_x^2 = \int (x^2 - 2\langle x\rangle x + \langle x\rangle^2)\,|\Psi|^2\,dx = \langle x^2\rangle - 2\langle x\rangle\langle x\rangle + \langle x\rangle^2 = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2 .
> $$

^der-a3-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-2|Def. §A3.4.2]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-3|Def. §A3.4.3]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-4|Def. §A3.4.4]]

## Operators for position, momentum and energy

> [!definition] Definition §A3.4.5: Position, Momentum and Energy Operators
> An **operator** turns a function into another function. The operators of position, momentum ($x$-component) and energy act on $\Psi(x, t)$ as
>
> $$
> \hat x\,\Psi = x\,\Psi, \qquad \hat p\,\Psi = -i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}, \qquad \hat E\,\Psi = i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> A power of an operator means applying it repeatedly: $\hat p^2\Psi = \hat p(\hat p\Psi) = -\hbar^2\,\partial^2\Psi/\partial x^2$. The kinetic-energy operator is $\hat K = \hat p^2/2m = -(\hbar^2/2m)\,\partial^2/\partial x^2$, and the **Hamiltonian** (total-energy) operator is $\hat H = \hat p^2/2m + U(x)$, with the potential energy acting by multiplication.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.1, eqs. (7.9)–(7.11) · Tipler §6-4, eqs. (6-48)–(6-51), Table 6-1 · PHY 284, Handout "Expectation values and operators" and class 23 slides*

^def-a3-4-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.4.3: Plane Waves Have Sharp Momentum and Energy
> For the plane wave $\Psi = Ae^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ of a free particle,
>
> $$
> \hat p\,\Psi = \hbar k\,\Psi = p\,\Psi, \qquad \hat E\,\Psi = \hbar\omega\,\Psi = E\,\Psi :
> $$
>
> the operators return the same function multiplied by the momentum and the energy that de Broglie's relations assign to it.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Handout "Expectation values and operators" and class 23 slides*

^thm-a3-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate: $\partial\Psi/\partial x = ik\,\Psi$ and $\partial\Psi/\partial t = -i\omega\,\Psi$. Hence $\hat p\Psi = -i\hbar(ik)\Psi = \hbar k\,\Psi$ and $\hat E\Psi = i\hbar(-i\omega)\Psi = \hbar\omega\,\Psi$, which are $p\Psi$ and $E\Psi$ by [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]].

^der-a3-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-5|Def. §A3.4.5]], [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]]

> [!principle] Principle §A3.4.4: Expectation Values of Momentum and Energy
> For a normalized wave function $\Psi$, the averages of many measurements of momentum, of its square and of energy are
>
> $$
> \langle p\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Psi^*\Bigl(-i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\Bigr)dx, \qquad \langle p^2\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Psi^*\Bigl(-\hbar^2\,\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2}\Bigr)dx, \qquad \langle E\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Psi^*\Bigl(i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}\Bigr)dx ,
> $$
>
> and $\langle K\rangle = \langle p^2\rangle/2m$. In general, the expectation value of a quantity $Q$ with operator $\hat Q$ is $\langle Q\rangle = \int \Psi^*\,\hat Q\,\Psi\,dx$: the operator acts on $\Psi$, the result is multiplied by $\Psi^*$, and the product is integrated.
>
> *Domain: non-relativistic quantum mechanics of one particle in one dimension (the $y$- and $z$-components of momentum have $-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial y$, $-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial z$).*
> *Source: UP3 §7.1, eqs. (7.9)–(7.11) · Tipler §6-4, eq. (6-48) · PHY 284, Handout "Expectation values and operators"*

^pr-a3-4-4

> [!remark] Remark: Why this rule, and why its layer
> At this level the rule is assumed (UP: "derived in a more advanced course"; the PHY 284 handout calls it an axiom). It is not arbitrary. On a plane wave $\hat p$ simply returns the momentum $\hbar k$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-3|Theorem §A3.4.3]]). A wave packet is a sum of plane waves $\Psi = \Psi_1 + \Psi_2 + \cdots$ with momenta $p_1, p_2, \dots$; then $\hat p\Psi = p_1\Psi_1 + p_2\Psi_2 + \cdots$, and the sandwich integral comes out as the weighted average $p_1P_1 + p_2P_2 + \cdots$ of the momenta in the packet, each weighted by its probability. Making this exact needs the Fourier transform $\phi(p)$ of $\Psi$, the wave function in "momentum language", with $|\phi(p)|^2$ the momentum density: Chapter B1 (planned), where the rule becomes a theorem. One cannot instead use $\int p(x)|\Psi|^2\,dx$, since there is no function $p(x)$: position and momentum do not both have definite values ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]). The order in the sandwich matters for $\hat p$, which differentiates: it must act on $\Psi$, not on $\Psi^*$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.1 · Tipler §6-4 · PHY 284, Handout "Expectation values and operators" and class 24 slides*

^rem-a3-4-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.4.5: A Real Wave Function Has Zero Mean Momentum
> If $\Psi(x, t) = \psi(x)\,e^{-i\omega t}$ with $\psi$ real and normalized (and $\psi \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$, or $\psi = 0$ at the walls of a box), then $\langle p\rangle = 0$.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 7.4 · Tipler Example 6-5*

^thm-a3-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The time factors cancel: $\Psi^*\,\partial\Psi/\partial x = e^{i\omega t}\psi\,\psi'\,e^{-i\omega t} = \psi\psi'$. By [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]] and the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]),
>
> $$
> \langle p\rangle = -i\hbar\int \psi\,\psi'\,dx = -i\hbar\Bigl[\tfrac12\psi^2\Bigr]_{\text{ends}} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> since $\psi$ vanishes at both ends. (A measured mean momentum must be real; here $-i\hbar$ times a real integral could only be real if the integral is zero.) A real $\psi$ is a standing wave, an equal superposition of waves running toward $+x$ and $-x$, so the particle is as likely to move left as right.

^der-a3-4-5

*Uses:* [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!example] Example §A3.4.2: Expectation Values in a Box
> A particle confined between rigid walls at $x = 0$ and $x = L$ has, in its lowest-energy state ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]), the wave function $\Psi = A\sin(\pi x/L)\,e^{-i\omega t}$ for $0 \le x \le L$ and $\Psi = 0$ outside. Normalize it and find $\langle x\rangle$, $\langle x^2\rangle$, $\langle p\rangle$, $\langle p^2\rangle$ and $\langle K\rangle$.
>
> *Normalization.* $\int_0^L A^2\sin^2(\pi x/L)\,dx = A^2L/2 = 1$, so $A = \sqrt{2/L}$.
>
> *Position.* $|\Psi|^2 = (2/L)\sin^2(\pi x/L)$ is symmetric about $x = L/2$, so $\langle x\rangle = L/2$. Integrating by parts twice,
>
> $$
> \langle x^2\rangle = \frac2L\int_0^L x^2\sin^2\frac{\pi x}{L}\,dx = L^2\Bigl(\frac13 - \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\Bigr), \qquad \sigma_x = \sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2} = L\sqrt{\frac1{12} - \frac{1}{2\pi^2}} = 0.181\,L .
> $$
>
> *Momentum.* $\psi$ is real, so $\langle p\rangle = 0$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-5|Theorem §A3.4.5]]). Applying $\hat p^2$, $-\hbar^2\,\partial^2\Psi/\partial x^2 = (\pi\hbar/L)^2\,\Psi$, so
>
> $$
> \langle p^2\rangle = \Bigl(\frac{\pi\hbar}{L}\Bigr)^2\int_0^L |\Psi|^2\,dx = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{L^2}, \qquad \sigma_p = \frac{\pi\hbar}{L}, \qquad \langle K\rangle = \frac{\langle p^2\rangle}{2m} = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2} = \frac{h^2}{8mL^2} .
> $$
>
> The particle is on average at the centre and has zero average momentum, yet it is not at rest: its kinetic energy is not zero. For an electron in a box of atomic size, $L = 0.1\ \mathrm{nm}$, $\langle K\rangle = 37.6\ \mathrm{eV}$, the standing-wave energy of [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^rem-a3-1-4|Remark: What the standing-wave picture explains]]. Since $U = 0$ inside, this is also the energy of the state; $\hat E$ gives $\langle E\rangle = \hbar\omega$, so the time factor oscillates at $\omega = \pi^2\hbar/2mL^2$.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 7.4 · Tipler Example 6-5, eq. (6-47)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $|\Psi|^2 = \Psi^*\Psi$ uses the complex conjugate and modulus ([[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-2|LADR Def. 4.2]]) and $z\bar z = |z|^2$ ([[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-4|LADR Thm. 4.4]]).
> - The normalization integral over the whole line is a doubly improper integral ([[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-2|451 Def. §36.2]]); "normalizable" means "square-integrable".
> - Square-integrable functions form the space $L^2$ with inner product $\int f\bar g\,dx$ ([[§16 Definition and Examples#^ex-16-3|556 Ex. §16.3]]), a Hilbert space ([[§17 Cauchy–Schwarz and the Induced Norm#^ex-17-1|556 Ex. §17.1]]): the state space of quantum mechanics at level B. A plane wave is not in it, just as position has no eigenvectors there ([[§24 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-24-3|556 Prop. §24.3]]).
