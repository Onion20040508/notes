A wave packet that is narrow in space must contain a wide range of wave numbers ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets|§A3.3]]). For a matter wave the wave number is the momentum divided by $\hbar$, so a particle whose position is sharply defined cannot have a sharply defined momentum, and conversely. This is Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (1927). It sets the scale of quantum effects: it explains why a confined particle can never be at rest, estimates the size and energy of the hydrogen atom, and, in its energy–time form, gives spectral lines their natural width.

## Position and momentum

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.1: Heisenberg Uncertainty Relation
> For any normalized wave function, the standard deviations of position and of the momentum along the same axis ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-4|Def. §A3.4.4]]) satisfy
>
> $$
> \Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2} .
> $$
>
> The product equals $\hbar/2$ for a Gaussian wave packet and is larger for every other shape.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.2, eq. (7.15); §6.6, eq. (6.63) · Tipler §5-5, eq. (5-26) · PHY 284, class 22 and 24 slides*

^thm-a3-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The idea, not a full proof. A normalizable wave function is a wave packet, a superposition of plane waves $e^{ikx}$ with a spread of wave numbers ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^def-a3-3-2|Def. §A3.3.2]]). Its spread in position and its spread in wave number obey the classical uncertainty relation of all waves, $\sigma_x\sigma_k \ge \tfrac12$ with equality for a Gaussian ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-5|Theorem §A3.3.5]]). Each plane-wave component $e^{ikx}$ has the definite momentum $p = \hbar k$ ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-3|Theorem §A3.4.3]]), so the distribution of momentum is the distribution of wave number rescaled by $\hbar$, and $\sigma_p = \hbar\sigma_k$. Multiplying, $\sigma_x\sigma_p = \hbar\,\sigma_x\sigma_k \ge \hbar/2$. Two steps are taken on trust here: the sharp Fourier inequality $\sigma_x\sigma_k \ge \tfrac12$ for every packet, and the fact that the momentum density is $|\phi(p)|^2$ for the Fourier transform $\phi$ of $\Psi$. Both are proved in Chapter B1 (planned), where the relation follows for any wave function from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Here it can be checked on examples: the Gaussian gives exactly $\hbar/2$ ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^ex-a3-5-1|Example §A3.5.1]]), and the lowest state in a box gives $\sigma_x\sigma_p = (0.181\,L)(\pi\hbar/L) = 0.568\,\hbar$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^ex-a3-4-2|Example §A3.4.2]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Principle or theorem?
> University Physics presents the relation as a principle, Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, and says it "can be derived in a more advanced course". In this vault it is a theorem: it follows from the de Broglie relations, the Born rule and Fourier analysis, as sketched above, and its full proof is level B (Chapter B1, planned). The name "principle" is kept because it is universal. Its precise form needs a precise meaning of $\Delta$: with standard deviations the bound is $\hbar/2$; with rougher measures of width, such as the full width of a packet, statements like $\Delta x\,\Delta p \sim \hbar$ or $\sim h$ are equally correct as orders of magnitude.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.2 · Tipler §5-5*

^rem-a3-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: What the relation says
> - **It is about the state, not the instrument.** Even with perfect apparatus, a particle with a narrow position distribution has a wide momentum distribution. The bound comes from the wave nature of matter, not from technical limitations; real measurements usually do much worse than $\hbar/2$.
> - **It is about spreads over many particles.** $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ are the standard deviations of the results of position measurements and of momentum measurements on many identically prepared particles. A single position measurement can give a sharp value, but the particle then has a wide spread of momenta. In the limits: a plane wave has $\Delta p = 0$ and $\Delta x = \infty$; a particle with $\Delta x \to 0$ has $\Delta p \to \infty$.
> - **No point in phase space.** A classical state is a point $(x, p)$; a quantum state occupies a region of the $(x, p)$ plane of area at least of order $\hbar$. The region can be squeezed in $x$ at the expense of $p$ or the other way round, but not shrunk.
> - **Small for large objects.** If the speed of an electron is known to $\Delta u = 1.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m/s}$, its position cannot be defined better than $\Delta x = \hbar/(2m\Delta u) = 5.8\ \mathrm{cm}$; for a $6.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ bowling ball with the same $\Delta u$, $\Delta x = 8.8\times10^{-33}\ \mathrm m$, unmeasurably small. Planck's constant is so small that the relation never matters for everyday bodies.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.2, Example 7.5 · Tipler §5-5 · PHY 284, class 22 slides*

^rem-a3-5-2

> [!remark] Remark: Heisenberg's microscope
> Heisenberg's own illustration shows how the act of measuring enforces the relation. To locate an electron, scatter light of wavelength $\lambda$ off it into a microscope whose lens subtends a half-angle $\theta$. Diffraction limits the resolution to $\Delta x \approx \lambda/(2\sin\theta)$, up to a factor near $1$ ($0.61\lambda/\sin\theta$ by [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-2|WO Theorem §A7.4.2]]), so a short wavelength is needed. But at least one photon, of momentum $h/\lambda$, must be scattered, and it can enter the lens anywhere within the angle $\theta$: its $x$-momentum, and by momentum conservation the electron's recoil, is uncertain by $\Delta p_x \approx (h/\lambda)\sin\theta$ (a Compton collision, [[§A1.3 The Compton Effect|§A1.3]]). Shorter wavelengths sharpen $\Delta x$ and enlarge $\Delta p_x$ in the same proportion, and $\Delta x\,\Delta p_x \approx h/2$, independent of $\lambda$ and $\theta$. Using electrons instead of photons changes nothing, since $p = h/\lambda$ for both.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-5 (Exploring: the gamma-ray microscope)*

^rem-a3-5-3

> [!example] Example §A3.5.1: The Gaussian Wave Packet
> Take $\psi(x) = C\,e^{-x^2/2a^2}$ with $a > 0$. Find $C$, $\langle x\rangle$, $\langle x^2\rangle$, $\langle p\rangle$, $\langle p^2\rangle$ and check the uncertainty relation.
>
> *Normalization.* With $u = x/a$ and $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-u^2}\,du = \sqrt\pi$,
>
> $$
> 1 = C^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/a^2}\,dx = C^2a\sqrt\pi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C = \frac{1}{\pi^{1/4}\sqrt a} .
> $$
>
> *Position.* $|\psi|^2$ is even, so $\langle x\rangle = 0$. With $\int u^2e^{-u^2}\,du = \sqrt\pi/2$ (integrate by parts),
>
> $$
> \langle x^2\rangle = C^2\int x^2e^{-x^2/a^2}\,dx = C^2a^3\,\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2} = \frac{a^2}{2} .
> $$
>
> *Momentum.* $\psi$ is real, so $\langle p\rangle = 0$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-5|Theorem §A3.4.5]]). From $\psi' = -(x/a^2)\psi$ and $\psi'' = (x^2/a^4 - 1/a^2)\psi$,
>
> $$
> \langle p^2\rangle = -\hbar^2\int\psi\,\psi''\,dx = -\hbar^2\Bigl(\frac{\langle x^2\rangle}{a^4} - \frac{1}{a^2}\Bigr) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2a^2} .
> $$
>
> *Uncertainties.* $\Delta x = \sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle} = a/\sqrt2$ and $\Delta p = \sqrt{\langle p^2\rangle} = \hbar/(a\sqrt2)$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-2|Theorem §A3.4.2]]), so
>
> $$
> \Delta x\,\Delta p = \frac{a}{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{a\sqrt2} = \frac{\hbar}{2} ,
> $$
>
> the minimum allowed by [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]], whatever the width $a$. Widening the packet (larger $a$) increases $\Delta x$ and decreases $\Delta p$ in the same proportion. The momentum distribution of a Gaussian is again a Gaussian, $|\phi(p)|^2 \propto e^{-a^2p^2/\hbar^2}$ (figure).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 24 slides*

![[ph-qm-a3-5-1.svg]]
*Two Gaussian wave functions, of width $a = 0.5\,a_0$ (blue) and $a = 2\,a_0$ (red). Left: their position densities $|\Psi(x)|^2$; right: their momentum densities $|\phi(p)|^2$. The state narrower in position (blue) is four times wider in momentum, and the other way round: in both, $\Delta x\,\Delta p = \hbar/2$.*

## Consequences

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.2: Zero-Point Energy of a Confined Particle
> A particle of mass $m$ confined to a region of length $L$ (its wave function vanishes outside an interval of length $L$) has an average kinetic energy
>
> $$
> \langle K\rangle \ge \frac{\hbar^2}{2mL^2} > 0 .
> $$
>
> A confined particle can never be at rest; its lowest possible energy, the **zero-point energy**, is not zero.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-6, eq. (5-28) · PHY 284, class 22 slides*

^thm-a3-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Spread in position.* Let $c$ be the midpoint of the interval, so $|x - c| \le L/2$ wherever $|\Psi|^2 \neq 0$, and $\langle (x - c)^2\rangle \le L^2/4$. Expanding as in [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-2|Theorem §A3.4.2]], $\langle (x - c)^2\rangle = \sigma_x^2 + (\langle x\rangle - c)^2 \ge \sigma_x^2$. Hence $\sigma_x \le L/2$.
>
> *Spread in momentum.* By [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]], $\sigma_p \ge \hbar/(2\sigma_x) \ge \hbar/L$.
>
> *Kinetic energy.* $\langle p^2\rangle = \sigma_p^2 + \langle p\rangle^2 \ge \sigma_p^2$ (the variance formula of [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-2|Theorem §A3.4.2]], for $p$), so $\langle K\rangle = \langle p^2\rangle/2m \ge \sigma_p^2/2m \ge \hbar^2/(2mL^2)$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]]).

^der-a3-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-2|Theorem §A3.4.2]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: How good is the bound?
> For an electron in a box of atomic size, $L = 0.1\ \mathrm{nm}$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\hbar^2}{2mL^2} = \frac{(\hbar c)^2}{2mc^2L^2} = \frac{(197.3\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm})^2}{2(0.511\times10^6\ \mathrm{eV})(0.1\ \mathrm{nm})^2} = 3.8\ \mathrm{eV},
> $$
>
> the right order of magnitude for the kinetic energies of electrons in atoms. The exact lowest energy in a box with rigid walls is $\pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2 = 37.6\ \mathrm{eV}$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^ex-a3-4-2|Example §A3.4.2]], [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]), ten times the bound: the uncertainty principle gives a floor and the scale, not the exact value. University Physics makes a similar estimate with $\Delta x = L$ and gets $\hbar^2/8mL^2 \approx 1\ \mathrm{eV}$; such estimates differ by factors of a few, depending on how the width is chosen. For a speck of mass $10^{-9}\ \mathrm{kg}$ in a box of $1\ \mu\mathrm m$, the bound is $5.6\times10^{-48}\ \mathrm J$, and the zero-point motion is unobservable.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-6, Examples 5-6 and 5-7 · UP3 Example 7.6*

^rem-a3-5-4

> [!example] Example §A3.5.2: Size and Energy of the Hydrogen Atom
> Why does the electron not fall into the proton? Treat an electron at a distance of order $r$ from the proton. Its position is uncertain by $\Delta x \approx r$, so its momentum by $\Delta p \approx \hbar/r$, and with $\langle p\rangle = 0$ its kinetic energy is about $(\Delta p)^2/2m = \hbar^2/2mr^2$. Adding the Coulomb potential energy $-ke^2/r$ ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-2|EM Theorem §A3.1.2]]),
>
> $$
> E(r) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2mr^2} - \frac{ke^2}{r} .
> $$
>
> Squeezing the electron closer lowers the potential energy but raises the kinetic energy faster. The minimum is where $dE/dr = -\hbar^2/mr^3 + ke^2/r^2 = 0$:
>
> $$
> r_m = \frac{\hbar^2}{mke^2} = 0.0529\ \mathrm{nm}, \qquad E_m = \frac{\hbar^2}{2mr_m^2} - \frac{ke^2}{r_m} = -\frac{ke^2}{2r_m} = -\frac{mk^2e^4}{2\hbar^2} = -13.6\ \mathrm{eV} .
> $$
>
> These are the Bohr radius and the ground-state energy of hydrogen ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^def-a2-2-1|Def. §A2.2.1]], [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]], [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom|§A5.1]]). The exact agreement is luck, owed to the choice $\Delta x = r$, $\Delta p = \hbar/r$; any other reasonable choice ($2r$, $r/2$, the factor $\tfrac12$ in $\hbar/2$) changes the numbers by small factors but keeps the order of magnitude. The point stands: the uncertainty principle makes the atom stable and fixes its size.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-6*

^ex-a3-5-2

> [!example] Example §A3.5.3: Nucleons, and No Electrons, in a Nucleus
> A nucleus is about $\Delta x = 1.0\ \mathrm{fm}$ across. A proton or neutron confined to it has $\Delta p \ge \hbar/2\Delta x$; with $\hbar c = 197.3\ \mathrm{MeV\cdot fm}$, $\Delta p\,c \ge 98.7\ \mathrm{MeV}$. For a nucleon ($mc^2 = 938\ \mathrm{MeV}$) this is small enough for Newtonian mechanics:
>
> $$
> \Delta v = \frac{\Delta p}{m} = \frac{\Delta p\,c}{mc^2}\,c = 0.105\,c = 3.2\times10^7\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad K \approx \frac{(\Delta p\,c)^2}{2mc^2} = \frac{(98.7\ \mathrm{MeV})^2}{2(938\ \mathrm{MeV})} = 5.2\ \mathrm{MeV} .
> $$
>
> Nucleons in nuclei move at about a tenth of the speed of light, with kinetic energies of a few MeV, the energy scale of nuclear physics ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy|§A7.1]]). An electron confined to the same region would have $pc \ge 98.7\ \mathrm{MeV} \gg mc^2 = 0.511\ \mathrm{MeV}$, so it is ultrarelativistic: $E = \sqrt{(pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2} \approx 99\ \mathrm{MeV}$ and $K \approx 98\ \mathrm{MeV}$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|REL Theorem §A3.3.1]]). That is far more than the MeV energies typical of nuclei: an electron cannot be held inside a nucleus.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 7 and solutions*

## Energy and time

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.3: Energy–Time Relation
> A state that exists only for a time $\Delta t$, or an energy measurement that lasts a time $\Delta t$, has an energy defined only to within $\Delta E$, where
>
> $$
> \Delta E\,\Delta t \gtrsim \frac{\hbar}{2} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.2, eq. (7.16) · Tipler §5-5, eqs. (5-25), (5-27)*

^thm-a3-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The idea. A wave that lasts only a time $\Delta t$ contains angular frequencies spread over $\Delta\omega$, with $\Delta\omega\,\Delta t \gtrsim 1$, and $\sigma_\omega\sigma_t \ge \tfrac12$ for standard deviations ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-5|Theorem §A3.3.5]]). To fix a frequency precisely, the wave must be watched for many cycles. With $E = \hbar\omega$ ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]]), $\Delta E = \hbar\,\Delta\omega$, and multiplying by $\hbar$ gives the relation. It is not of the same kind as [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]: time is the parameter on which $\Psi$ depends, not a quantity measured on the particle with an operator and a standard deviation, so $\Delta t$ means a characteristic time, such as the lifetime of a state, and the relation is used as an estimate. Its precise forms are level B (Chapter B1, planned).

^der-a3-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-5|Theorem §A3.3.5]], [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A3.5.1: Lifetime; Natural Line Width
> An excited state of an atom decays at a random time; its **lifetime** $\tau$ is the mean time before decay. Because the state exists only for about $\tau$, its energy is uncertain by about $\Gamma = \hbar/\tau$, the **natural line width**; the photons emitted in its decay to the ground state, whose energy is sharp, have a spread of energies of about $\Gamma$, and the spectral line has this width.
>
> *Source: Tipler §5-6 · UP3 §7.2*

^def-a3-5-1

> [!example] Example §A3.5.4: Width of a Spectral Line
> An atom stays in an excited state for about $\Delta t = 1.0\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm s$ and then emits a photon of average frequency $f = 7.1\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$ (violet light, $\lambda = c/f = 420\ \mathrm{nm}$). By [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]] with $\Delta E = h\,\Delta f$,
>
> $$
> \Delta E \approx \frac{\hbar}{2\Delta t} = \frac{6.58\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{eV\cdot s}}{2(1.0\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm s)} = 3.3\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{eV}, \qquad \Delta f = \frac{\Delta E}{h} = \frac{1}{4\pi\Delta t} = 8.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{Hz},
> $$
>
> so $\Delta f/f = 1.1\times10^{-8}$: the emitted light is monochromatic to about one part in $10^8$. With Tipler's $\Gamma = \hbar/\tau$ ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^def-a3-5-1|Def. §A3.5.1]]) the estimate is twice as large, $6.6\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{eV}$; only the order of magnitude matters. In practice, at room temperature, lines are about ten times broader still, from the Doppler effect of the moving atoms.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 7.7 · Tipler §5-6, Example 5-8*

^ex-a3-5-4

> [!remark] Remark: Borrowing energy for a short time
> The energy–time relation also limits processes that would violate energy conservation by $\Delta E$: they can last only about $\Delta t \approx \hbar/\Delta E$. A proton can emit a pion, $p \to n + \pi^+$, although the pion's rest energy of $140\ \mathrm{MeV}$ is not available, if it reabsorbs it within $\Delta t \approx \hbar/\Delta E = (6.58\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{MeV\cdot s})/(140\ \mathrm{MeV}) = 4.7\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm s$. Even at nearly the speed of light the pion gets no farther than $c\,\Delta t \approx 1.4\ \mathrm{fm}$, which is the range of the nuclear force: such short-lived *virtual* particles carry the forces between particles ([[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws|§A8.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 7 and solutions · Tipler §5-5*
