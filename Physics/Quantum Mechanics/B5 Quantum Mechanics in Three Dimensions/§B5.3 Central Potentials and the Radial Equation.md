Level A separated the Schrödinger equation of hydrogen in spherical coordinates as an idea ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^der-a5-1-3|Derivation §A5.1.3]]) and remarked that the angular part is the same for every spherically symmetric potential ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^rem-a5-1-2|Remark: What the separation shows]]). With the angular momentum operators of [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics]] this becomes exact: for any central potential the stationary states are $R(r)\,Y^m_\ell(\theta, \phi)$, and $u = rR$ obeys a one-dimensional Schrödinger equation on the half-line $r > 0$ with an effective potential, the quantum form of the classical radial problem ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|CM Theorem §B2.1.4]]). This section derives the radial equation, its behaviour at $r = 0$ and $r = \infty$, the general structure of the levels, and solves the linear potential $V = Ar$; hydrogen follows in [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom]].

*Notation.* In the radial equation $m$ is the mass of the particle; the magnetic quantum number does not appear there (Griffiths, footnote 9).

## Separation in spherical coordinates

> [!definition] Definition §B5.3.1: Central Potential; Spherical Coordinates
> A potential is **central** (spherically symmetric) if it depends only on the distance from the origin, $V(\mathbf r) = V(r)$. Spherical coordinates are $x = r\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y = r\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z = r\cos\theta$, with $r \ge 0$, the polar angle $0 \le \theta \le \pi$ and the azimuth $0 \le \phi < 2\pi$; the volume element is
>
> $$
> d^3\mathbf r = r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi = r^2\,dr\,d\Omega, \qquad d\Omega = \sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi . \qquad (4.30)
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.1.1, §4.1.2, eq. (4.30), Figure 4.1 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lectures 20–21, §1, eq. (1.7)*

^def-b5-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.1: Separation of the Schrödinger Equation for a Central Potential
> For a central potential the time-independent Schrödinger equation can be written
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{2mr^2}\Bigl[-\hbar^2\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Bigl(r^2\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Bigr) + L^2\Bigr]\psi + V(r)\,\psi = E\psi , \qquad (4.133)
> $$
>
> and it has the solutions $\psi(r, \theta, \phi) = R(r)\,Y^m_\ell(\theta, \phi)$, which are simultaneous eigenfunctions of $\hat H$, $L^2$ and $L_z$ with eigenvalues $E$, $\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)$, $\hbar m$, provided that $u(r) = rR(r)$ satisfies the **radial equation**
>
> $$
> -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} + \Bigl[V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}\Bigr]u = Eu . \qquad (4.37)
> $$
>
> The normalization splits as $\int_0^\infty\lvert u\rvert^2\,dr = 1$ (4.39) and $\int\lvert Y^m_\ell\rvert^2\,d\Omega = 1$. The radial equation depends on $\ell$ but not on $m$, so every level of a central potential is at least $(2\ell + 1)$-fold degenerate. Every stationary state is a linear combination of such products.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.1.1, §4.1.3, §4.3.2, eqs. (4.13)–(4.17), (4.35)–(4.39), (4.133) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lectures 20–21, §4, eqs. (4.1)–(4.10) · Greensite ch. 11, §11.5*

^thm-b5-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The Hamiltonian.* In spherical coordinates the Laplacian is ([[§33 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-33-1|452 Thm. §33.1]])
>
> $$
> \nabla^2 = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Bigl(r^2\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Bigr) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\Bigl(\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\Bigr) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}, \qquad (4.13)
> $$
>
> and the angular part is $-L^2/\hbar^2r^2$ by [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-6|Theorem §B5.2.6]], eq. (4.132). Inserting into $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V\psi = E\psi$ gives (4.133).
>
> *Separation.* Try $\psi = R(r)\,Y(\theta, \phi)$ (4.15). Since $L^2$ acts only on the angles, (4.133) becomes, after multiplying by $2mr^2/(RY)$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl\{-\frac{\hbar^2}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\Bigl(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\Bigr) + 2mr^2\bigl[V(r) - E\bigr]\Bigr\} + \frac{L^2Y}{Y} = 0 .
> $$
>
> The first bracket depends only on $r$, the second term only on the angles, so both are constant. Calling the constant $\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)$, the angular equation is $L^2Y = \hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y$, solved (with $L_zY = \hbar mY$) exactly by $Y^m_\ell$ with integer $0 \le \lvert m\rvert \le \ell$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-7|Theorem §B5.2.7]]). The radial equation is (4.35), $\frac{d}{dr}\bigl(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\bigr) - \frac{2mr^2}{\hbar^2}[V - E]R = \ell(\ell+1)R$. With $R = u/r$: $\frac{dR}{dr} = \frac{ru' - u}{r^2}$, so $r^2\frac{dR}{dr} = ru' - u$ and $\frac{d}{dr}\bigl(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\bigr) = ru''$. Dividing (4.35) by $r$ and multiplying by $-\hbar^2/2m$ gives (4.37).
>
> *Normalization.* With (4.30), $\int\lvert\psi\rvert^2\,d^3\mathbf r = \int_0^\infty\lvert R\rvert^2r^2\,dr\cdot\int\lvert Y\rvert^2\,d\Omega$ (4.31), and $\lvert R\rvert^2r^2 = \lvert u\rvert^2$.
>
> *Completeness.* $\hat H$ commutes with $L^2$ and $L_z$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]]), so each eigenspace of $\hat H$ is mapped into itself by $L^2$ and $L_z$ and can be spanned by common eigenfunctions ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^der-b5-1-3|Derivation §B5.1.3]]); expanding the angular dependence of any eigenfunction in the complete set $Y^m_\ell$ (Mathematical Methods, planned) shows that these are the products found above.

> [!definition] Definition §B5.3.2: Radial Function; Effective Potential; Centrifugal Term
> In $\psi = R(r)\,Y^m_\ell$, $R$ is the **radial wave function** and $u = rR$ the **reduced radial function**. The **effective potential** for angular momentum quantum number $\ell$ is
>
> $$
> V_{\text{eff}}(r) = V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}, \qquad (4.38)
> $$
>
> and the added piece is the **centrifugal term** (centrifugal barrier). The radial equation (4.37) is the one-dimensional Schrödinger equation for $u$ in $V_{\text{eff}}$ on $0 < r < \infty$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.1.3, eqs. (4.36)–(4.38) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lectures 20–21, §4, eqs. (4.5)–(4.7)*

^def-b5-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: The classical effective potential, quantized
> Classically, a particle with angular momentum $L$ in a central field moves radially as a one-dimensional particle in $U + L^2/2mr^2$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-3|CM Def. §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|CM Theorem §B2.1.4]]). The quantum centrifugal term is the same with $L^2 \to \hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)$, the eigenvalue of $L^2$; the radial kinetic term $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}u''$ plays the part of $p_r^2/2m$, which level A used as an idea ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^der-a5-1-4|Derivation §A5.1.4]]). The barrier pushes states of larger $\ell$ away from the origin.

## Behaviour at the origin and at infinity

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.2: Boundary Behaviour of the Radial Function
> Let $r^2V(r) \to 0$ as $r \to 0$ (the potential is less singular than the centrifugal term, as for the Coulomb potential). Then every acceptable solution of the radial equation behaves near the origin as
>
> $$
> u(r) \approx C\,r^{\ell+1}, \qquad R(r) \approx C\,r^\ell \qquad (r \to 0),
> $$
>
> so $u(0) = 0$ for every $\ell$, including $\ell = 0$: the radial problem is a one-dimensional problem on $r > 0$ with an infinite wall at $r = 0$. Only $\ell = 0$ states have $\psi(0) \ne 0$. If moreover $V$ falls off at least as fast as $1/r$ as $r \to \infty$, a bound state ($E < 0$) decays as $u \sim e^{-\kappa r}$ (up to slower, power-law factors), $\kappa = \sqrt{-2mE}/\hbar$.
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lectures 20–21, §4, eqs. (4.11)–(4.16) · Griffiths §4.1.3, §4.2.1, eqs. (4.57)–(4.59), footnote 10*

^thm-b5-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Near $r = 0$.* There the centrifugal term dominates both $V$ (by assumption) and $E$, and the most singular terms of (4.37) must balance:
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2u}{dr^2} \approx \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}\,u .
> $$
>
> Powers $u = r^s$ solve this when $s(s - 1) = \ell(\ell+1)$, i.e. $s = \ell + 1$ or $s = -\ell$. So $u \approx Cr^{\ell+1} + Dr^{-\ell}$.
> - For $\ell \ge 1$, $\int_0\lvert u\rvert^2\,dr$ with $u \sim r^{-\ell}$ diverges at $0$, so $D = 0$.
> - For $\ell = 0$ the second solution tends to a constant, $R \sim D/r$, which is square integrable with the weight $r^2$. It is excluded because $\psi \propto 1/r$ does not solve the Schrödinger equation at the origin: $\nabla^2(1/r) = 0$ for $r > 0$ ([[§33 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^rem-33-2|452 Remark: Verification: Radial Functions]]), but as a distribution $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\delta^3(\mathbf r)$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|EM Theorem §B1.2.2]]), the potential of a point charge, and no such delta function is present in $V$ (Zwiebach; Griffiths, footnote 10, cites a general argument for $u(0) = 0$).
>
> Hence $u \approx Cr^{\ell+1}$, $u(0) = 0$, and $R = u/r \approx Cr^\ell$ vanishes at the origin unless $\ell = 0$.
>
> *Near $r = \infty$.* There $V$ and the centrifugal term vanish, and (4.37) becomes $u'' = \kappa^2u$ with $\kappa^2 = -2mE/\hbar^2 > 0$, whose solutions are $Ae^{-\kappa r} + Be^{\kappa r}$ (4.57); normalizability forces $B = 0$. (For the Coulomb potential the neglected $1/r$ term changes this only by a power of $r$; [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom]].)

*Uses:* [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-1|Theorem §B5.3.1]], [[§33 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^rem-33-2|452 Remark: Verification: Radial Functions]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-1|451 Def. §36.1]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|EM Theorem §B1.2.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.3: Structure of the Spectrum of a Central Potential
> For a central potential satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem §B5.3.2, and each $\ell = 0, 1, 2, \dots$:
> 1. the bound levels of the radial equation are nondegenerate; they can be labelled $E_{N\ell}$, $N = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ in increasing order, and the $N$th has $N - 1$ nodes in $0 < r < \infty$;
> 2. the level $E_{N\ell}$ of the three-dimensional problem contains the $2\ell + 1$ states $R_{N\ell}(r)Y^m_\ell$, $m = -\ell, \dots, \ell$; it is more degenerate only if levels with different $\ell$ happen to coincide (accidental degeneracy).
>
> The **principal quantum number** $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ simply numbers the distinct levels in order of energy; how $n$ is related to $N$ and $\ell$ depends on the potential.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.1.3, Example 4.1 and footnote 12 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lectures 20–21, §4*

^thm-b5-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *(1)* Let $u_1$, $u_2$ be two bound solutions of (4.37) with the same $E$ and $\ell$. Their Wronskian $W = u_1u_2' - u_2u_1'$ has $W' = u_1u_2'' - u_2u_1'' = 0$, because both $u_i''$ equal $\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(V_{\text{eff}} - E)u_i$. So $W$ is constant, and as $r \to 0$ both $u_i \sim r^{\ell+1}$, $u_i' \sim r^\ell$ give $W \to 0$. Thus $W \equiv 0$, $(u_2/u_1)' = W/u_1^2 = 0$ where $u_1 \ne 0$, and $u_2$ is a multiple of $u_1$. This is the one-dimensional nondegeneracy theorem of [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], with the wall at $r = 0$ playing the role of a vanishing boundary condition; the node count is the node theorem, [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]].
>
> *(2)* $Y^m_\ell$ does not enter the radial equation, so each radial solution combines with all $2\ell + 1$ harmonics of that $\ell$; by Theorem §B5.3.1 there are no other states. Two different $\ell$ have different radial equations, and nothing forces their eigenvalues to coincide.

> [!remark] Remark: Essential and accidental degeneracy
> The $2\ell + 1$ of part (2) is forced by rotational symmetry: $L_x$, $L_y$, $L_z$ all commute with $\hat H$ but not with each other ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]). Level A's [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^rem-a5-1-2|Remark: What the separation shows]] observed that $E$ cannot depend on $m$ "because no direction in space is preferred"; this is the proof. The Coulomb potential (energies depend on $n = N + \ell$ only, [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom]]) and the isotropic oscillator have more; Griffiths traces the extra degeneracy to hidden symmetries in his Chapter 6 (level C: hydrogen in [[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-5|Theorem §C8.1.5]], the oscillator in [[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^rem-c8-1-7|Remark: The hidden symmetry of the isotropic oscillator]]).

^rem-b5-3-2

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The isotropic oscillator by angular momentum
> The isotropic oscillator $V = \frac12m\omega^2r^2$ is central, so its levels $E_n = (n + \frac32)\hbar\omega$ ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-7|Theorem §B5.1.7]]) can also be sorted by $\ell$. Solving the radial equation by the power-series method of [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom]] (Griffiths Problem 4.47, not worked here) gives $n = 2(N - 1) + \ell$, so the level $n$ contains $\ell = n, n - 2, \dots$, down to $1$ or $0$. The count agrees: $\sum_{\ell = n, n-2, \dots}(2\ell + 1) = \frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}$, e.g. $5 + 1 = 6$ for $n = 2$ and $7 + 3 = 10$ for $n = 3$. Levels with different $\ell$ coincide, the accidental degeneracy of [[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^rem-b5-1-5|Remark: Degeneracies compared]].

^rem-b5-3-3

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: A shallow spherical well need not bind
> Because $u(0) = 0$, the $\ell = 0$ states of a three-dimensional well $V(r)$ are the odd states of the one-dimensional well $V(\lvert x\rvert)$. A one-dimensional finite well always has an even bound state, but its first odd state exists only if the well is deep enough ([[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^thm-a4-4-4|Theorem §A4.4.4]], [[§B3.2 Square Wells Revisited#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]). So the finite spherical well $V = -V_0$ for $r \le a$ has no bound state at all unless $V_0a^2 \ge \pi^2\hbar^2/8m$ (Griffiths Problem 4.11): the condition that a quarter wavelength fit into the well.

^rem-b5-3-4

> [!example]- ★ Example §B5.3.1: The Infinite Spherical Well
> Find the stationary states of $V = 0$ for $r \le a$, $V = \infty$ for $r > a$ (4.40).
>
> Inside, with $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ (4.42), the radial equation is $u'' = [\ell(\ell+1)/r^2 - k^2]u$ (4.41), with $u(a) = 0$.
>
> *$\ell = 0$.* $u = A\sin kr + B\cos kr$, and $u(0) = 0$ (Theorem §B5.3.2) gives $B = 0$; $\sin ka = 0$ gives $k = N\pi/a$:
>
> $$
> E_{N0} = \frac{N^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}, \qquad u_{N0} = \sqrt{\frac2a}\,\sin\frac{N\pi r}{a}, \qquad N = 1, 2, \dots \qquad (4.43)\text{–}(4.44)
> $$
>
> the one-dimensional infinite well ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]), with $N - 1$ nodes.
>
> *Any $\ell$.* The regular solution is $u = Ar\,j_\ell(kr)$, $R = Aj_\ell(kr)$ (4.47), with the **spherical Bessel functions** $j_\ell(x) = (-x)^\ell\bigl(\frac1x\frac{d}{dx}\bigr)^\ell\frac{\sin x}{x}$ (4.46): $j_0 = \frac{\sin x}{x}$, $j_1 = \frac{\sin x}{x^2} - \frac{\cos x}{x}$, and $j_\ell(x) \approx \frac{2^\ell\ell!}{(2\ell+1)!}x^\ell$ for small $x$ (Table 4.4). (The spherical Neumann functions $n_\ell$, built the same way from $-\cos x/x$, behave as $x^{-\ell-1}$ and are excluded.) That $rj_\ell(kr)$ solves the $\ell$ equation follows by a ladder argument: with $A_\ell = \frac{d}{dr} - \frac{\ell+1}{r}$ and $A_\ell^\dagger = -\frac{d}{dr} - \frac{\ell+1}{r}$,
>
> $$
> A_\ell^\dagger A_\ell = -\frac{d^2}{dr^2} + \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}, \qquad A_\ell A_\ell^\dagger = -\frac{d^2}{dr^2} + \frac{(\ell+1)(\ell+2)}{r^2},
> $$
>
> so $A_\ell$ maps solutions for $\ell$ (with eigenvalue $k^2$) to solutions for $\ell + 1$; and from (4.46), $A_\ell\bigl[rj_\ell(kr)\bigr] = -k\,r\,j_{\ell+1}(kr)$, since $j_{\ell+1}(x) = -x^\ell\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(j_\ell/x^\ell\bigr)$. Starting from $\sin kr$ at $\ell = 0$ gives every $\ell$.
>
> The wall requires $j_\ell(ka) = 0$ (4.48): $ka = \beta_{N\ell}$, the $N$th zero of $j_\ell$, and $E_{N\ell} = \hbar^2\beta_{N\ell}^2/2ma^2$ (4.50), each $(2\ell+1)$-fold degenerate. Computed zeros (in units $\hbar^2/2ma^2$ for $E$):
>
> | state $(N, \ell)$ | $(1,0)$ | $(1,1)$ | $(1,2)$ | $(2,0)$ | $(1,3)$ | $(2,1)$ | $(1,4)$ | $(2,2)$ | $(1,5)$ | $(3,0)$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $\beta_{N\ell}$ | 3.142 | 4.493 | 5.763 | 6.283 | 6.988 | 7.725 | 8.183 | 9.095 | 9.356 | 9.425 |
> | $E/(\hbar^2/2ma^2)$ | 9.87 | 20.19 | 33.22 | 39.48 | 48.83 | 59.68 | 66.95 | 82.72 | 87.53 | 88.83 |
>
> In the table no two values of $\ell$ share a level: each degeneracy is exactly $2\ell + 1$, the minimum Theorem §B5.3.3 allows. For $\ell = 1$ the condition is $\tan x = x$, whose large roots approach $(N + \frac12)\pi$ (Griffiths Problem 4.10).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §4.1.3, Example 4.1, eqs. (4.40)–(4.51), Table 4.4, Problem 4.10 · Greensite ch. 11, §11.5, eqs. (11.129)–(11.137); the ladder argument and the numerical zeros are added here*

## The linear potential

> [!model] Model §B5.3.4: Linear Central Potential
> A particle of mass $m$ moves in the confining central potential
>
> $$
> V(r) = Ar, \qquad A > 0,
> $$
>
> a force of constant magnitude $A$ toward the origin. PHY 308 introduces it as a semi-quantitative model of the low-energy interaction between quarks. Its natural scales, from $\hbar$, $m$ and $A$ alone, are the length and energy
>
> $$
> r_0 = \Bigl(\frac{\hbar^2}{2mA}\Bigr)^{1/3}, \qquad \varepsilon_0 = Ar_0 = \Bigl(\frac{\hbar^2A^2}{2m}\Bigr)^{1/3} .
> $$
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 5, Problem 5 · Griffiths §9.3, eqs. (9.34)–(9.37) (the linear potential near a turning point)*

^mod-b5-3-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.5: $s$-States of the Linear Potential
> The $\ell = 0$ bound states of [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^mod-b5-3-4|Model §B5.3.4]] are
>
> $$
> u_N(r) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r_0}\,\lvert\mathrm{Ai}'(-a_N)\rvert}\,\mathrm{Ai}\Bigl(\frac{r}{r_0} - a_N\Bigr), \qquad E_N = a_N\,\varepsilon_0 = a_N\Bigl(\frac{\hbar^2A^2}{2m}\Bigr)^{1/3}, \qquad N = 1, 2, 3, \dots,
> $$
>
> where $\mathrm{Ai}$ is the Airy function and $-a_N$ are its zeros: $a_1 = 2.338$, $a_2 = 4.088$, $a_3 = 5.521$. The ground state energy is $E_1 = 2.338\,(\hbar^2A^2/2m)^{1/3}$.
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 5, Problem 5 · Griffiths §9.3, eqs. (9.34)–(9.38), Table 9.1; the zeros and $\mathrm{Ai}'(-a_N)$ computed here*

^thm-b5-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $\ell = 0$ the radial equation (4.37) is $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}u'' + Aru = Eu$, i.e. $u'' = \frac{2mA}{\hbar^2}\bigl(r - \frac EA\bigr)u$. With $z = (r - E/A)/r_0$ and $r_0^3 = \hbar^2/2mA$,
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2u}{dz^2} = r_0^2\cdot\frac{2mA}{\hbar^2}\,r_0z\,u = z\,u ,
> $$
>
> Airy's equation ([[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^def-b9-4-1|Def. §B9.4.1]]; Griffiths eq. (9.37)). Its solutions are combinations of $\mathrm{Ai}(z)$ and $\mathrm{Bi}(z)$; for large $z$, $\mathrm{Bi}(z) \sim e^{\frac23z^{3/2}}/\sqrt\pi z^{1/4}$ grows and $\mathrm{Ai}(z) \sim e^{-\frac23z^{3/2}}/2\sqrt\pi z^{1/4}$ decays (Table 9.1), so normalizability leaves $u = C\,\mathrm{Ai}(r/r_0 - E/\varepsilon_0)$, using $E/(Ar_0) = E/\varepsilon_0$. The condition $u(0) = 0$ of [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]] reads $\mathrm{Ai}(-E/\varepsilon_0) = 0$: $E/\varepsilon_0$ must be one of the $a_N$. ($\mathrm{Ai}$ has zeros only on the negative axis, where it oscillates, so all $E_N > 0$, as they must be for a potential that is positive everywhere.)
>
> *Normalization.* From Airy's equation, $\frac{d}{dz}\bigl[\mathrm{Ai}'^2 - z\,\mathrm{Ai}^2\bigr] = 2\mathrm{Ai}'\,z\mathrm{Ai} - \mathrm{Ai}^2 - 2z\,\mathrm{Ai}\,\mathrm{Ai}' = -\mathrm{Ai}^2$, so $\int_{-a_N}^\infty\mathrm{Ai}^2\,dz = \mathrm{Ai}'(-a_N)^2$ (the bracket vanishes at $\infty$ and $\mathrm{Ai}(-a_N) = 0$). With $dr = r_0\,dz$ this gives the constant in front.

> [!example] Example §B5.3.2: The Linear Potential in Momentum Space
> For $V = Ar$ and $\ell = 0$, find the ground-state energy by working in the momentum representation.
>
> *The trick.* For $\ell = 0$ the problem is the one-dimensional equation $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}u'' + Aru = Eu$ on $r > 0$ with $u(0) = 0$. Solve the same equation on the whole line $-\infty < r < \infty$ (potential $Ar$, not $A\lvert r\rvert$) and impose $u(0) = 0$ afterwards: on the whole line the potential is unbounded below as $r \to -\infty$, every $E$ is allowed, and the solutions form a continuum normalized to $\delta(E - E')$, as in the PHY 308 hint.
>
> *Momentum space.* With $\phi(p) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int u(r)\,e^{-ipr/\hbar}\,dr$ ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]], [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^thm-b2-3-6|Theorem §B2.3.6]]), the operator $\hat r$ acts as $i\hbar\,d/dp$ and $\hat p$ as multiplication by $p$. The equation becomes first order:
>
> $$
> \frac{p^2}{2m}\phi + i\hbar A\,\frac{d\phi}{dp} = E\phi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \phi_E(p) = C\exp\Bigl[\frac{i}{\hbar A}\Bigl(\frac{p^3}{6m} - Ep\Bigr)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> $\lvert\phi_E\rvert$ is constant, and $\int\phi_E^*\phi_{E'}\,dp = \lvert C\rvert^2\int e^{i(E - E')p/\hbar A}\,dp = \lvert C\rvert^2\,2\pi\hbar A\,\delta(E - E')$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|WO Theorem §B4.4.3]]), so $C = 1/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar A}$.
>
> *Back to position space.*
>
> $$
> u_E(r) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\phi_E(p)\,e^{ipr/\hbar}\,dp = \frac{2C}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_0^\infty\cos\Bigl[\frac{1}{\hbar A}\Bigl(\frac{p^3}{6m} + (Ar - E)p\Bigr)\Bigr]dp ,
> $$
>
> because the real part of the integrand is even in $p$ and the imaginary part odd. Substitute $p = (2m\hbar A)^{1/3}s$: then $p^3/6m\hbar A = s^3/3$ and $(Ar - E)p/\hbar A = sz$ with $z = (Ar - E)/\varepsilon_0 = r/r_0 - E/\varepsilon_0$. By the integral representation $\mathrm{Ai}(z) = \frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\cos\bigl(\frac{s^3}{3} + sz\bigr)ds$ (Griffiths Table 9.1),
>
> $$
> u_E(r) = \frac{2\pi C\,(2m\hbar A)^{1/3}}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\;\mathrm{Ai}\Bigl(\frac{r}{r_0} - \frac{E}{\varepsilon_0}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Quantization.* $u(0) = 0$ requires $\mathrm{Ai}(-E/\varepsilon_0) = 0$, so $E = a_N\varepsilon_0$ and the ground state is
>
> $$
> E_1 = 2.338\,\Bigl(\frac{\hbar^2A^2}{2m}\Bigr)^{1/3},
> $$
>
> in agreement with Theorem §B5.3.5. The momentum route turns a second-order equation into a first-order one because $V$ is linear in $\hat r$; the price is the detour through the whole line. The $s$-wave $u_N$ is the restriction of $u_{E_N}$ to $r > 0$, renormalized there.
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 5, Problem 5; solved here (Airy integral from Griffiths Table 9.1)*
