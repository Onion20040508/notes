*Sources: Sakurai & Napolitano, Modern Quantum Mechanics (3rd ed.), §8.2.2, §8.3.2, §8.4, §8.5, Problems 8.11, 8.15–8.17 · Likharev, Essential Graduate Physics — QM, §9.6–9.7.*

What are the stationary states of the Dirac equation, free and in a Coulomb field? Hydrogen is solved at level B and at level C in the nonrelativistic theory ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-2|Theorem §B5.4.2]], [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]]); its fine structure is first-order perturbation theory ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]]), whose terms the previous section derived from the Dirac equation ([[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-6|Theorem §C13.2.6]]), and level B quoted the exact Dirac energies without derivation ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^rem-b8-3-4|★ Remark: The exact Dirac result and the Lamb shift]]). This section derives them. First the free solutions, four spinors per momentum, labelled by the sign of the energy and the helicity. Then the central-potential problem: conservation of $\mathbf J$ and parity ([[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-4|Theorem §C13.2.4]], [[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-7|Theorem §C13.2.7]]) and the spin-angular functions of [[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-5|Theorem §C7.1.5]] reduce it to two coupled first-order radial equations, which the Coulomb potential lets one solve by a terminating series, as in the nonrelativistic case. The energies depend only on $n$ and $j$; what remains, the Lamb shift, belongs to quantum electrodynamics.

*Notation.* As in [[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^cau-c13-2-1|Caution: Dirac matrices]] and [[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^cau-c13-1-1|Caution: Units, metric and symbols in Chapter C13★]]; Gaussian units, $V(r) = -Ze^2/r$, $\alpha = e^2/\hbar c$, $a_0 = \hbar/mc\alpha$. $u^{(\pm)}(\mathbf p)$ are free spinors; $u(r)$, $v(r)$ radial functions (the radial parts themselves, not $r$ times them); $\lambda = j + \frac12$. Sakurai's exponent $\gamma$ in (8.139)–(8.144) is written $s$ here, to keep $\gamma$ for the Dirac matrices.

## Free particles

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.3.1: Free-Particle Spinors
> For a free particle of momentum $\mathbf p$, $\Psi = u\,e^{i(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x - Et)/\hbar}$ solves the Dirac equation iff $(c\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p + \beta mc^2)u = Eu$, with $E = \pm E_p$. For two-component $\chi$,
>
> $$
> u^{(+)} = \begin{pmatrix}\chi\\[2pt] \dfrac{c\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p}{E_p + mc^2}\,\chi\end{pmatrix} \quad (E = +E_p), \qquad u^{(-)} = \begin{pmatrix}-\dfrac{c\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p}{E_p + mc^2}\,\chi\\[2pt] \chi\end{pmatrix} \quad (E = -E_p),
> $$
>
> with $u^\dagger u = 2E_p/(E_p + mc^2)$ for normalized $\chi$. The helicity $\boldsymbol\Sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf p}$ commutes with $H$; choosing $\chi$ with $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf p}\,\chi = \pm\chi$ makes $u$ a helicity eigenstate. For $\mathbf p = p\hat{\mathbf z}$ these are Sakurai's
>
> $$
> u_R^{(+)} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\ 0\\ \frac{cp}{E_p + mc^2}\\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad u_L^{(+)} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\ 1\\ 0\\ \frac{-cp}{E_p + mc^2}\end{pmatrix}, \quad u_R^{(-)} = \begin{pmatrix}\frac{-cp}{E_p + mc^2}\\ 0\\ 1\\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad u_L^{(-)} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\ \frac{cp}{E_p + mc^2}\\ 0\\ 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad (8.67)
> $$
>
> $R$ ($L$) for positive (negative) helicity. At rest ($\mathbf p = 0$) the four solutions are the unit columns, upper two with $E = mc^2$, lower two with $E = -mc^2$ (8.64). For $p \ll mc$ the small components are of order $v/2c$: the upper (lower) pair dominates for positive (negative) energy, as for the Feshbach–Villars components ([[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^thm-c13-1-5|Theorem §C13.1.5]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.2.2, eqs. (8.64)–(8.67), Problem 8.11 · Likharev §9.6, eqs. (9.103)–(9.105); the general-$\mathbf p$ form derived here*

^thm-c13-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $H = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}mc^2 & c\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p\\ c\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p & -mc^2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ and $X = c\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p/(E_p + mc^2)$, $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p)^2 = p^2$ and $c^2p^2 = (E_p - mc^2)(E_p + mc^2)$: the upper row of $Hu^{(+)}$ is $mc^2\chi + c\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf pX\chi = [mc^2 + (E_p - mc^2)]\chi = E_p\chi$, and the lower row is $c\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p\,\chi(1 - \frac{mc^2}{E_p + mc^2}) = E_pX\chi$. For $u^{(-)}$ the upper row is $mc^2(-X\chi) + c\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p\,\chi = c\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p\,\chi\bigl(1 - \frac{mc^2}{E_p + mc^2}\bigr) = E_pX\chi = -E_p(-X\chi)$, and the lower row $c\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p(-X\chi) - mc^2\chi = -(E_p - mc^2 + mc^2)\chi = -E_p\chi$. Normalization: $u^\dagger u = 1 + c^2p^2/(E_p + mc^2)^2 = 2E_p/(E_p + mc^2)$. Helicity: $[\Sigma_i, \beta] = 0$ and $\sum_{ij}p_ip_j[\Sigma_i, \alpha_j] = 2i\sum\epsilon_{jik}p_ip_j\alpha_k = 0$ (symmetric times antisymmetric) ([[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-4|Theorem §C13.2.4]]), and $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf p}$ commutes with $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p$, so both blocks of $u^{(\pm)}$ have the helicity of $\chi$. For $\mathbf p = p\hat{\mathbf z}$, $\chi = (1, 0)$ or $(0, 1)$ gives (8.67).

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the four solutions
> Two solutions per sign of the energy are the two spin states; the helicity, the spin along the momentum, is the label that commutes with the free Hamiltonian, while $\Sigma_z$ along a fixed axis does not unless $\mathbf p \parallel \hat{\mathbf z}$. The negative-energy spinors are the states of the Dirac sea, and their charge-conjugates are positron states of positive energy ([[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^rem-c13-2-7|Remark: The Dirac sea, the positron, and CPT]]; Sakurai Problem 8.14). Sakurai normalizes the spinors to $2E_p/(E_p + mc^2)$ as in (8.67) and multiplies by $e^{-ip_\mu x^\mu/\hbar}$; with this normalization the density is the Lorentz factor times $\bar uu = 2mc^2/(E_p + mc^2)$ ([[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-3|Theorem §C13.2.3]]). Likharev stresses that exact eigenstates mix particle and antiparticle components, the root of effects such as the Klein paradox at a high potential step (named there, not treated).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.2.2–8.2.3, Problems 8.12, 8.14 · Likharev §9.6*

## Central potentials

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.3.2: Separation of the Dirac Equation in a Central Potential
> Let $H = c\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p + \beta mc^2 + V(r)$ and $\Psi = \binom{\psi_1}{\psi_2}$.
> 1. $H$ commutes with $\mathbf J^2$, $J_z$ and the parity $\mathscr P$ ([[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-4|Theorem §C13.2.4]], [[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-7|Theorem §C13.2.7]]), and $\beta\Psi(-\mathbf x) = \pm\Psi(\mathbf x)$ requires $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ to have opposite parities (8.111)–(8.112). With the spin-angular functions $\mathscr Y_\ell^{jm}$, $\ell = j \pm \frac12$ ([[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-5|Theorem §C7.1.5]]), the common eigenfunctions are
>
> $$
> \Psi_A = \begin{pmatrix}u_A(r)\,\mathscr Y^{jm}_{j-1/2}\\ -i\,v_A(r)\,\mathscr Y^{jm}_{j+1/2}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Psi_B = \begin{pmatrix}u_B(r)\,\mathscr Y^{jm}_{j+1/2}\\ -i\,v_B(r)\,\mathscr Y^{jm}_{j-1/2}\end{pmatrix} , \qquad (8.113)
> $$
>
> of parity $(-1)^{j-1/2}$ and $(-1)^{j+1/2}$: the parity of the upper component's $\ell$. Orbital angular momentum is not a good quantum number; $\ell$ of the upper component serves as a label.
> 2. $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})\,\mathscr Y^{jm}_{\ell = j\mp1/2} = -\mathscr Y^{jm}_{\ell = j\pm1/2}$ (8.125), and $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p = (\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})\bigl[-i\hbar\,\partial_r + \frac{i}{r}\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf L\bigr]$ (8.115)–(8.116), with $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf L\,\mathscr Y^{jm}_\ell = \hbar\kappa\,\mathscr Y^{jm}_\ell$, $\kappa = \lambda - 1$ for $\ell = j - \frac12$ and $\kappa = -(\lambda + 1)$ for $\ell = j + \frac12$ (8.117)–(8.120).
> 3. The radial functions of $\Psi_A$ satisfy
>
> $$
> \bigl[E - mc^2 - V(r)\bigr]u - \hbar c\Bigl[\frac{d}{dr} + \frac{\lambda + 1}{r}\Bigr]v = 0, \qquad \bigl[E + mc^2 - V(r)\bigr]v + \hbar c\Bigl[\frac{d}{dr} - \frac{\lambda - 1}{r}\Bigr]u = 0 , \qquad (8.128)
> $$
>
> and those of $\Psi_B$ the same equations with $\lambda \to -\lambda$ (8.129).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.3.2, eqs. (8.82)–(8.84), §8.4, eqs. (8.108)–(8.129); $\hbar$, $c$ restored and the $\theta = 0$ evaluation checked against the phase convention of Theorem §C7.1.5 here*

^thm-c13-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* A common eigenfunction of $\mathbf J^2$, $J_z$ with eigenvalues $j$, $m$ has each two-component part built from $\mathscr Y^{jm}_\ell$ with $\ell = j \pm \frac12$, and under $\mathbf x \to -\mathbf x$, $\mathscr Y^{jm}_\ell \to (-1)^\ell\mathscr Y^{jm}_\ell$. $\beta\Psi(-\mathbf x) = \bigl(\psi_1(-\mathbf x), -\psi_2(-\mathbf x)\bigr)$, so a parity eigenstate needs $\psi_1$ of parity $\pm$ and $\psi_2$ of parity $\mp$ (8.111)–(8.112): one of them carries $\ell = j - \frac12$ and the other $\ell = j + \frac12$, which differ by one. The parity of $\Psi_A$ is that of $\mathscr Y_{j-1/2}$, $(-1)^{j-1/2}$; the $-i$ is a convention that makes the radial equations real.
>
> *2.* $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}$ commutes with $\mathbf J$ (a scalar under rotations) and changes parity, so it maps $\mathscr Y^{jm}_{j\mp1/2}$ to a multiple $c\,\mathscr Y^{jm}_{j\pm1/2}$; $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})^2 = 1$ gives $c = \pm1$. Evaluate at $\theta = 0$, where $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf r} = \sigma_z$ and $Y_\ell^\mu(0, \phi) = \sqrt{(2\ell + 1)/4\pi}\,\delta_{\mu0}$ (8.122): (3.384) gives $\mathscr Y^{jm}_{j-1/2}(0) = \sqrt{(j + \frac12)/4\pi}\,(\delta_{m,1/2}, \delta_{m,-1/2})$ and $\mathscr Y^{jm}_{j+1/2}(0) = \sqrt{(j + \frac12)/4\pi}\,(-\delta_{m,1/2}, \delta_{m,-1/2})$, so $\sigma_z\mathscr Y_{j-1/2}(0) = -\mathscr Y_{j+1/2}(0)$ and $c = -1$ (8.123)–(8.125). Next, $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p = (\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})^2\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p = (\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})[\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf p + i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot(\hat{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf p)]$, with $\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf p = -i\hbar\,\partial_r$ and $\hat{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf p = \mathbf L/r$. $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf L = \frac2\hbar\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf L = \frac1\hbar(\mathbf J^2 - \mathbf L^2 - \mathbf S^2)$ has eigenvalue $\hbar[j(j + 1) - \ell(\ell + 1) - \frac34]$, which is $\hbar(j - \frac12) = \hbar(\lambda - 1)$ for $\ell = j - \frac12$ and $-\hbar(j + \frac32) = -\hbar(\lambda + 1)$ for $\ell = j + \frac12$.
>
> *3.* In blocks, $(E - mc^2 - V)\psi_1 = c\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p\,\psi_2$ and $(E + mc^2 - V)\psi_2 = c\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p\,\psi_1$ (8.114). For $\psi_2 = -iv\,\mathscr Y_{j+1/2}$: $c\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p\,\psi_2 = c(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})\bigl[-\hbar v' - \hbar(\lambda + 1)v/r\bigr]\mathscr Y_{j+1/2} = \hbar c\,[v' + (\lambda + 1)v/r]\,\mathscr Y_{j-1/2}$, which gives the first equation. For $\psi_1 = u\,\mathscr Y_{j-1/2}$: $c\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p\,\psi_1 = c(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})(-i\hbar)[u' - (\lambda - 1)u/r]\mathscr Y_{j-1/2} = i\hbar c\,[u' - (\lambda - 1)u/r]\,\mathscr Y_{j+1/2}$; equating with $(E + mc^2 - V)(-iv)\mathscr Y_{j+1/2}$ and multiplying by $i$ gives the second. For $\Psi_B$ the roles of the two $\ell$ values are exchanged, which exchanges $\lambda - 1 \leftrightarrow -(\lambda + 1)$, i.e. $\lambda \to -\lambda$.

## The hydrogen atom

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.3.3: Bound States of the Dirac–Coulomb Problem
> For $V = -Ze^2/r$ with $Z\alpha < j + \frac12$, the bound-state energies are
>
> $$
> E_{nj} = mc^2\Biggl[1 + \frac{(Z\alpha)^2}{\Bigl(n - j - \frac12 + \sqrt{(j + \frac12)^2 - (Z\alpha)^2}\Bigr)^2}\Biggr]^{-1/2}, \qquad n = n' + j + \tfrac12,\quad n' = 0, 1, 2, \dots \qquad (8.150)
> $$
>
> 1. **Labels.** $n$ is the principal quantum number and $j = \frac12, \frac32, \dots, n - \frac12$. For $j < n - \frac12$ there are two states of opposite parity, upper components with $\ell = j - \frac12$ and $\ell = j + \frac12$ (e.g. $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$), and they are **degenerate**: $E$ depends on $n$ and $j$ only. For $j = n - \frac12$ ($n' = 0$) only $\ell = j - \frac12$ occurs (e.g. $1S_{1/2}$, $2P_{3/2}$). With the $2j + 1$ values of $m$ the level $n$ has $2n^2$ states, as without relativity.
> 2. **Wave functions.** $u, v \propto x^s e^{-\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon^2}\,x}\times(\text{polynomial of degree } n')$, with $x = mcr/\hbar$, $\varepsilon = E/mc^2$ and $s = -1 + \sqrt{(j + \frac12)^2 - (Z\alpha)^2}$; for $j = \frac12$, $s < 0$, a weak, integrable singularity at the origin (8.139)–(8.144).
> 3. **Limits.** To order $(Z\alpha)^2$, $E = mc^2 - \frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^2}{2n^2}$, the Bohr levels (8.151); to order $(Z\alpha)^4$, the fine-structure formula [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]]. For $Z\alpha \to j + \frac12$ the solution ceases to exist; for $j = \frac12$ at $Z\alpha = 1$, where $E_{1s} \to 0$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.4.1, eqs. (8.130)–(8.151), Problems 8.15–8.16 · Likharev §9.7, eqs. (9.129)–(9.130) (result only); the exclusion of $n' = 0$ for $\ell = j + \frac12$ and the degeneracy count derived here*

^thm-c13-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Scaled equations.* With $\varepsilon = E/mc^2$, $x = mcr/\hbar$ and $V = -Z\alpha\hbar c/r$, (8.128) becomes (8.133)
>
> $$
> \Bigl[\varepsilon - 1 + \frac{Z\alpha}{x}\Bigr]u - \Bigl[\frac{d}{dx} + \frac{\lambda + 1}{x}\Bigr]v = 0, \qquad \Bigl[\varepsilon + 1 + \frac{Z\alpha}{x}\Bigr]v + \Bigl[\frac{d}{dx} - \frac{\lambda - 1}{x}\Bigr]u = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Large $x$.* $(\varepsilon - 1)u = v'$ and $(\varepsilon + 1)v = -u'$ give $v'' = (1 - \varepsilon^2)v$ (8.134)–(8.136); a bound state has $\varepsilon < 1$, so $u, v \sim e^{-kx}$ with $k = \sqrt{1 - \varepsilon^2}$ (8.137)–(8.138).
>
> *Series.* Put $u = e^{-kx}x^s\sum_ia_ix^i$, $v = e^{-kx}x^s\sum_ib_ix^i$ (8.139)–(8.140). The lowest power, $x^{s-1}$, gives $Z\alpha\,a_0 - (s + \lambda + 1)b_0 = 0$ and $(s - \lambda + 1)a_0 + Z\alpha\,b_0 = 0$ (8.141); a nonzero solution needs $(Z\alpha)^2 + (s + 1 + \lambda)(s + 1 - \lambda) = 0$ (8.142), i.e. $s = -1 \pm\sqrt{\lambda^2 - (Z\alpha)^2}$ (8.143). The lower sign gives $s \approx -1 - \lambda \le -2$, too singular to be normalizable; so $s = -1 + \sqrt{\lambda^2 - (Z\alpha)^2}$, real only for $Z\alpha < \lambda$. The coefficient of $x^{s+i-1}$, $i \ge 1$, gives
>
> $$
> (1 - \varepsilon)a_{i-1} - Z\alpha\,a_i - k\,b_{i-1} + (\lambda + 1 + s + i)\,b_i = 0, \qquad (1 + \varepsilon)b_{i-1} + Z\alpha\,b_i - k\,a_{i-1} - (\lambda - 1 - s - i)\,a_i = 0 . \qquad (8.145)
> $$
>
> Multiplying by $\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon}$ and $\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon}$ and adding, the $a_{i-1}$, $b_{i-1}$ terms cancel (because $k = \sqrt{1 - \varepsilon}\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon}$), leaving
>
> $$
> \frac{b_i}{a_i} = \frac{Z\alpha\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon} + (\lambda - 1 - s - i)\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon}}{Z\alpha\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon} + (\lambda + 1 + s + i)\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon}} . \qquad (8.146)
> $$
>
> For large $i$, $b_i/a_i \to -\sqrt{(1 - \varepsilon)/(1 + \varepsilon)}$ and (8.145) gives $a_i/a_{i-1} \sim 2k/i$, so a nonterminating series grows like $e^{2kx}$ and $u, v \sim e^{+kx}$ (Problem 8.15): the series must stop. If $a_i = b_i = 0$ for $i = n' + 1$, (8.145) at $i = n' + 1$ gives $b_{n'}/a_{n'} = \sqrt{(1 - \varepsilon)/(1 + \varepsilon)}$ (8.147)–(8.148). Setting (8.146) at $i = n'$ equal to this, with $\sigma_\pm = \sqrt{1 \pm \varepsilon}$:
>
> $$
> \sigma_+\bigl[Z\alpha\sigma_+ + (\lambda - 1 - s - n')\sigma_-\bigr] = \sigma_-\bigl[Z\alpha\sigma_- + (\lambda + 1 + s + n')\sigma_+\bigr] \;\Longrightarrow\; 2Z\alpha\,\varepsilon = 2(1 + s + n')\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon^2} , \qquad (8.149)
> $$
>
> and with $N = 1 + s + n' = n' + \sqrt{\lambda^2 - (Z\alpha)^2}$, $\varepsilon^2(Z\alpha)^2 = N^2(1 - \varepsilon^2)$, i.e. $\varepsilon = [1 + (Z\alpha)^2/N^2]^{-1/2}$, which with $n' = n - \lambda$ is (8.150). The equations for $\Psi_B$ differ by $\lambda \to -\lambda$, which leaves $s$ and the condition (8.149) unchanged: same energies.
>
> *Which $n' = 0$ states exist.* For $n' = 0$ the series is the single term $i = 0$, and both $b_0/a_0 = Z\alpha/(s + \lambda + 1)$ from (8.141) and $b_0/a_0 = \sqrt{(1 - \varepsilon)/(1 + \varepsilon)} > 0$ must hold. Here $N = s + 1 = \sqrt{\lambda^2 - (Z\alpha)^2}$ and $\varepsilon = N/\lambda$, so $\sqrt{(1 - \varepsilon)/(1 + \varepsilon)} = \sqrt{(\lambda - N)/(\lambda + N)} = Z\alpha/(\lambda + N)$, which equals $Z\alpha/(s + \lambda + 1)$: consistent for $\Psi_A$. For $\Psi_B$ the first ratio is $Z\alpha/(s + 1 - \lambda) = Z\alpha/(N - \lambda) < 0$: no state. Hence for $n' = 0$ only $\ell = j - \frac12$.
>
> *Counting and limits.* For given $n$: $j = n - \frac12$ contributes $2j + 1 = 2n$ states, each $j < n - \frac12$ contributes $2(2j + 1)$, and $2n + \sum_{j = 1/2}^{n - 3/2}2(2j + 1) = 2n + 2(n^2 - n) = 2n^2$. Expanding (8.150) in $Z\alpha$ gives (8.151) at second order, and at fourth order the expansion carried out in [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^rem-b8-3-4|★ Remark: The exact Dirac result and the Lamb shift]] (Sakurai Problem 8.16). For $j = \frac12$, $n' = 0$: $\varepsilon = \sqrt{1 - (Z\alpha)^2}$, which reaches $0$ at $Z\alpha = 1$.

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level B
> - **Layer change.** Level B obtained the fine structure as first-order perturbation theory around the Bohr levels, with a spin–orbit model, and quoted the exact formula from Griffiths Problem 7.22 ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^rem-b8-3-4|★ Remark: The exact Dirac result and the Lamb shift]]). Here (8.150) is derived from [[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^pr-c13-2-1|Principle §C13.2.1]] and is exact within the single-particle Dirac theory, to all orders in $Z\alpha$. Its expansion to $(Z\alpha)^4$ is done in that level-B remark, same result and same algebra, and is not repeated.
> - **The degeneracies.** The nonrelativistic $\ell$ degeneracy ([[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^rem-c6-2-3|Remark: A symmetry, not an accident]]) is broken, but not completely: states with equal $n$ and $j$ and opposite parity stay degenerate to all orders, $2S_{1/2}$ with $2P_{1/2}$, $3P_{3/2}$ with $3D_{3/2}$. Parity distinguishes them, but the energy does not.
> - **The same method.** The structure of the solution is that of [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]]: an exponential at infinity, a power at the origin fixed by an indicial equation, and quantization by termination of a series. What is new is that two radial functions are coupled at first order, and that the power $s$ is irrational, $s + 1 = \sqrt{(j + \frac12)^2 - (Z\alpha)^2}$ instead of $\ell + 1$, as for the spinless particle ([[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^ex-c13-1-1|Example §C13.1.1]]).
> - **Limits of the theory.** At $Z\alpha \approx 1$, i.e. $Z \approx 137$, the binding is of order $mc^2$ and "spontaneous $e^+e^-$ production would occur": the single-particle description cannot be trusted there (Sakurai). For hydrogen, the Lamb shift between $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$, and the electron's $g - 2$, require quantum electrodynamics (Sakurai §8.4.1, §8.5; [[Quantum Field Theory]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.4.1, §8.5 · Likharev §9.7*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C13.3.1: Fine Structure and Lamb Shift of n = 2 against Measurement
> Sakurai's Problem 8.17 quotes NIST transition energies from $1S_{1/2}$ to the $n = 2$ levels of hydrogen: $82\,258.954\,399\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ to $2S_{1/2}$, $82\,258.919\,113\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ to $2P_{1/2}$, $82\,259.285\,001\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ to $2P_{3/2}$. Compare the $2P_{3/2}$–$2P_{1/2}$ and $2S_{1/2}$–$2P_{1/2}$ splittings with (8.150).
>
> *Fine structure.* The data give $E(2P_{3/2}) - E(2P_{1/2}) = 0.365\,888\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ ($10.969\ \mathrm{GHz}$). From (8.150) with $n = 2$, $j = \frac32$ ($n' = 0$) and $j = \frac12$ ($n' = 1$), to leading order $E_{2,3/2} - E_{2,1/2} = mc^2\alpha^4/32$ (the $n = 2$ case of [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]]; level B's [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^ex-b8-3-1|Example §B8.3.1]] found the same $10.9\ \mathrm{GHz}$). Evaluated exactly with the reduced mass $\mu = m/(1 + m/M_p)$ in place of $m$ and $\alpha = 1/137.036$, it is $0.365\,04\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ ($10.944\ \mathrm{GHz}$); with $m$ itself, $0.365\,24\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. Agreement to $0.2\ \%$; the remaining $25\ \mathrm{MHz}$ is of the same order as the radiative correction discussed next (the sources do not analyse it).
>
> *Lamb shift.* (8.150) predicts $E(2S_{1/2}) = E(2P_{1/2})$ exactly. The data give $E(2S_{1/2}) - E(2P_{1/2}) = 0.035\,286\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}} = 1057.8\ \mathrm{MHz}$, about a tenth of the fine structure, with $2S_{1/2}$ higher: the Lamb shift, the "profound discrepancy" between Sakurai's Fig. 8.2 and (8.150). It is explained by the interaction with the quantized radiation field (Sakurai §8.5; [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^rem-b8-3-4|★ Remark: The exact Dirac result and the Lamb shift]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 8.17 (data table) and §8.4.1, Fig. 8.2; numbers computed here (the reduced-mass substitution is an approximation, not in the sources)*

> [!example] Example §C13.3.2: The Ground State of Hydrogen-Like Ions
> Find the $1S_{1/2}$ energy and wave function for nuclear charge $Z$, and evaluate them for hydrogen and for the hydrogen-like uranium ion U$^{91+}$ ($Z = 92$).
>
> *Energy.* $n = 1$, $j = \frac12$, $n' = 0$, $\lambda = 1$: $s + 1 = \sqrt{1 - (Z\alpha)^2}$ and
>
> $$
> E_{1s} = mc^2\sqrt{1 - (Z\alpha)^2} .
> $$
>
> *Wave function.* $k = \sqrt{1 - \varepsilon^2} = Z\alpha$, so $kx = Zr/a_0$: the exponential is exactly the Bohr one. $u = a_0'\,x^se^{-Zr/a_0}$ and $v = b_0'\,x^se^{-Zr/a_0}$ with $b_0'/a_0' = Z\alpha/(1 + \sqrt{1 - (Z\alpha)^2}) = \sqrt{(1 - \varepsilon)/(1 + \varepsilon)}$ ([[§C13.3★ Solutions of the Dirac Equation and the Hydrogen Atom#^thm-c13-3-3|Theorem §C13.3.3]]). Since $u$ and $v$ have the same shape and the $\mathscr Y$ are normalized, the probability carried by the lower components is $\frac{b_0'^2}{a_0'^2 + b_0'^2} = \frac{1 - \varepsilon}{2}$.
>
> *Numbers* ($mc^2 = 511.0\ \mathrm{keV}$, $\alpha = 1/137.036$):
>
> | | $Z\alpha$ | binding $mc^2 - E_{1s}$ | Bohr $\frac12mc^2(Z\alpha)^2$ | $s$ | lower-component probability |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | H | $0.00730$ | $13.6059\ \mathrm{eV}$ | $13.6057\ \mathrm{eV}$ | $-2.7\times10^{-5}$ | $1.3\times10^{-5}$ |
> | U$^{91+}$ | $0.671$ | $132.3\ \mathrm{keV}$ | $115.2\ \mathrm{keV}$ | $-0.259$ | $0.129$ |
>
> For hydrogen the relativistic binding exceeds Bohr's by $mc^2\alpha^4/8 = 0.18\ \mathrm{meV}$, the $n = 1$ fine-structure shift. For uranium the excess is $17\ \mathrm{keV}$ (15 %), the small components carry 13 % of the probability, and $|\Psi|^2 \propto r^{2s} = r^{-0.52}$ diverges, integrably, at the nucleus: a point nucleus is no longer a good model at this level of precision, and the binding is a quarter of $mc^2$. The figure shows how the Dirac, Klein–Gordon and Schrödinger ground states part as $Z\alpha$ grows.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.4.1, eqs. (8.139)–(8.150); worked here*
