*Sources: the user's 511 notes, §"Spin Precession" · the user's series, Part IV, §3 · Sakurai & Napolitano, Modern Quantum Mechanics (3rd ed.), §8.2–8.3 · Likharev, Essential Graduate Physics — QM, §9.6–9.7, Problems 9.19–9.20.*

Is there a relativistic wave equation that is first order in time, so that a positive density can be conserved? Dirac's answer requires the coefficients of the equation to be matrices, and the wave function to have four components; spin one half and the electron's magnetic moment then come out instead of being put in. Level B postulated spin ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]), took the electron's $g \approx 2$ from experiment ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]]; level A: [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^def-a5-2-3|Def. §A5.2.3]]), and modelled the spin–orbit interaction with a Thomas factor inserted by hand ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^mod-b8-3-3|Model §B8.3.3]]); level C derived spin one half from rotations ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations]]) but not its magnetic moment. This section derives the Dirac equation, its algebra of $\gamma$ matrices and its positive density; finds spin as the part of the conserved angular momentum that is not orbital; obtains $g = 2$ in the nonrelativistic limit; and, one order further, the relativistic kinetic correction, the spin–orbit coupling with its Thomas factor and the Darwin term, the three terms of hydrogen's fine structure ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]]). Its discrete symmetries (parity, charge conjugation, time reversal) close it. Relativity B supplies the metric and the Lorentz group ([[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^def-b1-1-3|REL Def. §B1.1.3]], [[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-1|REL Def. §B1.2.1]]).

*Notation.* As in [[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^cau-c13-1-1|Caution: Units, metric and symbols in Chapter C13★]]: $\hbar$ and $c$ explicit, signature $+\,-\,-\,-$, Gaussian units with charge $e$ ($e < 0$ for the electron), $\boldsymbol\pi = \mathbf p - \frac ec\mathbf A$ the kinematical momentum ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^def-c4-3-1|Def. §C4.3.1]]; Sakurai writes $\tilde{\mathbf p}$). $\Psi = \binom{\psi_1}{\psi_2}$ is a four-component wave function with two-component upper and lower parts; $\boldsymbol\sigma$ are the Pauli matrices.

> [!caution] Caution: Dirac matrices
> - **Representation.** Sakurai's $4\times4$ matrices, written as $2\times2$ blocks of $2\times2$ matrices (the *Dirac representation*), are
>
> $$
> \beta = \gamma^0 = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol\alpha = \begin{pmatrix}0 & \boldsymbol\sigma\\ \boldsymbol\sigma & 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad \gamma^i = \beta\alpha_i = \begin{pmatrix}0 & \sigma^i\\ -\sigma^i & 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol\sigma & 0\\ 0 & \boldsymbol\sigma\end{pmatrix}, \quad \gamma^5 = i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3 = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}
> $$
>
> (eqs. (8.52), (8.55), (8.66), (8.106)–(8.107), Problem 8.9), with $\bar\Psi = \Psi^\dagger\gamma^0$. Physical results do not depend on the representation; other ones are common in quantum field theory.
> - **Signature.** The anticommutator $\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}$ (8.48) uses $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$, the signature of Relativity B, so $(\gamma^0)^2 = 1$ and $(\gamma^i)^2 = -1$. With the opposite signature (Griffiths and Schutz, [[§B1.1 The Metric and Index Notation#^cau-b1-1-2|REL Caution: Sign conventions for the metric]]) the same matrices satisfy $\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = -2\eta^{\mu\nu}$.
> - **Units.** Sakurai writes $(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)\Psi = 0$ and $H = \boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p + \beta m$; with $\hbar$, $c$ these are $(i\hbar\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - mc)\Psi = 0$ and $H = c\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p + \beta mc^2$. Bold $\boldsymbol\alpha$ is a Dirac matrix, plain $\alpha = e^2/\hbar c$ the fine-structure constant.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.2, eqs. (8.46)–(8.55), §8.3.5, eqs. (8.106)–(8.107), Problem 8.9 · Likharev §9.6, eqs. (9.97)–(9.99)*

## The equation

> [!principle] Principle §C13.2.1: The Dirac Equation
> A spin-½ particle of mass $m$ is described by a four-component wave function $\Psi(\mathbf x, t)$ obeying
>
> $$
> \bigl(i\hbar\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - mc\bigr)\Psi = 0, \qquad \{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}\,\mathbb 1 , \qquad (8.46),\ (8.48)
> $$
>
> or, in Hamiltonian form,
>
> $$
> i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = H\Psi, \qquad H = c\,\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p + \beta mc^2, \qquad \alpha_i = \gamma^0\gamma^i, \quad \beta = \gamma^0 , \qquad (8.51)\text{–}(8.53)
> $$
>
> with Hermitian $\boldsymbol\alpha$, $\beta$. In external potentials, by minimal coupling ([[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^pr-c13-1-3|Principle §C13.1.3]]),
>
> $$
> \Bigl(i\hbar\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - \frac ec\gamma^\mu A_\mu - mc\Bigr)\Psi = 0, \qquad H = c\,\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\boldsymbol\pi + \beta mc^2 + e\Phi . \qquad (8.54),\ (8.68)\text{–}(8.69),\ (8.86)
> $$
>
> *Domain: a pointlike spin-½ particle (the electron, the muon) in classical external fields, as a single particle; it fails where pairs can be created, for energies of order $2mc^2$ or Coulomb fields with $Z\alpha \to 1$ (Sakurai §8.4.1, §8.5). Corrections from the quantized radiation field (the Lamb shift, $g - 2$) are beyond it.*
> *Source: Sakurai §8.2, eqs. (8.46)–(8.55), §8.2.4, eqs. (8.68)–(8.69), §8.3.3, eq. (8.86) · Likharev §9.6, eqs. (9.95)–(9.100)*

^pr-c13-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.2.2: Why Matrices, and Why Four by Four
> 1. Every component of every solution of $(i\hbar\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - mc)\Psi = 0$ satisfies the Klein–Gordon equation exactly when $\frac12\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = \eta^{\mu\nu}$: $(\gamma^0)^2 = 1$, $(\gamma^i)^2 = -1$, $\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu = -\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu$ for $\mu \ne \nu$ (8.47)–(8.49). Equivalently $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3, \beta$ each square to $1$ and anticommute pairwise.
> 2. The $\gamma^\mu$, $\alpha_i$ and $\beta$ are traceless. Matrices with these properties exist only in even dimension $n \ge 4$; the Dirac representation realizes $n = 4$. $\gamma^0$ is Hermitian and the $\gamma^i$ are anti-Hermitian.
> 3. $H = c\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p + \beta mc^2$ satisfies $H^2 = c^2\mathbf p^2 + m^2c^4$. For each momentum $\mathbf p$ its eigenvalues are $+E_p$ and $-E_p$, each twice.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.2, eqs. (8.47)–(8.55), Problems 8.8–8.9, 8.11 · Likharev §9.6, eq. (9.100); the dimension bound and part 3 derived here*

^thm-c13-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Apply $-i\hbar\gamma^\nu\partial_\nu - mc$: $(-i\hbar\gamma^\nu\partial_\nu - mc)(i\hbar\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - mc)\Psi = \bigl(\hbar^2\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu\partial_\nu\partial_\mu + m^2c^2\bigr)\Psi = 0$, the cross terms cancelling. Since $\partial_\nu\partial_\mu$ is symmetric, $\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu\partial_\nu\partial_\mu = \frac12\{\gamma^\nu, \gamma^\mu\}\partial_\nu\partial_\mu$, which is $\Box = \eta^{\nu\mu}\partial_\nu\partial_\mu$ for every $\Psi$ exactly when $\frac12\{\gamma^\nu, \gamma^\mu\} = \eta^{\nu\mu}$. Then $\beta^2 = (\gamma^0)^2 = 1$, $\alpha_i^2 = \gamma^0\gamma^i\gamma^0\gamma^i = -(\gamma^0)^2(\gamma^i)^2 = 1$, and the anticommutation of the $\gamma$'s carries over to $\alpha_i$, $\beta$.
>
> *2.* For $\mu \ne \nu$, $\gamma^\mu = (\gamma^\nu)^{-1}\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu = -(\gamma^\nu)^{-1}\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu$, and the cyclicity of the trace ([[§31 Trace꞉ A Connection Between Matrices and Operators#^ladr-8-57|LADR Thm. 8.57]]) gives $\operatorname{tr}\gamma^\mu = -\operatorname{tr}\gamma^\mu = 0$; likewise for $\alpha_i$ and $\beta$. $\beta^2 = 1$ means its eigenvalues are $\pm1$, and tracelessness means equally many of each, so $n$ is even. In $n = 2$, the traceless matrices form the three-dimensional span of $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3$, but four matrices that square to $1$ and anticommute pairwise are linearly independent: if $\sum_ac_aM_a = 0$, anticommuting with $M_b$ gives $2c_b = 0$. So $n^2 - 1 \ge 4$, and with $n$ even, $n \ge 4$. The block matrices of the caution satisfy the relations because $\sigma_i\sigma_j + \sigma_j\sigma_i = 2\delta_{ij}$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]). Hermiticity: $\gamma^0 = \beta$; $(\gamma^i)^\dagger = \alpha_i\beta = -\beta\alpha_i = -\gamma^i$.
>
> *3.* $H^2 = c^2\sum_{ij}\alpha_i\alpha_jp_ip_j + mc^3\sum_i(\alpha_i\beta + \beta\alpha_i)p_i + \beta^2m^2c^4 = c^2\mathbf p^2 + m^2c^4$, since $p_ip_j$ is symmetric and $\{\alpha_i, \alpha_j\} = 2\delta_{ij}$. So every eigenvalue is $\pm E_p$; $\operatorname{tr}(c\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p + \beta mc^2) = 0$ in the $4$-dimensional space of a fixed $\mathbf p$, so each sign occurs twice.

> [!remark] Remark: The square root of the Klein–Gordon operator
> The Dirac operator is a square root of the Klein–Gordon operator, $(-i\hbar\gamma^\nu\partial_\nu - mc)(i\hbar\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - mc) = \hbar^2\Box + m^2c^2$, which ordinary numbers cannot provide ([[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^rem-c13-1-1|Remark: Why square]]); matrices can, at the price of four components. The Pauli matrices already satisfy the three-dimensional version, $\{\sigma_i, \sigma_j\} = 2\delta_{ij}$, so that $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p)^2 = \mathbf p^2$; the user's series flags this anticommutator as "a structure the Klein–Gordon-to-Dirac story will one day need". Dirac's equation adds a fourth anticommuting matrix for the mass term and the time direction.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.2, eq. (8.47) and the paragraph after (8.55) · the user's series, Part IV, §3*

^rem-c13-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.2.3: A Positive Conserved Density
> For every solution of the Dirac equation (with or without external fields),
>
> $$
> \rho = \Psi^\dagger\Psi \ge 0, \qquad \mathbf j = c\,\Psi^\dagger\boldsymbol\alpha\Psi, \qquad \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf j = 0 ; \qquad (8.56)
> $$
>
> covariantly, $j^\mu = c\,\bar\Psi\gamma^\mu\Psi = (c\rho, \mathbf j)$ and $\partial_\mu j^\mu = 0$ (8.57)–(8.58). For a free plane wave $\Psi \propto e^{-ip\cdot x/\hbar}$, $j^\mu = (p^\mu/m)\,\bar\Psi\Psi$ (8.61), so $\rho = (E/mc^2)\bar\Psi\Psi$ with $\bar\Psi\Psi = \psi_1^\dagger\psi_1 - \psi_2^\dagger\psi_2$ (8.62)–(8.63).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.2.1, eqs. (8.56)–(8.63), Problem 8.10 · Likharev §9.6, eqs. (9.101)–(9.102), Problem 9.16; the continuity equation with fields derived here*

^thm-c13-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $i\hbar\,\partial_t(\Psi^\dagger\Psi) = \Psi^\dagger(H\Psi) - (H\Psi)^\dagger\Psi$. The terms $\beta mc^2$ and $e\Phi$ drop out, being Hermitian matrices times real functions, and so does $-e\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf A$. What remains is $c[\Psi^\dagger\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot(-i\hbar\nabla\Psi) - (-i\hbar\nabla\Psi)^\dagger\cdot\boldsymbol\alpha\Psi] = -i\hbar c\,\nabla\cdot(\Psi^\dagger\boldsymbol\alpha\Psi)$, using $\boldsymbol\alpha^\dagger = \boldsymbol\alpha$. Divide by $i\hbar$. Covariantly, $c\bar\Psi\gamma^0\Psi = c\Psi^\dagger\Psi$ and $c\bar\Psi\gamma^i\Psi = c\Psi^\dagger\gamma^0\gamma^i\Psi = c\Psi^\dagger\alpha_i\Psi$. *Plane wave.* $(\gamma^\mu p_\mu - mc)\Psi = 0$ (8.59); its adjoint, with $\gamma^0\gamma^{\mu\dagger}\gamma^0 = \gamma^\mu$, is $\bar\Psi(\gamma^\mu p_\mu - mc) = 0$ (8.60). Writing $\Psi = \gamma^\nu p_\nu\Psi/mc$ on the right and $\bar\Psi = \bar\Psi\gamma^\nu p_\nu/mc$ on the left of $\bar\Psi\gamma^\mu\Psi$, and averaging, $j^\mu = \frac{c}{2mc}\bar\Psi\{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\}p_\nu\Psi = \frac{p^\mu}{m}\bar\Psi\Psi$ (8.61).

^der-c13-2-3

*Uses:* [[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^pr-c13-2-1|Principle §C13.2.1]], [[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-2|Theorem §C13.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from the Klein–Gordon equation
> The density of [[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^thm-c13-1-1|Theorem §C13.1.1]] contained $\partial_t\Psi$ and could have either sign; the Dirac density is a sum of squared moduli, because the equation is first order in time and $\Psi$ alone is the initial datum. This positivity "was one of the main reasons it was adopted" (Sakurai). The negative energies remain ([[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-2|Theorem §C13.2.2]], 3); in the plane-wave form $\rho = (E/mc^2)\bar\Psi\Psi$, they are positive density with $\bar\Psi\Psi < 0$, the lower components dominating ([[§C13.3★ Solutions of the Dirac Equation and the Hydrogen Atom#^thm-c13-3-1|Theorem §C13.3.1]]). The factor $E/mc^2$ is the Lorentz factor, as expected from the contraction of $d^3x$ in the direction of motion: $\rho$ is the time component of a four-vector, not a scalar.

## Spin

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.2.4: Spin One Half from the Dirac Equation
> 1. For the free Hamiltonian, and for $H = c\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p + \beta mc^2 + V(r)$,
>
> $$
> [H, \mathbf L] = -i\hbar c\,\boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf p, \qquad \Bigl[H, \frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\Sigma\Bigr] = +i\hbar c\,\boldsymbol\alpha\times\mathbf p, \qquad [H, \mathbf J] = 0 \quad\text{for}\quad \mathbf J = \mathbf L + \frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\Sigma \qquad (8.79)\text{–}(8.81)
> $$
>
> (for a central $V$): neither the orbital nor the spin angular momentum is conserved, only their sum.
> 2. $\mathbf S = \frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\Sigma$ satisfies $[S_i, S_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}S_k$ and $\mathbf S^2 = \frac34\hbar^2$: the extra components of $\Psi$ carry spin one half, once in the upper and once in the lower pair.
> 3. $\mathbf J$ generates rotations of Dirac wave functions, acting on the orbital argument and, by the spin-½ rotation matrix ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]]), on each two-component block; a rotation by $2\pi$ multiplies every Dirac wave function by $-1$ ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-3|Theorem §C5.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.3.1, eqs. (8.79)–(8.81), §8.2.2, eq. (8.66) · Likharev §9.6, eqs. (9.106)–(9.110), Problems 9.17–9.18; parts 2–3 derived here*

^thm-c13-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\boldsymbol\alpha$, $\beta$ commute with $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf p$, and $[\beta, \mathbf L] = 0$; $[V(r), \mathbf L] = 0$ ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]). So $[H, L_i] = c\,\alpha_l\epsilon_{ijk}[p_l, x_j]p_k = -i\hbar c\,\epsilon_{ijk}\alpha_jp_k$ (8.79). For $\boldsymbol\Sigma$: $\beta\Sigma_i = \Sigma_i\beta$ (both block diagonal), $\Sigma_i$ commutes with $V(r)$, and $[\alpha_l, \Sigma_i] = 2i\epsilon_{lik}\alpha_k$, from $[\sigma_l, \sigma_i] = 2i\epsilon_{lik}\sigma_k$ in the off-diagonal blocks. Hence $[H, \Sigma_i] = 2ic\,\epsilon_{lik}\alpha_kp_l = 2ic\,\epsilon_{ijk}\alpha_jp_k$ (relabelling and using cyclicity of $\epsilon$) (8.80), and the two commutators cancel in $\mathbf L + \frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\Sigma$.
>
> *2.* $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ is $\boldsymbol\sigma$ in each diagonal block, and $\frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\sigma$ satisfies the angular-momentum algebra with $\mathbf S^2 = \frac34\hbar^2$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-3|Theorem §B6.1.3]]).
>
> *3.* $\exp(-i\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf J/\hbar) = \exp(-i\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf L/\hbar)\exp(-i\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\Sigma/2)$, the two exponents commuting; the second factor is the matrix of [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]] in each block, which is $-1$ at $\theta = 2\pi$, while the orbital factor is $1$ there ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-4|Theorem §C5.3.4]]).

^der-c13-2-4

*Uses:* [[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^pr-c13-2-1|Principle §C13.2.1]], [[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]], [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-3|Theorem §B6.1.3]], [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]], [[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-4|Theorem §C5.3.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Spin changes layer
> At level B spin was a principle, a new degree of freedom with the angular-momentum algebra, independent of the orbital motion ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]); at level C, [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations]] showed what spin one half is as a representation of rotations, but not why the electron has it. In Dirac's theory it is forced: a first-order equation needs at least four components (Theorem §C13.2.2), and conservation of angular momentum requires adding $\frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\Sigma$ to $\mathbf L$ (Theorem §C13.2.4). Dirac "discovered the need for $j = 1/2$ angular momentum states in nature" (Sakurai); in Likharev's words the theory "replaces the Pauli Hamiltonian postulate with that of a simpler (and hence more plausible) Lorentz-invariant Hamiltonian". What it does not explain is why particles of other spins exist; the Klein–Gordon field describes spin 0 ([[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^thm-c13-1-6|Theorem §C13.1.6]], 5), and other equations describe other spins.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.2 (introduction), §8.3.1 · Likharev §9.6*

^rem-c13-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Lorentz covariance and the rapidity
> Neither source writes out how $\Psi$ transforms between inertial frames; the standard result is recorded here, with its check. Let $x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-1|REL Def. §B1.2.1]]). Then $\partial_\mu = \Lambda^\nu{}_\mu\partial'_\nu$, and multiplying the Dirac equation by an invertible matrix $S(\Lambda)$ shows that $\Psi'(x') = S(\Lambda)\Psi(x)$ satisfies the same equation in the primed frame provided
>
> $$
> S^{-1}\gamma^\mu S = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\,\gamma^\nu .
> $$
>
> For a boost of rapidity $\varphi$ along $\hat{\mathbf n}$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-3|REL Def. §B1.2.3]]), $S = \cosh\frac\varphi2 - \hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\alpha\,\sinh\frac\varphi2 = e^{-\varphi\,\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\alpha/2}$; for a rotation of the axes by $\theta$ about $\hat{\mathbf n}$, $S = e^{+i\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\Sigma/2}$, two copies of the spin-½ rotation matrix with the angle reversed because the axes, not the system, are turned ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^cau-c5-1-1|Caution: Active and passive rotations]]). *Check for a boost along $x$:* $\alpha_1$ anticommutes with $\gamma^0$ and $\gamma^1$ and commutes with $\gamma^2$, $\gamma^3$, so $S^{-1}\gamma^0S = S^{-2}\gamma^0 = (\cosh\varphi + \alpha_1\sinh\varphi)\gamma^0 = \gamma^0\cosh\varphi - \gamma^1\sinh\varphi$ and $S^{-1}\gamma^1S = \gamma^1\cosh\varphi - \gamma^0\sinh\varphi$, which are the rows of the boost ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-7|REL Theorem §B1.2.7]]). Two features: the angles enter halved, as for spinors under rotations; and $S$ is not unitary for boosts, consistent with $\Psi^\dagger\Psi$ being the time component of the four-vector $j^\mu$ ([[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-3|Theorem §C13.2.3]]) rather than a scalar; the scalar is $\bar\Psi\Psi$. The generators $\frac i4[\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu]$ of these $S$ are the Lorentz algebra that Relativity B deferred ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^rem-b1-2-6|REL ★ Remark: Infinitesimal Lorentz transformations]]); their systematic use is quantum field theory ([[Quantum Field Theory I]]).

## The nonrelativistic limit

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.2.5: The Pauli Equation and g = 2
> Let $E = mc^2 + E'$ with $|E'|$ and $|e\Phi|$ small compared with $mc^2$. To lowest order in $v/c$ the lower components are $\psi_2 \approx \frac{\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol\pi}{2mc}\psi_1$, of relative size $v/2c$, and the upper ones obey
>
> $$
> \Bigl[\frac{\boldsymbol\pi^2}{2m} - \boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B + e\Phi\Bigr]\psi_1 = E'\psi_1, \qquad \boldsymbol\mu = g\,\frac{e}{2mc}\,\mathbf S, \qquad \mathbf S = \frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\sigma, \qquad g = 2 , \qquad (8.73)\text{–}(8.76)
> $$
>
> the Schrödinger–Pauli equation of a charged particle with a spin magnetic moment. In SI ($e/c \to q$), $\boldsymbol\mu = (q/m)\mathbf S$: for the electron $\gamma = -e/m$, twice the classical gyromagnetic ratio.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.2.4, eqs. (8.68)–(8.76) (there with $\Phi = 0$) · Likharev §9.7, eqs. (9.112)–(9.123)*

^thm-c13-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In blocks, $H\Psi = E\Psi$ reads $(E - e\Phi - mc^2)\psi_1 = c\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol\pi\,\psi_2$ and $(E - e\Phi + mc^2)\psi_2 = c\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol\pi\,\psi_1$ (8.69). In the second, $E - e\Phi + mc^2 \approx 2mc^2$ (8.70), so $\psi_2 \approx \boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol\pi\,\psi_1/2mc$; inserting into the first, $(E' - e\Phi)\psi_1 = \frac{(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)^2}{2m}\psi_1$. For vector operators $\mathbf a$, $\mathbf b$ commuting with $\boldsymbol\sigma$, $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf a)(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf b) = \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b + i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)$, from $\sigma_i\sigma_j = \delta_{ij} + i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]); the order of $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$ is kept. The components of $\boldsymbol\pi$ do not commute: $[\pi_i, \pi_j] = \frac{i\hbar e}{c}\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$ ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-3|Theorem §C4.3.3]]), so $(\boldsymbol\pi\times\boldsymbol\pi)_k = \frac12\epsilon_{kij}[\pi_i, \pi_j] = \frac{i\hbar e}{c}B_k$ (Sakurai's (8.72) in natural units: $\tilde{\mathbf p}\times\tilde{\mathbf p} = ie\mathbf B$). Hence $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)^2 = \boldsymbol\pi^2 - \frac{\hbar e}{c}\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf B$, and $\frac{\hbar e}{2mc}\boldsymbol\sigma = \frac{e}{mc}\mathbf S = 2\cdot\frac{e}{2mc}\mathbf S$.

> [!remark] Remark: g = 2 changes layer
> Level B introduced the spin magnetic moment as a definition with an empirical constant, $\gamma = -e/m$ for the electron "almost exactly" ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]]), and its Zeeman and fine-structure calculations used $g_s = 2$ as input ([[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting#^thm-b8-4-1|Theorem §B8.4.1]]). "This brings us full circle", Sakurai writes, back to the Stern–Gerlach experiment of his first chapter ([[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space#^mod-c1-1-2|Model §C1.1.2]]). The relativistic Landau levels ([[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^ex-c13-2-2|Example §C13.2.2]]) show $g = 2$ at work exactly.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.2.6: Fine-Structure Terms from the Dirac Equation
> Let $\mathbf A = 0$, $V = e\Phi$, and keep terms of relative order $(v/c)^2$. The two-component function $\tilde\psi = \bigl(1 + \frac{\mathbf p^2}{8m^2c^2}\bigr)\psi_1$, normalized to the same order as $\Psi$, satisfies $H_{\text{eff}}\tilde\psi = E'\tilde\psi$ with
>
> $$
> H_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m} + V - \frac{\mathbf p^4}{8m^3c^2} + \frac{\hbar}{4m^2c^2}\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot(\nabla V\times\mathbf p) + \frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\,\nabla^2V .
> $$
>
> The terms are the relativistic correction to the kinetic energy, the **spin–orbit** term and the **Darwin** term. For a central potential the spin–orbit term is
>
> $$
> H_{\text{so}} = \frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\,\frac1r\frac{dV}{dr}\,\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf L ,
> $$
>
> and for $V = -Ze^2/r$, $H_{\text{so}} = \dfrac{Ze^2}{2m^2c^2r^3}\,\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf L$ and $H_{\text{D}} = \dfrac{\pi\hbar^2Ze^2}{2m^2c^2}\,\delta^3(\mathbf r)$.
>
> *Source: Likharev §9.7, eqs. (9.124)–(9.128) (the three terms stated, the renormalization of $\psi_+$ described, the calculation not given) · Sakurai §8.4.1, text after eq. (8.151); derivation given here*

^thm-c13-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Exact elimination.* With $\mathbf A = 0$, $(E' - V)\psi_1 = c\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p\,\psi_2$ and $(2mc^2 + E' - V)\psi_2 = c\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p\,\psi_1$, so $(E' - V)\psi_1 = \boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p\,\frac{c^2}{2mc^2 + E' - V}\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p\,\psi_1$. Expanding $\frac{c^2}{2mc^2 + E' - V} = \frac1{2m}\bigl(1 - \frac{E' - V}{2mc^2} + \cdots\bigr)$ and using $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p)^2 = \mathbf p^2$,
>
> $$
> (E' - V)\psi_1 = \Bigl[\frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m} - \frac{1}{4m^2c^2}(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p)(E' - V)(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p)\Bigr]\psi_1 . \qquad (*)
> $$
>
> *Normalization.* $\int(\psi_1^\dagger\psi_1 + \psi_2^\dagger\psi_2) = 1$ with $\psi_2 \approx \boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p\,\psi_1/2mc$ gives $\int\psi_1^\dagger\bigl(1 + \frac{\mathbf p^2}{4m^2c^2}\bigr)\psi_1 = 1$, i.e. $\int\tilde\psi^\dagger\tilde\psi = 1$ to this order: $\tilde\psi$ is the function with a probability interpretation (Likharev's renormalization). Substitute $\psi_1 = (1 - \frac{\mathbf p^2}{8m^2c^2})\tilde\psi$ in $(*)$ and multiply by $1 - \frac{\mathbf p^2}{8m^2c^2}$ from the left. Since $(E' - V - \frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m})\tilde\psi$ is already of order $c^{-2}$, the only surviving cross term comes from commuting $\mathbf p^2$ through $V$:
>
> $$
> \Bigl(E' - V - \frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m}\Bigr)\tilde\psi - \frac{[\mathbf p^2, V]}{8m^2c^2}\tilde\psi = -\frac{1}{4m^2c^2}(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p)(E' - V)(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p)\,\tilde\psi .
> $$
>
> *The right side.* $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p)V(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p) = V\mathbf p^2 + [\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p, V]\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p = V\mathbf p^2 - i\hbar(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\nabla V)(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p) = V\mathbf p^2 - i\hbar\nabla V\cdot\mathbf p + \hbar\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot(\nabla V\times\mathbf p)$, by the product rule for $\boldsymbol\sigma$ used in Theorem §C13.2.5. And $(E' - V)\mathbf p^2\tilde\psi = \mathbf p^2(E' - V)\tilde\psi + [\mathbf p^2, V]\tilde\psi \approx \frac{\mathbf p^4}{2m}\tilde\psi + [\mathbf p^2, V]\tilde\psi$. So the right side is $-\frac{1}{4m^2c^2}\bigl[\frac{\mathbf p^4}{2m} + [\mathbf p^2, V] + i\hbar\nabla V\cdot\mathbf p - \hbar\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot(\nabla V\times\mathbf p)\bigr]\tilde\psi$.
>
> *Collecting.* $E'\tilde\psi = \bigl[\frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m} + V - \frac{\mathbf p^4}{8m^3c^2} - \frac{[\mathbf p^2, V]}{8m^2c^2} - \frac{i\hbar\nabla V\cdot\mathbf p}{4m^2c^2} + \frac{\hbar}{4m^2c^2}\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot(\nabla V\times\mathbf p)\bigr]\tilde\psi$. With $[\mathbf p^2, V] = -\hbar^2\nabla^2V - 2i\hbar\nabla V\cdot\mathbf p$, the two $\nabla V\cdot\mathbf p$ terms cancel and $+\frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\nabla^2V$ remains. *Central potential.* $\nabla V = \frac{dV}{dr}\frac{\mathbf r}{r}$, so $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot(\nabla V\times\mathbf p) = \frac1r\frac{dV}{dr}\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf L$ and $\frac{\hbar}{4m^2c^2}\boldsymbol\sigma = \frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\mathbf S$. *Coulomb.* $\frac1r\frac{dV}{dr} = \frac{Ze^2}{r^3}$ and $\nabla^2\frac1r = -4\pi\delta^3(\mathbf r)$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|EM Theorem §B1.2.2]]), so $\nabla^2V = 4\pi Ze^2\delta^3(\mathbf r)$.

> [!remark] Remark: The three terms change layer
> - **Kinetic.** $-\mathbf p^4/8m^3c^2$ is level B's relativistic correction ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-2|Theorem §B8.3.2]]), there obtained by expanding $\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}$; same term, now from the Dirac Hamiltonian.
> - **Spin–orbit.** In SI ($e^2 \to e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0$, [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^cau-c6-2-1|Caution: Gaussian and SI units]]) $H_{\text{so}}$ for hydrogen is $\frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{1}{m^2c^2r^3}\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf L$, exactly level B's [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^mod-b8-3-3|Model §B8.3.3]]. There it was assembled from a rest-frame magnetic field, $g = 2$ "from Dirac", and a Thomas factor $\frac12$ "taken from relativistic kinematics without derivation" ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^rem-b8-3-2|Remark: Where the spin–orbit term comes from]]). Here the coefficient, Thomas factor included, is a theorem: the model becomes a consequence of [[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^pr-c13-2-1|Principle §C13.2.1]].
> - **Darwin.** New. It acts only where $\nabla^2V \ne 0$, for the Coulomb field only at the nucleus, so only $s$ states feel it. With $|\psi_{n00}(0)|^2 = Z^3/\pi a_0^3n^3$ ([[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^ex-c6-2-2|Example §C6.2.2]] for $n = 1$) and $a_0 = \hbar/mc\alpha$, first-order perturbation theory gives $E_{\text{D}} = \frac{\pi\hbar^2Ze^2}{2m^2c^2}\cdot\frac{Z^3}{\pi a_0^3n^3} = \frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3}$ (Likharev eq. (9.128)). Added to the relativistic correction for $\ell = 0$, $-\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{8n^4}(8n - 3)$, it gives $-\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{8n^4}(4n - 3) = \frac{E_n^2}{2mc^2}\bigl(3 - \frac{4n}{j + \frac12}\bigr)$ with $j = \frac12$: the fine-structure formula [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]] for $s$ states, where level B had to rely on the formally indeterminate $\ell = 0$ limit of the spin–orbit term (Griffiths' footnote 18, quoted in its [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^der-b8-3-5|Derivation]]). For hydrogen $1s$, $E_{\text{D}} = mc^2\alpha^4/2 = 7.2\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV}$.
>
> *Source: Likharev §9.7, eqs. (9.125)–(9.128) and the discussion there · Sakurai §8.4.1*

## Discrete symmetries

> [!theorem] Theorem §C13.2.7: Parity, Charge Conjugation and Time Reversal
> 1. **Parity.** If $V(-\mathbf x) = V(\mathbf x)$ and $\mathbf A = 0$, then with $\Psi(\mathbf x, t)$ also $\Psi_P(\mathbf x, t) = \beta\,\Psi(-\mathbf x, t)$ solves the equation: the parity operator on Dirac wave functions is $\mathscr P = \pi\beta$, the coordinate reflection $\pi$ times $U_P = \beta$, which satisfies $U_P^\dagger\boldsymbol\alpha U_P = -\boldsymbol\alpha$, $U_P^\dagger\beta U_P = \beta$, $U_P^2 = 1$ (8.82)–(8.84).
> 2. **Charge conjugation.** If $\Psi$ solves the equation with charge $e$, then $\Psi_C = i\gamma^2\Psi^* = U_C\bar\Psi^{\mathsf T}$, $U_C = i\gamma^2\gamma^0$, solves it with charge $-e$ (8.86)–(8.93). For a free solution $\propto e^{-ip\cdot x/\hbar}$, $\Psi_C \propto e^{+ip\cdot x/\hbar}$: charge conjugation maps positive- to negative-energy solutions.
> 3. **Time reversal.** If $\mathbf A = 0$ and $V$ is real, with $\Psi(\mathbf x, t)$ also $\Psi_T(\mathbf x, t) = \gamma^1\gamma^3\,\Psi^*(\mathbf x, -t)$ solves the equation (8.99)–(8.104). The time-reversal operator is antiunitary, $\mathscr T = U_TK$ with $U_T = \gamma^1\gamma^3$, which in the Dirac representation is $i\sigma_y$ in each $2\times2$ block, the spin-½ time reversal of [[§C8.3★ Time Reversal]] (Sakurai Problem 8.13).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.3.2–8.3.4, eqs. (8.82)–(8.104) · Likharev §9.6; the verifications with $\hbar$, $c$ written out here*

^thm-c13-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Write $H = c\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p + \beta mc^2 + V$. Since $\mathbf p = -i\hbar\nabla$, $(\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p\Psi)(-\mathbf x) = -\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p[\Psi(-\mathbf x)]$, so $i\hbar\,\partial_t\Psi_P = \beta[-c\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p + \beta mc^2 + V]\Psi(-\mathbf x) = [c\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p + \beta mc^2 + V]\beta\Psi(-\mathbf x)$, by $\beta\boldsymbol\alpha = -\boldsymbol\alpha\beta$ and $V(-\mathbf x) = V(\mathbf x)$.
>
> *2.* Complex conjugation of $(i\hbar\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - \frac ec\gamma^\mu A_\mu - mc)\Psi = 0$ gives $(-i\hbar\gamma^{\mu*}\partial_\mu - \frac ec\gamma^{\mu*}A_\mu - mc)\Psi^* = 0$ (8.87). In the Dirac representation $\gamma^0$, $\gamma^1$, $\gamma^3$ are real and $\gamma^{2*} = -\gamma^2$; with $\tilde C = i\gamma^2$, $\tilde C^{-1} = \tilde C$ (since $(\gamma^2)^2 = -1$), and $\tilde C\gamma^{\mu*}\tilde C^{-1} = -\gamma^\mu$ for all $\mu$ (8.88), (8.90): $\gamma^2$ anticommutes with $\gamma^0, \gamma^1, \gamma^3$ and $\tilde C(-\gamma^2)\tilde C = -\gamma^2$. Multiplying by $\tilde C$ gives $(i\hbar\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu + \frac ec\gamma^\mu A_\mu - mc)\tilde C\Psi^* = 0$ (8.89). Also $\Psi^* = (\Psi^\dagger)^{\mathsf T} = (\bar\Psi\gamma^0)^{\mathsf T} = \gamma^{0\mathsf T}\bar\Psi^{\mathsf T} = \gamma^0\bar\Psi^{\mathsf T}$.
>
> *3.* $i\hbar\,\partial_t\Psi_T(\mathbf x, t) = -i\hbar\,U_T(\partial_t\Psi^*)(\mathbf x, -t) = U_T[(i\hbar\,\partial_t\Psi)(\mathbf x, -t)]^* = U_TH^*U_T^{-1}\Psi_T$, with $H^* = -c\boldsymbol\alpha^*\cdot\mathbf p + \beta mc^2 + V$ because $\mathbf p^* = -\mathbf p$. In blocks, $\gamma^1\gamma^3 = \operatorname{diag}(-\sigma_1\sigma_3, -\sigma_1\sigma_3) = \operatorname{diag}(i\sigma_2, i\sigma_2)$, and $\sigma_2\boldsymbol\sigma^*\sigma_2 = -\boldsymbol\sigma$ gives $U_T\boldsymbol\alpha^*U_T^{-1} = -\boldsymbol\alpha$, while $U_T$ commutes with $\beta$. So $U_TH^*U_T^{-1} = H$.

> [!remark] Remark: The Dirac sea, the positron, and CPT
> The negative energies of [[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-2|Theorem §C13.2.2]] would let an electron radiate its way down without limit. Dirac used the Pauli principle: suppose all negative-energy states are filled (a "sea" of infinite charge and energy to which we are insensitive). A positive-energy electron cannot fall into it; a photon of energy above $2mc^2$ can lift an electron out of it, leaving a hole that behaves as a particle with the electron's mass and the opposite charge, the **positron**, found by Anderson in 1933 (Sakurai Fig. 8.1). Charge conjugation (Theorem §C13.2.7, 2) is the formal version of particle $\leftrightarrow$ hole. Sakurai also combines the three operations: $\mathscr C\mathscr P\mathscr T\,\Psi(\mathbf x, t) = i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3\Psi(-\mathbf x, -t) = \gamma^5\Psi(-\mathbf x, -t)$, which exchanges the upper and lower components and turns an electron wave function into a positron one (8.105)–(8.107); that every Lorentz-invariant quantum field theory is CPT invariant, so that particles and antiparticles have equal masses, is a theorem of quantum field theory. The sea itself is an artifact of the single-particle picture; in field theory it is replaced by a field operator that annihilates electrons and creates positrons, as $\hat\psi$ did for spin 0 ([[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^thm-c13-1-7|Theorem §C13.1.7]]), now with anticommutators ([[Quantum Field Theory I]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §8.2.3, Fig. 8.1, §8.3.5, eqs. (8.105)–(8.107) · Likharev §9.5, Fig. 9.6, §9.7 (end)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C13.2.1: The Velocity Operator and Zitterbewegung
> In the Heisenberg picture, find the velocity of a free Dirac electron, solve its equation of motion, and interpret the result.
>
> *Velocity.* By [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]], $\dot{\mathbf x} = \frac i\hbar[H, \mathbf x] = \frac i\hbar c\,\alpha_j[p_j, \mathbf x] = c\boldsymbol\alpha$. Each component of the velocity operator is $c$ times a matrix with eigenvalues $\pm1$ ($\alpha_i^2 = 1$): a measurement of one velocity component would give $\pm c$, while the momentum, which commutes with $H$, is constant. Velocity and momentum are no longer proportional.
>
> *Equation of motion.* From $\{\alpha_i, H\} = 2cp_i$ ([[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-2|Theorem §C13.2.2]], 1), $[H, \alpha_i] = 2H\alpha_i - 2cp_i$, so $\dot\alpha_i = \frac{2i}{\hbar}(H\alpha_i - cp_i)$. With $H$ and $\mathbf p$ constant and $H$ invertible ($H^2 \ge m^2c^4$), the operator $\boldsymbol\eta = \boldsymbol\alpha - c\mathbf pH^{-1}$ obeys $\dot{\boldsymbol\eta} = \frac{2i}{\hbar}H\boldsymbol\eta$, so $\boldsymbol\eta(t) = e^{2iHt/\hbar}\boldsymbol\eta(0)$, and integrating $\dot{\mathbf x} = c\boldsymbol\alpha$,
>
> $$
> \mathbf x(t) = \mathbf x(0) + c^2\mathbf pH^{-1}\,t + \frac{\hbar c}{2i}H^{-1}\bigl(e^{2iHt/\hbar} - 1\bigr)\boldsymbol\eta(0) .
> $$
>
> *Interpretation.* The second term is uniform motion at the group velocity $c^2\mathbf p/E$ ([[§B4.1 The Klein–Gordon Equation#^thm-b4-1-6|REL Theorem §B4.1.6]]). The third oscillates at angular frequency $2E/\hbar \ge 2mc^2/\hbar = 1.55\times10^{21}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ with amplitude $\hbar c/2E \le \hbar/2mc = 1.9\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm m$ for an electron: the **Zitterbewegung**. Since $\{H, \boldsymbol\eta\} = \{H, \boldsymbol\alpha\} - 2c\mathbf p = 0$, $\boldsymbol\eta$ maps positive-energy states to negative-energy ones. In a state of definite energy and momentum, or in any packet built from positive-energy solutions only, $\langle\boldsymbol\eta\rangle = 0$: the electron moves uniformly with $\langle\dot{\mathbf x}\rangle = c^2\langle\mathbf p/H\rangle$. The trembling is an interference between positive- and negative-energy components, which any packet localized to better than about $\hbar/mc$ must contain; it is one more sign that a single-particle reading fails at the Compton scale.
>
> *Source: Likharev Problem 9.19 (statement); solved here*

> [!example] Example §C13.2.2: Relativistic Landau Levels
> A Dirac particle of charge $e$ moves in a uniform magnetic field $\mathbf B = B\hat{\mathbf z}$ ($\Phi = 0$). Find the exact energy levels and compare with the nonrelativistic and the spinless results.
>
> *Squaring.* $H = c\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\boldsymbol\pi + \beta mc^2$. As in [[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-2|Theorem §C13.2.2]], 3, the cross terms with $\beta$ cancel, but now $(\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)^2 = \boldsymbol\pi^2 + i\boldsymbol\Sigma\cdot(\boldsymbol\pi\times\boldsymbol\pi)$ (using $\alpha_i\alpha_j = \delta_{ij} + i\epsilon_{ijk}\Sigma_k$), and $\boldsymbol\pi\times\boldsymbol\pi = \frac{i\hbar e}{c}\mathbf B$ (proof of [[§C13.2★ The Dirac Equation#^thm-c13-2-5|Theorem §C13.2.5]]):
>
> $$
> H^2 = c^2\boldsymbol\pi^2 - e\hbar c\,B\,\Sigma_z + m^2c^4 ,
> $$
>
> exactly. *Spectrum.* $\boldsymbol\pi^2 = \pi_x^2 + \pi_y^2 + p_z^2$, and $\pi_x^2 + \pi_y^2 = \frac{2\hbar|e|B}{c}\bigl(n_L + \frac12\bigr)$, $n_L = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^ex-c4-3-3|Example §C4.3.3]]); $\Sigma_z = \pm1$ and $p_z$ commute with it and with each other. The positive square roots are the particle levels:
>
> $$
> E^2 = m^2c^4 + c^2p_z^2 + \hbar c|e|B\,\bigl(2n_L + 1 - \operatorname{sgn}(e)\,\Sigma_z\bigr) = m^2c^4 + c^2p_z^2 + 2n\,\hbar c|e|B, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots
> $$
>
> For the electron ($e < 0$) the level $n = 0$ has $n_L = 0$ and $\Sigma_z = -1$, spin opposite to $\mathbf B$ and magnetic moment along it; every level $n \ge 1$ is doubly degenerate, $(n_L = n, \Sigma_z = -1)$ and $(n_L = n - 1, \Sigma_z = +1)$.
>
> *Comparisons.* For $2n\hbar|e|B/mc \ll mc^2$, $E \approx mc^2 + \frac{p_z^2}{2m} + n\,\hbar\omega_c$, $\omega_c = |e|B/mc$: the Landau ladder of [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^ex-c4-3-3|Example §C4.3.3]], $\hbar\omega_c(n_L + \frac12)$, plus the spin energy $\pm\frac12\hbar\omega_c$ of a moment with $g = 2$. The degeneracy of the spin-flipped neighbours is the statement that the spin splitting equals the cyclotron spacing, i.e. $g = 2$ exactly, at all energies. A spinless particle ([[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^pr-c13-1-3|Principle §C13.1.3]]) has $E^2 = m^2c^4 + c^2p_z^2 + \hbar c|e|B(2n_L + 1)$, without the zero level (Likharev Problem 9.13). The levels are no longer equally spaced once $\hbar|e|B/mc$ approaches $mc^2$, i.e. near $B \sim m^2c^3/\hbar|e|$, which in SI is $m^2c^2/\hbar e = 4.4\times10^9\ \mathrm T$ for the electron.
>
> *Source: Likharev Problems 9.13 and 9.20 (statements); solved here*
