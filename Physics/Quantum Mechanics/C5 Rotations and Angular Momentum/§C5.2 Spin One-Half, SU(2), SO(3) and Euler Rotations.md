What does a rotation do to a spin-$\tfrac12$ state, and what group is it really acting with? Level B built the spin-$\tfrac12$ system from the commutation relations: spinors ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^def-b6-1-1|Def. §B6.1.1]]), the Pauli matrices and their algebra ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^def-b6-1-2|Def. §B6.1.2]], [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]), spin along any axis ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]) and Larmor precession ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-3|Theorem §B6.2.3]]); these are used here without repetition. Level C adds the finite rotation operator $e^{-i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\phi/2}$, its half-angles and the resulting sign change under a $2\pi$ rotation, the neutron-interferometry test of that sign, and the group behind it: spin-$\tfrac12$ states carry not $SO(3)$ but $SU(2)$, which covers $SO(3)$ twice. Euler angles give a parametrization of both groups that the rotation matrices of [[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices]] are built on.

*Notation.* As Sakurai, $|\pm\rangle$ are the eigenkets of $S_z$ with eigenvalues $\pm\hbar/2$ (level B: $\chi_\pm$, spin up and spin down), a spinor is $\chi = (\langle+|\alpha\rangle, \langle-|\alpha\rangle)^{\mathsf T} = (c_+, c_-)^{\mathsf T}$, and $\doteq$ means "is represented by" in this basis. $\mathbf S = \frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\sigma$, and $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3$ are $\sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z$. The rotation operator of [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|Principle §C5.1.2]] is $\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi)$, with $\mathscr D_z(\phi) = \mathscr D(\hat z, \phi)$.

## The rotation operator for spin one-half

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.2.1: Rotation Operator for Spin One-Half
> For a spin-$\tfrac12$ system the rotation through $\phi$ about $\hat n$ is
>
> $$
> \mathscr D(\hat n, \phi) = \exp\Bigl(-\frac{i\,\mathbf S\cdot\hat n\,\phi}{\hbar}\Bigr) \doteq \exp\Bigl(-\frac{i\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\,\phi}{2}\Bigr) = 1\cos\frac\phi2 - i\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\,\sin\frac\phi2
> = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\frac\phi2 - in_z\sin\frac\phi2 & (-in_x - n_y)\sin\frac\phi2 \\ (-in_x + n_y)\sin\frac\phi2 & \cos\frac\phi2 + in_z\sin\frac\phi2 \end{pmatrix} . \qquad (3.60)\text{–}(3.63)
> $$
>
> In ket form, about $z$: $\exp(-iS_z\phi/\hbar)|\alpha\rangle = e^{-i\phi/2}|+\rangle\langle+|\alpha\rangle + e^{i\phi/2}|-\rangle\langle-|\alpha\rangle$ (3.32). Spinors transform as $\chi \to \exp(-i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\phi/2)\,\chi$ while the matrices $\sigma_k$ stay fixed (3.64); it is $\chi^\dagger\sigma_k\chi$, not $\sigma_k$, that rotates like a vector,
>
> $$
> \chi^\dagger\sigma_k\chi \;\to\; \sum_l R_{kl}\,\chi^\dagger\sigma_l\chi, \qquad\text{e.g.}\qquad e^{i\sigma_3\phi/2}\,\sigma_1\,e^{-i\sigma_3\phi/2} = \sigma_1\cos\phi - \sigma_2\sin\phi . \qquad (3.65),\ (3.66)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.2.1, eqs. (3.25)–(3.32), and §3.2.5, eqs. (3.60)–(3.66) · the user's 511 notes, §"Spin 1/2 Systems and Finite Rotations" · the user's series, Part IV, §11, eq. (spin-rotation)*

^thm-c5-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\mathbf S = \frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\sigma$ satisfies the commutation relations ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]], part 2), so its exponential is the rotation operator of [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-3|Theorem §C5.1.3]]. By part 3 of the same theorem $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n)^2 = 1$, so $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n)^k$ is $1$ for even and $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n$ for odd $k$ (3.61), and the exponential series splits into the series of $\cos\frac\phi2$ and $\sin\frac\phi2$. With $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n = \begin{pmatrix} n_z & n_x - in_y \\ n_x + in_y & -n_z\end{pmatrix}$ (3.56) this is the matrix shown. In the ket form, $|\pm\rangle$ are eigenkets of $S_z$, so $e^{-iS_z\phi/\hbar}$ multiplies them by $e^{\mp i\phi/2}$. The vector law is [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-5|Theorem §C5.1.5]] with $\mathbf J = \mathbf S$, taken between spinors; (3.66) is its $2\times2$ form for a rotation about $z$.

> [!remark] Remark: The smallest stage, and what level B left open
> Two dimensions is the smallest space on which the commutation relations have a nonzero solution, and, in Sakurai's words, it is "not a priori obvious that nature takes advantage" of it; atomic spectroscopy and magnetic resonance show that it does. Level B used $\mathbf S = \frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\sigma$ as a set of observables; here the same matrices generate the rotations of the spin state, which is what [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices]] (Connections) left to level C. The half-angles in (3.63) come from the factor $\tfrac12$ in $\mathbf S = \frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\sigma$, that is, from what spin $\tfrac12$ is.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.2.2: Spin Up Along a Direction Is a Rotated State
> Let $\hat n$ have polar angle $\beta$ and azimuth $\alpha$. Rotating $|+\rangle$ first about $y$ by $\beta$, then about $z$ by $\alpha$, gives the eigenket of $\mathbf S\cdot\hat n$ with eigenvalue $+\hbar/2$:
>
> $$
> |\mathbf S\cdot\hat n; +\rangle = \mathscr D_z(\alpha)\,\mathscr D_y(\beta)\,|+\rangle \doteq \begin{pmatrix} \cos\frac\beta2\,e^{-i\alpha/2} \\ \sin\frac\beta2\,e^{i\alpha/2} \end{pmatrix} . \qquad (3.70)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.2.5, eqs. (3.68)–(3.70), Fig. 3.3 · the user's 511 notes, §"Eigenspinor of σ·n"*

^thm-c5-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $\mathscr D = \mathscr D_z(\alpha)\mathscr D_y(\beta)$ and $R = R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta)$, which turns $\hat z$ into $\hat n = (\sin\beta\cos\alpha, \sin\beta\sin\alpha, \cos\beta)$. By [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-5|Theorem §C5.1.5]], $\mathscr D^\dagger(\mathbf S\cdot\hat n)\mathscr D = \sum_{i,j}n_iR_{ij}S_j = (R^{\mathsf T}\hat n)\cdot\mathbf S = \hat z\cdot\mathbf S = S_z$. Hence $(\mathbf S\cdot\hat n)\,\mathscr D|+\rangle = \mathscr D S_z|+\rangle = \frac\hbar2\,\mathscr D|+\rangle$. The matrix form is the product of [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]] with $\hat n = \hat y$, then $\hat z$:
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\alpha/2} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\alpha/2} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \cos\frac\beta2 & -\sin\frac\beta2 \\ \sin\frac\beta2 & \cos\frac\beta2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\frac\beta2\,e^{-i\alpha/2} \\ \sin\frac\beta2\,e^{i\alpha/2} \end{pmatrix} .
> $$

*Uses:* [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-5|Theorem §C5.1.5]], [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The same eigenspinor, a new reason, a different phase
> [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]] found the eigenspinor $\chi^{(r)}_+ = (\cos\frac\theta2, e^{i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2)^{\mathsf T}$ by solving the eigenvalue problem of a $2\times2$ matrix. Here it is the spin-up state carried to the direction $\hat n$ by a rotation, and no eigenvalue problem is solved; the two answers differ by the overall phase $e^{-i\alpha/2}$, which has no physical meaning. The phase does tell a story: (3.70) changes sign when $\alpha \to \alpha + 2\pi$, because the rotation that produced it has gone once more around the $z$ axis (Theorem §C5.2.3 below), while level B's choice is single-valued in the azimuth.

## The rotation by 2π

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.2.3: A Rotation by 2π Reverses the Sign of a Spinor
> For spin $\tfrac12$ and every axis $\hat n$,
>
> $$
> \mathscr D(\hat n, 2\pi) \doteq \exp\Bigl(-\frac{i\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\,\phi}{2}\Bigr)\Big|_{\phi = 2\pi} = -1, \qquad |\alpha\rangle_{R(2\pi)} = -|\alpha\rangle, \qquad \mathscr D(\hat n, 4\pi) = +1 . \qquad (3.33),\ (3.67)
> $$
>
> A $720^\circ$ rotation is needed to return the ket to itself. Expectation values are unchanged by the $2\pi$ rotation, since $|\alpha\rangle$ and $\langle\alpha|$ both change sign. In particular $\mathscr D(R(\hat n, 2\pi)) = -\mathscr D(1)$: the group property of [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|Principle §C5.1.2]] holds for spin $\tfrac12$ only up to a sign.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.2.1, eqs. (3.31)–(3.33), and §3.2.5, eq. (3.67) · the user's series, Part IV, §11, eq. (minus-one) · the user's 511 notes, §"The 2π Rotation Problem"*

^thm-c5-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Set $\phi = 2\pi$ in (3.62): $\cos\pi = -1$, $\sin\pi = 0$. For $\phi = 4\pi$, $\cos2\pi = 1$, $\sin2\pi = 0$. Expectation values: ${}_R\langle\alpha|A|\alpha\rangle_R = (-\langle\alpha|)A(-|\alpha\rangle) = \langle\alpha|A|\alpha\rangle$.

> [!remark] Remark: Spin precession is a rotation in time
> Sakurai's §3.2.2 revisits spin precession with the rotation operator: for $H = \omega S_z$ (an electron in $\mathbf B = B\hat z$, $\omega = |e|B/m_ec$ in his Gaussian units, (3.34)–(3.35)) the time-evolution operator is $\mathscr U(t, 0) = \mathscr D_z(\omega t)$ (3.36), so [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-5|Theorem §C5.1.5]] gives the precession of $\langle\mathbf S\rangle$ (3.37) and Theorem §C5.2.3 the state-ket period $\tau_{\text{state ket}} = 4\pi/\omega = 2\tau_{\text{precession}}$ (3.38)–(3.39). The home of these statements is [[§C3.2 Two-State Systems, Spin Precession and Neutrino Oscillations#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]] (parts 1, 3 and 5), with its units caution [[§C3.2 Two-State Systems, Spin Precession and Neutrino Oscillations#^cau-c3-2-1|Caution: Units and signs of the spin moment]]; here they supply the rotation angle $\phi = \omega T$ of the interferometer below.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.2.2, eqs. (3.34)–(3.39)*

^rem-c5-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.2.4: Interference of a Rotated and an Unrotated Beam
> A beam of spin-$\tfrac12$ particles is split into two paths that are recombined; on path $B$ the spin is rotated through $\phi$ about $\hat n$ (for neutrons, by Larmor precession in a field region: $\phi = \omega T$, with $T$ the time spent in the field), on path $A$ it is not. If the two paths arrive with amplitudes $c_1$ and $c_2$, $c_1^*c_2 = |c_1c_2|e^{i\delta}$, the intensity in the recombined beam is
>
> $$
> I \propto |c_1|^2 + |c_2|^2 + 2|c_1c_2|\Bigl[\cos\delta\cos\frac\phi2 + \sin\delta\,\sin\frac\phi2\,\langle\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\rangle\Bigr],
> $$
>
> which for a spin state with $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n = \pm1$ is Sakurai's sinusoid $\cos(\mp\omega T/2 + \delta)$ (3.42). Either way $I$ is periodic in $\phi$ with period $4\pi$, not $2\pi$: the beams interfere destructively at $\phi = 2\pi$ when they interfered constructively at $\phi = 0$ (for $\delta = 0$). For a neutron of speed $v$ crossing a field region of length $l$, $\phi = |\gamma_n|Bl/v$, and successive intensity maxima are separated by
>
> $$
> \Delta B = \frac{4\pi v}{|\gamma_n|\,l} = \frac{4\pi\hbar c}{|g_n|\,e\,\bar\lambda\,l}, \qquad |\gamma_n| = \frac{|g_n|e}{m_pc}, \quad g_n \simeq -1.91, \quad \bar\lambda = \frac{\hbar}{m_nv}, \qquad (3.40)\text{–}(3.43)
> $$
>
> (Gaussian units on the right; $m_n \approx m_p$). If the ket returned to itself after $2\pi$, $\Delta B$ would be half as large.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.2.3, eqs. (3.40)–(3.43), Fig. 3.2 · the user's series, Part IV, §12, eq. (interference-fringe)*

^thm-c5-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At recombination the spatial parts of the two paths coincide, so the state is $c_1|\chi\rangle + c_2\,\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi)|\chi\rangle$ for the incoming spin state $|\chi\rangle$, and its squared norm is $|c_1|^2 + |c_2|^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}\bigl(c_1^*c_2\langle\chi|\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi)|\chi\rangle\bigr)$ ($\mathscr D$ is unitary). By [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]], $\langle\chi|\mathscr D|\chi\rangle = \cos\frac\phi2 - i\sin\frac\phi2\,\langle\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\rangle$ with $\langle\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\rangle$ real, and the real part of $e^{i\delta}$ times this is the bracket. If $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n = \pm1$ it is $\cos\delta\cos\frac\phi2 \pm \sin\delta\sin\frac\phi2 = \cos(\mp\frac\phi2 + \delta)$. The Larmor angle: $\omega = |\gamma_n|B$ (Sakurai's (3.40) with the neutron's moment $g_ne\hbar/2m_pc$ in place of the electron's $e\hbar/2m_ec$) and $T = l/v$. Successive maxima are $\Delta\phi = 4\pi$ apart, so $|\gamma_n|\Delta B\,l/v = 4\pi$; with $v = \hbar/m_n\bar\lambda$ and $m_n \approx m_p$ this is Sakurai's (3.43).

> [!remark] Remark: The experiment
> An overall sign of every ket is unobservable; the interferometer makes the sign *relative*, between a rotated and an unrotated part of one state. Two groups did the experiment with thermal neutrons in silicon single-crystal interferometers (Rauch et al., Phys. Lett. A 54 (1975) 425; Werner et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 35 (1975) 1053, cited by Sakurai), sweeping $B$ at fixed $T$; they confirmed (3.43), the $4\pi$ prediction, "to an accuracy of a fraction of a percent" (Sakurai). For equal amplitudes and $\delta = 0$ the bracket of Theorem §C5.2.4 is $\cos(\phi/2)$ for *every* spin state, polarized or not, so an unpolarized beam shows the full $720^\circ$ fringe pattern. (For equal amplitudes and $\delta = 0$ there is no contrast factor, and the spin state enters only through the term $\sin\delta\,\langle\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\rangle$ when $\delta \ne 0$. Sakurai gives no numerical fringe period; the series quotes $\approx 716^\circ \pm 4^\circ$ without tying it to a run. A value of that precision seems to belong to a later measurement by Rauch's group (1978), but none of the sources here confirms it, so it is not used.)
>

## SO(3) and SU(2)

> [!definition] Definition §C5.2.1: The Groups SO(3), U(2) and SU(2)
> $SO(3)$ is the group of rotations of [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^def-c5-1-1|Def. §C5.1.1]]: real $3\times3$ matrices with $RR^{\mathsf T} = 1$ and $\det R = 1$. The condition $RR^{\mathsf T} = 1$ is $6$ independent equations (the product is symmetric), so a rotation has $9 - 6 = 3$ parameters (3.71). $U(2)$ is the group of unitary $2\times2$ complex matrices, $U^\dagger U = 1$, and the **special unitary group** $SU(2)$ consists of those with $\det U = 1$ (**unimodular**). The **Cayley–Klein parameters** of a matrix of the form
>
> $$
> U(a, b) = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ -b^* & a^* \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1, \qquad (3.77),\ (3.78)
> $$
>
> are the complex numbers $a$, $b$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.3.1–3.3.2, eqs. (3.71)–(3.84) (in the 3rd-edition PDF the displays (3.71)–(3.74) are shifted by one item against the text: (3.71) shows the closure product $(R_1R_2)(R_1R_2)^{\mathsf T} = 1$ instead of $RR^{\mathsf T} = 1$; the user's 511 notes have the intended assignment) · the user's 511 notes, §"SO(3), SU(2), and Euler Rotations"*

^def-c5-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.2.5: The Structure of SU(2)
> 1. Every element of $SU(2)$ is $U(a, b)$ for unique $a, b$ with $|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1$; as a set, $SU(2)$ is the unit sphere $S^3$ of $\mathbb C^2 = \mathbb R^4$. Every element of $U(2)$ is $e^{i\gamma}U(a, b)$ with $\gamma$ real (3.84).
> 2. $U(a_1, b_1)\,U(a_2, b_2) = U(a_1a_2 - b_1b_2^*,\ a_1b_2 + a_2^*b_1)$ and $U(a, b)^{-1} = U(a^*, -b)$ (3.81), (3.83).
> 3. The elements of $SU(2)$ are exactly the spin-$\tfrac12$ rotation matrices: $\exp(-i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\phi/2) = U(a, b)$ with
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Re}a = \cos\frac\phi2, \qquad \operatorname{Im}a = -n_z\sin\frac\phi2, \qquad \operatorname{Re}b = -n_y\sin\frac\phi2, \qquad \operatorname{Im}b = -n_x\sin\frac\phi2, \qquad (3.80)
> $$
>
> and every $U(a, b)$ arises in this way with $0 \le \phi \le 2\pi$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.3.2, eqs. (3.76)–(3.84) · the user's series, Part IV, §12, eq. (su2-param) · the user's 511 notes, §"Unitary Unimodular Group"*

^thm-c5-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* The columns of a unitary matrix are orthonormal ([[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-57|LADR Thm. 7.57]]). If the first column is $(a, c)^{\mathsf T}$ with $|a|^2 + |c|^2 = 1$, the second, being a unit vector orthogonal to it in $\mathbb C^2$, is $\lambda(-c^*, a^*)^{\mathsf T}$ with $|\lambda| = 1$; then $\det U = \lambda(|a|^2 + |c|^2) = \lambda$. So $\det U = 1$ forces $\lambda = 1$, and with $b = -c^*$ the matrix is $U(a, b)$. Conversely $U(a, b)^\dagger U(a, b) = 1$ by multiplication (3.79), and $\det U(a, b) = |a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1$. Writing $a = x_0 + ix_3$, $b = x_2 + ix_1$, the condition is $x_0^2 + x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 = 1$. For $U \in U(2)$, $|\det U| = 1$ ([[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-58|LADR Thm. 9.58]]); write $\det U = e^{2i\gamma}$, then $e^{-i\gamma}U \in SU(2)$.
>
> *2.* Multiply the matrices: the first row of the product is $(a_1a_2 - b_1b_2^*,\ a_1b_2 + b_1a_2^*)$, and the second row has the required form because $SU(2)$ is closed under products. $U(a, b)U(a^*, -b) = 1$ by (3.81).
>
> *3.* By [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]], $\exp(-i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\phi/2)$ has $a = \cos\frac\phi2 - in_z\sin\frac\phi2$, $b = (-in_x - n_y)\sin\frac\phi2$, $-b^* = (-in_x + n_y)\sin\frac\phi2$, $a^* = \cos\frac\phi2 + in_z\sin\frac\phi2$: it is $U(a, b)$ with (3.80). Conversely, given $U(a, b)$, choose $\phi \in [0, 2\pi]$ with $\cos\frac\phi2 = \operatorname{Re}a$; then $(\operatorname{Im}a)^2 + |b|^2 = \sin^2\frac\phi2$, so for $\sin\frac\phi2 \ne 0$ the vector $\hat n = -(\operatorname{Im}b, \operatorname{Re}b, \operatorname{Im}a)/\sin\frac\phi2$ is a unit vector satisfying (3.80); for $\sin\frac\phi2 = 0$, $U = \pm1$ and any $\hat n$ will do.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.2.6: SU(2) Is a Double Cover of SO(3)
> For $U \in SU(2)$ define a linear map $R(U)$ of $\mathbb R^3$ by
>
> $$
> U\,(\mathbf x\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\,U^\dagger = \bigl(R(U)\,\mathbf x\bigr)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma .
> $$
>
> Then
> 1. $R(U) \in SO(3)$, and $R(U_1U_2) = R(U_1)R(U_2)$: $R$ is a group homomorphism $SU(2) \to SO(3)$;
> 2. $R\bigl(\exp(-i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\phi/2)\bigr) = R(\hat n, \phi)$, so $R$ is onto;
> 3. $R(U) = R(U')$ if and only if $U' = \pm U$: the kernel is $\{1, -1\}$, and $SO(3) \cong SU(2)/\{\pm1\}$.
>
> Each rotation corresponds to exactly two elements $\pm U$ of $SU(2)$; the rotation by $2\pi$, which is the identity of $SO(3)$, corresponds to $-1$ when reached continuously from $U = 1$ (Theorem §C5.2.3). The two groups are not isomorphic, but they are **locally isomorphic**: near the identity the correspondence is one-to-one.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.3.2, the paragraph after (3.84) · the user's series, Part IV, §12, eqs. (covering-map), (two-to-one) · the user's 511 notes, §"Relationship Between SO(3) and SU(2)"*

^thm-c5-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *$R(U)$ is a real linear map.* $X = \mathbf x\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ runs over the Hermitian traceless $2\times2$ matrices as $\mathbf x$ runs over $\mathbb R^3$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]], part 4). $UXU^\dagger$ is again Hermitian and has the same trace, so it is $\mathbf x'\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ for a unique real $\mathbf x'$, depending linearly on $\mathbf x$.
>
> *It preserves lengths.* $\det(\mathbf x\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma) = -x_3^2 - (x_1^2 + x_2^2) = -|\mathbf x|^2$, and $\det(UXU^\dagger) = \det X$. So $R(U)$ is orthogonal.
>
> *Homomorphism.* $U_1U_2X(U_1U_2)^\dagger = U_1(U_2XU_2^\dagger)U_1^\dagger$.
>
> *Part 2, and $\det R(U) = 1$.* Let $U_\phi = \exp(-i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\phi/2)$ and $\mathbf x(\phi) = R(U_\phi)\mathbf x$. Differentiating $U_\phi XU_\phi^\dagger$ gives $-\frac i2[\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n, \mathbf x(\phi)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma]$, and $[\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf a, \boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf b] = 2i\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)$ (part 3 of the same theorem) turns this into $(\hat n\times\mathbf x(\phi))\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$. So $\mathbf x' = \hat n\times\mathbf x = A_{\hat n}\mathbf x$ with $\mathbf x(0) = \mathbf x$, whose solution is $R(\hat n, \phi)\mathbf x$ ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-1|Theorem §C5.1.1]]). Every $U$ is some $U_\phi$ ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-5|Theorem §C5.2.5]]), so every $R(U)$ is a rotation, with determinant $+1$. Onto: every $R \in SO(3)$ is some $R(\hat n, \phi)$. Indeed $\det(R - 1) = \det\bigl(R^{\mathsf T}(R - 1)\bigr) = \det(1 - R^{\mathsf T}) = \det(1 - R) = -\det(R - 1)$, so $\det(R - 1) = 0$ and $R$ fixes a unit vector $\hat n$; on the plane perpendicular to $\hat n$ it acts as an orthogonal map of determinant $1$, a rotation of the plane through some $\phi$.
>
> *Part 3.* $R(U) = 1$ means $U\sigma_kU^\dagger = \sigma_k$, i.e. $U$ commutes with $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3$, hence with every $2\times2$ matrix (part 4 of the same theorem), so $U = \lambda1$; $\det U = \lambda^2 = 1$ gives $\lambda = \pm1$. Both signs do lie in the kernel, so the kernel is $\{\pm1\}$, and $R(U) = R(U')$ iff $U^{-1}U'$ is in the kernel. The isomorphism $SU(2)/\{\pm1\} \cong SO(3)$ is the first isomorphism theorem ([[§38 The First Isomorphism Theorem#^thm-38-1|493 Thm. §38.1]]). Finally $U_{\phi + 2\pi} = -U_\phi$ by (3.62): continuing $\phi$ from $0$ to $2\pi$ walks from $1$ to $-1$ while $R(\hat n, \phi)$ returns to $1$. Near $U = 1$ only $U$ itself, not $-U$, is close to $1$, which is the local isomorphism.

> [!remark] Remark: What the two groups mean for states
> A spin-$\tfrac12$ ket is acted on by $SU(2)$: "rotate by $\phi$ about $\hat n$" names an element of $SO(3)$, and the ket needs one of its two preimages $\pm U$. Continuity selects one: following the rotation from the identity, a $2\pi$ turn lands on $-1$. The commutation relations of [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-4|Theorem §C5.1.4]] were derived near the identity, where the two groups agree, so they cannot tell $SO(3)$ from $SU(2)$; that is why the algebra will produce both integer and half-integer angular momenta ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-4|Theorem §C5.3.4]]), the half-integer ones being those that carry the sign. Sakurai: the Cayley–Klein parameters described the motion of gyroscopes long before quantum mechanics.

^rem-c5-2-5

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Why the sign cannot be removed
> Could a cleverer choice of phases make $\mathscr D(\hat n, 2\pi) = +1$ for spin $\tfrac12$? No, and the obstruction is topological. $SU(2) \cong S^3$ is simply connected, and $SO(3) = SU(2)/\{\pm1\}$ is $S^3$ with antipodal points identified, the real projective space $\mathbb{RP}^3$ ([[Real and complex projective spaces]], [[Spheres Sⁿ]]). In $SO(3)$ the loop "rotate by $0$ to $2\pi$ about $\hat n$" cannot be shrunk to a point (its lift runs from $1$ to $-1$, between antipodes), while the $4\pi$ loop can: $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb Z_2$ (the "belt trick" is the tabletop version). A representation may assign this loop a sign, and no continuous redefinition changes a discrete assignment. $SU(2)$ is the universal cover of $SO(3)$; its representations split into those blind to the sign (integer $j$, true representations of $SO(3)$) and those that carry it (half-integer $j$), the dichotomy of [[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-4|Theorem §C5.3.4]].
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part IV, §12 (Remark: Why the sign cannot be redefined away; stated there)*

## Euler rotations

> [!definition] Definition §C5.2.2: Euler Angles
> The **Euler rotation** with angles $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$ turns the system in three steps: by $\alpha$ about the space-fixed $z$ axis; then by $\beta$ about the body $y$ axis, which the first step has carried to $y'$; then by $\gamma$ about the body $z$ axis, which the second step has carried to $z'$:
>
> $$
> R(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) \equiv R_{z'}(\gamma)\,R_{y'}(\beta)\,R_z(\alpha), \qquad 0 \le \alpha < 2\pi, \quad 0 \le \beta \le \pi, \quad 0 \le \gamma < 2\pi . \qquad (3.85)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.3.3, eq. (3.85), Fig. 3.4 · the user's 511 notes, §"Euler Rotations"*

^def-c5-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: The middle rotation is about y
> Most classical-mechanics texts, and this vault's rigid-body chapter ([[§B10.3 The Heavy Symmetric Top#^def-b10-3-1|CM Def. §B10.3.1]]), turn about the body $x$ axis (the line of nodes) in the second step. Quantum mechanics uses $y$, so that the only rotation that mixes different $m$, $e^{-iJ_y\beta/\hbar}$, has real matrix elements ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-2|Theorem §C5.3.2]]). Since $R_x(\theta) = R_z(-\tfrac\pi2)R_y(\theta)R_z(\tfrac\pi2)$, the two sets of angles are related by $\alpha = \varphi - \pi/2$, $\beta = \theta$, $\gamma = \psi + \pi/2$, with $(\varphi, \theta, \psi)$ the angles of CM Def. §B10.3.1.

^cau-c5-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.2.7: Euler Rotations About Fixed Axes
> 1. In terms of rotations about the space-fixed axes,
>
> $$
> R(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = R_z(\alpha)\,R_y(\beta)\,R_z(\gamma) . \qquad (3.89)
> $$
>
> 2. Every rotation has Euler angles in the ranges of Def. §C5.2.2.
> 3. The corresponding rotation operator is $\mathscr D(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = \mathscr D_z(\alpha)\mathscr D_y(\beta)\mathscr D_z(\gamma)$ (3.90); for spin $\tfrac12$,
>
> $$
> \mathscr D^{(1/2)}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i(\alpha + \gamma)/2}\cos\frac\beta2 & -e^{-i(\alpha - \gamma)/2}\sin\frac\beta2 \\ e^{i(\alpha - \gamma)/2}\sin\frac\beta2 & e^{i(\alpha + \gamma)/2}\cos\frac\beta2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathscr D^{(1/2)}_{m'm}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = \bigl\langle\tfrac12, m'\bigr|\,e^{-iJ_z\alpha/\hbar}e^{-iJ_y\beta/\hbar}e^{-iJ_z\gamma/\hbar}\,\bigl|\tfrac12, m\bigr\rangle , \qquad (3.91),\ (3.92)
> $$
>
> the two-dimensional (**$j = \tfrac12$ irreducible**) representation of the rotation. Every element of $SU(2)$ has this form, with $\alpha + \gamma$ ranging over an interval of length $4\pi$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.3.3, eqs. (3.86)–(3.92) · the user's 511 notes, §"Euler Rotations for Spin 1/2" · the user's series, Part IV, §12, eq. (D-half)*

^thm-c5-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *A lemma.* For rotations $Q$ and $R(\hat n, \phi)$, $QR(\hat n, \phi)Q^{-1} = R(Q\hat n, \phi)$: a rotation preserves cross products, $Q(\hat n\times Q^{-1}\mathbf v) = Q\hat n\times\mathbf v$, so $QA_{\hat n}Q^{-1} = A_{Q\hat n}$, and exponentiating gives the claim (Theorem §C5.1.1).
>
> *1.* The body axis $y'$ is $R_z(\alpha)\hat y$, so $R_{y'}(\beta) = R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta)R_z^{-1}(\alpha)$ (3.86); the body axis $z'$ is $R_{y'}(\beta)\hat z$, so $R_{z'}(\gamma) = R_{y'}(\beta)R_z(\gamma)R_{y'}^{-1}(\beta)$ (3.87). Then
>
> $$
> R_{z'}(\gamma)R_{y'}(\beta)R_z(\alpha) = R_{y'}(\beta)R_z(\gamma)R_z(\alpha) = R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta)R_z^{-1}(\alpha)R_z(\gamma)R_z(\alpha) = R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta)R_z(\gamma), \qquad (3.88)
> $$
>
> since rotations about the same axis commute.
>
> *2.* Given $R$, let $\beta \in [0, \pi]$ and $\alpha$ be the polar angle and azimuth of $R\hat z$; then $R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta)\hat z = R\hat z$, so $Q = (R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta))^{-1}R$ is a rotation that fixes $\hat z$. It maps the $xy$ plane to itself with determinant $1$, so $Q = R_z(\gamma)$ for some $\gamma$.
>
> *3.* Apply the group property of [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|Principle §C5.1.2]] to (3.89) and use [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-1|Theorem §C5.2.1]] with $\hat n = \hat z$, $\hat y$: $e^{-i\sigma_3\alpha/2} = \operatorname{diag}(e^{-i\alpha/2}, e^{i\alpha/2})$ and $e^{-i\sigma_2\beta/2} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\frac\beta2 & -\sin\frac\beta2 \\ \sin\frac\beta2 & \cos\frac\beta2\end{pmatrix}$; multiplying the three matrices gives (3.91). It is $U(a, b)$ with $a = e^{-i(\alpha + \gamma)/2}\cos\frac\beta2$, $b = -e^{-i(\alpha - \gamma)/2}\sin\frac\beta2$, and any $(a, b)$ on $S^3$ can be written so: $\beta$ from $|a| = \cos\frac\beta2$, then $\alpha + \gamma$ and $\alpha - \gamma$ from the phases of $a$ and $-b$, each determined modulo $4\pi$. That $J_y$ has purely imaginary matrix elements makes the middle factor real (Sakurai's footnote 4).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C5.2.1: The Field Step in a Neutron Interferometer
> Thermal neutrons of speed $v = 2200\ \mathrm{m/s}$ cross a field region of length $l = 2.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ on one arm of an interferometer. Which field change $\Delta B$ separates successive intensity maxima, and at which field do the arms first cancel (for $\delta = 0$)?
>
> The neutron's gyromagnetic ratio from $g_n \simeq -1.91$ is, in SI units, $|\gamma_n| = |g_n|e/m_p = 1.91\times(1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C})/(1.673\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}) = 1.83\times10^8\ \mathrm{s^{-1}\,T^{-1}}$. The time in the field is $T = l/v = 9.1\ \mu\mathrm s$. By Theorem §C5.2.4,
>
> $$
> \Delta B = \frac{4\pi v}{|\gamma_n|\,l} = \frac{4\pi\times2200\ \mathrm{m/s}}{(1.83\times10^8\ \mathrm{s^{-1}T^{-1}})(0.020\ \mathrm m)} = 7.5\ \mathrm{mT},
> $$
>
> a field of laboratory-bench size that is easily swept. The arms cancel first at $\phi = 2\pi$, i.e. at $B = \Delta B/2 = 3.8\ \mathrm{mT}$, where the spin on arm $B$ has made exactly one full precession; a ket that returned to itself after $2\pi$ would give a maximum there. The de Broglie wavelength is $\lambda = h/m_nv = 1.8\ \text{Å}$, matched to Bragg reflection in silicon, which is what splits and recombines the beam.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part IV, §12, Example (The experiment and its numbers), checked here · Sakurai eqs. (3.40), (3.43)*

> [!example] Example §C5.2.2: One Rotation From Three Euler Rotations
> The Euler rotation $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$ is a single rotation about some axis by some angle $\theta$. Find $\theta$ and the axis.
>
> By [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-5|Theorem §C5.2.5]] the spin-$\tfrac12$ matrix (3.91) equals $\exp(-i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat n\theta/2) = U(a, b)$, and $\operatorname{Re}a = \cos\frac\theta2$. Here $a = e^{-i(\alpha + \gamma)/2}\cos\frac\beta2$, so
>
> $$
> \cos\frac\theta2 = \cos\frac\beta2\,\cos\frac{\alpha + \gamma}2 .
> $$
>
> The axis follows from (3.80) with $b = -e^{-i(\alpha - \gamma)/2}\sin\frac\beta2$:
>
> $$
> \hat n\,\sin\frac\theta2 = \Bigl(-\sin\frac\beta2\sin\frac{\alpha - \gamma}2,\ \ \sin\frac\beta2\cos\frac{\alpha - \gamma}2,\ \ \cos\frac\beta2\sin\frac{\alpha + \gamma}2\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Checks:* the squared length of the right side is $1 - \cos^2\frac\beta2\cos^2\frac{\alpha+\gamma}2 = \sin^2\frac\theta2$, as it must be. For $\beta = 0$ the three rotations are about $z$ and $\theta = \alpha + \gamma$ about $\hat z$; for $\alpha = \gamma = 0$, $\theta = \beta$ about $\hat y$. (A numerical check of the matrix identity for $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = (0.4, 1.0, -0.7)$ gives $\theta = 1.040$.) The sign of $\cos\frac\theta2$ depends on which of the two elements $\pm U$ represents the rotation (Theorem §C5.2.6); the rotation angle in $[0, \pi]$ is the one with $|\cos\frac\theta2|$. The trace, $\operatorname{tr}\mathscr D^{(1/2)} = 2\cos\frac\theta2$, is the quickest route: the rotation angle can be read off any representation matrix from its trace.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 3.10; solved here*
