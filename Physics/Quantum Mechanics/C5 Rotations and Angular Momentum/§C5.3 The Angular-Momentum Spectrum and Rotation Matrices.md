On which spaces can rotations act, and how does a finite rotation mix the states of one such space? The first question was answered at level B by ladder operators: the eigenvalues of $J^2$ and $J_z$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]]), the matrix elements of $J_\pm$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-5|Theorem §B5.2.5]]) and the matrices for any spin ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-7|Theorem §B6.1.7]]); Sakurai's derivation is the same argument, so those results are linked, not repeated. What changes is their reach: since the commutation relations are now a theorem about the generators of rotations of any system ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-4|Theorem §C5.1.4]]), the catalogue of multiplets is the catalogue of all ways a system can respond to rotations. New at level C are the finite rotations inside a multiplet, the Wigner functions $\mathscr D^{(j)}_{m'm}$ and the reduced matrices $d^{(j)}_{m'm}(\beta)$, their properties, and the sign $(-1)^{2j}$ of a $2\pi$ rotation that sorts the multiplets into those of $SO(3)$ and those of $SU(2)$ only.

*Notation.* $|j, m\rangle$ are orthonormal common eigenkets of $\mathbf J^2$ and $J_z$ forming one ladder (a **multiplet**), with the phases fixed by positive $J_\pm$ coefficients (the Condon–Shortley convention, as at level B). Other quantum numbers, if present, are suppressed: rotations do not act on them. Euler angles $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$ are those of [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^def-c5-2-2|Def. §C5.2.2]].

## The spectrum, as at level B

> [!remark] Remark: Eigenvalues and ladder matrix elements
> For any angular momentum, the common eigenkets of $\mathbf J^2 = J_x^2 + J_y^2 + J_z^2$ and $J_z$ satisfy
>
> $$
> \mathbf J^2|j, m\rangle = j(j+1)\hbar^2|j, m\rangle, \qquad J_z|j, m\rangle = m\hbar|j, m\rangle, \qquad j \in \{0, \tfrac12, 1, \tfrac32, \dots\}, \quad m = -j, -j+1, \dots, j, \qquad (3.185),\ (3.186)
> $$
>
> $$
> \langle j', m'|J_\pm|j, m\rangle = \sqrt{(j \mp m)(j \pm m + 1)}\;\hbar\;\delta_{j'j}\,\delta_{m', m\pm1} . \qquad (3.193)
> $$
>
> These are [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]] and [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-5|Theorem §B5.2.5]], and Sakurai's derivation (3.153)–(3.193) is theirs in different bookkeeping: he bounds $b^2 \le a$ by writing $\mathbf J^2 - J_z^2 = \frac12(J_+J_+^\dagger + J_+^\dagger J_+)$, finds $a = b_{\max}(b_{\max} + \hbar) = b_{\min}(b_{\min} - \hbar)$, hence $b_{\min} = -b_{\max}$, and $b_{\max} - b_{\min} = n\hbar$. PHY 511, HW 9 (Sakurai Problem 3.19: prove $\mathbf J^2 = J_z^2 + J_+J_- - \hbar J_z$ and derive the coefficient $c_-$ in $J_-|j, m\rangle = c_-|j, m - 1\rangle$) is [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^der-b5-2-5|Derivation §B5.2.5]]. Sakurai's emphasis is the level-C point: "the quantization of angular momentum … is a direct consequence of the angular-momentum commutation relations, which, in turn, follow from the properties of rotations together with the definition of $J_k$ as the generator of rotation."
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.5.1–3.5.3, eqs. (3.153)–(3.193) · the user's 511 notes, §"Eigenvalues and Eigenstates of Angular Momentum" · the user's series, Part IV, §13*

> [!remark] Remark: Three readings of the catalogue
> The user's series reads the result three ways. *The halves are half the answer:* nothing was assumed but the commutators, and the algebra delivers integer and half-integer $j$ on an equal footing; [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations]] has already identified the half-integers as the multiplets that ride the double cover (Theorem §C5.3.4 below), and $j = \tfrac12$ reproduces the Pauli matrices from nothing. *Same engine, different termination:* the oscillator's norm constraint bounds its ladder on one side only, giving one infinite tower ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum]], [[§C3.4 The Harmonic Oscillator and Coherent States Revisited]]); here $\lVert J_\pm|j, m\rangle\rVert^2 = \hbar^2[j(j+1) - m(m\pm1)] \ge 0$ squeezes from both sides, giving one finite tower per $j$, the finite dimensions that [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^rem-c5-1-5|Remark: A non-Abelian group, and room for finite dimensions]] allowed. *The catalogue is complete:* every irreducible finite-dimensional representation of the algebra is one of these towers (stated in the series; Theorem §C5.3.1 below proves the converse, that each tower is irreducible).

## Rotation matrices

> [!definition] Definition §C5.3.1: Wigner Functions; Reduced Rotation Matrices
> The **Wigner functions** of a rotation $R$ (about $\hat n$ through $\phi$) are the matrix elements of its rotation operator within a multiplet,
>
> $$
> \mathscr D^{(j)}_{m'm}(R) = \langle j, m'|\exp\Bigl(-\frac{i\,\mathbf J\cdot\hat n\,\phi}{\hbar}\Bigr)|j, m\rangle , \qquad (3.194)
> $$
>
> and the **reduced rotation matrix** (Wigner's $d$-function) is that of a rotation about $y$,
>
> $$
> d^{(j)}_{m'm}(\beta) = \langle j, m'|\exp\Bigl(-\frac{iJ_y\beta}{\hbar}\Bigr)|j, m\rangle . \qquad (3.203)
> $$
>
> The $(2j+1)\times(2j+1)$ matrix $\mathscr D^{(j)}(R)$ is the **$(2j+1)$-dimensional irreducible representation** of the rotation.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.5.4, eqs. (3.194), (3.203) · the user's 511 notes, §"Representations of the Rotation Operator" · the user's series, Part IV, §12 (Finite rotations tabulated)*

^def-c5-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.3.1: Rotations Act Within a Multiplet, Irreducibly
> 1. **Rotations do not change $j$:** $\mathbf J^2\mathscr D(R)|j, m\rangle = j(j+1)\hbar^2\,\mathscr D(R)|j, m\rangle$, and
>
> $$
> \mathscr D(R)|j, m\rangle = \sum_{m'}|j, m'\rangle\,\mathscr D^{(j)}_{m'm}(R) : \qquad (3.195),\ (3.201)
> $$
>
> $\mathscr D^{(j)}_{m'm}(R)$ is the amplitude for the rotated state to be found in $|j, m'\rangle$. On a space spanned by several multiplets the matrix of any $\mathscr D(R)$ is block diagonal, one block $\mathscr D^{(j)}(R)$ per multiplet (3.196).
> 2. **Group and unitarity:** $\sum_{m'}\mathscr D^{(j)}_{m''m'}(R_1)\,\mathscr D^{(j)}_{m'm}(R_2) = \mathscr D^{(j)}_{m''m}(R_1R_2)$ (3.198) and $\mathscr D^{(j)}_{m'm}(R^{-1}) = \mathscr D^{(j)\ast}_{mm'}(R)$ (3.199). For half-integer $j$ the product rule holds up to the sign of [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-3|Theorem §C5.2.3]].
> 3. **Irreducibility:** no subspace of a multiplet other than $\{0\}$ and the multiplet itself is mapped into itself by every $\mathscr D(R)$; a block cannot be split further by any choice of basis (3.197).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.5.4, eqs. (3.195)–(3.201) · the user's 511 notes, §"Representations of the Rotation Operator"*

^thm-c5-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\mathbf J^2$ commutes with every $J_k$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]], whose proof used only the commutators), hence with every function of $\mathbf J\cdot\hat n$, in particular with $\mathscr D(R)$ (3.195). More precisely, the span of one ladder is mapped into itself by $J_z$ and $J_\pm$ (3.193), hence by $J_x$, $J_y$, every $\mathbf J\cdot\hat n$ and its exponential $\mathscr D(R)$; so $\mathscr D(R)|j, m\rangle$ is a combination of the $|j, m'\rangle$ of the same ladder, and the completeness relation within the ladder, $\sum_{m'}|j, m'\rangle\langle j, m'| = 1$ there, gives (3.201). A space spanned by several ladders is the direct sum of these invariant subspaces, so the matrix of $\mathscr D(R)$ is block diagonal.
>
> *2.* Take matrix elements of $\mathscr D(R_1)\mathscr D(R_2) = \mathscr D(R_1R_2)$ ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|Principle §C5.1.2]]) and insert the completeness relation. $\mathscr D(R^{-1}) = \mathscr D(R)^{-1} = \mathscr D(R)^\dagger$, and $\langle j, m'|\mathscr D(R)^\dagger|j, m\rangle = \langle j, m|\mathscr D(R)|j, m'\rangle^{\ast}$.
>
> *3.* Let $W \ne \{0\}$ be a subspace of the multiplet with $\mathscr D(R)W \subseteq W$ for all $R$. Differentiating $\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi)w$ at $\phi = 0$ shows that $W$ is mapped into itself by every $J_k$, hence by $J_z$ and $J_\pm$. $J_z$ restricted to $W$ is Hermitian, so $W$ contains an eigenvector of $J_z$, i.e. some $|j, m\rangle$. Applying $J_+$ and $J_-$ repeatedly gives all $|j, m'\rangle$, since the coefficients $\sqrt{(j \mp m)(j \pm m + 1)}$ vanish only at the ends of the ladder. So $W$ is the whole multiplet.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.3.2: Euler-Angle Form and Symmetries of the Rotation Matrices
> 1. With Euler angles,
>
> $$
> \mathscr D^{(j)}_{m'm}(\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = \langle j, m'|e^{-iJ_z\alpha/\hbar}e^{-iJ_y\beta/\hbar}e^{-iJ_z\gamma/\hbar}|j, m\rangle = e^{-i(m'\alpha + m\gamma)}\,d^{(j)}_{m'm}(\beta) : \qquad (3.202)
> $$
>
> only the middle rotation mixes different $m$.
> 2. $d^{(j)}(\beta)$ is a real orthogonal matrix, $d^{(j)}_{m'm}(0) = \delta_{m'm}$, and $d^{(j)}(-\beta) = d^{(j)}(\beta)^{\mathsf T} = d^{(j)}(\beta)^{-1}$.
> 3. $d^{(j)}_{m'm}(\beta) = (-1)^{m - m'}\,d^{(j)}_{mm'}(\beta) = d^{(j)}_{-m,-m'}(\beta)$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.5.4, eqs. (3.202)–(3.203) and footnote 4 · PDG, Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, spherical harmonics, and d functions (the symmetry relations) · the user's 511 notes, §"Euler Angle Representation"*

^thm-c5-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* The outer factors act on $J_z$ eigenkets: $e^{-iJ_z\gamma/\hbar}|j, m\rangle = e^{-im\gamma}|j, m\rangle$ and $\langle j, m'|e^{-iJ_z\alpha/\hbar} = e^{-im'\alpha}\langle j, m'|$.
>
> *2.* In the basis $|j, m\rangle$, $J_\pm$ have real entries (3.193), so $-iJ_y/\hbar = -(J_+ - J_-)/2\hbar$ is a real antisymmetric matrix $K$. Its exponential $d(\beta) = e^{\beta K}$ is real, with $d(\beta)^{\mathsf T} = e^{-\beta K} = d(-\beta) = d(\beta)^{-1}$.
>
> *3.* Let $P = \operatorname{diag}\bigl((-1)^{j - m}\bigr)$. Since $J_\pm$ change $m$ by one, $PKP = -K$, so $P\,d(\beta)\,P = d(-\beta) = d(\beta)^{\mathsf T}$; entrywise $(-1)^{2j - m - m'}d_{m'm} = d_{mm'}$, and $(-1)^{2j - m - m'} = (-1)^{2(j - m)}(-1)^{m - m'} = (-1)^{m - m'}$ because $j - m$ is an integer. Next let $Q$ be the reversal $|j, m\rangle \mapsto |j, -m\rangle$. The coefficient of $J_+$ at $m$ equals that of $J_-$ at $-m$, both $\sqrt{(j - m)(j + m + 1)}$, so $QJ_+Q = J_-$, $QKQ = -K$, and $Q\,d(\beta)\,Q = d(\beta)^{\mathsf T}$: $d_{-m', -m} = d_{mm'}$, which is the second relation after renaming.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.3.3: The Reduced Matrices for j = 1/2 and j = 1
> In the bases ordered by decreasing $m$,
>
> $$
> d^{(1/2)}(\beta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\frac\beta2 & -\sin\frac\beta2 \\ \sin\frac\beta2 & \cos\frac\beta2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad (3.204)
> $$
>
> and for $j = 1$, where $(J_y/\hbar)^3 = J_y/\hbar$ so that $e^{-iJ_y\beta/\hbar} = 1 - (J_y/\hbar)^2(1 - \cos\beta) - i(J_y/\hbar)\sin\beta$ (3.207)–(3.208),
>
> $$
> d^{(1)}(\beta) = \begin{pmatrix} \frac12(1 + \cos\beta) & -\frac1{\sqrt2}\sin\beta & \frac12(1 - \cos\beta) \\ \frac1{\sqrt2}\sin\beta & \cos\beta & -\frac1{\sqrt2}\sin\beta \\ \frac12(1 - \cos\beta) & \frac1{\sqrt2}\sin\beta & \frac12(1 + \cos\beta) \end{pmatrix} . \qquad (3.209)
> $$
>
> The PDG table lists the independent $d^{(j)}_{m'm}$ for $j \le 2$; the others follow from Theorem §C5.3.2.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.5.4, eqs. (3.204)–(3.209), and Problem 3.38 · PDG, d functions · the user's 511 notes, §"Examples" (of §"Representations of the Rotation Operator")*

^thm-c5-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *$j = \tfrac12$:* the middle factor of (3.91), [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-7|Theorem §C5.2.7]].
>
> *$j = 1$:* from $J_y = (J_+ - J_-)/2i$ (3.205) and (3.193), whose nonzero coefficients for $j = 1$ are all $\sqrt2\,\hbar$,
>
> $$
> \frac{J_y}\hbar = \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i & 0 \\ i & 0 & -i \\ 0 & i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{J_y}\hbar\Bigr)^2 = \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \qquad (3.206)
> $$
>
> $J_y/\hbar$ is Hermitian with eigenvalues $1, 0, -1$ (like $J_z/\hbar$; Theorem §C5.3.4 below), so $(J_y/\hbar)\bigl((J_y/\hbar)^2 - 1\bigr) = 0$, which is (3.207). In the exponential series the odd powers are all $J_y/\hbar$ and the even powers from the second on are all $(J_y/\hbar)^2$; summing gives (3.208). Then $-i(J_y/\hbar)\sin\beta = \frac{\sin\beta}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, and adding $1 - (1 - \cos\beta)(J_y/\hbar)^2$ gives (3.209). For $j \ge \tfrac32$ the powers of $J_y$ no longer close after the square, and Sakurai notes that this method "becomes time consuming".

## The rotation by 2π, for any j

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.3.4: Integer and Half-Integer Multiplets
> In a multiplet of angular momentum $j$, a rotation by $2\pi$ about any axis is
>
> $$
> \mathscr D(\hat n, 2\pi)\,|j, m\rangle = (-1)^{2j}\,|j, m\rangle .
> $$
>
> For integer $j$, $\mathscr D^{(j)}(R)$ is a single-valued function of the rotation: a representation of $SO(3)$. For half-integer $j$, $\mathscr D^{(j)}(R(\hat n, \phi + 2\pi)) = -\mathscr D^{(j)}(R(\hat n, \phi))$: a representation of $SU(2)$ that is double-valued on $SO(3)$. In particular a state cannot superpose integer and half-integer $j$ and still be turned into itself, up to a phase, by a $2\pi$ rotation.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.2.1 and §3.6.2, eqs. (3.33), (3.249) (the cases $j = \frac12$ and orbital $\ell$) · the user's series, Part IV, §12 (Remark: Why the sign cannot be redefined away) and §13 (The halves are half the answer)*

^thm-c5-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Choose a rotation $Q$ with $Q\hat z = \hat n$. By [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-5|Theorem §C5.1.5]], $\mathscr D(Q)^\dagger J_i\mathscr D(Q) = \sum_kQ_{ik}J_k$, so $\mathscr D(Q)^\dagger(\hat n\cdot\mathbf J)\mathscr D(Q) = (Q^{\mathsf T}\hat n)\cdot\mathbf J = J_z$. Thus $\hat n\cdot\mathbf J$ is unitarily equivalent to $J_z$ on the multiplet ($\mathscr D(Q)$ maps the multiplet to itself by Theorem §C5.3.1): it has the eigenvalues $m\hbar$, $m = -j, \dots, j$, with eigenkets $\mathscr D(Q)|j, m\rangle$. On each of these, $e^{-2\pi i\,\hat n\cdot\mathbf J/\hbar}$ is $e^{-2\pi im} = (-1)^{2m} = (-1)^{2j}$, since $j - m$ is an integer; the eigenkets span the multiplet, so $\mathscr D(\hat n, 2\pi) = (-1)^{2j}$ there. Then $\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi + 2\pi) = \mathscr D(\hat n, \phi)\mathscr D(\hat n, 2\pi)$ gives the last statements. For a superposition $a|j_1, m_1\rangle + b|j_2, m_2\rangle$ with $2j_1$ even and $2j_2$ odd, the $2\pi$ rotation gives $a|j_1, m_1\rangle - b|j_2, m_2\rangle$, not a multiple of the original if $ab \ne 0$.

^der-c5-3-4

*Uses:* [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-5|Theorem §C5.1.5]], [[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-1|Theorem §C5.3.1]], [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-3|Theorem §C5.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Which multiplets occur is not decided by the algebra
> The commutation relations allow every $j$; the system decides which occur. For spin $\tfrac12$ the space is two-dimensional and $j = \tfrac12$ ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations]]); for a particle without spin the rotations act on wave functions, a $2\pi$ rotation of a wave function changes nothing, and Theorem §C5.3.4 then excludes half-integer $j$ ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-3|Theorem §C5.4.3]]). The vector representation of [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^ex-c5-1-2|Example §C5.1.2]] is $j = 1$, as Example §C5.3.2 below makes explicit.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C5.3.1: A Slightly Tilted Top Rung
> An eigenstate $|j, m = j\rangle$ is rotated by an infinitesimal angle $\varepsilon$ about the $y$ axis. Without the explicit form of $d^{(j)}$, find to order $\varepsilon^2$ the probability that the rotated state is still found in the original state.
>
> Following the hint, write $J_y = (J_+ - J_-)/2i$. On the top rung $J_+|j, j\rangle = 0$ and $J_-|j, j\rangle = \sqrt{2j}\,\hbar|j, j-1\rangle$ by (3.193), so
>
> $$
> J_y|j, j\rangle = \frac{i\hbar}{2}\sqrt{2j}\,|j, j-1\rangle, \qquad \langle j, j|J_y|j, j\rangle = 0, \qquad \langle j, j|J_y^2|j, j\rangle = \lVert J_y|j, j\rangle\rVert^2 = \frac{j\hbar^2}{2} .
> $$
>
> Expanding $e^{-iJ_y\varepsilon/\hbar} = 1 - iJ_y\varepsilon/\hbar - J_y^2\varepsilon^2/2\hbar^2 + O(\varepsilon^3)$, the amplitude to remain is $d^{(j)}_{jj}(\varepsilon) = 1 - \frac j4\varepsilon^2 + O(\varepsilon^3)$, real by Theorem §C5.3.2, and
>
> $$
> P = \bigl|d^{(j)}_{jj}(\varepsilon)\bigr|^2 = 1 - \frac j2\,\varepsilon^2 + O(\varepsilon^3) .
> $$
>
> *Check by the complement:* to first order the only other state reached is $|j, j-1\rangle$, with amplitude $-\frac{i\varepsilon}\hbar\langle j, j-1|J_y|j, j\rangle = \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sqrt{2j}$ and probability $\frac j2\varepsilon^2$; the two add to one. For $j = \tfrac12$ and $1$ the exact elements $\cos^2\frac\varepsilon2$ and $\bigl(\frac{1 + \cos\varepsilon}2\bigr)^2$ (Theorem §C5.3.3) expand to $1 - \varepsilon^2/4$ and $1 - \varepsilon^2/2$; in general $d^{(j)}_{jj}(\beta) = \cos^{2j}\frac\beta2$ (PDG, for $j \le 2$; checked numerically here for $j \le \tfrac52$). The larger $j$, the faster the top rung is depleted: the transverse spread $\langle J_x^2 + J_y^2\rangle = j\hbar^2$ grows with $j$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-5|Theorem §B5.2.5]]), while the relative spread $\sqrt{j}/j$ shrinks toward the classical limit.
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 9 (Sakurai Problem 3.17); solved here*

> [!example] Example §C5.3.2: Spherical Components Rotate with the j = 1 Matrix
> For a vector $\mathbf V$ define the spherical components
>
> $$
> V_{\pm1} = \mp\frac{V_x \pm iV_y}{\sqrt2}, \qquad V_0 = V_z .
> $$
>
> Using $d^{(1)}$, evaluate $\sum_{q'}d^{(1)}_{qq'}(\beta)V_{q'}$ and show that the result is what the rotation of $\mathbf V$ about the $y$ axis requires.
>
> With (3.209):
>
> $$
> \sum_{q'}d^{(1)}_{0q'}V_{q'} = \frac{\sin\beta}{\sqrt2}V_{+1} + \cos\beta\,V_0 - \frac{\sin\beta}{\sqrt2}V_{-1} = -\sin\beta\,V_x + \cos\beta\,V_z ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \sum_{q'}d^{(1)}_{+1,q'}V_{q'} = \frac{1 + \cos\beta}2V_{+1} - \frac{\sin\beta}{\sqrt2}V_0 + \frac{1 - \cos\beta}2V_{-1} = -\frac1{\sqrt2}\bigl[(\cos\beta\,V_x + \sin\beta\,V_z) + iV_y\bigr],
> $$
>
> and $q = -1$ follows from Theorem §C5.3.2 (or by the same arithmetic): $\frac1{\sqrt2}[(\cos\beta\,V_x + \sin\beta\,V_z) - iV_y]$. The rotated vector $\mathbf V' = R_y(\beta)\mathbf V$ has $V_x' = \cos\beta\,V_x + \sin\beta\,V_z$, $V_y' = V_y$, $V_z' = -\sin\beta\,V_x + \cos\beta\,V_z$ ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^def-c5-1-1|Def. §C5.1.1]]), so in every case
>
> $$
> \sum_{q'}d^{(1)}_{qq'}(\beta)\,V_{q'} = V'_q, \qquad\text{the spherical components of } R_y(\beta)\mathbf V .
> $$
>
> A rotation about $z$ multiplies $V_x \pm iV_y$, hence $V_{\pm1}$, by $e^{\pm i\alpha}$, which is $\mathscr D^{(1)*}_{qq}(\alpha, 0, 0) = e^{iq\alpha}$. Together with the result for $y$ and the Euler form (Theorem §C5.3.2), for every rotation
>
> $$
> (R\mathbf V)_q = \sum_{q'}\mathscr D^{(1)*}_{qq'}(R)\,V_{q'},
> $$
>
> the complex conjugate being invisible for rotations about $y$, where $d^{(1)}$ is real. Equivalently, with $V_q = \hat e_q\cdot\mathbf V$ and the basis $\hat e_{\pm1} = \mp(\hat x \pm i\hat y)/\sqrt2$, $\hat e_0 = \hat z$ of [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^ex-c5-1-2|Example §C5.1.2]], the basis vectors transform exactly as the kets $|1, q\rangle$: $R\hat e_q = \sum_{q'}\hat e_{q'}\mathscr D^{(1)}_{q'q}(R)$ (check: $R_z(\alpha)\hat e_{+1} = e^{-i\alpha}\hat e_{+1}$). The $j = 1$ representation *is* the rotation of ordinary vectors, written in this basis. The signs $\mp$ in $V_{\pm1}$ are the Condon–Shortley phases; without them the off-diagonal signs of $d^{(1)}$ would not match. When the $V_q$ are operators, the same transformation law, $\mathscr D^\dagger(R)V_q\mathscr D(R) = \sum_{q'}\mathscr D^{(1)\ast}_{qq'}(R)V_{q'}$, defines a spherical tensor of rank 1 ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 10 (Sakurai Problem 3.42); solved here, for a vector of numbers*

> [!remark] Remark: Level B's spin-1 measurement as a rotation
> [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^ex-b6-1-2|Example §B6.1.2]] diagonalized $\mathsf S_x$ for spin 1 to find that $|1, 1\rangle$ gives $S_x = \hbar, 0, -\hbar$ with probabilities $\tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac14$. With rotation matrices no diagonalization is needed. $R_y(\pi/2)$ turns $\hat z$ into $\hat x$, so (as in the derivation of Theorem §C5.3.4) the eigenkets of $S_x$ are $\mathscr D_y(\pi/2)|1, m'\rangle$, and the amplitudes are $\langle 1, 1|\mathscr D_y(\pi/2)|1, m'\rangle = d^{(1)}_{1m'}(\pi/2) = \tfrac12, -\tfrac1{\sqrt2}, \tfrac12$: the same probabilities, read off the first row of (3.209). More generally, the probability that a measurement of $\mathbf J\cdot\hat n$ in $|j, m\rangle$ gives $m'\hbar$, for $\hat n$ at polar angle $\beta$, is $|d^{(j)}_{mm'}(\beta)|^2$.
