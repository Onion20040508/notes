Why do orbital angular momentum, spin and every sum of them obey one and the same algebra, $[J_i, J_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$? Level B derived these relations for $\mathbf L = \hat{\mathbf r}\times\hat{\mathbf p}$ from the canonical commutators ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]]), then made them the definition of angular momentum in general ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^def-b5-2-2|Def. §B5.2.2]]) and postulated them for spin ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]); classically $L_z$ generates rotations through the Poisson bracket ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-5|CM Theorem §B8.1.5]], [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-6|CM Theorem §B8.1.6]]). At level C angular momentum is *defined* as the generator of rotations of state kets, as momentum generates translations ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator]]) and the Hamiltonian generates time evolution ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation]]). The commutation relations then become a theorem about the rotation group: they record, at second order, how rotations about different axes fail to commute.

*Notation.* Rotations are **active**: the physical system is turned, the coordinate axes stay fixed. $R(\hat n, \phi)$ is the rotation through $\phi$ about the unit vector $\hat n$, $R_x$, $R_y$, $R_z$ those about the coordinate axes. $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is the Levi-Civita symbol ($\epsilon_{xyz} = 1$), repeated indices are summed. Kets are written $|\alpha\rangle$, the ket of the rotated system $|\alpha\rangle_R$, and the rotation operator $\mathscr D(R)$ (Sakurai's notation; $\mathscr D$ for *Drehung*). $\mathbf J$ denotes angular momentum of any kind; $\mathbf L$ and $\mathbf S$ are reserved for orbital angular momentum and spin. $\hbar$ is explicit throughout.

## Rotations of three-dimensional space

> [!definition] Definition §C5.1.1: Rotation; the Group SO(3)
> A **rotation** is a linear map of $\mathbb R^3$ that preserves lengths and orientation. In a right-handed Cartesian basis it is a real $3\times3$ matrix $R$ acting on the components of a vector,
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix} V_x' \\ V_y' \\ V_z' \end{pmatrix} = R\begin{pmatrix} V_x \\ V_y \\ V_z \end{pmatrix}, \qquad R^{\mathsf T}R = RR^{\mathsf T} = 1, \qquad \det R = +1 . \qquad (3.1)
> $$
>
> The rotations form a group under matrix multiplication, the **special orthogonal group** $SO(3)$. The rotation $R(\hat n, \phi)$ is counterclockwise for $\phi > 0$ as seen from the tip of $\hat n$ (a right-handed screw turned by $\phi$ advances along $\hat n$). About the coordinate axes,
>
> $$
> R_z(\phi) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi & -\sin\phi & 0 \\ \sin\phi & \cos\phi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
> R_x(\phi) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\phi & -\sin\phi \\ 0 & \sin\phi & \cos\phi \end{pmatrix}, \quad
> R_y(\phi) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi & 0 & \sin\phi \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\sin\phi & 0 & \cos\phi \end{pmatrix} ; \qquad (3.3)\ \text{and cyclically}
> $$
>
> $R_x$ and $R_y$ follow from $R_z$ by the cyclic relabelling $x \to y \to z \to x$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.1.1, eqs. (3.1)–(3.5), and §3.3.1 · the user's 511 notes, §"Rotations and Angular Momentum Commutation Relations"*

^def-c5-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Active and passive rotations
> If instead the system is held fixed and the coordinate axes are turned, the same matrix $R_z(\phi)$ with $\phi > 0$ describes a *clockwise* turn of the axes, and the components transform with $R^{-1} = R^{\mathsf T}$. Both conventions are in use (Sakurai §3.1.1 calls them active and passive); all formulas in this chapter are active, and mixing the two flips the sign of every angle. The Euler angles of classical mechanics ([[§B10.3 The Heavy Symmetric Top#^def-b10-3-1|CM Def. §B10.3.1]]) also differ in the axis of the middle rotation; see [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations]].

^cau-c5-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.1.1: Infinitesimal Rotations and Their Commutator
> 1. **Generators.** For each unit vector $\hat n$ the rotations about $\hat n$ form a one-parameter group, $R(\hat n, \phi)R(\hat n, \psi) = R(\hat n, \phi + \psi)$, and
>
> $$
> R(\hat n, \phi) = \exp(\phi A_{\hat n}), \qquad A_{\hat n}\mathbf V = \hat n\times\mathbf V, \qquad (A_{\hat n})_{ij} = -\epsilon_{kij}\,n_k .
> $$
>
> Write $A_k$ for $A_{\hat e_k}$; for example $R_z(\varepsilon) = 1 + \varepsilon A_z + \tfrac12\varepsilon^2A_z^2 + O(\varepsilon^3)$ is the matrix (3.4) with entries $1 - \varepsilon^2/2$, $\mp\varepsilon$.
> 2. **Their algebra.** $[A_i, A_j] = \epsilon_{ijk}A_k$.
> 3. **Rotations fail to commute at second order.** To order $\varepsilon^2$,
>
> $$
> R_x(\varepsilon)R_y(\varepsilon) - R_y(\varepsilon)R_x(\varepsilon) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\varepsilon^2 & 0 \\ \varepsilon^2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = R_z(\varepsilon^2) - 1, \qquad (3.7)
> $$
>
> or in group form, $R_x(\varepsilon)R_y(\varepsilon)R_x(-\varepsilon)R_y(-\varepsilon) = R_z(\varepsilon^2) + O(\varepsilon^3)$, and cyclically. Infinitesimal rotations about different axes commute to first order; at second order the failure is a rotation about the third axis.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.1.1, eqs. (3.4)–(3.9) · the user's series, Part IV, §11, eq. (rotation commutator) · the user's 511 notes, §"Finite Versus Infinitesimal Rotations"*

^thm-c5-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Turning through $\psi$ and then through $\phi$ about the same axis is turning through $\phi + \psi$. A differentiable one-parameter group of matrices satisfies $R(\phi + \varepsilon) = R(\varepsilon)R(\phi)$, hence $dR/d\phi = R'(0)R$ with $R(0) = 1$, whose solution is $R(\phi) = \exp(\phi R'(0))$. To find $R'(0)$, split $\mathbf V = (\hat n\cdot\mathbf V)\hat n + \mathbf V_\perp$. The parallel part is fixed and the perpendicular part turns in its plane: $R(\hat n, \phi)\mathbf V = (\hat n\cdot\mathbf V)\hat n + \cos\phi\,\mathbf V_\perp + \sin\phi\,\hat n\times\mathbf V_\perp$. At $\phi = 0$ the derivative is $\hat n\times\mathbf V_\perp = \hat n\times\mathbf V$, whose $i$-th component is $\epsilon_{ikj}n_kV_j = -\epsilon_{kij}n_kV_j$. For $\hat n = \hat z$, $A_z = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $A_z^2 = -\operatorname{diag}(1, 1, 0)$, which gives (3.4).
>
> *2.* For every $\mathbf V$, $[A_i, A_j]\mathbf V = \hat e_i\times(\hat e_j\times\mathbf V) - \hat e_j\times(\hat e_i\times\mathbf V) = (\hat e_i\times\hat e_j)\times\mathbf V$ (the Jacobi identity of the cross product, from $\mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c) = \mathbf b(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c) - \mathbf c(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)$), and $\hat e_i\times\hat e_j = \epsilon_{ijk}\hat e_k$.
>
> *3.* With $R_i(\pm\varepsilon) = 1 \pm \varepsilon A_i + \tfrac12\varepsilon^2A_i^2 + O(\varepsilon^3)$, the first-order and the squared terms of $R_xR_y$ and $R_yR_x$ agree, so $R_x(\varepsilon)R_y(\varepsilon) - R_y(\varepsilon)R_x(\varepsilon) = \varepsilon^2[A_x, A_y] + O(\varepsilon^3) = \varepsilon^2A_z$, which is $R_z(\varepsilon^2) - 1$ up to $O(\varepsilon^4)$. For the group form, multiply the four factors $e^{a}e^{b}e^{-a}e^{-b}$ with $a = \varepsilon A_x$, $b = \varepsilon A_y$: the first-order terms cancel, and the second-order terms are $\tfrac12(a^2 + b^2 + a^2 + b^2) + (ab - a^2 - ab - ba - b^2 + ab) = ab - ba$, so the product is $1 + \varepsilon^2A_z + O(\varepsilon^3) = R_z(\varepsilon^2) + O(\varepsilon^3)$. Multiplying the matrices out reproduces Sakurai's (3.6a), (3.6b).

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The Lie algebra of SO(3)
> The matrices $A_{\hat n}$ are exactly the real antisymmetric $3\times3$ matrices, the tangent space of $SO(3)$ at the identity ([[§25 The Geometric Tangent Space#^thm-25-5|591 Thm. §25.5]]), three-dimensional because $\dim SO(3) = 3$ ([[§23 The Orthogonal and Unitary Groups as Smooth Manifolds#^ex-23-1|591 Ex. §23.1]]). With the commutator as product they form the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$, and part 2 says that this algebra is $\mathbb R^3$ with the cross product. The group is recovered from it by the exponential of part 1. The same construction for the Lorentz group, whose rotation subgroup is $SO(3)$ ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^thm-b1-2-5|REL Theorem §B1.2.5]]), gives six generators, three of them these ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^rem-b1-2-6|REL ★ Remark: Infinitesimal Lorentz transformations]]).

## Rotation operators on kets

> [!principle] Principle §C5.1.2: Rotation Operators
> To every rotation $R$ corresponds an operator $\mathscr D(R)$ on the ket space of the system that takes the state of the system to the state of the rotated system,
>
> $$
> |\alpha\rangle_R = \mathscr D(R)\,|\alpha\rangle , \qquad (3.10)
> $$
>
> with the following properties.
> 1. **Unitarity:** $\mathscr D(R)^\dagger\mathscr D(R) = 1$, so that all probabilities are the same before and after the rotation.
> 2. **Group property:** $\mathscr D(1) = 1$, $\mathscr D(R_1)\mathscr D(R_2) = \mathscr D(R_1R_2)$, $\mathscr D(R^{-1}) = \mathscr D(R)^{-1}$ (3.17a–d).
> 3. **Smoothness:** for an infinitesimal rotation about $\hat n$,
>
> $$
> \mathscr D(\hat n, d\phi) = 1 - \frac{i}{\hbar}\,(\mathbf J\cdot\hat n)\,d\phi + O(d\phi^2), \qquad \mathbf J\cdot\hat n = n_xJ_x + n_yJ_y + n_zJ_z . \qquad (3.15)
> $$
>
> The three operators $J_x, J_y, J_z$ defined by (3.15) are Hermitian (by 1) and are called the **angular momentum** of the system: $J_k/\hbar$ generates rotations about the $k$-th axis, as $p_x/\hbar$ generates translations along $x$ and $H/\hbar$ generates time evolution (3.11)–(3.14).
>
> *Domain: any quantum system in three-dimensional space; the rotation operators exist whatever the Hamiltonian, and whether they commute with it is a separate question (central potentials, [[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials]]). The group property is needed only near the identity. Far from it, it may hold only up to a sign: for half-integer angular momentum the rotation by $2\pi$, which is the identity of $SO(3)$, is represented by $-1$ ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-3|Theorem §C5.2.3]]).*
> *Source: Sakurai §3.1.2–3.1.3, eqs. (3.10)–(3.17) · the user's series, Part IV, §11, eq. (J-generator) · the user's 511 notes, §"Infinitesimal Rotations in Quantum Mechanics"*

^pr-c5-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Angular momentum is defined, not built
> Sakurai stresses that $\mathbf J$ is *not* defined as $\mathbf x\times\mathbf p$: spin, to which the whole formalism applies, has nothing to do with position and momentum. In classical mechanics one proves that $\vec r\times\vec p$ generates rotations ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-6|CM Theorem §B8.1.6]]); in quantum mechanics one defines $\mathbf J$ as the generator and then proves, for a particle without spin, that it equals $\hat{\mathbf x}\times\hat{\mathbf p}$ ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-1|Theorem §C5.4.1]]). Linearity of $\mathbf J\cdot\hat n$ in $\hat n$, assumed in (3.15), is forced by the group property: to first order $R(\hat n, d\phi) = 1 + d\phi\,(n_xA_x + n_yA_y + n_zA_z) = R_x(n_x\,d\phi)R_y(n_y\,d\phi)R_z(n_z\,d\phi) + O(d\phi^2)$ by Theorem §C5.1.1, and the factors on the right are represented by $1 - \frac i\hbar n_kJ_k\,d\phi$.

^rem-c5-1-2

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Why unitary, and why only up to a phase
> A rotation should preserve every transition probability $|\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle|^2$. Wigner's theorem (stated, not proved, in the user's series; [[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-1|Theorem §C8.1.1]]) says that any map of rays with this property is implemented by an operator that is unitary or antiunitary; a rotation is connected to the identity, which is unitary, so it is unitary. Because states are rays, composition need only hold up to a phase, $\mathscr D(R_1)\mathscr D(R_2) = e^{i\gamma}\mathscr D(R_1R_2)$. For the rotation group the phase that cannot be removed by redefinitions is a sign, the sign of [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-3|Theorem §C5.2.3]]. The antiunitary option is realized by time reversal ([[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^thm-c8-3-3|Theorem §C8.3.3]]).
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part IV, §11 (Theorem: Wigner, stated)*

^rem-c5-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.1.3: Finite Rotations
> A rotation through a finite angle $\phi$ about $\hat n$ is represented by
>
> $$
> \mathscr D(\hat n, \phi) = \lim_{N\to\infty}\Bigl[1 - \frac{i}{\hbar}\,(\mathbf J\cdot\hat n)\,\frac\phi N\Bigr]^N = \exp\Bigl(-\frac{i\,\mathbf J\cdot\hat n\,\phi}{\hbar}\Bigr) = 1 - \frac{i\,\mathbf J\cdot\hat n\,\phi}{\hbar} - \frac{(\mathbf J\cdot\hat n)^2\phi^2}{2\hbar^2} + \cdots, \qquad (3.16)
> $$
>
> a unitary operator for every $\phi$, with $\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi)\mathscr D(\hat n, \psi) = \mathscr D(\hat n, \phi + \psi)$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.1.3, eq. (3.16) · the user's 511 notes, §"Finite Rotations in Quantum Mechanics"*

^thm-c5-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the group property and Theorem §C5.1.1(1), $\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi + d\phi) = \mathscr D(\hat n, d\phi)\,\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi)$; with (3.15) this is the differential equation $\frac{d}{d\phi}\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi) = -\frac i\hbar(\mathbf J\cdot\hat n)\,\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi)$ with $\mathscr D(\hat n, 0) = 1$, whose solution is the exponential, defined by its power series ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-6|Def. §B2.1.6]]). Cutting $\phi$ into $N$ equal steps and composing gives the limit form, the same compounding taken literally. Since $\mathbf J\cdot\hat n$ is Hermitian, $\bigl(e^{-iK\phi/\hbar}\bigr)^\dagger = e^{iK\phi/\hbar}$ is its inverse, so $\mathscr D$ is unitary ([[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-51|LADR Def. 7.51]]); exponents that commute add.

## The commutation relations

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.1.4: Fundamental Commutation Relations of Angular Momentum
> The generators of rotations of any quantum system satisfy
>
> $$
> [J_x, J_y] = i\hbar J_z, \qquad [J_y, J_z] = i\hbar J_x, \qquad [J_z, J_x] = i\hbar J_y, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad [J_i, J_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}J_k . \qquad (3.19),\ (3.20)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.1.4, eqs. (3.18)–(3.20) · the user's series, Part IV, §11 (the algebra by both roads) · the user's 511 notes, §"Commutation Relations for Angular Momentum"*

^thm-c5-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Represent both sides of the group form of Theorem §C5.1.1(3). By the group property the left side is represented by $\mathscr D_x(\varepsilon)\mathscr D_y(\varepsilon)\mathscr D_x(-\varepsilon)\mathscr D_y(-\varepsilon)$, with $\mathscr D_k(\pm\varepsilon) = e^{\mp i\varepsilon J_k/\hbar}$ (Theorem §C5.1.3). The product of four exponentials $e^{a}e^{b}e^{-a}e^{-b}$ equals $1 + [a, b] + O(\varepsilon^3)$ for $a, b$ of order $\varepsilon$ (the same bookkeeping as in Derivation §C5.1.1); with $a = -i\varepsilon J_x/\hbar$, $b = -i\varepsilon J_y/\hbar$ it is
>
> $$
> 1 - \frac{\varepsilon^2}{\hbar^2}\,[J_x, J_y] + O(\varepsilon^3) .
> $$
>
> The right side is a rotation that differs from $R_z(\varepsilon^2)$ by $O(\varepsilon^3)$, represented by $1 - \frac i\hbar J_z\varepsilon^2 + O(\varepsilon^3)$ by (3.15). Comparing the terms of order $\varepsilon^2$: $-[J_x, J_y]/\hbar^2 = -iJ_z/\hbar$, that is $[J_x, J_y] = i\hbar J_z$. The other two relations follow in the same way from $[A_y, A_z] = A_x$ and $[A_z, A_x] = A_y$. Sakurai's (3.18) runs the argument on the difference form (3.7) instead, matching $\mathscr D$ of each side term by term; the group form used here (the user's series) applies the representation only to products of rotations, where the group property is stated.

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from level B
> The relations are those of [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]], but their status has changed. Level B derived them for $\mathbf L = \hat{\mathbf r}\times\hat{\mathbf p}$ alone, from $[\hat x_i, \hat p_j] = i\hbar\delta_{ij}$, and for everything else took them as a definition ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^def-b5-2-2|Def. §B5.2.2]]) or a postulate ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^pr-b6-1-1|Principle §B6.1.1]]). Here they are a theorem for the rotation generators of *any* system, with no reference to position or momentum: they are the multiplication table of $SO(3)$ near the identity, $[A_i, A_j] = \epsilon_{ijk}A_k$, carried over to kets. This is the reason announced in [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^rem-b5-2-1|Remark: The classical brackets, with iħ]] for why every kind of angular momentum obeys them, and why the classical brackets $\{L_x, L_y\} = L_z$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-6|CM Theorem §B8.1.6]]) have the same form: the flow generated by $\vec L$ on phase space is the same rotation group. The user's series calls these "the algebra by both roads": Dirac's rule $\{\cdot,\cdot\} \to [\cdot,\cdot]/i\hbar$ applied to the classical brackets, and the geometry of turning; they agree because the bracket algebra of mechanics was the geometry of the symmetry group all along.

^rem-c5-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: A non-Abelian group, and room for finite dimensions
> Generators that do not commute make the group **non-Abelian**: the translations of space form an Abelian group, since $[p_i, p_j] = 0$ ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-6|Theorem §C2.2.6]]), the rotations do not. Sakurai: the relations (3.20) "summarize in a compact manner *all* the basic properties of rotations in three dimensions". Their right-hand sides contain no multiple of the identity. That matters: $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$ has no finite-dimensional solutions, because a commutator of matrices has trace zero and $i\hbar\,1$ does not ([[§33 Trace꞉ A Connection Between Matrices and Operators#^ladr-8-49|LADR Thm. 8.49]], [[§33 Trace꞉ A Connection Between Matrices and Operators#^ladr-8-57|LADR Thm. 8.57]]), while traceless matrices can satisfy $[J_i, J_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$. Rotations can therefore act on finite-dimensional spaces, the smallest being the two-dimensional space of spin $\tfrac12$ ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations]]); all of them are found in [[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices]].
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part IV, §11 (Remark: Why the laboratory was allowed) · Sakurai §3.1.4*

## Angular momentum transforms as a vector

> [!theorem] Theorem §C5.1.5: Angular Momentum Is a Vector Operator
> Under a rotation about $z$,
>
> $$
> \mathscr D_z^\dagger(\phi)\,J_x\,\mathscr D_z(\phi) = J_x\cos\phi - J_y\sin\phi, \qquad \mathscr D_z^\dagger(\phi)\,J_y\,\mathscr D_z(\phi) = J_y\cos\phi + J_x\sin\phi, \qquad \mathscr D_z^\dagger(\phi)\,J_z\,\mathscr D_z(\phi) = J_z, \qquad (3.24)
> $$
>
> and for any rotation $\mathscr D^\dagger(R)\,J_i\,\mathscr D(R) = \sum_j R_{ij}J_j$. Consequently the expectation values rotate like the components of a classical vector:
>
> $$
> \langle J_k\rangle \;\to\; {}_R\langle\alpha|J_k|\alpha\rangle_R = \sum_l R_{kl}\,\langle J_l\rangle . \qquad (3.30)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.1.4, eqs. (3.21)–(3.24), and §3.2.1, eq. (3.30) · the user's 511 notes, §"Transformation of Angular Momentum Operators"*

^thm-c5-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Fix $\hat n$ and let $\mathbf W(\phi)$ be the triple of operators $W_i(\phi) = \mathscr D^\dagger(\hat n, \phi)\,J_i\,\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi)$. Differentiating with Theorem §C5.1.3,
>
> $$
> \frac{dW_i}{d\phi} = \frac i\hbar\,\mathscr D^\dagger\,[\mathbf J\cdot\hat n,\, J_i]\,\mathscr D = \frac i\hbar\,n_k\,(i\hbar\,\epsilon_{kij})\,W_j = -\epsilon_{kij}n_k\,W_j = (A_{\hat n})_{ij}\,W_j ,
> $$
>
> by Theorem §C5.1.4. So $\mathbf W$ obeys the same linear equation as a rotating vector, $\mathbf W' = A_{\hat n}\mathbf W$ with $\mathbf W(0) = \mathbf J$, and $\mathbf W(\phi) = \exp(\phi A_{\hat n})\mathbf J = R(\hat n, \phi)\mathbf J$ (Theorem §C5.1.1). For $\hat n = \hat z$ the first row of $R_z(\phi)$ gives $J_x\cos\phi - J_y\sin\phi$, the second $J_x\sin\phi + J_y\cos\phi$. Taking the expectation value in $|\alpha\rangle$ gives (3.30). Sakurai sums the same series with the Baker–Hausdorff lemma, his (2.168) ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]]): the nested commutators $[J_z, [J_z, \dots, J_x]]$ alternate between $i\hbar J_y$-type and $\hbar^2J_x$-type terms and resum to the cosine and sine.

> [!remark] Remark: Operators that rotate like $\mathbf J$
> $\mathscr D^\dagger\mathbf J\mathscr D = R\mathbf J$ makes $\mathbf J$ the first example of a **vector operator**; its infinitesimal form is $[J_i, J_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$ itself, read as "$\mathbf J$ commutes with $\mathbf J$ like a vector". Position, momentum and spin obey the same law, $[J_i, V_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}V_k$; the general theory is [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]] and [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]]. For $\hat{\mathbf x}$, $\hat{\mathbf p}$ and $\mathbf L$ these commutators are [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]], (4.122).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C5.1.1: Two Quarter-Turns in Either Order
> Compare $R_x(\pi/2)R_z(\pi/2)$ (first $z$, then $x$) with $R_z(\pi/2)R_x(\pi/2)$, and identify each product as a single rotation.
>
> From (3.3),
>
> $$
> R_x(\tfrac\pi2)R_z(\tfrac\pi2) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
> R_z(\tfrac\pi2)R_x(\tfrac\pi2) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> They differ. The first maps $\hat x$ to $\hat z$ ($R_z$ turns $\hat x$ into $\hat y$, then $R_x$ turns $\hat y$ into $\hat z$); the second maps $\hat x$ to $\hat y$ ($R_x$ leaves $\hat x$ alone). Each is a rotation $R(\hat n, \theta)$: its axis is the eigenvector with eigenvalue $1$ and its angle follows from $\operatorname{tr}R(\hat n, \theta) = 1 + 2\cos\theta$, which is $0$ for both, so $\theta = 2\pi/3$. Solving $R\mathbf v = \mathbf v$ gives the axes $(1, -1, 1)/\sqrt3$ and $(1, 1, 1)/\sqrt3$; the antisymmetric parts, $R - R^{\mathsf T} = 2\sin\theta\,A_{\hat n}$, fix the sense: both are $+120^\circ$ turns. So two quarter-turns compose to a $120^\circ$ turn, about an axis that depends on the order (the second product is the cyclic permutation $x \to y \to z \to x$ of the axes). Finite rotations about different axes do not commute, and their product is a rotation about a third axis, as Theorem §C5.1.1 found at second order for small angles.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.1.1 (the comparison of $R_z(\pi/2)$ and $R_x(\pi/2)$ in both orders, his Fig. 3.1); the products and axes computed here*

> [!example] Example §C5.1.2: The Vector Representation
> Show that the $3\times3$ matrices $(\mathcal J_k)_{ij} = -i\hbar\,\epsilon_{kij}$ satisfy the angular-momentum commutation relations, that they generate the ordinary rotations of $\mathbb R^3$, and find $\mathcal J^2$ and the eigenvectors of $\mathcal J_z$.
>
> By Theorem §C5.1.1, $\mathcal J_k = i\hbar A_k$, so $[\mathcal J_i, \mathcal J_j] = (i\hbar)^2\epsilon_{ijk}A_k = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\mathcal J_k$, and $\exp(-i\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal J}\cdot\hat n\,\phi/\hbar) = \exp(\phi A_{\hat n}) = R(\hat n, \phi)$: these are the rotation operators of Principle §C5.1.2 for a system whose states are complex 3-vectors, rotated as vectors. Since $A_k\mathbf V = \hat e_k\times\mathbf V$, $A_k^2\mathbf V = \hat e_k(\hat e_k\cdot\mathbf V) - \mathbf V$ and $\sum_kA_k^2 = 1 - 3\cdot1 = -2\cdot1$, so
>
> $$
> \mathcal J^2 = \mathcal J_x^2 + \mathcal J_y^2 + \mathcal J_z^2 = -\hbar^2\sum_kA_k^2 = 2\hbar^2\,1 = j(j+1)\hbar^2\,1 \quad\text{with } j = 1 .
> $$
>
> $\mathcal J_z = \hbar\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i & 0 \\ i & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalues $\hbar, 0, -\hbar$, with eigenvectors $\mp(\hat x \pm i\hat y)/\sqrt2$ and $\hat z$: the Cartesian vectors carry angular momentum $1$, and the circular combinations are the states with $J_z = \pm\hbar$ (the signs chosen as in [[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^ex-c5-3-2|Example §C5.3.2]]). This is the angular momentum of a vector field such as the photon's polarization, which is why Sakurai suggests the problem for "understanding the photon spin".
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 3.18; solved here*
