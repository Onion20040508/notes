The simplest problem with a complete answer: a particle trapped between two impenetrable walls. Solving the time-independent Schrödinger equation ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]]) with the walls as boundary conditions shows, in a few lines, the central features of bound quantum systems: discrete energies, a nonzero lowest energy, standing-wave states that are orthogonal to one another, and a classical limit at large quantum numbers.

## The model and its stationary states

> [!model] Model §A4.2.1: Infinite Square Well (Particle in a Box)
> A particle of mass $m$ moves along $x$ with potential energy
>
> $$
> U(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & 0 \le x \le L,\\ \infty, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
> $$
>
> Assumptions: between the walls the particle is free; the walls are perfectly rigid, so $\psi = 0$ outside and, by continuity, $\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0$; one dimension. Realizations that the model approximates: a bead on a frictionless wire between two stops; an electron between electrodes whose repelling potential is made very high and steep; an electron moving along a thin wire, or inside a metal; a proton or neutron confined to a nucleus; electrons in nanostructures.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.4, eq. (7.31) · Tipler §6-2, eq. (6-21)*

^mod-a4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.2.2: Energies and Stationary States of the Infinite Well
> The acceptable solutions of the time-independent Schrödinger equation for [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^mod-a4-2-1|Model §A4.2.1]] exist only for the energies
>
> $$
> E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2} = n^2\,\frac{h^2}{8mL^2} = n^2E_1, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots,
> $$
>
> and the normalized stationary states are
>
> $$
> \psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L} \quad (0 \le x \le L), \qquad \Psi_n(x, t) = \psi_n(x)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar},
> $$
>
> with $\psi_n = 0$ outside the box. The integer $n$ is the **energy (principal) quantum number**: $n = 1$ is the ground state, $n = 2$ the first excited state, and so on.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.4, eqs. (7.41)–(7.45), (7.51) · Tipler §6-2, eqs. (6-24)–(6-32)*

^thm-a4-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Inside the box $U = 0$ and the equation is $\psi'' = -k^2\psi$ with $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$. A solution that is not identically zero must oscillate here (a combination of exponentials vanishing at both walls is zero), so $E > 0$ and $\psi = A\sin kx + B\cos kx$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-6|Theorem §A4.1.6]]).
> - $\psi(0) = 0$ gives $B = 0$, so $\psi = A\sin kx$ with $A \ne 0$ (otherwise $\psi \equiv 0$ cannot be normalized).
> - $\psi(L) = 0$ gives $\sin kL = 0$, so $kL = n\pi$, $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$; $n = 0$ gives $\psi \equiv 0$, and negative $n$ only flip the sign of $\psi$.
> - Then $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m = n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2$; with $\hbar = h/2\pi$ this is $n^2h^2/8mL^2$.
> - Normalization: $1 = \int_0^L A^2\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\,dx = A^2L/2$, because $\sin^2 = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2\theta)$ and the cosine integrates to zero over the $n$ whole periods in $[0, L]$; so $A = \sqrt{2/L}$ (any phase factor $e^{i\delta}$ is physically irrelevant).
>
> Finally multiply by $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]). The slope $\psi_n'$ jumps to zero at the walls, which is allowed because $U$ jumps by an infinite amount there ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]]).

^der-a4-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Standing waves and de Broglie
> $kL = n\pi$ says that a whole number of half wavelengths fits into the box, $\lambda_n = 2L/n$: the condition for standing waves on a string fixed at both ends ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|WO Theorem §A2.6.2]]), and the box states have exactly the shapes of the string's normal modes. With $p = h/\lambda$ ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]]) and $E = p^2/2m$ the standing-wave condition alone gives $E_n = n^2h^2/8mL^2$, as anticipated in [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^rem-a3-1-4|Remark: What the standing-wave picture explains]]. Writing $\sin k_nx = (e^{ik_nx} - e^{-ik_nx})/2i$ shows each $\Psi_n$ as the superposition of two waves of equal amplitude travelling in opposite directions, as for a classical standing wave ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-1|WO Theorem §A2.6.1]]). The quantization of energy comes entirely from the boundary conditions: a free particle can have any energy.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-2 · PHY 284, handout "Particle in a box"*

^rem-a4-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Features of the spectrum
> - **No rest.** $E_1 > 0$: a confined particle cannot have zero kinetic energy, unlike a classical particle that may sit still in the box. This **zero-point energy** is what the uncertainty principle demands: $\Delta x \lesssim L$ forces $\Delta p \gtrsim \hbar/L$, and $\langle K\rangle \ge \hbar^2/2mL^2$ ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]), a bound the exact $E_1$ exceeds by the factor $\pi^2$.
> - **Scale.** $E_1 = \pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2 = (hc)^2/8mc^2L^2$ grows as the box shrinks and as the mass decreases. With $hc = 1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}$ and $mc^2 = 0.511\ \mathrm{MeV}$, an electron in a box of atomic size, $L = 0.1\ \mathrm{nm}$, has $E_1 = 37.6\ \mathrm{eV}$, the order of atomic binding energies.
> - **Spacing.** $E_{n+1} - E_n = (2n + 1)E_1$ grows with $n$, but relative to $E_n$ it falls as $2/n$: at large $n$ the levels look continuous. A jump between levels can be carried away by a photon of energy $hf = E_{n'} - E_n$, whose frequencies reveal the size of the box.
> - **Nodes.** $\psi_n$ has $n - 1$ nodes inside the box; each step up in energy adds one.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.4, eq. (7.46) · Tipler §6-2, Example 6-4*

^rem-a4-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: The centred box
> Shifting the box to $-L/2 \le x \le L/2$ changes nothing physical: the energies are the same and $\psi_n(x)$ is the old one evaluated at $x + L/2$,
>
> $$
> \psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{L}}\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\ \ (n \text{ odd}), \qquad \psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{L}}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\ \ (n \text{ even}),
> $$
>
> up to a sign. In a potential symmetric about $x = 0$ the stationary states are even or odd functions, and here they alternate, starting with an even ground state. The finite well and the oscillator use this symmetric form ([[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well]], [[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 8*

^rem-a4-2-3

## Orthonormality

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.2.3: Orthonormality of the Box States
> The stationary states of the infinite well are orthonormal:
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_n^*(x)\,\psi_m(x)\,dx = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\sin\frac{m\pi x}{L}\,dx = \delta_{nm} = \begin{cases} 1, & n = m,\\ 0, & n \ne m.\end{cases}
> $$
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 28–29 slides; HW 9*

^thm-a4-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $n = m$ this is the normalization of [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]. For $n \ne m$ use $\sin a\sin b = \tfrac12[\cos(a - b) - \cos(a + b)]$:
>
> $$
> \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{m\pi x}{L}\,dx = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^L\Bigl[\cos\frac{(n - m)\pi x}{L} - \cos\frac{(n + m)\pi x}{L}\Bigr]dx = \frac1L\Bigl[\frac{L\sin((n - m)\pi x/L)}{(n - m)\pi} - \frac{L\sin((n + m)\pi x/L)}{(n + m)\pi}\Bigr]_0^L = 0 ,
> $$
>
> since $n \pm m$ are nonzero integers and $\sin(j\pi) = 0$.

^der-a4-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Stationary states as a basis
> Orthonormality is the function version of perpendicular unit vectors, with $\int\psi_n^*\psi_m\,dx$ in the role of the dot product. The box states are the Fourier sine functions on $[0, L]$, and the same is true of stationary states in general: states of different energy are orthogonal, and the set is complete, so any wave function in the box is a sum $\sum c_n\psi_n$. This is what makes superpositions easy to handle ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 28–29 slides*

^rem-a4-2-4

## Expectation values and the classical limit

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.2.4: Expectation Values in a Box State
> In the stationary state $\psi_n$ of the infinite well,
>
> $$
> \langle x\rangle = \frac L2, \qquad \langle x^2\rangle = L^2\Bigl(\frac13 - \frac{1}{2n^2\pi^2}\Bigr), \qquad \langle p\rangle = 0, \qquad \langle p^2\rangle = 2mE_n = \Bigl(\frac{n\pi\hbar}{L}\Bigr)^2 ,
> $$
>
> all independent of time. (For $n = 1$ these are worked out in [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^ex-a3-4-2|Example §A3.4.2]].)
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.4, eqs. (7.47)–(7.48) · Tipler §6-4, eqs. (6-45)–(6-48), Example 6-5 · PHY 284, handout "Stationary states"*

^thm-a4-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Time independence: [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-5|Theorem §A4.1.5]]; $\langle p\rangle = 0$ because $\psi_n$ is real ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-5|Theorem §A3.4.5]]). $|\psi_n|^2 = \tfrac2L\sin^2(n\pi x/L)$ is symmetric about $x = L/2$, so $\langle x - L/2\rangle = 0$. For $\langle x^2\rangle$ write $\sin^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2\theta)$ with $q = 2n\pi/L$:
>
> $$
> \langle x^2\rangle = \frac1L\int_0^L x^2\,dx - \frac1L\int_0^L x^2\cos qx\,dx = \frac{L^2}{3} - \frac1L\cdot\frac{2L}{q^2} = \frac{L^2}{3} - \frac{L^2}{2n^2\pi^2},
> $$
>
> where two integrations by parts give $\int_0^L x^2\cos qx\,dx = 2L/q^2$ (the boundary terms with $\sin qL = 0$ vanish and $\cos qL = 1$). For $\langle p^2\rangle$, $\hat p^2\psi_n = -\hbar^2\psi_n'' = 2mE_n\psi_n$ by the Schrödinger equation with $U = 0$, so $\langle p^2\rangle = 2mE_n\int|\psi_n|^2dx = 2mE_n$.

^der-a4-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-5|Theorem §A4.1.5]], [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-3|Def. §A3.4.3]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-5|Theorem §A3.4.5]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: What these numbers mean
> - **Average versus most likely.** $\langle x\rangle = L/2$ for every $n$, yet for even $n$ the density vanishes at $L/2$: the expectation value is the mean of many measurements, not a likely outcome. The most probable positions are the maxima of $\sin^2$, at $x = (2j + 1)L/2n$, $j = 0, \dots, n - 1$.
> - **Momentum.** $\langle p\rangle = 0$ but $\langle p^2\rangle \ne 0$: the particle is equally likely to be found moving either way, with $|p| = n\pi\hbar/L = \hbar k_n$, the two travelling waves of the standing wave.
> - **Uncertainty.** $\sigma_x^2 = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-2|Theorem §A3.4.2]]) $= L^2\bigl(\tfrac1{12} - \tfrac{1}{2n^2\pi^2}\bigr)$ and $\sigma_p = n\pi\hbar/L$, so
>
> $$
> \sigma_x\sigma_p = \hbar\sqrt{\frac{n^2\pi^2}{12} - \frac12}\,; \qquad n = 1:\ \ \sigma_x = 0.181L,\ \ \sigma_x\sigma_p = 0.568\,\hbar \ge \frac\hbar2 ,
> $$
>
> consistent with the uncertainty relation ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]) and, for the ground state, not far from its minimum.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, handout "Stationary states"; HW 8*

^rem-a4-2-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.2.5: Classical Limit of the Box
> The probability of finding the particle in the state $\psi_n$ between $a$ and $b$ ($0 \le a < b \le L$) is
>
> $$
> P_n(a, b) = \frac{b - a}{L} - \frac{1}{2n\pi}\Bigl[\sin\frac{2n\pi b}{L} - \sin\frac{2n\pi a}{L}\Bigr], \qquad \Bigl|P_n(a, b) - \frac{b - a}{L}\Bigr| \le \frac{1}{n\pi} .
> $$
>
> As $n \to \infty$ it tends to $(b - a)/L$, the probability for a classical particle bouncing between the walls at constant speed, whose probability density is the uniform $1/L$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.4, eq. (7.50) · Tipler §6-2*

^thm-a4-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\int_a^b \tfrac2L\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\,dx = \tfrac1L\int_a^b\bigl(1 - \cos(2n\pi x/L)\bigr)dx$ gives the formula; the bracket is at most $2$ in size. Classically the time spent in $dx$ is $dx/u$ at the constant speed $u$, the same for every $dx$, so the classical density is uniform and normalized to $1/L$.

^der-a4-2-5

*Uses:* [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Correspondence
> At low $n$ the quantum and classical pictures differ completely: in the ground state the particle is most likely near the middle and almost never near the walls. At large $n$ the density oscillates so rapidly (the figure, $n = 20$) that any measurement with finite resolution sees its average, the classical $1/L$. This is Bohr's correspondence principle ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^pr-a2-2-6|Principle §A2.2.6]]) at work. For macroscopic bodies $n$ is astronomically large ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^ex-a4-2-3|Example §A4.2.3]]), adjacent levels are indistinguishable, and classical mechanics is adequate.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.2.1: A Simple Model of the Nucleus
> Treat a proton in a nucleus as a particle in a box of width $L = 1.00 \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm m = 10.0\ \mathrm{fm}$. With $mc^2 = 938\ \mathrm{MeV}$ and $hc = 1240\ \mathrm{MeV\cdot fm}$,
>
> $$
> E_1 = \frac{(hc)^2}{8mc^2L^2} = \frac{(1240\ \mathrm{MeV\cdot fm})^2}{8(938\ \mathrm{MeV})(10.0\ \mathrm{fm})^2} = 2.05\ \mathrm{MeV}, \qquad E_2 = 4E_1 = 8.20\ \mathrm{MeV}.
> $$
>
> The transition $2 \to 1$ emits a photon of energy $E_2 - E_1 = 6.15\ \mathrm{MeV}$, frequency $f = (6.15 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{eV})/(4.14 \times 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{eV\cdot s}) = 1.49 \times 10^{21}\ \mathrm{Hz}$: a gamma ray, about ten million times more energetic than visible light. Compared with the electron in an atom-sized box ($37.6\ \mathrm{eV}$, [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^rem-a4-2-2|Remark: Features of the spectrum]]), the box is $10^4$ times smaller, a factor $10^8$ in $1/L^2$, partly offset by the $1836$ times larger mass: nuclear energies are MeV where atomic ones are eV.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 7.8*

^ex-a4-2-1

> [!example] Example §A4.2.2: Where Is the Particle?
> Find the probability of finding the particle in each third of the box, for $n = 1$ and $n = 2$. By [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-5|Theorem §A4.2.5]],
>
> $$
> P_n\bigl(0, \tfrac L3\bigr) = \frac13 - \frac{\sin(2n\pi/3)}{2n\pi} :\qquad P_1 = \frac13 - \frac{\sqrt3/2}{2\pi} = 0.196, \qquad P_2 = \frac13 + \frac{\sqrt3/2}{4\pi} = 0.402 .
> $$
>
> The densities are symmetric about $L/2$, so the right third has the same probability and the middle third gets the rest:
>
> | | left third | middle third | right third |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $n = 1$ | $0.196$ | $0.609$ | $0.196$ |
> | $n = 2$ | $0.402$ | $0.196$ | $0.402$ |
> | classical | $0.333$ | $0.333$ | $0.333$ |
>
> In the ground state the particle crowds toward the middle; in the first excited state it avoids the middle, where $\psi_2$ has its node. Likewise $P_1(0, L/4) = \tfrac14 - \tfrac1{2\pi} = 0.091$ against the classical $0.25$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 8; S22 final, problem 2 (checked against the posted solution, $0.402$) · Tipler Example 6-3*

^ex-a4-2-2

> [!example] Example §A4.2.3: A Classical Particle in a Box
> A $0.40\ \mathrm{kg}$ cart moves at $0.50\ \mathrm{m/s}$ on a frictionless air track between bumpers $2.0\ \mathrm m$ apart, bouncing elastically. Which quantum number would it have as a particle in a box? Its energy is $K = \tfrac12(0.40\ \mathrm{kg})(0.50\ \mathrm{m/s})^2 = 0.050\ \mathrm J$, and
>
> $$
> E_1 = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2} = \frac{\pi^2(1.055 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s})^2}{2(0.40\ \mathrm{kg})(2.0\ \mathrm m)^2} = 3.4 \times 10^{-68}\ \mathrm J, \qquad n = \sqrt{K/E_1} = 1.2 \times 10^{33} .
> $$
>
> Neighbouring levels differ by a fraction $2/n \approx 10^{-33}$ of the energy, far below anything measurable: the quantum description is correct but indistinguishable from the classical one. The converse fails: classical mechanics says nothing right about an electron in its lowest states.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 7.9 (its printed $E_1 = 1.70 \times 10^{-68}$ J is off by a factor 2; $n$ is unaffected)*

^ex-a4-2-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Orthonormality of $\sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(n\pi x/L)$ is the orthonormality of a Fourier basis ([[§20 Orthonormal Bases#^ladr-6-22|LADR Def. 6.22]], [[§20 Orthonormal Bases#^ladr-6-23|LADR Ex. 6.23]]) for the inner product $\int_0^L fg\,dx$.
> - Completeness of the box states (every square-integrable function on $[0, L]$ is a convergent sum $\sum c_n\psi_n$) is an orthonormal basis in the Hilbert-space sense ([[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^def-20-4|556 Def. §20.4]]), with the completeness relation $\sum|\psi_n\rangle\langle\psi_n| = \mathbf 1$ ([[§23 The Completeness Relation#^thm-23-2|556 Thm. §23.2]]); its proof is Fourier analysis (level B, planned).
> - The box in three dimensions, with one quantum number per direction, is the free-electron model of a metal ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model]]).
> - The classical counterparts: on a string fixed at both ends the separable solutions are exactly the standing waves $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ ([[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-1|WO Theorem §B4.2.1]]); a square membrane has the shapes and degeneracies of the states of a particle in a square box ([[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^rem-b7-2-2|WO Remark: What one hears and sees]]).
> - The box energies summed with Boltzmann factors give the translational partition function of an ideal gas: [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^thm-b8-1-2|TH Theorem §B8.1.2]].
