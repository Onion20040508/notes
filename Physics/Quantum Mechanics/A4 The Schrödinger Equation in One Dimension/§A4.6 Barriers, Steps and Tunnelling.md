So far the particle was trapped, and trapping quantized its energy. Now it comes in from far away, meets a change in the potential energy and moves on: any energy is allowed, and the question becomes how much of the incoming wave is reflected and how much transmitted. Two results have no classical counterpart. A particle with enough energy to pass a step is sometimes reflected anyway, and a particle with too little energy to climb a barrier sometimes appears on the other side. This **tunnelling** explains alpha decay and is the working principle of the scanning tunnelling microscope.

## Free particles and scattering states

> [!definition] Definition §A4.6.1: Bound State; Scattering State
> A stationary state is a **bound state** if $E < U(x)$ for all large $|x|$: the particle is confined, $\psi \to 0$ at $\pm\infty$, and the allowed energies are discrete. It is a **scattering (unbound) state** if $E > U(x)$ as $x \to +\infty$ or $x \to -\infty$ (or both): the particle can escape to infinity, $\psi$ oscillates there, and every energy in a continuous range is allowed.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, handout "Free particle and scattering states" · Tipler §6-6*

^def-a4-6-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.6.1: Free-Particle States and Wave Packets
> For $U = 0$ the stationary states are the plane waves
>
> $$
> \Psi_k(x, t) = e^{i(kx - \omega t)}, \qquad \hbar\omega = E = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}, \qquad k = \pm\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar},
> $$
>
> moving toward $+x$ for $k > 0$ and toward $-x$ for $k < 0$, for every $E > 0$. None of them is normalizable. Normalizable solutions are superpositions over a continuous range of $k$, wave packets:
>
> $$
> \Psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(k)\,e^{i(kx - \hbar k^2t/2m)}\,dk, \qquad \phi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi(x, 0)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx ,
> $$
>
> where $\phi$, the Fourier transform of the initial wave function, gives the amount of each momentum $p = \hbar k$ in the packet. Such a packet is the wave packet of [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^def-a3-3-2|Def. §A3.3.2]]: it moves at the group velocity, the particle velocity ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]]), and spreads as it goes ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^rem-a3-3-3|Remark: Matter waves are dispersive, so free packets spread]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, handout "Free particle and scattering states"; class 32–33 slides · UP3 §7.3*

^thm-a4-6-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each plane wave solves the equation with $U = 0$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]]), and $|e^{i(kx - \omega t)}|^2 = 1$ has an infinite integral. The packet is a superposition of solutions, now an integral instead of a sum ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]): differentiating under the integral sign, each component satisfies the equation, so the packet does. At $t = 0$ the packet is $\Psi(x, 0) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\phi(k)e^{ikx}dk$; that $\phi$ is then given by the second formula is the Fourier inversion theorem, quoted here ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-1|WO Theorem §B4.4.1]]; used at level B in [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-2|Theorem §B1.4.2]]).

^der-a4-6-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]], [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Working with plane waves anyway
> Scattering problems are solved with plane-wave stationary states and then read as beams. A steady beam of particles of one energy is described by $Ae^{ikx}$ with $|A|^2$ the number of particles per unit length; the number crossing a point per unit time, the **flux**, is that density times the speed $v = \hbar k/m$. A single particle is a long wave packet built from plane waves of nearly the same $k$; each component is reflected and transmitted with the plane-wave amplitudes, and after the encounter the packet has split into a reflected and a transmitted packet. A measurement then finds the particle in one or the other, with probabilities $R$ and $T$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-6 · PHY 284, class 33–34 slides*

^rem-a4-6-1

> [!definition] Definition §A4.6.2: Reflection and Transmission Coefficients
> For a beam incident from the left, with incident wave $Ae^{ik_1x}$, reflected wave $Be^{-ik_1x}$ and transmitted wave $Ce^{ik_2x}$ (wave numbers $k_1$ before and $k_2$ after the obstacle), the **reflection coefficient** $R$ and the **transmission coefficient** $T$ are the reflected and transmitted fractions of the incident flux:
>
> $$
> R = \frac{|B|^2}{|A|^2}, \qquad T = \frac{k_2}{k_1}\,\frac{|C|^2}{|A|^2} .
> $$
>
> For a single particle they are the probabilities of reflection and of transmission. When the potential energy is the same on both sides ($k_2 = k_1$, as for a barrier), $T = |C|^2/|A|^2$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-6, eq. (6-68) · PHY 284, handout "Free particle and scattering states" · UP3 §7.6, eq. (7.71)*

## The potential step

> [!model] Model §A4.6.2: Potential Step
>
> $$
> U(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x < 0,\\ U_0, & x > 0,\end{cases} \qquad U_0 > 0,
> $$
>
> with particles of energy $E$ incident from the left. Assumptions: the rise is abrupt (short compared with the de Broglie wavelength), as at the surface of a metal; one dimension. Classically, every particle with $E > U_0$ passes, slowed to $v' = \sqrt{2(E - U_0)/m}$, and every particle with $E < U_0$ is turned back.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-6 · PHY 284, handout "Free particle and scattering states"*

^mod-a4-6-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.6.3: Step with E > U₀
> With $k_1 = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and $k_2 = \sqrt{2m(E - U_0)}/\hbar$, the stationary state is $Ae^{ik_1x} + Be^{-ik_1x}$ for $x < 0$ and $Ce^{ik_2x}$ for $x > 0$, with
>
> $$
> \frac BA = \frac{k_1 - k_2}{k_1 + k_2}, \qquad \frac CA = \frac{2k_1}{k_1 + k_2}, \qquad R = \Bigl(\frac{k_1 - k_2}{k_1 + k_2}\Bigr)^2 = \Bigl(\frac{\sqrt E - \sqrt{E - U_0}}{\sqrt E + \sqrt{E - U_0}}\Bigr)^2, \qquad T = \frac{4k_1k_2}{(k_1 + k_2)^2},
> $$
>
> and $R + T = 1$. Some particles are reflected although they have enough energy to pass.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-6, eqs. (6-66)–(6-69) · PHY 284, handout "Free particle and scattering states"; class 32–33 slides*

^thm-a4-6-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In each region $U$ is constant and $E > U$, so $\psi$ is a combination of $e^{\pm ik_jx}$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-6|Theorem §A4.1.6]]); on the right there is no wave coming in from $+\infty$, so only $Ce^{ik_2x}$. Continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at $x = 0$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]]):
>
> $$
> A + B = C, \qquad ik_1(A - B) = ik_2C .
> $$
>
> Adding $k_1\times$(first) to the second divided by $i$ gives $2k_1A = (k_1 + k_2)C$; then $B = C - A = \tfrac{k_1 - k_2}{k_1 + k_2}A$. With [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^def-a4-6-2|Def. §A4.6.2]], $T = \tfrac{k_2}{k_1}\cdot\tfrac{4k_1^2}{(k_1 + k_2)^2}$, and $R + T = \tfrac{(k_1 - k_2)^2 + 4k_1k_2}{(k_1 + k_2)^2} = 1$. The second form of $R$ uses $k \propto \sqrt{\text{kinetic energy}}$.

^der-a4-6-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-6|Theorem §A4.1.6]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]], [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^def-a4-6-2|Def. §A4.6.2]], [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^mod-a4-6-2|Model §A4.6.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.6.4: Step with E < U₀
> With $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and $\alpha = \sqrt{2m(U_0 - E)}/\hbar$, the stationary state is $Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx}$ for $x < 0$ and $Ce^{-\alpha x}$ for $x > 0$, with
>
> $$
> \frac BA = \frac{k - i\alpha}{k + i\alpha}, \qquad \frac CA = \frac{2k}{k + i\alpha}, \qquad R = 1, \qquad \frac{|C|^2}{|A|^2} = \frac{4E}{U_0} .
> $$
>
> Every particle is reflected, as classically, but the wave penetrates the step: $|\psi|^2 = |C|^2e^{-2\alpha x}$, with penetration depth $1/\alpha$ ([[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-6, eqs. (6-70)–(6-71) · PHY 284, handout "Free particle and scattering states"*

^thm-a4-6-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $x > 0$, $E < U_0$ gives $e^{\pm\alpha x}$, and $e^{+\alpha x}$ is excluded because it blows up. The matching conditions are those of the previous derivation with $ik_2$ replaced by $-\alpha$, that is $k_2 = i\alpha$: $B/A = (k - i\alpha)/(k + i\alpha)$, whose numerator and denominator are complex conjugates, so $|B/A| = 1$. Also $|C/A|^2 = 4k^2/(k^2 + \alpha^2) = 4E/U_0$, since $k^2 + \alpha^2 = 2mU_0/\hbar^2$. The decaying wave carries no flux, so $T = 0$.

^der-a4-6-4

*Uses:* [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-3|Theorem §A4.6.3]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Waves at a step
> - **Reflection without a classical cause.** For $E > U_0$, $R$ depends only on the mismatch of $k_1$ and $k_2$, not on which is larger, so a step *down* reflects as much as a step up of the same size. As $E/U_0 \to \infty$, $k_2 \to k_1$ and $R \to 0$; as $E \to U_0^+$, $R \to 1$ (figure below, green). This is how every wave behaves where the medium changes: light is partly reflected at a glass surface ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction|WO §A4.2]]).
> - **The transmitted wave is longer.** Past the step the kinetic energy $E - U_0$ is smaller, so $k_2 < k_1$ and the de Broglie wavelength grows; the particles move more slowly, and $|C|^2$ can exceed $|A|^2$ because slow particles pile up, which is why $T$ carries the factor $k_2/k_1$.
> - **Total reflection with an evanescent tail.** For $E < U_0$ the wave reaches into the step and turns back, like light totally reflected inside glass, which also extends a decaying field into the air beyond the surface ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^def-a4-3-1|WO Def. §A4.3.1]]). Bring a second glass surface within that tail and light leaks across ("frustrated total internal reflection"): optical tunnelling.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-6 · PHY 284, handout "Free particle and scattering states"*

## The barrier and tunnelling

> [!model] Model §A4.6.5: Rectangular Barrier
>
> $$
> U(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x < 0,\\ U_0, & 0 \le x \le L,\\ 0, & x > L,\end{cases}
> $$
>
> a barrier of height $U_0$ and width $L$, with particles of energy $E$ incident from the left. Assumptions: abrupt edges; one dimension. Classically, all particles with $E < U_0$ are reflected and all with $E > U_0$ pass.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.6, eq. (7.59) · Tipler §6-6 · PHY 284, handout "Quantum tunnelling"*

^mod-a4-6-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.6.6: Transmission Through a Barrier
> Let $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ outside the barrier. For $E < U_0$, with $\alpha = \sqrt{2m(U_0 - E)}/\hbar$,
>
> $$
> T = \Bigl[1 + \frac{U_0^2\sinh^2(\alpha L)}{4E(U_0 - E)}\Bigr]^{-1} \;\approx\; 16\,\frac{E}{U_0}\Bigl(1 - \frac{E}{U_0}\Bigr)e^{-2\alpha L} \quad (\alpha L \gg 1) .
> $$
>
> For $E > U_0$, with $k' = \sqrt{2m(E - U_0)}/\hbar$ inside,
>
> $$
> T = \Bigl[1 + \frac{U_0^2\sin^2(k'L)}{4E(E - U_0)}\Bigr]^{-1}, \qquad T = 1 \ \text{ when } k'L = n\pi .
> $$
>
> In both cases $R = 1 - T$. Particles with $E < U_0$ get through with probability $T > 0$: **tunnelling**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.6, eqs. (7.73)–(7.81) · PHY 284, handout "Quantum tunnelling"; class 34 slides · Tipler §6-6, eqs. (6-75)–(6-76)*

^thm-a4-6-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $E < U_0$ the regions give $\psi_{\mathrm I} = Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx}$, $\psi_{\mathrm{II}} = Ce^{-\alpha x} + De^{\alpha x}$, $\psi_{\mathrm{III}} = Fe^{ikx}$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-6|Theorem §A4.1.6]]; no wave enters from the right). Match $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at both edges ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]]) and work from right to left.
> - At $x = L$: $Ce^{-\alpha L} + De^{\alpha L} = Fe^{ikL}$ and $\alpha(De^{\alpha L} - Ce^{-\alpha L}) = ikFe^{ikL}$, so
>
> $$
> Ce^{-\alpha L} = \tfrac12Fe^{ikL}\Bigl(1 - \frac{ik}{\alpha}\Bigr), \qquad De^{\alpha L} = \tfrac12Fe^{ikL}\Bigl(1 + \frac{ik}{\alpha}\Bigr).
> $$
>
> - At $x = 0$: $A + B = C + D$ and $ik(A - B) = \alpha(D - C)$; adding, $2A = \bigl(1 + \tfrac{i\alpha}{k}\bigr)C + \bigl(1 - \tfrac{i\alpha}{k}\bigr)D$.
> - Substitute and multiply out, using $\bigl(1 + \tfrac{i\alpha}{k}\bigr)\bigl(1 - \tfrac{ik}{\alpha}\bigr) = 2 + i\gamma$ and $\bigl(1 - \tfrac{i\alpha}{k}\bigr)\bigl(1 + \tfrac{ik}{\alpha}\bigr) = 2 - i\gamma$ with $\gamma = \tfrac\alpha k - \tfrac k\alpha$:
>
> $$
> A = \tfrac14Fe^{ikL}\bigl[(2 + i\gamma)e^{\alpha L} + (2 - i\gamma)e^{-\alpha L}\bigr] = Fe^{ikL}\Bigl[\cosh\alpha L + \frac{i\gamma}{2}\sinh\alpha L\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> - Hence $T = |F/A|^2 = \bigl[\cosh^2\alpha L + \tfrac{\gamma^2}{4}\sinh^2\alpha L\bigr]^{-1} = \bigl[1 + \bigl(1 + \tfrac{\gamma^2}{4}\bigr)\sinh^2\alpha L\bigr]^{-1}$, and $1 + \tfrac{\gamma^2}{4} = \tfrac14\bigl(\tfrac\alpha k + \tfrac k\alpha\bigr)^2 = \dfrac{(\alpha^2 + k^2)^2}{4\alpha^2k^2} = \dfrac{U_0^2}{4E(U_0 - E)}$, since $\hbar^2(\alpha^2 + k^2) = 2mU_0$, $\hbar^2k^2 = 2mE$, $\hbar^2\alpha^2 = 2m(U_0 - E)$.
> - Thick barrier: for $\alpha L \gg 1$, $\sinh\alpha L \approx \tfrac12e^{\alpha L}$ is huge, the $1$ is negligible, and $T \approx \dfrac{16E(U_0 - E)}{U_0^2}e^{-2\alpha L}$.
> - $E > U_0$: nothing above used that $\alpha$ is real. Inside, $e^{\mp\alpha x}$ with $\alpha = -ik'$ are the oscillating waves $e^{\pm ik'x}$; then $\sinh^2(\alpha L) = -\sin^2(k'L)$ and $U_0 - E = -(E - U_0)$, giving the second formula. It equals $1$ exactly when $\sin k'L = 0$.
>
> $R = 1 - T$ because no particles are created or lost: what is not transmitted is reflected (in the step this was checked directly).

> [!remark] Remark: What controls tunnelling
> The factor $e^{-2\alpha L} = \exp\bigl(-2L\sqrt{2m(U_0 - E)}/\hbar\bigr)$ dominates. Doubling the width squares this small factor, while changing $U_0 - E$ enters only under a square root, so the width matters most: to block tunnelling, make the barrier thicker rather than higher. The mass enters as $\sqrt m$ in the exponent, so a proton tunnels vastly less than an electron ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^ex-a4-6-3|Example §A4.6.3]]), and for everyday objects $T$ is zero for all practical purposes. The resonances above the barrier are the matter-wave version of a thin film in air: the step up at $x = 0$ and the step down at $x = L$ reflect with opposite signs ($B/A \propto k - k'$ and $\propto k' - k$), so when the round trip $2L$ is a whole number of wavelengths $2\pi/k'$ the two reflected waves cancel and everything is transmitted ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^thm-a6-3-2|WO Theorem §A6.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, handout "Quantum tunnelling"; class 34 slides · UP3 §7.6*

## Tunnelling in nature and technology

> [!remark] Remark: Alpha decay
> Many heavy nuclei emit $\alpha$ particles (helium nuclei) with kinetic energies between about $4$ and $9$ MeV, yet their half-lives range from $160\ \mu\mathrm s$ (polonium-214) to $4.5 \times 10^9$ years (uranium-238). Gamow, and independently Condon and Gurney (1928), explained this as tunnelling. Inside the nucleus the $\alpha$ particle is held by the strong nuclear force, modelled as a well; outside it is repelled by the daughter nucleus of charge $Ze$ with the Coulomb potential energy $2Ze^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$. An $\alpha$ particle of energy $E$ must tunnel from the nuclear radius $R$ to the point $R_0$ where $E = 2Ze^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0R_0$, a barrier of width $L = R_0 - R$. A larger $E$ makes the barrier both lower and thinner; through the exponential this shortens the lifetime enormously. The decay rate is roughly the tunnelling probability per approach times the number of approaches per second, about $v/2R$ for an $\alpha$ particle of speed $v$ bouncing inside; Gamow's resulting formula agrees well with the measured decay rates across this whole range, and accounts for the empirical Geiger–Nuttall rule ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^law-a7-2-6|Law §A7.2.6]]). The agreement is in the trend: the crude model gets the Geiger–Nuttall slope (1.72 against the fitted 1.61), but misses absolute lifetimes by a factor of order $10^6$ ([[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^ex-b9-4-2|Example §B9.4.2]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.6 · Tipler §6-6 · PHY 284, handout "Quantum tunnelling"*

^rem-a4-6-4

> [!remark] Remark: Field emission and the scanning tunnelling microscope
> Electrons in a metal are held in by a step of height equal to the work function $\phi$ ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^def-a1-2-3|Def. §A1.2.3]]). A strong external electric field $E_g$ tilts the potential outside into a triangular barrier, of width $L = \phi/eE_g$ for an electron that must climb $\phi$; a stronger field makes it thinner and the tunnelling current grows exponentially (**field emission**). In the **scanning tunnelling microscope** (Binnig and Rohrer, 1981) a sharp metal tip is held a few tenths of a nanometre above a conducting surface, with a small bias voltage between them; electrons tunnel across the gap. A change in the gap of about one atomic diameter changes the current by a factor of order $10^4$, so a feedback loop that keeps the current constant while the tip scans traces the surface atom by atom.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.6 · Tipler §6-6*

^rem-a4-6-5

> [!remark] Remark: Tunnel devices and the ammonia clock
> - **Tunnel diode** (1957): electrons tunnel through a thin barrier at the junction of two semiconductors; the current is not proportional to the voltage and can even fall as the voltage rises, which is useful in fast switches.
> - **Resonant-tunnelling diode:** a quantum dot between two barriers has discrete levels ([[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^rem-a4-4-5|Remark: Quantum wells and quantum dots]]); electrons tunnel through it only when the bias voltage lines up their energy with a dot level, so the current switches on and off sharply as the voltage is tuned.
> - **Ammonia inversion:** the nitrogen atom of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ sits in a double well, above or below the plane of the three hydrogens. In the lowest states it tunnels back and forth through the central barrier, at $2.3786 \times 10^{10}$ Hz; this frequency served as the standard of the first atomic clocks. The motion is the two-state hopping of [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^ex-a4-3-2|Example §A4.3.2]].
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.6 · Tipler §6-6 · PHY 284, handout "Quantum tunnelling"*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.6.1: Electrons at a Step
> Electrons meet a step of height $U_0 = 2.0$ eV, of the order of a metal's work function.
> - **$E = 4.0$ eV $= 2U_0$.** $k_2/k_1 = \sqrt{(E - U_0)/E} = 0.707$, so by [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-3|Theorem §A4.6.3]]
>
> $$
> R = \Bigl(\frac{1 - 0.707}{1 + 0.707}\Bigr)^2 = 0.029, \qquad T = \frac{4(0.707)}{(1.707)^2} = 0.971 .
> $$
>
> Three percent are turned back by a step they could easily climb. The de Broglie wavelength, $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2mK} = 1.226\ \mathrm{nm}/\sqrt{K/\mathrm{eV}}$, grows from $0.61$ nm to $0.87$ nm across the step.
> - **$E = 0.20$ eV $= 0.1U_0$.** All are reflected ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-4|Theorem §A4.6.4]]), but $|\psi|^2 = 0.4|A|^2e^{-2\alpha x}$ inside, with
>
> $$
> \alpha = \frac{\sqrt{2mc^2(U_0 - E)}}{\hbar c} = \frac{\sqrt{2(511 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{eV})(1.8\ \mathrm{eV})}}{197.3\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}} = 6.87\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}, \qquad \frac1\alpha = 0.15\ \mathrm{nm} .
> $$
>
> At $x = 0.5$ nm, about two atomic diameters into the step, $|\psi|^2$ is down by $e^{-6.9} \approx 10^{-3}$: penetration is real but confined to atomic distances.
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 6-6 (the second case); the first case worked here*

^ex-a4-6-1

> [!example] Example §A4.6.2: Tunnelling Between Nanowires
> Two copper nanowires are separated by an insulating oxide layer that acts as a barrier of height $U_0 = 10.0$ eV. Estimate the tunnelling probability for electrons of $7.00$ eV and of $9.00$ eV through layers $5.00$ nm and $1.00$ nm thick. With $mc^2 = 511$ keV and $\hbar c = 0.1973\ \mathrm{keV\cdot nm}$, $2m/\hbar^2 = 26.25\ \mathrm{eV^{-1}nm^{-2}}$, so $\alpha = \sqrt{26.25\,(U_0 - E)/\mathrm{eV}}\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$:
>
> $$
> E = 7.00\ \mathrm{eV}:\ \alpha = 8.87\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}},\ \ T \approx 16(0.7)(0.3)\,e^{-17.7\,L/\mathrm{nm}} = 3.36\,e^{-17.7\,L/\mathrm{nm}};
> $$
>
> $$
> E = 9.00\ \mathrm{eV}:\ \alpha = 5.12\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}},\ \ T \approx 16(0.9)(0.1)\,e^{-10.2\,L/\mathrm{nm}} = 1.44\,e^{-10.2\,L/\mathrm{nm}} .
> $$
>
> | | $L = 5.00$ nm | $L = 1.00$ nm |
> |---|---|---|
> | $E = 7.00$ eV | $9.7 \times 10^{-39}$ | $6.6 \times 10^{-8}$ |
> | $E = 9.00$ eV | $8.1 \times 10^{-23}$ | $5.1 \times 10^{-5}$ |
>
> ($\alpha L \ge 5$ throughout, so the approximation agrees with the exact formula to the digits shown.) Thinning the layer from $5$ to $1$ nm raises $T$ by $10^{31}$ at $7$ eV; raising the energy from $7$ to $9$ eV at $1$ nm gains only a factor $800$. Barriers a fraction of a nanometre thick pass measurable tunnelling currents, which is what the STM exploits.
>

> [!example] Example §A4.6.3: Electron Versus Proton
> A $1.0$-eV electron has a tunnelling probability $T = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}$ through a barrier of height $2.5$ eV. How wide is the barrier, and what is $T$ for a $1.0$-eV proton? For the electron $\alpha = \sqrt{2(511 \times 10^3)(1.5)}\ \mathrm{eV}/197.3\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm} = 6.27\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$ and the prefactor is $16(0.4)(0.6) = 3.84$:
>
> $$
> e^{2\alpha L} = \frac{3.84}{1.0 \times 10^{-4}} = 3.84 \times 10^4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad L = \frac{\ln(3.84 \times 10^4)}{2(6.27\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}})} = 0.84\ \mathrm{nm} .
> $$
>
> For the proton $\alpha$ is larger by $\sqrt{m_p/m_e} = \sqrt{1836} = 42.8$: $\alpha_p = 269\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$, $2\alpha_pL = 452$, and $T \approx 3.84\,e^{-452} \approx 10^{-196}$. The same barrier that lets one electron in $10^4$ through is, for protons, a perfect wall.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Final practice 2023, problem 4 (checked against the posted answers)*
