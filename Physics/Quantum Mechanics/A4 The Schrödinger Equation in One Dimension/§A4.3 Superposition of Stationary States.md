A stationary state has a sharp energy and nothing measurable about it changes in time ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-5|Theorem §A4.1.5]]). Real motion, a particle sloshing from one side of a box to the other, or an atom radiating, needs more. Because the Schrödinger equation is linear, sums of stationary states with different energies are also solutions, and every solution is such a sum. This section shows how to build them, what an energy measurement on them yields, and why their probability densities oscillate at the frequencies $(E_m - E_n)/h$.

## The general solution

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.3.1: Superposition of Stationary States
> Let $U(x)$ be independent of time, with stationary states $\psi_n(x)$ of energies $E_n$. For any complex constants $c_n$,
>
> $$
> \Psi(x, t) = \sum_n c_n\,\psi_n(x)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}, \qquad \Psi(x, 0) = \sum_n c_n\,\psi_n(x),
> $$
>
> is a solution of the Schrödinger equation (for infinite sums, provided the series converges well enough to be differentiated term by term). If two or more $E_n$ differ, it is not a stationary state: it is a state of **mixed energy**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.3 (linear combinations of separated solutions); §7.4 · Tipler §6-1 · PHY 284, class 28–29 slides; handout "Simple harmonic oscillator" (general solution)*

^thm-a4-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each term $\psi_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ solves the equation ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]]), and sums of solutions are solutions ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-2|Theorem §A4.1.2]]). If the sum were of the separated form $\psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, then $|\Psi|^2$ would be constant in time, which fails as soon as two terms with different energies overlap ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-5|Theorem §A4.3.5]]).

^der-a4-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-2|Theorem §A4.1.2]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Every state is such a sum
> The bound stationary states are orthonormal, $\int\psi_n^*\psi_m\,dx = \delta_{nm}$ ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-3|Theorem §A4.2.3]] for the box; true in general), and **complete**: every acceptable initial wave function can be written as $\sum c_n\psi_n$, much as any vector is a combination of basis vectors, here in an infinite-dimensional space. For the box this is the Fourier sine series. So the theorem gives *the* solution of every initial-value problem:
> 1. expand $\Psi(x, 0)$ in stationary states;
> 2. attach to each term its own phase factor $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$.
>
> Each piece turns at its own rate; the relative phases between the pieces change with time, and that is where the motion comes from. Completeness is quoted here and proved at level B for the box and the oscillator ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]], [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]); for a general observable it is a postulate ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]]). For unbound particles the sum becomes an integral over a continuous energy ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-1|Theorem §A4.6.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 28–29 slides*

^rem-a4-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.3.2: Coefficients and Normalization
> With orthonormal $\psi_n$, the coefficients of $\Psi(x, 0) = \sum_n c_n\psi_n$ are
>
> $$
> c_n = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_n^*(x)\,\Psi(x, 0)\,dx ,
> $$
>
> and the norm of the superposition is
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Psi(x, t)|^2\,dx = \sum_n |c_n|^2 \qquad\text{for all } t,
> $$
>
> so $\Psi$ is normalized exactly when $\sum_n |c_n|^2 = 1$, and stays normalized.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 28–29 slides; HW 9 · Tipler §6-4*

^thm-a4-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Multiply $\Psi(x, 0) = \sum_m c_m\psi_m$ by $\psi_n^*$ and integrate: $\int\psi_n^*\Psi(x, 0)\,dx = \sum_m c_m\int\psi_n^*\psi_m\,dx = \sum_m c_m\delta_{nm} = c_n$. For the norm,
>
> $$
> \int|\Psi|^2dx = \sum_{n, m} c_n^*c_m\,e^{i(E_n - E_m)t/\hbar}\int\psi_n^*\psi_m\,dx = \sum_{n, m}c_n^*c_m\,e^{i(E_n - E_m)t/\hbar}\,\delta_{nm} = \sum_n |c_n|^2 ;
> $$
>
> the time-dependent cross terms all carry a factor $\int\psi_n^*\psi_m\,dx = 0$.

^der-a4-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]], [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-3|Theorem §A4.2.3]], [[§33 Properties of the Riemann Integral#^thm-33-2|451 Thm. §33.2]]

## Measuring the energy

> [!principle] Principle §A4.3.3: Energy Measurements on a Superposition
> If a particle is in the normalized state $\Psi = \sum_n c_n\psi_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$, a measurement of its energy gives one of the stationary-state energies $E_n$, never anything in between, with probability
>
> $$
> P(E_n) = |c_n|^2 .
> $$
>
> Immediately after a measurement that gave $E_n$, the particle is in the stationary state $\psi_n$.
>
> *Domain: ideal measurements, in the non-relativistic one-particle setting of [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]]; energies $E_n$ that each belong to a single stationary state (true for bound states in one dimension).*
> *Source: PHY 284, class 28–29 slides · UP3 §7.4 ("any energy measurement ... must return a value equal to one of these allowed energies")*

^pr-a4-3-3

> [!remark] Remark: Status of the rule
> At this level the rule is a postulate: it is the Born rule ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-1|Principle §A3.4.1]]) carried over from position to energy, with $|c_n|^2$ in the role of $|\Psi(x)|^2\,dx$. It does not repeat the expectation-value rule ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]]): that rule gives only the *average* $\langle E\rangle$ of many measurements, while this one says which values a single measurement can give, how often each occurs, and what state the particle is left in. The two agree, since the average of the outcomes $E_n$ with the probabilities $|c_n|^2$ is the $\langle E\rangle$ of [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-4|Theorem §A4.3.4]]; and the rule is consistent with the rest: $|c_n|^2 \ge 0$ and $\sum|c_n|^2 = 1$ ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]), and a stationary state, with a single $c_n = 1$, gives its energy with certainty ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-5|Theorem §A4.1.5]]). The second sentence, the "collapse" of the state ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^rem-a3-4-3|Remark: Measurement and the Copenhagen interpretation]]), means that repeating the measurement at once gives the same $E_n$. Level B states the general measurement postulate for any observable, of which the Born rule, this rule and the expectation-value rule are special cases ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]]).

^rem-a4-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.3.4: Mean Energy of a Superposition
> In the normalized state $\Psi = \sum_n c_n\psi_ne^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$, the mean energy ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]]) is
>
> $$
> \langle E\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi^*\,i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}\,dx = \sum_n |c_n|^2E_n ,
> $$
>
> independent of time.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 29 slides · Tipler §6-4*

^thm-a4-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $i\hbar\,\partial\Psi/\partial t = \sum_m c_mE_m\psi_me^{-iE_mt/\hbar}$. Then
>
> $$
> \langle E\rangle = \sum_{n, m}c_n^*c_mE_m\,e^{i(E_n - E_m)t/\hbar}\int\psi_n^*\psi_m\,dx = \sum_n |c_n|^2E_n
> $$
>
> by orthonormality. This is the weighted average of the possible outcomes with the probabilities of [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^pr-a4-3-3|Principle §A4.3.3]], as it must be.

^der-a4-3-4

*Uses:* [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]], [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-3|Theorem §A4.2.3]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-5|Def. §A3.4.5]]

> [!caution] Caution: Phases, and what the coefficients do not say
> - $\langle E\rangle$ is usually not an allowed energy: an equal mix of $E_1$ and $4E_1$ has $\langle E\rangle = 2.5E_1$, which no single measurement ever gives.
> - The probabilities fix only $|c_n|$. The **relative phases** of the $c_n$ are physical: they decide where the particle is at $t = 0$ (for $c_2 = -c_1$ the box state below starts on the right instead of the left). A **common phase** $e^{i\delta}$ multiplying all $c_n$ changes nothing measurable ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^rem-a3-4-2|Remark: What is observable and what is not]]). So "10% in $E_1$, 90% in $E_2$" does not determine the state.
> - A collection of many particles, some in one state and some in another, is not the same thing as each particle in a superposition (a "mixture" rather than a "pure state"); the distinction belongs to later courses.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, handout "Superposition of states" (note on an ill-posed exam question); class 29 slides*

## Time dependence

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.3.5: Two-State Superposition
> Let $\Psi = c_1\psi_1e^{-iE_1t/\hbar} + c_2\psi_2e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}$ with real $\psi_1, \psi_2$, and write $c_1^*c_2 = |c_1c_2|\,e^{i\phi}$. Then
>
> $$
> |\Psi(x, t)|^2 = |c_1|^2\psi_1^2 + |c_2|^2\psi_2^2 + 2|c_1c_2|\,\psi_1\psi_2\cos(\omega_{21}t - \phi), \qquad \omega_{21} = \frac{E_2 - E_1}{\hbar},
> $$
>
> and, with $x_{12} = \int x\,\psi_1\psi_2\,dx$,
>
> $$
> \langle x\rangle(t) = |c_1|^2\langle x\rangle_1 + |c_2|^2\langle x\rangle_2 + 2|c_1c_2|\,x_{12}\cos(\omega_{21}t - \phi) .
> $$
>
> The density and the mean position oscillate at the **Bohr frequency** $f_{21} = (E_2 - E_1)/h$; $\langle p\rangle = m\,d\langle x\rangle/dt$ oscillates with it.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 29 slides; HW 9; handout "Stationary states" ($\langle p\rangle = m\,d\langle x\rangle/dt$)*

^thm-a4-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $|\Psi|^2 = \Psi^*\Psi$ has the two squared terms, whose phase factors cancel, and the cross terms
>
> $$
> c_1^*c_2\,\psi_1\psi_2\,e^{i(E_1 - E_2)t/\hbar} + c_1c_2^*\,\psi_1\psi_2\,e^{-i(E_1 - E_2)t/\hbar} = 2\,\mathrm{Re}\bigl(|c_1c_2|e^{i\phi}e^{-i\omega_{21}t}\bigr)\psi_1\psi_2 = 2|c_1c_2|\psi_1\psi_2\cos(\omega_{21}t - \phi).
> $$
>
> Multiplying by $x$ and integrating gives $\langle x\rangle$, with $\langle x\rangle_j = \int x\,\psi_j^2\,dx$.

^der-a4-3-5

*Uses:* [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]], [[§1 Rⁿ and Cⁿ#^ladr-1-1|LADR Def. 1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why atoms radiate at $\Delta E/h$
> A charged particle in a superposition of two stationary states has a charge distribution that oscillates at $f_{21} = (E_2 - E_1)/h$, like a small antenna, and an oscillating charge radiates at its own frequency. That is exactly the photon frequency of the transition between the two levels, the Bohr frequency condition ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^mod-a2-2-1|Model §A2.2.1]]), now as a consequence rather than a postulate. A stationary state alone has a static charge cloud and does not radiate. If $x_{12} = 0$ the mean position does not move and this simple dipole radiation does not occur: the origin of **selection rules**, such as $\Delta n = \pm1$ for the oscillator ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^rem-a4-5-3|Remark: Equal spacing, photons and Planck]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-4 (transitions between energy states), §6-5*

> [!remark] Remark: From superpositions to classical motion
> Adding many box states with suitable coefficients builds a narrow wave packet that bounces between the walls like a classical particle, spreading slowly as it goes. The two-state example is the crudest version of this. In the oscillator, whose levels are equally spaced, every superposition repeats exactly with the classical period ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^rem-a4-5-6|Remark: Superpositions oscillate at the classical frequency]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 28–29 slides*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.3.1: Equal Mix of the Two Lowest Box States
> A particle in the infinite well starts in $\Psi(x, 0) = A[\psi_1(x) + \psi_2(x)]$. Write $\omega = E_1/\hbar = \pi^2\hbar/2mL^2$, so $E_2 = 4E_1$ and $\omega_{21} = 3\omega$.
> - **Normalization.** $|A|^2(1 + 1) = 1$, so $A = 1/\sqrt2$ ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]).
> - **Time dependence.** $\Psi(x, t) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\psi_1e^{-i\omega t} + \psi_2e^{-4i\omega t}\bigr]$ and
>
> $$
> |\Psi|^2 = \frac1L\Bigl[\sin^2\frac{\pi x}{L} + \sin^2\frac{2\pi x}{L} + 2\sin\frac{\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{2\pi x}{L}\cos 3\omega t\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> - **Mean position.** $\langle x\rangle_1 = \langle x\rangle_2 = L/2$, and with $\sin a\sin b = \tfrac12[\cos(a - b) - \cos(a + b)]$ and $\int_0^L x\cos(j\pi x/L)\,dx = -2L^2/j^2\pi^2$ for odd $j$,
>
> $$
> x_{12} = \frac1L\int_0^L x\Bigl[\cos\frac{\pi x}{L} - \cos\frac{3\pi x}{L}\Bigr]dx = -\frac{16L}{9\pi^2}, \qquad \langle x\rangle = \frac L2 - \frac{16L}{9\pi^2}\cos 3\omega t .
> $$
>
> It oscillates at angular frequency $3\omega = (E_2 - E_1)/\hbar$ with amplitude $16L/9\pi^2 = 0.180L$.
> - **Mean momentum.** $\langle p\rangle = m\,\dfrac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = \dfrac{16mL\cdot3\omega}{9\pi^2}\sin 3\omega t = \dfrac{8\hbar}{3L}\sin 3\omega t$ (using $m\omega = \pi^2\hbar/2L^2$); a direct calculation of $\int\Psi^*(-i\hbar\,\partial_x)\Psi\,dx$ gives the same.
> - **Energy.** A measurement gives $E_1$ or $4E_1$, each with probability $\tfrac12$; $\langle E\rangle = \tfrac52E_1$, constant.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 9, problem 1 (C. Wang, Spring 2023); solved here*

^ex-a4-3-1

> [!example] Example §A4.3.2: A Particle Hopping Between Two Wells
> In some double-well potential the two lowest stationary states are (lengths in arbitrary units)
>
> $$
> \psi_a = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\ \text{on } -2 < x < -1 \text{ and on } 1 < x < 2, \qquad \psi_b = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\ \text{on } -2 < x < -1, \quad -\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\ \text{on } 1 < x < 2,
> $$
>
> zero elsewhere, with energies $\hbar\omega$ and $2\hbar\omega$. At $t = 0$ the particle is in $\Psi = C(\psi_a + \psi_b)$. The sum is $\sqrt2C$ on the left segment and $0$ on the right, so $C = 1/\sqrt2$ and the particle starts entirely in the left well. Then
>
> $$
> \Psi(x, t) = \tfrac12\bigl(e^{-i\omega t} \pm e^{-2i\omega t}\bigr) \quad (+ \text{ left}, - \text{ right}), \qquad P_{\text{left}} = \cos^2\frac{\omega t}{2}, \quad P_{\text{right}} = \sin^2\frac{\omega t}{2} .
> $$
>
> The particle moves completely to the right well at $t = \pi/\omega$ and back at $2\pi/\omega$; $\langle x\rangle = -\tfrac32\cos^2\tfrac{\omega t}2 + \tfrac32\sin^2\tfrac{\omega t}2 = -\tfrac32\cos\omega t$. The energy does not change: $\langle E\rangle = \tfrac32\hbar\omega$ at all times. The hopping frequency is set by the splitting $E_b - E_a$ of the two levels; the ammonia molecule, whose nitrogen atom hops through a barrier this way, was used as the first atomic clock ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-6|Remark: Tunnel devices and the ammonia clock]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, S22 final, problem 4 (checked against the posted solution)*

^ex-a4-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: When the mean position stands still
> The density of a superposition always changes in time, but $\langle x\rangle$ need not. For a box state mixed from $\psi_1$ and $\psi_3$ (say with probabilities $\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac34$), $\psi_1\psi_3$ is symmetric about $L/2$, so $x_{12}$-type terms vanish and $\langle x\rangle = L/2$ for all $t$, while $|\Psi|^2$ breathes symmetrically at $\omega_{31} = 8E_1/\hbar$; $\langle E\rangle = \tfrac14E_1 + \tfrac34\cdot9E_1 = 7E_1$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, Final practice 2023, problem 3 (with answers)*
