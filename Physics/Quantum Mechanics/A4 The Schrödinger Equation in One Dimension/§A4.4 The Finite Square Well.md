Real walls are never infinitely high. Lowering them to a finite height $U_0$ keeps the essentials of the box (discrete energies, standing waves inside) but adds something with no classical counterpart: the wave function leaks into the walls, where a classical particle of the same energy could never be. The price is that the energies are no longer given by a formula: they are roots of a transcendental equation, found graphically or numerically.

## The model and the form of its bound states

> [!model] Model §A4.4.1: Finite Square Well
> A particle of mass $m$ moves along $x$ with potential energy
>
> $$
> U(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & |x| < a,\\ U_0, & |x| > a,\end{cases} \qquad U_0 > 0,
> $$
>
> a well of width $L = 2a$ and depth $U_0$, placed symmetrically about $x = 0$. The **bound states** are those with $0 < E < U_0$; for $E > U_0$ the particle is not confined (scattering states, [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^def-a4-6-1|Def. §A4.6.1]]). Assumptions: the walls rise abruptly (an idealization of a potential that changes over a distance short compared with the de Broglie wavelength); one dimension. Realizations: an electron between grids and electrodes at a finite potential difference; an electron in a thin layer of one semiconductor sandwiched between layers of another (a quantum well), or in a quantum dot.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-3 · PHY 284, handout "Finite well"*

^mod-a4-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Classical and quantum expectations
> Classically a particle with $E < U_0$ bounces between the walls exactly as in the infinite well, with any energy, and never enters $|x| > a$, where its kinetic energy $E - U_0$ would be negative. Quantum mechanically the bound states are still standing waves with a growing number of nodes, but (i) only a finite number of them exist, (ii) their energies lie below the infinite-well values for the same width, and (iii) $\psi$ is nonzero inside the walls.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, handout "Finite well"; class 30 slides*

^rem-a4-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.4.2: Form of the Bound States
> A bound state of the finite well with energy $0 < E < U_0$ has, with
>
> $$
> k = \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\sqrt{2m(U_0 - E)}}{\hbar}, \qquad k^2 + \alpha^2 = \frac{2mU_0}{\hbar^2},
> $$
>
> one of the two forms
>
> $$
> \text{even: }\ \psi(x) = \begin{cases} C\cos(ka)\,e^{\alpha(x + a)}, & x \le -a,\\ C\cos kx, & |x| \le a,\\ C\cos(ka)\,e^{-\alpha(x - a)}, & x \ge a,\end{cases} \qquad
> \text{odd: }\ \psi(x) = \begin{cases} -C\sin(ka)\,e^{\alpha(x + a)}, & x \le -a,\\ C\sin kx, & |x| \le a,\\ C\sin(ka)\,e^{-\alpha(x - a)}, & x \ge a.\end{cases}
> $$
>
> Inside the well $\psi$ oscillates; outside it decays exponentially.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, handout "Finite well" · Tipler §6-3, eqs. (6-33)–(6-34), §6-5 (parity)*

^thm-a4-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In each region $U$ is constant, so [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-6|Theorem §A4.1.6]] gives $A\sin kx + B\cos kx$ inside and $Ce^{\alpha x} + De^{-\alpha x}$ outside. Normalizability ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]]) kills the growing exponential on each side: only $e^{-\alpha x}$ survives for $x > a$ and only $e^{\alpha x}$ for $x < -a$. Symmetry: $U(-x) = U(x)$, so if $\psi(x)$ solves the equation with energy $E$ so does $\psi(-x)$. For a bound state in one dimension two solutions with the same energy differ only by a constant factor, $\psi(-x) = c\,\psi(x)$; reflecting twice gives $c^2 = 1$, so $\psi$ is even or odd. An even $\psi$ has only the cosine inside and equal tails; an odd one only the sine and opposite tails. The coefficients of the tails are fixed by continuity of $\psi$ at $x = \pm a$.

^der-a4-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-6|Theorem §A4.1.6]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]], [[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^mod-a4-4-1|Model §A4.4.1]]

## The quantization condition

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.4.3: Energy Condition of the Finite Well
> Put $z = ka$ and $z_0 = \dfrac{a\sqrt{2mU_0}}{\hbar}$, so that $\alpha a = \sqrt{z_0^2 - z^2}$ and $E = \dfrac{\hbar^2z^2}{2ma^2} = U_0\,\dfrac{z^2}{z_0^2}$. The bound-state energies are given by the roots $0 < z < z_0$ of
>
> $$
> \text{even states: }\ \tan z = \sqrt{\frac{z_0^2}{z^2} - 1}, \qquad\qquad \text{odd states: }\ -\cot z = \sqrt{\frac{z_0^2}{z^2} - 1} .
> $$
>
> These are transcendental equations: they have no closed-form solution and are solved graphically or numerically.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, handout "Finite well"; class 30 slides · Tipler §6-3 (graphical solution)*

^thm-a4-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Even states: write the tail for $x \ge a$ as $Ae^{-\alpha(x - a)}$ with $A$ not yet known. At $x = a$, continuity of $\psi$ and of $\psi'$ give
>
> $$
> C\cos ka = A, \qquad -kC\sin ka = -\alpha A .
> $$
>
> Dividing the second by the first eliminates the constants: $k\tan ka = \alpha$, so $\tan z = \alpha/k = \alpha a/z = \sqrt{z_0^2 - z^2}/z = \sqrt{z_0^2/z^2 - 1}$. (Equivalently $\tan ka = \sqrt{(U_0 - E)/E}$.) Odd states: $C\sin ka = A$ and $kC\cos ka = -\alpha A$ give $-k\cot ka = \alpha$, the second equation. The matching at $x = -a$ gives nothing new, by symmetry. The relation $k^2 + \alpha^2 = 2mU_0/\hbar^2$ times $a^2$ is $z^2 + (\alpha a)^2 = z_0^2$.

^der-a4-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^thm-a4-4-2|Theorem §A4.4.2]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.4.4: Number and Position of the Levels
> Number the bound states $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ in order of energy; odd $n$ are even states, even $n$ odd states. Then:
> 1. the $n$th root lies in $(n - 1)\tfrac\pi2 < z_n < n\tfrac\pi2$, and it exists exactly when $z_0 > (n - 1)\tfrac\pi2$; so there are $N$ bound states, the smallest integer $N \ge 2z_0/\pi$;
> 2. there is always at least one bound state (even), however shallow or narrow the well;
> 3. there is only one bound state if $z_0 \le \pi/2$, that is if $U_0 \le \dfrac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$, the ground energy of the infinite well of the same width $L = 2a$;
> 4. each level lies below the corresponding infinite-well level: $E_n < \dfrac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$;
> 5. as $U_0 \to \infty$ at fixed width, $z_n \to n\pi/2$ and the levels approach those of the infinite well.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, handout "Finite well"; class 30 slides · Tipler §6-3*

^thm-a4-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The right side $g(z) = \sqrt{z_0^2/z^2 - 1}$ decreases from $+\infty$ (as $z \to 0$) to $0$ (at $z = z_0$). On each interval $\bigl[j\pi, j\pi + \tfrac\pi2\bigr)$, $\tan z$ increases from $0$ to $+\infty$, so it meets $g$ exactly once if $j\pi < z_0$ and not at all otherwise; on each $\bigl[j\pi + \tfrac\pi2, (j + 1)\pi\bigr)$, $-\cot z$ does the same, meeting $g$ once iff $j\pi + \tfrac\pi2 < z_0$. Elsewhere one side is negative and the other positive. Listing the intervals in order gives item 1, and $j = 0$ for $\tan$ gives item 2. Item 3 is item 1 with $n = 2$: $z_0 \le \pi/2$ means $a^2\cdot2mU_0/\hbar^2 \le \pi^2/4$. Item 4: $E_n = \hbar^2z_n^2/2ma^2 < \hbar^2(n\pi/2)^2/2ma^2 = n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/2m(2a)^2$. Item 5: as $z_0 \to \infty$, $g(z) \to \infty$ at each fixed $z$, so the intersection on each branch moves up to the branch's asymptote $n\pi/2$.

*Uses:* [[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]], [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]], [[§18 Properties of Continuous Functions#^thm-18-3|451 Thm. §18.3]]

*The graphical solution for the well of [[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^ex-a4-4-1|Example §A4.4.1]] ($z_0 = 8.10$). The red curve $\sqrt{z_0^2/z^2 - 1}$ meets the blue branches of $\tan z$ (even states) and the green branches of $-\cot z$ (odd states) alternately, once on each branch that starts below $z_0$: six bound states. > [!remark] Remark: Why the levels are lower
> In the infinite well $\psi$ must vanish at the walls, so exactly $n$ half wavelengths fit inside. In the finite well $\psi$ only has to join smoothly onto a decaying tail, so a little less than $n$ half wavelengths fit: the wavelength inside is longer, the curvature and the kinetic energy smaller. Put differently, the wave function has more room, because it spreads into the walls.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-3 · PHY 284, handout "Finite well"; class 30 slides*

^rem-a4-4-2

## Penetration into the walls

> [!definition] Definition §A4.4.1: Classically Forbidden Region; Penetration Depth
> For a particle of energy $E$, the **classically forbidden region** is where $U(x) > E$. In a region of constant $U_0 > E$ the bound-state wave function decays as $e^{-\alpha x}$ and the probability density as $e^{-2\alpha x}$; the **penetration depth** is
>
> $$
> \delta = \frac1\alpha = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m(U_0 - E)}} ,
> $$
>
> the distance over which $\psi$ falls by a factor $e$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, handout "Finite well" · Tipler §6-3, eq. (6-34)*

^def-a4-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Does the particle have negative kinetic energy there?
> Finding the particle in the wall seems to need $K = E - U_0 < 0$. No measurement shows that. To find it in the wall at all, one must localize it within about $\Delta x \approx \delta$ of the edge; by the uncertainty relation ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]) this gives it a momentum spread $\Delta p \approx \hbar/\delta = \hbar\alpha$ and a kinetic energy of order
>
> $$
> \frac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m} \approx \frac{\hbar^2\alpha^2}{2m} = U_0 - E ,
> $$
>
> just enough to make up the deficit. The penetration itself is real, and it has consequences: if the wall is thin, the tail reaches the other side and the particle can escape, tunnelling ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-6|Theorem §A4.6.6]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-3 · PHY 284, handout "Finite well"*

^rem-a4-4-3

> [!remark] Remark: Any well quantizes
> Nothing here depends on the walls being square. In any well, $\psi$ oscillates where $E > U(x)$ and bends away from the axis where $E < U(x)$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^rem-a4-1-8|Remark: Reading the shape of ψ from the curvature]]); only at special energies does it bend just enough to die away on both sides. Every confining potential therefore has discrete bound-state energies, with the $n$th state having $n - 1$ nodes, while above the rim the energies form a continuum. The finite well is the simplest case where this can be worked out; the oscillator is another ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-3*

^rem-a4-4-4

## Example

> [!example] Example §A4.4.1: An Electron in a 1-nm Well
> An electron is trapped in a finite well of width $L = 2a = 1.00\ \mathrm{nm}$ and depth $U_0 = 10.0\ \mathrm{eV}$. With $\hbar c = 197.3\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}$ and $mc^2 = 511\ \mathrm{keV}$,
>
> $$
> z_0 = \frac{a\sqrt{2mc^2U_0}}{\hbar c} = \frac{(0.500\ \mathrm{nm})\sqrt{2(511 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{eV})(10.0\ \mathrm{eV})}}{197.3\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}} = 8.10 ,
> $$
>
> and $2z_0/\pi = 5.16$, so there are $N = 6$ bound states ([[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^thm-a4-4-4|Theorem §A4.4.4]]). Solving the conditions of [[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]] numerically and using $E_n = U_0z_n^2/z_0^2$:
>
> | $n$ | $z_n$ | $E_n$ (eV) | infinite well (eV) | $\delta_n$ (nm) | $P(\lvert x\rvert > a)$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | 1.397 | 0.298 | 0.376 | 0.063 | 0.003 |
> | 2 | 2.790 | 1.19 | 1.50 | 0.066 | 0.014 |
> | 3 | 4.171 | 2.65 | 3.38 | 0.072 | 0.033 |
> | 4 | 5.532 | 4.66 | 6.02 | 0.084 | 0.067 |
> | 5 | 6.847 | 7.14 | 9.40 | 0.116 | 0.134 |
> | 6 | 8.007 | 9.77 | 13.5 | 0.405 | 0.437 |
>
> The infinite-well values are $n^2(hc)^2/8mc^2L^2 = n^2(0.376\ \mathrm{eV})$; the probability outside follows from normalizing the even or odd form of [[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^thm-a4-4-2|Theorem §A4.4.2]]. Low states barely notice the finite depth: the ground state is $21\%$ below the infinite-well value, about what an infinite well widened by one penetration depth on each side would give ($(1 + 2\delta_1/L)^{-2} = 0.79$, a rough check made here), and it spends only $0.3\%$ of the time in the walls. The top state, $0.23\ \mathrm{eV}$ below the rim, reaches $0.4\ \mathrm{nm}$ into the walls and is found there $44\%$ of the time. A second bound state would disappear for $U_0 < 0.376\ \mathrm{eV}$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 30 slides (graphical solution for $z_0 = 8$, six states); numbers chosen and solved here*

*The six bound states of Example §A4.4.1, each $\psi_n$ (blue) drawn on its energy level, with the well outline in grey. Inside, $\psi_n$ oscillates with $n - 1$ nodes; outside it decays exponentially, barely for $n = 1$ and over a long tail for $n = 6$, just below the rim. > [!remark] Remark: Quantum wells and quantum dots
> Layered semiconductors make finite wells to order. A thin layer confining electrons in one direction (a quantum well) is the heart of the diode lasers that read optical discs and bar codes; a speck of gallium arsenide in aluminium arsenide confines them in all three directions (a quantum dot), with a few discrete levels set by its size. Because the walls are finite, electrons can also tunnel in and out of such a dot, which is the principle of the resonant-tunnelling diode ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-6|Remark: Tunnel devices and the ammonia clock]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-3 · UP3 §7.6*
