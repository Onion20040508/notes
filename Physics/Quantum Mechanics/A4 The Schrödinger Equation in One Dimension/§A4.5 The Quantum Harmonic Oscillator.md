Near the bottom of almost any potential well the potential energy is a parabola, so small vibrations are harmonic ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-3|WO Theorem §A1.2.3]]): atoms in molecules and solids, and the modes of the electromagnetic field in a cavity. The quantum oscillator was one of the first problems Schrödinger solved. Its energies are equally spaced, $E_n = (n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega$, which recovers Planck's quantized oscillators of the blackbody problem and adds a zero-point energy $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$ that no classical oscillator has.

## The classical oscillator, for comparison

> [!remark] Remark: Classical recap
> A particle of mass $m$ on a spring of constant $k$ ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^mod-a1-1-1|WO Model §A1.1.1]]) has $U = \tfrac12kx^2 = \tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$ with $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ and moves as $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$. Its energy $E = \tfrac12kA^2$ ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-1|WO Theorem §A1.2.1]]) can take any value $\ge 0$; $E = 0$ means the particle at rest at the bottom. It stays between the turning points $x = \pm A$, where $U = E$ ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^def-a4-7-1|CM Def. §A4.7.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.5*

^rem-a4-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.1: Classical Position Distribution of an Oscillator
> For a classical oscillator of amplitude $A$ observed at a random time, the probability density of its position is
>
> $$
> P_{\text{cl}}(x) = \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}}, \qquad |x| < A,
> $$
>
> and zero for $|x| > A$. It is smallest at the centre and grows without bound at the turning points.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-5 · UP3 §7.5*

^thm-a4-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The probability of finding the particle in $dx$ is the fraction of a period it spends there. In one half period $T/2 = \pi/\omega$ it crosses every $dx$ once, taking $dt = dx/|v|$, with $|v| = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$ from energy conservation $\tfrac12mv^2 + \tfrac12m\omega^2x^2 = \tfrac12m\omega^2A^2$. So $P_{\text{cl}}\,dx = \dfrac{dx/|v|}{\pi/\omega} = \dfrac{dx}{\pi\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}}$. Check: $\int_{-A}^{A}\dfrac{dx}{\pi\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}} = \dfrac1\pi\bigl[\arcsin(x/A)\bigr]_{-A}^{A} = 1$.

^der-a4-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-2|WO Theorem §A1.2.2]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-2|451 Def. §36.2]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

## The quantum oscillator

> [!model] Model §A4.5.2: Quantum Harmonic Oscillator
> A particle of mass $m$ moves along $x$ with potential energy
>
> $$
> U(x) = \tfrac12m\omega^2x^2 \qquad (\text{equivalently } \tfrac12kx^2,\ k = m\omega^2),
> $$
>
> for all $x$. Assumptions: the parabola holds everywhere (real potentials, such as that between the atoms of a molecule, are parabolic only near their minimum, so the model describes the low-lying states); one dimension; for a diatomic molecule with one much heavier atom, the heavy atom is taken to be at rest and $m$ is the mass of the light one. The natural length and energy scales are
>
> $$
> a = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}}, \qquad \hbar\omega .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.5, eqs. (7.54)–(7.55) (UP writes $\beta = 1/a$) · Tipler §6-5 · PHY 284, handout "Simple harmonic oscillator"*

^mod-a4-5-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.3: Energies and Stationary States of the Oscillator
> The acceptable solutions of the time-independent Schrödinger equation for [[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^mod-a4-5-2|Model §A4.5.2]] exist only for
>
> $$
> E_n = \Bigl(n + \frac12\Bigr)\hbar\omega, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots,
> $$
>
> with stationary states
>
> $$
> \psi_n(x) = N_n\,H_n\!\Bigl(\frac xa\Bigr)\,e^{-x^2/2a^2},
> $$
>
> where $N_n$ is a normalization constant and $H_n$ is the Hermite polynomial of degree $n$:
>
> $$
> H_0(u) = 1, \quad H_1(u) = 2u, \quad H_2(u) = 4u^2 - 2, \quad H_3(u) = 8u^3 - 12u .
> $$
>
> The two lowest normalized states are $\psi_0 = (a\sqrt\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/2a^2}$ and $\psi_1 = (2a\sqrt\pi)^{-1/2}\,(2x/a)\,e^{-x^2/2a^2}$. The states alternate between even ($n$ even) and odd ($n$ odd), and $\psi_n$ has $n$ nodes.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.5, eqs. (7.56)–(7.57) · Tipler §6-5 · PHY 284, handout "Simple harmonic oscillator"; class 31 slides; HW 10*

^thm-a4-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> This is the idea of the proof; the step marked (∗) is proved at level B ([[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]]).
> - **Dimensionless form.** With $u = x/a$ and $\varepsilon = 2E/\hbar\omega$, the equation $-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + \tfrac12m\omega^2x^2\psi = E\psi$ becomes, since $\hbar^2/ma^2 = \hbar\omega$ and $m\omega^2a^2 = \hbar\omega$,
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2\psi}{du^2} = (u^2 - \varepsilon)\,\psi .
> $$
>
> - **Behaviour far out.** For large $|u|$ the $u^2$ term dominates, and $e^{-u^2/2}$ has $\psi'' = (u^2 - 1)\psi \approx u^2\psi$: deep in the forbidden region the decay is Gaussian, faster than the exponential of a square well, because $U - E$ keeps growing.
> - **Ansatz.** Put $\psi = f(u)\,e^{-u^2/2}$. Then $\psi'' = (f'' - 2uf' + (u^2 - 1)f)e^{-u^2/2}$, and the equation becomes Hermite's equation
>
> $$
> f'' - 2uf' + (\varepsilon - 1)f = 0 .
> $$
>
> - **Quantization.** If $f$ is a polynomial with leading term $c\,u^n$, the $u^n$ terms give $-2nc + (\varepsilon - 1)c = 0$, so $\varepsilon = 2n + 1$, that is $E = (n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega$. (∗) For other $\varepsilon$ the solutions grow like $e^{u^2}$, so $\psi$ is not normalizable; only the polynomial solutions, the Hermite polynomials, are acceptable.
> - **Check.** $f = H_2 = 4u^2 - 2$: $f'' - 2uf' + (\varepsilon - 1)f = 8 - 16u^2 + 4(4u^2 - 2) = 0$ for $\varepsilon = 5$, so $E_2 = \tfrac52\hbar\omega$.
>
> Each $H_n$ contains only even or only odd powers, which gives the parity; its $n$ real zeros are the nodes.

^der-a4-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]], [[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^mod-a4-5-2|Model §A4.5.2]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.4: The Ground State
> The Gaussian
>
> $$
> \psi_0(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a\sqrt\pi}}\,e^{-x^2/2a^2}, \qquad a = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}},
> $$
>
> is normalized and solves the time-independent Schrödinger equation of the oscillator with $E_0 = \tfrac12\hbar\omega$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, handout "Simple harmonic oscillator"; class 31 slides · UP3 §7.5*

^thm-a4-5-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate: $\psi_0' = -\dfrac{x}{a^2}\psi_0$ and $\psi_0'' = \Bigl(\dfrac{x^2}{a^4} - \dfrac1{a^2}\Bigr)\psi_0$. Then
>
> $$
> -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_0'' + \frac12m\omega^2x^2\psi_0 = \Bigl[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^4}x^2 + \frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2} + \frac12m\omega^2x^2\Bigr]\psi_0 = \frac12\hbar\omega\,\psi_0 ,
> $$
>
> because $\hbar^2/ma^4 = m\omega^2$ makes the $x^2$ terms cancel, and $\hbar^2/2ma^2 = \tfrac12\hbar\omega$. The cancellation happens only for this $a$: any other width leaves an $x^2$ term, and no constant $E$ works. Normalization: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2/a^2}dx = a\sqrt\pi$, the Gaussian integral with $x = au$.

## How the quantum oscillator differs

> [!remark] Remark: Three departures from the classical oscillator
> - **Zero-point energy.** The lowest energy is $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$, not $0$: a particle at rest at the bottom would have sharp position and sharp momentum. The uncertainty principle alone gives this bound, for any state. Since $\langle p^2\rangle = \sigma_p^2 + \langle p\rangle^2 \ge \sigma_p^2$ and likewise $\langle x^2\rangle \ge \sigma_x^2$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-2|Theorem §A3.4.2]]), and $\alpha + \beta \ge 2\sqrt{\alpha\beta}$ for $\alpha, \beta \ge 0$,
>
> $$
> \langle E\rangle = \frac{\langle p^2\rangle}{2m} + \frac12m\omega^2\langle x^2\rangle \ge \frac{\sigma_p^2}{2m} + \frac12m\omega^2\sigma_x^2 \ge \omega\,\sigma_x\sigma_p \ge \frac12\hbar\omega
> $$
>
> by [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]. The Gaussian ground state reaches the minimum: it has $\sigma_x\sigma_p = \hbar/2$ ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^ex-a3-5-1|Example §A3.5.1]]) and equal average kinetic and potential energies.
> - **Beyond the turning points.** The ground-state turning points are $x = \pm a$ (where $\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2 = \tfrac12\hbar\omega$), and $\int_{|x| > a}|\psi_0|^2dx = \mathrm{erfc}(1) = 0.157$: about $16\%$ of the time the particle is found where a classical one could never be.
> - **Where it is found.** In the ground state the density peaks at the centre; a classical oscillator is most likely found near its turning points, where it moves slowly ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-1|Theorem §A4.5.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.5 · PHY 284, handout "Simple harmonic oscillator"*

^rem-a4-5-2

> [!remark] Remark: Equal spacing, photons and Planck
> $E_{n+1} - E_n = \hbar\omega = hf$ for every $n$, unlike the box, whose spacing grows with $n$. An oscillating charge with these levels can absorb or emit energy only in multiples of $hf$. Moreover (stated without proof in Tipler) $\int\psi_n\,x\,\psi_m\,dx = 0$ unless $m = n \pm 1$, so radiation changes $n$ by one step (the **selection rule** $\Delta n = \pm1$; compare [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^rem-a4-3-3|Remark: Why atoms radiate]]), and the emitted photon has exactly the classical frequency $f$ of the oscillator. This is Planck's hypothesis for the cavity oscillators ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]]), now derived, with one correction: the energies are $(n + \tfrac12)hf$, not $nhf$. Only differences matter for radiation, so Planck's law is unaffected.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.5, eq. (7.58) · Tipler §6-5*

^rem-a4-5-3

> [!remark] Remark: Equipartition, and frozen vibrations
> Classically each oscillator in thermal equilibrium has mean energy $k_BT$, half kinetic and half potential ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|TH Theorem §A2.3.2]]). A quantum oscillator cannot take up energy in amounts smaller than $\hbar\omega$. When $k_BT \gg \hbar\omega$ the steps are negligible and equipartition holds; when $k_BT \ll \hbar\omega$ collisions almost never supply a whole quantum, the oscillator stays in its ground state, and the vibration contributes nothing to the heat capacity. This is why molecular vibrations are "frozen" at room temperature ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^rem-a2-3-2|TH Remark: Frozen degrees of freedom]]): $\hbar\omega = 0.37\ \mathrm{eV}$ for HCl ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^ex-a4-5-1|Example §A4.5.1]]) against $k_BT = 0.026\ \mathrm{eV}$. Planck's mean energy $\hbar\omega/(e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT} - 1)$ interpolates between the two regimes ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 25 slides (Planck's mean energy); S22 final, problem 1c*

^rem-a4-5-4

> [!remark] Remark: Correspondence at large n
> The classical turning points of $E_n$ are at $A_n = \sqrt{2n + 1}\,a$, growing as $\sqrt n$. Already $|\psi_1|^2$ vanishes at the centre and peaks toward the turning points. For large $n$, $|\psi_n|^2$ oscillates rapidly inside $\pm A_n$, and its local average follows the classical density of [[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-1|Theorem §A4.5.1]], as the figure shows for $n = 12$; only a thin tail spills past the turning points. This is the correspondence principle ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^pr-a2-2-6|Principle §A2.2.6]]) once more.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.5, Example 7.10 · Tipler §6-5*

^rem-a4-5-5

> [!remark] Remark: Superpositions oscillate at the classical frequency
> A general state is $\Psi = \sum_n c_n\psi_ne^{-i(n + 1/2)\omega t}$ ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]). After one classical period $T = 2\pi/\omega$ every term has turned by $(2n + 1)\pi$, so $\Psi(x, t + T) = -\Psi(x, t)$: every probability density and expectation value repeats exactly with the classical period. Wave packets in an oscillator therefore swing back and forth without spreading out over time, unlike in a box, whose frequencies $E_n/\hbar \propto n^2$ are not multiples of one frequency.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 31 slides; handout "Simple harmonic oscillator"*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.5.1: Vibrations of the HCl Molecule
> Chlorine is 35 times heavier than hydrogen, so treat Cl as fixed and the H atom ($m = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}$ kg) as an oscillator. The lowest-frequency infrared line of HCl is at $f = 8.88 \times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{Hz}$, emitted when the molecule drops by one vibrational level. The level spacing is
>
> $$
> \Delta E = hf = (4.14 \times 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{eV\cdot s})(8.88 \times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{Hz}) = 0.368\ \mathrm{eV},
> $$
>
> and the bond's force constant is
>
> $$
> k = m\omega^2 = m(2\pi f)^2 = (1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg})(2\pi \times 8.88 \times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})^2 = 520\ \mathrm{N/m},
> $$
>
> the stiffness of a strong laboratory spring, acting on a single atom. Vibrational spacings of tenths of an eV put molecular vibration spectra in the infrared; the zero-point energy $\tfrac12hf = 0.18$ eV remains even at absolute zero.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 7.11*

^ex-a4-5-1

> [!example] Example §A4.5.2: A Superposition That Swings
> An oscillator starts in $\Psi(x, 0) = A[3\psi_0(x) + 4\psi_1(x)]$.
> - **Normalization.** $|A|^2(9 + 16) = 1$, so $A = \tfrac15$ ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]).
> - **Energy.** A measurement gives $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$ with probability $\tfrac{9}{25}$ or $\tfrac32\hbar\omega$ with probability $\tfrac{16}{25}$; $\langle E\rangle = \tfrac{9}{25}\cdot\tfrac12\hbar\omega + \tfrac{16}{25}\cdot\tfrac32\hbar\omega = \tfrac{57}{50}\hbar\omega = 1.14\,\hbar\omega$.
> - **Motion.** $\Psi(x, t) = \tfrac35\psi_0e^{-i\omega t/2} + \tfrac45\psi_1e^{-3i\omega t/2}$. With $\langle x\rangle_0 = \langle x\rangle_1 = 0$ (each $|\psi_n|^2$ is even) and
>
> $$
> x_{01} = \int x\,\psi_0\psi_1\,dx = \frac{2}{\sqrt2\,a^2\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^2e^{-x^2/a^2}dx = \frac{2}{\sqrt2\,a^2\sqrt\pi}\cdot\frac{\sqrt\pi\,a^3}{2} = \frac{a}{\sqrt2},
> $$
>
> [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-5|Theorem §A4.3.5]] gives
>
> $$
> \langle x\rangle = 2\cdot\frac35\cdot\frac45\cdot\frac{a}{\sqrt2}\cos\omega t = \frac{24}{25}\,\frac{a}{\sqrt2}\cos\omega t = 0.68\,a\cos\omega t, \qquad \langle p\rangle = m\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = -\frac{24}{25}\,\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt2\,a}\sin\omega t ,
> $$
>
> using $m\omega a = \hbar/a$. The mean position oscillates exactly like a classical oscillator, at the classical $\omega$ ($= (E_1 - E_0)/\hbar$), and $\langle p\rangle$ runs a quarter period ahead of it, as $p$ does for a classical oscillator.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 10, problem 2 (the solutions are handwritten and not available; solved here)*

^ex-a4-5-2

> [!example] Example §A4.5.3: Freezing a Playground Swing
> How cold must a child's swing be for it to sit in its quantum ground state? Say it swings once per second, $f \approx 1\ \mathrm{Hz}$, so the level spacing is $hf = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}$ J. The thermal energy must be below one quantum:
>
> $$
> k_BT \lesssim hf \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T \lesssim \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm J}{1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}} = 5 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm K .
> $$
>
> No macroscopic oscillator is ever that cold, so a swing always sits at an astronomically large $n$, and its quantization is invisible (compare [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^ex-a1-1-1|Example §A1.1.1]]). Molecular vibrations are the opposite case ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^rem-a4-5-4|Remark: Equipartition, and frozen vibrations]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, S22 final, problem 1c (checked against the posted solution)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The ground-state normalization and $x_{01}$ use the Gaussian integral $\int e^{-u^2}du = \sqrt\pi$ ([[§36 Improper Integrals#^ex-36-4|451 Ex. §36.4]]) and, for $\int u^2e^{-u^2}du = \sqrt\pi/2$, one integration by parts ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]).
> - The Hermite functions $\psi_n$ form an orthonormal basis of $L^2(\mathbb R)$ ([[§24 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^def-24-4|556 Def. §24.4]]); the full solution, by the series method or by ladder operators, is level B ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]], [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]]; completeness, [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]).
