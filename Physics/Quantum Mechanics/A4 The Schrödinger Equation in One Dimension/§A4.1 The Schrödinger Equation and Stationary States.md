Chapter A3 gave the wave function a meaning: $|\Psi|^2$ is a probability density ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability]]). What it did not give is a rule for how $\Psi$ changes in time, the role that Newton's second law plays for a classical particle. That rule is the Schrödinger equation. When the potential energy does not depend on time it has special solutions, the stationary states, which have a definite energy; the rest of the chapter finds them for one potential after another.

## The equation of motion

> [!remark] Remark: Where the equation comes from
> The equation cannot be derived, only made plausible. A free plane wave $\Psi = Ae^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ with the de Broglie relations $p = \hbar k$, $E = \hbar\omega$ ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves]]) satisfies
>
> $$
> -i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} = p\,\Psi, \qquad i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = E\,\Psi :
> $$
>
> a derivative in $x$ brings down the momentum, a derivative in $t$ the energy. For light, the classical wave equation turns into $\omega = ck$, that is $E = pc$, the energy–momentum relation of a massless particle. Running the argument backwards for a massive, non-relativistic particle, one asks for a linear wave equation that reproduces $E = p^2/2m + U$; replacing $E$ and $p^2$ by the derivatives above gives the equation below. Two features are forced: it relates the *first* time derivative to the *second* space derivative, and it contains $i$, because a real $\cos(kx - \omega t)$ turns into a sine under $\partial/\partial t$ but stays a cosine under $\partial^2/\partial x^2$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 26 slides; handout "Schrödinger equation" · UP3 §7.3*

^rem-a4-1-1

> [!principle] Principle §A4.1.1: The Schrödinger Equation
> The wave function $\Psi(x, t)$ of a particle of mass $m$ moving along the $x$ axis with potential energy $U(x, t)$ obeys
>
> $$
> -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{\partial^2\Psi(x, t)}{\partial x^2} + U(x, t)\,\Psi(x, t) = i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\Psi(x, t)}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> *Domain: one particle, non-relativistic (kinetic energy $\ll mc^2$), spin ignored, potential energy given as a function of position and time. In three dimensions $\partial^2/\partial x^2$ becomes the sum over $x, y, z$ ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom]]).*
> *Source: UP3 §7.3, eq. (7.23) · Tipler §6-1, eq. (6-6)*

^pr-a4-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: What the principle says
> - **It is the quantum analogue of $\vec F = m\vec a$** ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]]). Once the potential energy, equivalently the force $F = -\partial U/\partial x$ ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|CM Theorem §A4.5.2]]), is specified, the equation determines the motion. What it determines is not a trajectory $x(t)$ but $\Psi(x, t)$, from which probabilities follow by the Born rule ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability]]).
> - **First order in time.** Knowing $\Psi(x, 0)$ at every $x$ fixes $\Psi(x, t)$ at all later times. Classically one needs position *and* velocity; here the whole initial function plays that role.
> - **Its justification is experimental.** Like Newton's laws it is postulated and judged by its predictions for atoms, molecules, solids and nuclei. Like them it fails at speeds near $c$; the relativistic wave equation of the electron is Dirac's (1928).
> - **$\Psi$ is complex** and is not itself measured; only $|\Psi|^2 = \Psi^*\Psi$ and quantities built from it are.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-1 · UP3 §7.3*

^rem-a4-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.2: Superposition of Solutions
> If $\Psi_1$ and $\Psi_2$ solve the Schrödinger equation for the same $U(x, t)$, then so does $a_1\Psi_1 + a_2\Psi_2$ for any complex constants $a_1, a_2$; likewise for any finite sum.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-1 · UP3 §7.3*

^thm-a4-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Every term of the equation is linear in $\Psi$: derivatives of a sum are sums of derivatives and constants pass through them, and $U\Psi$ is linear. Substituting $a_1\Psi_1 + a_2\Psi_2$, the left side becomes $a_1(\text{left side for }\Psi_1) + a_2(\text{left side for }\Psi_2)$ and the same for the right side; each bracket balances, so the sum does.

^der-a4-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]], [[§4 Partial Derivatives#^def-4-1|452 Def. §4.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why linearity matters
> Linearity is what makes matter waves interfere: the amplitudes of two possibilities add, and the probability $|\Psi_1 + \Psi_2|^2$ contains the cross term $2\,\mathrm{Re}(\Psi_1^*\Psi_2)$ ([[§A3.2 Wave–Particle Duality]]). It also lets localized wave packets be built from plane waves ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets]]) and general motions from stationary states ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States]]).

^rem-a4-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.3: The Free-Particle Plane Wave
> For a free particle ($U = $ const, taken as $0$) the plane wave
>
> $$
> \Psi(x, t) = A\,e^{i(kx - \omega t)}
> $$
>
> solves the Schrödinger equation exactly when $\hbar\omega = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, that is $E = p^2/2m$ with $p = \hbar k$, $E = \hbar\omega$. The real waves $A\sin(kx - \omega t)$ and $A\cos(kx - \omega t)$ are not solutions.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.3, eqs. (7.26)–(7.27) · Tipler §6-1, eq. (6-7)*

^thm-a4-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\partial^2\Psi/\partial x^2 = (ik)^2\Psi = -k^2\Psi$ and $\partial\Psi/\partial t = -i\omega\Psi$, so the equation reads $\tfrac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\Psi = i\hbar(-i\omega)\Psi = \hbar\omega\Psi$, true for all $x, t$ iff $\hbar\omega = \hbar^2k^2/2m$. For $\Psi = A\cos(kx - \omega t)$ the left side is $\tfrac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}A\cos(kx - \omega t)$ while the right side is $i\hbar\omega A\sin(kx - \omega t)$; no choice of constants makes a cosine equal a sine at all $x$ and $t$. The sine fails the same way.

^der-a4-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]], [[§1 Rⁿ and Cⁿ#^ladr-1-1|LADR Def. 1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: A plane wave is not a particle
> $|Ae^{i(kx - \omega t)}|^2 = |A|^2$ is the same everywhere, so $\int|\Psi|^2\,dx$ diverges and the plane wave cannot be normalized. It describes a particle of sharp momentum and completely unknown position, the limit $\Delta x \to \infty$ of the uncertainty principle ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle]]). A real free particle is a superposition of plane waves, a wave packet ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets]], [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling]]).

^cau-a4-1-1

## Separation of variables and stationary states

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.4: Separation of Variables
> Let the potential energy $U(x)$ be independent of time. A product $\Psi(x, t) = \psi(x)\,\varphi(t)$, not identically zero, solves the Schrödinger equation if and only if, for some constant $E$,
>
> $$
> \varphi(t) = e^{-iEt/\hbar} \quad(\text{up to a constant factor}), \qquad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + U(x)\,\psi(x) = E\,\psi(x) .
> $$
>
> The second equation is the **time-independent Schrödinger equation**. The constant $E$ is the energy of the particle: the time factor oscillates at $\omega = E/\hbar$, the de Broglie frequency of a particle of energy $E$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.3, eqs. (7.28)–(7.30) · Tipler §6-1, eqs. (6-10)–(6-18)*

^thm-a4-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Substitute $\Psi = \psi(x)\varphi(t)$; the partial derivatives become ordinary ones:
>
> $$
> -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\psi''(x)\,\varphi(t) + U(x)\,\psi(x)\,\varphi(t) = i\hbar\,\psi(x)\,\varphi'(t) .
> $$
>
> Divide by $\psi\varphi$ where it is nonzero:
>
> $$
> -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{\psi''(x)}{\psi(x)} + U(x) = i\hbar\,\frac{\varphi'(t)}{\varphi(t)} .
> $$
>
> The left side depends only on $x$, the right side only on $t$. Changing $t$ at fixed $x$ cannot change the left side, so the right side is a constant; call it $E$ (the separation constant). The right side then gives $\varphi' = -(iE/\hbar)\varphi$, solved by $\varphi = e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ times a constant, which can be absorbed into $\psi$. The left side, multiplied by $\psi$, is the time-independent equation. Conversely, if $\psi$ solves it, the product with $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ makes both sides of the full equation equal to $E\psi\varphi$. The factor $e^{-iEt/\hbar} = \cos(Et/\hbar) - i\sin(Et/\hbar)$ oscillates at $\omega = E/\hbar$; comparing with $E = \hbar\omega$ identifies $E$ as the energy. For the free particle it is $\hbar^2k^2/2m$, the kinetic energy ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]]).

^der-a4-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]], [[§4 Partial Derivatives#^def-4-1|452 Def. §4.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A4.1.1: Stationary State; Energy Eigenfunction
> A **stationary state** is a solution of the separated form
>
> $$
> \Psi(x, t) = \psi(x)\,e^{-iEt/\hbar},
> $$
>
> where $\psi$ is an acceptable solution ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]]) of the time-independent Schrödinger equation with energy $E$. The spatial factor $\psi$ is also called an **energy eigenfunction** (or eigenstate) and $E$ its **energy eigenvalue**. Notation: capital $\Psi(x, t)$ for the full wave function, lower-case $\psi(x)$ for the spatial factor.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.3–7.4 · Tipler §6-1*

^def-a4-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.5: Properties of Stationary States
> In a stationary state $\Psi = \psi\,e^{-iEt/\hbar}$:
> - the probability density does not change in time, $|\Psi(x, t)|^2 = |\psi(x)|^2$, so $\Psi$ is normalized iff $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi|^2\,dx = 1$;
> - expectation values of position, of any function of $x$, and of momentum are constant in time; if $\psi$ is real, $\langle p\rangle = 0$;
> - the energy is sharp: $\langle E\rangle = E$ and $\langle E^2\rangle = E^2$, so the spread $\sigma_E = \sqrt{\langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2}$ is zero.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-1, eq. (6-19) · PHY 284, handout "Stationary states"; class 26 slides*

^thm-a4-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $|e^{-iEt/\hbar}| = 1$, so $\Psi^*\Psi = \psi^*e^{+iEt/\hbar}\,\psi\,e^{-iEt/\hbar} = \psi^*\psi$. In an expectation value $\int\Psi^*\,\hat Q\,\Psi\,dx$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability]]) with $\hat Q = f(x)$ or $\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dx$, the operator does not act on $t$, so the two phase factors again cancel. For real normalizable $\psi$,
>
> $$
> \langle p\rangle = -i\hbar\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi\,\psi'\,dx = -i\hbar\Bigl[\tfrac12\psi^2\Bigr]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> since $\psi \to 0$ at $\pm\infty$; a real number cannot equal a nonzero imaginary one anyway. For the energy, use the energy operator $i\hbar\,\partial/\partial t$: $i\hbar\,\partial\Psi/\partial t = E\Psi$, so $\langle E\rangle = E\int|\Psi|^2dx = E$; applying it twice gives $E^2\Psi$, so $\langle E^2\rangle = E^2$.

^der-a4-1-5

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-2|451 Def. §36.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Stationary does not mean motionless
> The wave function of a stationary state is not frozen: every point turns in the complex plane at the same angular frequency $E/\hbar$, all in step, like the points of a classical standing wave that oscillate in phase ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^def-a2-6-1|WO Def. §A2.6.1]]). What does not change is everything measurable: $|\Psi|^2$, all expectation values and the energy. A state of sharp energy has an unlimited time spread, consistent with $\Delta E\,\Delta t \ge \hbar/2$ ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle]]). Change in time, and with it emission and absorption of light, needs a superposition of different energies ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 26 and 28 slides*

^rem-a4-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: Only energy differences are measurable
> Adding a constant $U_0$ to the potential energy shifts every $E$ by $U_0$ and multiplies every stationary state by the common phase $e^{-iU_0t/\hbar}$, which cancels in every $|\Psi|^2$. So the zero of $U$ can be chosen at will (the bottom of a well, the region far away, ...), as in classical mechanics; frequencies of emitted light depend only on energy differences.

^rem-a4-1-5

> [!remark] Remark: Operator form
> With the momentum operator $\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dx$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability]]), the time-independent equation reads $\hat H\psi = E\psi$, where $\hat H = \hat p^2/2m + U(x)$ is the energy (Hamiltonian) operator, the classical energy $p^2/2m + U$ with $p$ replaced by $\hat p$. A stationary state is an eigenfunction of $\hat H$, whence the name. The same recipe, applied to the classical energy of several particles in three dimensions, gives their Schrödinger equation.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-4*

^rem-a4-1-6

## Acceptable solutions

> [!definition] Definition §A4.1.2: Acceptable (Well-Behaved) Wave Function
> A solution $\psi(x)$ of the time-independent Schrödinger equation is **acceptable** if
> 1. $\psi$ is finite, single-valued and continuous everywhere;
> 2. $d\psi/dx$ is continuous everywhere, except at points where $U$ jumps by an infinite amount;
> 3. $\psi \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$ fast enough that $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi|^2\,dx$ is finite (for bound states).
>
> In a region where $U = \infty$, $\psi = 0$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.3 · Tipler §6-1*

^def-a4-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Why these conditions
> - $|\psi|^2$ is a probability density, so $\psi$ may not jump or be multivalued, and it must be normalizable ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability]]).
> - The equation gives $\psi'' = \tfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}(U - E)\psi$. Where $U$ is finite, even if it jumps, $\psi''$ is finite, so $\psi'$ cannot jump. At an infinite jump (a rigid wall) this argument fails, and $\psi'$ may have a kink.
> - In practice these conditions are the **boundary conditions** of each problem: solve the equation piece by piece where $U$ has a simple form, then join the pieces with $\psi$ and $\psi'$ continuous and discard solutions that blow up. That this selects only certain energies is the origin of energy quantization.
> - Scattering problems (beams of free particles) use plane waves that violate condition 3 as idealizations ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling]]).

^rem-a4-1-7

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.6: Solutions Where the Potential Energy Is Constant
> On an interval where $U(x) = U_0$ is constant, the time-independent Schrödinger equation has the general solution
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> E > U_0:&\quad \psi = A\sin kx + B\cos kx = Ce^{ikx} + De^{-ikx}, &\quad k &= \frac{\sqrt{2m(E - U_0)}}{\hbar},\\
> E < U_0:&\quad \psi = Ce^{\alpha x} + De^{-\alpha x}, &\quad \alpha &= \frac{\sqrt{2m(U_0 - E)}}{\hbar} .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> Where the particle would be classically allowed ($E > U_0$), $\psi$ oscillates with the de Broglie wave number of the kinetic energy $E - U_0$; where it would be classically forbidden ($E < U_0$), $\psi$ is a combination of growing and decaying exponentials.
>
> *Source: UP3 §7.4, eq. (7.35); §7.6, eqs. (7.68), (7.74) · PHY 284, handout "Finite well"*

^thm-a4-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The equation becomes $\psi'' = -k^2\psi$ if $E > U_0$ and $\psi'' = \alpha^2\psi$ if $E < U_0$. Each is a linear second-order equation with constant coefficients, whose solution space is two-dimensional; $\sin kx$, $\cos kx$ (equivalently $e^{\pm ikx}$, by $e^{\pm ikx} = \cos kx \pm i\sin kx$) solve the first, $e^{\pm\alpha x}$ the second, as differentiating twice shows, and in each case the two are independent.

^der-a4-1-6

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the shape of ψ from the curvature
> Even when $U$ varies, $\psi''/\psi = \tfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}\bigl(U(x) - E\bigr)$ fixes the curvature. Where $E > U(x)$, $\psi''$ has the sign opposite to $\psi$: the graph bends back toward the axis and oscillates, with shorter wavelength where the kinetic energy $E - U$ is larger. Where $E < U(x)$, $\psi''$ has the sign of $\psi$: the graph bends away from the axis and, unless it is aimed exactly right, runs off to $\pm\infty$. For a well (allowed in the middle, forbidden outside) only special energies give a $\psi$ that dies away on both sides; the figure shows this for the oscillator potential of [[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator]]. This qualitative argument already shows why bound particles have discrete energies.
>
> *Source: Tipler §6-3 · UP3 §7.4*

## Example

> [!example] Example §A4.1.1: Reading the Potential from a Stationary State
> A particle of mass $m$ has the stationary state $\psi(x) = N e^{-x^2/2a^2}$ with energy $E$. Which potential energy produces it? Solve the time-independent equation for $U$:
>
> $$
> U(x) = E + \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{\psi''}{\psi}, \qquad \psi' = -\frac{x}{a^2}\psi, \quad \psi'' = \Bigl(\frac{x^2}{a^4} - \frac{1}{a^2}\Bigr)\psi ,
> $$
>
> so $U(x) = E - \dfrac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2} + \dfrac{\hbar^2}{2ma^4}\,x^2$. With the zero of $U$ at the minimum, $E = \hbar^2/2ma^2$ and $U = \tfrac12 m\omega^2x^2$ with $\omega = \hbar/ma^2$: a harmonic oscillator, of which the Gaussian is the ground state with $E = \tfrac12\hbar\omega$ ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator]]). The single-humped, nodeless shape and the decay on both sides are what a lowest state in a single well looks like; a narrower Gaussian (smaller $a$) needs a stiffer well and has a higher energy.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, S22 final, bonus problem; Final practice 2023, problem 6 (the question of which potential gives a wave function); worked here*

^ex-a4-1-1

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The equation is a linear partial differential equation ([[§4 Partial Derivatives#^def-4-1|452 Def. §4.1]]); its solutions form a complex vector space ([[§1 Rⁿ and Cⁿ#^ladr-1-1|LADR Def. 1.1]]), which is what the superposition theorem says.
> - Normalizability is the convergence of an improper integral ([[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-2|451 Def. §36.2]]); at level B the acceptable wave functions are elements of the space $L^2$ of square-integrable functions ([[§14 The Function Spaces Lᵖ(Ω)#^def-14-1|556 Def. §14.1]]), a Hilbert space ([[§17 Cauchy–Schwarz and the Induced Norm#^def-17-1|556 Def. §17.1]]), and the stationary-state equation becomes the eigenvalue problem of the Hamiltonian (Chapter B1, planned).
