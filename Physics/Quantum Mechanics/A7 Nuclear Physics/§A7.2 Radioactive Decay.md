Most nuclides are unstable: they turn into other nuclides by emitting radiation. Becquerel found the effect in 1896 in uranium salts. Its nuclear origin shows in three ways: it does not depend on the chemical compound, it is unaffected by temperature and pressure, and the radiation carries MeV energies, far beyond electron transitions. This section derives the exponential decay law from the assumption that decay is random, describes the three kinds of radiation and the energy bookkeeping of each, and uses decay as a clock.

## The decay law

> [!definition] Definition §A7.2.1: Radioactive Decay; Parent and Daughter
> **Radioactive decay** is the spontaneous transformation of an unstable nucleus, the **parent**, into another nuclear state, the **daughter**, with the emission of radiation (particles or photons). All nuclei beyond bismuth ($Z > 83$) are radioactive ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^def-a7-1-4|Def. §A7.1.4]]), and so are many lighter ones.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.3–10.4; Tipler §11-2*

^def-a7-2-1

> [!model] Model §A7.2.1: Decay at Constant Probability
> - *Assumes:* each nucleus of a given radioactive species decays in any short interval $dt$ with probability $\lambda\,dt$, where $\lambda$ is the same for all such nuclei, does not depend on how long the nucleus has existed, and is independent of what the other nuclei do. Because the electrons shield the nucleus and nuclear energies are MeV, $\lambda$ does not depend on the chemical compound, temperature or pressure.
> - *Explains:* the exponential decrease of activity found by Rutherford in 1900, and the random scatter of counts from a source.
> - *Fails:* only in small ways. Electron capture uses an atomic electron, so its rate depends slightly on the electron density at the nucleus (very high pressure can change it).
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-3; UP3 §10.3*

^mod-a7-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §A7.2.2: Decay Constant; Activity; Becquerel, Curie
> The constant $\lambda$ of [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^mod-a7-2-1|Model §A7.2.1]] is the **decay constant** (probability of decay per unit time). The **activity** of a sample is its decay rate, $A = -dN/dt$, where $N(t)$ is the number of undecayed nuclei. It is measured in **becquerels**, $1\ \mathrm{Bq} = 1$ decay/s, or in **curies**, $1\ \mathrm{Ci} = 3.70\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{Bq}$, originally the activity of 1 g of radium-226.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.3, eq. (10.17); Tipler §11-3, eqs. (11-23)–(11-24)*

^def-a7-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.2.2: Radioactive Decay Law
> A sample of $N_0$ nuclei at $t = 0$ contains, on average,
>
> $$
> N(t) = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t}
> $$
>
> undecayed nuclei at time $t$, and its activity is
>
> $$
> A(t) = \lambda N(t) = A_0\,e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad A_0 = \lambda N_0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.3, eqs. (10.7)–(10.18); Tipler §11-3, eqs. (11-17)–(11-19)*

^thm-a7-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Of the $N$ nuclei present at time $t$, each decays in the next $dt$ with probability $\lambda\,dt$, independently ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^mod-a7-2-1|Model §A7.2.1]]). The expected number of decays is $\lambda N\,dt$, and each one lowers $N$ by one, so
>
> $$
> \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N .
> $$
>
> Then $\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl(N e^{\lambda t}\bigr) = \Bigl(\dfrac{dN}{dt} + \lambda N\Bigr)e^{\lambda t} = 0$, so $N e^{\lambda t}$ is constant, equal to its value $N_0$ at $t = 0$, and $N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}$. The activity is $-dN/dt = \lambda N$ ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^def-a7-2-2|Def. §A7.2.2]]). Here $N$ is an expected value: actual counts scatter about it by roughly $\sqrt N$, which is negligible for the $10^{20}$ nuclei of a macroscopic sample.

^der-a7-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^mod-a7-2-1|Model §A7.2.1]], [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^def-a7-2-2|Def. §A7.2.2]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]

> [!definition] Definition §A7.2.3: Half-Life; Mean Lifetime
> The **half-life** $T_{1/2}$ is the time in which half of the nuclei present decay. The **mean lifetime** $\bar T$ (Tipler writes $\tau$) is the average time a nucleus exists before it decays.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.3, eqs. (10.12)–(10.16); Tipler §11-3*

^def-a7-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.2.3: Half-Life, Lifetime and Decay Constant
>
> $$
> T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda}, \qquad \bar T = \frac1\lambda, \qquad T_{1/2} = (\ln 2)\,\bar T .
> $$
>
> After $n$ half-lives, $N = N_0/2^n$; in general $N = N_0\,(1/2)^{t/T_{1/2}}$. The activity halves in each half-life and falls by the factor $e$ in each mean lifetime, and
>
> $$
> \ln A = \ln A_0 - \lambda t ,
> $$
>
> so a plot of $\ln A$ against $t$ is a straight line of slope $-\lambda$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.3, eqs. (10.12)–(10.20); Tipler §11-3, eqs. (11-20)–(11-22)*

^thm-a7-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $N = N_0/2$ at $t = T_{1/2}$ in [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]]: $\tfrac12 = e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}}$, so $\lambda T_{1/2} = \ln 2$. Then $e^{-\lambda t} = \bigl(e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}}\bigr)^{t/T_{1/2}} = (1/2)^{t/T_{1/2}}$. Taking logarithms of $A = A_0e^{-\lambda t}$ gives the straight line.
>
> For the mean lifetime: of the $N_0$ nuclei, $\lambda N\,dt = \lambda N_0e^{-\lambda t}\,dt$ decay between $t$ and $t + dt$, so the fraction with lifetime in that interval is $f(t)\,dt = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt$ (and $\int_0^\infty f\,dt = 1$). The average lifetime is, integrating by parts,
>
> $$
> \bar T = \int_0^\infty t\,\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt = \Bigl[-t\,e^{-\lambda t}\Bigr]_0^\infty + \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\,dt = 0 + \frac1\lambda .
> $$

^der-a7-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]], [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^def-a7-2-3|Def. §A7.2.3]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-1|451 Def. §36.1]]

![[ph-qm-a7-2-1.svg]]
*The decay law $N/N_0 = e^{-\lambda t}$ (blue) with time in units of the half-life. Each half-life halves what is left (dots at $\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac1{16}$); after one mean lifetime $\bar T = T_{1/2}/\ln 2 \approx 1.44\,T_{1/2}$ the fraction $1/e \approx 0.37$ remains (red). The activity follows the same curve.*

> [!caution] Caution: Three time scales, and no ageing
> - $\lambda$, $\bar T = 1/\lambda$ and $T_{1/2} = 0.693/\lambda$ are different numbers. A fit of counts to $N_0e^{-t/\tau}$ returns the mean lifetime $\tau$; the half-life is $\tau\ln 2$.
> - A nucleus does not age. One that has survived ten half-lives still decays in the next half-life with probability ½. The law says nothing about when a given nucleus will decay, only how many of many will.

^cau-a7-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Decay is random: counting statistics
> Counting a source repeatedly for equal intervals gives numbers that scatter about their mean $\mu$ with standard deviation close to $\sqrt\mu$, distributed nearly as a Gaussian when $\mu$ is large. In the PHY 286 radioactivity lab, sixty one-second counts of a $^{60}$Co source gave $\mu = 127.5$ and $\sigma = 11.7$, against $\sqrt\mu = 11.3$, with 42 of the 60 counts (70%) within $\mu \pm \sigma$, close to the 68% expected for a Gaussian. Doubling the interval to 2 s doubled the mean (about 251.5 in three runs) while $\sigma$ grew only to 14.6–18.6, around $\sqrt{252} = 15.9$: the relative scatter $1/\sqrt\mu$ shrinks as more decays are counted.
>
> *Source: PHY 286, Radioactivity lab (the user's analysis)*

^rem-a7-2-1

> [!example] Example §A7.2.1: The Half-Life of Indium-116
> In the PHY 286 lab a foil of stable $^{115}$In is exposed to neutrons; some nuclei capture one and become radioactive $^{116}$In. A Geiger–Müller tube then counts its decays in 180 intervals of 30 s (90 min), and the counts per interval are fitted to $N(t) = N_0e^{-t/\tau} + B$, where $B$ is the background from cosmic rays and natural radioactivity. With $B$ fixed at the separately measured 10.6 counts per interval, the user's fit gave $N_0 = 226 \pm 2$ and $\tau = 5181 \pm 115$ s, so by [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-3|Theorem §A7.2.3]]
>
> $$
> T_{1/2} = \tau\ln 2 = (5181\ \mathrm s)(0.693) = 3.59\times10^3\ \mathrm s = 59.9 \pm 1.3\ \mathrm{min}.
> $$
>
> The fitted $\tau$ is the mean lifetime, the "1/e time" of the lab manual, not the half-life; the half-life is the shorter $\tau\ln 2$. The treatment of the background matters. With $B = 0$ the fit gave $\tau = 5587 \pm 122$ s, 8% longer: by the end of the run ($t \approx 1.04\tau$) the signal is down to about 80 counts per interval, so the constant background is a sizeable part of the late counts and, if ignored, looks like a slower decay. With $B$ left free, $\tau = 5000 \pm 775$ s: consistent with the fixed-background fit but seven times less precise, because a 90-minute run is too short to tell a constant from a slow exponential. Measuring the background separately and fixing it gives the best result.
>
> *Source: PHY 286, Radioactivity lab (the user's analysis); lab manual for the set-up*

## Alpha, beta and gamma decay

> [!definition] Definition §A7.2.4: Alpha, Beta and Gamma Decay
> Writing the electron as $^{\ \,0}_{-1}\mathrm e$ and the positron as $^{\ \,0}_{+1}\mathrm e$ so that the sub- and superscripts balance:
> - **Alpha decay**: the nucleus emits a $^4$He nucleus, $\ ^A_Z\mathrm X \to {}^{A-4}_{Z-2}\mathrm X' + {}^4_2\mathrm{He}$.
> - **Beta-minus decay**: a neutron in the nucleus becomes a proton, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino, $\ ^A_Z\mathrm X \to {}^{\ \ \,A}_{Z+1}\mathrm X' + {}^{\ \,0}_{-1}\mathrm e + \bar\nu_e$; for a free neutron, $n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu_e$.
> - **Beta-plus decay**: a proton in the nucleus becomes a neutron, emitting a positron and a neutrino, $\ ^A_Z\mathrm X \to {}^{\ \ \,A}_{Z-1}\mathrm X' + {}^{\ \,0}_{+1}\mathrm e + \nu_e$. A free proton cannot do this, being lighter than the neutron.
> - **Electron capture**: a proton captures an inner (usually K-shell) atomic electron, $\ ^A_Z\mathrm X + {}^{\ \,0}_{-1}\mathrm e \to {}^{\ \ \,A}_{Z-1}\mathrm X' + \nu_e$.
> - **Gamma decay**: a nucleus in an excited state (marked $^*$) drops to a lower state by emitting a photon, $\ ^A_Z\mathrm X^* \to {}^A_Z\mathrm X + \gamma$; neither $A$ nor $Z$ changes.
>
> The electron or positron of beta decay does not exist in the nucleus beforehand ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^rem-a7-1-1|Remark: Why there are no electrons in the nucleus]]); it is created in the decay, by the weak interaction ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^def-a8-2-2|Def. §A8.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.4, eqs. (10.21)–(10.24); Tipler §11-4, eqs. (11-38)–(11-44)*

^def-a7-2-4

> [!remark] Remark: The three radiations in matter
> Alpha rays barely pass a sheet of paper; beta rays penetrate about 3 mm of aluminium; gamma rays need centimetres of lead. A magnetic field bends α rays one way, $\beta^-$ the other, and leaves γ rays undeflected, which identified them as helium nuclei, electrons (or positrons) and high-energy photons. Alpha particles from one source have a single energy and hence a well-defined range; beta particles have a spread of energies up to an endpoint, so no sharp range. In the PHY 286 lab, beta rays from $^{90}$Sr passed 0.81 mm of aluminium but were stopped by 6.46 mm (the count fell to background), gamma rays from $^{60}$Co passed every absorber with a transmitted fraction falling with thickness, and $^{116}$In, whose radiation passed paper but neither aluminium nor lead, was identified as a beta emitter.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.4; PHY 286, Radioactivity lab manual and the user's analysis*

^rem-a7-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.2.4: Decay Energies from Atomic Masses
> The energy $Q$ released in a decay, shared as kinetic energy among the products, is, in terms of the neutral-atom masses $M_P$ of the parent and $M_D$ of the daughter,
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \alpha:&\quad Q = \bigl[M_P - M_D - M(^4\mathrm{He})\bigr]c^2, \\
> \beta^-:&\quad Q = \bigl[M_P - M_D\bigr]c^2, \\
> \beta^+:&\quad Q = \bigl[M_P - M_D - 2m_e\bigr]c^2, \\
> \text{electron capture}:&\quad Q = \bigl[M_P - M_D\bigr]c^2 .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> A decay mode is possible only if its $Q > 0$. In particular $\beta^+$ decay needs $M_P - M_D > 2m_e = 1.022\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$, while electron capture needs only $M_P > M_D$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-4, eqs. (11-37), (11-42), (11-43); UP3 Example 10.7*

^thm-a7-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Total energy is conserved ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|REL Principle §A3.2.3]]), so the kinetic energy released is the drop in rest energy, $Q = (\sum m_{\text{initial}} - \sum m_{\text{final}})c^2$ with nuclear masses. Write each nuclear mass as atomic mass minus electrons, $m_{\text{nuc}} = M - Zm_e$, neglecting electron binding energies (eV to keV) and the neutrino mass (below a few eV/$c^2$). For $\beta^-$ the daughter has $Z + 1$ protons:
>
> $$
> (M_P - Zm_e) - \bigl(M_D - (Z+1)m_e\bigr) - m_e = M_P - M_D .
> $$
>
> For $\beta^+$ the daughter has $Z - 1$: $(M_P - Zm_e) - \bigl(M_D - (Z-1)m_e\bigr) - m_e = M_P - M_D - 2m_e$. For electron capture an electron is consumed: $(M_P - Zm_e) + m_e - \bigl(M_D - (Z-1)m_e\bigr) = M_P - M_D$. For alpha decay, $(M_P - Zm_e) - \bigl(M_D - (Z-2)m_e\bigr) - \bigl(M(^4\mathrm{He}) - 2m_e\bigr) = M_P - M_D - M(^4\mathrm{He})$. A negative $Q$ would need energy that the parent at rest does not have.

^der-a7-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|REL Principle §A3.2.3]], [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^rem-a7-1-5|Remark: Use atomic masses throughout]], [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^def-a7-2-4|Def. §A7.2.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.2.5: Energy Sharing in Alpha Decay
> A parent at rest that emits an α particle (mass $M_\alpha$) leaving the daughter (mass $M_D$) in its ground state gives the α particle the definite kinetic energy
>
> $$
> K_\alpha = \frac{M_D}{M_D + M_\alpha}\,Q \approx \frac{A - 4}{A}\,Q ,
> $$
>
> and the recoiling daughter the rest, $K_D = Q - K_\alpha$. Decays to excited states of the daughter give α particles of correspondingly lower, again definite, energies: the α spectrum consists of sharp lines.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-4, Fig. 11-19; UP3 §10.4, Example 10.7*

^thm-a7-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The total momentum is zero before the decay and so after it ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|CM Theorem §A5.2.2]]): the α particle and the daughter fly apart with equal and opposite momenta of magnitude $p$. Since $Q$ (a few MeV) is far below $M_\alpha c^2 = 3727$ MeV, kinetic energies are classical, $K = p^2/2M$. Hence $K_\alpha/K_D = M_D/M_\alpha$, and with $K_\alpha + K_D = Q$ this gives $K_\alpha = QM_D/(M_D + M_\alpha)$. With masses close to $A$ u for the parent, $M_D/(M_D + M_\alpha) \approx (A - 4)/A$. Two bodies share the energy in only one way; if the daughter is left excited by $E^*$, replace $Q$ by $Q - E^*$.

^der-a7-2-5

*Uses:* [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|CM Theorem §A5.2.2]], [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|CM Def. §A4.2.1]], [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-4|Theorem §A7.2.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: The beta spectrum and the neutrino
> If beta decay produced only the daughter and the electron, the electron's energy would be fixed by [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-5|Theorem §A7.2.5]]'s argument. Instead, beta electrons have every energy from zero up to an endpoint $E_{\max} \approx Q$. Energy, momentum and (since neutron, proton and electron all have spin ½) angular momentum would all seem violated. Pauli proposed in 1930 that a third, neutral, nearly massless particle carries off the rest; Fermi built his theory of beta decay on it in 1933 and named it the neutrino; Cowan and Reines detected it in 1956. A three-body final state shares the energy in a continuous range of ways. The endpoint lying so close to $Q$ shows that the neutrino mass is tiny (below about 2 eV/$c^2$).
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-4; UP3 §10.4*

^rem-a7-2-3

> [!law] Law §A7.2.6: Geiger–Nuttall Rule
> For alpha emitters, the half-life falls steeply as the α energy rises:
>
> $$
> \log t_{1/2} = a\,E_\alpha^{-1/2} + b ,
> $$
>
> with constants $a > 0$ and $b$ fitted to experiment. α energies between about 4 and 9 MeV correspond to half-lives from below a microsecond to billions of years.
>
> *Valid when:* for the naturally occurring alpha emitters, as an empirical fit (Geiger and Nuttall, 1911); the constants depend on the nuclear charge.
> *Source: Tipler §11-4, eq. (11-30), Fig. 11-16*

^law-a7-2-6

> [!remark] Remark: Alpha decay is tunnelling
> Inside the nucleus an α particle has positive energy $E_\alpha$ but is held by the Coulomb barrier, which outside the nuclear radius $R$ is $2Ze^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$ (charge $2e$ of the α particle, $Ze$ of the daughter) and rises far above $E_\alpha$. Classically it could never leave; quantum mechanically it tunnels ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-6|Theorem §A4.6.6]], [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-4|§A4.6, Remark: Alpha decay]]). The decay constant is the transmission probability $T$ per attempt times the rate of attempts, $\lambda = Tv/(2R)$, with $v$ the α particle's speed inside. A lower $E_\alpha$ means a wider and higher barrier, and $T$ depends exponentially on both; this is why small energy changes alter half-lives by many orders of magnitude, as in [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^law-a7-2-6|Law §A7.2.6]]. Treating alpha decay as barrier penetration was one of the first successes of wave mechanics. The wave-mechanical result (Taagepera and Nurmia) is $\log_{10}(t_{1/2}/\mathrm{y}) = 1.61\,(Z E_\alpha^{-1/2} - Z^{2/3}) - 28.9$, with $E_\alpha$ in MeV and $Z$ that of the daughter.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-4, eqs. (11-31)–(11-32)*

^rem-a7-2-4

> [!remark] Remark: Gamma rays
> Nuclear levels are spaced by keV to MeV, so gamma photons have wavelengths of order $hc/E = 1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}/1\ \mathrm{MeV} \approx 10^{-3}$ nm. Gamma emission usually follows an α or β decay that leaves the daughter excited. The nucleus recoils with energy $(hf)^2/(2Mc^2)$, negligible since $hf \ll Mc^2$, so $hf$ is the level difference. Most excited states decay very quickly (lifetimes down to about $10^{-11}$ s are measured directly), but transitions that must carry off much angular momentum are strongly suppressed, and such long-lived states (**isomers** or **metastable** states, marked m) can last hours or years, like $^{99\mathrm m}$Tc (6.0 h, used in medicine, [[§A7.4 Biological Effects and Medical Applications#^def-a7-4-3|Def. §A7.4.3]]). An excited nucleus can also hand its energy directly to an inner atomic electron, which is ejected (**internal conversion**).
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-4, eqs. (11-46)–(11-47); UP3 §10.7*

^rem-a7-2-5

> [!example] Example §A7.2.2: Alpha Decay of Plutonium-239
> In $^{239}\mathrm{Pu} \to {}^{235}\mathrm U + {}^4\mathrm{He}$, the masses are $M(^{239}\mathrm{Pu}) = 239.052157$ u, $M(^{235}\mathrm U) = 235.043924$ u and $M(^4\mathrm{He}) = 4.002602$ u. By [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-4|Theorem §A7.2.4]],
>
> $$
> Q = (239.052157 - 235.043924 - 4.002602)\ \mathrm u\,c^2 = (0.005631\ \mathrm u)(931.5\ \mathrm{MeV/u}) = 5.25\ \mathrm{MeV}.
> $$
>
> By [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-5|Theorem §A7.2.5]] the α particle takes $K_\alpha = (235.04/239.05)(5.25\ \mathrm{MeV}) = 5.16$ MeV and the uranium nucleus recoils with only $0.09$ MeV. That is millions of times a chemical reaction energy per atom. If the $^{235}$U is left excited, it later emits gamma rays and the α energy is lower by the excitation energy.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 10.7*

^ex-a7-2-2

> [!example] Example §A7.2.3: Which Decays Can Neptunium-233 Undergo?
> The atomic masses are $M(^{233}_{\ 93}\mathrm{Np}) = 233.040805$ u, $M(^{233}_{\ 92}\mathrm U) = 233.039630$ u, $M(^{233}_{\ 94}\mathrm{Pu}) = 233.042963$ u, $M(^{229}_{\ 91}\mathrm{Pa}) = 229.032085$ u, and $m_e = 0.000549$ u. With [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-4|Theorem §A7.2.4]]:
> - α decay to $^{229}$Pa: $Q/c^2 = 233.040805 - 229.032085 - 4.002602 = 0.006118$ u, $Q = 5.70$ MeV. Allowed.
> - $\beta^-$ decay to $^{233}$Pu: $Q/c^2 = 233.040805 - 233.042963 = -0.002158$ u, $Q = -2.01$ MeV. Forbidden.
> - $\beta^+$ decay to $^{233}$U: $Q/c^2 = 0.001175 - 2(0.000549) = 0.000077$ u, $Q = 0.072$ MeV. Allowed, barely.
> - Electron capture to $^{233}$U: $Q/c^2 = 0.001175$ u, $Q = 1.09$ MeV. Allowed.
>
> The $2m_e$ in the $\beta^+$ balance nearly closes that channel while leaving electron capture open; observed decays of $^{233}$Np are almost all electron captures. Energy decides what is possible, not how often each possibility happens.
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 11-13*

## Decay series

> [!definition] Definition §A7.2.5: Decay Series
> When the daughter of a decay is itself radioactive, decays follow one another in a **decay series** that ends at a stable nuclide. On a chart with $N$ up and $Z$ across, an α decay is a step of 2 down and 2 left, a $\beta^-$ decay a step of 1 down and 1 right. Elements with $Z > 92$ are **transuranic**; they do not occur in nature, having decayed since the Earth formed.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.4; Tipler §11-4*

^def-a7-2-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.2.7: The Four Decay Series
> In a chain of α, β and γ decays, the mass number changes only in steps of 4, so the remainder of $A$ on division by 4 never changes. Heavy nuclides therefore fall into four separate series:
>
> | Series | Starts with | Ends with |
> |---|---|---|
> | $A = 4n$ (thorium) | $^{232}$Th ($1.4\times10^{10}$ y) | $^{208}$Pb |
> | $A = 4n + 1$ (neptunium) | $^{237}$Np ($2\times10^{6}$ y) | $^{209}$Bi |
> | $A = 4n + 2$ (uranium) | $^{238}$U ($4.5\times10^{9}$ y) | $^{206}$Pb |
> | $A = 4n + 3$ (actinium) | $^{235}$U ($7.0\times10^{8}$ y) | $^{207}$Pb |
>
> The neptunium series is extinct in nature: its longest-lived member lives far shorter than the age of the Earth, about $4.5\times10^9$ y.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-4; UP3 §10.4, Figs. 10.13–10.15*

^thm-a7-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^def-a7-2-4|Def. §A7.2.4]] an α decay lowers $A$ by 4 and beta and gamma decays leave $A$ unchanged, so $A \bmod 4$ is the same for every member of a chain. A chain started from a nuclide with $A = 4n + k$ contains only nuclides with that $k$, and chains with different $k$ never meet. A series survives in nature only if a member lives at least about as long as the Earth has existed; $2\times10^6$ y is about $2000$ times shorter than $4.5\times10^9$ y, so the $^{237}$Np present at the start has long since become $^{209}$Bi.

> [!remark] Remark: Natural radioactivity and the heat of the Earth
> Natural radioactivity comes from the three surviving series, from isolated long-lived nuclides such as $^{40}$K ($1.25\times10^9$ y), and from nuclides made continually by cosmic rays, such as $^{14}$C. Without a heat source the Earth would have cooled to its present state in about $10^9$ years; the decay of $^{238}$U, $^{232}$Th and $^{40}$K keeps its interior hot.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.4; Tipler §11-9*

## Radioactive dating

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.2.8: Radioactive Dating
> If a fraction $N/N_0$ of the original radioactive nuclei remains in a sample, its age is
>
> $$
> t = \frac1\lambda\ln\frac{N_0}{N} = \frac{T_{1/2}}{\ln 2}\ln\frac{N_0}{N} .
> $$
>
> If the parent decays (directly or through short-lived steps) to a stable daughter, none of which was present at the start, and no atoms entered or left the sample, then with $N_P$ parent and $N_D$ daughter atoms now,
>
> $$
> t = \frac{T_{1/2}}{\ln 2}\,\ln\Bigl(1 + \frac{N_D}{N_P}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.3; Tipler §11-9, eqs. (11-91)–(11-92)*

^thm-a7-2-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Solve $N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}$ ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]]) for $t$ and use $\lambda = \ln 2/T_{1/2}$ ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-3|Theorem §A7.2.3]]). In the closed parent–daughter system every decayed parent became a daughter, so $N_0 = N_P + N_D$ and $N_0/N_P = 1 + N_D/N_P$.

^der-a7-2-8

*Uses:* [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]], [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-3|Theorem §A7.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Carbon-14 and rock clocks
> - **Carbon-14.** Neutrons from cosmic rays make $^{14}$C in the atmosphere by $n + {}^{14}\mathrm N \to {}^{14}\mathrm C + p$; it decays back by $^{14}\mathrm C \to {}^{14}\mathrm N + e^- + \bar\nu_e$ with $T_{1/2} = 5730$ y. Living things exchange carbon with the air and keep the atmospheric ratio $^{14}\mathrm C/^{12}\mathrm C \approx 1.3\times10^{-12}$ (Tipler: $1.35\times10^{-12}$, giving 15.6 decays per minute per gram of carbon). At death the exchange stops and the ratio falls with the decay law, so the remaining fraction $N/N_0$ gives the time since death. The method assumes the atmospheric ratio was the same in the past. It was not quite (it was about 1.5 times higher 9000 years ago, and fossil fuels and bomb tests have changed it), so dates are calibrated against tree rings and other methods. Counting $^{14}$C atoms directly by accelerator mass spectrometry reaches back about 50 000 years.
> - **Rocks.** Longer-lived pairs, such as $^{40}$K → $^{40}$Ar ($1.25\times10^9$ y) and $^{87}$Rb → $^{87}$Sr, date rocks with the second formula. Earth's oldest rocks and all meteorites come out near $4.5\times10^9$ years, the age of the solar system.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.3; Tipler §11-9*

^rem-a7-2-7

> [!example] Example §A7.2.4: Wooden Furniture in a Burial Cave
> Wood from an ancient grave retains 80% of the $^{14}$C of living wood, $N/N_0 = 0.80$. By [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-8|Theorem §A7.2.8]],
>
> $$
> t = \frac{5730\ \mathrm y}{0.693}\,\ln\frac{1}{0.80} = (8268\ \mathrm y)(0.223) = 1.84\times10^3\ \mathrm y .
> $$
>
> The furniture is nearly 2000 years old. A typical 5% uncertainty in carbon dating puts it between about 1750 and 1950 years; historical records can check the range.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 10.6*
