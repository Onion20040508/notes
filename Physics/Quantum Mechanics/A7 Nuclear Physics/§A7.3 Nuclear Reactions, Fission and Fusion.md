A decay happens by itself; a reaction is caused by a particle that hits a nucleus. The energy bookkeeping is the same: rest energy turns into kinetic energy or the reverse ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy|REL §A3.2]]). The binding-energy curve ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-5|Law §A7.1.5]]) says where energy can be gained: by splitting the heaviest nuclei (fission) and by joining the lightest (fusion). Fission runs reactors; fusion powers the stars and made most of the elements.

## Reactions and Q values

> [!definition] Definition §A7.3.1: Nuclear Reaction; Q Value
> In a **nuclear reaction** a particle $x$ strikes a nucleus $X$ and produces a nucleus $Y$ and a particle $y$: $x + X \to Y + y$, written $X(x, y)Y$. Its **Q value** is
>
> $$
> Q = (m_x + m_X - m_y - m_Y)\,c^2 .
> $$
>
> The reaction is **exothermic** if $Q > 0$ (rest energy is released) and **endothermic** if $Q < 0$. Since the total charge is the same on both sides, atomic masses may be used in place of nuclear ones (the electrons cancel).
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-7, eq. (11-58); UP3 §10.6*

^def-a7-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.3.1: Q as Kinetic-Energy Gain and as Binding-Energy Gain
> The Q value equals the increase in total kinetic energy,
>
> $$
> Q = K_{\text{final}} - K_{\text{initial}} .
> $$
>
> If the reaction only rearranges nucleons (the total numbers of protons and of neutrons are the same before and after), $Q$ is also the increase in total binding energy,
>
> $$
> Q = \sum_{\text{final}} E_b - \sum_{\text{initial}} E_b ,
> $$
>
> where a free nucleon has $E_b = 0$. Reactions that end in more tightly bound nuclei release energy.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-7, §11-8; UP3 §10.5–10.6*

^thm-a7-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The total energy $\sum (mc^2 + K)$ of an isolated system is conserved ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|REL Principle §A3.2.3]]), so $\sum_i m c^2 + K_{\text{initial}} = \sum_f mc^2 + K_{\text{final}}$, which is the first formula. For the second, write each nuclear mass by [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^def-a7-1-5|Def. §A7.1.5]] as $m = Zm_p + Nm_n - E_b/c^2$. Summed over either side, the terms $Zm_p + Nm_n$ are the same, because the total proton and neutron numbers are; they cancel in $Q$, leaving $Q = \sum_f E_b - \sum_i E_b$.

^der-a7-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|REL Principle §A3.2.3]], [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^def-a7-1-5|Def. §A7.1.5]], [[§A7.3 Nuclear Reactions, Fission and Fusion#^def-a7-3-1|Def. §A7.3.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.3.2: Threshold Energy
> An endothermic reaction ($Q < 0$) on a target nucleus of mass $M$ at rest, caused by a projectile of mass $m$ with non-relativistic speed, needs the projectile kinetic energy
>
> $$
> K_{\text{th}} = \frac{m + M}{M}\,|Q| .
> $$
>
> It exceeds $|Q|$ because the products must keep moving to carry the projectile's momentum.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-7, eqs. (11-59)–(11-61)*

^thm-a7-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the centre-of-mass frame ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|CM Def. §A5.4.1]]) the total momentum is zero, so the products can be left at rest, and the least kinetic energy that does the job is $|Q|$. There the projectile and target have equal and opposite momenta of size $p$: $v = p/m$, $V = p/M$, and
>
> $$
> E_{\text{CM}} = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{p^2}{2M} = \frac{p^2}{2}\,\frac{m + M}{mM} .
> $$
>
> The laboratory frame moves at $-V$ relative to the CM frame, so that the target is at rest; there the projectile has velocity $v + V$ ([[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|CM Theorem §A2.8.1]]) and momentum $p_{\text{lab}} = m(v + V) = p(m + M)/M$. Its kinetic energy is
>
> $$
> K_{\text{lab}} = \frac{p_{\text{lab}}^2}{2m} = \frac{p^2}{2m}\Bigl(\frac{m + M}{M}\Bigr)^2 = \frac{m + M}{M}\,E_{\text{CM}} .
> $$
>
> Setting $E_{\text{CM}} = |Q|$ gives $K_{\text{th}}$. For photons or relativistic projectiles the same idea is carried out with the invariant mass ([[§A8.3 Particle Accelerators and Detectors#^thm-a8-3-2|Theorem §A8.3.2]]).

^der-a7-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|CM Def. §A5.4.1]], [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|CM Theorem §A2.8.1]], [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|CM Def. §A4.2.1]], [[§A7.3 Nuclear Reactions, Fission and Fusion#^def-a7-3-1|Def. §A7.3.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A7.3.2: Cross Section; Barn
> If $I$ particles per unit time per unit area fall on a target and $R$ reactions of a given kind occur per unit time per target nucleus, the **cross section** for that reaction is $\sigma = R/I$, an effective target area. Each possible outcome has its own (partial) cross section; their sum is the total cross section. Nuclear cross sections are of the order of the square of a nuclear radius and are measured in **barns**, $1\ \mathrm b = 10^{-28}\ \mathrm{m^2}$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-7, eqs. (11-62)–(11-63)*

^def-a7-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Reactions with neutrons
> Neutrons feel no Coulomb barrier, so even slow ones reach the nucleus. A fast neutron scattered many times in matter slows to **thermal** energies, of order $kT \approx 0.025$ eV at room temperature. Slow neutrons are readily captured: apart from sharp resonances, the capture cross section grows as $1/v$, in proportion to the time the neutron spends near the nucleus. Some nuclei capture enormously: $^{113}$Cd reaches about 57 000 b, which makes cadmium a neutron absorber. Many low-energy reactions go through a **compound nucleus** that shares the energy among all its nucleons and lives about $10^{-16}$ s, long compared with the $10^{-21}$ s a projectile needs to cross it, then decays without memory of how it formed.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-7*

^rem-a7-3-1

## Fission

> [!definition] Definition §A7.3.3: Fission; Fissile Nuclide
> In **fission** a heavy nucleus splits into two medium-mass **fragments** and a few neutrons. It is usually **induced** by neutron capture, for example
>
> $$
> n + {}^{235}\mathrm U \to {}^{141}\mathrm{Ba} + {}^{92}\mathrm{Kr} + 3n, \qquad n + {}^{235}\mathrm U \to {}^{140}\mathrm{Xe} + {}^{94}\mathrm{Sr} + 2n,
> $$
>
> among many possible fragment pairs; uneven splits are far more likely than even ones, with fragment yields peaking near $A \approx 95$ and $A \approx 140$. Thermal-neutron fission of $^{235}$U releases on average about 2.4 neutrons. A nuclide that fissions after capturing a slow neutron, like $^{235}$U or $^{239}$Pu, is **fissile**. Very heavy nuclei can also fission **spontaneously**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.5; Tipler §11-8*

^def-a7-3-3

> [!remark] Remark: Why fission releases about 200 MeV, and why only some nuclei are fissile
> - **The curve.** Uranium has about 7.6 MeV per nucleon, the fragments about 8.4–8.7 MeV. By [[§A7.3 Nuclear Reactions, Fission and Fusion#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]] each fission releases roughly 1 MeV per nucleon, about 200 MeV in all; for the split into $^{142}$Ba and $^{92}$Kr, Tipler's rounded binding energies give $1189.5 + 800.9 - 1797.1 = 193.3$ MeV. Most of it appears as kinetic energy of the fragments, driven apart by their Coulomb repulsion, and ends as heat. A chemical reaction releases a few eV per molecule.
> - **The liquid drop.** In Bohr and Wheeler's picture ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^mod-a7-1-6|Model §A7.1.6]]), the absorbed neutron sets the drop oscillating; if the deformation is large enough, the Coulomb repulsion wins over surface tension and the drop splits. The energy needed to get over the barrier is the **critical energy**. Capture of a neutron by $^{235}$U leaves $^{236}$U with 6.5 MeV of excitation, above its critical energy of 6.2 MeV, so it fissions; capture by $^{238}$U gives $^{239}$U only 5.2 MeV, below its 5.9 MeV, so slow neutrons cannot fission $^{238}$U.
> - **Spontaneous fission** must tunnel through the same barrier and is rare: $^{238}$U has an α half-life of $4.5\times10^9$ y but a spontaneous-fission half-life of about $10^{16}$ y.
> - **Neutron-rich fragments.** The fragments inherit uranium's neutron excess, which puts them far from the line of stability; they shed prompt neutrons and then $\beta^-$-decay towards it. Hence radioactive waste, and also medical isotopes such as $^{99}$Mo, the source of $^{99\mathrm m}$Tc.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-8, Example 11-19; UP3 §10.5*

^rem-a7-3-2

> [!definition] Definition §A7.3.4: Chain Reaction; Critical Mass; Moderator
> The neutrons released in fission can cause further fissions, a **chain reaction**. It is **critical** (self-sustaining) when on average exactly one neutron from each fission causes another fission; the smallest mass of fissile material for which this happens, rather than too many neutrons escaping, is the **critical mass**. A **moderator** (water, graphite) slows the fast fission neutrons to thermal energies, at which $^{235}$U fissions much more readily; **control rods** of a strong absorber such as cadmium set the rate.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.5; Tipler §11-8*

^def-a7-3-4

> [!remark] Remark: Reactors and bombs
> - **Reactors.** Fermi's group ran the first self-sustaining chain reaction on 2 December 1942 in Chicago, in a pile of uranium and graphite. Natural uranium is 99.3% $^{238}$U and only 0.7% $^{235}$U. In a pressurized-water reactor the water both moderates and carries the heat to a steam generator whose steam drives a turbine. If the water boils away, moderation stops and with it the chain reaction, though, as Chernobyl and Fukushima showed, safety systems can be overwhelmed.
> - **Waste and breeding.** Long-lived fission products must be stored for a very long time. A **breeder reactor** turns $^{238}$U into fissile plutonium, $n + {}^{238}\mathrm U \to {}^{239}\mathrm U \xrightarrow{\beta^-} {}^{239}\mathrm{Np} \xrightarrow{\beta^-} {}^{239}\mathrm{Pu}$, multiplying the usable fuel by about 100, at the price of plutonium's toxicity and weapons use.
> - **Bombs** assemble a supercritical mass faster than it can blow itself apart: a gun firing one sub-critical piece of $^{235}$U into another, or chemical explosives imploding a shell of $^{239}$Pu. A reactor does not hold enough $^{235}$U to explode like a bomb.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.5; Tipler §11-8*

^rem-a7-3-3

## Fusion

> [!definition] Definition §A7.3.5: Fusion; Thermonuclear Fusion
> In **fusion** two light nuclei combine into a heavier one, as in $^2\mathrm H + {}^3\mathrm H \to {}^4\mathrm{He} + n + 17.6\ \mathrm{MeV}$. The nuclei must come within the range of the nuclear force against their Coulomb repulsion; in **thermonuclear** fusion they get the energy from the random thermal motion of a very hot plasma.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.6; Tipler §11-8*

^def-a7-3-5

> [!remark] Remark: The Coulomb barrier and the temperature of fusion
> Two protons 3 fm apart have Coulomb energy $ke^2/r = 0.48$ MeV. If each brought half of it as its average thermal energy, $\tfrac32kT = 0.24$ MeV ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-2|TH Theorem §A2.2.2]]), the plasma would need $T \approx 1.9\times10^9$ K. The Sun's core is only about $1.5\times10^7$ K ($kT$ around 1 keV), yet it fuses hydrogen: the protons in the high-energy tail of the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^thm-a2-4-1|TH Theorem §A2.4.1]]) tunnel through the barrier ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-6|Theorem §A4.6.6]]). The first step is so slow that it limits the Sun's burning rate and gives it a lifetime of billions of years. On Earth, the deuterium–tritium reaction needs $kT \approx 10$ keV ($T \approx 10^8$ K), and the plasma must be held long enough: Lawson's break-even criterion is $n\tau > 10^{20}\ \mathrm{s/m^3}$ for density $n$ and confinement time $\tau$. Magnetic confinement in a torus (the tokamak: JET, ITER) has barely reached break-even, and inertial confinement of laser-compressed pellets is being pursued; a practical fusion power plant is still decades away. Tritium can be bred from lithium, $n + {}^7\mathrm{Li} \to {}^4\mathrm{He} + {}^3\mathrm H + n$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-8, Example 11-24, eq. (11-72); UP3 §10.6*

^rem-a7-3-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.3.3: Energy of the Proton–Proton Chain
> In the Sun's core, hydrogen burns to helium by the **proton–proton chain**
>
> $$
> {}^1\mathrm H + {}^1\mathrm H \to {}^2\mathrm H + e^+ + \nu_e, \qquad {}^2\mathrm H + {}^1\mathrm H \to {}^3\mathrm{He} + \gamma, \qquad {}^3\mathrm{He} + {}^3\mathrm{He} \to {}^4\mathrm{He} + {}^1\mathrm H + {}^1\mathrm H ,
> $$
>
> the first two steps occurring twice for each helium nucleus formed, with net effect $4\,{}^1\mathrm H \to {}^4\mathrm{He} + 2e^+ + 2\nu_e + 2\gamma$. Counting the annihilation of the two positrons with two electrons, the energy released per helium nucleus is
>
> $$
> Q = \bigl[4M(^1\mathrm H) - M(^4\mathrm{He})\bigr]c^2 = (0.028698\ \mathrm u)(931.5\ \mathrm{MeV/u}) = 26.7\ \mathrm{MeV},
> $$
>
> part of which the neutrinos carry out of the Sun unseen.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.6; Tipler §11-8, Fig. 11-55*

^thm-a7-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take as initial state four hydrogen atoms (4 protons, 4 electrons) and as final state one helium atom (2 protons, 2 neutrons, 2 electrons), two neutrinos and photons. The two positrons made in the first steps annihilate with the two remaining electrons ($e^+ + e^- \to 2\gamma$, [[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]]), so no massive particle other than the helium atom is left. Energy conservation ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|REL Principle §A3.2.3]]) with the neutrino masses neglected gives $Q = [4M(^1\mathrm H) - M(^4\mathrm{He})]c^2$, and $4(1.007825) - 4.002602 = 0.028698$ u. As a check, the step values in Tipler, $0.42$, $5.49$ and $12.86$ MeV, plus two annihilations of $2m_ec^2 = 1.022$ MeV each, give $2(0.42 + 5.49) + 12.86 + 2(1.022) = 26.7$ MeV.

^der-a7-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|REL Principle §A3.2.3]], [[§A7.3 Nuclear Reactions, Fission and Fusion#^def-a7-3-1|Def. §A7.3.1]], [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^rem-a7-1-5|Remark: Use atomic masses throughout]]

> [!caution] Caution: Electron bookkeeping in the solar balance
> Subtracting two electron masses from $4M(^1\mathrm H) - M(^4\mathrm{He})$, as University Physics does (giving 25.7 MeV), mixes the two possible balances. Without the annihilation energy the release is $[4M(^1\mathrm H) - M(^4\mathrm{He}) - 4m_e]c^2 = 24.7$ MeV; with it, 26.7 MeV. The positrons always annihilate in the dense core, so 26.7 MeV is what the Sun gets (Tipler's value).

^cau-a7-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Stars make the elements
> When a star's core runs out of hydrogen it contracts, heats and burns helium: $^4\mathrm{He} + {}^4\mathrm{He} \to {}^8\mathrm{Be} + \gamma$, $^8\mathrm{Be} + {}^4\mathrm{He} \to {}^{12}\mathrm C + \gamma$, $^{12}\mathrm C + {}^4\mathrm{He} \to {}^{16}\mathrm O + \gamma$. Stars like the Sun stop there. In massive stars, at core temperatures above $6\times10^8$ K, carbon and oxygen burn on to sodium, magnesium, silicon and finally iron and nickel. Fusion beyond iron absorbs energy ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^rem-a7-1-6|Remark: Reading the curve]]), so the iron core collapses, heats to about $5\times10^9$ K, and the star explodes as a supernova; its flood of neutrons builds elements heavier than iron, and the explosion spreads them into space, where they condense into new stars and planets. Many atoms of our bodies were made in stars.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.6*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A7.3.1: Threshold of the Reaction ¹³C(p, n)¹³N
> With atomic masses $M(^{13}\mathrm C) = 13.003355$ u, $M(^1\mathrm H) = 1.007825$ u, $M(^{13}\mathrm N) = 13.005738$ u and $m_n = 1.008665$ u,
>
> $$
> \frac{Q}{c^2} = (13.003355 + 1.007825) - (13.005738 + 1.008665) = 14.011180 - 14.014403 = -0.003223\ \mathrm u ,
> $$
>
> so $Q = -3.00$ MeV: the reaction is endothermic. Protons fired at $^{13}$C at rest need, by [[§A7.3 Nuclear Reactions, Fission and Fusion#^thm-a7-3-2|Theorem §A7.3.2]],
>
> $$
> K_{\text{th}} = \frac{1.007825 + 13.003355}{13.003355}\,(3.00\ \mathrm{MeV}) = 3.23\ \mathrm{MeV}.
> $$
>
> The extra 0.23 MeV, about $m/M$ of $|Q|$, goes into the motion of the centre of mass and cannot be used.
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 11-17*

^ex-a7-3-1

> [!example] Example §A7.3.2: Spontaneous Fission of Uranium-238
> For $^{238}\mathrm U \to {}^{95}\mathrm{Sr} + {}^{140}\mathrm{Xe} + 3n$, with $M(^{238}\mathrm U) = 238.050784$ u, $M(^{95}\mathrm{Sr}) = 94.919388$ u, $M(^{140}\mathrm{Xe}) = 139.921610$ u and $m_n = 1.008665$ u (charge balances: $92 = 38 + 54$, so atomic masses may be used), the products total $94.919388 + 139.921610 + 3(1.008665) = 237.866993$ u, and
>
> $$
> Q = (238.050784 - 237.866993)\ \mathrm u\,c^2 = (0.183791\ \mathrm u)(931.5\ \mathrm{MeV/u}) = 171.2\ \mathrm{MeV}.
> $$
>
> This is less than the roughly 200 MeV of an even split into tightly bound fragments, partly because three nucleons leave as free neutrons with no binding energy. Fission is statistical: the same nucleus splits in many ways with various numbers of neutrons, and those neutrons make chain reactions possible.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 10.9*

^ex-a7-3-2

> [!example] Example §A7.3.3: Energy per Kilogram, Fission against Fusion
> **Fission.** One kilogram of $^{235}$U is $(1000\ \mathrm g)/(235.04\ \mathrm{g/mol}) = 4.25$ mol, or $2.56\times10^{24}$ nuclei. At 200 MeV per fission,
>
> $$
> E = (2.56\times10^{24})(200\ \mathrm{MeV})(1.60\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{J/MeV}) = 8.2\times10^{13}\ \mathrm J ,
> $$
>
> the energy of about 14 000 barrels of crude oil.
>
> **Fusion.** One D–T reaction releases 17.6 MeV from $(2.014 + 3.016)\ \mathrm u = 8.35\times10^{-27}$ kg of fuel, which is $2.1\times10^{27}$ MeV $= 3.4\times10^{14}$ J per kilogram.
>
> Per reaction fission gives about ten times more, but per kilogram fusion gives about four times more (Tipler finds 4.3 with the binding energies of the Kr–Ba split): fusion releases about 3.5 MeV per nucleon against about 1 MeV in fission, and fusion fuel is far more plentiful.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 10.10; Tipler Example 11-19*

^ex-a7-3-3

> [!example] Example §A7.3.4: How Fast the Sun Loses Mass
> The Sun radiates $P = 3.8\times10^{26}$ W, nearly all from the proton–proton chain. Each chain releases $Q = 26.7$ MeV $= 4.28\times10^{-12}$ J ([[§A7.3 Nuclear Reactions, Fission and Fusion#^thm-a7-3-3|Theorem §A7.3.3]]), so about $P/Q = 8.9\times10^{37}$ helium nuclei form every second. Whatever the reactions, the radiated energy leaves as rest energy, so the Sun's mass falls at the rate
>
> $$
> \frac{P}{c^2} = \frac{3.8\times10^{26}\ \mathrm W}{(3.0\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s})^2} = 4.2\times10^9\ \mathrm{kg/s}.
> $$
>
> Over the $5\times10^9$ y $= 1.6\times10^{17}$ s until its core runs out of hydrogen, that is $6.7\times10^{26}$ kg, only about $0.03\%$ of its $2.0\times10^{30}$ kg: burning hydrogen hardly changes the Sun's mass.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 10.11 (with Tipler's 26.7 MeV)*
