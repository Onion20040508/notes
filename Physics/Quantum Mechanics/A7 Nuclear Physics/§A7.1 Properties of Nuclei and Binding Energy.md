Rutherford's experiments ([[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom|§A2.1]]) put almost all of an atom's mass into a nucleus some $10^{-15}$–$10^{-14}$ m across. This section says what the nucleus is made of, how big it is, what holds it together, and how much energy that binding is worth. The binding energy per nucleon, read off from measured masses, organises everything that follows: decay ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay|§A7.2]]), fission and fusion ([[§A7.3 Nuclear Reactions, Fission and Fusion|§A7.3]]). PHY 284 did not cover nuclear and particle physics, so Chapters A7–A8 rest on University Physics and Tipler alone.

## Composition and notation

> [!definition] Definition §A7.1.1: Nucleon; Atomic, Neutron and Mass Number; Nuclide
> A nucleus consists of **protons** (charge $+e$) and **neutrons** (charge $0$), together called **nucleons**; the two have nearly equal masses (938.27 and 939.57 MeV/$c^2$). The **atomic number** $Z$ is the number of protons, the **neutron number** $N$ the number of neutrons, and the **mass number**
>
> $$
> A = Z + N
> $$
>
> the number of nucleons. A nuclear species with given $Z$ and $N$ is a **nuclide**, written $^{A}_{Z}\mathrm X$ with $\mathrm X$ the chemical symbol, e.g. $^{12}_{6}\mathrm C$ (6 protons, 6 neutrons). Since the symbol fixes $Z$, one often writes just $^{12}\mathrm C$ or C-12. The helium-4 nucleus is also called the **alpha particle**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.1, eqs. (10.1)–(10.2); Tipler §11-2*

^def-a7-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A7.1.2: Isotopes, Isotones, Isobars
> Nuclides with the same $Z$ and different $N$ are **isotopes** of one element (hydrogen $^1$H, deuterium $^2$H, tritium $^3$H); nuclides with the same $N$ are **isotones**; nuclides with the same $A$ are **isobars** ($^{14}$C and $^{14}$N). Isotopes have the same electron shells and hence the same chemistry: heavy water looks and tastes like ordinary water.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.1; Tipler §11-2*

^def-a7-1-2

> [!definition] Definition §A7.1.3: Atomic Mass Unit; Atomic Mass of an Element
> The **atomic mass unit** is one twelfth of the mass of a neutral $^{12}$C atom,
>
> $$
> 1\ \mathrm u = 1.66054\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg} = 931.5\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2 .
> $$
>
> A nuclide's mass in u is close to its mass number $A$. The **atomic mass of an element** is the abundance-weighted average of the masses of its isotopes; for copper, $(62.929595)(0.6909) + (64.927786)(0.3091) = 63.55\ \mathrm{g/mol}$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.1–10.2; Tipler §11-2*

^def-a7-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Why there are no electrons in the nucleus
> Before the neutron was found (Chadwick, 1932), a nucleus of charge $Ze$ and mass about $A$ proton masses was pictured as $A$ protons and $A - Z$ electrons. Three facts rule this out.
> - **Confinement energy.** An electron confined to $\Delta x \approx 10^{-14}$ m has momentum of order $h/\Delta x \approx 6.6\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$, so $pc \approx 120\ \mathrm{MeV} \gg m_ec^2$ and its kinetic energy is of order $100$ MeV ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]; at such momenta $K \approx pc$, [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|REL Theorem §A3.3.1]]). Electrons from beta decay carry only a few MeV, and no attraction strong enough to hold them is seen.
> - **Magnetic moments.** Nuclear moments are of the order of the nuclear magneton $e\hbar/2m_p$, about 2000 times smaller than the Bohr magneton an electron would bring.
> - **Spin and statistics.** $^{14}$N has spin 1 and behaves as a boson. Fourteen protons and seven electrons, 21 spin-½ particles, would give a half-integral spin; seven protons and seven neutrons give an integral one.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-1*

^rem-a7-1-1

## Size and density

> [!law] Law §A7.1.1: Nuclear Radius
> Nuclei are nearly spherical, with radius
>
> $$
> r = r_0 A^{1/3}, \qquad r_0 \approx 1.2\ \mathrm{fm} \quad (1\ \mathrm{fm} = 10^{-15}\ \mathrm m).
> $$
>
> *Valid when:* $A$ is not too small, and with $r_0$ depending on what is measured: electron scattering gives the charge distribution, with mean radius $(1.07 \pm 0.02)A^{1/3}$ fm and a surface layer about $2.4$ fm thick in which the density falls from 90% to 10% of its central value; neutron attenuation, which feels the nuclear force, gives $r_0 \approx 1.4$ fm. Most nuclei are spherical; some rare-earth nuclei are ellipsoids whose axes differ by up to about 20%.
> *Source: UP3 §10.1, eq. (10.3); Tipler §11-2, eqs. (11-3), (11-5), (11-7)*

^law-a7-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.1.2: Nuclear Density Is the Same for All Nuclei
> Since a nucleus of mass number $A$ has mass $m \approx A\ \mathrm u$, its density
>
> $$
> \rho = \frac{m}{\tfrac43\pi r^3} \approx \frac{3\ \mathrm u}{4\pi r_0^3} \approx 2\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}
> $$
>
> does not depend on $A$. Nucleons are packed like the molecules of a liquid drop, not like the electrons of an atom, whose size hardly grows with the number of electrons.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.1, Example 10.1; Tipler §11-2*

^thm-a7-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-1|Law §A7.1.1]] the volume is $\tfrac43\pi r^3 = \tfrac43\pi r_0^3 A$, proportional to $A$, and so is the mass. Their ratio is $A\ \mathrm u/(\tfrac43\pi r_0^3 A) = 3\ \mathrm u/(4\pi r_0^3)$. With $r_0 = 1.2$ fm this is $3(1.66\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg})/\bigl(4\pi(1.2\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm m)^3\bigr) = 2.3\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$.

^der-a7-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-1|Law §A7.1.1]], [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^def-a7-1-3|Def. §A7.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: How nuclear radii are measured
> - **Rutherford scattering.** Alpha particles on lead follow the Rutherford formula ([[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-4|Theorem §A2.1.4]]) up to about 27 MeV. Above that they come close enough to feel the nuclear force and the scattered intensity drops below the prediction; the distance of closest approach at the break gives the radius.
> - **Electron diffraction.** Electrons of a few hundred MeV have de Broglie wavelengths of a few fm ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]]) and diffract off the nucleus like light off a circular obstacle. The first minimum lies at $\sin\theta \approx 0.61\lambda/r$, the circular-aperture result $1.22\lambda/D$ with $D = 2r$ ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-1|WO Theorem §A7.4.1]]). For 420-MeV electrons on $^{16}$O, $\lambda = h/p \approx hc/E = 2.95$ fm and the minimum is at about $44^\circ$, so $r \approx (0.61)(2.95\ \mathrm{fm})/\sin 44^\circ = 2.6$ fm.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-2, Example 11-2*

^rem-a7-1-2

## The nuclear force and stability

> [!law] Law §A7.1.3: Properties of the Nuclear Force
> Nucleons attract one another by the **strong nuclear force**, which
> - is **short-range**: strong within about 2.5 fm, essentially zero beyond 3 fm, and strongly **repulsive** at less than about 0.5 fm (a hard core);
> - is **charge-independent**: after the Coulomb part of the p–p force is removed, the n–n, n–p and p–p forces are the same;
> - is **strong**: at 1–2 fm it exceeds the Coulomb repulsion of two protons by about two orders of magnitude. A nucleon in a nucleus sits in a potential well some 40–50 MeV deep;
> - **saturates**: each nucleon interacts only with its near neighbours.
>
> *Valid when:* as a summary of nucleon–nucleon scattering and nuclear spectroscopy. No closed formula for the force, like Coulomb's law, is known; it is also spin-dependent and has a small non-central part.
> *Source: Tipler §11-5; UP3 §10.1*

^law-a7-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Three forces compared
> - Two protons 1 fm apart have Coulomb energy $ke^2/r = 1.44\ \mathrm{MeV\cdot fm}/1\ \mathrm{fm} = 1.44$ MeV ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-2|EM Theorem §A3.1.2]]), repulsive; yet removing a nucleon from $^4$He costs about 20 MeV. The binding must come from a stronger, attractive force that also acts on neutrons.
> - Gravity cannot help: for two protons $F_{\text{grav}}/F_{\text{Coul}} = Gm_p^2/(ke^2) \approx 8\times10^{-37}$, at any distance ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|CM Principle §A8.1.1]], [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|EM Principle §A1.2.2]]).
> - A depth estimate: a nucleon in a box of width 2 fm has ground-state energy $h^2/(8ma^2) \approx 50$ MeV ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]), so a well of that depth is needed to bind it.
> - Yukawa (1935) explained the short range by the exchange of a massive particle; from a range of about 1 fm he predicted a mass near 200 MeV/$c^2$, and the pion ($140$ MeV/$c^2$) was found in cosmic rays in 1947. The estimate is the range formula of [[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]]; today the force between nucleons is understood as a residue of the force between quarks.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-5, Example 11-14, Example 11-15*

^rem-a7-1-3

> [!definition] Definition §A7.1.4: Stable and Radioactive Nuclides; Line of Stability
> A nuclide is **stable** if its ground state does not decay, and **radioactive** otherwise ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^def-a7-2-1|Def. §A7.2.1]]). Of more than 3000 known nuclides, 266 are stable. Plotted with $N$ against $Z$ (the **chart of the nuclides**), the stable nuclides lie along a band, the **line of stability**: $N = Z$ for light nuclei, $N > Z$ for $Z \gtrsim 15$–$20$, reaching $N/Z \approx 1.5$ for lead. No nuclide with $Z > 83$ is stable.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.1; Tipler §11-2*

^def-a7-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: Why $N \approx Z$, and why heavy nuclei need extra neutrons
> - **Exclusion principle.** Protons and neutrons are distinct spin-½ fermions, each obeying the exclusion principle ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]]) separately. In a box with levels $E_1, 4E_1, 9E_1, 16E_1, \dots$ ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]), seven neutrons must fill four levels: $2(1 + 4 + 9)E_1 + 16E_1 = 44E_1$. Four neutrons and three protons fill only two levels in each ladder: two of each kind in $E_1$ and the other three in $4E_1$, $4E_1 + 3(4E_1) = 16E_1$. Equal numbers minimise the kinetic energy.
> - **Coulomb repulsion.** Its energy grows like $Z^2$, while the nuclear attraction saturates. In a heavy nucleus adding two neutrons costs less energy than adding a neutron and a proton, so $N - Z$ grows with $Z$.
> - **Pairing and magic numbers.** Of the 266 stable nuclides, 159 are even–even, 50 odd-$Z$ even-$N$, 53 even-$Z$ odd-$N$ and only 4 odd–odd: like nucleons pair up. Nuclides with $Z$ or $N$ equal to 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126 (**magic numbers**) are unusually stable, a sign of shells as in atoms (the shell model, Tipler §11-6, is not developed at this level).
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-2, Fig. 11-9, Table 11-2; UP3 §10.1*

^rem-a7-1-4

## Binding energy

Mass–energy equivalence ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^thm-a3-2-4|REL Theorem §A3.2.4]]), the binding energy of any bound system ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-3|REL Def. §A3.2.3]]) and the mass of a bound system ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^thm-a3-2-5|REL Theorem §A3.2.5]]) are homed in Relativity: a bound system is lighter than its separated parts by its binding energy over $c^2$. The simplest nucleus, the deuteron, is worked out there ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^ex-a3-2-3|REL Example §A3.2.3]]): $E_b = 2.224$ MeV, 1.11 MeV per nucleon, and a gamma ray of at least that energy splits it into a proton and a neutron. For nuclei the deficit is large enough to measure as a mass difference.

> [!definition] Definition §A7.1.5: Mass Defect; Nuclear Binding Energy
> The **mass defect** of a nucleus of mass $m_{\text{nuc}}$ is
>
> $$
> \Delta m = Zm_p + (A - Z)m_n - m_{\text{nuc}} .
> $$
>
> By [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^thm-a3-2-5|REL Theorem §A3.2.5]] its **binding energy** ([[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-3|REL Def. §A3.2.3]]), the energy needed to separate it into free protons and neutrons and the energy released when it forms from them, is $E_b = (\Delta m)c^2$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.2, eqs. (10.4)–(10.5); Tipler §11-2, eq. (11-10)*

^def-a7-1-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.1.4: Binding Energy from Atomic Masses
> With $M(^A\mathrm X)$ the mass of the neutral atom and $M(^1\mathrm H) = 1.007825$ u that of the hydrogen atom,
>
> $$
> E_b = \bigl[Z\,M(^1\mathrm H) + N m_n - M(^A\mathrm X)\bigr]c^2 , \qquad m_n = 1.008665\ \mathrm u .
> $$
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-2, eq. (11-11); UP3 Example 10.3*

^thm-a7-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> An atom's mass is its nuclear mass plus $Z$ electron masses, less the electrons' binding energy over $c^2$: $M(^A\mathrm X) = m_{\text{nuc}} + Zm_e - B_{\text{el}}/c^2$, and likewise $M(^1\mathrm H) = m_p + m_e - 13.6\ \mathrm{eV}/c^2$. Electronic binding energies are eV to keV per electron, against nuclear binding energies of MeV per nucleon, so drop them. Then $Z\,M(^1\mathrm H) + Nm_n - M(^A\mathrm X) = Zm_p + Zm_e + Nm_n - m_{\text{nuc}} - Zm_e = \Delta m$: the $Z$ electron masses cancel. Multiply by $c^2$ and use [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^def-a7-1-5|Def. §A7.1.5]].

^der-a7-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^def-a7-1-5|Def. §A7.1.5]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-3|REL Def. §A3.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Use atomic masses throughout
> Mass spectrometers measure ions, and tables list neutral-atom masses. Writing every mass in a nuclear energy balance as an atomic mass makes the electrons cancel, as above; the one exception, $\beta^+$ decay, is handled in [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-4|Theorem §A7.2.4]].

^rem-a7-1-5

> [!definition] Definition §A7.1.6: Binding Energy per Nucleon
> The **binding energy per nucleon** is $\mathrm{BEN} = E_b/A$, the average energy needed to remove one nucleon from the nucleus; it plays the role that the ionization energy plays for an atom.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.2, eq. (10.6); Tipler §11-2*

^def-a7-1-6

> [!law] Law §A7.1.5: The Binding-Energy Curve
> Plotted against $A$, the binding energy per nucleon
> - rises steeply for the light nuclei (1.1 MeV for $^2$H, 2.8 MeV for $^3$H), with $^4$He (7.07 MeV), $^{12}$C and $^{16}$O standing out above their neighbours;
> - is roughly constant, about 8 MeV, for $A \gtrsim 16$;
> - peaks at about 8.8 MeV near $A \approx 56$–$65$ (iron, nickel, copper);
> - falls slowly for heavier nuclei, to about 7.6 MeV for uranium.
>
> *Valid when:* as a description of measured masses; it is a fact about nature, not derived here. The liquid-drop model ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^mod-a7-1-6|Model §A7.1.6]]) reproduces its smooth trend, but not the light-nucleus peaks.
> *Source: UP3 §10.2, Fig. 10.7; Tipler §11-2, Fig. 11-10*

^law-a7-1-5

![[ph-qm-a7-1-1.svg]]
*Binding energy per nucleon computed with [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^thm-a7-1-4|Theorem §A7.1.4]] from the atomic masses quoted in University Physics and Tipler (blue dots; the tightly bound $^4$He in red). The curve climbs to a broad maximum near $A \approx 60$ and falls slowly towards uranium; the grey arrows show why fusing light nuclei and splitting heavy ones both move towards tighter binding and release energy.*

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the curve
> - **Strength.** A few MeV to 9 MeV per nucleon, against 13.6 eV to ionize hydrogen: nuclear energies are about a million times atomic and chemical ones.
> - **Saturation.** If every nucleon attracted every other, $E_b$ would grow like $A(A-1)/2$ and BEN like $A$. A constant BEN means a fixed number of partners per nucleon, its near neighbours ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-3|Law §A7.1.3]]), which also gives the constant density of [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^thm-a7-1-2|Theorem §A7.1.2]].
> - **Rise and fall.** Light nuclei have a large fraction of nucleons at the surface, with fewer neighbours, so BEN is low. In heavy nuclei the Coulomb repulsion, which acts between all pairs of protons, takes over.
> - **Energy release.** Moving towards the peak releases energy: fusion of light nuclei and fission of heavy ones ([[§A7.3 Nuclear Reactions, Fission and Fusion|§A7.3]]). In stars fusion stops at iron, since beyond it no reaction releases energy.

^rem-a7-1-6

> [!model] Model §A7.1.6: Liquid-Drop Model
> The nucleus is treated as a drop of incompressible nuclear fluid.
> - *Assumes:* constant density ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^thm-a7-1-2|Theorem §A7.1.2]]) and short-range, saturating attraction, so that the binding energy is a sum of smooth terms: a **volume** term proportional to $A$; a **surface** correction proportional to the area $4\pi r^2 \propto A^{2/3}$ (a surface tension, since surface nucleons have fewer neighbours); a **Coulomb** correction, the energy $\tfrac35\,(Ze)^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r) \propto Z^2/A^{1/3}$ of a uniformly charged sphere; a term favouring $N \approx Z$ (exclusion principle); and a **pairing** term favouring even–even over odd–odd nuclei (Tipler quotes its coefficient as $\pm 12$ MeV/$c^2$; in the usual form it is divided by $\sqrt A$, so it amounts to about 1 MeV for heavy nuclei). With fitted coefficients this is Weizsäcker's **semi-empirical mass formula**.
> - *Explains:* the smooth binding-energy curve, the valley of stability, and fission, pictured as a drop set oscillating until it splits ([[§A7.3 Nuclear Reactions, Fission and Fusion#^rem-a7-3-2|§A7.3, Remark: Why fission releases about 200 MeV]]).
> - *Fails:* for very light nuclei, where one nucleon more or less changes everything, and for the fluctuations near the magic numbers, which need the shell model.
>
> *Source: Tipler §11-2, §11-4, §11-6, eq. (11-1); UP3 §10.5*

^mod-a7-1-6

> [!remark] Remark: The formula itself
> Neither University Physics nor Tipler's printed text gives the coefficients of the semi-empirical mass formula (Tipler puts them in an online section), so they are not quoted here. The dependences above follow from [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-1|Law §A7.1.1]]: surface area $\propto r^2 \propto A^{2/3}$ and Coulomb energy $\propto Z^2/r \propto Z^2/A^{1/3}$.

^rem-a7-1-7

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A7.1.1: Size and Density of the Iron Nucleus
> For $^{56}$Fe, [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-1|Law §A7.1.1]] gives $r = (1.2\ \mathrm{fm})(56)^{1/3} = (1.2\ \mathrm{fm})(3.83) = 4.6$ fm, so its diameter is about $10^{-14}$ m. With $m \approx 56$ u,
>
> $$
> \rho = \frac{56\ \mathrm u}{\tfrac43\pi(4.6\ \mathrm{fm})^3} = 0.138\ \mathrm{u/fm^3} = (0.138)\frac{1.66\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}}{10^{-45}\ \mathrm{m^3}} = 2.3\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3} .
> $$
>
> For comparison, $^4$He has $r = 1.9$ fm and $^{238}$U $r = 7.4$ fm: from the lightest to the heaviest nuclei the radius grows only by a factor of 4. A cubic metre of nuclear matter has the mass of a cube of water 61 km on a side.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 10.1; Tipler Example 11-1*

^ex-a7-1-1

> [!example] Example §A7.1.2: The Alpha Particle
> For $^4$He, $Z = N = 2$ and $M(^4\mathrm{He}) = 4.002602$ u. By [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^thm-a7-1-4|Theorem §A7.1.4]],
>
> $$
> E_b = \bigl[2(1.007825) + 2(1.008665) - 4.002602\bigr]\ \mathrm u\,c^2 = (0.030378\ \mathrm u)(931.5\ \mathrm{MeV/u}) = 28.3\ \mathrm{MeV},
> $$
>
> so BEN $= 28.3/4 = 7.07$ MeV, far above $^2$H (1.1 MeV, [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^ex-a3-2-3|REL Example §A3.2.3]]) and $^3$H (2.8 MeV). This tight binding is why heavy nuclei emit whole alpha particles rather than single nucleons ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^def-a7-2-4|Def. §A7.2.4]]), and why the end product of hydrogen fusion is helium.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 10.3*
