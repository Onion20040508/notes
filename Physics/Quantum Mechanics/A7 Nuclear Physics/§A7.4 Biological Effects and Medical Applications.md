Nuclear radiation ionizes the atoms it passes, and in living tissue ionization can damage DNA. The same radiation, aimed or traced, diagnoses and treats disease. This section gives the units in which doses are measured, the size of the effects, and the two main imaging methods; it is descriptive.

## Dose and its effects

> [!definition] Definition §A7.4.1: Absorbed Dose; Rad
> The **absorbed dose** is the energy of ionizing radiation deposited per kilogram of tissue. University Physics measures it in **rad** (radiation absorbed dose),
>
> $$
> 1\ \mathrm{rad} = 0.01\ \mathrm{J/kg}.
> $$
>
> The SI unit, used in University Physics' problems, is the **gray**, $1\ \mathrm{Gy} = 1\ \mathrm{J/kg} = 100$ rad.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.7*

^def-a7-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §A7.4.2: Relative Biological Effectiveness; Dose Equivalent; Rem, Sievert
> The **relative biological effectiveness** (RBE) of a radiation is the factor by which it does more damage than X-rays or gamma rays for the same absorbed dose. The **dose equivalent** is the absorbed dose times the RBE: in **rem** (roentgen equivalent man), $\text{rem} = \text{rad}\times\text{RBE}$; in **sieverts**, $\text{Sv} = \text{Gy}\times\text{RBE}$, so $1\ \mathrm{Sv} = 100\ \mathrm{rem}$. Doses below 0.1 Sv are called low, 0.1–1 Sv moderate, above 1 Sv high.
>
> | Radiation | RBE (approximate) |
> |---|---|
> | X-rays, γ rays, β rays above 32 keV | 1 |
> | β rays below 32 keV | 1.7 |
> | Thermal to slow neutrons (below 20 keV) | 2–5 |
> | Fast neutrons, protons (1–10 MeV) | 10 (body), 32 (eyes) |
> | α rays, heavy ions | 10–20 |
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.7, Table 10.3*

^def-a7-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: How radiation harms cells
> Radiation damages a cell by ionizing atoms along its path, above all in the DNA. Cells repair DNA constantly (up to a million damages per cell per day), but if radiation impairs the repair, the cell may become permanently dormant, kill itself (programmed cell death), or divide without control, the start of a tumour. Damage depends on the dose, the radiation and the tissue. A short-range α particle deposits its energy in a small volume, where damage is dense and hard to repair: hence the high RBE, and the danger of α emitters taken into the body, though outside it their radiation hardly penetrates the skin. Rapidly dividing cells (blood, the lining of the gut, hair follicles) suffer first, which is why falling blood counts are the first sign of exposure and nausea and hair loss follow at higher doses.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.7*

^rem-a7-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Effects of a single whole-body dose, and everyday doses
> | Dose (Sv) | Immediate effect on adults |
> |---|---|
> | 0–0.1 | none observable |
> | 0.1–1 | slight to moderate fall in white-cell count |
> | 1–2 | marked fall in blood counts, brief nausea; rarely fatal |
> | 2–5 | nausea, hair loss, severe blood damage, haemorrhage; deaths |
> | 4.5 | fatal to half of those exposed within 32 days if untreated |
> | 5–20 | failure of the gut and blood systems; limited survival |
> | above 20 | death within hours (central nervous system) |
>
> For comparison, the average background dose is about 2.8 mSv per year worldwide (3.5 mSv in the US), from radon (the largest part), cosmic rays, rocks and building materials, the body's own $^{40}$K and $^{14}$C, and medical uses. A chest X-ray gives about 0.02 mSv, an abdominal CT scan about 10 mSv. Whether someone has received less than about 10 mSv is hard to establish at all.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.7, Tables 10.4–10.6*

^rem-a7-4-2

## Medical applications

> [!definition] Definition §A7.4.3: Radioactive Tracer; SPECT; PET
> - A **radioactive tracer** (radiopharmaceutical) is a compound in which a radioactive isotope replaces a stable one. Chemistry does not tell isotopes apart ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^def-a7-1-2|Def. §A7.1.2]]), so the tracer goes where the ordinary compound goes, and detectors outside the body locate it by its radiation. About 80% of tracers use $^{99\mathrm m}$Tc, a metastable state ([[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^rem-a7-2-5|Remark: Gamma rays]]) that emits a single 0.142-MeV γ ray and has a 6.0-h half-life; iodine isotopes image the thyroid and $^{201}$Tl the heart.
> - **Single-photon emission computed tomography** (SPECT) rotates a ring of gamma detectors around the patient to build a three-dimensional image, with a resolution of about 1 cm.
> - **Positron emission tomography** (PET) uses $\beta^+$ emitters such as $^{11}$C, $^{13}$N, $^{15}$O and $^{18}$F, which can stand in for atoms of natural body compounds. Each positron annihilates with an electron into two 0.511-MeV photons moving in opposite directions ([[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]]); detecting both fixes the line on which the decay happened. Resolution is about 0.5 cm.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.7; Tipler §11-4*

^def-a7-4-3

> [!remark] Remark: Therapy and magnetic resonance
> Radiation that destroys cells is also used to destroy tumours. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) involves no radioactivity: it maps hydrogen nuclei in soft tissue through the transitions between the spin states of protons in a magnetic field.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.7; Tipler §11-2*

^rem-a7-4-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A7.4.1: The Same Energy as Gamma Rays or Alpha Particles
> A 50.0-kg person absorbs 1.00 J of radiation over the whole body. The absorbed dose is $(1.00\ \mathrm J)/(50.0\ \mathrm{kg}) = 0.0200\ \mathrm{J/kg} = 2.00$ rad. As γ rays (RBE 1) this is $2.00$ rem $= 20$ mSv, a low dose. As α radiation (RBE 20) it is $(2.00\ \mathrm{rad})(20) = 40.0$ rem $= 0.40$ Sv, a moderate dose with a measurable fall in white-cell count. The same energy, in the form of α rays, has twenty times the biological effect.
>
> *Source: UP3 §10.7*

^ex-a7-4-1

> [!example] Example §A7.4.2: The Body's Own Carbon-14
> One kilogram of carbon contains $(6.02\times10^{23}/12.0\ \mathrm g)(1000\ \mathrm g) = 5.02\times10^{25}$ atoms, of which in living tissue a fraction $1.3\times10^{-12}$, or $6.52\times10^{13}$, are $^{14}$C. With $\lambda = 0.693/(5730\ \mathrm y) = 3.83\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm s^{-1}$, [[§A7.2 Radioactive Decay#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]] gives the activity
>
> $$
> A = \lambda N = (3.83\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})(6.52\times10^{13}) = 250\ \mathrm{Bq} = 6.8\ \mathrm{nCi}.
> $$
>
> About 20% of the body is carbon, so a 70-kg person carries some 14 kg of it and about 3500 $^{14}$C decays happen in the body every second. This is part of everyone's natural background and lies far below doses that do harm.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 10.5*

^ex-a7-4-2
