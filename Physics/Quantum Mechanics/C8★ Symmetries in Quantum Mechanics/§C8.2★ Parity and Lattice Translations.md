What can symmetries that come in discrete steps, an inversion of space or a shift by one lattice period, say about states and transitions? Level B used parity as a property of wave functions in one dimension ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]]), quoted $Y^m_\ell(-\hat{\mathbf r}) = (-1)^\ell Y^m_\ell(\hat{\mathbf r})$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^rem-b5-2-6|★ Remark: Reading the spherical harmonics]]), and proved Bloch's theorem from a translation operator on functions ([[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^thm-b7-3-4|Theorem §B7.3.4]]); batch 1 excluded $\Delta\ell = 0$ in the dipole rule by a parity integral ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-7|Theorem §C7.2.7]]) and defined translations on kets ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-7|Theorem §C2.2.7]]). Relativity lists the inversion $P$ among the Lorentz transformations ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-2|REL Def. §B1.2.2]]). Level C defines parity as an operator on kets, spin included, sorts operators into polar and axial vectors, scalars and pseudoscalars, proves the parity selection rule for any operator of definite parity, and treats a lattice translation as a symmetry whose eigenvalue is the crystal momentum, with tight binding as the model that makes the band visible.

*Notation.* Sakurai's: $\pi$ is the parity operator (Griffiths $\hat\Pi$); $\mathcal T(\mathbf l)$ is the translation of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-7|Theorem §C2.2.7]] (Sakurai writes $\tau(l)$ in §4.3); $\varepsilon_\alpha = \pm1$ is the parity of $|\alpha\rangle$. Kets of a one-electron atom are $|n\ell m\rangle$ or $|n\ell jm\rangle$.

## Space inversion

> [!principle] Principle §C8.2.1: The Parity Operator
> Space inversion acts on the kets of any system by a unitary operator $\pi$, $|\alpha\rangle \to \pi|\alpha\rangle$ (4.41), with the following properties.
> 1. **Position is reversed:** $\langle\alpha|\pi^\dagger\mathbf x\,\pi|\alpha\rangle = -\langle\alpha|\mathbf x|\alpha\rangle$ for every state, realized as
>
> $$
> \pi^\dagger\mathbf x\,\pi = -\mathbf x , \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \mathbf x\,\pi = -\pi\,\mathbf x . \qquad (4.42)\text{–}(4.44)
> $$
>
> 2. **Inversion commutes with rotations:** $\pi\,\mathscr D(R) = \mathscr D(R)\,\pi$ for every rotation (4.55), as the $3\times3$ matrix $-1$ commutes with every rotation matrix (4.53)–(4.54).
> 3. **Phase convention:** $\pi|\mathbf x'\rangle = |-\mathbf x'\rangle$, other labels such as the spin unchanged.
>
> *Domain: every system; the electromagnetic and strong interactions have $[H, \pi] = 0$, the weak interaction does not (Remark: Parity is not conserved). Item 2 is postulated for the unitary operators by analogy with the matrices (Sakurai: "it is natural to postulate"); item 3 is a choice of phases.*
> *Source: Sakurai §4.2, eqs. (4.41)–(4.47), (4.53)–(4.55) · Griffiths §6.1.1, §6.4.1–6.4.2, eqs. (6.16)–(6.17), (6.21) · the user's series, Part IV, §8 (Remark: Parity)*

^pr-c8-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.2.2: Properties of Parity
> 1. $\pi^2 = 1$ and $\pi^{-1} = \pi^\dagger = \pi$ (4.47): $\pi$ is a Hermitian observable with eigenvalues $\pm1$.
> 2. Momentum is odd: $\pi^\dagger\mathbf p\,\pi = -\mathbf p$, $\{\pi, \mathbf p\} = 0$ (4.50).
> 3. Angular momentum is even: $[\pi, \mathbf J] = 0$ (4.56), for orbital angular momentum, spin and their sums alike.
> 4. Wave functions: $\langle\mathbf x'|\pi|\alpha\rangle = \psi_\alpha(-\mathbf x')$ (4.60), and $|\alpha\rangle$ has parity $\pm1$ exactly when $\psi_\alpha(-\mathbf x') = \pm\psi_\alpha(\mathbf x')$ (4.64). More generally $\pi^\dagger f(\mathbf x)\,\pi = f(-\mathbf x)$.
> 5. Orbital angular-momentum eigenkets have parity $(-1)^\ell$: $\pi|\alpha, \ell m\rangle = (-1)^\ell|\alpha, \ell m\rangle$ (4.70), because $Y^m_\ell(-\hat{\mathbf n}) = (-1)^\ell Y^m_\ell(\hat{\mathbf n})$ (4.69).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.2, eqs. (4.45)–(4.52), (4.56), §4.2.1, eqs. (4.59)–(4.70) · Griffiths §6.4, Problems 6.1, 6.8, 6.10 · the user's PHY 511 formula sheet, §"Orbital Angular Momentum" (Parity)*

^thm-c8-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* By Principle §C8.2.1 (1), $\mathbf x\,\pi|\mathbf x'\rangle = -\pi\mathbf x|\mathbf x'\rangle = -\mathbf x'\,\pi|\mathbf x'\rangle$ (4.46), so $\pi|\mathbf x'\rangle$ is a position eigenket with eigenvalue $-\mathbf x'$, $e^{i\delta}|-\mathbf x'\rangle$ in general (4.45) and $|-\mathbf x'\rangle$ by item 3. Then $\pi^2|\mathbf x'\rangle = |\mathbf x'\rangle$ for every base ket, so $\pi^2 = 1$, and with unitarity $\pi^{-1} = \pi^\dagger = \pi$. From $\pi^2 = 1$ every eigenvalue $\varepsilon$ satisfies $\varepsilon^2 = 1$.
>
> *2.* A translation followed by inversion equals inversion followed by the opposite translation (Sakurai Fig. 4.2): $\pi\,\mathcal T(d\mathbf x')|\mathbf x'\rangle = |-\mathbf x' - d\mathbf x'\rangle = \mathcal T(-d\mathbf x')\,\pi|\mathbf x'\rangle$, so $\pi\,\mathcal T(d\mathbf x') = \mathcal T(-d\mathbf x')\,\pi$ (4.48). With $\mathcal T(d\mathbf x') = 1 - i\mathbf p\cdot d\mathbf x'/\hbar$ ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-4|Principle §C2.2.4]]) the first-order terms give $\pi\,\mathbf p\,\pi^\dagger = -\mathbf p$ (4.49), which is $\pi^\dagger\mathbf p\,\pi = -\mathbf p$ since $\pi^\dagger = \pi$.
>
> *3.* Insert $\mathscr D = 1 - \frac i\hbar\mathbf J\cdot\hat n\,d\phi$ ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|Principle §C5.1.2]]) into $\pi\mathscr D = \mathscr D\pi$. For orbital angular momentum it also follows from items 1–2: $\pi^\dagger(\mathbf x\times\mathbf p)\pi = (-\mathbf x)\times(-\mathbf p)$ (4.51)–(4.52).
>
> *4.* $\langle\mathbf x'|\pi = \bigl(\pi^\dagger|\mathbf x'\rangle\bigr)^\dagger = \bigl(\pi|\mathbf x'\rangle\bigr)^\dagger = \langle-\mathbf x'|$. Then $\pi|\alpha\rangle = \pm|\alpha\rangle$ reads $\psi_\alpha(-\mathbf x') = \pm\psi_\alpha(\mathbf x')$ (4.61)–(4.64). For a function of position, $\pi^\dagger f(\mathbf x)\pi|\mathbf x'\rangle = \pi f(\mathbf x)|-\mathbf x'\rangle = f(-\mathbf x')|\mathbf x'\rangle$.
>
> *5.* The wave function is $R(r)Y^m_\ell(\theta, \phi)$ (4.65), and $\mathbf x' \to -\mathbf x'$ is $r \to r$, $\theta \to \pi - \theta$, $\phi \to \phi + \pi$ (4.66). Sakurai's shortcut: for $m = 0$, $Y^0_\ell \propto P_\ell(\cos\theta)$, and $P_\ell(-u) = (-1)^\ell P_\ell(u)$ because Rodrigues' formula ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^def-b5-2-3|Def. §B5.2.3]]) applies $\ell$ derivatives to the even function $(u^2 - 1)^\ell$. The other $m$ are reached by powers of $L_\pm$, which commute with $\pi$ by item 3, so they share the parity of $m = 0$.

> [!definition] Definition §C8.2.1: Polar and Axial Vectors; Scalars and Pseudoscalars
> Among vector operators ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]), a **polar vector** is odd and an **axial vector** (pseudovector) is even under parity; among scalar operators, a **true scalar** is even and a **pseudoscalar** is odd:
>
> | | parity | rotations | examples |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | polar vector $\mathbf V$ | $\pi^\dagger\mathbf V\pi = -\mathbf V$, $\{\pi, V_i\} = 0$ | $[J_i, V_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}V_k$ | $\mathbf x$, $\mathbf p$ |
> | axial vector $\mathbf V$ | $\pi^\dagger\mathbf V\pi = \mathbf V$, $[\pi, V_i] = 0$ | $[J_i, V_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}V_k$ | $\mathbf L$, $\mathbf S$, $\mathbf J$ |
> | true scalar $f$ | $[\pi, f] = 0$ | $[J_i, f] = 0$ | $\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf p$, $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$, $\mathbf p^2$, $r$ |
> | pseudoscalar $f$ | $\{\pi, f\} = 0$ | $[J_i, f] = 0$ | $\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf x$, $\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf p$ |
>
> Rotations do not tell the two kinds apart; parity does.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.2, eqs. (4.56)–(4.58) · Griffiths §6.4.2, Example 6.2, Problem 6.9, Table 6.1 (written there with $\mathbf L$; with spin, $\mathbf J$)*

^def-c8-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from levels A and B
> Level B's parity was the reflection $\psi(x) \to \psi(-x)$ of a one-dimensional wave function, and its theorem rested on the absence of degeneracy in one dimension ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]]; the user's series, §8, is the same two-line argument with the operator $\hat P$). Here $\pi$ is defined on kets by what it does to $\mathbf x$ (Principle §C8.2.1), and everything else is derived: the odd momentum, the even angular momentum including spin, and the $(-1)^\ell$ that [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^rem-b5-2-6|★ Remark: Reading the spherical harmonics]] stated and marked "level C (planned)". This is a layer change in the sense of rule 2: the same results with a reason that does not depend on the dimension or on a wave function. The inversion $\mathbf x \to -\mathbf x$ is also the matrix $P = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$ of relativity ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-2|REL Def. §B1.2.2]]), not connected to the identity; that is why Wigner's theorem leaves its unitarity to be argued separately ([[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-1|Theorem §C8.1.1]]).

^rem-c8-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Intrinsic parity
> On a spin multiplet, the part of $\pi$ acting on the spin commutes with $S_z$ and $S_\pm$ (Theorem §C8.2.2, 3), so it maps each $|s, m_s\rangle$ to a multiple of itself and the multiple is the same for all $m_s$: it is a number (Griffiths Problem 6.12). The number is a convention, set to $+1$ by Principle §C8.2.1 (3); Griffiths notes (footnote 14) that relativistic theory fixes the relative sign of a particle and its antiparticle, the electron $+1$ and the positron $-1$ (Chapter C13★, planned).

## Parity and energy eigenstates

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.2.3: Nondegenerate Eigenkets Have Definite Parity
> Let $[H, \pi] = 0$ (4.71).
> 1. A nondegenerate eigenket $|n\rangle$ of $H$ (4.72) is a parity eigenket, $\pi|n\rangle = \pm|n\rangle$.
> 2. Every eigenspace of $H$ is the orthogonal sum of its even and odd parts, so the eigenkets can always be chosen of definite parity; a degenerate eigenket need not have one.
>
> Examples: the oscillator's $|n\rangle$ has parity $(-1)^n$, since $|0\rangle$ is even and $a^\dagger$, linear in $x$ and $p$, is odd (4.74). Counterexamples, all degenerate: in hydrogen, $c_p|2p\rangle + c_s|2s\rangle$ (4.75); for the free particle, $|\mathbf p'\rangle$, whose wave function $e^{i\mathbf p'\cdot\mathbf x'/\hbar}$ has no parity while $\cos(\mathbf p'\cdot\mathbf x'/\hbar)$ and $\sin(\mathbf p'\cdot\mathbf x'/\hbar)$ do.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.2.1, Theorem 6, eqs. (4.71)–(4.75) · Griffiths §6.4.1 (simultaneous eigenstates; footnote 12)*

^thm-c8-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Put $|\alpha_\pm\rangle = \frac12(1 \pm \pi)|n\rangle$ (4.73). Since $\pi^2 = 1$, $\pi|\alpha_\pm\rangle = \frac12(\pi \pm 1)|n\rangle = \pm|\alpha_\pm\rangle$; since $\pi$ commutes with $H$, $H|\alpha_\pm\rangle = E_n|\alpha_\pm\rangle$. They add to $|n\rangle$, so at least one is nonzero, and by nondegeneracy each nonzero one is a multiple of $|n\rangle$. If both were nonzero, $|n\rangle$ would be an eigenket of $\pi$ with both eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$; so exactly one is nonzero and $\pi|n\rangle = \pm|n\rangle$. (Sakurai: "despite the $\pm$, (4.73) defines just one state.")
>
> *2.* $\pi$ maps each eigenspace $V_E$ into itself ([[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-3|Theorem §C8.1.3]], 1), and $\frac12(1 \pm \pi)$ are complementary orthogonal projections (Hermitian, idempotent because $\pi^2 = 1$, with product zero), which split $V_E$ into its even and odd parts.

*Uses:* [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^thm-c8-2-2|Theorem §C8.2.2]], [[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-3|Theorem §C8.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Operators that anticommute
> If two Hermitian operators anticommute, $AB = -BA$, and $|\Psi\rangle$ is an eigenket of both with eigenvalues $a$, $b$, then $ab|\Psi\rangle = AB|\Psi\rangle = -BA|\Psi\rangle = -ba|\Psi\rangle$, so $ab = 0$ (Sakurai Problem 4.3; Likharev Problem 4.24). With $A = \pi$ (eigenvalues $\pm1$, never zero) and $B = p_x$: a parity eigenket has $p_x = 0$ if it is a momentum eigenket at all, which is why only the zero-momentum plane wave has a parity. More usefully, the expectation value of an odd operator vanishes in every parity eigenket: $\pi^\dagger B\pi = -B$ gives $\langle\alpha|B|\alpha\rangle = -\langle\alpha|\pi^\dagger B\pi|\alpha\rangle = -\varepsilon_\alpha^2\langle\alpha|B|\alpha\rangle = -\langle\alpha|B|\alpha\rangle$.

^rem-c8-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.2.4: Tunnelling in a Symmetric Double Well
> Let $V(-x) = V(x)$ be a one-dimensional double well whose two lowest levels, $|S\rangle$ (even) and $|A\rangle$ (odd), are far below the others. Then:
> 1. $E_S < E_A$ (4.76).
> 2. The kets $|R\rangle = (|S\rangle + |A\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and $|L\rangle = (|S\rangle - |A\rangle)/\sqrt2$ (4.77), concentrated in the right and left wells (Figure), are exchanged by parity and are not stationary. Starting from $|R\rangle$, up to an overall phase
>
> $$
> |R; t\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(|S\rangle + e^{-i\omega t}|A\rangle\bigr), \qquad |\langle L|R; t\rangle|^2 = \sin^2\frac{\omega t}{2}, \qquad \omega = \frac{E_A - E_S}{\hbar} : \qquad (4.78),\ (4.79)
> $$
>
> the particle tunnels to the left well in the time $\pi/\omega$ and back.
> 3. If the middle barrier becomes infinitely high, $E_A - E_S \to 0$: $|R\rangle$ and $|L\rangle$ become stationary, and the ground level is degenerate and has eigenkets that are not parity eigenkets, although $H$ is even.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.2.2, eqs. (4.76)–(4.79), Figs. 4.3–4.4 · Likharev §2.6 (two coupled wells)*

^thm-c8-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* In one dimension bound states are nondegenerate, the ground state has no node and the next state one ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]]). An odd function vanishes at $0$, so the nodeless ground state is the even $|S\rangle$; Sakurai's reason, that $|A\rangle$ curves more, is the same fact.
>
> *2.* $\pi|S\rangle = |S\rangle$ and $\pi|A\rangle = -|A\rangle$ give $\pi|R\rangle = |L\rangle$. Each stationary state picks up its own phase, $|R; t\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(e^{-iE_St/\hbar}|S\rangle + e^{-iE_At/\hbar}|A\rangle\bigr)$ ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-5|Theorem §C3.1.5]]), which is the display times $e^{-iE_St/\hbar}$. Then $\langle L|R; t\rangle = \frac12(1 - e^{-i\omega t})$, whose squared modulus is $\frac14\cdot 2(1 - \cos\omega t) = \sin^2(\omega t/2)$. In the basis $|R\rangle$, $|L\rangle$ this is the two-state system of [[§C3.2 Two-State Systems, Spin Precession and Neutrino Oscillations#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]] with diagonal elements $\frac12(E_S + E_A)$ and off-diagonal $-\frac12(E_A - E_S)$.
>
> *3.* With an infinite barrier the two halves decouple; each well alone has the same spectrum, so the even and odd combinations of the single-well ground states are degenerate, and every combination of them, $|R\rangle$ and $|L\rangle$ in particular, is stationary. (Example §C8.2.1 shows $E_A - E_S \propto e^{-2\kappa a}$ quantitatively.)

> [!remark] Remark: Broken symmetry, ammonia and handed molecules
> Part 3 is the simplest case of a **broken symmetry**: with degeneracy, the stationary states need not share the symmetry of $H$ (Sakurai: "the symmetry of the world need not be the symmetry of energy eigenkets"). A ferromagnet is the large version: a rotationally invariant Hamiltonian, ground states with all spins along one arbitrary direction. Ammonia is the finite-barrier version: the nitrogen atom above or below the plane of the hydrogens plays $|R\rangle$ and $|L\rangle$, and the splitting corresponds to about $24{,}000$ MHz, a 1-cm microwave (Sakurai; level A quotes $2.3786\times10^{10}$ Hz in [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-6|Remark: Tunnel devices and the ammonia clock]], and [[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]] has the bonding and antibonding pair). Many organic molecules, sugars and amino acids, occur as one handed form only; their tunnelling time is of order $10^4$–$10^6$ years, so in practice they are $|R\rangle$ or $|L\rangle$ for good (Sakurai §4.2.2).

## Parity selection rules

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.2.5: Parity Selection Rule
> Let $\pi|\alpha\rangle = \varepsilon_\alpha|\alpha\rangle$, $\pi|\beta\rangle = \varepsilon_\beta|\beta\rangle$, and let $A$ have definite parity, $\pi^\dagger A\,\pi = \varepsilon_AA$. Then
>
> $$
> \langle\beta|A|\alpha\rangle = 0 \qquad\text{unless}\qquad \varepsilon_\beta = \varepsilon_A\,\varepsilon_\alpha .
> $$
>
> 1. Odd operators ($\mathbf x$, $\mathbf p$, $\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf x$) connect only states of opposite parity, even operators ($\mathbf L$, $\mathbf S$, $\mathbf x^2$) only states of the same parity. For $A = \mathbf x$ this is $\langle\beta|\mathbf x|\alpha\rangle = 0$ unless $\varepsilon_\alpha = -\varepsilon_\beta$ (4.81), Laporte's rule.
> 2. If $[H, \pi] = 0$, a nondegenerate energy eigenket has no permanent electric dipole moment: $\langle n|\mathbf x|n\rangle = 0$ (4.84).
> 3. For a one-electron atom, $|n\ell jm\rangle$ has parity $(-1)^\ell$, and a spherical tensor built from position, $T^{(k)}_q \propto r^kY^q_k(\hat{\mathbf x})$, has $\varepsilon_T = (-1)^k$; its matrix elements vanish unless $\ell + \ell' + k$ is even.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.2.3, eqs. (4.80)–(4.84) · Griffiths §6.4.3, eqs. (6.25)–(6.26), Problem 6.11 · the user's PHY 511 formula sheet, §"Spherical Tensors from Spherical Harmonics" (Parity Selection Rule) · the user's series, Part IV, §16*

^thm-c8-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Since $\pi^\dagger = \pi$ and $\pi\pi^\dagger = 1$, $\langle\beta|A|\alpha\rangle = \varepsilon_A\langle\beta|\pi^\dagger A\,\pi|\alpha\rangle = \varepsilon_A\,(\pi|\beta\rangle)^\dagger A\,(\pi|\alpha\rangle) = \varepsilon_A\varepsilon_\beta\varepsilon_\alpha\,\langle\beta|A|\alpha\rangle$ (4.82), using that the $\varepsilon$'s are real. Unless $\varepsilon_A\varepsilon_\beta\varepsilon_\alpha = 1$, the matrix element equals its own negative. *1* is the case $\varepsilon_A = \pm1$, with $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf p$ odd and $\mathbf L$, $\mathbf S$ even by Theorem §C8.2.2. *2*: by Theorem §C8.2.3 such an $|n\rangle$ has a parity, and $\beta = \alpha$ in 1. *3*: the angular part of $|n\ell jm\rangle$ is a combination of $Y^{m_\ell}_\ell$ times spin states, all of parity $(-1)^\ell$ (Theorem §C8.2.2, 5 and Remark: Intrinsic parity); and $\pi^\dagger f(\mathbf x)\pi = f(-\mathbf x)$ with $r^kY^q_k(-\hat{\mathbf x}) = (-1)^kr^kY^q_k(\hat{\mathbf x})$. Then $(-1)^{\ell'} = (-1)^k(-1)^\ell$.

^der-c8-2-5

*Uses:* [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^thm-c8-2-2|Theorem §C8.2.2]], [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^thm-c8-2-3|Theorem §C8.2.3]], [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^def-c8-2-1|Def. §C8.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The dipole rules completed
> Combined with the Wigner–Eckart theorem this finishes the electric-dipole rules. The rank-1 tensor $\mathbf x$ allows $|\ell' - \ell| \le 1$ and $m' = m + q$ ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-7|Theorem §C7.2.7]], whose derivation did the parity step as an integral and pointed here), and Theorem §C8.2.5, 3 with $k = 1$ removes $\ell' = \ell$: $\Delta\ell = \pm1$. That is the part of level A's rules marked "parity, level C (planned)" ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-2|Theorem §A5.4.2]]); that transition *rates* are governed by $|\langle f|\mathbf x|i\rangle|^2$ still needs time-dependent perturbation theory and the atom–field interaction ([[§C9.6 The Interaction Picture and the Atom–Field Interaction]]). The rule was found empirically in spectra as Laporte's rule; Wigner showed it is the parity selection rule (Sakurai). The user's series draws the consequence: $2s \to 1s$ is dipole-forbidden, and the $2s$ level is metastable. The electric quadrupole, $k = 2$, connects equal parities, $\Delta\ell = 0, \pm2$ (not $0 \to 0$), as the user's formula sheet lists. Degenerate states may carry a permanent dipole: the hydrogen combinations $(\psi_{200} \pm \psi_{210})/\sqrt2$ have $\langle z\rangle = \mp3a$, the origin of the linear Stark effect ([[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^ex-b8-2-2|Example §B8.2.2]]; Griffiths Problem 6.13).

^rem-c8-2-5

> [!caution] Caution: The parity of a tensor operator is not always $(-1)^k$
> The user's formula sheet states the parity rule for a tensor operator "with parity $\pi_T = (-1)^k$ (e.g., built from $Y^q_k$ or position vectors)", and that qualifier matters. Axial vectors are rank-1 tensors with $\varepsilon_T = +1$: the magnetic-dipole operator $\propto \mathbf L + 2\mathbf S$ connects states of the *same* parity, the opposite of the electric dipole. Pseudoscalars are rank-0 tensors with $\varepsilon_T = -1$, as in Example §C8.2.2. Rotations fix $k$; the parity must be read off separately (Def. §C8.2.1).

^cau-c8-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Parity is not conserved
> The weak interaction is not invariant under parity. Lee and Yang proposed in 1956 that it might not be, and Wu et al. (1957) found it in the beta decay $^{60}\mathrm{Co} \to {}^{60}\mathrm{Ni} + e^- + \bar\nu_e$ of polarized nuclei: electrons are emitted preferentially opposite to the nuclear spin, a rate depending on the pseudoscalar $\langle\mathbf S\rangle\cdot\mathbf p$, which would be impossible with a parity-invariant $H$ (Sakurai §4.2.4, Fig. 4.6). As a consequence, states once thought to have pure parity carry tiny admixtures of the opposite parity (Example §C8.2.2). Relativity puts it the other way round: the postulates require invariance only under the proper orthochronous Lorentz group, and whether a law also respects $P$ is a question for the dynamics ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^rem-b1-2-2|REL Remark: Parity and time reversal are questions for dynamics]]).

## Lattice translations

> [!definition] Definition §C8.2.2: Periodic Potential; Lattice Translation
> A one-dimensional potential is **periodic** with period $a$ if $V(x \pm a) = V(x)$; in three dimensions, if $V(\mathbf r + \mathbf R) = V(\mathbf r)$ for every vector $\mathbf R = n_1\mathbf a_1 + n_2\mathbf a_2 + n_3\mathbf a_3$ ($n_j$ integers) of a **Bravais lattice** with primitive vectors $\mathbf a_j$. The **lattice translations** are the translation operators $\mathcal T(\mathbf R) = e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf R/\hbar}$ of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-7|Theorem §C2.2.7]], with
>
> $$
> \mathcal T^\dagger(\mathbf R)\,\mathbf x\,\mathcal T(\mathbf R) = \mathbf x + \mathbf R , \qquad \mathcal T(\mathbf R)|\mathbf x'\rangle = |\mathbf x' + \mathbf R\rangle . \qquad (4.85)
> $$
>
> For a periodic potential $\mathcal T^\dagger(\mathbf R)V(\mathbf x)\mathcal T(\mathbf R) = V(\mathbf x + \mathbf R) = V(\mathbf x)$ (4.86), the kinetic energy is invariant under every translation, and so
>
> $$
> \mathcal T^\dagger(\mathbf R)\,H\,\mathcal T(\mathbf R) = H , \qquad [H, \mathcal T(\mathbf R)] = 0 \qquad (4.87),\ (4.88)
> $$
>
> for lattice vectors, though not for other displacements. $\mathcal T(\mathbf R)$ is unitary but not Hermitian, so its eigenvalues are phases.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.3, eqs. (4.85)–(4.88) · Griffiths §6.2.2, eq. (6.10), Fig. 6.6 · Likharev §3.4, eqs. (3.105)–(3.106)*

^def-c8-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: Which way a translation operator shifts
> Sakurai's $\tau(l)$, this vault's $\mathcal T(l)$ and Griffiths' $\hat T(a)$ (his (6.1)) move the system to the right: the wave function becomes $\psi(x - a)$. Level B's $\hat T_a$ ([[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^def-b7-3-1|Def. §B7.3.1]]) is $\hat T_af(x) = f(x + a)$, that is $\mathcal T(-a) = e^{+ipa/\hbar}$. So an eigenket with $\mathcal T(a)|\psi\rangle = e^{-i\theta}|\psi\rangle$ has $\psi(x + a) = e^{i\theta}\psi(x)$, the form of [[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^thm-b7-3-4|Theorem §B7.3.4]] with $\theta = qa$.

^cau-c8-2-2

> [!model] Model §C8.2.6: Tight-Binding Chain
> A particle in a one-dimensional periodic potential with high barriers between the sites (Sakurai Fig. 4.7). Assumptions:
> 1. each site $n$ carries one localized ket $|n\rangle$, all related by translation, $\mathcal T(a)|n\rangle = |n + 1\rangle$ (4.89), and orthonormal;
> 2. $H$ has equal diagonal elements, $\langle n|H|n\rangle = E_0$ (4.92);
> 3. only neighbouring sites are coupled by tunnelling, $\langle n'|H|n\rangle = 0$ unless $n' = n$ or $n \pm 1$ (4.93), with $\langle n \pm 1|H|n\rangle = -\Delta$ (4.94), the same for all $n$ by translation invariance;
> 4. other states of the sites are far away in energy.
>
> Then $H|n\rangle = E_0|n\rangle - \Delta|n + 1\rangle - \Delta|n - 1\rangle$ (4.95). For infinitely high barriers $\Delta = 0$, and every $|n\rangle$ is an eigenket with energy $E_0$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.3, eqs. (4.89)–(4.95) · Likharev §2.7 (tight-binding approximation)*

^mod-c8-2-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.2.7: The Tight-Binding Band
> In Model §C8.2.6 the kets
>
> $$
> |\theta\rangle = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{in\theta}|n\rangle , \qquad -\pi < \theta \le \pi , \qquad (4.90),\ (4.96)
> $$
>
> are simultaneous eigenkets of the lattice translation and of $H$:
>
> $$
> \mathcal T(a)|\theta\rangle = e^{-i\theta}|\theta\rangle , \qquad H|\theta\rangle = (E_0 - 2\Delta\cos\theta)\,|\theta\rangle . \qquad (4.91),\ (4.97)
> $$
>
> With $\theta = ka$, $E(k) = E_0 - 2\Delta\cos ka$ for $|k| \le \pi/a$ (4.103): the infinitely degenerate level $E_0$ of $\Delta = 0$ spreads into a band from $E_0 - 2\Delta$ to $E_0 + 2\Delta$, independent of the shape of the potential. The $|\theta\rangle$ are orthogonal in the delta-normalized sense, $\langle\theta'|\theta\rangle = 2\pi\,\delta(\theta - \theta')$. On a ring of $N$ sites, $|n + N\rangle = |n\rangle$, the allowed values are $\theta = 2\pi j/N$, $j = 0, 1, \dots, N - 1$, giving $N$ levels on the curve.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.3, eqs. (4.90)–(4.97), (4.103), Figs. 4.8–4.9 · Likharev §2.7, Problem 3.15*

^thm-c8-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Translation: $\mathcal T(a)|\theta\rangle = \sum_ne^{in\theta}|n + 1\rangle = \sum_ne^{i(n-1)\theta}|n\rangle = e^{-i\theta}|\theta\rangle$ (4.91). Energy, with (4.95) and the same shift of summation index:
>
> $$
> H|\theta\rangle = E_0\sum_ne^{in\theta}|n\rangle - \Delta\sum_n\bigl(e^{i(n-1)\theta} + e^{i(n+1)\theta}\bigr)|n\rangle = (E_0 - 2\Delta\cos\theta)\,|\theta\rangle .
> $$
>
> Normalization: by orthonormality $\langle\theta'|\theta\rangle = \sum_ne^{in(\theta - \theta')}$, the Fourier series of $2\pi\delta(\theta - \theta')$ on $(-\pi, \pi]$, as for momentum kets ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]]). On the ring $\mathcal T(a)^N = 1$, so $e^{-iN\theta} = 1$. At $\Delta = 0$ both $|n\rangle$ and $|\theta\rangle$ are eigenkets of $H$ with the same $E_0$; only the $|\theta\rangle$ are eigenkets of $\mathcal T(a)$, which the infinite degeneracy permits ([[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-3|Theorem §C8.1.3]]).

*Uses:* [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^mod-c8-2-6|Model §C8.2.6]], [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]], [[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-3|Theorem §C8.1.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.2.8: Bloch's Theorem from Lattice Translations
> Let $V$ be periodic with Bravais lattice $\{\mathbf R\}$ (Def. §C8.2.2).
> 1. The lattice translations commute with one another and with $H$, so the energy eigenkets can be chosen as common eigenkets of all of them, $\mathcal T(\mathbf R)|\psi\rangle = e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}|\psi\rangle$, labelled by a wave vector $\mathbf k$ determined modulo the reciprocal lattice ($\mathbf G$ with $\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf a_j \in 2\pi\mathbb Z$).
> 2. The wave function of any such eigenket satisfies
>
> $$
> \psi(\mathbf r + \mathbf R) = e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}\,\psi(\mathbf r) , \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \psi(\mathbf r) = e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\,u_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r) , \quad u_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r + \mathbf R) = u_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r) . \qquad (4.100)\text{–}(4.102)
> $$
>
> In one dimension, $\mathcal T(a)|\theta\rangle = e^{-i\theta}|\theta\rangle$ gives $\psi(x' - a) = e^{-i\theta}\psi(x')$ and $\psi = e^{ikx'}u_k(x')$ with $\theta = ka$ (4.98)–(4.101). Only the eigenvalue of $\mathcal T(a)$ is used, so the theorem holds beyond tight binding.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.3, eqs. (4.98)–(4.102) · Griffiths §6.2.2, eqs. (6.11)–(6.12), Problem 6.5 · Likharev §3.4, eqs. (3.107)–(3.108), Problem 4.31*

^thm-c8-2-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* All $\mathcal T(\mathbf R)$ are functions of the commuting momenta ($[p_i, p_j] = 0$, [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-6|Theorem §C2.2.6]]), so they commute with one another, and with $H$ by (4.88). By [[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-3|Theorem §C8.1.3]], 2 (on a crystal made finite by periodic boundary conditions, as in [[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^thm-b7-3-4|Theorem §B7.3.4]]), every energy eigenspace has a basis of common eigenkets, on which each $\mathcal T(\mathbf a_j)$ acts by a phase $e^{-i\theta_j}$. Choose $\mathbf k$ with $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf a_j = \theta_j$, possible because the $\mathbf a_j$ are a basis; then $\mathcal T(\mathbf R) = \prod_j\mathcal T(\mathbf a_j)^{n_j}$ acts by $e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}$. Adding a $\mathbf G$ with $\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf a_j \in 2\pi\mathbb Z$ changes no phase.
>
> *2.* $\langle\mathbf r|\mathcal T(\mathbf R)|\psi\rangle = \psi(\mathbf r - \mathbf R)$ ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-7|Theorem §C2.2.7]], 2), so $\psi(\mathbf r - \mathbf R) = e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}\psi(\mathbf r)$ for all $\mathbf R$, which is the first form (replace $\mathbf R$ by $-\mathbf R$). Put $u_{\mathbf k} = e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\psi$: $u_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r + \mathbf R) = e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}\psi(\mathbf r) = u_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)$ (4.102); conversely this form satisfies the first.

^der-c8-2-8

*Uses:* [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-6|Theorem §C2.2.6]], [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-7|Theorem §C2.2.7]], [[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-3|Theorem §C8.1.3]], [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^def-c8-2-2|Def. §C8.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from level B, and crystal momentum
> The one-dimensional proof of [[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^thm-b7-3-4|Theorem §B7.3.4]] is this one: a unitary translation commuting with $H$ acts by a phase on a common eigenbasis, and level B is not repeated (rule 1). What level C adds is the three-dimensional form, in which the Abelian group of lattice translations is diagonalized at once and $\mathbf k$ appears as the label of its eigenvalues, and the tight-binding model, where the band can be seen forming as $\Delta$ grows. It also answers the two forward pointers: [[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^rem-b7-3-2|the remark after Theorem §B7.3.4]] (Griffiths postponed the proof to his chapter 6), and the question of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-1|Principle §C2.2.1]], whether $H$ is translation invariant: for a crystal only under the lattice, so momentum is not conserved, but the phase $e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}$ is ([[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^rem-c8-1-3|Remark: Discrete symmetries and conservation]]). $\hbar\mathbf k$ is the **crystal momentum**, conserved modulo $\hbar\mathbf G$. Bloch waves are, apart from the periodic factor, travelling waves, which is why an electron can cross a perfect crystal without scattering (Griffiths §6.2.2). The interval $|k| \le \pi/a$ is the (first) Brillouin zone; Sakurai's text attaches the name to the band of energies, but it denotes the range of $k$. That $E(\mathbf k) = E(-\mathbf k)$ even without inversion symmetry is a consequence of time reversal ([[§C8.3★ Time Reversal]]; Griffiths footnote 48).

^rem-c8-2-7

> [!remark] Remark: Two wells and infinitely many
> The double well and the periodic chain are the two extremes of a potential with a finite number of troughs (Sakurai): two localized kets give an even and an odd combination split by tunnelling, $N$ give $N$ combinations $\theta = 2\pi j/N$ spread over a band, and both become degenerate when the barriers become impenetrable. In each case the localized kets are permuted by the symmetry, and the symmetric combinations are found by diagonalizing the symmetry operator, $\pi$ or $\mathcal T(a)$.

^rem-c8-2-8

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C8.2.1: Tunnel Splitting of a Rectangular Double Well
> Sakurai Problem 4.6: $V = \infty$ for $|x| > a + b$, $V = 0$ for $a < |x| < a + b$, $V = V_0 > 0$ for $|x| < a$. Assuming $V_0$ much larger than the low-lying energies, find the splitting of the two lowest levels.
>
> *Matching.* Let $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and $\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}/\hbar$. In the right well $\psi \propto \sin k(a + b - x)$, which vanishes at the wall; in the barrier $\psi \propto \cosh\kappa x$ for $|S\rangle$ and $\sinh\kappa x$ for $|A\rangle$ (Theorem §C8.2.3). Matching $\psi'/\psi$ at $x = a$:
>
> $$
> k\cot kb = -\kappa\tanh\kappa a \quad (S) , \qquad k\cot kb = -\kappa\coth\kappa a \quad (A) .
> $$
>
> *Expansion.* For $\kappa \gg k$ the right sides are large, so $kb$ is just below $\pi$: put $kb = \pi - \delta$, $\cot kb = -\cot\delta \approx -1/\delta$. Then $\delta \approx k/(\kappa\tanh\kappa a)$ for $S$ and $k/(\kappa\coth\kappa a)$ for $A$, and
>
> $$
> \delta_S - \delta_A \approx \frac k\kappa(\coth\kappa a - \tanh\kappa a) = \frac{k}{\kappa}\,\frac{2}{\sinh 2\kappa a} \approx \frac{4k}{\kappa}\,e^{-2\kappa a} .
> $$
>
> Since $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ with $k = (\pi - \delta)/b$, $dE/d\delta = -\hbar^2k/mb$, and with $k \approx \pi/b$, $\kappa \approx \kappa_0 = \sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar$:
>
> $$
> E_A - E_S \approx \frac{\hbar^2k}{mb}(\delta_S - \delta_A) \approx \frac{4\pi^2\hbar^2}{mb^3\kappa_0}\,e^{-2\kappa_0a} = \frac{8E_0}{\kappa_0b}\,e^{-2\kappa_0a} , \qquad E_0 = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mb^2} ,
> $$
>
> where $E_0$ is the ground level of one well alone. Valid for $\kappa_0b \gg 1$ and $\kappa_0a \gg 1$.
>
> *Reading.* The splitting falls off as $e^{-2\kappa_0a}$, the square of the amplitude decay across the barrier of width $2a$, the same exponent as a WKB transmission probability $e^{-2\gamma}$ ([[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^thm-b9-4-2|Theorem §B9.4.2]]); the tunnelling time $\pi\hbar/(E_A - E_S)$ of Theorem §C8.2.4 grows accordingly. Check, in units $\hbar = 2m = 1$ with $b = 1$, $a = 0.5$, $V_0 = 100$: solving the matching conditions numerically gives $E_A - E_S = 3.9\times10^{-4}$, the formula $3.6\times10^{-4}$ (computed here). Both levels also lie below $E_0$, by about $2E_0/\kappa_0b$, because the wave function leaks into the barrier.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 4.6; solved here*

> [!example] Example §C8.2.2: A Parity-Violating Admixture in an Alkali Atom
> Sakurai Problem 4.5: the weak interaction adds to an alkali atom the potential $V = \lambda\bigl[\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x)\,\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf p + \mathbf S\cdot\mathbf p\,\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x)\bigr]$, with the nucleus at the origin. Which states $|n'\ell'j'm'\rangle$ are mixed into the ground state $|n, \ell = 0, j = \frac12, m\rangle$, and how would one compute the coefficients?
>
> *Symmetry alone.* $\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf p$ is a scalar under rotations and odd under parity, a pseudoscalar (Def. §C8.2.1), and $\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x)$ is a true scalar; $V$ is a Hermitian pseudoscalar. A scalar connects only equal $j$ and $m$ (Wigner–Eckart with $k = 0$, [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-6|Theorem §C7.2.6]]); an odd operator connects only opposite parities (Theorem §C8.2.5). From $\ell = 0$, $j = \frac12$: $j' = \frac12$, $m' = m$, and $(-1)^{\ell'} = -1$ with $\ell' = j' \pm \frac12$, so $\ell' = 1$. Only the $n'p_{1/2}$ states, with the same $m$, mix in.
>
> *Coefficients.* To first order ([[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]]) $C_{n'} = \langle n'p_{1/2}m|V|ns_{1/2}m\rangle/(E_{ns} - E_{n'p})$, which needs the radial functions only at the origin. The term $\delta^{(3)}\,\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf p$ gives $\psi^\dagger_{n'p}(0)\cdots$, which vanishes because $p$-waves vanish at the origin; the term $\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf p\,\delta^{(3)}$ gives $[(\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf p)\psi_{n'p}](0)^\dagger\,\psi_{ns}(0)$, proportional to $R'_{n'1}(0)\,R_{n0}(0)$, and both factors are nonzero. So there is no further restriction on $n'$: every $p_{1/2}$ level (and the continuum) contributes, weighted by the energy denominators.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 4.5; solved here*

> [!example] Example §C8.2.3: A Ring of Wells
> Find the tight-binding levels of $N$ identical wells on a ring (each coupled to its two neighbours by $-\Delta$), and in particular of three wells at the corners of an equilateral triangle (Likharev Problem 3.15).
>
> *Levels.* The ring is Model §C8.2.6 with $|n + N\rangle = |n\rangle$; the rotation by one site is $\mathcal T$ with $\mathcal T^N = 1$, and by Theorem §C8.2.7
>
> $$
> E_j = E_0 - 2\Delta\cos\frac{2\pi j}{N} , \qquad |\theta_j\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{2\pi ijn/N}|n\rangle , \qquad j = 0, 1, \dots, N - 1 .
> $$
>
> For $N = 3$ and $\Delta > 0$: a nondegenerate ground level $E_0 - 2\Delta$ (the symmetric combination) and a twofold level $E_0 + \Delta$ ($j = 1, 2$). The figure shows $N = 8$: eight points on the band curve, pairs $\pm j$ degenerate, single levels at the band edges.
>
> *Why the pairs.* $j$ and $N - j \equiv -j$ have the same energy because $\cos$ is even. The translation group is Abelian and forces nothing (Theorem §C8.1.3, 2); the degeneracy comes from a second symmetry that reverses the sense of the ring, a reflection $|n\rangle \to |-n\rangle$, which maps $|\theta\rangle$ to $|-\theta\rangle$ and does not commute with $\mathcal T$. Even without a reflection symmetry, time reversal, complex conjugation of the real coefficients, maps $|\theta\rangle$ to $|-\theta\rangle$ ([[§C8.3★ Time Reversal]]).
>
> *Source: Likharev Problem 3.15 · Sakurai §4.3; solved here*
