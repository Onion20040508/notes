*Sources: the user's series, Part IV, §11, §17 · Sakurai & Napolitano, Modern Quantum Mechanics (3rd ed.), §4.4 · Griffiths & Schroeter, Introduction to Quantum Mechanics (3rd ed.), Problems 6.36–6.37 and footnotes 44–48 of ch. 6.*

What does it mean to run a quantum motion backwards, and what does that symmetry imply? Classically, a trajectory traversed with reversed velocities is again a solution of Newton's equations in a static potential, and of the Lorentz-force equations if the magnetic field is reversed with its currents (Maxwell's equations, [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^pr-b8-4-2|EM Principle §B8.4.2]]). Level B met the quantum shadow of this without naming it: complex conjugation maps solutions to solutions, so stationary states can be taken real ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]]); relativity lists $T$ among the Lorentz transformations ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^def-b1-2-2|REL Def. §B1.2.2]]); and Wigner's theorem left room for symmetries implemented by antiunitary operators ([[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-1|Theorem §C8.1.1]]; [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^rem-c5-1-3|★ Remark: Why unitary, and why only up to a phase]]), the deferral that the user's series repeats in its outlook (§17). Level C shows that reversal of motion must be antiunitary, works out how it acts on spinless particles and on spin, and derives its consequences: real wave functions, quenching of angular momentum, vanishing expectation values, and Kramers degeneracy for an odd number of electrons.

*Notation.* Sakurai's: $\theta$ is a general antiunitary operator, $\Theta$ the time-reversal operator, $K$ complex conjugation; $|\tilde\alpha\rangle = \theta|\alpha\rangle$. Spin states $|\pm\rangle$ are $S_z$ eigenkets, $|\hat{\mathbf n}; \pm\rangle$ eigenkets of $\mathbf S\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$.

## Antiunitary operators

> [!definition] Definition §C8.3.1: Antilinear and Antiunitary Operators; Complex Conjugation
> An operator $\theta$ is **antilinear** if
>
> $$
> \theta\bigl(c_1|\alpha\rangle + c_2|\beta\rangle\bigr) = c_1^*\,\theta|\alpha\rangle + c_2^*\,\theta|\beta\rangle , \qquad (4.116b)
> $$
>
> and **antiunitary** if moreover it is invertible and $\langle\tilde\beta|\tilde\alpha\rangle = \langle\beta|\alpha\rangle^{\ast}$ for all kets (4.116a). Given an orthonormal basis $\{|a'\rangle\}$, the **complex-conjugation operator** $K$ conjugates the expansion coefficients and leaves the base kets alone:
>
> $$
> K\sum_{a'}|a'\rangle\langle a'|\alpha\rangle = \sum_{a'}\langle a'|\alpha\rangle^*\,|a'\rangle . \qquad (4.118),\ (4.119)
> $$
>
> $K$ depends on the basis: in the $S_z$ basis it exchanges the two $S_y$ eigenkets, $K\bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}|+\rangle \pm \frac{i}{\sqrt2}|-\rangle\bigr) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}|+\rangle \mp \frac{i}{\sqrt2}|-\rangle$ (4.121), while in the $S_y$ basis it fixes them.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.4.1, Definition 1, eqs. (4.115)–(4.121) · Griffiths ch. 6, footnote 46*

^def-c8-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.3.1: Structure of Antiunitary Operators
> 1. $K$ is antiunitary and $K^2 = 1$. For every unitary $U$, $\theta = UK$ is antiunitary (4.117); conversely every antiunitary $\theta$ is of this form, with $U = \theta K$.
> 2. The product of two antiunitary operators is unitary; that of an antiunitary and a unitary operator is antiunitary.
> 3. For every linear operator $X$,
>
> $$
> \langle\beta|X|\alpha\rangle = \langle\tilde\alpha|\,\theta X^\dagger\theta^{-1}\,|\tilde\beta\rangle . \qquad (4.139)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.4.1, eqs. (4.117), (4.122)–(4.124), §4.4.2, eqs. (4.139)–(4.142); the converse in 1 and item 2 added here*

^thm-c8-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Antilinearity of $K$ is (4.118). With $|\alpha\rangle = \sum c_{a'}|a'\rangle$, $|\beta\rangle = \sum d_{a'}|a'\rangle$: $\langle K\beta|K\alpha\rangle = \sum d_{a'}c^{\ast}_{a'} = \bigl(\sum d^{\ast}_{a'}c_{a'}\bigr)^{\ast} = \langle\beta|\alpha\rangle^{\ast}$, and $K^2 = 1$. For $\theta = UK$: $\theta(c|\alpha\rangle) = U(c^{\ast}K|\alpha\rangle) = c^{\ast}\theta|\alpha\rangle$ (4.122), and $\langle\theta\beta|\theta\alpha\rangle = \langle K\beta|U^\dagger U|K\alpha\rangle = \langle\beta|\alpha\rangle^{\ast}$ (Sakurai checks this by expanding both kets, (4.123)–(4.124)). Conversely, $U = \theta K$ is linear (two conjugations cancel) and $\langle\theta K\beta|\theta K\alpha\rangle = \langle K\beta|K\alpha\rangle^{\ast} = \langle\beta|\alpha\rangle$, so it is unitary, and $\theta = \theta K^2 = UK$.
>
> *2.* Two antilinear factors give a linear product, which preserves inner products by applying (4.116a) twice; one antilinear factor leaves the product antilinear, and the inner product is conjugated once.
>
> *3.* Let $|\gamma\rangle = X^\dagger|\beta\rangle$, whose bra is $\langle\beta|X$. Then $\langle\beta|X|\alpha\rangle = \langle\gamma|\alpha\rangle = \langle\tilde\alpha|\tilde\gamma\rangle$ by (4.116a), and $|\tilde\gamma\rangle = \theta X^\dagger|\beta\rangle = \theta X^\dagger\theta^{-1}|\tilde\beta\rangle$ (4.140)–(4.142).

^der-c8-3-1

*Uses:* [[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^def-c8-3-1|Def. §C8.3.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Antiunitary operators act on kets only
> Dirac's notation was made for linear operators. For antiunitary $\Theta$, $\langle\beta|\Theta|\alpha\rangle$ always means $\langle\beta|\cdot\bigl(\Theta|\alpha\rangle\bigr)$ (4.136), never $\bigl(\langle\beta|\Theta\bigr)\cdot|\alpha\rangle$ (4.137); a bra $\langle\beta|\Theta$ is not defined, and neither is $\Theta^\dagger$: everything is written with $\Theta^{-1}$ (Sakurai (4.135)–(4.138); Griffiths, footnote 46, likewise avoids $\Theta^\dagger$). In particular $\langle\beta|\Theta X\Theta^{-1}|\alpha\rangle$ is a matrix element of the *linear* operator $\Theta X\Theta^{-1}$, which is safe.

## The time-reversal operator

> [!principle] Principle §C8.3.2: Reversal of Motion
> There is an operator $\Theta$, invertible and preserving $|\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle|$, that takes each state to the **motion-reversed** state: positions unchanged, momenta and angular momenta reversed,
>
> $$
> \langle\tilde\alpha|\mathbf x|\tilde\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|\mathbf x|\alpha\rangle , \qquad \langle\tilde\alpha|\mathbf p|\tilde\alpha\rangle = -\langle\alpha|\mathbf p|\alpha\rangle , \qquad \langle\tilde\alpha|\mathbf J|\tilde\alpha\rangle = -\langle\alpha|\mathbf J|\alpha\rangle , \qquad (4.147),\ (4.151)
> $$
>
> realized as $\Theta\mathbf x\Theta^{-1} = \mathbf x$, $\Theta\mathbf p\Theta^{-1} = -\mathbf p$, $\Theta\mathbf J\Theta^{-1} = -\mathbf J$ (4.148), (4.150), (4.156). A system is **time-reversal invariant** if reversing the state and then evolving it forward gives the reversal of the state evolved backward: for every $|\alpha\rangle$ and small $\delta t$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(1 - \frac{iH}{\hbar}\,\delta t\Bigr)\Theta|\alpha\rangle = \Theta\Bigl(1 - \frac{iH}{\hbar}\,(-\delta t)\Bigr)|\alpha\rangle . \qquad (4.127),\ (4.128)
> $$
>
> *Domain: "time reversal" is a misnomer for reversal of motion (Wigner's term, Sakurai). The definition applies to any system; invariance holds for a closed system with electrostatic and magnetic interactions when all sources are reversed with it, fails for a particle in an external magnetic field, which is not reversed (Theorem §C8.3.7), and is violated slightly by the weak interaction ([[§B1.2 Lorentz Transformations and the Lorentz Group#^rem-b1-2-2|REL Remark: Parity and time reversal are questions for dynamics]]). Classically: if $\mathbf x(t)$ solves $m\ddot{\mathbf x} = -\nabla V$, so does $\mathbf x(-t)$ (4.104), and Maxwell's equations with the Lorentz force are invariant under $t \to -t$ with $\mathbf E \to \mathbf E$, $\mathbf B \to -\mathbf B$, $\rho \to \rho$, $\mathbf j \to -\mathbf j$, $\mathbf v \to -\mathbf v$ (4.105)–(4.106).*
> *Source: Sakurai §4.4, eqs. (4.104)–(4.106), §4.4.2, eqs. (4.125)–(4.128), (4.147)–(4.151), (4.156) · Griffiths Problem 6.36, eqs. (6.82)–(6.83), footnotes 44–45*

^pr-c8-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.3.3: Time Reversal Is Antiunitary
> 1. Time-reversal invariance in the sense of Principle §C8.3.2 means $-iH\Theta = \Theta iH$ on every ket (4.129).
> 2. $\Theta$ cannot be unitary: then $H\Theta = -\Theta H$ (4.130), every $\Theta|n\rangle$ would have energy $-E_n$ (4.131), and the free particle, with $\Theta^{-1}\mathbf p^2\Theta = \mathbf p^2$, would have $\Theta^{-1}H\Theta = -H$ (4.132), a spectrum from $-\infty$ to $0$. Nor could $[x_i, p_j] = i\hbar\delta_{ij}$ survive with $\mathbf x$ even and $\mathbf p$ odd (4.152)–(4.154).
> 3. With $\Theta$ antiunitary, invariance is
>
> $$
> \Theta H = H\Theta , \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \mathcal U(t)\,\Theta\,\mathcal U(t) = \Theta \quad\text{for all } t , \qquad (4.134)
> $$
>
> with $\mathcal U(t) = e^{-iHt/\hbar}$: evolve for $t$, reverse, evolve for $t$ again, and the state returns reversed (Griffiths (6.83)). The commutation relations $[x_i, p_j] = i\hbar\delta_{ij}$ and $[J_i, J_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$ are then compatible with the signs of Principle §C8.3.2, and $\Theta$ commutes with every rotation operator.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.4.2, eqs. (4.129)–(4.134), (4.152)–(4.156) · Griffiths Problem 6.36(b)*

^thm-c8-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Expanding (4.128) to first order in $\delta t$: $-\frac{iH}{\hbar}\Theta|\alpha\rangle\,\delta t = \Theta\frac{iH}{\hbar}|\alpha\rangle\,\delta t$ for all $|\alpha\rangle$.
>
> *2.* A linear $\Theta$ commutes with the number $i$, so (4.129) becomes $-H\Theta = \Theta H$, and $H\Theta|n\rangle = -\Theta H|n\rangle = -E_n\Theta|n\rangle$. For the free particle $H = \mathbf p^2/2m$ and $\Theta^{-1}\mathbf p^2\Theta = (-\mathbf p)^2 = \mathbf p^2$ contradict $\Theta^{-1}H\Theta = -H$ unless $H = 0$. And applying $\Theta$ to $[x_i, p_j]|\ \rangle = i\hbar\delta_{ij}|\ \rangle$ gives, with $\Theta\mathbf x\Theta^{-1} = \mathbf x$ and $\Theta\mathbf p\Theta^{-1} = -\mathbf p$, $-[x_i, p_j]\Theta|\ \rangle = \Theta\,i\hbar\delta_{ij}|\ \rangle$, which for linear $\Theta$ says $-i\hbar = i\hbar$.
>
> *3.* For antiunitary $\Theta$, $\Theta iH|\alpha\rangle = -i\Theta H|\alpha\rangle$ (4.133), and (4.129) becomes $\Theta H = H\Theta$. The commutator now reads $-[x_i, p_j]\Theta|\ \rangle = -i\hbar\delta_{ij}\Theta|\ \rangle$ (4.154), consistent; with $\mathbf J$ odd, $\Theta[J_i, J_j]\Theta^{-1} = [J_i, J_j]$ and $\Theta\,i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k\Theta^{-1} = -i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}(-J_k)$ agree likewise. For any power series, antilinearity gives $\Theta f(H)\Theta^{-1} = f^{\ast}(\Theta H\Theta^{-1})$ with conjugated coefficients, so if $\Theta H = H\Theta$ then $\Theta e^{-iHt/\hbar} = e^{+iHt/\hbar}\Theta$, i.e. $\mathcal U(t)\Theta\,\mathcal U(t) = \Theta$; conversely differentiating this at $t = 0$ gives $-iH\Theta - \Theta iH = 0$, i.e. $\Theta H = H\Theta$. Rotations: $\Theta\bigl(-\frac i\hbar\mathbf J\cdot\hat n\,\phi\bigr)\Theta^{-1} = +\frac i\hbar(-\mathbf J\cdot\hat n)\phi$, so $\Theta\mathscr D(R)\Theta^{-1} = \mathscr D(R)$ (Sakurai: "note the extra $i$!").

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.3.4: Even and Odd Observables
> A Hermitian observable $A$ is **even** or **odd** under time reversal if $\Theta A\Theta^{-1} = \pm A$ (4.144). Then
>
> $$
> \langle\beta|A|\alpha\rangle = \pm\langle\tilde\beta|A|\tilde\alpha\rangle^* , \qquad \langle\alpha|A|\alpha\rangle = \pm\langle\tilde\alpha|A|\tilde\alpha\rangle . \qquad (4.145),\ (4.146)
> $$
>
> $\mathbf x$, $H$ (if invariant), $\mathbf x^2$ and $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$ are even; $\mathbf p$, $\mathbf L$, $\mathbf S$, $\mathbf J$ are odd.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.4.2, eqs. (4.143)–(4.146)*

^thm-c8-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Theorem §C8.3.1, 3 with $X = A = A^\dagger$ gives $\langle\beta|A|\alpha\rangle = \langle\tilde\alpha|\Theta A\Theta^{-1}|\tilde\beta\rangle = \pm\langle\tilde\alpha|A|\tilde\beta\rangle = \pm\langle\tilde\beta|A|\tilde\alpha\rangle^*$ (4.143), (4.145). For $\beta = \alpha$ the expectation value is real. Products: $\Theta(AB)\Theta^{-1} = (\Theta A\Theta^{-1})(\Theta B\Theta^{-1})$, so $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$ is even as a product of two odd vectors.

## Spinless particles

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.3.5: Time Reversal for a Spinless Particle
> With the phase convention $\Theta|\mathbf x'\rangle = |\mathbf x'\rangle$:
> 1. $\Theta$ is complex conjugation in the position basis: $\psi(\mathbf x') \to \psi^*(\mathbf x')$ (4.157)–(4.159). If $\psi(\mathbf x, t)$ solves the Schrödinger equation with a real potential, so does $\psi^*(\mathbf x, -t)$ (4.107)–(4.108).
> 2. In momentum space, $\phi(\mathbf p') \to \phi^*(-\mathbf p')$, and $\Theta|\mathbf p'\rangle = |-\mathbf p'\rangle$ (4.164).
> 3. $\Theta|\ell, m\rangle = (-1)^m|\ell, -m\rangle$ (4.160)–(4.161): the probability current circulates the other way. $\Theta^2 = +1$ (4.174).
> 4. **Reality.** If $[\Theta, H] = 0$ and $|n\rangle$ is nondegenerate, then $\Theta|n\rangle = e^{i\delta}|n\rangle$ and the wave function $\langle\mathbf x'|n\rangle$ is a real function times a constant phase (4.162)–(4.163).
> 5. **Quenching.** In such a state $\langle\mathbf L\rangle = 0$; and if $\langle\mathbf x'|n\rangle = \sum_{\ell m}F_{\ell m}(r)\,Y^m_\ell(\theta, \phi)$ is chosen real, $F_{\ell, -m} = (-1)^mF^{\ast}_{\ell m}$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.4.3, Theorem 7, eqs. (4.157)–(4.164), §4.4.4, eq. (4.174); Problems 4.8, 4.9, 4.11 · Griffiths Problem 6.36(c)–(d)*

^thm-c8-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\Theta\mathbf x\Theta^{-1} = \mathbf x$ and real $\mathbf x'$ give $\mathbf x\,\Theta|\mathbf x'\rangle = \Theta\mathbf x|\mathbf x'\rangle = \mathbf x'\Theta|\mathbf x'\rangle$, so $\Theta|\mathbf x'\rangle$ is $|\mathbf x'\rangle$ up to a phase, chosen $1$. By antilinearity, $\Theta|\alpha\rangle = \int d^3x'\,\Theta|\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x'|\alpha\rangle^{\ast} = \int d^3x'\,|\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x'|\alpha\rangle^{\ast}$ (4.158): $\Theta = K$ in this basis. Conjugating $i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi = (-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V)\psi$ and replacing $t$ by $-t$ shows that $\psi^{\ast}(\mathbf x, -t)$ solves the same equation; for $\psi = u_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ it is $u_n^{\ast}e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ (4.108).
>
> *2.* $\Theta|\mathbf p'\rangle = \int d^3x'\,|\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf p'\rangle^{\ast}$, and $\langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf p'\rangle^{\ast} = (2\pi\hbar)^{-3/2}e^{-i\mathbf p'\cdot\mathbf x'/\hbar} = \langle\mathbf x'|-\mathbf p'\rangle$ ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]]). Then $\Theta|\alpha\rangle = \int d^3p'\,|-\mathbf p'\rangle\langle\mathbf p'|\alpha\rangle^{\ast} = \int d^3p'\,|\mathbf p'\rangle\langle-\mathbf p'|\alpha\rangle^{\ast}$.
>
> *3.* $\langle\hat{\mathbf n}|\ell, m\rangle = Y^m_\ell$ is conjugated, and $Y^{m\ast}_\ell = (-1)^mY^{-m}_\ell$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-7|Theorem §B5.2.7]]). $\Theta^2 = K^2 = 1$.
>
> *4.* $H\Theta|n\rangle = \Theta H|n\rangle = E_n\Theta|n\rangle$ (4.162), so by nondegeneracy $\Theta|n\rangle = c|n\rangle$ with $|c| = 1$ ($\Theta$ preserves norms). By 1 this says $\psi_n^{\ast} = c\,\psi_n$. Write $c = e^{2i\gamma}$; then $u = e^{i\gamma}\psi_n$ satisfies $u^{\ast} = e^{-i\gamma}\psi_n^{\ast} = e^{i\gamma}\psi_n = u$.
>
> *5.* $\mathbf L$ is odd, so Theorem §C8.3.4 and $|\tilde n\rangle = e^{i\delta}|n\rangle$ give $\langle n|\mathbf L|n\rangle = -\langle\tilde n|\mathbf L|\tilde n\rangle = -\langle n|\mathbf L|n\rangle$. For the coefficients, $\psi^{\ast} = \sum F^{\ast}_{\ell m}(-1)^mY^{-m}_\ell = \sum_{\ell m}(-1)^mF^{\ast}_{\ell,-m}Y^m_\ell$, and $\psi = \psi^{\ast}$ term by term gives $F_{\ell m} = (-1)^mF^{\ast}_{\ell,-m}$.

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from level B
> [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]] made stationary states real by conjugating the Schrödinger equation, and in one dimension every bound state is real up to a phase. Theorem §C8.3.5, 4 is the same statement in any dimension (rule 2: same result, new reason): its reason is time-reversal invariance, and it says when reality fails. A complex nondegenerate eigenfunction needs an $H$ that does not commute with $\Theta$, such as a charge in a magnetic field, where $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A + \mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p$ is odd ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^def-c4-3-1|Def. §C4.3.1]]); degenerate complex eigenfunctions are allowed, $\psi_{n\ell m}$ with $m \ne 0$ being degenerate with $\psi_{n\ell,-m}$ and $e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x/\hbar}$ with $e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x/\hbar}$ (Sakurai; Problem 4.8(b)). Quenching is the three-dimensional cousin of level B's $\langle p\rangle = 0$ in a real state: an atom whose orbital degeneracy is lifted by an asymmetric environment carries no orbital angular momentum on average (Sakurai Problem 4.11).

## Spin and Kramers degeneracy

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.3.6: Time Reversal with Spin
> 1. **Spin one-half.** In the $S_z$ basis, up to a phase $\eta$,
>
> $$
> \Theta = \eta\,e^{-i\pi S_y/\hbar}K = -i\eta\Bigl(\frac{2S_y}{\hbar}\Bigr)K , \qquad \Theta\bigl(c_+|+\rangle + c_-|-\rangle\bigr) = \eta\bigl(c_+^*|-\rangle - c_-^*|+\rangle\bigr) , \qquad (4.168),\ (4.171)
> $$
>
> it maps $|\hat{\mathbf n}; +\rangle$ to $\eta|\hat{\mathbf n}; -\rangle$ (4.166), and $\Theta^2 = -1$ whatever $\eta$ (4.172)–(4.173).
> 2. **Any system.** For particles with spins, in a basis of $\mathbf x$ and $S_z$ eigenkets, $\Theta = \eta\,e^{-i\pi S_y/\hbar}K$ with $S_y$ the total spin, and in a multiplet of total angular momentum $\Theta = \eta\,e^{-i\pi J_y/\hbar}K$ (4.176). In both,
>
> $$
> \Theta^2 = e^{-2\pi iJ_y/\hbar} = (-1)^{2j} : \qquad (4.175)
> $$
>
> $+1$ for integer $j$, $-1$ for half-integer $j$. A system of electrons has $\Theta^2 = -1$ exactly when the number of electrons is odd, whatever their orbital state and even if it is not a $\mathbf J^2$ eigenstate.
> 3. **Phases.** $\Theta|j, m\rangle = e^{i\delta}\,i^{2m}|j, -m\rangle$ with $\delta$ independent of $m$, and one may take $\Theta|j, m\rangle = i^{2m}|j, -m\rangle$ (4.182); for integer $j$ this is $(-1)^m$, as in Theorem §C8.3.5, 3 (4.180)–(4.181).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.4.4, eqs. (4.165)–(4.182); Problems 4.7(b), 4.10 · Griffiths Problem 6.37(a), eq. (6.84) (that is $\eta = 1$), footnote 47*

^thm-c8-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Why this operator.* In the $S_z$ basis (and in the $|j, m\rangle$ basis with Condon–Shortley phases) the matrices of $J_x$ and $J_z$ are real and that of $J_y$ is imaginary, so $K\mathbf JK = (J_x, -J_y, J_z)$. The rotation by $\pi$ about $y$ then changes the signs of the $x$ and $z$ components and keeps $y$ ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-5|Theorem §C5.1.5]]), so $\Theta\mathbf J\Theta^{-1} = -\mathbf J$ for $\Theta = e^{-i\pi J_y/\hbar}K$. In the position basis $K$ already keeps $\mathbf x$ and reverses $\mathbf p = -i\hbar\nabla$; the rotation acts on spin only. The phase $\eta$ is free, as for any symmetry operator (Theorem §C8.1.1). Sakurai reaches the same operator by demanding $\Theta|\hat{\mathbf n}; +\rangle = \eta|\hat{\mathbf n}; -\rangle$ for the states $|\hat{\mathbf n}; \pm\rangle$ obtained by rotations (4.165)–(4.167); Example §C8.3.1 checks it.
>
> *1.* For spin one-half $e^{-i\pi S_y/\hbar} = \cos\frac\pi2 - i\sigma_y\sin\frac\pi2 = -i\sigma_y$, so $|+\rangle \to |-\rangle$, $|-\rangle \to -|+\rangle$ (4.170), which gives (4.171). Applying it twice, $\Theta^2(c_+|+\rangle + c_-|-\rangle) = \eta\,\Theta(c_+^{\ast}|-\rangle - c_-^{\ast}|+\rangle) = \eta\eta^{\ast}(-c_+|+\rangle - c_-|-\rangle)$ (4.172).
>
> *2.* $-iJ_y$ is a real matrix in this basis, so $K$ commutes with $e^{-i\pi J_y/\hbar}$, and $\Theta^2 = \eta\eta^{\ast}\,e^{-i\pi J_y/\hbar}K\,e^{-i\pi J_y/\hbar}K = e^{-2\pi iJ_y/\hbar}$ (4.177). A rotation by $2\pi$ is $(-1)^{2j}$ on a multiplet ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-4|Theorem §C5.3.4]]) (4.178), and on a product of particles it is the product of the factors $-1$ from the half-integer spins.
>
> *3.* $\Theta J_z\Theta^{-1} = -J_z$ and $\Theta\mathbf J^2\Theta^{-1} = \mathbf J^2$, so $\Theta|j, m\rangle = \lambda_m|j, -m\rangle$. Since $\Theta J_x\Theta^{-1} = -J_x$ and $\Theta(iJ_y)\Theta^{-1} = -i(-J_y)$, $\Theta J_+\Theta^{-1} = -J_-$. Apply $\Theta$ to $J_+|j, m\rangle = \hbar c_m|j, m+1\rangle$, $c_m = \sqrt{(j - m)(j + m + 1)}$ real: $\hbar c_m\lambda_{m+1}|j, -m-1\rangle = -J_-\lambda_m|j, -m\rangle = -\hbar c_m\lambda_m|j, -m-1\rangle$. So $\lambda_{m+1} = -\lambda_m$, which is $\lambda_m = e^{i\delta}i^{2m}$ (Sakurai Problem 4.10). As a check, $\Theta^2|j, m\rangle = \Theta\bigl(e^{i\delta}i^{2m}|j, -m\rangle\bigr) = e^{-i\delta}i^{-2m}e^{i\delta}i^{-2m}|j, m\rangle = (-1)^{2m}|j, m\rangle$, in agreement with 2 since $2m \equiv 2j \pmod 2$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.3.7: Kramers Degeneracy
> Let $[\Theta, H] = 0$ and $\Theta^2 = -1$, as for any system with an odd number of electrons (Theorem §C8.3.6, 2). Then:
> 1. for every energy eigenket $|n\rangle$, $\Theta|n\rangle$ is an eigenket with the same energy and orthogonal to $|n\rangle$;
> 2. every (finite-dimensional) energy eigenspace has even dimension: each level is at least twofold degenerate.
>
> Electrostatic interactions $V = e\phi(\mathbf x)$ ($e$ the particle's charge, of either sign, as in Sakurai §4.4.5) are even under $\Theta$ (4.188)–(4.189), so no electric field, however complicated, lifts this degeneracy. Magnetic couplings $\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A + \mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p$ with an external field are odd, so $[\Theta, H] \ne 0$ (4.193)–(4.194), and a magnetic field can lift it. For $\Theta^2 = +1$ (integer $j$, an even number of electrons) there is no such degeneracy.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.4.5, eqs. (4.188)–(4.194) · Griffiths Problem 6.37(b) and its comment (Kramers' degeneracy); item 2's even dimension proved here*

^thm-c8-3-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $H\Theta|n\rangle = \Theta H|n\rangle = E_n\Theta|n\rangle$. For antiunitary $\Theta$, $\langle\Theta\beta|\Theta\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|\beta\rangle$; take $|\alpha\rangle = |n\rangle$, $|\beta\rangle = \Theta|n\rangle$: $\langle\Theta^2n|\Theta n\rangle = \langle n|\Theta n\rangle$, and with $\Theta^2 = -1$ the left side is $-\langle n|\Theta n\rangle$. So $\langle n|\Theta n\rangle = 0$. (Sakurai argues by contradiction: if $\Theta|n\rangle = e^{i\delta}|n\rangle$, then $\Theta^2|n\rangle = e^{-i\delta}e^{i\delta}|n\rangle = +|n\rangle$ (4.191)–(4.192).)
>
> *2.* $\Theta$ maps the eigenspace $V$ onto itself. Pick a unit $|1\rangle \in V$; by 1, $|1\rangle$ and $\Theta|1\rangle$ are orthonormal. Their orthogonal complement $W$ in $V$ is mapped into itself by $\Theta$: for $|w\rangle \in W$, $\langle1|\Theta w\rangle = \langle\Theta\Theta w|\Theta1\rangle = -\langle w|\Theta1\rangle = 0$ and $\langle\Theta1|\Theta w\rangle = \langle w|1\rangle = 0$. Repeat in $W$; the dimension drops by two at each step, so it is even.
>
> *Fields.* $\Theta\phi(\mathbf x)\Theta^{-1} = \phi(\mathbf x)$ since $\phi$ is a real function of the even $\mathbf x$; $\mathbf S$ and $\mathbf p$ are odd, while an *external* $\mathbf B$ or $\mathbf A$ is a fixed c-number function and is not reversed.

> [!caution] Caution: Kramers degeneracy and Kramers' relation
> Two results carry Kramers' name. Kramers degeneracy (this section) is a twofold degeneracy for an odd number of electrons; Kramers' relation ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-5|Theorem §B5.4.5]]) is a recursion among the radial moments $\langle r^s\rangle$ of hydrogen. They are unrelated. Historically Kramers found the degeneracy in explicit solutions, and Wigner identified its cause as time-reversal invariance (Sakurai).

^cau-c8-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: No time-reversal quantum number
> Unlike parity, $[\Theta, H] = 0$ gives no conservation law: $\Theta\,\mathcal U(t, t_0) = \mathcal U(t_0, t)\,\Theta \ne \mathcal U(t, t_0)\,\Theta$ (4.190), and an antiunitary operator has no eigenvalues in the usual sense, since $\Theta(c|\alpha\rangle) = c^*\Theta|\alpha\rangle$ lets any phase be absorbed ([[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^rem-c8-1-3|Remark: Discrete symmetries and conservation]]). Its consequences are restrictions instead: phases of wave functions (Theorem §C8.3.5), vanishing expectation values (Theorem §C8.3.8) and degeneracies (Theorem §C8.3.7). An external magnetic field breaks the symmetry because it is held fixed; reversing the currents that produce it as well restores it, which is the classical statement (4.105)–(4.106) applied to the whole world (Sakurai; Griffiths footnote 44; the Lorentz force is [[§B6.1 The Lorentz Force and Currents#^pr-b6-1-1|EM Principle §B6.1.1]], and a current changes sign under time reversal, [[§B8.1 Ohm's Law and Electromotive Force#^rem-b8-1-1|EM Remark: What level B adds to Ohm's law]]).

^rem-c8-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.3.8: Expectation Values of Tensor Operators
> Let $[\Theta, H] = 0$, let $|\alpha, j, m\rangle$ be a multiplet whose other labels $\alpha$ are unchanged by $\Theta$, $\Theta|\alpha, j, m\rangle = i^{2m}|\alpha, j, -m\rangle$, and let $T^{(k)}_q$ be a Hermitian spherical tensor that is even or odd, $\Theta T^{(k)}_0\Theta^{-1} = \pm T^{(k)}_0$ (4.184). Then
>
> $$
> \langle\alpha, j, m|T^{(k)}_0|\alpha, j, m\rangle = \pm(-1)^k\,\langle\alpha, j, m|T^{(k)}_0|\alpha, j, m\rangle , \qquad (4.187)
> $$
>
> so the expectation value vanishes for even tensors of odd rank and for odd tensors of even rank. For $k = 1$: $\langle\mathbf x\rangle = 0$ in such a state, even when it is not a parity eigenket, e.g. a mixture $c_s|s_{1/2}\rangle + c_p|p_{1/2}\rangle$ produced by a parity-violating but time-reversal-invariant interaction (Example §C8.2.2 in [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.4.4, eqs. (4.183)–(4.187)*

^thm-c8-3-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By Theorem §C8.3.4 with $|\tilde\alpha\rangle = i^{2m}|\alpha, j, -m\rangle$ (the phases cancel), $\langle\alpha, j, m|T^{(k)}_0|\alpha, j, m\rangle = \pm\langle\alpha, j, -m|T^{(k)}_0|\alpha, j, -m\rangle$ (4.183), (4.185). The rotation $\mathscr D = \mathscr D(0, \pi, 0)$ reverses $J_z$ and commutes with $H$ and $\mathbf J^2$, so $|\alpha, j, -m\rangle = \mathscr D|\alpha, j, m\rangle$ up to a phase. By the rotation law of a spherical tensor ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^def-c7-2-3|Def. §C7.2.3]]), $\mathscr D^\dagger T^{(k)}_0\mathscr D = \sum_{q'}\mathscr D^{(k)*}_{0q'}T^{(k)}_{q'}$; the terms $q' \ne 0$ change $m$ and drop out between $|\alpha, j, m\rangle$ states, and $\mathscr D^{(k)}_{00}(0, \pi, 0) = P_k(\cos\pi) = (-1)^k$ ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-4|Theorem §C5.4.4]]) (4.186). Hence $\langle T^{(k)}_0\rangle_{-m} = (-1)^k\langle T^{(k)}_0\rangle_m$, and combining the two relations gives (4.187).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C8.3.1: Reversing a Plane Wave and a Spin
> (a) Show that $\psi^*(\mathbf x, -t)$ is the plane wave with reversed momentum. (b) Show that $-i\sigma_y\chi^*(\hat{\mathbf n})$ is the eigenspinor of $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$ with eigenvalue $-1$, where $\chi(\hat{\mathbf n})$ has eigenvalue $+1$.
>
> *(a)* $\psi = e^{i(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x - Et)/\hbar}$ gives $\psi^{\ast}(\mathbf x, -t) = e^{-i(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x + Et)/\hbar} = e^{i((-\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf x - Et)/\hbar}$: momentum $-\mathbf p$, the same energy. It is complex although $H$ is invariant, because $|\mathbf p\rangle$ and $|-\mathbf p\rangle$ are degenerate (Theorem §C8.3.5, 4 does not apply).
>
> *(b)* For $\hat{\mathbf n}$ with polar angle $\beta$ and azimuth $\alpha$, $\chi(\hat{\mathbf n}) = \bigl(\cos\frac\beta2,\ e^{i\alpha}\sin\frac\beta2\bigr)^{\mathsf T}$ ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-2|Theorem §C5.2.2]], up to a phase). With $-i\sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$,
>
> $$
> -i\sigma_y\chi^* = \begin{pmatrix} -e^{-i\alpha}\sin\frac\beta2 \\ \cos\frac\beta2 \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> It is orthogonal to $\chi$: $\cos\frac\beta2\cdot(-e^{-i\alpha}\sin\frac\beta2) + e^{-i\alpha}\sin\frac\beta2\cos\frac\beta2 = 0$; in two dimensions the orthogonal complement of the $+1$ eigenspinor of the Hermitian $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$ is its $-1$ eigenspinor, so $-i\sigma_y\chi^*(\hat{\mathbf n}) = \chi(-\hat{\mathbf n})$ up to a phase: the spin points the other way. This is (4.169), Theorem §C8.3.6, 1 with $\eta = 1$ written on spinors, Griffiths' $\Theta(a, b)^{\mathsf T} = (-b^*, a^*)^{\mathsf T}$ (6.84). Applying it twice: $(a, b) \to (-b^*, a^*) \to (-a, -b)$, the $\Theta^2 = -1$ of a single spin.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problems 4.7, 4.8(b) · Griffiths Problems 6.36(d), 6.37(a); solved here*

> [!example] Example §C8.3.2: A Spin-1 Crystal-Field Hamiltonian, and Spin 3/2
> Sakurai Problem 4.12: solve $H = AS_z^2 + B(S_x^2 - S_y^2)$ for spin $1$ exactly. Is $H$ invariant under time reversal, and how do its eigenstates transform? Compare spin $\frac32$.
>
> *Spectrum.* $S_x^2 - S_y^2 = \frac12(S_+^2 + S_-^2)$ changes $m$ by $\pm2$, and $\langle1, -1|S_-^2|1, 1\rangle = (\sqrt2\hbar)^2 = 2\hbar^2$. So $|1, 0\rangle$ is an eigenket with $E = 0$, and on $|1, 1\rangle$, $|1, -1\rangle$ the matrix is $\hbar^2\begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ B & A \end{pmatrix}$:
>
> $$
> E = \hbar^2(A \pm B) , \qquad |\pm\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(|1, 1\rangle \pm |1, -1\rangle\bigr) ; \qquad E = 0 , \qquad |1, 0\rangle .
> $$
>
> *Time reversal.* $H$ is quadratic in the odd $\mathbf S$, hence even: $[\Theta, H] = 0$. With $\Theta|1, m\rangle = (-1)^m|1, -m\rangle$ (Theorem §C8.3.6, 3): $\Theta|1, 0\rangle = |1, 0\rangle$ and $\Theta|\pm\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(-|1, -1\rangle \mp |1, 1\rangle) = \mp|\pm\rangle$. Each eigenket goes into itself up to a phase, as it must for a nondegenerate level, and therefore $\langle\mathbf S\rangle = 0$ in all three (Theorem §C8.3.4): the spin is quenched, as the orbital angular momentum is in Theorem §C8.3.5, 5. Directly: $\langle S_z\rangle_\pm = \frac12(\hbar - \hbar) = 0$, and $S_x$, $S_y$ change $m$ by one, which connects $\pm1$ only to $0$. (A Hamiltonian of this form describes the zero-field splitting of a spin in a crystal, Sakurai.)
>
> *Spin $\frac32$.* Now $\Theta^2 = -1$. $H$ couples $m = \frac32$ with $-\frac12$ and $\frac12$ with $-\frac32$; on the first pair the matrix is $\hbar^2\begin{pmatrix} \frac94A & \sqrt3B \\ \sqrt3B & \frac14A \end{pmatrix}$ (from $\langle-\frac12|S_-^2|\frac32\rangle = \hbar^2\sqrt3\cdot2$), with eigenvalues $\hbar^2\bigl(\frac54A \pm \sqrt{A^2 + 3B^2}\bigr)$, and $\Theta$ maps this pair onto the second pair, which has the same matrix. So the four states form two **Kramers doublets**, whatever $A$ and $B$ (checked numerically, computed here). Only a magnetic field, a term odd in $\mathbf S$, can split them.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 4.12; solved here*

> [!example] Example §C8.3.3: Why E(k) = E(−k) in a Crystal without Inversion Symmetry
> A spinless particle moves in a periodic potential $V(x + a) = V(x)$ that is not even. Show that the Bloch energies satisfy $E_n(k) = E_n(-k)$.
>
> $H = p^2/2m + V(x)$ with real $V$ commutes with $\Theta = K$ (position basis, Theorem §C8.3.5, 1). If $\psi_{nk}$ is a Bloch eigenfunction, $\psi_{nk}(x + a) = e^{ika}\psi_{nk}(x)$ ([[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^thm-c8-2-8|Theorem §C8.2.8]]), then $\psi^*_{nk}$ is an eigenfunction with the same energy and $\psi^*_{nk}(x + a) = e^{-ika}\psi^*_{nk}(x)$: a Bloch function with wave number $-k$. Hence every $E_n(k)$ also occurs at $-k$. Parity would give the same pairing ($\pi$ maps $k$ to $-k$), but only for an even $V$; time reversal needs only a real potential (Griffiths ch. 6, footnote 48). With spin one-half and spin–orbit coupling the statement becomes $E_{n\uparrow}(k) = E_{n\downarrow}(-k)$, Kramers pairs at opposite wave vectors, one of the "interesting applications to electrons in crystals" Sakurai mentions.
>
> *Source: Griffiths ch. 6, footnote 48 · Sakurai §4.4.5; solved here*
