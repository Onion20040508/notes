Why does a symmetry give a conserved quantity, and why does it make energy levels degenerate? Classically, Noether's theorem trades each continuous symmetry for a constant of the motion ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-2|CM Theorem §B6.4.2]]), and the constant generates the symmetry through the Poisson bracket ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-5|CM Theorem §B8.1.5]]). Level B had the quantum conservation law, $[\hat H, \hat Q] = 0$ ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-8|Theorem §B2.4.8]]), and the fact that conserved observables which do not commute force degeneracy ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]); batch 1 met the generators one at a time: momentum for translations ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-4|Principle §C2.2.4]]), the Hamiltonian for time evolution ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]]), angular momentum for rotations ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|Principle §C5.1.2]]), with the $(2j+1)$-fold degeneracy of rotationally invariant Hamiltonians ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]). This section states the general mechanism for kets: symmetries act by unitary or antiunitary operators (Wigner), invariance of $H$ is the same as conservation of the generator, and a symmetry group acts on every energy level. It then proves Pauli's $SO(4)$ solution of hydrogen, which [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem]] stated.

*Notation.* Sakurai's: kets $|\alpha\rangle$, a symmetry operator $\mathcal S$ with Hermitian generator $G$, translations $\mathcal T(\mathbf l)$, rotations $\mathscr D(R)$, time evolution $\mathcal U(t, t_0)$, Heisenberg-picture operators $A^{(H)}(t)$. The Coulomb problem is in Gaussian units, $V = -Ze^2/r$ with $e > 0$ the elementary charge (as in [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem]]); for SI replace $e^2 \to e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ ([[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^cau-c6-2-1|Caution: Gaussian and SI units for the Coulomb problem]]). $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is the Levi-Civita symbol, repeated indices are summed, and $\hbar$ is explicit.

## Symmetry operations

> [!definition] Definition §C8.1.1: Symmetry Operation; Symmetry Operator; Invariant Hamiltonian
> A **symmetry operation** is a one-to-one map of the states of a system onto themselves, $|\alpha\rangle \to |\tilde\alpha\rangle$, that preserves every transition probability:
>
> $$
> |\langle\tilde\beta|\tilde\alpha\rangle| = |\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle| . \qquad (4.113)
> $$
>
> The operator $\mathcal S$ with $|\tilde\alpha\rangle = \mathcal S|\alpha\rangle$ is the **symmetry operator** of the operation; it is so called whether or not the system has the symmetry. Translations $\mathcal T(\mathbf l)$, rotations $\mathscr D(R)$ and parity $\pi$ are unitary; time reversal $\Theta$ is antiunitary ([[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^def-c8-3-1|Def. §C8.3.1]]). If the operations form a continuous family through the identity, the operator close to the identity is
>
> $$
> \mathcal S = 1 - \frac{i\varepsilon}{\hbar}\,G , \qquad (4.7)
> $$
>
> with $G$ Hermitian, the **generator**. The Hamiltonian is **invariant** under $\mathcal S$, and the system has the symmetry, if
>
> $$
> \mathcal S^\dagger H\,\mathcal S = H , \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad [H, \mathcal S] = 0 \quad (\mathcal S \text{ unitary}) . \qquad (4.8),\ (4.13)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.1.2, eqs. (4.7)–(4.8), §4.1.3, eq. (4.13), §4.4.1, eqs. (4.110)–(4.113) · Griffiths §6.1, §6.2.2, eqs. (6.9)–(6.10)*

^def-c8-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.1.1: Wigner's Theorem
> Let $T$ be a symmetry operation in the sense of Def. §C8.1.1, a one-to-one map of rays preserving $|\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle|$. Then there is an operator $\mathcal S$, either unitary or antiunitary, such that $T$ sends the ray of $|\alpha\rangle$ to the ray of $\mathcal S|\alpha\rangle$ for every $|\alpha\rangle$; $\mathcal S$ is unique up to a phase factor. Moreover:
> 1. both kinds of operator preserve every transition probability;
> 2. every member of a one-parameter group of symmetry operations, $T(\lambda)T(\mu) = T(\lambda + \mu)$, is implemented by a unitary operator;
> 3. operators implementing a group of operations compose only up to a phase, $\mathcal S(g_1)\,\mathcal S(g_2) = e^{i\gamma(g_1, g_2)}\,\mathcal S(g_1g_2)$.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part IV, §11, Theorem (Wigner) [stated there] · Sakurai §4.4.1, after eq. (4.124) ("The proof of this assertion is actually very difficult", with a reference to Gottfried and Yan §7.1)*

^thm-c8-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The main statement is not proved in the sources* (Wigner 1931; neither Sakurai nor the series gives the proof). Items 1–3 are proved here.
>
> *1.* A unitary $\mathcal S$ gives $\langle\mathcal S\beta|\mathcal S\alpha\rangle = \langle\beta|\alpha\rangle$ (4.112); an antiunitary one gives $\langle\tilde\beta|\tilde\alpha\rangle = \langle\beta|\alpha\rangle^{\ast}$ (4.116a). Either way the modulus is unchanged.
>
> *2.* $T(\lambda) = T(\lambda/2)\,T(\lambda/2)$, so $T(\lambda)$ is implemented by the square $\mathcal S^2$ of whatever implements $T(\lambda/2)$. The square of a unitary operator is unitary. The square of an antiunitary operator is linear, $\mathcal S^2(c|\alpha\rangle) = \mathcal S(c^{\ast}\mathcal S|\alpha\rangle) = c\,\mathcal S^2|\alpha\rangle$, and preserves inner products, $\langle\mathcal S^2\beta|\mathcal S^2\alpha\rangle = \langle\mathcal S\beta|\mathcal S\alpha\rangle^{\ast} = \langle\beta|\alpha\rangle$; so it is unitary too. (This square-root argument is added here.)
>
> *3.* A ket and its multiples by phases are one physical state, so $\mathcal S(g_1)\mathcal S(g_2)$ and $\mathcal S(g_1g_2)$ implement the same map of rays, and by the uniqueness clause they differ by a phase.

^der-c8-1-1

*Uses:* [[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^def-c8-1-1|Def. §C8.1.1]], [[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^def-c8-3-1|Def. §C8.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What Wigner's theorem settles
> It is the reason behind the first item of every batch-1 principle: unitarity of translations ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-1|Principle §C2.2.1]]), of time evolution ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^pr-c3-1-1|Principle §C3.1.1]]) and of rotations ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|Principle §C5.1.2]]); each is a continuous group, so item 2 applies, and item 3 is where the sign of spin one-half enters ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^rem-c5-1-3|★ Remark: Why unitary, and why only up to a phase]]). Discrete operations are not covered by item 2: parity turns out unitary ([[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations]]), while reversal of motion must be antiunitary ([[§C8.3★ Time Reversal]]), the option that remark left open.

## Invariance and conservation

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.1.2: Symmetries and Conserved Generators
> Let $H$ be independent of time and $\mathcal S(\lambda) = e^{-i\lambda G/\hbar}$ a one-parameter group of unitary operators with a time-independent Hermitian generator $G$. The following are equivalent:
> 1. $H$ is invariant: $\mathcal S(\lambda)^\dagger H\,\mathcal S(\lambda) = H$ for all $\lambda$;
> 2. $[G, H] = 0$;
> 3. $G$ is a constant of the motion: $dG^{(H)}/dt = 0$, that is $G^{(H)}(t) = G$ for all $t$. (4.9)–(4.10)
>
> Then also $[G, \mathcal U(t, t_0)] = 0$: a ket that is an eigenket of $G$ at $t_0$ remains one, with the same eigenvalue $g'$ (4.11)–(4.12), and the whole probability distribution of $G$ is constant in time.
>
> More generally, let the generator depend explicitly on time, $G = G(t)$. The transformations $1 - i\varepsilon G(t)/\hbar$ map every solution of the Schrödinger equation to a solution if and only if
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial G}{\partial t} + \frac{i}{\hbar}\,[H, G] = 0 , \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \frac{dG^{(H)}}{dt} = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.1.2, eqs. (4.7)–(4.12) · Griffiths §6.2.2, eq. (6.13), §6.3, §6.8.2 · the user's series, Part IV, §5.2 (Remark: the quantum Noether dictionary) and §7.1 ("the quantum Noether loop closes"); the time-dependent case generalizes Griffiths Problem 6.35 and is derived here*

^thm-c8-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1 ⇒ 2.* Expand in $\lambda$: $\mathcal S^\dagger H\mathcal S = H + \frac{i\lambda}{\hbar}[G, H] + O(\lambda^2)$; invariance for all $\lambda$ kills the first-order term.
>
> *2 ⇒ 1.* $H$ commutes with every power of $G$, hence with the power series $\mathcal S(\lambda)$, and $\mathcal S^\dagger H\mathcal S = \mathcal S^\dagger\mathcal S H = H$.
>
> *2 ⇔ 3.* By the Heisenberg equation ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]), $dG^{(H)}/dt = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\,\mathcal U^\dagger[G, H]\,\mathcal U$, which vanishes exactly when $[G, H] = 0$ ($\mathcal U$ is invertible).
>
> *Eigenkets.* $\mathcal U(t, t_0) = e^{-iH(t - t_0)/\hbar}$ ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]]) is a power series in $H$, so it commutes with $G$, and $G\,\mathcal U|g'\rangle = \mathcal U\,G|g'\rangle = g'\,\mathcal U|g'\rangle$. The constancy of the distribution is level B's [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-8|Theorem §B2.4.8]], whose proof uses only $[H, G] = 0$.
>
> *Time-dependent generator.* Let $i\hbar\,\partial_t|\psi\rangle = H|\psi\rangle$ and $|\psi'\rangle = \mathcal S(t)|\psi\rangle$ with $\mathcal S$ unitary. Then
>
> $$
> i\hbar\,\partial_t|\psi'\rangle = \bigl[\mathcal S H\mathcal S^\dagger + i\hbar\,(\partial_t\mathcal S)\,\mathcal S^\dagger\bigr]|\psi'\rangle ,
> $$
>
> and $|\psi'\rangle$ solves the same equation for every $|\psi\rangle$ exactly when the bracket equals $H$. With $\mathcal S = 1 - i\varepsilon G(t)/\hbar$, to first order in $\varepsilon$: $\mathcal S H\mathcal S^\dagger = H - \frac{i\varepsilon}{\hbar}[G, H]$ and $i\hbar(\partial_t\mathcal S)\mathcal S^\dagger = \varepsilon\,\partial_tG$. The condition is $\partial_tG - \frac{i}{\hbar}[G, H] = 0$, and the left side is $\mathcal U\,\frac{dG^{(H)}}{dt}\,\mathcal U^\dagger$ by Theorem §C3.3.4 (with its explicit-time term). For time-independent $G$ it reduces to item 2.

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from level B
> Level B proved that an observable commuting with $\hat H$ is conserved ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-8|Theorem §B2.4.8]]) and left the connection with symmetry to classical mechanics ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^rem-b2-4-5|Remark: Conservation laws, classical and quantum]], which promised this section). Theorem §C8.1.2 closes the loop: invariance of $H$ under a group of transformations *is* conservation of the group's generator, in both directions. It is the quantum form of Noether's theorem ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-2|CM Theorem §B6.4.2]]) in its Hamiltonian reading ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^rem-b6-4-6|CM Remark: Noether in Hamiltonian form]]), and it is the single statement behind the three rows of the user's series' dictionary ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^rem-c2-2-3|Remark: Two roads to one commutator]]):
> - **translations:** $H$ commutes with $\mathcal T(\mathbf l)$ for all $\mathbf l$ iff $[\mathbf p, H] = 0$; for interacting particles with $V$ depending only on the separations, the generator is the total momentum, which is conserved however strong the interaction (Griffiths Problem 6.7);
> - **time translations:** an experiment done today gives the same result as one done yesterday, $\mathcal U(t_0 + \delta, t_0)$ independent of $t_0$, iff $\partial H/\partial t = 0$, and then $H$, the generator, is conserved (Griffiths §6.8.2);
> - **rotations:** $[\mathbf J, H] = 0$ and angular momentum is conserved ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]).
>
> In the classical list a boost belongs too ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-5|CM Theorem §B6.4.5]]); its quantum generator depends on time, the case of the last part of the theorem (Example §C8.1.2).

^rem-c8-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Discrete symmetries and conservation
> A discrete symmetry has no generator, but the argument still runs on the operator itself: a unitary $\mathcal S$ with $[\mathcal S, H] = 0$ commutes with $\mathcal U(t, t_0)$, so an eigenket of $\mathcal S$ stays an eigenket with the same eigenvalue. If $\mathcal S$ is also Hermitian, like parity with $\pi^2 = 1$, it is an observable with values $\pm1$: a conserved **multiplicative quantum number** ([[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations]]). If it is unitary but not Hermitian, like a lattice translation $\tau(a)$, its eigenvalue $e^{-i\theta}$ is conserved: the crystal momentum, defined only modulo $2\pi/a$ (Griffiths §6.2.2, footnote 10). An antiunitary symmetry gives no conservation law at all: $\Theta\,\mathcal U(t, t_0) = \mathcal U(t_0, t)\,\Theta \ne \mathcal U(t, t_0)\,\Theta$ (Sakurai (4.190); [[§C8.3★ Time Reversal]]).

## Symmetry and degeneracy

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.1.3: A Symmetry Group Acts on Every Energy Level
> Let $\mathcal G$ be a group of unitary operators, each commuting with $H$, and let $V_E$ be a (finite-dimensional) eigenspace of $H$.
> 1. Every $\mathcal S \in \mathcal G$ maps $V_E$ into itself: if $H|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$, then
>
> $$
> H\,(\mathcal S|n\rangle) = \mathcal S H|n\rangle = E_n\,(\mathcal S|n\rangle) . \qquad (4.14)
> $$
>
> If $\mathcal S(\lambda)$ depends on a continuous parameter, all the kets $\mathcal S(\lambda)|n\rangle$ have the energy $E_n$.
> 2. If the operators of $\mathcal G$ commute with one another, $V_E$ has an orthonormal basis of common eigenkets of $H$ and of every $\mathcal S \in \mathcal G$, on which each $\mathcal S$ acts by a phase. A commuting symmetry group by itself forces no degeneracy.
> 3. If two operators of $\mathcal G$ do not commute on $V_E$, then $\dim V_E \ge 2$. In general $V_E$ is an orthogonal sum of subspaces that $\mathcal G$ maps into themselves and that contain no smaller such subspace; the degeneracy forced by the symmetry is the dimension of these pieces: $2j + 1$ for rotations ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]), $(2k + 1)^2 = n^2$ for the Coulomb problem (Theorem §C8.1.5).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.1.3, eqs. (4.13)–(4.18) · Griffiths §6.6, eq. (6.39), Example 6.3 · Likharev Problem 4.25*

^thm-c8-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1* is (4.14).
>
> *2.* By 1 the restrictions of the $\mathcal S \in \mathcal G$ to $V_E$ are commuting unitary operators on a finite-dimensional space. Induct on $\dim V_E$. If every $\mathcal S$ is a multiple of the identity on $V_E$, any orthonormal basis will do. Otherwise some $\mathcal S_0$ has at least two distinct eigenvalues; being unitary, hence normal, it has an orthonormal eigenbasis ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]) with eigenvalues of modulus $1$ ([[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-54|LADR Thm. 7.54]]), and its eigenspaces are smaller, mutually orthogonal, and mapped into themselves by every $\mathcal S$ commuting with $\mathcal S_0$ ([[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-75|LADR Thm. 5.75]]). Apply the induction hypothesis to each.
>
> *3.* If $\dim V_E = 1$, each $\mathcal S$ acts on $V_E$ as a number and any two commute there. This is the argument of [[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]], with unitary symmetry operators in place of observables. For the decomposition: if $W \subset V_E$ is mapped into itself by all of $\mathcal G$, so is its orthogonal complement in $V_E$, since $\langle w|\mathcal S v\rangle = \langle\mathcal S^{-1}w|v\rangle = 0$ for $w \in W$, $v \perp W$, and $\mathcal S^{-1} = \mathcal S^\dagger \in \mathcal G$ maps $W$ into $W$. Splitting off invariant subspaces until none splits further ends in finitely many steps.

^der-c8-1-3

*Uses:* [[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]], [[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-54|LADR Thm. 7.54]], [[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-75|LADR Thm. 5.75]], [[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: The rotation case, and breaking a symmetry
> Sakurai's application, a rotationally invariant $H$ whose levels are $(2j+1)$-fold degenerate because $\mathscr D(R)|n; j, m\rangle = \sum_{m'}|n; j, m'\rangle\mathscr D^{(j)}_{m'm}(R)$ (4.15)–(4.18), is [[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]] with the same argument, and Griffiths' ladder version (Example 6.3: $L_\pm$ commutes with $H$ and steps through the $m$'s) is part 2 of that theorem; they are not repeated here. An atomic electron in $V(r) + V_{LS}(r)\,\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$ keeps the $(2j+1)$-fold degeneracy, since both terms are rotation scalars. A uniform external field along $z$ reduces the symmetry group to the rotations about $z$, which commute with each other, so by part 2 nothing forces different $m$ to share an energy, and they split: the Zeeman effect ([[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting]]) and the Stark effect ([[§C9.2 Atoms in External Fields, Fine and Hyperfine Structure]]). Part 2 assumes unitary operators: an antiunitary symmetry alone can force degeneracy (Kramers, [[§C8.3★ Time Reversal]]).

## The hidden symmetry of the Coulomb problem

> [!definition] Definition §C8.1.2: Runge–Lenz Operator
> For the hydrogen-like atom ([[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^mod-c6-2-1|Model §C6.2.1]])
>
> $$
> H = \frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m} - \frac{Ze^2}{r} , \qquad (4.21)
> $$
>
> the **Runge–Lenz operator** is the Hermitian vector operator
>
> $$
> \mathbf M = \frac{1}{2m}\bigl(\mathbf p\times\mathbf L - \mathbf L\times\mathbf p\bigr) - \frac{Ze^2}{r}\,\mathbf x = \frac1m\bigl(\mathbf p\times\mathbf L - i\hbar\,\mathbf p\bigr) - Ze^2\,\hat{\mathbf x} , \qquad \hat{\mathbf x} = \frac{\mathbf x}{r} , \qquad (4.20)
> $$
>
> the symmetrized form of the classical Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector $\mathbf p\times\mathbf L/m - Ze^2\,\mathbf x/r$ (4.19).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.1.4, eqs. (4.19)–(4.21) · Griffiths Problem 6.34*

^def-c8-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Normalizations of the Runge–Lenz vector
> The classical vector of [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-5|CM Theorem §B2.2.5]], $\vec A = \vec v\times\vec L - \alpha\hat r$ with $\alpha = Ze^2$ and $\vec v = \vec p/m$, is Sakurai's (4.19) exactly. Griffiths writes $\hat{\mathbf M} = (\hat{\mathbf p}\times\hat{\mathbf L} - \hat{\mathbf L}\times\hat{\mathbf p})/2m + V(r)\,\mathbf r$ with $V = -e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$, the same operator in SI units for $Z = 1$. Other books divide by $m$ or by $Ze^2$, or, on a bound level, multiply by $\sqrt{-m/2E}$ (equivalently, multiply $m\mathbf M$ by $1/\sqrt{-2mE}$); only the last, Sakurai's $\mathbf N$ (Theorem §C8.1.5), has the dimension of angular momentum. The two forms in (4.20) agree because $\mathbf L\times\mathbf p = -\mathbf p\times\mathbf L + 2i\hbar\,\mathbf p$ (derivation of Theorem §C8.1.4, step 0).

^cau-c8-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.1.4: Algebra of the Runge–Lenz Operator
> 1. $\mathbf M$ is conserved: $[\mathbf M, H] = 0$. (4.22)
> 2. $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf M = \mathbf M\cdot\mathbf L = 0$. (4.23)
> 3. $\mathbf M^2 = \dfrac{2}{m}\,H\,(\mathbf L^2 + \hbar^2) + Z^2e^4$. (4.24)
> 4. $\mathbf M$ is a vector operator: $[M_i, L_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}M_k$. (4.26)
> 5. $[M_i, M_j] = -i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,\dfrac{2}{m}\,H\,L_k$. (4.27)
>
> Together with $[L_i, L_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}L_k$ (4.25) these are all the commutators among $H$, $\mathbf L$, $\mathbf M$. Classically, 3 is $|\vec A|^2 = \alpha^2 + 2EL^2/m$ of CM Theorem §B2.2.5; the $\hbar^2$ is the quantum correction.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.1.4, eqs. (4.22)–(4.27) (stated: "It can be shown", with Problem 3.31) · Griffiths Problem 6.34, relations (i)–(vii); derived here (and checked symbolically on test functions)*

^thm-c8-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *0. Tools.* $\mathbf x$, $\mathbf p$, $\mathbf L$, $\hat{\mathbf x}$ and $\mathbf x/r^3$ are vector operators under $\mathbf L$ ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]); $r$, $1/r$ and $\mathbf p^2$ commute with $\mathbf L$. $\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf L = \mathbf L\cdot\mathbf x = \mathbf p\cdot\mathbf L = \mathbf L\cdot\mathbf p = 0$ (each is $\epsilon_{ijk}$ contracted with a product symmetric in two indices, after one commutator $[x_i, p_i]$ that $\epsilon_{iik} = 0$ removes). $[p_j, f(\mathbf x)] = -i\hbar\,\partial_jf$ ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]] in three dimensions); with $G_{ij} \equiv \partial_i\hat x_j = \delta_{ij}/r - x_ix_j/r^3$ (symmetric) one has $\partial_j\hat x_j = 2/r$ and $\partial_jG_{ij} = \nabla^2\hat x_i = -2x_i/r^3$. Two contractions are used throughout: $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{klm} = \delta_{il}\delta_{jm} - \delta_{im}\delta_{jl}$ and $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{kil} = 2\delta_{jl}$. For any vector operator $\mathbf W$, $[L_j, W_k] = i\hbar\epsilon_{jkl}W_l$ gives
>
> $$
> (\mathbf L\times\mathbf W)_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\bigl(W_kL_j + i\hbar\,\epsilon_{jkl}W_l\bigr) = -(\mathbf W\times\mathbf L)_i + 2i\hbar\,W_i ;
> $$
>
> with $\mathbf W = \mathbf p$ this gives the second form of (4.20). Write $\mathbf B = \mathbf p\times\mathbf L - i\hbar\mathbf p$, so $\mathbf M = \mathbf B/m - Ze^2\hat{\mathbf x}$. Hermiticity: for Hermitian components $\bigl((\mathbf A\times\mathbf C)_i\bigr)^\dagger = \epsilon_{ijk}C_kA_j = -(\mathbf C\times\mathbf A)_i$, so the first form of (4.20) is Hermitian.
>
> *1.* $H$ commutes with $\mathbf L$ ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]) and $[H, p_j] = [-Ze^2/r, p_j] = i\hbar Ze^2x_j/r^3$. Hence $[H, \mathbf p\times\mathbf L] = i\hbar Ze^2\,(\mathbf x/r^3)\times\mathbf L$, and $\bigl((\mathbf x/r^3)\times\mathbf L\bigr)_i = \bigl(x_i(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf p) - r^2p_i\bigr)/r^3 = -G_{ij}p_j$. So
>
> $$
> \frac1m[H, B_i] = \frac{i\hbar Ze^2}{m}\Bigl(-G_{ij}p_j - i\hbar\frac{x_i}{r^3}\Bigr) , \qquad Ze^2[H, \hat x_i] = \frac{Ze^2}{2m}\bigl(p_j[p_j, \hat x_i] + [p_j, \hat x_i]p_j\bigr) = -\frac{i\hbar Ze^2}{2m}\bigl(p_jG_{ij} + G_{ij}p_j\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Subtracting, $[H, M_i] = \frac{i\hbar Ze^2}{2m}\bigl([p_j, G_{ij}] - 2i\hbar x_i/r^3\bigr) = \frac{i\hbar Ze^2}{2m}\bigl(-i\hbar\,\partial_jG_{ij} - 2i\hbar x_i/r^3\bigr) = 0$.
>
> *2.* $(\mathbf p\times\mathbf L)\cdot\mathbf L = p_j\,(\mathbf L\times\mathbf L)_j = i\hbar\,\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf L = 0$, using $\mathbf L\times\mathbf L = i\hbar\mathbf L$ (the commutation relations, [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-4|Theorem §C5.1.4]]). With $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf L = 0$ and $\hat{\mathbf x}\cdot\mathbf L = r^{-1}\,\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf L = 0$, $\mathbf M\cdot\mathbf L = 0$; then $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf M = (\mathbf M\cdot\mathbf L)^\dagger = 0$.
>
> *3.* Three products: $(\mathbf p\times\mathbf L)^2 = p_jL_kp_jL_k - p_jL_k\,p_kL_j = \mathbf p^2\mathbf L^2$ (move $L_k$ past $p_j$; the commutator term is $\epsilon$ against $p_jp_l$, and $L_kp_k = 0$); $(\mathbf p\times\mathbf L)\cdot\mathbf p = 2i\hbar\,\mathbf p^2$; $\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf p\times\mathbf L) = 0$. Hence $\mathbf B^2 = \mathbf p^2(\mathbf L^2 + \hbar^2)$. Next $\hat{\mathbf x}\cdot(\mathbf p\times\mathbf L) = \mathbf L^2/r$ and $(\mathbf p\times\mathbf L)\cdot\hat{\mathbf x} = \mathbf L^2/r + 2i\hbar\,\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf x}$, so
>
> $$
> \mathbf B\cdot\hat{\mathbf x} + \hat{\mathbf x}\cdot\mathbf B = \frac{2\mathbf L^2}{r} + i\hbar\,[p_j, \hat x_j] = \frac{2(\mathbf L^2 + \hbar^2)}{r} .
> $$
>
> With $\hat{\mathbf x}^2 = 1$, $\mathbf M^2 = \frac{\mathbf p^2}{m^2}(\mathbf L^2 + \hbar^2) - \frac{2Ze^2}{mr}(\mathbf L^2 + \hbar^2) + Z^2e^4 = \frac2m H(\mathbf L^2 + \hbar^2) + Z^2e^4$, since $\mathbf L^2$ commutes with $\mathbf p^2$ and $1/r$.
>
> *4.* Under a rotation $\mathscr D^\dagger\mathbf V\mathscr D = R\mathbf V$ for $\mathbf V = \mathbf x, \mathbf p, \mathbf L$, and $R(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b) = R\mathbf a\times R\mathbf b$ for $R \in SO(3)$, so $\mathbf p\times\mathbf L$ rotates as a vector; so do $\mathbf p$ and $\hat{\mathbf x} = \mathbf x\cdot r^{-1}$ with $r$ invariant. The commutator form is [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-1|Theorem §C7.2.1]].
>
> *5.* Since $[M_i, M_j] = \epsilon_{ijk}(\mathbf M\times\mathbf M)_k$ (the $\epsilon$-contraction above), it suffices to show $\mathbf M\times\mathbf M = -i\hbar\frac2mH\mathbf L$. Write $\mathbf W = \mathbf p\times\mathbf L$. Then $\mathbf p\times\mathbf W = \mathbf p(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf L) - \mathbf p^2\mathbf L = -\mathbf p^2\mathbf L$ and $\mathbf W\times\mathbf p = \mathbf p^2\mathbf L$, so the cross terms of $\mathbf B\times\mathbf B$ cancel; and $(\mathbf W\times\mathbf W)_i = W_jp_iL_j - W_jp_jL_i = i\hbar\mathbf p^2L_i - 2i\hbar\mathbf p^2L_i$, using $[W_j, p_i] = i\hbar(\delta_{ij}\mathbf p^2 - p_ip_j)$ and $\mathbf W\cdot\mathbf L = 0$. Thus $\mathbf B\times\mathbf B = -i\hbar\,\mathbf p^2\mathbf L$. For the mixed terms, $\hat{\mathbf x}\times\mathbf W = i\hbar\mathbf L/r - (\hat{\mathbf x}\cdot\mathbf p)\mathbf L$, $\mathbf W\times\hat{\mathbf x} = (\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf x})\mathbf L + i\hbar\,\mathbf p\times\hat{\mathbf x}$ and $\hat{\mathbf x}\times\mathbf p = \mathbf L/r$, so
>
> $$
> \mathbf B\times\hat{\mathbf x} + \hat{\mathbf x}\times\mathbf B = [p_j, \hat x_j]\,\mathbf L + \frac{i\hbar\mathbf L}{r} - \frac{i\hbar\mathbf L}{r} = -\frac{2i\hbar\,\mathbf L}{r} .
> $$
>
> Since $\hat{\mathbf x}\times\hat{\mathbf x} = 0$, $\mathbf M\times\mathbf M = -\frac{i\hbar}{m^2}\mathbf p^2\mathbf L + \frac{2i\hbar Ze^2}{mr}\mathbf L = -\frac{2i\hbar}{m}\bigl(\frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m} - \frac{Ze^2}{r}\bigr)\mathbf L$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C8.1.5: SO(4) Symmetry and the Coulomb Spectrum (Pauli)
> On an eigenspace $V_E$ of bound states, $E < 0$, put
>
> $$
> \mathbf N = \Bigl(-\frac{m}{2E}\Bigr)^{1/2}\mathbf M , \qquad \mathbf I = \tfrac12(\mathbf L + \mathbf N) , \qquad \mathbf K = \tfrac12(\mathbf L - \mathbf N) . \qquad (4.28),\ (4.32),\ (4.33)
> $$
>
> 1. On $V_E$ the six Hermitian operators $\mathbf L$, $\mathbf N$ close under commutation, (4.29):
>
> $$
> [L_i, L_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}L_k , \qquad [N_i, L_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}N_k , \qquad [N_i, N_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}L_k ,
> $$
>
> the algebra of rotations in four dimensions, $\mathfrak{so}(4)$.
> 2. $\mathbf I$ and $\mathbf K$ are two commuting angular momenta, conserved, with $\mathbf I^2 = \mathbf K^2$ on $V_E$ (4.34):
>
> $$
> [I_i, I_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}I_k , \qquad [K_i, K_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}K_k , \qquad [I_i, K_j] = 0 .
> $$
>
> 3. If $\mathbf I^2 = \mathbf K^2 = k(k+1)\hbar^2$ with $k \in \{0, \frac12, 1, \frac32, \dots\}$, then
>
> $$
> E = -\frac{mZ^2e^4}{2\hbar^2}\,\frac{1}{(2k+1)^2} , \qquad (4.37)
> $$
>
> the Bohr formula with $n = 2k + 1 = 1, 2, 3, \dots$
> 4. An irreducible multiplet of the pair $(\mathbf I, \mathbf K)$ has dimension $(2k+1)^2 = n^2$ and contains each orbital angular momentum $\ell = 0, 1, \dots, n - 1$ exactly once. This is the whole hydrogen level ([[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]]): its degeneracy $n^2$ is forced by the symmetry.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §4.1.4, eqs. (4.28)–(4.37) (after Schiff; first done by Pauli) · Griffiths Problem 6.34, footnote 43 · the user's series, Part IV, §14 (Remark: The degeneracy is not an accident) · the user's hydrogen derivation, §7 ("a hidden SO(4) symmetry")*

^thm-c8-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\mathbf L$ and $\mathbf M$ commute with $H$ (Theorem §C8.1.4, 1), so they map $V_E$ into itself, and on $V_E$ one may replace $H$ by the number $E$. Since $-m/2E > 0$, $\mathbf N$ is Hermitian. The first relation is Theorem §C5.1.4, the second is Theorem §C8.1.4, 4 times a constant, and Theorem §C8.1.4, 5 gives $[N_i, N_j] = \bigl(-\frac{m}{2E}\bigr)\bigl(-i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}\frac2mE\,L_k\bigr) = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}L_k$.
>
> *2.* Using $[L_i, N_j] = -[N_j, L_i] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}N_k$,
>
> $$
> [I_i, K_j] = \tfrac14\bigl([L_i, L_j] - [L_i, N_j] + [N_i, L_j] - [N_i, N_j]\bigr) = \tfrac{i\hbar}4\epsilon_{ijk}\bigl(L_k - N_k + N_k - L_k\bigr) = 0 ,
> $$
>
> and in the same way $[I_i, I_j] = \frac{i\hbar}{4}\epsilon_{ijk}(2L_k + 2N_k) = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}I_k$, likewise for $\mathbf K$. Both commute with $H$. Finally $\mathbf I^2 - \mathbf K^2 = \frac12(\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf N + \mathbf N\cdot\mathbf L) = 0$ by Theorem §C8.1.4, 2.
>
> *3.* By 2 and the general spectrum of an angular momentum ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]]), $\mathbf I^2$ and $\mathbf K^2$ have the eigenvalues $i(i+1)\hbar^2$, $k(k+1)\hbar^2$ with $i, k \in \{0, \frac12, 1, \dots\}$, and $i = k$. On $V_E$, Theorem §C8.1.4, 3 gives $\mathbf M^2 = \frac2mE(\mathbf L^2 + \hbar^2) + Z^2e^4$, so
>
> $$
> \mathbf I^2 + \mathbf K^2 = \tfrac12(\mathbf L^2 + \mathbf N^2) = \tfrac12\Bigl(\mathbf L^2 - \frac{m}{2E}\mathbf M^2\Bigr) = \tfrac12\Bigl(-\hbar^2 - \frac{m}{2E}Z^2e^4\Bigr) . \qquad (4.35),\ (4.36)
> $$
>
> On a common eigenket the left side is $2k(k+1)\hbar^2$, hence $(2k+1)^2\hbar^2 = -mZ^2e^4/2E$, which is (4.37).
>
> *4.* Two commuting angular momenta with fixed $\mathbf I^2 = \mathbf K^2 = k(k+1)\hbar^2$ act irreducibly on the $(2k+1)^2$ products $|k, m_I\rangle|k, m_K\rangle$; that every irreducible multiplet of such a pair is of this product form is standard representation theory, used here without proof (Sakurai: "two independent groups SU(2)"). $\mathbf L = \mathbf I + \mathbf K$ is their sum, so by the Clebsch–Gordan series ([[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-2|Theorem §C7.1.2]]) the multiplet contains $\ell = 0, 1, \dots, 2k = n - 1$, each once, and $\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}(2\ell + 1) = n^2$. The solution of the radial equation ([[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]]) finds exactly these states at $E_n$, so each level is one multiplet.

> [!remark] Remark: Why four dimensions
> Rotations in $n$ dimensions turn one coordinate plane at a time, so there are $\binom n2 = n(n-1)/2$ generators: one in the plane ($L_z$), three in space, six in four dimensions. Sakurai makes the four-dimensional reading concrete by inventing a coordinate $x_4$ with conjugate $p_4$ and setting $L_{ab} = x_ap_b - x_bp_a$: then $L_{12}, L_{23}, L_{31}$ are $L_3, L_1, L_2$, and $N_i = L_{i4}$ (4.30) obey (4.29); for instance $[N_1, L_2] = [x_1p_4 - x_4p_1,\, x_3p_1 - x_1p_3] = i\hbar(x_3p_4 - x_4p_3) = i\hbar N_3$ (4.31). The fourth axis is not a direction of physical space: $\mathbf N$ is built from $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf p$ and acts only inside one energy level, which is why the algebra closes only after $H$ is replaced by $E$. The real orthogonal $n\times n$ matrices are $R = \exp\bigl(i\sum_q\phi^q\tau^q\bigr)$ with $\tau^q$ imaginary antisymmetric (4.38), and the commutators of the $\tau^q$ define the group's structure constants (4.39)–(4.40); for $n = 3$ they are $\epsilon_{pqr}$, the algebra of [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-1|Theorem §C5.1.1]] (compare [[§3 Basic Examples of Groups#^def-3-7|493 Def. §3.7]], [[§22 The Orthogonal and Unitary Groups as Smooth Manifolds#^ex-22-1|591 Ex. §22.1]]). Rewriting (4.29) as (4.34) shows that the algebra of $SO(4)$ is that of $SU(2)\times SU(2)$.

^rem-c8-1-5

> [!remark] Remark: What this proves, and the classical orbit
> [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^rem-c6-2-3|Remark: A symmetry, not an accident]] stated Pauli's solution and deferred the proof here; level B pointed to it in [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^rem-b5-4-2|★ Remark: Where the accidental degeneracy comes from]]. Theorems §C8.1.4–§C8.1.5 give the spectrum without solving any differential equation, and they explain both halves of the level structure: $\ell \le n - 1$ and the degeneracy $n^2$. What the algebra alone does not show is that every $k$ occurs; the ladder of Example §C8.1.3, or the radial solution, supplies that. Classically $\mathbf M$ is the vector pointing to the periapsis of a closed Kepler ellipse ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-5|CM Theorem §B2.2.5]], [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^rem-b2-2-4|CM Remark: Why Kepler orbits close]]). Any departure from $1/r$ makes it turn and the orbit precess ([[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^rem-b2-3-2|CM Remark: Precession as a turning Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector]]); quantum mechanically the same departure breaks $SO(4)$ to $SO(3)$ and splits the different $\ell$ of one $n$, as shielding does in many-electron atoms, where low $\ell$ lies lower ([[§B7.2 Atoms#^rem-b7-2-4|Remark: What level B adds to the periodic table]]) (Sakurai: "even a small deviation from a $1/r$ potential leads to precession of this axis").

^rem-c8-1-6

> [!remark] Remark: The hidden symmetry of the isotropic oscillator
> The oscillator's level $N$ has degeneracy $(N+1)(N+2)/2$, more than one rotation multiplet ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^rem-c6-1-5|★ Remark: Conserved operators beyond the angular momentum]], which deferred the group to this chapter). Its nine conserved operators $a_i^\dagger a_j$ close under commutation, $[a_i^\dagger a_j, a_k^\dagger a_l] = \delta_{jk}\,a_i^\dagger a_l - \delta_{il}\,a_k^\dagger a_j$ (from $[a_j, a_k^\dagger] = \delta_{jk}$); this is the Lie algebra of $U(3)$, with $N$ commuting with everything and the eight traceless combinations $a_i^\dagger a_j - \frac13\delta_{ij}N$ generating $SU(3)$ (computed here). They act on the level $N$, spanned by the $(N+1)(N+2)/2$ monomials $(a_x^\dagger)^{n_x}(a_y^\dagger)^{n_y}(a_z^\dagger)^{n_z}|0\rangle$ with $n_x + n_y + n_z = N$; that this space is one irreducible multiplet of $SU(3)$, so that again the whole degeneracy is forced, is standard group theory not in the sources (Griffiths, footnote 24, refers to Fradkin, Am. J. Phys. 33, 207 (1965)). Rotations sit inside as the antisymmetric part $L_k = -i\hbar\epsilon_{kij}a_i^\dagger a_j$, and the level splits into $\ell = N, N-2, \dots$ under them.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C8.1.1: Which Degeneracies of a Square Well Are Explained by Its Symmetry
> A particle in a two-dimensional infinite square well of side $L$ centred at the origin has the states $\psi_{ab}(x, y) = f_a(x)f_b(y)$, $f_n(x) = \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin\bigl[\frac{n\pi}{L}(x - \frac L2)\bigr]$, with $E_{ab} = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}(a^2 + b^2)$ (Griffiths Problem 6.33). Which degeneracies does the symmetry group of the square (the dihedral group: rotations by multiples of $90^\circ$ and four reflections) explain?
>
> *How the symmetries act.* $f_n(-x) = (-1)^{n+1}f_n(x)$. The rotation by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise sends a function to $(R\psi)(x, y) = \psi(y, -x)$, so
>
> $$
> R\,\psi_{ab} = (-1)^{b+1}\,\psi_{ba} ;
> $$
>
> the reflection in the diagonal, $D\psi(x, y) = \psi(y, x)$, gives $D\psi_{ab} = \psi_{ba}$, and the reflection $P_x\psi(x, y) = \psi(-x, y)$ gives $P_x\psi_{ab} = (-1)^{a+1}\psi_{ab}$.
>
> *$a$, $b$ of opposite parity* (e.g. $\psi_{12}$, $\psi_{21}$). On the pair, $R^2 = -1$ and $P_xR\psi_{ab} = -RP_x\psi_{ab}$: two symmetries that do not commute, so by Theorem §C8.1.3, 3 the pair is one irreducible two-dimensional piece and the degeneracy is **required by symmetry**.
>
> *$a$, $b$ of equal parity, $a \ne b$* (e.g. $\psi_{13}$, $\psi_{31}$). The combinations $\psi_\pm = (\psi_{ab} \pm \psi_{ba})/\sqrt2$ are eigenfunctions of $R$, $D$ and $P_x$ with different eigenvalues: for $a$, $b$ odd, $R\psi_\pm = \pm\psi_\pm$, $D\psi_\pm = \pm\psi_\pm$, $P_x\psi_\pm = \psi_\pm$. Each spans a one-dimensional piece by itself, and no symmetry of the square relates them (Griffiths' part (c) for $a = 5$, $b = 7$). Their degeneracy is explained by a larger, dynamical symmetry: $H = H_x + H_y$ separates, $H_x$ (the energy of the $x$ motion) is conserved, and it does not commute with $D$, since $DH_xD = H_y$; by Theorem §C8.1.3, 3 applied to the group generated by $D$ and the evolutions $e^{-isH_x/\hbar}$, every $\psi_{ab}$ with $a \ne b$ is degenerate with $\psi_{ba}$.
>
> *Truly accidental.* $E_{17} = E_{71} = E_{55}$, since $1 + 49 = 25 + 25$. $\psi_{55}$ has $H_x$-energy $25$ units, $\psi_{17}$ has $1$; neither the square's group nor $H_x$, $H_y$ connects them. The coincidence is number theory, not symmetry.
>
> *Moral.* "Accidental" degeneracy means degeneracy not explained by a symmetry one has identified; Griffiths (footnote 20): "In most such cases, the degeneracy turns out to be no accident at all", the prime case being hydrogen (Theorem §C8.1.5). Any degeneracy can be "explained" by some operator commuting with $H$, e.g. $|\psi_{55}\rangle\langle\psi_{17}| + |\psi_{17}\rangle\langle\psi_{55}|$; the content of a symmetry explanation is a natural group, fixed independently of the spectrum.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 6.33, §6.6, footnotes 1, 20, 42; solved here*

> [!example] Example §C8.1.2: Galilean Boosts, a Symmetry with a Time-Dependent Generator
> For a particle of mass $m$ on a line, Griffiths defines the boost by velocity $v$ at time $t$ as
>
> $$
> \Gamma(v, t) = \exp\Bigl[\frac{i v}{\hbar}\,G(t)\Bigr] , \qquad G(t) = m x - t\,p .
> $$
>
> Find how it acts, when it is a symmetry, and what is conserved.
>
> *Action.* $[G, x] = -t[p, x] = i\hbar t$ and $[G, p] = m[x, p] = i\hbar m$ are numbers, so the Baker–Hausdorff series ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]]) stops after one term:
>
> $$
> \Gamma^\dagger x\,\Gamma = x + vt , \qquad \Gamma^\dagger p\,\Gamma = p + mv .
> $$
>
> The boosted state is displaced by $vt$ and moves faster by $v$. At $t = 0$ this is the momentum shift $e^{imvx/\hbar}$ of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^ex-c2-2-2|Example §C2.2.2]]; at later times the boost must also translate, and with $e^{A+B} = e^Ae^Be^{-[A,B]/2}$ for a central commutator, $\Gamma(v, t) = e^{i(mvx - \frac12mv^2t)/\hbar}\,\mathcal T(vt)$ (Griffiths part (b)).
>
> *Symmetry.* $H = p^2/2m + V(x)$ does not commute with $G$: $\frac i\hbar[H, G] = \frac i\hbar\bigl(m[\frac{p^2}{2m}, x] - t[V, p]\bigr) = p + tV'(x)$. But $G$ depends on $t$, and the criterion of Theorem §C8.1.2 is
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial G}{\partial t} + \frac i\hbar[H, G] = -p + p + tV'(x) = t\,V'(x) ,
> $$
>
> which vanishes for every $t$ only for the free particle. So boosts are a symmetry of free motion although $\Gamma^\dagger H\Gamma = (p + mv)^2/2m \ne H$: the invariant object is the Schrödinger equation, not the operator $H$. With a potential, the boosted wave function solves the Schrödinger equation with the moving potential $V(x - vt)$ (Griffiths part (c)).
>
> *Conservation.* For $V = 0$, $G^{(H)}(t) = m\,x^{(H)}(t) - t\,p^{(H)}(t)$ is constant, equal to $m x$: with $p^{(H)}$ constant this is $x^{(H)}(t) = x + p\,t/m$, the free motion of [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-5|Theorem §C3.3.5]]. For many particles $G = \sum m_ax_a - tP$, and its conservation is the uniform motion of the centre of mass, the conserved quantity that boosts give classically ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-5|CM Theorem §B6.4.5]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 6.35; solved here*

> [!example] Example §C8.1.3: The Runge–Lenz Ladder
> In the hydrogen-like atom, let $M_\pm = M_x \pm iM_y$ and let $|n\ell m\rangle$ be the bound states of [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]] (Condon–Shortley phases). Show that $M_+|n\ell\ell\rangle = c_{n\ell}\,|n, \ell+1, \ell+1\rangle$ and find $|c_{n\ell}|$.
>
> *Where $M_+$ and $M_z$ lead.* $\mathbf M$ commutes with $H$ and is a vector (Theorem §C8.1.4, 1 and 4), so it stays inside the level $n$ and changes $\ell$ by at most one, with $m' = m + q$ for the spherical component $M_q$ (Wigner–Eckart, [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-6|Theorem §C7.2.6]]). $M_+ = -\sqrt2\,M_{+1}$ raises $m = \ell$ to $\ell + 1$, which forces $\ell' = \ell + 1$: $M_+|n\ell\ell\rangle = c_{n\ell}|n, \ell+1, \ell+1\rangle$. $M_z = M_0$ keeps $m = \ell$, so $\ell' \in \{\ell, \ell + 1\}$; the part with $\ell' = \ell$ vanishes by the projection theorem ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-8|Theorem §C7.2.8]]), because it is proportional to $\langle\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf M\rangle = 0$. The ratio of the two Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, $\langle\ell1;\ell0|\ell+1,\ell\rangle/\langle\ell1;\ell1|\ell+1,\ell+1\rangle = 1/\sqrt{\ell+1}$, then gives
>
> $$
> M_z|n\ell\ell\rangle = -\frac{c_{n\ell}}{\sqrt{2(\ell+1)}}\,|n, \ell+1, \ell\rangle .
> $$
>
> *The norm.* $M_-M_+ = M_x^2 + M_y^2 + i[M_x, M_y] = \mathbf M^2 - M_z^2 + \frac{2\hbar}{m}HL_z$ by Theorem §C8.1.4, 5. On $|n\ell\ell\rangle$, with Theorem §C8.1.4, 3 and $\frac2m E_n\hbar^2 = -Z^2e^4/n^2$,
>
> $$
> M_-M_+|n\ell\ell\rangle = Z^2e^4\Bigl[1 - \frac{(\ell+1)^2}{n^2}\Bigr]|n\ell\ell\rangle - M_z^2|n\ell\ell\rangle .
> $$
>
> Taking the expectation value, $\|M_+|n\ell\ell\rangle\|^2 = |c_{n\ell}|^2$ (since $M_- = M_+^\dagger$) and $\|M_z|n\ell\ell\rangle\|^2 = |c_{n\ell}|^2/2(\ell+1)$, so
>
> $$
> |c_{n\ell}|^2 = Z^2e^4\,\frac{2(\ell+1)}{2\ell+3}\,\frac{n^2 - (\ell+1)^2}{n^2} .
> $$
>
> *Reading.* $c_{n\ell} \ne 0$ for $\ell < n - 1$ and $c_{n,n-1} = 0$: starting from $|n00\rangle$, $M_+$ climbs $\ell = 0, 1, \dots, n-1$ inside one level and stops, while $L_-$ fills in the $m$'s of each $\ell$. This is the level structure of Theorem §C8.1.5 built state by state (Griffiths Figure 6.9 draws it for $n = 3$). Check: for $n = 2$, $\ell = 0$ the formula gives $|c_{20}|^2 = Z^2e^4/2$; applying the differential operator $M_+$ to $\psi_{200}$ gives $-\frac{Ze^2}{\sqrt2}\psi_{211}$ (computed here).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 6.34(a)–(c), Figure 6.9; solved here with the Wigner–Eckart and projection theorems in place of Griffiths' Problem 6.19*
