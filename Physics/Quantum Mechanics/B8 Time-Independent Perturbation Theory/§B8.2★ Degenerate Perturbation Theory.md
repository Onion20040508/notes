*Sources: Griffiths & Schroeter, Introduction to Quantum Mechanics (3rd ed.), §7.2 · Greensite, Physics 430 lecture notes, ch. 17.*

Most levels worth perturbing are degenerate: hydrogen's level $n$ has $n^2$ orbital states ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-6|Theorem §A5.1.6]]), and degeneracy is what symmetry produces ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]). For such a level the formulas of [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory]] divide by zero ([[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^cau-b8-1-1|Caution: Degenerate levels]]), and even the first-order energy is undefined, because it is not known which combination of the degenerate states the perturbed state grows out of. This section answers: which combinations, and how does the level split? The answer is to diagonalize the perturbation inside the degenerate subspace, a finite-dimensional eigenvalue problem; and when an operator commuting with both $H^0$ and $H'$ is available, its eigenstates do the job with no diagonalization at all. Level A met the outcome, levels split by a field ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^def-a5-2-2|Def. §A5.2.2]]), without the method.

## Good states

> [!definition] Definition §B8.2.1: Degenerate Subspace; W Matrix; Good States
> Let $\psi_1^0, \dots, \psi_q^0$ be orthonormal eigenstates of $H^0$ with the same eigenvalue $E^0$, spanning the **degenerate subspace** of that level; every combination $\psi^0 = \sum_i\alpha_i\psi_i^0$ is again an eigenstate with eigenvalue $E^0$. For a perturbation $H'$, the **W matrix** of the level is the $q\times q$ matrix of $H'$ in this subspace,
>
> $$
> W_{ij} = \langle\psi_i^0|H'|\psi_j^0\rangle, \qquad W_{ji} = W_{ij}^* .
> $$
>
> The **good states** are the limits, as $\lambda \to 0$, of the exact eigenstates of $H^0 + \lambda H'$ that grow out of the level: the particular combinations of the $\psi_i^0$ to which the perturbed states reduce when the perturbation is switched off.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.1, eqs. (7.16)–(7.18), (7.28), §7.2.3, eq. (7.40); Greensite ch. 17, eq. (17.68)*

^def-b8-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.2.1: First-Order Degenerate Perturbation Theory
> The first-order energy corrections $E^1$ of a $q$-fold degenerate level are the eigenvalues of the W matrix, and the good states are given by its eigenvectors:
>
> $$
> \sum_{j}W_{ij}\,\alpha_j = E^1\alpha_i \quad (i = 1, \dots, q), \qquad \psi^0 = \sum_j\alpha_j\psi_j^0 .
> $$
>
> $W$ is Hermitian, so its eigenvalues are real and its eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal. For a two-fold level,
>
> $$
> E^1_\pm = \frac12\Bigl[W_{aa} + W_{bb} \pm \sqrt{(W_{aa} - W_{bb})^2 + 4|W_{ab}|^2}\,\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> If the eigenvalues of $W$ are distinct, the degeneracy is **lifted at first order** and the good states are fixed; once they are used as the unperturbed states, the nondegenerate formulas of [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory]] apply to them.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.1, eqs. (7.24)–(7.33), §7.2.3, eqs. (7.40)–(7.42), Problem 7.13; Greensite ch. 17, §17.4, eqs. (17.68)–(17.79)*

^thm-b8-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write the unknown good state as $\psi^0 = \sum_j\alpha_j\psi_j^0$ and expand $\psi = \psi^0 + \lambda\psi^1 + \cdots$, $E = E^0 + \lambda E^1 + \cdots$ in $(H^0 + \lambda H')\psi = E\psi$. Since $H^0\psi^0 = E^0\psi^0$, the order-$\lambda$ terms give, exactly as in [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^der-b8-1-1|the nondegenerate case]],
>
> $$
> H^0\psi^1 + H'\psi^0 = E^0\psi^1 + E^1\psi^0 .
> $$
>
> Take the inner product with $\psi_i^0$, one of the degenerate states. By Hermiticity $\langle\psi_i^0|H^0\psi^1\rangle = E^0\langle\psi_i^0|\psi^1\rangle$, which cancels the matching term on the right. With $\langle\psi_i^0|\psi_j^0\rangle = \delta_{ij}$ what remains is
>
> $$
> \sum_j\alpha_j\langle\psi_i^0|H'|\psi_j^0\rangle = E^1\alpha_i ,
> $$
>
> the eigenvalue equation for $W$. $W$ is the matrix of the Hermitian operator $H'$ restricted to the subspace, so $W_{ji} = W_{ij}^*$; a Hermitian matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors ([[§23 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-12|LADR Thm. 7.12]], [[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]). For $q = 2$, nonzero $(\alpha, \beta)$ requires
>
> $$
> \det\begin{pmatrix} W_{aa} - E^1 & W_{ab}\\ W_{ba} & W_{bb} - E^1\end{pmatrix} = (W_{aa} - E^1)(W_{bb} - E^1) - |W_{ab}|^2 = 0 ,
> $$
>
> a quadratic whose roots are $E^1_\pm$. If the roots are distinct, each determines its eigenvector up to a factor, so the good states are fixed. In the basis of good states $W$ is diagonal, the off-diagonal elements that caused the division by zero vanish, and the nondegenerate formulas can be used.

> [!remark] Remark: When first order does not decide
> - If $W_{ab} = 0$ from the start, the original states were already good, and $E^1 = W_{aa}$, $W_{bb}$ are the nondegenerate results (Griffiths eq. 7.35). One was lucky; the next theorem says how to arrange the luck.
> - If $W$ has a repeated eigenvalue, every combination in that eigenspace solves the first-order equation. The first-order energies are still right, but the good states are not determined; they are fixed only at higher order (second-order degenerate theory, Griffiths Problems 7.39–7.41) or by the theorem below (Griffiths footnotes 6–7). A $W$ that happens to vanish identically does not mean the original states are good (Griffiths Problem 7.39).
> - Degenerate perturbation theory is just the diagonalization of $H$ inside the degenerate block. Greensite's form: replace the $\psi_i^0$ of the level by the eigenvectors of $W$ and keep all other unperturbed states; then every first-order coefficient within the block vanishes, and the nondegenerate machinery runs unchanged (his eqs. 17.71–17.74).

^rem-b8-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.2.2: Good States from a Commuting Observable
> Let $A$ be a Hermitian operator that commutes with $H^0$ and with $H'$. If the degenerate states $\psi_a^0$ and $\psi_b^0$ of $H^0$ are also eigenstates of $A$ with distinct eigenvalues,
>
> $$
> A\psi_a^0 = \mu\psi_a^0, \qquad A\psi_b^0 = \nu\psi_b^0, \qquad \mu \neq \nu ,
> $$
>
> then $\psi_a^0$ and $\psi_b^0$ are the good states. The same holds for a $q$-fold level whose states are labelled by distinct eigenvalues of $A$ (or of several commuting such operators): $W$ is then diagonal in that basis, and $E^1_i = \langle\psi_i^0|H'|\psi_i^0\rangle$ by the nondegenerate formula.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.2.2, eqs. (7.36)–(7.38), §7.2.3*

^thm-b8-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $A$ commutes with $H(\lambda) = H^0 + \lambda H'$ for every $\lambda$, so there are simultaneous eigenstates $\psi_\gamma(\lambda)$, with $H(\lambda)\psi_\gamma(\lambda) = E(\lambda)\psi_\gamma(\lambda)$ and $A\psi_\gamma(\lambda) = \gamma\,\psi_\gamma(\lambda)$ ([[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-76|LADR Thm. 5.76]] in finite dimensions). Since $A$ is Hermitian and $\mu$ real,
>
> $$
> \gamma\,\langle\psi_a^0|\psi_\gamma(\lambda)\rangle = \langle\psi_a^0|A\psi_\gamma(\lambda)\rangle = \langle A\psi_a^0|\psi_\gamma(\lambda)\rangle = \mu\,\langle\psi_a^0|\psi_\gamma(\lambda)\rangle ,
> $$
>
> so $(\gamma - \mu)\langle\psi_a^0|\psi_\gamma(\lambda)\rangle = 0$ for every $\lambda$, and also in the limit $\lambda \to 0$. Likewise $(\gamma - \nu)\langle\psi_b^0|\psi_\gamma(0)\rangle = 0$. A good state is a limit $\psi_\gamma(0) = \alpha\psi_a^0 + \beta\psi_b^0$. If $\gamma = \mu$, then $\gamma \neq \nu$, so $\beta = \langle\psi_b^0|\psi_\gamma(0)\rangle = 0$ and the good state is $\psi_a^0$; if $\gamma = \nu$, it is $\psi_b^0$. For several states the same argument applies to each pair. Diagonality of $W$ also follows directly: $(\mu - \nu)\,W_{ab} = \langle\psi_a^0|(AH' - H'A)|\psi_b^0\rangle = 0$, so $W_{ab} = 0$ whenever $\mu \neq \nu$.

^der-b8-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^def-b8-2-1|Def. §B8.2.1]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]], [[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-76|LADR Thm. 5.76]]

> [!remark] Remark: Symmetry chooses the good states
> The operator $A$ usually expresses a symmetry that $H'$ shares with $H^0$ (Griffiths: symmetries are operators commuting with $H$). The theorem is more general than diagonalizing $W$: it fixes the good states even when the degeneracy is lifted only at second or higher order (Griffiths footnote 7). It also explains what is left: symmetry that survives the perturbation keeps some degeneracy. A field along $z$ leaves rotations about $z$ intact, so $L_z$ (or $J_z$) still commutes with $H$, and states differing only in the sign of $m_\ell$ (or $m_j$) can remain degenerate (Example §B8.2.2; Greensite §17.4.3). In the fine structure and the Zeeman effect ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen]], [[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting]]) the work is mostly in finding the right $A$'s: $L^2$, $L_z$, $S_z$ for one perturbation; $L^2$, $J^2$, $J_z$ for another. A list of commuting operators whose eigenvalues label the states of the level uniquely is a complete set of commuting observables for that level ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]]).

^rem-b8-2-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B8.2.1: Two-Dimensional Oscillator with an xy Perturbation
> Take $H^0 = p^2/2m + \tfrac12 m\omega^2(x^2 + y^2)$ and $H' = \epsilon\,m\omega^2xy$. The first excited level $E^0 = 2\hbar\omega$ is two-fold degenerate, with $\psi_a^0 = \psi_0(x)\psi_1(y)$ and $\psi_b^0 = \psi_1(x)\psi_0(y)$ built from one-dimensional oscillator states (the two-dimensional case of [[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-7|Theorem §B5.1.7]]).
>
> *W matrix.* $W_{aa} = \epsilon m\omega^2\int|\psi_0|^2x\,dx\int|\psi_1|^2y\,dy = 0$, as both integrands are odd; likewise $W_{bb} = 0$. With $x = \sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}\,(\hat a_+ + \hat a_-)$,
>
> $$
> W_{ab} = \epsilon m\omega^2\Bigl[\int\psi_0\,x\,\psi_1\,dx\Bigr]^2 = \epsilon m\omega^2\,\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega} = \epsilon\,\frac{\hbar\omega}2, \qquad \mathsf W = \epsilon\,\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> Its eigenvalues give $E^1 = \pm\epsilon\hbar\omega/2$, and its eigenvectors the good states $\psi_\pm^0 = (\psi_b^0 \pm \psi_a^0)/\sqrt2$ (Theorem §B8.2.1).
>
> *By symmetry.* Rotation by $\pi$ commutes with $H^0$ and $H'$ but has the same eigenvalue $-1$ on both $\psi_a^0$ and $\psi_b^0$, so it does not help. The exchange $D: (x, y) \mapsto (y, x)$, a reflection in the diagonal, commutes with both and swaps $\psi_a^0 \leftrightarrow \psi_b^0$; its eigenstates $\psi_\pm^0$ have the distinct eigenvalues $\pm1$, so by Theorem §B8.2.2 they are good, with no matrix to diagonalize.
>
> *Exactly.* In the rotated coordinates $x' = (x + y)/\sqrt2$, $y' = (x - y)/\sqrt2$, $H$ is two independent oscillators of frequencies $\omega_\pm = \sqrt{1 \pm \epsilon}\,\omega$, with energies $E_{mn} = (m + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega_+ + (n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega_-$. The two states from the excited level, $E_{10}$ and $E_{01}$, expand to $2\hbar\omega \pm \epsilon\hbar\omega/2 + O(\epsilon^2)$, and as $\epsilon \to 0$ they tend to $\psi_\pm^0$: the good states of Def. §B8.2.1, found three ways.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Examples 7.2–7.4, eqs. (7.19)–(7.23), (7.34), (7.39), Problem 7.10; Greensite ch. 17, §17.4.2*

> [!example] Example §B8.2.2: The Linear Stark Effect in Hydrogen
> A hydrogen atom sits in a uniform electric field $\mathcal E$ along $z$; the electron's potential energy gains $H'_S = e\mathcal E z = e\mathcal E r\cos\theta$ (spin and fine structure ignored). The ground state is nondegenerate and $E_1^1 = e\mathcal E\langle 100|z|100\rangle = 0$, since $|\psi_{100}|^2$ is even and $z$ odd. The level $n = 2$ has the four states $\psi_{200}, \psi_{211}, \psi_{210}, \psi_{21,-1}$.
>
> *Which matrix elements survive.* Write $m_\ell$ for the magnetic quantum number (Griffiths' chapter-7 notation, stated in [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen]]; in this example no mass appears). $L_z$ commutes with $z$, so $(m_\ell - m_\ell')\hbar\,\langle n\ell m_\ell|z|n\ell'm_\ell'\rangle = \langle n\ell m_\ell|[L_z, z]|n\ell'm_\ell'\rangle = 0$: only $m_\ell = m_\ell'$ survives (the argument of Theorem §B8.2.2). Under the parity $\mathbf r \to -\mathbf r$, $\psi_{n\ell m}$ has sign $(-1)^\ell$ and $z$ is odd, so $\langle n\ell m_\ell|z|n\ell m_\ell\rangle = 0$. The only nonzero elements connect $\psi_{200}$ and $\psi_{210}$. With $\psi_{200} = (8\pi a^3)^{-1/2}(1 - r/2a)\,e^{-r/2a}$ and $\psi_{210} = (32\pi a^3)^{-1/2}(r/a)\,e^{-r/2a}\cos\theta$ ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-4|Theorem §B5.4.4]]),
>
> $$
> \langle 200|z|210\rangle = \frac{1}{16\pi a^4}\int\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega\int_0^\infty\Bigl(1 - \frac{r}{2a}\Bigr)r^4e^{-r/a}\,dr = \frac{1}{16\pi a^4}\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3}\cdot\bigl(24a^5 - 60a^5\bigr) = -3a ,
> $$
>
> using $\int_0^\infty r^ke^{-r/a}dr = k!\,a^{k+1}$. So, ordering the basis as $(200, 211, 210, 21{-1})$, $W_{13} = W_{31} = -3ea\mathcal E$ and all other elements vanish. The eigenvalues are
>
> $$
> E^1 = +3ea\mathcal E, \quad -3ea\mathcal E, \quad 0, \quad 0 ,
> $$
>
> with good states $(\psi_{200} - \psi_{210})/\sqrt2$, $(\psi_{200} + \psi_{210})/\sqrt2$, and any combination of $\psi_{211}$, $\psi_{21,-1}$. The four-fold level splits into **three**, linear in the field. In the two shifted states $\langle z\rangle = \pm3a$: the atom carries an electric dipole moment $\mathbf p = -e\langle\mathbf r\rangle$ of size $3ea$, independent of the field, and the shift is $-\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal E}$ ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^def-a1-5-1|EM Def. §A1.5.1]]). A nondegenerate state cannot have such a permanent moment (its density has definite parity); $n = 2$ can, because the degenerate $2s$ and $2p$ states of opposite parity mix. The leftover degeneracy of $m_\ell = \pm1$ is the symmetry about the field axis that survives (Remark: Symmetry chooses the good states).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 7.45 (with its partial answer $W_{13} = W_{31} = -3ea\mathcal E_{\text{ext}}$); Greensite ch. 17, §17.4.3*

^ex-b8-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: Sign of the Stark matrix element in Greensite
> Greensite (eq. 17.108) gives $\langle 210|z|200\rangle = +3a_0$; with the wave functions he writes, the radial integral is negative and the element is $-3a_0$, as in Griffiths' partial answer above. The energies $\pm3ea\mathcal E$ do not depend on this sign; only the labels of the two good states $(\psi_{200} \pm \psi_{210})/\sqrt2$ swap. His eq. (17.103) also writes the perturbation as $-e\mathcal E_z z$, the opposite sign to his eq. (17.8); again only the labelling is affected.
