*Sources: Griffiths & Schroeter, Introduction to Quantum Mechanics (3rd ed.), §7.1 · Greensite, Physics 430 lecture notes, ch. 17.*

Exactly solvable Hamiltonians are rare: the box, the oscillator, hydrogen. Real systems differ from them by small extra terms: an anharmonic piece in a molecular bond, an external field, a relativistic correction. This section answers the question: if the eigenvalues and eigenstates of $H^0$ are known, what are those of $H^0 + H'$ when $H'$ is small? Level A had no tool for this; it quoted the fine structure ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-9|Theorem §A5.2.9]]) and the Zeeman effect ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-3|Theorem §A5.2.3]]) without one. The tool is an expansion in powers of the perturbation, built on two level-B facts: the eigenfunctions of a Hermitian $H^0$ are orthonormal and complete ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]]). Its first-order result, that an energy shifts by the expectation value of the perturbation, is the workhorse of Chapter B8.

*Notation for Chapter B8.* Griffiths' superscripts: $0$ marks unperturbed quantities, $1, 2, \dots$ the order of a correction ($E_n^2$ is the second-order correction, not a square). Kets $|\psi_n^0\rangle$ and wave functions $\psi_n^0$ are used interchangeably, with the inner product of [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-2|Def. §B2.1.2]].

## The perturbation series

> [!definition] Definition §B8.1.1: Perturbation; Unperturbed Problem; Corrections of Order k
> Let $H^0$ be a Hamiltonian whose eigenvalue problem is solved,
>
> $$
> H^0\psi_n^0 = E_n^0\psi_n^0, \qquad \langle\psi_n^0|\psi_m^0\rangle = \delta_{nm},
> $$
>
> with $\{\psi_n^0\}$ complete. The **perturbed** Hamiltonian is
>
> $$
> H = H^0 + \lambda H',
> $$
>
> where the Hermitian operator $H'$ is the **perturbation** and $\lambda$ a bookkeeping parameter, small while we sort terms and set to $1$ at the end. One seeks the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of $H$, $H\psi_n = E_n\psi_n$, as power series in $\lambda$:
>
> $$
> \psi_n = \psi_n^0 + \lambda\psi_n^1 + \lambda^2\psi_n^2 + \cdots, \qquad E_n = E_n^0 + \lambda E_n^1 + \lambda^2 E_n^2 + \cdots .
> $$
>
> $E_n^k$ and $\psi_n^k$ are the **$k$th-order corrections**. The level $E_n^0$ is **nondegenerate** if no other $\psi_m^0$ has the same energy.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.1.1, eqs. (7.1)–(7.6); Greensite ch. 17, eqs. (17.11)–(17.14)*

^def-b8-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: What the series assumes
> The ansatz presumes that $E_n(\lambda)$ and $\psi_n(\lambda)$ are analytic at $\lambda = 0$, so that their Taylor coefficients ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) are the corrections: Greensite defines $E_n^{k} = \frac{1}{k!}\,d^kE_n/d\lambda^k\big|_{\lambda = 0}$. Nothing guarantees that the series converges at $\lambda = 1$; often it does not, and the first terms are still accurate (an asymptotic series). In practice one stops at first order for states and second order for energies. Both sources reach the same order-by-order equations; Greensite also writes the general $N$th-order recursion (his eq. 17.31).

^rem-b8-1-1

## First order

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.1: First-Order Energy Correction
> If $E_n^0$ is nondegenerate, the first-order correction to the energy is the expectation value of the perturbation in the unperturbed state:
>
> $$
> E_n^1 = \langle\psi_n^0|H'|\psi_n^0\rangle .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.1.2, eq. (7.9); Greensite ch. 17, eq. (17.35)*

^thm-b8-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert both series into $(H^0 + \lambda H')\psi_n = E_n\psi_n$ and collect powers of $\lambda$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> &H^0\psi_n^0 + \lambda\bigl(H^0\psi_n^1 + H'\psi_n^0\bigr) + \lambda^2\bigl(H^0\psi_n^2 + H'\psi_n^1\bigr) + \cdots \\
> &\quad= E_n^0\psi_n^0 + \lambda\bigl(E_n^0\psi_n^1 + E_n^1\psi_n^0\bigr) + \lambda^2\bigl(E_n^0\psi_n^2 + E_n^1\psi_n^1 + E_n^2\psi_n^0\bigr) + \cdots .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> The identity holds for every small $\lambda$, so the coefficients of each power agree. Order $\lambda^0$ is the unperturbed equation. Orders $\lambda^1$ and $\lambda^2$ give
>
> $$
> H^0\psi_n^1 + H'\psi_n^0 = E_n^0\psi_n^1 + E_n^1\psi_n^0, \qquad (7.7)
> $$
>
> $$
> H^0\psi_n^2 + H'\psi_n^1 = E_n^0\psi_n^2 + E_n^1\psi_n^1 + E_n^2\psi_n^0 . \qquad (7.8)
> $$
>
> Take the inner product of (7.7) with $\psi_n^0$. Because $H^0$ is Hermitian ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]) and its eigenvalue is real,
>
> $$
> \langle\psi_n^0|H^0\psi_n^1\rangle = \langle H^0\psi_n^0|\psi_n^1\rangle = E_n^0\langle\psi_n^0|\psi_n^1\rangle ,
> $$
>
> which cancels the first term on the right. With $\langle\psi_n^0|\psi_n^0\rangle = 1$ what remains is $\langle\psi_n^0|H'|\psi_n^0\rangle = E_n^1$. Nondegeneracy has not been used yet; it is needed for the state (next theorem), and without it the unperturbed state $\psi_n^0$ itself is not determined ([[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory]]).

^der-b8-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the first-order formula
> - The shift is an average: the perturbation weighted by the unperturbed probability density, $E_n^1 = \int|\psi_n^0|^2\,H'\,dx$ for a potential $H' = V'(x)$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-3|Def. §A3.4.3]]). A perturbation placed where $\psi_n^0$ is small hardly moves the level.
> - Raising the floor of the infinite well ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]) by a constant $V_0$ gives $E_n^1 = V_0$ for every $n$, and here first order is exact: a constant shifts every eigenvalue and changes no eigenfunction, so all higher corrections vanish. Raising only the left half, $0 < x < a/2$, gives $E_n^1 = (2V_0/a)\int_0^{a/2}\sin^2(n\pi x/a)\,dx = V_0/2$: every level sees, on average, half the bump, because each $|\psi_n^0|^2$ is symmetric about the centre (Griffiths Example 7.1).

^rem-b8-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.2: First-Order Correction to the State
> If the unperturbed spectrum is nondegenerate, then, with the normalisation choice $\langle\psi_n^0|\psi_n^1\rangle = 0$,
>
> $$
> \psi_n^1 = \sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\langle\psi_m^0|H'|\psi_n^0\rangle}{E_n^0 - E_m^0}\,\psi_m^0 .
> $$
>
> To this order $\psi_n^0 + \psi_n^1$ is still normalized.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §7.1.2, eqs. (7.10)–(7.13) and footnote 4; Greensite ch. 17, eqs. (17.22)–(17.23), (17.34)*

^thm-b8-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Rewrite (7.7) as $(H^0 - E_n^0)\psi_n^1 = -(H' - E_n^1)\psi_n^0$, an inhomogeneous equation for $\psi_n^1$. If $\psi_n^1$ solves it, so does $\psi_n^1 + \alpha\psi_n^0$, since $(H^0 - E_n^0)\psi_n^0 = 0$; use this freedom to remove the $\psi_n^0$ component. By completeness ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]], [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]) expand $\psi_n^1 = \sum_{m\neq n}c_m^{(n)}\psi_m^0$. Then
>
> $$
> \sum_{m\neq n}\bigl(E_m^0 - E_n^0\bigr)c_m^{(n)}\psi_m^0 = -\bigl(H' - E_n^1\bigr)\psi_n^0 .
> $$
>
> The inner product with $\psi_l^0$, $l \neq n$, picks out one term on the left, and $\langle\psi_l^0|\psi_n^0\rangle = 0$ on the right:
>
> $$
> \bigl(E_l^0 - E_n^0\bigr)c_l^{(n)} = -\langle\psi_l^0|H'|\psi_n^0\rangle \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_l^{(n)} = \frac{\langle\psi_l^0|H'|\psi_n^0\rangle}{E_n^0 - E_l^0} .
> $$
>
> The division is legitimate exactly when no $E_l^0$, $l \neq n$, equals $E_n^0$. (For $l = n$ the same inner product reproduces Theorem §B8.1.1.) Finally $\langle\psi_n^0 + \psi_n^1|\psi_n^0 + \psi_n^1\rangle = 1 + \langle\psi_n^1|\psi_n^1\rangle$, which is $1$ up to second order. Greensite reaches the same choice by normalising $\langle\psi_n^0|\psi_n\rangle = 1$ for every $\lambda$, which forces $\langle\psi_n^0|\psi_n^k\rangle = 0$ at every order, and normalises properly at the end.

^der-b8-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]], [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]], [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Degenerate levels
> The denominators $E_n^0 - E_m^0$ vanish when another state shares the energy of $\psi_n^0$, and then Theorem §B8.1.2 (and the second-order formula below) is meaningless, unless the matching numerator $\langle\psi_m^0|H'|\psi_n^0\rangle$ also vanishes. Since symmetry makes degeneracy the rule (hydrogen's $n^2$-fold levels), most applications need the degenerate theory of [[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory]]: there one first chooses the right ("good") unperturbed states, after which the formulas of this section apply unchanged.

^cau-b8-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: When is a perturbation small?
> The first-order state is a small admixture only if every coefficient is small, $|\langle\psi_m^0|H'|\psi_n^0\rangle| \ll |E_n^0 - E_m^0|$ for $m \neq n$: matrix elements of the perturbation must be small compared with the spacing of the levels they connect. In addition $|E_n^1|$ should be small compared with the level spacing near $E_n^0$ (Greensite §17.1). "Small" is therefore not an absolute statement about $H'$, but a comparison with $H^0$'s spectrum, and the same $H'$ can be small for low levels and not for high ones (Example §B8.1.1; Griffiths' comment to Problem 7.4 makes the same point).

^rem-b8-1-3

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Second-order energies
> Taking the inner product of (7.8) with $\psi_n^0$, the $H^0$ terms cancel as before, and $\langle\psi_n^0|\psi_n^1\rangle = 0$ removes the $E_n^1$ term:
>
> $$
> E_n^2 = \langle\psi_n^0|H'|\psi_n^1\rangle = \sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\bigl|\langle\psi_m^0|H'|\psi_n^0\rangle\bigr|^2}{E_n^0 - E_m^0}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. 7.14–7.15; Greensite eq. 17.37), using Theorem §B8.1.2 and $\langle\psi_n^0|H'|\psi_m^0\rangle = \langle\psi_m^0|H'|\psi_n^0\rangle^*$. Each level is pushed away from the others it is coupled to: a state below $E_n^0$ pushes it up, a state above pushes it down. For the ground state every denominator is negative, so $E_0^2 \le 0$ (Griffiths Problem 8.6(b)); this is consistent with the variational bound of [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle]], by which first order can never underestimate the ground-state energy. Going further (second-order state, third-order energy) is rarely useful; Greensite gives the general recursion.

^rem-b8-1-4

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Perturbation theory as approximate diagonalization
> In the basis $\{\psi_n^0\}$, $H^0$ is the diagonal matrix of the $E_n^0$ and $H = H^0 + H'$ has matrix elements $E_n^0\delta_{mn} + H'_{mn}$ ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]). Solving $H\psi = E\psi$ means diagonalizing this Hermitian matrix, which is always possible ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]). The formulas of this section diagonalize it approximately when its off-diagonal elements are small compared with the differences of the diagonal ones: $E_n \approx E_n^0 + H'_{nn} + \sum_{m\neq n}|H'_{mn}|^2/(E_n^0 - E_m^0)$. They apply to any nearly diagonal matrix, not only to Hamiltonians (Greensite §17.3).

^rem-b8-1-5

## The Feynman–Hellmann theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.3: Feynman–Hellmann Theorem
> Let $H(\lambda)$ depend on a real parameter $\lambda$, with normalized eigenstate $\psi_n(\lambda)$ and eigenvalue $E_n(\lambda)$. If $E_n$ is nondegenerate (or, if it is degenerate, $\psi_n$ is one of the good states of [[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory]]), then
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial E_n}{\partial\lambda} = \Bigl\langle\psi_n\Bigm|\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\Bigm|\psi_n\Bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 7.38, eq. (7.110)*

^thm-b8-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Fix $\lambda$ and change it by $\delta$. By Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), $H(\lambda + \delta) = H(\lambda) + \delta\,\partial H/\partial\lambda + O(\delta^2)$. Treat $H(\lambda)$ as the unperturbed Hamiltonian and $\delta\,\partial H/\partial\lambda$ as the perturbation. Theorem §B8.1.1 gives
>
> $$
> E_n(\lambda + \delta) = E_n(\lambda) + \delta\,\Bigl\langle\psi_n(\lambda)\Bigm|\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\Bigm|\psi_n(\lambda)\Bigr\rangle + O(\delta^2) .
> $$
>
> Subtract $E_n(\lambda)$, divide by $\delta$ and let $\delta \to 0$. (Directly: differentiate $E_n = \langle\psi_n|H|\psi_n\rangle$; the two terms with $\partial\psi_n/\partial\lambda$ combine, by Hermiticity, into $E_n\,\partial\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle/\partial\lambda = 0$.)

^der-b8-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Expectation values for free
> The theorem turns a known spectrum into expectation values with no integrals: differentiate $E_n$ with respect to a parameter of $H$. For the oscillator, $E_n = (n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega$ and $\partial H/\partial\omega = m\omega x^2$ give $\langle V\rangle = \tfrac12 E_n$ at once (Griffiths Problem 7.38(b)). For hydrogen, differentiating the Bohr energies with respect to $e$ and to $\ell$ gives $\langle 1/r\rangle$ (again [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-6|Theorem §B5.4.6]]) and $\langle 1/r^2\rangle$ ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-1|Theorem §B8.3.1]]), which the fine structure needs.

^rem-b8-1-6

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B8.1.1: The Anharmonic Oscillator
> Add a quartic term to the oscillator: $H = H^0 + \kappa x^4$ with $H^0 = p^2/2m + \tfrac12 m\omega^2x^2$ and $\kappa$ small (Greensite writes $\lambda$ for $\kappa$). The unperturbed levels $(n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega$ are nondegenerate ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]). Write $x = \sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}\,(\hat a_+ + \hat a_-)$ ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]]). In $(\hat a_+ + \hat a_-)^4$ only the six orderings with two raising and two lowering operators return $|n\rangle$ to itself; with $\hat a_+|n\rangle = \sqrt{n+1}\,|n+1\rangle$ and $\hat a_-|n\rangle = \sqrt n\,|n-1\rangle$ ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]) they contribute
>
> $$
> \underbrace{(n+1)(n+2)}_{\hat a_-\hat a_-\hat a_+\hat a_+} + \underbrace{(n+1)^2}_{\hat a_-\hat a_+\hat a_-\hat a_+} + \underbrace{n(n+1)}_{\hat a_-\hat a_+\hat a_+\hat a_-} + \underbrace{n(n+1)}_{\hat a_+\hat a_-\hat a_-\hat a_+} + \underbrace{n^2}_{\hat a_+\hat a_-\hat a_+\hat a_-} + \underbrace{n(n-1)}_{\hat a_+\hat a_+\hat a_-\hat a_-} = 3(2n^2 + 2n + 1) .
> $$
>
> By Theorem §B8.1.1,
>
> $$
> E_n \approx \Bigl(n + \frac12\Bigr)\hbar\omega + 3\kappa\Bigl(\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\Bigr)^2\bigl(2n^2 + 2n + 1\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The quartic term raises every level, and raises high levels more: the spacing $E_{n+1} - E_n = \hbar\omega + 3\kappa(\hbar/m\omega)^2(n + 1)$ grows with $n$, as for a spring that stiffens when stretched. The correction grows like $n^2$ while the unperturbed energy grows like $n$, so for any $\kappa > 0$ the expansion fails once $n$ is large enough (Remark: When is a perturbation small?). The reason is physical: a highly excited state spreads to large $|x|$, where $\kappa x^4$ exceeds $\tfrac12 m\omega^2x^2$ and is no longer a small addition.
>
> *Source: Greensite ch. 17, §17.2, eqs. (17.42)–(17.50)*

^ex-b8-1-1

> [!example]- ★ Example §B8.1.2: A Charged Oscillator in a Uniform Field
> A particle of charge $q$ in the oscillator potential is placed in a weak uniform electric field $\mathcal E$ along $x$, so $H' = -q\mathcal E x$. The matrix elements $\langle m|x|n\rangle = \sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}\,\bigl(\sqrt{n+1}\,\delta_{m,n+1} + \sqrt n\,\delta_{m,n-1}\bigr)$ ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]) vanish for $m = n$, so $E_n^1 = 0$: by symmetry, pushing right costs as much as pushing left gains. At second order (Remark: Second-order energies) only $m = n \pm 1$ contribute:
>
> $$
> E_n^2 = q^2\mathcal E^2\,\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\Bigl[\frac{n+1}{-\hbar\omega} + \frac{n}{\hbar\omega}\Bigr] = -\frac{q^2\mathcal E^2}{2m\omega^2},
> $$
>
> the same for every level. By Theorem §B8.1.2 the first-order state is
>
> $$
> \psi_n^1 = \frac{q\mathcal E}{\hbar\omega}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\,\bigl(\sqrt{n+1}\,\psi_{n+1}^0 - \sqrt n\,\psi_{n-1}^0\bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Exact check.* Completing the square, $\tfrac12 m\omega^2x^2 - q\mathcal E x = \tfrac12 m\omega^2(x - x_0)^2 - q^2\mathcal E^2/2m\omega^2$ with $x_0 = q\mathcal E/m\omega^2$: the exact states are the oscillator states displaced to $x_0$ and every level is lowered by exactly $q^2\mathcal E^2/2m\omega^2$. So second order is exact and all higher corrections vanish. The displaced state $\psi_n^0(x - x_0) \approx \psi_n^0 - x_0\,d\psi_n^0/dx$ reproduces $\psi_n^1$, since $d/dx = -\sqrt{m\omega/2\hbar}\,(\hat a_+ - \hat a_-)$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 7.6*

^ex-b8-1-2

> [!example]- ★ Example §B8.1.3: The Two-Level System and the Radius of Convergence
> Take two states with $H^0 = \mathrm{diag}(E_a^0, E_b^0)$, $\Delta = E_b^0 - E_a^0 > 0$, and a purely off-diagonal perturbation, $H'_{aa} = H'_{bb} = 0$, $H'_{ab} = V$. The exact eigenvalues of the $2\times2$ matrix are
>
> $$
> E_\pm = \frac{E_a^0 + E_b^0}{2} \pm \frac12\sqrt{\Delta^2 + 4|V|^2} .
> $$
>
> Expanding the square root, $E_- = E_a^0 - |V|^2/\Delta + |V|^4/\Delta^3 - \cdots$ and $E_+ = E_b^0 + |V|^2/\Delta - \cdots$: first order vanishes and the second-order terms are those of Remark: Second-order energies, the two levels repelling each other. The binomial series $\sqrt{1 + u}$, $u = 4|V|^2/\Delta^2$, converges only for $u < 1$, that is
>
> $$
> \Bigl|\frac{V}{\Delta}\Bigr| < \frac12 .
> $$
>
> Beyond this the perturbation series diverges, however many terms are kept (figure below), although the exact energies are perfectly smooth. This is Griffiths' moral: perturbation theory needs the matrix elements of $H'$ to be small compared with the level spacings.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 7.4*
