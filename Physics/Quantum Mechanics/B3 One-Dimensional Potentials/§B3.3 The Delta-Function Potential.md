The delta-function potential is a well (or barrier) of zero width and finite area. It is artificial, but it has both a bound state and scattering states and is solvable in a few lines, which makes it the cleanest place to see what a potential does to $\psi$: at the delta function the slope jumps, by an amount proportional to $\psi$ there. Level A met scattering through the step and the barrier, with reflection and transmission defined as fractions of a beam's flux ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^def-a4-6-2|Def. §A4.6.2]]). This section makes that definition precise with the probability current of [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]], proves $R + T = 1$ for every real potential, and computes $R$ and $T$ for the delta function. The delta well is also the zero-width limit of the finite well ([[§B3.2 Square Wells Revisited#^thm-b3-2-5|Theorem §B3.2.5]]).

## The potential and its bound state

> [!model] Model §B3.3.1: Delta-Function Well and Barrier
> A particle of mass $m$ in the potential (Griffiths eq. (2.117))
>
> $$
> V(x) = -\alpha\,\delta(x) ,
> $$
>
> with $\delta$ the Dirac delta function ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|WO Def. §B4.4.2]]; Griffiths eqs. (2.114)–(2.116)): $\int f(x)\,\delta(x - a)\,dx = f(a)$ for continuous $f$. For $\alpha > 0$ this is a **delta-function well**, for $\alpha < 0$ a **barrier**; $\alpha$ has units of energy times length, and $V = 0$ for $x \ne 0$. It idealizes a well or barrier much narrower than every wavelength in the problem, specified only by its area $\int V\,dx = -\alpha$ (the limit of [[§B3.2 Square Wells Revisited#^thm-b3-2-5|Theorem §B3.2.5]] (3)). Since $V \to 0$ at $\pm\infty$, states with $E < 0$ are bound and states with $E > 0$ scattering states ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^rem-b3-1-5|Remark: Bound and scattering states]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.2, eqs. (2.114)–(2.118) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 13, §1, eq. (1.1) · the user's Potential solutions notes, §6.1 · PHY 308, HW 3, Problem 1*

^mod-b3-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: The sign of α
> Griffiths and Zwiebach write the well as $-\alpha\,\delta(x)$ with $\alpha > 0$. The user's Potential solutions notes (§6) and Classical notes (§13.6) write $V = \alpha\,\delta(x)$, a barrier for $\alpha > 0$ and a well for $\alpha < 0$, and PHY 308 (HW 3, Problem 1) writes the barrier $V_0\,\delta(x)$. Formulas carry over with $\alpha \to -\alpha$ (or $\alpha \to -V_0$); quantities that depend only on $\alpha^2$, such as $R$ and $T$, do not change.

^cau-b3-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.2: Derivative Jump at a Delta Function
> A solution of the time-independent Schrödinger equation with $V(x) = -\alpha\,\delta(x)$ is continuous at $x = 0$, and its slope jumps by (Griffiths eq. (2.128))
>
> $$
> \Delta\psi'(0) = \psi'(0^+) - \psi'(0^-) = -\frac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2}\,\psi(0) .
> $$
>
> More generally, a term $g\,\delta(x - x_0)$ in the potential produces $\Delta\psi'(x_0) = (2mg/\hbar^2)\,\psi(x_0)$. A well ($g < 0$) bends $\psi$ toward the axis at $x_0$, a barrier away from it; at a node of $\psi$ the delta function has no effect.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.2, eqs. (2.126)–(2.128) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 13, §1, eqs. (1.11)–(1.14) · the user's Potential solutions notes, §6.2 · the user's Classical notes, §13.6*

^thm-b3-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Continuity of $\psi$ is [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]] (1). Its item (2) gives $\Delta\psi'(x_0) = \tfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}\lim_{\epsilon\to0}\int_{x_0 - \epsilon}^{x_0 + \epsilon}V\psi\,dx$, and with $V = g\,\delta(x - x_0)$ plus a bounded part the integral is $g\,\psi(x_0)$ for every $\epsilon > 0$ by the sifting property ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|WO Theorem §B4.4.3]]), the bounded part contributing nothing in the limit. For the well, $g = -\alpha$. If $\psi(x_0) > 0$ and $g < 0$ the slope decreases across $x_0$: a peak, bent toward the axis.

^der-b3-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|WO Theorem §B4.4.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.3: The Bound State of the Delta-Function Well
> For every $\alpha > 0$ the delta-function well has exactly one bound state (Griffiths eq. (2.132)):
>
> $$
> \psi(x) = \frac{\sqrt{m\alpha}}{\hbar}\,e^{-m\alpha|x|/\hbar^2}, \qquad E = -\frac{m\alpha^2}{2\hbar^2} .
> $$
>
> It is even, decays with $\kappa = m\alpha/\hbar^2$, and has a kink at the origin. The delta-function barrier ($\alpha < 0$) has no bound state.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.2, eqs. (2.119)–(2.132) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 13, §1, eqs. (1.2)–(1.17) · the user's Potential solutions notes, §6.3 · the user's Classical notes, §13.6*

^thm-b3-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $x \ne 0$ and $E < 0$ the equation is $\psi'' = \kappa^2\psi$ with $\kappa = \sqrt{-2mE}/\hbar > 0$ (Griffiths eqs. (2.119)–(2.120)); normalizability leaves $Be^{\kappa x}$ for $x < 0$ and $Fe^{-\kappa x}$ for $x > 0$, and continuity at $0$ gives $F = B$ (2.125). The potential is even, so a bound state is even or odd ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]]); the solution just found is even. An odd bound state would have $\psi(0) = 0$, so by [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]] the delta function would not act on it, and it would be an odd solution of the free equation, a multiple of $\sinh\kappa x$, which is not normalizable (Zwiebach's argument); so there is no second state. The jump condition fixes $\kappa$: $\psi'(0^\pm) = \mp\kappa B$, so $\Delta\psi' = -2\kappa B = -\tfrac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2}B$, that is
>
> $$
> \kappa = \frac{m\alpha}{\hbar^2}, \qquad E = -\frac{\hbar^2\kappa^2}{2m} = -\frac{m\alpha^2}{2\hbar^2}
> $$
>
> (eqs. (2.129)–(2.130)). Normalizing, $2|B|^2\int_0^\infty e^{-2\kappa x}dx = |B|^2/\kappa = 1$, so $B = \sqrt\kappa = \sqrt{m\alpha}/\hbar$ (2.131). For $\alpha < 0$ the jump condition would require $\kappa = m\alpha/\hbar^2 < 0$, a growing solution; equivalently $V \ge 0$ everywhere, so $E > V_{\min} = 0$ ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]) and there is nothing with $E < 0$.

> [!remark] Remark: Why the answer had to look like this
> Zwiebach's dimensional argument predicts the result before any calculation. The only constants are $m$, $\hbar$ and $\alpha$ (energy × length). The length they form is $\hbar^2/m\alpha$, and the energy $m\alpha^2/\hbar^2$, so a bound state, if there is one, must have $E = -c\,m\alpha^2/\hbar^2$ with a pure number $c$ (the calculation gives $c = \tfrac12$) and a size of order $\hbar^2/m\alpha$. A stronger well binds more deeply and more tightly. The single bound state is the weak-binding behaviour of [[§B3.2 Square Wells Revisited#^thm-b3-2-5|Theorem §B3.2.5]]: the delta well is "weak" in the sense $z_0 \to 0$ although it is infinitely deep, so it never acquires a second state.
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 13, §1, eqs. (1.2)–(1.5) · Griffiths Problem 2.31*

^rem-b3-3-1

## Reflection and transmission

> [!definition] Definition §B3.3.1: Reflection and Transmission Coefficients
> Let $V(x)$ tend to constants $V_L$ as $x \to -\infty$ and $V_R$ as $x \to +\infty$, and let $E > V_L$. A **scattering state incident from the left** is a stationary state of the form
>
> $$
> \psi(x) \to \begin{cases} A\,e^{ik_Lx} + B\,e^{-ik_Lx}, & x \to -\infty,\\ F\,e^{ik_Rx}, & x \to +\infty \ (E > V_R),\end{cases} \qquad k_{L,R} = \frac{\sqrt{2m(E - V_{L,R})}}{\hbar} ,
> $$
>
> with no wave coming in from the right; if $E < V_R$ the right-hand part decays instead. The terms are the **incident**, **reflected** and **transmitted waves**, and their probability currents ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]]) are
>
> $$
> J_{\text{inc}} = \frac{\hbar k_L}{m}|A|^2, \qquad J_{\text{ref}} = \frac{\hbar k_L}{m}|B|^2, \qquad J_{\text{tr}} = \frac{\hbar k_R}{m}|F|^2 \quad (J_{\text{tr}} = 0 \text{ if } E < V_R) .
> $$
>
> The **reflection** and **transmission coefficients** are the ratios of these currents:
>
> $$
> R = \frac{J_{\text{ref}}}{J_{\text{inc}}} = \frac{|B|^2}{|A|^2}, \qquad T = \frac{J_{\text{tr}}}{J_{\text{inc}}} = \frac{k_R}{k_L}\,\frac{|F|^2}{|A|^2} .
> $$
>
> When $V_L = V_R$, $T = |F|^2/|A|^2$. For a particle described by a wave packet whose energies lie close to $E$, $R$ and $T$ are the probabilities of reflection and transmission.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.2, eqs. (2.141)–(2.142); Problem 2.34(c), eq. (2.175) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 16, §2, eqs. (2.9)–(2.15) · the user's Potential solutions notes, §1.2 (Step 4) · the user's Classical notes, §14 · Greensite ch. 8, §8.2*

^def-b3-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from level A
> Level A defined $R$ and $T$ as fractions of a beam's flux, the number density $|A|^2$ times the speed $\hbar k/m$ ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^def-a4-6-2|Def. §A4.6.2]], [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-1|Remark: Working with plane waves anyway]]). Here the flux is the probability current of the single-particle wave function, which for a plane wave is exactly density times velocity ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^rem-b1-1-2|Remark: Reading the current]]). Nothing changes numerically; what is gained is a definition that applies to any $\psi$, so that $R + T = 1$ can be proved instead of assumed, and that explains the factor $k_R/k_L$: the transmitted wave moves at a different speed. The scattering states themselves are not normalizable ([[§19 The Function Spaces Lᵖ(Ω)#^def-19-1|556 Def. §19.1]]); a real particle is a packet of them ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets]]), and since such a packet always contains a range of energies, $R$ and $T$ are the reflection and transmission probabilities for energies near $E$ (Griffiths). That a time-dependent process, a particle coming in and leaving, can be read off a stationary state is the limit of long packets with sharp momentum (Greensite §8.2), in which incident and reflected waves overlap over a long stretch.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.2 (pp. 89–90) · Greensite ch. 8, §8.2*

^rem-b3-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.4: Conservation of Current and R + T = 1
> In every stationary state of a real potential the probability current $J$ is independent of $x$, also across finite jumps and delta functions of $V$. For a scattering state incident from the left ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]]), the current on the left is $J = \tfrac{\hbar k_L}{m}\bigl(|A|^2 - |B|^2\bigr) = J_{\text{inc}} - J_{\text{ref}}$ (incident and reflected waves do not interfere in $J$), on the right it is $J_{\text{tr}}$, and
>
> $$
> R + T = 1 .
> $$
>
> If $E < V_R$, then $T = 0$ and $R = 1$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths eq. (2.143); Problem 2.34(c)–(d) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 16, §2, eqs. (2.10)–(2.16); Lecture 17, eqs. (1.5)–(1.6) · the user's Potential solutions notes, §1.2, §4 (Step 3) · the user's Classical notes, §14 · Greensite ch. 8, eq. (8.81)*

^thm-b3-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Constancy.* $V$ and $E$ are real, so $\psi^{\ast}$ solves the same equation with the same energy, and by [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]]
>
> $$
> J = \frac{\hbar}{2mi}\bigl(\psi^*\psi' - \psi\,\psi^{*\prime}\bigr) = \frac{\hbar}{2mi}\,W(\psi^*, \psi),
> $$
>
> a multiple of the Wronskian of two solutions with equal energy, constant on the whole line by [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]], including across jumps and delta functions. (Equivalently: for a stationary state $\rho = |\psi|^2$ does not depend on $t$, so the continuity equation $\partial\rho/\partial t + \partial J/\partial x = 0$ of [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]] gives $\partial J/\partial x = 0$, Zwiebach's argument.)
>
> *The current on the left.* With $\psi = Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx}$ ($k = k_L$),
>
> $$
> \psi^*\psi' = ik\bigl(A^*e^{-ikx} + B^*e^{ikx}\bigr)\bigl(Ae^{ikx} - Be^{-ikx}\bigr) = ik\bigl(|A|^2 - |B|^2\bigr) + ik\bigl(z^* - z\bigr), \qquad z = A^*Be^{-2ikx} .
> $$
>
> Since $z^* - z = -2i\operatorname{Im}z$, the cross term $ik(z^* - z) = 2k\operatorname{Im}z$ is real and drops out of $J = \tfrac\hbar m\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\psi')$, leaving $J = \tfrac{\hbar k}{m}(|A|^2 - |B|^2)$. On the right, $\psi = Fe^{ik_Rx}$ gives $J = \hbar k_R|F|^2/m$, and a real decaying exponential (times a constant) gives $J = 0$ ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]).
>
> *Conclusion.* Equating the constant current on both sides, $J_{\text{inc}} - J_{\text{ref}} = J_{\text{tr}}$; dividing by $J_{\text{inc}}$ gives $1 - R = T$. If $E < V_R$, $J_{\text{tr}} = 0$ and $R = 1$.

*Uses:* [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]], [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]], [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]], [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.5: Scattering by a Delta Function
> For the delta-function well $V = -\alpha\,\delta(x)$ and a particle of energy $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m > 0$ incident from the left, put $\beta = m\alpha/\hbar^2k$ (Griffiths eq. (2.138)). Then
>
> $$
> B = \frac{i\beta}{1 - i\beta}\,A, \qquad F = \frac{1}{1 - i\beta}\,A ,
> $$
>
> $$
> R = \frac{\beta^2}{1 + \beta^2} = \frac{1}{1 + 2\hbar^2E/m\alpha^2}, \qquad T = \frac{1}{1 + \beta^2} = \frac{1}{1 + m\alpha^2/2\hbar^2E}
> $$
>
> (Griffiths eqs. (2.140)–(2.144)). For the barrier, $\alpha \to -\alpha$: the amplitudes become $B = -i\beta A/(1 + i\beta)$, $F = A/(1 + i\beta)$, and $R$, $T$ are unchanged. In particular, for the barrier $V_0\,\delta(x)$ of PHY 308,
>
> $$
> R = \frac{1}{1 + 2\hbar^2E/mV_0^2}, \qquad T = \frac{1}{1 + mV_0^2/2\hbar^2E} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.2, eqs. (2.133)–(2.144) · PHY 308, HW 3, Problem 1 · the user's Potential solutions notes, §6.4*

^thm-b3-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $x \ne 0$, $\psi'' = -k^2\psi$, so $\psi = Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx}$ for $x < 0$ and $\psi = Fe^{ikx} + Ge^{-ikx}$ for $x > 0$ (Griffiths eqs. (2.134)–(2.135)); incidence from the left means $G = 0$ (2.139). Continuity at $0$: $F + G = A + B$ (2.136). The jump ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]]): $\psi'(0^+) - \psi'(0^-) = ik(F - G - A + B) = -\tfrac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2}(A + B)$, or, dividing by $ik$ and using $\tfrac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2}\tfrac{1}{ik} = -2i\beta$,
>
> $$
> F - G = A(1 + 2i\beta) - B(1 - 2i\beta) \qquad\text{(2.138)} .
> $$
>
> With $G = 0$ and $F = A + B$: $A + B = A(1 + 2i\beta) - B(1 - 2i\beta)$, so $2B(1 - i\beta) = 2i\beta A$ and $B = \tfrac{i\beta}{1 - i\beta}A$; then $F = A + B = \tfrac{A}{1 - i\beta}$. Since $|1 - i\beta|^2 = 1 + \beta^2$ and $k_L = k_R$, [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]] gives $R = \beta^2/(1 + \beta^2)$ and $T = 1/(1 + \beta^2)$; their sum is $1$, as [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-4|Theorem §B3.3.4]] requires. Finally $\beta^2 = m^2\alpha^2/\hbar^4k^2 = m\alpha^2/2\hbar^2E$ (using $k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2$), which gives the forms in $E$. Replacing $\alpha$ by $-\alpha$ replaces $\beta$ by $-\beta$, which leaves $\beta^2$ unchanged; for $V_0\,\delta(x)$ put $\alpha = -V_0$.

> [!caution] Caution: The reflection amplitude in the user's Potential solutions notes
> The user's Potential solutions notes (§6.4), for $V = \alpha\,\delta(x)$ with the jump $\psi'(0^+) - \psi'(0^-) = (2m\alpha/\hbar^2)\psi(0)$ of their §6.2, write $r = -\beta/(1 - \beta)$ and $t = 1/(1 - \beta)$ with $\beta = m\alpha/i\hbar^2k$; with that jump condition one finds $r = \beta/(1 - \beta)$, so the minus sign in $r$ should be absent, while $t$ is right (continuity requires $1 + r = t$, which holds only with the plus sign). Their $\alpha$ is Griffiths' $-\alpha$, so their $\beta$ equals Griffiths' $i\beta$, and the corrected amplitudes $r = i\beta/(1 - i\beta)$, $t = 1/(1 - i\beta)$ are those of [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]; $R = |r|^2$ and $T = |t|^2$ are unaffected by the sign.

^cau-b3-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the result
> - **One energy scale.** $T = 1/(1 + |E_b|/E)$ and $R = 1/(1 + E/|E_b|)$, where $|E_b| = m\alpha^2/2\hbar^2$ is the binding energy of the well's bound state: slow particles ($E \ll |E_b|$) are almost all reflected, fast ones ($E \gg |E_b|$) almost all transmitted, and $R = T = \tfrac12$ at $E = |E_b|$. The higher the energy, the more likely the transmission (Griffiths).
> - **Tunnelling through an infinite barrier.** The barrier is infinitely high at one point, yet transmits exactly as well as the well of the same strength; classically it would reflect every particle, and a classical particle would pass over the well with certainty. Quantum scattering is richer: transmission when classically forbidden, reflection when classically allowed (Griffiths).
> - **Phases.** Unlike the probabilities, the amplitudes distinguish well from barrier: $B/A = i\beta/(1 - i\beta)$ for the well and $-i\beta/(1 + i\beta)$ for the barrier, complex conjugates of each other. The phases matter as soon as two scatterers act together ([[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.2 (pp. 89–90), Figure 2.15*

^rem-b3-3-3

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The bound state as a pole of the transmission amplitude
> Write the transmission amplitude of the well as a function of $k$: $t(k) = F/A = 1/(1 - i\beta) = k/(k - i\,m\alpha/\hbar^2)$. Continued to complex $k$, it has a single pole, at $k = i\kappa$ with $\kappa = m\alpha/\hbar^2$, and $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m = -\hbar^2\kappa^2/2m = -m\alpha^2/2\hbar^2$ is exactly the bound-state energy of [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]]. At the pole there is an outgoing wave with no incoming wave, and $e^{ikx} = e^{-\kappa x}$ decays: the bound state is a "scattering state" without incident wave. For the barrier the pole moves to $k = -i\kappa$, where $e^{ikx}$ grows, and there is no bound state. Zwiebach develops this into the general picture: in the complex $k$ plane bound states are poles on the positive imaginary axis and resonances are poles just below the real axis ([[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^rem-b3-4-7|★ Remark: Phase shifts, Levinson's theorem and resonances]]).
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 19, §3 (bound states and resonances as poles); the delta-function case worked out here from Griffiths eq. (2.140)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.3.1: Uncertainty in the Delta-Well Bound State
> Check the uncertainty principle for the bound state $\psi = \sqrt\kappa\,e^{-\kappa|x|}$, $\kappa = m\alpha/\hbar^2$.
>
> $\psi$ is even and real, so $\langle x\rangle = 0$ and $\langle p\rangle = 0$ ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]]). Next,
>
> $$
> \langle x^2\rangle = 2\kappa\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-2\kappa x}\,dx = 2\kappa\cdot\frac{2}{(2\kappa)^3} = \frac{1}{2\kappa^2} = \frac{\hbar^4}{2m^2\alpha^2} .
> $$
>
> For $\langle p^2\rangle = -\hbar^2\int\psi\psi''\,dx$ the kink matters. With $\tfrac{d}{dx}|x| = 2\theta(x) - 1$ and $\theta' = \delta$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|WO Theorem §B4.4.3]]; Griffiths Problem 2.23(b)),
>
> $$
> \psi' = -\kappa\,[2\theta(x) - 1]\,\psi, \qquad \psi'' = \kappa^2\psi - 2\kappa\,\delta(x)\,\psi(0) ,
> $$
>
> since $[2\theta - 1]^2 = 1$; this is the jump of [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]] in distributional form. Hence
>
> $$
> \langle p^2\rangle = -\hbar^2\bigl[\kappa^2 - 2\kappa\,\psi(0)^2\bigr] = -\hbar^2\bigl[\kappa^2 - 2\kappa^2\bigr] = \hbar^2\kappa^2 = \Bigl(\frac{m\alpha}{\hbar}\Bigr)^2 ,
> $$
>
> Griffiths' partial answer; integrating by parts first, $\hbar^2\int|\psi'|^2dx = \hbar^2\kappa^2$, gives the same. So $\sigma_x = 1/\sqrt2\kappa$, $\sigma_p = \hbar\kappa$ and
>
> $$
> \sigma_x\sigma_p = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt2} \approx 0.71\,\hbar > \frac\hbar2 .
> $$
>
> Dropping the delta-function term would have given $\langle p^2\rangle = -\hbar^2\kappa^2 < 0$, an impossible answer: away from the origin $\psi$ curves away from the axis, as in any forbidden region, and the whole positive kinetic energy is supplied by the kink. As a check, $\langle p^2\rangle/2m = \hbar^2\kappa^2/2m = |E|$ and $\langle V\rangle = -\alpha|\psi(0)|^2 = -\alpha\kappa = 2E$, which add up to $E$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.24 (solved here)*

> [!example]- ★ Example §B3.3.2: Two Delta-Function Wells
> Find the bound states of $V(x) = -\alpha\bigl[\delta(x + a) + \delta(x - a)\bigr]$ with $\alpha, a > 0$, in units of the single-well values $\kappa_0 = m\alpha/\hbar^2$ and $E_0 = -m\alpha^2/2\hbar^2$.
>
> For $E = -\hbar^2\kappa^2/2m < 0$ the potential is even, so the bound states are even or odd ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]]): $\cosh\kappa x$ or $\sinh\kappa x$ between the wells, joined continuously to $e^{-\kappa(|x| - a)}$ tails. The jump at $x = a$ ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]]) gives, for the even state, $-\kappa\cosh\kappa a - \kappa\sinh\kappa a = -2\kappa_0\cosh\kappa a$, and similarly for the odd one:
>
> $$
> \text{even: } \kappa\,(1 + \tanh\kappa a) = 2\kappa_0, \qquad \text{odd: } \kappa\,(1 + \coth\kappa a) = 2\kappa_0 .
> $$
>
> The left sides increase with $\kappa$; the even one starts at $0$, so there is always exactly one even state, while the odd one starts at $1/a$, so an odd state exists only if $2\kappa_0 > 1/a$, that is $\alpha > \hbar^2/2ma$. For Griffiths' two cases, solving numerically:
> - $\alpha = \hbar^2/ma$ ($\kappa_0a = 1$): two states, $\kappa a = 1.109$ and $0.797$, $E = -1.23\,\hbar^2/2ma^2$ (even) and $-0.635\,\hbar^2/2ma^2$ (odd), i.e. $1.23\,E_0$ and $0.635\,E_0$;
> - $\alpha = \hbar^2/4ma$ ($\kappa_0a = \tfrac14$): one state, $\kappa a = 0.369$, $E = -0.137\,\hbar^2/2ma^2 = 2.18\,E_0$.
>
> *Limits.* As $a \to 0$, $\tanh\kappa a \to 0$ and $\kappa \to 2\kappa_0$: one well of strength $2\alpha$, $E \to 4E_0$. As $a \to \infty$, $\tanh$ and $\coth \to 1$ and both states tend to $E_0$: two separate wells with the same level, a degeneracy that any finite separation lifts ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^cau-b3-1-1|Caution: Where degeneracy does occur]]). Expanding $\tanh y \approx 1 - 2e^{-2y}$ and $\coth y \approx 1 + 2e^{-2y}$ gives $\kappa \approx \kappa_0(1 \pm e^{-2\kappa_0a})$ and a splitting
>
> $$
> E_{\text{odd}} - E_{\text{even}} \approx 4|E_0|\,e^{-2\kappa_0a} ,
> $$
>
> exponentially small in the separation $2a$ measured in units of the decay length $1/\kappa_0$: it comes from the overlap of the tails, that is, from tunnelling between the wells. The even (nodeless) combination lies lower. This is the mechanism of the two-well hopping of [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^ex-a4-3-2|Example §A4.3.2]] and, in three dimensions, of the bonding and antibonding states of $\mathrm H_2^+$ ([[§B9.2★ Helium and the Hydrogen Molecule#^mod-b9-2-3|Model §B9.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.27 (solved here; the splitting formula worked out here)*
