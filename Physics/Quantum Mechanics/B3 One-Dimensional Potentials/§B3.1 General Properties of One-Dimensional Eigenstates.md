What can be said about the stationary states of a particle on a line before the potential is even specified? Level A solved one potential at a time and noticed patterns along the way: the solutions obey matching conditions ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]]), every well quantizes and its $n$th state has $n - 1$ nodes ([[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^rem-a4-4-4|Remark: Any well quantizes]]), the states of a symmetric well are even or odd, and the finite-well solution simply assumed that two bound states of equal energy are proportional ([[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^der-a4-4-2|derivation of Theorem §A4.4.2]]). This section proves these patterns for an arbitrary real potential $V(x)$: the matching conditions, the bound $E > V_{\min}$, the absence of degeneracy, the reality of the eigenfunctions, parity, and the node theorem. One tool does most of the work, the Wronskian of two solutions.

*Setting.* The potential energy is written $V$, not $U$ ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^cau-b1-1-1|Caution: V, not U]]). Stationary states $\Psi = \psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]], [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates|§B1.3]]) solve the time-independent Schrödinger equation (Griffiths eq. (2.5))

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\psi'' + V(x)\,\psi = E\,\psi, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \psi'' = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\,\bigl[V(x) - E\bigr]\,\psi .
$$

$V$ is real. Following Zwiebach, it is allowed to be piecewise continuous with finite jumps, to contain delta functions $g\,\delta(x - x_0)$, and to be $+\infty$ beyond a point (a **hard wall**); derivatives of delta functions are excluded. $E$ is real ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]).

## Matching conditions and the Wronskian

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.1: Matching Conditions
> Let $\psi$ solve the time-independent Schrödinger equation for a potential of the kind above.
> 1. $\psi$ is continuous everywhere.
> 2. At every point $x_0$,
>
> $$
> \Delta\psi'(x_0) \equiv \lim_{\epsilon \to 0}\bigl[\psi'(x_0 + \epsilon) - \psi'(x_0 - \epsilon)\bigr] = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\,\lim_{\epsilon \to 0}\int_{x_0 - \epsilon}^{x_0 + \epsilon} V(x)\,\psi(x)\,dx .
> $$
>
> 3. Hence $\psi'$ is continuous wherever $V$ is finite, also where $V$ jumps by a finite amount; at a delta function $g\,\delta(x - x_0)$ it jumps by $\Delta\psi' = (2mg/\hbar^2)\,\psi(x_0)$ ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]]).
> 4. At a hard wall, $\psi = 0$ in the region where $V = \infty$ and $\psi = 0$ at the wall; $\psi'$ jumps there from a finite value to $0$.
> 5. If $\psi(x_0) = \psi'(x_0) = 0$ at a point where $V$ is finite, then $\psi \equiv 0$. In particular a nonzero solution never touches the axis with zero slope: every zero of $\psi$ is a crossing, a **node**, or a hard wall.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.5.2, eqs. (2.124), (2.126)–(2.127) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 10, §2, eqs. (2.23)–(2.31); Lecture 11, §1; Lecture 12, §3*

^thm-b3-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *2 and 3.* Integrate the equation from $x_0 - \epsilon$ to $x_0 + \epsilon$ (Griffiths eq. (2.126)):
>
> $$
> -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\bigl[\psi'(x_0 + \epsilon) - \psi'(x_0 - \epsilon)\bigr] + \int_{x_0 - \epsilon}^{x_0 + \epsilon} V\psi\,dx = E\int_{x_0 - \epsilon}^{x_0 + \epsilon}\psi\,dx ,
> $$
>
> using the fundamental theorem of calculus for the first term. $\psi$ is bounded, so the right side is at most $2\epsilon\,|E|\sup|\psi| \to 0$; this gives item 2. If $V$ is bounded near $x_0$, even with a jump, the same estimate kills the potential term, so $\Delta\psi' = 0$. A term $g\,\delta(x - x_0)$ in $V$ contributes $g\,\psi(x_0)$ to the integral for every $\epsilon$.
>
> *1.* If $\psi$ jumped at $x_0$, then $\psi'$ would contain a term proportional to $\delta(x - x_0)$ and $\psi''$ one proportional to $\delta'(x - x_0)$. The equation would then require $(V - E)\psi$ to contain $\delta'$, which a potential with at most delta functions times a function with at most a jump cannot supply (Zwiebach's argument).
>
> *4.* In the region $V = V_0 > E$ beyond a step, the decaying solution is $e^{-\kappa x}$ with $\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}/\hbar$; as $V_0 \to \infty$ it vanishes beyond the step and, by continuity, at it. The limit is carried out for the finite well in [[§B3.2 Square Wells Revisited#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]]; demanding continuity of $\psi'$ as well would force $\psi \equiv 0$ by item 5, so $\psi'$ must jump.
>
> *5.* $\psi'' = \tfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}(V - E)\psi$ is a linear second-order equation; on an interval where $V$ is continuous its solution is fixed uniquely by $\psi(x_0)$ and $\psi'(x_0)$ (uniqueness theorem for linear ordinary differential equations, Mathematical Methods (planned)). The zero function has these data, so it is the solution. Zwiebach gives the quick version for analytic $V$: differentiating the equation shows that all derivatives of $\psi$ vanish at $x_0$, so the Taylor series is zero. Across a finite jump or a delta function, items 1–3 fix $\psi$ and $\psi'$ on the far side, so uniqueness propagates along the whole line.

> [!remark] Remark: From conditions to consequences
> Level A listed continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$ among the conditions a wave function must meet ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]]), with the infinite wall as an exception. At level B only the continuity of $\psi$ is a requirement, and even that is forced by the equation once $V$ has no $\delta'$; the behaviour of $\psi'$ is derived, including the exact size of its jump at a delta function or a wall. Item 5 is used again and again below: it is the reason a nonzero solution cannot "kiss" the axis, and the reason two solutions with the same value and slope at one point coincide everywhere.

^rem-b3-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B3.1.1: Wronskian
> The **Wronskian** of two solutions $\psi_1$, $\psi_2$ of the time-independent Schrödinger equation (for the same $V$, possibly different energies) is
>
> $$
> W(\psi_1, \psi_2)(x) = \psi_1(x)\,\psi_2'(x) - \psi_1'(x)\,\psi_2(x) .
> $$
>
> It is antisymmetric, $W(\psi_2, \psi_1) = -W(\psi_1, \psi_2)$, and linear in each argument.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 2.44–2.45 (the combination, unnamed) · PHY 308, HW 2, Problem 3*

^def-b3-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.2: The Wronskian Identity
> Let $\psi_1$, $\psi_2$ solve the time-independent Schrödinger equation for the same real potential with energies $E_1$, $E_2$. Then, away from delta functions and walls,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dx}\bigl(\psi_1\psi_2' - \psi_1'\psi_2\bigr) = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\,(E_1 - E_2)\,\psi_1\psi_2 ,
> $$
>
> and $W(\psi_1, \psi_2)$ is continuous across finite jumps of $V$, across delta functions and at hard walls. Consequently:
> 1. if $E_1 = E_2$, the Wronskian is constant on the whole line;
> 2. if $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ are bound states ($\psi_j, \psi_j' \to 0$ as $|x| \to \infty$) with $E_1 \ne E_2$, then $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_1\psi_2\,dx = 0$.
>
> In the notation of PHY 308 ($\phi_n = \psi_1$, $\phi_m = \psi_2$) the identity reads $\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl(\dfrac{d\phi_m}{dx}\phi_n - \phi_m\dfrac{d\phi_n}{dx}\Bigr) = \dfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E_n - E_m)\,\phi_n\phi_m$.
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 2, Problem 3 · Griffiths Problem 2.45(a), Problem 2.44 (hint)*

^thm-b3-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate: the terms $\psi_1'\psi_2'$ cancel, leaving $W' = \psi_1\psi_2'' - \psi_1''\psi_2$. Insert $\psi_j'' = \tfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}(V - E_j)\psi_j$:
>
> $$
> W' = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\,\psi_1\psi_2\,\bigl[(V - E_2) - (V - E_1)\bigr] = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\,(E_1 - E_2)\,\psi_1\psi_2 ;
> $$
>
> the potential drops out. At a finite jump of $V$ both $\psi_j$ and $\psi_j'$ are continuous ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]), so $W$ is. At a delta function $g\,\delta(x - x_0)$, $\psi_j'$ jumps by $c\,\psi_j(x_0)$ with the same $c = 2mg/\hbar^2$ for both, so $W$ jumps by $\psi_1(x_0)\,c\,\psi_2(x_0) - c\,\psi_1(x_0)\,\psi_2(x_0) = 0$. At a hard wall both $\psi_j$ vanish, so $W = 0$ on both sides.
>
> *1.* $W' = 0$ on each interval, and $W$ is continuous across the special points, so $W$ is one constant ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]).
>
> *2.* Integrate the identity over the line: $\tfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E_1 - E_2)\int\psi_1\psi_2\,dx = W(\infty) - W(-\infty) = 0$, because $W$ is built from $\psi_j$, $\psi_j'$, which vanish at $\pm\infty$; divide by $E_1 - E_2 \ne 0$. (For complex $\psi_1$ apply this to $\psi_1^{\ast}$, which solves the equation with the same real $E_1$, to get $\int\psi_1^{\ast}\psi_2\,dx = 0$.)

## Energy, degeneracy, reality and parity

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.3: The Energy Exceeds the Minimum of the Potential
> Every normalizable solution of the time-independent Schrödinger equation has
>
> $$
> E > V_{\min} = \inf_x V(x) .
> $$
>
> The inequality is strict: a quantum particle cannot have the energy of a classical particle at rest at the bottom of the well.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.2 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 12, §1 (fact 1); Lecture 10, §3 (the energy integral)*

^thm-b3-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $\psi$ be normalized, with $\psi^*\psi' \to 0$ at the ends of its domain ($\pm\infty$ for a bound state; at a hard wall $\psi = 0$). Multiply the equation by $\psi^*$, integrate, and integrate the kinetic term by parts ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]): the boundary term $-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}[\psi^*\psi']$ vanishes, and
>
> $$
> E = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int|\psi'|^2\,dx + \int V\,|\psi|^2\,dx \;\ge\; \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int|\psi'|^2\,dx + V_{\min} .
> $$
>
> (A delta function in $V$ contributes $g|\psi(x_0)|^2$ to the second integral, and $V_{\min} = -\infty$ if $g < 0$, so the claim is empty there.) Equality would need $\int|\psi'|^2dx = 0$, so $\psi$ constant; a nonzero constant is not normalizable on the line, and it violates $\psi = 0$ at a wall. Hence $E > V_{\min}$.
>
> Griffiths' hint gives the same result pictorially: if $E < V_{\min}$, then $\psi''/\psi = \tfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}(V - E) > 0$ everywhere, so $\psi$ curves away from the axis wherever it is nonzero ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^rem-a4-1-8|Remark: Reading the shape of ψ from the curvature]]); a function that is convex where positive and concave where negative cannot go to zero at both ends without being zero.

^der-b3-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: The classical analogue
> Classically the motion is confined to $V(x) \le E$ ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-1|CM Theorem §A4.7.1]]), so $E \ge V_{\min}$, with equality for a particle at rest at the minimum. The quantum statement is the same inequality made strict: the kinetic term $\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int|\psi'|^2dx = \langle p^2\rangle/2m$ cannot vanish for a normalizable state. This is the zero-point energy that level A estimated from the uncertainty relation ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]). It also saves work: for the finite well $-V_0 < E$ is known before solving ([[§B3.2 Square Wells Revisited#^mod-b3-2-1|Model §B3.2.1]]), and the infinite well has no states with $E \le 0$ (Griffiths Problem 2.3).

^rem-b3-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.4: No Degenerate Bound States in One Dimension
> Let $V$ be defined on the whole line and not split into separate pieces by regions where $V = \infty$. If $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ are bound states with the same energy $E$ (both $\psi_j, \psi_j' \to 0$ as $|x| \to \infty$), then $\psi_2 = c\,\psi_1$ for a constant $c$. Every bound-state energy in one dimension is nondegenerate.
>
> More generally, the conclusion holds as soon as both solutions vanish, with their slopes, at *one* end of the line.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.44 and footnote 57 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 12, §1 (fact 2) · Greensite ch. 8, §8.4*

^thm-b3-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]] (1), $W(\psi_1, \psi_2)$ is a constant. At an end where both functions and their slopes go to zero, $W \to 0$; so $W \equiv 0$.
>
> Choose a point $x_0$ where $V$ is finite and $\psi_1(x_0) \ne 0$, and put $c = \psi_2(x_0)/\psi_1(x_0)$ and $\varphi = \psi_2 - c\,\psi_1$. Then $\varphi$ solves the same equation, $\varphi(x_0) = 0$, and
>
> $$
> \varphi'(x_0) = \psi_2'(x_0) - \frac{\psi_2(x_0)}{\psi_1(x_0)}\,\psi_1'(x_0) = \frac{W(\psi_1, \psi_2)(x_0)}{\psi_1(x_0)} = 0 .
> $$
>
> By [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]] (5), $\varphi \equiv 0$, that is $\psi_2 = c\,\psi_1$. The hypothesis on infinite barriers is needed for the last step: uniqueness propagates along the line only through regions where the equation holds, and an impenetrable region cuts the line into independent problems (Griffiths' footnote 57: two isolated infinite wells have degenerate states, the particle in one well or in the other).

^der-b3-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]], [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Where degeneracy does occur
> - **Scattering states.** For $E$ above $V(-\infty)$ and $V(+\infty)$, nothing vanishes at either end and each energy has two independent solutions, a particle coming in from the left and one from the right ([[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension]]); the free particle's $e^{\pm ikx}$ are the simplest pair.
> - **No infinity.** On a ring the boundary condition is periodicity, $W$ is constant but need not vanish, and the levels are doubly degenerate ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^ex-b3-1-1|Example §B3.1.1]]).
> - **Disconnected wells.** Two wells separated by an infinitely high barrier give degenerate pairs; any finite barrier, however high, couples them and splits each pair ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^ex-b3-3-2|Example §B3.3.2]]).
> - **More dimensions.** In two and three dimensions degeneracy is common, as for the cubical box ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]], [[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]).

^cau-b3-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.5: Real Eigenfunctions
> 1. If $\psi$ solves the time-independent Schrödinger equation with energy $E$, so do $\psi^*$, $\operatorname{Re}\psi$ and $\operatorname{Im}\psi$. Every energy eigenspace therefore has a basis of real functions, and the stationary states can always be taken real.
> 2. Every bound state of a one-dimensional potential is real up to a constant phase: $\psi = e^{i\theta}\,u$ with $u$ real.
>
> In a real stationary state the probability current vanishes and $\langle p\rangle = 0$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.1(b) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 12, §1, Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 · Greensite ch. 8, §8.4, eqs. (8.42)–(8.43)*

^thm-b3-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Conjugate the equation: $V$ and $E$ are real, and $(\psi'')^{\ast} = (\psi^{\ast})''$, so $\psi^{\ast}$ is a solution with the same $E$. By linearity so are $\operatorname{Re}\psi = \tfrac12(\psi + \psi^{\ast})$ and $\operatorname{Im}\psi = \tfrac{1}{2i}(\psi - \psi^{\ast})$, and $\psi = \operatorname{Re}\psi + i\operatorname{Im}\psi$.
>
> *2.* For a bound state, $\operatorname{Re}\psi$ and $\operatorname{Im}\psi$ are bound states of the same energy, so by [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]] one is a real multiple of the other (or zero). If $\operatorname{Re}\psi \ne 0$, then $\operatorname{Im}\psi = c\operatorname{Re}\psi$ with $c$ real and $\psi = (1 + ic)\operatorname{Re}\psi = \sqrt{1 + c^2}\,e^{i\theta}\operatorname{Re}\psi$ with $\tan\theta = c$; if $\operatorname{Re}\psi = 0$, $\psi = i\operatorname{Im}\psi$.
>
> The last statement: for real $\psi$, $\psi^*\psi' = \psi\psi'$ is real, so $J = (\hbar/m)\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\psi') = 0$ ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]) and $\langle p\rangle = 0$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-5|Theorem §A3.4.5]]); a constant phase changes neither.

^der-b3-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-5|Theorem §A3.4.5]]

> [!definition] Definition §B3.1.2: Node; Even and Odd States
> A **node** of a stationary state $\psi$ is a zero of $\psi$ in the interior of the region where the particle can be; zeros at hard walls, and the limits $\psi \to 0$ at $\pm\infty$, are not nodes. For a potential with $V(-x) = V(x)$, a state is **even** (of even parity) if $\psi(-x) = \psi(x)$ and **odd** (of odd parity) if $\psi(-x) = -\psi(x)$.
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 11, §1 (feature 1); Lecture 12, §1 · Greensite ch. 8, eqs. (8.50)–(8.51)*

^def-b3-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.6: Parity of Eigenstates in an Even Potential
> Let $V(-x) = V(x)$.
> 1. If $\psi(x)$ solves the time-independent Schrödinger equation with energy $E$, so does $\psi(-x)$. The eigenstates can therefore be chosen even or odd: $\psi_\pm(x) = \tfrac12\bigl[\psi(x) \pm \psi(-x)\bigr]$.
> 2. Every bound state is either even or odd.
> 3. An even solution has $\psi'(0) = 0$ and an odd one $\psi(0) = 0$ (where $V$ is finite at $0$), so even and odd states can be found by solving on $x \ge 0$ with these conditions at the origin.
>
> Scattering states need not be even or odd: a wave incident from the left is not ([[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.1(c); §2.6 (use for the finite well) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 12, §1, Theorem 2 and Corollary 2; §4 (shooting conditions (4.2)–(4.4)) · Greensite ch. 8, §8.4, eqs. (8.44)–(8.51)*

^thm-b3-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Put $\phi(x) = \psi(-x)$. By the chain rule $\phi''(x) = \psi''(-x)$, and the equation at the point $-x$ reads $\psi''(-x) = \tfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}[V(-x) - E]\psi(-x)$. With $V(-x) = V(x)$ this is $\phi'' = \tfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}[V(x) - E]\phi$. The sum and difference of two solutions with the same $E$ are solutions; one of them may vanish, but not both unless $\psi = 0$.
>
> *2.* For a bound state, $\psi(-x)$ is a bound state of the same energy, so $\psi(-x) = c\,\psi(x)$ by [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]. Replacing $x$ by $-x$ gives $\psi(x) = c\,\psi(-x) = c^2\psi(x)$, so $c^2 = 1$ and $c = \pm1$. Level A used exactly this step for the finite well ([[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^der-a4-4-2|derivation of Theorem §A4.4.2]]), quoting the nondegeneracy that Theorem §B3.1.4 now supplies.
>
> *3.* Differentiating $\psi(-x) = \pm\psi(x)$ gives $-\psi'(-x) = \pm\psi'(x)$; at $x = 0$ the even case gives $\psi'(0) = -\psi'(0) = 0$, the odd case $\psi(0) = -\psi(0) = 0$.

## The node theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.7: Node Theorem
> Number the bound states of a one-dimensional potential by increasing energy, $E_1 < E_2 < E_3 < \cdots$ (they are nondegenerate, [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]), and take them real ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]]).
> 1. *Interlacing.* If $E_m < E_n$, then between any two consecutive zeros of $\psi_m$ the function $\psi_n$ has at least one node; here $\pm\infty$ and hard walls count as zeros of $\psi_m$.
> 2. The number of nodes strictly increases with the energy, and $\psi_k$ has at least $k - 1$ nodes.
> 3. In fact $\psi_k$ has exactly $k - 1$ nodes. In particular the ground state has no node.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.45 and §2.2 (property 2, "universal") · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 13, §2 · PHY 308, HW 2, Problem 3 (the identity used)*

^thm-b3-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Let $x_1 < x_2$ be consecutive zeros of $\psi_m$; changing signs if necessary, $\psi_m > 0$ on $(x_1, x_2)$, so $\psi_m'(x_1) \ge 0$ and $\psi_m'(x_2) \le 0$. Integrate the Wronskian identity ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]) with $\psi_1 = \psi_n$, $\psi_2 = \psi_m$ from $x_1$ to $x_2$; since $\psi_m(x_1) = \psi_m(x_2) = 0$, only the $\psi_m'\psi_n$ terms survive (Griffiths Problem 2.45(b)):
>
> $$
> \psi_m'(x_2)\,\psi_n(x_2) - \psi_m'(x_1)\,\psi_n(x_1) = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\,(E_n - E_m)\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\psi_m\,\psi_n\,dx .
> $$
>
> (If $x_1 = -\infty$ or $x_2 = +\infty$ the corresponding boundary term is $0$, since $\psi_m' \to 0$ and $\psi_n$ stays bounded; at a wall it is $0$ because $\psi_n = 0$ there.) Suppose $\psi_n$ had no zero in $(x_1, x_2)$; changing its sign if necessary, $\psi_n > 0$ there and, by continuity, $\psi_n(x_1), \psi_n(x_2) \ge 0$. Then the left side is $\le 0$ while the right side is $> 0$, a contradiction. A zero of $\psi_n$ inside the interval is a node by [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]] (5).
>
> *2.* If $\psi_m$ has $N$ nodes, its zeros (with the two ends) cut the line into $N + 1$ open intervals, and $\psi_n$ has a node in each, so at least $N + 1$ nodes. By induction on $k$, $\psi_k$ has at least $k - 1$ nodes.
>
> *3.* Equality needs the opposite bound, that $\psi_k$ cannot have *too many* nodes; the source gives an argument, not a full proof (Zwiebach calls it "intuitive, non-rigorous"). Place $x = 0$ at a minimum of $V$ and replace $V$ by the **screened potential** $V_a$: equal to $V$ for $|x| < a$ and $+\infty$ outside. For small $a$ this is a narrow, flat-bottomed infinite well, whose $k$th state, a sine arch repeated $k$ times, has $k - 1$ nodes ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]). As $a$ grows, assume the states deform continuously into those of $V$. A new node could appear only by entering through a wall, which needs $\psi'(\pm a) = 0$ at the moment of entry, or by the graph touching the axis and splitting into two crossings, which needs $\psi = \psi' = 0$ at a point; both are excluded by [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]] (5). Two levels cannot exchange their order on the way without passing through a degeneracy, which Theorem §B3.1.4 forbids. So the $k$th state keeps its $k - 1$ nodes. A rigorous proof is the oscillation theorem of Sturm–Liouville theory (Mathematical Methods, planned).

> [!remark] Remark: Parity alternates
> In an even potential the node count fixes the parity. The nodes of an even or odd state come in pairs $\pm x_j$, plus possibly $x = 0$. An odd state always has the node $x = 0$; an even state never does, since $\psi(0) = 0$ together with $\psi'(0) = 0$ ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]] (3)) would give $\psi \equiv 0$. So even states have an even number of nodes, odd states an odd number, and with exactly $k - 1$ nodes the ground state is even and the parities alternate: even, odd, even, … This is the pattern level A met in the centred box ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^rem-a4-2-3|Remark: The centred box]]), the finite well ([[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^thm-a4-4-4|Theorem §A4.4.4]]) and the oscillator ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]); here it follows for every even potential.

## Bound and scattering states

> [!remark] Remark: Bound and scattering states, and why bound energies are discrete
> Level A called a state bound if $E < V(x)$ for all large $|x|$ ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^def-a4-6-1|Def. §A4.6.1]]). Griffiths sharpens this: because the particle can tunnel through any finite barrier, only the potential at infinity matters (Griffiths eq. (2.112)),
>
> $$
> E < V(-\infty) \text{ and } E < V(+\infty) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{bound state}, \qquad E > V(-\infty) \text{ or } E > V(+\infty) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{scattering state},
> $$
>
> which for potentials vanishing at infinity is $E < 0$ versus $E > 0$ (eq. (2.113)). A classical particle trapped in a pocket of the potential above which $V$ falls off again to below $E$ is bound classically but not quantum mechanically (Griffiths Figure 2.11(c)); compare the classical division into finite and infinite motion ([[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^def-b1-3-1|CM Def. §B1.3.1]]).
>
> Counting conditions shows why the three energy ranges behave differently (Greensite §8.1; Zwiebach Lecture 11). Fix $E$ and count: the solution has two free constants, one of which is only the normalization.
> - *Below both asymptotic values*, the solution must decay at both ends, two conditions on one remaining constant plus $E$: only special energies work, and the bound-state energies are **discrete** and, by Theorem §B3.1.4, **nondegenerate**.
> - *Between* $V(-\infty)$ and $V(+\infty)$, the solution oscillates on one side and must decay on the other: one condition, satisfied for **every** $E$, and the state is unique up to a factor (the one-sided form of Theorem §B3.1.4). The potential step with $E < V_0$ is the example ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-4|Theorem §A4.6.4]]).
> - *Above both*, there is no condition: a **continuum** with two independent solutions at each energy.
>
> Zwiebach's shooting method turns the first case into a numerical recipe: integrate outward from $x = 0$ (with $\psi'(0) = 0$ or $\psi(0) = 0$ in an even potential) and adjust $E$ until the solution switches from diverging upward to diverging downward; level A showed this for the oscillator ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^rem-a4-1-8|Remark: Reading the shape of ψ from the curvature]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.1.1: A Bead on a Ring
> A bead of mass $m$ slides without friction on a circular wire of circumference $L$: $V = 0$, with the coordinate $x$ along the wire and the periodicity condition $\psi(x + L) = \psi(x)$. Find the stationary states and explain their degeneracy.
>
> *Energies are non-negative.* The argument of [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]] works on $[0, L)$, the boundary terms at $0$ and $L$ cancelling by periodicity: $E = \tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int_0^L|\psi'|^2dx \ge 0$, with $E = 0$ only for the constant. *Solutions.* With $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, $\psi = e^{ikx}$ is periodic iff $kL = 2\pi n$, so
>
> $$
> \psi_n(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt L}\,e^{2\pi inx/L}, \qquad E_n = \frac{2\pi^2\hbar^2n^2}{mL^2}, \qquad n = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots
> $$
>
> *Degeneracy.* $\psi_n$ and $\psi_{-n}$ have the same energy; only $\psi_0$ is nondegenerate. They are distinguished by their momenta $\pm 2\pi\hbar n/L$: clockwise and counterclockwise circulation. Theorem §B3.1.4 does not apply because there is no infinity at which the Wronskian must vanish: it is constant, as Theorem §B3.1.2 requires, but nonzero, $W(\psi_n, \psi_{-n}) = \tfrac1L\bigl[e^{ikx}(-ik)e^{-ikx} - (ik)e^{ikx}e^{-ikx}\bigr] = -2ik/L$. The real combinations $\cos(2\pi nx/L)$ and $\sin(2\pi nx/L)$ (Theorem §B3.1.5) are standing waves. In the language of [[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]], momentum and the reflection $x \to -x$ both commute with $\hat H$ but not with each other, which forces the degeneracy.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.46 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 10, §3, eqs. (3.4)–(3.18)*

^ex-b3-1-1

> [!example] Example §B3.1.2: The Half Oscillator
> A spring that can be stretched but not compressed: $V(x) = \tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$ for $x > 0$ and $V = \infty$ for $x < 0$. Find the allowed energies.
>
> On $x > 0$ the equation is the oscillator's, and the wall requires $\psi(0) = 0$ ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]] (4)). Given such a solution, its odd extension $\psi(-x) = -\psi(x)$ solves the full oscillator equation on the whole line: $V$ is even ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]] (1)), $\psi$ is continuous at $0$, and the derivative of an odd function is even, so $\psi'$ is continuous too. Conversely every odd state of the full oscillator vanishes at $0$ and restricts to a solution. So the half oscillator's states are the odd oscillator states, renormalized to the half line, and with the oscillator spectrum $E_n = (n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega$ ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]) in which odd $n$ belong to odd states ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]):
>
> $$
> E = \tfrac32\hbar\omega,\ \tfrac72\hbar\omega,\ \tfrac{11}{2}\hbar\omega,\ \dots, \qquad \psi(x) = \sqrt2\,\psi_n(x) \ (x > 0,\ n \text{ odd}) .
> $$
>
> The levels are spaced by $2\hbar\omega$, and the ground state costs three times the full oscillator's zero-point energy: confining the particle to half the line raises its kinetic energy. Its wave function $\sqrt2\,\psi_1$ has no node in $x > 0$ (the zero at the wall is not a node), as [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]] requires. The WKB rule for a well with one vertical wall reproduces these energies exactly ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^ex-b9-3-1|Example §B9.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.41*

^ex-b3-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The Wronskian, the uniqueness of solutions with given $\psi(x_0)$, $\psi'(x_0)$, and the comparison and oscillation theorems behind the node theorem belong to the theory of linear second-order equations and of Sturm–Liouville problems (Mathematical Methods, planned). The constancy of $W$ uses [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]; the energy integral uses integration by parts, [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]].
> - Bound states are elements of $L^2(\mathbb R)$ ([[§14 The Function Spaces Lᵖ(Ω)#^def-14-1|556 Def. §14.1]]); scattering states are not, which is why the degeneracy theorem is a statement about bound states only.
> - Parity as an operator, $\hat\Pi\psi(x) = \psi(-x)$, commuting with $\hat H$ when $V$ is even, is the simplest symmetry of the Hamiltonian; the general treatment of symmetries as operators is level C: parity in [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^pr-c8-2-1|Principle §C8.2.1]] and [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^thm-c8-2-3|Theorem §C8.2.3]], symmetries in general in [[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^def-c8-1-1|Def. §C8.1.1]]. The three-dimensional version, with $\vec r \to -\vec r$, gives the parity $(-1)^\ell$ of the spherical harmonics ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^rem-b5-2-6|★ Remark: Reading the spherical harmonics]]).
> - Classical counterparts: motion confined to $U \le E$ ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-1|CM Theorem §A4.7.1]]), finite and infinite motion ([[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^def-b1-3-1|CM Def. §B1.3.1]]).
> - Level C: real eigenfunctions (Theorem §B3.1.5) as a consequence of time-reversal invariance — [[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^thm-c8-3-5|Theorem §C8.3.5]]; the node theorem (Theorem §B3.1.7) orders the even and odd states of a symmetric double well — [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^thm-c8-2-4|Theorem §C8.2.4]].
