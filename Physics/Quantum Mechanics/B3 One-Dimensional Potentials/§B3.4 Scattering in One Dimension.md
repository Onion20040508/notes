Level A solved the two basic scattering problems: the potential step, with $R$ and $T$ for energies above and below it ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-3|Theorem §A4.6.3]], [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-4|Theorem §A4.6.4]]), and the rectangular barrier, with the exact transmission coefficient, tunnelling below the top and complete transmission at $k'L = n\pi$ above it ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-6|Theorem §A4.6.6]]). [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential]] then defined $R$ and $T$ through probability currents and proved $R + T = 1$ for every real potential ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]], [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-4|Theorem §B3.3.4]]). This section completes the square-well family: the scattering states of the finite *well*, with its transmission resonances at the energies of the infinite well, from which the barrier results follow by a change of sign and the delta function by a limit. It then returns to the step to show what the stationary states say about real particles, and points to the general methods: transfer matrices for potentials built of pieces, and the WKB approximation for barriers of arbitrary shape ([[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas]]).

## Scattering by the finite square well

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.4.1: Scattering by a Finite Square Well
> For the finite well of [[§B3.2 Square Wells Revisited#^mod-b3-2-1|Model §B3.2.1]] ($V = -V_0$ for $|x| < a$) and energy $E > 0$, put $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and $l = \sqrt{2m(E + V_0)}/\hbar$. The scattering state incident from the left (Griffiths eqs. (2.161)–(2.165)),
>
> $$
> \psi(x) = \begin{cases} Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx}, & x < -a,\\ C\sin lx + D\cos lx, & |x| < a,\\ Fe^{ikx}, & x > a,\end{cases}
> $$
>
> has (eqs. (2.170)–(2.171))
>
> $$
> B = i\,\frac{\sin 2la}{2kl}\,\bigl(l^2 - k^2\bigr)\,F, \qquad F = \frac{e^{-2ika}\,A}{\cos 2la - i\,\dfrac{k^2 + l^2}{2kl}\,\sin 2la} ,
> $$
>
> and the transmission coefficient (eq. (2.172)), with $R = 1 - T$,
>
> $$
> T^{-1} = 1 + \frac{V_0^2}{4E(E + V_0)}\,\sin^2\Bigl(\frac{2a}{\hbar}\sqrt{2m(E + V_0)}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> 1. *Transmission resonances.* $T = 1$ exactly when $2la = n\pi$, that is at (eqs. (2.173)–(2.174))
>
> $$
> E_n + V_0 = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m(2a)^2}, \qquad n > \frac{2z_0}{\pi} ,
> $$
>
> the energies of the infinite well of the same width, measured from the bottom; the condition on $n$ makes $E_n > 0$.
> 2. *Barrier.* For the barrier $V = +V_0$ on $|x| < a$ ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^mod-a4-6-5|Model §A4.6.5]] with $L = 2a$), replace $V_0 \to -V_0$:
>
> $$
> T^{-1} = 1 + \frac{V_0^2}{4E(E - V_0)}\sin^2\frac{2a\sqrt{2m(E - V_0)}}{\hbar}\ \ (E > V_0), \qquad T^{-1} = 1 + \frac{V_0^2}{4E(V_0 - E)}\sinh^2\frac{2a\sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}}{\hbar}\ \ (E < V_0),
> $$
>
> and at the top, $E = V_0$, $T = 1/(1 + z_0^2)$ with $z_0 = a\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar$. These are the formulas that level A stated for the barrier ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-6|Theorem §A4.6.6]], with $U_0 = V_0$, $L = 2a$, $\alpha = \sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}/\hbar$), now derived.
> 3. *Delta limit.* As $a \to 0$ with $2aV_0 = \alpha$ fixed, $T \to 1/(1 + m\alpha^2/2\hbar^2E)$, the result for the delta function ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.6, eqs. (2.161)–(2.174); Problems 2.31–2.33 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 17, §1, eqs. (1.2)–(1.14) · Greensite ch. 8, §8.4–8.5, eqs. (8.68)–(8.91) · the user's Potential solutions notes, §3.3, §4 · the user's Classical notes, §13.3–13.4*

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The forms in each region follow from [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-6|Theorem §A4.1.6]]; no wave enters from the right. Although $V$ is even, the state is not even or odd: incidence from one side breaks the symmetry, and scattering states are degenerate ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]]). Continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$ ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]) at $x = -a$ and $x = a$ gives (eqs. (2.166)–(2.169))
>
> $$
> Ae^{-ika} + Be^{ika} = -C\sin la + D\cos la, \qquad ik\bigl[Ae^{-ika} - Be^{ika}\bigr] = l\bigl[C\cos la + D\sin la\bigr],
> $$
>
> $$
> C\sin la + D\cos la = Fe^{ika}, \qquad l\bigl[C\cos la - D\sin la\bigr] = ikFe^{ika} .
> $$
>
> *Step 1* (Griffiths Problem 2.32): solve the last two for $C$ and $D$. Multiplying the first by $\sin la$ and the second by $\cos la/l$ and adding, and similarly for $D$,
>
> $$
> C = \Bigl[\sin la + i\frac kl\cos la\Bigr]e^{ika}F, \qquad D = \Bigl[\cos la - i\frac kl\sin la\Bigr]e^{ika}F .
> $$
>
> *Step 2:* insert into the first two equations, using $\cos^2 - \sin^2 = \cos 2la$ and $2\sin\cos = \sin 2la$:
>
> $$
> Ae^{-ika} + Be^{ika} = e^{ika}F\Bigl[\cos 2la - i\frac kl\sin 2la\Bigr], \qquad Ae^{-ika} - Be^{ika} = e^{ika}F\Bigl[\cos 2la - i\frac lk\sin 2la\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Adding gives $2Ae^{-ika} = e^{ika}F\bigl[2\cos 2la - i\bigl(\tfrac kl + \tfrac lk\bigr)\sin 2la\bigr]$, which is the formula for $F$; subtracting gives $2Be^{ika} = ie^{ika}F\sin 2la\,\bigl(\tfrac lk - \tfrac kl\bigr)$, the formula for $B$.
>
> *Step 3:* $T = |F|^2/|A|^2$ (same $k$ on both sides, [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]]). With $c = \cos 2la$, $s = \sin 2la$,
>
> $$
> T^{-1} = c^2 + \frac{(k^2 + l^2)^2}{4k^2l^2}\,s^2 = 1 + \frac{(l^2 - k^2)^2}{4k^2l^2}\,s^2, \qquad \frac{(l^2 - k^2)^2}{4k^2l^2} = \frac{(2mV_0/\hbar^2)^2}{4\,(2m/\hbar^2)^2E(E + V_0)} = \frac{V_0^2}{4E(E + V_0)} .
> $$
>
> $R = 1 - T$ by [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-4|Theorem §B3.3.4]]; directly, $|B/A|^2 = \tfrac{(l^2 - k^2)^2}{4k^2l^2}s^2\,T$, and the two add to $1$.
>
> *1.* The added term is $\ge 0$ and vanishes only when $\sin 2la = 0$, i.e. $2la = n\pi$, which with $l^2 = 2m(E + V_0)/\hbar^2$ is (2.174). Since $(2la)^2 = 4z_0^2(1 + E/V_0) > 4z_0^2$, only $n > 2z_0/\pi$ occur.
>
> *2.* Nothing above used the sign of $V_0$. For $V_0 \to -V_0$, $l = \sqrt{2m(E - V_0)}/\hbar$; for $E < V_0$ it is imaginary, $l = i\kappa$, and $\sin^2(2la) = -\sinh^2(2\kappa a)$ together with $E - V_0 = -(V_0 - E)$ gives the second form (Griffiths Problem 2.33). As $E \to V_0$ from either side the argument $\theta$ of $\sin$ or $\sinh$ tends to $0$, $\sin^2\theta \approx \sinh^2\theta \approx \theta^2 = 8ma^2|E - V_0|/\hbar^2$, and the added term tends to $\tfrac{V_0^2}{4E}\cdot\tfrac{8ma^2}{\hbar^2} \to \tfrac{2mV_0a^2}{\hbar^2} = z_0^2$ at $E = V_0$ (where inside the barrier $\psi$ is linear in $x$).
>
> *3.* With $V_0 = \alpha/2a$ and $a \to 0$, $\sin^2 2la \approx (2la)^2 = 8ma^2(E + V_0)/\hbar^2$ and $\tfrac{V_0^2}{4E(E + V_0)}\sin^2 2la \to \tfrac{V_0^2}{4E}\cdot\tfrac{8ma^2}{\hbar^2} = \tfrac{m(2aV_0)^2}{2\hbar^2E} = \tfrac{m\alpha^2}{2\hbar^2E}$ (Griffiths Problem 2.31).

> [!remark] Remark: Why the resonances sit at the infinite-well energies
> $2la = n\pi$ says that the well, of width $2a$, holds a whole number of half de Broglie wavelengths, $2a = n\lambda/2$ (Zwiebach eq. (1.15); Greensite eq. (8.85)): the standing-wave condition of the infinite well. Then the waves reflected at the two edges, which come with opposite signs, cancel, and nothing is reflected. This is the thin-film cancellation that level A described for the barrier ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-3|Remark: What controls tunnelling]], [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^thm-a6-3-2|WO Theorem §A6.3.2]]); the formula even has the shape of the Airy function of a Fabry–Perot etalon, $1/[1 + \mathcal F\sin^2(\Delta/2)]$ with round-trip phase $\Delta = 2\cdot 2la$ and $\mathcal F = V_0^2/4E(E + V_0)$ ([[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-2|WO Theorem §B10.4.2]]). Between resonances $T$ dips to $1/(1 + \mathcal F)$; since $\mathcal F$ falls with energy, the dips fill in and the well becomes transparent at high energy, while the resonances spread apart (Zwiebach's observation): consecutive ones are separated by $\Delta E = (2n + 1)\pi^2\hbar^2/2m(2a)^2$.
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 17, §1 · Greensite ch. 8, §8.4 · Griffiths §2.6, Figure 2.18*

> [!example] Example §B3.4.1: Electrons over the 1-nm Well
> Electrons scatter on the well of [[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^ex-a4-4-1|Example §A4.4.1]]: width $2a = 1.00$ nm, depth $V_0 = 10.0$ eV, $z_0 = 8.10$, which has six bound states. Where is the well transparent, and how much is transmitted at low energy?
>
> The infinite-well levels for this width are $n^2(0.376\ \mathrm{eV})$ ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]), and resonances need $n > 2z_0/\pi = 5.16$. By [[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]] (1), $E_n = n^2(0.376\ \mathrm{eV}) - 10.0\ \mathrm{eV}$:
>
> | $n$ | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $E_n$ (eV) | 3.54 | 8.43 | 14.07 | 20.46 | 27.60 |
>
> (Here $n$ labels the infinite-well level that fits the well, not a bound state.) Away from the resonances, $T$ from eq. (2.172) is $0.12$ at $0.1$ eV, $0.32$ at $1.0$ eV and $0.55$ at $2.0$ eV; between the first two resonances it dips to $0.78$ (near $5.6$ eV), between the next two to $0.90$. As $E \to 0$, $T \approx 4E(E + V_0)/[V_0^2\sin^2 2z_0] \to 0$ linearly in $E$: slow particles are reflected by a *well*, which a classical particle would cross with certainty.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.6, eqs. (2.172)–(2.174) (numbers computed here for the well of level A)*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The Ramsauer–Townsend effect
> In 1921 Ramsauer and, independently, Townsend and Bailey (1922) found that slow electrons pass through noble-gas atoms (argon, krypton, xenon) almost without scattering at energies around $1$ eV, while scattering more at lower and higher energies; classical physics has no explanation (Griffiths' footnote 51 notes that this was seen before quantum mechanics). An atom of a noble gas is neutral with a closed shell, so an incoming electron feels nothing until it penetrates the electron cloud, where it is attracted by the nucleus: a short-range attractive well. Taking $R$ as a stand-in for the scattering cross section, the vanishing of $R$ at a transmission resonance of [[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]] is the effect. The one-dimensional square well shows the mechanism but does not fit the data; a quantitative account needs a three-dimensional spherical well and the partial-wave theory of scattering (level C: [[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^ex-c10-3-2|Example §C10.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 17, §2 · Griffiths §2.6, footnote 51 · Greensite ch. 8, §8.4*

## The step revisited

> [!remark] Remark: The step in the language of currents
> The step of level A ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^mod-a4-6-2|Model §A4.6.2]]) fits the framework of [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential]] directly, with $V_L = 0$, $V_R = V_0$; the derivation in the user's Potential solutions notes (§1) is level A's, with $r = B/A$ and $t = C/A$.
> - *Above the step,* $T = \tfrac{k_2}{k_1}|C/A|^2$ is [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]] with $k_R/k_L = k_2/k_1$ (Griffiths eq. (2.175)), and $R + T = 1$ is current conservation ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-4|Theorem §B3.3.4]]): $J = \tfrac{\hbar k_1}{m}(|A|^2 - |B|^2)$ on the left equals $\tfrac{\hbar k_2}{m}|C|^2$ on the right (Zwiebach).
> - *At* $E = V_0$, $k_2 = 0$, $B = A$ and $C = 2A$: $\psi = 2A\cos k_1x$ on the left and the constant $2A$ on the right, which carries no current, so $R = 1$; $R$ and $T$ are continuous in $E$ across the top of the step (Zwiebach).
> - *Below the step,* $B/A = (k - i\kappa)/(k + i\kappa)$ has modulus $1$ ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-4|Theorem §A4.6.4]]); writing $k - i\kappa = -i(\kappa + ik)$ shows $B/A = -e^{2i\delta(E)}$ with
>
> $$
> \delta(E) = \arctan\frac{k}{\kappa} = \arcsin\sqrt{E/V_0} \in \bigl(0, \tfrac\pi2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Then on the left $\psi = 2iAe^{i\delta}\sin(kx - \delta)$ and $|\psi|^2 = 4|A|^2\sin^2(kx - \delta)$: a standing wave whose nodes are those of a wave reflected at the point $x_0 = \delta/k$ inside the step, where the extrapolated sine would vanish. The wave behaves as if reflected a little beyond the edge.
> - *A step down* ($V_0 < 0$) reflects with the same $R = \bigl(\tfrac{k_1 - k_2}{k_1 + k_2}\bigr)^2$, since $R$ depends only on the mismatch of the wave numbers ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-2|Remark: Waves at a step]]).
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 16, §2–3, eqs. (2.7)–(2.16), (3.17)–(3.24) · Griffiths Problems 2.34–2.35 · the user's Potential solutions notes, §1 · the user's Classical notes, §13.1*

> [!example] Example §B3.4.2: A Neutron Entering a Nucleus, and a Car at a Cliff
> When a free neutron enters a nucleus its potential energy drops abruptly from $0$ to about $-12$ MeV. A fission neutron of kinetic energy $4$ MeV strikes such a nucleus. What is the probability that it is absorbed rather than reflected at the surface?
>
> Model the surface as a step down of $V_0 = 12$ MeV. The kinetic energy is $4$ MeV outside and $16$ MeV inside, so $k_2/k_1 = \sqrt{16/4} = 2$ and
>
> $$
> R = \Bigl(\frac{k_1 - k_2}{k_1 + k_2}\Bigr)^2 = \Bigl(\frac{1 - 2}{1 + 2}\Bigr)^2 = \frac19 \approx 0.11, \qquad T = 1 - R = \frac89 \approx 0.89 .
> $$
>
> About one neutron in nine bounces off the edge of the well although nothing classical stops it. (Its de Broglie wavelength outside, $\lambda = hc/\sqrt{2mc^2K} = (1240\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm})/\sqrt{2(940)(4)}\ \mathrm{MeV} = 14$ fm, is larger than a nucleus, so the abrupt step is a rough model.) The same numbers answer Griffiths' first question: any particle meeting a drop of three times its kinetic energy has $k_2 = 2k_1$ and $R = 1/9$.
>
> Should a car rolling off a cliff then bounce back one time in nine? No. A step reflects only if the potential changes abruptly on the scale of the wavelength; a $1000$-kg car at $10$ m/s has $\lambda = h/mv \approx 7 \times 10^{-38}$ m, and its potential energy falls gradually as it drops, over metres. Where $V$ changes little over a wavelength the wave follows it without reflecting: the semiclassical regime ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^cau-b9-3-1|Caution: Slowly varying compared with what]]; Zwiebach's criterion, Lecture 12, §2).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.35 (solved here)*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Wave packets at a step, and the reflection delay
> A real particle is a packet $\Psi_{\text{inc}} = \int f(k)\,e^{ikx - iE(k)t/\hbar}dk$ with $f$ peaked at $k_0$, and each component scatters with the amplitudes of the stationary state. The peak of each part sits where its phase is stationary in $k$ at $k_0$ (the envelope moves at the group velocity, [[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^thm-b6-2-3|WO Theorem §B6.2.3]]). Above the step: the incident peak is at $x = \hbar k_0t/m$ for $t < 0$, the reflected one at $x = -\hbar k_0t/m$ and the transmitted one at $x = \hbar\bar k_0t/m$ for $t > 0$, with $\bar k = \sqrt{k^2 - 2mV_0/\hbar^2}$: all three meet the step at $t = 0$, and the transmitted packet is slower. Below the step the reflected packet carries the phase $-e^{2i\delta(E)}$ of [[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^rem-b3-4-3|Remark: The step in the language of currents]], and stationarity of $2\delta(k) - kx - \hbar k^2t/2m$ gives
>
> $$
> x = -\frac{\hbar k_0}{m}\bigl(t - \Delta t\bigr), \qquad \Delta t = 2\hbar\,\frac{d\delta}{dE} = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{E(V_0 - E)}} = \frac{2}{\kappa v}, \qquad v = \frac{\hbar k}{m} :
> $$
>
> the packet comes back late by exactly the time needed to travel one penetration depth $1/\kappa$ into the step and back. The delay grows without bound near $E = 0$ and $E = V_0$. For a $1$-eV electron at a $2$-eV step, $\Delta t = 6.6 \times 10^{-16}$ s. The optical counterpart is the Goos–Hänchen shift of a totally reflected beam ([[§B9.3 Total Internal Reflection and the Evanescent Wave#^rem-b9-3-1|WO Remark: Frustrated total reflection and the Goos–Hänchen shift]]). Zwiebach also explains why finding the particle in the step does not show a negative kinetic energy, the argument of [[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^rem-a4-4-3|Remark: Does the particle have negative kinetic energy there?]].
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 16, §4, eqs. (3.25), (4.26)–(4.45) (the form $2/\kappa v$ worked out here)*

## Arbitrary barriers and general methods

> [!remark] Remark: Barriers of arbitrary shape
> For a localized barrier of any shape, the user's Potential solutions notes (§5) and Classical notes (§13.5) list three routes: the WKB approximation, perturbation theory when the barrier is weak, and numerical integration; in each case $R$ and $T$ are read off the asymptotic waves with [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]], and $R + T = 1$ holds by [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-4|Theorem §B3.3.4]]. The WKB result for tunnelling, $T \approx e^{-2\gamma}$ with $\gamma = \tfrac1\hbar\int|p(x)|\,dx$ over the forbidden region, is [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^thm-b9-4-2|Theorem §B9.4.2]]. For a rectangular barrier $\gamma = 2a\sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}/\hbar$, and $e^{-2\gamma}$ is the exponential of the thick-barrier form of [[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^thm-b3-4-1|Theorem §B3.4.1]] (2) (level A's $16\tfrac E{V_0}(1 - \tfrac E{V_0})e^{-2\alpha L}$, [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-6|Theorem §A4.6.6]]), without the prefactor that the WKB approximation cannot resolve; [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^ex-b9-4-1|Example §B9.4.1]] compares the two numerically.
>
> *Source: the user's Potential solutions notes, §5 · the user's Classical notes, §13.5*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Scattering and transfer matrices
> For a potential confined to a finite region, with $\psi = Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx}$ on the left and $Fe^{ikx} + Ge^{-ikx}$ on the right, the four boundary conditions leave two linear relations among the amplitudes. Griffiths writes them in two ways: the **scattering matrix** $S$ gives the outgoing amplitudes from the incoming ones, $(B, F)^{\mathsf T} = S\,(A, G)^{\mathsf T}$ (eq. (2.180)), and the **transfer matrix** $M$ gives the right-hand amplitudes from the left-hand ones, $(F, G)^{\mathsf T} = M\,(A, B)^{\mathsf T}$ (eq. (2.183)). For a real potential:
> - $\psi^*$ is a solution too, with the roles of incoming and outgoing waves exchanged: $(G^*, F^*)^{\mathsf T} = M\,(B^*, A^*)^{\mathsf T}$. Comparing with the definition, $M_{22} = M_{11}^*$ and $M_{21} = M_{12}^*$.
> - Current conservation ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-4|Theorem §B3.3.4]]), $|A|^2 - |B|^2 = |F|^2 - |G|^2$ for all amplitudes, gives $\det M = |M_{11}|^2 - |M_{12}|^2 = 1$.
> - Incidence from the left ($G = 0$): $B/A = -M_{21}/M_{22}$ and $F/A = \det M/M_{22} = 1/M_{22}$. Incidence from the right ($A = 0$): transmitted $B/G = 1/M_{22}$, reflected $F/G = M_{12}/M_{22}$. Hence
>
> $$
> T_l = T_r = \frac{1}{|M_{22}|^2}, \qquad R_l = R_r = \frac{|M_{12}|^2}{|M_{22}|^2} :
> $$
>
> transmission and reflection are the same from both sides, for any shape of the potential.
> - For pieces separated by regions with $V = 0$, the output amplitudes of the first piece are the input of the second, so $M = M_2M_1$ (eq. (2.184)), which is what makes $M$ useful.
> - For a delta-function well $-\alpha\,\delta(x - d)$, Griffiths' eqs. (2.136) and (2.138), shifted to $x = d$, give
>
> $$
> M_d = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + i\beta & i\beta\,e^{-2ikd}\\ -i\beta\,e^{2ikd} & 1 - i\beta\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \beta = \frac{m\alpha}{\hbar^2k},
> $$
>
> with $\det M_d = 1$ and $T = 1/|1 - i\beta|^2 = 1/(1 + \beta^2)$, as in [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-5|Theorem §B3.3.5]]; for a barrier, $\beta \to -\beta$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 2.53–2.54, eqs. (2.178)–(2.184) (properties derived here)*

> [!example]- ★ Example §B3.4.3: Resonant Tunnelling through Two Delta Barriers
> Find the transmission through $V(x) = \alpha\bigl[\delta(x + a) + \delta(x - a)\bigr]$, $\alpha > 0$, two barriers a distance $2a$ apart.
>
> With the barrier matrices of [[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^rem-b3-4-6|★ Remark: Scattering and transfer matrices]] ($\beta \to -\beta$), $M = M_{a}M_{-a}$ and
>
> $$
> M_{22} = (M_a)_{21}(M_{-a})_{12} + (M_a)_{22}(M_{-a})_{22} = (i\beta e^{2ika})(-i\beta e^{2ika}) + (1 + i\beta)^2 = (1 + i\beta)^2 + \beta^2e^{4ika} .
> $$
>
> Writing $1 + i\beta = \sqrt{1 + \beta^2}\,e^{i\varphi}$ with $\varphi = \arctan\beta$, and $1 + \cos\theta = 2\cos^2(\theta/2)$,
>
> $$
> T = \frac{1}{|M_{22}|^2} = \frac{1}{1 + 4\beta^2(1 + \beta^2)\cos^2(2ka - \varphi)} .
> $$
>
> So $T = 1$ whenever $4ka - 2\arctan\beta = (2n + 1)\pi$: at these energies the particle passes two barriers *with certainty*, each of which alone would transmit only $T_1 = 1/(1 + \beta^2)$. As $\beta \to \infty$ (opaque barriers) the condition becomes $2ka = (n + 1)\pi$, the levels of an infinite well of width $2a$ between the barriers: the particle tunnels in, resonates in a quasi-bound state, and tunnels out. Between resonances $T$ falls to $1/(1 + 4\beta^2(1 + \beta^2)) \approx T_1^2/4$. The coefficient $4\beta^2(1 + \beta^2) = 4R_1/(1 - R_1)^2$, with $R_1 = \beta^2/(1 + \beta^2)$ the reflectance of one barrier, is exactly the coefficient of finesse of a Fabry–Perot etalon with two mirrors of reflectance $R_1$ ([[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-2|WO Theorem §B10.4.2]]).
>
> *Numbers.* For $m\alpha a/\hbar^2 = 3$ (so $\beta = 3/ka$) the first resonances are at $ka = 1.358$, $2.769$, $4.235$, below the box values $\pi/2$, $\pi$, $3\pi/2$; the first has a full width at half maximum of only $0.082$ in $ka$, where a single barrier transmits $17\%$, and between the first two resonances $T$ is about $3\%$. This is the principle of the resonant-tunnelling diode ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-6|Remark: Tunnel devices and the ammonia clock]]). For two delta *wells* (Griffiths Problem 2.28), $\beta \to -\beta$ in the same formulas.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 2.28, 2.54(c)–(d) (solved here; numbers computed here)*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Phase shifts, Levinson's theorem and resonances
> Zwiebach develops scattering on a half line, a potential $V(x)$ of range $R$ in front of a hard wall at $x = 0$, as a model of three-dimensional scattering in the radial coordinate. Every incident wave is reflected, so all information sits in one real **phase shift** $\delta(E)$: beyond the range, $\psi = e^{i\delta}\sin(kx + \delta)$, compared with $\sin kx$ without the potential, and the scattered wave $\psi - \sin kx = e^{i\delta}\sin\delta\,e^{ikx}$ has amplitude $A_s = e^{i\delta}\sin\delta$.
> - **Time delay.** A reflected packet is delayed by $\Delta t = 2\hbar\,d\delta/dE$, as at the step above; causality bounds it from below by about $-2R/v$, but a long positive delay is possible: the particle is temporarily trapped.
> - **Levinson's theorem.** The number of bound states is $N_b = \tfrac1\pi\bigl[\delta(0) - \delta(\infty)\bigr]$. Proof idea: add a second wall far away so that all states are countable, and compare the counts with and without the potential; positive-energy states lost as the potential is switched on reappear as bound states.
> - **Resonances.** Near a resonance $\delta$ increases rapidly through $\pi/2$ (mod $\pi$), and $|A_s|^2 = \sin^2\delta$ follows the **Breit–Wigner** form $\tfrac14\Gamma^2/[(E - E_\alpha)^2 + \tfrac14\Gamma^2]$, with width $\Gamma$ and lifetime $\tau = \hbar/\Gamma$; the time delay at the peak is $4\tau$. In the complex $k$ plane a resonance is a pole of $A_s$ just below the real axis, at $k = \alpha - i\beta$, while bound states are poles on the positive imaginary axis ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^rem-b3-3-4|★ Remark: The bound state as a pole of the transmission amplitude]]). The quasi-bound states between the two barriers of Example §B3.4.3 are resonances of this kind; unstable particles, such as the Higgs boson with $\Gamma \approx 4$ MeV, are the same phenomenon in particle physics.
>
> Scattering theory in three dimensions, with partial waves, phase shifts and cross sections, is level C: [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-1|Theorem §C10.1.1]], [[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^thm-c10-3-2|Theorem §C10.3.2]], [[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^thm-c10-3-3|Theorem §C10.3.3]]; Levinson's theorem and resonances there: [[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^rem-c10-3-6|★ Remark: Bound states as poles of the S matrix; Levinson's theorem]], [[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^rem-c10-3-7|★ Remark: Resonances and the Breit–Wigner formula]].
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 18, §1; Lecture 19, §1–3*
