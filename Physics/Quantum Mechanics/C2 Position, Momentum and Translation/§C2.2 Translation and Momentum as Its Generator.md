Where does $[x, p] = i\hbar$ come from? Level B postulated the momentum operator, $\hat p = -i\hbar\,\partial/\partial x$ ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]], [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^pr-b1-2-2|Principle §B1.2.2]]), and obtained the canonical commutator from it by the product rule ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]]). It mentioned Dirac's alternative, to replace Poisson brackets by commutators, only as a prescription ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^rem-b2-4-1|Remark: From Poisson brackets to commutators]], [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^rem-b8-1-5|CM Remark: From brackets to commutators]]). Level C reverses the logic. It states what a spatial displacement of a physical system must do, shows that such an operator has a Hermitian generator, identifies that generator with momentum divided by $\hbar$, and derives all the canonical commutation relations. The form $-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial x'$ of momentum on wave functions then becomes a theorem ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space|§C2.3]]). The same construction, with different symmetries, gives the Hamiltonian ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation|§C3.1]]) and angular momentum ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations|§C5.1]]).

> [!caution] Caution: Notation; active translations
> The translation operator is written $\mathcal T(d\mathbf x')$, as in the other level-C notes. Sakurai's symbol is a script capital printed, in the e-book used here, in a font where it looks like a script J; it is usually read as a script T. The user's 511 notes write $\mathcal T$ as well. The user's series writes $T(a)$. Translations here are **active**: the system is moved by $+d\mathbf x'$ and the coordinates stay put. The **passive** alternative, in which the origin of the coordinate system is moved by $-d\mathbf x'$ and the same state is described anew, gives the same formulas with opposite signs. Sakurai avoids it, and the two must not be mixed. With the active convention, $\mathcal T(\mathbf l)$ shifts wave functions to the right, $\psi(\mathbf x') \to \psi(\mathbf x' - \mathbf l)$, and $\mathcal T(\mathbf l) = e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf l/\hbar}$ carries a minus sign.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.3*

## Translations and their generator

> [!principle] Principle §C2.2.1: Properties of Translations
> For every infinitesimal displacement $d\mathbf x'$ there is an operator $\mathcal T(d\mathbf x')$, the **infinitesimal translation**, that moves a state localized at $\mathbf x'$ to one localized at $\mathbf x' + d\mathbf x'$ and leaves everything else, such as the spin, unchanged:
>
> $$
> \mathcal T(d\mathbf x')\,|\mathbf x'\rangle = |\mathbf x' + d\mathbf x'\rangle ,
> $$
>
> where a possible phase factor is set to $1$ by convention. It has the following properties.
> 1. **Unitarity** (probability conservation): $\mathcal T^\dagger(d\mathbf x')\,\mathcal T(d\mathbf x') = 1$, so a normalized ket stays normalized.
> 2. **Composition**: $\mathcal T(d\mathbf x'')\,\mathcal T(d\mathbf x') = \mathcal T(d\mathbf x' + d\mathbf x'')$; two displacements, in any directions, compose to their vector sum.
> 3. **Inverse**: $\mathcal T(-d\mathbf x') = \mathcal T^{-1}(d\mathbf x')$.
> 4. **Identity limit**: $\lim_{d\mathbf x' \to 0}\mathcal T(d\mathbf x') = 1$, and $\mathcal T(d\mathbf x') - 1$ is of first order in $d\mathbf x'$.
>
> *Domain: nonrelativistic quantum mechanics in homogeneous Euclidean space; active translations (Caution above). The properties are demanded of the translation of any isolated system. Whether the Hamiltonian is unchanged by them, so that momentum is conserved, is a separate question about the dynamics ([[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-2|Theorem §C8.1.2]]).*
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.3, eqs. (1.194), (1.197)–(1.201) · the user's 511 notes, §"Properties of Translation Operator" · the user's series, Part IV, §5.2*

^pr-c2-2-1
> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.2: The Generator of Translations
> To first order in $d\mathbf x'$,
>
> $$
> \mathcal T(d\mathbf x') = 1 - i\,\mathbf K\cdot d\mathbf x' ,
> $$
>
> where $\mathbf K = (K_x, K_y, K_z)$ are Hermitian operators with the dimension of inverse length. Conversely, an operator of this form with Hermitian $\mathbf K$ satisfies properties 1–4 of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-1|Principle §C2.2.1]] to first order. The translated state has the shifted wave function
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf x'|\mathcal T(d\mathbf x')|\alpha\rangle = \langle\mathbf x' - d\mathbf x'|\alpha\rangle .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.3, eqs. (1.195)–(1.196), (1.202)–(1.204) (sufficiency); the user's series, Part IV, §5.2 (Hermiticity from unitarity) · the user's 511 notes, §"Generator of Translation"*

^thm-c2-2-2
> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Form.* By property 4, $\mathcal T(d\mathbf x') = 1 + O(d\mathbf x')$, and differentiability at $d\mathbf x' = 0$ makes the first-order term linear in the components: $\mathcal T(d\mathbf x') = 1 - i\sum_jK_j\,dx'_j$, which defines $K_j$. The $i$ is a convention that makes $K_j$ Hermitian. $\mathbf K\cdot d\mathbf x'$ is dimensionless, so $\mathbf K$ has the dimension of inverse length.
>
> *Hermiticity.* $\mathcal T^\dagger\mathcal T = (1 + i\mathbf K^\dagger\cdot d\mathbf x')(1 - i\mathbf K\cdot d\mathbf x') = 1 - i(\mathbf K - \mathbf K^\dagger)\cdot d\mathbf x' + O\bigl((d\mathbf x')^2\bigr)$. Property 1 for every direction of $d\mathbf x'$ forces $K_j^\dagger = K_j$. This is the argument that makes the Hamiltonian Hermitian ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]]).
>
> *Conversely.* With Hermitian $\mathbf K$, the same computation gives unitarity to first order. $(1 - i\mathbf K\cdot d\mathbf x'')(1 - i\mathbf K\cdot d\mathbf x') = 1 - i\mathbf K\cdot(d\mathbf x' + d\mathbf x'') + O\bigl((d\mathbf x')^2\bigr)$ gives composition. $(1 + i\mathbf K\cdot d\mathbf x')(1 - i\mathbf K\cdot d\mathbf x') = 1 + O\bigl((d\mathbf x')^2\bigr)$ gives the inverse, and the identity limit is evident.
>
> *Wave function.* Expand $|\alpha\rangle$ in position kets ([[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^pr-c2-1-2|Principle §C2.1.2]]) and shift the integration variable over all space:
>
> $$
> \mathcal T(d\mathbf x')|\alpha\rangle = \int d^3x''\,|\mathbf x'' + d\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x''|\alpha\rangle = \int d^3x''\,|\mathbf x''\rangle\langle\mathbf x'' - d\mathbf x'|\alpha\rangle .
> $$
>
> The bra $\langle\mathbf x'|$ and $\langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf x''\rangle = \delta^3(\mathbf x' - \mathbf x'')$ give the shifted wave function.

*Uses:* [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-1|Principle §C2.2.1]], [[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^pr-c2-1-2|Principle §C2.1.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.3: Position and the Generator of Translations
> $$
> [\mathbf x, \mathcal T(d\mathbf x')] = d\mathbf x', \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad [x_i, K_j] = i\,\delta_{ij} ,
> $$
>
> with $d\mathbf x'$ and $\delta_{ij}$ multiplied by the identity operator.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.3, eqs. (1.205a)–(1.209) · the user's 511 notes, §"Momentum as a Generator of Translation" · the user's series, Part IV, §5.2*

^thm-c2-2-3
> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> On a position eigenket,
>
> $$
> \mathbf x\,\mathcal T(d\mathbf x')|\mathbf x'\rangle = (\mathbf x' + d\mathbf x')\,|\mathbf x' + d\mathbf x'\rangle, \qquad \mathcal T(d\mathbf x')\,\mathbf x|\mathbf x'\rangle = \mathbf x'\,|\mathbf x' + d\mathbf x'\rangle ,
> $$
>
> so $[\mathbf x, \mathcal T(d\mathbf x')]|\mathbf x'\rangle = d\mathbf x'\,|\mathbf x' + d\mathbf x'\rangle = d\mathbf x'\,|\mathbf x'\rangle$ up to second order in $d\mathbf x'$. The position eigenkets are complete, so an operator identity follows: $[\mathbf x, \mathcal T(d\mathbf x')] = d\mathbf x'$. Inserting [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], the $1$ commutes with everything, and the $i$-th component reads $-i\sum_j[x_i, K_j]\,dx'_j = dx'_i$. Choosing $d\mathbf x'$ along the $j$-th axis gives $[x_i, K_j] = i\,\delta_{ij}$.

## Momentum

> [!principle] Principle §C2.2.4: Momentum Is the Generator of Translations
> The generator of translations is the momentum divided by Planck's constant over $2\pi$:
>
> $$
> \mathbf K = \frac{\mathbf p}{\hbar}, \qquad \mathcal T(d\mathbf x') = 1 - \frac{i\,\mathbf p\cdot d\mathbf x'}{\hbar} ,
> $$
>
> where $\hbar$ is the constant of de Broglie's relation $2\pi/\lambda = p/\hbar$. So $\mathbf K$ is the operator of the wave number, $2\pi$ over the wavelength.
>
> *Domain: one particle, or the total momentum of an isolated system (the generator of translations of all its parts); nonrelativistic. In the presence of a vector potential, $\mathbf p$ remains the generator of translations (the canonical momentum), but it is no longer $m\,d\mathbf x/dt$ ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^def-c4-3-1|Def. §C4.3.1]]).*
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.4, eqs. (1.210)–(1.214) · the user's 511 notes, §"Momentum as a Generator of Translation" · the user's series, Part IV, §5.2*

^pr-c2-2-4
> [!remark] Remark: Why momentum, and why this constant
> - **Borrowed from classical mechanics.** "For fundamental properties we will borrow only names from classical physics" (Schwinger, quoted by Sakurai). Classically, the infinitesimal translation $\mathbf X = \mathbf x + d\mathbf x$, $\mathbf P = \mathbf p$ is the canonical transformation with generating function $F(\mathbf x, \mathbf P) = \mathbf x\cdot\mathbf P + \mathbf p\cdot d\mathbf x$. Here $\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf P$ generates the identity, and the momentum, multiplying $d\mathbf x$, generates the displacement ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-4|CM Theorem §B8.2.4]] with $\epsilon G = \mathbf p\cdot d\mathbf x$; [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-5|CM Theorem §B8.1.5]]: $\delta f = \epsilon\{f, p\} = \epsilon\,\partial f/\partial q$). The quantum $1 - i\mathbf K\cdot d\mathbf x'$ has the same structure, which suggests $\mathbf K \propto \mathbf p$.
> - **The constant.** $\mathbf K$ has the dimension of inverse length, so the proportionality needs a universal constant with the dimension of action. Quantum mechanics cannot fix its value: it measures the mismatch between the macroscopic units in which classical physics was set up and the units natural to microscopic physics (Sakurai's analogy: $e^2/r$ in Gaussian units against $e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$ in SI). Experiment fixes it through de Broglie's relation, and then $\hbar = 1.055\times10^{-34}$ J s.
> - **The logical status.** With $\mathbf p$ *defined* as $\hbar\mathbf K$, the statement "a momentum eigenstate is a plane wave of wavelength $h/p$" ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]]) restates the identification. The user's series (Part IV, §5.3) calls the plane-wave result de Broglie's relation; with $\mathbf p$ defined as $\hbar\mathbf K$ it is a restatement of that definition rather than an independent derivation. The physical content of the principle, that this $\mathbf p$ is the momentum $m\,d\mathbf x/dt$ of mechanics, is checked once dynamics is introduced. For $H = \mathbf p^2/2m + V(\mathbf x)$ the Heisenberg equations give $d\mathbf x/dt = \mathbf p/m$ ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-6|Theorem §C3.3.6]]), which also confirms that the $\hbar$ in $\mathcal T$ is the $\hbar$ in the time-evolution operator ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^rem-c3-1-1|Remark: Layer change from level B]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.4 · the user's series, Part IV, §5.2–5.3*

^rem-c2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.5: Finite Translations
> A translation by a finite distance $\Delta x'$ along the $x$ axis is the exponential of the momentum component along that axis:
>
> $$
> \mathcal T(\Delta x'\,\hat{\mathbf x}) = \lim_{N \to \infty}\Bigl(1 - \frac{ip_x\,\Delta x'}{N\hbar}\Bigr)^N = \exp\Bigl(-\frac{ip_x\,\Delta x'}{\hbar}\Bigr), \qquad \exp(X) \equiv 1 + X + \frac{X^2}{2!} + \cdots ,
> $$
>
> and likewise $\mathcal T(s\,\hat{\mathbf n}) = \exp\bigl(-i(\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf n})s/\hbar\bigr)$ along any unit vector $\hat{\mathbf n}$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.4, eqs. (1.217)–(1.219) · the user's 511 notes, §"Finite Translation"*

^thm-c2-2-5
> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Composing $N$ collinear steps of length $\Delta x'/N$ (property 2 of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-1|Principle §C2.2.1]]), each of the form of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-4|Principle §C2.2.4]], gives the product, whose error terms are $N\cdot O(N^{-2}) \to 0$. Its limit is the exponential, as for numbers, because only the single operator $p_x$ occurs and its powers commute with one another. Equivalently, $\mathcal T(s\hat{\mathbf n})$ obeys $\frac{d}{ds}\mathcal T(s\hat{\mathbf n}) = \lim_{ds \to 0}\bigl[\mathcal T(ds\,\hat{\mathbf n}) - 1\bigr]\mathcal T(s\hat{\mathbf n})/ds = -\frac i\hbar(\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf n})\,\mathcal T(s\hat{\mathbf n})$ with $\mathcal T(0) = 1$, whose solution is the power series. Functions of an operator by power series: [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-6|Def. §B2.1.6]]. The time-evolution operator $e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ is built the same way ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]]).

## The canonical commutation relations

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.6: Canonical Commutation Relations
> $$
> [x_i, x_j] = 0, \qquad [p_i, p_j] = 0, \qquad [x_i, p_j] = i\hbar\,\delta_{ij} .
> $$
>
> Consequently:
> 1. $x$ and $p_x$ are incompatible: they have no simultaneous eigenket, and $\langle(\Delta x)^2\rangle\langle(\Delta p_x)^2\rangle \ge \hbar^2/4$ in every state. $x$ and $p_y$ are compatible.
> 2. $p_x$, $p_y$, $p_z$ are mutually compatible, so there are simultaneous eigenkets $|\mathbf p'\rangle = |p'_x, p'_y, p'_z\rangle$ with $p_i|\mathbf p'\rangle = p'_i|\mathbf p'\rangle$.
> 3. Translations in different directions commute, and the translation group in three dimensions is abelian ([[§1 The Definition of a Group#^def-1-2|493 Def. §1.2]]): when the generators of a group of transformations commute, so do the transformations.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.4–1.6.5, eqs. (1.215)–(1.228) · the user's 511 notes, §"Momentum as a Generator of Translation", §"Commutation of Translations", §"The Canonical Commutation Relations" · the user's series, Part IV, §5.2*

^thm-c2-2-6
> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Position with position:* [[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^thm-c2-1-3|Theorem §C2.1.3]].
>
> *Position with momentum:* $[x_i, p_j] = \hbar\,[x_i, K_j] = i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}$ by [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]] and [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-4|Principle §C2.2.4]].
>
> *Momentum with momentum.* Displacements add as vectors, and vector addition is commutative. By property 2 of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-1|Principle §C2.2.1]], going from $A$ to $B$ by $\Delta x'\hat{\mathbf x}$ and then $\Delta y'\hat{\mathbf y}$ is the same as going the other way round: $\mathcal T(\Delta y'\hat{\mathbf y})\mathcal T(\Delta x'\hat{\mathbf x}) = \mathcal T(\Delta x'\hat{\mathbf x})\mathcal T(\Delta y'\hat{\mathbf y})$. Expand both finite translations ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-5|Theorem §C2.2.5]]) to second order:
>
> $$
> 0 = \Bigl[1 - \frac{ip_y\Delta y'}{\hbar} - \frac{p_y^2(\Delta y')^2}{2\hbar^2} + \cdots,\ 1 - \frac{ip_x\Delta x'}{\hbar} - \frac{p_x^2(\Delta x')^2}{2\hbar^2} + \cdots\Bigr] = -\frac{\Delta x'\,\Delta y'}{\hbar^2}\,[p_y, p_x] + \cdots ,
> $$
>
> since every term containing only $p_x$ or only $p_y$ commutes with the $1$ and with itself. $\Delta x'$ and $\Delta y'$ are arbitrary, so $[p_y, p_x] = 0$, and likewise for the other pairs. Then all the $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf l$ commute, and $e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf l/\hbar}e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf l'/\hbar} = e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf l + \mathbf l')/\hbar}$, which is item 3.
>
> *Items 1–2.* A simultaneous eigenket $|\psi\rangle$ of $x$ and $p_x$ would give $[x, p_x]|\psi\rangle = (x'p'_x - p'_xx')|\psi\rangle = 0$, contradicting $[x, p_x]|\psi\rangle = i\hbar|\psi\rangle$. The inequality is the generalized uncertainty relation with $\langle[x, p_x]\rangle = i\hbar$; its proof is the one at level B ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-4|Theorem §B2.4.4]]; Sakurai's $\langle(\Delta A)^2\rangle$ is level B's $\sigma_A^2$). Commuting observables have a complete set of simultaneous eigenkets ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-6|Theorem §B2.4.6]], [[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty]]).

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level B
> The relations are those of [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]] and [[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]], but their layer has changed. Level B postulated $\hat p = -i\hbar\,\partial_x$ and *computed* $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$ with the product rule of differentiation; $[\hat p_i, \hat p_j] = 0$ was the equality of mixed partials. Here the principles are the properties of translations and the identification $\mathbf K = \mathbf p/\hbar$. The commutators are theorems, and the differential operator is derived from them in [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]. Each commutator now has a geometric meaning: $[x, p] \ne 0$ because a translation changes where the particle is, and $[p_x, p_y] = 0$ because translations commute. Rotations about different axes do not commute, and the same argument will give $[J_x, J_y] = i\hbar J_z$ ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-4|Theorem §C5.1.4]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.4–1.6.5*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.2.7: How Finite Translations Act
> For every displacement $\mathbf l$, $\mathcal T(\mathbf l) = \exp(-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf l/\hbar)$ is unitary, $\mathcal T(\mathbf l)\mathcal T(\mathbf l') = \mathcal T(\mathbf l + \mathbf l')$, $\mathcal T(\mathbf l)^\dagger = \mathcal T(-\mathbf l)$, and:
> 1. $[x_i, \mathcal T(\mathbf l)] = l_i\,\mathcal T(\mathbf l)$ and $[\mathbf p, \mathcal T(\mathbf l)] = 0$; hence $\mathcal T^\dagger(\mathbf l)\,\mathbf x\,\mathcal T(\mathbf l) = \mathbf x + \mathbf l$ and $\mathcal T^\dagger(\mathbf l)\,\mathbf p\,\mathcal T(\mathbf l) = \mathbf p$, so $\langle\mathbf x\rangle \to \langle\mathbf x\rangle + \mathbf l$ and $\langle\mathbf p\rangle \to \langle\mathbf p\rangle$ in every state;
> 2. $\mathcal T(\mathbf l)|\mathbf x'\rangle = |\mathbf x' + \mathbf l\rangle$, and the translated wave function is $\langle\mathbf x'|\mathcal T(\mathbf l)|\alpha\rangle = \langle\mathbf x' - \mathbf l|\alpha\rangle$;
> 3. a momentum eigenket is an eigenket of every translation, $\mathcal T(\mathbf l)|\mathbf p'\rangle = e^{-i\mathbf p'\cdot\mathbf l/\hbar}|\mathbf p'\rangle$; a position eigenket is an eigenket of none. The eigenvalue is a complex phase, because $\mathcal T$ is unitary but not Hermitian.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.4, eqs. (1.226)–(1.227); Problems 1.32–1.33 · the user's 511 notes, §"Momentum Eigenkets" · the user's series, Part IV, §5.2, eq. (q-T-commutator)*

^thm-c2-2-7
> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\mathcal T(\mathbf l) = \mathcal T(|\mathbf l|\,\hat{\mathbf n})$ with $\hat{\mathbf n} = \mathbf l/|\mathbf l|$ is the exponential of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-5|Theorem §C2.2.5]]. The exponential of $-i$ times a Hermitian operator is unitary, with adjoint $\exp(+i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf l/\hbar) = \mathcal T(-\mathbf l)$. The group law is item 3 of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-6|Theorem §C2.2.6]].
>
> *1.* From $[x_i, p_j] = i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}$, the product rule and induction give $[x_i, F(\mathbf p)] = i\hbar\,\partial F/\partial p_i$ for every power series $F$. This is the argument of [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]], which uses only the commutator, not the differential operator; it is Sakurai Problem 1.31(a). With $F = e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf l/\hbar}$, $\partial F/\partial p_i = -\frac{il_i}{\hbar}F$, so $[x_i, \mathcal T(\mathbf l)] = l_i\mathcal T(\mathbf l)$. Multiplying on the left by $\mathcal T^\dagger$ gives $\mathcal T^\dagger x_i\mathcal T = x_i + l_i$. This is also the Baker–Hausdorff lemma in its terminating case ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]], case 1). $\mathbf p$ commutes with every function of $\mathbf p$. Taking expectation values in $\mathcal T(\mathbf l)|\alpha\rangle$ gives the rest.
>
> *2.* By 1, $x_i\,\mathcal T(\mathbf l)|\mathbf x'\rangle = \mathcal T(\mathbf l)(x_i + l_i)|\mathbf x'\rangle = (x'_i + l_i)\,\mathcal T(\mathbf l)|\mathbf x'\rangle$, so $\mathcal T(\mathbf l)|\mathbf x'\rangle$ is a position eigenket with eigenvalue $\mathbf x' + \mathbf l$. Unitarity preserves the delta normalization, so $\mathcal T(\mathbf l)|\mathbf x'\rangle = e^{i\theta}|\mathbf x' + \mathbf l\rangle$. The phase is $1$ because compounding infinitesimal translations, each with phase $1$ (the convention of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-1|Principle §C2.2.1]]), never produces one. The wave function follows from $\langle\mathbf x'|\mathcal T(\mathbf l) = \bigl(\mathcal T(-\mathbf l)|\mathbf x'\rangle\bigr)^\dagger = \langle\mathbf x' - \mathbf l|$.
>
> *3.* $\mathcal T(\mathbf l) = F(\mathbf p)$ acts on a simultaneous eigenket of the $p_i$ as $F(\mathbf p')$ ([[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^thm-c2-1-1|Theorem §C2.1.1]], last line). By 2, $\mathcal T(\mathbf l)$ moves every $|\mathbf x'\rangle$ to a different eigenket ($\mathbf l \neq 0$).

## Brackets, commutators and generators

> [!remark] Remark: Two roads to one commutator
> The same commutator can be reached by a second road, Dirac's (1925): keep the algebra of classical Poisson brackets and change its realization, $[A, B]_{\text{classical}} \to [A, B]/i\hbar$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^rem-b8-1-5|CM Remark: From brackets to commutators]]). Then $[x_i, p_j]_{\text{classical}} = \delta_{ij}$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-1|CM Theorem §B8.1.1]]) becomes Theorem §C2.2.6. The rule is plausible because both brackets share the algebra: antisymmetry, linearity, the Leibniz rule and the Jacobi identity ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-2|CM Theorem §B8.1.2]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-1|Theorem §B2.4.1]]; Sakurai's (1.232a–f) are these, and their proofs are the level-B ones). They differ in two ways, and the factor $i\hbar$ repairs both. The Poisson bracket has the dimension of the plain product divided by an action, because of its derivatives $\partial_q\partial_p$, and the $\hbar$ supplies the action. The Poisson bracket of real functions is real, while the commutator of Hermitian operators is anti-Hermitian, and the $i$ restores Hermiticity. The rule is a prescription, ambiguous for products such as $q^2p^2$ whose operator order is undetermined (Groenewold's theorem, stated in the user's series, Part IV, §2.2).
>
> Sakurai deliberately takes the translation road. It never uses a classical bracket, so it applies to observables without classical analogues: spin has nothing to do with $q$ and $p$, yet its commutators follow from rotations exactly as these followed from translations ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-4|Theorem §C5.1.4]]). The user's series takes both roads, the bracket in §2 and translations in §5, and reads their agreement as the first entry of a quantum Noether dictionary:
>
> | symmetry | generator | home |
> |---|---|---|
> | spatial translation | $\mathbf p$ | this section |
> | time translation | $H$ | [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^pr-c3-1-1\|Principle §C3.1.1]] |
> | rotation | $\mathbf J$ | [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2\|Principle §C5.1.2]] |
>
> In classical mechanics the same functions generate the same flows through Poisson brackets ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-5|CM Theorem §B8.1.5]], [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^rem-b8-1-4|CM Remark: Noether's theorem, both ways]]). Two facts about the algebra itself, whichever road leads to it. It has no finite-dimensional realization, since the trace of a commutator of matrices vanishes and the trace of $i\hbar\cdot1$ does not ([[§31 Trace꞉ A Connection Between Matrices and Operators#^ladr-8-57|LADR Thm. 8.57]]; Stony Brook PHY 511, HW 2, Problem 1). And up to unitary equivalence it has only one irreducible realization in its exponentiated (Weyl) form (the Stone–von Neumann theorem, stated in the user's series, Part IV, §2.4, after Hall ch. 14), which is why matrix mechanics and wave mechanics are one theory.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.5, eqs. (1.229)–(1.232f) · the user's series, Part IV, §2.2–2.4, §5.2 (Remark: Two roads to one commutator) · the user's 511 notes, §"Our Approach vs Dirac's Approach"*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C2.2.1: From the Commutator Back to Translations
> Sakurai Problem 1.30. (a) Evaluate the classical Poisson bracket $[x, F(p_x)]_{\text{classical}}$. (b) For the quantum operators, evaluate $[x, \exp(ip_xa/\hbar)]$. (c) Show that $\exp(ip_xa/\hbar)|x'\rangle$ is an eigenket of $x$, and find its eigenvalue.
>
> *(a)* $[x, F(p_x)]_{\text{classical}} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial x}\frac{\partial F}{\partial p_x} - \frac{\partial x}{\partial p_x}\frac{\partial F}{\partial x} = F'(p_x)$.
>
> *(b)* By $[x, F(p_x)] = i\hbar\,F'(p_x)$ (the proof of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-7|Theorem §C2.2.7]], 1), which is $i\hbar$ times the classical answer as Dirac's rule says, $[x, e^{ip_xa/\hbar}] = i\hbar\cdot\frac{ia}{\hbar}\,e^{ip_xa/\hbar} = -a\,e^{ip_xa/\hbar}$.
>
> *(c)* $x\,e^{ip_xa/\hbar}|x'\rangle = \bigl(e^{ip_xa/\hbar}x - a\,e^{ip_xa/\hbar}\bigr)|x'\rangle = (x' - a)\,e^{ip_xa/\hbar}|x'\rangle$: an eigenket with eigenvalue $x' - a$. Indeed $e^{ip_xa/\hbar} = \mathcal T(-a\hat{\mathbf x})$.
>
> *What it shows.* Here the logic runs backwards from the section's: the commutator alone, with no geometry assumed, makes $e^{ip_xa/\hbar}$ move position eigenkets rigidly. Hence if $x$ has one eigenvalue $x'$, it has $x' - a$ for every real $a$. Any operator pair with $[x, p_x] = i\hbar$ forces the spectrum of $x$ to be the whole real line, never a discrete set or a half-line. So the canonical commutator and the existence of translations are two forms of one assumption. The same algebra with the roles exchanged is Example §C2.2.2.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 1.30; solved here*

> [!example] Example §C2.2.2: Boosts, the Translations of Momentum
> Sakurai Problem 1.34. Define an infinitesimal boost $\mathcal B(d\mathbf p')$ by $\mathcal B(d\mathbf p')|\mathbf p'\rangle = |\mathbf p' + d\mathbf p'\rangle$, and try $\mathcal B(d\mathbf p') = 1 + i\mathbf W\cdot d\mathbf p'$ with $\mathbf W$ Hermitian.
>
> *Properties.* Exactly as in [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], with $-\mathbf K$ replaced by $\mathbf W$: $\mathcal B^\dagger\mathcal B = 1 + i(\mathbf W - \mathbf W^\dagger)\cdot d\mathbf p' + O(d\mathbf p'^2) = 1$, and two boosts compose to the boost by $d\mathbf p' + d\mathbf p''$, the inverse being $\mathcal B(-d\mathbf p')$.
>
> *The generator.* Mirror [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-3|Theorem §C2.2.3]]: $\mathbf p\,\mathcal B|\mathbf p'\rangle = (\mathbf p' + d\mathbf p')|\mathbf p' + d\mathbf p'\rangle$ and $\mathcal B\,\mathbf p|\mathbf p'\rangle = \mathbf p'|\mathbf p' + d\mathbf p'\rangle$, so $[\mathbf p, \mathcal B(d\mathbf p')] = d\mathbf p'$, that is $i[p_i, W_j] = \delta_{ij}$. $\mathbf W\cdot d\mathbf p'$ is dimensionless, so $\mathbf W$ has the dimension of length over action. The choice $\mathbf W = \mathbf x/\hbar$ satisfies $[p_i, x_j/\hbar] = -i\delta_{ij}$, which is the canonical commutation relation $[x_j, p_i] = i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}$ read backwards. Position generates translations in momentum:
>
> $$
> \mathcal B(d\mathbf p') = 1 + \frac{i\,\mathbf x\cdot d\mathbf p'}{\hbar}, \qquad \mathcal B(\boldsymbol\Xi) = e^{i\mathbf x\cdot\boldsymbol\Xi/\hbar}, \qquad \mathcal B^\dagger(\boldsymbol\Xi)\,\mathbf p\,\mathcal B(\boldsymbol\Xi) = \mathbf p + \boldsymbol\Xi .
> $$
>
> (The last relation is the Baker–Hausdorff lemma, [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]], case 1, with $G = -\mathbf x\cdot\boldsymbol\Xi/|\boldsymbol\Xi|$. The Stony Brook solutions compute the inverse, $e^{ibq}\,p\,e^{-ibq} = \mathcal B(\boldsymbol\Xi)\,p\,\mathcal B^\dagger(\boldsymbol\Xi) = p - b\hbar$ with $\Xi = b\hbar$.) The signs differ from the translation's, $1 - i\mathbf p\cdot d\mathbf x'/\hbar$, because $[p, x] = -[x, p]$.
>
> *Meaning.* On wave functions $\mathcal B(\boldsymbol\Xi)$ multiplies by the plane wave $e^{i\boldsymbol\Xi\cdot\mathbf x'/\hbar}$. It leaves $|\psi|^2$ and $\langle\mathbf x\rangle$ alone and raises $\langle\mathbf p\rangle$ by $\boldsymbol\Xi$: with $\boldsymbol\Xi = m\mathbf v$ it is the Galilean boost to velocity $\mathbf v$ at the instant $t = 0$ (compare [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^rem-b1-4-3|★ Remark: A moving packet is a boosted packet at rest]]). Its action on momentum-space wave functions, $\langle\mathbf p'|\mathcal B(\boldsymbol\Xi)|\alpha\rangle = \langle\mathbf p' - \boldsymbol\Xi|\alpha\rangle$, gives the position operator in the momentum basis ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problems 1.34, 1.36(b) · Stony Brook PHY 511 (Fall 2025), HW 4, Problem 1(b)–(c) and its solution; solved here*
