What is a wave function, once states are kets? Level B worked with wave functions from the start. It postulated how momentum acts on them ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]) and introduced the momentum-space wave function as a Fourier transform, in two normalizations, $\phi(k)$ and $\Phi(p)$ ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^def-b1-4-1|Def. §B1.4.1]], [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]], [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^thm-b2-3-6|Theorem §B2.3.6]]). Its Gaussian packets and minimum-uncertainty states are in [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-4|Theorem §B1.4.4]] and [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-5|Theorem §B2.4.5]]. At level C a wave function is a set of expansion coefficients, $\langle x'|\alpha\rangle$ or $\langle p'|\alpha\rangle$, and level B's postulate that momentum acts as $-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial x'$ becomes a theorem of the translation structure of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator|§C2.2]]. So do its level-B consequences, now with a new reason: a momentum eigenstate is a plane wave, and position acts as $i\hbar\,\partial/\partial p'$ (from the boost). New here are the ket form of the minimum-uncertainty condition, which gives Gaussians in both spaces without a Fourier integral, the Wigner function (PHY 511, HW 3), and momentum-space matrix elements of a potential.

> [!caution] Caution: Momentum-space notation and Fourier conventions
> Sakurai and the user's notes write the momentum-space wave function as $\phi_\alpha(p') = \langle p'|\alpha\rangle$. This is level B's $\Phi(p)$ ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]]), with $1/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$ in both directions of the transform. It is **not** level B's wave-number amplitude $\phi(k)$ ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^def-b1-4-1|Def. §B1.4.1]], $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ in both directions), which is
>
> $$
> \phi_{\text{B}}(k) = \sqrt\hbar\;\phi_\alpha(\hbar k) = \langle k'|\alpha\rangle\big|_{k' = k}, \qquad |k'\rangle = \sqrt\hbar\,|p' = \hbar k'\rangle
> $$
>
> ([[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^ex-c2-1-2|Example §C2.1.2]]). WO's transform carries $1/2\pi$ forward ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^cau-b1-4-1|Caution: Fourier conventions in QM and in WO]]). To avoid a clash with level B's $\phi(k)$, this vault writes Sakurai's momentum-space function as $\varphi_\alpha(p')$. Uncertainties: Sakurai's $\langle(\Delta A)^2\rangle$, with $\Delta A = A - \langle A\rangle$ an operator, is level B's $\sigma_A^2$.

## The position-space wave function

> [!definition] Definition §C2.3.1: Wave Functions; Eigenfunctions; Transformation Function
> For a state ket $|\alpha\rangle$ of a particle on a line:
> - its **wave function** (position-space wave function) is $\psi_\alpha(x') = \langle x'|\alpha\rangle$, its component along the position eigenket $|x'\rangle$;
> - its **momentum-space wave function** is $\varphi_\alpha(p') = \langle p'|\alpha\rangle$, its component along the momentum eigenket $|p'\rangle$, with $p|p'\rangle = p'|p'\rangle$, $\langle p'|p''\rangle = \delta(p' - p'')$;
> - the **eigenfunction** of an observable $A$ with eigenvalue $a'$ is $u_{a'}(x') = \langle x'|a'\rangle$, the wave function of the eigenket $|a'\rangle$;
> - the **transformation function** from the $x$- to the $p$-representation is $\langle x'|p'\rangle$, the continuous analogue of the transformation matrix between two bases ([[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence]]). Read as a function of $x'$ at fixed $p'$, it is the wave function of $|p'\rangle$.
>
> In three dimensions, $\psi_\alpha(\mathbf x') = \langle\mathbf x'|\alpha\rangle$ and $\varphi_\alpha(\mathbf p') = \langle\mathbf p'|\alpha\rangle$. $|\psi_\alpha(x')|^2\,dx'$ and $|\varphi_\alpha(p')|^2\,dp'$ are the probabilities of finding position in $dx'$ and momentum in $dp'$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.7.1, eqs. (1.233)–(1.237), (1.241); §1.7.3, eqs. (1.254)–(1.258) · the user's 511 notes, §"Position-Space Wave Function", §"Momentum-Space Wave Function"*

^def-c2-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.1: Inner Products and Matrix Elements in the Position Basis
> 1. $\displaystyle\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle = \int dx'\,\psi_\beta^*(x')\,\psi_\alpha(x')$: the inner product is the overlap integral of the wave functions.
> 2. If $\{|a'\rangle\}$ is a complete orthonormal set with $|\alpha\rangle = \sum_{a'}c_{a'}|a'\rangle$, then $\psi_\alpha(x') = \sum_{a'}c_{a'}\,u_{a'}(x')$, with $c_{a'} = \langle a'|\alpha\rangle$.
> 3. For any operator $A$, $\langle\beta|A|\alpha\rangle = \int dx'\int dx''\,\psi_\beta^*(x')\,\langle x'|A|x''\rangle\,\psi_\alpha(x'')$.
> 4. For a function of position, $\langle x'|f(x)|x''\rangle = f(x')\,\delta(x' - x'')$, and the double integral collapses:
>
> $$
> \langle\beta|f(x)|\alpha\rangle = \int dx'\,\psi_\beta^*(x')\,f(x')\,\psi_\alpha(x') ,
> $$
>
>    where $f(x)$ on the left is an operator and $f(x')$ on the right a number.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.7.1, eqs. (1.238)–(1.246) · the user's 511 notes, §"Overlap Integral", §"Eigenfunction Expansion", §"Expectation Values"*

^thm-c2-3-1
> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert $\int dx'\,|x'\rangle\langle x'| = 1$ ([[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^pr-c2-1-2|Principle §C2.1.2]]) once for 1 and twice for 3, and use $\langle\beta|x'\rangle = \langle x'|\beta\rangle^*$. For 2, apply $\langle x'|$ to $|\alpha\rangle = \sum_{a'}|a'\rangle\langle a'|\alpha\rangle$. For 4, use the last line of [[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^thm-c2-1-1|Theorem §C2.1.1]] with $\xi = x$ (Sakurai's example is $f(x) = x^2$, which appears in the oscillator), then integrate over $x''$ against the delta.

^der-c2-3-1
*Uses:* [[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^pr-c2-1-2|Principle §C2.1.2]], [[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^thm-c2-1-1|Theorem §C2.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: One probability rule, not two
> Level B *defined* the inner product of states as the overlap integral ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-2|Def. §B2.1.2]]). Here $\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle$ is defined abstractly, and its equality with the overlap integral follows from the completeness of $|x'\rangle$. Elementary wave mechanics also presents two probability rules: $|c_{a'}|^2$ for the outcome $a'$, and $|\psi(x')|^2$ for position. In Dirac's formalism they are one rule, since $\psi_\alpha(x')$ *is* an expansion coefficient, in the position basis, exactly as $c_{a'}$ is one in the $A$ basis. Level B reached the same unification by reading its earlier rules as cases of the generalized statistical interpretation ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^rem-b2-3-3|Remark: Level A's rules as special cases]]). A one-line consequence (Sakurai Problem 1.6(d)): sandwiching $\sum_{a'}|a'\rangle\langle a'| = 1$ between $\langle x'|$ and $|x''\rangle$ gives $\sum_{a'}u_{a'}(x')\,u_{a'}^*(x'') = \delta(x' - x'')$, read rigorously in [[§26 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^rem-26-4|556 Remark: How the Discrete and Continuous Relations Fit Together]].
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.7.1*

## Momentum in the position basis

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.2: Momentum in the Position Basis
> For every state $|\alpha\rangle$,
>
> $$
> p\,|\alpha\rangle = \int dx'\,|x'\rangle\Bigl(-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x'}\langle x'|\alpha\rangle\Bigr), \qquad \langle x'|p|\alpha\rangle = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x'}\,\psi_\alpha(x'), \qquad \langle x'|p|x''\rangle = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x'}\,\delta(x' - x'') ,
> $$
>
> so that
>
> $$
> \langle\beta|p|\alpha\rangle = \int dx'\,\psi_\beta^*(x')\Bigl(-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x'}\Bigr)\psi_\alpha(x'), \qquad \langle x'|p^n|\alpha\rangle = (-i\hbar)^n\frac{\partial^n}{\partial x'^n}\,\psi_\alpha(x') .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.7.2, eqs. (1.247)–(1.253) · the user's 511 notes, §"Momentum Operator in the Position Basis" · the user's series, Part IV, §5.3*

^thm-c2-3-2
> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Compute the infinitesimal translation of $|\alpha\rangle$ in two ways. By [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-4|Principle §C2.2.4]] it is $\bigl(1 - ip\,\Delta x'/\hbar\bigr)|\alpha\rangle$. By [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]] and Taylor's theorem,
>
> $$
> \mathcal T(\Delta x')|\alpha\rangle = \int dx'\,|x' + \Delta x'\rangle\langle x'|\alpha\rangle = \int dx'\,|x'\rangle\langle x' - \Delta x'|\alpha\rangle = \int dx'\,|x'\rangle\Bigl(\langle x'|\alpha\rangle - \Delta x'\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x'}\langle x'|\alpha\rangle\Bigr)
> $$
>
> to first order. The zeroth-order terms agree by completeness. Equating the first-order terms, $-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Delta x'\,p|\alpha\rangle = -\Delta x'\int dx'\,|x'\rangle\,\partial_{x'}\langle x'|\alpha\rangle$, which is the first formula. Apply $\langle x''|$ and use $\langle x''|x'\rangle = \delta(x'' - x')$ for the second. Put $|\alpha\rangle = |x''\rangle$, whose wave function is $\delta(x' - x'')$, for the third. Sandwich with $\langle\beta|$ ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], 1) for the matrix element. For powers, apply the second formula to the ket $p^{n-1}|\alpha\rangle$, whose wave function is $(-i\hbar\partial_{x'})^{n-1}\psi_\alpha$ by induction.

> [!remark] Remark: From postulate to theorem, and what fixes the form
> At levels A and B, $\hat p = -i\hbar\,\partial_x$ and the "sandwich" rule for $\langle p\rangle$ were postulated ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]], [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^pr-b1-2-2|Principle §B1.2.2]]), or reached through one of the routes compared in [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^rem-b1-2-1|Remark: One assumption, three ways to make it]]. Here they are derived: in Sakurai's words, (1.251) "is not a postulate; rather, it has been derived using the basic properties of momentum". What was assumed instead is the content of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator|§C2.2]]: translations exist, have the stated properties, and are generated by $\mathbf p/\hbar$. One more ingredient is easy to miss, the phase convention $\mathcal T(d\mathbf x')|\mathbf x'\rangle = |\mathbf x' + d\mathbf x'\rangle$. Had the position kets been rephased, $|x'\rangle^\sim = e^{i\chi(x')}|x'\rangle$ (still delta-normalized eigenkets of $x$), the wave functions would become $\psi^\sim = e^{-i\chi}\psi$, and the same $p$ would act on them as $-i\hbar\,\partial_{x'} + \hbar\chi'(x')$. The form $-i\hbar\,\partial_{x'}$ belongs to a choice of phases. Changing it is a gauge transformation ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]]). That $p$ is Hermitian, checked at level B by an integration by parts ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]), is here built in from the unitarity of translations ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.7.2, after eq. (1.251); the rephasing is worked out here*

## Momentum eigenfunctions and the momentum-space wave function

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.3: Momentum Eigenfunctions and the Fourier Pair
> With the transformation function chosen real and positive at $x' = 0$,
>
> $$
> \langle x'|p'\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{ip'x'}{\hbar}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> a plane wave of wavelength $2\pi\hbar/p'$. The two wave functions of a state are Fourier transforms of each other:
>
> $$
> \psi_\alpha(x') = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int dp'\,\exp\Bigl(\frac{ip'x'}{\hbar}\Bigr)\varphi_\alpha(p'), \qquad \varphi_\alpha(p') = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int dx'\,\exp\Bigl(\frac{-ip'x'}{\hbar}\Bigr)\psi_\alpha(x') ,
> $$
>
> and $\int dp'\,|\varphi_\alpha(p')|^2 = \int dx'\,|\psi_\alpha(x')|^2 = 1$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.7.3, eqs. (1.259)–(1.266b) · the user's 511 notes, §"Transformation Function" · the user's series, Part IV, §5.3–5.4*

^thm-c2-3-3
> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $|\alpha\rangle = |p'\rangle$ in [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]: $p'\langle x'|p'\rangle = -i\hbar\,\partial_{x'}\langle x'|p'\rangle$, with solution $\langle x'|p'\rangle = N\,e^{ip'x'/\hbar}$. To normalize, insert the momentum closure relation $\int dp'\,|p'\rangle\langle p'| = 1$ (the $|p'\rangle$ are complete, the eigenkets of an observable, [[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^thm-c2-1-1|Theorem §C2.1.1]]) between two position kets:
>
> $$
> \delta(x' - x'') = \langle x'|x''\rangle = \int dp'\,\langle x'|p'\rangle\langle p'|x''\rangle = |N|^2\int dp'\,e^{ip'(x' - x'')/\hbar} = 2\pi\hbar\,|N|^2\,\delta(x' - x'') ,
> $$
>
> by the Fourier representation of the delta function and its scaling rule ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|WO Theorem §B4.4.3]], parts 2 and 4). So $|N| = 1/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$, and the convention makes $N$ positive. (The phase of $N$ could depend on $p'$. The convention fixes it, and it agrees with $\mathcal B(\Xi)|p'\rangle = |p' + \Xi\rangle$ in [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]].) The Fourier pair is $\langle x'|\alpha\rangle = \int dp'\,\langle x'|p'\rangle\langle p'|\alpha\rangle$ and $\langle p'|\alpha\rangle = \int dx'\,\langle p'|x'\rangle\langle x'|\alpha\rangle$, with $\langle p'|x'\rangle = \langle x'|p'\rangle^*$. The norms agree because both equal $\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle$ ([[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^thm-c2-1-1|Theorem §C2.1.1]], fourth line), which is Plancherel's identity ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-4|WO Theorem §B4.4.4]]) obtained without analysis.

*Uses:* [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]], [[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^thm-c2-1-1|Theorem §C2.1.1]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|WO Theorem §B4.4.3]]

Level B obtained the same plane waves by solving the eigenvalue equation of the postulated $-i\hbar\,d/dx$ ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]) and defined $\Phi(p)$ as a Fourier transform ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]]). Here the differential equation comes from translations, and the Fourier transform is the change of basis between two continuous bases: "the mathematics we have developed somehow 'knows' Fourier's work on integral transforms" (Sakurai). The plane wave appears without the Schrödinger equation, which has not yet been written down ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation|§C3.1]]). Its wavelength $h/p'$ restates the identification $\mathbf K = \mathbf p/\hbar$ ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^rem-c2-2-1|Remark: Why momentum, and why this constant]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.4: Position in the Momentum Basis
> The boost $\mathcal B(\Xi) = e^{ix\Xi/\hbar}$ translates momentum eigenkets, $\mathcal B(\Xi)|p'\rangle = |p' + \Xi\rangle$, and position acts on momentum-space wave functions as a derivative:
>
> $$
> \langle p'|x|\alpha\rangle = i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial p'}\,\varphi_\alpha(p'), \qquad \langle p'|x|p''\rangle = i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial p'}\,\delta(p' - p''), \qquad \langle\beta|x|\alpha\rangle = \int dp'\,\varphi_\beta^*(p')\,i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial p'}\,\varphi_\alpha(p') .
> $$
>
> In the momentum representation $p$ multiplies and $x$ differentiates. The formulas are those of the position representation with $x \leftrightarrow p$ and $i \to -i$, the sign coming from $[p, x] = -[x, p]$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problems 1.34, 1.36 · the user's series, Part IV, §5.4 · the user's midterm formula sheet ("Position operator in momentum basis")*

^thm-c2-3-4
> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The boost moves momentum kets exactly.* In the position basis, $\langle x'|e^{ix\Xi/\hbar}|p'\rangle = e^{ix'\Xi/\hbar}\langle x'|p'\rangle = e^{i(p' + \Xi)x'/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar} = \langle x'|p' + \Xi\rangle$ by [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]] (and [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], 4, for the function $e^{ix\Xi/\hbar}$ of position). Completeness of $|x'\rangle$ gives $\mathcal B(\Xi)|p'\rangle = |p' + \Xi\rangle$, phase included. This is the finite form of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^ex-c2-2-2|Example §C2.2.2]].
>
> *Derivative.* Mirror [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]. To first order in $\Delta p'$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(1 + \frac{ix\,\Delta p'}{\hbar}\Bigr)|\alpha\rangle = \int dp'\,|p' + \Delta p'\rangle\langle p'|\alpha\rangle = \int dp'\,|p'\rangle\langle p' - \Delta p'|\alpha\rangle = \int dp'\,|p'\rangle\Bigl(\varphi_\alpha(p') - \Delta p'\,\frac{\partial\varphi_\alpha}{\partial p'}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> The first-order terms give $x|\alpha\rangle = \int dp'\,|p'\rangle\,i\hbar\,\partial_{p'}\varphi_\alpha(p')$. Apply $\langle p''|$, or put $|\alpha\rangle = |p''\rangle$, or sandwich with $\langle\beta|$, as before. Level B obtained the same formula by differentiating the Fourier integral under the integral sign ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^thm-b2-3-6|Theorem §B2.3.6]]); here it follows from the symmetry of the commutator.

## Minimum-uncertainty kets

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.5: Minimum-Uncertainty Kets
> A normalized state $|\alpha\rangle$ of a particle on a line has $\langle(\Delta x)^2\rangle\langle(\Delta p)^2\rangle = \hbar^2/4$ if and only if it is an eigenket of the non-Hermitian operator $x + i\gamma p$ for some real $\gamma \neq 0$:
>
> $$
> (x + i\gamma p)\,|\alpha\rangle = \mu\,|\alpha\rangle .
> $$
>
> For such a ket, necessarily $\gamma > 0$, $\mu = \langle x\rangle + i\gamma\langle p\rangle$, and
>
> $$
> \langle(\Delta x)^2\rangle = \frac{\gamma\hbar}{2}, \qquad \langle(\Delta p)^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2\gamma}, \qquad \langle\{\Delta x, \Delta p\}\rangle = 0 .
> $$
>
> In the position basis these are exactly the Gaussians $\psi_\alpha(x') \propto \exp\bigl[i\langle p\rangle x'/\hbar - (x' - \langle x\rangle)^2/2\gamma\hbar\bigr]$, those of [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-5|Theorem §B2.4.5]] with $a = 1/\gamma$. For $\gamma = 1/m\omega$, $x + i\gamma p$ is $\sqrt{2\hbar/m\omega}$ times the oscillator's lowering operator $a$, and the minimum-uncertainty kets of that width are the coherent states ([[§C3.4 The Harmonic Oscillator and Coherent States Revisited#^thm-c3-4-3|Theorem §C3.4.3]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 1.20(b)–(c) · the user's series, Part IV, §4 (minimum-uncertainty condition) and §5.5 · the user's 511 notes, §"Gaussian Wave Packets" · PHY 511, HW 3, Problem 3 (note at the end)*

^thm-c2-3-5
> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *If.* Taking the expectation value of the eigenvalue equation gives $\mu = \langle x\rangle + i\gamma\langle p\rangle$, since $\langle x\rangle$ and $\langle p\rangle$ are real. So $(\Delta x + i\gamma\Delta p)|\alpha\rangle = 0$. Write $\Delta x\Delta p = \frac12\{\Delta x, \Delta p\} + \frac12[x, p]$ with $[x, p] = i\hbar$ ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-6|Theorem §C2.2.6]]), and note that $\langle\{\Delta x, \Delta p\}\rangle$ is real (the anticommutator of Hermitian operators is Hermitian). Applying $\langle\alpha|\Delta x$ to the equation,
>
> $$
> 0 = \langle(\Delta x)^2\rangle + i\gamma\langle\Delta x\Delta p\rangle = \Bigl(\langle(\Delta x)^2\rangle - \frac{\gamma\hbar}{2}\Bigr) + \frac{i\gamma}{2}\langle\{\Delta x, \Delta p\}\rangle .
> $$
>
> The real and imaginary parts give $\langle(\Delta x)^2\rangle = \gamma\hbar/2$ and $\langle\{\Delta x, \Delta p\}\rangle = 0$. Since $\langle(\Delta x)^2\rangle = \|\Delta x|\alpha\rangle\|^2 > 0$ for a normalizable state (otherwise $|\alpha\rangle$ would be an eigenvector of $x$, and there are none: [[§26 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-26-3|556 Prop. §26.3]]), $\gamma > 0$: for $\gamma < 0$ there is no eigenket at all. Applying $\langle\alpha|\Delta p$ instead, $0 = \langle\Delta p\Delta x\rangle + i\gamma\langle(\Delta p)^2\rangle = \frac12\langle\{\Delta x, \Delta p\}\rangle - \frac{i\hbar}{2} + i\gamma\langle(\Delta p)^2\rangle$, so $\langle(\Delta p)^2\rangle = \hbar/2\gamma$. The product is $\hbar^2/4$.
>
> *Only if.* Equality in the uncertainty relation requires equality in the Schwarz inequality and a vanishing anticommutator term in its proof. That forces $\Delta p|\alpha\rangle = c\,\Delta x|\alpha\rangle$ with $c$ purely imaginary. The argument is level B's ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-5|Theorem §B2.4.5]], derivation), in Sakurai's form (Problem 1.20(b)). $c \neq 0$, since otherwise $\langle(\Delta p)^2\rangle = 0$ and the product would vanish. With $c = i/\gamma$, $(\Delta x + i\gamma\Delta p)|\alpha\rangle = 0$, the eigenvalue equation with $\mu = \langle x\rangle + i\gamma\langle p\rangle$.
>
> *Position basis.* By [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]] the equation reads $(x' + \gamma\hbar\,\partial_{x'})\psi_\alpha = \mu\psi_\alpha$. This is a first-order linear equation, $\partial_{x'}\ln\psi_\alpha = (\mu - x')/\gamma\hbar$, whose solutions are the stated Gaussians, normalizable exactly when $\gamma > 0$. *Oscillator.* $a = \sqrt{m\omega/2\hbar}\,(x + ip/m\omega)$ ([[§C3.4 The Harmonic Oscillator and Coherent States Revisited|§C3.4]]).

*Uses:* [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-6|Theorem §C2.2.6]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-5|Theorem §B2.4.5]], [[§26 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-26-3|556 Prop. §26.3]], [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level B
> [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-5|Theorem §B2.4.5]] characterized the minimal states by solving a differential equation in the position basis, and needed normalizability to exclude the wrong sign of its constant. Here the characterization is basis-free: minimal states are the eigenkets of $x + i\gamma p$, and the operator algebra alone forces $\gamma > 0$. Because the condition is an operator equation, it can be solved in any basis. In the momentum basis it gives the Gaussian $\varphi_\alpha$ directly ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^ex-c2-3-1|Example §C2.3.1]]), and in the number basis of the oscillator it gives the coherent-state expansion ([[§C3.4 The Harmonic Oscillator and Coherent States Revisited|§C3.4]]). The width $\gamma$ is free. Only for $\gamma = 1/m\omega$ does the state keep its minimal product while it evolves in an oscillator of frequency $\omega$; other widths are squeezed relative to that oscillator.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 1.20 · the user's series, Part IV, §5.5*

## Three dimensions

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.3.6: Wave Functions in Three Dimensions
> With base kets $\mathbf x|\mathbf x'\rangle = \mathbf x'|\mathbf x'\rangle$ or $\mathbf p|\mathbf p'\rangle = \mathbf p'|\mathbf p'\rangle$, normalized and complete as
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf x''\rangle = \delta^3(\mathbf x' - \mathbf x''), \quad \langle\mathbf p'|\mathbf p''\rangle = \delta^3(\mathbf p' - \mathbf p''), \quad \int d^3x'\,|\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x'| = \int d^3p'\,|\mathbf p'\rangle\langle\mathbf p'| = 1 ,
> $$
>
> momentum acts on wave functions as $-i\hbar$ times the gradient,
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf p|\alpha\rangle = -i\hbar\nabla'\psi_\alpha(\mathbf x'), \qquad \langle\beta|\mathbf p|\alpha\rangle = \int d^3x'\,\psi_\beta^*(\mathbf x')\,(-i\hbar\nabla')\,\psi_\alpha(\mathbf x') ,
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf p'\rangle = \frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{3/2}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{i\mathbf p'\cdot\mathbf x'}{\hbar}\Bigr), \qquad \varphi_\alpha(\mathbf p') = \frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{3/2}}\int d^3x'\,\exp\Bigl(\frac{-i\mathbf p'\cdot\mathbf x'}{\hbar}\Bigr)\psi_\alpha(\mathbf x') ,
> $$
>
> with the inverse transform carrying $e^{+i\mathbf p'\cdot\mathbf x'/\hbar}$. A wave function in one dimension has the dimension $(\text{length})^{-1/2}$, and in three dimensions $(\text{length})^{-3/2}$, since $|\psi_\alpha|^2$ integrated over a line or a volume is dimensionless.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.7.5, eqs. (1.275)–(1.283b) · the user's 511 notes, §"Generalization to Three Dimensions"*

^thm-c2-3-6
> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Run the derivation of [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]] with $\mathcal T(\Delta x'_j\,\hat{\mathbf e}_j)$ for each axis: the shifted wave function $\psi_\alpha(\mathbf x' - \Delta x'_j\hat{\mathbf e}_j)$ produces $-i\hbar\,\partial\psi_\alpha/\partial x'_j$. Since the $p_j$ commute ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-6|Theorem §C2.2.6]]), $|\mathbf p'\rangle$ is a simultaneous eigenket, and its wave function satisfies $-i\hbar\,\partial_j\langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf p'\rangle = p'_j\langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf p'\rangle$ for $j = 1, 2, 3$. The solution is the product of three one-dimensional plane waves. It is normalized because $\delta^3$ is the product of three one-dimensional deltas ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|EM Def. §B1.2.1]]) and the normalization of [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]] holds factor by factor. The transforms are the closure relations inserted, as in one dimension.

^der-c2-3-6
*Uses:* [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]], [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]], [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-6|Theorem §C2.2.6]], [[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|EM Def. §B1.2.1]]

Level B postulated $\hat{\vec p} = -i\hbar\nabla$ ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^def-b5-1-1|Def. §B5.1.1]]). Here it is derived. With it, the kinetic energy $\mathbf p^2/2m$ becomes $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla'^2$ on wave functions, which turns the Schrödinger equation for kets into the wave equation ([[§C3.5 Wave Mechanics, the Linear Potential and WKB Revisited|§C3.5]]). The same move with rotations in place of translations gives $L_z = -i\hbar\,\partial/\partial\phi$ ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-1|Theorem §C5.4.1]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C2.3.1: The Gaussian Packet in Both Bases, Without a Fourier Integral
> PHY 511, HW 3, Problem 3 asks for $\langle p\rangle$, $\langle p^2\rangle$ and $\langle(\Delta p)^2\rangle$ of Sakurai's Gaussian packet
>
> $$
> \psi_\alpha(x') = \frac{1}{\pi^{1/4}\sqrt d}\exp\Bigl(ikx' - \frac{x'^2}{2d^2}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> computed (a) in the position basis and (b) from the momentum-space wave function, and (c) a check that the product of uncertainties is minimal. The direct computations are Gaussian integrals: this is the packet of [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-4|Theorem §B1.4.4]] at $t = 0$ with $\sigma = d/\sqrt2$, $k_0 = k$, $x_0 = 0$, whose item 1 already gives $\langle p\rangle = \hbar k$ and $\sigma_p = \hbar/2\sigma$ by Fourier transform. The ket route avoids every integral except the normalization.
>
> *The eigenvalue equation.* By [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-2|Theorem §C2.3.2]], $\langle x'|(x + i\gamma p)|\alpha\rangle = (x' + \gamma\hbar\,\partial_{x'})\psi_\alpha = \bigl[x'(1 - \gamma\hbar/d^2) + i\gamma\hbar k\bigr]\psi_\alpha$. For $\gamma = d^2/\hbar$ the $x'$-dependence cancels:
>
> $$
> \Bigl(x + \frac{id^2}{\hbar}\,p\Bigr)|\alpha\rangle = id^2k\,|\alpha\rangle .
> $$
>
> *(a) and (c).* [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-5|Theorem §C2.3.5]] now gives everything. $\mu = \langle x\rangle + i\gamma\langle p\rangle = id^2k$, so $\langle x\rangle = 0$ and $\langle p\rangle = \hbar k$. Then $\langle(\Delta x)^2\rangle = \gamma\hbar/2 = d^2/2$, $\langle(\Delta p)^2\rangle = \hbar/2\gamma = \hbar^2/2d^2$, hence $\langle p^2\rangle = \hbar^2/2d^2 + \hbar^2k^2$ and $\langle(\Delta x)^2\rangle\langle(\Delta p)^2\rangle = \hbar^2/4$. These are Sakurai's (1.268)–(1.273), with no integral done.
>
> *(b), momentum space.* By [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-4|Theorem §C2.3.4]], the same ket equation in the $p$-basis reads $(i\hbar\,\partial_{p'} + i\gamma p')\varphi_\alpha = \mu\varphi_\alpha$, that is
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\varphi_\alpha}{\partial p'} = \Bigl(-\frac{i\mu}{\hbar} - \frac{\gamma p'}{\hbar}\Bigr)\varphi_\alpha = \frac{d^2}{\hbar^2}(\hbar k - p')\,\varphi_\alpha \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \varphi_\alpha(p') = N\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(p' - \hbar k)^2d^2}{2\hbar^2}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Normalization gives $|N|^2\sqrt\pi\,\hbar/d = 1$. The eigenvalue equation fixes $\varphi_\alpha$ only up to a constant, so the phase of $N$ comes from one value of the transform of [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]]: at $p' = \hbar k$ the integrand is the positive function $e^{-x'^2/2d^2}$, so $N > 0$ and $N = \sqrt{d/\hbar\sqrt\pi}$, Sakurai's (1.274). Its moments, $\int p'|\varphi_\alpha|^2dp' = \hbar k$ and $\int p'^2|\varphi_\alpha|^2dp' = \hbar^2k^2 + \hbar^2/2d^2$, agree with (a), as HW 3 asks.
>
> *Limits.* As $d \to \infty$, $\psi_\alpha$ becomes a plane wave and $\varphi_\alpha$ a spike at $\hbar k$. As $d \to 0$, $\psi_\alpha$ becomes a spike and $\varphi_\alpha$ flat: a sharply localized state superposes all momenta, including relativistic ones, $p' \gtrsim mc$, where nonrelativistic quantum mechanics stops being valid ([[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^pr-c2-1-2|Principle §C2.1.2]], domain).
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 3, Problem 3 · Sakurai §1.7.4, eqs. (1.267)–(1.274); Problems 1.20(c), 1.35 · the user's 511 notes, §"Gaussian Wave Packets"; solved here*

> [!example] Example §C2.3.2: The Wigner Function
> PHY 511, HW 3, Problem 4 (there with $\hbar = 1$). For a wave function $\psi(x)$ with momentum-space wave function $\varphi(p)$, the **Wigner function** (phase-space distribution) is
>
> $$
> W(x, p) = \frac{1}{\pi\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,\psi^*(x + y)\,\psi(x - y)\,e^{2ipy/\hbar} ,
> $$
>
> where $x$ and $p$ are numbers, not operators. $W$ is real: substituting $y \to -y$ turns it into its complex conjugate. Show (a) $\int W\,dp = |\psi(x)|^2$, (b) $\int W\,dx = |\varphi(p)|^2$, (c) $|\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle|^2 = 2\pi\hbar\iint W_1W_2\,dx\,dp$.
>
> *(a)* $\int dp\,e^{2ipy/\hbar} = 2\pi\hbar\,\delta(2y) = \pi\hbar\,\delta(y)$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|WO Theorem §B4.4.3]], parts 2 and 4). The $y$-integral then sets $y = 0$: $\int W\,dp = \psi^*(x)\psi(x)$.
>
> *(b)* Change variables to $u = x + y$, $v = x - y$, with Jacobian $|\partial(x, y)/\partial(u, v)| = \frac12$ and $2y = u - v$:
>
> $$
> \int W\,dx = \frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\int du\int dv\,\psi^*(u)\,\psi(v)\,e^{ip(u - v)/\hbar} = \Bigl[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int du\,\psi(u)e^{-ipu/\hbar}\Bigr]^*\Bigl[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int dv\,\psi(v)e^{-ipv/\hbar}\Bigr] = |\varphi(p)|^2 ,
> $$
>
> by the Fourier pair of [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-3|Theorem §C2.3.3]].
>
> *(c)* The $p$-integral of $W_1W_2$ gives $\int dp\,e^{2ip(y + y')/\hbar} = \pi\hbar\,\delta(y + y')$, leaving
>
> $$
> \iint W_1W_2\,dx\,dp = \frac{1}{\pi\hbar}\int dx\int dy\,\psi_1^*(x + y)\,\psi_1(x - y)\,\psi_2^*(x - y)\,\psi_2(x + y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\,\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle\,\langle\psi_2|\psi_1\rangle ,
> $$
>
> after the same change of variables ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-1|Theorem §C2.3.1]], 1).
>
> *Meaning.* $W$ has the marginals of a joint distribution of position and momentum, yet it is not one. If $\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle = 0$, (c) gives $\iint W_1W_2 = 0$, so for overlapping orthogonal states one of the two must be negative somewhere. For the Gaussian of Example §C2.3.1 the integral is the one done there, and
>
> $$
> W(x, p) = \frac{1}{\pi\hbar}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{x^2}{d^2} - \frac{d^2(p - \hbar k)^2}{\hbar^2}\Bigr] \ \ge 0 ,
> $$
>
> a Gaussian hump on the phase-space point $(0, \hbar k)$ with widths $d/\sqrt2$ and $\hbar/\sqrt2\,d$. Check (c) with $\psi_1 = \psi_2$: $2\pi\hbar\iint W^2 = 2\pi\hbar\cdot\frac{1}{\pi^2\hbar^2}\cdot\sqrt{\pi/2}\,d\cdot\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\hbar/d = 1$. A superposition of two such packets (figure) adds an oscillating, partly negative term midway between them. The fringes are invisible in $|\psi(x)|^2$ and appear in $|\varphi(p)|^2$. The Wigner function belongs with density matrices ([[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^rem-c11-2-4|★ Remark: The Wigner function of a density operator]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 3, Problem 4; solved here with $\hbar$ restored*

> [!example] Example §C2.3.3: A Central Potential Between Momentum Eigenkets
> Sakurai Problem 1.29. (a) If $A|a'\rangle = a'|a'\rangle$ and the transformation matrix to a basis $\{|b'\rangle\}$ is known, then $\langle b''|f(A)|b'\rangle = \sum_{a'}f(a')\,\langle b''|a'\rangle\langle a'|b'\rangle$, by inserting $\sum_{a'}|a'\rangle\langle a'|$ and using $f(A)|a'\rangle = f(a')|a'\rangle$. (b) Evaluate $\langle\mathbf p''|F(r)|\mathbf p'\rangle$ with $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$, where $x$, $y$, $z$ are operators.
>
> *(b)* The continuous version of (a), with $A \to \mathbf x$ and [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]], gives
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf p''|F(r)|\mathbf p'\rangle = \int d^3x'\,\langle\mathbf p''|\mathbf x'\rangle F(r')\langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf p'\rangle = \frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^3}\int d^3x'\,F(r')\,e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x'/\hbar}, \qquad \mathbf q = \mathbf p'' - \mathbf p' .
> $$
>
> A potential depends on position only, so its momentum matrix element depends only on the momentum transfer $\mathbf q$: it is the Fourier transform of the potential. For a central $F$, take the polar axis along $\mathbf q$. The angular integral is $2\pi\int_{-1}^1e^{-iqr'u/\hbar}du = 4\pi\,\sin(qr'/\hbar)\big/(qr'/\hbar)$, so
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf p''|F(r)|\mathbf p'\rangle = \frac{1}{2\pi^2\hbar^2q}\int_0^\infty r\,F(r)\,\sin\frac{qr}{\hbar}\,dr, \qquad q = |\mathbf p'' - \mathbf p'| .
> $$
>
> *Yukawa and Coulomb.* For $F = e^{-\kappa r}/r$, $\int_0^\infty e^{-\kappa r}\sin(qr/\hbar)\,dr = (q/\hbar)\big/(\kappa^2 + q^2/\hbar^2)$, and
>
> $$
> \Bigl\langle\mathbf p''\Big|\frac{e^{-\kappa r}}{r}\Big|\mathbf p'\Bigr\rangle = \frac{1}{2\pi^2\hbar}\,\frac{1}{q^2 + \hbar^2\kappa^2} \ \xrightarrow{\ \kappa \to 0\ }\ \frac{1}{2\pi^2\hbar\,q^2} .
> $$
>
> The Coulomb potential $-Ze^2/r$ (Gaussian units) therefore has momentum-space matrix element $-Ze^2/2\pi^2\hbar q^2$: the $1/q^2$ that becomes Rutherford's cross-section in the Born approximation ([[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^ex-c10-1-1|Example §C10.1.1]]). The bare Coulomb transform is defined as the $\kappa \to 0$ limit, since $\int r\,\sin(qr/\hbar)\,dr/r$ does not converge absolutely.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 1.29; solved here*
