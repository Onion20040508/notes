How does the ket formalism of Chapter C1, built on finite sums over eigenkets, handle an observable such as position, whose values fill a continuum? Level B already has the answer in function language: Dirac orthonormality and the continuous resolution of the identity ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^def-b2-2-3|Def. §B2.2.3]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]]), and the probability density of a continuous outcome ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]], part 3). Functional Analysis is the rigorous home: position has no eigenvectors in $L^2$, point evaluation is not a bra, and $\int_B|x\rangle\langle x|\,dx$ is a genuine projection ([[§37 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-37-3|556 Prop. §37.3]], [[§37 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-37-4|556 Prop. §37.4]], [[§37 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-37-5|556 Prop. §37.5]]). What level C adds is Sakurai's working formalism for an abstract ket space: a single dictionary that turns every discrete formula into its continuous form, the position eigenkets $|x'\rangle$ as the postulated base kets from which wave functions will be built, and the state after a position measurement made with a detector of finite width.

> [!caution] Caution: Notation at levels B and C
> Level C follows Sakurai. Operators carry no hats; an eigenket carries the eigenvalue with a prime, $x|x'\rangle = x'|x'\rangle$, so $x$ is the operator and $x'$ a number (a length such as $0.23$ cm). Level B writes $\hat x$, $|x\rangle$ and the eigenfunction $g_y(x) = \delta(x - y)$ ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]). The same symbol $\delta$ is the Kronecker delta $\delta_{a'a''}$ for discrete labels and the Dirac delta $\delta(x' - x'')$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|WO Def. §B4.4.2]]) for continuous ones. $d^3x'$ is a volume element and $\mathbf x'$ a point; $\delta^3(\mathbf x' - \mathbf x'') = \delta(x' - x'')\,\delta(y' - y'')\,\delta(z' - z'')$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|EM Def. §B1.2.1]]).

## The continuum dictionary

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.1.1: The Continuum Dictionary
> Let $\xi$ be an observable with a continuous spectrum, $\xi|\xi'\rangle = \xi'|\xi'\rangle$, whose eigenkets are normalized and complete in the sense
>
> $$
> \langle\xi'|\xi''\rangle = \delta(\xi' - \xi''), \qquad \int d\xi'\,|\xi'\rangle\langle\xi'| = 1 .
> $$
>
> Then every relation for a discrete observable $A$ has a continuous analogue, obtained by replacing the Kronecker delta by the Dirac delta and the sum over eigenvalues by an integral:
>
> | discrete ($A$, eigenkets $\lvert a'\rangle$) | continuous ($\xi$, eigenkets $\lvert\xi'\rangle$) |
> |---|---|
> | $\langle a'\lvert a''\rangle = \delta_{a'a''}$ | $\langle\xi'\lvert\xi''\rangle = \delta(\xi' - \xi'')$ |
> | $\sum_{a'}\lvert a'\rangle\langle a'\rvert = 1$ | $\int d\xi'\,\lvert\xi'\rangle\langle\xi'\rvert = 1$ |
> | $\lvert\alpha\rangle = \sum_{a'}\lvert a'\rangle\langle a'\lvert\alpha\rangle$ | $\lvert\alpha\rangle = \int d\xi'\,\lvert\xi'\rangle\langle\xi'\lvert\alpha\rangle$ |
> | $\sum_{a'}\lvert\langle a'\lvert\alpha\rangle\rvert^2 = 1$ | $\int d\xi'\,\lvert\langle\xi'\lvert\alpha\rangle\rvert^2 = 1$ |
> | $\langle\beta\lvert\alpha\rangle = \sum_{a'}\langle\beta\lvert a'\rangle\langle a'\lvert\alpha\rangle$ | $\langle\beta\lvert\alpha\rangle = \int d\xi'\,\langle\beta\lvert\xi'\rangle\langle\xi'\lvert\alpha\rangle$ |
> | $\langle a''\rvert A\lvert a'\rangle = a'\,\delta_{a''a'}$ | $\langle\xi''\rvert\xi\lvert\xi'\rangle = \xi'\,\delta(\xi'' - \xi')$ |
>
> (the fourth line for normalized $|\alpha\rangle$). More generally, for every function $f$, $\langle\xi''|f(\xi)|\xi'\rangle = f(\xi')\,\delta(\xi'' - \xi')$ and $f(\xi) = \int d\xi'\,f(\xi')\,|\xi'\rangle\langle\xi'|$: an observable with a continuous spectrum is "diagonal" with a delta function on the diagonal. For a spectrum with discrete and continuous parts, sums and integrals appear side by side.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.1, eqs. (1.183)–(1.184f) · the user's 511 notes, §"Continuous Spectra" · the user's series, Part IV, §5.1*

^thm-c2-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The first two lines are the hypotheses. Apply the closure relation to $|\alpha\rangle$ for the third line, and sandwich it between $\langle\beta|$ and $|\alpha\rangle$ for the fifth; with $\langle\alpha|\xi'\rangle = \langle\xi'|\alpha\rangle^*$ and $|\beta\rangle = |\alpha\rangle$, the fifth gives the fourth. For the last line, $\xi|\xi'\rangle = \xi'|\xi'\rangle$ and the normalization give $\langle\xi''|\xi|\xi'\rangle = \xi'\langle\xi''|\xi'\rangle = \xi'\,\delta(\xi'' - \xi')$. Repeating, $\xi^n|\xi'\rangle = \xi'^n|\xi'\rangle$, so $f(\xi)|\xi'\rangle = f(\xi')|\xi'\rangle$ for every polynomial or power series $f$ ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-6|Def. §B2.1.6]]), which gives the matrix elements of $f(\xi)$; and $f(\xi)|\alpha\rangle = \int d\xi'\,f(\xi)|\xi'\rangle\langle\xi'|\alpha\rangle = \int d\xi'\,f(\xi')|\xi'\rangle\langle\xi'|\alpha\rangle$ for every $|\alpha\rangle$, which is the spectral form. The spectral form also defines $f(\xi)$ for functions without a power series. In finite dimensions it is the spectral theorem ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]); its rigorous infinite-dimensional version replaces $|\xi'\rangle\langle\xi'|\,d\xi'$ by a projection-valued measure ([[§37 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^rem-37-3|556 Remark: Reading the continuous completeness relation]]).

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-6|Def. §B2.1.6]]

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level B
> Level B stated the second and third lines for eigenfunctions in $L^2$ and for $x$ and $p$ ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]]); the argument is the same, and its completeness hypothesis is the axiom [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]]. The theorem is new in generality: it holds for any observable on any ket space, with no wave functions anywhere, and it adds the last line, which says that functions of $\xi$ are computed on the eigenvalues. Sakurai's "danger signals" are the places where the replacement $\delta_{a'a''} \to \delta(\xi' - \xi'')$ breaks: $\langle\xi'|\xi'\rangle = \delta(0)$ is infinite, so $|\xi'\rangle$ is not a normalizable state, and $|\xi'\rangle\langle\xi'|$, unlike $|a'\rangle\langle a'|$, is not a projection ([[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^cau-c2-1-2|Caution: A point detector is an idealization]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.1*

## Position eigenkets and position measurement

> [!principle] Principle §C2.1.2: Position Eigenkets and Position Measurements
> 1. **Base kets.** The position operator $x$ of a particle on a line has an eigenket $|x'\rangle$ for every real $x'$, with $\langle x''|x'\rangle = \delta(x'' - x')$, and these eigenkets are complete: every state ket is
>
> $$
> |\alpha\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx'\,|x'\rangle\langle x'|\alpha\rangle .
> $$
>
>    In three dimensions $|\mathbf x'\rangle = |x', y', z'\rangle$ is a simultaneous eigenket of $x$, $y$, $z$ with eigenvalues $x'$, $y'$, $z'$; $\langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf x''\rangle = \delta^3(\mathbf x' - \mathbf x'')$, and $|\alpha\rangle = \int d^3x'\,|\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x'|\alpha\rangle$.
> 2. **Measurement with a finite detector.** A detector that registers the particle when it lies in the interval $I = (x' - \Delta/2,\ x' + \Delta/2)$ clicks with probability
>
> $$
> P(I) = \int_{I}dx''\,|\langle x''|\alpha\rangle|^2 ,
> $$
>
>    and when it clicks the state changes abruptly to the part of $|\alpha\rangle$ inside $I$, renormalized:
>
> $$
> |\alpha\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx''\,|x''\rangle\langle x''|\alpha\rangle \ \xrightarrow{\ \text{click}\ }\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{P(I)}}\int_{x' - \Delta/2}^{x' + \Delta/2}dx''\,|x''\rangle\langle x''|\alpha\rangle .
> $$
>
>    If $\langle x''|\alpha\rangle$ hardly changes across $I$, $P(I) \approx |\langle x'|\alpha\rangle|^2\,dx'$ with $dx' = \Delta$: $|\langle x'|\alpha\rangle|^2$ is the probability density of position. Its integral over the whole line is $\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle = 1$.
>
> *Domain: one particle in nonrelativistic quantum mechanics, with internal degrees of freedom such as spin ignored (they add a discrete label, [[§C1.5 Composite Systems and Tensor Products]]); an ideal detector that acts as a sharp filter on position. Relativistic quantum mechanics has no position eigenkets of this kind: localizing a particle to less than its Compton wavelength creates pairs (Sakurai §1.7, footnote 18; why a one-particle relativistic wave equation cannot describe pair creation: [[§C13.1★ Relativistic Wave Equations and the Klein–Gordon Field#^rem-c13-1-2|Remark: What the negative energies could mean]]).*
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.2, eqs. (1.185)–(1.192b) · the user's 511 notes, §"Position Eigenkets and Position Measurements" · the user's series, Part IV, §5.1*

^pr-c2-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: What is postulated, and what is new
> - **Completeness of the position eigenkets is an axiom.** Sakurai says the $|x'\rangle$ "are postulated to form a complete set", and his footnote 11 explains why it cannot be a theorem here: the completeness of the energy eigenfunctions of a particular Hamiltonian can be proved by Sturm–Liouville theory ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^rem-b2-2-1|Remark: When completeness is a theorem, and what it requires]]), but only after the Schrödinger wave equation has been derived, and deriving it ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation|§C3.1]], [[§C3.5 Wave Mechanics, the Linear Potential and WKB Revisited|§C3.5]]) needs the position kets first. It is the special case of [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]] on which all of wave mechanics rests.
> - **The probability is not new; the state after is.** Part 2's probability is the continuous Born rule of [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]], part 3, integrated over $I$. Level B left the state after a continuous measurement vague ("a narrow packet around the value found"); here it is specified exactly: the wave function is cut off outside the detector and renormalized, the continuous analogue of the selective measurement (filtration) of a discrete observable ([[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty]]), in the same way that a Stern–Gerlach filter throws a spin state into $|S_z;+\rangle$.
> - **Rigorous form.** The operator $E(I) = \int_I dx''\,|x''\rangle\langle x''|$ multiplies the wave function by the indicator function of $I$. It is an orthogonal projection, and $\langle\alpha|E(I)|\alpha\rangle = \int_I|\psi_\alpha|^2$ ([[§37 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-37-5|556 Prop. §37.5]]). Part 2 is therefore the projection postulate applied to the projection $E(I)$, $|\alpha\rangle \mapsto E(I)|\alpha\rangle/\|E(I)|\alpha\rangle\|$, and every object in it is well defined, though $|x''\rangle$ is not.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.2 and ch. 1, footnote 11*

^rem-c2-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: A point detector is an idealization
> Sakurai first imagines a detector that clicks "only when the particle is precisely at $x'$", after which the state is $|x'\rangle$. This cannot be taken literally. $|x'\rangle$ has $\langle x'|x'\rangle = \delta(0)$ and is not a state; position has no eigenvectors in $L^2$ at all ([[§37 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-37-3|556 Prop. §37.3]]). As $\Delta \to 0$, the unnormalized post-measurement ket $\int_I dx''\,|x''\rangle\langle x''|\alpha\rangle$ has squared norm $P(I) \to 0$, so it tends to the zero ket. What tends to $|x'\rangle\langle x'|\alpha\rangle$ (in the sense of distributions) is the same ket divided by $\Delta$. The normalized post-measurement state converges to no vector at all: its density $|\psi_\alpha|^2/P(I)$ on $I$ approaches $\delta(x'' - x')$. Likewise $\Lambda_{x'} = |x'\rangle\langle x'|$ is not a projection, since $\Lambda_{x'}^2 = \delta(0)\,\Lambda_{x'}$; the projections are the $E(I)$ of the remark above. The formulas $\lim_{\Delta \to 0}\Delta^{-1}|\alpha\rangle_{\text{after}} = |x'\rangle\langle x'|\alpha\rangle$ and "$\Lambda_{x'}$ projects onto $|x'\rangle$" hold only in the formal sense just described: the limit holds after division by $\Delta$, and $\Lambda_{x'}$ projects only up to the infinite factor $\delta(0)$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.2; the limits are worked out here*

^cau-c2-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C2.1.3: The Position Components Commute
> If the simultaneous eigenkets $|\mathbf x'\rangle$ of Principle §C2.1.2 are complete, then
>
> $$
> [x_i, x_j] = 0 \qquad (i, j = 1, 2, 3;\ x_1, x_2, x_3 = x, y, z) :
> $$
>
> the three components of position are compatible observables and can be measured together to arbitrary accuracy.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.2, eq. (1.193) · the user's 511 notes, §"Three Dimensions"*

^thm-c2-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> On every base ket, $x_ix_j|\mathbf x'\rangle = x_i'x_j'|\mathbf x'\rangle = x_jx_i|\mathbf x'\rangle$, so $[x_i, x_j]|\mathbf x'\rangle = 0$. By completeness, $[x_i, x_j]|\alpha\rangle = \int d^3x'\,[x_i, x_j]|\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x'|\alpha\rangle = 0$ for every $|\alpha\rangle$. This is the continuous form of "a complete set of common eigenkets implies commuting" ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-6|Theorem §B2.4.6]], part 1).

^der-c2-1-3

*Uses:* [[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^pr-c2-1-2|Principle §C2.1.2]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-6|Theorem §B2.4.6]]

At level B, $[\hat r_i, \hat r_j] = 0$ was trivial: multiplications by functions commute ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]]). Here it is the algebraic content of the physical assumption that $x$, $y$ and $z$ have common sharp values. The other two canonical commutators, $[p_i, p_j] = 0$ and $[x_i, p_j] = i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}$, come from translations in [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator|§C2.2]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C2.1.1: A Detector of Finite Width
> A particle is in the Gaussian state $\langle x'|\alpha\rangle = \psi(x') = \pi^{-1/4}d^{-1/2}e^{-x'^2/2d^2}$. A detector covers $I = (x_0 - \Delta/2,\ x_0 + \Delta/2)$.
>
> *Click probability.* With $|\psi|^2 = e^{-x'^2/d^2}/\sqrt\pi\,d$ and $\operatorname{erf}(u) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^ue^{-s^2}ds$,
>
> $$
> P(I) = \int_I\frac{e^{-x''^2/d^2}}{\sqrt\pi\,d}\,dx'' = \frac12\Bigl[\operatorname{erf}\Bigl(\frac{x_0 + \Delta/2}{d}\Bigr) - \operatorname{erf}\Bigl(\frac{x_0 - \Delta/2}{d}\Bigr)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> How good is the narrow-detector rule $P \approx |\psi(x_0)|^2\Delta$? Expanding the integrand about $x_0$, the odd terms cancel over the symmetric interval, and $P(I) = |\psi(x_0)|^2\Delta + \frac{1}{24}\,(|\psi|^2)''(x_0)\,\Delta^3 + O(\Delta^5)$. For a centred detector, $(|\psi|^2)''(0) = -2|\psi(0)|^2/d^2$, so $P = |\psi(0)|^2\Delta\,\bigl(1 - \Delta^2/12d^2 + \dots\bigr)$. With $\Delta = d/5$: $P = \operatorname{erf}(0.1) = 0.11246$, against $\Delta/\sqrt\pi\,d = 0.11284$, an error of $0.3\%$. For the off-centre window $I = (0.1d,\ 0.9d)$ of the figure, $P = 0.342$, and the rule gives $|\psi(0.5d)|^2\cdot0.8d = 0.352$, $3\%$ too high, because the window is no longer narrow on the scale $d$.
>
> *State after a click.* $\langle x''|\alpha\rangle_{\text{after}} = \psi(x'')/\sqrt{P(I)}$ for $x'' \in I$ and $0$ outside: the Gaussian cut off by the detector, with jumps at both edges.
>
> *Momentum after a click.* The momentum distribution is the squared Fourier transform ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]]; Fourier pair in [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space|§C2.3]]). Integrating by parts once, with $I = (a, b)$, gives for large $|p'|$
>
> $$
> \langle p'|\alpha\rangle_{\text{after}} \approx \frac{\hbar}{ip'}\,\frac{\psi(a)\,e^{-ip'a/\hbar} - \psi(b)\,e^{-ip'b/\hbar}}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar\,P(I)}}, \qquad |\langle p'|\alpha\rangle_{\text{after}}|^2 \approx \frac{\hbar\,\bigl[\psi(a)^2 + \psi(b)^2 - 2\psi(a)\psi(b)\cos(p'\Delta/\hbar)\bigr]}{2\pi\,P(I)\,p'^2} .
> $$
>
> The tail falls only like $1/p'^2$, so $\langle p^2\rangle = \int p'^2\,|\langle p'|\alpha\rangle|^2\,dp' = \infty$. A sharp position filter leaves the particle with infinite mean kinetic energy, for the same reason as the particle released from a slot ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^ex-b1-4-1|Example §B1.4.1]]): the wave function jumps. Before the measurement the momentum distribution was the Gaussian $e^{-p'^2d^2/\hbar^2}\,d/\hbar\sqrt\pi$, with every moment finite. A real detector has soft edges, and its effect is then not a projection but a smooth weighting (a generalized measurement, which these notes do not cover: Chapter C11 treats only projective measurements on ensembles; density matrices: [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^def-c11-1-2|Def. §C11.1.2]], [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-1|Theorem §C11.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §1.6.2, eqs. (1.187)–(1.188) (the setting); computed here*

> [!example] Example §C2.1.2: Relabelling a Continuum
> The label of a continuous spectrum can be changed, but the normalization of the kets must change with it. Let $\eta = g(\xi)$ with $g$ smooth and strictly monotonic, and $\eta' = g(\xi')$. Then
>
> $$
> |\eta'\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|g'(\xi')|}}\,|\xi'\rangle \qquad\text{satisfies}\qquad \langle\eta'|\eta''\rangle = \delta(\eta' - \eta''), \qquad \int d\eta'\,|\eta'\rangle\langle\eta'| = 1 .
> $$
>
> *Check.* By the composition rule for delta functions ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-1|EM Theorem §B1.2.1]], part 1), $\delta(\eta' - \eta'') = \delta\bigl(g(\xi') - g(\xi'')\bigr) = \delta(\xi' - \xi'')/|g'(\xi')|$. On the other side, $\langle\eta'|\eta''\rangle = |g'(\xi')\,g'(\xi'')|^{-1/2}\,\delta(\xi' - \xi'')$, and the delta lets $\xi'' = \xi'$ inside the prefactor, so the two sides agree. For closure, $d\eta' = |g'(\xi')|\,d\xi'$ cancels the factor $1/|g'|$.
>
> 1. *Wave number.* The momentum eigenkets $|p'\rangle$ are delta-normalized in $p'$ ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]; derived in [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space|§C2.3]]). For the wave number $k = p/\hbar$, $g' = 1/\hbar$, so $|k'\rangle = \sqrt\hbar\,|p' = \hbar k'\rangle$, with $\langle x'|k'\rangle = e^{ik'x'}/\sqrt{2\pi}$. The component $\langle k'|\alpha\rangle = \sqrt\hbar\,\langle p' = \hbar k'|\alpha\rangle$ is level B's wave-number amplitude $\phi(k)$ ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^def-b1-4-1|Def. §B1.4.1]]). This is the factor $\sqrt\hbar$ in level B's conversion $\Phi(p) = \phi(p/\hbar)/\sqrt\hbar$ ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]]): it comes from the normalization of the kets, not from the Fourier transform.
> 2. *Energy, for a free particle on a line.* Each energy $E > 0$ is twice degenerate, with momenta $\pm p_E$, $p_E = \sqrt{2mE}$. On each branch $dp/dE = m/p_E$, so
>
> $$
> |E, \pm\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{m}{p_E}}\;|{\pm p_E}\rangle, \qquad \langle E, s|E', s'\rangle = \delta_{ss'}\,\delta(E - E'), \qquad \sum_{s = \pm}\int_0^\infty dE\,|E, s\rangle\langle E, s| = 1 ,
> $$
>
>    with wave functions $\langle x'|E, \pm\rangle = \sqrt{m/2\pi\hbar\,p_E}\;e^{\pm ip_Ex'/\hbar}$. Their probability current is $|A|^2\,p_E/m = 1/2\pi\hbar$ ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-1|Theorem §B1.4.1]]), the same at every energy: energy-normalized plane waves carry a flux of one particle per time $h$. This is the normalization used for scattering states and transition rates ([[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^thm-c10-3-1|Theorem §C10.3.1]]; densities of final states, [[§C9.5 Fermi's Golden Rule#^def-c9-5-1|Def. §C9.5.1]]).
>
> *Source: Stony Brook PHY 511 (Fall 2025), HW 4, Problem 4 and its solution (delta-function rules) · Sakurai §1.6.1; the relabellings are worked out here*
