Every hot body glows, and an ideal absorber, a blackbody, glows with a spectrum that depends on its temperature alone. Classical physics, which shares the energy equally among the modes of the radiation, predicts a spectrum that grows without bound at short wavelengths. In 1900 Planck removed the divergence by assuming that energy is exchanged with the radiation only in quanta $hf$. This is where Planck's constant enters physics; the next two sections turn the quantum into a particle of light.

## The blackbody spectrum

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.1: Cavity Radiator; Blackbody Radiation; Spectral Intensity
> A blackbody ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^mod-a1-5-5|TH Model §A1.5.5]]) is realised by a small hole in the wall of a closed cavity whose walls are kept at temperature $T$, a **cavity radiator**: radiation entering the hole is absorbed after many reflections before it can leave again. The radiation coming out of the hole, in equilibrium with the walls, is **blackbody radiation**. Its **spectral intensity** $I(\lambda, T)$ is the power emitted per unit area of the hole per unit wavelength: $I(\lambda,T)\,d\lambda$ is the power per unit area carried by wavelengths between $\lambda$ and $\lambda + d\lambda$, and the total power per unit area is
>
> $$
> \frac PA = \int_0^\infty I(\lambda, T)\,d\lambda .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.1; Tipler §3-2*

^def-a1-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: What the measurements showed
> By the end of the nineteenth century the spectrum had been measured carefully (see the figure below for its shape):
> - it is a single hump that vanishes at both very short and very long wavelengths, and it depends only on $T$, not on the material or the shape of the cavity;
> - the hotter the body, the larger the curve at every wavelength and the shorter the wavelength of the peak: red glow, then orange, yellow and white as a body is heated;
> - the area under the curve, the total power, grows as $T^4$ (Stefan, 1879; [[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-6|TH Law §A1.5.6]]), and the peak wavelength falls as $1/T$ (Wien, 1893).
>
> Real surfaces follow the blackbody curve only approximately; the spectra of ordinary stars follow it closely, which is why a star's colour gives its surface temperature.

^rem-a1-1-1

## The classical prediction and its failure

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.1.1: Rayleigh–Jeans Law (Classical Prediction)
> If each mode of the electromagnetic field in the cavity holds the classical equipartition energy $k_BT$, the energy per unit volume per unit wavelength in the cavity and the spectral intensity are
>
> $$
> u(\lambda) = \frac{8\pi k_BT}{\lambda^4}, \qquad I(\lambda, T) = \frac c4\,u(\lambda) = \frac{2\pi c\,k_BT}{\lambda^4} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Tipler §3-2, eqs. (3-6)–(3-9); UP3 §6.1*

^thm-a1-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Three ingredients, each classical.
> 1. *Hole and cavity.* The power leaving a small hole is proportional to the energy density inside; the geometric factor, from averaging over the directions in which radiation strikes the hole, is $c/4$, so $I(\lambda,T) = \tfrac c4\,u(\lambda)$ (quoted by Tipler; the angular average is not done here).
> 2. *Counting modes.* The field in a cavity is a superposition of standing waves, as on a string fixed at both ends ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|WO Theorem §A2.6.2]]). In a cube of side $L$ the allowed wave vectors are $\vec k = \tfrac{\pi}{L}(n_x, n_y, n_z)$ with positive integers $n_i$, one point per volume $(\pi/L)^3$ of $k$-space. The number of modes with wave number below $k$ is the volume of an eighth of a sphere divided by $(\pi/L)^3$, times 2 for the two polarizations: $N = 2\cdot\tfrac18\cdot\tfrac43\pi k^3 (L/\pi)^3 = k^3L^3/3\pi^2$. With $k = 2\pi/\lambda$, $N = 8\pi L^3/3\lambda^3$, and the number per unit volume per unit wavelength is $\lvert dN/d\lambda\rvert / L^3 = 8\pi/\lambda^4$. The result does not depend on the shape of the cavity (stated by Tipler; the general proof belongs to Thermal level B, planned).
> 3. *Energy per mode.* Each mode oscillates like a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator, whose energy has two quadratic terms; by equipartition ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|TH Theorem §A2.3.2]]) its mean energy is $2\cdot\tfrac12 k_BT = k_BT$.
>
> Multiplying, $u(\lambda) = (8\pi/\lambda^4)\,k_BT$, and $I = \tfrac c4 u$.

^der-a1-1-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^def-a1-1-1|Def. §A1.1.1]], [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|WO Theorem §A2.6.2]], [[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|TH Theorem §A2.3.2]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: The ultraviolet catastrophe
> The Rayleigh–Jeans law agrees with experiment at long wavelengths but fails completely at short ones: $I \propto \lambda^{-4}$ grows without bound as $\lambda \to 0$, whereas the measured spectrum falls to zero. Worse, $\int_0^\infty u(\lambda)\,d\lambda$ diverges, so every cavity would hold infinite energy, and every glowing body would have the same colour, with its emission piled up in the ultraviolet and beyond. The mode count is correct; what fails is the classical average energy per mode. Ad hoc cut-offs (Rayleigh, Lorentz, Jeans) found no physical justification.

^cau-a1-1-1

## Planck's hypothesis

> [!principle] Principle §A1.1.2: Planck's Quantization Hypothesis
> The oscillators in the cavity walls that emit and absorb radiation of frequency $f$ can have only the energies
>
> $$
> E_n = n\,hf, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots ,
> $$
>
> so they exchange energy with the radiation only in whole multiples of the **quantum** $\Delta E = hf$. An absorption raises an oscillator to a higher state, an emission lowers it; an amount of energy that is not a multiple of $hf$ is neither absorbed nor emitted.
>
> *Domain: as Planck stated it, the oscillators that keep cavity radiation in thermal equilibrium (equivalently, the modes of the radiation). Einstein's photon hypothesis ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]) makes it a property of light itself, and the quantum oscillator ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]) derives the equal spacing $hf$ from the Schrödinger equation.*
> *Source: UP3 §6.1, eqs. (6.9)–(6.10); Tipler §3-2, eq. (3-13); PHY 284, class 14 slides*

^pr-a1-1-2

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.2: Planck's Constant; Quantum Number
> The constant $h$ in [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]] is **Planck's constant**,
>
> $$
> h = 6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s} = 4.136\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{eV\cdot s}, \qquad \hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi} = 1.055\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s} = 6.58\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{eV\cdot s},
> $$
>
> $\hbar$ being the **reduced Planck constant** ("h-bar"). Its unit, energy times time, is that of action and also that of angular momentum. The integer $n$ labelling the allowed energies is a **quantum number**, and each allowed energy is a **quantum state** of the oscillator. In electron-volts ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-2|EM Def. §A3.2.2]]) the combination $hc = 1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}$ converts wavelengths to quantum energies.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.1, eq. (6.10), §6.3; PHY 284, class 15 slides*

^def-a1-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Where the ladder starts
> University Physics lists $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$; Tipler and the PHY 284 slides start at $n = 0$. The spacing $hf$, which is all that the exchange of energy depends on, is the same. For the thermal average below the state $n = 0$ must be included: it is the state a high-frequency oscillator is almost always in. The quantum oscillator ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]) has the levels $(n + \tfrac12)hf$; a shift of every level by the same amount changes no energy difference.

^rem-a1-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.1.3: Mean Energy of a Planck Oscillator
> In thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$, an oscillator of frequency $f$ with the energies of [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]] has mean energy
>
> $$
> \bar E = \frac{hf}{e^{hf/k_BT} - 1} .
> $$
>
> For $hf \ll k_BT$ this tends to the classical value $k_BT$; for $hf \gg k_BT$ it is $\bar E \approx hf\,e^{-hf/k_BT}$, exponentially small.
>
> *Source: Tipler §3-2, eqs. (3-14)–(3-17); PHY 284, class 15 slides*

^thm-a1-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In equilibrium a state of energy $E$ is occupied with probability proportional to the Boltzmann factor $e^{-E/k_BT}$ ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^rem-a2-4-2|TH Remark: Boltzmann's generalisation]]). Write $x = hf/k_BT$ and $q = e^{-x}$, $0 < q < 1$. The probability of state $n$ is $f_n = A\,q^n$, and normalization, $\sum_{n\ge0} f_n = 1$, gives with the geometric series $A = 1/\sum q^n = 1 - q$. The mean energy is
>
> $$
> \bar E = \sum_{n=0}^\infty nhf\,f_n = hf\,(1-q)\sum_{n=1}^\infty n q^n = hf\,(1-q)\,\frac{q}{(1-q)^2} = \frac{hf\,q}{1-q} = \frac{hf}{e^{x} - 1} ,
> $$
>
> using $\sum n q^n = q/(1-q)^2$. For small $x$, $e^x - 1 = x + O(x^2)$ by Taylor's theorem, so $\bar E \to hf/x = k_BT$; for large $x$ the $1$ is negligible next to $e^x$.

^der-a1-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]], [[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^rem-a2-4-2|TH Remark: Boltzmann's generalisation]], [[§14 Series#^ex-14-4|451 Ex. §14.4]], [[§26 Differentiation and Integration of Power Series#^ex-26-1|451 Ex. §26.1]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why large steps suppress short wavelengths
> Energy reaches a mode through random thermal exchanges, and a step of size $hf$ is taken with relative probability $e^{-hf/k_BT}$. When $hf \ll k_BT$ the ladder is effectively a ramp and the mode gets its classical share $k_BT$. When $hf \gg k_BT$ even the first rung is almost never reached and the mode stays nearly empty. Short-wavelength modes are many, but each holds almost nothing, and this is exactly what cures the catastrophe. At $hf = k_BT$, $\bar E = k_BT/(e - 1) = 0.582\,k_BT$ (Tipler, Example 3-3).

^rem-a1-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.1.4: Planck's Radiation Law
> The spectral intensity of blackbody radiation is
>
> $$
> I(\lambda, T) = \frac{2\pi h c^2}{\lambda^5}\,\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda k_BT} - 1} ,
> $$
>
> and the energy density in the cavity is $u(\lambda) = \dfrac{8\pi hc}{\lambda^5}\,\dfrac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda k_BT} - 1}$. At long wavelengths ($hc/\lambda \ll k_BT$) it reduces to the Rayleigh–Jeans law; at short wavelengths it falls to zero as $\lambda^{-5}e^{-hc/\lambda k_BT}$. It agrees with the measured spectra at all wavelengths and temperatures.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.1, eq. (6.11); Tipler §3-2, eq. (3-18)*

^thm-a1-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Repeat the derivation of [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-1|Theorem §A1.1.1]] with the same mode count $8\pi/\lambda^4$, but give each mode of frequency $f = c/\lambda$ ([[§A12.5 The Electromagnetic Spectrum#^thm-a12-5-1|EM Theorem §A12.5.1]]) the quantum mean energy of [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]] instead of $k_BT$:
>
> $$
> u(\lambda) = \frac{8\pi}{\lambda^4}\cdot\frac{hc/\lambda}{e^{hc/\lambda k_BT} - 1}, \qquad I = \frac c4\,u = \frac{2\pi hc^2}{\lambda^5}\,\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda k_BT}-1} .
> $$
>
> The two limits are the two limits of $\bar E$: $\bar E \to k_BT$ gives back $2\pi ck_BT/\lambda^4$, and $\bar E \approx (hc/\lambda)e^{-hc/\lambda k_BT}$ gives the exponential fall.

^der-a1-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-1|Theorem §A1.1.1]], [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]], [[§A12.5 The Electromagnetic Spectrum#^thm-a12-5-1|EM Theorem §A12.5.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: How the law was found, and what it confirms
> Planck first found a formula that fitted the data and then searched for a derivation; the quantum assumption was the price. He and his contemporaries took it for a mathematical device until 1905, when Einstein gave the quantum the meaning of a particle of light ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]). Fitting the law to measured spectra was also the first way of determining $h$. The best blackbody spectrum ever measured is that of the cosmic microwave background, which fits Planck's law at $T = 2.725\ \mathrm K$ (Tipler; see [[§A8.4 The Big Bang and the Early Universe#^def-a8-4-2|Def. §A8.4.2]]).

^rem-a1-1-4

## Wien's and Stefan's laws from Planck's law

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.1.5: Wien's Displacement Law
> The spectral intensity $I(\lambda, T)$ is largest at the wavelength $\lambda_{\max}$ with
>
> $$
> \lambda_{\max}\,T = \frac{hc}{4.965\,k_B} = 2.898\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\cdot K} .
> $$
>
> Hotter bodies peak at shorter wavelengths, in inverse proportion to $T$. Wien found the law empirically in 1893; it follows from Planck's law, which also gives the constant.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.1, eq. (6.1); Tipler §3-2, eq. (3-5)*

^thm-a1-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $x = hc/\lambda k_BT$. Then [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-4|Theorem §A1.1.4]] reads $I = 2\pi hc^2\,(k_BT/hc)^5\,g(x)$ with $g(x) = x^5/(e^x - 1)$. Since $x$ is a strictly decreasing differentiable function of $\lambda$, by the chain rule $dI/d\lambda = 0$ exactly where $g'(x) = 0$, and the maximum of $I$ in $\lambda$ sits at the maximum of $g$, an interior point where $g' = 0$:
>
> $$
> g'(x) = \frac{5x^4(e^x - 1) - x^5e^x}{(e^x-1)^2} = 0 \iff (5 - x)\,e^x = 5 \iff x = 5\,(1 - e^{-x}) .
> $$
>
> Besides $x = 0$ ($\lambda \to \infty$, where $I \to 0$) this has exactly one root; iterating $x \mapsto 5(1 - e^{-x})$ from $x = 5$ gives $4.966$, then $4.965$. Hence $\lambda_{\max} = hc/(4.965\,k_BT)$, and with $hc/k_B = 1.4388\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m\cdot K}$, $\lambda_{\max}T = 2.898\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\cdot K}$.

^der-a1-1-5

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-4|Theorem §A1.1.4]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^thm-29-1|451 Thm. §29.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Peak in wavelength is not peak in frequency
> The spectrum per unit frequency peaks at $hf = 2.82\,k_BT$ (the same calculation with $x^3/(e^x-1)$), not at $f = c/\lambda_{\max}$, which would be $hf = 4.97\,k_BT$. The two distributions measure power per unit $\lambda$ and per unit $f$, and equal intervals $d\lambda$ correspond to unequal intervals $df = c\,d\lambda/\lambda^2$. Wien's law in the form above refers to the wavelength spectrum.

^cau-a1-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.1.6: Stefan–Boltzmann Law from Planck's Law
> The total power per unit area emitted by a blackbody is
>
> $$
> \frac PA = \int_0^\infty I(\lambda,T)\,d\lambda = \sigma T^4, \qquad \sigma = \frac{2\pi^5k_B^4}{15\,h^3c^2} = 5.670\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W/(m^2\cdot K^4)} .
> $$
>
> This is the Stefan–Boltzmann law ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-6|TH Law §A1.5.6]]) with $e = 1$, now with its constant expressed through $h$, $c$ and $k_B$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.1, eq. (6.4); Tipler §3-2, Example 3-4*

^thm-a1-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Substitute $x = hc/\lambda k_BT$ in the improper integral: $\lambda = hc/(xk_BT)$, $d\lambda = -\dfrac{hc}{k_BT}\dfrac{dx}{x^2}$, and $\lambda$ from $0$ to $\infty$ becomes $x$ from $\infty$ to $0$. Then
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty \frac{2\pi hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac{d\lambda}{e^{hc/\lambda k_BT} - 1} = 2\pi hc^2\Bigl(\frac{k_BT}{hc}\Bigr)^4\int_0^\infty \frac{x^3}{e^x - 1}\,dx = \frac{2\pi k_B^4T^4}{h^3c^2}\cdot\frac{\pi^4}{15} .
> $$
>
> The integral converges (the integrand behaves as $x^2$ near $0$ and as $x^3e^{-x}$ at infinity); its value $\pi^4/15$ is taken from tables, as in Tipler. The $T^4$ comes out of the substitution alone, before the integral is evaluated.

^der-a1-1-6

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-4|Theorem §A1.1.4]], [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^def-a1-1-1|Def. §A1.1.1]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-1|451 Def. §36.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Law here, theorem there
> In Thermal Physics the blackbody is a model ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^mod-a1-5-5|TH Model §A1.5.5]]), the Stefan–Boltzmann law is taken from experiment ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^law-a1-5-6|TH Law §A1.5.6]]), and the shift of the spectrum to shorter wavelengths with rising temperature is only described ([[§A1.5 Mechanisms of Heat Transfer#^rem-a1-5-5|TH Remark: The thermal spectrum]]); Wien's $1/T$ law was likewise first found empirically. Here both are theorems of Planck's law, which also predicts the values of $\sigma$ and of Wien's constant from $h$, $c$ and $k_B$. Conversely, measuring $\sigma$ and Wien's constant determines $h$ and $k_B$.

^rem-a1-1-5

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.1.1: Is a Mass on a Spring Quantized?
> A $1.0$-kg mass on a spring of constant $1000\ \mathrm{N/m}$ oscillates with amplitude $0.10\ \mathrm m$. Treat it as a Planck oscillator. Its frequency is $f = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{k/m} = 5.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$, so its energy steps are
>
> $$
> \Delta E = hf = (6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s})(5.0\ \mathrm{Hz}) = 3.3\times10^{-33}\ \mathrm J ,
> $$
>
> while its energy is $E = \tfrac12 kA^2 = 5.0\ \mathrm J$: $\Delta E/E \approx 7\times10^{-34}$. The steps are immeasurably fine, and macroscopic oscillators behave as if their energy were continuous. A molecule vibrating at $5.0\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$ instead has $hf = 3.3\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm J = 2.1\ \mathrm{eV}$, about 80 times $k_BT$ at room temperature: such vibrations are "frozen" ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^rem-a2-3-2|TH Remark: Frozen degrees of freedom]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 Examples 6.3 and 6.4*

^ex-a1-1-1

> [!example] Example §A1.1.2: A Hot Star Seen in the Ultraviolet
> A satellite finds that a star's spectrum peaks at $\lambda_{\max} = 82.8\ \mathrm{nm}$. Treating the star as a blackbody, Wien's law ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-5|Theorem §A1.1.5]]) gives
>
> $$
> T = \frac{2.898\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\cdot K}}{82.8\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm m} = 3.50\times10^4\ \mathrm K .
> $$
>
> How bright is the star at $70\ \mathrm{nm}$ and at $100\ \mathrm{nm}$, relative to the peak? By Planck's law ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-4|Theorem §A1.1.4]]), with $hc/k_BT = 1.4388\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m\cdot K}/T = 411\ \mathrm{nm}$,
>
> $$
> \frac{I(\lambda)}{I(\lambda_{\max})} = \Bigl(\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda}\Bigr)^5\frac{e^{411/82.8} - 1}{e^{411/\lambda} - 1} = \begin{cases} 0.930, & \lambda = 70\ \mathrm{nm},\\ 0.923, & \lambda = 100\ \mathrm{nm}. \end{cases}
> $$
>
> The peak is lopsided: $70\ \mathrm{nm}$, only $13\ \mathrm{nm}$ short of the peak, is already about as far down as $100\ \mathrm{nm}$, $17\ \mathrm{nm}$ beyond it, because the curve rises steeply on the short side and falls slowly on the long side. (The instructor's solution gives $0.929$ and $0.924$, the difference being rounding.)
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 5 (and solutions)*

^ex-a1-1-2
