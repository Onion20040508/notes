Light falling on a clean metal knocks electrons out of it, and the way it does so contradicts the wave theory of light in three places: the electrons appear at once, their energy does not depend on how bright the light is, and below a certain frequency none appear at all. Einstein explained all three in 1905 by taking Planck's quantum literally: light itself comes in particles, photons, each absorbed whole by one electron. This section is the home of the photon hypothesis $E = hf$, $p = h/\lambda$ and of the photoelectric equation.

## The experiment

> [!definition] Definition §A1.2.1: Photoelectric Effect; Photoelectron; Photocurrent
> The **photoelectric effect** is the emission of electrons from a surface, usually a metal, on which electromagnetic radiation of sufficiently high frequency falls; the emitted electrons are **photoelectrons**. In the standard setup the illuminated electrode (the photoelectrode, or emitter) and a collecting electrode sit in an evacuated tube, so that the electrons lose no energy to gas molecules, and a variable, reversible potential difference is applied between them. The current of photoelectrons reaching the collector is the **photocurrent**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.2; Tipler §3-3*

^def-a1-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.2.2: Stopping Potential
> If the collector is made negative relative to the emitter, the photocurrent decreases as the reverse voltage grows and stops at a definite value. The magnitude $\Delta V_s$ of this reverse potential difference is the **stopping potential** (Tipler writes $V_0$).
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.2; Tipler §3-3*

^def-a1-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.2.1: Stopping Potential Measures the Maximum Kinetic Energy
> The largest kinetic energy with which photoelectrons leave the emitter is
>
> $$
> K_{\max} = e\,\Delta V_s .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.2, eq. (6.12); Tipler §3-3, eq. (3-20)*

^thm-a1-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Between the electrodes only the electric force acts, so for an electron (charge $q = -e$) $\Delta K = -q\,\Delta V = e\,\Delta V$ ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-3|EM Theorem §A3.2.3]]). With the stopping potential applied the collector is lower in potential by $\Delta V_s$, $\Delta V = -\Delta V_s$, so an electron starting with kinetic energy $K_i$ arrives with $K_i - e\Delta V_s$. The current stops when even the fastest electrons just fail to arrive: $K_{\max} - e\Delta V_s = 0$.

^der-a1-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^def-a1-2-2|Def. §A1.2.2]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-3|EM Theorem §A3.2.3]]

> [!law] Law §A1.2.2: Observed Features of the Photoelectric Effect
> For monochromatic light on a given clean metal surface:
> 1. **No lag.** Photoelectrons appear as soon as the light is switched on, however weak it is.
> 2. **Intensity sets the number, not the energy.** The saturation photocurrent (all photoelectrons collected) is proportional to the light intensity, but the stopping potential, and so $K_{\max}$, does not depend on the intensity.
> 3. **Cut-off.** Below a cut-off frequency $f_c$, characteristic of the metal, no electrons are emitted at any intensity.
> 4. **Linear in frequency.** Above $f_c$, $K_{\max}$ (equivalently $\Delta V_s$) grows linearly with $f$, and the slope is the same for every metal.
>
> *Valid when:* the surface is clean and in vacuum and the light is monochromatic, with photon energies of a few eV (visible and ultraviolet light). Established by Hertz (1887) and Lenard (1900); the linear law was measured precisely by Millikan in 1914–1916.
> *Source: UP3 §6.2; Tipler §3-3*

^law-a1-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: What the wave theory predicts instead
> A classical wave delivers energy continuously, at a rate proportional to its intensity and spread over the surface. Then a brighter beam should give faster electrons; any frequency should eject electrons if one waits long enough; and in dim light an electron should need a measurable time to collect enough energy ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^ex-a1-2-2|Example §A1.2.2]]). All three are contradicted by [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^law-a1-2-2|Law §A1.2.2]]. In the PHY 284 demonstration, a $100\,000$-lumen visible flashlight leaves a negatively charged electroscope untouched, while a weak ultraviolet lamp discharges it at once.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 15 slides*

^rem-a1-2-1

## Einstein's photon hypothesis

> [!principle] Principle §A1.2.3: Einstein's Photon Hypothesis
> Electromagnetic radiation of frequency $f$ and wavelength $\lambda = c/f$ consists of **photons**: massless particles that move at $c$, each carrying the energy and momentum
>
> $$
> E_f = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}, \qquad p_f = \frac{E_f}{c} = \frac h\lambda, \qquad \vec p_f = \hbar\vec k ,
> $$
>
> where $\vec k$ is the wave vector, along the direction of propagation, of magnitude $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. Photons are emitted and absorbed whole: in an interaction a photon gives up all its energy and ceases to exist, or it is not absorbed at all. The intensity of a beam is the number of photons crossing unit area per unit time, times $hf$.
>
> *Domain: all electromagnetic radiation in its exchanges of energy and momentum with matter. How photons propagate (interference, diffraction) is still described by the wave, read as a probability ([[§A3.2 Wave–Particle Duality#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]]).*
> *Source: UP3 §6.2, eq. (6.13); §6.3, eqs. (6.18)–(6.22); Tipler §3-3*

^pr-a1-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Why the momentum is $h/\lambda$
> - *Relativity.* A particle of zero mass has $E = pc$, by the energy–momentum relation $E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|REL Theorem §A3.3.1]]); with $E = hf$ this gives $p = hf/c = h/\lambda$. A massless particle must move at $c$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-2|REL Theorem §A3.3.2]]), so the photon has no rest frame and no Newtonian kinetic energy $\tfrac12mv^2$.
> - *Electromagnetism.* Classically, a body that absorbs energy $U$ from a wave receives momentum $U/c$ along the direction of propagation ([[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure#^thm-a12-4-1|EM Theorem §A12.4.1]]). Summing $p_f = E_f/c$ over many photons reproduces this. For instance, a $100$-W beam absorbed for $10^4\ \mathrm s$ by a $1$-g body in free space delivers $E = 10^6\ \mathrm J$ and $p = E/c = 3.3\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$, giving it a speed of $3.3\ \mathrm{m/s}$; the kinetic energy, $5.5\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm J$, is a tiny fraction of the energy absorbed, the rest heating the body (PHY 284, HW 5).
> - The photon's momentum was confirmed directly by Compton scattering ([[§A1.3 The Compton Effect#^thm-a1-3-2|Theorem §A1.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.3; PHY 284, class 16 slides; PHY 284, HW 5 (and solutions)*

^rem-a1-2-2

> [!definition] Definition §A1.2.3: Work Function
> The **work function** $\phi$ of a metal is the least energy needed to remove an electron from its surface: the binding energy of the most loosely held electrons. It is a property of the metal (and of its surface), a few electron-volts for common metals:
>
> | Metal | Na | Al | Pb | Zn | Fe | Cu | Ag | Pt |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $\phi$ (eV) | 2.46 | 4.08 | 4.14 | 4.31 | 4.50 | 4.70 | 4.73 | 6.35 |
>
> Tables differ somewhat: Tipler gives $2.28\ \mathrm{eV}$ for sodium.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.2, Table 6.1; Tipler §3-3, Table 3-1*

^def-a1-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.2.4: Einstein's Photoelectric Equation
> For light of frequency $f$ falling on a metal of work function $\phi$,
>
> $$
> K_{\max} = e\,\Delta V_s = hf - \phi .
> $$
>
> Photoelectrons are emitted only above the **cut-off frequency** $f_c$, or below the **cut-off wavelength** $\lambda_c$,
>
> $$
> f_c = \frac\phi h, \qquad \lambda_c = \frac c{f_c} = \frac{hc}{\phi}, \qquad hc = 1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm} .
> $$
>
> A plot of $K_{\max}$ against $f$ is a straight line of slope $h$, the same for all metals, crossing the $f$ axis at $f_c$ and the $K_{\max}$ axis at $-\phi$; equivalently, $\Delta V_s$ against $f$ has slope $h/e$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.2, eqs. (6.14)–(6.16); Tipler §3-3, eqs. (3-21)–(3-22)*

^thm-a1-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]] the light arrives as photons of energy $hf$, and each photon that is absorbed gives all of it to one electron. Energy conservation for that electron: of the energy $hf$, at least $\phi$ ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^def-a1-2-3|Def. §A1.2.3]]) is spent in leaving the metal; electrons that start deeper, or lose energy on the way out, keep less. The most loosely bound electrons leave with the most, $K_{\max} = hf - \phi$, and $K_{\max} = e\Delta V_s$ by [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^thm-a1-2-1|Theorem §A1.2.1]]. A kinetic energy cannot be negative, so emission requires $hf \ge \phi$, that is $f \ge \phi/h = f_c$; and $\lambda_c = c/f_c$ ([[§A12.5 The Electromagnetic Spectrum#^thm-a12-5-1|EM Theorem §A12.5.1]]).

^der-a1-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]], [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^def-a1-2-3|Def. §A1.2.3]], [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^thm-a1-2-1|Theorem §A1.2.1]], [[§A12.5 The Electromagnetic Spectrum#^thm-a12-5-1|EM Theorem §A12.5.1]]

![[ph-qm-a1-2-1.svg]]

The solid parts are observed; the dashed extensions meet the vertical axis at $-\phi$. Both lines have the same slope $h$. > [!remark] Remark: How the photon picture accounts for every feature
> Point by point for [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^law-a1-2-2|Law §A1.2.2]]:
> 1. *No lag:* one photon interacts with one electron, at once. Dim light means few photons, not weak ones, and some are absorbed immediately.
> 2. *Intensity:* doubling the intensity doubles the number of photons per second and so the number of photoelectrons and the saturation current, which levels off once all of them are collected. The energy of each photon, and so $K_{\max}$, is unchanged.
> 3. *Cut-off:* a photon with $hf < \phi$ cannot free an electron, however many such photons arrive.
> 4. *Slope:* $K_{\max} = hf - \phi$ has slope $h$, independent of the metal. When Einstein predicted this nothing linked $h$ to the photoelectric effect; Millikan's measurements gave a value of $h$ agreeing with Planck's.
>
> Together with the blackbody spectrum ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^pr-a1-1-2|Principle §A1.1.2]]) this was the second, independent appearance of the same constant, which is why the quantum had to be taken seriously.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.2.1: Work Function and Planck's Constant from Two Wavelengths
> Light of $450\ \mathrm{nm}$ ejects photoelectrons from potassium with stopping potential $0.52\ \mathrm V$; at $300\ \mathrm{nm}$ the stopping potential is $1.90\ \mathrm V$. Using only these data, $c$ and $e$, find $\phi$ and $h$.
>
> Write [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^thm-a1-2-4|Theorem §A1.2.4]] for both wavelengths, $e\Delta V_{s,i} = hc/\lambda_i - \phi$, and subtract:
>
> $$
> hc = \frac{e(\Delta V_{s,2} - \Delta V_{s,1})}{1/\lambda_2 - 1/\lambda_1} = \frac{1.38\ \mathrm{eV}}{(1/300 - 1/450)\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}} = 1242\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm} ,
> $$
>
> so $h = 1242\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}/c = 4.14\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{eV\cdot s} = 6.63\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s}$. Then $\phi = hc/\lambda_1 - e\Delta V_{s,1} = 2.76\ \mathrm{eV} - 0.52\ \mathrm{eV} = 2.24\ \mathrm{eV}$, and the cut-off wavelength is $\lambda_c = hc/\phi = 554\ \mathrm{nm}$: green light just works, yellow and red light eject nothing from potassium. Two points fix the line of the figure above; Millikan used many to show that it is a line.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 5 (and solutions)*

^ex-a1-2-1

> [!example] Example §A1.2.2: The Classical Time Lag
> Violet light ($400\ \mathrm{nm}$) of intensity $10^{-2}\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$ falls on potassium ($\phi = 2.22\ \mathrm{eV}$). If the energy were spread over the surface as a wave, an atom of radius $10^{-10}\ \mathrm m$ would intercept
>
> $$
> (10^{-2}\ \mathrm{W/m^2})\,\pi(10^{-10}\ \mathrm m)^2 = 3.1\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm W ,
> $$
>
> and would need $t = (2.22\ \mathrm{eV})(1.60\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J/eV})/(3.1\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm W) = 1.1\times10^3\ \mathrm s \approx 19$ minutes before it could release an electron. No such delay is ever seen. In the photon picture each photon carries $hc/\lambda = 3.10\ \mathrm{eV} = 4.96\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm J$, so the beam delivers $(10^{-2}\ \mathrm{W/m^2})/(4.96\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm J) = 2.0\times10^{16}$ photons per square metre per second: only about one photon per atom every half hour, but with some $10^{19}$ surface atoms per square metre, some of them absorb a photon, and emit an electron, at once. The wave calculation gives the right *average* rate; it is wrong about *when* the energy arrives.
>
> *Source: Tipler §3-3, Examples 3-6 and 3-7*

^ex-a1-2-2

> [!example] Example §A1.2.3: Planck's Constant from Light-Emitting Diodes
> In a light-emitting diode an electron crossing the junction gives up its energy as one photon, so the voltage $V_a$ at which the LED starts to emit should satisfy $eV_a \approx hc/\lambda$, up to an offset $\phi$ for losses in the junction: $V_a = (hc/e)(1/\lambda) + \phi/e$. A plot of $V_a$ against $1/\lambda$ for LEDs of several colours has slope $hc/e$. (The physics of the junction belongs to [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^rem-a6-3-3|Remark: What junctions do]].)
>
> In the user's measurement, six LEDs had wavelengths from $557$ to $665\ \mathrm{nm}$ (measured with a grating) and activation voltages from about $1.4$ to $2.1\ \mathrm V$. Straight-line fits gave
>
> $$
> h = (9.7 \pm 1.8)\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s} \ \ \text{(turn-on seen by eye)}, \qquad h = (1.08 \pm 0.30)\times10^{-33}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s} \ \ \text{(extrapolated I-V curves)} ,
> $$
>
> $1.5$ and $1.6$ times the accepted $6.63\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s}$, that is $1.7$ and $1.4$ standard deviations above it. The method is imprecise because $1/\lambda$ varies by only $20\%$ across the six LEDs, so differences of a tenth of a volt in $V_a$, which depend on the semiconductor of each LED and on how "turn-on" is judged, change the slope a lot; and each wavelength, read from the grating pattern on a ruler, was uncertain by about $14\%$, nearly the whole spread. The order of magnitude is robust: $eV_a \approx 2\ \mathrm{eV}$ at $\lambda \approx 600\ \mathrm{nm}$ gives $h \approx (2\ \mathrm{eV})(600\ \mathrm{nm})/c = 4\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{eV\cdot s}$.
>
> *Source: PHY 286, Planck's constant lab (the user's analysis); PHY 286, Planck's constant lab manual*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The momentum $h/\lambda$ of the photon is the case $m = 0$ of the relativistic energy–momentum relation ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|REL Theorem §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-2|REL Theorem §A3.3.2]]); de Broglie turned the same relations around for matter ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]]).
> - In the hydrogen atom the photon energy $hf$ equals the difference of two atomic energy levels (Bohr's frequency condition, [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^mod-a2-2-1|Model §A2.2.1]]; [[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]).
> - Run backwards, an electron of energy $eV$ turned into a single photon, the same energy balance gives the short-wavelength cutoff $\lambda_{\min} = hc/eV$ of an X-ray tube (the Duane–Hunt rule, [[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-5|Theorem §A5.4.5]]).
> - Relativity level B: $E = hf$ and $\vec p = \hbar\vec k$ say that the photon's four-momentum is $\hbar$ times the wave four-vector, which is a four-vector because the phase is invariant — [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^def-b2-1-4|REL Def. §B2.1.4]], [[§B2.1 Four-Velocity, Four-Momentum and Collisions#^thm-b2-1-9|REL Theorem §B2.1.9]].
> - Level C: the photoelectric effect computed with a classical light wave and a quantized atom, the photoelectron's angular distribution — [[§C9.6 The Interaction Picture and the Atom–Field Interaction#^rem-c9-6-6|Remark: The photoelectric effect]].
