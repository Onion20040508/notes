The photoelectric effect shows that light delivers energy in quanta $hf$. Compton's X-ray experiment (1923) shows that it also delivers momentum in quanta $h/\lambda$: X-rays scattered by electrons come out with a longer wavelength, by an amount that follows exactly from treating the scattering as a collision between two relativistic particles. After Compton the photon was accepted as a particle in its own right.

## Compton's experiment

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.1: Compton Effect; Scattering Angle; Compton Shift
> In **Compton's experiment** monochromatic X-rays of wavelength $\lambda$ fall on a target (graphite in the original work), and a crystal spectrometer measures the wavelength of the radiation scattered at an angle $\theta$, the **scattering angle**, from the incident direction. At every $\theta \neq 0$ the scattered spectrum has two peaks: one at the incident $\lambda$ and one at a longer wavelength $\lambda'$. The **Compton effect** is the appearance of the shifted peak, and $\Delta\lambda = \lambda' - \lambda$ is the **Compton shift**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.3; Tipler §3-4*

^def-a1-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: The classical expectation
> In the wave picture the incident field drives an electron to oscillate at the incident frequency, and the oscillating electron re-radiates at that same frequency ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^rem-a12-2-2|EM Remark: Antennas]]): scattered light should have the incident wavelength, at every angle. The unshifted peak fits this picture; the shifted one, growing with angle, does not.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 16 slides*

^rem-a1-3-1

## The photon–electron collision

> [!model] Model §A1.3.1: Compton's Collision Model
> The X-ray beam is a stream of photons ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]), and the shifted peak comes from single collisions of a photon with one electron of the target.
> - *Assumes:* the electron is **free and at rest** before the collision (the valence electrons of carbon are bound by about $11\ \mathrm{eV}$, negligible beside the $17.4\ \mathrm{keV}$ of Compton's X-rays); photon and electron form an **isolated system**, so the total momentum and the total relativistic energy are conserved ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^pr-a3-1-1|REL Principle §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|REL Principle §A3.2.3]]); the electron obeys $E^2 = (pc)^2 + (m_ec^2)^2$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-1|REL Theorem §A3.3.1]]) and the photons $E = pc$ ([[§A3.3 Invariant Mass and the Energy–Momentum Relation#^thm-a3-3-2|REL Theorem §A3.3.2]]).
> - *Fails:* for tightly bound inner electrons, where the whole atom takes up the recoil (this gives the unshifted peak), and for photons whose energy is not large compared with the binding energy.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.3; Tipler, More Chapter 3 #1 (PHY 284 handout)*

^mod-a1-3-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.2: Compton Wavelength
> The **Compton wavelength** of the electron is
>
> $$
> \lambda_c = \frac{h}{m_ec} = \frac{hc}{m_ec^2} = \frac{1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}}{511\ \mathrm{keV}} = 2.43\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{nm} = 2.43\ \mathrm{pm},
> $$
>
> where $m_e$ is the electron's mass (University Physics writes $m_0$). It is the wavelength of a photon whose energy equals the electron's rest energy. (In §A1.2, $\lambda_c$ also denotes the photoelectric cut-off wavelength; the context tells which is meant.)
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.3, eq. (6.29); Tipler §3-4*

^def-a1-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.3.2: Compton Shift
> Within [[§A1.3 The Compton Effect#^mod-a1-3-1|Model §A1.3.1]], a photon scattered through the angle $\theta$ has its wavelength increased by
>
> $$
> \Delta\lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_ec}\,(1 - \cos\theta) = \lambda_c\,(1 - \cos\theta) .
> $$
>
> The shift ranges from $0$ at $\theta = 0$ through $\lambda_c$ at $90^\circ$ to $2\lambda_c = 4.86\ \mathrm{pm}$ for backscattering, $\theta = 180^\circ$. It depends neither on the incident wavelength nor on the target material.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.3, eqs. (6.23)–(6.30); Tipler §3-4, eq. (3-25)*

^thm-a1-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let the incident photon have momentum $\vec p_f$ (magnitude $p_f = h/\lambda$), the scattered photon $\tilde{\vec p}_f$ (magnitude $\tilde p_f = h/\lambda'$) at angle $\theta$ to it, and the recoiling electron momentum $\vec p$ and energy $E$. Photon energies are $p_fc$ and $\tilde p_fc$ ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]). Conservation laws of the isolated system:
>
> $$
> \text{energy:}\quad p_fc + m_ec^2 = \tilde p_fc + E, \qquad\qquad \text{momentum:}\quad \vec p_f = \tilde{\vec p}_f + \vec p .
> $$
>
> *Energy.* Solve for $E$ and square: $E^2 = \bigl[(p_f - \tilde p_f)c + m_ec^2\bigr]^2$. Insert $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m_e^2c^4$ and divide by $c^2$:
>
> $$
> p^2 = (p_f - \tilde p_f)^2 + 2m_ec\,(p_f - \tilde p_f) .
> $$
>
> *Momentum.* $\vec p = \vec p_f - \tilde{\vec p}_f$, so $p^2 = p_f^2 + \tilde p_f^2 - 2\vec p_f\cdot\tilde{\vec p}_f = p_f^2 + \tilde p_f^2 - 2p_f\tilde p_f\cos\theta$.
>
> *Eliminate $p^2$.* Equating the two expressions, the squares $p_f^2$ and $\tilde p_f^2$ cancel and
>
> $$
> -2p_f\tilde p_f + 2m_ec\,(p_f - \tilde p_f) = -2p_f\tilde p_f\cos\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_ec\,(p_f - \tilde p_f) = p_f\tilde p_f\,(1 - \cos\theta) .
> $$
>
> Divide by $m_ec\,p_f\tilde p_f$: $\dfrac{1}{\tilde p_f} - \dfrac{1}{p_f} = \dfrac{1 - \cos\theta}{m_ec}$, and with $1/\tilde p_f = \lambda'/h$, $1/p_f = \lambda/h$ this is $\lambda' - \lambda = \dfrac{h}{m_ec}(1 - \cos\theta)$. The recoil direction was never needed: for given $\theta$ the three conservation equations ($E$, $p_x$, $p_y$) fix $\lambda'$, $p$ and the recoil angle $\varphi$, and $\varphi$ follows from the momentum triangle once $\lambda'$ is known.

> [!remark] Remark: The unshifted peak, and why X-rays
> - *Unshifted peak.* A photon that hits a tightly bound inner electron in effect collides with the whole atom. The same formula then holds with the atom's mass in place of $m_e$; for carbon that is about $2\times10^4$ times larger, and the shift is negligible. Both peaks are therefore predicted, and their separation follows [[§A1.3 The Compton Effect#^thm-a1-3-2|Theorem §A1.3.2]] at every angle, for graphite, calcite, molybdenum and other targets.
> - *Why X-rays.* $\Delta\lambda$ is at most a few picometres, whatever $\lambda$ is, so the relative shift $\Delta\lambda/\lambda \le 2\lambda_c/\lambda$ is noticeable only for X-rays and gamma rays ([[§A1.3 The Compton Effect#^ex-a1-3-1|Example §A1.3.1]]).
> - *Significance.* Only a particle carrying momentum $h/\lambda$ explains the shift; the success of a calculation with relativistic energy and momentum also counted as a test of relativity. Today "Compton scattering" means the scattering of photons by free charged particles. With the photoelectric effect and pair production, it is one of the three main ways photons interact with matter.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.3.1: X-Rays versus Visible Light
> Compton used the molybdenum K$_\alpha$ line, $\lambda = 71.1\ \mathrm{pm}$. At $\theta = 90^\circ$ the shift is $\Delta\lambda = \lambda_c(1 - 0) = 2.43\ \mathrm{pm}$, so $\lambda' = 73.5\ \mathrm{pm}$: a $3.4\%$ change, easily resolved. In energies,
>
> $$
> E = \frac{1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}}{0.0711\ \mathrm{nm}} = 17.44\ \mathrm{keV}, \qquad E' = \frac{1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}}{0.07353\ \mathrm{nm}} = 16.86\ \mathrm{keV} ,
> $$
>
> and the electron carries off $K = E - E' = 0.58\ \mathrm{keV}$. The largest possible shift, at $180^\circ$, is $2\lambda_c = 4.86\ \mathrm{pm}$; backscattered photons are rare (typically under $5\%$ of the beam), so such measurements need sensitive detectors. Green light of $500\ \mathrm{nm}$ scattered at $90^\circ$ would be shifted by the same $2.43\ \mathrm{pm}$, a fraction $5\times10^{-6}$ of its wavelength: unobservable, which is why the effect waited for X-rays.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 6.8; Tipler §3-4; PHY 284, class 16 slides*

^ex-a1-3-1

> [!example] Example §A1.3.2: A Free Electron Cannot Absorb a Photon
> Could a free electron at rest swallow a photon of energy $E_\gamma$ whole, as in the photoelectric effect? Afterwards the electron alone would carry the photon's momentum, $p = E_\gamma/c$, and energy conservation would require
>
> $$
> E_\gamma + m_ec^2 = \sqrt{(pc)^2 + (m_ec^2)^2} = \sqrt{E_\gamma^2 + (m_ec^2)^2} .
> $$
>
> Squaring, $E_\gamma^2 + 2E_\gamma m_ec^2 + (m_ec^2)^2 = E_\gamma^2 + (m_ec^2)^2$, so $E_\gamma = 0$: there was no photon. Energy and momentum cannot both be conserved. The photoelectric effect therefore needs a bound electron, whose atom or crystal takes up the momentum; a free electron can only *scatter* a photon, as in the Compton effect, where the outgoing photon balances the books. (The instructor's solution makes the same argument in the centre-of-momentum frame, where the final electron would be at rest with energy $m_ec^2$, less than the initial energy.)
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 5 (and solutions)*

^ex-a1-3-2
