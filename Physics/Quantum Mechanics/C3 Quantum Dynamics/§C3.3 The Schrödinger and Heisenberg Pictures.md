Everything measurable is a matrix element $\langle\beta|X|\alpha\rangle$, and a unitary transformation can be charged either to the kets or to the operator. Applied to time evolution this gives two bookkeepings of one physics: the Schrödinger picture of [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation|§C3.1]], with moving kets, and the Heisenberg picture, with frozen kets and moving observables. Level B worked only with moving states: it derived $d\langle Q\rangle/dt = \frac i\hbar\langle[\hat H, \hat Q]\rangle$ ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]]), Ehrenfest's theorem ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]) and conservation laws ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-8|Theorem §B2.4.8]]) for expectation values, by differentiating integrals of wave functions. Here those statements become operator identities, valid before any state is chosen: the Heisenberg equation of motion, which is the classical Poisson-bracket equation ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|CM Theorem §B8.1.3]]) with the bracket replaced by a commutator, and Newton's second law for the position operator, of which Ehrenfest's theorem is the expectation value. New at this level are the pictures themselves, the motion of base kets and the transition amplitude. A third bookkeeping, the interaction picture, belongs to time-dependent perturbation theory and is homed in [[§C9.6 The Interaction Picture and the Atom–Field Interaction|§C9.6]].

*Notation.* $\mathscr U(t) = \mathscr U(t, 0)$, equal to $e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ for a time-independent $H$; superscripts $(S)$ and $(H)$ mark Schrödinger- and Heisenberg-picture operators and are dropped when the picture is clear. $\{A, B\} = AB + BA$ is the anticommutator; $\delta A = A - \langle A\rangle$.

## Unitary transformations, two ways

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.1: Two Equivalent Ways to Apply a Unitary Transformation
> Let $U$ be unitary (for instance a translation $\mathcal T(d\mathbf x')$ or a time evolution $\mathscr U(t, t_0)$, operators that actually change the physical state, unlike the change of basis of [[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence|§C1.4]]).
> 1. Inner products of state kets are invariant: $\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle \to \langle\beta|U^\dagger U|\alpha\rangle = \langle\beta|\alpha\rangle$.
> 2. For every operator $X$, $(\langle\beta|U^\dagger)\,X\,(U|\alpha\rangle) = \langle\beta|\,(U^\dagger XU)\,|\alpha\rangle$. So transforming all state kets, $|\alpha\rangle \to U|\alpha\rangle$ with operators unchanged (*approach 1*), gives the same matrix elements, hence the same predictions, as keeping the kets and transforming every operator, $X \to U^\dagger XU$ (*approach 2*).
> 3. The map $X \mapsto U^\dagger XU$ preserves sums, products, adjoints and therefore commutators, functions of operators, $f(X) \mapsto f(U^\dagger XU)$, and spectra: if $X|x'\rangle = x'|x'\rangle$, then $(U^\dagger XU)(U^\dagger|x'\rangle) = x'\,U^\dagger|x'\rangle$.
>
> For an infinitesimal translation, approach 2 moves the position operator itself: $\mathbf x \to \bigl(1 + \tfrac{i}{\hbar}\mathbf p\cdot d\mathbf x'\bigr)\mathbf x\bigl(1 - \tfrac{i}{\hbar}\mathbf p\cdot d\mathbf x'\bigr) = \mathbf x + \tfrac{i}{\hbar}[\mathbf p\cdot d\mathbf x', \mathbf x] = \mathbf x + d\mathbf x'$, the way a classical coordinate changes; both approaches give $\langle\mathbf x\rangle \to \langle\mathbf x\rangle + d\mathbf x'$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.2.1, eqs. (2.75)–(2.82) · the user's 511 notes, §"Unitary Operators"; part 3 derived here*

^thm-c3-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1–2 are the associative axiom ([[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations|§C1.2]]) and $U^\dagger U = 1$. For 3, use $UU^\dagger = 1$: $U^\dagger XU\,U^\dagger YU = U^\dagger XYU$, $(U^\dagger XU)^\dagger = U^\dagger X^\dagger U$, hence $U^\dagger[X, Y]U = [U^\dagger XU, U^\dagger YU]$; a power series in $X$ is carried term by term; and $U^\dagger XU\,U^\dagger|x'\rangle = U^\dagger X|x'\rangle = x'\,U^\dagger|x'\rangle$. For the translation, $[\mathbf p\cdot d\mathbf x', x_k] = dx'_j\,[p_j, x_k] = -i\hbar\,dx'_k$ ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator|§C2.2]]), so $\frac{i}{\hbar}[\mathbf p\cdot d\mathbf x', \mathbf x] = d\mathbf x'$, to first order.

*Uses:* [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations|§C1.2]], [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator|§C2.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.2: Baker–Hausdorff Lemma
> For a Hermitian $G$, a real parameter $\lambda$ and any operator $A$,
>
> $$
> e^{iG\lambda}A\,e^{-iG\lambda} = A + i\lambda[G, A] + \frac{(i\lambda)^2}{2!}\bigl[G, [G, A]\bigr] + \cdots + \frac{(i\lambda)^n}{n!}\bigl[G, [G, \dots[G, A]\dots]\bigr] + \cdots
> $$
>
> (rigorous when the operators are bounded, a formal identity otherwise). Two cases terminate or sum in closed form:
> 1. if $[G, A] = c$ is a number, $e^{iG\lambda}Ae^{-iG\lambda} = A + i\lambda c$; for example $e^{ip\ell/\hbar}\,x\,e^{-ip\ell/\hbar} = x + \ell$;
> 2. if $[G, A] = \kappa A$ for a number $\kappa$, then $e^{iG\lambda}Ae^{-iG\lambda} = e^{i\kappa\lambda}A$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.3.2, eq. (2.168) (proof left to the reader there; given here) · the user's 511 notes, §"Time Development of the Oscillator" · PHY 511, HW 5, Problem 3(i) (the translated position operator)*

^thm-c3-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $f(\lambda) = e^{iG\lambda}Ae^{-iG\lambda}$. Since $G$ commutes with $e^{\pm iG\lambda}$, $f'(\lambda) = e^{iG\lambda}\,i(GA - AG)\,e^{-iG\lambda} = e^{iG\lambda}\,i[G, A]\,e^{-iG\lambda}$: differentiating replaces $A$ by $i[G, A]$. Repeating, $f^{(n)}(0) = i^n[G, [G, \dots[G, A]\dots]]$ ($n$ nested commutators), and the Taylor series of $f$ about $0$ is the lemma. *1:* all commutators beyond the first vanish, because $[G, c] = 0$; with $G = p$, $\lambda = \ell/\hbar$ and $[p, x] = -i\hbar$, $i\lambda c = i(\ell/\hbar)(-i\hbar) = \ell$. *2:* the $n$-th nested commutator is $\kappa^nA$, and $\sum(i\kappa\lambda)^n/n! = e^{i\kappa\lambda}$.

## The two pictures

> [!definition] Definition §C3.3.1: Schrödinger Picture; Heisenberg Picture
> In the **Schrödinger picture** observables are fixed operators $A^{(S)}$ and state kets move, $|\alpha, t_0 = 0; t\rangle_S = \mathscr U(t)|\alpha, t_0 = 0\rangle$ ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]). In the **Heisenberg picture** state kets are frozen at their value at $t = 0$, $|\alpha, t_0 = 0; t\rangle_H = |\alpha, t_0 = 0\rangle$, and observables move:
>
> $$
> A^{(H)}(t) = \mathscr U^\dagger(t)\,A^{(S)}\,\mathscr U(t), \qquad A^{(H)}(0) = A^{(S)} .
> $$
>
> The two pictures are approaches 1 and 2 of [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]] applied to $U = \mathscr U(t)$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.2.2, eqs. (2.83)–(2.87) · the user's 511 notes, §"State Kets and Observables in the Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures" · the user's series, Part IV, §7.1*

^def-c3-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.3: The Pictures Are Equivalent
> 1. Every expectation value, and every matrix element between evolved states, is the same in both pictures: ${}_S\langle\alpha; t|A^{(S)}|\alpha; t\rangle_S = \langle\alpha|A^{(H)}(t)|\alpha\rangle$.
> 2. Algebraic relations among observables hold at every **equal time** in the Heisenberg picture: $[x_i^{(H)}(t), p_j^{(H)}(t)] = i\hbar\delta_{ij}$, $[S_i^{(H)}(t), S_j^{(H)}(t)] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}S_k^{(H)}(t)$, and $f(A)^{(H)}(t) = f\bigl(A^{(H)}(t)\bigr)$ for every function $f$ given by a power series, for instance $V(\mathbf x)^{(H)} = V(\mathbf x^{(H)}(t))$.
> 3. $A^{(H)}(t)$ has the same eigenvalues as $A^{(S)}$ at all times.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.2.2, eq. (2.88) · the user's series, Part IV, §7.1 (corollary "The canonical structure rides along"); parts 2–3 from Theorem §C3.3.1*

^thm-c3-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> All three are [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]] with $U = \mathscr U(t)$: part 1 is item 2 there, parts 2 and 3 are item 3, applied to the canonical commutators of [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator|§C2.2]] and the spin commutators of [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations|§C1.2]]. At *different* times nothing forces commutation; see Theorem §C3.3.5.

## The Heisenberg equation of motion

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.4: Heisenberg Equation of Motion
> Heisenberg-picture observables obey
>
> $$
> \frac{dA^{(H)}}{dt} = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\bigl[A^{(H)}, H^{(H)}\bigr] + \Bigl(\frac{\partial A^{(S)}}{\partial t}\Bigr)^{(H)}, \qquad H^{(H)} = \mathscr U^\dagger H\,\mathscr U ,
> $$
>
> the last term present only if $A^{(S)}$ depends explicitly on time. For a time-independent $H$, $H^{(H)} = H$, and for $A^{(S)}$ without explicit time dependence
>
> $$
> \frac{dA^{(H)}}{dt} = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\bigl[A^{(H)}, H\bigr] .
> $$
>
> Consequently an observable commuting with $H$ is a **constant of the motion** as an operator: $A^{(H)}(t) = A^{(S)}$ for all $t$. Taking the expectation value in the frozen state gives level B's $d\langle A\rangle/dt = \frac i\hbar\langle[H, A]\rangle + \langle\partial A/\partial t\rangle$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.2.3, eqs. (2.89)–(2.95) · the user's 511 notes, §"The Heisenberg Equation of Motion" · the user's series, Part IV, §7.1 · the user's PHY 511 midterm formula sheet (with the explicit-time term)*

^thm-c3-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]], $\partial_t\mathscr U = \frac{1}{i\hbar}H\mathscr U$ and, taking adjoints, $\partial_t\mathscr U^\dagger = -\frac{1}{i\hbar}\mathscr U^\dagger H$. Differentiate $A^{(H)} = \mathscr U^\dagger A^{(S)}\mathscr U$ by the product rule and insert $\mathscr U\mathscr U^\dagger = 1$ next to $H$:
>
> $$
> \frac{dA^{(H)}}{dt} = -\frac{1}{i\hbar}\,\mathscr U^\dagger H\mathscr U\,\mathscr U^\dagger A^{(S)}\mathscr U + \mathscr U^\dagger\frac{\partial A^{(S)}}{\partial t}\mathscr U + \frac{1}{i\hbar}\,\mathscr U^\dagger A^{(S)}\mathscr U\,\mathscr U^\dagger H\mathscr U = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\bigl[A^{(H)}, H^{(H)}\bigr] + \Bigl(\frac{\partial A^{(S)}}{\partial t}\Bigr)^{(H)} .
> $$
>
> For time-independent $H$, $\mathscr U = e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ is a function of $H$ and commutes with it, so $H^{(H)} = H$. If $[A^{(S)}, H] = 0$, then $[A^{(H)}, H] = \mathscr U^\dagger[A^{(S)}, H]\mathscr U = 0$ ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]], 3), so $dA^{(H)}/dt = 0$. The expectation value in a frozen ket can be differentiated under the bracket.

^der-c3-3-4

*Uses:* [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]], [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]], [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The classical bracket, transcribed
> - **Layer change.** Level B's [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]] (Griffiths' "generalized Ehrenfest theorem") is the expectation value of Theorem §C3.3.4, and its conservation laws ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-8|Theorem §B2.4.8]]) become the statement that a commuting observable is a constant operator. The derivation uses only the properties of $\mathscr U$, not wave functions.
> - **Correspondence.** Classically, a function $A(q, p)$ without explicit time dependence evolves by $dA/dt = \{A, H\}$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|CM Theorem §B8.1.3]]; Sakurai writes the Poisson bracket $[A, H]_{\text{classical}}$). The Heisenberg equation is the same sentence with $\{\,,\} \to [\,,]/i\hbar$, Dirac's rule ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^rem-b8-1-5|CM Remark: From brackets to commutators]]; [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^rem-b2-4-1|Remark: From Poisson brackets to commutators]]), carried from kinematics to dynamics; historically Dirac wrote the equation down and named it after Heisenberg.
> - **No classical analogue needed.** The equation makes sense for observables that are not functions of $q$ and $p$: $dS_i^{(H)}/dt = \frac{1}{i\hbar}[S_i^{(H)}, H]$ describes spin precession (Example §C3.3.1). Sakurai's conclusion: the correct classical equation follows from the quantum one by $[\,,]/i\hbar \to [\,,]_{\text{classical}}$, but not conversely; classical mechanics can be derived from quantum mechanics.
> - **Choosing $H$.** For systems with a classical analogue, $H$ is taken of the classical form with $x_i, p_i$ promoted to operators, ordering ambiguities resolved by Hermiticity ($xp \to \frac12(xp + px)$); otherwise $H$ is guessed and tested against experiment.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.2.3–2.2.4, eqs. (2.94)–(2.96) · the user's series, Part IV, §7.1*

^rem-c3-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.5: The Free Particle in the Heisenberg Picture
> For $H = \mathbf p^2/2m$, in the Heisenberg picture:
> 1. $\mathbf p(t) = \mathbf p(0)$ and $\mathbf x(t) = \mathbf x(0) + \mathbf p(0)\,t/m$, the classical straight-line motion as an operator identity;
> 2. positions at different times do not commute,
>
> $$
> [x_i(t), x_i(0)] = -\frac{i\hbar t}{m}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \Delta x_i(t)\,\Delta x_i(0) \ge \frac{\hbar t}{2m}\quad(t \ge 0)
> $$
>
> in every state (with $\Delta A = \langle(\delta A)^2\rangle^{1/2}$): a particle sharply localized now is poorly localized later;
> 3. in every state the variance grows as
>
> $$
> \langle(\Delta x_i)^2\rangle_t = \langle(\Delta x_i)^2\rangle_0 + \frac{t}{m}\bigl\langle\{\delta x_i, \delta p_i\}\bigr\rangle_0 + \frac{t^2}{m^2}\langle(\Delta p_i)^2\rangle_0 ,
> $$
>
> and for a minimum-uncertainty packet at $t = 0$ the middle term vanishes, giving $\Delta x(t) = \Delta x(0)\sqrt{1 + \bigl(\hbar t/2m\,\Delta x(0)^2\bigr)^2}$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.2.4, eqs. (2.98)–(2.104); Problem 2.8 (part 3) · the user's 511 notes, §"Free Particles: Ehrenfest's Theorem" · the user's series, Part IV, §7.2 and §8.2*

^thm-c3-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $p_i$ commutes with any function of $\mathbf p$, so $dp_i/dt = \frac{1}{i\hbar}[p_i, H] = 0$ ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]). With $[x_i, F(\mathbf p)] = i\hbar\,\partial F/\partial p_i$ (Sakurai eq. (2.97a); level B: [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]]), $dx_i/dt = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\cdot\frac{1}{2m}\,i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial p_i}\sum_jp_j^2 = p_i/m = p_i(0)/m$; integrate.
>
> *2.* $[x_i(t), x_i(0)] = [x_i(0) + p_i(0)t/m, x_i(0)] = \frac tm[p_i(0), x_i(0)] = -i\hbar t/m$. The uncertainty relation for two observables, $\langle(\Delta A)^2\rangle\langle(\Delta B)^2\rangle \ge \frac14|\langle[A, B]\rangle|^2$ ([[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty|§C1.3]]; level B: [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]]), applied to $A = x_i(t)$, $B = x_i(0)$ in the frozen state gives $\ge \hbar^2t^2/4m^2$.
>
> *3.* By part 1, $\delta x_i(t) = \delta x_i(0) + \frac tm\delta p_i(0)$, all expectations in the frozen state. Square and take the expectation; the cross terms give $\frac tm\langle\delta x\,\delta p + \delta p\,\delta x\rangle$. A minimum-uncertainty state satisfies $\delta p\,|\alpha\rangle = c\,\delta x\,|\alpha\rangle$ with $c$ purely imaginary (the equality case of the uncertainty relation; [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-5|Theorem §B2.4.5]]). Then $\langle\delta x\,\delta p\rangle = c\,\langle(\delta x)^2\rangle$ is imaginary, $\langle\delta p\,\delta x\rangle$ is its complex conjugate, and the anticommutator, twice the real part, vanishes. With $\Delta p(0) = \hbar/2\Delta x(0)$ the variance is $\Delta x(0)^2 + \hbar^2t^2/4m^2\Delta x(0)^2$. This is the spreading level B computed by integrating the Gaussian ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-4|Theorem §B1.4.4]]), now without a wave function and with the general formula for any initial state.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.6: Newton's Second Law for Operators; Ehrenfest's Theorem
> For $H = \mathbf p^2/2m + V(\mathbf x)$, with $V$ a function of the position operators, the Heisenberg-picture operators obey
>
> $$
> \frac{d\mathbf x}{dt} = \frac{\mathbf p}{m}, \qquad \frac{d\mathbf p}{dt} = -\nabla V(\mathbf x), \qquad m\,\frac{d^2\mathbf x}{dt^2} = -\nabla V(\mathbf x) ,
> $$
>
> the quantum analogue of Newton's second law, meaningful as an operator equation only in the Heisenberg picture. Its expectation value, which holds in either picture, is **Ehrenfest's theorem**:
>
> $$
> m\,\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\langle\mathbf x\rangle = \frac{d\langle\mathbf p\rangle}{dt} = -\bigl\langle\nabla V(\mathbf x)\bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> $\hbar$ has disappeared: the centre of a wave packet moves like a classical particle in $V$, to the extent that $\langle\nabla V(\mathbf x)\rangle$ may be replaced by $\nabla V(\langle\mathbf x\rangle)$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.2.4, eqs. (2.105)–(2.110) · the user's 511 notes, §"Free Particles: Ehrenfest's Theorem" · the user's series, Part IV, §7.2–7.3*

^thm-c3-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $[p_i, G(\mathbf x)] = -i\hbar\,\partial G/\partial x_i$ (Sakurai eq. (2.97b); [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]]), [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]] gives $dp_i/dt = \frac{1}{i\hbar}[p_i, V(\mathbf x)] = -\partial V/\partial x_i$. $x_i$ commutes with $V(\mathbf x)$, so $dx_i/dt = p_i/m$ as for the free particle ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-5|Theorem §C3.3.5]]). Apply the Heisenberg equation once more to $dx_i/dt$: $\frac{d^2x_i}{dt^2} = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\bigl[\frac{p_i}{m}, H\bigr] = \frac1m\frac{dp_i}{dt}$. All operators are Heisenberg-picture operators at the same time, and $V(\mathbf x)^{(H)} = V(\mathbf x^{(H)})$ by [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]], 2. Taking the expectation in the frozen ket commutes with $d/dt$; by Theorem §C3.3.3, 1 the result is picture independent. (Sakurai's text cites eq. (2.32) for this combination; the equation meant is (2.106).)

> [!remark] Remark: Ehrenfest, a second time

Here it is the expectation value of an operator identity, Newton's law "wearing hats" in the series' phrase, which is the quantum transcription of the classical calculation that gets $m\ddot q = -U'(q)$ from Poisson brackets alone ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^ex-b8-1-1|CM Example §B8.1.1]]). ## Base kets and transition amplitudes

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.3.7: Base Kets and Transition Amplitudes
> Let $H$ be time independent and $A|a'\rangle = a'|a'\rangle$ at $t = 0$.
> 1. In the Schrödinger picture the base kets $|a'\rangle$ do not move: unlike state kets, they are eigenkets of a fixed operator.
> 2. In the Heisenberg picture the eigenkets of $A^{(H)}(t)$ are
>
> $$
> |a', t\rangle_H = \mathscr U^\dagger(t)\,|a'\rangle, \qquad A^{(H)}(t)\,|a', t\rangle_H = a'\,|a', t\rangle_H, \qquad A^{(H)}(t) = \sum_{a'}|a', t\rangle_H\,a'\,{}_H\langle a', t| ,
> $$
>
> with eigenvalues unchanged; they rotate oppositely to Schrödinger state kets, obeying the "wrong-sign Schrödinger equation" $i\hbar\,\partial_t|a', t\rangle_H = -H\,|a', t\rangle_H$.
> 3. Expansion coefficients are the same in both pictures: $c_{a'}(t) = \langle a'|\bigl(\mathscr U|\alpha\rangle\bigr) = \bigl(\langle a'|\mathscr U\bigr)|\alpha\rangle$. In particular, for a system prepared at $t = 0$ in the eigenket $|a'\rangle$ of $A$, the **transition amplitude** to be found at time $t$ in the eigenket $|b'\rangle$ of $B$ ($A$ and $B$ the same or different) is, in either picture,
>
> $$
> \langle b'|\,\mathscr U(t, 0)\,|a'\rangle .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.2.5, eqs. (2.111)–(2.121), Table 2.1 · the user's 511 notes, §"Base Kets and Transition Amplitudes"*

^thm-c3-3-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *2.* Multiply $A(0)|a'\rangle = a'|a'\rangle$ by $\mathscr U^\dagger$ and insert $\mathscr U\mathscr U^\dagger = 1$: $\mathscr U^\dagger A(0)\mathscr U\,\mathscr U^\dagger|a'\rangle = a'\,\mathscr U^\dagger|a'\rangle$, which is the eigenvalue equation for $A^{(H)}(t)$ ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]], 3). The spectral sum: $\sum_{a'}\mathscr U^\dagger|a'\rangle a'\langle a'|\mathscr U = \mathscr U^\dagger A^{(S)}\mathscr U$. For the wrong sign: $\mathscr U^\dagger = e^{iHt/\hbar}$, so $i\hbar\,\partial_t\mathscr U^\dagger = -H\mathscr U^\dagger$.
>
> *3.* Associativity: $\langle a'|(\mathscr U|\alpha\rangle) = (\langle a'|\mathscr U)|\alpha\rangle$, and $\langle a'|\mathscr U$ is the bra of $\mathscr U^\dagger|a'\rangle = |a', t\rangle_H$. With $|\alpha\rangle = |a'\rangle$ and the base bra $\langle b'|$: Schrödinger, fixed bra times moving ket; Heisenberg, moving bra times fixed ket; the same number.

*Uses:* [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]], [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The bookkeeping in one table, and matrix mechanics
> A common misconception is that in the Schrödinger picture all kets move and in the Heisenberg picture all kets stand still; the base kets do the opposite of the state kets. Pictorially, the cosine of the angle between state ket and base ket is the same whether the state ket turns one way or the base ket the other.
>
> | | Schrödinger picture | Heisenberg picture |
> |---|---|---|
> | state ket | moving: $\mathscr U(t)\lvert\alpha\rangle$ | stationary |
> | observable | stationary | moving: $\mathscr U^\dagger A\,\mathscr U$, Heisenberg equation |
> | base ket | stationary | moving oppositely: $\mathscr U^\dagger\lvert a'\rangle$ |
>
> - The wave function $\langle\mathbf x'|\alpha; t\rangle$ is either a fixed position bra times a moving state ket or a moving position bra times a fixed state ket; its time dependence is Schrödinger's wave equation ([[§C3.5 Wave Mechanics, the Linear Potential and WKB Revisited|§C3.5]]), and the amplitude $\langle\mathbf x''|\mathscr U(t, 0)|\mathbf x'\rangle$ is the propagator ([[§C4.1 Propagators|§C4.1]]).
> - In the energy basis the Heisenberg operator's matrix elements are $\langle m|A^{(H)}(t)|n\rangle = A_{mn}\,e^{i\omega_{mn}t}$, $\omega_{mn} = (E_m - E_n)/\hbar$: every observable is a table of numbers oscillating at the Bohr frequencies ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-6|Theorem §C3.1.6]]). That is what Heisenberg wrote down in 1925; matrix mechanics is the Heisenberg picture in the energy basis, and the 1925–1926 rivalry with wave mechanics is a choice of picture.
> - When quantum mechanics meets relativity it is the Heisenberg picture that generalizes, with operators carrying the spacetime label ([[Quantum Field Theory I]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.2.5, Table 2.1 · the user's series, Part IV, §7.3–7.4 (Remarks "Matrix mechanics recovered" and "One theory, many coordinates")*

> [!remark] Remark: A third picture

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C3.3.1: Spin Precession in the Heisenberg Picture
> A spin $\tfrac12$ sits in $\mathbf B = B\hat z$, $H = \omega S_z$. Write and solve the Heisenberg equations for $S_x$, $S_y$, $S_z$ with initial values $S_i(0)$.
>
> *Equations.* With $[S_x, S_z] = -i\hbar S_y$, $[S_y, S_z] = i\hbar S_x$ ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-3|Theorem §C3.3.3]], 2, at equal times),
>
> $$
> \frac{dS_x}{dt} = \frac{\omega}{i\hbar}[S_x, S_z] = -\omega S_y, \qquad \frac{dS_y}{dt} = \frac{\omega}{i\hbar}[S_y, S_z] = \omega S_x, \qquad \frac{dS_z}{dt} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> that is $d\mathbf S/dt = \omega\hat z\times\mathbf S$, a classical-looking precession equation for operators that have no classical counterpart.
>
> *Solution.* $S_z(t) = S_z(0)$. Differentiating once more, $\ddot S_x = -\omega^2S_x$ with $\dot S_x(0) = -\omega S_y(0)$:
>
> $$
> S_x(t) = S_x(0)\cos\omega t - S_y(0)\sin\omega t, \qquad S_y(t) = S_y(0)\cos\omega t + S_x(0)\sin\omega t .
> $$
>
> *Check by the lemma.* $[S_z, S_\pm] = \pm\hbar S_\pm$, so case 2 of [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-2|Theorem §C3.3.2]] with $G = \omega S_z$, $\lambda = t/\hbar$, $\kappa = \pm\hbar\omega$ gives $S_\pm(t) = e^{\pm i\omega t}S_\pm(0)$, whose real and imaginary parts are the two lines above. Taking expectation values in $|S_x;+\rangle$ reproduces $\langle S_x\rangle = \frac\hbar2\cos\omega t$, $\langle S_y\rangle = \frac\hbar2\sin\omega t$ of [[§C3.2 Two-State Systems, Spin Precession and Neutrino Oscillations#^thm-c3-2-3|Theorem §C3.2.3]]; here the rotation is carried by the operators, valid for every state at once.
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 5, Problem 1 (= Sakurai Problem 2.1; solved here)*

> [!example] Example §C3.3.2: The Dipole Sum Rule
> For $H = p^2/2m + V(x)$ in one dimension with energy eigenkets $|a'\rangle$, prove
>
> $$
> \sum_{a'}\bigl|\langle a''|x|a'\rangle\bigr|^2\,(E_{a'} - E_{a''}) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}
> $$
>
> for every eigenket $|a''\rangle$, by considering $[[H, x], x]$.
>
> *The double commutator is a number.* $[H, x] = -i\hbar p/m$ ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]]; the same commutator that gives $dx/dt = p/m$), so $[[H, x], x] = -\frac{i\hbar}{m}[p, x] = -\frac{\hbar^2}{m}$.
>
> *Its diagonal element in the energy basis.* $[[H, x], x] = Hx^2 - 2xHx + x^2H$. Insert $1 = \sum_{a'}|a'\rangle\langle a'|$ and use $H|a''\rangle = E_{a''}|a''\rangle$, $x^\dagger = x$:
>
> $$
> \langle a''|[[H, x], x]|a''\rangle = 2E_{a''}\sum_{a'}|\langle a'|x|a''\rangle|^2 - 2\sum_{a'}E_{a'}|\langle a'|x|a''\rangle|^2 = -2\sum_{a'}(E_{a'} - E_{a''})\,|\langle a''|x|a'\rangle|^2 .
> $$
>
> Equating with $-\hbar^2/m$ gives the rule (the Thomas–Reiche–Kuhn sum rule). The matrix elements $\langle a''|x|a'\rangle$ are those of the dipole approximation to atomic transitions ([[§C9.6 The Interaction Picture and the Atom–Field Interaction#^thm-c9-6-8|Theorem §C9.6.8]], where the rule returns as the oscillator-strength sum rule).
>
> *Check on the oscillator.* Only $a' = n \pm 1$ contribute, with $|\langle n\pm1|x|n\rangle|^2 = \frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}(n + 1)$ or $\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}n$ ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]) and energy differences $\pm\hbar\omega$: $\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\hbar\omega\,[(n + 1) - n] = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}$ for every $n$.
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 5, Problem 4 (= Sakurai Problem 2.6; solved here)*
