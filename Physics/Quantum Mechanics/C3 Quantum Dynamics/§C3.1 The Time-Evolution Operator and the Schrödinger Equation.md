How does a state ket change with time? Levels A and B answered by postulate: the Schrödinger equation ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]], [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-4|Principle §B2.3.4]]), solved by expanding in stationary states ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]]); level B noted in passing that the resulting map is unitary ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^rem-b2-3-2|Remark: Time evolution is unitary]]) and proved the energy–time relation in Mandelstam–Tamm form ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-9|Theorem §B2.4.9]]). Level C reverses the logic, as [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator|§C2.2]] did for translations: it demands of the time-evolution operator $\mathscr U(t, t_0)$ what probability and causality require (unitarity, composition, continuity), names its generator the Hamiltonian, and *derives* the Schrödinger equation, for kets and for $\mathscr U$ itself. The completeness relation used to expand in energy eigenkets is the rigorous fact of [[§25 The Completeness Relation#^thm-25-2|556 Thm. §25.2]]. What is new beyond that: the formal solution for a time-dependent $H$ (the time-ordered exponential), the energy-eigenket form of $\mathscr U$ for any complete set of observables commuting with $H$, Bohr frequencies for every expectation value, and the correlation amplitude as a second, sharper reading of the energy–time relation.

*Notation.* Sakurai's: $|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle$ is the state at time $t$ of a system that was in $|\alpha\rangle = |\alpha, t_0\rangle$ at $t_0$; $\mathscr U(t, t_0)$ is the time-evolution operator (level B: $\hat U(t)$); eigenkets carry primes, $A|a'\rangle = a'|a'\rangle$. Operators carry no hats. $\hbar$ is explicit.

## The time-evolution operator

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.1: Time-Evolution Operator
> The **time-evolution operator** $\mathscr U(t, t_0)$ is the operator on the ket space that takes the state at time $t_0$ to the state at time $t$, whatever that state is:
>
> $$
> |\alpha, t_0; t\rangle = \mathscr U(t, t_0)\,|\alpha, t_0\rangle, \qquad \lim_{t \to t_0}|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle = |\alpha, t_0\rangle .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.1.1, eqs. (2.1)–(2.5) · the user's 511 notes, §"Time Evolution Operator"*

^def-c3-1-1

> [!principle] Principle §C3.1.1: Properties of Time Evolution
> The time-evolution operator has the following properties.
> 1. **Unitarity** (probability conservation): $\mathscr U^\dagger(t, t_0)\,\mathscr U(t, t_0) = 1$. If $|\alpha, t_0\rangle = \sum_{a'}c_{a'}(t_0)|a'\rangle$, the individual $|c_{a'}(t)|$ may change, but $\sum_{a'}|c_{a'}(t)|^2 = \sum_{a'}|c_{a'}(t_0)|^2$.
> 2. **Composition**: $\mathscr U(t_2, t_0) = \mathscr U(t_2, t_1)\,\mathscr U(t_1, t_0)$ for $t_2 > t_1 > t_0$ (read from right to left).
> 3. **Continuity, and the Hamiltonian as generator**: $\mathscr U(t_0, t_0) = 1$, and to first order in $dt$
>
> $$
> \mathscr U(t_0 + dt, t_0) = 1 - \frac{i}{\hbar}\,H\,dt ,
> $$
>
> where $H$ (possibly depending on $t_0$) is the **Hamiltonian**, the observable of energy. The frequency $H/\hbar$ is fixed by the Planck–Einstein relation $E = \hbar\omega$ and by the classical role of the Hamiltonian as the generator of time evolution; the $\hbar$ is the same as in the translation operator $\mathcal T(d\mathbf x') = 1 - i\mathbf p\cdot d\mathbf x'/\hbar$ ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator|§C2.2]]).
>
> *Domain: a closed system, or one in prescribed external fields (then $H$ depends on time); between measurements ([[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty|§C1.3]]); nonrelativistic.*
> *Source: Sakurai §2.1.1, eqs. (2.9)–(2.21), (2.31) · the user's 511 notes, §"Time Evolution Operator" · the user's series, Part IV, §6.1*

^pr-c3-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.2: The Generator Is Hermitian
> If $\mathscr U(t_0 + dt, t_0) = 1 - i\Omega\,dt + O(dt^2)$ is unitary to first order in $dt$, then $\Omega^\dagger = \Omega$; conversely, a Hermitian $\Omega$ makes $1 - i\Omega\,dt$ unitary to first order, and compounding two such steps differs from $1$ by a term of first order in $dt_1 + dt_2$. Hence the Hamiltonian of Principle §C3.1.1 is Hermitian: an observable.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.1.1, eqs. (2.15)–(2.18) (stated there as an assertion; derived here)*

^thm-c3-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\mathscr U^\dagger\mathscr U = (1 + i\Omega^\dagger dt)(1 - i\Omega\,dt) + O(dt^2) = 1 + i(\Omega^\dagger - \Omega)\,dt + O(dt^2)$. This equals $1$ to first order exactly when $\Omega^\dagger = \Omega$. For composition, $(1 - i\Omega\,dt_2)(1 - i\Omega\,dt_1) = 1 - i\Omega(dt_1 + dt_2) + O(dt^2)$, which has the form of a single step of length $dt_1 + dt_2$ (Sakurai eq. (2.17)).

^der-c3-1-2

*Uses:* [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^pr-c3-1-1|Principle §C3.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Layer change from level B
> At levels A and B the Schrödinger equation was a principle. Here the principle is the list of properties of $\mathscr U$, and the equation becomes a theorem ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]]): its linearity, its conservation of probability and its first order in time are inherited from the linearity, unitarity and one-parameter composition of $\mathscr U$, rather than postulated. The construction is the one that made momentum the generator of translations ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator|§C2.2]]); the user's series calls the pair (momentum ↔ spatial shifts, Hamiltonian ↔ waiting) the rows of a quantum Noether dictionary, and classically it is the statement that $H$ generates the flow in time ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-5|CM Theorem §B8.1.5]]). Two pieces of fine print: that the same $\hbar$ appears in $\mathcal T$ and $\mathscr U$ is checked by the classical limit $d\langle\mathbf x\rangle/dt = \langle\mathbf p\rangle/m$ ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures|§C3.3]]); and that every continuous one-parameter unitary group has a self-adjoint generator is Stone's theorem, which the series states (citing Hall, §10.2) without proof.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.1.1, eq. (2.22) · the user's series, Part IV, §6.1*

## The Schrödinger equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.3: The Schrödinger Equation
> 1. The time-evolution operator obeys
>
> $$
> i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\,\mathscr U(t, t_0) = H\,\mathscr U(t, t_0), \qquad \mathscr U(t_0, t_0) = 1 .
> $$
>
> 2. Every state ket obeys the **Schrödinger equation**
>
> $$
> i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\,|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle = H\,|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle .
> $$
>
> 3. Conversely, for a Hermitian $H(t)$ the solution of 1 is unitary and has the composition property, so Principle §C3.1.1 and the operator equation are equivalent.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.1.2, eqs. (2.23)–(2.27) · the user's 511 notes, §"The Schrödinger Equation" · the user's series, Part IV, §6.1*

^thm-c3-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Compose an evolution from $t_0$ to $t$ (not necessarily short) with a step from $t$ to $t + dt$ (Principle §C3.1.1, 2 and 3):
>
> $$
> \mathscr U(t + dt, t_0) = \mathscr U(t + dt, t)\,\mathscr U(t, t_0) = \Bigl(1 - \frac{i}{\hbar}H\,dt\Bigr)\mathscr U(t, t_0) + O(dt^2) ,
> $$
>
> so $\bigl[\mathscr U(t + dt, t_0) - \mathscr U(t, t_0)\bigr]/dt \to -\frac{i}{\hbar}H\,\mathscr U(t, t_0)$; multiply by $i\hbar$.
>
> *2.* Apply 1 to $|\alpha, t_0\rangle$, which does not depend on $t$, and use [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]].
>
> *3.* The adjoint of 1 is $-i\hbar\,\partial_t\mathscr U^\dagger = \mathscr U^\dagger H$ ($H^\dagger = H$, [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]]). Then
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigl(\mathscr U^\dagger\mathscr U\bigr) = \frac{i}{\hbar}\,\mathscr U^\dagger H\,\mathscr U - \frac{i}{\hbar}\,\mathscr U^\dagger H\,\mathscr U = 0 ,
> $$
>
> and $\mathscr U^\dagger\mathscr U = 1$ at $t = t_0$, hence always. For composition, fix $t_1$: both $\mathscr U(t, t_0)$ and $\mathscr U(t, t_1)\,\mathscr U(t_1, t_0)$ solve $i\hbar\,\partial_tX = HX$ and agree at $t = t_1$, so they agree for all $t$ by uniqueness of solutions of a linear differential equation (immediate in finite dimensions and for bounded $H$).

^der-c3-1-3

*Uses:* [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^pr-c3-1-1|Principle §C3.1.1]], [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]], [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.4: Formal Solutions; the Time-Ordered Exponential
> 1. If $H$ does not depend on time,
>
> $$
> \mathscr U(t, t_0) = \exp\Bigl[-\frac{iH(t - t_0)}{\hbar}\Bigr] = \lim_{N\to\infty}\Bigl[1 - \frac{(iH/\hbar)(t - t_0)}{N}\Bigr]^N .
> $$
>
> 2. If $H$ depends on time but $[H(t'), H(t'')] = 0$ for all $t', t''$ (for instance a spin in a field of fixed direction and varying strength),
>
> $$
> \mathscr U(t, t_0) = \exp\Bigl[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t_0}^t dt'\,H(t')\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> 3. In general (for instance a field whose direction changes, $H \propto \mathbf S\cdot\mathbf B(t)$), $\mathscr U$ is the **Dyson series**, equivalently the **time-ordered exponential**:
>
> $$
> \mathscr U(t, t_0) = 1 + \sum_{n=1}^\infty\Bigl(\frac{-i}{\hbar}\Bigr)^n\int_{t_0}^t dt_1\int_{t_0}^{t_1}dt_2\cdots\int_{t_0}^{t_{n-1}}dt_n\,H(t_1)H(t_2)\cdots H(t_n) = \mathrm T\exp\Bigl[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t_0}^t dt'\,H(t')\Bigr],
> $$
>
> where in each product the times decrease from left to right, and $\mathrm T$ (time ordering) rearranges any product of $H$'s in that order before integrating over the whole cube $[t_0, t]^n$, with $1/n!$. If $\|H(t')\| \le h$ on $[t_0, t]$, the $n$-th term has norm at most $\bigl(h(t - t_0)/\hbar\bigr)^n/n!$, so the series converges.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.1.2, eqs. (2.28)–(2.33) (case 3 stated there, proof deferred to its ch. 5; proved here) · the user's 511 notes, §"The Schrödinger Equation"*

^thm-c3-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *3 first.* By [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]], $\mathscr U$ is the solution of the integral equation
>
> $$
> \mathscr U(t, t_0) = 1 - \frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t_0}^t dt_1\,H(t_1)\,\mathscr U(t_1, t_0) ,
> $$
>
> which contains the differential equation and the initial condition. Substituting the equation into itself repeatedly produces the series. Check directly: call the $n$-th term $\mathscr U_n(t)$; differentiating the upper limit of its outermost integral gives $\partial_t\mathscr U_n = -\frac{i}{\hbar}H(t)\,\mathscr U_{n-1}(t)$ ($\mathscr U_0 = 1$), so the sum satisfies $i\hbar\,\partial_t\mathscr U = H\mathscr U$, and every $\mathscr U_n$ vanishes at $t = t_0$. The ordered region $t \ge t_1 \ge \cdots \ge t_n \ge t_0$ has volume $(t - t_0)^n/n!$, which gives the bound and absolute convergence; term-by-term differentiation is then legitimate. For the time-ordered form: the cube $[t_0, t]^n$ splits into $n!$ ordered regions, one per ordering of the $t_k$; on each, $\mathrm T[H(t_1)\cdots H(t_n)]$ equals the ordered product, and relabelling the variables shows every region contributes the same integral, so $\int_{\text{cube}}\mathrm T[\cdots] = n!\int_{\text{ordered}}(\cdots)$.
>
> *2.* If all the $H(t')$ commute, ordering is irrelevant, $\mathrm T[H(t_1)\cdots H(t_n)] = H(t_1)\cdots H(t_n)$, and the integral over the cube factorizes: the $n$-th term is $\frac{1}{n!}\bigl(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t_0}^tH\bigr)^n$, the exponential series.
>
> *1.* Constant $H$ is the special case $\int_{t_0}^tH = H(t - t_0)$. Sakurai checks it by differentiating the exponential series term by term (eqs. (2.29)–(2.30)); the limit form is the compounding of $N$ infinitesimal steps, exactly as finite translations were built from infinitesimal ones ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator|§C2.2]]).

^der-c3-1-4

*Uses:* [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: The second-order term of the exponential
> In the user's 511 notes, §"The Schrödinger Equation", the expansion of $\exp[-iH(t - t_0)/\hbar]$ (eq. (2.29) there) writes the second-order coefficient as $\bigl[(-it)^2/2\bigr]\bigl[H(t - t_0)/\hbar\bigr]^2$. The time already sits in the second factor; the coefficient is $(-i)^2/2$, as in Sakurai eq. (2.29), so the term is $-\tfrac12\bigl[H(t - t_0)/\hbar\bigr]^2$.

## Energy eigenkets

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.5: Evolution in a Basis of Energy Eigenkets
> Let $H$ be time independent, and let $A$ be an observable with $[A, H] = 0$ whose eigenkets $|a'\rangle$ are nondegenerate (more generally, let $K' = (a', b', c', \dots)$ label the simultaneous eigenkets of a complete set of mutually compatible observables all commuting with $H$). Then:
> 1. each $|a'\rangle$ is an **energy eigenket**, $H|a'\rangle = E_{a'}|a'\rangle$;
> 2. the time-evolution operator is diagonal in this basis,
>
> $$
> \mathscr U(t, 0) = \exp\Bigl(-\frac{iHt}{\hbar}\Bigr) = \sum_{a'}|a'\rangle\,\exp\Bigl(-\frac{iE_{a'}t}{\hbar}\Bigr)\langle a'| ;
> $$
>
> 3. for $|\alpha, 0\rangle = \sum_{a'}c_{a'}|a'\rangle$, $c_{a'} = \langle a'|\alpha\rangle$, each coefficient only turns its phase,
>
> $$
> c_{a'}(t) = c_{a'}(0)\,\exp\Bigl(-\frac{iE_{a'}t}{\hbar}\Bigr), \qquad |c_{a'}(t)| = |c_{a'}(0)| ;
> $$
>
> 4. a system prepared in a simultaneous eigenket of $A$ and $H$ stays in it, up to the phase $e^{-iE_{a'}t/\hbar}$: an observable compatible with $H$ is a **constant of the motion**.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.1.3, eqs. (2.34)–(2.43) · the user's 511 notes, §"Energy Eigenkets" · the user's series, Part IV, §6.2*

^thm-c3-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $A(H|a'\rangle) = HA|a'\rangle = a'(H|a'\rangle)$, so $H|a'\rangle$ lies in the eigenspace of $a'$, which is one-dimensional: $H|a'\rangle = E_{a'}|a'\rangle$ (the compatible-observables theorem of [[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty|§C1.3]]; at level B [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-6|Theorem §B2.4.6]]). With a complete set of compatible observables the joint eigenspaces are one-dimensional and the same argument applies.
>
> *2.* $H^n|a'\rangle = E_{a'}^n|a'\rangle$, so the exponential series gives $e^{-iHt/\hbar}|a'\rangle = e^{-iE_{a'}t/\hbar}|a'\rangle$. Insert the completeness relation $1 = \sum_{a'}|a'\rangle\langle a'|$ on the right of $\mathscr U$ (in infinite dimensions the sum converges strongly, [[§25 The Completeness Relation#^thm-25-2|556 Thm. §25.2]], [[§25 The Completeness Relation#^cor-25-4|556 Cor. §25.4]]).
>
> *3–4.* $c_{a'}(t) = \langle a'|\mathscr U(t, 0)|\alpha\rangle = e^{-iE_{a'}t/\hbar}\langle a'|\alpha\rangle$ by 2; for $|\alpha\rangle = |a'\rangle$ only one term survives.

^der-c3-1-5

*Uses:* [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-6|Theorem §B2.4.6]], [[§25 The Completeness Relation#^cor-25-4|556 Cor. §25.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: The basic task of quantum dynamics
> With $H$ time independent, solving the dynamics reduces to: find a complete set of compatible observables that also commute with $H$; find their eigenvalues; expand the initial ket in their simultaneous eigenkets; attach the phases $e^{-iE_{K'}t/\hbar}$. The series puts it as "the entire dynamics of quantum mechanics is a set of rotating phases" and solving a system means finding its spectrum. In position representation this is level B's expansion in stationary states ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]]), and the spectral form 2 is the one level B wrote down as an aside ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^rem-b2-3-2|Remark: Time evolution is unitary]]); what is new is that it now follows from the properties of $\mathscr U$, and that the labels may be any complete commuting set, such as hydrogen's $(n, \ell, m)$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.1.3 · the user's series, Part IV, §6.2*

## Time dependence of expectation values

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.6: Expectation Values; Bohr Frequencies
> Let $H$ be time independent with energy eigenkets $|a'\rangle$, and let $B$ be any observable without explicit time dependence (it need not commute with $A$ or $H$). In the state $|\alpha, 0; t\rangle$ with $|\alpha, 0\rangle = \sum_{a'}c_{a'}|a'\rangle$,
>
> $$
> \langle B\rangle_t = \sum_{a'}\sum_{a''}c_{a'}^*c_{a''}\,\langle a'|B|a''\rangle\,e^{-i\omega_{a''a'}t}, \qquad \omega_{a''a'} = \frac{E_{a''} - E_{a'}}{\hbar} .
> $$
>
> 1. **Stationary states.** In an energy eigenstate every $\langle B\rangle$ is constant: $\langle B\rangle_t = \langle a'|B|a'\rangle$.
> 2. **Nonstationary states.** $\langle B\rangle_t$ is a sum of a constant (diagonal terms) and of terms oscillating at the **Bohr frequencies** $\omega_{a''a'}$, one for each pair of populated levels connected by $B$ ($\langle a'|B|a''\rangle \ne 0$).
> 3. **Constants of the motion.** $\langle B\rangle_t$ is constant for every initial state if and only if $[B, H] = 0$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.1.4, eqs. (2.44)–(2.48) · the user's 511 notes, §"Time Dependence of Expectation Values" · the user's series, Part IV, §6.2*

^thm-c3-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-5|Theorem §C3.1.5]], $|\alpha, 0; t\rangle = \sum_{a''}c_{a''}e^{-iE_{a''}t/\hbar}|a''\rangle$, and its bra is $\sum_{a'}c_{a'}^*e^{iE_{a'}t/\hbar}\langle a'|$; sandwich $B$ between them. *1:* one term, $a' = a''$, with phase $1$. *2:* read off the formula. *3:* if $[B, H] = 0$, the term $a' \ne a''$ needs $\langle a'|B|a''\rangle \ne 0$ with $E_{a'} \ne E_{a''}$, but $\langle a'|[B, H]|a''\rangle = (E_{a''} - E_{a'})\langle a'|B|a''\rangle = 0$, so only frequency-zero terms remain. Conversely, if $\langle B\rangle_t$ is constant for every initial state, then $\frac{d}{dt}\langle B\rangle\big|_{t=0} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle\alpha|[H, B]|\alpha\rangle = 0$ for every $|\alpha\rangle$ ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]]); $i[H, B]$ is Hermitian, and a Hermitian operator all of whose expectation values vanish is zero (polarization), so $[B, H] = 0$.

^der-c3-1-6

*Uses:* [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-5|Theorem §C3.1.5]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]]

> [!remark] Remark: What is new, and Bohr's condition
> Part 1 is level B's [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]] (same argument), and part 3 the operator side of [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-8|Theorem §B2.4.8]]. Part 2 generalizes level A's two-state beat ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-5|Theorem §A4.3.5]]) to every observable and every superposition: the spectrum of frequencies present in any measurable time dependence is the set of differences $(E_{a''} - E_{a'})/\hbar$, the frequencies Bohr postulated for emitted light ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^mod-a2-2-1|Model §A2.2.1]]). The amplitudes are the matrix elements $\langle a'|B|a''\rangle$, which is why an observable with zero matrix element between two levels (a selection rule) shows no beat at their frequency. Matrix elements oscillating at Bohr frequencies are Heisenberg's matrix mechanics; the operator form is in [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures|§C3.3]].

^rem-c3-1-3

> [!caution] Caution: Two formula-sheet shortcuts
> - The user's PHY 511 midterm formula sheet writes "$\langle A\rangle_t = \text{const} \iff [A, H] = 0$". As part 3 of Theorem §C3.1.6 shows, this holds when "constant" means constant for every state. For one given state, $[A, H] = 0$ is sufficient but not necessary: in a stationary state every $\langle A\rangle$ is constant.
> - The user's final formula sheet, §6.3, writes the coefficient evolution as $c_{a'}(t) = c_{a'}(t_0)\exp(-iE_{a'}t/\hbar)$. With a general reference time the exponent is $-iE_{a'}(t - t_0)/\hbar$; the formula as written assumes $t_0 = 0$, as Sakurai does in eq. (2.39).

## Correlation amplitude and the energy–time relation

> [!definition] Definition §C3.1.2: Correlation Amplitude
> The **correlation amplitude** of a state $|\alpha\rangle$ prepared at $t = 0$ is
>
> $$
> C(t) = \langle\alpha|\alpha, t_0 = 0; t\rangle = \langle\alpha|\mathscr U(t, 0)|\alpha\rangle .
> $$
>
> $|C(t)|$ measures how much the state at time $t$ still resembles the initial one; $|C(t)|^2$ is the probability that a measurement of the projector $|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|$ ("is the system still in $|\alpha\rangle$?") answers yes, the **survival probability**.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.1.7, eq. (2.66) · the user's 511 notes, §"Correlation Amplitude and the Energy-Time Uncertainty Relation"*

^def-c3-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.1.7: The Correlation Amplitude and the Energy Spread
> Let $H$ be time independent and $|\alpha\rangle = \sum_{a'}c_{a'}|a'\rangle$ in energy eigenkets.
> 1. $C(t)$ is the Fourier transform of the energy distribution of the state:
>
> $$
> C(t) = \sum_{a'}|c_{a'}|^2\,e^{-iE_{a'}t/\hbar} = \int dE\,w(E)\,e^{-iEt/\hbar}, \qquad w(E) = |g(E)|^2\rho(E) ,
> $$
>
> where for a quasi-continuous spectrum $\sum_{a'} \to \int dE\,\rho(E)$, $\rho$ the density of energy eigenstates, $c_{a'} \to g(E)$, and $\int dE\,w(E) = 1$. Hence $C(0) = 1$, $|C(t)| \le 1$, $C(-t) = C(t)^*$.
> 2. $|C(t)| = 1$ for all $t$ if and only if all populated levels have the same energy, i.e. $|\alpha\rangle$ is an energy eigenket; then $C(t) = e^{-iE_{a'}t/\hbar}$.
> 3. If the energy spread $\Delta E = \bigl(\langle H^2\rangle - \langle H\rangle^2\bigr)^{1/2}$ is finite, the state starts to lose its identity at a rate set by it:
>
> $$
> |C(t)|^2 = 1 - \Bigl(\frac{\Delta E\,t}{\hbar}\Bigr)^2 + o(t^2) .
> $$
>
> 4. For a distribution peaked at $E_0$ with width $\Delta E$, $C(t) = e^{-iE_0t/\hbar}\int dE\,w(E)\,e^{-i(E - E_0)t/\hbar}$, and the integrand cancels strongly once $t \gg \hbar/\Delta E$: $|C|$ becomes appreciably smaller than $1$ after a time of order $t \simeq \hbar/\Delta E$. For a Gaussian $w$ of standard deviation $\Delta E$, exactly $|C(t)| = e^{-(\Delta E\,t)^2/2\hbar^2}$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.1.7, eqs. (2.67)–(2.73) (parts 1, 2 for eigenkets, 4) · the user's 511 notes, §"Correlation Amplitude and the Energy-Time Uncertainty Relation"; parts 2 (converse) and 3 and the Gaussian case derived here*

^thm-c3-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* By [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-5|Theorem §C3.1.5]], $\mathscr U|\alpha\rangle = \sum_{a''}c_{a''}e^{-iE_{a''}t/\hbar}|a''\rangle$; take the inner product with $\sum_{a'}c^*_{a'}\langle a'|$ and use orthonormality. Since $w \ge 0$ with total weight $1$, $|C| \le \int w = 1$.
>
> *2.* With $p_{a'} = |c_{a'}|^2 \ge 0$, $\sum p = 1$: $\bigl|\sum p_{a'}e^{-iE_{a'}t/\hbar}\bigr| = 1 = \sum p_{a'}$ forces equality in the triangle inequality, so all populated phases coincide: $e^{-i(E_{a'} - E_{a''})t/\hbar} = 1$ for every $t$, i.e. $E_{a'} = E_{a''}$.
>
> *3.* $C(t) = \langle e^{-iHt/\hbar}\rangle$, so with finite second moment $C$ is twice differentiable, $C'(0) = -i\langle H\rangle/\hbar$, $C''(0) = -\langle H^2\rangle/\hbar^2$. For $f = |C|^2 = C\bar C$: $f(0) = 1$, $f'(0) = C'(0) + \overline{C'(0)} = 0$, and $f''(0) = C''\bar C + 2C'\bar C' + C\bar C''\big|_0 = -2\bigl(\langle H^2\rangle - \langle H\rangle^2\bigr)/\hbar^2$. Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) gives the expansion.
>
> *4.* For $|E - E_0| \gg \hbar/t$ the phase $e^{-i(E - E_0)t/\hbar}$ turns many times across the width $\Delta E$ of $w$, and the contributions cancel (Sakurai's argument). For the Gaussian, $\int dE\,(2\pi\Delta E^2)^{-1/2}e^{-(E - E_0)^2/2\Delta E^2}e^{-i(E - E_0)t/\hbar} = e^{-\Delta E^2t^2/2\hbar^2}$, the Fourier transform of a Gaussian (completing the square).

> [!remark] Remark: Two readings of the energy–time relation
> - **Sakurai's reading** (eq. (2.74)): "the state ket of a physical system ceases to retain its original form after a time interval of order $\hbar/\Delta E$", often written $\Delta t\,\Delta E \simeq \hbar$. Theorem §C3.1.7 makes it precise: part 3 at short times, part 4 for a broad distribution. It is of a very different nature from the uncertainty relation between two observables ([[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty|§C1.3]]): time is a parameter, not an operator, so there is no Robertson inequality with a "$\Delta t$" in it (the series adds Pauli's observation that no self-adjoint time operator can be conjugate to a Hamiltonian that is bounded below). $\Delta E$ is the energy spread of the prepared superposition, not an error in measuring energy.
> - **Mandelstam–Tamm** (level B, [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-9|Theorem §B2.4.9]]): $\Delta E\,\tau_A \ge \hbar/2$, with $\tau_A = \Delta A/|d\langle A\rangle/dt|$ the time for the statistics of an observable $A$ to shift by one standard deviation. PHY 511, HW 5, Problem 5 asks for exactly this inequality, and the series derives it the same way; the proof is level B's (rule: same result, same argument), so its home stays there.
> - **The two together.** Applying Mandelstam–Tamm to the projector $A = |\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|$, whose expectation value is $|C(t)|^2$, turns Sakurai's order-of-magnitude statement into an inequality (★ Remark below).
> - **A check** (Sakurai): for the spin precession of [[§C3.2 Two-State Systems, Spin Precession and Neutrino Oscillations|§C3.2]], the state $|S_x;+\rangle$ in $H = \omega S_z$ has $C(t) = \tfrac12\bigl(e^{-i\omega t/2} + e^{i\omega t/2}\bigr) = \cos(\omega t/2)$ and $\Delta E = \hbar\omega/2$: the state starts losing its identity after $\sim 1/\omega = \hbar/(E_+ - E_-)$, in agreement with eq. (2.60).
> - Level A's version ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]]) rested on the duration–bandwidth property of Fourier transforms; part 1 of Theorem §C3.1.7 shows why: $C(t)$ *is* a Fourier transform of the energy distribution. Lifetimes and line widths return with time-dependent perturbation theory (Chapter C9, Approximation Methods, planned).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.1.7 · the user's series, Part IV, §6.3 (Remark "Energy–time is not Robertson") · PHY 511, HW 5, Problem 5*

^rem-c3-1-4

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: No state decays faster than cos² of ΔE t/ħ
> Let $P = |\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|$ and $p(t) = \langle P\rangle_t = |C(t)|^2$. Since $P^2 = P$, its spread is $\Delta P = \sqrt{p(1 - p)}$, and Mandelstam–Tamm ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-9|Theorem §B2.4.9]]) gives $\Delta E\sqrt{p(1 - p)} \ge \frac\hbar2|\dot p|$. Write $p = \cos^2\theta$ with $\theta(0) = 0$, $0 \le \theta \le \pi/2$: then $\sqrt{p(1 - p)} = \cos\theta\sin\theta$ and $\dot p = -2\cos\theta\sin\theta\,\dot\theta$, so $|\dot\theta| \le \Delta E/\hbar$ and $\theta(t) \le \Delta E\,t/\hbar$:
>
> $$
> |C(t)|^2 \ge \cos^2\Bigl(\frac{\Delta E\,t}{\hbar}\Bigr) \qquad \Bigl(0 \le t \le \frac{\pi\hbar}{2\Delta E}\Bigr).
> $$
>
> In particular no state can evolve into one orthogonal to itself in less than $\pi\hbar/2\Delta E$. The bound is attained: an equal superposition of two levels a distance $\delta$ apart has $\Delta E = \delta/2$ and $|C|^2 = \cos^2(\delta t/2\hbar)$ exactly, as in the spin check above. A Lorentzian energy distribution of full width $\Gamma$ gives the exponential decay $|C|^2 = e^{-\Gamma t/\hbar}$, which falls linearly at $t = 0$, faster than part 3 of Theorem §C3.1.7 allows; there is no contradiction, because its $\Delta E$ is infinite.
>
> *Source: derived here from Theorem §B2.4.9 and Def. §C3.1.2 (not in the course sources)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C3.1.1: A Hamiltonian That Is Not Hermitian
> Let $|a'\rangle$, $|a''\rangle$ be two eigenkets of a Hermitian $A$ ($a' \ne a''$) and take as "Hamiltonian" $H_{\text{bad}} = \delta\,|a'\rangle\langle a''|$, $\delta$ real. What fails?
>
> *The missing property.* $H_{\text{bad}}^\dagger = \delta\,|a''\rangle\langle a'| \ne H_{\text{bad}}$: it is not Hermitian, so by [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-2|Theorem §C3.1.2]] it cannot generate a unitary time evolution.
>
> *The evolution.* Since $\langle a''|a'\rangle = 0$, $H_{\text{bad}}^2 = \delta^2|a'\rangle\langle a''|a'\rangle\langle a''| = 0$, so the exponential series stops after two terms ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]]): $e^{-iH_{\text{bad}}t/\hbar} = 1 - iH_{\text{bad}}t/\hbar$. For $|\psi(0)\rangle = c'|a'\rangle + c''|a''\rangle$ (other eigenkets of $A$, if any, are untouched),
>
> $$
> |\psi(t)\rangle = \Bigl(c' - \frac{i\delta t}{\hbar}\,c''\Bigr)|a'\rangle + c''|a''\rangle .
> $$
>
> *The norm.* With $\lambda = \delta t/\hbar$,
>
> $$
> \langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle = |c' - i\lambda c''|^2 + |c''|^2 = 1 + 2\lambda\,\operatorname{Im}\bigl(c'^*c''\bigr) + \lambda^2|c''|^2 .
> $$
>
> Unless $c'' = 0$ the norm grows without bound, quadratically in $t$: total probability is not conserved, and the probabilities of the two outcomes of $A$ no longer add to one. A Hermitian $H$ with the same off-diagonal element, $\delta(|a'\rangle\langle a''| + |a''\rangle\langle a'|)$, gives the norm-preserving oscillation of [[§C3.2 Two-State Systems, Spin Precession and Neutrino Oscillations|§C3.2]].
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 4, Problem 3 (solved here); the same point is Sakurai Problems 2.2 and 2.11(e)*

> [!example] Example §C3.1.2: Two Spins Coupled for a Finite Time
> Two spin-$\tfrac12$ particles $P$ and $P'$ start in $|+\rangle_P\otimes(a|+\rangle + b|-\rangle)_{P'}$, $|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1$ ([[§C1.5 Composite Systems and Tensor Products|§C1.5]]). During $0 \le t \le T$ they interact through
>
> $$
> H(t) = \tfrac14\,g(t)\,\sigma_z^P\sigma_y^{P'}, \qquad \int_0^Tg(t)\,dt = \pi\hbar ,
> $$
>
> and $g = 0$ outside. Find the state at $T$.
>
> *Case 2 of Theorem §C3.1.4.* $H(t)$ depends on time only through the number $g(t)$, so $[H(t'), H(t'')] = 0$, and
>
> $$
> \mathscr U(T, 0) = \exp\Bigl[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\cdot\frac14\Bigl(\int_0^Tg\,dt\Bigr)\sigma_z^P\sigma_y^{P'}\Bigr] = \exp\Bigl[-\frac{i\pi}{4}\,\sigma_z^P\sigma_y^{P'}\Bigr] = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(1 - i\,\sigma_z^P\sigma_y^{P'}\bigr),
> $$
>
> because $(\sigma_z^P\sigma_y^{P'})^2 = (\sigma_z^P)^2(\sigma_y^{P'})^2 = 1$ (the two factors act on different particles and commute), so $e^{-i\phi X} = \cos\phi - iX\sin\phi$ for $X^2 = 1$.
>
> *Acting on the state.* $\sigma_z^P|+\rangle = |+\rangle$ and $\sigma_y(a, b)^{\mathsf T} = (-ib, ia)^{\mathsf T}$, so
>
> $$
> \mathscr U(T, 0)\,|+\rangle\otimes\begin{pmatrix}a\\ b\end{pmatrix} = |+\rangle\otimes\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}a - b\\ a + b\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> *Reading.* $P$ is unchanged, and $P'$ has been acted on by $e^{-i\pi\sigma_y/4}$, the spin-$\tfrac12$ rotation by $\pi/2$ about the $y$-axis ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations|§C5.2]]): $|+\rangle_{P'}$ becomes $|S_x;+\rangle$. Had $P$ been in $|-\rangle$, $\sigma_z^P \to -1$ and $P'$ would have turned by $-\pi/2$. The coupling is a *controlled rotation*: with $P$ in $(|+\rangle + |-\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and $P'$ in $|+\rangle$, the final state is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(|+\rangle\otimes|S_x;+\rangle + |-\rangle\otimes|S_x;-\rangle\bigr)$, which is entangled, although the initial state was a product.
>
> *Source: PHY 511, HW 4, Problem 5 (solved here)*
