Wave mechanics is where levels A and B started: the Schrödinger wave equation as a principle ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]], [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-4|Principle §B2.3.4]]), the probability current and its modulus–phase form ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]], [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]), the classical limit through the Hamilton–Jacobi equation ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^rem-b8-3-4|CM Remark: The classical limit of the Schrödinger equation]]), the linear potential with a wall ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]) and the WKB approximation with its connection formulas ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization|§B9.3]], [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas|§B9.4]]). At level C the wave equation is no longer assumed: it is the ket equation of [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation|§C3.1]] read in the position basis. Most of Sakurai's §2.4–2.5 then coincides with level B in result and argument, and this section links rather than repeats. What it adds: the derivation of the wave equation, the symmetric linear potential $k|x|$ with both parities (the even states belong to the zeros of $\mathrm{Ai}'$), and a test of WKB against numerics for $V = \alpha x^6$ (PHY 511, HW 7).

## The wave equation as a representation

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.1: Schrödinger's Wave Equation from the Ket Equation
> Let $H = \mathbf p^2/2m + V(\mathbf x)$ with a **local** potential, $\langle\mathbf x''|V(\mathbf x)|\mathbf x'\rangle = V(\mathbf x')\,\delta^3(\mathbf x' - \mathbf x'')$, $V(\mathbf x')$ a real function. Then the wave function $\psi(\mathbf x', t) = \langle\mathbf x'|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle$ of a Schrödinger-picture state satisfies
>
> $$
> i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(\mathbf x', t) = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla'^2\psi(\mathbf x', t) + V(\mathbf x')\,\psi(\mathbf x', t) ,
> $$
>
> and the wave function of an energy eigenket, $u_E(\mathbf x') = \langle\mathbf x'|E\rangle$, with $\langle\mathbf x'|E, t_0 = 0; t\rangle = u_E(\mathbf x')\,e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, satisfies the time-independent equation
>
> $$
> -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla'^2u_E(\mathbf x') + V(\mathbf x')\,u_E(\mathbf x') = E\,u_E(\mathbf x') .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.4.1–2.4.2, eqs. (2.176)–(2.186) · the user's 511 notes, §"Time-Dependent Wave Equation", §"The Time-Independent Wave Equation" · the user's series, Part IV, §6.1, §8*

^thm-c3-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the inner product of the Schrödinger equation for kets ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]]) with the position eigenbra $\langle\mathbf x'|$, which does not move in the Schrödinger picture ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]], 1), so $\partial_t$ passes through it: $i\hbar\,\partial_t\langle\mathbf x'|\alpha; t\rangle = \langle\mathbf x'|H|\alpha; t\rangle$. Kinetic term: $\langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf p^2|\beta\rangle = -\hbar^2\nabla'^2\langle\mathbf x'|\beta\rangle$, from $\langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf p|\beta\rangle = -i\hbar\nabla'\langle\mathbf x'|\beta\rangle$ applied twice ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space|§C2.3]]). Potential term: locality gives $\langle\mathbf x'|V(\mathbf x) = V(\mathbf x')\langle\mathbf x'|$ (insert $\int d^3x''\,|\mathbf x''\rangle\langle\mathbf x''|$ and use the delta function), where $V(\mathbf x')$ is now a number. For an energy eigenket, [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-5|Theorem §C3.1.5]] gives the factor $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$; substituting into the time-dependent equation and cancelling it leaves the time-independent one (in wave mechanics the commuting observable $A$ of §C3.1 can be taken to be $H$ itself, so the label $a'$ is just $E$).

> [!remark] Remark: What level C adds, and what stays at level B
> - **Layer change.** "The starting point of many textbooks" (Sakurai) is here a theorem: wave mechanics is the Schrödinger equation for kets in the $\mathbf x$-basis, valid for Hamiltonians of the form $\mathbf p^2/2m + V(\mathbf x)$ with $V$ local. Other Hamiltonians give other wave equations: a time-dependent $V(\mathbf x, t)$, a nonlocal but separable potential ($v_1(\mathbf x'')v_2(\mathbf x')$ in place of the delta function), or the momentum-dependent coupling $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A + \mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p$ to a vector potential ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect|§C4.3]]).
> - **Boundary conditions and quantization** (Sakurai, quoting the theory of partial differential equations): if $E < \lim_{|\mathbf x'|\to\infty}V(\mathbf x')$ in every direction, the condition $u_E \to 0$ at infinity admits solutions only for a discrete set of $E$; in one dimension $u_E$ oscillates where $E > V$ and is damped where $E < V$. Level B's home: [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^rem-b3-1-5|Remark: Bound and scattering states, and why bound energies are discrete]].
> - **Interpretation** (Sakurai §2.4.3): $\rho = |\langle\mathbf x'|\alpha; t\rangle|^2$ is a probability density because $\langle\mathbf x'|\alpha; t\rangle$ is an expansion coefficient in the position basis. The continuity equation $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\mathbf j = 0$ with $\mathbf j = (\hbar/m)\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\nabla\psi)$ is [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]] (level B writes $J$); the reality of $V$ is essential, and an imaginary part models absorption ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^ex-b1-1-2|Example §B1.1.2]]); $\int d^3x\,\mathbf j = \langle\mathbf p\rangle_t/m$ is [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]. Writing $\psi = \sqrt\rho\,e^{iS/\hbar}$ gives $\mathbf j = \rho\nabla S/m$ ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]): the spatial variation of the phase carries the flux, normal to the surfaces of constant phase, and for a plane wave $\nabla S = \mathbf p$. Sakurai's caution: reading $\nabla S/m$ as a velocity field and $\mathbf j$ as $\rho$ times it, point by point, would require measuring position and velocity simultaneously, which the uncertainty relation forbids.
> - **Classical limit** (Sakurai §2.4.4): substituting $\sqrt\rho\,e^{iS/\hbar}$ into the wave equation and dropping terms with $\hbar|\nabla^2S| \ll |\nabla S|^2$ leaves the Hamilton–Jacobi equation for $S$, Hamilton's principal function, and for stationary states $S = W - Et$ with $W$ the characteristic function and $\mathbf p_{\text{class}} = \nabla W$. This is [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^rem-b8-3-4|CM Remark: The classical limit of the Schrödinger equation]], same derivation; geometrical optics is to wave optics what classical mechanics is to wave mechanics.
> - **Elementary solutions** (Sakurai §2.5.1–2.5.2): box normalization $u_E = L^{-3/2}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}$ with $\mathbf k = 2\pi\mathbf n/L$, the density of states $dN/dE = m^{3/2}E^{1/2}L^3/\sqrt2\,\pi^2\hbar^3$ ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-4|TH Theorem §B10.1.4]], one spin state) and $\mathbf j = \rho\mathbf v$; and the oscillator by the Hermite generating function ([[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]], [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]]). Same results and arguments; homes unchanged. (Sakurai's text calls the degeneracy of $(\pm n_x, \pm n_y, \pm n_z)$ "sixfold"; when all three are nonzero the sign choices are $2^3 = 8$.)
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.4.1–2.4.4, eqs. (2.187)–(2.204); §2.5.1–2.5.2, eqs. (2.205)–(2.233) · the user's 511 notes, §"Interpretations of the Wave Function" · the user's series, Part IV, §8.1*

## The symmetric linear potential

> [!model] Model §C3.5.2: Symmetric Linear Potential
> A particle of mass $m$ on a line in
>
> $$
> V(x) = k|x|, \qquad k > 0 ,
> $$
>
> a force of constant magnitude $k$ toward the origin, with turning points $x = \pm E/k$. Its natural scales, built from $\hbar$, $m$ and $k$, are
>
> $$
> x_0 = \Bigl(\frac{\hbar^2}{mk}\Bigr)^{1/3}, \qquad E_0 = kx_0 = \Bigl(\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{m}\Bigr)^{1/3} .
> $$
>
> Uses: with $x$ the quark–antiquark separation $r$, a model of quarkonium, with $k \approx 1\ \mathrm{GeV/fm} \simeq 1.6\times10^5\ \mathrm N$ (the weight of about 16 tons); with $k = mg$ and an impenetrable floor at $x = 0$, the bouncing ball, whose states are the odd states of this model.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.5.3, eqs. (2.234)–(2.236), (2.240) · the user's 511 notes, §"The Linear Potential"*

^mod-c3-5-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C3.5.3: Bound States of the Symmetric Linear Potential
> In [[§C3.5 Wave Mechanics, the Linear Potential and WKB Revisited#^mod-c3-5-2|Model §C3.5.2]], put $y = x/x_0$, $\varepsilon = E/E_0$ and $z = 2^{1/3}(y - \varepsilon)$. For $x \ge 0$ the time-independent equation becomes Airy's equation $d^2u/dz^2 = z\,u$, and the bound states are
>
> $$
> u(x) = C\,\mathrm{Ai}\bigl(2^{1/3}(|x|/x_0 - \varepsilon)\bigr) \ \text{(even)}, \qquad u(x) = C\,\operatorname{sgn}(x)\,\mathrm{Ai}\bigl(2^{1/3}(|x|/x_0 - \varepsilon)\bigr) \ \text{(odd)},
> $$
>
> with the energies fixed by the zeros of $\mathrm{Ai}'$ and $\mathrm{Ai}$:
>
> $$
> \text{even: } \mathrm{Ai}'(-2^{1/3}\varepsilon) = 0, \quad 2^{1/3}\varepsilon = 1.019,\ 3.248,\ 4.820,\ \dots; \qquad \text{odd: } \mathrm{Ai}(-2^{1/3}\varepsilon) = 0, \quad 2^{1/3}\varepsilon = 2.338,\ 4.088,\ 5.521,\ \dots
> $$
>
> In order of energy, $E/E_0 = 0.809,\ 1.856,\ 2.578,\ 3.245,\ 3.826,\ 4.382,\ \dots$: even and odd states alternate, starting with the even ground state $E_1 = (1.019/2^{1/3})\,(\hbar^2k^2/m)^{1/3}$. Normalization: $\int u^2\,dx = 2C^2\,(x_0/2^{1/3})\bigl[\mathrm{Ai}'(z_1)^2 - z_1\mathrm{Ai}(z_1)^2\bigr]$ with $z_1 = -2^{1/3}\varepsilon$, which is $2C^2(x_0/2^{1/3})\,|z_1|\,\mathrm{Ai}(z_1)^2$ for even and $2C^2(x_0/2^{1/3})\,\mathrm{Ai}'(z_1)^2$ for odd states.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.5.3, eqs. (2.236)–(2.239), Fig. 2.3 · the user's 511 notes, §"The Linear Potential"; the ordering, the energies in units of $E_0$ and the normalization computed here*

^thm-c3-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Parity.* $V$ is even, so each nondegenerate bound state is even or odd ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]]); it suffices to solve on $x \ge 0$ and impose at $x = 0$ either $u(0) = 0$ (odd) or $u'(0) = 0$ (even: $u'$ is odd, and continuous because $V$ is finite, [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]).
>
> *Airy.* For $x > 0$, $u'' = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(kx - E)u$. With $x = x_0y$ and $E = E_0\varepsilon$, $\frac{d^2u}{dy^2} = \frac{2mk x_0^3}{\hbar^2}(y - \varepsilon)u = 2(y - \varepsilon)u$, since $x_0^3 = \hbar^2/mk$; with $z = 2^{1/3}(y - \varepsilon)$, $\frac{d^2u}{dz^2} = 2^{-2/3}\cdot2(y - \varepsilon)u = zu$. Of the two Airy functions only $\mathrm{Ai}$ decays as $z \to \infty$ ([[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^def-b9-4-1|Def. §B9.4.1]]), so $u \propto \mathrm{Ai}(z)$; $x = 0$ is $z = -2^{1/3}\varepsilon$, which turns the two boundary conditions into zeros of $\mathrm{Ai}'$ and of $\mathrm{Ai}$.
>
> *Order.* The zeros of $\mathrm{Ai}$ and $\mathrm{Ai}'$ on the negative axis interlace, $-1.019 > -2.338 > -3.248 > -4.088 > \cdots$, so the levels alternate in parity, as the node theorem requires ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]]); dividing by $2^{1/3} = 1.2599$ gives the list.
>
> *Normalization.* From Airy's equation, $\frac{d}{dz}\bigl[\mathrm{Ai}'^2 - z\mathrm{Ai}^2\bigr] = -\mathrm{Ai}^2$, so $\int_{z_1}^\infty\mathrm{Ai}^2\,dz = \mathrm{Ai}'(z_1)^2 - z_1\mathrm{Ai}(z_1)^2$ (as in [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-5|Theorem §B5.3.5]]); $dx = (x_0/2^{1/3})\,dz$ and the two half-lines contribute equally.

*Uses:* [[§C3.5 Wave Mechanics, the Linear Potential and WKB Revisited#^mod-c3-5-2|Model §C3.5.2]], [[§C3.5 Wave Mechanics, the Linear Potential and WKB Revisited#^thm-c3-5-1|Theorem §C3.5.1]], [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]], [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-6|Theorem §B3.1.6]], [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]], [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^def-b9-4-1|Def. §B9.4.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Two scales, and the even-state condition
> - Level B's linear central potential $V = Ar$ uses $r_0 = (\hbar^2/2mA)^{1/3}$ and $\varepsilon_0 = (\hbar^2A^2/2m)^{1/3}$ ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^mod-b5-3-4|Model §B5.3.4]]), which absorb the factor $2^{1/3}$: there $E_N = a_N\varepsilon_0$ with $a_N = 2.338, 4.088, \dots$; here the same odd-state energies read $E = (a_N/2^{1/3})E_0$. The two agree, since $\varepsilon_0 = E_0/2^{1/3}$.
> - For the even states Sakurai writes "$u_E'(0) = 0$, since $u_E(\epsilon) - u_E(-\epsilon) \equiv 0$, even for $\epsilon \to 0$". The vanishing difference of $u$ only expresses evenness; the argument needs the derivative, $u'(\epsilon) - u'(-\epsilon) \to 0$ by continuity of $u'$, together with $u'(-\epsilon) = -u'(\epsilon)$. The user's 511 notes, §"The Linear Potential", state it with $u'$, correctly.
> - The symbol $k$ is Sakurai's force constant here, not the local wave number $k(x)$ of WKB.

^cau-c3-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Bouncing neutrons
> The odd states are the levels of a ball bouncing on a floor, $V = mgx$ for $x > 0$. For neutrons, $m = 1.675\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$, the length scale is $x_0 = (\hbar^2/m^2g)^{1/3} = 7.39\ \mu\mathrm m$, and the classical turning heights of the lowest states are $(2.338/2^{1/3})x_0 = 14\ \mu\mathrm m$, $(4.088/2^{1/3})x_0 = 24\ \mu\mathrm m$, $(5.521/2^{1/3})x_0 = 32\ \mu\mathrm m$. Nesvizhevsky et al. (*Phys. Rev. D* **67** (2003) 102002) sent ultracold neutrons through a slit of adjustable height above a mirror: no neutrons pass below about $14\ \mu\mathrm m$, and the transmitted rate rises in steps near $24$ and $32\ \mu\mathrm m$ (Sakurai Fig. 2.4), where classically it would rise smoothly. A rare macroscopic display of quantized bound states in a gravitational field; level B's WKB treatment of the same problem is [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^ex-b9-3-2|Example §B9.3.2]].
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.5.3, Fig. 2.4*

## WKB revisited

> [!remark] Remark: Sakurai's WKB is level B's
> Sakurai writes $u_E = e^{iW(x)/\hbar}$, so that the Schrödinger equation becomes $i\hbar W'' - (W')^2 + \hbar^2k(x)^2 = 0$ with $k(x) = \sqrt{2m(E - V)}/\hbar$ (and $k = -i\kappa$ where $E < V$), assumes $\hbar|W''| \ll |W'|^2$, and iterates once: $W_0' = \pm\hbar k$, then $W \approx \pm\hbar\int^xk\,dx' + \frac{i\hbar}{2}\ln k$, so $u_E \approx k^{-1/2}e^{\pm i\int^xk\,dx'}$. This is the $\hbar$-expansion of [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^rem-b9-3-1|Remark: The expansion in powers of ħ, and the classical action]], giving [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-1|Theorem §B9.3.1]]. The rest of his §2.5.4 matches level B item for item, so those stay the homes: the Airy asymptotics and connection formulas, eqs. (2.251)–(2.253) ([[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^thm-b9-4-5|Theorem §B9.4.5]]); the quantization condition $\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\sqrt{2m(E - V)}\,dx = (n + \frac12)\pi\hbar$, eq. (2.254), and its relation to Sommerfeld–Wilson's $\oint p\,dq = nh$ ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-4|Theorem §B9.3.4]], [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^rem-b9-3-3|Remark: The old quantum theory, corrected]]); the bouncing ball by the odd states of $mg|x|$, eqs. (2.258)–(2.262), and Table 2.2 of WKB against exact energies ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^ex-b9-3-2|Example §B9.3.2]]); and the validity condition $\lambda/2\pi \ll 2(E - V)/|dV/dx|$, eq. (2.264), the short-wavelength limit ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^cau-b9-3-1|Caution: Slowly varying compared with what]]). Sakurai's reading, that $W$ is Hamilton's characteristic function and that the WKB condition is the condition (2.201) for the classical limit, is the point of the level-B remark; the deeper reason the classical action sits in the phase is the path integral ([[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral|§C4.2]]). Notation for decaying solutions: [[§B9.4 Tunnelling and the Connection Formulas#^cau-b9-4-1|Caution: κ in the user's WKB notes]].
>
> *Source: Sakurai §2.5.4, eqs. (2.241)–(2.264), Table 2.2 · the user's 511 notes, §"The WKB (Semiclassical) Approximation" · the user's series, Part IV, §8.5 (Remark "Slow potentials: WKB in brief")*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C3.5.1: WKB for Both Parities of the Linear Potential
> Apply the two-turning-point rule ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-4|Theorem §B9.3.4]]) to $V = k|x|$ and compare with Theorem §C3.5.3.
>
> The turning points are $\pm E/k$, and the slopes there are finite, so
>
> $$
> \int_{-E/k}^{E/k}\sqrt{2m(E - k|x|)}\,dx = 2\cdot\frac{2\sqrt{2m}}{3k}E^{3/2} = \Bigl(n + \frac12\Bigr)\pi\hbar \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2^{1/3}\,\frac{E_n}{E_0} = \Bigl[\frac{3\pi}{4}\Bigl(n + \frac12\Bigr)\Bigr]^{2/3}, \quad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots
> $$
>
> ($E_0 = (\hbar^2k^2/m)^{1/3}$; the algebra is that of the bouncing ball.) By the node theorem $n$ even labels the even states and $n$ odd the odd ones. Writing $n = 2j - 2$ and $n = 2j - 1$ ($j = 1, 2, \dots$), the rule predicts the zeros of $\mathrm{Ai}'$ and $\mathrm{Ai}$:
>
> | $j$ | 1 | 2 | 3 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | even: WKB $[\frac{3\pi}{2}(j - \frac34)]^{2/3}$ | 1.115 | 3.262 | 4.826 |
> | even: exact zero of $\mathrm{Ai}'$ | 1.019 | 3.248 | 4.820 |
> | odd: WKB $[\frac{3\pi}{2}(j - \frac14)]^{2/3}$ | 2.320 | 4.082 | 5.517 |
> | odd: exact zero of $\mathrm{Ai}$ | 2.338 | 4.088 | 5.521 |
>
> The odd row is level B's bouncing ball (Sakurai's Table 2.2), as it must be: an odd state of $k|x|$ vanishes at $0$ like a wall. The even ground state is the worst case, $9.5\%$ high: the rule gives it a phase $\int k\,dx = \pi/2$ between the turning points, a quarter of a wavelength, while the connection formulas assume several wavelengths of nearly linear potential around each turning point; from the second even state on the error is below $0.5\%$. Both rows are the leading terms of the standard asymptotic formulas for the zeros of $\mathrm{Ai}$ and $\mathrm{Ai}'$.
>
> *Source: derived here from Sakurai eqs. (2.238)–(2.239), (2.254); exact zeros computed*

^ex-c3-5-1

> [!example] Example §C3.5.2: WKB Against Numerics for a Sextic Well
> For $V = \alpha x^6$ ($\alpha > 0$), find the WKB energies, in particular for $n = 0$ and $n = 4$; rescale the Schrödinger equation to $\psi''(\xi) - (\xi^6 - K)\psi = 0$; solve it numerically and compare.
>
> *WKB.* The turning points are $\pm x_t$, $x_t = (E/\alpha)^{1/6}$. With $x = x_tu$,
>
> $$
> \int_{-x_t}^{x_t}\sqrt{2m(E - \alpha x^6)}\,dx = \sqrt{2mE}\,x_t\,I, \qquad I = \int_{-1}^1\sqrt{1 - u^6}\,du = \frac13B\Bigl(\frac16, \frac32\Bigr) = 1.82149 ,
> $$
>
> ($B$ the beta function, after $v = u^6$). Setting this equal to $(n + \frac12)\pi\hbar$ and using $\sqrt E\,x_t = E^{2/3}\alpha^{-1/6}$:
>
> $$
> E_n^{\text{WKB}} = \Bigl[\frac{(n + \frac12)\pi\hbar}{\sqrt{2m}\,I}\Bigr]^{3/2}\alpha^{1/4} .
> $$
>
> For a general power $V = \alpha|x|^\nu$ the same steps give $E_n \propto (n + \frac12)^{2\nu/(\nu + 2)}$, with $I_\nu = \frac2\nu B(\frac1\nu, \frac32)$: linear in $n$ for the oscillator ($\nu = 2$, $I_2 = \pi/2$, exactly $(n + \frac12)\hbar\omega$), $n^{2/3}$ for the linear potential, and approaching the $n^2$ of a box as $\nu \to \infty$.
>
> *Rescaling.* With $x = L\xi$, $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + \alpha x^6\psi = E\psi$ becomes $\psi_{\xi\xi} = \frac{2mL^2}{\hbar^2}(\alpha L^6\xi^6 - E)\psi$. Choose $2m\alpha L^8/\hbar^2 = 1$, i.e. $L = (\hbar^2/2m\alpha)^{1/8}$: then $\psi'' - (\xi^6 - K)\psi = 0$ with
>
> $$
> K = \frac{2mL^2}{\hbar^2}E = \frac{E}{(\hbar^2/2m)^{3/4}\alpha^{1/4}}, \qquad K_n^{\text{WKB}} = \Bigl[\frac{(n + \frac12)\pi}{I}\Bigr]^{3/2} .
> $$
>
> *Numerics against WKB.* Solving the rescaled equation on $|\xi| \le 4$ by finite differences (8000 and 16000 points, extrapolated) gives
>
> | $n$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $K_n$ numerical | 1.1448 | 4.3386 | 9.0731 | 14.935 | 21.714 |
> | $K_n^{\text{WKB}}$ | 0.8008 | 4.1612 | 8.9536 | 14.832 | 21.622 |
> | WKB error | $-30\%$ | $-4.1\%$ | $-1.3\%$ | $-0.7\%$ | $-0.4\%$ |
>
> The numerically found $\psi_4$ has four nodes, as an $n = 4$ state must ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]]). So WKB misses the ground state badly, by $30\%$: the sextic well is nearly a box with soft walls, and for $n = 0$ the whole classical region holds a quarter of a wavelength ($\int k\,dx = \pi/2$), while the connection formulas assume a linear potential over several wavelengths around each turning point. By $n = 4$ it is within $0.5\%$, well inside the $10\%$ asked for, and its wave function is indistinguishable from the exact one except within a fraction of a wavelength of the turning points (figure). The "wag the dog" method of the HW is the shooting version of the same computation: integrate from one side with a trial $K$ and adjust $K$ until the solution stops diverging to $\pm\infty$ on the other; the instructor's Mathematica notebook is not a typed source and is not used here.
