Two spin-$\frac12$ particles in the singlet fly apart, and two distant observers measure spin components along axes of their choice. The joint probabilities and the correlation $-\hat{\mathbf a}\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}$ were computed in [[§C1.5 Composite Systems and Tensor Products#^ex-c1-5-2|Example §C1.5.2]], whose ★ remark ([[§C1.5 Composite Systems and Tensor Products#^rem-c1-5-2|Remark: Bell's inequality]]) states the inequality and points here; the singlet itself is [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-4|QM Theorem §B6.3.4]], and each particle alone is in the reduced state $\frac12\mathbf 1$ ([[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^ex-c11-3-1|Example §C11.3.1]]). Can these correlations be explained by properties the particles carry from the source, as Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen argued they must? Bell's answer is no: every local hidden-variable theory obeys an inequality that quantum mechanics violates, and experiment sides with quantum mechanics. This section derives the inequality in Bell's and Wigner's forms and in the CHSH form used by experiments (with the quantum maximum $2\sqrt2$), shows the violation, and adds the two theorems that keep the resulting "action at a distance" harmless, no signalling (homed in [[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^thm-c11-3-5|Theorem §C11.3.5]]) and no cloning, with teleportation as an application.

*Notation.* $|S\rangle = \frac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(|{+}{-}\rangle - |{-}{+}\rangle\bigr)$ is the singlet (Sakurai's (3.427); $|\Psi^-\rangle$ of §C1.5). Results of spin measurements along unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}, \hat{\mathbf c}$ are recorded in units of $\hbar/2$, i.e. as $\pm1$. $P(\hat{\mathbf a}+;\hat{\mathbf b}+)$ is the probability that observer A finds $+$ for $\mathbf S_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf a}$ and observer B finds $+$ for $\mathbf S_2\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}$; $E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b})$ is the average of the product of the two results (Griffiths's $P(\mathbf a, \mathbf b)$), and $\theta_{ab}$ the angle between $\hat{\mathbf a}$ and $\hat{\mathbf b}$.

## The singlet correlations, and EPR

> [!remark] Remark: The correlations without collapse
> [[§C1.5 Composite Systems and Tensor Products#^ex-c1-5-2|Example §C1.5.2]] obtains $P(\hat{\mathbf a}\pm;\hat{\mathbf b}\pm) = \frac12\sin^2\frac{\theta_{ab}}2$, $P(\hat{\mathbf a}\pm;\hat{\mathbf b}\mp) = \frac12\cos^2\frac{\theta_{ab}}2$ and $E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}) = -\hat{\mathbf a}\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}$ by letting A's measurement collapse the pair. The same numbers follow with no reference to the order of the measurements. The singlet has total spin zero, so $(\boldsymbol\sigma_1 + \boldsymbol\sigma_2)|S\rangle = 0$ (each component of $\mathbf S$ annihilates a state with $\mathbf S^2 = 0$, since $\langle S|\mathbf S^2|S\rangle = \sum_i\|S_i|S\rangle\|^2$), i.e. $\boldsymbol\sigma_2|S\rangle = -\boldsymbol\sigma_1|S\rangle$. Then, operators of different particles commuting,
>
> $$
> E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}) = \langle S|(\boldsymbol\sigma_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf a})(\boldsymbol\sigma_2\cdot\hat{\mathbf b})|S\rangle = -\langle S|(\boldsymbol\sigma_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf a})(\boldsymbol\sigma_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf b})|S\rangle = -\hat{\mathbf a}\cdot\hat{\mathbf b} - i(\hat{\mathbf a}\times\hat{\mathbf b})\cdot\langle\boldsymbol\sigma_1\rangle = -\hat{\mathbf a}\cdot\hat{\mathbf b} ,
> $$
>
> using $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf a)(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf b) = \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b + i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-4|QM Theorem §B6.1.4]]) and $\langle\boldsymbol\sigma_1\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(\rho_1\boldsymbol\sigma) = 0$ for $\rho_1 = \frac12\mathbf 1$. With the projectors $\frac12(1 \pm \boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf n})$, $P(\hat{\mathbf a}+;\hat{\mathbf b}+) = \frac14\bigl(1 + \langle\boldsymbol\sigma_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf a}\rangle + \langle\boldsymbol\sigma_2\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}\rangle + E\bigr) = \frac14(1 - \cos\theta_{ab}) = \frac12\sin^2\frac{\theta_{ab}}2$, Sakurai's (3.437), and $P(\hat{\mathbf a}+;\hat{\mathbf b}-) = \frac14(1 - E) = \frac12\cos^2\frac{\theta_{ab}}2$. Sakurai's Table 3.1 is the special case of axes $z$ and $x$: same axis, results always opposite; perpendicular axes, no correlation; each list alone, random.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.10.1, Table 3.1, §3.10.3, eqs. (3.436)–(3.437) · Griffiths §12.2, eq. (12.4) · Likharev §10.3, eqs. (10.24)–(10.29); the operator route derived here*

> [!remark] Remark: The EPR argument
> - **EPR.** Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen (1935) argued that quantum mechanics is incomplete. Their thought experiment used two particles with definite $x_1 - x_2$ and $p_1 + p_2$, possible because $[x_1 - x_2, p_1 + p_2] = [x_1, p_1] - [x_2, p_2] = 0$ (Likharev); Bohm's version uses the singlet (Griffiths: a $\pi^0$ decaying into $e^-e^+$; Sakurai: $\eta \to \mu^+\mu^-$ or low-energy proton–proton scattering in the $^1S_0$ state). If A finds $S_z = +$, B's $S_z$ is certainly $-$; if A instead measures $S_x$, B's $S_x$ is certain. Since A can choose after the particles have separated, Einstein's **locality principle** — "the real factual situation of the system $S_2$ is independent of what is done with the system $S_1$, which is spatially separated from the former" — suggests that particle 2 carried definite values of both $S_z$ and $S_x$ all along, values that quantum mechanics does not contain. The extra quantities are **hidden variables**.
> - **Not just an urn.** One black and one white ball in an urn are perfectly anticorrelated too. The quantum pair can be "analyzed either in terms of black and white or in terms of blue and red" (Sakurai), and the correlations for every pair of axes must be reproduced at once; that is where hidden variables fail (Theorem §C11.4.2).
> - **The orthodox reading.** A measurement is a selection; a measurement on what looks like part of the system is a measurement on the whole (Sakurai). Griffiths notes that the collapse cannot propagate at a finite speed: if it did, measuring the positron before "the news" arrived would sometimes give both spins up, violating angular-momentum conservation, which is never seen.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.10.1–3.10.2, eq. (3.428), footnote 15 · Griffiths §12.1 · Likharev §10.3, footnote 28*

## Bell's inequality

> [!definition] Definition §C11.4.1: Local Hidden-Variable Theory
> A **local hidden-variable theory** of the spin-correlation experiment assigns to each pair a complete state $\lambda$ (one number or many), distributed over pairs with a probability density $\rho(\lambda) \ge 0$, $\int\rho(\lambda)\,d\lambda = 1$, that does not depend on the analyzer settings, and two functions
>
> $$
> A(\hat{\mathbf a}, \lambda) = \pm1, \qquad B(\hat{\mathbf b}, \lambda) = \pm1
> $$
>
> giving the results at the two detectors; **locality** means that $A$ does not depend on $\hat{\mathbf b}$ nor $B$ on $\hat{\mathbf a}$. To reproduce the perfect anticorrelation of the singlet along equal axes it must have $B(\hat{\mathbf a}, \lambda) = -A(\hat{\mathbf a}, \lambda)$ for all $\lambda$. Then $E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}) = \int\rho(\lambda)A(\hat{\mathbf a}, \lambda)B(\hat{\mathbf b}, \lambda)\,d\lambda$. Wigner's model (Sakurai) is the special case in which $\lambda$ is one of eight "types", such as $(\hat{\mathbf a}+, \hat{\mathbf b}-, \hat{\mathbf c}+)$ for particle 1 with the opposite type for particle 2, with populations $N_1, \dots, N_8$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §12.2, eqs. (12.5)–(12.7) and footnote 10 · Sakurai §3.10.2, eq. (3.431), Table 3.2 · Likharev §10.3, eqs. (10.27), (10.34)*

^def-c11-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.4.1: Bell's Inequality
> In every local hidden-variable theory, for any three unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}, \hat{\mathbf c}$:
> 1. (Bell's form)
>
> $$
> \bigl|E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}) - E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf c})\bigr| \le 1 + E(\hat{\mathbf b}, \hat{\mathbf c}) ;
> $$
>
> 2. (Wigner's form)
>
> $$
> P(\hat{\mathbf a}+;\hat{\mathbf b}+) \le P(\hat{\mathbf a}+;\hat{\mathbf c}+) + P(\hat{\mathbf c}+;\hat{\mathbf b}+) .
> $$
>
> No assumption is made about the nature or number of the hidden variables or about $\rho(\lambda)$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §12.2, eqs. (12.8)–(12.12) · Sakurai §3.10.2, eqs. (3.432)–(3.435) · Likharev §10.3, eqs. (10.34)–(10.36)*

^thm-c11-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1 (Griffiths).* With $B(\hat{\mathbf b}, \lambda) = -A(\hat{\mathbf b}, \lambda)$, $E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}) = -\int\rho A(\hat{\mathbf a})A(\hat{\mathbf b})\,d\lambda$ (arguments $\lambda$ suppressed). Since $A(\hat{\mathbf b})^2 = 1$,
>
> $$
> E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}) - E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf c}) = -\int\rho\bigl[A(\hat{\mathbf a})A(\hat{\mathbf b}) - A(\hat{\mathbf a})A(\hat{\mathbf c})\bigr]d\lambda = -\int\rho\bigl[1 - A(\hat{\mathbf b})A(\hat{\mathbf c})\bigr]A(\hat{\mathbf a})A(\hat{\mathbf b})\,d\lambda .
> $$
>
> Here $|A(\hat{\mathbf a})A(\hat{\mathbf b})| = 1$ and $\rho[1 - A(\hat{\mathbf b})A(\hat{\mathbf c})] \ge 0$, so $|E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}) - E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf c})| \le \int\rho[1 - A(\hat{\mathbf b})A(\hat{\mathbf c})]\,d\lambda = 1 + E(\hat{\mathbf b}, \hat{\mathbf c})$.
>
> *2 (Sakurai, for Wigner's types).* A finds $\hat{\mathbf a}+$ and B finds $\hat{\mathbf b}+$ exactly when particle 1 is of a type $(\hat{\mathbf a}+, \hat{\mathbf b}-, \cdot)$, types 3 and 4 of Table 3.2, so $P(\hat{\mathbf a}+;\hat{\mathbf b}+) = (N_3 + N_4)/\sum N_i$; likewise $P(\hat{\mathbf a}+;\hat{\mathbf c}+) = (N_2 + N_4)/\sum N_i$ and $P(\hat{\mathbf c}+;\hat{\mathbf b}+) = (N_3 + N_7)/\sum N_i$. Since all $N_i \ge 0$, $N_3 + N_4 \le (N_2 + N_4) + (N_3 + N_7)$. *For a general $\lambda$:* the event $\{A(\hat{\mathbf a}) = +, A(\hat{\mathbf b}) = -\}$, which is (A finds $\hat{\mathbf a}+$, B finds $\hat{\mathbf b}+$), lies inside the union of $\{A(\hat{\mathbf a}) = +, A(\hat{\mathbf c}) = -\}$ and $\{A(\hat{\mathbf c}) = +, A(\hat{\mathbf b}) = -\}$ (if $A(\hat{\mathbf c}) = -$ it is in the first set, if $+$ in the second), and probabilities of nested and united events obey the inequality.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.4.2: Quantum Mechanics Violates Bell's Inequality
> For the singlet, take $\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}, \hat{\mathbf c}$ in a plane with $\hat{\mathbf c}$ bisecting, $\theta_{ab} = 2\theta$, $\theta_{ac} = \theta_{cb} = \theta$. Wigner's form becomes
>
> $$
> \sin^2\theta \le 2\sin^2\frac\theta2 ,
> $$
>
> which is false for every $0 < \theta < \frac\pi2$: the left side exceeds the right by $\cos\theta\,(1 - \cos\theta)$, most at $\theta = \frac\pi3$ ($0.75$ against $0.50$); at $\theta = \frac\pi4$ it reads $0.500 \le 0.293$. Bell's form fails for $\hat{\mathbf a}\perp\hat{\mathbf b}$ and $\hat{\mathbf c}$ at $45^\circ$ to both: $|0 - (-0.707)| \le 1 - 0.707$ would require $0.707 \le 0.293$. Hence no local hidden-variable theory reproduces the predictions of quantum mechanics: the difference is experimental, not metaphysical.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.10.3, eqs. (3.438)–(3.441) · Griffiths §12.2, Fig. 12.3 · Likharev §10.3, eqs. (10.32)–(10.33); the largest violation derived here*

^thm-c11-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§C11.4★ Entanglement, EPR and Bell's Inequality#^rem-c11-4-1|Remark: The correlations without collapse]], each probability in Wigner's form is $\frac12\sin^2$ of half the corresponding angle; cancelling $\frac12$ gives Sakurai's (3.438), $\sin^2\frac{\theta_{ab}}2 \le \sin^2\frac{\theta_{ac}}2 + \sin^2\frac{\theta_{cb}}2$, and with the bisecting geometry (3.439) the stated form. Then $\sin^2\theta - 2\sin^2\frac\theta2 = (1 - \cos^2\theta) - (1 - \cos\theta) = \cos\theta(1 - \cos\theta)$, positive for $0 < \theta < \frac\pi2$; as a function of $x = \cos\theta$, $x - x^2$ is largest at $x = \frac12$. At $\theta = \frac\pi4$: $\sin^2\frac\pi4 = 0.500$, $2\sin^2\frac\pi8 = 0.293$. For Bell's form use $E = -\cos\theta$: $E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}) = 0$, $E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf c}) = E(\hat{\mathbf b}, \hat{\mathbf c}) = -\cos45^\circ$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.4.3: The CHSH Inequality and Its Quantum Maximum
> Let observer A choose between two axes $\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf a}'$ and observer B between $\hat{\mathbf b}, \hat{\mathbf b}'$, and let
>
> $$
> \mathcal S = E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}) - E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}') + E(\hat{\mathbf a}', \hat{\mathbf b}) + E(\hat{\mathbf a}', \hat{\mathbf b}') .
> $$
>
> 1. In every local hidden-variable theory ([[§C11.4★ Entanglement, EPR and Bell's Inequality#^def-c11-4-1|Def. §C11.4.1]]), with or without perfect anticorrelation, $|\mathcal S| \le 2$.
> 2. In quantum mechanics, for any state of two spins and any axes, $|\mathcal S| \le 2\sqrt2$.
> 3. The singlet reaches $2\sqrt2$: with coplanar axes in the order $\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}, \hat{\mathbf a}', \hat{\mathbf b}'$, successive ones $\theta$ apart, $\mathcal S(\theta) = 3E(\theta) - E(3\theta) = -3\cos\theta + \cos3\theta$, and $\mathcal S(\pi/4) = -2\sqrt2 = -2.83$.
>
> Unlike Bell's form, the inequality does not assume perfect anticorrelation along equal axes, which a real apparatus achieves only approximately, and it uses only two settings on each side.
>
> *Source: derived here within the framework of Griffiths §12.2 and Sakurai §3.10; the inequality is due to Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt (1969) and the bound $2\sqrt2$ to Tsirelson (1980); neither appears in the sources*

^thm-c11-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* For each $\lambda$, with $A = A(\hat{\mathbf a}, \lambda)$, $A' = A(\hat{\mathbf a}', \lambda)$, $B = B(\hat{\mathbf b}, \lambda)$, $B' = B(\hat{\mathbf b}', \lambda)$, all $\pm1$:
>
> $$
> A(B - B') + A'(B + B') = \pm2 ,
> $$
>
> since one of $B - B'$, $B + B'$ is $0$ and the other $\pm2$. Averaging with $\rho(\lambda) \ge 0$ gives $|\mathcal S| \le \int\rho\,|\pm2|\,d\lambda = 2$.
>
> *2.* Let $A = \boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf a}$ and so on, so $A^2 = A'^2 = B^2 = B'^2 = 1$ and $\mathcal S = \langle\hat{\mathcal S}\rangle$ with $\hat{\mathcal S} = A\otimes(B - B') + A'\otimes(B + B')$. Squaring and using that operators of different particles commute,
>
> $$
> \hat{\mathcal S}^2 = 4 + [A, A']\otimes[B, B'] .
> $$
>
> Each commutator has norm at most $2$ (each term of $AA' - A'A$ has norm $1$), so every eigenvalue of the Hermitian $\hat{\mathcal S}^2$ is at most $8$ and $|\langle\hat{\mathcal S}\rangle| \le \sqrt{\langle\hat{\mathcal S}^2\rangle} \le 2\sqrt2$, in any state, pure or mixed.
>
> *3.* $E = -\cos\theta_{xy}$ ([[§C11.4★ Entanglement, EPR and Bell's Inequality#^rem-c11-4-1|Remark: The correlations without collapse]]), and the angles are $\theta_{ab} = \theta_{a'b} = \theta_{a'b'} = \theta$, $\theta_{ab'} = 3\theta$. At $\theta = \pi/4$: $-3\cdot0.707 + \cos\frac{3\pi}4 = -2.83$. The local model of Example §C11.4.1 gives $\mathcal S = 3(-1 + \frac{2\theta}\pi) - (-1 + \frac{6\theta}\pi) = -2$ for $3\theta \le \pi$: it touches the bound but never crosses it (figure below).

> [!remark] Remark: Experiments, and what is ruled out
> - **Tests.** Most experiments measured photon-polarization correlations, for pairs from an atomic cascade $(j = 0) \to (j = 1) \to (j = 0)$ in Ca or Hg or from positronium decay, using the correspondence of polarization with spin ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^rem-c11-1-2|Remark: From sphere to ball, and the polarization of light]]); one used the protons of low-energy proton–proton scattering. Aspect and collaborators (1982) changed the analyzer settings while the photons were in flight, too late for any signal slower than light to inform the other side. Later work closed the remaining locality and detection loopholes (Likharev cites Rowe et al. 2001 and Handsteiner et al. 2017). The inequality is violated, in one case by more than nine standard deviations, in agreement with quantum mechanics; Likharev notes that a Bell test is now routine in experiments with entangled qubits.
> - **Verdict.** Local realism is untenable. Bell's theorem does not exclude *nonlocal* hidden variables, such as Bohm's pilot wave, which keep realism at the price of instantaneous influences (Griffiths, footnote 14; Likharev §10.4); EPR, which assumed locality to prove realism, ended by refuting locality, "the outcome Einstein would have liked least" (Bell, quoted by Griffiths).
> - **Interpretations.** Likharev lists the Copenhagen reading (with emphasis on ensembles), nonlocal reality and many worlds, and notes that none has yet suggested an experiment that would exclude the others.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.10.3, eqs. (3.442)–(3.443) · Griffiths §12.2, footnotes 12–14 · Likharev §10.3, footnote 34, §10.4*

## No signalling, no cloning

> [!remark] Remark: Correlation without communication
> Particle 2 "knows" which component of particle 1 was measured, yet this cannot be used to send a message. Whatever A does, B's reduced density operator stays $\frac12\mathbf 1$ ([[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^thm-c11-3-5|Theorem §C11.3.5]]; for one measurement on a pure state, [[§C1.5 Composite Systems and Tensor Products#^thm-c1-5-4|Theorem §C1.5.4]], part 3), so B's own list is a random sequence of $+$ and $-$ whether A measured $S_z$, switched to $S_x$ without warning, or did nothing. Griffiths's way of saying it: before A's measurement the positron is described by a *subsystem* density matrix, after it by an *ignorance* density matrix, and the two are identical (12.36), (12.40). The correlations appear only when the two notebooks are compared, which needs an ordinary channel. Griffiths distinguishes "causal" influences, which change something measurable on the receiving side and cannot outrun light, from "ethereal" ones, visible only as correlations between two lists, which may; the collapse is of the second kind, and in another frame the order of the two measurements is reversed with no paradox.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.10.3 (closing paragraphs) · Griffiths §12.2 (pp. 571–572), §12.3.3*

^rem-c11-4-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.4.4: No-Cloning Theorem
> There is no device that copies an arbitrary unknown state: no linear operator $\mathscr U$ (in particular no unitary time evolution) and fixed "blank" state $|X\rangle$ with
>
> $$
> \mathscr U\,|\psi\rangle|X\rangle = |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle \qquad\text{for all }|\psi\rangle .
> $$
>
> A unitary device can clone the members of a set of states only if any two of them are either orthogonal or equal (up to phase): it may copy spin up and spin down, but then it fails for every nontrivial superposition, such as the eigenstates of $S_x$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §12.4, eqs. (12.41)–(12.45) · Likharev §8.5, eqs. (8.164)–(8.167); the inner-product proof derived here*

^thm-c11-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Linearity (Griffiths, Likharev).* Suppose $\mathscr U|\psi_1\rangle|X\rangle = |\psi_1\rangle|\psi_1\rangle$ and $\mathscr U|\psi_2\rangle|X\rangle = |\psi_2\rangle|\psi_2\rangle$ for two linearly independent kets. For $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|\psi_1\rangle + \beta|\psi_2\rangle$ linearity gives $\alpha|\psi_1\rangle|\psi_1\rangle + \beta|\psi_2\rangle|\psi_2\rangle$, whereas a copy would be $\alpha^2|\psi_1\rangle|\psi_1\rangle + \beta^2|\psi_2\rangle|\psi_2\rangle + \alpha\beta\bigl(|\psi_1\rangle|\psi_2\rangle + |\psi_2\rangle|\psi_1\rangle\bigr)$. The four products are linearly independent, so equality needs $\alpha\beta = 0$, $\alpha^2 = \alpha$, $\beta^2 = \beta$: only the trivial cases.
>
> *Inner products.* If a unitary $\mathscr U$ clones $|\phi\rangle$ and $|\psi\rangle$, then $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\langle X|X\rangle = \bigl(\langle\phi|\langle X|\bigr)\mathscr U^\dagger\mathscr U\bigl(|\psi\rangle|X\rangle\bigr) = \langle\phi|\psi\rangle^2$, so, with $\langle X|X\rangle = 1$, $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \langle\phi|\psi\rangle^2$ and $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle \in \{0, 1\}$. If the copies are allowed an arbitrary phase, $\mathscr U|\psi\rangle|X\rangle = e^{i\theta_\psi}|\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle$, the same steps give $|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle| = |\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2$, so $|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle| \in \{0, 1\}$: orthogonal, or the same ray.

> [!remark] Remark: What cloning would break, and what can be copied
> - **Signalling.** With a cloner, Bob could make a million copies of his positron and measure $S_z$ on half and $S_x$ on the other half; whichever half came out uniform would tell him what Alice measured, faster than light (Griffiths's scheme). No cloning protects [[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^thm-c11-3-5|Theorem §C11.3.5]] and with it relativity.
> - **Cryptography.** An eavesdropper cannot copy the photons of a key and pass the originals on undisturbed, so her measurements leave traces (Griffiths §12.4; the protocol and the $25\%$ error rate she causes are [[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty#^ex-c1-3-3|Example §C1.3.3]], from the PHY 511 activity). Likharev names the BB84 (Bennett and Brassard) and Ekert protocols, the latter based on entangled pairs.
> - **Quasi-cloning.** A CNOT gate maps $(a_0|0\rangle + a_1|1\rangle)|0\rangle \mapsto a_0|00\rangle + a_1|11\rangle$: the amplitudes are duplicated, but into an entangled state, not two copies; each qubit alone is then in the dephased mixture $|a_0|^2|0\rangle\langle0| + |a_1|^2|1\rangle\langle1|$ ([[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^thm-c11-3-2|Theorem §C11.3.2]]). This is the basic step of quantum error correction (Likharev, Fig. 8.7). And a *known* state can be prepared as often as one likes (Likharev, footnote 81).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §12.4, footnotes 20–26 · Likharev §8.5, eqs. (8.168)–(8.169), footnotes 80–81, 98*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C11.4.1: A Local Model That Obeys Bell: Spinning Baseballs
> Two baseballs are launched in opposite directions with opposite angular momenta $\pm\mathbf S$, the direction $\hat{\mathbf s}$ of $\mathbf S$ random and uniform over the sphere. Detectors record only signs: $A(\hat{\mathbf a}) = \operatorname{sign}(\hat{\mathbf s}\cdot\hat{\mathbf a})$ for the first ball and $B(\hat{\mathbf b}) = \operatorname{sign}(-\hat{\mathbf s}\cdot\hat{\mathbf b})$ for the second. Compute $E(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b})$, compare with quantum mechanics, and check Bell's inequality.
>
> *Correlation.* Here $\lambda = \hat{\mathbf s}$, a local deterministic theory: classical mechanics. $AB = -\operatorname{sign}(\hat{\mathbf s}\cdot\hat{\mathbf a})\operatorname{sign}(\hat{\mathbf s}\cdot\hat{\mathbf b})$. The two signs differ exactly when $\hat{\mathbf s}$ lies in one of the two lunes between the great circles perpendicular to $\hat{\mathbf a}$ and to $\hat{\mathbf b}$; each lune has opening angle $\eta = \theta_{ab}$ and area $2\eta$ on the unit sphere, so the signs differ with probability $4\eta/4\pi = \eta/\pi$. Hence
>
> $$
> E_{\text{cl}}(\eta) = -\Bigl[\Bigl(1 - \frac\eta\pi\Bigr) - \frac\eta\pi\Bigr] = -1 + \frac{2\eta}\pi, \qquad 0 \le \eta \le \pi ,
> $$
>
> Griffiths's answer. Quantum mechanics gives $-\cos\eta$; the two agree at $\eta = 0$, $\frac\pi2$ and $\pi$ and nowhere else (figure, panel (a)); the quantum correlation is stronger near $\eta = 0$ and $\pi$.
>
> *Bell's inequality holds.* $|E_{\text{cl}}(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}) - E_{\text{cl}}(\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf c})| = \frac2\pi|\theta_{ab} - \theta_{ac}| \le \frac2\pi\theta_{bc} = 1 + E_{\text{cl}}(\hat{\mathbf b}, \hat{\mathbf c})$, by the triangle inequality for the great-circle distances between the points $\hat{\mathbf a}, \hat{\mathbf b}, \hat{\mathbf c}$ on the sphere (Griffiths's hint). As it must ([[§C11.4★ Entanglement, EPR and Bell's Inequality#^thm-c11-4-1|Theorem §C11.4.1]]): the inequality is a theorem about every model of this kind, and the singlet is the exception.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 12.3 (solved here)*

> [!example] Example §C11.4.2: Quantum Teleportation
> Alice holds a qubit $1$ in an unknown state $|\psi\rangle_1 = a_0|0\rangle + a_1|1\rangle$; qubits $2$ (Alice's) and $3$ (Bob's) share the singlet $\frac1{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle - |10\rangle)_{23}$. Show that two classical bits suffice for Bob to end up with $|\psi\rangle$.
>
> *Rewrite the three-qubit state* in the Bell basis of qubits $1, 2$ ([[§C1.5 Composite Systems and Tensor Products#^ex-c1-5-1|Example §C1.5.1]], with $0 \leftrightarrow +$, $1 \leftrightarrow -$): $|\Phi^\pm\rangle = \frac1{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle \pm |11\rangle)$, $|\Psi^\pm\rangle = \frac1{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle \pm |10\rangle)$. Expanding $|\psi\rangle_1\otimes\frac1{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle - |10\rangle)_{23}$ and collecting,
>
> $$
> |\psi\rangle_1|S\rangle_{23} = \tfrac12\Bigl[|\Phi^+\rangle_{12}\bigl(-a_1|0\rangle + a_0|1\rangle\bigr)_3 + |\Phi^-\rangle_{12}\bigl(a_1|0\rangle + a_0|1\rangle\bigr)_3 + |\Psi^+\rangle_{12}\bigl(-a_0|0\rangle + a_1|1\rangle\bigr)_3 - |\Psi^-\rangle_{12}\bigl(a_0|0\rangle + a_1|1\rangle\bigr)_3\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> (Check one term: the coefficient of $|00\rangle_{12}|1\rangle_3$ on the left is $\frac{a_0}{\sqrt2}$; on the right only $|\Phi^\pm\rangle$ contain $|00\rangle$, giving $\frac12\cdot\frac1{\sqrt2}(a_0 + a_0)$.)
>
> *Protocol.* Alice measures qubits $1, 2$ in the Bell basis (for instance $\sigma_z\otimes\sigma_z$ and $\sigma_x\otimes\sigma_x$, which commute); each outcome has probability $\frac14$, independent of $a_0, a_1$, so Alice learns nothing about $|\psi\rangle$. She sends Bob the two bits naming the outcome, and he applies to qubit $3$: nothing for $\Psi^-$ (the overall sign is irrelevant), $\sigma_z$ for $\Psi^+$, $\sigma_x$ for $\Phi^-$, and $\sigma_z\sigma_x = i\sigma_y$ for $\Phi^+$. In every case qubit $3$ ends in $a_0|0\rangle + a_1|1\rangle$.
>
> *Consistency.* Qubit $1$ has been left in a Bell state with qubit $2$, so the state was moved, not copied ([[§C11.4★ Entanglement, EPR and Bell's Inequality#^thm-c11-4-4|Theorem §C11.4.4]]). Before the two bits arrive, Bob's qubit is described by the average over Alice's four outcomes, $\frac14\sum$ of the four states above, which is $\frac12\mathbf 1$: nothing travels faster than the classical message ([[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices#^thm-c11-3-5|Theorem §C11.3.5]]).
>
> *Source: Likharev §8.5, eqs. (8.179)–(8.181), Fig. 8.8 (expansion and corrections worked out here)*
