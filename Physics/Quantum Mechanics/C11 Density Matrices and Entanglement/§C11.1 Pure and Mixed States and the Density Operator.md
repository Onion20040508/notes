Silver atoms straight from the oven have no preferred spin direction, yet every ket of a spin one-half points somewhere ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-6|QM Theorem §B6.1.6]]): no ket describes the beam. Level B's postulates assumed a state known completely, and the Domain line of [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-1|QM Principle §B2.3.1]] deferred statistical mixtures to this level; the same holds for the ket formalism of [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations|§C1.2]] and [[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty|§C1.3]]. This section adds a second, classical layer of probability, an ensemble of kets with weights, and packages it into one basis-free object, von Neumann's density operator $\rho$. New at this level: $\rho$ itself, the ensemble average as a trace, the purity test, the Bloch ball that completes level B's Bloch sphere, and the coherences that the position representation of $\rho$ carries. Its mathematical home is the theory of positive operators and the trace ([[§24 Positive Operators#^ladr-7-34|LADR Def. 7.34]], [[§31 Trace꞉ A Connection Between Matrices and Operators#^ladr-8-50|LADR Thm. 8.50]]); how $\rho$ moves and what it is in thermal equilibrium is [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics|§C11.2]], and $\rho$ as the state of a part of a larger system is [[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices|§C11.3]].

*Notation.* Kets $|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle$ are normalized; $w_i$ are fractional populations; $N$ is the dimension of the ket space; $\operatorname{tr}$ is the trace; $\boldsymbol\sigma$ are the Pauli matrices, $\mathbf S = \frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\sigma$. Ensemble averages are written $\langle A\rangle$ (Sakurai writes $[A]$; see the caution below).

## Ensembles

> [!definition] Definition §C11.1.1: Pure and Mixed Ensembles
> A **pure ensemble** is a collection of identically prepared physical systems all characterized by the same ket $|\alpha\rangle$ ([[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]]); the beam leaving a Stern–Gerlach filter is one ([[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space#^mod-c1-1-2|Model §C1.1.2]]). In a **mixed ensemble** a fraction $w_1$ of the members is characterized by $|\alpha^{(1)}\rangle$, a fraction $w_2$ by $|\alpha^{(2)}\rangle$, and so on, with
>
> $$
> w_i \ge 0, \qquad \sum_i w_i = 1 .
> $$
>
> The kets $|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle$ need not be orthogonal, and their number may exceed $N$ (for spin $\tfrac12$: $40\%$ along $+z$, $30\%$ along $+x$, $30\%$ along $-y$). A **completely random** ensemble has no preferred state, like the unpolarized oven beam, a $50{:}50$ mixture of $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$; a mixture between the two extremes, such as $70\%$ along $+x$ and $30\%$ along $-z$, is **partially polarized**.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.1–3.4.2, eqs. (3.95), (3.97), footnote 5 · Griffiths §12.3.2, eq. (12.30)*

^def-c11-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: A mixture is not a superposition
> - The $50{:}50$ mixture of $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ and the superposition $\frac1{\sqrt2}(|+\rangle + |-\rangle) = |S_x;+\rangle$ both give $S_z = \pm\frac\hbar2$ with probability $\frac12$, but they are different physics. Rotate a Stern–Gerlach analyzer: the mixture always splits into two beams of equal intensity, while the superposition, a pure state with spin along $+x$, gives $\cos^2(\beta/2)$ and $\sin^2(\beta/2)$, with ratio $1{:}0$ when the analyzer points along $x$. The relative phase of $c_\pm$ carries the information about the $xy$-plane; the weights $w_\pm$ carry no phase at all.
> - The $w_i$ are classical probabilities, like the fraction of men in a graduating class (Sakurai's comparison); do not confuse them with $|c_i|^2$. A superposition of two kets is still a pure state, one vector of the ket space; a mixed ensemble is not represented by any single vector (Griffiths, footnote 19).
> - *Words.* Sakurai says pure and mixed *ensembles* and keeps *state* for a ket; Griffiths and Likharev say pure and mixed *states*, and Likharev counts pure states as special mixed states. Sakurai writes the ensemble average as $[A]$ and keeps $\langle A\rangle$ for pure ensembles (his footnote 6); these notes write $\langle A\rangle$ for both, since the pure case is a special case ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-1|Theorem §C11.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.1, eq. (3.96) · Griffiths §12.3.2, footnotes 18–19 · Likharev §7.1, footnote 5*

> [!definition] Definition §C11.1.2: Ensemble Average; Density Operator; Density Matrix
> The **ensemble average** of an observable $A$ over the mixed ensemble of Definition §C11.1.1 is the expectation value in each member state, weighted by the populations:
>
> $$
> \langle A\rangle = \sum_i w_i\,\langle\alpha^{(i)}|A|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle = \sum_i\sum_{a'} w_i\,\bigl|\langle a'|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle\bigr|^2\,a' ,
> $$
>
> where $|a'\rangle$ are the eigenkets of $A$. Probability enters twice: quantum mechanically in $|\langle a'|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle|^2$, classically in $w_i$. The **density operator** and its **density matrix** in a basis $\{|b'\rangle\}$ are
>
> $$
> \rho = \sum_i w_i\,|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle\langle\alpha^{(i)}| , \qquad \langle b''|\rho|b'\rangle = \sum_i w_i\,\langle b''|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle\langle\alpha^{(i)}|b'\rangle .
> $$
>
> For a pure ensemble $\rho = |\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|$, the projector of [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-5|QM Def. §B2.1.5]]. The matrix of $|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|$ is the outer product of the column $\langle b'|\alpha\rangle$ with the row $\langle\alpha|b''\rangle = \langle b''|\alpha\rangle^*$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.2, eqs. (3.98), (3.100)–(3.101), (3.104), (3.109) · Griffiths §12.3, eqs. (12.14), (12.27)–(12.29) · Likharev §7.1, eqs. (7.5)–(7.9)*

^def-c11-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.1.1: Everything Measurable Is Fixed by ρ
> 1. **Averages.** $\langle A\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(\rho A)$, and the trace may be evaluated in any basis.
> 2. **Probabilities.** A measurement of $A$ gives $a'$ with probability $P(a') = \operatorname{tr}(\rho\Lambda_{a'})$, with $\Lambda_{a'}$ the projector onto the eigenspace of $a'$; for a nondegenerate $a'$, $P(a') = \langle a'|\rho|a'\rangle$, a diagonal element of the density matrix.
> 3. **After the measurement.** The members that gave $a'$ form the ensemble $\rho' = \Lambda_{a'}\rho\Lambda_{a'}/P(a')$. If the results are not sorted, the whole ensemble becomes $\sum_{a'}\Lambda_{a'}\rho\Lambda_{a'}$: in the eigenbasis of $A$ the diagonal elements are kept and the elements between different eigenvalues are erased.
> 4. Consequently two ensembles with the same $\rho$ give the same statistics for every measurement, before and after it; no experiment can tell them apart.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.2, eqs. (3.99), (3.102) · Griffiths eq. (12.33); parts 2–4 derived here from the measurement postulate of [[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty|§C1.3]] applied member by member*

^thm-c11-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Insert $\sum_{b'}|b'\rangle\langle b'| = 1$ twice:
>
> $$
> \langle A\rangle = \sum_i w_i\sum_{b', b''}\langle\alpha^{(i)}|b'\rangle\langle b'|A|b''\rangle\langle b''|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle = \sum_{b', b''}\Bigl(\sum_i w_i\langle b''|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle\langle\alpha^{(i)}|b'\rangle\Bigr)\langle b'|A|b''\rangle = \sum_{b', b''}\langle b''|\rho|b'\rangle\langle b'|A|b''\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(\rho A) .
> $$
>
> The property of the ensemble that does not depend on $A$ has factored out. The trace of an operator does not depend on the basis ([[§31 Trace꞉ A Connection Between Matrices and Operators#^ladr-8-50|LADR Thm. 8.50]]).
>
> *2.* A member in $|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle$ gives $a'$ with probability $\langle\alpha^{(i)}|\Lambda_{a'}|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle$ and is then left in $\Lambda_{a'}|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle$, normalized ([[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty#^def-c1-3-1|Def. §C1.3.1]]; Sakurai's $\Lambda_{a'}$ is the projector $P_{a'}$ of §C1.3); weighting by $w_i$ gives $\sum_iw_i\langle\alpha^{(i)}|\Lambda_{a'}|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(\rho\Lambda_{a'})$ by part 1 with $A = \Lambda_{a'}$.
>
> *3.* Let $p_i = \langle\alpha^{(i)}|\Lambda_{a'}|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle$. A member in $|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle$ that gives $a'$ is left in $\Lambda_{a'}|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle/\sqrt{p_i}$. Among the members that gave $a'$, the fraction that started in $|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle$ is $w_ip_i/P(a')$ (Bayes). Hence
>
> $$
> \rho' = \sum_i\frac{w_ip_i}{P(a')}\,\frac{\Lambda_{a'}|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle\langle\alpha^{(i)}|\Lambda_{a'}}{p_i} = \frac{\Lambda_{a'}\rho\Lambda_{a'}}{P(a')} .
> $$
>
> Unsorted, the outcome $a'$ occurs with probability $P(a')$, so the ensemble is $\sum_{a'}P(a')\rho' = \sum_{a'}\Lambda_{a'}\rho\Lambda_{a'}$. Between eigenkets $|a', k\rangle$, $|a'', l\rangle$ this has the element $\langle a', k|\rho|a'', l\rangle$ if $a' = a''$ and $0$ otherwise.
>
> *4.* Parts 1–3 use the members only through $\rho$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.1.2: Which Operators Are Density Operators
> 1. Every density operator is Hermitian, has $\operatorname{tr}\rho = 1$, and is positive: $\langle\beta|\rho|\beta\rangle \ge 0$ for every ket $|\beta\rangle$.
> 2. Hence it has an orthonormal eigenbasis $\{|k\rangle\}$ with eigenvalues $0 \le \lambda_k \le 1$, $\sum_k\lambda_k = 1$, and
>
> $$
> \rho = \sum_k\lambda_k\,|k\rangle\langle k| :
> $$
>
> every ensemble is equivalent (Theorem §C11.1.1, part 4) to a mixture of *orthonormal* kets, the eigenkets of $\rho$, with populations $\lambda_k$ (Likharev's special basis $\{w\}$).
> 3. Conversely, every Hermitian, positive operator of unit trace is the density operator of an ensemble, namely the one of part 2.
> 4. In dimension $N$ a density matrix has $N^2 - 1$ independent real parameters: $N^2$ for a Hermitian matrix, less one for the trace.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.2, eq. (3.103) and p. 125 (parameter count; Problem 3.16 for spin 1) · Likharev §7.1, eqs. (7.10)–(7.15) · Griffiths eqs. (12.31)–(12.32); positivity and the converse derived here*

^thm-c11-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Each $|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle\langle\alpha^{(i)}|$ is Hermitian and the $w_i$ are real. $\operatorname{tr}\rho = \sum_iw_i\langle\alpha^{(i)}|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle = \sum_iw_i = 1$. $\langle\beta|\rho|\beta\rangle = \sum_iw_i|\langle\alpha^{(i)}|\beta\rangle|^2 \ge 0$.
>
> *2.* A Hermitian operator has an orthonormal eigenbasis with real eigenvalues ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]). Positivity with $|\beta\rangle = |k\rangle$ gives $\lambda_k = \langle k|\rho|k\rangle \ge 0$; the trace is the sum of the eigenvalues, so $\sum_k\lambda_k = 1$ and each $\lambda_k \le 1$.
>
> *3.* Given such an operator, its spectral decomposition is of the form of [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^def-c11-1-2|Def. §C11.1.2]] with $w_k = \lambda_k \ge 0$, $\sum w_k = 1$.
>
> *4.* A Hermitian $N\times N$ matrix has $N$ real diagonal entries and $\frac12N(N-1)$ complex entries above the diagonal, $N^2$ real numbers.

^der-c11-1-2

*Uses:* [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^def-c11-1-2|Def. §C11.1.2]], [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]], [[§24 Positive Operators#^ladr-7-34|LADR Def. 7.34]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.1.3: Purity
> For every density operator on an $N$-dimensional space
>
> $$
> \frac1N \le \operatorname{tr}\rho^2 \le 1 .
> $$
>
> 1. $\operatorname{tr}\rho^2 = 1$ $\iff$ $\rho^2 = \rho$ $\iff$ the eigenvalues are $1, 0, \dots, 0$ $\iff$ $\rho = |\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|$, a pure ensemble. In its eigenbasis a pure $\rho$ is $\operatorname{diag}(0, \dots, 0, 1, 0, \dots, 0)$.
> 2. $\operatorname{tr}\rho^2 = 1/N$ $\iff$ $\rho = \frac1N\mathbf 1$, the completely random ensemble; its matrix is $\frac1N\mathbf 1$ in *every* basis.
>
> $\operatorname{tr}\rho^2$, the **purity**, therefore tests whether an ensemble is pure; a mixed ensemble has $\rho^2 \ne \rho$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.2, eqs. (3.105)–(3.108), §3.4.5, eq. (3.126), Problem 3.11 · Griffiths eq. (12.35), Problem 12.6*

^thm-c11-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Upper bound, Sakurai's route.* $\operatorname{tr}\rho^2 = \sum_{i,j}w_iw_j\operatorname{tr}\bigl(|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle\langle\alpha^{(i)}|\alpha^{(j)}\rangle\langle\alpha^{(j)}|\bigr) = \sum_{i,j}w_iw_j|\langle\alpha^{(i)}|\alpha^{(j)}\rangle|^2 \le \sum_{i,j}w_iw_j = 1$ by the Schwarz inequality ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-1|QM Theorem §B2.1.1]]). Equality requires $|\langle\alpha^{(i)}|\alpha^{(j)}\rangle| = 1$ whenever $w_iw_j > 0$, i.e. all kets with nonzero weight equal up to a phase: a pure ensemble.
>
> *Via the eigenvalues* ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-2|Theorem §C11.1.2]]): $\operatorname{tr}\rho^2 = \sum_k\lambda_k^2$. Since $0 \le \lambda_k \le 1$, $\sum\lambda_k^2 \le \sum\lambda_k = 1$, with equality iff every $\lambda_k$ is $0$ or $1$, i.e. one $\lambda_k = 1$; then $\rho = |k\rangle\langle k|$. $\rho^2 = \rho$ means $\lambda_k^2 = \lambda_k$ for all $k$, the same condition. Lower bound: by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in $\mathbb R^N$, $1 = (\sum_k\lambda_k\cdot1)^2 \le N\sum_k\lambda_k^2$, with equality iff all $\lambda_k = 1/N$, i.e. $\rho = \frac1N\mathbf 1$. The identity has the same matrix in every orthonormal basis.

*Uses:* [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-2|Theorem §C11.1.2]], [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-1|QM Theorem §B2.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Many mixtures, one ρ
> The unpolarized beam is equally a $50{:}50$ mixture of $|S_z;\pm\rangle$ and of $|S_x;\pm\rangle$:
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix}\tfrac12 & 0\\ 0 & \tfrac12\end{pmatrix} = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac12 & \tfrac12\\ \tfrac12 & \tfrac12\end{pmatrix} + \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac12 & -\tfrac12\\ -\tfrac12 & \tfrac12\end{pmatrix} ,
> $$
>
> and Sakurai calls it gratifying that the formalism gives this automatically: a beam with no preferred direction should not depend on the axis used to describe it. In general a mixed ensemble can be decomposed into pure ensembles in many ways (Example §C11.1.1; for a beam of particles, a mixture of plane waves or of wave packets), and by Theorem §C11.1.1, part 4, asking which decomposition is "really" present has no experimental meaning. The eigen-decomposition of Theorem §C11.1.2 is the one canonical choice. Section [[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices|§C11.3]] adds a second, physically different origin for the same $\rho$: a subsystem of an entangled pure state.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.2, eqs. (3.112)–(3.113), and §3.4.4*

## Spin one-half: the Bloch ball

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.1.4: Spin-One-Half Density Operators Fill the Bloch Ball
> 1. Every density operator of a spin $\tfrac12$ (or any two-state system) is
>
> $$
> \rho = \tfrac12\bigl(\mathbf 1 + \mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\bigr), \qquad \mathbf b = \langle\boldsymbol\sigma\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(\rho\boldsymbol\sigma) \in \mathbb R^3, \qquad |\mathbf b| \le 1 ,
> $$
>
> so the three ensemble averages $\langle S_k\rangle = \frac\hbar2b_k$ determine the ensemble; $\mathbf b$ is the **Bloch vector**.
> 2. The eigenvalues are $\frac12(1 \pm |\mathbf b|)$ with eigenkets $|\hat{\mathbf b};\pm\rangle$, and $\operatorname{tr}\rho^2 = \frac12(1 + |\mathbf b|^2)$. Every spin-$\frac12$ ensemble is equivalent to a beam partially polarized along $\hat{\mathbf b}$: a fraction $\frac12(1 + |\mathbf b|)$ with spin up and $\frac12(1 - |\mathbf b|)$ with spin down along $\hat{\mathbf b}$; $|\mathbf b|$ is its degree of polarization.
> 3. Pure ensembles are the surface $|\mathbf b| = 1$, where $\mathbf b$ is the direction $\hat{\mathbf n}$ of the ket $|\hat{\mathbf n};+\rangle$; mixed ensembles fill the interior; the completely random ensemble is the centre.
> 4. Mixing is convex: if $\rho = \sum_iw_i\rho_i$, then $\mathbf b = \sum_iw_i\mathbf b_i$.
> 5. A Stern–Gerlach analyzer along $\hat{\mathbf n}$ passes the fraction $P(\hat{\mathbf n};+) = \frac12(1 + \mathbf b\cdot\hat{\mathbf n})$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.2, p. 125, and Problem 3.13(b) · Griffiths Problem 12.8 (Bloch vector, Bloch sphere) · the user's series, Part IV, §3 (Remark "The sphere is the boundary of a ball")*

^thm-c11-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* The matrices $\mathbf 1, \sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z$ span the real vector space of Hermitian $2\times2$ matrices (four real parameters), so $\rho = \frac12(c_0\mathbf 1 + \mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$ with real $c_0, \mathbf b$. Since $\operatorname{tr}\sigma_k = 0$ and $\operatorname{tr}\sigma_j\sigma_k = 2\delta_{jk}$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-4|QM Theorem §B6.1.4]]), $\operatorname{tr}\rho = c_0 = 1$ and $\operatorname{tr}(\rho\sigma_k) = b_k$.
>
> *2.* $(\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2 = |\mathbf b|^2\mathbf 1$, so $\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ has eigenvalues $\pm|\mathbf b|$, with the eigenkets $|\hat{\mathbf b};\pm\rangle$ of spin along $\hat{\mathbf b}$ ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-5|QM Theorem §B6.1.5]]). Positivity ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-2|Theorem §C11.1.2]]) of the smaller eigenvalue $\frac12(1 - |\mathbf b|)$ is $|\mathbf b| \le 1$. $\operatorname{tr}\rho^2 = \frac14\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbf 1 + 2\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma + |\mathbf b|^2\mathbf 1\bigr) = \frac12(1 + |\mathbf b|^2)$. The spectral decomposition is the stated mixture.
>
> *3.* By [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-3|Theorem §C11.1.3]], pure means $\operatorname{tr}\rho^2 = 1$, i.e. $|\mathbf b| = 1$; then $\rho = |\hat{\mathbf b};+\rangle\langle\hat{\mathbf b};+|$. $\mathbf b = 0$ gives $\frac12\mathbf 1$.
>
> *4.* $\mathbf b$ is linear in $\rho$. *5.* The projector onto $|\hat{\mathbf n};+\rangle$ is $\frac12(\mathbf 1 + \hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$, and $\operatorname{tr}\bigl[\rho\,\frac12(\mathbf 1 + \hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\bigr] = \frac12(1 + \mathbf b\cdot\hat{\mathbf n})$ ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-1|Theorem §C11.1.1]]).

> [!remark] Remark: From sphere to ball, and the polarization of light
> - **Layer change.** Level B found that pure spin states correspond one to one to the points of a sphere ([[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^thm-b6-1-6|QM Theorem §B6.1.6]], [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^rem-b6-1-3|QM Remark: Spin one-half as a sphere of directions]]). The density operator fills in the ball: the sphere is its boundary, the user's series makes the same point (Part IV, §3). The precession of $\langle\boldsymbol\sigma\rangle$ in [[§C3.2 Two-State Systems, Spin Precession and Neutrino Oscillations#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]] is the motion of $\mathbf b$ on the surface; [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics|§C11.2]] lets it move inside.
> - **Light.** Sakurai's correspondence between spin $\frac12$ and photon polarization ([[§C1.1 Stern–Gerlach and the Ket Space#^rem-c1-1-2|Remark: The polarization analogy]]: $S_z\pm$ with $x$- and $y$-polarization, $S_x\pm$ with the $45^\circ$ and $135^\circ$ diagonals, $S_y\pm$ with circular polarization) extends to mixtures. Partially polarized light, an incoherent mixture of an unpolarized part $I_u$ and a polarized part $I_p$ ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^def-b8-1-3|WO Def. §B8.1.3]]), has the $2\times2$ polarization density matrix $\rho = \frac{I_u}{I_u + I_p}\cdot\frac12\mathbf 1 + \frac{I_p}{I_u + I_p}|e\rangle\langle e|$, so by part 4 its Bloch vector has length $|\mathbf b| = I_p/(I_u + I_p)$: the degree of polarization $P$ of optics is $|\mathbf b|$. (The components of $(I_u + I_p)\,\mathbf b$ are what optics calls the Stokes parameters, a name the sources do not use.) A linear analyzer at angle $\varphi$ projects onto linear states, which lie on the great circle through $S_z\pm$ and $S_x\pm$, perpendicular to the circular axis; by part 5 it sees only the component of $\mathbf b$ in that plane. That is why a rotating polarizer cannot tell circular from unpolarized light ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^cau-b8-1-3|WO Caution: Partial elliptical polarization]]).

## Continuous bases

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.1.5: The Density Matrix in the Position Representation
> With position eigenkets $|\mathbf x'\rangle$ and wave functions $\psi_i(\mathbf x') = \langle\mathbf x'|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle$:
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf x''|\rho|\mathbf x'\rangle = \sum_i w_i\,\psi_i(\mathbf x'')\,\psi_i^*(\mathbf x'), \qquad \langle A\rangle = \int d^3x'\!\int d^3x''\,\langle\mathbf x''|\rho|\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x'|A|\mathbf x''\rangle .
> $$
>
> 1. The diagonal $\langle\mathbf x'|\rho|\mathbf x'\rangle = \sum_iw_i|\psi_i(\mathbf x')|^2$ is the probability density of position (whence the name *density* matrix), and $\int d^3x'\,\langle\mathbf x'|\rho|\mathbf x'\rangle = 1$.
> 2. $\langle\mathbf x'|\rho|\mathbf x''\rangle = \langle\mathbf x''|\rho|\mathbf x'\rangle^*$.
> 3. The off-diagonal elements, the **coherences**, are not determined by the position probabilities; they fix the momentum distribution:
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf p'|\rho|\mathbf p'\rangle = \frac1{(2\pi\hbar)^3}\int d^3x''\!\int d^3x'\,e^{-i\mathbf p'\cdot(\mathbf x'' - \mathbf x')/\hbar}\,\langle\mathbf x''|\rho|\mathbf x'\rangle .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.4, eqs. (3.124)–(3.125) · Likharev §7.2, eqs. (7.27)–(7.32), (7.54)–(7.57)*

^thm-c11-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert $\int d^3x'\,|\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x'| = 1$ twice in $\operatorname{tr}(\rho A)$ ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-1|Theorem §C11.1.1]]); the matrix element of $\rho$ is Definition §C11.1.2 with $|b'\rangle \to |\mathbf x'\rangle$. Part 1 is Theorem §C11.1.1, part 2, for the position projector, and $\sum w_i = 1$; part 2 is Hermiticity. Part 3: $\langle\mathbf p'|\rho|\mathbf p'\rangle = \int\!\int\langle\mathbf p'|\mathbf x''\rangle\langle\mathbf x''|\rho|\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x'|\mathbf p'\rangle$ with $\langle\mathbf x|\mathbf p\rangle = (2\pi\hbar)^{-3/2}e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x/\hbar}$ ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space|§C2.3]]).

^der-c11-1-5

*Uses:* [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^def-c11-1-2|Def. §C11.1.2]], [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-1|Theorem §C11.1.1]], [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space|§C2.3]]

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C11.1.1: Mixing Spin Up Along z with Spin Down Along y
> A large collection of spin-$\frac12$ particles is a mixture of $|S_z;+\rangle$ (fraction $a$) and $|S_y;-\rangle$ (fraction $1 - a$). Find $\rho$, the ensemble averages of $\mathbf S$, the purity, and for $a = \frac12$ a different decomposition.
>
> *Density matrix.* With $|S_y;-\rangle = \frac1{\sqrt2}(|+\rangle - i|-\rangle)$, the outer product has the elements $\frac1{\sqrt2}\cdot\bigl(\frac{-i}{\sqrt2}\bigr)^* = \frac i2$ above the diagonal, so
>
> $$
> \rho = a\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix} + \frac{1 - a}2\begin{pmatrix}1 & i\\ -i & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\frac{1 + a}2 & \frac{i(1 - a)}2\\[2pt] -\frac{i(1 - a)}2 & \frac{1 - a}2\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> *Averages.* The Bloch vectors of the two members are $(0, 0, 1)$ and $(0, -1, 0)$, so by convexity ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-4|Theorem §C11.1.4]]) $\mathbf b = (0, -(1 - a), a)$:
>
> $$
> \langle S_x\rangle = 0, \qquad \langle S_y\rangle = -(1 - a)\frac\hbar2, \qquad \langle S_z\rangle = a\,\frac\hbar2 .
> $$
>
> The same follows from the matrix: with $(\sigma_y)_{+-} = -i$, $(\sigma_y)_{-+} = i$, $\operatorname{tr}(\rho\sigma_y) = \rho_{+-}(\sigma_y)_{-+} + \rho_{-+}(\sigma_y)_{+-} = \tfrac{i(1 - a)}2\,i + \bigl(-\tfrac{i(1 - a)}2\bigr)(-i) = -(1 - a)$. *Checks:* $a = 1$ gives spin up along $z$, $a = 0$ spin down along $y$.
>
> *Purity.* $\operatorname{tr}\rho^2 = \frac12\bigl(1 + a^2 + (1 - a)^2\bigr) = 1 - a(1 - a)$: pure only at $a = 0, 1$, least pure at $a = \frac12$ with $\operatorname{tr}\rho^2 = \frac34$, $|\mathbf b| = 1/\sqrt2$. The mixture never reaches the centre, because the two Bloch vectors are not opposite.
>
> *Another decomposition.* At $a = \frac12$, $\hat{\mathbf n} = \mathbf b/|\mathbf b| = \frac1{\sqrt2}(0, -1, 1)$, the direction halfway between $+z$ and $-y$. By Theorem §C11.1.4 the same ensemble is a mixture of $|\hat{\mathbf n};+\rangle$ with weight $\frac12(1 + \frac1{\sqrt2}) = 0.854$ and $|\hat{\mathbf n};-\rangle$ with weight $0.146$: two orthogonal kets instead of two non-orthogonal ones, and no experiment distinguishes the two descriptions ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-1|Theorem §C11.1.1]], part 4). The figure above shows both.
>
> *Compare* Sakurai's Example 4, $75\%$ $S_z+$ and $25\%$ $S_x+$: $\mathbf b = \frac34(0, 0, 1) + \frac14(1, 0, 0)$, so $\langle S_x\rangle = \frac\hbar8$, $\langle S_y\rangle = 0$, $\langle S_z\rangle = \frac{3\hbar}8$, his eq. (3.118).
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 3.12 (solved here); Sakurai §3.4.2, Example 4, eqs. (3.116)–(3.118)*

> [!example] Example §C11.1.2: A Mixed Ensemble of Oscillator States
> A mixed ensemble of oscillators is formed with equal parts of $|\alpha\rangle = \frac1{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)$, $|\beta\rangle = \frac1{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle + |2\rangle)$ and $|2\rangle$. Find $\rho$, the ensemble average of the energy, the purity and $\langle x\rangle$.
>
> *Density matrix.* In the basis $|0\rangle, |1\rangle, |2\rangle$, $|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|$ has $\frac12$ in the upper-left $2\times2$ block, $|\beta\rangle\langle\beta|$ in the lower-right one, and $|2\rangle\langle2|$ a single $1$:
>
> $$
> \rho = \frac13\bigl(|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha| + |\beta\rangle\langle\beta| + |2\rangle\langle2|\bigr) = \frac16\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 & 0\\ 1 & 2 & 1\\ 0 & 1 & 3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{tr}\rho = \frac{1 + 2 + 3}6 = 1 .
> $$
>
> *Energy.* $H$ is diagonal with $E_n = (n + \frac12)\hbar\omega$, so only the populations count:
>
> $$
> \langle H\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(\rho H) = \hbar\omega\Bigl(\frac16\cdot\frac12 + \frac26\cdot\frac32 + \frac36\cdot\frac52\Bigr) = \frac{11}6\hbar\omega .
> $$
>
> Check, member by member: $\langle\alpha|H|\alpha\rangle = \hbar\omega$, $\langle\beta|H|\beta\rangle = 2\hbar\omega$, $\langle2|H|2\rangle = \frac52\hbar\omega$, average $\frac13(1 + 2 + \frac52)\hbar\omega = \frac{11}6\hbar\omega$.
>
> *Purity.* $\operatorname{tr}\rho^2$ is the sum of the squares of all elements, $\frac1{36}(1 + 1 + 1 + 4 + 1 + 1 + 9) = \frac12$.
>
> *Position.* With $x = \sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}\,(a + a^\dagger)$ ([[§C3.4 The Harmonic Oscillator and Coherent States Revisited|§C3.4]]), $\operatorname{tr}(\rho a) = \sum_n\sqrt n\,\rho_{n,n-1} = \rho_{10} + \sqrt2\,\rho_{21}$, so at the time of preparation
>
> $$
> \langle x\rangle = \sqrt{\frac\hbar{2m\omega}}\;2\Bigl(\frac16 + \frac{\sqrt2}6\Bigr) = \frac{1 + \sqrt2}3\sqrt{\frac\hbar{2m\omega}} \approx 0.80\sqrt{\frac\hbar{2m\omega}} .
> $$
>
> The ensemble with the same populations $\frac16, \frac13, \frac12$ but no coherences, a mixture of $|0\rangle, |1\rangle, |2\rangle$, has the same energy but $\langle x\rangle = 0$: the energy sees only the diagonal of $\rho$ in the energy basis, the position also the elements next to it. How these coherences oscillate is [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics|§C11.2]].
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 3.14 (solved here; $\langle x\rangle$ and the purity added)*

> [!example] Example §C11.1.3: Two Wave Packets, Superposed or Mixed
> Let $\phi(x)$ be a real packet of width $s$, for definiteness $\phi(x) = (2\pi s^2)^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/4s^2}$, and let $a \gg s$. Compare the pure ensemble $\psi_+ = \frac1{\sqrt2}\bigl[\phi(x - a) + \phi(x + a)\bigr]$ (normalized up to the overlap $\int\phi(x - a)\phi(x + a)\,dx = e^{-a^2/2s^2}$, negligible here) with the $50{:}50$ mixture $M$ of the two packets.
>
> *Position.* By [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-5|Theorem §C11.1.5]],
>
> $$
> \rho_M(x'', x') = \tfrac12\bigl[\phi(x'' - a)\phi(x' - a) + \phi(x'' + a)\phi(x' + a)\bigr], \qquad \rho_+(x'', x') = \rho_M(x'', x') + \tfrac12\bigl[\phi(x'' - a)\phi(x' + a) + \phi(x'' + a)\phi(x' - a)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> In the $(x'', x')$ plane both have lumps at $(a, a)$ and $(-a, -a)$ on the diagonal; the superposition has two more, at $(a, -a)$ and $(-a, a)$, far off the diagonal. The diagonals agree, so the position statistics are identical.
>
> *Momentum.* The momentum wave function of $\phi(x \mp a)$ is $e^{\mp ipa/\hbar}\tilde\phi(p)$. The mixture gives $|\tilde\phi(p)|^2$; the superposition gives
>
> $$
> P_+(p) = \tfrac12|\tilde\phi(p)|^2\,\bigl|e^{-ipa/\hbar} + e^{ipa/\hbar}\bigr|^2 = 2\,|\tilde\phi(p)|^2\cos^2\frac{pa}\hbar ,
> $$
>
> fringes of spacing $\pi\hbar/a$ under the same envelope. They come entirely from the off-diagonal lumps (part 3 of Theorem §C11.1.5): erase those, and the superposition becomes the mixture. The $50{:}50$ mixture of $\psi_+$ and $\psi_- = \frac1{\sqrt2}[\phi(x - a) - \phi(x + a)]$ is again $M$, since the off-diagonal lumps cancel: one more decomposition of the same $\rho$. For the pure superposition the same fringes appear in the Wigner function as an oscillating term midway between the packets ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^ex-c2-3-2|Example §C2.3.2]]); the mixture has no such term. Erasing coherences of exactly this kind is what an environment does ([[§C11.5★ Open Quantum Systems and Decoherence|§C11.5★]]).
>
> *Source: constructed here, after Sakurai §3.4.4 (a beam as a mixture of plane waves or of packets) and Likharev §7.2 (the density matrix carries more than the probability density)*
