How does an ensemble evolve, and which ensemble is thermal equilibrium? The members of an undisturbed ensemble evolve by the unitary operator of [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation|§C3.1]], so $\rho$ obeys an equation that looks like the Heisenberg equation of [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]] with the opposite sign. It is the quantum form of Liouville's equation for a phase-space density ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-4|CM Theorem §B7.2.4]], [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^def-b5-4-1|TH Def. §B5.4.1]]), the analogue that Classical Mechanics and Thermal Physics promised at this level. Thermal Physics level B derived the Boltzmann distribution for the probabilities of energy eigenstates, from a reservoir ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|TH Theorem §B7.1.1]]) or from maximal Gibbs entropy ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-4|TH Theorem §B6.3.4]]). New here: the von Neumann entropy, a functional of $\rho$ that needs no chosen set of microstates; the proof that equilibrium has no coherences in the energy basis; and the canonical density operator $e^{-\beta H}/Z$ as an operator, which answers questions in any representation, such as the position-space coherence of a thermal particle.

*Notation.* $\rho(t)$ is the density operator at time $t$ (Schrödinger picture); $\mathscr U(t, t_0)$ the time-evolution operator; $\beta = 1/kT$; $\sigma$ the dimensionless entropy, $S = k\sigma$; $\langle A\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(\rho A)$ as in [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator|§C11.1]].

## The von Neumann equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.2.1: The von Neumann Equation
> Let an ensemble be left undisturbed, so that its populations $w_i$ do not change and every member evolves by the same $\mathscr U(t, t_0)$. Then
>
> $$
> \rho(t) = \mathscr U(t, t_0)\,\rho(t_0)\,\mathscr U^\dagger(t, t_0), \qquad i\hbar\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = -[\rho, H] = [H, \rho] .
> $$
>
> For a time-independent $H$ with eigenkets $|n\rangle$, the density matrix in the energy basis is $\rho_{nm}(t) = \rho_{nm}(t_0)\,e^{-i(E_n - E_m)(t - t_0)/\hbar}$: the populations $\rho_{nn}$ are constant, and the coherences rotate at the Bohr frequencies of [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-6|Theorem §C3.1.6]].
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.3, eqs. (3.119)–(3.121), Problem 3.15(a) · Griffiths eq. (12.34), Problem 12.4(b) · Likharev §7.3, eqs. (7.64)–(7.66)*

^thm-c11-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each member ket evolves as $|\alpha^{(i)}, t_0; t\rangle = \mathscr U|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle$ ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]]), so $|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle\langle\alpha^{(i)}| \to \mathscr U|\alpha^{(i)}\rangle\langle\alpha^{(i)}|\mathscr U^\dagger$; sum with the fixed $w_i$. Differentiate with $i\hbar\,\partial_t\mathscr U = H\mathscr U$ and $-i\hbar\,\partial_t\mathscr U^\dagger = \mathscr U^\dagger H$ ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]]):
>
> $$
> i\hbar\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = H\mathscr U\rho(t_0)\mathscr U^\dagger - \mathscr U\rho(t_0)\mathscr U^\dagger H = H\rho - \rho H .
> $$
>
> For constant $H$, $\mathscr U = e^{-iH(t - t_0)/\hbar}$ and $\langle n|\mathscr U = e^{-iE_n(t - t_0)/\hbar}\langle n|$.

*Uses:* [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]], [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]], [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^def-c11-1-2|Def. §C11.1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: The sign, the picture, and closed systems only
> - The equation "looks like the Heisenberg equation of motion except that the sign is wrong" (Sakurai). It is not a Heisenberg equation: $\rho$ is built from Schrödinger-picture kets and bras, and it is not an observable. In the Heisenberg picture $\rho$ is frozen and the observables move ([[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-2|Theorem §C11.2.2]]).
> - It holds for a closed system, and for a system whose Hamiltonian involves only its own variables (possibly depending on time). For a system coupled to an environment it is valid only for system plus environment together, not for the density operator of the system alone (Likharev §7.3, footnote 28, regretting that some textbooks quote the equation without this qualification). What replaces it is [[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices|§C11.3]] (uncoupled parts) and [[§C11.5★ Open Quantum Systems and Decoherence|§C11.5★]] (coupled ones).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.3 · Likharev §7.3, text after eq. (7.67) and footnote 28*

^cau-c11-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.2.2: Ensemble Averages in Either Picture
> With $\mathscr U = \mathscr U(t, 0)$ and $A^{(H)}(t) = \mathscr U^\dagger A^{(S)}\mathscr U$,
>
> $$
> \langle A\rangle_t = \operatorname{tr}\bigl(\rho(t)\,A^{(S)}\bigr) = \operatorname{tr}\bigl(\rho(0)\,A^{(H)}(t)\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> so the Heisenberg equation ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]) gives $\frac{d}{dt}\langle A\rangle = \frac1{i\hbar}\langle[A, H]\rangle + \langle\partial A/\partial t\rangle$ for mixed ensembles as well; in particular Ehrenfest's theorem holds for ensemble averages.
>
> *Source: derived here; the interaction-picture analogue is Likharev §7.6, eq. (7.167)*

^thm-c11-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\operatorname{tr}(\mathscr U\rho(0)\mathscr U^\dagger A) = \operatorname{tr}(\rho(0)\mathscr U^\dagger A\mathscr U)$ by cyclicity of the trace ([[§33 Trace꞉ A Connection Between Matrices and Operators#^ladr-8-49|LADR Thm. 8.49]]). Differentiate the right side under the trace; $\rho(0)$ is constant. Equivalently, with Theorem §C11.2.1, $i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{tr}(\rho A) = \operatorname{tr}([H, \rho]A) = \operatorname{tr}(\rho[A, H])$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.2.3: Unitary Evolution Preserves Purity
> Under the evolution of Theorem §C11.2.1 the eigenvalues of $\rho$ do not change. Hence $\operatorname{tr}\rho^n$, the purity $\operatorname{tr}\rho^2$ and the entropy of [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^def-c11-2-1|Def. §C11.2.1]] are constants of the motion. A pure ensemble cannot evolve into a mixed one, nor a mixed one into a pure one, as long as the time evolution is governed by the Schrödinger equation.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 3.15(b) (solved here)*

^thm-c11-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\rho(t) = \mathscr U\rho(t_0)\mathscr U^\dagger$ with $\mathscr U^\dagger = \mathscr U^{-1}$: if $\rho(t_0)|k\rangle = \lambda_k|k\rangle$, then $\rho(t)\,\mathscr U|k\rangle = \lambda_k\,\mathscr U|k\rangle$, and $\{\mathscr U|k\rangle\}$ is again an orthonormal basis. Every function of $\rho$ built from its eigenvalues, $\operatorname{tr}\rho^n = \sum_k\lambda_k^n$ or $-\sum_k\lambda_k\ln\lambda_k$, is unchanged. Purity is $\operatorname{tr}\rho^2 = 1$ ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-3|Theorem §C11.1.3]]).

*Uses:* [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-1|Theorem §C11.2.1]], [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-3|Theorem §C11.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: The quantum Liouville equation
> - **Correspondence.** Classically a statistical state is a density $\rho_{\text{cl}}(q, p, t)$ of representative points in phase space, and Liouville's theorem gives $\partial\rho_{\text{cl}}/\partial t = -\{\rho_{\text{cl}}, H\}$ ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-4|CM Theorem §B7.2.4]]; Sakurai's eq. (3.122), with the Poisson bracket $[\,,]_{\text{classical}}$ of [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-1|CM Def. §B8.1.1]]). Dirac's rule $\{\,,\} \to [\,,]/i\hbar$ ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^rem-c3-3-1|Remark: The classical bracket, transcribed]]) turns it into Theorem §C11.2.1; the classical average $\int\rho_{\text{cl}}A\,d\Gamma/\int\rho_{\text{cl}}\,d\Gamma$ (3.123) becomes $\operatorname{tr}(\rho A)$. Hence the name density operator, and Likharev notes that the von Neumann equation is sometimes called the Liouville equation.
> - **Stationary ensembles.** In both theories a function of the Hamiltonian is stationary: $\{f(H), H\} = 0$ classically ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-1|TH Theorem §B5.4.1]]), $[f(H), H] = 0$ here.
> - **No entropy growth.** Theorem §C11.2.3 is the quantum version of the statement that the fine-grained entropy of a classical ensemble never changes ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-1|TH Theorem §B5.4.1]], [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^rem-b6-3-3|TH Remark: Fine-grained entropy does not grow]]). In quantum mechanics entropy grows when a system is entangled with, or perturbed randomly by, an environment ([[§C11.3 Composite Systems and Reduced Density Matrices|§C11.3]], [[§C11.5★ Open Quantum Systems and Decoherence|§C11.5★]]).
> - **Pure states.** A pure classical state is one moving point; a pure quantum ensemble is a projector, and its "points" never sharpen beyond the uncertainty relations.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.3, eqs. (3.122)–(3.123), footnote 7 · Likharev §7.3, footnote 26*

## Entropy

> [!definition] Definition §C11.2.1: Von Neumann Entropy
> The **von Neumann entropy** of a density operator is
>
> $$
> \sigma = -\operatorname{tr}(\rho\ln\rho) = -\sum_k\lambda_k\ln\lambda_k ,
> $$
>
> computed in the basis in which $\rho$ is diagonal, with eigenvalues $\lambda_k$ and $0\ln0 = 0$. The **entropy** of the ensemble, per member, is $S = k\sigma$; Sakurai notes that this may be taken as the definition of entropy in quantum statistical mechanics.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.5, eqs. (3.127)–(3.128), (3.133)*

^def-c11-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.2.4: Properties of the von Neumann Entropy
> On an $N$-dimensional space:
> 1. $0 \le \sigma \le \ln N$; $\sigma = 0$ exactly for pure ensembles and $\sigma = \ln N$ exactly for the completely random one, $\rho = \frac1N\mathbf 1$.
> 2. **Coherences lower the entropy.** In any orthonormal basis $\{|e_j\rangle\}$, the diagonal elements $p_j = \langle e_j|\rho|e_j\rangle$ form a probability distribution whose Gibbs entropy is at least $\sigma$:
>
> $$
> -\sum_jp_j\ln p_j \ \ge\ \sigma ,
> $$
>
> with equality only if $\rho$ is diagonal in that basis. Erasing the off-diagonal elements in some basis (an unread measurement, [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-1|Theorem §C11.1.1]], part 3) never lowers the entropy.
> 3. For spin $\frac12$ with Bloch vector $\mathbf b$, $\sigma = -p_+\ln p_+ - p_-\ln p_-$ with $p_\pm = \frac12(1 \pm |\mathbf b|)$: a decreasing function of $|\mathbf b|$, from $\ln2$ at the centre of the Bloch ball to $0$ on its surface.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.5, eqs. (3.128)–(3.132); parts 2–3 derived here*

^thm-c11-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\sigma$ is the Gibbs entropy (in units of $k$) of the distribution $\lambda_k$ ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^def-b6-3-1|TH Def. §B6.3.1]]), whose bounds, $0$ exactly for a certain outcome and $\ln N$ exactly for the uniform distribution, are [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-3|TH Theorem §B6.3.3]]; a single $\lambda_k = 1$ is a pure ensemble ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-3|Theorem §C11.1.3]]). Sakurai's check: each term $-\lambda\ln\lambda$ vanishes for $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda = 1$ (3.131), and $\frac1N\mathbf 1$ gives $-\sum\frac1N\ln\frac1N = \ln N$ (3.129).
>
> *2.* With $\rho = \sum_k\lambda_k|k\rangle\langle k|$, $p_j = \sum_kD_{jk}\lambda_k$ where $D_{jk} = |\langle e_j|k\rangle|^2 \ge 0$ and $\sum_jD_{jk} = \sum_kD_{jk} = 1$ (both are orthonormal bases). The function $f(x) = -x\ln x$ is strictly concave on $[0, 1]$, so for each $j$, since $p_j$ is the $D_{jk}$-weighted average of the $\lambda_k$, $f(p_j) \ge \sum_kD_{jk}f(\lambda_k)$ (Jensen's inequality). Summing over $j$: $\sum_jf(p_j) \ge \sum_kf(\lambda_k) = \sigma$. Equality forces, for each $j$, all $\lambda_k$ with $D_{jk} > 0$ to be equal, i.e. $|e_j\rangle$ lies in one eigenspace of $\rho$; then every $|e_j\rangle$ is an eigenket and $\rho$ is diagonal in that basis. The erased ensemble $\sum_jp_j|e_j\rangle\langle e_j|$ has entropy $-\sum_jp_j\ln p_j$.
>
> *3.* The eigenvalues of $\frac12(\mathbf 1 + \mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$ are $p_\pm$ ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^thm-c11-1-4|Theorem §C11.1.4]]); $\frac{d\sigma}{d|\mathbf b|} = \frac12\ln\frac{p_-}{p_+} < 0$ for $|\mathbf b| > 0$.

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from the Gibbs entropy
> Thermal Physics' Gibbs entropy $-k\sum_iP_i\ln P_i$ ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^def-b6-3-1|TH Def. §B6.3.1]]) needs a list of microstates and their probabilities. For a quantum ensemble the list is a basis, and part 2 of Theorem §C11.2.4 shows that different bases give different numbers: $\sigma$ is the smallest of them, attained in the eigenbasis of $\rho$. It is a property of the ensemble alone. Sakurai's picture: a pure ensemble is a regiment of marching soldiers ($\sigma = 0$), the completely random ensemble a crowd of drunken soldiers wandering in all directions ($\sigma = \ln N$).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.5*

## Thermal equilibrium

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.2.5: The Canonical Density Operator
> Among all density operators with a prescribed ensemble average of the energy, $\operatorname{tr}(\rho H) = U$ (with $U$ strictly between the lowest and the highest eigenvalue of $H$), the entropy $\sigma$ is largest for exactly one,
>
> $$
> \rho = \frac{e^{-\beta H}}{Z}, \qquad Z = \operatorname{tr}e^{-\beta H} = \sum_ke^{-\beta E_k}, \qquad U = -\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta} ,
> $$
>
> where the sum runs over states (a degenerate level counted with its multiplicity) and $\beta$ is fixed by $U$. Its matrix in the energy basis is diagonal with $\rho_{kk} = e^{-\beta E_k}/Z$, and $\langle A\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(e^{-\beta H}A)/Z$ for every observable. It is stationary, $[\rho, H] = 0$. With $\beta = 1/kT$: as $\beta \to 0$ it becomes the completely random ensemble $\frac1N\mathbf 1$; as $\beta \to \infty$, the projector onto the ground level divided by its degeneracy $g_0$, $P_0/g_0$ (a pure ensemble if the ground state is nondegenerate). Without the energy constraint the maximum is $\frac1N\mathbf 1$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.5, eqs. (3.134)–(3.147) · Likharev §7.1, eqs. (7.24)–(7.25) (Gibbs distribution); the reduction to diagonal $\rho$ without assuming stationarity derived here*

^thm-c11-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Reduction to populations.* Sakurai assumes that in equilibrium $\partial\rho/\partial t = 0$, so $[\rho, H] = 0$ by Theorem §C11.2.1 and $\rho$, $H$ have a common eigenbasis (3.134). The assumption is not needed. Fix an orthonormal eigenbasis $\{|k\rangle\}$ of $H$ and, for any admissible $\rho$, let $\rho_d = \sum_k\rho_{kk}|k\rangle\langle k|$ be its diagonal part. Then $\operatorname{tr}(\rho_dH) = \sum_k\rho_{kk}E_k = \operatorname{tr}(\rho H) = U$, and $\sigma(\rho_d) \ge \sigma(\rho)$ with equality only if $\rho = \rho_d$ ([[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-4|Theorem §C11.2.4]], part 2). So a maximizer is diagonal in this basis, and it remains to maximize $-\sum_k\rho_{kk}\ln\rho_{kk}$ subject to $\sum_k\rho_{kk} = 1$ and $\sum_k\rho_{kk}E_k = U$.
>
> *The populations.* Lagrange multipliers give Sakurai's $\sum_k\delta\rho_{kk}[(\ln\rho_{kk} + 1) + \beta E_k + \gamma] = 0$ (3.138), hence $\rho_{kk} = e^{-\beta E_k - \gamma - 1}$ (3.139), and normalization gives $\rho_{kk} = e^{-\beta E_k}/\sum_le^{-\beta E_l}$ (3.140). That this stationary point is the unique maximum, and that a $\beta$ exists for every admissible $U$, is the proof of [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-4|TH Theorem §B6.3.4]], which applies verbatim to the populations. Without the energy constraint, $\beta = 0$ and $\rho_{kk} = 1/N$ (3.141).
>
> *The operator.* $\sum_k\frac{e^{-\beta E_k}}Z|k\rangle\langle k| = e^{-\beta H}/Z$ by the spectral definition of a function of $H$; within a degenerate level all weights are equal, so the result does not depend on the eigenbasis chosen. $Z = \sum_ke^{-\beta E_k} = \operatorname{tr}e^{-\beta H}$ (3.142)–(3.143), and $-\partial_\beta\ln Z = \sum_kE_ke^{-\beta E_k}/Z = U$ (3.146). As $\beta \to 0$ all weights tend to $1/N$; as $\beta \to \infty$ the weights of excited levels vanish relative to the ground level, whose $g_0$ states keep equal weights $1/g_0$. The identification $\beta = 1/kT$ with $k$ Boltzmann's constant (3.147) is that of [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|TH Theorem §B7.1.1]], where the probabilities are derived from a heat reservoir; Sakurai suggests checking it with oscillators (Example §C11.2.2).

*Uses:* [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-4|Theorem §C11.2.4]], [[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^thm-b6-3-4|TH Theorem §B6.3.4]], [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|TH Theorem §B7.1.1]], [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^def-b7-1-2|TH Def. §B7.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level B of Thermal Physics
> - **Layer change.** Thermal Physics obtains the canonical probabilities $P(s) = e^{-\beta E_s}/Z$ for microstates $s$ that are, at bottom, energy eigenstates, taken as given. Here the statistical operator is derived: that equilibrium has no coherences in the energy basis is part of the result (part 2 of Theorem §C11.2.4), and the answer is an operator, $\rho = e^{-\beta H}/Z$, which "is the most basic equation from which everything follows" (Sakurai). It can be written in any basis: the position representation of [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-6|Theorem §C11.2.6]] and Example §C11.2.2 has coherences that no list of probabilities contains.
> - **Same results, no new box.** Sakurai's spin-$\frac12$ paramagnet (3.148)–(3.152), $\langle S_z\rangle = -\frac\hbar2\tanh\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}2$ and the Brillouin–Curie susceptibility, is [[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-5|TH Theorem §B7.1.5]] with $\mu B = \frac12\hbar\omega$; the oscillator's $U = \frac{\hbar\omega}2\coth\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}2$ (Likharev's eq. (7.26)) is [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^ex-b7-2-1|TH Example §B7.2.1]]. Likharev stresses that the Gibbs distribution is general, compatible with Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac statistics ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|TH Theorem §B10.1.2]]): the oscillator's mean excitation number $\bar n = 1/(e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1)$ is the Bose–Einstein occupation at zero chemical potential.
> - **Imaginary time.** $Z$ is the trace of the propagator at $t = -i\hbar\beta$ ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-6|Theorem §C4.1.6]], [[§C4.1 Propagators#^ex-c4-1-3|Example §C4.1.3]]); the whole unnormalized matrix $\langle x''|e^{-\beta H}|x'\rangle$ is the propagator $K(x'', -i\hbar\beta; x', 0)$, since $e^{-\beta H} = \mathscr U(-i\hbar\beta, 0)$. Likharev calls this correspondence the basis of thermodynamic Green's functions.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.5 · Likharev §7.1, eqs. (7.24)–(7.26), §7.2, eqs. (7.41)–(7.42)*

^rem-c11-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C11.2.6: Thermal Density Matrix of a Free Particle
> For a free particle of mass $m$ in one dimension, on a length $L$ with periodic boundary conditions and $kT$ much larger than the level spacing, the canonical density matrix in the position representation is
>
> $$
> \langle x''|\rho|x'\rangle = \frac1L\exp\Bigl[-\frac{\pi(x'' - x')^2}{\lambda_{\text{th}}^2}\Bigr] = \frac1L\exp\Bigl[-\frac{mkT\,(x'' - x')^2}{2\hbar^2}\Bigr], \qquad \lambda_{\text{th}} = \frac h{\sqrt{2\pi mkT}} ,
> $$
>
> with $Z = L/\lambda_{\text{th}}$ and $\lambda_{\text{th}}$ the thermal wavelength of [[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^def-b8-1-1|TH Def. §B8.1.1]]. The diagonal is flat (every position equally likely), while the coherences extend only over $|x'' - x'| \lesssim \lambda_{\text{th}}$, Likharev's correlation length $x_c = \hbar/\sqrt{mkT} = \lambda_{\text{th}}/\sqrt{2\pi}$. As $T \to \infty$ the unnormalized matrix $Z\langle x''|\rho|x'\rangle = \lambda_{\text{th}}^{-1}e^{-\pi(x'' - x')^2/\lambda_{\text{th}}^2}$ tends to $\delta(x'' - x')$ (Likharev's eq. (7.40)): a classical mixture of points. The momentum distribution is the Maxwell–Boltzmann $e^{-p^2/2mkT}$ ([[§B5.1 The Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution and Effusion#^thm-b5-1-2|TH Theorem §B5.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: Likharev §7.2, eqs. (7.33)–(7.40) · Sakurai §2.6.1 (propagator and partition function)*

^thm-c11-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The eigenkets are $\langle x|p\rangle = L^{-1/2}e^{ipx/\hbar}$, $E_p = p^2/2m$, with $p = 2\pi\hbar n/L$; for many thermally populated levels $\sum_p \to \frac{L}{2\pi\hbar}\int dp$. Then
>
> $$
> \langle x''|e^{-\beta H}|x'\rangle = \sum_p\frac1L\,e^{ip(x'' - x')/\hbar}e^{-\beta p^2/2m} = \frac1{2\pi\hbar}\int dp\,e^{-\beta p^2/2m + ip(x'' - x')/\hbar} = \sqrt{\frac m{2\pi\beta\hbar^2}}\,e^{-m(x'' - x')^2/2\beta\hbar^2}
> $$
>
> by the Gaussian integral of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]. The prefactor is $\sqrt{2\pi mkT}/h = 1/\lambda_{\text{th}}$, so $Z = \int_0^Ldx\,\langle x|e^{-\beta H}|x\rangle = L/\lambda_{\text{th}}$; and $\frac{m}{2\beta\hbar^2} = \frac{mkT}{2\hbar^2} = \frac{\pi}{\lambda_{\text{th}}^2}$ since $\lambda_{\text{th}}^2 = 2\pi\hbar^2/mkT$. The same result is the free propagator ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]]) $\sqrt{m/2\pi i\hbar t}\,e^{im(x'' - x')^2/2\hbar t}$ at $t = -i\hbar\beta$: $i\hbar t = \hbar^2\beta$ and $im/2\hbar t = -m/2\hbar^2\beta$. The density matrix is diagonal in $p$ with weights $e^{-\beta p^2/2m}/Z$, the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution.

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The Wigner function of a density operator
> The Wigner function of a pure state and its marginals are worked out in [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^ex-c2-3-2|Example §C2.3.2]]. For an ensemble, Likharev defines it from the density matrix by a Fourier transform in the difference coordinate,
>
> $$
> W(X, P) = \frac1{2\pi\hbar}\int d\tilde X\,\Bigl\langle X + \frac{\tilde X}2\Bigr|\rho\Bigl|X - \frac{\tilde X}2\Bigr\rangle\,e^{-iP\tilde X/\hbar} ,
> $$
>
> which for $\rho = |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$ is the formula of Example §C2.3.2 (put $\tilde X = -2y$). What the ensemble form adds:
> - $W$ is linear in $\rho$, so the Wigner function of a mixture is the weighted sum of those of its members, and the marginal properties $\int W\,dP = \langle X|\rho|X\rangle$, $\int W\,dX = \langle P|\rho|P\rangle$ carry over term by term.
> - For the thermal free particle of Theorem §C11.2.6, the $\tilde X$-integral of the Gaussian $\frac1L e^{-mkT\tilde X^2/2\hbar^2}$ gives $W = \frac1L(2\pi mkT)^{-1/2}e^{-P^2/2mkT}$: independent of $X$ and exactly the classical Gibbs distribution on the phase plane. For the thermal oscillator, $W$ is a Gaussian in both $X$ and $P$, approaching the classical $e^{-H(X, P)/kT}$ only for $kT \gg \hbar\omega$.
> - For Fock states $n \ge 1$, $W$ is negative somewhere (for $n = 1$ it equals $-1/\pi\hbar$ at the origin), so it is not a probability density; Likharev recommends it only as a semi-quantitative picture of mixed states on the phase plane.
>
> *Source: Likharev §7.2, eqs. (7.50)–(7.63), Fig. 7.3*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C11.2.1: A Partially Polarized Beam in a Magnetic Field
> Sakurai's $75{:}25$ mixture of $S_z+$ and $S_x+$, $\rho(0) = \begin{pmatrix}7/8 & 1/8\\ 1/8 & 1/8\end{pmatrix}$, Bloch vector $\mathbf b(0) = (\frac14, 0, \frac34)$ ([[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^ex-c11-1-1|Example §C11.1.1]]), enters a uniform field along $z$ with $H = \omega S_z$ ($\omega = |e|B/m_ec$ for an electron in Sakurai's Gaussian units; [[§C3.2 Two-State Systems, Spin Precession and Neutrino Oscillations#^cau-c3-2-1|Caution: Units and signs of the spin moment]]). Find $\rho(t)$, the averages of $\mathbf S$, and the entropy, and compare with the thermal ensemble in the same field.
>
> *Evolution.* $E_\pm = \pm\frac12\hbar\omega$, so by [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-1|Theorem §C11.2.1]] the populations stay $\frac78, \frac18$ and $\rho_{+-}(t) = \frac18e^{-i\omega t}$:
>
> $$
> \rho(t) = \begin{pmatrix}7/8 & e^{-i\omega t}/8\\ e^{i\omega t}/8 & 1/8\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \langle S_x\rangle = \frac\hbar8\cos\omega t, \quad \langle S_y\rangle = \frac\hbar8\sin\omega t, \quad \langle S_z\rangle = \frac{3\hbar}8 ,
> $$
>
> using $\rho_{+-} = \frac12(b_x - ib_y)$. The Bloch vector precesses about $z$ at $\omega$, $d\mathbf b/dt = \omega\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf b$, exactly as the pure-state Bloch vector of [[§C3.2 Two-State Systems, Spin Precession and Neutrino Oscillations#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]] (part 4): the equation is linear in $\rho$, so it holds inside the ball as on its surface. The same follows from the Heisenberg equations $\dot S_x = -\omega S_y$, $\dot S_y = \omega S_x$ and Theorem §C11.2.2.
>
> *Invariants.* $|\mathbf b| = \sqrt{1/16 + 9/16} = \sqrt{10}/4 = 0.791$ for all $t$, so the purity $\frac12(1 + |\mathbf b|^2) = \frac{13}{16}$ and the entropy, with $p_\pm = \frac12(1 \pm 0.791) = 0.895, 0.105$, $\sigma = 0.335$, are constant ([[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-3|Theorem §C11.2.3]], [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-4|Theorem §C11.2.4]]).
>
> *Thermal ensemble.* $\rho_{\text{th}} = e^{-\beta\omega S_z}/Z$ is diagonal, with $\mathbf b_{\text{th}} = -\tanh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)\,\hat{\mathbf z}$, Sakurai's (3.150), and does not move. The precessing beam never approaches it: an isolated ensemble keeps its entropy, and relaxation to $\rho_{\text{th}}$ needs an environment ([[§C11.5★ Open Quantum Systems and Decoherence#^thm-c11-5-6|Theorem §C11.5.6]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.4.2, Example 4, and §3.4.5, eqs. (3.148)–(3.150); evolution and entropy computed here*

> [!example] Example §C11.2.2: The Thermal Oscillator in Position Space
> Find the canonical density matrix $\langle x''|\rho|x'\rangle$ of an oscillator of mass $m$ and frequency $\omega$, its position distribution, and the range of its coherences.
>
> *Imaginary-time propagator.* With $\theta = \beta\hbar\omega$, put $\omega T = -i\theta$ in the oscillator propagator of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-5|Theorem §C4.1.5]]: $\sin(-i\theta) = -i\sinh\theta$, $\cos(-i\theta) = \cosh\theta$, so $i\sin\omega T = \sinh\theta$ and
>
> $$
> \langle x''|e^{-\beta H}|x'\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\pi\hbar\sinh\theta}}\exp\Bigl\{-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar\sinh\theta}\Bigl[(x''^2 + x'^2)\cosh\theta - 2x''x'\Bigr]\Bigr\} ,
> $$
>
> Likharev's eq. (7.44); its trace is $Z = 1/2\sinh(\theta/2)$ ([[§C4.1 Propagators#^ex-c4-1-3|Example §C4.1.3]]). In centre and difference coordinates $X = \frac12(x'' + x')$, $\Delta = x'' - x'$, the bracket divided by $\sinh\theta$ is $2X^2\tanh\frac\theta2 + \frac12\Delta^2\coth\frac\theta2$, so
>
> $$
> \langle x''|\rho|x'\rangle \propto \exp\Bigl[-\frac{m\omega}\hbar\tanh\frac\theta2\,X^2 - \frac{m\omega}{4\hbar}\coth\frac\theta2\,\Delta^2\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Position distribution.* On the diagonal ($\Delta = 0$) a Gaussian with
>
> $$
> \langle x^2\rangle = \frac\hbar{2m\omega}\coth\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}2 , \qquad \bigl\langle\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2\bigr\rangle = \frac{\hbar\omega}4\coth\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}2 = \frac U2 ,
> $$
>
> half of the energy $U$ of [[§B7.2 Free Energy from the Partition Function#^ex-b7-2-1|TH Example §B7.2.1]]. Check by operators: $x^2 = \frac\hbar{2m\omega}(a + a^\dagger)^2$ has diagonal elements $\frac\hbar{2m\omega}(2n + 1)$, and $2\bar n + 1 = \coth\frac\theta2$ with $\bar n = 1/(e^\theta - 1)$. Limits: $kT \ll \hbar\omega$ gives $\frac\hbar{2m\omega}$, the ground state's spread, and in fact $\rho \to |0\rangle\langle0|$ since then $\tanh\frac\theta2 = \coth\frac\theta2 = 1$ and the exponent is $-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}(x''^2 + x'^2)$; $kT \gg \hbar\omega$ gives $\langle x^2\rangle = kT/m\omega^2$, the classical Boltzmann distribution and equipartition ([[§B8.2 Equipartition and the Heat Capacities of Molecules#^thm-b8-2-1|TH Theorem §B8.2.1]]), Sakurai's suggested check that $\beta = 1/kT$.
>
> *Coherence.* The coherences fall off as $e^{-\Delta^2/2\ell_c^2}$ with $\ell_c^2 = \frac{2\hbar}{m\omega}\tanh\frac\theta2$. At high temperature $\tanh\frac\theta2 \to \frac\theta2$ and $\ell_c \to \hbar/\sqrt{mkT}$, the free particle's $x_c$ of Theorem §C11.2.6, much smaller than the width $\sqrt{kT/m\omega^2}$ of the distribution: a nearly classical mixture of positions. At low temperature $\ell_c^2 \to 2\hbar/m\omega$, four times $\langle x^2\rangle$: the coherence spans the whole ground-state packet.
>
> *Source: Likharev §7.2, eqs. (7.43)–(7.49) · Sakurai §3.4.5 (the oscillator check suggested on p. 129); the coherence length computed here*
