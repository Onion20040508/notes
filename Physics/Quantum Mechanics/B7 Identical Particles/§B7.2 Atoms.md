*Sources: Griffiths & Schroeter, Introduction to Quantum Mechanics (3rd ed.), §5.2 · Greensite, Physics 430 lecture notes, ch. 15.*

Level A built atoms from the independent-electron model ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^mod-a5-3-3|Model §A5.3.3]]): hydrogen-like orbitals, filled in the empirical order of [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^law-a5-3-5|Law §A5.3.5]], at most $2(2\ell+1)$ electrons per subshell ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^thm-a5-3-4|Theorem §A5.3.4]]). This section writes down the actual Hamiltonian of an atom and asks what the symmetrization requirement of [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions]] adds. For helium it fixes the spin of the ground state and splits the excited states into two families, para- and orthohelium, whose energy difference is the exchange integral. For heavier atoms it determines which total angular momenta $L$, $S$, $J$ a configuration can have, and Hund's rules pick the ground term. Computing helium's ground-state energy properly needs the variational method ([[§B9.2★ Helium and the Hydrogen Molecule]]).

## The atomic Hamiltonian

> [!model] Model §B7.2.1: Atom with a Fixed Nucleus
> A neutral atom of atomic number $Z$ is a nucleus of charge $Ze$, held fixed at the origin, and $Z$ electrons of mass $m$ and charge $-e$, with Hamiltonian
>
> $$
> \hat H = \sum_{j=1}^Z\Bigl\{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla_j^2 - \frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Ze^2}{r_j}\Bigr\} + \frac12\,\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_{j\ne k}^Z\frac{e^2}{|\mathbf r_j - \mathbf r_k|} .
> $$
>
> The braces hold the kinetic and potential energy of each electron in the nuclear field; the double sum is the mutual repulsion, the $\tfrac12$ correcting for counting each pair twice. The states solve $\hat H\psi = E\psi$ for $\psi(\mathbf r_1, \dots, \mathbf r_Z)$ times a spin state, with the whole antisymmetric under the exchange of any two electrons ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^pr-b7-1-3|Principle §B7.1.3]]). *Assumptions:* infinitely heavy nucleus (the reduced-mass trick works only for two bodies, and the correction is tiny); nonrelativistic; Coulomb forces only, so no spin–orbit or other magnetic terms and a point nucleus. Except for $Z = 1$ the equation has not been solved exactly.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.2, eqs. (5.36)–(5.37), footnotes 14–15; Greensite §15.3, eq. (15.60)*

^mod-b7-2-1

## Helium

> [!model] Model §B7.2.2: Independent-Electron Helium
> Helium ($Z = 2$) has
>
> $$
> \hat H = \Bigl\{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla_1^2 - \frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{2e^2}{r_1}\Bigr\} + \Bigl\{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla_2^2 - \frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{2e^2}{r_2}\Bigr\} + \frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{e^2}{|\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2|} ,
> $$
>
> two hydrogen-like Hamiltonians with nuclear charge $2e$ plus the repulsion. The **independent-electron model** of helium drops the last term. It is the crudest form of [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^mod-a5-3-3|Model §A5.3.3]], with no average screening at all.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.2.1, eq. (5.38); Greensite §15.2*

^mod-b7-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.3: States of Independent-Electron Helium
> In Model §B7.2.2 the spatial stationary states are built from hydrogen wave functions with half the Bohr radius, $\psi_{n\ell m}$ with $a \to a/2$, and the energies are four times the Bohr energies:
>
> $$
> E = 4\,(E_n + E_{n'}), \qquad E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \mathrm{eV}}{n^2} .
> $$
>
> 1. The **ground state** is
>
> $$
> \psi_0(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2) = \psi_{100}(\mathbf r_1)\psi_{100}(\mathbf r_2) = \frac8{\pi a^3}\,e^{-2(r_1 + r_2)/a}, \qquad E_0 = 8\,(-13.6\ \mathrm{eV}) = -109\ \mathrm{eV},
> $$
>
> spatially symmetric, so its spin state is the **singlet**. (Measured: $-78.975\ \mathrm{eV}$.)
> 2. The bound **excited states** have one electron in $\psi_{100}$ and the other in $\psi_{n\ell m}$, $n \ge 2$. The spatially symmetric combination goes with the singlet (**parahelium**, $S = 0$), the antisymmetric one with the triplet (**orthohelium**, $S = 1$):
>
> $$
> \psi_\pm = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl[\psi_{100}(\mathbf r_1)\psi_{n\ell m}(\mathbf r_2) \pm \psi_{n\ell m}(\mathbf r_1)\psi_{100}(\mathbf r_2)\bigr], \qquad \psi_+\chi_{\text{singlet}}, \quad \psi_-\chi_{\text{triplet}} .
> $$
>
> The ground state is necessarily parahelium. In this model para- and orthohelium states with the same $n\ell m$ have the same energy $4(E_1 + E_n)$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.2.1, eqs. (5.39)–(5.43); Greensite §15.2, eqs. (15.46)–(15.49)*

^thm-b7-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Without the repulsion $V = V_1(\mathbf r_1) + V_2(\mathbf r_2)$, so products of one-electron states are stationary with $E = E_a + E_b$ ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]]). Each factor solves the hydrogen problem ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom]]) with $e^2 \to Ze^2 = 2e^2$; since the Bohr radius is $\propto 1/e^2$ and the energies $\propto e^4$, this gives $a \to a/2$ and $E_n \to 4E_n$ (Griffiths Problem 4.19). With $a/2$ in $\psi_{100} = (\pi a^3)^{-1/2}e^{-r/a}$, $\psi_{100} = \bigl(8/\pi a^3\bigr)^{1/2}e^{-2r/a}$, and $\psi_{100}(\mathbf r_1)\psi_{100}(\mathbf r_2) = \psi_0$. $\psi_0$ is symmetric under $\mathbf r_1 \leftrightarrow \mathbf r_2$, so by [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-7|Theorem §B7.1.7]] it requires the singlet; there is no antisymmetric spatial state with both electrons in $\psi_{100}$. For different orbitals both spatial symmetries exist, each paired with its spin state by the same theorem. States with both electrons excited lie above $E = 4E_1 = -54.4\ \mathrm{eV}$, the energy of $\mathrm{He}^+$ plus a free electron at rest (already $4(E_2 + E_2) = -13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$ is far above it): one electron drops to the ground state and the energy released ejects the other, so they are not bound states.

^der-b7-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B7.2 Atoms#^mod-b7-2-2|Model §B7.2.2]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-7|Theorem §B7.1.7]], [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom]]

> [!remark] Remark: The repulsion is not small
> The error of $-109\ \mathrm{eV}$ against $-79\ \mathrm{eV}$ is the neglected repulsion, which is positive and about $30\ \mathrm{eV}$: a 30 % correction. Level A estimated it by averaging the repulsion over $\psi_0$, which gives $+34\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $E \approx -75\ \mathrm{eV}$ ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^ex-a5-3-1|Example §A5.3.1]]; Griffiths Problem 5.15). Letting each electron see a screened nuclear charge and choosing the screening variationally does better ([[§B9.2★ Helium and the Hydrogen Molecule]]). The spin assignments of Theorem §B7.2.3 do not depend on these corrections: the measured ground state is indeed a singlet.

^rem-b7-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.4: Para- and Orthohelium Split by the Exchange Integral
> In the states $\psi_\pm$ of Theorem §B7.2.3 (with any normalised spin state), the electron–electron repulsion $V_{ee} = \dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0|\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2|}$ has the expectation value
>
> $$
> \langle V_{ee}\rangle_\pm = J \pm K ,
> $$
>
> with the **direct** (Coulomb) and **exchange** integrals
>
> $$
> J = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\!\!\int\frac{|\psi_{100}(\mathbf r_1)|^2\,|\psi_{n\ell m}(\mathbf r_2)|^2}{|\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2|}\,d^3r_1\,d^3r_2, \qquad
> K = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\!\!\int\frac{\psi_{100}^*(\mathbf r_1)\psi_{n\ell m}(\mathbf r_1)\;\psi_{n\ell m}^*(\mathbf r_2)\psi_{100}(\mathbf r_2)}{|\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2|}\,d^3r_1\,d^3r_2 .
> $$
>
> Both are positive. Hence parahelium (spatially symmetric, singlet) lies above orthohelium (antisymmetric, triplet) with the same orbitals, by about $2K$.
>
> *Source: Greensite §15.2, eqs. (15.50)–(15.59); Griffiths §5.2.1, p. 268, Figure 5.1*

^thm-b7-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $V_{ee}$ does not act on spin and the spin state is normalised, so only the spatial integral remains. Write $a = 100$, $b = n\ell m$. Expanding $\psi_\pm^*V_{ee}\psi_\pm$ gives four terms with the factor $\tfrac12$: the two "direct" ones, $|\psi_a(1)|^2|\psi_b(2)|^2$ and $|\psi_b(1)|^2|\psi_a(2)|^2$, each integrate to $J$ (rename $\mathbf r_1 \leftrightarrow \mathbf r_2$ in the second; $V_{ee}$ is symmetric), and the two "exchange" ones, with sign $\pm$, are $K$ and its complex conjugate. So $\langle V_{ee}\rangle_\pm = J \pm \operatorname{Re}K$, and it remains to show that $K$ is real and positive. $J > 0$ because its integrand is.
>
> Put $g(\mathbf r) = \psi_{100}^*(\mathbf r)\,\psi_{n\ell m}(\mathbf r)$, so that $K = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\!\!\int g(\mathbf r_1)\,\overline{g(\mathbf r_2)}\,|\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2|^{-1}\,d^3r_1d^3r_2$, the electrostatic self-energy of the complex "charge density" $g$. The three-dimensional Fourier representation $\dfrac1{|\mathbf r|} = \displaystyle\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{4\pi}{k^2}\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$ (the one-dimensional transform is [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|WO Def. §B4.4.1]]; the 3D version and the Coulomb transform belong to Mathematical Methods, planned) turns this into
>
> $$
> K = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{4\pi}{k^2}\,\bigl|\hat g(\mathbf k)\bigr|^2, \qquad \hat g(\mathbf k) = \int g(\mathbf r)\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\,d^3r ,
> $$
>
> since $\int\!\!\int g(\mathbf r_1)\overline{g(\mathbf r_2)}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2)} = \hat g(\mathbf k)\overline{\hat g(\mathbf k)}$. The integrand is nonnegative, and positive somewhere because $g \ne 0$. So $K > 0$, and $\psi_+$ has the larger repulsion.

^der-b7-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B7.2 Atoms#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|WO Def. §B4.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why the triplet is lower, and how good the estimate is
> - The physical reason is the exchange force ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-6|Theorem §B7.1.6]]): the antisymmetric spatial state vanishes at $\mathbf r_1 = \mathbf r_2$ and keeps the electrons apart, so they repel less. The spins enter only through the symmetry they impose; there is no magnetic spin–spin force in Model §B7.2.1. The same mechanism is Hund's first rule ([[§B7.2 Atoms#^law-b7-2-6|Law §B7.2.6]]) and, in solids, the origin of ferromagnetism (level C).
> - Adding $J \pm K$ to the unperturbed energy is first-order perturbation theory ([[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory]]); $\psi_\pm$ are the right combinations to use because the repulsion does not mix them, the "good states" of [[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory]]. Since the repulsion is not small, the numbers are only qualitative (Greensite).
> - If electrons were bosons or distinguishable particles the level scheme would change completely: no ortho/para distinction for distinguishable electrons, and no triplet with a symmetric space part for bosons (Griffiths Problem 5.14).

^rem-b7-2-2

> [!example] Example §B7.2.1: Reading the Helium Spectrum
> Griffiths' Figure 5.1 gives the levels of helium measured from the ground state of $\mathrm{He}^+$ ($-54.4\ \mathrm{eV}$). For the $1s2s$ configuration, parahelium ($2\,{}^1S$) lies at about $-4.0\ \mathrm{eV}$ and orthohelium ($2\,{}^3S$) at about $-4.8\ \mathrm{eV}$; for $1s2p$ the two are about $-3.4$ and $-3.6\ \mathrm{eV}$ (read off the figure). What do these numbers say about $J$ and $K$?
>
> *Exchange.* By Theorem §B7.2.4 the splitting is $2K$: $K \approx 0.4\ \mathrm{eV}$ for $1s2s$ and $K \approx 0.1\ \mathrm{eV}$ for $1s2p$. The 2p orbital, with its node at the nucleus, overlaps the compact 1s orbital less than 2s does, and $g = \psi_{100}^*\psi_{n\ell m}$ is correspondingly smaller.
>
> *Direct.* The independent-electron model puts $1s2s$ at $4(E_1 + E_2) = -68.0\ \mathrm{eV}$, that is, $4E_2 = -13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$ relative to $\mathrm{He}^+$. The mean of the two measured levels is about $-4.4\ \mathrm{eV}$, so $J \approx 9\ \mathrm{eV}$: the outer electron is held by about $4\ \mathrm{eV}$ instead of $13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$, close to the $3.4\ \mathrm{eV}$ of a 2s electron bound to a single proton. The inner electron screens one unit of the nuclear charge, the effect behind [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^def-a5-3-3|Def. §A5.3.3]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.2.1, Figure 5.1; Greensite §15.2*

## Many-electron atoms

> [!definition] Definition §B7.2.1: Configuration; Term Symbol
> The **configuration** of an atom lists the occupied one-electron orbitals $(n, \ell)$, with the number of electrons in each as an exponent, $\ell$ written as a letter (s, p, d, f, then g, h, …): carbon's ground configuration is $(1s)^2(2s)^2(2p)^2$ ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^def-a5-3-2|Def. §A5.3.2]]). The atom as a whole has a total orbital angular momentum $L$, total spin $S$ and grand total $J$ (capitals for totals), recorded in the **term symbol**
>
> $$
> {}^{2S+1}L_J ,
> $$
>
> with $L$ written as a capital letter S, P, D, F, …; carbon's ground state is ${}^3P_0$ ($S = 1$, $L = 1$, $J = 0$). For one electron this is the notation of [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^def-a5-2-4|Def. §A5.2.4]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.2.2, eqs. (5.44)–(5.45); Greensite §15.3, eqs. (15.67), (15.72)–(15.74)*

^def-b7-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.5: Closed Subshells Have Zero Angular Momentum
> A completely filled subshell $(n\ell)^{2(2\ell+1)}$ admits exactly one antisymmetric state, and it has $L = S = J = 0$. Hence closed subshells contribute nothing to the term of an atom, which is determined by the electrons in open subshells alone: atoms with only closed subshells (He, Be, Ne, Mg, Ar, Ca, …) have ground term ${}^1S_0$, and atoms with a single electron outside closed subshells (Li, Na, K, …) have ${}^2S_{1/2}$.
>
> *Source: Greensite §15.3, p. 252; Griffiths Table 5.1*

^thm-b7-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The subshell has $2(2\ell+1)$ spin-orbitals ($m = -\ell, \dots, \ell$, each with $m_s = \pm\tfrac12$), and $2(2\ell+1)$ electrons must occupy distinct ones ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]]): there is a single Slater determinant, a one-dimensional space of states. The total $\mathbf L$ and $\mathbf S$ act on each electron's orbital and spin without changing $n$ and $\ell$, so they map this space into itself; it is therefore spanned by a common eigenstate of $L^2$, $L_z$, $S^2$, $S_z$. A multiplet with $L > 0$ would contain $2L + 1 > 1$ states, so $L = 0$; likewise $S = 0$, and then $J = 0$. (Directly: $M_L = \sum m = 0$ and $M_S = \sum m_s = 0$.) With closed subshells the remaining electrons' $L$, $S$ add to zero ([[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]] with $j_2 = 0$); one s electron gives $L = 0$, $S = \tfrac12$, $J = \tfrac12$.

^der-b7-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]], [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]

> [!law] Law §B7.2.6: Hund's Rules
> For the ground state of an atom, consistent with the Pauli principle:
> 1. the term with the largest total spin $S$ lies lowest;
> 2. for that $S$, the term with the largest total orbital angular momentum $L$ allowed by overall antisymmetry lies lowest;
> 3. if the open subshell $(n, \ell)$ is no more than half filled, the lowest level has $J = |L - S|$; if it is more than half filled, $J = L + S$.
>
> *Valid when: ground terms of atoms with one open subshell in which the electrostatic interaction dominates spin–orbit coupling (the light atoms; Griffiths' Table 5.1 runs to krypton, beyond which fine structure starts to reorder states). Greensite calls the rules "folklore" that lets one guess the term "sometimes".*
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 5.18(a)–(c), footnote 20; Greensite §15.3, p. 251–252*

^law-b7-2-6

> [!remark] Remark: Where the rules come from
> Rule 1 is the exchange effect of Theorem §B7.2.4: a symmetric spin state forces an antisymmetric spatial state, the electrons keep apart and repel less (Greensite). Rule 3 orders the levels of one term by the spin–orbit energy, which is proportional to $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$ and hence to $\tfrac12[J(J+1) - L(L+1) - S(S+1)]$ ([[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-5|Theorem §B6.3.5]]); why its sign reverses beyond half filling is not derived in the sources. All three are empirical rules here, not theorems.

^rem-b7-2-3

> [!example] Example §B7.2.2: Ground Terms of Carbon, Nitrogen and Oxygen
> *Carbon,* $(1s)^2(2s)^2(2p)^2$. The closed subshells contribute nothing (Theorem §B7.2.5). Two $\ell = 1$ electrons can have $L = 2, 1, 0$ and two spins $S = 1, 0$ ([[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]]). Both electrons have the same radial function, so the symmetry of the spatial state is that of the coupled angular state. From the $1\times1$ Clebsch–Gordan table: $|2\,2\rangle = |1\,1\rangle|1\,1\rangle$ is symmetric, and $J_-$, being symmetric in the two particles, keeps the whole $L = 2$ multiplet symmetric; $|1\,1\rangle = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(|1\,1\rangle|1\,0\rangle - |1\,0\rangle|1\,1\rangle\bigr)$ is antisymmetric, and $|0\,0\rangle = \tfrac1{\sqrt3}\bigl(|1\,1\rangle|1\,{-1}\rangle - |1\,0\rangle|1\,0\rangle + |1\,{-1}\rangle|1\,1\rangle\bigr)$ symmetric. By [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-7|Theorem §B7.1.7]] the triplet ($S = 1$) needs the antisymmetric $L = 1$, and the singlet the symmetric $L = 2$ or $0$: the configuration gives the terms ${}^3P$, ${}^1D$, ${}^1S$, with $9 + 5 + 1 = 15 = \binom62$ states, the number of ways to put two electrons into six spin-orbitals. Rule 1 picks ${}^3P$ (there is no ${}^3D$: Griffiths' question "why doesn't carbon have $L = 2$?"); the subshell is a third full, so rule 3 gives $J = |1 - 1| = 0$: **${}^3P_0$**.
>
> *A quicker route*, the "top of the ladder" (Griffiths' hint): among states with the largest $M_S$, find the largest $M_L$ the exclusion principle allows; that is the $S$ and the $L$ of the lowest term. (Every state with the maximal $M_S$ belongs to a term with the maximal $S$, and each such term contributes $M_L$ from $-L$ to $L$.)
>
> *Nitrogen,* $(2p)^3$: the largest $M_S$ is $\tfrac32$, all spins up, which forces the three different orbitals $m = 1, 0, -1$, so $M_L = 0$ is the only value: $S = \tfrac32$, $L = 0$, and $J = \tfrac32$: **${}^4S_{3/2}$**.
>
> *Oxygen,* $(2p)^4$: at most three spins up, so the largest $M_S$ is $1$ (up in $m = 1, 0, -1$, down in one orbital); the largest $M_L$ puts the down electron in $m = 1$, $M_L = 1$. So $S = 1$, $L = 1$, and the subshell is more than half full: $J = L + S = 2$, **${}^3P_2$**. All three agree with Griffiths' Table 5.1.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 5.17–5.18, Table 5.1, Table 4.8; Greensite §15.3, p. 252*

^ex-b7-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: What level B adds to the periodic table
> Level A explained the rows of the periodic table from shell capacities and the empirical filling order, with shielding and penetration making low $\ell$ lie lower ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^rem-a5-3-2|Remark: shielding and penetration]], [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^rem-a5-3-3|Remark: The periodic table from shells and subshells]]); Griffiths gives the same account. If the hydrogen degeneracy in $\ell$ survived, the rows would have lengths $2, 8, 18, 32, \dots$ instead of $2, 8, 8, 18, 18, \dots$; screening by the inner electrons is what reorders them. Level B adds the *terms*: each configuration of Table 5.1 comes with a ground term ${}^{2S+1}L_J$ fixed by antisymmetry (Theorem §B7.2.5, Example §B7.2.2) and Hund's rules, which is what spectroscopy measures. Chromium $(4s)(3d)^5$ (${}^7S_3$) and copper $(4s)(3d)^{10}$ (${}^2S_{1/2}$) are the familiar exceptions to the filling order.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.2.2, Table 5.1*

^rem-b7-2-4

> [!example]- ★ Example §B7.2.3: The Ground State of Lithium
> Ignoring the repulsion, lithium ($Z = 3$) puts two electrons in $\psi_{100}$ and the third in $\psi_{200}$ (repulsion favours 2s over 2p, Griffiths footnote 16), with hydrogen-like orbitals of radius $a/3$ and energy $E = 9\,(2E_1 + E_2) = 9\,(-27.2 - 3.4)\ \mathrm{eV} = -275\ \mathrm{eV}$. The state cannot be a product of a spatial and a spin factor ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^rem-b7-1-5|★ Remark: Three spins one-half]]); it is the Slater determinant ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^def-b7-1-3|Def. §B7.1.3]]) of the spin-orbitals $\psi_{100}\chi_+$, $\psi_{100}\chi_-$, $\psi_{200}\chi_+$, or the same with $\psi_{200}\chi_-$. Expanding the determinant along the $\psi_{200}$ column gives Griffiths' form: the $1s^2$ pair in its spatially symmetric singlet, times the third electron, antisymmetrised over which electron is the odd one out. The ground state is doubly degenerate, ${}^2S_{1/2}$ (Theorem §B7.2.5).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 5.16, 5.10*

^ex-b7-2-3

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The average potential of the independent-electron model
> The "spherically symmetric average potential" of [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^mod-a5-3-3|Model §A5.3.3]] can be made concrete (Greensite §15.3): each electron moves in the field of the nucleus plus the charge density of all the others, averaged over angles; the orbitals determine the potential and the potential the orbitals, and the two are iterated to self-consistency (the **Hartree approximation**). Because the potential is no longer $\propto 1/r$, the energies depend on $\ell$, which produces the filling order. Hartree's product wave function is not antisymmetrised and so misses the exchange term $K$ of Theorem §B7.2.4; using a Slater determinant instead (Hartree–Fock) is level C.
