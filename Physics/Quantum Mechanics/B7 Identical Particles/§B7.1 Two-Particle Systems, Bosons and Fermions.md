Level A stated that the wave function of two identical particles is symmetric or antisymmetric ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^thm-a5-3-1|Theorem §A5.3.1]]) and postulated the exclusion principle for electrons ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]]). This section sets up the quantum mechanics of several particles: the two-particle wave function, the exchange operator, and the symmetrization requirement as a principle for any number of identical particles, with or without interactions. The exclusion principle then becomes a theorem, and its many-particle form is the Slater determinant. Two consequences are computed: the "exchange force", by which identical bosons huddle together and identical fermions keep apart, and the rule that couples the spin state of two electrons to the symmetry of their spatial wave function.

## Two particles

> [!definition] Definition §B7.1.1: Two-Particle Wave Function; Entangled State
> The state of two particles (ignoring spin) is a wave function $\Psi(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2, t)$ of both positions, with
>
> $$
> i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \hat H\Psi, \qquad \hat H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_1}\nabla_1^2 - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m_2}\nabla_2^2 + V(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2, t) ,
> $$
>
> where $\nabla_i$ differentiates with respect to $\mathbf r_i$. $|\Psi(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2, t)|^2\,d^3r_1\,d^3r_2$ is the probability of finding particle 1 in $d^3r_1$ *and* particle 2 in $d^3r_2$, and $\int|\Psi|^2\,d^3r_1\,d^3r_2 = 1$. For a time-independent $V$, separation of variables gives stationary states $\Psi = \psi(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2)\,e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ with $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_1}\nabla_1^2\psi - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m_2}\nabla_2^2\psi + V\psi = E\psi$. A two-particle state is **entangled** if it cannot be written as a product $\psi_a(\mathbf r_1)\psi_b(\mathbf r_2)$ of one-particle states. With spin, each $\mathbf r_i$ is accompanied by a spin variable, and the state space is the tensor product of the two one-particle spaces ([[§38 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-71|LADR Def. 9.71]]; [[§B6.1 Spin One-Half and the Pauli Matrices#^def-b6-1-3|Def. §B6.1.3]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1, eqs. (5.1)–(5.7), and footnote 1*

^def-b7-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.1: Non-Interacting Particles
> If each particle feels only an external potential, $V(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2) = V_1(\mathbf r_1) + V_2(\mathbf r_2)$, the time-independent Schrödinger equation separates: the products
>
> $$
> \psi(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2) = \psi_a(\mathbf r_1)\,\psi_b(\mathbf r_2), \qquad E = E_a + E_b ,
> $$
>
> are stationary states, where $\psi_a$ solves the one-particle equation for particle 1 in $V_1$ with energy $E_a$, and $\psi_b$ that for particle 2 in $V_2$ with $E_b$. Superpositions of such products, entangled ones included, solve the time-dependent equation. (If instead the particles interact only through $V(|\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2|)$, the problem reduces to free centre-of-mass motion and one relative particle of reduced mass, as in hydrogen: Griffiths Problem 5.1, [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom]].)
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1, eqs. (5.8)–(5.13)*

^thm-b7-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert $\psi = \psi_a(\mathbf r_1)\psi_b(\mathbf r_2)$ and divide by $\psi$:
>
> $$
> \Bigl[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_1}\frac{\nabla_1^2\psi_a}{\psi_a} + V_1(\mathbf r_1)\Bigr] + \Bigl[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_2}\frac{\nabla_2^2\psi_b}{\psi_b} + V_2(\mathbf r_2)\Bigr] = E .
> $$
>
> The first bracket depends only on $\mathbf r_1$, the second only on $\mathbf r_2$; their sum is constant only if each is a constant, $E_a$ and $E_b$ with $E_a + E_b = E$, which are the one-particle equations. Superpositions solve the time-dependent equation by linearity ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-2|Theorem §A4.1.2]]).

## Identical particles

> [!definition] Definition §B7.1.2: Exchange Operator
> The **exchange operator** $\hat P$ interchanges the two particles: it swaps their positions, their spins and any other labels,
>
> $$
> \hat P\,|(1, 2)\rangle = |(2, 1)\rangle, \qquad \text{for wave functions}\quad (\hat P\psi)(\mathbf r_1\sigma_1, \mathbf r_2\sigma_2) = \psi(\mathbf r_2\sigma_2, \mathbf r_1\sigma_1) .
> $$
>
> A state is **symmetric** if $\hat P\psi = \psi$ and **antisymmetric** if $\hat P\psi = -\psi$. For $N$ particles, $\hat P_{ij}$ swaps particles $i$ and $j$. Particles are **identical** if no measurement can tell them apart ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^def-a5-3-1|Def. §A5.3.1]]): then $m_1 = m_2$ and $V(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2, t) = V(\mathbf r_2, \mathbf r_1, t)$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.4, eq. (5.30) and footnote 11; Greensite ch. 15, eq. (15.20)*

^def-b7-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.2: Exchange Symmetry Is Conserved
> $\hat P^2 = 1$, so the eigenvalues of $\hat P$ are $\pm1$; $\hat P$ is Hermitian and unitary. For identical particles $[\hat P, \hat H] = 0$, so $d\langle\hat P\rangle/dt = 0$: a state that starts symmetric (antisymmetric) stays symmetric (antisymmetric) for all time, and the energy eigenstates can be chosen symmetric or antisymmetric.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.4, eqs. (5.31)–(5.32)*

^thm-b7-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Swapping twice restores every wave function, so $\hat P^2 = 1$; if $\hat P\psi = \lambda\psi$ with $\psi \ne 0$ then $\psi = \lambda^2\psi$ and $\lambda = \pm1$. $\hat P$ preserves the inner product (rename the integration variables), so it is unitary, and with $\hat P^{-1} = \hat P$ also Hermitian. For identical particles, $(\hat H\hat P\psi)(1, 2) = (\hat H\psi)(2, 1)$ because the kinetic terms have equal masses and $V$ is symmetric; so $\hat H\hat P = \hat P\hat H$. Then $d\langle\hat P\rangle/dt = \frac i\hbar\langle[\hat H, \hat P]\rangle = 0$ ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]]); more directly, $\hat P$ commutes with the evolution, so $\hat P\Psi(t)$ and $\pm\Psi(t)$ solve the same equation from the same initial state. Commuting with $\hat H$, $\hat P$ maps each energy eigenspace into itself, where its eigenvectors span ([[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-76|LADR Thm. 5.76]] for finite-dimensional eigenspaces).

^der-b7-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]], [[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-76|LADR Thm. 5.76]]

> [!principle] Principle §B7.1.3: Symmetrization Requirement
> The state of a system of identical particles is not merely allowed but required to be symmetric or antisymmetric under the interchange of any two of them:
>
> $$
> |(1, 2, \dots, i, \dots, j, \dots, n)\rangle = \pm\,|(1, 2, \dots, j, \dots, i, \dots, n)\rangle ,
> $$
>
> with the plus sign for **bosons** and the minus sign for **fermions**. Which sign a species takes is fixed by its spin (**spin–statistics connection**): particles of integer spin are bosons, particles of half-integer spin are fermions. For two non-interacting identical particles in one-particle states $\psi_a$ and $\psi_b$, the allowed states are therefore
>
> $$
> \psi_\pm(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2) = A\bigl[\psi_a(\mathbf r_1)\psi_b(\mathbf r_2) \pm \psi_b(\mathbf r_1)\psi_a(\mathbf r_2)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Domain: all identical particles in three dimensions; a composite particle (an atom, a nucleus) behaves as a boson if it contains an even number of fermions and as a fermion if odd ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^rem-b10-1-2|TH Remark: Which particles are which]]). In nonrelativistic quantum mechanics the spin–statistics connection is an axiom; it is proved in relativistic quantum field theory ([[Quantum Field Theory]], for free fields, [[§C5b.1 Canonical Quantization of the Dirac Field#^pr-c5b-1-7|QFT Principle §C5b.1.7]], the general proof still to be filled; level C gives only a heuristic reason, [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^rem-c12-1-3|Remark: Why the sign follows the spin]]).*
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.1, eqs. (5.17)–(5.18), §5.1.4, eqs. (5.33)–(5.34), footnotes 3, 12; Greensite ch. 15, eqs. (15.18)–(15.22)*

^pr-b7-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: How the level-A statements change layer
> - Level A *derived* the alternative "symmetric or antisymmetric" from the requirement that $|\psi|^2$ not change under exchange ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^der-a5-3-1|Derivation of Theorem §A5.3.1]], marked "idea"). That argument shows what is possible ($\hat P$ has eigenvalues $\pm1$, Theorem §B7.1.2), not what is required: a symmetric Hamiltonian alone does not force a symmetric or antisymmetric state, as two distinguishable particles with a symmetric Hamiltonian (an electron and a positron) show. The requirement is a new law, on a par with the Schrödinger equation (Griffiths footnote 12), so it appears here as a principle; Greensite reaches it from the hypothesis that interchanging identical particles does not change the physical state.
> - [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]] splits in two. Its first sentence, antisymmetry for electrons, is the fermion case of Principle §B7.1.3. Its second sentence, no two electrons in the same state, is now a theorem (Theorem §B7.1.4) and holds for every species of fermion. "It is not a weird ad hoc assumption applying only to electrons" (Griffiths).

^rem-b7-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.4: Pauli Exclusion Principle
> Two identical fermions cannot occupy the same one-particle state (including spin): for $\psi_a = \psi_b$ the antisymmetric combination vanishes,
>
> $$
> \psi_-(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2) = A\bigl[\psi_a(\mathbf r_1)\psi_a(\mathbf r_2) - \psi_a(\mathbf r_1)\psi_a(\mathbf r_2)\bigr] = 0 .
> $$
>
> For orthonormal $\psi_a \ne \psi_b$ the normalisation is $A = 1/\sqrt2$ for both signs; for bosons in the same state, $\psi_+ = \psi_a(\mathbf r_1)\psi_a(\mathbf r_2)$ is already normalised. More generally, no totally antisymmetric state of $N$ fermions has two fermions in the same one-particle state. This is the second half of level A's [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]], now a theorem derived from Principle §B7.1.3 (layer change: [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^rem-b7-1-1|Remark: How the level-A statements change layer]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.1, p. 256, Problem 5.4; Greensite ch. 15, eqs. (15.37)–(15.39)*

^thm-b7-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The vanishing is immediate from Principle §B7.1.3. *Normalisation:* $\|\psi_a\otimes\psi_b \pm \psi_b\otimes\psi_a\|^2 = \|\psi_a\|^2\|\psi_b\|^2 + \|\psi_b\|^2\|\psi_a\|^2 \pm 2\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\langle\psi_a|\psi_b\rangle\langle\psi_b|\psi_a\rangle\bigr) = 2$ for orthonormal states (the inner product of products is the product of inner products, [[§38 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-80|LADR Thm. 9.80]]), so $A = 1/\sqrt2$. For $\psi_a = \psi_b$ the boson sum is $2\psi_a\otimes\psi_a$ and $A = \tfrac12$. For $N$ particles, an antisymmetric state is an alternating function of the one-particle labels; if two of its arguments carry the same state, swapping them changes nothing and also changes the sign, so the state is zero ([[§36 Alternating Multilinear Forms#^ladr-9-30|LADR Thm. 9.30]]); the Slater determinant below makes this explicit.

## Many fermions: the Slater determinant

> [!definition] Definition §B7.1.3: Slater Determinant; Permanent
> Let $\psi_{a_1}, \dots, \psi_{a_N}$ be orthonormal one-particle states (**spin-orbitals**: functions of $x = (\mathbf r, \sigma)$, position and spin variable). The **Slater determinant** is the $N$-particle wave function
>
> $$
> \Psi(x_1, \dots, x_N) = \frac1{\sqrt{N!}}\,\det\begin{pmatrix}
> \psi_{a_1}(x_1) & \psi_{a_2}(x_1) & \cdots & \psi_{a_N}(x_1) \\
> \psi_{a_1}(x_2) & \psi_{a_2}(x_2) & \cdots & \psi_{a_N}(x_2) \\
> \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\
> \psi_{a_1}(x_N) & \psi_{a_2}(x_N) & \cdots & \psi_{a_N}(x_N)
> \end{pmatrix}
> = \frac1{\sqrt{N!}}\sum_{\pi}\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\,\psi_{a_1}(x_{\pi(1)})\cdots\psi_{a_N}(x_{\pi(N)}),
> $$
>
> the sum running over all permutations $\pi$ of $1, \dots, N$ ([[§36 Alternating Multilinear Forms#^ladr-9-32|LADR Def. 9.32]]). The **permanent** is the same sum without the signs; it gives totally symmetric (boson) states.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 5.8 and footnote 6; Problem 5.10(b)*

^def-b7-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.5: Properties of the Slater Determinant
> 1. $\Psi$ is antisymmetric under the exchange of any two particles $x_i \leftrightarrow x_j$.
> 2. $\Psi = 0$ if two of the spin-orbitals coincide (or, more generally, are linearly dependent): the exclusion principle for $N$ fermions.
> 3. $\Psi$ is normalised when the spin-orbitals are orthonormal.
> 4. If the fermions do not interact, $\hat H = \sum_{i=1}^N\hat h(x_i)$, and $\hat h\psi_{a_k} = \varepsilon_{a_k}\psi_{a_k}$, then $\hat H\Psi = (\varepsilon_{a_1} + \cdots + \varepsilon_{a_N})\Psi$. The ground state of $N$ non-interacting fermions is the Slater determinant of the $N$ lowest spin-orbitals.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 5.8, footnote 6, Problem 5.12; Greensite ch. 15, eqs. (15.37)–(15.39)*

^thm-b7-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1, 2.* The determinant is an alternating multilinear function of its rows ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-45|LADR Thm. 9.45]], applied to the transpose, [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-56|LADR Thm. 9.56]]): swapping rows $i$ and $j$, which is $x_i \leftrightarrow x_j$, reverses its sign ([[§36 Alternating Multilinear Forms#^ladr-9-30|LADR Thm. 9.30]]). As a function of its columns it is also alternating, so it vanishes when two columns (two spin-orbitals) are equal or, more generally, linearly dependent ([[§36 Alternating Multilinear Forms#^ladr-9-28|LADR Thm. 9.28]]).
>
> *3.* With the permutation sum (Leibniz formula, [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-46|LADR Thm. 9.46]]),
>
> $$
> \langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle = \frac1{N!}\sum_{\pi, \rho}\operatorname{sgn}\pi\,\operatorname{sgn}\rho\int\prod_{k=1}^N\overline{\psi_{a_k}(x_{\pi(k)})}\,\psi_{a_k}(x_{\rho(k)})\;dx_1\cdots dx_N
> = \frac1{N!}\sum_{\pi, \rho}\operatorname{sgn}\pi\,\operatorname{sgn}\rho\prod_{j=1}^N\bigl\langle\psi_{a_{\pi^{-1}(j)}}\big|\psi_{a_{\rho^{-1}(j)}}\bigr\rangle ,
> $$
>
> because the variable $x_j$ (integrated, with its spin summed) appears in the orbital $\psi_{a_{\pi^{-1}(j)}}$ on the left and $\psi_{a_{\rho^{-1}(j)}}$ on the right. By orthonormality the product is $1$ if $\pi = \rho$ and $0$ otherwise, and $(\operatorname{sgn}\pi)^2 = 1$; the $N!$ surviving terms give $1$.
>
> *4.* Each product $\psi_{a_1}(x_{\pi(1)})\cdots\psi_{a_N}(x_{\pi(N)})$ is an eigenfunction of $\sum_i\hat h(x_i)$ with eigenvalue $\sum_k\varepsilon_{a_k}$, by the separation argument of [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]] for $N$ particles; a linear combination of eigenfunctions with one eigenvalue is again one. By part 2 the spin-orbitals must be distinct, and the energy is least when they are the $N$ lowest.

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^def-b7-1-3|Def. §B7.1.3]], [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-45|LADR Thm. 9.45]], [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-46|LADR Thm. 9.46]], [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-56|LADR Thm. 9.56]], [[§36 Alternating Multilinear Forms#^ladr-9-28|LADR Thm. 9.28]], [[§36 Alternating Multilinear Forms#^ladr-9-30|LADR Thm. 9.30]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What a Slater determinant does and does not describe
> For $N = 2$ the Slater determinant is $\psi_-$ of Principle §B7.1.3. A single Slater determinant is the exact ground state only for non-interacting fermions; for interacting ones (electrons repelling each other) the true state is a sum of many Slater determinants, and "which orbitals are occupied" stops being a meaningful question (Griffiths Problem 5.11, the Laughlin wave function). The occupation-number description of quantum gases, one state per set of occupation numbers $\{n_i\}$ with $n_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for fermions, counts exactly these determinants ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^def-b10-1-1|TH Def. §B10.1.1]]).

## Exchange forces

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.6: Exchange Force
> Let one particle be in $\psi_a(x)$ and the other in $\psi_b(x)$, orthonormal states in one dimension. For distinguishable particles ($\psi = \psi_a(x_1)\psi_b(x_2)$),
>
> $$
> \bigl\langle(x_1 - x_2)^2\bigr\rangle_d = \langle x^2\rangle_a + \langle x^2\rangle_b - 2\langle x\rangle_a\langle x\rangle_b ;
> $$
>
> for identical bosons ($+$) and fermions ($-$), in the states $\psi_\pm$ with $A = 1/\sqrt2$,
>
> $$
> \bigl\langle(x_1 - x_2)^2\bigr\rangle_\pm = \bigl\langle(x_1 - x_2)^2\bigr\rangle_d \mp 2\,\bigl|\langle x\rangle_{ab}\bigr|^2, \qquad \langle x\rangle_{ab} = \int x\,\psi_a(x)^*\psi_b(x)\,dx .
> $$
>
> Identical bosons are on average closer together, identical fermions farther apart, than distinguishable particles in the same two states. The correction vanishes unless the two wave functions overlap.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.2, eqs. (5.19)–(5.26)*

^thm-b7-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Expand $\langle(x_1 - x_2)^2\rangle = \langle x_1^2\rangle + \langle x_2^2\rangle - 2\langle x_1x_2\rangle$. *Distinguishable:* the integrals factorise, $\langle x_1^2\rangle = \int x_1^2|\psi_a(x_1)|^2dx_1\int|\psi_b(x_2)|^2dx_2 = \langle x^2\rangle_a$, $\langle x_2^2\rangle = \langle x^2\rangle_b$, $\langle x_1x_2\rangle = \langle x\rangle_a\langle x\rangle_b$.
>
> *Identical:* $|\psi_\pm|^2 = \tfrac12\bigl[|\psi_a(x_1)|^2|\psi_b(x_2)|^2 + |\psi_b(x_1)|^2|\psi_a(x_2)|^2 \pm \psi_a(x_1)^{\ast}\psi_b(x_1)\,\psi_b(x_2)^{\ast}\psi_a(x_2) \pm \text{c.c.}\bigr]$. In $\langle x_1^2\rangle$ the cross terms contain $\int\psi_b(x_2)^{\ast}\psi_a(x_2)\,dx_2 = 0$, so $\langle x_1^2\rangle = \tfrac12(\langle x^2\rangle_a + \langle x^2\rangle_b) = \langle x_2^2\rangle$ (naturally, since the particles cannot be told apart). In $\langle x_1x_2\rangle$ they survive:
>
> $$
> \langle x_1x_2\rangle = \tfrac12\bigl(\langle x\rangle_a\langle x\rangle_b + \langle x\rangle_b\langle x\rangle_a \pm \langle x\rangle_{ab}\langle x\rangle_{ba} \pm \langle x\rangle_{ba}\langle x\rangle_{ab}\bigr) = \langle x\rangle_a\langle x\rangle_b \pm |\langle x\rangle_{ab}|^2 ,
> $$
>
> using $\langle x\rangle_{ba} = \overline{\langle x\rangle_{ab}}$. Adding up gives the result. If $\psi_a\psi_b = 0$ everywhere, $\langle x\rangle_{ab} = 0$.

^der-b7-1-6

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^pr-b7-1-3|Principle §B7.1.3]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Not a force, and why chemistry is possible
> No agency pushes the particles: the "exchange force" is a purely geometrical consequence of the symmetrization requirement, with no classical counterpart (Griffiths). Because it needs overlapping wave functions, an electron in an atom in Chicago and one in Seattle can be treated as distinguishable without error; in principle every electron is antisymmetrized with every other, and only the smallness of overlaps lets chemists talk about one atom at a time. Where wave functions do overlap, the effect is large: it keeps closed electron shells apart ([[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^rem-a6-1-1|Remark in §A6.1]]), makes helium's triplet states lie below its singlets ([[§B7.2 Atoms#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]]), and supplies the degeneracy pressure of a metal ([[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^rem-b7-3-1|Remark: What is quantum mechanics and what is statistical mechanics]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.2, pp. 260–261, footnote 5*

## Spin and the symmetry of the spatial state

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.7: Spin and Space for Two Electrons
> For two identical spin-$\tfrac12$ fermions whose state is a product of a spatial and a spin part, $\psi(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2)\,\chi(1, 2)$, the whole must be antisymmetric:
>
> $$
> \psi(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2)\,\chi(1, 2) = -\psi(\mathbf r_2, \mathbf r_1)\,\chi(2, 1) .
> $$
>
> So a **symmetric** spatial state goes with the antisymmetric **singlet**, and an **antisymmetric** spatial state with one of the three symmetric **triplet** states ([[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-4|Theorem §B6.3.4]]). In particular two electrons can share one spatial orbital, provided their spins are in the singlet; they cannot share both the orbital and the spin state. If $\hat H$ does not involve spin, its eigenstates can be chosen in this product form, with the spatial state an eigenstate of the spatial exchange.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.1.3, eqs. (5.27)–(5.29), footnotes 7–8; Greensite §15.1, eqs. (15.29)–(15.34)*

^thm-b7-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The exchange operator of Def. §B7.1.2 swaps positions and spins at once, so it acts on a product as $\hat P(\psi\chi) = (\hat P_{\text{space}}\psi)(\hat P_{\text{spin}}\chi)$, and Principle §B7.1.3 requires $-1$ for fermions. Both factors can be taken to be eigenvectors of their own exchange: the spin states of two spins $\tfrac12$ decompose into the triplet ($+1$) and the singlet ($-1$) by Theorem §B6.3.4, and a spatial wave function splits as $\psi = \tfrac12(\psi + \hat P_{\text{space}}\psi) + \tfrac12(\psi - \hat P_{\text{space}}\psi)$. The product of the two eigenvalues must be $-1$, which pairs them as stated. For a spin-independent $\hat H$ symmetric in the particles, $\hat H$ commutes with $\hat P_{\text{space}}$ and with the total spin, so their common eigenstates exist ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]]). A spatial state $\psi_a(\mathbf r_1)\psi_a(\mathbf r_2)$ is symmetric and so needs the singlet; with both spins up the spin state is the symmetric $|1\,1\rangle$, which requires an antisymmetric spatial state, and that vanishes for one orbital.

^der-b7-1-7

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^pr-b7-1-3|Principle §B7.1.3]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]], [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-4|Theorem §B6.3.4]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: "Spinless fermions"
> When spin is ignored, as in Theorem §B7.1.6, "identical fermions" means fermions in the same spin state (both spin up, say), whose spin part is symmetric, so the spatial part must be antisymmetric. A truly spinless fermion is a contradiction (Griffiths footnote 4). With spin included, two electrons can be in the spatially *symmetric* state, and then they huddle like bosons.

^cau-b7-1-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B7.1.1: Two Particles in an Oscillator
> Two non-interacting particles of mass $m$ share the potential $\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$. Find the ground state, with its energy, if they are (a) identical bosons, (b) identical fermions in the same spin state, (c) spin-$\tfrac12$ fermions; show that the wave functions satisfy the symmetrization requirement and comment on the role of spin.
>
> The one-particle levels are $E_n = (n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega$ ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]; derived in [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]), with $\psi_0 = \bigl(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\bigr)^{1/4}e^{-\xi^2/2}$ and $\psi_1 = \bigl(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\bigr)^{1/4}\sqrt2\,\xi\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$, $\xi = \sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,x$ ([[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials]]). By [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]] products of one-particle states are stationary with $E = E_n + E_{n'}$.
>
> *(a) Bosons.* Both can occupy $n = 0$:
>
> $$
> \psi(x_1, x_2) = \psi_0(x_1)\psi_0(x_2) = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}}\,e^{-(\xi_1^2 + \xi_2^2)/2}, \qquad E = \hbar\omega ,
> $$
>
> symmetric because $\xi_1^2 + \xi_2^2$ is.
>
> *(b) Fermions in one spin state.* Both in $n = 0$ is forbidden (Theorem §B7.1.4); the lowest antisymmetric state uses $n = 0$ and $n = 1$:
>
> $$
> \psi(x_1, x_2) = \frac1{\sqrt2}\bigl[\psi_0(x_1)\psi_1(x_2) - \psi_1(x_1)\psi_0(x_2)\bigr] = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}}\,(\xi_2 - \xi_1)\,e^{-(\xi_1^2 + \xi_2^2)/2}, \qquad E = \tfrac12\hbar\omega + \tfrac32\hbar\omega = 2\hbar\omega .
> $$
>
> Swapping $x_1 \leftrightarrow x_2$ reverses $\xi_2 - \xi_1$ and leaves the Gaussian unchanged: antisymmetric. It vanishes at $x_1 = x_2$.
>
> *(c) Spin-$\tfrac12$ fermions.* By [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-7|Theorem §B7.1.7]] the symmetric spatial state of (a) is allowed when paired with the antisymmetric singlet:
>
> $$
> \Psi = \psi_0(x_1)\psi_0(x_2)\;\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle - |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle\bigr), \qquad E = \hbar\omega ,
> $$
>
> nondegenerate. Exchanging the particles (positions and spins) multiplies it by $(+1)(-1) = -1$.
>
> *Role of spin.* The spin degree of freedom gives a second "slot" in each oscillator level. The antisymmetry demanded of fermions is carried by the spin singlet, so the spatial state is free to be the symmetric boson ground state, and the ground energy drops from $2\hbar\omega$ to $\hbar\omega$. At the next level, $E = 2\hbar\omega$, spin-$\tfrac12$ fermions have four states: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\psi_0\psi_1 + \psi_1\psi_0)$ with the singlet, and $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\psi_0\psi_1 - \psi_1\psi_0)$ with each of the three triplet states. For many fermions each level holds two, which is the origin of the shell filling in atoms ([[§B7.2 Atoms]]) and of the Fermi sea ([[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^thm-b7-3-3|Theorem §B7.3.3]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 6, Problem 6.6 (solved here); Griffiths Example 5.1 does the same for the infinite well*

> [!example] Example §B7.1.2: How Far Apart Are Two Particles in an Oscillator?
> One particle is in $\psi_0$, the other in $\psi_1$ of the oscillator. With $\langle x^2\rangle_n = (n + \tfrac12)\hbar/m\omega$, $\langle x\rangle_n = 0$ and $\langle x\rangle_{01} = \langle 0|x|1\rangle = \sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}$ ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]), [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-6|Theorem §B7.1.6]] gives
>
> $$
> \bigl\langle(x_1 - x_2)^2\bigr\rangle_d = \frac{2\hbar}{m\omega}, \qquad \bigl\langle(x_1 - x_2)^2\bigr\rangle_+ = \frac{\hbar}{m\omega}, \qquad \bigl\langle(x_1 - x_2)^2\bigr\rangle_- = \frac{3\hbar}{m\omega} .
> $$
>
> The full distribution of the separation shows more. Change to $r = \xi_1 - \xi_2$ and $R = \tfrac12(\xi_1 + \xi_2)$ (Jacobian $1$, [[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^thm-25-6|452 Thm. §25.6]]), so that $\xi_1^2 + \xi_2^2 = 2R^2 + \tfrac12r^2$. The three wave functions are proportional to $\xi_2\,e^{-(\xi_1^2+\xi_2^2)/2}$ (distinguishable), $(\xi_1 + \xi_2)\,e^{\cdots}$ (bosons) and $(\xi_2 - \xi_1)\,e^{\cdots}$ (fermions), that is, to $(R - \tfrac r2)$, $2R$ and $-r$ times $e^{-R^2 - r^2/4}$. Integrating $|\psi|^2$ over $R$ with the Gaussian integrals ([[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^ex-25-4|452 Ex. §25.4]]) $\int e^{-2R^2}dR = \sqrt{\pi/2}$, $\int R^2e^{-2R^2}dR = \tfrac14\sqrt{\pi/2}$ and normalising,
>
> $$
> P_d(r) = \frac{(1 + r^2)\,e^{-r^2/2}}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}, \qquad P_+(r) = \frac{e^{-r^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}, \qquad P_-(r) = \frac{r^2\,e^{-r^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}},
> $$
>
> with $r$ in units of $\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}$; their second moments are $2$, $1$, $3$, as above. The boson distribution peaks at zero separation; the fermion distribution vanishes there.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 5.7*

> [!remark] Remark: If matter were made of bosons
> PHY 308's HW 6.4 asks what would happen if the fundamental matter particles were bosons. (No official solution is available; this is the answer the sources support.)
> - **Atoms.** Every electron would drop into the 1s orbital. There would be no shells, no periodic repetition of chemical properties and no valence: "chemistry would be very dull indeed" (Griffiths §5.2.2). Each added electron would sit close to the nucleus, so atoms would get smaller and more tightly bound as $Z$ grows, instead of keeping roughly the same size.
> - **Bulk matter.** The exclusion-principle repulsion between closed shells ([[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^rem-a6-1-1|Remark in §A6.1]]) and the degeneracy pressure of the electron gas, which Griffiths offers as a partial answer to why a cold solid does not collapse ([[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^rem-b7-3-1|Remark: What is quantum mechanics and what is statistical mechanics]]), would both be absent. Without them nothing but electrostatics would hold matter apart, and nothing would support white dwarfs or neutron stars (Greensite ch. 16; [[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars|TH §B13.3★]]).
> - **Solids.** With no Fermi sea, all conduction electrons would sit at the bottom of the lowest band; the distinction between filled and partly filled bands, and with it that between insulators and metals, would disappear. At low temperature such a gas would condense ([[§B12.1★ Bose–Einstein Condensation|TH §B12.1★]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 6, Problem 6.4; Griffiths §5.2.2, §5.3.1*

^rem-b7-1-4

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Three spins one-half
> Two spin-$\tfrac12$ particles have one antisymmetric spin state, the singlet. Three have none: a totally antisymmetric spin state of three spin-$\tfrac12$ particles is impossible (Griffiths Problem 5.10), for the same reason that there are no nonzero alternating $3$-linear forms on a $2$-dimensional space ([[§36 Alternating Multilinear Forms#^ladr-9-29|LADR Thm. 9.29]]). So the state of three electrons cannot be a single product of a spatial and a spin factor; it must be a sum of such products, which a Slater determinant of spin-orbitals provides automatically (lithium, [[§B7.2 Atoms#^ex-b7-2-3|★ Example §B7.2.3]]).
