Level A treated the conduction electrons of a metal as a gas of free fermions in a box ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^mod-a6-2-2|Model §A6.2.2]]) and described the effect of the ions only qualitatively: a Bloch form for the states, gaps at the Bragg condition, bands that are full or partly full ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^mod-a6-3-2|Model §A6.3.2]], [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-3|Theorem §A6.3.3]], [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-4|Theorem §A6.3.4]]). The thermodynamics of the free-electron gas, with its Fermi energy and degeneracy pressure, is done in statistical mechanics ([[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-2|TH Theorem §B13.1.2]]). This section supplies the quantum mechanics behind both: the states of the box and how many fit below a given energy, Bloch's theorem with a proof, and an exactly solvable periodic potential, the Dirac comb, whose spectrum consists of bands separated by gaps.

## The free electron gas

> [!model] Model §B7.3.1: Free Electron Gas
> A solid is a rectangular box $0 < x < l_x$, $0 < y < l_y$, $0 < z < l_z$ of volume $V = l_xl_yl_z$, containing $N$ atoms each of which contributes $d$ free electrons (typically $d = 1, 2, 3$). Inside, the electrons feel no force; the walls are impenetrable:
>
> $$
> V(x, y, z) = \begin{cases} 0, & \text{inside the box}, \\ \infty, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}
> $$
>
> *Assumptions:* the electrons do not interact with each other or with the ions (Sommerfeld's model); they are identical spin-$\tfrac12$ fermions; the solid is "cold", in its collective ground state. This is [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^mod-a6-2-2|Model §A6.2.2]] and, at any temperature, [[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^mod-b13-1-1|TH Model §B13.1.1]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.1, eq. (5.46), footnote 21; Greensite §16.2, eqs. (16.27)–(16.28)*

^mod-b7-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.3.2: Box States in k-Space
> The one-electron stationary states of Model §B7.3.1 are
>
> $$
> \psi_{n_xn_yn_z} = \sqrt{\frac{8}{l_xl_yl_z}}\,\sin\Bigl(\frac{n_x\pi}{l_x}x\Bigr)\sin\Bigl(\frac{n_y\pi}{l_y}y\Bigr)\sin\Bigl(\frac{n_z\pi}{l_z}z\Bigr), \qquad E_{n_xn_yn_z} = \frac{\hbar^2\pi^2}{2m}\Bigl(\frac{n_x^2}{l_x^2} + \frac{n_y^2}{l_y^2} + \frac{n_z^2}{l_z^2}\Bigr) = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} ,
> $$
>
> with $n_x, n_y, n_z = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ and wave vector $\mathbf k = (n_x\pi/l_x, n_y\pi/l_y, n_z\pi/l_z)$, each with two spin states. In $\mathbf k$-space the allowed vectors form a lattice in the octant $k_x, k_y, k_z > 0$, with one point for each block of volume
>
> $$
> \frac{\pi^3}{l_xl_yl_z} = \frac{\pi^3}{V} .
> $$
>
> Consequently a shell of radius $k$ and thickness $dk$ (an octant of it) contains $2\cdot\frac{\frac18 4\pi k^2\,dk}{\pi^3/V} = \frac{V}{\pi^2}k^2\,dk$ electron states.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.1, eqs. (5.47)–(5.51), (5.55); Greensite §16.2, eqs. (16.29)–(16.30)*

^thm-b7-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The states and energies are those of the particle in a rectangular box, obtained by separating the Schrödinger equation in Cartesian coordinates into three infinite wells ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-5|Theorem §B5.1.5]]; for the cube, [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]): $k_xl_x = n_x\pi$ with $n_x \ge 1$ from $X(0) = X(l_x) = 0$, and likewise for $y$, $z$. What is new is the picture in $\mathbf k$-space. The planes $k_x = \pi/l_x, 2\pi/l_x, \dots$ (and likewise in $y$, $z$) cut it into blocks of volume $(\pi/l_x)(\pi/l_y)(\pi/l_z)$, with one allowed point per block. For a shell, divide its volume by the volume per point and multiply by $2$ for spin.

^der-b7-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^mod-b7-3-1|Model §B7.3.1]], [[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-5|Theorem §B5.1.5]], [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.3.3: The Fermi Sphere
> The ground state of the $Nd$ electrons of Model §B7.3.1 fills, two electrons per point, the octant of a sphere in $\mathbf k$-space of radius
>
> $$
> k_F = \bigl(3\rho\pi^2\bigr)^{1/3}, \qquad \rho = \frac{Nd}{V} ,
> $$
>
> the **free electron density**. The boundary of the occupied region is the **Fermi surface**, and its energy the **Fermi energy**,
>
> $$
> E_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\bigl(3\rho\pi^2\bigr)^{2/3} .
> $$
>
> As a state of $Nd$ fermions, it is the Slater determinant of the $Nd$ lowest spin-orbitals. If the electrons were bosons (or distinguishable), all would be in $\psi_{111}$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.1, eqs. (5.52)–(5.54), footnote 22; Greensite §16.2, eqs. (16.31)–(16.38)*

^thm-b7-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the exclusion principle ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]]) the lowest total energy puts two electrons (opposite spins) into each of the lowest box states, and $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ depends only on $|\mathbf k|$, so the occupied points are those with $k < k_F$. Since $N$ is huge, the jagged edge of the occupied lattice points can be replaced by the smooth sphere, and the number of points by volume over volume per point (Theorem §B7.3.2): $\frac18\cdot\frac43\pi k_F^3 = \frac{Nd}2\cdot\frac{\pi^3}V$, which gives $k_F^3 = 3\pi^2Nd/V$. Then $E_F = \hbar^2k_F^2/2m$.

^der-b7-3-3

*Uses:* [[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^thm-b7-3-2|Theorem §B7.3.2]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: What is quantum mechanics and what is statistical mechanics
> Theorem §B7.3.3 is [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^thm-a6-2-4|Theorem §A6.2.4]] and [[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-2|TH Theorem §B13.1.2]] (Griffiths writes $\rho$ for the electron density that Thermal writes $n$). The quantum-mechanical input is only the counting: one spatial state per volume $\pi^3/V$ of $\mathbf k$-space, two spin states, at most one electron per spin-orbital. Integrating the energies $\hbar^2k^2/2m$ over the shells of Theorem §B7.3.2 gives Griffiths' total energy $E_{\text{tot}} = \hbar^2k_F^5V/10\pi^2m$, a mean energy $\tfrac35E_F$ per electron, and from $dE_{\text{tot}} = -P\,dV$ the **degeneracy pressure** $P = \tfrac23E_{\text{tot}}/V$, which owes nothing to electron repulsion or thermal motion and is a partial answer to why a cold solid does not collapse ("exclusion pressure", Griffiths); all of this, with the density of states and the bulk modulus, is derived in [[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-2|TH Theorem §B13.1.2]] and [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-4|TH Theorem §B10.1.4]] (which also shows that the result does not depend on the shape of the box or on the boundary conditions). Greensite applies the same counting to neutrons and estimates the radius of a neutron star ([[§B13.3★ White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars|TH §B13.3★]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.1, eqs. (5.55)–(5.57), footnote 23; Greensite §16.2, eqs. (16.39)–(16.49)*

## Bloch's theorem

> [!definition] Definition §B7.3.1: Periodic Potential; Translation Operator
> A one-dimensional potential is **periodic** with period $a$ if $V(x + a) = V(x)$ for all $x$. The **translation operator** $\hat T_a$ shifts a function by one period,
>
> $$
> (\hat T_af)(x) = f(x + a) ;
> $$
>
> formally $\hat T_a = e^{ia\hat p/\hbar}$, the Taylor series of $f(x + a)$ written with $\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dx$. To avoid edge effects the crystal of $N$ cells is wrapped into a ring, $\psi(x + Na) = \psi(x)$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.2, eqs. (5.58), (5.62); Greensite §16.1, eqs. (16.3)–(16.9)*

^def-b7-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.3.4: Bloch's Theorem
> For a periodic potential the stationary states, solutions of $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + V\psi = E\psi$, can be chosen to satisfy
>
> $$
> \psi(x + a) = e^{iqa}\,\psi(x)
> $$
>
> for a constant $q$, the **Bloch wave number**, which may depend on $E$ but not on $x$. With the ring condition $\psi(x + Na) = \psi(x)$, $q$ is real and quantised,
>
> $$
> q = \frac{2\pi n}{Na}, \qquad n = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots ,
> $$
>
> with only $N$ distinct values of $e^{iqa}$ ($q$ and $q + 2\pi/a$ are equivalent). Equivalently $\psi(x) = e^{iqx}u(x)$ with $u(x + a) = u(x)$, the Bloch form of [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^mod-a6-3-2|Model §A6.3.2]]; $|\psi|^2$ is periodic. It suffices to solve the Schrödinger equation in one cell; the condition generates the solution everywhere.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.2, eqs. (5.59)–(5.63), footnotes 25–26; Greensite §16.1, eqs. (16.10)–(16.19)*

^thm-b7-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Work on the ring: functions with $\psi(x + Na) = \psi(x)$, inner product $\int_0^{Na}\bar f g\,dx$.
>
> *$\hat T_a$ commutes with $\hat H$.* $\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$ commutes with a shift, and $(\hat T_a V\psi)(x) = V(x + a)\psi(x + a) = V(x)(\hat T_a\psi)(x)$ by periodicity. So $\hat T_a\hat H = \hat H\hat T_a$.
>
> *$\hat T_a$ is unitary, and $\hat T_a^N = 1$.* $\int_0^{Na}\overline{f(x + a)}g(x + a)\,dx = \int_a^{Na+a}\bar fg\,dx = \int_0^{Na}\bar fg\,dx$, the integrand having period $Na$; and $N$ shifts by $a$ are one trip round the ring.
>
> *Common eigenvectors.* Fix an energy $E$. Its eigenspace consists of solutions of a second-order linear differential equation, so it has dimension at most $2$, and $\hat T_a$ maps it into itself because the two operators commute. On this finite-dimensional space $\hat T_a$ is unitary, hence normal, so it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors (complex spectral theorem, [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]), with eigenvalues of absolute value $1$ ([[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-54|LADR Thm. 7.54]]): $\hat T_a\psi = \lambda\psi$ with $\lambda = e^{iqa}$, $q$ real. This is $\psi(x + a) = e^{iqa}\psi(x)$. Finally $\hat T_a^N = 1$ gives $e^{iNqa} = 1$, $Nqa = 2\pi n$. Setting $u(x) = e^{-iqx}\psi(x)$, $u(x + a) = e^{-iqx}e^{-iqa}e^{iqa}\psi(x) = u(x)$.

^der-b7-3-4

*Uses:* [[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^def-b7-3-1|Def. §B7.3.1]], [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]], [[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-54|LADR Thm. 7.54]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why one cannot simply argue from $|\psi|^2$
> It is tempting to reason backwards: the probability density must be periodic, $|\psi(x + a)|^2 = |\psi(x)|^2$, so $\psi(x + a) = e^{i\theta}\psi(x)$. But that only gives a phase $e^{i\theta(x)}$ that may vary with $x$ (Griffiths footnote 26); the content of Bloch's theorem is that the phase is constant, which needs the operator argument. Griffiths postpones the proof to his Chapter 6 (translations as symmetries, level C); the argument above is Greensite's, made precise with the two-dimensionality of the eigenspaces. The same structure, a translation-invariant system whose modes pick up a fixed phase per cell, with $q$ and $q + 2\pi/a$ equivalent, appears for the classical bead chain ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-3|WO Theorem §B3.1.3]]).

## The Dirac comb

> [!model] Model §B7.3.5: Dirac Comb
> An electron in one dimension moves in a periodic row of delta-function spikes, one per lattice site,
>
> $$
> V(x) = \alpha\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\delta(x - ja), \qquad \alpha > 0 ,
> $$
>
> on a ring of $N$ cells. *Assumptions:* one electron at a time (no electron–electron interaction); the ions are fixed. Repulsive spikes stand in for the ions only to keep all energies positive: the model is chosen to show the effect of periodicity, not to be realistic. Kronig and Penney's classic model uses a periodic row of rectangular barriers or wells, with the same qualitative result.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.2, eq. (5.64), footnotes 24, 27–29*

^mod-b7-3-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.3.6: Band Condition for the Dirac Comb
> With $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$, the allowed energies of the Dirac comb are those for which
>
> $$
> \cos(qa) = \cos(ka) + \frac{m\alpha}{\hbar^2k}\,\sin(ka)
> $$
>
> has a solution with one of the allowed Bloch numbers $q$ (Theorem §B7.3.4). With $z = ka$ and the dimensionless strength $\beta = m\alpha a/\hbar^2$, the right side is
>
> $$
> f(z) = \cos z + \beta\,\frac{\sin z}{z} ,
> $$
>
> and energies with $|f(z)| > 1$ are **forbidden** (gaps), those with $|f(z)| \le 1$ **allowed** (bands). In the cell $0 \le x \le a$ the stationary state is, up to normalisation,
>
> $$
> \psi(x) = C\bigl\{\sin(kx) + e^{-iqa}\sin[k(a - x)]\bigr\} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.2, eqs. (5.65)–(5.73), Problem 5.24(a); Greensite §16.1, eqs. (16.20)–(16.26)*

^thm-b7-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Since $V \ge 0$, all energies are positive and $k$ is real. In $0 < x < a$ the potential vanishes, $\psi'' = -k^2\psi$, and
>
> $$
> \psi(x) = A\sin(kx) + B\cos(kx) \qquad (0 < x < a) .
> $$
>
> By Bloch's theorem the solution in the cell to the left is $\psi(x) = e^{-iqa}\psi(x + a)$:
>
> $$
> \psi(x) = e^{-iqa}\bigl\{A\sin[k(x + a)] + B\cos[k(x + a)]\bigr\} \qquad (-a < x < 0) .
> $$
>
> At the spike at $x = 0$, $\psi$ is continuous and $\psi'$ jumps by $\frac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2}\psi(0)$ ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]], with the sign of $\alpha$ for a spike):
>
> $$
> B = e^{-iqa}\bigl[A\sin(ka) + B\cos(ka)\bigr], \qquad kA - e^{-iqa}k\bigl[A\cos(ka) - B\sin(ka)\bigr] = \frac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2}B .
> $$
>
> The first gives $A\sin(ka) = \bigl[e^{iqa} - \cos(ka)\bigr]B$. Multiply the second by $\sin(ka)$, insert this, and cancel $kB$:
>
> $$
> \bigl[e^{iqa} - \cos(ka)\bigr]\bigl[1 - e^{-iqa}\cos(ka)\bigr] + e^{-iqa}\sin^2(ka) = \frac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2k}\sin(ka) .
> $$
>
> Multiplying out, the left side is $e^{iqa} - 2\cos(ka) + e^{-iqa}\bigl[\cos^2(ka) + \sin^2(ka)\bigr] = 2\cos(qa) - 2\cos(ka)$, which gives the band condition. Since $\cos(qa)$ lies in $[-1, 1]$ for real $q$, $|f(z)| > 1$ admits no solution. For the wave function, multiply $\psi = A\sin kx + B\cos kx$ by $\sin ka$ and use the same relation: $\psi\sin ka = B\bigl[e^{iqa}\sin kx + \sin(ka - kx)\bigr]$, which is the stated form with $C = Be^{iqa}/\sin ka$. (The step divides by $\sin ka$; at $ka = n\pi$ the condition reads $\cos qa = \pm1$, and these band edges are reached as limits.)

^der-b7-3-6

*Uses:* [[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^mod-b7-3-5|Model §B7.3.5]], [[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^thm-b7-3-4|Theorem §B7.3.4]], [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.3.7: Bands and Gaps of the Dirac Comb
> For $\beta > 0$:
> 1. There is a gap from $E = 0$ up to the bottom of the first band ($f(0^+) = 1 + \beta > 1$).
> 2. Every band ends at the top exactly at $ka = n\pi$, the free-electron wave number of the Bragg condition, where $f = (-1)^n$ and $qa = 0$ or $\pi$; immediately above it lies a gap. The top of band $n$ is $E_n^{\text{well}} = n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2$, the $n$-th level of an infinite well of width $a$.
> 3. Each band holds $N$ spatial states, one for each Bloch number; the energies with $q \ne 0, \pi/a$ are doubly degenerate ($\pm q$). With spin, a band holds $2N$ electrons.
> 4. *Weak and strong spikes.* As $\beta \to 0$ the gaps close and $E = \hbar^2(q + 2\pi n/a)^2/2m$, free electrons. For $\beta \gg n\pi$ band $n$ shrinks onto the well level, with width $\Delta E_n \approx 4E_n^{\text{well}}/\beta$. At high energy, $z \gg \beta$, the gaps become narrow compared with the bands: fast electrons are nearly free.
> 5. *Filling.* With $d$ free electrons per atom, $Nd$ electrons fill the lowest $Nd/2$ spatial states: $d = 1$ half fills the first band, $d = 2$ fills it, $d = 3$ half fills the second. A partly filled top band makes a conductor; a full top band followed by a gap an insulator, or a semiconductor if the gap is small.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §5.3.2, pp. 283–284, Figures 5.5–5.6, Problems 5.24(b), 5.27, footnotes 30–31; Greensite §16.1, p. 257–258*

^thm-b7-3-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\sin z/z \to 1$ as $z \to 0$. *2.* At $z = n\pi$, $f = \cos n\pi = (-1)^n$, and $f'(z) = -\sin z + \beta(z\cos z - \sin z)/z^2$ gives $f'(n\pi) = (-1)^n\beta/(n\pi)$. So $(-1)^nf$ increases through $1$ at $z = n\pi$: just below, $|f| < 1$ (allowed); just above, $|f| > 1$ (forbidden). There $\cos qa = (-1)^n$, so $qa = 0$ or $\pi$. *3.* Within a band each admissible value $\cos(2\pi n/N)$ of $\cos qa$ meets $f$ once (Griffiths); the $N$ values of $e^{iqa}$ give $N$ states, and $q$, $-q$ give the same $\cos qa$, hence the same energy, but different eigenvalues of $\hat T_a$, so different states, except when $e^{iqa} = \pm1$. Two spin states per spatial state ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]]). *4.* For $\beta = 0$, $\cos qa = \cos ka$, so $k = \pm q$ modulo $2\pi/a$. Near $z = n\pi$, $\cos z \approx (-1)^n$ and $\sin z \approx (-1)^n(z - n\pi)$, so $f \approx (-1)^n\bigl[1 + \beta(z - n\pi)/(n\pi)\bigr]$; $|f| \le 1$ requires $-2n\pi/\beta \le z - n\pi \le 0$, so $\Delta z \approx 2n\pi/\beta$ and $\Delta E \approx \frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\,2z\,\Delta z \approx \frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\,\frac{4n^2\pi^2}{\beta} = \frac{4E_n^{\text{well}}}\beta$. Since $|f - \cos z| \le \beta/z$, for $z \gg \beta$ the forbidden stretches, where $|\cos z|$ is within $\beta/z$ of $1$, are a small fraction of each period. *5.* Fill the lowest spin-orbitals ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]]) and apply the conduction argument of [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-4|Theorem §A6.3.4]]: electrons can be accelerated only into empty states just above them.

> [!remark] Remark: How the level-A statements change layer
> - The Bloch form, stated in [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^mod-a6-3-2|Model §A6.3.2]], is now a theorem (Theorem §B7.3.4).
> - The gaps "at the Bragg condition" ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-3|Theorem §A6.3.3]], an idea argument) appear exactly: the band edges sit at $ka = n\pi$, that is, at $k = n\pi/a$, where the Bloch number $qa$ is $0$ or $\pi$ and the state is a standing wave.
> - Levels broadening into bands as atoms are brought together ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]]) is the strong-spike limit of Theorem §B7.3.7(4), read backwards: each isolated-well level spreads into a band of $N$ states whose width grows as the coupling $1/\beta$ between neighbouring wells grows.
> - The classification into conductors, insulators and semiconductors ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-4|Theorem §A6.3.4]]) follows from the count of $2N$ states per band. In the free-electron model every solid would be a metal, since there are no large gaps (Griffiths). Near a band edge the band is curved like a free particle with an effective mass $m^*$ ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^def-a6-3-3|Def. §A6.3.3]]).

^rem-b7-3-3

> [!example] Example §B7.3.1: The Bands of a Strong Comb
> Take $\beta = 10$ and, for the sake of argument, $\alpha/a = 1\ \mathrm{eV}$ (Griffiths Problem 5.25). Then $\hbar^2/ma^2 = (\alpha/a)/\beta = 0.1\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $E = \frac{\hbar^2z^2}{2ma^2} = \frac{z^2}{20}\ \mathrm{eV}$. The bottom of the first band is the root of $f(z) = 1$ below $\pi$; solving $\cos z + 10\sin z/z = 1$ numerically gives $z = 2.628$, so
>
> $$
> E_{\text{bottom}} = \frac{(2.628)^2}{20}\ \mathrm{eV} = 0.345\ \mathrm{eV} .
> $$
>
> The first four bands (lower edges from $|f| = 1$, upper edges at $z = n\pi$):

> | band | $z$ range | $E$ range (eV) | width (eV) | gap above (eV) |

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Attractive spikes and the Kronig–Penney model
> Greensite uses delta-function *wells*, $V = -g\sum_j\delta(x - ja)$, which model the attraction of the ions better. Positive energies obey the same condition with the sign of the $\sin$ term reversed, $\cos qa = \cos ka - \frac{mg}{\hbar^2k}\sin ka$ (Greensite eq. (16.26)); now there are also negative-energy states (Griffiths Problem 5.26). Putting $k = i\kappa$, $E = -\hbar^2\kappa^2/2m$, gives $\cos qa = \cosh\kappa a - \frac{mg}{\hbar^2\kappa}\sinh\kappa a$. For widely spaced wells, $\kappa a \gg 1$, the right side is about $\tfrac12e^{\kappa a}\bigl(1 - mg/\hbar^2\kappa\bigr)$, which stays within $[-1, 1]$ only if $\kappa \approx mg/\hbar^2$: the band collapses onto the single bound state $E = -mg^2/2\hbar^2$ of one delta well ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]]), the tight-binding picture of [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]] in its simplest form. The original Kronig–Penney model uses rectangular wells; its band condition is longer but has the same features (Griffiths footnote 29). In three dimensions with realistic potentials the bands are more complicated, but allowed bands separated by gaps remain the signature of a periodic potential; an odd $d$ guarantees a partly filled band, while Mott insulators show that electron–electron interactions can still make such a material insulate (Griffiths footnote 30).
