When no solvable Hamiltonian is close to the true one, what can still be said about its levels, and how good is the answer? Level B proved the variational principle, the bound on an excited state by orthogonality and the matrix method for a linear combination of orthonormal functions ([[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle]]: [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]], [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]], [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]]), and applied it to helium, $\mathrm H_2^+$ and $\mathrm H_2$ ([[§B9.2★ Helium and the Hydrogen Molecule]]). Sakurai's proof of the principle, by expanding the trial ket in energy eigenkets, is level B's, so it is linked and not repeated. Level C adds three things: the energy functional is stationary at every eigenket, with an exact formula for the error; the energy alone bounds how close the trial ket is to the true ground state; and the Ritz method in a basis that need not be orthonormal, which is a generalized Hermitian eigenvalue problem and the working form of the method (Gaussian bases, molecular orbitals). The PHY 512 schedule lists the Ritz method and its applications; no typed course material exists.

*Notation.* As in [[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory]]: $H|k\rangle = E_k|k\rangle$ with $E_0 \le E_1 \le \cdots$ the exact levels (the continuum, if any, lies above the bound states used). For a nonzero ket $|\alpha\rangle$, $\bar H[\alpha] = \langle\alpha|H|\alpha\rangle/\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle$ is the **energy functional** (Sakurai's $\bar H$, (5.151); level B's Rayleigh quotient, [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^def-b9-1-1|Def. §B9.1.1]]). Column vectors $\mathbf c \in \mathbb C^N$, $\mathbf c^\dagger$ their conjugate transposes.

## The energy functional

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.3.1: Stationarity of the Energy Functional
> $\bar H$ is stationary at $|\alpha\rangle$, that is $\bar H[\alpha + \delta\alpha] = \bar H[\alpha] + O(\lVert\delta\alpha\rVert^2)$ for all $|\delta\alpha\rangle$, if and only if $H|\alpha\rangle = \bar H[\alpha]\,|\alpha\rangle$: the stationary points are the eigenkets and the stationary values the eigenvalues. Near an eigenket the error is quadratic, and exactly: if $|\alpha\rangle = |k\rangle + |\eta\rangle$ with $\langle k|\eta\rangle = 0$,
>
> $$
> \bar H[\alpha] - E_k = \frac{\langle\eta|(H - E_k)|\eta\rangle}{1 + \langle\eta|\eta\rangle} .
> $$
>
> At the ground state the right side is $\ge 0$ for every $|\eta\rangle$ (a minimum, level B's variational principle); at an excited state it is positive along higher eigenkets and negative along lower ones (a saddle).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.4, eqs. (5.155)–(5.158) (stationarity and the quadratic error at the ground state); the general statement and the exact error formula are derived here*

^thm-c9-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Normalize $\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle = 1$ and write $\bar H = \bar H[\alpha]$. Then
>
> $$
> \bar H[\alpha + \delta\alpha] = \frac{\bar H + \langle\delta\alpha|H|\alpha\rangle + \langle\alpha|H|\delta\alpha\rangle + O(\delta^2)}{1 + \langle\delta\alpha|\alpha\rangle + \langle\alpha|\delta\alpha\rangle + O(\delta^2)} = \bar H + \langle\delta\alpha|(H - \bar H)|\alpha\rangle + \langle\alpha|(H - \bar H)|\delta\alpha\rangle + O(\delta^2) .
> $$
>
> The first-order term is $2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\delta\alpha|(H - \bar H)|\alpha\rangle$ ($H$ is Hermitian). It vanishes for all $|\delta\alpha\rangle$, including $i|\delta\alpha\rangle$, if and only if $\langle\delta\alpha|(H - \bar H)|\alpha\rangle = 0$ for all $|\delta\alpha\rangle$, that is, $(H - \bar H)|\alpha\rangle = 0$. For the error formula, $\langle k|\eta\rangle = 0$ and $H|k\rangle = E_k|k\rangle$ kill the cross terms: $\langle\alpha|H|\alpha\rangle = E_k + \langle\eta|H|\eta\rangle$ and $\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle = 1 + \langle\eta|\eta\rangle$, so $\bar H - E_k = (\langle\eta|H|\eta\rangle - E_k\langle\eta|\eta\rangle)/(1 + \langle\eta|\eta\rangle)$. Expanding $|\eta\rangle$ in eigenkets, $\langle\eta|(H - E_k)|\eta\rangle = \sum_j(E_j - E_k)|\langle j|\eta\rangle|^2$, whose terms have the sign of $E_j - E_k$. Any trial ket not orthogonal to $|k\rangle$ can be scaled to the form $|k\rangle + |\eta\rangle$, which does not change $\bar H$.

*Uses:* [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]], [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from level B
> Level B showed for the ground state that an error $\epsilon$ in the wave function costs $\epsilon^2$ in the energy ([[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^rem-b9-1-1|Remark: Why the bound is better than the guess]]), and Sakurai states the same as $\bar H - E_0 \sim O(\epsilon^2)$ when $\langle k|\tilde0\rangle \sim O(\epsilon)$ (5.156)–(5.157). Theorem §C9.3.1 is the general form: the Schrödinger equation is the stationarity condition of $\bar H$ on the whole ket space, and every eigenket, not only the ground state, is a stationary point. That is why a variational estimate of an excited level needs an extra constraint (orthogonality to lower states, [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]], or a symmetry that excludes them): without one, $\bar H$ can always be lowered toward $E_0$. Classically the analogous statement is that the equations of motion are the stationarity condition of the action ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^pr-b6-2-1|CM Principle §B6.2.1]]).

^rem-c9-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.3.2: How Close Is the Trial Ket? A Bound from the Energy
> Let $|\tilde0\rangle$ be a normalized trial ket, $E_0$ a nondegenerate ground level, and $E_*$ the lowest level above $E_0$ whose eigenkets have nonzero overlap with $|\tilde0\rangle$ (for example the lowest level of the trial ket's symmetry). If $\bar H[\tilde0] < E_*$, then
>
> $$
> |\langle0|\tilde0\rangle|^2 \ge \frac{E_* - \bar H[\tilde0]}{E_* - E_0} . \qquad (5.169)\text{–}(5.170)
> $$
>
> So a variational energy close to $E_0$, together with a known gap, certifies that the trial ket is close to the true ground state, without computing $\langle0|\tilde0\rangle$. Likharev stresses that the variational method by itself has "uncontrollable accuracy", saying nothing about how close the trial function is to the true one; a known gap partly restores that control.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.4, eqs. (5.169)–(5.172) (for the infinite well, with $E_{\ast} = 9E_0$); stated generally here · Likharev §2.9 (the remark on accuracy)*

^thm-c9-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Expand $|\tilde0\rangle = \sum_kc_k|k\rangle$, $\sum_k|c_k|^2 = 1$ (5.153). By assumption $c_k = 0$ for every $k \neq 0$ with $E_k < E_*$, so
>
> $$
> \bar H = |c_0|^2E_0 + \sum_{E_k\ge E_*}|c_k|^2E_k \ \ge\ |c_0|^2E_0 + (1 - |c_0|^2)E_* . \qquad (5.169)
> $$
>
> Solving for $|c_0|^2 = |\langle0|\tilde0\rangle|^2$ gives the bound. (Continuum states, if present, lie above $E_*$ and only strengthen the inequality.)

*Uses:* [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]]

> [!example] Example §C9.3.1: A One-Parameter Family for the Infinite Well
> For the well $V = 0$ on $|x| < a$, infinite outside, with exact $E_0 = \pi^2\hbar^2/8ma^2$ and $\langle x|0\rangle = a^{-1/2}\cos(\pi x/2a)$ (5.161)–(5.162), take the even trial functions
>
> $$
> \langle x|\tilde0\rangle = a^\lambda - |x|^\lambda \qquad (5.165)
> $$
>
> (level B tried a triangle on the same well, [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^ex-b9-1-2|Example §B9.1.2]]; this family contains the parabola $\lambda = 2$ and has a kink at $x = 0$ for $\lambda < 2$, allowed by [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^cau-b9-1-1|Caution: What counts as a trial function]]).
>
> *Energy.* Using $\langle T\rangle = (\hbar^2/2m)\int|\psi'|^2dx$: the numerator is $\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\cdot2\int_0^a\lambda^2x^{2\lambda-2}dx = \frac{\hbar^2\lambda^2a^{2\lambda-1}}{m(2\lambda - 1)}$ and the norm $2\int_0^a(a^\lambda - x^\lambda)^2dx = \frac{4\lambda^2a^{2\lambda+1}}{(\lambda+1)(2\lambda+1)}$, so
>
> $$
> \bar H(\lambda) = \frac{(\lambda + 1)(2\lambda + 1)}{2\lambda - 1}\,\frac{\hbar^2}{4ma^2} . \qquad (5.166)
> $$
>
> The parabola gives $\bar H(2) = \frac{10}{\pi^2}E_0 = 1.0132\,E_0$ (5.164). Setting $d\bar H/d\lambda = 0$ gives $4\lambda^2 - 4\lambda - 5 = 0$, $\lambda = (1 + \sqrt6)/2 = 1.72$ (5.167), and $\bar H_{\min} = \frac{5 + 2\sqrt6}{\pi^2}E_0 = 1.00298\,E_0$ (5.168): within $0.3\,\%$.
>
> *Overlap.* The trial function is even, so it has no overlap with the odd first excited state; the next even level is $E_2 = 9E_0$. Theorem §C9.3.2 with $E_{\ast} = 9E_0$ gives
>
> $$
> |\langle0|\tilde0\rangle|^2 \ge \frac{9 - 1.00298}{8} = 0.99963 , \qquad \langle0|\tilde0\rangle = \cos\theta,\ \theta \lesssim 1.1^\circ \qquad (5.170)\text{–}(5.172)
> $$
>
> The bound is honest but not tight: direct integration of the optimal trial function against $\cos(\pi x/2a)$ gives $|\langle0|\tilde0\rangle|^2 = 0.99988$, $\theta = 0.64^\circ$. The energy is off by $3\times10^{-3}$, the state by $1.2\times10^{-4}$ in probability: the quadratic relation of Theorem §C9.3.1 at work, with the gap as the conversion factor.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.4, eqs. (5.161)–(5.172); the exact overlap is computed here*

## The Ritz method in a general basis

> [!definition] Definition §C9.3.1: Ritz Problem; Overlap and Hamiltonian Matrices
> Choose $N$ linearly independent kets $|\phi_1\rangle, \dots, |\phi_N\rangle$ in the domain of $H$, not necessarily orthonormal, spanning the **Ritz space** $\mathcal V_N$. A trial ket in $\mathcal V_N$ is $|\alpha\rangle = \sum_jc_j|\phi_j\rangle$, and its energy is
>
> $$
> \bar H(\mathbf c) = \frac{\mathbf c^\dagger\mathsf H\,\mathbf c}{\mathbf c^\dagger\mathsf S\,\mathbf c}, \qquad \mathsf H_{ij} = \langle\phi_i|H|\phi_j\rangle, \qquad \mathsf S_{ij} = \langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle ,
> $$
>
> with the **Hamiltonian matrix** $\mathsf H$ and the **overlap matrix** $\mathsf S$, both Hermitian. The **Ritz method** (linear variational method) takes the stationary points of $\bar H(\mathbf c)$ as approximate eigenkets.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 8.30, eqs. (8.88)–(8.91) (orthonormal case); Sakurai §5.4, eq. (5.159) (minimizing over parameters); the nonorthogonal form is stated here*

^def-c9-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.3.3: The Ritz Equations as a Generalized Eigenvalue Problem
> 1. $\mathsf S$ is positive definite.
> 2. $\bar H(\mathbf c)$ is stationary exactly when
>
> $$
> \mathsf H\,\mathbf c = \varepsilon\,\mathsf S\,\mathbf c, \qquad \varepsilon = \bar H(\mathbf c), \qquad \det(\mathsf H - \varepsilon\mathsf S) = 0 .
> $$
>
> 3. There are $N$ real roots $\varepsilon_1 \le \cdots \le \varepsilon_N$ (the **Ritz values**) with eigenvectors that can be chosen $\mathsf S$-orthonormal, $\mathbf c_i^\dagger\mathsf S\mathbf c_j = \delta_{ij}$; then also $\mathbf c_i^\dagger\mathsf H\mathbf c_j = \varepsilon_i\delta_{ij}$. The **Ritz kets** $|\chi_i\rangle = \sum_j(\mathbf c_i)_j|\phi_j\rangle$ are orthonormal and diagonalize $P_NHP_N$, the Hamiltonian compressed to $\mathcal V_N$ by the orthogonal projector $P_N$.
> 4. $\varepsilon_1 = \min_{\mathcal V_N}\bar H \ge E_0$, and enlarging the basis ($\mathcal V_N \subset \mathcal V_{N'}$) can only lower $\varepsilon_1$.
> 5. With a Cholesky factorization $\mathsf S = \mathsf R^\dagger\mathsf R$ and $\mathbf d = \mathsf R\mathbf c$, the problem becomes the ordinary Hermitian eigenproblem $(\mathsf R^{-\dagger}\mathsf H\mathsf R^{-1})\,\mathbf d = \varepsilon\,\mathbf d$. For an orthonormal basis $\mathsf S = 1$, and this is level B's [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 8.30, eqs. (8.88)–(8.91), footnote 26 (orthonormal case, smallest eigenvalue as a bound); Sakurai §5.4 (stationarity); the generalization to $\mathsf S \neq 1$ is derived here*

^thm-c9-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. $\mathbf c^\dagger\mathsf S\mathbf c = \lVert\sum_jc_j|\phi_j\rangle\rVert^2 \ge 0$, with equality only if $\sum_jc_j|\phi_j\rangle = 0$, which linear independence forbids for $\mathbf c \neq 0$ ([[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-62|LADR Def. 7.62]]).
> 2. Treat $c_i$ and $c_i^*$ as independent (Griffiths footnote 26: equivalent to varying real and imaginary parts). Then
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\bar H}{\partial c_i^*} = \frac{(\mathsf H\mathbf c)_i}{\mathbf c^\dagger\mathsf S\mathbf c} - \frac{\mathbf c^\dagger\mathsf H\mathbf c}{(\mathbf c^\dagger\mathsf S\mathbf c)^2}\,(\mathsf S\mathbf c)_i ,
> $$
>
>    which vanishes for all $i$ exactly when $\mathsf H\mathbf c = \bar H\,\mathsf S\mathbf c$; the derivative with respect to $c_i$ gives the complex conjugate equation. A nonzero solution needs $\det(\mathsf H - \varepsilon\mathsf S) = 0$. (This is Theorem §C9.3.1 restricted to $\mathcal V_N$: $\langle\phi_i|(H - \varepsilon)|\alpha\rangle = 0$ for all $i$.)
> 3. By [[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-63|LADR Thm. 7.63]], $\mathsf S = \mathsf R^\dagger\mathsf R$ with $\mathsf R$ invertible. Put $\mathbf d = \mathsf R\mathbf c$ and $\mathsf K = \mathsf R^{-\dagger}\mathsf H\mathsf R^{-1}$, Hermitian. Multiplying $\mathsf H\mathbf c = \varepsilon\mathsf S\mathbf c$ by $\mathsf R^{-\dagger}$ gives $\mathsf K\mathbf d = \varepsilon\mathbf d$ (this is item 5). By the spectral theorem ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]) $\mathsf K$ has $N$ real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors $\mathbf d_i$; then $\mathbf c_i = \mathsf R^{-1}\mathbf d_i$ satisfy $\mathbf c_i^\dagger\mathsf S\mathbf c_j = \mathbf d_i^\dagger\mathbf d_j = \delta_{ij}$ and $\mathbf c_i^\dagger\mathsf H\mathbf c_j = \mathbf d_i^\dagger\mathsf K\mathbf d_j = \varepsilon_i\delta_{ij}$. These say $\langle\chi_i|\chi_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$ and $\langle\chi_i|H|\chi_j\rangle = \varepsilon_i\delta_{ij}$; since the $|\chi_i\rangle$ span $\mathcal V_N$, $P_NHP_N = \sum_i\varepsilon_i|\chi_i\rangle\langle\chi_i|$.
> 4. Expanding any trial ket of $\mathcal V_N$ in the Ritz kets, $|\alpha\rangle = \sum_ia_i|\chi_i\rangle$, gives $\bar H = \sum_i\varepsilon_i|a_i|^2/\sum_i|a_i|^2 \ge \varepsilon_1$, with equality at $|\chi_1\rangle$; so $\varepsilon_1$ is the minimum of $\bar H$ over $\mathcal V_N$, which is $\ge E_0$ by [[§B9.1★ The Variational Principle#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]]. A minimum over a larger set cannot be larger.

> [!remark] Remark: Ritz, perturbation theory and molecular orbitals
> - *Degenerate perturbation theory is a Ritz calculation.* Take $H = H_0 + \lambda V$ and $\mathcal V_N = D$, a degenerate eigenspace of $H_0$ with orthonormal basis. Then $\mathsf S = 1$ and $\mathsf H = E_D^{(0)} + \lambda\,[P_0VP_0]$, so the Ritz values are exactly the first-order energies of [[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory#^thm-c9-1-4|Theorem §C9.1.4]] and the Ritz kets the good states. Adding nearby levels to the basis is the quasi-degenerate diagonalization of [[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory#^rem-c9-1-4|Remark: The procedure, and nearly degenerate levels]]; truncating Likharev's exact linear system in the unperturbed basis is the same Ritz method with the first $N$ unperturbed kets.
> - *Nonorthogonal bases are the normal case.* The LCAO treatment of $\mathrm H_2^+$ ([[§B9.2★ Helium and the Hydrogen Molecule#^mod-b9-2-3|Model §B9.2.3]]) uses two atomic orbitals with overlap integral $I$: a $2\times2$ problem with $\mathsf S = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1 & I\\ I & 1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, whose eigenvectors the exchange symmetry fixes as the bonding and antibonding combinations, normalized with $1/\sqrt{2(1 \pm I)}$. The variational helium calculation of the same section, with one nonlinear parameter, is level B's and is not repeated (Sakurai treats it in §7.4).
> - *Numerical caution.* When basis functions are nearly linearly dependent, $\mathsf S$ is nearly singular and the reduction of item 5 amplifies rounding errors; the condition number of $\mathsf S$ (ratio of its largest to smallest eigenvalue) measures the danger (Example §C9.3.2).

> [!example] Example §C9.3.2: The Quartic Oscillator in a Basis of Gaussians
> For $H = p^2/2m + \kappa x^4$ there is no small parameter: perturbation theory around an oscillator does not apply, since the quadratic term is absent. Use units $\hbar = m = \kappa = 1$; the exact even levels (computed here by direct numerical diagonalization) are $E_0 = 0.667986$, $E_2 = 4.696795$, $E_4 = 10.244308$.
>
> *One Gaussian.* With $\psi = e^{-bx^2}$: $\langle T\rangle = b/2$, $\langle x^4\rangle = 3/16b^2$, so $\bar H(b) = b/2 + 3/16b^2$, minimal at $b^3 = 3/4$, $b = 0.9086$, giving $\bar H = \frac34b = 0.68142$, $2.0\,\%$ above $E_0$ (Griffiths Problem 8.1(b)).
>
> *Several Gaussians.* Take even Gaussians $\phi_j = e^{-a_jx^2}$ with widths in a nested sequence $a = 0.9,\ 1.35,\ 0.6,\ 2.025,\ 0.4,\ 3.04,\ 0.267$ (ratio $1.5$, alternately sharper and broader), and use the first $N$. All matrix elements are Gaussian integrals ($c = a_i + a_j$):
>
> $$
> \mathsf S_{ij} = \sqrt{\frac\pi c}, \qquad \langle\phi_i|\tfrac{p^2}{2}|\phi_j\rangle = \frac{a_ia_j\sqrt\pi}{c^{3/2}}, \qquad \langle\phi_i|x^4|\phi_j\rangle = \frac{3\sqrt\pi}{4c^{5/2}} .
> $$
>
> The basis is far from orthogonal (the normalized overlap of the first two functions is $0.990$), so Theorem §C9.3.3 with $\mathsf S \neq 1$ is needed. Solving $\mathsf H\mathbf c = \varepsilon\mathsf S\mathbf c$ gives
>
> | $N$ | $\varepsilon_1$ | $\varepsilon_2$ | $\varepsilon_3$ | condition number of $\mathsf S$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | 0.68148 | — | — | 1 |
> | 2 | 0.68096 | 4.80667 | — | $2\times10^2$ |
> | 3 | 0.66999 | 4.73148 | 14.442 | $8\times10^3$ |
> | 4 | 0.66867 | 4.71608 | 10.396 | $1\times10^5$ |
> | 5 | 0.66827 | 4.70242 | 10.395 | $1\times10^6$ |
> | 6 | 0.66806 | 4.70061 | 10.257 | $6\times10^6$ |
> | 7 | 0.66803 | 4.69883 | 10.254 | $3\times10^7$ |
>
> ($N = 1$ is the one-Gaussian result with $a$ fixed at $0.9$ instead of optimized.) Every $\varepsilon_1$ is above $E_0$ and they decrease with $N$, as Theorem §C9.3.3 (4) guarantees; with seven fixed Gaussians the relative error is $7\times10^{-5}$, three hundred times smaller than that of the best single Gaussian with its width optimized. In this calculation the higher Ritz values also stay above the exact levels of the same rank and decrease with $N$, so they estimate excited states (the figure); the general theorem behind this is not proved in the sources. The last column shows the price: by $N = 7$ the overlap matrix is close to singular.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 8.1(b) (one Gaussian, $V = \alpha x^4$) and 8.30 (linear combinations); the multi-Gaussian basis, the table and the exact levels are computed here*
