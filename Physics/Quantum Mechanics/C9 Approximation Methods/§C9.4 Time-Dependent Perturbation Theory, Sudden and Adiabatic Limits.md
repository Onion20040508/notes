Up to now every Hamiltonian in this subject was time independent, or, like the spin in a field turning at a constant rate ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-6|Theorem §B6.2.6]]), could be made so by a change of frame. What happens to a system in an energy eigenstate of $H_0$ when a time-dependent $V(t)$ is switched on, and with what probability is it later found in another eigenstate? Level B stopped short of this ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^rem-b6-2-4|Remark: The rotating frame]]), and level C so far supplied only the formal tool: the time-ordered exponential, or Dyson series, of [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]]. This section applies that series to $V$ alone, after the known motion under $H_0$ has been factored out, and so turns it into a perturbation expansion: first- and second-order transition amplitudes, constant and harmonic perturbations, and absorption and stimulated emission as resonances. Two limits need no small $V$, only extreme time scales: the sudden approximation, and the adiabatic theorem with its geometric (Berry) phase. All of it is new at the vault level (rule 3); the long-time limit of the transition probabilities, Fermi's golden rule, is [[§C9.5 Fermi's Golden Rule|§C9.5]], and the interaction picture as a full picture of dynamics is homed in [[§C9.6 The Interaction Picture and the Atom–Field Interaction|§C9.6]].

*Notation.* As Sakurai's ch. 5: $H = H_0 + V(t)$ with $H_0$ time independent and $H_0|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$ known (no primes in this chapter); $V_{nm}(t) = \langle n|V(t)|m\rangle$; $\omega_{nm} = (E_n - E_m)/\hbar = -\omega_{mn}$; $\mathscr U(t, t_0)$ is the time-evolution operator of [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^def-c3-1-1|Def. §C3.1.1]] for the full $H$; $|i\rangle$ is the initial state. Units are Gaussian where charges appear ([[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^cau-c6-2-1|Caution: Gaussian and SI units for the Coulomb problem]]). Charge signs: Example §C9.4.1 writes the electron's charge as $-|e|$, valid in either convention; in the Aharonov–Bohm remark $e$ is the particle's charge, as in [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^cau-c4-3-1|§C4.3]] and Sakurai ch. 5 ($e < 0$ for the electron; [[§C9.2 Atoms in External Fields, Fine and Hyperfine Structure#^cau-c9-2-1|§C9.2]] instead takes $e > 0$).

## Transition amplitudes

> [!definition] Definition §C9.4.1: Interaction-Picture Amplitudes and Evolution Operator
> Write the Schrödinger-picture state as an expansion in the eigenkets of $H_0$ with the free phases split off,
>
> $$
> |\alpha, t_0 = 0; t\rangle_S = \sum_n c_n(t)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\,|n\rangle, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad c_n(t) = \langle n|\,e^{iH_0t/\hbar}\,|\alpha, t_0 = 0; t\rangle_S .
> $$
>
> The $c_n(t)$ are the **interaction-picture amplitudes**: without $V$ they would be constant, so all their time dependence is caused by $V$. The **interaction-picture perturbation** and **evolution operator** are
>
> $$
> V_I(t) = e^{iH_0t/\hbar}\,V(t)\,e^{-iH_0t/\hbar}, \qquad \mathscr U_I(t, t_0) = e^{iH_0t/\hbar}\,\mathscr U(t, t_0)\,e^{-iH_0t_0/\hbar} .
> $$
>
> They belong to the interaction (Dirac) picture, whose states and observables are defined in [[§C9.6 The Interaction Picture and the Atom–Field Interaction|§C9.6]]; this section needs only these three objects.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.5.1, eqs. (5.173)–(5.176); §5.5.2, eq. (5.179); §5.7.2, eq. (5.268) · Griffiths §11.1.1, eq. (11.10) (two levels) · Likharev §6.5, eq. (6.82) (the $a_n(t)$)*

^def-c9-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.4.1: Exact Equations for the Amplitudes
> 1. $\mathscr U_I$ obeys a Schrödinger equation with $V_I$ in place of $H$:
>
> $$
> i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathscr U_I(t, t_0) = V_I(t)\,\mathscr U_I(t, t_0), \qquad \mathscr U_I(t_0, t_0) = 1 .
> $$
>
> 2. Equivalently, the amplitudes obey the coupled linear equations
>
> $$
> i\hbar\,\frac{dc_n}{dt} = \sum_m V_{nm}(t)\,e^{i\omega_{nm}t}\,c_m(t) ,
> $$
>
> which are exact and contain the whole Schrödinger equation.
> 3. If the system is in $|i\rangle$ at $t_0$, with the phase chosen as $|i, t_0; t_0\rangle_S = e^{-iE_it_0/\hbar}|i\rangle$, then
>
> $$
> c_n(t) = \langle n|\,\mathscr U_I(t, t_0)\,|i\rangle, \qquad |c_n(t)|^2 = \bigl|\langle n|\,\mathscr U(t, t_0)\,|i\rangle\bigr|^2 ,
> $$
>
> the probability of finding the system in $|n\rangle$ at time $t$: the transition amplitude of [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-7|Theorem §C3.3.7]] up to a phase. (The equality of moduli needs $|n\rangle$, $|i\rangle$ to be eigenkets of $H_0$; for other kets the two amplitudes differ in modulus as well.)
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.5.2, eqs. (5.182)–(5.189); §5.7.1–5.7.2, eqs. (5.261)–(5.275) · Griffiths §11.1.1, eqs. (11.13)–(11.17); Problem 11.24 · Likharev §6.5, eqs. (6.79)–(6.85)*

^thm-c9-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* By [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]], $i\hbar\,\partial_t\mathscr U = (H_0 + V)\mathscr U$. Differentiate $\mathscr U_I = e^{iH_0t/\hbar}\mathscr U\,e^{-iH_0t_0/\hbar}$; the derivative of the first factor gives $-H_0e^{iH_0t/\hbar}\mathscr U\cdots$, which cancels the $H_0$ term:
>
> $$
> i\hbar\,\partial_t\mathscr U_I = -H_0\,\mathscr U_I + e^{iH_0t/\hbar}(H_0 + V)\,\mathscr U\,e^{-iH_0t_0/\hbar} = e^{iH_0t/\hbar}\,V\,e^{-iH_0t/\hbar}\;e^{iH_0t/\hbar}\,\mathscr U\,e^{-iH_0t_0/\hbar} = V_I\,\mathscr U_I ,
> $$
>
> using that $e^{iH_0t/\hbar}$ commutes with $H_0$ and inserting $1 = e^{-iH_0t/\hbar}e^{iH_0t/\hbar}$. At $t = t_0$, $\mathscr U = 1$, so $\mathscr U_I = 1$.
>
> *2.* The ket $|\alpha; t\rangle_I = e^{iH_0t/\hbar}|\alpha; t\rangle_S = \sum_nc_n(t)|n\rangle$ equals $\mathscr U_I(t, 0)|\alpha\rangle$, so by part 1 it obeys $i\hbar\,\partial_t|\alpha; t\rangle_I = V_I|\alpha; t\rangle_I$. Apply $\langle n|$, insert $\sum_m|m\rangle\langle m|$ and use $\langle n|e^{iH_0t/\hbar}Ve^{-iH_0t/\hbar}|m\rangle = e^{i(E_n - E_m)t/\hbar}V_{nm}$. Conversely the equations for all $n$ determine $|\alpha; t\rangle_S$, so nothing was lost.
>
> *3.* With the stated phase, $e^{iH_0t_0/\hbar}|i, t_0; t_0\rangle_S = |i\rangle$, so $|i, t_0; t\rangle_I = \mathscr U_I(t, t_0)|i\rangle$ and $c_n = \langle n|\mathscr U_I|i\rangle$. Since $\langle n|e^{iH_0t/\hbar} = e^{iE_nt/\hbar}\langle n|$ and $e^{-iH_0t_0/\hbar}|i\rangle = e^{-iE_it_0/\hbar}|i\rangle$, $\langle n|\mathscr U_I(t, t_0)|i\rangle = e^{i(E_nt - E_it_0)/\hbar}\langle n|\mathscr U(t, t_0)|i\rangle$, a pure phase times the Schrödinger amplitude. For kets that are not eigenkets of $H_0$ the operators $e^{\pm iH_0t/\hbar}$ do not reduce to phases.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.4.2: Dyson Series in the Interaction Picture
> 1. The interaction-picture evolution operator is the time-ordered exponential of $V_I$,
>
> $$
> \mathscr U_I(t, t_0) = 1 + \sum_{n=1}^\infty\Bigl(\frac{-i}{\hbar}\Bigr)^n\int_{t_0}^tdt_1\int_{t_0}^{t_1}dt_2\cdots\int_{t_0}^{t_{n-1}}dt_n\;V_I(t_1)\,V_I(t_2)\cdots V_I(t_n) = \mathrm T\exp\Bigl[-\frac i\hbar\int_{t_0}^tdt'\,V_I(t')\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> with the $n$-th term bounded in norm by $\bigl(v(t - t_0)/\hbar\bigr)^n/n!$ if $\|V(t')\| \le v$; the $n$-th term is of $n$-th order in the perturbation.
> 2. Expanding $c_n(t) = c_n^{(0)} + c_n^{(1)} + c_n^{(2)} + \cdots$ accordingly, for a system in $|i\rangle$ at $t_0$:
>
> $$
> c_n^{(0)} = \delta_{ni}, \qquad c_n^{(1)}(t) = -\frac i\hbar\int_{t_0}^tdt'\;e^{i\omega_{ni}t'}\,V_{ni}(t') ,
> $$
>
> $$
> c_n^{(2)}(t) = \Bigl(-\frac i\hbar\Bigr)^2\sum_m\int_{t_0}^tdt'\int_{t_0}^{t'}dt''\;e^{i\omega_{nm}t'}\,V_{nm}(t')\;e^{i\omega_{mi}t''}\,V_{mi}(t'') ,
> $$
>
> and the transition probability for $n \ne i$ is $P(i \to n) = \bigl|c_n^{(1)}(t) + c_n^{(2)}(t) + \cdots\bigr|^2$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.7.1–5.7.2, eqs. (5.260)–(5.278) (convergence set aside there) · Griffiths §11.1.2, eqs. (11.19)–(11.25); Problem 11.23 (Dyson's formula) · Likharev §6.5, eq. (6.90)*

^thm-c9-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Theorem §C9.4.1 gives $\mathscr U_I$ as the solution of $i\hbar\,\partial_t\mathscr U_I = V_I\mathscr U_I$, $\mathscr U_I(t_0, t_0) = 1$, the same initial-value problem as for $\mathscr U$ with $H(t)$ replaced by $V_I(t)$. The proof of [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]], case 3, used nothing else, and is rigorous for bounded $V$ and formal otherwise, as there (iteration of the integral equation $\mathscr U_I = 1 - \frac i\hbar\int_{t_0}^tV_I(t')\,\mathscr U_I(t', t_0)\,dt'$, the volume $(t - t_0)^n/n!$ of the ordered region, and the rearrangement into the time-ordered form), so it applies verbatim; $\|V_I(t')\| = \|V(t')\|$ because conjugation by a unitary preserves the norm.
>
> *2.* Take $\langle n|\cdots|i\rangle$ of the series (Theorem §C9.4.1, 3). The first-order term is $-\frac i\hbar\int\langle n|V_I(t')|i\rangle\,dt'$ with $\langle n|V_I(t')|i\rangle = e^{i\omega_{ni}t'}V_{ni}(t')$. In the second-order term insert $1 = \sum_m|m\rangle\langle m|$ between the two factors $V_I(t')V_I(t'')$ ($t'' \le t'$). Grouping the terms by order in $V$ gives the $c_n^{(k)}$.

*Uses:* [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-1|Theorem §C9.4.1]], [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why expand in the interaction picture; reading the terms
> - **What the interaction picture buys.** [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]] already wrote $\mathscr U$ as a Dyson series, but in powers of the full $H$, which is not small; truncating it makes no sense. Here the large, known part $H_0$ is solved exactly and kept in the phases $e^{i\omega_{nm}t}$, and only $V$ is expanded. Sakurai stresses that this operator form, rather than Dirac's 1927 iteration of the $c_n$ equations, is what carries over to many-body theory and quantum electrodynamics, where Dyson introduced it.
> - **Pictures of the terms** (Griffiths, Figs. 11.1–11.3). Read $c_n^{(1)}$ from right to left: the system stays in $|i\rangle$ from $t_0$ to $t'$, picking up the phase $e^{-iE_it'/\hbar}$, jumps to $|n\rangle$ at $t'$ with amplitude $-\frac i\hbar V_{ni}(t')$, and stays there; every $t'$ contributes coherently. In $c_n^{(2)}$ the system passes through an intermediate state $|m\rangle$ between $t''$ and $t'$, whose energy need not equal $E_i$ or $E_n$: a **virtual transition**. The amplitudes of all routes add before squaring, so they can interfere.
> - **Probability.** Truncated amplitudes violate $\sum_n|c_n|^2 = 1$ at the order neglected: $|c_i^{(0)} + c_i^{(1)}|^2 = 1$ only to first order (Griffiths).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.7.1, footnote 11 · Griffiths §11.1.2*

^rem-c9-4-1

> [!caution] Caution: Three ways to write a two-level perturbation
> Griffiths writes a two-level problem as $\Psi = c_a\psi_ae^{-iE_at/\hbar} + c_b\psi_be^{-iE_bt/\hbar}$ with $H'_{ab} = \langle\psi_a|H'|\psi_b\rangle$, $\omega_0 = (E_b - E_a)/\hbar$, and a sinusoidal perturbation as $H' = V\cos\omega t$. Sakurai writes a harmonic perturbation as $\mathcal V e^{i\omega t} + \mathcal V^\dagger e^{-i\omega t}$, so $V\cos\omega t$ has $\mathcal V = \mathcal V^\dagger = V/2$, and every Griffiths formula with $|V_{ab}|^2$ becomes one with $4|\mathcal V^\dagger_{ba}|^2$. Likharev uses $\hat A e^{-i\omega t} + \hat A^\dagger e^{i\omega t}$, so his $\hat A$ is Sakurai's $\mathcal V^\dagger$, and he calls the amplitudes $a_n(t)$ and the occupancies $W_n = |a_n|^2$. The physics is the same; only the factors of 2 and the signs of $\omega$ move.

## Constant and harmonic perturbations

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.4.3: A Constant Perturbation Switched On
> Let $V(t) = 0$ for $t < 0$ and $V(t) = V$ (time independent) for $t \ge 0$, and let the system be in $|i\rangle$ at $t_0 = 0$. To first order, for $n \ne i$,
>
> $$
> c_n^{(1)}(t) = \frac{V_{ni}}{E_n - E_i}\bigl(1 - e^{i\omega_{ni}t}\bigr), \qquad \bigl|c_n^{(1)}(t)\bigr|^2 = \frac{4|V_{ni}|^2}{|E_n - E_i|^2}\,\sin^2\Bigl[\frac{(E_n - E_i)\,t}{2\hbar}\Bigr] = \frac{|V_{ni}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{\sin^2(\omega_{ni}t/2)}{(\omega_{ni}/2)^2} .
> $$
>
> 1. For fixed $n$ with $E_n \ne E_i$ the probability oscillates and never exceeds $4|V_{ni}|^2/|E_n - E_i|^2$.
> 2. For $E_n = E_i$ it grows as $|V_{ni}|^2t^2/\hbar^2$.
> 3. As a function of $\omega_{ni}$ at fixed $t$, it is a peak of height $|V_{ni}|^2t^2/\hbar^2$ at $\omega_{ni} = 0$ with first zeros at $\omega_{ni} = \pm2\pi/t$: after the perturbation has acted for a time $t$, only final states with $|E_n - E_i| \lesssim 2\pi\hbar/t$ are appreciably populated, $\Delta t\,\Delta E \sim \hbar$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.7.3, eqs. (5.279)–(5.285), Fig. 5.9 · Griffiths Problem 11.3*

^thm-c9-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In Theorem §C9.4.2 with $t_0 = 0$ and constant $V_{ni}$: $c_n^{(1)} = -\frac i\hbar V_{ni}\int_0^te^{i\omega_{ni}t'}dt' = -\frac{V_{ni}}{\hbar\omega_{ni}}\bigl(e^{i\omega_{ni}t} - 1\bigr)$, and $\hbar\omega_{ni} = E_n - E_i$. Then $|1 - e^{ix}|^2 = 2 - 2\cos x = 4\sin^2(x/2)$. Part 2 is the limit $\omega_{ni} \to 0$, using $\sin y/y \to 1$; part 3 reads off the zeros of $\sin(\omega_{ni}t/2)$.

^der-c9-4-3

*Uses:* [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-2|Theorem §C9.4.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Which energy–time relation this is
> Sakurai stresses that the $\Delta t\,\Delta E \sim \hbar$ of Theorem §C9.4.3 is not an uncertainty relation between observables: $\Delta t$ is how long the perturbation has acted, $\Delta E$ how far from $E_i$ the final energies may lie, and time is a parameter. A short perturbation tolerates "energy nonconservation" by a large amount; a long one enforces $E_n \approx E_i$. It belongs with the correlation-amplitude reading of [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-7|Theorem §C3.1.7]] ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^rem-c3-1-4|Remark: Two readings of the energy–time relation]]): in both, a Fourier transform in time trades duration for spread in energy. Level B's Mandelstam–Tamm inequality ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-9|Theorem §B2.4.9]]) is a third, sharper statement. The quadratic growth in part 2 looks alarming for a probability; what is measured in practice is the sum over a quasi-continuum of final states, which grows linearly in $t$: that is the golden rule, [[§C9.5 Fermi's Golden Rule#^thm-c9-5-1|Theorem §C9.5.1]].
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.7.3, eqs. (5.284)–(5.285)*

^rem-c9-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.4.4: Harmonic Perturbation; Absorption and Stimulated Emission
> Let $V(t) = \mathcal V e^{i\omega t} + \mathcal V^\dagger e^{-i\omega t}$ ($\omega > 0$, $\mathcal V$ any operator, so that $V$ is Hermitian) act from $t = 0$ on a system in $|i\rangle$. To first order,
>
> $$
> c_n^{(1)}(t) = \frac1\hbar\Bigl[\frac{1 - e^{i(\omega_{ni} + \omega)t}}{\omega_{ni} + \omega}\,\mathcal V_{ni} + \frac{1 - e^{i(\omega_{ni} - \omega)t}}{\omega_{ni} - \omega}\,\mathcal V^\dagger_{ni}\Bigr], \qquad \mathcal V^\dagger_{ni} \equiv \langle n|\mathcal V^\dagger|i\rangle .
> $$
>
> This is the constant-perturbation result with $\omega_{ni} \to \omega_{ni} \pm \omega$, so for large $t$ a transition is appreciable only if
> - $E_n \approx E_i - \hbar\omega$ (**stimulated emission**, from the $\mathcal Ve^{i\omega t}$ term; possible only if $|i\rangle$ is excited), or
> - $E_n \approx E_i + \hbar\omega$ (**absorption**, from the $\mathcal V^\dagger e^{-i\omega t}$ term).
>
> Near the absorption resonance, $|\omega_{ni} - \omega| \ll \omega_{ni} + \omega$, the other term is negligible and
>
> $$
> P_{i\to n}(t) \approx \frac{4|\mathcal V^\dagger_{ni}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{\sin^2\bigl[(\omega_{ni} - \omega)t/2\bigr]}{(\omega_{ni} - \omega)^2} .
> $$
>
> The two processes have equal strength: $|\mathcal V_{in}|^2 = |\mathcal V^\dagger_{ni}|^2$, so the matrix element for stimulated emission $n \to i$ equals that for absorption $i \to n$. The perturbation is an inexhaustible source or sink of energy $\hbar\omega$; the system alone does not conserve energy.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.7.4, eqs. (5.298)–(5.305), Fig. 5.12 · Griffiths §11.1.3, eqs. (11.29)–(11.35); §11.2.2 · Likharev §6.5, eqs. (6.86)–(6.91)*

^thm-c9-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By Theorem §C9.4.2, $c_n^{(1)} = -\frac i\hbar\int_0^t\bigl(\mathcal V_{ni}e^{i(\omega_{ni} + \omega)t'} + \mathcal V^\dagger_{ni}e^{i(\omega_{ni} - \omega)t'}\bigr)dt'$, and each integral is the one of Theorem §C9.4.3 with the shifted frequency. By that theorem each term is large only within $\sim 2\pi/t$ of its zero, and the two zeros $\omega_{ni} = \mp\omega$ are $2\omega$ apart; once $t \gg 1/\omega$ at most one term matters. Keeping the second, $|1 - e^{ixt}|^2/x^2 = 4\sin^2(xt/2)/x^2$ with $x = \omega_{ni} - \omega$ gives $P$. Finally $\langle i|\mathcal V|n\rangle = \langle n|\mathcal V^\dagger|i\rangle^*$ ([[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-2|Theorem §C1.2.2]]), so $|\mathcal V_{in}| = |\mathcal V^\dagger_{ni}|$.

> [!caution] Caution: First order fails at resonance
> Near resonance the first-order probability grows to $|\mathcal V^\dagger_{ni}|^2t^2/\hbar^2$, which exceeds $1$ for $t > \hbar/|\mathcal V^\dagger_{ni}|$; the expansion holds only while $P \ll 1$, i.e. for $|\mathcal V^\dagger_{ni}|\,t/\hbar \ll 1$ at resonance, or for all $t$ if the detuning satisfies $|\omega_{ni} - \omega| \gg |\mathcal V^\dagger_{ni}|/\hbar$ (Likharev's condition (6.92)). The exact two-level solution in the rotating-wave approximation, Rabi's formula ([[§C9.6 The Interaction Picture and the Atom–Field Interaction#^thm-c9-6-3|Theorem §C9.6.3]]; for a rotating field exactly, [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-6|Theorem §B6.2.6]]), shows what happens instead: the population flops back and forth at a frequency that grows with the strength of the drive, and never exceeds $1$. The figure compares the two.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.1.3 and Problem 11.9 · Likharev §6.5, eqs. (6.92), (6.101)–(6.102) · Sakurai Problem 5.37*

## Sudden changes

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.4.5: Sudden Approximation
> Let $H(t)$ change from $H_{\text{i}}$ (for $t \le 0$) to $H_{\text{f}}$ (for $t \ge T$), and let $c$ be any real constant (a shift of the energy zero, which changes only an overall phase). Then for every state
>
> $$
> \bigl\|\mathscr U(T, 0)|\alpha\rangle - e^{-icT/\hbar}|\alpha\rangle\bigr\| \le \frac T\hbar\,\sup_{0\le t\le T}\|H(t) - c\| ,
> $$
>
> where the norm of $H - c$ is taken on the states that matter (for bounded $H$, the operator norm). So if the change takes a time $T$ short compared with $\hbar/\Delta E$, $\Delta E$ the spread of the relevant energies (Sakurai: $T \ll 2\pi/\omega_{ab}$ for the relevant level spacings $\hbar\omega_{ab}$), the state does not change during the switch: $|\alpha(T)\rangle \approx |\alpha(0)\rangle$ up to a phase. A system in the eigenket $|i\rangle$ of $H_{\text{i}}$ is afterwards found in the eigenket $|n_{\text{f}}\rangle$ of $H_{\text{f}}$ with probability
>
> $$
> P(i \to n_{\text{f}}) = \bigl|\langle n_{\text{f}}|i\rangle\bigr|^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.6.1, eqs. (5.212)–(5.213) (the scaled-time argument; the bound is derived here) · Griffiths §11.5.2, Example 11.3 (the suddenly expanded well)*

^thm-c9-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\mathscr U_c(t) = e^{ict/\hbar}\mathscr U(t, 0)$ is the evolution operator for $H - c$ (it obeys $i\hbar\,\partial_t\mathscr U_c = (H - c)\mathscr U_c$, [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]]), so $\mathscr U_c(T) - 1 = -\frac i\hbar\int_0^T(H(t) - c)\,\mathscr U_c(t)\,dt$. Since $\mathscr U_c(t)$ is unitary, the norm of the integrand applied to $|\alpha\rangle$ is at most $\|H(t) - c\|$, and integrating gives the bound; multiply by the phase $e^{-icT/\hbar}$. Sakurai reaches the same conclusion by writing $t = sT$: in the dimensionless time $s$ the Schrödinger equation reads $i\,\partial_s\mathscr U = (HT/\hbar)\mathscr U$, and $HT/\hbar \to 0$ as $T \to 0$. For $t \ge T$ the system evolves under $H_{\text{f}}$; expanding $|i\rangle = \sum_{\text{f}}|n_{\text{f}}\rangle\langle n_{\text{f}}|i\rangle$ and applying the measurement postulate ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]]) gives the probability.

^der-c9-4-5

*Uses:* [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]], [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: What a sudden change does to the energy
> The state survives the jump, but it is in general not an eigenstate of $H_{\text{f}}$: the energy is no longer sharp, and $\langle H_{\text{f}}\rangle = \langle i|H_{\text{f}}|i\rangle$ need not equal $E_{\text{i}}$, since the agent that changes $H$ can do work. In Griffiths' example of an infinite well whose wall is suddenly moved out, $H_{\text{f}}$ and $H_{\text{i}}$ agree wherever the old wave function lives, so $\langle H_{\text{f}}\rangle = E_{\text{i}}$: no work is done, as in the free expansion of a gas, yet a measurement can return a lower or a higher eigenvalue. If the same wall is moved slowly, the particle ends in the ground state of the wide well and energy is extracted (the adiabatic theorem below), as by a slowly withdrawn piston.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.5.2, Example 11.3; Problem 11.18*

## Slow changes: the adiabatic theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.4.6: Adiabatic Theorem
> Let $H(t)$ have, for every $t$, a discrete spectrum $H(t)|n; t\rangle = E_n(t)|n; t\rangle$ with orthonormal eigenkets depending smoothly on $t$, and let the level $E_n(t)$ stay nondegenerate (no crossing with other levels). Expand $|\alpha; t\rangle = \sum_nc_n(t)\,e^{i\theta_n(t)}|n; t\rangle$ with the **dynamical phase** $\theta_n(t) = -\frac1\hbar\int_0^tE_n(t')\,dt'$. Then:
> 1. *exactly,*
>
> $$
> \dot c_m = -c_m\,\langle m; t|\partial_t|m; t\rangle - \sum_{n\ne m}c_n\,e^{i(\theta_n - \theta_m)}\,\frac{\langle m; t|\dot H|n; t\rangle}{E_n - E_m} ;
> $$
>
> 2. if $H$ changes slowly enough that
>
> $$
> \hbar\,\bigl|\langle m; t|\dot H|n; t\rangle\bigr| \ll (E_n - E_m)^2 \qquad (m \ne n) ,
> $$
>
> a system that starts in $|n; 0\rangle$ stays in the instantaneous eigenstate $|n; t\rangle$:
>
> $$
> |\alpha; t\rangle \simeq e^{i\theta_n(t)}\,e^{i\gamma_n(t)}\,|n; t\rangle, \qquad \gamma_n(t) = i\int_0^t\langle n; t'|\,\partial_{t'}|n; t'\rangle\,dt' ,
> $$
>
> with the leakage into each $|m; t\rangle$ of order $\hbar|\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle|/(E_n - E_m)^2$. The extra phase $\gamma_n$ is real; it is the **geometric phase** (Definition §C9.4.2 below).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.6.2, eqs. (5.214)–(5.227) (following Griffiths' earlier edition) · Griffiths §11.5.2, eqs. (11.92)–(11.94) (statement; the proof, "not easy", is not in the 3rd edition) · Likharev §6.5, eq. (6.74); the leakage estimate by integration by parts is added here*

^thm-c9-4-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Insert the expansion into $i\hbar\,\partial_t|\alpha; t\rangle = H(t)|\alpha; t\rangle$. Since $H|n; t\rangle = E_n|n; t\rangle$ and $\hbar\dot\theta_n = -E_n$, so that $i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}e^{i\theta_n} = E_ne^{i\theta_n}$, the terms with $E_n$ cancel and
>
> $$
> \sum_ne^{i\theta_n}\bigl[\dot c_n|n; t\rangle + c_n\,\partial_t|n; t\rangle\bigr] = 0 .
> $$
>
> Apply $\langle m; t|$ (orthonormality at equal times): $\dot c_m = -\sum_nc_ne^{i(\theta_n - \theta_m)}\langle m; t|\partial_t|n; t\rangle$. For $m \ne n$, differentiate $H|n; t\rangle = E_n|n; t\rangle$ and apply $\langle m; t|$: $\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle + E_m\langle m|\partial_tn\rangle = E_n\langle m|\partial_tn\rangle$, so $\langle m|\partial_tn\rangle = \langle m|\dot H|n\rangle/(E_n - E_m)$. This gives 1.
>
> *2.* *The leakage.* Start with $c_k(0) = \delta_{kn}$ and suppose the $c_m$, $m \ne n$, stay small. To first order $\dot c_m \approx -c_n\,e^{i(\theta_n - \theta_m)}\,f(t)$ with $f = \langle m|\dot H|n\rangle/(E_n - E_m)$, $|c_n| \approx 1$. The phase $\varphi = \theta_n - \theta_m$ turns at the rate $\dot\varphi = -(E_n - E_m)/\hbar$. Integrate by parts, $\int_0^tf\,e^{i\varphi}dt' = \bigl[\frac{f}{i\dot\varphi}e^{i\varphi}\bigr]_0^t - \int_0^t\frac{d}{dt'}\Bigl(\frac{f}{i\dot\varphi}\Bigr)e^{i\varphi}dt'$: the boundary term has modulus $|f/\dot\varphi| = \hbar|\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle|/(E_n - E_m)^2 \equiv \epsilon$, small by assumption. The remaining integrand, which involves $\ddot H$ and $\dot E$, is of order $\epsilon/T$ if $H$ changes on the time scale $T$; bounded crudely over a time $T$ it would be of order $\epsilon$ as well, but it multiplies the same fast phase, and a second integration by parts makes it smaller than $\epsilon$ by the factor $\hbar/(T|E_n - E_m|) \ll 1$. (The fast-turning phase averages the source term away; this is why the condition compares $\dot H$ with the square of a gap.) Neglecting the sum in 1 is the adiabatic approximation.
>
> *The phase.* Then $\dot c_n = -c_n\langle n|\partial_tn\rangle$, so $c_n(t) = e^{i\gamma_n(t)}c_n(0)$ with $\gamma_n = i\int_0^t\langle n|\partial_{t'}n\rangle dt'$. Differentiating $\langle n|n\rangle = 1$ gives $\langle\partial_tn|n\rangle + \langle n|\partial_tn\rangle = 0$: $\langle n|\partial_tn\rangle$ is purely imaginary and $\gamma_n$ real. *Check:* for constant $H$ choose $|n; t\rangle = |n\rangle$; then $\gamma_n = 0$ and $|\alpha; t\rangle = e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle$, the stationary state. (Sakurai checks this with $|n; t\rangle = e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle$, a choice that puts the dynamical phase into the kets; then $\gamma_n = E_nt/\hbar$ cancels $\theta_n$, the same state.)

^der-c9-4-6

*Uses:* [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Sakurai's statement of the adiabatic condition
> Sakurai's eq. (5.222) states the condition as $|\langle m; t|\dot H|n; t\rangle|/E_{nm} \equiv 1/\tau \ll \langle m; t|[\partial_t|m; t\rangle] \sim E_m/\hbar$. The quantity $\langle m; t|\partial_t|m; t\rangle$ on the right is the Berry-connection term of the same equation, which depends on the phase convention of the eigenkets and is not of order $E_m/\hbar$ in general. What the derivation compares $1/\tau$ with is the transition frequency $|\omega_{nm}| = |E_n - E_m|/\hbar$, giving the condition of Theorem §C9.4.6, 2; in words (Sakurai's own, and Griffiths'), the Hamiltonian must change slowly compared with the internal frequencies of the system.

^cau-c9-4-3

> [!remark] Remark: Adiabatic, classically and thermally
> - **Two time scales** (Griffiths §11.5.1). A pendulum whose support is moved gently keeps swinging in a parallel plane with the same amplitude; carried around a closed loop on a sphere, its plane comes back turned by the enclosed solid angle (the Foucault pendulum is this, with the Earth's rotation doing the carrying; its precession rate is [[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^thm-b9-2-5|CM Theorem §B9.2.5]], there from the Coriolis force). The internal period must be short compared with the time over which the external parameters change. The quantum analogue of the turned plane is Berry's phase.
> - **Adiabatic invariants** (Likharev §6.5). In the WKB limit the action $\oint p\,dr$ of a quantized orbit is an adiabatic invariant: the theorem keeps the quantum number $n$, and Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-4|Theorem §B9.3.4]]) ties $n$ to the action. Likharev remarks that the classical proof of the same invariance is harder; classical action–angle variables and adiabatic invariants are Classical Mechanics level C (planned; [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation|CM §B8.3]]).
> - **Born–Oppenheimer** (Griffiths). Solving for the electrons with the nuclei clamped, then letting the nuclei move slowly on the resulting energy curve, as in level B's hydrogen molecule ion ([[§B9.2★ Helium and the Hydrogen Molecule#^mod-b9-2-3|Model §B9.2.3]]), is the adiabatic theorem applied to the electrons.
> - **Thermodynamics.** In thermodynamics "adiabatic" means "without heat exchange", a different definition. The two meet: a slow quantum change keeps every occupation number, so the entropy, a function of the occupations, is unchanged. This is the step "every mode keeps its occupation" in the adiabatic expansion of black-body radiation ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-7|TH Theorem §B11.1.7]]).
> - **Level B's slow turning.** The exact precessing-field solution showed that a spin follows a slowly turning field, with transition probability at most $(\omega/\omega_1)^2\sin^2\theta$ ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^ex-b6-2-2|Example §B6.2.2]], "the adiabatic regime, level C"). Theorem §C9.4.6 is the general statement; Example §C9.4.3 extracts the phase as well.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.5.1, Figs. 11.12–11.14, eq. (11.89) · Likharev §6.5, eq. (6.75) and footnote 23*

> [!definition] Definition §C9.4.2: Berry Connection, Berry Curvature, Geometric Phase
> Let $H = H(\mathbf R)$ depend on parameters $\mathbf R = (R_1, R_2, \dots)$, with nondegenerate eigenkets $|n(\mathbf R)\rangle$, $H(\mathbf R)|n(\mathbf R)\rangle = E_n(\mathbf R)|n(\mathbf R)\rangle$, and let $\mathbf R(t)$ trace a closed curve $C$ slowly, $\mathbf R(T) = \mathbf R(0)$. The **Berry connection** (vector potential) of level $n$, its **curvature** (in three-dimensional parameter space) and the **geometric (Berry's) phase** of the loop are
>
> $$
> \mathbf A_n(\mathbf R) = i\,\langle n(\mathbf R)|\,\nabla_{\mathbf R}\,|n(\mathbf R)\rangle, \qquad \mathbf B_n(\mathbf R) = \nabla_{\mathbf R}\times\mathbf A_n(\mathbf R), \qquad \gamma_n(C) = \oint_C\mathbf A_n\cdot d\mathbf R ,
> $$
>
> the last being the phase $\gamma_n(T)$ of Theorem §C9.4.6 for one circuit, since $\langle n|\partial_tn\rangle = \langle n|\nabla_{\mathbf R}n\rangle\cdot\dot{\mathbf R}$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.6.3, eqs. (5.231)–(5.236) · Griffiths §11.5.2, eq. (11.95)*

^def-c9-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.4.7: Berry's Phase Is Geometric and Gauge Invariant
> 1. $\mathbf A_n$, $\mathbf B_n$ and $\gamma_n(C)$ are real.
> 2. Under a change of phase of the eigenkets, $|n(\mathbf R)\rangle \to e^{i\delta(\mathbf R)}|n(\mathbf R)\rangle$ with $\delta$ single valued, $\mathbf A_n \to \mathbf A_n - \nabla_{\mathbf R}\delta$, the form of a gauge transformation of the electromagnetic vector potential ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-5|Theorem §C4.3.5]]); $\mathbf B_n$ and $\gamma_n(C)$ are unchanged. So $\gamma_n(C)$ depends only on the path $C$ in parameter space, not on how fast it is traversed or on phase conventions: it is a property of the geometry. By Stokes's theorem $\gamma_n(C) = \int_S\mathbf B_n\cdot d\mathbf a$ over any surface $S$ bounded by $C$.
> 3. In a form that needs no differentiation of kets,
>
> $$
> \mathbf B_n(\mathbf R) = i\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{\langle n|\nabla_{\mathbf R}H|m\rangle\times\langle m|\nabla_{\mathbf R}H|n\rangle}{(E_m - E_n)^2} ,
> $$
>
> which is large near points of parameter space where $E_m(\mathbf R) = E_n(\mathbf R)$: these act as sources of $\mathbf B_n$ and contribute to $\gamma_n(C)$ although the path avoids them.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.6.3, eqs. (5.237)–(5.243) · Griffiths §11.5.2, eq. (11.95) and footnote 37*

^thm-c9-4-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\langle n|\nabla n\rangle$ is imaginary by the argument of Theorem §C9.4.6 (differentiate $\langle n|n\rangle = 1$), so $\mathbf A_n$ is real, and so are its curl and line integral.
>
> *2.* $i\langle n|e^{-i\delta}\nabla(e^{i\delta}|n\rangle) = i\langle n|\nabla n\rangle + i\cdot i\nabla\delta = \mathbf A_n - \nabla\delta$. A gradient has zero curl, and around a closed loop $\oint\nabla\delta\cdot d\mathbf R = 0$ because $\delta$ is single valued (a multivalued $\delta$ that returns increased by $2\pi k$ changes $\gamma_n$ by $2\pi k$, invisible in $e^{i\gamma_n}$). Stokes's theorem in the parameter space gives the surface form; Sakurai notes that for more than three parameters it needs the generalized Stokes theorem of differential forms.
>
> *3.* Since $\nabla\times\nabla(\cdot) = 0$, $\mathbf B_n = i\,\nabla\times\langle n|\nabla n\rangle = i\,(\nabla\langle n|)\times(\nabla|n\rangle)$. Insert $1 = \sum_m|m\rangle\langle m|$ between the two factors. The $m = n$ term vanishes: $\langle n|\nabla n\rangle$ is imaginary, so $(\nabla\langle n|)|n\rangle = -\langle n|\nabla n\rangle$ is parallel to it, and the cross product of parallel vectors is zero. For $m \ne n$, differentiating the eigenvalue equation as in Theorem §C9.4.6 gives $\langle m|\nabla n\rangle = \langle m|\nabla H|n\rangle/(E_n - E_m)$, and its conjugate gives $(\nabla\langle n|)|m\rangle = \langle n|\nabla H|m\rangle/(E_n - E_m)$; the product has denominator $(E_n - E_m)^2$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.4.8: Berry's Phase for Spin One-Half
> A spin $\tfrac12$ with magnetic moment $\mu$ in a magnetic field $\mathbf R(t)$ of nonzero magnitude, $H = -\frac{2\mu}\hbar\,\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf R(t)$, has $E_\pm = \mp\mu R$ for the states $|\pm\rangle$ along $\hat{\mathbf R}$, and Berry curvature
>
> $$
> \mathbf B_\pm(\mathbf R) = \mp\frac{\hat{\mathbf R}}{2R^2} ,
> $$
>
> the field of a "monopole" of strength $\mp\frac12$ at the degeneracy point $\mathbf R = 0$. If the field direction is carried slowly around a closed loop that subtends the solid angle $\Omega$ at $\mathbf R = 0$,
>
> $$
> \gamma_\pm(C) = \mp\frac12\,\Omega ,
> $$
>
> independent of $\mu$, of the field strength and of the shape of the loop. For a field on a cone of half-angle $\theta$ about $\hat z$, $\Omega = 2\pi(1 - \cos\theta)$ and $\gamma_+ = -\pi(1 - \cos\theta)$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.6.4, eqs. (5.244)–(5.253), Fig. 5.7 (neutron measurements of Richardson et al., 1988); Problem 5.44 (the direct route, solved here) · Griffiths §11.5.2, Example 11.4; Problem 11.21*

^thm-c9-4-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Through the curvature.* $\nabla_{\mathbf R}H = -\frac{2\mu}\hbar\mathbf S$ and $(E_\pm - E_\mp)^2 = 4\mu^2R^2$, and the sum in Theorem §C9.4.7, 3 has one term. By rotational symmetry evaluate it with $\hat{\mathbf R} = \hat z$, where $|\pm\rangle$ are $S_z$ eigenkets. From $\mathbf S = \frac12(S_+ + S_-)\hat x + \frac1{2i}(S_+ - S_-)\hat y + S_z\hat z$, $\langle\pm|\mathbf S|\mp\rangle = \frac\hbar2(\hat x \mp i\hat y)$, so $\langle\pm|\mathbf S|\mp\rangle\times\langle\mp|\mathbf S|\pm\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}4(\hat x \mp i\hat y)\times(\hat x \pm i\hat y) = \pm\frac{i\hbar^2}2\hat z$. Then $\mathbf B_\pm = i\cdot\frac{4\mu^2}{\hbar^2}\cdot\frac{\pm i\hbar^2/2}{4\mu^2R^2}\hat z = \mp\frac{\hat z}{2R^2}$, and for general direction $\hat z \to \hat{\mathbf R}$. The flux through a surface spanning $C$ is $\mp\frac12\int\hat{\mathbf R}\cdot d\mathbf a/R^2 = \mp\frac12\Omega$.
>
> *Directly (Sakurai Problem 5.44).* For $\hat{\mathbf R}$ at polar angle $\theta$ and azimuth $\phi$, the spin-up ket of [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-2|Theorem §C5.2.2]] is $(\cos\frac\theta2e^{-i\phi/2}, \sin\frac\theta2e^{i\phi/2})$, which changes sign when $\phi \to \phi + 2\pi$; multiply by $e^{i\phi/2}$ to get the single-valued choice $|+\rangle \doteq (\cos\frac\theta2, e^{i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2)$ (valid away from $\theta = \pi$). Then $\langle+|\partial_\phi+\rangle = i\sin^2\frac\theta2$ and $\langle+|\partial_\theta+\rangle = 0$. Around the cone at fixed $\theta$, $\gamma_+ = i\oint\langle+|\partial_\phi+\rangle d\phi = -2\pi\sin^2\frac\theta2 = -\pi(1 - \cos\theta) = -\frac12\Omega$. The connection is $\mathbf A_+ = -\frac{\sin^2(\theta/2)}{R\sin\theta}\hat\phi$, whose curl is $-\hat{\mathbf R}/2R^2$ away from the negative $z$-axis, where this gauge is singular, like a Dirac string.

*Uses:* [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-7|Theorem §C9.4.7]], [[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-2|Theorem §C5.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Berry's phase, Aharonov–Bohm and the monopole
> - **Aharonov–Bohm as a Berry phase** (Sakurai §5.6.5, after Holstein). Confine an electron in a small box at position $\mathbf R$ and carry the box around a flux line $\Phi_B$ it never touches. With the vector potential, the box states are $\langle\mathbf r|n(\mathbf R)\rangle = \exp\bigl\{\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\int_{\mathbf R}^{\mathbf r}\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf r'\bigr\}\psi_n(\mathbf r - \mathbf R)$, so $\langle n|\nabla_{\mathbf R}n\rangle = -ie\mathbf A(\mathbf R)/\hbar c$, and the geometric phase of one circuit is $\gamma_n = \frac e{\hbar c}\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf R = \frac{e\Phi_B}{\hbar c}$: the Aharonov–Bohm phase of [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-7|Theorem §C4.3.7]], here with the box's position as the slow parameter.
> - **Monopoles.** The curvature of Theorem §C9.4.8 is a monopole field in parameter space. Requiring the measurable $e^{i\gamma}$ to be the same for two surfaces spanning the same loop gives $\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a = 2\pi N$ over any closed surface ($N = \mp1$ for the states $|\pm\rangle$ of spin $\frac12$; $N = -2m$ for the state of spin projection $m\hbar$ along $\hat{\mathbf R}$ in general), and Sakurai rederives Dirac's quantization condition $2ee_M/\hbar c = N$ of [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^thm-c4-3-8|Theorem §C4.3.8]] in this language.
> - **Experiment.** The geometric phase was measured with polarized slow and ultracold neutrons in a rotating field (Bitter and Dubbers 1987; Richardson et al. 1988): the measured $\gamma_\pm$ follow $\mp\Omega/2$, and the sense of rotation confirms that the neutron's magnetic moment is negative (Sakurai Fig. 5.7). It is also seen with photons in a helically wound optical fibre (Tomita and Chiao 1986).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.6.3–5.6.5, eqs. (5.254)–(5.259), Figs. 5.7–5.8*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C9.4.1: A Hydrogen Atom in a Decaying Field
> A hydrogen atom in its ground state $|1\,0\,0\rangle$ sits between capacitor plates. The field is $\mathbf E = 0$ for $t < 0$ and $\mathbf E = E_0e^{-t/\tau}\hat z$ for $t > 0$. To first order, find the probability of finding the atom at $t \gg \tau$ in each $n = 2$ state, and discuss $\tau \to \infty$ and $\tau \to 0$.
>
> *Perturbation and selection.* The electron (charge $-|e|$) has $V(t) = |e|E_0e^{-t/\tau}z$ for $t > 0$. The $2s$ and $2p_{\pm1}$ amplitudes vanish: $\langle2\,0\,0|z|1\,0\,0\rangle = 0$ by parity and $\langle2\,1\,{\pm1}|z|1\,0\,0\rangle = 0$ because $z$ carries $q = 0$ ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-7|Theorem §C7.2.7]]). Only $2p_0$ is reached, with $z_{21} \equiv \langle2\,1\,0|z|1\,0\,0\rangle = \frac{128\sqrt2}{243}a_0 = 0.745\,a_0$ ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^ex-c7-2-2|Example §C7.2.2]]).
>
> *Amplitude.* With $\omega \equiv \omega_{21} = (E_2 - E_1)/\hbar = \frac38\,e^2/\hbar a_0$, Theorem §C9.4.2 gives for $t \gg \tau$
>
> $$
> c^{(1)}_{2p_0} = -\frac i\hbar|e|E_0z_{21}\int_0^\infty e^{i\omega t' - t'/\tau}dt' = -\frac{i|e|E_0z_{21}}{\hbar\,(1/\tau - i\omega)}, \qquad P(1s \to 2p_0) = \frac{e^2E_0^2\,z_{21}^2}{\hbar^2\,(\omega^2 + \tau^{-2})} .
> $$
>
> *Long decay, $\omega\tau \gg 1$:* $P \to \bigl(|e|E_0z_{21}/(E_2 - E_1)\bigr)^2$, which does not vanish. The field is switched on suddenly, so just after $t = 0$ the atom is still in $|1s\rangle$ (Theorem §C9.4.5); expanded in the eigenstates of $H_0 + |e|E_0z$, it has a component of amplitude $\approx |e|E_0z_{21}/(E_1 - E_2)$ along the field-mixed "$2p_0$-like" state (the first-order mixing of time-independent perturbation theory, [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]]). The field then dies slowly, and by Theorem §C9.4.6 the populations of the instantaneous eigenstates no longer change: sudden on, adiabatic off, and the final $n = 2$ population is the squared mixing coefficient.
>
> *Short pulse, $\omega\tau \ll 1$:* $P \approx (|e|E_0\tau\,z_{21}/\hbar)^2 \to 0$ at fixed $E_0$: the impulse $\int|e|E\,dt = |e|E_0\tau$ is too small to excite. (A pulse switched on and off slowly, on both sides, excites nothing in the adiabatic limit.)
>
> *Validity.* First order needs $P \ll 1$: $|e|E_0z_{21} \ll \hbar\sqrt{\omega^2 + \tau^{-2}}$. There is a second condition, special to hydrogen: $2s$ and $2p_0$ are degenerate and the field couples them directly, $|\langle2\,0\,0|z|2\,1\,0\rangle| = 3a_0$ (the linear Stark effect, [[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^ex-b8-2-2|Example §B8.2.2]]). That coupling feeds $2s$ from $2p_0$ at second order, at the rate $3|e|E_0a_0/\hbar$ for a time $\sim\tau$, so the first-order answer (and $P(2s) = 0$) also needs $3|e|E_0a_0\tau/\hbar \ll 1$; for a nondegenerate excited level this condition does not arise.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 5.35 (solved here)*

^ex-c9-4-1

> [!example] Example §C9.4.2: Beta Decay of Tritium
> A tritium atom (electron bound to a triton, $Z = 1$) is in its ground state. The triton beta-decays, $^3\mathrm H \to {}^3\mathrm{He}^+ + e^- + \bar\nu$, and the nuclear charge becomes $Z = 2$ abruptly. Find the probability that the atomic electron ends in the ground state of $^3\mathrm{He}^+$, and check the sudden approximation, given that the decay releases about $18\ \mathrm{keV}$ and the atom is about $1$ Å across.
>
> *Overlap.* The hydrogen-like ground state is $\psi_Z = (Z^3/\pi a_0^3)^{1/2}e^{-Zr/a_0}$ ([[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]]). By Theorem §C9.4.5,
>
> $$
> \langle\psi_{Z_2}|\psi_{Z_1}\rangle = \frac{(Z_1Z_2)^{3/2}}{\pi a_0^3}\int_0^\infty4\pi r^2e^{-(Z_1 + Z_2)r/a_0}dr = \frac{(Z_1Z_2)^{3/2}}{\pi a_0^3}\cdot\frac{8\pi a_0^3}{(Z_1 + Z_2)^3} = \frac{8(Z_1Z_2)^{3/2}}{(Z_1 + Z_2)^3} = \frac{16\sqrt2}{27} ,
> $$
>
> so $P(1s \to 1s) = \frac{512}{729} = 0.702$. About 30 % of decays leave the ion excited or ionized; by orthogonality of $\psi_{Z_1}$ (an $\ell = 0$ state) to all $\ell \ne 0$ states, only $s$ states of the ion are populated.
>
> *Is it sudden?* The relevant level spacings of $\mathrm{He}^+$ are of order $E(2) - E(1) = 4\cdot13.6\ \mathrm{eV}\cdot\frac34 = 40.8\ \mathrm{eV}$, a period $h/\Delta E = 1.0\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm s$. The beta electron, with kinetic energy up to $18\ \mathrm{keV}$ ($v \approx 0.26\,c$ at the top), crosses $1$ Å in $T \approx 10^{-10}\ \mathrm m/(0.26 \times 3\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}) \approx 1.3\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm s$, about $1\,\%$ of that period (and typical decay electrons, of a few keV, are only twice slower). The condition $T \ll 2\pi/\omega_{ab}$ holds. Sakurai notes that this calculation matters for the experiments that try to measure the neutrino mass from the end point of the tritium beta spectrum (KATRIN): the final atomic states shift the electron's energy.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.6.1 and Problem 5.42 (solved here)*

> [!example] Example §C9.4.3: Berry's Phase from the Exact Precessing-Field Solution
> A spin $\tfrac12$ with $\gamma > 0$ sits in the field of [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-6|Theorem §B6.2.6]]: magnitude $B_0$, turning about $\hat z$ at angular velocity $\omega > 0$ on a cone of half-angle $\theta$, $H(t) = -\frac\hbar2\omega_1\,\hat n(t)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, $\omega_1 = \gamma B_0 > 0$. It starts spin up along $\hat n(0)$, the lower level, $E = -\hbar\omega_1/2$. Use the exact solution to verify the adiabatic theorem and find the geometric phase after one turn, $T = 2\pi/\omega$, in the limit $\omega \ll \omega_1$.
>
> *Staying in the instantaneous eigenstate.* Level B found the probability of being spin down along $\hat n(t)$ to be $(\omega^2\sin^2\theta/\Omega^2)\sin^2(\Omega t/2)$ with $\Omega^2 = \omega_1^2 + \omega^2 + 2\omega_1\omega\cos\theta$ ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^ex-b6-2-2|Example §B6.2.2]]); its maximum $\omega^2\sin^2\theta/\Omega^2 = (\omega/\omega_1)^2\sin^2\theta\,\bigl(1 + O(\omega/\omega_1)\bigr)$ vanishes in the adiabatic limit. Theorem §C9.4.6 predicts exactly this size: $\dot H = -\frac\hbar2\omega_1\dot{\hat n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with $|\dot{\hat n}| = \omega\sin\theta$ perpendicular to $\hat n$, so $\hbar|\langle-|\dot H|+\rangle| = \frac{\hbar^2}2\omega_1\omega\sin\theta$, and with $(E_+ - E_-)^2 = \hbar^2\omega_1^2$ each of the two boundary terms of the integration by parts has modulus $\omega\sin\theta/2\omega_1$; their sum oscillates between $0$ and $\omega\sin\theta/\omega_1$, the square root of the exact maximum.
>
> *The phase after one turn.* By part 3 of that theorem, $\chi(T) = \mathsf U(T)\bigl[\cos\frac{\Omega T}2 + i\sin\frac{\Omega T}2\,\hat n'\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\bigr]\chi(0)$, with $\mathsf U(T) = \operatorname{diag}(e^{-i\pi}, e^{i\pi}) = -\mathbb 1$. Since $\hat n(T) = \hat n(0)$, the state returns to the same eigenket, and its amplitude there is
>
> $$
> \chi(0)^\dagger\chi(T) = -\Bigl[\cos\frac{\Omega T}2 + i\sin\frac{\Omega T}2\,\hat n'\cdot\hat n(0)\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> For $\omega \ll \omega_1$: $\hat n'\cdot\hat n(0) = (\omega_1 + \omega\cos\theta)/\Omega = 1 - O(\omega^2/\omega_1^2)$, and $\Omega = \omega_1 + \omega\cos\theta + \frac{\omega^2\sin^2\theta}{2\omega_1} + \cdots$, so $\frac{\Omega T}2 = \frac{\omega_1T}2 + \pi\cos\theta + O(\omega/\omega_1)$. The amplitude is $e^{i\pi}e^{i\Omega T/2}$ up to corrections that vanish as $\omega/\omega_1 \to 0$, a total phase $\frac{\omega_1T}2 + \pi\cos\theta + \pi$. Subtract the dynamical phase $\theta_+ = -ET/\hbar = \frac{\omega_1T}2$:
>
> $$
> \gamma_+ = \pi\cos\theta + \pi \equiv -\pi(1 - \cos\theta) \pmod{2\pi} = -\tfrac12\,\Omega_{\text{cone}} ,
> $$
>
> in agreement with Theorem §C9.4.8. The first correction is $+\pi(\omega/\omega_1)\sin^2\theta/2$ (from the $\omega^2$ term of $\Omega$). A direct numerical integration of the Schrödinger equation (computed) gives, for $\theta = 60^\circ$: $\gamma_+ = -1.513$ at $\omega/\omega_1 = 0.05$ and $-1.559$ at $0.01$, approaching $-\pi/2 = -1.571$ as predicted.
>
