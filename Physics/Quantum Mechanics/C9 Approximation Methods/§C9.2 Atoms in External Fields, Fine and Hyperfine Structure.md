What does a static field, or the atom's own relativistic and magnetic corrections, do to atomic levels, and which of these questions does angular-momentum algebra answer without computing? Level B derived the linear Stark effect of hydrogen's $n = 2$ ([[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^ex-b8-2-2|Example §B8.2.2]]), the fine structure ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen]]), the Zeeman effect in weak, strong and intermediate fields and the ground-state hyperfine splitting ([[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting]]); level C proved the Landé factor from the projection theorem ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-8|Theorem §C7.2.8]], [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^ex-c7-2-3|Example §C7.2.3]]). Those formulas, numbers and level diagrams stay where they are. This section adds what needs the machinery of [[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory]] and of tensor operators: the quadratic Stark effect and the polarizability (a closure bound and the exact value by the Dalgarno–Lewis method), the crossover from quadratic to linear Stark effect when levels are nearly degenerate, the radial identities and the Darwin term that complete the fine structure for $s$ states, the Zeeman Hamiltonian from minimal coupling with its diamagnetic term, and hyperfine multiplets of any $J$ and $I$ treated with the projection theorem.

*Notation and units.* Gaussian units as in Sakurai, with the conversion to level B's SI in [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^cau-c6-2-1|Caution: Gaussian and SI units for the Coulomb problem]]. The electron has charge $-e$, $e > 0$ (as in [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem]]); $m$ is its mass, $a_0 = \hbar^2/me^2$, $\alpha = e^2/\hbar c$ the fine-structure constant, $\mu_B = e\hbar/2mc$ the Bohr magneton. A static electric field is $\boldsymbol{\mathcal E} = \mathcal E\hat{\mathbf z}$, a magnetic field $\mathbf B = B\hat{\mathbf z}$. The polarizability is $\alpha_{\text{pol}}$ (as in [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model|WO §B13.1]]) to keep it apart from $\alpha$. Kets $|n\,l\,m\rangle$, $|n\,l\,j\,m\rangle$ are hydrogen-like states ([[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]]).

## The quadratic Stark effect

> [!definition] Definition §C9.2.1: Static Polarizability
> Let an atom in a nondegenerate state $|k^{(0)}\rangle$ be placed in a weak uniform field $\mathcal E\hat{\mathbf z}$. Its **static polarizability** $\alpha_{\text{pol}}$ is defined by the energy shift
>
> $$
> \Delta_k = -\tfrac12\,\alpha_{\text{pol}}\,\mathcal E^2 + O(\mathcal E^3) . \qquad (5.67)
> $$
>
> In Gaussian units $\alpha_{\text{pol}}$ has the dimension of a volume.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.1.5, eq. (5.67)*

^def-c9-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.2.1: The Quadratic Stark Effect
> Let $H_0 = \mathbf p^2/2m + V_0(r)$ be central and $|k^{(0)}\rangle = |n\,l\,m\rangle$ an energy eigenket that is nondegenerate apart from the $2l + 1$ values of $m$ (spin ignored), and let $V = e\mathcal Ez$. Then:
> 1. There is no first-order shift: $z_{kk} = 0$ (5.64). A nondegenerate state has no permanent electric dipole moment.
> 2. The second-order shift is
>
> $$
> \Delta_k = e^2\mathcal E^2\sum_{j\neq k}\frac{|z_{jk}|^2}{E_k^{(0)} - E_j^{(0)}} , \qquad (5.63)
> $$
>
>    where only states with $l' = l \pm 1$ and $m' = m$ contribute (5.65), bound and continuum alike, and $m$ remains a good quantum number to all orders.
> 3. Hence $\alpha_{\text{pol}} = 2e^2\sum_{j\neq k}|z_{jk}|^2/(E_j^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)})$, positive for the ground state (5.68), and the dipole moment induced in the perturbed state is
>
> $$
> \langle d_z\rangle = \langle -ez\rangle = \alpha_{\text{pol}}\,\mathcal E = -\frac{\partial\Delta_k}{\partial\mathcal E} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.1.5, eqs. (5.62)–(5.68), Problem 5.10*

^thm-c9-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. $H_0$ is invariant under parity, so an eigenket whose level is not degenerate with states of the other parity is a parity eigenket (level B: $\psi_{nlm}$ has parity $(-1)^l$; in ket form, [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^thm-c8-2-3|Theorem §C8.2.3]]). $z$ is odd, so $\langle k^{(0)}|z|k^{(0)}\rangle = 0$ by the parity selection rule ([[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^thm-c8-2-5|Theorem §C8.2.5]], 2).
> 2. $z = x_0$ is the $q = 0$ component of the rank-1 tensor $\mathbf x$, so the selection rules of [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-7|Theorem §C7.2.7]] give $m' = m$ and $l' = l \pm 1$ (5.65). Equivalently, $[V, L_z] = 0$ (5.66): the field leaves rotations about $z$ as a symmetry, $L_z$ commutes with $H$, and by [[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory#^rem-c9-1-3|Remark: The W matrix as the first term of an exact reduction]] the states $|n\,l\,m\rangle$ are good to every order; $V$ never connects different $m$ inside the level, so the nondegenerate formulas apply. The shift is then $\Delta_k^{(2)}$ of [[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory#^thm-c9-1-2|Theorem §C9.1.2]] with $V_{jk} = e\mathcal Ez_{jk}$; the sum runs over a complete set, which for hydrogen includes the continuum ([[§40 Bound States Need Not Be Complete꞉ Hydrogen#^cor-40-2|556 Cor. §40.2]]).
> 3. Comparing with (5.67) gives $\alpha_{\text{pol}}$; for the ground state every denominator $E_j - E_k$ is positive. For the induced moment, the first-order ket is $|k^{(1)}\rangle = R_k\,e\mathcal Ez|k^{(0)}\rangle$, and to first order in $\mathcal E$
>
> $$
> \langle -ez\rangle = -e\bigl(\langle k^{(0)}|z|k^{(1)}\rangle + \langle k^{(1)}|z|k^{(0)}\rangle\bigr) = -2e^2\mathcal E\sum_{j\neq k}\frac{|z_{jk}|^2}{E_k - E_j} = \alpha_{\text{pol}}\mathcal E .
> $$
>
>    Independently, the Feynman–Hellmann theorem ([[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]) with $\partial H/\partial\mathcal E = ez = -d_z$ gives $\partial E_k/\partial\mathcal E = -\langle d_z\rangle$, which is $-\partial\Delta_k/\partial\mathcal E$ (Sakurai Problem 5.10 asks for both routes).

> [!remark] Remark: No true bound states in a field, and where the linear effect lives
> - The potential $e\mathcal Ez$ tends to $-\infty$ as $z \to -\infty$, so strictly no state is bound: the electron can tunnel out, and every level becomes a resonance with a finite lifetime (Sakurai's editor's note to (5.62)). Perturbation theory gives the real part of the shift; the imaginary part, the ionization rate, is exponentially small in $1/\mathcal E$ and invisible at every order, so the series is asymptotic rather than convergent.
> - A permanent dipole, and a shift linear in $\mathcal E$, need a level that contains both parities: hydrogen's $n \ge 2$ levels. That is degenerate theory, and level B's [[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^ex-b8-2-2|Example §B8.2.2]] is its standard case (Sakurai §5.2.1 does the same calculation, with the same $\pm3ea_0\mathcal E$; in the language of [[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory#^thm-c9-1-4|Theorem §C9.1.4]] it diagonalizes $P_0VP_0$ on $n = 2$, where the Wigner–Eckart rules leave only the $2s$–$2p_0$ element). It is not repeated here; Example §C9.2.2 asks what happens when the degeneracy is not exact.

^rem-c9-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.2.2: Closure Bound on the Polarizability
> For a ground state $|0^{(0)}\rangle$ of definite parity, let $E_1^{(0)}$ be the lowest excited level. Then
>
> $$
> \alpha_{\text{pol}} < \frac{2e^2\,\langle 0^{(0)}|z^2|0^{(0)}\rangle}{E_1^{(0)} - E_0^{(0)}} .
> $$
>
> For hydrogen ($\langle z^2\rangle = \frac13\langle r^2\rangle = a_0^2$, $E_2 - E_1 = 3e^2/8a_0$) this is $\alpha_{\text{pol}} < \frac{16}{3}a_0^3 \approx 5.3\,a_0^3$ (5.72).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.1.5, eqs. (5.69)–(5.72)*

^thm-c9-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Every denominator in Theorem §C9.2.1 (3) satisfies $E_j - E_0 \ge E_1 - E_0$ (5.71), with strict inequality for the infinitely many higher states that carry part of the sum, so
>
> $$
> \alpha_{\text{pol}} < \frac{2e^2}{E_1 - E_0}\sum_{j\neq0}|z_{j0}|^2 = \frac{2e^2}{E_1 - E_0}\Bigl(\langle z^2\rangle - |z_{00}|^2\Bigr) = \frac{2e^2\langle z^2\rangle}{E_1 - E_0} ,
> $$
>
> by closure, $\sum_j|z_{j0}|^2 = \langle0^{(0)}|z\,\bigl(\sum_j|j^{(0)}\rangle\langle j^{(0)}|\bigr)\,z|0^{(0)}\rangle$ (5.69), and $z_{00} = 0$. For hydrogen's $1s$ state, $\langle r^2\rangle = (4/a_0^3)\int_0^\infty r^4e^{-2r/a_0}dr = 3a_0^2$ and spherical symmetry gives $\langle z^2\rangle = \langle r^2\rangle/3$ (5.70); the gap is $\frac{e^2}{2a_0}\bigl(1 - \frac14\bigr)$, so the bound is $2e^2a_0^2\cdot8a_0/3e^2 = 16a_0^3/3$.

*Uses:* [[§C9.2 Atoms in External Fields, Fine and Hyperfine Structure#^thm-c9-2-1|Theorem §C9.2.1]], [[§38 The Completeness Relation#^thm-38-2|556 Thm. §38.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.2.3: The Dalgarno–Lewis Method
> Let $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ be nondegenerate. If an operator $F$ satisfies
>
> $$
> [F, H_0]\,|n^{(0)}\rangle = (V - V_{nn})\,|n^{(0)}\rangle ,
> $$
>
> then the first-order ket is $|n^{(1)}\rangle = (F - \langle F\rangle)|n^{(0)}\rangle$, with $\langle F\rangle = \langle n^{(0)}|F|n^{(0)}\rangle$, and the second-order energy needs no sum over states:
>
> $$
> \Delta_n^{(2)} = \langle n^{(0)}|(V - V_{nn})\,F|n^{(0)}\rangle .
> $$
>
> For $H_0 = \mathbf p^2/2m + V_0(\mathbf x)$, a real wave function $\psi_n = \langle\mathbf x|n^{(0)}\rangle$ and $F = F(\mathbf x)$ a function, the condition is the differential equation
>
> $$
> \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\nabla\cdot\bigl(\psi_n^2\,\nabla F\bigr) = (V - V_{nn})\,\psi_n^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.1.5 (names the method after Dalgarno and Lewis and quotes its result (5.73)); Griffiths Problem 7.51(a) and footnote 5 (solving the first-order equation directly); the operator form is derived here*

^thm-c9-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The order-$\lambda$ part of [[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory#^thm-c9-1-1|Theorem §C9.1.1]] is $(E_n^{(0)} - H_0)|n^{(1)}\rangle = (V - V_{nn})|n^{(0)}\rangle$ with $\langle n^{(0)}|n^{(1)}\rangle = 0$, and its solution is unique, $|n^{(1)}\rangle = R_nV|n^{(0)}\rangle$. Now
>
> $$
> (E_n^{(0)} - H_0)\,F|n^{(0)}\rangle = F E_n^{(0)}|n^{(0)}\rangle - H_0F|n^{(0)}\rangle = [F, H_0]\,|n^{(0)}\rangle = (V - V_{nn})|n^{(0)}\rangle ,
> $$
>
> so $F|n^{(0)}\rangle$ solves the equation; subtracting $\langle F\rangle|n^{(0)}\rangle$, which $E_n^{(0)} - H_0$ annihilates, restores orthogonality. Then $\Delta_n^{(2)} = \langle n^{(0)}|V|n^{(1)}\rangle = \langle VF\rangle - V_{nn}\langle F\rangle$. In the position representation $[F, \mathbf p^2/2m]\psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\bigl(F\nabla^2\psi - \nabla^2(F\psi)\bigr) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\bigl(\psi\nabla^2F + 2\nabla F\cdot\nabla\psi\bigr)$, and $F$ commutes with $V_0$; multiplying by $\psi_n$, $\psi_n^2\nabla^2F + 2\psi_n\nabla\psi_n\cdot\nabla F = \nabla\cdot(\psi_n^2\nabla F)$.

*Uses:* [[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory#^thm-c9-1-1|Theorem §C9.1.1]], [[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory#^thm-c9-1-2|Theorem §C9.1.2]]

> [!example] Example §C9.2.1: The Polarizability of Hydrogen
> Find $\alpha_{\text{pol}}$ for the ground state $\psi_{100} = (\pi a_0^3)^{-1/2}e^{-r/a_0}$, with $V = e\mathcal Ez$, $V_{nn} = 0$.
>
> *Solving for $F$.* Try $F = z\,h(r)$. With $\nabla\psi_{100} = -\psi_{100}\hat{\mathbf r}/a_0$, $\partial_r(zh) = (z/r)(h + rh')$ and $\nabla^2(zh) = z(h'' + 4h'/r)$, the condition of Theorem §C9.2.3 divided by $\psi_{100}z$ becomes
>
> $$
> h'' + \frac{4h'}{r} - \frac{2}{a_0}\Bigl(h' + \frac hr\Bigr) = \frac{2me\mathcal E}{\hbar^2} .
> $$
>
> A linear $h = \beta r + \gamma$ works: the $1/r$ terms require $4\beta - 2\gamma/a_0 = 0$, the constants $-4\beta/a_0 = 2me\mathcal E/\hbar^2$. With $ma_0/\hbar^2 = 1/e^2$, $\beta = -\mathcal E/2e$ and $\gamma = -a_0\mathcal E/e$:
>
> $$
> F = -\frac{\mathcal E}{e}\Bigl(a_0 + \frac r2\Bigr)z, \qquad \psi \approx \psi_{100}\Bigl[1 - \frac{\mathcal E}{e}\Bigl(a_0 + \frac r2\Bigr)r\cos\theta\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> ($\langle F\rangle = 0$ by parity) of the form $(A + Br + Cr^2)e^{-r/a_0}\cos\theta$ that Griffiths suggests, with $A = 0$. The electron cloud is pushed toward $-z$, against the field, as a negative charge must be.
>
> *The energy.* $\Delta^{(2)} = \langle VF\rangle = -\mathcal E^2\bigl(a_0\langle z^2\rangle + \tfrac12\langle z^2r\rangle\bigr)$. With $\langle z^2\rangle = a_0^2$ and $\langle z^2r\rangle = \frac13\langle r^3\rangle = \frac13\cdot\frac{4}{a_0^3}\int_0^\infty r^5e^{-2r/a_0}dr = \frac13\cdot\frac{15}{2}a_0^3$,
>
> $$
> \Delta^{(2)} = -\Bigl(1 + \frac54\Bigr)a_0^3\mathcal E^2 = -\frac94\,a_0^3\mathcal E^2, \qquad \alpha_{\text{pol}} = \frac92\,a_0^3 = 4.5\,a_0^3 , \qquad (5.73)
> $$
>
> Sakurai's value, which he notes agrees with experiment and with an exact solution in parabolic coordinates. In SI, $\Delta^{(2)} = -\frac94(4\pi\varepsilon_0)a_0^3\mathcal E^2 = -m(3a_0^2e\mathcal E/2\hbar)^2$, Griffiths' answer.
>
> *Checks.* The value lies below the closure bound $16a_0^3/3$ of Theorem §C9.2.2. The $2p$ state alone contributes $2e^2|\langle210|z|100\rangle|^2/(E_2 - E_1)$, with $|\langle210|z|100\rangle| = 0.745\,a_0$ ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^ex-c7-2-2|Example §C7.2.2]]): $2\cdot0.555\cdot\frac83\,a_0^3 = 2.96\,a_0^3$, two thirds of the total; the rest comes from $n \ge 3$ and the continuum, which a truncated sum would miss and the Dalgarno–Lewis method includes automatically. Numerically $a_0^3 = 0.1482\ \text{Å}^3$, so $\alpha_{\text{pol}} = 0.667\ \text{Å}^3$, which is the measured $\alpha/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 0.667\times10^{-30}\ \mathrm{m^3}$ for hydrogen in [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^law-b4-2-3|EM Law §B4.2.3]].
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.1.5, eqs. (5.68)–(5.73); Griffiths Problem 7.51(a) (hint and answer); solved here*

> [!example] Example §C9.2.2: From Quadratic to Linear Stark Effect
> In real hydrogen $2s_{1/2}$ lies above $2p_{1/2}$ by the Lamb shift, $\delta = 1057.8$ MHz, i.e. $h\delta = 4.37\ \mu\mathrm{eV}$, and $2p_{3/2}$ lies about ten times higher (the fine-structure splitting $10.9$ GHz of [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^ex-b8-3-1|Example §B8.3.1]]). How does the pair $2s_{1/2}$, $2p_{1/2}$ respond to a field $\mathcal E$ small enough to ignore $2p_{3/2}$?
>
> *The matrix.* $V = e\mathcal Ez$ is the $q = 0$ component of a vector operator, also under $\mathbf J = \mathbf L + \mathbf S$, so it connects only equal $m_j$ ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-7|Theorem §C7.2.7]]): two identical $2\times2$ blocks, $m_j = \pm\frac12$. By [[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-5|Theorem §C7.1.5]], $|2p_{1/2}, \tfrac12\rangle = -\sqrt{\tfrac13}\,|2\,1\,0\rangle|{+}\rangle + \sqrt{\tfrac23}\,|2\,1\,1\rangle|{-}\rangle$, and with $\langle210|z|200\rangle = -3a_0$ ([[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^ex-b8-2-2|Example §B8.2.2]]) only the first term survives:
>
> $$
> \langle 2p_{1/2}, \tfrac12|\,z\,|2s_{1/2}, \tfrac12\rangle = \sqrt3\,a_0, \qquad H = \begin{pmatrix} h\delta & \sqrt3\,ea_0\mathcal E\\ \sqrt3\,ea_0\mathcal E & 0\end{pmatrix}
> $$
>
> (energies from $2p_{1/2}$). The exact eigenvalues are
>
> $$
> E_\pm = \frac{h\delta}{2} \pm \sqrt{\Bigl(\frac{h\delta}{2}\Bigr)^2 + 3e^2a_0^2\mathcal E^2} .
> $$
>
> *Two limits.* For $\sqrt3\,ea_0\mathcal E \ll h\delta$ the levels are effectively nondegenerate and the shifts are quadratic: $2s_{1/2}$ rises by $3e^2a_0^2\mathcal E^2/h\delta$, $2p_{1/2}$ falls by the same, the second-order formula of [[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory#^thm-c9-1-2|Theorem §C9.1.2]]. For $\sqrt3\,ea_0\mathcal E \gg h\delta$ the Lamb shift is negligible, degenerate theory applies, and the shifts are linear, $\pm\sqrt3\,ea_0\mathcal E$ about the centroid. The crossover, $\sqrt3\,ea_0\mathcal E = h\delta/2$, is at $\mathcal E \approx 240$ V/cm ($0.8$ statvolt/cm), a modest laboratory field: this is Sakurai's point that degenerate theory is right whenever the matrix elements exceed the splitting, and that in between one must diagonalize in the space of the nearby levels ([[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory#^rem-c9-1-4|Remark: The procedure, and nearly degenerate levels]]).
>
> *Why $\sqrt3$ and not $3$.* Without spin the linear shift is $\pm3ea_0\mathcal E$. With spin, $2p_0$ is shared between $j = \frac12$ and $j = \frac32$: $|\langle2p_{1/2}|z|2s\rangle|^2 + |\langle2p_{3/2}|z|2s\rangle|^2 = 3a_0^2 + 6a_0^2 = 9a_0^2$, and the two-level problem sees only the first part.
>
> *Time reversal.* The blocks $m_j = \pm\frac12$ give identical energies, so every level stays doubly degenerate in any $\mathcal E$: the field is even under time reversal, and Kramers' theorem keeps a twofold degeneracy for one electron ([[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^thm-c8-3-7|Theorem §C8.3.7]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 5.19 (from Gottfried), §5.2.1 (last paragraph); solved here*

## Fine structure: what is new at level C

> [!remark] Remark: What stays at level B
> The relativistic kinetic correction $-\mathbf p^4/8m^3c^2$ and the spin–orbit term $\frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\frac1r\frac{dV_c}{dr}\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$ are derived in Sakurai §5.3.1–5.3.2 by the same arguments as level B ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-2|Theorem §B8.3.2]], [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^mod-b8-3-3|Model §B8.3.3]], [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-4|Theorem §B8.3.4]], general central potential [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^ex-b8-3-1|Example §B8.3.1]]): writing $\mathbf p^4/4m^2 = (H_0 - V)^2$, getting $\langle1/r\rangle$ and $\langle1/r^2\rangle$ by Feynman–Hellmann, and choosing the $|l\,s\,j\,m\rangle$ basis because $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$ is a scalar operator commuting with $\mathbf J$ ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]); Sakurai's "cardinal rule", choose unperturbed kets that diagonalize the perturbation, is level B's [[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]]. The formulas, the $n = 2$ diagram and the Balmer-line numbers stay at level B. Two pieces are new: the radial identities below, which give $\langle1/r^3\rangle$ and $|\psi(0)|^2$ without wave functions, and the Darwin term, which settles the $s$ states. For an alkali atom the same first-order shift, with the radial integral $\langle\frac1r\frac{dV_c}{dr}\rangle_{nl}$ of the screened potential, gives Landé's interval rule, a splitting $\propto(2l + 1)$ between $j = l\pm\frac12$ (5.113); the sodium $D$ lines at $5890$ and $5896$ Å put the $3p_{3/2}$–$3p_{1/2}$ splitting at $2.1$ meV, with $3p_{3/2}$ above because the radial integral is positive.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.3.1–5.3.2, eqs. (5.95)–(5.104), (5.110)–(5.116), Fig. 5.2; the $2.1$ meV is computed here from the two wavelengths*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.2.4: Radial Identities for a Central Potential
> Let $\psi = R_{nl}(r)Y_l^m$ be a normalized bound eigenfunction of $\mathbf p^2/2m + V(r)$, with $V$ no more singular than $1/r$ at the origin. Then
> 1. for $l \ge 1$ the mean radial force vanishes,
>
> $$
> \Bigl\langle\frac{dV}{dr}\Bigr\rangle = \frac{l(l + 1)\hbar^2}{m}\Bigl\langle\frac{1}{r^3}\Bigr\rangle ; \qquad (5.123)
> $$
>
> 2. for $l = 0$ the same balance acquires a contact term,
>
> $$
> |\psi(0)|^2 = \frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\Bigl\langle\frac{dV}{dr}\Bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> For the hydrogen-like $V = -Ze^2/r$, with $\langle1/r^2\rangle = Z^2/[(l + \frac12)n^3a_0^2]$ ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-1|Theorem §B8.3.1]] with $a \to a_0/Z$), these give
>
> $$
> \Bigl\langle\frac{1}{r^3}\Bigr\rangle = \frac{Z^3}{l(l + \frac12)(l + 1)\,n^3a_0^3}\quad(l \ge 1), \qquad |\psi_{n00}(0)|^2 = \frac{Z^3}{\pi n^3a_0^3} . \qquad (5.124)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.3.2, eqs. (5.120)–(5.124), Problem 5.22*

^thm-c9-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Sakurai's route to item 1: in an eigenket of $H_0$, $\langle[H_0, A]\rangle = 0$ for any $A$ (5.121). Take $A = p_r = -i\hbar(\partial_r + 1/r)$, which commutes with the radial kinetic energy, so only the centrifugal and potential terms survive (5.122); with $[f(r), p_r] = i\hbar f'(r)$ this is $\langle -l(l+1)\hbar^2/mr^3 + dV/dr\rangle = 0$ (5.123).
>
> The same balance follows from the radial equation, and that version shows what changes at $l = 0$. With $u = rR_{nl}$, $\int_0^\infty u^2dr = 1$ and $V_{\text{eff}} = V + l(l+1)\hbar^2/2mr^2$, the equation $u'' = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(V_{\text{eff}} - E)u$ gives
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dr}\bigl(u'^2\bigr) = 2u'u'' = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(V_{\text{eff}} - E)\,\frac{d}{dr}\bigl(u^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Integrate from $0$ to $\infty$ and integrate by parts on the right; at infinity everything decays. At the origin $u \sim r^{l+1}$ ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-2|Theorem §C6.1.2]]), so $(V_{\text{eff}} - E)u^2 \to 0$ for every $l$ (as $r^{2l}$, or as $r$ for $l = 0$ with a $1/r$ potential). Hence
>
> $$
> -u'(0)^2 = -\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty\frac{dV_{\text{eff}}}{dr}\,u^2\,dr = -\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\Bigl\langle\frac{dV}{dr} - \frac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{mr^3}\Bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> For $l \ge 1$, $u'(0) = 0$: item 1. For $l = 0$ the centrifugal term is absent and $u'(0) = R_{n0}(0)$, while $|\psi(0)|^2 = R_{n0}(0)^2|Y_0^0|^2 = R_{n0}(0)^2/4\pi$: item 2. For Coulomb, $dV/dr = Ze^2/r^2$; item 1 with $\hbar^2/me^2 = a_0$ gives $\langle1/r^3\rangle = Z\langle1/r^2\rangle/[a_0l(l+1)]$, and item 2 with $l = 0$ gives $|\psi(0)|^2 = \frac{mZe^2}{2\pi\hbar^2}\cdot\frac{2Z^2}{n^3a_0^2} = \frac{Z^3}{\pi n^3a_0^3}$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.2.5: The Darwin Term and the Fine Structure of s States
> The third relativistic correction to a one-electron atom, the **Darwin term**, is
>
> $$
> V_D = -\frac{1}{8m^2c^2}\sum_{i=1}^3\bigl[p_i, [p_i, V_c]\bigr] = \frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\,\nabla^2V_c .
> $$
>
> For $V_c = -Ze^2/r$ its first-order shift is nonzero only for $l = 0$,
>
> $$
> \Delta_D^{(1)} = \frac{\pi\hbar^2Ze^2}{2m^2c^2}\,|\psi_{n00}(0)|^2 = \frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3}\,\delta_{l0} ,
> $$
>
> and the relativistic, spin–orbit and Darwin shifts together give, for every $l$ including $l = 0$,
>
> $$
> \Delta_{nj}^{(1)} = -\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3}\Bigl[\frac{1}{j + \frac12} - \frac{3}{4n}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.3.2 (remark after (5.125)), Problem 5.17*

^thm-c9-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $[p_i, f(\mathbf x)] = -i\hbar\,\partial_if$, so $[p_i, [p_i, V_c]] = -\hbar^2\partial_i^2V_c$, and summing gives $V_D = (\hbar^2/8m^2c^2)\nabla^2V_c$. With $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\delta^3(\mathbf x)$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|EM Theorem §B1.2.2]]), $\nabla^2V_c = 4\pi Ze^2\delta^3(\mathbf x)$ and $\langle V_D\rangle = \frac{\pi\hbar^2Ze^2}{2m^2c^2}|\psi(0)|^2$, which vanishes unless $l = 0$. Theorem §C9.2.4 gives $|\psi_{n00}(0)|^2 = Z^3/\pi n^3a_0^3$, so $\Delta_D^{(1)} = \frac{\hbar^2Z^4e^2}{2m^2c^2n^3a_0^3} = \frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3}$, using $\hbar^2e^2/(m^2c^2a_0^3) = mc^2\alpha^4$.
>
> The relativistic correction is $-\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3}\bigl[\frac{1}{l + \frac12} - \frac{3}{4n}\bigr]$ (5.104b). For $l = 0$, $j = \frac12$: adding the Darwin term replaces $\frac{1}{l + \frac12} = 2$ by $2 - 1 = 1 = \frac{1}{j + \frac12}$, and there is no spin–orbit term. For $l \ge 1$ the spin–orbit shift (5.125), written with $E_n^{(0)} = -mc^2(Z\alpha)^2/2n^2$, is $\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{4n^3}\cdot\frac{1}{(l+1)(l+\frac12)}$ for $j = l + \frac12$ and $-\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{4n^3}\cdot\frac{1}{l(l+\frac12)}$ for $j = l - \frac12$; adding it to the relativistic term turns $\frac{1}{l+\frac12}$ into $\frac{1}{l+\frac12} - \frac{1}{2(l+1)(l+\frac12)} = \frac{1}{l+1}$, respectively $\frac{1}{l+\frac12} + \frac{1}{2l(l+\frac12)} = \frac1l$, which is $\frac{1}{j+\frac12}$ in both cases.

> [!remark] Remark: What the Darwin term settles
> Level B's [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]] has the same formula, but for $l = 0$ its spin–orbit step is indeterminate ($\langle1/r^3\rangle$ diverges while $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S = 0$), and level B justified the $s$ states only by the exact Dirac energies ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^rem-b8-3-4|★ Remark: The exact Dirac result and the Lamb shift]]). ## The Zeeman effect: what is new at level C

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.2.6: The Zeeman Hamiltonian from Minimal Coupling
> For one electron in a central potential and a uniform field $\mathbf B$, in the gauge $\mathbf A = \frac12\mathbf B\times\mathbf x$ (5.126), the Hamiltonian $\frac{1}{2m}\bigl(\mathbf p + \frac ec\mathbf A\bigr)^2 + V_c(r)$ plus the spin moment $-\frac{e}{mc}\mathbf S$ ($g = 2$) is
>
> $$
> H = H_0 + H_{LS} + \frac{\mu_B}{\hbar}\,\mathbf B\cdot(\mathbf L + 2\mathbf S) + \frac{e^2}{8mc^2}\,|\mathbf B\times\mathbf x|^2 ,
> $$
>
> and for $\mathbf B = B\hat{\mathbf z}$ the last two terms are $\frac{\mu_BB}{\hbar}(L_z + 2S_z) + \frac{e^2B^2}{8mc^2}(x^2 + y^2)$ (5.134)–(5.136). The linear term is level B's Zeeman perturbation; the quadratic one is the **diamagnetic term**.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.3.3, eqs. (5.126)–(5.136)*

^thm-c9-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Start from [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^pr-c4-3-2|Principle §C4.3.2]] with charge $-e$:
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\mathbf p + \frac ec\mathbf A\Bigr)^2 = \mathbf p^2 + \frac ec(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A + \mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p) + \frac{e^2}{c^2}\mathbf A^2 . \qquad (5.129)
> $$
>
> In the position representation $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A\,\psi = \mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p\,\psi - i\hbar(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)\psi$ (5.130), and $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf B\times\mathbf x) = 0$ for uniform $\mathbf B$, so $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A = \mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p$ (5.131). Then $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p = \frac12(\mathbf B\times\mathbf x)\cdot\mathbf p = \frac12\mathbf B\cdot(\mathbf x\times\mathbf p) = \frac12\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L$ (5.132) (the cyclic rearrangement is allowed because $\mathbf B$ is a number and $x_i$, $p_j$ with $i \neq j$ commute), so the cross term is $\frac{e}{2mc}\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L = \frac{\mu_B}\hbar\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf L$. The square is $\mathbf A^2 = \frac14|\mathbf B\times\mathbf x|^2$, $= \frac14B^2(x^2 + y^2)$ for $\mathbf B = B\hat{\mathbf z}$ (5.133). The spin adds $-\boldsymbol\mu_s\cdot\mathbf B = \frac{e}{mc}\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf B = \frac{2\mu_B}{\hbar}\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf B$ (5.135) ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^mod-b6-2-2|Model §B6.2.2]]); $H_{LS}$ is the spin–orbit term already in $H_0$'s level structure.

^der-c9-2-6

*Uses:* [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^pr-c4-3-2|Principle §C4.3.2]], [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^mod-b6-2-2|Model §B6.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Layer change from level B, and the diamagnetic term
> Level B wrote the Zeeman perturbation as $-(\boldsymbol\mu_l + \boldsymbol\mu_s)\cdot\mathbf B$, adding two magnetic moments, and noted that a term quadratic in $B$ was dropped ([[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting#^thm-b8-4-1|Theorem §B8.4.1]]). Here the orbital part is derived from the charged-particle Hamiltonian, and the dropped term is explicit. For the hydrogen ground state ($L = 0$, and spin aside) it is the whole orbital effect: to first order $\Delta E = \frac{e^2B^2}{8mc^2}\langle x^2 + y^2\rangle = \frac{e^2B^2}{8mc^2}\cdot2a_0^2$, so with $\Delta E = -\frac12\chi B^2$ the atom is diamagnetic with $\chi = -e^2a_0^2/2mc^2 = -\frac12r_ea_0^2 = -3.9\times10^{-30}\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ per atom, $-2.4\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{cm^3/mol}$ (Sakurai Problem 5.25; $r_e = e^2/mc^2$). Compared with $\mu_BB$ it is smaller by $ea_0^2B/2\hbar c \approx 2\times10^{-6}$ at $1$ T, which is why it is negligible whenever $\mathbf L + 2\mathbf S$ has nonzero matrix elements, and dominant for closed-shell atoms such as helium (Sakurai Problem 5.26 compares with helium's measured $1.88\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{cm^3/mol}$). Classical electromagnetism gives the same sign and scale from the induced orbital moment ([[§B7.1 Magnetization and Bound Currents#^thm-b7-1-2|EM Theorem §B7.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.3.3, remark after (5.135), Problems 5.25–5.26; numbers computed here*

^rem-c9-2-5

> [!remark] Remark: Weak, strong and intermediate fields, by symmetry
> The three regimes of the linear term are at level B and level C already. Weak field: in a $j$ multiplet $\mathbf L + 2\mathbf S$ acts as $g_J\mathbf J$ by the projection theorem, which is Sakurai's second derivation of Landé's formula ((5.142); [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^ex-c7-2-3|Example §C7.2.3]], level B [[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting#^thm-b8-4-2|Theorem §B8.4.2]]). Strong field (Paschen–Back): $L_z$, $S_z$ are almost good and $H_{LS}$ contributes $\hbar^2m_lm_s\langle\frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\frac1r\frac{dV_c}{dr}\rangle$ (5.143)–(5.146), level B [[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting#^thm-b8-4-3|Theorem §B8.4.3]]. Intermediate fields for $n = 2$: [[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting#^thm-b8-4-4|Theorem §B8.4.4]] (Sakurai Fig. 5.3, Problem 5.24). What level C adds is the reason the $8\times8$ problem falls apart into small blocks. $L_z + 2S_z = J_z + S_z$, and $S_z$ is the $q = 0$ component of a vector operator under $\mathbf J$; by the Wigner–Eckart theorem ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-6|Theorem §C7.2.6]]) it connects only equal $m$ and $|\Delta j| \le 1$, and it commutes with $\mathbf L^2$. So for one electron the field mixes only $j = l + \frac12$ with $j = l - \frac12$ at the same $l$ and $m$: $2\times2$ blocks, plus $1\times1$ blocks for the stretched states $|m| = l + \frac12$ and for $s$ states. The stretched states are the exception in Sakurai's Table 5.1: there $m = m_l + m_s$ can be made in only one way, so $L_z$ and $S_z$ are good at every field.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.3.3, eqs. (5.137)–(5.146), Table 5.1 and the remark on $T^{(1)}_{q=0}$*

## Hyperfine structure with the projection theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.2.7: Hyperfine Multiplets and Their Zeeman Splitting
> Let an electronic level $|\alpha; J\,M_J\rangle$ couple to a nucleus of spin $I$ through a magnetic interaction $H_{\text{hf}} = \mathbf I\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal W}$, where $\boldsymbol{\mathcal W}$ is a vector operator of the electrons, and let the hyperfine energies be small compared with the distance to other electronic levels.
> 1. Within the $(2J + 1)(2I + 1)$ states of the level, $H_{\text{hf}}$ acts as $A\,\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf J$, with the $M_J$-independent constant $A = \langle\alpha; J\,M_J|\mathbf J\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal W}|\alpha; J\,M_J\rangle/\hbar^2J(J + 1)$. The good states are the $|F\,M_F\rangle$ of $\mathbf F = \mathbf J + \mathbf I$, $F = |J - I|, \dots, J + I$, with
>
> $$
> E_F = \frac{A\hbar^2}{2}\bigl[F(F + 1) - J(J + 1) - I(I + 1)\bigr], \qquad E_F - E_{F-1} = A\hbar^2F
> $$
>
>    (an interval rule of the same form as Landé's for $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$).
> 2. In a field weak compared with the hyperfine splitting, and neglecting the nuclear moment, each $F$ level splits as $\Delta E = g_F\,\mu_BB\,M_F$ with
>
> $$
> g_F = g_J\,\frac{F(F + 1) + J(J + 1) - I(I + 1)}{2F(F + 1)} .
> $$
>
> For hydrogen's ground state ($J = I = \frac12$): $F = 1$ above $F = 0$ by $A\hbar^2$, the $21$-cm splitting of [[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting#^thm-b8-4-5|Theorem §B8.4.5]], and $g_{F=1} = 1$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.11.4, eq. (3.483) (projection theorem), §5.3.2–5.3.3, eqs. (5.113), (5.142) (the same steps for $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$ and $g_J$), applied here to $\mathbf F = \mathbf J + \mathbf I$; Sakurai Problem 5.40; Griffiths §7.5*

^thm-c9-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. First-order degenerate theory ([[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory#^thm-c9-1-4|Theorem §C9.1.4]]) needs the matrix of $P_0H_{\text{hf}}P_0$ on the level. The states factor as electronic $\otimes$ nuclear, $\mathbf I$ acts on the second factor and $\boldsymbol{\mathcal W}$ on the first, so in spherical components $\mathbf I\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal W} = \sum_q(-1)^qI_q\mathcal W_{-q}$ needs only $\langle\alpha; J\,M_J'|\mathcal W_{-q}|\alpha; J\,M_J\rangle$. By the projection theorem ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-8|Theorem §C7.2.8]]) these equal $A\,\langle J\,M_J'|J_{-q}|J\,M_J\rangle$ with the stated $A$. So on the level $H_{\text{hf}}$ has the matrix of $A\,\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf J$, and $\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf J = \frac12(\mathbf F^2 - \mathbf J^2 - \mathbf I^2)$ is diagonal in the coupled basis of [[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^def-c7-1-1|Def. §C7.1.1]] ([[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-5|Theorem §B6.3.5]] with $\mathbf J^{(1)} = \mathbf J$, $\mathbf J^{(2)} = \mathbf I$), with eigenvalue $\frac{\hbar^2}2[F(F+1) - J(J+1) - I(I+1)]$. The difference of consecutive $F$ is $\frac{A\hbar^2}{2}[F(F+1) - (F-1)F] = A\hbar^2F$.
> 2. The Zeeman term is $\frac{\mu_BB}{\hbar}(L_z + 2S_z)$, which within the electronic level acts as $g_J\frac{\mu_BB}{\hbar}J_z$ ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^ex-c7-2-3|Example §C7.2.3]]). Within an $F$ multiplet, $\mathbf J$ is a vector operator under $\mathbf F$ (it commutes with $\mathbf I$), so the projection theorem again gives $\langle J_z\rangle_{F M_F} = \frac{\langle\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf J\rangle}{\hbar^2F(F+1)}\,M_F\hbar$ with $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf J = \frac12(\mathbf F^2 + \mathbf J^2 - \mathbf I^2)$. Multiplying by $g_J\mu_BB/\hbar$ gives $g_F$. For hydrogen, $g_J = 2$ and $F = 1$: $g_F = 2\cdot\frac{2 + \frac34 - \frac34}{4} = 1$.

*Uses:* [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-8|Theorem §C7.2.8]], [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^ex-c7-2-3|Example §C7.2.3]], [[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory#^thm-c9-1-4|Theorem §C9.1.4]], [[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^def-c7-1-1|Def. §C7.1.1]], [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-5|Theorem §B6.3.5]]

> [!example] Example §C9.2.3: The Hydrogen Ground State at Any Field (Breit–Rabi)
> Take $H = A\,\mathbf S_e\cdot\mathbf S_p + \frac{2\mu_BB}{\hbar}S_{e,z}$ on the four spin states of hydrogen's $1s$ level, neglecting the proton's own Zeeman term (smaller by $g_pm/2m_p \approx 1.5\times10^{-3}$). Write $\Delta E = A\hbar^2 = 5.87\ \mu$eV for the zero-field splitting ([[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting#^thm-b8-4-5|Theorem §B8.4.5]]) and $x = 2\mu_BB/\Delta E$.
>
> *Exact solution.* $M_F = m_e + m_p$ commutes with $H$, so $H$ is block diagonal in $M_F$. The stretched states $|{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle$, $|{\downarrow\downarrow}\rangle$ are eigenstates with $E = \frac{\Delta E}{4} \pm \mu_BB$. On $\{|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle, |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle\}$, with $\mathbf S_e\cdot\mathbf S_p = S_{e,z}S_{p,z} + \frac12(S_{e+}S_{p-} + S_{e-}S_{p+})$,
>
> $$
> H = \begin{pmatrix} -\frac{\Delta E}{4} + \mu_BB & \frac{\Delta E}{2}\\[0.5ex] \frac{\Delta E}{2} & -\frac{\Delta E}{4} - \mu_BB\end{pmatrix}, \qquad E = -\frac{\Delta E}{4} \pm \frac{\Delta E}{2}\sqrt{1 + x^2} .
> $$
>
> *Weak field by perturbation theory.* With $H_0 = A\,\mathbf S_e\cdot\mathbf S_p$ the triplet is a degenerate level, and Theorem §C9.2.7 (2) with $g_F = 1$ gives the first-order shifts $\mu_BB\,M_F = +\mu_BB, 0, -\mu_BB$, the exact slopes of the stretched states. The two $M_F = 0$ states (triplet and singlet) are coupled by $\langle1\,0|\,2\mu_BBS_{e,z}/\hbar\,|0\,0\rangle = \mu_BB$ across the gap $\Delta E$, so second order ([[§C9.1 Time-Independent Perturbation Theory, the Formal Theory#^thm-c9-1-5|Theorem §C9.1.5]]) pushes them apart by $\pm(\mu_BB)^2/\Delta E$; the exact roots expand to $\pm\frac{\Delta E}{2}(1 + \frac{x^2}{2})$, the same.
>
> *Strong field.* For $x \gg 1$, $E \approx \pm\mu_BB + A\hbar^2m_em_p$: the electron spin follows the field, $m_e$ and $m_p$ become good, and the hyperfine term is a small correction, the hyperfine analogue of Paschen–Back. The crossover $x = 1$ is at $B = \Delta E/2\mu_B = 50.7$ mT ($507$ G).
>
> *Two remarks on the problem statement.* Sakurai Problem 5.40 writes the Zeeman term as $(eB/m_ec)\,\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf B$, with the field appearing twice; the intended term is $(|e|/m_ec)\,\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf B = 2\mu_B\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf B/\hbar$, as above. Problem 5.39, the positronium version, has the extra symmetry that its Zeeman term $\propto S_{1z} - S_{2z}$ leaves the $M_F = \pm1$ states unshifted.

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: The van der Waals interaction
> Two hydrogen atoms in their ground states with protons a fixed distance $r \gg a_0$ apart interact through $V = e^2\bigl[\frac1r + \frac{1}{|\mathbf r + \mathbf r_2 - \mathbf r_1|} - \frac{1}{|\mathbf r + \mathbf r_2|} - \frac{1}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r_1|}\bigr]$ (5.147), whose multipole expansion begins with the dipole–dipole term $V \approx \frac{e^2}{r^3}(x_1x_2 + y_1y_2 - 2z_1z_2)$ (5.149), $\mathbf r$ along $z$. Every term of the expansion carries spherical harmonics with $l \ge 1$ on each atom, so its expectation value in the product of $1s$ states vanishes: no first-order energy. Second order (5.150) is negative, as for any ground state, and scales as $r^{-6}$: the long-range attraction between neutral atoms. A closure bound in the manner of Theorem §C9.2.2 makes it quantitative: every excited pair state lies at least $2(E_2 - E_1) = 3e^2/4a_0$ above the ground state, and $\sum_k|V_{k0}|^2 = \langle V^2\rangle = \frac{e^4}{r^6}\bigl(\langle x^2\rangle^2 + \langle y^2\rangle^2 + 4\langle z^2\rangle^2\bigr) = \frac{6e^4a_0^4}{r^6}$, so
>
> $$
> 0 > E^{(2)}(r) \ge -\frac{6e^4a_0^4/r^6}{3e^2/4a_0} = -\frac{8e^2a_0^5}{r^6} .
> $$
>
> This is the microscopic origin of the $r^{-6}$ tail of the Lennard-Jones potential ([[§B9.1 Real Gases and the van der Waals Equation#^rem-b9-1-4|TH ★ Remark: The shape of B(T) for real gases]]); Griffiths Problem 7.37 reaches the same $r^{-6}$ law in a model of two coupled oscillators. The van der Waals force is not on the PHY 512 schedule.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.3.4, eqs. (5.147)–(5.150), Fig. 5.4; Griffiths Problem 7.37; the closure bound is computed here*
