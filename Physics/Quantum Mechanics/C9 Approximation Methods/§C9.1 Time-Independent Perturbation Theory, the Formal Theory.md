How does a known spectrum change when a small term is added to the Hamiltonian, to any order, and what replaces the nondegenerate formulas when levels coincide? Level B answered at first and second order by matching powers of $\lambda$ in the wave equation ([[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory]]: first-order energy and state, ★ second-order energy, Feynman–Hellmann) and, for a degenerate level, by diagonalizing the W matrix ([[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory]]); the mathematics underneath is the spectral theorem ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]) and the completeness relation ([[§25 The Completeness Relation#^thm-25-2|556 Thm. §25.2]]). Level C recasts the theory in Sakurai's operator form. A complementary projector and a reduced resolvent turn the Schrödinger equation into two exact equations, whose expansion gives every order by one recursion; the normalization of the perturbed ket becomes a physical quantity, the renormalization constant $Z$; and degenerate theory becomes an exact effective-Hamiltonian problem inside the degenerate subspace, which also settles what to do when first order does not lift the degeneracy. There is no typed PHY 512 material; the course schedule (nondegenerate, then degenerate time-independent perturbation theory) fixes the scope.

*Notation.* As Sakurai: $H = H_0 + \lambda V$ with $\lambda$ a bookkeeping parameter set to $1$ at the end; unperturbed kets $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ and energies $E_n^{(0)}$; perturbed kets $|n\rangle$ and energies $E_n$; $N$th-order corrections $|n^{(N)}\rangle$, $\Delta_n^{(N)}$ (superscripts in parentheses); $V_{nk} \equiv \langle n^{(0)}|V|k^{(0)}\rangle$, matrix elements between **unperturbed** kets. In denominators $E_k$ is short for $E_k^{(0)}$ when no confusion arises. $\hbar$ is explicit.

> [!caution] Caution: Sakurai's and Griffiths' notation
> Level B (Griffiths) writes $H = H^0 + \lambda H'$, $\psi_n^0$, $E_n^0$ and corrections $E_n^1, E_n^2, \dots$ with bare superscripts ([[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]]). Here the perturbation is $V$ (not a potential in general: for an atom in a field it is only the field term), the orders carry parentheses, $E_n^{(0)}$ is the unperturbed energy and $\Delta_n = E_n - E_n^{(0)}$ the whole shift. Level B's W matrix ([[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^def-b8-2-1|Def. §B8.2.1]]) is the matrix of $P_0VP_0$ below.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.1.1, §5.1.3, eqs. (5.1)–(5.4), (5.20), (5.43); Griffiths §7.1.1*

## Nondegenerate theory to all orders

> [!definition] Definition §C9.1.1: Energy Shift; Complementary Projector; Reduced Resolvent; Intermediate Normalization
> Let $H_0|k^{(0)}\rangle = E_k^{(0)}|k^{(0)}\rangle$ with $\{|k^{(0)}\rangle\}$ orthonormal and complete, $\sum_k|k^{(0)}\rangle\langle k^{(0)}| = 1$, and let the level $E_n^{(0)}$ be nondegenerate. Write $|n\rangle$ for the eigenket of $H = H_0 + \lambda V$ that tends to $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ as $\lambda \to 0$, with eigenvalue $E_n$. Then:
> - the **energy shift** is $\Delta_n = E_n - E_n^{(0)}$ (5.20);
> - the **complementary projector** is $\phi_n = 1 - |n^{(0)}\rangle\langle n^{(0)}| = \sum_{k\neq n}|k^{(0)}\rangle\langle k^{(0)}|$ (5.23);
> - the **reduced resolvent** is
>
> $$
> R_n \equiv \frac{\phi_n}{E_n^{(0)} - H_0} = \sum_{k\neq n}\frac{|k^{(0)}\rangle\langle k^{(0)}|}{E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}} , \qquad (5.24),\ (5.32)
> $$
>
>   well defined because the excluded denominator is the only vanishing one; $R_n$ commutes with $\phi_n$ and $H_0$, and $R_n|n^{(0)}\rangle = 0$, $\langle n^{(0)}|R_n = 0$ (5.33);
> - **intermediate normalization** is the convention $\langle n^{(0)}|n\rangle = 1$ for every $\lambda$ (5.31), in place of $\langle n|n\rangle = 1$; the ket is normalized at the end if needed.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.1.3, eqs. (5.18)–(5.24), (5.29)–(5.33)*

^def-c9-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.1.1: The Exact Perturbation Equations
> In intermediate normalization the perturbed ket and energy shift satisfy, exactly,
>
> $$
> |n\rangle = |n^{(0)}\rangle + R_n\,(\lambda V - \Delta_n)\,|n\rangle , \qquad (5.34)
> $$
>
> $$
> \Delta_n = \lambda\,\langle n^{(0)}|V|n\rangle . \qquad (5.35)
> $$
>
> Nothing has been approximated: the pair is the eigenvalue problem $H|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$ rewritten so that $\lambda$ appears only explicitly.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.1.3, eqs. (5.21)–(5.35)*

^thm-c9-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Rearrange $(H_0 + \lambda V)|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$ as
>
> $$
> \bigl(E_n^{(0)} - H_0\bigr)|n\rangle = (\lambda V - \Delta_n)|n\rangle . \qquad (5.21)
> $$
>
> Apply $\langle n^{(0)}|$: the left side vanishes because $\langle n^{(0)}|H_0 = E_n^{(0)}\langle n^{(0)}|$, so $\langle n^{(0)}|(\lambda V - \Delta_n)|n\rangle = 0$ (5.22). With $\langle n^{(0)}|n\rangle = 1$ this is (5.35). It also says that $(\lambda V - \Delta_n)|n\rangle$ has no component along $|n^{(0)}\rangle$, so it equals $\phi_n(\lambda V - \Delta_n)|n\rangle$ (5.25). Now apply $\phi_n$ to (5.21); $\phi_n$ commutes with $H_0$, and on the range of $\phi_n$ the operator $E_n^{(0)} - H_0$ is invertible with inverse $R_n$ (its eigenvalues there are $E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)} \neq 0$, by nondegeneracy):
>
> $$
> \phi_n|n\rangle = R_n(\lambda V - \Delta_n)|n\rangle .
> $$
>
> Finally, by closure, $|n\rangle = |n^{(0)}\rangle\langle n^{(0)}|n\rangle + \phi_n|n\rangle = |n^{(0)}\rangle + \phi_n|n\rangle$, which is (5.34). (Sakurai reaches the same equation by noting that the naive inversion (5.26) misses a solution $c_n|n^{(0)}\rangle$ of the homogeneous equation, and fixing $c_n = \langle n^{(0)}|n\rangle = 1$, (5.27)–(5.31).)

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.1.2: The Perturbation Series to All Orders
> Expand $|n\rangle = \sum_{N\ge0}\lambda^N|n^{(N)}\rangle$ (with $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ the unperturbed ket) and $\Delta_n = \sum_{N\ge1}\lambda^N\Delta_n^{(N)}$ (5.36). Then for every $N \ge 1$:
> 1. the energy to order $N$ needs the ket only to order $N - 1$:
>
> $$
> \Delta_n^{(N)} = \langle n^{(0)}|V|n^{(N-1)}\rangle ; \qquad (5.37)
> $$
>
> 2. the kets follow by recursion, and every correction is orthogonal to $|n^{(0)}\rangle$:
>
> $$
> |n^{(N)}\rangle = R_n\Bigl[V|n^{(N-1)}\rangle - \sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\Delta_n^{(k)}|n^{(N-k)}\rangle\Bigr], \qquad \langle n^{(0)}|n^{(N)}\rangle = 0 .
> $$
>
> 3. Explicitly, through second order in the ket and third order in the energy,
>
> $$
> |n^{(1)}\rangle = R_nV|n^{(0)}\rangle = \sum_{k\neq n}|k^{(0)}\rangle\frac{V_{kn}}{E_n - E_k} , \qquad (5.39)
> $$
>
> $$
> |n^{(2)}\rangle = R_nVR_nV|n^{(0)}\rangle - V_{nn}R_n^2V|n^{(0)}\rangle = \sum_{k\neq n}\sum_{l\neq n}|k^{(0)}\rangle\frac{V_{kl}V_{ln}}{(E_n - E_k)(E_n - E_l)} - \sum_{k\neq n}|k^{(0)}\rangle\frac{V_{nn}V_{kn}}{(E_n - E_k)^2} , \qquad (5.41),\ (5.44)
> $$
>
> $$
> \Delta_n^{(1)} = V_{nn}, \qquad \Delta_n^{(2)} = \sum_{k\neq n}\frac{|V_{nk}|^2}{E_n - E_k} , \qquad (5.40),\ (5.42)
> $$
>
> $$
> \Delta_n^{(3)} = \sum_{k\neq n}\sum_{l\neq n}\frac{V_{nk}V_{kl}V_{ln}}{(E_n - E_k)(E_n - E_l)} - V_{nn}\sum_{k\neq n}\frac{|V_{nk}|^2}{(E_n - E_k)^2} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.1.3, eqs. (5.36)–(5.44); the third-order energy is (5.37) with $N = 3$, worked out here (Griffiths footnote 5 points to Landau–Lifshitz for it)*

^thm-c9-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Both equations of Theorem §C9.1.1 hold for every $\lambda$ near $0$, so after inserting the series the coefficients of each power of $\lambda$ agree (5.38). In (5.35), $\lambda\langle n^{(0)}|V|n\rangle$ has $\lambda^N$-coefficient $\langle n^{(0)}|V|n^{(N-1)}\rangle$: item 1. In (5.34),
>
> $$
> \sum_N\lambda^N|n^{(N)}\rangle = |n^{(0)}\rangle + R_n\Bigl(\lambda V - \sum_{k\ge1}\lambda^k\Delta_n^{(k)}\Bigr)\sum_j\lambda^j|n^{(j)}\rangle ,
> $$
>
> and the $\lambda^N$-coefficient is $|n^{(N)}\rangle = R_nV|n^{(N-1)}\rangle - \sum_{k=1}^{N}\Delta_n^{(k)}R_n|n^{(N-k)}\rangle$. The $k = N$ term contains $R_n|n^{(0)}\rangle = 0$, which gives item 2; every correction lies in the range of $R_n$, which $\langle n^{(0)}|R_n = 0$ makes orthogonal to $|n^{(0)}\rangle$. For $N = 1$, $|n^{(1)}\rangle = R_nV|n^{(0)}\rangle$; for $N = 2$, $|n^{(2)}\rangle = R_nV|n^{(1)}\rangle - \Delta_n^{(1)}R_n|n^{(1)}\rangle$ with $\Delta_n^{(1)} = V_{nn}$. Item 1 then gives $\Delta_n^{(2)} = \langle n^{(0)}|VR_nV|n^{(0)}\rangle$ and
>
> $$
> \Delta_n^{(3)} = \langle n^{(0)}|VR_nVR_nV|n^{(0)}\rangle - V_{nn}\,\langle n^{(0)}|VR_n^2V|n^{(0)}\rangle .
> $$
>
> Inserting the spectral form of $R_n$ (and $R_n^2 = \sum_{k\neq n}|k^{(0)}\rangle\langle k^{(0)}|/(E_n - E_k)^2$, [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]]) and $V_{kn} = V_{nk}^*$ gives the sums in item 3.

> [!remark] Remark: What level C adds to level B's first and second order
> The $N = 1, 2$ results are level B's [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]], [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]] and [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^rem-b8-1-4|★ Remark: Second-order energies]], with the same values; they are not repeated as boxes. What changes is the layer of the argument. Level B matched powers of $\lambda$ in a differential equation and solved each order by expanding in eigenfunctions; here two exact operator equations, written without any basis, generate all orders by one recursion, and the basis enters only at the end, through $R_n$. Level B's choice $\langle\psi_n^0|\psi_n^1\rangle = 0$, which it justified order by order, is intermediate normalization, and item 2 shows it holds to all orders. The recursion is what makes higher orders mechanical: the third-order energy above, which level B did not reach, takes two lines.

^rem-c9-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Level repulsion and convergence
> - Second order pushes two levels connected by $V$ apart, the lower down and the upper up by the same $|V_{ij}|^2/|E_j^{(0)} - E_i^{(0)}|$; every second-order term for the ground state is negative (level B: [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^rem-b8-1-4|★ Remark: Second-order energies]]). Sakurai calls the repulsion a special case of the **no-level-crossing theorem**: levels connected by the perturbation do not cross as $\lambda$ is varied. Levels that $V$ never connects, for instance of different symmetry under an operator commuting with $H_0$ and $V$, may cross freely.
> - The series is a power series in $\lambda$, and it presumes that $E_n(\lambda)$ and $|n\rangle$ are analytic near $\lambda = 0$ (Sakurai §5.1.1). Its radius of convergence is set by the singularities of $E_n(\lambda)$ in the complex $\lambda$-plane, which can lie well inside $|\lambda| = 1$ even when the physics is smooth (the two-level case, [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^ex-b8-1-3|★ Example §B8.1.3]]). For $H_0$ the kinetic energy alone the series is the Born series, which at energy $E^{(0)} < 0$ converges if and only if neither $H_0 + V$ nor $H_0 - V$ has a bound state of energy $\le E^{(0)}$ (Sakurai, quoting Newton); the Born series proper is [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation]].
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.1.3, remarks after (5.44)*

## Wave-function renormalization

> [!definition] Definition §C9.1.2: Renormalized Ket; Wave-Function Renormalization Constant
> The ket of Theorem §C9.1.1 is not normalized. The **renormalized ket** is $|n\rangle_N = Z_n^{1/2}|n\rangle$ with ${}_N\langle n|n\rangle_N = 1$ and $Z_n > 0$ (5.45). Because $\langle n^{(0)}|n\rangle = 1$,
>
> $$
> Z_n = \bigl|\langle n^{(0)}|n\rangle_N\bigr|^2 , \qquad (5.46)
> $$
>
> the **wave-function renormalization constant**: the probability that the perturbed eigenstate is found in the unperturbed state it grew out of.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.1.4, eqs. (5.45)–(5.47)*

^def-c9-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.1.3: The Renormalization Constant
> 1. To second order,
>
> $$
> Z_n^{-1} = \langle n|n\rangle = 1 + \lambda^2\sum_{k\neq n}\frac{|V_{kn}|^2}{(E_n - E_k)^2} + O(\lambda^3), \qquad Z_n = 1 - \lambda^2\sum_{k\neq n}\frac{|V_{kn}|^2}{(E_n - E_k)^2} + O(\lambda^3) ; \qquad (5.48)
> $$
>
>    the subtracted term is the probability of "leakage" into the other unperturbed states, and $Z_n < 1$.
> 2. Exactly, with $V$ and all other $E_k^{(0)}$ held fixed,
>
> $$
> Z_n = \frac{\partial E_n}{\partial E_n^{(0)}} . \qquad (5.49)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.1.4, eqs. (5.47)–(5.49) (Sakurai checks (5.49) to second order and states that it holds generally; the proof below is given here)*

^thm-c9-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. ${}_N\langle n|n\rangle_N = Z_n\langle n|n\rangle = 1$ (5.47). Expand $\langle n|n\rangle$ with the series of Theorem §C9.1.2; the cross terms $\langle n^{(0)}|n^{(N)}\rangle$ vanish, so $\langle n|n\rangle = 1 + \lambda^2\langle n^{(1)}|n^{(1)}\rangle + O(\lambda^3)$, and $\langle n^{(1)}|n^{(1)}\rangle = \langle n^{(0)}|VR_n^2V|n^{(0)}\rangle = \sum_{k\neq n}|V_{kn}|^2/(E_n - E_k)^2$.
> 2. Write $H_0 = \sum_kE_k^{(0)}|k^{(0)}\rangle\langle k^{(0)}|$ ([[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]]) and regard $E_n^{(0)}$ as a parameter of $H = H_0 + \lambda V$, with the kets $|k^{(0)}\rangle$, the other $E_k^{(0)}$ and $V$ fixed. Then $\partial H/\partial E_n^{(0)} = |n^{(0)}\rangle\langle n^{(0)}|$. For small $\lambda$ the level $E_n$ is nondegenerate, so the Feynman–Hellmann theorem ([[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]) applies to the normalized eigenket:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial E_n}{\partial E_n^{(0)}} = {}_N\langle n|\,\bigl(|n^{(0)}\rangle\langle n^{(0)}|\bigr)\,|n\rangle_N = \bigl|\langle n^{(0)}|n\rangle_N\bigr|^2 = Z_n .
> $$
>
>    Check at second order: differentiating $E_n = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda V_{nn} + \lambda^2\sum_{k\neq n}|V_{nk}|^2/(E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)})$ gives $1 - \lambda^2\sum_{k\neq n}|V_{nk}|^2/(E_n - E_k)^2$, which is item 1.

> [!example] Example §C9.1.1: Changing the Spring Constant
> Perturb the oscillator $H_0 = p^2/2m + \frac12m\omega^2x^2$ by $V = \frac12\epsilon\,m\omega^2x^2$, $\epsilon \ll 1$. The exact answer is known, $\omega \to \omega' = \sqrt{1 + \epsilon}\,\omega$ (5.52), which makes this "the silliest problem in the world" for perturbation theory (Sakurai) and therefore a clean test of Theorems §C9.1.2 and §C9.1.3.
>
> *Matrix elements.* With $x^2 = \frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}(a + a^\dagger)^2$ ([[§C3.4 The Harmonic Oscillator and Coherent States Revisited#^thm-c3-4-1|Theorem §C3.4.1]]), $V$ connects $|n\rangle$ only to $|n\rangle$ and $|n \pm 2\rangle$. The ones needed for the ground state through third order are
>
> $$
> V_{00} = \frac{\epsilon\hbar\omega}{4}, \qquad V_{20} = \frac{\epsilon\hbar\omega}{2\sqrt2}, \qquad V_{22} = \frac{5\epsilon\hbar\omega}{4} \qquad (5.54)
> $$
>
> ($V_{40}$ vanishes, $V_{22} = \frac{\epsilon m\omega^2}{2}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}(2\cdot2 + 1)$). The only energy denominator is $E_0 - E_2 = -2\hbar\omega$.
>
> *Ket and energy.* By Theorem §C9.1.2,
>
> $$
> |0\rangle = |0^{(0)}\rangle - \frac{\epsilon}{4\sqrt2}\,|2^{(0)}\rangle + O(\epsilon^2) , \qquad (5.55a)
> $$
>
> with no $|1^{(0)}\rangle$ component, as parity requires ($H$ is even; [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations]]). The energy shifts are $\Delta_0^{(1)} = \epsilon\hbar\omega/4$, $\Delta_0^{(2)} = |V_{20}|^2/(-2\hbar\omega) = -\epsilon^2\hbar\omega/16$ (5.55b), and, from the third-order formula, where only $k = l = 2$ survives in the double sum,
>
> $$
> \Delta_0^{(3)} = \frac{V_{02}V_{22}V_{20}}{(-2\hbar\omega)^2} - V_{00}\frac{|V_{20}|^2}{(-2\hbar\omega)^2} = \frac{5\epsilon^3\hbar\omega}{128} - \frac{\epsilon^3\hbar\omega}{128} = \frac{\epsilon^3\hbar\omega}{32} .
> $$
>
> The exact ground energy $\frac12\hbar\omega\sqrt{1 + \epsilon} = \frac12\hbar\omega\bigl(1 + \frac\epsilon2 - \frac{\epsilon^2}{8} + \frac{\epsilon^3}{16} - \cdots\bigr)$ (5.56) gives the shift $\hbar\omega\bigl(\frac\epsilon4 - \frac{\epsilon^2}{16} + \frac{\epsilon^3}{32}\bigr)$: all three orders agree.
>
> *Renormalization.* Theorem §C9.1.3 gives $Z_0 = 1 - |V_{20}|^2/(2\hbar\omega)^2 = 1 - \epsilon^2/32$. Exactly, $Z_0$ is the squared overlap of the two oscillator ground states, $\psi_\omega(x) = (m\omega/\pi\hbar)^{1/4}e^{-m\omega x^2/2\hbar}$ and the same with $\omega'$; the Gaussian integral gives
>
> $$
> Z_0 = \bigl|\langle 0_\omega|0_{\omega'}\rangle\bigr|^2 = \frac{2\sqrt{\omega\omega'}}{\omega + \omega'} = \frac{2(1 + \epsilon)^{1/4}}{1 + (1 + \epsilon)^{1/2}} = 1 - \frac{\epsilon^2}{32} + O(\epsilon^3)
> $$
>
> (with $\delta = \sqrt{1 + \epsilon} - 1 \approx \epsilon/2$, the middle expression is $2\sqrt{1 + \delta}/(2 + \delta) = 1 - \delta^2/8 + \cdots$). The overlap is below $1$ only at second order: the state changes at first order, the probability of staying put at second. Sakurai also checks the wave function: $x_0 = \sqrt{\hbar/m\omega} \to x_0(1 + \epsilon)^{-1/4}$ in the Gaussian reproduces (5.55a) through the Hermite polynomial $H_2$ (5.57)–(5.61).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.1.5, eqs. (5.50)–(5.61), Problems 5.5, 5.8; the third-order term and the exact $Z_0$ are computed here*

## Degenerate theory

> [!definition] Definition §C9.1.3: Degenerate Subspace Projectors; Resolvent outside the Subspace
> Let the unperturbed level $E_D^{(0)}$ be $g$-fold degenerate, with eigenspace $D$ spanned by orthonormal $|m^{(0)}\rangle$ (level B's degenerate subspace, [[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^def-b8-2-1|Def. §B8.2.1]]). Then $P_0 = \sum_{m\in D}|m^{(0)}\rangle\langle m^{(0)}|$ projects onto $D$, $P_1 = 1 - P_0$ onto the rest, and
>
> $$
> R_D \equiv \frac{P_1}{E_D^{(0)} - H_0} = \sum_{k\notin D}\frac{|k^{(0)}\rangle\langle k^{(0)}|}{E_D^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}} .
> $$
>
> The perturbed eigenkets $|l\rangle$ that grow out of the level have limits $|l^{(0)}\rangle = \lim_{\lambda\to0}|l\rangle \in D$: level B's good states. In general they are particular combinations of the $|m^{(0)}\rangle$, to be determined.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.2, text before (5.75), eqs. (5.79), (5.86)*

^def-c9-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.1.4: The Exact Effective Hamiltonian in the Degenerate Subspace
> Let $H|l\rangle = E|l\rangle$ with $E$ not an eigenvalue of $P_1(H_0 + \lambda V)P_1$ on the range of $P_1$ (true for $E$ near $E_D^{(0)}$ and small $\lambda$). Then the component outside $D$ is fixed by the component inside,
>
> $$
> P_1|l\rangle = \lambda\,G(E)\,VP_0|l\rangle , \qquad G(E) = P_1\,\frac{1}{E - H_0 - \lambda P_1VP_1}\,P_1 , \qquad (5.78)
> $$
>
> and the component inside $D$ solves the $g$-dimensional problem
>
> $$
> \Bigl[E - E_D^{(0)} - \lambda P_0VP_0 - \lambda^2P_0V\,G(E)\,VP_0\Bigr]P_0|l\rangle = 0 . \qquad (5.80)
> $$
>
> The eigenvalue problem has been reduced exactly to $D$, at the price of an energy-dependent effective Hamiltonian $E_D^{(0)} + \lambda P_0VP_0 + \lambda^2P_0VG(E)VP_0$. In the limit $\lambda \to 0$ it gives first-order degenerate perturbation theory: the first-order shifts $\Delta_l^{(1)}$ are the eigenvalues of the $g\times g$ matrix of $P_0VP_0$, roots of $\det[V - \Delta^{(1)}] = 0$, and the good states $|l^{(0)}\rangle$ are its eigenvectors, with $\Delta_l^{(1)} = \langle l^{(0)}|V|l^{(0)}\rangle$ (5.81)–(5.84).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.2, eqs. (5.75)–(5.84)*

^thm-c9-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert $1 = P_0 + P_1$ in $(E - H_0 - \lambda V)|l\rangle = 0$ and use $H_0P_0 = E_D^{(0)}P_0$:
>
> $$
> 0 = (E - E_D^{(0)} - \lambda V)P_0|l\rangle + (E - H_0 - \lambda V)P_1|l\rangle . \qquad (5.75)
> $$
>
> Project with $P_0$ and with $P_1$; $H_0$ commutes with both projectors, so $P_0H_0P_1 = 0$:
>
> $$
> (E - E_D^{(0)} - \lambda P_0VP_0)P_0|l\rangle - \lambda P_0VP_1|l\rangle = 0 , \qquad (5.76)
> $$
>
> $$
> -\lambda P_1VP_0|l\rangle + P_1(E - H_0 - \lambda P_1VP_1)P_1|l\rangle = 0 . \qquad (5.77)
> $$
>
> On the range of $P_1$ the operator $E - H_0 - \lambda P_1VP_1$ is invertible by assumption, so (5.77) gives (5.78). Substituting it into the last term of (5.76) gives (5.80). For the limit, $G(E) \to R_D$ stays bounded, so to first order (5.80) reads $(E - E_D^{(0)} - \lambda P_0VP_0)P_0|l^{(0)}\rangle = 0$ (5.81): an eigenvalue equation for the Hermitian operator $P_0VP_0$ on $D$, whose matrix in the basis $|m^{(0)}\rangle$ has elements $\langle m^{(0)}|V|m'^{(0)}\rangle$ (5.82)–(5.83). It has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]); in that basis $V$ restricted to $D$ is diagonal, which gives (5.84).

> [!remark] Remark: The W matrix as the first term of an exact reduction
> Level B obtained the first-order rule, diagonalize the W matrix ([[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]]), by taking inner products of the first-order equation with the degenerate states; the result and its proof are the same as the limit taken above, so they are not boxed again. What is new is that the rule is now the leading term of an exact equation, (5.80), whose next term says how the distant levels act: through $\lambda^2P_0VG(E)VP_0$, a second-order coupling that runs out of $D$ and back. Two consequences follow at once. If $D$ were the whole space, $P_1 = 0$ and diagonalizing $P_0VP_0$ would be exact (Sakurai). And if a Hermitian $A$ commutes with $H_0$ and $V$ and maps $D$ into itself, it commutes with $P_0$, $P_1$, $G(E)$ and so with the whole effective Hamiltonian; the good states can then be chosen as eigenkets of $A$ to all orders, which extends level B's [[§B8.2★ Degenerate Perturbation Theory#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]] beyond first order.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.1.5: Second-Order Degenerate Perturbation Theory
> Let $v$ be an eigenvalue of $P_0VP_0$ on $D$, with eigenspace $D_v \subseteq D$ and projector $Q_v$. The levels that grow out of $D_v$ have
>
> $$
> E = E_D^{(0)} + \lambda v + \lambda^2\Delta^{(2)} + O(\lambda^3),
> $$
>
> where the $\Delta^{(2)}$ are the eigenvalues of $Q_vVR_DVQ_v$ on $D_v$, that is, of the matrix
>
> $$
> M_{ij} = \sum_{k\notin D}\frac{V_{ik}V_{kj}}{E_D^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}} , \qquad |i^{(0)}\rangle, |j^{(0)}\rangle \in D_v ,
> $$
>
> and the good states are its eigenvectors. In particular:
> 1. If every eigenvalue $v_i$ of $P_0VP_0$ is simple (the degeneracy is lifted at first order), each good state $|l_i^{(0)}\rangle$ has
>
> $$
> \Delta_{l_i}^{(2)} = \sum_{k\notin D}\frac{|V_{kl_i}|^2}{E_D^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}} , \qquad (5.88)
> $$
>
>    and its first-order ket has a part outside $D$ and a part inside,
>
> $$
> P_1|l_i^{(1)}\rangle = \sum_{k\notin D}|k^{(0)}\rangle\frac{V_{kl_i}}{E_D^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}} , \qquad P_0|l_i^{(1)}\rangle = \sum_{j\neq i}|l_j^{(0)}\rangle\,\frac{\langle l_j^{(0)}|VR_DV|l_i^{(0)}\rangle}{v_i - v_j} . \qquad (5.79),\ (5.86),\ (5.87)
> $$
>
>    The second part is of order $\lambda$ although it comes from a $\lambda^2$ term, because it is divided by first-order splittings. This is Sakurai's rule: beyond first order use the nondegenerate formulas with all states of $D$ excluded from the sums.
> 2. If $v$ is a repeated eigenvalue (the degeneracy is not lifted at first order, for example $P_0VP_0 = 0$), first order does not fix the good states inside $D_v$; second order does, through $M$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.2, eqs. (5.79), (5.85)–(5.88), Problem 5.15; Griffiths Problem 7.40*

^thm-c9-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Use (5.80) with intermediate normalization $\langle l^{(0)}|l\rangle = 1$ and expand $E = E_D^{(0)} + \lambda\Delta^{(1)} + \lambda^2\Delta^{(2)} + \cdots$, $P_0|l\rangle = |l^{(0)}\rangle + \lambda|\chi\rangle + \cdots$ with $\langle l^{(0)}|\chi\rangle = 0$. Since $E - H_0 - \lambda P_1VP_1 = E_D^{(0)} - H_0 + O(\lambda)$ on the range of $P_1$, $G(E) = R_D + O(\lambda)$. The orders $\lambda$ and $\lambda^2$ of (5.80) are
>
> $$
> (\Delta^{(1)} - P_0VP_0)|l^{(0)}\rangle = 0 , \qquad (\Delta^{(1)} - P_0VP_0)|\chi\rangle + (\Delta^{(2)} - P_0VR_DVP_0)|l^{(0)}\rangle = 0 .
> $$
>
> The first gives $\Delta^{(1)} = v$ and $|l^{(0)}\rangle \in D_v$. Apply $Q_v$ to the second: $Q_v$ is the spectral projector of the Hermitian $P_0VP_0$ for $v$ ([[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]]), so $Q_vP_0VP_0 = vQ_v$ and $Q_v(v - P_0VP_0) = 0$. What remains is $Q_vVR_DVQ_v|l^{(0)}\rangle = \Delta^{(2)}|l^{(0)}\rangle$ (using $|l^{(0)}\rangle = Q_v|l^{(0)}\rangle$), the eigenvalue problem for $M$; it is Hermitian, so [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]] applies. This proves the general statement and item 2.
>
> For item 1, $D_{v_i}$ is spanned by $|l_i^{(0)}\rangle$ alone, so $\Delta^{(2)} = \langle l_i^{(0)}|VR_DV|l_i^{(0)}\rangle$, which is (5.88) after inserting $R_D$. Taking instead the inner product of the order-$\lambda^2$ equation with $\langle l_j^{(0)}|$, $j \neq i$, kills the $\Delta^{(2)}$ term and gives $(v_i - v_j)\langle l_j^{(0)}|\chi\rangle = \langle l_j^{(0)}|VR_DV|l_i^{(0)}\rangle$, which is the inside part (5.86). The outside part is (5.78) at order $\lambda$: $P_1|l^{(1)}\rangle = R_DVP_0|l^{(0)}\rangle$, which is (5.79).

> [!remark] Remark: The procedure, and nearly degenerate levels
> Sakurai's summary: (1) identify the degenerate kets and build the $g\times g$ matrix of $V$; (2) diagonalize it; (3) its eigenvalues are the first-order shifts and its eigenvectors the good zeroth-order kets; (4) for higher orders use the nondegenerate formulas with the states of $D$ excluded from the sums. Level B reached steps 1–3; Theorem §C9.1.5 is step 4 and its failure case.
>
> Real levels are rarely exactly degenerate. If two unperturbed levels differ by less than the matrix elements of $V$ between them, the nondegenerate denominators are small and the series is useless, while degenerate theory with the small splitting ignored is accurate (Sakurai Problem 5.14(c)). The safe course, in Sakurai's words for the Stark effect, is to put all nearby levels into $P_0$ and diagonalize $H$ exactly in the space they span. Likharev's form of the same idea: the exact eigenvalue problem in the unperturbed basis is a linear system, and keeping only a near-degenerate group of $N$ states turns it into an $N\times N$ diagonalization; for two states the result is the level anticrossing of a two-level system. Example §C9.2.2 of [[§C9.2 Atoms in External Fields, Fine and Hyperfine Structure]] is the standard case, and the Ritz method of [[§C9.3 The Ritz Variational Method]] is the same diagonalization in any chosen subspace.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.2 (summary after (5.88)), §5.2.1 (last paragraph), Problem 5.14; Likharev §6.1, eqs. (6.7), (6.24)–(6.27)*

> [!example] Example §C9.1.2: A Degeneracy Not Lifted at First Order
> Three unperturbed states with $H_0 = \mathrm{diag}(E_1, E_1, E_2)$, $E_2 > E_1$, and a perturbation that couples the degenerate pair only through the third state:
>
> $$
> H = \begin{pmatrix} E_1 & 0 & a\\ 0 & E_1 & b\\ a^* & b^* & E_2\end{pmatrix} , \qquad |a|, |b| \ll E_2 - E_1 .
> $$
>
> *First order fails.* $D = \mathrm{span}\{|1\rangle, |2\rangle\}$ and $P_0VP_0 = 0$: both first-order shifts vanish and every combination of $|1\rangle$, $|2\rangle$ is "good" at this order.
>
> *Naive nondegenerate theory.* Treating $|1\rangle$ and $|2\rangle$ separately (the $1/0$ terms have zero numerators) gives $E_1 - |a|^2/(E_2 - E_1)$ and $E_1 - |b|^2/(E_2 - E_1)$. Only their sum is right.
>
> *Second-order degenerate theory.* Here $D_v = D$ and $R_D = |3\rangle\langle3|/(E_1 - E_2)$, so Theorem §C9.1.5 asks for the eigenvalues of
>
> $$
> M = \frac{1}{E_1 - E_2}\begin{pmatrix} |a|^2 & ab^*\\ a^*b & |b|^2\end{pmatrix} = \frac{\mathbf u\mathbf u^\dagger}{E_1 - E_2}, \qquad \mathbf u = \begin{pmatrix} a\\ b\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> A rank-one matrix: the eigenvalues are $-(|a|^2 + |b|^2)/(E_2 - E_1)$, eigenvector $\mathbf u$, and $0$, eigenvector $(b^*, -a^*)$. So the good states are $(a|1\rangle + b|2\rangle)/\|\mathbf u\|$, pushed down by $\|\mathbf u\|^2/(E_2 - E_1)$, and $(b^*|1\rangle - a^*|2\rangle)/\|\mathbf u\|$, not shifted at all; $E_2$ rises by $\|\mathbf u\|^2/(E_2 - E_1)$.
>
> *Exact.* $H(b^*|1\rangle - a^*|2\rangle) = E_1(b^*|1\rangle - a^*|2\rangle) + (a^*b^* - b^*a^*)|3\rangle$, so that state is an exact eigenstate with energy $E_1$. The orthogonal pair couples through $\|\mathbf u\|$ as a two-level system, with $E_\pm = \frac{E_1 + E_2}2 \pm \sqrt{\bigl(\frac{E_2 - E_1}{2}\bigr)^2 + \|\mathbf u\|^2}$, whose expansion reproduces the second-order results. The figure shows the case $a = b = g$: the naive answer misses the splitting completely.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 5.15 (from Schiff; see also Griffiths Problems 7.39–7.40); solved here*
