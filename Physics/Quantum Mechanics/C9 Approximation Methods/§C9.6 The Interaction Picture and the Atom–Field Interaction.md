How does an atom absorb and emit light, how fast, and what does a strong field do to its levels? Level A described absorption, stimulated and spontaneous emission by Einstein's coefficients and stated the selection rules with a photon-angular-momentum argument ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-2|Theorem §A5.4.2]], [[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-6|Theorem §A5.4.6]]); it defined the natural line width as $\hbar/\tau$ by the energy–time relation ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^def-a3-5-1|Def. §A3.5.1]]). Level B solved a spin in a rotating field exactly ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-6|Theorem §B6.2.6]]); batch 1 supplied the pictures ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]]), the charged-particle Hamiltonian ([[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^pr-c4-3-2|Principle §C4.3.2]]) and the vanishing dipole matrix elements ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-7|Theorem §C7.2.7]]); Electromagnetism supplied classical dipole radiation and radiation damping ([[§B11.3★ Dipole Radiation#^thm-b11-3-4|EM Theorem §B11.3.4]], [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^ex-b11-4-3|EM Example §B11.4.3]]). New here: the interaction picture as a third picture of dynamics (promised in [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^rem-c3-3-4|§C3.3]]); the driven two-level system with the rotating-wave approximation, dressed states, the AC Stark shift and the Autler–Townes doublet; absorption and emission rates of an atom in a classical field, oscillator strengths and their sum rule; spontaneous emission rates and lifetimes; and the shift and width of a decaying level. The field is classical throughout; its quantization is a ★ remark at the end.

*Notation.* As in [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits|§C9.4]]: $H = H_0 + V(t)$, $H_0|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$, $\omega_{ni} = (E_n - E_i)/\hbar$, harmonic perturbations $\mathcal Ve^{i\omega t} + \mathcal V^\dagger e^{-i\omega t}$. Sakurai's $e$ is the charge of the particle ($e < 0$ for the electron) and $\alpha = e^2/\hbar c \approx 1/137$; $\mathbf d = e\mathbf x$ is the electric dipole operator of a one-electron atom.

## The interaction picture

> [!definition] Definition §C9.6.1: Interaction (Dirac) Picture
> For $H = H_0 + V(t)$ with $H_0$ time independent, the **interaction picture** assigns to a Schrödinger-picture state ket and observable
>
> $$
> |\alpha, t_0; t\rangle_I = e^{iH_0t/\hbar}\,|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle_S, \qquad A_I(t) = e^{iH_0t/\hbar}\,A_S\,e^{-iH_0t/\hbar}, \qquad\text{in particular}\quad V_I(t) = e^{iH_0t/\hbar}\,V(t)\,e^{-iH_0t/\hbar} .
> $$
>
> At $t = 0$ the three pictures coincide. The interaction picture is the Heisenberg picture of [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^def-c3-3-1|Def. §C3.3.1]] with the full $H$ replaced by $H_0$ in the exponentials: the known motion is put on the operators, the unknown motion caused by $V$ is left on the kets.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.5.2, eqs. (5.177)–(5.181) · the user's series, Part IV, §7.4 (the interaction picture flagged and deferred)*

^def-c9-6-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.6.1: Dynamics in the Interaction Picture
> 1. State kets obey a Schrödinger equation with $V_I$ in place of $H$, and observables a Heisenberg equation with $H_0$ in place of $H$:
>
> $$
> i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle_I = V_I(t)\,|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle_I, \qquad \frac{dA_I}{dt} = \frac1{i\hbar}\bigl[A_I, H_0\bigr] \quad (A_S \text{ without explicit time dependence}) .
> $$
>
> 2. The state ket evolves by the operator $\mathscr U_I(t, t_0) = e^{iH_0t/\hbar}\mathscr U(t, t_0)e^{-iH_0t_0/\hbar}$ of [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^def-c9-4-1|Def. §C9.4.1]], $|\alpha, t_0; t\rangle_I = \mathscr U_I(t, t_0)|\alpha, t_0; t_0\rangle_I$, which is the Dyson series in $V_I$ ([[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-2|Theorem §C9.4.2]]); its components in the eigenkets of $H_0$ are the amplitudes $c_n(t)$ of time-dependent perturbation theory.
> 3. All matrix elements and expectation values agree with those of the other two pictures: ${}_I\langle\beta; t|A_I(t)|\alpha; t\rangle_I = {}_S\langle\beta; t|A_S|\alpha; t\rangle_S$.
> 4. If $V = 0$ the interaction picture is the Heisenberg picture (states frozen); if $H_0 = 0$ it is the Schrödinger picture.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.5.2, eqs. (5.182)–(5.187), Table 5.2; §5.7.1–5.7.2, eqs. (5.261)–(5.268) · Likharev §6.5, footnote 26*

^thm-c9-6-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Differentiate $e^{iH_0t/\hbar}|\alpha; t\rangle_S$ and use $i\hbar\,\partial_t|\alpha; t\rangle_S = (H_0 + V)|\alpha; t\rangle_S$ ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]]): the $H_0$ terms cancel, leaving $e^{iH_0t/\hbar}V|\alpha; t\rangle_S = V_I\,e^{iH_0t/\hbar}|\alpha; t\rangle_S$. For the operator, differentiate $e^{iH_0t/\hbar}A_Se^{-iH_0t/\hbar}$ as in the derivation of the Heisenberg equation ([[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-4|Theorem §C3.3.4]]), with $H_0$ for $H$.
>
> *2.* $e^{iH_0t/\hbar}\mathscr U(t, t_0)|\alpha; t_0\rangle_S = \mathscr U_I(t, t_0)\,e^{iH_0t_0/\hbar}|\alpha; t_0\rangle_S$; this is [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-1|Theorem §C9.4.1]], with $c_n = \langle n|\alpha; t\rangle_I$.
>
> *3.* Kets and operators are transformed together by the same unitary $e^{iH_0t/\hbar}$, which leaves every matrix element unchanged (items 1–2 of [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^thm-c3-3-1|Theorem §C3.3.1]]): ${}_I\langle\beta|A_I|\alpha\rangle_I = {}_S\langle\beta|e^{-iH_0t/\hbar}\,e^{iH_0t/\hbar}A_Se^{-iH_0t/\hbar}\,e^{iH_0t/\hbar}|\alpha\rangle_S$.
>
> *4.* With $V = 0$ part 1 freezes the kets and $H_0 = H$; with $H_0 = 0$ the transformation is the identity.

> [!remark] Remark: Three pictures
> The bookkeeping of [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^rem-c3-3-3|§C3.3, Remark: The bookkeeping in one table]] gains a middle column (Sakurai's Table 5.2):
>
> | | Heisenberg | interaction (Dirac) | Schrödinger |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | state ket | no change | evolution by $V_I$ | evolution by $H$ |
> | observable | evolution by $H$ | evolution by $H_0$ | no change |
>
> The interaction picture is the natural setting of time-dependent perturbation theory ([[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits|§C9.4]]) and of scattering, and it is the picture in which quantum field theory computes: the free fields evolve as Heisenberg operators under $H_0$, the interaction is expanded in Dyson's series ([[Quantum Field Theory I]]). The user's series flags it at the end of its treatment of pictures as "the natural home of time-dependent perturbation theory; it belongs to the sequel course", and lists transition rates, the golden rule "and the actual strength of the spectral lines" as deferred in its outlook (§17); this chapter delivers them. Likharev obtains the amplitude equations without the picture, to keep the physics transparent, and notes that they are the interaction picture in disguise.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.5.2, Table 5.2 · the user's series, Part IV, §7.4 (Remark "One theory, many coordinates") and §17 · Likharev §6.5, footnote 26*

## A two-level system in a resonant field

> [!model] Model §C9.6.2: Two-Level System Driven at Frequency ω
> Two eigenstates of $H_0$, $E_2 > E_1$, coupled by a field of angular frequency $\omega$:
>
> $$
> H_0 = E_1|1\rangle\langle1| + E_2|2\rangle\langle2|, \qquad V(t) = \gamma\,e^{i\omega t}|1\rangle\langle2| + \gamma\,e^{-i\omega t}|2\rangle\langle1| ,
> $$
>
> with $\gamma$ and $\omega$ real and positive; $\omega_{21} = (E_2 - E_1)/\hbar$ and the **detuning** is $\Delta = \omega - \omega_{21}$. Instances:
> - a spin in a static field $B_0\hat z$ plus a field $B_1$ rotating in the $xy$-plane (exactly of this form, with $\hbar\omega_{21} = |e|\hbar B_0/m_ec$ and $\gamma = -e\hbar B_1/2m_ec$ for an electron, Sakurai eqs. (5.198)–(5.203); level B's [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-6|Theorem §B6.2.6]]);
> - the inversion doublet $|S\rangle$, $|A\rangle$ of ammonia in a microwave cavity, coupled by the dipole moment (the maser);
> - two levels of an atom in light of frequency near $\omega_{21}$, with $\gamma = \frac12E_0|\langle2|\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf d|1\rangle|$ for a field $E_0\boldsymbol\epsilon\cos\omega t$, after the rotating-wave approximation (Remark below).
>
> *Assumptions:* the field is classical and prescribed; all other levels are far off resonance and ignored; no decay. For a linearly polarized drive, the counter-rotating terms are dropped.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.5.3–5.5.5, eqs. (5.190)–(5.211) · Likharev §6.5, eqs. (6.93)–(6.94) · Griffiths §11.1.3, Problem 11.9*

^mod-c9-6-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.6.3: Rabi's Formula
> In Model §C9.6.2, with $c_1(0) = 1$, $c_2(0) = 0$, exactly
>
> $$
> |c_2(t)|^2 = \frac{\gamma^2/\hbar^2}{\gamma^2/\hbar^2 + \Delta^2/4}\,\sin^2\Omega t, \qquad |c_1(t)|^2 = 1 - |c_2(t)|^2, \qquad \Omega = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma^2}{\hbar^2} + \frac{\Delta^2}4} .
> $$
>
> 1. At resonance, $\Delta = 0$, the system flops completely between $|1\rangle$ (absorption) and $|2\rangle$ (emission) with $\Omega = \gamma/\hbar$: $|c_2|^2 = 1$ at $t = \pi\hbar/2\gamma$ (a "$\pi$ pulse"), back to $|1\rangle$ at $t = \pi\hbar/\gamma$, and so on.
> 2. The maximum $|c_2|^2_{\max} = (\gamma^2/\hbar^2)/(\gamma^2/\hbar^2 + \Delta^2/4)$ is a Lorentzian in $\omega$ with full width at half maximum $4\gamma/\hbar$: the weaker the drive, the sharper the resonance (and a strong drive broadens it, **power broadening**).
> 3. For $\gamma \ll \hbar|\Delta|$, $|c_2|^2 \approx \frac{4\gamma^2}{\hbar^2\Delta^2}\sin^2\frac{\Delta t}2$, the first-order result of [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-4|Theorem §C9.4.4]].
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.5.3, eqs. (5.192)–(5.197), Figs. 5.5–5.6; Problem 5.37 (solved here) · Likharev §6.5, eqs. (6.95)–(6.102), Fig. 6.9 · Griffiths Problem 11.9*

^thm-c9-6-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-1|Theorem §C9.4.1]], with $V_{12} = \gamma e^{i\omega t}$, $V_{21} = \gamma e^{-i\omega t}$, $V_{11} = V_{22} = 0$ and $\omega_{12} = -\omega_{21}$:
>
> $$
> i\hbar\,\dot c_1 = \gamma\,e^{i\Delta t}c_2, \qquad i\hbar\,\dot c_2 = \gamma\,e^{-i\Delta t}c_1 .
> $$
>
> The explicit time dependence goes away with $c_1 = e^{i\Delta t/2}b_1$, $c_2 = e^{-i\Delta t/2}b_2$:
>
> $$
> i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}b_1\\b_2\end{pmatrix} = \tilde H\begin{pmatrix}b_1\\b_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \tilde H = \begin{pmatrix}\hbar\Delta/2 & \gamma\\ \gamma & -\hbar\Delta/2\end{pmatrix} = \hbar\Omega\,\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma, \qquad \hat n = \Bigl(\frac{\gamma}{\hbar\Omega}, 0, \frac{\Delta}{2\Omega}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> $\tilde H$ is a time-independent two-state Hamiltonian, so ([[§C3.2 Two-State Systems, Spin Precession and Neutrino Oscillations#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]]) $b(t) = e^{-i\Omega t\,\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma}b(0) = (\cos\Omega t - i\sin\Omega t\,\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\,b(0)$. With $b(0) = (1, 0)$, $b_2(t) = -in_x\sin\Omega t = -i\frac{\gamma}{\hbar\Omega}\sin\Omega t$, and $|c_2| = |b_2|$. Parts 1–3: set $\Delta = 0$; the half maximum of $|c_2|^2_{\max}$ is at $\Delta^2/4 = \gamma^2/\hbar^2$, $\Delta = \pm2\gamma/\hbar$; for $\gamma/\hbar \ll |\Delta|$, $\Omega \to |\Delta|/2$.

*Uses:* [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-1|Theorem §C9.4.1]], [[§C3.2 Two-State Systems, Spin Precession and Neutrino Oscillations#^thm-c3-2-2|Theorem §C3.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The rotating-wave approximation, and what level C adds to level B
> - **A rotating field is exactly the model.** For a spin in $B_0\hat z + B_1(\hat x\cos\omega t + \hat y\sin\omega t)$, the coupling has only the two terms of Model §C9.6.2, and Theorem §C9.6.3 is exact. That is level B's rotating-frame solution ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-6|Theorem §B6.2.6]]); here the same transformation, $c_n = e^{\pm i\Delta t/2}b_n$, is a passage to a rotating frame in the abstract two-level space, valid for any physical realization (rule 2).
> - **A linear field is not.** A field $2B_1\hat x\cos\omega t$ is the sum of a co-rotating and a counter-rotating field (Sakurai eq. (5.204)); in an atom, $E_0\cos\omega t = \frac{E_0}2(e^{i\omega t} + e^{-i\omega t})$ couples $|1\rangle \to |2\rangle$ through both exponentials. The counter-rotating part acts like the model with $\omega \to -\omega$, detuned by about $2\omega_{21}$; when $\gamma/\hbar \ll \omega_{21}$ (for spins, $B_1 \ll B_0$, Sakurai eqs. (5.206)–(5.207)) its effect is small and fast. Dropping it is the **rotating-wave approximation** (RWA; Griffiths Problem 11.9; Likharev footnote 30, who compares the van der Pol method for classical oscillators). Its leading effect is a small shift of the resonance: by Theorem §C9.6.12 the counter-rotating term, detuned by $\omega_{21} + \omega \approx 2\omega_{21}$, pushes the two levels apart by $\gamma^2/2\hbar\omega_{21}$ each, so the resonance moves up by about $\gamma^2/\hbar^2\omega_{21}$ (the Bloch–Siegert shift).
> - **The maser** (Sakurai §5.5.5). Ammonia's parity doublet has $\langle A|\mathbf d|A\rangle = \langle S|\mathbf d|S\rangle = 0$ but $\langle S|\mathbf x|A\rangle \ne 0$. An inhomogeneous electric field selects molecules in the upper state $|A\rangle$; a cavity tuned to $(E_A - E_S)/\hbar$, whose transit time is half a Rabi cycle, $\frac\pi2\hbar/\gamma$, lets each molecule leave in $|S\rangle$, its energy given to the field: Microwave Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.
> - **Promises kept.** Batch 1 deferred "general driven two-level systems … and transitions induced by oscillating fields" to this chapter ([[§C3.2 Two-State Systems, Spin Precession and Neutrino Oscillations#^rem-c3-2-4|§C3.2, Remark: Beyond static two-level Hamiltonians]]); level B's resonance ([[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^rem-b6-2-5|Remark: Magnetic resonance]]) promised "the quantum mechanics of transitions driven by oscillating fields in general" for level C: the general case is this model plus the RWA, and away from two isolated levels, Theorem §C9.4.4 and the golden rule.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.5.4–5.5.5, eqs. (5.198)–(5.211), footnote 5 · Griffiths Problem 11.9 · Likharev §6.5, footnote 30*

^rem-c9-6-2

> [!caution] Caution: Which Rabi frequency
> Sakurai's $\Omega$ (and Griffiths' $\omega_r$, Likharev's $\Omega$) is half the angular frequency at which the populations oscillate: $|c_2|^2 \propto \sin^2\Omega t = \frac12(1 - \cos2\Omega t)$. Many texts call $2\Omega$, or its resonant value $2\gamma/\hbar$, the Rabi frequency. Level B's $\Omega$ in [[§B6.2 Spin in a Magnetic Field#^thm-b6-2-6|Theorem §B6.2.6]] is the full precession frequency in the rotating frame, $P \propto \sin^2(\Omega t/2)$, i.e. twice the $\Omega$ used here. Griffiths' $V_{ab}$ for a perturbation $V\cos\omega t$ is $2\gamma$.

^cau-c9-6-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.6.4: Dressed States and the AC Stark Shift of a Two-Level System
> In Model §C9.6.2, let $\cos\varphi = \Delta/2\Omega$, $\sin\varphi = \gamma/\hbar\Omega$ ($0 < \varphi < \pi$). The eigenkets of the rotating-frame Hamiltonian $\tilde H$,
>
> $$
> |\tilde +\rangle = \cos\tfrac\varphi2|1\rangle + \sin\tfrac\varphi2|2\rangle, \qquad |\tilde -\rangle = -\sin\tfrac\varphi2|1\rangle + \cos\tfrac\varphi2|2\rangle, \qquad \tilde H|\tilde\pm\rangle = \pm\hbar\Omega|\tilde\pm\rangle ,
> $$
>
> the **dressed states**, give two exact solutions of the driven problem,
>
> $$
> |\psi_\pm(t)\rangle = e^{\mp i\Omega t}\Bigl[\langle1|\tilde\pm\rangle\,e^{-i(E_1/\hbar - \Delta/2)t}|1\rangle + \langle2|\tilde\pm\rangle\,e^{-i(E_2/\hbar + \Delta/2)t}|2\rangle\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> in which every component oscillates at a fixed frequency: $|\psi_\pm\rangle$ has the **quasi-energy** $\varepsilon_\pm = E_1 - \hbar\Delta/2 \pm \hbar\Omega$ on $|1\rangle$ and $\varepsilon_\pm + \hbar\omega$ on $|2\rangle$. Every solution is a superposition of the two, and the Rabi oscillation is their beat at $2\Omega$.
> 1. At resonance the dressed states are $(|1\rangle \pm |2\rangle)/\sqrt2$, split by $2\gamma$.
> 2. **AC Stark (light) shift.** Far from resonance, $|\Delta| \gg \gamma/\hbar$, the dressed state that continues $|1\rangle$ has quasi-energy $E_1 + \delta E_1$ and the one that continues $|2\rangle$ has $E_2 + \delta E_2$, with
>
> $$
> \delta E_1 = \frac{\gamma^2}{\hbar\Delta} = \frac{\gamma^2}{\hbar(\omega - \omega_{21})}, \qquad \delta E_2 = -\frac{\gamma^2}{\hbar\Delta} .
> $$
>
> For red detuning ($\omega < \omega_{21}$) the lower level is pushed down and the upper one up; for blue detuning they are pushed together. The shifts are proportional to the intensity of the drive ($\gamma^2 \propto E_0^2$) and do not depend on the sign of $\gamma$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.5.3 (the rotating-frame equations behind (5.193)) · Likharev §6.5, eqs. (6.95)–(6.99) and footnote 32 (the shift of the lowest level, "a particular case of the ac Stark effect"); the dressed-state solutions, their quasi-energies and the shifts are worked out here*

^thm-c9-6-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\tilde H = \hbar\Omega\,\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with $\hat n = (\sin\varphi, 0, \cos\varphi)$ (derivation of Theorem §C9.6.3), whose eigenkets with eigenvalues $\pm1$ are the stated ones ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-2|Theorem §C5.2.2]] with $\beta = \varphi$, $\alpha = 0$). So $b(t) = e^{\mp i\Omega t}(\langle1|\tilde\pm\rangle, \langle2|\tilde\pm\rangle)$ solves the $b$ equations; undo the substitutions, $c_1 = e^{i\Delta t/2}b_1$, $c_2 = e^{-i\Delta t/2}b_2$, and restore the free phases $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ of [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^def-c9-4-1|Def. §C9.4.1]]. The $|2\rangle$ frequency is $E_2/\hbar + \Delta/2 \pm \Omega = (E_1/\hbar - \Delta/2 \pm \Omega) + \omega_{21} + \Delta$, and $\omega_{21} + \Delta = \omega$. The two $|\psi_\pm\rangle$ are orthonormal at every time (the $b$ vectors are), hence a basis of solutions; the beat frequency of $e^{\mp i\Omega t}$ is $2\Omega$.
>
> *1.* $\Delta = 0$: $\varphi = \pi/2$, $\hbar\Omega = \gamma$.
>
> *2.* Let $\Delta > 0$. Then $\cos\varphi \to 1$, $|\tilde +\rangle \to |1\rangle$, and $\varepsilon_+ = E_1 - \frac{\hbar\Delta}2 + \frac{\hbar\Delta}2\sqrt{1 + 4\gamma^2/\hbar^2\Delta^2} = E_1 + \frac{\gamma^2}{\hbar\Delta} + O(\gamma^4)$. The state continuing $|2\rangle$ is $|\tilde -\rangle$, with $|2\rangle$ component at $\varepsilon_- + \hbar\omega = E_2 + \frac{\hbar\Delta}2 - \hbar\Omega = E_2 - \frac{\gamma^2}{\hbar\Delta} + O(\gamma^4)$. For $\Delta < 0$ the roles of $|\tilde\pm\rangle$ exchange and the same formulas result. Theorem §C9.6.12 below recovers both shifts from second-order perturbation theory, for any number of levels.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.6.5: Autler–Townes Doublet
> Let the pair $|1\rangle$, $|2\rangle$ be driven as in Model §C9.6.2, and let a weak **probe** of frequency $\omega_p$ couple a third level $|3\rangle$ (energy $E_3 < E_2$, initially occupied and not touched by the strong field) to $|2\rangle$: $W(t) = w\,e^{-i\omega_pt}|2\rangle\langle3| + w^*e^{i\omega_pt}|3\rangle\langle2|$. To first order in $w$, absorption of the probe from $|3\rangle$ is resonant not at $\omega_{23} = (E_2 - E_3)/\hbar$ but at the two frequencies
>
> $$
> \omega_p = \omega_{23} + \frac\Delta2 \pm \Omega, \qquad\text{with strengths}\qquad |\langle2|\tilde\pm\rangle|^2 = \frac12\Bigl(1 \mp \frac{\Delta}{2\Omega}\Bigr), \quad\text{summing to } 1 .
> $$
>
> 1. At resonance of the strong field the probe line splits into two equal lines at $\omega_{23} \pm \gamma/\hbar$, separated by $2\gamma/\hbar$, the frequency of the population flopping of Theorem §C9.6.3: the **Autler–Townes doublet**.
> 2. Far from resonance the strong line sits at $\omega_{23} - \gamma^2/\hbar^2\Delta$, displaced by the AC Stark shift $\delta E_2$ of Theorem §C9.6.4, and a weak line of strength $\approx\gamma^2/\hbar^2\Delta^2$ appears near $\omega_{23} + \Delta$, where probe and drive together bridge $|3\rangle \to |1\rangle$ (a two-photon resonance, $\hbar\omega_p - \hbar\omega = E_1 - E_3$).
>
> The doublet is resolved when $2\Omega$ exceeds the widths of the levels and of the probe.
>
> *Source: no typed source treats the Autler–Townes effect (PHY 512 covered it in lecture in the week of March 30; Sakurai, Griffiths and Likharev do not). Derived here from Model §C9.6.2 and Theorems §C9.4.2, §C9.6.4.*

^thm-c9-6-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take as "unperturbed" problem the driven pair plus the untouched level, whose exact solutions are $|\psi_\pm(t)\rangle$ (Theorem §C9.6.4) and $e^{-iE_3t/\hbar}|3\rangle$, and treat $W$ to first order, as in [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-2|Theorem §C9.4.2]] (the derivation used only that the unperturbed evolution is known and unitary). The first-order amplitude to land in $|\psi_\pm\rangle$ is
>
> $$
> c_\pm^{(1)}(t) = -\frac i\hbar\int_0^tdt'\,\langle\psi_\pm(t')|\,W(t')\,e^{-iE_3t'/\hbar}|3\rangle = -\frac{i\,w}\hbar\,\langle\tilde\pm|2\rangle\int_0^tdt'\,e^{i(E_2/\hbar + \Delta/2 \pm \Omega - E_3/\hbar - \omega_p)t'} ,
> $$
>
> since $W$ takes $|3\rangle$ only to $|2\rangle$, and the $|2\rangle$ component of $|\psi_\pm\rangle$ oscillates at $E_2/\hbar + \Delta/2 \pm \Omega$. This is a constant-perturbation integral ([[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-3|Theorem §C9.4.3]]): it grows with $t$ only at the stated resonances, with strength $|w|^2|\langle2|\tilde\pm\rangle|^2$, and with any finite width of the levels or the probe the absorbed power is a sum of two lines with these weights. From Theorem §C9.6.4, $|\langle2|\tilde +\rangle|^2 = \sin^2\frac\varphi2 = \frac12(1 - \cos\varphi)$ and $|\langle2|\tilde -\rangle|^2 = \frac12(1 + \cos\varphi)$, with $\cos\varphi = \Delta/2\Omega$. *1:* $\Delta = 0$ gives $\Omega = \gamma/\hbar$ and weights $\frac12$. *2:* for $\Delta \gg \gamma/\hbar$, $\frac\Delta2 - \Omega \approx -\frac{\gamma^2}{\hbar^2\Delta}$ with weight $\to 1$, and $\frac\Delta2 + \Omega \approx \Delta + \frac{\gamma^2}{\hbar^2\Delta}$ with weight $\frac12(1 - \cos\varphi) \approx \gamma^2/\hbar^2\Delta^2$; similarly for $\Delta \ll -\gamma/\hbar$ with the branches exchanged.

## Atoms in a classical radiation field

> [!model] Model §C9.6.6: Atom in a Classical Radiation Field
> An atomic electron of charge $e$ and mass $m_e$ in the field of an electromagnetic wave has, in the Coulomb gauge $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 0$, the Hamiltonian
>
> $$
> H = \underbrace{\frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m_e} + e\phi(\mathbf x)}_{H_0} \;\underbrace{-\;\frac{e}{m_ec}\,\mathbf A(\mathbf x, t)\cdot\mathbf p}_{V(t)} ,
> $$
>
> with $\phi$ the static atomic potential. For a monochromatic plane wave of linear polarization $\boldsymbol\epsilon$ and propagation direction $\hat{\mathbf n}$ ($\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\hat{\mathbf n} = 0$), $\mathbf k = (\omega/c)\hat{\mathbf n}$,
>
> $$
> \mathbf A = 2A_0\boldsymbol\epsilon\cos(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x - \omega t), \qquad V(t) = \mathcal Ve^{i\omega t} + \mathcal V^\dagger e^{-i\omega t}, \qquad \mathcal V^\dagger = -\frac{eA_0}{m_ec}\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf p ,
> $$
>
> so the $e^{-i\omega t}$ term drives absorption and the $e^{+i\omega t}$ term stimulated emission ([[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-4|Theorem §C9.4.4]]).
>
> *Assumptions:* the field is classical (not quantized) and prescribed, unaffected by the atom; nonrelativistic electron; the term $e^2\mathbf A^2/2m_ec^2$ is dropped (it is second order in the field and does not cause one-photon transitions in first order); the spin coupling $-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B$, which gives the weaker magnetic transitions, is dropped. Where stated, the further idealization $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} \approx 1$ (wavelength long compared with the atom) is made: the **electric dipole approximation**, whose accuracy and consequences are Theorem §C9.6.8.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.8.1, eqs. (5.307)–(5.313) · Griffiths §11.2.1 (the field's electric part only, $H' = -q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf r$, eqs. (11.38)–(11.41))*

^mod-c9-6-6

> [!remark] Remark: Where the Hamiltonian comes from
> It is the charged-particle Hamiltonian of [[§C4.3 Potentials, Gauge Transformations and the Aharonov–Bohm Effect#^pr-c4-3-2|Principle §C4.3.2]], applied to a time-dependent $\mathbf A$, expanded: $(\mathbf p - e\mathbf A/c)^2 = \mathbf p^2 - \frac ec(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A + \mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p) + \frac{e^2}{c^2}\mathbf A^2$. In the Coulomb gauge $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A = \mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p$ (since $[p_j, A_j] = -i\hbar\,\partial_jA_j$ sums to $-i\hbar\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 0$), giving $-\frac e{m_ec}\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p$; and $\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf p$ commutes with $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}$ because $\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf k = 0$, so the order in $\mathcal V^\dagger$ does not matter. In the Coulomb gauge a radiation field needs no scalar potential, so $\phi$ is the atom's own. The dropped $\mathbf A^2$ term is quadratic in the field amplitude and enters only processes involving two photons.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.8.1, eqs. (5.307)–(5.311)*

^rem-c9-6-3

> [!definition] Definition §C9.6.2: Absorption Cross-Section; Oscillator Strength
> The **absorption cross-section** for $i \to n$ is
>
> $$
> \sigma_{\text{abs}} = \frac{\text{energy per unit time absorbed by the atom } (i \to n)}{\text{energy flux of the radiation field}} .
> $$
>
> The **oscillator strength** of the transition $i \to n$, for polarization along $x$, is
>
> $$
> f_{ni} = \frac{2m_e\omega_{ni}}\hbar\,|\langle n|x|i\rangle|^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.8.1, eq. (5.316); §5.8.2, eq. (5.330)*

^def-c9-6-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.6.7: Absorption by a Plane Wave
> In Model §C9.6.6, the rate of absorption $i \to n$ and the absorption cross-section are
>
> $$
> w_{i\to n} = \frac{2\pi}\hbar\,\frac{e^2}{m_e^2c^2}\,|A_0|^2\,\bigl|\langle n|e^{i(\omega/c)\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf x}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf p|i\rangle\bigr|^2\,\delta(E_n - E_i - \hbar\omega) ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \sigma_{\text{abs}} = \frac{4\pi^2\hbar}{m_e^2\omega}\Bigl(\frac{e^2}{\hbar c}\Bigr)\bigl|\langle n|e^{i(\omega/c)\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf x}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf p|i\rangle\bigr|^2\,\delta(E_n - E_i - \hbar\omega) ,
> $$
>
> which has the dimension of an area. Stimulated emission $n \to i$ has the same matrix element in modulus. If $|n\rangle$ lies in a continuum the $\delta$ is integrated with $\rho(E_n)$ (the photoelectric effect); for a discrete $|n\rangle$ with a finite lifetime, collisions, or light that is not monochromatic, it is replaced by a normalized line shape, e.g. the Lorentzian $\delta(\omega - \omega_{ni}) \to \frac{\Gamma/\hbar}{2\pi}\bigl[(\omega - \omega_{ni})^2 + \Gamma^2/4\hbar^2\bigr]^{-1}$ (Sakurai eq. (5.315)), with $\Gamma$ the width of Theorem §C9.6.11 (not the coupling $\gamma$ of Model §C9.6.2).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.8.1, eqs. (5.312)–(5.320) · Griffiths §11.2.2, eqs. (11.42)–(11.43)*

^thm-c9-6-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Rate.* [[§C9.5 Fermi's Golden Rule#^thm-c9-5-2|Theorem §C9.5.2]] (absorption) with $\mathcal V^\dagger_{ni} = -\frac{eA_0}{m_ec}\langle n|e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf p|i\rangle$.
>
> *Flux.* $\mathbf E = -\frac1c\partial_t\mathbf A = -\frac{2\omega A_0}c\boldsymbol\epsilon\sin(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x - \omega t)$ and $|\mathbf B| = |\nabla\times\mathbf A| = |\mathbf E|$ for a plane wave, so the time-averaged energy density is $\mathcal U = \frac1{8\pi}\bigl(\langle E^2\rangle + \langle B^2\rangle\bigr) = \frac{\omega^2|A_0|^2}{2\pi c^2}$ and the energy flux $c\,\mathcal U = \frac{\omega^2|A_0|^2}{2\pi c}$.
>
> *Cross-section.* Each absorption takes $\hbar\omega$: $\sigma = \hbar\omega\,w/(c\,\mathcal U) = \hbar\omega\cdot\frac{2\pi e^2|A_0|^2|M|^2\delta}{\hbar m_e^2c^2}\cdot\frac{2\pi c}{\omega^2|A_0|^2} = \frac{4\pi^2e^2}{m_e^2c\,\omega}|M|^2\delta$, which is the stated form. *Dimension* (Sakurai): $[1/M^2T][M^2L^2/T^2]T = L^2$, using $\alpha$ dimensionless and $\delta(E)$ of dimension $1/E$. *Emission:* $|\mathcal V_{in}| = |\mathcal V^\dagger_{ni}|$ ([[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-4|Theorem §C9.4.4]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.6.8: Electric Dipole Approximation; Oscillator-Strength Sum Rule
> 1. The dipole approximation $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} \approx 1$ of Model §C9.6.6 neglects terms smaller by powers of $kR_{\text{atom}}$, and for a light atom $kR_{\text{atom}} \sim Z/137 \ll 1$.
> 2. For $H_0 = \mathbf p^2/2m_e + V(\mathbf x)$, $\langle n|\mathbf p|i\rangle = im_e\omega_{ni}\langle n|\mathbf x|i\rangle$, so in this approximation
>
> $$
> \sigma_{\text{abs}}(\omega) = 4\pi^2\alpha\,\omega_{ni}\,\bigl|\langle n|\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf x|i\rangle\bigr|^2\,\delta(\omega - \omega_{ni}) ,
> $$
>
> and the transition is governed by the matrix element of the dipole operator $\mathbf d = e\mathbf x$ (an **E1 transition**). Its selection rules are those of the rank-1 tensor $\mathbf x$ ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-7|Theorem §C7.2.7]]): $\Delta\ell = \pm1$, $\Delta m = q$ with $q = 0$ for $\boldsymbol\epsilon = \hat z$ and $q = \pm1$ for $\boldsymbol\epsilon$ in the $xy$-plane; with spin, $\Delta j = 0, \pm1$, not $0 \to 0$.
> 3. **Sum rule.** For any state $|i\rangle$, $\sum_nf_{ni} = 1$ (Thomas–Reiche–Kuhn). If $|i\rangle$ is the ground state, the integrated absorption cross-section is
>
> $$
> \int\sigma_{\text{abs}}(\omega)\,d\omega = \sum_n4\pi^2\alpha\,\omega_{ni}|\langle n|x|i\rangle|^2 = \frac{2\pi^2\alpha\hbar}{m_e}\sum_nf_{ni} = \frac{2\pi^2e^2}{m_ec} ,
> $$
>
> independent of $\hbar$ and of the atom: in total, an atom absorbs exactly as much as one classical, harmonically bound electron.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.8.2, eqs. (5.321)–(5.332) · Griffiths §11.2.1, §11.3.3*

^thm-c9-6-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1* (Sakurai). $\hbar\omega$ is of the order of the level spacing, $Ze^2/(a_0/Z)$, so $\frac c\omega \sim \frac{\hbar c\,R_{\text{atom}}}{Ze^2} = \frac{137R_{\text{atom}}}Z$ with $R_{\text{atom}} \sim a_0/Z$; hence $kR_{\text{atom}} \sim Z/137$. The matrix elements of $x$, $x^2, \dots$ are of order $R_{\text{atom}}, R_{\text{atom}}^2, \dots$, so each further term of $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} = 1 + i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x + \cdots$ is smaller by $kR_{\text{atom}}$.
>
> *2.* $[\mathbf x, H_0] = [\mathbf x, \mathbf p^2]/2m_e = i\hbar\mathbf p/m_e$ ([[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-6|Theorem §C2.2.6]]), so $\langle n|\mathbf p|i\rangle = \frac{m_e}{i\hbar}\langle n|[\mathbf x, H_0]|i\rangle = \frac{m_e}{i\hbar}(E_i - E_n)\langle n|\mathbf x|i\rangle = im_e\omega_{ni}\langle n|\mathbf x|i\rangle$. Insert into Theorem §C9.6.7 with $\delta(E_n - E_i - \hbar\omega) = \delta(\omega_{ni} - \omega)/\hbar$: $\frac{4\pi^2\hbar\alpha}{m_e^2\omega}\,m_e^2\omega_{ni}^2|\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf x_{ni}|^2\frac{\delta(\omega - \omega_{ni})}{\hbar}$, and $\omega = \omega_{ni}$ on the support. $\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf x = \sum_q(\cdots)x_q$ in spherical components, which gives the selection rules by Theorem §C7.2.7.
>
> *3.* $\sum_nf_{ni} = \frac{2m_e}{\hbar^2}\sum_n(E_n - E_i)|\langle n|x|i\rangle|^2 = 1$ is the sum rule proved from $[[H_0, x], x] = -\hbar^2/m_e$ in [[§C3.3 The Schrödinger and Heisenberg Pictures#^ex-c3-3-2|Example §C3.3.2]] (the proof there is for one dimension; for $H_0 = \mathbf p^2/2m_e + V(\mathbf x)$ the same double commutator gives the same result for each Cartesian component). For the ground state all $\omega_{ni} > 0$, so integrating the $\delta$'s gives $\sum4\pi^2\alpha\omega_{ni}|x_{ni}|^2 = \frac{2\pi^2\alpha\hbar}{m_e}\sum f_{ni}$, and $\alpha\hbar = e^2/c$.

> [!remark] Remark: The dipole approximation, classically and in Griffiths' form
> - **The classical oscillator agrees.** A charge bound harmonically and damped only by its own radiation has a resonance with peak cross-section $3\lambda_0^2/2\pi$ and width $\gamma_{\text{cl}} = \omega_0^2\tau$, $\tau = 2e^2/3m_ec^3$ ([[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^ex-b11-4-3|EM Example §B11.4.3]], SI converted by the dictionary above). The area under its Lorentzian is $\frac\pi2\gamma_{\text{cl}}\,\sigma_{\max} = \frac\pi2\omega_0^2\tau\cdot\frac{6\pi c^2}{\omega_0^2} = 3\pi^2c^2\tau = \frac{2\pi^2e^2}{m_ec}$: the classical oscillation sum rule, which the quantum one reproduces (Sakurai: "how 'new quantum mechanics' led to the correct classical result"). So the oscillator strength $f_{ni}$ is the fraction of a classical electron that the transition $i \to n$ represents, the meaning of the $f_j$ in the Lorentz model of dispersion ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-7|WO Theorem §B13.1.7]]), which Optics could only fit ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^rem-b13-1-5|WO Remark: The quantum picture of absorption lines]]).
> - **$\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p$ versus $-\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E$.** Griffiths writes the perturbation as $-q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf r$ with a uniform field $E_0\boldsymbol\epsilon\cos\omega t$. Since $E_0 = 2\omega A_0/c$, its absorption amplitude has modulus $\frac12eE_0|\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf x_{ni}| = \frac{eA_0\omega}c|\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf x_{ni}|$, while Sakurai's is $\frac{eA_0}{m_ec}|\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf p_{ni}| = \frac{eA_0\omega_{ni}}c|\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf x_{ni}|$: equal on resonance, where the rate is nonzero.
> - **Forbidden lines.** When $\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf x_{ni} = 0$, the next terms of $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}$ (magnetic dipole and electric quadrupole) or the spin term take over, with rates smaller by powers of $(kR_{\text{atom}})^2$; if an allowed decay exists it dominates.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.8.2, eq. (5.332) and the remark after it; §5.8.4 (after (5.359)) · Griffiths §11.2.1, eq. (11.41)*

> [!remark] Remark: What this proves of level A's selection rules
> Level A stated the rules $\Delta l = \pm1$, $\Delta m_l = 0, \pm1$, $\Delta j = 0, \pm1$ "from the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, level C (planned)" ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^der-a5-4-2|derivation of Theorem §A5.4.2]]); level C proved which dipole matrix elements vanish ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^rem-c7-2-4|§C7.2, Remark: What this proves of level A's rules]]). Theorems §C9.6.7–8 close the argument: in the long-wavelength limit the rates of absorption, stimulated and (below) spontaneous emission are proportional to $|\langle f|\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf x|i\rangle|^2$, so a vanishing dipole matrix element means a vanishing E1 rate, and a "forbidden" transition proceeds only through the smaller higher-order terms. Level A's photon-spin argument is the statement that $\mathbf x$ is a rank-1 tensor. The same holds for the rotational and vibrational rules ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics|§B5.2]], [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^rem-b4-1-4|§B4.1, Remark: The selection rule, now proved]]): rates are set by $\langle m|x|n\rangle$.

> [!remark] Remark: The photoelectric effect

For a K-shell electron (hydrogen-like, $a_0 \to a_0/Z$) the matrix element reduces to the Fourier transform of the atomic wave function at $\mathbf q = \mathbf k_f - (\omega/c)\hat{\mathbf n}$, and Sakurai obtains
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = 32e^2k_f\,\frac{(\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf k_f)^2}{m_ec\,\omega}\,\frac{Z^5}{a_0^5}\,\frac1{\bigl[(Z^2/a_0^2) + q^2\bigr]^4} :

This is level A's photoelectric effect ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect|§A1.2]]) as a transition into a continuum; its one-dimensional toy version is [[§C9.5 Fermi's Golden Rule#^ex-c9-5-2|Example §C9.5.2]].

## Rates in thermal radiation and spontaneous emission

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.6.9: Absorption and Stimulated Emission in Isotropic Broadband Radiation
> In incoherent, unpolarized radiation arriving equally from all directions, with energy density $u(\omega)$ per unit angular frequency that varies slowly across the line, the rate of absorption $i \to n$ ($E_n > E_i$) and the rate of stimulated emission $n \to i$ are equal:
>
> $$
> R_{i\to n} = R_{n\to i} = B\,u(\omega_{ni}), \qquad B = \frac{4\pi^2}{3\hbar^2}\,\bigl|\langle n|\mathbf d|i\rangle\bigr|^2 = \frac{4\pi^2e^2}{3\hbar^2}\,\bigl|\langle n|\mathbf x|i\rangle\bigr|^2 ,
> $$
>
> where $|\langle n|\mathbf x|i\rangle|^2 = \sum_{j}|\langle n|x_j|i\rangle|^2$. In SI, $B = \pi|\mathbf d_{ni}|^2/3\varepsilon_0\hbar^2$ (Griffiths (11.54)). This is Einstein's $B$ coefficient, for the energy density per unit angular frequency.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.2.3, eqs. (11.44)–(11.54) · Sakurai §5.8.1, eqs. (5.315)–(5.316) (line shapes); the Gaussian form derived here from Theorem §C9.6.8*

^thm-c9-6-9

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Treat the radiation as many incoherent plane waves; their rates add. A beam of direction $\hat{\mathbf n}$ and polarization $\boldsymbol\epsilon$ carrying energy density $u_{\hat n\epsilon}(\omega)\,d\omega$ in $d\omega$ delivers photons at the rate $c\,u_{\hat n\epsilon}\,d\omega/\hbar\omega$ per unit area, so by Theorem §C9.6.8 it causes transitions at the rate $\int\sigma_{\text{abs}}(\omega)\frac{c\,u_{\hat n\epsilon}(\omega)}{\hbar\omega}d\omega = \frac{4\pi^2\alpha c}\hbar|\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf x_{ni}|^2u_{\hat n\epsilon}(\omega_{ni})$, the $\delta$ picking out $\omega = \omega_{ni}$ (Griffiths does the same integral with the $\sin^2$ kernel). Summing over directions and the two polarizations, $u_{\hat n\epsilon}$ adds up to $u$, and $|\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf v|^2$ is replaced by its average over all orientations of $\boldsymbol\epsilon$, $\langle\epsilon_j\epsilon_l\rangle = \delta_{jl}/3$, giving $|\mathbf v|^2/3$ (Griffiths (11.53)). With $\alpha c/\hbar = e^2/\hbar^2$ this is $B\,u$. Stimulated emission has the same matrix element in modulus (Theorem §C9.6.7). SI: $e^2 \to e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ turns $4\pi^2e^2|\mathbf x|^2$ into $\pi|\mathbf d|^2/\varepsilon_0$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.6.10: Spontaneous Emission Rate
> An atom in the excited state $|n\rangle$ decays spontaneously to the lower state $|i\rangle$, emitting radiation of frequency $\omega = \omega_{ni}$, at the rate (Einstein's $A$ coefficient)
>
> $$
> A_{n\to i} = \frac{4\omega^3}{3\hbar c^3}\,\bigl|\langle i|\mathbf d|n\rangle\bigr|^2 = \frac43\,\alpha\,\frac{\omega^3}{c^2}\,\bigl|\langle i|\mathbf x|n\rangle\bigr|^2 \qquad \Bigl(\text{SI: } \frac{\omega^3|\mathbf d_{in}|^2}{3\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c^3}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> related to the $B$ of Theorem §C9.6.9 by $A/B = \hbar\omega^3/\pi^2c^3$. The lifetime of $|n\rangle$ is $\tau_n = 1/\sum_iA_{n\to i}$, the sum over all lower states, degenerate substates counted separately. For a $p \to s$ transition between radial states $R_{\alpha}$ and $R_{\alpha'}$,
>
> $$
> \frac1{\tau_{p\to s}} = \frac49\,\alpha\,\frac{\omega^3}{c^2}\,|\mathscr R_{\alpha'\alpha}|^2, \qquad \mathscr R_{\alpha'\alpha} = \int_0^\infty r^2dr\,R^*_{\alpha'}(r)\,r\,R_\alpha(r) ,
> $$
>
> the same for every $m$ of the $p$ state.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §11.3.1–11.3.2, eqs. (11.55)–(11.68) (Einstein's argument) · Sakurai §5.8.4, eqs. (5.344)–(5.359) (the direct calculation and the $p \to s$ formula) · Likharev §9.3, eq. (9.53)*

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Einstein's argument* ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-6|Theorem §A5.4.6]], here per unit angular frequency and with $B$ known). Put atoms in thermal radiation at temperature $T$ and consider one pair of states $i$, $n$. In equilibrium transitions $n \to i$ balance $i \to n$ (detailed balance), so with populations $N_n/N_i = e^{-\hbar\omega/k_BT}$,
>
> $$
> N_n\bigl[A + B\,u(\omega)\bigr] = N_i\,B\,u(\omega) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u(\omega) = \frac{A/B}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT} - 1} ,
> $$
>
> using $B_{i\to n} = B_{n\to i}$ (Theorem §C9.6.9). Planck's law per unit angular frequency, $u(\omega) = \frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2c^3}\frac1{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT} - 1}$ ([[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-4|TH Theorem §B11.1.4]]), agrees at all $T$ only if $A/B = \hbar\omega^3/\pi^2c^3$. Then $A = \frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2c^3}\cdot\frac{4\pi^2}{3\hbar^2}|\mathbf d_{ni}|^2 = \frac{4\omega^3}{3\hbar c^3}|\mathbf d_{ni}|^2$; $A$ is a property of the atom, so it holds outside equilibrium too.
>
> *The $p \to s$ formula.* For the final $s$ state and an initial $p$ state of given $m$, only one spherical component $x_q$, $q = -m$, has a nonzero matrix element (Theorem §C7.2.7), and by the Wigner–Eckart theorem ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-6|Theorem §C7.2.6]]) its modulus is the same for all $m$: $|\langle s|\mathbf x|p, m\rangle|^2 = \frac13|\mathscr R|^2$, since $\langle s|z|p, 0\rangle = \frac1{\sqrt3}\mathscr R$ (the angular integral $\int Y_0^{0*}\cos\theta\,Y_1^0\,d\Omega = 1/\sqrt3$, as in [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^ex-c7-2-2|Example §C7.2.2]]). Hence $A = \frac43\alpha\frac{\omega^3}{c^2}\cdot\frac{|\mathscr R|^2}3$.
>
> *Sakurai's direct route.* Sakurai instead puts the field in a box of side $L$, gives the mode of frequency $\omega$ the energy $\hbar\omega$, which fixes $A_0 = (2\pi\hbar c^2/\omega L^3)^{1/2}$, counts modes with $\rho(E) = \omega^2L^3/2\pi^2\hbar c^3$ per polarization (all directions), and applies the golden rule for stimulated emission (Theorem §C9.5.2) with the dipole approximation; summing over the two polarizations and averaging over the $p$ substates gives the same $\frac49\alpha\omega^3|\mathscr R|^2/c^2$, free of $L$. Why the mode must be given $\hbar\omega$ (and not, say, its zero-point energy $\hbar\omega/2$) is answered only by quantizing the field (★ Remark below).

## Level shifts and decay widths

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.6.11: Energy Shift and Decay Width
> Let a constant perturbation be switched on infinitely slowly, $V(t) = e^{\eta t}V$ with $\eta \to 0^+$, on a system in $|i\rangle$ at $t \to -\infty$. Then, to second order in $V$, the amplitude of the initial state behaves as
>
> $$
> c_i(t) = e^{-i\Delta_it/\hbar}, \qquad \Delta_i = V_{ii} + \sum_{m\ne i}\frac{|V_{mi}|^2}{E_i - E_m + i\hbar\eta} + \cdots ,
> $$
>
> so that $E_i \to E_i + \Delta_i$ with a complex $\Delta_i$:
> 1. the **level shift** is $\operatorname{Re}\Delta_i = V_{ii} + \mathrm P\sum_{m\ne i}\frac{|V_{mi}|^2}{E_i - E_m}$ (principal value), the result of time-independent perturbation theory where the denominators do not vanish;
> 2. the **decay width** $\Gamma_i = -2\operatorname{Im}\Delta_i = 2\pi\sum_{m\ne i}|V_{mi}|^2\delta(E_i - E_m) = \hbar\sum_mw_{i\to m}$ is $\hbar$ times the total golden-rule rate out of $|i\rangle$;
> 3. $|c_i(t)|^2 = e^{-\Gamma_it/\hbar}$: the state decays exponentially with **lifetime** $\tau_i = \hbar/\Gamma_i$, and $|c_i|^2 + \sum_{m\ne i}|c_m|^2 = 1$ to this order;
> 4. the energy distribution of the decaying state is a **Lorentzian** of full width at half maximum $\Gamma_i$,
>
> $$
> |f(E)|^2 \propto \frac1{\bigl[E - (E_i + \operatorname{Re}\Delta_i)\bigr]^2 + \Gamma_i^2/4} ,
> $$
>
> so that $\tau_i\Gamma_i = \hbar$: lifetime and line width are the two faces of $\operatorname{Im}\Delta_i$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.9, eqs. (5.360)–(5.384) (after Wigner and Weisskopf, 1930) · Likharev §6.6, eqs. (6.113)–(6.114)*

^thm-c9-6-11

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Amplitudes.* By [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-2|Theorem §C9.4.2]] with $t_0 \to -\infty$: $c_m^{(1)}(t) = -\frac i\hbar V_{mi}\frac{e^{\eta t + i\omega_{mi}t}}{\eta + i\omega_{mi}}$ for $m \ne i$ (its $\frac{d}{dt}|c_m|^2 = \frac{2|V_{mi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\frac{\eta e^{2\eta t}}{\eta^2 + \omega_{mi}^2} \to \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|V_{mi}|^2\delta(E_m - E_i)$ as $\eta \to 0$, the golden rule again, using $\frac{\eta}{\eta^2 + x^2} \to \pi\delta(x)$). For the initial state, $c_i^{(1)} = -\frac{iV_{ii}}{\hbar\eta}e^{\eta t}$ and
>
> $$
> c_i^{(2)} = \Bigl(\frac{-i}\hbar\Bigr)^2|V_{ii}|^2\frac{e^{2\eta t}}{2\eta^2} + \Bigl(\frac{-i}\hbar\Bigr)\sum_{m\ne i}\frac{|V_{mi}|^2e^{2\eta t}}{2\eta(E_i - E_m + i\hbar\eta)} .
> $$
>
> *A rate equation.* These terms diverge as $\eta \to 0$, but their logarithmic derivative does not: dividing $\dot c_i$ by $c_i = 1 + c_i^{(1)} + c_i^{(2)}$ and expanding to second order, the $1/\eta$ terms cancel and
>
> $$
> \frac{\dot c_i}{c_i} \simeq -\frac i\hbar V_{ii} - \frac i\hbar\sum_{m\ne i}\frac{|V_{mi}|^2}{E_i - E_m + i\hbar\eta} ,
> $$
>
> independent of $t$. A constant logarithmic derivative means $c_i = e^{-i\Delta_it/\hbar}$, normalized to $c_i(0) = 1$, with $\Delta_i$ as stated.
>
> *Real and imaginary parts.* $\frac1{x + i\varepsilon} = \frac{x}{x^2 + \varepsilon^2} - \frac{i\varepsilon}{x^2 + \varepsilon^2}$; as $\varepsilon \to 0^+$ the first tends to the principal value $\mathrm P\frac1x$ and the second to $-i\pi\delta(x)$ (the Lorentzian of area $\pi$ shrinks to a spike). So $\operatorname{Im}\Delta_i^{(2)} = -\pi\sum|V_{mi}|^2\delta(E_i - E_m)$, which is $-\frac\hbar2\sum w_{i\to m}$ by [[§C9.5 Fermi's Golden Rule#^thm-c9-5-1|Theorem §C9.5.1]]. Then $|c_i|^2 = e^{2\operatorname{Im}\Delta_it/\hbar} = e^{-\Gamma_it/\hbar}$, and for small $t$, $|c_i|^2 + \sum|c_m|^2 = (1 - \Gamma_it/\hbar) + \sum w_{i\to m}t = 1$.
>
> *Line shape.* The Schrödinger-picture amplitude is $e^{-i(E_i + \operatorname{Re}\Delta_i)t/\hbar - \Gamma_it/2\hbar}$ for $t > 0$; writing it as $\int f(E)e^{-iEt/\hbar}dE$ and inverting the Fourier transform gives $f(E) \propto 1/\bigl(E - E_i - \operatorname{Re}\Delta_i + i\Gamma_i/2\bigr)$, whose modulus squared is the Lorentzian; its half maximum lies at $\pm\Gamma_i/2$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C9.6.12: Levels in an Oscillating Field; the AC Stark Shift
> For a harmonic perturbation $V(t) = e^{\eta t}\bigl(\mathcal Ve^{i\omega t} + \mathcal V^\dagger e^{-i\omega t}\bigr)$ switched on slowly, the complex second-order shift of a level $|i\rangle$ is
>
> $$
> \Delta_i^{(2)} = \sum_m\Bigl[\frac{|\mathcal V^\dagger_{mi}|^2}{E_i - E_m + \hbar\omega + i\hbar\eta} + \frac{|\mathcal V_{mi}|^2}{E_i - E_m - \hbar\omega + i\hbar\eta}\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> neglecting terms that oscillate at $2\omega$. Its imaginary part gives $\Gamma_i = \hbar\,(\text{absorption} + \text{stimulated-emission rates out of } |i\rangle)$ by Theorem §C9.5.2, and its real part is the **AC Stark shift**. For an electric field $\mathbf E = E_0\boldsymbol\epsilon\cos\omega t$ in the dipole approximation, $V = -E_0\cos\omega t\,\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf d$, away from resonances:
>
> $$
> \delta E_i = -\frac14\,\alpha_{\text{pol},i}(\omega)\,E_0^2, \qquad \alpha_{\text{pol},i}(\omega) = \frac2\hbar\sum_m\frac{\omega_{mi}\,|\langle m|\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf d|i\rangle|^2}{\omega_{mi}^2 - \omega^2} ,
> $$
>
> with the **dynamic polarizability** $\alpha_{\text{pol},i}(\omega)$ (written so as not to clash with the fine-structure constant $\alpha$). As $\omega \to 0$ it becomes the static polarizability $2\sum_m|\langle m|\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mathbf d|i\rangle|^2/(E_m - E_i)$ of the quadratic Stark effect ([[§C9.2 Atoms in External Fields, Fine and Hyperfine Structure#^def-c9-2-1|Def. §C9.2.1]], [[§C9.2 Atoms in External Fields, Fine and Hyperfine Structure#^thm-c9-2-1|Theorem §C9.2.1]]), and $-\frac14\alpha_{\text{pol}}E_0^2 = -\frac12\alpha_{\text{pol}}\langle E^2\rangle_t$ is the static shift $-\frac12\alpha_{\text{pol}}\mathcal E^2$ averaged over a cycle of $\mathcal E = E_0\cos\omega t$. For two levels near resonance, keeping only the resonant denominator, it reproduces the shifts $\pm\gamma^2/\hbar\Delta$ of Theorem §C9.6.4.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §5.9, eq. (5.385) (the generalization to harmonic perturbations, stated there) · Likharev §6.2, footnote 5, and §6.5, footnote 32 (the ac Stark effect named); the formula, the dynamic polarizability and the two checks are worked out here*

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> As in Theorem §C9.6.11, $c_m^{(1)} = -\frac i\hbar\bigl[\mathcal V_{mi}\frac{e^{(\eta + i(\omega_{mi} + \omega))t}}{\eta + i(\omega_{mi} + \omega)} + \mathcal V^\dagger_{mi}\frac{e^{(\eta + i(\omega_{mi} - \omega))t}}{\eta + i(\omega_{mi} - \omega)}\bigr]$. > [!example] Example §C9.6.2: A Charged Oscillator Decays at the Classical Damping Rate
> A particle of charge $e$ and mass $m$ on a spring of frequency $\omega$ (level B's oscillator, [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]) starts in $|n\rangle$. Find its spontaneous decay rate and radiated power, and compare with the classical oscillator.
>
> *Rate.* $\langle n'|x|n\rangle \ne 0$ only for $n' = n \pm 1$, and for emission $n' = n - 1$, with $|\langle n - 1|x|n\rangle|^2 = n\hbar/2m\omega$; the photon has the classical frequency $\omega$. By Theorem §C9.6.10,
>
> $$
> A_n = \frac{4e^2\omega^3}{3\hbar c^3}\cdot\frac{n\hbar}{2m\omega} = n\,\frac{2e^2\omega^2}{3mc^3} = n\,\gamma_{\text{cl}}, \qquad \gamma_{\text{cl}} = \omega^2\tau_{\text{AL}}, \quad \tau_{\text{AL}} = \frac{2e^2}{3mc^3} .
> $$
>
> $\gamma_{\text{cl}}$ is the damping constant of a classical charged oscillator due to its own radiation reaction ([[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^ex-b11-4-3|EM Example §B11.4.3]], where $\tau = \mu_0q^2/6\pi mc$ in SI). The first excited state decays at exactly the classical damping rate.
>
> *Power.* Each photon carries $\hbar\omega$, so $P = \hbar\omega A_n = n\hbar\omega\,\gamma_{\text{cl}} = \gamma_{\text{cl}}\bigl(E - \tfrac12\hbar\omega\bigr)$ with $E = (n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega$. Classically, by Larmor's formula ([[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-2|EM Theorem §B11.4.2]]) averaged over a cycle of amplitude $x_0$, $P = \frac{e^2x_0^2\omega^4}{3c^3}$, and with $E = \frac12m\omega^2x_0^2$, $P = \gamma_{\text{cl}}E$. The two agree for $E \gg \hbar\omega$; the quantum formula protects the ground state, which does not radiate.
>

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Spontaneous emission from the quantized field
> The classical field cannot explain why an isolated excited atom decays at all: with no field there is no perturbation, and an energy eigenstate is stationary. Einstein's argument and Sakurai's "one quantum per mode" calculation give the right rate without saying why. The answer needs the field quantized (Likharev §9.1–9.3; Sakurai §7.8, which Sakurai's footnote 14 points to for $A_0 = (2\pi\hbar c^2/\omega L^3)^{1/2}$; the user's series, Part IV, §17, "The second closing: quantizing the field"):
> - Each mode of the field in a box is a harmonic oscillator with ladder operators $a$, $a^\dagger$ ([[§C3.4 The Harmonic Oscillator and Coherent States Revisited|§C3.4]]), and the electric-field operator of a mode is proportional to a combination of $a$ and $a^\dagger$; its eigenstates $|n\rangle$ hold $n$ photons of energy $\hbar\omega$.
> - In the dipole coupling $-\mathbf d\cdot\hat{\mathbf E}$ (an instance of Theorem §C9.5.4), emission creates a photon. The matrix element of $a^\dagger$ is $\langle n + 1|a^\dagger|n\rangle = \sqrt{n + 1}$, so the emission rate into a mode holding $n$ photons is $\Gamma_e = (n + 1)\,\Gamma_s$, and absorption, with $\langle n - 1|a|n\rangle = \sqrt n$, has $\Gamma_a = n\,\Gamma_s$ (Likharev (9.55)–(9.58)). The $n$ is stimulated emission, proportional to the field intensity; the $1$ is spontaneous emission, "stimulated by the ground state of the electromagnetic field", present with no photons at all. Sakurai's and Griffiths' (Problem 11.11) "one quantum $\hbar\omega$ per mode" is this $n + 1$ at $n = 0$, not the zero-point energy $\hbar\omega/2$.
> - Summing over modes with the mode density $\omega^2V/\pi^2c^3$ gives Dirac's 1927 formula $\Gamma_s = \frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{4\omega^3}{3\hbar c^3}|\langle\text{fin}|\hat{\mathbf d}|\text{ini}\rangle|^2$ (Likharev (9.53)), Theorem §C9.6.10 in SI. Detailed balance with $\Gamma_e = (n + 1)\Gamma_s$, $\Gamma_a = n\Gamma_s$ and Boltzmann populations then *yields* the Bose–Einstein (Planck) occupation $\bar n = 1/(e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT} - 1)$, reversing Einstein's logic (Likharev (9.60)–(9.63); [[§B11.1★ Blackbody Radiation from Statistical Mechanics#^thm-b11-1-4|TH Theorem §B11.1.4]]).
> - Since the rate is proportional to the density of field modes, it can be engineered: in a high-$Q$ cavity it is suppressed between resonances and enhanced on them (the Purcell effect; Likharev §9.4, footnote 24).
>
> The quantized radiation field, photons as its excitations, and quantum electrodynamics belong to [[Quantum Field Theory I]]; the formalism of creation and annihilation operators is Chapter C12★ ([[§C12.2★ Second Quantization|§C12.2★]]).
