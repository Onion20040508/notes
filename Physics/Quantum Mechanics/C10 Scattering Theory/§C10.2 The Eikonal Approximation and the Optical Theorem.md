What does conservation of probability say about the scattering amplitude, and how can one go beyond the Born approximation when the potential is not weak but the energy is high? In one dimension level B's answer to the first question was $R + T = 1$, the conservation of the probability current ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-4|QM Theorem §B3.3.4]]). In three dimensions the corresponding statement, the optical theorem, ties the total cross-section to the forward amplitude alone, and it shows that the first Born approximation cannot be the whole story. For the second question, level C combines the Lippmann–Schwinger amplitude of [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]] with the semiclassical wave function $e^{iS/\hbar}$ ([[§C3.5 Wave Mechanics, the Linear Potential and WKB Revisited#^rem-c3-5-1|Remark: What level C adds, and what stays at level B]]; level B's WKB, [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-1|QM Theorem §B9.3.1]]). The result is the eikonal approximation, whose classical limit is the impulse approximation of Classical Mechanics ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-7|CM Theorem §B4.2.7]]). Both topics were part of PHY 512; no typed course material exists.

*Notation* as in [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation|§C10.1]]: incident $\mathbf k = k\hat{\mathbf z}$, scattered $\mathbf k'$, angle $\theta$, $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, $v = \hbar k/m$; $f(\theta = 0) \equiv f(\mathbf k, \mathbf k)$ is the forward amplitude. In the eikonal, $\mathbf x = \mathbf b + z\hat{\mathbf z}$ with $\mathbf b \perp \hat{\mathbf z}$, and $b = |\mathbf b|$ is the impact parameter of [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^def-b4-2-1|CM Def. §B4.2.1]].

## The optical theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.2.1: The Optical Theorem
> For every potential (real, local or not),
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Im}f(\theta = 0) = \frac{k\,\sigma_{\text{tot}}}{4\pi} , \qquad \sigma_{\text{tot}} = \int d\Omega\,\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} . \qquad (6.60)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.2.2, eqs. (6.60)–(6.69) · Griffiths Problem 10.19, eq. (10.104) · Likharev §3.3, eq. (3.99)*

^thm-c10-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The bra form of Lippmann–Schwinger.* The adjoint of (6.35) for $|\mathbf k\rangle$ is $\langle\mathbf k| = \langle\psi^{(+)}| - \langle\psi^{(+)}|V(E - H_0 - i\varepsilon)^{-1}$. Applying it to $V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle$,
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf k|V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle = \langle\psi^{(+)}|V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle - \langle\psi^{(+)}|V\,\frac{1}{E - H_0 - i\varepsilon}\,V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle . \qquad (6.61)
> $$
>
> The first term is real, an expectation value of the Hermitian $V$.
>
> *The imaginary part of the resolvent.* For a function $f$ regular on the real axis and a pole at $z_0 = x_0 + i\varepsilon$, deform the real axis into a small semicircle of radius $\delta$ below $x_0$ (Sakurai Fig. 6.4). The two straight pieces give the principal value $\mathscr P\!\int f(x)\,dx/(x - x_0)$ (6.63); on the semicircle $z - x_0 = \delta e^{i\phi}$, $\phi$ from $-\pi$ to $0$, the integral tends to $i\pi f(x_0)$ (6.64). Hence
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{f(x)}{x - x_0}\,dx = \mathscr P\!\!\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{f(x)}{x - x_0}\,dx + i\pi f(x_0), \qquad \lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\frac{1}{E - H_0 - i\varepsilon} = \mathscr P\frac{1}{E - H_0} + i\pi\,\delta(E - H_0) , \qquad (6.65)\text{–}(6.66)
> $$
>
> the second form by writing $(E - H_0 - i\varepsilon)^{-1} = \int dE'\,\delta(E - E')/(E' - H_0 - i\varepsilon)$. The principal-value operator is Hermitian, so its expectation value is real, and
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Im}\langle\mathbf k|V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle = -\pi\langle\psi^{(+)}|V\,\delta(E - H_0)\,V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle = -\pi\langle\mathbf k|T^\dagger\delta(E - H_0)T|\mathbf k\rangle , \qquad (6.67)
> $$
>
> using $V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle = T|\mathbf k\rangle$.
>
> *Counting.* Insert $\sum_{\mathbf k'}|\mathbf k'\rangle\langle\mathbf k'|$ (box normalization) next to the delta function, and use $f = -(mL^3/2\pi\hbar^2)\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle$ ([[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]]):
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Im}f(\mathbf k, \mathbf k) = \frac{mL^3}{2\hbar^2}\sum_{\mathbf k'}|\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle|^2\,\delta\Bigl(E - \frac{\hbar^2k'^2}{2m}\Bigr) = \frac{mL^3}{2\hbar^2}\Bigl(\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mL^3}\Bigr)^2\frac{L^3}{(2\pi)^3}\int d^3k'\,|f(\mathbf k', \mathbf k)|^2\,\delta\Bigl(E - \frac{\hbar^2k'^2}{2m}\Bigr) . \qquad (6.68)\text{–}(6.69)
> $$
>
> The constants combine to $\hbar^2/4\pi m$, and $\int k'^2dk'\,\delta(E - \hbar^2k'^2/2m) = mk/\hbar^2$, so $\operatorname{Im}f(\mathbf k, \mathbf k) = (k/4\pi)\int d\Omega'\,|f|^2 = k\sigma_{\text{tot}}/4\pi$. The energy delta function restricts the sum to $|\mathbf k'| = k$: every direction, with energy conserved.

> [!remark] Remark: The shadow, and the one-dimensional version
> *Where the flux goes.* Behind the target, at a large distance $z$ and near the axis, $|\mathbf x| \approx z + b^2/2z$, so $\psi \approx e^{ikz}\bigl[1 + f(0)\,e^{ikb^2/2z}/z\bigr]$. The probability flowing through a large plane perpendicular to the beam contains, besides the incident flux, the interference term $2\operatorname{Re}\bigl[f(0)\int d^2b\,e^{ikb^2/2z}/z\bigr]$. The Fresnel integral is $\int d^2b\,e^{ikb^2/2z} = 2\pi iz/k$ (the convergent limit of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-3|Theorem §C4.1.3]]), so the interference term is $2\operatorname{Re}[2\pi if(0)/k] = -(4\pi/k)\operatorname{Im}f(0)$. This is negative: the forward scattered wave interferes destructively with the incident wave, removing from the beam exactly the flux $\sigma_{\text{tot}}$ that the scattered wave carries off in all directions. Sakurai calls this the *shadow* ([[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^thm-c10-3-6|Theorem §C10.3.6]] works it out for the hard sphere). The factor $i$ from the Fresnel zones of [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^def-b11-3-1|WO Def. §B11.3.1]] is why the *imaginary* part of $f(0)$ appears.
>
> *One dimension.* There is no transverse plane, hence no factor $i$. Write the scattering state as $\psi = e^{ikx} + f_\pm e^{ik|x|}$ for $x \gtrless 0$, so $T = 1 + f_+$ and $R = f_-$. Then level B's $|T|^2 + |R|^2 = 1$ ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-4|QM Theorem §B3.3.4]]) is
>
> $$
> -2\operatorname{Re}f_+ = |f_+|^2 + |f_-|^2 :
> $$
>
> the forward amplitude fixes the total scattered probability. For the delta well of [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^ex-c10-1-3|Example §C10.1.3]], $f_\pm = iu/(1 - iu)$ with $u = \gamma/2k$, so $\operatorname{Re}f_+ = -u^2/(1 + u^2)$ and $|f_\pm|^2 = u^2/(1 + u^2)$, as required. $R + T = 1$ is thus the one-dimensional optical theorem; Theorem §C10.2.1 is the same conservation law, with the factor $i$ that a beam of finite width brings in three dimensions.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.5 (shadow; the flux computation is added here) · the one-dimensional form worked out here*

^rem-c10-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: The first Born approximation violates the optical theorem
> For a real potential $f^{(1)}(0) = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int V\,d^3x$ is real ([[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-5|Theorem §C10.1.5]], item 4), so $\operatorname{Im}f^{(1)}(0) = 0$, while $\sigma_{\text{tot}} > 0$. There is no contradiction order by order: $\sigma_{\text{tot}}$ is of second order in $V$, and so is the imaginary part of the forward amplitude, which comes from $f^{(2)}$ ([[§C10.2 The Eikonal Approximation and the Optical Theorem#^ex-c10-2-2|Example §C10.2.2]]). Likharev adds that the Born approximation cannot describe a dark shadow at all, and lists the second Born term and the eikonal as the two remedies.
>
> *Source: Likharev §3.3, discussion after eq. (3.99) · Sakurai §6.3, remark 2 after (6.83)*

## The eikonal approximation

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.2.2: The Eikonal Approximation
> Assume that $V$ varies little over a wavelength ($1/k \ll$ the range $a$ of $V$), that $E \gg |V|$, and that the scattering angle is small. $V$ itself need not satisfy the Born condition. Then, along straight lines parallel to $\mathbf k = k\hat{\mathbf z}$,
>
> $$
> \psi^{(+)}(\mathbf b + z\hat{\mathbf z}) \simeq \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\,e^{ikz}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{im}{\hbar^2k}\int_{-\infty}^zV\bigl(\sqrt{b^2 + z'^2}\bigr)\,dz'\Bigr] , \qquad (6.160)
> $$
>
> and the scattering amplitude is
>
> $$
> f(\theta) \simeq -ik\int_0^\infty db\;b\,J_0(kb\theta)\,\bigl[e^{2i\Delta(b)} - 1\bigr], \qquad \Delta(b) \equiv -\frac{m}{2k\hbar^2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}V\bigl(\sqrt{b^2 + z^2}\bigr)\,dz , \qquad (6.167)\text{–}(6.168)
> $$
>
> where $J_0$ is the Bessel function of order zero. Impact parameters beyond the range of $V$ contribute nothing.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.5, eqs. (6.155)–(6.168) · Likharev §3.3, eq. (3.102)*

^thm-c10-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Wave function.* Where $V$ changes little over a wavelength, the semiclassical form $\psi^{(+)} \sim e^{iS(\mathbf x)/\hbar}$ applies, with $S$ obeying the Hamilton–Jacobi equation $(\nabla S)^2/2m + V = E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ (6.155)–(6.156) ([[§C3.5 Wave Mechanics, the Linear Potential and WKB Revisited#^rem-c3-5-1|Remark: What level C adds, and what stays at level B]]; [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-1|CM Theorem §B8.3.1]]). For small deflections take the classical path to be the undeflected straight line at impact parameter $b$, and integrate along it:
>
> $$
> \frac S\hbar = kz + \int_{-\infty}^z\Bigl[\sqrt{k^2 - \tfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}V} - k\Bigr]dz' \simeq kz - \frac{m}{\hbar^2k}\int_{-\infty}^zV\,dz' , \qquad (6.157)\text{–}(6.159)
> $$
>
> with the constant fixed so that $S/\hbar \to kz$ as $V \to 0$, and $\sqrt{k^2 - 2mV/\hbar^2} \simeq k - mV/\hbar^2k$ for $E \gg |V|$. With the plane-wave normalization this is (6.160). It is not of the form plane wave plus spherical wave and describes only the forward region, but it can be used inside the exact amplitude.
>
> *Amplitude.* Put (6.160) into the exact amplitude $f = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(2\pi)^3\int d^3x'\,\frac{e^{-i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf x'}}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}V(\mathbf x')\psi^{(+)}(\mathbf x')$ ([[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]], continuum normalization) (6.161). Use cylindrical coordinates $d^3x' = b\,db\,d\phi_b\,dz'$. Since $\mathbf k \perp \mathbf b$ and $(\mathbf k - \mathbf k')\cdot\hat{\mathbf z} = O(\theta^2)$, the phase is $(\mathbf k - \mathbf k')\cdot\mathbf x' \simeq -\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf b = -kb\theta\cos\phi_b$ (6.162)–(6.163), and
>
> $$
> \int_0^{2\pi}d\phi_b\,e^{-ikb\theta\cos\phi_b} = 2\pi J_0(kb\theta) . \qquad (6.165)
> $$
>
> The $z$-integral is exact, because the integrand is a derivative:
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\,V\exp\Bigl[\frac{-im}{\hbar^2k}\int_{-\infty}^zV\,dz'\Bigr] = \frac{i\hbar^2k}{m}\Bigl[\exp\Bigl(\frac{-im}{\hbar^2k}\int_{-\infty}^zV\,dz'\Bigr)\Bigr]_{z=-\infty}^{z=+\infty} = \frac{i\hbar^2k}{m}\bigl[e^{2i\Delta(b)} - 1\bigr] . \qquad (6.166)
> $$
>
> Collecting, $f = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\cdot2\pi\cdot\frac{i\hbar^2k}{m}\int b\,db\,J_0(kb\theta)\bigl[e^{2i\Delta} - 1\bigr]$, which is (6.167).

^der-c10-2-2

*Uses:* [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]], [[§C3.5 Wave Mechanics, the Linear Potential and WKB Revisited#^rem-c3-5-1|Remark: What level C adds, and what stays at level B]], [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-1|CM Theorem §B8.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The Born approximation as the weak-phase limit
> If $|\Delta(b)| \ll 1$ for all $b$, expand $e^{2i\Delta} - 1 \simeq 2i\Delta$:
>
> $$
> f \simeq 2k\int_0^\infty b\,db\,J_0(kb\theta)\,\Delta(b) = -\frac{m}{\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty b\,db\,J_0(kb\theta)\int dz\,V = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int d^3x\,e^{i\mathbf q_\perp\cdot\mathbf b}\,V ,
> $$
>
> the first Born amplitude ([[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-5|Theorem §C10.1.5]]) with the longitudinal transfer $q_z = O(k\theta^2)$ neglected, as Sakurai observes below (6.161). The Born condition at high energy is therefore $|\Delta| \sim |V_0|a/\hbar v \ll 1$ ([[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^rem-c10-1-7|Remark: When is the Born approximation valid?]]): the phase that the potential adds along the path must be small. The eikonal drops this requirement and keeps the whole phase $e^{2i\Delta}$; it needs only $E \gg |V|$ and $ka \gg 1$. The domains of the two approximations are therefore different (Sakurai), and the eikonal works for potentials that are long in the beam direction, where Born fails (Likharev).

^rem-c10-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: The classical limit, and the impact parameter
> When $|\Delta| \gg 1$ the integrand of (6.167) oscillates rapidly, and only impact parameters where the phase is stationary contribute. For $kb\theta \gg 1$, $J_0(kb\theta)$ is a combination of $e^{\pm i(kb\theta - \pi/4)}/\sqrt{kb\theta}$, so stationarity of $2\Delta(b) \pm kb\theta$ requires $k\theta = 2|\Delta'(b)|$. With $\frac{d}{db}\int_{-\infty}^\infty V\,dz = 2b\int_b^\infty\frac{dV}{dr}\,\frac{dr}{\sqrt{r^2 - b^2}}$ (substituting $z = \sqrt{r^2 - b^2}$) and $\hbar k = mv$,
>
> $$
> \theta = \frac2k\,|\Delta'(b)| = \frac{2b}{mv^2}\,\Bigl|\int_b^\infty\frac{dV}{dr}\,\frac{dr}{\sqrt{r^2 - b^2}}\Bigr| ,
> $$
>
> which is the small-angle deflection of classical mechanics, [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-7|CM Theorem §B4.2.7]], the impulse integrated along the straight path. $\hbar$ has cancelled. The eikonal is the wave version of the impulse approximation, and the classical trajectory is the impact parameter of stationary phase. The same correspondence assigns angular momentum $\ell\hbar = bp$ to impact parameter $b = \ell/k$ (Sakurai §6.5.1), which [[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^thm-c10-3-7|Theorem §C10.3.7]] uses to turn the phase shifts into $\Delta(b)$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.5–6.5.1 (the stationary-phase step is added here)*

^rem-c10-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.2.3: The Eikonal Satisfies the Optical Theorem
> For real $\Delta(b)$ the eikonal amplitude gives
>
> $$
> \frac{4\pi}{k}\operatorname{Im}f(0) = 8\pi\int_0^\infty b\,db\,\sin^2\Delta(b) = \int_0^\infty 2\pi\theta\,d\theta\,|f(\theta)|^2 ,
> $$
>
> the second integral extended over all small-angle space, $\theta$ from $0$ to $\infty$, which is legitimate because $f$ is concentrated at $\theta \lesssim 1/ka$. So $\sigma_{\text{tot}} = 2\int d^2b\,(1 - \cos2\Delta)$, a sum over impact parameters of $4\sin^2\Delta \le 4$, and for a potential of range $a$, $\sigma_{\text{tot}} \le 4\pi a^2$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.5 (stated, with the proof referred to Gottfried; derived here)*

^thm-c10-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At $\theta = 0$, $J_0 = 1$ and $\operatorname{Im}\bigl[-ik(e^{2i\Delta} - 1)\bigr] = -k(\cos2\Delta - 1) = 2k\sin^2\Delta$, so $(4\pi/k)\operatorname{Im}f(0) = 8\pi\int b\,db\,\sin^2\Delta$. For the integrated cross-section, in the small-angle region $d\Omega = 2\pi\theta\,d\theta$, and Hankel's orthogonality relation (the radial form of the two-dimensional Fourier completeness relation; Mathematical Methods, planned)
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty\theta\,d\theta\,J_0(kb\theta)\,J_0(kb'\theta) = \frac{\delta(b - b')}{k^2b}
> $$
>
> gives
>
> $$
> 2\pi\int_0^\infty\theta\,d\theta\,|f|^2 = 2\pi k^2\int\!\!\int b\,db\,b'\,db'\bigl[e^{2i\Delta(b)} - 1\bigr]\bigl[e^{-2i\Delta(b')} - 1\bigr]\frac{\delta(b - b')}{k^2b} = 2\pi\int_0^\infty b\,db\,\bigl|e^{2i\Delta} - 1\bigr|^2 ,
> $$
>
> and $|e^{2i\Delta} - 1|^2 = 4\sin^2\Delta$. The bound follows from $\sin^2\Delta \le 1$ and $\Delta = 0$ for $b > a$.

^der-c10-2-3

*Uses:* [[§C10.2 The Eikonal Approximation and the Optical Theorem#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]], [[§C10.2 The Eikonal Approximation and the Optical Theorem#^thm-c10-2-1|Theorem §C10.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Twice the geometric cross-section
> Let a strong potential make $\Delta(b)$ large and rapidly varying for all $b < a$. Then $\sin^2\Delta$ averages to $\tfrac12$ there, and Theorem §C10.2.3 gives $\sigma_{\text{tot}} \to 8\pi\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12a^2 = 2\pi a^2$, twice the area $\pi a^2$ that a classical particle would hit ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^rem-b4-2-2|CM Remark: Finite and infinite total cross-sections]]). The same $2\pi a^2$ appears for the hard sphere at high energy, where the partial-wave sum splits it into a reflected part $\pi a^2$ and a forward diffraction peak $\pi a^2$, the shadow ([[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^thm-c10-3-6|Theorem §C10.3.6]]). The optical theorem forces the second half: a target cannot remove flux from the beam without the destructive forward interference of [[§C10.2 The Eikonal Approximation and the Optical Theorem#^rem-c10-2-1|Remark: The shadow, and the one-dimensional version]], and that interference is itself scattering into a cone of angle $\sim 1/ka$. In optics this is the extinction paradox (Likharev §3.8), and the shadow peak is the Fraunhofer pattern of an opaque disk, which by Babinet's principle is the Airy pattern of a hole ([[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-4|WO Theorem §B11.1.4]], [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-3|WO Theorem §B11.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.5, eqs. (6.173)–(6.184) · Likharev §3.8, discussion of Fig. 3.25 (the eikonal average is worked out here)*

^rem-c10-2-4

> [!remark] Remark: Yukawa and Coulomb eikonal phases
> For $V = V_0e^{-\mu r}/\mu r$, with $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-\mu r}dz/\mu r = (2/\mu)\int_b^\infty e^{-\mu r}dr/\sqrt{r^2 - b^2} = (2/\mu)K_0(\mu b)$ (Sakurai's hint in Problem 6.8, $K_0$ the modified Bessel function),
>
> $$
> \Delta(b) = -\frac{mV_0}{\hbar^2k\mu}\,K_0(\mu b) .
> $$
>
> It falls off as $e^{-\mu b}$ beyond the range. In the Coulomb limit $V_0/\mu = ZZ'e^2$, write $\eta = ZZ'e^2/\hbar v$; then $\Delta = -\eta K_0(\mu b) \to \eta\bigl[\ln(\mu b/2) + \gamma_E\bigr]$ for $\mu b \ll 1$, where $\gamma_E$ is Euler's constant. The $b$-dependence, $\eta\ln b$, is finite. The rest is a constant phase that diverges as $\mu \to 0$: the overall phase of $f$ depends on how the Coulomb field is cut off. That is the Coulomb phase of [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^cau-c10-1-2|Caution: The Coulomb potential is not short-ranged]], invisible in $|f|^2$. Stationary phase gives $\theta = 2\eta/kb = 2ZZ'e^2/mv^2b$, the small-angle limit of Rutherford's $b(\chi)$ ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-5|CM Theorem §B4.2.5]]). Doing the $b$-integral of (6.167) with $e^{2i\eta\ln b}$ (a standard Bessel integral, continued analytically) gives $|f| = 2\eta/k\theta^2$. That is Rutherford's $|f| = ZZ'e^2/4E\sin^2(\theta/2)$ at small angles, for every $\eta$, including the classical regime $\eta \gg 1$ where Born is invalid.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 6.8(b) (solved here; the Coulomb limit worked out here)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C10.2.1: A Gaussian Potential: Eikonal, Born and Exact
> For $V = V_0e^{-r^2/a^2}$, find $\Delta(b)$ and the high-energy phase shifts (Sakurai Problem 6.8(a)), and compare eikonal, first Born and exact results for $ka = 10$ and $U \equiv 2mV_0a^2/\hbar^2 = -20$, an attractive well with $E/|V_0| = (ka)^2/|U| = 5$.
>
> *Eikonal phase.* $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-(b^2 + z^2)/a^2}dz = a\sqrt\pi\,e^{-b^2/a^2}$, so
>
> $$
> \Delta(b) = -\frac{\sqrt\pi\,mV_0a}{2\hbar^2k}\,e^{-b^2/a^2} = -\frac{\sqrt\pi\,U}{4ka}\,e^{-b^2/a^2}, \qquad \delta_\ell \simeq \Delta(\ell/k) = -\frac{\sqrt\pi\,U}{4ka}\,e^{-\ell^2/(ka)^2}
> $$
>
> ([[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^thm-c10-3-7|Theorem §C10.3.7]]). The phase shifts fall off faster than exponentially once $\ell \gtrsim ka$, Sakurai's assertion that $\delta_\ell \to 0$ rapidly for $\ell \gg kR$. Here $\Delta(0) = 0.886$: not small, so the Born approximation is not safe.
>
> *Born.* By (6.72) with $\int d^3x\,e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}e^{-r^2/a^2} = \pi^{3/2}a^3e^{-q^2a^2/4}$: $f^{(1)} = -\frac{\sqrt\pi}{4}Ua\,e^{-q^2a^2/4}$. With $q^2 = 2k^2(1 - \cos\theta)$, $\int|f^{(1)}|^2d\Omega = \frac{\pi^2U^2a^2}{8(ka)^2}\bigl(1 - e^{-2(ka)^2}\bigr)$, Griffiths Problem 10.20 with his $\mu = 1$.
>

> [!example] Example §C10.2.2: The Optical Theorem at Second Order
> Show in two ways that, for a central potential, to lowest order in $V$
>
> $$
> \sigma_{\text{tot}} \simeq \frac{m^2}{\pi\hbar^4}\int d^3x\int d^3x'\,V(r)\,V(r')\,\frac{\sin^2k|\mathbf x - \mathbf x'|}{k^2|\mathbf x - \mathbf x'|^2} :
> $$
>
> (a) by integrating the first Born cross-section; (b) from the optical theorem with the *second* Born forward amplitude.
>
> Write $\boldsymbol\rho = \mathbf x - \mathbf x'$.
>
> *(a)* From (6.72), $|f^{(1)}|^2 = (m/2\pi\hbar^2)^2\int\!\!\int V(\mathbf x)V(\mathbf x')\,e^{i(\mathbf k - \mathbf k')\cdot\boldsymbol\rho}$. The integral over directions of $\mathbf k'$ is $\int d\Omega'\,e^{-i\mathbf k'\cdot\boldsymbol\rho} = 4\pi\sin(k\rho)/k\rho$, so
>
> $$
> \sigma^{(1)} = \frac{m^2}{\pi\hbar^4k}\int\!\!\int V(\mathbf x)V(\mathbf x')\,\cos(\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\rho)\,\frac{\sin k\rho}{\rho} ,
> $$
>
> where $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\rho}$ was replaced by its real part, the imaginary part being odd under $\mathbf x \leftrightarrow \mathbf x'$.
>
> *(b)* At $\mathbf k' = \mathbf k$, (6.86) is $f^{(2)}(0) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\bigl(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\bigr)^2\int\!\!\int V(\mathbf x')V(\mathbf x'')\,G_+(\mathbf x', \mathbf x'')\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf x'' - \mathbf x')}$. $G_+$ is symmetric, so symmetrizing replaces the exponential by $\cos\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\rho$. All remaining factors are real except $G_+$, whose imaginary part is $\operatorname{Im}(-e^{ik\rho}/4\pi\rho) = -\sin k\rho/4\pi\rho$ ([[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-3|Theorem §C10.1.3]]). Hence
>
> $$
> \frac{4\pi}{k}\operatorname{Im}f^{(2)}(0) = \frac{4\pi}{k}\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi}\Bigl(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\Bigr)^2\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\!\!\int VV'\cos(\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\rho)\frac{\sin k\rho}{\rho} = \sigma^{(1)} .
> $$
>
> The optical theorem holds order by order (Likharev's eq. (3.101)). For a central potential $\sigma_{\text{tot}}$ does not depend on the direction of $\mathbf k$, so it can be averaged over $\hat{\mathbf k}$, which turns $\cos\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\rho$ into $\sin k\rho/k\rho$ and gives the formula. (As printed, Sakurai's formula needs this symmetry; for a general $V$ the factor $\cos\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\rho$ stays.) As $k \to 0$, $\sin^2k\rho/k^2\rho^2 \to 1$ and $\sigma \to 4\pi\bigl(\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int V\bigr)^2 = 4\pi|f^{(1)}(0)|^2$, the isotropic low-energy limit.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 6.2 (solved here) · Likharev §3.3, eq. (3.101)*
