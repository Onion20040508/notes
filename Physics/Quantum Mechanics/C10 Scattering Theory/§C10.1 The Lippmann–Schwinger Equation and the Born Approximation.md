A particle of momentum $\hbar\mathbf k$ meets a localized potential: what is the probability per unit solid angle that it leaves along $\mathbf k'$? In one dimension level B needed two numbers, $R$ and $T$, defined by probability currents and tied by $R + T = 1$ ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^def-b3-3-1|QM Def. §B3.3.1]], [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-4|QM Theorem §B3.3.4]]), and Classical Mechanics computed cross-sections from trajectories ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^def-b4-2-1|CM Def. §B4.2.1]], [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-5|CM Theorem §B4.2.5]]). Level C treats scattering as a transition between continuum eigenkets of $H_0$, driven by $V$ in the interaction picture ([[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-2|Theorem §C9.4.2]]) and counted as in the golden rule ([[§C9.5 Fermi's Golden Rule#^thm-c9-5-1|Theorem §C9.5.1]]). The transition operator $T$ obeys the Lippmann–Schwinger equation, whose kernel is the energy-domain form of the retarded propagator of [[§C4.1 Propagators|§C4.1]], and its expansion in powers of $V$ is the Born series. PHY 512 covered the Born approximation, but no typed 512 material exists, so the examples come from Sakurai, Griffiths and Likharev.

*Notation (Sakurai).* $H = H_0 + V$ with $H_0 = \mathbf p^2/2m$, eigenkets $|\mathbf k\rangle$ and $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$. The incident wave vector is $\mathbf k$ and the scattered one $\mathbf k'$, with $|\mathbf k'| = k$ for elastic scattering. $\theta$ is the angle between them, and $\mathbf q = \mathbf k - \mathbf k'$ is the wave-vector transfer, $|\mathbf q| = 2k\sin(\theta/2)$. $\varepsilon \to 0^+$ throughout. Plane waves are first box-normalized, $\langle\mathbf x|\mathbf k\rangle = e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}/L^{3/2}$ in a cube of side $L$, then continuum-normalized, $\langle\mathbf x|\mathbf k\rangle = e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}/(2\pi)^{3/2}$, the wave-number relabelling of the momentum eigenkets ([[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^ex-c2-1-2|Example §C2.1.2]], item 1; in three dimensions [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^thm-c2-3-6|Theorem §C2.3.6]]); $L$ drops out of every observable. For two particles, $m$ is the reduced mass and angles are centre-of-mass angles ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-1|CM Theorem §B4.2.1]]). Coulomb potentials are in Gaussian units, $ZZ'e^2/r$, with $Z$, $Z'$ the signed charge numbers, so only $e^2$ appears and the sign convention for $e$ (§C4.3 vs §C9.2) does not matter in this chapter; the SI conversion is [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^cau-c6-2-1|Caution: Gaussian and SI units for the Coulomb problem]].

## Cross-sections and the transition operator

> [!definition] Definition §C10.1.1: Scattering Problem; Differential Cross-Section
> A particle of mass $m$ scatters off a time-independent potential, $H = H_0 + V(\mathbf x)$, $H_0 = \mathbf p^2/2m$ (6.1)–(6.3). $V$ is **local** if $\langle\mathbf x'|V|\mathbf x''\rangle = V(\mathbf x')\,\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x' - \mathbf x'')$ (6.50), and of **finite range** $R$ if it vanishes for $|\mathbf x| > R$.
>
> An incident plane wave $|\mathbf k\rangle$ carries the probability current density $\mathbf j = (\hbar\mathbf k/m)/L^3$, of magnitude $v/L^3$ with $v = \hbar k/m$ (6.21) ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^def-b1-1-1|QM Def. §B1.1.1]]). If $w(\mathbf k \to \mathbf k')$ is the transition rate into final wave vectors in the solid angle $d\Omega$ about $\hat{\mathbf k}'$, the **differential cross-section** is defined by
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\,d\Omega = \frac{w(\mathbf k \to \mathbf k')}{|\mathbf j|} , \qquad (6.22)
> $$
>
> the area of incident beam whose particles end up in $d\Omega$, and the **total cross-section** is $\sigma_{\text{tot}} = \int(d\sigma/d\Omega)\,d\Omega$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.1–6.1.1, eqs. (6.1)–(6.3), (6.21)–(6.22); §6.2, eq. (6.50) · Griffiths §10.1.2*

^def-c10-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: What changes from classical mechanics and from level B
> Classically, $d\sigma$ counts the particles of a ring of impact parameters that a deterministic deflection function sends into a cone ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-2|CM Theorem §B4.2.2]]). Quantum mechanically there are no trajectories: Definition §C10.1.1 is a ratio of a transition rate to a probability current, the same quantities that level B used for $R$ and $T$ in one dimension, where "scattering into a direction" means only forward or back. The classical number, an area, is kept; its meaning changes from a count of trajectories to a ratio of probability fluxes (Griffiths derives the same $d\sigma = |f|^2d\Omega$ by comparing the probability in the incident and scattered parts of the wave, eq. (10.14)).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.1.1: The T Matrix, the S Matrix and the Cross-Section
> Let $V$ be switched on adiabatically, as $Ve^{\varepsilon t}$, and let $|i\rangle$, $|n\rangle$ be eigenkets of $H_0$ with $\hbar\omega_{ni} = E_n - E_i$. Define the numbers $T_{ni}$ by
>
> $$
> \langle n|\mathscr U_I(t, -\infty)|i\rangle = \delta_{ni} - \frac i\hbar\,T_{ni}\int_{-\infty}^te^{i\omega_{ni}t' + \varepsilon t'}\,dt' , \qquad (6.10)
> $$
>
> with $\varepsilon \to 0$ taken before $t \to \infty$. Then:
> 1. the **S matrix** is
>
> $$
> S_{ni} \equiv \lim_{t\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\langle n|\mathscr U_I(t, -\infty)|i\rangle = \delta_{ni} - 2\pi i\,\delta(E_n - E_i)\,T_{ni} ; \qquad (6.11)
> $$
>
> 2. for $n \ne i$ the transition rate is independent of time,
>
> $$
> w(i \to n) = \frac{2\pi}\hbar\,|T_{ni}|^2\,\delta(E_n - E_i) ; \qquad (6.16)
> $$
>
> 3. for elastic scattering $|\mathbf k\rangle \to |\mathbf k'\rangle$ in the box, the density of final states in $d\Omega$ is $\rho(E) = (mk/\hbar^2)(L/2\pi)^3\,d\Omega$ (6.19), and
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \Bigl(\frac{mL^3}{2\pi\hbar^2}\Bigr)^2\,|T_{\mathbf k'\mathbf k}|^2 . \qquad (6.22)
> $$
>
> To first order in $V$, $T_{ni} = V_{ni}$, and item 2 is Fermi's golden rule ([[§C9.5 Fermi's Golden Rule#^thm-c9-5-1|Theorem §C9.5.1]]); $T$ replaces $V$ to all orders.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.1–6.1.1, eqs. (6.4)–(6.22)*

^thm-c10-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* The first-order Dyson term ([[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-2|Theorem §C9.4.2]]) has the form (6.10) with $T_{ni} = V_{ni}$ (Sakurai eq. (6.9)); (6.10) defines $T_{ni}$ so that the form holds exactly. The factor $e^{\varepsilon t'}$ makes the integral converge at $t' \to -\infty$, the switching-on in the remote past. With $\varepsilon \to 0$ first, $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{i\omega_{ni}t'}dt' = 2\pi\delta(\omega_{ni}) = 2\pi\hbar\,\delta(E_n - E_i)$, which gives (6.11).
>
> *2.* For $n \ne i$ the integral is elementary,
>
> $$
> \langle n|\mathscr U_I(t, -\infty)|i\rangle = -\frac i\hbar\,T_{ni}\,\frac{e^{i\omega_{ni}t + \varepsilon t}}{i\omega_{ni} + \varepsilon}, \qquad w = \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl[\frac{|T_{ni}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{e^{2\varepsilon t}}{\omega_{ni}^2 + \varepsilon^2}\Bigr] = \frac{|T_{ni}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{2\varepsilon\,e^{2\varepsilon t}}{\omega_{ni}^2 + \varepsilon^2} . \qquad (6.13)
> $$
>
> Since $\int d\omega/(\omega^2 + \varepsilon^2) = \pi/\varepsilon$ (6.14), $\varepsilon e^{2\varepsilon t}/(\omega^2 + \varepsilon^2) \to \pi\delta(\omega) = \pi\hbar\,\delta(E_n - E_i)$ at fixed $t$ (6.15), and $w$ is (6.16), with no $t$ left.
>
> *3.* In the box, $\mathbf k' = 2\pi\mathbf n/L$ with $\mathbf n$ a vector of integers, so $E_{\mathbf n} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\bigl(\frac{2\pi}L\bigr)^2|\mathbf n|^2$ and $\Delta E = \frac{\hbar^2}{m}\bigl(\frac{2\pi}L\bigr)^2|\mathbf n|\,\Delta|\mathbf n|$. A shell of radius $|\mathbf n| = Lk/2\pi$ and thickness $\Delta|\mathbf n|$ holds $|\mathbf n|^2\Delta|\mathbf n|\,d\Omega$ states in $d\Omega$ (6.18), so $\rho = \Delta n/\Delta E = (m/\hbar^2)(L/2\pi)^2|\mathbf n|\,d\Omega = (mk/\hbar^2)(L/2\pi)^3d\Omega$. Integrating (6.16) over $E_n$ against $\rho$ gives $w = mkL^3|T_{ni}|^2d\Omega/(2\pi)^2\hbar^3$ (6.20), and dividing by $|\mathbf j| = \hbar k/mL^3$ gives (6.22).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.1.2: The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation
> 1. The $T_{ni}$ of Theorem §C10.1.1 solve the linear system
>
> $$
> T_{ni} = V_{ni} + \sum_mV_{nm}\,\frac{T_{mi}}{E_i - E_m + i\hbar\varepsilon} . \qquad (6.26)
> $$
>
> 2. Define $|\psi^{(+)}\rangle$ by $T_{ni} = \langle n|V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle$ for all $n$ (6.27), and the **transition operator** $T$ by $T|i\rangle = V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle$. Then
>
> $$
> |\psi^{(+)}\rangle = |i\rangle + \frac{1}{E_i - H_0 + i\hbar\varepsilon}\,V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle , \qquad T = V + V\,\frac{1}{E_i - H_0 + i\hbar\varepsilon}\,T , \qquad (6.29),\ (6.31)
> $$
>
> and $d\sigma/d\Omega = (mL^3/2\pi\hbar^2)^2|\langle n|V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle|^2$ (6.30).
> 3. $|\psi^{(+)}\rangle$ is an eigenket of the full Hamiltonian with the energy of $|i\rangle$: $(H_0 + V)|\psi^{(+)}\rangle = E_i|\psi^{(+)}\rangle$.
> 4. Evolving backwards from a plane wave in the far future, (6.33)–(6.34), gives instead $|\psi^{(-)}\rangle$, with $-i\hbar\varepsilon$. Renaming $\hbar\varepsilon \to \varepsilon$ and writing $E$ for $E_i$, both are
>
> $$
> |\psi^{(\pm)}\rangle = |i\rangle + \frac{1}{E - H_0 \pm i\varepsilon}\,V|\psi^{(\pm)}\rangle . \qquad (6.35)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.1.2–6.2, eqs. (6.23)–(6.35)*

^thm-c10-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Insert (6.10), evaluated as in the derivation of Theorem §C10.1.1, $\langle m|\mathscr U_I(t', -\infty)|i\rangle = \delta_{mi} + \frac1\hbar T_{mi}\,e^{i\omega_{mi}t' + \varepsilon t'}/(-\omega_{mi} + i\varepsilon)$ (6.23), into the exact Dyson equation $\langle n|\mathscr U_I(t, -\infty)|i\rangle = \delta_{ni} - \frac i\hbar\sum_mV_{nm}\int_{-\infty}^te^{i\omega_{nm}t'}\langle m|\mathscr U_I(t', -\infty)|i\rangle\,dt'$ (6.24). The $\delta_{mi}$ term gives $\frac1\hbar V_{ni}\,e^{i\omega_{ni}t + \varepsilon t}/(-\omega_{ni} + i\varepsilon)$. In the other term $\omega_{nm} + \omega_{mi} = \omega_{ni}$, so its time integral is the same, $-\frac i\hbar\int_{-\infty}^te^{i\omega_{ni}t' + \varepsilon t'}dt' = \frac1\hbar\,e^{i\omega_{ni}t + \varepsilon t}/(-\omega_{ni} + i\varepsilon)$, multiplied by $\frac1\hbar\sum_mV_{nm}T_{mi}/(-\omega_{mi} + i\varepsilon)$ (6.25). The right side therefore has the form (6.23) with $T_{ni}$ replaced by $V_{ni} + \sum_mV_{nm}T_{mi}/\hbar(-\omega_{mi} + i\varepsilon)$; comparing, and using $-\hbar\omega_{mi} = E_i - E_m$, gives (6.26).
>
> *2.* With (6.27), (6.26) reads $\langle n|V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle = \langle n|V|i\rangle + \sum_m\langle n|V|m\rangle\langle m|V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle/(E_i - E_m + i\hbar\varepsilon)$ (6.28). Since $\sum_m|m\rangle\langle m|/(E_i - E_m + i\hbar\varepsilon) = (E_i - H_0 + i\hbar\varepsilon)^{-1}$ (a function of $H_0$, [[§C1.2 Kets, Bras, Operators and Matrix Representations#^thm-c1-2-3|Theorem §C1.2.3]]), this is $\langle n|V$ applied to (6.29), and it holds for every $n$. Applying $V$ to (6.29) and using $V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle = T|i\rangle$ gives the operator equation (6.31). (6.30) is (6.22) with (6.27).
>
> *3.* Apply $E_i - H_0$ to (6.29). $(E_i - H_0)|i\rangle = 0$, and $(E_i - H_0)(E_i - H_0 + i\hbar\varepsilon)^{-1} \to 1$ as $\varepsilon \to 0$, so $(E_i - H_0)|\psi^{(+)}\rangle = V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle$.
>
> *4.* Sakurai's (6.33) writes the formal solution for $t_0 \to +\infty$; the regulator $e^{-\varepsilon t'}$ (6.34) then flips the sign of $i\varepsilon$ everywhere above.

> [!remark] Remark: Why the sign of iε matters
> $E$ lies in the continuous spectrum of $H_0$, so $(E - H_0)^{-1}$ does not exist; $\pm i\varepsilon$ moves the energy off the real axis, and the two signs are two different boundary conditions, not two approximations. Below, $+$ gives an outgoing spherical wave and $-$ an incoming one (Theorem §C10.1.4). The physically prepared situation, a beam fired at a target, is $|\psi^{(+)}\rangle$: the time-dependent derivation produced $+i\varepsilon$ from switching $V$ on in the past. The same $+i\varepsilon$ made the energy transform of the propagator converge in [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-7|Theorem §C4.1.7]], where it expressed causality, $K = 0$ for $t < t_0$.

## The free Green's function

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.1.3: The Free Green's Function
> 1. For $\varepsilon > 0$ the **outgoing resolvent** of $H_0$ is the Laplace–Fourier transform of the retarded free evolution operator,
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{E - H_0 + i\varepsilon} = -\frac i\hbar\int_0^\infty dt\;e^{i(E + i\varepsilon)t/\hbar}\,e^{-iH_0t/\hbar} ,
> $$
>
> so its position matrix element is the energy transform of the retarded free propagator $\theta(t)K(\mathbf x, t; \mathbf x', 0)$ of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-4|Theorem §C4.1.4]].
> 2. With $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$,
>
> $$
> G_\pm(\mathbf x, \mathbf x') \equiv \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Bigl\langle\mathbf x\Bigl|\frac{1}{E - H_0 \pm i\varepsilon}\Bigr|\mathbf x'\Bigr\rangle = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\frac{e^{\pm ik|\mathbf x - \mathbf x'|}}{|\mathbf x - \mathbf x'|} . \qquad (6.37),\ (6.47)
> $$
>
> 3. $G_\pm$ is a Green's function of the Helmholtz equation,
>
> $$
> (\nabla^2 + k^2)\,G_\pm(\mathbf x, \mathbf x') = \delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x - \mathbf x') , \qquad (6.48)
> $$
>
> and for $\mathbf x \ne \mathbf x'$ it solves $H_0G_\pm = EG_\pm$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.2, eqs. (6.36)–(6.48) · Griffiths §10.4.1, eqs. (10.52)–(10.66); item 1 is the operator form of Sakurai eqs. (2.287)–(2.289)*

^thm-c10-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* On an eigenket of $H_0$ with eigenvalue $E'$ the right side is $-\frac i\hbar\int_0^\infty e^{i(E - E' + i\varepsilon)t/\hbar}dt = 1/(E - E' + i\varepsilon)$, the computation of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-7|Theorem §C4.1.7]]; both sides are functions of $H_0$ that agree on its eigenkets. Sandwiching between $\langle\mathbf x|$ and $|\mathbf x'\rangle$ turns $e^{-iH_0t/\hbar}$ into $K(\mathbf x, t; \mathbf x', 0)$, and $\int_0^\infty$ is the retardation $\theta(t)$ of [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-2|Theorem §C4.1.2]].
>
> *2.* Insert momentum eigenkets (continuum normalization, $\langle\mathbf x|\mathbf k'\rangle = e^{i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf x}/(2\pi)^{3/2}$), on which $H_0 = \hbar^2k'^2/2m$, and absorb the factor $2m/\hbar^2$ into $\varepsilon$:
>
> $$
> G_\pm = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}\int d^3k'\,\frac{e^{i\mathbf k'\cdot(\mathbf x - \mathbf x')}}{k^2 - k'^2 \pm i\varepsilon} = \frac{1}{8\pi^2}\,\frac{1}{i\rho}\int_{-\infty}^\infty k'\,dk'\,\frac{e^{ik'\rho} - e^{-ik'\rho}}{k^2 - k'^2 \pm i\varepsilon}, \qquad \rho = |\mathbf x - \mathbf x'| , \qquad (6.43)\text{–}(6.44)
> $$
>
> after the angular integral $\int_{-1}^1e^{ik'\rho\mu}d\mu = (e^{ik'\rho} - e^{-ik'\rho})/ik'\rho$ and extension of the even integrand to $k' < 0$. The poles are at $k' = \pm k_0$ with $k_0 = k \pm i\varepsilon$. For $G_+$: close the $e^{ik'\rho}$ term in the upper half-plane (the arc contributes nothing as $\operatorname{Im}k' \to +\infty$); it encloses $k' = +k_0$, with residue $-k_0e^{ik_0\rho}/2k_0$, giving $2\pi i(-\tfrac12e^{ik\rho}) = -\pi ie^{ik\rho}$ (6.45)–(6.46). Close the $-e^{-ik'\rho}$ term in the lower half-plane, clockwise, around $k' = -k_0$: residue $+\tfrac12e^{ik\rho}$, contribution $-2\pi i\cdot\tfrac12e^{ik\rho}$, the same. In total $G_+ = (1/8\pi^2i\rho)(-2\pi ie^{ik\rho}) = -e^{ik\rho}/4\pi\rho$. For $G_-$ the poles sit on the other sides and $k \to -k$.
>
> *3.* For $\rho > 0$, $\nabla^2(e^{\pm ik\rho}/\rho) = \rho^{-1}\,d^2(e^{\pm ik\rho})/d\rho^2 = -k^2e^{\pm ik\rho}/\rho$. Near $\rho = 0$, $e^{\pm ik\rho}/\rho = 1/\rho \pm ik + O(\rho)$, and $\nabla^2(1/\rho) = -4\pi\delta^{(3)}$ ([[§B1.2 The Dirac Delta Function and the Helmholtz Theorem#^thm-b1-2-2|EM Theorem §B1.2.2]]), which with the factor $-1/4\pi$ gives the delta function. Equivalently, $(E - H_0)$ acting on the resolvent gives $1$, and in position space $E - H_0 = (\hbar^2/2m)(\nabla^2 + k^2)$.

> [!remark] Remark: One kernel in time, energy and space
> [[§C4.1 Propagators|§C4.1]] met the free propagator in three readings, and [[§C4.1 Propagators#^rem-c4-1-4|Remark: One kernel, three readings]] promised scattering theory as the energy-domain one. Here it is: the retarded propagator, transformed to fixed energy, is $(2m/\hbar^2)G_+$, a spherical wave $e^{ik\rho}/\rho$ leaving the source point. It is the wavelet of Huygens–Fresnel optics, the kernel of the Fresnel–Kirchhoff integral ([[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-3|WO Theorem §B11.1.3]]). Griffiths finds $G$ by Fourier transform and notes that any solution $G_0$ of the homogeneous Helmholtz equation can be added (eq. (10.66)), which corresponds to how the contour skirts the poles. The $+i\varepsilon$ removes the ambiguity: it is the outgoing-wave (radiation) condition, and in time it is causality.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.1.4: The Integral Equation and the Scattering Amplitude
> For a local potential, (6.35) in position space is the integral equation
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf x|\psi^{(\pm)}\rangle = \langle\mathbf x|i\rangle - \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\int d^3x'\,\frac{e^{\pm ik|\mathbf x - \mathbf x'|}}{4\pi|\mathbf x - \mathbf x'|}\,V(\mathbf x')\,\langle\mathbf x'|\psi^{(\pm)}\rangle . \qquad (6.52)
> $$
>
> For a finite-range potential, $|i\rangle = |\mathbf k\rangle$ and $r = |\mathbf x| \to \infty$, with $\mathbf k' \equiv k\hat{\mathbf r}$ the wave vector pointing to the observation point,
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf x|\psi^{(+)}\rangle \xrightarrow{r\ \text{large}} \frac{1}{L^{3/2}}\Bigl[e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} + \frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\,f(\mathbf k', \mathbf k)\Bigr] , \qquad (6.57)
> $$
>
> the incident plane wave plus an outgoing spherical wave, with the **scattering amplitude**
>
> $$
> f(\mathbf k', \mathbf k) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\,L^3\int d^3x'\,\frac{e^{-i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf x'}}{L^{3/2}}\,V(\mathbf x')\,\langle\mathbf x'|\psi^{(+)}\rangle = -\frac{mL^3}{2\pi\hbar^2}\,\langle\mathbf k'|V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle = -\frac{mL^3}{2\pi\hbar^2}\,\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle , \qquad (6.58)
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(\mathbf k', \mathbf k)|^2 . \qquad (6.59)
> $$
>
> In continuum normalization, $f(\mathbf k', \mathbf k) = -(4\pi^2m/\hbar^2)\,\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle$. The state $|\psi^{(-)}\rangle$ is the plane wave plus an *incoming* spherical wave $e^{-ikr}/r$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.2, eqs. (6.49)–(6.59) · Griffiths §10.1.2, eqs. (10.12)–(10.14); §10.4.2, eqs. (10.67)–(10.76)*

^thm-c10-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Multiply (6.35) by $\langle\mathbf x|$ and insert $\int d^3x'\,|\mathbf x'\rangle\langle\mathbf x'|$ (6.36); by Theorem §C10.1.3 the kernel is $(2m/\hbar^2)G_\pm$, and locality (6.51) turns $\langle\mathbf x'|V|\psi^{(\pm)}\rangle$ into $V(\mathbf x')\langle\mathbf x'|\psi^{(\pm)}\rangle$, which gives (6.52). Only $|\mathbf x'| \le R$ contributes. For $r \gg r'$, with $\alpha$ the angle between $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf x'$,
>
> $$
> |\mathbf x - \mathbf x'| = r\Bigl(1 - \frac{2r'}{r}\cos\alpha + \frac{r'^2}{r^2}\Bigr)^{1/2} \approx r - \hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf x' , \qquad e^{\pm ik|\mathbf x - \mathbf x'|} \approx e^{\pm ikr}\,e^{\mp i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf x'} , \qquad (6.54)\text{–}(6.56)
> $$
>
> while $1/|\mathbf x - \mathbf x'| \approx 1/r$ suffices in the denominator, where the next term is smaller by $r'/r$ and not multiplied by $k$. With $\langle\mathbf x|\mathbf k\rangle = e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}/L^{3/2}$ this is (6.57) with the first form of (6.58); the integral is $L^{3/2}\langle\mathbf k'|V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle$ by locality, and $V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle = T|\mathbf k\rangle$. Comparing $|f|^2 = (mL^3/2\pi\hbar^2)^2|\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle|^2$ with (6.22) gives (6.59). A continuum ket is $(L/2\pi)^{3/2}$ times a box ket, so $\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle_{\text{box}} = (2\pi/L)^3\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle_{\text{cont}}$, which gives the continuum form. For $G_-$ the same steps give $e^{-ikr}/r$.

> [!remark] Remark: Two routes to the same formula
> Griffiths starts from the asymptotic form (his eq. (10.12)) as a physical requirement and gets $d\sigma/d\Omega = |f|^2$ by comparing the probability flowing through $d\sigma$ in the incident wave with that through $r^2d\Omega$ in the spherical wave (eq. (10.14)); his exact amplitude (10.76) is the first form of (6.58) in wave-function language. Here the asymptotic form is a consequence of the Lippmann–Schwinger equation, and the cross-section formula follows from the transition rate of Theorem §C10.1.1. The second route also explains the operator meaning of $f$: it is, up to a constant, the on-shell matrix element $\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle$, and the $1/r$ of the spherical wave is the $1/\rho$ of the Green's function. Sakurai notes that a real beam is a wave packet; the plane-wave treatment holds when the packet is much wider than the range of $V$ (§6.2.1).

> [!caution] Caution: Sakurai's and Griffiths' conventions
> - Griffiths calls the *incident* wave vector $\mathbf k'$ and the *scattered* one $\mathbf k$ (his eqs. (10.71), (10.78)), the reverse of Sakurai, and defines $\boldsymbol\kappa = \mathbf k' - \mathbf k$ (10.85), incident minus scattered, which is the same vector as $\mathbf q = \mathbf k - \mathbf k'$ here: the letters are swapped, the vector is not. Griffiths calls $\hbar(\mathbf k - \mathbf k')$ in his letters, i.e. $-\hbar\mathbf q$, the momentum transfer. Only $|\boldsymbol\kappa| = |\mathbf q| = 2k\sin(\theta/2)$ enters the formulas for central potentials.

> - Sakurai box-normalizes in §6.1–6.3 and continuum-normalizes from §6.4 on; $f$ is the same in both, but matrix elements of $T$ differ by $(2\pi/L)^3$.

## The Born series

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.1.5: The Born Series and the First Born Approximation
> Write $G = (E - H_0 + i\varepsilon)^{-1}$.
> 1. Iterating (6.31) gives the **Born series**
>
> $$
> T = V + VGV + VGVGV + \cdots , \qquad |\psi^{(+)}\rangle = |\mathbf k\rangle + GV|\mathbf k\rangle + GVGV|\mathbf k\rangle + \cdots , \qquad (6.71)
> $$
>
> and correspondingly $f = f^{(1)} + f^{(2)} + \cdots$, $f^{(n)}$ of $n$-th order in $V$.
> 2. The **first Born approximation**, $T \approx V$ (equivalently $|\psi^{(+)}\rangle \approx |\mathbf k\rangle$), is the Fourier transform of the potential at the wave-vector transfer:
>
> $$
> f^{(1)}(\mathbf k', \mathbf k) = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int d^3x'\;e^{i(\mathbf k - \mathbf k')\cdot\mathbf x'}\,V(\mathbf x') . \qquad (6.72)
> $$
>
> 3. For a central potential $V(r)$, with $q = |\mathbf k - \mathbf k'| = 2k\sin(\theta/2)$ (6.73),
>
> $$
> f^{(1)}(\theta) = -\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\,\frac1q\int_0^\infty rV(r)\sin(qr)\,dr . \qquad (6.74)
> $$
>
> 4. Consequently, in the first Born approximation for a central potential: $f$ depends on $E$ and $\theta$ only through $q$; $f$ is real; $d\sigma/d\Omega$ does not depend on the sign of $V$; at low energy $f \to -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int V\,d^3x$, independent of $\theta$; and $f$ is small at large $q$, where the integrand oscillates rapidly.
> 5. The second-order term is
>
> $$
> f^{(2)}(\mathbf k', \mathbf k) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\int d^3x'\int d^3x''\;e^{-i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf x'}\,V(\mathbf x')\,\Bigl[\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\,G_+(\mathbf x', \mathbf x'')\Bigr]\,V(\mathbf x'')\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x''} . \qquad (6.86)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.1.2, eq. (6.32); §6.3–6.3.1, eqs. (6.70)–(6.74), (6.86) and the five remarks after (6.83) · Griffiths §10.4.2–10.4.3, eqs. (10.79), (10.88), (10.101)*

^thm-c10-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Substitute (6.31) into itself repeatedly; for (6.71) apply $G$ and add $|\mathbf k\rangle$, using $T|\mathbf k\rangle = V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle$ and (6.29). Inserted into $f = -(4\pi^2m/\hbar^2)\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle$ (Theorem §C10.1.4), the $n$-th term of $T$ gives $f^{(n)}$.
>
> *2.* With $T = V$ and continuum normalization, $\langle\mathbf k'|V|\mathbf k\rangle = (2\pi)^{-3}\int d^3x'\,e^{-i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf x'}V(\mathbf x')e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x'}$, a function of the transfer $\mathbf q$ only ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^ex-c2-3-3|Example §C2.3.3]] computed it in momentum units), and $-(4\pi^2m/\hbar^2)(2\pi)^{-3} = -m/2\pi\hbar^2$.
>
> *3.* Take the polar axis along $\mathbf q$: $\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{-1}^1d\mu\,e^{iqr\mu} = 4\pi\sin(qr)/qr$, so $f^{(1)} = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\,4\pi\int_0^\infty r^2V\,\frac{\sin qr}{qr}\,dr$, which is (6.74). The triangle of $\mathbf k$, $\mathbf k'$ (both of length $k$) and $\mathbf q$ gives $q^2 = 2k^2(1 - \cos\theta) = 4k^2\sin^2(\theta/2)$.
>
> *4.* Read off (6.74): $k$ and $\theta$ enter only through $q$; the integral of a real function is real; $V \to -V$ flips the sign of $f$ only; as $q \to 0$, $\sin(qr)/q \to r$ and $4\pi\int r^2V\,dr = \int V\,d^3x$.
>
> *5.* The second term of (6.71) in continuum normalization: $\langle\mathbf k'|VGV|\mathbf k\rangle = (2\pi)^{-3}\int\!\!\int e^{-i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf x'}V(\mathbf x')\langle\mathbf x'|G|\mathbf x''\rangle V(\mathbf x'')e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x''}$, with $\langle\mathbf x'|G|\mathbf x''\rangle = (2m/\hbar^2)G_+(\mathbf x', \mathbf x'')$ (Theorem §C10.1.3), and again $-(4\pi^2m/\hbar^2)(2\pi)^{-3} = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}$.

^der-c10-1-5

*Uses:* [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-2|Theorem §C10.1.2]], [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-3|Theorem §C10.1.3]], [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Kicks and propagators
> Read $f^{(2)}$ from right to left: the incident wave is kicked by $V$ at $\mathbf x''$, travels to $\mathbf x'$ as the outgoing wavelet $G_+$, is kicked again, and leaves along $\mathbf k'$ (Sakurai Fig. 6.7). Griffiths draws the whole series this way (eq. (10.101), Fig. 10.13), calls the Green's function the *propagator* and $V$ the *vertex*, and notes that this picture inspired Feynman diagrams ([[Quantum Field Theory]]). The first term has a classical analogue: the impulse approximation, which integrates the force along the undeflected straight path ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-7|CM Theorem §B4.2.7]]; Griffiths §10.4.3). It also has an optical one. That $f^{(1)}$ is the Fourier transform of $V$ at $\mathbf q$ is the statement that the far-field pattern of a weak scatterer is the Fraunhofer transform of the scattering medium ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns|WO §B11.2]]). Likharev notes (footnote 33) that Rayleigh used the same first-order approximation for light in 1881; its long-wavelength limit, a point-like scatterer, is [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-2|WO Theorem §B13.3.2]].

^rem-c10-1-5

> [!remark] Remark: The Born cross-section is the golden rule
> With $T = V$, Theorem §C10.1.1 is Fermi's golden rule ([[§C9.5 Fermi's Golden Rule#^thm-c9-5-1|Theorem §C9.5.1]]) with plane-wave final states, and the cross-section is $(m/2\pi\hbar^2)^2|\tilde V(\mathbf q)|^2 = |f^{(1)}|^2$, where $\tilde V(\mathbf q) = \int d^3x\,e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}V(\mathbf x)$. That computation, with the same box counting, is [[§C9.5 Fermi's Golden Rule#^ex-c9-5-1|Example §C9.5.1]]; it is not repeated here, and its Coulomb limit is Example §C10.1.1 below. What the Lippmann–Schwinger equation adds is the replacement of $V$ by $T$, that is, the higher terms of the series, and with them unitarity ([[§C10.2 The Eikonal Approximation and the Optical Theorem|§C10.2]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.1.1, the remark after (6.16)*

^rem-c10-1-6

> [!remark] Remark: When is the Born approximation valid?
> The approximation replaces $\langle\mathbf x|\psi^{(+)}\rangle$ by $\langle\mathbf x|\mathbf k\rangle$ inside the potential, so the integral term of (6.52) must be small there compared with the plane wave. For a potential of typical size $V_0$ and range $a$ (Sakurai's rough estimate):
> - at low energy, $ka \ll 1$, the phases are $\approx 1$ and the condition is $m|V_0|a^2/\hbar^2 \ll 1$ (6.84): $|V_0|$ small compared with the confinement energy $\hbar^2/ma^2$ of the well;
> - at high energy, $ka \gg 1$, the phases oscillate and the condition relaxes to $(2m|V_0|a/\hbar^2k)\ln(ka) \ll 1$ (6.85). Up to the logarithm this is $|V_0|a/\hbar v \ll 1$, with $v = \hbar k/m$: the phase that the potential adds while the particle crosses it must be small (Likharev eq. (3.77) states the same two limits). That phase is the eikonal phase of [[§C10.2 The Eikonal Approximation and the Optical Theorem|§C10.2]].
>
> The Born approximation therefore improves with energy. For the Yukawa potential $V = V_0e^{-\mu r}/\mu r$ the range is $1/\mu$, and the low-energy condition reads $m|V_0|/\hbar^2\mu^2 \ll 1$. Sakurai compares it with the strength at which an attractive Yukawa potential first binds. Integrating the zero-energy radial equation $u'' + g\,e^{-x}u/x = 0$ ($x = \mu r$) numerically gives the threshold
>
> $$
> \frac{2m|V_0|}{\hbar^2\mu^2} \ge 1.680 ,
> $$
>
> where the printed text has $2.7$ (Sakurai p. 262). Either way, a potential strong enough to bind is too strong for the Born approximation at low energy. [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^ex-c10-1-2|Example §C10.1.2]] shows how it fails, and [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^ex-c10-1-3|Example §C10.1.3]] shows why: the bound state limits the convergence of the series.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.3, eqs. (6.84)–(6.85) · Likharev §3.3, eqs. (3.75)–(3.77) (the Yukawa threshold computed here)*

^rem-c10-1-7

> [!remark] Remark: Diffraction minima measure a nucleus
> For a uniform sphere, $V = V_0$ for $r \le a$ (6.75), (6.74) gives
>
> $$
> f^{(1)}(\theta) = -\frac{2mV_0a^3}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{1}{(qa)^2}\Bigl[\frac{\sin qa}{qa} - \cos qa\Bigr] , \qquad (6.76)
> $$
>
> which vanishes at $qa = 4.49, 7.73, 10.9, \dots$, the roots of $\tan x = x$. Sakurai's Fig. 6.6 (800-MeV protons on calcium isotopes) shows such minima. For $^{40}$Ca the first one lies near $\theta = 7.9^\circ$ (read from the figure). In the centre-of-mass frame the proton's momentum is $1399$ MeV/$c$, so $k = 7.09\ \mathrm{fm}^{-1}$, and $q = 2k\sin(3.95^\circ) = 0.977\ \mathrm{fm}^{-1}$ gives $a = 4.49/q = 4.6$ fm. The empirical $1.4A^{1/3}$ fm of Sakurai Problem 6.3 is $4.8$ fm; the nuclear-radius law of level A gives the same scaling ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-1|QM Law §A7.1.1]]). The minima move to smaller angles from $^{40}$Ca to $^{48}$Ca (first minimum near $7.5^\circ$). Their ratio, $1.05$, is close to $(48/40)^{1/3} = 1.06$, as $a \propto A^{1/3}$ predicts. A square well is a crude nuclear potential: the later minima of the data are more evenly spaced than the roots of $\tan x = x$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.3, eqs. (6.75)–(6.76), Fig. 6.6, Problem 6.3 (estimate made here)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C10.1.1: Yukawa, Coulomb and Rutherford
> Find the first Born cross-section for the Yukawa potential $V = V_0e^{-\mu r}/\mu r$ (6.77), its total cross-section, and its Coulomb limit.
>
> *Amplitude.* By (6.74), with $\int_0^\infty e^{-\mu r}\sin(qr)\,dr = \operatorname{Im}\bigl[1/(\mu - iq)\bigr] = q/(\mu^2 + q^2)$ (6.79) (the momentum matrix element of $e^{-\mu r}/r$ in [[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space#^ex-c2-3-3|Example §C2.3.3]]),
>
> $$
> f^{(1)}(\theta) = -\frac{2mV_0}{\mu\hbar^2}\,\frac{1}{q^2 + \mu^2}, \qquad \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \Bigl(\frac{2mV_0}{\mu\hbar^2}\Bigr)^2\frac{1}{\bigl[2k^2(1 - \cos\theta) + \mu^2\bigr]^2} . \qquad (6.78),\ (6.81)
> $$
>
> *Total cross-section.* With $c = \cos\theta$, $A = 2k^2 + \mu^2$, $B = 2k^2$: $\int_{-1}^1dc/(A - Bc)^2 = 2/(A^2 - B^2) = 2/\mu^2(\mu^2 + 4k^2)$, so
>
> $$
> \sigma_{\text{tot}} = \frac{16\pi m^2V_0^2}{\hbar^4\mu^4\,(\mu^2 + 4k^2)} ,
> $$
>
> finite, falling as $1/E$ at high energy.
>
> *Coulomb limit.* Let $\mu \to 0$ with $V_0/\mu = ZZ'e^2$ fixed, so that $V \to ZZ'e^2/r$. Then $f^{(1)} = -2mZZ'e^2/\hbar^2q^2$, and with $q^2 = 4k^2\sin^2(\theta/2)$ and $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$,
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{(2m)^2(ZZ'e^2)^2}{\hbar^4}\,\frac{1}{16k^4\sin^4(\theta/2)} = \Bigl(\frac{ZZ'e^2}{4E}\Bigr)^2\frac{1}{\sin^4(\theta/2)} . \qquad (6.82),\ (6.83)
> $$
>
> $\hbar$ has dropped out: this is Rutherford's formula, [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-5|CM Theorem §B4.2.5]] with $\alpha = ZZ'e^2$, and level A's [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-4|QM Theorem §A2.1.4]]. The total cross-section diverges as $\mu \to 0$, as the classical one does for a field of infinite range ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^rem-b4-2-2|CM Remark: Finite and infinite total cross-sections]]). The result is the same, but the reason is new: it comes from the Fourier transform of $1/r$, not from hyperbolic orbits.
>
> *How good is it?* By the high-energy criterion of [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^rem-c10-1-7|Remark: When is the Born approximation valid?]] with $V_0a \to ZZ'e^2$, the Born approximation needs $ZZ'e^2/\hbar v \ll 1$. For Rutherford's $5$-MeV alpha particles on gold, $v/c = 0.052$ and $ZZ'e^2/\hbar v = 2\cdot79/(137\cdot0.052) \approx 22$: far outside the Born regime. That large ratio is the closest-approach distance in units of the reduced wavelength, so the classical picture is the right one there. Griffiths remarks that for the Coulomb potential classical mechanics, the Born approximation and quantum field theory all give the same cross-section; the formula is robust. (The exact Coulomb solution, which shows this, is not in the sources.)
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.3, eqs. (6.77)–(6.83) · Griffiths Examples 10.5–10.6, eqs. (10.90)–(10.94); Problem 10.12 (solved here)*

> [!caution] Caution: The Coulomb potential is not short-ranged

The partial-wave method of [[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts|§C10.3]] does not apply as it stands, because $1/r$ falls more slowly than the centrifugal term (his footnote 4). And the amplitude obtained as the $\mu \to 0$ limit of the Yukawa amplitude misses a phase factor (his footnote 20, citing Messiah). > [!example] Example §C10.1.2: The Square Well, Born Against Exact
> For $V = V_0$ ($r < R$), $V = 0$ ($r > R$), compare the first Born approximation with the exact result at zero energy, as a function of $\lambda = 2mV_0R^2/\hbar^2$ (positive for a barrier, negative for a well).
>
> *Born.* By item 4 of Theorem §C10.1.5, $f \to -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}V_0\frac{4\pi R^3}{3} = -\frac{2mV_0R^3}{3\hbar^2}$ (Griffiths eqs. (10.82)–(10.84)), isotropic. In terms of the scattering length $a = -\lim_{k\to0}f$ ([[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^def-c10-3-1|Def. §C10.3.1]]), $a_{\text{Born}} = \lambda R/3$, and $\sigma \to 4\pi a^2 = 4\pi R^2(\lambda/3)^2$.
>
> *Exact.* The exact zero-energy result ([[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^thm-c10-3-8|Theorem §C10.3.8]]) is, with $g = \sqrt{|\lambda|}$,
>
> $$
> \frac aR = 1 - \frac{\tanh g}{g} \quad(\lambda = g^2 > 0), \qquad \frac aR = 1 - \frac{\tan g}{g} \quad(\lambda = -g^2 < 0) .
> $$
>
> Expanding either form, $a/R = \lambda/3 - 2\lambda^2/15 + \cdots$. The first term is the first Born approximation. The second is the second Born term, $-\frac{2mV_0R^3}{3\hbar^2}\bigl[1 - \frac{4mV_0R^2}{5\hbar^2}\bigr]$ for $f$, which is the answer to Griffiths Problem 10.15, since $2\lambda/5 = 4mV_0R^2/5\hbar^2$.
>
> *What the figure shows.*
> - For $|\lambda| \lesssim 1$ Born is accurate, as (6.84) requires.
> - For a strong barrier the exact $a$ saturates at $R$: the barrier becomes a hard sphere, with $\sigma \to 4\pi R^2$ ([[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^thm-c10-3-6|Theorem §C10.3.6]]). Born instead grows as $\lambda^2$ without bound.
> - For a well the exact $\sigma$ diverges at $\lambda = -\pi^2/4$. That is where the well acquires its first bound state ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^rem-b5-3-4|QM ★ Remark: A shallow spherical well need not bind]]; the threshold $\beta = \pi/2$ of [[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^ex-c6-1-1|Example §C6.1.1]]). No finite order of the Born series sees it.
>
> The power series of $\tan g/g$ in $\lambda = -g^2$ has its nearest singularity at $g = \pi/2$. So the Born series for $a$ converges only for $|\lambda| < \pi^2/4 \approx 2.47$, *on the repulsive side too*: for $\lambda = 4$ the second-order result is already negative, although nothing happens to the barrier. The bound state of the attractive well of the same strength sets the radius of convergence.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.3, eqs. (6.75)–(6.76), (6.84) · Griffiths Example 10.4, eqs. (10.81)–(10.84); Problems 10.10, 10.15 (solved here; the convergence radius and the figure computed here)*

> [!example] Example §C10.1.3: The Delta Potential, Solved by the Lippmann–Schwinger Equation
> In one dimension, take the incident wave $\phi(x) = \langle x|i\rangle = e^{ikx}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ and the attractive potential $V(x) = -(\gamma\hbar^2/2m)\delta(x)$, $\gamma > 0$. Find the one-dimensional $G_+$, solve the Lippmann–Schwinger equation, and find $T(k)$, $R(k)$ and their poles. Here $\psi = T\phi$ for $x > 0$ and $\psi = \phi + R\,e^{-ikx}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ for $x < 0$.
>
> *Green's function.* As in Theorem §C10.1.3, $G_+(x, x') = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int dk'\,e^{ik'(x - x')}/(k^2 - k'^2 + i\varepsilon)$. For $x - x' > 0$ close above, around $k' = k + i\varepsilon$, residue $-e^{ik(x - x')}/2k$; for $x - x' < 0$ close below. Either way
>
> $$
> G_+(x, x') = -\frac{i}{2k}\,e^{ik|x - x'|} ,
> $$
>
> an outgoing wave in both directions (Griffiths eq. (10.102) has the same kernel, $(2m/\hbar^2)G_+ = -(im/\hbar^2k)e^{ik|x - x_0|}$).
>
> *Solution.* $\psi(x) = \phi(x) + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\int G_+(x, x')V(x')\psi(x')\,dx' = \phi(x) + \frac{i\gamma}{2k}\,e^{ik|x|}\,\psi(0)$. At $x = 0$ this is one linear equation, $\psi(0) = \phi(0) + \frac{i\gamma}{2k}\psi(0)$, so $\psi(0) = \phi(0)/(1 - i\gamma/2k)$, and
>
> $$
> T(k) = \frac{1}{1 - i\gamma/2k}, \qquad R(k) = \frac{i\gamma/2k}{1 - i\gamma/2k}, \qquad |T|^2 + |R|^2 = \frac{1 + \gamma^2/4k^2}{1 + \gamma^2/4k^2} = 1 .
> $$
>
> With $\gamma = 2m\alpha/\hbar^2$ and $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, $|T|^2 = 1/(1 + m\alpha^2/2\hbar^2E)$. That is level B's [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-5|QM Theorem §B3.3.5]], which was found by matching $\psi$ and the jump of $\psi'$ (Sakurai's part (d)). The same result comes here from the integral equation, which a delta function makes algebraic.
>
> *Poles.* $T$ and $R$ have a pole where $1 - i\gamma/2k = 0$, at $k = i\gamma/2 \equiv i\kappa$, i.e. $E = -\hbar^2\gamma^2/8m = -m\alpha^2/2\hbar^2$. This is the bound state of [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^thm-b3-3-3|QM Theorem §B3.3.3]], as anticipated in [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^rem-b3-3-4|QM ★ Remark: The bound state as a pole of the transmission amplitude]].
>
> *Born series.* Expanding, $T = \sum_n(i\gamma/2k)^n$ and $R = \sum_{n\ge1}(i\gamma/2k)^n$: the Born series is a geometric series. To first order $|R|^2 \approx \gamma^2/4k^2 = (m\alpha/\hbar^2k)^2$, Griffiths' one-dimensional Born formula (10.103) for a delta function. The series converges only for $|\gamma/2k| < 1$, i.e. $k > \kappa$, or $E > |E_{\text{bound}}|$: once more the bound state sets the limit of the Born series, as in Example §C10.1.2.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 6.1 (solved here) · Griffiths Problems 10.16–10.18*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Inelastic electron–atom scattering and form factors
> Sakurai's §6.9 applies the Born approximation (golden rule) to a fast electron hitting an atom in its ground state $|0\rangle$, which it may leave in an excited state $|n\rangle$ (inelastic scattering, with $k' \ne k$). The interaction is $V = -Ze^2/r + \sum_i e^2/|\mathbf x - \mathbf x_i|$ (6.253), and the exchange of the projectile with atomic electrons is neglected for a fast electron. The Fourier transform of $1/|\mathbf x - \mathbf x_i|$ is $(4\pi/q^2)e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x_i}$ (6.256)–(6.257), so everything is expressed by the **form factor**
>
> $$
> ZF_n(\mathbf q) = \Bigl\langle n\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^Ze^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x_i}\Bigr|0\Bigr\rangle , \qquad \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(0 \to n) = \frac{4m_e^2(Ze^2)^2}{\hbar^4q^4}\,\frac{k'}{k}\,\bigl|-\delta_{n0} + F_n(\mathbf q)\bigr|^2 , \qquad (6.259),\ (6.261)
> $$
>
> the Fourier transform of the atomic electron density between the two states. For $n \ne 0$ this is $4Z^2a_0^2(k'/k)|F_n|^2/(qa_0)^4$ with $a_0 = \hbar^2/m_ee^2$ (6.263). Three points:
> - *Elastic scattering is screened.* $F_0(\mathbf q) \to 1$ as $q \to 0$, so $-1 + F_0$ cancels the Rutherford $1/q^4$ at small angles: a neutral atom has a finite forward cross-section. For hydrogen, $F_0(q) = 16/[4 + (qa_0)^2]^2$, and for a fast positron (charge $+e$, all signs reversed) this gives Sakurai Problem 6.12's $d\sigma/d\Omega = (4m^2e^4/\hbar^4q^4)\{1 - 16/[4 + (qa_0)^2]^2\}^2$.
> - *Stopping power.* Weighting the inelastic cross-sections with the energy losses $E_n - E_0$ leads, after the sum rules Sakurai only cites, to Bohr's and Bethe's formula $-dE/dx = (4\pi NZe^4/m_ev^2)\ln(2m_ev^2/I)$ (6.267). It is independent of the projectile's mass and depends on its charge and speed, which is why particle tracks identify charge and velocity.
> - *Nuclear size.* At $q \sim 1/R_{\text{nucleus}}$ the nucleus is not a point. Its charge density $N(r)$ multiplies the Rutherford cross-section by $|F(\mathbf q)|^2$, $F = \int d^3x\,e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}N(r) = 1 - \tfrac16q^2\langle r^2\rangle + \cdots$ (6.268)–(6.275). The slope of $F$ at small $q$ measures the mean square charge radius, as in Hofstadter's electron-scattering experiments behind [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-1|QM Law §A7.1.1]].
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.9–6.9.1, eqs. (6.247)–(6.275); Problem 6.12*
