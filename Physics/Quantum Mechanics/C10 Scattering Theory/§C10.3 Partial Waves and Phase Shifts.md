For a central potential, angular momentum is conserved. How much does that simplify scattering? The free states of definite angular momentum are already known: the spherical Bessel functions of [[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]] (level B's [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^ex-b5-3-1|QM ★ Example §B5.3.1]]), with angular parts that are rotation matrices ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-4|Theorem §C5.4.4]]) and Legendre polynomials ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^def-b3-4-1|EM Def. §B3.4.1]]). One dimension had a counterpart: on a half-line every wave is reflected, and all the information sits in one phase shift ([[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^rem-b3-4-7|QM ★ Remark: Phase shifts, Levinson's theorem and resonances]]). Level C derives the partial-wave expansion from symmetry rather than from separation of variables. $T$ is a scalar operator, so by Wigner–Eckart it is diagonal in $|E\,\ell\,m\rangle$; conservation of probability then reduces each partial wave to a single real phase shift $\delta_\ell(k)$. The section computes phase shifts for the hard sphere and at low energy, where the scattering length governs, and connects them to the eikonal of [[§C10.2 The Eikonal Approximation and the Optical Theorem|§C10.2]]. PHY 512 covered partial waves; resonances, poles, symmetry and identical-particle scattering (Sakurai §6.6.3–6.8) are beyond its schedule and appear as folded ★ remarks.

*Notation.* Kets $|E\,\ell\,m\rangle$ are free spherical waves with $H_0 = E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, $\mathbf L^2 = \ell(\ell+1)\hbar^2$ and $L_z = m\hbar$; $m$ also denotes the mass, which cannot be confused in a formula. Continuum normalization, $\langle\mathbf x|\mathbf k\rangle = e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}/(2\pi)^{3/2}$. $j_\ell$, $n_\ell$ and $h^{(1,2)}_\ell = j_\ell \pm in_\ell$ are the spherical Bessel, Neumann and Hankel functions (Sakurai's sign of $n_\ell$, $n_0 = -\cos\rho/\rho$, as in [[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]]); a prime on them denotes the derivative with respect to the argument. $R$ is the range of $V$.

## Spherical waves and the partial-wave expansion

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.3.1: Free Spherical Waves and Rayleigh's Formula
> 1. The free states $|E\,\ell\,m\rangle$, normalized as $\langle E'\ell'm'|E\,\ell\,m\rangle = \delta_{\ell\ell'}\delta_{mm'}\delta(E - E')$ (6.87), have the momentum- and position-space wave functions
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf k|E\,\ell\,m\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{mk}}\,\delta\Bigl(\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} - E\Bigr)\,Y^m_\ell(\hat{\mathbf k}), \qquad \langle\mathbf x|E\,\ell\,m\rangle = \frac{i^\ell}{\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{2mk}{\pi}}\;j_\ell(kr)\,Y^m_\ell(\hat{\mathbf r}) . \qquad (6.107a),\ (6.107b)
> $$
>
> 2. A plane wave is a superposition of all of them, $|\mathbf k\rangle = \sum_{\ell m}|E\,\ell\,m\rangle\big|_{E = \hbar^2k^2/2m}\,\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{mk}}Y^{m*}_\ell(\hat{\mathbf k})$ (6.101), or in position space (**Rayleigh's formula**)
>
> $$
> e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} = \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty(2\ell + 1)\,i^\ell\,j_\ell(kr)\,P_\ell(\hat{\mathbf k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}) . \qquad (6.104)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.4.1, eqs. (6.87)–(6.107) · Griffiths §10.2.2, eq. (10.28) · Likharev §3.8, eq. (3.225)*

^thm-c10-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Angular dependence.* $L_z|k\hat{\mathbf z}\rangle = (xp_y - yp_x)|k\hat{\mathbf z}\rangle = 0$, since $p_x$ and $p_y$ vanish on it (6.89), so $|k\hat{\mathbf z}\rangle$ contains only $m' = 0$ components (6.90). A general $|\mathbf k\rangle$ is $\mathscr D(\phi, \theta, 0)|k\hat{\mathbf z}\rangle$ (6.91). Hence $\langle E\,\ell\,m|\mathbf k\rangle = \mathscr D^{(\ell)}_{m0}(\phi, \theta, 0)\,\langle E\,\ell\,0|k\hat{\mathbf z}\rangle$ (6.92), and $\mathscr D^{(\ell)}_{m0}(\phi, \theta, 0) = \sqrt{4\pi/(2\ell+1)}\,Y^{m*}_\ell(\theta, \phi)$ ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-4|Theorem §C5.4.4]]) gives the angular factor $Y^m_\ell(\hat{\mathbf k})$ of $\langle\mathbf k|E\,\ell\,m\rangle$ (6.93).
>
> *Radial factor.* $\langle\mathbf k|(H_0 - E)|E\,\ell\,m\rangle = (\hbar^2k^2/2m - E)\langle\mathbf k|E\,\ell\,m\rangle = 0$ forces $g_{\ell E}(k) = N\delta(\hbar^2k^2/2m - E)$ (6.94)–(6.97). Then
>
> $$
> \langle E'\ell'm'|E\,\ell\,m\rangle = \int k''^2dk''\,d\Omega''\,|N|^2\delta\bigl(E'' - E'\bigr)\delta\bigl(E'' - E\bigr)Y^{m'*}_{\ell'}Y^m_\ell = |N|^2\frac{mk}{\hbar^2}\,\delta(E - E')\,\delta_{\ell\ell'}\delta_{mm'} , \qquad (6.98)
> $$
>
> with $E'' = \hbar^2k''^2/2m$, $dE''/dk'' = \hbar^2k''/m$ and the orthonormality of the $Y^m_\ell$. So $N = \hbar/\sqrt{mk}$ (6.99). This is the three-dimensional form of the energy relabelling in [[§C2.1 Continuous Spectra and Position Eigenkets#^ex-c2-1-2|Example §C2.1.2]], item 2, where the energy-normalized one-dimensional plane waves were set aside for scattering.
>
> *Rayleigh's formula.* As a function of $\mu = \hat{\mathbf k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}$, $e^{ikr\mu}$ has the Legendre expansion ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-2|EM Theorem §B3.4.2]], item 5) with coefficients $\frac{2\ell+1}{2}\int_{-1}^1e^{ikr\mu}P_\ell(\mu)\,d\mu = (2\ell+1)\,i^\ell j_\ell(kr)$, by the integral representation $j_\ell(\rho) = \frac{1}{2i^\ell}\int_{-1}^1e^{i\rho\mu}P_\ell(\mu)\,d\mu$ (6.105) (Sakurai (3.283), quoted in [[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials|§C6.1]]).
>
> *Position space.* The regular free solution with angular part $Y^m_\ell$ is $c_\ell j_\ell(kr)Y^m_\ell(\hat{\mathbf r})$ ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]]) (6.102). Expanding $\langle\mathbf x|\mathbf k\rangle = \sum_{\ell m}\int dE\,\langle\mathbf x|E\,\ell\,m\rangle\langle E\,\ell\,m|\mathbf k\rangle$ and summing over $m$ with the addition theorem $\sum_mY^m_\ell(\hat{\mathbf r})Y^{m*}_\ell(\hat{\mathbf k}) = \frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_\ell(\hat{\mathbf k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})$ ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^rem-c5-4-5|★ Remark: The addition theorem from the group property]]) gives $\sum_\ell\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_\ell\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{mk}}c_\ell j_\ell$ (6.103). Comparing with Rayleigh's formula divided by $(2\pi)^{3/2}$ gives $c_\ell = (i^\ell/\hbar)\sqrt{2mk/\pi}$ (6.106), since $4\pi/(2\pi)^{3/2} = \sqrt{2/\pi}$.

> [!remark] Remark: Every impact parameter in one plane wave
> A plane wave is infinitely wide, so it contains every impact parameter $b$, and semiclassically $\ell\hbar = bp$, i.e. $b = \ell/k$: Rayleigh's sum over all $\ell$ is the quantum form of that statement (Sakurai). Sakurai also reads (6.107a) as a decay amplitude. A spin-$j$ parent that decays into two spinless particles conserves angular momentum, so the relative momentum of the products has the angular wave function $Y^m_j(\hat{\mathbf k})$. Example: $^{20}$Ne$^* \to {}^{16}$O $+ {}^4$He from spin $1$ gives $\sin^2\theta$ for $m = \pm1$ and $\cos^2\theta$ for $m = 0$, and an isotropic distribution if the parent is unpolarized (6.108)–(6.109).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.3.2: The Partial-Wave Expansion
> Let $V = V(r)$ be central. Then:
> 1. $T$ commutes with $\mathbf L$ and is diagonal in the spherical-wave basis on the energy shell,
>
> $$
> \langle E\,\ell'\,m'|T|E\,\ell\,m\rangle = T_\ell(E)\,\delta_{\ell\ell'}\delta_{mm'} , \qquad (6.110)
> $$
>
> with $T_\ell$ independent of $m$.
> 2. With the **partial-wave amplitudes** $f_\ell(k) \equiv -\pi T_\ell(E)/k$ (6.114) and $\theta$ the angle between $\mathbf k'$ and $\mathbf k$,
>
> $$
> f(\theta) = \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty(2\ell + 1)\,f_\ell(k)\,P_\ell(\cos\theta) . \qquad (6.115)
> $$
>
> 3. At large $r$ each partial wave consists of an incoming wave, unchanged by $V$, and an outgoing wave whose coefficient is changed from $1$ to $1 + 2ikf_\ell$:
>
> $$
> \langle\mathbf x|\psi^{(+)}\rangle \xrightarrow{r\ \text{large}} \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\sum_\ell(2\ell + 1)\,\frac{P_\ell(\cos\theta)}{2ik}\Bigl[\bigl(1 + 2ikf_\ell(k)\bigr)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r} - \frac{e^{-i(kr - \ell\pi)}}{r}\Bigr] . \qquad (6.117)\text{–}(6.118)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.4.2, eqs. (6.110)–(6.118) · Griffiths §10.2.1, eqs. (10.21)–(10.27) · Likharev §3.8, eqs. (3.219)–(3.224)*

^thm-c10-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $H_0$ and $V(r)$ commute with $\mathbf L$ ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]), hence so does every term of the Born series (6.71): $T$ is a scalar operator ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]). The Wigner–Eckart theorem for rank $0$ ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-6|Theorem §C7.2.6]]) makes its matrix elements diagonal in $\ell$ and $m$ and independent of $m$.
>
> *2.* Insert spherical waves into $f = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(2\pi)^3\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle$ ([[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^thm-c10-1-4|Theorem §C10.1.4]]). The two energy delta functions of (6.107a) put both energies at $\hbar^2k^2/2m$ and leave the factor $\hbar^2/mk$:
>
> $$
> f = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(2\pi)^3\frac{\hbar^2}{mk}\sum_{\ell m}T_\ell(E)\,Y^m_\ell(\hat{\mathbf k}')Y^{m*}_\ell(\hat{\mathbf k}) = -\frac{4\pi^2}{k}\sum_{\ell m}T_\ell\,Y^m_\ell(\hat{\mathbf k}')Y^{m*}_\ell(\hat{\mathbf k}) . \qquad (6.111)
> $$
>
> With $\mathbf k$ along $z$, $Y^m_\ell(\hat{\mathbf z}) = \sqrt{(2\ell+1)/4\pi}\,\delta_{m0}$ (6.112) and $Y^0_\ell = \sqrt{(2\ell+1)/4\pi}\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)$ (6.113), which gives (6.115).
>
> *3.* Substitute Rayleigh's formula and $j_\ell(kr) \to \bigl[e^{i(kr - \ell\pi/2)} - e^{-i(kr - \ell\pi/2)}\bigr]/2ikr$ (6.116) into $\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\bigl[e^{ikz} + f\,e^{ikr}/r\bigr]$. Since $i^\ell e^{-i\ell\pi/2} = 1$ and $i^\ell e^{i\ell\pi/2} = e^{i\ell\pi}$, the plane wave contributes $\frac{P_\ell}{2ik}\bigl[e^{ikr} - e^{-i(kr - \ell\pi)}\bigr]/r$ per unit $(2\ell+1)$, and (6.115) adds $f_\ell e^{ikr}/r = \frac{2ikf_\ell}{2ik}e^{ikr}/r$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.3.3: Unitarity and Phase Shifts
> For a real potential, conservation of probability holds in each partial wave separately:
>
> $$
> S_\ell(k) \equiv 1 + 2ikf_\ell(k), \qquad |S_\ell(k)| = 1 , \qquad (6.121)\text{–}(6.122)
> $$
>
> so $S_\ell = e^{2i\delta_\ell}$ with a real **phase shift** $\delta_\ell(k)$, defined modulo $\pi$ (6.123). Then
>
> $$
> f_\ell = \frac{e^{2i\delta_\ell} - 1}{2ik} = \frac{e^{i\delta_\ell}\sin\delta_\ell}{k} = \frac{1}{k\cot\delta_\ell - ik}, \qquad f(\theta) = \frac1k\sum_{\ell=0}^\infty(2\ell + 1)\,e^{i\delta_\ell}\sin\delta_\ell\,P_\ell(\cos\theta) , \qquad (6.124)\text{–}(6.126)
> $$
>
> $$
> \sigma_{\text{tot}} = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_{\ell=0}^\infty(2\ell + 1)\sin^2\delta_\ell , \qquad (6.127)
> $$
>
> and the optical theorem ([[§C10.2 The Eikonal Approximation and the Optical Theorem#^thm-c10-2-1|Theorem §C10.2.1]]) holds term by term. Each partial cross-section obeys the **unitarity bound**
>
> $$
> \sigma_\ell \le \sigma^{(\ell)}_{\max} = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2\ell + 1) , \qquad (6.131)
> $$
>
> reached when $\sin^2\delta_\ell = 1$. In the complex plane, $kf_\ell = \frac i2 + \frac12e^{-i\pi/2 + 2i\delta_\ell}$ lies on the **unitarity circle** of radius $\tfrac12$ about $i/2$ (6.129).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.4.3, eqs. (6.119)–(6.131), Fig. 6.8 · Griffiths §10.3, eqs. (10.41)–(10.48), Problem 10.19*

^thm-c10-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For a stationary state $\partial|\psi|^2/\partial t = 0$, so $\nabla\cdot\mathbf j = 0$ (6.119), and by Gauss's theorem the net flux through a large sphere vanishes (6.120). On the sphere the radial current is a sum over $\ell$ without cross terms. The angular integrals of products $P_\ell P_{\ell'}$ vanish for $\ell \ne \ell'$ ([[§B3.4 Separation of Variables in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-b3-4-2|EM Theorem §B3.4.2]], item 4), and incoming and outgoing waves do not interfere in the $r$-averaged flux. For each $\ell$ the outgoing flux $\propto|1 + 2ikf_\ell|^2$ must equal the incoming flux $\propto1$. This is (6.122) (Sakurai's "because of angular-momentum conservation, this must hold for each partial wave separately").
>
> The forms of $f_\ell$ are algebra: $e^{2i\delta} - 1 = 2ie^{i\delta}\sin\delta$, and $e^{i\delta}\sin\delta = 1/(\cot\delta - i)$. For $\sigma_{\text{tot}}$ integrate $|f|^2$ with orthogonality, $\int d\Omega\,P_\ell P_{\ell'} = 4\pi\delta_{\ell\ell'}/(2\ell + 1)$ (6.127). At $\theta = 0$, $P_\ell(1) = 1$, so $\operatorname{Im}f(0) = \frac1k\sum(2\ell+1)\sin^2\delta_\ell = \frac{k}{4\pi}\sigma_{\text{tot}}$ (6.128). The unitarity bound is $\sin^2\delta_\ell \le 1$. Finally $\frac i2 + \frac12e^{-i\pi/2}e^{2i\delta} = \frac i2\bigl(1 - e^{2i\delta}\bigr) = \frac{e^{2i\delta} - 1}{2i} = kf_\ell$.

> [!remark] Remark: What level C adds, and the one-dimensional picture
> Griffiths obtains (6.126)–(6.127) by solving the Schrödinger equation: separate variables, match the exterior solution $\sum i^\ell(2\ell+1)[j_\ell + ika_\ell h^{(1)}_\ell]P_\ell$ (his eq. (10.29)), and identify the amplitude $a_\ell = e^{i\delta_\ell}\sin\delta_\ell/k$ (10.46). Here the same formulas rest on two principles, rotational invariance (Wigner–Eckart) and conservation of probability (unitarity). Sakurai stresses that this route generalizes to situations where a potential description fails. It is the same result for a new reason, a layer change.
>
> The physical picture is the one-dimensional half-line of [[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^rem-b3-4-7|QM ★ Remark: Phase shifts, Levinson's theorem and resonances]]. With $u = rA_\ell$ and $u(0) = 0$, each partial wave is a wave on $0 < r < \infty$ with a wall at the origin. Everything is reflected; the incoming part $e^{-i(kr - \ell\pi)}$ is untouched, and the outgoing part returns with the phase $e^{2i\delta_\ell}$. Griffiths: phase-shifted by $\delta_\ell$ on the way in and again on the way out (his §10.3). The one-dimensional phase shift below a step, $\delta = \arctan(k/\kappa)$ ([[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^rem-b3-4-3|QM Remark: The step in the language of currents]]), is the same object for a single "partial wave".

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.3.4: Phase Shifts from the Logarithmic Derivative
> Let $V = 0$ for $r > R$. Outside, the radial wave function is fixed by the phase shift,
>
> $$
> A_\ell(r) = e^{i\delta_\ell}\bigl[\cos\delta_\ell\,j_\ell(kr) - \sin\delta_\ell\,n_\ell(kr)\bigr] \qquad (r > R), \qquad (6.138)
> $$
>
> normalized so that $A_\ell = j_\ell$ when $V = 0$, with $\langle\mathbf x|\psi^{(+)}\rangle = (2\pi)^{-3/2}\sum i^\ell(2\ell+1)A_\ell(r)P_\ell(\cos\theta)$ (6.134). If $\beta_\ell = (r\,dA_\ell/dr)/A_\ell$ at $r = R$ is the logarithmic derivative of the interior solution, which is obtained by integrating
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2u_\ell}{dr^2} + \Bigl(k^2 - \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}V - \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}\Bigr)u_\ell = 0 , \qquad u_\ell = rA_\ell, \qquad u_\ell(0) = 0 \qquad (6.141)\text{–}(6.143)
> $$
>
> from $r = 0$ to $R$ (numerically if necessary), then
>
> $$
> \tan\delta_\ell = \frac{kR\,j'_\ell(kR) - \beta_\ell\,j_\ell(kR)}{kR\,n'_\ell(kR) - \beta_\ell\,n_\ell(kR)} . \qquad (6.140)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.4.4, eqs. (6.132)–(6.144) · Griffiths §10.2.2 · Likharev §3.8, eq. (3.232)*

^thm-c10-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Outside the range, the regular solution is no longer required, so $A_\ell = c^{(1)}_\ell h^{(1)}_\ell + c^{(2)}_\ell h^{(2)}_\ell$ (6.135). Since $h^{(1)}_\ell \to e^{i(kr - \ell\pi/2)}/ikr$ and $h^{(2)}_\ell \to -e^{-i(kr - \ell\pi/2)}/ikr$ (6.133), comparison with (6.117) written with $1 + 2ikf_\ell = e^{2i\delta_\ell}$ (6.136) gives $c^{(1)}_\ell = \frac12e^{2i\delta_\ell}$, $c^{(2)}_\ell = \frac12$ (6.137). Then
>
> $$
> A_\ell = \tfrac12\bigl[(e^{2i\delta_\ell} + 1)j_\ell + i(e^{2i\delta_\ell} - 1)n_\ell\bigr] = e^{i\delta_\ell}\bigl[\cos\delta_\ell\,j_\ell - \sin\delta_\ell\,n_\ell\bigr] .
> $$
>
> The interior radial equation is [[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-2|Theorem §C6.1.2]]'s with $E > 0$, and $u(0) = 0$ is its boundary condition at the origin ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^rem-c6-1-2|Remark: A second reason for u(0) = 0, from probability conservation]]). $\psi$ and its derivative are continuous at $R$, hence so is $\beta_\ell$ (6.144). The phase $e^{i\delta}$ cancels from $\beta_\ell = kR\,[j'_\ell\cos\delta - n'_\ell\sin\delta]/[j_\ell\cos\delta - n_\ell\sin\delta]$ (6.139), and solving for $\tan\delta_\ell$ gives (6.140).

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.3.5: Born Phase Shifts and the Threshold Law
> 1. To first order in $V$,
>
> $$
> \delta_\ell \simeq -\frac{2mk}{\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty j_\ell(kr)^2\,V(r)\,r^2\,dr ,
> $$
>
> so a weak repulsive potential gives negative and a weak attractive one positive phase shifts.
> 2. For a potential of finite range $R$ and $kR \ll 1$, $\delta_\ell \propto k^{2\ell+1}$ (the **threshold law**): only $\ell = 0$ survives at low energy, and the scattering is isotropic.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.6, eqs. (6.185)–(6.187); Problems 6.5, 6.9 (the first-order form is derived here from (6.72) and (6.104))*

^thm-c10-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Expand both plane waves in the first Born amplitude (6.72) by Rayleigh's formula, $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} = \sum(2\ell+1)i^\ell j_\ell P_\ell(\hat{\mathbf k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})$ and $e^{-i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf x} = \sum(2\ell'+1)(-i)^{\ell'}j_{\ell'}P_{\ell'}(\hat{\mathbf k}'\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})$. The integral over directions of $\hat{\mathbf r}$, $\int d\Omega_r\,P_\ell(\hat{\mathbf k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})P_{\ell'}(\hat{\mathbf k}'\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}) = \frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\delta_{\ell\ell'}P_\ell(\hat{\mathbf k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf k}')$, follows from the addition theorem and orthonormality of the $Y^m_\ell$. With $i^\ell(-i)^\ell = 1$,
>
> $$
> f^{(1)}(\theta) = -\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\sum_\ell(2\ell + 1)\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)\int_0^\infty j_\ell(kr)^2\,V\,r^2\,dr .
> $$
>
> Comparing with (6.115), $f_\ell \simeq -\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\int j_\ell^2Vr^2\,dr$, and for small phase shifts $f_\ell = e^{i\delta}\sin\delta/k \simeq \delta_\ell/k$ (6.130). Sakurai's exact version, from the radial Lippmann–Schwinger equation of his Problem 6.9, is $e^{i\delta_\ell}\sin\delta_\ell/k = -\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\int j_\ell(kr)V(r)A_\ell(r)r^2\,dr$ (6.186), which reduces to item 1 when $A_\ell \simeq j_\ell$.
>
> *2.* Inside the range $kr \le kR \ll 1$, so $j_\ell(kr) \simeq (kr)^\ell/(2\ell+1)!!$ ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]]), and the integral is $\propto k^{2\ell}$. Hence $\delta_\ell/k \propto k^{2\ell}$. Sakurai argues the same from (6.186) whenever $A_\ell$ is close to $j_\ell$, that is, unless a bound state or resonance sits near $E = 0$.

> [!remark] Remark: Which partial waves matter
> The effective potential of the $\ell$-th wave is $V + \hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)/2mr^2$ (6.185). A classical particle with angular momentum $\ell\hbar$ has impact parameter $b = \ell/k$ and never reaches $r < b$. So only $\ell \lesssim kR$ feel a potential of range $R$: at low energy, $kR \ll 1$, only the $s$-wave ($\ell = 0$); at high energy about $kR$ partial waves, and then the eikonal of [[§C10.2 The Eikonal Approximation and the Optical Theorem|§C10.2]] replaces the sum. Griffiths (footnote 15): partial waves are the tool at low energy, the Born approximation at high energy.

## The hard sphere

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.3.6: Hard-Sphere Scattering
> For the impenetrable sphere, $V = \infty$ for $r < R$ and $V = 0$ for $r > R$ (the hard sphere of [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^mod-b4-2-3|CM Model §B4.2.3]]), the exact phase shifts are
>
> $$
> \tan\delta_\ell = \frac{j_\ell(kR)}{n_\ell(kR)}, \qquad \delta_0 = -kR . \qquad (6.148)\text{–}(6.149)
> $$
>
> 1. *Low energy*, $kR \ll 1$: $\tan\delta_\ell \simeq -(kR)^{2\ell+1}\big/\bigl\{(2\ell + 1)[(2\ell - 1)!!]^2\bigr\}$ (6.152), only the $s$-wave counts, the scattering is isotropic, and
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \simeq R^2, \qquad \sigma_{\text{tot}} \to 4\pi R^2 , \qquad (6.153)\text{–}(6.154)
> $$
>
> four times the geometric cross-section: the whole surface area of the sphere (Griffiths).
> 2. *High energy*, $kR \gg 1$: about $kR$ partial waves contribute, $\delta_\ell \simeq -(kR - \ell\pi/2)$, and
>
> $$
> \sigma_{\text{tot}} \to 2\pi R^2 = \underbrace{\pi R^2}_{\text{reflection}} + \underbrace{\pi R^2}_{\text{shadow}} , \qquad (6.176),\ (6.182)
> $$
>
> where the shadow amplitude $f_{\text{shad}} = \frac{i}{2k}\sum_{\ell \le kR}(2\ell+1)P_\ell(\cos\theta) \simeq iRJ_1(kR\theta)/\theta$ is the Fraunhofer diffraction peak of a disk of radius $R$ (6.179), and the optical theorem holds with $\frac{4\pi}{k}\operatorname{Im}f_{\text{shad}}(0) = 2\pi R^2$ (6.184).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.4.5, eqs. (6.145)–(6.154); §6.5.1, eqs. (6.173)–(6.184) · Griffiths Example 10.3, eqs. (10.30)–(10.36), Problems 10.3, 10.6 · Likharev §3.8, eqs. (3.226)–(3.231), Fig. 3.25*

^thm-c10-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The wave function vanishes at $r = R$, so $A_\ell(R) = 0$ (6.146) and (6.138) gives $j_\ell\cos\delta_\ell - n_\ell\sin\delta_\ell = 0$ at $kR$, which is (6.148). For $\ell = 0$, $\tan\delta_0 = (\sin kR/kR)/(-\cos kR/kR) = -\tan kR$; then $A_0 \propto \sin(kr + \delta_0)/kr = \sin k(r - R)/kr$ (6.150): the free wave $\sin kr$ pushed out by $R$ (Sakurai Fig. 6.9). Griffiths finds the same through $a_\ell = ij_\ell(ka)/kh^{(1)}_\ell(ka)$ (10.33), using the orthogonality of the $P_\ell$ to separate the $\ell$'s.
>
> *1.* With $j_\ell \simeq x^\ell/(2\ell+1)!!$ and $n_\ell \simeq -(2\ell-1)!!/x^{\ell+1}$ (6.151), $\tan\delta_\ell = j_\ell/n_\ell$ gives (6.152), so $\delta_\ell = O((kR)^{2\ell+1})$. Then $\sigma \simeq \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sin^2\delta_0 = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sin^2kR \to 4\pi R^2$, and $d\sigma/d\Omega = \sin^2\delta_0/k^2 \to R^2$.
>
> *2.* For $\ell \ll kR$, the large-argument forms $j_\ell \simeq \sin(x - \ell\pi/2)/x$ and $n_\ell \simeq -\cos(x - \ell\pi/2)/x$ (6.175) give $\tan\delta_\ell = -\tan(kR - \ell\pi/2)$, so $\delta_\ell \simeq -(kR - \ell\pi/2)$ and $\sin^2\delta_\ell = \sin^2(kR - \ell\pi/2)$ (6.174). Consecutive $\ell$ have $\sin^2\delta_\ell + \sin^2\delta_{\ell+1} = 1$, so in $\sum_{\ell\le kR}(2\ell+1)\sin^2\delta_\ell$ one may put $\sin^2 \to \tfrac12$, and $\sum_{\ell \le kR}(2\ell+1) \simeq (kR)^2$. Hence $\sigma \simeq \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(kR)^2\cdot\frac12 = 2\pi R^2$ (6.176).
>
> To see where it goes, split (6.126), cut off at $\ell_{\max} = kR$ (6.177):
>
> $$
> f = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2ik}\sum_{\ell=0}^{kR}(2\ell+1)e^{2i\delta_\ell}P_\ell}_{f_{\text{refl}}} + \underbrace{\frac{i}{2k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{kR}(2\ell+1)P_\ell}_{f_{\text{shad}}} .
> $$
>
> In $\int|f_{\text{refl}}|^2d\Omega$ orthogonality removes the cross terms: $\frac{2\pi}{4k^2}\sum(2\ell+1)^2\frac{2}{2\ell+1} = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum(2\ell+1) = \pi R^2$ (6.178). $f_{\text{shad}}$ is purely imaginary and peaked forward, where all $P_\ell(1) = 1$ add coherently. With $P_\ell(\cos\theta) \simeq J_0(\ell\theta)$ and $\sum_\ell \to k\int db$, $\ell = kb$, it becomes $ik\int_0^Rb\,db\,J_0(kb\theta) = iRJ_1(kR\theta)/\theta$ (6.179), and $\int|f_{\text{shad}}|^2d\Omega \simeq 2\pi R^2\int_0^\infty J_1(\xi)^2d\xi/\xi = \pi R^2$ (6.180), since that integral is $\tfrac12$. The interference term averages to zero, because the phase of $f_{\text{refl}}$ alternates ($2\delta_{\ell+1} = 2\delta_\ell + \pi$) while $f_{\text{shad}}$ is imaginary (6.181). Finally $\frac{4\pi}{k}\operatorname{Im}f_{\text{shad}}(0) = \frac{4\pi}{k}\cdot\frac{1}{2k}(kR)^2 = 2\pi R^2$ (6.183)–(6.184): the shadow alone carries the optical theorem.

> [!remark] Remark: The quantum and the classical hard sphere
> Classically the hard sphere scatters isotropically with $\sigma = \pi R^2$ ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-4|CM Theorem §B4.2.4]]). Quantum mechanically the low-energy scattering is also isotropic, but four times larger. Waves much longer than the sphere feel their way around it, and the cross-section is its surface area (Griffiths). At short wavelengths one might expect the classical limit, but the cross-section tends to $2\pi R^2$. Half of it is the classical reflection, $\pi R^2$, isotropic like the classical result. The other half is diffraction into a cone of angle $\sim 1/kR$: the shadow, the wave counterpart of the region behind the sphere where the particle cannot be ([[§C10.2 The Eikonal Approximation and the Optical Theorem#^rem-c10-2-4|Remark: Twice the geometric cross-section]]). An opaque disk diffracts like a hole by Babinet's principle ([[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-4|WO Theorem §B11.1.4]]), and $f_{\text{shad}}$ is the Airy amplitude $J_1(kR\theta)/\theta$ ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-3|WO Theorem §B11.2.3]]). A detector that cannot resolve angles $\lesssim 1/kR$ counts the shadow as unscattered and measures $\pi R^2$: that is how the classical value is recovered. Likharev calls the $2\pi R^2$ the extinction paradox, first found for light. The approach to $2$ is slow (figure). For a dilute gas of hard-sphere molecules ([[§B5.2★ Mean Free Path and Transport Coefficients#^mod-b5-2-1|TH Model §B5.2.1]]) the long-wavelength value matters only when the thermal wavelength ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^def-b8-1-1|TH Def. §B8.1.1]]) exceeds the molecular diameter.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 10.3 · Sakurai §6.5.1 · Likharev §3.8*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.3.7: Phase Shifts in the Eikonal Limit
> When many partial waves contribute ($kR \gg 1$) and $V$ satisfies the conditions of the eikonal approximation, the phase shifts are the eikonal phases at the classical impact parameter,
>
> $$
> \delta_\ell \simeq \Delta(b)\big|_{b = \ell/k}, \qquad \Delta(b) = -\frac{m}{2k\hbar^2}\int_{-\infty}^\infty V\bigl(\sqrt{b^2 + z^2}\bigr)\,dz , \qquad (6.170)
> $$
>
> and the partial-wave sum (6.126) becomes the eikonal amplitude of [[§C10.2 The Eikonal Approximation and the Optical Theorem#^thm-c10-2-2|Theorem §C10.2.2]]. For a weak potential, the Born phase shift of Theorem §C10.3.5 reduces to $\Delta(\ell/k)$ when $\ell \gg 1$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.5.1, eqs. (6.169)–(6.172) (the Born check is added here)*

^thm-c10-3-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Treat $\ell$ as continuous with $\ell = kb$. Then $\sum_\ell \to k\int db$, $2\ell + 1 \to 2kb$, and for large $\ell$ and small $\theta$, $P_\ell(\cos\theta) \simeq J_0(\ell\theta) = J_0(kb\theta)$. Phase shifts vanish beyond $\ell_{\max} = kR$ (6.171). With $\delta_\ell \to \Delta(b)$,
>
> $$
> f = \frac{1}{2ik}\sum_\ell(2\ell + 1)\bigl(e^{2i\delta_\ell} - 1\bigr)P_\ell(\cos\theta) \to \frac{1}{2ik}\,k\int db\,2kb\,\bigl(e^{2i\Delta(b)} - 1\bigr)J_0(kb\theta) = -ik\int_0^\infty b\,db\,J_0(kb\theta)\bigl[e^{2i\Delta(b)} - 1\bigr] , \qquad (6.172)
> $$
>
> which is (6.167); comparing the two forms identifies $\delta_\ell$ with $\Delta(\ell/k)$.
>
> *Check from the Born phase shift.* For $\ell \gg 1$ the function $u = kr\,j_\ell(kr)$ vanishes inside the classical turning point $r = b = \ell/k$ and outside it oscillates with the WKB amplitude $[1 - b^2/r^2]^{-1/4}$ ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-1|QM Theorem §B9.3.1]]); averaging the square of the oscillation to $\tfrac12$, $j_\ell^2 \simeq 1/\bigl(2k^2r\sqrt{r^2 - b^2}\bigr)$ for $r > b$. Then Theorem §C10.3.5 gives
>
> $$
> \delta_\ell \simeq -\frac{2mk}{\hbar^2}\int_b^\infty\frac{V\,r\,dr}{2k^2\sqrt{r^2 - b^2}} = -\frac{m}{\hbar^2k}\int_b^\infty\frac{V\,r\,dr}{\sqrt{r^2 - b^2}} = \Delta(b) ,
> $$
>
> since $\int_{-\infty}^\infty V\,dz = 2\int_b^\infty V\,r\,dr/\sqrt{r^2 - b^2}$.

## Low-energy scattering

> [!definition] Definition §C10.3.1: Scattering Length; Effective Range
> For a finite-range potential the $s$-wave phase shift behaves as $\delta_0 \simeq -ka$ (modulo $\pi$) as $k \to 0$. The **scattering length** $a$ is defined by
>
> $$
> \lim_{k\to0}k\cot\delta_0 = -\frac1a , \qquad (6.197)
> $$
>
> and the **effective range** $r_0$ by the next term of the expansion, $k\cot\delta_0 = -\frac1a + \frac12r_0k^2 + \cdots$ (6.206). Then $f \to -a$ and $\sigma_{\text{tot}} \to 4\pi a^2$ (6.198). Geometrically, the zero-energy exterior solution $u = rA_0$ obeys $u'' = 0$, so $u \propto r - a$ (6.193)–(6.194): $a$ is the intercept on the $r$-axis of the straight line that continues the outside wave function inward.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.6.2, eqs. (6.193)–(6.198), (6.206)*

^def-c10-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.3.8: Low-Energy $s$-Wave Scattering by a Square Well
> Let $V = V_0$ for $r < R$ and $0$ outside (6.188), and $\lambda = 2mV_0R^2/\hbar^2$.
> 1. For $E > V_0$ put $k'^2 = k^2 - 2mV_0/\hbar^2$; the $s$-wave phase shift is given by
>
> $$
> \tan(kR + \delta_0) = \frac{k}{k'}\tan k'R , \qquad (6.189)\text{–}(6.191)
> $$
>
> and for $E < V_0$, $k' \to i\kappa'$ with $\hbar^2\kappa'^2/2m = V_0 - E$ and $\tan \to \tanh$ (6.190′)–(6.191′). An attractive well ($V_0 < 0$) pulls the wave in, $\delta_0 > 0$; a barrier pushes it out, $\delta_0 < 0$ (Sakurai Fig. 6.11).
> 2. With $g = \sqrt{|\lambda|}$, the scattering length is
>
> $$
> a = R\Bigl(1 - \frac{\tanh g}{g}\Bigr) \quad(\lambda = g^2 > 0), \qquad a = R\Bigl(1 - \frac{\tan g}{g}\Bigr) \quad(\lambda = -g^2 < 0) .
> $$
>
> For a barrier, $0 < a < R$, with $a \to R$ (the hard sphere) as $\lambda \to \infty$. For a well, $|a| \to \infty$ at $g = \pi/2, 3\pi/2, \dots$, the depths at which a new $s$ bound state appears, and $a = 0$ where $\tan g = g$ ($g = 4.493, 7.725, \dots$).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.6.1–6.6.2, eqs. (6.188)–(6.197), Figs. 6.11–6.12 (item 2 derived here)*

^thm-c10-3-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Inside, $u = rA_0 \propto \sin k'r$, regular at the origin (6.190); outside, $u \propto \sin(kr + \delta_0)$ (6.189). Continuity of $u'/u$ at $R$: $k'\cot k'R = k\cot(kR + \delta_0)$, which is (6.189)–(6.191). Below the top of a barrier, $\sin k'r \to \sinh\kappa'r$ and $k'\cot k'R \to \kappa'\coth\kappa'R$.
>
> *2.* As $k \to 0$, $k' \to k'_0$ with $k'_0R = \sqrt{-\lambda} = g$ for a well, and $kR + \delta_0 \simeq (k/k'_0)\tan k'_0R$, so $\delta_0 \simeq -k\bigl[R - \tan(k'_0R)/k'_0\bigr]$. By Definition §C10.3.1, $a = R - R\tan g/g$. For a barrier $\tan g/g \to \tanh g/g$, which lies between $0$ and $1$. $\tan g$ diverges at $g = (n + \frac12)\pi$. There the zero-energy interior solution reaches $r = R$ with zero slope and continues outside as a constant ($u'' = 0$, $u' = 0$), which is the bound state at threshold: the condition $\beta > \pi/2$ of [[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^ex-c6-1-1|Example §C6.1.1]] and [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^rem-b5-3-4|QM ★ Remark: A shallow spherical well need not bind]].

*Uses:* [[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^thm-c10-3-4|Theorem §C10.3.4]], [[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^def-c10-3-1|Def. §C10.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Zero-energy resonances, and a caution on "half a cycle"
> Deepen a well from zero. While less than a quarter wavelength fits inside, $a < 0$ and grows in size. At $g = \pi/2$ a quarter wavelength fits, $a = \pm\infty$, and $\sigma \to 4\pi/k^2$: the unitarity limit of the $s$-wave is reached at zero energy. Sakurai describes this as $\delta_0 = \pi/2$ at low energy; it is a **zero-energy resonance**. Just beyond, $a$ is large and positive and the well holds a loosely bound state (Theorem §C10.3.9). [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^ex-c10-1-2|Example §C10.1.2]] shows this divergence against the Born approximation, which misses it entirely.
>
> Sakurai continues: when half a cycle fits inside, $\delta_0 = \pi$ and the $s$-wave cross-section vanishes. In the low-energy limit this needs a correction. Half a cycle, $g = \pi$, gives $a = R(1 - \tan\pi/\pi) = R$, the hard-sphere value, not zero. The $s$-wave cross-section vanishes ($\delta_0 \to 0 \bmod \pi$ as $k \to 0$) only when the interior log-derivative matches the free exterior one, $k'_0\cot k'_0R = 1/R$, i.e. $\tan g = g$ at $g = 4.493$, about $1.43$ half cycles. The qualitative point stands: a strongly attractive well can be transparent to slow particles.

> [!theorem] Theorem §C10.3.9: Scattering Length and a Shallow Bound State
> If an attractive potential of range $R$ has a large positive scattering length, $a \gg R$, it has an $s$-wave bound state with
>
> $$
> \kappa \simeq \frac1a, \qquad E_{\text{BE}} = \frac{\hbar^2\kappa^2}{2m} \simeq \frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2} , \qquad (6.201)\text{–}(6.202)
> $$
>
> so a scattering experiment near zero energy measures the binding energy without knowledge of the potential (Wigner).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.6.2, eqs. (6.199)–(6.202), Fig. 6.12*

^thm-c10-3-9

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $a \gg R$ the zero-energy exterior solution $u \propto r - a$ is nearly flat for $r \sim R$ (Sakurai Fig. 6.12c), and so is the bound-state tail $e^{-\kappa r}$ for $\kappa \simeq 0$. Inside, both the $E = 0^+$ scattering solution and the $E = 0^-$ bound state are $\sin k'r$ with $\hbar^2k'^2/2m = E - V_0 \simeq |V_0|$ (6.199), the same function. So their logarithmic derivatives at $R$ agree:
>
> $$
> \frac{-\kappa e^{-\kappa r}}{e^{-\kappa r}}\Big|_R = -\kappa = \frac{1}{R - a} \simeq -\frac1a \qquad (R \ll a) . \qquad (6.200)\text{–}(6.201)
> $$
>
> A bound state requires $\kappa > 0$, hence $a > 0$. A large *negative* $a$ (Fig. 6.12b) belongs to a well just too shallow to bind.

> [!example] Example §C10.3.1: A Weak Well at Low Energy
> For $V = V_0$ ($r < R$), $0$ outside, with $|V_0| \ll E$ and $kR \ll 1$, show that the cross-section is isotropic with $\sigma_{\text{tot}} = \frac{16\pi}{9}\frac{m^2V_0^2R^6}{\hbar^4}$. Then show that at slightly higher energy $d\sigma/d\Omega = A + B\cos\theta$, and find $B/A$.

> *Phase shifts.* By Theorem §C10.3.5 with $j_0 \simeq 1$ and $j_1 \simeq kr/3$ inside the well,
>
> $$
> \delta_0 \simeq -\frac{2mkV_0}{\hbar^2}\int_0^Rr^2\,dr = -\frac{2mV_0R^3}{3\hbar^2}\,k, \qquad \delta_1 \simeq -\frac{2mkV_0}{\hbar^2}\int_0^R\frac{k^2r^4}{9}\,dr = -\frac{2mV_0R^5}{45\hbar^2}\,k^3 .
> $$

> *Isotropic limit.* Only $\delta_0$ matters, $d\sigma/d\Omega = \sin^2\delta_0/k^2 \simeq (2mV_0R^3/3\hbar^2)^2$, and $\sigma_{\text{tot}} = 4\pi(2mV_0R^3/3\hbar^2)^2 = \frac{16\pi}{9}\frac{m^2V_0^2R^6}{\hbar^4}$: the low-energy Born result of [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^ex-c10-1-2|Example §C10.1.2]], as it must be for a weak potential.

> *First anisotropy.* Keeping $\ell = 1$, $f \simeq (\delta_0 + 3\delta_1\cos\theta)/k$ to first order in the small phase shifts, so
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \simeq \frac{\delta_0^2 + 6\delta_0\delta_1\cos\theta}{k^2}, \qquad \frac BA = \frac{6\delta_1}{\delta_0} = \frac{2}{5}(kR)^2 > 0 ,

> for either sign of $V_0$, since $\delta_0$ and $\delta_1$ have the same sign. The interference of the $s$- and $p$-waves tilts the distribution forward, growing as $E$. Check against the Born amplitude (6.76): for small $qR$, $(\sin x/x - \cos x)/x^2 = \frac13(1 - x^2/10 + \cdots)$, so $|f|^2 \propto 1 - (qR)^2/5 = 1 - \frac25(kR)^2(1 - \cos\theta)$, the same ratio.

> [!example] Example §C10.3.2: The Ramsauer–Townsend Effect in Three Dimensions
> Slow electrons ($E \approx 0.1$ eV) cross argon, krypton and xenon atoms almost without scattering. Model the atom as an attractive square well of radius $R = 2$ Å (Sakurai's numbers, giving $kR = 0.324$ at $0.1$ eV). How deep must it be for $\delta_0 = \pi$ at $0.1$ eV, and what is then left of the cross-section?
>
> For the electron, $\hbar^2/2m_eR^2 = 0.952$ eV. Theorem §C10.3.8 with $\delta_0 = \pi$ requires $\tan kR = (k/k')\tan k'R$, solved numerically by $k'R = 4.501$. So $|V_0| = 0.952\ \mathrm{eV}\times\bigl[(4.501)^2 - (0.324)^2\bigr] = 19.2$ eV. The well holds one $s$ and one $p$ bound state. The $s$-wave is then fully transparent at $0.1$ eV, $\sigma_0 = 0$, and the scattering length is small, $a = 0.017R$. The remaining cross-section comes from higher $\ell$. Computing $\delta_1$, $\delta_2$ with Theorem §C10.3.4 gives
>
> | $E$ (eV) | $0.01$ | $0.1$ | $1$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $\sigma_{\text{tot}}/\pi R^2$ | $0.001$ | $0.019$ | $0.78$ |
>
> At $0.1$ eV, $\sigma_{\text{tot}}$ is $2\%$ of the geometric cross-section, and $0.05\%$ of the $s$-wave unitarity limit $4\pi/k^2 = 38\pi R^2$; by $1$ eV it has grown forty-fold. The $p$- and $d$-waves are not negligible here. For a square well $a = 0$ means $\tan g = g$, which is also $j_1(g) = 0$, the threshold condition for an $\ell = 2$ bound state, so this well has a $d$ level just at zero energy (computed here). Sakurai's $s$-wave argument assumes $\ell \ne 0$ waves are unimportant; for real atoms that assumption and the square well are both rough. One dimension shows the same mechanism with transmission resonances ([[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^rem-b3-4-2|QM ★ Remark: The Ramsauer–Townsend effect]]), which noted that a quantitative account needs partial waves in three dimensions: this is that account.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.6.1, eqs. (6.188)–(6.192) (numbers computed here) · Griffiths §2.6, footnote 51 (via QM §B3.4)*

> [!example] Example §C10.3.3: The Deuteron from Neutron–Proton Scattering
> The triplet ($^3S_1$) neutron–proton scattering length is $a_t = 5.4\times10^{-13}$ cm $= 5.4$ fm (6.204), and the deuteron's binding energy is $2.22$ MeV (6.203). How well does Theorem §C10.3.9 predict the binding, and what effective range does the measured binding imply?
>
> *Zero-range estimate.* With the reduced mass $\mu = m_N/2$, $m_Nc^2 = 938$ MeV and $\hbar/m_Nc = 2.1\times10^{-14}$ cm,
>
> $$
> E_{\text{BE}} \simeq \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu a_t^2} = m_Nc^2\Bigl(\frac{\hbar}{m_Nca_t}\Bigr)^2 = 938\ \mathrm{MeV}\Bigl(\frac{0.21}{5.4}\Bigr)^2 = 1.4\ \mathrm{MeV} , \qquad (6.205)
> $$
>
> against $2.22$ MeV measured. The interior functions are not identical, and $a_t \gg R$ holds only roughly for the deuteron (Sakurai).
>
> *Effective range.* Keep the next term of Definition §C10.3.1. The bound state is the pole of $f_0 = 1/(k\cot\delta_0 - ik)$ at $k = i\kappa$ ([[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^rem-c10-3-6|★ Remark: Bound states as poles of the S matrix; Levinson's theorem]]), so $-1/a_t - \frac12r_0\kappa^2 = -\kappa$, i.e. $r_0 = 2(\kappa - 1/a_t)/\kappa^2$. The measured binding gives $\kappa = \sqrt{m_NE_{\text{BE}}}/\hbar = \sqrt{938\times2.22}/197.3\ \mathrm{fm}^{-1} = 0.231\ \mathrm{fm}^{-1}$, against $1/a_t = 0.185\ \mathrm{fm}^{-1}$. This requires $r_0 \approx 1.7$ fm, a third of $a_t$. The force has a range comparable to the deuteron's size, which is why the zero-range formula is off by $40\%$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.6.2, eqs. (6.203)–(6.206) (effective range computed here)*

## Beyond the PHY 512 schedule

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Bound states as poles of the S matrix; Levinson's theorem
> Far out, the $s$-wave is $S_0(k)e^{ikr}/r - e^{-ikr}/r$ (6.207), and a bound state is $e^{-\kappa r}/r$ (6.208): an outgoing wave at $k = i\kappa$ *without* an incoming one. The ratio of the coefficients, $S_0$, must therefore be infinite: a bound state is a pole of $S_\ell(k)$ on the positive imaginary $k$-axis (Sakurai Fig. 6.13). The simplest function with a pole at $i\kappa$, $|S_0| = 1$ for real $k$, and $S_0(0) = 1$ is
>
> $$
> S_0(k) = \frac{-k - i\kappa}{k - i\kappa}, \qquad f_0 = \frac{S_0 - 1}{2ik} = \frac{1}{-\kappa - ik} , \qquad (6.212)\text{–}(6.213)
> $$
>
> and comparison with $f_0 = 1/(k\cot\delta_0 - ik)$ gives $k\cot\delta_0 = -\kappa$, i.e. $a = 1/\kappa$ (6.215). This recovers Theorem §C10.3.9 from analyticity and unitarity alone. (Sakurai's editor's note: (6.212) is chosen for simplicity; for reasonable potentials, unlike the hard sphere, $\delta_0 \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$.) The one-dimensional version is [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^rem-b3-3-4|QM ★ Remark: The bound state as a pole of the transmission amplitude]], and [[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^ex-c10-1-3|Example §C10.1.3]] found the pole from the Lippmann–Schwinger equation.
>
> *Levinson's theorem.* The $s$-wave radial problem, $u(0) = 0$ on $0 < r < \infty$, is exactly the half-line problem of [[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^rem-b3-4-7|QM ★ Remark: Phase shifts, Levinson's theorem and resonances]]. So Zwiebach's result applies as it stands: $\delta_0(0) - \delta_0(\infty) = N_b\pi$, with $N_b$ the number of $s$-wave bound states. The phase shift at threshold counts them, which is why it is defined modulo $\pi$ only until one fixes $\delta_0(\infty) = 0$. (No source here proves the version for $\ell \ge 1$.)
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.6.3, eqs. (6.207)–(6.215), Fig. 6.13 · Zwiebach via QM §B3.4*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Resonances and the Breit–Wigner formula
> For $\ell \ne 0$ an attractive well plus the centrifugal barrier makes a pocket (Sakurai Fig. 6.14). With an infinitely high barrier it would hold true bound states at $E > 0$; through a finite one the particle tunnels out, giving a **quasi-bound state** of finite lifetime. As $E$ passes its energy $E_r$, $\delta_\ell$ rises rapidly through $\pi/2$, from below, and $\sigma_\ell$ touches the unitarity bound (6.131). Expanding $\cot\delta_\ell \simeq -\frac{2}{\Gamma}(E - E_r)$ near $E_r$ (6.221), (6.223),
>
> $$
> f_\ell = \frac{1}{k\cot\delta_\ell - ik} = -\frac{\Gamma/2}{k\bigl[(E - E_r) + i\Gamma/2\bigr]}, \qquad \sigma_\ell = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\,\frac{(2\ell + 1)(\Gamma/2)^2}{(E - E_r)^2 + \Gamma^2/4} , \qquad (6.222),\ (6.224)
> $$
>
> the **Breit–Wigner** one-level formula: a Lorentzian of full width $\Gamma$ at half maximum, if $1/k^2$ varies little across it. That is the line shape of a driven oscillator near resonance ([[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-2|WO Theorem §B1.4.2]]). The amplitude has a pole at $E = E_r - i\Gamma/2$, just below the real axis. The rapid rise of $\delta_\ell$ means a delay $2\hbar\,d\delta_\ell/dE = 4\hbar/\Gamma$ at the peak: the particle is held for about the lifetime $\hbar/\Gamma$.
> - *A computed case.* Sakurai's Fig. 6.15 shows $\ell = 3$ for an attractive well with $2m|V_0|R^2/\hbar^2 = 5.5^2$, with a resonance at $kR = 1.41$. Integrating with Theorem §C10.3.4 confirms $\delta_3 = \pi/2$ at $kR = 1.411$ and gives $\Gamma = 0.090\,\hbar^2/2mR^2$, a full width of $0.032$ in $kR$ (computed here).
> - *A $\delta$-shell.* For $(2m/\hbar^2)V = \gamma\delta(r - R)$ (Sakurai Problem 6.10) the shell itself traps $s$-waves. For $\gamma \to \infty$ the inside has bound states at $kR = n\pi$ and the outside sees a hard sphere, $\delta_0 = -kR$ (6.217)–(6.218). For large finite $\gamma$, sharp resonances near $kR = n\pi$ ride on the smooth hard-sphere background: Sakurai's model of neutron–nucleus scattering. Griffiths' $s$-wave phase shift for the shell, $\delta_0 = -\cot^{-1}\bigl[\cot ka + ka/\beta\sin^2ka\bigr]$ with $\beta = 2ma\alpha/\hbar^2$ (Problem 10.7), is the starting point.
> - *Spin.* With spins $s_1$, $s_2$ and total $j$ in the partial wave, the bound becomes $\frac{4\pi}{k^2}g$, $g = (2j+1)/(2s_1+1)(2s_2+1)$ (6.225). The $\pi^+p$ elastic peak at a centre-of-mass momentum of about $0.23$ GeV/$c$ reaches the $j = \frac32$ limit (Sakurai Fig. 6.16): the $\Delta(1232)$ resonance.
>
> One dimension shows the same physics in the double barrier of [[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^ex-b3-4-3|QM ★ Example §B3.4.3]] and in [[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension#^rem-b3-4-7|QM ★ Remark: Phase shifts, Levinson's theorem and resonances]], whose Breit–Wigner form this is in three dimensions.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.7, eqs. (6.216)–(6.225), Figs. 6.14–6.16; Problems 6.10, 6.13 · Griffiths Problem 10.7*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Symmetry in scattering: parity, time reversal and detailed balance
> If a unitary $U$ leaves $H_0$ and $V$ invariant, it leaves every term of the Born series invariant, so $UTU^\dagger = T$ and $\langle\tilde{\mathbf k}'|T|\tilde{\mathbf k}\rangle = \langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle$ with $|\tilde{\mathbf k}\rangle = U|\mathbf k\rangle$ (6.229)–(6.232). Rotation invariance makes $T$ depend only on the angle between $\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf k'$, the fact behind partial waves. Parity gives $\langle-\mathbf k'|T|-\mathbf k\rangle = \langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle$ (6.234). Time reversal is antiunitary ([[§C8.3★ Time Reversal#^thm-c8-3-3|Theorem §C8.3.3]]); it turns $(E - H_0 + i\varepsilon)^{-1}$ into $(E - H_0 - i\varepsilon)^{-1}$, so $\Theta T\Theta^{-1} = T^\dagger$ (6.235)–(6.236), and it exchanges initial and final states: $\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle = \langle-\mathbf k|T|-\mathbf k'\rangle$ (6.241). Combined with parity ([[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^pr-c8-2-1|Principle §C8.2.1]]), $f(\mathbf k, \mathbf k') = f(\mathbf k', \mathbf k)$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(\mathbf k \to \mathbf k') = \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(\mathbf k' \to \mathbf k) , \qquad (6.243)\text{–}(6.244)
> $$
>
> the principle of **detailed balance**. With spin, the matrix elements pick up phases $i^{-2m_s + 2m'_s}$ (6.245), and detailed balance holds for cross-sections averaged over initial and summed over final spin states (6.246).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.8, eqs. (6.229)–(6.246)*

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Identical particles
> For two identical particles in the centre-of-mass frame, the relative coordinate $\mathbf x = \mathbf x_1 - \mathbf x_2$ obeys the one-body equation with the reduced mass (Griffiths Problem 10.23). The symmetrization requirement ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^pr-b7-1-3|QM Principle §B7.1.3]]; its level-C form, from permutation operators, is [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate|§C12.1★]]) makes the spatial wave function even or odd under $\mathbf x \to -\mathbf x$, which sends $\theta \to \pi - \theta$. A detector at $\theta$ cannot tell whether it sees the projectile or the recoiling target, and the amplitudes add before squaring:
>
> $$
> e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} \pm e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} + \bigl[f(\theta) \pm f(\pi - \theta)\bigr]\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}, \qquad \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(\theta) \pm f(\pi - \theta)|^2 . \qquad (6.226)\text{–}(6.227)
> $$
>
> - *Spinless bosons* ($+$): $|f(\theta)|^2 + |f(\pi - \theta)|^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}[f(\theta)f^*(\pi - \theta)]$, enhanced at $90^\circ$ to twice the classical sum. Since $P_\ell(-\cos\theta) = (-1)^\ell P_\ell(\cos\theta)$, only even $\ell$ survive in $f(\theta) + f(\pi - \theta)$.
> - *Spin-½ fermions, unpolarized, spin-independent $V$:* singlet (weight $\frac14$, space-symmetric) and triplet (weight $\frac34$, space-antisymmetric) add incoherently,
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \tfrac14|f(\theta) + f(\pi - \theta)|^2 + \tfrac34|f(\theta) - f(\pi - \theta)|^2 = |f(\theta)|^2 + |f(\pi - \theta)|^2 - \operatorname{Re}\bigl[f(\theta)f^*(\pi - \theta)\bigr] , \qquad (6.228)
> $$
>
> with destructive interference at $90^\circ$, which has been observed (Sakurai). Triplet scattering alone vanishes at $90^\circ$ (Griffiths 10.23(e)).
>
> Classical mechanics adds the counts, $|f(\theta)|^2 + |f(\pi - \theta)|^2$ ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-6|CM Theorem §B4.2.6]], last case, and [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^cau-b4-2-1|CM Caution: Classical identical particles]]). The interference term is the quantum correction that the caution there anticipated. For Coulomb scattering of identical particles it is sensitive to the phase of $f$, which the Born amplitude gets wrong ([[§C10.1 The Lippmann–Schwinger Equation and the Born Approximation#^cau-c10-1-2|Caution: The Coulomb potential is not short-ranged]]; Griffiths footnote 20); the correct result (Mott's formula) is not derived in the sources.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §6.8, eqs. (6.226)–(6.228) · Griffiths Problem 10.23*
