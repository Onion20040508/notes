Two sets of facts about atoms were on the table around 1910. Atoms emit and absorb light only at sharp wavelengths, and for hydrogen these obey a simple empirical formula, the Rydberg formula, that nobody could explain. And α particles fired at a gold foil are occasionally thrown straight back, which forced Rutherford's conclusion that the positive charge and almost all the mass of an atom sit in a tiny nucleus. This section states both; the next combines them, with Planck's quantum, into Bohr's model.

## Line spectra

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.1: Emission and Absorption Line Spectra
> Light from a gas excited by an electric discharge, dispersed by a prism or grating, shows bright lines at particular wavelengths on a dark background: the **emission line spectrum**. White light passed through a cool gas shows dark lines at particular wavelengths on the continuous spectrum: the **absorption line spectrum** (the dark Fraunhofer lines of sunlight are an example). Each element has its own characteristic set of lines, and, as Kirchhoff and Bunsen found (1854–1861), an element's absorption lines lie at wavelengths where it also emits.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.4; Tipler §4-1*

^def-a2-1-1

> [!law] Law §A2.1.1: Rydberg Formula for Hydrogen
> The wavelengths of the spectral lines of atomic hydrogen are
>
> $$
> \frac1\lambda = R_H\Bigl(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\Bigr), \qquad n_f = 1, 2, 3, \dots, \quad n_i = n_f + 1,\ n_f + 2, \dots ,
> $$
>
> with the Rydberg constant for hydrogen $R_H = 1.0968\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Each $n_f$ gives a **series**: Lyman ($n_f = 1$, ultraviolet), Balmer ($n_f = 2$, visible and near ultraviolet), Paschen ($n_f = 3$, infrared), Brackett ($4$), Pfund ($5$), and so on. The case $n_f = 2$ is **Balmer's formula** (1885), which gives the four visible lines $656$, $486$, $434$ and $410\ \mathrm{nm}$ for $n_i = 3, \dots, 6$. As $n_i \to \infty$ the lines of a series crowd together toward the **series limit** $\lambda = n_f^2/R_H$.
>
> *Valid when:* atomic hydrogen, at the resolution of classical spectroscopy (to about one part in $10^4$). For other one-electron ions the same form holds with $Z^2$ and a slightly different constant ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-4|Theorem §A2.2.4]], [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-5|Theorem §A2.2.5]]); at higher resolution each line splits into close components, the fine structure ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-9|Theorem §A5.2.9]]). The X-ray lines of heavier atoms, from transitions between inner shells, follow the same pattern with $Z^2$ replaced by a screened $(Z - b)^2$: Moseley's law ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^law-a5-4-3|Law §A5.4.3]], [[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-4|Theorem §A5.4.4]]).
> *Source: UP3 §6.4, eqs. (6.31)–(6.32); Tipler §4-1, eqs. (4-1)–(4-2)*

^law-a2-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Which Rydberg constant?
> University Physics quotes $R_H = 1.09737\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$; that is the constant $R_\infty$ for an infinitely heavy nucleus. Hydrogen's own constant, the one that fits its lines, is smaller by $0.05\%$, $R_H = 1.09678\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$ (Tipler), because the proton recoils ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-5|Theorem §A2.2.5]]). To three or four figures the two are interchangeable; in precise work, and to see isotope shifts, they are not.

^cau-a2-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: An empirical formula waiting for a reason
> Balmer (1885) found his formula by inspection; Rydberg and Ritz generalised it, and new series turned up where it said they would (Paschen 1908, Lyman 1916, Brackett 1922, Pfund 1924). That integers should govern the wavelengths had no explanation until Bohr (1913, [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-4|Theorem §A2.2.4]]). Multiplying by $hc$ turns the formula into a statement about photon energies, $hc/\lambda = hcR_H\,(1/n_f^2 - 1/n_i^2)$ with $hcR_H = 13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$ ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]): every line is a *difference* of two terms of the form $13.6\ \mathrm{eV}/n^2$, which is the clue Bohr followed. In PHY 286 Experiment 3 the visible Balmer lines are measured with a grating spectrometer ([[§A7.3 Diffraction Gratings#^thm-a7-3-1|WO Theorem §A7.3.1]]), first calibrated on krypton lines of known wavelength, and $R$ is extracted from $1/\lambda$ against $\tfrac14 - 1/n_i^2$.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 17 slides; PHY 286, Atomic spectra lab manual*

## The nuclear atom

> [!remark] Remark: Thomson's atom
> J. J. Thomson discovered the electron (1897), with a charge-to-mass ratio about 1800 times that of the hydrogen ion and the same for every cathode material, so electrons are a common ingredient of atoms. His model (1904) put the electrons inside a sphere of diffuse positive charge the size of the atom, about $10^{-10}\ \mathrm m$ (the "plum pudding"). It failed on two counts: electrostatic forces alone cannot hold charges in stable equilibrium, so the electrons would have to move and radiate, and no arrangement produced the observed spectral frequencies. A diffuse atom is also too "soft" to deflect a fast α particle by more than a fraction of a degree in one encounter.
>
> *Source: Tipler §4-2; UP3 §6.4; PHY 284, class 17 slides*

^rem-a2-1-2

> [!model] Model §A2.1.2: Rutherford's Nuclear Atom
> An atom of atomic number $Z$ consists of a **nucleus** of charge $+Ze$, which contains almost all the mass of the atom in a region at most about $10^{-14}\ \mathrm m$ across, and $Z$ electrons at distances of order $10^{-10}\ \mathrm m$. For the scattering of α particles:
> - *Assumes:* the nucleus is a **point charge**, so the α particle feels only the Coulomb repulsion $F = kq_\alpha Q/r^2$ ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|EM Principle §A1.2.2]]) with $Q = Ze$; the nucleus is **fixed** (it is much heavier than the α particle: gold is about $50$ times heavier); the light electrons hardly deflect the α particle; each α particle is deflected by **one** nucleus (thin foil).
> - *Fails:* when the α particle is energetic enough to reach the nucleus, where the nuclear force acts ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-3|Law §A7.1.3]]); and it says nothing about how the electrons are arranged. Electrons orbiting the nucleus like planets would radiate and spiral in, and would give a continuous spectrum, not lines ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^rem-a2-2-1|Remark: The classical planetary atom]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.4; Tipler §4-2*

^mod-a2-1-2

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.2: Impact Parameter; Scattering Angle; Cross Section
> A projectile approaching a scattering centre along a straight line has **impact parameter** $b$: the perpendicular distance from the centre to that line. Its **scattering angle** $\theta$ is the angle between its final and initial directions of motion. The **cross section** for scattering through more than $\theta$ is the area $\sigma = \pi b^2$ in which a projectile must be aimed to be scattered beyond $\theta$: the number scattered per second by one nucleus, divided by the number of incident particles per second per unit area.
>
> *Source: Tipler §4-2*

^def-a2-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.1.3: Impact Parameter and Scattering Angle
> In [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]], a particle of charge $q_\alpha$, mass $m_\alpha$ and initial speed $v$ (kinetic energy $E_k = \tfrac12m_\alpha v^2$) with impact parameter $b$ is scattered by the nucleus of charge $Q$ through the angle $\theta$ with
>
> $$
> b = \frac{kq_\alpha Q}{m_\alpha v^2}\cot\frac\theta2 = \frac{kq_\alpha Q}{2E_k}\cot\frac\theta2 .
> $$
>
> The smaller the impact parameter, the larger the deflection; $b = 0$ (head-on) gives $\theta = 180^\circ$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §4-2, eq. (4-3)*

^thm-a2-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put the nucleus at the origin $O$.
> - *Speed.* Far from the nucleus the potential energy vanishes, so energy conservation ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-3|EM Theorem §A3.2.3]]) makes the final speed equal to the initial speed $v$.
> - *Angular momentum.* The force is along $\vec r$, so it exerts no torque about $O$ and the angular momentum is constant ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-4|CM Theorem §A6.6.4]]). Initially it is $m_\alpha vb$ ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^def-a6-6-1|CM Def. §A6.6.1]]); in polar coordinates it is $m_\alpha r^2\,d\psi/dt$. Hence $dt = r^2\,d\psi/(vb)$.
> - *Momentum change.* The orbit is symmetric about the line from $O$ through the point of closest approach. Initial and final momenta both have magnitude $m_\alpha v$ and differ in direction by $\theta$, so $\Delta\vec p$ points along this symmetry line with $\lvert\Delta\vec p\rvert = 2m_\alpha v\sin(\theta/2)$. Measure the polar angle $\psi$ from the symmetry line; between the incoming and outgoing asymptotes the particle sweeps $\psi$ from $-\tfrac12(\pi - \theta)$ to $+\tfrac12(\pi - \theta)$.
> - *Impulse.* By the impulse–momentum theorem ([[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^thm-a5-1-1|CM Theorem §A5.1.1]]) $\lvert\Delta\vec p\rvert$ is the time integral of the force component along the symmetry line, $F\cos\psi$ (the perpendicular component integrates to zero by symmetry):
>
> $$
> \lvert\Delta\vec p\rvert = \int \frac{kq_\alpha Q}{r^2}\cos\psi\,\frac{r^2\,d\psi}{vb} = \frac{kq_\alpha Q}{vb}\int_{-(\pi-\theta)/2}^{(\pi-\theta)/2}\cos\psi\,d\psi = \frac{2kq_\alpha Q}{vb}\cos\frac\theta2 .
> $$
>
> Setting this equal to $2m_\alpha v\sin(\theta/2)$ gives $b = \dfrac{kq_\alpha Q}{m_\alpha v^2}\cot\dfrac\theta2$. The factor $r^2$ cancels: that the force is exactly inverse-square is what makes the integral elementary.

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.1.4: Rutherford Scattering Formula
> Let $N_0$ particles per second, of kinetic energy $E_k$, fall on a thin foil of thickness $t$ containing $n$ nuclei of charge $Q = Ze$ per unit volume. In [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]]:
> 1. the fraction scattered through angles larger than $\theta$ is $f = \pi b^2\,nt$, with $b$ from [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-3|Theorem §A2.1.3]];
> 2. a detector of area $A_{sc}$ at distance $r$ from the foil, at angle $\theta$, counts
>
> $$
> \Delta N = N_0\,nt\,\frac{A_{sc}}{r^2}\Bigl(\frac{kq_\alpha Q}{4E_k}\Bigr)^2\frac{1}{\sin^4(\theta/2)} \quad\text{per second;}
> $$
>
> for α particles ($q_\alpha = 2e$) the bracket is $kZe^2/2E_k$. The count is proportional to $1/\sin^4(\theta/2)$, to the foil thickness, to $Z^2$ and to $1/E_k^2$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §4-2, eqs. (4-4)–(4-6); Tipler, More Chapter 4 #1 (PHY 284 handout), eqs. (4-7)–(4-10)*

^thm-a2-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let the beam have area $A$ and $I_0 = N_0/A$ particles per second per unit area.
> 1. A particle is scattered beyond $\theta$ if it passes one nucleus with impact parameter less than $b(\theta)$; $b$ decreases as $\theta$ grows. One nucleus scatters $I_0\pi b^2$ particles per second beyond $\theta$ ([[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^def-a2-1-2|Def. §A2.1.2]]). The beam meets $nAt$ nuclei; in a thin foil their target areas do not overlap, so $I_0\pi b^2\,nAt$ particles per second are scattered beyond $\theta$, the fraction $\pi b^2 nt$ of $N_0 = I_0A$.
> 2. Write $a = kq_\alpha Q/2E_k$, so $b = a\cot(\theta/2)$ and $\lvert db/d\theta\rvert = \tfrac a2\csc^2(\theta/2)$. Particles with impact parameters between $b$ and $b + db$ pass through a ring of area $2\pi b\,\lvert db\rvert$ and are scattered between $\theta$ and $\theta + d\theta$; one nucleus sends
>
> $$
> I_0\,2\pi b\,\lvert db\rvert = I_0\,\pi a^2\cot\tfrac\theta2\csc^2\tfrac\theta2\,d\theta = I_0\,\pi a^2\,\frac{\cos(\theta/2)}{\sin^3(\theta/2)}\,d\theta
> $$
>
> particles per second into that range. At distance $r$ they cross the band of a sphere of area $2\pi r\sin\theta\cdot r\,d\theta = 4\pi r^2\sin\tfrac\theta2\cos\tfrac\theta2\,d\theta$, so the number per unit area is $I_0\,a^2/(4r^2\sin^4\tfrac\theta2)$. Multiply by the detector area $A_{sc}$ and by the $nAt$ nuclei in the beam, and use $I_0A = N_0$ and $a/2 = kq_\alpha Q/4E_k$.

^der-a2-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-3|Theorem §A2.1.3]], [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^def-a2-1-2|Def. §A2.1.2]], [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^mod-a2-1-2|Model §A2.1.2]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: The test (Geiger and Marsden, 1913)
> Geiger and Marsden counted scintillations by eye, several hundred thousand in all, and verified every prediction of [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-4|Theorem §A2.1.4]]: the $1/\sin^4(\theta/2)$ dependence from $5^\circ$ to $150^\circ$, over which the count changed by a factor of about $250\,000$; proportionality to the foil thickness; growth with the target's atomic weight (approximately its square, from carbon to gold); and the $1/E_k^2$ dependence, tested by slowing the α particles with absorbers. Their earlier measurement that about $1$ in $8000$ α particles is scattered by more than $90^\circ$ ([[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^ex-a2-1-2|Example §A2.1.2]]) is impossible for a Thomson atom. Rutherford called it as incredible as a shell bouncing back off tissue paper.
>
> *Source: Tipler §4-2; Tipler, More Chapter 4 #1 (PHY 284 handout)*

^rem-a2-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.1.5: Distance of Closest Approach
> In a head-on collision ($b = 0$) the particle stops and turns back at the distance
>
> $$
> r_d = \frac{kq_\alpha Q}{E_k} = \frac{kq_\alpha Q}{\tfrac12m_\alpha v^2}
> $$
>
> from the nucleus; in other collisions it stays farther away. If the measured scattering follows [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-4|Theorem §A2.1.4]] at the largest angles, the nucleus is no larger than $r_d$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §4-2, eq. (4-11)*

^thm-a2-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With the nucleus fixed, the head-on particle moves along a line and momentarily stops at the turning point. Energy conservation between a far point ($U = 0$, $K = E_k$) and the turning point ($K = 0$, $U = kq_\alpha Q/r_d$, [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-2|EM Theorem §A3.1.2]]) gives $E_k = kq_\alpha Q/r_d$. In [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-3|Theorem §A2.1.3]] the constant $kq_\alpha Q/2E_k$ is therefore $r_d/2$.

^der-a2-1-5

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-2|EM Theorem §A3.1.2]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^thm-a3-2-3|EM Theorem §A3.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: How big is a nucleus?
> For the $7.7$-MeV α particles of Geiger and Marsden on gold ($Z = 79$), with $ke^2 = 1.44\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}$,
>
> $$
> r_d = \frac{2(79)(1.44\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm})}{7.7\times10^6\ \mathrm{eV}} = 3.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{nm} = 3.0\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm m ,
> $$
>
> and the data fit the point-nucleus formula: the gold nucleus is smaller than this, some $10^4$ times smaller than the atom. With aluminium ($Z = 13$) the same α particles came close enough for the data to deviate at large angles; slowing them restored agreement, and the deviation set in at $r_d \approx 10^{-14}\ \mathrm m$, Rutherford's estimate of the aluminium nucleus (its radius is actually about $3.6\ \mathrm{fm}$, [[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^law-a7-1-1|Law §A7.1.1]]). Nuclear sizes are measured in femtometres, $1\ \mathrm{fm} = 10^{-15}\ \mathrm m$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §4-2*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.1.1: First Lines and Limits of Three Series
> From [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]] with $R_H = 1.0968\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$: the longest wavelength of a series has $n_i = n_f + 1$, the shortest is the limit $n_i \to \infty$.
>
> | Series | $n_f$ | first line, $1/\lambda$ | first line | series limit $n_f^2/R_H$ | region |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | Lyman | 1 | $\tfrac34 R_H$ | $121.6\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $91.2\ \mathrm{nm}$ | ultraviolet |
> | Balmer | 2 | $\tfrac{5}{36} R_H$ | $656.5\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $364.7\ \mathrm{nm}$ | visible to near UV |
> | Paschen | 3 | $\tfrac{7}{144} R_H$ | $1876\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $820.6\ \mathrm{nm}$ | infrared |
>
> Only the Balmer series reaches the visible, and infinitely many lines lie between its first line ($\mathrm H_\alpha$, red) and its limit. These three series do not overlap, but the higher ones do: the Brackett series ($n_f = 4$) runs from $4052\ \mathrm{nm}$ down to its limit $1459\ \mathrm{nm}$, below the first Paschen line, and the Pfund series ($n_f = 5$) from $7460\ \mathrm{nm}$ down to $2279\ \mathrm{nm}$, well inside the Brackett range.
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 4-1; UP3 Example 6.9*

^ex-a2-1-1

> [!example] Example §A2.1.2: How Many α Particles Bounce Back?
> α particles of $5.0\ \mathrm{MeV}$ fall on a gold foil ($Z = 79$, density $19.3\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$, molar mass $197\ \mathrm{g/mol}$) $1.0\ \mu\mathrm m$ thick. What fraction is scattered by $90^\circ$ or more?
>
> The impact parameter for $\theta = 90^\circ$ ([[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-3|Theorem §A2.1.3]], $\cot 45^\circ = 1$) is
>
> $$
> b = \frac{2(79)(1.44\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm})}{2(5.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{eV})} = 2.28\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{nm} = 2.28\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm m ,
> $$
>
> the density of nuclei is $n = \rho N_A/M = (19.3)(6.02\times10^{23})/197\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}} = 5.90\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, and by [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-4|Theorem §A2.1.4]]
>
> $$
> f = \pi b^2\,nt = \pi(2.28\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm m)^2(5.90\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}})(1.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm m) = 9.6\times10^{-5} ,
> $$
>
> about $1$ in $10\,000$, in line with Geiger and Marsden's first measurement of about $1$ in $8000$. The target area each nucleus presents for such large deflections, $\pi b^2 = 1.6\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, is about $5\times10^{-8}$ of the cross-section of an atom of radius $10^{-10}\ \mathrm m$: the atom is almost empty.
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 4-2*

^ex-a2-1-2

> [!example] Example §A2.1.3: Moving the Detector
> A detector at $\theta = 45^\circ$ counts $450$ α particles per minute from a silver foil. With everything else unchanged, it is moved to $90^\circ$. Only the factor $1/\sin^4(\theta/2)$ in [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-4|Theorem §A2.1.4]] changes:
>
> $$
> \Delta N_{90^\circ} = 450\ \mathrm{min^{-1}}\,\frac{\sin^4 22.5^\circ}{\sin^4 45^\circ} = 450\ \mathrm{min^{-1}}\,\frac{0.0214}{0.250} = 39\ \mathrm{min^{-1}} .
> $$
>
> Doubling the angle cuts the rate by a factor of about $12$, a steep but finite fall; Geiger and Marsden followed it over a factor of about $250\,000$ ([[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^rem-a2-1-3|Remark: The test]]).
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 4-3*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The impulse integral in [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-3|Theorem §A2.1.3]] is evaluated by the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]); the ring area uses $db/d\theta$ by the chain rule ([[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]).
> - The orbits are the repulsive counterpart of the conic-section orbits of gravitation ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-1|CM Theorem §A8.3.1]]); the full classical treatment, with the orbit equation and the differential cross section, is mechanics at level B ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-1|CM Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-5|CM Theorem §B4.2.5]], [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^rem-b4-2-3|CM Remark: What this adds to the level-A derivation]]); the impulse method of Theorem §A2.1.3, extended to any weak field as small-angle scattering: [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-7|CM Theorem §B4.2.7]].
> - Departures from the Rutherford formula at high energies later measured nuclear radii ([[§A7.1 Properties of Nuclei and Binding Energy#^rem-a7-1-2|Remark: How nuclear radii are measured]]), and large-angle scattering of fast electrons by protons revealed quarks ([[§A8.2 Quarks and the Standard Model#^rem-a8-2-1|Remark: How we know quarks are there]]).
