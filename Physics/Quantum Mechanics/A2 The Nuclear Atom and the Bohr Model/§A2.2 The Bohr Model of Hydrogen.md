Rutherford's atom has an electron circling a tiny nucleus, but by classical electrodynamics such an atom collapses in a fraction of a microsecond and radiates a continuous spectrum. In 1913 Bohr kept the classical orbit and added two nonclassical rules: only orbits of angular momentum $n\hbar$ are allowed, and the atom radiates only when the electron jumps between them, one photon per jump. The result reproduces the Rydberg formula and expresses the Rydberg constant through $m_e$, $e$, $h$ and $c$. The model is wrong in its picture of orbits, but its energy levels are right, and it introduced quantum numbers and stationary states into atomic physics.

## Why a new model was needed

> [!remark] Remark: The classical planetary atom
> An electron in a circular orbit of radius $r$ about a nucleus of charge $+Ze$ is held by the Coulomb force, $kZe^2/r^2 = mv^2/r$. Its energy is $E = \tfrac12mv^2 - kZe^2/r = -kZe^2/2r$, and it revolves at $f = v/2\pi r \propto r^{-3/2}$. But an accelerating charge radiates ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^rem-a12-2-2|EM Remark: Antennas]]), here at the frequency of revolution. Losing energy, the electron moves to smaller $r$, where it revolves faster and radiates faster still, and it would spiral into the nucleus in less than a microsecond, emitting a continuously rising frequency on the way. Real atoms are stable, do not radiate unless excited, and when excited emit sharp lines. Classical physics fails twice: on stability and on the spectrum.
>
> *Source: Tipler §4-3, eqs. (4-12)–(4-14); UP3 §6.4; PHY 284, class 17 slides*

## Bohr's postulates

> [!model] Model §A2.2.1: Bohr Model of a One-Electron Atom
> An electron (mass $m_e$, charge $-e$) is bound to a nucleus of charge $+Ze$: hydrogen ($Z = 1$) or a hydrogen-like ion such as $\mathrm{He^+}$ or $\mathrm{Li^{2+}}$.
> 1. **Classical orbits.** The electron moves on a circle centred on the nucleus, held by the Coulomb attraction ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|EM Principle §A1.2.2]]) and obeying Newton's laws; the nucleus is fixed (infinitely heavy).
> 2. **Quantized angular momentum; stationary states.** Only the orbits whose angular momentum is a whole multiple of $\hbar$ are allowed,
>
> $$
> L_n = m_e v_n r_n = n\hbar, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots ,
> $$
>
> and in them the electron does not radiate. These are the **stationary states**.
> 3. **Frequency condition.** The atom emits or absorbs radiation only when the electron jumps from one stationary state to another, as a single photon ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]) carrying the energy difference:
>
> $$
> hf = \lvert E_i - E_f\rvert .
> $$
>
> - *Assumes:* in addition, nonrelativistic motion and only the Coulomb force (no spin, no magnetic effects); the jumps happen instantaneously, by a mechanism the model does not describe.
> - *Fails:* for atoms with more than one electron; for the intensities of lines and the rates of transitions; for the fine structure of lines; and its picture of a definite orbit is wrong ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^rem-a2-2-6|Remark: Limits]]). The energy levels of hydrogen it predicts are confirmed by the Schrödinger equation ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom|§A5.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.4, eqs. (6.33)–(6.35); Tipler §4-3, eqs. (4-15), (4-17)*

^mod-a2-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Bohr's own route, and a reason for $n\hbar$
> Bohr first stated the quantum condition as an energy rule echoing Planck's: the kinetic energy in the $n$th orbit is $K = \tfrac12 nhf_{\text{rev}}$, with $f_{\text{rev}} = v/2\pi r$ the frequency of revolution, and he fixed the factor $\tfrac12$ with the correspondence principle ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^pr-a2-2-6|Principle §A2.2.6]]). For circular orbits the two rules agree: $\tfrac12mv^2 = \tfrac12 nh\,v/2\pi r$ is $mvr = nh/2\pi = n\hbar$. Angular momentum quantization is the simpler form, and the one that survives in quantum mechanics. De Broglie (1924) gave it a reason: an electron wave that closes on itself around the orbit must fit a whole number of wavelengths into the circumference ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves|§A3.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 17–18 slides; Tipler §4-3; UP3 §6.5*

^rem-a2-2-2

## Orbits and energies

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.1: Bohr Radius; Fine-Structure Constant
> The **Bohr radius** and the **fine-structure constant** are
>
> $$
> a_0 = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{m_ee^2} = \frac{\hbar^2}{m_eke^2} = 5.29\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm m = 0.0529\ \mathrm{nm}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{ke^2}{\hbar c} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c} = \frac{1}{137.0} ,
> $$
>
> with $k = 1/4\pi\varepsilon_0$. $\alpha$ is dimensionless, the same in every system of units, and measures the strength of the electromagnetic interaction. Useful combinations: $ke^2 = 1.44\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}$, $\hbar c = 197.3\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}$, $m_ec^2 = 511\ \mathrm{keV}$, and $a_0 = \hbar/(m_ec\,\alpha)$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.4, eq. (6.39); Tipler §4-3, eqs. (4-19), (4-31); PHY 284, class 17 slides*

^def-a2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.2: Radii and Speeds of the Bohr Orbits
> In [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^mod-a2-2-1|Model §A2.2.1]] the allowed orbits have
>
> $$
> r_n = \frac{n^2}{Z}\,a_0, \qquad v_n = \frac{Z}{n}\,\alpha c = \frac{kZe^2}{n\hbar}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
> $$
>
> For hydrogen the radii are $a_0, 4a_0, 9a_0, \dots$ and no others; the speed is largest in the first orbit, $v_1 = \alpha c = 2.19\times10^6\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx c/137$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.4, eqs. (6.36)–(6.40), (6.51); Tipler §4-3, eqs. (4-16)–(4-18); PHY 284, class 17 slides*

^thm-a2-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The Coulomb force supplies the centripetal force ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^thm-a3-9-1|CM Theorem §A3.9.1]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{m_ev^2}{r} = \frac{kZe^2}{r^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_ev^2r = kZe^2 .
> $$
>
> Divide by the quantum condition $m_evr = n\hbar$: $v_n = kZe^2/n\hbar = (Z/n)\,\alpha c$ by [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^def-a2-2-1|Def. §A2.2.1]]. Then $r_n = n\hbar/(m_ev_n) = n^2\hbar^2/(m_ekZe^2) = n^2a_0/Z$.

^der-a2-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^mod-a2-2-1|Model §A2.2.1]], [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^def-a2-2-1|Def. §A2.2.1]], [[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^thm-a3-9-1|CM Theorem §A3.9.1]], [[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|EM Principle §A1.2.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.3: Energy Levels
> The energy of the electron in the $n$th orbit (the energy of the atom) is
>
> $$
> E_n = -\frac{Z^2E_0}{n^2}, \qquad E_0 = \frac{m_ek^2e^4}{2\hbar^2} = \frac{m_ee^4}{8\varepsilon_0^2h^2} = \tfrac12 m_ec^2\alpha^2 = 2.18\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm J = 13.6\ \mathrm{eV} .
> $$
>
> In each orbit $K_n = -E_n$ and $U_n = 2E_n$. For hydrogen, $E_n = -13.6\ \mathrm{eV}/n^2$: $-13.6$, $-3.40$, $-1.51$, $-0.85\ \mathrm{eV}, \dots$, crowding toward $0$ as $n \to \infty$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.4, eqs. (6.41)–(6.46), (6.52); Tipler §4-3, eqs. (4-20), (4-24), (4-32)*

^thm-a2-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From $m_ev^2r = kZe^2$ (derivation of [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]), $K = \tfrac12m_ev^2 = kZe^2/2r$, while the potential energy of the electron–nucleus pair is $U = -kZe^2/r$ ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-2|EM Theorem §A3.1.2]]). So $U = -2K$ and $E = K + U = -K = -kZe^2/2r$, as for a circular orbit in gravity ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-5|CM Theorem §A8.2.5]]). Insert $r_n = n^2\hbar^2/(m_ekZe^2)$:
>
> $$
> E_n = -\frac{kZe^2}{2}\cdot\frac{m_ekZe^2}{n^2\hbar^2} = -\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\cdot\frac{m_ek^2e^4}{2\hbar^2} .
> $$
>
> With $ke^2 = \alpha\hbar c$, $E_0 = \tfrac12m_e(\alpha c)^2 = \tfrac12(511\ \mathrm{keV})/137.0^2 = 13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$: the ground-state binding energy is the kinetic energy of an electron moving at $\alpha c$.

^der-a2-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-2|EM Theorem §A3.1.2]], [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^def-a2-2-1|Def. §A2.2.1]], [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-5|CM Theorem §A8.2.5]]

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.2: Ground State; Excited States; Ionization Energy
> The lowest energy state, $n = 1$, is the **ground state**; the states $n = 2, 3, \dots$ are **excited states** (the first, second, … excited state). The integer $n$ is the **principal** (energy) **quantum number**. The **ionization energy** of a state is the least energy needed to remove the electron from it to rest at infinity, $E_\infty - E_n = -E_n$; from the ground state of hydrogen it is $13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$, also called the binding energy of the electron. States with $E < 0$ are bound; above $E = 0$ the electron is free and its energy is not quantized.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.4, eqs. (6.46)–(6.47); Tipler §4-3*

^def-a2-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.4: Spectrum of a One-Electron Atom
> A transition from $n_i$ to $n_f < n_i$ emits a photon of wavelength
>
> $$
> \frac1\lambda = Z^2R_\infty\Bigl(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\Bigr), \qquad R_\infty = \frac{E_0}{hc} = \frac{m_ek^2e^4}{4\pi c\hbar^3} = 1.0974\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} ,
> $$
>
> and absorption of the same wavelength takes the atom from $n_f$ up to $n_i$. For $Z = 1$ this is the empirical Rydberg formula ([[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]), now with the Rydberg constant computed from $m_e$, $e$, $h$ and $c$; each series is the set of jumps that end on one level.
>
> *Source: UP3 §6.4, eqs. (6.48)–(6.50); Tipler §4-3, eqs. (4-21)–(4-23)*

^thm-a2-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the frequency condition of [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^mod-a2-2-1|Model §A2.2.1]] and [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]],
>
> $$
> \frac{hc}{\lambda} = hf = E_{n_i} - E_{n_f} = Z^2E_0\Bigl(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> positive since $n_i > n_f$ (a photon's energy is positive; [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]], with $c = f\lambda$). Divide by $hc$: $R_\infty = E_0/hc = m_ek^2e^4/(2\hbar^2\cdot 2\pi\hbar c)$. Numerically $13.6\ \mathrm{eV}/(1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}) = 1.097\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$. For absorption the photon supplies the same energy difference.

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.5: Reduced-Mass Correction
> If the nucleus has finite mass $M$, the results above hold with the electron mass replaced by the **reduced mass** $\mu = m_eM/(m_e + M)$, with $r$ the electron–nucleus separation. In particular the energies are multiplied by $\mu/m_e$ and the Rydberg constant of the atom is
>
> $$
> R_M = \frac{R_\infty}{1 + m_e/M} .
> $$
>
> For hydrogen ($M = m_p = 1836\,m_e$), $R_H = 1.09678\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $0.054\%$ below $R_\infty$; for heavier nuclei the correction is smaller still.
>
> *Source: Tipler §4-3, eqs. (4-25)–(4-27)*

^thm-a2-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Electron and nucleus both circle their centre of mass ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|CM Def. §A5.4.1]]) with the same angular velocity $\omega$, at distances $r_e$ and $r_N$ with $m_er_e = Mr_N$ and $r_e + r_N = r$; hence $r_e = Mr/(m_e + M)$ and $m_er_e = \mu r$. Then:
> - *force on the electron:* $kZe^2/r^2 = m_e\omega^2r_e = \mu\,\omega^2 r$;
> - *total angular momentum:* $m_e\omega r_e^2 + M\omega r_N^2 = \omega\,\dfrac{m_eM^2 + Mm_e^2}{(m_e+M)^2}\,r^2 = \mu\,\omega r^2$;
> - *total kinetic energy:* $\tfrac12m_e\omega^2r_e^2 + \tfrac12M\omega^2r_N^2 = \tfrac12\mu\,\omega^2r^2$, while $U = -kZe^2/r$.
>
> These are exactly the equations of the fixed-nucleus model for a particle of mass $\mu$ at radius $r$ with speed $v = \omega r$. Quantizing the total angular momentum, $\mu vr = n\hbar$, the derivations of [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]] and [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]] go through with $m_e \to \mu$: $E_n \propto \mu$, so $R_M = R_\infty\,\mu/m_e = R_\infty/(1 + m_e/M)$.

## The correspondence principle

> [!principle] Principle §A2.2.6: Bohr's Correspondence Principle
> In the limit of large quantum numbers, where the quantized energies lie close together, the predictions of quantum theory must agree with those of classical physics.
>
> *Domain: a guiding requirement on any quantum theory, used to fix or check its rules; it constrains the theory only where classical physics is known to work (large orbits, large energies). It remains valid in modern quantum mechanics.*
> *Source: Tipler §4-3; PHY 284, class 18 slides*

^pr-a2-2-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.7: Large-$n$ Limit of the Bohr Model
> For large $n$, the frequency of the photon emitted in the jump $n \to n - 1$ approaches the classical frequency of revolution in the $n$th orbit:
>
> $$
> f_{n\to n-1} \;\to\; f_{\text{rev}} = \frac{v_n}{2\pi r_n} = \frac{Z^2m_ek^2e^4}{2\pi\hbar^3n^3} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Tipler §4-3, eqs. (4-28)–(4-29); PHY 284, class 18 slides*

^thm-a2-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]],
>
> $$
> f_{n\to n-1} = \frac{Z^2E_0}{h}\Bigl[\frac{1}{(n-1)^2} - \frac1{n^2}\Bigr] = \frac{Z^2E_0}{h}\cdot\frac{2n - 1}{n^2(n-1)^2} \approx \frac{Z^2E_0}{h}\cdot\frac{2}{n^3} = \frac{Z^2m_ek^2e^4}{2\pi\hbar^3n^3}
> $$
>
> for $n \gg 1$, using $E_0 = m_ek^2e^4/2\hbar^2$ and $h = 2\pi\hbar$. By [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], $f_{\text{rev}} = v_n/2\pi r_n = (kZe^2/n\hbar)\big/\bigl(2\pi n^2\hbar^2/m_ekZe^2\bigr) = Z^2m_ek^2e^4/(2\pi\hbar^3n^3)$, the same. A classical charge radiates at its frequency of revolution, so for large orbits Bohr's rule and classical electrodynamics agree, as [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^pr-a2-2-6|Principle §A2.2.6]] demands.

^der-a2-2-7

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]], [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^pr-a2-2-6|Principle §A2.2.6]], [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^rem-a12-2-2|EM Remark: Antennas]]

> [!remark] Remark: Rydberg atoms
> At large $n$ the atom is huge and loosely bound: $r_n = n^2a_0$ and $E_n = -13.6\ \mathrm{eV}/n^2$. For $n = 1000$ a hydrogen atom would be about $0.1\ \mathrm{mm}$ across, bound by only $10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}$, far below thermal energies ($k_BT \approx 0.025\ \mathrm{eV}$ at room temperature), so collisions ionize such atoms at once. With tunable lasers, "Rydberg atoms" with $n$ of several hundred have been made in vacuum and live for milliseconds, which allows the slow, nearly classical motion of the electron to be studied directly.
>
> *Source: Tipler §4-3 (Exploring: Giant atoms)*

## Evidence and limits

> [!law] Law §A2.2.8: Franck–Hertz Result
> Electrons accelerated through mercury vapour lose kinetic energy to the atoms only in definite amounts: the current that reaches a collector against a small retarding voltage drops sharply each time the accelerating voltage passes a multiple of $4.9\ \mathrm V$. The vapour emits ultraviolet light of wavelength $hc/(4.9\ \mathrm{eV}) = 253\ \mathrm{nm}$, a line of the mercury spectrum, only when the accelerating voltage exceeds $4.9\ \mathrm V$.
>
> *Valid when:* low-pressure mercury vapour and electron energies up to some $15\ \mathrm{eV}$; other gases show their own characteristic energy losses (for hydrogen the first is $E_2 - E_1 = 10.2\ \mathrm{eV}$). First observed by Franck and Hertz in 1914.
> *Source: Tipler §4-5*

^law-a2-2-8

> [!remark] Remark: What Franck and Hertz showed
> Below $4.9\ \mathrm{eV}$ an electron can only bounce elastically off a mercury atom, because the atom has no state between its ground state and its first excited state $4.9\ \mathrm{eV}$ higher. Above it, the electron can give exactly $4.9\ \mathrm{eV}$ to the atom, which then falls back by emitting one photon of that energy. Further dips at $9.8\ \mathrm V$, $14.7\ \mathrm V$ come from electrons that excite two or three atoms in succession. Atomic energy levels are therefore discrete, the same levels are reached by collisions as by light, and the photon energy equals the level spacing, all measured with voltmeters and ammeters alone. Bohr's theory cannot compute mercury's levels (80 electrons), but it predicted that such levels exist.
>
> *Source: Tipler §4-5*

^rem-a2-2-4

> [!remark] Remark: Successes of the model
> - It derives the Rydberg formula and the value of the Rydberg constant from $m_e$, $e$, $h$ and $c$ ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-4|Theorem §A2.2.4]]), and it predicted the hydrogen series that were found later (Lyman 1916, Brackett 1922, Pfund 1924).
> - It predicts the small differences in $R$ between atoms through the reduced mass ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-5|Theorem §A2.2.5]]); in 1931 Urey used the shift of the Balmer lines to discover deuterium.
> - The spectra of $\mathrm{He^+}$ and other one-electron ions follow with $Z^2$, as long as $Z$ is not too large; the same scaling gives the characteristic X-ray lines of heavy atoms (Moseley's law, [[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers|§A5.4]]).
> - Its size scale is right: $a_0 = 0.053\ \mathrm{nm}$ compares well with the $0.22\ \mathrm{nm}$ diameter of the hydrogen molecule. The speed $v_1 = c/137$ is small enough to justify Newtonian mechanics; relativistic corrections are of order $(v/c)^2 = \alpha^2 \approx 5\times10^{-5}$, the size of the fine structure.
>
> *Source: Tipler, More Chapter 4 #2 (PHY 284 handout); Tipler §4-3; UP3 §6.4*

^rem-a2-2-5

> [!remark] Remark: Limits
> - It cannot handle atoms with more than one electron, nor how atoms bind to each other.
> - It says nothing about how a jump happens or how long it takes, so it cannot predict the relative intensities of lines.
> - It cannot explain the fine structure: Sommerfeld's relativistic elliptical orbits gave the right size of splitting by accident; the real cause involves the electron's spin ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin|§A5.2]]).
> - Its postulates have no foundation in the physics they borrow: Coulomb's law and Newton's laws are used, but the electrodynamics that makes accelerated charges radiate is suspended, and only certain angular momenta are allowed, for no stated reason.
>
> These were overcome by wave mechanics (de Broglie, Schrödinger, Heisenberg and others in the 1920s: [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves|§A3.1]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States|§A4.1]]). In the Schrödinger hydrogen atom ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom|§A5.1]]) the energies $-13.6\ \mathrm{eV}/n^2$ survive, but the electron has no definite orbit, and $n\hbar$ is not the angular momentum of the state. The Bohr model remains a useful picture and gives the right energies.
>
> *Source: PHY 284, class 18 slides; Tipler, More Chapter 4 #2 (PHY 284 handout); Tipler §4-3*

^rem-a2-2-6

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.2.1: Absorbing a 93.7-nm Photon
> A hydrogen atom in its ground state absorbs a photon of wavelength $93.7\ \mathrm{nm}$ (a Lyman line). Which state does it reach, how large is it, and how much energy would now ionize it?
>
> Absorption from $n_f = 1$ to $n_i = n$ requires, by [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-4|Theorem §A2.2.4]], $1/\lambda = R_H(1 - 1/n^2)$, so
>
> $$
> n = \Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{\lambda R_H}\Bigr)^{-1/2} = \Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{(93.7\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm m)(1.0968\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}})}\Bigr)^{-1/2} = 6.09 \approx 6 ,
> $$
>
> not exactly an integer because $\lambda$ is given to three figures. In the state $n = 6$ (the fifth excited state) the orbit radius is $r_6 = 36a_0 = 1.90\ \mathrm{nm}$, $36$ times the ground-state radius, and the energy is $E_6 = -13.6\ \mathrm{eV}/36 = -0.378\ \mathrm{eV}$. Ionizing the atom now takes only $0.378\ \mathrm{eV}$, $36$ times less than from the ground state ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^def-a2-2-2|Def. §A2.2.2]]). Reading off levels from measured lines in this way is how spectra reveal atomic structure.
>

> [!example] Example §A2.2.2: Hydrogen, Ionized Helium and the Reduced Mass
> $\mathrm{He^+}$ ($Z = 2$) has levels $E_n = -4(13.6\ \mathrm{eV})/n^2 = -54.4\ \mathrm{eV}/n^2$: $-54.4$, $-13.6$, $-6.04$, $-3.40$, $-2.18\ \mathrm{eV}$ for $n = 1, \dots, 5$, and ionization energy $54.4\ \mathrm{eV}$. Every even level $n = 2m$ of $\mathrm{He^+}$ has the energy $-13.6\ \mathrm{eV}/m^2$ of the hydrogen level $m$, so for every hydrogen line $m_i \to m_f$ there is an $\mathrm{He^+}$ line $2m_i \to 2m_f$ at nearly the same wavelength. For example, $\mathrm{He^+}$ $4 \to 2$ matches hydrogen's Lyman $\alpha$ ($2 \to 1$).
>
> *Nearly*, because of the reduced mass ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-5|Theorem §A2.2.5]]). With $M_\alpha = 6.645\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $R_{\mathrm{He}} = R_\infty/(1 + m_e/M_\alpha) = 1.09722\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, against $R_H = 1.09678\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Both lines have $1/\lambda = \tfrac34 R$ (for $\mathrm{He^+}$: $Z^2(\tfrac14 - \tfrac1{16}) = \tfrac34$), so
>
> $$
> \lambda_{\mathrm H} = \frac{4}{3R_H} = 121.568\ \mathrm{nm}, \qquad \lambda_{\mathrm{He^+}} = \frac{4}{3R_{\mathrm{He}}} = 121.519\ \mathrm{nm} ,
> $$
>
> $0.050\ \mathrm{nm}$ apart, which a good spectrometer resolves. The same effect separates the $\mathrm H_\alpha$ lines of hydrogen and deuterium by $0.18\ \mathrm{nm}$, the shift with which Urey discovered deuterium. (The instructor's solution compares the first Balmer line of $\mathrm{He^+}$, $164.0\ \mathrm{nm}$, with Lyman $\alpha$, and finds the reduced-mass correction negligible at three figures; the matched pair above is where it shows.)
>
> *Source: PHY 284, HW 6 (and solutions); Tipler §4-3*
