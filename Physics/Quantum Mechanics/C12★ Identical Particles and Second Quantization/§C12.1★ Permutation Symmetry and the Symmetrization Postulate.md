What is the state space of $N$ identical particles? Level B answered with wave functions: the exchange operator and its conservation ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]]), the symmetrization requirement ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^pr-b7-1-3|Principle §B7.1.3]]), the Pauli principle and the Slater determinant ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^def-b7-1-3|Def. §B7.1.3]]), the exchange force and the pairing of spin with spatial symmetry ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-6|Theorem §B7.1.6]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-7|Theorem §B7.1.7]]), and helium ([[§B7.2 Atoms#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]]). Level C starts from the composite-system rule, which puts $N$ particles on the tensor product $\mathcal V^{\otimes N}$ ([[§C1.5 Composite Systems and Tensor Products#^pr-c1-5-1|Principle §C1.5.1]]), and asks what identity does to it. Permutations become operators that represent the symmetric group $S_N$; identity becomes the statement that every observable commutes with them; and the symmetrization postulate becomes a choice of state space: of all the ways $S_N$ can act on $\mathcal V^{\otimes N}$, nature uses only the two one-dimensional ones. Projectors, dimensions and normalizations follow, and for two spins one half the exchange operator turns into a spin operator. Helium is not repeated here; it stays at level B.

*Notation (Sakurai).* $|k'\rangle$ is a one-particle ket, $k'$ a collective label for a complete set of commuting observables; in a product $|k^{(1)}\rangle|k^{(2)}\rangle\cdots|k^{(N)}\rangle$ the $j$th factor belongs to particle $j$. $\mathcal V$ is the one-particle ket space, $\mathcal V^{\otimes N}$ the $N$-particle one. Permutations $\pi \in S_N$ compose right first, as in Group Theory ([[§3 Basic Examples of Groups#^def-3-5|493 Def. §3.5]]); $(i\,j)$ is a transposition. $\hbar$ is explicit.

## Permutation operators

> [!definition] Definition §C12.1.1: Permutation Operators
> For $\pi \in S_N$ the **permutation operator** $P_\pi$ is the linear operator on $\mathcal V^{\otimes N}$ with
>
> $$
> P_\pi\,|k^{(1)}\rangle|k^{(2)}\rangle\cdots|k^{(N)}\rangle = |k^{(\pi^{-1}(1))}\rangle|k^{(\pi^{-1}(2))}\rangle\cdots|k^{(\pi^{-1}(N))}\rangle :
> $$
>
> the one-particle state carried by particle $i$ is afterwards carried by particle $\pi(i)$. For a transposition, $P_{ij} \equiv P_{(i\,j)}$ interchanges particles $i$ and $j$; for $N = 2$, $P_{12}|k'\rangle|k''\rangle = |k''\rangle|k'\rangle$ is level B's exchange operator ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]]). Sakurai's cyclic operator $P_{123}|k'\rangle|k''\rangle|k'''\rangle = |k''\rangle|k'''\rangle|k'\rangle$ is $P_\pi$ for $\pi = (1\,3\,2)$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §7.1, eqs. (7.1)–(7.4), §7.5, eqs. (7.56), (7.61) · Likharev §8.1, eq. (8.2); the form for a general $\pi$ written out here*

^def-c12-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C12.1.1: The Symmetric Group Acting on Kets
> 1. $P_\pi P_\sigma = P_{\pi\sigma}$ and $P_{\mathrm{id}} = 1$: $\pi \mapsto P_\pi$ is a representation of $S_N$ on $\mathcal V^{\otimes N}$ ([[§20 Polynomial Rings, Permutation Matrices, and Representations#^def-20-7|493 Def. §20.7]]). Each $P_\pi$ is unitary, $P_\pi^\dagger = P_{\pi^{-1}}$.
> 2. $P_{ij} = P_{ji}$, $P_{ij}^2 = 1$; $P_{ij}$ is Hermitian and unitary with eigenvalues $\pm1$ (7.5), (7.57). Every $P_\pi$ is a product of transpositions, e.g. $P_{123} = P_{12}P_{13}$ (7.62), and for $N \ge 3$ transpositions do not commute in general: $[P_{ij}, P_{kl}] \ne 0$ (7.58).
> 3. Let $A$ be a one-particle operator and $A_k$ the operator $A$ acting on particle $k$ ($A$ in the $k$th factor, $1$ elsewhere). Then
>
> $$
> P_\pi\,A_k\,P_\pi^{-1} = A_{\pi(k)} , \qquad\text{in particular}\qquad P_{12}A_1P_{12}^{-1} = A_2 \quad (7.8) :
> $$
>
> permutations change the particle labels of observables. An operator $F(A_1, \dots, A_N; B_1, \dots, B_N; \dots)$ built from particle observables commutes with every $P_\pi$ exactly when it is symmetric in the labels, like the Hamiltonian $H = \sum_k\mathbf p_k^2/2m + \sum_kV_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf x_k) + \frac12\sum_{k\ne l}V_{\text{pair}}(|\mathbf x_k - \mathbf x_l|)$ (7.9)–(7.10).
> 4. If $[P_\pi, H] = 0$ for all $\pi$ and $P_\pi|\alpha\rangle = c(\pi)|\alpha\rangle$ for all $\pi$ at $t = 0$, the same holds at every time: symmetric (antisymmetric) kets stay symmetric (antisymmetric), the $N$-particle form of [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]].
>
> *Source: Sakurai §7.1, eqs. (7.4)–(7.10), §7.5, eqs. (7.56)–(7.62) · Likharev §8.1, eqs. (8.2)–(8.5); parts 1 and 3 for general $\pi$ derived here*

^thm-c12-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* On a product of basis kets, $P_\sigma$ moves the state of particle $i$ to particle $\sigma(i)$, and $P_\pi$ then moves it on to $\pi(\sigma(i))$; so $P_\pi P_\sigma$ and $P_{\pi\sigma}$ agree on a basis, hence everywhere. If $\{|k_a\rangle\}$ is an orthonormal basis of $\mathcal V$, the products $|k_{a_1}\rangle\cdots|k_{a_N}\rangle$ are an orthonormal basis of $\mathcal V^{\otimes N}$ ([[§38 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-80|LADR Thm. 9.80]]) and $P_\pi$ permutes them, so it preserves inner products: it is unitary, and $P_\pi^\dagger = P_\pi^{-1} = P_{\pi^{-1}}$ by the homomorphism property.
>
> *2.* $(i\,j)^2 = \mathrm{id}$ gives $P_{ij}^2 = 1$; unitary and equal to its inverse means Hermitian; then $P_{ij}|\psi\rangle = \lambda|\psi\rangle$ forces $\lambda^2 = 1$. Every permutation is a product of transpositions ([[§12 Multiplying and Conjugating Cycles#^prop-12-2|493 Prop. §12.2]]), and part 1 turns the product into a product of operators: $(1\,2)(1\,3) = (1\,3\,2)$, which is Sakurai's $P_{123}$. The other order gives $(1\,3)(1\,2) = (1\,2\,3)$, a different permutation, so on $|k'\rangle|k''\rangle|k'''\rangle$ with distinct labels $P_{12}P_{13} \ne P_{13}P_{12}$.
>
> *3.* By linearity it suffices to take $A = |a\rangle\langle b|$. Apply $P_\pi A_kP_\pi^{-1}$ to a product ket: $P_\pi^{-1}$ moves the state of particle $\pi(k)$ to particle $k$, $A_k$ acts on it, and $P_\pi$ moves the result back to particle $\pi(k)$, leaving every other factor where it was. That is $A_{\pi(k)}$. A function of particle operators is conjugated factor by factor, $P_\pi F(A_1, \dots)P_\pi^{-1} = F(A_{\pi(1)}, \dots)$, which equals $F$ for all $\pi$ exactly when $F$ is symmetric under relabelling.
>
> *4.* $[P_\pi, H] = 0$ gives $[P_\pi, \mathscr U(t)] = 0$ for $\mathscr U = e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ ([[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-4|Theorem §C3.1.4]]), so $P_\pi\mathscr U|\alpha\rangle = \mathscr UP_\pi|\alpha\rangle = c(\pi)\,\mathscr U|\alpha\rangle$.

> [!remark] Remark: Exchange degeneracy
> Measure, on two particles, a complete set of commuting one-particle observables, and find $k'$ for one and $k''$ for the other ($k' \ne k''$). Every ket $c_1|k'\rangle|k''\rangle + c_2|k''\rangle|k'\rangle$ (7.3) gives these outcomes, so the measurement does not fix the state, unlike the one-particle case ([[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty#^thm-c1-3-2|Theorem §C1.3.2]]). Sakurai calls this **exchange degeneracy**. For three particles with distinct labels the $3! = 6$ kets $P_\pi|k'\rangle|k''\rangle|k'''\rangle$ span a six-dimensional space of indistinguishable alternatives (7.59). Only one combination in it is totally symmetric and only one totally antisymmetric (7.60); the other four have mixed symmetry. The symmetrization postulate below removes the degeneracy by declaring which of these kets are physical.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §7.1, eq. (7.3), §7.5, eqs. (7.59)–(7.60)*

## Indistinguishability and the symmetrization postulate

> [!principle] Principle §C12.1.2: Indistinguishability
> For a system of $N$ identical particles every observable commutes with every permutation operator,
>
> $$
> [P_\pi, A] = 0 \qquad\text{for all observables } A \text{ and all } \pi \in S_N ;
> $$
>
> in particular $[P_\pi, H] = 0$ (7.10), and observables are symmetric functions of the particle labels ([[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^thm-c12-1-1|Theorem §C12.1.1]], 3). Equivalently, $\langle\alpha|A|\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|P_\pi^\dagger AP_\pi|\alpha\rangle$ for every ket and every observable: no measurement distinguishes a state from its permuted state.
>
> *Domain: particles of the same species in the same internal state (electrons; also composite particles such as atoms, if their internal states coincide: Likharev). A label that can be measured, such as a spatial separation that is maintained, makes particles distinguishable in practice although they are identical in kind ([[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^rem-c12-1-4|Remark: Distant particles need not be symmetrized]]).*
> *Source: Sakurai §7.1, eqs. (7.9)–(7.10) · Likharev §8.1, text after eq. (8.3) · the user's series, Part IV, §17.2; the equivalence of the two forms derived here*

^pr-c12-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation of the equivalence
> If $[P_\pi, A] = 0$ then $P_\pi^\dagger AP_\pi = P_\pi^\dagger P_\pi A = A$ by unitarity. Conversely, if $\langle\alpha|(P_\pi^\dagger AP_\pi - A)|\alpha\rangle = 0$ for every ket, the operator $P_\pi^\dagger AP_\pi - A$ vanishes, because an operator on a complex inner-product space whose diagonal matrix elements all vanish is zero ([[§23 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-13|LADR Thm. 7.13]]); then $AP_\pi = P_\pi A$.

*Uses:* [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^thm-c12-1-1|Theorem §C12.1.1]], [[§23 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-13|LADR Thm. 7.13]]

> [!definition] Definition §C12.1.2: Symmetrizer and Antisymmetrizer; Totally Symmetric and Antisymmetric Kets
> On $\mathcal V^{\otimes N}$ the **symmetrizer** and **antisymmetrizer** are
>
> $$
> S = \frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\pi\in S_N}P_\pi , \qquad A = \frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\pi\in S_N}\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\,P_\pi ,
> $$
>
> with $\operatorname{sgn}$ the sign of a permutation ([[§21 The Sign Homomorphism and the Alternating Group#^def-21-2|493 Def. §21.2]]); for $N = 2$ they are $S_{12} = \frac12(1 + P_{12})$ and $A_{12} = \frac12(1 - P_{12})$ (7.12). A ket is **totally symmetric** if $P_{ij}|\alpha\rangle = |\alpha\rangle$ and **totally antisymmetric** if $P_{ij}|\alpha\rangle = -|\alpha\rangle$, for every pair $i \ne j$ (7.16). The totally symmetric kets form the subspace $\operatorname{Sym}^N\mathcal V$, the totally antisymmetric ones $\Lambda^N\mathcal V$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §7.1, eqs. (7.12)–(7.13), §7.2, eq. (7.16); the $N$-particle operators written out here*

^def-c12-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §C12.1.3: Projectors, Dimensions and Normalized Kets
> 1. $P_\pi S = SP_\pi = S$ and $P_\pi A = AP_\pi = \operatorname{sgn}(\pi)A$. $S$ and $A$ are orthogonal projections, $S^2 = S = S^\dagger$, $A^2 = A = A^\dagger$, $SA = AS = 0$, with ranges $\operatorname{Sym}^N\mathcal V$ and $\Lambda^N\mathcal V$. A ket is totally symmetric (antisymmetric) iff $P_\pi|\alpha\rangle = |\alpha\rangle$ ($= \operatorname{sgn}(\pi)|\alpha\rangle$) for every $\pi$.
> 2. For $N = 2$, $S + A = 1$. For $N \ge 3$ and $\dim\mathcal V \ge 2$, $S + A \ne 1$: kets of mixed symmetry exist. If $\dim\mathcal V = d$ is finite,
>
> $$
> \dim\operatorname{Sym}^N\mathcal V = \binom{d + N - 1}{N}, \qquad \dim\Lambda^N\mathcal V = \binom dN, \qquad \dim\mathcal V^{\otimes N} = d^N .
> $$
>
> For two particles with two available states ($d = 2$) this is the count of Sakurai: three boson states, one fermion state, four "classical" (distinguishable) ones (7.18)–(7.20).
> 3. Let $|k_1\rangle, |k_2\rangle, \dots$ be orthonormal and let a product ket contain $|k_i\rangle$ exactly $n_i$ times, $\sum_in_i = N$. The normalized symmetric ket with these occupations is
>
> $$
> \sqrt{\frac{N!}{\prod_in_i!}}\;S\,|k^{(1)}\rangle\cdots|k^{(N)}\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{\prod_in_i!}{N!}}\sum_{\text{distinct arrangements}}|k^{(\cdot)}\rangle\cdots|k^{(\cdot)}\rangle , \qquad (7.63),\ (7.64)
> $$
>
> and if all labels are distinct the normalized antisymmetric ket is
>
> $$
> \sqrt{N!}\;A\,|k^{(1)}\rangle\cdots|k^{(N)}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\begin{vmatrix}|k^{(1)}\rangle & |k^{(2)}\rangle & \cdots & |k^{(N)}\rangle\\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots\\ |k^{(1)}\rangle & |k^{(2)}\rangle & \cdots & |k^{(N)}\rangle\end{vmatrix} , \qquad (7.65)
> $$
>
> the **Slater determinant**, in which the row of an entry is the particle that carries it (level B: [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^def-b7-1-3|Def. §B7.1.3]]). If a label repeats, $A$ annihilates the product: the Pauli principle ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]]). Symmetric (antisymmetric) kets with different occupation numbers are orthogonal, and these kets form orthonormal bases of $\operatorname{Sym}^N\mathcal V$ and $\Lambda^N\mathcal V$, labelled by the occupation numbers $\{n_i\}$ (with $n_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for $\Lambda^N$).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §7.1, eqs. (7.12)–(7.13), §7.2, eqs. (7.16), (7.18)–(7.20), §7.5, eqs. (7.59)–(7.65) · Likharev §8.3, eqs. (8.60), (8.80); the projector properties and dimensions derived here*

^thm-c12-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $P_\sigma S = \frac1{N!}\sum_\pi P_{\sigma\pi} = S$, because $\pi \mapsto \sigma\pi$ is a bijection of $S_N$ ([[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^thm-c12-1-1|Theorem §C12.1.1]], 1); likewise $SP_\sigma = S$. Since the sign is a homomorphism ([[§21 The Sign Homomorphism and the Alternating Group#^thm-21-3|493 Thm. §21.3]]), $\operatorname{sgn}(\pi) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\pi)$, so $P_\sigma A = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\frac{1}{N!}\sum_\pi\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\pi)P_{\sigma\pi} = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)A$. Then $S^2 = \frac1{N!}\sum_\sigma P_\sigma S = S$, $A^2 = \frac1{N!}\sum_\sigma\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)^2A = A$, and $SA = \frac1{N!}\sum_\sigma\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)A = 0$ because even and odd permutations are equally many for $N \ge 2$ ([[§21 The Sign Homomorphism and the Alternating Group#^prop-21-5|493 Prop. §21.5]]). $S^\dagger = \frac1{N!}\sum P_{\pi^{-1}} = S$, and likewise for $A$, since $\operatorname{sgn}(\pi^{-1}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\pi)$. A ket in the range of $S$ is fixed by every $P_\pi$; conversely, if $P_\pi|\alpha\rangle = |\alpha\rangle$ for all $\pi$ then $S|\alpha\rangle = |\alpha\rangle$. Invariance under all transpositions implies invariance under all permutations, since transpositions generate $S_N$ ([[§12 Multiplying and Conjugating Cycles#^prop-12-2|493 Prop. §12.2]]); for antisymmetry a product of $m$ transpositions gives $(-1)^m = \operatorname{sgn}(\pi)$.
>
> *2.* For $N = 2$, $S_{12} + A_{12} = 1$. For $N \ge 3$ take orthonormal $|a\rangle, |b\rangle$ and $|\beta\rangle = |a\rangle|a\rangle|b\rangle|a\rangle\cdots|a\rangle$. Then $A|\beta\rangle = AP_{12}|\beta\rangle = -A|\beta\rangle$, so $A|\beta\rangle = 0$, while $S|\beta\rangle \ne |\beta\rangle$ because $P_{23}|\beta\rangle \ne |\beta\rangle$; hence $(S + A)|\beta\rangle \ne |\beta\rangle$. Dimensions: by part 3 the normalized symmetric kets built from basis vectors are labelled by occupation numbers $(n_1, \dots, n_d)$ with $\sum n_i = N$, i.e. by multisets of size $N$ from $d$ labels, of which there are $\binom{d+N-1}{N}$; antisymmetric ones need distinct labels, i.e. subsets of size $N$: $\binom dN$. For $N = d = 2$: $3$ and $1$.
>
> *3.* In $S|k^{(1)}\rangle\cdots|k^{(N)}\rangle = \frac1{N!}\sum_\pi P_\pi|\cdots\rangle$ each distinct arrangement of the labels occurs $\prod_in_i!$ times (the permutations that only shuffle equal labels), and there are $N!/\prod n_i!$ distinct arrangements, orthonormal by [[§38 Tensor Products#^ladr-9-80|LADR Thm. 9.80]]. Hence $S|\cdots\rangle = \frac{\prod n_i!}{N!}\sum_{\text{distinct}}$ has norm squared $\bigl(\frac{\prod n_i!}{N!}\bigr)^2\frac{N!}{\prod n_i!} = \frac{\prod n_i!}{N!}$, which gives both forms of (7.64). With distinct labels, all $N!$ terms of $A|\cdots\rangle$ are orthonormal, norm squared $N!/(N!)^2$; the signed sum is the Leibniz expansion of the determinant ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-46|LADR Thm. 9.46]]). Two products with different occupation numbers have no arrangement in common, so their symmetrizations are orthogonal; since $S$ (or $A$) maps the product basis onto these kets (up to zero and normalization), they span the range.

> [!principle] Principle §C12.1.4: Symmetrization Postulate
> The physical kets of $N$ identical particles of a given species form one of two subspaces of $\mathcal V^{\otimes N}$:
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Sym}^N\mathcal V \quad\text{(bosons)}\qquad\text{or}\qquad \Lambda^N\mathcal V \quad\text{(fermions)} ,
> $$
>
> the same one for every $N$ and every state of that species; kets of mixed symmetry are never physical (7.16). Which subspace is fixed by the spin (**spin–statistics connection**): particles of half-integer spin are fermions, particles of integer spin are bosons (7.17). For identical particles this replaces the composite-system rule of [[§C1.5 Composite Systems and Tensor Products#^pr-c1-5-1|Principle §C1.5.1]] by its symmetric or antisymmetric part.
>
> *Domain: identical particles in three space dimensions; a composite particle counts as a boson or a fermion according to the number of fermions it contains (the ³He nucleus is a fermion, the ⁴He nucleus a boson; Sakurai). In two space dimensions other exchange phases ("anyons") are possible (Sakurai, footnote 2). In nonrelativistic quantum mechanics the spin–statistics connection is an empirical postulate; in relativistic quantum field theory it is a theorem (Fierz 1939, Pauli 1940; Likharev, footnote 6; [[Quantum Field Theory]], derived there for free fields, [[§C5b.1 Canonical Quantization of the Dirac Field#^pr-c5b-1-3|QFT Principle §C5b.1.3]], the general proof still to be filled).*
> *Source: Sakurai §7.2, eqs. (7.16)–(7.17) and footnote 2 · Likharev §8.1, eqs. (8.7)–(8.11) and footnotes 5–6 · the user's series, Part IV, §17.2*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C12.1.5: Only Two Ways for a Permutation to Act by a Phase
> Let $|\alpha\rangle \ne 0$ satisfy $P_\pi|\alpha\rangle = c(\pi)|\alpha\rangle$ for every $\pi \in S_N$ (a permuted state is the same physical state). Then either $c(\pi) = 1$ for all $\pi$, so $|\alpha\rangle \in \operatorname{Sym}^N\mathcal V$, or $c(\pi) = \operatorname{sgn}(\pi)$ for all $\pi$, so $|\alpha\rangle \in \Lambda^N\mathcal V$. In particular, the exchange of any pair gives the same sign as the exchange of any other pair.
>
> *Source: the user's series, Part IV, §17.2 (statement: "indistinguishability demands physical states be eigenstates", "every species commits globally to one sign"); proof given here*

^thm-c12-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From $P_{\pi\sigma}|\alpha\rangle = P_\pi P_\sigma|\alpha\rangle$ and $|\alpha\rangle \ne 0$, $c(\pi\sigma) = c(\pi)c(\sigma)$: $c$ is a homomorphism from $S_N$ to the nonzero complex numbers, and $|c| = 1$ since $P_\pi$ is unitary. For a transposition $\tau$, $c(\tau)^2 = c(\tau^2) = c(\mathrm{id}) = 1$, so $c(\tau) = \pm1$. Any two transpositions are conjugate, $(k\,l) = \sigma(i\,j)\sigma^{-1}$ for a $\sigma$ with $\sigma(i) = k$, $\sigma(j) = l$ ([[§12 Multiplying and Conjugating Cycles#^prop-12-1|493 Prop. §12.1]]), and the target group is abelian, so $c((k\,l)) = c(\sigma)c((i\,j))c(\sigma)^{-1} = c((i\,j))$: all transpositions share one value $\varepsilon = \pm1$. Writing $\pi$ as a product of $m$ transpositions ([[§12 Multiplying and Conjugating Cycles#^prop-12-2|493 Prop. §12.2]]), $c(\pi) = \varepsilon^m$, which is $1$ if $\varepsilon = 1$ and $(-1)^m = \operatorname{sgn}(\pi)$ if $\varepsilon = -1$ ([[§21 The Sign Homomorphism and the Alternating Group#^thm-21-3|493 Thm. §21.3]]). The conclusion follows from [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^thm-c12-1-3|Theorem §C12.1.3]], 1.

> [!remark] Remark: How the symmetrization requirement changes layer
> - **Same law, new object.** Level B states the requirement on wave functions, pair by pair ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^pr-b7-1-3|Principle §B7.1.3]]). At level C it is a choice of the state space itself. Three things become visible that the wave-function form hides. First, the choice is *consistent*: by [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^pr-c12-1-2|Principle §C12.1.2]] no observable and no time evolution leads out of $\operatorname{Sym}^N\mathcal V$ or $\Lambda^N\mathcal V$, so the rest of $\mathcal V^{\otimes N}$ can be discarded without contradiction. Second, the sign is *global*: if permuted states are the same physical state, [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^thm-c12-1-5|Theorem §C12.1.5]] forces one sign for all pairs, the series' "every species commits globally to one sign". Third, the *empirical content* is the exclusion of mixed symmetry, the four of Sakurai's six three-particle kets that are neither symmetric nor antisymmetric ([[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^rem-c12-1-1|Remark: Exchange degeneracy]]); on them permutations do not act by phases. "It is an empirical fact that a mixed symmetry does not occur" (Sakurai).
> - **Not repeated.** The Pauli principle and the Slater determinant ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]]) are the $\Lambda^N$ case of [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^thm-c12-1-3|Theorem §C12.1.3]], 3, with the same proofs; the occupation numbers that label the bases are those of quantum statistics ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^def-b10-1-1|TH Def. §B10.1.1]]). They become the coordinates of Fock space in [[§C12.2★ Second Quantization]].

> [!remark] Remark: Why the sign follows the spin
> Neither source proves the spin–statistics connection; both give a reason to expect it. Likharev: in free space, exchanging two particles can be viewed as rotating the pair by $\pi$ about an axis; each particle in a state of definite $m_s$ then picks up $e^{\pm i\pi m_s}$, a product $+1$ for integer and $(\pm i)^2 = -1$ for half-integer spin. The series puts the same thought in one line: the sign that a $2\pi$ rotation writes on a spinor ([[§C5.2 Spin One-Half, SU(2), SO(3) and Euler Rotations#^thm-c5-2-3|Theorem §C5.2.3]]) "grows up to become the minus sign of exchange". These are heuristics: a rotation of the pair moves both particles along paths, and identifying its phase with the exchange operator needs more structure than nonrelativistic quantum mechanics has. The proof uses Lorentz invariance and locality of quantum fields ([[Quantum Field Theory]]; for the free spin-0 and spin-½ fields, [[§C5b.1 Canonical Quantization of the Dirac Field#^pr-c5b-1-3|QFT Principle §C5b.1.3]], with the causality argument [[§C5b.7 Wightman Functions, the Anticommutator Function and Microcausality#^thm-c5b-7-5|QFT Theorem §C5b.7.5]]; the general proof is still to be filled there).
>
> *Source: Likharev §8.1, text after eq. (8.11) · the user's series, Part IV, §17.2*

> [!remark] Remark: Distant particles need not be symmetrized
> For two electrons in orbitals $\omega_A$, $\omega_B$ without mutual interaction, the joint density of the (anti)symmetrized spatial state is
>
> $$
> \tfrac12\bigl\{|\omega_A(\mathbf x_1)|^2|\omega_B(\mathbf x_2)|^2 + |\omega_A(\mathbf x_2)|^2|\omega_B(\mathbf x_1)|^2 \pm 2\operatorname{Re}\bigl[\omega_A(\mathbf x_1)\omega_B(\mathbf x_2)\omega_A^*(\mathbf x_2)\omega_B^*(\mathbf x_1)\bigr]\bigr\} , \qquad (7.35)
> $$
>
> the last term being the **exchange density**. If $|\omega_A|^2$ is concentrated in a region $A$ and $|\omega_B|^2$ in a distant region $B$, then for $\mathbf x_1 \in A$, $\mathbf x_2 \in B$ only the first term survives (7.36): the probability of finding one electron near $\mathbf x_1$ and one near $\mathbf x_2$ is $|\omega_A(\mathbf x_1)|^2|\omega_B(\mathbf x_2)|^2\,d^3x_1d^3x_2$ plus the same term with the labels exchanged, exactly the classical answer for one particle in each region. "We never have to worry about the question of antisymmetrization with 10 billion electrons, nor is it necessary to take into account the antisymmetrization requirement between an electron in New York and an electron in Beijing" (Sakurai). The exchange force of level B ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-6|Theorem §B7.1.6]]) is the same exchange term where the orbitals overlap.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §7.3, eqs. (7.34)–(7.36), Fig. 7.3*

^rem-c12-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: A factor of the square root of two
> Forgetting the symmetrization changes numbers that experiments test. Sakurai's example: the conserved-vector-current prediction for the $\beta$–$\alpha$ correlation in the decays $^8\mathrm{Li} \to {}^8\mathrm{Be}^* + e^- + \bar\nu_e$ and $^8\mathrm B \to {}^8\mathrm{Be}^* + e^+ + \nu_e$, with $^8\mathrm{Be}^* \to \alpha + \alpha$, was derived from the electromagnetic reaction $\alpha + \alpha \to {}^8\mathrm{Be}^* \to {}^8\mathrm{Be} + \gamma$. The two $\alpha$ particles are identical bosons, and the first analysis did not symmetrize their wave function; correcting this changed the prediction by a factor $\sqrt2$, and the discrepancy with the measured correlation disappeared (McKeown et al., 1980 and 1982). The normalization factor $1/\sqrt2$ of (7.11a) is the place where such a factor enters.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §7.1, eqs. (7.11), (7.14)–(7.15), Fig. 7.2*

## Two particles with spin

> [!theorem] Theorem §C12.1.6: The Exchange Operator of Two Spins; Exchange Hamiltonian
> 1. For two particles with spin, the exchange operator factorizes into commuting parts that interchange the positions and the spins, $P_{12} = P_{12}^{(\text{space})}P_{12}^{(\text{spin})}$ (7.27); on wave functions $\phi(\mathbf x_1, \mathbf x_2)\chi(m_{s1}, m_{s2})$ they act as $\phi \mapsto \phi(\mathbf x_2, \mathbf x_1)$, $\chi \mapsto \chi(m_{s2}, m_{s1})$ (7.31).
> 2. For two spins one half,
>
> $$
> P_{12}^{(\text{spin})} = \frac12\Bigl(1 + \frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2\Bigr) . \qquad (7.28)
> $$
>
> 3. Two electrons occupy orthonormal orbitals $\omega_A \ne \omega_B$ and interact through a spin-independent $V(|\mathbf x_1 - \mathbf x_2|)$. On the four antisymmetric states built from $\omega_A$, $\omega_B$ and the two spins, the matrix of $V$ is that of the spin operator
>
> $$
> H_{\text{ex}} = I - \frac J2 - \frac{2J}{\hbar^2}\,\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2 ,
> $$
>
> with the direct and exchange integrals $I = \int\!\!\int|\omega_A(\mathbf x_1)|^2V|\omega_B(\mathbf x_2)|^2$ and $J = \int\!\!\int\omega_A(\mathbf x_1)\omega_B(\mathbf x_2)V\omega_A^*(\mathbf x_2)\omega_B^*(\mathbf x_1)$ (7.55): the singlet has $I + J$, the triplet $I - J$ (7.54). A spin-independent interaction acts as if the spins were coupled, with strength set by the overlap of the orbitals.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §7.3, eqs. (7.24)–(7.31), §7.4, eqs. (7.53)–(7.55) and the closing remark on Heisenberg and ferromagnetism; part 3 in this operator form derived here*

^thm-c12-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* On the basis kets $|\mathbf x_1, m_{s1}; \mathbf x_2, m_{s2}\rangle$, $P_{12}$ interchanges both labels (7.25); interchanging the positions and then the spins does the same, and the two operations commute because they act on different factors of $\mathcal V_{\text{space}}^{\otimes2}\otimes\mathcal V_{\text{spin}}^{\otimes2}$.
>
> *2.* $\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2 = \frac12\bigl(\mathbf S^2 - \mathbf S_1^2 - \mathbf S_2^2\bigr)$ equals $\hbar^2/4$ on the triplet and $-3\hbar^2/4$ on the singlet (7.29) ([[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-4|Theorem §B6.3.4]]), so the right side of (7.28) is $+1$ on the triplet and $-1$ on the singlet. $P_{12}^{(\text{spin})}$ has the same eigenvalues there, since the triplet kets are symmetric and the singlet antisymmetric (7.24). Two operators that agree on a basis are equal.
>
> *3.* By [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^pr-c12-1-4|Principle §C12.1.4]], $P_{12} = -1$ on two-electron states; with part 1 and $(P_{12}^{(\text{spin})})^2 = 1$, $P_{12}^{(\text{space})} = -P_{12}^{(\text{spin})}$ there. The four states are $\frac1{\sqrt2}[\omega_A(\mathbf x_1)\omega_B(\mathbf x_2) \pm \omega_A(\mathbf x_2)\omega_B(\mathbf x_1)]$ times the singlet (upper sign) or one of the three triplet kets (lower sign) (7.34). $V$ does not act on spins and commutes with $\mathbf S$, so it has no matrix elements between different spin states; its diagonal elements are $I \pm J$ by the computation behind (7.54), i.e. $I + J\,P_{12}^{(\text{space})} = I - J\,P_{12}^{(\text{spin})}$. Inserting (7.28) gives $H_{\text{ex}}$.

^der-c12-1-6

*Uses:* [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^pr-c12-1-4|Principle §C12.1.4]], [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-4|Theorem §B6.3.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: What is and is not new for two electrons
> That a symmetric spatial state goes with the singlet and an antisymmetric one with the triplet is [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-7|Theorem §B7.1.7]], same result and same argument; the energies $I \pm J$ and the ordering of para- and orthohelium, with $J > 0$ and the triplet lower, are [[§B7.2 Atoms#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]] (Sakurai's Figs. 7.5–7.6 show the same splitting). What [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^thm-c12-1-6|Theorem §C12.1.6]] adds is the reading of the splitting as a spin–spin coupling $-(2J/\hbar^2)\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2$ produced by a Hamiltonian that contains no spin at all. This is Heisenberg's explanation of the spin dependence of helium, and, applied to neighbouring atoms in a solid, the believed origin of ferromagnetism (Sakurai, who adds that ferromagnets are much harder to calculate from first principles).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C12.1.1: Two Identical Spin-One Particles in s States
> Two nonidentical spin-1 particles with no orbital angular momentum can have total angular momentum $j = 0, 1, 2$. What changes if they are identical?
>
> *Exchange symmetry of the coupled spin states.* For two particles of spin $s$, the Clebsch–Gordan symmetry relation $\langle s\,s;m_1\,m_2|s\,s;S\,M\rangle = (-1)^{2s-S}\langle s\,s;m_2\,m_1|s\,s;S\,M\rangle$ ([[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients#^thm-c7-1-4|Theorem §C7.1.4]]) says that $P_{12}^{(\text{spin})}$ acts on the total-spin-$S$ multiplet as $(-1)^{2s-S}$: exchanging $m_1 \leftrightarrow m_2$ in $|S\,M\rangle = \sum\langle\cdots\rangle|m_1\rangle|m_2\rangle$ multiplies it by that sign. For $s = 1$: $S = 2$ and $S = 0$ are symmetric, $S = 1$ is antisymmetric.
>
> *Statistics.* Spin-1 particles are bosons ([[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^pr-c12-1-4|Principle §C12.1.4]]), so the total state is symmetric. With $L = 0$, $j = S$.
> - Both in the *same* s orbital: the spatial factor is symmetric, so the spin factor must be too: $j = 0$ or $j = 2$; $j = 1$ is forbidden. The count checks with [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^thm-c12-1-3|Theorem §C12.1.3]]: $1 + 5 = 6 = \binom{3 + 2 - 1}{2}$, the dimension of $\operatorname{Sym}^2$ of the three-dimensional spin space.
> - In *different* s orbitals (say $1s$ and $2s$): the spatial factor can be symmetrized or antisymmetrized, so $j = 0, 2$ occur with the symmetric and $j = 1$ with the antisymmetric spatial combination. All three values survive, but each $j$ comes with one spatial state instead of two.
>
> (For two spin-$\tfrac12$ particles the same rule $(-1)^{2s-S} = (-1)^{1-S}$ gives the symmetric triplet and antisymmetric singlet, as it must.)
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 7.4 (statement); solved here*

^ex-c12-1-1

> [!example] Example §C12.1.2: Three Spinless Bosons on a Rotating Triangle
> Three identical spin-0 particles sit at the corners of a rigid equilateral triangle, which can rotate about the axis $z$ through its centre, normal to its plane. What values of $J_z$ are allowed?
>
> *Configuration.* With labelled particles, the configuration is fixed by the angle $\varphi$ of particle 1 about $z$; particles 2 and 3 sit at $\varphi + 2\pi/3$ and $\varphi + 4\pi/3$. The state is a wave function $\Phi(\varphi)$, periodic with period $2\pi$, and $J_z = L_z = -i\hbar\,\partial/\partial\varphi$; free rotation has eigenfunctions $e^{im\varphi}$, $m \in \mathbb Z$, with energies $\hbar^2m^2/2I$.
>
> *Symmetry.* The cyclic permutation that moves the state of particle 1 to particle 2, of 2 to 3 and of 3 to 1 produces the configuration with particle 1 at $\varphi - 2\pi/3$: in terms of $\Phi$, $(P_\pi\Phi)(\varphi) = \Phi(\varphi + 2\pi/3)$ for $\pi = (1\,2\,3)$. For bosons $P_\pi\Phi = \Phi$ ([[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^pr-c12-1-4|Principle §C12.1.4]]), so $\Phi(\varphi + 2\pi/3) = \Phi(\varphi)$, and for $\Phi = e^{im\varphi}$ this requires $e^{2\pi im/3} = 1$:
>
> $$
> m = 0, \pm3, \pm6, \dots
> $$
>
> Two thirds of the rotational levels of the distinguishable-particle rotor are missing. (The transpositions reverse the cyclic order of the corners and are not realized by a rotation about $z$, so they add no further condition on $m$ for this planar rotor.) The same mechanism, applied to identical nuclei, removes rotational levels of symmetric molecules.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 7.6 (statement); solved here*
