A crystal is a molecule repeated without end: the same ionic, covalent or dipole bonds of [[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra]] now hold a regular lattice together, and for an ionic crystal the binding energy can be computed from the geometry alone. Metals need a new idea. Their valence electrons belong to no atom but roam the whole crystal; treated as a gas of free particles in a box, but a gas that obeys the exclusion principle, they fill the energy levels up to a high Fermi energy. This explains at once why metals conduct and why their electrons contribute almost nothing to the heat capacity that classical kinetic theory predicts. (As for the whole of Chapter A6, the sources are University Physics and Tipler only.)

## Crystal structures

> [!definition] Definition §A6.2.1: Crystal, Lattice, Unit Cell
> A **crystalline solid** consists of atoms, ions or molecules arranged in a regular pattern, the **lattice**, that repeats over many atomic diameters; the smallest repeating unit is the **unit cell**. A solid with only short-range order, such as glass, is **amorphous**; it softens gradually instead of melting at a definite temperature. Common cubic arrangements of identical atoms are:
> - **simple cubic** (sc): atoms at the corners of the cubic cell;
> - **body-centred cubic** (bcc): an extra atom at the centre of the cube (8 nearest neighbours);
> - **face-centred cubic** (fcc): an extra atom at the centre of each face (12 nearest neighbours).
>
> In the **NaCl structure** Na⁺ and Cl⁻ alternate along all three axes, so that each ion has 6 nearest neighbours of the opposite charge; each kind of ion by itself forms an fcc lattice. In the **CsCl structure** each ion sits at the centre of a cube of 8 ions of the other kind.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.3, Figs. 9.8–9.9; Tipler §10-1*

^def-a6-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §A6.2.2: Madelung Constant
> In an ionic crystal with nearest-neighbour distance $r$, the Coulomb energy of one ion with all the others is written
>
> $$
> U_{\text{coul}} = -\alpha\,\frac{ke^2}{r} ,
> $$
>
> and the dimensionless **Madelung constant** $\alpha$ depends only on the geometry: $\alpha = 1.7476$ for the NaCl structure and $1.7627$ for the CsCl structure.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.3, eqs. (9.14)–(9.18); Tipler §10-1, eqs. (10-1)–(10-2), Table 10-1*

^def-a6-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: Summing the Madelung series
> For NaCl, an ion has 6 opposite neighbours at $r$, 12 like ones at $\sqrt2\,r$, 8 opposite ones at $\sqrt3\,r$, 6 like ones at $2r$, and so on, which suggests $\alpha = 6 - 12/\sqrt2 + 8/\sqrt3 - 6/2 + \cdots$. Summed shell by spherical shell this series does not settle down: distant ions matter and the order of summation matters. Summing over growing neutral cubes converges, slowly, to $1.7476$ (Tipler §10-1).

^cau-a6-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.2.1: Lattice Energy of an Ionic Crystal
> With the exclusion-principle repulsion modelled as $A/r^n$ (as in [[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^mod-a6-1-1|Model §A6.1.1]]), the energy of an ion in the crystal is
>
> $$
> U(r) = -\alpha\frac{ke^2}{r} + \frac{A}{r^n} = -\alpha\frac{ke^2}{r_0}\Bigl[\frac{r_0}{r} - \frac1n\Bigl(\frac{r_0}{r}\Bigr)^n\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> minimal at the equilibrium separation $r_0$, where
>
> $$
> U_{\min} = -\alpha\frac{ke^2}{r_0}\Bigl(1 - \frac1n\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> The **dissociation (lattice) energy**, needed to separate the crystal into free ions, is $U_{\text{diss}} = \alpha\frac{ke^2}{r_0}\bigl(1 - \frac1n\bigr)$ per ion pair. For NaCl, $n \approx 8$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.3, eqs. (9.19)–(9.25); Tipler §10-1, eqs. (10-3)–(10-6)*

^thm-a6-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At equilibrium the force vanishes: $\frac{dU}{dr}\big|_{r_0} = \alpha\frac{ke^2}{r_0^2} - \frac{nA}{r_0^{n+1}} = 0$, so $A = \alpha ke^2 r_0^{n-1}/n$. Substituting $A$ eliminates it in favour of the measurable $r_0$ and gives the bracketed form; at $r = r_0$ the bracket is $1 - 1/n$. Per ion pair: a crystal of $N$ pairs has $2N$ ions, and summing $U$ over all of them counts every pair interaction twice, so the energy of the crystal is $\tfrac12\cdot 2N\cdot U = NU$, that is, $U_{\min}$ per ion pair.

^der-a6-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Kinds of crystal bonding
> - **Ionic crystals** (NaCl, KCl): tightly bound (about 8 eV per ion pair), so hard, high-melting (1000–2000 K), transparent (visible photons, at most about 3 eV, cannot excite their electrons), poor conductors (no free electrons), and often soluble in water, whose large dipole moment breaks the ionic bonds.
> - **Covalent crystals** (diamond, Si, Ge): each atom shares electrons with four neighbours at the corners of a regular tetrahedron, the directed bonds of hybridized orbitals ([[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^rem-a6-1-2|§A6.1]]). Diamond has one of the largest cohesive energies, 7.37 eV per atom, and melts only near 4000 K.
> - **Metallic crystals**: one or two valence electrons per atom move through the whole crystal; the attraction between the positive ion lattice and this electron gas binds it, with 1–3 eV per atom. In lithium, for example, the electrons spread over the lattice sit closer to the ion cores on average than in a free atom, which lowers their potential energy by more than confinement raises their kinetic energy (Tipler §10-1).
> - **Molecular crystals** (ice, solid neon, solid $\mathrm C_{60}$): whole molecules held by dipole–dipole forces, weakly bound and low-melting.
>
> The spacing $r_0$ itself is measured by X-ray diffraction ([[§A7.5 X-Ray Diffraction and Holography#^thm-a7-5-1|WO Theorem §A7.5.1]]) or from the density (Example §A6.2.1).

^rem-a6-2-1

## The free-electron model

> [!model] Model §A6.2.2: Free-Electron Model of a Metal
> The conduction electrons of a metal (about one per atom for monovalent metals) are treated as a gas of non-interacting particles moving freely inside the metal: the potential energy is constant inside and rises to an impenetrable wall at the surface, so each electron is a particle in a three-dimensional box ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]). The electrons obey the exclusion principle: each box state $(n_1, n_2, n_3)$ holds at most two electrons, spin up and spin down.
>
> *Assumptions:* the periodic potential of the ions is replaced by its average; electron–electron interactions are ignored; the surface barrier is infinite (it is not: the photoelectric effect frees electrons). The model cannot say why some solids conduct and others do not; that needs the band theory of [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-4|Theorem §A6.3.4]].
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.4; Tipler §10-3*

^mod-a6-2-2

> [!definition] Definition §A6.2.3: Density of States; Fermi Factor; Fermi Energy
> - The **density of states** $g(E)$ is the number of electron states per unit energy near $E$: $g(E)\,dE$ states lie between $E$ and $E + dE$.
> - The **Fermi factor** $F(E)$ is the probability that a state of energy $E$ is occupied. The number of electrons with energy between $E$ and $E + dE$ is then $n(E)\,dE = g(E)\,F(E)\,dE$.
> - At $T = 0$ the electrons fill the lowest states, two to a box state, up to the **Fermi energy** $E_F$: $F(E) = 1$ for $E < E_F$ and $F(E) = 0$ for $E > E_F$.
> - The **Fermi temperature** is $T_F = E_F/k_B$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.4, eqs. (9.30), (9.32); Tipler §10-3, eq. (10-23)*

^def-a6-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.2.3: Density of States of a Free-Electron Gas
> For free electrons in a volume $V$, counting both spin states,
>
> $$
> g(E) = \frac{\pi V}{2}\Bigl(\frac{8m_e}{h^2}\Bigr)^{3/2} E^{1/2} .
> $$
>
> The number of available states grows as $\sqrt E$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.4, eq. (9.29); Tipler §10-3*

^thm-a6-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take a cube of side $L$, $V = L^3$. By Theorem §A5.1.1 the box states have $E = \frac{h^2}{8m_eL^2}(n_1^2 + n_2^2 + n_3^2)$ (since $\pi^2\hbar^2/2 = h^2/8$). Picture each state as a point $(n_1, n_2, n_3)$ with positive integer coordinates; there is one point per unit volume of this "$n$-space". The states with energy below $E$ lie inside the sphere of radius $R = \sqrt{8m_eL^2E}/h$, in the octant where all $n_i > 0$. For the enormous numbers involved ($R \sim 10^8$ for a centimetre-sized piece) the count is the volume, so with two spin states
>
> $$
> N(E) = 2\cdot\frac18\cdot\frac43\pi R^3 = \frac{\pi}{3}\Bigl(\frac{8m_e}{h^2}\Bigr)^{3/2} V E^{3/2}, \qquad g(E) = \frac{dN}{dE} = \frac{\pi V}{2}\Bigl(\frac{8m_e}{h^2}\Bigr)^{3/2}E^{1/2} .
> $$

^der-a6-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]], [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^mod-a6-2-2|Model §A6.2.2]], [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.2.4: Fermi Energy of a Free-Electron Gas
> $N$ free electrons in a volume $V$ (number density $n_e = N/V$) have at $T = 0$ the Fermi energy
>
> $$
> E_F = \frac{h^2}{8m_e}\Bigl(\frac{3N}{\pi V}\Bigr)^{2/3} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\bigl(3\pi^2 n_e\bigr)^{2/3} ,
> $$
>
> and an average energy $\langle E\rangle = \tfrac35 E_F$. For metals $E_F$ is a few electron-volts (Ag 5.5 eV, Cu 7.0 eV, Al 11.7 eV) and $T_F$ is of order $10^4$–$10^5\ \mathrm K$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.4, eq. (9.31), Table 9.3; Tipler §10-3, eqs. (10-19)–(10-22), Table 10-3*

^thm-a6-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At $T = 0$ every state up to $E_F$ is filled, so $N = N(E_F) = \frac{\pi}{3}\bigl(\frac{8m_e}{h^2}\bigr)^{3/2} V E_F^{3/2}$ (Derivation §A6.2.3); solve for $E_F$. With $h = 2\pi\hbar$ the second form follows. The average energy is
>
> $$
> \langle E\rangle = \frac1N\int_0^{E_F} E\,g(E)\,dE = \frac{\int_0^{E_F}E^{3/2}\,dE}{\int_0^{E_F}E^{1/2}\,dE} = \frac{\tfrac25 E_F^{5/2}}{\tfrac23 E_F^{3/2}} = \frac35 E_F .
> $$

^der-a6-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^thm-a6-2-3|Theorem §A6.2.3]], [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^def-a6-2-3|Def. §A6.2.3]]

![[ph-qm-a6-2-1.svg]]

(a) The Fermi factor is a step at $E_F$ for $T = 0$ (blue) and is rounded over a few $k_BT$ at $k_BT = 0.05\,E_F$ (red; for a real metal at room temperature $k_BT/E_F \approx 0.005$, sharper still). (b) Occupied states $n(E) = g(E)F(E)$: the density of states $g \propto \sqrt E$ (grey dashed) filled up to $E_F$ at $T = 0$ (blue area); at $T > 0$ (red) only electrons within a few $k_BT$ of $E_F$ have moved up.*

> [!remark] Remark: Temperature above zero
> At $T > 0$ the Fermi factor becomes the **Fermi–Dirac distribution** $F(E) = 1/\bigl(e^{(E - E_F)/k_BT} + 1\bigr)$, equal to $\tfrac12$ at $E = E_F$ (Tipler §10-3, §10-6; derived in statistical mechanics, with the chemical potential in place of $E_F$: [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^thm-b10-1-2|TH Theorem §B10.1.2]], [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^rem-b10-1-4|TH Remark: Reading the distributions]]). Since $k_BT \ll E_F$, it differs from the step only within a few $k_BT$ of $E_F$: an electron deep in the distribution cannot absorb an energy of order $k_BT$ from the lattice, because every state that energy could reach is already full. Only the electrons near the top are thermally active. Picture filling a glass with water: the level is $E_F$, and the glass widens upward because $g$ grows as $\sqrt E$ (UP3 §9.4).

^rem-a6-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.2.5: Heat Capacity of the Electron Gas
> The conduction electrons contribute to the molar heat capacity of a metal only
>
> $$
> C_{V,\text{el}} = \frac{\pi^2}{2}\,R\,\frac{T}{T_F} ,
> $$
>
> a small fraction of $R$ at ordinary temperatures (about $0.02\,R$ for copper at $300\ \mathrm K$), increasing linearly with $T$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §10-4, eqs. (10-29)–(10-30)*

^thm-a6-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Idea; the coefficient needs the full Fermi–Dirac distribution and the Sommerfeld expansion ([[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-3|TH Theorem §B13.1.3]], [[§B13.1★ The Degenerate Fermi Gas and Metals#^thm-b13-1-4|TH Theorem §B13.1.4]]).* Heating to $T$ excites only the fraction of order $k_BT/E_F$ of the electrons that lie within $k_BT$ of $E_F$, and each gains energy of order $k_BT$. The energy of $N$ electrons therefore rises above its zero-temperature value $\tfrac35 NE_F$ by about $N(k_BT)^2/E_F$; Sommerfeld's calculation gives $U = \tfrac35 NE_F + \tfrac{\pi^2}{4}N(k_BT)^2/E_F$. Differentiating with respect to $T$ for one mole ($Nk_B = R$): $C_{V,\text{el}} = \tfrac{\pi^2}{2}Nk_B\,k_BT/E_F = \tfrac{\pi^2}{2}R\,T/T_F$. For copper, $T_F = 8.19\times10^4\ \mathrm K$ and $C_{V,\text{el}} = 4.93\times(300/81\,900)\,R = 0.018\,R$.

^der-a6-2-5

*Uses:* [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^thm-a6-2-4|Theorem §A6.2.4]], [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^rem-a6-2-2|Remark in §A6.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Contrast with kinetic theory
> - **Classical electron gas.** If the conduction electrons were a classical ideal gas, equipartition ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-2|TH Theorem §A2.3.2]]) would give each an average kinetic energy $\tfrac32 k_BT$ and a metal $\tfrac32 R$ more heat capacity per mole than an insulator: $3R + \tfrac32 R = \tfrac92 R$. Measured molar heat capacities of metals are close to the Dulong–Petit value $3R$ of the lattice alone ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^thm-a2-3-3|TH Theorem §A2.3.3]]). This puzzle of the classical theory is solved by Theorem §A6.2.5.
> - **Energies and speeds.** Even at absolute zero the average electron energy is $\tfrac35 E_F$, about 4 eV in copper, a hundred times $\tfrac32 k_BT$ at room temperature; a classical gas would need some $30\,000\ \mathrm K$ to match it. The electrons near the top move at the **Fermi speed** $u_F = \sqrt{2E_F/m_e}$, $1.6\times10^6\ \mathrm{m/s}$ for copper, against $1.1\times10^5\ \mathrm{m/s}$ for the classical mean speed at $300\ \mathrm K$ ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^thm-a2-4-2|TH Theorem §A2.4.2]]). The electron gas of a metal is a quantum gas: its behaviour is set by the exclusion principle, not by temperature.

^rem-a6-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Conduction in the quantum free-electron model
> The Drude model ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^mod-a5-2-3|EM Model §A5.2.3]]) gives Ohm's law with $\sigma = ne^2\lambda/(m_e\langle v\rangle)$ but a resistivity several times too large and varying as $\sqrt T$ instead of $T$. The quantum version keeps the formula with two changes (Tipler §10-4). The speed is the Fermi speed $u_F$, since only electrons near $E_F$ can change state. And the mean free path $\lambda$ is not the spacing of the ions: a perfectly periodic lattice does not scatter an electron wave at all, so only departures from perfect order scatter, namely thermal vibrations of the ions and impurities. The vibrations give a scattering area proportional to $T$, hence $\rho \propto T$ as observed ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-2|EM Law §A5.3.2]]), and the measured conductivity of copper implies $\lambda \approx 39\ \mathrm{nm}$, about a hundred ion spacings. Impurities add a temperature-independent part, which is why the resistance of a real metal levels off at low temperature.

^rem-a6-2-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.2.1: The Lattice Energy of Rock Salt
> NaCl has density $2.16\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$ and molar mass $58.4\ \mathrm{g/mol}$ ($23.0 + 35.4$). Find $r_0$ and the lattice energy per mole ($\alpha = 1.7476$, $n = 8$).
>
> Each ion occupies a cube of side $r_0$, so one mole (two moles of ions) fills $2N_Ar_0^3$:
>
> $$
> r_0^3 = \frac{M}{2N_A\rho} = \frac{58.4\ \mathrm g}{2(6.022\times10^{23})(2.16\ \mathrm{g/cm^3})} = 2.245\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{cm^3}, \qquad r_0 = 0.282\ \mathrm{nm} .
> $$
>
> By Theorem §A6.2.1,
>
> $$
> U_{\text{diss}} = 1.7476\cdot\frac{1.440\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}}{0.282\ \mathrm{nm}}\Bigl(1 - \frac18\Bigr) = 7.81\ \mathrm{eV}\ \text{per ion pair} .
> $$
>
> With $1\ \mathrm{eV}$ per pair $= 96.5\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$ this is $753\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$, within 5 % of the measured $787\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$. In the crystal the ions are farther apart (0.282 nm) than in the NaCl molecule (0.236 nm), but each feels many neighbours.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 9.3; Tipler Example 10-1*

^ex-a6-2-1

> [!example] Example §A6.2.2: How Close Are the Levels in a Metal Cube?
> An electron is in a metal cube of side $2.0\ \mathrm{cm}$. Find the lowest energy level and the spacing to the next.
>
> By Theorem §A5.1.1, $E_{111} = 3\pi^2\hbar^2/(2m_eL^2)$:
>
> $$
> E_{111} = \frac{3\pi^2(1.055\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s})^2}{2(9.11\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg})(2.0\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm m)^2} = 4.52\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm J = 2.82\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{eV} .
> $$
>
> The next level, $(2,1,1)$, $(1,2,1)$ and $(1,1,2)$, has $6/3$ times this energy, $5.64\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{eV}$; the spacing is $2.82\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{eV}$. Room-temperature thermal energy, $k_BT = 0.026\ \mathrm{eV}$, is $10^{13}$ times larger. In any macroscopic piece of metal the levels form a continuum for all practical purposes, which is what justifies describing them by a density of states.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 9.4 (recomputed with $\hbar = 1.055\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s}$)*

^ex-a6-2-2

> [!example] Example §A6.2.3: Fermi Energy of Silver
> Silver has density $10.50\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$ and molar mass $107.9\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, and contributes one conduction electron per atom. Find $E_F$ and $T_F$ and compare with $k_BT$ at room temperature.
>
> $n_e = \rho N_A/M = (10.50)(6.022\times10^{23})/107.9\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}} = 5.86\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}} = 5.86\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$. By Theorem §A6.2.4,
>
> $$
> E_F = \frac{(1.055\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s})^2}{2(9.11\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg})}\bigl(3\pi^2\times5.86\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\bigr)^{2/3} = 8.8\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm J = 5.5\ \mathrm{eV},
> $$
>
> and $T_F = E_F/k_B = 6.4\times10^4\ \mathrm K$, far above the melting point of any metal. $E_F/k_BT \approx 210$ at $300\ \mathrm K$: on the scale of the electron gas, room temperature is very cold.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 9.5; Tipler Example 10-6*

^ex-a6-2-3
