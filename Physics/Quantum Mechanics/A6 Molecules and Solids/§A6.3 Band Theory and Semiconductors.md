The resistivity of solids spans more than twenty orders of magnitude, from about $10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot\mathrm m$ for copper to $10^{16}\ \Omega\cdot\mathrm m$ for quartz. The free-electron model of [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model]] cannot explain this, because it ignores the ions. Taking their periodic potential into account, the allowed electron energies group into bands separated by gaps, and whether the highest occupied band is full or partly empty decides between insulator and conductor. Between the two lie the semiconductors, whose conductivity can be tailored by adding impurities; joined together, they make the diodes and transistors of electronics.

## Energy bands

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.3.1: Energy Bands from Atomic Levels
> When $N$ identical atoms are brought together into a solid, each atomic energy level splits into $N$ closely spaced levels, which for $N \sim 10^{23}$ form a practically continuous **energy band**. A band that grows from an atomic subshell of orbital quantum number $l$ has room for $2(2l+1)N$ electrons ($2N$ for an s band, $6N$ for a p band). Bands may be separated by **energy gaps** containing no states, or they may overlap.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.5, Figs. 9.15–9.16; Tipler §10-6*

^thm-a6-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Idea.* For two atoms, the single atomic level splits into the bonding and antibonding levels of [[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]], by an amount that grows as the wave functions overlap more. For four atoms there are four combinations of the atomic wave functions (all in phase, alternating, …), with four energies; for $N$ atoms, $N$ energies spread over a range of width set by the overlap, not by $N$. With $N$ huge the spacing is negligible. Each of the $2l+1$ orbital states of the subshell gives its own family of $N$ levels, and each level holds two electrons by the exclusion principle ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]]), hence $2(2l+1)N$.

^der-a6-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]], [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]]

> [!model] Model §A6.3.2: Electron in a Periodic Potential
> An electron in a crystal of lattice spacing $a$ moves in a potential energy with the period of the lattice, $U(x + a) = U(x)$ (in one dimension); the **Kronig–Penney model** replaces the true $U$ by a periodic row of square wells. The stationary states have the **Bloch form**
>
> $$
> \psi(x) = u_k(x)\,e^{ikx}, \qquad u_k(x + a) = u_k(x) ,
> $$
>
> a free-electron plane wave modulated by a function with the period of the lattice.
>
> *Assumptions:* a perfect, infinite lattice; one electron at a time in the average field of the ions and the other electrons.
>
> *Source: Tipler §10-6, eqs. (10-36)–(10-40); UP3 §9.5, Fig. 9.14*

^mod-a6-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.3.3: Gaps at the Bragg Condition
> In a one-dimensional periodic potential the energy $E(k)$ is close to the free-electron value $\hbar^2k^2/2m_e$ except near
>
> $$
> k = \pm\frac{n\pi}{a}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots,
> $$
>
> where it jumps: no travelling wave exists for energies inside the gap. The allowed ranges of energy are the bands.
>
> *Source: Tipler §10-6, eq. (10-41), Figs. 10-19 and 10-20*

^thm-a6-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Idea; solving the Kronig–Penney model is level B.* Each ion reflects a little of the electron wave. The reflections from successive ions add in phase when the round-trip path $2a$ is a whole number of wavelengths, $2a = n\lambda$: Bragg's law $2a\sin\theta = n\lambda$ ([[§A7.5 X-Ray Diffraction and Holography#^thm-a7-5-1|WO Theorem §A7.5.1]]) at $\theta = 90^\circ$, that is, $k = 2\pi/\lambda = n\pi/a$. There the reflected wave is as strong as the incident one, and the two form standing waves. For $n = 1$ the two possibilities are $\psi_1 \propto \sin(\pi x/a)$, with nodes at the ions, and $\psi_2 \propto \cos(\pi x/a)$, with maxima at the ions. Electron charge piled up at the positive ions has lower potential energy, so $\psi_2$ lies below $\psi_1$; the difference of their energies is the gap, and no state has an energy in between.

^der-a6-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^mod-a6-3-2|Model §A6.3.2]], [[§A7.5 X-Ray Diffraction and Holography#^thm-a7-5-1|WO Theorem §A7.5.1]], [[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A6.3.1: Valence Band, Conduction Band, Band Gap
> The highest band that is filled at $T = 0$ is the **valence band**; the next band above it is the **conduction band**. The energy difference between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band is the **band gap** $E_g$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.5; Tipler §10-6*

^def-a6-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.3.4: Conductors, Insulators and Semiconductors
> A solid conducts electricity well if its highest occupied band is only partly filled, or overlaps an empty band (**conductor**). If the highest occupied band is completely filled and separated from the next by a gap, the solid is an **insulator** when $E_g$ is large (several eV, as in diamond, 5.5 eV) and a **semiconductor** when $E_g$ is about 1 eV or less (Si 1.14 eV, Ge 0.67 eV), so that thermal excitation puts an appreciable number of electrons into the conduction band at room temperature.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.5, Table 9.4; Tipler §10-6, Fig. 10-22, Table 10-5*

^thm-a6-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A field $\vec E$ conducts by giving electrons slightly more energy and momentum in one direction. An electron can take up this small energy only if an empty state lies just above it; by the exclusion principle it cannot move into an occupied one. In a partly filled band there are empty states immediately above the top electrons, so the electron distribution shifts and a current flows. Sodium is an example: its $N$ atoms bring $N$ 3s electrons to a 3s band with room for $2N$, which is half full. Magnesium's 3s band is full, but the empty 3p band overlaps it, so magnesium conducts too. In a completely filled band every state is taken, no electron can change its state, and the band carries no current. Electrons must first be lifted across the gap, with a probability that falls like $e^{-E_g/2k_BT}$ (Example §A6.3.1); for $E_g \sim 1\ \mathrm{eV}$ this is small but not negligible at $300\ \mathrm K$, for $E_g \sim 5\ \mathrm{eV}$ it is zero for all purposes.

^der-a6-3-4

*Uses:* [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]], [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^def-a6-3-1|Def. §A6.3.1]], [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]], [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^rem-a6-2-2|§A6.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Semiconductors get better with heat
> In a metal, raising the temperature only increases the scattering of electrons by lattice vibrations, so the resistivity rises ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-2|EM Law §A5.3.2]]). In a semiconductor it also lifts more electrons across the gap, and this exponential growth in the number of carriers wins: semiconductors have a negative temperature coefficient of resistivity.

^rem-a6-3-1

## Holes and doping

> [!definition] Definition §A6.3.2: Hole; Intrinsic Semiconductor
> An empty state in an otherwise filled valence band is a **hole**. When a neighbouring electron moves into it, the hole moves the opposite way, so a current carried by valence electrons can be described as positive holes moving with the field. A pure semiconductor, in which every conduction electron comes from the valence band, has equal densities of conduction electrons and holes, $n = p$; it is **intrinsic**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.6; Tipler §10-6*

^def-a6-3-2

> [!definition] Definition §A6.3.3: Effective Mass
> Near the edges of a band, an electron responds to forces as a free particle of **effective mass** $m^*$, defined by the curvature of the band:
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{m^*} = \frac{1}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{d^2E}{dk^2} .
> $$
>
> For a free electron $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m_e$ gives $m^* = m_e$; near a gap the curvature is larger, and $m^*$ is typically $0.01$ to $0.2\,m_e$ in semiconductors.
>
> *Source: Tipler §10-6, eq. (10-42)*

^def-a6-3-3

> [!definition] Definition §A6.3.4: Doping; Donors and Acceptors; n- and p-Type
> **Doping** is the controlled replacement of a few atoms of a semiconductor by **impurity atoms** of a neighbouring column. A **donor** has one more valence electron than the host (arsenic or phosphorus in silicon); its extra electron is easily freed into the conduction band, and the doped material is **n-type** (negative carriers). An **acceptor** has one fewer (aluminium or gallium in silicon); it captures an electron from the valence band and leaves a mobile hole, and the material is **p-type**. The carriers supplied by the impurities are the **majority carriers**; those created by thermal excitation across the gap are the **minority carriers**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.6, Figs. 9.19–9.21; Tipler §10-7*

^def-a6-3-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.3.5: Donor Levels Are Hydrogen-Like
> The extra electron of a donor, bound to the singly charged donor ion inside a crystal of dielectric constant $\kappa$, has the levels
>
> $$
> E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \mathrm{eV}}{n^2}\,\frac{m^*/m_e}{\kappa^2}, \qquad \langle r_n\rangle \approx n^2 a_0\,\kappa\,\frac{m_e}{m^*} ,
> $$
>
> measured from the bottom of the conduction band. For silicon these are a few hundredths of an eV below the band, comparable to $k_BT$ at room temperature, so donors are nearly all ionized. Acceptors give similar levels just above the valence band.
>
> *Source: Tipler §10-7, eqs. (10-43)–(10-44); UP3 §9.6*

^thm-a6-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the hydrogen model ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-3|Theorem §A5.1.3]]), $E_n = -\frac{m_e(ke^2)^2}{2\hbar^2n^2}$ and $a_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{m_eke^2}$, and make two replacements. The surrounding crystal is a dielectric, which weakens the Coulomb attraction by $\kappa$: $ke^2 \to ke^2/\kappa$. The electron moves in the periodic potential of the lattice, which near the band edge acts as a change of mass: $m_e \to m^*$ (Def. §A6.3.3). Then $E_n$ is multiplied by $(m^*/m_e)/\kappa^2$ and $a_0$ by $\kappa\,m_e/m^*$. Ionizing the donor puts the electron at the bottom of the conduction band, so that is where $E_\infty = 0$ lies.

^der-a6-3-5

*Uses:* [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-3|Theorem §A5.1.3]], [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^def-a6-3-3|Def. §A6.3.3]], [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^def-a6-3-4|Def. §A6.3.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Do holes really behave as positive particles?
> Yes: the Hall effect shows it. In a strip carrying current in a magnetic field, the carriers are pushed to one edge whatever their sign, and the sign of the transverse Hall voltage reveals the sign of the majority carriers ([[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^thm-a7-5-2|EM Theorem §A7.5.2]], [[§A7.5 The Hall Effect and Applications#^law-a7-5-3|EM Law §A7.5.3]]): negative in n-type, positive in p-type material. The size of $V_H = v_dBw$ gives the drift speed and, with $j = nqv_d$, the carrier density. Doping one part per million can raise the conductivity by orders of magnitude.

^rem-a6-3-2

## Diodes and transistors

> [!definition] Definition §A6.3.5: p–n Junction; Depletion Layer; Bias
> A **p–n junction** is the boundary between p-type and n-type regions of one crystal. Electrons diffuse from the n side into the p side and fill holes there, leaving positive donor ions on the n side and negative acceptor ions on the p side: a thin **depletion layer**, almost free of carriers, whose electric field and potential step stop further diffusion. An external voltage $V_b$ with the positive terminal on the p side is a **forward bias** (it lowers the step); with the positive terminal on the n side it is a **reverse bias** (it raises the step).
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.7, Figs. 9.23–9.24; Tipler §10-8*

^def-a6-3-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.3.6: Current Through a p–n Junction
> The net current through a p–n junction at temperature $T$ with bias voltage $V_b$ (positive for forward bias) is
>
> $$
> I_{\text{net}} = I_0\bigl(e^{eV_b/k_BT} - 1\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> where $I_0$ is the current carried by minority carriers. Forward current grows exponentially; reverse current saturates at $-I_0$, which is tiny. The junction is a **diode**: a one-way valve for current.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.7, eqs. (9.35)–(9.36); Tipler §10-8, eq. (10-49)*

^thm-a6-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $V$ be the potential step at the unbiased junction. Of the $N$ conduction electrons on the n side, only those with energy above $eV$ can climb it; for these high-energy electrons the Fermi–Dirac distribution reduces to the Boltzmann factor ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^rem-a2-4-2|TH §A2.4]]), so their number is about $Ne^{-eV/k_BT}$. A forward bias $V_b$ lowers the step to $V - V_b$, so the majority current becomes proportional to $Ne^{-e(V - V_b)/k_BT} = (Ne^{-eV/k_BT})\,e^{eV_b/k_BT}$, that is, $I_0\,e^{eV_b/k_BT}$, where $I_0$ is its value without bias. The minority carriers (electrons thermally created on the p side, holes on the n side) are swept across by the junction field whatever the bias, giving a constant current $-I_0$; at $V_b = 0$ the two balance. Adding them gives the formula. The same holds for holes, so $I_0$ collects both kinds.

^der-a6-3-6

*Uses:* [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^def-a6-3-5|Def. §A6.3.5]], [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^def-a6-3-4|Def. §A6.3.4]], [[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^rem-a2-4-2|TH §A2.4]]

![[ph-qm-a6-3-2.svg]]

> [!remark] Remark: What junctions do
> - **Breakdown.** A large enough reverse bias tears electrons from bonds or accelerates the few free ones until they knock out others, and the current suddenly rises (Zener and avalanche breakdown); the sharp breakdown voltage is used as a voltage reference.
> - **Light.** A forward-biased junction in which electrons and holes recombine emits light (a **light-emitting diode**); with high current and a resonant cavity formed by the cleaved crystal faces it lases ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^mod-a5-4-8|Model §A5.4.8]]). Run backwards, light with $hf > E_g$ creates electron–hole pairs that the junction field separates: a **solar cell**, giving about 0.6 V.
> - **Tunnel diodes.** In very heavily doped junctions the depletion layer is so thin that electrons tunnel through it ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-6|Theorem §A4.6.6]]).
> - Diodes are the standard example of a non-ohmic element ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-5|EM Law §A5.3.5]]).

^rem-a6-3-3

> [!definition] Definition §A6.3.6: Junction Transistor; Current Gain
> A **junction transistor** is a thin layer of one type of semiconductor, the **base**, between two layers of the other type, the **emitter** (heavily doped) and the **collector**: n–p–n or p–n–p. A small **base current** $I_B$ controls a much larger **collector current** $I_C$,
>
> $$
> I_C = \beta\,I_B ,
> $$
>
> with **current gain** $\beta \gg 1$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.7, eq. (9.37); Tipler §10-8*

^def-a6-3-6

> [!remark] Remark: Amplifiers and switches
> In an n–p–n transistor the forward-biased emitter–base junction injects electrons into the thin base; most of them diffuse across it before recombining and are swept into the collector by the reverse-biased base–collector junction. A small signal at the base thus controls a large current: a microphone's tiny voltage can drive a loudspeaker (an **amplifier**). Driven fully open or shut, the transistor is a switch for digital signals. Integrated circuits pack millions to billions of transistors, with the conducting paths between them, on one chip of silicon.

^rem-a6-3-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.3.1: Why the Size of the Gap Matters
> In a pure semiconductor or insulator the Fermi level lies near the middle of the gap, so a state at the bottom of the conduction band is $E_g/2$ above $E_F$. Compare its occupation probability at $300\ \mathrm K$ ($k_BT = 0.0259\ \mathrm{eV}$) for germanium ($E_g = 0.67\ \mathrm{eV}$), silicon ($1.14\ \mathrm{eV}$) and diamond ($5.48\ \mathrm{eV}$).
>
> With $F = 1/(e^{(E - E_F)/k_BT} + 1) \approx e^{-E_g/2k_BT}$ ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^rem-a6-2-2|§A6.2]]):
>
> | | Ge | Si | diamond |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $E_g/2k_BT$ | $12.9$ | $22.0$ | $106$ |
> | $F$ | $2\times10^{-6}$ | $3\times10^{-10}$ | $10^{-46}$ |
>
> A crystal has some $10^{23}$ valence electrons per cubic centimetre, so in Ge and Si an appreciable number reach the conduction band, while in diamond none do. Warming silicon from $300\ \mathrm K$ to $350\ \mathrm K$ raises its factor by $e^{0.57(1/0.0259 - 1/0.0302)} \approx 22$: this is the steep temperature dependence of semiconductors.
>
> *Source: Tipler §10-6, Table 10-4 (the method); UP3 Table 9.4 (the gaps); numbers computed here*

^ex-a6-3-1

> [!example] Example §A6.3.2: An Arsenic Donor in Silicon
> In silicon, $\kappa = 11.8$ and the electron's effective mass is about $0.2\,m_e$. How far below the conduction band is the donor level of arsenic, and how large is the donor electron's orbit?
>
> By Theorem §A6.3.5 with $n = 1$:
>
> $$
> E_1 = -(13.6\ \mathrm{eV})\,\frac{0.2}{(11.8)^2} = -0.020\ \mathrm{eV}, \qquad \langle r_1\rangle = (0.0529\ \mathrm{nm})\,\frac{11.8}{0.2} = 3.1\ \mathrm{nm} .
> $$
>
> The binding is less than $k_BT = 0.026\ \mathrm{eV}$, so at room temperature the donors give up their electrons. The orbit is about 60 Bohr radii, some 13 interatomic spacings (0.235 nm), and encloses thousands of silicon atoms, which is why treating the crystal as a uniform dielectric is reasonable.
>
> *Source: Tipler §10-7 (eqs. 10-43, 10-44, Table 10-5); UP3 §9.6*

^ex-a6-3-2

> [!example] Example §A6.3.3: A Diode as a Thermometer
> A diode passes $0.45\ \mathrm A$ under a forward bias of $0.12\ \mathrm V$; its reverse saturation current is $2.2\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm A$. What is its temperature?
>
> In reverse bias $I_{\text{net}} \approx -I_0$, so $I_0 = 2.2\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm A$. By Theorem §A6.3.6,
>
> $$
> e^{eV_b/k_BT} = 1 + \frac{I_{\text{net}}}{I_0} = 1 + \frac{0.45}{2.2\times10^{-8}} = 2.05\times10^7, \qquad \frac{eV_b}{k_BT} = \ln(2.05\times10^7) = 16.8 ,
> $$
>
> $$
> T = \frac{eV_b}{16.8\,k_B} = \frac{0.12\ \mathrm{eV}}{16.8\times(8.617\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/K})} = 83\ \mathrm K .
> $$
>
> The forward current at fixed voltage depends exponentially on $T$, which is why diodes serve as temperature sensors (and why their characteristics drift when they heat up).
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 9.6 (numbers recomputed here)*

^ex-a6-3-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The Bloch form says that translating a stationary state by one lattice spacing multiplies it by the phase $e^{ika}$: the state is an eigenvector of the translation operator, with eigenvalue $e^{ika}$ ([[§14 Invariant Subspaces#^ladr-5-5|LADR Def. 5.5]]). Bloch's theorem and the solution of the Kronig–Penney model are solid-state physics beyond level A.

> - Doping and junctions in statistical mechanics: the occupation of a hydrogen-like donor level ([[§B13.2 Semiconductors#^thm-b13-2-5|TH Theorem §B13.2.5]]) and the carrier density of an n-type semiconductor ([[§B13.2 Semiconductors#^thm-b13-2-6|TH Theorem §B13.2.6]]); the built-in potential of a p–n junction from a uniform chemical potential ([[§B13.2 Semiconductors#^thm-b13-2-7|TH Theorem §B13.2.7]]), and the diode law of Theorem §A6.3.6 from the Boltzmann factor ([[§B13.2 Semiconductors#^rem-b13-2-2|TH Remark: Bias, rectification and light]]).
