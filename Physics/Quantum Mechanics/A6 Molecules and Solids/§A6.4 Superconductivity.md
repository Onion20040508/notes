Below a critical temperature, many metals and compounds lose their electrical resistance entirely and push magnetic fields out of their interior. Neither property fits the electron gas of [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model]], in which resistance comes from scattering and can be reduced but never removed. The explanation, found by Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer in 1957, is a collective quantum state: electrons bind into pairs through the vibrations of the lattice, and all the pairs move together. The phenomena are summarized in Electromagnetism ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^rem-a5-3-4|EM, Remark on superconductors]]); this section gives the laws and the quantum picture.

## The superconducting state

> [!definition] Definition §A6.4.1: Superconductor; Critical Temperature
> A **superconductor** is a material whose electrical resistivity drops to zero below a **critical temperature** $T_c$ characteristic of the material. A current in a superconductor with no electric field inside it is a **supercurrent**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.8; Tipler §10-9*

^def-a6-4-1

> [!law] Law §A6.4.1: Zero Resistance
> Below $T_c$ the resistance of a superconductor is zero to the precision of every measurement: currents set up in a superconducting ring persist for years with no source and no measurable decay. Mercury, the first superconductor (Kamerlingh Onnes, 1911), has $T_c = 4.2\ \mathrm K$; known values range from below $0.1\ \mathrm K$ to above $100\ \mathrm K$.
>
> *Valid when: $T < T_c$, applied magnetic field below the critical field (Law §A6.4.4), current below a critical value (which produces the critical field at the surface).*
> *Source: UP3 §9.8, Table 9.5; Tipler §10-9, Fig. 10-44, Table 10-6*

^law-a6-4-1

> [!law] Law §A6.4.2: Meissner Effect
> A superconductor in a weak applied magnetic field has $\vec B = 0$ in its interior: when a sample is cooled through $T_c$ in a field, the field lines are expelled. A superconductor is a perfect diamagnet ([[§A8.6 Magnetism in Matter#^def-a8-6-1|EM Def. §A8.6.1]]); screening supercurrents in a thin surface layer produce a field that cancels the applied one inside. A magnet held above a superconductor is repelled and floats.
>
> *Valid when: $T < T_c$ and $B$ below the critical field $B_c$ (type I) or $B_{c1}$ (type II, Def. §A6.4.2). Discovered by Meissner and Ochsenfeld in 1933.*
> *Source: UP3 §9.8, Fig. 9.29; Tipler §10-9, Fig. 10-46*

^law-a6-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.4.3: A Perfect Conductor Would Trap, Not Expel, the Field
> A material whose only special property was zero resistivity would keep constant whatever magnetic flux passes through it when it became perfectly conducting. The Meissner effect is therefore an independent property, not a consequence of zero resistance; the expulsion of the field, not zero resistance, is the test of superconductivity.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.8, eq. (9.38); Tipler §10-9*

^thm-a6-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In a conductor the current density and field are related by $\vec E = \rho\,\vec j$ ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-1|EM Law §A5.3.1]]). With $\rho = 0$ and $\vec j$ finite, $\vec E = 0$ everywhere inside. By Faraday's law, around any closed loop inside the material $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = -d\Phi_B/dt$ ([[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^pr-a9-1-1|EM Principle §A9.1.1]]), so $d\Phi_B/dt = 0$: the flux through every such loop is frozen at its value when the resistance vanished. A sample cooled in a field would keep the field inside. Real superconductors expel it, so they do something zero resistance alone does not require.

^der-a6-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-1|EM Law §A5.3.1]], [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^pr-a9-1-1|EM Principle §A9.1.1]], [[§A9.1 Faraday's Law#^def-a9-1-1|EM Def. §A9.1.1]]

> [!law] Law §A6.4.4: Critical Magnetic Field
> A magnetic field larger than the **critical field** $B_c(T)$ destroys superconductivity. The critical field vanishes at $T_c$ and grows as the temperature falls, approximately as
>
> $$
> B_c(T) = B_c(0)\Bigl[1 - \Bigl(\frac{T}{T_c}\Bigr)^2\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> *Valid when: $0 \le T \le T_c$; an empirical fit, good to a few per cent for the elements. Values of $B_c(0)$: Al 0.011 T, Hg 0.041 T, Pb 0.080 T, Nb 0.20 T.*
> *Source: UP3 §9.8, eq. (9.39), Table 9.5; Tipler §10-9, Fig. 10-45*

^law-a6-4-4

> [!definition] Definition §A6.4.2: Type I and Type II Superconductors
> A **type I** superconductor (mostly pure elements such as Al, Hg, Pb) shows the complete Meissner effect up to a single critical field $B_c$, which is small (well below 1 T), and is normal above it. A **type II** superconductor (alloys and compounds such as $\mathrm{Nb_3Sn}$, NbTi and the copper-oxide ceramics) has two critical fields: below $B_{c1}$ it expels the field completely; between $B_{c1}$ and $B_{c2}$ it is in a **mixed (vortex) state**, in which the field penetrates along thin filaments of normal material, each carrying one quantum of flux, while the rest stays superconducting with zero resistance; above $B_{c2}$ it is normal. $B_{c2}$ can be tens of tesla.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.8, Fig. 9.31, Table 9.5; Tipler §10-9, Figs. 10-47 to 10-49*

^def-a6-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Why there is a critical field
> Expelling the field means running screening currents, which store the field energy $B^2/2\mu_0$ per unit volume that the excluded field would have had (Tipler §10-9). Superconductivity lowers the energy of the material by only a limited amount; when the cost of keeping the field out exceeds it, the normal state wins. Type II materials lower the cost by letting the field in through vortices, which is why their upper critical fields are so high and why the windings of high-field magnets (MRI scanners, accelerator magnets) are made of them.

^rem-a6-4-1

## The BCS theory

> [!law] Law §A6.4.5: Isotope Effect
> For samples of one element made of different isotopes, the critical temperature depends on the average isotopic mass $M$ as
>
> $$
> M^\alpha\,T_c = \text{constant} ,
> $$
>
> with $\alpha \approx 0.5$ for many elements (Hg 0.50, Pb 0.49) and smaller values for others (Cd 0.32, Os 0.15, $\mathrm{Nb_3Sn}$ 0.08).
>
> *Valid when: conventional (low-temperature) superconductors; discovered in 1950.*
> *Source: Tipler §10-9, eq. (10-55), Table 10-7*

^law-a6-4-5

> [!model] Model §A6.4.6: BCS Theory of Superconductivity
> *Assumptions and picture* (Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer, 1957):
> - An electron moving through the lattice attracts the positive ions near its path and displaces them slightly, leaving a region of extra positive charge (a lattice vibration, a phonon); a second electron is attracted to that region. The net effect is a weak attraction between electrons near the Fermi energy, mediated by the lattice.
> - Below $T_c$ this attraction binds electrons into **Cooper pairs** of opposite spin and opposite momentum. A pair has spin zero and is not restricted by the exclusion principle, so all the pairs occupy one and the same quantum state and move together, like a marching band. A pair is large: its two electrons are some $10^3\ \mathrm{nm}$ apart, and millions of other pairs overlap it.
> - The paired state is separated from the unpaired states by an **energy gap** around $E_F$; breaking one pair costs about
>
> $$
> E_g \approx 3.5\,k_BT_c \quad (\text{at } T = 0),
> $$
>
> of order $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}$. The gap shrinks as $T$ rises and closes at $T_c$.
>
> *Successes:* zero resistance, the Meissner effect, the critical field and its temperature dependence, the isotope effect (the lattice is involved), and the quantization of magnetic flux in units $h/2e$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.8, Figs. 9.32–9.33; Tipler §10-9, eq. (10-56), Example 10-12*

^mod-a6-4-6

> [!remark] Remark: Why the resistance is zero
> In a normal metal, resistance comes from electrons being scattered, one at a time, by lattice vibrations and impurities, exchanging energies of order $k_BT$ ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^rem-a6-2-4|§A6.2]]). In a superconductor the current is carried by the pairs, all with the same total momentum. Scattering that merely exchanges momentum between pairs leaves the total, and so the current, unchanged; the only way to slow the current is to break a pair, which needs at least $E_g$. Below $T_c$ the thermal energy cannot supply this, so the pairs flow unimpeded. This also suggests why the best normal conductors, copper, silver and gold, do not superconduct: their electrons interact so weakly with the lattice that they cannot pair.

^rem-a6-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Flux quanta, high-$T_c$ materials, applications
> - **Flux quantization.** The magnetic flux through a superconducting ring can take only the values $\Phi = n\,h/2e$, with the **flux quantum** $h/2e = 2.07\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{T\cdot m^2}$; each vortex of a type II superconductor carries one. The $2e$ is the charge of a Cooper pair, direct evidence for pairing (Tipler §10-9).
> - **High-temperature superconductors.** In 1986–1987 copper-oxide ceramics were found with $T_c$ up to $92\ \mathrm K$ ($\mathrm{YBa_2Cu_3O_7}$) and later above $130\ \mathrm K$, so that liquid nitrogen ($77\ \mathrm K$) suffices instead of liquid helium ($4.2\ \mathrm K$). They are type II with very high $B_{c2}$, brittle, and not fully explained by BCS theory. The highest $T_c$ of a conventional superconductor at ordinary pressure is $39\ \mathrm K$ ($\mathrm{MgB_2}$, 2001).
> - **Uses:** superconducting magnets for MRI and particle accelerators, sensitive magnetometers based on Josephson junctions (tunnelling of pairs through a thin insulator, [[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-6|Theorem §A4.6.6]]), and lossless cables where cooling is affordable.

^rem-a6-4-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.4.1: Can a Niobium Wire Carry 300 A?
> A niobium wire of radius $0.25\ \mathrm{mm}$ in liquid helium ($4.2\ \mathrm K$) is to carry $300\ \mathrm A$. Does it stay superconducting? ($T_c = 9.3\ \mathrm K$, $B_c(0) = 0.20\ \mathrm T$.)
>
> By Law §A6.4.4 the critical field at $4.2\ \mathrm K$ is $B_c = (0.20\ \mathrm T)\bigl[1 - (4.2/9.3)^2\bigr] = (0.20\ \mathrm T)(0.80) = 0.16\ \mathrm T$. The current's own field at the surface of the wire ([[§A8.4 Ampère's Law#^thm-a8-4-2|EM Theorem §A8.4.2]]) is
>
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a} = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\cdot m/A})(300\ \mathrm A)}{2\pi(2.5\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm m)} = 0.24\ \mathrm T .
> $$
>
> This exceeds $0.16\ \mathrm T$, so superconductivity breaks down at the surface: the wire cannot carry this current even with no external field. A critical field implies a critical current, $I_c = 2\pi aB_c/\mu_0 = 200\ \mathrm A$ here.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 9.7*

^ex-a6-4-1

> [!example] Example §A6.4.2: The Isotope Effect in Mercury
> Mercury with average isotopic mass $199.5\ \mathrm u$ has $T_c = 4.185\ \mathrm K$. Predict $T_c$ for a sample of average mass $203.4\ \mathrm u$, using $\alpha = 0.50$.
>
> By Law §A6.4.5, $T_c' = T_c\,(M/M')^{\alpha} = (4.185\ \mathrm K)(199.5/203.4)^{0.50} = (4.185\ \mathrm K)(0.9904) = 4.145\ \mathrm K$, matching the measured $4.146\ \mathrm K$. A change of 2 % in the mass of the ions, with the electrons untouched, shifts $T_c$ by 1 %: the lattice takes part in superconductivity. For ions vibrating like masses on springs, frequencies scale as $M^{-1/2}$, the same power.
>
> *Source: Tipler §10-9 (data and law)*

^ex-a6-4-2

> [!example] Example §A6.4.3: The Energy Gap of Cadmium
> Cadmium has $T_c = 0.517\ \mathrm K$. Estimate its energy gap and the longest wavelength of light that can break a Cooper pair.
>
> By Model §A6.4.6, $E_g \approx 3.5\,k_BT_c = 3.5(8.617\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/K})(0.517\ \mathrm K) = 1.56\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV}$; the measured value is $1.50\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV}$, 4 % lower. A photon needs at least this energy:
>
> $$
> \lambda_{\max} = \frac{hc}{E_g} = \frac{1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}}{1.56\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV}} = 7.9\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{nm} \approx 8\ \mathrm{mm},
> $$
>
> a microwave. The gap is about $10^4$ times smaller than a semiconductor's band gap, which is why superconductivity survives only at very low temperatures.
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 10-11*

^ex-a6-4-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The flux argument of Theorem §A6.4.3 is Faraday's law in integral form, whose differential form $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial t$ follows by Stokes' theorem ([[§20 Stokes' Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-20-1|452 Thm. §20.1]]); in that form, $\vec E = 0$ inside gives $\partial\vec B/\partial t = 0$ at every interior point.
> - The many-body theory behind Cooper pairing (second quantization, BCS ground state) is level C.
> - Cooper pairs are bosons, and their condensation is compared with Bose–Einstein condensation in [[§B12.1 Bose–Einstein Condensation#^rem-b12-1-4|TH Remark: Real condensates]].
