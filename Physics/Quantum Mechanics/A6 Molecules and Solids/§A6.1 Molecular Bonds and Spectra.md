Atoms bind into molecules because the electrons of the combined system have a lower total energy than those of the separate atoms. Two mechanisms do most of the work: an electron can be handed from one atom to another, leaving two ions that attract (the ionic bond), or a pair of electrons can be shared, concentrating negative charge between the nuclei (the covalent bond). Once bound, a molecule can also rotate and vibrate, and quantum mechanics quantizes both motions; their energy levels, read from infrared and microwave spectra, give the bond length and the stiffness of the bond. PHY 284 ended before this chapter, so Chapter A6 rests on University Physics and Tipler alone. The treatment is qualitative where the full calculation is beyond level A.

## Ionic bonds

> [!definition] Definition §A6.1.1: Ionic, Covalent and van der Waals Bonds
> An **ionic bond** forms when an electron transfers from one atom to another and the resulting positive and negative ions attract. A **covalent bond** forms when two atoms share electrons. A **van der Waals bond** is the much weaker attraction between electric dipoles of molecules, permanent or induced; when it involves hydrogen, as between water molecules, it is a **hydrogen bond**. Many bonds mix mechanisms.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.1; Tipler §9-1 to §9-3*

^def-a6-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A6.1.2: Electron Affinity; Equilibrium Separation; Dissociation Energy
> The **electron affinity** of an atom is the energy released when it accepts an extra electron (3.62 eV for chlorine). The **equilibrium separation** $r_0$ (bond length) of a molecule is the nuclear separation at which its energy is lowest. The **dissociation energy** $U_{\text{diss}}$ is the energy needed to separate the molecule from that state into its constituents (ions or neutral atoms, which must be stated).
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.1, eqs. (9.1)–(9.3); Tipler §9-1*

^def-a6-1-2

> [!model] Model §A6.1.1: Energy of an Ionic Molecule
> For a diatomic ionic molecule such as NaCl, measured from the two neutral atoms at rest far apart, the energy as a function of the ion separation $r$ is
>
> $$
> U(r) = E_{\text{transfer}} - \frac{ke^2}{r} + U_{\text{ex}}(r), \qquad U_{\text{ex}}(r) = \frac{A}{r^n} .
> $$
>
> $E_{\text{transfer}}$ is the ionization energy of the metal atom minus the electron affinity of the other; $-ke^2/r$ is the Coulomb energy of the two ions ($ke^2 = 1.440\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}$); $U_{\text{ex}}$ is the repulsion of the closed electron shells when they overlap, written as an empirical power law whose constants $A$ and $n$ are fitted to data.
>
> *Assumptions:* point-like ions outside the overlap region; zero-point vibrational energy and van der Waals attraction (both small, of opposite sign) neglected.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.1, eq. (9.2); Tipler §9-1, eqs. (9-1)–(9-2)*

^mod-a6-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Where the repulsion comes from
> As the ions approach, the wave functions of their filled core shells begin to overlap. Electrons of the two cores with identical sets of quantum numbers are then in the same region of space, and the exclusion principle ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]]) forces some of them into higher states. The total energy rises steeply: this "exclusion-principle repulsion" is what keeps every molecule except $\mathrm H_2$ from collapsing, whatever its bonding type (Tipler §9-1).

^rem-a6-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.2: Dissociation Energy of an Ionic Molecule
> The molecule forms if the minimum of $U(r)$ is negative. Its energy of formation from neutral atoms and its dissociation energy into ions are
>
> $$
> U_{\text{form}} = E_{\text{transfer}} - \frac{ke^2}{r_0} + U_{\text{ex}}(r_0), \qquad U_{\text{diss}} = -U_{\text{form}} \quad\text{(to neutral atoms)} ,
> $$
>
> where $r_0$ minimizes $U$. Since $E_{\text{transfer}} > 0$ for every pair of atoms, the bond exists only because the Coulomb energy at $r_0$ exceeds the cost of making the ions.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.1, eqs. (9.2)–(9.3), Example 9.1*

^thm-a6-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Follow the energy from two neutral atoms far apart (zero) to the molecule: transferring the electron costs $E_{\text{transfer}}$; bringing the ions from infinity to $r_0$ changes the energy by $-ke^2/r_0$ ([[§A3.1 Electric Potential Energy#^thm-a3-1-2|EM Theorem §A3.1.2]]) plus the repulsion $U_{\text{ex}}(r_0)$. The sum is $U(r_0)$, the minimum of $U$, and separating the molecule again requires $-U(r_0)$.

^der-a6-1-2

## Covalent bonds

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.3: Bonding and Antibonding States of a Double Well
> An electron shared by two identical potential wells has stationary states that are symmetric ($\psi_S$) or antisymmetric ($\psi_A$) about the midpoint. Far apart the two have the same energy; as the wells approach, the level splits: $\psi_S$, which is large between the wells, becomes the lower (**bonding**) state, and $\psi_A$, which vanishes at the midpoint, the higher (**antibonding**) state.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.1, Fig. 9.3; Tipler §9-2, Figs. 9-3 to 9-6*

^thm-a6-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Idea.* The potential is symmetric under reflection through the midpoint, so $\lvert\psi\rvert^2$ must be too, and $\psi$ is symmetric or antisymmetric. Far apart, each is the ground state of one well added to or subtracted from the ground state of the other, with the energy of a single well. In the limit where the wells merge into one twice as wide, $\psi_S$ (no node) turns into the ground state of the wide well and $\psi_A$ (one node at the centre) into its first excited state, which has higher energy ([[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^thm-a4-4-4|Theorem §A4.4.4]]). In between, the energies move apart continuously. For two protons ($\mathrm H_2^+$) the same happens with $e^{-r/a_0}$ around each nucleus: in $\psi_S$ the electron's charge piles up between the protons and pulls them together.

^der-a6-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^thm-a4-4-4|Theorem §A4.4.4]], [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-7|Theorem §A5.1.7]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.4: The Covalent Bond of $\mathrm H_2$
> Two hydrogen atoms bind when both electrons occupy the bonding (space-symmetric) state, which requires their spins to be antiparallel (total spin 0). The molecule then has $r_0 = 0.074\ \mathrm{nm}$ and binding energy $4.5\ \mathrm{eV}$, compared with $r_0 = 0.106\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $2.7\ \mathrm{eV}$ for the one-electron ion $\mathrm H_2^+$. With parallel spins the space part must be antisymmetric, and no bound state exists.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.1; Tipler §9-2, Figs. 9-7 and 9-8*

^thm-a6-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Idea; the energies come from solving the Schrödinger equation, which is beyond level A.* The total wave function of the two electrons must be antisymmetric under exchange ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]]). Write it as a space part times a spin part. A space-symmetric part, the bonding combination of Theorem §A6.1.3 for both electrons, must be paired with an antisymmetric spin part, which is the antiparallel (spin-0) combination; a space-antisymmetric part goes with parallel spins. Only the first concentrates both electrons between the protons, so only it binds. The second electron adds its charge to the bond, which is why $\mathrm H_2$ is shorter and more strongly bound than $\mathrm H_2^+$. At very small $r$ the repulsion of the protons wins, which fixes $r_0$.

^der-a6-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]], [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]], [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^thm-a5-3-1|Theorem §A5.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Saturation, shapes and mixed bonds
> - **Saturation.** A bonding orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins. A third hydrogen atom approaching $\mathrm H_2$ would have to put its electron into the antibonding state, so $\mathrm H_3$ does not form; two helium atoms have four electrons, two of which would be antibonding, so $\mathrm{He}_2$ is not covalently bound. Helium condenses only through the weak van der Waals attraction, at 4.2 K.
> - **Shapes.** Oxygen ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^4$) bonds with the singly occupied $p_x$ and $p_y$ orbitals, which point at right angles ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^rem-a5-1-3|Remark in §A5.1]]), so the H–O–H angle would be $90^\circ$; the repulsion of the two hydrogens opens it to $104.5^\circ$. Using superpositions of degenerate atomic states in bonding is called **hybridization**; it also gives the tetrahedral shape of methane and of the bonds in diamond.
> - **Ionic or covalent?** A purely ionic NaCl molecule would have dipole moment $er_0$; a purely covalent one between identical atoms has none. The measured dipole moment, as a fraction of $er_0$, measures how ionic a bond is (Example §A6.1.2).
> - **Dipole–dipole bonds.** The field of a dipole falls off as $1/r^3$ ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^def-a1-5-1|EM Def. §A1.5.1]]), and a nonpolar molecule acquires an induced dipole ([[§A1.5 Electric Dipoles#^def-a1-5-2|EM Def. §A1.5.2]]); the resulting van der Waals energy varies as $-1/r^6$ and is short-ranged (Tipler §9-3). Hydrogen bonds between water molecules (about 0.5 eV) hold ice together and link the two strands of DNA.

^rem-a6-1-2

## Rotational levels

> [!model] Model §A6.1.5: Rigid Diatomic Rotor
> A diatomic molecule is modelled as two point masses $m_1$, $m_2$ at a fixed separation $r_0$, rotating about an axis through their centre of mass perpendicular to the line joining them. Its moment of inertia is
>
> $$
> I = \mu r_0^2, \qquad \mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\ \ (\text{reduced mass}) .
> $$
>
> *Assumptions:* the bond does not stretch as the molecule rotates (it does slightly at large angular momentum); rotation about the molecular axis carries negligible energy.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.2, eq. (9.8); Tipler §9-4, eqs. (9-16)–(9-17)*

^mod-a6-1-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.6: Rotational Energy Levels
> The rigid rotor has the energy levels
>
> $$
> E_l = l(l+1)\,\frac{\hbar^2}{2I} = l(l+1)\,E_{0r}, \qquad E_{0r} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2I}, \qquad l = 0, 1, 2, \dots,
> $$
>
> each $(2l+1)$-fold degenerate. Photons are absorbed or emitted only with $\Delta l = \pm1$ and only by molecules with a permanent electric dipole moment; adjacent levels differ by
>
> $$
> \Delta E_{l \to l+1} = 2(l+1)\,E_{0r} ,
> $$
>
> so the pure rotational spectrum is a series of equally spaced lines, $2E_{0r}$ apart. Symmetric molecules such as $\mathrm H_2$ and $\mathrm N_2$ have none.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.2, eqs. (9.4)–(9.10); Tipler §9-4, eqs. (9-11)–(9-15)*

^thm-a6-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With the centre of mass at rest, $m_1 r_1 = m_2 r_2$ and $r_1 + r_2 = r_0$ give $r_1 = \frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}r_0$, $r_2 = \frac{m_1}{m_1+m_2}r_0$, so $I = m_1 r_1^2 + m_2 r_2^2 = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1+m_2}r_0^2 = \mu r_0^2$ ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^def-a6-2-1|CM Def. §A6.2.1]]). Classically the rotational energy is $\tfrac12 I\omega^2 = L^2/2I$ with $L = I\omega$ ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-1|CM Theorem §A6.2.1]], [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-3|CM Theorem §A6.6.3]]). The rotor's wave function depends only on the two angles that give the direction of the axis, and the angular part of the Schrödinger equation is the same as for any central potential, so $L^2 = l(l+1)\hbar^2$ ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-4|Theorem §A5.1.4]]); hence $E_l = l(l+1)\hbar^2/2I$. Then $E_{l+1} - E_l = [(l+1)(l+2) - l(l+1)]E_{0r} = 2(l+1)E_{0r}$. The selection rule is that of Theorem §A5.4.2: the photon carries one unit of angular momentum, and it couples to the rotation only through a rotating electric dipole.

^der-a6-1-6

## Vibrational levels

> [!model] Model §A6.1.7: Harmonic Diatomic Oscillator
> Near its minimum the molecular potential energy is approximated by a parabola, $U \approx \tfrac12 K(r - r_0)^2$, as if the two atoms were joined by an ideal spring of force constant $K$. The relative motion is that of one particle of mass $\mu$ on that spring.
>
> *Assumptions:* small vibrations (the real potential is softer at large stretch, so higher levels crowd together); vibration and rotation treated independently.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.2, eq. (9.11); Tipler §9-4, Figs. 9-23 and 9-24*

^mod-a6-1-7

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.8: Vibrational Energy Levels
> The harmonic diatomic oscillator has the equally spaced levels
>
> $$
> E_n = \Bigl(n + \frac12\Bigr)\hbar\omega, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{K}{\mu}}, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots,
> $$
>
> and electric-dipole transitions within one electronic state obey $\Delta n = \pm1$, so a single vibrational absorption frequency $f = \omega/2\pi$ appears, equal to the classical vibration frequency.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.2, eqs. (9.12)–(9.13); Tipler §9-4, eqs. (9-20)–(9-21)*

^thm-a6-1-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The quantum harmonic oscillator of mass $m$ and spring constant $K$ has the levels $(n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega$ with $\omega = \sqrt{K/m}$ ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]). Two bodies on a spring move relative to each other like a single body of mass $\mu$ (Tipler §9-4), so $m \to \mu$, as in the classical result $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|WO Theorem §A1.1.2]]). The selection rule is stated, not derived, here (the matrix elements of $x$ behind it: [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]; its derivation from the time-dependent Schrödinger equation is level C: [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^thm-c9-4-4|Theorem §C9.4.4]], [[§C9.6 The Interaction Picture and the Atom–Field Interaction#^thm-c9-6-8|Theorem §C9.6.8]]).

^der-a6-1-8

*Uses:* [[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^mod-a6-1-7|Model §A6.1.7]], [[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]], [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|WO Theorem §A1.1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Molecular quantum numbers
> In this section $n$ counts vibrational quanta and $l$ rotational ones of the whole molecule; they are not the quantum numbers of an electron (Tipler writes $v$ for the vibrational one). University Physics uses $n$ and $l$ for both, as here.

^cau-a6-1-1

## Vibration–rotation spectra

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.9: Vibration–Rotation Absorption Spectrum
> In infrared absorption from the vibrational ground state ($n = 0 \to 1$) the rotational quantum number must change too, $\Delta l = \pm1$. Starting from rotational level $l$, the absorbed photon energies are
>
> $$
> \Delta E_{l \to l+1} = \hbar\omega + 2(l+1)E_{0r}\quad (l = 0, 1, 2, \dots), \qquad \Delta E_{l \to l-1} = \hbar\omega - 2l\,E_{0r}\quad (l = 1, 2, \dots).
> $$
>
> The spectrum is two branches of lines spaced $2E_{0r}$, separated by a gap of $4E_{0r}$ centred on $\hbar\omega$, where there is no line.
>
> *Source: UP3 §9.2, Figs. 9.5–9.6; Tipler §9-4, eqs. (9-22)–(9-28)*

^thm-a6-1-9

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The initial energy is $\tfrac12\hbar\omega + l(l+1)E_{0r}$ (Theorems §A6.1.6 and §A6.1.8). Going to $n = 1$ and $l + 1$, the final energy is $\tfrac32\hbar\omega + (l+1)(l+2)E_{0r}$; the difference is $\hbar\omega + 2(l+1)E_{0r}$. Going to $l - 1$, the final energy is $\tfrac32\hbar\omega + (l-1)l\,E_{0r}$; the difference is $\hbar\omega - 2l E_{0r}$. The smallest values on the two sides are $\hbar\omega + 2E_{0r}$ (from $l = 0$) and $\hbar\omega - 2E_{0r}$ (from $l = 1$); $l = 0$ cannot go down, so the line at $\hbar\omega$ itself is missing.

^der-a6-1-9

*Uses:* [[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^thm-a6-1-6|Theorem §A6.1.6]], [[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^thm-a6-1-8|Theorem §A6.1.8]]

> [!remark] Remark: Three energy scales
> - **Electronic** transitions: a few eV, visible and ultraviolet light.
> - **Vibrational**: about 0.1 to 0.5 eV (0.27 eV for CO, 0.36 eV for HCl, 0.55 eV for $\mathrm H_2$), infrared.
> - **Rotational**: $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-2}\ \mathrm{eV}$ ($E_{0r} = 1.3\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}$ for HCl, $7.6\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}$ for light $\mathrm H_2$), far infrared and microwaves.
>
> At room temperature $k_BT = 0.026\ \mathrm{eV}$: collisions populate many rotational levels but almost no excited vibrational ones. This is the quantum reason behind the "frozen" degrees of freedom of a diatomic gas ([[§A2.3 Heat Capacity and Equipartition#^rem-a2-3-2|TH §A2.3]]): for $\mathrm H_2$ the first rotational step, $2E_{0r} = 0.015\ \mathrm{eV}$, corresponds to $k_BT$ at about $180\ \mathrm K$, the vibrational step $\hbar\omega = 0.55\ \mathrm{eV}$ to about $6000\ \mathrm K$. Since the scales differ so much, each electronic transition seen at low resolution is a band, which resolves into vibrational and then rotational lines.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.1.1: The Energy Budget of NaCl
> Sodium's ionization energy is $5.14\ \mathrm{eV}$, chlorine's electron affinity $3.62\ \mathrm{eV}$, the equilibrium separation of the NaCl molecule $r_0 = 0.236\ \mathrm{nm}$, and the exclusion-principle repulsion there is $0.32\ \mathrm{eV}$. Find the dissociation energy.
>
> By Theorem §A6.1.2, with $E_{\text{transfer}} = 5.14 - 3.62 = 1.52\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $ke^2/r_0 = (1.440\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm})/(0.236\ \mathrm{nm}) = 6.10\ \mathrm{eV}$,
>
> $$
> U_{\text{form}} = 1.52\ \mathrm{eV} - 6.10\ \mathrm{eV} + 0.32\ \mathrm{eV} = -4.26\ \mathrm{eV}, \qquad U_{\text{diss}} = 4.26\ \mathrm{eV} .
> $$
>
> Making the ions costs 1.5 eV, but their Coulomb attraction at 0.236 nm releases four times as much.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 9.1*

^ex-a6-1-1

> [!example] Example §A6.1.2: How Ionic Is NaCl?
> The measured electric dipole moment of the NaCl molecule is $3.00\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm{C\cdot m}$. Compare it with that of a purely ionic molecule.
>
> A pure ionic bond puts charge $+e$ on Na and $-e$ on Cl, a distance $r_0 = 0.236\ \mathrm{nm}$ apart: $p_{\text{ionic}} = er_0 = (1.60\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm C)(2.36\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm m) = 3.78\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm{C\cdot m}$. The ratio $3.00/3.78 = 0.79$: the bond is about 79 % ionic, and the transferred electron spends part of its time back near the sodium, a 21 % covalent share.
>
> *Source: Tipler §9-2 (Covalent or Ionic?)*

^ex-a6-1-2

> [!example] Example §A6.1.3: Bond Length and Stiffness of CO from Its Spectrum
> The rotational transition $l = 0 \to 1$ of CO absorbs $4.77\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV}$ (wavelength $2.6\ \mathrm{mm}$), and its vibrational frequency is $6.42\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{Hz}$. Find the bond length and the force constant.
>
> *Reduced mass:* $\mu = (12\ \mathrm u)(16\ \mathrm u)/(28\ \mathrm u) = 6.86\ \mathrm u = 1.139\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$.
>
> *Bond length.* By Theorem §A6.1.6, $\Delta E_{0\to1} = 2E_{0r} = \hbar^2/I$, so $I = \hbar^2/\Delta E$ and $r_0 = \sqrt{I/\mu}$:
>
> $$
> I = \frac{(1.055\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s})^2}{(4.77\times10^{-4})(1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm J)} = 1.46\times10^{-46}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}, \qquad r_0 = \sqrt{\frac{1.46\times10^{-46}}{1.139\times10^{-26}}}\ \mathrm m = 0.113\ \mathrm{nm} .
> $$
>
> *Stiffness.* By Theorem §A6.1.8, $K = (2\pi f)^2\mu = (2\pi\times6.42\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})^2(1.139\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}) = 1.85\times10^3\ \mathrm{N/m}$, the stiffness of a strong steel spring on an atomic scale. The vibrational quantum $hf = 0.27\ \mathrm{eV}$ is ten times $k_BT$ at room temperature, the rotational step $4.8\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV}$ some fifty times smaller than $k_BT$.
>
> *Source: Tipler Examples 9-5 and 9-6*
