A circulating electron is a tiny current loop, so an atom with orbital angular momentum is a magnetic dipole. In a magnetic field its energy depends on $m_l$, and the $2l+1$ states of a level, degenerate in [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom]], separate: the Zeeman effect. Atoms with no orbital angular momentum at all are still deflected by an inhomogeneous field, into exactly two beams. This is the evidence, together with the fine structure of spectral lines, for a new property that the Schrödinger equation does not contain: the electron's intrinsic angular momentum, or spin.

## The orbital magnetic moment

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.2.1: Orbital Magnetic Moment
> An electron with orbital angular momentum $\vec L$ has the magnetic dipole moment
>
> $$
> \vec\mu = -\frac{e}{2m_e}\,\vec L ,
> $$
>
> antiparallel to $\vec L$ because the electron's charge is negative. The ratio of $\mu$ to $L$ does not depend on the size of the orbit. More generally, a body of charge $q$ and mass $M$ rotating with angular momentum $\vec L$ has $\vec\mu = (q/2M)\vec L$ whenever the charge-to-mass ratio is the same throughout.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.2, eqs. (8.13)–(8.19); Tipler §7-4, eqs. (7-37)–(7-39)*

^thm-a5-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the electron on a circle of radius $r$ at speed $v$, as in the Bohr model. It passes each point once per period $T = 2\pi r/v$, so the loop carries the current $I = e/T = ev/(2\pi r)$, and the magnetic moment of a plane loop is $\mu = IA$ ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^def-a7-4-1|EM Def. §A7.4.1]]):
>
> $$
> \mu = \frac{ev}{2\pi r}\,\pi r^2 = \frac{evr}{2} .
> $$
>
> For circular motion $\vec r \perp \vec p$, so $L = m_e v r$ and $\mu = (e/2m_e)L$. The current flows opposite to the electron's motion, so by the right-hand rule $\vec\mu$ points opposite to $\vec L$. Neither $r$ nor $v$ survives, which is why the relation is carried over unchanged to quantum mechanics, where there is no orbit; the Zeeman effect below confirms it.

^der-a5-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^def-a7-4-1|EM Def. §A7.4.1]], [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^def-a6-6-1|CM Def. §A6.6.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A5.2.1: Bohr Magneton
> The **Bohr magneton** is the natural unit of atomic magnetic moments,
>
> $$
> \mu_B = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_e} = 9.27\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/T} = 5.79\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/T} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.2; Tipler §7-4, eq. (7-42)*

^def-a5-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.2.2: Quantization of the Orbital Moment
> In a state with quantum numbers $l$, $m_l$,
>
> $$
> \mu = \sqrt{l(l+1)}\,\mu_B, \qquad \mu_z = -m_l\,\mu_B .
> $$
>
> An s state ($l = 0$) has no orbital magnetic moment.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.2, eqs. (8.20)–(8.21); Tipler §7-4, eqs. (7-40)–(7-41)*

^thm-a5-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert $L = \sqrt{l(l+1)}\,\hbar$ and $L_z = m_l\hbar$ ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-4|Theorem §A5.1.4]]) into $\vec\mu = -(e/2m_e)\vec L$: $\mu = (e\hbar/2m_e)\sqrt{l(l+1)}$ and $\mu_z = -(e\hbar/2m_e)\,m_l$. Quantized angular momentum means quantized magnetic moment.

^der-a5-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]], [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-4|Theorem §A5.1.4]], [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^def-a5-2-1|Def. §A5.2.1]]

## The Zeeman effect

> [!definition] Definition §A5.2.2: Zeeman Effect
> The **Zeeman effect** is the splitting of atomic energy levels, and hence of spectral lines, by an external magnetic field.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.2; Tipler §7-5*

^def-a5-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.2.3: Zeeman Splitting of an Orbital Level
> In a uniform field $\vec B = B\hat k$, ignoring spin, a state with magnetic quantum number $m_l$ gains the energy
>
> $$
> U = -\vec\mu\cdot\vec B = m_l\,\mu_B B .
> $$
>
> A level of orbital quantum number $l$ splits into $2l + 1$ equally spaced levels, $\mu_B B$ apart, symmetric about the field-free energy; the splitting is proportional to $B$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.2, eqs. (8.22)–(8.25); Tipler §7-4, eqs. (7-46)–(7-47)*

^thm-a5-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A magnetic dipole in a field has potential energy $U = -\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$ ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-3|EM Theorem §A7.4.3]]). With $\vec B$ along $z$, $U = -\mu_z B = -(-m_l\mu_B)B = m_l\mu_B B$ by Theorem §A5.2.2. The quantum number $m_l$ runs from $-l$ to $l$ in steps of 1, which gives $2l+1$ levels spaced by $\mu_B B$. The field picks out the $z$ axis; without it no direction is preferred and the energy cannot depend on $m_l$.

^der-a5-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-3|EM Theorem §A7.4.3]], [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]

![[ph-qm-a5-2-1.svg]]
*Normal Zeeman effect (schematic, splitting exaggerated). Without a field the 2p → 1s transition gives one line at $f_0$. In a field the 2p level splits into $m_l = +1, 0, -1$, $\mu_B B$ apart, while 1s ($l = 0$) does not; the three transitions (red arrows) give three equally spaced lines, $\Delta f = \mu_B B/h$ apart.*

> [!remark] Remark: Lines, not just levels
> A transition between two split levels does not give every possible combination of sublevels: photons change $m_l$ by $0$ or $\pm1$ only (the selection rule of [[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-2|Theorem §A5.4.2]]). Between an $l = 2$ and an $l = 1$ level, both split, the allowed lines still fall at only three frequencies, $f_0$ and $f_0 \pm \mu_B B/h$, since the spacing is the same in both levels. This three-line pattern, the **normal Zeeman effect**, is what the spinless model predicts. Most atoms show more complicated patterns, because spin contributes to the magnetic moment with a different ratio to angular momentum (Remark on the $g$ factor below); explaining them took the full theory of spin (Tipler §7-5). Measured Zeeman splittings of solar spectral lines map the magnetic field over the Sun's surface (magnetograms).

^rem-a5-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Torque and precession
> In a field the dipole feels the torque $\vec\tau = \vec\mu\times\vec B$ ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-2|EM Theorem §A7.4.2]]). Because the atom also has angular momentum, the torque does not line $\vec\mu$ up with $\vec B$; like a spinning top in gravity, $\vec L$ precesses about the field direction, keeping its angle with $\vec B$, and with it $L_z$, fixed (Tipler §7-4). Turning the moment to a new orientation requires energy, supplied or carried off by a photon.

^rem-a5-2-2

## Electron spin

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.2.4: Force on a Magnetic Moment in a Nonuniform Field
> If $\vec B = B_z(z)\,\hat k$ varies along $z$, a magnetic moment feels the net force
>
> $$
> F_z = \mu_z\,\frac{dB_z}{dz} ,
> $$
>
> along $+z$ or $-z$ according to the sign of $\mu_z$. A uniform field exerts a torque but no net force.
>
> *Source: Tipler §7-4, eqs. (7-50)–(7-51)*

^thm-a5-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The force is minus the gradient of the potential energy $U = -\vec\mu\cdot\vec B = -\mu_z B_z(z)$. With $\mu_z$ fixed (it is quantized and does not change as the atom moves), $F_z = -\partial U/\partial z = \mu_z\,dB_z/dz$, and $F_x = F_y = 0$ since $U$ does not depend on $x$ or $y$.

^der-a5-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-3|EM Theorem §A7.4.3]], [[§4 Partial Derivatives#^def-4-1|452 Def. §4.1]]

> [!law] Law §A5.2.5: The Stern–Gerlach Result
> A collimated beam of neutral silver atoms sent through a magnet whose field $B_z$ increases along $z$ splits into **two** separate beams, deflected equally up and down; there is no undeflected beam and no continuous smear. Hydrogen atoms in their ground state do the same.
>
> *Valid when: atoms whose ground state has zero orbital angular momentum and a single unpaired outer electron (Ag in 1922 by Stern and Gerlach; H in 1927 by Phipps and Taylor), in a field gradient along one axis.*
> *Source: UP3 §8.3; Tipler §7-4 (Exploring: Stern–Gerlach Experiment)*

^law-a5-2-5

> [!remark] Remark: Why two beams were a surprise
> By Theorem §A5.2.4 each atom is deflected in proportion to its $\mu_z$. Classically $\vec\mu$ may point in any direction, so the beam should spread into a continuous band. An orbital moment would give $2l + 1$ beams, an *odd* number, and for the $l = 0$ ground states of silver and hydrogen just one undeflected beam. Two beams fit neither: they require an angular momentum whose $z$-component takes two values.

^rem-a5-2-3

> [!principle] Principle §A5.2.6: Electron Spin
> Every electron carries an intrinsic angular momentum $\vec S$, its **spin**, which is not due to motion through space. Its magnitude and $z$-component are quantized like orbital angular momentum, with the **spin quantum number** $s = \tfrac12$:
>
> $$
> S = \sqrt{s(s+1)}\,\hbar = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\,\hbar, \qquad S_z = m_s\hbar, \qquad m_s = +\tfrac12\ (\text{spin up})\ \text{or}\ -\tfrac12\ (\text{spin down}).
> $$
>
> The spin carries the magnetic moment
>
> $$
> \vec\mu_s = -g_s\,\frac{e}{2m_e}\,\vec S, \qquad g_s \approx 2\ (2.002319), \qquad \text{so}\quad \mu_{s,z} = -g_s m_s\mu_B \approx \mp\mu_B .
> $$
>
> A complete description of an electron in an atom needs five quantum numbers: $n$, $l$, $m_l$, $s = \tfrac12$ (the same for every electron, usually left out) and $m_s$.
>
> *Domain: all electrons, free or bound. In non-relativistic quantum mechanics spin is an added postulate; it follows from Dirac's relativistic wave equation, and $g_s$ beyond 2 from quantum electrodynamics (level C).*
> *Source: UP3 §8.3, eqs. (8.26)–(8.33), Table 8.4; Tipler §7-4, eqs. (7-36), (7-48)–(7-49)*

^pr-a5-2-6

> [!remark] Remark: Why spin is a principle here
> - **History.** In 1925 Pauli proposed a fourth, two-valued quantum number to explain the fine structure of spectra and the periodic table. Goudsmit and Uhlenbeck interpreted it the same year as the $z$-component of an intrinsic angular momentum. The Stern–Gerlach result of 1922, made before spin was imagined, was then understood: the silver atom's angular momentum is that of its one unpaired electron, $2s + 1 = 2$ orientations.
> - **Not a spinning ball.** The name suggests a charged sphere turning on its axis, and the picture is useful for remembering directions, but it fails: $s$ is half an integer while every orbital $l$ is an integer, and $g_s \approx 2$ while a rotating body with uniform charge-to-mass ratio has $g = 1$ (Theorem §A5.2.1). Lorentz objected that an electron spinning fast enough to carry this angular momentum would be inconsistent with special relativity (Tipler §7-4, note 7).
> - **Other particles.** Protons, neutrons and quarks also have spin $\tfrac12$; photons have spin 1. Spin is one of the labels by which particles are classified ([[§A8.1 Particles and Conservation Laws#^def-a8-1-1|Def. §A8.1.1]]).

^rem-a5-2-4

> [!definition] Definition §A5.2.3: g Factor
> The **$g$ factor** of an angular momentum $\vec J$ is the dimensionless number $g$ in
>
> $$
> \vec\mu = -g\,\mu_B\,\frac{\vec J}{\hbar}
> $$
>
> for an electron. Orbital motion has $g_L = 1$ (Theorem §A5.2.1); spin has $g_s \approx 2$. Because the two differ, the total magnetic moment of an atom is not in general parallel to its total angular momentum.
>
> *Source: Tipler §7-4, eqs. (7-43)–(7-45), (7-49)*

^def-a5-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.2.7: Spin States in a Magnetic Field
> In a uniform field $B\hat k$, an electron with zero orbital angular momentum (a free electron, or the 1s electron of hydrogen) has the two energies
>
> $$
> U = -\mu_{s,z}B = g_s m_s\mu_B B \approx \pm\mu_B B ,
> $$
>
> spin up above spin down, separated by $\Delta E = 2\mu_B B$. A photon of frequency $f = 2\mu_B B/h$ flips the spin.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.3, Example 8.4; Tipler §7-4*

^thm-a5-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> As in Theorem §A5.2.3, $U = -\mu_z B$ ([[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-3|EM Theorem §A7.4.3]]), now with $\mu_{s,z} = -g_s m_s\mu_B$ from Principle §A5.2.6. For $m_s = \pm\tfrac12$ and $g_s = 2$, $U = \pm\mu_B B$; the difference is $2\mu_B B$, and the photon energy is $hf = \Delta E$.

^der-a5-2-7

*Uses:* [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^pr-a5-2-6|Principle §A5.2.6]], [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-3|EM Theorem §A7.4.3]], [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]

## Spin–orbit coupling and fine structure

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.2.8: Total Angular Momentum of One Electron
> The orbital and spin angular momenta of an electron add to the **total angular momentum** $\vec J = \vec L + \vec S$, which is quantized in the same way:
>
> $$
> J = \sqrt{j(j+1)}\,\hbar, \qquad J_z = m_j\hbar, \quad m_j = -j, -j+1, \dots, j, \qquad m_j = m_l + m_s ,
> $$
>
> with $j = l + \tfrac12$ or $j = l - \tfrac12$ when $l \ge 1$, and $j = \tfrac12$ when $l = 0$. In the vector picture the two cases are spin "parallel" and "antiparallel" to $\vec L$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §7-5, eqs. (7-52)–(7-55)*

^thm-a5-2-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Idea; the rules for adding angular momenta are proved at level B ([[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]], [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^thm-b6-3-2|Theorem §B6.3.2]], [[§B6.3 Addition of Angular Momenta#^rem-b6-3-1|Remark: What level A assumed, now proved]]).* Take as given that $\vec J$ obeys the same quantization rules as $\vec L$. Components along $z$ add, $J_z = L_z + S_z$, so $m_j = m_l + m_s$. For fixed $l$ there are $2(2l+1)$ combinations of $m_l$ and $m_s$. The largest value, $m_j = l + \tfrac12$, occurs once, so there is a $j = l + \tfrac12$ family with its $2j+1 = 2l+2$ values of $m_j$. That leaves $2(2l+1) - (2l+2) = 2l$ states, whose largest $m_j$ is $l - \tfrac12$; they form a family with $2j+1 = 2l$, so $j = l - \tfrac12$. For $l = 0$ only the first family exists.

^der-a5-2-8

*Uses:* [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-4|Theorem §A5.1.4]], [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^pr-a5-2-6|Principle §A5.2.6]]

> [!definition] Definition §A5.2.4: Spectroscopic Notation for Atomic States
> An atomic state is written $n\,{}^{2s+1}L_j$: $n$ is the principal quantum number, the capital letter S, P, D, F, … gives the (total) orbital quantum number $0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$, the left superscript $2s+1$ the number of spin orientations, and the subscript the total angular momentum quantum number $j$. For one electron $2s+1 = 2$ (a **doublet**): the ground state of hydrogen is $1\,{}^2S_{1/2}$, and its 2p level consists of $2\,{}^2P_{3/2}$ and $2\,{}^2P_{1/2}$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §7-5 (Spectroscopic Notation)*

^def-a5-2-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.2.9: Fine-Structure Splitting
> The interaction of the electron's spin moment with the magnetic field produced by its own orbital motion, **spin–orbit coupling**, splits every level with $l \ge 1$ into two, $j = l + \tfrac12$ and $j = l - \tfrac12$; the state with $j = l + \tfrac12$ (spin parallel to $\vec L$) lies higher. S levels are not split. The splitting is small: about $4.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}$ for the 2p level of hydrogen, about $2\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}$ for the 3p level of sodium.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.3; Tipler §7-5 (Spin–Orbit Coupling)*

^thm-a5-2-9

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Qualitative, in the Bohr picture.* In the electron's rest frame the proton circles the electron in the same sense as the electron circles the proton in the lab, so it forms a current loop whose field $\vec B$ at the electron points along $\vec L$. The spin moment has energy $U = -\vec\mu_s\cdot\vec B$, lowest when $\vec\mu_s$ is along $\vec B$. Since $\vec\mu_s$ is antiparallel to $\vec S$, that is when $\vec S$ is antiparallel to $\vec L$, which is $j = l - \tfrac12$; the parallel arrangement, $j = l + \tfrac12$, lies higher. With $\mu_{s,z} \approx \mp\mu_B$ the two differ by about $2\mu_B B$. For $l = 0$ there is no orbital motion, no field and no splitting. (The full fine structure of hydrogen also contains a relativistic correction of the same size; both are computed at level B: [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-2|Theorem §B8.3.2]], [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-4|Theorem §B8.3.4]], [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]], [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^rem-b8-3-3|Remark: What level B adds to the fine structure of level A]].)

^der-a5-2-9

*Uses:* [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^pr-a5-2-6|Principle §A5.2.6]], [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-8|Theorem §A5.2.8]], [[§A7.4 Torque on a Current Loop#^thm-a7-4-3|EM Theorem §A7.4.3]]

> [!definition] Definition §A5.2.5: Fine and Hyperfine Structure
> **Fine structure** is the splitting of spectral lines into closely spaced components by spin–orbit coupling. **Hyperfine structure** is the much smaller splitting caused by the interaction of the electron's magnetic moment with the magnetic moment of the nucleus (spin–spin coupling); transitions between hyperfine levels are **spin-flip transitions**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.3; Tipler §7-8, §9-6*

^def-a5-2-5

> [!remark] Remark: The 21-cm line and the scale of atomic energies
> - **Hyperfine structure of hydrogen.** The proton has spin $\tfrac12$ and a magnetic moment about a thousand times smaller than the electron's (its mass is larger; Tipler §7-4, note 10). The ground state 1s splits into a level with electron and proton spins aligned and one with them opposed, $5.9\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{eV}$ apart. The spin-flip transition emits a photon of wavelength $\lambda = hc/\Delta E = (1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm})/(5.9\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{eV}) \approx 21\ \mathrm{cm}$, frequency $1.42\ \mathrm{GHz}$. Radio astronomers map the cold hydrogen of the spiral arms of galaxies with this line, which passes through dust; the hydrogen maser runs on it ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^rem-a5-4-4|Remark in §A5.4]]).
> - **Hierarchy.** Gross structure (Bohr levels): electron-volts. Fine structure: $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}$. Zeeman splitting in a laboratory field of $1\ \mathrm T$: $\mu_B B = 5.8\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}$. Hyperfine structure: $10^{-6}\ \mathrm{eV}$. Each layer is resolved only with a better spectrometer.

^rem-a5-2-5

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.2.1: Zeeman Splitting of the Red Hydrogen Line
> In the spinless model, by how much does a field of $1.00\ \mathrm T$ split the Balmer line $\mathrm H_\alpha$ ($\lambda = 656.3\ \mathrm{nm}$)?
>
> By Theorem §A5.2.3 the components differ in photon energy by $\Delta E = \mu_B B = (5.79\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/T})(1.00\ \mathrm T) = 5.79\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}$. From $\lambda = hc/E$, a small energy change shifts the wavelength by $\lvert\Delta\lambda\rvert = \lambda^2\Delta E/hc$:
>
> $$
> \Delta\lambda = \frac{(656.3\ \mathrm{nm})^2(5.79\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV})}{1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}} = 0.020\ \mathrm{nm} .
> $$
>
> The photon energy is $1240/656.3 = 1.89\ \mathrm{eV}$, so the splitting is 3 parts in $10^5$: visible only with a high-resolution spectrometer, and proportional to $B$, which is how it measures fields.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.2 (the splitting); numbers chosen here*

^ex-a5-2-1

> [!example] Example §A5.2.2: Spin Flip in a 1.5 T Field
> A hydrogen atom in its ground state ($l = 0$) sits in a uniform field $B = 1.5\ \mathrm T$. What frequency of radiation flips the electron's spin?
>
> Only spin contributes. By Theorem §A5.2.7, $\Delta E = 2\mu_B B$, so
>
> $$
> f = \frac{2\mu_B B}{h} = \frac{2(5.79\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/T})(1.5\ \mathrm T)}{4.136\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{eV\cdot s}} = 4.2\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{Hz} ,
> $$
>
> a microwave of wavelength $c/f = 7\ \mathrm{mm}$. The frequency is proportional to $B$: doubling the field doubles it. Absorption at this frequency (electron spin resonance) detects unpaired electrons.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 8.4*

^ex-a5-2-2

> [!example] Example §A5.2.3: The Magnetic Field Inside a Hydrogen Atom
> The $2\,{}^2P_{3/2}$ and $2\,{}^2P_{1/2}$ levels of hydrogen are $4.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}$ apart. Estimate the magnetic field that the 2p electron feels from its orbital motion.
>
> By Derivation §A5.2.9 the splitting is about $2\mu_B B$, so
>
> $$
> B \approx \frac{\Delta E}{2\mu_B} = \frac{4.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}}{2(5.79\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/T})} = 0.39\ \mathrm T ,
> $$
>
> nearly $10^4$ times Earth's field. A laboratory field of this size competes with the internal one, which is why the Zeeman patterns of real atoms change character as $B$ grows.
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 7-4*
