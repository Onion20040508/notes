The Bohr model ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen]]) got the energy levels of hydrogen right by postulating quantized circular orbits. The Schrödinger equation, solved in three dimensions for the Coulomb potential of the proton, gives the same energies without any postulate beyond the equation itself, and much more: three quantum numbers instead of one, a quantized angular momentum whose values differ from Bohr's, and probability clouds instead of orbits. This section states the results; their proof is [[· B5 Quantum Mechanics in Three Dimensions|Chapter B5]] ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-1|Theorem §B5.3.1]], [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-2|Theorem §B5.4.2]]). PHY 284 ended with one-dimensional problems (its class 35 review), so this chapter rests on University Physics and Tipler.

## Warm-up: a particle in a cubical box

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.1.1: Particle in a Three-Dimensional Box
> A particle of mass $m$ confined to a cube $0 < x, y, z < L$ (potential energy $0$ inside, infinite outside) has the stationary states
>
> $$
> \psi_{n_1 n_2 n_3}(x,y,z) = \Bigl(\frac{2}{L}\Bigr)^{3/2} \sin\frac{n_1\pi x}{L}\,\sin\frac{n_2\pi y}{L}\,\sin\frac{n_3\pi z}{L}, \qquad E_{n_1 n_2 n_3} = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}\bigl(n_1^2 + n_2^2 + n_3^2\bigr),
> $$
>
> with three independent quantum numbers $n_1, n_2, n_3 = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, one for each coordinate. The ground state is $(1,1,1)$ with $E_{111} = 3E_1$, where $E_1 = \pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2$; the next level $6E_1$ belongs to the three states $(2,1,1)$, $(1,2,1)$, $(1,1,2)$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §7-1, eqs. (7-2)–(7-4); UP3 §9.4, eqs. (9.27)–(9.28)*

^thm-a5-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Inside the box the time-independent Schrödinger equation is $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\bigl(\partial_x^2 + \partial_y^2 + \partial_z^2\bigr)\psi = E\psi$. Try a product $\psi = \psi_1(x)\psi_2(y)\psi_3(z)$ and divide by $\psi$:
>
> $$
> -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\psi_1''}{\psi_1} - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\psi_2''}{\psi_2} - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\psi_3''}{\psi_3} = E .
> $$
>
> The first term depends on $x$ alone, the second on $y$ alone, the third on $z$ alone, and their sum is constant; so each is a constant $\varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 + \varepsilon_3 = E$. Each factor then solves the one-dimensional box problem with $\psi_i = 0$ at the walls, so $\psi_i \propto \sin(n_i\pi x_i/L)$ and $\varepsilon_i = n_i^2\pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2$ ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]). The product of three normalized one-dimensional functions is normalized, since the volume integral factorizes.

^der-a5-1-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]], [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §A5.1.1: Degenerate Level
> An energy level is **degenerate** if more than one independent stationary state has that energy; the number of such states is its **degeneracy**.
>
> *Source: Tipler §7-1*

^def-a5-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Degeneracy comes from symmetry
> The threefold degeneracy of the level $6E_1$ is the symmetry of the cube: exchanging the axes maps one state onto another. In a box with three slightly different edges $L_1, L_2, L_3$ the energy becomes $\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m}\bigl(n_1^2/L_1^2 + n_2^2/L_2^2 + n_3^2/L_3^2\bigr)$ and the three states separate. Breaking a symmetry lifts the degeneracy it causes; an external magnetic field does exactly this to hydrogen in [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-3|Theorem §A5.2.3]]. The cubical box returns in [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^mod-a6-2-2|Model §A6.2.2]] as the model of the conduction electrons of a metal.

## The hydrogen atom in spherical coordinates

> [!model] Model §A5.1.2: Schrödinger Model of the Hydrogen Atom
> The electron (mass $m_e$, charge $-e$) moves in the electric field of a proton held fixed at the origin, with potential energy
>
> $$
> U(r) = -\frac{ke^2}{r}, \qquad k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0},
> $$
>
> and its stationary states obey the three-dimensional time-independent Schrödinger equation
>
> $$
> -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\Bigl(\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial z^2}\Bigr) - \frac{ke^2}{r}\,\psi = E\,\psi .
> $$
>
> *Assumptions:* the proton does not move (it is about 1840 times heavier); the electron is non-relativistic; its spin and all magnetic interactions are ignored (they are added in [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin]]). Because $U$ depends only on $r$, spherical coordinates $(r, \theta, \phi)$ are used: $x = r\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y = r\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z = r\cos\theta$, with $\theta$ the polar angle from the $+z$ axis and $\phi$ the azimuthal angle from the $+x$ axis.
>
> *Refinements:* the motion of the proton is included by replacing $m_e$ with the reduced mass $\mu = m_e/(1 + m_e/M)$, a 0.05 % change for hydrogen, exactly as in the Bohr model ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^thm-a2-2-5|Theorem §A2.2.5]]). A **hydrogen-like ion** (one electron, nuclear charge $Ze$, such as He$^+$) has $U = -Zke^2/r$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.1, eqs. (8.1)–(8.2); Tipler §7-1, eqs. (7-6)–(7-8)*

^mod-a5-1-2

> [!definition] Definition §A5.1.2: Quantum Numbers of Hydrogen; Spectroscopic Notation
> The stationary states of the hydrogen model are labelled $\psi_{nlm_l}$ by three integers:
> - the **principal quantum number** $n$, which fixes the energy;
> - the **orbital angular momentum quantum number** $l$, which fixes the magnitude of the orbital angular momentum;
> - the **magnetic** (or **angular momentum projection**) **quantum number** $m_l$, which fixes its $z$-component.
>
> In **spectroscopic notation** the values $l = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$ are written s, p, d, f, g, … (after the sharp, principal, diffuse and fundamental series of early spectroscopy; then alphabetical), preceded by $n$: the ground state is 1s, the state $n = 2$, $l = 1$ is 2p, and $n = 3$ has the states 3s, 3p, 3d.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.1, Tables 8.2–8.3; Tipler §7-2*

^def-a5-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.1.3: Stationary States and Energies of Hydrogen
> The Schrödinger equation of Model §A5.1.2 has normalizable solutions of the form $\psi_{nlm_l}(r,\theta,\phi) = R_{nl}(r)\,\Theta_{lm_l}(\theta)\,\Phi_{m_l}(\phi)$, with $\Phi_{m_l}(\phi) = e^{im_l\phi}$, exactly for
>
> $$
> n = 1, 2, 3, \dots, \qquad l = 0, 1, \dots, n-1, \qquad m_l = -l, -l+1, \dots, l-1, l,
> $$
>
> and the energy depends on $n$ alone:
>
> $$
> E_n = -\frac{m_e k^2 e^4}{2\hbar^2}\,\frac{1}{n^2} = -\frac{ke^2}{2a_0}\,\frac{1}{n^2} = -\frac{13.6\ \mathrm{eV}}{n^2}, \qquad a_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{m_e ke^2} = 0.0529\ \mathrm{nm}.
> $$
>
> These are the energies of the Bohr model. For a hydrogen-like ion, $E_n = -Z^2(13.6\ \mathrm{eV})/n^2$. An electron in a stationary state does not radiate.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.1, eq. (8.3); Tipler §7-2, eqs. (7-24)–(7-27)*

^thm-a5-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Idea; the full solution is [[· B5 Quantum Mechanics in Three Dimensions|Chapter B5]]: [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-1|Theorem §B5.3.1]], [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-2|Theorem §B5.4.2]].* In spherical coordinates the Schrödinger equation reads
>
> $$
> -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\Bigl[\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Bigl(r^2\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}\Bigr) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\Bigl(\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\theta}\Bigr) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial\phi^2}\Bigr] + U(r)\,\psi = E\,\psi .
> $$
>
> Insert $\psi = R\,\Theta\,\Phi$ and multiply by $2m_e r^2/(\hbar^2 R\Theta\Phi)$:
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\Bigl(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\Bigr) + \frac{2m_e r^2}{\hbar^2}\bigl(E - U(r)\bigr) = -\frac{1}{\Theta\sin\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\Bigl(\sin\theta\,\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\Bigr) - \frac{1}{\Phi\sin^2\theta}\frac{d^2\Phi}{d\phi^2} .
> $$
>
> The left side depends only on $r$, the right side only on the angles, so both equal one constant, called $l(l+1)$ with hindsight. Multiplying the angular equation by $\sin^2\theta$ separates $\phi$ in the same way, with a second constant $m_l^2$. Each ordinary differential equation then admits acceptable solutions only for special values of its constant:
> - $\Phi'' = -m_l^2\Phi$ gives $\Phi = e^{im_l\phi}$; the wave function must return to itself when $\phi$ increases by $2\pi$, so $m_l$ is an integer.
> - The $\theta$ equation has solutions that stay finite at both poles $\theta = 0, \pi$ only if $l$ is a non-negative integer with $l \ge \lvert m_l\rvert$ (the associated Legendre functions; with $\Phi$ they form the spherical harmonics $Y_{lm_l}$).
> - The radial equation is
>
> $$
> -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\Bigl(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\Bigr) + \Bigl[-\frac{ke^2}{r} + \frac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2m_e r^2}\Bigr]R = E\,R ,
> $$
>
> a one-dimensional problem in an effective potential: the Coulomb well plus a centrifugal barrier. As for the finite well ([[§A4.4 The Finite Square Well#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]]), a bound state ($E < 0$) is normalizable only for discrete energies, and these turn out to be $E_n = -ke^2/(2a_0 n^2)$ with $n \ge l + 1$. Theorem §A5.1.7 below checks the simplest case, $n = 1$, by direct substitution.

^der-a5-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^mod-a5-1-2|Model §A5.1.2]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]], [[§33 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-33-1|452 Thm. §33.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the separation shows
> - **Quantum numbers come from boundary conditions**, one per coordinate, exactly as $n_1, n_2, n_3$ did for the box. Here they are interlocked ($\lvert m_l\rvert \le l < n$) because the coordinates are not equivalent.
> - **The angular part is universal.** $U$ enters only the radial equation, so $\Theta$, $\Phi$ and the allowed values of $l$ and $m_l$ are the same for every spherically symmetric potential. This is why the same angular momentum rules hold for the outer electron of sodium, for a whole rotating molecule ([[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^thm-a6-1-6|Theorem §A6.1.6]]) and for nucleons in a nucleus.
> - **Energy independent of $l$ is special to $1/r$.** Classically, the energy of a Kepler ellipse depends only on its major axis, not on its eccentricity; large $l$ corresponds to nearly circular orbits, $l = 0$ to motion straight through the centre. For any other central potential, for example the shielded potential seen by an outer electron in a many-electron atom, $E$ depends on $n$ and $l$ ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^mod-a5-3-3|Model §A5.3.3]]). The energy cannot depend on $m_l$ for any central potential, because no direction in space is preferred.

^rem-a5-1-2

## Angular momentum

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.1.4: Quantization of Orbital Angular Momentum
> In a state $\psi_{nlm_l}$ of any spherically symmetric potential, the orbital angular momentum $\vec L = \vec r \times \vec p$ has the definite magnitude and $z$-component
>
> $$
> L = \sqrt{l(l+1)}\,\hbar, \qquad L_z = m_l\hbar, \qquad l = 0, 1, 2, \dots, \quad m_l = -l, \dots, l .
> $$
>
> For hydrogen, $l \le n - 1$. The $z$ axis can be chosen freely; physically it is the direction singled out by the experiment, for example an applied magnetic field.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.1, eqs. (8.4)–(8.5); Tipler §7-2, eqs. (7-22)–(7-23)*

^thm-a5-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Idea.* The magnitude: classically, for a central force the momentum splits into a radial part $p_r$ and a part perpendicular to $\vec r$ of size $L/r$, so the energy is $E = p_r^2/2m_e + L^2/(2m_e r^2) + U(r)$ (Tipler eq. 7-17). In the Schrödinger equation of Derivation §A5.1.3 the radial derivatives play the role of $p_r^2/2m_e$, and the angular derivatives divided by $r^2$ play the role of $L^2/(2m_e r^2)$: the operator for $L^2$ is $-\hbar^2$ times the angular bracket. The separated angular equation says precisely that this operator applied to $Y_{lm_l}$ gives $l(l+1)\hbar^2\,Y_{lm_l}$, so $L^2 = l(l+1)\hbar^2$ is definite.
>
> The $z$-component: the angle $\phi$ measures rotation about the $z$ axis, and $L_z$ is to $\phi$ what $p_x$ is to $x$: its operator is $-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial\phi$, by the same reasoning that gives $-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial x$ for momentum ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-5|Def. §A3.4.5]]). On $\Phi = e^{im_l\phi}$,
>
> $$
> -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}e^{im_l\phi} = m_l\hbar\,e^{im_l\phi},
> $$
>
> so $L_z = m_l\hbar$. Single-valuedness made $m_l$ an integer, and finiteness at the poles bounded it by $l$.

^der-a5-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-3|Theorem §A5.1.3]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-5|Def. §A3.4.5]], [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^def-a6-6-1|CM Def. §A6.6.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.1.5: Space Quantization
> The angle $\theta$ between $\vec L$ and the $z$ axis can take only the values
>
> $$
> \cos\theta = \frac{L_z}{L} = \frac{m_l}{\sqrt{l(l+1)}} .
> $$
>
> For $l \ge 1$ the vector never points exactly along $\pm z$, since $\lvert m_l\rvert \le l < \sqrt{l(l+1)}$. Only $L$ and $L_z$ are definite: $L_x$ and $L_y$ are not, so $\vec L$ can be pictured anywhere on a cone of half-angle $\theta$ about the $z$ axis.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.1, eqs. (8.6)–(8.8); Tipler §7-2*

^thm-a5-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $L_z = L\cos\theta$ for any vector; insert $L_z = m_l\hbar$ and $L = \sqrt{l(l+1)}\,\hbar$ from Theorem §A5.1.4. If $\vec L$ lay exactly along $z$, then $L_x = L_y = 0$ and all three components would be known at once, which the uncertainty principle for angular momentum forbids except when $L = 0$ (Tipler §7-2; the operator argument is level B: [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-6|Theorem §B2.4.6]]).

^der-a5-1-5

*Uses:* [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-4|Theorem §A5.1.4]], [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.1.6: Degeneracy of the Hydrogen Levels
> The level $E_n$ of the hydrogen model contains $n^2$ states $\psi_{nlm_l}$ (twice as many once electron spin is counted, [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^pr-a5-2-6|Principle §A5.2.6]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.1, Example 8.1; Tipler §7-3*

^thm-a5-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For each $l = 0, \dots, n-1$ there are $2l + 1$ values of $m_l$, so the number of states is $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1) = 2\cdot\frac{(n-1)n}{2} + n = n^2$. For $n = 3$: $1 + 3 + 5 = 9$ states (3s, three 3p, five 3d), all at $E_3 = -13.6\ \mathrm{eV}/9 = -1.51\ \mathrm{eV}$.

## Wave functions and probability

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.1.7: Ground State of Hydrogen
> The ground state ($n = 1$, $l = m_l = 0$, the 1s state) is spherically symmetric:
>
> $$
> \psi_{100}(r) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}}\,e^{-r/a_0}, \qquad E_1 = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e a_0^2} = -\frac{ke^2}{2a_0} = -13.6\ \mathrm{eV} .
> $$
>
> Its orbital angular momentum is zero. The first excited level contains $\psi_{200} \propto (2 - r/a_0)\,e^{-r/2a_0}$ and $\psi_{21m_l} \propto (r/a_0)\,e^{-r/2a_0}$ times $\cos\theta$ ($m_l = 0$) or $\sin\theta\,e^{\pm i\phi}$ ($m_l = \pm1$).
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.1, Table 8.1; Tipler §7-3, eqs. (7-30)–(7-35)*

^thm-a5-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For a function of $r$ alone the Laplacian is $\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\bigl(r^2\frac{d\psi}{dr}\bigr)$. Try $\psi = e^{-r/a}$ with $a$ unknown: $\frac{d\psi}{dr} = -\frac{\psi}{a}$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\Bigl(-\frac{r^2}{a}\psi\Bigr) = \frac{\psi}{a^2} - \frac{2\psi}{ar} .
> $$
>
> The Schrödinger equation becomes $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\bigl(\frac{1}{a^2} - \frac{2}{ar}\bigr)\psi - \frac{ke^2}{r}\psi = E\psi$. It must hold at every $r$, so the $1/r$ terms cancel separately, $\frac{\hbar^2}{m_e a} = ke^2$, which gives $a = \hbar^2/(m_e ke^2) = a_0$; the constant terms give $E = -\hbar^2/(2m_e a_0^2) = -ke^2/(2a_0)$. With $ke^2 = 1.440\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}$ and $a_0 = 0.0529\ \mathrm{nm}$ this is $-13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$.
>
> *Normalization.* With the shell volume $dV = 4\pi r^2\,dr$ and $\int_0^\infty r^2 e^{-br}\,dr = 2/b^3$,
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty e^{-2r/a_0}\,4\pi r^2\,dr = 4\pi\cdot\frac{2}{(2/a_0)^3} = \pi a_0^3 ,
> $$
>
> so the constant in front is $1/\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}$.

^der-a5-1-7

*Uses:* [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^mod-a5-1-2|Model §A5.1.2]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-2|Def. §A3.4.2]], [[§33 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^thm-33-1|452 Thm. §33.1]], [[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^ex-25-3|452 Ex. §25.3]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-1|451 Def. §36.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A5.1.3: Radial Probability Density
> The probability of finding the electron between $r$ and $r + dr$, in any direction, is $P(r)\,dr$; $P(r)$ is the **radial probability density** (a probability per unit length). For a spherically symmetric state ($l = 0$),
>
> $$
> P(r)\,dr = \lvert\psi_{n00}(r)\rvert^2\,4\pi r^2\,dr ;
> $$
>
> in general $P(r) = r^2\lvert R_{nl}(r)\rvert^2$ when the angular factor is normalized over the sphere. The probability of finding the electron in a region is $\int\lvert\psi\rvert^2\,dV$ over that region, and $1$ over all space.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.1, eqs. (8.9)–(8.11); Tipler §7-3, eq. (7-32)*

^def-a5-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.1.8: Radial Distribution of the Ground State
> In the ground state,
>
> $$
> P(r) = \frac{4}{a_0^3}\,r^2 e^{-2r/a_0} ,
> $$
>
> which is largest at $r = a_0$, the radius of the first Bohr orbit, while the mean distance is $\langle r\rangle = \tfrac32 a_0$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.1, eq. (8.12); Tipler §7-3*

^thm-a5-1-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $P(r) = \lvert\psi_{100}\rvert^2\,4\pi r^2 = \frac{4\pi r^2}{\pi a_0^3}e^{-2r/a_0}$. Then $\frac{dP}{dr} = \frac{4}{a_0^3}\bigl(2r - \frac{2r^2}{a_0}\bigr)e^{-2r/a_0}$ vanishes for $r > 0$ only at $r = a_0$. For the mean, use $\int_0^\infty r^3 e^{-br}\,dr = 3!/b^4$ (repeated integration by parts):
>
> $$
> \langle r\rangle = \int_0^\infty r\,P(r)\,dr = \frac{4}{a_0^3}\cdot\frac{6}{(2/a_0)^4} = \frac{3}{2}a_0 .
> $$
>
> The mean exceeds the most probable value because $P$ has a long tail at large $r$.

> [!remark] Remark: Excited states and orbitals
> - **Radial structure.** $P(r)$ for the state $(n, l)$ has $n - l$ maxima. The state with the largest $l = n - 1$ has a single maximum at $n^2 a_0$, the radius of the $n$th Bohr orbit; smaller $l$ means more maxima, and the innermost one lies close to the nucleus. Low-$l$ electrons therefore *penetrate* the inner region, which decides the order in which subshells fill in many-electron atoms ([[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^rem-a5-3-2|Remark in §A5.3]]).
> - **Angular shape.** For $l = 0$, $\lvert\psi\rvert^2$ is spherically symmetric. For $l = 1$, $m_l = 0$ it is proportional to $\cos^2\theta$, a dumbbell along $z$; for $m_l = \pm1$ to $\sin^2\theta$, a doughnut around $z$. The angular shape depends only on $l$ and $m_l$, not on $n$.
> - **Real orbitals.** Because the three 2p states have the same energy, any superposition of them is again a stationary state; suitable combinations give the real orbitals $p_x$, $p_y$, $p_z$, dumbbells along the three axes. These directed orbitals are the starting point of chemical bonding ([[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^rem-a6-1-2|Remark in §A6.1]]).

^rem-a5-1-3

> [!definition] Definition §A5.1.4: Atomic Orbital
> An **atomic orbital** is the region of space that encloses a given large fraction, usually 90 %, of the electron's probability in a particular state; it is the "probability cloud" drawn in pictures of atoms.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.1*

^def-a5-1-4

## Schrödinger and Bohr compared

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from the Bohr model
> - **Energies:** identical, $E_n = -13.6\ \mathrm{eV}/n^2$, but now with $n^2$ states per level instead of one.
> - **Angular momentum:** Bohr postulated $L = n\hbar$; the Schrödinger equation gives $L = \sqrt{l(l+1)}\,\hbar$ with $l < n$. In the ground state $L = 0$, not $\hbar$.
> - **Position:** there is no orbit. The electron of the ground state can be found at any distance; $a_0$ is only the most probable one (Theorem §A5.1.8), and the uncertainty principle forbids following a path from one place to the next.
> - **What survives:** the Bohr radii reappear as the peaks of the $l = n - 1$ distributions, and a stationary state still does not radiate.
>
> Why Bohr's model worked as well as it did, and where it fails, is discussed in [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^rem-a2-2-5|Remark: Successes of the model]] and [[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^rem-a2-2-6|Remark: Limits]].

^rem-a5-1-4

> [!caution] Caution: $L_z$ never equals $L$
> It is tempting to say that in the state $m_l = l$ the angular momentum "points along $z$". It does not: $L_z = l\hbar < \sqrt{l(l+1)}\,\hbar = L$. Only as $l \to \infty$ does the smallest angle, $\cos^{-1}\bigl(l/\sqrt{l(l+1)}\bigr)$, go to zero, and the many allowed directions fill in to the continuum of classical physics (the correspondence principle).

^cau-a5-1-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.1.1: Allowed Directions for $l = 2$
> For a state with $l = 2$, find $L$, the possible values of $L_z$ and the angles between $\vec L$ and the $z$ axis.
>
> By Theorem §A5.1.4, $L = \sqrt{2\cdot 3}\,\hbar = \sqrt6\,\hbar = 2.45\,\hbar$ and $m_l = 0, \pm1, \pm2$, so $L_z = 0, \pm\hbar, \pm2\hbar$. By Theorem §A5.1.5, $\cos\theta = m_l/\sqrt6$:
>
> | $m_l$ | $2$ | $1$ | $0$ | $-1$ | $-2$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $\cos\theta$ | $0.816$ | $0.408$ | $0$ | $-0.408$ | $-0.816$ |
> | $\theta$ | $35.3^\circ$ | $65.9^\circ$ | $90^\circ$ | $114.1^\circ$ | $144.7^\circ$ |
>
> Only five cones are allowed, and none of them is the $z$ axis itself (figure above).
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 7-1; UP3 Example 8.2 (the case $l = 1$)*

^ex-a5-1-1

> [!example] Example §A5.1.2: Is the Electron Inside the Bohr Radius?
> In the ground state, find the probability that the electron is closer to the proton than $a_0$, and the probability that it is farther than $2a_0$.
>
> With $x = 2r/a_0$, $P(r)\,dr = \tfrac12 x^2 e^{-x}\,dx$, and $\int_0^X \tfrac12 x^2 e^{-x}\,dx = 1 - e^{-X}\bigl(1 + X + \tfrac12 X^2\bigr)$. For $r < a_0$ ($X = 2$):
>
> $$
> P(r < a_0) = 1 - 5e^{-2} = 1 - 0.677 = 0.323 .
> $$
>
> For $r > 2a_0$ ($X = 4$): $P = e^{-4}(1 + 4 + 8) = 13e^{-4} = 0.238$.
>
> Although $a_0$ is the most probable distance, the electron is inside it only a third of the time and beyond twice it nearly a quarter of the time: the "size" of the atom is a spread, not a radius.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.1, eq. (8.12); Tipler §7-3 (the distribution); numbers computed here*
