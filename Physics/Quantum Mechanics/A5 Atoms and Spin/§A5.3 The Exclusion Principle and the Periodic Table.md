Why do the 29 electrons of copper not all crowd into the 1s state, the lowest one? Classically nothing would stop them. Two quantum facts do: electrons are identical, so that exchanging two of them cannot change anything observable, and they obey the exclusion principle, so that no two of them can be in the same state. With the hydrogen-like states of [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom]] and the spin of [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin]], this one principle builds the shell structure of atoms and the layout of the periodic table.

## Identical particles

> [!definition] Definition §A5.3.1: Identical Particles; Exchange Symmetry
> Particles are **identical** if no measurement can tell them apart (all electrons are identical). A wave function $\psi(x_1, x_2)$ of two particles is **symmetric** under exchange if $\psi(x_2, x_1) = +\psi(x_1, x_2)$ and **antisymmetric** if $\psi(x_2, x_1) = -\psi(x_1, x_2)$, where each $x_i$ stands for all the coordinates (and spin) of particle $i$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §7-6; UP3 §9.1 (exchange symmetry)*

^def-a5-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.3.1: Wave Functions of Two Identical Particles
> The wave function of two identical particles must be symmetric or antisymmetric under exchange. For two non-interacting identical particles in single-particle states $\psi_n$ and $\psi_m$ the acceptable combinations are
>
> $$
> \psi_S = C\bigl[\psi_n(x_1)\psi_m(x_2) + \psi_n(x_2)\psi_m(x_1)\bigr], \qquad \psi_A = C\bigl[\psi_n(x_1)\psi_m(x_2) - \psi_n(x_2)\psi_m(x_1)\bigr],
> $$
>
> and the antisymmetric one vanishes identically when $n = m$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §7-6, eqs. (7-57)–(7-61)*

^thm-a5-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Idea.* Labels "1" and "2" on identical particles are bookkeeping only, so the probability density cannot depend on them: $\lvert\psi(x_2,x_1)\rvert^2 = \lvert\psi(x_1,x_2)\rvert^2$. Then $\psi(x_2,x_1) = e^{i\alpha}\psi(x_1,x_2)$ for some phase; exchanging twice restores the original, so $e^{2i\alpha} = 1$ and $e^{i\alpha} = \pm1$.
>
> If the particles do not interact, for example in a box, the Schrödinger equation for $\psi(x_1,x_2)$ is solved by products $\psi_n(x_1)\psi_m(x_2)$ with energy $E_n + E_m$. Such a product says "particle 1 is in state $n$", which for $n \ne m$ is neither symmetric nor antisymmetric. But $\psi_n(x_2)\psi_m(x_1)$ is a solution with the same energy, so their sum and difference are solutions too ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-2|Theorem §A4.1.2]]); exchanging $x_1$ and $x_2$ leaves the sum unchanged and reverses the sign of the difference. For $n = m$ the two terms of $\psi_A$ are equal and cancel.

^der-a5-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^def-a5-3-1|Def. §A5.3.1]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-2|Theorem §A4.1.2]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-1|Principle §A3.4.1]]

> [!principle] Principle §A5.3.2: Pauli Exclusion Principle
> The total wave function (space and spin) of a system of electrons is **antisymmetric** under the exchange of any two of them. Consequently **no two electrons in an atom can have the same set of quantum numbers $(n, l, m_l, m_s)$**: each single-particle state holds at most one electron.
>
> *Domain: electrons, and every other particle of half-integer spin (protons, neutrons, quarks). Particles of integer spin (photons, alpha particles, deuterons, mesons) have symmetric wave functions and do not obey it.*
> *Source: UP3 §8.4; Tipler §7-6*

^pr-a5-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Status of the principle
> Pauli introduced it in 1925, together with the fourth quantum number, to make sense of the periodic table and of atomic spectra; it is assumed here, not derived. The second sentence follows from the first by Theorem §A5.3.1: two electrons in the same state $(n, l, m_l, m_s)$ would need $\psi_A$ with $n = m$, which is zero. The link between half-integer spin and antisymmetry is a theorem of relativistic quantum field theory ([[Quantum Field Theory]], derived there for free fields, [[§C5b.2 The Dirac Propagator and Spin–Statistics#^thm-c5b-2-6|QFT Theorem §C5b.2.6]], the general proof still to be filled; level C gives only a heuristic reason, [[§C12.1★ Permutation Symmetry and the Symmetrization Postulate#^rem-c12-1-3|Remark: Why the sign follows the spin]]). The principle reaches far beyond atoms: it causes the repulsion between closed electron shells that keeps molecules and solids from collapsing ([[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^rem-a6-1-1|Remark in §A6.1]]), and it fills the energy levels of a metal up to the Fermi energy ([[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^thm-a6-2-4|Theorem §A6.2.4]]).

^rem-a5-3-1

## Many-electron atoms

> [!model] Model §A5.3.3: Independent-Electron Model of an Atom
> An atom of atomic number $Z$ is built by adding $Z$ electrons to a nucleus of charge $Ze$. Each electron is assumed to move independently in a **spherically symmetric average potential** produced by the nucleus and all the other electrons. Its states are then labelled, as in hydrogen, by $(n, l, m_l, m_s)$, but because the potential is not $\propto 1/r$ the energy depends on both $n$ and $l$. In the ground state the electrons occupy the lowest-energy states allowed by the exclusion principle.
>
> *Assumptions:* the repulsion between electrons enters only through the average potential (the actual electron–electron correlations are neglected); magnetic interactions are ignored; the atom is neutral.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.4; Tipler §7-7*

^mod-a5-3-3

> [!definition] Definition §A5.3.2: Shell, Subshell, Electron Configuration, Valence Electron
> Electrons with the same $n$ form a **shell**, labelled K, L, M, N, … for $n = 1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$; those with the same $n$ and $l$ form a **subshell** (1s, 2s, 2p, …). The **electron configuration** lists the occupied subshells with the number of electrons as a superscript, for example $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^3$ for nitrogen, or $[\mathrm{He}]\,2s^2 2p^3$ with a noble-gas core in brackets. Electrons in the outermost shell are **valence electrons**; they take part in chemical bonding.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.4; Tipler §7-5, §7-7*

^def-a5-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.3.4: Capacity of Subshells and Shells
> A subshell of orbital quantum number $l$ holds at most
>
> $$
> N_l = 2(2l + 1) = 4l + 2
> $$
>
> electrons (2, 6, 10, 14 for s, p, d, f), and the shell $n$ holds at most $2n^2$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.4, eq. (8.35), Example 8.5*

^thm-a5-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For given $n$ and $l$ there are $2l + 1$ values of $m_l$ and two of $m_s$, hence $2(2l+1)$ distinct sets of quantum numbers, and by Principle §A5.3.2 at most one electron in each. Summing over $l = 0, \dots, n-1$: $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1} 2(2l+1) = 2n^2$, twice the count of [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-6|Theorem §A5.1.6]]. For $n = 3$: $2 + 6 + 10 = 18 = 2\cdot 3^2$.

^der-a5-3-4

*Uses:* [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^pr-a5-3-2|Principle §A5.3.2]], [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-6|Theorem §A5.1.6]], [[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^pr-a5-2-6|Principle §A5.2.6]]

> [!law] Law §A5.3.5: Filling Order of Subshells
> In the ground states of atoms the subshells are filled in the order
>
> $$
> 1s,\ 2s,\ 2p,\ 3s,\ 3p,\ 4s,\ 3d,\ 4p,\ 5s,\ 4d,\ 5p,\ 6s,\ 4f,\ 5d,\ 6p,\ 7s,\ \dots
> $$
>
> For the same $n$, a subshell of smaller $l$ lies lower; from potassium on, an s subshell of the next shell fills before the d subshell of the previous one (4s before 3d), and 4f fills only after 6s.
>
> *Valid when: ground states of neutral atoms; there are irregularities, for example chromium ($Z = 24$) and copper ($Z = 29$) have one 4s electron instead of two.*
> *Source: UP3 §8.4; Tipler §7-7, Fig. 7-19*

^law-a5-3-5

> [!definition] Definition §A5.3.3: Ionization Energy; Effective Nuclear Charge
> The (first) **ionization energy** of an atom is the energy needed to remove its most loosely bound electron; the voltage numerically equal to it in eV is the **ionization potential**. An outer electron that moves in the field of the nucleus partly screened by inner electrons behaves as if bound to an **effective nuclear charge** $Z_{\text{eff}}\,e$, with $Z_{\text{eff}} < Z$; with hydrogen-like energies, $E \approx -Z_{\text{eff}}^2(13.6\ \mathrm{eV})/n^2$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §7-7*

^def-a5-3-3

> [!remark] Remark: Why low $l$ lies lower: shielding and penetration
> An outer electron sees the nuclear charge $+Ze$ partly cancelled by the inner electrons. If it stayed entirely outside them it would see $Z_{\text{eff}} = 1$. But low-$l$ wave functions have inner maxima close to the nucleus ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^rem-a5-1-3|Remark in §A5.1]]): a 2s electron *penetrates* the 1s cloud more often than a 2p electron, sees a larger $Z_{\text{eff}}$ and is more tightly bound. The effect grows with the number of inner electrons, and for potassium it is large enough to put 4s below 3d. The degeneracy in $l$ of hydrogen is a property of the pure $1/r$ potential and disappears as soon as there is more than one electron.

^rem-a5-3-2

## The periodic table

> [!remark] Remark: The periodic table from shells and subshells
> - **Periods.** Each row starts with a new s subshell and ends when a p subshell is full (He ends the first row with 1s full). The noble gases He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn ($Z = 2, 10, 18, 36, 54, 86$) have closed subshells, very high ionization energies (He 24.6 eV, Ne 21.6 eV) and almost no chemistry. The next element starts a new shell; its one outer electron is far out and weakly bound (Li 5.39 eV, Na 5.14 eV, K 4.34 eV), and the atomic radius jumps up.
> - **Blocks.** The two left-hand columns fill s subshells, the six right-hand columns p subshells ($4l + 2 = 6$), and the ten-column gap between them, the transition metals, fills d subshells ($l = 2$, $4l + 2 = 10$). The lanthanides and actinides fill f subshells (14 each).
> - **Groups.** Elements in one column have the same outer configuration and therefore similar chemistry. The alkali metals (Li, Na, K, …) have one s electron outside a closed core and give it up easily; the halogens (F, Cl, Br, …) lack one electron of a closed p subshell and take one readily. Sodium and chlorine together form $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$, held by the ionic bond ([[§A6.1 Molecular Bonds and Spectra#^ex-a6-1-1|Example §A6.1.1]]). The transition metals differ mainly in an inner d subshell while their outer 4s electrons stay the same, which is why their chemistry is so alike.
>
> Before quantum mechanics the table was an empirical arrangement by chemical behaviour; Moseley's X-ray measurements fixed the order by atomic number ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^law-a5-4-3|Law §A5.4.3]]), and the exclusion principle explained it.

^rem-a5-3-3

> [!caution] Caution: Shells do not fill one after another
> The shell $n = 3$ can hold 18 electrons, yet argon, with $3s^2 3p^6$ and the 3d subshell empty, already closes the third period at 8 elements. The capacities $2n^2$ count states, not the order of filling; the order is set by the energies of Law §A5.3.5.

^cau-a5-3-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.3.1: The Ground State of Helium
> Estimate the energy of the helium ground state and compare it with the measured energy, $-79.0\ \mathrm{eV}$ (the sum of the two ionization energies, $24.6\ \mathrm{eV} + 54.4\ \mathrm{eV}$).
>
> Neglecting the repulsion between the electrons, each moves in the field of the $Z = 2$ nucleus with hydrogen-like energy $-Z^2(13.6\ \mathrm{eV})/n^2$. Both can be in 1s only with opposite spins (Principle §A5.3.2), so the configuration is $1s^2$ with total spin zero, and
>
> $$
> E \approx 2\times\bigl(-4\times13.6\ \mathrm{eV}\bigr) = -108.8\ \mathrm{eV} .
> $$
>
> The average repulsion between the two 1s electrons, computed with these wave functions, is about $+34\ \mathrm{eV}$, giving $E \approx -74.8\ \mathrm{eV}$, about 5 % above experiment. The exclusion principle fixes the configuration; the electron–electron repulsion is a 30 % correction, which is why the independent-electron model of Model §A5.3.3 uses an average potential rather than the bare nucleus.
>
> *Source: Tipler §7-7, eqs. (7-63)–(7-66)*

^ex-a5-3-1

> [!example] Example §A5.3.2: Effective Charge Seen by Lithium's Outer Electron
> The ionization energy of lithium ($1s^2 2s$) is $5.39\ \mathrm{eV}$. What effective nuclear charge does the 2s electron see?
>
> Set $Z_{\text{eff}}^2(13.6\ \mathrm{eV})/2^2 = 5.39\ \mathrm{eV}$ (Def. §A5.3.3):
>
> $$
> Z_{\text{eff}} = 2\sqrt{\frac{5.39}{13.6}} = 1.26 .
> $$
>
> Perfect shielding by the two 1s electrons would give $Z_{\text{eff}} = 3 - 2 = 1$; the excess $0.26$ measures how often the 2s electron penetrates the 1s cloud. A 2p electron penetrates less, so 2p lies above 2s and lithium's ground state is $1s^2 2s$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §7-7 (Lithium)*

^ex-a5-3-2

> [!example] Example §A5.3.3: Writing Electron Configurations
> Find the ground-state configurations of aluminium ($Z = 13$), potassium ($Z = 19$) and iron ($Z = 26$).
>
> Fill the subshells in the order of Law §A5.3.5 with at most $4l + 2$ electrons each (Theorem §A5.3.4):
> - Al: $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^1 = [\mathrm{Ne}]\,3s^2 3p$; three valence electrons, one of them unpaired in 3p.
> - K: after argon's 18 electrons the 19th goes into 4s, not 3d: $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,4s^1$, an alkali metal like Na.
> - Fe: $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^6$ ($18 + 2 + 6 = 26$); a transition metal, filling the inner 3d subshell behind a full 4s.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.4, Table 8.6; Tipler §7-7*

^ex-a5-3-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The antisymmetric combination is alternating in the two single-particle states: $(\psi_n, \psi_m) \mapsto \psi_n(x_1)\psi_m(x_2) - \psi_n(x_2)\psi_m(x_1)$ is bilinear and vanishes when the two states coincide, the property that defines alternating forms ([[§32 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-14|LADR Def. 9.14]]). Many-electron antisymmetric wave functions (Slater determinants) are level B ([[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^def-b7-1-3|Definition §B7.1.3]], [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]]).
> - The same exclusion principle, applied to the electrons of a whole crystal, gives the Fermi gas of [[§A6.2 Crystalline Solids and the Free-Electron Model#^mod-a6-2-2|Model §A6.2.2]] and the filled bands of [[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^thm-a6-3-4|Theorem §A6.3.4]].
> - Statistical mechanics of identical particles: classically, indistinguishability is the correct Boltzmann counting ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^def-b6-3-3|TH Def. §B6.3.3]]); quantum mechanically, the occupation numbers of bosons and fermions ([[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^def-b10-1-1|TH Def. §B10.1.1]], [[§B10.1 Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac Distributions#^rem-b10-1-2|TH Remark: Which particles are which]]).
> - Level B: the exchange symmetry of Theorem §A5.3.1 as the symmetrization requirement — [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^pr-b7-1-3|Principle §B7.1.3]], from which the exclusion principle (Principle §A5.3.2) becomes a theorem — [[§B7.1 Two-Particle Systems, Bosons and Fermions#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]].
> - Level B: the independent-electron model (Model §A5.3.3) and the helium of Example §A5.3.1 — [[§B7.2 Atoms#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]], [[§B7.2 Atoms#^rem-b7-2-5|Remark: The average potential of the independent-electron model]]; the electron repulsion in helium computed, without and with screening — [[§B9.2★ Helium and the Hydrogen Molecule#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.2★ Helium and the Hydrogen Molecule#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]]; the effective nuclear charge (Def. §A5.3.3) made quantitative — [[§B9.2★ Helium and the Hydrogen Molecule#^rem-b9-2-2|Remark: Screening, made quantitative]].
