Almost everything known about atoms was read from their light. A photon is emitted or absorbed when an electron jumps between two of the levels of [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom]] to [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table]], and the photon's energy is the difference of the two. Not every pair of levels is connected: selection rules decide which lines appear. Transitions of the outer electrons give optical spectra; transitions into vacancies of the innermost shell give X-rays, whose regular dependence on the atomic number fixed the order of the periodic table. And a photon passing an excited atom can trigger the emission of a second, identical photon: that is the principle of the laser.

## Emission and absorption

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.4.1: Photon Energy in an Atomic Transition
> When an atom passes from a state of energy $E_i$ to one of lower energy $E_f$ by emitting a photon, or from $E_f$ to $E_i$ by absorbing one, the photon has
>
> $$
> hf = E_i - E_f, \qquad \lambda = \frac{hc}{E_i - E_f} = \frac{1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}}{E_i - E_f} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.5, eq. (8.36); Tipler §7-8*

^thm-a5-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Energy is conserved in the emission or absorption (the recoil energy of the heavy atom is negligible), so the photon carries off or brings in exactly $E_i - E_f$. By the photon hypothesis its frequency is that energy divided by $h$ ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]]), and $\lambda = c/f$. In the Bohr model this "frequency condition" was a postulate ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen#^mod-a2-2-1|Model §A2.2.1]]); here the stationary states and their energies come from the Schrödinger equation, and only energy conservation is added.

^der-a5-4-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]], [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-3|Theorem §A5.1.3]]

> [!definition] Definition §A5.4.1: Selection Rule; Allowed and Forbidden Transitions
> A **selection rule** restricts which pairs of states can be connected by the emission or absorption of a photon (an **electric dipole transition**). Transitions that obey it are **allowed**; the others are **forbidden**, which means not impossible but very improbable, so that an excited state with no allowed way down lives unusually long (a **metastable state**).
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.5, §8.6, §9.2; Tipler §7-2, §9-6*

^def-a5-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.4.2: Selection Rules for One-Electron Transitions
> In allowed (electric dipole) transitions of a one-electron atom, or of the outer electron of an alkali atom,
>
> $$
> \Delta l = \pm1, \qquad \Delta m_l = 0, \pm1, \qquad \Delta j = 0, \pm1\ \ (\text{but not } j = 0 \to j = 0),
> $$
>
> while the principal quantum number may change by any amount.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.5, eq. (8.37); Tipler §7-2, eq. (7-28), §7-8, eq. (7-67)*

^thm-a5-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Idea; the derivation from the time-dependent Schrödinger equation is level C: [[§C9.6 The Interaction Picture and the Atom–Field Interaction#^thm-c9-6-8|Theorem §C9.6.8]], [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-7|Theorem §C7.2.7]], [[§C9.6 The Interaction Picture and the Atom–Field Interaction#^rem-c9-6-5|Remark: What this proves of level A's selection rules]].* The photon carries intrinsic angular momentum: it has spin 1. Total angular momentum is conserved in the emission, so the atom's angular momentum changes by one unit at most, and its $z$-component by $-\hbar$, $0$ or $+\hbar$; this gives $\Delta m_l = 0, \pm1$ and $\lvert\Delta l\rvert \le 1$. That $\Delta l = 0$ is excluded as well follows from a symmetry of the states under reflection through the nucleus (parity), whose treatment is level C ([[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^thm-c8-2-5|Theorem §C8.2.5]], [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^rem-c8-2-5|Remark: The dipole rules completed]]). Nothing ties the photon to $n$, so $\Delta n$ is free.

*Hydrogen energy levels, to scale, sorted by $l$. Levels with the same $n$ have the same energy. Allowed transitions change $l$ by one unit: the Lyman $\alpha$ line (blue) is 2p → 1s, and the Balmer $\alpha$ line at 656.3 nm (red) is produced by three different transitions between $n = 3$ and $n = 2$, all with the same energy. The direct decay 2s → 1s (grey dashed) is forbidden.*

> [!remark] Remark: Reading atomic spectra
> - **Hydrogen.** Ignoring spin, every level with given $n$ has one energy, so all transitions $n_i \to n_f$ give a single line, and the Lyman, Balmer and Paschen series of the Rydberg formula ([[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]) follow from Theorem §A5.4.1. Fine structure ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-9|Theorem §A5.2.9]]) splits the lines slightly.
> - **Alkali atoms** (Li, Na, K, …) have one electron outside a closed core. Their optical spectra come from that electron alone and resemble hydrogen's, except that its energy depends on $l$ as well as $n$ (the shielding of [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^rem-a5-3-2|§A5.3]]), and every level with $l \ge 1$ is a fine-structure doublet. Exciting a core electron takes tens to thousands of electron-volts and gives ultraviolet and X-ray lines.
> - **Absorption** spectra are simpler than emission spectra: at ordinary temperatures nearly all atoms are in the ground state, so cold hydrogen absorbs only the Lyman lines. The dark Fraunhofer lines of sunlight are absorption lines; the sodium D lines got their name there.
> - **Fluorescence.** An atom excited several levels up by an ultraviolet photon may return in several smaller steps, emitting lower-energy photons, some visible. Fluorescent lamps and dyes convert ultraviolet into visible light this way.

## X-rays

> [!definition] Definition §A5.4.2: Characteristic X-Rays; Braking Radiation
> When a fast electron knocks an electron out of an inner shell of a target atom, an electron from a higher shell drops into the vacancy and emits an X-ray photon. These **characteristic X-rays** are named by the shell of the vacancy: K X-rays end in the K shell ($n = 1$), with **K$\alpha$** from the L shell ($n = 2$), **K$\beta$** from M ($n = 3$), K$\gamma$ from N; L X-rays end in the L shell, and so on. Electrons decelerated in the target also emit a continuous spectrum, **braking radiation** (bremsstrahlung). The characteristic lines sit as sharp peaks on the continuum and identify the target element.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.5; Tipler §4-4*

^def-a5-4-2

> [!law] Law §A5.4.3: Moseley's Law
> For a given characteristic X-ray line, the square root of its frequency is a linear function of the atomic number $Z$ of the emitting element:
>
> $$
> \sqrt f = A_n\,(Z - b) ,
> $$
>
> with $b \approx 1$ for the K series and $b \approx 7.4$ for the L series, and a constant $A_n$ for each line.
>
> *Valid when: characteristic lines of elements heavy enough to emit X-rays (Moseley's 1913–1914 data run from aluminium, $Z = 13$, to gold, $Z = 79$); a plot of $\sqrt f$ against $Z$ (a Moseley plot) is a set of straight lines.*
> *Source: Tipler §4-4, eq. (4-34), Fig. 4-19; UP3 §8.5, eq. (8.41)*

^law-a5-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.4.4: K$\alpha$ Frequency in the Screened Bohr Model
> If the electron that fills a K-shell vacancy moves in the field of the nucleus screened by the one remaining K electron, the K$\alpha$ photon has
>
> $$
> E_{K\alpha} \approx (Z - 1)^2\,(10.2\ \mathrm{eV}), \qquad f_{K\alpha} = \frac34\,cR_\infty\,(Z-1)^2, \qquad \sqrt{f_{K\alpha}} = \sqrt{\tfrac34 cR_\infty}\,(Z - 1) ,
> $$
>
> with $R_\infty = 1.097\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, so that $\sqrt{\tfrac34 cR_\infty} = 4.97\times10^7\ \mathrm{Hz^{1/2}}$. This is Moseley's law for the K$\alpha$ line, with $b = 1$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.5, eqs. (8.39)–(8.41); Tipler §4-4, eqs. (4-35)–(4-37)*

^thm-a5-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With a K vacancy, an L electron "sees" the charge $Z e$ of the nucleus reduced by the single remaining K electron to about $(Z - 1)e$. Treat both levels as those of a hydrogen-like ion of charge $Z - 1$ ([[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-3|Theorem §A5.1.3]]): $E_n \approx -(Z-1)^2(13.6\ \mathrm{eV})/n^2$. By Theorem §A5.4.1,
>
> $$
> hf = E_2 - E_1 = (Z-1)^2(13.6\ \mathrm{eV})\Bigl(1 - \frac14\Bigr) = (Z-1)^2(10.2\ \mathrm{eV}) .
> $$
>
> Since $13.6\ \mathrm{eV} = hcR_\infty$, $f = \frac34 cR_\infty(Z-1)^2$, and taking the square root gives the linear law. Screening the K level by $1$ as well is crude (the inner electron sees almost the full $Z$), but the model reproduces the measured K$\alpha$ lines to about a percent for medium $Z$.

^der-a5-4-4

*Uses:* [[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]], [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-3|Theorem §A5.1.3]], [[§A5.3 The Exclusion Principle and the Periodic Table#^def-a5-3-3|Def. §A5.3.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.4.5: Short-Wavelength Cutoff of the Continuous Spectrum
> Electrons accelerated through a potential difference $V$ produce braking radiation only down to the wavelength
>
> $$
> \lambda_{\min} = \frac{hc}{eV} = \frac{1240\ \mathrm{nm}}{V/(1\ \mathrm{volt})} ,
> $$
>
> which depends on the tube voltage and not on the target material (the Duane–Hunt rule, first found empirically).
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.5, Fig. 8.23; Tipler §3-4, eq. (3-24)*

^thm-a5-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> An electron arrives at the target with kinetic energy $eV$ ([[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-2|EM Def. §A3.2.2]]). It can lose this energy in many collisions, giving many photons, but no single photon can carry more than all of it: $hf \le eV$, so $\lambda = c/f \ge hc/eV$. The work function of the target, a few eV, is negligible beside $eV$ of tens of keV. The cutoff is the photoelectric effect run backwards.

^der-a5-4-5

*Uses:* [[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|Principle §A1.2.3]], [[§A3.2 Potential and Potential Difference#^def-a3-2-2|EM Def. §A3.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: What Moseley's law did
> - **Atomic number.** Before 1913 the periodic table was ordered by atomic mass, with awkward exceptions: argon (39.95) is heavier than potassium (39.10), yet chemically belongs before it. Moseley's straight lines required argon to have $Z = 18$ and potassium $Z = 19$; ordering by $Z$ removed every such exception and identified $Z$ with the nuclear charge. Gaps in his lines at $Z = 43$, $61$ and $75$ predicted elements found later (promethium, $Z = 61$, in 1945).
> - **Why X-ray spectra are regular.** Optical spectra involve outer electrons and change irregularly from element to element. X-ray lines involve the innermost shells, which feel the nuclear charge almost directly and are shielded from the outer electrons and from chemical bonding; their energies grow smoothly, roughly as $Z^2$. Hydrogen emits no X-rays: its levels lie within $13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$.
> - **The continuum and its cutoff.** The braking radiation ends sharply at $\lambda_{\min}$ of Theorem §A5.4.5; for a 35 kV tube, $\lambda_{\min} = 1240/35\,000\ \mathrm{nm} = 0.035\ \mathrm{nm}$. Measuring $\lambda_{\min}$ at known $V$ gives $h/e$.
> - **Auger electrons.** Instead of emitting the K$\alpha$ photon, the atom can hand the energy to a third, outer electron, which leaves with a kinetic energy characteristic of the element; Auger spectroscopy identifies surface impurities this way (Tipler §4-4).
> - **Uses.** X-ray images are shadows: dense material absorbs more. CT scanners combine many such images into slices. X-ray diffraction reveals crystal and molecular structure ([[§A7.5 X-Ray Diffraction and Holography#^thm-a7-5-1|WO Theorem §A7.5.1]]).

^rem-a5-4-2

## Stimulated emission

> [!definition] Definition §A5.4.3: Absorption, Spontaneous and Stimulated Emission
> Consider two levels $E_1 < E_2$ of an atom, with $hf = E_2 - E_1$, in radiation of spectral energy density $u(f)$ (energy per unit volume per unit frequency). Three processes connect them:
> - **absorption** $E_1 \to E_2$, with probability per atom per unit time $B_{12}\,u(f)$;
> - **spontaneous emission** $E_2 \to E_1$, with probability per unit time $A_{21}$, independent of the radiation; $\tau = 1/A_{21}$ is the **mean lifetime** of the excited state (typically $10^{-8}\ \mathrm s$ for allowed atomic transitions; it sets the natural width of the line, [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^def-a3-5-1|Def. §A3.5.1]]);
> - **stimulated emission** $E_2 \to E_1$, triggered by a photon of energy $hf$, with probability per unit time $B_{21}\,u(f)$. The emitted photon has the same frequency, direction and phase as the triggering one: the two are **coherent** ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^def-a6-1-1|WO Def. §A6.1.1]]).
>
> $A_{21}$, $B_{12}$ and $B_{21}$ are the **Einstein coefficients**.
>
> *Source: Tipler §9-5; UP3 §8.6*

^def-a5-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.4.6: Einstein Relations
> For two levels of equal degeneracy,
>
> $$
> B_{12} = B_{21}, \qquad \frac{A_{21}}{B_{21}} = \frac{8\pi hf^3}{c^3} ,
> $$
>
> and in thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$ the ratio of spontaneous to stimulated emission is
>
> $$
> \frac{A_{21}}{B_{21}\,u(f)} = e^{hf/k_BT} - 1 .
> $$
>
> Absorption and stimulated emission are equally probable per atom; spontaneous emission dominates when $hf \gg k_BT$, stimulated emission when $hf \ll k_BT$.
>
> *Source: Tipler §9-5, eqs. (9-35)–(9-42)*

^thm-a5-4-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put atoms and radiation in equilibrium at temperature $T$. The populations follow the Boltzmann factor ([[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^rem-a2-4-2|TH §A2.4]]), $N_2/N_1 = e^{-(E_2 - E_1)/k_BT} = e^{-hf/k_BT}$. Equilibrium means as many upward as downward transitions per unit time:
>
> $$
> N_1 B_{12}\,u(f) = N_2\bigl(A_{21} + B_{21}\,u(f)\bigr) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u(f) = \frac{A_{21}/B_{21}}{\dfrac{B_{12}}{B_{21}}\,e^{hf/k_BT} - 1} .
> $$
>
> The radiation in equilibrium is blackbody radiation, whose energy density is Planck's law ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-4|Theorem §A1.1.4]]), written per unit frequency with $\lambda = c/f$ and $u(f) = u(\lambda)\,c/f^2$: $u(f) = \dfrac{8\pi hf^3}{c^3}\,\dfrac{1}{e^{hf/k_BT} - 1}$. The two expressions agree at every temperature only if $B_{12}/B_{21} = 1$ and $A_{21}/B_{21} = 8\pi hf^3/c^3$. Dividing $A_{21}$ by $B_{21}u(f)$ with Planck's $u$ gives the last formula. (Einstein ran the argument in 1917 and so predicted stimulated emission: without the $B_{21}$ term, Planck's law could not be recovered.)

^der-a5-4-6

*Uses:* [[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^def-a5-4-3|Def. §A5.4.3]], [[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-4|Theorem §A1.1.4]], [[§A2.4 Distribution of Molecular Speeds#^rem-a2-4-2|TH §A2.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why stimulated emission is normally invisible
> At room temperature $k_BT = 0.026\ \mathrm{eV}$. For a visible transition, $hf \approx 2\ \mathrm{eV}$, $hf/k_BT \approx 77$ and spontaneous emission wins by the factor $e^{77} \approx 10^{33}$; for a microwave transition with $hf \approx 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV}$, $hf/k_BT \approx 0.004$ and stimulated emission wins by about 260 to 1 (Tipler Example 9-7). Moreover, in equilibrium $N_1 \gg N_2$, so any photon of energy $hf$ is far more likely to be absorbed than to stimulate an emission. A laser must therefore drive the populations far from equilibrium.

^rem-a5-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.4.7: Condition for Light Amplification
> Radiation of frequency $f = (E_2 - E_1)/h$ passing through a collection of atoms gains more photons by stimulated emission than it loses by absorption if and only if
>
> $$
> N_2 > N_1 ,
> $$
>
> a **population inversion** (for levels of equal degeneracy).
>
> *Source: Tipler §9-6; UP3 §8.6*

^thm-a5-4-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Per unit time the radiation gains $N_2 B_{21}\,u(f)$ photons by stimulated emission and loses $N_1 B_{12}\,u(f)$ by absorption. With $B_{12} = B_{21}$ (Theorem §A5.4.6) the net gain is $B_{21}u(f)\,(N_2 - N_1)$, positive exactly when $N_2 > N_1$. Spontaneously emitted photons go in all directions with random phases and do not add to the coherent beam.

^der-a5-4-7

*Uses:* [[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^def-a5-4-3|Def. §A5.4.3]], [[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-6|Theorem §A5.4.6]]

## Lasers

> [!model] Model §A5.4.8: Laser
> A **laser** (light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation) consists of
> - a **gain medium** whose atoms are kept in population inversion between two levels by **pumping** (an intense flash, an electric discharge, collisions), with the upper laser level **metastable** so that atoms accumulate in it;
> - an **optical resonator**: two parallel mirrors, one almost totally reflecting and one partly transmitting, between which light travelling along the axis passes many times, each pass stimulating more emission.
>
> The output is coherent and nearly monochromatic. In a **three-level laser** the lower laser level is the ground state, so more than half the atoms must be pumped out of it; in a **four-level laser** the lower level is an excited state that empties quickly, so inversion is easy to reach. *Assumptions:* gain per round trip at least equal to all losses (transmission through the output mirror, scattering); only modes standing along the axis, $L = m\lambda/2$, are amplified.
>
> *Source: UP3 §8.6; Tipler §9-6*

^mod-a5-4-8

![[ph-qm-a5-4-2.svg]]
*Level schemes (not to scale). (a) Ruby, a three-level laser: a flash lamp pumps chromium ions into broad bands (blue), they drop without radiating to a metastable level 1.79 eV above the ground state, and lasing at 694.3 nm (red) returns them to the ground state. (b) Helium–neon, a four-level laser: a discharge excites helium to 20.61 eV, collisions pass the energy to neon's nearly equal 20.66 eV level, and lasing at 632.8 nm ends on the normally empty 18.70 eV level, which empties by spontaneous emission and collisions with the tube wall.*

> [!remark] Remark: Ruby, helium–neon, and why laser light is useful
> - **Ruby** (Maiman, 1960): $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$ with about 0.05 % chromium; the 1.79 eV metastable level gives $\lambda = (1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm})/(1.79\ \mathrm{eV}) = 694\ \mathrm{nm}$. As a three-level system it must be pumped hard and works in pulses.
> - **Helium–neon** (1961): 1.96 eV between the neon levels gives $1240/1.96 = 633\ \mathrm{nm}$; the 0.05 eV mismatch between the helium and neon levels is supplied by the atoms' kinetic energy. It runs continuously.
> - **Uses.** Because the light is coherent it can be focused to a spot near the diffraction limit ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-1|WO Theorem §A7.4.1]]): CD and DVD players use red laser light, Blu-ray players blue light of 405 nm, whose shorter wavelength resolves smaller pits and stores more data. Lasers also read bar codes, carry signals in optical fibres, cut tissue in surgery and measure the distance to the Moon to millimetres. Semiconductor (diode) lasers are p–n junctions in forward bias ([[§A6.3 Band Theory and Semiconductors#^rem-a6-3-3|Remark in §A6.3]]). The **maser**, the microwave forerunner (Townes, 1953), works the same way; today's hydrogen maser runs on the 21-cm hyperfine transition ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^def-a5-2-5|Def. §A5.2.5]]) and serves as an atomic clock (Tipler §9-6).

^rem-a5-4-4

> [!caution] Caution: Coherent is not the same as monochromatic
> Light of a single frequency is monochromatic; light whose waves keep a fixed phase relation is coherent. A laser is both because every stimulated photon copies the phase and frequency of the one that triggered it. A narrow filter in front of a lamp makes light nearly monochromatic but not coherent across the beam ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^def-a6-1-1|WO Def. §A6.1.1]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.4.1: The Sodium Doublet
> The yellow light of sodium consists of two lines, $589.00\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $589.59\ \mathrm{nm}$, both ending on the 3s ground state. (a) What is the orbital angular momentum of the upper states? (b) How far apart are they in energy?
>
> (a) The final state has $l = 0$. By Theorem §A5.4.2 only $\Delta l = \pm1$ is allowed, so the initial states have $l = 1$: they are 3p, with $L = \sqrt{2}\,\hbar = 1.41\,\hbar$.
>
> (b) Both lines end on the same level, so the difference of the photon energies is the splitting of the upper levels:
>
> $$
> \Delta E = hc\Bigl(\frac{1}{589.00\ \mathrm{nm}} - \frac{1}{589.59\ \mathrm{nm}}\Bigr) = (1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm})\,\frac{0.59\ \mathrm{nm}}{(589.00\ \mathrm{nm})(589.59\ \mathrm{nm})} = 2.1\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV} .
> $$
>
> This is the fine-structure splitting of 3p into $3\,{}^2P_{3/2}$ (589.0 nm) and $3\,{}^2P_{1/2}$ (589.6 nm) ([[§A5.2 Orbital Magnetic Moment and Electron Spin#^thm-a5-2-9|Theorem §A5.2.9]]): one part in a thousand of the photon energy, $1240/589.3 = 2.10\ \mathrm{eV}$, but easily resolved by a good spectrometer.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 8.6; Tipler §7-8*

^ex-a5-4-1

> [!example] Example §A5.4.2: The K$\alpha$ Line of Molybdenum
> Predict the wavelength of the K$\alpha$ line of molybdenum ($Z = 42$); Moseley measured $0.0721\ \mathrm{nm}$.
>
> By Theorem §A5.4.4, $1/\lambda = f/c = \frac34 R_\infty(Z-1)^2$:
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{4}{3R_\infty(Z-1)^2} = \frac{4}{3(1.097\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}})(41)^2} = 7.23\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm m = 0.0723\ \mathrm{nm} ,
> $$
>
> within 0.3 % of the measurement; the photon energy is $1240/0.0723\ \mathrm{eV} = 17.2\ \mathrm{keV}$. An X-ray tube needs more than this voltage per electron charge to knock out a K electron of molybdenum.
>
> *Source: Tipler Example 4-8*

^ex-a5-4-2

> [!example] Example §A5.4.3: The Balmer Lines in the Lab
> In PHY 286 the spacing of a diffraction grating was first calibrated with known krypton lines, $d = 3.463\ \mu\mathrm m$, and then the visible hydrogen lines were measured in several orders ($d\sin\theta = m\lambda$, [[§A7.3 Diffraction Gratings#^thm-a7-3-1|WO Theorem §A7.3.1]]). Averaging the second orders $m = +2$ and $m = -2$, the measured wavelengths were $689.1$, $510.6$, $458.4$ and $429.2\ \mathrm{nm}$. Identify the lines and find the Rydberg constant.
>
> The Balmer lines are $1/\lambda = R(1/4 - 1/n^2)$ with $n = 3, 4, 5, 6$ ([[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^law-a2-1-1|Law §A2.1.1]]). Ratios of wavelengths do not depend on the calibration of $d$, so they identify the lines:
>
> | line | $\lambda$ (nm) | $\lambda/\lambda_\alpha$ measured | predicted $\frac{1/4 - 1/9}{1/4 - 1/n^2}$ | $n$ | $R$ ($10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$) |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | H$\alpha$ | 689.1 | 1 | 1 | 3 | 1.045 |
> | H$\beta$ | 510.6 | 0.741 | 0.741 | 4 | 1.045 |
