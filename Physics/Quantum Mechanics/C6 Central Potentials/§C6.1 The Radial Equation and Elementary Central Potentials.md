What does rotational symmetry of a Hamiltonian buy, and how far does it reduce a three-dimensional problem? Level B separated the Schrödinger equation of a central potential in spherical coordinates, derived the radial equation for $u = rR$ with its effective potential and boundary behaviour ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-1|Theorem §B5.3.1]], [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]]), solved the infinite spherical well ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^ex-b5-3-1|Example §B5.3.1]]) and counted the levels of the isotropic oscillator in Cartesian coordinates ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-7|Theorem §B5.1.7]]). Those results are used here as they stand. Level C starts one step earlier, from the rotation operators of [[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations]]: commuting with all of them is what makes $E$ independent of $m$ in any ket space, spin included. On that basis this section adds the general reduction of the radial equation for bound states, the free particle as a set of $|E\,\ell\,m\rangle$ states, the isotropic oscillator solved through its radial equation, and the oscillator's angular momentum written with ladder operators.

*Notation.* Kets carry their eigenvalues without primes: $|E\,\ell\,m\rangle$ is a common eigenket of $H$, $\mathbf L^2$, $L_z$ (Sakurai's $|Elm\rangle$); $\mathbf J$ is angular momentum of any kind, $\mathbf L$ orbital; $m$ is the magnetic quantum number and $m$ also the mass in $H = \mathbf p^2/2m + V(r)$, which cannot be confused in a formula. $\mathscr D(R)$ is the rotation operator. The oscillator's ladder operators are $a_i$, $a_i^\dagger$ ($i = x, y, z$), level B's $\hat a_-$, $\hat a_+$ ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]]). $\hbar$ is explicit.

## Rotational invariance

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.1.1: Rotationally Invariant Hamiltonians
> Let $H$ commute with every rotation operator, $\mathscr D(R)\,H\,\mathscr D(R)^\dagger = H$. Then:
> 1. $[\mathbf J, H] = 0$ and $[\mathbf J^2, H] = 0$, so $H$, $\mathbf J^2$, $J_z$ have a common orthonormal eigenbasis $|E\,j\,m\rangle$ (plus whatever further labels a degenerate level needs);
> 2. the energy does not depend on $m$: $J_\pm|E\,j\,m\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{(j \mp m)(j \pm m + 1)}\,|E\,j\,m \pm 1\rangle$ with the same $E$, so every energy eigenspace is a sum of complete multiplets, each $(2j+1)$-fold degenerate;
> 3. a rotation maps each multiplet into itself, $\mathscr D(R)\,|E\,j\,m\rangle = \sum_{m'}|E\,j\,m'\rangle\,\mathscr D^{(j)}_{m'm}(R)$.
>
> Conversely, $[\mathbf J, H] = 0$ implies $[\mathscr D(R), H] = 0$ for all $R$. For a spinless particle, $H = \dfrac{\mathbf p^2}{2m} + V(r)$ with $r^2 = \mathbf x^2$ has this property:
>
> $$
> [\mathbf L, \mathbf p^2] = [\mathbf L, \mathbf x^2] = 0, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad [\mathbf L, H] = [\mathbf L^2, H] = 0, \qquad (3.264),\ (3.265)
> $$
>
> and the stationary states can be labelled $|E\,\ell\,m\rangle$ with $H|E\,\ell\,m\rangle = E|E\,\ell\,m\rangle$, $\mathbf L^2|E\,\ell\,m\rangle = \ell(\ell+1)\hbar^2|E\,\ell\,m\rangle$, $L_z|E\,\ell\,m\rangle = m\hbar|E\,\ell\,m\rangle$ (3.266)–(3.268).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.7, eqs. (3.263)–(3.268) · the user's 511 notes, §"Schrödinger's Equation for Central Potentials" · the user's series, Part IV, §14 (central potentials: three dimensions become one)*

^thm-c6-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *From rotations to generators and back.* For an infinitesimal rotation $\mathscr D = 1 - \frac i\hbar\mathbf J\cdot\hat n\,d\phi$ ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^pr-c5-1-2|Principle §C5.1.2]]), $\mathscr DH\mathscr D^\dagger = H - \frac i\hbar[\mathbf J\cdot\hat n, H]\,d\phi + O(d\phi^2)$; invariance for every $\hat n$ gives $[J_k, H] = 0$. Conversely, if $[J_k, H] = 0$ then $H$ commutes with every power of $\mathbf J\cdot\hat n$, hence with $\mathscr D(\hat n, \phi) = \exp(-i\mathbf J\cdot\hat n\,\phi/\hbar)$ ([[§C5.1 Rotations and the Angular-Momentum Commutation Relations#^thm-c5-1-3|Theorem §C5.1.3]]). And $[\mathbf J^2, H] = \sum_k\bigl(J_k[J_k, H] + [J_k, H]J_k\bigr) = 0$.
>
> *1.* $H$, $\mathbf J^2$ and $J_z$ are commuting Hermitian operators, so they have a common orthonormal eigenbasis ([[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-76|LADR Thm. 5.76]] in finite dimensions; in general this is the compatibility of observables, [[§C1.3 Measurements, Compatible Observables and Uncertainty#^thm-c1-3-2|Theorem §C1.3.2]]).
>
> *2.* $J_\pm = J_x \pm iJ_y$ commute with $H$ and with $\mathbf J^2$, so $J_\pm|E\,j\,m\rangle$ is again an eigenket of $H$ with eigenvalue $E$ and of $\mathbf J^2$ with $j$, and of $J_z$ with $m \pm 1$ ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^rem-c5-3-1|Remark: Eigenvalues and ladder matrix elements]]). Its squared norm is $\hbar^2(j \mp m)(j \pm m + 1)$, which vanishes only at the ends of the ladder. So all $2j+1$ values of $m$ occur at the same energy, and the level splits into whole multiplets.
>
> *3.* $\mathscr D(R)$ commutes with $H$ and $\mathbf J^2$, so it maps the subspace with given $E$ and $j$ into itself; on the multiplet spanned by $|E\,j\,m\rangle$, $m = -j, \dots, j$, its matrix is $\mathscr D^{(j)}(R)$ by definition of the rotation matrices ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^def-c5-3-1|Def. §C5.3.1]], [[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-3-1|Theorem §C5.3.1]]).
>
> *Central potentials.* $\mathbf L = \mathbf x\times\mathbf p$ satisfies $[L_i, x_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}x_k$ and $[L_i, p_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}p_k$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]], (4.122); $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf p$ are vector operators, [[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^def-c7-2-1|Def. §C7.2.1]]). Hence
>
> $$
> [L_i, p_jp_j] = [L_i, p_j]\,p_j + p_j\,[L_i, p_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,(p_kp_j + p_jp_k) = 0,
> $$
>
> because $p_kp_j + p_jp_k$ is symmetric in $j, k$ and $\epsilon_{ijk}$ antisymmetric; the same computation gives $[L_i, \mathbf x^2] = 0$. $V(r)$ is a function of the operator $\mathbf x^2$ and commutes with everything that commutes with $\mathbf x^2$. (This is [[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^rem-c5-4-2|Remark: Scalars commute with L]], PHY 511 HW 9.) For a spinless particle $\mathbf J = \mathbf L$, and 1–2 give the labels $|E\,\ell\,m\rangle$.

> [!remark] Remark: What changed from level B
> Level B obtained the $(2\ell+1)$-fold degeneracy from the separated equation: $Y^m_\ell$ does not enter the radial equation ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-3|Theorem §B5.3.3]]), and $[\hat H, L_i] = 0$ was checked with differential operators ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]]); its [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^rem-b5-3-2|Remark: Essential and accidental degeneracy]] traced the degeneracy to non-commuting symmetries ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]). Theorem §C6.1.1 states the cause directly and in more generality: invariance under the rotation group makes each energy eigenspace carry a representation of that group, and a multiplet of $\mathbf J^2$ cannot be split by an $H$ that commutes with $J_\pm$. Nothing here uses wave functions, so the statement covers spin and any number of particles, with $\mathbf J$ the total angular momentum ([[§C7.1 Addition of Angular Momenta and Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients]]). Degeneracy beyond whole multiplets of one $j$, as in the oscillator and hydrogen, needs more symmetry than rotations (Remark below, and [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem]]).

## The radial equation

The coordinate representation turns (3.266)–(3.268) into the radial equation exactly as at level B, by the same argument, with the kinetic energy split into a radial part and $\mathbf L^2/2mr^2$ ((3.230), [[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-2|Theorem §C5.4.2]]): $\langle\mathbf x|E\,\ell\,m\rangle = R_{E\ell}(r)\,Y^m_\ell(\theta, \phi)$, and with $R_{E\ell} = u_{E\ell}/r$ (3.270)

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u_{E\ell}}{dr^2} + \Bigl[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)\hbar^2}{2mr^2} + V(r)\Bigr]u_{E\ell} = E\,u_{E\ell}, \qquad \int_0^\infty\lvert u_{E\ell}\rvert^2\,dr = 1, \qquad (3.271),\ (3.272)
$$

which is [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-1|Theorem §B5.3.1]]; the effective potential (3.273) and Sakurai's "angular momentum barrier" are [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^def-b5-3-2|Def. §B5.3.2]]. For $r^2V \to 0$ at the origin, $u_{E\ell} \approx Ar^{\ell+1} + B/r^\ell$ (3.275), and $B = 0$ (3.276), so $R_{E\ell} \to r^\ell$: this is [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]], with the same two reasons (non-normalizable for $\ell \ge 1$; for $\ell = 0$, $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\delta^3(\mathbf x)$ cannot be balanced). Sakurai adds a third. Read backwards, the radial equation also gives the potential from a known eigenfunction; that exercise (PHY 511, HW 10, Sakurai Problem 3.23) is [[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^ex-c5-4-2|Example §C5.4.2]].

> [!remark] Remark: A third reason for u(0) = 0, from probability conservation
> Keep both terms near the origin, $u \approx Ar^{\ell+1} + Br^{-\ell}$. In the stationary state $\psi = (u/r)\,Y^m_\ell$, the probability per unit time flowing out through a sphere of radius $r$ is, with the current $\mathbf j = \frac\hbar m\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\nabla\psi)$ of [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]] and $\int\lvert Y^m_\ell\rvert^2d\Omega = 1$,
>
> $$
> \Phi(r) = \oint j_r\,r^2\,d\Omega = \frac\hbar m\,r^2\operatorname{Im}\Bigl(R^*\frac{dR}{dr}\Bigr) = \frac\hbar m\operatorname{Im}\bigl(u^*u'\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> since $r^2R^*R' = u^*u' - \lvert u\rvert^2/r$. The terms $\lvert A\rvert^2$ and $\lvert B\rvert^2$ are real, and the cross terms give
>
> $$
> \operatorname{Im}(u^*u') = \operatorname{Im}\bigl[(\ell+1)AB^* - \ell A^*B\bigr] = (2\ell + 1)\operatorname{Im}(AB^*) ,
> $$
>
> independent of $r$. If $A$ and $B$ have a relative phase, a stationary state sends a fixed probability current $(2\ell+1)\frac\hbar m\operatorname{Im}(AB^*)$ through arbitrarily small spheres: the origin acts as a source (or sink) of probability, which the Schrödinger equation with a regular potential does not allow. This rules out $B \ne 0$ for complex coefficients; a real ratio is excluded by the earlier arguments.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.7.1 and Problem 3.27 (solved here)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.1.2: Radial Equation for Bound States, Ends Removed
> Let $r^2V(r) \to 0$ as $r \to 0$ and $V(r) \to 0$ as $r \to \infty$, and let $E < 0$. With
>
> $$
> \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{-2mE}}{\hbar}, \qquad \rho = \kappa r, \qquad u_{E\ell}(\rho) = \rho^{\ell+1}e^{-\rho}\,w(\rho), \qquad (3.277),\ (3.278)
> $$
>
> the radial equation (3.271) becomes
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2w}{d\rho^2} + 2\Bigl(\frac{\ell+1}{\rho} - 1\Bigr)\frac{dw}{d\rho} + \Bigl[\frac{V}{E} - \frac{2(\ell+1)}{\rho}\Bigr]w = 0 , \qquad (3.279)
> $$
>
> with $V = V(\rho/\kappa)$. A bound state is a solution $w$ that is regular at $\rho = 0$ and grows more slowly than $e^{\rho}$ (up to powers), so that $u$ is normalizable.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.7.1, eqs. (3.277)–(3.279) (the manipulations, left to the reader there, are done below) · the user's 511 notes, §"Schrödinger's Equation for Central Potentials" · the user's series, Part IV, §14*

^thm-c6-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Divide (3.271) by $-\hbar^2\kappa^2/2m = E$ and use $\rho$ as variable: $2mV/(\hbar^2\kappa^2) = -V/E$ and $-2mE/(\hbar^2\kappa^2) = 1$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2u}{d\rho^2} = \Bigl[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{\rho^2} + 1 - \frac VE\Bigr]u .
> $$
>
> For $g = \rho^{\ell+1}e^{-\rho}$, $\frac{g'}{g} = \frac{\ell+1}\rho - 1$ and $\frac{g''}{g} = \bigl(\frac{\ell+1}\rho - 1\bigr)^2 - \frac{\ell+1}{\rho^2} = \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{\rho^2} - \frac{2(\ell+1)}\rho + 1$. Insert $u = gw$, $u'' = g''w + 2g'w' + gw''$, and divide by $g$:
>
> $$
> w'' + 2\Bigl(\frac{\ell+1}\rho - 1\Bigr)w' + \Bigl[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{\rho^2} - \frac{2(\ell+1)}\rho + 1\Bigr]w = \Bigl[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{\rho^2} + 1 - \frac VE\Bigr]w ,
> $$
>
> which is (3.279). The factors $\rho^{\ell+1}$ and $e^{-\rho}$ are the behaviours at the two ends ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]]); a solution $w \sim e^{2\rho}$ would restore the growing exponential $u \sim e^{+\rho}$.

^der-c6-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-1|Theorem §B5.3.1]], [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: How the reduction is used
> Level B peeled the two ends off by hand for hydrogen only ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-2|Theorem §B5.4.2]]). Theorem §C6.1.2 does it once for every potential that vanishes at infinity; the remaining equation is a question about $V/E$ alone. For the Coulomb potential $V/E$ is proportional to $1/\rho$, and (3.279) becomes Kummer's equation ([[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^thm-c6-2-2|Theorem §C6.2.2]]). For the oscillator $V$ does not vanish at infinity, and $e^{-\rho}$ must be replaced by the Gaussian $e^{-\rho^2/2}$ (Theorem §C6.1.4).

## The free particle

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.1.3: Free Particle in Spherical Coordinates
> For $V = 0$ and $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m > 0$, put $\rho = kr$ (3.280). The radial equation becomes
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2R}{d\rho^2} + \frac2\rho\frac{dR}{d\rho} + \Bigl[1 - \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{\rho^2}\Bigr]R = 0 , \qquad (3.281)
> $$
>
> whose solutions are combinations of the **spherical Bessel** and **spherical Neumann functions**
>
> $$
> j_\ell(\rho) = (-\rho)^\ell\Bigl[\frac1\rho\frac{d}{d\rho}\Bigr]^\ell\Bigl(\frac{\sin\rho}\rho\Bigr), \qquad n_\ell(\rho) = -(-\rho)^\ell\Bigl[\frac1\rho\frac{d}{d\rho}\Bigr]^\ell\Bigl(\frac{\cos\rho}\rho\Bigr) , \qquad (3.282)
> $$
>
> with $j_0 = \frac{\sin\rho}\rho$, $j_1 = \frac{\sin\rho}{\rho^2} - \frac{\cos\rho}\rho$, $j_2 = \bigl(\frac3{\rho^3} - \frac1\rho\bigr)\sin\rho - \frac{3\cos\rho}{\rho^2}$, $n_0 = -\frac{\cos\rho}\rho$, $n_1 = -\frac{\cos\rho}{\rho^2} - \frac{\sin\rho}\rho$ (3.284)–(3.286), (B.41). Their limits are
>
> $$
> j_\ell(\rho) \xrightarrow[\rho\to0]{} \frac{\rho^\ell}{(2\ell+1)!!}, \quad n_\ell(\rho) \xrightarrow[\rho\to0]{} -\frac{(2\ell-1)!!}{\rho^{\ell+1}}; \qquad j_\ell(\rho) \xrightarrow[\rho\to\infty]{} \frac{\cos\bigl[\rho - (\ell+1)\pi/2\bigr]}\rho, \quad n_\ell(\rho) \xrightarrow[\rho\to\infty]{} \frac{\sin\bigl[\rho - (\ell+1)\pi/2\bigr]}\rho . \qquad (\text{B.42}),\ (\text{B.44})
> $$
>
> So the free states regular at the origin are $\langle\mathbf x|E\,\ell\,m\rangle \propto j_\ell(kr)\,Y^m_\ell(\theta, \phi)$: for each $k$ a complete set of angular-momentum eigenstates, an alternative to the plane waves $|\mathbf p\rangle$ ([[§C2.3 Wave Functions in Position and Momentum Space]]). Where the origin is excluded, $n_\ell$ is allowed too, and the combinations $h^{(1)}_\ell = j_\ell + in_\ell \to e^{i[\rho - (\ell+1)\pi/2]}/\rho$ (B.47), (B.49) describe outgoing spherical waves; at negative energy, $h^{(1)}_\ell(i\kappa r)$ is the solution that decays as $e^{-\kappa r}$ (B.52), e.g. $h^{(1)}_0(i\kappa r) = -e^{-\kappa r}/\kappa r$ (B.53).
>
> What changes from level B: [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^ex-b5-3-1|Example §B5.3.1]] already has $j_\ell$, its small-$\rho$ limit and the ladder argument, as tools for one bound-state problem. New here are $n_\ell$, both asymptotic regimes, the Hankel functions and the decaying solutions at $E < 0$, and the reading of $j_\ell(kr)Y^m_\ell$ as the $|E\,\ell\,m\rangle$ basis of free states.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.7.2, eqs. (3.280)–(3.286), and App. B.5, eqs. (B.39)–(B.53) · the user's 511 notes, §"The Free Particle and Infinite Spherical Well"*

^thm-c6-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Solutions.* With $R = u/r$, (3.281) is the radial equation (3.271) for $V = 0$ divided by $E$. That $\rho\,j_\ell(\rho)$ solves it for each $\ell$ is shown at level B by a ladder argument, the operator $A_\ell = \frac{d}{d\rho} - \frac{\ell+1}\rho$ mapping solutions for $\ell$ to solutions for $\ell + 1$ ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^ex-b5-3-1|Example §B5.3.1]]). The ladder only uses the differential operator, not the starting function, so starting from $\rho\,n_0 = -\cos\rho$, which also solves the $\ell = 0$ equation $u'' = -u$, it produces $\rho\,n_\ell$ up to a constant factor (both families obey the same Rayleigh-type formula (3.282)). The two are independent (one is regular at $0$, the other is not), so they span the solutions.
>
> *Small $\rho$.* Expand $\frac{\sin\rho}\rho = \sum_s\frac{(-1)^s\rho^{2s}}{(2s+1)!}$. The operator $\frac1\rho\frac{d}{d\rho}$ maps $\rho^{2s}$ to $2s\,\rho^{2s-2}$, so $\ell$ applications kill the terms $s < \ell$ and the lowest survivor ($s = \ell$) becomes $\frac{(-1)^\ell}{(2\ell+1)!}\,2^\ell\ell!$. Multiplying by $(-\rho)^\ell$ gives $\frac{2^\ell\ell!}{(2\ell+1)!}\rho^\ell = \frac{\rho^\ell}{(2\ell+1)!!}$, since $(2\ell+1)! = (2\ell+1)!!\,2^\ell\ell!$. The same count for $\frac{\cos\rho}\rho = \frac1\rho - \frac\rho2 + \cdots$ starts from $\rho^{-1}$, which $\ell$ applications turn into $(-1)^\ell(2\ell-1)!!\,\rho^{-2\ell-1}$, so $n_\ell \to -(2\ell-1)!!/\rho^{\ell+1}$.
>
> *Large $\rho$.* The leading term comes from letting every derivative fall on the trigonometric factor: $\bigl[\frac1\rho\frac d{d\rho}\bigr]^\ell\frac{\sin\rho}\rho \approx \rho^{-\ell-1}\sin(\rho + \ell\pi/2)$, so $j_\ell \approx (-1)^\ell\sin(\rho + \ell\pi/2)/\rho = \sin(\rho - \ell\pi/2)/\rho = \cos[\rho - (\ell+1)\pi/2]/\rho$; likewise for $n_\ell$. The Hankel limits follow.

*Uses:* [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-1|Theorem §B5.3.1]], [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^ex-b5-3-1|Example §B5.3.1]]

The **infinite spherical well** ($V = 0$ for $r < a$, $u(a) = 0$) quantizes $k$ by $j_\ell(ka) = 0$, with the zeros $ka = \pi, 2\pi, 3\pi$ ($\ell = 0$), $4.49, 7.73, 10.90$ ($\ell = 1$), $5.76, 9.10, 12.32$ ($\ell = 2$) (3.287)–(3.289), and no degeneracy between different $\ell$; this is level B's [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^ex-b5-3-1|Example §B5.3.1]], derived the same way (the zeros were recomputed here: $4.4934$, $7.7253$, $10.9041$ and $5.7635$, $9.0950$, $12.3229$). Sakurai also gives $j_\ell(z) = \frac{1}{2i^\ell}\int_{-1}^1e^{izs}P_\ell(s)\,ds$ (3.283), the bridge between the bases $|\mathbf p\rangle$ and $|E\,\ell\,m\rangle$ used in scattering ([[§C10.3 Partial Waves and Phase Shifts#^thm-c10-3-1|Theorem §C10.3.1]]).

## The isotropic oscillator

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.1.4: Isotropic Oscillator by the Radial Equation
> For $H = \dfrac{\mathbf p^2}{2m} + \dfrac12m\omega^2r^2$ (3.290), the bound states $|q\,\ell\,m\rangle$ have
>
> $$
> E_{q\ell} = \Bigl(2q + \ell + \frac32\Bigr)\hbar\omega \equiv \Bigl(N + \frac32\Bigr)\hbar\omega, \qquad q, \ell = 0, 1, 2, \dots, \qquad N = 2q + \ell , \qquad (3.302)
> $$
>
> with radial functions $R = \rho^\ell e^{-\rho^2/2}f(\rho)$, $\rho = r\sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}$, where $f$ is a polynomial of degree $2q$ in $\rho$ containing only even powers; $q$ counts the radial nodes. The level $N$ contains $\ell = N, N-2, \dots, 1$ or $0$, so all its states have parity $(-1)^N$, and its degeneracy is
>
> $$
> \sum_{\ell = N, N-2, \dots}(2\ell + 1) = \frac{(N+1)(N+2)}{2} ,
> $$
>
> the count found in Cartesian coordinates ([[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-7|Theorem §B5.1.7]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.7.3, eqs. (3.290)–(3.304) · the user's 511 notes, §"The Isotropic Harmonic Oscillator"*

^thm-c6-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Dimensionless form.* With $E = \frac12\hbar\omega\lambda$ and $r = (\hbar/m\omega)^{1/2}\rho$ (3.291), (3.271) becomes $u'' - \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{\rho^2}u + (\lambda - \rho^2)u = 0$ (3.292). Since $V$ grows at infinity, Theorem §C6.1.2 does not apply; instead write $u = \rho^{\ell+1}e^{-\rho^2/2}f(\rho)$ (3.293). For $g = \rho^{\ell+1}e^{-\rho^2/2}$, $g'/g = \frac{\ell+1}\rho - \rho$ and $g''/g = \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{\rho^2} + \rho^2 - (2\ell + 3)$, and $u = gf$ gives, after multiplication by $\rho$,
>
> $$
> \rho f'' + 2\bigl[(\ell+1) - \rho^2\bigr]f' + \bigl[\lambda - (2\ell + 3)\bigr]\rho f = 0 . \qquad (3.294)
> $$
>
> *Series.* With $f = \sum_na_n\rho^n$ (3.295), the coefficient of $\rho^0$ is $2(\ell+1)a_1$, so $a_1 = 0$ (3.296), and the coefficient of $\rho^{n+1}$ gives $(n+2)(n+2\ell+3)a_{n+2} = (2n + 2\ell + 3 - \lambda)a_n$:
>
> $$
> a_{n+2} = \frac{2n + 2\ell + 3 - \lambda}{(n+2)(n+2\ell+3)}\,a_n . \qquad (3.298)
> $$
>
> Only even powers survive. If the series does not stop, $a_{n+2}/a_n \to 2/n = 1/q$ with $n = 2q$ (3.299), the ratio of the series of $e^{\rho^2}$ (3.300), and $u \sim \rho^{\ell+1}e^{+\rho^2/2}$ is not normalizable. So the series terminates at some even $n = 2q$: $2n + 2\ell + 3 - \lambda = 0$ (3.301), i.e. $\lambda = 4q + 2\ell + 3$ and $E = \frac12\hbar\omega\lambda$ is (3.302). That $q$ counts nodes is stated by Sakurai; it is consistent with the recursion, whose coefficients alternate in sign for $n < 2q$, so that $f$, as a polynomial in $\rho^2$, has at most $q$ positive roots (Descartes' rule).
>
> *Degeneracy.* For given $N$, $\ell = N - 2q$ runs over $N, N - 2, \dots$ down to $0$ or $1$, each with $2\ell + 1$ values of $m$; the parity of $Y^m_\ell$ is $(-1)^\ell = (-1)^N$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^rem-b5-2-6|Remark: Reading the spherical harmonics]]). With $p = \lfloor N/2\rfloor$ and $\ell = N - 2i$,
>
> $$
> \sum_{i=0}^{p}(2N - 4i + 1) = (p+1)(2N + 1 - 2p) = \begin{cases}(\tfrac N2 + 1)(N+1), & N = 2p,\\ \tfrac{N+1}{2}(N+2), & N = 2p+1,\end{cases}
> $$
>
> both equal to $(N+1)(N+2)/2$.

*Uses:* [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-1|Theorem §B5.3.1]], [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]], [[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^thm-b5-1-7|Theorem §B5.1.7]]

> [!remark] Remark: Two bases for one level
> The same Hamiltonian is $H = H_x + H_y + H_z$ with $H_i = \hbar\omega(a_i^\dagger a_i + \frac12)$ (3.303), with eigenkets $|n_x\,n_y\,n_z\rangle$ and $E = (n_x + n_y + n_z + \frac32)\hbar\omega$ (3.304). The level $N$ is one $\frac12(N+1)(N+2)$-dimensional space with two orthonormal bases, $|n_x\,n_y\,n_z\rangle$ and $|q\,\ell\,m\rangle$; they are related by a unitary matrix $\langle n_xn_yn_z|q\ell m\rangle$ ([[§C1.4 Change of Basis and Unitary Equivalence#^thm-c1-4-1|Theorem §C1.4.1]]), computed for $N \le 2$ in Example §C6.1.2. The rotational symmetry explains why the $2\ell + 1$ states of each $\ell$ coincide (Theorem §C6.1.1); it does not explain why $\ell = N$ and $\ell = N - 2$ coincide. The nuclear shell model starts from these levels and from those of the spherical well (Sakurai Fig. 3.6). Level B noted the same degeneracy and the radial counting without working it ([[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^rem-b5-3-3|★ Remark: The isotropic oscillator by angular momentum]], [[§B5.1 The Schrödinger Equation in Three Dimensions#^rem-b5-1-5|★ Remark: Degeneracies compared]]).

^rem-c6-1-4

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Conserved operators beyond the angular momentum
> All nine operators $a_i^\dagger a_j$ commute with $H$: since $[a_i^\dagger a_j, a_k^\dagger a_k] = a_i^\dagger\delta_{jk}a_k - a_k^\dagger\delta_{ik}a_j$, summing over $k$ gives $a_i^\dagger a_j - a_i^\dagger a_j = 0$. As a $3\times3$ array of operators they split like any dyadic ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem#^thm-c7-2-2|Theorem §C7.2.2]]): the trace is $N = \sum_ia_i^\dagger a_i$; the antisymmetric part is the angular momentum, $L_k = -i\hbar\epsilon_{kij}a_i^\dagger a_j$ (Example §C6.1.2); and the symmetric traceless part, five operators such as $Q_{zz} = a_z^\dagger a_z - \frac12(a_x^\dagger a_x + a_y^\dagger a_y)$, is a rank-2 tensor that commutes with $H$ but not with $\mathbf L^2$. It connects different $\ell$ inside one level: with the states of Example §C6.1.2, $Q_{zz}|q{=}1, \ell{=}0, m{=}0\rangle = -\sqrt2\,|q{=}0, \ell{=}2, m{=}0\rangle$ (computed here). An operator that commutes with $H$ and maps an $\ell = 0$ state onto an $\ell = 2$ state forces the two to have the same energy, which is the "accidental" degeneracy of the oscillator. The group these operators generate, and the analogous Runge–Lenz symmetry of hydrogen, are not treated in the batch-1 sources; symmetries in general are Chapter C8★ ([[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-3|Theorem §C8.1.3]]), where the group of the $a_i^\dagger a_j$ and the Runge–Lenz symmetry are taken up ([[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^rem-c8-1-7|Remark: The hidden symmetry of the isotropic oscillator]], [[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-5|Theorem §C8.1.5]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C6.1.1: The Finite Spherical Box
> A particle is confined by $V = 0$ for $r < a$ and $V = V_0 = \hbar^2\beta^2/2ma^2$ for $r > a$. Find the lowest $\ell = 0$ level for $\beta = 4, 10, 25, 100$, and compare with the infinite well.
>
> For $\ell = 0$, $u'' = -k^2u$ inside, with $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, and $u'' = \kappa^2u$ outside, with $V_0 - E = \hbar^2\kappa^2/2m$. Regularity gives $u = A\sin kr$ inside; decay gives $u = Ce^{-\kappa r}$ outside, which is $h^{(1)}_0(i\kappa r)$ of Theorem §C6.1.3 up to a factor $r$. Continuity of $u$ and $u'$ at $r = a$ gives $k\cot ka = -\kappa$. In the variable $x = ka$, with $(ka)^2 + (\kappa a)^2 = \beta^2$,
>
> $$
> x\cot x = -\sqrt{\beta^2 - x^2}, \qquad \frac{E}{\hbar^2/2ma^2} = x^2 .
> $$
>
> The right side is negative, so $x > \pi/2$, and a bound state exists only for $\beta > \pi/2$ (level B's [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^rem-b5-3-4|★ Remark: A shallow spherical well need not bind]]). Solving numerically:
>
> | $\beta$ | $4$ | $10$ | $25$ | $100$ | $\infty$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $x = ka$ | $2.4746$ | $2.8523$ | $3.0205$ | $3.1105$ | $\pi$ |
> | $E/(\hbar^2/2ma^2)$ | $6.124$ | $8.136$ | $9.123$ | $9.675$ | $\pi^2 = 9.870$ |
>
> The energies approach the infinite-well value (3.287) from below. For large $\beta$, $x\cot x \approx -\beta$ near $x = \pi$ gives $\pi - x \approx \pi/\beta$, so $E \approx \pi^2(1 - 2/\beta)\,\hbar^2/2ma^2$: the wave function leaks a distance $1/\kappa \approx a/\beta$ into the wall, and the well acts as if its radius were $a(1 + 1/\beta)$. The deeper boxes also hold excited $s$ levels: for $\beta = 10$ the next two are $32.25$ and $70.95$ (infinite well: $4\pi^2 = 39.48$, $9\pi^2 = 88.83$).
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 3.28 (solved here, numerically) · Sakurai App. B.5, eqs. (B.45)–(B.53)*

> [!example] Example §C6.1.2: Oscillator States in Two Bases
> For the isotropic oscillator, show that $L_i = -i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}a_j^\dagger a_k$ and $\mathbf L^2 = \hbar^2\bigl[N(N+1) - a_k^\dagger a_k^\dagger a_ja_j\bigr]$ with $N = a_j^\dagger a_j$ (sums over repeated indices), and express $|q\,\ell\,m\rangle$ for $N \le 2$ in the basis $|n_x\,n_y\,n_z\rangle$.
>
> *Angular momentum.* With $x_j = \sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}\,(a_j + a_j^\dagger)$ and $p_k = i\sqrt{m\omega\hbar/2}\,(a_k^\dagger - a_k)$ ([[§C3.4 The Harmonic Oscillator and Coherent States Revisited]]),
>
> $$
> L_i = \epsilon_{ijk}x_jp_k = \frac{i\hbar}2\,\epsilon_{ijk}\bigl(a_ja_k^\dagger - a_ja_k + a_j^\dagger a_k^\dagger - a_j^\dagger a_k\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The terms $a_ja_k$ and $a_j^\dagger a_k^\dagger$ are symmetric in $j, k$ and drop out; $\epsilon_{ijk}a_ja_k^\dagger = \epsilon_{ijk}(a_k^\dagger a_j + \delta_{jk}) = -\epsilon_{ijk}a_j^\dagger a_k$ (relabel $j \leftrightarrow k$). So $L_i = -i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}a_j^\dagger a_k$, which is Sakurai's $i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}a_ja_k^\dagger$. Then, with $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{ilm} = \delta_{jl}\delta_{km} - \delta_{jm}\delta_{kl}$,
>
> $$
> \mathbf L^2 = -\hbar^2\bigl(a_j^\dagger a_ka_j^\dagger a_k - a_j^\dagger a_ka_k^\dagger a_j\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The first term is $a_j^\dagger(a_j^\dagger a_k + \delta_{jk})a_k = a_j^\dagger a_j^\dagger a_ka_k + N$. In the second, $\sum_ka_ka_k^\dagger = N + 3$ and $a_j^\dagger N = (N - 1)a_j^\dagger$, so it is $a_j^\dagger(N + 3)a_j = (N + 2)N$. Hence $\mathbf L^2 = \hbar^2[N(N+2) - N - a_k^\dagger a_k^\dagger a_ja_j] = \hbar^2[N(N+1) - a_k^\dagger a_k^\dagger a_ja_j]$. On an $N = 2$ state the last term measures the component along $\sum_k(a_k^\dagger)^2|0\rangle$, the rotation-invariant (scalar) pair of quanta.
>
> *The states.* $N = 1$ has only $\ell = 1$. $|0\,1\,0\rangle$ must be killed by $L_z = -i\hbar(a_x^\dagger a_y - a_y^\dagger a_x)$, and $L_\pm$ must act with the standard coefficients; this gives
>
> $$
> |0\,1\,0\rangle = |0\,0\,1\rangle_c, \qquad |0\,1\,{\pm1}\rangle = \mp\frac{|1\,0\,0\rangle_c \pm i\,|0\,1\,0\rangle_c}{\sqrt2} ,
> $$
>
> in which the kets with subscript $c$ are the Cartesian $|n_x\,n_y\,n_z\rangle_c$ and those on the left are $|q\,\ell\,m\rangle$. $N = 2$ has $\ell = 2$ ($q = 0$) and $\ell = 0$ ($q = 1$):
>
> $$
> |0\,2\,{\pm2}\rangle = \tfrac12\bigl(|2\,0\,0\rangle_c \pm i\sqrt2\,|1\,1\,0\rangle_c - |0\,2\,0\rangle_c\bigr), \qquad |0\,2\,{\pm1}\rangle = \mp\frac{|1\,0\,1\rangle_c \pm i\,|0\,1\,1\rangle_c}{\sqrt2}, \qquad |0\,2\,0\rangle = \frac{2\,|0\,0\,2\rangle_c - |2\,0\,0\rangle_c - |0\,2\,0\rangle_c}{\sqrt6},
> $$
>
> $$
> |1\,0\,0\rangle = -\frac{|2\,0\,0\rangle_c + |0\,2\,0\rangle_c + |0\,0\,2\rangle_c}{\sqrt3} .
> $$
>
> *Checks.* Each state has the stated $\mathbf L^2$ and $L_z$ eigenvalues, and $L_-|0\,2\,2\rangle = 2\hbar|0\,2\,1\rangle$, $L_-|0\,2\,1\rangle = \sqrt6\hbar|0\,2\,0\rangle$, $L_-|0\,1\,1\rangle = \sqrt2\hbar|0\,1\,0\rangle$, as Theorem §C6.1.1 requires (verified by matrix computation in the truncated Fock space). In coordinates, with $\psi_1(x) = \sqrt2\,x\,\psi_0(x)$ and $\psi_2(x) = \frac{2x^2 - 1}{\sqrt2}\psi_0(x)$ in units $\hbar/m\omega = 1$, $|0\,2\,2\rangle$ becomes $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(x + iy)^2\psi_0(x)\psi_0(y)\psi_0(z)$ and $|1\,0\,0\rangle_c$ becomes $-\frac{2r^2 - 3}{\sqrt6}\,e^{-r^2/2}/\pi^{3/4}$: the first is $r^2e^{-r^2/2}$ times $Y^2_2$ ($\ell = 2$, $q = 0$, no radial node), the second is spherically symmetric with one radial node at $r^2 = \frac32$ ($q = 1$), as Theorem §C6.1.4 predicts. The overall phase of each multiplet is a choice; here the radial function is positive near the origin, and $|q\,\ell\,m\rangle$ then carries the phase of $Y^m_\ell$.
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 3.29 (solved here)*
